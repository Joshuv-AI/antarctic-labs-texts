Gradient-based data and parameter dimension reduction for Bayesian models: an information theoretic perspective
Ricardo Baptista, Youssef Marzouk, Olivier Zahm

Abstract
We consider the problem of reducing the dimensions of parameters and data in non-Gaussian Bayesian inference problems. Our goal is to identify an “informed” subspace of the parameters and an “informative” subspace of the data so that a high-dimensional inference problem can be approximately reformulated in low-to-moderate dimensions, thereby improving the computational efficiency of many inference techniques. To do so, we exploit gradient evaluations of the log-likelihood function. Furthermore, we use an information-theoretic analysis to derive a bound on the posterior error due to parameter and data dimension reduction. This bound relies on logarithmic Sobolev inequalities, and it reveals the appropriate dimensions of the reduced variables. We compare our method with classical dimension reduction techniques, such as principal component analysis and canonical correlation analysis, on applications ranging from mechanics to image processing.

\pi_{X|Y}(x|y)\propto\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\pi_{X}(x).

Here,

\pi_{Y|X}

can scale poorly with dimension, particularly when accurate characterizations of posterior structure are desired.

Dimension reduction has received increasing attention as a way of reducing the computational cost of inference procedures.
On the one hand, many recently proposed methods seek to reduce the dimension of the parameter

Cui et al. 2014

Zahm et al. 2022

Projecting

Izmailov et al. 2020

On the other hand, reducing the dimension of

is critical to performing
inference in settings with high-dimensional data, such as spectra and time-series signals
(
Ma et al. 2013
)
. For instance, approximate Bayesian computation (ABC) generates posterior samples by comparing simulated and observed data

Previous work in ABC reduces the data dimension by seeking low-dimensional summary statistics designed to retain information about the parameters

Fearnhead and Prangle 2012

Giraldi et al. 2018

Trippe et al. 2019

Optimal experimental design can also be seen as a way of reducing the data dimension, by sub-selecting the most important

Jagalur-Mohan and Marzouk 2021

Y

In this work, our goal is to concurrently reduce the dimensions of the parameter and of the data using gradients of the log-likelihood
\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
, where
d
and
m
are the dimensions of
X
and
Y
, respectively. To do so, we compute the eigenvectors of the diagnostic matrices

\displaystyle H_{X}

\displaystyle=\int\big(\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}\big)^{T}\big(\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}\big)\mathrm{d}\pi_{X,Y}

(1)

\displaystyle H_{Y}

\displaystyle=\int\big(\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}\big)\big(\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}\big)^{T}\mathrm{d}\pi_{X,Y},

(2)

and define the informed parameters
X_{r}
and the informative data
Y_{s}
as projections of
X
and
Y
onto the first
r\ll d
and
s\ll m
components of their respective eigenbases.
Our definitions of
H_{X}
and
H_{Y}
follow from an information theoretic analysis of the approximation error due to simultaneous dimension reduction of
X
and
Y
.
Specifically, we bound the expected Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence from the approximate posterior to the exact posterior as follows:

\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}||\pi^{*}_{X|Y})\right]\leq\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})^{2}\left(\sum_{i>r}\lambda_{i}(H_{X})+\sum_{j>s}\lambda_{j}(H_{Y})\right),

where
\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(x|y)\propto\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}(y_{s}|x_{r})\pi_{X}(x)
is the posterior approximation defined using the marginal conditional distribution
\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}
of the reduced parameter and reduced data.
In the expression above,
\lambda_{i}(\cdot)
denotes the
i
th largest eigenvalue of a matrix and
\overline{C}(\cdot)

is the subspace logarithmic Sobolev constant of a probability density. This constant will be defined and discussed extensively later in the paper.
The derivation of this error bound relies on
a result in
Baptista et al. 2021
establishing gradient-based bounds on conditional mutual information.
Fast decay of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(H_{X})
and
\lambda_{j}(H_{Y})
above ensures small error for low dimensions
r,s
.

For Gaussian error models of the form
\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\propto\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\|G(x)-y\|^{2}_{2})
, the diagnostic matrices simplify to

H_{X}=\int\nabla G^{T}\nabla G\mathrm{d}\pi_{X}\qquad\text{and}\qquad H_{Y}=\int\nabla G\nabla G^{T}\mathrm{d}\pi_{X},

where
\nabla G(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
is the Jacobian of the (nonlinear) forward model
G\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
In this particular case,
H_{X}
is the same diagnostic matrix as one introduced in earlier work
(
Cui and Zahm 2021
;
Cui et al. 2021
)
. The general form of
H_{X}
proposed in (
1

Furthermore, a gradient-based method for data reduction—particularly one offering error guarantees in the general setting tackled here—has not, to the best of our knowledge, been previously proposed.
The information theoretic error analysis of simultaneous data and parameter reduction that we develop in this paper is new as well.
We also show how this approach generalizes classic dimension reduction techniques, such as canonical correlation analysis.

Section

7

2
Reducing parameter and data dimensions

Our joint parameter–data dimension reduction method relies on the detection of conditional independence between blocks of variables. Given two unitary matrices
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, partitioned as column blocks
U=[U_{r},U_{\perp}]
and
V=[V_{s},V_{\perp}]
with
U_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
and
V_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times s}
, we decompose
X
as

X=U_{r}X_{r}+U_{\perp}X_{\perp}\qquad\text{where }\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}X_{r}=U_{r}^{T}X\\
X_{\perp}=U_{\perp}^{T}X\end{array}\right.,

(3)

and
Y
as

Y=V_{s}Y_{s}~+V_{\perp}Y_{\perp}~\qquad\text{where }\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}Y_{s}=V_{s}^{T}Y\\
Y_{\perp}=V_{\perp}^{T}Y\end{array}\right..

(4)

In this decomposition, if
X_{\perp}
is independent of the data
Y
after conditioning on
X_{r}
—that is, if
X_{\perp}\perp\!\!\!\perp Y|X_{r}
—then
X_{\perp}
is interpreted as the un-informed component of the parameter.
In the same way, if
Y_{\perp}
is independent of
X
after conditioning on
Y_{s}
—i.e.,
Y_{\perp}\perp\!\!\!\perp X|Y_{s}
—then
Y_{\perp}
is interpreted as the non-informative component of the data.
Under these two conditional independence properties
2
2

2

In fact,
X_{\perp}\perp\!\!\!\perp Y|X_{r}
and
Y_{\perp}\perp\!\!\!\perp X_{r}|Y_{s}
are sufficient to write the factorization in (
5
).
, the joint probability density function of
X
and
Y
factorizes as
\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)=\pi_{X_{\perp}|X_{r}}(x_{\perp}|x_{r})\pi_{X_{r},Y_{s}}(x_{r},y_{s})\pi_{Y_{\perp}|Y_{s}}(y_{\perp}|y_{s})
, so that the posterior satisfies
\pi_{X|Y}=\pi^{*}_{X|Y}
with

\displaystyle\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(x|y)

\displaystyle:=\pi_{X_{r}|Y_{s}}(x_{r}|y_{s})\pi_{X_{\perp}|X_{r}}(x_{\perp}|x_{r}).

(5)

In other words, the inference problem of characterizing
X|Y
can be transformed into a lower-dimensional inference problem that involves characterizing
X_{r}|Y_{s}
.
In practice, however, the conditional independence criteria
X_{\perp}\perp\!\!\!\perp Y|X_{r}
and
Y_{\perp}\perp\!\!\!\perp X|Y_{s}
might not be exactly satisfied, and so
\pi_{X|Y}\neq\pi^{*}_{X|Y}
in general.
In this case, our goal is to identify the unitary matrices
U
and
V
and to select the smallest possible (in a sense to be clarified later) effective dimensions
r\leq d
and
s\leq m
so that the KL divergence from
\pi^{*}_{X|Y}
to
\pi_{X|Y}
is controlled in expectation over the data; that is

\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))\right]\leq\epsilon,

(6)

for some prescribed tolerance
\epsilon>0
.
The following proposition shows that, given the parameter and data decompositions (
3
) and (
4
), the posterior approximation
\pi^{*}_{X|Y}
in (
5
) is optimal for the expected KL loss.

.

Let
(X,Y)\sim\pi_{X,Y}
be decomposed as in (
3
) and (
4
). Then the posterior approximation
\pi^{*}_{X|Y}
defined in (

) satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\widetilde{\pi}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))\right],

(7)

for any posterior approximation of the form
\widetilde{\pi}_{X|Y}(x|y)=f_{1}(x_{r},y_{s})f_{2}(x_{\perp},x_{r})
with non-negative functions
f_{1},f_{2}
.

Thus, once the matrices
U,V
are identified and the effective dimensions
r,s
are determined, the optimal posterior approximation (
5
) is accessible via

\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(x|y)\propto\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}(y_{s}|x_{r})\pi_{X}(x),

where the reduced likelihood
\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}(y_{s}|x_{r})
is accessible by marginalizing the likelihood function
\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)
over

\displaystyle\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}(y_{s}|x_{r})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m-s}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-r}}\pi_{Y|X}(V_{s}y_{s}+V_{\perp}y_{\perp}|U_{r}x_{r}+U_{\perp}x_{\perp})\pi_{X_{\perp}|X_{r}}(x_{\perp}|x_{r})\mathrm{d}x_{\perp}\mathrm{d}y_{\perp}

(8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi_{X_{r}}(x_{r})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m-s}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-r}}\pi_{X,Y}(U_{r}x_{r}+U_{\perp}X_{\perp},V_{s}y_{s}+V_{\perp}y_{\perp})\mathrm{d}x_{\perp}\mathrm{d}y_{\perp}.

We note that with

(i.e., no data reduction), the reduced likelihood
\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}=\pi_{Y|X_{r}}

2.1
Gradient-based bound on expected posterior approximation error

In this section we present our main result, which consists in a gradient-based bound on the expected KL divergence (
6
). This bound will guide the construction of the matrices
U
and
V
. In the following,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the canonical norm of the Euclidean space.

Definition 1

(Logarithmic Sobolev inequality)
.

A random variable
Z
with density
\pi_{Z}
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfies the
logarithmic Sobolev inequality
if there exists a constant
C<\infty
such that

\int h(z)\log\left(\frac{h(z)}{\int h\mathrm{d}\pi_{Z}}\right)\pi_{Z}(z)\mathrm{d}z\leq\frac{C}{2}\int\|\nabla h(z)\|^{2}h(z)\,\pi_{Z}(z)\mathrm{d}z,

(9)

holds for any smooth function
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. The smallest constant
C=C(\pi_{Z})
such that (
9
) holds is called the
logarithmic Sobolev constant
of
Z
.

Definition 2

(Subspace logarithmic Sobolev inequality)
.

A random variable
Z
with density
\pi_{Z}
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfies the
subspace logarithmic Sobolev inequality
if there exists a constant
\overline{C}<\infty
such that for any unitary matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and for any block decomposition
W=[W_{t},W_{\perp}]
with
W_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times t},t\leq p
, and for any
z_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-t}
, the conditional random vector
Z_{t}|Z_{\perp}=z_{\perp}
with
Z_{t}=W_{t}^{T}Z
and
Z_{\perp}=W_{\perp}^{T}Z

satisfies the logarithmic Sobolev inequality with

C(\pi_{Z_{t}|Z_{\perp}=z_{\perp}})\leq\overline{C}.

(10)

The smallest constant
\overline{C}=\overline{C}(\pi_{Z})
such that (
10
) holds is called the
subspace logarithmic Sobolev constant
of
Z
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(X,Y)
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
which satisfies the subspace logarithmic Sobolev inequality with constant
\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})<\infty
. Then for any unitary matrices
U=[U_{r},U_{\perp}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
V=[V_{s},V_{\perp}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot,Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot,Y))\right]\leq\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})^{2}\left(\Tr(U_{\perp}^{T}H_{X}U_{\perp})+\Tr(V_{\perp}^{T}H_{Y}V_{\perp})\right).

(11)

Here,
\pi^{*}_{X|Y}
is as in (
5
) and the matrices
H_{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
H_{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
are given by

\displaystyle H_{X}

\displaystyle=\int\big(\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\big)^{T}\big(\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\big)\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

(12)

\displaystyle H_{Y}

\displaystyle=\int\big(\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\big)\big(\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\big)^{T}\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

(13)

where the matrix
\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
is defined by

\Big(\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\Big)_{i,j}=\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{y_{i}}\log\pi_{Y|X}(y|x).

Proof.

See Section
3
.
∎

Throughout this paper, we will use the bound (
11
) by finding structured unitary matrices
U,V
that minimize the right-hand side of (
11
). Due to their central role, the matrices
H_{X}
and
H_{Y}
are called the
diagnostic matrices
.

Before going further, let us comment on the assumption
\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})<\infty
. As shown in
Zahm et al. 2022
, a sufficient condition for a distribution
\pi_{Z}
to satisfy the subspace log-Sobolev inequality is that it has convex support and that its density be of the form
\pi_{Z}(z)=\exp(-V(z)-\Psi(z))
, where
V
is a smooth convex function such that
\nabla^{2}V(z)\succeq\rho I
for some
\rho>0
, and where
\Psi
is a function with bounded oscillation such that
\kappa=\sup\Psi-\inf\Psi<\infty
. Then, from the Bakry–Émery theorem
(
Bakry and Émery 1985
)
and the Holley–Stroock perturbation lemma
(
Holley and Stroock 1987
)
, we obtain
\overline{C}(\pi_{Z})\leq\exp(\kappa)/\rho
.
As shown in the following example, this condition is (trivially) satisfied when the joint distribution
\pi_{X,Y}
is Gaussian.
We refer the reader to
Zahm et al. 2022
for additional examples of distributions that satisfy the subspace log-Sobolev inequality. In these general cases, the constant
\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})
will not be available or computable in practice. Yet we can still exploit the bound (
11
) without having access to
\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})
.

Example 1

(Gaussian joint density)
.

Let
\pi_{X,Y}
be the joint density

\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|y-Gx\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}\right),

(14)

where
G\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
.
This corresponds to a Bayesian inverse problem with standard normal prior, linear forward model
x\mapsto Gx
, and standard normal observational error. Given that
\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\propto\exp(-V(x,y))
with the quadratic potential

V(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
y\end{smallmatrix})^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
y\end{smallmatrix})
where

\Sigma\coloneqq\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}\begin{pmatrix}X\\
Y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\textrm{I}_{d}+G^{T}G&-G^{T}\\
-G&\textrm{I}_{m}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\textrm{I}_{d}&G^{T}\\
G&GG^{T}+\textrm{I}_{m}\end{bmatrix},

we deduce (see
Zahm et al. 2022
) that
\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})
is bounded by
\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)
, i.e., the largest eigenvalue of the joint covariance matrix
\Sigma
.
As shown in Appendix
B
,
\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)
can be computed explicitly, so that we obtain

\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(2+\sigma_{\max}(G)^{2}+\sigma_{\max}(G)\sqrt{\sigma_{\max}(G)^{2}+4}\right),

(15)

where
\sigma_{\max}(G)
is the maximum singular value of
G
.
Furthermore, the diagnostic matrices
H_{X}
and
H_{Y}
in (
12
) and (
13
) are given by
H_{X}=G^{T}G
and
H_{Y}=GG^{T}
.

Next we show that the joint distribution
\pi_{X,Y}
arising in certain
nonlinear
inverse problems can also satisfy the subspace logarithmic Sobolev inequality.

Example 2

(Nonlinear inverse problem with Gaussian noise and Gaussian prior)
.

Let the joint density of
X
and
Y
be

\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|y-G(x)\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}\right),

where
G\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a nonlinear forward model.
Denoting the Jacobian of the forward model by
\nabla G(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
, we can write

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}\log\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-\nabla_{X}\nabla_{X}\log\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)&-\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}\log\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\\
-\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)&-\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\textrm{I}_{d}+\nabla G(x)^{T}\nabla G(x)&-\nabla G(x)^{T}\\
-\nabla G(x)&\textrm{I}_{m}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}A(x,y)&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

where the matrix
A(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is given by
(A(x,y))_{i,j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{i,j}^{2}G_{k}(x)(y-G(x))_{k}.

As in the previous example, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\textrm{I}_{d}+\nabla G(x)^{T}\nabla G(x)&-\nabla G(x)^{T}\\
-\nabla G(x)&\textrm{I}_{m}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\lambda(x)^{-1},

where
\lambda(x)=\frac{1}{2}(2+\sigma_{\max}(\nabla G(x))^{2}+\sigma_{\max}(\nabla G(x))\sqrt{\sigma_{\max}(\nabla G(x))^{2}+4})
so that

-\nabla^{2}\log\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\succeq(\lambda(x)^{-1}-\lambda_{\max}(A(x,y)))\begin{bmatrix}\textrm{I}_{d}&0\\
0&\textrm{I}_{m}\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, if there exists a constant
C<\infty
such that
\lambda(x)^{-1}-\lambda_{\max}(A(x,y))\geq 1/C
uniformly over
x,y
, then
\pi_{X,Y}
satisfies the subspace log-Sobolev inequality with
\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})\leq C
.
Furthermore, since
\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)=\nabla G(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
, the diagnostic matrices are given by

\displaystyle H_{X}

\displaystyle=\int\nabla G(x)^{T}\nabla G(x)\pi_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle H_{Y}

\displaystyle=\int\nabla G(x)\nabla G(x)^{T}\pi_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

2.2
Constructing
U,V
by minimizing the upper bound

In this section, we assume the reduced dimensions
r
and
s
are prescribed. (A discussion of how to select
r,s
is postponed to Section
2.3
.)
We propose two different approaches to build the unitary matrices
U=[U_{r},U_{\perp}]
and
V=[V_{s},V_{\perp}]
.
The first, referred to as
optimal rotation
, consists in minimizing the upper bound (
11
) by solving

\min_{U_{\perp},V_{\perp}}\Tr(U_{\perp}^{T}H_{X}U_{\perp})+\Tr(V_{\perp}^{T}H_{Y}V_{\perp}),

(16)

subject to
U_{\perp}^{T}U_{\perp}=\textrm{I}_{d-r}
and
V_{\perp}^{T}V_{\perp}=\textrm{I}_{m-s}
.
The second approach, referred to as
optimal permutation
, consists in solving (
16
) with the additional constraint that
U,V
are permutation matrices. That way,
X_{r}=U_{r}^{T}X
and
Y_{s}=V_{s}^{T}Y
contain a subset of coordinates of
X
and
Y
and hence the dimension reduction corresponds to a coordinate selection.

In both approaches, the optimal solutions
U,V
are independent of the reduced dimensions
r
and
s
. More specifically, there exist matrices
U,V
(independent of
r,s
) such that the solution to (
16
) can be extracted from the last columns of
U,V
for any
r,s
.

Optimal rotation

We recall Corollary 4.3.39 in
Horn and Johnson 2012
for the variational characterization of eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices:

Proposition 2
.

Let
H\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be a symmetric positive definite matrix with eigenpairs
(\lambda_{i},w_{i})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
, meaning
Hw_{i}=\lambda_{i}w_{i}
, where
\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1}
and
\|w_{i}\|_{2}=1
for all
i
. Then, for any
t<p
we have

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}W_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(p-t)}\\
W_{\perp}^{T}W_{\perp}=\textrm{I}_{p-t}\end{subarray}}\Tr(W_{\perp}^{T}HW_{\perp})=\sum_{i=t+1}^{p}\lambda_{i},

where the solution is given by
W_{\perp}=[w_{t+1},\dots,w_{p}]
.

Let
(\lambda_{i}(H_{X}),u_{i})
and
(\lambda_{i}(H_{Y}),v_{i})
denote the
i
-th largest eigenpairs of
H_{X}
and
H_{Y}
, respectively.
Then, Proposition
2
ensures that for any
r,s
,

\displaystyle U_{\perp}

\displaystyle=[u_{r+1},\ldots,u_{d}]

\displaystyle V_{\perp}

\displaystyle=[v_{s+1},\ldots,v_{m}],

is the optimal solution to (
16
). This choice yields the optimal bound

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))\right]\leq\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})^{2}\left(\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(H_{X})+\sum_{i=s+1}^{m}\lambda_{i}(H_{Y})\right).

(17)

The eigenvectors
u_{i},v_{i}
can be precomputed without knowing
r
and
s
. The advantage of this construction is that to increase
r
or
s
, one only needs to compute the additional eigenvectors.

Remark 1
.

In practice, it is sufficient to compute the matrices
U_{r}=[u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}]
and
V_{s}=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{s}]
to reduce the parameter and data dimensions; see Section
6
. The (possibly much larger) matrices
U_{\perp}
and
V_{\perp}
are never assembled in practice.

Optimal permutation

We now constrain
U
and
V
to be permutation matrices so that
UX=(X_{\sigma_{X}(1)},\ldots,X_{\sigma_{X}(d)})
and
VY=(Y_{\sigma_{Y}(1)},\ldots,Y_{\sigma_{Y}(m)})
where
\sigma_{X}
and
\sigma_{Y}
are permutations of
\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
\{1,\ldots,m\}
, respectively. Then (
16
) becomes

\min_{\sigma_{X},\sigma_{Y}}\left(\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}(H_{X})_{\sigma_{X}(i),\sigma_{X}(i)}+\sum_{i=s+1}^{m}(H_{Y})_{\sigma_{Y}(i),\sigma_{Y}(i)}\right).

(18)

The optimal permutations
\sigma_{X}
and
\sigma_{Y}
are those which sort the
diagonal
terms of
H_{X}
and
H_{Y}
in decreasing order; that is

\displaystyle(H_{X})_{\sigma_{X}(i),\sigma_{X}(i)}

\displaystyle\geq(H_{X})_{\sigma_{X}(i+1),\sigma_{X}(i+1)}

\displaystyle(H_{Y})_{\sigma_{Y}(i),\sigma_{Y}(i)}

\displaystyle\geq(H_{Y})_{\sigma_{Y}(i+1),\sigma_{Y}(i+1)}.

This choice yields the upper bound

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))\right]\leq\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})^{2}\left(\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}(H_{X})_{\sigma_{X}(i),\sigma_{X}(i)}+\sum_{i=s+1}^{m}(H_{Y})_{\sigma_{Y}(i),\sigma_{Y}(i)}\right).

(19)

Let us note that because permutation matrices are unitary matrices, the bound in (
19
) is larger than or equal to the optimal bound in (
17
). Thus the optimal permutation approach might be less efficient than the optimal rotation approach, but it preserves the interpretability of the reduced components.

2.3
Selecting the reduced dimensions

We now discuss the problem of selecting the reduced dimensions. We propose to select
r
and
s
by minimizing the computational cost of exploring the reduced posterior
\pi^{*}_{X|Y}
under the constraint that
\pi^{*}_{X|Y}
is sufficiently accurate.

Let
c(r,s)\geq 0
be a function that reflects the computational cost and dimension dependence/scaling of solving the reduced Bayesian inference problem with posterior
\pi^{*}_{X|Y}
as in (
5
).
The choice of
c(r,s)
strongly depends on the inference method (e.g., different MCMC algorithms, variational inference, conditional transport maps
(
Chewi et al. 2021
;
Papamakarios and Murray 2016
;
Cui et al. 2021
)
). For instance, we may have
c(r,s)=\alpha_{X}r+\alpha_{Y}s
or
c(r,s)=\alpha_{X}r^{2}+\alpha_{Y}s^{2}
for some weights
\alpha_{X},\alpha_{Y}\geq 0
which prescribe the relative cost of truncating the parameter or data dimensions.
Given a prescribed tolerance
\epsilon
, the ideal way to select
r,s
is to solve

\displaystyle\min_{r,s}

\displaystyle\;c(r,s)\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))\right]\leq\epsilon.

(20)

Because the expected KL divergence is not accessible in practice, we rather select
r,s
by solving

\displaystyle\min_{r,s}

\displaystyle\;c(r,s)\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad B(r,s)\leq\epsilon^{\prime},

(21)

where
B(r,s)
is defined by either

\displaystyle B(r,s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(H_{X})+\sum_{i=s+1}^{m}\lambda_{i}(H_{Y}),

\displaystyle\text{or}\qquad B(r,s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}(H_{X})_{\sigma_{X}(i),\sigma_{X}(i)}+\sum_{i=s+1}^{m}(H_{Y})_{\sigma_{Y}(i),\sigma_{Y}(i)},

depending on whether one uses the optimal rotation approach (Section
2.2
) or the optimal permutation approach (Section
2.2
) to build
U,V
.
Given that

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))\right]\leq\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})B(r,s),

the solution to (
21
) with
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon/\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})
provides a feasible approximate solution to (
20
).
In the case where the log-Sobolev constant is not known, we propose to select
r,s
by solving (
21
) with
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon
. While the resulting solution may not satisfy
\mathbb{E}[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))]\leq\epsilon
, it still provides a good heuristic for selecting the reduced dimensions, as illustrated in Section
7
. Note that solving (
21
) for different tolerances yields a mapping
\epsilon^{\prime}\mapsto(r,s)
, which yields the same locus of optimal reduced dimensions
r,s
as the solution to (
20
).

Remark 2
.

When
c(r,s)=\alpha_{X}r+\alpha_{Y}s
, the optimization problem in (
21
) can be formulated as a
0-1
knapsack problem, which is known to be NP-complete
(
Kellerer et al. 2004
)
. Given that
(r,s)
is only two-dimensional, we can often enumerate all combinations of reduced dimensions to find the optimal solutions. An alternative approximate solution, which does not require enumeration, is to split the constraint and to select
(r,s)
individually based on a weighted error tolerance for the parameters and data. For example, we can identify
(r,s)
by finding the smallest integers that meet the constraints

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(H_{X})\leq\frac{\alpha_{X}}{\alpha_{X}+\alpha_{Y}}\epsilon,\quad\quad\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}(H_{Y})\leq\frac{\alpha_{Y}}{\alpha_{X}+\alpha_{Y}}\epsilon.

The setting
\alpha_{X}=\alpha_{Y}
corresponds to choosing the reduced dimensions so that the errors from both reductions are balanced.

3
Information theory and proof of Theorem
1

In this section we relate the posterior approximation error to information-theoretic quantities that measure conditional independence. We then show how to bound these quantities to derive the upper bound in Theorem
1
.

We begin by defining mutual information and conditional mutual information, which are two well known measures of the strength of dependence between random variables.

Definition 3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two random variables with joint density
\pi_{X,Y}
. The mutual information between
X
and
Y
is given by

I(X;Y)\coloneqq\int\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\log\left(\frac{\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)}{\pi_{X}(x)\pi_{Y}(y)}\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

where
\pi_{Y}(y)=\int\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x
and
\pi_{X}(x)=\int\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y
.

The mutual information is equivalently expressed as the KL divergence from the product of the marginal densities to the joint probability density function, i.e.,
I(X;Y)=D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X,Y}||\pi_{X}\pi_{Y})
. The mutual information measures the dependence of
X
and
Y
. In particular,
I(X;Y)=0
if and only if
\pi_{X,Y}=\pi_{X}\pi_{Y}
, meaning that
X
and
Y
are independent.

Definition 4
.

The conditional mutual information between random variables
X
and
Y
given a third random variable
Z
with joint density
\pi_{X,Y,Z}
is given by

I(X;Y|Z)\coloneqq\int\pi_{X,Y,Z}(x,y,z)\log\left(\frac{\pi_{X,Y|Z}(x,y|z)}{\pi_{X|Z}(x|z)\pi_{Y|Z}(y|z)}\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z,

where
\pi_{X|Z}(x|z)=\int\pi_{X,Y|Z}(x,y|z)\mathrm{d}y
and
\pi_{Y|Z}(y|z)=\int\pi_{X,Y|Z}(x,y|z)\mathrm{d}x
.

Analogously, the conditional mutual information
I(X;Y|Z)
is defined as the KL divergence from the product
\pi_{Y|Z}\pi_{X|Z}
to the conditional density
\pi_{X,Y|Z}
in expectation over
Z
, meaning
I(X;Y|Z)=\mathbb{E}[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X,Y|Z}(\cdot,\cdot|Z)||\pi_{X|Z}(\cdot|Z)\pi_{Y|Z}(\cdot|Z))]
. The conditional mutual information serves as a measure of conditional independence between random variables, i.e.,
I(X;Y|Z)=0
if and only if
X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y|Z
.

The following proposition shows that the expected KL divergence from the optimal posterior approximation (given some decomposition (
3
)–(
4
)) to the true posterior is related to a difference between (conditional) mutual informations.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\pi_{X|Y}
be the distribution of
X|Y
and
\pi^{*}_{X|Y}
be the optimal posterior approximation in (
5
) with
r
-dimensional informed parameters and
s
-dimensional informative data. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))\right]

\displaystyle=I(X;Y)-I(X_{r};Y_{s})

(22)

\displaystyle=I(X_{\perp};Y|X_{r})+I(X;Y_{\perp}|Y_{s})-I(X_{\perp};Y_{\perp}|X_{r},Y_{s}).

(23)

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.
∎

Remark 3
.

The right-hand sides of (
22
) and of (
23
) simplify if we only consider reducing either the dimension of the parameter or the data solely. For instance, if the data are not reduced, i.e.,
Y_{s}=Y
, we have

\mathbb{E}[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))]=I(X_{\perp};Y|X_{r}).

Analogously, if the parameter is not reduced, i.e.,
X_{r}=X
, we have

\mathbb{E}[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))]=I(X;Y_{\perp}|Y_{s}).

Remark 4
.

An important property of mutual information is that it is invariant to invertible marginal transformations of the variables.
For instance, by applying the linear transformations
\overline{X}=AX
and
\overline{Y}=BX
for some invertible matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, we have
I(X;Y)=I(\overline{X};\overline{Y})
but also
I(X_{\perp};Y|X_{r})=I(X_{\perp};\overline{Y}|X_{r})
and
I(X;Y_{\perp}|Y_{s})=I(\overline{X};Y_{\perp}|Y_{s})
.

While the (conditional) mutual information is tractable to compute for Gaussians and certain classes of parametric distributions, it does not admit a closed-form expression for arbitrary non-Gaussian distributions. For a density that satisfies the subspace log-Sobolev inequality in (
10
), the following proposition provides an upper bound for the conditional mutual information based on the integrated mixed partial derivatives of the log-density.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\pi_{X,Y,Z}
be the joint density of random variables
(X,Y,Z)
, satisfying the subspace logarithmic Sobolev inequality with constant
\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y,Z})
. Then, the conditional mutual information is upper bounded by

I(X;Y|Z)\leq\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y,Z})^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{X,Y,Z}(X,Y,Z)\|_{F}^{2},

(24)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm.

Proof.

The proof follows closely from the proof of Theorem 2 in
Baptista et al. 2021
. It is given in Appendix
A
.
∎

Collecting the results in Propositions
3
and
4
, we now give the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Because the conditional mutual information is positive, Proposition
3
lets us write

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))\right]\leq I(X_{\perp};Y|X_{r})+I(X;Y_{\perp}|Y_{s}).

By Proposition
4
, each term on the right-hand side above is upper bound by the expectation of mixed partial derivatives of
\pi_{X,Y}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{X_{\perp}}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{X,Y}(X,Y)\|_{F}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y_{\perp}}\log\pi_{X,Y}(X,Y)\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{X,Y}(X,Y)U_{\perp}\|_{F}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|V_{\perp}^{T}\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{X,Y}(X,Y)\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}(Y|X)U_{\perp}\|_{F}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|V_{\perp}^{T}\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y|X}(Y|X)\|_{F}^{2}\right).

Expanding the Frobenius norm using the trace, we arrive at equation (
11
).
∎

4
Gaussian error models

In this section, we consider the data-generating process
Y=G(X)+\varepsilon
, where
G\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a (nonlinear) forward model and
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}})
is a Gaussian observational error which is independent of
X
.
This situation corresponds to a likelihood function
\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\propto\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\|y-G(x)\|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1}}^{2})
and a joint density of the form of

\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|y-G(x)\|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)\pi_{X}(x),

(25)

where
\pi_{X}
is any prior density.
Without further assumptions, the subspace log-Sobolev constant
\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})
remains unknown.

4.1
Whitening

Next, we propose a change of variables for
X
and
Y
which can be interpreted as a preconditioning of the dimension reduction procedure. Notice that with a change of variables
\overline{X}=AX
and
\overline{Y}=BY
, the left-hand side of (
11
) remains unchanged (see Section
3
) while the right-hand side is modified in several ways through the subspace log-Sobolev constant and the diagnostic matrices. There is freedom in this choice.
Finding a change of variables which minimizes the right-hand side of (
11
)—i.e., which yields the
tightest
upper bound on the posterior approximation error—is a difficult task, mostly because the subspace log-Sobolev constant
\overline{C}(\pi_{X_{A},Y_{B}})
is not readily available.
Instead, we propose a heuristic which consists of whitening the parameter and the data as follows:

\overline{X}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1/2}X\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{Y}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}Y,

(26)

where
\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}\coloneqq\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X)
is the prior covariance, assuming it exists.
Then, we reduce the dimensions of
\overline{X}
and
\overline{Y}
using the corresponding diagnostic matrices
H_{\overline{X}}
and
H_{\overline{Y}}
which, using (
25
) and (
26
), are given by

\displaystyle H_{\overline{X}}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{1/2}\left(\int\nabla G(x)^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1}\nabla G(x)\pi_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x\right)\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{1/2}

(27)

\displaystyle H_{\overline{Y}}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}\left(\int\nabla G(x)\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}\nabla G(x)^{T}\pi_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x\right)\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}.

(28)

Denoting by
\overline{U}_{r}=[\overline{u}_{1},\ldots,\overline{u}_{r}]
and
\overline{V}_{s}=[\overline{v}_{1},\ldots,\overline{v}_{s}]
the matrices containing the first eigenvectors of
H_{\overline{X}}
and
H_{\overline{Y}}
, respectively, the reduced parameter and the reduced data are
X_{r}=\overline{U}_{r}^{T}\overline{X}
and
Y_{s}=\overline{V}_{s}^{T}\overline{Y}
which, using (
26
), are given by

\displaystyle X_{r}=U_{r}^{T}X

\displaystyle\qquad\text{where}\qquad U_{r}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1/2}\overline{U}_{r}

(29)

\displaystyle Y_{s}=V_{s}^{T}Y

\displaystyle\qquad\text{where}\qquad V_{s}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}\overline{V}_{s}.

(30)

With the above definition, the matrices
U_{r}
and
V_{s}
have no longer orthogonal columns in the Euclidean sense, but they satisfy
U_{r}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}U_{r}=\textrm{I}_{r}
and
V_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}V_{s}=\textrm{I}_{s}
.
The error bound (
11
) thus becomes

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot,Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot,Y))\right]\leq\overline{C}(\pi_{\overline{X},\overline{Y}})^{2}\left(\Tr(\overline{U}_{\perp}^{T}H_{\overline{X}}\overline{U}_{\perp})+\Tr(\overline{V}_{\perp}^{T}H_{\overline{Y}}\overline{V}_{\perp})\right).

(31)

Remark 5

(Generalized eigenvalue problems)
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\overline{X}}=\int\nabla G(x)^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1}\nabla G(x)\pi_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{H}_{\overline{Y}}=\int\nabla G(x)\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}\nabla G(x)^{T}\pi_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x,

so that
H_{\overline{X}}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{1/2}\mathcal{H}_{\overline{X}}\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{1/2}
and
H_{\overline{Y}}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}\mathcal{H}_{\overline{Y}}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}
.
With the change of variables
u_{i}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1/2}\overline{u}_{i}
and
v_{i}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}\overline{v}_{i}
,
the simple eigenvalue problems
H_{\overline{X}}\overline{u}_{i}=\lambda_{i}(H_{\overline{X}})\overline{u}_{i}
and
H_{\overline{Y}}\overline{v}_{i}=\lambda_{i}(H_{\overline{Y}})\overline{v}_{i}
are equivalent to the
generalized
eigenvalue problems

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\overline{X}}w_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}(\mathcal{H}_{\overline{X}},\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1})\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1}w_{i},\qquad u_{i}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1}w_{i},

(32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\overline{Y}}v_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}(\mathcal{H}_{\overline{Y}},\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}})\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}v_{i},

(33)

where
\lambda_{i}(\mathcal{H}_{\overline{X}},\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1})=\lambda_{i}(H_{\overline{X}})
and
\lambda_{i}(\mathcal{H}_{\overline{Y}},\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}})=\lambda_{i}(H_{\overline{Y}})
3
3

3

We use the notation
\lambda_{i}(A,B)
to denote the generalized eigenvalues of the matrix pencil
(A,B)
.
.
We note that
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{X}}
is the same diagnostic matrix introduced in
Cui and Zahm 2021
in the setting of Gaussian error models.
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{X}}
is also similar to the diagnostic proposed in
Cui et al. 2014
for finding the likelihood-informed subspace, with the key difference being that
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{X}}
integrates over the prior distribution instead of the posterior.

Remark 6
.

Instead of the decompositions (
3
) and (
4
), the proposed change of variables (
26
) yields a decomposition of
X
and
Y
of the form

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{1/2}\left(\overline{U}_{r}X_{r}+\overline{U}_{\perp}X_{\perp}\right)

\displaystyle\text{where \ \ }\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}X_{r}=\overline{U}_{r}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1/2}X\\
X_{\perp}=\overline{U}_{\perp}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1/2}X\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{1/2}\left(\overline{V}_{s}Y_{s}+\overline{V}_{\perp}Y_{\perp}\right)

\displaystyle\text{where \ \ }\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}Y_{s}=\overline{V}_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}Y\\
Y_{\perp}=\overline{V}_{\perp}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}Y\end{array}\right..

4.2
Linear–Gaussian setting

Now we consider the case where the forward model is linear, i.e.,
x\mapsto Gx
where
G\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
is a matrix.
In this case, the diagnostic matrices
{H}_{\overline{X}}
and
{H}_{\overline{Y}}
are written as

\displaystyle H_{\overline{X}}

\displaystyle=(\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{1/2}G^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2})(\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}G\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{1/2})

\displaystyle H_{\overline{Y}}

\displaystyle=(\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}G\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{1/2})(\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{1/2}G^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}).

The eigendecompositions of
H_{\overline{X}}
and
H_{\overline{Y}}
can be obtained by computing the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the so-called “whitened forward model”:

\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{1/2}G^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\min\{d,m\}}\sigma_{i}\overline{u}_{i}\overline{v}_{i}^{T}.

(34)

In particular, the non-zero eigenvalues of the parameter-space and data-space diagnostic matrices are now the same, i.e.,
\lambda_{i}(H_{\overline{X}})=\lambda_{i}(H_{\overline{Y}})=\sigma_{i}^{2}
for all
i\leq\min\{d,m\}
; any remaining eigenvalues, i.e., for
\min\{d,m\}<i\leq\max\{d,m\}
are zero.

The eigendecompositions of
H_{\overline{X}}
and
H_{\overline{Y}}
(or, equivalently, the generalized eigendecompositions (
32
) and (
33
) of
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{X}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{Y}}
, as in Remark
5
) have been used to reduce the parameter and data dimensions in linear–Gaussian inverse problems.
Spantini et al. 2015
solve (
32
) to approximate the posterior covariance as a low-rank update of the prior covariance. These eigenvectors also yield a projector for the parameter which matches the one derived above. Furthermore, Algorithm 1 in
Spantini et al. 2015
solves both eigenvalue problems, (
32
) and (
33
), to derive an approximation to the posterior mean (which minimizes a Bayes risk with weighted squared error loss) as a linear projection of the data
Y
.

Giraldi et al. 2018
show the equivalence between the solution to (
33
) and finding the vectors that solve
\max_{V_{s}}I(V_{s}^{T}Y,X)
, which Proposition
3
then shows is equivalent to minimizing the expected KL divergence from the true posterior, with reduced data. To minimize this expected KL divergence for linear inverse problems over the column vectors
V_{s}
,
Giraldi et al. 2018
use Riemannian optimization algorithms on a Grassmannian manifold; they extend their approach to nonlinear forward models simply by using a Laplace approximation of the posterior.
Lastly,
Jagalur-Mohan and Marzouk 2021
derive mutual information bounds for coordinate selection of data in linear–Gaussian problems. These bounds are used to develop various greedy algorithms, with guarantees for cardinality-constrained optimization.

4.3
Gap in the linear–Gaussian setting

We analyze now the gap
in (
31

5
Comparisons to PCA and CCA

Two popular methods for linear dimension reduction are principal component analysis (PCA) and canonical correlation analysis (CCA).

PCA consists in reducing the dimension of a mean-zero random vector
X
by minimizing the
L^{2}
error
\mathbb{E}[\|X-U_{r}U_{r}^{T}X\|^{2}]
over matrices
U_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
with orthogonal columns
(
Hotelling 1933
;
Jolliffe 2002
)
. The solution is
U_{r}=[u_{1}^{\textrm{PCA}},\dots,u_{r}^{\textrm{PCA}}]
where
u_{i}^{\textrm{PCA}}
are the leading eigenvectors of the covariance matrix
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X)
. That is,

\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X)u_{i}^{\textrm{PCA}}=\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X))u_{i}^{\textrm{PCA}}.

(39)

The same procedure can be applied to reduce the dimension of
Y
, which yields
V_{s}=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{s}]
where
v_{i}^{\textrm{PCA}}
are the leading eigenvectors of the covariance matrix
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(Y)
. That is,

\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(Y)v_{i}^{\textrm{PCA}}=\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(Y))v_{i}^{\textrm{PCA}}.

(40)

There are two main drawbacks of using this dimension reduction method for Bayesian inference problems.
The first is that PCA is an
unsupervised
dimension reduction method. That is, the directions identified by PCA are meant to reconstruct
X
and
Y
marginally, but it does not account for the dependence between
X
and
Y
.
Second, an accurate low-dimensional PCA approximation depends on fast decay of the eigenvalues of the covariances
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X)
and
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(Y)
. In many inference problems, however, we can have low-dimensional structure without having sharp decay in the spectra of these covariances; cf. Example
1
where
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X)=\textrm{I}_{d}
and
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(Y)=\textrm{I}_{m}+GG^{T}
.

Alternatively, CCA seeks linear combinations of
X
and
Y
that are maximally correlated
(
Hotelling 1992
;
Hardoon et al. 2004
)
. That is, CCA solves

(U_{r}^{\textrm{CCA}},V_{r}^{\textrm{CCA}})=\argmax_{\begin{subarray}{c}U_{r}^{T}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X)U_{r}=\textrm{I}_{r}\\
V_{r}^{T}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(Y)V_{r}=\textrm{I}_{r}\end{subarray}}\Tr(U_{r}^{T}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X,Y)V_{r}),

(41)

where
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X,Y)
is the cross-covariance of
X
and
Y
, and
r\leq\min\{d,m\}
.
The vectors
(U_{r}^{\textrm{CCA}})^{T}X
and
(V_{r}^{\textrm{CCA}})^{T}Y
are called the
pairs of canonical variables
. It can be shown that
U_{r},V_{r}
can be found by solving the generalized eigenvalue problems

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X,Y)\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(Y)^{-1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(Y,X)u_{i}^{\textrm{CCA}}

\displaystyle=\rho_{i}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X)u_{i}^{\textrm{CCA}}

(42)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(Y,X)\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X)^{-1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(X,Y)v_{i}^{\textrm{CCA}}

\displaystyle=\rho_{i}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(Y)v_{i}^{\textrm{CCA}},

(43)

where the eigenvectors are ordered based on a descending order for the eigenvalues
\rho_{i}^{2}\in[-1,1]
.

The next proposition shows that, for linear–Gaussian likelihood models (and potentially a non-Gaussian prior),
our dimension reduction approach using whitening (see Section
4.1
) is the same as CCA. The proof of this result is provided in Appendix
A
.

Proposition 5
.

Let
Y=GX+\varepsilon
, where
G\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
and
\varepsilon
is independent of
X
, with mean
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0
and covariance
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\varepsilon)=\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}
. Then, the solution to (
41
) is given by

U_{r}^{CCA}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1/2}\overline{U}_{r},\qquad\text{and}\qquad V_{r}^{CCA}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}\overline{V}_{r},

where
\overline{U}_{r}
and
\overline{V}_{r}
are the matrices containing the first
r
eigenvectors of the diagnostic matrices
H_{\overline{X}},H_{\overline{Y}}
defined in (
27
) and (
28
), respectively.
Furthermore, we have
\rho_{i}=\lambda_{i}/(1+\lambda_{i})
where
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i}(H_{\overline{X}})=\lambda_{i}(H_{\overline{Y}})
.

The method proposed in the present paper can thus be seen as a generalization of CCA to nonlinear models. To do so, we use gradient information from the log-likelihood,
whereas CCA uses only covariance information. We will show in Section
7
that our approach yields more accurate posterior approximations than CCA for the same reduced dimensions. Lastly, we note that CCA can only identify subspaces of the parameter and data of the
same
dimension, i.e.,
r=s
. In contrast, our proposed approach can trade off these two dimensions while meeting a desired error tolerance (see Section
2.3
).

6.1
Inference methods based on likelihood evaluations

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms are popular methods for sampling from posterior distributions. They require the ability to evaluate the posterior density (up to a normalizing constant), and hence to evaluate the likelihood function (and in general also the prior density), to accept or reject a proposed move. In the present setting, the likelihood function is the reduced likelihood given by

\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}(y_{s}|x_{r})=\int\pi_{Y_{s}|X}(y_{s}|U_{r}x_{r}+U_{\perp}x_{\perp})\pi_{X_{\perp}|X_{r}}(x_{\perp}|x_{r})\mathrm{d}x_{\perp},

(44)

where the data-marginalized likelihood
\pi_{Y_{s}|X}
above is given by

\pi_{Y_{s}|X}(y_{s}|x)=\int\pi_{Y|X}(V_{s}y_{s}+V_{\perp}y_{\perp}|x)\mathrm{d}y_{\perp}.

(45)

The next example shows that when the likelihood is Gaussian, one can analytically compute the integral in (
45
) so that
\pi_{Y_{s}|X}
is accessible in closed form.

Example 3
.

For the (whitened) Gaussian-likelihood model in Section
4.1
,
we have the rotated data model

\displaystyle Y_{s}=V_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}Y=V_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}G(x)+V_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}\varepsilon

\displaystyle Y_{\perp}=V_{\perp}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}Y=V_{\perp}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}G(x)+V_{\perp}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}\varepsilon.

Given that the observational noise components
V_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}\varepsilon
and
V_{\perp}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}\varepsilon
are independent and have identity covariance, the data-marginalized likelihood is Gaussian with the form

\pi_{Y_{s}|X}(y_{s}|x)=(2\pi)^{-s/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|y_{s}-V_{s}^{T}\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1/2}G(x)\|_{2}^{2}\right).

While the integral in (
45
) can be computed analytically, there is in general no closed form expression for the integral in (
44
). Thus, the reduced likelihood
\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}
needs to be estimated numerically.
We consider here the Monte-Carlo estimator

\widehat{\pi}_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}(y_{s}|x_{r})=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\pi_{Y_{s}|X}(y_{s}|U_{r}x_{r}+U_{\perp}\text{X}_{\perp}^{i}),\quad\text{X}_{\perp}^{i}\sim\pi_{X_{\perp}|X_{r}}(\cdot|x_{r}).

(46)

We refer to
Cui and Zahm 2021
for an intensive discussion on different sampling strategies, and on the impact of the sample size
\ell
versus the truncated dimension
r
. As shown in
Zahm et al. 2022
;
Cui and Tong 2021
, the variance of the estimator (
46
) is low when the error bound
\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(H_{X})
is small. In practice, it is sufficient to use few samples (e.g.,
\ell=1
) or even deterministic approximations (e.g., by setting
\text{X}_{\perp}^{i}
to the conditional prior mean). More interestingly, taking the perspective of pseudo-marginal MCMC
(
Andrieu and Roberts 2009
)
, it is shown in
Cui and Zahm 2021
that redrawing fresh samples
\text{X}_{\perp}^{i}
in (
46
) at each MCMC iteration permits sampling from the
exact
reduced posterior.

6.2
Inference methods based on joint samples

Transportation of measure underpins another broad class of algorithms for generating conditional samples
(
Marzouk et al. 2016
;
Kovachki et al. 2020
)
. These methods require having access to samples from the joint distribution
\pi_{X,Y}
in order to construct an invertible map (e.g., using invertible neural networks as in
Radev et al. 2020
or polynomial expansions as in
Baptista et al. 2022
) that transforms samples from the joint distribution to samples from the standard normal distribution. This map is then used to draw samples from the conditional distribution
\pi_{X|Y=y}
for any value
y
, thereby amortizing the cost of inference for multiple realizations of the data.

Recalling the overall scheme presented at the start of Section
6
, we need to sample from the reduced posterior
\pi_{X_{r}|Y_{s}=y_{s}}
for some
y_{s}
. We begin by considering the reduced joint distribution
\pi_{X_{r},Y_{s}}
. Samples from
\pi_{X_{r},Y_{s}}
are obtained by projecting samples
(X^{i},Y^{i})\sim\pi_{X,Y}
as follows:
(X_{r}^{i},Y_{s}^{i})=(U_{r}^{T}X^{i},V_{s}^{T}Y^{i})
.
Using these samples, we then build a (block)-triangular map
S\colon\mathbb{R}^{r+s}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r+s}
such that

S(Y_{s},X_{r})=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}S^{\mathcal{Y}}(Y_{s})\\
S^{\mathcal{X}}(Y_{s},X_{r})\end{array}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\textrm{I}_{r+s}),

where both
y_{s}\mapsto S^{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{s})
and
x_{r}\mapsto S^{\mathcal{X}}(y_{s},x_{r})
are invertible functions. Once
S
is built, sampling from
\pi_{X_{r}|Y_{s}=y_{s}}
requires solving the equation
S^{\mathcal{X}}(y_{s},\widetilde{X}_{r})=Z_{r}
for
\widetilde{X}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, where
Z_{r}
is a sample from
\mathcal{N}(0,\textrm{I}_{r})
. By construction, we have
\widetilde{X}_{r}\sim\pi_{X_{r}|Y_{s}=y_{s}}
; see
Marzouk et al. 2016
for a proof. Furthermore, the map
S^{\mathcal{X}}
enables evaluations of the conditional density via the change of variables formula,
\pi_{X_{r}|Y_{s}}(x_{r}|y_{s})=\eta\circ S^{\mathcal{X}}(y_{s},x_{r})|\nabla_{X_{r}}S^{\mathcal{X}}(y_{s},x_{r})|
where
\eta
denotes the density of the standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{r})
. Let us remark that this procedure does not utilize the first map component
S^{\mathcal{Y}}
, and so it is unnecessary to construct it in practice.

In this setting, reducing the dimensions of both the parameter and the data alleviates the computational burden of the map construction:
S^{\mathcal{X}}
becomes a function of
s+r
variables, rather than of
m+d
variables. It is also worth noting that this reduced-dimensional approach to amortized inference is feasible only when the projection
V_{s}
is independent of the data realization
y
, which is the case in our approach.

7.1
Linear elasticity inverse problem

Our first numerical example is to infer the inhomogeneous Young’s modulus of a (wrench-shaped) physical body
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
given some measurements of the displacement on its boundary
(
Lam et al. 2020
;
Smetana and Zahm 2020
)
. This is a challenging inverse problem as both the Young’s modulus and observed displacements are spatially distributed quantities and hence high-dimensional vectors after discretization; moreover, they are indirectly related via a partial differential equation that induces a nonlinear forward model.

Let
u\colon\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
represent the displacement field given an external force
f
applied on a subset of
\partial\mathcal{D}
. The displacement field
u
satisfies the coupled elliptic PDE
\text{div}(K:\epsilon(u))=0
everywhere on
\mathcal{D}
, where
\epsilon(u)=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T})
is the strain field and
K
is the Hooke tensor, such that

K:\epsilon(u)\coloneqq\frac{E}{1+\nu}\epsilon(u)+\frac{\nu E}{1-\nu^{2}}\Tr(\epsilon(u))\textrm{I}_{2}.

(47)

Here,
\nu=0.3
is Poisson’s ratio and
E\colon\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the Young’s modulus. The displacement field is also subject to a Dirichlet boundary condition, i.e.,
u=0
on the right hand side of the wrench; see the dashed lines in Figure
2(a)
.
We model the Young’s modulus field with a log-normal prior, i.e.,
\log E\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C)
where
C(s,s^{\prime})=\varsigma^{2}\exp(-\|s-s^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}/\ell^{2})
is a squared exponential covariance kernel on
\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}
with correlation length
\ell=1
and marginal variance
\varsigma^{2}=1
.

To solve the PDE numerically, we apply the finite element method
(
Zienkiewicz et al. 2000
)
. We first discretize the domain using a mesh with
925
elements and we approximate the stochastic field
\log(E)
with a piecewise constant field whose values are gathered in a random vector
X
of dimension
d=925
. We denote by
u^{h}(X)
the Galerkin projection of
u(X)
onto the space of piecewise affine functions.
We then extract the vertical displacements of
u^{h}(X)
at the
m=48
nodes located along the line where the force is applied; see Figure
2(b)
.
Denoting the corresponding (linear) extraction operator by
L
, the forward model
G\colon\mathbb{R}^{925}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{48}
is written as
G(X)=Lu^{h}(X)
.
The data
Y=G(X)+\varepsilon
are perturbed with a zero-mean Gaussian noise
\varepsilon
which is independent of

. The covariance of
\varepsilon
is defined as
\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}=LR^{-1}L^{T}
, where
R
is the Riesz map associated with the

.
This way, the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1}}
corresponds to the standard trace norm on
H^{1/2}(\partial\mathcal{D})
; see
(
Zahm 2015
, Chapter 5)
.
This example falls into the framework of Gaussian error models, discussed in Section
4
, with a Gaussian prior distribution.

We consider next an inference problem with a non-Gaussian likelihood. The goal is to infer the location of a feature in a high-dimensional image as well as an image hyperparameter; see

8
Discussion

This work proposes a gradient-based method for concurrently reducing the dimensionality of the data and parameters of Bayesian models, in a general non-Gaussian setting. We reduce dimensionality by identifying an
informed
subspace of the parameter and an
informative
subspace of the data; this identification is performed prior to realizing the observations.
These subspaces yield posterior approximations that depart from the prior only along a low-dimensional subspace, and via conditioning only on a low-dimensional subspace of the data. We obtain these subspaces by constructing and minimizing a tractable quadratic upper bound for the expected KL divergence from the posterior approximation to the true posterior distribution. This bound is derived from logarithmic Sobolev inequalities, and can be used to evaluate and compare the quality of any data- and parameter-space projectors. We also show how the same ideas can be used for coordinate selection in both the data and parameters, by minimizing the upper bound under more restricted conditions.

In the specific case of linear–Gaussian models, with the reduced data and parameter subspaces fixed to the same dimension, our construction recovers canonical correlation analysis (CCA). In general, however, our approach yields more accurate posterior approximations than those obtained with both CCA and PCA, as well as interpretable projections of the parameters and data, for a range of non-Gaussian inference problems. Our formulation also generalizes and lends theoretical support to heuristics recently considered in specialized settings, for instance in sequential data assimilation
(
Provost et al. 2022
)
.
We outline some directions for future work below.

Gradient-free methods
. The optimal projections of the variables are defined via the eigendirections of two diagnostic matrices
H_{X}
and
H_{Y}
, containing mixed partial derivatives of the log-likelihood function. For certain applications, these derivatives might be unavailable or computationally expensive to evaluate. It would be interesting to develop estimators for
H_{X}
and
H_{Y}
based only on differences between forward model evaluations. Furthermore, understanding the sample complexity of these estimators—specifically, the estimators of the leading eigenspaces of
H_{X}
and
H_{Y}
—will be useful to determine the number of samples required to reliably achieve posterior approximations of a given error. (See
Zahm et al. 2022
;
Lam et al. 2020
for analysis of subspace approximation errors in settings where gradients are available.)

Nonlinear dimension reduction
. For strongly nonlinear forward models, linear dimension reduction may require many modes to achieve a small posterior approximation error. In these cases, one might instead seek few
nonlinear
functions of the parameters that are informed by nonlinear features of the data. See
Bigoni et al. 2022
for an approach to identifying these parameter features in the context of surrogate modeling. It will be interesting to extend our current guarantees on posterior approximation error to nonlinear features, and to compare the resulting features to those identified by nonlinear supervised dimension reduction methods such as
Andrew et al. 2013
;
Michaeli et al. 2016
.

Proof of Proposition
3
.

Let
\pi_{X,Y}
be a joint density of
(X,Y)
with posterior density

\pi_{X|Y}(x|y)=\frac{\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\pi_{X}(x)}{\pi_{Y}(y)}.

Let the density for the optimal posterior approximation be

\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(x|y)=\frac{\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}(y_{s}|x_{r})\pi_{X}(x)}{\pi^{*}_{Y}(y_{s})},

where
\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}(y_{s}|x_{r})=\int\pi_{Y|X}(y_{s},y_{\perp}|x_{r},x_{\perp})\pi_{X_{\perp}|X_{r}}(x_{\perp}|x_{r})\mathrm{d}x_{\perp}\mathrm{d}y_{\perp}
is the approximate likelihood function.
For this likelihood, the approximate data marginal satisfies

\displaystyle\pi^{*}_{Y}(y_{s})

\displaystyle=\int\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}(y_{s}|x_{r})\pi_{X_{r}}(x_{r})\textrm{d}x_{r}

\displaystyle=\int\int\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\pi_{X}(x)\textrm{d}y_{\perp}\textrm{d}x=\int\pi_{Y_{s}|X}(y_{s}|x)\pi_{X}(x)\textrm{d}x=\pi_{Y_{s}}(y_{s}).

The KL divergence from the optimal posterior approximation to the true posterior in expectation over the data is then given by

\displaystyle\quad\,\,\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y)||\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(\cdot|Y))\right]

\displaystyle=\int\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\log\frac{\pi_{X|Y}(x|y)}{\pi^{*}_{X|Y}(x|y)}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\log\frac{\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\pi_{X}(x)/\pi_{Y}(y)}{\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}(y_{s}|x_{r})\pi_{X}(x)/\pi_{Y_{s}}(y_{s})}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\log\frac{\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)}{\pi_{Y}(y)}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y-\int\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\log\frac{\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}(y_{s}|x_{r})}{\pi_{Y_{s}}(y_{s})}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\log\frac{\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)}{\pi_{Y}(y)}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y}_{I(X;Y)}-\underbrace{\int\pi_{X_{r},Y_{s}}(x_{r},y_{s})\log\frac{\pi_{Y_{s}|X_{r}}(y_{s}|x_{r})}{\pi_{Y_{s}}(y_{s})}\mathrm{d}x_{r}\mathrm{d}y_{s}}_{I(X_{r};Y_{s})}.

(56)

Lastly, from the chain rule for mutual information we have

\displaystyle I(X;Y)-I(X_{r},Y_{s})=I(X_{\perp};Y|X_{r})+I(X,Y_{\perp}|Y_{s})-I(X_{\perp};Y_{\perp}|X_{r},Y_{s}).

∎

Proof of Proposition
4
.

The proof is done in two steps. First we assume that
Z=\emptyset
so that
I(X;Y|Z)=I(X;Y)
becomes the mutual information.
We can write

\displaystyle I(X;Y)

\displaystyle=\int h(x,y)\log\left(h(x,y)\right)\,\pi_{X}(x)\pi_{Y}(y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

where
h(x,y)=\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)/(\pi_{X}(x)\pi_{Y}(y))
.
Because
\pi_{X,Y}
satisfies the logarithmic Sobolev inequality with constant bounded by
\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})
, we have that the product density
\pi_{X}\otimes\pi_{Y}
also satisfies the logarithmic Sobolev inequality with constant bounded by
\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})
; see
Guionnet and Zegarlinksi 2003
. Then, since
\int h(x,y)\pi_{Y}(y)\pi_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x=\int\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x=1
, the logarithmic Sobolev inequality gives

\displaystyle I(X;Y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})}{2}\int\|\nabla\log h(x,y)\|^{2}\,h(x,y)\,\pi_{X}(x)\pi_{Y}(y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})}{2}\int\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{X}\log h(x,y)\\
\nabla_{Y}\log h(x,y)\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2}\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})}{2}\int\left(\int\left\|F_{1}(x,y)-\nabla_{X}\log\pi_{X}(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\mathrm{d}y\right)\pi_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\,+\frac{\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})}{2}\int\left(\int\left\|F_{2}(x,y)-\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y}(y)\right\|_{2}^{2}\pi_{X|Y}(x|y)\mathrm{d}x\right)\pi_{Y}(y)\mathrm{d}y,

(57)

where
F_{1}(x,y)=\nabla_{X}\log\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)
and
F_{2}(x,y)=\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)
.
Next we are going to apply the Poincaré inequality to bound the two terms in the last expression.
Recall that the logarithmic Sobolev inequality (
9
) implies the Poincaré inequality (see
Bakry et al. 2014
) so we have

\int\|f-\int f\mathrm{d}\rho\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\leq\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})\int\|\nabla f\|_{F}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho,

(58)

for any smooth vector-valued function
f
, where
\rho
can be either
\pi_{X|Y}(\cdot|y)
or
\pi_{Y|X}(\cdot|x)
. Here,
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm.
Notice for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
we can write

\displaystyle\int F_{1}(x,y)\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{X}\log\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\frac{\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)}{\pi_{X}(x)}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\nabla_{X}\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y}{\pi_{X}(x)}=\frac{\nabla_{X}\pi_{X}(x)}{\pi_{X}(x)}=\nabla_{X}\log\pi_{X}(x),

which means that
\nabla_{X}\log\pi_{X}(x)
is the conditional expectation of
F_{1}(X,Y)
conditioned on
X=x
.
In the same way,
\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{Y}(y)
is the conditional expectation of
F_{2}(X,Y)
conditioned on
Y=y
.
This observations permits us to apply the Poincaré inequality (
58
) to the two terms in (
57
). This gives

\displaystyle I(X;Y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})}{2}\int\left(\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})\int\left\|\nabla_{Y}F_{1}(x,y)\right\|_{F}^{2}\pi_{Y|X}(y|x)\mathrm{d}y\right)\pi_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\,+\frac{\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})}{2}\int\left(\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})\int\left\|\nabla_{X}F_{2}(x,y)\right\|_{F}^{2}\pi_{X|Y}(x|y)\mathrm{d}x\right)\pi_{Y}(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})^{2}}{2}\int\left(\|\nabla_{Y}F_{1}(x,y)\|_{F}^{2}+\|\nabla_{X}F_{2}(x,y)\|_{F}^{2}\right)\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x.

Because
(\nabla_{Y}F_{1}(x,y))^{T}=\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)=\nabla_{X}F_{2}(x,y)
, we obtain

\displaystyle I(X;Y)\leq\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y})^{2}\int\|\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\|_{F}^{2}\pi_{X,Y}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x,

(59)

which is (
24
) when
Z=\emptyset
.

Now we assume that
Z\neq\emptyset
. Applying the previous result to the conditional density
\pi_{X,Y|Z}(x,y|z)
yields

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle\pi_{X,Y|Z}(x,y|z)\log\left(\frac{\pi_{X,Y|Z}(x,y|z)}{\pi_{X|Z}(x|z)\pi_{Y|Z}(y|z)}\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:tmp2689}}{\leq}\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y|Z=z})^{2}\int\|\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{X,Y|Z}(x,y|z)\|_{F}^{2}\pi_{X,Y|Z}(x,y|z)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y,Z})^{2}\int\|\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\log\pi_{X,Y,Z}(x,y,z)\|_{F}^{2}\pi_{X,Y|Z}(x,y|z)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x,

where we used the fact that
\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y|Z=z})\leq\overline{C}(\pi_{X,Y,Z})
for all
z
; recall Equation (
10
).
Multiplying with the marginal
\pi_{Z}(z)
and integrating over
z
yields (
24
) and concludes the proof.
∎
