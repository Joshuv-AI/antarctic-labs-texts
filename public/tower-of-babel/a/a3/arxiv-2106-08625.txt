Abstract

An upper estimate for the number of negative eigenvalues below the essential spectrum for the magnetic Schrödinger operator with Aharonov-Bohm magnetic field in a strip is obtained.

Its further shown that the estimate does not hold in absence of Aharonov-Bohm magnetic field.

Chapter 1
Introduction

1.1
Background

The study of plasma, nuclear, solid state, semiconductor physics, and astrophysics
[
13
,
26
,
32
,
52
,
66
]
requires the knowledge about the number of bound-states solutions of the Schrödinger equation.
The differential operator appearing in the Schrödinger equation is called Schrödinger operator and has got deep roots in non-relativistic quantum mechanics
[
16
]
. The Schrödinger operator in a specific quantum system offers a full characterization of its physical system. Eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of an operator usually have a direct physical interpretation of the physical system it models, for example, eigenvalue estimates for magnetic Schrödinger operators play an important role in the study of asymptotic properties of the discrete spectrum
[
42
]
, scattering theory
[
62
]
, and stability of matter in magnetic fields
[
46
]
. This therefore makes the study of spectral properties of Schrödinger operators an interesting object in Mathematical Physics.

A number of mathematicians inspired by the need to come up with generalizations of the Schrödinger operator in the 1950s, posed a number of questions concerning the Schrödinger operator
H_{V}=-\Delta+V
,
V\geqslant 0
defined on a Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Such questions include defining the Schrödinger operator as a self adjoint operator in a proper Hilbert space and determining its spectral properties on quantitative and qualitative levels.

Complete set of answers to these questions were found with regularity conditions on the potential and more relaxed with time
[
51
]
. Some result such as bounds on the number of negative eigenvalues of the operator were obtained by Rozenblum
[
56
]
, Lieb
[
45
]
and Cwikel
[
19
]
. Today, this result is known as the Cwikel-Lieb-Rozenblum inequality (CLR)
[
7
]
.

Later, the researchers directed their interest also to the operators involving both electric field potential and the magnetic fields such as the magnetic Schrödinger operators and the Pauli operators. Earlier studies found in the work of Avron et al.
[
5
]
, Reed
[
51
]
, Simon
[
60
]
, Leinfelder and Simader
[
43
]
& Melgaard and Rozenblum
[
48
]
lead to obtaining the analogous of the CLR inequality for the magnetic inequality. Spectral theory problems of the magnetic Schrödinger operator to date have not been fully characterized such as regularity of the potential discussed in view of Barseghyan and Truc
[
10
]
.
Problems such as obtaining the bounds for the number of negative eigenvalues for the Schrödinger operator in two dimension to this moment is still an open problem (see
[
27
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
59
]
and the references therein).

The spectral theory of Schrödinger operators has during the past been restricted only to the qualitative questions of spectral theory, self adjoint realization, semi-boundedness, discreteness of the whole of the negative spectrum while the quantitative results on the spectrum lag behind to this moment.

In this study, the question of obtaining an estimate for the number of negative eigenvalues for the magnetic Schrödinger operator in a strip subject to Neumann boundary conditions is considered. A related problem was solved in
[
6
]
in the case of the Aharonov-Bohm magnetic field represented by a Dirac delta function and in
[
39
]
for a reasonably large class of magnetic fields. However, in the two cases considered, they do not consider the case of a strip where the boundary conditions affect the bottom of the essential spectrum and estimates obtained in
[
6
]
fail in regular fields.

One of the tools used to obtain the bound for the number of eigenvalues is the diamagnetic inequality. In
[
53
]
, its pointed out that using only the diamagnetic inequality alone to derive eigenvalue estimates for the magnetic Schrödinger operator from the non-magnetic ones would, in particular, lead automatically to estimates not depending on the magnetic field. Thus obtaining eigenvalue estimates for the magnetic Schrödinger operator becomes a separate problem, and many methods used in the non-magnetic situation do not work in the magnetic case. In this study the Hardy inequality, Bargmann type inequality and the Sturm-Liouville operator theory are particularly used to obtain the eigenvalue estimates for the magnetic Schrödinger operator in a strip with Aharonov-Bohm type magnetic field.

Structure of the thesis

In Chapter 4, we give some recommendations and suggestions for further study.

1.3
Magnetic Schrödinger operators

1.3.1
Magnetic Laplacian

The classical Laplace operator
H_{0}=-\Delta
defined by

H_{0}=-\Delta=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}\text{ on }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

is a positive self-adjoint operator whose spectrum is real and its essential spectrum is absolutely continuous, that is,
\sigma(-\Delta)=[0,\infty)
. The discrete spectrum is empty
[
42
]
, describes the energy of a particle in an electric field.

To describe the energy of a particle in an external magnetic field

, the Laplacian
\Delta=\nabla^{2}
is replaced by
(\frac{1}{i}\nabla+A)^{2}
, where the vector potential

satisfies
curlA=B

[
29
]
. We denote the magnetic Laplacian by

. Then

H_{B}=\bigg(\frac{1}{i}\nabla+A\bigg)^{2}\text{\;on\;}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(1.3.1)

It is shown in
[
14
]
that the operator

is non-negative and symmetric in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and therefore it can be extended to self adjoint non-negative operator. Balinsky et al
[
6
]
proved that the essential spectrum of the operator

is
[0,\infty)
(see
[
20
, Theorem 6.1]
) and its discrete spectrum is empty if the potential
A
is of the Aharonov-Bohm type (see
[
8
, Section 5.5]
or
[
20
, Section 6]
, for more details).

1.4
Statement of the problem

Given a function
V:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to{[0,\infty)}
, consider the Schrödinger operator

H_{V}:=-\Delta-V\text{\;on\;}L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(1.4.1)

under certain assumptions, about

, such as if

is uniformly locally square integrable for

or is uniformly locally in

with
p>\frac{n}{2}
for

, the operator (
1.4.1
) is well-defined and self-adjoint on

. Moreover, if

as

, its essential spectrum is the interval
[0,\infty)
and
the discrete spectrum consists of eigenvalues of finite multiplicity
[
42
]
.
In quantum mechanics, we call these negative eigenvalues the bound states and they describe energy levels of a quantum system, their respective eigenfunctions correspond to energy states
[
61
]
. According to the CLR inequality, the number of negative eigenvalues of
H_{V}
for
n\geqslant 3
is estimated by

Neg(V,\mathbb{R}^{n})\leqslant C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}V(x)^{n/2}dx,

(1.4.2)

where
Neg(V,\mathbb{R}^{n})
denotes the number of negative eigenvalues and

a constant which depends on the dimension. It is known that the CLR inequality does not hold for
n=2
(see
[
59
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
and the reference there in). Some of the reasons are that the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is not continuously embedded in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

[
35
,
36
]
, the Hardy inequality

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}dx\leqslant\bigg(\frac{n-2}{2}\bigg)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u(x)|^{2}dx,u\in{C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\})},

(1.4.3)

does not hold for dimension two
[
41
]
, and the other is that the operator (
1.4.1
) in dimension two has weakly coupled eigenvalues, that is, if
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V\geqslant 0
, then
Neg(-\Delta-\lambda V,0)\geqslant 1
for any
\lambda>0

[
39
]
.

It is shown in
[
6
,
39
,
41
]
that when an additional magnetic field is introduced, the above situation improves due to the diamagnetic effects. Balinsky et al
[
6
]
obtained an analogy of the CLR-inequality in two dimension in case the magnetic potential is the Aharonov-Bohm type, Kovarik
[
39
]
, obtained an estimate for the number of negative eigenvalues of the magnetic Schrödinger operator for general magnetic fields. Laptev
[
40
]
obtained an estimate for the number of negative eigenvalues for a magnetic Schrödinger operator in dimension two with Ahoronov-Bohm magnetic field with an optimal constant. Barseghyan et al in
[
9
]
, considered the magnetic Schrödinger operators on the two dimensional unit disk with a radially symmetric magnetic field which explodes to infinity at the boundary, they proved a bound for the eigenvalue moments and a bound for the number of negative eigenvalues for such operators.

In this study, the estimate due to Balinsky et al
[
6
]
is extended to the case of a straight strip where the boundary operators are of the Neumann type. In particular, we derive an upper estimate for the number of discrete negative eigenvalues of the operator

H_{B,V}:=\bigg(\frac{1}{i}\nabla+A\bigg)^{2}-V\text{\;on\;}L^{2}(S),

(1.4.4)

with magnetic field
B=\nabla\times A
, where
A
is the magnetic field potential with non-integer flux and
S:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}^{2}}:x_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}},0<x_{2}<d\},d>0
is an infinite straight two-dimensional strip of constant width

. The boundary conditions on the lower and upper axes of

are maintained to be of Neumann type. In particular, the question of whether it is possible to establish the analogue of the CLR inequality (
1.4.2
) for the operator (
1.4.4
), is considered.

1.5
Aim of the study

The aim was to estimate the number of negative eigenvalues of the magnetic Schrödinger operator (
1.4.4
), in a strip in terms of the strength or regularity properties of the electric potential.

1.5.1
Objectives

The study was intended to achieve the following specific objectives

(i).

To obtain an analogue of the CLR inequality when the magnetic field is the Aharonov-Bohm magnetic field.

(ii).

To establish the relationship between the estimate and the properties of the magnetic field.

1.6
Justification

Recent developments in technology such as coherent electron beams
[
58
]
, highly sensitive electron detectors and photo-lithography, ability to observe microscopic objects previously thought unobservable such as the dynamics of quantized vortices in superconductors by Lorentz microscopy all have been attributed to the Aharonov-Bohm effect. These measurement and observation techniques are expected to play a more important role in future research and development in nano-science technology, medicine (magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) machine), transportation (Maglev train), quantum computers, squids, wireless communication, generators and accelerators. The study is therefore motivated by physical models such as those involved in the theory of the surface superconductivity
[
24
]

Recent study in
[
3
]
show that electric field control of magnetism in metals have attracted wide spread attention, Yamada et al.
[
67
]
pointed out that microscopic mechanism is still controversial.

The need to settle such controversies and have a better understanding of the above phenomena greatly is motivating to investigate the magnetic Schrödinger equation which gives a better description of such phenomena via the magnetic Schrödinger operator. In particular estimates for number of negative eigenvalues for the magnetic Schrödinger operator give a detailed description of the nucleation of superconductivity for super conductors of type II and an accurate estimate for the critical field, magnetic anisotrophy energy of systems and stability of matter in magnetic fields
[
7
,
46
,
62
]

Review of basic facts in spectral theory

2.2.1
Sobolev spaces

Vector space of functions that have weak derivatives are called the Sobolev spaces
[
2
,
44
]
. The motivation behind these spaces is that the solutions of partial differential equations if they exit belong to Sobolev spaces.

Let
\Omega\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
be an open set,
\partial\Omega
denotes its boundary,
u\in{C^{\prime}(\Omega)}
and
\varphi\in{C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)}
.

Integration by parts gives

\int_{\Omega}u\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{j}}dx=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\varphi dx

Let
u\in{C^{k}(\Omega)}
,
k=1,2,\dots,
and let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in{\mathbb{N}^{n}}
be a multi-index such that the order of multi-index
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}
is at most
k
. We denote

D^{\alpha}u=\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}u}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots\partial x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}}.

The order of a multi-index tells the total number of differentiations. Successive integration by parts yields

\int_{\Omega}uD^{\alpha}\varphi dx=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\int_{\Omega}D^{\alpha}u\varphi dx

Definition 2.2.1
.

Let
u\in{L_{loc}^{1}(\Omega)}
. For a given multi-index
\alpha
, a function
v\in{L_{loc}^{1}(\Omega)}
is called the
\alpha^{th}
weak derivative of
u
if

\int_{\Omega}\varphi vdx=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\int_{\Omega}uD^{\alpha}\varphi dx\text{ \;for all\; }\varphi\in{C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

A locally integrable function is said to be
k
-times weakly differentiable if for all
|\alpha|\leqslant k
, the weak derivative
D^{\alpha}u
exists.

Definition 2.2.2
.

Let
m
be a positive integer and let
1\leqslant p\leqslant\infty
. The Sobolev space
W^{m,p}(\Omega)
is defined by

W^{m,p}(\Omega)=\{u\in{L^{p}(\Omega)}|D^{\alpha}u\in{L^{p}(\Omega)},\text{\;for all \;}|\alpha|\leqslant m\}.

The space
W^{m,p}(\Omega)
is a vector space contained in
L^{p}(\Omega)
and is endowed with the norm
\|.\|_{m,p,\Omega}
defined as follows

\|u\|_{m,p,\Omega}=\bigg(\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant m}\|D^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}(\Omega)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}\qquad\text{if}\qquad 1\leqslant p<\infty

and

\|u\|_{m,\infty,\Omega}=\max_{|\alpha|\leqslant m}\|D^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

For
m=p=2
,
W^{2,2}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space and
H^{2}(\Omega)
is its standard notation.

2.2.2
The spectrum of a self-adjoint operator

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space,
\mathcal{D}\subset{\mathcal{H}}
a linear subset and
T:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{H}
a linear map.

Let
T
be densely defined operator, that is, to say
\overline{\mathcal{D}(T)}=\mathcal{H}
. Then the adjoint operator
T^{*}
can be constructed as follows

D(T^{*}):=\{v\in{\mathcal{H}}\text{ }|\text{ }\exists h\in{\mathcal{H}}:\langle Tu,v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle u,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\forall u\in{\mathcal{D}(T)}\}.

(2.2.1)

The vector
h
is uniquely determined by
v
, and we set
h=T^{*}v
. Thus

\langle Tu,v\rangle=\langle u,T^{*}v\rangle\quad\forall u\in{\mathcal{D}(T)},\forall v\in{D(T^{*})}.

Definition 2.2.3
.

An operator
T
which fulfills
T^{*}=T
is said to be
self-adjoint
.

An operator
T
, such that
\overline{\mathcal{D}(T)}=\mathcal{H}
and

\langle Tu,v\rangle=\langle u,Tv\rangle,\forall u,v\in{\mathcal{D}(T)}.

is called
symmetric
.

T:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is said to be bounded if there exists a constant
c>0
such that
\|Tu\|\leqslant c\|u\|
for all
u\in{\mathcal{D}(T)}
.

Denote by
I
the identity operator in the complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
the space of bounded linear operators in
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 2.2.4

(Resolvent set:
\rho(T)
)
.

Let
T
be a closed linear operator. The complex number
\lambda
is called a
regular point
if the operator
T-\lambda I
is invertible with an inverse
(T-\lambda I)^{-1}
defined every where on
\mathcal{H}
that belongs to
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. The set of all regular points constitute the resolvent set of
T
denoted by
\rho(T)
.

Definition 2.2.5

(Resolvent)
.

The resolvent of an operator
T
is the operator valued function
\lambda\mapsto(T-\lambda I)^{-1}
mapping the set
\rho(T)
into
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

Definition 2.2.6

(Spectrum:
\sigma(T)
)
.

Let
T
be a closed linear operator. The complement of its resolvent set
\rho(T)
is the spectrum of
T
. The spectrum is denoted by
\sigma(T)
, and
\sigma(T)=\mathbb{C}\backslash\rho(T)
.

If
T
is bounded, the resolvent set
\rho(T)
is open while the spectrum
\sigma(T)
is closed. If
T^{*}=T
, then the spectrum of
T
is nonempty and lies on the real axis.

Definition 2.2.7

(Eigenvalue)
.

If there exists a non-zero vector
x\in{\mathcal{D}(T)}
and a complex number
\lambda
for which
Tx=\lambda x
, then
\lambda
is called an eigenvalue of
T
and

is an associated eigenvector.

Definition 2.2.8

(Point spectrum and continuous spectrum)
.

The set of all eigenvalues of a self-adjoint operator
T
is called the
point spectrum
denoted by
\sigma_{p}(T)
while the continuous spectrum denoted by
\sigma_{c}(T)
is defined by

\sigma_{c}(T)=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}\text{ }:\text{ }T-\lambda I\text{ is injective and has dense range, but is not surjective}\}

.

The union of point spectrum
\sigma_{p}(T)
and the continuous spectrum
\sigma_{c}(T)
of a self adjoint operator
T
represents its spectrum
\sigma(T)
and it is said to have a purely continuous spectrum if
\sigma_{p}(T)=\emptyset
.

Definition 2.2.9
.

The union of the continuous spectrum
\sigma_{c}(T)
and the set of eigenvalues of infinite multiplicity of a self adjoint operator
T
is called the essential spectrum.

We denote by
\sigma_{ess}(T)
, the essential spectrum of the self adjoint operator
T
. If
\sigma_{ess}(T)=\emptyset
, then
T
has a discrete spectrum.

2.3
The quadratic form of a self-adjoint operator

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote a complete separable Hilbert space. Let
\langle f,g\rangle
and
\|f\|
,
f,g\in{\mathcal{H}}
, denote respectively, the operations of inner product and norm in
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 2.3.1
.

Let a linear subspace
\mathcal{D}(q)\subset\mathcal{H}
be fixed. A mapping
q:\mathcal{D}(q)\times\mathcal{D}(q)\to\mathbb{C}
which satisfies

(i)

,

(ii)

for all

is called a sesquilinear (quadratic) form. If
\mathcal{D}(q)
is dense in

, then
q
is densely defined.

The form

is said to be symmetric if
q[u,v]=q\overline{[v,u]}
and lower semi-bounded if
q[u]\geqslant c\|u\|^{2}
, where
c
is a constant and
q[u]:=q[u,u]
. A symmetric sesquilinear form is called a Hermitian form.

Definition 2.3.2
.

Let
T:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be a linear operator. A core of
T
is a subset
B
of
\mathcal{D}(T)
such that the closure of the restriction of
T
to
B
is
\overline{T}
.

Theorem 2.3.1

(
[
65
, Theorem 5.37]
)
.

Let
q
be a densely defined closed, symmetric and linear semi-bounded form in
\mathcal{H}
. Then there exists a unique self-adjoint operator

on
\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{D}(q)
and
q[u,v]=\langle Tu,v\rangle
for every
u\in{\mathcal{D}(T)}
and
v\in{\mathcal{D}(q)}
. If
u\in{\mathcal{D}(q)}
and
w\in{\mathcal{H}}
such that
q[u,v]=\langle w,v\rangle
holds for every
v
in the core of
q
, then
u\in{\mathcal{D}(T)}
and
Tu=w
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
q
be a Hermitian form with a domain
\mathcal{D}(q)\subset\mathcal{H}
. Set

Neg(q):=\sup\{\dim\ell\text{ }|\text{ }q{[u]}<0,\forall u\in{\ell\backslash\{0\}}\},

(2.3.1)

where
\ell
denotes a linear subspace of
\mathcal{D}(q)
.
The number
Neg(q)
is called the Morse index of
q
in
\mathcal{D}(q)
. If
q
is the quadratic form of a self adjacent operator
T
with no essential spectrum in
(-\infty,0)
, then
Neg(q)
is called the number of negative eigenvalues of
T
repeated according to their multiplicity (
[
18
, Section 2.1]
).

2.4

2.4.1
Hardy inequality

Theorem 2.4.1

(
[
28
, Theorem 327]
)
.

If
p>1
,
f(x)\geqslant 0
, and
F(x)=\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt
, then

\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{F}{x}\bigg)^{p}dx\leqslant\bigg(\frac{p}{p-1}\bigg)^{p}\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{p}dx.

(2.4.1)

If the right-hand side is finite, equality holds if and only if
f=0
almost everywhere.

The study of the Hardy-type inequalities related to the Magnetic Schrödinger operator (
1.3.1
), in the recent years has risen a lot of interest in the spectral analysis of magnetic Schrödinger operators (see
[
41
, Theorems 1 and 2]
).

The classical Hardy inequality

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}dx\leqslant\bigg(\frac{n-2}{2}\bigg)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u(x)|^{2}dx,\text{ }u\in{C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\})},

(2.4.2)

does not hold for the dimension
n=2
. Laptev and Weidl in
[
41
]
discovered that by replacing the gradient
\frac{1}{i}\nabla
with ”magnetic” gradient
\frac{1}{i}\nabla+A
, the situation improves, where
A
is given by

A(r,\theta)=\frac{\alpha(\theta)}{r}(\sin{\theta},-\cos{\theta}),

(2.4.3)

where
\alpha
is the magnetic flux.

A magnetic Hardy-type inequality (see
[
41
, Theorem 1]
)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|u(x)|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}dx\leqslant C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|(\frac{1}{i}\nabla+A+)u(x)|^{2}dx,

(2.4.4)

holds true with the constant
C
strongly depending on the magnetic field and for the Aharonov-Bohm type magnetic potential, the inequality holds with a sharp constant.

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|u(x)|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}dx\leqslant C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg|\bigg(\frac{1}{i}\nabla+A\bigg)u(x)\bigg|^{2}dx,u\in{C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\{0\})}

(2.4.5)

holds true with the sharp constant
C=\bigg(\min_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|k-\alpha|\bigg)^{-2}
. Here
\alpha
is the circulation of
A
round the origin and

\alpha=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}Adx,

where
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is a unit sphere.

2.4.2
Sturm-Liouville operator and Bargmann inequality

Let
\{p,q,w\}
be the Sturm-Liouville coefficients defined on the interval
(a,b)
. Then the general Sturm-Liouville differential equation (see
[
23
, Section 7]
) is given by

-\bigg(p(x)y^{\prime}(x)\bigg)^{\prime}+q(x)y(x)=\lambda w(x)y(x)\text{ for all $x\in{(a,b)}$},

(2.4.6)

where
w(x)>0
is a weight function
w:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
.

The classical Sturm Liouville differential equation (see
[
23
, Section 7]
) is given by

-y^{\prime\prime}(x)=\lambda y(x)\text{ for all $x\in{(-\infty,+\infty)}$}.

(2.4.7)

A detail history of Sturm-Liouville theory can be found in
[
47
]
.
A sturm Liouville differential operator is thus given by

\tau:=-\bigg(p(x)y^{\prime}(x)\bigg)^{\prime}+q(x)y(x).

(2.4.8)

Generally, well-posed Sturm-Liouville boundary value problem generate self-adjoint differential operators in
L^{2}(a,b)
.

Lemma 2.4.2

(
[
57
,
Lemma 1.2]
)
.

Let
\tau
be a self-adjoint Sturm Liouville operator on the interval
(a,b)
and
\lambda
be a real number. If
\tau
has
n
eigenvalues below
\lambda
, counting multiplicities, then there is a non-zero solution of the equation

\tau u=\lambda u

(2.4.9)

with
n+1
zeros in
(a,b)
.

Let
M\in{L_{loc}^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
and consider the following self adjoint Sturm-Liouville operator

\tau=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{c}{x^{2}}-M(x),\text{ on $L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$},

(2.4.10)

where
c
is a constant.

Lemma 2.4.3
.

If the operator (
2.4.10
) has
n
negative eigenvalues (counting multiplicities), then

\int_{\mathbb{R}}M(x)dx>n\sqrt{4c+1}.

(2.4.11)

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4.2
, there is a solution of

-y^{\prime\prime}+\big(\frac{c}{x^{2}}-M(x)\big)y=0

(2.4.12)

that has
n+1
positive zeros, in a bounded interval on
\mathbb{R}
.

Let
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
be any two consecutive zeros , then

z(x):=x\frac{y^{\prime}(x)}{y(x)},\text{ $x\in{(x_{1},x_{2})}$}

is locally absolutely continuous.

\displaystyle z^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=1.\frac{y^{\prime}(x)}{y(x)}+x.\bigg(\frac{y(x).y^{\prime\prime}(x)-y^{\prime}(x)y^{\prime}(x)}{(y(x))^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{y^{\prime}(x)}{y(x)}+x.\bigg(\frac{y^{\prime\prime}(x)}{y(x)}-\bigg(\frac{y^{\prime}(x)}{y(x)}\bigg)^{2}\bigg).

Since
\frac{y^{\prime\prime}(x)}{y(x)}=\frac{c}{x^{2}}-M(x)
and
\frac{y^{\prime}(x)}{y(x)}=\frac{1}{x}z(x)
, it follows that

\displaystyle z^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}z(x_{1})+x.\bigg(\big(\frac{c}{x^{2}}-M(x)\big)-\bigg(\frac{1}{x}z(x)\bigg)^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{x}\bigg((z(x))^{2}-z(x)\bigg)+\frac{c}{x}-xM(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{x}\bigg(\bigg(z(x)-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\bigg)+\frac{c}{x}-xM(x).

Thus,
z(x)
satisfies

z^{\prime}(x)=\frac{-1}{x}(z(x)-\frac{1}{2})^{2}+\frac{c+\frac{1}{4}}{x}-xM(x)

and

\lim_{x\to x_{1}}z(x)=\infty,\text{ $\lim_{x\to x_{2}}z(x)=-\infty.$}

By setting

\frac{-1}{x}(z(x)-\frac{1}{2})^{2}+\frac{c+\frac{1}{4}}{x}=0,

we obtain

z_{\pm}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\pm\sqrt{c+\frac{1}{4}}.

We shall therefore have the two points

\displaystyle x_{{+}}:=\max\{x\in{(x_{1},x_{2})}|z(x)\geqslant z_{+}\}\in{(x_{1},x_{2})},

\displaystyle x_{{-}}:=\min\{x\in{(x_{+},x_{2})}|z(x)\leqslant z_{-}\}\in{(x_{+},x_{2})}

such that
z(x_{\pm})=z_{\pm}(x)
, for
z(x_{1})\in[z_{-},z_{+}]
and
x\in{[x_{+},x_{-}]}
.

This implies that
z^{\prime}(x)\geqslant-M(x_{1})
for
x\in{[x_{+},x_{-}]}
.

Thus

\displaystyle\int_{x_{+}}^{x_{-}}-M(x_{1})dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{x_{+}}^{x_{-}}z^{\prime}(x)dx=z_{-}-z_{+}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{c+\frac{1}{4}}\bigg)-\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{c+\frac{1}{4}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{c+\frac{1}{4}}=-\sqrt{4c+1}.

So

-\int_{x_{+}}^{x_{-}}M(x)dx\leqslant-\sqrt{4c+1}.

Hence

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}M(x)dx>n\sqrt{4c+1}.

(2.4.13)

∎

2.4.3
Diamagnetism

For Schrödinger operators, introduction of a magnetic field raises the energy.

Theorem 2.4.4

(Diamagnetic inequality
[
24
, Theorem 2.1.1]
)
.

Let
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be in
L_{loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and suppose that
f\in{L_{loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
is such that
(-i\nabla+A)f\in{L_{loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. Then
|f|\in{H_{loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and

\big|\nabla|f|\big|\leqslant\big|(-i\nabla+A)f\big|

(2.4.14)

almost everywhere.

The prove of Theorem
2.4.4
requires us to differentiate the absolute value which we state in the proposition below.

Proposition 2.4.1

(
[
24
, Proposition 2.1.2]
)
.

Suppose that
f\in{L_{loc}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
with
\nabla f\in{L_{loc}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
. Then also
\nabla|f|\in{L_{loc}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and with the notation

Signz=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\frac{\bar{z}}{|z|},\text{\; for\;}z\neq 0,\\
0,\text{\; for \;}z=0,\\
\end{array}\right.

(2.4.15)

we have

\nabla|f|(x)=\Re\{Sign\big(f(x)\big)\nabla f(x)\}\text{ for almost every }x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}.

(2.4.16)

In particular,

\big|\nabla|f|\leqslant\big|\nabla f\big|

almost everywhere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
u\in{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and define
|z|_{\epsilon}=\sqrt{|z|^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}-\epsilon
, for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
\epsilon>0
. We observe that

0\leqslant|z|_{\epsilon}\leqslant|z|\text{ and }\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}|z|_{\epsilon}=|z|.

Then the function
|u|_{\epsilon}
defined, for
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
by

|u|_{\epsilon}(x)=|u(x)|_{\epsilon},

belongs to
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

\nabla|u|_{\epsilon}=\frac{\Re\big(\bar{u}\nabla u\big)}{\sqrt{|u|^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}}.

(2.4.17)

Now let
f
be as in the position and define
f_{\delta}
as the convolution

f_{\delta}=f*\rho_{\delta},

with
\rho_{\delta}
being a standard approximation of unity for convolution. Explicitly, we take a
\rho\in{C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
with

\rho\geqslant 0,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(x)dx=1,

and define
\rho_{\delta}(x):=\delta^{-n}\rho(x/\delta)
, for
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
as
\delta\to 0
.

Take a test function
\varphi\in{C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
. We may extract a subsequence
\{\delta_{k}\|_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\}
(with
\delta_{k}\to 0
for
k\to\infty
) such that
f_{\delta_{k}}(x)\to f(x)
for almost every
x\in{supp\varphi}
. We restrict our attention to this subsequence. for simplicity of notation, we omit the
k
from the notation and write
\lim_{\delta\to 0}
instead of
\lim_{k\to\infty}
.

We now calculate, using dominated convergence and (
2.4.17
),

\displaystyle\int\big(\nabla\varphi\big)|f|dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int\big(\nabla\varphi\big)|f|_{\epsilon}dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int\big(\nabla\varphi\big)|f_{\delta}|_{\epsilon}dx

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int\varphi\frac{\Re\big(\bar{f_{\delta}}\nabla f_{\delta}\big)}{\sqrt{|f_{\delta}|^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}}dx.

Using the pointwise convergence of
f_{\delta}(x)
and
\big\|\nabla f_{\delta}-\nabla f\big\|_{L^{1}(supp\varphi)}\to 0
, we can take the limit
\delta\to 0
and get

\int\big(\nabla\varphi\big)|f|dx=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int\varphi\frac{\Re\big(\bar{f}\nabla f\big)}{\sqrt{|f|^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}}dx.

(2.4.18)

Now,
\varphi\nabla f\in{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and
\bar{f(x)}\big(\big|f(x)\big|^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\big)^{-1/2}\to signf(x)
as
\epsilon\to 0
, so we get (
2.4.16
) from (
2.4.18
) by dominated convergence.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4.4
.

Since
A\in{{L_{loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}
and
f\in{{L_{loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}
, the assumption
\big(\nabla+iA\big)f\in{{L_{loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}
implies that
\nabla f\in{{L_{loc}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}
. Therefore, we can use Proposition
2.4.1
to conclude that (
2.4.16
) holds for
f
. Since
\Re\big\{sign(f)iAf\big\}=0
, we can rewrite (
2.4.16
) as

\nabla|f|=\Re\big\{sign(f)\big(\nabla+iA\big)f\big\},

(2.4.19)

and therefore, since
|z|\geqslant|\Re(z)|
for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we get (
2.4.14
).
∎

2.5
Review of known results

Let
\Omega
be some open set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
g(x)=\{g_{j,k}(x)\text{ $|$ }1\leqslant j,k\leqslant n\}
be a (possibly, complex valued) matrix functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, positive definite almost everywhere such that there exists a function
\gamma(x)\geqslant 0,\gamma>0
almost everywhere such that

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}g_{j,k}(x)\xi_{i}\bar{\xi_{k}}\geqslant\gamma(x)|\xi|^{2}.

(2.5.1)

For any
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d})
. If
\gamma(x)\geqslant\gamma_{0}>0
, then the matrix is called
uniformly elliptic
.

Let
\gamma^{-1}\in{L^{p}(\Omega)}
,
p\geqslant\max(\frac{n}{2},1)
,
V\in{L_{loc}^{1}(\Omega)}
,
(V+\alpha)\in{L^{q}(\Omega)}
for some
\alpha
,
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{p}=\frac{2}{n}
,
q>1
.

Using a standard application of the Birman-Schwinger Principle and semi group theory, Melgaard and Rozenblum
[
48
]
, obtained an upper estimate for the number of negative eigenvalues for a magnetic Schrödinger operator (
1.3.2
), where
A(x)=(A_{1}(x),\dots,A_{n}(x))
is the magnetic potential, defined in a bounded domain
\Omega
(for example a cube) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose further that the operator is a uniform elliptic operator (in particular, for the unit matrix
g
) and setting
p=\infty
,
q=\frac{n}{2}
. Then

Neg(H_{A,V})\leqslant c\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}V^{n/2}(x)dx,\qquad n\geqslant 3,

(2.5.2)

where
c
is a constant
[
48
, Theorem 4.6]
. Clearly, (
2.5.2
) is similar to the well known CLR inequality for the electric Schrödinger operators.

Let
\Omega
be a space with a
\sigma
-finite measure. Any self-adjoint nonnegative operator
T
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
generates a strongly continuous contractive semi group given by

Q(t)=Q_{B}(t)=e^{-tT},\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant\infty.

(2.5.3)

Denote
Q(t;x,y)=Q_{B}(t;x,y)
and let

M_{B}(t):=ess\sup_{x}\int_{\Omega}|Q_{B}(t;x,y)|^{2}dy.

(2.5.4)

Let
G(z)
be a non-negative convex function on
[0,\infty)
, polynomially growing at infinity and such that
z^{-1}G(z)
is integrable at zero. With any such
G
we associate another function

g(\lambda):=\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{-1}G(z)e^{-z/\lambda}dz.

(2.5.5)

Suppose

\int_{a}^{\infty}M_{B}(t)dt<\infty,\qquad a>0.

(2.5.6)

and let
T
generate a positively dominated semigroup, and
V\geqslant 0
be form bounded with respect
T
, with a bound smaller than 1.
Then
T-V
defines a self adjoint operator.

Theorem 2.5.1

(
[
55
, Theorem 2.4]
)
.

Let
B\in{\cal{P}}
and
T\in{{\cal{P}\cal{D}}(B)}
. Suppose that
M_{B}(t)
satisfies (
2.5.6
) and
M_{B}(t)={\cal{O}}(t^{\alpha})
at zero, with some
\alpha>0
. Fix a nonnegative convex function
G
polynomially growing at infinity and such that
G(z)=0
near zero, put
g={\cal{L}(G)}
. Then

Neg(T-V)\leqslant\frac{1}{g(1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{t}M_{B}(t)G(tV(x))dxdt,

(2.5.7)

as long as the expression on the right hand side is finite.

Using the result in (
2.5.7
), Rozenblum and Solomyak
[
55
]
obtained the quantity
Neg(H_{A,V})
for the magnetic Schrödinger operator (
1.3.2
) with a given magnetic vector potential
A(x)=\{A_{j}(x)\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}\in{L_{loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
as

Neg(H_{A,V})\leqslant c(G)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}V(x)^{\frac{n}{2}}dx,\qquad n\geqslant 3,

(2.5.8)

where
c(G)=(2\pi)^{\frac{-n}{2}}g(1)^{-1}\int_{a}^{\infty}(t-a)t^{\frac{-n}{2}-1}dt
. For
n=3
,

the constant

turns out the best constant (see
[
12
, Section 5.2]

[

]

3.3
Dependence of the estimate on the magnetic field.

The estimate (

,

on

Therefore theorem (

Chapter 4
Conclusion and Recommendations

4.1
Conclusion

In this study, the spectral estimate of the magnetic Schrödinger operator with Aharonov-Bohm magnetic field in a straight strip subject to Neumann boundary conditions has been obtained. In particular, we have obtained an upper estimate (
3.2.1
) for the number of negative eigenvalues for the operator (
3.1.1
). We have shown in §(
3.3
) that the upper estimate (
3.2.1
) depends on the properties of the magnetic field
B
and that such an estimate fails in the absence of magnetic field and only holds in the presence of the Aharonov-Bohm magnetic field with the flux satisfying the non integer condition.

4.2
Recommendations

There are several directions in which one can extend the above results, for instance, treating the problem using Numerical techniques, one can investigate whether mixed (Dirichlet, Neumann and Robin) boundary conditions applied on the strip can generate similar results.

An open problem here concerns the optimal constant (what is the best constant?) in the estimate. In an attempt to answer this question, one has two options; one is either treating it analytically or using Numerical techniques.

Also, one can extend the above results to a curved quantum waveguide with magnetic fields, where in this case the curvature also contributes to the electric potential.

Finally, instead of restricting the magnetic field to non-integer flux values, it would be interesting to consider a reasonably large class of fields by removing the singularities at integer points.
