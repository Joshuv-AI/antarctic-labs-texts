Countable Tensor Products of Hermite Spaces and Spaces of Gaussian Kernels
M. Gnewuch, M. Hefter, A. Hinrichs, K. Ritter

Abstract
. In recent years finite tensor products of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs) of Gaussian kernels on the one hand and of Hermite spaces on the other hand have been considered in tractability analysis of multivariate problems. In the present paper we study countably infinite tensor products for both types of spaces. We show that the incomplete tensor product in the sense of von Neumann may be identified with an RKHS whose domain is a proper subset of the sequence space ℝ ℕ {\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}} . Moreover, we show that each tensor product of spaces of Gaussian kernels having square-summable shape parameters is isometrically isomorphic to a tensor product of Hermite spaces; the corresponding isomorphism is given explicitly, respects point evaluations, and is also an L 2 L^{2} -isometry. This result directly transfers to the case of finite tensor products. Furthermore, we provide regularity results for Hermite spaces of functions of a single variable.



1.
Introduction

This paper is motivated by the study of integration and

L^{2}
-approximation in a tensor product setting for problems
with a large or infinite number
d
of variables. Specifically,
we consider Hilbert spaces
H(M)
with reproducing kernels

M:=\bigotimes_{j}m_{j}

of tensor product form. Here we have

j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
if
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
if
d=\infty
,
and we assume that either all of the kernels

m_{j}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

are Hermite or all are Gaussian kernels. The same
terminology, Hermite kernel or Gaussian kernel,
will be used for the corresponding tensor
product kernels
M
, and the reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs)
with Hermite kernels are known as Hermite spaces, see
Irrgeher and Leobacher 2015
.
Analogously, and despite a different use in stochastic analysis,
the RKHSs of Gaussian kernels will be called Gaussian spaces
throughout this paper.

For both types of kernels, Hermite and Gaussian, the functions
in
H(M)
are defined on the space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if

d\in{\mathbb{N}}
or on a subset of the sequence space
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
if
d=\infty
.
Moreover, the measure
\mu
that defines the integral or
the
L^{2}
-norm is the corresponding
product of the standard normal distribution
\mu_{0}
.

We are primarily interested in the case
d=\infty
.
This is the technically more
demanding case from which corresponding results
for the case
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
can be deduced easily.
In the present paper we provide an analysis of the function spaces,
while the complexity of integration and
L^{2}
-approximation will
be studied in a follow-up paper.

Our approach is as follows. At first, we study the spaces
H(m_{j})

of functions of a single variable, and then we turn to the countable
tensor product of these spaces, which allows to carry over the results
for
d=1
to the tensor product Hilbert space
in a canonical way.
Finally, we identify the tensor product Hilbert space with an
RKHS
H(M)
for the tensor product kernel

M\colon{\mathfrak{X}}\times{\mathfrak{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

on a suitably chosen domain

{\mathfrak{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}.

Let us refer to prior work on tractability of integration and

L^{2}
-approximation in the case
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, i.e., for finite tensor
products of either Hermite or Gaussian kernels.
Both types of kernels have been studied separately, so far.
For Hermite spaces the first contribution is due to

Irrgeher and Leobacher 2015
, followed by

Irrgeher et al. 2015
,

Irrgeher et al. 2016a
,
and

Irrgeher et al. 2016b

with a focus on spaces of analytic functions,
and by

Dick et al. 2018

with a focus on spaces of finite smoothness.
For Gaussian spaces the first contribution is due to

Fasshauer et al. 2012
, followed by

Kuo et al. 2017
,

Sloan and Woźniakowski 2018
, and

Karvonen et al. 2021
.
To the best of our knowledge, the case
d=\infty
has not been
studied yet neither for Hermite nor for Gaussian kernels.

We show, in particular, that Hermite spaces and Gaussian spaces
with
d=\infty
are closely related in the following sense:
Every Gaussian space with square-summable shape parameters
is isometrically isomorphic to a Hermite space with an explicitly
given isometry that respects point evaluations and is also an

L^{2}(\mu)
-isometry. We add that the same holds true for every
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. This result may explain an observation due to

Karvonen et al. 2021

concerning the analysis of integration and approximation problems,
namely that techniques
similar to those used in the Gaussian case are used in the Hermite
case.
A similar observation was made earlier by
Kuo et al. 2017
,
were the authors write that Gauss-Hermite quadratures used in

Irrgeher et al. 2015
for integrands stemming from Hermite spaces work well
also on Gaussian spaces, although the latter ones do not contain any
non-trivial polynomial.

In the following we present the construction and a sketch of results for
Hermite spaces. To define the Hermite kernels
m_{j}:=k_{j}

we employ the
L^{2}(\mu_{0})
-normalized Hermite
polynomials
h_{\nu}
of degree
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
see Section
3.1
for the definition and
basic properties.
For any choice of Fourier
weights
\alpha_{\nu,j}>0
with

\inf_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu,j}>0

and

\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1}\cdot|h_{\nu}(x)|^{2}<\infty

for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
the corresponding Hermite kernel is given by

\phantom{\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}.}k_{j}(x,y):=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1}\cdot h_{\nu}(x)\cdot h_{\nu}(y),\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}.

A general set (H1)–(H3) of summability and monotonicity
assumptions for the Fourier weights is presented in
Section
3.2
. In this introduction we focus on two particular
kinds of Fourier
weights, namely the case of polynomial growth, where

\phantom{\qquad\quad\nu,j\in{\mathbb{N}},}\alpha_{\nu,j}:=(\nu+1)^{r_{j}},\qquad\quad\nu,j\in{\mathbb{N}},

with
r_{j}>0
, and the case of (sub-)exponential
growth, where

\phantom{\qquad\quad\nu,j\in{\mathbb{N}},}\alpha_{\nu,j}:=2^{r_{j}\cdot\nu^{b_{j}}},\qquad\quad\nu,j\in{\mathbb{N}},

with
r_{j},b_{j}>0
. The latter kind of Fourier weights with

b_{j}=1
for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
is the proper choice when we study the
relation between Hermite and Gaussian spaces,
see Section
5
.

Let us consider Hermite spaces of functions of a
single variable. For any fixed
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
the regularity of the functions
from
H(k_{j})
is determined by the asymptotic behavior of

\alpha_{\nu,j}
as
\nu\to\infty
, see

Irrgeher and Leobacher 2015
,
Irrgeher et al. 2015
, and
Dick et al. 2018

for specific results. For Fourier weights
with a polynomial growth the low regularity limit is
r_{j}=1/2
, where
we loose convergence of
k_{j}
. See Example
3.5
and cf.
Dick et al. 2018
, where convergence is established for
r_{j}>5/6
.
For Fourier weights
with a (sub-)exponential growth the elements of
H(k_{j})
belong to the
Gevrey class of index
\max(1/(2b_{j}),1)
; in particular, they are real
analytic functions if
b_{j}\geq 1/2
. See Lemma
3.7
and
cf.
Irrgeher and Leobacher 2015
and
Irrgeher et al. 2015
, where
analyticity is established for
b_{j}\geq 1
, as well as

Irrgeher et al. 2016b
, where for
b_{j}\in{]0,1[}
the inclusion
of
H(k_{j})
in the Gevrey class of index
1/b_{j}
is claimed.

Furthermore, Hermite spaces with Fourier weights of polynomial
growth with
r_{j}
varying in
{]1/2,\infty[}
and of exponential
growths with
b_{j}=1
and with
r_{j}
varying in
{]0,\infty[}

form interpolation scales with equality of the norms due to quadratic
interpolation and the original norm on the RKHS.
See Remark
2.3
and Examples
3.5
and
3.6
.

Next we present the main results for Hermite spaces of functions of
infinitely many variables, where we require

r_{j}>1/2\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{-r_{j}}<\infty

in the case of polynomial growth
and

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{-r_{j}}<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}b_{j}>0

in the case of (sub-)exponential growth.
In both cases we additionally require

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\alpha_{0,j}-1|<\infty.

See Section
3.3
.
We show that the maximal domain
{\mathfrak{X}}
for the tensor product kernel

K:=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}k_{j}
is related to an intersection of
weighted
\ell^{2}
-spaces and satisfies

\ell^{\infty}\subsetneq{\mathfrak{X}}\subsetneq{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}

as well as

\mu({\mathfrak{X}})=1.

See Proposition
3.10
and Lemma
3.17
.
We conclude, in particular, that the Hermite spaces
H(K)
of
functions of infinitely many variables are never spaces of
functions on the domain
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

In the case of exponential growth with

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}b_{j}\geq 1

the maximal domain is explicitly given as

{\mathfrak{X}}=\bigg\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\colon\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{-r_{j}}\cdot x_{j}^{2}<\infty\bigg\}.

See Proposition
3.18
and cf. Proposition
3.19
, which deals with the case of polynomial and
sub-exponential growth.

Moreover, we show that the incomplete tensor product
H^{({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{0}^{-1/2})}
,
see
von Neumann 1939

and Definition
A.2
,
of the Hermite spaces
H(k_{j})
based on the constant functions

\alpha_{0,j}^{-1/2}
as unit vectors may be identified with the Hermite space

H(K)
on the maximal domain
{\mathfrak{X}}
in a canonical way. See Lemma
3.8

and Theorem
A.6
.
We add that the tensor product norm on
H(K)
respects the
infinite-dimensional ANOVA decomposition of a function, i.e.,
different ANOVA components are pairwise orthogonal in
H(K)
.
See Remark
3.14
.

Next we come to Gaussian spaces. The Gaussian kernel

m_{j}:=\ell_{j}
with shape parameter
\sigma_{j}>0
is given by

\phantom{\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}.}\ell_{j}(x,y):=\exp(-\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot(x-y)^{2}),\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Gaussian spaces
H(\ell_{j})
and finite tensor products thereof
are thoroughly analyzed in

Steinwart et al. 2006
, see also
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
.
In the following we sketch our main results for Gaussian
spaces in the case
d=\infty
with shape parameters

\sigma_{j}>0
satisfying

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sigma_{j}^{2}<\infty.

We show that the incomplete tensor product

G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
of the Gaussian spaces
H(\ell_{j})
based on the unit
vectors
v_{j}(x):=\exp(-\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot x^{2})
may be identified
with a Gaussian space
H(L)
in a canonical way.
The corresponding domain for the tensor product kernel
L:=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ell_{j}
is given by

{\mathfrak{X}}=\bigg\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\colon\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot x_{j}^{2}<\infty\bigg\},

which is a proper subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
despite the convergence of
\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\ell_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})

for all
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
See Lemma
4.2
, Remark
4.6
, and
Theorem
A.6
. We add that
\mu({\mathfrak{X}})=1
.

The identification of the Gaussian spaces
H(L)
and the Hermite
spaces
H(K)
with incomplete tensor products allows to establish
the following result via tensorization.
For any Gaussian space
H(L)
we consider the Hermite space
H(K)
with
Fourier weights

\phantom{\qquad\quad\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\ j\in{\mathbb{N}},}\alpha_{\nu,j}:=\frac{1}{(1-\beta_{j})\cdot\beta^{\nu}_{j}},\qquad\quad\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\ j\in{\mathbb{N}},

where

\beta_{j}:=1-\frac{2}{1+c_{j}^{2}}\qquad\text{with}\qquad c_{j}:=\left(1+8\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)^{1/4}.

Moreover, we define

Qf({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}\cdot\exp\biggl(-\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\frac{c_{j}^{2}-1}{4}\,x_{j}^{2}\biggr)\cdot f\bigl(c_{1}x_{1},c_{2}x_{2},\dots\bigr)

for
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
.
Then
Q
is an isometric isomorphism on
L^{2}(\mu)
and

Q|_{H(K)}
is an isometric isomorphism between
H(K)
and
H(L)
.
See Theorem
5.8
.
Another feature of
Q
that is highly relevant
in the context of integration and approximation problems
is the obvious fact that for every
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
a single function value
of
f\in H(K)
suffices to compute
Qf({\boldsymbol{x}})
and vice versa.

Let us close the introduction with some remarks concerning
notation, terminology, and conventions.
We consider the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
{\mathbb{R}}
and, in particular,
the standard normal distribution
\mu_{0}
on this space.
Moreover, we consider the corresponding product
\sigma

{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}

\mu

For

and
{\mathbb{K}}\in\{{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}\}

For all

,

,

we have
\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\overline{a_{i}}a_{j}\cdot K(x_{i},x_{j})\geq 0
.

For any sequence
(a_{j})_{j\geq J_{0}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}
such that

(\prod_{j=J_{0}}^{J}a_{j})_{J\geq J_{0}}
is convergent we put

(1.1)

\prod_{j=J_{0}}^{\infty}a_{j}:=\lim_{J\to\infty}\prod_{j=J_{0}}^{J}a_{j}.

The collection of arbitrary finite partial products
of
(a_{j})_{j\geq J_{0}}
forms a net; the corresponding stronger
notion of convergence is discussed in Section
A.1
.

We use the following notation for subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
The space of all sequences
{\boldsymbol{x}}:=(x_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that

\{j\in{\mathbb{N}}\colon x_{j}\neq 0\}
is finite is denoted by

c_{00}
, and
\ell^{\infty}
denotes the space of all bounded sequences.
For any sequence
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}:=(\omega_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of positive real numbers
and
1\leq p<\infty
we use
\ell^{p}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})
to denote
the space of all sequences
{\boldsymbol{x}}
such that

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\omega_{j}\cdot|x_{j}|^{p}<\infty.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we use
{\boldsymbol{N}}_{n}
to denote the set of all
sequences
(\nu_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
such that

\nu_{j}=0
for every
j>n
. Moreover, we put

{\boldsymbol{N}}:=\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\boldsymbol{N}}_{n}
, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{N}}=c_{00}\cap{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

We use
{\boldsymbol{U}}
to denote the set of all finite subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}
.

2.
L^{2}
-Subspaces as Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces

Consider a space
L^{2}(\rho):=L^{2}(E,\rho)
over
{\mathbb{K}}\in\{{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}\}
with
respect to a measure
\rho
on a
\sigma
-algebra on a set
E
.
Let
N
denote a countable set, and let
({\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu})_{\nu\in N}
denote a
family of square-integrable functions on
E
such that the corresponding
equivalence classes
e_{\nu}
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\rho)
.
Moreover, let
(\alpha_{\nu})_{\nu\in N}
denote a family of
positive real numbers, which will be called
Fourier weights
in
view of the following result
that is essentially due to
Gnewuch et al. 2019
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that

(2.1)

\inf_{\nu\in N}\alpha_{\nu}>0

and

(2.2)

\sum_{\nu\in N}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\cdot|{\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu}(x)|^{2}<\infty

for every
x\in E
. If
(c_{\nu})_{\nu\in N}\in{\mathbb{K}}^{N}
satisfies

\sum_{\nu\in N}\alpha_{\nu}\cdot|c_{\nu}|^{2}<\infty,

then
\sum_{\nu\in N}c_{\nu}\cdot e_{\nu}
converges in

L^{2}(\rho)
and

\sum_{\nu\in N}c_{\nu}\cdot{\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu}(x)
is absolutely convergent
for every
x\in E
. Moreover,

\phantom{\qquad\quad x,y\in E,}K(x,y):=\sum_{\nu\in N}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\cdot{\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu}(x)\cdot\overline{{\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu}(y)},\qquad\quad x,y\in E,

defines a reproducing kernel on

E\times E
. The corresponding Hilbert space is given by

(2.3)

H(K)=\Bigl\{\sum_{\nu\in N}c_{\nu}\cdot{\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu}\colon\text{$(c_{\nu})_{\nu\in N}\in{\mathbb{K}}^{N}$ with
$\sum_{\nu\in N}\alpha_{\nu}\cdot|c_{\nu}|^{2}<\infty$}\Bigr\}

and

\phantom{\qquad{\mathfrak{f}},{\mathfrak{g}}\in H(K).}\langle{\mathfrak{f}},{\mathfrak{g}}\rangle_{H(K)}=\sum_{\nu\in N}\alpha_{\nu}\cdot\int_{E}{\mathfrak{f}}\cdot\overline{{\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu}}\,d\rho\cdot\int_{E}{\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu}\cdot\overline{{\mathfrak{g}}}\,d\rho,\qquad{\mathfrak{f}},{\mathfrak{g}}\in H(K).

Proof.

The convergence of

\sum_{\nu\in N}c_{\nu}\cdot e_{\nu}
and

\sum_{\nu\in N}c_{\nu}\cdot{\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu}(y)
, as claimed, is easily
verified.

The remaining part of the proof is based on the following
facts. The linear subspace

(2.4)

{\mathcal{H}}:=\Bigl\{f\in L^{2}(\rho)\colon\sum_{\nu\in N}\alpha_{\nu}\cdot|\langle f,e_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}|^{2}<\infty\Bigr\}

of
L^{2}(\rho)
,
equipped with the scalar product

(2.5)

\phantom{\qquad f,g\in{\mathcal{H}},}\langle f,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}:=\sum_{\nu\in N}\alpha_{\nu}\cdot\langle f,e_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}\cdot\langle e_{\nu},g\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)},\qquad f,g\in{\mathcal{H}},

is a Hilbert space with a continuous embedding of norm

\sup_{\nu\in N}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1/2}
into
L^{2}(\rho)
.
The elements
h_{\nu}:=\alpha_{\nu}^{-1/2}e_{\nu}
with
\nu\in N
form an
orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}
.

We employ the first part of the proof to define a linear
mapping
\Phi\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{K}}^{E}
by

\phantom{\qquad\quad x\in E,}\Phi f(x):=\sum_{\nu\in N}\langle f,e_{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}\cdot{\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu}(x),\qquad\quad x\in E,

i.e.,
\Phi f
is the pointwise
limit of the
L^{2}
-Fourier partial sums of
f\in{\mathcal{H}}
.
Obviously,
\Phi h_{\nu}=\alpha_{\nu}^{-1/2}{\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu}
.
The mapping
\Phi
is injective, see

Gnewuch et al. 2019
, so that

\Phi({\mathcal{H}})
, equipped with the scalar product

\phantom{\qquad\quad f,g\in{\mathcal{H}},}\langle\Phi f,\Phi g\rangle=\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\qquad\quad f,g\in{\mathcal{H}},

is a Hilbert space of
{\mathbb{K}}
-valued functions on
E
.
It follows that
(\Phi({\mathcal{H}}),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is a reproducing
kernel Hilbert space, see

Gnewuch et al. 2019
,
and its reproducing kernel is
K
,
see
Gnewuch et al. 2019
.
Finally, observe that

\Phi({\mathcal{H}})=\Bigl\{\sum_{\nu\in N}c_{\nu}\cdot{\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu}\colon\text{$(c_{\nu})_{\nu\in N}\in{\mathbb{K}}^{N}$ with
$\sum_{\nu\in N}\alpha_{\nu}\cdot|c_{\nu}|^{2}<\infty$}\Bigr\}.\qed

Subsequently, we do no longer distinguish between
square-integrable functions on
E
and elements of
L^{2}(\rho)
;
in particular, we identify
{\mathfrak{e}}_{\nu}
and
e_{\nu}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Assume that (
2.1
) and (
2.2
)
for every
x\in E
are satisfied.
According to Proposition
2.1
,

H(K)\subseteq L^{2}(\rho)
with a continuous embedding,
which is compact if and only if for every
r>0
the set

\{\nu\in N\colon\alpha_{\nu}\leq r\}
is finite.
The proof of the proposition reveals that

{\mathcal{H}}
is a feature space and
\varphi\colon E\to{\mathcal{H}}
,
given by
\varphi(x):=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\cdot e_{\nu}(x)\cdot e_{\nu}
, is a feature map of
K
.
See, e.g.,
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
for these concepts.

Additionally, assume that

e_{\nu^{*}}=1

for some
\nu^{*}\in N
.
Then

K^{*}:=K-\alpha_{\nu^{*}}^{-1}

is a reproducing kernel, too. Furthermore,

\int_{E}f\,d\rho=0

for every
f\in H(K^{*})
, since

\int_{E}e_{\nu}\,d\rho=\langle e_{\nu},e_{\nu^{*}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}=0

for
\nu\neq\nu^{*}
. In particular, this yields

H(1)\cap H(K^{*})=\{0\},

and
\int_{E}f\,d\rho
is the orthogonal projection
of
f\in H(K)
onto
H(1)
in
H(K)
as well as in
L^{2}(\rho)
.

Remark 2.3
.

We discuss interpolation of Hilbert spaces in the present
framework. To this end we consider two families

(\alpha_{\nu,0})_{\nu\in N}
and

(\alpha_{\nu,1})_{\nu\in N}
of Fourier weights such that

\inf_{\nu\in N}\alpha_{\nu,0}>0
and

\{\nu\in N\colon\alpha_{\nu,1}/\alpha_{\nu,0}\leq r\}
is
finite for every
r>0
. Clearly

\inf_{\nu\in N}\alpha_{\nu,1}>0
, too.
Analogously to (
2.4
) and (
2.5
)
we obtain
linear subspaces
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
of
L^{2}(\rho)
, based on

(\alpha_{\nu,0})_{\nu\in N}
and

(\alpha_{\nu,1})_{\nu\in N}
,
respectively. It follows that

{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
is a dense subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
with a compact
embedding.

For quadratic interpolation of the spaces
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
by means of
the
K
-method and the
J
-method we obtain

K_{\theta,2}({\mathcal{H}}_{0},{\mathcal{H}}_{1})=J_{\theta,2}({\mathcal{H}}_{0},{\mathcal{H}}_{1})={\mathcal{H}}

for every
0<\theta<1
, where
{\mathcal{H}}
is the linear subspace of

L^{2}(\rho)
based on the Fourier weights

\phantom{\qquad\quad\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}.}\alpha_{\nu}:=\alpha_{\nu,1}^{\theta}\cdot\alpha_{\nu,0}^{1-\theta},\qquad\quad\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Furthermore, we have equality of norms in these spaces.
See
Chandler-Wilde et al. 2015
.
Assume, additionally, that (
2.2
) is satisfied for

\alpha_{\nu}:=\alpha_{\nu,0}
. Then the same conclusions hold true
for the associated reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.

3.
Hermite Spaces

3.1.
Functions of a Single Variable

Here we consider the space

L^{2}(\mu_{0}):=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mu_{0})
for the standard normal
distribution
\mu_{0}
over
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}}
.

The normalized Hermite polynomials
h_{\nu}
of degree

\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mu_{0})

and can be obtained by orthonormalizing the monomials. In particular,
we have
h_{0}(x)=1
and
h_{1}(x)=x
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
An explicit representation for any
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
is

(3.1)

\phantom{\qquad\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}.}h_{\nu}(x)=\sqrt{\nu!}\sum^{\lfloor\nu/2\rfloor}_{k=0}(-1)^{k}\frac{x^{\nu-2k}}{2^{k}\,k!\,(\nu-2k)!},\qquad\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}},

see
Szegö 1975
.
For basic properties of
these functions we refer to, e.g.,
Bogachev 1998
.
In the next lemma we collect some asymptotic properties of the
Hermite polynomials.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\bigl(\nu^{1/4}\cdot|h_{\nu}(x)|\bigr)<\infty

as well as Cramér’s inequality

(3.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}|h_{\nu}(x)|\leq\exp(x^{2}/4).

For
x=0
we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\inf_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\bigl(\nu^{1/4}\cdot|h_{2\nu}(x)|\bigr)>0.

Proof.

For every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

(3.5)

\sup_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\frac{\Gamma(\nu/2+1)}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}\cdot 2^{\nu/2}\cdot\sqrt{\nu!}\cdot|h_{\nu}(x)|<\infty,

see, e.g.,
Szegö 1975
.
Moreover, Stirling’s approximation yields
the strong asymptotics

(3.6)

\frac{\Gamma(\nu/2+1)}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}\cdot 2^{\nu/2}\cdot\sqrt{\nu!}\approx(\pi/2)^{1/4}\cdot\nu^{1/4}

as
\nu\to\infty
.
Combine (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) to obtain (
3.2
).
For Cramér’s inequality (
3.3
) we refer to, e.g.,
Indritz 1961
.
For
x=0
and
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
we have

(3.7)

|h_{2\nu}(x)|=\sqrt{(2\nu)!}/(\nu!\cdot 2^{\nu}),

see, e.g.,
Szegö 1975
.
Furthermore, (
3.6
) implies

(3.8)

\frac{\nu!\cdot 2^{\nu}}{\sqrt{(2\nu)!}}\approx\pi^{1/4}\cdot\nu^{1/4}

as
\nu\to\infty
.
Combine (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) to obtain (
3.4
).
∎

We will also employ the following estimate (with a suboptimal constant).

Lemma 3.2
.

For every
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

|h_{\nu}(x)|\leq 2^{\nu}\max(1,|x|^{\nu}).

Proof.

Due to (
3.1
) we have

|h_{\nu}(x)|\leq\sum^{\lfloor\nu/2\rfloor}_{k=0}\frac{\sqrt{\nu!}}{2^{k}\,k!\,(\nu-2k)!}\cdot\max(1,|x|^{\nu}).

Furthermore, the elementary estimate
(2k)!\leq 2^{2k}(k!)^{2}

implies

\frac{\sqrt{\nu!}}{2^{k}\,k!}\leq\frac{\nu!}{(2k)!}

for
k\leq\nu/2
, and therefore

\sum^{\lfloor\nu/2\rfloor}_{k=0}\frac{\sqrt{\nu!}}{2^{k}\,k!\,(\nu-2k)!}\leq\sum^{\lfloor\nu/2\rfloor}_{k=0}\frac{\nu!}{(2k)!\,(\nu-2k)!}\leq\sum^{\nu}_{k=0}\frac{\nu!}{k!\,(\nu-k)!}=2^{\nu}.\qed

With
e_{\nu}=h_{\nu}
and Fourier weights
\alpha_{\nu}\in{]0,\infty[}

for
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, the summability property (
2.2
) reads as

(3.9)

\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\cdot|h_{\nu}(x)|^{2}<\infty.

It is closely related to the condition

(3.10)

\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\cdot\nu^{-1/2}<\infty.

Lemma 3.3
.

If (
3.10
) is satisfied,
then (
3.9
) holds for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

The statement follows directly from (
3.2
).
∎

Due to (
3.4
)
the reverse implication holds true under mild additional assumptions. For
instance, if (
3.9
) holds for
x=0
and

\alpha_{\nu}\leq\alpha_{\nu+1}
holds for all
\nu
sufficiently
large, then (
3.10
) is satisfied.

Definition 3.4
.

Assume that
\inf_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu}>0
and that (
3.9
) is satisfied for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The reproducing kernel

(3.11)

\phantom{\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}},}k(x,y):=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\cdot h_{\nu}(x)\cdot h_{\nu}(y),\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}},

is called a
Hermite kernel
and the Hilbert space
H(k)
is called a
Hermite space
of functions of a single real variable.

Observe that Proposition
2.1
provides a characterization
of the Hermite space
H(k)

as a linear subspace of
L^{2}(\mu_{0})
.
For definition and terminology we refer to

Irrgeher and Leobacher 2015

and
Dick et al. 2018
,
where
\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}<\infty
or

\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha_{\nu}\cdot\nu^{-1/6}<\infty
, respectively,
is assumed, cf. (
3.10
) and Lemma
3.3
.

Example 3.5
.

Let
r>0
.
If
\alpha_{\nu}:=(\nu+1)^{r}
for
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, then
(
3.10
) is satisfied if and only if
r>1/2
.
Moreover, the spaces
H(k)
with
r>1/2
form an
interpolation scale, see Remark
2.3
.
In the particular
case
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
the space
H(k)
is, up to equivalence of norms, the
Sobolev space of all continuous
functions in
L^{2}(\mu_{0})
with weak derivatives up to order
r

belonging to
L^{2}(\mu_{0})
,
see, e.g.,
Bogachev 1998

and
Dick et al. 2018
.
Unfortunately, it seems to be unknown if there is a corresponding
natural interpolation scale of weighted Sobolev spaces of fractional
order. The known interpolation results for weighted Sobolev spaces
of functions on
{\mathbb{R}}
require weights that decrease slower
at infinity than the Gaussian weight function, see

Triebel 1978
.
Hence an intrinsic characterization of the functions in
H(k)
for
non-integer
r
via fractional smoothness remains open.

Necessary as well as sufficient conditions, involving
classical differentiability, for
f\in H(k)
to hold true are
presented in
Irrgeher and Leobacher 2015
in the case
r>1
.

Example 3.6
.

Let
r,b>0
.
If
\alpha_{0}:=1
and
\alpha_{\nu}:=2^{r\cdot\nu^{b}}
for
\nu\geq 1
,
then (
3.10
) is satisfied in any case, and
H(k)
consists of

C^{\infty}
-functions.
Moreover, in the case
b=1
the spaces
H(k)
with
r>0
form an
interpolation scale, see Remark
2.3
.
If
b\geq 1
then the elements of
H(k)
are real analytic
functions, see
Irrgeher and Leobacher 2015
for the case
b=1
, which
trivially extends to any
b\geq 1
.
It is also observed in
Irrgeher et al. 2016b
without proof that, for

0<b<1
, the elements of
H(k)
belong to the Gevrey class

G^{\sigma}({\mathbb{R}})
of index
\sigma:=1/b
.
By definition,
G^{\sigma}({\mathbb{R}})
with
index
\sigma\geq 1
consists of all infinitely differentiable functions

f
on
{\mathbb{R}}
such that for any
R>0
there exists
c>0
with

|f^{(\ell)}(x)|\leq(c^{\ell+1}\ell!)^{\sigma}

for
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
x\in\left[-R,R\right]
.
The Gevrey class
G^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
coincides with the class of real analytic
functions on
{\mathbb{R}}
, while
G^{\sigma}({\mathbb{R}})
contains compactly supported
functions
f\neq 0
for
\sigma>1
, see
Rodino 1993
.
The following lemma improves upon the known regularity results for
functions in
H(k)
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
k
be the Hermite kernel specified in Example
3.6

and let

\sigma:=\max(1,1/2b).

Then

H(k)\subseteq G^{\sigma}({\mathbb{R}}).

In particular,
H(k)
contains only real analytic functions if
b\geq 1/2
.

Proof.

The first part of the proof follows the argument of
Irrgeher and Leobacher 2015
and is
based on the formula

h_{\nu}^{(\ell)}=\sqrt{\frac{\nu!}{(\nu-\ell)!}}h_{\nu-\ell}

for
\ell\leq\nu

that allows it to directly relate the coefficients in the orthogonal
expansion of
f^{(\ell)}
with respect to the Hermite polynomials
with the coefficients in the expansion of
f\in H(k)
.
Then Cramér’s inequality (
3.3
) together with the Cauchy-Schwarz
inequality lead to the pointwise estimate

|f^{(\ell)}(x)|\leq\exp(x^{2}/4)\cdot\|f\|_{H(k)}\cdot\left(\sum_{\nu=\ell}^{\infty}\frac{\nu!}{(\nu-\ell)!}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\right)^{1/2}.

It follows that
f

belongs to the Gevrey class
G^{\sigma}({\mathbb{R}})
with
index
\sigma\geq 1
if there exists
c>0
such that

(3.12)

\sum_{\nu=\ell}^{\infty}\frac{\nu!}{(\nu-\ell)!}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\leq(c^{\ell+1}\ell!)^{2\sigma}

for
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.
We now use

\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}=2^{-r\cdot\nu^{b}}\leq 2^{r}\cdot 2^{-r\cdot t^{b}}

for
t\in[\nu,\nu+1]
to estimate

\sum_{\nu=\ell}^{\infty}\frac{\nu!}{(\nu-\ell)!}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\leq\sum_{\nu=\ell}^{\infty}\nu^{\ell}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\leq 2^{r}\int_{\ell}^{\infty}t^{\ell}2^{-r\cdot t^{b}}\,dt\leq c^{\ell+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{(\ell+1)/b-1}e^{-u}\,du

with
c>0
depending only on
r
and
b
.
The last integral is just
\Gamma\left(\frac{\ell+1}{b}\right)
. Now using Stirling’s formula both as upper bound for

\Gamma\left(\frac{\ell+1}{b}\right)
and as lower bound for
\ell!
shows that there exists
c>0
depending only on
r
and
b
such that

\sum_{\nu=\ell}^{\infty}\frac{\nu!}{(\nu-\ell)!}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\leq(c^{\ell+1}\ell!)^{1/b}.

Now comparing with (
3.12
) proves the lemma.
∎

3.2.
Functions of Infinitely Many Variables

Our construction of Hermite spaces of functions of infinitely many
real variables is based on Fourier weights

\alpha_{\nu,j}\in\left]0,\infty\right[
for
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and

j\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the following properties:

(H1)

\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1}\cdot\nu^{-1/2}<\infty

for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}

and

\alpha_{\nu,j}\leq\alpha_{\nu+1,j}
for all
\nu,j\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(H2)

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\alpha_{0,j}-1|<\infty
,

(H3)

there exists an integer
j_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}},\ j\geq j_{0}}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1}<\infty
.

If (H1) is satisfied then
we may consider the
Hermite kernels

\phantom{\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}},}k_{j}(x,y):=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1}\cdot h_{\nu}(x)\cdot h_{\nu}(y),\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}},

as well as the Hermite
spaces
H(k_{j})
as linear subspaces of
L^{2}(\mu_{0})
,
see Proposition
2.1
and Lemma
3.3
. Observe that

k_{j}^{*}:=k_{j}-\alpha_{0,j}^{-1}

is a reproducing kernel, too, which follows from
h_{0}=1
.
Cf. Remark
2.2
.
Property (H2) provides a certain amount of flexibility
for the choice of the Fourier weights
\alpha_{0,j}
;
values different from
1
naturally arise in Section
5
.

In each of the spaces
H(k_{j})
we choose the constant function

\alpha_{0,j}^{-1/2}
as unit vector
and we study the corresponding incomplete tensor product

(3.13)

H^{({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{0}^{-1/2})}:=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(H(k_{j})\right)^{(\alpha_{0,j}^{-1/2})},

see Definition
A.2
.

We will employ Theorem
A.6
to identify the space
H^{({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{0}^{-1/2})}
with
a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of functions on a suitable
subset
{\mathfrak{X}}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
For any non-empty domain
{\mathfrak{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
that ensures the
convergence of the partial products
\prod_{j=1}^{J}k_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})
as

J\to\infty
we consider the tensor product kernel
K

given by

(3.14)

\phantom{\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}.}K({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}k_{j}(x_{j},y_{j}),\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}.

For a finite subset
{\mathfrak{u}}
of
{\mathbb{N}}
, we put

c_{{\mathfrak{u}}}:=\prod_{j\in{\mathfrak{u}}}\alpha_{0,j}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{0,j}^{-1},

as well as

\phantom{\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}.}k^{\ast}_{{\mathfrak{u}}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):=\prod_{j\in{\mathfrak{u}}}k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},y_{j}),\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}.

Lemma 3.8
.

Assume that
(H1)
and
(H2)
are satisfied.
Then the maximal domain for
K
is given by

(3.15)

{\mathfrak{X}}:=\Bigl\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\colon\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}k_{j}^{*}(x_{j},x_{j})<\infty\Bigr\}.

Moreover,

(3.16)

\phantom{\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}},}K({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\sum_{{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}}c_{\mathfrak{u}}\cdot k^{\ast}_{\mathfrak{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}),\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}},

with absolute convergence.

Proof.

At first, we consider the particular case that
\alpha_{0,j}=1

for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
For
J\in{\mathbb{N}}
let
{\boldsymbol{U}_{\!\!J}}
denote the power set of

\{1,\dots,J\}
.
For
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\prod_{j=1}^{J}k_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{J}(1+k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})).

Since

\ln\left(\prod_{j=1}^{J}(1+k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},x_{j}))\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\ln(1+k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},x_{j}))

and
t/2\leq\ln(1+t)\leq t
for all
0\leq t\leq 1
, we conclude that

\prod_{j=1}^{J}k_{j}(x_{j},x_{j})
converges as
J\to\infty

if and only if
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
.
Using

(3.17)

|k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})|\leq\sqrt{k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},x_{j})}\cdot\sqrt{k_{j}^{*}(y_{j},y_{j})}

and the Cauchy Schwarz inequality for sums, we obtain

\begin{split}\sum_{{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}_{\!\!J}}}\prod_{j\in{\mathfrak{u}}}|k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})|&\leq\left(\sum_{{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}_{\!\!J}}}\prod_{j\in{\mathfrak{u}}}k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},x_{j})\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\sum_{{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}_{\!\!J}}}\prod_{j\in{\mathfrak{u}}}k^{*}_{j}(y_{j},y_{j})\right)^{1/2}\\
&=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{J}(1+k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},x_{j}))\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\prod_{j=1}^{J}(1+k^{*}_{j}(y_{j},y_{j}))\right)^{1/2}.\end{split}

Consequently,

\prod_{j=1}^{J}(1+k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},y_{j}))=\sum_{{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}_{\!\!J}}}\prod_{j\in{\mathfrak{u}}}k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})

is absolutely convergent as
J\to\infty
.
We conclude that

{\mathfrak{X}}
is the maximal domain for
K
, and
(
3.16
) is satisfied with absolute convergence.

The general case is easily reduced to the particular case,
since

\prod^{J}_{j=1}\alpha_{0,j}
converges and

\lim_{j\to\infty}\alpha_{0,j}=1
due to (H2) and since

(3.18)

k_{j}=\alpha_{0,j}^{-1}\cdot(1+\alpha_{0,j}\cdot k_{j}^{*}).\qed

Observe that
{\mathfrak{X}}
does not depend on the Fourier weights

\alpha_{0,j}
.

Definition 3.9
.

Assume that (H1) and (H2) are satisfied. The reproducing kernel
K
defined by (
3.14
) on the maximal domain

{\mathfrak{X}}
given by (
3.15
)
is called a
Hermite kernel
and the Hilbert space
H(K)
is called a
Hermite space
of functions of infinitely many real variables.

Let
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
. It follows from (
3.17
) that

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|k_{j}^{*}(x_{j},y_{j})|<\infty
.
If (H1) and (H2) are satisfied, then
the product in (
3.14
) is convergent in the sense of the definition
in Section
A.1
,
and
K({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=0
is equivalent to the existence of
j\in{\mathbb{N}}

with
k_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})=0
.

The Hermite space
H(K)
is, in the sense of
Theorem
A.6
, the incomplete tensor product
H^{({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{0}^{-1/2})}
of the
Hermite spaces
H(k_{j})
based on the constant functions

\alpha_{0,j}^{-1/2}
for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
as unit vectors as in (
3.13
).

We now analyze the maximal domain
{\mathfrak{X}}
in more detail.
Put
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\nu}^{-1}:=(\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
for
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
and observe that (H3) implies
\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1}<\infty
.
The space
\ell^{2\nu}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\nu}^{-1})
consists of all sequences

{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1}\cdot x_{j}^{2\nu}<\infty.

In the sequel, we consider the countable product
\mu
of the standard
normal distribution
\mu_{0}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Assume that
(H1)
is satisfied.
Then we have

{\mathfrak{X}}\subseteq\bigcap_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ell^{2\nu}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\nu}^{-1})\subsetneq{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}.

Assume that
(H1)
and
(H3)
are satisfied.
Then we have

\mu({\mathfrak{X}})=1

and

\ell^{\infty}\subsetneq{\mathfrak{X}}.

Proof.

Put
\ell^{2\nu}:=\ell^{2\nu}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\nu}^{-1})
,
and observe that already
\ell^{2}\subsetneq{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
We prove the remaining part of the first statement by induction.

Since
h_{1}(x_{j})=x_{j}
and

\alpha_{1,j}^{-1}\cdot|h_{1}(x_{j})|^{2}\leq k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},x_{j})
, we
obtain
{\mathfrak{X}}\subseteq\ell^{2}
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
, let
\nu>1
, and
assume that
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\ell^{2\kappa}
holds for all

1\leq\kappa<\nu
.
Since
h_{\nu}
is either an even or an odd polynomial,
h_{\nu}^{2}
is
an even polynomial of degree
2\nu
and can thus be
written as

h^{2}_{\nu}(x)=\sum^{\nu}_{\kappa=0}\beta_{\kappa}\cdot x^{2\kappa}

with suitable
\beta_{\kappa}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, where
\beta_{\nu}\neq 0
.
Consequently,

x^{2\nu}=1/\beta_{\nu}\cdot\left(|h_{\nu}(x)|^{2}-\sum_{\kappa=0}^{\nu-1}\beta_{\kappa}\cdot x^{2\kappa}\right).

Due to the second condition in (H1) and our induction hypothesis
we have for all
1\leq\kappa<\nu

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha^{-1}_{\nu,j}\cdot x^{2\kappa}_{j}\leq\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha^{-1}_{\kappa,j}\cdot x^{2\kappa}_{j}<\infty.

Furthermore,

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1}\cdot|h_{\nu}(x_{j})|^{2}\leq\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},x_{j})<\infty.

Altogether this yields

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha^{-1}_{\nu,j}\cdot x^{2\nu}_{j}<\infty,

implying that
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\ell^{2\nu}
,
which proves the first statement.

Since

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}}k_{j}^{*}(x_{j},x_{j})\,d\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1},

we get

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}k_{j}^{*}(x_{j},x_{j})\,d\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})<\infty

from (H3) using the monotone convergence theorem. Hence

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}k_{j}^{*}(x_{j},x_{j})<\infty
holds
\mu
-a.e., i.e.,

\mu({\mathfrak{X}})=1
.
Combine Cramér’s inequality, see Lemma
3.1
,
and (H3) to conclude that
\ell^{\infty}\subseteq{\mathfrak{X}}
.
Finally,
\ell^{\infty}\neq{\mathfrak{X}}
, since
\mu(\ell^{\infty})=0
.
∎

Recall that
{\boldsymbol{N}}
denotes the set of all sequences

{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:=(\nu_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
such that

\{j\in{\mathbb{N}}\colon\nu_{j}\neq 0\}
is finite.
For
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}
we set

h_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}h_{\nu_{j}}(x_{j})

for
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
, which is well-defined since
h_{0}=1
. Moreover,
we set

\alpha_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{\nu_{j},j},

which is well-defined if (H2) is assumed.

In addition to the original Fourier weights we also consider

\alpha_{\nu,j}^{\prime}:=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $\nu=0$,}\\
\alpha_{\nu,j},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and we use
\alpha^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
,
K^{\prime}
, and
k_{j}^{\prime}
to denote the
corresponding products and the corresponding reproducing kernels,
respectively.
The counterpart to (H2) is trivially satisfied
for the new Fourier weights, and the counterparts to
(H1) and (H3)
follow from the respective properties of the
original Fourier weights.

Lemma 3.11
.

Assume that
(H2)
is satisfied. Then we have

c_{\min}:=\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}}\frac{\alpha_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}{\alpha^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\min(\alpha_{0,j},1)>0

and

Proof.

Use (H2) to conclude that

\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}}\frac{\alpha_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}{\alpha^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}=\lim_{J\to\infty}\biggl(\prod_{j=1}^{J}\min(\alpha_{0,j},1)\cdot\prod_{j=J+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{0,j}\biggr)=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\min(\alpha_{0,j},1)>0.

The second statement is verified analogously.
∎

Lemma 3.12

∎

Remark 3.14
.

Assume that (H1)–(H3) are satisfied.
For each
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}k^{\ast}_{j}(x,y)\,d\mu_{0}(y)=0

for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, cf. Remark
2.2
. This property
induces a function space decomposition of the tensor product
space
H(K)
of ANOVA-type, cf.
Baldeaux and Gnewuch 2014
. Let us make
this more precise:
Due to (
3.16
) we may write
H(K)
as orthogonal sum

H(K)=\bigoplus_{{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}}H(c_{{\mathfrak{u}}}\cdot k^{\ast}_{{\mathfrak{u}}}).

Note that due to
c_{{\mathfrak{u}}}>0
we have

H(c_{{\mathfrak{u}}}\cdot k^{\ast}_{{\mathfrak{u}}})=H(k^{\ast}_{{\mathfrak{u}}})

as vector spaces for all
{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}
.
Hence each function
f\in H(K)
can be uniquely represented as

f=\sum_{{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}}f_{{\mathfrak{u}}},

where
f_{{\mathfrak{u}}}\in H(k^{\ast}_{{\mathfrak{u}}})
for each
{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}
,
and its norm is given by

\|f\|^{2}_{H(K)}=\sum_{{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}}\|f_{{\mathfrak{u}}}\|^{2}_{H(c_{{\mathfrak{u}}}\cdot k^{\ast}_{{\mathfrak{u}}})}=\sum_{{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}}c_{{\mathfrak{u}}}^{-1}\cdot\|f_{{\mathfrak{u}}}\|^{2}_{H(k^{\ast}_{{\mathfrak{u}}})}.

This function decomposition is the infinite-dimensional

ANOVA-decomposition
of
f
.
More precisely, we obtain

f_{\emptyset}=\int_{{\mathfrak{X}}}f\,d\mu

and, recursively, for arbitrary
\emptyset\neq{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}

\phantom{\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}},}f_{{\mathfrak{u}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbb{N}}\setminus{\mathfrak{u}}}}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{{\mathfrak{u}}},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{{\mathbb{N}}\setminus{\mathfrak{u}}})\,d\mu_{0}^{{\mathbb{N}}\setminus{\mathfrak{u}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{{\mathbb{N}}\setminus{\mathfrak{u}}})-\sum_{{\mathfrak{v}}\subsetneq{\mathfrak{u}}}f_{{\mathfrak{v}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}},

and

\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\sum_{{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}}\|f_{{\mathfrak{u}}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}

as well as

\sigma^{2}(f)=\sum_{\emptyset\neq{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}}\sigma^{2}(f_{{\mathfrak{u}}}),

where
\sigma^{2}(\cdot)
denotes the variance.
In particular, for
f\in H(K)
the ANOVA-components
f_{{\mathfrak{u}}}

with
{\mathfrak{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}
are orthogonal in
H(K)
as well as in
L^{2}(\mu)
,
and each function
f_{{\mathfrak{u}}}
depends only on the variables
x_{j}

with
j\in{\mathfrak{u}}
.

Remark 3.15
.

Infinite tensor products
K
of reproducing kernels
k_{j}\colon D\times D\to{\mathbb{R}}
provide a convenient setting for the study of
computational problems for functions of infinitely many variables,
and most often the whole Cartesian product
D^{\mathbb{N}}
is the natural
domain for the kernel
K
.
The complexity of problems of this kind has
first been analyzed in

Hickernell and Wang 2001
,
Hickernell et al. 2010
,
Kuo et al. 2010
,
and we refer to, e.g.,
Gnewuch et al. 2019

for recent results and references.
So far two different types of spaces of functions of infinitely many
variables have been studied: First,
weighted spaces
, where the
weights model the importance of different groups of variables. This
kind of weights was first introduced in
Sloan and Woźniakowski 1998
.
Second,
spaces of increasing smoothness
based on Fourier weights,
which is the approach we follow in this paper. The effect of increasing
smoothness on the tractability of multivariate problems was first studied
in
Papageorgiou and Woźniakowski 2010
.

Assume that
\alpha_{0,j}=1
for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Due to the representation (
3.16
) the kernel
K
is the superposition
of finite tensor products of reproducing kernels
k_{j}^{*}
.
In such a setting the

multivariate decomposition method

has been established as a powerful generic algorithm for
integration and
L^{2}
-approximation, if additionally
all of the
k_{j}^{*}
are weighted anchored kernels,
which leads to a so-called anchored function space decomposition.
By definition,
k_{j}^{*}
is
anchored
at
x\in D
if

k_{j}^{*}(x,x)=0.

We refer to the pioneering papers
Kuo et al. 2010
and
Plaskota and Wasilkowski 2011
,
and to
Gilbert et al. 2018
for a recent contribution.

Observe, however, that in the present setting of Hermite spaces
none of the kernels
k_{j}^{*}
is anchored at any point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
since
h_{1}(x)=x
for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
h_{2}(0)\neq 0
.
As explained in Remark
3.14
, instead of having an
anchored function space decomposition,
H(K)
has an
ANOVA-decomposition. This prohibits the
application of the standard error analysis of the
multivariate decomposition method developed in
Plaskota and Wasilkowski 2011
for
integration and in
Wasilkowski 2012
for
L^{2}
-approximation and of standard
arguments to derive lower bounds for deterministic algorithms
on
H(K)
.

In the ANOVA setting the multivariate
decomposition method has been analyzed directly in
Dick and Gnewuch 2014

for integration in the randomized setting
and
indirectly via suitable embedding theorems in
Gnewuch et al. 2017
;
Gnewuch et al. 2019
for integration and
L^{2}
-approximation in the randomized and
the deterministic setting.

While
Dick and Gnewuch 2014
and
Gnewuch et al. 2017
treat weighted spaces
H(K)

and allow for domains
{\mathfrak{X}}
of
K
that are proper subsets
of
D^{\mathbb{N}}
, the paper

Gnewuch et al. 2019
studies spaces of increasing smoothness whose kernels
are defined on the whole Cartesian product
D^{\mathbb{N}}
. It is
important to note that Hermite spaces
H(K)
are never spaces of
functions on the domain
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
, see Proposition
3.10
.
In a forthcoming paper we will develop the approach
from
Gnewuch et al. 2019
further and apply it, in particular,
to Hermite spaces
H(K)
.

Remark 3.16
.

Let

Lemma 3.17
.

In both cases,
(PG)
and
(EG)
, we have

(H1)–(H3).

Proof.

Due to the considerations in Examples
3.5
and
3.6
,
respectively, (H1) is satisfied in both cases, and
(H2) is satisfied by assumption.

For proving (H3) we use

\sum_{k\geq k_{0}}k^{-\tau}\leq k_{0}^{-\tau}+\int_{k_{0}}^{\infty}k^{-\tau}\,d\tau=k_{0}^{-\tau}\cdot\Big(1+\frac{k_{0}}{\tau-1}\Big)

for
\tau>1
and
k_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Consider the case (PG). Here we choose
j_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

r_{j}\geq 2
for every
j\geq j_{0}
. It follows that

\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1}=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}(\nu+1)^{-r_{j}}\leq 2^{-r_{j}}\cdot 3

for
j\geq j_{0}
, which yields (H3), as claimed.

Consider the case (EG).
Here we put
b:=\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}b_{j}>0
and
choose
j_{0}\geq 2
and

c>0
such that
2^{r_{j}}\geq j^{c}

for every
j\geq j_{0}
, see Remark
3.16
. It follows that

\alpha_{\nu,j}=2^{r_{j}\cdot\nu^{b_{j}}}\geq 2^{r_{j}\cdot\nu^{b}}\geq j^{c\cdot\nu^{b}}

for
j\geq j_{0}
and
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We choose
\nu_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such
that
c\cdot\nu_{0}^{b}>1
. For
\nu\geq\nu_{0}
this yields

\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1}\leq j_{0}^{-c\cdot\nu^{b}}\cdot\Big(1+\frac{j_{0}}{c\cdot\nu_{0}^{b}-1}\Big),

while

\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{-1}\leq\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}2^{-r_{j}}<\infty

for

. Since

\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}j_{0}^{-c\cdot\nu^{b}}<\infty,

we obtain (H3), as claimed.
∎

According to Proposition
3.10
and Lemma
3.17
,
the maximal domain
{\mathfrak{X}}
satisfies

{\mathfrak{X}}\subseteq\bigcap_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ell^{2\nu}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\nu}^{-1})\subseteq\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}^{-1})

in both cases, (PG) and (EG).

Proposition 3.18
.

In the case
(EG)
with exponential growth, i.e., if

b_{j}\geq 1
for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

{\mathfrak{X}}=\bigcap_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ell^{2\nu}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\nu}^{-1})=\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}^{-1}).

Proof.

It suffices to show that

\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}^{-1})\subseteq{\mathfrak{X}}.

Since
{\mathfrak{X}}
becomes larger if we increase the
b_{j}
,
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and

\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}^{-1})
is independent of the choice of the
b_{j}
,
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
we actually only need to consider the case where

b_{j}=1
for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}^{-1})
. To prove
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
it suffices to
prove

(3.22)

\sum_{j\geq j_{1}}k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},x_{j})<\infty

for some
j_{1}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
For all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
we put
z_{j}:=\max(1,|x_{j}|)
. Due to
Lemma
3.2
there exists a
C>0
such that

|h_{\nu}(x_{j})|^{2}\leq C^{\nu}|z_{j}|^{2\nu}

for all
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Therefore we obtain, for all
j_{1}\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq j_{1}}k^{*}_{j}(x_{j},x_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq j_{1}}\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha^{-1}_{\nu,j}\cdot|h_{\nu}(x_{j})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\geq j_{1}}\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{-r_{j}\,\nu}C^{\nu}\cdot|z_{j}|^{2\,\nu}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Bigg(C\sum_{j\geq j_{1}}2^{-r_{j}}\cdot|z_{j}|^{2}\Bigg)^{\nu}.

Since
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}^{-1})

and (
3.21
) is satisfied,
we have

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{-r_{j}}|z_{j}|^{2}<\infty.

Hence we may choose
j_{1}
to be large enough to yield

\sum_{j\geq j_{1}}2^{-r_{j}}|z_{j}|^{2}<C^{-1}.

This choice obviously ensures (
3.22
).
∎

Proposition 3.19
.

Consider the case
(PG)
or the case
(EG)
with sub-exponential
growth, i.e.,
\limsup_{j\to\infty}b_{j}<1
.
If
\hat{r}>1/\ln(2)
then we have

\bigcap_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ell^{2\nu}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\nu}^{-1})\subsetneq\bigcap_{\nu=1}^{\nu_{0}}\ell^{2\nu}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\nu}^{-1})

for every
\nu_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

Since
\hat{r}>1/\ln(2)
, we may
choose
0<\varepsilon<1
and
j_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

2^{r_{j}}\geq j^{1+\varepsilon}

for
j\geq j_{0}
. In the case (EG) with sub-exponential growth we
may furthermore assume

b_{j}\leq 1-\varepsilon

for
j\geq j_{0}
.
Moreover, we choose
1<a<1+\varepsilon
, and we put

\delta:=1-a/(1+\varepsilon)>0
. Let
\nu_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with

x_{j}^{2\nu_{0}}=2^{r_{j}}\cdot j^{-a}

we show that

(3.23)

{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\bigcap_{\nu=1}^{\nu_{0}}\ell^{2\nu}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\nu}^{-1}),

but

(3.24)

{\boldsymbol{x}}\not\in\ell^{2k\nu_{0}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k\nu_{0}}^{-1})

for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
sufficiently large.

Let
j\geq j_{0}
and
1\leq\nu\leq\nu_{0}
.
Since
|x_{j}|\geq 1
and
\alpha_{\nu,j}\geq 2^{r_{j}}
, we obtain

Consequently, we have (

).

Let
j\geq j_{0}
and

.
Note that

x_{j}^{2k\nu_{0}}=2^{r_{j}k}\cdot j^{-ak}\geq 2^{r_{j}k\delta}.

Consider the case (EG) with sub-exponential growth. Here we have

For every

with

) in this case.

this implies

∎

simple characterization of this domain.

4.
Gaussian Spaces

Now we study Hilbert spaces of Gaussian
kernels, which we shortly address as Gaussian spaces.
As before, we take
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}}
.

4.1.
Functions of a Single Variable

At first we consider spaces of univariate functions.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\sigma>0
.
The reproducing kernel

\ell_{\sigma}
given by

\phantom{\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}},}\ell_{\sigma}(x,y):=\exp\left(-\sigma^{2}\cdot(x-y)^{2}\right),\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}},

is called a
Gaussian kernel

and the Hilbert space
H(\ell_{\sigma})
is called a
Gaussian space
of functions of a single real variable with
shape parameter

\sigma
.

We collect some facts about the spaces
H(\ell_{\sigma})
from

Steinwart et al. 2006
,
see also
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
.
Each function
f\in H(\ell_{\sigma})
is the real part of
an entire function
g
restricted to the real line, where
g
belongs
to the complex reproducing kernel Hilbert space with kernel
\ell_{\sigma}

extended to
{\mathbb{C}}
in the obvious way. In particular, if
f
is constant
on any open non-empty interval, then
f
is the zero function.
Moreover, the functions

\phantom{\qquad\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}},}e_{\nu,\sigma}(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu!}}\,\bigl(\sqrt{2}\sigma\bigr)^{\nu}x^{\nu}\cdot\ell_{\sigma}(x,0),\qquad\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}},

with
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
form an orthonormal basis of
H(\ell_{\sigma})
.
It is easily verified that

(4.1)

\ell_{\sigma}(x,y)=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}e_{\nu,\sigma}(x)\cdot e_{\nu,\sigma}(y)

with absolute convergence for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
If
0<\sigma<\sigma^{\prime}
,
then
H(\ell_{\sigma})\subsetneq H(\ell_{\sigma^{\prime}})

with a non-compact continuous embedding of norm

\sqrt{\sigma^{\prime}/\sigma}
.
Observe that the identity (
4.1
) for the Gaussian kernel
is reminiscent of
definition (
3.11
) of the Hermite kernel.
Based on Mehler’s formula instead
of (
4.1
), a characterization of
H(\ell_{\sigma})
as an

L^{2}
-subspace in the sense of Proposition
2.1
will be
given in Section
5.1
.

4.2.
Functions of Infinitely Many Variables

Gaussian spaces of functions of infinitely many real variables are
based on a sequence
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}:=(\sigma_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of shape parameters

\sigma_{j}>0
;
for the corresponding Gaussian kernels and basis functions in
the univariate case we use the
short hands
\ell_{j}:=\ell_{\sigma_{j}}
and
e_{\nu,j}:=e_{\nu,\sigma_{j}}
.

We proceed similar to Section
3.2
, but we will
encounter some important differences along the way.
First of all,
1\not\in H(\ell_{j})
.
In each of the spaces
H(\ell_{j})
we therefore choose the unit vector

v_{j}:=e_{0,j},

i.e.,
v_{j}(x)=\exp(-\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot x^{2})
, and we
study the corresponding incomplete tensor product

G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}:=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(H(\ell_{j})\right)^{(v_{j})}.

We will employ Theorem
A.6
again to identify the space
G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}

with a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of functions on a suitable
subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
. In the present case
we even have the convergence of the partial products

\prod_{j=1}^{J}\ell_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})
as
J\to\infty

for all
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Even more, the products
\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ell_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})
converge
in the sense of Section
A.1
, since
\ell_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})\in\left]0,1\right]
. Hence we may study the tensor product kernel

L
given by

(4.2)

\phantom{\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}.}L({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\ell_{j}(x_{j},y_{j}),\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}.

One might expect that
G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
may be identified
with
H(L)
in a canonical way, analogously to the result
for Hermite spaces.

For any reproducing kernel
M
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
and any
non-empty set
{\mathfrak{Y}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
we use
M_{\mathfrak{Y}}
to denote the
restriction
M|_{{\mathfrak{Y}}\times{\mathfrak{Y}}}
.

The elementary tensors

e_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}e_{\nu_{j},j}

with
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}
form an orthonormal basis of
G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
,
see Remark
A.4
.
We will identify the tensor product space
G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
with
a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
H(L_{\mathfrak{Y}})

for a suitable non-empty domain
{\mathfrak{Y}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
via the
linear mapping

\Phi\colon G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathfrak{Y}}},

given by

(4.3)

\Phi f({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}}\langle f,e_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\rangle_{G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}e_{\nu_{j},j}(x_{j})

for
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{Y}}
, cf. Theorem
A.6
. More precisely,
\Phi

should, of course, be well-defined and should induce an isometric
isomorphism between
G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
and
H(L_{\mathfrak{Y}})
.
It turns out, in particular, that we cannot achieve the latter
goal with
{\mathfrak{Y}}={\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
; instead, as shown below,
{\mathfrak{Y}}={\mathfrak{X}}
with

(4.4)

{\mathfrak{X}}:=\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{2})

is the proper choice. By definition,

{\mathfrak{X}}
consists of all sequences
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
such that

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sigma^{2}_{j}\cdot x_{j}^{2}<\infty.

Obviously,

L({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\begin{cases}\exp\bigl(-\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot(x_{j}-y_{j})^{2}\bigr),&\text{if ${\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}$,}\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for all
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\ell_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})-1|<\infty\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}.

In particular,

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|v_{j}(x_{j})-1|<\infty\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}.

Proof.

Since
\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ell_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})
converges and

\ell_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})\neq 0
for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the convergence of

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\ell_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})-1|
is equivalent to

L({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\neq 0
, i.e., to
{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
.
The second statement is just a special case of the first
statement, namely with
{\boldsymbol{y}}:=0
.
∎

Lemma
4.2
and Theorem
A.6
immediately imply that
\Phi

induces an isometric isomorphism between the incomplete tensor
product
G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
and the reproducing kernel Hilbert space

H(L_{\mathfrak{X}})
. In this sense
H(L_{\mathfrak{X}})

is the
incomplete tensor product of the Gaussian
spaces
H(\ell_{j})
based on the unit vectors
v_{j}=e_{0,j}
for

j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}:=(\sigma_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a a sequence of shape parameters
\sigma_{j}>0
.
The reproducing kernel
L_{\mathfrak{X}}
defined by (
4.2
) on the domain

{\mathfrak{X}}
given by (
4.4
)
is called a
Gaussian kernel
and the Hilbert space
H(L_{\mathfrak{X}})
is called a
Gaussian space
of functions of infinitely many real variables.

Larger domains
{\mathfrak{Y}}\supsetneq{\mathfrak{X}}
in the definition
of
\Phi
in (
4.3
) are discussed in the sequel.
The definition of
\tilde{L}
in the following lemma is the
counterpart to the representation (
4.1
) of
\ell_{j}
in the
univariate case. Moreover, the lemma ensures that
\Phi
is well-defined
for any choice of
{\mathfrak{Y}}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
.
The series

\tilde{L}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}e_{\nu_{j},j}(x_{j})\cdot e_{\nu_{j},j}(y_{j})

is absolutely convergent, and

\tilde{L}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\begin{cases}L({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}),&\text{if ${\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}$,}\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and let
{\boldsymbol{N}}_{n}
denote the set of all
sequences
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}
with
\nu_{j}=0
for every
j>n
. Since
(
4.1
) yields

\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}|e_{\nu,j}(x)|^{2}=1
for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}

and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we obtain

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|e_{\nu_{j},j}(x_{j})|^{2}=1.

It follows that

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}|e_{\nu_{j},j}(x_{j})|^{2}=\prod^{\infty}_{j=n+1}|e_{0,j}(x_{j})|^{2}=\exp\biggl(-2\sum^{\infty}_{j=n+1}\sigma_{j}^{2}x_{j}^{2}\biggr),

and hereby

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}|e_{\nu_{j},j}(x_{j})|^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}|e_{\nu_{j},j}(x_{j})|^{2}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if ${\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}$,}\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

This yields the absolute convergence, as claimed, and

\tilde{L}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=0
if
{\boldsymbol{x}}\not\in{\mathfrak{X}}
or
{\boldsymbol{y}}\not\in{\mathfrak{X}}
.

Next we verify
\tilde{L}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=L({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
for

{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
. In the latter case

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}e_{\nu_{j},j}(x_{j})\cdot e_{\nu_{j},j}(y_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\ell_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})\cdot\exp\biggl(-\sum^{\infty}_{j=n+1}\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot(x_{j}^{2}+y_{j}^{2})\biggr).

In the limit
n\to\infty
we obtain the claim.
∎

Because of its series representation, the function

\tilde{L}
is a reproducing kernel on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Since
L-\tilde{L}=L\cdot 1_{{\mathfrak{Z}}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}}

with
{\mathfrak{Z}}:={\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\setminus{\mathfrak{X}}
, the function
L-\tilde{L}
is a
reproducing kernel on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
, too.

Proposition 4.5
.

Assume that

{\mathfrak{X}}\subsetneq{\mathfrak{Y}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}.

Then

(i)

H(L_{\mathfrak{Y}})
is the orthogonal sum of the closed, proper subspaces

H(\tilde{L}_{\mathfrak{Y}})
and
H(L_{\mathfrak{Y}}-\tilde{L}_{\mathfrak{Y}})
,

(ii)

H(\tilde{L}_{\mathfrak{Y}})=\{f\in H(L_{\mathfrak{Y}})\colon f_{{\mathfrak{Y}}\setminus{\mathfrak{X}}}=0\}
,

(iii)

\Phi
is an isometric isomorphism between
G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
and

H(\tilde{L}_{\mathfrak{Y}})
.

Proof.

For
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{Y}}\setminus{\mathfrak{X}}
we have
\tilde{L}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}})=0

and
(L-\tilde{L})({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}})=1
,
while
\tilde{L}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}})=1
and
(L-\tilde{L})({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}})=0
for

{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
, see Lemma
4.4
.
It follows that
H(\tilde{L}_{\mathfrak{Y}})
and
H(L_{\mathfrak{Y}}-\tilde{L}_{\mathfrak{Y}})
are
non-trivial, but have a trivial intersection. This yields (i) and
(ii).

Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{Y}}\setminus{\mathfrak{X}}
. Note that
\tilde{L}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}})=0
is
equivalent to

\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}e_{\nu_{j},j}(x_{j})=0

for every
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in{\boldsymbol{N}}
.
It follows that
\Phi f({\boldsymbol{x}})=0
for every
f\in G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
.
We conclude that
G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
is identified with

H(\tilde{L}_{\mathfrak{Y}})
via
\Phi
, as claimed in (iii).
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Lemma
4.4
and Proposition
4.5

show that the proper choice of domain of the reproducing kernel

L
defined in (
4.2
) is
{\mathfrak{X}}:=\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{2})
, since this
is the maximal domain such that the canonical mapping

\Phi\colon G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathfrak{X}}
defined in (
4.3
) defines an
isometric isomorphism between
G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
and

\Phi(G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})})=H(L_{\mathfrak{X}})=H(\tilde{L}_{\mathfrak{X}})
.
If we consider strictly larger domains
{\mathfrak{Y}}
, then we still have

\Phi(G^{({\boldsymbol{v}})})=H(\tilde{L}_{\mathfrak{Y}})
, but
H(\tilde{L}_{\mathfrak{Y}})\neq H(L_{\mathfrak{Y}})
.

5.
Isomorphisms between Gaussian Spaces and Hermite Spaces

5.1.
Functions of a Single Variable

It is known that the Gaussian kernel
\ell_{\sigma}
with shape parameter

\sigma>0
can be represented in terms of the Hermite polynomials

h_{\nu}
,
see, e.g.,
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
and
Fasshauer et al. 2012
.
A starting point to derive such a representation is Mehler’s formula

\exp\left(\frac{1}{1-\beta^{2}}\cdot\left(\beta\cdot xy-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta^{2}\cdot(x^{2}+y^{2})\right)\right)=m(x,y)

with

m(x,y):=\left(1-\beta^{2}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\beta^{\nu}\cdot h_{\nu}(x)\cdot h_{\nu}(y),

which holds for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\beta\in{]0,1[}
,
see
Erdélyi et al. 1953
. Note that

(5.1)

\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\beta^{\nu}\cdot|h_{\nu}(x)|^{2}<\infty

for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
already follows from Cramér’s inequality,
see Lemma
3.1
.
We add that Mehler’s formula also yields a closed
form representation of Hermite kernels in the case (EG) with
b=1
,
see
Irrgeher and Leobacher 2015
.

Using

\beta\cdot xy-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta^{2}\cdot(x^{2}+y^{2})=-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta\cdot(x-y)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(1-\beta)\cdot(x^{2}+y^{2})

and introducing a scaling parameter
c>0
,
Mehler’s formula may be rewritten as

\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}\beta}{2(1-\beta^{2})}\cdot(x-y)^{2}\right)=\exp\left(-\tau\cdot(x^{2}+y^{2})\right)\cdot m(cx,cy)

with

\tau:=\frac{c^{2}\beta}{2(1+\beta)}.

For
f\in L^{2}(\mu_{0})
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
we define

qf(x):=c^{1/2}\cdot\exp(-\tau\cdot x^{2})\cdot f(cx).

If

(5.2)

\frac{c^{2}\beta}{2(1-\beta^{2})}=\sigma^{2}

then

\ell_{\sigma}(x,y)=\frac{\left(1-\beta^{2}\right)^{1/2}}{c}\cdot\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\beta^{\nu}\cdot qh_{\nu}(x)\cdot qh_{\nu}(y)

for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The mapping
q
is an isometric isomorphism on
L^{2}(\mu_{0})
if and
only if

(5.3)

\tau=\frac{c^{2}-1}{4}.

Proof.

Let
t\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be given by
t(x):=cx
.
The image measure
t\mu_{0}
of
\mu_{0}
with respect to
t
is
the normal distribution with zero mean and variance
c^{2}
.
The density of
t\mu_{0}
with respect to
\mu_{0}

is therefore given by the density ratio

\varphi(x):=c^{-1}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2c^{2}}\right)\cdot\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)=c^{-1}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c^{2}-1}{2c^{2}}\cdot x^{2}\right).

For
f\in L^{2}(\mu_{0})
this yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|qf(x)|^{2}\,d\mu_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=c\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(-2\tau/c^{2}\cdot|t(x)|^{2}\right)\cdot|f(t(x))|^{2}\,d\mu_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=c\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\cdot\exp\left(-2\tau/c^{2}\cdot|x|^{2}\right)\cdot|f(x)|^{2}\,d\mu_{0}(x).

We conclude that
q
is an isometry on
L^{2}(\mu_{0})
if and only if

\frac{c^{2}-1}{2c^{2}}=\frac{2\tau}{c^{2}},

which is equivalent to (
5.3
).
Assume that (
5.3
) is satisfied. It follows that

qf(x)=\left|\varphi(t(x))\right|^{-1/2}\cdot f(t(x)).

For
g\in L^{2}(\mu_{0})
and
f(x):=\left|\varphi(x)\right|^{1/2}\cdot g\left(t^{-1}(x)\right)
we obtain
f\in L^{2}(\mu_{0})
as well
as
qf=g
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

For every
\sigma>0
the unique solution

(\beta,c)\in{]0,1[}\times{]0,\infty[}
of
(
5.2
) and (
5.3
) is given by

(5.4)

c=\left(1+8\sigma^{2}\right)^{1/4}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta=1-\frac{2}{1+c^{2}}.

Proof.

Let
(\beta,c)\in{]0,1[}\times{]0,\infty[}
satisfy
(
5.2
) and (
5.3
).
From (
5.3
) we obtain

c^{2}=\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta},

and together with (
5.2
) this yields

\frac{\beta}{2(1-\beta)^{2}}=\sigma^{2}.

Since
\beta\mapsto\beta/(1-\beta)^{2}
is strictly increasing
on
{]0,1[}
, we conclude that the first component
\beta
of
the solution of (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) is uniquely determined.
Due to (
5.2
) the same holds true for the second component
c
.

Conversely, it is straightforward to verify that
c
and
\beta

according to (
5.4
) belong to
{]0,\infty[}
and
{]0,1[}
,
respectively, and satisfy (
5.2
) and (
5.3
).
∎

In the sequel we assume that
c
and
\beta
are chosen according
to (
5.4
), and we introduce the Fourier weights

\phantom{\qquad\quad\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.}\alpha_{\nu}:=\frac{1}{(1-\beta)\cdot\beta^{\nu}},\qquad\quad\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

Since

\frac{\left(1-\beta^{2}\right)^{1/2}}{c}=1-\beta,

we obtain the representation

(5.5)

\phantom{\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}},}\ell_{\sigma}(x,y)=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\cdot qh_{\nu}(x)\cdot qh_{\nu}(y),\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}},

of the Gaussian kernel
\ell_{\sigma}
from Mehler’s formula and
Lemma
5.2
. Cf.
Fasshauer et al. 2012
,
who consider the normal distribution with zero mean and
variance
1/2
, instead of the standard normal distribution
\mu_{0}
,
together with unnormalized Hermite polynomials.

Due to Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
5.2
the functions
qh_{\nu}
with

\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mu_{0})
, and
(
5.1
) corresponds to (
2.2
). We conclude that
Proposition
2.1
,
with the orthonormal basis
(qh_{\nu})_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
, provides a
characterization of
H(\ell_{\sigma})
as a linear
subspace of
L^{2}(\mu_{0})
.

We relate the Gaussian space
H(\ell_{\sigma})
to the Hermite space

H(k_{\sigma})
with

(5.6)

\phantom{\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}.}k_{\sigma}(x,y):=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\cdot h_{\nu}(x)\cdot h_{\nu}(y),\qquad\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Proposition 5.3
.

The mapping
q|_{H(k_{\sigma})}
is
an isometric isomorphism between
H(k_{\sigma})
and
H(\ell_{\sigma})
.

Proof.

We use Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
5.2
as well as
(
5.5
) and Proposition
2.1
.
Let
c_{\nu}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, satisfy

\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu}\cdot|c_{\nu}|^{2}<\infty
.
Then
f:=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}c_{\nu}\cdot h_{\nu}
is in
H(k_{\sigma})
,
and the sum converges in
L^{2}(\mu_{0})
and absolutely at every
point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Observe that
the pointwise convergence is preserved by
q
, and therefore

qf(x)=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}c_{\nu}\cdot qh_{\nu}(x)
.
Moreover,
qf=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}c_{\nu}\cdot qh_{\nu}
with
convergence in
L^{2}(\mu_{0})
. It follows that
qf\in H(\ell_{\sigma})

and
\|f\|_{H(k_{\sigma})}=\|qf\|_{H(\ell_{\sigma})}
.
Identity (
2.3
), applied to
H(k_{\sigma})
as well as to

H(\ell_{\sigma})
, yields that
q(H(k_{\sigma}))=H(\ell_{\sigma})
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

We combine Proposition
2.1
,
Lemma
5.1
, Lemma
5.2
, and
Proposition
5.3

to conclude that
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H(k_{\sigma})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\mu_{0})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H(\ell_{\sigma})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\mu_{0})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q|_{H(k_{\sigma})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}

is a commutative diagram of bounded linear operators with isometric
isomorphisms in the vertical direction.

We close this subsection by commenting on inclusion relations
between Gaussian spaces and Hermite spaces.

Remark 5.5
.

Consider an arbitrary Hermite kernel
k
and an arbitrary Gaussian
kernel
\ell_{\sigma}
. Since

|f(x)-f(y)|^{2}\leq 2\,\|f\|^{2}_{H(\ell_{\sigma})}\cdot(1-\ell_{\sigma}(x,y))

for all
f\in H(\ell_{\sigma})
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the functions from

H(\ell_{\sigma})
are bounded. Therefore the space
H(\ell_{\sigma})

does not contain any non-trivial polynomial, while
H(k)
obviously
contains the whole space
\Pi
of polynomials.
In particular,

H(k)\not\subseteq H(\ell_{\sigma}).

Observe that
\ell_{\sigma}
is translation-invariant.
General conditions for translation-invariant kernels

\ell
that guarantee
H(\ell)\cap\Pi=\{0\}

have recently been established in
Dette and Zhigljavsky 2021
and
Karvonen 2021
.

Remark 5.6
.

We briefly discuss inclusions of Gaussian spaces
in Hermite spaces. More precisely, we consider Hermite spaces

H(k)
with Fourier weights
\alpha_{\nu}:=\beta^{-\nu}
for
any
\beta\in{]0,1[}
.
The following holds true for every
\sigma>0
.
There exist
0<\beta^{(1)}\leq\beta^{(2)}<1
such that

H(\ell_{\sigma})\not\subseteq H(k)\qquad\text{if $0<\beta<\beta^{(1)}$}

and

H(\ell_{\sigma})\subsetneq H(k)\qquad\text{if $\beta^{(2)}<\beta<1$.}

In particular, for
\beta
according to (
5.4
), where

H(k)=H(k_{\sigma})
as vector spaces and

q|_{H(k_{\sigma})}
is an isometric isomorphism between
H(k_{\sigma})
and

H(\ell_{\sigma})
,
we have
\beta<\beta^{(1)}
, and therefore

H(\ell_{\sigma})\not\subseteq H(k_{\sigma}).

So far, our analysis only yields values
\beta^{(1)}<\beta^{(2)}
; therefore we skip the proof.

5.2.
Functions of Infinitely Many Variables

As in Section
4.2
,
we consider again a sequence
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
of shape parameters

\sigma_{j}>0
and the corresponding sequence of Gaussian
kernels
\ell_{j}
. We study Gaussian spaces and Hermite spaces
of functions of infinitely many variables with Fourier weights
given by

\phantom{\qquad\quad\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\ j\in{\mathbb{N}},}\alpha_{\nu,j}:=\frac{1}{(1-\beta_{j})\cdot\beta^{\nu}_{j}},\qquad\quad\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\ j\in{\mathbb{N}},

where

c_{j}:=\left(1+8\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)^{1/4}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta_{j}:=1-\frac{2}{1+c_{j}^{2}}\,,

cf. Lemma
5.2
,
under the assumption

(5.7)

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sigma^{2}_{j}<\infty.

Note that (
5.7
) is equivalent to

(5.8)

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|c_{j}-1|<\infty.

As in Section
4.2
, we consider the domain
{\mathfrak{X}}:=\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{2})
.

We will establish the counterpart to Remark
5.4

via tensorization and identification of the corresponding
incomplete tensor product spaces with the reproducing kernel Hilbert
spaces
H(K)
and
H(L_{\mathfrak{X}})
or the space
L^{2}(\mu)
,
respectively.

Lemma 5.7
.

Assume that (
5.7
) is satisfied. Then we have

(H1)
–
(H3)
, and
{\mathfrak{X}}
is the maximal domain of the kernel
K

and satisfies
\mu({\mathfrak{X}})=1
.

Proof.

From (
5.7
) we obtain
the weak asymptotics

\alpha^{-1}_{1,j}=(1-\beta_{j})\cdot\beta_{j}\asymp\beta_{j}\asymp\sigma^{2}_{j},

the convergence
\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\beta_{j}<\infty
,
and
\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}_{1})=\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{2})
for

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}_{1}:=(\alpha_{1,j}^{-1})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

Let
r_{j}>0
be defined by
\beta_{j}=2^{-r_{j}}
.
Note that the Fourier weights

\phantom{\qquad\quad\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\ j\in{\mathbb{N}},}\beta_{j}^{-\nu}=2^{r_{j}\cdot\nu},\qquad\quad\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\ j\in{\mathbb{N}},

are of the form (EG) with

Appendix A
Countable Tensor Products of Hilbert Spaces

Tensor products of arbitrary families of Hilbert spaces have been
introduced (actually named complete direct products) and thoroughly
studied in
von Neumann 1939
. In the present paper we are interested in
countably infinite tensor products, and thus we consider a sequence

(H_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of Hilbert spaces
H_{j}\neq\{0\}
over the same
scalar field
{\mathbb{K}}\in\{{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}\}
.

As shown in Sections
A.2
and
A.3
, the main results from

von Neumann 1939
on complete and incomplete tensor products of Hilbert
spaces can be represented within the framework of reproducing kernel
Hilbert spaces in a concise way.
Tensor products of bounded linear operators are discussed in
Section
A.4
.

In Sections
A.5
and
A.6
we consider particular cases,
where the results are due to
Rüßmann 2020
and
Guichardet 1969
,
respectively.
In Sections
A.5

we study tensor products of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
and their relation to Hilbert spaces with
reproducing kernels of tensor product form.
In Sections
A.6

we consider tensor products of
L^{2}
-spaces and their
relation to
L^{2}
-spaces with respect to the corresponding
product measures.

A.1.
Convergence of Products

We discuss the convergence of infinite products in
{\mathbb{K}}
,
see
von Neumann 1939
and cf. (
1.1
).
Let
(z_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
denote a sequence in
{\mathbb{K}}
.
We say that the product
\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}
is

convergent
with value

z\in{\mathbb{K}}
, if the following holds for every
\varepsilon>0
.
There exists a finite set
I_{0}\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
such that

|\prod_{j\in I}z_{j}-z|\leq\varepsilon
holds
for every finite set
I_{0}\subseteq I\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
. The value
of a convergent product is uniquely determined; hence we
put
\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}:=z
, given convergence with value
z
.
In the case of convergence we obviously also have convergence of

\bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{J}z_{j}\bigr)_{J\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, as required in
(
1.1
), and

\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}z_{j}
.

Moreover, we say that
\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}
is

quasi-convergent
, if

\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|z_{j}|
converges. The latter is necessary, but
not sufficient for convergence of
\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}
,
and for non-convergent, but quasi-convergent products we
put
\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}:=0
. The following properties
are equivalent:

(i)

\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}
is convergent and
\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}\neq 0
,

(ii)

\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}
is quasi-convergent and

\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}\neq 0
,

(iii)

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|z_{j}-1|<\infty
and
z_{j}\neq 0
for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Furthermore,
\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|z_{j}-1|<\infty
implies

\lim_{J\to\infty}\prod_{j=J}^{\infty}z_{j}=1
.
If we have
z_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
z_{j}\geq 1
for
every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then the convergence of
\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}

is obviously equivalent to the convergence of

\bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{J}z_{j}\bigr)_{J\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

A.2.
The Complete Tensor Product

In the sequel we use the notation
{\boldsymbol{f}}=(f_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}

with
f_{j}\in H_{j}
for elements
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in\times_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}H_{j}
.
For the construction of the complete tensor product of
the spaces
H_{j}
we define

C:=\Bigl\{{\boldsymbol{f}}\in\times_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}H_{j}\colon\text{$\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|f_{j}\|_{H_{j}}$ converges}\Bigr\}.

For
{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C
the product

\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\langle f_{j},g_{j}\rangle_{H_{j}}
is quasi-convergent,
see
von Neumann 1939
. Hence we may define a mapping

{\mathcal{K}}\colon C\times C\to{\mathbb{K}}
by

\phantom{\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C,}{\mathcal{K}}({\boldsymbol{g}},{\boldsymbol{f}}):=\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\langle f_{j},g_{j}\rangle_{H_{j}},\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C,

and
von Neumann 1939
implies that
{\mathcal{K}}
is a reproducing kernel.

Cf.
von Neumann 1939
for the following definition.

Definition A.1
.

The
complete tensor product
of the spaces
H_{j}
is the
Hilbert space

H:=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}H_{j}:=H({\mathcal{K}})

with reproducing kernel
{\mathcal{K}}
.

For
any
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C

the function

\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}:={\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})\in H

is called an
elementary tensor
. Its norm is

\|\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}\|_{H}=\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|f_{j}\|_{H_{j}}
, and
the span of elementary tensors is dense in
H
.

It is desirable to work on smaller domains
\widetilde{C}\subsetneq C

consisting of sequences that are easier to handle. If the restriction
mapping
f\mapsto f|_{\widetilde{C}}
defined on
H({\mathcal{K}})
is injective,
it is actually already an isometric isomorphism onto

H({\mathcal{K}}|_{\widetilde{C}\times\widetilde{C}})
.
Hence we may identify in a natural way the reproducing kernel Hilbert
spaces
H=H({\mathcal{K}})
and
H({\mathcal{K}}|_{\widetilde{C}\times\widetilde{C}})
.

In this sense, it suffices to consider the elements of
H

on the domain

C_{0}:=\Bigl\{{\boldsymbol{f}}\in\times_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}H_{j}\colon\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|\|f_{j}\|_{H_{j}}-1\right|<\infty\Bigr\}\subsetneq C.

Indeed, for
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C
we have

{\mathcal{K}}({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{f}})\neq 0
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C_{0}
and

f_{j}\neq 0
for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
. In particular,

{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})=0
for
{\boldsymbol{f}}\not\in C_{0}
, so that
all elements
g\in H
vanish on
C\setminus C_{0}
.

Consider
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C
and a sequence
(z_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathbb{K}}
with a
convergent product. For
z:=\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}
and

{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}
given by
f^{\prime}_{j}:=z_{j}\cdot f_{j}
we have

{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}\in C
and

z\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})={\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}),

see
von Neumann 1939
.
This property allows to further reduce the domain, namely
to consider the elements of
H
only on

V:=\{{\boldsymbol{f}}\in\times_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}H_{j}\colon\text{$\|f_{j}\|_{H_{j}}=1$ for every $j\in{\mathbb{N}}$}\}\subsetneq C_{0}.

Indeed, for
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C
with
{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})\neq 0
and

{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}\in V
given by

(A.1)

f^{\prime}_{j}:=\|f_{j}\|^{-1}_{H_{j}}\cdot f_{j}

we obtain

{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})=\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|f_{j}\|_{H_{j}}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}).

A.3.
The Incomplete Tensor Product

Incomplete tensor products are subspaces of
H
and constructed in the
following way. The property

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|\langle f_{j},g_{j}\rangle_{H_{j}}-1\right|<\infty

for
{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C_{0}
defines an equivalence relation on
C_{0}
,
see
von Neumann 1939
. Observe that
{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C_{0}
has
at most a finite number of components
g_{j}=0
. If we replace
all of them by non-zero elements of the corresponding
Hilbert spaces
H_{j}
, we obtain
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C_{0}
, equivalent to

{\boldsymbol{g}}
, with
{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})\neq 0
.
For
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C_{0}
with
{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})\neq 0

and
{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}\in V
given by (
A.1
)
we have the equivalence of
{\boldsymbol{f}}
and
{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}
.
For a sequence
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V
of unit vectors
v_{j}\in H_{j}

we consider its equivalence class

C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}:=\Bigl\{{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C_{0}\colon\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left|\langle v_{j},f_{j}\rangle_{H_{j}}-1\right|<\infty\Bigr\}.

Observe that
\{C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\colon{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V\}
is a partition
of the set
C_{0}
.
Cf.
von Neumann 1939
for the following definition.

Definition A.2
.

The
incomplete tensor product

of the spaces
H_{j}
based on
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V

is the closed linear subspace

H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}:=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}H_{j}^{(v_{j})}:=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})\colon{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\}

of
H
, equipped with the induced norm.

It follows that for every
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in V

(A.2)

{\boldsymbol{w}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\quad\Rightarrow\quad H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}=H^{({\boldsymbol{w}})}

and

{\boldsymbol{w}}\not\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\quad\Rightarrow\quad H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\perp H^{({\boldsymbol{w}})}.

The latter orthogonality property follows from

{\mathcal{K}}({\boldsymbol{g}},{\boldsymbol{f}})=0
for all
non-equivalent
{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C_{0}
.
Consequently, it suffices to consider the elements of
H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
on
the domain
C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
, since every
g\in H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}

vanishes on
C\setminus C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
.

The observations above yield the following representation of
H
as an
orthogonal sum of Hilbert spaces: If
\mathcal{R}\subseteq V
is a
system of representers of the equivalence classes

C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
,
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V
, then

H=\bigoplus_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{R}}H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}.

The following approximation is employed in the proof of

von Neumann 1939
. Because of its particular relevance
for this paper we present the result together with a proof.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
. For
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{J}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}

given by

f_{J,j}:=\begin{cases}f_{j},&\text{if $j<J$,}\\
v_{j},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

we have

\lim_{J\to\infty}\|{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})-{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{J})\|_{H}=0.

Proof.

Let
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
. Assume at first that
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in V
.
Then we obtain

1/2\cdot\|{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})-{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{J})\|^{2}_{H}=1-\Re\prod_{j\geq J}\langle v_{j},f_{j}\rangle_{H_{j}}.

This yields the convergence as claimed.

Assume now that
{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})\neq 0
.
Then we put
z_{j}:=\|f_{j}\|_{H_{j}}
. For
{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}\in V
according to
(
A.1
) we already know that

{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{J}^{\prime})
converges to
{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime})
in
H
.
Furthermore,

\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}z_{j}\cdot\|{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime})-{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\prime}_{J})\|_{H}=\Bigl\|{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})-\prod_{j\geq J}z_{j}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{J})\Bigr\|_{H}

and

\Bigl\|{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{J})-\prod_{j\geq J}z_{j}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{J})\Bigr\|^{2}_{H}=\Bigl|1-\prod_{j=J}^{\infty}z_{j}\Bigr|^{2}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{J-1}z_{j}^{2}.

This yields the convergence as claimed, since

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|z_{j}-1|<\infty
also in this case.

It remains to observe that
{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{J})=0
for
J

sufficiently large, if
{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})=0
.
∎

Subsequently, we discuss further properties of incomplete tensor
products, which may be derived with the help of Lemma
A.3

in a straightforward way.

First of all,

H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})\colon{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\},

where

C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}:=\{{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C_{0}\colon\text{$\{j\in{\mathbb{N}}\colon f_{j}\neq v_{j}\}$ is finite}\}\subsetneq C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}.

Consequently, it suffices to consider the elements of
H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
on
the domain
C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
, since

(A.3)

g({\boldsymbol{f}})=\lim_{J\to\infty}g({\boldsymbol{f}}_{J})

for every
g\in H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
and every
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
.

As a closed linear subspace of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space,

H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, too.
Moreover, the reproducing kernel
{\mathcal{K}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\colon C\times C\to{\mathbb{K}}
of
H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
satisfies

{\mathcal{K}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}({\boldsymbol{g}},{\boldsymbol{f}})=\begin{cases}{\mathcal{K}}({\boldsymbol{g}},{\boldsymbol{f}}),&\text{if ${\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Observe that
{\mathcal{K}}({\boldsymbol{g}},{\boldsymbol{f}})=\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\langle f_{j},g_{j}\rangle_{H_{j}}
is convergent, and not just quasi-convergent,
for
{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
.

Instead of
{\mathcal{K}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
and
H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
one may actually
consider the kernel

{\mathcal{K}}_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}:={\mathcal{K}}|_{C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\times C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}}

and the space
H({\mathcal{K}}_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})})
, since the restriction

g\mapsto g|_{C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}}
defines an isometric isomorphism between

H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
and
H({\mathcal{K}}_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})})
. The corresponding inverse
extension map is essentially given by (
A.3
).

Remark A.4
.

Consider any choice of orthonormal bases
(e_{\nu,j})_{\nu\in N_{j}}

in each of the spaces
H_{j}
, and assume that
0\in N_{j}
for
notational convenience. Let
\mathcal{N}
denote the set of all
sequences
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:=(\nu_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
\nu_{j}\in N_{j}
for every

j\in{\mathbb{N}}
and with
\{j\in{\mathbb{N}}\colon\nu_{j}\neq 0\}
being finite.
If

e_{0,j}=v_{j}

for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then the elementary tensors

(A.4)

e_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}e_{\nu_{j},j}

with
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{N}

form an orthonormal basis of
H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
.

A.4.
Tensor Products of Operators

We now consider two incomplete tensor products

H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}:=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}H_{j}^{(v_{j})}\quad\text{and}\quad G^{({\boldsymbol{w}})}:=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{j}^{(w_{j})}

together with a sequence of bounded linear operators

T_{j}\colon H_{j}\to G_{j}.

We assume that
T_{j}v_{j}=w_{j}
for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
and that

\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|T_{j}\|
converges. Observe that
T_{j}v_{j}=w_{j}

implies
\|T_{j}\|\geq 1
.
Letting

T\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}T_{j}f_{j}

for elementary tensors
\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}
with

{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
and extending this linearly defines a
linear operator from the span

H_{0}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
of these elementary tensors to

G^{({\boldsymbol{w}})}
.
This follows similarly as the corresponding statement for finite
algebraic tensor products, see, e.g.,
Hackbusch 2012
.
It is readily checked that this operator is bounded, in fact it
has norm

\|T\|=\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|T_{j}\|.

Again, this follows similarly as for finite Hilbert space tensor
products, see, e.g.,
Hackbusch 2012
.
Since

H_{0}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
is dense in
H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
,

T
uniquely extends to a linear
operator
T\colon H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\to G^{({\boldsymbol{w}})}
with the same norm.
This operator
is called the
tensor product
of
the operators
T_{j}
and denoted by
\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}T_{j}
.
If, additionally, each operator
T_{j}
is an isomorphism and

\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|T_{j}^{-1}\|

converges as well, then
T\colon H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\to G^{({\boldsymbol{w}})}
is an
isomorphism with inverse
T^{-1}=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}T_{j}^{-1}
.

A.5.
Incomplete Tensor Products of Reproducing Kernel
Hilbert Spaces

Here we consider the particular case

\phantom{\qquad\quad j\in{\mathbb{N}},}H_{j}:=H(k_{j}),\qquad\quad j\in{\mathbb{N}},

with reproducing kernels
k_{j}\colon D_{j}\times D_{j}\to{\mathbb{K}}
.
For every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
we assume that there exists a point
x\in D_{j}
with
k_{j}(\cdot,x)\neq 0
, which is equivalent to
H_{j}\neq\{0\}
.
We put
D:=\times_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}D_{j}
.

To
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in D
we associate

\tau({\boldsymbol{x}}):=(k_{j}(\cdot,x_{j}))_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\times_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}H_{j},

and we put

{\mathfrak{X}}_{0}:=\Bigl\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in D\colon\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|k_{j}(x_{j},x_{j})-1|<\infty\Bigr\}

as well as

{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}:=\Bigl\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}_{0}\colon\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}|v_{j}(x_{j})-1|<\infty\Bigr\}

for
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in D
.
Observe that
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}_{0}
if and only if
\tau({\boldsymbol{x}})\in C_{0}
.
Moreover,
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
if and only if
\tau({\boldsymbol{x}})\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
.

The following result is due to
Rüßmann 2020
.

Lemma A.5
.

If
{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\neq\emptyset
, then

H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,\tau({\boldsymbol{x}}))\colon{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\}.

Proof.

Put
{\mathcal{H}}:=\operatorname{span}\{{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,\tau({\boldsymbol{x}}))\colon{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\}
.
It suffices to prove the following result.
For every
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\cap v
there exists a sequence

(f_{J})_{J\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathcal{H}}
such that

\lim_{J\to\infty}\|{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})-f_{J}\|_{H}=0.

For a fixed choice of
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
with

k_{j}(\cdot,x_{j})\neq 0
for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\cap v
by

w_{j}:=\|k_{j}(\cdot,x_{j})\|^{-1}_{H_{j}}\cdot k_{j}(\cdot,x_{j}).

Since
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{w}})}
, we get
C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}=C^{({\boldsymbol{w}})}
.
Furthermore, we define
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{J}\in C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{w}})}\cap v
for
J\in{\mathbb{N}}
by

f_{J,j}:=\begin{cases}f_{j},&\text{if $j\leq J$,}\\
w_{j},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Lemma
A.3
yields

\lim_{J\to\infty}\|{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}})-{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{J})\|_{H}=0.

Fix
J\in{\mathbb{N}}
and consider the norm given by

\|(h_{1},\dots,h_{J})\|:=\max_{j=1,\dots,J}\|h_{j}\|_{H_{j}}
on
H_{1}\times\dots\times H_{J}
.
By
\zeta_{J}(h_{1},\dots,h_{J})={\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{g}})
with

g_{j}:=\begin{cases}h_{j},&\text{if $j\leq J$,}\\
w_{j},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

we obtain a continuous mapping
\zeta_{J}
from

H_{1}\times\dots\times H_{J}
.
into
H
.
For every
j\leq J
we choose a sequence
(h_{j,n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

in
\operatorname{span}\{k_{j}(\cdot,x)\colon x\in D_{j}\}

with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_{j}-h_{j,n}\|_{H_{j}}=0
.
It follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\zeta_{J}(h_{1,n},\dots,h_{J,n})-{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{J})\|_{H}=0.

Finally,

\zeta_{J}(h_{1,n},\dots,h_{J,n})\in{\mathcal{H}}
, which completes the
proof.
∎

Assume that
{\mathfrak{X}}_{0}\neq\emptyset
.
In this case

\phantom{\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}_{0},}K({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):={\mathcal{K}}(\tau({\boldsymbol{x}}),\tau({\boldsymbol{y}}))=\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}k_{j}(x_{j},y_{j}),\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}_{0},

yields a reproducing kernel
K\colon{\mathfrak{X}}_{0}\times{\mathfrak{X}}_{0}\to{\mathbb{K}}

of tensor product form.
The complete tensor product

H
of the reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
H(k_{j})

is a feature space and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\mapsto{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,\tau({\boldsymbol{x}}))
with
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}_{0}
is a feature map of
K
.
Consequently,

H(K)=\{g\circ\tau|_{{\mathfrak{X}}_{0}}\colon g\in H\}

and

\|f\|_{H(K)}=\min\{\|g\|_{H}\colon g\circ\tau|_{{\mathfrak{X}}_{0}}=f\}.

Assume that
{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\neq\emptyset
. Then analogous results
hold true for the tensor product kernel

K^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}:=K|_{{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\times{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}}

and the incomplete tensor product
H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
of the reproducing
kernel Hilbert spaces
H(k_{j})
.

In the sense of the following result,
which is due to
Rüßmann 2020
,

H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
is
the reproducing kernel Hilbert space with the tensor product kernel

K^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
.

Theorem A.6
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V
.
If
{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\neq\emptyset
, then

\Phi\colon H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}\to{\mathbb{K}}^{{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}},

given by

\phantom{\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})},}\Phi g({\boldsymbol{x}}):=g(\tau({\boldsymbol{x}})),\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})},

induces an isometric isomorphism between
H^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
and
H(K^{({\boldsymbol{v}})})
.
In particular, for
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}

the product
\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}(x_{j})
converges and

\Bigg(\Phi\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}\Bigg)({\boldsymbol{x}})=\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}(x_{j}).

Proof.

Due to Lemma
A.5
the mapping
\Phi
is injective,
and thus it induces an isometric isomorphism as claimed.
Furthermore, for
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
,

\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}(\tau({\boldsymbol{x}}))=\langle{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{f}}),{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,\tau({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle_{H}=\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}(x_{j})

with convergence as claimed, since
\tau({\boldsymbol{x}})\in C^{({\boldsymbol{v}})}
.
∎

A.6.
Incomplete Tensor Products of
L^{2}
-Spaces

Here we consider the particular case

\phantom{\qquad\quad j\in{\mathbb{N}},}H_{j}:=L^{2}(\rho_{j}):=L^{2}(D_{j},\rho_{j}),\qquad\quad j\in{\mathbb{N}},

for a sequence
(\rho_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of probability measures on

\sigma
-algebras on sets
D_{j}
.
Let
\rho:=\times_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\rho_{j}
denote the product measure on
the product
\sigma
-algebra on
D:=\times_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}D_{j}
.
Recall that we identify square-integrable
functions and the elements of the corresponding
L^{2}
-space.
We now show
that the incomplete tensor product
H^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
based on the
constant function
1
as the unit vector for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}

is, in a canonical way, isometrically isomorphic to
L^{2}(\rho):=L^{2}(D,\rho)
.

We introduce the following notation.
For
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
and
J\in{\mathbb{N}}
we consider the
function
{\boldsymbol{x}}\mapsto\prod_{j=1}^{J}f_{j}(x_{j})
, which belongs to

L^{2}(\rho)
. In the case of convergence in
L^{2}(\rho)
as
J\to\infty
,
we use
\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}
to denote the limit; to indicate this
convergence we say that
\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}
exists in
L^{2}(\rho)
.

See
Guichardet 1969
for the following result.

Theorem A.7
.

For every
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
we have existence of

\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}
in
L^{2}(\rho)
. Moreover,
the mapping
\Psi\colon L^{2}(\rho)\to{\mathbb{K}}^{C}
given by

\phantom{\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})},}\Psi\gamma({\boldsymbol{g}}):=\int_{D}\gamma\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\overline{g_{j}}\,d\rho,\qquad\quad{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{u}})},

and
\Psi\gamma({\boldsymbol{g}}):=0
for

{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C\setminus C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}

defines an isometric isomorphism between
L^{2}(\rho)
and
H^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
.
In particular,

(A.5)

\Psi^{-1}\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}

for
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
.

Proof.

The existence of
\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}
in
L^{2}(\rho)
trivially
holds true for
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
.
Let
\psi\colon C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}\to L^{2}(\rho)
be given by

\psi{\boldsymbol{f}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}
.
Since
\langle\psi{\boldsymbol{f}},\psi{\boldsymbol{g}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}={\mathcal{K}}({\boldsymbol{g}},{\boldsymbol{f}})

for
{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
,
the mapping
\psi
is a feature map and
L^{2}(\rho)

is a feature space for the reproducing kernel
{\mathcal{K}}_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
.
The space
H({\mathcal{K}}_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})})
may therefore be
described via the linear mapping

\Psi_{1}\colon L^{2}(\rho)\to{\mathbb{K}}^{C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}}
,
given by

\phantom{\quad\qquad{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}.}\Psi_{1}\gamma({\boldsymbol{g}}):=\langle\gamma,\psi{\boldsymbol{g}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}=\int_{D}\gamma\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\overline{g_{j}}\,d\rho,\quad\qquad{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}.

Since
\operatorname{span}\psi(C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})})
is a dense subset of
L^{2}(\rho)
,
the mapping
\Psi_{1}
is injective.
We conclude that
\Psi_{1}
induces an isometric isomorphism
between
L^{2}(\rho)
and
H({\mathcal{K}}_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})})
, which will also
be denoted by
\Psi_{1}
.

Recall that
h\mapsto h|_{C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}}
defines an isometric
isomorphism between
H^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
and
H({\mathcal{K}}_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})})
. Its
inverse
\Psi_{2}\colon H({\mathcal{K}}_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})})\to H^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}

is given by (
A.3
), i.e., by the extension

\Psi_{2}h({\boldsymbol{g}}):=\begin{cases}\lim_{J\to\infty}h({\boldsymbol{g}}_{J}),&\text{if ${\boldsymbol{g}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}$},\\
0,&\text{if ${\boldsymbol{g}}\in C\setminus C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}$.}\end{cases}

Altogether, we obtain the isometric isomorphism

\Psi:=\Psi_{2}\circ\Psi_{1}\colon L^{2}(\rho)\to H^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
.

Let
\gamma\in L^{2}(\rho)
.
By definition of
\Psi
and
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{J}
,

\Psi\gamma({\boldsymbol{g}})=\begin{cases}\lim_{J\to\infty}\int_{D}\gamma\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\overline{g_{J,j}}\,d\rho,&\text{if ${\boldsymbol{g}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}$,}\\
0,&\text{if ${\boldsymbol{g}}\in C\setminus C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}$.}\end{cases}

In the particular case
\gamma:=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}
with

{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
we get

\Psi\gamma({\boldsymbol{g}})=\lim_{J\to\infty}\int_{D}\prod_{j=1}^{J}f_{j}(x_{j})\cdot\overline{g_{j}}(x_{j})\,d\rho({\boldsymbol{x}})={\mathcal{K}}({\boldsymbol{g}},{\boldsymbol{f}})=\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}({\boldsymbol{g}})

for
{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
, i.e., (
A.5
) is satisfied for

{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C_{\star}^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
.
In the general case
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
this implies

\Psi^{-1}\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{J,j}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{J,j}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\prod_{j=1}^{J}f_{j}(x_{j})

for
\rho
-a.e.
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in D
. Moreover,

\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{J,j}
converges to
\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}

in
H^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
as
J\to\infty
, see Lemma
A.3
. It follows that

\lim_{J\to\infty}\biggl\|\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{J,j}-\Psi^{-1}\bigotimes_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}f_{j}\biggr\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}=0,

i.e.,
\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}
exists in
L^{2}(\rho)
and
(
A.5
) is satisfied for
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
. Consequently,
with
{\boldsymbol{g}}\in C^{({\boldsymbol{1}})}
in place of
{\boldsymbol{f}}
,

\Psi\gamma({\boldsymbol{g}})=\lim_{J\to\infty}\int_{D}\gamma\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\overline{g_{J,j}}\,d\rho=\int_{D}\gamma\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\overline{g_{j}}\,d\rho

for
\gamma\in L^{2}(\rho)
.
∎
