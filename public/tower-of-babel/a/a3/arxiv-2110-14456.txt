Leading-order nonlinear gravitational waves from reheating magnetogeneses Preprint: NORDITA-2021-080
Yutong He, Alberto Roper Pol, Axel Brandenburg

Abstract
We study the leading-order nonlinear gravitational waves (GWs) produced by an electromagnetic (EM) stress in reheating magnetogenesis scenarios. Both nonhelical and helical magnetic fields are considered. By numerically solving the linear and leading-order nonlinear GW equations, we find that the GW energy from the latter is usually larger. We compare their differences in terms of the GW spectrum and parameterize the GW energy difference due to the nonlinear term, Δ ​ ℰ GW \Delta\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW} , in terms of EM energy ℰ EM \mathcal{E}_{\rm EM} as Δ ​ ℰ GW = ( p ~ ​ ℰ EM / k ∗ ) 3 \Delta\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}=(\tilde{p}\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}/k_{*})^{3} , where k ∗ k_{*} is the characteristic wave number, p ~ = 0.84 \tilde{p}=0.84 and 0.88 0.88 are found in the nonhelical and helical cases, respectively, with reheating around the QCD energy scale, while p ~ = 0.45 \tilde{p}=0.45 is found at the electroweak energy scale. We also compare the polarization spectrum of the linear and nonlinear cases and find that adding the nonlinear term usually yields a decrease in the polarization that is proportional to the EM energy density. We parameterize the fractional polarization suppression as | Δ ​ 𝒫 GW / 𝒫 GW | = r ~ ​ ℰ EM / k ∗ |\Delta\mathcal{P}_{\rm GW}/\mathcal{P}_{\rm GW}|=\tilde{r}\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}/k_{*} and find r ~ = 1.2 × 10 − 1 \tilde{r}=1.2\times 10^{-1} , 7.2 × 10 − 4 7.2\times 10^{-4} , and 3.2 × 10 − 2 3.2\times 10^{-2} for the helical cases with reheating temperatures T r = 300 ​ TeV T_{\rm r}=300{\rm\,TeV} , 8 ​ GeV 8{\rm\,GeV} , and 120 ​ MeV 120{\rm\,MeV} , respectively. Prospects of observation by pulsar timing arrays, space-based interferometers, and other novel detection proposals are also discussed.



I
Introduction

The recent detections of gravitational wave (GW) events produced by the collision of compact binary objects by the LIGO-Virgo collaboration
mark the beginning of the rich field of
GW astronomy
Abbott et al. 2016a
;
Abbott et al. 2016b
;
Abbott et al. 2017
.
Over 50 events have been detected so far by Advanced LIGO and
Advanced
Virgo,
which are collected in the GW
transient catalogs
Abbott et al. 2019
;
Abbott et al. 2021
.
These events yield propagating oscillatory strains of the metric tensor, which were
predicted by linearized general relativity (GR).
On the other hand,
a different type of perturbations in the spacetime metric yielding non-oscillatory strains
have been predicted by nonlinear GR, although they have
not yet been detected.
This effect, known as GW memory, is manifested by a permanent displacement of freely-falling test masses after the passage of GWs

Zel’dovich and Polnarev 1974
;
Turner 1978
;
Epstein 1978
;
Braginskii and Thorne 1987
;
Christodoulou 1991
;
Blanchet and Damour 1992
;
Thorne 1992
.
It is a consequence of the facts that GR admits highly-degenerate
Bondi-Metzner-Sachs (BMS) vacua
Bondi et al. 1962
;
Sachs 1962

and that the initial and final metrics, although both flat, differ by a BMS supertranslation induced by the passing GWs.
Theoretically, it has been shown that the GW memory effect is equivalent to the temporal Fourier transform of the Weinberg soft graviton
theorem
Strominger and Zhiboedov 2016
;
He et al. 2015
,
which is a universal formula relating to each other two scattering matrices that differ by a zero-energy (soft) graviton
Weinberg 1965
.

Recently, the memory effect has seen a pick-up in interest
in the GW community extensively due to the expected improvement in
the coming years in
sensitivity by upgraded GW detectors.
In particular, the advanced LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA network
will be able to detect an ensemble of memory signals
from binary mergers
Lasky et al. 2016
;
McNeill et al. 2017
;
Hübner et al. 2020
;
Boersma et al. 2020
.
On the other hand, space-based
interferometers like the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna
(LISA), planned to be launched in 2034
Amaro-Seoane et al. 2017
, will be capable of
storing the permanent displacements since the test masses are
actually free-falling, unlike in ground-based detectors
Favata 2009a
.
It is expected that LISA will detect compact binaries
memory signals individually
Favata 2009a
;
Islo et al. 2019
;
Yang and Martynov 2018
.
In the lower-frequency end, pulsar timing arrays (PTAs)
are also considered as potential memory detectors and
certain constraints have been put on the detectable signals

Wang et al. 2015
;
Arzoumanian et al. 2015
;
Madison et al. 2017
;
Aggarwal et al. 2020
.
Astrophysical sources of the memory effect have been studied
in binary black holes

Favata 2009a
;
Pollney and Reisswig 2011
;
Khera et al. 2021
;
Ebersold and Tiwari 2020
;
Islo et al. 2019
,
supernova neutrinos

Turner 1978
;
Epstein 1978
;
Mukhopadhyay et al. 2021
, and gamma-ray
burst jets
Burrows and Hayes 1996
;
Segalis and Ori 2001
;
Sago et al. 2004
;
Hiramatsu et al. 2005
.
As for cosmological sources, cosmic
strings have been considered
Aurrekoetxea et al. 2020
;
Jenkins and Sakellariadou 2021
.
In particular, the memory strain from compact binary objects has been
expected to be only one order of magnitude weaker than the standard GW
strain
Favata 2009a
;
Khera et al. 2021
.
As a test of gravity theories, the memory effect has been studied as a
result of massive gravity
Kilicarslan and Tekin 2019
, scalar-tensor theories

Hou 2021
;
Hou and Zhu 2021
;
Koyama 2020
, as well as in the strong-field regime

Shore 2018
.
In addition, novel phenomena, different from the conventional displacement memory effect, have been proposed.
These are the velocity

Grishchuk and Polnarev 1989
;
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Divakarla and Whiting 2021
and spin memory effect
Pasterski et al. 2016
;
Nichols 2017
;
Mitman et al. 2020
;
Tahura et al. 2021
,
where the freely-falling particles would acquire permanent relative velocities and
spins, respectively, after the passage of GWs.

However, despite the plethora of literature on GW memory effect to date, to the best of our knowledge,
none has explored the effect with continuous sources on cosmological scales, which are ubiquitous in the early universe.
With the recent advance of numerical simulations, there has been
significant progress in calculating the GW backgrounds (GWBs) from distributed
sources such as homogeneous hydrodynamic and hydromagnetic turbulence
in the early universe

Roper Pol et al. 2020a
;
Roper Pol et al. 2020b
.
We are interested in studying the leading-order nonlinear term producing
gravitational radiation, for which we require strong sources.
For this reason, we consider in this work the production of GWs by
reheating magnetogeneses, which yield electromagnetic (EM) strengths
that depend only on the initial field and can grow several orders

;
Brandenburg et al. 2021a
.
For simplicity, we defer the study of the nonlinear effect produced by hydromagnetic turbulence
from cosmological phase transitions to future work, since it requires
a fully relativistic framework to reach plasma and/or Alfvén velocities

There have been recent analytical works on nonhelical
and helical magnetogeneses during the reheating era

Sharma et al. 2018

Numerical simulations of GWs from these magnetogeneses have also

II
Reheating magnetogenesis

A promising mechanism that can produce helical and nonhelical relic magnetic fields today is reheating magnetogenesis.
It is made possible by the coupling of the
inflaton field and the Maxwell Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}^{\rm EM}=F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}

Ratra 1992
, where

F_{\mu\nu}\equiv\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
is the
Faraday tensor in terms of the EM four-potential
A_{\mu}
.
However, due to the conformal invariance of
\mathcal{L}^{\rm EM}
,
the EM amplitudes decay rapidly as the square of the scale factor during inflation.
Therefore, violation of conformal invariance is required to obtain a growing EM field
Turner and Widrow 1988
;
Dolgov 1993
.
This can be done, in the nonhelical case, via a coupling term of the form

\mathcal{L}^{\rm nhel}=f^{2}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}
, with
f
being a time-dependent function
that scales as

f(a)\begin{cases}\propto a^{\alpha}\;\;&(\text{during inflation}),\\
\propto a^{-\beta}\;\;&(\text{during reheating}),\\
=1\;\;&(\text{during RD}),\end{cases}

(1)

where
RD refers to the radiation-dominated era, which starts at
the end of reheating,

a
is the scale factor,
\alpha
is chosen to be 2, to avoid
the backreaction problem
Sharma et al. 2017
, or 1, which enables reheating temperatures above the electroweak (EW) scale
Brandenburg et al. 2021a
(see Sec.
IV
),
and
\beta>0
parameterizes the reheating temperature
T_{\rm r}
.
The detailed relations between
T_{\rm r}
and
\beta
can be found in Ref.
Sharma et al. 2017
;
Sharma et al. 2018
.
Note that, in order to realize the standard electromagnetism,
f(a)=1
is
needed by the onset of radiation era, which we normalize to
\eta_{*}=1

Roper Pol et al. 2020a
.

On the other hand, helical magnetic fields can also be generated
by a modified coupling term of the form

\mathcal{L}^{\rm hel}=\gamma f^{2}F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}
, where
\gamma\neq 0
is a
constant,
f
obeys the same scaling relations as in
Eq. (
1
),
and
\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}=\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}/2
is the dual of the Faraday tensor,
with
\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}
being the fully antisymmetric tensor.

In addition, recall that the conformal time
\eta
relates to the scale
factor
a
as
a\propto\eta^{2/(1+3w)}
, where
w\equiv p/\rho
is the
equation of state of the universe.
During the radiation era with
w=1/3

a\propto\eta
.
During the matter-dominated reheating era,

a\propto\eta^{2}
.
Since we adopt the normalization such that
a_{*}=1

marks the onset of the radiation era at
\eta_{*}=1

Roper Pol et al. 2020a
,
we have the following relations
Sharma et al. 2017

a=\begin{cases}(\eta+1)^{2}/4\;\;&(\text{during reheating}),\\
\eta\;\;&(\text{during RD}).\end{cases}

(2)

Equations of motion for the four-potential

The EM vector

\tilde{\bm{A}}
can split into

\tilde{A}_{-}

(3)

where

\tilde{\bm{e}}_{\pm}(\bm{k})=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}[\tilde{\bm{e}}_{1}(\bm{k})\pm\tilde{\bm{e}}_{2}(\bm{k})]

(4)

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\pm}\equiv f\tilde{A}_{\pm}

is the second derivative with respect to conformal time
\eta
.
Here
\gamma
is a constant, and the terms involving the coupling function

1

2

\frac{a^{\prime\prime}}{a}=\frac{2}{(\eta+1)^{2}}\;,\;\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}=-\frac{2\beta}{\eta+1}\;,\;\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f}=\frac{2\beta(2\beta+1)}{(\eta+1)^{2}}.

(6)

In the helical basis, helicity is proportional to the difference between the

+
and
-
modes.
This means that
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{+}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{-}

\gamma=0

In the helical case,
\gamma=1

The helical term in Eq. (
5

\gamma

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{+}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{-}

III
GW Equations

III.1
Leading-order nonlinear equation

Given the metric
g_{\mu\nu}=\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}
, where
\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}

is the background metric tensor and
|h_{\mu\nu}|\ll 1
correspond to high-frequency (compared to the slowly varying background)
small perturbations,
then the Einstein field equations (EFEs) can be expanded to arbitrary
orders in
h_{\mu\nu}
, following Refs.
Isaacson 1968a
;
Isaacson 1968b
;
see Ch. 20.3 of Ref.
Misner et al. 1973
and Ch. 1 of Ref.
Maggiore 2007
for textbook references.

In general, EFEs read
G_{\mu\nu}=\kappa T_{\mu\nu}
.
Here
T_{\mu\nu}
is the stress-energy tensor of the source,

G_{\mu\nu}\equiv R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R
is the Einstein
tensor in terms of the Ricci tensor
R_{\mu\nu}
and Ricci scalar
R
,
and
\kappa\equiv 8\pi G_{\rm N}
, where
G_{\rm N}
is Newton’s gravitational constant.
As we are interested in nonlinear GWs, we expand
G_{\mu\nu}

up to, at least, quadratic order in
h_{\mu\nu}
, giving

G_{\mu\nu}=\bar{G}_{\mu\nu}+G_{\mu\nu}^{(1)}+G_{\mu\nu}^{(2)}+{\cal O}(h^{3})=\kappa T_{\mu\nu},

(7)

where the superscripts (1) and (2) denote
\mathcal{O}(h)
and
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
,
respectively, and
\bar{G}_{\mu\nu}
depends on
\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}
.
The equation for the
\mathcal{O}(h)
terms can be recast as

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}^{(1)}=\kappa(T_{\mu\nu}+t_{\mu\nu}),

(8)

where
we have introduced the effective stress of GWs, generally
referred to as the Landau-Lifshitz (LL) pseudotensor
Landau and Lifshitz 1962
,

t_{\mu\nu}=-G_{\mu\nu}^{(2)}/\kappa
, with
G_{\mu\nu}^{(2)}
satisfying

G_{\mu\nu}^{(2)}=R_{\mu\nu}^{(2)}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}R^{(2)}.

(9)

The RHS of Eq. (
8
) shows the two terms
sourcing GWs.
Rewriting with the trace-reversed perturbations

\bar{h}_{\mu\nu}\equiv h_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}h
,
with
h=\bar{g}^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}
, and applying
the harmonic gauge
\partial^{\mu}\bar{h}_{\mu\nu}=0
, the linearized Einstein
tensor becomes
G_{\mu\nu}^{(1)}=-\frac{1}{2}\Box\bar{h}_{\mu\nu}
,
where
\Box\equiv\partial_{\rho}\partial^{\rho}=-\partial_{t}^{2}+\bm{\nabla}^{2}
is the d’Alembert
operator.
Therefore, the linearized EFEs, i.e., omitting terms of order
{\cal O}(h^{2})
and higher, correspond to the usual GW equation:

\Box\bar{h}_{\mu\nu}=-2\kappa T_{\mu\nu},

(10)

while the expansion up to second order includes
the additional leading-order nonlinear term
t_{\mu\nu}
.
Since we are interested in the resulting propagation of GWs
at the location of the observer, we can assume a flat background space-time
away from the source.

The second-order Ricci tensor is
Maggiore 2007

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\big({\textstyle{1\over 2}}\partial_{\mu}h_{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\nu}h^{\alpha\beta}+h^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}h_{\alpha\beta}-h^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\beta}h_{\alpha\mu}

(11)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle h^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\beta}h_{\alpha\nu}+h^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}h_{\mu\nu}+\partial^{\beta}h^{\alpha}_{\ \nu}\partial_{\beta}h_{\alpha\mu}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\partial^{\beta}h^{\alpha}_{\ \nu}\partial_{\alpha}h_{\beta\mu}-\partial_{\beta}h^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\nu}h_{\alpha\mu}+\partial_{\beta}h^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}h_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}h^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\mu}h_{\alpha\nu}-{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\partial^{\alpha}h\,\partial_{\alpha}h_{\mu\nu}+{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\partial^{\alpha}h\,\partial_{\nu}h_{\alpha\mu}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\partial^{\alpha}h\partial_{\mu}h_{\alpha\nu}\bigr).

We choose the traceless and harmonic gauge, such that

h=0
(and hence,
\bar{h}_{\mu\nu}=h_{\mu\nu}
, so we can drop the overbar from now on) and
\partial^{\mu}h_{\mu\nu}=0
.

The energy density carried by GWs corresponds to the curvature they induce
on the background so we want to identify the terms
of the LL pseudotensor that contribute to the background metric
by averaging over the high frequencies that characterize the
strain perturbations
h_{\mu\nu}
compared to the background

\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}
.
Therefore, Eq. (
11
) gets simplified to
(see Ref.
Maggiore 2007
for details)

\langle t_{\mu\nu}\rangle=-\frac{1}{\kappa}\langle R_{\mu\nu}^{(2)}\rangle=\frac{1}{4\kappa}\langle\partial_{\mu}h_{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\nu}h^{\alpha\beta}\rangle,

(12)

where the angle brackets refer to the aforementioned average.
If we require
k^{i}\tilde{h}_{i}=0
,
then the strains are expressed in the traceless-transverse (TT) gauge,
such that the
gauge-invariant 00-component corresponds to the GW energy
density
Arnowitt et al. 1961
,

\langle t_{00}\rangle=\frac{1}{4\kappa}\langle\partial_{t}h_{ij}^{\rm TT}\partial_{t}h_{ij}^{\rm TT}\rangle,

(13)

and the spatial components are

\langle t_{ij}\rangle=\frac{1}{4\kappa}\langle\partial_{i}h_{lm}^{\rm TT}\partial_{j}h_{lm}^{\rm TT}\rangle.

(14)

In the present context of continuous sources during cosmological epochs,
we consider the leading-order nonlinear GW equation, given in
Eq. (
8
), and choose
the term that survives the average over high frequencies from all the nonlinear

11
), which corresponds to the memory effect

Will and Wiseman 1996
;
Favata 2009a
;
Favata 2010

\Box h_{ij}^{\rm nlin}=-2\kappa(T_{ij}+t_{ij}),

(15)

where the “nlin” superscript refers to the GW strains sourced by the

t_{ij}=\frac{1}{4\kappa}\partial_{i}h_{lm}^{\rm TT}\partial_{j}h_{lm}^{\rm TT}.

(16)

This is the equation, adapted to an expanding universe, that we use in our study of
the early universe in Sec.
III.3
.
We continue using the term “memory effect,” even though there may not be
any obvious permanent displacement of freely-falling test masses due to the GWB.

III.2
Polarization modes of nonlinear solution

We express the linear polarization modes of
the GW strain in Fourier space as

\tilde{h}_{+,\times}(t,\bm{k})=\tilde{e}_{+,\times}^{\,ij}(\bm{k})\tilde{h}_{ij}(t,\bm{k})

Varshalovich et al. 1988
.
The
+
and
\times
modes are obtained as a result of the TT projection,
and
\tilde{e}_{+,\times}^{\,ij}
are the basis tensors,

\tilde{e}_{+}^{\,ij}=\tilde{e}_{i}^{1}\tilde{e}_{j}^{1}-\tilde{e}_{i}^{2}\tilde{e}_{j}^{2},\quad\tilde{e}_{\times}^{\,ij}=\tilde{e}_{i}^{1}\tilde{e}_{j}^{2}+\tilde{e}_{i}^{2}\tilde{e}_{j}^{1},

(17)

related to the helical polarization basis, given in Eq.
4

for a vector field, as

\tilde{e}^{\,ij}_{\pm}=(\tilde{e}_{+}^{\,ij}\pm i\tilde{e}^{\,ij}_{\times})/\sqrt{2}

Caprini et al. 2004
.
In the same way, we define
\tilde{T}_{+,\times}
and
\tilde{t}_{+,\times}
that source
the corresponding GW mode.

To study the polarization modes of
t_{ij}
, we take the WKB or eikonal approximation, i.e.,
we approximate the solution
\tilde{h}_{\mu\nu}={\cal C}_{\mu\nu}e^{ik_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}}\approx{\cal C}_{\mu\nu}e^{i\phi}
,
being
{\cal C}_{\mu\nu}
the amplitudes of the perturbations,
and
\phi
the constant GW phase.
The effective stress tensor of GWs, given in Eq. (
16
),
can be expressed as [see Eq. (4.5) of Ref.
Isaacson 1968b
]

t_{\mu\nu}^{\rm\,WKB}=\frac{{\cal C}^{2}}{4\kappa}k_{\mu}k_{\nu}\sin^{2}\phi,

(18)

where
{\cal C}^{2}={\cal C}^{\mu\nu}{\cal C}_{\mu\nu}
and
k^{\mu}=(\omega=k,k^{i})
,
with
k^{i}
being the characteristic wave number and
\omega

the angular frequency,
or, equivalently, as

t_{ij}^{\rm\,WKB}=t_{00}\hat{k}_{i}\hat{k}_{j}=\frac{1}{4\kappa}\bigl(\partial_{t}h_{ab}^{\rm TT}\partial_{t}h_{ab}^{\rm TT}\bigr)\hat{k}_{i}\hat{k}_{j}.

(19)

This expression is usually used in analytical studies of the
memory effect; see, e.g., Refs.
Favata 2009b
;
Jenkins and Sakellariadou 2021
.
Note that in our present work, we source the GWs
directly from

,
such that the WKB approximation is not necessary.
However, Eq. (
19
) is useful since it allows us to show
that only one polarization mode is sourced by the
effective stress tensor.
To see this, we Fourier transform Eq. (
19
) to get

\tilde{t}_{ij}^{\rm\,WKB}=\frac{1}{4\kappa}{\cal F}\bigl(\partial_{t}h_{ab}^{\rm TT}\partial_{t}h_{ab}^{\rm TT}\bigr)*{\cal F}(\hat{k}_{i}\hat{k}_{j}),

(20)

where
\cal F
refers to the Fourier transform and is equivalent to
previous notation, which uses a tilde.
One can show that
{\cal F}(\hat{k}_{i}\hat{k}_{j})\propto\delta_{ij}
and, hence,

\tilde{t}_{ij}^{\rm\,WKB}
only has one non-zero mode,
since
\delta^{+}=1
and
\delta^{\times}=0
.
Hence, the GW Eq. (
15
) can be expressed as

\Box\tilde{h}^{\rm nlin}_{+}=-2\kappa(\tilde{T}_{+}+\tilde{t}_{+}),\quad\Box\tilde{h}^{\rm nlin}_{\times}=-2\kappa\tilde{T}_{\times},

(21)

and the polarization modes of the nonlinear strains are
\tilde{h}^{\rm nlin}_{+}=\tilde{h}_{+}+\Delta\tilde{h}_{+}
and
\tilde{h}^{\rm nlin}_{\times}=\tilde{h}_{\times}
, where
\tilde{h}_{+,\times}
are the linear solutions sourced by

\tilde{T}_{+,\times}
and
\Delta\tilde{h}_{+}
is the additional strain sourced by

\tilde{t}_{+}
.

III.4
Order-of-magnitude estimates

III.4.1
Effects on the Maxwell equation

Nonlinear effects at the order
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
can potentially enter GW production at the order
\mathcal{O}(h)
in two ways.
The first way, as has been shown in Sec.
III.1
, is to have a
term
t_{ij}=\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
, given in Eq. (
16
), directly on the RHS of the
standard GW equation, i.e., Eq. (
15
),
thereby acting as an additional source.
The second way is to
see how
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
changes the regular EM source and whether in turn
the modified EM source could also affect GW productions at the linear
order
\mathcal{O}(h)
.

For the latter, we use the first of Maxwell’s equations in curved spacetime

\nabla_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\mu}\bigl(\sqrt{-g}F^{\mu\nu}\bigr)=\mu_{0}j^{\nu}=0.

(28)

We have set the current to vanish during the reheating era, where the relevant Maxwell’s equations are in vacuum.
This can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\partial_{\mu}\sqrt{-g}}{\sqrt{-g}}F^{\mu\nu}

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\biggl(\dfrac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}(\bar{g}^{\rho\sigma}h_{\rho\sigma})+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})\biggr)F^{\mu\nu},

\displaystyle\Box A^{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{O}(h)+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})\Big)\mathcal{O}(A),

(30)

where we have used

\frac{\partial_{\mu}\sqrt{-g}}{\sqrt{-g}}=\frac{1}{2}\bar{g}^{\rho\sigma}\partial_{\mu}g_{\rho\sigma}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}(\bar{g}^{\rho\sigma}h_{\rho\sigma})+\mathcal{O}(h^{2}),

(31)

and applied the Lorenz gauge condition
\partial_{\mu}A^{\mu}=0
,
such that
\partial_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}=\Box A^{\nu}
.

Therefore, Eq. (
30
) shows that the leading-order nonlinearity in the GWs,
of the order
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
, does modify the four-potential, which registers a difference

\Delta A=\mathcal{O}(h^{2})\mathcal{O}(A).

(32)

However, the result of such additional EM amplitude
\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}
, which is proportional to
A^{2}
; see Eq. (
23
), introduces a modification to the production
of GWs at
h={\cal O}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM})=\mathcal{O}(A^{2})
; see Eq. (
22
), of larger order than quadratic in
h
.
For this reason, we only consider the additional GWs produced by their
self-backreaction at
t_{ij}=\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
, rather than the backreaction from the vector
potential at
\mathcal{O}((\Delta A)^{2})=\mathcal{O}(h^{4})\mathcal{O}(A^{2})=\mathcal{O}(h^{5})
.

III.4.2
GW energy

The standard GW equation is linear in the strain and its sourcing energy,
i.e.,
h=\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM})
.
Since GW energy is quadratic in strain, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}=\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
, we have a quadratic relation

\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}=\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}^{2}),

(33)

which has been verified by a number of earlier simulations
Roper Pol et al. 2020b
;
Brandenburg et al. 2021b
;
Brandenburg et al. 2021c
;
Roper Pol et al. 2021
.
Adding nonlinearities of the order
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
to the source means that

h^{\rm nlin}=h+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
.
Therefore, the nonlinear GW energy relates to the source energies as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}^{\rm nlin}

\displaystyle=\bigl[(\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW})^{1/2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})\bigr]^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}+\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}^{1/2}\mathcal{O}(h^{2})+{\cal O}(h^{4})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}^{3})+{\cal O}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}^{4}),

(34)

which implies that the leading-order energy difference

\Delta\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}\equiv\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}^{\rm nlin}-\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}
should be proportional to the
cube of the sourcing energy, i.e.,

\Delta\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}=\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}^{3}).

(35)

This relation will be verified and quantified numerically in Sec.
IV.3
.

III.4.3
GW polarization

The GW polarization is defined to be
Kahniashvili et al. 2005

\mathcal{P}_{\rm GW}(k)=\frac{H_{\rm GW}(k)}{E_{\rm GW}(k)}.

(36)

The differences in GW spectra due to the nonlinear solution; see Eq. (
21
), are

\displaystyle E_{\rm GW}^{\rm nlin}(k)-E_{\rm GW}(k)\propto

\displaystyle\,\Delta\tilde{h}_{+}\tilde{h}_{+}^{*}+\tilde{h}_{+}\Delta\tilde{h}_{+}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,{\cal O}(h^{3})={\cal O}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}^{3}),

(37)

\displaystyle H_{\rm GW}^{\rm nlin}(k)-H_{\rm GW}(k)\propto

\displaystyle\,\Delta\tilde{h}_{+}\tilde{h}_{\times}^{*}-\Delta\tilde{h}_{+}^{*}\tilde{h}_{\times}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,{\cal O}(h^{3})={\cal O}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}^{3}).

(38)

Note that Eq. (
37
) is consistent with Eq. (
34
).
We can now express the relative differences in the GW polarization due to the addition
of the nonlinear term (omitting the
k
dependence to simplify the notation) as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\rm GW}-\mathcal{P}_{\rm GW}^{\rm nlin}}{\mathcal{P}_{\rm GW}}=

\displaystyle\,1-\frac{H_{\rm GW}^{\rm nlin}E_{\rm GW}}{H_{\rm GW}E_{\rm GW}^{\rm nlin}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,1-\frac{1+H_{\rm GW}^{-1}\,{\cal O}(h^{3})}{1+E_{\rm GW}^{-1}\,{\cal O}(h^{3})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,1-(1+H_{\rm GW}^{-1}\,{\cal O}(h^{3}))(1-E_{\rm GW}^{-1}\,{\cal O}(h^{3}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,E_{\rm GW}^{-1}\,{\cal O}(h^{3})-H_{\rm GW}^{-1}\,{\cal O}(h^{3})

\displaystyle =

\displaystyle\,{\cal O}(h)-\mathcal{P}_{\rm GW}^{-1}{\cal O}(h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,{\cal O}(h)={\cal O}(\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}).

(39)

We then expect a decrease in polarization due to the
nonlinear contributions proportional to the EM
energy density and to the polarization obtained in
the linearized approach, which will be verified in
Sec.
IV.2
.
We find that the sign of the difference and its exact value
depend on the balance between the two terms of order
{\cal O}(h)
,
which correspond to those given in Eqs. (
37
) and (
38
)
divided by
H_{\rm GW}(k)
and
E_{\rm GW}(k)
, respectively.

as studied in Ref.

for LISA and in Refs.

The detectability of polarized GWBs produced by primordial helical

has been recently studied in Ref.

V
Conclusions

In this work, we take the GW equation beyond the standard linear order and study the
consequent spectral features due to such nonlinearities in the context of
cosmological GWBs driven by inflationary-generated primordial
magnetic fields during reheating era.
Instead of providing a stationary piece to the final strain for
localized transient events such as binary black hole mergers,
the displacement memory effect in the context of a primordial continuous source
manifests in an overall boost in the GW energy and helicity.
In terms of energy spectra
E_{\rm GW}(k)
, the nonlinear boosts closely follow
the spectral shapes of the linear solutions, but become more evident by
bulging on top of the linear solutions, when the latter start decreasing
sharply.
The bulges are also extended over a longer range of
k
in the presence of helicity.
We find that the boost of GW energy density due to the inclusion of the leading-order
nonlinear term occurs at all wave numbers of the spectrum and we predict
analytically that it is due to terms of order
{\cal O}(h^{3})
; see Eqs. (
34
) and (
37
).
However, we find a small shift in the phase of the GW mode oscillations due to
the nonlinear term.
Hence, at some specific times, this shift can compensate the nonlinear
boost and smaller values of the nonlinear solution can be found.

Another nonlinear aspect related to helicity can be seen in the polarization spectra

\mathcal{P}_{\rm GW}(k)
where, for
\beta=7.3
, the polarization is weakened approximately at the
k

range where the bulges occur.
However, this weakening is not visible for
\beta=2.7
, until the unrealistically large
\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}=10
is reached.
Similarly, in the case with
\beta=1.7
, we find a slight decrease in the spectral
polarization, which is only significant in the case with
\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}=1
.
In general, we find that the addition of the leading-order nonlinear term
suppresses polarization at all wave numbers and we show analytically
that this decrease depends on the polarization of the linearized solution and that it is
proportional to terms of the order
{\cal O}(h)
.
This occurs because the nonlinear boost is, in general, stronger in the
GW energy density than in its helicity.

In terms of the total energy, the relation
\Delta\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}=(p\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM})^{3}
is found.
Together with the quadratic relation
\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}=(q\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM})^{2}
, found in earlier similar numerical computations,
the coefficients
q
and
p
are determined numerically and given in Table
3
.
Furthermore, the scaled coefficients
\tilde{p}\equiv k_{*}p
and

\tilde{q}\equiv k_{*}q
are
found to be a better parameterization, presenting less variation over different
scenarios, which is consistent with the scaling
\mathcal{E}_{\rm GW}=(q\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}/k_{*})^{2}
, also obtained
in previous numerical computations.
Similarly, regarding the total GW polarization,
we find the linear relation
\Delta\mathcal{P}_{\rm GW}=r\mathcal{P}_{\rm GW}\mathcal{E}_{\rm EM}
, where
we define the polarization suppression coefficients
r
, given in Table
3
.

Finally, the detection prospects of such nonlinear GWs are discussed.
We find that PTAs and astrometry methods are relatively promising
in detecting certain low end-of-reheating temperature magnetogenesis-induced GWs.
However, it would be rather challenging to detect nonlinear features
directly, since they correspond to a second-order addition to the linear GWBs.
Hence, very accurate models for the linear contribution are
needed in order to remove it and recover the nonlinear part.
The signals produced considering end-of-reheating temperatures between 8 and
100{\rm\,GeV}
are the most challenging to detect, due to the gap of proposed GW
detectors around the
\mu
Hz band.
The
\mu
Ares concept proposed within the ESA Voyage 2050 programme
Sesana et al. 2021
could allow
to detect this type of signals.
Space-borne GW detectors like LISA, Taiji, and DECIGO,
and atomic interferometers,
also seem promising candidates to detect higher temperature inflationary
scenarios with expected large SNR for the signals produced by helical
magnetic fields.
In addition, the combination of a network of space detectors, e.g., LISA and Taiji, can provide measurements of the polarization of such signals.
Again, the specific features of the nonlinear contribution are of second-order,
so very accurate models and predictions of the linear counterparts are required.
Overall, further studies are required for a more detailed understanding
of nonlinear GWs themselves, as well as the parameter space of various
GW generation processes.
