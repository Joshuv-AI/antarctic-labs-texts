Abstract




Thermodynamics and the Joule-Thomson expansion of dilaton black holes in 2 +1 dimensions

Abstract

In this paper, we study thermodynamics and its applications to the static charged dilaton black hole in 2+1 dimensions. We compute the first law and the Smarr relations for the black hole and introduce a new thermodynamical parameter in order to satisfy the first law. We compute specific heat capacities, internal energy and free energy for the black hole and study local and global stability of the black hole. For
N=1
, the black hole is locally stable for all values of the horizon radius and does not go through phase transitions. Maxwell’s relations are presented for this case. For
N=6/7
, small black holes are locally stable and large black holes are not. There is a first order phase transition between small black holes and the thermal AdS space.
We have also studied the Joule-Thomson expansion for black holes with
N=1
. It is also noted that unlike the charged BTZ black hole, the charged dilaton black hole does not violate the Reverse Isoperimetric Inequality for certain values of parameters of the theory.

Key words
: static, dilaton, black hole, anti-de Sitter space, first law, Smarr relations, Joule-Thomson expansion, Hawking-Page phase transition

Introduction to static charged dilaton black hole in 2+1 dimensions

S=\int d^{3}x\sqrt{-g}\,\left(R-\frac{B}{2}(\nabla\phi)^{2}-e^{-4a\phi}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-V(\phi)\right)\,,

\phi
,

V(\phi)=2e^{b\phi}\Lambda
,

,

\Lambda<0
. Hence, when
\Lambda
appears in our work, it will be a
-\Lambda
in Chan and Manns work
[
21
]
.

We consider the following line element

ds^{2}=-f(r)dt^{2}+f^{-1}(r)dr^{2}+r^{2}R^{2}(r)d\theta^{2}\,,

(2)

where

R(r)=\gamma\,r^{\frac{N}{2}-1}

(3)

and

f(r)=-\frac{2mr^{1-\frac{N}{2}}}{N}-\frac{8\Lambda\beta^{2-N}r^{N}}{(3N-2)N}+\frac{8Q^{2}}{(2-N)N}\,.

Here
\gamma
is a integration constant. Note that the dimensions are:
[r]=L
,
[m]=L^{\frac{N}{2}-1}
,
[\Lambda]=L^{-2}
,
[\beta]=L
,
[Q]=L^{0}
and
[\gamma]=L^{1-\frac{N}{2}}
, where

From Eqs. (
1

[

\frac{1}{rR(r)}\frac{d(rR(r))}{dr}=-\frac{B}{2}\left(\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right)^{2}\,,

(5)

which implies the equation

\frac{d\phi}{dr}=\frac{k}{r}\,,

(6)

where

k=\pm\sqrt{\frac{N(2-N)}{2B}}\,.

(7)

Then

\phi=k\ln\left(\frac{r}{\beta}\right)\,.

(8)

Here

3
Thermodynamics of the black holes for
N=1
case

When
N=1
, the black hole solution is given by following metric:

ds^{2}=-f(r)dt^{2}+f^{-1}(r)dr^{2}+r^{2}R^{2}(r)d\theta^{2}\,,

(9)

where

R(r)=\gamma r^{-1/2}

(10)

and

f(r)=(-2m\sqrt{r}-8\Lambda\beta r+8Q^{2})\,.

(11)

Here
\gamma
is a integration constant. Note that the dimensions are:
[r]=L
,
[m]=L^{-1/2}
,
[\Lambda]=L^{-2}
,
[\beta]=L
,
[Q]=L^{0}
and
[\gamma]=L^{1/2}
, where
L
represents units of length.

Following Ref.
[
39
]
we can obtain the entropy of the above black hole as,

S=\frac{2\pi r_{+}R(r_{+})}{4}=\frac{\pi}{2}\gamma\sqrt{r_{+}}\,

(12)

and the total mass of the black hole as,

M=\gamma\frac{m}{8}\,.

(13)

Here
r_{+}
is the event horizon radius. Since
f(r)=0
at the horizons, one can obtain two horizon radius as

r_{\pm}=\left(\frac{M\pm\sqrt{M^{2}+\beta\gamma^{2}Q^{2}\Lambda}}{\beta\Lambda\gamma}\right)^{2}\,.

(14)

Notice that
r_{+}
is the event horizon and
r_{-}
is the Cauchy horizon. We have substituted for
m
in this expression. There will be an extreme black hole when,

M=\gamma Q\sqrt{-\beta\Lambda}

(15)

Hence, black hole exists only when
M\geq\gamma Q\sqrt{-\beta\Lambda}
. Horizon radius of the extreme black hole is,

r_{ex}=-\frac{Q^{2}}{\beta\Lambda}\,.

(16)

One can obtain an expression for the mass
M
as,

M=-\frac{\Lambda\beta\gamma}{2}\sqrt{r_{+}}+\frac{Q^{2}\gamma}{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_{+}}}\,.

(17)

Note that we have substituted for
m
from eq.
(\ref{mass})
.

In addition to the thermodynamic quantities
S
and
Q
, we consider the cosmological constant as a thermodynamic pressure given by
[
40
]
,

P=-\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi}\,.

(18)

3.1
First law of black hole thermodynamics and the Smarr formula for
N=1

If we rewrite the mass
M
in terms of
Q,S
and
P
as,

M(S,P,Q,R)=\frac{\pi\gamma^{2}Q^{2}}{4S}+8PS\beta\,.

(19)

We then obtain the following expression for the first law of thermodynamics

dM=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial S}\right)_{P,Q,\beta}dS+\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial P}\right)_{S,Q,\beta}dP+\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial q}\right)_{S,P,\beta}dQ+\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial\beta}\right)_{S,P,Q}d\beta\,\,\,,

(20)

or

dM=TdS+VdP+\Phi dQ+Bd\beta\,\,\,,

(21)

where
\beta
is taken as a new thermodynamic quantity. Note that we have considered
M
as the enthalpy
H=U+PV
in the extended phase space in deriving the first law for the black hole.

Then the temperature can be calculated from the first law of black hole thermodynamics as

T=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial S}\right)_{P,Q,\beta}=-\frac{Q^{2}}{\pi\,r_{+}}-\frac{\Lambda\beta}{\pi}\,.

(22)

The Hawking temperature obtained using the surface gravity is

T_{H}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left|\frac{dg_{tt}}{dt}\sqrt{-g^{tt}g^{rr}}\right|_{r_{+}}=-\frac{2M}{\pi\gamma\sqrt{r_{+}}}-\frac{2}{\pi}\Lambda\beta=-\frac{Q^{2}}{\pi\,r_{+}}-\frac{\Lambda\beta}{\pi}\,.

(23)

It is clear that
T_{H}=T
. Notice that when the black hole is extreme, then
T=0
. Also when
r_{+}\rightarrow\infty
,
T\rightarrow-\frac{\Lambda\beta}{\pi}
. There are no local minima or maxima for
T
. Hence black holes cannot exist for
T<0
or
T>-\frac{\Lambda\beta}{\pi}
. Fig.
(\ref{cpt})
shows the behavior of
T
vs
r_{+}
for
N=1
case.

If
P=-\Lambda/(8\pi)
then one can obtain the thermodynamic volume from the first law given in eq.
(\ref{firstlaw})
as,

V=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial P}\right)_{S,Q,\beta}=4\pi\beta\gamma\sqrt{r_{+}}\,,

(24)

which is different from the geometric volume,
V_{geo}=\pi r_{+}^{2}
.

The electric potential at the horizon is given by

\Phi=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial Q}\right)_{S,P,\beta}=\frac{\gamma\,Q}{\sqrt{r_{+}}}\,.

(25)

Where
Q
is related to the electric charge
Q_{BTZ}
of the BTZ black hole
[
20
,
41
]
as follows from Ref.
[
39
]

Q_{BTZ}=\frac{\gamma\,Q}{2}\,.

(26)

Finally we get the thermodynamic quantity
\cal{B}
conjugate to the parameter
\beta

{\cal{B}}=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial\beta}\right)_{S,P,Q}=-\frac{\gamma\Lambda\sqrt{r_{+}}}{2}\,.

(27)

Now to obtain the Smarr formula we follow the scaling arguments of Ref.
[
42
]

\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)M=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial S}\right)S+(-2)\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial P}\right)P+(0)\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial Q}\right)Q+(1)\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial\beta}\right)\beta\,\,\,,

(28)

namely

-\frac{M}{2}=\frac{1}{2}TS-2VP+\cal{B}\beta\,\,\,.

(29)

Now, some clarification and justification is in order as to why we added the
{\cal{B}}d\beta
term to the first law of thermodynamics in eq.
(\ref{firstlaw})
. If we had not done so, then the dimensional analysis of the right side of the Smarr formula will look like
\frac{1}{2}TS-2VP
which is not equal to
-\frac{M}{2}
. Hence the only way one can develop the first law which is consistent with the Smarr formula is to add the
{\cal{B}}d\beta
to the first law of thermodynamics and to include the
\cal{B}\beta
term in the Smarr formula analysis.

It may be surprising that the factor that accompanies the term
TS
in the Smarr formula is
1/2
in eq.
(\ref{smarr})
. Let us consider the following toy model in 3+1-dimensions

ds^{2}=-f(r)dt^{2}+f^{-1}(r)dr^{2}+r^{2}R(r)^{2}\,d\Omega^{2}\,,

(30)

where
R(r)=\gamma\,r^{-1/2}
, and,

f(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r}\,,

(31)

The entropy in this case is given by
[
43
]
,

S=\pi r^{2}_{+}R(r_{+})^{2}=\pi\gamma^{2}\,r_{+}\,.

(32)

Hence,

M=\frac{r_{+}}{2}=\frac{S}{2\pi\gamma^{2}}\,.

(33)

Here the dimensions are:
[r]=L
,
[M]=L^{1}
and
[\gamma]=L^{1/2}
.

Then the temperature is obtained via the first law as,

T=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial S}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi\gamma^{2}}\,.

(34)

By combining eq.
(\ref{entropy2})
and eq.
(\ref{mass2})
, a relation between
M,T
and
S
can be obtained as,

(1)M=(1)TS\,\,\,.

(35)

This is the correct Smarr formula with a factor of 1 instead of 2 for the
TS
term.

The same factor 1 must be considered even if the metric function (together with metric (
30
)) is

f(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r}+\frac{Q^{2}}{r^{2}}\,.

(36)

Here the Smarr formula is

M=(1)TS+(2)\Phi Q\,.

(37)

3.2
Other thermodynamical quantities

We will calculate few more thermodynamical quantities,
C_{P,Q},C_{V,Q}
and internal energy
U
of the black hole as follows:

C_{P,Q}=T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_{P}=\frac{\pi\sqrt{r_{+}}(-Q^{2}+8\pi\beta Pr_{+})}{4Q^{2}}

(38)

C_{V,Q}=T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_{V}=0,

(39)

since when volume is kept constant, i.e.
r_{+}
is kept constant,
S
does not change. Since the enthalpy is given by

which is equal to
U+PV
, one can compute

as follows:

U=M-PV=\frac{\gamma Q^{2}}{2\sqrt{r_{+}}}\,.

(40)

If we write

as a function of temperature

U=-\frac{VT}{8\beta}+PV\,.

(41)

If we write

U=\frac{2\pi\beta\gamma^{2}Q^{2}}{V}\,.

(42)

As it can be observed, the internal energy depends on both the temperature and the pressure. In an ideal gas, the internal energy only depends on temperature.

Helmholtz free energy is

F=U-TS=\frac{16\pi\beta\gamma^{2}Q^{2}}{V}-\frac{TV}{\beta}\,.

(43)

One can substitute
\Lambda=-8\pi P
in eq.
(\ref{temp1})
to obtain the state equation as,

P=\frac{T}{8\beta}+\frac{Q^{2}}{8\pi\beta r_{+}}\,.

(44)

The pressure

is plotted vs

in Fig.

3.3
Thermodynamic stability

For the black hole to be locally stable,
C_{P,Q}>0
. From eq.
(\ref{cp})
this implies,

P>\frac{Q^{2}}{8\pi\beta r_{+}}

(45)

or,

-\Lambda>\frac{Q^{2}}{\beta r_{+}}\,.

(46)

Hence it is clear that if the black hole to be locally stable, then
\Lambda
has to be negative or has or be in anti-de Sitter space. This is very similar to the Schwarzschild anti-de Sitter black hole where it was demonstrated that
-\Lambda r_{+}^{2}>1
for
C_{p}
to be positive
[
6
]
. When
C_{P,Q}
and
T
are plotted against
r_{+}
, it is clear that both are positive for the same values of
r_{+}
as demonstrated in Fig.
(\ref{cpt})
. Both
C_{P,Q}
and
T
are zero when the black hole is extreme with
r_{ex}=-\frac{Q^{2}}{\beta\Lambda}
. Hence, provided
\Lambda<0
, all black holes are locally stable.

3.4
Maxwells relations

We can find the Maxwells’ relation for thermodynamical quantities from the Helmholtz free energy
F
. Since
F=U-TS
and
H=M=U+PV
, one can obtain
F
as,

F=M-PV-TS\,.

(49)

Combining
dM
in eq.
(\ref{firstlaw})
with
dF
from eq.
(\ref{helm})
, we obtain,

dF=-PdV-SdT+\Phi dQ+{\cal{B}}d\beta

(50)

which is equivalent to,

dF=\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\right)_{T,Q,\beta}dV+\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_{V,Q,\beta}dT+\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial Q}\right)_{V,T,\beta}dQ+\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\beta}\right)_{V,T,Q}d\beta\,\,\,.

(51)

From the above differential, following relations are obtained:

S=-\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_{Q,\beta,V}

(52)

P=-\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\right)_{T,Q,\beta}

(53)

\Phi=\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial Q}\right)_{T,\beta,V}

(54)

{\cal{B}}=\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\beta}\right)_{T,V,Q}

(55)

From above four relations, one can differentiate to obtain the following additional 5 relations:

-\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial Q}\right)_{T,\beta,V}=\left(\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial V}\right)_{T,Q,\beta}

(56)

-\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial Q}\right)_{T,\beta,V}=\left(\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial T}\right)_{Q,\beta,V}

(57)

\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_{T,Q,\beta}=\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{Q,\beta,V}

(58)

-\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\beta}\right)_{Q,T,V}=\left(\frac{\partial\cal{B}}{\partial V}\right)_{T,Q,\beta}

(59)

-\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial\beta}\right)_{Q,T,V}=\left(\frac{\partial\cal{B}}{\partial T}\right)_{V,Q,\beta}

(60)

3.5
Reverse Isoperimetric Inequality

There is a conjecture that the isoperimteric ratio
\cal{R}
given by,

{\cal{R}}=\left(\frac{(D-1)V}{\omega_{D-2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{D-1}}\left(\frac{\omega_{D-2}}{A}\right)^{\frac{1}{D-2}}

(61)

will always satisfy
{\cal{R}}\geq 1
when
V
is the thermodynamical volume and
A
is the area of the horizon
[
44
]
. Here,
\omega_{d}
is the area of the
d
-dimensional unit sphere. For the given black hole,

A=2\pi\gamma\sqrt{r_{+}}\,\,;\hskip 14.22636ptV=4\pi\beta\gamma\sqrt{r_{+}}\,\,;\hskip 14.22636pt\omega_{d}=\frac{2\pi^{(d+1)/2}}{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}

(62)

leading to

{\cal{R}}=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi\beta}{\gamma\sqrt{r_{+}}}}\,.

(63)

Since
\beta
and
\gamma
are integrating constants, and their values are not determined a priori, it is not possible to say whether
{\cal{R}}\geq 1
. One may recall that in charged BTZ black holes,
{\cal{R}}<1
leading to a violation of the Reverse Isoperimetric Inequality
[
45
]
. Hence, inclusion of the dilaton field leads to the possibility of charged black holes in 2+1 dimensions to satisfy the Reverse Isoperimetric Inequality.

4
Joule-Thomson expansion

First, let us describe the Joule-Thomson expansion for a thermodynamical system as follows:
In a Joule-Thomson experiment, gas will flow constantly along a thermally insulated tube which is divided into two compartments. There would be a porus plug in between the compartments. The gas will be at higher pressure and will move into a low pressure section. The enthalpy will remain constant along the expansion process; hence it is a iso-enthalpic process. The purpose of this experiment is to find how the temperature
T
varies with pressure
P
at constant enthalpy
H
, which is given by
\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_{H}
and is known as Joule-Thomson coefficient
\mu
.

We will omit the derivation of the Joule-Thomson coefficient since detailed derivations are given in
[
46
]
.
\mu
is given by,

\mu=\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_{H}=\frac{1}{C_{P,Q}}\left[T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_{P}-V\right]\,.

(64)

Here,
C_{P,Q}
is the heat capacity at constant pressure and is given in eq.
(\ref{cp})
. One can determine if the gas will cool or heat after the adiabatic expansion by evaluating the coefficient
\mu
. If
\mu>0
then the temperature decreases and if
\mu<0
then the temperature increases. Note that in the Joule-Thomson expansion, change of pressure is negative.

For the dilaton black hole considered in this paper, we will keep charge
Q
constant when computing
\mu
. Hence
\mu
is given by,

\mu=\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_{H}=\frac{1}{C_{P,Q}}\left[T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_{P}-V\right]

=\frac{1}{C_{P,Q}}\left[T\frac{\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial r_{+}}\right)_{P}}{\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial r_{+}}\right)_{P}}-V\right]

=8\beta\left(\frac{-3Q^{2}+8\pi\beta Pr_{+}}{-Q^{2}+8\pi\beta Pr_{+}}\right)\,.

(65)

In Fig.
(\ref{mutemp})
, the Joule-Thomson coefficient
\mu
and the temperature
T
are plotted against the horizon radius
r_{+}
for different values of charge
Q
. One can observe that there is a divergent point and a zero point for
\mu
. For the
T
vs
r_{+}
graph, it is clear that the divergent point of
\mu
coincide with the zero point of temperature.
T=0
for extreme black holes where the horizon is given by

r_{+}=r_{-}=\frac{M}{8\Lambda}
.

One can define a quantity called the inversion temperature,
T_{i}
and inversion pressure,
P_{i}
when
\mu=0
.
T_{i}
is given by,

T_{i}=V\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_{P}\,.

(66)

The gas will heat if one starts above the inversion temperature and will cool if one starts below the inversion temperature. The inversion pressure
P_{i}
is calculated from
\mu=0
via the eq.
(\ref{muc})
as,

P_{i}=\frac{3Q^{2}}{8\pi\beta r_{+}}\,.

(67)

Substituting
P_{i}
to
T
in eq.
(\ref{pi})
, the inversion temperature is obtained as,

T_{i}=\frac{2Q^{2}}{\pi r_{+}}

(68)

By eliminating
\frac{Q^{2}}{r_{+}}
from both
P_{i}
and
T_{i}
, one can obtain a relation between the inversion temperature and inversion pressure as,

T_{i}=\frac{16\beta}{3}P_{i}\,.

(69)

It can be observed that the relation is independent of the charge
Q
. It only depends on
\beta
. The inversion temperature increases monotonically with the inversion pressure
P_{i}
.
There is only one inversion curve for the dilaton black hole. In contrast, for the Van der Waals fluid, there are two inversion curves as shown in
[
46
]
. The relation between
T_{i}
and
P_{i}
is given in the Fig.
(\ref{tipi})
. Furthermore, the dilaton black hole does not have critical behavior such as black holes that have demonstrated behavior as the van der Waals fluids as shown in section (3.1). Hence a ratio between
T_{i}
and
T_{c}
does not exists. This behavior is similar to the BTZ black hole as shown in
[
47
]
.

The minimum inversion temperature,
T^{min}_{i}
is obtained at
P_{i}=0
which is,

T^{min}_{i}=0\,.

(70)

Hence,
T_{i}^{min}
occurs when the black hole is extreme. This is similar to the case for the BTZ black hole
[
47
]

Thermodynamics of the black hole for the general N case

In this section we will address thermodynamics of the dilaton black holes for general
N
values for the metric in eq.
(\ref{metric1})
, eq.
(\ref{metric2})
and eq.
(\ref{metric3})
.
First we will replace the mass
m
with mass
M
in
f(r)
with the following relations as given in
[
39
]
,

M=\frac{\gamma\,m}{8}\,

(71)

f(r)=-\frac{16Mr^{1-\frac{N}{2}}}{N\gamma}-\frac{8\Lambda\beta^{2-N}r^{N}}{(3N-2)N}+\frac{8Q^{2}}{(2-N)N}\,.

(72)

In order to address thermodynamics of black holes, some comments for
N\neq 1
cases are in order: There is no general method to find black hole event horizons for
N\neq 1
case as discussed in
[
21
]
. In
[
21
]
it was demonstrated that black hole event horizons exists for
2>N>\frac{2}{3}
for
\Lambda<0
. Since it is not possible to find
r_{+}
values for a general
N
value even within
2>N>\frac{2}{3}
, Chan and Mann
[
21
]
discussed examples for
N=\frac{6}{5},\frac{6}{7},\frac{4}{3},\frac{4}{5}
. In this paper, we will choose
N=\frac{6}{7}
to discuss specific values of thermodynamical quantities. First, we will present thermodynamical quantities for a general
N
value and later will present detailed analysis of thermodynamics of the black hole for
N=\frac{6}{7}
.

Following Ref.
[
39
]
we can obtain the entropy

S=\frac{2\pi r_{+}R(r_{+})}{4}=\frac{\pi}{2}\gamma\,r_{+}^{N/2}\,.

(73)

Considering the fact that
f(r_{+})=0
we get

M=\frac{\gamma}{2}r_{+}^{\frac{N}{2}-1}\left(-\frac{\Lambda\beta^{2-N}r_{+}^{N}}{3N-2}-\frac{Q^{2}}{N-2}\right)\,.

(74)

5.1
First law of black hole thermodynamics

If we rewrite the mass
M
in terms of
S,P,Q
, we obtain,

M(S,P,Q,\beta)=\frac{2^{5-\frac{2}{N}}\pi^{-2+\frac{2}{N}}P\beta^{2-N}\gamma^{\frac{2}{N}-2}}{(3N-2)S^{\frac{2}{N}-3}}+\frac{\pi^{\frac{2}{N}-1}Q^{2}\gamma^{2/N}}{(2-N)4^{1/N}S^{\frac{2}{N}-1}}\,.

(75)

We can write the following expression for the first law of thermodynamics as,

dM=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial S}\right)_{P,Q,\beta}dS+\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial P}\right)_{S,Q,\beta}dP+\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial q}\right)_{S,P,\beta}dQ+\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial\beta}\right)_{S,P,Q}d\beta\,\,\,,

(76)

or

dM=TdS+VdP+\Phi dQ+{\cal{B}}d\beta\,\,\,.

(77)

Then the temperature can be calculated from the first law of black hole thermodynamics as

T=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial S}\right)_{P,Q,\beta}=\frac{-\Lambda\beta^{2-N}r_{+}^{N}-Q^{2}}{N\pi r_{+}}\,.

(78)

The Hawking temperature obtained using the surface gravity is

T_{H}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left|\frac{dg_{tt}}{dt}\sqrt{-g^{tt}g^{rr}}\right|_{r_{+}}=\frac{2M(N-2)r_{+}^{-N/2}}{\pi N\gamma}-\frac{2\Lambda\beta^{2-N}r_{+}^{N-1}}{\pi(3N-2)}=\frac{-\Lambda\beta^{2-N}r_{+}^{N}-Q^{2}}{N\pi r_{+}}\,,

(79)

which is equal to
T
.

If
P=-\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi}
, then one can obtain the corresponding thermodynamic volume as,

V=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial P}\right)_{S,Q,\beta}=\frac{4\pi\beta^{2-N}\gamma\,r_{+}^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}}{3N-2}\,,

(80)

which is different from the geometric volume.
The electric potential at the horizon is given by

\Phi=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial Q}\right)_{S,P,\beta}=\frac{Q\gamma}{(2-N)r_{+}^{1-\frac{N}{2}}}\,.

(81)

Finally we get the thermodynamic quantity
\cal{B}
conjugate to the parameter
\beta

{\cal{B}}=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial\beta}\right)_{S,P,Q}=-\frac{(N-2)\Lambda\beta^{1-N}\gamma\,r_{+}^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}}{2(2-3N)}\,.

(82)

5.2
Smarr formula

Now to obtain the Smarr formula we follow the scaling arguments of Ref.
[
42
]

\left(\frac{N}{2}-1\right)M=\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial S}\right)S+(-2)\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial P}\right)P+(0)\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial Q}\right)Q+(1)\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial\beta}\right)\beta\,\,\,,

(83)

namely

\left(\frac{N}{2}-1\right)M=\frac{N}{2}TS-2VP+{\cal{B}}\beta\,\,\,.

(84)

5.3
Thermodynamical quantities

We will calculate few more thermodynamical quantities for
N\neq 1
case as follows:

C_{{P,Q}}=\frac{-N\pi\gamma r^{N/2}_{+}\left(\beta^{2}\Lambda r^{N}_{+}+Q^{2}\beta^{N}\right)}{4Q^{2}\beta^{N}-4(N-1)\beta^{2}\Lambda r^{N}_{+}}\,.

(85)

Since the enthalpy is given by
M
, which is equal to
U+PV
, one can compute
U
as follows:

U=M-PV=\frac{\gamma Q^{2}r_{+}^{\frac{N}{2}-1}}{4-2N}\,.

(86)

If we write
U
as a function of temperature
T
and pressure
P
, it is,

U=-\frac{\pi\gamma Nr_{+}^{\frac{N}{2}}T}{4-2N}+\frac{8\pi\beta^{2-N}\gamma r_{+}^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}P}{4-2N}

(87)

Combining eq.
(\ref{tempN})
and
\Lambda=-8\pi P
, one can obtain the state equation as,

P=\frac{NT}{8\beta^{2-N}r_{+}^{N-1}}+\frac{Q^{2}}{8\pi r_{+}^{N}\beta^{2-N}}\,.

(88)

For the general case with
N\neq 1
, the Gibbs free energy is given by the following expression

G=-\frac{\beta^{-N}\gamma\,r^{\frac{N}{2}-1}_{+}\left[(3N-2)\beta^{N}Q^{2}-\left(N^{2}-3N+2\right)\beta^{2}\Lambda r^{N}_{+}\right]}{N\left(3N^{2}-8N+4\right)}\,\,\,.

(89)

6
Conclusion

In this paper we have studied the static charged dilaton black hole in 2+1 dimensions. We have studied thermodynamics in the extended phase space where
P=-\Lambda/8\pi
. We have separated the paper into two major groups:
N=1
case and
N=6/7
case. We will discuss those two cases below.

6.1
N=1
case

For
N=1
case, the black hole has two horizons given the mass
M>\gamma Q\sqrt{-\beta\Lambda}
. It can have extreme black holes or naked singularities. The first law for the black hole is derived with
\beta
as a thermodynamical quantity. A new thermodynamical quantity
\cal{B}
is introduced as the conjugate quantity to
\beta
. Introduction of
\beta
as a thermodynamical quantity is essential to have the first law to be satisfied. Thermodynamic volume is not the geometric volume. For
N=1
case, the temperature
T
approaches
-\frac{\Lambda\beta}{\pi}
and does not have a local maxima or a minima. Other thermodynamical quantities such as specific heat, internal energy, Helmholtz free energy, Gibbs free energy are derived and Maxwells relations are derived. Here, black holes are locally stable and there are no phase transitions. Reverse Isoperimetric Inequality is satisfied for certain values of the parameters in the theory. This behavior is unlike the charged BTZ black hole where the Reverse Isoperimetric Inequality is violated. Joule Thomson expansion is studied in detail and Joule Thomson coefficient
\mu
is calculated. It is clear that
\mu
diverges at
T=0
where the black hole is extreme. Inversion temperature is calculated and is observed that it is proportional to the inversion pressure
P_{i}
. Isenthalpic curves are plotted and we have discussed how the black hole heat or cool with respect to the inversion curve.

6.2
N=\frac{6}{7}
case

We have derived thermodynamical quantities such as temperature, entropy, thermodynamic volume, electric potential, specific heats, internal energy, and Gibbs free energy for a general
N
value. The first law and the Smarr relations are presented.
\beta
is treated as a thermodynamical quantity in order to have the first law to be satisfied.

There is no general method to compute black hole horizons for a general
N
value. According to the authors in
[
21
]
. It was demonstrated that black holes existed for
2>N>\frac{2}{3}
for
\Lambda<0
and black holes with
N=\frac{6}{5},\frac{6}{7},\frac{4}{3},\frac{4}{5}
were discussed in
[
21
]
. In this paper, we have chosen
N=\frac{6}{7}
as an example for
N\neq 1
case to analyze thermodynamics. Black hols with such an
N
value has the possibility to have two horizons, degenerate or no horizons depending on the mass and charge. In the extended phase space where
P=-\Lambda/(8\pi)
, the pressure has a minimum when plotted against
r_{+}
. Hence black holes can only exist for the pressure greater than a certain value. The temperature has a maxima (
T_{max})
and black holes cannot exist with temperature greater than
T_{max}
. Black holes are locally stable for a range of
r_{+}
. The range lies in between when the black holes are extreme and at when the temperature is extreme. In order to understand the global stability we have studied the Gibbs free energy. Small black holes are preferred state for smaller temperature values and the thermal AdS space is preferred after
T_{max}
. Hence, there is a first order phase transition between small black holes and the thermal AdS space. This is very similar to the phase transition for the Schwarzschild AdS black hole sin 4 dimensions referred to as hawking-Page phase transition.

Hence the charged dilaton black hole has presented with interesting behavior as opposed to the charged BTZ black hole. In extending this work, it would be interesting how adding spin to these black hole will change the thermodynamical behavior. Hence it would be interesting to study the spinning dilaton black holes presented by Chan and Mann in
[
22
]
.
