A new proof of Nishioka’s theorem in Mahler’s method
Boris Adamczewski, Colin Faverjon

Abstract
. In a recent work [ 3 ] , the authors established new results about general linear Mahler systems in several variables from the perspective of transcendental number theory, such as a multivariate extension of Nishioka’s theorem. Working with functions of several variables and with different Mahler transformations leads to a number of complications, including the need to prove a general vanishing theorem and to use tools from ergodic Ramsey theory and Diophantine approximation ( e.g. , a variant of the p p -adic Schmidt subspace theorem). These complications make the proof of the main results proved in [ 3 ] rather intricate. In this article, we describe our new approach in the special case of linear Mahler systems in one variable. This leads to a new, elementary, and self-contained proof of Nishioka’s theorem, as well as of the lifting theorem more recently obtained by Philippon [ 22 ] and the authors [ 1 ] . Though the general strategy remains the same as in [ 3 ] , the proof turns out to be greatly simplified. Beyond its own interest, we hope that reading this article will facilitate the understanding of the proof of the main results obtained in [ 3 ] .



1.
Introduction

Throughout this paper, we let
q\geq 2
denote a fixed integer. An
M_{q}
-function
is a power series
f(z)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[[z]]
satisfying
a linear equation of the form

p_{0}(z)f(z)+p_{1}(z)f(z^{q})+\cdots+p_{m}(z)f(z^{q^{m}})=0\,,

where
p_{0}(z),\ldots,p_{m}(z)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[z]
are not all zero.
In the study of
M_{q}
-functions, it is often more convenient to consider, instead of linear Mahler equations, linear systems of functional equations of the form

(1.1)

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{1}(z)\\
\vdots\\
f_{m}(z)\end{array}\right)=A(z)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{1}(z^{q})\\
\vdots\\
f_{m}(z^{q})\end{array}\right)\,,

where
A(z)\in{\rm GL}_{m}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z))
and
f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[[z]]
. Then, each power series
f_{i}(z)
is an
M_{q}
-function. We recall that an
M_{q}
-function
is meromorphic in the open unit disc of
\mathbb{C}
(see, for instance,
[
9
, Théorème 31]
). Furthermore, it admits the unit circle as a natural boundary, unless it is a rational function
[
24
, Théorème 4.3]
.
A point
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
is said to be

regular
with respect to (
1.1
) if the matrix
A(\alpha^{q^{k}})
is both well-defined and invertible
for all integers
k\geq 0
.

In this framework, the main aim of Mahler’s method is to transfer results about the absence of algebraic
(resp. linear) relations between the functions
f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z)
over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
to the absence
of algebraic (resp. linear) relations over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
between their values at non-zero algebraic points lying in the open unit disc (assuming, of course, that these values are well-defined).
In 1990, Ku. Nishioka
[
19
]
proved the following theorem, which is the analog of the Siegel-Shidlovskii theorem
in the theory of Siegel
E
-functions (see
[
25
]
).
Given a field
\mathbb{K}
, a field extension
\mathbb{L}
of
\mathbb{K}
, and elements

a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
in
\mathbb{L}
,
we let
{\rm tr.deg}_{\mathbb{K}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
denote the transcendence degree over

\mathbb{K}
of the field extension
\mathbb{K}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
.

Theorem 1.1

(Nishioka’s theorem)
.

Let
f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z)
be
M_{q}
-functions related by a
q
-Mahler system of the form (
1.1
) and let
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
,

0<|\alpha|<1
, be regular with respect to this system. Then

{\rm tr.deg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}(f_{1}(\alpha),\ldots,f_{m}(\alpha))={\rm tr.deg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)}(f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z))\,.

Nishioka’s theorem is undoubtedly a landmark result in Mahler’s method, but it also suffers from some limitation which prevent it to cover important applications (see the discussion in Sections 1 and 2 of
[
1
]
and also the results in
[
2
]
). For such applications, the following refinement of Nishioka’s theorem, which we called
lifting theorem
(or
théorème de permanence
in French), is needed.

Theorem 1.2

(Lifting theorem)
.

Let
f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z)
be
M_{q}
-functions related by a
q
-Mahler system of the form (
1.1
) and let
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
,

0<|\alpha|<1
, be regular with respect to this system. Then for any homogenous polynomial
P\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]
such that

P(f_{1}(\alpha),\ldots,f_{m}(\alpha))=0\,,

there exists a polynomial
\overline{P}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[z,X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]
, homogeneous in
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
, such that

\displaystyle\overline{P}(z,f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z))=0

and

\displaystyle\overline{P}(\alpha,X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})=P(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}).

Again, Theorem
1.2
has an analog in the theory of
E
-functions: the lifting theorem proved by Beukers
[
8
]
using André’s theory of arithmetic Gevrey series
[
5
,
6
]
.
A slightly weaker version of Theorem
1.2
was first proved by Philippon
[
22
]
. Theorem
1.2
was then deduced in
[
1
]

from Philippon’s lifting theorem. In
[
1
,
22
]
, the lifting theorem is derived from Nishioka’s theorem. Thanks to the work of André
[
7
]
, pursued by Naguy and Szamuely
[
17
]
, we now have a general
approach based on a suitable Galois theory of linear differential and difference equations that allows one to deduce theorems of the type of Theorem
1.2
from theorems of the type of
Theorem
1.1
.

The proof of Nishioka’s theorem deeply relies on tools from commutative algebra, related to elimination theory, which were introduced and developed by Nesterenko in the framework of transcendental number theory at the end of the 1970s (see, for instance,
[
18
]
). Recently, Fernandes
[
11
]
observed that Nishioka’s theorem can also be derived from a general
algebraic independence criterion due to Philippon
[
23
]
. However, Philippon’s criterion is also based on the same tools, so that, in the end, both proofs rely on the same argument. The proof of Nishioka’s theorem has the advantage that it can be quantified (see, for instance,
[
19
]
),
leading to algebraic independence measures. Its main deficiency is that it can hardly be generalised to Mahler systems in several variables.

In this note, we use the approach recently introduced by the authors
[
3
]
to provide new and more elementary proofs of both Nishioka’s theorem and the lifting theorem.
This approach takes its roots in the original one initiated by Mahler
[
16
]
and developed much later by Kubota
[
13
]
, Loxton and van der Poorten
[
15
]
, and Nishioka
[
20
,
21
]
. The main improvement comes from the introduction of the so-called
relation matrices
whose existence is ensured by Hilbert Nullstellensatz.
In contrast with
[
1
,
22
]
, we first prove the lifting theorem and then deduce Nishioka’s theorem by using a classical
argument, as in Shidlovskii’s proof of the Siegel-Shidlovskii theorem (see
[
25
]
or
[
10
]
). Beyond its elementary aspect, this new approach has the great advantage of being generalisable within the framework of Mahler’s method in several variables, as has been done in
[
3
]
. We hope that reading first
this article will facilitate the understanding of the proof of the main results in
[
3
]
.
In order to avoid the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
being buried in tedious computations, we occasionally just outline the main argument and provide more detail in the appendix at the end of the paper.
There, we also prove some auxiliary results
that can be used to make our proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
as elementary and self-contained as possible.

2.
Lifting the linear relations

We first prove Theorem
1.2
in the particular case of linear relations.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z)
be
M_{q}
-functions related by a
q
-Mahler system of the form (
1.1
), and let
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
,
0<|\alpha|<1
, be regular with respect to this system. Let
L\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]
be a linear form such that

L(f_{1}(\alpha),\ldots,f_{m}(\alpha))=0\,.

Then, there exists
\overline{L}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[z,X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]
, linear in
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
, such that

\displaystyle\overline{L}(z,f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z))=0

and

\displaystyle\overline{L}(\alpha,X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})=L(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})\,.

The proof of this theorem is dividing in three subsections. We first establish the existence and properties of some special matrices
which can be associated with a linear Mahler system. We call them the
relation matrices
. Then, we construct an auxiliary function and use it to prove a
key lemma
about the structure of the linear relations between
f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z)
. Finally, we show how this lemma allows us to lift any linear relation over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
between
f_{1}(\alpha),\ldots,f_{m}(\alpha)
into a linear relation over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
between
f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z)
.
Throughout this section, we keep the notation of Theorem
2.1
.

2.1.
Notation

Let
d
be a positive integer and
R
be a commutative ring.
Given an indeterminate
x
, we let
R[[x]]
denote the ring of formal power series with coefficients in
R
. If
R\subset\mathbb{C}
,
we let
R\{x\}
denote the ring of convergent power series with coefficients in
R
, that is those elements of
R[[x]]
that are analytic in some neighborhood of the origin.
Given a
d
-tuple of non-negative integers

{\boldsymbol{k}}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})
, we set
|{\boldsymbol{k}}|:=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{d}
.
If
X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}
are indeterminates, we set
\boldsymbol{X}^{\boldsymbol{k}}:=X_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots X_{d}^{k_{d}}
.
The total degree of a polynomial in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}]
is defined by

\deg\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in K}a_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right):=\max\{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|:{\boldsymbol{k}}\in K\,,\,a_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\not=0\}\,.

Given an
m\times n
matrix
M:=(m_{i,j})
with coefficients in
R
and
an
m\times n
matrix
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=(\mu_{i,j})
with nonnegative integer coefficients, we set

M^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq m\\
1\leq j\leq n\end{subarray}}m_{i,j}^{\mu_{i,j}}\,.

We use the standard Landau notation
\mathcal{O}
. We also use the notation
\gg
as follows.
Writing that some property holds true for all integers
\lambda\gg 1
means that the corresponding property holds true for all
\lambda
large enough;
writing that some property holds true for all integers

\lambda_{1}\gg\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
means
that the corresponding property holds true for all
\lambda_{1}
that is sufficiently large w.r.t.
\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{3}
; writing that some property holds true for all integers

\lambda_{1}\gg\lambda_{2}\gg\lambda_{3}
means
that the corresponding property holds true for all
\lambda_{1}
that is sufficiently large w.r.t.
\lambda_{2}
,
assuming that
\lambda_{2}
is itself sufficiently large w.r.t.
\lambda_{3}
.

2.2.
Relation matrices

To shorten the notation, we set

{\boldsymbol{f}}(z):=(f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z))^{\top}\,.

For every integer
k\geq 0
, we set

A_{k}(z):=A(z)A(z^{q})\cdots A(z^{q^{k-1}})\,,

so that
A_{0}(z)={\rm I}_{m}
, the identity matrix of size
m
,
A_{1}(z)=A(z)
, and

(2.1)

{\boldsymbol{f}}(z)=A_{k}(z){\boldsymbol{f}}(z^{q^{k}})\,,\quad\quad\forall k\geq 0\,.

Let
\boldsymbol{Y}:=(y_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}
denote a matrix of indeterminates. Given a field
\mathbb{K}
and a non-negative integer
\delta_{1}
, we let
\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{Y}]_{\delta_{1}}

denote the set of polynomials of degree at most
\delta_{1}
in each indeterminate

y_{i,j}
. Given two non-negative integers
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
, we let

\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
denote the set of polynomials
P\in\mathbb{K}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]
of degree at most
\delta_{1}
in every indeterminate
y_{i,j}
and of degree at most
\delta_{2}
in
z
.
The identity theorem and the fact that
\alpha
is a regular point with respect to (
1.1
) ensure that
every polynomial
P\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)[\boldsymbol{Y}]
is well-defined at the point
(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})
for all
k\gg 1
.
Set

\mathcal{I}:=\{P\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)[\boldsymbol{Y}]\ :\ P(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})=0\,,\;\forall k\gg 1\}\,.

2.2.1.
Estimates for the dimension of certain vector spaces

Let
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
be two non-negative integers. Set

\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1}):=\mathcal{I}\cap\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)[\boldsymbol{Y}]_{\delta_{1}}
and

\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2}):=\mathcal{I}\cap\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
.
Note that
\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
is a vector subspace of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
, and
let
\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
denote a complement to
\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
in

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
d(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
denote the dimension of

\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
There exists a positive integer
c_{1}(\delta_{1})
, that does not depend on
\delta_{2}
, such that

d(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\sim c_{1}(\delta_{1})\delta_{2}\,,\mbox{ as $\delta_{2}$ tends to infinity. }

Proof.

Set
h:=(\delta_{1}+1)^{m^{2}}
and let
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{h}
denote an enumeration of the set of all
matrices in
{\mathcal{M}}_{m}(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})
whose entries are at most
\delta_{1}
. Any polynomial
P\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)[\boldsymbol{Y}]_{\delta_{1}}

has a unique decomposition of the form

P(\boldsymbol{Y},z):=\sum_{j=1}^{h}p_{j}(z)\boldsymbol{Y}^{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{j}}\,,

where
p_{j}(z)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
,
1\leq j\leq h
.
Since, by definition,
\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1})
does not contain any non-zero elements of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, it is a strict
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
-subspace of

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)[\boldsymbol{Y}]_{\delta_{1}}
.
Thus, there exist an integer
d\geq 1
and
d
vectors of polynomials
(b_{i,1}(z),\ldots,b_{i,h})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[z]^{h}
,
1\leq i\leq d
,
which are linearly independent over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
and such that
for all
p_{1}(z),\ldots,p_{h}(z)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
:

(2.2)

\sum_{j=1}^{h}p_{j}(z)\boldsymbol{Y}^{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{j}}\in\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1})\Leftrightarrow\sum_{j=1}^{h}b_{i,j}(z)p_{j}(z)=0\;\;\;\;\forall i,\;1\leq i\leq d\,.

Since these polynomials only depend on
\delta_{1}
(and
\mathcal{I}
), there exists
\delta^{\prime}_{1}\geq 0
, which only depends on
\delta_{1}

(and
\mathcal{I}
), such that

b_{i,j}(z)=:\sum_{\kappa=0}^{\delta_{1}^{\prime}}b_{i,j,\kappa}z^{\kappa}\,,\quad\quad b_{i,j,\kappa}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\,.

Let us consider

P(\boldsymbol{Y},z)=\sum_{j=1}^{h}p_{j}(z)\boldsymbol{Y}^{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{j}}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
and set

p_{j}(z)=:\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}}p_{j,\lambda}z^{\lambda}\,,

where the numbers
p_{j,\lambda}
belong to
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
and
p_{j,\lambda}:=0
if
\lambda>\delta_{2}
or
\lambda<0
.
By (
2.2
),
P
belongs to
\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
if and only if

(2.3)

\sum_{j=1}^{h}\sum_{\kappa=0}^{\delta_{1}^{\prime}}b_{i,j,\kappa}p_{j,\gamma-\kappa}=0\,,\quad\quad\forall(\gamma,i)\,,\,0\leq\gamma\leq\delta_{2}+\delta^{\prime}_{1}\,,\,1\leq i\leq d\,.

The number of linearly independent equations in (
2.3
) is equal to the dimension of
\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
. As
\delta_{2}
tends to infinity, it is equivalent to the number of linearly independent equations in (
2.3
) such that
\delta^{\prime}_{1}\leq\gamma\leq\delta_{2}
.
When
\gamma
,
\delta^{\prime}_{1}\leq\gamma\leq\delta_{2}
, is fixed, the number of linearly independent equations in (
2.3
) does not depend on
\gamma
. Hence there exists a positive integer
c(\delta_{1})
which does not depend on
\delta_{2}
such that

\dim\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\sim c(\delta_{1})\delta_{2}\,,\mbox{ as $\delta_{2}$ tends to infinity,}

as wanted. A more detailed argument is provided in Section
A.1
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

For every pair of non-negative integers
(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
, one has

\dim\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(2\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\leq 2^{m^{2}}\dim\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\,.

Proof.

Every
P\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{2\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
can be decomposed as

(2.4)

P(\boldsymbol{Y},z)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{2^{m^{2}}}e_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y})^{\delta_{1}}P_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y},z)\,,

where we let
e_{1}(\boldsymbol{Y}),\ldots,e_{2^{m^{2}}}(\boldsymbol{Y})
denote the
2^{m^{2}}
distinct monomials of degree at most
1
in each
y_{i,j}
,
and where the polynomials
P_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y},z)
all belong to
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
. If each polynomial
P_{\ell}
belongs to
\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
then
P\in\mathcal{I}(2\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
. Hence, the decomposition (
2.4
) defines a linear map

\left(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}/\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\right)^{2^{m^{2}}}\mapsto\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{2\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}/\mathcal{I}(2\delta_{1},\delta_{2})

that is surjective. The result follows.
∎

2.2.2.
Nullstellensatz and relation matrices

In this section, we show how Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz allows us to ensure the existence of a matrix
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
,
whose coordinates are all algebraic over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
, and
which we call a
relation matrix
. Such a matrix encodes the linear relations over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
between the functions

f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z)
and is the cornerstone of the proof
of Theorem
2.1
.

We first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

The set
\mathcal{I}
is a radical ideal of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)[\boldsymbol{Y}]
.

Proof.

Checking that
\mathcal{I}
is an ideal of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)[\boldsymbol{Y}]
is not difficult. If
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
,
then
P_{1}+P_{2}
vanishes at
(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})
for all
k\gg 1
and hence
P_{1}+P_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
.
Now let
P_{1}\in\mathcal{I}
and
P_{2}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)[\boldsymbol{Y}]
. On the one hand,
P_{1}(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})=0

for all
k\gg 1
and

P_{2}(\boldsymbol{Y},z)
is well-defined at
(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})
for
k\gg 1
. We deduce that

P_{1}(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})P_{2}(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})=0\quad\quad\forall k\gg 1\,.

Hence
P_{1}P_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
. Let
P\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)[\boldsymbol{Y}]
be such that
P^{r}\in\mathcal{I}
for some
r
. If
k
is a non-negative integer such that
P(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})^{r}=0
, then
P(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})=0
.
Hence
P\in\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{I}
is a radical ideal.
∎

Throughout this article, we let
\mathbb{A}\subset\bigcup_{d\geq 1}\overline{\mathbb{Q}}((z^{1/d}))
denote the algebraic closure of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
in the field of Puiseux series. By the Newton-Puiseux Theorem,
\mathbb{A}
is algebraically closed.

Lemma 2.5
.

There exists a matrix
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)\in{\rm GL}_{m}(\mathbb{A})
such that

P({\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z),z)=0\,,

for all polynomials
P\in\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

Let us consider the affine algebraic set
\mathcal{V}
associated with the radical ideal
\mathcal{I}
. That is,

{\mathcal{V}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)\in\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathbb{A})\ :\ P({\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z),z)=0\,,\;\forall P\in\mathcal{I}\}\,.

According to the weak form of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz (see, for instance,
[
14
, Theorem 1.4, p. 379]
),

\mathcal{V}
is non-empty as soon as

\mathcal{I}
is a proper ideal of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)[\boldsymbol{Y}]
. But the definition of
\mathcal{I}
implies that non-zero constant polynomials do not belong to

\mathcal{I}
. Hence
\mathcal{V}
is non-empty.

Now, let us assume by contradiction that
\det{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)=0
for all
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)
in
\mathcal{V}
.
By Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz (see, for instance,
[
14
, Theorem 1.5, p. 380]
),
the polynomial
\det\boldsymbol{Y}
belongs to the radical of the ideal

\mathcal{I}
. Hence
\det\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathcal{I}
for
\mathcal{I}
is radical. Thus,
\det A_{k}(\alpha)=0

for
k\gg 1
. This provides a contradiction since
A_{k}(\alpha)
is invertible
for all
k\geq 0
. We thus deduce that there exists an invertible matrix
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)

in
\mathcal{V}
, as wanted.
∎

Definition 2.6
.

A matrix
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)\in{\rm GL}_{m}(\mathbb{A})
satisfying the property of Lemma

2.5
is called a
relation matrix
.

The next lemma plays a central role in the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)\in{\rm GL}_{m}(\mathbb{A})

be a relation matrix.
Then

P\left({\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)A_{k}(z),z^{q^{k}}\right)=0\,,

for all
P\in\mathcal{I}
and all
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
P\in\mathcal{I}
,
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)\in{\rm GL}_{m}(\mathbb{A})
be a relation
matrix, and
k
be a non-negative integer. Set
Q(\boldsymbol{Y},z):=P(\boldsymbol{Y}A_{k}(z),z^{q^{k}})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)[\boldsymbol{Y}]
.
For every
\ell\gg 1
, the polynomial
Q(\boldsymbol{Y},\alpha^{q^{\ell}})
is well-defined and we have

Q(A_{\ell}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{\ell}})=P(A_{\ell}(\alpha)A_{k}(\alpha^{q^{\ell}}),(\alpha^{q^{\ell}})^{q^{k}})=P(A_{k+\ell}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k+\ell}})=0\,,

since
A_{\ell}(\alpha)A_{k}(\alpha^{q^{\ell}})=A_{k+\ell}(\alpha)
. Hence
Q\in\mathcal{I}
and

P({\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)A_{k}(z),z^{q^{k}})=Q({\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z),z)=0\,,

as wanted.
∎

2.2.3.
Analyticity and relation matrices

We address now the question of the analyticity of relation matrices.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)\in{\rm GL}_{m}(\mathbb{A})
be a relation matrix. Then the three following properties holds for
k\gg 1
.

(a)

The point
\alpha^{q^{k}}
belongs to the disc of convergence of each of the functions

f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z)
.

(b)

Each coordinate of
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)
defines an analytic function on some neighborhood of
\alpha^{q^{k}}
.

(c)

The matrix
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\alpha^{q^{k}})
is invertible.

Proof.

Since

\lim_{k\to\infty}\alpha^{q^{k}}=0
and
f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z)
are analytic on some neighborhood of
0
, Property (a) holds for
k\gg 1
. Recall that an algebraic function has only finitely many singularities and finitely many zeros. Hence, for
k\gg 1
,
\alpha^{q^{k}}
is neither a singularity of one of the coordinates of
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)
nor a zero of
\det{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)
. We deduce that Properties (b) and (c) hold for
k\gg 1
.
∎

2.3.
The key Lemma

Let

L(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})=:\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tau_{j}X_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}]\,

be defined as in Theorem
2.1
.
Set
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}:=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{m}
and
\boldsymbol{X}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})^{\top}
, so that
L(\boldsymbol{X})={\boldsymbol{\tau}}\boldsymbol{X}
.
Given a matrix of indeterminates

\boldsymbol{Y}:=(y_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}
, we set

F(\boldsymbol{Y},z):=\sum_{i,j}\tau_{i}y_{i,j}f_{j}(z)={\boldsymbol{\tau}}\boldsymbol{Y}{\boldsymbol{f}}(z)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\{z\}[\boldsymbol{Y}]\,,

where we recall that
{\boldsymbol{f}}(z):=(f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z))^{\top}
. Note that
F
is a linear form in
\boldsymbol{Y}
.
Evaluating at
({\rm I}_{m},\alpha)
, where
{\rm I}_{m}
is the identity matrix of size
m
, we obtain that

(2.5)

F({\rm I}_{m},\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau_{i}f_{i}(\alpha)=L({\boldsymbol{f}}(\alpha))=0\,.

Remark 2.9
.

We have
F(\boldsymbol{Y},z)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{f}}(z)]\subset\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\{z\}[\boldsymbol{Y}]
. Also,
F(\boldsymbol{Y},z)
can be seen as an element of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y}][[z]]
,
as we will sometimes do in what follows.

2.3.1.
Iterated relations

For every
k\geq 0
, Equality (
2.1
) implies the following equality in
\mathbb{A}[\boldsymbol{Y}]
:

(2.6)

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{Y},z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\boldsymbol{Y}{\boldsymbol{f}}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\boldsymbol{Y}A_{k}(z){\boldsymbol{f}}(z^{q^{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{Y}A_{k}(z),z^{q^{k}})\,.

The point
\alpha
being regular with respect to (
1.1
),
we deduce from (
2.5
) that

(2.7)

F(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})=0\,,\quad\quad\forall k\geq 0\,.

2.3.2.
The matrices
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z)

From now on, we fix a relation matrix
{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}(z)
and a non-negative integer
k_{0}

satisfying the properties of Lemma

2.8
. Set

(2.8)

\xi:=\alpha^{q^{k_{0}}}\,.

Item (a) in Lemma
2.8
ensures the existence of a positive
real number
r_{1}<1
such that
0<|\xi|<r_{1}
and such that all the power series

f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z)
have a radius of convergence larger than
r_{1}
.
Then, by Item (b) in the same lemma, we can choose

r_{2}>0
satisfying
0<|\xi|+r_{2}<r_{1}
and such that the
coefficients of the matrix
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)
are analytic on the disc
\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{2})
.
For every
k\geq k_{0}
, we set

(2.9)

{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z):=A_{k_{0}}(\alpha){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\alpha^{q^{k_{0}}})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)A_{k-k_{0}}(z)\,

so that we have
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(\xi)=A_{k}(\alpha)
, for every
k\geq k_{0}
.

Remark 2.10
.

By Lemma
2.8
, the coefficients of

{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k_{0}}(z)
are analytic on the disc

\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{2})
. On the other hand, one has

{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z)={\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k-1}(z)A(z^{q^{k-1-k_{0}}})\,,\quad\quad\forall k>k_{0}\,.

This implies that, for every
k\geq k_{0}
, the coefficients of

{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z)
are analytic on some neighborhood of
\xi
, that is on some disc

\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{k})\subset\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{2})
.
In what follows, we will consider the expression

F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})
. Formally, it is a polynomial in

f_{1}(z^{q^{k-k_{0}}}),\ldots,f_{m}(z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})
and the coordinates of

{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z)
.
Note that it also defines an analytic function on

\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{k})\subset\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{2})
.
In addition,
F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k_{0}}(z),z)
is analytic on

\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{2})
.
Indeed, the functions
f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z)
are analytic on

\mathcal{D}(0,r_{1})\supset\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{2})
, while our choice of

k_{0}
ensures that the coordinates of

{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k_{0}}(z)
are analytic on
\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{2})
.

2.3.3.
The key lemma

The end of the section is devoted to proof of the following result.

Lemma 2.11
.

One has

F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k_{0}}(z),z)=0
.

In what follows, we argue by contradiction, assuming that

(2.10)

F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k_{0}}(z),z)\not=0\,.

We divide the proof of Lemma
2.11
into the four steps (AF), (UB), (NV), and (LB),
following the classical proof scheme in transcendental number theory. In Step (AF) we build an
auxiliary function
by considering some sort of Padé approximant of type I for the first powers of
F(\boldsymbol{Y},z)
. In Step (UB) we compute some
upper bound
for the absolute value of the evaluation of our auxiliary function at
(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})
, for large
k
, by means of analytic estimates. In Step (NV) we prove that our auxiliary function is
non-vanishing
at
(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})
for infinitely many
k
. In Step (LB),
we provide a
lower bound
for the absolute value of the evaluation of our auxiliary function at
(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})
, for infinitely many
k
, by using Liouville’s inequality. Finally, we show that the steps (UB) and (LB) lead to a contradiction.

Step (AF).

Given a formal power series
E:=\sum_{\lambda\geq 0}e_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{Y})z^{\lambda}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y}][[z]]

and an integer
p>0
, we let

E_{p}:=\sum_{\lambda=0}^{p-1}e_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{Y})z^{\lambda}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]

denote the truncation of
E
at order
p
with respect to
z
.
We recall that

\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
is a complement to

\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\delta_{1}\geq 0
and
\delta_{2}\gg\delta_{1}
be two integers.
Let
p:=\left\lfloor\frac{\delta_{1}\delta_{2}}{2^{m^{2}+2}}\right\rfloor
.
Then there exist polynomials
P_{i}\in\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
,

0\leq i\leq\delta_{1}
, not all zero,
such that the formal power series

E(\boldsymbol{Y},z):=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\delta_{1}}P_{j}(\boldsymbol{Y},z)F(\boldsymbol{Y},z)^{j}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y}][[z]]

satisfies

E_{p}({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})=0
for all
k\geq k_{0}
.

Proof.

Set

\mathcal{J}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2}):=\{P\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[z,\boldsymbol{Y}]\ :\ P(A_{k_{0}}(\alpha){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\alpha^{q^{k_{0}}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{Y},z)\in\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\}\,.

The
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
-vector spaces
\mathcal{J}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
and
\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
have same dimension.
This follows directly from the fact that the map

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}&\rightarrow&\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}\\
P(\boldsymbol{Y},z)&\mapsto&P(A_{k_{0}}(\alpha){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\alpha^{q^{k_{0}}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{Y},z)\end{array}

is an isomorphism, the matrix
A_{k_{0}}(\alpha){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\alpha^{q^{k_{0}}})^{-1}
being invertible.
Furthermore, we have

(2.11)

P({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})=0\,,\quad\quad\forall P\in\mathcal{J}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2}),\;\forall k\geq k_{0}\,.

Indeed, if

P\in\mathcal{J}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
, then

P(A_{k_{0}}(\alpha){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\alpha^{q^{k_{0}}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{Y},z)\in\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
, and Lemma

2.7
implies that

P(A_{k_{0}}(\alpha){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\alpha^{q^{k_{0}}})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)A_{k}(z),z^{q^{k}})=0\,,\quad\quad\forall k\geq 0\,.

For
k\geq k_{0}
, replacing
k
by
k-k_{0}
in the previous equality, we obtain that

P(A_{k_{0}}(\alpha){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\alpha^{q^{k_{0}}})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)A_{k-k_{0}}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})=0\,.

By (
2.9
), we thus have
P({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})=0
.

Let
p
be as in the lemma and let us consider
the three
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
-linear maps:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\right)^{\delta_{1}+1}\\
(P_{0}(\boldsymbol{Y},z),\ldots,P_{\delta_{1}}(\boldsymbol{Y},z))\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\big\downarrow

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y}]_{2\delta_{1}}[[z]]\\
E(\boldsymbol{Y},z):=\sum_{j=0}^{\delta_{1}}P_{j}(\boldsymbol{Y},z)F(\boldsymbol{Y},z)^{j}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\big\downarrow

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{2\delta_{1},p-1}\\
E_{p}(\boldsymbol{Y},z)\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\big\downarrow

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{2\delta_{1},p-1}/\mathcal{J}(2\delta_{1},p-1)\\
E_{p}(\boldsymbol{Y},z)\mod\mathcal{J}(2\delta_{1},p-1)\end{array}\right.

Note that these maps are well-defined.
By Lemma
2.2
, the dimension of the
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
-vector space
\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})

is at least equal to
\frac{c_{1}(\delta_{1})}{2}\delta_{2}
, assuming that
\delta_{2}
is large enough.
It follows that

(2.16)

\dim_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\left(\left(\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\right)^{\delta_{1}+1}\right)\geq\frac{c_{1}(\delta_{1})}{2}(\delta_{1}+1)\delta_{2}\,.

For every pair of non-negative integers
(u,v)
, set

\overline{\mathcal{J}}(u,v):=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{u,v}/\mathcal{J}(u,v)\,.

Since
\mathcal{J}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
and
\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
have same dimension,
Lemma
2.3
implies that

\dim_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\overline{\mathcal{J}}(2\delta_{1},p-1)\leq 2^{m^{2}}\dim_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\overline{\mathcal{J}}(\delta_{1},p-1)\,.

Now, if
\delta_{2}
is sufficiently large, Lemma
2.2
ensures that

\dim_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\overline{\mathcal{J}}(\delta_{1},p-1)\leq 2c_{1}(\delta_{1})p\,.

On the other hand, the choice of
p
ensures that

2^{m^{2}}\left(2c_{1}(\delta_{1})p\right)<\frac{c_{1}(\delta_{1})}{2}(\delta_{1}+1)\delta_{2}

and (
2.16
) implies that

\dim_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\left(\left(\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\right)^{\delta_{1}+1}\right)>\dim_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\left(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},z]_{2\delta_{1},p-1}/\mathcal{J}(2\delta_{1},p-1)\right)\,.

Hence the
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
-linear map defined by

(P_{0}(\boldsymbol{Y},z),\ldots,P_{\delta_{1}}(\boldsymbol{Y},z))\mapsto E_{p}(\boldsymbol{Y},z)\mod\mathcal{J}(2\delta_{1},p-1)

has a non-trivial kernel.
We deduce the existence of polynomials
P_{0},\ldots,P_{\delta_{1}}
in
\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
,
not all zero,
such that

E_{p}\in\mathcal{J}(2\delta_{1},p-1)
.
By (
2.11
), we obtain that
E_{p}({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})=0
for all
k\geq k_{0}
.
This ends the proof.
∎

Let
E\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y}][[z]]
be a formal power series satisfying the properties of Lemma
2.12
and
let
v_{0}
be the smallest
index such that the polynomial
P_{v_{0}}
is non-zero. Then the formal power series

\mathfrak{E}(\boldsymbol{Y},z):=\sum_{j\geq v_{0}}P_{j}(\boldsymbol{Y},z)F(\boldsymbol{Y},z)^{j-v_{0}}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y}][[z]]\,

is the auxiliary function that we were looking for. Note that we have

(2.17)

\mathfrak{E}(\boldsymbol{Y},z)F(\boldsymbol{Y},z)^{v_{0}}=E(\boldsymbol{Y},z)\,.

Warning.

The function
\mathfrak{E}({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})
can be though of as a
simultaneous Padé approximant of type I for the first

\delta_{1}
th powers of
F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})
. However, we have to be careful:

F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})
it is not necessarily a power series in
z
. It is a linear combination of

f_{1}(z^{q^{k-k_{0}}}),\ldots,f_{m}(z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})

whose coefficients are only known to be algebraic over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
. We only know that

F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})
is analytic in some neighborhood of the point
\xi
.

Step (UB).

The aim of this step is to prove that there exists a real number
c_{2}>0
such that

(2.18)

|\mathfrak{E}(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})|\leq e^{-c_{2}q^{k}\delta_{1}\delta_{2}}\,,\quad\quad\forall k\gg\delta_{2}\gg\delta_{1}\,.

According to Remark
2.10
, the functions

\mathfrak{E}({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})
,
F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k_{0}}(z),z)^{v_{0}}
, and

E({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})
are
all analytic on the disc
\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{k})
. Hence they respectively have power series expansions of the form

(2.19)

\displaystyle\quad\mathfrak{E}({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda=0}^{+\infty}e_{\lambda,k}(z-\xi)^{\lambda}\,,\quad e_{\lambda,k}\in\mathbb{C}\,,

(2.20)

\displaystyle F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k_{0}}(z),z)^{v_{0}}

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda=0}^{+\infty}a_{\lambda}(z-\xi)^{\lambda}\,,\quad a_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{C}\,,

(2.21)

\displaystyle\quad E({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda=0}^{+\infty}\epsilon_{\lambda,k}(z-\xi)^{\lambda}\,,\quad\epsilon_{\lambda,k}\in\mathbb{C}\,.

We need the following result whose proof is postponed after the end of the argument for proving our main upper bound (
2.18
).

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
p
be defined as in Lemma
2.12
.
There exists a real number
\gamma>0
that does not depend on the integers

\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
,
\lambda
, and
k
, and such that

|\epsilon_{\lambda,k}|\leq e^{-\gamma q^{k}p}\,,\quad\quad\forall k\gg\delta_{2}\gg\delta_{1},\lambda\,.

Using (
2.6
), we get that

F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})=F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k_{0}}(z),z)\,,\;\;\forall k\geq k_{0}\,.

By (
2.17
), we thus have

(2.22)

\mathfrak{E}({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k_{0}}(z),z)^{v_{0}}=E({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})\,,

for all
k\geq k_{0}
and all
z\in\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{k})
.
We use now our assumption that
F({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k_{0}}(z),z)
is non-zero (see (
2.10
)). There thus exists at least
one non-zero coefficient
a_{\lambda}
in (
2.20
). Let us consider the least integer
\lambda_{0}

such that
a_{\lambda_{0}}\not=0
.
Identifying the coefficients of

(z-\xi)^{\lambda_{0}}
in the power series expansion of both sides of
(
2.22
) with the help of (
2.19
), (
2.20
), and (
2.21
),
we obtain that

(2.23)

e_{0,k}a_{\lambda_{0}}=\epsilon_{\lambda_{0},k}\,,\;\;\forall k\geq k_{0}\,.

Since
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(\xi)=A_{k}(\alpha)
(see (
2.9
)) and
a_{\lambda_{0}}
depends only on
\delta_{1}
but not on
k
, we infer from
Lemma
2.13
, Equality (
2.23
),
and the definition of
p
(see Lemma
2.12
),
the existence of a real number
c_{2}>0
that does not depend on

\delta_{1}
,
\delta_{2}
, and
k
,
such that

\displaystyle\left|\mathfrak{E}(A_{k}(\alpha),\alpha^{q^{k}})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\mathfrak{E}({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(\xi),\xi^{q^{k-k_{0}}})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|e_{0,k}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\epsilon_{\lambda_{0},k}|/|a_{\lambda_{0}}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-c_{2}q^{k}\delta_{1}\delta_{2}}\,,\quad\quad\forall k\gg\delta_{2}\gg\delta_{1}\,.

This proves the upper bound (
2.18
), as wanted.

Now, it remains to prove Lemma
2.13
.

Proof of Lemma
2.13
.

Set

G(\boldsymbol{Y},z):=E(\boldsymbol{Y},z)-E_{p}(\boldsymbol{Y},z)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\{z\}[\boldsymbol{Y}]\,,

where
p
is defined as in Lemma
2.12
.
By Lemma
2.12
, we have

(2.24)

G({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})=E({\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}(z),z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})\,,\quad\quad\forall k\geq k_{0}\,.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{s}
denote an enumeration of all the
m\times m
matrices with coefficients in the set
\{0,1,\ldots,2\delta_{1}\}
.
There exists a unique decomposition of the form

G(\boldsymbol{Y},z)=:\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{\lambda=p}^{\infty}g_{\lambda,i}z^{\lambda}\boldsymbol{Y}^{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{i}}\,,

where
g_{\lambda,i}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
For every
i
,
1\leq i\leq s
, we define the formal power series

G_{i}(z):=\sum_{\lambda=p}^{\infty}g_{\lambda,i}z^{\lambda}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[[z]]\,.

By definition of
F(\boldsymbol{Y},z)
, these series belong to
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[z,{\boldsymbol{f}}(z)]
. In particular, they are analytic on some
disc
\mathcal{D}(0,r)
with
r>r_{1}
(where
r_{1}
is defined at the beginning of Section
2.3.2
).
From the Cauchy-Hadamard inequality, there exists a positive real number
\gamma_{1}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})

such that

(2.25)

|g_{\lambda,i}|\leq\gamma_{1}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})r_{1}^{-\lambda}\,,\quad\quad\forall\lambda\geq 0\,.

For every
k\geq k_{0}
,
G_{i}(z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})
can thus be written as

(2.26)

G_{i}(z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})=:\sum_{\lambda=q^{k-k_{0}}p}^{\infty}g_{\lambda,i,k}z^{\lambda}\,,

with
g_{\lambda,i,k}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. Furthermore, this power series is absolutely convergent
on the disc
\mathcal{D}(0,r_{1})
. Since
r_{1}\leq 1
, we deduce from (
2.25
) that

(2.27)

|g_{\lambda,i,k}|\leq\gamma_{1}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})r_{1}^{-\lambda q^{k_{0}-k}}\leq\gamma_{1}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})r_{1}^{-\lambda}\,,

for all
\lambda\geq 0
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
, and
k\geq k_{0}
.
On the other hand, every function

G_{i}(z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})
,
1\leq i\leq s
,
k\geq k_{0}
, is analytic on the disc
\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{k})
.
Thus, we can write

(2.28)

G_{i}(z^{q^{k-k_{0}}})=:\sum_{\lambda=0}^{\infty}h_{\lambda,i,k}(z-\xi)^{\lambda}\,,

where
h_{\lambda,i,k}\in\mathbb{C}
.
Since by assumption
\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{k})\subset\mathcal{D}(0,r_{1})
,
the two power series expansions (
2.26
) and (
2.28
)
match on
\mathcal{D}(\xi,r_{k})
. Using the equality

(2.29)

z^{\gamma}=((z-\xi)+\xi)^{\gamma}=\sum_{\lambda=0}^{\gamma}\binom{\gamma}{\lambda}\xi^{\gamma-\lambda}(z-\xi)^{\lambda}

and identifying, for every
\lambda\geq 0
, the coefficients of

(z-\xi)^{\lambda}
in (
2.26
) and (
2.28
),
we deduce that

(2.30)

h_{\lambda,i,k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\geq q^{k-k_{0}}p\\
\gamma\geq\lambda\end{subarray}}\binom{\gamma}{\lambda}g_{\gamma,i,k}\xi^{\gamma-\lambda}\,.

For
\gamma\geq\lambda
, one has

(2.31)

\binom{\gamma}{\lambda}=\frac{\gamma!}{(\gamma-\lambda)!\lambda!}\leq\gamma^{\lambda}\,.

Given
\lambda\geq 0
, we have that

\lambda<q^{k-k_{0}}p
as soon as
k
is large enough, and
since
|\xi|<r_{1}
, we infer from
(
2.27
) and (
2.30
)
the existence of a real number
\gamma_{2}>0
that does not
depend on
\delta_{1}
,
\delta_{2}
,
\lambda
, and
k
,

In this section, we end the proof of Theorem

.
In order to deduce Theorem

.

4.

In this section, we show how to deduce Nishioka’s theorem from the lifting theorem.

Proof of Theorem

.

We first note that the inequality

always holds. Hence we only have to prove that

Let

in

We also let

denote the dimension of the
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)

d
in

Note that the functions

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\

\hline\cr&&&\\
&&A(z)&\\

Using (

Remark 4.1

In the proof of Theorem

and

can be easily achieved by elementary means (see Section

).

Remark 4.2
.

At the end of our proof of Theorem
1.2
, we used the fact that the field extension
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z,f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z))
is a regular extension of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
. We stress that this argument is not needed to deduce Nishioka’s theorem. Indeed,
without using it, we still obtain that every

-linear relation between

can be lifted into a linear relation over the algebraic closure

of

between

. Then we could reproduce the previous argument, just replacing

{\rm tr.deg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)}(f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z))={\rm tr.deg}_{\mathbb{A}}(f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{m}(z))\,,

1

\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{C}

, and where the absolute values

A.3.
Kronecker product

For the sake of completeness, we provide a proof of the basic properties that we used about Kronecker product in Section
3
.

Lemma A.2
.

The following properties hold.

(i)

If
A
,
B
,
C
, and
D
are matrices such that the products
AB
and

CD
are well-defined, then

(AB)\otimes(CD)=(A\otimes C)(B\otimes D)
.

(ii)

If
A
and
B
are two square matrices respectively of size
m
and
n
, then

\det(A\otimes B)=(\det A)^{n}(\det B)^{m}\,.

In particular, if
A
is a square matrix of size
m
and
d\geq 1
is an integer, then

\det A^{\otimes d}=(\det A)^{md}\,.

Proof.

Let us first prove (i). Set
A:=(a_{i,j})_{i,j}
and
B:=(b_{j,k})_{j,k}
. Then we have the block matrix decompositions

A\otimes C=(a_{i,j}C)_{i,j},\ \text{ and }\ B\otimes D=(b_{j,k}D)_{j,k}\,.

According to this block decomposition, the
(i,k)
th block of
(A\otimes C)(B\otimes D)
is

\sum_{j}a_{i,j}b_{j,k}CD\,.

In the mean time,
AB=(\sum_{j}a_{i,j}b_{j,k})_{i,k}
and thus, the
(i,k)
th block of
(AB)\otimes(CD)
is precisely

\sum_{j}a_{i,j}b_{j,k}CD
.

Now, let us prove (ii).
Consider the matrix
A\otimes{\rm I}_{n}
. After a suitable permutation of the rows and the columns,
one obtains the matrix
{\rm I}_{n}\otimes A
, which is a block diagonal matrix. Hence

\det(A\otimes{\rm I}_{n})=\det({\rm I}_{n}\otimes A)=(\det A)^{n}\,.

Similarly,
\det({\rm I}_{m}\otimes B)=(\det B)^{m}
. We infer from (i) that

A\otimes B=(A{\rm I}_{m})\otimes({\rm I}_{n}B)=(A\otimes{\rm I}_{n})({\rm I}_{m}\otimes B)\,.

Hence

\det(A\otimes B)=\det({\rm I}_{n}\otimes A)\det({\rm I}_{m}\otimes B)=(\det A)^{n}(\det B)^{m}

as wanted. The last property follows now by induction on
d
.
∎

A.4.
Hilbert’s theorem about transcendence degree

In Section
3
,
we use a result of Hilbert to deduce Theorem
1.1
from Theorem
1.2
.
Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field,
\mathbb{L}
be a field extension of
\mathbb{K}
,
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}\in\mathbb{L}
, and
\varphi(d)
denote the dimension of the

\mathbb{K}
-vector space formed by the polynomials in
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}
of total degree at most
d
. Hilbert’s theorem states that
\varphi(d)
is a polynomial with degree

t:={\rm tr.deg}_{\mathbb{K}}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m})
for all
d
large enough.
As mentionned in Remark
4.1
, we do not need the full strength of Hilbert’s theorem.
The following elementary lemma is indeed sufficient to deduce Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma A.3
.

We continue with the notation above. There exist two positive real numbers
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
that does not depend on
d
and such that

\gamma_{1}d^{t}\leq\varphi(d)\leq\gamma_{2}d^{t},\quad\forall d\geq 1\,.

Proof.

Suppose first that
t=0
. Then,
\mathbb{K}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m})
is a finitely generated extension of
\mathbb{K}
which is algebraic over
\mathbb{K}
. It follows that it has finite degree, say
\delta
, over
\mathbb{K}
. Then, for any
d
,

1\leq\varphi(d)\leq\delta\,.

Taking
\gamma_{1}=1
and
\gamma_{2}=\delta
, this proves the lemma when
t=0
. Suppose now that
t\geq 1
. Without any loss of generality, we can assume that
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{t}
are algebraically independent over
\mathbb{K}
.

We first prove the lower bound. By assumption, all the monomials in
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{t}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{K}
.
Since there are
\binom{d+t}{t}
distinct monomials in
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{t}
of degree at most
d
, we obtain that

\varphi(d)\geq\binom{d+t}{t}\geq\frac{d^{t}}{t!}\,\cdot

Taking
\gamma_{1}=(t!)^{-1}
, we obtain the expected lower bound.

Now, let us prove the upper bound by induction on
m\geq t
, the integer
t
being fixed. Suppose that
m=t
, then

\varphi(d)=\binom{d+t}{t}\leq(t+1)d^{t}\,.

We assume now that
m>t
and that the upper bound holds when considering
m-1
elements in
\mathbb{L}
.
By assumption,
\xi_{m}
is algebraic over
\mathbb{K}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1})
.
We first prove the case where
\xi_{m}
is integer over
\mathbb{K}[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1}]
. In that case, there exist an integer
\delta\geq 1
and polynomials
P_{0},\ldots,P_{\delta-1}\in\mathbb{K}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m-1}]
such that

\xi_{m}^{\delta}=\sum_{i=0}^{\delta-1}P_{i}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1})\xi_{m}^{i}\,.

Let
d_{0}
denote the maximum of the degree of
P_{0},\ldots,P_{\delta-1}
. Let
d\geq\delta
, then any polynomial of
degree at most
d
in
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}
can be decomposed as

Q_{0}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1})+Q_{1}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1})\xi_{m}+\cdots+Q_{\delta-1}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1})\xi_{m}^{\delta-1}\,,

where
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{\delta-1}
are polynomials of degree at most
(1+d_{0})d
. By induction, there exists a positive number

\gamma
such that there are at most

\gamma((1+d_{0})d)^{t}

linearly independent polynomials in
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1}
whose degree is at most
(1+d_{0})d
.
Setting
\gamma_{2}:=\delta\gamma(1+d_{0})^{t}
, we obtain the expected upper bound when

\xi_{m}
is integer over
\mathbb{K}[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1}]
.

In the general case, there exists a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{K}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m-1}]
such that
p(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1})\xi_{m}
is
integer over
\mathbb{K}[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1}]
. Let
d_{1}
denote the degree of
p
. Then the multiplication by
p(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1})^{d}
induces an injection between the vector space of polynomials of degree at most
d
in
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}
and the vector space of polynomials of degree at most
d(d_{1}+1)
in
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1},p(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1})\xi_{m}
.
The dimension of the latter is at most

\gamma(d_{1}+1)^{t}d^{t}

for some
\gamma>0
, since
p(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1})\xi_{m}
is integer over
\mathbb{K}[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m-1}]
.
Setting
\gamma_{2}:=\gamma(d_{1}+1)^{t}
, we obtain the expected upper bound.
∎
