1.
Introduction

Let
w
be a word in the free group
F_{d}
. For any group
G
, we have the following map called
word map

w\colon G^{d}\rightarrow G

given by evaluation
(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{d})\mapsto w(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{d})
. The image
w(G^{d})
of
w
is simply denoted by
w(G)
.
Some of the general questions in the subject are about image sizes, fibers, and Waring-like problems. Several fundamental results have been proved for finite simple and quasisimple groups (see, for example,
[
LST11
,
GLOST18
]
). In
[
Lu14
]
, Lubotzky characterized that exactly those subsets of a finite simple group that are invariant under automorphisms of the group and contain identity are images of some word. For nilpotent groups, this need not be true, which is studied in
[
KKK22
]
. In this article, we explore another notion based on properties of images of word maps, namely chirality, for some finite groups of Lie type.

A group
G
is said to be
chiral
if there is some word
w
such that
w(G)\neq w(G)^{-1}
. Otherwise,
G
is said to be
achiral
. A word
w
for which
w(G)\neq w(G)^{-1}
is said to be a
witness of chirality
. All Abelian groups are achiral since word maps (equivalently, power maps) are homomorphisms. In
[
CH19
]
, it is shown that the Mathieu group
M_{11}
is chiral, and a witness to chirality is obtained. In
[
CH18
]
, the chirality of certain nilpotent groups is explored. In this article, we aim to study the property of chirality for the alternating groups, finite classical groups, and some exceptional groups. This problem is closely related to the problem of reality (see Lemma
2.1
) and the understanding of automorphisms for these groups. In Section
3
, we see that most classical groups are achiral.

However, when we look at exceptional groups, it turns out
\mathrm{G}_{2}(q)
, when
char(\mathbb{F}_{q})\neq 2,3
, is chiral for certain
q
. More precisely (see Corollary
6.2
) the group
\mathrm{G}_{2}(q)
is chiral if and only if
q
satisfies one of the following conditions:

(1)

the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has a primitive
3^{rd}
root of unity and doesn’t have a primitive
9^{th}
root of unity, i.e.,
q\equiv 1\mod 3
and
q\not\equiv 1\mod 9
.

(2)

\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
(the group of elements with norm
1
) contains a primitive
3^{rd}
root of unity and
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
doesn’t have
9^{th}
primitive root of unity, i.e.,
q\equiv 2\mod 3
and
q^{2}\not\equiv 1\mod 9
.

Due to Lemma
2.1
, the search for chirality requires identifying non-real elements in this group which are not fixed by field automorphisms. It turns out that there is a pair of non-real conjugacy classes, that is, there are two non-real conjugacy classes when they exist (depending on
q
). In Section
6
, first, we determine the non-real conjugacy classes which are of independent interest (see Theorem
6.1
). The study of real conjugacy classes done in
[
ST05
,
ST08
]
reduces our task to finding isolated matrices (see Definitions
4.1
and
5.1
) in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
and
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
. Then, we use this to establish that
\mathrm{G}_{2}(q)
is chiral (Corollary
6.2
). We hope to explore other exceptional groups in the future for chirality, too.

2.
Preliminaries

We study the property of chirality for some finite simple groups of Lie type, which is closely related to the problem of reality. An element
g\in G
is
real
if
g^{-1}=xgx^{-1}
for some
x\in G
, and
G
is called
real
if every element of
G
is real.
First, we recall a few basic results to build criteria to test chirality. We begin with recalling
[
CH18
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a group such that for every
g\in G
there is a homomorphism
\varphi\colon G\rightarrow G
, that may depend on
g
, such that
\varphi(g)=g^{-1}
. Then
G
is achiral.

The following is an immediate consequence.

Corollary 2.2
.

If a group
G
is real, then
G
is achiral.

Proof.

Since
G
is real, for every
g
in
G
there is some
x\in G
such that
g^{-1}=xgx^{-1}
. Choose
\varphi
to be the inner automorphism
\mathrm{Int}_{x}
and apply Lemma
2.1
.
∎

In this article, we will provide several examples of achiral groups which are not real groups (for example, see Section
3
). Note that even when a group
G
is not real, as long as its elements are mapped to their inverses by a homomorphism, it is achiral. The following basic lemma will be useful in our further explorations.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
1\longrightarrow N\overset{i}{\longrightarrow}G\overset{\pi}{\longrightarrow}Q\longrightarrow 1
be a short exact sequence of groups. Then the following hold:

(1)

If
G
is achiral, then so is
Q
.

(2)

If
G
is real then
N
is achiral.

Proof.

(1)

This follows from
[
CH18
, Proposition 4.1]
.

(2)

Let
n\in N
then
n^{-1}=gng^{-1}
for some
g\in G
as
G
is real. Therefore the map
\varphi:N\rightarrow N
given by
\varphi(x)=gxg^{-1}
(for all
x\in N
) is an automorphism of
N
(as
N\lhd G
) such that
\varphi(n)=n^{-1}
. Thus by Lemma
2.1
,
N
is achiral.

∎

3.
Chirality of classical groups

In this section, we explore various classical groups and show several examples of achiral groups. We note that the automorphisms of groups of Lie type are well known due to the work of Dieudonné
[
Di51
]
and Steinberg
[
St60
]
. We use these automorphisms to show that elements of the group under consideration can be conjugated to its inverse by an automorphism (not necessarily an inner automorphism).

Proposition 3.1
.

(1)

The alternating group
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is achiral.

(2)

The projective general linear group
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(k)
and its covering groups are achiral.

(3)

If
G
is a group of type
A_{n-1}
, then
G
is achiral.

(4)

The orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}_{n}(k)
is achiral. Thus, simple groups of Lie type
B_{l}
and
D_{l}
are achiral.

Proof.

(1)

Since
\mathcal{A}_{n}\lhd S_{n}
and
S_{n}
is a real group then, in view of Lemma
2.3
(
2
),
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is achiral.

(2)

Let
g\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(k)
then
{}^{t}g=xgx^{-1}
for some
x\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(k)
. Let
\gamma
be the automorphism of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(k)
given by
\gamma(y)={}^{t}y^{-1}
for all
y\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(k)
.
Then

\varphi(g)=\mathrm{Int}_{x^{-1}}\circ\gamma(g)=\mathrm{Int}_{x^{-1}}({{}^{t}}g^{-1})=(x^{-1}{}^{t}gx)^{-1}=g^{-1}.

Thus, by Lemma
2.1

\mathrm{GL}_{n}(k)
is achiral. Note that in view of Lemma
2.3
(
1
)
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(k)/C
is also achiral, for any central subgroup
C
of
\mathcal{Z}(\mathrm{GL}_{n}(k))
.

(3)

Let
g\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(k)
then
{}^{t}g=xgx^{-1}
for some
x\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(k)
. Thus the map

\varphi_{x^{-1}}\colon\mathrm{SL}_{n}(k)\rightarrow\mathrm{SL}_{n}(k)

given by
\varphi_{x^{-1}}(h)=x^{-1}hx
(for all
h\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(k)
) is an automorphism of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(k)
. Now, consider the automorphism
\gamma
of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(k)
given by
\gamma(h)={}^{t}h^{-1}
for all
h\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(k)
. Therefore the automorphism
\psi:=\varphi_{x^{-1}}\circ\gamma
of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(k)
satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\psi(g)=\varphi_{x^{-1}}\circ\gamma(g)=\varphi_{x^{-1}}({}^{t}g^{-1})=x^{-1}{}^{t}g^{-1}x=g^{-1}.

Hence by Lemma
2.1

\mathrm{SL}_{n}(k)
is achiral. Which in turn implies that
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(k)/C
is also achiral by Lemma
2.3
(
1
) for any central subgroup
C
of
\mu_{n}=\mathcal{Z}(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(k))
. In particular,
\mathrm{PSL}_{n}(k)
is achiral.

(4)

Since
\mathrm{O}_{n}(k)
is real then
\mathrm{SO}_{n}(k)
and its derived subgroup
\Omega_{n}(k)
is achiral (by Lemma
2.3
(
2
)). Therefore by Lemma
2.3
(
1
),
\mathrm{P\Omega_{n}}(k)
is also achiral.

∎

We note that by
[
FZ82
, Corollary C]

\mathrm{Spin}_{2l+1}(q)
is achiral.

Remark 3.2
.

We don’t have to assume that
G
is finite in the above result.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field of odd characteristic. Consider the
unitary group

\mathrm{U}_{n}(q):=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q^{2})\mid{}^{t}\overline{g}g=I_{n}\},

and the symplectic group

\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q):=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}_{2n}(q)\mid{}^{t}gJg=J\},\text{ where }J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}.

The extended symplectic group is
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}^{\pm}(q):=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}_{2n}(q)\mid{}^{t}gJg=\pm J\}.

Proposition 3.3
.

(1)

The unitary group
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
is achiral.

(2)

If
G
is a finite group of type
{}^{2}A_{n-1}
, then
G
is achiral.

Proof.

(1)

Let
g\in\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
. Two elements of
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
are conjugate in
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
if and only if they are conjugate in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q^{2})
(by
[
Ma81
, (6.1)]
). Since
{}^{t}g\in\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
, and
g
and
{}^{t}g
are conjugate in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q^{2})
, we have that
{}^{t}g=xgx^{-1}
for some
x\in\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
. Thus the automorphism
\psi:=\mathrm{Int}_{x^{-1}}\circ\gamma
of
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
satisfies
\psi(g)=g^{-1}
, where
\gamma(y)={}^{t}y^{-1}
for all
y\in\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
. Hence, by Lemma
2.1

\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
is achiral.

(2)

For any
g\in\mathrm{SU}_{n}(q)
, the automorphism
\psi:=\varphi_{x^{-1}}\circ\gamma
of
\mathrm{SU}_{n}(q)
satisfies
\psi(g)=g^{-1}
by similar computation. Thus by Lemma
2.1

\mathrm{SU}_{n}(q)
is also achiral. This completes the proof by Lemma
2.3
(
1
).

∎

Proposition 3.4
.

If
G
is a finite group of Lie type of
C_{n}
, then
G
is achiral.

Proof.

First note that if
k
is a finite field of characteristic
2
, then
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(k)
is real, and hence achiral by Corollary
2.2
.
If
q\equiv 1\;(\mathrm{mod}\;4)
, then
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
is real (see
[
FZ82
, Theorem E (i)]
), which in turn implies that
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
is achiral by Corollary
2.2
. Now if
q\equiv 3\;(\mathrm{mod}\;4)
, then by
[
FZ82
, Theorem E(ii)]
every element is conjugate to its inverse by an element of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}^{\pm}(q)
. Since
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
is a normal subgroup of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}^{\pm}(q)
, then for every element
g\in\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
there is an automorphism
\varphi
(say) of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
such that
\varphi(g)=g^{-1}
. Hence, by Lemma
2.1

\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
is achiral in this case, too. Therefore by Lemma
2.3
(
1
)
\mathrm{PSp}_{2n}(q)
is also achiral.
∎

4.
Isolated matrices in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)

To study chirality of
\mathrm{G}_{2}(q)
, we would require to understanding certain types of matrices in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
and
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
. We do this in this section and the following one. We begin with a definition.

Definition 4.1
.

A matrix
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
will be called
isolated
if
A
is neither conjugate to
{}^{t}\!A
, nor to
A^{-1}
.

Note that being isolated is a property of conjugacy class. The following lemma is an immediate consequence of the definition.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
is isolated, then so are
{}^{t}\!A,A^{-1}
and
{}^{t}\!A^{-1}
.

Before proceeding further, we note the following. To understand the conjugacy class of
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(q)
, one often examines if its conjugacy class in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
is a union of more than one conjugacy class of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(q)
. Representatives of the classes in the union are conjugates of
A
by an element of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
. This phenomenon is referred to as the
splitting of a conjugacy class
. This is useful in identifying the cases when a matrix is not isolated. This is because
A
and
{}^{t}\!A
are conjugates in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
; and if the conjugacy class of
A
in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
does not split in
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(q)
then
A
would not be isolated. In the case of
\mathrm{SU}_{n}(q)
, a similar strategy will be used in the next section.

We recall the Lemma 6.6 and Theorem 6.8 from
[
ST05
]
.

Proposition 4.3
.

(1)

Let
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(q)
and

\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)}(A)
be the centralizer of
A
in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
. Let

N_{A}=\det(\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)}(A))\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
.
Then,
g_{1}Ag_{1}^{-1}
is conjugate to
g_{2}Ag_{2}^{-1}
in
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(q)
, where
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
, if and only if
\det(g_{1})\equiv\det(g_{2})\mod N_{A}
.

(2)

Let
A\in\mathrm{SU}_{n}(q)
and

\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)}(A)
be the centralizer of
A
in
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
.
Let
N_{A}=\det(\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)}(A))\subset\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
.
Then,
g_{1}Ag_{1}^{-1}
is conjugate to
g_{2}Ag_{2}^{-1}
in
\mathrm{SU}_{n}(q)
, where
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
, if and only if
\det(g_{1})\equiv\det(g_{2})\mod N_{A}
.

We use this to establish that most of the matrices in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
are not isolated.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
A
be in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
. Suppose, its characteristic polynomial
\chi_{A}(X)
and
the minimal polynomial
m_{A}(X)
do not satisfy
\chi_{A}(X)=(X-\alpha)^{3}=m_{A}(X)
, where
\alpha^{3}=1
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. Then the conjugacy class of
A
in
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)
does not split in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
. Further, in these cases,
A
is not isolated.

Proof.

Using Proposition
4.3
we rule out the possibility of
A
in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
being isolated in all cases except when the characteristic polynomial is equal to the minimal polynomial given by
\chi_{A}(X)=(X-\alpha)^{3}=m_{A}(X)
, where
\alpha^{3}=1
. We note that
A
and
{}^{t}\!A
are always conjugates in
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)
. Thus, if the conjugacy class of
A
in
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)
does not split in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
, the matrix
A
can not be isolated in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
. For this we need to compute
N_{A}=\det(\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)}(A))
and show
N_{A}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
, hence the matrix
A
can not be isolated. If
\chi_{A}(X)
is a degree
3
irreducible polynomial then the
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)}(A)=\mathbb{F}_{q}[A]^{\times}\cong\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}^{*}
. Since the norm map is surjective for finite extensions of finite fields, we are done in this case. When
\chi_{A}(X)=(X-a)p(X)
where
p(X)
is a degree
2
irreducible then the
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)}(A)=\mathbb{F}_{q}[A]^{\times}\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\times\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{*}
and again
N_{A}
is the whole of the set. When
\chi_{A}(X)
has at least
2
distinct roots, we can easily compute the centralizer and show
N_{A}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. This leaves out the case when
\chi_{A}(X)=(X-\alpha)^{3}
. The case that
m_{A}(X)=(X-\alpha)
or
(X-\alpha)^{2}
can be dealt with similarly as the centralizer would be
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)
in the first case and would have a block of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(q)
in the second case. This completes the proof.
∎

Thus, to find an isolated matrix
A
we need to look into the case
m_{A}(X)=(X-\alpha)^{3}
with
\alpha^{3}=1
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
with the minimal polynomial
m_{A}(X)=(X-\alpha)^{3}
. Then, the conjugacy class of
A
in
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)
splits in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
if and only if
q\equiv 1\mod 3
. Hence, if
q\not\equiv 1\mod 3
, the matrix
A
is not isolated.

Proof.

We note that in this case
\chi_{A}(X)=m_{A}(X)=(X-\alpha)^{3}
the matrix
A
is conjugate to
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&1&\\
&\alpha&1\\
&&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
in
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)
. Further, its centralizer in
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)
is given by

\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)}(A)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c\\
&a&b\\
&&a\end{pmatrix}\mid a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*},b,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\}.

Hence, in this case
N_{A}=(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*})^{3}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. Thus, the conjugacy class of
A
in
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)
splits into

|\frac{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}{(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*})^{3}}|=1
or
3
classes in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
be the group homomorphism given by
x\mapsto x^{3}
. Note that the conjugacy class of
A
in
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)
splits if and only if
|\frac{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}{(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*})^{3}}|\neq 1
, which is equivalent to
\ker(\phi)\neq 1
. This, in turn, is equivalent to
3\mid(q-1)
. This proves the required result.
∎

Let us fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
with
\alpha^{3}=1
. Such a non-trivial
\alpha
exists in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
if and only if
q\equiv 1\mod 3
. This is also a condition required for the splitting of the conjugacy class of
A
. For the matrix
A=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&1&\\
&\alpha&1\\
&&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
the conjugacy class of
A
in
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(q)
splits into the following three classes in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
:

A
,
A_{1}=\mathrm{diag}(\alpha,1,1).A.\mathrm{diag}(\alpha,1,1)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\alpha&\\
&\alpha&1\\
&&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
and
A_{2}=\mathrm{diag}(\alpha^{2},1,1).A.\mathrm{diag}(\alpha^{2},1,1)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\alpha^{2}&\\
&\alpha&1\\
&&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

The matrix
A
is conjugate to
{}^{t}\!A
and hence is not an isolated matrix.

Proof.

We check that
\begin{pmatrix}&&-1\\
&-1&\\
-1&&\end{pmatrix}A={}^{t}\!A\begin{pmatrix}&&-1\\
&-1&\\
-1&&\end{pmatrix}
in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

The matrix
A_{1}
(respectively
A_{2}
) is conjugate to
{}^{t}\!A_{1}
(respectively
{}^{t}\!A_{2}
) if and only if
X^{3}=\alpha^{-1}
(respectively
X^{3}=\alpha^{-2}
) has a solution.

Proof.

Let
B=(b_{ij})\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
be such that
BA_{1}={}^{t}\!A_{1}B
. A simple computation leads to
B=\begin{pmatrix}&&b_{13}\\
&b_{13}\alpha&b_{23}\\
b_{13}&b_{23}&b_{33}\end{pmatrix}

with the condition
\det(B)=-b_{13}^{3}\alpha=1
. The required condition is equivalent to
X^{3}=\alpha^{-1}
. A similar calculation can be done for
A_{2}
.
∎

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
char(\mathbb{F}_{q})\neq 2,3
and
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
. Then, the isolated matrices in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
exist if and only if (the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has a primitive
3^{rd}
root of unity and doesn’t have a primitive
9^{th}
root of unity)
q\equiv 1\mod 3
and
q\not\equiv 1\mod 9
. Further, there are exactly two conjugacy classes given by the matrices
A_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\alpha&\\
&\alpha&1\\
&&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
and
A_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\alpha^{2}&\\
&\alpha&1\\
&&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
which are isolated. Here,
\alpha
is a primitive
3^{rd}
root of unity. Further, it turns out that
A_{1}
is conjugate to
{}^{t}\!A_{2}
.

Proof.

From Lemmas
4.4
,
4.5
,
4.6
, it follows that the isolated matrices can be found if and only if
q\equiv 1\mod 3
. Further, the only possibilities for isolated matrices, up to conjugacy, are
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
(and their inverses). From Lemma
4.7
, it follows that
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are not conjugate to their transpose if and only if
\mathbb{F}_{q}
does not have a primitive
9^{th}
root of unity, which happens if and only if
q\not\equiv 1\mod 9
. Further,
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
both are not real because of the eigenvalue considerations.

One can check that
\begin{pmatrix}&&-1\\
&-\alpha^{2}&\\
-\alpha&&\end{pmatrix}A_{1}={}^{t}\!A_{2}\begin{pmatrix}&&-1\\
&-\alpha^{2}&\\
-\alpha&&\end{pmatrix}
.
∎

5.
Isolated matrices in
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)

We note that all hermitian forms over a finite field are equivalent, thus giving conjugate groups as unitary groups. We choose one which makes the calculations convenient, namely the following:
{\mathcal{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}&&1\\
&1&\\
1&&\end{pmatrix}
and

\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)=\{A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q^{2})\mid{}^{t}\!{A}{\mathcal{B}}\bar{A}={\mathcal{B}}\}.

Consider the norm map
N\colon\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
which is
x\mapsto x\bar{x}=x^{1+q}
. Denote the norm
1
elements by
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
, which is of size
q+1
and gives the determinant of
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
. Similar to the case of
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
, we define the notion of isolated matrices here too, which is a property of the conjugacy class.

Definition 5.1
.

A matrix
A\in\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
is called
isolated
if
A
is neither conjugate to
{\bar{A}}^{-1}
nor to
A^{-1}
.

The following is immediate:

Lemma 5.2
.

If
A\in\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
is isolated, then so are
\bar{A}
,
{\bar{A}}^{-1}
, and
A^{-1}
.

To find isolated matrices, we take a similar approach as in the case of
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
. We know that for
A\in\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
we have
A
is conjugate to
{}^{t}\!{\bar{A}^{-1}}
in
\mathrm{U}_{3}(q)
, which is conjugate to
\bar{A}^{-1}
. This is because conjugacy classes of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q^{2})
are in one-to-one correspondence with that of
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
. Thus, by analyzing how conjugacy classes of
A
taken in
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
split in
\mathrm{SU}_{n}(q)
, we will narrow down our problem to a few cases. For this, we use Proposition
4.3
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
A
be in
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
of which the characteristic polynomial and the minimal polynomial do not satisfy
\chi_{A}(X)=(X-\alpha)^{3}=m_{A}(X)
where
\alpha^{3}=1
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
. Then the conjugacy class of
A
in
\mathrm{U}_{3}(q)
does not split in
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
. Further, in these cases,
A
is not isolated.

Proof.

In view of Proposition
4.3
we need to rule out all other cases of
A
in
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
except when the characteristic polynomial is equal to the minimal polynomial given by
\chi_{A}(X)=(X-\alpha)^{3}=m_{A}(X)
where
\alpha^{3}=1
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
. For this, we need to show that in all those cases,
N_{A}=\det(\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{U}_{3}(q)}(A))
is the whole of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
. The centralizer of various representatives of conjugacy classes in
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
is available in
[
SS20
, Section 4]
. Using that, we get our desired result.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
A\in\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
with the minimal polynomial
m_{A}(X)=(X-\alpha)^{3}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
. Then, the conjugacy class of
A
in
\mathrm{U}_{3}(q)
splits in
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
if and
only if (
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
contains a primitive
3^{rd}
root of unity)
q\equiv 2\mod 3
. Hence, if
q\not\equiv 2\mod 3
, the matrix
A
is not isolated.

Proof.

First we find an appropriate representative
A
with
\chi_{A}(X)=m_{A}(X)=(X-\alpha)^{3}
in
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
. Fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
with
\alpha^{3}=1
. We can take
A=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-\alpha^{2}&-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}\\
&\alpha&1\\
&&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
and verify that
{}^{t}\!{A}{\mathcal{B}}\bar{A}={\mathcal{B}}
. Now the centralizer of
A
in
\mathrm{U}_{3}(q)
will have the form
\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&*&*\\
&a&*\\
&&a\end{pmatrix}\mid a\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}\right\}
and hence
N_{A}=(\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1})^{3}
. Thus, the conjugacy class of
A
in
\mathrm{U}_{3}(q)
splits in
|\frac{\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}}{(\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1})^{3}}|=1
or
3
classes
in
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
be the group homomorphism given by
x\mapsto x^{3}
. Note that the conjugacy class of
A
in
\mathrm{U}_{3}(q)
splits if and only if
|\frac{\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}}{(\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1})^{3}}|\neq 1
if and only if
ker(\phi)\neq 1
if and only if
3\mid(q+1)
. This proves the required result.
∎

We note that
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
has a non-trivial
\alpha
if and only if
q\equiv 2\mod 3
. We fix a nontrivial element
\alpha\neq 1
with this property which ensures the existence of a conjugacy class of
A
in
\mathrm{U}_{3}(q)
which splits in
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
as follows:
A
,
A_{1}=\mathrm{diag}(1,\alpha,1).A.\mathrm{diag}(1,\alpha,1)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-\alpha&-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}\\
&\alpha&\alpha\\
&&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
, and

A_{2}=\mathrm{diag}(1,\alpha^{2},1).A.\mathrm{diag}(1,\alpha^{2},1)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-1&-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}\\
&\alpha&\alpha^{2}\\
&&\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

Now we note that,

Lemma 5.5
.

The matrix
A_{1}
is not isolated.

Proof.

Let
g=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,-1)\in\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
. Then
gA_{1}g^{-1}=\bar{A_{1}}^{-1}
. Therefore,
A_{1}
is not an isolated matrix.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

The matrix
A
(respectively
A_{2}
) is conjugate to
{}^{t}\!A
(respectively
{}^{t}\!A_{2}
) if and only if
X^{3}=\alpha
has a solution in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
B=(b_{ij})\in\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
be such that
BA={}^{t}\!AB
. A simple computation leads to
B=\begin{pmatrix}&&b_{13}\\
&-b_{13}\alpha^{2}&b_{23}\\
b_{13}&b_{23}&b_{33}\end{pmatrix}
with the condition
\det(B)=b_{13}^{3}\alpha^{2}=1
. The required condition is equivalent to
X^{3}=\alpha
.
∎

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
char(\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})\neq 2,3
and
A\in\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
. Then, the isolated matrices in
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
exist if and only if (
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
contains a primitive
3^{rd}
root of unity and
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
doesn’t have
9^{th}
primitive root of unity)
q\equiv 2\mod 3
and
q^{2}\not\equiv 1\mod 9
. Further, there are exactly two conjugacy classes given by the matrices
A=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-\alpha^{2}&-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}\\
&\alpha&1\\
&&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
and
A_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-1&-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}\\
&\alpha&\alpha^{2}\\
&&\alpha\end{pmatrix}
which are isolated, where
\alpha
is a primitive
3^{rd}
root of unity.

Proof.

From Lemmas
5.3
,
5.4
,
5.5
, it follows that the only possibility for isolated matrices up to conjugacy is
A
and
A_{2}
if they exist, which is equivalent to
q\equiv 2\mod 3
. From Lemma
5.6
, it follows that
A
and
A_{2}
are not conjugate to their transpose if and only if
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
does not have a primitive
9^{th}
root of unity which is if and only if
q^{2}\not\equiv 1\mod 9
. Further,
A
and
A_{2}
are not real because of the eigenvalue considerations.
∎

6.
Chirality in
\mathrm{G}_{2}(q)

In this section, we will focus on the exceptional group
\mathrm{G}_{2}(q)
and show that it is chiral. We assume that
char(\mathbb{F}_{q})\neq 2,3
. We note that all automorphisms of
\mathrm{G}_{2}(q)
are either inner or field automorphisms in this case. Also, since
\mathrm{G}_{2}(q)
is a finite simple group, it is enough to work with automorphisms (see
[
Lu14
]
). If a group
G
is chiral then there exists an
x
in
G
(said to be a
witness of chirality
) such that
\varphi(x)\neq x^{-1}
for all
\varphi\in\textup{Aut}(G)
. Further, note that only automorphisms are either inner or field automorphisms. Thus our problem reduces to finding non-real elements which are not fixed by field automorphisms. We aim to characterize such elements. First, we determine non-real elements.

Let
k=\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field of characteristic
\neq 2,3
. Real conjugacy classes are discussed in
[
ST05
,
ST08
]
and are classified in terms of strongly real (which are a product of two involutions) classes. We briefly recall some of it. Let
\mathfrak{C}
be an octonion (also called Cayley) algebra over
k
. It is a non-commutative, non-associative algebra of dimension
8
over the field
k
. Since
k
is finite, it is unique up to isomorphism, namely the split octonion algebra. The groups of type
\mathrm{G}_{2}
over
k
are the group of
k
-algebra automorphisms of
\mathfrak{C}
. Again, over a finite field, the group of type
\mathrm{G}_{2}
is split, hence unique up to isomorphism, and is given by the algebra automorphisms of split octonion algebra. Thus, we will take
\mathrm{G}_{2}(q)=\textup{Aut}(\mathfrak{C})
for our purposes. For our work here, we need to find non-real elements in this group. We have the following:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
char(\mathbb{F}_{q})\neq 2,3
. The group
\mathrm{G}_{2}(q)
has non-real conjugacy classes if and only if
q
satisfies one of the following cases:

(1)

The field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has a primitive
3^{rd}
root of unity and doesn’t have a primitive
9^{th}
root of unity, i.e.,
q\equiv 1\mod 3
and
q\not\equiv 1\mod 9
.

(2)

The field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
contains a primitive
3^{rd}
root of unity and
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
doesn’t have
9^{th}
primitive root of unity, i.e.,
q\equiv 2\mod 3
and
q^{2}\not\equiv 1\mod 9
.

Proof.

Let
k=\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\mathfrak{C}
be the octonion algebra over
k
. Let
t\in\textup{Aut}(\mathfrak{C})
. If
t
is unipotent, then by Lemma A.1.1 in
[
ST08
]
, it is strongly real and hence real. Thus, we may assume that we are dealing with
t
non-unipotent. Now, if
t
is not unipotent, then by Lemma A.1.2 in
[
ST08
]
either
t
leaves a quaternion subalgebra invariant or fixes a quadratic étale subalgebra
L
of
\mathfrak{C}
pointwise. If
t
leaves a quaternion subalgebra invariant, then
t
is strongly real and hence real. This leaves us with the case that
t
fixes a quadratic étale subalgebra
L
of
\mathfrak{C}
pointwise. The algebra
L\cong k\times k
or
L
is a quadratic field extension. In the first case
t\in\textup{Aut}(\mathfrak{C}/L)\cong\mathrm{SL}_{3}(k)
and in the second case
t\in\textup{Aut}(\mathfrak{C}/L)\cong\mathrm{SU}_{3}(L)
.

Let us denote the image of
t\in\textup{Aut}(\mathfrak{C})
under the map
\textup{Aut}(\mathfrak{C}/L)\cong\mathrm{SL}_{3}(k)
as
A
when
L\cong k\times k
. Similarly, Let us denote the image of
t\in\textup{Aut}(\mathfrak{C})
under the map
\textup{Aut}(\mathfrak{C}/L)\cong\mathrm{SU}_{3}(L)
when
L
is a quadratic extension of
k
as
A
. Note that
L\cong\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
in our case. Since we are dealing with a finite field, there is a unique hermitian form to get the unitary group (up to conjugacy).

Now, we have
t
a non-unipotent, and further suppose characteristic polynomial
\chi_{A}(X)\neq m_{A}(X)
, the minimal polynomial. Then, from the proof of Theorem A.1.4
[
ST08
]
,
t
is strongly real. More generally, if the characteristic polynomial of
A
is reducible and the minimal polynomial of
A
is not of the form
(X-\alpha)^{3}
, then
t
is strongly real and hence real (see Neumann
[
N90
]
, Satz 6 and Satz 8).

Thus, we are left with the two cases (i)
\chi_{A}(X)=m_{A}(X)=(X-\alpha)^{3}
, and (ii)
\chi_{A}(X)=m_{A}(X)
irreducible of degree three. These are the possible cases where we could have non-real
elements, which we analyze further. We recall the following from
[
ST08
]
Proposition A.2.2 and A.3.2:

(1)

when
L\cong k\times k
,
t
is real in
\textup{Aut}(\mathfrak{C})
if and only if
A
is conjugate to either
A^{-1}
or
{}^{t}\!A
in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(k)
.

(2)

When
L
is a quadratic field extension,
t
is real in
\textup{Aut}(\mathfrak{C})
if and only if either
A
or
\bar{A}
is conjugate to
A^{-1}
in
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(L)
.

Thus, our problem of finding non-real elements reduces to finding isolated matrices in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(q)
and
\mathrm{SU}_{3}(q)
. This is done in Theorem
4.8
and Theorem
5.7
, which completes the proof.
∎

Given Lemma
2.1
and Theorem
6.1
by noting that the non-real elements we get are not fixed by field automorphisms, we conclude the following:

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
char(\mathbb{F}_{q})\neq 2,3
. The group
\mathrm{G}_{2}(q)
is chiral if and only if
q
satisfies one of the following cases:

(1)

the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has a primitive
3^{rd}
root of unity and doesn’t have a primitive
9^{th}
root of unity, i.e.,
q\equiv 1\mod 3
and
q\not\equiv 1\mod 9
.

(2)

\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{1}
contains a primitive
3^{rd}
root of unity and
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
doesn’t have
9^{th}
primitive root of unity, i.e,
q\equiv 2\mod 3
and
q^{2}\not\equiv 1\mod 9
.

Finally, we also note that, although we get two pairs of isolated matrices in the above cases, they give only one pair of non-real classes in
\mathrm{G}_{2}(q)
.
