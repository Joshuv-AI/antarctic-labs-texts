Elementary analysis of isolated zeroes of a polynomial system
Mitali Bafna, Madhu Sudan, Santhoshini Velusamy, David Xiang

Abstract
Wooley ( J. Number Theory , 1996) gave an elementary proof of a Bezout like theorem allowing one to count the number of isolated integer roots of a system of polynomial equations modulo some prime power. In this article, we adapt the proof to a slightly different setting. Specifically, we consider polynomials with coefficients from a polynomial ring 𝔽 ⁡ [ t ] \mathbb{F}[t] for an arbitrary field 𝔽 \mathbb{F} and give an upper bound on the number of isolated roots modulo t s t^{s} for an arbitrary positive integer s s . In particular, using s = 1 s=1 , we can bound the number of isolated roots of a system of polynomials over an arbitrary field 𝔽 \mathbb{F} .

Wooley
[

We start with some basic notation and then state the main theorem.
For positive integer

denote the set
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Throughout this paper we will be working with the multivariate polynomial ring
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
for

for some field
\mathbb{F}
, or
R=\mathbb{F}[t]
the ring of polynomials in

\mathbb{F}

\mathbb{F}

For

. In particular even when

Note

Definition 1.1

(Isolated Zero)
.

For a system of polynomials
\textbf{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\in(\mathbb{F}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}])^{n}
, we say that
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
is an isolated zero of
f
if

f_{i}(\mathbf{a})=0
for every
i\in[n]
and
\det(J(\textbf{f};\mathbf{a}))\neq 0
.
Let
\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f})
denote the number of isolated zeroes of
f
.

For a system of polynomials,
\textbf{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\in(\mathbb{F}[t][X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}])^{n}
and a positive integer
s
, we say that
\mathbf{a}\in(\mathbb{F}[t]/t^{s})^{n}
is an isolated zero of
f
modulo
t^{s}
if

f_{i}(\mathbf{a})=0\pmod{t^{s}}
for every
i\in[n]
and
\det(J(\textbf{f};\mathbf{a}))\neq 0(\pmod{t})
.
Let
\mathcal{N}_{s}(\textbf{f})
denote the number of isolated zeroes of
f
modulo
t^{s}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\textbf{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
be a sequence of polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[t]\Big[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\Big]
with
\deg(f_{i})\leq k_{i}
.
Then for every positive integer
s
, we have
\mathcal{N}_{s}(\textbf{f})\leq k_{1}\cdots k_{n}
.

From the theorem above, we get the following immediate corollary for counting the isolated zeroes of a system of polynomials over fields. This is one of the main implications of Bezout’s theorem.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\textbf{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
be a sequence of polynomials in
\mathbb{F}\Big[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\Big]
with
\deg(f_{i})\leq k_{i}
.
Then
\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f})\leq k_{1}\cdots k_{n}
.

Proof.

The corollary follows by viewing
\textbf{f}\in\mathbb{F}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{n}
as elements of
(\mathbb{F}[t][X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}])^{n}
and noting that an isolated zero
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
of
f
is also an isolated zero modulo
t
of
f
. Thus we have

\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f})\leq\mathcal{N}_{1}(\textbf{f})\leq k_{1}\cdots k_{n}
.
∎

2
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Our proof follows the same outline as that in
[
1
]
. We give an overview here.

We consider the setting where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{F}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
. Roughly the presence of isolated zeroes implies that
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
can not be algebraically dependent. Our first lemma shows a low-degree algebraic dependence between
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
and the polynomial
X_{1}
. Specifically we find a polynomial
\Psi(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n},Z)
of low-degree in
Z
such that
\Psi(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},X_{1})
is the zero polynomial. The intent would be to substitute values from
\mathbb{F}
for
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
, say
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
, so that the resulting polynomial
Q(Z)=\Psi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},Z)
has a zero at
a_{1}
for every
\textbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
that is an isolated zero of
\textbf{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
. A natural choice would be to set
Y_{i}=0
and then we do have
\Psi(0,\ldots,0,a_{1})=0
as desired. This would be interesting if we knew
\Psi(0,\ldots,0,Z)
is not identically zero, but this is not easy to establish! The central idea in
[
1
]
is to set
Y_{i}
to some value
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{F}[t]
such that
\alpha_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{t}
. It is easy to find
\alpha_{i}
’s satisfying these conditions while ensuring
Q(Z)
is not identically zero. And we do get

Q(a_{i})\equiv\Psi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},a_{i})\equiv 0\pmod{t}
. But it is no longer clear why getting zeroes modulo
t
of a polynomial that may itself be zero modulo
t
might be interesting. Here
[
1
]
uses a clever idea of lifting the zeroes in
\mathbb{F}[t]
of
Q(Z)
modulo
t
, into zeroes from the field
\mathbb{F}((t))
of
Q(Z)
, where
\mathbb{F}((t))
is the field of formal Laurent series over
\mathbb{F}
in the variable
t
. This field contains
\mathbb{F}[t]
and so
Q(Z)
becomes a polynomial over this field as well. The lifting itself is not a straightforward application of Hensel lifting, but rather depends on the way
Q(Z)
was defined and in particular relies on the fact that
a
is an
isolated
zero of
f
. Putting these ideas together one gets a relatively simple proof of a Bezout-like theorem.

We start below with a definition of the formal power series ring
\mathbb{F}[[t]]
and the formal Laurent series field
\mathbb{F}((t))
. We then present our “algebraic dependence lemma” showing a low-degree algebraic dependence between
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
and the polynomial
X_{1}
. (See Lemma
2.2
.) Next, we give a Hensel lifting lemma that shows how to lift isolated zeroes modulo small powers of
t
from
\mathbb{F}[t]
of a system of polynomials to a zero from
\mathbb{F}[[t]]
. (See Lemma
2.4
.) Finally we combine these lemmas to obtain a proof of Theorem
1.2
at the end of this section.

Definition 2.1

(Formal Power Series and Formal Laurent Series)
.

The formal power series ring
\mathbb{F}[[t]]
has as its members all formal infinite sums

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}a_{i}t^{i}
where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
. Addition and multiplication are defined in the usual way.
The formal Laurent series ring
\mathbb{F}((t))
has as its members all formal infinite sums

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{i}t^{i}
where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
and the set
\{a_{i}\neq 0|i\leq 0\}
is finite. Addition and multiplication are defined in the usual way.

It is well-known that, for every field
\mathbb{F}
,
\mathbb{F}[[t]]
is an integral domain and
\mathbb{F}((t))
is a field. We also use the fact that
\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}[t]\subseteq\mathbb{F}[[t]]\subseteq\mathbb{F}((t))
where the inclusions preserve the ring operations.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
be polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[t]\Big[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\Big]
with respective degrees
k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{n}
. There exists a non-zero polynomial
\Psi\in\mathbb{F}[t]\Big[Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n},Z\Big]
with
\deg_{Z}(\Psi)\leq k_{1}\cdots k_{n}
such that
\Psi(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n},X_{1})=0
.

Remark 2.3
.

We note that in
[
1
]
there is an additional requirement that
\deg_{X_{1}}(\Psi)>0
. We do not make that a requirement (and this relaxation seems to clean up some steps of the proof below). On the other hand this condition is implied by the other conditions, so we are able to apply our
\Psi
in the same way as it is in
[
1
]
.

Proof.

In what follows let
B
and
D
be two non-negative integers that will be specified later.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{D}
denote the vector space of all polynomials of degree at most
D
in
\mathbb{F}(t)[X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}]
. The dimension of this space is just the number of monomials of degree at most
D
which is given by
\binom{D+n}{n}
.

Now consider the following set of monomials,

M_{B,D}=\left\{Y_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots Y_{n}^{d_{n}}Z^{r}\middle|d_{i},r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},r\leq B,\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}k_{i}+r\leq D\right\}.

Now let
S_{B,D}
be the evaluations of the elements of
M_{B,D}
at
Y_{i}=f_{i}
for
i\in[n]
and
Z=X_{1}
, i.e., let

S_{B,D}=\left\{f_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots f_{n}^{d_{n}}X_{1}^{r}\middle|d_{i},r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},r\leq B,\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}k_{i}+r\leq D\right\}.

S_{B,D}
is thus a subset of
\mathbb{F}(t)[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
. Furthermore every polynomial in
S_{B,D}
has degree at most
D
and so
S_{B,D}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{D}
. In what follows we show that
|M_{B,D}|>\dim(\mathcal{V}_{D})
(for an appropriate choice of
D
) to get a non-trivial linear dependence among these monomials when evaluated at
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},X_{1})
. This will yield the non-zero polynomial
\Psi
we are seeking.

For
\textbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})^{n}
and
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, denote by
S(r;\textbf{m})
the number of
n
-tuples
\textbf{d}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})^{n}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}d_{i}+m_{i}\leq D-r~~~\forall i\in[n].

Note that the quantity we wish to lower bound is
|S_{B,D}|=\sum_{r=0}^{B}S(r;\mathbf{0})
. Since
S(r;\textbf{m})
is a non-increasing function of
m
we also have for every
\textbf{m}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})^{n}
,
S(r;\textbf{m})\leq S(r;\mathbf{0})
. Using this fact, we get the following inequality,

\displaystyle k_{1}k_{2}\cdots k_{n}S(r;\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{k_{1}-1}\sum_{m_{2}=0}^{k_{2}-1}\dots\sum_{m_{n}=0}^{k_{n}-1}S(r;\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{k_{1}-1}\sum_{m_{2}=0}^{k_{2}-1}\dots\sum_{m_{n}=0}^{k_{n}-1}S(r;\mathbf{m})

\displaystyle=\binom{D+n-r}{n},

(1)

where the last equality follows from the observation that the previous expression exactly counts the number of solutions of the inequality,

e_{1}+e_{2}+\cdots+e_{n}\leq D-r,e_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

Applying Inequality (
1
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{B}S(r;0)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{k_{1}\ldots k_{n}}\sum_{r=0}^{B}\binom{D+n-r}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{B+1}{k_{1}\ldots k_{n}}\binom{D+n}{n}(1-\mathcal{O}(D^{-1})),

where the constant in the
\mathcal{O}
notation only depends on
B,\textbf{k},n
(and does not depend on
D
). It follows that for
B=k_{1}\dots k_{n}
, we can choose
D
sufficiently large so that
(1+1/(k_{1}\cdots k_{n}))(1-O(D^{-1}))>1
and so

\sum_{r=0}^{R}S(r;0)>\binom{D+n}{n}=\dim(\mathcal{V}_{d}).

We thus conclude that
|M_{B,D}|>\dim(\mathcal{V}_{d})
and so there is a non-zero sequence
(C_{m}\in\mathbb{F}(t))_{m\in M_{B,D}}
such that for the polynomial
\Psi_{0}(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n},Z)\triangleq\sum_{m\in M_{B,D}}C_{m}\cdot m
we have
\Psi_{0}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},X_{1})=0
. By construction
\deg_{Z}(\Psi_{0})\leq k_{1}\cdots k_{n}
. By clearing the denominators of the coefficients
C_{m}
we can get a polynomial
\Psi\in\mathbb{F}[t]\big[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n},Z\big]
with the same
Z
-degree which also vanishes at
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},X_{1})
. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\textbf{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})\in\left(\mathbb{F}[t]\Big[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\Big]\right)^{n}
. Suppose that
\textbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}[[t]]^{n}
satisfies
g_{j}(\textbf{a})\equiv 0\pmod{t^{s}}
for
1\leq j\leq n
and
\det(J(\textbf{g};\textbf{a}))\neq 0\mod t
. Then there exists
\textbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}[[t]]^{n}
such that
g_{j}(\textbf{b})=0
for all
1\leq j\leq n
and
\textbf{b}\equiv\textbf{a}\pmod{t^{s}}
.

Proof.

Let
\textbf{a}=\sum_{i}a(i)t^{i}
, where
a(i)\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
. We prove by induction on
i\in\{s,s+1,\ldots\}
that there exists
\textbf{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}[[t]]^{n}
, with
a_{s}=a
, satisfying:

1.

g_{j}(\textbf{a}_{i})\equiv 0\mod t^{i}
for all
j
,

2.

\textbf{a}_{i}\equiv\textbf{a}_{i-1}\mod t^{i-1}
, for every
i>s
.

Note that the case of
i=s
is true by the hypothesis of the lemma. Assume the inductive assumption is true for some
i\geq s
; we will show how to construct
\textbf{a}_{i+1}
. We will show that there exists
\textbf{b}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that
\textbf{a}_{i+1}\triangleq\textbf{a}_{i}+t^{i}\textbf{b}_{i}
satisfies

g_{j}(\textbf{a}_{i+1})\equiv 0\mod t^{i+1}
for all
j
.
By construction, we have that the second condition is satisfied, since
\textbf{a}_{i+1}=\textbf{a}_{i}=\textbf{a}\pmod{t^{s}}
, hence we only need to find
b_{i}
such that the first condition is satisfied.

We first recall how polynomials behave under local perturbations. For every
P\in\mathbb{F}[[t]][X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
, there exist polynomials
P^{(k\ell)}\in\mathbb{F}[[t]][X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]
, for
k,\ell\in[n]
such that

P(X+Y)=P(X)+\sum_{k\in[n]}\frac{\partial P}{\partial X_{k}}(X)Y_{k}+\sum_{k,\ell\in[n]}P^{(k\ell)}(X,Y)Y_{k}Y_{\ell}.

where
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
and
Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
. Applying it to our functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
at
X=\textbf{a}_{i}
and
Y=\textbf{b}_{i}t^{i}
we have

\textbf{g}(\textbf{a}_{i}+t^{i}\textbf{b}_{i})=\textbf{g}(\textbf{a}_{i})+t^{i}\cdot J(\textbf{f};\textbf{a}_{i})\cdot\textbf{b}_{i}+t^{2i}\textbf{h}(\textbf{a}_{i},\textbf{b}_{i}),

for some
\textbf{h}\in(\mathbb{F}[[t]][X,Y])^{n}
.
Thus to get
\textbf{g}(\textbf{a}_{i}+t^{i}\textbf{b}_{i})=0\mod t^{i+1}
we need
\textbf{g}(\textbf{a}_{i})+t^{i}\cdot J(\textbf{f};\textbf{a}_{i})\cdot\textbf{b}_{i}=0\mod t^{i+1}
. Since

\textbf{g}(\textbf{a}_{i})=0\mod t^{i}
we can divide the equation above by
t^{i}
to see that we need
J(\textbf{f};\textbf{a}_{i})\cdot\textbf{b}_{i}=-t^{-i}\textbf{g}(\textbf{a}_{i})\mod t
. Note that since we are working modulo
t
, we have that the equation:
(J(\textbf{f};\textbf{a}_{i})\mod t)\cdot\textbf{b}_{i}=(-t^{-i}\textbf{g}(\textbf{a}_{i})\mod t)
, is over field constants. We also have that
\det(J(\textbf{g},\textbf{a}_{i}))=\det(J(\textbf{g},\textbf{a})_{\neq}0\pmod{t}
and so the system above can be inverted, to yield
\textbf{b}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
. This yields
\textbf{a}_{i+1}
as desired and completes the inductive step.

The
b
as required by the lemma is now obtained as follows: Let
\textbf{b}_{i}
for
i\geq s
be as obtained in the above inductive proof. Let
b(i)=a(i)
, for all
i<s
and let
b(i)=a(i)+\textbf{b}_{i}
, for all
i\geq s
. Then
\textbf{b}=\sum_{i}b(i)t^{i}
gives the element of
\mathbb{F}[[t]]^{n}
satisfying
g_{j}(\textbf{b})=0
for all
j\in[n]
and
\textbf{b}\equiv\textbf{a}\pmod{t^{s}}
.

∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
:

Assume for contradiction that the system
\textbf{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
has at least
1+\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}
isolated zeroes modulo
t^{s}
. Let
S
denote such a set of cardinality
1+\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}
. Furthermore assume that the projections of the elements of
S
to their first coordinate are distinct modulo
t^{s}
, i.e., for
S_{1}\triangleq\{a_{1}|\textbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in S\}
we assume
|S|=|S_{1}|=1+\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}
and that for
a_{1},a^{\prime}_{1}\in S_{1}
we have

a_{1}\neq a^{\prime}_{1}\pmod{t^{s}}
.
(We can ensure this by applying a generic affine transformation from
\mathbb{F}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}^{n}
to the variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
which will ensure that the first coordinates are all distinct. If the field
\mathbb{F}
is finite we can apply this transformation over any large extension field.)

Let
\Psi\in\mathbb{F}[t][Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{n},Z]
denote the non-zero polynomial given by applying Lemma
2.2
to the system
\textbf{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
. So
\Psi
satisfies
\deg_{Z}(\Psi)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}
and
\Psi(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n},X_{1})=0
. Since
\Psi
is a non-zero polynomial, there always exists constants
c_{1},c_{2},\dots c_{n}\in\mathbb{F}
such that the polynomial,
Q(Z)\triangleq\Psi(c_{1}t^{s},c_{2}t^{s},\dots,c_{n}t^{s},Z)\in\mathbb{F}[t][Z]
is a non-zero polynomial of degree at most
\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}
. We will first show that the elements of
S_{1}
are zeros of
Q\mod t^{s}
. We will then be able to lift these to distinct zeroes of
Q
in the ring
\mathbb{F}[[t]]
and hence in the field
\mathbb{F}((t))
. This will yield the desired contradiction since
Q
is a non-zero univariate polynomial with degree at most
\prod k_{i}
, whereas we will show that it has
|S_{1}|
distinct roots, which is more roots than its degree.

Consider an isolated zero modulo
t^{s}
, say
\textbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})\in S
, of the system
f
. We have

\displaystyle Q(a_{1})

\displaystyle\equiv\Psi(c_{1}t^{s},...,c_{d}t^{s},a_{1})\pmod{t^{s}}

\displaystyle\equiv\Psi(0,...,0,a_{1})\pmod{t^{s}}~~~\mbox{(Since $c_{i}t^{s}\pmod{t^{s}}\equiv 0$)}

\displaystyle\equiv\Psi(f_{1}(\textbf{a}),\dots,f_{n}(\textbf{a}),a_{1})\pmod{t^{s}}~~~\mbox{(Since $f_{i}(\textbf{a})\pmod{t^{s}}\equiv 0$)}

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{t^{s}}.

Next we show that every such zero
a_{1}\in\mathbb{F}[t]
of
Q\mod t^{s}
can be lifted to a distinct zero of
Q
in the ring
\mathbb{F}[[t]]
.

We first note that if there exists
\textbf{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{F}[[t]]^{n}
such that for all
i
,
f_{i}(\textbf{b})=c_{i}t^{s}
, then we have that
Q(b_{1})=\Psi(c_{1}t^{s},\ldots,c_{n}t^{s},b_{1})=\Psi(f_{1}(\textbf{b}),\ldots,f_{n}(\textbf{b}),b_{1})=0
. Thus to get our lifted
b_{1}
it suffices to find a
b_{1}
such that
b_{1}=a_{1}\pmod{t^{s}}
and
f_{i}(\textbf{b})=c_{i}t^{s},\forall i
. Now let
g_{i}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=f_{i}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-c_{i}t^{s}
, for
i\in[n]
. By construction we have
that
a
is an isolated zero of
\textbf{g}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})
modulo
t^{s}
(the shifts by
c_{i}t^{s}
do not alter this). Thus by applying Lemma
2.4
to
g
and
a
we get that there exists
\textbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}[[t]]^{n}
that is a zero of
g
, and thus satisfies

f_{i}(\textbf{b})=c_{i}t^{s}
for every
i\in[n]
. Thus, we conclude that
Q(b_{1})=0

Further discussion

There are two main differences between Theorem
1.2
and the well-known Bezout’s theorem: (1) The usual theorem can count isolated zeroes with multiplicities (and in particular a mutliple zero is counted as several isolated zeroes), whereas we do not count such zeroes. (2) The usual theorem allows more polynomials than the number of variables and this is particularly helpful in counting the number of zeroes on subspaces even when the polynomials have algebraic dependence. It would be interesting to see if the methods from
[
1
]
and this paper can be extended to either of these cases.
