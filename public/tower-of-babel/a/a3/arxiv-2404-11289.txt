1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and motivation

Space–time nonlocal problems involving fractional powers of a parabolic
operator arise in physics, biology, probability theory and statistics.
The flat parabolic Signorini problem and certain models for semipermeable
membranes can be formulated as obstacle problems for the fractional heat
operator
(\partial_{t}-\Delta)^{s}
, where
s\in(0,1)
and
\Delta
denotes
the Laplacian, acting on
functions
u\colon J\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
for a given time interval

J\subseteq\mathbb{R}

and a connected non-empty open spatial domain
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
see e.g.
[
1
,
28
]
.
In the context of
continuous time random walks, equations of the form

(\partial_{t}-\Delta)^{s}u=f

for
f\colon J\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}

are considered examples of
master equations
governing the (non-separable)
joint probability
distribution of jump lengths and waiting times
[
6
]
.
The case where
f
is replaced by
spatiotemporal Gaussian noise
\dot{\mathcal{W}}
has applications to the statistical
modeling of spatial and temporal dependence in data: The resulting class of
fractional
parabolic
stochastic
partial differential equations (SPDEs) has
been proposed and
analyzed in
[
14
,
16
]
as a spatiotemporal
generalization of the
SPDE approach
to spatial statistical
modeling,
which was initiated by Lindgren, Rue and
Lindström
[
18
]

and has subsequently gained widespread popularity
[
17
]
.

After
[
28
]
and
[
22
]
independently generalized
the Caffarelli–Silvestre extension approach from the fractional elliptic to the
parabolic setting, there has been a surge of literature on
space–time nonlocal problems involving fractional powers of
\partial_{t}+L

for more general elliptic operators
L
acting on functions
u\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
, see for instance
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
7
,
15
,
19
]
. In
particular, in
[
28
, Remark 1.2]
, the

natural Dirichlet problem
for the nonlocal space–time operator
(\partial_{t}+L)^{s}
, given
by

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} (\partial_{t}+L)^{s}u(t,x)&=f(t,x),&&(t,x)\in J\times\mathcal{D},\\
u(t,x)&=g(t,x),&&(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus(J\times\mathcal{D}),\end{aligned}\right.

is introduced,
where
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus(J\times\mathcal{D})\to\mathbb{R}
is a given function
prescribing the values of
u
outside of the spatiotemporal region

J\times\mathcal{D}
.
The definition of
(\partial_{t}+L)^{s}
, given in
Section
2.4
below, generalizes that of the
Riemann–Liouville fractional time derivative
\partial_{t}^{s}
(i.e., the case
where
L=0
) using the theory of semigroups.
Equations
involving only a fractional time derivative have been studied widely; see for
instance the monographs
[
13
,
26
,
25
,
8
]
for
an
introduction to the subject.

In the integer-order case, the space–time differential operator is
local, so
that the analog to (
1.1
) is
an initial boundary value problem. Identifying any
u\colon J\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
with
u\colon J\to X
, where
J\coloneqq(t_{0},\infty)
and
X
is a Banach space to be
thought of as containing functions from

\mathcal{D}

to

\mathbb{R}
, the corresponding infinite-dimensional initial value problem for
s=1
is the
abstract Cauchy problem

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} (\partial_{t}+A)u(t)&=f(t),\quad t\in J,\\
u(t_{0})&=x\in X.\end{aligned}\right.

Here,
A\colon\mathsf{D}(A)\subseteq X\to X
is a linear operator, whose domain

\mathsf{D}(A)
can be used to encode (Dirichlet) boundary conditions, and
f\colon J\to X
is a given forcing function.
Although there exist various definitions of solutions to (
1.2
)
(e.g., mild, strong and
L^{p}
-solutions, see Section
2.3
),
the main focus of this article is
on mild solutions. The mild solution to (
1.2
) can only be defined
under the assumption that
-A
is the infinitesimal generator of a suitably
regular semigroup
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
of bounded linear operators on
X
,
see Section
2.2
.
Intuitively, the relation between
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
A
can be viewed as a
generalization of the way in which the matrix exponential
S(t)=e^{-tA}
is
associated to a matrix
A
.
If, moreover, the right-hand side
f
is sufficiently (Bochner) integrable,
then the
mild solution
of (
1.2
) is defined by

(1.3)

u(t)\coloneqq S(t-t_{0})x+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}S(t-\tau)f(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau,\quad t\in J,

which is commonly known as the
variation of constants formula
,
again by analogy with the finite-dimensional (matrix) case.

In this work we consider an abstract counterpart
of (
1.1
) in the setting
of (
1.2
), namely the following Dirichlet problem
for
(\partial_{t}+A)^{s}
with

s\in(0,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
:

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} (\partial_{t}+A)^{s}u(t)&=0,&&t\in(t_{0},\infty),\\
u(t)&=g(t),&&t\in(-\infty,t_{0}],\end{aligned}\right.

where
g\colon(-\infty,t_{0}]\to X
. We restrict ourselves to
J=(t_{0},\infty)

since
(\partial_{t}+A)^{s}u(t)
depends only on the values of
u

to the left of

t\in\mathbb{R}
, see Section
2.4
below.
Moreover, we only consider
f\equiv 0
since the problem is linear in

u

and the mild solution formula for
f\not\equiv 0
and
g\equiv 0
(or

J=\mathbb{R}
) is known to be given by a Riemann–Liouville type fractional
parabolic integral,
cf.
[
28
, Theorem 1.17]
.
We will define the concept of an
L^{p}
-solution
to (
1.4
),
and show that it can be expressed in terms of
g
and

(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
in the following way:

(1.5)

\displaystyle u(t)\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{-\{s\}}}{\tau+1}S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))g(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{\{s\}+k-1}}{\Gamma(\{s\}+k)}S(t-t_{0})[(\partial_{t}+A)^{\{s\}+k-1}g](t_{0}),\quad t\in(t_{0},\infty),

where
\Gamma
denotes the gamma function and

s=\lfloor s\rfloor+\{s\}
for
\lfloor s\rfloor\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and

{\{s\}\in(0,1)}
.
This formula
generalizes (
1.3
) to fractional orders, and is
therefore taken as the definition of the
mild solution

to (
1.4
).

1.2.
Contributions

The main contribution of this work is the
introduction and motivation of (
1.5
) as the
definition of the mild solution to (
1.4
)
for
s\in(0,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
and bounded continuous
g
, rigorously
formulated in Definition
4.2
.
This definition is motivated by Theorem
4.5
, which
shows that
L^{p}
-solutions
to (
1.4
)
satisfy (
1.5
) under certain natural conditions.
Although its proof relies on the uniform exponential
stability of

(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
, the resulting formula is well-defined under the more general
assumption
that
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
is uniformly bounded. In particular, this includes the
case

A=0
,
meaning that (
1.5
) with
S(\,\cdot\,)\equiv\operatorname{Id}_{X}

can be
viewed as
a solution to the
Dirichlet problem associated to the fractional time derivative

\partial_{t}^{s}
.
Likewise, if

(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
is uniformly exponentially stable, then the integral
in (
1.5
) also converges for
\{s\}=0
, so
that (
1.5
) remains meaningful for integers

s=n\in\mathbb{N}
and reduces to the integer-order solution formula:

(1.6)

u(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{k}}{k!}S(t-t_{0})[(\partial_{t}+A)^{k}g](t_{0}),\quad t\in(t_{0},\infty).

If
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
is merely uniformly bounded, then we can still show that
the
first term
of (
1.5
) converges to
S(t-t_{0})g(t_{0})

as
\{s\}\uparrow 1
for all

t\in(t_{0},\infty)
, see Proposition
4.7
.
For constant initial data
g\equiv x\in X
, we find
that (
1.5
) can be conveniently expressed in terms of an
operator-valued incomplete
gamma
function, see Corollary
4.8
.

In addition to (
1.5
), we define solution concepts for the
Cauchy problems associated to fractional parabolic
Riemann–Liouville and Caputo type derivative operators
(see Proposition
5.1
and Definitions
5.2

and
5.3
) for comparison.
The
higher-order terms comprising the summation in (
1.5
)
turn out to
be analogous to the corresponding terms in the Riemann–Liouville solution.
The integral term in (
1.5
), however, is continuous at

t_{0}
under mild
conditions on
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
or
g
, in contrast to the lowest-order term
in the Riemann–Liouville formula, which has a singularity there. As opposed to
the Caputo type initial value problem, the solutions
to (
1.4
)
are in general different for distinct
s_{1},s_{2}\in(n,n+1)
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

To the best of the author’s knowledge, the solution formula given
by (
1.5
) is
new even in the scalar-valued case
X\coloneqq\mathbb{C}
,

A\coloneqq a\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
and

(S(t))_{t\geq 0}=(e^{-at})_{t\geq 0}
, as are the Riemann–Liouville and
Caputo type solutions for
a\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
.

1.3.
Outline

This article is structured as follows. In Section
2
,
we establish some notation and collect preliminary results regarding
semigroups, fractional calculus, first-order abstract Cauchy problems and
the Phillips functional calculus associated to semigroup generators.
These notions are first used in Section
3
to
investigate
problem (
1.4
) for
t_{0}=-\infty
, i.e., in the
absence of prescribed initial data.
Section
4
is concerned with the
rigorous definition of mild and
L^{p}
-solutions
to (
1.4
); after establishing the relation
between these two concepts, we focus on the mild solution and establish some of
its most important properties. The comparison with the solution
concepts
associated to
Riemann–Liouville and
Caputo type initial value problems is presented in
Section
5
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

Let
\mathbb{N}\coloneqq\{1,2,3,\dots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
denote
the sets of positive and non-negative integers, respectively.
We write
\lfloor\,\cdot\,\rfloor
and
\lceil\,\cdot\,\rceil
for the
floor
and ceiling functions; the fractional part of
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
is
defined by
\{\alpha\}\coloneqq\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
.
The maximum (respectively, minimum) of two real numbers
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
is
denoted
by
\alpha\vee\beta
(respectively,
\alpha\wedge\beta
).
The function
t\mapsto t^{\beta}_{+}
is defined by
t^{\beta}_{+}\coloneqq t^{\beta}

if
t\in(0,\infty)
and
t^{\beta}_{+}\coloneqq 0
otherwise.
The real and
imaginary parts of a complex number
z\in\mathbb{C}
are respectively denoted by

\operatorname{Re}{z}
and
\operatorname{Im}{z}
; if
z\neq 0
, then its argument is written as
\arg z\in(-\pi,\pi]
. The open and closed right half-planes of the complex
plane are denoted by

\mathbb{C}_{+}\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z>0\}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0\},

respectively.
The identity map on a set
B
is denoted by
\operatorname{Id}_{B}\colon B\to B
and we write
\mathbf{1}_{B_{0}}\colon B\to\{0,1\}
for the indicator
function of a subset
B_{0}\subseteq B
.

Throughout this article,
(X,\|\,\cdot\,\|_{X})
denotes a Banach
space over the complex scalar field
\mathbb{C}
; the real case can be treated using
complexifications.
The Banach space of bounded linear operators from
X
to a Banach space
Y

with the uniform operator
norm is denoted by
\mathscr{L}(X;Y)
; for
X=Y
we set
\mathscr{L}(X)\coloneqq\mathscr{L}(X;X)
.
The notation
A\colon\mathsf{D}(A)\subseteq X\to X
indicates that
A
is a (possibly unbounded) linear operator on
X
with
domain
\mathsf{D}(A)
and graph

\mathsf{G}(A)\coloneqq\{(x,Ax):x\in\mathsf{D}(A)\}
.

Let
(S,\mathscr{A}\!,\mu)
be a measure space. For
f\colon S\to\mathbb{R}

and
x\in X
, define
{f\otimes x\colon S\to X}
by
[f\otimes x](s)\coloneqq f(s)x
. A function
f\colon S\to X
is said to be strongly
\mu
-measurable if
it is the
\mu
-almost everywhere (“a.e.”) limit of

\mu
-simple functions, i.e., linear combinations of

\mathbf{1}_{B}\otimes x
with
B\in\mathscr{A},\mu(B)<\infty
and
x\in X
. For

p\in[1,\infty]
, let
L^{p}(S;X)
denote the Bochner space of (equivalence classes
of)
p
-integrable functions with norm

\|f\|_{L^{p}(S;X)}\coloneqq\begin{cases}(\int_{S}\|f(s)\|_{X}^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s))^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}},&\text{if }p\in[1,\infty);\\
\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{s\in S}\|f(s)\|_{X},&\text{if }p=\infty.\end{cases}

Intervals
J\subseteq\mathbb{R}
are
equipped with the Lebesgue
\sigma
-algebra and measure. The Banach space of
bounded
continuous functions
u\colon J\to X
, endowed with the supremum norm, is
denoted
by

(C_{\mathrm{b}}(J;X),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\infty})
.

2.2.
Strongly measurable semigroups of bounded linear operators

A family
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
of bounded linear operators on a complex Banach
space

X
is said to be a
semigroup
if
S(0)=\operatorname{Id}_{X}
and,
for all
t,s\geq 0
, we have
S(t+s)=S(t)S(s)
. It is called a

strongly measurable semigroup
if, in addition, the orbit

t\mapsto S(t)x
of any
x\in X
is a strongly measurable mapping from

[0,\infty)
to
X
. For a strongly measurable semigroup, it holds that

t\mapsto S(t)x
is
continuous on
(0,\infty)
for every
x\in X
, with continuity at zero
if and only if
x\in\overline{\mathsf{D}(A)}
. In what follows, we exclusively
consider
locally
bounded
strongly measurable semigroups, which satisfy

(2.1)

\exists M_{0}\in[1,\infty),\,w\in\mathbb{R}:\quad\|S(t)\|_{\mathscr{L}(X)}\leq M_{0}e^{-wt},\quad\forall t\in[0,\infty).

Analogously to the relation between a matrix
G
and its corresponding
matrix
exponential function
S(t)\coloneqq e^{tG}
, we can more generally associate
an
infinitesimal generator

G\colon\mathsf{D}(G)\subseteq X\to X
to any
locally bounded strongly measurable semigroup
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
. It is defined
by the following
property: For all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>w
, the operator

\lambda\operatorname{Id}_{X}-G
admits a bounded inverse, and we have

\langle(\lambda\operatorname{Id}_{X}-G)^{-1}x,x^{*}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\langle S(t)x,x^{*}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\quad\text{for all }x\in X\text{ and }x^{*}\in X^{*},

where
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
denotes the duality pairing
between

X
and its dual
X^{*}
.
We refer the reader to
[
12
, Appendix K]

for a more detailed summary of the theory of (strongly) measurable semigroups.

The above notions will be applied to the operator
A

from (
1.4
) on
X
, on which we impose the
following standing assumptions:

Assumption 2.1
.

Let
-A\colon\mathsf{D}(A)\subseteq X\to X
be the infinitesimal generator of
a locally bounded strongly measurable semigroup

(S(t))_{t\geq 0}\subseteq\mathscr{L}(X)
, which
satisfies (
2.1
).
More precisely, we suppose that
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
is either

(i)

uniformly bounded
, meaning that
w\in[0,\infty)
, or

(ii)

uniformly exponentially stable
, meaning that

w\in(0,\infty)
.

We may sometimes additionally assume that
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
is

(iii)

bounded analytic
, i.e.,

(0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto S(t)\in\mathscr{L}(X)
admits a bounded holomorphic
extension to

\Sigma_{\varphi}\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\lvert\arg z\rvert<\varphi\}

for some
\varphi\in(0,\frac{1}{2}\pi)
.

2.3.
Solution concepts for first-order abstract Cauchy problems

In this section we define and relate the concepts of strong, mild and

L^{p}
-solutions to the first-order Cauchy problem (
1.2
) on an interval

J=(t_{0},\infty)
, where
t_{0}\in[-\infty,\infty)
. For a more
detailed reference, see
[
12
, Chapter 17]
.

Let
L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\overline{J};X)
denote the space of strongly measurable functions
from
\overline{J}
to
X
which are integrable on every compact subset of
\overline{J}
.
For
b<a
we set
\int_{a}^{b}\coloneqq-\int_{b}^{a}
.

Definition 2.2

(Strong solution)
.

A strongly measurable function
u\colon J\to X
is said to be a

strong solution
of (
1.2
) associated with

f\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\overline{J};X)
if

(i)

u(t)\in\mathsf{D}(A)
for almost all
t\in J
and

t\mapsto Au(t)\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\overline{J};X)
;

(ii)

for almost all
t\in J
we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle u(t)+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}Au(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=x+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}f(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }t_{0}\in\mathbb{R};

\displaystyle u(t)+\int_{0}^{t}Au(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=u(0)+\int_{0}^{t}f(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }t_{0}=-\infty.

As antiderivatives of locally integrable functions are continuous,
see
[
11
, Proposition 2.5.9]
, it follows that any strong solution
admits
a continuous representative, so that the pointwise evaluation of
u

in (
2.2
) is meaningful. In fact, identifying
u
with
this representative, it turns out to be (classically) differentiable for
almost
all
t\in J
, where it holds that

u^{\prime}(t)+Au(t)=f(t),

see
[
12
, Equation (17.3)]
. This implies
u^{\prime}=f-Au\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\overline{J};X)
, hence
u
is weakly differentiable and its
weak derivative
\partial_{t}u
coincides a.e. with
u^{\prime}
,
again by
[
11
, Proposition 2.5.9]
.

Next, we turn to the definition of a mild solution to (
1.2
). We
will
see in Proposition
2.4
below that mild solutions
are
weaker than strong solutions.

Definition 2.3

(Mild solution)
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
(ii)

is satisfied
and let
J\coloneqq(t_{0},\infty)
for a given
t_{0}\in[-\infty,\infty)
.
The
mild solution
to (
1.2
) with

f\in L^{p}(J;X)
for
p\in[1,\infty]

is the
function
u\in C_{\mathrm{b}}(\overline{J};X)
defined
for all
t\in\overline{J}
by

(2.3)

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq S(t-t_{0})x+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}S(t-\tau)f(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\text{ if }t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }x\in\overline{\mathsf{D}(A)};

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{-\infty}^{t}S(t-\tau)f(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\text{ if }t_{0}=-\infty.

The continuity of the mild solution
u
defined by (
2.3
)
follows from
[
12
, Proposition K.1.5(3)]
and
Proposition
2.6
(b)
.
The following is a slight extension
of
[
12
, Proposition 17.1.3]
for the class of time intervals
considered in this work.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
(ii)

holds.
Let
J\coloneqq(t_{0},\infty)
for a given
t_{0}\in[-\infty,\infty)
,

f\in L^{p}(J;X)
for some
{p\in[1,\infty]}
and
x\in\overline{\mathsf{D}(A)}

if
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, for every
u\in C_{\mathrm{ub}}(\overline{J};X)
, the following assertions are
equivalent:

(i)

u
is a strong solution of (
1.2
) in the sense of
Definition
2.2
;

(ii)

u
is the mild solution of (
1.2
) in the sense of
Definition
2.3
and
u
is (classically)
differentiable almost everywhere with
u^{\prime}\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\overline{J};X)
;

(iii)

u
is the mild solution of (
1.2
) in the sense of
Definition
2.3
,
u(t)\in\mathsf{D}(A)
for almost
all
t\in J
and
t\mapsto Au(t)\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\overline{J};X)
.

Proof.

If
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, then the required modifications of the proof
of
[
12
, Proposition 17.1.3]
are straightforward. We

2.4.
Fractional parabolic calculus

Fractional calculus classically refers to the idea of generalizing the
basic
operations of calculus, namely differentiation and integration, beyond the
integer-order cases (which simply consist of
repeated application). For example, a fractional
derivative
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}f
of a sufficiently smooth given function

f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
should be defined for non-integer

\alpha\in(0,\infty)
, in such a way that

\partial_{t}^{1/2}\partial_{t}^{1/2}f=\partial_{t}f
.
To this end, a common first object of study is the
Riemann–Liouville
fractional
integral
, defined for suitable
f
by

(2.4)

I^{s}_{\text{RL}}f(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{-\infty}^{t}(t-\tau)^{s-1}f(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau,\quad s\in(0,\infty),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

Since this is a fractional-order generalization of
the integration operator
I^{1}_{\text{RL}}
, which can itself be viewed as an
inverse of the
derivative
\partial_{t}
, we can interpret
I^{s}_{\text{RL}}
as

\partial_{t}^{-s}
. This leads us to define the following fractional
derivatives:

(2.5)

[\partial_{t}^{s}]_{\text{RL}}\coloneqq\partial_{t}^{\lceil s\rceil}I^{\lceil s\rceil-s}\quad\text{and}\quad[\partial_{t}^{s}]_{\text{C}}\coloneqq I^{\lceil s\rceil-s}\partial_{t}^{\lceil s\rceil},

which are known as the Riemann–Liouville and Caputo fractional
(time)
derivatives, respectively. For a comprehensive overview of these and other
fractional time derivatives, as well as their application in fractional
differential equations, we refer to the monographs
[
13
,
26
,
25
]
.

Under the assumption that
-A
generates a semigroup
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}

satisfying Assumption
2.1
(ii)
, we
can extend these notions to define the lesser-known fractional

parabolic

integration and
differentiation operators
\mathfrak{I}^{s}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{s}
, which are the
rigorous
definitions of the expressions
(\partial_{t}+A)^{-s}
and
(\partial_{t}+A)^{s}

from Section
1
, respectively.
For
s\in(0,\infty)
, let the integration kernel
k_{s}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathscr{L}(X)

be defined
by
k_{s}(\tau)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\tau^{s-1}_{+}S(\tau)

for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}

Given

its
Riemann–Liouville type fractional parabolic integral

\mathfrak{I}^{s}u\colon\mathbb{R}\to X

of order

by

(2.6)

\mathfrak{I}^{s}u(t)\coloneqq k_{s}*u(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{s-1}S(\tau)u(t-\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

whenever this Bochner integral
converges for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
For
s=0

\mathfrak{I}^{0}\coloneqq\operatorname{Id}_{X}
.
When viewed as a linear operator, the fractional parabolic integral

turns out to map its domain

boundedly to itself for every

s\in[0,\infty)
. In fact, we have the following properties of
(\mathfrak{I}^{s})_{s\in[0,\infty)}
, which will be used
throughout this work:

Proposition 2.6

Suppose that

(ii)

to (

Definition 4.2

(Mild solution)
.

Let Assumption

be satisfied, suppose that

,
{p\in[1,\infty]}
,
{t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{-\{s\}}}{\tau+1}S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))g(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

Proposition 4.3

Example 4.6

(The fractional heat operator

Substituting these formulae into equation (

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(\pi s)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\tau^{-s}}{\tau+1}K_{t(\tau+1)}(x-y)g(-t\tau,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle u(t,x)={}

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi(\sqrt{4\pi t})^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tau^{-\{s\}}(\tau+1)^{-\frac{d}{2}-1}\exp\biggl(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{2}}{4t(\tau+1)}\biggr)g(-t\tau,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle+\frac{t^{\{s\}}}{\Gamma(\{s\}+1)}S(t)\mathfrak{D}^{\{s\}}g(0).

The fractional parabolic derivative

or

[

\mathfrak{D}^{\alpha}f(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma^{-\alpha-1}[S(\sigma)f(t-\sigma)-f(t)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Therefore, supposing that

is sufficiently regular, we have
for all
t\in(0,\infty)
,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\frac{t^{\{s\}}}{\Gamma(\{s\}+1)}S(t)\mathfrak{D}^{\{s\}}g(0)

(4.13)

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{t^{\{s\}}\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma^{-\{s\}-1}[S(t+\sigma)g(-\sigma)-S(t)g(0)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma

(4.14)

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\{s\}-1}[S(t(\tau+1))g(-\tau t)-S(t)g(0)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau,

where we used the reflection formula for the gamma

to obtain the prefactor on the second line, and the change of variables

\displaystyle\frac{t^{\{s\}}}{\Gamma(\{s\}+1)}[S(t)\mathfrak{D}^{\{s\}}g(0)](x)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &=\frac{\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tau^{-\{s\}-1}[K_{t}(x-y)g(0,y)-K_{t(1+\tau)}(x-y)g(-\tau t,y)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\\
&=\frac{\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi(\sqrt{4\pi t})^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tau^{-\{s\}-1}\begin{aligned} \biggl[&\exp\biggl(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{2}}{4t}\biggr)g(0,y)\\

Substituting this into (

\displaystyle u(

\displaystyle t,x)=\frac{\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi(\sqrt{4\pi t})^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\biggl[\tau^{-\{s\}-1}\exp\biggl(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{2}}{4t}\biggr)g(0,y)

\displaystyle+\bigl(\tau^{-\{s\}}(\tau+1)^{-\frac{d}{2}-1}-\tau^{-\{s\}-1}(\tau+1)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\bigr)\exp\biggl(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{2}}{4t(\tau+1)}\biggr)g(-t\tau,y)\biggr]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

for all

.

4.1.
Properties of the mild solution

In this section, we further investigate the mild solution concept
introduced in Definition
4.2
by
establishing some of its key properties. To this end, we start with the central
observation that
formula (
4.3
) has close connections to the
normalized upper incomplete gamma function, whose principal branch

\overline{\Gamma}(\alpha,\,\cdot\,)\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0)\to\mathbb{C}

for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
is
defined by

(4.17)

\overline{\Gamma}(\alpha,z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{z}^{\infty}\zeta^{\alpha-1}e^{-\zeta}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0),

integrating over any contour from
z
to
\infty
avoiding
(-\infty,0)
,
see
[
23
, Chapter 8]
.

The relation to (
4.3
) follows from the following identities.
For
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\{0\}
,

[
23
, Equations (5.5.3), (13.4.4) and (13.6.6)]
yield

(4.18)

\overline{\Gamma}(\alpha,z)=\frac{\sin(\pi\alpha)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{-\alpha}}{1+\tau}e^{-z(1+\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

In particular, for
t\in(0,\infty)
, the change of variables

\sigma\coloneqq t(1+\tau)
produces

(4.19)

\overline{\Gamma}(\alpha,tz)=\frac{t^{\alpha}\sin(\pi\alpha)}{\pi}\int_{t}^{\infty}(\sigma-t)^{-\alpha}\sigma^{-1}e^{-\sigma z}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Moreover, for all
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we
have the following recurrence relations
[
23
, Equations (8.8.12) and
(8.4.10)]
:

(4.20)

\overline{\Gamma}(\alpha,z)=\overline{\Gamma}(\{\alpha\},z)+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}\frac{z^{k+\{\alpha\}-1}}{\Gamma(k+\{\alpha\})}e^{-z},\quad\overline{\Gamma}(n,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}e^{-z}.

As a first application of these identities, we present the following
proof:

Proof of
Proposition
4.3
.

The estimate on
\|u(t)\|_{X}
follows by applying the triangle
inequality, (
2.1
) and
identities (
4.18
)–(
4.20
)
to (
4.3
). The continuity assertions rely on the strong continuity
of
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
, see the remarks below
Assumption
2.1
. They are immediate for the terms
involving
\mathfrak{D}^{\{s\}+k}g(t_{0})
. For the integral term, we note
that the norm of the integrand is dominated by

\tau\mapsto M_{0}\|g\|_{\infty}\frac{\tau^{-\{s\}}}{\tau+1}
, which
is integrable
in view of (
4.18
). Combined with the continuity of

t\mapsto S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))g(t_{0}-(t-t_{0}))
on
(t_{0},\infty)
and
possibly at
t_{0}
, the dominated convergence theorem yields the result.
∎

Next we comment on the precise way in which the fractional-order mild
solution formula (
4.3
) of Definition
4.2

reduces to formula (
4.4
) for integer orders
s=n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Substituting
s=n
(i.e.,
\lfloor s\rfloor=n
and
\{s\}=0
)
in the higher-order terms of (
4.3
) and shifting the index of
summation yields

(4.21)

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{k}}{k!}S(t-t_{0})[(\partial_{t}+A)^{k}g](t_{0}),\quad\forall t\in(t_{0},\infty),

as desired.
Moreover, the first term in (
4.3
) vanishes as required, provided that
the integral remains convergent for
\{s\}=0
. This occurs under
Assumption
2.1
(ii)
, but may fail in
general if only Assumption
2.1
(i)

is satisfied, hence in this case we cannot argue via direct substitution.
Instead, we have to consider limits as
s\to n
. Let
u_{s}
denote the
mild solution from Definition
4.2
of order
s\in(0,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
. Then

(4.22)

\displaystyle u_{n+\varepsilon}(t)={}

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(\pi\varepsilon)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{-\varepsilon}}{\tau+1}S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))g(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(4.23)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{k+\varepsilon}}{\Gamma(k+\varepsilon+1)}S(t-t_{0})\mathfrak{D}^{k+\varepsilon}g(t_{0}),

(4.24)

\displaystyle u_{n-\varepsilon}(t)={}

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(\pi\varepsilon)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{\varepsilon-1}}{\tau+1}S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))g(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(4.25)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{k-\varepsilon}}{\Gamma(k-\varepsilon+1)}S(t-t_{0})\mathfrak{D}^{k-\varepsilon}g(t_{0}),

for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
t\in(t_{0},\infty)
.
Substituting
\varepsilon=0
into the summations on
lines (

) and (

resulting expressions
with (

), we see that the integral terms on

) and (

converge to zero and

, respectively, as

,
in

cannot expect the continuity of
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathfrak{D}^{k\pm\varepsilon}g(t_{0})
in general).
The following proposition states when these convergences hold:

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
g\in C_{\mathrm{b}}((-\infty,t_{0}];X)
be given. If
Assumption
2.1
(i)
is satisfied,
then for all

it holds that

(4.26)

\frac{\sin(\pi\varepsilon)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{\varepsilon-1}}{\tau+1}S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))g(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\to S(t-t_{0})g(t_{0})\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

If, in addition,

2.1
(ii)

(4.27)

\frac{\sin(\pi\varepsilon)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{-\varepsilon}}{\tau+1}S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))g(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\to 0\quad\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

Proof.

Fix
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in(t_{0},\infty)
.
First we define the function
f_{t,t_{0}}\colon\mathbb{R}\to X
by

(4.28)

which is bounded and continuous at

by the assumptions on

(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
and

.
Next, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we define

\psi_{t,t_{0},\varepsilon}(r)\coloneqq\frac{(t-t_{0})^{1-\varepsilon}\sin(\pi\varepsilon)}{\pi}(r+(t-t_{0}))^{-1}_{+}r^{\varepsilon-1}_{+},\quad r\in\mathbb{R}.

Shifting the integration variable by

and
applying (

) with

, we find

(4.30)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi_{t,t_{0},\varepsilon}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=\frac{(t-t_{0})^{1-\varepsilon}\sin(\pi\varepsilon)}{\pi}\int_{t-t_{0}}^{\infty}r^{-1}(r-(t-t_{0}))^{\varepsilon-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=1.

Moreover, we have for any

\displaystyle\int_{\{\lvert r\rvert\geq\delta\}}\lvert\psi_{t,t_{0},\varepsilon}(r)\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=\frac{(t-t_{0})^{1-\varepsilon}\sin(\pi\varepsilon)}{\pi}\int_{\delta}^{\infty}(r+t-t_{0})^{-1}r^{\varepsilon-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

as

of
[

.

\frac{\sin(\pi\varepsilon)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{\varepsilon-1}}{\tau+1}S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))g(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau=[\psi_{t,t_{0},\varepsilon}*f_{t,t_{0}}](0),

the first assertion now follows by applying the obvious vector-valued
generalization
of
[

, which gives

[\psi_{t,t_{0},\varepsilon}*f_{t,t_{0}}](0)\to f_{t,t_{0}}(0)=S(t-t_{0})g(t_{0})\quad\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

(ii)
holds.
By Proposition

, the left-hand side

for all

. Since

.
∎

(iii)
is satisfied,
then

(4.37)

\|u(t)\|_{X}\leq\biggl[M_{0}\overline{\Gamma}(\{s\},w(t-t_{0}))+e^{-w(t-t_{0})}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\frac{M_{\{s\}+k-1}}{\Gamma(\{s\}+k)}\biggr]\|x\|_{X},

where
w\in[0,\infty)
and
M_{0},M_{\{s\}},\dots,M_{s}\in[1,\infty)

are as in (
2.1
)
and (
4.35
).

Proof.

The substitution
g\equiv x
and
the change of variables
\sigma\coloneqq(t-t_{0})(1+\tau)
in the first

) produces

(4.38)

\frac{\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi}(t-t_{0})^{\{s\}}\int_{t-t_{0}}^{\infty}\sigma^{-1}(\sigma-(t-t_{0}))^{-\{s\}}S(\sigma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\,x,

which is equal to
\overline{\Gamma}(\{s\},(t-t_{0})A)x

by (
4.19
).

Now suppose that
s\in(1,\infty)
and let

2.1
(ii)

be satisfied. Then for any
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
we have

\mathfrak{I}^{\alpha}g(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{\alpha-1}S(\tau)x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau=A^{-\alpha}x

by (
2.13
), so that

\mathfrak{D}^{\beta}g(t)=(\partial_{t}+A)^{\lceil\beta\rceil}\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil\beta\rceil-\beta}g(t)=A^{\lceil\beta\rceil}A^{\beta-\lceil\beta\rceil}x=A^{\beta}x

for all
\beta\in[0,s-1]
, hence the remaining terms of (
4.3
)
become

(4.39)

\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{\{s\}+k-1}}{\Gamma(\{s\}+k)}S(t-t_{0})A^{\{s\}+k-1}x,

proving (
4.36
) in view of (
4.34
).
Estimate (
4.37
) follows from (
2.1
)
(and (
4.35
)).
∎

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem

If Assumption

(ii)

to (

4.2.1.
Integer-order case

Let us consider the case
s=n\in\mathbb{N}
, in which the operator
\mathfrak{D}^{n}=(\partial_{t}+A)^{n}
is local in time. Note that the first-order case
s=1

was already treated in Section

. The following
proposition is the key ingredient in the proof of
Theorem
4.5
for
s=n
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
(ii)

holds. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in[1,\infty]
,
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and

u\in\mathsf{D}((\partial_{t}+A)^{n})
.
For all
t\in(t_{0},\infty)
, we have

(4.40)

\mathfrak{I}^{n}_{t_{0}}(\partial_{t}+A)^{n}u(t)=u(t)-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{k}}{k!}S(t-t_{0})[(\partial_{t}+A)^{k}u](t_{0}).

Here,
\mathfrak{I}_{t_{0}}^{s}\in\mathscr{L}(L^{p}(t_{0},\infty;X))
denotes the
(Riemann–Liouville type) fractional
parabolic
integral, defined by

(4.41)

\mathfrak{I}_{t_{0}}^{s}u(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}(t-\tau)^{s-1}S(t-\tau)u(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau,\quad u\in L^{p}(J;X),\;\text{a.e.\ }t\in(t_{0},\infty).

Proof.

We use induction on
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
For the base case
n=1
, let us fix an arbitrary

u\in W^{1,p}(J;X)\cap L^{p}(J;\mathsf{D}(A))\hookrightarrow C_{\mathrm{b}}(\overline{J};X)
.
Since
u(t)\in\mathsf{D}(A)
a.e., we find in particular that
u(t_{0})\in\overline{\mathsf{D}(A)}
.
Now the result follows by applying
Proposition
2.4
(i)
\implies
(ii)

with

f\coloneqq u^{\prime}+Au\in L^{p}(J;X)
.

Now suppose that the statement is true for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We present the argument for
t_{0}=0
, the other cases being analogous.
Fix
u\in\mathsf{D}((\partial_{t}+A)^{n+1})
and
apply the induction hypothesis to the function

(\partial_{t}+A)u\in\mathsf{D}((\partial_{t}+A)^{n})
,
yielding

(4.42)

(\partial_{t}+A)u(t)=\mathfrak{I}^{n}_{t_{0}}(\partial_{t}+A)^{n+1}u(t)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}S(t)[(\partial_{t}+A)^{k+1}u](0)

for all
t\in\overline{J}
. Applying
\mathfrak{I}^{1}
to both sides of the
above equation, we can use the case
n=1
along with
Propositions
2.6
(c)
–
(d)

to find

(4.43)

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{I}^{n+1}_{t_{0}}(\partial_{t}+A)^{n+1}u(t)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{t^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}S(t)[(\partial_{t}+A)^{k+1}u](0)+S(t)u(0)

∎

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{I}^{n+1}_{t_{0}}(\partial_{t}+A)^{n+1}u(t)+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}S(t)[(\partial_{t}+A)^{k}u](0).

We can now prove the integer-order case of
Theorem
4.5
:

Proof of Theorem
4.5
(for
s=n\in\mathbb{N}
).

Let
u
be an
L^{p}
-solution to (
4.1
). By
Definition
4.2
and the
observations in the beginning of
Section
4.2
, we have
u\in C_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbb{R};X)
,
u\equiv g
on
(-\infty,t_{0}]
and
(\partial_{t}+A)^{n}u=0
a.e. on
(t_{0},\infty)
.
Applying
\mathfrak{I}_{t_{0}}^{s}
to both sides of the latter and using
Proposition
4.9
on the left-hand side, we find for all
t\in(t_{0},\infty)
:

(4.44)

u(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{k}}{k!}S(t-t_{0})[(\partial_{t}+A)^{k}u](t_{0}).

Note that the operators
(\partial_{t}+A)^{k}
are local in time and
that, in
fact, we can choose to interpret
\partial_{t}
as a left derivative. Thus,
since
u\equiv g
on
(-\infty,t_{0}]
, we obtain (
4.4
).
∎

If
u\in\mathsf{D}((\partial_{t}+A)^{k})
is sufficiently regular, say if

u\in C^{j}(\overline{J};\mathsf{D}(A^{k-j}))
(
j
times continuously differentiable)
for all

, then we have the pointwise binomial

[(\partial_{t}+A)^{k}u](t)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}A^{k-j}u^{(j)}(t),\quad\forall t\in\overline{J},

where
u^{(j)}
denotes the
j
th (classical) derivative of
u
.
Substituting this into (

), using the definition of binomial
coefficients, interchanging the order of summation and shifting the inner

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}

\displaystyle\frac{t^{k}}{k!}S(t)[(\partial_{t}+A)^{k}u](0)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{t^{k}}{j!(k-j)!}S(t)A^{k-j}u^{(j)}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{k=j}^{n-1}\frac{t^{k}}{j!(k-j)!}S(t)A^{k-j}u^{(j)}(0)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{t^{j}}{j!}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-j-1}\frac{t^{\ell}}{\ell!}S(t)A^{\ell}u^{(j)}(0).

Moreover, note that for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\mathsf{D}(A^{n-1})
and
t\in(0,\infty)
we have

(4.45)

\overline{\Gamma}(n,tA)x=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}S(t)A^{k}x,

cf. (
4.20
). Together, these observations imply
that, in this situation, equation (

) takes on the following form:

(4.46)

\mathfrak{I}^{n}_{t_{0}}(\partial_{t}+A)^{n}u(t)=u(t)-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{k}}{k!}\overline{\Gamma}(n-k,(t-t_{0})A)x_{k}.

4.2.2.
Fractional-order case

Now we turn to the proof of Theorem
4.5
in the case
that
s\in(0,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
. It relies on the following result, which
is the fractional-order analog to Proposition

.

Theorem 4.10
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
(ii)

holds.
Let
f\in L^{p}(-\infty,t_{0};X)
for some
p\in[1,\infty]
and

t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
\widetilde{f}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R};X)
denote its
extension by zero to the whole of
\mathbb{R}
.
Let
s\in(0,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
be such that (

)
is
satisfied.
Then the following
identity holds for
all

:

(4.47)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}^{s}\widetilde{f}(t)=\frac{\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{-\{s\}}}{\tau+1}S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))\mathfrak{I}^{s}f(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(4.48)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{\{s\}+k-1}}{\Gamma(\{s\}+k)}S(t-t_{0})\mathfrak{I}^{\lfloor s\rfloor-k+1}f(t_{0}).

Before proving this result, we show how it can indeed be
used to finish the proof of Theorem
4.5
:

Proof of Theorem
4.5
(
s\in(0,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
).

Let
u\in\mathsf{D}(\mathfrak{D}^{s})
be an
L^{p}
-solution
to (
4.1
). Applying
\mathfrak{D}^{s}
to
the second line of (
4.1
) yields

\mathfrak{D}^{s}u=\mathfrak{D}^{s}g

a.e. on
(-\infty,t_{0})
.
Combined with the first line, i.e.,

\mathfrak{D}^{s}u=0
a.e. on
(t_{0},\infty)
, we
find

\mathfrak{D}^{s}u=\widetilde{f}\quad\text{a.e.\ on }\mathbb{R},

where
f\coloneqq\mathfrak{D}^{s}g\in L^{p}(-\infty,t_{0};X)
. Now we apply
\mathfrak{I}^{s}
to both sides of this
equation, and use Proposition
3.3
(b)
and
Theorem
4.10
to the left-hand and right-hand sides,
respectively. Together, this yields, for all
t\in(t_{0},\infty)
,

(4.49)

u(t)=\begin{aligned} \frac{\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi}&\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{-\{s\}}}{\tau+1}S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))\mathfrak{I}^{s}[\mathfrak{D}^{s}g](t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{\{s\}+k-1}}{\Gamma(\{s\}+k)}S(t-t_{0})\mathfrak{I}^{\lfloor s\rfloor-k+1}[\mathfrak{D}^{s}g](t_{0}).\end{aligned}

In order to conclude that
u
satisfies equation (
4.3
), it
remains to observe that
\mathfrak{I}^{s}\mathfrak{D}^{s}g=g
and

\mathfrak{I}^{\lfloor s\rfloor-k+1}\mathfrak{D}^{s}g=\mathfrak{D}^{s-(\lfloor s\rfloor-k+1)}g=\mathfrak{D}^{\{s\}+k-1}g
for all

.
Indeed, the former follows from the natural analog of
Proposition
3.3
(b)
for functions defined
on

; for the latter, let

(4.50)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}^{m}\mathfrak{D}^{s}g=\mathfrak{I}^{m}\mathfrak{D}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\mathfrak{I}^{\lfloor s\rfloor-s}g

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{I}^{m}\mathfrak{D}^{m}\mathfrak{D}^{\lfloor s\rfloor-m}\mathfrak{I}^{\lfloor s\rfloor-s}g=\mathfrak{D}^{\lfloor s\rfloor-m}\mathfrak{I}^{\lfloor s\rfloor-m-(s-m)}g

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{D}^{\lfloor s-m\rfloor}\mathfrak{I}^{\lfloor s-m\rfloor-(s-m)}g=\mathfrak{D}^{s-m}g.

Here, we used the definition of

, the additivity of

, and the aforementioned analog to

. This completes the

.
∎

The proof of Theorem
4.10
involves expressing
an integral in terms of
fractional binomial coefficients, given by
[
23
, Equations (1.2.6),
(5.2.4) and (5.2.5)]
:

(4.52)

\binom{\alpha}{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{k!}\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}(\alpha-\ell)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{k!\,\Gamma(\alpha-k+1)},\quad\alpha\in(0,\infty),\,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Since the author is not aware of a direct reference for the following integral
identity,
a
proof is presented below for the sake of self-containedness.

Lemma 4.11
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
a,b\in(0,\infty)

and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

(4.53)

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(\pi\alpha)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\nicefrac{{a}}{{b}}}

\displaystyle\frac{\tau^{-\alpha}(a-b\tau)^{\alpha+n-1}}{\tau+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=(a+b)^{\alpha+n-1}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{\alpha+n-1}{n-k}a^{n-k}b^{\,k+\alpha-1}.

Proof.

By the change of variables
\sigma\coloneqq\frac{b}{a}\tau

and
[
23
, Equation (5.5.3)]
,
the validity of the identity (
4.53
) is
equivalent to that of

(4.54)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1}

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{-\alpha}(1-\sigma)^{\alpha+n-1}}{\sigma+\frac{b}{a}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=a^{1-n}b^{-\alpha}(a+b)^{\alpha+n-1}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{\alpha+n-1}{n-k}\biggl(\frac{b}{a}\biggr)^{\!k-1}.

We will verify this identity using induction on
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The base case

n=0
is a consequence of
[
23
, Equations (5.12.4)
and (5.12.1)]
:

(4.55)

\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{-\alpha}(1-\sigma)^{\alpha-1}}{\sigma+\frac{b}{a}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma=\left(1+\tfrac{b}{a}\right)^{\!\alpha-1}\left(\tfrac{b}{a}\right)^{\!-\alpha}=ab^{-\alpha}(a+b)^{\alpha-1}.

Now suppose that (
4.54
) holds for a given
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. In order to establish the identity for
n+1
, we write

1-\sigma=1+\frac{b}{a}-(\sigma+\frac{b}{a})
and
apply the induction hypothesis
and
[
23
, Equation (5.12.1)]
, respectively, to the resulting two
integrals:

(4.56)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{-\alpha}(1-\sigma)^{\alpha+n}}{\sigma+\frac{b}{a}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\frac{1+\frac{b}{a}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{-\alpha}(1-\sigma)^{\alpha+n-1}}{\sigma+\frac{b}{a}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\\
&-\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{-\alpha}(1-\sigma)^{\alpha+n-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=a^{-n}b^{-\alpha}(a+b)^{\alpha+n}-\left(\!1+\frac{b}{a}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{\alpha+n-1}{n-j}\biggl(\frac{b}{a}\biggr)^{\!j-1}-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n)}{n!\,\Gamma(\alpha)}.

For the latter two terms, we have

(4.57)

\displaystyle\left(\!1+\frac{b}{a}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{\alpha+n-1}{n-k}\biggl(\frac{b}{a}\biggr)^{\!k-1}+\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n)}{n!\,\Gamma(\alpha)}

(4.58)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{\alpha+n-1}{n-k}\biggl(\frac{b}{a}\biggr)^{\!k-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{\alpha+n-1}{n-k}\biggl(\frac{b}{a}\biggr)^{\!k}

(4.59)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\biggl[\binom{\alpha+n-1}{n-k}+\binom{\alpha+n-1}{n-k+1}\biggr]\biggl(\frac{b}{a}\biggr)^{\!k-1}+\biggl(\frac{b}{a}\biggr)^{\!\!n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\binom{\alpha+n}{n+1-k}\biggl(\frac{b}{a}\biggr)^{\!k-1}\!\!.

Indeed, to obtain the second line we note that

\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n)}{n!\,\Gamma(\alpha)}=\binom{\alpha+n-1}{n}
by (
4.52
),
which is the term
k=0
of the second summation on the second line;
shifting its index of summation and splitting off the last term yields
the first expression on the third line.
The final step uses
[
23
, Equation (1.2.7)]
and the fact that

(\frac{b}{a})^{n}

corresponds to the term
k=n+1
in the desired formula.
Putting the previous two displays together proves the induction step and
thereby the lemma.
∎

Remark 4.12
.

An alternative way to derive (
4.53
) is by noting that
the
integral can be expressed in terms of a hypergeometric
function
[
23
, Equation (15.6.1)]
to which one can apply the
transformation formula
[
23
, Equation (15.8.2)]
. This results in
a difference of two hypergeometric functions, whose
definitions can be written out to respectively yield an infinite and a
finite sum: The former is the fractional binomial expansion of

(a+b)^{\alpha+n-1}
and the latter consists of its first
n
terms,
and together this gives (
4.53
). In particular, we note
that (
4.53
) is formally equal to
the tail of a fractional binomial series. The
proof of Lemma
4.11
is more direct and avoids the need to
address the convergence of an infinite series.

Proof of Theorem
4.10
.

Fixing
t\in(t_{0},\infty)
, the semigroup law implies

(4.60)

S(t-r)=S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))S(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau-r)

for all
\tau\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-\infty,t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau)
.
This identity,
followed by (
2.1
), Hölder’s inequality
and equation (
4.18
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau}\biggl\|\frac{\tau^{-\{s\}}(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau-r)^{\gamma-1}}{\tau+1}S(t-r)f(r)\biggr\|_{X}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\quad\leq M_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{-\{s\}}}{\tau+1}e^{-w(t-t_{0})(\tau+1)}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau}\|k_{s}(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau-r)f(r)\|_{X}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{M_{0}\pi}{\sin(\pi\{s\})}\overline{\Gamma}(\{s\},w(t-t_{0}))\|k_{s}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(0,\infty;\mathscr{L}(X))}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R};X)}<\infty.

This justifies the use of Fubini’s theorem in the following:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{-\{s\}}}{\tau+1}S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))\mathfrak{I}^{s}f(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau}\frac{\tau^{-\{s\}}(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau-r)^{s-1}}{\tau+1}S(t-r)f(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{0}}\biggl[\int_{0}^{\frac{t_{0}-r}{t-t_{0}}}\frac{\tau^{-\{s\}}(t_{0}-r-(t-t_{0})\tau)^{s-1}}{\tau+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\biggr]S(t-r)f(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,

where we used (
4.60
) once more.
Lemma
4.11
and
equation (
4.52
) produce

(4.61)

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{t_{0}-r}{t-t_{0}}}\frac{\tau^{-\{s\}}(t_{0}-r-(t-t_{0})\tau)^{\{s\}+\lfloor s\rfloor-1}}{\tau+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=(t-r)^{s-1}-\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\frac{\Gamma(s)}{(\lfloor s\rfloor-k)!\,\Gamma(\{s\}+k)}(t_{0}-r)^{\lfloor s\rfloor-k}(t-t_{0})^{k+\{s\}-1}.

The previous two displays and the identity

S(t-r)=S(t-t_{0})S(t_{0}-r)
yield

(4.62)

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(\pi\{s\})}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{-\{s\}}}{\tau+1}S((t-t_{0})(\tau+1))\mathfrak{I}^{s}f(t_{0}-(t-t_{0})\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &-\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{\{s\}+k-1}S(t-t_{0})}{\Gamma(\{s\}+k)}\frac{1}{(\lfloor s\rfloor-k)!}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{0}}(t_{0}-r)^{\lfloor s\rfloor-k}S(t_{0}-r)f(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\\
&+\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{0}}(t-r)^{s-1}S(t-r)f(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{\{s\}+k-1}S(t-t_{0})}{\Gamma(\{s\}+k)}\mathfrak{I}^{\lfloor s\rfloor-k+1}f(t_{0})+\mathfrak{I}^{s}\widetilde{f}(t)

which is precisely (
4.48
). The final assertion follows from
Proposition
3.3
(b)
.
∎

5.
Comparison to Riemann–Liouville and Caputo Cauchy problems

In this section, we compare the Dirichlet
problem (
4.1
)
to fractional-order abstract Cauchy problems of the form

(5.1)

(\partial_{t}+A)^{s}u(t)=0,\quad t\in(t_{0},\infty),

augmented with initial conditions which depend on the interpretation of
the abstract space–time operator
(\partial_{t}+A)^{s}
,
acting on functions
u\colon J\to X
with
J=(t_{0},\infty)
for

t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
(instead of
J=\mathbb{R}
as in the previous sections). More
precisely, we will interpret

(\partial_{t}+A)^{s}
as a Riemann–Liouville or Caputo type fractional
parabolic derivative, respectively, on
L^{p}(J;X)

for
p\in[1,\infty]
, and determine the corresponding initial conditions
and mild solution formulae, see Definitions
5.2

and
5.3
.

For fractional time derivatives
\partial_{t}^{s}
, i.e., the case
A=0
,
the resulting solution concepts are well-known and commonly studied, see for
instance
[
13
, Chapter 3]
. In this case, the analog
to (
2.6
) has less
favorable
mapping properties
[
13
, Section 2.3]
and the well-posedness
of (
1.5
) is less clear.
We will show that, as in the case
A=0
,
the lowest-order term of the solution to the Riemann–Liouville type initial
value problem has a singularity at
t_{0}
in
general, whereas
the Caputo initial value problem yields the same solution for any two

s_{1},s_{2}\in(n,n+1)
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
In contrast, the solution from
Definition
4.2
is continuous at
t_{0}
under mild
assumptions on
g
or
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
and changes for all choices of
s\in(0,\infty)
.

Firstly, let us recall the Riemann–Liouville type fractional
parabolic
integral
\mathfrak{I}_{t_{0}}^{s}
on
L^{p}(J;X)
defined by (
4.41
).
Then, the Riemann–Liouville and Caputo type fractional
parabolic
derivatives
are respectively defined by

\mathfrak{D}^{s}_{\mathrm{RL}}\coloneqq(\partial_{t}+A)^{\lceil s\rceil}\mathfrak{I}_{t_{0}}^{\lceil s\rceil-s}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{D}^{s}_{\mathrm{C}}\coloneqq\mathfrak{I}_{t_{0}}^{\lceil s\rceil-s}(\partial_{t}+A)^{\lceil s\rceil}

on their maximal domains.
In order to derive mild solution formulae for
L^{p}
-solutions to the equations
\mathfrak{D}^{s}_{\mathrm{RL}}u=0
and
\mathfrak{D}^{s}_{\mathrm{C}}u=0
, we proceed analogously to
[
13
, Chapter 3]
and express
\mathfrak{I}_{t_{0}}^{s}\mathfrak{D}^{s}_{\mathrm{RL}}u
and
\mathfrak{I}_{t_{0}}^{s}\mathfrak{D}^{s}_{\mathrm{C}}u
in terms of initial data from
u
(compare with Proposition
4.9
and Theorem
4.10
), so that applying
\mathfrak{I}^{s}_{t_{0}}
on both sides of the equations motivates the definitions.
The integer-order case
s=n\in\mathbb{N}
,
where

\mathfrak{D}^{n}=\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{RL}}^{n}=\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{C}}^{n}=(\partial_{t}+A)^{n},

was treated in Section
4.2.1
.
From these results, we derive the following proposition regarding

\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{RL}}^{s}
and

\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{C}}^{s}
for fractional
s\in(0,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
:

Proposition 5.1
.

Let Assumption
2.1
(i)

be satisfied. If
s\in(0,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
,
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
p\in[1,\infty]
and
u\in\mathsf{D}(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{RL}}^{s})
, then for almost all
t\in J\coloneqq(t_{0},\infty)
:

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}_{t_{0}}^{s}\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{RL}}^{s}u(t)

\displaystyle=u(t)-\frac{(t-t_{0})^{\{s\}-1}}{\Gamma(\{s\})}S(t-t_{0})\mathfrak{I}_{\mathrm{RL}}^{1-\{s\}}u(t_{0})

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{k+\{s\}-1}}{\Gamma(k+\{s\})}S(t-t_{0})\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{RL}}^{k+\{s\}-1}u(t_{0}).

If

u\in\mathsf{D}(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{C}}^{s})
is such that

u\in C^{j}(\overline{J};\mathsf{D}(A^{n-1-j}))

for all
j\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, then we have for almost all
t\in J\coloneqq(t_{0},\infty)
:

(5.3)

\mathfrak{I}_{t_{0}}^{s}\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{C}}^{s}u(t)=u(t)-\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{k}}{k!}\overline{\Gamma}(\lceil s\rceil-k,(t-t_{0})A)u^{(k)}(t_{0})\quad\text{a.e.}

Proof.

For the sake of notational convenience we only present the case
t_{0}=0
.
The definition of
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{RL}}^{s}
, along with
Propositions
2.6
(c)
–
(d)

and
4.9
, yields

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil s\rceil-s}_{0}\mathfrak{I}^{s}_{0}\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{RL}}^{s}u

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil s\rceil}_{0}\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{RL}}^{s}u=\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil s\rceil}_{0}(\partial_{t}+A)^{\lceil s\rceil}\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil s\rceil-s}_{0}u

(5.5)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil s\rceil-s}_{0}u-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(\,\cdot\,)^{k}}{k!}S(\,\cdot\,)[(\partial_{t}+A)^{k}\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil s\rceil-s}_{0}u](0)

(5.6)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil s\rceil-s}_{0}\biggl[u-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(\,\cdot\,)^{k-\lceil s\rceil+s}S(\,\cdot\,)}{\Gamma(k+s-\lceil s\rceil+1)}[(\partial_{t}+A)^{k}\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil s\rceil-s}_{0}u](0)\biggr]

for any
u\in\mathsf{D}(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{RL}}^{s})
.
The first assertion then follows from Proposition
3.3
and
the injectivity of
\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil s\rceil-s}_{0}
.

If
u\in\mathsf{D}(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{C}}^{s})
, combining the definition with
Proposition
2.6
(c)

produces

(5.7)

\mathfrak{I}^{s}_{0}\mathfrak{D}_{\mathrm{C}}^{s}u=\mathfrak{I}^{s}_{0}\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil s\rceil-s}_{0}(\partial_{t}+A)^{\lceil s\rceil}u=\mathfrak{I}^{\lceil s\rceil}_{0}(\partial_{t}+A)^{\lceil s\rceil}u,

so that the result follows from Proposition
4.9

and the discussion below it, in particular
equation (
4.46
).
∎

Note that

\mathfrak{I}_{0}^{1-\{s\}}u
need not vanish at
t_{0}=0
. Indeed, even if it
is continuous, it may not
satisfy (
4.41
) pointwise, as evidenced by the example

u\coloneqq k_{\{s\}}\otimes x

for
p\in[1,\frac{1}{\{s\}-1})
and
x\in\overline{\mathsf{D}(A)}\setminus\{0\}
,
see
Proposition
2.6
(d)
.

Proposition
5.1
motivates the following definition of
the
Riemann–Liouville fractional abstract Cauchy type problem and its
corresponding solution.

Definition 5.2
.

Let Assumption
2.1
(i)

be satisfied. For any
s\in(0,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
and
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
the mild solution to the Riemann–Liouville abstract Cauchy type
problem

(5.8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathfrak{D}^{s}_{\mathrm{RL}}u(t)&=0,&&t\in J\coloneqq(t_{0},\infty),\\
\mathfrak{I}^{1-\{s\}}_{t_{0}}u(t_{0})&=x_{0}\in X,&&\\
\mathfrak{D}^{k+\{s\}-1}_{\mathrm{RL}}u(t_{0})&=x_{k}\in\overline{\mathsf{D}(A)},&&k\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor s\rfloor\},\end{aligned}\right.

is the function

u_{\mathrm{RL}}\in C(J;X)
defined by

(5.9)

u_{\mathrm{RL}}(t)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{k+\{s\}-1}}{\Gamma(k+\{s\})}S(t-t_{0})x_{k},\quad t\in J.

Compared with
Definition
4.2
, we first note that the terms
k\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor s\rfloor\}
in (
5.9
) are almost identical to
those of (
4.3
), up to the difference between taking Riemann–Liouville
type fractional parabolic derivatives of the function
u
defined on
J
and
Weyl type derivatives of
g
defined on
\mathbb{R}\setminus J
. The remaining term, on
the other hand, differs significantly. In (
5.8
), we see that

x_{0}
is the prescribed value of
\mathfrak{I}^{1-\{s\}}_{t_{0}}u
at
t_{0}
and

u_{\mathrm{RL}}
is continuous at
t_{0}
if and only if
x_{0}=0
, in view of
the singularity occurring there for
x_{0}\neq 0
. In contrast, the
solution to (
4.1
) given by
Definition
4.2
is bounded by
Proposition
4.3
, does in fact prescribe the
value
u(t_{0})=g(t_{0})
and is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
under some further
regularity assumptions.

The following definition of a Caputo type initial value
problem and corresponding solution can also be derived from
Proposition
5.1
:

Definition 5.3
.

Let Assumption
2.1
(i)

be satisfied. For any
s\in(0,\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
and
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
the mild solution to the Caputo abstract Cauchy problem

(C-ACP
s
)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathfrak{D}^{s}_{\mathrm{C}}u(t)&=0,&t&\in J\coloneqq(t_{0},\infty),\\
u^{(k)}(t_{0})&=x_{k}\in\mathsf{D}(A^{\lfloor s\rfloor-k}),&k&\in\{0,\dots,\lfloor s\rfloor\}.\end{aligned}\right.

is the function

u_{\mathrm{C}}\in C(J;X)
defined by

(5.10)

u_{\mathrm{C}}(t)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}\frac{(t-t_{0})^{k}}{k!}\overline{\Gamma}(\lceil s\rceil-k,(t-t_{0})A)x_{k},\quad t\in J.

Note that this definition has the same form as
the integer-order abstract Cauchy problem from
Definition
4.2
, i.e., formula (
4.4
).
Analogously, for sufficiently regular

x_{k}

or
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
, this solution allows for the specification of the
value of
u_{\mathrm{C}}(t_{0})
.
However, in contrast to the solution in
the sense of Definition
4.2
, we observe that the form
of
u_{\mathrm{C}}
only changes “discretely in
s
,” i.e., the solutions for
any two
s_{1},s_{2}\in(n,n+1)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
are given by the same formula.
