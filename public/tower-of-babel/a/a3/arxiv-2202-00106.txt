On the Strong Comparison Principle for Degenerate Elliptic Problems with Convection
Jiří Benedikt, Petr Girg, Lukáš Kotrla, Peter Takáč

Abstract
. The weak and strong comparison principles ( WCP and SCP , respectively) are investigated for quasilinear elliptic boundary value problems with the p p -Laplacian in one space dimension, Δ p ​ ( u ) = def d d ​ x ​ ( | u ′ | p − 2 ​ u ′ ) . \Delta_{p}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}\right)\,. We treat the “degenerate” case of 2 < p < ∞ 2<p<\infty and allow also for the nontrivial convection velocity b : [ − 1 , 1 ] → ℝ b:\,[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R} in the underlying domain Ω = ( − 1 , 1 ) \Omega=(-1,1) . We establish the WCP under a rather general, “natural sufficient condition” on the convection velocity, b ⁡ ( x ) b(x) , and the reaction function, φ ⁡ ( x , u ) \varphi(x,u) . Furthermore, we establish also the SCP under a number of various additional hypotheses. In contrast, with these hypotheses being violated, we construct also a few rather natural counterexamples to the SCP and discuss their applications to an interesting classical problem of fluid flow in porous medium, “seepage flow of fluids in inclined bed” . Our methods are based on a mixture of classical and new techniques.

1.
Introduction

The
weak
and

strong comparison principles

(
WCP
and
SCP
, respectively,
for brevity)
play an important role in the theory of elliptic and parabolic problems
for partial differential equations.
In our present work we focus on such problems in one space dimension
that “idealize” long, thin domains

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
of type

\Omega=\omega\times(a,b)

where solutions are expected to be rather homogeneous
on the relatively small cross-section

\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
(a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
)
of the relatively long domain
\Omega=\omega\times(a,b)
.
Here, we assume that

\mathrm{diameter}(\omega)\ll|b-a|
meaning that the ratio

\mathrm{diameter}(\omega)/|b-a|
is a sufficiently small number

{}<1
.

In general, in an arbitrary domain

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
of space dimension
N
(
N\geq 1
),
we consider the elliptic partial differential equation

(1.1)

\left\{\hskip 5.69054pt\begin{aligned} {}-\Delta_{p}u-\mathbf{b}(x)\cdot\nabla u+\varphi(x,u)&=f(x)&&\qquad\mbox{ for }\,x\in\Omega\,;\\
u&=0&&\qquad\mbox{ on }\,\partial\Omega\,.\end{aligned}\right.\hfill

Here, we assume that
2<p<\infty
,

\Delta_{p}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\mathrm{div}\left(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\right)

stands for
p
-Laplacian,

\mathbf{b}:\,\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N}

is an essentially bounded measurable vector field
which stands for the
convection velocity
,
often termed also

drift or advection velocity
,
the
reaction function

\varphi:\,\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

is continuously partially differentiable with respect
to the second variable,
u
, with the partial derivative

\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial u}(x,u)

>0
for all

(x,u)\in\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})
, and

\varphi(x,0)=0
for all
x\in\Omega
.
Finally,
f:\,\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
describes the volumetric source
density,
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
; sometimes we will take

f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C(\Omega)
or similar.

Under some reasonable hypotheses on the vector field
\mathbf{b}

and the function
\varphi
,
a standard
weak comparison
result for
this Dirichlet boundary value problem is established in the monograph by

D. Gilbarg
and
N. S. Trudinger

[
9
, Sect. 10, pp. 263–271]
,
Theorem 10.1 (p. 263) and Theorem 10.7 (p. 268).
In contrast, the corresponding
strong comparison
and

Hopf-type comparison
results are, in general,

not valid
under analogous hypotheses; cf.
M. Guedda
and
L. Véron

[
10
]
,
Prop. 2.1 (pp. 886–887) and Prop. 2.2 (p. 888),
and later work in

M. Cuesta
and
P. Takáč

[
4
,
5
]
.
We begin this article by giving a few simple counterexamples
to the strong comparison principle.
Our counterexamples will be constructed in the one-dimensional domain

\Omega=(-1,1)\subset\mathbb{R}
.
In fact, they have motivated the entire work presented in this article.
Our main goal is to find reasonable sufficient conditions
on the (now) scalar field

\mathbf{b}\equiv b:\,[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{1}

and the reaction function
\varphi

that would exclude such counterexamples;
we will formulate sufficient conditions on
b
and
\varphi

that will guarantee the validity of the strong comparison principle.
While assuming
\varphi\equiv 0
, we formulate sufficient conditions on
the
convection velocity

b
in Section
3
.
The main purpose of the counterexamples will be to “illustrate”
the necessity of our sufficient conditions.

1.1.
Mathematical Problems of weak and strong
Elliptic Comparison Principles

The one-dimensional analogue of
the elliptic partial differential equation (
1.1
)
has been investigated in the work by

M. Cuesta
and
P. Takáč

[
4
,
5
]
.
More precisely, this work treats the special case with

b(x)\equiv 0
in an open interval

\Omega=(-1,1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{1}
and

\varphi(x,u)=\lambda\,|u|^{p-2}u
for all

x\in(-1,1)
and all
u\in\mathbb{R}=(-\infty,\infty)
,
with a constant
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
large enough,
say,
\lambda\geq\lambda_{p}>0
.
In this way, the original problem (
1.1
) is reduced to
the following two-point boundary value problem,

(1.2)

\left\{\hskip 5.69054pt\begin{aligned} {}-\left(|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}+\lambda\,|u|^{p-2}u&=f(x)\qquad\mbox{ for }\,x\in(-1,1)\,;\\
u(\pm 1)&=0\,.\end{aligned}\right.

This clearly is a variational problem on the Sobolev space

W_{0}^{1,p}(-1,1)
with the left-hand side

[\mathcal{A}u](x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}{}-\left(|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}+\lambda\,|u|^{p-2}u\,,\quad x\in(-1,1)\,,

being a
monotone operator

\mathcal{A}:\,W_{0}^{1,p}(-1,1)\to W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(-1,1)

from its domain
W_{0}^{1,p}(-1,1)
into the dual space

W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(-1,1)=\left[W_{0}^{1,p}(-1,1)\right]^{\prime}

where
p^{\prime}=p/(p-1)
.
We note the continuous imbedding

L^{\infty}(-1,1)=\left[L^{1}(-1,1)\right]^{\prime}\hookrightarrow W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(-1,1)

that is dual to the Sobolev imbedding

W_{0}^{1,p}(-1,1)\hookrightarrow L^{1}(-1,1)\,.

The strict monotonicity of
\mathcal{A}
hinges upon the fact that

\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial u}(x,u)=\lambda\,(p-1)\,|u|^{p-2}>0

is valid for all

(x,u)\in\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})
.
It forces the
WCP

(
weak comparison principle
,
proved in Proposition
2.1
),
by a standard variational argument that we will explain in
Section
2
below.
In this step, what matters is the following observation:

The larger the constant
\lambda\geq 0
,
the “stronger” the strict monotonicity of
\mathcal{A}
.

For
\lambda=0
, even the
SCP

(
strong comparison principle
)
for problem (
1.2
) is established in

[
4
,
5
]
.
Nevertheless, the strong comparison principle fails to hold for every

\lambda>0
large enough, say,
\lambda\geq\lambda_{p}>0
,
as is shown in

M. Cuesta
and
P. Takáč

[
5
, Sect. 4, Example 4.1, pp. 740–741]
;
see Example
1.1
in §
1.2
below.

This is precisely the problem we treat in the present article:

Conditions that are favorable for the validity of
the
WCP

may have a
negative effect
on the validity of
the
SCP
.

The latter may fail to hold under those conditions
(in Example
1.1
).

Our strategy will be as follows:
In Section
2
we establish
the
WCP

under rather simple, but still “reasonably” general conditions
on the
convection velocity

b:\,\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
in the interval

\Omega=(-1,1)\subset\mathbb{R}

and the
reaction function

\varphi:\,\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

In the remaining parts of this work we then
assume
the validity of
the
WCP

and try to formulate “reasonable” sufficient conditions
that guarantee the validity of the
SCP
.
We also investigate the “sharpness” of our sufficient conditions
in Section
3
.
We provide an interesting new counterexample to the
SCP

in Example
3.6
for the special case
\varphi\equiv 0
.

A classical treatment of
the
WCP
and

can be found in the monographs by

and

[

]

(see also the article

]

.

Last but not least, we should mention that the first sharp results

]

Several engineering problems connected with

-Laplacian,

As far as applications of the two comparison principles,

WCP
and

,
to these engineering problems are concerned,
the following difference between them is of current interest:

If the source term
f(x)
on the right-hand side
in problem (
1.1
) is increased to a function
g(x)

(
g\geq f
almost everywhere (a.e.) in
\Omega
)
only
locally
near a point
x_{0}\in\Omega
, i.e.,

g(x)>f(x)
holds for almost all
x\in B_{r}(x_{0})\subset\Omega

in a small ball
B_{r}(x_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\,|x-x_{0}|<r\}\subset\Omega

of radius
r>0
centered at
x_{0}\in\Omega
,

does
the solution
u(x)

increase
to
v(x)

(for notation, see problems (
1.3
) in
the next paragraph, §
1.2
)

(i)

only
locally
near the point
x_{0}\in\Omega
, i.e.,

v(x)>u(x)
holds for all
x\in\Omega
near the point
x_{0}
, say,
for all
x\in B_{R}(x_{0})\subset\Omega
,
but
v(x)=u(x)
holds for all

x\in\Omega\setminus B_{R}(x_{0})
, with some radius
R
,
r<R<\infty
,

or

(ii)

the inequality
v(x)>u(x)
holds for all
x\in\Omega
, that is,

globally
throughout the entire domain
\Omega
?

The first statement,
(i)
, means that the
SCP

fails to hold, whereas the second statement,
(ii)
, is equivalent to
the
SCP
.

1.2.
A Simple Counterexample to the Strong Comparison Principle

We begin by recalling a special case from the work by

M. Cuesta
and
P. Takáč

[
5
, Sect. 4, Example 4.1, pp. 740–741]
,
where a counterexample to the SCP for
the two-point boundary value problem in eq. (
1.2
) above
is constructed by making the special choice
b(x)\equiv 0
and

\varphi(x,u)=\lambda\,|u|^{p-2}u
for all

x\in(-1,1)
and all
u\in\mathbb{R}=(-\infty,\infty)

in the following two-point boundary value problems
with
2<p<\infty
:

(1.3)

\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} {}-\left(|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}-b(x)\,u^{\prime}+\varphi(x,u)&=f(x)\,,\\
{}-\left(|v^{\prime}|^{p-2}v^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}-b(x)\,v^{\prime}+\varphi(x,v)&=g(x)\,;\\
\quad\mbox{ with }\quad u(\pm 1)\leq v(\pm 1)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad f(x)&\leq g(x)\;\mbox{ for }\,x\in(-1,1)\,.\end{aligned}\right.

The constant
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
that appears in the reaction function

\varphi(x,u)=\lambda\,u^{p-2}u

in eq. (
1.3
)
has to be chosen large enough, say,
\lambda\geq\lambda_{p}>0
.
As we work with nonnegative solutions

u,v:\,[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
to problems (
1.3
) only,
it suffices to define the reaction function

\varphi(x,u)=\lambda\,u^{p-1}
for all

x\in(-1,1)
and all
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)
only.

As the boundary value problems in (
1.3
)
in our present article allow for the (additional) convection terms

b(x)\,u^{\prime}
and
b(x)\,v^{\prime}
, respectively,
our counterexample below is a slight modification of
that one mentioned above
[
5
, Example 4.1]

(with
b(x)\equiv 0
).

1.3.
Interpretation for non-Newtonian fluid flows
(e.g., of a pollutant)

The case of vanishing
convection velocity

\mathbf{b}:\,\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N}

in the general
N
-dimensional elliptic problem (
1.1
)
leads to a very popular variational problem for
the
critical points

of the corresponding energy functional

(1.20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\Phi(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x\,,\quad u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\,;

\displaystyle\mbox{ with }\quad\Phi(x,s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{s}\varphi(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}t\quad\mbox{ for }\,(x,s)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\,,

defined on the Sobolev space
W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
.
This variational problem has been investigated in numerous articles
during at least the last seven decades.
A classical reference for this quasilinear elliptic problem is
the monograph by
J.-L. Lions

[
11
]
.
More recent results can be found in

P. Drábek

[
6
]
,

P. Drábek
,
A. Kufner
, and
F. Nicolosi

[
7
]
, and

P. Takáč

[
17
]
,
and in the numerous references therein
(
[
6
,
7
,
17
]
).

The variational approach does not allow for
the
convection term

\mathbf{b}(x)\cdot\nabla u
in problem (
1.1
).
It can be treated by methods of

pseudo-monotone operators

(
[
11
, Chapt. 2, §2.4 – §2.5, pp. 179–182]
)
or fixed points for the variational problem
(
[
11
, Chapt. 2, §2.6, pp. 182–190]
).
A more recent approach to the non-variational problem (
1.1
)
with a convection term can be found in

J. García-Melián
,
J. C. Sabina de Lis
, and

P. Takáč

[
8
]
.

Finally, if the WCP is valid
(as stated in Proposition
2.1
below),
then also
monotone methods

can be applied to the non-variational problem (
1.1
).

From the engineer’s point of view
(cf.
[
3
]
),
the
WCP

means that increasing the content of certain substance
(e.g., a pollutant such as oil or nitrate)
in the model described by the elliptic partial differential equation
(
1.1
) by means of the source function
f(x)

leads to an increase of the substance density
u(x)

throughout the entire domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
The point here is that the increase from
u(x)
to, say,

v(x)
does not have to be strict at every point
x\in\Omega
,
that is, the strict inequality
u(x)<v(x)
might not hold in spite of
the increase of
f(x)
to
g(x)
with
f(x)\leq g(x)

for every
x\in\Omega
and
f\not\equiv g
in
\Omega
.
Here, we have replaced the pair
(f,u)
by the new pair
(g,v)

in eq. (
1.1
); cf. eqs. (
1.3
).

In particular, if pollution by the substance with
the density
u(x)
in the model is caused locally by
the source function
f(x)
,
by increasing the source function
f(x)
to
g(x)
with
f(x)\leq g(x)

and
f\not\equiv g
,
a global pullution increase
from the substance density
u(x)
to
v(x)
with
u(x)<v(x)

(throughout the entire domain
\Omega
),
occurs if and only if the
SCP
is valid.

As usual, given a pair of functions

f,g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
the relation
f\not\equiv g
in
\Omega
means that the set

\{x\in\Omega:\,f(x)\neq g(x)\}

has positive
N
-dimensional Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
If
f,g\in C(\Omega)
, then

f\not\equiv g
in
\Omega
means that the set

\{x\in\Omega:\,f(x)\neq g(x)\}

contains an open ball
B_{r}(x_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\,|x-x_{0}|<r\}\subset\Omega

of radius
r>0
.

2.
The Weak Comparison Principle

In this section we focus on the
WCP
,
a weaker form of the
Comparison Principle

that we wish to investigate.
To this end, let us consider
the two-point boundary value problems (
1.3
)
stated in the Introduction
(Section
1
, §
1.2
).
In the next paragraph, §
2.1
,
we provide a simple, natural sufficient condition on the functions

b:\,[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
and

\varphi:\,[-1,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

which guarantee the validity of the
WCP
,
that is to say,

u(x)\leq v(x)
for all
x\in[-1,1]
.

2.1.
A Natural Sufficient Condition

We assume that the functions
b(x)
,
\varphi(x,u)
,
f(x)
, and
g(x)

satisfy the following hypothesis:

(M)

b:\,[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable,
both

\varphi,\;\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial u}:\,[-1,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

are continuous, and the following

“monotonicity”
inequality is satisfied:

(2.1)

\frac{1}{2}\,b^{\prime}(x)+\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial u}(x,s)\geq 0\quad\mbox{ for all }\,(x,s)\in(-1,1)\times\mathbb{R}\,.

In addition, we assume that
f,g\in L^{\infty}(-1,1)
.

In order to obtain a weak comparison principle for problem
(
1.3
) analogous to Theorem 10.7 in

[
9
, Sect. 10, §10.4, p. 268]
,
we establish the following rather general result:

Proposition 2.1

(Weak Comparison Principle)
.

Assume
Hypothesis
(M)
.
If
f,g\in L^{\infty}(-1,1)
satisfy

f\leq g
a.e. in
(-1,1)
, and

u(-1)\leq v(-1)
and
u(+1)\leq v(+1)
, then

u\leq v
holds everywhere in
[-1,1]
.

Proof.

We follow similar ideas as does the proof of Theorem 10.7
(under condition (iii))
in
[
9
, pp. 270–271]
.
Subtracting the differential equations for
u
and
v
in (
1.3
)
from each other, we arrive at

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}-|v^{\prime}|^{p-2}v^{\prime}\right)-b(x)\,(u^{\prime}-v^{\prime})+\left[\varphi(x,u)-\varphi(x,v)\right]

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle=f(x)-g(x)\leq 0\quad\mbox{ for }\,x\in(-1,1)\,.

We multiply this inequality by the function

w\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(u-v)^{+}
,

w\in W_{0}^{1,p}(-1,1)
,
which has the vanishing boundary values
w(\pm 1)=0
,
and integrate with respect to
x\in(-1,1)
, thus arriving at

(2.2)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\left(|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}-|v^{\prime}|^{p-2}v^{\prime}\right)w^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{-1}^{1}b(x)\,(u^{\prime}-v^{\prime})\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+\int_{-1}^{1}\left[\varphi(x,u)-\varphi(x,v)\right]w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-1}^{1}(f-g)\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 0\,.

Since the boundary conditions

u(-1)\leq v(-1)
and
u(+1)\leq v(+1)
guarantee

w(\pm 1)=0
, the second integral on the left-hand side of
eq. (
2.2
) becomes

(2.3)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}b(x)\,(u^{\prime}-v^{\prime})\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}b(x)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\,[w(x)]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x={}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}b^{\prime}(x)\,[w(x)]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

In order to treat the first and third integrals on the left-hand side
of eq. (
2.2
), we use the standard formulas

(2.4)

\displaystyle|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}-|v^{\prime}|^{p-2}v^{\prime}

\displaystyle{}=(p-1)\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left|(1-\theta)\,u^{\prime}(x)+\theta\,v^{\prime}(x)\right|^{p-2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right](u^{\prime}-v^{\prime})\,,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\varphi(x,u)-\varphi(x,v)

\displaystyle{}=\left[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial u}\bigl(x,\,(1-\theta)\,u(x)+\theta\,v(x)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right](u-v)\,.

We insert eqs. (
2.3
), (
2.4
), and
(
2.5
) into eq. (
2.2
), thus obtaining

\displaystyle(p-1)\int_{-1}^{1}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left|(1-\theta)\,u^{\prime}(x)+\theta\,v^{\prime}(x)\right|^{p-2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right][w^{\prime}(x)]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}b^{\prime}(x)\,[w(x)]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+\int_{-1}^{1}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial u}\bigl(x,\,(1-\theta)\,u(x)+\theta\,v(x)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right][w(x)]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-1}^{1}(f-g)\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 0

or, equivalently,

(2.6)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle(p-1)\int_{-1}^{1}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left|(1-\theta)\,u^{\prime}(x)+\theta\,v^{\prime}(x)\right|^{p-2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right][w^{\prime}(x)]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+\int_{-1}^{1}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\,b^{\prime}(x)+\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial u}\Bigl(x,\,(1-\theta)\,u(x)+\theta\,v(x)\Bigr)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right][w(x)]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}(f-g)\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 0\,.

Finally, we take

in inequality (

) and insert it into (
2.6
);
it forces

almost everywhere throughout
(-1,1)

as the first integral on the left-hand side must be non-positive.
The boundary conditions

then yield

for all
x\in[-1,1]
, that is,

in

, as claimed.

Although the following two corollaries are concerned with
an
“interior”-type

, they are direct consequences of

that we have just proved above in Proposition
2.1
.
This is the reason why we have decided to state them right after
our proof of Proposition
2.1
.
In both corollaries that follow next, we assume that the reaction function

\varphi(x,s)\equiv\varphi(x)
is independent from the dummy variable

.
In this case, the monotonicity inequality (

)
is reduced to

for all
x\in(-1,1)
.
We may take

without loss of generality by replacing

and

in eqs. (

) by

and
g(x)-\varphi(x)
,
respectively, thus arriving at

(2.7)

\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} &{}-\left(|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}-b(x)\,u^{\prime}=f(x)\,,\qquad{}-\left(|v^{\prime}|^{p-2}v^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}-b(x)\,v^{\prime}=g(x)\,;\\
&\quad\mbox{ with }\quad u(\pm 1)\leq v(\pm 1)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad f(x)\leq g(x)\;\mbox{ for }\,x\in(-1,1)\,.\end{aligned}\right.

Corollary 2.2

(“Interior” Strong Comparison Principle)
.

In the situation of
Proposition
2.1
,
with
\varphi(x,\,\cdot\,)\equiv 0
,
we have the following two (mutually exclusive) alternatives:

(a)

The set

\displaystyle P_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\{x\in(-1,1):\,u(x)<v(x)\}

is not empty.
Then there is a pair of points
-1<a_{-1}<a_{1}<1
such that

u(a_{-1})<v(a_{-1})
and
u(a_{1})<v(a_{1})
.
Given any such a pair, set

\eta=\min\{v(a_{-1})-u(a_{-1}),\,v(a_{1})-u(a_{1})\}>0\,.

Furthermore, in problem (
2.7
) above we have

v(x)\geq u(x)+\eta>u(x)
for every
x\in[a_{-1},\,a_{1}]
.
Finally,
P_{1}
is an open interval in
\mathbb{R}
.

(b)

The set
P_{1}
is empty.
This is the case if and only if all

u(-1)=v(-1)
,
u(1)=v(1)
, and
f=g
a.e. in
(-1,1)

hold in the boundary value problems in (
2.7
).

Proof of
Corollary
2.2
.
Let us begin with Alternative
(a)
, i.e., let

P_{1}\not=\emptyset
.
Thanks to the continuity of both functions

u,v:\,[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
,

P_{1}
is a nonempty open subset of the interval
(-1,1)
.
Consequently, there are two points

-1<a_{-1}<a_{1}<1
such that

\eta=\min\{v(a_{-1})-u(a_{-1}),\,v(a_{1})-u(a_{1})\}>0\,.

We apply the
WCP

from Proposition
2.1
on the interval

[a_{-1},\,a_{1}]
in place of
[-1,1]
and for the pair of functions

u(x)+\eta
,
v
instead of
u(x)
,
v(x)
, respectively.
By these arguments, the inequalities

u(a_{-1})+\eta\leq v(a_{-1})
and

u(a_{1})+\eta\leq v(a_{1})
imply
u(x)+\eta\leq v(x)

for every
x\in[a_{-1},\,a_{1}]
.
Consequently,
P_{1}
is an open and connected subset of
(-1,1)

and, thus, an open interval itself.

In contrast, Alternative
(b)
forces
u\equiv v

throughout the entire interval
[-1,1]
.
Problems (
2.7
) then yield
f=g
a.e. in
(-1,1)
.

Our corollary is proved.

In order to prevent the equality
u(x)\equiv v(x)
on the interval

(-1,\,-1/2)
in our Example

above,
we make the following natural hypothesis:

Hypothesis
\mathbf{H}_{\pm 1}
.

Let
f,g\in L^{\infty}(-1,1)
and

0\leq f(x)\leq g(x)
hold for almost all
x\in(-1,1)
.
In addition, we assume that for every
\delta>0
small enough we have

f\not\equiv g
in both intervals

(-1,-1+\delta)
and
(1-\delta,1)
, that is to say, both sets

\{x\in(-1,1):\,f(x)<g(x)\}\cap(-1,-1+\delta)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\{x\in(-1,1):\,f(x)<g(x)\}\cap(1-\delta,1)

have positive Lebesgue measure for every

small enough.

Consequently, Hypothesis
\mathbf{H}_{\pm 1}

is valid with any number

\delta\in\left(0,\,1/2\right)

in Lemma

below.
This lemma will then yield

v^{\prime}(1)<u^{\prime}(1)\leq 0\leq u^{\prime}(-1)<v^{\prime}(-1)
.

As an immediate consequence we obtain
the following boundary point lemma of Hopf type.

Lemma 3.7

(Hopf’s Maximum Principle at
x=\pm 1
)
.

Assume that
Hypothesis

\mathbf{H}_{\pm 1}
is valid.
Then the following
boundary point lemma
holds:

v^{\prime}(-1)<0<v^{\prime}(1)
.

In particular, the linear elliptic equation
(
3.6
) for the difference

w\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}v-u
on
(-1,1)

is regular in
(-1,1)
near the endpoints
x=\pm 1
, i.e.,
there is some
\delta>0
small enough such that
the inequality
D(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})(x)\geq\mathrm{const}>0

holds strictly for every

x\in(-1,-1+\delta)\cup(1-\delta,1)\,.

Consequently, we have even
the
Hopf boundary point comparison
:

v^{\prime}(1)<u^{\prime}(1)\leq 0\leq u^{\prime}(-1)<v^{\prime}(-1)
.

Proof of
Lemma
3.7
.
We apply the
Hopf boundary point lemma
from

P. Tolksdorf

[
18
, Prop. 3.2.1 and 3.2.2, p. 801]
or

J. L. Vázquez

[
19
, Theorem 5, p. 200]
.
This result, due to
E. Hopf
, is described and proved in all details
in the monograph by

P. Pucci
and
J. Serrin

[
13
, §5.4]
, Theorem 5.5.1 on p 120; cf. also

[
13
, §5.1]
, Example on p. 104.

Owing to
g\geq 0
in
(-1,1)
and

g\not\equiv 0
in both intervals

(-1,-1+\delta)
and
(1-\delta,1)
,
for every
\delta>0
small enough, we thus get
v^{\prime}(-1)<0<v^{\prime}(1)
.
In particular, the linear elliptic equation
(
3.6
) for the difference

w\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}v-u
on
(-1,1)
being regular,
we arrive at

w^{\prime}(-1)<0<w^{\prime}(1)
by the new version of
the
Hopf boundary point lemma

established recently in the article by

D. E. Apushkinskaya
and
A. I. Nazarov

[
1
]
.

Hence, the desired inequalities

v^{\prime}(1)<u^{\prime}(1)\leq 0\leq u^{\prime}(-1)<v^{\prime}(-1)

follow.

Theorem 3.8

(Hopf’s “Global” Strong Comparison Principle)
.

Let us consider
problem
(
3.5
)
for
u
and
v
on
(-1,1)
.
If also

f,g\in L^{\infty}(-1,1)
with

0\leq f\leq g
a.e. in
(-1,1)
and

Hypothesis

\mathbf{H}_{\pm 1}
is valid, then

Hopf’s (“Global”) Strong Comparison Principle

holds:

v^{\prime}(1)<u^{\prime}(1)\leq 0\leq u^{\prime}(-1)<v^{\prime}(-1)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad 0\leq u(x)<v(x)\quad\mbox{ for all }\,x\in(-1,1)\,.

Proof of
Theorem
3.8
.
Let us recall that both functions

u,v:\,[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
are continuously differentiable.
We begin by applying Proposition
3.5

to conclude that the set

P_{1}=\{x\in(-1,1):\,u(x)<v(x)\}\quad\mbox{ is an open interval in $\mathbb{R}$, i.e., }\

P_{1}=(a_{-1},\,a_{1})
holds with a pair of points

-1\leq a_{-1}<a_{1}\leq 1
; hence, the complementary set in
(-1,1)

equals to

\displaystyle P_{0}=\{x\in(-1,1):\,u(x)=v(x)\}=(-1,a_{-1}]\cup[a_{1},1)\,.

On the other hand, by Lemma
3.7
above,
if we choose
\delta>0
small enough, such that both inequalities,

u^{\prime}(\xi)<v^{\prime}(\xi)
and
v^{\prime}(\eta)<u^{\prime}(\eta)
, are valid for all

\xi\in(-1,-1+\delta)
and
\eta\in(1-\delta,1)
,
then we must have also

u(\xi)<v(\xi)
and
u(\eta)<v(\eta)
again for all

\xi\in(-1,-1+\delta)
and
\eta\in(1-\delta,1)
,
thanks to the Dirichlet boundary conditions

u(-1)=v(-1)
and
u(1)=v(1)
.
But this result forces

(-1,-1+\delta)\cup(1-\delta,1)\subset P_{1}

which clearly contradicts the statement

(-1,1)\setminus P_{1}=P_{0}=(-1,a_{-1}]\cup[a_{1},1)\quad\mbox{ unless }\,P_{0}=\emptyset\,,

i.e.,
P_{1}=(-1,1)
holds as desired.
Lemma
3.7
yields also

v^{\prime}(1)<u^{\prime}(1)\leq 0\leq u^{\prime}(-1)<v^{\prime}(-1)\,.

The remaining part of Hopf’s Comparison Principle
follows directly from
P_{1}=(-1,1)
.

The theorem is proved.

The case of constant convection velocity

In this paragraph we will assume that the convection velocity

b:\,(-1,1)\to\mathbb{R}
is a given constant, i.e.,

b(x)\equiv b_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
x\in(-1,1)
.
Hence, Proposition
2.1

applies and, thus, the
WCP
is valid.
In this simplified model, we will be able to prove a weaker form of
Theorem
3.8
,
Theorem
3.9
below,
under the following hypothesis,

Hypothesis
\mathbf{H}_{0}
.

Let
f,g\in L^{\infty}(-1,1)
and

0\leq f(x)\leq g(x)
hold for almost all
x\in(-1,1)

together with
f\not\equiv g
in the interval
(-1,1)
, i.e., the set

\{x\in(-1,1):\,f(x)<g(x)\}

has positive Lebesgue measure.
In addition, we assume that

\varphi(x,\,\cdot\,)\equiv 0
and
the
convection velocity

b(x)=b_{0}\equiv\mathrm{const}
is constant.

We note that
\mathbf{H}_{0}
is weaker than

\mathbf{H}_{\pm 1}

used in Lemma

and Theorem
3.8
.
This weaker result, in Theorem

below,
is motivated and closely related to our results above,

.

Theorem 3.9

(Another Hopf’s “Global” Comparison Principle)
.

Let us consider
problem
(
3.5
)
for
u
and
v
on
(-1,1)
, with

b(x)=b_{0}\equiv\mathrm{const}
being constant on
(-1,1)
.
Assume that

f,g\in L^{\infty}(-1,1)
satisfy

Hypothesis

\mathbf{H}_{0}
and

u(x_{0})=v(x_{0})
holds at some point

x_{0}\in(-1,1)
.
Then we have also

u^{\prime}(x_{0})=v^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
together with the following two alternatives:

Alt. 1:

If
b_{0}\leq 0
then we must have

u(x)=v(x)
for all
x\in(-1,x_{0}]
together with

f(x)=g(x)
for almost every
x\in(-1,x_{0})
, that is, both,

u\equiv v
and
f\equiv g
in
(-1,x_{0})
.

Alt. 2:

If
b_{0}\geq 0
then we must have

u(x)=v(x)
for all
x\in[x_{0},1)
together with

f(x)=g(x)
for almost every
x\in(x_{0},1)
, that is, both,

u\equiv v
and
f\equiv g
in
(x_{0},1)
.

Remark 3.10
.

The result in
Alt. 1
reflects the scenario in
Example
3.6
with

b_{0}=-\genfrac{(}{)}{}{1}{3}{2}^{1-p}\genfrac{(}{)}{}{1}{p - 2}{p - 1}^{1-p}\hfil\penalty<0

and

.

Proof of
Theorem
3.9
.
Let us recall that both functions

u,v:\,[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
are continuously differentiable.
We begin by applying Proposition
3.5

to conclude that the set

P_{1}=\{x\in(-1,1):\,u(x)<v(x)\}\quad\mbox{ is an open interval in $\mathbb{R}$, i.e., }\ P_{1}=(a_{-1},\,a_{1})

with a pair of points
-1\leq a_{-1}<a_{1}\leq 1
.
Hence, its complementary set in
(-1,1)
equals to

\displaystyle P_{0}=\{x\in(-1,1):\,u(x)=v(x)\}=(-1,a_{-1}]\cup[a_{1},1)\,.

We rewrite the general problem (
3.5
)
in the special form with
b(x)=b_{0}\equiv\mathrm{const}

being a real constant,
b_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,

(3.15)

\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} &{}-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\,\left(|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}+b_{0}\,u\right)=f(x)\,,\qquad{}-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\,\left(|v^{\prime}|^{p-2}v^{\prime}+b_{0}\,v\right)=g(x)\,;\\
&\quad u(\pm 1)=v(\pm 1)=0\,,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\\
&\quad 0\leq f\leq g\quad\mbox{ a.e.\ in }\,(-1,1)\,,\quad f\not\equiv g\quad\mbox{ in }(-1,1)\,.\end{aligned}\right.

In analogy with our proof of Theorem
3.8
above,
we wish to show that either

P_{1}=(x_{0},\,1)
or else
P_{1}=(-1,\,x_{0})
holds,
this time without assuming the validity of
Hypothesis
\mathbf{H}_{\pm 1}
.
The desired equivalent result states that either

P_{0}=(-1,\,x_{0}]
or else
P_{0}=[x_{0},\,1)
, respectively.

Thus, let
x_{0}\in(-1,1)
be a point satisfying
u(x_{0})=v(x_{0})
.
Then the “local” inequality
u(x)\leq v(x)
for all

x\in(x_{0}-\delta,\,x_{0}+\delta)\subset(-1,1)

forces also
u^{\prime}(x_{0})=v^{\prime}(x_{0})
.
Since
u\not\equiv v
in
(-1,1)
, we can choose
x_{0}\in(-1,1)

above in such a way that

at least one
of the following two statements is valid:

Ineq. 1:

u(x)<v(x)
holds for every
x\in(x_{0}-\delta,\,x_{0})
.
Ineq. 2:

u(x)<v(x)
holds for every
x\in(x_{0},\,x_{0}+\delta)
.

(We note that these inequalities may hold simultaneously.)

Furthermore, the linear elliptic equation (
3.6
)
for the difference

w\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}v-u
on
(-1,1)

is uniformly elliptic unless
u^{\prime}(x_{0})=v^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
is valid.
However, if
u^{\prime}(x_{0})=v^{\prime}(x_{0})\neq 0
,
then the Hopf boundary point lemma would force either

u^{\prime}(x_{0})>v^{\prime}(x_{0})
or else
u^{\prime}(x_{0})<v^{\prime}(x_{0})
, respectively.
This would contradict the equality
u^{\prime}(x_{0})=v^{\prime}(x_{0})
.
Thus, we have a point
x_{0}\in(-1,1)
such that

u(x_{0})=v(x_{0})
and
u^{\prime}(x_{0})=v^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
; cf. Lemma
3.1

(
WCP
at
x_{0}
).

Next, we use
x_{0}\in(-1,1)
as the initial point
for our
“shooting method”

applied to the special problem (
3.15
) above,
with the initial values
u(x_{0})=v(x_{0})
and
u^{\prime}(x_{0})=v^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
:

\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} |u^{\prime}(x)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(x)+b_{0}\,u(x)&=|u^{\prime}(x_{0})|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(x_{0})+b_{0}\,u(x_{0})\textstyle-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&=b_{0}\,u(x_{0})\textstyle-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\quad\mbox{ for all }\,x\in(-1,1)\,,\\
|v^{\prime}(x)|^{p-2}v^{\prime}(x)+b_{0}\,v(x)&=|v^{\prime}(x_{0})|^{p-2}v^{\prime}(x_{0})+b_{0}\,v(x_{0})\textstyle-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&=b_{0}\,v(x_{0})\textstyle-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\quad\mbox{ for all }\,x\in(-1,1)\,;\\
&u(\pm 1)=v(\pm 1)=0\,,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\\
0\leq f\leq g&\quad\mbox{ a.e.\ in }\,(-1,1)\,,\quad f\not\equiv g\quad\mbox{ in }(-1,1)\,.\end{aligned}\right.

Subtracting the first differential equation (for
u
)
from the second one (for
v
) we arrive at

(3.16)

\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} &|v^{\prime}(x)|^{p-2}v^{\prime}(x)-|u^{\prime}(x)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(x)={}-b_{0}(v(x)-u(x))\\
&\textstyle{}-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}(g(s)-f(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\quad\mbox{ for all }\,x\in(-1,1)\,,\\
&u(\pm 1)=v(\pm 1)=0\,,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\\
&0\leq f\leq g\quad\mbox{ a.e.\ in }\,(-1,1)\,,\quad f\not\equiv g\quad\mbox{ in }(-1,1)\,.\end{aligned}\right.

Case

b_{0}\leq 0
:
We have

{}-b_{0}(v(x)-u(x))\geq 0
and thus
eq. (
3.16
) yields

\displaystyle|v^{\prime}(x)|^{p-2}v^{\prime}(x)-|u^{\prime}(x)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(x)\textstyle\geq{}-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}(g(s)-f(s))\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{x_{0}}(g(s)-f(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\geq 0\quad\mbox{ for all }\,x\in(-1,x_{0}]\,.

Consequently, we must have

(v-u)^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in(-1,x_{0}]
which forces

v(x)-u(x)=0
for all
x\in(-1,x_{0}]
, thanks to

v(-1)-u(-1)=0
and
v(x_{0})-u(x_{0})=0
.
This is possible only if
f(x)=g(x)
holds for almost every

x\in(-1,x_{0})
,
as described in alternative “Alt. 1”.

Case

b_{0}\geq 0
:
We have

{}-b_{0}(v(x)-u(x))\leq 0
and thus
eq. (
3.16
) yields

|v^{\prime}(x)|^{p-2}v^{\prime}(x)-|u^{\prime}(x)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(x)\textstyle\leq{}-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}(g(s)-f(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\leq 0\quad\mbox{ for all }\,x\in[x_{0},1)\,.

Consequently, we have

(v-u)^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
for all
x\in[x_{0},1)
whence

v(x)-u(x)=0
for all
x\in[x_{0},1)
, thanks to

v(x_{0})-u(x_{0})=0
and
v(1)-u(1)=0
.
This entails
f(x)=g(x)
for almost every
x\in(x_{0},1)
,
as claimed in alternative “Alt. 2”.

The proof is finished.
