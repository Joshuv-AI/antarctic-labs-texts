On the true detection probability of the uniformly optimal search plan
Liang Hong

Abstract
The gold standard for designing a search plan is to select a target distribution and then find the uniformly optimal search plan based on it. This approach has been successfully applied in several high-profile civil and military search missions. Since the target distribution is subjective and chosen at an analyst’s discretion, it is natural to ask whether this approach can generate a search plan that maximizes the true detection probability at each moment. This article gives a negative answer by establishing that, under mild conditions, for a given target distribution and the optimal search plan based on it, there is another target distribution whose induced uniformly optimal search plan leads to an increased true detection probability at every moment. In particular, it implies that the problem of finding a search plan that maximizes the true detection probability at each moment remains unsolved. Keywords and phrases: Bayesian updating; maritime operations; search and detection; stationary target.



1
Introduction

The optimal search theory studies the best way of searching for a target given a limited budget when the target location is uncertain. It originated in the US Navy when its Anti-Submarine Warfare Operations Research Group needed to find a way to efficiently detect hostile submarines during World War II (e.g., Koopman 1945, 1956a, b, c). Later progress of the theory was largely driven by real-world maritime search missions too; see, for instance, Stone and Stanshine (1971), Stone (1973, 1975, 1976), Richardson and Stone (1971), Richardson et al. (1980), and Stone et al. (2014a). Most recently, the game-theoretic approach to the search problem has received considerable attention; see, for instance, Alpern and Gal (2003), Clarkson et al. (2020), Lidbetter (2020), Alpern et al. (2021), and Lin (2021). Though we have witnessed incredible advancement in naval technology since World War II, the optimal search theory remains relevant and important, as evidenced by recent notable naval accidents, such as the loss of Indonesia’s KRI Nanggala submarine in April 2021, the collision of the USS Connecticut with a seamount in the South China Sea in October 2021, and the crash of an F-35C of the US Navy in the South China Sea January 2022. Moreover, the optimal search theory can also be applied to space exploration endeavors, such as NASA’s Artemis campaign and Mars Missions.

The gold standard for designing a search plan is based on the Bayesian approach: to choose a target distribution and then obtain the uniformly optimal search plan based on it. This approach has been successfully employed in several high-profile civil and military search missions; see Richardson and Stone (1971), Richardson et al. (1980), Stone (1992), and Stone et al. (2014a), and references therein. This approach also forms the theoretical underpinnings of the U.S. Coast Guard’s Search and Rescue Optimal Planning System (SAROPS) and its predecessor Computer Assisted Search Planning (CASP); see, for example, Stone (1975) and Kratzke et al. (2010). The uniformly optimal search plan plays a key role in this approach. Arkin (1964) first established sufficient conditions for a uniformly optimal search plan to exist in the Euclidean search space. Stone (1973, 1975, 1976) extended and generalized his work. Properties of the uniformly optimal search plan have been widely studied (e.g., Stone 1975, Stone et al. 2016, Hong 2024). By its definition, the uniformly optimal search plan maximizes the detection probability at each moment. Since the target distribution is subjective and there is no true target distribution, the detection probability a uniformly optimal search plan maximizes is the subjective detection probability based on the chosen target distribution, not the true detection probability. Given this observation, it is natural to ask whether an analyst can find a uniformly optimal search plan whose induced uniformly optimal search plan maximizes the true detection probability at each moment. The article gives a negative answer. Specifically, it will be established that, under certain conditions, for any chosen target distribution and the optimal search plan based on it, there is a different target distribution whose induced uniformly optimal search plan results in a larger true detection probability at every moment.

This result has several interesting consequences. First, this result demonstrates that the aforementioned gold standard will not lead to a genuinely optimal solution to the search problem. Moreover, this result serves as a caveat to practitioners: a uniformly optimal search plan maximizes the subjective detection probability at every moment, but it does not necessarily maximize the true detection probability at every moment. Finally, this result shows that the search problem is profoundly deep and challenging. The problem of finding a search plan that maximizes the true detection probability at each moment remains unsolved.

The remainder of the article is organized as follows. In Section 2, we establish our notational conventions by reviewing the search problem and the uniformly optimal search plan. Next, in Section 3, we provide two motivating examples to build intuition. Then, we establish our main results in Section 4; there we treat the discrete case and the continuous case separately. Finally, we conclude the article with some remarks in Section 5.

2
Notation and setup

We are interested in searching for a stationary target where the exact target location
x
is unknown, the equipment used for detection, such as a sonar or radar, is not perfect, and the amount of effort available is limited. Though the exact target location is unknown, we often have some information about it. To avail ourselves of this information and, at the same time, quantify the uncertainty of the target location, we first pin down an area, in our assessment, that definitely contains the target. This area is called the
possibility area
and denoted as
X
. Then we construct a
target distribution
for
x
that is supported on
X
. We will use
\Pi
to denote the (cumulative) distribution function of the proposed target distribution. That is, the possibility area
X
is the support of
\Pi
.
X
can be either countable (i.e., finite or countably infinite) or uncountable. If
X
is countable, the smallest region over which search effort can be allocated is represented by a cell; we refer to this case as the
discrete case
. Without loss of generality, we may number these cells using positive integers. Therefore, we always take
X
to be a subset of
\{1,2,\ldots\}
in the discrete case. If
X
is uncountable, then it is assumed that the search effort can be allocated in a continuous manner; this case will be referred to as the
continuous case
. In the continuous case, we assume
X
is a subset of the
n
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Throughout, we assume
\Pi
admits a density function
\pi
. That is,
\pi
denotes the probability mass function (i.e., the discrete density function) in the discrete case and the probability density function in the continuous case. Note that we have implicitly taken the Bayesian approach because in most realistic cases, such as a marine accident, the scenario will never repeat itself under identical conditions (i.e., the sample size of the available data is
1
), rendering a frequentist approach inappropriate. Note that the target distribution is subjective and there is no true target distribution. Since the true target location is uncertain in the search problem, the target distribution is always assumed to be non-degenerate.

Once the possibility area
X
is nailed down, we must decide how to allocate the effort in
X
. Let
\mathbb{R}_{+}
denote the non-negative real line
0,\infty)
. In the discrete case, we define an
allocation
on
X
to be a function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
; for the continuous case, we define an
allocation
on
X
to be a function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\int_{A}f(x)dx
equals the amount of search effort allocated in
A
where
A
is any subset of
X
. Therefore, the total effort invested in the discrete case and the continuous case are
\sum_{x\in X}f(x)
and
\int_{X}f(x)dx
respectively.

In the search problem, there are two sources of uncertainty: (i) uncertainty of the target location, (ii) uncertainty of detection due to imperfection of the sensor. The former is quantified by a target distribution. To account for the latter, we will use a
detection function

d:X\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow[0,1]
. In the discrete case,
d(x,y)
is the conditional probability of detecting the target when
y
amount of effort is placed in cell
x
given that the target is in cell
x
. For the continuous case,
d(x,y)
denotes the conditional probability of detecting the target if the effort density equals
y
at
x
given that the target is at
x
. Different from the target distribution, the detection function is objective and depends on the sensor. Throughout, we assume the detection function has been either correctly derived from physical laws or reliably estimated from repeated testing.

Given a target distribution
\Pi
, the allocation
f
, and the detection function
d
, the
subjective probability of detection
, denoted as
P[f]
, equals

P[f]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{x\in X}d(x,f(x))\pi(x),&\hbox{in the discrete case,}\\
\int_{X}d(x,f(x))\pi(x)dx,&\hbox{in the continuous case.}\end{array}\right.

Since
\pi
is subjective,
P[f]
is not the true probability of detection. Let
x_{0}
denote the true cell that contains the target in the discrete case and the true target location in the continuous case. Then
the
true probability of detection
, denoted as
P^{\#}[f]
, equals

P^{\#}[f]=d(x_{0},f(x_{0})).

(1)

Every search has a budget. To account for this, we first define a cost function
c:X\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
. In the discrete case,
c(x,y)
denotes the cost of applying
y
effort in cell
x
; in the continuous case,
c(x,y)
stands for the cost density of applying effort density
y
at location
x
. For an allocation
f
on
X
, the cost resulting from
f
, denoted as
C[f]
, is given by

C[f]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{x\in X}c(x,f(x)),&\hbox{in the discrete case,}\\
\int_{X}c(x,f(x))dx,&\hbox{in the continuous case.}\end{array}\right.

Unless otherwise stated, we assume the cost is proportional to allocation, i.e.,
c(x,y)=y
for all
x\in X
.

A
search plan
on
X
is a function
\varphi:X\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

(i)

\varphi(\cdot,t)
is an allocation on
X
for all
t\geq 0
;

(ii)

\varphi(x,\cdot)
is an increasing function for all
x\in X
.

Condition (i) ensures that
\varphi(\cdot,t)
is an allocation in the first
t
units of time. Condition (ii) says that no effort can be undone as the search progresses. Let
T
be the time to find the target using
\varphi
. We will use
\mu(\varphi)
to denote the expectation of
T
with respect to the subjective probability of detection, i.e., the subjective mean time to find the target. It is well-known that (e.g., Lemma 3.4 of Kallenberg 2002)

\mu(\varphi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-P[\varphi(\cdot,t)])dt.

Similarly, we will use
\mu^{\#}
to denote the expectation of
T
with respect to the true probability of detection, i.e., the true mean time to find the target. We have

\mu^{\#}(\varphi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-P^{\#}[\varphi(\cdot,t)])dt.

(2)

We define the
cumulative effort function

E
to be a non-negative function with domain
\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
E(t)
is the effort available by time
t
. We assume
E
is increasing and
E(t)>0
for all
t>0
. There are many different search plans available to a search team. We need a criterion for comparing two distinct search plans. Ideally, it would be the true probability of detection. Since the true target location is unknown, the true probability of detection cannot be calculated in actuality. Hence, the best we can do is to use the subjective probability of detection. Given a target distribution
\Pi
, the
uniformly optimal search plan for
\Pi
and
E(t)
maximizes the subjective probability of detection at each time
t\geq 0
, subject to the constrain
C[\varphi^{\star}(\cdot,t)]\leq E(t)
. To make a precise definition, let
\Phi(E)
be the class of search plans
\varphi
such that

\sum_{x\in X}\varphi(x,t)=E(t),\quad\text{for all $t\geq 0$},

(3)

for the discrete case, and

\int_{X}\varphi(x,t)dx=E(t),\quad\text{for all $t\geq 0$},

(4)

for the continuous case. A search plan
\varphi^{\star}\in\Phi(E)
is said to be
uniformly optimal for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
if

P[\varphi^{\star}(\cdot,t)]=\max\{P[\varphi(\cdot,t)\mid\varphi\in\Phi(E)]\}\quad\text{for all $t\geq 0$},

(5)

where
P[\varphi^{\star}]
is the subjective probability of detection based on
\Pi
. A uniformly optimal search plan has several desirable properties; see Chapter 3 of Stone (1975) and Hong (2024). However, a uniformly optimal search plan does not always exist, as Example 2.2.9 of Stone (1975) shows. But we have the following sufficient conditions for the existence of uniformly optimal search plans; see, for instance, Section 2.4 of Stone (1975).

Theorem 2.1
.

(i)

If
\Pi
is a target distribution on a discrete possibility area
X
,
d(x,0)=0
, and
d(x,\cdot)
is continuous, concave, and increasing for each
x\in X
, there exists a uniformly optimal search plan for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
.

(ii)

If
\Pi
is a target distribution on a continuous possibility area
X
,
d(x,0)=0
, and
d(x,\cdot)
is increasing and right-continuous for each
x\in X
, there exists a uniformly optimal search plan for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
.

A class of detection functions, called
regular functions
, satisfy these sufficient conditions. A detection function
d
is said to be
regular
if
d(x,0)=0
and
\partial d(x,y)/\partial y
is continuous, positive, and strictly decreasing for all
x\in X
. A detection function
d
is said to be
homogeneous
if
d(x_{1},y)=d(x_{2},y)
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
and
y\geq 0
. One of the most frequently used detection functions is the
exponential function
:

d(x,y)=1-e^{-\alpha_{x}y},\quad y\geq 0,

where
\alpha_{x}>0
is called the
rate
of
d
at
x\in X
. A
homogeneous and exponential
detection function with rate
\alpha>0
takes the form

d(x,y)=1-e^{-\alpha y},\quad y\geq 0.

When the detection function is regular, a semi-closed form of the uniformly optimal search plan can be derived under mild conditions; see Chapter 2 of Stone (1975) or Chapter 5 of Washburn (2014). In particular, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2

(Stone 1975)
.

If the cost function takes the form
c(x,y)=y
for all
y\geq 0
and
x\in X
and the detection function is regular, a uniformly optimal search plan
\varphi^{\star}
within
\Phi(E)
can be found for any target distribution
\Pi
as follows. Define

q_{x}(y)=\pi(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x,y),\quad x\in X\text{ and $y\geq 0$},

(6)

q^{-1}_{x}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{the inverse function of $q_{x}(y)$ evaluated at $\lambda$},&\hbox{if $0<\lambda\leq q_{x}(0)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{if $\lambda>q_{x}(0)$,}\end{array}\right.

(7)

and

Q(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{x\in X}q_{x}^{-1}(\lambda),&\hbox{in the discrete case,}\\
\int_{X}q_{x}^{-1}(\lambda)dx,&\hbox{in the continuous case.}\end{array}\right.

(8)

Then a uniformly optimal search plan for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
is given by
\varphi^{\star}(x,t)=q_{x}^{-1}(Q^{-1}(E(t)))
where
Q^{-1}
is the inverse function of
Q
.

Here
q_{x}(y)
accounts for both the uncertainty of the target location and the future change in detection. Intuitively, it represents the “rate of return” for allocating more effort to the location
x
given that
y
amount of effort has already been allocated there. Thus,
q^{-1}_{x}(\lambda)
stands for the amount of allocation already required at
x
so the rate of return for placing more allocation equals
\lambda
, or
0
if the rate of return is less than
\lambda
before any allocation at
x
. The idea behind Theorem
2.2
is as follows: we distribute search effort by gradually decreasing the rate of return
\lambda
that is allowed into the allocation; hence, effort distributed is proportional to the areas with the highest rate of return at the current moment. We stop when the rate of return
\lambda
falls to the threshold that corresponds to the event “ total allocation has reached the budget
E
”. This threshold is quantified by the function
Q
, where
Q(\lambda)
is the total allocation if every location
x
in the search space is allocated effort until the rate of return
q_{x}(y)
for allocating more effort there falls to
\lambda
.

3
Two motivating examples

Here we first look at two concrete examples, one discrete and one continuous, to build the intuition for the key results in the next section.

Example 1
.

This example slightly generalizes the example in Introduction of Stone (1975). Suppose the possibility area
X=\{1,2\}
, the true target location is cell
1
, and the search budget at time
t
is
E(t)
where
E(t)>\ln[p/(1-p)]
. We take the target distribution to be
\pi(1)=p
and
\pi(2)=1-p
, where
1/2<p<1
. The detection function
d
is homogeneous and exponential with rate
\alpha=1
, i.e.,
d(x,y)=1-e^{-y},\ y\geq 0
for all
x\in X
. It is clear that the hypothesis of Theorem
2.2
is satisfied; hence, the uniformly optimal search plan
\varphi^{\star}
exists. By the algorithm in Theorem
2.2
or the same argument as in Introduction of Stone (1975), we know
\varphi^{\star}
is given by

\varphi^{\star}(1,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}E(t),&\hbox{if $0<E(t)\leq\ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)$,}\\
\frac{1}{2}\left[E(t)+\ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\right],&\hbox{if $E(t)>\ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)$,}\end{array}\right.

and

\varphi^{\star}(2,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\hbox{if $0<E(t)\leq\ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)$,}\\
\frac{1}{2}\left[E(t)-\ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\right],&\hbox{if $E(t)>\ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)$.}\end{array}\right.

Then the subjective probability of detection equals

\displaystyle P[\varphi^{\star}(\cdot,t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi(1)d(1,\varphi^{\star}(1,E(t)))+\pi(2)d(2,\varphi^{\star}(2,E(t)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p\left(1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left[E(t)+\ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\right]}\right)+(1-p)\left(1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left[E(t)-\ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\right]}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2\sqrt{p(1-p)}e^{-\frac{1}{2}E(t)}.

Since the true target location is cell
1
, the true probability of detection, as a function of
p
, is

\displaystyle P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}(\cdot,t)](p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(1,\varphi^{\star}(1,E(t)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left[E(t)+\ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\right]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-e^{-E(t)/2}\sqrt{\frac{1-p}{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-e^{-E(t)/2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{p}-1},

which is increasing in
p
on
(0,1)
. Also, it follows from (
2
) that the true mean time to find the target
\mu^{\#}(\varphi)(p)
, as a function of
p
, is decreasing in
p
on
(0,1)
. Therefore, regardless of the value of
p
, there is a
\widetilde{p}\in(0,1)
such that
\widetilde{p}>p
,
P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}(\cdot,t)](\widetilde{p})>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}(\cdot,t)](p)
and
\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})(\widetilde{p})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})(p)
.

Remark.
By symmetry, if the true target location is cell
2
, then there is a
\widetilde{p}\in(0,1)
such that
0<\widetilde{p}<p
and
P^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})(\widetilde{p})>P^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})(p)
and
\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})(\widetilde{p})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})(p)
. Therefore, we will have the same conclusion.

Example 2
.

This example slightly generalizes Examples 2.2.1 and 2.2.7 of Stone (1975).
Suppose the possibility area is
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the true target location is
x_{0}
. Here the search is assumed to be conducted at speed
v
using a sensor with a sweep width
W
. Thus, if there is time

available for search, then the search budget will be
E(t)=Wvt
.
The target distribution is circular normal, i.e.,

\pi(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}e^{-\frac{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},\quad(x_{1},x_{2})\in X=\mathbb{R}^{2},

(9)

and the detection function is nonhomogeneous exponential:
d(x,y)=1-e^{-\alpha_{x}y}
for all

and
y\geq 0
where
\alpha_{x}>0
. For convenience, we will use polar coordinates. Then
x_{0}=(r_{0},\theta_{0})
and

q_{(r,\theta)}^{-1}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{2\pi\sigma^{2}\lambda}{\alpha_{x}}\right)+\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]&\hbox{if $r^{2}\leq 2\sigma^{2}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{x}}{2\pi\sigma^{2}\lambda}\right)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{if $r^{2}>2\sigma^{2}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{x}}{2\pi\sigma^{2}\lambda}\right)$.}\end{array}\right.

Therefore,

\displaystyle Q(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}q_{(r,\theta)}^{-1}(\lambda)rdrd\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2\pi}{\alpha_{x}}\int_{0}^{\sigma\left[2\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{x}}{2\pi\sigma^{2}\lambda}\right)\right]^{1/2}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{2\pi\sigma^{2}\lambda}{\alpha_{x}}\right)+\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]rdr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\sigma^{2}}{\alpha_{x}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{2\pi\sigma^{2}\lambda}{\alpha_{x}}\right)\right]^{2},

and

Q^{-1}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\alpha_{x}}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\exp\left[-\left(\frac{\alpha_{x}\lambda}{\pi\sigma^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\right],&\hbox{if $r^{2}\leq 2\sigma^{2}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{x}}{2\pi\sigma^{2}\lambda}\right)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{if $r^{2}>2\sigma^{2}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{x}}{2\pi\sigma^{2}\lambda}\right)$.}\end{array}\right.

It follows that the uniformly optimal plan for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
exists and is given by (in polar coordinates)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\star}((r,\theta),t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}\left[\left(\frac{\alpha_{x}Wvt}{\pi\sigma^{2}}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right],&\hbox{if $r^{2}\leq 2\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{\alpha_{x}Wvt}{\pi\sigma^{2}}\right)^{1/2}$,}\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}\left[H_{x}\sqrt{t}-\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right],&\hbox{if $0<r\leq R_{x}(t)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{if $r>R_{x}(t)$,}\end{array}\right.

where
R^{2}_{x}(t)=2\sigma^{2}H_{x}\sqrt{t}
and
H_{x}=\sqrt{\alpha_{x}Wv/\pi\sigma^{2}}
. In this case, the allocation at a point

\alpha_{x}

\theta_{x}

P[\varphi^{\star}(\cdot,t)]=1-(1+H_{x}\sqrt{t})e^{-H_{x}\sqrt{t}},\quad t\geq 0.

(12)

Also, the true probability of detection is a function is
\sigma
and equals

\displaystyle P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}](\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(x_{0},\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1-e^{-\left(H_{x_{0}}\sqrt{t}-\frac{r_{0}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)},&\hbox{if $0<r_{0}\leq R_{x_{0}}(t)$;}\\

h(\sigma)=H_{x_{0}}\sqrt{t}-\frac{r_{0}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}=\left(\frac{\alpha_{x_{0}}Wv}{\pi}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sigma}-\frac{r^{2}_{0}}{2\sigma^{2}}.

Then

is differentiable and its derivative equals

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\left[\frac{r^{2}_{0}}{\sigma^{2}}-\left(\frac{\alpha_{x_{0}}Wv}{\pi}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sigma}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\frac{r^{2}_{0}}{\sigma^{2}}-H_{x_{0}}\sqrt{t}\right)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\frac{2r^{2}_{0}}{\sigma^{2}}-H_{x_{0}}\sqrt{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0,

where the last equality follows from the fact

if and only if

. Thus,

is decreasing in

, which implies

is decreasing in
\sigma
too. By (

), we also know that the true mean time to find the target

\sigma
. Therefore, there is a
\widetilde{\sigma}>0
such that

,
P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}](\widetilde{\sigma})>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}](\sigma)
, and
\mu^{\#}(\varphi)(\widetilde{\sigma})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi)(\sigma)
.

4
Main results

Suppose the uniformly optimal search plan for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
exists. We will show that there is another target distribution
\widetilde{\Pi}
such that
P^{\#}[\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}]>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}]
where
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}
is the uniformly optimal search plan for
\widetilde{\Pi}
and
E(t)
. We remind the reader that the target distribution is always non-degenerate due to the uncertainty of the true target location in the search problem.

4.1
Misspecified models

If
x_{0}\in X
, we say our model is
well-specified
; otherwise, we say our model is
misspecified
. In practice, we use all the available information to specify the possibility area
X
, but there is no guarantee that our model is well-specified. For example, the initial model used in the search of Malaysia Airlines Flight 370 was misspecified due to misformation. If the target is found during a search, then we know our model is well-specified. But that is hindsight. In many cases, there is no way to be absolutely sure that our model is well-specified before the search begins. Therefore, the case of a misspecified model deserves consideration.

If our model is misspecified, then the possibility area
X
, being the support of the target distribution
\Pi
, will not contain the target. Since a search plan only allocates effort on the possibility area
X
, the true probability of detection will be zero. Then it is trivial to see that if
\widetilde{\Pi}
is a new target distribution whose support
\widetilde{X}
contain
x_{0}
and
\varphi
is any search plan (based on
\widetilde{\Pi}
) that puts nonzero effort on
x_{0}
, then we will have a positive true probability of detection using
\widetilde{\Pi}
. Next, we turn to the nontrivial case, i.e., the well-specified case.

4.2
Well-specified models

The above two motivating examples provide some insight into the well-specified case. If a new target distribution increases the rate of returns at
x_{0}
in a way that the total allocation does not decrease, then the value of
\varphi^{\star}
at
x_{0}
will increase. This will increase the true probability of detection and shorten the true mean time to find the target.

4.2.1
The discrete case

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume
X
is discrete and
x_{0}
is the true cell in which the target is located. Suppose the cost function takes the form
c(x,y)=y
for all
y\geq 0
and
x\in X
, the detection function is regular,
\Pi
is a target distribution on
X
, and
\varphi^{\star}
is the uniformly optimal search plan for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
such that
0\leq\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)<E(t)
. If either of the following conditions holds:

(i)

there is an
x_{1}\in X
such that
x_{1}\neq x_{0}
and
\pi(x_{1})>\pi(x_{0})
,

(ii)

1>\pi(x_{0})\geq\pi(x)
for all
x\neq x_{0}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x_{0},y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x_{1},y)
for some
x_{1}\neq x_{0}
,

then there exists another target distribution
\widetilde{\Pi}\neq\Pi
such that
P^{\#}[\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}]>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}]
and
\mu^{\#}(\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})
, where
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}
is the uniformly optimal search plan for
\widetilde{\Pi}
within
\Phi(E)
.

Proof.

The inverse function of the rate of return
q_{x}(y)=\pi(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\pi(x,y)
is given by

q^{-1}_{x}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{the inverse function of $q_{x}(y)$ evaluated at $\lambda$},&\hbox{if $0<\lambda\leq q_{x}(0)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{if $\lambda>q_{x}(0)$.}\end{array}\right.

(14)

Suppose
\widetilde{\pi}
is the new target density to be constructed. Since
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x,y)
is independent of the target density
\pi
,
\widetilde{\pi}(x_{0})>\pi(x_{0})
will imply
\widetilde{q}_{x_{0}}(y)>q_{x_{0}}(y)
for all
y\geq 0
. On the other hand,
q_{x}^{-1}(\lambda)
equals the inverse of
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\pi(x,y)
evaluated at
\frac{\lambda}{\pi(x)}
when
\lambda\leq q_{x}(0)
. Since
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\pi(x,y)
is strictly decreasing, we have

\widetilde{q}^{-1}_{x}(\lambda)\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}>q_{x_{0}}^{-1}(\lambda),&\hbox{for $0<\lambda\leq\widetilde{q}_{x_{0}}(0)$,}\\
=q_{x_{0}}^{-1}(\lambda)=0,&\hbox{if $\lambda>\widetilde{q}_{x_{0}}(0)$.}\end{array}\right.

(15)

Without loss of generality, we may assume
\widetilde{Q}^{-1}(E(t))\leq\widetilde{q}_{x_{0}}(0)
.
We are going to construct a new target distribution
\widetilde{\Pi}
such that

\widetilde{q}_{x_{0}}^{-1}(\widetilde{Q}^{-1}(E(t)))>q_{x_{0}}^{-1}(Q^{-1}(E(t))),

which will imply
P^{\#}[\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}]>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}]
and
\mu^{\#}(\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})
via (
1
) and (
2
). To this end, consider two cases: (i) there is an
x_{1}\in X
such that
x_{1}\neq x_{0}
and
\pi(x_{1})>\pi(x_{0})
and (ii)
1>\pi(x_{0})\geq\pi(x)
for all
x\neq x_{0}
.

In Case (i), we construct the new target density
\widetilde{\pi}
by switching the probability masses of
\pi
on
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
and keep everything else intact. That is, we define

\widetilde{\pi}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\pi(x_{1}),&\hbox{if $x=x_{0}$,}\\
\pi(x_{0}),&\hbox{if $x=x_{1}$,}\\
\pi(x),&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Then
\widetilde{\pi}(x_{0})>\widetilde{\pi}(x_{1})
. By (
15
), we have
\widetilde{q}^{-1}_{x_{0}}(\lambda)>q^{-1}_{x_{0}}(\lambda)
for
0<\lambda\leq\widetilde{q}_{x_{0}}(0)
. Also, Condition (ii) implies

\widetilde{Q}(\lambda)=\sum_{x\in X}\widetilde{q}_{x}^{-1}(\lambda)=\sum_{x\in X}q_{x}^{-1}(\lambda)=Q(\lambda),\quad\text{for all $\lambda>0$},

which further implies
\widetilde{Q}^{-1}(\lambda)=Q^{-1}(\lambda)
for all
\lambda>0
. Therefore,

\widetilde{q}^{-1}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{Q}^{-1}(E(t)))=\widetilde{q}^{-1}_{x_{0}}(Q^{-1}(E(t)))>q_{x_{0}}^{-1}(Q^{-1}(E(t))).

In Case (ii), take
0<\epsilon<\min\{\pi(x_{1}),1-\pi(x_{0})\}
and construct a new target density as follows.

\widetilde{\pi}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\pi(x_{0})+\epsilon,&\hbox{if $x=x_{0}$,}\\
\pi(x_{1})-\epsilon,&\hbox{if $x=x_{1}$,}\\
\pi(x),&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

By the same argument as in Case (i), we have

\widetilde{q}_{x_{0}}^{-1}(\lambda)>q_{x_{0}}^{-1}(\lambda)>q_{x_{1}}^{-1}(\lambda)>\widetilde{q}_{x_{1}}^{-1}(\lambda),\quad\text{for all $\lambda>0$.}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}(\lambda)-Q(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\widetilde{q}^{-1}_{x}(\lambda)-\sum_{x\in X}q^{-1}_{x}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\widetilde{q}^{-1}_{x_{0}}(\lambda)-q^{-1}_{x_{0}}(\lambda)\right]-\left[\widetilde{q}^{-1}_{x_{1}}(\lambda)-q^{-1}_{x_{1}}(\lambda)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d\left(x_{0},y\right)\right)^{-1}\bigg|_{y=\frac{\lambda}{\pi(x_{0})+\epsilon}}-\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d\left(x_{0},y\right)\right)^{-1}\bigg|_{y=\frac{\lambda}{\pi(x_{0})}}\right]

\displaystyle-\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d\left(x_{1},y\right)\right)^{-1}\bigg|_{y=\frac{\lambda}{\pi(x_{1})-\epsilon}}-\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d\left(x_{1},y\right)\right)^{-1}\bigg|_{y=\frac{\lambda}{\pi(x_{1})}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d\left(x_{0},y\right)\right)^{-1}\bigg|_{y=\frac{\lambda}{\pi(x_{0})+\epsilon}}-\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d\left(x_{0},y\right)\right)^{-1}\bigg|_{y=\frac{\lambda}{\pi(x_{0})}}\right]

\displaystyle-\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d\left(x_{0},y\right)\right)^{-1}\bigg|_{y=\frac{\lambda}{\pi(x_{0})-\epsilon}}-\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d\left(x_{0},y\right)\right)^{-1}\bigg|_{y=\frac{\lambda}{\pi(x_{0})}}\right]

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 0,

where the penultimate equality follows from our assumption
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x_{0},y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x_{1},y)
for
x_{1}\neq x_{0}
, and the last inequality follows from the fact that
\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d\left(x_{0},y\right)\right)^{-1}
is strictly decreasing for all
x\in X
(because
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x,y)
is strictly decreasing for all
x\in X
) and
\frac{\lambda}{\pi(x_{0})+\epsilon}<\frac{\lambda}{\pi(x_{0})}<\frac{\lambda}{\pi(x_{0})-\epsilon}
. Thus,
\widetilde{Q}^{-1}(\lambda)<Q^{-1}(\lambda)
. Since
q_{x_{0}}^{-1}
is decreasing, we have

\widetilde{q}_{x_{0}}^{-1}(\widetilde{Q}^{-1}(E(t)))>q_{x_{0}}^{-1}(\widetilde{Q}^{-1}(E(t)))>q_{x_{0}}^{-1}(Q^{-1}(E(t))).

∎

Remark.
The idea of the above proof is as follows. In Case (i),
x_{0}
has received less target probability mass than
x_{1}
, then we can simply switch their target probability masses and keep everything else the same; this will increase the rate of return at
x_{0}
, but it will not change the total allocation. As a result, the amount of effort at
x_{0}
will increase and hence the true probability of detection will go up too. In Case (ii),
x_{0}
has already received the highest prior probability mass. To increase the rate of return at
x_{0}
, we need to take some probability mass from
x_{1}
. But we need to make sure the resulting total allocation
\widetilde{Q}
will be no less than the original total allocation
Q
. The condition
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x_{0},y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x_{1},y)
for some
x_{1}\neq x_{0}
ensures that this is the case. This condition is fairly mild and is satisfied as far as
d
is homogeneous. In particular, this condition is satisfied by many commonly used detection functions, such as the homogeneous and exponential detection function and the inverse cube detection function.

Corollary 1
.

Assume
X
is discrete and
x_{0}
is the true cell in which the target is located. Suppose the cost function takes the form
c(x,y)=y
for all
y\geq 0
and
x\in X
, the detection function is homogeneous and regular,
\Pi
is a non-degenerate target distribution on
X
(i.e.,
\pi(x)<1
for all
x\in X
), and
\varphi^{\star}
is the uniformly optimal search plan for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
such that
0\leq\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)<E(t)
. Then there exists another target distribution
\widetilde{\Pi}\neq\Pi
such that
P^{\#}[\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}]>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}]
and
\mu^{\#}(\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})
, where
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}
is the uniformly optimal search plan for
\widetilde{\Pi}
within
\Phi(E)
.

However, the next example shows that the condition “
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x_{0},y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x_{1},y)
for some
x_{1}\neq x_{0}
” is a sufficient but not necessary condition for the conclusion of Theorem
4.1
to hold.

Example 3

(Generalization of Example
1
)
.

Suppose
X=\{1,2\}
, the target density is
\pi(1)=p
and
\pi(2)=1-p
where
1/2<p<1
, and the detection function is
d(x,y)=1-e^{-\alpha_{x},y}
for
x\in X
and
y\geq 0
where
\alpha_{1}>0
,
\alpha_{2}>0
, and
\alpha_{1}\neq\alpha_{2}
. Then
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x,y)=\alpha_{x}e^{-\alpha_{x}y}
for
y\geq 0
. Thus,

\displaystyle q_{1}^{-1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}\ln\left(\frac{p\alpha_{1}}{\lambda}\right),&\hbox{if $0<\lambda\leq p\alpha_{1}$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle q_{2}^{-1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}\ln\left[\frac{(1-p)\alpha_{2}}{\lambda}\right],&\hbox{if $0<\lambda\leq(1-p)\alpha_{2}$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

We need to consider two cases: (i)
(1-p)\alpha_{2}<p\alpha_{2}
, and (ii)
(1-p)\alpha_{2}\geq p\alpha_{2}
.

In Case (i), we have

\displaystyle Q(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{1}^{-1}(\lambda)+q_{2}^{-1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\ln\left[\frac{(p\alpha_{1})^{1/\alpha_{1}}((1-p)\alpha_{2})^{1/\alpha_{2}}}{\lambda^{1/\alpha_{1}+1/\alpha_{2}}}\right],&\hbox{if $0<\lambda\leq(1-p)\alpha_{2}$,}\\
\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}\ln\left(\frac{p\alpha_{1}}{\lambda}\right),&\hbox{$(1-p)\alpha_{2}<\lambda<p\alpha_{1}$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

Hence,

Q^{-1}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left[e^{-\lambda}(p\alpha_{1})^{1/\alpha_{1}}((1-p)\alpha_{2})^{1/\alpha_{2}}\right]^{\frac{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}},&\hbox{if $0<\lambda\leq\ln\left[\frac{p\alpha_{1}}{(1-p)\alpha_{2}}\right]^{1/\alpha_{1}}$,}\\
p\alpha_{1}e^{-\lambda\alpha_{1}},&\hbox{if $\lambda>\ln\left[\frac{p\alpha_{1}}{(1-p)\alpha_{2}}\right]^{1/\alpha_{1}}$.}\end{array}\right.

It follows that the uniformly optimal plan for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
is given by

\displaystyle\varphi^{\star}(1,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{1}^{-1}(Q^{-1}(E(t)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}E(t),&\hbox{if $0<E(t)\leq\ln\left[\frac{p\alpha_{1}}{(1-p)\alpha_{2}}\right]^{1/\alpha_{1}}$,}\\
\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\left(\alpha_{2}E(t)+\ln\left[\frac{p\alpha_{1}}{(1-p)\alpha_{2}}\right]\right),&\hbox{if $E(t)>\ln\left[\frac{p\alpha_{1}}{(1-p)\alpha_{2}}\right]^{1/\alpha_{1}}$,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\varphi^{\star}(2,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{2}^{-1}(Q^{-1}(E(t)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\hbox{if $0<E(t)\leq\ln\left[\frac{p\alpha_{1}}{(1-p)\alpha_{2}}\right]^{1/\alpha_{1}}$,}\\
\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\left(\alpha_{1}E(t)-\ln\left[\frac{p\alpha_{1}}{(1-p)\alpha_{2}}\right]\right),&\hbox{if $E(t)>\ln\left[\frac{p\alpha_{1}}{(1-p)\alpha_{2}}\right]^{1/\alpha_{1}}$.}\end{array}\right.

Regardless of whether
x_{0}=1
or
x_{0}=2
, the same argument as in Example
1
shows that there is a
\widetilde{p}\neq p
such that
P^{\#}[\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}]>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}]
and
\mu^{\#}(\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})
, where
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}
is the uniformly optimal search plan for
\widetilde{\pi}
, and
\widetilde{\pi}(1)=\widetilde{p}
and
\widetilde{\pi}(2)=1-\widetilde{p}
. The analysis for Case (ii) is completely similar and hence is omitted.

Corollary
1
shows that the conclusion of Theorem
4.1
will hold if the detection is homogeneous and exponential. Example
3
suggests that the conclusion of Theorem
4.1
might still be true as far as the detection function is exponential (homogeneous or not). This is indeed the case, as the next theorem shows.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume
X
is discrete and
x_{0}
is the true cell in which the target is located. Suppose the cost function takes the form
c(x,y)=y
for all
y\geq 0
and
x\in X
, the detection function is exponential, i.e.,
d(x,y)=1-e^{-\alpha_{x}y}
for all
y\geq 0
where
\alpha_{x}>0
,
\Pi
is a non-degenerate target distribution on
X
, and
\varphi^{\star}
is the uniformly optimal search plan for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
such that
0\leq\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)<E(t)
. Then there exists another target distribution
\widetilde{\Pi}\neq\Pi
such that
P^{\#}[\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}]>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}]
and
\mu^{\#}(\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})
, where
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}
is the uniformly optimal search plan for
\widetilde{\Pi}
within
\Phi(E)
.

Proof.

Let
X=\{1,2,\dots,m\}
where
m\geq 2
and
\sum_{x=1}^{m}\pi(x)=1
where each
0<\pi(x)<1
for each
x\in X
. The calculation here is similar to that in Example 2.2.8 of Stone (1975). For each
x\in X
we have

q_{x}^{-1}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}\ln\left[\frac{\pi(x)\alpha_{x}}{\lambda}\right],&\hbox{if $0<\lambda\leq\pi(x)\alpha_{x}$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Since we can always rename each cell in
X
, we may assume without loss of generality that

\pi(1)\alpha_{1}\leq\pi(2)\alpha_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\pi(m)\alpha_{m}
. Then

Q(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\ln\left(\frac{\prod_{x=1}^{m}\left[\pi(x)\alpha_{x}\right]^{1/\alpha_{x}}}{\lambda^{\sum_{x=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}}}\right),&\hbox{if $0<\lambda\leq\pi(1)\alpha_{1}$,}\\
\ln\left(\frac{\prod_{x=2}^{m}\left[\pi(x)\alpha_{x}\right]^{1/\alpha_{x}}}{\lambda^{\sum_{x=2}^{m}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}}}\right),&\hbox{if $\pi(1)\alpha_{1}<\lambda\leq\pi(2)\alpha_{2}$,}\\
\ldots,&\hbox{$\ldots$}\\
\frac{1}{\alpha_{m}}\ln\left[\frac{\pi(m)\alpha_{m}}{\lambda}\right],&\hbox{$\pi(m-1)\alpha_{m-1}<\lambda\leq\pi(m)\alpha_{m}$,}\\
0,&\hbox{$\lambda>\pi(m)\alpha_{m}$.}\end{array}\right.

Therefore,

Q^{-1}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(e^{-\lambda}\prod_{x=1}^{m}\left[\pi(x)\alpha_{x}\right]^{1/\alpha_{x}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\sum_{x=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}}},&\hbox{if $0<\lambda\leq\ln\left(\frac{\prod_{x=1}^{m}\left[\pi(x)\alpha_{x}\right]^{1/\alpha_{x}}}{\left[\pi(1)\alpha_{1}\right]^{\sum_{x=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}}}\right)$,}\\
\left(e^{-\lambda}\prod_{x=2}^{m}\left[\pi(x)\alpha_{x}\right]^{1/\alpha_{x}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\sum_{x=2}^{m}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}}},&\hbox{if $\ln\left(\frac{\prod_{x=1}^{m}\left[\pi(x)\alpha_{x}\right]^{1/\alpha_{x}}}{\left[\pi(1)\alpha_{1}\right]^{\sum_{x=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}}}\right)<\lambda\leq\ln\left(\frac{\prod_{x=2}^{m}\left[\pi(x)\alpha_{x}\right]^{1/\alpha_{x}}}{\left[\pi(1)\alpha_{1}\right]^{\sum_{x=2}^{m}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}}}\right)$,}\\
\ldots,&\hbox{$\ldots$}\\
\left(e^{-\lambda}[\pi(m)\alpha_{m}]^{1/\alpha_{m}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\alpha_{m}}}},&\hbox{$\lambda>\frac{1}{\alpha_{m}}\ln\left[\frac{\pi(m)\alpha_{m}}{\pi(m-1)\alpha_{m-1}}\right]$.}\end{array}\right.

It follows that the uniformly optimal search plan
\varphi^{\star}
for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
is a piecewise function and each piece of
\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)
is a monotonic function of
\pi(x_{0})
. For example, the first piece of

\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)
equals

\frac{1}{\alpha_{x_{0}}}\ln\left(\frac{[\pi(x_{0})\alpha_{x_{0}}]}{\left[e^{E(t)}\prod_{x=1}^{m}[\pi(x)\alpha_{x}]^{1/\alpha_{x}}\right]^{\sum_{x=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}}}\right).

Since the exponent of
\pi(x_{0})
in this expression is positive, we can increase the value of
\pi(x_{0})
and decrease the value of
\pi(x)
for some
x\neq x_{0}
to make this expression larger. The same can be said for other pieces of
\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)
. Therefore, we can construct a new target distribution
\widetilde{\Pi}\neq\Pi
such that
P^{\#}[\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}]>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}]
and
\mu^{\#}(\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})
.
∎

4.2.2
The continuous case

The technique we used in the proof of Theorem
4.1
does not apply in the continuous case because the probability that a continuous random variable takes a given value is zero. Therefore, the strategy is to construct a new target distribution
\widetilde{\Pi}
on
X
that puts more probability mass on a neighborhood of
x_{0}
than
\Pi
does. Note that the new target distribution
\widetilde{\Pi}
and the original target distribution
\Pi
need not belong to the same distribution family. In practice, two analysts can choose two different target distributions belonging to two different distribution families.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume
X
is continuous and
x_{0}
is the true target location. Suppose the cost function takes the form
c(x,y)=y
for all
y\geq 0
and
x\in X
, the detection function is homogeneous and regular,
\Pi
is a target distribution, and
\varphi^{\star}
is the uniformly optimal search plan for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
. Then there exists a target distribution
\widetilde{\Pi}\neq\Pi
such that
P^{\#}[\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}]>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}]
and
\mu^{\#}(\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})
, where
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}
is the uniformly optimal search plan for
\widetilde{\Pi}
within
\Phi(E)
.

Proof.

Let
B_{n}(r)
denote the
n
-dimensional ball with center
x_{0}
and radius
r>0
and
V_{n}(r)
denote its volume. Define a new target distribution as follows:

\widetilde{\pi}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{V_{n}(r)},&\hbox{for $x\in B_{n}(r)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

where
\frac{1}{V_{n}(r)}>\pi(x_{0})
. By assumption, we have
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x,y)=d^{\prime}(y)
and

\widetilde{q}_{x}(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{V_{n}(r)}d^{\prime}(y),&\hbox{if $x\in B_{n}(r)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\widetilde{q}_{x}^{-1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{inverse of $d^{\prime}(y)$ evaluated at $\frac{\lambda}{\widetilde{\pi}(x)}$},&\hbox{for $0<\lambda\leq\widetilde{q}_{x}(0)$ and $x\in B_{n}(r)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{inverse of $d^{\prime}(y)$ evaluated at $\lambda V_{n}(r)$},&\hbox{for $0<\lambda\leq\frac{d^{\prime}(0)}{V_{n}(r)}$ and $x\in B_{n}(r)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

and

\widetilde{Q}(\lambda)=\int_{X}\widetilde{q}_{x}^{-1}(\lambda)dx=V_{n}(r)\times\widetilde{q}_{x}^{-1}(\lambda).

Therefore,

\widetilde{Q}^{-1}(\lambda)=\left(\widetilde{q}_{x}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\left(\lambda/V_{n}(r)\right)=\widetilde{q}_{x}\left(\lambda/V_{n}(r)\right),

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{q}_{x}^{-1}(\widetilde{Q}^{-1}(E(t)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{q}_{x}^{-1}\left(\widetilde{q}_{x}\left(E(t)/V_{n}(r)\right)\right),&\hbox{for $0<\widetilde{q}_{x}\left(E(t)/V_{n}(r)\right)\leq\widetilde{q}_{x}(0)$ and $x\in B_{n}(r)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{E(t)}{V_{n}(r)},&\hbox{$x\in B_{n}(r)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

where the last equality follows from the fact that
q_{x}(y)=\pi(x)d^{\prime}(y)
is strictly decreasing in
y
for each
x\in X
.
Therefore, regardless of the value of
\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)
, there exists
r>0
small enough such that
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}(x_{0},t)>\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)
. It follows from (
1
) and (
2
) that
P^{\#}[\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}]>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}]
and
\mu^{\#}(\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})
.
∎

Remark.
Here we do not have the constraint
\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)<E(t)
as in the discrete case. In the discrete case,
\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)
is the amount of effort allocated at cell
x_{0}
; hence such a constraint is needed. In the continuous case,
\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)
is the value of an allocation/effort density at
x_{0}
, not the amount of effort placed at the location
x_{0}
. Therefore, it is possible that
\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)\geq E(t)
. Also, we cannot switch the values of a continuous target density at two different points without destroying its continuity. Therefore, the assumption of Theorem
4.3
is stronger than that of Theorem
4.1
.

The next example shows that the conclusion of Theorem
4.3
may still hold when the detection function is not homogeneous.

Example 4
.

Suppose
a<0<b
,
(a,b)\subseteq X\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, the target distribution is

\pi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{b-a},&\hbox{if $a<x<b$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

and the detection function is

d(x,y)=1-e^{-\alpha_{x}y},\quad y\geq 0,

where

\alpha_{x}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\beta_{1},&\hbox{if $x\geq 0$,}\\
\beta_{2},&\hbox{if $x<0$,}\end{array}\right.

and
\beta_{1}>\beta_{2}>0
. Then

q_{x}(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\beta_{1}}{b-a}e^{-\beta_{1}y},&\hbox{if $0\leq x<b$,}\\
\frac{\beta_{2}}{b-a}e^{-\beta_{2}y},&\hbox{if $a<x<0$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise,}\\
\end{array}\right.

and

\varphi^{\star}(x,t)q_{x}^{-1}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\beta_{1}}\ln\left[\frac{\beta_{1}}{\lambda(b-a)}\right],&\hbox{if $0<\lambda\leq\frac{\beta_{1}}{b-a}$ and $0\leq x<b$,}\\
\frac{1}{\beta_{2}}\ln\left[\frac{\beta_{2}}{\lambda(b-a)}\right],&\hbox{if $0<\lambda\leq\frac{\beta_{2}}{b-a}$ and $a<x<0$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\\
\end{array}\right.

It follows that

\displaystyle Q(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{0}\frac{1}{\beta_{2}}\ln\left[\frac{\beta_{2}}{\lambda(b-a)}\right]dx+\int_{0}^{b}\frac{1}{\beta_{1}}\ln\left[\frac{\beta_{1}}{\lambda(b-a)}\right]dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-a}{\beta_{2}}\ln\left[\frac{\beta_{2}}{\lambda(b-a)}\right]+\frac{b}{\beta_{1}}\ln\left[\frac{\beta_{1}}{\lambda(b-a)}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\beta_{1}^{b/\beta_{1}}\beta_{2}^{-a/\beta_{2}}}{\left[\lambda(b-a)\right]^{b/\beta_{1}-a/\beta_{2}}}\right),

and

\displaystyle Q^{-1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b-a}\left(e^{-\lambda}\beta_{1}^{b/\beta_{1}}\beta_{2}^{-a/\beta_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{b/\beta_{1}-a/\beta_{2}}}.

Therefore, the uniformly optimal search plan for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
is given by

\displaystyle\varphi^{\star}(x,t)=q_{x}^{-1}(Q^{-1}(E(t)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\beta_{1}}\left[\frac{E(t)}{b/\beta_{1}-a/\beta_{2}}+\frac{a/\beta_{2}}{b/\beta_{1}-a/\beta_{2}}\ln\beta_{1}+\frac{a/\beta_{2}}{b/\beta_{1}-a/\beta_{2}}\ln\beta_{2}\right],&\hbox{if $E(t)>\frac{a}{\beta_{2}}\ln(\beta_{1}/\beta_{2})$ and $0\leq x<b$,}\\
\frac{1}{\beta_{2}}\left[\frac{E(t)}{b/\beta_{1}-a/\beta_{2}}-\frac{b/\beta_{1}}{b/\beta_{1}-a/\beta_{2}}\ln\beta_{1}+\frac{b/\beta_{1}}{b/\beta_{1}-a/\beta_{2}}\ln\beta_{2}\right],&\hbox{if $E(t)>\frac{b}{\beta_{1}}\ln(\beta_{2}/\beta_{1})$ and $a<x<0$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\\
\end{array}\right.

Take
\beta_{1}=2\beta_{2}
and
\beta_{2}>0
. Assume
x_{0}=0
and
E(t)>\frac{a}{\beta_{2}}\ln 2
. Then we have

\displaystyle\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\beta_{2}}\left[\frac{E(t)\beta_{2}}{2b-a}+\frac{2a}{b-2a}\ln(2\beta_{2})+\frac{a}{2b-a}\ln\beta_{2}\right].

Consider the following new target distribution

\widetilde{\pi}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\widetilde{b}-a},&\hbox{if $a<x<\widetilde{b}$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Let
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}
be the uniformly optimal search plan for
\widetilde{\Pi}
within
\Phi(E)
. We can always choose
\widetilde{b}>0
sufficiently small such that
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}(x_{0},t)>\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)
; hence
P^{\#}[\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}]>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}]
and
\mu^{\#}(\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})
.

The next theorem shows that the conclusion of Theorem
4.3
holds when the detection function is exponential (homogeneous or not).

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume
X
is continuous and
x_{0}
is the true target location. Suppose the cost function takes the form
c(x,y)=y
for all
y\geq 0
and
x\in X
, the detection function is exponential, i.e.,
d(x,y)=1-e^{-\alpha_{x}y}
for all
y\geq 0
where
\alpha_{x}>0
,
\Pi
is a target distribution on
X
, and
\varphi^{\star}
is the uniformly optimal search plan for
\Pi
within
\Phi(E)
. Then there exists another target distribution
\widetilde{\Pi}\neq\Pi
such that
P^{\#}[\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}]>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}]
and
\mu^{\#}(\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})
, where
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}
is the uniformly optimal search plan for
\widetilde{\Pi}
within
\Phi(E)
.

Proof.

We still use
B_{n}(r)
to denote the
n
-dimensional ball with center
x_{0}
and radius
r>0
. Consider the following target distribution:

\widetilde{\pi}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}\left(\int_{B_{n}(r)}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}dx\right)^{-1},&\hbox{if $x\in B_{n}(r)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

For convenience, we let
I_{r}
denote the integral
\int_{B_{n}(r)}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}dx
. By assumption, we have
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}d(x,y)=\alpha_{x}e^{-\alpha_{x}y}
for
x\in X
and
y\geq 0
. Thus,

\widetilde{q}_{x}(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}I_{r}^{-1}e^{-\alpha_{x}y},&\hbox{if $x\in B_{n}(r)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise,}\\
\end{array}\right.

and

\widetilde{q}_{x}^{-1}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}\ln\left(\lambda I_{r}\right),&\hbox{if $0<\lambda\leq I_{r}^{-1}$ and $x\in B_{n}(r)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\\
\end{array}\right.

Therefore,

\widetilde{Q}(\lambda)=\int_{X}\widetilde{q}_{x}^{-1}(\lambda)dx=-\int_{B_{n}(r)}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}\ln\left(\lambda I_{r}\right)dx=-I_{r}\ln\left(\lambda I_{r}\right),

and

\widetilde{Q}^{-1}(\lambda)=I_{r}^{-1}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{I_{r}}}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}(x,t)=\widetilde{q}_{x}^{-1}(\widetilde{Q}^{-1}(E(t)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}\frac{E(t)}{\int_{B_{n}(r)}\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}dx},&\hbox{if $x\in B_{n}(r)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\\
\end{array}\right.

Regardless of the value of
\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)
, the continuity of integrals ensures that we can choose
r>0
sufficiently small such that
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}(x_{0},t)>\varphi^{\star}(x_{0},t)
. Then
P^{\#}[\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}]>P^{\#}[\varphi^{\star}]
and
\mu^{\#}(\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star})<\mu^{\#}(\varphi^{\star})
.
∎

5
Concluding remarks

We have focused only on the case of a stationary target. For a moving target, the target distribution will be the law of a stochastic process (i.e., a family of distributions), but it is subjective too. Therefore, the key results in this article are expected to generalize to the case of a moving target. The same can be said for the multiple target tracking problem (e.g., Stone et al. 2014b).

The optimal search theory has made remarkable progress since WWII. Some people believe all interesting problems in the optimal search theory have been solved or reduced to simple numerical problems. This article dispels such a belief. As we have seen, the current “best practice” in the search community does not generate a search plan that maximizes the true detection probability at each moment. The problem of finding a search plan that maximizes the true detection probability at each moment remains one of the most important unsolved problems. This problem may be unsolvable. If this is the case, we need to establish this fact. However, if this problem is solvable, we will likely need a new approach other than the Bayesian approach. Either way, this problem warrants our future efforts.
