Abstract
. The inducibility of a graph represents its maximum density as an induced subgraph over all possible sequences of graphs of size growing to infinity. This invariant of graphs has been extensively studied since its introduction in 1975 1975 by Pippenger and Golumbic. In 2017 2017 , Czabarka, Székely and Wagner extended this notion to leaf-labeled rooted binary trees, which are objects widely studied in the field of phylogenetics. They obtain the first results and bounds for the densities and inducibilities of such trees. Following up on their work, we apply Razborov’s flag algebra theory to this setting, introducing the flag algebra of rooted leaf-labeled binary trees. This framework allows us to use polynomial optimization methods, based on semidefinite programming, to efficiently obtain new upper bounds for the inducibility of trees and to improve existing ones. Additionally, we obtain the first outer approximations of profiles of trees, which represent all possible simultaneous densities of a pair of trees in a sequence of trees of growing sizes. Finally, we are able to prove the non-convexity of some of these profiles. Keywords Inducibility ⋅ \cdot binary tree ⋅ \cdot graph profile ⋅ \cdot flag algebras ⋅ \cdot semidefinite programming

1.
Introduction

Asymptotic extremal graph theory studies the behaviour of graphs whose number of vertices grows towards infinity. It is an old and well-studied area of graph theory in which many areas of mathematics meet, and where even simple-sounding conjectures can prove extremely challenging.
A main topic in this area is the problem of finding the inducibility of a graph, i.e., its maximum density as an induced subgraph in an arbitrarily large graph
[
43
]
. It is a very hard problem that remains open even for many “easy” graphs despite being widely studied: for instance, the inducibility of the
5
-cycle was determined in
2018

[
5
]
, more than
40
years after being conjectured by Pippenger and Golumbic
[
43
]
. An adjacent problem consists in determining the profile of a set of graphs: that is, the exact relations between the densities of these graphs in a common large graph. In
2017
, Czabarka, Székely and Wagner
[
15
]
extended the notions of density and inducibility to leaf-labeled rooted binary trees, a type of trees stemming from phylogenetics. They obtained the first results and bounds for the inducibilities of these trees.
In this paper, we follow up on their work and apply, for the first time, flag algebra theory to the setting of leaf-labeled rooted binary trees. Flag algebras are a powerful tool in extremal combinatorics, introduced in
2007
by Razborov
[
45
]
. They have been successfully used to tackle and solve several very challenging problems of graph theory
[
46
,
24
,
28
,
31
,
4
,
36
,
6
]
, by allowing the application of tools from polynomial optimization, computationally in the form of semidefinite programming, to extremal combinatorics. Here, we use them to develop a computer-assisted way to obtain strong rigorous bounds on inducibilities of trees. As main results, we recover all known inducibilities of small trees (up to a small epsilon), obtain over
300
new bounds on inducibilities, and the first outer approximations of profiles of trees, for some of which we prove nonconvexity. To do so, we solve the first instance of a generalized problem of moments in flag algebras.

In what follows we describe the organization of the paper.
In Section
2
, we start by defining the setting of leaf-labeled rooted binary trees introduced by Czabarka et al.
[
15
]
and the notions of densities, inducibilities and tree-profiles in this context. Section
3
defines the flag algebra of rooted binary trees and explains the main tools of Razborov’s flag algebra theory we use in this setting. We describe in Section
4
how we can turn it into a computational approach based on semidefinite programming and polynomial optimization tools. This allows us to compute, in a systematic manner, bounds on the inducibilities of trees. We detail in Section
5
our results: we recover all known inducibilities of small trees, improve the existing bounds on one tree of special interest, and obtain more than
300
new bounds as well as the first outer approximations of tree-profiles. Furthermore, we give some exact results on parts of the tree-profiles of two types of trees: the caterpillar tree of size
k

2.
Density and inducibility of rooted leaf-labeled binary trees

2.1.
Rooted leaf-labeled binary trees and subtrees

In this article, we are working with rooted leaf-labeled binary trees. “Leaf-labeled” means that only the leaves are considered “vertices.” This view on vertices significantly changes how we define subtrees (these trees are sometimes called “topological trees”
[
19
]
). The vertices (in the usual graph sense) which are not leaves are called
inner vertices
. “Rooted” means we consider trees in which one vertex, if there is one, has been designated as the tree’s
root
. The empty graph
\varnothing
and the graph consisting of only one vertex are rooted trees. The vertex of the latter is a root and a leaf simultaneously. “Binary” means that every inner vertex has exactly two children.

In this paper, we want to investigate the densities of subtrees in large trees and asymptotic constructions. Typically, trees are sparse objects: As we consider larger and larger trees, the probability that a uniformly sampled induced subgraph of a fixed number of vertices is an independent set approaches one. In the leaf-labeled setting, this changes: trees are no longer sparse but dense objects. We will always obtain another tree if we sample a subtree coming from a subset of leaves, as described below. Accordingly, it is possible to define meaningful subtree densities in trees and their limits here.

In this paper, every mention of a tree will now refer to a leaf-labeled rooted binary tree, except when explicitly stated otherwise.

T
, we denote by
\mathcal{L}(T)
its set of leaves, and we define its size

as its number of leaves. We consider the trees up to isomorphism; two trees
T
and

are isomorphic whenever there is a graph isomorphism between them which preserves the root. We denote this by
T\cong S
. We call the set of all trees up to isomorphism
\mdmathbb{T}
, and let
\mdmathbb{T}_{n}\subseteq\mdmathbb{T}
denote the subset of trees with
n
leaves. We denote by
\varnothing
the empty tree and consider it to be of size

. For

: The first few numbers in the sequence are

2.2.
Inducibility of trees

Inducibility of graphs.

The notion of inducibility of graphs has been introduced in
1975
by Pippenger and Golumbic
[
43
]
, who studied the maximum frequency with which a fixed graph on
k
vertices can appear in another graph whose number of vertices goes to infinity. It is an extensively studied topic that has been approached in many different ways. In particular, Razborov’s flag algebra theory has already been successfully applied to problems of inducibility in graphs. Indeed, it was used by Sperfeld in
2011
to determine new bounds on the inducibility of several oriented graphs. In
2012
, Falgas-Ravry and Vaughan
[
25
]
introduced the software ‘Flagmatic’ to determine exact inducibilities and upper bounds for several
3
-graphs up to
5
vertices, as well as for oriented star graphs of size
3
and
4
. In
2013
, Hirst
[
29
]
determined the inducibilities of
K_{1,1,2}
and the so-called paw graph. In
2016
, Balogh, Hu, Lidický and Pfender
[
5
]
proved, using flag algebras, that the maximum density of the induced
5
-cycle
C_{5}
is achieved by an iterated blow-up of the 5-cycle. Following up on this, Lidický, Mattes and Pfender
[
35
]
determined in
2022
the maximum number of induced copies of
C_{5}
in a graph on
n
vertices, as well as all the graphs in which this maximum is attained.

Inducibility of leaf-labeled rooted binary trees.

Czabarka et al.
[
15
]
adapted the concept of inducibility in graphs to rooted leaf-labeled binary trees in the following way: We consider two trees
S
and
T
, with
|S|=k
and
|T|=n
(
n\geq k
).
\mathcal{C}(S,T)
is the number of subtrees of
T
that are isomorphic to
S
. The
subtree density
of the tree
S
in the tree
T
is

\displaystyle p(S;T)\coloneqq\frac{\mathcal{C}(S,T)}{\binom{n}{k}}.

It is the proportion of copies of
S
in
T
over all subtrees of the same size of
T
. In other words, it is the probability of obtaining a subtree isomorphic to
S
when picking uniformly at random a subtree of size
|S|
in
T
. As such, we can reformulate the definition as

\displaystyle p(S;T)

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{P}[T[\mathbf{V}]\cong{S}]\in[0,1],

where
\mathbf{V}
is a uniformly random subset of leaves of
T
of size
|S|
, and
T[\mathbf{V}]
is the subtree of
T
induced by
\mathbf{V}
.

We now consider an increasing sequence of trees
\mathcal{T}=(T_{k})_{k\geq 0}
, i.e. such that
(|T_{k}|)_{k\geq 0}
is strictly increasing.
We define the subtree density of a tree
S
in this increasing sequence of trees as

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(S)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}p(S;T_{n})=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mdmathbb{P}[T_{n}[V_{n}]\cong{S}]\in[0,1],

where
\mathbf{V_{n}}
is a random subset of leaves of
T_{n}
of size
|S|
. Note that the vector of all densities
(p(S;T_{n}))_{S\in\mdmathbb{T}}
lies in
[0,1]^{\mdmathbb{T}}
, which is compact by Tychonoff’s theorem. Thus, we can always find a subsequence such that all the densities converge. From now on, we always work (implicitly) with such
converging increasing sequences
.

The
inducibility
of the tree
S
is then the maximum subtree density of
S
in any converging increasing sequences of trees:

(1)

\displaystyle I(S)\coloneqq\max\limits_{\mathcal{T}}\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(S).

This coincides with the definition of the inducibility of a tree in
[
15
]
as the limit superior of the maximum subtree density of
S
in a tree:

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{|T|\rightarrow\infty}p(S;T)=\limsup\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\max\limits_{T:|T|=n}p(S;T)

Indeed, by Tychonoff’s theorem, the sequence of maximizers of
p(S;\cdot)
of size
n
has a converging subsequence. Thus, the definition of the inducibility of trees in
[
15
]
matches our definition of
I(S)
, and the maximum in (
1
) is attained.

Previous results on the inducibility of trees.

In
2017
, Czabarka et al.
[
15
]
proved that every binary tree has positive inducibility and that the only trees with inducibility
1
are the caterpillar trees. They also obtained the first inducibilities of binary trees: the complete binary tree of height
2
,
the even tree of size
r
; and provided bounds for other trees. They applied these results to crossings in random tanglerams, which are pairs of binary trees of same size, whose leaves are joined by a perfect matching.
.

Several articles have also focused on the inducibility of
d
-ary trees, i.e., trees in which each of the non-leaf vertices of has between
2
and
d
children.
In
2018
, Dossou-Olory and Wagner
[
19
]
studied the relation between inducibilities with fixed degree and with bounded degree; they also provided a lower bound on the limit inferior on the density of the binary caterpillar. Additionally, they computed in
[
20
]
stronger bounds on the inducibility of small, but very challenging binary and ternary trees. In
2021
, they were able to obtain more exact inducibilities and bounds on binary and
d
-ary trees, and obtained results on the speed of convergence of the maximum density of a tree
[
21
]
.
In
2020
, Czabarka et al.
[
14
]
proved the following theorem: for any
d
-ary tree
S
(i.e., each of the non-leaf vertices of
S
has between
2
and
d
children),

\max\limits_{|T|=n}p(S;T)=I(S)+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}).

They showed as well that, for any binary tree
S
, the inducibility of
S
in binary trees is equal to its inducibility in
d
-ary trees and in strictly
d
-ary trees (trees in which every vertex has exactly
0
or
d
children).

In
2016
, Alon, Naves and Sudakov
[
2
]
studied the density of some patterns of size
4
(“quartets”) in tree-pairs of trivalent trees, which are unrooted trees in which every non-leaf vertex has exactly three neighbors. To do so, they used flag algebra calculus that they adapt to this particular setting. They then solved a semidefinite program to obtain bounds and various results on the density of such patterns. They obtained as well some further results on (unrooted) caterpillar trees, by seeing them as permutations and using the flag algebras of permutations.

2.3.
Tree profiles

The
Tree-profile
of trees
T
and
S
is the set of pairs of densities
(\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(T),\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(S))
that can be attained simultaneously from the same increasing sequence of trees
\mathcal{T}
.

\displaystyle\profile(T,S)\coloneqq\{(\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(T),\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(S))\mid\mathcal{T}\text{ is an increasing sequence of trees}\}\subseteq[0,1]^{2}

Graph profiles were first introduced in
1979
by Erdős, Lovász, and Spencer
[
23
]
, who investigated how the densities of two graphs behaved with respect to each other. In particular, they showed that the profile of any
m
connected graphs is full-dimensional in
\mdmathbb{R}^{m}
. However, graph profiles have proven to be very challenging to study; they are not necessarily convex or even semialgebraic sets, and still very little is known about them.
A major breakthrough on the topic was obtained in
2008
by Razborov
[
46
]
, who used his flag algebra theory to obtain the exact relation between the density of triangles in a graph and its edge density. In other words, he gave the first full description of a graph profile, the profile of the triangle and the edge graph. This problem was introduced by Turán in
1941

[
54
]
and was until then still (mostly) unsolved. The case of edge versus the complete graph
K_{4}
was then solved by Nikiforov in
2010

[
40
]
. The general case of
K_{n}
and the edge was solved in
2016
by Reiher
[
49
]
, proving a conjecture of Lovász and Simonovits
[
37
]
.

There is no known full profile of three or more connected graphs, though there exist partial results. In
2013
, Huang et al.
[
30
]
studied profiles of four induced graphs with up to three vertices, and described exactly the profile of 3-cliques and 3-anticliques on three vertices. They also gave an exact description in the triangle-free case, with the use of flag algebras. Glebov et al.
[
27
]
followed on this work in
2016
by determining all profiles of two induced graphs of size
3
.
Graph profiles of the edge and the
k
-edge path have been determined in
2016
by Nagy for
n=4

[
39
]
, notably using results from Ahlswede and Katona who proved the upper boundary in the case
k=2
in
1978

[
1
]
.
In
2018
, Reiher and Wagner
[
48
]
obtained an upper bound on the profile of the edge and the
k
-star for all integers
k\leq 2
.
Recently, Cairncross and Muyabi
[
11
]
obtained exact profiles of ordered graphs, and the first results on profiles of colored graphs.
In
2022
, Blekherman et al.
[
8
]
computed and studied the tropicalization of graph and hypergraph profiles and, through it, exhibited limitations for the sums of squares method to prove graph density inequalities.

In
2016
, Bubeck and Linial
[
10
]
defined the density of a tree
S
in another tree
T
as the number of copies of
S
divided by the number of connected subgraphs of size
\lvert S\rvert
inducing a tree in
T
. In this context, they investigated the limit sets of
k
-profiles of trees, that is, the profile of all trees on
k
vertices. They showed that these profiles are always convex. Following up on this paper, Chan et al.
[
12
]
proved further results on the densities and inducibilities of such trees.

3.
The flag algebra of rooted binary trees

Flag algebras, first introduced by Alexander Razborov in 2007
[
45
]
, are one of the most powerful and promising tools in extremal combinatorics. They provide a way to formulate extremal problems in graphs analogously to polynomial optimization problems. These can then be relaxed into semidefinite programs that can efficiently be solved by a computer, thus providing automated certificates for (often tight) bounds for such problems.
The similarities to polynomial optimization are not coincidental: recent developments have brought to light that sums of squares in flag algebras correspond to solving the limit of a sequence of polynomial optimization problems. The problems in the sequence grow in the number of variables (but not degree), counterbalanced by exhibiting more and more symmetries
[
44
]
. Interpreted this way, flag algebras can be recovered from the
representation stability
of the problem, relating to
dimension free descriptions
of the relevant cones, a more general concept recently investigated in the setting of convex optimization in
[
34
]
.

Flag algebras have been used for a wide variety of problems, from densities in

graphs
[
24
,
28
]
to hypergraphs
[
31
]
, permutations
[
4
]
, Ramsey numbers
[
36
]
, crossing numbers of graphs
[
6
]
, and more.
Flag algebras have been used by Alon, Naves and Sudakov in
[
2
]
to study tree-pairs of trivalent phylogenetic trees. To the best of our knowledge, they have so far not been used on other types of trees, in particular not on rooted binary trees. A thorough overview of the theory of flag algebras is given in
[
16
]
, another one in
[
9
]
, although more focused on non-induced flag algebras. We explain it here in the setting of trees.

3.1.
Model theoretic view

Razborov introduced flag algebras in the setting of first-order model theory
[
45
]
. He works with a language
L
containing predicate symbols (to be interpreted in models as
relations
between tuples of elements), but no functions or distinguished elements. The theory
T
is assumed to be universal, i.e., all its axioms can be written in the form

\forall x_{1}\forall x_{2}\ldots\forall x_{n}\colon\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),

where
\phi
is a quantifier free formula in
L
. Furthermore, he assumes existence of an infinite model of
T
, implying that models of each finite size exist.
To avoid having to reprove Razborov’s results in our setting, we provide a model theoretic description of leaf-labeled trees. For this, we give in Theorem
3.1
a theory with one three-argument predicate
\mathcal{P}(\cdot;\cdot,\cdot)
and three axioms such that the models of the theory are our trees.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 3
, and let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq[n]^{3}
be a relation. We write
\mathcal{P}(i;j,k)
for
(i,j,k)\in\mathcal{P}
. The two following statements are equivalent:

(1)

There exists a unique tree
T
such that, for any three distinct leaves
i,j,k\in\mathcal{L}(T)
,

(2)

\mathcal{P}(i;j,k)\Leftrightarrow\text{ in the subtree of T induced by $\{i,j,k\}$, $i$ is at height $1$}

No relations between non-distinct
i,j,k
hold.

(2)

\mathcal{P}
satisfies the following axioms for all distinct
i,j,k,\ell\in[n]
:

(a)

\mathcal{P}(i;j,k)\ \Leftrightarrow\ \mathcal{P}(i;k,j)
,

(b)

exactly one of
\mathcal{P}(i;j,k),\mathcal{P}(j;i,k),\mathcal{P}(k;i,j)
is true,

(c)

\mathcal{P}(i;j,k)\Rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\ell;j,k)\lor\mathcal{P}(i;j,\ell)
.

Proof.

We start by proving that
1
implies
2
.
Let us assume that
\mathcal{P}\subseteq[n]^{3}
satisfies
1
:
\mathcal{P}
then represents a unique tree
T
of size
n
. Axioms
2a
and
2b
follow directly from the definition of a tree. To prove axiom
2c
, let us consider distinct
i,j,k,\ell\in[n]
. We assume that
\mathcal{P}(i;j,k)
is true. We then consider the subtree induced by
\{\ell,j,k\}
in
T
. If
\ell
is at height
1
in this subtree,
\mathcal{P}(\ell;j,k)
is true and
2c
holds. If not, we can assume without loss of generality that
j
is at height
1
, i.e.,
\mathcal{P}(j;\ell,k)
holds. Then, since
i
is at height
1
in the subtree induced by
\{i,j,k\}
,
i
has to be at height
1
as well in the subtree induced by
\{i,j,\ell\}
, hence
\mathcal{P}(i;j,\ell)
is true and
2c
holds as well.

We now prove that
2
implies
1
. To do so, we proceed by induction.
For
n=3
, let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\{1,2,3\}^{3}
fulfill the axioms of
2
. Then, there exists
i\in\{1,2,3\}
such that
\mathcal{P}(i;j,k)
and
\mathcal{P}(i;k,j)
hold, where
\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}
. By assumption,
\mathcal{P}
is false on the remaining tuples in
\{1,2,3\}^{3}
. Then, with
T
the tree of size
3
with leaf
i
at height
1
and leaves
j
and
k
at height
2
, represented in Figure
7
,
1
holds.

\{i,j,k\}
with
\mathcal{P}(i;j,k)

Let
n\geq 4
, and suppose that for any
3\leq k\leq n
, any
\mathcal{P}\subseteq[k]^{3}
satisfying
2
also satisfies
1
. Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq[n+1]^{3}
fulfill the axioms of
2
. We denote by
\mathcal{P}|_{n}
be the restriction of
\mathcal{P}
to
[n]^{3}
. By induction,
\mathcal{P}|_{n}
uniquely describes a tree
T_{n}
of size
n
. We will now show that
\mathcal{P}
uniquely describes a tree
T_{n+1}
obtained by attaching to
T_{n}
the leaf
(n+1)
in a position uniquely defined by
\mathcal{P}
.

We define a graph
G
with vertices
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and an edge for every pair
\{i,j\}
such that
\mathcal{P}(n+1;i,j)
holds. We denote by
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
the two subtrees joining at the root of
T_{n}
, and by
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
their respective sets of leaves:
\mathcal{L}_{1}=\mathcal{L}(T_{1})
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}=\mathcal{L}(T_{2})
.
Note that for every
i
in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
(resp.
\mathcal{L}_{2}
), for every
j,k
in
\mathcal{L}_{2}
(resp.
\mathcal{L}_{1}
),
\mathcal{P}(i;j,k)
holds. Indeed, leaves from the same branch will have a common inner node higher than the root, but two leaves in separate branches will only join at the root node. By the same reasoning,

We make the following claims:

Claim 3.1.1
.

At least one of
{G}[\mathcal{L}_{1}]
and
{G}[\mathcal{L}_{2}]
is a complete graph.

Proof.

Let us assume that neither
{G}[\mathcal{L}_{1}]
nor
{G}[\mathcal{L}_{2}]
are complete. This means that there exist

in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and

in

such that

and

are false.
Since

\mathcal{L}_{1}
and both

and

are in

,
\mathcal{P}(i;i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
holds. As
\mathcal{P}
fulfills

, this implies
\mathcal{P}(n+1;i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\lor\mathcal{P}(i;i^{\prime},n+1)
. Because we assumed

does not hold,
\mathcal{P}(i;i^{\prime},n+1)

\mathcal{P}(i^{\prime};i,n+1)

\mathcal{P}(n+1;\cdot,\cdot)

Proof.

3.3.
Densities of flags

We can extend the notion of density in trees to flags. We define the density of a
\sigma
-tree-flag
(S,\theta)
in another
\sigma
-tree-flag
(T,\eta)
as the probability of obtaining a tree-flag isomorphic to
(S,\theta)
when choosing uniformly at random a
\mathbf{\sigma}
-subtree-flag of size
|S|
in
T
. i.e. it is the probability

\displaystyle p((S,\theta);(T,\eta))

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mdmathbb{P}[(T,\eta)[\mathbf{V}\cup\im\eta]\cong(S,\theta)]\in[0,1],

where
\mathbf{V}
is a random subset of
\mathcal{L}(T)\setminus\im(\eta)
of size
|S|-|\sigma|
.

Densities of flags follow the
chain rule
in Lemma
3.1.1
.

Lemma 3.1.1

(Chain rule for flags
[
45
]
)
.

For
S,T\in\mdmathbb{T}^{\sigma}
and an integer
n
such that
\lvert S\rvert\leq n\leq\lvert T\rvert
we have

(3)

\displaystyle p(S;T)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{S^{\prime}\in\mdmathbb{T}^{\sigma}_{n}}p(S;S^{\prime})p(S^{\prime};T).

Analogously to the unlabeled case, we define the density of a flag
(S,\theta)
in an increasing sequence of flags
\mathcal{T}=(T_{n},\eta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
(
(|T_{n}|)_{n\geq 0}
is strictly increasing) as

\displaystyle{}_{\mathcal{T}}(S,\theta)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}p((S,\theta);(T_{n},\eta_{n}))\in[0,1].

To differentiate flag densities from (unlabeled) densities, we denote them by
\psi
instead of
\phi
. If
\mathcal{T}
is a sequence of flags, but we write
\phi_{\mathcal{T}}
, then we use
\mathcal{T}
for the corresponding sequence of (unlabeled) trees
(T_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.
As before, we assume the limits exist; we are working with
converging sequences of flags
, again obtainable by choosing appropriate subsequences of flags.

We can linearly extend densities of tree-flags to formal (real) linear combinations of tree-flags

3.4.
Products of flags

Our goal is now to understand products of densities of flags. Given two
\sigma
-flags
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mdmathbb{T}^{\sigma}
we want to find a quantum tree
S_{1}\cdot S_{2}\in\mdmathbb{R}\mdmathbb{T}^{\sigma}
such that

{}_{\mathcal{T}}(S_{1}){}_{\mathcal{T}}(S_{2})={}_{\mathcal{T}}(S_{1}\cdot S_{2})

for all increasing sequences of flags
\mathcal{T}

To compute these, we need to define the
sunflower density
of two flags
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mdmathbb{T}^{\sigma}
in another flag
T=(t,\theta)\in\mdmathbb{T}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle p(S_{1},S_{2};T)\coloneqq\mdmathbb{P}[T[\mathbf{V}_{1}]\cong S_{1}\wedge T[\mathbf{V}_{2}]\cong S_{2}]\in[0,1],

where
\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L}(T))
is a uniformly random

with center
\mathbf{V}_{1}\cap\mathbf{V}_{2}=\im\theta
and petals of sizes
\lvert\mathbf{V}_{i}\setminus\im\theta\rvert=\lvert S_{i}\rvert-\lvert\sigma\rvert

S_{1}
and
S_{2}
fit in
T
simultaneously, matching only on the type
\sigma
.

The chain rule in Lemma
3.1.1
can be generalized to compute the sunflower density of several tree-flags. For every
\lvert S_{1}\rvert+\lvert S_{2}\rvert-\lvert\sigma\rvert\leq n\leq\lvert T\rvert

(4)

\displaystyle p(S_{1},S_{2};T)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{S^{\prime}\in\mdmathbb{T}^{\sigma}_{n}}p(S_{1},S_{2};S^{\prime})p(S^{\prime};T)

holds (Lemma 2.2 in
[
45
]

Theorem 3.2

3.5.

To go back from the space of linear combinations of tree-flags to the set of quantum trees, we use the

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-\lvert\sigma\rvert)!}{n!}\cdot\frac{\lvert\text{Aut}(T|_{0})\rvert}{\lvert\text{Aut}(T)\rvert}

is such that

is a

-tree-flag isomorphic to

. We can extend this operator to quantum graphs: this then provides us with a linear map from

to the space of (unlabeled) quantum trees

(Theorem 3.1 in

]

Quantum trees
S\in\mathcal{A}^{\sigma}
correspond to functions which send tree sequences
\mathcal{T}
to real numbers
{}_{\mathcal{T}}(S)
. Thus, analogously to polynomial optimization
[
32
]
, we can use the sums of squares method to compute bounds on extremal problems in trees. The idea is simple: If
f=c_{1}T_{1}+\cdots+c_{n}T_{n}
is a quantum flag in the algebra
\mathcal{A}^{\sigma}
, then both its square
f^{2}
and, more importantly, its unlabeled square
\llbracket f^{2}\rrbracket
are nonnegative functions on tree sequences (see Theorem 3.14 in
[
45
]
). Thus, every squared and unlabeled quantum flag proves an inequality of the form
\llbracket f^{2}\rrbracket\geq 0
in
\mathcal{A}^{\varnothing}
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
be a vector of
n
tree-flags of type
\sigma
.
Quantum flags involving the flags of
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
are of the form

f=c^{\top}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma},

where
c\in\mdmathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of coefficients of the quantum flag.
Unlabeled squares of quantum flags can be written as

\llbracket f^{2}\rrbracket=\llbracket(c^{\top}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma})^{2}\rrbracket=\llbracket\langle cc^{\top},\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}^{\top}\rangle\rrbracket=\langle cc^{\top},\llbracket\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}^{\top}\rrbracket\rangle,

where

\langle A,B\rangle\coloneqq\tr(A^{\top}B)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}A_{ij}B_{ij}

denotes the
trace inner product
of (symmetric) matrices
A,B\in S^{n}
.

Here,
cc^{\top}
is a rank-one
positive semidefinite matrix
, i.e.
v^{\top}(cc^{\top})v=(c^{\top}v)^{2}\geq 0
for all vectors
v\in\mdmathbb{R}^{n}
. Positive semidefinite matrices form a convex cone
S^{n}_{\succcurlyeq 0}
. We denote
X\in S^{n}_{\succcurlyeq 0}
by
X\succcurlyeq 0
. Hence, flag sums of squares
\llbracket\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}^{2}\rrbracket
, where the
f_{i}
are quantum flags in
\mathcal{A}^{\sigma}
, are of the form

\langle M_{\sigma},\llbracket\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}^{\top}\rrbracket\rangle

where
M_{\sigma}\in S^{n}_{\succcurlyeq 0}
is a positive semidefinite matrix.

Of course, we can combine sums of squares coming from algebras
\mathcal{A}^{\sigma}
of different types
\sigma
. In general, we can work with a family
(\mathcal{F}_{\sigma})_{\sigma}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
are vectors of flags of type
\sigma
. We call

f=\sum_{\sigma}\langle M_{\sigma},\llbracket\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}^{\top}\rrbracket\rangle,

where the
M_{\sigma}
are positive semidefinite matrices of appropriate sizes, a
sum of squares certificate
for the nonnegativity of a quantum tree
f\in\mathcal{A}^{\varnothing}
.

4.1.
A hierarchy of SDPs

Comparing the coefficients of a quantum flag
f\in\mathcal{A}^{\varnothing}
with the coefficients in the sum of squares
\sum_{\sigma}\langle M_{\sigma},\llbracket\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}^{\top}\rrbracket\rangle
(up to quotienting out
\mathcal{K}^{\varnothing}
) leads to linear constraints on the coefficients of the positive semidefinite matrices
M_{\sigma}
. To implement the quotient algebra
\mathcal{A}^{\varnothing}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{\mdmathbb{R}\mdmathbb{T}^{\varnothing}}}{{\mathcal{K}^{\varnothing}}}
we can add free variables corresponding to a basis of
\mathcal{K}^{\varnothing}
.

What remains to decide is which vectors of flags
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
to use. We chose to implement the analogue of the SDP hierarchy used by the software Flagmatic, as it is described in Section 2.3 of
[
25
]
. For each natural number
L\in\mdmathbb{N}
, we define a level
L
of the hierarchy, which we denote by
\mathrm{SOS}_{L}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\varnothing}
. For level
L
of the hierarchy, we consider flags of types
\sigma
with
|\sigma|\leq L
and
|\sigma|\equiv L\mod 2
. We then form the vector
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
to consist of all
\sigma
-flags with exactly
(L-|\sigma|)/2
unlabeled leaves up to (label preserving) isomorphism. This way, all products of flags in each
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}^{\top}
result in a tree with exactly
L
vertices. We would not gain anything here by considering types with
|\sigma|+1\equiv L\mod 2
, or flags with fewer unlabeled leaves due to the quotient relations (
5
).

With this choice of types and vectors of flags, we define the
L
th level of the hierarchy as

\mathrm{SOS}_{L}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{\sigma}\langle M_{\sigma},\llbracket\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}^{\top}\rrbracket\rangle\mid M_{\sigma}\succcurlyeq 0\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\varnothing}.

4.2.
Computational approach

We implemented the flag algebra of trees as part of the Julia package
FlagSOS.jl
1
1

1

https://github.com/DanielBrosch/FlagSOS.jl
introduced in the thesis
[
9
]
. All code to recover the results of the paper is available as ancillary file.

Computing products of tree flags.

We compute all relevant products between flags simultaneously in a preprocessing step: We first generate all trees up to isomorphism (up to
L
leaves, where
L
is the level of the hierarchy). For each such tree
T
we then compute all pairs of (possibly overlapping) subtrees
(S_{1},S_{2})
of
T
such that their union of leaves is the set of leaves of
T
. Adding labels to the vertices in the overlap gives us a contribution to the product of the corresponding flags .

Rounding.

The matrices
M_{\sigma}
returned by the solver are only approximately positive semi-definite, i.e. the matrices may have eigenvalues slightly below zero. To work around this, we do the following: We (numerically) compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrices
M_{\sigma}
, and fix the negative eigenvalues to zero. We then round the eigenvectors, scaled by the square roots of the positive eigenvalues, to rationals, to compute a decomposition
M_{\sigma}\approx V_{\sigma}V_{\sigma}^{\top}\succcurlyeq 0
, where
V_{\sigma}
is a rational rectangular matrix. As
V_{\sigma}V_{\sigma}^{\top}
is positive semidefinite, we obtain a rational certificate:

\sum_{\sigma}\langle M_{\sigma},\llbracket\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}^{\top}\rrbracket\rangle+f_{\text{err}}=\sum_{\sigma}\langle V_{\sigma}V_{\sigma}^{\top},\llbracket\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}^{\top}\rrbracket\rangle\geq 0.

Here
f_{\text{err}}=\sum_{T}c_{T}T\in\mathcal{A}^{\varnothing}
is an error term resulting from the rounding. By definition of trees
T
as density functionals, we know each
T
takes values in the interval
[0,1]
. This way we can bound the error term to take values in the interval

-\sum_{t}|c_{T}|\leq f_{\text{err}}\leq\sum_{t}|c_{T}|.

We add this error with the appropriate sign to computed bounds to obtain rigorous bounds.

Solvers.

We computed all numerical bounds in this paper with Mosek
[
3
]
on a server equipped with an AMD EPYC 7532 32-Core Processor @ 3.30GHz and 1024GB of RAM. The loss in objective after applying the above rounding procedure was, in all cases, of order
10^{-6}
. The exact bounds in Section
5.1.2
were computed using the ClusteredLowRankSolver
[
33
]
.

5.
Results

In this section, we show and explain some of the more interesting results we obtained thanks to the flag algebra of trees on the inducibility of trees and on tree-profiles.

5.1.
Inducibility

We can build an SDP based hierarchy for approximating the inducibility of a tree
S
the following way:

\displaystyle I(S)\coloneqq\max_{\mathcal{T}}\phi_{\mathcal{T}}(S)=\max S=\min\{t\mid t-S\geq 0\}\leq\min\{t\mid t-S\in\mathrm{SOS}_{L}\}\eqqcolon I_{L}(S),

where
L\geq|S|
is the level of the hierarchy. We are able to compute
I_{11}(S)
numerically for all trees with up to
11
leaves in at most
10
seconds each.
All these results are given in Appendix
B
.
We present here our results for some selected trees. We start by giving a full example, the inducibility of the even tree
E_{5}
. We detail how one can use known optimal constructions (i.e. tree limits attaining the inducibility) to find the exact certificate. Then, we give other exact results we managed to obtain in a similar fashion, recovering a known inducibility proven in
[
15
]
and determining two new inducibilities. Finally, we detail the progression of the bounds for three small trees where the bound does not appear to have converged yet. These include a tree with known strong, but not tight, upper and lower bounds, obtained in
[
20
]
.

5.2.
Tree-profiles

We can compute a hierarchy of outer approximations of profiles of trees
\profile(T,S)\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
. The idea is the following: We partition the interval
[\min T,\max T]
into smaller intervals

[\min T,\max T]=[a_{1}=\min T,a_{2}]\cup[a_{2},a_{3}]\cup\ldots\cup[a_{n-1},a_{n}=\max T].

On each interval
[a_{i},a_{i+1}]
we can then compute functions
f_{\mathrm{{lower}}}
and
f_{\mathrm{{upper}}}
which lower- (resp. upper-) bound the slice

\profile(T,S)\cap\left([a_{i},a_{i+1}]\times\mdmathbb{R}\right),

in the sense that
S-f_{\mathrm{{lower}}}(T)\geq 0
and
f_{\mathrm{{upper}}}(T)-S\geq 0
if
T\in[a_{i},a_{i+1}]
. We can optimize over (a suitable family of) integrable functions, trying to match them as closely as possible to the profile:

\displaystyle\max_{f_{\mathrm{{lower}}}}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\int_{a_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}f_{\mathrm{{lower}}}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\min_{f_{\mathrm{{upper}}}}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\int_{a_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}f_{\mathrm{{upper}}}(x)\,dx

s.t.

\displaystyle S-f_{\mathrm{{lower}}}(T)\geq 0\text{ if }T\in[a_{i},a_{i+1}].

s.t.

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{{upper}}}(T)-S\geq 0\text{ if }T\in[a_{i},a_{i+1}].

Note that
f(T)\in\mathcal{A}^{\varnothing}
if
f
is a polynomial in
\mdmathbb{R}[x]
. While we can compute a high enough level of the hierarchy to use quadratic functions if
T
has less than
6
leaves, in practice it does not seem to result in much better approximations when compared to linear functions. Thus, we compute linear upper and lower bounds
f_{\mathrm{{lower}}}(x)=ax+b,f_{\mathrm{{upper}}}(x)=cx+d
on the (sliced) profile by approximating

\displaystyle\max_{a,b}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}a(a_{i+1}^{2}-a_{i}^{2})+b(a_{i+1}-a_{i})

\displaystyle\min_{c,d}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}c(a_{i+1}^{2}-a_{i}^{2})+d(a_{i+1}-a_{i})

s.t.

\displaystyle S-aT-b\geq 0\text{ if }T\in[a_{i},a_{i+1}].

s.t.

\displaystyle cT+d-S\geq 0\text{ if }T\in[a_{i},a_{i+1}].

This kind of problem is an instance of the flag algebraic analogue to the
generalized problem of moments
, see for example the dual formulation (equation 4) in the survey
[
17
]
.

We can relax this problem (following the outer approximation in
[
17
]
) to an SDP by replacing the nonnegativity constraint by being an element of the
truncated quadratic module
generated by
T-a_{i}\geq 0
and
a_{i+1}-T\geq 0

M_{L}(T-a_{i},a_{i+1}-T)\coloneqq\left\{s_{0}+(T-a_{i})s_{1}+(a_{i+1}-T)s_{2}\mid s_{0}\in\mathrm{SOS}_{L},\ s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathrm{SOS}_{L-|T|}\right\}.

Note that, by definition, the elements of
M_{L}(T-a_{i},a_{i+1}-T)\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\varnothing}
are nonnegative on sequences of trees whose
T
density lies in
[a_{i},a_{i+1}]
. Optimizing over the quadratic module amounts to solving an SDP. Here
M_{L}(T-a_{i},a_{i+1}-T)
was truncated such that it only contains quantum trees with at most
L
leaves.

What we call level
L
of the outer approximation of the
(T,S)
-profile is the piece-wise linear bound obtained from solving all pairs of SDPs for each slice of the profile:

\displaystyle\max_{a,b}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}a(a_{i+1}^{2}-a_{i}^{2})+b(a_{i+1}-a_{i})

\displaystyle\min_{c,d}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}c(a_{i+1}^{2}-a_{i}^{2})+d(a_{i+1}-a_{i})

s.t.

\displaystyle S-aT-b\in M_{L}(T-a_{i},a_{i+1}-T).

s.t.

\displaystyle cT+d-S\in M_{L}(T-a_{i},a_{i+1}-T).

In all profile approximations pictured in this paper we sliced them into 100 sections.

eight particularly interesting outer approximations of tree-profiles, where several level of the hierarchy are represented. We will now study three of them more thoroughly.

\mathrm{Cat}_{k}

•

6.
Concluding remarks and open problems

Applying the flag algebra theory to rooted binary trees has proved extremely efficient to obtain bounds on the densities and inducibilities of these trees. These very promising results give us many directions in which to continue researching on this topic: we list here the main ones.

Proving open conjectures on the inducibilities of trees.

There are several conjectures about the maximizers of the density of a graph over all graphs of a fixed size. For example, in
[
15
]
, Czabarka et al. made the following conjecture: for every
n\geq k
,
E_{n}
has the largest number of copies of
E_{k}
among all binary trees with
n
leaves. In a similar fashion to what has been done in
[
35
]
, it may be possible to prove these conjectures using flag algebras.
In
[
20
]
, Dossou-Olory and Wagner make the hypothesis that some trees may have an irrational inducibility, following on a question stated in
[
15
]
. As we mentioned in Section
5.1
, they consider the
tree

{forest}

as a possible tree with irrational inducibility. If the inducibility of this tree is indeed irrational, proving it could be possible using our framework, and would answer this open question.

Further investigation on the tree-profiles.

There is still a lot to be discovered about tree-profiles. Our next goal would be to compute the exact description of the tree-profiles in Figure
13
. This means, on the one hand proving Conjecture
1
, and on the other hand determining the lower boundary which seems, as shown before, much more challenging to approach. Studying other tree-profiles in detail would also be of great interest and could lead us to learn more about the behavior and characteristics and the densities of these trees. In particular, we notice in Figure
23
in Appendix
C
a sharp angle present on the
x
-axis at every level of the hierarchy above
1
. This leads us to think that we could obtain an exact Turán number of

{forest}

excluding

{forest}

.

Adapting to other types of trees.

The settings to which this can be extended are numerous:
d
-ary and strictly
d
-ary trees, unrooted trees, colored trees, tree-pairs of phylogenetic trees as in
[
2
]
…
