Cosmological Constraints from Galaxy Clusters and Groups in the eROSITA Final Equatorial Depth Survey 2022 Cosmological Constraints from Galaxy Clusters and Groups in the eROSITA Final Equatorial Depth Survey – 27
I-Non Chiu, Matthias Klein, Joseph Mohr, Sebastian Bocquet

Abstract
We present the first cosmological study of a sample of eROSITA clusters, which were identified in the eROSITA Final Equatorial Depth Survey (eFEDS). In a joint selection on X-ray and optical observables, the sample contains 455 455 clusters within a redshift range of 0.1 < z < 1.2 0.1<z<1.2 , of which 177 177 systems are covered by the public data from the Hyper Suprime-Cam (HSC) survey that enables uniform weak-lensing cluster mass constraints. With minimal assumptions, at each cluster redshift we empirically model (1) the scaling relations between the cluster halo mass and the observables, which include the X-ray count rate, the optical richness, and the weak-lensing mass, and (2) the X-ray selection in terms of the completeness function 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . Using the richness distribution of the clusters, we directly measure the X-ray completeness and adopt those measurements as informative priors for the parameters of 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . In a blinded analysis, we obtain the cosmological constraints Ω m = 0.245 − 0.058 + 0.048 \Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.245^{+0.048}_{-0.058} , σ 8 = 0.833 − 0.063 + 0.075 \sigma_{8}=0.833^{+0.075}_{-0.063} and S 8 ≡ σ 8 ​ ( Ω m / 0.3 ) 0.3 = 0.791 − 0.031 + 0.028 S_{8}\equiv\sigma_{8}\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/0.3\right)^{0.3}=0.791^{+0.028}_{-0.031} in a flat Λ ​ CDM \Lambda\textrm{CDM} cosmology. Extending to a flat w ​ CDM w\textrm{CDM} cosmology leads to the constraint on the equation of state parameter of the dark energy of w = − 1.25 ± 0.47 w=-1.25\pm 0.47 . The eFEDS constraints are in good agreement with the results from the Planck mission, the galaxy-galaxy lensing and clustering analysis of the Dark Energy Survey, and the cluster abundance analysis of the SPT-SZ survey at a level of ≲ 1 ​ σ \lesssim 1\sigma . With the empirical modelling, this work presents the first fully self-consistent cosmological constraints based on a synergy between wide-field X-ray and weak lensing surveys.

Understanding the selection of a cluster sample is of crucial importance in cluster cosmology.
Despite the fact that a large sample of clusters simultaneously spanning a wide range of mass and redshift can be constructed in an optical survey, the modeling of the associated selection function is significantly impacted by projection effects

Conversely, ICM-selected samples are less affected by projection effects and thus provide cleaner datasets for cosmological studies.
The detection of clusters in the SZE, which at frequencies below the SZE null of
220
GHz are identified by the shadow they cast (i.e. decrease in flux) on the cosmic microwave background, is primarily impacted by the primary CMB anisotropy and the statistical noise in the mm-wave sky maps. The SZE signature as extracted using angular filtering with arcminute scale angular resolution
is very sensitive to cluster mass and only weakly dependent on redshift.
Therefore, samples selected from ground-based SZE surveys with arcminute-scale angular resolution
correspond to nearly mass-limited samples where the limiting mass changes only moderately with redshift.
Because the SZE samples tend to probe the most massive and rarest clusters, the resulting cluster cosmology is currently limited by the relatively small cluster samples.

Meanwhile, X-ray surveys with sufficiently deep imaging can construct a cluster sample with a mass range down to the scale of galaxy groups at low redshift and can identify the more massive clusters out to high redshift.
Despite the ICM-based selection, X-ray selected samples suffer from considerable amounts of contamination at a level of

The “empirical” modeling here refers to an approach where we allow the data themselves to constrain the underlying properties, e.g., the scaling law between the observables and the cluster halo mass, with only minimal assumptions informed by prior knowledge from theory, simulations and previous observational studies.
The empirical modeling has advantages over other methods, especially those heavily relying on simulations:
First, the empirically calibrated results are not sensitive to the specific configuration or subgrid physics in simulations that are necessary for the modeling.
For instance, an X-ray selection function directly derived from simulations and the resulting cosmological constraints would be biased if the simulated clusters do not accurately and precisely reflect the observed cluster population.
In fact, this is the case for the eFEDS simulation
(
Liu
et al. 2022b
)
, where the number of synthetic clusters with low fluxes was over-predicted (we return to discuss this in terms of the X-ray scaling relation and the selection function in Section
4.1
and Section
4.2
, respectively).
Second, the modeling is primarily determined by what the data require and therefore fully captures the behavior of the data.
This also allows us to identify and to account for the residual bias or unexpected trends
of the model with respect to the data.
With minimal assumptions and a flexible modeling scheme, the critical and final step in a cosmological analysis is to assess the consistency between the resulting best-fit model and the observed data (e.g., through a goodness-of-fit test), and to incorporate the full systematics into the cosmological constraints (e.g., through a marginalization over so-called nuisance parameters).

In this work, as in
Bocquet
et al. 2019
, the minimal assumptions are made in the empirical modeling, as follows.

•

Informed by the knowledge of cosmic structure formation through observations and numerical simulations, we assume that the relation between the observables and the cluster halo mass at each redshift can be well-described by a power-law function with log-normal intrinsic scatter.
More precisely, the adopted forms of the mass–observable relations encapsulate the freedom to follow the self-similar expectations
(
Kaiser 1986
;
Böhringer et al. 2012
)
for these relations but at the same time allow for deviations from that behavior
(
Mohr & Evrard 1997
;
Mohr
et al. 1999
;
Lin
et al. 2004
;
Chiu et al. 2016a
;
Chiu et al. 2016b
;
Chiu et al. 2018
;
Bulbul
et al. 2019
;
Chiu et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
if preferred by the data. Moreover, we include possible correlated scatter between the observables.

•

We assume that the simulation-calibrated bias in the observed count rate
\eta
and weak-lensing mass
M_{\mathrm{WL}}
can be captured by a mean relation modeled as a power law of the cluster halo mass and redshift with log-normal scatter.
The systematics of this bias have been fully quantified in
Chiu et al. 2022

and are accounted for by a marginalization over the associated parameters.

•

We assume that the completeness at a given observed count rate
\eta
at each redshift due to the X-ray selection of the eFEDS clusters can be described by an error function, which is well motivated and informed by the dedicated simulations
(
Clerc
et al. 2018
)
and what is measured in the eFEDS catalog of point sources
(
Bulbul
et al. 2022
)
.
The systematics of the X-ray selection function are assessed with
the completeness that is independently measured in this work (see Section
4.2.2
).

We use the sample of clusters in the

The X-ray sources are detected using the X-ray imaging within the energy band of

Each X-ray source is characterized by, including but not limited to,

(2) the extent likelihood

(3) the extent

\eta

The parameter

is limited to a maximum value,
\mathtt{EXT}_{\mathrm{up}}=60
arcsec, which is used if

Conversely,

This configuration has a profound effect on the feasibility of utilizing these parameters as the X-ray selection observables, which we return to discuss in Section

After the source detection, the catalog of eFEDS clusters is constructed using the criteria of

We refer the reader to

For each cluster candidate centering at the X-ray center, the MCMF algorithm is employed to deliver

Applying an upper-limit cut in

In this work, we impose the threshold

The photometric redshift of each cluster is adopted as the cluster redshift.
The accuracy of the photometric redshift estimate with respect to the spectroscopic redshift in terms of

2.2
The weak-lensing data and analysis

In this work, we make use of the weak-lensing data from the survey of the Hyper Suprime-Cam (HSC) Subaru Strategy Program
(
Aihara
et al. 2018a
)
.
By design, the eFEDS survey partially overlaps the HSC survey, enabling us to uniformly measure the weak-lensing observable for each cluster and perform the weak-lensing mass calibration.
The weak-lensing mass calibration, as well as various X-ray observable-to-mass-and-redshift relations, was thoroughly and carefully studied in
Chiu et al. 2022
. Here we apply the identical method, but with some changes to data and systematics estimates, which
are summarized below.

We use the public weak-lensing shape catalog from the first-year HSC data
(
Mandelbaum
et al. 2018a
, S16A;)
in the second data release
3
3

3

https://hsc-release.mtk.nao.ac.jp/doc/index.php/s16a-shape-catalog-pdr2/
.
This is the main difference between our analysis here and the previous
Chiu et al. 2022
analysis, which used the latest (and proprietary) shape catalog
(
Li et al. 2022
, S19A;)
.
As a result, only
177
out of
455
eFEDS clusters, corresponding to
\approx 39\%
of the cosmological sample, have weak-lensing measurements.
Roughly
\approx 2
times more clusters are covered by the S19A data, and therefore we expect the statistical power of the weak-lensing mass calibration in this work would decrease by
\approx 1-1/\sqrt{2}\approx 30\%
compared to
Chiu et al. 2022
.

For each cluster, the weak-lensing observable is the individual reduced shear profile
g_{+}
, which is extracted at a range of the projected radius between
\approx 0.5h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}
and
\approx 3.5h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}
around the X-ray center.
Note that we do not use the cluster core (
<0.5~h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}
) in the analysis.
The shear profiles are estimated using background sources that are selected based on the photometric redshift distributions, estimated by the code
DEmp

(
Hsieh &
Yee 2014
)
.
The shape measurement of galaxies is rigorously calibrated against intensive image simulations
(
Mandelbaum
et al. 2018c
)
, delivering the systematic uncertainty of the multiplicative bias at a level of
\lesssim 1.7\%
, sufficiently accurate for cluster lensing.
Various null tests were quantified in
Oguri
et al. 2018b
and suggest no significant residual bias that would impact our analysis.
We stress that the shear profile
g_{+}
is stored in angular radius,
g_{+}\left(\theta\right)
, and is self-consistently compared to the model prediction calculated using the redshift-distance relation given the cosmology in each step of the likelihood exploration.
The lensing covariance matrix, which represents the measurement uncertainty of the observed
g_{+}
, includes not only the shape noise but also the scatter due to the uncorrelated large-scale structure along the line of sight
(
Hoekstra 2003
)
.

With the methodology introduced in
Grandis et al. 2021
, the weak-lensing analysis carefully includes the systematic uncertainties associated with
(1) the calibration of the multiplicative bias,
(2) the bias due to the photometric redshift of background sources, as quantified to be
\lesssim 1\%
and
\lesssim 5\%
at the cluster redshifts
z\lesssim 0.6
and
0.6\lesssim z\lesssim 1.3
, respectively,
(3) the cluster member contamination, which is suggested to be subdominant (
\lesssim 6\%
at a level of
2\sigma
at the cluster core),
(4) the miscentering of the X-ray centers, as described by the fraction
f_{\mathrm{mis}}
of miscentered clusters and the characteristic scale
\sigma_{\mathrm{mis}}
of the miscentering,
(5) the inaccurate assumption about the halo profile due to the presence of correlated large-scale structure or triaxiality,
(6) the radial binning used in the fitting, and importantly
(7) the hydrodynamical effects of baryons.
The overall systematic uncertainty of the weak-lensing analysis is quantified by using the hybrid of large dark-matter-only and hydro Magneticum simulations
4
4

4

http://www.magneticum.org/index.html
that include the lensing systematics mentioned above.
This results in an empirically calibrated relation between the weak-lensing mass
M_{\mathrm{WL}}
and the halo mass
M
as a function of cluster redshift.
The weak-lensing systematic are then marginalized over in the mass calibration analysis (see Section
3.3
) through the calibrated weak-lensing mass-to-mass-and-redshift (
M_{\mathrm{WL}}
–
M
–
z

Note that a similar method of quantifying the weak-lensing systematics was also employed in
Schrabback
et al. 2018
,
Dietrich
et al. 2019
, and
Sommer
et al. 2022
, demonstrating that this is a successful way to empirically calibrate the weak-lensing mass
M_{\mathrm{WL}}
in the context of a cluster cosmology analysis.

In this work, we further improve the characterization of the uncertainty on the weak-lensing systematics, as the second difference to the analysis in
Chiu et al. 2022
.
Specifically, the uncertainty of the weak-lensing mass bias,
b_{\mathrm{WL}}\equiv M_{\mathrm{WL}}/M
, is reduced to
3\%
as opposed to
5.7\%
used in
Chiu et al. 2022
.
Unfortunately, this reduction in the systematics floor leads to a negligible difference in our final results, because the constraints are dominated by the cluster abundance instead of the weak-lensing mass calibration in this work.
We provide more details on the updated weak-lensing systematics in Section
4.1.3
.

In summary, we adopt the same weak-lensing analysis method as in
Chiu et al. 2022
, but there are two differences in the dataset and the systematic estimate.
The first is that we use the public, first-year HSC data, reducing the size of the weak-lensing sample by a factor of
\approx 2
.
The second is that we update the uncertainty on the weak-lensing mass systematics from
5.7\%
to
3\%
. The latter change has a negligible impact on our final results.

3
Likelihoods

In this section, we describe the likelihood used to constrain cosmology using the eFEDS clusters and HSC weak lensing.
The complete derivation of the likelihood has been given in the appendix of
Bocquet
et al. 2015
, which we refer the reader to for more details
(
Benson
et al. 2013
;
Liu et al. 2015
;
de Haan
et al. 2016
, see also)
.
In short, the likelihood compares the number of eFEDS clusters in the multidimensional space of observables with that predicted from our cosmological model, including both the survey selection and the uncertainty in relating the observables to the underlying halo mass.
In this way, constraints on cosmology are obtained.

To carry out cluster cosmology in a survey, we first construct a sample by placing selections on observed quantities, which we call the “selection observables” (e.g., X-ray fluxes in an X-ray survey).
In the hope of improving the constraints by adding more information, we typically obtain additional observations of the clusters, resulting in the so-called “follow-up observables” (e.g., weak-lensing measurements).
Given a set of selection and follow-up observables, denoted as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}
, respectively,
the likelihood of observing the number of clusters with
\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\right\}
at redshift
z
follows a Poisson distribution and can be written as

\ln L(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})=\sum_{i}\ln\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mathrm{d}z}\right)\Bigg|_{i\mathrm{-th~cluster}}\\
-\int\int\int\left[\Theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z)\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mathrm{d}z}\right]\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mathrm{d}z\,,

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}
is the parameter vector that we want to constrain,

i
runs over the observed clusters, and

\Theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z)
is the selection function that only depends on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}
and
z
.
Note that both
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}
can be vectors, in which case we have multiple observables used in the selection and follow-up of the clusters.

By using Bayes’ theorem, the differential number in equation (
1
) can be decomposed into two components,

\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mathrm{d}z}=P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}z}\,,

(2)

where the first factor
P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})
describes the probability of observing
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}
for a cluster selected by the observable
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}
at redshift
z
, and the second factor
\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}z}
is the differential number of clusters in the space of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}
.

With equation (
2
), it is clear that the total number
N_{\mathrm{tot}}
of clusters expected to be detected in the survey, which is the second term on the right-hand side of equation (
1
), can be rewritten as

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{tot}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\int\int\Theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z)\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mathrm{d}z}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mathrm{d}z\,

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\int\int\Theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z)\left[P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}z}\right]\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mathrm{d}z\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\int\Theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z)\left[\int P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\right]\times\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}z}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}\mathrm{d}z\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\int\Theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z)\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}z}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}z\,.

The resulting equation (
3
) means that, because no selection is applied to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}
, the expected total number of observed clusters in the survey does not depend on the follow-up observable, as expected.

Finally, the full likelihood in equation (
1
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle\ln L(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{i}\ln\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}z}\right)\Bigg|_{i\mathrm{-th~cluster}}\right.

(4)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left.\int\int\Theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z)\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}z}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}z\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\ln P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})\Bigg|_{i\mathrm{-th~cluster}}\,,

where the first two terms in the brackets are the “number counts” likelihood for clusters purely selected by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}
;
the final term
P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})
is the so-called “mass calibration” likelihood, which characterizes the probability of obtaining the follow-up observable
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}
for the cluster selected by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}
at redshift
z
.
If the follow-up observations are randomly assigned to the sample selected by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}
(e.g., the coverage of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}
is only subject to the footprint of the survey), the mass calibration likelihood
only need to include the clusters with available
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}
.

We make the following two remarks.
First, the full likelihood described in equation (
4
) is exact.
In this way, the mass information can be extracted directly (e.g., from the weak-lensing observable) in the mass calibration likelihood and is incorporated into the number counts likelihood to constrain cosmology.
Second, the mass calibration likelihood does not depend on the survey selection, as long as no extra selection is applied to the follow-up observable
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}
.
Conversely, if an additional selection is applied to the follow-up observable (e.g., a signal-to-noise cut on the weak-lensing observable), the resulting constraints will be biased
(
Umetsu
et al. 2020
, see intensive validations in Appendix A of)
.

3.1
Applications to eFEDS

In this study, the sample of eFEDS clusters is first selected through a complex selection on the X-ray observables, namely the detection likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
, the extent likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
, and the extent
\mathtt{EXT}
, then followed by a selection on the optical richness
\lambda
(see Section
2.1
for more details).

After the construction of the sample, we follow up each cluster in weak lensing and obtain a shear profile
g_{+}
(\theta)
if the cluster is covered by the HSC footprint.
That is, we have the follow-up observable,
\mathcal{O}=g_{+}(\theta)
.

In this work, we choose the observed count rate
\eta
as the X-ray selection observable.
This is because the observed count rate
\eta
is a more physically intuitive quantity than
\left\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT}\right\}
,
it is closely related to the X-ray luminosity of the cluster, and it has previously been demonstrated to be a reliable mass proxy
(
Chiu et al. 2022
)
.

A complete modeling of the X-ray selection requires the relations between the X-ray observables, namely
\left\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT}\right\}
, and the underlying true quantities, e.g., the cluster halo mass.
Motivated by
Grandis
et al. 2020
,
we empirically model the impact of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
and
\mathtt{EXT}
on the X-ray count rate
\eta
based selection as introducing incompleteness into a purely
\eta
selected sample. The resulting completeness function
\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)
depends on the observed count rate
\eta
and redshift
z
. This modeling choice is a crucial point that we return to in Section
4.2
, where we provide an in-depth discussion of the modeling of the completeness
\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)
.

Therefore, our modeling includes the X-ray and optical selection observables
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}=\left\{\eta,\lambda\right\}
.
Incorporating the completeness function
\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)
, the differential number count in equation (
4
) then reads

\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{d}z}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\eta,\lambda,z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}\lambda\mathrm{d}z}\,.

(5)

Substituting equation (
5
) into equation (
4
), the full likelihood is written as

\displaystyle\ln L(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})=\left[\sum_{i}\ln\left(\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\eta,\lambda,z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}\lambda\mathrm{d}z}\right)\Bigg|_{i\mathrm{-th~cluster}}\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\int\limits_{{z}_{\mathrm{min}}}^{{z}_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}z\int\limits_{\eta=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\eta\int\limits_{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}(z)}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda~\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\eta,\lambda,z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}\lambda\mathrm{d}z}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{j}\ln P(g_{+}(\theta)|\eta,\lambda,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})\Bigg|_{j\mathrm{-th~cluster}}\,,

(6)

where we have employed the selection function
\Theta\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},z\right)
as

\Theta\left(\eta,\lambda,z\right)=\Theta\left(\lambda,z\right)\equiv\mathcal{H}(\lambda-\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(z))\times\\
\mathcal{H}(z-{z}_{\mathrm{min}})\times\left(1-\mathcal{H}(z-{z}_{\mathrm{max}})\right)\,,

(7)

in which
\mathcal{H}
is the heaviside step function,

{z}_{\mathrm{min}}
and
{z}_{\mathrm{max}}
are fixed to
0.1
and
1.2
, as the minimum and maximum redshifts used in this work, and

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(z)
is the redshift-dependent richness threshold that enforces the MCMF filtering criterion of
f_{\mathrm{cont}}<0.3

(
Klein
et al. 2022
)
.
Although the observed count rate
\eta
is used as the X-ray selection observable, we stress that the selection function
\Theta
does not depend on
\eta
.
This is because the X-ray selection in
\eta
has been incorporated into the completeness
\mathcal{C}(\eta,z)
. Therefore, in equation (
3.1
) we
integrate over the full range of
\eta
when accounting for the X-ray selection.

Finally, we note that because
the weak-lensing data are available only for a subset of our clusters that lie within the HSC survey footprint,
the index
j
in equation (
3.1
) runs over only those clusters with available weak-lensing profiles
g_{+}
(\theta)
.

3.2
Number counts likelihood

To evaluate the number counts likelihood, we calculate the differential number of clusters in the observable space as

\frac{\mathrm{d}N(\eta,\lambda,z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}\lambda\mathrm{d}z}=\int\mathrm{d}M~P\left(\eta,\lambda|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}N\left(M,z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)}{\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}z}\,,

(8)

in which
\frac{\mathrm{d}N\left(M,z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)}{\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}z}
is related to the differential comoving volume
V_{\mathrm{c}}\left(z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)
at redshift
z
per solid angle and the survey solid angle
\Omega_{\mathrm{survey}}
as

\frac{\mathrm{d}N\left(M,z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)}{\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}z}=\frac{\mathrm{d}n\left(M,z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)}{\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}z}\times V_{\mathrm{c}}\left(z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)\times\Omega_{\mathrm{survey}}\,,

(9)

where
\frac{\mathrm{d}n\left(M,z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)}{\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}z}
is the halo mass function following the dark-matter-only formula in
Bocquet
et al. 2016
,
and
P\left(\eta,\lambda|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)
is the joint distribution of
\eta
and
\lambda
given the cluster halo mass
M
and redshift
z
.
We note that the halo mass function is expressed as

\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}z}=f\left(\sigma,z\right)\frac{\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{m}}}{M}\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\sigma^{-1}}{\mathrm{d}M}\,,

where
\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{m}}
is the mean comoving matter density,
\sigma
is the root mean square of the density field smoothed by a kernel with a comoving scale of
R_{\mathrm{sm}}=\left(\frac{3M}{4\pi\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{m}}}\right)^{1/3}
, and the function
f\left(\sigma,z\right)
is calibrated against the simulation
(
Bocquet
et al. 2016
, see equation (4) in)
.
The cosmological dependence of the halo mass function is largely encoded in the terms
\sigma
and
\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{m}}
, while the shape of
\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}z}
is nearly universal across a wide range of redshifts and cosmologies.

The joint distribution
P\left(\eta,\lambda|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)
includes two components: the uncertainty in observable due to the measurement noise, and the intrinsic scatter of the observables at a fixed cluster mass and redshift.
Specifically, this can be written as

P\left(\eta,\lambda|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)=\int\mathrm{d}\hat{\eta}\int\mathrm{d}\hat{\lambda}~P\left(\eta|\hat{\eta},z\right)P\left(\lambda|\hat{\lambda}\right)P\left(\hat{\eta},\hat{\lambda}|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)\,,

(10)

where
\hat{\eta}
and
\hat{\lambda}
are the intrinsic observables of the count rate and richness, respectively,

P\left(\hat{\eta},\hat{\lambda}|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)
characterizes the intrinsic scatter of the observables,
and
P\left(\eta|\hat{\eta},z\right)
(
P\left(\lambda|\hat{\lambda}\right)
) describes the dispersion of observed count rate (richness) with respect to the intrinsic observable
\hat{\eta}
(
\hat{\lambda}
) due to the measurement uncertainty.
Note that we additionally include a redshift dependence in characterizing the measurement uncertainty of the count rate,
P\left(\eta|\hat{\eta},z\right)
.
This redshift dependence is suggested and empirically calibrated by the data, as we describe in more detail in Appendix
A
.

We assume a bivariate log-normal distribution for
P\left(\hat{\eta},\hat{\lambda}|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)
around the mean value of

\left(\left\langle\ln\hat{\eta}|M,z\right\rangle,\left\langle\ln\hat{\lambda}|M,z\right\rangle\right)

at a given cluster mass and redshift, characterized by the intrinsic scatter covariance matrix

\Sigma_{\eta,\lambda}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\sigma_{\eta}}^{2}&\sigma_{\eta}~\sigma_{\lambda}~\rho_{\eta,\lambda}\\
\sigma_{\eta}~\sigma_{\lambda}~\rho_{\eta,\lambda}&{\sigma_{\lambda}}^{2}\\
\end{array}\right)\,,

(11)

where
\rho_{\eta,\lambda}
is the correlation coefficient between the count rate and richness.
Note that the variables in equation (
11
) denote the scatter of and the correlation between the intrinsic quantities (
\hat{\eta}
and
\hat{\lambda}
), despite that the notations of the observed quantities (
\eta
and
\lambda
) are used in the subscripts for simplicity.
The mean values of the count rate and richness,
\left\langle\ln\hat{\eta}|M,z\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\ln\hat{\lambda}|M,z\right\rangle
, at a given the mass and redshift are determined by the scaling relations given in Sections
4.1.1
and
4.1.2
, respectively.

We model the distribution of the observed richness due to the measurement uncertainty as a Poisson distribution in the Gaussian limit.
That is,

P\left(\lambda|\hat{\lambda}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\delta_{\lambda}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\lambda-\hat{\lambda}\right)^{2}}{{2\delta_{\lambda}}^{2}}\right)\,,

(12)

where
\delta_{\lambda}=1.1\times\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}}
, in which we increase the measurement uncertainty by a factor of
1.1
.
We obtain this factor by fitting the parameter
a
in the functional form of
\delta_{\lambda}=a\times\sqrt{\lambda}
between the observed richness
\lambda
and its observed uncertainty
\delta_{\lambda}
for the eFEDS clusters.

Meanwhile, the distribution of the observed count rate at a given intrinsic rate is modeled as a Gaussian,

P\left(\eta|\hat{\eta},z\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\delta_{\eta}\left(\eta,z\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\eta-\hat{\eta}\right)^{2}}{2{\delta_{\eta}\left(\eta,z\right)}^{2}}\right)\,,

(13)

where we empirically characterize the measurement uncertainty
\delta_{\eta}\left(\eta,z\right)
as a function of the “observed”
\eta
and
z
based on the fitting results of the eFEDS clusters
(
Klein
et al. 2019
;
Grandis
et al. 2020
, see also)
.
We refer the reader to Appendix
A
for more discussions of
\delta_{\eta}\left(\eta,z\right)
.

3.3
Mass calibration likelihood

We evaluate the mass calibration likelihood in the same manner as in
Chiu et al. 2022
, where we refer the reader for a complete description.
In what follows, we provide a brief summary.

For each cluster that is covered by the HSC footprint, we have a shear profile
g_{+}
(\theta)
, as the follow-up observable, and the two selection observables, the observed count rate
\eta
and richness
\lambda
.
The goal is to calculate the probability of observing
g_{+}
(\theta)
for the cluster at redshift
z
given the selection observables, i.e., the last term in equation (
3.1
).
In this work, as well as in
Chiu et al. 2022
, we approximate this probability as

\displaystyle P(g_{+}(\theta)|\eta,\lambda,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{P(g_{+}(\theta),\eta,\lambda,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{P(\eta,\lambda,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}\,

(14)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}\left(\eta,z\right)P(g_{+}(\theta),\eta,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}\left(\eta,z\right)P(\eta,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P(g_{+}(\theta),\eta,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}{P(\eta,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})}\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(g_{+}(\theta)|\eta,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})\,,

where
\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}\left(\eta,z\right)
is the completeness of the cluster at a given
\eta
and redshift
z
in the presence of the richness selection, and is cancelled out in both numerator and denominator.
Physically, it means that the richness selection does not introduce any significant additional selection on the weak-lensing observables, and there the Malmquist bias due to the richness cut is negligible.
That is,
\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}
does not have a dependence on
g_{+}
.
It is a reasonable assumption, because the richness selection in this work is conservatively chosen to only remove the contamination (i.e., the false-positive detection) in the purely X-ray selected sample based on the observed richness (see Section
2.1
).
Thus, the overall selection of a contamination-free sample can be described by the X-ray selection, to first order.

It is straightforward to see
\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}\left(\eta,z\right)=\int_{\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(z)}\mathrm{d}\lambda P(\lambda|\eta,z)
, where the interval
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(z)
defines the selection of the richness at a given redshift
z
.
To have the completeness factors cancelled out in equation (
14
), we have implicitly made an assumption of

P(\lambda|\eta,g_{+}(\theta),z)\approx P(\lambda|\eta,z)
, such that

\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}\left(\eta,g_{+}(\theta),z\right)=\int_{\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(z)}\mathrm{d}\lambda P(\lambda|\eta,g_{+}(\theta),z)\approx\int_{\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(z)}\mathrm{d}\lambda P(\lambda|\eta,z)=\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}\left(\eta,z\right)
.
Equivalently, this assumes that the intrinsic correlation between the weak-lensing mass
M_{\mathrm{WL}}
and richness
\lambda
is subdominant, i.e.,
\rho_{\lambda,\mathrm{WL}}\approx 0
.

It is worth mentioning that great efforts have been made in studying the projection effect of optically selected clusters, mostly based on simulations
(
Song et al. 2012
;
Sunayama
et al. 2020
;
Zhang &
Annis 2022
;
Wu et al. 2022
, e.g.)
but also with observations
(
Sunayama 2022
;
Zu et al. 2022
)
.
The projection is expected to have a major impact on optically selected clusters, because it simultaneously affects and entangles the weak-lensing
signals and the richness selection at a fixed cluster mass.
However, this is not the case in this study, where the main selection of eFEDS clusters relies on the X-ray observables instead of the optical richness that acts as an auxiliary observable, which is effectively used only to clean the eFEDS sample.
The assessment of the projection effect based on observed clusters is subject to various systematics
(
Murata et al. 2020
, such as the cluster detection algorithm, e.g.,)

and thus remains quantitatively inconclusive.
Moreover, a recent cosmological analyses
(
Costanzi
et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
found that the posterior of the
correlated scatter
\rho_{\lambda,\mathrm{WL}}
between the weak-lensing mass and optical richness is statistically consistent with zero
and has no significant impact on the final results.
Therefore, the approximation of
\rho_{\lambda,\mathrm{WL}}=0
is expected to be a subdominant effect in this work.
We defer the inclusion of the possible correlated scatter
\rho_{\lambda,\mathrm{WL}}
to a future work.

Equation (
14
) is then evaluated as

P(g_{+}(\theta)|\eta,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})=\frac{\int\mathrm{d}MP(g_{+}(\theta),\eta|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})\frac{\mathrm{d}N\left(M,z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)}{\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}z}}{\int\mathrm{d}MP(\eta|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})\frac{\mathrm{d}N\left(M,z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\right)}{\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}z}}\,,

(15)

where
P(\eta|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})
(
P(g_{+}(\theta),\eta|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})
) is the probability of observing
\eta
(
g_{+}
(\theta)
and
\eta
) given the cluster mass and redshift.
Note that the inclusion of the halo mass function in equation (
15
) is needed to account for the Eddington bias raised from the
steeply falling distribution of the underlying halo mass.

We calculate the probability
P(\eta|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})
as

P(\eta|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})=\int\mathrm{d}\hat{\eta}\,P(\eta|\hat{\eta},z)P(\hat{\eta}|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}),

(16)

in which the first factor
P(\eta|\hat{\eta},z)
describes the distribution of observed
\eta
at a given intrinsic count rate
\hat{\eta}
due to the measurement uncertainty, following equation (
13
),
and the second factor
P(\hat{\eta}|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})
characterizes the distribution of the intrinsic rate
\hat{\eta}
at the given mass and redshift. The intrinsic rate
\hat{\eta}
at a given mass and redshift is assumed to exhibit an intrinsic scatter, which we model to be log-normal

P(\hat{\eta}|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{\eta}\hat{\eta}}\exp\left(\frac{\left(\ln\hat{\eta}-\left\langle\ln\hat{\eta}|M,z\right\rangle\right)^{2}}{2{\sigma_{\eta}}^{2}}\right)\,,

(17)

where
\left\langle\ln\hat{\eta}|M,z\right\rangle
is the mean count rate at the given mass and redshift predicted with the parameters

The broken power-law mass scaling

In what follows, we introduce one novel aspect of this work regarding the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation.
It is known that there exists a deficit of the cluster population with low extent likelihoods (
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}\lesssim 10
) compared to that predicted by the eFEDS simulations
(
Liu
et al. 2022b
)
.
Specifically, the distribution of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
in the eFEDS catalog shows a peak at
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}\approx 10
and decays at
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}\lesssim 10
, which is distinctly different from the simulations that show a power-law distribution of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
without a turnover.
This discrepancy cannot be explained by the systematics due to the
eROSITA
detection pipeline, because the same end-to-end processing has been employed on both the eFEDS observations and the simulations.
This suggests that either the synthetic clusters that were injected into the simulations are different from those of observed in eFEDS—perhaps due to the recipes used to generate the X-ray emissions of synthetic clusters being inaccurate.
In such a case, the X-ray selection (or the completeness as defined in Section
4.2
) quantified from the simulations would not be expected to accurately reflect the true selection of the observed sample. Such an error in the selection model would generally result in biased posteriors on cosmological parameters.

On possible explanation for the deficit seen at the low-
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
end could come from the different impact of, e.g., feedback processes in the low-mass systems relative to the high-mass clusters.
It is important to note that there exists a scaling between
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}

(
Klein
et al. 2022
, see Figure 16 in)
, in which
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
again strongly scales with the observed count rate
\eta
;
this suggests that the missing population of low-
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
clusters could be dominated by those with low
\eta
, which would mostly be low-mass clusters with
M\lesssim 10^{14}h^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
at
z\lesssim 0.4
given the nature of an X-ray selected sample.
Such a deficit of clusters at low-
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
could occur if the mass scaling of the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation at the low-mass end is steeper than the mass scaling exhibited by more massive clusters.
This is also physically intuitive: halos at the group mass scale have shallower gravitational potential wells, and therefore the ICM is more easily expelled from the potential wells due to energetic feedback within the virial regions or even an early preheating due to AGNs and star formation activities prior to the cluster formation
(see, e.g.,
Mohr & Evrard 1997
, as this relates to the overall mass trend)
.
This would result in a smaller observed flux per halo mass for groups than massive clusters.
In the eFEDS simulations
(
Liu
et al. 2022b
)
, the low-mass clusters were injected into the X-ray imaging as though they are scaled-down versions of massive clusters
(
Comparat
et al. 2020
, see Section 3.1.1 in)
, hence the population of synthetic galaxy groups might be neither complete nor accurately capturing the changing impact of ICM physics at the lowest mass scales.
Motivated by this, we introduce a broken power-law feature at the group scale as

\left\langle\ln\left(\frac{\eta}{\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}}\Bigg|M,z\right)\right\rangle\Bigg|_{\mathrm{broken}}=\left\langle\ln\left(\frac{\eta}{\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}}\Bigg|M,z\right)\right\rangle+\\
\left(\left(B_{\eta}-B_{\mathrm{grp}}\right)\times\Delta\right)\times\ln\left(1+\left(\frac{M}{M_{\mathrm{grp}}}\right)^{\frac{-1}{\Delta}}\right)\,,

(23)

where the bracket on the right side follows the single power-law
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation as defined in equation (
19
),

B_{\mathrm{grp}}
is the index of the “broken” power law at the mass range of
M\lesssim M_{\mathrm{grp}}\equiv 10^{14}h^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
,
and the term
\Delta
is the smoothing factor which is fixed as
\Delta\equiv\ln\left(\sqrt{M_{\mathrm{piv}}/M_{\mathrm{grp}}}\right)
.
In such a parameterization, the power-law index of the mass scaling remains intact as
B_{\eta}
at
M\gtrsim M_{\mathrm{grp}}
and becomes
B_{\mathrm{grp}}
if
M\lesssim M_{\mathrm{grp}}
.
The case of
B_{\mathrm{grp}}>B_{\eta}
implies a scenario where the X-ray luminosity of groups is less than what would have been expected given the behavior of the more massive clusters.
Such a trend of a steepening mass scaling could explain the over-predicted number of the simulated clusters at the low-
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
end.

It is worth mentioning that few investigations have been made to study the broken power-law feature in terms of the X-ray luminosity-to-mass relation
(
Lovisari et al. 2015
;
Barnes
et al. 2017
;
Schellenberger & Reiprich 2017
;
Pop et al. 2022
)
.
With the unique and relatively large eFEDS sample, which probes over two orders of magnitude in cluster mass, the joint analysis of the weak-lensing mass calibration and the cluster abundance provides a good opportunity to observationally examine the assumption of a single power-law relation in mass.
This novel characterization of the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation is incorporated into our blinded analysis, where we ask how sensitive the blinded posteriors are to the choice of single versus broken power law (see Section
4.4
for more details).

4.2
Modeling of the X-ray selection

4.2.1
Theoretical framework

We denote
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}
as the X-ray observables that are actually used in selecting the eFEDS clusters,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}\equiv\left\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT}\right\}
.
Then, the total number of clusters that can be detected in the survey is

N_{\mathrm{tot}}=\int\mathrm{d}z\int\limits_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}\mathrm{d}z}\,,

(29)

where the interval
\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}}
defines the observable space of the X-ray selection, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}}=\left\{\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT}\right)|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}>5,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}>6,\mathtt{EXT}>0\right\}
, and the differential number of clusters reads

\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}\mathrm{d}z}=\int\mathrm{d}M\left[P\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}|M,z\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}N\left(M,z\right)}{\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}z}\right]\,.

(30)

As seen in equation (
30
), one needs to calculate
P\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}|M,z\right)
that requires
the modeling of the
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}
–
M
–
z
relation, which describes the relation between the X-ray observables and the underlying halo mass at each redshift.
A challenge is that none of these selection observables, which have been applied to define the eFEDS sample, have a well established connection to the cluster halo mass and redshift
(
Pacaud
et al. 2006
, see, e.g.,)
. Of course one can attempt to use image simulations to characterize these relations, but then the accuracy of the resulting selection function will only be good in the limit that the simulated and real cluster properties are approximately identical. While this is a worthy goal over the long term, here we proceed using an empirical calibration of the X-ray selection that uses the observed sample characteristics to inform our selection model. We describe this below.

Following previous X-ray, SZE and optical cluster forecasts and analyses
(
Haiman
et al. 2001
;
Vikhlinin
et al. 2009b
;
Vanderlinde
et al. 2010
;
Rykoff
et al. 2014
;
Bocquet
et al. 2019
;
Klein
et al. 2019
;
Grandis
et al. 2020
;
Chiu et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
, we adopt a cluster observable for our eFEDS analysis—the X-ray count rate
\eta
—that has a well defined relation with cluster mass and redshift. This observable is the
eROSITA
photon based flux of a cluster and is therefore reflecting the cluster luminosity distance, X-ray spectral energy distribution (SED) and X-ray luminosity. The X-ray SED variation is driven by ICM temperature, which along with X-ray luminosity closely tracks mass and redshift. In particular, the X-ray luminosity has been extensively studied and is known to provide a cluster mass proxy that is comparable in scatter properties to SZE signal-to-noise ratios and optical richness. Finally, the eFEDS rate-to-mass-to-redshift relation has been previously studied
(
Chiu et al. 2022
)
and shown to be a high-quality mass proxy.

Given this choice, we can use Bayes’ theorem, we then have

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt\int\limits_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}|M,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}\left[\int\limits\mathrm{d}\eta P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}|\eta,M,z)P(\eta|M,z)\right]\,

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits\mathrm{d}\eta\left[\int\limits_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}|\eta,M,z)\right]P(\eta|M,z)\,.

Thus, the modeling of the X-ray selection is then equivalent to understanding the impact of the
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}
-selection on a count rate selected cluster sample.
In this work, we make an assumption that the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}
can be empirically described the observed count rate
\eta
at the cluster redshift
z
without residual dependence on the cluster mass
M
, i.e.,

P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}|\eta,M,z)\approx P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}|\eta,z)\,.

(32)

Physically, equation (
32
) is a statement that the cluster halo mass can be represented by the count rate
\eta
at a particular redshift. This in effect is just a restatement of equation (
19
).
Indeed, the two most important factors affecting whether a cluster is selected in X-rays are
(1) the total flux, which determines
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
and
\eta
and has a direct relation with the X-ray luminosity and luminosity distance
D_{\mathrm{L}}
and hence the mass and redshift, and
(2) the angular scale of the cluster, or the extendedness, which directly determines
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
and
\mathtt{EXT}
and has a dependence on the cluster mass as well as the angular diameter distance
D_{\mathrm{A}}
and hence the redshift
z
.

We therefore model the completeness of
\eta
in the presence of the
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}
-selection,

\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)\equiv\int\limits_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}|\eta,z)\,.

(33)

Substituting equations (
30
)–(
33
) into equation (
29
), the total number of the clusters is evaluated as

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{tot}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits\mathrm{d}z\int\limits\mathrm{d}M\int\limits\mathrm{d}\eta\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)P\left(\eta|M,z\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}N\left(M,z\right)}{\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}z}\,

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits\mathrm{d}z\int\limits\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)\left[\int\limits\mathrm{d}MP\left(\eta|M,z\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}N\left(M,z\right)}{\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}z}\right]\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits\mathrm{d}z\int\limits\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}z}\,,

which results in the modeling in equation (
5
).
It is seen in equation (
34
) that the modeling of the X-ray selection has become a task to characterize the completeness
\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)
.

Motivated by
Clerc
et al. 2018
, where they modeled the completeness as a function of the flux, the angular scale of core radii, and the exposure time,
we model the completeness function
\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)
as

\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\erf\left(\frac{\ln\eta-\ln\eta_{50,z}}{s_{\eta}}\right)\right)\,,

(35)

where
\erf
is the error function with the scaling factor
s_{\eta}
, and
\eta_{50,z}
is the count rate which has
50\%
completeness at the redshift
z
, characterized as

\eta_{50,z}=\eta_{50}\times\left(\frac{D_{\mathrm{A}}(z)}{D_{\mathrm{A}}(z_{\mathrm{piv}})}\right)^{\gamma_{z}}\,,

(36)

in which
\eta_{50}
is the count rate with
50\%
completeness at the pivot redshift
z_{\mathrm{piv}}=0.35
,
and
\gamma_{z}
is the scaling index of the angular diameter distance dependence.
We ignore the dependence on the exposure time in this work, given that the imaging depth is uniform in the eFEDS survey
(
Liu et al. 2022a
, see Section
2.1
and also)
.
The inclusion of an exposure-time dependence will likely be helpful in the modeling of the
eROSITA
All-Sky Survey sample at some future date.

Note that
Bulbul
et al. 2022
recently derived the completeness of the eFEDS clusters in terms of the count rate
by analyzing the population of mis-identified clusters in the point-source catalog
(
Salvato
et al. 2022
)
and the simulations
(
Liu
et al. 2022b
)
, showing a similar functional form as in equation (
35
). However, we stress that the completeness presented in
Bulbul
et al. 2022
cannot be directly utilized in a cosmological analysis.
This is because the observed count rate of an eFEDS cluster mis-identified as a point source is estimated in the point-source mode (specifically with
\mathtt{EXT}=0
) of the
eROSITA
pipeline, which is significantly different from that used for an extended source, which is based upon a
\beta
-modeling fitting.
That is, the observed count rate of a mis-identified cluster is estimated within an aperture largely following the point spread function of
eROSITA
instead of the cluster X-ray surface brightness profile.
As a result, the observed count rate of the population of the mis-identified clusters is expected to have a systematic offset with respect to that would have been measured for an extended source.
Hence, the completeness derived based on a mix of these two kinds of count rates could merely serve as a qualitative gauge of the X-ray selection but not as a sufficiently accurate modeling to support a cosmological analysis.
In the next section, we present the direct measurement of the X-ray completeness.

4.2.2
Measurements of the completeness

Measurements in the eFEDS data

Our goal is to quantify the completeness of the eFEDS sample in terms of the observed count rate
\eta
.
We do this by leveraging the fact that the eFEDS clusters missing in the extended-source catalog are detected in the point-source catalog.
In other words, the joint sample of the clusters in the extended-source and point-source catalogs is complete down to a threshold in the count rate.
Therefore, we can derive the completeness function as the ratio of the number of the eFEDS clusters in the extended-source catalog to that of the total number of clusters in the joint catalog.

In Section
4.2.1
, we have stressed that the estimated count rate of the clusters mis-identified in the point-source catalog cannot be directly compared with those in the extended-source catalog, due to the fact that different schemes are used to estimate the count rate for these two populations.
This prevents us from directly using the count rate estimated in the point-source catalog.
However, the optical richness
\lambda
of the clusters in both the extended-source and point-source catalogs is consistently estimated with the MCMF algorithm
(
Klein
et al. 2022
)
.
This uniform estimation of the richness enables us to quantify the X-ray completeness by jointly studying the richness distribution of the cluster populations in both the extended-source and point-source catalogs.

The procedure to measure the completeness is described, as follows.

1.

We first characterize the relation between the observed richness
\lambda
and count rate
\eta
at the cluster redshift
z
based on the sample of eFEDS clusters that are correctly identified in the extended-source catalog.
This results in the richness-to-rate-and-redshift (
\lambda
–
\eta
–
z
) relation.

2.

We derive the richness distributions of MCMF counterparts around all sources in the point-source catalog in seven redshift bins.
We then repeat the same analysis along random lines of sight to obtain the random-LOS richness distribution, representing the sample contamination, which is also employed for the optical confirmation of the eFEDS candidates
(
Klein
et al. 2022
, see Section
2.1
and also)
.

3.

With a proper normalization, we subtract the random-LOS richness distribution
from that of the MCMF counterparts around the eFEDS point sources.
The residual richness distribution, denoted as
N_{\mathrm{res}}(\lambda,z)
, represents the population of eFEDS clusters that are mis-identified in the point-source catalog.

4.

We convert the residual richness distribution
N_{\mathrm{res}}(\lambda,z)
to the corresponding distribution of the count rate, denoted as
N_{\mathrm{ps}}^{\mathrm{\prime}}(\eta,z)
,
using the
\lambda
–
\eta
–
z
relation described in step (i) above.
In this step, we make the assumption that the clusters in both the extended-source and point-source catalogs represent the same population in terms of their optical properties.
This assumption is validated by the eFEDS data, because the richness distributions of these two populations are consistent with each other
(
Klein
et al. 2022
;
Bulbul
et al. 2022
)
.

5.

The resulting distribution
N_{\mathrm{ps}}^{\mathrm{\prime}}(\eta,z)
cannot be directly compared to the count rate distribution estimated from the clusters in the extended-source catalog, because the distribution
N_{\mathrm{ps}}^{\mathrm{\prime}}(\eta,z)
is expressed in the count rate that is obtained by directly inverting the observed richness through the
\lambda
–
\eta
–
z
relation.
That is, the distribution
N_{\mathrm{ps}}^{\mathrm{\prime}}(\eta,z)
is impacted by the scatter in the richness at a fixed count rate.
We remove this scatter by deconvolving
N_{\mathrm{ps}}^{\mathrm{\prime}}(\eta,z)
by a kernel that is calibrated against the cluster sample in the extended-source catalog (see more details below).
This results in the “richness-inferred” count rate distribution
N_{\mathrm{ps}}(\eta,z)
of the clusters that are mis-identified in the point-source catalog.
We stress that the
distribution

N_{\mathrm{ps}}(\eta,z)

is expressed in terms of the observed count rate
\eta
that could have been estimated in the extended-source mode of the
eROSITA
pipeline, because the
\lambda
–
\eta
–
z
relation is derived based on the sample in the extended-source catalog.

6.

We account for the over sampling of the eFEDS field by X-ray sources.
Due to the high density of X-ray sources in eFEDS and the typical size of clusters (which have a radius of
R_{500\mathrm{c}}\approx 3
arcmin), it is possible that the same cluster or group contributes multiple times to the residual richness distribution and hence in
N_{\mathrm{ps}}(\eta,z)
.
In
Salvato
et al. 2022
, they accounted for this effect by only selecting those X-ray sources as clusters that have the closest positional match to the optical center of the MCMF counterparts.
This reduces the sample size by
\approx 25\%
.
We therefore re-scale
N_{\mathrm{ps}}(\eta,z)
by
0.75
, i.e.,
N_{\mathrm{ps}}(\eta,z)\rightarrow 0.75\times N_{\mathrm{ps}}(\eta,z)
.
Without including the factor, the best-fit parameters of the resulting completeness change by less than
1\sigma
.
Because the posteriors are adopted as the priors on the parameters of the completeness that will be self-calibrated in the analysis, this oversampling correction does not have significant impact on our final results.
Moreover, we stress that the resulting completeness, which characterizes the X-ray selection, only affects the modeling of the cluster abundance, and does not enter into the weak-lensing mass calibration.

7.

By combining with the count rate distribution
N_{\mathrm{clu}}(\eta,z)
of the eFEDS clusters that are correctly identified in the extended-source catalog, we derive the completeness as

\mathcal{C}(\eta,z)=\frac{N_{\mathrm{clu}}(\eta,z)}{N_{\mathrm{clu}}(\eta,z)+N_{\mathrm{ps}}(\eta,z)}\,.

(37)

In what follows, we provide details of the modeling of the richness-to-rate-and-redshift relation and the deconvolution procedure used in the step (v).

In concordance with the richness-to-mass-and-redshift (
\lambda
–
M
–
z
) and the count rate-to-mass-and-redshift (
\eta
–
M
-
z
) relations, the
\lambda
–
\eta
–
z
relation is obtained by modeling
P(\lambda|\eta,z)
as a log-normal distribution with intrinsic scatter
\sigma_{\lambda|\eta,z}
around the mean richness characterized as a power-law function of
\eta
and
(1+z)
.
Specifically, we maximize the likelihood

\mathrm{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\lambda|\eta})=\prod\limits_{i}\frac{P(\lambda_{i}|\eta_{i},z_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\lambda|\eta})}{P(\lambda>\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(z)|\eta_{i},z_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\lambda|\eta})}\,,

(38)

where
i
runs over the eFEDS clusters,

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\lambda|\eta}
records the parameters of the
\lambda
–
\eta
–
z
relation, and

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(z)
is the richness threshold due to the cut of
f_{\mathrm{cont}}<0.3
(see Section
2.1
).
The inclusion of the denominator in equation (
38
) is needed to account for the Malmquist bias caused by the redshift-dependent richness cut.
The resulting
\lambda
–
\eta
–
z
relation is then obtained as

\left\langle\ln\left(\frac{\lambda}{\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}}\Bigg|\eta_{\mathrm{corr}},z\right)\right\rangle=\ln\left(21.81\pm 0.66\right)+\\
\left(0.68\pm 0.03\right)\times\ln\left(\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{corr}}}{0.1~\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}}\right)+\\
\left(-0.70\pm 0.27\right)\times\ln\left(\frac{1+z}{1+z_{\mathrm{piv}}}\right)\,,

(39)

with intrinsic scatter of
\sigma_{\lambda|\eta,z}=0.45\pm 0.02
and the normalized count rate
\eta_{\mathrm{corr}}
at
z_{\mathrm{piv}}=0.35
defined as

\ln\left(\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{corr}}}{\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}}\right)\equiv\ln\left(\frac{\eta}{\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}}\right)-2\ln\left(\frac{E(z)}{E(z_{\mathrm{piv}})}\right)\\
+2\ln\left(\frac{D_{\mathrm{L}}(z)}{D_{\mathrm{L}}(z_{\mathrm{piv}})}\right)-\gamma_{\mathrm{f}}\ln\left(\frac{z}{z_{\mathrm{piv}}}\right)\,,

(40)

where we fix
\gamma_{\mathrm{f}}=0.42
(see Section
4.1.1
).
This result is visualized in Figure
2
, where we can see that the resulting
\lambda
–
\eta
–
z
relation provides a good description of the data.
It is worth mentioning that the parameterization of equation (
39
)
assumes the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation
is described by a single power-law scaling.
Indeed, as seen in Figure
2
, no significant broken power-law feature is revealed in the scaling between
\lambda
and
\eta
.

With the
\lambda
–
\eta
–
z
relation, we perform the deconvolution described in step (v).
Specifically, the distribution
N_{\mathrm{ps}}^{\mathrm{\prime}}(\eta,z)
is deconvolved using a
log-normal kernel
that remains to be derived.
We derive this log-normal kernel by
using the sample of clusters detected in the extended-source catalog, under the assumption that the populations of the clusters detected in the extended-source and point-source catalogs follow a consistent
\lambda
–
\eta
–
z
relation with the count rate
\eta
estimated in the extended mode of the
eROSITA
pipeline.
To do so, we follow the same procedure described in the step (iv) to derive the count rate distribution, denoted as
N_{\mathrm{clu}}^{\prime}(\eta,z)
, of the extended sample using the count rate directly converted through the
\lambda
–
\eta
–
z
relation.
With a fitting procedure, we derive
the kernel by requiring that the convolution of the kernel and the distribution
N_{\mathrm{clu}}^{\prime}(\eta,z)
results in the count rate distribution
N_{\mathrm{clu}}(\eta,z)
, which is derived using the count rate estimated in the “extended-source" mode.
After obtaining
the kernel, we perform the deconvolution of the distribution
N_{\mathrm{ps}}^{\prime}(\eta,z)
to obtain the distribution
N_{\mathrm{ps}}(\eta,z)
for the cluster sample detected in the point-source catalog.
Note that the scatter parameter of the resulting log-normal kernel is slightly larger than the value of
\sigma_{\lambda|\eta,z}/0.68
, where
\sigma_{\lambda|\eta,z}
is the measured intrinsic scatter of the richness at a fixed count rate and redshift, and the factor
0.68
is the best-fit count rate scaling of the richness in equation (
39
).
This is expected, because the dispersion in
\lambda
at a given
\eta
contains both contributions from the measurement uncertainty and the intrinsic scatter.
By calibrating using the cluster sample in the extended-source catalog, this deconvolution process ensures that (1) we take into account not only the measurement uncertainty but also the intrinsic scatter of
\lambda
at a given count rate, and that (2) the resulting
N_{\mathrm{ps}}(\eta,z)
of the clusters mis-identified in the point-source catalog is unbiased with respect to that which could have been estimated using the count rate estimate from the extended-source mode of the
eROSITA
pipeline.

We find no compelling evidence for a redshift dependence in the completeness measurements
\mathcal{C}
inferred from the richness distribution.
We therefore derive the richness-inferred completeness without any redshift binning, as the final estimate.
That is, the completeness is effectively derived at the pivotal redshift of the sample, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z_{\mathrm{piv}}\right)
, where
z_{\mathrm{piv}}=0.35
.
In Figure
3
, we show the resulting
\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z_{\mathrm{piv}}\right)
as the black points with the
68\%
confidence level indicated by the open interval.
The blue vertical band indicates the count rate where the completeness is
50\%
, constrained by the joint modeling of the weak-lensing mass calibration and the cluster abundance (see Section
5.3.4
).
As seen, the results show that the richness-inferred completeness is
\approx 50\%
at
\eta\approx 0.06

\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}
, in excellent agreement with that from the joint modeling, and decreases to zero at
\eta\lesssim 0.02

\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}
.
We note that there exists a mildly decreasing completeness at the high-
\eta
end (
\eta\gtrsim 0.3

\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}
).
In this work, we are not able to further explore the cause of this trend at such a high count rate, because more precise measurements of
\mathcal{C}
are limited by the large Poisson noise.
A significantly larger sample from the first-year
eROSITA
survey will shed further light on this.

After deriving the completeness, we fit the functional form of equation (
35
) to the measured completeness (equation (
37
)) and obtain

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{\eta_{50}}{\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}}&=&0.0624\pm 0.0057\,,\\
s_{\eta}&=&0.6514\pm 0.1687\,.\end{array}

(41)

Note that we fix
\gamma_{z}
to be zero in deriving equation (
41
), given that we do not see a significant difference
in the measured completeness in different redshift bins.
That is, the best-fit results in equation (
41
) represent the overall completeness of the eFEDS clusters at
all redshifts.
The parameter measurements in equation (
41
) are used as priors on the parameters of
\left(\eta_{50},s_{\eta}\right)
in the number counts likelihood (see Section
3
) to empirically account for the X-ray selection.
Although equation (
41
) is obtained with
\gamma_{z}=0
, we stress that the parameter
\gamma_{z}
is left free and self-calibrated in the number counts likelihood.
In Section
5
, indeed, we observe that the self-calibrated constraint on
\gamma_{z}
is consistent with zero.

We make one remark regarding the redshift scaling of the completeness function.
The redshift-independent completeness function obtained in this work suggests that the quantity
\mathtt{EXT}
contains very little information about the X-ray core size of the cluster.
This is not surprising, given that (1)
eROSITA
is not capable of resolving the ICM structure of the majority of clusters due to the relatively large PSF (FWHM
\approx 28″
), and (2)
\mathtt{EXT}
is designed to act as a nuisance parameter to maximize the extent likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
under the detection configuration, rather than actually characterizing the angular size of the ICM core.
This conclusion is supported by the fact that no clear scaling between
\mathtt{EXT}
and quantities other than
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
is seen in the eFEDS sample.

Measurements in the eFEDS simulations

Finally, we turn to derive the completeness from the eFEDS simulations
(
Liu
et al. 2022b
)
.
It is important to stress that we independently derive the completeness from the eFEDS simulations only for the purpose of a comparison with that inferred using the richness distribution; we do not use the simulation-inferred completeness in the analysis.

By directly using the count rate estimates
\eta
provided in
the output catalogs
7
7

7

https://erosita.mpe.mpg.de/edr/eROSITAObservations/Catalogues/liuT/eFEDS_catalog_V3.3.html
of the eFEDS simulations, we derive

N_{\mathrm{clu}}(\eta,z)
and
N_{\mathrm{ps}}(\eta,z)
for the clusters
in the extended-source and point-source catalogs, respectively.
Then, the resulting completeness is derived using equation (
37
).
However, the completeness estimated in this way
(
Bulbul
et al. 2022
, also as in)
is biased, as mentioned before.
This is because we are mixing the count rates in both the “extended-source” and “point-source” modes
to derive the completeness
\mathcal{C}
.
The resulting completeness
\mathcal{C}
is marked as the grey crosses in Figure
3
, showing a significant overestimation of the X-ray completeness at a given count rate as opposed to our richness-inferred measurements (black points).
This is expected, since the count rates of the mis-identified clusters provided in the output catalog are estimated in a point-source aperture and hence systematically underestimated than what would have been measured as extended sources.

With a further attempt, we account for the bias in the “point-source-mode” count rate of the clusters
mis-identified in the point-source catalog by applying a correction factor.
We use two methods to empirically derive the correction.
In the first method, referred to as “correction 1”, we empirically derive the relation between the input X-ray flux
f_{\mathrm{X}}
and the measured “extended-source-mode” count rate
\eta
based on the sample of the clusters in the extended-source catalog—this gives the
f_{\mathrm{X}}
–
\eta
relation.
This relation is straightforward to obtain, because both the quantities
f_{\mathrm{X}}
and
\eta
are provided in the output catalogs of the eFEDS simulations.
Then, for a simulated cluster mis-identified as a point source, we directly convert the input X-ray flux
f_{\mathrm{X}}
of the cluster to the count rate using the
f_{\mathrm{X}}
–
\eta
relation (ignoring the scatter).
This resulting
f_{\mathrm{X}}
-inferred count rate then represents the count rate that would have been measured in the extended mode of the
eROSITA
pipeline, under the assumption that the relation between
f_{\mathrm{X}}
and
\eta
is the same for the clusters in the extended-source and point-source catalogs.
With this
f_{\mathrm{X}}
-inferred count rate for the mis-identified clusters in the point-source catalog, the resulting completeness is derived and shown as the red squares in Figure
3
.

In the second method, referred to as“correction 2”, we empirically fit an angular size-dependent relation for the correction
C_{\eta}(\eta,\theta_{500})
, where the quantity
\theta_{500}
is the angular size of the clusters and directly provided in the output catalogs of the eFEDS simulations.
The dependence on
\theta_{500}
is included to account for the bias due to the inappropriate choice of the aperture size used by the
eROSITA
pipeline for the clusters in the point-source catalog.
The relation
C_{\eta}\left(\eta,\theta_{500}\right)
is parameterized as
C_{\eta}\left(\eta,\theta_{500}\right)\propto a{\theta_{500}}^{b}
, where
a
and
b
are to be fitted.
With a
\chi^{2}
fitting, the best-fit relation
C_{\eta}\left(\eta,\theta_{500}\right)
is obtained by requiring that the distribution of the mis-identified clusters in the space of
\left(f_{\mathrm{X}},\eta\right)
follows the
f_{\mathrm{X}}
–
\eta
relation of the clusters in the extended-source catalog.
In this way, we obtain the corrected count rate of the mis-identified clusters by multiplying the correction factor
C_{\eta}\left(\eta,\theta_{500}\right)
to the “point-source-mode” count rate provided in the catalog.
The resulting completeness function is shown by the green stars in Figure
3
.

If the simulations are a perfect representation of the eFEDS observations, we would expect that the X-ray completeness with the correction methods applied could reproduce the richness-based measurements.
As seen, both correction methods (red squares and green stars) give the completeness functions that are not in good agreement with our measurements (black points).
The methods of “correction 1” and ‘correction 2”, respectively, underestimate and overestimate the completeness at
\eta\gtrsim 0.1~\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}
and
\eta\lesssim 0.07~\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}
.
This suggests that the simulations do not perfectly reproduce the eFEDS observations.
Meanwhile, both correction methods increase the
50\%
completeness
\eta_{50}
with respect to that without any corrections, showing that
the “raw” simulation-based completeness estimate (grey crosses) is significantly overestimated at a given count rate.
Despite the efforts in making the simulations as identical to the eFEDS observations as possible
(
Liu
et al. 2022b
)
, the “raw” simulation-based X-ray completeness
(
Bulbul
et al. 2022
, as also in)
is significantly different to the richness-based measurements.
Even with the two correction methods, the corrected completeness estimates from the simulations still do not perfectly agree with the measurements.
Our results pose a concern for a study of eFEDS clusters that heavily relies on the completeness function derived from the simulations.

Again, we stress that we present these two correction methods merely for the purpose of the comparison; we do not use them in any analyses presented in this work.

4.3
Modeling of cosmology

In this work, we focus on constraining two models of cosmology, namely the flat
\Lambda\textrm{CDM}
and
w\textrm{CDM}
models.
Each model is characterized by the mean matter density
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
, the mean baryon density
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}
,
the degree of fluctuations in the density field at the present epoch
\sigma_{8}
, the spectral index of the primordial power spectrum
n_{\mathrm{s}}
, the current Hubble parameter

H_{0}
in an unit of
\mathrm{km}/\mathrm{sec}/\mathrm{Mpc}
, and the dark energy equation of state parameter
w
.
The curvature is fixed to zero (
\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}=0
) for both models, and
w
is fixed to
-1
for the
\Lambda\textrm{CDM}
model.
The temperature of the Cosmic Microwave Background today is fixed to
2.7255
Kelvin
(
Fixsen
et al. 2009
)
,
while the effective number of neutrino species is set to be
3.046

(
Planck
Collaboration et al. 2020
)
.
We make no attempt to constrain the sum of neutrino masses,
which are not included in evaluating the predictions from the cosmological models.
We do not expect significant impact raising from neglecting massive neutrinos given the current sample size; this statement is also supported by the analysis in the SPT-SZ survey, where the inclusions of massive neutrinos did not alter the modeling of the cluster abundance in any significant ways
(
Bocquet
et al. 2019
)
.

The overall parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}
is defined as

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}\equiv\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{c}}\cup\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\eta}\cup\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\lambda}\cup\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{WL}}\cup\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{mis}}\cup\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\rho}\cup\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathcal{C}}\,,

where

•

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{c}}\equiv\left\{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}},\Omega_{\mathrm{b}},\sigma_{8},n_{\mathrm{s}},H_{0},w\right\}

\mathcal{D}
given
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}

We explore the parameter space using the Affine Invariant Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampler,

Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013

Foreman-Mackey
et al. 2019
)

4.4
Blinding

In this work, we constrain cosmology in a blind analysis to not only avoid confirmation bias but also to assess the adequacy of the modeling strategy we have chosen.
Because the weak-lensing mass calibration had been carried out blindly in
Chiu et al. 2022
and the method identical to that in the present analysis,
the same blinding scheme on the mass calibration shall not be used in this work.
Taking this opportunity, we employ a blinding strategy to validate the two most important and novel aspects of our analysis, which are (1) the empirical calibration of the completeness due to the X-ray selection and (2) the broken-power law feature in the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation.
We describe the blinding strategy, as follows.

The blinding takes place at the parameter level.
Specifically, the actual mean values and the uncertainties of the cosmological parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathrm{c}}
, except for the modeling of the weak-lensing mass calibration alone, are entirely hidden from the analyst by a random scaling. Therefore, there is no information about the mean values
and uncertainties
of any parameters during the blinded analysis.

Our requirement for unblinding is that the following criteria are met:

•

In a validation test, the same analysis code can recover (i.e., within the
68\%
confidence levels) the input values that are used to generate the mock sample of clusters in a sky footprint of
1400
deg
2
(corresponding to a size at least ten times larger than eFEDS). This validates the accuracy of the code (limits systematics to
\approx 1/3
the scale of the statistical uncertainties reflected in the final posteriors).

•

The best-fit model provides a good description of the data in the observable space of the count rate
\eta
, richness
\lambda
, and the redshift
z
; that is, the goodness-of-fit test shows no sign of significant tension between the data and the best-fit model.
See more details of the goodness-of-fit tests in Section
5.3.3
.

•

The constraints on cosmology in the modeling of the cluster abundance alone without the Gaussian priors (inferred from equation (
41
)) on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathcal{C}}
are consistent at a level of
1\sigma
when derived from the full sample and the other sub-samples.
These subsamples include (1) the low-
z
sample with
0.1<z<0.35
, (2) the high-
z
sample with
0.35<z<1.2
, and (3) the high-
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
sample with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}>15
.
Except the comparison between the low-
z
and high-
z
sub-samples, we note that other comparisons contain non-trivial correlations in the sub-samples that we do not account for.
However, the consistency among them indicates that the empirical modeling of the completeness (see Section
4.2.1
) is sufficiently accurate for different samples of eFEDS clusters and does not have a significant impact on the resulting posteriors of cosmological parameters.

•

With the weak-lensing informed priors on the parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\eta}
of the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation, the resulting constraints in the modeling of the cluster abundance alone are consistent with and without the Gaussian priors
applied to the completeness parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathcal{C}}
.
This implies that our independent measurement of the completeness is consistent with those obtained from the self-calibration of the completeness model using the cluster abundance.

•

The resulting constraints on both the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation and cosmological parameters are consistent with and without the broken power-law feature of the observed count rate (see Section
4.1
).
This means that the single power-law relation of previously assumed relation between
\eta
and
M
provides a sufficiently accurate description for both galaxy clusters and groups, and does not significantly bias the cosmological constraints.

We confirm that these criteria are met before the unblinding.
No post-unblinding changes are made in this work, except for an additional test.
In a post-unblinding stage, we additionally performed a test to examine the impact from the weak-lensing informed priors applied to the parameters
\left(A_{\eta},B_{\eta},\delta_{\eta},\gamma_{\eta}\right)
of the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation in the modeling of the cluster abundance.
See more details in the last paragraph of Section
4.3
.

are insensitive to the Gaussian priors applied to the X-ray completeness
\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)
parameters
\left(\eta_{50},s_{\eta}\right)

This suggests that our empirical modeling provides (1) a flexible scheme to describe the completeness and (2) an accurate characterization of the X-ray completeness.
This validates our empirical modeling of the X-ray completeness of the eFEDS extent selected sample.

Owing to the lack of sufficient coverage in both the mass and redshift ranges, we make one remark that the redshift-dependent parameters of the

Joint weak-lensing and the cluster abundance analysis

In Sections

5.2

(1) the constraints on the

–

–

(2) neither the modeling of the weak-lensing mass calibration nor the cluster abundance prefer a broken power-law feature of the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation at the low-mass end of
M\approx M_{\mathrm{grp}}
.
Therefore, we can combine the two datasets to obtain a joint constraint on the scaling relations and cosmology.
Because the data do not provide compelling evidence of a broken power-law scaling of the count rate, we use the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation with a single power-law form as the fiducial analysis in this work.
Moreover, given that the X-ray completeness
\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)
self-calibrated by the cluster abundance is fully consistent with that independently measured using the richness distribution (see Figure
5
and Section
5.2
), the constraints in equation (

) are consistently applied as the Gaussian priors to the X-ray completeness parameters of
\left(\eta_{50},s_{\eta}\right)
in the joint modeling. Note that the results presented in this section are obtained assuming the flat

.

The uncertainties on

, and

5.3.1
Individual cluster mass estimates

Using Bayes’ theorem and following the procedure in
Chiu et al. 2022
, we compute the mass posterior
P(M|\eta,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\eta})
of individual clusters inferred using the best-fit
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation based on the observed count rate
\eta
at the cluster redshift
z
as

P(M|\eta,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\eta})\propto P(\eta|M,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\eta})~P(M|z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\eta})\,,

(43)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\eta}
is the best-fit parameter vector of the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation.
Note that we do not marginalize over the chain of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\eta}
and hence do not include the systematic uncertainty in the mass posterior.
That is,
P(M|\eta,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\eta})
only includes the dispersion introduced by the measurement uncertainty and intrinsic scatter about the count rate to mass relation.
The mean masses of individual clusters, estimated as
M_{500\mathrm{c}}\equiv\left\langle M\right\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}MP(M|\eta,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\eta})
, are plotted versus redshift in Figure
10
, where we also plot the samples from the South Pole Telescope survey
(
Bleem et al. 2015
;
Huang
et al. 2020
)
, the
Planck
catalog
(
Planck
Collaboration et al. 2015
)
, the
XXL
survey
(
Pacaud
et al. 2016
)
, and the
ROSAT
-based MARD-Y3 sample
(
Klein
et al. 2019
)
.
As seen, the eFEDS sample used in this work spans a mass range between
\approx 10^{13.3}h^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}\approx 2\times 10^{13}h^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
and
\approx 10^{14.7}h^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}\approx 5\times 10^{14}h^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
.
The sample contains a significant fraction (
\approx 50\%
) of galaxy groups with mass of
M\lesssim 10^{14}h^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
at redshifts
z\lesssim 0.5
, which is an important part of the cluster phase space for the
eROSITA
survey.
Surpassing other cluster surveys, as seen in Figure
10
, the eFEDS survey provides a unique sample that delivers clusters with
M\gtrsim 10^{14}h^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
out to a redshift beyond unity but also that probes a mass range of over an order of magnitude in a broad range of redshift.

With the mass posterior
P(M|\eta,z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}_{\eta})
for each cluster we randomly
sample a mass estimate from the posterior as the “ensemble” mass.
The ensemble mass here is referred to as the individual mass estimate that acts as an
ensemble statistic inferred from the overall population modeling
(
Bocquet
et al. 2019
;
Chiu et al. 2020
, see also)
.
The uncertainty of the cluster mass, which is derived as the dispersion of the mass posterior,
is at a level of
\approx 20\%
for a cluster with
M_{500\mathrm{c}}\approx 10^{14}h^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
.
We provide these mass measurements in Table
4
in Appendix
B
.

5.3.2
Constraints on cluster scaling relations

Based on the joint modeling, the resulting best-fit relation of the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation reads

\left\langle\ln\left(\frac{\eta}{\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{sec}}\Bigg|M,z\right)\right\rangle=\ln\left(0.133^{+0.026}_{-0.020}\right)+\\
\left[\left(1.86^{+0.20}_{-0.15}\right)+\left(-0.58^{+0.43}_{-0.50}\right)\ln\left(\frac{1+z}{1+z_{\mathrm{piv}}}\right)\right]\times\ln\left(\frac{M}{M_{\mathrm{piv}}}\right)+\\
2\times\ln\left(\frac{E(z)}{E(z_{\mathrm{piv}})}\right)+\left(-0.83^{+0.44}_{-0.50}\right)\times\ln\left(\frac{1+z}{1+z_{\mathrm{piv}}}\right)\\
-2\times\ln\left(\frac{D_{\mathrm{L}}\left(z\right)}{D_{\mathrm{L}}\left(z_{\mathrm{piv}}\right)}\right)+\ln\left(b_{\eta}\left(M,z\right)\right)\,,

(44)

with the log-normal intrinsic scatter
\sigma_{\eta}=0.332^{+0.076}_{-0.089}
.
We find that the mass slope (
B_{\eta}=1.86^{+0.20}_{-0.15}
) is steeper than that obtained in
Chiu et al. 2022
at a level of
\approx 1\sigma
, in which they constrained the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation in the weak-lensing mass calibration alone using the latest HSC weak-lensing data
(
Li et al. 2022
)
.
Meanwhile, the resulting redshift scaling (
\gamma_{\eta}=-0.83^{+0.44}_{-0.50}
) reveals a negative deviation from the self-similar prediction (
\gamma_{\eta}=0
) at a level of
\lesssim 2\sigma
, which is different from what was found in
Chiu et al. 2022
.
We note that the differences of the constraints on the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation between this work and
Chiu et al. 2022
are driven by the modeling of the cluster abundance and are not statistically significant due to the large errorbars.
In Figure
11
, we plot the count rate
\eta
of the eFEDS clusters as a function of the ensemble mass (the left panel) and the cluster redshift (the right panel) with the
68\%
confidence level of the best-fit model indicated by the grey area.
Note that the grey area, which represents the uncertainty of the best-fit mean relation in equation (
44
), is calculated using the chains of the scaling relation parameters marginalizing over the systematics.
When producing the mass (redshift) trend in the left (right) panel, we normalize the observed data to the pivotal redshift
z_{\mathrm{piv}}=0.35
(pivotal mass
M_{\mathrm{piv}}=1.4\times 10^{14}h^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) after dividing them by the known redshift (mass) scaling predicted by the best-fit model.
As seen, no sign of bias in the best-fit model is revealed with respect to the data.
Moreover, the mass scaling of the count rate is well described by a single power-law function without any clear feature that might hint at a broken power law at the low-mass end.

We make one remark regarding the mass trend of X-ray observable-to-mass-and-redshift relations, as follow.
In
Chiu et al. 2022
, we constrained various X-ray observable scaling relations, including the X-ray temperature
T_{\mathrm{X}}
, luminosity
L_{\mathrm{X}}
, ICM mass
M_{\mathrm{gas}}
, and
Y_{\mathrm{X}}\equiv T_{\mathrm{X}}\times M_{\mathrm{gas}}
.
Overall, their mass trends were all found to be statistically consistent with or mildly steeper than the self-similar prediction at a level of
\lesssim 1.7\sigma
.
On the other hand, the analysis with the same methodology was adopted in deriving the X-ray observation-to-mass-and-redshift relations for a sample of SZE-selected clusters at a similar redshift range of
0.2<z<1.5
in the SPT-SZ survey
(
Bulbul
et al. 2019
, see also
Chiu et al. 2018
)
, where it was found that the mass trends are generally steeper than the self-similar prediction at a level of
\gtrsim 3\sigma
.
This mild inconsistency is intriguing, given the fact that the two samples (eFEDS and SPT) are both selected by their ICM properties but span different mass ranges.
However, we stress that the cluster mass
M
in
Bulbul
et al. 2019
was calibrated in the joint modeling of the weak-lensing mass calibration and the cluster abundance
(
Bocquet
et al. 2019
)
, as opposed to only the weak-lensing mass calibration as in
Chiu et al. 2022
.
When including the cluster abundance of the eFEDS clusters, as done in this work, we find that the mass trend of the count rate shows a steeper slope than that from the weak-lensing mass calibration alone (at the
\approx 1\sigma
level).
Considering the degeneracy of the mass trends between the count rate and the other X-ray observables
(
Chiu et al. 2022
)
, the steepening mass trend of the count rate when including the cluster abundance would pull the mass slope of the X-ray observables toward a higher value.
This would result in an even steeper slope than the self-similar prediction, bringing the eFEDS results into better agreement with the SPT sample results
(
Bulbul
et al. 2019
)
.
A study using a larger sample of clusters in the first-year
eROSITA
survey with a wide weak-lensing coverage will shed light on this in the future.

The best-fit
\lambda
–
M
–
z
relation at a fixed mass and redshift inferred from the joint modeling yields

\left\langle\ln\lambda|M,z\right\rangle=\ln\left(37.1^{+4.6}_{-5.0}\right)+\\
\left[\left(1.05^{+0.13}_{-0.12}\right)+\left(-0.72^{+0.45}_{-0.37}\right)\ln\left(\frac{1+z}{1+z_{\mathrm{piv}}}\right)\right]\times\ln\left(\frac{M}{M_{\mathrm{piv}}}\right)\\
+\left(-0.77^{+0.41}_{-0.54}\right)\times\ln\left(\frac{1+z}{1+z_{\mathrm{piv}}}\right)\,,

(45)

with the log-normal intrinsic scatter of
0.291^{+0.133}_{-0.078}
.
We find that the mass trend (
B_{\lambda}=1.05^{+0.13}_{-0.12}

5.3.3
The goodness of fit

We quantify the goodness of fit of the best-fit model by comparing the cluster numbers between the model prediction and the observed data in the observable space of the count rate
\eta
, richness
\lambda
, and the redshift
z
.
Specifically, we bin each observable dimension (
\eta
,
\lambda
,
z
) spanned by the eFEDS sample into
10
logarithmic bins, resulting in a total
10^{3}
cells in the three-dimensional space, and assess the consistency of the cluster numbers between the prediction and the data.
Given the nature of Poisson distributions, the modified
Cash 1979
statistic, which is attributed to Castor
(
Kaastra 2017
;
Baker &
Cousins 1984
)
, is used to estimate the goodness of fit in terms of the test statistic
C_{\chi}
.
For a sufficiently large number of events, as in the case of
\approx 450
clusters in this work, the test statistic
C_{\chi}
has a useful property that allows us to estimate the goodness of fit for a Poissonian model in a manner similar to the
\chi^{2}
test for a Gaussian distribution.

Adopting the best-fit parameters obtained in the joint modeling
with a single power-law
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation, the test statistic expects a mean value
\left\langle C_{\chi}\right\rangle
and variance
{\Delta C_{\chi}}^{2}
of
\left\langle C_{\chi}\right\rangle=328.57
and
{\Delta C_{\chi}}^{2}=23.02^{2}
, respectively.
Meanwhile, the data statistic with respect to the best-fit model has a value
C_{\mathrm{data}}=337.47
, which is in excellent agreement with the Gaussian distribution of
\mathcal{N}\left(\left\langle C_{\chi}\right\rangle,{\Delta C_{\chi}}^{2}\right)
.
Assuming the
w\textrm{CDM}
cosmology or including the broken power-law feature in the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation both give goodness of fit in agreement with the expectations at a similar level.
Based on this test statistic, we conclude that the best-fit model provides an excellent description of the eFEDS dataset.

6
Discussions

In this work, we obtain cosmological constraints through an analysis of the optically confirmed subset of the eFEDS X-ray selected cluster sample, with support from weak lensing mass calibration carried out using the HSC Subaru Strategy Program S16A dataset.
Leveraging prior knowledge of structure formation from theory, simulations, and observations accumulated over the last few decades, we adopt flexible functional forms in the X-ray, optical and weak lensing observable-to-mass-and-redshift relations, and then empirically calibrate them within the cosmological analysis. This empirical calibration ensures that the calibrated relations follow the mass and redshift variations of the actual cluster dataset, and is crucial for minimizing systematic errors in the parameter posteriors.

The X-ray selection is modeled using an empirically determined completeness
\mathcal{C}
(
\eta
,
z
) of the X-ray selected sample as a function of count rate and redshift with minimal assumptions informed by the data and simulations. This approach allows us to bypass the direct modeling of the complex X-ray selection on the quantities of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
, and
\mathtt{EXT}
that is typically subject to biases due to inconsistencies between the clusters in the real sample and those in image simulations.
We calibrate the X-ray completeness function using
(1) independent measurements inferred from the richness distribution of the extent selected and full cluster sample (see Section
4.2.2
) and
(2) a self-calibration approach where the parameters of
\mathcal{C}\left(\eta,z\right)
are allowed to float in the cosmological analysis.
Both approaches are in good agreement, and the small differences do not significantly affect the cosmological constraints.
In addition, neither the weak-lensing mass calibration nor the cluster abundance implies a mass scaling of the X-ray count rate
\eta
that deviates from a single power law over the considerable mass range of the eFEDS sample.
Validation tests indicate that the empirical modeling provides an accurate description of the data, allowing us to extract cosmological parameter posteriors.

Despite these successes, one issue demands further investigation in a future work.
The characterization of the mapping
P\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}|\eta\right)
between the observed count rate
\eta
and the X-ray observables
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}\equiv\left\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT},,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}\right\}
.
The former is the X-ray mass proxy adopted in this work and the latter are the X-ray selection observables adopted in defining the initial eFEDS cluster candidate list.
modeling the mapping
P\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}|\eta\right)
is challenging and is sensitive to the detailed pipeline configuration used in the cluster detection.
Given a fixed signal-to-noise ratio of the count rate, denoted as
\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}\equiv\eta/\delta_{\eta}
, we find that there exists an extremely tight power-law relation between
\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
.
This is expected, because the detection likelihood describes the (log-)probability of a source being a real detection as opposed to a noise fluctuation.
Given the detection likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
, the
eROSITA
pipeline additionally proceeds the estimation of the extent likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
and the extent
\mathtt{EXT}
.
There exhibits a strong and positive correlation between
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
with a considerable amount of scatter in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
at a fixed
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}

(
Klein
et al. 2022
, see Figure 16 in)
.
Moreover, the scatter of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
at a fixed
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
is strongly driven by the extent
\mathtt{EXT}
.
Specifically, at a fixed
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
, a cluster with higher extent
\mathtt{EXT}
will also exhibit a higher extent likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
.
This is again expected, because a higher
\mathtt{EXT}
implies a more extended source and naturally a higher
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
.

Therefore, it is possible to establish a relation between
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}
and
\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}
via a two-step empirical modeling, namely

P\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}|\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}\right)\equiv P\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}|\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}\right)\approx P\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT}|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}\right)P(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}|\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta})
, in which
P\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT}|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}\right)
characterizes the correlation between
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
and
\mathtt{EXT}
at a fixed
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
.
Note that in the above equation we assume that the scatter of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
and
\mathtt{EXT}
at a fixed
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
does not correlate with
\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}
.
That is,
P\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT}|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}},\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}\right)\approx P\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT}|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}\right)
.
This assumption is physically intuitive, meaning that the extendedness of a source does not depend on the signal-to-noise ratio of the detection, to first order.
Since we can empirically model the relation between
\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}
and
\eta
, in which they are related via the measurement uncertainty
\delta_{\eta}
, we can then model the mapping between
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}
and
\eta
as

\displaystyle P\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}|\eta\right)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}P\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}|\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}\right)P\left(\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}|\eta\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle P\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT}|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}\right)P\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}|\eta\right)\,,

where
P\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}|\eta\right)\equiv\int\mathrm{d}\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}P(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}|\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta})P\left(\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta}|\eta\right)
.
This establishes the direct mapping between
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}
and
\eta
.
Moreover, given the well behaved relation between count rate
\eta
and halo mass
M
at each redshift
z
, it is possible to construct a direct mapping between
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}
and the quantities of mass and redshift.

The modeling of
P(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}|\mathtt{SNR}_{\eta})
is straightforward, by design.
However, the characterization of
P\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT}|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}\right)
is challenging due to the scatter of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
and
\mathtt{EXT}
, depending on the configuration of the
eROSITA
source detection.
We find that the bivariate distribution of
\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathtt{EXT}\right)
at a fixed
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
contains a mix of at least two components, each with different patterns of the scatter.
The first pattern represents the population of the clusters with
\mathtt{EXT}
smaller than the upper limit of the detection, i.e.,
\mathtt{EXT}<60″
.
For such a population, the relation between
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
and
\mathtt{EXT}
, which causes the scatter of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
at a fixed
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
, does not follow a behavior that can be easily parameterized.
This is consistent with
Clerc
et al. 2018
, where they did not find a well characterized relation between
\mathtt{EXT}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
.
The other pattern is attributed to the population with
\mathtt{EXT}=60″
forced by the detection pipeline.
In this population, the shape of the ICM profile is essentially fixed in the source detection regardless of the actual angular size of the cluster.
This results in an upper limit of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
at a fixed
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
, denoted as
{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}}_{,\mathrm{upper}}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}})
, and the characterization of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
for the cluster is determined along the
{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}}_{,\mathrm{upper}}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}})
–
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
relation.
The upper limit of
\mathtt{EXT}
alters the “native” relation between
\mathtt{EXT}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
that could have been estimated with
\mathtt{EXT}>60″
.
Moreover, a combination of these two populations (
\mathtt{EXT}<60″
and
\mathtt{EXT}=60″
) is challenging to model and hence requires the modeling based on either intensive simulations or an empirical method as done in this work.
As mentioned in Section
4.1
, the simulation-based modeling

(
Liu et al. 2022a
;
Liu
et al. 2022b
;
Bulbul
et al. 2022
, as in)
requires accurate and complete information on cluster physics
and, inevitably, inherits systematic uncertainties due to the lack of realism in the simulations.
Any discrepancy seen between the data and simulations (as the case for the eFEDS simulations which over-predict the low-
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
clusters) would pose a concern that the associated cosmological posteriors would be biased.
In this work, we therefore choose the approach of empirical modeling and calibration informed by the data themselves.

Recall that the quantity
\mathtt{EXT}
acts as a nuisance parameter to maximize
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
in the source detection rather than an actual measurement of the core radius of the ICM profile.
Therefore, we do not expect
\mathtt{EXT}
to contain much information about the cluster ICM distribution.
In fact, this is in line with the fact that no well behaved relation between
\mathtt{EXT}
and other X-ray properties is seen for the eFEDS clusters
(see also
Clerc
et al. 2018
;
Liu
et al. 2022b
, for simulation validations)
.
This is also supported by our results (in Section
5.3.4
) that the X-ray completeness does not reveal a sign of redshift dependence, suggesting that (1)
\mathtt{EXT}
is nearly insensitive to the actual angular size of the core radius, which is expected to be inversely proportional to the angular diameter distance,
\theta\propto{D_{\mathrm{A}}}^{-1}
, and that (2) the detectability of a source mainly depends on the count rate.
Moreover, it is worth mentioning that the maximization of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
(through three free parameters, R.A., Dec., and
\mathtt{EXT}
) in X-ray imaging is in analogy to that of the signal-to-noise ratio in SZE surveys
(
Vanderlinde
et al. 2010
)
.
The former resides in a regime of Poisson noise, owing to the statistical property of X-ray sky images, while the latter has Gaussian noise that is straightforward to characterize
(
Grandis et al. 2019
, see discussions in Appendix A in)
.
It is then possible to study the noise properties of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
in a Poisson regime and derive a maximum likelihood estimator that can be directly used as a mass proxy, as in the case of SZE surveys.
This task is beyond the scope of this work and is indeed worth investigating in the future.

The form of the X-ray selection in terms of the completeness function
\mathcal{C}
(
\eta
,
z
) is such that the number of clusters with low-
\eta
are suppressed.
Meanwhile, the inclusion of the broken power-law mass scaling (
B_{\mathrm{grp}}>B_{\eta}
) of the count rate reduces the population of low-mass clusters at a fixed
\eta
given the scatter
\sigma_{\eta}
.
Therefore, we expect some degeneracy between
B_{\mathrm{grp}}
and the parameters of
\mathcal{C}
(
\eta
,
z
).
In our analysis, we find that
B_{\mathrm{grp}}
is degenerate with
\eta_{50}
(but not strongly with
s_{\eta}
and
\gamma_{z}
) when we remove the Gaussian priors on the X-ray completeness.
This is shown in the parameter space spanned by
B_{\mathrm{grp}}
and
\eta_{50}
in Figure

Moreover, no significant difference in the cosmological parameters

and

In an extreme case where we fix the parameters of the completeness to the best-fit values (i.e.,

This suggests that (1) the current cosmological constraints in eFEDS are not limited by the precision of the completeness function, and that (2)
a more precise characterization on the completeness function in a future work will provide a better constraint on the mass scaling of clusters at the low mass end.

It is worth mentioning that fixing the X-ray completeness to the “raw” simulation-based estimate (without the corrections described in Section

and

Additionally, including the correction methods (either using the method 1 or 2; see Section

Conclusions

We present the first cosmological constraints using a sample of
455

eROSITA
clusters with a mass range of
2\times 10^{13}h^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}\lesssim M\lesssim 5\times 10^{14}h^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
at redshift
0.1<z<1.2
in the eFEDS survey.
The sample is selected in X-rays and further confirmed in the optical imaging using the MCMF algorithm
(
Klein
et al. 2022
)
, resulting in a joint selection on both the X-ray and optical observables.
The galaxy richness
\lambda
is used as the optical observable to facilitate the optical confirmation, for which the contamination due to the random line-of-sight superposition of X-ray and optical systems is required to be below
30\%
(i.e.,
f_{\mathrm{cont}}<0.3
).
With the optical confirmation to remove spurious sources, the contamination in the cluster sample is expected to be at a level of
6\%
, which is significantly lower than that (
\approx 20\%
) of the catalog purely constructed in X-rays.

We make use of the public data of the HSC survey, which covers
177
eFEDS clusters, to perform the weak-lensing mass calibration that was thoroughly and carefully studied in
Chiu et al. 2022
.
The weak-lensing analysis approach employed in this work is the same as in
Chiu et al. 2022
, with two differences:
First, the public weak lensing data covers less of the eFEDS region, resulting a factor of
\approx 30\%
reduction in the statistical power.
Second, an improved analysis allows us to update the uncertainty on the weak-lensing systematic error floor from
5.7\%
to
3\%
, but this has a negligible impact on the final results.
The weak-lensing systematics are intensively calibrated against large cosmological simulations, in which the observed weak-lensing properties, as quantified in
Chiu et al. 2022
, were added in
Grandis et al. 2021
.
Importantly, the weak-lensing systematics associated with baryonic effects is also taken into account.

With the minimal assumptions, namely

•

the scaling relations between the observables (X-ray, optical, weak lensing) and the underlying halo mass
M
of clusters across a range of redshift
z
can be described by a power law function depending on
M
and
z
with log-normal intrinsic scatter,

•

the technique of weak lensing, which is nearly independent of any baryonic assumptions and hence is a direct probe to the full potential, can provide an unbiased estimate of the cluster halo mass when calibrated against simulations, and

•

the X-ray selection can be modeled using a completeness function
\mathcal{C}
(
\eta
,
z
) in the X-ray mass proxy
\eta
whose form is well supported by the data and the dedicated simulations
(
Clerc
et al. 2018
;
Liu
et al. 2022b
;
Bulbul
et al. 2022
)
,

we perform the empirical modeling to simultaneously fit for the scaling relations, X-ray completeness, and cosmology.
The empirical modeling refers to the approach that allows the precise model to be determined by the data themselves.
This avoids the potential biases raised in a methodology that heavily relies on image and catalog simulations, where there are characteristically differences between real dataset and the simulations.
In fact, this is the case for the previously published eFEDS simulations, which
over-predict the clusters with low extent likelihood (
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}\lesssim 10
) compared to the observations
(
Liu
et al. 2022b
)
.

In this work, three scaling relations are modeled, including
the count rate-to-mass-and-redshift (
\eta
–
M
–
z
) relation (X-ray mass observable),
the richness-to-mass-and-redshift (
\lambda
–
M
–
z
) relation (optical mass observable), and
the weak-lensing mass-to-mass-and-redshift (
M_{\mathrm{WL}}
–
M
–
z
) relation.
The optical selection or cluster confirmation, which reduces the contamination relative to the X-ray-only selected sample by a factor of 3.3, is accounted for by a redshift-dependent cut on the cluster richness.
Meanwhile, the X-ray selection is modeled using a completeness function
\mathcal{C}
(
\eta
,
z
) of the observed count rate with the dependence on the cluster redshift.
The weak-lensing systematics are taken into account through a marginalization over the priors applied to the parameters of the
M_{\mathrm{WL}}
–
M
–
z
relation.

Two novel aspects are introduced and studied in detail in this paper.
First, we generalize the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation to include a broken power-law scaling at the low-mass end, with the attempt to reconcile the discrepancy seen between the eFEDS observations and simulations. No compelling evidence for a broken power law is found.
Second, we perform an independent measurement of the X-ray completeness using the observed richness distribution in the optical.
The constraints from the richness-inferred completeness are applied as priors to the parameters of the completeness function in the cosmological analysis.

In this work, the
50\%
completeness in X-rays is constrained to occur at the count rate of
\eta\approx 0.06~\mathrm{counts}/\mathrm{s}
with a scale factor of
s_{\eta}\approx 0.5
.
We find no clear dependence on the cluster redshift in the X-ray completeness.
There exhibits good agreement between the richness-inferred X-ray completeness and the self-calibrated completeness extracted using the cluster abundance.
Removing the priors to allow the self-calibration on the X-ray completeness has no significant impact on the cosmological constraints.

In a blind analysis, the cosmological constraints are derived in the joint modeling of the cluster abundance and the weak-lensing mass calibration.
The cluster abundance is modeled in the observable space of the count rate
\eta
, the richness
\lambda
, and the redshift
z
, accounting for the selection.
In the
\Lambda\textrm{CDM}
cosmology, we obtain the constraints on
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
,
\sigma_{8}
and
S_{8}
\left(\equiv\sigma_{8}\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/0.3\right)^{0.3}\right)

as

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.245^{+0.048}_{-0.058}\,,

\displaystyle\sigma_{8}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.833^{+0.075}_{-0.063}\,,

\displaystyle S_{8}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.791^{+0.028}_{-0.031}\,.

Extending the analysis to the
w\textrm{CDM}
cosmology yields the fully consistent results of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\sigma_{8}
with the constraint on the equation of state of dark energy,

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1.25\pm 0.47\,.

We assess the consistency between the best-fit model and the data, showing excellent agreement without bias in the distributions of the observables
\eta
,
\lambda
, and
z
.
These cosmological constraints are robust against
the priors applied to the parameters of the completeness function,
the assumed functional form (single or broken power law) for the mass scaling of the
\eta
–
M
–
z
relation, and
the choice of different sample selections, including the subsamples at low redshift
0.1<z<0.35
, at high redshift
0.35<z<1.2
, and with high extent likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}>15
.

We compare the cosmological constraints with those from the
Planck
mission (the
\mathtt{TTTEEE}+\mathtt{lowE}
constraint), the third-year 3
\times
2-point analysis from DES, and the cluster sample from the SPT-SZ survey.
The eFEDS results on
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
,
\sigma_{8}
,
S_{8}
, and
w
are in good agreement with the external constraints at a level of
\lesssim 1\sigma
.
Moreover, based on a sample of
455
eFEDS clusters, our results deliver constraints that are
comparable to these independent methods, demonstrating the promise of the analysis based on the full
eROSITA
survey in the future.

Apart from the cosmological constraints, we present the
\eta
–
M
–
z
and
\lambda
–
M
–
z
relations.
The constraints on the scaling relations are
significantly stronger than and also statistically consistent with the previous results inferred from the weak-lensing calibration alone
(
Chiu et al. 2022
)
.
The
\lambda
–
M
–

relation shows a mass scaling that is consistent with the self-similar prediction (
\lambda\propto M^{1}
) and a mildly decreasing redshift trend (
\delta_{\lambda}=-0.72^{+0.45}_{-0.37}
) at a level of
\approx 1.7\sigma
.
Meanwhile, we find that the
\eta

–
z
prefers a mass trend that is steeper than, but still statistically consistent with, the slope inferred from the weak-lensing calibration only.
Hence, including the modeling of the cluster abundance is expected to increase the mass slope of other X-ray observables, leading to better agreement with scaling relation studies using the SPT sample
(
Chiu et al. 2016a
;
Chiu et al. 2018
;
Bulbul
et al. 2019
)
.
In both the modeling of the weak-lensing mass calibration and the cluster abundance, there is no strong evidence supporting the broken power-law feature in the mass scaling of the count rate.

, we provide the updated and improved mass estimates of the individual clusters.

.

Outlook

These are the first fully self consistent constraints on cosmology using the abundance of X-ray selected clusters calibrated by weak lensing from a wide-field photometric survey.
Moreover, this paper demonstrates the success of empirical modeling in X-ray cluster cosmology, following the precursor studies using X-ray and SZE-selected clusters
(
Vikhlinin
et al. 2009a
;
Mantz
et al. 2010
;
Benson
et al. 2013
;
de Haan
et al. 2016
;
Bocquet
et al. 2019
;
Costanzi
et al. 2021
)
.
It is also important to stress that the cosmological constraints are obtained using a large sample of clusters optically confirmed by the MCMF algorithm
(
Klein
et al. 2018
)
with the contamination rate significantly reduced from
\approx 20\%
to
\approx 6\%
without discarding a large number of real clusters.
In what follows, we provide some aspects future improvements to pave a way forward.

It is of critical importance to understand the discrepancy between the eFEDS data and the simulations to have a more accurate description of the clusters at the low-
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
end (or with low signal-to-noise ratios).
This will support the construction and calibration of the X-ray completeness in a future work.

Meanwhile, according to our finding in the distribution of the X-ray observables
\left\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}},\mathtt{EXT}\right\}
, we advocate to remove the upper limit on
\mathtt{EXT}
in the cluster detection to have a more well behaved scaling between
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}
.
As an analogy to the SZE experiments, moreover, it is possible and worth an investigation to quantify
the uncertainty of the maximum likelihood estimator of the X-ray cluster detection in a regime of Poisson noise.
Such a study will require intensive validations using accurate and end-to-end simulations, if available.

In this work, we only explore the mass-dependent slope in the
\eta
–
M
relation in terms of the broken power law.
With the all-sky sample from the
eROSITA
survey, we will be in the position to constrain the mass and redshift dependence of the intrinsic scatter, whereas in the current analysis we assume the same value for the whole population of clusters in the eFEDS survey.

With the optical confirmation, the residual contamination to the eFEDS sample is at a level of
6\%
, for which we do not expect to have a significant impact on the final results.
In a future work with a larger sample, wherein the Poisson noise of cluster counts becomes significantly smaller, a proper treatment of the contamination will be required
(
Bocquet
et al. 2019
, as in)
.

Given the good agreement between the weak-lensing mass calibration and the cluster abundance of the eFEDS sample, the next step forward is to combine the halo clustering of clusters to obtain a comprehensive constraint on cosmology
(
Chiu et al. 2020
;
Marulli
et al. 2021
)
.

As the sample size increases, we anticipate the increasing demand on computing resources in a future work.
Therefore, investigations into how to expedite the calculation are inevitably needed
(
To et al. 2022
;
Boruah et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
.

The measurement uncertainty of observed count rates

To evaluate equation (
13
) in the number counts likelihood, we empirically derive the measurement uncertainty
\delta_{\eta}
based on the observed data following a method introduced in
Grandis
et al. 2020
.
Specifically, we model the measurement uncertainty
\delta_{\eta}
as a log-normal distribution with intrinsic scatter around the mean value described as a power-law function of the observed count rate
\eta
, the exposure time
t_{\mathrm{exp}}
and the cluster redshift
z
.
In the sample of the eFEDS clusters selected in this work, we find that the logarithmic measurement uncertainty at a given
\left(\eta,t_{\mathrm{exp}},z\right)
is described by the mean value,

\left\langle\ln\left(\frac{\delta_{\eta}}{~\mathrm{counts/sec}}\Bigg|\eta,z,t_{\mathrm{exp}}\right)\right\rangle=\left(0.0156\pm 0.00016\right)+\left(0.565\pm 0.014\right)\times\ln\left(\frac{\eta}{0.1~\mathrm{counts/sec}}\right)+\left(-0.078\pm 0.021\right)\times\ln\left(\frac{z}{0.35}\right)\\
+\left(-0.383\pm 0.034\right)\times\ln\left(\frac{t_{\mathrm{exp}}}{1180~\mathrm{sec}}\right)\,,

(57)

with the log-normal scatter of
0.211\pm 0.007
.

In this work, we use the mean value of the measurement uncertainty predicted at the grid of the observed count rate
\eta
and the cluster redshift
z
to evaluate equation (
13
) in the number counts likelihood.
Given that the exposure time is nearly uniform in the eFEDS imaging and does not depend on the physical properties (e.g.,
M
,
z
, and
\eta
), we ignore the dependence on the exposure time when predicting
\delta_{\eta}
, i.e.,
t_{\mathrm{exp}}
is fixed to the median exposure time
1180
seconds in equation (
57
).
Because the scatter of the measurement uncertainty is a second-order perturbation to the count rate distribution of clusters at a given mass and redshift, we also choose to ignore the intrinsic scatter of
\delta_{\eta}
.
In addition, this scatter is expected to be absorbed into the intrinsic scatter of the intrinsic count rate at a fixed mass and redshift (see equation (
24
)), given that we empirically model the parameter
\sigma_{\eta}
in the likelihood.
Therefore, we don’t expect that the approximations made in predicting
\delta_{\eta}
would introduce significant bias to our results.

In Figure
22
, we show the observed fractional error of the count rate,
\delta_{\eta}/\eta
, of the eFEDS clusters in the left panel, while the modeling residual is presented in the middle panel.
As seen in the middle panel, no obvious trend of the modeling residual is revealed with respect to the count rate and redshift, suggesting that the modeling of
\delta_{\eta}
indeed provides a good description of the data.
In the right panel, the overall distribution of the modeling residual is shown, suggesting no bias in the modeling with (red) and without (black) the dependence on the exposure time.
This validates our approach that we ignore the exposure-time dependence in predicting
\delta_{\eta}
.
The modeling residual as a function of the exposure time is shown in the embedded plot of the right panel, where we can see that (1) the exposure time is nearly uniform centering on the median of
1180
seconds and that (2) the
t_{\mathrm{exp}}
trend is mostly driven by the outliers with extremely low (
\lesssim 500
seconds) and high (
\gtrsim 2000
seconds) exposure times.

It is worth mentioning that the count rate error at a fixed count rate is expected to scale as
1/\sqrt{t_{\mathrm{exp}}}
assuming a Poisson distribution of photon counts.
In the
eROSITA
All-Sky Survey, the exposure time will vary across the sky and hence the inclusion of the
t_{\mathrm{exp}}
dependence will be needed.
We defer the improvements, the inclusion of
t_{\mathrm{exp}}
dependence and the scatter of
\delta_{\eta}
, to a future work with a much larger sample.
