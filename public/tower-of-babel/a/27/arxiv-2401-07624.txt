Abstract
The study of intersection problems on families of sets is one of the most important topics in extremal combinatorics. As is well-known, extremal problems involving certain intersection constraints are equivalent to those with certain union constraints by taking complement of sets. A family of sets is called s s -union if the union of any two sets in this family has size at most s s . Katona [Acta Math. Hungar. 15 (1964)] provided the maximum size of an s s -union family of sets of [ n ] [n] , and he also determined the extremal families up to isomorphism. Recently, Frankl [J. Combin. Theory Ser. B 122 (2017) 869–876] sharpened this result by establishing the maximum size of an s s -union family that is not a subfamily of the so-called Katona family. In this paper, we determine the maximum size of an s s -union family that is neither contained in the Katona family nor in the Frankl family. Moreover, we characterize all extremal families achieving the upper bounds.

Introduction

Let
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
The power set

subsets of
[n]
.
For every subset

,
we denote by

in
[n]
.
We write

for the collection

-element subsets of
[n]
,
and

for the collections of all subsets of
[n]

with size at most

.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of subsets of
[n]
.
We say that
\mathcal{F}
is
k
-uniform if
all sets of
\mathcal{F}
have size
k
.
If
\mathcal{F}
is non-uniform, we usually denote

\mathcal{F}_{i}=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:|F|=i\}=\mathcal{F}\cap{[n]\choose i}
.
Given a set

and a permutation

, we denote

.
We say that
\mathcal{G}
and

\sigma\in S_{n}
such that

\{\sigma(G):G\in\mathcal{G}\}=\mathcal{H}
.
For isomorphic families
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
, we denote
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{H}
whenever there are no confusions.
For two families
\mathcal{G}

,

, denoted by

,

.

1.1
Uniform intersecting families

A family
\mathcal{F}
of sets is called

t
-intersecting
if
|A\cap B|\geq t
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
.
For
t=1
, we just say ‘intersecting’ instead of ‘
1
-intersecting’.
A
full star
is a family that consists of all the
k
-subsets of
[n]

that contains a fixed element.
The celebrated Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem
[
5
]
states that
when
n\geq 2k
, a full star is the unique intersecting
k
-uniform family
attaining the maximum size.

Theorem 1.1
(Erdős–Ko–Rado
[
5
]
)

Let
n\geq 2k
and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq{[n]\choose k}
be an
intersecting family.
Then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-1\choose k-1}.

When
n>2k
,
the equality holds if and only if

\mathcal{F}=\{F\in\tbinom{[n]}{k}:i\in F\}

for some
i\in[n]
.

The Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem
is widely regarded as a cornerstone of extremal combinatorics
and has various generalizations and applications.
For related problems, we recommend
[
14
]
for the interested readers.
Erdős, Ko and Rado
[
5
]
also proved that
there exists an integer
n_{0}(k,t)
such that if
n\geq n_{0}(k,t)
and

\mathcal{F}\subseteq{[n]\choose k}

is
t
-intersecting, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-t\choose k-t}
.
The smallest possible such
n_{0}(k,t)

is
(t+1)(k-t+1)
. This was proved by Frankl
[
6
]
for
t\geq 15
,
and then completely solved by Wilson
[
32
]
for all
t
.

Theorem 1.2
(Exact Erdős–Ko–Rado Theorem
[
6
,
32
]
)

Let
k>t\geq 1
be integers and let

\mathcal{F}\subseteq{[n]\choose k}

be a
t
-intersecting family.
If
{n\geq(t+1)(k-t+1)}
,
then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-t\choose k-t}.

The equality holds if and only if

\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to

\bigl\{F\in\tbinom{[n]}{k}:[t]\subseteq F\bigr\}
or

\{F\in{[n]\choose k}:|F\cap[t+2]|\geq t+1\}
.
For the case
n>(t+1)(k-t+1)
, the former family is the unique extremal family.

Stability results for uniform
t
-intersecting families can be found in

[
7
,
6
,
1
]
.
Moreover, the study of stabilities for
r
-wise
t
-intersecting families
has risen in popularity in the past few years; see
[
30
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
Note that the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem bounds the maximum size of
k
-uniform

t
-intersecting families. In 1964, Katona
[
23
]
studied
the problem for
non-uniform

t
-intersecting families.

Theorem 1.3
(Katona
[
23
]
)

Let
n\geq t\geq 2
be integers and let

\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a
t
-intersecting family.

(1)
If
n+t=2a
for an integer
a\geq 1
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\sum_{k\geq a}{n\choose k}.

The equality holds if and only if

\mathcal{F}=\{F\subseteq[n]:|F|\geq a\}
.

(2)
If
n+t=2a+1
for an integer
a\geq 1
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-1\choose a}+\sum_{k\geq a+1}{n\choose k}.

The equality holds if and only if

\mathcal{F}=\{F\subseteq[n]:|F|\geq a+1\}\cup{[n-1]\choose a}
.

For the case
t=1
, it is easy to see that every (non-uniform) intersecting family
of sets of
[n]
has size at most
2^{n-1}
, and there are
many extremal families attaining this bound.
There are various extension and generalization of Katona’s theorem;
see, e.g.,
[
25
,
29
,
9
,
11
,
12
]
for more details.

1.2
Non-uniform families with
s
-union property

We say that a family
\mathcal{F}
has the

s
-union property
,
or simply that
\mathcal{F}
is
s
-union
if

|F\cup F^{\prime}|\leq s
holds for every pair
F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
.
In this paper, we mainly investigate the

s
-union families.
We define
m(n,s)
as the maximum of
|\mathcal{F}|

over all
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
having the
s
-union property.
Clearly, we have
m(n,0)=1
,
m(n,1)=2
,
m(n,n)=2^{n}
and
m(n,n-1)=2^{n-1}
,
where the last equality holds by considering the
2^{n-1}
pairs

\{F,F^{c}\}
for each
F\in 2^{[n]}
and
\mathcal{F}

contains at most one set of such pairs.

In what follows, we focus mainly on stabilities for

non-uniform

t
-intersecting families.
In fact, the extremal problems for
t
-intersecting families can be reduced to
that for
s
-union families. Indeed,
it is easy to see that
\mathcal{F}
is
t
-intersecting if and only if the dual family

\mathcal{F}^{c}:=\{[n]\setminus F:F\in\mathcal{F}\}

is
(n-t)
-union. Note that
\mathcal{F}
and

\mathcal{F}^{c}
have the same size.
For notational convenience, we shall study the extremal problems
in the language of the
s
-union property, rather than
t
-intersecting property.
In particular, Katona’s result in Theorem
1.3

can be equivalently written as the following.

Theorem 1.4
(Katona
[
23
]
)

Let
2\leq s\leq n-2
be integers and let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}

be
s
-union.

(1)
If
s=2d
for an integer
d\geq 1
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\sum_{0\leq i\leq d}{n\choose i},

with equality if and only if

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{K}(n,2d):={[n]\choose\leq d}
.

(2)
If
s=2d+1
for an integer
d\geq 1
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\sum_{0\leq i\leq d}{n\choose i}+{n-1\choose d},

with equality if and only if

\mathcal{F}\!=\!\mathcal{K}(n,2d+1)\!:=\!{[n]\choose\leq d}\cup\bigl\{F\!\in\!{[n]\choose d+1},y\in F\bigr\}

for some
y\in[n]
.

In 2017, Frankl
[
11
]
proved a stability result for Katona’s theorem;
that is, he determined the maximum size of an
s
-union family
that is not a subfamily of the Katona family
\mathcal{K}(n,s)
.

Theorem 1.5
(Frankl
[
11
]
)

Let
2\leq s\leq n-2
be integers and let

\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}

be
s
-union.

(1)
If
s=2d
for an integer
d\geq 1

and
\mathcal{F}\nsubseteq\mathcal{K}(n,2d)
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\sum_{0\leq i\leq d}{n\choose i}-{n-d-1\choose d}+1.

Moreover, the equality holds if and only if

\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to

\begin{matrix}\mathcal{H}(n,2d):={[n]\choose\leq d-1}\cup\{D\}\cup\{H\in{[n]\choose d}:H\cap D\neq\emptyset\}\end{matrix}

for some set
D\in{[n]\choose d+1}
. For the case

s=4
, apart from
\mathcal{H}(n,4)
,
there is one more possibility, namely,

\mathcal{H}^{*}(n,4):={[n]\choose\leq 1}\cup\{H\in\tbinom{[n]}{2}:H\cap[2]\neq\emptyset\}\cup\bigl\{\{1,2,i\}:i\in[3,n]\bigr\}.

(2)
If
s=2d+1
for an integer
d\geq 1

and
\mathcal{F}\nsubseteq\mathcal{K}(n,2d+1)
,
then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\sum_{0\leq i\leq d}{n\choose i}+{n-1\choose d}-{n-d-2\choose d}+1.

Moreover, the equality holds if and only if

\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to

\begin{matrix}\mathcal{H}(n,2d+1):={[n]\choose\leq d}\cup\{D\}\cup\{H\in{[n]\choose d+1}:y\in H,H\cap D\neq\emptyset\}\end{matrix}

for some fixed element
y\in[n]
and
set
D\subseteq[n]\setminus\{y\}
with
|D|=d+1
.
For the case
s=5
, there is also one more possibility, namely,

\mathcal{T}(n,5):={[n]\choose\leq 2}\cup\bigl\{F\in\tbinom{[n]}{3}:|F\cap[3]|\geq 2\bigr\}.

We remark here that
the extremal family
\mathcal{H}^{*}(n,4)
is missed in
[

]
.
As mentioned early,
the analogous stability results

for uniform intersecting families; see

,

Motivated by Theorems

Problem 1.6

What is the maximum size of an

To begin with, we show the case

.

•

For
s=2
, recall that

and

or
\mathcal{H}(n,2)
.

•

For

\mathcal{K}(n,3)=\bigl\{\emptyset,\{1\},\ldots,\{n\}\bigr\}\cup\{F\in{[n]\choose 2}:y\in F\}

\mathcal{H}(n,3)=\bigl\{\emptyset,\{1\},\ldots,\{n\}\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{\{d_{1},d_{2}\},\{y,d_{1}\},\{y,d_{2}\}\bigr\}

If

is

\bigl\{\emptyset,\{a,b,c\},\{a\},\{b\},\{c\}\bigr\}

for some

To state our results,
we define the extremal families formally.
Let

and

be integers, and

We define two families as below.

\begin{matrix}\mathcal{W}(n,2d):={[n]\choose\leq d-1}\cup\{D_{1},D_{2}\}\cup\bigl\{H\in{[n]\choose d}:H\cap D_{1}\neq\emptyset~\text{and}~H\cap D_{2}\neq\emptyset\bigr\}.\end{matrix}

Moreover, let
\mathcal{J}_{2}(n,d+1)
be a family introduced in

\mathcal{W}(n,2d+1):=\tbinom{[n]}{\leq d}\cup\mathcal{J}_{2}(n,d+1),

In addition, for the case

,

\mathcal{W}^{*}(n,6):=\tbinom{[n]}{\leq 2}\cup\{F\in\tbinom{[n]}{3}:F\cap[3]\neq\emptyset\}\cup\bigl\{H\in\tbinom{[n]}{4}:[3]\subseteq H\bigr\}

and

\mathcal{W}^{**}(n,6):=\tbinom{[n]}{\leq 2}\cup\{F\in\tbinom{[n]}{3}:F\cap[2]\neq\emptyset\}\cup\bigl\{H\in\tbinom{[n]}{4}:[2]\subseteq H\bigr\}.

For the case
s=7
, we also have two exceptional families as below

\mathcal{W}^{*}(n,7):=\tbinom{[n]}{\leq 3}\cup\{H\in\tbinom{[n]}{4}:\{2,3\}\subseteq H\}\cup\{H\in\tbinom{[n]}{4}:1\in H,H\cap\{2,3\}\neq\emptyset\}

\mathcal{W}^{**}(n,7):=\tbinom{[n]}{\leq 3}\cup\{H\in\tbinom{[n]}{4}:\{2,3,4\}\subseteq H\}\cup\{H\in\tbinom{[n]}{4}:1\in H,H\cap\{2,3,4\}\neq\emptyset\}.

In the following, we shall characterize the
s

(Main result)

\mathcal{F}\nsubseteq\mathcal{K}(n,s)

Preliminaries

2.1
Stabilities for intersecting families

We say that an intersecting family
\mathcal{F}
is

trivial
if all its members share a common element.
In other words,
an intersecting family is called trivial if it is a subfamily of a full star.
Erdős, Ko and Rado
[
5
]
asked for the maximum size of
a nontrivial intersecting family of
k
-element subsets of
[n]
.
In 1967, Hilton and Milner
[
19
]
answered this question
by proving the following result.
We denote by

\mathcal{EKR}(n,k)
the family of all

k
-element subsets of
[n]

containing a fixed element.

Theorem 2.1
(Hilton–Milner
[
19
]
)

Let
k\geq 2
and
n\geq 2k
be integers and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq{[n]\choose k}
be
an intersecting family.
If
\mathcal{F}\nsubseteq\mathcal{EKR}(n,k)
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-1\choose k-1}-{n-k-1\choose k-1}+1.

Moreover, for
n>2k
, the equality holds if and only if

\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to

\begin{matrix}\mathcal{HM}(n,k):=\Bigl\{G\in{[n]\choose k}:1\in G,G\cap[2,k+1]\neq\emptyset\Bigr\}\cup\Bigl\{[2,k+1]\Bigr\}.\end{matrix}

or in the case of
k=3
,
there is one more possibility, namely

\begin{matrix}\mathcal{T}(n,3):=\left\{F\in{[n]\choose 3}:|F\cap[3]|\geq 2\right\}.\end{matrix}

In 2017, Han and Kohayakawa
[
18
]

determined the maximum size
of a non-trivial intersecting uniform family that
is not a subfamily of the Hilton–Milner family.
To proceed, we introduce some notation and define families
\mathcal{J}_{i}(n,k)

and
\mathcal{G}_{i}(n,k)
.
Let
k\geq 3
,
i\leq k-1
and
n>2k
be positive integers.
For any
(k-1)
-element set
E\subseteq[n]
,
any
(i+1)
-element set
J\subseteq[n]\setminus E
,
x_{0}\in J
and
J_{i}=J\setminus\{x_{0}\}
, we define

\begin{matrix}\mathcal{J}_{i}(n,k):=\left\{G\in{[n]\choose k}:x_{0}\in G,G\cap(E\cup\{j\})\neq\emptyset~\text{for each $j\in J_{i}$}\right\}\cup\Bigl\{E\cup\{j\}:j\in J_{i}\Bigr\}.\end{matrix}

We next define the family
\mathcal{G}_{i}(n,k)
.
Suppose now that
i\in[2,k]
,

x_{0}\in[n]
and
E\subseteq[n]\setminus\{x_{0}\}
is an

i
-element set.
We define the

k
-uniform family
\mathcal{G}_{i}(n,k)

as

\begin{matrix}\mathcal{G}_{i}(n,k):=\left\{G\in{[n]\choose k}:E\subseteq G\right\}\cup\left\{G\in{[n]\choose k}:x_{0}\in G,G\cap E\neq\emptyset\right\}.\end{matrix}

The result of Han and Kohayakawa
[
18
]

can be stated as below.

Theorem 2.2
(Han–Kohayakawa
[
18
]
)

Let
k\geq 3
,
n>2k
and
\mathcal{H}
be an intersecting
k
-uniform
family of subsets of
[n]
. If
\mathcal{H}\nsubseteq\mathcal{EKR}(n,k)
and
\mathcal{H}\nsubseteq\mathcal{HM}(n,k)
,
and if
k=3
,
\mathcal{H}\nsubseteq\mathcal{G}_{2}(n,3)
, then

|\mathcal{H}|\leq{n-1\choose k-1}-{n-k-1\choose k-1}-{n-k-2\choose k-2}+2.

For
k=4
, the equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{J}_{2}(n,4)
,
\mathcal{G}_{2}(n,4)
or

\mathcal{G}_{3}(n,4)
; for every other
k
, equality holds if and only if

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{J}_{2}(n,k)
.

For more stability results on uniform intersecting families,
we refer the interested readers to the recent papers
[
26
,
22
,
28
]
.
The following lemma was provided Katona
[
23
]
; see
[
11
]

for a detailed proof.

Lemma 2.3
(See
[
23
,
11
]
)

If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
has
s
-union property, then
for every
i\in[0,s/2]
,

|\mathcal{F}_{i}|+|\mathcal{F}_{s+1-i}|\leq{n\choose i}.

(1)

Moreover, for

2.2
The shifting operation and the lexicographic order

The remainder of this section is devoted to a useful operation of families
used in this article and a lemma related to lexicographic order.
Let us recall the definition of the
(i,j)
-shift
S_{i,j}
.
Given a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
,
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
, we define

S_{i,j}(\mathcal{F})=\{S_{i,j}(F):F\in\mathcal{F}\},

where

S_{i,j}(F)=\begin{cases}F^{\prime}:=(F\setminus\{j\})\cup\{i\},&\text{if $j\in F,i\notin F$
and $F^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{F}$};\\
F,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This operation was introduced
by Erdős,
Ko and Rado
[
5
]
and is now
an important technique in extremal set theory;
see the comprehensive book
[
14
]
.
From the definition, we know that

|S_{i,j}({F})|=|{F}|

and
|S_{i,j}(\mathcal{F})|=|\mathcal{F}|
.
We say that
\mathcal{F}
is
left-shifted
if

S_{i,j}(\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{F}
for all

1\leq i<j\leq n
.
The following fact is frequently used in this paper.

Fact 2.4

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a left-shifted family
and
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
and

\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}

be two sets such that

a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}
and
b_{1}<\cdots<b_{k}
.
If
a_{i}\leq b_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k

and
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}\in\mathcal{F}
,
then
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}\in\mathcal{F}
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be two families
of subsets of
[n]
.
We say that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}

are
cross-intersecting
if

A\cap B\neq\emptyset
for any
A\in\mathcal{A}
and

B\in\mathcal{B}
.
Recently, the research on cross-intersecting families has attracted extensive attention;
see, e.g.,
[
15
,
31
]
.
It is well-known that applying the left-shifting operation
S_{i,j}

on two cross-intersecting families
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
,
the resulting two families are still cross-intersecting.

Fact 2.5
(See
[
5
]
)

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be families
of subsets of
[n]
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be
t
-intersecting.
If
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are cross-intersecting,
then
S_{i,j}(\mathcal{A})
and
S_{i,j}(\mathcal{B})
are also cross-intersecting.
Moreover,
S_{i,j}(\mathcal{A})
is also
t
-intersecting.

The following lemma follows from Fact
2.5
.

Lemma 2.6
(See
[
5
]
)

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq{[n]\choose a}

and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq{[n]\choose b}

cross-intersecting families and let
\mathcal{A}
be
t
-intersecting.
Then there exist left-shifted families
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subseteq{[n]\choose a}

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subseteq{[n]\choose b}
such that all of
the following hold.

(i)

|\mathcal{A}|=|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|
and
|\mathcal{B}|=|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|
;

(ii)

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
are cross-intersecting;

(iii)

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is
t
-intersecting.

Finally, let us define the lexicographic on the

k
-element subsets of
[n]
.
We say that
F
is smaller than
G

in the lexicographic order, denoted by

, if
\min(F\setminus G)<\min(G\setminus F)
holds.
For example,
\{1,2,3\}\prec\{1,3,4\}
.
Let
k\in[0,n]
and
m\in[0,{n\choose k}]
be positive integers.
We denote by

\mathcal{L}(n,k,m)
the family of the smallest
m
sets
from
{[n]\choose k}
in the lexicographic order.
Let
a,b,n
be positive integers with
n>a+b
.
Hilton
[
20
]
observed that

\mathcal{A}\subseteq{[n]\choose a}

and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq{[n]\choose b}
are cross-intersecting
if and only if

\mathcal{A}\cap\Delta_{a}(\mathcal{B}^{c})=\emptyset
,
where
\mathcal{B}^{c}=\{[n]\setminus B:B\in\mathcal{B}\}

denotes the family of complements of sets of
\mathcal{B}
.
This observation together with the

[

,

]

Let

and

3
A result for cross-intersecting families

The cross-intersecting property is a
natural extension on the intersecting property.
In this section,
we shall prove some important properties
of pairs of cross-intersecting families.

Theorem 3.1

Let
d\geq 3
and
n\geq 2d+1
be positive integers.
Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq{[n]\choose d+1}
and

\mathcal{B}\subseteq{[n]\choose d}
be cross-intersecting families.
If
|\mathcal{A}|\geq 2
and
\mathcal{A}
is
2
-intersecting, then

|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|\leq{n\choose d}-{n-d-1\choose d}-{n-d-2\choose d-1}+2.

For
n\geq 2d+2
, the above equality holds if and only if, under the isomorphism,
\mathcal{A}=\{[d+1],[d]\cup\{d+2\}\}
and
\mathcal{B}=\{B\in{[n]\choose d}:B\cap[d]\neq\emptyset~\text{or}~\{d+1,d+2\}\subseteq B\}
;
or two more possibilities when
d=3
, namely,

\mathcal{A}=\{A\in{[n]\choose 4}:[3]\subseteq A\}
and
\mathcal{B}=\{B\in{[n]\choose 3}:B\cap[3]\neq\emptyset\}
; or

\mathcal{A}=\{A\in{[n]\choose 4}:[2]\subseteq A\}
and

\mathcal{B}=\{B\in{[n]\choose 3}:B\cap[2]\neq\emptyset\}
.

To prove Theorem
3.1
,
we shall present a series of lemmas.
The following lemma
states that the condition
d\geq 3
in Theorem
3.1

is necessary since the result
is not true for the case
d=2
.

Lemma 3.2

Let
n\geq 6
be an integer and let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq{[n]\choose 3}
and

\mathcal{B}\subseteq{[n]\choose 2}
be cross-intersecting families. If
|\mathcal{A}|\geq 2
and
\mathcal{A}
is
2
-intersecting, then

|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|\leq{n\choose 2}-{n-3\choose 2}+1.

Equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{A}=\{\{1,2\}\cup\{i\}:i\in[3,n]\}
and
\mathcal{B}=\{B\in{[n]\choose 2}:1\in B\>{\rm or}\>2\in B\}
under isomorphism.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is
2
-intersecting
and
n\geq 6
, by Theorem
1.2
,
we get
|\mathcal{A}|\leq n-2
.
By Lemma
2.7
,
we may assume that
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
are the collections of the smallest
|\mathcal{A}|,|\mathcal{B}|
sets
in
{[n]\choose 3},{[n]\choose 2}
with respect to the lexicographical order, respectively.
Then

\mathcal{A}=\Bigl\{\{1,2,i\}:3\leq i\leq|\mathcal{A}|+2\Bigr\}.

Case 1.

|\mathcal{A}|=2
, that is,
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},A_{2}\}
,
where
A_{1}=\{1,2,3\}
and

A_{2}=\{1,2,4\}
.
Denote by

\mathcal{B}_{1}=\left\{B\in{[n]\choose 2}:B\cap A_{1}=\emptyset\right\},

and

\mathcal{B}_{2}=\left\{B\in{[n]\choose 2}:B\cap A_{1}=\{3\},B\cap A_{2}=\emptyset\right\}.

Since
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
are cross-intersecting,
we have

B\cap A_{i}\neq\emptyset
for both
i=1,2
and every
B\in\mathcal{B}
.
Then the families
\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}

and
\mathcal{B}
are pairwise disjoint.
Therefore

|\mathcal{B}|\leq{n\choose 2}-|\mathcal{B}_{1}|-|\mathcal{B}_{2}|={n\choose 2}-{n-3\choose 2}-(n-4)<{n\choose 2}-{n-3\choose 2}-1.

Case 2.

3\leq|\mathcal{A}|\leq n-2
.
Since
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
are cross-intersecting, we get

B\cap\{1,2\}\neq\emptyset
for each
B\in\mathcal{B}
. Then

\mathcal{B}\subseteq\left\{B\in{[n]\choose 2}:B\cap[2]\neq\emptyset\right\}
.
Hence ,we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|\leq n-2+{n\choose 2}-{n-2\choose 2}={n\choose 2}-{n-3\choose 2}+1.

Equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{A}=\{\{1,2\}\cup\{i\}:i\in[3,n]\}
and
\mathcal{B}=\{B\in{n\choose 2}:1\in B\>{\rm or}\>2\in B\}
under isomorphism.
Now we consider an arbitrary family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq{[n]\choose 3}
with
|\mathcal{A}|=n-2
.
Since
\mathcal{A}
is 2-intersecting, Theorem
1.2
implies
either
\mathcal{A}={T\choose 3}
for some
T\in{[n]\choose 4}
or

\mathcal{A}
is isomorphic to
\{\{1,2\}\cup\{i\}:i\in[3,n]\}
.
So assume that
\mathcal{A}={T\choose 3}
for
some
T\in{[n]\choose 4}
, which together with
|\mathcal{A}|=n-2

yields
n=6
.
Then
\mathcal{B}\subseteq{T\choose 2}
since
\mathcal{A}
and

\mathcal{B}
are cross-intersecting.
Then
|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|\leq{4\choose 3}+{4\choose 2}=10<{6\choose 2}-{3\choose 2}+1=13
.
So the extremal family is unique up to isomorphism.

Lemma 3.3

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer.
If
\mathcal{A}\subseteq{[2d+1]\choose d+1}
and

\mathcal{B}\subseteq{[2d+1]\choose d}
are cross-intersecting,
then

|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|\leq{2d+1\choose d}.

Proof.

For every
F\in{[2d+1]\choose d+1}
,
we denote
F^{c}=[2d+1]\setminus F
.
Since
\mathcal{A}
and

\mathcal{B}
are cross-intersecting,
we have
F\notin\mathcal{A}
or
F^{c}\notin\mathcal{B}
.
Thus, at most half of the sets in
{[2d+1]\choose d+1}\cup{[2d+1]\choose d}
belong to
\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}
.
Then

|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|\leq{1\over 2}\left({2d+1\choose d+1}+{2d+1\choose d}\right)={2d+1\choose d}.

This completes the proof.

The following result due to Frankl and Tokushige
[
8
]
is needed for our purpose.
We remark that a generalization was recent proved by Frankl and Wang
[
16
]
.

Lemma 3.4
(See
[
8
]
)

If
\mathcal{A}\subseteq{[n]\choose a}
and

\mathcal{B}\subseteq{[n]\choose b}
are non-empty
cross intersecting families with
n\geqslant a+b
and
a\leqslant b
,
then

|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|\leqslant{n\choose b}-{n-a\choose b}+1.

For
n>a+b
, the equality holds if and only if

\mathcal{A}=\{A\}

and
\mathcal{B}=\{B\in{[n]\choose b}:B\cap A\neq\emptyset\}
or for
(a,b)=(2,2)
, there is one more possible family

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}=\{S\in{[n]\choose 2}:1\in S\}
.

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.
First let us apply induction on
n\geq 2d+1
and
d\geq 3
.
For the base case
n=2d+1
, the result holds by Lemma
3.3
.
Assume that
n\geq 2d+2
and the result holds for integers less than
n
.
By Lemma
2.6
, we may assume that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are left-shifted.
Recall that

\mathcal{A}(n):=\{A\setminus\{n\}:n\in A\in\mathcal{A}\}
and

\mathcal{A}(\overline{n}):=\{A\in\mathcal{A}:n\notin A\}
.
For the family
\mathcal{B}
,
we define
\mathcal{B}(n)
and
\mathcal{B}(\overline{n})
similarly.

Claim 1.

|\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})|\geq 2
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{A}(n)=\emptyset
, then
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})
and the claim holds
since
|\mathcal{A}|\geq 2
.
If
\mathcal{A}(n)\neq\emptyset
,
there exists
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
n\in A
.
Since
n\geq 2d+2
and
\mathcal{A}
is left-shifted,
there exist two different elements

u,v\in[n]\setminus A
such that
(\mathcal{A}\setminus\{n\})\cup\{u\},(\mathcal{A}\setminus\{n\})\cup\{v\}\in\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})
.
Thus the claim holds.

We can easily see that
\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})
and
\mathcal{B}(\overline{n})
are cross-intersecting, and
\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})

is
2
-intersecting.
Note that
\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})\subseteq{[n-1]\choose d+1}

and
\mathcal{B}(\overline{n})\subseteq{[n-1]\choose d}
.
By applying the induction hypothesis, we have

|\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})|+|\mathcal{B}(\overline{n})|\leq{n-1\choose d}-{n-d-2\choose d}-{n-d-3\choose d-1}+2.

(2)

In the sequel, we shall proceed in three cases:
\mathcal{A}(n)=\emptyset
,
|\mathcal{A}(n)|=1
and

|\mathcal{A}(n)|\geq 2
.

Case 1.

\mathcal{A}(n)=\emptyset
.

Claim 1 gives
|\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})|\geq 2
.
Let
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})

and
x\in[n-1]
such that

x\in A_{1}\setminus A_{2}
.
We denote

\mathcal{B}_{1}=\left\{B\in{[n-1]\choose d-1}:B\cap A_{1}=\emptyset\right\},

and

\mathcal{B}_{2}=\left\{B\in{[n-1]\choose d-1}:B\cap A_{1}=\{x\},B\cap A_{2}=\emptyset\right\}.

Clearly, we have
|\mathcal{B}_{1}|={n-d-2\choose d-1}
and

|\mathcal{B}_{2}|\geq{n-d-3\choose d-2}
.
Note that
\mathcal{B}(n)
and
\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})
are cross-intersecting, and then
\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}
and

\mathcal{B}(n)
are pairwise disjoint. Therefore, we get

|\mathcal{B}(n)|\leq{n-1\choose d-1}-|\mathcal{B}_{1}|-|\mathcal{B}_{2}|\leq{n-1\choose d-1}-{n-d-2\choose d-1}-{n-d-3\choose d-2}.

(3)

Note that the equality in (
3
) holds if and only if

\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})=\{A_{1},A_{2}\}
with

|A_{1}\cap A_{2}|=d
and
\mathcal{B}(n)=\{B\in{[n-1]\choose d-1}:B\cap A_{1}\neq\emptyset~\text{and}~B\cap A_{2}\neq\emptyset\}

Combining the inequalities (
2

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|=|\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})|+|\mathcal{B}(\overline{n})|+|\mathcal{B}(n)|\leq{n\choose d}-{n-d-1\choose d}-{n-d-2\choose d-1}+2.

The equality holds if and only if both equalities
in (
2
) and (

Hence, equality in (
4

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})=\{A_{1},A_{2}\}
with

and
\mathcal{B}=\bigl\{B\in{[n]\choose d}:B\cap A_{1}\neq\emptyset~\text{and}~B\cap A_{2}\neq\emptyset\bigr\}
.

Case 2.

|\mathcal{A}(n)|=1
, that is, there exists
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
n\in A
.

For each
i=d+1,\ldots,n
, we denote

A_{i}=\{1,2,\ldots,d,i\}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\{A_{d+1}
,
A_{d+2}
,
\ldots
,

.
Since
\mathcal{A}

n\in A\in\mathcal{A}
,
we get
A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
for each

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})
.
Note that
\mathcal{A}
and

\mathcal{B}
are cross-intersecting,
which implies that

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{B}(n)

Since
n\geq 2d+2

|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|\geq d+1
.
Then
B\cap[d]\neq\emptyset
for every
B\in\mathcal{B}(n)
.
So

|\mathcal{B}(n)|\leq{n-1\choose d-1}-{n-d-1\choose d-1}.

(5)

The equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{B}(n)=\bigl\{B\in{[n-1]\choose d-1}:B\cap[d]\neq\emptyset\bigr\}

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})
.

Firstly, we assume that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})
.
Since
|\mathcal{A}(n)|=1

\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{A}=\{[d]\cup\{i\}:i\in[d+1,n]\}

Then
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\{B\in{[n]\choose d}:B\cap[d]\neq\emptyset\}

Hence

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(n-d)+{n\choose d}-{n-d\choose d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{n\choose d}-{n-d-1\choose d}-{n-d-2\choose d-1}+2+\left(n-d-2-{n-d-2\choose d-2}\right)

\displaystyle{n\choose d}-{n-d-1\choose d}-{n-d-2\choose d-1}+2.

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})|+|\mathcal{B}(\overline{n})|+|\mathcal{A}(n)|+|\mathcal{B}(n)|

\displaystyle{n\choose d}-{n-d-1\choose d}-{n-d-2\choose d-1}+2.

|\mathcal{A}(n)|\geq 2
.

\mathcal{A}(n)

2

\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{A}(n)

Let us apply the induction on

(7)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})|+|\mathcal{B}(\overline{n})|+|\mathcal{A}(n)|+|\mathcal{B}(n)|

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{n\choose 3}-{n-4\choose 3}-{n-5\choose 2}+2.

.

\{1,2,n-1,n\}\in\mathcal{A}
.

\mathcal{A}
is 2-intersecting.
Note that each set

and

\mathcal{B}=\{B\in{[n]\choose 3}:B\cap[2]\neq\emptyset\}
under the isomorphism.

So assume that

Note that
\mathcal{A}(n)\subseteq{[n-1]\choose d}
and

\mathcal{B}(n)\subseteq{[n-1]\choose d-1}

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}(n)|+|\mathcal{B}(n)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{n-1\choose d-1}-{(n-1)-(d-1)-1\choose d-1}-{(n-1)-(d-1)-2\choose d-2}+2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{n-1\choose d-1}-{n-d-1\choose d-1}-{n-d-2\choose d-2}+2,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}(\overline{n})|+|\mathcal{B}(\overline{n})|+|\mathcal{A}(n)|+|\mathcal{B}(n)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{n\choose d}-{n-d-1\choose d}-{n-d-2\choose d-1}+2+\left(2-{n-d-2\choose d-2}-{n-d-3\choose d-3}\right)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle{n\choose d}-{n-d-1\choose d}-{n-d-2\choose d-1}+2,

and

5
Concluding remarks

For two subsets
A
and
B
,
the symmetric difference of
A,B
is defined as

A\triangle B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)=(A\cup B)\setminus(A\cap B).

The distance of
A
and
B
is defined as

d(A,B)=|A\triangle B|
.
The family of all subsets
with the binary function
d
forms a metric space.
The diameter of a family
\mathcal{F}
is defined as
the maximum distance of pairs of sets in
\mathcal{F}
.
Observe that
|F\cup F^{\prime}|\leq s

implies
d(F,F^{\prime})\leq s
. However, the reverse is
not true.
In 1966, Kleitman
[
25
]
extended
Katona’s Theorem
1.4
to the families with given diameter.

Theorem 5.1
(Kleitman
[
25
]
)

Suppose that
n>s\geq 2
and

\mathcal{F}
is a family of subsets of
[n]
with diameter at most
s
,
that is,
d(F,F^{\prime})\leq s
for all distinct sets
F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
.

(1)

If
s=2d
for some
d\geq 1
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\sum_{0\leq i\leq d}{n\choose i}
.

(2)

If
s=2d+1
for some
d\geq 1
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\sum_{0\leq i\leq d}{n\choose i}+{n-1\choose d}
.

In 2017, Frankl

12

attaining the upper bound, and he
also established a stability result on Kleitman’s theorem,
that is, a diameter version of Theorem
1.5
.
Inspired by these results, we would like to propose the following problem
