Nonlocal balance equation: representation and approximation of solution
Yurii Averboukh

Abstract
We study a nonlocal balance equation that describes the evolution of a system consisting of infinitely many identical particles those move along a deterministic dynamics and can also either disappear or give a spring. In this case, the solution of the balance equation is considered in the space of nonnegative measures. We prove the superposition principle for the examined nonlocal balance equation. Furthermore, we interpret the source/sink term as a probability rate of jumps from/to a remote point. Using this idea and replacing the deterministic dynamics of each particle by a nonlinear Markov chain, we approximate the solution of the balance equation by a solution of a system of ODEs and evaluate the corresponding approximation rate. This result can be used for construction of numerical solutions of the nonlocal balance equation. MSC Classification (2020): 35R06, 70F45, 60J27. Keywords: balance equation, system of particles, superposition principle, Markov approximation.

Introduction

The paper is concerned with the study of a nonlocal balance equation, where it is assumed that the source/sink term describes the probability rate for a particle to disappear or give a spring. Therefore, the examined equation takes the form:

\partial_{t}m(t)+\operatorname{div}(f(t,x,m(t))m(t))=g(t,x,m(t))m(t).

(1)

It is endowed with the initial condition

m(0)=m_{0}.

Here,
m(t)
is a measure on the phase space that describes a distribution of particles. Within the framework of particle interpretation of the nonlocal balance equation, for each time
t
and measure
m
,
x\mapsto f(t,x,m)
is a velocity field, while
g(t,x,m)
provides a probability rate for a particle to disappear or to give a spring at the point
x
while the distribution of all particles at time
t
is
m
. Such form of the nonlocal balance equation is, in particular, used within the study of model of opinion dynamics with time-varying weights
[
14
,
22
]
.

The nonlocal balance equation is a natural extension of the nonlocal continuity equation. The latter was examined in several works (see
[
13
,
18
,
16
]
and reference therein) and finds various applications in the analysis of pedestrian flows, opinion dynamics, etc
[
8
,
25
,
21
,
1
]
. Notice that balance equation (
1
) becomes a continuity equation if we simply put
g\equiv 0
. In this case, the total quantity of elements of the system is preserved, i.e., the solution is a flow in the space of probability measures.
The general balance equation implies that the right-hand side is an arbitrary signed measure. Its solution is considered on the space of signed measures. This is due to the fact that, if the negative part of this right-hand side is singular w.r.t. the measure variable, the solution has also a negative part
[
18
, Theorem 7.1.1]
. The existence and uniqueness results for general balance equation were discussed in
[
23
,
26
,
27
]
. Additionally, papers
[
27
,
12
]
provide the sensitivity analysis of solution to the general balance equation. Furthermore, let us mention papers
[
15
,
9
]
where systems of balance laws were examined.

The main results of the paper are the following. First, we derive the superposition principle that represents a solution of balance equation (
1
) through a distribution on the space of curves with time-varying weights (see Theorem
4.3
). The second result is the conservation form of the balance equation. Here we rewrite the balance equation as an equation on a flow of probability measures with the right-hand side given by a Lévy-Khinchine generator acting on an augmented space (see Theorem
5.2
). The third result is an approximation of the solution of the original nonlocal balance equation by a solution of a system of ODEs. Here, we assume that the velocity filed
f
and the initial measure
m_{0}
are supported on a compact set
\mathcal{K}
. The approximating system of ODEs takes the form

\frac{d}{dt}\beta(t)=\beta(t)Q(t,\beta(t))+\beta(t)G(t,\beta(t)).

(2)

Here,

•

given
t\in[0,T]
,
\beta(t)
is a row-vector indexed by elements of some finite set
\mathcal{S}
,

•

for each time
t
and vector
\beta
,
G(t,\beta)
are
Q(t,\beta)
are matrices indexed alsp by elements of
\mathcal{S}
;

•

the matrix
G(t,\beta)
is diagonal;

•

the matrix
Q(t,\beta)
is Kolmogorov (we call a matrix Kolmogorov if it has nonnegative entries outside the diagonal whereas the sum of entries on each row is zero).

In the case where the set
\mathcal{S}
is close to the compact
\mathcal{K}
and the matrix

approximates the velocity field on the set
\mathcal{K}
(see condition
(QS1)
–
(QS3)
), we evaluate in Theorem

the distance between the measure
m(t)
and the empirical distribution
\sum_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}}\beta_{{x}}(t)\delta_{{x}}
for each time
t
(here
\delta_{{x}}
stands for the Dirac measure concentrated at
x
). Additionally, we give the way how one can construct a set
\mathcal{S}
and a matrix

to derive an arbitrary small approximation rate (see Proposition
7.3
). The matrix

is entirely determined by the function
g
and the set
\mathcal{S}
. Notice that, applying a numerical scheme to system of ODEs (
2
), one can obtain a numerical solution of nonlocal balance equation (
1
) with the controlled accuracy.

Let us briefly comment the main results.

The superposition principle obtained in the paper is a counterpart of the famous superposition principle for the continuity equation
[
3
,
5
,
28
,
10
,
4
]
. The latter states the equivalence between the Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions of systems of identical particles on an Euclidean space. Notice that for the linear balance equation the superposition principle was obtained previously in
[
11
,
20
]
. The key ingredient in the superposition principle obtained in the paper for nonlocal balance equation (
1
) that is nonlinear is an analog of the evaluation operator that assigns to a measure on the space of trajectories with varying weights a measure on the phase space describing the instantaneous distribution of particles. The latter takes into account not only a position occupied by a particle but also its weight.

Further, recall that the natural phase space for the nonlocal balance equation is the space of nonnegative measures. We endow this space with the distance first proposed by Piccoli and Rossi (see
[
23
,
24
,
26
,
25
]
) that also works for signed measures. It generalizes the famous Wasserstein distance.
Thus, we call this metric a PRW-distance (PRW-metric).
It turns out that the PRW-distance between two nonnegative measures on a Polish space can be computed using the standard Wasserstein distance between probability measures obtained by adding a mass to a remote point and normalization (see Section
2
for the details).

The concept of remote point gives an interpretation of the source/sink term. An appearance of a particle is viewed as a jump from this remote point, while a disappearance is a jump to the remote point. Writing down operators those describe these jump processes, we arrive at the conservation form of the nonlocal balance equation.

Due to fact that the
Q
is a Kolmogorov matrix, system (
2
) describes an evolution of a distribution in an infinite particle system where each particle can jump on the set
\mathcal{S}
or disappear/give a spring. Adding to the set
\mathcal{S}
the remote point, we derive the conservation form of (
2
) that determines a continuous-time Markov chain acting on the set
\mathcal{S}
augmented by the remote point (see Proposition
6.2
). Here, we, as above, view a disappearance of a particle as a jump to the remote point, while an appearance of a particle is regarded as a jump from the remote point. Notice that the approximation of the deterministic evolution with a controlled accuracy by a Markov chain was previously developed for mean field type systems (in this case, the evolution is given by a continuity equation) in
[
7
]
and for zero-sum differential games in
[
6
]
. The main novelty of the case examined in the paper is the presence of jumps from/to the remote point with the intensities depending on current distributions. Thus, to evaluate the approximation rate, we propose a synchronization of these jump processes in the original and approximating systems.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we give general notation and recall the notion of PRW-distance proposed in
[
23
,
26
]
. Moreover, this Section provides an equivalent form of the PRW-distance as a normalized usual Wasserstein distance between probabilities on a space augmented by a remote point. The equilibrium distribution of the curves with weights is discussed in Section
3
. Here, we, in particular, prove the existence and uniqueness result for the equilibrium distribution of curves with weights. In the next section, we derive the superposition principle for the examined nonlocal balance equation. Further, we rewrite the balance equation in the conservation form (see Section
5
). In Section
6
, we introduce an approximating system of ODEs. Finally, we evaluate the approximation rate in Section
7
.

Proof.

Let

In this section, we examine the equilibrium distribution of curves with time-varying weights satisfying the dynamics

\begin{split}&\frac{d}{dt}x(t)=f(t,x(t),\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t}),\\
&\frac{d}{dt}w(t)=g(t,x(t),\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t})w(t).\end{split}

(14)

The initial distribution is assumed to be the same as for balance equation (
1
) and equal to
m_{0}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
C>0
. We say that
\eta\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(\Gamma_{T}^{C})
is an equilibrium distribution of the weighted trajectories provided that

•

\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{0}=m_{0}
;

•

\eta
-a.e. curves
(x(\cdot),w(\cdot))
satisfy (
14
) and the initial condition

.

In the following, we impose the following conditions on

and

(A1)

(A2)

and

(A3)

and

and

(A4)

and

,

Theorem 3.2
.

For each
T>0
,
C>C_{1}\triangleq\|m_{0}\|e^{TC_{g}}
, there exists a unique equilibrium distribution of weighted trajectories lying in
\mathcal{P}^{1}(\Gamma_{T}^{C})

Proof.

First, let us define

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{T}\triangleq\Big\{(x(\cdot),w(\cdot))\in\Gamma_{T}^{C_{1}}:x(\cdot)\in\operatorname{Lip}_{C_{f}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d}),\,w(\cdot)\in\operatorname{Lip}_{C_{g}C_{1}}([0,T];\mathbb{R})\Big\}.

Furthermore, we denote

{P}\triangleq\Big\{\eta\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{T}):\ \ e_{0}\sharp\eta=(\operatorname{Id},\|m_{0}\|)(\|m_{0}\|^{-1}m)\Big\}.

Hereinafter,
e_{t}
stands for the evaluation operator form
\Gamma_{T}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,+\infty)
that assigns to each
\gamma\in\Gamma_{T}
its value at time
t
, i.e.,
e_{t}(\gamma)=\gamma(t)
.

The definition of the set
{P}
gives, in particular, that, for
\eta\in{P}
and
\eta
-a.e.
\gamma\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{T}
, one has

\|\gamma\|\leq TC_{f}+C_{1}+\|\gamma(0)\|.

Therefore, probabilities from
{P}
are uniformly integrable. The assumption
{P}\subset\mathcal{P}^{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{T})
, definition of
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{T}
and the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem give that
{P}
is tight. Therefore, we have that
{P}
is compact in the topology produced by the metric
W_{1}

[
3
, Proposition 7.1.5]
.

If
\eta\in{P}
, then denote by
\operatorname{traj}_{\eta}(x_{0})
the operator that assigns to
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the pair of functions
(x(\cdot),w(\cdot))
that satisfies

\begin{split}&\frac{d}{dt}x(t)=f(t,x(t),\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t}),\ \ x(0)=x_{0};\\
&\frac{d}{dt}w(t)=g(t,x(t),\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t})w(t),\ \ w(0)=\|m_{0}\|.\end{split}

Further, set

\Phi(\eta)\triangleq\operatorname{traj}_{\eta}\sharp(\|m_{0}\|^{-1}m_{0}).

(15)

By construction,
\Phi(\eta)\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma_{T})
. Further,

\lfloor{\Phi(\eta)}\rfloor_{0}=m_{0}.

Notice that
\eta
is a equilibrium distribution of curves with time-varying weights if and only if

\Phi(\eta)=\eta.

Let us show that the operator
\Phi
defined by (
15
) maps
{P}
into itself.
To this end, we choose
(x(\cdot),w(\cdot))=\operatorname{traj}_{\eta}(x_{0})
. For each
s,r\in[0,T]
, one has that

\|x(r)-x(s)\|\leq\int_{s}^{r}\|f(t,x(t),\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t})\|dt.

This and condition

yield that

Further, since

, we, using the Gronwall’s inequality, conclude that

|w(t)|\leq C_{1}
. Additionally, for
s,r\in[0,T]
,

|w(r)-w(s)|\leq\int_{s}^{r}w(t)|g(t,x(t),\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t})|dt\leq C_{g}C_{1}|r-s|.

Thus,
w(\cdot)\in\operatorname{Lip}_{C_{g}C_{1}}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
. Therefore, we have that
\operatorname{traj}_{\eta}(x_{0})\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{T}
, while by construction

e_{0}\sharp(\Phi(\eta))=(\operatorname{Id},\|m_{0}\|)(\|m_{0}\|^{-1}m).

This means that
\Phi(\eta)\in{P}
.

Now we prove that
\Phi
is a continuous mapping on
{P}
. Let
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{T})
and let
(x_{1}(\cdot),w_{1}(\cdot))=\operatorname{traj}_{\eta_{1}}(x_{0})
,
(x_{2}(\cdot),w_{2}(\cdot))=\operatorname{traj}_{\eta_{2}}(x_{0})
. We have that

\|x_{1}(t)-x_{2}(t)\|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|f(t^{\prime},x_{1}(t^{\prime}),\lfloor{\eta_{1}}\rfloor_{t^{\prime}})-f(t^{\prime},x_{2}(t^{\prime}),\lfloor{\eta_{2}}\rfloor_{t^{\prime}})\|.

Using the Lipschitz continuity and Proposition
2.5
, we obtain the estimate

\|x_{1}(t)-x_{2}(t)\|\leq\int_{0}^{t}C_{Lf}\|x_{1}(t^{\prime})-x_{2}(t^{\prime})\|dt+C_{2}W_{1}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2}).

Here
C_{Lf}
is a Lipschitz constant for the function
f
, while
C_{2}=C_{Lf}C_{1}
. By the Gronwall’s inequality, we conclude that

\|x_{1}(\cdot)-x_{2}(\cdot)\|\leq C_{3}W_{1}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2}).

(16)

Here
C_{3}
is a constant. Furthermore,

\begin{split}|w_{1}(t)-w_{2}(t)|&\leq\int_{0}^{t}|w_{1}(t^{\prime})g(t^{\prime},x_{1}(t^{\prime}),\lfloor{\eta_{1}}\rfloor_{t^{\prime}})-w_{2}(t^{\prime})g(t^{\prime},x_{2}(t^{\prime}),\lfloor{\eta_{1}}\rfloor_{t^{\prime}})|\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{t}\big[|w_{1}(t^{\prime})-w_{2}(t^{\prime})|\cdot|g(t^{\prime},x_{1}(t^{\prime}),\lfloor{\eta_{1}}\rfloor_{t^{\prime}})|\\
&{}\hskip 50.0pt+|w_{2}(t)|\cdot|g(t^{\prime},x_{1}(t^{\prime}),\lfloor{\eta_{1}}\rfloor_{t^{\prime}})-g(t^{\prime},x_{2}(t^{\prime}),\lfloor{\eta_{2}}\rfloor_{t^{\prime}})|\big]dt^{\prime}.\end{split}

Using the facts that

is bounded by
C_{g}
and Lipschitz continuous, the inequality

and estimate (
16
), we have that

|w_{1}(t)-w_{2}(t)|\leq C_{g}\int_{0}^{t}|w_{1}(t^{\prime})-w_{2}(t^{\prime})|dt^{\prime}+C_{4}W_{1}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2}).

This and the Gronwall’s inequality give the following estimate for some constant

:

|w_{1}(\cdot)-w_{2}(\cdot)|\leq C_{5}W_{1}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2}).

(17)

Now, to estimate

, we consider the plan between
\Phi(\eta_{1})
and
\Phi(\eta_{2})
defined by the rule:

\tilde{\pi}\triangleq(\operatorname{traj}_{\eta_{1}},\operatorname{traj}_{\eta_{2}})\sharp\pi,

where
\pi
is an optimal plan between
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
. From (
16
) and (
17
), we conclude that

W_{1}(\Phi(\eta_{1}),\Phi(\eta_{2}))\leq C_{6}W_{1}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2}),

where

is a constant determined only on
f
,
g
,
\|m_{0}\|
and
T
. This gives the continuity of
\Phi
. Therefore, by the Schauder fixed point theorem (see
[
2
, 17.56 Corollary]
), the mapping
\Phi:{P}\rightarrow{P}
admits a fixed point
\eta^{*}
. By construction of
\Phi
, we have that
\eta^{*}
is an equilibrium distribution of curves with weights. Furthermore, since

, the following estimate holds true:

\|\lfloor{\eta^{*}}\rfloor_{t}\|\leq C_{1}=\|m_{0}\|e^{C_{g}t}.

Now, let us show that the equilibrium distribution of the weighted curves is unique. Let
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
be two such distributions. This means that

\eta_{1}=\Phi(\eta_{1}),\ \ \eta_{2}=\Phi(\eta_{2}).

For each
t

\varrho(t)\triangleq\mathcal{W}_{1,b}(\lfloor{\eta_{1}}\rfloor_{t},\lfloor{\eta_{2}}\rfloor_{t}).

Now let
(x_{1}(\cdot,x_{0}),w_{1}(\cdot,x_{0}))=\operatorname{traj}_{\eta_{1}}(x_{0})
,
(x_{2}(\cdot,x_{0}),w_{2}(\cdot,x_{0}))=\operatorname{traj}_{\eta_{2}}(x_{0})
.
We have that

\begin{split}\|x_{1}(t,&x_{0})-x_{2}(t,x_{0})\|\\
&\leq\|x_{1}(0)+x_{2}(0)\|+\int_{0}^{t}(C_{Lf}\|x_{1}(t^{\prime},x_{0})-x_{2}(t^{\prime},x_{0})\|+C_{2}\varrho(t^{\prime}))dt^{\prime}.\end{split}

This and the Gronwall’s inequality imply that

\|x_{1}(t,x_{0})-x_{2}(t,x_{0})\|\leq C_{7}\int_{0}^{t}\varrho(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

(18)

Using the same arguments, one can show that

|w_{1}(t,x_{0})-w_{2}(t,x_{0})|\leq C_{8}\int_{0}^{t}\varrho(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

(19)

Now, notice that

\begin{split}\mathcal{W}_{1,b}(\lfloor{\eta_{1}}\rfloor_{t},\lfloor{\eta_{2}}\rfloor_{t})\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\|&x_{1}(t,x_{0})-x_{2}(t,x_{0})\|\\
&+b|w_{1}(t,x_{0})-w_{2}(t,x_{0})|)\|m_{0}\|^{-1}m_{0}(dx_{0}).\end{split}

From this, (

), (

), we conclude that

\varrho(t)=\mathcal{W}_{1,b}(\lfloor{\eta_{1}}\rfloor_{t},\lfloor{\eta_{2}}\rfloor_{t})\leq C_{9}\int_{0}^{t}\varrho(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

Using the Gronwall’s inequality once more, we deduce that

Plugging this equality back into (

. Using the definition of the operator

(see (

∎

4
Superposition principle for the nonlocal balance equation

First, let us recall the definition of the weak solution to balance equation (
1
).

Definition 4.1
.

We say that a continuous flow of measures
m(\cdot):[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a weak solution of balance equation (
1
) if, for each
\phi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
s\in[0,T]
, the following equality holds:

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(s,&x)m(s,dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(0,x)m_{0}(dx)\\
&=\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big[\partial_{t}\phi(t,x)+\langle\nabla\phi(x),f(t,x,m(t))\rangle+\phi(x)g(t,x,m(t))\big]m(t,dx)dt.\end{split}

Remark 4.2
.

Using the same methods as in
[
3
, Lemma 8.1.2]
, one can prove that
m(\cdot)
is a weak solution of (
1
) if and only, for every
\phi\in C^{1}_{0}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big[\partial_{t}\phi(t,x)+\langle\nabla\phi(x),f(t,x,m(t))\rangle+\phi(x)g(t,x,m(t))\big]m(t,dx)dt=0.

The main result of this section is the following superposition principle. There,
C>C_{1}
, where
C_{1}
is introduced in Theorem
3.2
.

Theorem 4.3
.

If
\eta\in\Gamma_{T}^{C}
is an equilibrium distribution of curves with time-varying weights, then
m(t)\triangleq\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t}
is a weak solution of nonlocal balance equation (
1
) on
[0,T]
. Conversely, if
m(\cdot)
is a weak solution of (
1
) satisfying the initial condition
m(0)=m_{0}
, then there exists an equilibrium distribution of curves with time-varying weights
\eta
such that
\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t}=m(t)
. In particular, there exists a unique solution of the initial value problem for balance equation (
1
).

Remark 4.4
.

As we mentioned in the Introduction, papers
[
23
,
26
,
27
]
contain the existence and uniqueness results for the balance equation with the right-hand side given by an arbitrary signed measure. However, they, in particular, assume that the source/sink term is uniformly continuous w.r.t. to the measure variable. This assumption is not directly applicable for the examined balance equation (
1
).

The proof of Theorem
4.3
relies on the following uniqueness result.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
v:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
z:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be continuous w.r.t. time variable. Additionally, assume that
v
is Lipschitz continuous w.r.t.
x
. Then, the initial value problem for the linear balance equation:

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}m(t)+\operatorname{div}(v(t,x)m(t))=z(t,x)m(t),\\
&m(0)=m_{0}\end{split}

(20)

allows at most one weak solution.

This lemma was proved in
[
20
, Lemma 3.5]
. However, for the sake of completeness, we give its proof in Appendix
A
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

First, let us prove the necessity part.

Let
\eta
be an equilibrium distribution of weighted curves. By Proposition
2.6
, the mapping
t\mapsto\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t}
is continuous. Now, let
\phi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The definition of the operation
\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t}
gives that, for every
s\in[0,T]
,

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi&(s,x)\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{s}(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(0,x)\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{0}(dx)\\
&=\int_{\Gamma_{T}^{C}}\big[\phi(s,x(s))w(s)-\phi(0,x(0))w(0)\big]\eta(d(x(\cdot),w(\cdot)))\\
&=\int_{\Gamma_{T}^{C}}\int_{0}^{s}\big[(\partial_{t}\phi(t,x(t))+\langle\phi(t,x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\rangle)w(t)\\
&{}\hskip 140.0pt+\phi(t,x(t))\dot{w}(t)\big]dt\eta(d(x(\cdot),w(\cdot)))\\
&=\int_{\Gamma_{T}^{C}}\int_{0}^{s}\Big[(\partial_{t}\phi(t,x(t))+\langle\phi(t,x(t)),f(t,x(t),\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t})\rangle)w(t)\\
&{}\hskip 120.0pt+\phi(t,x(t))g(t,x(t),\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t})w(t)\Big]dt\eta(d(x(\cdot),w(\cdot)))\\
&=\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big[(\partial_{t}\phi(t,x)+\langle\phi(t,x),f(t,x,\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t})\rangle)+\phi(t,x)g(t,x,\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t})\Big]\lfloor{\eta}\rfloor_{t}(dx)dt.\end{split}

Therefore, the flow of measures

is a weak solution of nonlocal balance equation (
1
).

To prove the sufficiency part, given
m(\cdot)
that is a weak solution of balance equation (

), we construct a distribution of weighted curves
\eta
corresponding to the linear balance equation (

) with

By the necessity part of the theorem, the mapping

solves (

4.5
yields that

.
∎

5

In this section, we rewrite balance equation (

) as a differential equation on a flow of probability measures defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}

\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the remote point
\star
introduced in §

Notice that, due to Theorem
3.2
and equation (

), we can assume that
m(\cdot)
is such that
\|m(t)\|\leq R
, where

Denoting

provides the isomorphism between
\mathcal{M}^{1}_{R}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\})=\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|\mu(dx)<\infty\}
. Notice that the inverse mapping is
\mu\mapsto R\mu|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, where
\mu|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
denotes the restriction of the probability
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e., for each
\Upsilon\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Recall that by (
5
) and Proposition
2.2
, for each two measures

Now, for

,

,

, put

f_{R}(t,x,\mu)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f(t,x,R\mu|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
0,&x=\star,\end{array}\right.

g_{R}(t,x,\mu)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}g(t,x,R\mu|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
0,&x=\star.\end{array}\right.

In the Introduction, we mentioned that the function
g
combines probability rates for the particle to disappear or to give a spring. Thus, it is convenient to distinguish the positive and negative parts of
g
:

g^{+}(t,x,m)\triangleq g(t,x,m)\vee 0,\ \ g^{-}(t,x,m)\triangleq(-g(t,x,m))\vee 0.

Similarly,

g^{+}_{R}(t,x,\mu)\triangleq g_{R}(t,x,\mu)\vee 0,\ \ g^{-}_{R}(t,x,\mu)\triangleq(-g_{R}(t,x,\mu))\vee 0.

Given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\})
,
m\triangleq R\cdot(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sharp\mu)
, the quantity

Rg^{+}_{R}(t,y,\mu)(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sharp\mu)(dy)\Delta t=g^{+}(t,y,m)m(dy)\Delta t

approximately describes the distribution of newly appeared particles on the time interval
[t,t+\Delta t]
, while
g^{-}_{R}(t,x,\mu)\Delta t+o(\Delta t)
is the probability of the particle that occupies the state
x
at time
t
to disappear on the time interval
[t,t+\Delta t]
.

For each
t\in[0,T]
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}
,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\})
, let
\nu^{-}(t,x,\mu,\cdot)
be a finite measure on
\{0,1\}
such that

\nu^{-}(t,x,\mu,\{1\})=g^{-}_{R}(t,x,\mu),\ \ \nu^{-}(t,x,\mu,\{0\})=0.

(21)

Further, denote by
\nu^{+}(t,x,\mu,\cdot)
the measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}
defined by the rule: for
\phi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\})
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}}\phi(y)\nu^{+}(t,m,\star,dy)\triangleq\mu^{-1}(\{\star\})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(y)g^{+}_{R}(t,y,\mu)\mu(dy)

(22)

and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}}\phi(y)\nu^{+}(t,x,\mu,dy)=0

(23)

when
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

With some abuse of notation, we denote by
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\})
the set of functions
\phi
from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}
to
\mathbb{R}
such that the restriction of
\phi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
lies in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Let us consider the following generator on
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\})
:

\begin{split}L_{t}[\mu]\phi(x)\triangleq\langle f_{R}&(t,x,\mu),\nabla\phi(x)\rangle\\
&+\int_{\{0,1\}}(\phi(x+u(\star-x))-\phi(x))\nu^{-}(t,x,\mu,du)\\
&+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}}(\phi(y)-\phi(x))\nu^{+}(t,x,\mu,dy).\end{split}

(24)

Now let us consider the following equation

\frac{d}{dt}\mu(t)=L_{t}^{*}[\mu(t)]\mu(t),

(25)

where
L_{t}^{*}[\mu]
is the operator adjoint to
L_{t}[\mu]
.

The solution of (
25
) is considered in the space of probability measures. In particular, it preserves the total mass equal to 1.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that
\mu(\cdot):[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\})
solve (
25
) provided that, for each
\phi\in C^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\})
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}}\phi(x)\mu(t,dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}}\phi(x)\mu(0,dx)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}}L_{\tau}[\mu(\tau)]\phi(x)\mu(\tau,dx)d\tau.

Theorem 5.2
.

A flow of probabilities
\mu(\cdot)
is a solution of the equation (
25
)
if and only if
m(\cdot)
defined by the rule
m(t)\triangleq R\mu(t)|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
satisfies balance equation (
1
). In particular, for each
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\})
, there exists a unique solution to equation (
25
) with the initial condition
\mu(0)=\mu_{0}\triangleq R^{-1}(m_{0}\triangleright_{R})
.

Proof.

Choose
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\})
and set
m\triangleq R\mu|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. Notice that each function
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\})
can be expressed in the form

\phi(x)=\tilde{\phi}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(x)+c\mathbbm{1}_{\{\star\}}(x),

where
\tilde{\phi}
is
C^{1}
function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, while
c
is a constant.
Therefore, due to the definitions of functions
f_{R}
,
g_{R}
and measures
\nu^{-}
,
\nu^{+}
, we obtain that

\begin{split}L_{t}[\mu]\phi(x)=\langle f(t,x,m),\tilde{\phi}(x)\rangle+(c&-\tilde{\phi}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(x))g^{-}(t,x,m)\\
+R^{-1}\mu^{-1}(\{\star\})&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\tilde{\phi}(y)-c)g^{+}(t,y,m)m(dy)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\star\}}(x).\end{split}

Thus, due to the fact that
g(t,x,m)=g^{+}(t,x,m)-g^{-}(t,x,m)
, we have the equality

\begin{split}R\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}}L_{t}[\mu]&\phi(x)\mu(dx)\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle&f(t,x,m),\tilde{\phi}(x)\rangle m(dx)\\
&+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{\phi}(x)g(t,x,m)m(dx)+c\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(t,x,m)m(dx).\end{split}

(26)

Now let
\mu(\cdot)
solve equation (
25
). We define
m(t)\triangleq R(\mu(t)|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
.
Using this and (
26
) with
c=0
, we conclude that
m(\cdot)
satisfies (
1
).

To prove the converse statement, we let
\mu_{0}\triangleq R^{-1}(m(0)\triangleright_{R})
and find
\mu(\cdot)
solving (
25
) with the initial condition
\mu(0)=\mu_{0}
. Therefore,
t\mapsto R(\mu(t)|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
is a solution of balance equation (
1
). The uniqueness result for this equation gives that
m(t)=R(\mu(t)|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
.

∎

We complete this section with the mean field particle system interpretation of the balance equation.

Remark 5.3
.

It is natural to say that a 5-tuple
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P},X)
is a mean field particle system representation of balance equation (
1
) provided that

•

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space with filtration;

•

X
is a
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
-adapted stochastic process with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\}
;

•

for every
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\{\star\})
, the process

\phi(X(t))-\int_{0}^{t}L_{\tau}[\mu(\tau)]\phi(X(\tau))d\tau

is a
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
-martingale such that

\mu(t)=X(t)\sharp\mathbb{P}.

(27)

Indeed, one can regard the stochastic process
X
as a dynamics of a sampling particle, while the probability
\mu(t)
gives a distribution of all particles in time
t
.

By construction, we have that
\mu(\cdot)
defined by (
27
) satisfies (
25
), while
m(\cdot)
defined by the rule
m(t)=R\mu(t)|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
solves (
1
).

One can show that there exists at least one particle system representation of (
1
). The accurate proof of this fact goes beyond the scope of the paper. The scheme of the proof follows the way introduced in
[
19
]
. We assume that we are given with the flow of probabilities
\mu(\cdot)
that solves (
27
). First, we discretize the dynamics and approximate the drift part of the dynamics
f_{R}(t,x,\mu)
by a Kolmogorov matrix. Then, we obtain a continuous time Markov chain. The corresponding stochastic process is uniformly stochastically continuous
[
19
, Theorem 5.4.1]
. Finally, one can argue as in
[
19
, Theorem 5.4.1]
and show that, when the discretization parameter tends to zero, the continuous time Markov chains converge. The limit provides the desired particle system representation.

6
Approximating system of ODEs

In this section, we introduce a system of ODEs that arises when we approximate the drift term
f
by a Kolmogorov matrix.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The fineness of
\mathcal{S}
is evaluated by a number

d(\mathcal{S})\triangleq\min\big\{\|{x}-{y}\|:\,{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S},\,{x}\neq{y}\big\}.

the diameter of
\mathcal{S}
is

\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{S})=\max\big\{\|{x}-{y}\|:\,{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S},\,{x}\neq{y}\big\}.

A signed measure on
\mathcal{S}
is always determined by a sequence
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}=(\beta_{{x}})_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}})\subset\mathbb{R}
. We denote the set of such sequences by
{l}_{1}(\mathcal{S})
. It is endowed with the norm

\|\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\|\triangleq\sum_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}}|\beta_{{x}}|.

We will assume that each such sequence is a row-vector. Further, the set of elements of
{l}_{1}(\mathcal{S})
with nonnegative entries is denoted by
{l}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{S})
. This space inherits the metric from
{l}_{1}(\mathcal{S})
.

The mapping

\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\mapsto{I}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}})\triangleq\sum_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}}\beta_{{x}}\delta_{{x}}

provides the isomorphism between
{l}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{S})
and

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S})
.

Recall that, on
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S})
, we consider the metric
\mathcal{W}_{1,b}
.

The distance between images w.r.t. the isomorphism
{I}
is evaluated in the following statement which is proved in the Appendix
B
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
b\geq\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{S})
. Then, the following estimates hold true:

b^{-1}\mathcal{W}_{1,b}({I}(\beta^{1}),{I}(\beta^{2}))\leq\|\beta^{1}-\beta^{2}\|\leq(d(\mathcal{S}))^{-1}\mathcal{W}_{1,b}({I}(\beta^{1}),{I}(\beta^{2})).

(28)

Now let us introduce a system of ODEs indexed by elements of
{l}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{S})
. In Section
7
we will show that it approximates original nonlocal balance equation (
1
).
To this end, for
{x}\in\mathcal{S}
, set

\hat{g}_{{x}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})\triangleq g(t,{x},{I}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}})).

Further, let
Q(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})=(Q_{{x},{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}}))_{{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S}}
be a Kolmogorov matrix, i.e.,
for each
{x}\in\mathcal{S}
,
Q_{{x},{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})\geq 0
when
{x}\neq{y}
, whereas

\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}Q_{{x},{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})=0.

The approximating system takes the following form:

\frac{d}{dt}\beta_{{y}}(t)=\sum_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}}\beta_{{x}}(t)Q_{{x},{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(t))+\beta_{{y}}(t)\hat{g}_{{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(t)).

Since we assume that
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}=(\beta_{{x}})_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}}
is a row-vector, one can rewrite this system in the vector form:

\frac{d}{dt}\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(t)=\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(t)Q(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(t))+\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(t)G(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(t)).

(29)

Here, we denote by
G(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})=(G_{{x},{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}}))_{{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S}}
the diagonal matrix determined by the rule:

G_{{x},{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\hat{g}_{{x}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}}),&{x}={y},\\
0,&{x}\neq{y}.\end{array}\right.

Due to the classical existence and uniqueness theorem for ODEs, system (
29
) has a unique solution for every initial condition.

To rewrite equation (
29
) in the conservation form, we first extend the phase space to
\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}
and, for each
t\in[0,T]
,
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\in{l}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{S})
, define the matrix
\mathcal{Q}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})=(\mathcal{Q}_{{x},{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}}))_{{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}
by the rule:

\mathcal{Q}_{{x},{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Q_{{x},{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}}),&{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S},\,{x}\neq{y}\\
\hat{g}^{-}_{{x}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}}),&{x}\in\mathcal{S},\,{y}=\star,\\
(R-\|\beta\|)^{-1}\hat{g}^{+}_{{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})\beta_{{y}},&{x}=\star,\,{y}\in\mathcal{S},\\
Q_{{x},{x}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})-\hat{g}^{-}_{{x}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}}),&{x}={y}\in\mathcal{S},\\
-(R-\|\beta\|)^{-1}\sum_{{z}\in\mathcal{S}}g^{+}_{{z}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})\beta_{{z}},&{x}={y}=\star.\end{array}\right.

(30)

One can directly check that
\mathcal{Q}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})
is a Kolmogorov matrix.

Further, we denote the set of sequences of nonnegative numbers indexed by elements of
\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}
by
{l}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})
. As above,
{I}
is the isomorphism between
{l}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})
and
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})
given by the rule: for
\beta_{\mathcal{S},\star}=(\beta_{{x}})_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}

{I}(\beta_{\mathcal{S},\star})\triangleq\sum_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}\beta_{{x}}\delta_{{x}}.

If
\beta_{\mathcal{S},\star}=(\beta_{{x}})_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}\in{l}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})
,
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}
is its restriction on
\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{Q}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S},\star})\triangleq\mathcal{Q}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}}).

Due to the definition of the matrix
\mathcal{Q}
(see (
30

then
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot)\triangleq(\beta_{{x}}(\cdot))_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}}
solves (
29
).

Now let us introduce the description of system (
29
) through the generator technique. As above, we will use the probability measures. Notice that each
\beta_{\mathcal{S},\star}
corresponds to the probability measure

If
t\in[0,T]
,
{x}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}
,

, then

\begin{split}L^{Q}_{t}[\mu]\phi({x})\triangleq\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}[&\phi({y})-\phi({x})]Q_{{x},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu|_{\mathcal{S}}))\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}}({x})\\
&+[\phi(\star)-\phi({x})]\hat{g}_{{x}}^{-}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu|_{\mathcal{S}}))\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}}({x})\\
&+\mu^{-1}(\{\star\})\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}[\phi(y)-\phi(\star)]\hat{g}_{{y}}^{+}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu|_{\mathcal{S}}))\mu(\{{y}\})\mathbbm{1}_{\{\star\}}({x}).\end{split}

(31)

Using the definitions of the measures of the
\nu^{-}
and
\nu^{+}
(see (

\begin{split}L^{Q}_{t}[\mu]\phi({x})\triangleq\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}[\phi({y})-\phi({x})]&Q_{{x},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu|_{\mathcal{S}}))\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\\
+\int_{\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}[\phi(x&+u(\star-x))-\phi({x})]\nu^{-}(t,{x},\mu,du)\\
&+\int_{\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}[\phi(y)-\phi(x)]\nu^{+}(t,{x},\mu,dy).\end{split}

The conservation form of (

) is the equation

\frac{d}{dt}\mu(t)=L_{t}^{Q,*}[\mu(t)]\mu(t).

(32)

Here
L_{t}^{Q,*}[\mu]
stands for the operator adjoint to

.

If

Rate of approximation of balance equation

7.1
Formulation of the approximation theorem

For simplicity, we assume that the balance equation operates on some compact. This means that we impose the following condition.

(A5)

There exists a compact
\mathcal{K}
such that
\operatorname{supp}(m_{0})\subset\mathcal{K}
and, for each
t\in[0,T]
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{K}
,
m\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K})
,

f(t,x,m)=0.

The following property directly follow from the imposed conditions.

Proposition 7.1
.

Assume conditions
(A1)
–
(A5)
. If
m(\cdot)
is a solution to balance equation (
1
) with the initial condition
m(0)=m_{0}
, then, for every
t\in[0,T]
,

\operatorname{supp}(m(t))\subset\mathcal{K}.

It suffices to define the functions
f_{R}
,
g^{+}
,
g^{-}
,
g_{R}+
,
g_{R}^{-}
and the measure
\nu^{-}
only for
x\in\mathcal{K}
. Furthermore,
\nu^{+}(t,\star,\mu,\cdot)
is now a measure on
\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\}
defined by the rule: for
\phi\in C_{b}(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})
,

\int_{\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\}}\phi(y)\nu^{+}(t,m,\star,dy)\triangleq\mu^{-1}(\{\star\})\int_{\mathcal{K}}\phi(y)g^{+}_{R}(t,y,\mu)\mu(dy).

(34)

Finally,
L
takes the form

\begin{split}L_{t}[\mu]\phi(x)\triangleq\langle f_{R}&(t,x,\mu),\nabla\phi(x)\rangle\\
&+\int_{\{0,1\}}(\phi(x+u(\star-x))-\phi(x))\nu^{-}(t,x,\mu,du)\\
&+\int_{\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\}}(\phi(y)-\phi(x))\nu^{+}(t,x,\mu,dy).\end{split}

The approximation result relies the following conditions on
\mathcal{S}
and
Q
: there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

(QS1)

for each
x\in\mathcal{K}
,

\min_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}\|x-{y}\|\leq\varepsilon;

(QS2)

for every
t\in[0,T]
,
{x}\in\mathcal{S}
,
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\in{l}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{S})
,

\Bigg\|f(t,{x},{I}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}}))-\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}({y}-{x})Q_{{x},{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})\Bigg\|\leq\varepsilon;

(QS3)

for every
t\in[0,T]
,
{x}\in\mathcal{S}
,
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\in{l}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{S})
,

\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}\|x-y\|^{2}Q_{{x},{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

It is reasonable to assume that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small. Without loss of generality, we will consider the case where
\varepsilon\leq 1
. Additionally, we assume that
b>\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{K})+1\geq\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{S})
.

Theorem 7.2
.

Assume that conditions
(QS1)
–
(QS3)
are in force. Given
c>0
, there exists a constant
\widehat{C}>0
determined by functions
f
,
g
and the constant
c
such that, if

•

\beta_{0}\in{l}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{S})
,
\|\beta_{0}\|_{1}\leq c
;

•

m_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K})
,
\|m_{0}\|\leq c
;

•

\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot):[0,T]\rightarrow{l}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{S})
solves (
29
) with the initial condition
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(0)=\beta_{0}
;

•

m(\cdot):[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K})
satisfies (
1
) and the initial condition
m(0)=m_{0}
,

then

\mathcal{W}_{1,b}(m(t),{I}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(t)))\leq\widehat{C}(\varepsilon+\mathcal{W}_{1,b}(m_{0},{I}(\beta_{0})).

This theorem is proved in §
7.3
. The proof relies on an auxiliary construction of the generator on
(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}
introduced in
\lx@sectionsign
7.2
.

Before, we turn to this auxiliary construction, let us present an example of the system that approximates balance equation (
1
) with arbitrary small accuracy.

Let
h>0
,
K_{h}\triangleq K+B_{h}
,

\mathcal{S}^{h}\triangleq\mathcal{K}_{h}\cap h\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

(35)

Here,
B_{h}
is a closed ball centered in the origin of the radius
h
,
\mathbb{Z}
states for the set of integers. Further, consider the coordinate-wise representation of the function
f

f(t,x,m)=(f_{1}(t,x,m),\ldots,f_{d}(t,x,m)).

Put

Q^{h}_{{x},{y}}(t,\beta_{\mathcal{S}})\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}h^{-1}|f_{i}(t,{x},{I}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}}m))|,&\begin{array}[]{l}{x}\in\mathcal{K},\\
\ \ {y}={x}+he_{i}\operatorname{sgn}(f_{i}(t,x,{I}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}}))),\end{array}\\
-h^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|f_{i}(t,{x},{I}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}}))|,&{x}={y}\in\mathcal{K},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(36)

Hereinafter,
e_{i}
stands for the
i
-th coordinate vector, while
\operatorname{sgn}
denotes the sign function defined by the rule:

\operatorname{sgn}(a)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&a>0,\\
-1,&a<0,\\
0,&a=0.\end{array}\right.

Proposition 7.3
.

The lattice
\mathcal{S}^{h}
and the Kolmogorov matrix defined by (

) and (

(QS1)
–

.

Proof.

The proof mimes one given in
[

]
. Condition

We have that

7.2
Coupled dynamics

We choose a constant
R
such that

R>ce^{C_{g}T}.

Notice that, due to Theorem
3.2
and equation (
29
), we can assume that
m(\cdot)
and
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot)
defined in Theorem
7.2
are such that
\|m(t)\|<R
,
\|\beta_{\mathcal{S}}(t)\|<R
.

The key idea of the proof of Theorem
7.2
is to construct a generator
\Lambda^{Q}
such that, if
\vartheta(\cdot):[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{P}^{1}((\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}))
solves

\frac{d}{dt}\vartheta(t)=\Lambda^{Q,*}_{t}[\vartheta(t)]\vartheta(t),

(37)

then the flows of probabilities
\mu_{1}(\cdot)
and
\mu_{2}(\cdot)
defined by the rule:

\mu_{i}(t)\triangleq\operatorname{p}^{i}\sharp\vartheta(t),\ \ i=1,2

are solutions of (
25
) and (
32
) respectively. Hereinafter,
\Lambda^{Q,*}_{t}[\vartheta]
is an operator adjoint to
\Lambda^{Q}_{t}[\vartheta]
. Notice that
\vartheta(t)
is a plan between
\mu_{1}(t)
and
\mu_{2}(t)
. We will evaluate
\int_{(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\vartheta(t,d(x_{1},x_{2}))
. This together with the link between distance on
\mathcal{P}^{1}(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})
and metric
\mathcal{W}_{1,b}
will give the proof of Theorem
7.2
.

The generator
\Lambda^{Q}
will describe the behavior of the couple of particles where the first particle can move on
\mathcal{K}
, while the second one walks randomly on
\mathcal{S}
. Additionally, both particles can jump to and from the remote point
\star
. The probability rates of these synchronized jumps will be given by Lévy measures
\chi^{-}
and
\chi^{+}
. They are defined as follows.

Let
t\in[0,T]
,
x_{1}\in\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\}
,
x_{2}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}
and let
\vartheta\in\mathcal{P}((\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}))
. Denote
\mu_{1}\triangleq\operatorname{p}^{1}\sharp\vartheta
,
\mu_{2}\triangleq\operatorname{p}^{2}\sharp\vartheta
.

First, we choose the measure
\chi^{-}(t,x_{1},x_{2},\vartheta,\cdot)
to be a measure on
\{0,1\}\times\{0,1\}
satisfying

•

\chi^{-}(t,x_{1},x_{2},\vartheta,\{(1,1)\})\triangleq g^{-}_{R}(t,x_{1},\mu_{1})\wedge g^{-}_{R}(t,x_{2},\mu_{2})
;

•

\chi^{-}(t,x_{1},x_{2},\vartheta,\{(1,0)\})\triangleq g^{-}_{R}(t,x_{1},\mu_{1})-(g^{-}_{R}(t,x_{1},\mu_{1})\wedge g^{-}_{R}(t,x_{2},\mu_{2}))
;

•

\chi^{-}(t,x_{1},x_{2},\vartheta,\{(0,1)\})\triangleq g^{-}_{R}(t,x_{2},\mu_{2})-(g^{-}_{R}(t,x_{1},\mu_{1})\wedge g^{-}_{R}(t,x_{2},\mu_{2}))
;

•

\chi^{-}(t,x_{1},x_{2},\vartheta,\{(0,0)\})\triangleq 0
.

Recall that by definition
g^{-}(t,\star,\mu)\equiv 0
.

Now, let us introduce the measure
\chi^{+}(t,x_{1},x_{2},\vartheta)
. For every
\phi\in C_{b}((\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}))
, we set

\begin{split}\int_{(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})}\phi(&y_{1},y_{2})\chi^{+}(t,\star,\star,\vartheta,d(y_{1},y_{2}))\triangleq\\
(\vartheta\{(\star,\star)\})^{-1}&\int_{\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{S}}\big[\phi(y_{1},y_{2})(g^{+}_{R}(t,y_{1},\mu_{1})\wedge g^{+}_{R}(t,y_{2},\mu_{2}))\\
+\phi(y_{1},\star&)(g^{+}_{R}(t,y_{1},\mu_{1})-(g^{+}_{R}(t,y_{1},\mu_{1})\wedge g^{+}_{R}(t,y_{2},\mu_{2})))\\
+\phi(\star,y_{2}&)(g^{+}_{R}(t,y_{2},\mu_{2})-(g^{+}_{R}(t,y_{1},\mu_{1})\wedge g^{+}_{R}(t,y_{2},\mu_{2})))\big]\vartheta(d(y_{1},y_{2})).\end{split}

(38)

When
(x_{1},x_{2})\neq(\star,\star)
, we put

\chi^{+}(t,x_{1},x_{2},\vartheta,\cdot)\equiv 0.

(39)

Notice that the synchronization of two jump-type processes was proposed in
[
19
]
(see also
[
17
]
, where the synchronization of stopping time was considered).

Finally, let us define the generator
\Lambda^{Q}
by the rule: for
\phi\in C((\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})
such that
\phi
is continuously differentiable w.r.t. the first variable on
\mathcal{K}
:

\begin{split}\Lambda^{Q}_{t}[\vartheta]\phi(x_{1}&,x_{2})\triangleq\\
\langle\nabla_{x_{1}}&\phi(x_{1},x_{2}),f_{R}(t,x_{1},\operatorname{p}^{1}\sharp\vartheta)\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{1})\\
&+\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}\phi(x_{1},{y})Q_{x_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R(\operatorname{p}^{1}\sharp\vartheta)|_{\mathcal{S}}))\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}}(x_{2})\\
&+\int_{\{0,1\}\times\{0,1\}}[\phi(x_{1}+u_{1}(\star-x_{1}),x_{2}+u_{2}(\star-x_{2}))-\phi(x_{1},x_{2})]\\
&{\hskip 190.0pt}\chi^{-}(t,x_{1},x_{2},\vartheta,d(u_{1},u_{2}))\\
&+\int_{(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})}[\phi(y_{1},y_{2})-\phi(x_{1},x_{2})]\chi^{+}(t,x_{1},x_{2},\vartheta,d(y_{1},y_{2})).\end{split}

(40)

In the following, we, with some abuse of notation, denote by
C^{1}((\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}))
the set of functions
\phi:(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that the mapping
x_{1}\mapsto\phi(x_{1},x_{2})
is continuously differentiable on
\mathcal{K}
.

Definition 7.4
.

We say that a flow of probabilities
\vartheta:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{P}^{1}((\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}))
is a solution to (
37
) if, for each
\phi\in C^{1}((\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}))
, one has that

\begin{split}\int_{(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})}\phi(x_{1},x_{2})\vartheta(t,&d(x_{1},x_{2}))\\
&-\int_{(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})}\phi(x_{1},x_{2})\vartheta(0,d(x_{1},x_{2}))\\
=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})}&(\Lambda^{Q}_{\tau}[\vartheta(\tau)]\phi(x_{1},x_{2}))\vartheta(\tau,d(x_{1},x_{2}))d\tau.\end{split}

(41)

Remark 7.5
.

As above, one can consider a particle representation of the flow
\vartheta(\cdot)
. It is a 6-tuple
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P},X,X^{Q})
such that the following conditions hold true:

•

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space with filtration;

•

X
and
X^{Q}
are
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
-adapted processes taking values in
\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\}
and
\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}
respectively;

•

if
\vartheta(\cdot)
is defined by the rule
\vartheta(t)\triangleq(X(t),X^{Q}(t))\sharp\mathbb{P}
, then the process

\phi(X(t),X^{Q}(t))-\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda^{Q}_{\tau}[\vartheta(\tau)]\phi(X(\tau),X^{Q}(\tau))d\tau

is a
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
-martingale.

Notice that
X(\cdot)
describes the continuous motion, while
X^{Q}
is a Markov chain approximation. The first and second terms of the generator
\Lambda^{Q}
provide the independent evolution on
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{S}
respectively. Simultaneously, the measure
\chi^{-}
gives the coordinated jumps from
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{S}
to the remote point
\star
. The reverse jumps are described by the measure
\chi^{+}
. It implies the jumps only if both components
X(t-)
and
X^{Q}(t-)
are in the remote point
\star
and distribute the state
X(t),X^{Q}(t)
according to the current measure
\vartheta(t)
. The way how one can construct a particle representation of a flow
\vartheta(\cdot)
is briefly discussed in Remark
6.3
.

It was mentioned above that the proof of Theorem

relies on the properties of the solution to (

Proposition 7.6
.

Given an initial measure

. If

is a solution of (

), then, at any time

is concentrated on

Proof.

Notice that such existence results are analogous to one proved in
[

,
18
]

Let
a>0
,
K^{a}\triangleq K+B_{a}
,
\mathcal{S}^{a}\triangleq K_{a}\cap a\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Further, if

,

For each

,

\begin{split}\Lambda^{a}_{t}[\vartheta^{a}]\phi(x_{1}&,x_{2})\triangleq\\

&+\int_{(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})}[\phi(y_{1},y_{2})-\phi(x_{1},x_{2})]\chi^{+}(t,x_{1},x_{2},\vartheta^{a},d(y_{1},y_{2})).\end{split}

Due to
[

, for each

\Bigg|\int_{(\mathcal{S}^{a}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})}(\Lambda^{a}_{t}\phi(x_{1},x_{2}))\vartheta^{a}(t)\Bigg|\leq C_{f}+C_{10}\|\phi\|.

\begin{split}\Bigg|\int_{(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})}\phi(&x_{1},x_{2})\vartheta^{a}(r,d(x_{1},x_{2}))\\

As above,

stands for a constant. Due to this estimate and the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality
[

]

such that

,

as

in

\vartheta(\cdot)
is a solution to (
37
). To this end, we prove that, if

•

\{\vartheta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{P}^{1}((\mathcal{S}^{a}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}))
,

•

\vartheta\in\mathcal{P}^{1}((\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}))

•

,

•

as

,

then, for each
x_{1}\in\mathcal{K}^{1}\cup\{\star\}
,
x_{2}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}
,

,

(44)

\begin{split}\vartheta\mapsto\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}\phi(&x_{1},{y})Q_{x_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R(\operatorname{p}^{1}\sharp\vartheta)|_{\mathcal{S}}))\\

,

,

,

\phi\in C^{1}(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})
, then

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\}}\phi(x)\mu_{1}(dx)=\int_{\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\}}L_{t}[\mu_{1}]\phi(x)\mu_{1}(dx).

This implies the first statement of the proposition.

The second statement is proved in the same way.
∎

7.3
Regularized distance between marginal distribution

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem
7.2
. As we mentioned above, the natural idea here is to study the evolution of the integral
\int_{(\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\})}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\vartheta(t,d(x_{1},x_{2}))
using equation (
41
). However, the distance function is not smooth. Therefore, we regularize it and evaluate the action of the generator
\Lambda^{Q}_{t}[\vartheta]
on the function

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2})\triangleq\sqrt{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}.

In the following lemmas, we gradually estimate the action of the parts of the generator
\Lambda^{Q}
on this function. These results will imply Theorem
7.2
. We start with the evaluation of the action of first two terms.

Lemma 7.8
.

For each
t\in[0,T]
,
x_{1}\in\mathcal{K}
,
{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
,
\vartheta\in\mathcal{P}((\mathcal{K}\cup\{\star\})\times(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}))
, and
\mu_{1}\triangleq\operatorname{p}^{1}\sharp\vartheta
,
\mu_{2}\triangleq\operatorname{p}^{2}\sharp\vartheta
,

\begin{split}\Bigl|\langle f_{R}(t,x_{1},\mu_{1}),\nabla_{x_{1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},{x}_{2})\rangle+\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}&\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},{y})Q_{{x}_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}}))\Bigr|\\
&\leq C_{Lf}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},{x}_{2})+RW_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}))+2\varepsilon.\end{split}

Proof.

First, we have that

\nabla_{x_{1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{x_{1}-x_{2}}{\sqrt{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}}.

(48)

Expanding the function
y\mapsto\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},y)
using the Taylor series about the point
x_{2}
, we have that

\begin{split}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},y)=\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2})&-\frac{\langle x_{1}-x_{2},y-x_{2}\rangle}{\sqrt{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}}\\
&+\frac{\|y-x_{2}\|^{2}(\|y^{\prime}-x_{1}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})-(\langle y^{\prime}-x_{1},y-x_{2}\rangle)^{2}}{2(\|y^{\prime}-x_{1}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{3/2}}.\end{split}

(49)

Here
y^{\prime}
lies in the segment
[x_{1},y]
. Since
|\langle x_{2}-x_{1},y-x_{2}\rangle|\leq\|y-x_{2}\|\cdot\|x_{2}-x_{1}\|
, we conclude that, for each
y\neq x_{2}
,

\begin{split}\Bigg|&\frac{\|y-x_{2}\|^{2}(\|y^{\prime}-x_{1}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})-(\langle y^{\prime}-x_{1},y-x_{2}\rangle)^{2}}{2(\|y^{\prime}-x_{1}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{3/2}}\Bigg|\\
&{}\hskip 30.0pt\leq\frac{\|y-x_{2}\|^{2}(\|y^{\prime}-x_{1}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})}{2(\|y^{\prime}-x_{1}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{3/2}}+\frac{|\langle y^{\prime}-x_{1},y-x_{2}\rangle|^{2}}{2(\|y^{\prime}-x_{1}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{3/2}}\leq\|y-x_{2}\|^{2}\varepsilon^{-1}.\end{split}

Using this and (
49
), we conclude that

\Bigg|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},y)-\Bigg(\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2})-\frac{\langle x_{1}-x_{2},y-x_{2}\rangle}{\sqrt{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}}\Bigg)\Bigg|\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\|y-x_{2}\|^{2}.

Since
Q(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}}))
is a Kolmogorov matrix, we derive that

\begin{split}\Bigg|\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},{y})&Q_{x_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}}))\\
&+\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S},{y}\neq x_{2}}\frac{\langle x_{1}-x_{2},y-x_{2}\rangle}{\sqrt{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}}Q_{x_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}}))\Bigg|\\
\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}&\|y-x_{2}\|^{2}Q_{x_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}})).\end{split}

Due to
(QS3)
, the right-hand side of this inequality is bounded by
\varepsilon
. Thus,

\begin{split}\Bigg|\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},{y})Q_{x_{2},{y}}&(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}}))\\
&+\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S},{y}\neq x_{2}}\frac{\langle x-x_{2},y-x_{2}\rangle}{\sqrt{\|x-x_{2}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}}Q_{x_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}}))\Bigg|\leq\varepsilon.\end{split}

(50)

The Lipschitz continuity of the function
f
gives that

\begin{split}\Bigl|\langle f_{R}(t,x_{1},\mu_{2}),\nabla_{x_{1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle+\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},&{y})Q_{x_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}}))\Bigr|\\
\leq\Bigl|\langle f_{R}(t,x_{2},\mu_{2}),\nabla_{x_{1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle+\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}&\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},{y})Q_{x_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}}))\Bigr|\\
&+C_{Lf}(\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|+RW_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})).\end{split}

(51)

Taking into account (
48
) and (
50
), we conclude that

\begin{split}\Bigl|\langle f_{R}(t,x_{1},\mu_{1}),\nabla_{x_{1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},&x_{2})\rangle+\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},{y})Q_{x_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}}))\Bigr|\\
\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}}&{}\Bigg|\Bigl\langle f_{R}(t,x_{2},\mu_{2})\\
&{}\hskip 20.0pt-\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}(y-x_{2})Q_{x_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}})),x_{1}-x_{2}\Bigr\rangle\Bigg|\\
&+C_{Lf}(\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|+RW_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}))+\varepsilon.\end{split}

Condition
(QS2)
and the definition of the function
f_{R}
yield that

\Bigg|f_{R}(t,x_{2},\mu_{2})-\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}(y-x_{2})Q_{x_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}}))\Bigg|\leq\varepsilon.

Therefore,

\begin{split}\Bigg|\langle f_{R}(t,x_{1},&\mu_{1}),\nabla_{x_{1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle+\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},{y})Q_{x_{2},{y}}(t,{I}^{-1}(R\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{S}}))\Bigg|\\
&\leq\varepsilon\frac{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|}{\sqrt{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}}+C_{Lf}(\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|+RW_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}))+\varepsilon.\end{split}

This, together with the inequality
\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\leq\sqrt{\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}=\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2})
imply the statement of the lemma.
∎

Appendix A
Uniqueness result for linear balance equation

In this section we proof Lemma
4.5
that is used in the proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

We will use the method borrowed from
[
3
, §8.1]
. First, let
m_{1}(\cdot)
,
m_{2}(\cdot)
be two solutions of (
20
). Thus, the flow of signed measures
m_{S}(\cdot)
defined by the rule

m_{S}(t)=m_{1}(t)-m_{2}(t)

also satisfies (
20
) in the weak sense with
m_{0}=0
.

If
(s,y)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then denote by
x(\cdot,s,y)
, the solution of the initial value problem

\frac{d}{dt}x(t,s,y)=v(t,x(t,s,y)),\ \ x(s,s,y)=y.

Further, let
w(\cdot,y)
satisfy

\frac{d}{dt}w(t,y)=-z(t,x(t,0,y))w(t,y),\ \ w(0,y)=1.

Notice that
w(t,y)>0
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Additionally, set

\zeta(s,y)=w(s,x(0,s,y)).

This implies that

\frac{d}{dt}\zeta(t,x(t,s,y))=-z(t,x(t,s,y))\zeta(t,x(t,s,y))

Choose an arbitrary function
\psi\in C_{c}^{1}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and set

\xi(s,y)\triangleq-\int_{s}^{T}\psi(t,x(t,s,y))(\zeta(t,x(t,s,y)))^{-1}dt.

Now let us introduce the function
\phi(s,y)\triangleq\xi(s,y)\zeta(s,y)
. Notice that
\phi(T,y)\equiv 0
. Moreover, if we define
y^{\prime}\triangleq x(0,s,y)
, then

\phi(s,y)=\phi(s,x(s,0,y^{\prime})).

Hence,

\begin{split}\partial_{s}\phi(&s,y)+\langle\nabla\phi(s,y),v(s,y)\rangle=\frac{d}{ds}\phi(s,x(s,0,y^{\prime}))\\
&=\psi(s,x(s,0,y^{\prime}))-\xi(s,x(s,0,y^{\prime}))\zeta(s,x(s,0,y^{\prime}))z(s,x(s,0,y^{\prime})).\end{split}

Due to the definitions of
y^{\prime}
and the function
\phi
, we have that

\partial_{s}\phi(s,y)+\langle\nabla\phi(s,y),v(s,y)\rangle=\psi(s,y)-\phi(s,y)z(s,y).

Therefore, using the equalities
m_{S}(0)=0
,
\phi(T,\cdot)\equiv 0
and the fact that
m_{S}(\cdot)
satisfies (
20
), we conclude that

\begin{split}0&=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[\partial_{t}\phi(t,x)+\langle\nabla\phi(t,x),v(t,x)\rangle+\phi(t,x)z(t,x)]m_{S}(t,dx)dt\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(t,x)m_{S}(t,dx)dt.\end{split}

This means that, for every
\psi\in C_{c}^{1}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(t,x)m_{S}(t,dx)dt=0.

Thus,
m_{1}(t)=m_{2}(t)
for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
.
∎

Appendix B
Distances between measures on a finite set

The aim of this appendix is to show the equivalence between distances
\|\beta^{1}-\beta^{2}\|
and
\mathcal{W}_{1,b}({I}(\beta^{1}),{I}(\beta^{2}))
stated in Section
6
.

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

First, given
\beta^{1}_{\mathcal{S}},\beta^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}\in{l}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{S})
, we denote by
\mathcal{A}(\beta^{1}_{\mathcal{S}},\beta^{2}_{\mathcal{S}})
the set of matrices
(a_{{x},{y}})_{{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}
with nonnegative entries such that, for each
{x}\in\mathcal{S}
and
y\in\mathcal{S}
,

\sum_{{y^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}a_{{x},{y^{\prime}}}=\beta^{1}_{{x}},\ \ \sum_{{x^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}a_{{x^{\prime}},{y}}=\beta^{2}_{{y}}.

From the definition of the metric
\mathcal{W}_{1,b}
and the isomorphism
\beta\mapsto{I}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}})
, it follows that

\mathcal{W}_{1,b}({I}(\beta^{1}_{\mathcal{S}}),{I}(\beta^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}))=\inf\Bigg\{\sum_{{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}\|{x}-{y}\|a_{{x},{y}}:(a_{{x},{y}})_{{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}\in\mathcal{A}(\beta^{1},\beta^{2})\Bigg\}.

Recall that, by convention, for
x\in\mathcal{K}
,
\|x-\star\|=\|\star-x\|=b
, while
\|\star-\star\|=0
.

Let us now prove the first inequality in (
28
). To this end, we consider the element
(a^{\prime}_{{x},{y}})_{{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}\subset\mathcal{A}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{1},\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2})
defined by the rule:

a^{\prime}_{{x},{y}}\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\beta_{{x}}^{1}\wedge\beta_{{x}}^{2},&{x}={y}\in\mathcal{S},\\
0,&{x}\neq{y},\,{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S},\\
\beta_{{x}}^{1}-(\beta_{{x}}^{1}\wedge\beta_{{x}}^{2}),&{x}\in\mathcal{S},\,{y}=\star,\\
\beta_{{y}}^{2}-(\beta_{{y}}^{1}\wedge\beta_{{y}}^{2}),&{x}=\star,\,{y}\in\mathcal{S}.\end{array}\right.

We have that

\begin{split}\mathcal{W}_{1,b}({I}(&\beta^{1}_{\mathcal{S}}),{I}(\beta^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}))\leq\sum_{{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}\|{x}-{y}\|a^{\prime}_{{x},{y}}\\
&=\sum_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}}b[(\beta^{1}_{{x}}-(\beta_{{x}}^{1}\wedge\beta_{{x}}^{2}))+(\beta^{2}_{{x}}-(\beta_{{x}}^{1}\wedge\beta_{{x}}^{2}))]\\
&=b\sum_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}}[(\beta_{{x}}^{1}\vee\beta_{{x}}^{2})-(\beta_{{x}}^{1}\wedge\beta_{{x}}^{2})]=b\sum_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}}|\beta_{{x}}^{1}-\beta_{{x}}^{2}|=b\|\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{1}-\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\|_{1}.\end{split}

This gives the first inequality in (
28
).

Further, for each
(a_{{x},{y}})_{{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S}}\in\mathcal{A}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{1},\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2})
, we have that

\begin{split}\sum_{{x},{y}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}\|x-y\|a_{{x},{y}}&\geq d(\mathcal{S})\sum_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\}}\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{\star\},{y}\neq{x}}a_{{x},{y}}\\
&\geq d(\mathcal{S})\sum_{{x}\in\mathcal{S}}|\beta^{1}_{{x}}-\beta^{2}_{{x}}|=d(\mathcal{S})\|\beta^{1}-\beta^{2}\|.\end{split}

Taking the minimum w.r.t. elements of
\mathcal{A}(\beta^{1},\beta^{2})
, we arrive at the second inequality in (
28
).
