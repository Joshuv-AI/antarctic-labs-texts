The Localization Method Applied to k k -Elongated Plane Partitions and Divisibility by 5
Koustav Banerjee, Nicolas Allen Smoot

Abstract
. The enumeration d k ​ ( n ) d_{k}(n) of k k -elongated plane partition diamonds has emerged as a generalization of the classical integer partition function p ⁡ ( n ) p(n) . We have discovered an infinite congruence family for d 5 ​ ( n ) d_{5}(n) modulo powers of 5. Classical methods cannot be used to prove this family of congruences. Indeed, the proof employs the recently developed localization method, and utilizes a striking internal algebraic structure which has not yet been seen in the proof of any congruence family. We believe that this discovery poses important implications on future work in partition congruences.

1.
Introduction

The theory of partition congruences has developed substantially over the last century, beginning with Ramanujan’s revolutionary congruences for the integer partition function
p(n)
. Prior to the twentieth century, the sequence
(p(n))_{n\geq 0}
was thought to be pseudorandom with respect to its divisibility properties. Ramanujan changed this perception almost overnight, with his discovery of three remarkable infinite congruence families for
p(n)
. We give these families here, with an appropriate adjustment on the powers of 7.

(1.1)

\displaystyle\text{If }n,\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}\text{ and }24n\equiv 1\pmod{\ell^{\alpha}},

\displaystyle\text{ then }p(n)\equiv 0\pmod{\ell^{\beta}},

with

(1.2)

\displaystyle\beta:=\begin{cases}\alpha&\text{ if }\ell\in\{5,11\},\\
\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\right\rfloor+1&\text{ if }\ell=7.\end{cases}

Other congruence families have been found to exist for a variety of partition functions and related arithmetic sequences which at least superficially resemble those of (
1.1
). However, the difficulty of proving such congruence families can vary substantially, with some contemporary families still resisting proof today.

The congruence family discussed in this paper was discovered by the first author, and regards the enumeration
d_{5}(n)
of
5
-elongated plane partitions of
n
. The function is part of a large class of partition functions,
d_{k}(n)
, which generalize
p(n)
, and whose properties have been closely studied by partition theorists over the last few years.

The congruence family is interesting on its own; however, the proof method is the most important aspect of our work. The classical techniques used to prove Ramanujan’s results (
1.1
) are insufficient to complete a proof. Instead, the recently developed localization method is utilized. The most critical part of the proof involves a surprising internal algebraic structure on the rational polynomials representing the individual cases of the family. It is this structure and possible analogues for other functions that we believe may lead to a deeper understanding of the theory of partition congruences, and possible resolution of standing conjectures.

1.2.
History

Much of our modern day understanding of the arithmetic properties of the integer partition function
p(n)
derives from Ramanujan’s groundbreaking work of (
1.1
) in 1919, albeit without the key modification (
1.2
).

This incredible result took decades to prove. Ramanujan himself appears to have understood the proof in the case that
\ell=5

[
9
]
. The first published proof for
\ell=5,7
appeared by Watson in 1938
[
25
]
. The case for
\ell=11
was much more resistant, and a proof was not found before Atkin’s work in 1967
[
4
]
.

This result, together with the techniques used to prove it, has stimulated a massive amount of work in partition theory. In particular, congruence families of a similar form have been found for a wide variety of more restrictive partition functions. Notably, there exists an extraordinary range of results in terms of the difficulty of proof. Some results are relatively unremarkable, and are proved regulary using the same methods that were used to prove (
1.1
) for
\ell=5,7
.

On the other hand, others are much more ambitious, and resistant to proof even today. For example, a congruence family for 2-colored Frobenius partitions modulo powers of 5 was proposed by Sellers in 1994
[
21
]
, but it was not proved until the work of Paule and Radu in 2012
[
16
]
.

Until recently, it was assumed that the genus of the underlying modular curve was responsible for the complications. For a given partition-like function
a(n)
, a typical infinite congruence family modulo powers of a given prime
\ell
will have the form

\displaystyle a(n)\equiv 0\pmod{\ell^{\beta}},

in which
n
is the inverse of some fixed positive integer modulo
\ell^{\alpha}
, and

\displaystyle\beta\rightarrow\infty\text{ as }\alpha\rightarrow\infty.

Such a family is generally associated with a sequence of modular functions
\mathcal{L}:=\left(L_{\alpha}\right)_{\alpha\geq 1}
, in which each
L_{\alpha}
effectively enumerates the congruence of
a(n)
modulo
\ell^{\alpha}
. This sequence of modular functions is in turn associated with a given compact Riemann surface
\mathrm{X}
, a classical modular curve. The topological properties of this curve have a profound impact on the difficulty in proving the associated congruence family.

In particular, it was found that for (
1.1
) with
\ell=5,7
, the associated modular curves have genus 0. On the other hand, in the case of (
1.1
) for
\ell=11
, and for the Andrews–Sellers congruences proved in
[
16
]
, the associated modular curves have genus 1. It was therefore assumed that our understanding of partition congruences when
\mathrm{X}
has genus 0 was complete. The discovery that classical techniques are not sufficient to prove all genus 0 congruences is quite recent.

Recent work done in
[
22
]
and
[
23
]
has shown that a second important topological property of
\mathrm{X}
must be taken into account—namely, the cusp count, i.e., the number of points required to properly compactify
\mathrm{X}
.

When the cusp count is 2, the associated functions
L_{\alpha}
generally have a pole at one cusp and a zero at the other. The space of functions on
\mathrm{X}
with a pole only at a single point is isomorphic to a
\mathbb{C}[x]
module with rank depending on the genus of
\mathrm{X}
. As a result, we can usually express
L_{\alpha}
as a polynomial of a finite number of basis functions which have a pole at a single cusp. Indeed, when the genus is 0,
L_{\alpha}
is expressible in terms of a single function, i.e., a Hauptmodul. This is the classical method used to prove nearly all congruence families in the past.

However, when the cusp count is greater than 2, the functions
L_{\alpha}
may have poles at multiple places. Thus, a polynomial expression of
L_{\alpha}
in terms of the basis functions at a given cusp is generally unlikely. The solution to this dilemma is to express
L_{\alpha}
as a
rational
polynomial in the basis functions, in which the denominator is governed by some multiplicative set
\mathcal{S}
.

For example, for

[

, the homomorphism was very simple, and it was only interpreted at the time as a minor idiosyncrasy on the coefficients of the corresponding

, the homomorphism is completely trivial.

We now hypothesize that this kernel structure plays a critical role in the theory of congruence families. Certainly, it allows a proof to be completed in a relatively straightforward manner, and we are extremely interested to know if this algebraic structure persists—or fails—in other examples.

\displaystyle D_{k}(q)=e^{\pi i\tau(k+1)/12}\frac{\eta(2\tau)^{k}}{\eta(\tau)^{3k+1}},

whence

is in effect a quotient of eta functions. Because of this, the function
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{k}(5n+j)q^{n}
should hold similar properties to (
2.12
). Indeed, we would like to adjust this function so that it follows a cleaner functional equation, i.e., exact symmetry for
\tau\rightarrow(a\tau+b)(c\tau+d)^{-1}
, for a large range of matrices
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}\right)
.

The subset of matrices we need are the congruence subgroups

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(N)=\Bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}):N|c\Bigg\},

for
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. Indeed,
\Gamma_{0}(N)
acts on the extended complex plane

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{H}}:=\mathbb{H}\cup\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}

by

\displaystyle\left(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},\tau\right)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}.

We express this group action by

\displaystyle\gamma\tau:=\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},

for any
\tau\in\hat{\mathbb{H}}
and any
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
. We define the orbits of this group action as the sets

\displaystyle[\tau]_{N}:=\left\{\gamma\tau:\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N)\right\}.

Definition 2.5
.

For
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, the classical modular curve of level
N
is the set of all orbits of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
applied to
\hat{\mathbb{H}}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{X}_{0}(N):=\left\{[\tau]_{N}:\tau\in\hat{\mathbb{H}}\right\}.

Most importantly, one can construct a complex structure for the modular curves
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
, and in so doing show that the curves are
Riemann surfaces
. This allows us to study meromorphic functions on
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
using most of the tools of classical complex analysis.

A description of the necessary complex structure is given in
[
11
, Chapters 2,3]
. The topology of
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
includes two important nonnegative integers: the genus, denoted
\mathfrak{g}\left(\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)\right)
, and the number of cusps, denoted
\epsilon_{\infty}\left(\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)\right)
. These can be computed according to formulae in
[
11
, Chapter 3, Sections 3.1, 3.8]
.

We note that the group action of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
can be restricted to
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}:=\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}
. Indeed, for
\tau\in\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
, we must have
[\tau]_{N}\subseteq\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
. Clearly such orbits partition
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
(in the set-theoretic sense). It can be proved that only a finite number of such orbits exist for any
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)

[
11
, Section 3.8]
.

Definition 2.6
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, the cusps of
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
are the orbits of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
applied to
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
.

To determine whether two elements of
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
are in the same cusp, we have the following
[
11
, Proposition 3.8.3]
:

Lemma 2.7
.

Two rational elements
a_{1}/c_{1},a_{2}/c_{2}
are in the same cusp of
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
if and only if there exist some
j,y\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\mathrm{gcd}(y,N)=1
and

(2.13)

\displaystyle y\cdot a_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv a_{1}+j\cdot c_{1}\pmod{N},

(2.14)

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv y\cdot c_{1}\pmod{N}.

The appeal of
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
is that we can construct meromorphic functions which are constrained by the surface’s manifold structure. With this in mind, we give the following definition of a modular function:

Definition 2.8
.

Let
f:\mathbb{H}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be holomorphic on
\mathbb{H}
. Then
f
is a modular function over
\Gamma_{0}(N)
if the following properties are satisfied for every
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
:

(1)

If
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N)
, we have
\displaystyle{f\left(\gamma\tau\right)=f(\tau)}.

(2)

We have

\displaystyle{f\left(\gamma\tau\right)=\sum_{n=n_{\gamma}}^{\infty}\alpha_{\gamma}(n)q^{n\gcd(c^{2},N)/N}},

with
n_{\gamma}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
\alpha_{\gamma}(n_{\gamma})\neq 0
. If
n_{\gamma}\geq 0
, then
f
is holomorphic at the cusp
[a/c]_{N}
. Otherwise,
f
has a pole of order
n_{\gamma}
, and principal part

(2.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=n_{\gamma}}^{-1}\alpha_{\gamma}(n)q^{n\gcd(c^{2},N)/N},

at the cusp
[a/c]_{N}
.

We refer to
\mathrm{ord}_{a/c}^{(N)}(f):=n_{\gamma}(f)
as the order of
f
at the cusp
[a/c]_{N}
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
be the set of all modular functions over
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, and
\mathcal{M}^{a/c}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)\subset\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
to be those modular functions over
\Gamma_{0}(N)
with a pole only at the cusp
[a/c]_{N}
. These are commutative algebras with 1, and standard addition and multiplication
[
17
, Section 2.1]
.

A modular function
f\in\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
induces a meromorphic function

\displaystyle\hat{f}

\displaystyle:\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}

\displaystyle:[\tau]_{N}\longrightarrow f(\tau).

In fact, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the set of meromorphic functions on
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
with poles only at the cusps and
\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
, with a matching correspondence between the function orders
[
13
, Chapter VI, Theorem 4A]
. Moreover, all possible poles for
\hat{f}
exist as a subset of
[\tau]_{N}\subseteq\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
, and (
2.15
) represents the principal part of
\hat{f}
in local coordinates near the cusp
[a/c]_{N}
.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
\mathrm{X}
be a compact Riemann surface, and let
\hat{f}:\mathrm{X}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be analytic on the entirety of
\mathrm{X}
. Then
\hat{f}
is be a constant function.

This is the most important underlying theorem in the subject, as the following corollary demonstrates:

Corollary 2.11
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, if
f\in\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
has nonnegative order at every cusp of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, then
f
is a constant.

This is a useful tool for demonstrating equivalence between modular functions. If we consider
f,g\in\mathcal{M}^{\infty}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
, in which
f
and
g
contain matching principal parts at
[\infty]_{N}
, then
f-g
, and therefore
\hat{f}-\hat{g}
, has no poles at all, making
\hat{f}-\hat{g}
(and therefore
f-g
), a constant.

2.3.
Construction of Modular Functions

We have defined the Riemann surfaces that we will work over, and whose topologies play a significant role in our work. We now wish to construct explicit modular functions, using
\eta
as a guide. The following theorem is due to Newman

Theorem 2.12

\prod_{\delta|N}\delta^{|r_{\delta}|}

An additional advantage in the use of

Finally, we give a theorem from

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{(1+5x(5\tau))^{5}}\sum_{j=0}^{4}\sum_{k=1}^{5}a_{j}(5\tau)b_{k}(5\tau)\cdot\frac{x^{m+j-5}}{(1+5x)^{n-k}}.

We multiply by
\mathcal{A}^{1-i}\cdot
, with
i=0,1
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{1-i}\cdot\frac{x^{m}}{(1+5x)^{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b_{0}(5\tau)}\sum_{k=1}^{5}b_{k}(5\tau)\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{4}a_{j}(5\tau)\cdot\mathcal{A}^{1-i}\cdot\frac{x^{m+j-5}}{(1+5x)^{n-k}}

(5.19)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{(1+5x(5\tau))^{5}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{4}\sum_{k=1}^{5}a_{j}(5\tau)b_{k}(5\tau)\cdot\mathcal{A}^{1-i}\cdot\frac{x^{m+j-5}}{(1+5x)^{n-k}}.

Remembering that

\displaystyle U_{5}(f(5\tau)\cdot g(\tau))=f(\tau)\cdot U_{5}(g(\tau)),

we can take the
U_{5}
operator of both sides of (
5.19
) and simplify:

\displaystyle U_{5}

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{A}^{1-i}\cdot\frac{x^{m}}{(1+5x)^{n}}\right)

(5.20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5}}\sum_{j=0}^{4}\sum_{k=1}^{5}a_{j}(\tau)b_{k}(\tau)\cdot U_{5}\left(\mathcal{A}^{1-i}\cdot\frac{x^{m+j-5}}{(1+5x)^{n-k}}\right).

∎

5.3.
The Action of
U^{(i)}
on
\mathbb{Z}[x]_{\mathcal{S}}

The previous lemma will be useful in proving a more direct representation of
U^{(i)}\left(x^{m}/(1+5x)^{n}\right)
. As in the cases of
\mathcal{V}^{(i)}_{n}
, we will define lower bounds on the 5-adic valuations of the numerator coefficients of
x^{r}
:

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(m,r)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{max}\left(0,\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m+2}{7}\right\rfloor-5\right),

\displaystyle\pi_{1}(m,r)

\displaystyle:=\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m}{7}\right\rfloor.

We also define a useful auxiliary function:

(5.21)

\displaystyle\phi(l):

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5l+13}{7}\right\rfloor.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
. A function
h:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
is a
discrete array
if for any fixed
(m_{1},m_{2},...,m_{n-1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
,
h(m_{1},m_{2},...,m_{n-1},m)
has finite support with respect to
m
.

Theorem 5.3
.

There exist discrete arrays
h_{1},h_{0}:\mathbb{Z}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
such that

\displaystyle U^{(1)}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+5x)^{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n}}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil m/5\right\rceil}h_{1}(m,n,r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{1}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r},

\displaystyle U^{(0)}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+5x)^{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n+6}}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m+1)/5\right\rceil}h_{0}(m,n,r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{0}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}.

Proof.

We prove the the theorem by induction over
m
and
n
. Let us suppose that Theorem
5.3
is true for all
m\leq m_{0}-1
,
n\leq n_{0}-1
for some fixed
m_{0},n_{0}\geq 5
. For convenience, as we work with the operator
U^{(i)}
, we will define
\delta:=1-i
and
\kappa:=6-6i
. We then write

\displaystyle U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+5x)^{n_{0}}}\right)

(5.22)

\displaystyle=-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5}}\sum_{j=0}^{4}\sum_{k=1}^{5}a_{j}(\tau)b_{k}(\tau)\cdot U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}+j-5}}{(1+5x)^{n_{0}-k}}\right)

\displaystyle=-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5}}\sum_{j=0}^{4}\sum_{k=1}^{5}\frac{a_{j}(\tau)b_{k}(\tau)}{(1+5x)^{5(n_{0}-k)+\kappa}}

(5.23)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+\delta)/5\right\rceil}h_{i}(m_{0}+j-5,n_{0}-k,r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0}+j-5,r)}\cdot x^{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n_{0}+\kappa}}\sum_{j=0}^{4}\sum_{k=1}^{5}w(j,k)(\tau)

(5.24)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+\delta)/5\right\rceil}h_{i}(m_{0}+j-5,n_{0}-k,r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0}+j-5,r)}\cdot x^{r},

wherein we define

\displaystyle w(j,k)(\tau):=

\displaystyle-a_{j}(\tau)b_{k}(\tau)(1+5x)^{5(k-1)}

(5.25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{30}v(j,k,l)\cdot 5^{\left\lfloor\frac{5l+j+1}{7}\right\rfloor}\cdot x^{l}.

Expanding and recombining, we have

\displaystyle U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+5x)^{n_{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n_{0}+\kappa}}\sum_{j=0}^{4}\sum_{k=1}^{5}\sum_{l=1}^{30}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+\delta)/5\right\rceil}

(5.26)

\displaystyle v(j,k,l)\cdot h_{i}(m_{0}+j-5,n_{0}-k,r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0}+j-5,r)+\left\lfloor\frac{5l+j+1}{7}\right\rfloor}\cdot x^{r+l}.

We need to verify that the coefficients of
x^{r+l}
are divisible by
5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0},r+l)}
, and that
r+l
is bounded below by
\left\lceil(m_{0}+\delta)/5\right\rceil
. On the latter matter, we note that

(5.27)

\displaystyle r+l

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+j-5+\delta}{5}\right\rceil+l

(5.28)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{5}-\frac{5-j}{5}\right\rceil+l

(5.29)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{5}\right\rceil-1+l

(5.30)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{5}\right\rceil.

Regarding the former matter, we first take
i=1
. Notice that

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{M}{7}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{N}{7}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor\frac{M+N-6}{7}\right\rfloor.

From this, we have

(5.31)

\displaystyle\pi_{1}(m_{0}+j-5,r)+\left\lfloor\frac{5l+j+1}{7}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m_{0}-j+5}{7}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{5l+j+1}{7}\right\rfloor

(5.32)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5(r+l)-m_{0}}{7}\right\rfloor

(5.33)

\displaystyle=\pi_{1}(m_{0},r).

For
i=0
, we have to more carefully expand

\displaystyle U^{(0)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+5x)^{n_{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n_{0}+6}}\sum_{j=0}^{4}\sum_{k=1}^{5}w(j,k)(\tau)

(5.34)

\displaystyle\times\Big(\sum_{\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+1)/5\right\rceil\leq r\leq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+33)/5\right\rceil-1}h_{i}(m_{0}+j-5,n_{0}-k,r)\cdot x^{r}

(5.35)

\displaystyle+\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+33)/5\right\rceil}h_{0}(m_{0}+j-5,n_{0}-k,r)\cdot 5^{\left\lfloor\frac{5r-(m_{0}+j-5)+2}{7}\right\rfloor-5}\cdot x^{r}\Big).

Expanding
w(j,k)(\tau)
again,

\displaystyle U^{(0)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+5x)^{n_{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n_{0}+6}}\sum_{j=0}^{4}\sum_{k=1}^{5}\sum_{l=1}^{30}\Big(\sum_{\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+1)/5\right\rceil\leq r\leq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+33)/5\right\rceil-1}

(5.36)

\displaystyle v(j,k,l)\cdot h_{0}(m_{0}+j-5,n_{0}-k,r)\cdot 5^{\left\lfloor\frac{5l+j+1}{7}\right\rfloor}\cdot x^{r+l}

\displaystyle+\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+33)/5\right\rceil}

(5.37)

\displaystyle v(j,k,l)\cdot h_{0}(m_{0}+j-5,n_{0}-k,r)\cdot 5^{\left\lfloor\frac{5r-(m_{0}+j-5)+2}{7}\right\rfloor-5+\left\lfloor\frac{5l+j+1}{7}\right\rfloor}\cdot x^{r+l}\Big).

For
r\geq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+33)/5\right\rceil
, we have
\pi_{0}(m_{0}+j-5,r)=\left\lfloor\frac{5r-(m_{0}+j-5)+2}{7}\right\rfloor-5\geq 0
. We find that the coefficient of
x^{r+l}
has a 5-adic valuation of at least

(5.38)

\displaystyle\left(\left\lfloor\frac{5r-(m_{0}+j-5)+2}{7}\right\rfloor-5\right)+\left\lfloor\frac{5l+j+1}{7}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5(r+l)-m_{0}+2}{7}\right\rfloor-5=\pi_{0}(m_{0},r+l).

For
\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+1)/5\right\rceil\leq r\leq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+33)/5\right\rceil-1
, we have
\pi_{0}(m_{0}+j-5,r)=0
. Let us suppose that
r+l\leq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+33)/5\right\rceil-1
. In this case, we only need the power of 5 to be 0.

On the other hand, if
r+l\geq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-5+33)/5\right\rceil
, then we have

(5.39)

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{5l+j+1}{7}\right\rfloor-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{5(r+l)-m_{0}+2}{7}\right\rfloor-5\right)

(5.40)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5l+j+36}{7}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{5(r+l)-m_{0}+2}{7}\right\rfloor

(5.41)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5(r+l)-m_{0}+2}{7}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{m-5r+34}{7}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{5(r+l)-m_{0}+2}{7}\right\rfloor

(5.42)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{m_{0}-5r+34}{7}\right\rfloor.

We need to show that
m_{0}-5r+34\geq 0
. But of course this is equivalent to

(5.43)

\displaystyle r\leq\frac{m_{0}+34}{5},

which must be true since

(5.44)

\displaystyle r\leq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+j-5+33}{5}\right\rceil-1\leq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+32}{5}\right\rceil.

Therefore in all cases for either value of
i
we have the coefficient of
x^{r+l}
divisible by
5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0},r+l)}
.

\displaystyle U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+5x)^{n_{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n_{0}+\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq 4,\\
1\leq k\leq 5,\\
1\leq l\leq 30\end{subarray}}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{5}\right\rceil}

(5.45)

\displaystyle v(j,k,l)\cdot h_{i}(m_{0}+j-5,n_{0}-k,r-l)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0}+j-5,r)+\left\lfloor\frac{5l+j+1}{7}\right\rfloor}\cdot x^{r}.

(5.46)

\displaystyle H_{i}(m,n,r):=\begin{cases}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq 4,\\
1\leq k\leq 5,\\
1\leq l\leq 30\end{subarray}}&\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m+\delta}{5}\right\rceil-1+l}\hat{H}(i,j,k,l,r),\ r\geq l\\
0,\ r<l\end{cases}

\displaystyle\hat{H}(i,j,k,l,r):=v(j,k,l)\cdot h_{i}(m+j-5,n-k,r-l)\cdot 5^{\epsilon(i,j,l,m,r)},

\displaystyle\epsilon(i,j,l,m,r):=\pi_{i}(m+j-5,r-l)+\left\lfloor\frac{5l+j+1}{7}\right\rfloor-\pi_{i}(m,r)

\displaystyle U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+5x)^{n_{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n_{0}+\kappa}}

(5.47)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{5}\right\rceil}H_{i}(m_{0},n_{0},r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0},r)}\cdot x^{r}.

∎

5.4.
An Important Inequality

We will need a more detailed understanding of the arithmetic properties of the functions
h_{i}
. With this in mind, we give the following useful inequality:

Lemma 5.4
.

For
l\geq 0
and
i\in\{0,1\}
, we have

(5.48)

\displaystyle\pi_{i}(m,r-l)+\phi(l)-\pi_{i}(m,r)\geq 1.

Proof.

Notice that for
l=0
, the inequality reduces to

(5.49)

\displaystyle\phi(0)\geq 1,

which is true since
\phi(0)=1
. We therefore consider the case in which
l>0
.

5.4.1.
The Case for
i=0

Consider first that
\pi_{0}(m,r-l),\pi_{0}(m,r)=0
. This reduces to

(5.50)

\displaystyle\phi(l)\geq 1,

which follows from
\phi(l)\geq\phi(0)\geq 1
. If
\pi_{0}(m,r-l)=0,
and
\pi_{0}(m,r)>0
, then

(5.51)

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\frac{m+33}{5}\right\rceil\text{ and }r-l\leq\left\lceil\frac{m+33}{5}\right\rceil-1=\left\lceil\frac{m+28}{5}\right\rceil,

(5.52)

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle\geq r-\left\lceil\frac{m+28}{5}\right\rceil.

Knowing this, we can rewrite

(5.53)

\displaystyle\phi(l)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5l+13}{7}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m-28+13}{7}\right\rfloor

(5.54)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m-15}{7}\right\rfloor

(5.55)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m+2}{7}\right\rfloor-5\right)+1

(5.56)

\displaystyle=\pi_{0}(m,r)+1.

If
\pi_{0}(m,r-l)>0,
and
\pi_{0}(m,r)>0
, then

(5.57)

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(m,r-l)+

\displaystyle\phi(l)

(5.58)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5(r-l)-m+2}{7}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{5l+13}{7}\right\rfloor

(5.59)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m+9}{7}\right\rfloor

(5.60)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m+2}{7}\right\rfloor+1

(5.61)

\displaystyle=\pi_{0}(m,r)+1.

5.4.2.
The Case for
i=1

We simply have

(5.62)

\displaystyle\pi_{1}(m,r-l)+

\displaystyle\phi(l)

(5.63)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5(r+l)-m}{7}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{5l+13}{7}\right\rfloor

(5.64)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m+7}{7}\right\rfloor

(5.65)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m}{7}\right\rfloor+1

(5.66)

\displaystyle=\pi_{1}(m,r)+1.

∎

5.5.
Some Congruence Properties For
h_{i}

We now state a result which will become extremely useful in the proof of our main theorem:

Theorem 5.5
.

For all
m,n,r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
i\in\{0,1\}
, we have

(5.67)

\displaystyle h_{i}(m,n,r)

\displaystyle\equiv h_{i}(m,n-5,r)\pmod{5}.

Proof.

In contrast to the proof of Theorem
5.3
, let us examine
U^{(i)}\left(x^{m}/(1+5x)^{n}\right)
while holding
m
fixed, and varying
n
through the modular equation of
1+5x
. In this case we have

\displaystyle U^{(i)}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+5x)^{n}}\right)

(5.68)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5}}\sum_{k=1}^{5}b_{k}(\tau)\cdot U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+5x)^{n-k}}\right)

(5.69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5}}\sum_{k=1}^{5}\frac{b_{k}(\tau)}{(1+5x)^{5(n-k)-\kappa}}\sum_{r\geq 1}h_{i}(m_{0},n-k,r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0},r)}\cdot x^{r}

(5.70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n+\kappa}}\sum_{k=1}^{5}\hat{w}(k)(\tau)\sum_{r\geq 1}h_{i}(m_{0},n-k,r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0},r)}\cdot x^{r},

in which we can expand

\displaystyle\hat{w}(k)(\tau):

\displaystyle=b_{k}(\tau)(1+5x)^{5(k-1)}

(5.71)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}&\displaystyle{\sum_{l=0}^{20}\hat{v}(k,l)\cdot 5^{\phi(l)}\cdot x^{l}},\ k<5\\
&\displaystyle{1+\sum_{l=1}^{20}\hat{v}(5,l)\cdot 5^{\phi(l)}\cdot x^{l}},\ k=5.\end{cases}

We can now express

\displaystyle U^{(i)}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+5x)^{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n+\kappa}}

(5.72)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq 4,\\
0\leq l\leq 20,\\
r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{5}\right\rceil\end{subarray}}\hat{v}(k,l)\cdot h_{i}(m_{0},n-k,r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0},r)+\phi(l)}\cdot x^{r+l}

(5.73)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l\leq 20,\\
r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{5}\right\rceil\end{subarray}}\hat{v}(5,l)\cdot h_{i}(m_{0},n-5,r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0},r)+\phi(l)}\cdot x^{r+l}

(5.74)

\displaystyle+\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{5}\right\rceil}h_{i}(m_{0},n-5,r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0},r)}\cdot x^{r}\Bigg).

Relabeling our powers of
x
, we have

(5.75)

\displaystyle U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+5x)^{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n+\kappa}}\times

\displaystyle\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq 4,\\
0\leq l\leq 20,\\
r\geq l+\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{5}\right\rceil\end{subarray}}\hat{v}(k,l)\cdot h_{i}(m_{0},n-k,r-l)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0},r-l)+\phi(l)}\cdot x^{r}

(5.76)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l\leq 20,\\
r\geq l+\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{5}\right\rceil\end{subarray}}\hat{v}(5,l)\cdot h_{i}(m_{0},n-5,r-l)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0},r-l)+\phi(l)}\cdot x^{r}

(5.77)

\displaystyle+\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{5}\right\rceil}h_{i}(m_{0},n-5,r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0},r)}\cdot x^{r}\Bigg)

(5.78)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n+\kappa}}

\displaystyle\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{5}\right\rceil}h_{i}(m_{0},n,r)\cdot 5^{\pi_{i}(m_{0},r)}\cdot x^{r}.

Notice that
\pi_{i}(m,r-l)+\phi(l)\geq\pi_{i}(m,r)+1

. Therefore, if we divide out

, we must have

\displaystyle h_{i}(m,n,r)

\displaystyle\equiv h_{i}(m,n-5,r)\pmod{5}.

∎

By explicitly computing
h_{1}(m,n,r)
for any explicit

, and 5 consecutive values of

, as we have done in our Mathematica supplement, we have the following result:

Corollary 5.6
.

For all

, with

we have the following componentwise matrix congruence:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}(2,n,1)&h_{1}(3,n,1)&h_{1}(4,n,1)&h_{1}(5,n,1)&0&0&0\\
0&0&h_{1}(4,n,2)&h_{1}(5,n,2)&h_{1}(6,n,2)&h_{1}(7,n,2)&h_{1}(8,n,2)\end{pmatrix}

(5.79)

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&1&2&1&0&0&0\\
0&0&4&0&1&1&1\end{pmatrix}\pmod{5}

Notice that this gives us the initial part of

as shown in (

).

6.
Main Theorem

We are now at the point that we may begin proving our main theorem. Since we know that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}L_{1}\in\mathcal{V}^{(1)}_{6},

we want to show that applying
U^{(1)}
to a member of
\mathcal{V}^{(1)}_{n}
will produce a member of
\mathcal{V}^{(0)}_{n^{\prime}}
in which all coefficients are divisible by an extra power of 5. Then applying
U^{(0)}
to
\mathcal{V}^{(0)}_{n}
, one should achieve a member of
\mathcal{V}^{(1)}_{n^{\prime}}
.

6.1.
First Intermediate Theorem

However, this strategy will need to be slightly modified. We start by proving the following slightly less ambitious theorem:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(0)}
. Then

(6.1)

\displaystyle U^{(0)}\left(f\right)\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{5n+6}.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(0)}
. Then we can express
f
as

\displaystyle f=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{n}}\sum_{m\geq 1}s(m)\cdot 5^{\theta_{0}(m)}\cdot x^{m}.

(6.2)

\displaystyle U^{(0)}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 1}s(m)\cdot 5^{\theta_{0}(m)}\cdot U^{(0)}\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+5x)^{n}}\right)

(6.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n+6}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m+1)/5\right\rceil}s(m)\cdot h_{0}(m,n,r)5^{\theta_{0}(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}

(6.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n+6}}\sum_{r\geq 1}\sum_{m\geq 1}s(m)\cdot h_{0}(m,n,r)5^{\theta_{0}(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}.

We need to prove that for
m+1\leq 5r
,

(6.5)

\displaystyle\theta_{0}(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)-\theta_{1}(r)\geq 0.

6.1.1.

\left\lceil\frac{m+1}{5}\right\rceil\leq r\leq\left\lceil\frac{m+33}{5}\right\rceil-1

We have
\pi_{0}(m,r)=0
. Notice also that
m\geq 5r-28
.

For
1\leq m\leq 4
, we have
\theta_{0}(m)=0
. We also have
1\leq r\leq 7
, and
\theta_{1}(r)=0
. Thus (
6.5
) follows trivially.

For
m\geq 5
we have
\theta_{0}(m)=\left\lfloor\frac{5m-1}{7}\right\rfloor-2
, and
r\geq 2
. If
2\leq r\leq 7
, then (
6.5
) again follows trivially. If
r\geq 8
, then

\displaystyle\theta_{0}(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)

\displaystyle=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{5m-1}{7}\right\rfloor-2\right)+0-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{5r-2}{7}\right\rfloor-5\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{25m-29}{7}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{5r-2}{7}\right\rfloor+3

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5r-2}{7}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{20r-1}{7}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{5r-2}{7}\right\rfloor-1

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{20r-1}{7}\right\rfloor-1\geq 0.

6.1.2.

r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m+33}{5}\right\rceil

We have
\pi_{0}(m,r)=\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m+2}{7}\right\rfloor-5
.

For
1\leq m\leq 4
, we have
\theta_{0}(m)=0
and
r\geq 7
. For
r=7
,
\theta_{1}(r)=0
, and

\displaystyle\theta_{0}(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m+2}{7}\right\rfloor-5\geq\theta_{1}(r).

For
r\geq 8
, we have

\displaystyle\theta_{0}(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m+2}{7}\right\rfloor-5

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5r-2}{7}\right\rfloor-5=\theta_{1}(r).

For
m\geq 5
,

\displaystyle\theta_{0}(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5m-1}{7}\right\rfloor-2+\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m+2}{7}\right\rfloor-5

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5r+4m-5}{7}\right\rfloor-7

\displaystyle\geq\left(\left\lfloor\frac{5r-2}{7}\right\rfloor-5\right)+\left\lfloor\frac{4m-3}{7}\right\rfloor-2

\displaystyle\geq\theta_{1}(r).

∎

6.2.
Second Intermediate Theorem

So far, this is what one would more or less expect from the classical proofs of congruence families. However, the matter becomes more complicated on application of
U^{(1)}
, as the following theorem shows:

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(1)}
with
n\equiv 1\pmod{5}
. Then

(6.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}U^{(1)}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{V}_{5n}^{(0)}.

Proof.

Let us take a function
f
of the form

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{n}}\sum_{m\geq 1}s(m)\cdot 5^{\theta_{1}(m)}\cdot x^{m}.

Applying
U^{(1)}
, we have

\displaystyle U^{(1)}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n}}\sum_{m\geq 2}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil m/5\right\rceil}s(m)\cdot h_{1}(m,n,r)\cdot 5^{\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{5n}}\sum_{r\geq 1}\sum_{m\geq 2}s(m)\cdot h_{1}(m,n,r)\cdot 5^{\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}.

For
m\leq 5r
we consider the relation

(6.7)

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,r)-\theta_{0}(r)-1\geq 0.

We first verify that (
6.7
) is true for
r\geq 3
.

6.2.1.

r=3

Here
\theta_{0}(3)=0
, and
m
ranges over
2\leq m\leq 15
.

For
2\leq m\leq 7
,
\theta_{1}(m)=0
and

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,3)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{15-m}{7}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{15-7}{7}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq 1=\theta_{0}(3)+1.

For
m=8
,

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(8)+\pi_{1}(8,3)

\displaystyle=2+1\geq 1=\theta_{0}(3)+1.

For
m\geq 9
,

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,3)

\displaystyle=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{5m-2}{7}\right\rfloor-5\right)+\left\lfloor\frac{15-m}{7}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{15+4m-8}{7}\right\rfloor-5

\displaystyle\geq 1=\theta_{0}(3)+1.

6.2.2.

r=4

Here
\theta_{0}(4)=0
, and
m
ranges over
2\leq m\leq 20
.

For
2\leq m\leq 7
,
\theta_{1}(m)=0
and

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,4)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{20-m}{7}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{20-7}{7}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq 1=\theta_{0}(4)+1.

For
m\geq 8
,

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,4)

\displaystyle=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{5m-2}{7}\right\rfloor-5\right)+\left\lfloor\frac{20-m}{7}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{20+4m-8}{7}\right\rfloor-5

\displaystyle\geq 1=\theta_{0}(4)+1.

6.2.3.

r\geq 5

Here
\theta_{0}(r)=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{5r-1}{7}\right\rfloor-2\right)
, and
m\geq 2
.

For
2\leq m\leq 7
,
\theta_{1}(m)=0
and

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,r)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5r-7}{7}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5r}{7}\right\rfloor-1

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5r-1}{7}\right\rfloor-1

\displaystyle=\theta_{0}(r)+1.

For
m=8
,

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(8)+\pi_{1}(8,r)

\displaystyle=0+\left\lfloor\frac{5r-8}{7}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{5r-1}{7}\right\rfloor-1

\displaystyle=\theta_{0}(r)+1.

For
m\geq 9
,

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,r)

\displaystyle=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{5m-2}{7}\right\rfloor-5\right)+\left\lfloor\frac{5r-m}{7}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5r+4m-8}{7}\right\rfloor-5

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5r-1}{7}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{4m}{7}\right\rfloor-6

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5r-1}{7}\right\rfloor-1

\displaystyle=\theta_{0}(r)+1.

6.2.4.

1\leq r\leq 2

Let us first consider the case for
r=1
. We know that we will receive a contribution for
2\leq m\leq 5
. In each of these cases, we see that

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,1)

\displaystyle=0+\left\lfloor\frac{5-m}{7}\right\rfloor=0

\displaystyle\neq\theta_{0}(1)+1=1.

On the other hand, for
r=2
, we have already shown that we get the correct contribution for
m\geq 9
. Moreover, for
2\leq m\leq 3
,

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,2)

\displaystyle=0+\left\lfloor\frac{10-m}{7}\right\rfloor\geq 1

\displaystyle=\theta_{0}(2)+1.

However, for
4\leq m\leq 7
,

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,2)

\displaystyle=0+\left\lfloor\frac{10-m}{7}\right\rfloor=0

\displaystyle\neq\theta_{0}(1)+1=1.

The only possible resolution to this problem is that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=2}^{5}s(m)\cdot h_{1}(m,n,1)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{5},

\displaystyle\sum_{m=4}^{8}s(m)\cdot h_{1}(m,n,2)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{5}.

By Corollary
5.6
, we can reduce this to

(6.8)

\displaystyle s(2)+s(3)+2s(4)+s(5)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{5},

(6.9)

\displaystyle 4s(4)+s(6)+s(7)+s(8)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{5}.

But notice that these are the components of the image of
\Omega
applied to
(s(m))_{m\geq 2}
. Because
(s(m))_{m\geq 2}\in\mathrm{ker}\left(\Omega\right)
, these relations must both reduce to 0, and we have accounted for the necessary powers of 5 in all cases.
∎

6.3.
Stability Theorem

We now see the necessity of the kernel properties of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(1)}
. However, we have not done enough. We need to prove
stability
of the properties of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(1)}
. That is, we need to show the following:

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(1)}
, with
n\equiv 1\pmod{5}
. Then

(6.10)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}U^{(0)}\circ U^{(1)}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{V}_{25n+6}^{(1)}.

It is this which will finally allow us to complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
, and with it Theorem
1.1
.

Proof.

We suppose that
f
has the form

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{n}}\sum_{m\geq 1}s(m)\cdot 5^{\theta_{1}(m)}\cdot x^{m}.

We proved in Theorem
6.2
above that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}U^{(1)}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{V}_{5n}^{(0)}.

Moreover, we know from Theorem
6.1
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}U^{(0)}\circ U^{(1)}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{25n+6}.

Because of this, we can expand

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}U^{(0)}\circ U^{(1)}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+5x)^{25n+6}}\sum_{w\geq 2}t(w)\cdot 5^{\theta_{1}(w)}\cdot x^{w},

with
t(w)
defined as

\displaystyle t(w):=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{5w-6}\sum_{m=1}^{5r}s(m)\cdot h_{1}(m,n,r)\cdot h_{0}(r,5n,w)

\displaystyle\times 5^{\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,r)+\pi_{0}(r,w)-\theta_{1}(w)-1}.

All that remains is to prove that
\left(t(w)\right)_{w\geq 2}\in\mathrm{ker}\left(\Omega\right)
.

Notice that for any fixed
w
, the values
m,r
are bounded. The variable
n
is unbounded. However, we can now take advantage of Theorem
5.5
, and the fact that
n\equiv 1\pmod{5}
by hypothesis, and we define

\displaystyle\hat{t}(w):=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{5w-6}\sum_{m=1}^{5r}s(m)\cdot h_{1}(m,1,r)\cdot h_{0}(r,5,w)

\displaystyle\times 5^{\theta_{1}(m)+\pi_{1}(m,r)+\pi_{0}(r,w)-1},

Obviously,

. If we examine

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}\left(28224s(2)+75264s(3)-3621954s(4)-3834516s(5)-2197503s(6)-731808s(7)-142758s(8)\right),

\displaystyle\hat{t}(4)=

\displaystyle 715008s(2)+1906688s(3)-67390288s(4)-71546272s(5)-41020056s(6)-13660416s(7)-2664816s(8),

\displaystyle\hat{t}(5)=

\displaystyle 25337256s(2)+67566016s(3)-6656426s(4)-33820304s(5)-21775257s(6)-7251552s(7)-1414602s(8),

\displaystyle\hat{t}(6)=

\displaystyle 457837968s(2)+1220901248s(3)+140880948572s(4)+147501656288s(5)+84383715654s(6)

\displaystyle+28101294144s(7)+5481881244s(8),

\displaystyle\hat{t}(7)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}(18893919144s(2)+50383784384s(3)+38559332136626s(4)+40484108853704s(5)+23170599952857s(6)

\displaystyle+7716226285152s(7)+1505248688202s(8)),

\displaystyle\hat{t}(8)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}(-116748977604s(2)-311330606944s(3)+1303629422734184s(4)+1369503027522686s(5)

\displaystyle+783890939008863s(6)+261049773444768s(7)+50924482319718s(8)).

This computation is explicitly carried out in our Mathematica supplement.

It is clear that (so long as

is always integer-valued)
\hat{t}(4),\hat{t}(5),\hat{t}(6)\in\mathbb{Z}
. For the remainder, we define the ideal

\displaystyle I:=\left<s(2)+s(3)+2s(4)+s(5),4s(4)+s(6)+2s(7)+s(8)\right>\leq\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}[s(2),s(3),...,s(8)].

\displaystyle 5\hat{t}(2),5\hat{t}(3),5\hat{t}(7),5\hat{t}(8)\in I.

This ensures that

\displaystyle\hat{t}(2),\hat{t}(3),\hat{t}(7),\hat{t}(8)\in\mathbb{Z}.

We now need to verify that

(6.11)

\displaystyle\hat{t}(2)+\hat{t}(3)+2\hat{t}(4)+\hat{t}(5)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{5},

(6.12)

\displaystyle 4\hat{t}(4)+\hat{t}(6)+\hat{t}(7)+\hat{t}(8)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{5}.

Expanding (
6.11
) and (
6.12
), we find that

(6.13)

\displaystyle\hat{t}(2)+\hat{t}(3)+2\hat{t}(4)+\hat{t}(5)=

\displaystyle 26772792s(2)+71394112s(3)-142142552s(4)

\displaystyle-177659828s(5)-104243454s(6)-34714944s(7)-6772044s(8),

(6.14)

\displaystyle 4\hat{t}(4)+\hat{t}(6)+\hat{t}(7)+\hat{t}(8)=

We reduce both of these polynomials modulo

to achieve 0.

Because
t(w)\equiv\hat{t}(w)\pmod{5}
, we have
\left(t(w)\right)_{w\geq 2}\in\mathrm{ker}\left(\Omega\right)
, and we have therefore proved (
6.10
).

We at last have enough that we can complete our main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

From (
4.9
)-(
4.11
), we know that

(6.15)

\displaystyle L_{1}=U^{(0)}(1).

We will prove this along with (
1.10
) in Section
7
below.

We already proved in (
5.13
) of Section
5
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}\cdot L_{1}=f_{1}\in\mathcal{V}^{(1)}_{6}.

Let us now suppose that for some
\alpha\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5^{\alpha}}\cdot L_{2\alpha-1}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(1)}.

Then there exists some
f_{2\alpha-1}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(1)}
such that

(6.16)

\displaystyle L_{2\alpha-1}=5^{\alpha}\cdot f_{2\alpha-1}.

On applying
U^{(1)}
, we have

(6.17)

\displaystyle L_{2\alpha}=U_{5}\left(L_{2\alpha-1}\right)=U_{5}\left(5^{\alpha}\cdot f_{2\alpha-1}\right)=5^{\alpha}\cdot U^{(1)}\left(f_{2\alpha-1}\right).

But by Theorem
6.2
, there exists some
f_{2\alpha}\in\mathcal{V}_{5n}^{(0)}
such that

(6.18)

\displaystyle U^{(1)}\left(f_{2\alpha-1}\right)=5\cdot f_{2\alpha}

Therefore, we have

(6.19)

\displaystyle L_{2\alpha}=5^{\alpha+1}\cdot f_{2\alpha}.

Moreover, on applying
U^{(0)}
we have

(6.20)

\displaystyle L_{2\alpha+1}=U_{5}\left(\mathcal{A}\cdot L_{2\alpha}\right)=U_{5}\left(\mathcal{A}\cdot 5^{\alpha+1}\cdot f_{2\alpha}\right)=5^{\alpha+1}\cdot U^{(0)}\left(f_{2\alpha}\right).

We recall from (
6.18
) that

(6.21)

\displaystyle U^{(0)}\left(f_{2\alpha}\right)=\frac{1}{5}U^{(0)}\circ U^{(1)}\left(f_{2\alpha-1}\right).

Because
f_{2\alpha-1}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(1)}
, we know that by Theorem
6.3
,

(6.22)

\displaystyle U^{(0)}\left(f_{2\alpha}\right)\in\mathcal{V}_{25n+6}^{(1)}.

Therefore, there exists some
f_{2\alpha+1}\in\mathcal{V}_{25n+6}^{(1)}
such that

(6.23)

\displaystyle U^{(0)}\left(f_{2\alpha}\right)=f_{2\alpha+1}.

Inserting this into
6.20
, we have

(6.24)

\displaystyle L_{2\alpha+1}=5^{\alpha+1}\cdot f_{2\alpha+1}.

By induction, we have

\displaystyle\frac{L_{2\alpha-1}}{5^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{V}_{n_{2\alpha-1}}^{(1)},

\displaystyle\frac{L_{2\alpha}}{5^{\alpha+1}}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{V}_{n_{2\alpha}}^{(0)}.

We need only verify that

\displaystyle n_{2\alpha-1}

\displaystyle=\psi(2\alpha-1),

\displaystyle n_{2\alpha}

\displaystyle=\psi(2\alpha)-1.

We note that for all
\alpha\geq 1
,

\displaystyle 5^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\equiv 1\pmod{4}.

We can then write

\displaystyle\psi(\alpha)=\left\lfloor\frac{5^{\alpha+1}}{4}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\frac{5^{\alpha+1}}{4}-\frac{1}{4}.

With this in mind we can quickly verify that

\displaystyle 5\cdot\psi(\alpha)

\displaystyle=5\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{5^{\alpha+1}}{4}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=5\cdot\left(\frac{5^{\alpha+1}}{4}-\frac{1}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{5^{\alpha+2}}{4}-\frac{1}{4}-1

\displaystyle=\psi(\alpha+1)-1.

We then have

\displaystyle 5\cdot\psi(2\alpha-1)

\displaystyle=\psi(2\alpha)-1,

\displaystyle 5\cdot\left(\psi(2\alpha)-1\right)+6

\displaystyle=5\cdot\psi(2\alpha)-5+6=\psi(2\alpha+1).

Because applying
U^{(1)}
to a rational polynomial with a denominator of

will result in a rational polynomial with denominator
(1+5x)^{5n}
, and similarly applying
U^{(0)}
will result in a denominator

, we need only verify that the denominator of

has power
\psi(1)=6
. This is true from (
1.10
).
∎

We now need to finish the proofs of Theorem

8.
Discussion

It is remarkable to note the implications of the importance of
\Omega
in our proof of Theorem
1.1
. In particular we are enormously interested in the importance of the kernel stability condition proved in Theorem
6.3
. Not only is the vector
\left(s(m)\right)_{m\geq 2}
associated with a given
f\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(1)}
in the kernel of
\Omega
, but this property is
genetic
: it is inherited by
\frac{1}{5}U^{(0)}\circ U^{(1)}\left(f\right)
.

It is this property which appears to explain why the congruence family is there at all. Suppose we take a typical sequence of functions over, say,
\mathrm{X}_{0}(10)
, with integer-valued Fourier coefficients, in which each function is related to its successor via some well-defined modified
U_{5}
and
T_{5}
operators. In this case, we might expect that this sequence would eventually become divisible by arbitrarily large powers of 5 (considering classical results, e.g,
[
20
, Theorem 8]
). However, this is not universal if we restrict ourselves to, say, an alternating pair of
U_{5}
operators as we have done here (see the discussions in
[
16
, Section 1.3]
and
[
18
, Section 6]
).

And even if 5-adic convergence to 0 is ensured, it may be very slow and irregular. This is because the members of our sequence will be composed of various different basis functions which will not all converge at the same rate. We certainly do not expect the precise, clean pattern of
5
-adic convergence that we see for strict congruence families. Sometimes a function in the sequence will be composed of basis functions in just the right manner that any anomalous terms not divisible by 5 will cancel out. Nevertheless, successive functions may not have this property, in which case anomalous terms may occur later in the sequence.

However, very occasionally a sequence will exist in which the initial functions are composed of basis functions in so delicate and precise a manner that the cancellation condition is genetic, in which case the accumulation of anomalous terms is prevented at every step. A congruence family is the manifestation of such a function sequence.

We see that the kernel of the congruence mapping
\Omega
describes this cancellation condition; and stability of membership in the kernel describes the conditions in which a congruence family can be found.

Thus, some combinatorial objects (in our case,
d_{5}(n)
) exhibit a given precise set of divisibility properties as a result of the way that certain associated modular functions relate to one another. In some ways, it is almost an inverse to the notion of a partition crank—that is, it gives an analytic explanation for why certain combinatorial properties exist.

The reason that this has been largely undiscovered before now is that for most classical congruence families (e.g., those for
p(n)
), the operator analogous to
\Omega
is trivial: it sends every associated modular vector
\left(s(m)\right)_{m\geq 0}
to zero, and the kernel is therefore the entire set. This in some manner constitutes a measure of the relative simplicity of the associated spaces of modular functions (e.g., the curve
\mathrm{X}_{0}(5)
associated with Ramanujan’s congruences for
p(n)
modulo powers of 5). In the case of other families, as in Theorem
1.1
, the analogous operator (in our case,
\Omega
itself) is nontrivial, and must be taken into account before a proper understanding of the congruence family can be achieved.

The most important question facing us appears to be
under what conditions
the kernel of the associated congruence operator is trivial. We believe that this is an especially promising long-term research question.
