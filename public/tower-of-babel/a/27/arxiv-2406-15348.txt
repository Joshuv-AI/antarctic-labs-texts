Chiral Spin Liquid and Quantum Phase Transition in the Triangular Lattice Hofstadter-Hubbard Model
Stefan Divic, Tomohiro Soejima (副島智大), Valentin Crépel, Michael P. Zaletel, Andrew Millis

Abstract
Recent advances in moiré engineering motivate the study of lattice models of strongly-correlated electrons subjected to substantial orbital magnetic flux. We analyze the triangular lattice Hofstadter-Hubbard model at one-quarter flux quantum per plaquette and a density of one electron per site, where a chiral spin liquid phase may exist between weak-coupling integer quantum Hall and strong-coupling 120 ∘ antiferromagnetic phases. We use matrix product state methods and analytical arguments to investigate this model compactified to cylinders of finite circumference. We uncover a glide particle-hole symmetry operation which, we argue, is spontaneously broken at the quantum Hall to spin liquid transition on odd-circumference cylinders. We numerically verify the spontaneous symmetry breaking and further demonstrate that this transition is associated with algebraic long-range correlations of various spin-singlet, charge-neutral operators. For even-circumference cylinders, the transition becomes a crossover associated with a large correlation length that grows substantially with circumference. Our findings suggest that in the two-dimensional limit, the transition to a chiral spin liquid phase is continuous and features critical fluctuations of the current.

Discussion—
Our numerical data on both even and odd-circumference cylinder geometries provides confirming evidence of a CSL phase and indicates that the CSL-IQH transition is in fact continuous in the Hofstadter-Hubbard model at
\pi/2
flux per triangle. The single-electron and spin gaps remain nonzero across the transition, while the current-current correlation function exhibits power-law behavior at the odd-
L_{y}
critical points and is also prominent on the even-
L_{y}
geometries. This raises the possibility of experimental detection of the transition and provides valuable guidance for its further numerical study. Using quantum Monte Carlo, fermionic PEPS, or neural quantum states, one may be able to access wider systems, yielding confirming evidence of quantum criticality, estimates of critical exponents, and possible signatures of conformal invariance
[
81
]
. Our results support Ref.
[
82
]
, which proposes a field theory of the 2+1D transition featuring critical fluctuations of the low-energy conserved current.

A broader message of this work is that the ability to extrapolate 2+1D physics from dimensionally-reduced data is greatly enhanced by analyzing how symmetries, ground states and their phase transitions respond to spatial compactification and flux threading.
In particular, the properties of transitions into topologically-ordered phases may generally be illuminated by studying cylinder compactifications in which topological ground state degeneracy manifests as SSB
[
83
,
84
]
.
Leveraging such techniques systematically, it would be valuable to characterize the contexts in which UV features of Mott and quantum Hall phases offer improved understanding of their charge fluctuation-driven quantum phase transitions.

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{1}=\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{3}a}\hat{x},\qquad\bm{b}_{2}=\frac{2\pi}{a}\left(-\frac{\hat{x}}{\sqrt{3}}+\hat{y}\right)\qquad\text{(YC)}.

(2)

This leads to a natural cylinder compactification: one may identify each point

\displaystyle\bm{r}=x\bm{a}_{1}+y\bm{a}_{2}

(3)

with points
\bm{r}^{\prime}
for which
y\equiv y^{\prime}\ (\text{mod }L_{y})
and also
x=x^{\prime}
. We refer to
L_{y}
as the “circumference” of the cylinder. Doing so for the YC lattice, one obtains the canonical “YC-
L_{y}
” cylinder geometry
[
8
]
, whose circumference is
L_{y}a
.

SII
Choosing the magnetic unit cell

Let the flux per parallelogram (
i.e.
, two triangles) be

\displaystyle\Phi=\frac{p}{q}\Phi_{0},

(4)

where
\Phi_{0}=\frac{2\pi\hbar}{e}\sim 2\pi
in units where
\hbar/e=1.
The above definition of flux is convenient for defining the geometry, but we briefly remark that the flux
\Phi/2
per triangle — which is the smallest traversable plaquette — is the quantity with respect to which physical behavior (
e.g.
, the Hofstadter spectrum) must be
\Phi_{0}
-periodic. In any case, the corresponding magnetic field strength is

\displaystyle B=\frac{\Phi}{A_{\text{par}}}=\frac{p\Phi_{0}/q}{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a^{2}}=\frac{2p\Phi_{0}}{\sqrt{3}q}a^{-2}.

(5)

Regardless of the parity of
q
, there will be
q
magnetic sublattice sites in the magnetic unit cell.
When
q
is
even
, the magnetic Bravais vectors are

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{1}=q\bm{a}_{1}-\frac{q}{2}\bm{a}_{2}\propto\hat{x},\qquad\bm{R}_{2}=\bm{a}_{2}\propto\hat{y},\qquad(q\ \text{even}),

(6)

whose total area
A_{\text{muc}}
is precisely that of
2q
triangles of area
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^{2}.

The associated reciprocal lattice vectors are

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{1}=\frac{1}{q}\bm{b}_{1}\propto\hat{x},\qquad\bm{G}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\bm{b}_{1}+\bm{b}_{2}\propto\hat{y},\qquad(q\ \text{even}).

(7)

As a sanity check, the total of the magnetic Brillouin zone (mBZ) is then

\displaystyle A_{\text{mBZ}}=|\bm{G}_{1}\times\bm{G}_{2}|=(2\pi)^{2}\frac{2}{q\sqrt{3}a^{2}}=\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{A_{\text{muc}}}.

(8)

When
q
is
odd
, the enclosing magnetic unit cell again contains exactly
2q
triangles, but we take

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{1}=q\bm{a}_{1}-\frac{q-1}{2}\bm{a}_{2},\qquad\bm{R}_{2}=\bm{a}_{2}\propto\hat{y},\qquad(q\ \text{odd}),

(9)

whose total area is once again

\displaystyle|\bm{R}_{1}\times\bm{R}_{2}|=q|\bm{a}_{1}\times\bm{a}_{2}|=\frac{q\sqrt{3}}{2}a^{2}=A_{\text{muc}}.

(10)

The reciprocal lattice vectors are now

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{1}=\frac{1}{q}\bm{b}_{1},\qquad\bm{G}_{2}=\frac{q-1}{2q}\bm{b}_{1}+\bm{b}_{2},\qquad(q\ \text{odd}),

(11)

which also satisfy the inverse area relationship, Eq. (
8
). For both even and odd denominator
q,
note that the enclosing magnetic unit cell contains exactly
2q
triangles, so
2\pi p\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
total flux.

SIII
Hamiltonian and choice of vector potential

In all cases, we adopt the standard convention for the on-site Hubbard repulsion:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{int}}=U\sum_{\bm{r}}n_{\bm{r}\uparrow}n_{\bm{r}\downarrow},\qquad U\geq 0.

(12)

Now we discuss the hoppings. Placing the underlying triangular lattice on the cylinder as described in Sec.
SI
, let the resulting system have circumference
L_{y}
,
i.e.
, the points
\bm{r}\sim\bm{r}+L_{y}\bm{a}_{2}
are geometrically identified. Let the lattice sites be indexed as in Eq. (
3
), which defines a set of coordinate coefficients
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}.
Since
\bm{a}_{2}
is purely vertical, then
x
indexes the cylinder “rings”, and
y
is an integer-valued spatial index within each ring.
To generate complex hoppings and modify the translation algebra into a magnetic translation algebra, we introduce an external
U(1)
vector potential. We assume that it is linear and that its curl is the magnetic field:

\displaystyle\bm{A}(\bm{r})=M\bm{r},\qquad\nabla\times\bm{A}(\bm{r})=B\hat{z},

(13)

where
B
is as in Eq. (
5
). The hoppings are obtained from the vector potential via the Peierl’s substitution, for which we adopt the following sign convention:

\displaystyle t_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\leftarrow\bm{r}}=t\exp\left(i\int^{\bm{r}^{\prime}}_{\bm{r}}\bm{A}(\bm{\ell})\cdot d\bm{\ell}\right)=t\exp\left(i\bm{A}\left(\frac{\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{r}}{2}\right)\cdot(\bm{r}^{\prime}-\bm{r})\right),

(14)

where the second equality is due to linearity of
\bm{A}(\bm{r})
. Our convention is that the single-particle Hamiltonian is (note the minus sign):

\displaystyle\hat{h}=-\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r}}c^{\dagger}(\bm{r}^{\prime})t_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\leftarrow\bm{r}}c(\bm{r}),

(15)

where we only include nearest-neighbour hopping on the triangular lattice, for which

\displaystyle t_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\leftarrow\bm{r}}=te^{i\bm{A}\left(\frac{\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{r}}{2}\right)\cdot(\bm{r}^{\prime}-\bm{r})}.

(16)

To constrain the form of the vector potential, we remark that the terms
x\hat{x}
and
y\hat{y}
are curl-free, whereas
Bx\hat{y}
and
-By\hat{x}
(the standard Landau gauge potentials on the rectangular lattice) have the correct curl. We therefore reason that the most general permissible
M
in Eq. (
13
) is given by

\displaystyle M=B\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\gamma-1\\
\gamma&\beta\end{pmatrix},

(17)

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. We will now demand that the choice of vector potential is compatible with our chosen geometry of magnetic Bravais vectors. Specifically, we ask that the Peierl’s hoppings generated by
\bm{A}
are
periodic
in the chosen magnetic Bravais vectors. This allows us to place the system easily on a torus or cylinder, provided the system is an integer tiling of magnetic unit cells.
Quantitatively, we demand that the RHS of Eq. (
14
) is invariant under shifts of the argument of
\bm{A}
by the magnetic Bravais vectors
\bm{R}_{1}
and
\bm{R}_{2}
, for all bond vectors
\bm{r}^{\prime}-\bm{r}
. By linearity of the vector potential, and since the bond vectors are linear combinations of
\bm{a}_{1}
and
\bm{a}_{2}
, it suffices to require that

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{i}\cdot\bm{A}(\bm{R}_{j})\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z},

(18)

for all
i,j
. Following Ref.
[
88
]
, an elegant solution can be found in terms of the reciprocal vectors of the underlying triangular lattice. We take the following since
\bm{b}_{1}\propto\hat{x}
, and we want the vector potential to be independent of
y
:

\displaystyle\bm{A}(\bm{r})=\frac{\Phi}{(2\pi)^{2}}(\bm{r}\cdot\bm{b}_{1})\bm{b}_{2},

(19)

where
\Phi
is the flux per
parallelogram
.
Noting the vector identity

\displaystyle\left[\nabla\times\left((\bm{r}\cdot\bm{Q})\bm{P}\right)\right]_{i}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_{j}\left(\bm{r}_{\ell}\bm{Q}_{\ell}\bm{P}_{k}\right)=\epsilon_{ijk}\bm{Q}_{j}\bm{P}_{k}=(\bm{Q}\times\bm{P})_{i},

(20)

then

\displaystyle\bm{B}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\nabla\times\bm{A}(\bm{r})=\frac{\Phi}{(2\pi)^{2}}\bm{b}_{1}\times\bm{b}_{2}=\frac{\Phi}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{A_{\text{par}}}\hat{z}=\frac{\Phi}{A_{\text{par}}}\hat{z}.

(21)

Moreover, it can be shown to have the required periodicity property. Note that

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{i}\cdot\bm{A}(\bm{R}_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi}{(2\pi)^{2}}(\bm{R}_{j}\cdot\bm{b}_{1})\bm{b}_{2}\cdot\bm{a}_{i}=\delta_{i,2}\frac{2\pi p/q}{2\pi}(\bm{R}_{j}\cdot\bm{b}_{1})=\delta_{i,2}\frac{p}{q}\bm{R}_{j}\cdot\bm{b}_{1},

(22)

which clearly vanishes when
j=2.
Otherwise,

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{i}\cdot\bm{A}(\bm{R}_{1})

\displaystyle=\delta_{i,2}\frac{p}{q}\left(\begin{cases}\left(q\bm{a}_{1}-\frac{q}{2}\bm{a}_{2}\right)\cdot\bm{b}_{1}&q\text{ even}\\
\left(q\bm{a}_{1}-\frac{q-1}{2}\bm{a}_{2}\right)\cdot\bm{b}_{1}&q\text{ odd}\end{cases}\right)=2\pi p\delta_{i,2},

(23)

so that, indeed, in either case:

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{i}\cdot\bm{A}(\bm{R}_{j})\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}.

(24)

Specializing to the case
(p,q)=(1,2)
relevant to the main text, we plot the resulting hopping amplitudes Eq. (
16
) in Fig.
S1
(a) for the YC parameterization of the 2D lattice. The hoppings only take values in
\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
and are evidently periodic in both
\bm{R}_{1}
and
\bm{R}_{2}
.
The color of each bond denotes the complex phase of
t_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\leftarrow\bm{r}}
(see Eq. (
15
)) in the direction of the arrow (pictured at the bond centers).

Appendix B
Threading flux

SI
Affine-linear vector potential implementation

When dimensionally-reducing the lattice system to the cylinder, the charge and spin flux through the cross sections of the cylinder distinguish gauge-inequivalent systems. This additional flux can be implemented by adding a constant offset to the vector potential:

\displaystyle\bm{A}(\bm{r})\longrightarrow\bm{A}_{\phi}(\bm{r})=\bm{A}(\bm{r})+\phi\frac{\bm{b}_{2}}{2\pi L_{y}},

(25)

where

\displaystyle\phi=\phi_{q}+\frac{\sigma_{z}}{2}\phi_{s}.

(26)

This modifies the hopping elements by

\displaystyle t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}}\longrightarrow t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}}^{\phi}=t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}}\exp\left(i\frac{\phi}{2\pi}\frac{\bm{b}_{2}\cdot\bm{\delta}}{L_{y}}\right).

(27)

Since we made the choice
\bm{A}\propto\bm{b}_{2}
for the original vector potential (in the absence of flux threading), then this is equivalent to a shift in the origin:

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{\phi}(\bm{r})=\bm{A}(\bm{r}+\bm{X}_{\phi})=\bm{A}(\bm{r})+\frac{\Phi}{(2\pi)^{2}}(\bm{X}_{\phi}\cdot\bm{b}_{1})\bm{b}_{2},

(28)

where

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{\phi}=\frac{\phi\bm{a}_{1}}{\Phi L_{y}}.

(29)

SII
Twisted boundary condition implementation

For the purposes of numerical implementation, particularly in hybrid space where we trade
y
for
k_{y}
, it is convenient to instead implement flux threading via a twist in the boundary conditions. Starting with periodic boundary conditions for the fermion operators at
\phi=0
, namely
c^{\dagger}(\bm{r}+L_{y}\bm{a}_{2})=c^{\dagger}(\bm{r})
, we define a new set of operators

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{\phi}(\bm{r})=c^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\exp(iy\phi/L_{y})=c^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\exp\left(i\phi\frac{\bm{b}_{2}\cdot\bm{r}}{2\pi L_{y}}\right).

(30)

Then instead of implementing Eq. (
25
), we can instead replace every instance of
c
in the second-quantized interacting Hamiltonian with
c_{\phi}
. This does not affect terms that depend solely on charge and spin densities,
e.g.
, the Hubbard interaction. However, it modifies the hoppings in a way that is equivalent to Eq. (
25
). Under the operator replacement, the hoppings are replaced by

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}(\bm{r}+\bm{\delta})t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}}c(\bm{r})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow c_{\phi}^{\dagger}(\bm{r}+\bm{\delta})t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}}c_{\phi}(\bm{r})

(31)

\displaystyle=c^{\dagger}(\bm{r}+\bm{\delta})\exp\left(i\phi\frac{\bm{b}_{2}\cdot(\bm{r}+\bm{\delta})}{2\pi L_{y}}\right)t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}}\exp\left(-i\phi\frac{\bm{b}_{2}\cdot\bm{r}}{2\pi L_{y}}\right)c(\bm{r})

(32)

\displaystyle=c^{\dagger}(\bm{r}+\bm{\delta})\exp\left(i\frac{\phi}{2\pi}\frac{\bm{b}_{2}\cdot\bm{\delta}}{L_{y}}\right)t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}}c(\bm{r}),

(33)

which is exactly equivalent to Eq. (
27
). To see that Eq. (
30
) corresponds to a twisting of the boundary condition on the fermion operators, we note that

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{\phi}(\bm{r}+L_{y}\bm{a}_{2})=c^{\dagger}(\bm{r}+L_{y}\bm{a}_{2})\exp\left(i\phi\frac{\bm{b}_{2}\cdot(\bm{r}+L_{y}\bm{a}_{2})}{2\pi L_{y}}\right)=c_{\phi}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\exp(i\phi).

(34)

The utility of this alternative formulation is that it leads to a clean numerical implementation upon fourier transforming
y\to k_{y}
. In particular, note that
(x,y)=(\bm{r}\cdot\bm{b}_{1}/2\pi,\bm{r}\cdot\bm{b}_{2}/2\pi)
and define

\displaystyle c_{\phi}^{\dagger}(x,k_{y})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{y}}}\sum_{y}e^{-ik_{y}y}c_{\phi}^{\dagger}(x,y)\iff c_{\phi}^{\dagger}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{y}}}\sum_{k_{y}}e^{ik_{y}y}c_{\phi}^{\dagger}(x,k_{y}).

(35)

Note that this is the Fourier convention of TeNPy. When we express the Hamiltonian in the hybrid
xk_{y}
representation, we find that all that is required is to shift the allowed values of
k_{y}.
To see this, we impose the twisted boundary condition Eq. (
34
) on the Fourier-expanded operators:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{y}}}\sum_{k_{y}}e^{ik_{y}(y+L_{y})}c_{\theta}^{\dagger}(x,k_{y})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{y}}}\sum_{k_{y}}e^{ik_{y}y}c_{\theta}^{\dagger}(x,k_{y})e^{i\phi},

(36)

so that

\displaystyle e^{ik_{y}L_{y}}=e^{i\phi}.

(37)

The allowed values of momenta are therefore quantized to

\displaystyle k_{y}=\frac{2\pi}{L_{y}}\left(n+\frac{\phi_{q}}{2\pi}+\frac{\sigma_{z}\phi_{s}}{4\pi}\right),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(38)

which are shifted relative to the
\phi=0
case. When the original Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\phi=0}
is written in hybrid space, some coefficients will depend on
k.
The only effect of the replacement Eq. (
30
) is then for those coefficients to instead be evaluated on this shifted set of allowed values
[
8
]
.

SII
Symmetries in the 2D plane

SII.1
Magnetic translations

Recall that we designed the vector potential so that the Hamiltonian would be invariant under
T_{y}
translations,
i.e.
, in the circumferential direction
\bm{a}_{2},
defined as

\displaystyle T_{y}c_{\bm{r}}^{\dagger}T_{y}^{-1}=c_{\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{2}}^{\dagger}.

(42)

To see this, note that

\displaystyle t_{\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{2}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{2}}

\displaystyle=t\exp\left(i\bm{A}\left(\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{2}+\bm{\delta}/2\right)\cdot\bm{\delta}\right)

(43)

\displaystyle=t\exp\left(i\bm{A}\left(\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}/2\right)\cdot\bm{\delta}\right)

(44)

\displaystyle=t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}},

(45)

since
\bm{A}(\bm{a}_{2})=0
by design. On the other hand, translation along
\bm{a}_{1}
introduces an additional term:

\displaystyle t_{\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1}}

\displaystyle=t\exp\left(i\bm{A}\left(\bm{a}_{1}+\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}/2\right)\cdot\bm{\delta}\right)

(46)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(i\bm{A}\left(\bm{a}_{1}\right)\cdot\bm{\delta}\right)t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}}

(47)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{i}{2}\bm{b}_{2}\cdot\bm{\delta}\right)t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}}.

(48)

This affects the hoppings in directions with some weight along
\bm{\delta}=\bm{a}_{2}
and, by comparison to Eq. (
29
) above, corresponds to threading
\phi=\pi L_{y}
through the cylinder.
In the 2D plane geometry—and on cylinder geometries of even circumference as we’ll later show—the Hamiltonian can be restored by composing the bare
T_{x}
with a large gauge transformation. In particular, define the magnetic translation operation by

\displaystyle T_{x}c_{\bm{r}}^{\dagger}T_{x}^{-1}=c_{\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1}}^{\dagger}\exp\left(\frac{i}{2}\bm{b}_{2}\cdot\bm{r}\right)=c_{\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1}}^{\dagger}(-1)^{y},

(49)

so that

\displaystyle T_{x}\hat{h}T_{x}^{-1}

\displaystyle=T_{x}\left(\sum c_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}^{\dagger}t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}}c_{\bm{r}}\right)T_{x}^{-1}

(50)

\displaystyle=\sum\exp\left(\frac{i}{2}\bm{b}_{2}\cdot(\bm{r}+\bm{\delta})\right)c_{\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1}+\bm{\delta}}^{\dagger}t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}}c_{\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1}}\exp\left(-\frac{i}{2}\bm{b}_{2}\cdot\bm{r}\right)

(51)

\displaystyle=\sum c_{\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1}+\bm{\delta}}^{\dagger}\left(\exp\left(\frac{i}{2}\bm{b}_{2}\cdot\bm{\delta}\right)t_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}}\right)c_{\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1}}

(52)

\displaystyle=\sum c_{\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1}+\bm{\delta}}^{\dagger}t_{\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1}+\bm{\delta}\leftarrow\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1}}c_{\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1}}

(53)

\displaystyle=\hat{h},

(54)

where the second-last equality follows from Eq. (
48
) above.

SII.2
Particle-hole

Consider the most naive definition of the particle-hole operation:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{P}}c^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}=c(\bm{r}),\qquad\tilde{\mathcal{P}}i\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}=+i,

(55)

where the second condition specifies that it is a linear (as opposed to anti-linear) unitary many-body operator. If 1 and 2 denote an adjacent pair of sites, then this naive particle-hole operation acts as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{P}}}(t_{12}c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{2}+t_{12}^{*}c_{2}^{\dagger}c_{1})\tilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{P}}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=t_{12}c_{1}c_{2}^{\dagger}+t_{12}^{*}c_{2}c_{1}^{\dagger}

(56)

\displaystyle=-t_{12}c_{2}^{\dagger}c_{1}-t_{12}^{*}c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{2}

(57)

\displaystyle=-t_{12}^{*}c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{2}-t_{12}c_{2}^{\dagger}c_{1},

(58)

so that it reverses the Peierl’s phase and shifts it by
\pi.
On the triangular lattice, this modifies the flux through each elementary triangular plaquette as follows:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\triangle}\to-\Phi_{\triangle}+\pi.

(59)

Requiring that this operation leave the flux invariant modulo
2\pi,
we find that we need

\displaystyle 2\Phi_{\triangle}=\pi\left(2n+1\right),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(60)

This is consistent with the observation that the Hofstadter spectrum on the triangular lattice only has bands with opposite energy for these values of
\Phi_{\triangle}
. In particular, in this work we consider
\Phi_{\triangle}=\pi/2
. We find at each momentum
\bm{k}
that both the energy and Berry curvature are exactly opposite, with the bands having Chern number
\pm 1.

For a generic choice of Peierls’ hopping phases consistent with this uniform magnetic flux, the naive particle-hole operation must be composed with a gauge transformation in order to exactly restore all the hopping elements. Given the particular form of the hoppings Eq. (
39
) to Eq. (
41
) for the two-band model, one may easily confirm that the following form of the particle-hole operation leaves the Hamiltonian invariant:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}c^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\mathcal{P}^{-1}=(-1)^{x+y}c^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{r}),\qquad\mathcal{P}i\mathcal{P}^{-1}=+i,

(61)

where
\mathsf{T}
is the transposition operation on the row vector of electronic annihilation operators (indexed by spin).

To obtain the form of the particle-hole symmetry presented in the main text, we compose the version in Eq. (
61
) with a conventional spin rotation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}c^{\dagger}_{x,y}\mathcal{P}^{-1}=(-1)^{x+y}c^{\mathsf{T}}_{x,y}(i\sigma_{y}),\quad\mathcal{P}i\mathcal{P}^{-1}=+i.

(62)

The spin rotation is chosen so that the spin operator

\displaystyle S_{\alpha}(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{2}c^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\sigma_{\alpha}c(\bm{r})

(63)

is left invariant. To see this, we note that the unitarity of
\mathcal{P}
implies that
\mathcal{P}c(\bm{r})\mathcal{P}^{-1}=(-1)^{x+y}(-i\sigma_{y})c^{\dagger\mathsf{T}}(\bm{r})
. This allows us to explicitly compute:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}S_{\alpha}(\bm{r})\mathcal{P}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{P}c^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\mathcal{P}^{-1}\sigma_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}c(\bm{r})\mathcal{P}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}c^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{r})\sigma_{y}\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{y}c^{\dagger\mathsf{T}}(\bm{r}).

(64)

When
\alpha\in\{x,z\}
we acquire a minus sign but then obtain another from fermion anti-commutation when undoing the transposes. On the other hand, when
\alpha=y
the Pauli matrices commute but the minus sign from fermion anti-commutation cancels with the fact that
\sigma_{y}
is an anti-symmetric matrix:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}S_{y}(\bm{r})\mathcal{P}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}c_{s}(\bm{r})(\sigma_{y})_{ss^{\prime}}c_{s^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})=-\frac{1}{2}c_{s^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})(\sigma_{y})_{ss^{\prime}}c_{s}(\bm{r})=+S_{y}(\bm{r}).

(65)

SIII
Fate of Symmetries on Cylinder

Let us elucidate when placing the lattice on a YC-
L_{y}
cylinder explicitly breaks particle-hole symmetry. We may use the naive definition of particle-hole Eq. (
55
) because the fluxes in question are gauge-invariant quantities. Focusing on a particular cylinder ring
r
penetrated with
\Phi_{r}
flux, note that

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{P}}}:\Phi_{r}\mapsto\begin{cases}-\Phi_{r}&L_{y}\text{ even},\\
\pi-\Phi_{r}&L_{y}\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

(66)

This follows from the application of Eq. (
58
) to each of the
L_{y}
nearest-neighbor links.
Therefore,
\tilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{P}}}
is a symmetry (up to a gauge transformation) when, for all rings
r
, we have that

\displaystyle\Phi_{r}\in\begin{cases}\pi\mathbb{Z}&L_{y}\text{ even},\\
\frac{\pi}{2}+\pi\mathbb{Z}&L_{y}\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

(67)

When
L_{y}
is even, Gauss’s law dictates that the fluxes
\Phi_{r_{1}}
and
\Phi_{r_{2}}
through two adjacent rings
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
must be equal. Note that this allows
T_{x}
to be a symmetry. Therefore, possibly after threading
\phi
external flux through
each ring of the cylinder, it is possible to tune all the
\Phi_{r}
simultaneously (to
0
or
\pi
) so that
\tilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{P}}}
is respected. Only in those cases is
\tilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{P}}}T_{x}
also a symmetry (up to a gauge transformation).

On the other hand, when
L_{y}
is odd, Gauss’s law instead dictates that

\displaystyle\Phi_{r_{2}}-\Phi_{r_{1}}=\pi\qquad\text{mod }2\pi.

(68)

This always rules out
T_{x}
as a symmetry. However, so long as

\Phi_{r_{1}}
is
\pi/2
or
-\pi/2,
both Eqs. (
67
) and (
68
) can be respected, so that
\tilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{P}}}
is a symmetry (up to a gauge transformation). On the other hand, if we instead tune
\Phi_{r_{1}}
to 0 or
\pi,
then
\tilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{P}}}T_{x}
is a symmetry (up to a gauge transformation) even though neither
T_{x}
nor
\tilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{P}}}
is individually a symmetry; for the
L_{y}=3,5
cylinders in the main text, we choose
\Phi_{r}=0
for all even rings
r
and
\Phi_{r}=\pi
for all odd rings
r
, which makes these cylinders invariant under
\tilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{P}}}T_{x}
.

Here we study the real-space entanglement spectrum on infinite-length cylinders at a partition into two semi-infinite halves. The MPS ansatz allows for the efficient computation of the ground state Schmidt decomposition:

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}|\Phi_{\alpha L}\rangle|\Phi_{\alpha R}\rangle,

(69)

where
|\Phi_{\alpha L/R}\rangle
are orthonormal bases for the left/right Hilbert spaces. The Schmidt values are non-negative and normalized as
\sum_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}=1,
with
\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}
being the probability of measuring subsystem
R
in the state
|\Phi_{\alpha R}\rangle
(which collapses
L
to
|\Phi_{\alpha L}\rangle
). Tracing out
R
, one obtains a mixed state for
L
with von Neumann entanglement entropy

\displaystyle S=-\sum_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}\log\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}.

(70)

In Ref.
[
57
]
, Li and Haldane conjectured that the entanglement spectrum at a bipartition
L\cup R
of a quantum Hall ground state encodes the low-energy spectrum of the half-system with a physical edge at the location of the bipartition. Given the reduced density matrix
\rho_{L}
, the entanglement spectrum is defined as the eigenvalues of the non-negative-definite Hermitian matrix
H_{L}
with
\rho_{L}=e^{-H_{L}}
, namely
\epsilon_{\alpha}=-\log\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}
. The Li-Haldane proposal has been further investigated in QH systems
[
89
,
60
,
62
]
, demonstrated exactly for free fermions
[
59
]
, and justified more generally using CFT
[
61
,
63
,
64
]
.

The entanglement eigenstates can be labeled by their charge, spin, and circumferential momentum quantum numbers. This enables us to define the Schmidt weight in sector
q=(Q,S_{z},k_{y})
as the restricted sum
W_{q}=\sum_{\alpha|q}\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}
over states
\alpha
with quantum number
q
, so that
\sum_{q}W_{q}=1
. In the infinite geometry, relative charges are unambiguous while the absolute charge is subtle
[
90
]
. For concreteness, we use the following convention: we fix the origin
(Q,S_{z})=(0,0)
so that
Q=0
has the largest Schmidt weight and the average of
S_{z}
is zero over the distribution. In each fixed
Q
sector, we further place the lowest-lying entanglement eigenvalue at
k_{y}=0
for our plots.

In Fig.

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}\in\{\pm\bm{a}_{1},\pm\bm{a}_{2},\pm(\bm{a}_{1}-\bm{a}_{2})\},

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{\bm{\delta}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{y}}\sum_{y}c^{\dagger}(x,y)c((x,y)+\bm{\delta}).

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{\bm{\delta}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{y}^{2}}\sum_{y}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}e^{iky}e^{-ik^{\prime}(y+\delta_{y})}c^{\dagger}(x,k)c(x+\delta_{x},k^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{y}^{2}}\sum_{y}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}e^{i(k-k^{\prime})y}e^{-ik^{\prime}\delta_{y}}c^{\dagger}(x,k)c(x+\delta_{x},k^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{y}}\sum_{k}e^{-ik\delta_{y}}c^{\dagger}(x,k)c(x+\delta_{x},k),

(75)

so that

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{\bm{a}_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{y}}\sum_{k}c^{\dagger}(x,k)c(x+1,k)

(76)

(77)

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{\bm{a}_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{y}}\sum_{k}e^{-ik}c^{\dagger}(x,k)c(x,k)

(78)

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{-\bm{a}_{2}}(x)

(79)

SI
Staggered Heisenberg Order Parameter

The following documents the numerical computation of the staggered Heisenberg order parameter in the hybrid
xk_{y}
fermion representation. The spin correlation operator
\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{j}
can be expressed in terms of fermionic operators by first noting that

\displaystyle S_{i}^{+}=c_{i\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\downarrow},\quad S_{i}^{-}=c_{i\downarrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\uparrow},\quad S_{i}^{z}=\frac{1}{2}(c_{i\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\uparrow}-c_{i\downarrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\downarrow}).

(80)

The spin correlation operator
\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{j}
can then be written in terms of these fermion operators as:

\displaystyle\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(c_{i\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\downarrow}c_{j\downarrow}^{\dagger}c_{j\uparrow}+c_{i\downarrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\uparrow}c_{j\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j\downarrow}\right)+S_{i}^{z}S_{j}^{z}.

(81)

Next, we express the staggered-Heisenberg order parameter in the hybrid
(x,k_{y})
operator representation, where
x
indexes cylinder rings. Because of translation invariance around the cylinder, it is useful to define the
y
-averaged inter-ring NN Heisenberg operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\text{H}}(x)=\frac{1}{L_{y}}\sum_{y}\sum_{a=x,y,z}S^{a}(x,y)S^{a}(x+1,y),

(82)

where

\displaystyle S^{a}(x,y)=c^{\dagger}(x,y)\frac{\sigma^{a}}{2}c(x,y)=\frac{1}{L_{y}}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}e^{i(k-k^{\prime})y}c^{\dagger}(x,k)\frac{\sigma^{a}}{2}c(x,k^{\prime}).

(83)

In general, the hybrid representation reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\text{H}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{y}}\sum_{y}\sum_{a=x,y,z}S^{a}(x,y)S^{a}(x+1,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{y}^{2}}\sum_{a=x,y,z}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\sum_{p,p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{L_{y}}\sum_{y}e^{i(k-k^{\prime}+p-p^{\prime})y}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times c^{\dagger}(x,k)\frac{\sigma^{a}}{2}c(x,k^{\prime})\cdot c^{\dagger}(x+1,p)\frac{\sigma^{a}}{2}c(x+1,p^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{y}^{2}}\sum_{a=x,y,z}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\sum_{p,p^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}(x,k)\frac{\sigma^{a}}{2}c(x,k^{\prime})\cdot c^{\dagger}(x+1,p)\frac{\sigma^{a}}{2}c(x+1,k-k^{\prime}+p).

SII
Relation to ionic Hubbard chain

Here, we make more precise the relationship between the “ionic Hubbard” chain and our model on those YC-
L_{y}
cylinders (with odd
L_{y}
and
\Phi_{\text{ext}}=0
)
which possess glide-PH symmetry
. In particular, the former model can be thought of as a truly 1+1D version of the latter,
i.e.
, a bipartite chain as opposed to a quasi-(1+1)D system with the hopping-connectivity of a cylinder. We first compare them at the level of symmetries. The ionic Hubbard Hamiltonian is defined as
[
92
]
:

\displaystyle H=-t\sum_{i,\sigma}(c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}c_{i+1,\sigma}+h.c.)+U\sum_{i}n_{i,\uparrow}n_{i,\downarrow}+\frac{\Delta}{2}\sum_{i,\sigma}(-1)^{i}n_{i,\sigma}.

(88)

The Hamiltonian is invariant under translation by two sites, with
\Delta
being the potential energy difference between the two sublattice sites in the unit cell. Moreover, it is invariant under site-centered inversion (but not bond-centered inversion), time-reversal, and a “duality” symmetry that is the combination of particle-hole with a single-site translation
[
80
]
. These symmetries of the ionic Hubbard chain correspond to the following symmetries of our model:
(T_{x})^{2},
site-centered
C_{2z}
,
M_{y}\mathcal{T}
, and glide-PH
\mathcal{P}T_{x}
.

Remarkably, the two models are also similar at the level of their ground states. The small-
U
ground state phase of the ionic Hubbard chain is a fully-symmetric band insulator. Is it widely believed that the phase which appears next upon increasing
U
is a “spontaneously-dimerized” phase which spontaneously breaks the duality and site-centered inversion symmetries
[
80
]
. Moreover, as mentioned in the main text, it is believed that these two states are separated by a 2D Ising critical point. This Ising picture has been substantiated both by weak-coupling bosonization arguments
[
77
,
78
]
and an effective strong-coupling effective spin model formulation
[
79
]
. However, at least one careful numerical study of the transition using finite DMRG finds features inconsistent with Ising criticality
[
93
]
, though the numerical observations are still consistent with a gap to charged excitations and a gapless neutral sector
[
94
]
. These two phases should be identified with the odd-circumference (symmetry-respecting) IQH and (symmetry-breaking) CSL phases, respectively.
