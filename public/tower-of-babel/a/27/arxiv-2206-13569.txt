Abstract
. In this paper, we show how geometry plays in the study of the Furstenberg conjecture (refer to [ 1 ] ). Let p > 1 p>1 and q > 1 q>1 be two relative prime positive integers. We prove that a non-atomic p p - and q q -invariant measure having balanced geometry must be the Lebesgue measure. In the proof, we will not assume the ergodicity of the measure. The result provides an intuitive geometric criterion to either prove the Furstenberg conjecture or construct a counter-example.

1.
Balanced Geometry.

Suppose
\mathbb{R}
is the real line, and suppose
\mathbb{Z}
is the set of integers. We call
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
the unit circle.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be the standard Borel
\sigma
-field on
\mathbb{T}
. Let
\mu_{0}(A)=\int_{A}dx
be the Lebesgue probability measure on
\mathbb{T}
. We use
|x-y|=\mu_{0}([x,y])
to denote the length of the interval
[x,y]
.
Let
\mu
be a non-atomic probability Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
with full support. We simply call it a measure.

Let
\mathbb{N}
be the set of natural numbers. For an integer
p>1
, consider the
p
-action on
\mathbb{T}
,

f_{p}:x\mapsto px\pmod{1}.

The
p
-action gives a partition on
\mathbb{T}
by intervals,

\eta_{1}=\Big\{I_{i}=\Big[\frac{i}{p},\frac{i+1}{p}\Big]\;\Big|\;0\leq i\leq p-1\Big\}.

Pull-back
\eta_{1}
, we get a sequence of nested partitions on
\mathbb{T}
by intervals

\eta_{n}=f^{-(n-1)}_{p}\eta_{1},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

The intervals in
\eta_{n}
can be labeled by using the symbolic space,

\Sigma_{n}=\prod_{0}^{n-1}\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\}

=\{w_{n}=i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{k}\cdots i_{n-1}\;|\;i_{k}\in\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\},0\leq k\leq n-1\}

with the shift

\sigma:w_{n}\in\Sigma_{n}\mapsto\sigma(w_{n})=i_{1}\cdots i_{n-1}\in\Sigma_{n-1}.

The interval
I_{w_{n}}
is defined inductively such that

f_{p}:I_{w_{n}}\in\eta_{n}\to I_{\sigma(w_{n})}\in\eta_{n-1}

is a homeomorphism and

\eta_{n}=\{I_{w_{n}}\;|\;w_{n}\in\Sigma_{n}\}.

For any
w_{n}=i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{n-1}\in\Sigma_{n}
, let
w_{n-1}=i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{n-2}\in\Sigma_{n-1}
. One can check that

I_{w_{n-1}}=\cup_{i=0}^{p-1}I_{w_{n-1}i}\quad\hbox{and}\quad f^{-1}_{p}(I_{w_{n-1}})=\cup_{i=0}^{p-1}I_{iw_{n-1}}.

Take the inverse limit of
\{(\Sigma_{n},\sigma)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, we have the full shift

\sigma:\Sigma\to\Sigma

where

\Sigma=\{w=i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{n-1}\cdots\;|\;i_{n-1}\in\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\},\;1\leq n<\infty\}

and

\sigma(w)=i_{1}\cdots i_{n-1}\cdots.

It gives a symbolic coding for every point in
\mathbb{T}
with

\{x_{w}\}=\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{w_{n}},\quad w=w_{n}i_{n}\cdots\in\Sigma.

This symbolic coding is almost one-to-one except for those endpoints of
I_{w_{n}}
on which this symbolic coding is two-to-one.

A measure
\mu
is said to be
p
-invariant if

\mu(f^{-1}_{p}(A))=\mu(A)

for all
A\in\mathcal{B}
.

Definition 1
.

We say a measure
\mu
has balanced geometry (with respective to the
p
-action) if there is a constant
C_{0}>0
such that
for any
n\geq 2
and any
w_{n-1}\in\Sigma_{n-1}
and any
0\leq i\leq p-1
,

\frac{\sum_{0\leq j\not=i\leq p-1}\mu(I_{jw_{n-1}})}{\mu(I_{iw_{n-1}})}\geq C_{0}.

The Lebesgue measure
\mu_{0}
is
p
-invariant and has balanced geometry. In Appendix II, we will give a non-trivial example (Example
1
) of
p
-invariant measures having balanced geometry and an example (Example
2
) of
2
-invariant measure having imbalanced geometry.

2.
Balanced Geometry And Rigidity.

The main result in this paper is the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
p>1
and
q>1
are two relative prime positive integers. Suppose
\mu
is a
p
- and
q
-invariant measure having balanced geometry.
Then
\mu
must be the Lebesgue measure
\mu_{0}
.

Proof.

We define a sequence of (probability) measures
\nu_{n}
on
\mathbb{T}
for
n\geq 1
,

\nu_{n}(A)=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{p^{n}-1}\mu\Big(A+\frac{k}{p^{n}}\Big)\quad A\in\mathcal{B}.

Let
g_{i}
,
0\leq i\leq p-1
, be all the inverse branches of
f_{p}
. Then

g_{w_{n}}=g_{i_{0}}\circ\cdots\circ g_{i_{n-1}}\quad w_{n}=i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{n-1}\in\Sigma_{n}

are all the inverse branches of
f^{n}_{p}
. Let
k=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}i_{j}p^{j}
, one can check that

g_{w_{n}}(x)=\frac{x}{p^{n}}+\frac{k}{p^{n}}\pmod{1}.

When
A
is contained in an interval in
\eta_{n}
, we have that

\nu_{n}(A)=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\sum_{w_{n}\in\Sigma_{n}}\mu(g_{w_{n}}(f^{n}_{p}(A)))=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\mu(f_{p}^{-n}(f^{n}_{p}(A)))=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\mu(f^{n}_{p}(A)).

The last equality holds because
\mu
is
p
-invariant.
One can check that
\nu_{n-1}(A)\leq p\nu_{n}(A)
for all
n\geq 1
(we denote
\nu_{0}=\mu
). Thus
\nu_{n-1}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu_{n}
and we have the Radon-Nikodym derivative
d\nu_{n-1}/d\nu_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
. Let

\phi_{n}=\frac{d\mu}{d\nu_{n}}\quad n\geq 1

be the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\mu
to
\nu_{n}
.

For
\nu_{n}
-a.e.
x_{w}
where
w=w_{k}\cdots=i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}\cdots\in\Sigma
and for any
k>n>m\geq 0
, we have that

\frac{\nu_{m}(I_{w_{k}})}{\nu_{m+1}(I_{w_{k}})}=\frac{\frac{1}{p^{m}}\mu(f_{p}^{-m}(f_{p}^{m}(I_{w_{k}})))}{\frac{1}{p^{m+1}}\mu(f_{p}^{-(m+1)}(f_{p}^{(m+1)}(I_{w_{k}})))}

=\frac{p\mu(I_{\sigma^{m}(w_{k})})}{\mu(f_{p}^{-1}(f_{p}(I_{\sigma^{m}(w_{k})})))}=\frac{p\mu(I_{\sigma^{m}(w_{k})})}{\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\mu(I_{j\sigma^{m+1}(w_{k})})}

=\frac{p}{1+\frac{\sum_{0\leq j\leq p-1,j\sigma^{m+1}(w_{k})\not=\sigma^{m}(w_{k})}\mu(I_{j\sigma^{m+1}(w_{k})})}{\mu(I_{\sigma^{m}(w_{k})})}}\leq\frac{p}{1+C_{0}}

because of balanced geometry. Therefore we have that

\frac{\mu(I_{w_{k}})}{\nu_{n}(I_{w_{k}})}=\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\frac{\nu_{m}(I_{w_{k}})}{\nu_{m+1}(I_{w_{k}})}\leq\frac{p^{n}}{(1+C_{0})^{n}}

and

(1)

~\phi_{n}(x_{w})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\mu(I_{w_{k}})}{\nu_{n}(I_{w_{k}})}\leq\frac{p^{n}}{(1+C_{0})^{n}}

(refer to Lemma
2
in Appendix I).

Let
B_{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{T}\;|\;\phi_{n}(x)\hbox{ is not defined}\}
. We have
\mu(B_{n})\leq\nu_{n}(B_{n})=0
.
Consider

A_{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{T}\setminus B_{n}\;|\;\phi_{n}(x)=0\}=\phi_{n}^{-1}(0).

It is measurable and

\mu(A_{n})=\int_{A_{n}}\;d\mu(x)=\int_{A_{n}}\phi_{n}(x)\;d\nu_{n}(x)=0

Let

K=\{x\;|\;\phi_{n}(x)>0\mbox{ for all }n\geq 1\}=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}(A_{n}\cup B_{n})^{c}=\Big(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}(A_{n}\cup B_{n})\Big)^{c}

Then we have that
\mu(K)=1
.
On
K
, we have

d\nu_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(x)}\;d\mu(x).

for all
n\geq 1
.

The measure
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure
\mu_{0}
if and only if its Fourier coefficient
f_{m}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{2\pi imx}d\mu(x)=0
for all integers
m\geq 1
. Denote
e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}
and let us fix an integer
m\geq 1
. Let
T_{n}
and
C_{1}
and
n_{0}
be the numbers in Lemma
1
in Appendix I. For each
n>n_{0}
, we define

\beta_{T_{n}}(x)=\frac{1}{T_{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{T_{n}-1}e(mq^{k}x).

Then

\int_{K}\frac{|\beta_{T_{n}}(x)|^{2}}{\phi_{n}(x)}\,d\mu(x)=\int_{K}|\beta_{T_{n}}(x)|^{2}\,d\nu_{n}(x)

=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\int_{K}\sum_{k=0}^{p^{n}-1}\Big|\beta_{T_{n}}\Big(x+\frac{k}{p^{n}}\Big)\Big|^{2}\,d\mu(x)=\int_{K}h(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}h(x)\,d\mu(x),

where

h(x)=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\frac{1}{T_{n}^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{T_{n}-1}\sum_{l=0}^{T_{n}-1}e(m(q^{k}-q^{l})x)\sum_{j=0}^{p^{n}-1}e\Big(m\Big(\frac{(q^{k}-p^{l})j}{p^{n}}\Big)\Big).

If
0\leq k\neq l\leq T_{n}
, then
q^{k}\neq q^{l}\mod p^{n}
. This implies that

\sum_{j=0}^{p^{n}-1}e\Big(m\Big(\frac{(q^{k}-q^{l})j}{p^{n}}\Big)\Big)=0.

So we have
h(x)=1/T_{n}
, and
\int_{\mathbb{T}}h(x)d\mu(x)=1/T_{n}
. This implies that

\int_{K}\frac{|\beta_{T_{n}}(x)|^{2}}{\phi_{n}(x)}\,d\mu(x)=\frac{1}{T_{n}}.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\beta_{T_{n}}(x)\,d\mu(x)\right|^{2}=\left|\int_{K}\beta_{T_{n}}(x)\,d\mu(x)\right|^{2}\leq\int_{K}\frac{|\beta_{T_{n}}(x)|^{2}}{\phi_{n}(x)}\,d\mu(x)\cdot\int_{K}\phi_{n}(x)\,d\mu(x)

=\frac{1}{T_{n}}\int_{K}\phi_{n}(x)\,d\mu(x)=\frac{1}{C_{1}p^{n}}\int_{K}\phi_{n}(x)\,d\mu(x)=\frac{1}{C_{1}p^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\phi_{n}(x)\,d\mu(x).

From (
1
), we have

\Big|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\beta_{n}(x)\,d\mu(x)\Big|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{C_{1}}(1+C_{0})^{\frac{n}{2}}}\to 0\quad\hbox{as}\quad n\to\infty.

Since
\mu
is
q
-invariant,

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\beta_{T_{n}}(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e(mx)\,d\mu(x),\;\;\forall\;n\geq n_{0},

we get

\int_{\mathbb{T}}e(mx)\,d\mu(x)=0.

Since
m\geq 1
is any integer,
\mu=\mu_{0}
. This completes the proof of the theorem.

∎

Remark 1
.

In Theorem
1
, we have no assumption about the ergodicity on
\mu
. The balanced geometry condition is not preserved under the ergodic decomposition. One can show that the balanced geometry condition plus the ergodicity assumption on
\mu
is essentially equivalent to the positivity of the metric entropy
h_{\mu}(f_{p})
. Theorem
1
plus the ergodicity assumption will give Rudolph’s theorem in
[
7
]
(see
[
6
]
in a more general setting).
However, the balanced geometry condition gives an intuitive geometric view to either verify Furstenburg’s conjecture or construct a counter-example (as suggested by Dennis Sullivan to the author). The counter-example should have very imbalanced geometry like the devil’s staircase but still need to be ergodic.

3.
Appendix I

In Appendix I, we give a detailed proof of a result in number theory (Lemma
1
) and state the martingale convergence theorem (Lemma
2
) in our case. Both of them are used in the proof of Theorem
1
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
p>1
and
q>1
are two relatively prime integers. Suppose
a\geq 1
is an interger.
Let

T_{n}=\#\Big(\{aq^{k}\pmod{p^{n}}\;\;|\;k=0,1,\cdots\}\Big)

Then there is a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(a,p,q)>0
and a constant integer
n_{0}=n_{0}(a,p,q)
such that

T_{n}=C_{1}p^{n},\;\;\forall\;n\geq n_{0}.

Proof.

Let
a=bp^{r}
with
\gcd(b,p)=1
. For every
n\geq r+1
, consider two integers
k>l

such that
aq^{k}=aq^{l}\pmod{p^{n}}
. That is,

aq^{l}(q^{k-l}-1)=mp^{n}

for some integer
m\geq 0
.
This implies that

bq^{l}(q^{k-l}-1)=mp^{n-r}

Since
b
and
q
are both relatively prime to
p
, we have
p^{n-r}|(q^{k-l}-1)
, that is,

q^{k-l}=1\pmod{p^{n-r}}.

Thus
T_{n}
is the smallest positive integer such that
q^{T_{n}}=1\mod p^{n-r}
.

Suppose
q^{T_{n}}=k_{n}p^{n-r}+1
for some integer
k_{n}\geq 1
.
For
n=r+1
, we have

q^{T_{r+1}}=k_{r+1}p+1
.
Let us write
k_{r+1}=u_{0}p^{s}
, where
(u_{0},p)=1
. Then

q^{T_{r+1}}=u_{0}p^{s+1}+1=u_{0}p^{(r+s+1)-r}+1=q^{T_{r+s+1}}.

This implies

q^{pT_{r+s+1}}=(u_{0}p^{s+1}+1)^{p}

=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}p\cr j\end{array}\right)(u_{0}p^{s+1})^{p-j}=(u_{0}p^{s+1})^{p}+p(u_{0}p^{s+1})^{p-1}+\ldots+p(u_{0}p^{s+1})+1

=u_{1}p^{s+2}+1.

Note that

u_{1}=u_{0}+p(\cdots)=u_{0}\pmod{p}
.
Since
(u_{0},p)=1
, we have
(u_{1},p)=1
and thus

u_{1}p^{s+2}+1=q^{T_{r+s+2}}.

Therefore,

pT_{r+s+1}=T_{r+s+2}.

Inductively we get

T_{r+s+1+m}=p^{m}T_{r+s+1},\;\;\forall\;m\geq 0.

Setting
n_{0}=r+s+1
we have

T_{n_{0}+m}=p^{m}T_{n_{0}},\;\;\forall\;m\geq 0.

That is,

\frac{T_{n_{0}+m}}{p^{n_{0}+m}}=\frac{T_{n_{0}}}{p^{n_{0}}},\;\;\forall m\geq 0.

Therefore, let

C_{1}=\frac{T_{n_{0}}}{p^{n_{0}}}.

Then

T_{n}=C_{1}p^{n},\quad\forall n\geq n_{0}.

∎

Consider
\mathcal{P}=(\mathbb{T},\mathcal{B},\nu_{n})
as a probability space where
\nu_{n}
is the probability measure constructed in the proof of Theorem
1
. Consider
X=\phi_{n}
as a random variable on
\mathcal{P}
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{k}
be the
\sigma
-field generated
by
\eta_{k}=\{I_{w_{k}}|w_{k}\in\Sigma_{k}\}
for every
k\geq 1
. Then we have a sequence of increasing
\sigma
-fields

\mathcal{B}_{1}\subset\mathcal{B}_{2}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{B}_{k}\subset\mathcal{B}_{k+1}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{B}

and
\mathcal{B}=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}_{k}
. Let
X_{k}=E(X|\mathcal{B}_{k})
be the expectation of
X
on
\mathcal{B}_{k}
, which is a
\mathcal{B}_{k}
-measurable function. Then we have
X_{k}=E(X_{k+1}|\mathcal{B}_{k})
,
\nu_{n}
-a.e.. Thus,

\{(X_{k},\mathcal{B}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

gives us a martingale on
\mathcal{P}
and

X_{k}(x)=\frac{\int_{I_{w_{k}}}Xd\nu_{n}}{\nu_{n}(I_{w_{k}})}=\frac{\mu(I_{w_{k}})}{\nu_{n}(I_{w_{k}})}\quad\hbox{for $\nu_{n}$-a.e. $x\in I_{w_{k}}$}.

Now we apply Theorem 5.7 in
[
8
]
to our case.

Lemma 2

(Martingale Convergence Theorem)
.

Since
X\in L_{1}(\nu_{n})
, the martingale
\{X_{k}=E(X|\mathcal{B}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
converges to
X
for almost all points with respect to
\nu_{n}
as
k
goes to
\infty
.

4.
Appendix II

In Appendix II, we give a non-trivial example of a
p
-invariant measure having balanced geometry (Example
1
) and an example of
p
-invariant measure having imbalanced geometry (Example
2
). From these two examples, one can see that the balanced geometry condition is interesting in studying the rigidity theory.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
is a covering map of degree
p
. We call it a circle endomorphism. We say
f
is
C^{1}
if it has a continuous derivative
f^{\prime}
. We say a
C^{1}
circle endomorphism
f
expanding if there are two constants
D>0
and
\lambda>1
such that

|(f^{n})^{\prime}(x)|\geq D\lambda^{n}\quad x\in\mathbb{T}.

We say
f
is
C^{1+\alpha}
if
f^{\prime}
is an
\alpha
-Hölder continuous function for some
0<\alpha\leq 1
, that is, there are two constants
0<d<1
and
D>0
such that

|f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(y)|\leq D|x-y|^{\alpha}\quad x,y\in\mathbb{T}\hbox{ with }|x-y|<d.

Any
C^{1+\alpha}
expanding endomorphism
f
has a unique smooth
f
-invariant probability measure
\nu
(refer to
[
3
,
4
]
). This means that we have a positive
\alpha
-Hölder continuous function
\rho(x)
on
\mathbb{T}
such that

\nu(A)=\int_{A}\rho(x)dx\quad\hbox{and}\quad\nu(f^{-1}(A))=\nu(A)\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{B}.

The formula
h_{0}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\rho(x)dx
defines a
C^{1+\alpha}
-diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{T}
. One can check that

g=h_{0}\circ f\circ h_{0}^{-1}

preserves the Lebesgue measure
\mu_{0}
.
Furthermore,
g
is topologically conjugate to
f_{p}
, that is, we have a homeomorphism
h:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
such that

g\circ h=h\circ f_{p}

Let
\mu=(h^{-1})_{*}\mu_{0}
defined as
\mu(A)=\mu_{0}(h(A))
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}
. In general,
\mu
is totally singular with respective to
\mu_{0}
since, otherwise,
g=f_{p}
(see
[
4
]
).
Actually, in general,
h\not=id
is a quasisymmetric homomorphism and as long as
h
is symmetric, then
h=id
(see
[
4
]
).

Example 1
.

The measure
\mu
constructed above is
p
-invariant and has balanced geometry.

Proof.

For
p
-invariance, it is because that for any
A\in\mathcal{B}
,

\mu(f_{p}^{-1}(A))=\mu_{0}(h(f_{p}^{-1}(A)))=\mu_{0}(g^{-1}(h(A)))=\mu_{0}(h(A))=\mu(A).

For balanced geometry, without loss of generality, we assume that

1<a=\min_{x\in\mathbb{T}}|g^{\prime}(x)|\leq A=\max_{x\in\mathbb{T}}|g^{\prime}(x)|<\infty.

For any
n\geq 2
and
w_{n-1}\in\Sigma_{n-1}
and
0\leq i\leq p-1
, we have that

f_{p}^{-1}(I_{w_{n-1}})=I_{iw_{n-1}}\cup\cup_{0\leq j\not=i\leq p-1}I_{jw_{n-1}}.

and

g^{-1}(h(I_{w_{n}}))=h(I_{iw_{n-1}})\cup\cup_{0\leq j\not=i\leq p-1}h(I_{jw_{n-1}}).

Since
g
preserves the Lebesgue measure
\mu_{0}
, we have that

|h(I_{w_{n-1}})|=|g^{-1}(h(I_{w_{n}}))|=|h(I_{iw_{n-1}})|+\sum_{0\leq j\not=i\leq p-1}|h(I_{jw_{n-1}})|.

Since
g(h(I_{iw_{n-1}}))=h(I_{w_{n-1}})
, we have that, for any
j\not=i
,

\frac{|g(h(I_{iw_{n-1}}))|}{|h(I_{iw_{n-1}})|}=1+\frac{|h(I_{jw_{n-1}})|}{|h(I_{iw_{n-1}})|}+\sum_{0\leq k\not=i,j\leq p-1}\frac{|h(I_{kw_{n-1}})|}{|h(I_{iw_{n-1}})|}.

This implies that

\frac{|h(I_{jw_{n-1}})|}{|h(I_{iw_{n-1}})|}\Big(1+\sum_{0\leq k\not=i,j\leq p-1}\frac{|h(I_{kw_{n-1}})|}{|h(I_{jw_{n-1}})|}\Big)

=\frac{|g(h(I_{iw_{n-1}}))|}{|h(I_{iw_{n-1}})|}-1\geq a-1.

Thus, we have that, for the constant
D=1+(p-2)A/a>0
,

\frac{\mu(I_{jw_{n-1}})}{\mu(I_{iw_{n-1}})}=\frac{|h(I_{jw_{n-1}})|}{|h(I_{iw_{n-1}})|}\geq C_{0}=\frac{a-1}{D}>0.

This proves that
\mu
has balanced geometry.

Example 2
.

The measure
\mu
is
2
-invariant and has imbalanced geometry.

Proof.

For
2
-invariance, it is because that for any
A\in\mathcal{B}
,

\mu(f_{p}^{-1}(A))=\mu_{0}(h(f_{p}^{-1}(A)))=\mu_{0}(g^{-1}(h(A)))=\mu_{0}(h(A))=\mu(A).

For any
n\geq 2
and
0_{n-1}=\underbrace{0\cdots 0}_{n-1}\in\Sigma_{n-1}
and
i=0
, we have that

f_{p}^{-1}(I_{0_{n-1}})=I_{0_{n}}\cup I_{10_{n-1}}.

and

g^{-1}(h(I_{0_{n-1}}))=h(I_{0_{n}})\cup h(I_{10_{n-1}}).

Since
g
preserves the Lebesgue measure
\mu_{0}
, we have that

|h(I_{0_{n-1}})|=|g^{-1}(h(I_{0_{n}}))|=|h(I_{0_{n}})|+|h(I_{10_{n-1}})|

and that

\frac{|h(I_{0_{n-1}})|}{|h(I_{0_{n}})|}=1+\frac{|h(I_{10_{n-1}})|}{|h(I_{0_{n}})|}

Since
g(h(I_{0_{n}}))=h(I_{0_{n-1}})
and
g^{\prime}(0)=1
,

\frac{|h(I_{10_{n-1}})|}{|h(I_{0_{n}})|}\to 0\quad\hbox{as}\quad n\to\infty.

This says that
\mu
has imbalanced geometry.
∎
