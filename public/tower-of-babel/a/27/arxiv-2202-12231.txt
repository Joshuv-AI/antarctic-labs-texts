Priyavrat Deshpande, Krishna Menon, Writika Sarkar

Abstract
. A hyperplane arrangement in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} is a finite collection of affine hyperplanes. Counting regions of hyperplane arrangements is an active research direction in enumerative combinatorics. In this paper, we consider the arrangement 𝒜 n ( m ) \mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)} in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} given by { x i = 0 ∣ i ∈ [ n ] } ∪ { x i = a k x j ∣ k ∈ [ − m , m ] , 1 ≤ i < j ≤ n } \{x_{i}=0\mid i\in[n]\}\cup\{x_{i}=a^{k}x_{j}\mid k\in[-m,m],1\leq i<j\leq n\} for some fixed a > 1 a>1 . It turns out that this family of arrangements is closely related to the well-studied extended Catalan arrangement of type A A . We prove that the number of regions of 𝒜 n ( m ) \mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)} is a certain generalization of Catalan numbers called two parameter Fuss-Catalan numbers. We then exhibit a bijection between these regions and certain decorated Dyck paths. We also compute the characteristic polynomial and give a combinatorial interpretation for its coefficients. Most of our results also generalize to sub-arrangements of 𝒜 n ( m ) \mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)} by relating them to deformations of the braid arrangement.

1.
Introduction

A
hyperplane arrangement

\mathcal{A}
is a finite collection of affine hyperplanes (i.e., codimension
1
subspaces and their translates) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Without loss of generality we assume that arrangements we consider are
essential
, i.e., the subspace spanned by the normal vectors is the ambient vector space.
A
flat
of
\mathcal{A}
is a nonempty intersection of some of the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}
; the ambient vector space is a flat since it is an intersection of no hyperplanes.
Flats are naturally ordered by reverse set inclusion; the resulting poset is called the
intersection poset
and is denoted by
\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{A})
.
A
region
of
\mathcal{A}
is a connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{A}
.
Counting the number of regions of arrangements using diverse combinatorial methods is an active area of research.

The
characteristic polynomial
of
\mathcal{A}
is defined as

\chi_{\mathcal{A}}(t):=\sum_{x\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{A})}\mu(\hat{0},x)\,t^{\dim(x)}

where
\mu
is the Möbius function of the intersection poset and
\hat{0}
corresponds to the flat
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Using the fact that every interval of the intersection poset of an arrangement is a geometric lattice, we have

(1)

\chi_{\mathcal{A}}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}c_{i}t^{i}

where
c_{i}
is a non-negative integer for all
0\leq i\leq n

[
11
, Corollary 3.4]
.
The characteristic polynomial is a fundamental combinatorial and topological invariant of the arrangement and plays a significant role throughout the theory of hyperplane arrangements.

In this article, our focus is on the enumerative aspects of (rational) arrangements in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In that direction we have the following seminal result by Zaslavsky.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the number of regions of
\mathcal{A}
is given by

\displaystyle r(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\chi_{\mathcal{A}}(-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}.

The finite field method, developed by Athanasiadis
[
1
]
, converts the computation of the characteristic polynomial to a point counting problem.
A combination of these two results allowed for the computation of the number of regions of several arrangements of interest.

Another way to count the number of regions is to give a bijective proof.
This approach involves finding a combinatorially defined set whose elements are in bijection with the regions of the given arrangement and are easier to count.
For example, the
braid arrangement
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by

\{x_{i}-x_{j}=0\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n\}.

It is straightforward to verify that its regions correspond to the permutations of
[n]
.
Hence the number of regions of the braid arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
n!
.

Another important class of arrangements is the extended Catalan arrangements.
For any
m,n\geq 1
, the
m
-Catalan arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by

\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}:=\{x_{i}-x_{j}=k\mid k\in[-m,m],1\leq i<j\leq n\}.

These arrangements and their sub-arrangements have been studied in great detail by Bernardi in
[
4
]
.
It is well-known that the number of regions of
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}
where
x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{n}
(also known as the dominant regions) is given by

A_{n}(m,1):=\frac{1}{n(m+1)+1}\binom{n(m+1)+1}{n}.

Using this, it is easy to see that
r(\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)})=n!\cdot A_{n}(m,1)
.
The numbers
A_{n}(m,1)
are called the extended Catalan numbers or the Fuss-Catalan numbers.

The two parameter Fuss-Catalan numbers or the Raney numbers are given by

A_{n}(m,r):=\frac{r}{n(m+1)+r}\binom{n(m+1)+r}{n}

for all positive integers
n,m,r
.
These numbers were first studied by Raney in
[
9
]
.

Just as the extended Catalan arrangement works for
r=1
, one could ask if, for each
r>1
, there is a nice family of arrangements whose regions are given by multiples of
A_{n}(m,r)
, preferably
n!\cdot A_{n}(m,r)
.
In the present article we study a family of arrangements that works for
r=2
.
For any
m,n\geq 1
, the arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

(2)

\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}:=\{x_{i}=0\mid i\in[n]\}\cup\{x_{i}=a^{k}x_{j}\mid k\in[-m,m],1\leq i<j\leq n\}

where
a>1
is a fixed real number.
In
Section

2
, we show that
a
does not affect the combinatorics of the arrangement.
The arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}
is a generalization of the arrangement
\alpha_{n}
(which is
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(1)}
with
a=2
) defined in
[
10
]
.

The usual Catalan numbers are given by
A_{n}(1,1)
.
They occur in various counting problems; in fact, a vast variety of combinatorial objects are counted by these numbers.
For example, the reader can look at the book
[
12
]
by Stanley which contains more than 200 interpretations of Catalan numbers.
The extended Catalan numbers also count many interesting objects.
Other than the regions of
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}
, the following objects are also counted by
n!\cdot A_{n}(m,1)
:

(1)

Certain non-nesting partitions (follows from
[
2
, Theorem 2.2]
) or an equivalent formulation called
m
-sketches (introduced by Bernardi
[
4
, Section 8]
).

(2)

Labeled
m
-ary trees (Bernardi
[
4
]
and Stanley
[
12
, part (b) of A14]
).

(3)

Generalized Dyck paths (
[
12
, part (c) of A14]
).

There are well-known bijections between the regions of
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}
and these combinatorial objects (for example, see
[
4
, Section 8.1]
). An outcome of our work is a generalization of these bijections.
In particular, we introduce analogs of above objects which correspond to the regions of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}
.

When studying an arrangement, another interesting question is whether the coefficients of its characteristic polynomial can be combinatorially interpreted.
By
Theorem

1.1
, we know that the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients is the number of regions.
Hence, one could ask if there is a statistic on the regions whose distribution is given by the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial.
The characteristic polynomial of the braid arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

t(t-1)\cdots(t-n+1)

[
11
, Corollary 2.2]
.
Hence, the coefficients are the Stirling numbers of the first kind.
Consequently, the distribution of the statistic ‘number of cycles’ on the set of permutations of
[n]
(which correspond to the regions of the arrangement) is given by the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial.

The main aim of this paper is to study the arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}
and its sub-arrangements.
In
Section

2
, we compute the characteristic polynomial of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}
.
We also relate the characteristic polynomial of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}
to that of
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}
.
In
Section

3
, we describe certain combinatorial objects called decorated Dyck paths and decorated non-nesting partitions and show that they are in bijection with the regions of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}
.
Finally in
Section

4
, we describe a statistic on decorated Dyck paths whose distribution is given by the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}
.
In all sections, we also show that the results generalize to several sub-arrangements of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}
.

as long as

is a positive integer.
Similarly, we have the deformation of the braid arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\mathcal{C}_{n}^{S}:=\{x_{i}-x_{j}=k\mid k\in S,1\leq i<j\leq n\}.

Theorem 2.10
.

For any finite set of integers
S
, we have

\chi_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{S}}(t)=\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{S}}(t-1).

To show this, we will take help of a slightly modified version of
Theorem

2.1
, for deformations of the braid arrangement (note that the arrangements
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{S}
are such deformations), also given by Athanasiadis in
[
3
]
. We state this in our context.

Theorem 2.11

(
[
3
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a deformation of the braid arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists an integer
k
such that for all integers
q
greater than
k
,

\chi(\mathcal{A},q)=\#\left(\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}\setminus V_{\mathcal{A}}\right)

where
V_{\mathcal{A}}
is the union of the hyperplanes in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
obtained by reducing
\mathcal{A}
mod
q
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.10
.

By
Theorem

2.6
, we choose
a
which is a primitive root modulo infinitely many primes. By
Theorem

2.11
, we choose
q=p-1
for a large enough prime
p
for which
a
is a primitive root. Now we have
\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{S}}(q)=|T|
where

T=\{(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}\mid x_{i}-x_{j}\neq k,k\in S,1\leq i<j\leq n\}.

Note that the condition
x_{i}-x_{j}\neq k\pmod{q}
is equivalent to
a^{x_{i}-x_{j}}\neq a^{k}\pmod{p}
, that is,
a^{x_{1}}\neq a^{k}\cdot a^{x_{j}}\pmod{p}
. Now the distinct powers of
a
with exponents modulo
q
give distinct (non zero) residues modulo
p
. Hence if we consider the set
T^{\prime}
defined as

T^{\prime}=\{(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}\mid y_{i}\neq 0,y_{i}\neq a^{k}y_{j},k\in S,1\leq i<j\leq n\},

then
|T|=|T^{\prime}|
. Now using
Theorem

2.1
on the arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{S}
, we get that
|T^{\prime}|=\chi_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{S}}(p)
, hence
\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{S}}(q)=|T|=|T^{\prime}|=\chi_{A_{n}^{S}}(p)=\chi_{A_{n}^{S}}(q+1)
. Since this holds for infinitely many
q=p-1
, and since
\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{S}},\chi_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{S}}
are both polynomials, it follows that for all
t
,

\chi_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{S}}(t)=\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{S}}(t-1).

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

Though we will only make use of
Theorem

2.10
in the stated form, we note that it can be generalized.
Let
n\geq 1
and
\mathbf{S}=(S_{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
be a sequence of finite sets of integers.
To such a tuple
\mathbf{S}
, we associate the arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{S}}:=\{x_{i}=0\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}\cup\{x_{i}=a^{k}x_{j}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n,\ k\in S_{i,j}\}

and the deformation of the braid arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{S}}:=\{x_{i}-x_{j}=k\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n,\ k\in S_{i,j}\}.

Then, just as above, we have

\chi_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{S}}}(t)=\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{S}}}(t-1).

The following corollary of
Theorem

2.10
gives us the relationship between the characteristic polynomials of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}
and
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}
.

Corollary 2.13
.

We have
\chi_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}}(t)=\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}}(t-1)
.

It was already shown by Athanasiadis in
[
1
, Section 5]
that

\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}}(t)=t(t-mn-1)(t-mn-2)\dots(t-mn-n+1).

Hence
Corollary

2.13
gives us a way to derive either of
\chi_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}}
and
\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}}
from each other.

The combinatorial relationship between
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}
and
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}
will be further detailed in
Section

Proposition 3.18

Any two decorated

The positions of the blocks relative to each other describe inequalities corresponding to hyperplanes of the form

for some

and distinct

.
Hence any two decorated non-nesting partitions in the same region of
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{(m)}
must result in the same labeled arc diagram when the red line is deleted.

Next, suppose that we are given a decorated non-nesting partition with a tangled block after the red line.
Suppose the label of the block is

Consider a decorated non-nesting partition obtained by changing the position of the red line so that the block labeled

We show that these two decorated non-nesting partitions cannot be in the same region of
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{(m)}

Since

This shows that any two decorated non-nesting partitions in the same region of

Result 3.21

For any

r(\Gamma_{n}^{(m)})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(mk+1)^{k-1}(m(n-k)+1)^{n-k-1}.

For any

r(\Delta_{n}^{(m)})=r(\Gamma_{n}^{(m)})-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k}k(m(k-1)+1)^{k-2}(m(n-k)+1)^{n-k-1}.

The ideas that we used to relate the regions of

Theorem 3.23

For any finite set of integers

r(\mathcal{A}_{n}^{S})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}r(\mathcal{C}_{k}^{S})r(\mathcal{C}_{n-k}^{S}).

4.
A statistic on decorated Dyck paths

In this section, we define a statistic on decorated
m
-Dyck paths of size
n
whose distribution is given by the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}
.

From
Corollary

2.13
, we know that
\chi_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}}(t)=\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}}(t-1)
.
This allows us to use the interpretation of the coefficients of
\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}}(t)
from
[
6
]
to describe our required statistic.
We first recall this interpretation.

We slightly extend the definition of a labeled
m
-Dyck path of size
n
(see
Definition

3.5
) by omitting the condition that the labels on the up-steps should be
[n]
.
However, we still assume that the labels are distinct positive integers.

A labeled Dyck path breaks up into
primitive
parts based on when it touches the
x
-axis.
If a labeled Dyck path has
k
primitive parts with label set
S
, then we break the path into
compartments
as follows.
If the number largest label is in the
i_{1}^{th}
primitive part, then the primitive parts up to the
i_{1}^{th}
form the first compartment.
Let
j
be the largest number in
S\setminus A
where
A
is the set of labels in first compartment.
If
j
is in the
i_{2}^{th}
primitive part then the primitive parts after the
i_{1}^{th}
up to the
i_{2}^{th}
form the second compartment.
Continuing this way, we break up a labeled Dyck path into compartments.

It can be checked that knowing the compartments (without the order in which they appear) is enough to reconstruct a labeled Dyck path.
Hence they form “connected components” of the labeled Dyck path.
This means that a labeled Dyck path with label set
S
is specified by a partition of

We have the following result from
[
6
]
.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
6
, Result 4.13]
)
.

The absolute value of the coefficient of
t^{j}
in
\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}}(t)
is the number of labeled
m
-Dyck paths with label set
[n]
which have
j
compartments.

We now turn to decorated Dyck paths.
Just as for non-nesting partitions, a decorated
m
-Dyck path of size
n
can be seen as an ordered pair of labeled
m
-Dyck paths having disjoint label sets whose union is
[n]
.
From the discussion above, this is the same as a collection of connected labeled Dyck paths having disjoint labels sets whose union is
[n]
along with a choice of some of these Dyck paths.
This can be seen by breaking up each labeled Dyck path in the pair into connected components and specifying which ones are from the second Dyck path.

We now describe the required statistic on decorated Dyck paths.
In the following theorem, we view decorated Dyck paths as a pair of labeled Dyck paths.

Theorem 4.3
.

The absolute value of the coefficient of
t^{j}
in
\chi_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}}(t)
is the number of decorated
m
-Dyck paths of size
n
for which the second labeled Dyck path has
j
compartments.

Proof.

We set
C(m,n,j)
to be the absolute value of the coefficient of
t^{j}
in
\chi_{\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(m)}}(t)
and
P(m,n,j)
to be the absolute value of the coefficient of
t^{j}
in
\chi_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(m)}}(t)
.
From
Corollary

2.13
and the fact that the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of any arrangement alternate in sign (see
[
11
, Corollary 3.4]
), we get

P(m,n,j)=\sum_{i=j}^{n}C(m,n,i)\binom{i}{j}.

The result now follows from the alternate description of decorated Dyck paths mentioned before the statement of the theorem.
