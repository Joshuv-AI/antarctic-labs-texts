Random cubic planar graphs converge to the Brownian sphere
Marie Albenque, Éric Fusy, Thomas Lehéricy

Abstract
In this paper, the scaling limit of random connected cubic planar graphs (respectively multigraphs) is shown to be the Brownian sphere. The proof consists in essentially two main steps. First, thanks to the known decomposition of cubic planar graphs into their 3-connected components, the metric structure of a random cubic planar graph is shown to be well approximated by its unique 3-connected component of linear size, with modified distances. Then, Whitney’s theorem ensures that a 3-connected cubic planar graph is the dual of a simple triangulation, for which it is known that the scaling limit is the Brownian sphere. Curien and Le Gall have recently developed a framework to study the modification of distances in general triangulations and in their dual. By extending this framework to simple triangulations, it is shown that 3-connected cubic planar graphs with modified distances converge jointly with their dual triangulation to the Brownian sphere.

Introduction

In recent years, a lot of progress has been achieved in the understanding of the scaling limit of random planar maps (planar graphs embedded in the sphere). Miermont
[
28
]
and Le Gall
[
24
]
established the first results of convergence of random planar maps towards the Brownian sphere. Since these major results, many other families of maps have also been proved to admit the Brownian sphere as their scaling limit.
Moreover, the properties of the Brownian sphere have been thoroughly investigated, we refer to the following surveys and references therein
[
25
,
29

30
]
for nice entry points to this field.

However, much less is known about the scaling limit of random planar
graphs
, which are graphs that can be embedded in the sphere but for which the embedding is not fixed.
In this article, we obtain the first result of convergence of a family of random planar graphs towards the Brownian sphere.
Our main result is the following (a graph or multigraph is called
cubic
if all its vertices have degree
3
):

Theorem 1
.

{thm:main}

Let
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
(resp.
\mathfrak{M}_{n}
) be a uniformly random connected cubic labeled planar graph (resp. multigraph) with
2n
vertices. Let
\mu_{\mathfrak{C}_{n}}
and
\mu_{\mathfrak{M}_{n}}
denote respectively the uniform distribution on
V(\mathfrak{C}_{n})
and
V(\mathfrak{M}_{n})
. Let
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}}
denote the graph distance. Then, there exist constants
\mathrm{c},\mathrm{c}_{m}\in(0,\infty)
such that the following convergence holds:

\left(V(\mathfrak{C}_{n}),\frac{1}{\mathrm{c}\cdot n^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}},\mu_{\mathfrak{C}_{n}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\mathrm{m}_{\infty},

(1)

and

\left(V(\mathfrak{M}_{n}),\frac{1}{\mathrm{c}_{m}\cdot n^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}},\mu_{\mathfrak{M}_{n}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\mathrm{m}_{\infty},

(2)

where the convergence holds in distribution for the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology for measured metric spaces, and
\mathrm{m}_{\infty}
denotes the Brownian sphere.

General strategy of the proof and organization of the paper

Decomposition of cubic planar graphs along their 3-connected components.

The first ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1
is the known decomposition of cubic planar graphs into 3-connected components. This decomposition allows us to use techniques similar to the ones developed in
[
5
,
17
]
and to prove that a random cubic planar graph (resp. cubic planar multigraph) admits a unique giant 3-connected component called its
3-connected core
. This is presented in Section
3
. A key feature is that the metric properties of the original graph can be very precisely approximated by the metric properties of its 3-connected core with random edge-lengths. More precisely, the length of a given edge follows asymptotically a distribution denoted
\nu_{\star}
.
To study this model, a first step is to introduce and study the distribution
\nu_{\star}
, this is undertaken in Section
4
.

Dealing with a 3-connected planar graph allows us to enter the world of planar maps. Indeed, by Whitney’s theorem
[
43
]
, a 3-connected planar graph is known to admit a unique embedding (up to mirror). Hence, 3-connected planar graphs and 3-connected planar maps are the same objects. By duality, 3-connected cubic planar graphs correspond to simple triangulations (i.e. triangulations with no loops nor multiple edges).

Modification of distances in simple triangulations.

In
[
13
]
, Curien and Le Gall studied modifications of distances in triangulations. Their techniques have also been adapted to quadrangulations and general maps by the third author
[
26
]
, and to Eulerian triangulations
[
10
]
. To establish the scaling limit of random 3-connected cubic planar maps with random i.i.d edge-lengths, we extend their framework in two directions. We deal with simple triangulations rather than general triangulations, and we consider the
general
first-passage percolation distance on the edges of the dual map rather than the dual graph distance or the Eden model (corresponding to exponential random variables on the edges of the dual map).
To be able to state our result precisely, we first introduce a couple of definitions and notations.

Fix
\mathrm{m}
a planar map, and write
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
for its dual. Let
\nu
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. We define a (random) metric on
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
as follows. Let
(w_{e})_{e\in E(\mathrm{m}^{\dagger})}
be a collection of random weights indexed by the set of edges of
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
, such that
(w_{e})
are i.i.d random variables sampled from
\nu
. We define the first-passage percolation distance
\mathrm{d}^{\star}_{\nu}
on
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
by setting, for any
u,v\in V(\mathrm{m}^{\dagger})
:

\mathrm{d}^{\star}_{\nu}(u,v)=\inf_{\gamma:u\rightarrow v}\sum_{e\in\gamma}w_{e},

(3)

where the infimum runs over all paths
\gamma
going from
u
to
v
in
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
.

A key ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1
is the following result, which is of independent interest:

Theorem 2
.

{th:LGC_simple}

Let
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
be a uniformly random simple triangulation with
n+2
vertices. Let
\mu_{\mathfrak{t}_{n}}
and
\mu_{\mathfrak{t}_{n}^{\dagger}}
denote respectively the uniform distribution on
V(\mathfrak{t}_{n})
and on
V(\mathfrak{t}_{n}^{\dagger})
. Assume that the support of
\nu
is included in
[\eta_{0},\infty)
for
\eta_{0}>0
, and that
\nu
has exponential tails.

Then, there exists a constant
c_{\nu}>0
such that the following convergence holds jointly in distribution for the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology:

\left(\left(V(\mathfrak{t}_{n}),\Big(\frac{3}{4n}\Big)^{1/4}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}},\mu_{\mathfrak{t}_{n}}\right),\left(V(\mathfrak{t}_{n}^{\dagger}),\Big(\frac{3}{4n}\Big)^{1/4}\frac{1}{c_{\nu}}\cdot\mathrm{d}^{\star}_{\nu},\mu_{\mathfrak{t}_{n}^{\dagger}}\right)\right)\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{(d)}(\mathrm{m}_{\infty},\mathrm{m}_{\infty}),

(4)

where
\mathrm{m}_{\infty}
is the Brownian sphere.

Note that the convergence of the first component was established by Addario-Berry and the first author in
[
1
]
. By applying this theorem to the case where
\nu=\delta_{1}
is the Dirac measure on
\{1\}
, and by applying Whitney’s theorem, we obtain as an immediate corollary:

Theorem 3
.

{thm:3connectedScaling}

Let
\mathfrak{K}_{n}
be a uniformly random 3-connected cubic planar graphs with
2n
vertices. Denote by
\mu_{\mathfrak{K}_{n}}
the uniform distribution on
V(\mathfrak{K}_{n})
. Then, there exists a constant
c_{3}>0
such that the following convergence holds in distribution for the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology:

\left(V(\mathfrak{K}_{n}),\frac{1}{c_{3}n^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}},\mu_{\mathfrak{K}_{n}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{(d)}\mathrm{m}_{\infty},

(5)

where
\mathrm{m}_{\infty}
is the Brownian sphere.

When adapting the framework of Curien and Le Gall to our setting, the main difficulty comes from the fact that we consider simple triangulations rather than (unconstrained) triangulations. The article
[
13
]
relies extensively on the so-called
skeleton decomposition
of triangulations, introduced by Krikun in
[
23
]
. However, the skeleton decomposition does not behave as nicely on simple triangulations as on general triangulations. To circumvent this issue, we apply the skeleton decomposition to the model of quasi-simple triangulations (defined in Section
5
). The very nice property of quasi-simple triangulations is that their skeleton decomposition can be encoded via exactly the same branching process as general triangulations. Therefore, many results of
[
13
]
extend to our setting effortlessly. This comes at the price that quasi-simple triangulations are not invariant under re-rooting (its root plays indeed a special role), so that some arguments need to be adapted. This is the purpose of Section
6
. On the other hand, in his independent preprint
[
38
]
, Benedikt Stufler deals directly with a skeleton decomposition for simple triangulations, where some forbidden configurations have to be handled.

From i.i.d edge weights to the scaling limit of random cubic planar graphs.

Given Theorem
2
, there remain two last steps to prove Theorem
1
. The first one deals with the metric property: indeed, we need to relax the hypothesis that the edge lengths are independent. The second one deals with the measure on the vertices. Roughly speaking, we have to prove that the uniform measure on the vertices of a random connected cubic planar graph on one hand, and on the vertices of its 3-connected core on the other hand, do not differ asymptotically.

More precisely, to prove Theorem
1
, we establish in fact the following joint convergence result:

Theorem 4
.

{th:convJointe}

Let
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
be a uniformly random connected cubic labeled planar graph (resp. multigraph) with
2n
vertices. Write
K(\mathfrak{C}_{n})
for its 3-connected core and
\Delta(\mathfrak{C}_{n})
for the (unlabeled, rooted) dual of
K(\mathfrak{C}_{n})
. We denote respectively by
\mu_{\mathfrak{C}_{n}}
,
\mu_{K(\mathfrak{C}_{n})}
and
\mu_{\Delta(\mathfrak{C}_{n})}
the uniform measures on
V(\mathfrak{C}_{n})
,
V(K(\mathfrak{C}_{n}))
and
V(\Delta(\mathfrak{C}_{n}))
. Moreover, we write
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{C}_{n}}
for the distance on
K(\mathfrak{C}_{n})
induced by the graph distance on
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
.

Then, there exist two positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
(with
c_{2}
explicit) such that:

\left(\left(\mathfrak{C}_{n},\frac{1}{c_{1}n^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}},\mu_{\mathfrak{C}_{n}}\right),\left(K(\mathfrak{C}_{n}),\frac{1}{c_{1}n^{1/4}}\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{C}_{n}},\mu_{K(\mathfrak{C}_{n})}\right),\left(\Delta(\mathfrak{C}_{n}),\frac{1}{c_{2}n^{1/4}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}},\mu_{\Delta(\mathfrak{C}_{n})}\right)\right)\\
\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{(d)}(\mathrm{m}_{\infty},\mathrm{m}_{\infty},\mathrm{m}_{\infty}),

(6)

where
\mathrm{m}_{\infty}
is the Brownian sphere.
Moreover, a similar result holds when replacing
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
by a random connected cubic planar multigraph
\mathfrak{M}_{n}
.

Given Theorem
2
, to establish the joint convergence of the last two coordinates, we construct in Section
7
an explicit coupling between the models with independent edge-lengths (sampled from
\nu_{\star}
) and
\left(K(\mathfrak{C}_{n}),\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{C}_{n}}\right)
. With this coupling, we prove in Theorem
36
that the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between both models tends to 0 in probability.

Finally, in Section
8
, to establish the joint convergence of the first two coordinates, we rely on the methodology developed by Addario-Berry and Wen in
[
2
]
. It involves an explicit projection from the vertices of
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
to the vertices of its
3
-connected core
K(\mathfrak{C}_{n})
. The key points are the property that each vertex is projected to a vertex at distance
o(n^{1/4})
with high probability, and the property that the projection of
\mu_{\mathfrak{C}_{n}}
is asymptotically close to
\mu_{K(\mathfrak{C}_{n})}
(in the sense of the Lévy-Prokhorov distance).

Let us finish by mentioning that an extensive index of notations concludes the paper.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Definitions on graphs and maps

In this article, we consider both multigraphs, and graphs (i.e., multigraphs with no loop nor multiple edges). A multigraph is called
vertex-labeled
if its
n
vertices
carry distinct labels in
[1..n]
, and is called
half-edge-labeled
if its
2m
half-edges carry distinct labels in
[1..2m]
. When dealing with random cubic planar multigraphs
(resp. graphs) we will usually consider that they are half-edge-labeled (resp. vertex-labeled). A connected multigraph is
rooted
if it comes with a distinguished oriented edge called
its
root edge
. The tail vertex of the root edge is called the
root vertex
.

Note that a connected graph or multigraph can be seen as a discrete metric space, where the distance between two vertices is the minimal length over the paths connecting them.
More generally, we will consider so-called
metric graphs
, i.e., graphs where every edge
e
carries a length
\ell(e)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
.
In such a graph seen as a metric space, the length of a path
\gamma=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{m})

is defined as
L(\gamma)=\ell(e_{1})+\cdots+\ell(e_{m})
, and the distance between two vertices is the length of a
shortest path connecting them (on the other hand, in the context of metric graphs, we will use the notation
|\gamma|
for the number of edges in
\gamma
).

A
planar map
(shortly, a map)
\mathrm{m}
is a connected multigraph properly embedded on the sphere, up to orientation-preserving homeomorphism. A
face
of
\mathrm{m}

is a connected component of the sphere cut by the embedding, a
corner
of
\mathrm{m}
is a sector between two consecutive edges around a vertex (a corner thus lies in a unique face of
\mathrm{m}
).

The
degree
of a vertex (resp. face) of
\mathrm{m}
is the number of corners that are incident to it. We write
E(\mathrm{m}),V(\mathrm{m})
and
F(\mathrm{m})
for the set of edges, vertices and faces of
\mathrm{m}
respectively.

A
rooted map
is a map that comes
with a distinguished oriented edge called its
root edge
, i.e., the underlying multigraph is rooted.
The face on the right of the root edge is called the
root face

(also called the
outer face
, since it is usually taken as the unbounded face
when the embedding of the map is represented on the plane), and the corner incident to the root vertex just after the root edge in clockwise order is called the
root corner
.
Similarly, vertices incident to the root face are called
outer vertices
, and
inner
vertices otherwise.

In general, maps are considered as unlabeled (whether on vertices or on half-edges).
Indeed, in contrast to the situation for multigraphs, the rooting operation is sufficient to suppress the possibility of having non-trivial automorphisms.
A map is
simple
if the underlying multigraph is a graph, i.e. if there is no double edge and no loop.

For
\mathrm{m}
a map, the
dual map

\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
is constructed as follows (see Figure
1
):
we add a “type F” vertex in every face of
\mathrm{m}
. Then for every edge
e
of
\mathrm{m}
, we draw an edge
e^{\dagger}
(the
dual edge
of
e
) between the type F vertices of the two faces that are incident to
e
, in such a way that
e^{\dagger}
crosses
e
and no other edge (including the new ones) — if both sides of
e
are incident to the same face, then
e^{\dagger}
is a loop. Then
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
has vertex set the set of type F vertices, and edge set the set of dual edges. If
\mathrm{m}
is rooted, the root edge of

\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
is taken as the dual of the root edge
e
of
\mathrm{m}
, oriented in such a way that it crosses
e
in the counterclockwise direction around the root vertex.

Figure 1:
Two examples of triangulations superimposed with their dual. The one on the left is non-simple while the one on the right is simple. The dual cubic map is
3
-connected only
for the example on the right.

One can see from the definition that
(\mathrm{m}^{\dagger})^{\dagger}
is the map obtained from
\mathrm{m}
by reversing the direction of its root edge. Furthermore, the one-to-one correspondence between the faces of
\mathrm{m}
and the vertices of
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
preserves the degree: if
f
is a face of
\mathrm{m}
, and
f^{\dagger}
is the associated vertex of
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
, then
\deg_{\mathrm{m}}(f)=\deg_{\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}}(f^{\dagger})
. The same holds for vertices of
\mathrm{m}
: every face of
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
contains a unique vertex of
\mathrm{m}
, and if
v
is a vertex of
\mathrm{m}
and
v^{\dagger}
is the face of
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
that contains it, then
\deg_{\mathrm{m}}(v)=\deg_{\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}}(v^{\dagger})
.

A multigraph or map is called
cubic
if every vertex has degree
3
. The
size
of such a multigraph or map is the integer
n\geqslant 1
such that
the number of vertices is
2n
and the number of edges is
3n
. By Euler’s formula, a cubic map of size
n
has
n+2
faces.
On the other hand, a
triangulation
is a map where all faces have degree
3
.
Since taking the dual of a map sends vertices of the primal to faces of the dual and vice-versa, it follows that the dual of triangulations are cubic maps; and conversely, the dual of cubic maps are triangulations. A triangulation with
n+2
vertices thus has
2n
faces and
3n
edges.

A multigraph is called
3
-connected
it is simple, has at least
4
vertices, and at least
3
vertices need to be deleted to disconnect it.
By Whitney’s theorem
[
43
]
, such a graph has exactly two embeddings on the sphere, which differ by a mirror.
A
3
-connected map
is a map whose underlying graph is
3
-connected. It is known that duality preserves
3
-connectivity
[
31
]
,
and that a triangulation is
3
-connected if and only if it is simple.

Remark 1
.

{rk:3conn}

For a 3-connected graph
\mathrm{k}
that is vertex-labeled and rooted, we can canonically choose one of the two embeddings
R,R^{\prime}
(which differ by a mirror) as follows.
In
R
we let
f_{1},f_{2}
be the two faces incident to the root edge
e
.
Let
v_{0}
be the root-vertex, and let
v_{1}
(resp.
v_{2}
) be the unique vertex of
f_{1}
(resp.
f_{2}
) adjacent to
v_{0}
and not incident to the root edge.
By 3-connectivity, we have
v_{1}\neq v_{2}
. If the label of
v_{1}
is smaller than the label of
v_{2}
, then we take
R
as the embedding associated to
\mathrm{k}
, otherwise we take
R^{\prime}
.
The
outer edges
of
\mathrm{k}
are those incident to the outer face in the canonical embedding.
Note that each
3
-connected (unlabeled) rooted map with
n
vertices has
n!/2
preimages under this correspondence.
.

2.2
Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology

{sec:defGHP}

We now define the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov distance. For details and proofs, we refer to
[
9
]
and
[
27
, Section 6]
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathrm{d})
be a metric space. For every
K\subset\mathcal{X}
and every
\varepsilon>0
let
K^{\varepsilon}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\mathrm{d}(x,K)<\varepsilon\}
. The Hausdorff distance between two compact sets
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
of
\mathcal{X}
is

\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{H}}(K_{1},K_{2})\overset{\hbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\inf\{\varepsilon>0\ :\ K_{1}\subset K_{2}^{\varepsilon}\text{ and }K_{2}\subset K_{1}^{\varepsilon}\}.

The Gromov–Hausdorff distance allows us to compare two compact metric spaces by isometrically embedding them in a common, larger metric space, and comparing the Hausdorff distance of their embedding. More formally for every pair of compact metric spaces
(\mathcal{X},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{X}})
and
(\mathcal{Y},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{Y}})
, we define their Gromov--Hausdorff distance as:

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}((\mathcal{X},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{X}}),(\mathcal{Y},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{Y}}))\overset{\hbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\inf\{\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{H}}(\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{X}),\Phi^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{Y}))\},

where the infimum is over every metric space
\mathcal{Z}
and isometries
\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
and
\Phi^{\mathcal{Y}}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}
. Since isometric spaces are at Gromov–Hausdorff “distance” zero,
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}
only defines a pseudo-distance on compact metric spaces, but it becomes a true distance when considered on
\mathbb{M}
the set of compact metric spaces seen up to isometry. Furthermore,
(\mathbb{M},\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}})
is a Polish space.

If
(\mathcal{Z},\mathrm{d})
is a metric space with Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z})
, we define the Lévy–Prokhorov distance between two Borel probability measures
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{Z}
as follows:

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\overset{\hbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\inf\{\varepsilon>0\ :\ \forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z}),\mu(A)\leqslant\nu(A^{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\text{ and }\nu(A)\leqslant\mu(A^{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\}.

If
\mathcal{Z}
is a Polish space then the Lévy–Prokhorov distance makes the set of probability measures on
\mathcal{Z}
a Polish space, and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{LP}}
metrizes the weak convergence of measures. There is also a characterization of
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
in terms of coupling, see for instance
[
20
]
. A
coupling
between
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
is a probability measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}
such that
\pi^{1}_{*}\nu=\mu
and
\pi^{2}_{*}\nu=\mu^{\prime}
(where
\pi^{1}
and
\pi^{2}
denote the canonical projections with respect to the first and second coordinate respectively). We denote by
\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
the set of couplings between
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
.
Then, if
(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z})_{\epsilon}
denotes the set
\{(z,z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\,,\,\mathrm{d}(z,z^{\prime})\leqslant\epsilon\}
, we have
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leqslant\epsilon
if and only if there exists a coupling
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
such that
\nu((\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z})_{\epsilon})\geqslant 1-\epsilon
.

A weighted metric space is a metric space equipped with a probability Borel measure.
The Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov distance
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GHP}}
is a metric on isometry classes of compact weighted metric spaces, defined as follows.
For any measured spaces
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z}
, measurable function
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
and measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
, write
\Phi_{*}\mu=\mu(\Phi^{-1}(\cdot))
for the pushforward of
\mu
under
\Phi
.
Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{X}})
and
(\mathcal{Y},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{Y}})
be two compact metric spaces equipped respectively with the probability measures
\mu^{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mu^{\mathcal{Y}}
; define

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GHP}}((\mathcal{X},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{X}},\mu^{\mathcal{X}})\,,\,(\mathcal{Y},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mu^{\mathcal{Y}}))\overset{\hbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\inf\left\{\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{H}}(\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{X}),\Phi^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{Y}))\ +\ \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{\mathcal{Z}}(\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}_{*}\mu^{\mathcal{X}},\Phi^{\mathcal{Y}}_{*}\mu^{\mathcal{Y}})\right\},

where (as in the definition of
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}
) the infimum is over every metric space
(\mathcal{Z},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{Z}})
and isometries
\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
and
\Phi^{\mathcal{Y}}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}
. Because isometric weighted metric spaces are at Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov “distance” zero, the function
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GHP}}
only defines a pseudo-metric; to make it a true metric, we consider
\mathbb{K}
the set of isometry classes of compact weighted metric spaces. Then
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GHP}}
defines a true metric on
\mathbb{K}
, and
(\mathbb{K},\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GHP}})
is a Polish space.

There exists also an equivalent description of
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GHP}}
based on couplings which is better suited for our purposes. Let us introduce the concept of correspondence. A
correspondence
between two sets
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is a subset
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
such that
\pi^{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{R})=\mathcal{X}
and
\pi^{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{R})=\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, where
\pi^{\mathcal{X}}
, resp.
\pi^{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}
is the canonical projection on
\mathcal{X}
, resp.
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. We write
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime})
for the set of correspondences between
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. Given a correspondence, define its
distortion

\dis(\mathcal{R})\overset{\hbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\sup\left\{\left|\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{X}}(x,y)-\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right|\ :\ (x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}\right\}.

The Gromov–Hausdorff distance can be expressed in terms of distortion:

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}((\mathcal{X},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{X}}),(\mathcal{Y},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{Y}}))=\frac{1}{2}\inf\{\dis(\mathcal{R}):\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\},

and this infimum is attained.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(\mathcal{X},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{X}},\mu^{\mathcal{X}})
and
\mathbf{Y}=(\mathcal{Y},\mathrm{d}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mu^{\mathcal{Y}})
be two compact weighted metric spaces. Recall the notation
\mathcal{M}(\mu^{\mathcal{X}},\mu^{\mathcal{Y}})
for the set of couplings between
\mu^{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mu^{\mathcal{Y}}
, then we have the following alternate characterization of the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov distance:

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GHP}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\inf\{\varepsilon>0\ :\ \exists\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}),\exists\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mu^{\mathcal{X}},\mu^{\mathcal{Y}}),\dis(\mathcal{R})\leqslant 2\varepsilon,\nu(\mathcal{R})\geqslant 1-\varepsilon\}.

3
The 3-connected core of cubic planar graphs

{sec:decompo}

3.1
Decomposition into 3-connected components

We recall here a decomposition
[
7
,
22
,
33
]
of so-called cubic networks (closely related to rooted cubic planar graphs) and of cubic planar (multi-)graphs
into 3-connected components.

A
cubic network
is a connected planar multigraph
\mathrm{N}
with two marked vertices
v_{-},v_{+}
called the
poles
, such that the poles have degree
1
and the other vertices have degree
3
. Note that the number of non-pole vertices has to be a positive even number
2n
, then
n\geqslant 0
is called the
size
of
\mathrm{N}
. The two edges adjacent to the poles are called the
legs
of
\mathrm{N}
, and the other edges are called
plain
. The
trivial network
is the one of size
0
, with a single edge connecting the two poles.
Note that a cubic network
\mathrm{N}
of positive size identifies to a rooted cubic planar multigraph
\overline{\mathrm{N}}
, which is obtained by merging the two poles, smoothing out the merged vertex, and orienting the resulting edge from the neighbor of
v_{-}
to the neighbor of
v_{+}
. Then
\mathrm{N}
is called
polyhedral
if
\overline{\mathrm{N}}
is 3-connected, in which
case the
outer edges
of
\mathrm{N}
are the (plain) edges of
\mathrm{N}
corresponding to the outer edges (without including the root edge) of
\overline{\mathrm{N}}
.
We also define the
double-edge network
as the cubic network of size
1
where the neighbors of the poles are connected by a double edge.

Given two non-trivial cubic networks
\mathrm{N}_{1},\mathrm{N}_{2}
, we let
\mathrm{N}:=\mathrm{N}_{1}+\mathrm{N}_{2}
be the cubic network obtained by merging the plus-pole of
\mathrm{N}_{1}
with the minus-pole of
\mathrm{N}_{2}
and smoothing out the merged vertex, so that the plus-pole of
\mathrm{N}
is the plus-pole of
\mathrm{N}_{2}
, and the minus-pole of
\mathrm{N}
is the minus-pole of
\mathrm{N}_{1}
. A cubic network
\mathrm{N}
that can be obtained as
\mathrm{N}=\mathrm{N}_{1}+\mathrm{N}_{2}
is called an
S
-network
, in which case
\mathrm{N}
has a unique decomposition as
\mathrm{N}=\mathrm{N}_{1}+\mathrm{N}_{2}
where
\mathrm{N}_{1}
is a (non-trivial) non-
S
-network, and by unfolding it also has a unique decomposition as
\mathrm{N}=\mathrm{N}_{1}+\cdots+\mathrm{N}_{k}
where each component
\mathrm{N}_{i}
is a non-trivial non-
S
-network.

In this article,
edge-substitution
in a graph
G
is the operation consisting, for each edge
e=(u_{-},u_{+})
of
G
(canonically endowed with a minus-extremity and a plus-extremity), of substituting it by a cubic network
\mathrm{N}_{e}
, so that the plus-pole and minus-pole of
\mathrm{N}_{e}
are identified respectively with
u_{-}
and
u_{+}
(note that substitution by the trivial network
leaves the edge unchanged). If
G
is itself a cubic network, only the plain edges are substituted.
A cubic network obtained by edge-substitution from the double-edge network (resp. from a polyhedral network) is called an
P
-network
(resp. a
H
-network
).

Note that
S
-networks,
P
-networks and
H
-networks are such that the two poles are at distance greater than
2
.
An
L
-network
is a cubic network
\mathrm{N}
whose poles are at distance
2
. In an
L
-network, let
u
be the common neighbor of the poles, and let
v
be its other neighbor (note that the edge
\{u,v\}
is an isthmus).
Let
\mathrm{l}_{1}
be the
L
-network of size
1
where
v
carries a loop. And let
\mathrm{l}_{2}
be the
L
-network of size
2
where
v
has two neighbors (apart from
u
) each carrying a loop.
As shown in
[
7
,
22
]
, an
L
-network
\mathrm{N}
is either obtained from
\mathrm{l}_{1}
where only the loop-edge is allowed to be substituted, or is obtained from
\mathrm{l}_{2}
where only the two
loop-edges are allowed to be substituted (the first and second case correspond respectively to
N\backslash v
having two or three connected components). The two cases are called
type 1 and type 2, respectively.

We have the following decomposition result for cubic networks, shown in
[
22
, Lem.1]
(the version for graphs appeared previously in
[
7
, Lem.1]
):

Lemma 5
.

{lem:dec_network}

Any non-trivial cubic network is in exactly one of the following classes:
\{
L
-network,
S
-network,
P
-network,
H
-network
\}
.

Figure 2:
Left: a connected (simple) cubic graph
\mathrm{g}
, realized as a cubic network upon rooting at a (non-isthmus) edge.
Right: the associated decomposition-tree.

The tree is considered as unrooted: it is intrinsic to
\mathrm{g}
(i.e., it does not depend on the root-choice), which is also
reflected by the difference in the choice of letters (a series-decomposition in the rooted setting corresponds to a “ring” of components in the unrooted setting, giving a node of type
R
).
The left part also shows the partition of the vertices of
\mathrm{g}
by components (tree-nodes) of type in
\{L,M,T\}
.

As detailed in
[
33
]
and illustrated in Figure
2
,
Lemma
5
yields a
tree-decomposition
of
cubic networks, i.e., a rooted (unembedded) tree
\tau(\mathrm{N})
can be associated to a non-trivial cubic network
\mathrm{N}
, whose inner nodes have labels in
\{L,R,M,T\}
. Precisely, if
\mathrm{N}
is an
S
-network, of the form
\mathrm{N}_{1}+\cdots+\mathrm{N}_{k}
, then the root node of
\tau(\mathrm{N})
is labeled
R
and the hanging subtrees are
\tau(\mathrm{N}_{1}),\ldots,\tau(\mathrm{N}_{k})
(these are not
S
-network hence do not have root label
R
).
If
\mathrm{N}
is an
P
-network or
H
-network,
then the root node is labeled
M
(resp.
T
) and the hanging subtrees are the trees associated to the substituted networks.
Finally, for
L
-networks, the root node has label
L
. In type 1, it has one child also labeled
L
and of arity in
\{0,1\}
, depending on whether the loop-edge of
\mathrm{l}_{1}
is unchanged or substituted by a non-trivial network
\mathrm{N}^{\prime}
(in which case the hanging subtree at the child corresponds to
\tau(\mathrm{N}^{\prime})
). In type
2
, it has two children labeled
L
, each corresponding to one of the two loops of
\mathrm{l}_{2}
. Each child again has arity in
\{0,1\}
, depending on whether the corresponding loop is left unchanged or substituted by a non-trivial network.

Each
T
-node of the tree
\tau(\mathrm{N})
corresponds to a certain rooted 3-connected planar graph
\mathrm{k}
whose vertex-set is a subset of the vertex-set of
\mathrm{N}
. Then
\mathrm{k}
is called
a
3-connected component
of
\mathrm{N}
. The hanging subtrees of the node correspond to the (non-trivial) cubic networks substituted at its edges.
As detailed in
[
33
]
, the tree-decomposition and 3-connected components are intrinsic to the underlying unrooted cubic planar graph (essentially it means that
rerooting the cubic multigraph at another non-isthmus edge amounts to rerooting the same underlying unrooted tree, a fact established by Tutte
[
42
]
for the decomposition of graphs
into 3-connected components, which can then be specialized to the cubic case). Accordingly, to any connected cubic planar graph
\mathrm{g}
, one can associate an unrooted decomposition tree, denoted
\tau(\mathrm{g})
.
Let us mention the following subtlety: when building the tree in the rooted case, every
R
-node has arity at least
2
, hence degree at least
3
if not at the root and degree at least
2
if at the root. In case the root node is of type
R
of arity
2
, then when unrooting the tree the corresponding node of degree
2
is smoothed out, so that all
R
-nodes in the unrooted tree are of degree at least
3
.
The (unrooted) decomposition-tree satisfies the following properties, which can be visualized in Figure
2
:

Claim 6
.

{claim:decomp_tree}

For
\mathrm{g}
a connected cubic planar multigraph and
\tau(\mathrm{g})
the associated decomposition-tree, the vertices of
\mathrm{g}
are partitioned among the
L
-components, the
M
-components, and the
T
-components (3-connected components) of
\tau(\mathrm{g})
(the
R
-components have no contribution).

The
L
-components are in one-to-one correspondence with the separating vertices of
\mathrm{g}
, the
M
-components are in one-to-one correspondence with vertex-pairs
\{u,v\}

such that
\mathrm{g}
is obtained by edge-substitution in the triple-bond connecting
u
and
v
.

The other vertices belong to the 3-connected components.
Precisely, for
V^{\prime}
a vertex-subset of
\mathrm{g}
with
|V^{\prime}|\geqslant 4
, and
\mathrm{k}
a 3-connected planar graph (unrooted) with vertex-set
V^{\prime}
,
\mathrm{k}
is a 3-connected component of
\mathrm{g}
if and only if
\mathrm{g}
is
\mathrm{k}
with networks substituted at the edges.

We focus our study first on
simple
cubic planar graphs; accordingly the cubic networks are also simple (extension of the arguments to multigraphs will be given
in Section
3.4
).
In the network-decomposition, this yields the constraint
that loops (when dealing with an
L
-network at the current node) are to be substituted by non-trivial non-
L
-networks, whereas when substituting in the double-edge network (when dealing
with a
P
-network at the current node), at least one of the two edges is to be substituted by a non-trivial network. On the decomposition-tree for unrooted graphs, being simple is equivalent to the fact that every leaf has label
T
, every
L
-node of degree
2
has exactly one neighbor of type
L
, and every
M
-node has degree in
\{2,3\}
; for networks the same constraints hold at non-root nodes, while the root node is allowed to be a leaf (i.e., to have arity
1
) of type
L
or type
M
.

We define families
\mathcal{F}=\cup_{n}\mathcal{F}_{n}
of cubic simple networks (with
n
the size-parameter), where
\mathcal{F}
is replaced by
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{H}
according to the type; we also define
\mathcal{D}
as the family of all simple cubic networks and
\mathcal{K}
as the family of polyhedral networks.
Each of these families is vertex-labeled, in the sense that the
2n
non-pole vertices have distinct labels in
[1..2n]
(on the other hand the two poles are unlabeled). We denote by
F(z)=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{(2n)!}|\mathcal{F}_{n}|z^{n}
the associated generating function, replacing
F
by the corresponding letter for each family.

We can express
K
in the following way.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be the set of rooted simple planar triangulations with
n+2
(unlabeled) vertices (and
2n
faces), and
T(x)=\sum_{n\geqslant 2}|\mathcal{T}_{n}|x^{n}
the corresponding ordinary generating function, which is known
[
41
]
to have the following exact expression:

T(x)=\sum_{n\geqslant 2}\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\binom{4n-3}{n-2}x^{n}=\xi^{2}(1-3\xi+\xi^{2}),

(7)

where
\xi
is the unique formal power series in
x
with constant term equal to 0 and defined by
x=\xi(1-\xi)^{3}
.

By duality,
simple
planar triangulations correspond to
3-connected
cubic planar maps. Thus, applying Whitney’s theorem and taking vertex-labeling into account, we have
(2n)!|\mathcal{T}_{n}|=2|\mathcal{K}_{n}|
, hence
K(x)=T(x)/2
.

Then a decomposition at the root of the decomposition tree of cubic networks yields the following system (from which the coefficients can be extracted):

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}D(z)&=&1+L(z)+S(z)+P(z)+H(z),\\[2.84544pt]
L(z)&=&\frac{1}{2}L(z)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}z(D(z)-1-L(z)),\\[2.84544pt]
S(z)&=&(D(z)-1-S(z))(D(z)-1),\\[2.84544pt]
P(z)&=&\frac{1}{2}z(D(z)^{2}-1),\\[2.84544pt]
H(z)&=&\frac{1}{D(z)}K\big(zD(z)^{3}\big)=\frac{1}{2D(z)}T\big(zD(z)^{3}\big).\end{array}\right.

(8)

In the second line the two terms correspond respectively to the root
L
-node having arity
2
or
1
. The last line (with
T(x)
given in (
7
)) relies on the fact that a cubic network of size
n
has
3n-1
plain edges.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\cup_{n}\mathcal{C}_{n}
be the family of vertex-labeled connected cubic planar graphs of size
n
, i.e., the
2n
vertices carry
distinct labels in
[1..2n]
. Let
C(z)=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{(2n)!}|\mathcal{C}_{n}|z^{n}
be the associated exponential generating function.
Using Claim
6
, one obtains the following expression for the exponential generating function
C^{\bullet}(z)=2zC^{\prime}(z)
of pointed (i.e., with a marked vertex) cubic planar graphs
[
33
]
:

C^{\bullet}(z)=\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big(D(z)-1-L(z)\big)L(z)+\frac{1}{6z}L(z)^{3}\Big)+\frac{z}{6}(D(z)^{3}-3D(z)+2)+\frac{1}{3}G(z),

(9)

where we define
G(z):=K(zD(z)^{3})
.
The three groups correspond to the marked vertex being involved in an
L
-component (with the two terms corresponding to the marked vertex
yielding two or three connected components upon its removal), an
M
-component, and a
T
-component respectively (the factor
1/3
in front of
G(z)
is due to having a marked vertex instead of a marked directed edge).

3.2
Largest 3-connected component

{sec:largest_3comp}

We now use singularity analysis of the involved generating functions, in order to show (in this subsection and the next one) that a random cubic connected planar graph almost surely has a unique giant 3-connected

3.3
Cubic planar graphs with a marked 3-connected component

{sec:marked_3c}

For two integers

, let

be the set of connected cubic planar graphs of size
n
having a marked 3-connected component of size
q
, and
denote by
\mathfrak{C}_{n}^{(q)}
a uniformly random element in
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{(q)}
.
It will also prove convenient for us to see
\mathfrak{C}_{n}^{(q)}
as closely related to a model with more independence. The
critical Boltzmann distribution
on
\mathcal{D}
is the probability distribution

\mu^{(\mathcal{D})}
defined as

\mu^{(\mathcal{D})}(\mathrm{d})=\frac{\rho^{|\mathrm{d}|}}{(2|\mathrm{d}|)!D(\rho)}\ \ \ \forall\ \mathrm{d}\in\mathcal{D},

(14)

where we recall that
\rho
is the radius of convergence of

.

Let
\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}
be a random connected cubic planar graph with a marked 3-connected component of size
q
constructed in the following way. Letting
\mathrm{K}
be a uniformly random 3-connected graph of size
q
, we replace (independently) each edge of
\mathrm{K}
by a cubic network sampled from
\mu^{(\mathcal{D})}
.
The resulting graph is
\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}
. Note that, for any
n,q\geqslant 1
,
\mathfrak{C}_{n}^{(q)}
is distributed as
\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}
conditioned to have size
n
.

As stated next, the conditioning probability is polynomial when
q=\alpha n+O(n^{2/3})
:

Lemma 9
.

{lem:size_core}

For every
C>0
, we can find
0<c_{-}<c_{+}<\infty
such that for every
q
with
|q-\alpha n|\leqslant Cn^{2/3}
, we have
n^{2/3}\mathbb{P}(|\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}|=n)\in(c_{-},c_{+})
.

Informally, this local limit theorem-type result ensures that when
q,n
are related by
q=\alpha n+O(n^{2/3})
, then
\mathbb{P}(|\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}|=n)=\Theta(n^{-2/3})
. We will
often use such an informal notation thereafter.

Proof.

Let
n^{(q)}
be the size of
\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}
. With
E(z)=zD(z)^{3}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(n^{(q)}=n)=[u^{n}]p(u)^{q},\ \ \mathrm{where}\ p(u):=\frac{E(\rho u)}{E(\rho)}.

With the notation (
10
), we have

p(u)=1+\frac{1}{\alpha}(u-1)+\frac{E_{3}}{E_{0}}(1-u)^{3/2}+O((u-1)^{2}).

A local limit theorem (for a stable law of parameter
3/2
) holds
[
5
, Theo.11(ii)]
: for
n=\frac{1}{\alpha}q-xq^{2/3}
, uniformly in
x
on any compact interval, we have

\mathbb{P}(n^{(q)}=n)\sim\frac{\tilde{c}A(\tilde{c}x)}{q^{2/3}},

with
\tilde{c}=(E_{0}/E_{3})^{2/3}
, and
\tilde{c}A(\tilde{c}x)
the Airy-map distribution of parameter
\tilde{c}
.
∎

Let
\mathrm{C}
be a connected cubic planar graph with a marked 3-connected component
\mathrm{K}
. Note that
\mathrm{C}
is obtained from
\mathrm{K}
by substituting every edge of
\mathrm{K}
by a cubic network; such a network is called an
attached network of
\mathrm{C}
.

Lemma 10
.

{lem:cnq}

Let
\epsilon>0
.
When
q,n
are related by
q=\alpha n+O(n^{2/3})
, then a.a.s. all attached networks of
\mathfrak{C}_{n}^{(q)}
have size at most
n^{2/3+\epsilon}
.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon/2
.
We argue via
\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}
and conditioning on the size.
The
3q
edges of the marked 3-connected component can be canonically ordered (e.g. lexicographic ordering on the pair of labels of the incident vertices), which yields a canonical ordering
M_{1},\ldots,M_{3q}
of the
3q
attached networks. In
\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}
these are independently drawn under
\mu^{(\mathcal{D})}
, and the size of
\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}
is
|\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}|=|M_{1}|+\cdots+|M_{3q}|
.
Using
[
17
, Theo.1(iii)-(b)]
, it follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}|<\frac{1}{\alpha}q-q^{2/3+\epsilon^{\prime}}\right)=O(\exp(-q^{\epsilon^{\prime}})).

(a large deviation result, obtained by a saddle-point estimate).

Similarly, for any
i\in[1..3q]
, we have

\mathbb{P}(|\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}|-|M_{i}|<\frac{1}{\alpha}q-q^{2/3+\epsilon^{\prime}})=O(\exp(-q^{\epsilon^{\prime}}))
(we apply the same argument to
(3q-1)
attached networks instead of
3q
). By the union bound, the probability that
|\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}|-|M_{i}|<\frac{1}{\alpha}q-q^{2/3+\epsilon^{\prime}}
for some
i\in[1..3q]
is
O(q\exp(-q^{\epsilon^{\prime}}))
.
When conditioning on
\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}
to have size
n
, the event that
|\mathfrak{C}^{(q)}|-|M_{i}|<\frac{1}{\alpha}q-q^{2/3+\epsilon^{\prime}}
for some
i\in[1..3q]
is the same as
|M_{i}|>n-\frac{1}{\alpha}q+q^{2/3+\epsilon^{\prime}}
for some
i\in[1..3q]
. Hence, when
q,n
are related by
q=\alpha n+O(n^{2/3})
, Lemma
9
ensures that the probability that
|M_{i}|>n-\frac{1}{\alpha}q+q^{2/3+\epsilon^{\prime}}
for some
i\in[1..3q]
is
O(n^{2/3}q\exp(-q^{\epsilon^{\prime}}))=o(1)
. Moreover, for
n
large enough, this event is implied by the event that
|M_{i}|>n^{2/3+\epsilon}
for some
i\in[1..3q]
.
∎

Remark 3
.

For
q=\alpha n+O(n^{2/3})
, as already observed in
[
40
]
the fact that
\alpha>1/2
ensures that for
n
large enough

coincides with the random connected cubic planar graph
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
conditioned to have its
3
-connected core of size
q
.
.

4.1
Pole-distance in cubic networks under the critical Boltzmann distribution

{sec:pole_dista}

For
\mathrm{N}
a cubic network, the
pole-distance
of
\mathrm{N}
is the distance between its two poles, it is denoted
\delta(N)
. We use the notation
\mathcal{D}

for the class of networks in both contexts (simple cubic networks, labeled at non-pole vertices, or cubic networks allowing for loops and multiedges, labeled at half-edges on plain edges).
Let
\nu_{\star}
be the distribution of
\delta(N)
, where
\mathrm{N}
is drawn under
\mu^{(\mathcal{D})}
(critical Boltzmann distribution on
\mathcal{D}
).
Our aim here is to show that
\nu_{\star}
has an exponential tail,
both in the case of graphs and multigraphs (the reason to show this is that
\nu_{\star}
will be the law of edge-lengths to be considered on the
3
-connected core, and
our subsequent analysis of modified distances in
3
-connected cores assumes the law of edge-lengths to have exponential tails).

Lemma 12
.

{lem:nustar}

There exist constants
a_{\star},b_{\star}>0
such that, for
X
drawn under
\nu_{\star}
,

\mathbb{P}(X>k)\leqslant a_{\star}\exp(-b_{\star}\,k)\ \ \mathrm{for\ all\ }k\geqslant 0.

Proof.

Similarly as in
[
11
, Lem.5.6]
, the approach is to define another parameter
\chi
on cubic networks that dominates
\delta
and is easier to track along the network decomposition, and to show via generating functions that
\chi
has an exponential tail.
For
N\in\mathcal{D}
,
\chi(N)
is defined as follows:

•

if
\mathrm{N}
is the trivial network then
\chi(N)=1
,

•

if
\mathrm{N}
is of type
L
then
\chi(N)=2
,

•

if
\mathrm{N}
is of type
R
, of the form
\mathrm{N}_{1}+\mathrm{N}_{2}
(with
\mathrm{N}_{1}
a not-
R
network), then

\chi(N)=\chi(\mathrm{N}_{1})+\chi(\mathrm{N}_{2})
,

•

if
\mathrm{N}
is of type
M
, with
\mathrm{N}_{1}
and
\mathrm{N}_{2}
the two networks substituted at each edge, then
\chi(N)=\chi(\mathrm{N}_{1})+\chi(\mathrm{N}_{2})+2
,

•

if
\mathrm{N}
has a 3-connected core
K
, and if
\mathrm{N}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{N}_{k}
are the cubic networks substituted at the outer edges of
K
, then
\chi(N)=2+\chi(\mathrm{N}_{1})+\cdots+\chi(\mathrm{N}_{k})
.

It is easy to check recursively along the decomposition that
\chi(N)\geqslant\delta(N)
for any
N\in\mathcal{D}
.

For each family
\mathcal{F}
of networks, we let
F(z,u)
be the bivariate generating function (with
z
for the size and
u
for the parameter
\chi
).
We also write
T(z,v)
for the bivariate generating function of rooted simple triangulations, with
v
for the root degree minus
1
. By Remark
1
,
T(z,v)/2
is the bivariate (exponential) generating function of 3-connected edge-rooted cubic planar graphs, with
v
conjugate to the number of outer edges minus
1
.
For the case of graphs, the network decomposition gives

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}D(z,u)&=&u+L(z,u)+S(z,u)+P(z,u)+H(z,u),\\
L(z,u)&=&u^{2}L(z),\\
S(z,u)&=&(D(z,u)-u-S(z,u))(D(z,u)-u),\\
P(z,u)&=&\frac{1}{2}u^{2}(D(z,u)^{2}-1),\\
H(z,u)&=&u^{2}\frac{1}{2D(z)}T(zD(z)^{3},D(z,u)/D(z)),\end{array}\right.

(21)

Hence, noting that
S(z,u)=\frac{(D(z,u)-u)^{2}}{D(z,u)-u+1}
, the series
D(z,u)
is solution of the equation in
y

A(z,u,y)=0,

(22)

where

A(z,u,y)=-y+u+u^{2}L(z)+\frac{(y-u)^{2}}{y-u+1}+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}(y^{2}-1)+\frac{u^{2}}{2D(z)}T(zD(z)^{3},\frac{y}{D(z)}).

We are now going to prove the property that there exists
u_{0}>1
such that
D(\rho,u_{0})
converges; this will prove the lemma, since for
\mathrm{N}
drawn under
\mu^{(\mathcal{D})}
one has

\mathbb{P}(\delta(N)\geqslant k)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\chi(N)\geqslant k)=\sum_{r\geqslant k}\frac{[u^{r}]D(\rho,u)}{D(\rho)}\leqslant\frac{D(\rho,u_{0})}{D(\rho)}u_{0}^{-k}.

What we will obtain more precisely is that, in a complex neighborhood of
(\rho,1)
(with
\rho
the radius of convergence of
D(z)
), we have a singular expansion for
D(z,u)
of the form

D(z,u)=\widetilde{D}(Z,U),\ \ \mathrm{with}\ Z=\sqrt{1-z/\rho}\ \mathrm{and}\ U=u-1,

where
\widetilde{D}(Z,U)
is analytic around
(0,0)
and satisfies
[Z^{1}]\widetilde{D}(Z,U)=0
, and
[Z^{2}]\widetilde{D}(Z,0)\neq 0
; and moreover we will check that
\rho
is the radius of convergence of
z\to D(z,u)
for
u\in\mathbb{R}
around
1
.

To prove that, we first obtain a similar singular expansion for
T(x,v)
. An explicit expression is given in
[
8
]
:

T(x,v)=\frac{1}{2vx}\Big(-2v^{2}x^{2}+vx\eta(1+2(1-\eta))-(1-\eta)\eta^{3}-(\eta vx-(1-\eta)\eta^{3})\sqrt{1-4vx/\eta^{2}}\Big),

where
\eta\equiv\eta(x)
is the algebraic series given by
x=\eta^{3}(1-\eta)
, which has an expansion of the form
\eta(x)=\widetilde{a}(x)+\widetilde{b}(x)\cdot(1-x/x_{\mathrm{c}})^{3/2}
, where
x_{\mathrm{c}}=27/256
, and
\widetilde{a}(x)
and
\widetilde{b}(x)
are analytic around
x_{\mathrm{c}}
, and
\eta(x_{\mathrm{c}})=\widetilde{A}(x_{\mathrm{c}})=3/4
. Plugging this expansion into the expression of

, one obtains an expansion of

around
(x_{\mathrm{c}},1)
of the form

T(x,v)=\widetilde{T}(X,V),\ \ \mathrm{with}\ X=\sqrt{1-x/x_{\mathrm{c}}}\ \mathrm{and}\ V=v-1,

where
\widetilde{T}(X,V)
is analytic around
(0,0)
and satisfies
[X^{1}]\widetilde{T}(X,V)=0
, and
[X^{2}]\widetilde{T}(X,0)\neq 0
.
Next, we have (from
[
7
,
33
]
) that
L(z)
and
D(z)
are of the form

f(z)=\widetilde{f}(Z)
with
\widetilde{f}(Z)
analytic around
0
and satisfying
\widetilde{f}^{\prime}(0)=0

and
\widetilde{f}^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0
; and moreover
\rho D(\rho)^{3}=x_{\mathrm{c}}
. Plugging these expansions into (
22
) (see
[
14
, Sec.2.6]
for a similar extraction) we can extract an expansion for

of the form
D(z,u)=\widetilde{D}(Z,U)
, where
\widetilde{D}(Z,U)
is analytic around
(0,0)
and satisfies
[Z^{1}]\widetilde{D}(Z,U)=0
, and
[Z^{2}]\widetilde{D}(Z,0)\neq 0

Taking

is analytic at

for

(because

). The analyticity of

The proof for the case of multigraphs is completely similar (the equation-system is the same, except for

∎

4.2
A first bound on the diameter

For later purpose, we need to establish a first bound on the diameter of random connected cubic planar graphs (resp. multigraphs),
which is the analogue of the one for random planar graphs obtained
in
[
11
]
. We say that a property
A_{n,\epsilon}
related to a random object of size
n
, and also formulated in terms of a real parameter
\epsilon>0
, holds
a.a.s. with exponential rate

if there are positive constants
a,b,c
such that
P(\mathrm{not}\ A_{n,\epsilon})\leqslant a\,\exp(-b\,n^{c\epsilon})
for all
n\geqslant 0
and
\epsilon>0
small enough. The
diameter
of a graph
\mathrm{g}
, denoted
\mathrm{Diam}(\mathrm{g})
, is the maximum of the pairwise distances between vertices in
\mathrm{g}
.

The purpose of this section is to prove the following result:

Proposition 13
.

{prop:diam}

Let
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
(resp.
\mathfrak{M}_{n}
) be the random connected cubic planar graph (resp. multigraph) on
2n
vertices. Then
\mathrm{Diam}(\mathfrak{C}_{n})\leqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}
(resp.
\mathrm{Diam}(\mathfrak{M}_{n})\leqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}
) a.a.s. with exponential rate.

As a first step, we establish the bound in the 3-connected case (starting in the dual setting of random simple triangulations).

Lemma 14
.

{lem:diam_Tn}

Let
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
be the random rooted simple triangulation with
n+2
vertices. Then
\mathrm{Diam}(\mathfrak{t}_{n})\leqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}
a.a.s. with exponential rate.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\prime}
be the set of rooted triangulations with a marked vertex (distinct from the root vertex), having
n+2
vertices.
We use the fact (to be detailed in Section
5.2
, and illustrated in Figure
4
) that

\mathcal{T}_{n-1}^{\prime}
injects into the set
\mathcal{Q}_{n,1}
of so-called quasi-simple triangulations of the 1-gon having
n
inner vertices, in such a way that the
underlying metric space is preserved; and that both
|\mathcal{Q}_{n,1}|
and
|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\prime}|
are

\Theta((256/27)^{n}n^{-3/2})
.
Let
G(x)
be the counting series of quasi-simple triangulations of the 1-gon with respect to the number of vertices minus
2
. For
i\geqslant 1
, let
G^{(i)}(x)
be the counting series of quasi-simple triangulations of the 1-gon where the root vertex and the marked vertex are at distance
i
, counted with respect to the number of vertices minus
2
. Let
\mathfrak{q}_{n}^{(1)}
be a uniformly random map in
\mathcal{Q}_{n,1}
.
Denoting by
L_{n}
the distance between the root vertex and the marked vertex in
\mathfrak{q}^{(1)}_{n-1}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(L_{n}=i)=\frac{[x^{n}]G^{(i)}(x)}{[x^{n}]G(x)}.

An explicit expression
4
4

4

The expression is given for symmetric simple triangular
k
-dissections of order
k\geqslant 3
such that the central vertex is at distance
i
from the outer boundary; the quotient of these dissections by the rotation of order
k
is precisely a quasi-simple triangulation whose root-vertex
is at distance
i
from the marked vertex.
for
G^{(i)}(x)
is given in
[
3
, Prop.5.3]
.
Precisely, there exists an (algebraic) counting series
Y(x)
, with a quartic dominant singularity at
27/256
,

Y(x)\sim 1-24^{1/4}(1-256\,x/27)^{1/4},

such that
G^{(i)}(x)
is expressed as

G^{(i)}(x)=Y^{i}(x)\cdot R(Y^{i}(x),Y(x),S(x)),

where
S(x)=\sqrt{Y(x)^{2}+10Y(x)+1}
, and where
R(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})
is an explicit rational expression that is continuous and positive at
(z_{1}=0,z_{2}=1,z_{3}=\sqrt{12})
. Let
x_{n}=27/256\cdot(1-1/n)
. Then
Y(x_{n})=1-(24/n)^{1/4}+o(n^{-1/4})
. Hence, for
\epsilon>0
and
i\geqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}
, we
have
Y^{i}(x_{n})=O(\exp(-b\,n^{\epsilon}))
, where
b
is any positive constant smaller than
24^{1/4}
(e.g.
b=24^{1/4}/2
). Given the expression of
G^{(i)}(x)
, we also have

G^{(i)}(x_{n})=O(\exp(-b\,n^{\epsilon}))
. Hence, for
i\geqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}
, we have

[x^{n}]G^{(i)}(x)\leqslant G^{(i)}(x_{n})x_{n}^{-n}=O\big(\exp(-b\,n^{\epsilon})(256/27)^{n}\big).

Since
[x^{n}]G(x)=|\mathcal{Q}_{n,1}|=\Theta((256/27)^{n}n^{-3/2})
, we conclude that
\mathbb{P}(L_{n}=i)=O(n^{3/2}\exp(-b\,n^{\epsilon}))

uniformly over
i\geqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}
, and thus

\mathbb{P}(L_{n}\geqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon})=O\big(n^{5/2}\exp(-b\,n^{\epsilon})\big)=O\big(\exp(-n^{\epsilon/2})\big),

so that
L_{n}\leqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}
a.a.s. with exponential rate.
Let
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
be the uniform random rooted simple triangulation with
n+2
vertices.
Let
\tilde{L}_{n}
be the distance between the root vertex and a random vertex (distinct from the root vertex) in
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
.
Note that
\tilde{L}_{n}
is distributed as
L_{n}
conditioned on the event that the triangulation associated with
\mathfrak{q}_{n-1}
is simple (via the
mapping described in Section
5.2
). Since that event has probability
\Theta(1)
,
we conclude that
\tilde{L}_{n}\leqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}
a.a.s. with exponential rate. Since the number of choices for a root and a marked vertex is
O(n^{2})
, the union-bound ensures that
\mathrm{Diam}(\mathfrak{t}_{n})\leqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}
a.a.s. with exponential rate.
∎

Lemma 15
.

{lem:diam_K}

Let
\mathfrak{K}_{n}
be the random 3-connected cubic planar graph on
2n
vertices. Then
\mathrm{Diam}(\mathfrak{K}_{n})\leqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}
a.a.s. with exponential rate.

Proof.

Up to choosing one of the two embeddings,
and choosing a root edge,
\mathrm{Diam}(\mathfrak{K}_{n})
is distributed as the diameter of the random rooted 3-connected cubic planar map with
2n
vertices.
Let
\mathrm{m}
be a planar map, and let
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
be its dual.
Let
v,v^{\prime}
be vertices in
\mathrm{m}
, let
f
(resp.
f^{\prime}
) be a face incident to
v
(resp. to
v^{\prime}
), and let
v_{f}
(resp.
v_{f^{\prime}}
) be the vertex of
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
corresponding
to
f
(resp. to
v^{\prime}
). From any path
\gamma=(v_{0}=v,\ldots,v_{r}=v^{\prime})
connecting

to
v^{\prime}
,
it is easy to derive a path
\gamma^{\dagger}
on
\mathrm{m}^{\dagger}
between

and

\mathrm{deg}(v_{0})+\cdots+\mathrm{deg}(v_{r})
.

Hence, if
\mathrm{maxDeg}(\mathrm{m})
denotes the maximal vertex-degree in
\mathrm{m}
, then we have

\mathrm{Diam}(\mathrm{m}^{\dagger})\leqslant(1+\mathrm{Diam}(\mathrm{m}))\cdot\mathrm{maxDeg}(\mathrm{m}).

Hence,
\mathrm{Diam}(\mathfrak{K}_{n})
is stochastically dominated by
(1+\mathrm{Diam}(\mathfrak{t}_{n}))\cdot\mathrm{maxDeg}(\mathfrak{t}_{n})
. Given Lemma
14
, it remains
to prove that

a.a.s. with exponential rate. Letting
\mathrm{rootDeg}(\mathfrak{t}_{n})

be the root degree in
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
, we have (with

defined in Section
4.1
)

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{rootDeg}(\mathfrak{t}_{n})=k)=\frac{[x^{n}v^{k}]T(x,v)}{[x^{n}]T(x,1)}.

The explicit expression
[
8
]
of

(recalled in Section
4.1
) guarantees, that there exists

such that

converges as a sum (any

fits). We thus have
[x^{n}v^{k}]T(x,v)\leqslant C\,(256/27)^{n}v_{0}^{-k}
, so that

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{rootDeg}(\mathfrak{t}_{n})=k)=O(n^{5/2}v_{0}^{-k}),

On the other hand, a standard re-rooting argument ensures that

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{maxDeg}(\mathfrak{t}_{n})=k)\leqslant 6n\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{rootDeg}(\mathfrak{t}_{n})=k)=O(n^{7/2}v_{0}^{-k}),

which is
O(v_{0}^{-n^{\epsilon}/2})
for
k\geqslant n^{\epsilon}
.
Hence,
\mathrm{maxDeg}(\mathfrak{t}_{n})\leqslant n^{\epsilon}
a.a.s. with exponential rate.
∎

Let
\mathrm{g}
be a (non-rooted) cubic connected planar multigraph, and let
\tau(\mathrm{g})
be the associated decomposition-tree. We call

-attached network within

one of the cubic networks assembled around an

-cycle of components (node of label

\tau(\mathrm{g})
, precisely it is the network associated to the subtree hanging from the non-

.
We let
\delta_{R}(\mathrm{g})
be the maximal value of
\delta(N)
over all
R
-attached networks within
\mathrm{g}
.
And we let
\xi_{R}(\mathrm{g})
be the maximal degree over all
R
-nodes of
\tau(\mathrm{g})
.
Similarly, we define a
T
-attached network
as a network substituted at one of the edges of a
T
-component of
\mathrm{g}
. It corresponds to an edge
e
with a
T
extremity in
\tau(\mathrm{g})
, precisely it is the network associated to the subtree hanging from the other side of

. We let
\delta_{T}(\mathrm{g})
be the maximal value of
\delta(N)
over all

\mathrm{g}

Similarly as in

Claim 16
.

{claim:bound_diam}

We have

\mathrm{Diam}(\mathrm{g})\leqslant(1+\mathrm{Diam}(\tau(\mathrm{g})))\cdot(2+\xi_{R}(\mathrm{g})\delta_{R}(\mathrm{g})+\xi_{T}(\mathrm{g})\delta_{T}(\mathrm{g})).

Proof.

A diametral path
\gamma
in
\mathrm{g}
induces a path
\gamma^{\prime}
in
\tau(\mathrm{g})
. Some edges on
\gamma
correspond to edges of
\gamma^{\prime}
(where
\gamma

leaves a component and enters another component). The other edges of
\gamma
are “consumed” within the nodes on
\gamma^{\prime}
.
For a

-node

visited by

, corresponding to a

-connected component

, the length of
\gamma
consumed within

is bounded by
\mathrm{Diam}(\mathrm{K})\cdot\mathrm{max}_{N\in\mathrm{att}_{w}}\delta(N)
, where
\mathrm{att}_{w}
is the set of

-attached networks at
\mathrm{K}
.
Similarly, for an
R
-node

, of degree

, visited by
\gamma
, the length of
\gamma

is bounded by

, where
\mathrm{att}_{w}
is the set of

-attached networks at

.
Finally, for an

(this happens if one of the three edges of the

).

In view of Claim

, in order to conclude the proof of Proposition
13
, it just remains to show the following.

Lemma 17
.

For
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
the random connected cubic planar graph with
2n
vertices, we have

\mathrm{Diam}(\tau(\mathfrak{C}_{n}))\leqslant n^{\epsilon},\ \ \delta_{R}(\mathfrak{C}_{n})\leqslant n^{\epsilon},\ \ \delta_{T}(\mathfrak{C}_{n})\leqslant n^{\epsilon},\ \ \xi_{R}(\mathfrak{C}_{n})\leqslant n^{\epsilon},\ \ \xi_{T}(\mathfrak{C}_{n})\leqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}

a.a.s. with exponential rate.

The same holds when replacing
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
by
\mathfrak{M}_{n}
.

Proof.

We give the proof details for graphs (the arguments for multigraphs are completely similar).
We start with
\xi_{T}(\mathfrak{C}_{n})
. Consider a 3-connected component
\mathrm{K}
in
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
, and let
n^{\prime}\leqslant n
be its size. Then
\mathrm{K}
is uniformly distributed
over cubic 3-connected planar graphs with
2n^{\prime}
vertices. If
n^{\prime}\leqslant n^{1/4}/2
, then obviously

\mathrm{Diam}(\mathrm{K})\leqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}
. If
n^{\prime}\in[n^{1/4}/2,n]
, then by Lemma
15

there are positive constants
a,b,c
such that, for

small enough, we have (where Lemma

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Diam}(\mathrm{K})\geqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon})\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Diam}(\mathrm{K})\geqslant n^{\prime}\,{}^{1/4+\epsilon})\leqslant a\,\exp\big(-b\,n^{\prime}\,{}^{c\epsilon})\leqslant a\,\exp\big(-b\,n^{c\epsilon/4}/2^{c\epsilon}\big).

Hence, uniformly over
n^{\prime}\in[0,n]
, the diameter of a random
3
-connected cubic planar graph of size

is smaller than
n^{1/4+\epsilon}
a.a.s. with exponential rate. Since the number of
3
-connected components in
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
is
O(n)
, by the union-bound we conclude that
\xi_{T}(\mathfrak{C}_{n})\leqslant n^{1/4+\epsilon}
a.a.s. with exponential rate.

We now deal with
\xi_{R}(\mathfrak{C}_{n})
. Let
\bar{S}(z):=L(z)+P(z)+H(z)
be the generating function for cubic networks that are eligible to be

-attached (i.e., non-trivial non-

cubic networks).
From the 1st and 3rd line in (
8
) (or directly, from the decomposition of

-networks), we get

.
Since
S(z)
converges at
\rho
, we must have
\bar{S}(\rho)\in(0,1)
. For
k\geqslant 3
, the generating function of connected cubic planar graphs with a marked

-component of degree

is equal to

. Hence, for
k\geqslant 3
,
the number of connected cubic planar graphs of size
n
having a marked

-component of degree

is bounded by
(2n)![z^{n}]\bar{S}(z)^{k}
, which itself is bounded
by
(2n)!\beta^{k}\rho^{-n}
, where

. The number of connected cubic planar graphs of size

having a marked

-component of degree larger than

is thus

. Since the number of

, the number of connected cubic planar graphs of size

-component of degree larger than

. Hence, the probability that

a.a.s. with exponential rate.

We now consider the parameter
\delta_{T}(\mathfrak{C}_{n})

-attached network of size

in

.

such that

Hence,
\delta(\mathrm{D}_{n^{\prime}})\leqslant n^{\epsilon}
a.a.s. with exponential rate, uniformly over

. Since the number of
T
-attached networks in
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
is

O(n)
, the union bound ensures that
\delta_{T}(\mathfrak{C}_{n})\leqslant n^{\epsilon}
a.a.s. with exponential rate. The same argument is easily adapted to
\delta_{R}(\mathfrak{C}_{n})
as well
(an
R
-attached network of size

is a network drawn under
\mu^{(\mathcal{D})}
conditioned to have size

and to be a non-trival non-

-network, an event
that holds with probability
\Theta(n^{\prime}\,{}^{-5/2})
).

We now consider
\mathrm{Diam}(\tau(\mathfrak{C}_{n}))
. We first rewrite the system (

) in a positive form, where

is the number of rooted simple
triangulations with

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}L(z)&=\frac{1}{2}L(z)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}z(S(z)+P(z)+H(z)),\\

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{F}(z,\mathbf{y})

[

, Sec.5]

tend to

. For

, let

for

. Thus, the height of the decomposition-tree is a height-parameter for the system, with the terminology of
[

. We can then rely on

, which ensures that for a random cubic network in each type

a.a.s. with exponential rate. Since the diameter of a rooted tree is at most twice its height,

a.a.s. with exponential rate.

5.1
Simple triangulations

For every

, we write

be the set of rooted simple triangulations with

, (4.5)]
, we get:

Z(p):=T_{p}\left(\frac{27}{256}\right)=\frac{1}{p(2p-3)}\left(\frac{16}{9}\right)^{p-2}\binom{2(p-1)}{p-1}.

(26)

From which we deduce:

Z(p)\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{64}{9}\right)^{p-2}p^{-5/2}=\frac{18}{64\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{64}{9}\right)^{p-1}p^{-5/2}.

(27)

The
critical Boltzmann distribution
on simple triangulations of the

-gon is the one assigning probability
(27/256)^{n}/Z(p)
to the elements in
\mathcal{T}_{n,p}
,
for
n\geqslant 0
.

Quasi-simple triangulations: definition and enumeration

{sub:quasiSimpleDef}

Definition 18

Remark 4
.

Observe that in a quasi-simple triangulation, each vertex can carry at most one loop, otherwise the two loops would form a 2-cycle that does not contain the pointed vertex.

Therefore, we can turn a quasi-simple triangulation of the 1-gon into a triangulation of the sphere by merging the two edges that form a triangular face with the root edge, rooting the modified map at the merged edge so that the tail vertex is the one incident to the deleted loop, see Figure

Reciprocally, the set of pointed and rooted simple triangulations of the sphere can be seen as a (strict) subset of quasi-simple triangulations of the 1-gon by opening the root edge and inserting a loop incident to the root vertex inside the 2-gon created.
.

For every

5

5

Lemma 20
.

{lem:largestSimple}

Let
\mathfrak{q}_{n}^{(1)}
be a uniformly random element of size
n

\mathcal{Q}_{(1)}

be the largest simple component of
\mathfrak{q}_{n}^{(1)}
along the decomposition (

be its half number of faces. Then a.a.s.

\mathcal{T}^{\scalebox{.4}{$\blacksquare$}},\mathcal{T}^{-}
, or
\mathcal{T}^{\circ\!-\!\circ}
(not

converges in law, hence is

Proof.

We rely on classical results of analytic combinatorics regarding the size of the largest component in combinatorial decompositions
[
19
]
.
For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{N}
, a combinatorial class
\mathcal{C}
is said to be of singularity-type

\rho

theorems of analytic combinatorics

then guarantee that

. The following
dictionary can then be obtained regarding the size of the greatest component (we also include a sum-rule to track which families along the decomposition have asymptotically non-zero probability of having the largest component):

Sum rule.
Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}

\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
have the same radius of convergence
\rho
, and have respective

\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\notin\mathbb{N}

(resp.
\mathcal{B}
) if

(resp.
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
).

Product rule.
Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}
, where
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
have the same radius of convergence
\rho
, and have respective
singularity types
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\notin\mathbb{N}
, both positive. Let
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
be the random structure in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. Let

be the size of its largest component (either in
\mathcal{A}
or in
\mathcal{B}
, in

if there is a tie).
Then

converges in law. In addition, the largest component is a.a.s. in
\mathcal{A}
(resp. in
\mathcal{B}
) if
\alpha<\alpha^{\prime}
(resp.
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
).

Sequence rule.
Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{Seq}(\mathcal{A})
(with no object of size
0
in
\mathcal{A}
), where
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A}
have same radius of convergence and same positive singularity type
\alpha\notin\mathbb{N}
(non-critical case in
[

be the random structure in
\mathcal{C}_{n}

be the size of the largest component (in

We can then use these rules repeatedly in the regular expression (

∎

We will also need the following statement, ensuring that, for the decomposition (

5.4
Quasi-simple triangulations of the cylinder and their skeleton decomposition

In this section, we describe the structure of the neighborhood of the root in a quasi-simple triangulation, and how it can be encoded via the so-called
skeleton decomposition
introduced by Krikun in
[
23
]
. We follow here the presentation given by Curien and Le Gall in
[
13
, Section 2.2]
for
general
triangulations, and we emphasize the necessary adjustments to deal with quasi-simple triangulations.
We first introduce quasi-simple triangulations of the cylinder, which will appear naturally as the hulls of quasi-simple triangulations.

{sub:skeleton}

Definition 22
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. A
quasi-simple triangulation
of the cylinder of height
r
is a rooted planar map such that:

1.

All its faces are triangles except for two distinguished faces: its root face (also called the bottom face) and the top face.

2.

The boundaries of the bottom and of the top face are disjoint simple cycles.

3.

Every vertex incident to the top face is at graph distance exactly
r
from the boundary of the bottom face, and every edge incident to the top face is also incident to a triangle whose third vertex is at distance
r-1
from the bottom face.

4.

Cycles of length 1 and 2 necessarily separate the bottom face and the top face.

If
\Delta
is a quasi-simple triangulation of the cylinder of height
r
, the boundary of its root face (bottom cycle) is denoted by
\partial\Delta
and its top cycle (boundary of the top face) is denoted by
\partial^{*}\Delta
. We call
\Delta
a quasi-simple triangulation of the
(|\partial\Delta|,|\partial^{*}\Delta|)
-cylinder.

Moreover, we denote by
\mathcal{Z}_{p,r}
the set of quasi-simple triangulations of the cylinder of height
r
with a root face of degree
p
.

Note that this is the same definition as
[
13
, Definition 1]
, except for item
4
.
To describe the encoding of a triangulation of the cylinder via the skeleton decomposition, we need additional definitions.

Definition 23
.

Let
\Delta
be a fixed quasi-simple triangulation of the cylinder of height
r
. For
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
, the
ball of radius
j
of
\Delta
– denoted
B_{j}(\Delta)
– is the submap of
\Delta
, which is spanned by its faces which are incident to at least one vertex at distance strictly smaller than
j
from
\partial\Delta
.

:

\Delta

\partial^{*}\Delta
.

Note that for any

\partial_{j}(\Delta):=\partial^{*}(B^{\bullet}_{j}(\Delta))
. We extend this notation to

and set
\partial_{0}(\Delta):=\partial\Delta

\partial_{j}(\Delta)
is incident to exactly one triangle whose third vertex belongs to
\partial_{j-1}(\Delta)

\mathrm{F}_{j}(\Delta)
for the set of all downward triangles at height
j

\bar{E}(\Delta)

as the vertices of a forest

Given the forest
\mathrm{f}:=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{q})

a (possibly empty) slot in the following way: the slot is included in

A forest

Proposition 25
.

The skeleton decomposition described above is a bijection between, quasi-simple triangulations
\Delta
of the
(p,q)
-cylinder of height
r
, and pairs
(\mathrm{f},(\mathrm{t}_{v})_{v\in\mathrm{f}^{\star}})
, where
\mathrm{f}
is a
(p,q,r)
-admissible forest and for any

the corresponding edge,

We write
(\mathfrak{F}(\Delta),(\mathrm{t}_{v})_{v\in\mathfrak{F}(\Delta)^{\star}})

Proof.

This proposition is very similar to the skeleton decomposition introduced originally by Krikun in
[
23
]
. However, in this paper, he dealt with

\partial\Delta

\partial\Delta^{\star}

\partial\Delta
and
\partial\Delta^{\star}

\partial\Delta
and
\partial\Delta^{\star}
.

One can argue via the universal cover of the cylinder, which is the periodic plane
(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\times\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, the constraint
of being quasi-simple on the cylinder is equivalent to being simple in the universal cover representation.
Thus, one just has to check that, on
(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\times\mathbb{R}
, the obtained (periodic) triangulation is simple. Since, in the universal cover representation,
each boundary
\partial_{j}(\Delta)
can conveniently be drawn as the horizontal line
\{y=j\}
, with the next added layer in the horizontal band
\{j\leqslant y\leqslant j+1\}
, the result follows directly.

has only one or two vertices at some generation

with

).

.

{prop:forestHull}

Remark 5

{rem:branching}

It follows from the definition of

Hence our definition of

Proof.

{ref:absContinuite}

There exists a constant

Proof.

The result follows directly from the uniform upper bound obtained in Lemma

Proposition 34
.

{prop:2point}

Denote by

by a random rooted simple triangulation having

vertices and a random marked vertex

and

Proof.

Again, we follow closely the proof of

. Only two modifications must be made. First, if we follow verbatim their proof, then at the very end, we need to adapt the upper bound they obtain. In particular, they fix three close values

and restrict their attention to the event

Secondly, write
\mathfrak{B}_{r}(\mathfrak{q}_{n}^{(1)},o_{n})
for the ball of radius

in

, and prove that for any

Given the convergence of simple triangulations to the Brownian sphere in the Gromov–Hausdorff-Prokhorov topology

To extend the result to simple triangulations, we rely on the same line of arguments as in the proof of Proposition

, and particularly on (

Moreover, when

6.4
Coupling between
\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{n}^{\dagger}
and proof of Theorem
2

Theorem 35
.

{th:correspondance}

Let
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
be uniformly random in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Consider the two following weighted metric spaces
\mathfrak{T}_{n}=(\mathfrak{t}_{n},\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}},\mu_{n})
and
\mathfrak{K}_{n}=(\mathfrak{t}_{n}^{\dagger},\mathrm{d}^{\star},\mu_{n}^{\dagger})
, where
\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{n}^{\dagger}
denote respectively the uniform measures on
V(\mathfrak{t}_{n})
and on
V(\mathfrak{t}_{n}^{\dagger})
.

Then, there exists an explicit correspondence
C_{n}
between
(\mathfrak{t}_{n},\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}})
and
(\mathfrak{t}_{n}^{\dagger},\mathrm{d}^{\star})
and an explicit coupling
\pi_{n}
between
\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{n}^{\dagger}
, such that:

\pi_{n}(C_{n})\geqslant 1-\frac{2}{n+2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\dis(C_{n})\leqslant\epsilon n^{1/4}.

Before giving the proof of this theorem, notice that in view of the known convergence of simple triangulations towards the Brownian sphere established in
[
1
]
, it concludes the proof of Theorem
2
.

Proof.

The correspondence
C_{n}
is defined as the set of pairs:

C_{n}:=\{(v,f),\text{ with }v\in V(\mathfrak{t}_{n}),\,f\in F(\mathfrak{t}_{n})\text{ and }v\triangleleft f\}.

Next, the coupling we construct between
\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{n}^{\dagger}
is based on the theory of Schnyder woods for simple triangulations
[
35
]
, that we now briefly recall. A simple triangulation
\mathfrak{t}
can be canonically endowed by an orientation of its edges, such that for each
v\in V(\mathfrak{t})
, writing
\mathrm{out}(v)
for the outdegree of
v
, we have:

\mathrm{out}(v)=\begin{cases}3&\text{if }v\text{ is not incident to the root face of }\mathfrak{t}\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(54)

Note that to ensure that this orientation is canonical, it is enough to require that directed counterclockwise cycles are not allowed, but this is not relevant for our purpose. However, this canonical orientation enables to construct an explicit coupling between
\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{n}^{\dagger}
.

Letting
o_{n}
be a random vertex of
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
, we associate to
o_{n}
a uniform face of
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
in the following way. If
o_{n}
is not incident to the root face of
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
, then let
e_{n}
be an edge chosen uniformly among one of the three outgoing edges from
o_{n}
. With probability 1/2, we pick
f_{n}
to be the edge on the left of
e_{n}
, and otherwise
f_{n}
is the edge on the right of
e_{n}
.

Now, assume
o_{n}
is incident to the root face of
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
. Then, with probability 1/3, we let
e_{n}
to be the unique outgoing edge from
o_{n}
, and with probability 2/3, we let
e_{n}
to be a uniform edge of
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
. In both cases, we proceed as above.

It is clear from the construction, that
e_{n}
is a uniform edge of
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
. Since each face has degree
3
,
f_{n}
is then a uniform face of
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
. Moreover, apart on an event of probability at most
2/(n+2)
(corresponding to the case where
o_{n}
is incident to the root face and
e_{n}
is sampled uniformly in
E(\mathfrak{t}_{n})
), we have
o_{n}\triangleleft f_{n}
.

To conclude the proof of the theorem, it is then enough to prove that
\dis(C_{n})\leqslant\epsilon n^{1/4}
, or in other words that:

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in V(\mathfrak{t}_{n}),f,g\in F(\mathfrak{t}_{n})\\
x\triangleleft f,y\triangleleft g\end{subarray}}|\mathrm{d}^{\star}(f,g)-c_{\nu}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}}(x,y)|>\epsilon n^{1/4}\right)\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0.

(55)

To prove this last statement, we only need to adapt slightly the framework of
[
13
, Theorem 1, p.680]
, and thus we only sketch the proof.

We start by presenting the general strategy of the proof. First, thanks to a rerooting argument, we can extend the result for the 2-point function stated in Proposition
34
to control the pairwise distance between any finite number of random points. Next, we rely on the known convergence of simple triangulations to the Brownian sphere in the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov sense (established in
[
1
]
) to transfer the compactness of the Brownian sphere from the continuum world to the discrete world. This allows us to approximate the metric structure of a large simple random triangulation by restricting our attention to a (large) fixed number of uniform points. Lastly (and this is the only difference with the proof of
[
13
, Theorem 1]
), Proposition
31
extends this approximation result from a simple triangulation to its dual.

Let us now state more formally those main steps. Let
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
be a uniform element of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Let
\Phi_{n}:V(\mathfrak{t}_{n})\rightarrow F(\mathfrak{t}_{n})
be a deterministic function (given
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
) which associates to each vertex
v
of
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
a face
f
with
v\triangleleft\Phi_{n}(v)
. Let
o^{\prime}_{n}
and
o^{\prime\prime}_{n}
be two independent uniform vertices of
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
.
By the same chain of arguments as in
[
13
, p.680]
, it follows from Proposition
34
that for any
\epsilon>0
:

\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathrm{d}^{\star}(\Phi_{n}(o^{\prime}_{n}),\Phi_{n}(o^{\prime\prime}_{n}))-c_{\nu}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}}(o^{\prime}_{n},o^{\prime\prime}_{n})|>\epsilon n^{1/4}\right)\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0.

(56)

Now let
(o_{n}^{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
be a sequence of independent uniform vertices of
\mathfrak{t}_{n}
. For any
\delta>0
, there exists
N\geqslant 1
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{x\in V(\mathfrak{t}_{n})}\left(\inf_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}}(x,o_{n}^{j})\right)<\epsilon n^{1/4}\right)>1-\delta.

(57)

By the triangle inequality, we can then write:

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in V(\mathfrak{t}_{n}),f,g\in F(\mathfrak{t}_{n})\\
x\triangleleft f,y\triangleleft g\end{subarray}}|\mathrm{d}^{\star}(f,g)-c_{\nu}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}}(x,y)|\\
\leqslant\quad\sup_{1\leqslant i\leqslant j\leqslant N}\left\{\Big|\mathrm{d}^{\star}(\Phi_{n}(o_{n}^{i}),\Phi_{n}(o_{n}^{j}))-c_{\nu}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}}(o_{n}^{i},o_{n}^{j})\Big|\right\}+2\sup_{x\in V(\mathfrak{t}_{n})}\left(\inf_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}}(x,o_{n}^{j})\right)\\
\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+2\sup_{f\in F(\mathfrak{t}_{n})}\left(\inf_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\mathrm{d}^{\star}(f,\Phi_{n}(o_{n}^{j}))\right)\\

We take
N
large enough so that (
57
) holds. By Proposition
31
, outside a set of probability at most
\delta
and provided that
n
is large enough, the last term in the upper bound can be bounded by
2\sup_{x\in V(\mathfrak{t}_{n})}\left(\inf_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{gr}}(x,o_{n}^{j})\right)+n^{1/8}
.
In view of (
57
) and (
56
) (applied simultaneously to all pairs
(o_{n}^{i},o_{n}^{j})
), this concludes the proof.
∎

7.2
First properties of the coupling

{sub:propCoupling}

To quantify the number of edges which do not have the same length in both models, we start by proving the following estimate:

Lemma 38
.

{lem:Ni}

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{N}}=(\widehat{N}_{1},\widehat{N}_{2},\ldots)
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}=(\widetilde{N}_{1},\widetilde{N}_{2},\ldots)
be independently sampled from
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{q}
and from
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{q}
respectively.
Then there exist positive constants
a
and
A
such that a.a.s. as
q\to\infty
, the following event
\mathcal{E}_{q}
holds:

•

for every positive integer
i\leqslant a\log(q)
, we have
\mathrm{min}(\widehat{N}_{i},\widetilde{N}_{i})\geqslant q^{3/4}
and
|\widehat{N}_{i}-\widetilde{N}_{i}|\leqslant q^{2/3}
.

•

for all
i\geqslant A\log(q)
, we have
\widehat{N}_{i}=\widetilde{N}_{i}=0
.

Proof.

Let
p_{i}:=\nu_{\star}(i)
. We can obtain a lower bound for
p_{i}
by noticing that any cubic network that is a series of
i

L
-networks has pole-distance
i+1
. Hence:

p_{i}\geqslant\frac{L(\rho)^{i-1}}{D(\rho)}\geqslant\frac{1}{D(\rho)}e^{-\kappa i},\text{ where }\kappa=\ln(1/L(\rho))>0,

and
L(\rho)
is the generating series of
L
-networks evaluated at its radius of convergence.

Let
a=\frac{1}{5\kappa}
.
Then, for
i\leqslant a\log(q)
, we have
p_{i}\geqslant\frac{1}{D(\rho)}q^{-1/5}
.
On the other hand, by definition,
\widehat{N}_{i}
follows a
\mathrm{Binomial}(3q,p_{i})
distribution, so that
\mathbb{E}(\widehat{N}_{i})=3q\,p_{i}\geqslant\frac{3}{D(\rho)}q^{4/5}
.
By Hoeffding’s inequality, we have that, for any
x>0
:

\mathbb{P}(|\widehat{N}_{i}-3q\,p_{i}|\geqslant xq^{1/2})\leqslant 2\exp(-2x^{2}/3),

so that
\mathbb{P}(|\widehat{N}_{i}-3q\,p_{i}|\geqslant q^{2/3}/2)\leqslant 2\exp(-q^{1/3}/6)
. By Lemma
9
,
\widetilde{N}_{i}
has the same law as
\widehat{N}_{i}
conditioned on an event of probability
\Theta(q^{-2/3})
, which implies that

\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{N}_{i}-3q\,p_{i}|\geqslant q^{2/3}/2)=O(q^{2/3}\exp(-q^{1/3}/6)).

Hence,

\mathbb{P}\left(\forall i\leqslant a\log(q),|\widehat{N}_{i}-3q\,p_{i}|\leqslant q^{2/3}/2\text{ and }|\widetilde{N}_{i}-3q\,p_{i}|\leqslant q^{2/3}/2\right)\xrightarrow[q\rightarrow\infty]{}1,

which gives the first property (using the lower bound on
3q\,p_{i}
).

Regarding the second property, we proved in Lemma
12
that
\nu_{\star}
has exponential tail. Hence, if
(\widehat{\delta}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\delta}_{3q})

is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables sampled from
\nu_{\star}
, then there exists
b>0
, such that
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{max}(\widehat{\delta}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\delta}_{3q})\geqslant i)=O(q\exp(-bi))
. This is equivalent to the fact that
\mathbb{P}(\widehat{N}_{j}\neq 0\ \mathrm{for\ some}\ j\geqslant i)=O(q\exp(-bi))
.
Using again Lemma
9
, we can derive from this property, the fact that
\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{N}_{j}\neq 0\ \mathrm{for\ some}\ j\geqslant i)=O(q^{5/2}\exp(-bi))
. Hence, setting
A=3/b
, this gives the desired result.
∎

.

{coro:coupling}

Conditionally on the event
\mathcal{E}_{q}
defined in the previous lemma, for any
e\in E(\mathfrak{K}_{q})
such that
\widehat{\ell}(e)\leqslant a\log(q)

By construction of the coupling (or rather of its variant described in Remark

(62)

Summing over the values of
