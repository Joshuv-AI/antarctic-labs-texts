Bonsai: A Generalized Look at Dual Deduplication
Hadi Sehat 1 , Anders Lindskov Kloborg 2 , Christian Mørup 2 , Elena Pagnin 2 , Daniel E. Lucani 1

Abstract
Cloud Service Providers (CSPs) offer a vast amount of storage space at competitive prices to cope with the growing demand for digital data storage. Dual deduplication is a recent framework designed to improve data compression on the CSP while keeping clients’ data private from the CSP. To achieve this, clients perform lightweight information-theoretic transformations to their data prior to upload. We investigate the effectiveness of dual deduplication, and propose an improvement for the existing state-of-the-art method. We name our proposal Bonsai as it aims at reducing storage fingerprint and improving scalability. In detail, Bonsai achieves (1) significant reduction in client storage, (2) reduction in total required storage (client + CSP), and (3) reducing the deduplication time on the CSP. Our experiments show that Bonsai achieves compression rates of 68% on the cloud and 5% on the client, while allowing the cloud to identify deduplications in a time-efficient manner. We also show that combining our method with universal compressors in the cloud, e.g., Brotli, can yield better overall compression on the data compared to only applying the universal compressor or plain Bonsai. Finally, we show that Bonsai and its variants provide sufficient privacy against an honest-but-curious CPS that knows the distribution of the Clients’ original data.



I
Introduction

Due to emerging applications that produce massive amounts of data,
many systems choose to use Cloud Service Providers (CSPs) to outsource the storage of their data.
In order to deliver a cost-effective service to their clients, CSPs tend to use compression techniques to reduce the fingerprint of the data.
This includes a number of file compression techniques, e.g., DEFLATE
[
1
]
, BZIP2
[
2
]
, Brotli
[
3
]
,
as well as delta encoding approaches, e.g., VCDIFF
[
4
]
,
which focus on individual chunks of data or files for compression.
As an alternative, data deduplication
[
5
]

removes duplicate data across different files and even different users in the system.
Data deduplication stores a copy of each unique chunk and represents files with pointers to these unique chunks.
In a nutshell, it avoids storing duplicates
[
6
]
.
In most practical storage systems, both deduplication and compression are used
for a more efficient reduction of the fingerprint of the data
[
7
]
.

Recently, generalized deduplication
[
8
]
has been proposed as an improvement to data deduplication.
In this case, the storage system is not only capable of removing exact duplicates,
but also chunks of files that are close to each other according to a certain metric
[
9
]
.
At its core, generalized deduplication
in a cloud setting works as follows.
The CSP
maintains a set of bases. For each received chunk of file, if the chunk is already present in the set of bases
the CSP deduplicates it by storing a file identifier and a pointer representing the base.
Otherwise,
the CSP looks for a base similar to the given chunk,
i.e., a chunk in the set of bases that has a short distance to the received chunk, e.g., according to the
Hamming distance
[
9
]
or the Swap distance
[
10
]
.
If such a base is found,
the CSP deduplicates this record in a generalized way
by storing a file identifier, a pointer representing the closest base, and a string containing the difference between the base and the chunk. Finally, if no similar bases are present in the set of stored bases,
the CSP considers the chunk as a new base to add to the list of bases.

A major drawback of allowing the CSP to carry out (generalized) deduplication
is that the CSP must be provided with the actual data.
This is not desirable in applications where clients may upload sensitive information or are concerned about data privacy.
A naive solution would be to encrypt the data and upload only ciphertexts.
This approach, however, drastically reduces the deduplication capability on the CSP, as secure encryption implies little or no correlation among ciphertexts.
An alternative approach is to use
convergence encryption
[
11
]
,
to maintain a balance between privacy and compression.
The main weakness of convergence encryption
is that a nosy CSP or even a malicious client can attack the system to discover which chunks have been stored previously in the system,
exposing sensitive information to the malicious party.
Moreover, the CSP can obtain exact information about the files of each user given sufficient time and resources, by breaking the encryption of the outsourced data
[
12
]
.

The work by Vestergaard et al.
[
13
]
proposes an alternative mechanism to convergence encryption, which is called Multi-Key revealing Encryption (MKRE).
In
[
13
]
chunks are transformed before upload so that any two similar chunks yield outsourced data that are close to each other.
Although this method is shown to be more robust against attempts to break the encryption,
its security is proven only in the programmable random oracle model,
which is not realistic as it cannot be faithfully implemented by hash functions.

Recently, the work by Sehat et al.
[
10
]
bypasses the programmable random oracle challenge and
introduces a unique privacy-aware deduplication mechanism for multi-client environments called
dual deduplication
.
In dual deduplication, clients perform
information-theoretic transformations on their data
before uploading it to the CSP.
These transformations aim at puncturing the data
to simultaneously increase deduplication capabilities on the CSP,
and creating uncertainty for the CSP about the possible pre-images
of the uploaded chunk.
From a high level perspective,
to the eye of a CSP or an attacker,
the dual deduplication system
proposed in
[
10
]
mimics a
deletion channel
from information theory
[
14
]
. In particular it is known that
retrieving the original data
after transmission through the deletion channel
is a hard problem.
predicting how input data will be modified by the channel is hard as well
[
14
]
.
While these properties are undesirable to establish a reliable communication between two parties,
dual deduplication uses them as key features for proving the privacy of the system.
In this work, we build on the dual deduplication idea introduced in
[
10
]
,
and mitigate the three major drawbacks of the original construction.
First, clients in
[
10
]
need to store a significant portion of their data locally.
This is contrary to the intent of outsourcing data to a CSP and minimizing local storage.
Second, the CSP in
[
10
]
must perform a brute force search to find the matches for each uploaded chunk to achieve the best compression rate.
Third, the system in
[
10
]
is proven private only as long as
the CSP lacks information about the probability distribution of the client’s original data.
This assumption does not hold for databases that are very homogeneous,
or with predictable data such as time-series data or e-mail texts.

The main goal of our paper is to face the aforementioned drawbacks
and provide concrete, efficient solutions to address these issues.
To this end, we propose
Bonsai
, a fully fledged, yet scaled down (in terms of storage requirements and complexity) alternative to the Yggdrasil approach proposed in
[
10
]
.
The name Bonsai is chosen to maintain a connection to and provide figurative comparison with the original dual deduplication method Yggdrasil, named after the tree of life in Norse mythology.
Moreover, in Bonsai the CSP transforms and stores the received data in a tree structure that resembles a bonsai, which is
a small-size version of a tree.

I-A

Our Contribution

We propose a new transformation mechanism for dual deduplication
that improves the
scrambling
of outsourced data,
and achieves better privacy guarantees than
[
10
]
.
Compared to
[
10
]
, our method requires less storage on the client side and maintains
good compression rates on the CSP.
This is done by employing a Pseudo-Random Number Generator (PRNG)
to determine the positions of the symbols to be deleted.
This change removes the need to store the positions of deleted elements on the client side, thus effectively reducing the storage requirements from 18% in
[
10
]
, to only 5% in Bonsai.

We present an innovative deduplication mechanism that lets the CSP
identify possible deduplications efficiently (instead of bruteforcing as in
[
10
]
).
Our technique stores bases on the CSP in a forest data structure, which
reduces the computational cost of finding deduplicates and adding new bases as well as reducing the storage cost for the bases.
In addition, we improve the compression potential on the CSP using an approach inspired by Huffman Codes and replacing the symbols with lower probability distribution.
Our method achieves a competitive compression rate of 68% in the cloud, while allowing deduplication and maintaining privacy guarantees.

We prove our proposed method to be privacy-aware, in the sense that
an honest-but-curious CSP faces a high degree of uncertainty when guessing what the original data of the clients is.
In contrast to
[
10
]
, this claim holds even assuming the CSP has knowledge of the distribution of clients’ original data, as well as of the number of performed deletions.

However, Brotli provides no privacy guarantees, in contrast to Bonsai.
Interestingly, combining Bonsai with Brotli (i.e., using Brotli to compress the result of the deduplication process) yields much better compression than each individual approach.
This suggests that Bonsai creates highly compressible data, even when the original data is homogenous.

Intuitively, this is due to the fact that Bonsai adds metadata to find matches across
different
data blocks, which is unnecessary for deduplicating identical chunks.
The gains are seen for smaller chunk sizes,
disappearing for larger chunk sizes due to the fact that the probability of finding an exact duplicates is reduced as the size of the chunks increases.

II
Related Work

Data deduplication is a powerful way to reduce the storage footprint of highly correlated data
[
5
,
16
]
.
Delta deduplication falls into the category of redundant data reduction techniques, which also include Delta Compression techniques such as Xdelta
[
17
]
.
The big difference between the two methods is the facts that Delta encoding techniques use copy/insert instructions to record the “difference” between two files
on string-level, while the data Deduplication techniques remove the “redundancy” between files, either on file-level
[
18
]
or chunk-level
[
19
]
.
The theoretical bounds of deduplication and generalized deduplication are discussed extensively in
[
20
,
9
,
21
]
.
Implementations of generalized deduplication have been proposed for file systems
[
22
]
and CSPs
[
23
]
.

The introduction of deduplication in the early 2000s sparked many research directions aiming at improving or enhancing the compression method.
Problems and proposed solutions can summarized into three major categories.

•

Increasing performance and throughput:
This line of research includes, but is not limited to optimization of deduplication
[
15
]
,
acceleration of computational tasks
[
24
]
,
reduction of the access time to metadata
[
25
]
,
and improvements of the chunking procedure based on the content
[
26
,
27
]
.

•

Reducing the storage requirement:
These solutions focus on reducing the fingerprint of the data.
Some of the major research outputs in this category deal with reducing the overhead of the metadata
[
28
]
,
improving the efficiency of deduplication in the cloud and reducing the size of fingerprints
[
29
]
,
and reducing the overhead of the data by establishing a communication
between the cloud and the clients
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
.

•

Improving the privacy and security of the clients’ data:
Privacy issues arise in Data Deduplication as all the data from the clients are deduplicated together, leaving the storage system vulnerable to leaking the information of the data. As this category is the closest line of research to the solution proposed in this paper, we take a closer look at this category.

For privacy-aware data owners (clients),
the CSP should be able to provide storage solutions without needing access to
the content of their data.
If conventional encryption is used to protect clients’ data, the compression potential on the cloud severely worsens
[
12
]
.
As an alternative, Message Leaked Encryption (MLE)
[
33
]
has been proposed, which allows one to encrypt data securely, while preserving the deduplication potential.
The most widely used MLE technique in deduplication techniques is called Convergence Encryption (CE)
[
34
]
.
However, CE has been shown to be exploitable by brute-force
[
35
]
,
and is vulnerable to frequency attacks
[
36
]
.
The proposed adjustments to improve CE, such as DUPLESS
[
37
]
, and Duan’s scheme
[
38
]
, require a trusted cloud or a third-party dealer to ensure the privacy of the data.
Alternative approaches rely on the Blockchain technology
[
39
]
, on gateways
[
40
]
,
sacrifice computational efficiency
[
41
]
,
or generate large overhead for the clients
[
42
]
.
More recently, Multi-Key Revealing Encryption
[
13
]
has been proposed to ensure privacy of the clients’ data while maintaining deduplication capabilities. This solution uses programmable random oracles which are not instantiatable in practice.

There have been surveys that describe the privacy solutions in more depth
[
43
,
44
]
.
We also note that
[
6
]
provides a general survey on deduplication with a comprehensive study of the state-of-the-art.

III
System Model and Performance Metrics

In this section, we describe our system model, the type of data handled by the system, the attacker model and, finally, the performance metrics used to evaluate Bonsai.

III-A

System Model

We adopt the dual deduplication system introduced in
[
10
]
.
Concretely, our system
\mathscr{S}
consists of a number of independent users, referred to as
{\sf Clients}
, and a single CSP, referred to as
{\sf Cloud}
.
The
{\sf Clients}
’ goal is to maximize the data they outsource to the
{\sf Cloud}
while retaining some level of privacy on each record; the
{\sf Cloud}
’s goal is to minimize the space required to store the
{\sf Clients}
’ outsourced data.
The system
\mathscr{S}
allows the
{\sf Clients}
to locally process their data, by applying a transformation
{\sf{trf.Client}}
on their data before upload
and storing the information about the transformations
locally.
Note that the
{\sf Cloud}
has no access to the information stored locally by any
{\sf Client}
.
This fact is crucial to argue the privacy of Bonsai. Finally, we assume that the communication channel between the
{\sf Clients}
and
{\sf Cloud}
is authenticated and error-free.

III-B

Data Type

The data handled by
{\sf Cloud}
and
{\sf Clients}
in
\mathscr{S}
are files
F
represented as strings of
n_{F}
symbols,
where each symbol
is a value
consisting of
k
bits.
i.e., our alphabet is
\mathbb{A}=\{0,1\}^{k}

and files are
F\in\mathbb{A}^{n_{F}}=\{0,1\}^{k\cdot n_{F}}
.
We denote the size of the alphabet as
N=|\mathbb{A}|=2^{k}-1
.

Recall that each
{\sf Client}
performs some transformations
{\sf{trf.Client}}
on their files
F
prior to upload.
In what follows, we denote the output of
{\sf{trf.Client}}
as an outsource-local pair
(F^{\prime},L)\leftarrow{\sf{trf.Client}}(F)
, where
F^{\prime}
, called outsource is the data
outsourced to the
{\sf Cloud}
; and
L
is the secret piece of information stored locally by
{\sf Client}
.
We call this secret information client-side deviation.
Note that both outsource and client-side deviation are strictly shorter than the original file; namely the length of outsource
|F^{\prime}|=n_{F^{\prime}}<n_{F}
and the length of the client-side deviation
|L|<n_{F}
.

III-C

Attacker Model

Our adversary
\mathcal{A}
is a honest-but-curious
{\sf Cloud}
.
Concretely,
\mathcal{A}
has access to the whole data stored in the
{\sf Cloud}
,
i.e., any record
F^{\prime}
ever outsourced by any
{\sf Client}
.
The goal of the adversary is to breach
{\sf Client}
’s privacy by retrieving their original data
F
.
i.e., to identify the complete string
F
from which the
{\sf Client}
derived the outsource
F^{\prime}
.
We recall that
\mathcal{A}
has no access to the client-side deviation
L
stored by the
{\sf Client}
locally.
In this work we consider two types of adversaries:

\bullet
A
weak
adversary,
\mathcal{A}^{\sf week}
, does not have any information on the distribution of clients’ original data
F
.
This weak adversary emulates settings where clients upload unpredictable data, e.g., very diverse types of files, or uncorrelated information.

\bullet
A
strong
adversary,
\mathcal{A}^{\sf strong}
, knows the distribution
\mathcal{D}
of the original files, i.e., the probability distribution with which a specific string
F
is produced by a
{\sf Client}
.
Notably,
\mathcal{A}^{\sf strong}
has knowledge of the correlation between the symbols in
F
.

As expected, the strong adversary is more realistic than the weak one.
While it is reasonable to assume that in real systems the CSP may have some information about the files generated by the clients;
knowing the exact probability distribution for any possible file
F
is a requirement hard to meet.
Hence, we believe a real-world attacker lies somewhere in-between the two types of adversaries we consider.

Our protocol, Bonsai, makes minimal use of cryptographic tools. Namely, the only cryptographic primitive required is a PRNG (Pseudo-Random Number Generator) function, run by the
{\sf Clients}
within the
{\sf{trf.Client}}
process. All other transformations employed in Bonsai are information theoretic.
To further strengthen our security outcomes, for each type of adversary, we consider two potential scenarios:

\bullet

Case 1
:
\mathcal{A}
has negligible probability of breaking the PRNG (this correspond to a classical adversary that is computationally bounded and runs in probabilistic polynomial time).

\bullet

Case 2
:
\mathcal{A}
can break the PRNG (this corresponds to quantum adversary that is computationally unbounded and equipped with a quantum computer, or an adversary that knows the seed input to the PRNG).

We remark that, in Bonsai the PRNG is employed to provide better compression on the
{\sf Client}
side. Therefore, it is possible to obtain a complete information theoretic secure version of protocol by replacing the use of the PRNG with a truly random function, though this impacts negatively the performance of the system
by reducing the compression potential on the
{\sf Cloud}
.

Finally we argue that assuming
\mathcal{A}
is not fully malicious is needed to guarantee the correctness of the overall system. For example, if the
{\sf Cloud}
tampers with outsourced items,
{\sf Clients}
may reconstruct incorrect data and the service loses reliability.
We consider that in the specific setting of outsourced storage, an honest-but-curious attacker is more realistic than a malicious one.
For this reason, we leave the analysis of the implications of having a malicious
{\sf Cloud}
or a malicious
{\sf Client}
as future work.

Compression Ratios

The main goal of Bonsai is to
reduce the fingerprint of the data via dual deduplication
(i.e., compressing data on both the
{\sf Client}
and the
{\sf Cloud}
sides).
Therefore, it is natural to define performance metrics that measure the compression ratio of the system by looking at the
{\sf Client}
, the
{\sf Cloud}
, and the overall system perspective.
We employ the performance metrics introduced by Yggdrasil, the state-of-the-art work on dual deduplication
[
10
]
.

In what follows,
DB
denotes the set of all the data of all the
{\sf Clients}
; and
DB_{i}
denotes the set of the data of a single
{\sf Client}

i
.
Both
DB
and
DB_{i}
contain a number of files
F
.
We assume that our system
\mathscr{S}
has a total of
{n}

{\sf Clients}
,
which are enumerated in a set
{U}=\{{\sf Client}_{1},....,{\sf Client}_{{n}}\}
.
In order to formally define the compression rate of Bonsai in different parties (
{\sf Client}
and
{\sf Cloud}
),
we use a function size(party, D),
which we define as follows.

size(
party, D):

On the input of
\sf{party}\in\{{\sf Cloud},{\sf Client}\}
and some data D,
this function outputs
the total number of bits required to store D in the given party.

Equipped with this notation we can define the
{\sf Client}
Compression ratio as :

\mathcal{C}_{\sf Client}=\frac{{{\sf size}}({\sf Client}_{i},DB_{i})}{|DB_{i}|},

(1)

where
|DB_{i}|
denotes the total size of the data in the posession of
{\sf Client}_{i}
.

As the
{\sf Cloud}
stores data from all the clients,
we calculate the total compression rate of the
{\sf Cloud}
with respect to

DB
, therefore, the
{\sf Cloud}

\mathcal{C}_{\sf Cloud}=\frac{{{\sf size}}({\sf Cloud},DB)}{|DB|}.

(2)

Another interesting compression metric is the total compression rate, which indicates the total storage required by all the parties (
{\sf Clients}
and the
{\sf Cloud}
) to store the whole
database

DB
compared to the size of the original

. This metric allows us to compare the performance of Bonsai with other compression methods that only store the data on the
{\sf Cloud}
.
Intuitively, the total required storage in Bonsai is equal to the summation of the required storage in all
{\sf Clients}
and the required storage on the
{\sf Cloud}
, therefore,
the total compression rate is:

\footnotesize\mathcal{C}=\frac{{{\sf size}}({\sf Cloud},DB)+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{{n}}{{\sf size}}({\sf Client},DB_{i})}{|DB|}.

III-E

Privacy Metrics

We estimate the privacy level of a dual deduplication system
\mathscr{S}
in terms of the uncertainty metric
\mathcal{U}
and the leakage
\mathcal{L}
.
The uncertainty metric shows the amount of unpredictability
that an adversary
\mathcal{A}
faces when
it attempts to guess the value of a given record
F
, having seen only its outsourced version
F^{\prime}
.
We acknowledge that the adversary may have some knowledge about the file
F
prior to receiving the
F^{\prime}
,
therefore, after receiving the
F^{\prime}
, the uncertainty of the adversary drops as it gains more information about the file
F
.
The leakage
\mathcal{L}
measures this drop in the uncertainty of the adversary
\mathcal{A}
after receiving
F^{\prime}
, i.e., the amount of information about
F
leaked to
\mathcal{A}
after receiving
F^{\prime}
.

We use the notation of Shannon’s entropy
[
45
]
to calculate the uncertainty metric and leakage for our adversaries.
We assume that
\mathcal{A}
uses its knowledge to generate the list of all possible preimages
\mathcal{I}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D},F^{\prime})
of a given base
F^{\prime}
. It is clear that the original file belongs in this list,
F\in\mathcal{I}
. Then the adversary tries to predict the real value of
F
using the preimages in
\mathcal{I}
and the probability that each one of them is the real
F
.
The uncertainty that the attacker faces to guess the original file
F
is equal to the entropy of the set
\mathcal{I}
. Therefore, we define uncertainty metric as follows.

Uncertainty Metric:

\mathcal{U}(F)=H(F|F^{\prime}),

(4)

where
H(F|F^{\prime})
is the Shannon’s entropy of
F
given
F^{\prime}
.
Based on our definition, the uncertainty prior to receiving
F^{\prime}
is equal to
H(F)
. Therefore, the leakage of information that occurs when sending
F^{\prime}
is calculated as follows.

Leakage:

\mathcal{L}=\frac{H(F)-H(F|F^{\prime})}{H(F)}.

(5)

Note that the Shannon’s entropy of file, i.e.,
H(F)
is calculated in the viewpoint
of the adversary.
This means that if we consider the weak adversary, the value for
H(F)
is equal to the Shannon’s entropy for
a uniformly random probability distribution and not the actual value of the Shannon’s entropy
based on the statistical properties of the database
DB
.

IV-A

Bonsai
in a nutshell

We employ a similar system model as
[
10
]
, namely, a single
{\sf Cloud}
storage provider connected to multiple
{\sf Clients}
.
{\sf Clients}
wish to outsource their data to the
{\sf Cloud}
in a privacy-preserving manner,
while the
{\sf Cloud}
wishes to reduce the storage footprint of the received outsourced data.

To achieve privacy-preserving dual deduplication, each
{\sf Client}
applies a transformation
{\sf{trf.Client}}()
to the original file
F
to generate the pair
(F^{\prime},L)\leftarrow{\sf{trf.Client}}(F)
.
Intuitively,
F^{\prime}
is the record
{\sf Client}
outsources to
{\sf Cloud}
,
and this should leak as little information about the original file
F
as possible (to preserve privacy).

L
is called the client-side deviation and condenses instructions on how to reconstruct
F
from
F^{\prime}
.
The
{\sf{trf.Client}}
entails a number of manipulations as shown in Section
IV-C
and Fig.
3
.
For the sake of simplicity, we describe only the main manipulation in
{\sf{trf.Client}}
, i.e.,
{\sf{Del}}
(deletion), in more detail.
As the name suggests, this transformation removes symbols from
F
. The outcome is a sub-string
F^{\prime}
of the original file
F
.
To build intuition,
{\sf{Del}}
acts as a deletion channel. Thus, it simultaneously performs two tasks
(1) it reduces the size of the outsourced record, and
(2) it makes it harder for the
{\sf Cloud}
(and thus an adversary
\mathcal{A}
) to guess the exact original data
F
from
F^{\prime}
.
To enable the reconstruction of
F
from
F^{\prime}
,

{\sf{Del}}
returns an additional output that contains the list of deleted positions (in the form of pointers) and the corresponding deleted values (in the form of alphabet symbols)
in form of the client-side deviation
L
.
With
L
at hand, the
{\sf Client}
can efficiently reconstruct
F
from
F^{\prime}
.
Fig.
2
illustrates an example of how
{\sf{Del}}
works.
In this example, the symbol ‘1’ from position ‘6’ is deleted.
The local deviation contains two values: ‘6’ is a pointer indicating the position of the deleted symbol, and ‘1’ is the deleted symbol.

In Bonsai, we employ a PRNG function to select the positions where we perform deletions.
Concretely, the
{\sf Client}
selects a pre-determined number of seeds
for each chunk, and interprets the output of the PRNG
of each seed
as a sequence of positions on which to apply
{\sf{Del}}
in the chunk. The use of seeds instead of storing positions alongside values, as done in
[
10
]
, significantly reduces the storage requirement on the
{\sf Client}
.
This means that all positions of deleted elements in
L
can be stored by a single seed.
Further details on how to combine the PRNG and
{\sf{Del}}
, and the other manipulations involved in
{\sf{trf.Client}}
, are given in Section
IV-C
.
We also analyze how the use of a PRNG impacts security in Section
V-E
.

Bonsai employs a novel way to perform generalized deduplication on the
{\sf Cloud}
side.
Upon receiving an outsource
F^{\prime}
, instead of looking for similar bases according to
classical distance metrics, e.g., Hamming
[
9
]

and Swap
[
10
]
,
the
{\sf Cloud}
in Bonsai processes
F^{\prime}
to generate a sorted base
{B}
and a set of strings, called
cloud-side deviation
.
After this transformation, the
{\sf Cloud}
applies deduplication on
{B}
.
We call this
{\sf Cloud}
-side process
{\sf{trf.Cloud}}
.
Its aim is to identify an alternative representation of
F^{\prime}
that deduplicates with higher probability.

The manipulations in
{\sf{trf.Cloud}}
are: grouping, Huffman coding and sorting as detailed in Section
IV-D
.
To give an intuition, the
{\sf Cloud}
in Bonsai identifies each symbol with three values.
We denote these values by
{\sf{BracketID}}
,

{\sf{SymbolID}}
, and
{\sf{ValueID}}
. These three values uniquely identify eqach symbol
(more details in section
IV-D
)
.
Upon receiving an
F^{\prime}
, the
{\sf Cloud}
generates a string containing the
{\sf{BracketID}}
of
for all symbols in
F^{\prime}
,
and sorts it in ascending order.
This string is called base and
is denoted by
{B}
.
As the base

is sorted,
the
{\sf Cloud}
stores the
{B}
of all of the received
F^{\prime}
in a forest structure (called
\mathcal{B}
),
to help find duplicates in time

,
where
{|\mathcal{B}|}
is the number of bases in
\mathcal{B}
.
Using this forest structure, instead of directly storing the
{B}
for each
F^{\prime}
,
the
{\sf Cloud}
assigns a pointer to
{B}
in
\mathcal{B}
,
and uses this pointer to store each
F^{\prime}
.
The
{\sf Cloud}
needs to store
the necessary information
to reconstruct
F^{\prime}
from

upon
{\sf Client}
’s request.
This information is stored
in what we call cloud-side deviation.
Cloud-side deviation contains of three strings:

,

In detail,

contains information about the position of the symbols
in the original outsource

;

{\sf{SymbolID}}
of the symbols; and

contains the
{\sf{ValueID}}
of the symbols.

{S}
is created by using an algorithm inspired by
Cycle Sort
[
46
]

to identify the required swaps
needed to sort

.
This algorithm
is detailed in Algorithm

, in Section
IV-D
.
In order to reduce the size of the

{\sf{SymbolID}}
and
{\sf{ValueID}}
for the symbols,
and ultimately having a bigger compression gain in the
{\sf Cloud}
,
the
{\sf Cloud}
uses Huffman coding to represent the
{\sf{SymbolID}}
and
{\sf{ValueID}}
of the symbols
in each Bracket. We discuss the choice of Huffman coding and its implications in Section

.

{\sf Policy}
and protocols between
{\sf Cloud}
and
{\sf Client}

The communication between
{\sf Cloud}
and
{\sf Client}
consists of two algorithms:
{\sf{Upload}}
and
{\sf{Get}}
.
With
{\sf{Upload}}
the
{\sf Client}
sends
F^{\prime}
to the
{\sf Cloud}
;
while with
{\sf{Get}}
the
{\sf Client}
requests back the uploaded data from the
{\sf Cloud}
.
In order to ensure consistency, the
{\sf Client}
uses a file identifier,
{\sf fid}
, for each outsource
F^{\prime}
it uploads to
{\sf Cloud}
. The identifier
{\sf fid}
is sent to the
{\sf Cloud}
alongside the
F^{\prime}
and is used by
{\sf{Get}}
as a reference to
F^{\prime}
.
Since we assume
{\sf Cloud}
not to be malicious,
{\sf fid}
does not serve as an integrity tool. Employing a cryptographic secure hash function would, however, mitigate some attack scenarios in the presence of a malicious cloud, or provide secure file sharing among
{\sf Clients}

[
47
]
.

In Bonsai, the
{\sf Cloud}
performs deduplication to optimize space and reduce the storage requirements.
Therefore, the compression potential increases if the received outsource
F^{\prime}
are similar to each other.
This is
achieved by enforcing a certain probability distribution on the uploaded records.
In order to ensure that the
{\sf Cloud}
receives
deduplication friendly
data,
Bonsai adopts policies on the probability distribution of symbols in
F^{\prime}
. Policies are publicly available to
{\sf Clients}
and should be fetched before
{\sf{Upload}}
and
taken into consideration when running
{\sf{trf.Client}}
.
In detail,
{\sf Policy}=[\mathcal{D^{\prime}},n_{F^{\prime}}]

where
n_{F^{\prime}}
is the expected size of
F^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
is defined an array of values
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}=[p^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,p^{\prime}_{N-1}]
,
and
p^{\prime}_{i}
indicates the probability of symbol
i
in the
{\sf Cloud}
.
This policy is calculated by the
{\sf Cloud}
from the set of stored bases

\mathcal{B}
.
This increases the potential for
deduplication between outsourced data
by creating
F^{\prime}
that are
similar to each other,
leading to higher deduplication rates
and lower storage requirement in the
{\sf Cloud}
and ultimately
higher compression gain.
The
{\sf Policy}
may include more detailed information to further increase compression capability of
{\sf Cloud}
or for other use cases such as ensuring reliability.
We leave other information that can be included in
{\sf Policy}
and the effect of such information on performance and compression potential of Bonsai to future work.

The
{\sf Cloud}
of
Bonsai

As mentioned, the
{\sf Cloud}
uses an innovative method to store the received

and find possible deduplications in the received data.
In this section, we explore the ideas and theory that lead to
{\sf{trf.Cloud}}
,
and describe
{\sf{trf.Cloud}}
and deduplication in the
{\sf Cloud}
in more detail.
We also show a toy example to illustrate

{\sf{trf.Cloud}}
visually.

Motivation

The main idea behind
{\sf{trf.Cloud}}
is to reduce the time needed to find potential deduplication between received data in the
{\sf Cloud}
.
The idea of
{\sf{trf.Cloud}}
consists of four main ideas
to increase the performance and compression rate in the
{\sf Cloud}
.
These ideas include splitting data in brackets, sorting, Huffman coding and changing values.

Data Split using Brackets
.
This process organizes symbols in brackets. Each bracket is assigned a
{\sf{BracketID}}
and each symbol in the bracket is identified with a
{\sf{SymbolID}}
.
The brackets are pre-defined and unique for each value of
k
.
Table
I
gives an example of the bracket system where
k=4
, where each column represents the
{\sf{BracketID}}
and each row represents the
{\sf{SymbolID}}
of a the given symbol.
Using this table, the
{\sf Cloud}
transforms the received outsource
F^{\prime}
into two different strings:
1. A base
{B}
which includes the
{\sf{BracketID}}
of the symbols, sorted in ascending order, which is used for deduplication; and
2. An addendum
{A}
which includes the
{\sf{SymbolID}}
of the symbols, which is added to the cloud-side deviation.
In order to reduce the number of bracket IDs used we divide the aforementioned table into four zones.
Each zone is a sub-table of size
2{{}^{\frac{k}{4}}}
. At its core, this approach is a sub-categorization where rarely used
{\sf{BracketID}}
values are omitted and
the symbols are fitted into fewer Brackets.
This is detailed in the Change Values section.

Sorting
.
After performing the data split using brackets, the generated
{B}
is sorted.
A String
{S}
indicating the set of swaps performed to sort the
{B}
is stored in cloud-side deviation.
The
{B}
is stored in a forest data structure to ease the search process and further compress the bases.
In order to reverse the process of sorting, we store the required swaps (
{\sf{Swap}}
) needed to sort
{B}
.
Due to the split in brackets, the
{\sf Cloud}
can perform swaps for
2{{}^{\frac{k}{2}}}
values instead of
N=2^{k}
values, reducing the overhead and time complexity to store and find the required swaps.

Huffman Coding.

We leverage the fact that the
F^{\prime}
received by the
{\sf Cloud}
follows a certain probability distribution (ensured by the policy),
and use Huffman coding in order to reduce the expected size of the
{\sf{SymbolID}}
that the
{\sf Cloud}
needs to store
in the cloud-side deviation.
To generate the
{\sf{SymbolID}}
of the symbols based on their probability distribution,
the
{\sf Cloud}
creates a table with

\frac{k}{2}
columns, which represent the
{\sf{BracketID}}
of symbols.
Then, the
{\sf Cloud}
populates the table by putting the symbols with highest probability distribution in
the table first.

The way that the table is populated depends on the particular structure of the data.
In this work, we populate the table by filling the rows first.
An example is illustrated in Fig.
4

for
k=4
.
Other methods for filling the table are possible, e.g., columns first, with implications in compression and system performance.
By filling the rows first, the
{\sf Cloud}
optimizes the size of
{\sf{SymbolID}}
for the symbols,
as the symbols with higher probability are assigned the same
{\sf{SymbolID}}
.
However, as the frequent symbols are assigned to different
{\sf{BracketID}}
,
the average number of swaps would be high.
On the other hand, if the table is filled by columns first,
the expected size of
{\sf{SymbolID}}
is higher, but the average number of swaps is smaller.
The choice of the method and the optimal outcome for the compression rate heavily depends on the
structure and statistical characteristics of the files in
DB
.

After populating the table, the
{\sf Cloud}
treats each row as a variable, and
calculates the probability of each row, which is equal to the summation of the probability of the symbols in the given row.
Then, the
{\sf Cloud}
generates the Huffman code for the rows of the table,
and sets the value of the Huffman code as the
{\sf{SymbolID}}
of that row.
As an intuition, consider the same probability distribution as the example in Eq. (
6
).
In this case, the procedure of generating the
{\sf{BracketID}}
and
{\sf{SymbolID}}
of symbols is illustrated in Fig.
4
.

Change Values.
The
{\sf{SymbolID}}
for symbols with high probability distribution is significantly shorter than the
symbols with lower probability.
However, the size of
{\sf{SymbolID}}
for symbols with lower probability distribution is large, and therefore, these symbols have diminishing effect on the compression potential.
It is also beneficial to have as few
{\sf{BracketID}}
as possible to reduce the number of swaps in the sorting stage.
Thus, we propose using Change Value transformation (
{\sf ChngV}
) and describe it for
k=8
.
Using this transformation, the
{\sf Cloud}
changes the value of symbols with the lowest probability to symbols with the highest probability.
In order to perform this transformation, we divide the brackets table into 4 zones, as described in the Bracket stage.
Table.
II
shows an example for
k=8
.
In this table, we assume that the symbols are sorted based on their probability distribution,
where
p_{0}
denotes the symbol with the highest probability, followed by
p_{1}
, and so on, e.g.,

p_{255}
is the symbol with the lowest probability.
The
{\sf Cloud}
fills the table by filling the first zone, which is the zone on the top left corner,
followed by the second zone on the top right corner and then bottom left and bottom right.
In each zone, the symbols are put in their place using the rows first methods, as discussed in the Huffman coding section.
Then, the
{\sf Cloud}
assigns a a variable for each row of all four zones,
generating the
{\sf{SymbolID}}
for each row as discussed earlier.
Also, each zone is assigned a Value ID
{\sf{ValueID}}
.
The
{\sf{ValueID}}
for each zone is generated using the same idea of
{\sf{SymbolID}}
.

In the end of this procedure, each symbol has a position
[i,j]
in its zone,
an assigned
{\sf{BracketID}}
,
{\sf{SymbolID}}
and
{\sf{ValueID}}
.
Upon receiving an outsource
F^{\prime}
, the
{\sf Cloud}
first performs a change value operation.
For each symbol, the
{\sf Cloud}
finds the position of the symbol in its zone in the table,
Assuming the symbol is in position
[i,j]
of one of the zones,
the
{\sf Cloud}
change the value of
that symbol
to the value in the position
[i,j]
in the first zone (top left corner).
Then, the
{\sf Cloud}
stores the
{\sf{ValueID}}
for the symbols in a string
{C}
,
which is added to the cloud-side deviation.
The transformed string is stored and is used to create
{B}
and
{A}
as discussed before.
Using this technique, the data becomes more homogenous, reducing the potential number of
{\sf{BracketID}}
, and therefore reducing the expected number of swaps,
while also reducing the expected length of the
{\sf{SymbolID}}
for the symbols by eliminating the symbols with lower probability
distribution.
This improvement is achieved by only storing an overhead value of
{\sf{ValueID}}
for each symbol,
which is smaller for symbols with higher probabiltiy distribution.

In the next step, the cloud sorts

We use Mergesort

In order to reconstruct

In order to identify the required swaps,

[

,
Our algorithm does not sort

into
{B}

In detail,
the algorithm finds the first position

{B}[j]\neq{H}[j]

Then, the algorithm finds the first possible position

Client Compression Ratio

We begin our analysis with studying the compression ratio on the client side, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}_{\sf Client}
in Eq. (
1
).
For each file
F
, the user stores the respective
L
and
{\sf fid}
.
To compute
\mathcal{C}_{\sf Client}
for one file
F
, we accurately measure the size of
L
.
As the
{\sf Client}
uses a PRNG to identify the position of the deleted elements,
the required size to store the positions is equal to the size of the PRNG seed.
we denote this size by
s_{seed}
.
The
{\sf Client}
performs
\alpha=n_{F}-n_{F^{\prime}}
subsequent
{\sf{Del}}
on
F
,
where the
{\sf Client}
stores the value of all deleted symbols in
L
.
Thus,
L
contains a total of
\alpha\cdot k
bits to store the value of the deleted symbols.

L
also contains 1 bit, indicating whether the
{\sf{Inv}}
transformation has been applied to
F^{\prime}
.
Therefore,
the required storage needed in
{\sf Client}
is equal to:

{{\sf size}}({\sf Client}_{i},F)=s_{seed}+\alpha\cdot k+s_{{\sf fid}}+1,

where
s_{{\sf fid}}
is the size in bits of a file identifier.

If the database for the client
i
, i.e.,
DB_{i}
has
{\sf{\#}}F
files, the required storage on the
{\sf Client}
side is:

{{\sf size}}({\sf Client}_{i},DB_{i})={\sf{\#}}F\cdot(s_{seed}+\alpha\cdot k+s_{{\sf fid}}+1).

The original size required for
DB_{i}
prior to the transformations of Bonsai is:

|DB_{i}|={\sf{\#}}F\cdot k\cdot n_{o}.

Thus,

\mathcal{C}_{\sf Client}=\frac{{{\sf size}}({\sf Client}_{i},DB_{i})}{|DB_{i}|}=\frac{s_{seed}+\alpha\cdot k+s_{{\sf fid}}+1}{k\cdot n_{F}}.

V-B

Cloud Compression Ratio

The compression rate on the
{\sf Cloud}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}_{\sf Cloud}
, according to Eq. (
2
), considering the different contributing effects.
As described is Section
IV
, the cloud stores
three strings
{A}
,
{S}
, and
{C}
for each received
F^{\prime}
, as well as the identifier
{\sf fid}
and a pointer to the base
{B}
.
The bases are stored in
\mathcal{B}
, where we denote the size of
\mathcal{B}
as the size of the forest
s_{\mathcal{B}}
used to represent all stored bases
Note that
s_{\mathcal{B}}<n_{F^{\prime}}\cdot|\mathcal{B}|
, as the forest structure removes the redundancy between different bases in
\mathcal{B}
.
Thus, the total storage in the cloud is equal to the cost of storing
{A}
,
{S}
,
{C}
, the
{\sf fid}
and a pointer to the respective
{B}
for each received
F^{\prime}
plus the required storage for the
\mathcal{B}
.
Our experiments show that
s_{\mathcal{B}}
is very small compared to the size of the data
as the
{\sf Cloud}
is able to perform deduplication on a large number of bases.

The expected size of the
{A}
is equal to the expected length of Huffman Coding, which is equal to the entropy of the data in the
{\sf Cloud}
, i.e.,
H({\sf Cloud})
.
The size of
{S}
is dependent on the number of swaps needed to sort the base
{B}
.
For each
{\sf{Swap}}
, the cloud needs to store 1 position for the position of the swapped elements,
and each position requires
\log n_{F^{\prime}}
space.

{S}
also includes a bitmap of symbols, which has the size of
n_{F}
bits.
Therefore, the
{\sf Cloud}
requires
n_{F}+n_{swap}\cdot\log n_{F^{\prime}}
storage for
{S}
,
where
n_{swap}
is the number of swaps required to sort
{H}
.
The number of swaps
n_{swap}
is heavily dependant on the structure of the data and the
way the files are chunked from the database
DB
.
In
{C}
the
{\sf Cloud}
stores the Huffman representation of the zones, so the size of this string is
also dependent on the structure and the probability distribution of the data.
In worst case, the size of
{\sf{ValueID}}
for each symbol is exactly 2 bits, leading to
2n_{F}
required storage for
{C}
.
In the following analysis, we use this value.
In practice, the
{\sf Cloud}
uses Huffmann codes to determine the size of
{\sf{ValueID}}
,
so the size of
{\sf{ValueID}}
for most probable symbols is usually 1 bit,
leading to
lower rquired storage for use cases.

For each
F^{\prime}
, we have:

\begin{split}E({{\sf size}}({\sf Cloud},F))=&H(\mathcal{D^{\prime}})+3n_{F}+n_{swap}\cdot\log n_{F^{\prime}}+\\
&s_{{\sf fid}}+s_{p},\end{split}

where
s_{p}
is the size of the pointer or identifier to the
{B}
in
\mathcal{B}
.

Thus, the total compression rate on the cloud is

\footnotesize\mathcal{C}_{\sf Cloud}=\frac{s_{\mathcal{B}}+{\sf{\#}}F(H(\mathcal{D^{\prime}})+3n_{F}+n_{swap}\cdot\log n_{F^{\prime}}+s_{{\sf fid}}+s_{p})}{{\sf{\#}}F\cdot k\cdot n_{F}}.

(9)

V-C

Global Compression Ratio

The required storage size in
{\sf Client}
side does not change and can be calculated as
{{\sf size}}
(
{\sf Client}
,
DB_{i}
) in section
V-A
.
Therefore, the global compression ratio of the system is given by the sum of the
{\sf Client}
compression ratio and the
{\sf Cloud}
one

\footnotesize\mathcal{C}=\frac{s_{\mathcal{B}}+{\sf{\#}}F(c+H(\mathcal{D^{\prime}})+\alpha\cdot k+3n_{F}+n_{swap}\cdot\log n_{F^{\prime}})}{{\sf{\#}}F\cdot k\cdot n_{F}},

(10)

where
c
is a constant number, denoting the size of the pointers and the random seed of the PRNG, and is equal to
c=s_{seed}+s_{p}+2s_{{\sf fid}}+1
.

V-D

Transformation Costs

In this section, we calculate the computation cost of the algorithms discussed in Section
IV
in
{\sf Cloud}
and
{\sf Client}
.
We provide two theorems for the computational cost of operations in
{\sf Cloud}
and
{\sf Client}
.

Theorem 1

The computational cost of the procedures applied in the
{\sf Client}
during
{\sf{Upload}}
is
O(t\cdot n_{F})
.

Proof:

The
{\sf Client}
performs
{\sf{Del}}
for each file prior to upload, which is in the
{\sf{Upload}}
procedure.
The
{\sf Client}
performs two set of instructions in
{\sf{Upload}}
.
First, performing
{\sf{Del}}
for each file and PRNG seed.
Second, calculating the probability distribution of the generated
F^{\prime}
to choose the one that adheres the best with the distribution
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
provided in the
{\sf Policy}
.

Using a vector data structure for file
F
, results in linear complexity for deletion of
n_{F}-n_{F^{\prime}}
elements in the size of the
F
, i.e.,
O(n_{F})
. This can be performed by copying elements not scheduled for deletion to a different vector of size
n_{F^{\prime}}
, skipping the copy of deleted elements. Since the procedure is performed
t
times for
t
different seeds,
the total complexity of this instruction is
O(t\cdot n_{F})
.

For each generated
F^{\prime}
and its inverted
F^{\prime}
, the
{\sf Client}
must calculate the probability distribution. Calculating the probability distribution requires reading all the symbols, i.e.,
O(n_{F})
operations. As we have
2\cdot t
possible
F^{\prime}
to evaluate, this instruction has compelxity
O(t\cdot n_{F})
.
Thus, the complexity at the
{\sf Client}
is
O(t\cdot n_{F})
.
∎

Theorem 2

The computational cost of procedure in the
{\sf Cloud}
after receiving an
F^{\prime}
form a
{\sf Client}
during
{\sf{Deduplication}}
is equal to
O(n_{F^{\prime}}(\log n_{F^{\prime}}+N))
when using MergeSort for the Sorting phase.

Proof:

The cloud performs three sets of transformations on the received
F^{\prime}
, namely
{\sf ChngV}
,
{\sf{Seperation}}
and
{\sf{Sort}}
.
Therefore, in order to calculate the time complexity at the
{\sf Cloud}
after receiving an
F^{\prime}
, we calculate the time complexity of these transformations.

In
{\sf ChngV}
, the
{\sf Cloud}
needs to (1) find the symbols in the Brackets table and (2)change the value of the symbol and insert the new value, as well as
{\sf{ValueID}}
in the two output strings
{G}
and
{C}
.
The most efficient way to perform this transformation is to search for each symbol in the outsource
F^{\prime}
instead of searching for each symbol in the table.
Searching for a symbol has a time complexity of
O(n_{F})
.
As the
{\sf Cloud}
has to search for
N
symbols in total, this step has complexity
O(n_{F}\cdot N)
.
As we use vector data structure, the
{\sf Cloud}
can create the two output strings in linear time, i.e.,
O(n_{F^{\prime}})
.
Therefore, the total time complexity of
{\sf ChngV}
is equal to
O(n_{F^{\prime}}\cdot N)
.

During
{\sf{Seperation}}
, the cloud creates 2 strings
{A}
and
{H}
from
{G}
.
For each symbol, the
{\sf Cloud}
needs to find the respective value of
{\sf{SymbolID}}
and
{\sf{BracketID}}
from the Brackets Table.
This process requires a brute-force search, and therefore has a time complexity of
O(N)
.
Populating both strings is done in linear time complexity after finding the
{\sf{SymbolID}}
and
{\sf{BracketID}}
of each symbol.
Thus, the total time complexity of this step is equal to
O(n_{F^{\prime}}\cdot N)
.

The computational cost of the transformations applied in the
{\sf Cloud}
during
{\sf{Sort}}
depends on the sorting algorithm that is used.
In this work, we use Mergesort in order to sort the
{H}
, which has a time complexity of
O(n_{F^{\prime}}\log n_{F^{\prime}})
.
Then, the
{\sf Cloud}
finds the swaps required using a linear comparison between the sorted base
{B}
and
{H}
.
As this algorithm is linear, it has a time complexity of
O(n_{F^{\prime}})
.
Therefore, in total, the time complexity of
{\sf{Sort}}
is equal to
O(n_{F^{\prime}}\log n_{F^{\prime}})
.

Combining these three time complexities gives the final time complexity of the
{\sf{Deduplication}}
procedure.
∎

Theorem 3

The computational cost of the transformations applied during the client in
{\sf{Get}}
is
O(n_{F})
.

Proof:

The
{\sf Client}
needs to insert the deleted values in
L
into their respective positions in the
F^{\prime}
.
This is linear in cost with the size of
n_{F}
.
∎

Theorem 4

The computational cost of the transformations applied during the client in
{\sf{Deco}}
is
O({|\mathcal{B}|}+n_{F^{\prime}}\cdot N)
.

Proof:

The cloud performs
{\sf{Deco}}
in four steps. First, retrieving the
{B}
from
\mathcal{B}
. Second, undoing the
{\sf{Swap}}
transformations. Third, recreating the symbols from their respective
{\sf{BracketID}}
and
{\sf{SymbolID}}
. Lastly, restoring the changed values.

Retrieving the base from
\mathcal{B}
requires a search on the received
{\sf fid}
.
Although the bases are stored in a structured way, there is no guarantee that the
{\sf fid}
is stored in the same way.
Therefore, searching for a
{\sf fid}
requires linear time dependent on the number of bases in the baseset, i.e.,
O({|\mathcal{B}|})
.

After retrieving the
{B}
, the
{\sf Cloud}
needs to reverse the swaps stored in
{S}
to retrieve
{H}
by first identifying the required swaps. This is done by a read through
{S}
,
that has linear complexity, i.e.,
O(n_{F^{\prime}})
.
Then, the cloud performs the swaps.
In the worst case,the number of swaps is linear to the size of the
{B}
, i.e.,
O(n_{F^{\prime}})
.

Restoring the
F^{\prime}
from
{H}
requires replacing each triple of
{\sf{SymbolID}}
,
{\sf{BracketID}}
and
{\sf{ValueID}}
with its respective symbol.
Therefore, for each symbol in
{H}
, the
{\sf Cloud}
needs to scan through the Brackets table to find the value associated
with the triple. In the worst case scenario, the
{\sf Cloud}
needs to scan through the whole table for each symbol, leading
to a time complexity of
O(N)
for each symbol. Therefore,
in worst case, the time complexity of this action is equal to
O(n_{F^{\prime}}\cdot N)
.

Combining these three time complexities concludes the proof.
∎

V-E

Privacy Analysis

In the following, we calculate Bonsai’s leakage
\mathcal{L}
and uncertainty
\mathcal{U}
for an honest-but-curious
{\sf Cloud}
.
In detail, we consider
\mathcal{A}^{\sf week}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\sf strong}
as introduced in Section
III-C
, and for each adversary type, we investigate two scenarios. First, the adversary has negligible probability of breaking the PRNG. Second, the adversary breaks the PRNG.

V-E
1
Weak Adversary

Recall that
\mathcal{A}^{\sf week}
has no information about the distribution of original files. This means that in its perspective, all possible values of
F
have the same probability of being the
{\sf Client}
’s data.
Therefore,
H(F)=k\cdot n_{F}
.
In scenario 1, the PRNG behaves like a truly random function in
\mathcal{A}^{\sf week}
’s view, and therefore it leaks no information about the position of the deleted elements.
Given
F^{\prime}
, the original string
F
can be any of the possible strings that generate
F^{\prime}
. As
\mathcal{A}^{\sf week}
does not have any extra information, each potential
F
has the same probability of being the original string, so
H(F|F^{\prime})=log(m)
, where
m
is the number of possible pre-images of
F^{\prime}
.
as it has been shown in
[
10
]
, we have:

m=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{F}-n_{F^{\prime}}}{n_{F}\choose j+n_{F^{\prime}}}(2^{k}-1)^{n_{F}-n_{F^{\prime}}-j}.

Therefore, the leakage for
\mathcal{A}^{\sf week}
is

\mathcal{L}(F^{\prime})=\frac{k\cdot n_{F}-\log(m)}{k\cdot n_{F}},

and the uncertainty
\mathcal{U}
that the
\mathcal{A}^{\sf week}
faces after receiving
F^{\prime}
is

\mathcal{U}=\log(m).

In scenario 2,
\mathcal{A}^{\sf week}
breaks the PRNG, this means that it gains knowledge about the position of the deleted symbols.
Therefore, the total number of possible pre-images of the
F^{\prime}
,
m
has a different value.
However, the adversary still cannot distinguish between the different possible pre-images, as in the adversary’s viewpoint,
all possible pre-images has the same probability of being the original data.
In order to generate a possible pre-image, the adversary can insert a symbol in the positions that the deletions has occurred.
As there are
n_{F}-n_{F^{\prime}}
positions and each position has
2^{k}
possible values as the symbol,
the number of pre-images of the
F^{\prime}
is equal to:

m=2^{k(n_{F}-n_{F^{\prime}})}.

The rest of the analysis is the same as the previous case, therefore, if the
\mathcal{A}^{\sf week}
breaks the PRNG, we have the following value of leakage after receiving the
F^{\prime}
:

\mathcal{L}(F^{\prime})=\frac{k\cdot n_{F}-\log(m)}{k\cdot n_{F}}=\frac{n_{F^{\prime}}}{n_{F}}.

The Uncertainty for the attacker in this case is equal to:

\mathcal{U}=\log(m)=k(n_{F}-n_{F^{\prime}}).

V-E
2
Strong Adversary

Recall that
\mathcal{A}^{\sf strong}
has knowledge about the probability distribution of symbols in the client.
We further assume here that the adversary has a negligible probability of breaking the PRNG, i.e., has no information about the position of the deleted symbols.
In this case,
H(F)\neq k\cdot n_{F}
.
When the cloud receives
F^{\prime}
, the original string
F
can be any of the possible strings that generate
F^{\prime}
. The cloud can generate all possible values of
F
.
The probability that a given
\mathcal{F}
is the actual data of the
{\sf Client}
is higher if 1.
\mathcal{F}
has higher probability based on
\mathcal{D}
and 2.
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
can be generated by
\mathcal{F}
with various deletions. We define
W(F=\mathcal{F}|F^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
is the number of ways that
F^{\prime}
can be generated from
F
, i.e., number of distinct occurrences of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{F}
as a subsequence. This value is calculated using a recursive algorithm and dynamic programming.
Using this variable, the probability of each
F
in cloud’s perspective is equal to:

P(F=\mathcal{F}|F^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime})=\frac{W(F=\mathcal{F}|F^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime})P(F=\mathcal{F})}{\sum\limits_{\mathcal{F}}W(F=\mathcal{F}|F^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime})P(F=\mathcal{F})}.

Using the value of
P(F=\mathcal{F}|F^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
, we calculate the value for
H(F|F^{\prime})
which is equal to the uncertainty of the
\mathcal{A}^{\sf strong}
after it receives
F^{\prime}
. The leakage of information to
\mathcal{A}^{\sf strong}
is calculated using the value of
\mathcal{U}
and Eq. (
5
).

If the adversary breaks the PRNG, this analysis still holds, and we need to calculate the value of
P(F=\mathcal{F}|F^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
for each possible pre-image.
However, the difference between the two cases is the more information that the adversary possesses if it breaks the PRNG means that the number of possible pre-images is lower,
therefore, after receiving the
F^{\prime}
, on average more information is leaked to the
{\sf Cloud}
.

The uncertainty provided against a strong adversary
is upper bounded by the uncertainty of the weak adversary,
i.e.,
k(n_{F}-n_{F^{\prime}})
.
This fact is useful to argue for privacy against a real adversary,
which lies somewhere between the weak and strong adversaries.
In Section
VI
we calculate the values of leakage and uncertainty
against weak and strong adversaries and
discuss the implications of it in real life scenarios.

VII
Conclusion

This paper proposes Bonsai, a privacy-aware dual deduplication system that preserve the privacy of
{\sf Client}
’s data while achieving attractive compression for cloud storage providers.
Similarly to Yggrasil
[
10
]
, Bonsai’s privacy relies on deletions of symbols from the original data; however, Bonsai achieves significantly better compression rates on the client side by replacing random deletion with deletion on locations indicated by a PRNG. This behavior mimics a deletion channel.
We implement a filtering method using multiple seeds of a PRNG function to ensure that the generated base can be deduplicated in the cloud with higher probability.

To improve its compression capabilities and enable efficient search, the
{\sf Cloud}
in Bonsai periodically generates a
{\sf Policy}
, which tells to the
{\sf Clients}
the expected probability distribution of symbols in the outsourced data.
The
{\sf Client}
uses the
{\sf Policy}
to generate deduplication-friendly data to outsource to the
{\sf Cloud}
.
Bonsai implements an innovative mapping function in the
{\sf Cloud}
combined with a forest data structure in order to
efficiently find and deduplicate incoming data.
Our experiments show that Bonsai achieves significant compression rates compared to state-of-the-art (generalized) deduplication approaches.

In addition, Bonsai protects the confidentiality of
{\sf Clients}
’ data thanks to a deletion-channel-like approach. We argue the proposed approach provides good privacy guarantees: our analysis shows that an attacker faces a high degree of uncertainty when trying to reconstruct
{\sf Clients}
’ original data from what is outsourced.
In real-life scenarios,
The strongest possible adversary faces an average 83.40 Shannon bits of entropy while trying to guess the original data,
even if the adversary has full knowledge about
the characteristics and statistics of the original data source.
We further show that
there is only 50%
chance that the adversary can guess the correct original data
from the received outsourced data,
by checking
2^{15}
possible strings for each chunk of data.
Even if the adversary has the possbility to check
2^{32}

possible strings,
The chance of guessing the correct file for each chunk is still less than 90%.

Future work will study additional mechanisms for Bonsai to improve compression, e.g., using more compact representations for the sorting step, improve privacy, e.g., combine random deletions with value changes or insertions at the
{\sf Client}
side, and efficient secure sharing mechanisms with third parties.
