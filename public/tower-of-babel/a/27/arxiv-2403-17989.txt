Study guide for “On restricted projections to planes in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} ”
Tainara Borges, Siddharth Mulherkar and Tongou Yang

Abstract
. This article is a study guide for “ On restricted projections to planes in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} ” by Gan, Guo, Guth, Harris, Maldague and Wang [ GGG+22 ] . We first present the main problems and preliminaries related to restricted projections in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . Then we introduce the high-low method and decoupling, which are the two central and novel ideas in their proofs. We hope to provide as many details as possible so that this study guide is self-contained, with the only exception of the Bourgain-Demeter decoupling inequality for curves in the appendix.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Marstrand’s and Mattila’s projection theorems

1.1.1.
Dimension preserving property in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, given a direction
u\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
on the unit circle, consider the orthogonal projection
p_{u}(x):=x\cdot u
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For each Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we consider the size of the image
p_{u}(A)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
relative to the size of
A
. In particular, for each
u
we ask if it is
dimension preserving
, that is, whether we have
1
1

1

Here and throughout this article,
\dim
will always denote the Hausdorff dimension.

(1.1)

\dim p_{u}(A)=\min\{1,\dim A\}.

Note that
\leq
in (
1.1
) is trivial since the range is a subset of
\mathbb{R}
and a projection cannot increase dimensions. The celebrated Marstrand’s projection theorem
[
Mar54
]
states that
\sigma^{1}
-a.e.
u\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
is dimension preserving for
A
, where
\sigma^{1}
denotes the standard surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. For this reason, we say a direction
u
is
exceptional
if it is not dimension preserving, that is, we have
<
holds in (
1.1
). Lastly, we remark that the set of exceptional directions naturally depend on the choice of
A
; for instance, if
A
is a straight line with direction
v\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, then
u
is dimension preserving for
A
if and only if
u\cdot v\neq 0
.

1.1.2.
Exceptional set estimate in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

In many cases, we can prove stronger results than dimension preserving properties, known as exceptional set estimates. Given a Borel
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For each
0<s<\min\{1,\dim A\}
, we consider the set of exceptional directions

(1.2)

E_{s}:=\{u\in\mathbb{S}^{1}:\dim p_{u}(A)<s\}.

If we can show that
\dim E_{s}<1
, then Marstrand’s theorem follows as a corollary. In fact, Kaufman
[
Kau68
]
and Falconer
[
Fal82
]
established the bound
\dim E_{s}\leq s
and
\dim E_{s}\leq\max\{0,1+s-\dim A\}
, respectively. For
\dim A<1
Kaufman’s estimate is better, while for
\dim A>1
Falconer’s estimate is better. Recently, Ren and Wang
[
RW23
]
showed that we always have
\dim E_{s}\leq\max\{0,2s-\dim A\}
, which is better than both Kaufman’s and Falconer’s results. Moreover, a simple example given by Kaufman and Mattila
[
KM75
]
shows that this is sharp.

1.1.3.
Dimension preserving property in higher dimensions

Now we generalize to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
. For each
1\leq k\leq d-1
, consider the set
G(d,k)
of all
k
-dimensional subspaces equipped with the standard Haar measure
\sigma(d,k)
. Mattila
[
Mat75
]
generalized Marstrand’s theorem
[
Mar54
]
to higher dimensions. More precisely, given
1\leq k\leq d-1
and a subspace
S\in G(d,k)
, consider the projection
p_{S}
from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
onto
S
. Then for every Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

(1.3)

\dim p_{S}(A)=\min\{k,\dim A\},\quad\text{for $\sigma(d,k)$-a.e. $S\in G(d,k)$}.

One may also consider exceptional estimates in higher dimensions; however, obtaining a sharp exceptional estimates will be much more difficult than in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and so we will not discuss this topic here.

1.2.
Restricted projection theorems in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

1.2.1.
Background

Now we consider
d=3
and
k=1,2
. For
k=1
we are considering projection to lines, namely, for each
e\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
we consider the function
p_{e}(x):=x\cdot e
. For
k=2
we are considering projection to planes, namely, for each
e\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
we consider the function
\pi_{e}(x):=x-(x\cdot e)e
.

Denote by
\sigma^{2}
the standard surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Mattila’s theorem implies that given any Borel
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
, for
\sigma^{2}
-a.e.
e\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
we have both

(1.4)

\dim p_{e}(A)=\min\{1,\dim A\},\quad\dim\pi_{e}(A)=\min\{2,\dim A\}.

However, this provides no information if we would like to study the projections of
A
along a subset
N\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{2}
with zero
\sigma^{2}
measure, most typically when
N
is the image of a continuous curve on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Nevertheless, this problem is still interesting, and we may ask the following main question: if
\gamma(\theta):[0,1]\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a continuous curve, and
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a Borel set, is it true that for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
\theta\in[0,1]
we have either equality below:

(1.5)

\dim p_{\gamma(\theta)}(A)=\min\{1,\dim A\},\quad\dim\pi_{\gamma(\theta)}(A)=\min\{2,\dim A\}?

The simple answer is no. In fact, if we take the image of
\gamma
to be the equator and
A
to be the straight line passing through the north and south poles, then the former equality of (
1.5
) fails to hold for any
\theta\in[0,1]
. Similarly, if we take the image of
\gamma
to be the equator and
A
a
2
-dimensional cube contained in the Equator plane, say
A=[-1,1]^{2}\times\{0\}
then the latter equality of (
1.5
) fails to hold for any
\theta\in[0,1]
.

However, this triviality can be easily avoided once we require that the image of
\gamma
“escapes from being a great circle”. More precisely, we require that
\gamma
be
C^{2}
, and that the
following non-degeneracy condition hold:

(1.6)

\det(\gamma,\gamma',\gamma'')(\theta)\neq 0,\quad\forall\,\theta\in[0,1].

Geometrically, this means that
\gamma
is the velocity of a curve with nonzero torsion. A model case of
\gamma
is a non-great circle which generates the standard light cone, that is,

(1.7)

\gamma(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\cos\sqrt{2}\,\theta,\sin\sqrt{2}\,\theta,1\right).

The factor
\sqrt{2}
here is to normalize so that
|\gamma^{\prime}|=1
as well. We remark here that an interesting property of the standard light cone is that
\gamma
and
\gamma\times\gamma^{\prime}
are two different parametrizations of the same circle, since as one can easily check
(\gamma\times\gamma^{\prime})(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-\cos\sqrt{2}\theta,-\sin\sqrt{2}\theta,1))
. This fails for a general non-degenerate curve
\gamma
.

1.2.2.
Literature review

With the non-degeneracy condition (
1.6
), Fässler and Orponen
[
FO14
]
put forward the following conjecture:

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
be
C^{2}
and satisfies the non-degeneracy condition (
1.6
). Then for every Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have

(1.8)

\dim p_{\gamma(\theta)}(A)=\min\{1,\dim A\},\quad\dim\pi_{\gamma(\theta)}(A)=\min\{2,\dim A\}

both hold for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
\theta\in[0,1]
.

In
[
FO14
]
, the authors mentioned that the former equality in (
1.8
) is easy when
\dim A\leq 1/2
, and the latter equality in (
1.8
) is easy when
\dim A\leq 1
(see
[
JJLL08
]
). In the same article, they were able to prove something a little stronger. More precisely, if
\dim A>1/2
, they showed there is some
\sigma>1/2
depending on
\dim A
such that
\dim p_{\gamma(\theta)}>\sigma
for a.e.
\theta
. If
\dim A>1
, then they showed there is some
\sigma>1
depending on
\dim A
such that
\dim\pi_{\gamma(\theta)}>\sigma
for a.e.
\theta
.

Since
[
FO14
]
, a dozens of partial results have been established towards the main conjecture, see for instance
[
JJK14
,
OO15
,
OV18
,
KOV22
,
Har22
]
. The most significant result among them is
[
KOV22
]
, which is the first to establish the former equality in (
1.8
) when
\gamma
is given by a non-great circle such as (
1.7
). Their main technique is to use Wolff’s
[
Wol00
]
incidence estimates on tangency of circles, which relies in turn on the algebraic property of the circle. Therefore, one major downside of this method is that it fails to work in our case when
\gamma
is merely
C^{2}
. We also remark here that
[
KOV22
]
established the following exceptional set estimate: for every
0<s<\min\{1,\dim A\}
, we have

(1.9)

\dim E_{s}\leq\frac{s+1}{2},

where the exceptional set
E_{s}
is given by

(1.10)

E_{s}:=\{\theta\in[0,1]:\dim p_{\gamma(\theta)}(A)<s\}.

The full proof of Conjecture
1.1
is given by Pramanik-Yang-Zahl
[
PYZ22
]
, Gan-Guth-Maldague
[
GGM22
]
and Gan-Guo-Guth-Harris-Maldague-Wang
[
GGG+22
]
. Among them,
[
PYZ22
]
and
[
GGM22
]
independently proved the former equality of (
1.1
), using very different methods, and obtaining different exceptional set estimates. More precisely,
[
PYZ22
]
proved a Kaufman type exceptional set estimate, namely,

(1.11)

\dim E_{s}\leq s,\quad\forall\,\,0<s<\min\{1,\dim A\}.

In contrast,
[
GGM22
]
proved a Falconer type exceptional set estimate, namely,

(1.12)

\dim E_{s}\leq\max\{0,1+s-\dim A\},\quad\forall\,\,0<s<\min\{1,\dim A\}.

As mentioned right after (
1.2
), neither estimate is strictly better than the other.

We remark that
[
Har23
]
also gives an analogue of Theorem
1.5
below in the case of projection to lines in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

[
GGG+22
]
proved the latter equality of (
1.1
) via a Falconer type exceptional set estimate (see Theorem
1.2
below).
This article is devoted to the understanding of
[
GGG+22
]
.

1.3.
Main results

We now focus solely on the paper
[
GGG+22
]
.

Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
be a
C^{2}
curve. We assume it satisfies the non-degeneracy condition in (
1.6
). We may also assume without loss of generality that
|\gamma^{\prime}|=1
, whence

(1.13)

\mathbf{e}_{1}:=\gamma,\quad\mathbf{e}_{2}:=\gamma^{\prime},\quad\mathbf{e}_{3}:=\gamma\times\gamma^{\prime}

form an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{R}^{3}
known as a Frenet frame.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem 1 in
[
GGG+22
]
)
.

Suppose
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a Borel set of Hausdorff dimension
\alpha\in(0,3]
. For
0<s<\min\{2,\alpha\}
, define the exceptional set

E_{s}=\{\theta\in[0,1]\colon\textnormal{dim}(\pi_{\theta}(A))<s\}

Then we have

\textnormal{dim}(E_{s})\leq\max\{1+s-\alpha,0\}

The proof of Theorem
1.2
will be given in Section
4.3
.

Theorem
1.2
gives the following corollary, which solves Conjecture
1.1
.

Corollary 1.3

(Corollary 1 in
[
GGG+22
]
)
.

Suppose
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a Borel set of Hausdorff dimension
\alpha
. Then we have

\textnormal{dim}(\pi_{\theta}(A))=\min\{2,\alpha\},\,\text{for a.e. }\theta\in[0,1]

Proof.

There are two possible cases:
\textnormal{dim}(A)=\alpha\leq 2
or
\textnormal{dim}(A)=\alpha>2
. In the first case, take
s_{n}=\alpha-1/n
. Theorem
1.2
gives

\textnormal{dim}(E_{s_{n}})\leq 1-1/n
. In particular,

\mathcal{L}^{1}(E_{s_{n}})=0

Since

E_{\alpha}=\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{s_{n}}

it also follows that
\mathcal{L}^{1}(E_{\alpha})=0
.
The case
\textnormal{dim}(A)>2
is similar, by taking
s_{n}=2-1/n
and
E_{2}=\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{s_{n}}
.
∎

Theorem 1.4

(Theorem 2 in
[
GGG+22
]
)
.

Suppose
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a Borel set of Hausdorff dimension greater than
2
. Then
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\pi_{\theta}(A))>0
for a.e.
\theta\in[0,1]
.

1.4.
Reduction of Theorem 2 to Theorem 8

We first state the following theorem, from which Theorem
1.4
follows as a corollary.

Theorem 1.5

(Theorem 8 in
[
GGG+22
]
)
.

If
a\in(2,3]
and
\mu
is a compactly supported Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that

(1.14)

\rho_{a}(\mu):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},r\in(0,1)}r^{-a}\mu(B^{3}(x,r))<\infty,

then
\pi_{\theta\#}\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{2}
, for a.e.
\theta\in[0,1]
. Here and throughout this article,
\pi_{\theta\#}\mu
denotes the standard push-forward measure, namely, for every Borel set
A\subseteq\pi_{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

(1.15)

\pi_{\theta\#}\mu(A):=\mu(\pi_{\theta}^{-1}(A)).

The proof of Theorem
1.5
will be given in Section
7
. We also remark that since
\mu
is compactly supported, we may actually regard
\mathcal{H}^{2}
as supported on the unit ball of
\pi_{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, for all
\theta\in[0,1]
. Also, Theorem
1.5
actually gives that
\pi_{\theta\#}\mu
can be identified with an integrable function on
\pi_{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for a.e.
\theta
.

We now prove Theorem
1.4
assuming Theorem
1.5
. For this purpose, we recall the preliminary concept of Frostman measure.

Definition 1.6

(Frostman measure)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set. A finite nonzero measure
\mu
supported in
A
is said to be an
a
-Frostman measure in
A
if
\rho_{a}(\mu)<\infty
(see (
1.14
) above).

We state Frostman’s Lemma below, where
\mathcal{H}^{a}
stands for the
a
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 1.7

(Frostman’s lemma, Theorem 2.7 in
[
Mat15
]
)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set. Then
A
admits an
a
-Frostman measure if and only if
\mathcal{H}^{a}(A)\in(0,\infty]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
assuming Theorem
1.5
.

We may of course assume
A\subseteq[-1,1]^{3}
. Since
\dim A\in(2,3]
, we have
\mathcal{H}^{a}(A)>0
where
a:=\frac{\dim A+2}{2}\in(2,\dim A)
(indeed, since
\mathcal{H}^{a}(A)=\infty
for such
a
). By Theorem
1.7
, we can find an
a
-Frostman measure
\mu
supported in
A
. By Theorem
1.5
, we have
\pi_{\theta\#}\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{2}
for a.e.
\theta\in[0,1]
.

For every such
\theta
, we claim that
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\pi_{\theta}(A))>0
. Otherwise, by definition of absolute continuity, we have
\pi_{\theta\#}\mu(\pi_{\theta}(A))=0
, that is,
\mu(\pi_{\theta}^{-1}(\pi_{\theta}(A)))=0
. Since
A\subseteq\pi_{\theta}^{-1}(\pi_{\theta}(A))
, this implies
\mu(A)=0
, a contradiction.
∎

1.5.
Preliminaries about
C^{2}
curves

Define

(1.16)

\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)=\gamma(\theta),\,\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)=\gamma^{\prime}(\theta),\,\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta)=\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)\times\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta).

Then for any
\theta\in[0,1]
,
\{\mathbf{e}_{i}(\theta)\colon i=1,2,3\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Lemma 1.8
.

We have the following identities:

(1.17)

\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathbf{e}_{2},\quad\mathbf{e}_{2}^{\prime}=-\mathbf{e}_{1}+\uptau\mathbf{e}_{3},\quad\mathbf{e}_{3}^{\prime}=-\uptau\mathbf{e}_{2},

where we denote

\uptau=\det(\gamma,\gamma',\gamma'')
.

Proof.

This is just a standard consequence of a Frenet frame with curvature
1
and torsion
\uptau
, but for completeness we also give a simple proof here. The first relation is just the definition. To prove the second relation, we will need the relation

(1.18)

\gamma\cdot\gamma^{\prime\prime}=-1,

which follows from differentiating
\gamma\cdot\gamma^{\prime}=0
and using
|\gamma^{\prime}|=1
. Now write

(1.19)

\mathbf{e}_{2}^{\prime}=\gamma^{\prime\prime}:=a_{1}\gamma+a_{2}\gamma^{\prime}+a_{3}\gamma^{\prime}\times\gamma^{\prime},

and using (
1.18
) and the fact that
\{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime},\gamma\times\gamma^{\prime}\}
forms an orthonormal basis, we get
a_{1}=-1
,
a_{2}=0
, and

(1.20)

a_{3}=\gamma^{\prime\prime}\cdot(\gamma\times\gamma^{\prime})=\det(\gamma,\gamma',\gamma'')=\uptau.

Similarly, we note that

(1.21)

\mathbf{e}_{3}^{\prime}=(\gamma\times\gamma^{\prime})^{\prime}=\gamma\times\gamma^{\prime\prime}.

Write

(1.22)

\gamma\times\gamma^{\prime\prime}=b_{1}\gamma+b_{2}\gamma^{\prime}+b_{3}\gamma\times\gamma^{\prime},

and using the fact that
\{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime},\gamma\times\gamma^{\prime}\}
forms an orthonormal basis, we get
b_{1}=0
and
b_{2}=-\uptau
. To find
b_{3}
, taking dot products with
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
on both sides of (
1.22
) and using
\gamma^{\prime}\cdot\gamma^{\prime\prime}=0
which comes from differentiating
|\gamma^{\prime}|^{2}=1
, we get
b_{3}\uptau=0
, and so
b_{3}=0
since
\uptau\neq 0
.

∎

The following slightly more generalized Taylor expansion theorem will be used throughout the article to deal with low regularity issues. In words, with the assumption below, we can pretend that
f
was a
C^{2}
function when we do Taylor expansions.

Proposition 1.9
.

Let
f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
C^{1}
function such that
f^{\prime}=gh
where
g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is
C^{1}
,
g(a)=0
and
h:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded. Then we have the estimate

(1.23)

|f(b)-f(a)|\leq\frac{1}{2}(b-a)^{2}\norm*{h}_{\infty}\norm*{g'}_{\infty}.

Proof.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus, we have

\displaystyle|f(b)-f(a)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}(b-a)f^{\prime}((1-t)a+tb)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq(b-a)\int_{0}^{1}\left|g((1-t)a+tb)h((1-t)a+tb)\right|dt

\displaystyle\leq(b-a)\norm*{h}_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\left|g((1-t)a+tb)\right|dt.

For each
t\in[0,1]
, we can use the fundamental theorem of calculus again to get

\displaystyle|g((1-t)a+tb)|

\displaystyle=|g((1-t)a+tb)-g(a)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}t(b-a)g^{\prime}((1-s)a+s((1-t)a+tb))ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\norm*{g'}_{\infty}|t(b-a)|ds

\displaystyle=t(b-a)\norm*{g'}_{\infty}.

The result then follows.
∎

We also need the following elementary proposition.

Proposition 1.10
.

If
|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\leq\delta^{1/2}
, then we have

(1.24)

\displaystyle|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\theta)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-1|

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta,\quad\forall i=1,2,3,

(1.25)

\displaystyle|\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{j}(\theta^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{1/2},\quad\forall j=1,3,

(1.26)

\displaystyle|\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta.

Proof.

The estimate (
1.25
) is easy, since each
\mathbf{e}_{i}(\theta)
is a Lipschitz function, and
\{\mathbf{e}_{i}(\theta):1\leq i\leq 3\}
is an orthonormal basis for each
\theta
. The inequality (
1.26
) follows from Taylor expanding
\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)
with respect to the centre
\theta^{\prime}
and using that
\{\mathbf{e}_{i}(\theta^{\prime}):1\leq i\leq 3\}
is an orthonormal basis.

To prove (
1.24
) for
i=1
, we note that it follows directly from Lemma
1.8
and Taylor expansion. For
i=3
we need to be slightly more careful since
\mathbf{e}_{3}
is merely
C^{1}
.
Take
f(t)=\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}(t)
and observe that by Lemma
1.8
one has
f^{\prime}(t)=-\tau(t)\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(t)=-\tau(t)g(t)
, for
g(t)=\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(t)
. Since
g(\theta)=0
and
f(\theta)=1
, Proposition
1.9
gives
|\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta)-1|\lesssim|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|^{2}\lesssim\delta
.

It remains to prove the inequality for
i=2
. The idea is similar to the proof of Proposition
1.9
: we compute, using Lemma
1.8
,

\displaystyle|\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)-1|

\displaystyle=|\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)-\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{e}^{\prime}_{2}((1-t)\theta+t\theta^{\prime})(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\theta^{\prime}-\theta|\int_{0}^{1}\left|\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}((1-t)\theta+t\theta^{\prime})\right|dt

\displaystyle+|\theta^{\prime}-\theta|\int_{0}^{1}\left|\uptau((1-t)\theta+t\theta^{\prime})\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}((1-t)\theta+t\theta^{\prime})\right|dt.

Using
|\uptau|\sim 1
and applying the already proved inequality (
1.25
) to control the inner products left inside the integrals by
\delta^{1/2}
, we are done.
∎

1.6.
Notation

(1)

\lessapprox
and
\gtrapprox
will mean up to logarithmic losses. More precisely, for two nonnegative functions
A(\delta),B(\delta)
, we use
A(\delta)\lessapprox B(\delta)
to mean the following: there exists some constant
\alpha>0
and
C>1
such that

(1.27)

A(\delta)\leq C|\log\delta|^{\alpha}B(\delta),\quad\forall\delta\in(0,1/2].

We can define
\gtrapprox
in a similar way.

(2)

We say a collection
\mathcal{C}
of sets is
A
-overlapping if
\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}1_{C}\leq A
.

(3)

Unless otherwise specified, all implicit constants in this article are allowed to depend on the curve
\gamma
.

(4)

For a family of subsets

Theorem 4.3

(Marstrand’s projection theorem)

5.5.
Analysis of low part

For the low part, we do not have orthogonality to aid us. Instead, we use the

5.7.1.

To use Theorem

Strictly speaking, the object we need to cover is not

Lemma 5.5

We have the following observations.

(2)

If

The proof is given in Section
5.11
after the main proof.

Let

\delta^{1/2}
-lattice contained in
[0,1]

5.5
immediately implies the following.

Lemma 5.6

The enlarged planks

. More precisely, for each

Moreover, the planks

5.10.
Proof of dyadic pigeonholing

We now prove the statement leading to (

we denote the

-lattice contained in

. For each

Let

Then it is easy to see that

Now by the pigeonholing principle, there exists some

\sum_{j=1}^{J}2^{j}\#\Omega_{j}\leq J2^{i}\#\Omega_{i}.

This gives

which is as required once we take

, since

Theorem 6.1

(Theorem 7 in
[
GGG+22
]
)
.

Fix
0\leq a\leq 2
. For each
\varepsilon>0
and each
0<c\leq 1
, there exists a constant
b(\varepsilon,a,c)>0
so that the following holds. Let
\delta>0
. Let
\mu
be a finite nonzero Borel measure supported in the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\rho_{a}(\mu)<\infty
(recall (
1.14

Let
\Theta
be a maximally
\delta
-separated subset of
[0,1]
. For each
\theta\in\Theta
, let
\mathbb{T}_{\theta}
be a collection of disjoint
\delta\times\delta\times 1

\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}1_{T}(x)\geq c\delta^{\varepsilon-1},\quad\forall x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu).

The main argument is a high-low decomposition, combined with an induction on scales argument. Decoupling is also used in the analysis of high part. Note that we have rescaled the physical space back to the unit ball, and so the frequency scale will be in

6.1.
A bootstrap inequality

To facilitate the induction, we first prove the following bootstrap inequality.

Lemma 6.2
.

With the statement of Theorem
6.1
, let
M(\delta,\varepsilon,c)=M_{a}(\delta,\varepsilon,c)
be the smallest constant for which

(6.3)

M(\delta,\varepsilon,c)\#\mathbb{T}\geq\mu(\mathbb{R}^{3})\rho_{a}(\mu)^{-1}\delta^{-1-a}.

Then there exists a small
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\varepsilon,a,c)\in(0,1)
such that for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
we have

(6.4)

M(\delta,\varepsilon,c)\leq\delta^{-2\varepsilon}M\left(\delta^{1-\sqrt{\varepsilon}},\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\sqrt{\varepsilon}},c_{0}c\right)+c^{-4}A(\varepsilon)\delta^{-10\sqrt{\varepsilon}},

where
c_{0}\in(0,1)
is an absolute constant and
A(\varepsilon)
is decreasing in
\varepsilon
.

Remark.
By (
6.1
) we see that
c\delta^{\varepsilon-1}\leq\#\Theta
so

(6.5)

M(\delta,\varepsilon,c)\leq c^{-1}\mu(\mathbb{R}^{3})\rho_{a}(\mu)^{-1}\delta^{-a-\varepsilon},

which is in particular less than
\delta^{-3}
for
\delta
small enough.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
assuming Lemma
6.2
.

The base cases when
\delta\geq\delta_{0}
or
\varepsilon>1/2
are trivial, so it suffices to assume
\delta<\delta_{0}
and
\varepsilon<1/2
and iterate the bootstrap inequality (
6.4
) in Lemma
6.2
.

Let
\delta<\delta_{0}
. We define three sequences
\delta_{n},\varepsilon_{n},c_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
as follows:

(6.6)

\displaystyle\delta_{1}:=\delta,\quad\varepsilon_{1}:=\varepsilon,\,c_{1}=c

(6.7)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n+1}:=\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{1-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}}},

(6.8)

\displaystyle\delta_{n+1}:=\delta_{n}^{1-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}}},

(6.9)

\displaystyle c_{n+1}:=c_{0}^{n}c.

Then for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\delta_{N}<\delta_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{N}<1/2
, we have, by direct computation

(6.10)

M(\delta,\varepsilon,c)\leq\delta^{-2N\varepsilon}M(\delta_{N+1},\varepsilon_{N+1},c_{N+1})+\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}^{-4}A(\varepsilon_{n})\delta^{-2(n-1)\varepsilon}\delta_{n}^{-10{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}}}}.

It is easy to see that
\varepsilon_{n}
is increasing and bounded from below by
\varepsilon
. Also, it diverges to
\infty
. By the definition of the
\delta_{n}
, we have

\delta_{n+1}=\delta_{n}^{\varepsilon_{n}/\varepsilon_{n+1}}=(\delta_{n-1})^{\frac{\varepsilon_{n-1}}{\varepsilon_{n}}\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n+1}}}=\delta_{n-1}^{\frac{\varepsilon_{n-1}}{\varepsilon_{n+1}}},

and by iterating this a few times

\delta_{n}=\delta^{\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_{n}}}
.
Therefore

(6.11)

\delta_{n}^{-10\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}}}=\delta^{-10\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}}}}\leq\delta^{-10\sqrt{\varepsilon}}.

If we let
N
be the smallest integer such that
\varepsilon_{N+1}\geq 1/2>\varepsilon_{N}
, then we have the following claim:

(6.12)

N\leq 5\varepsilon^{-1/2}.

Assuming this, using
M(\delta_{N+1},\varepsilon_{N+1},c_{N+1})\lesssim 1
and inequality (
6.11
), we can estimate from (
6.10
) that

\displaystyle M(\delta,\varepsilon,c)

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{-10\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{N}(c_{0}^{n-1}c)^{-4}A(\varepsilon_{n})\delta^{-10\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{-10\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left(1+N(c_{0}^{N-1}c)^{-4}A(\varepsilon)\delta^{-10\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\varepsilon,c}\delta^{-100\sqrt{\varepsilon}},

which proves Theorem
6.1
. It remains to prove (
6.12
). Write
x_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}
, so we have the iterative formula

(6.13)

x_{n+1}=x_{n}-\sqrt{x_{n}}.

Since
\varepsilon_{N+1}=\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{1-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{N}}}<\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2(\sqrt{2}-1)}
, one has
x_{N+1}=\varepsilon_{N+1}^{-1}>2-\sqrt{2}>1
.

Thus
2<x_{n}\leq\varepsilon^{-1}
for every
1\leq n\leq N
,
x_{N+1}\in(2-\sqrt{2},2]
, and
(x_{n})
is decreasing from
1
to
N+1
. The function
f(t)=t-\sqrt{t}
is strictly increasing for
t>1
, and so (
6.13
) implies

(6.14)

x_{n}=x_{n+1}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{4x_{n+1}+1}}{2}:=f^{-1}(x_{n+1}).

In reverse order, we write
y_{1}=x_{N+1}
and
y_{n+1}=f^{-1}(y_{n})
, so
y_{N+1}=\varepsilon^{-1}
.

The heuristic idea is as follows.
Since
f^{-1}(y)>y+\sqrt{y}
, if we view
y_{n}
as a continuous function in
n
, then the iterative formula roughly says
\frac{dy}{dn}>y^{1/2}
, which gives
y_{n}>n^{2}/4
.

The detail is as follows. We actually prove that
y_{n}>\frac{n^{2}y_{1}}{14}
. The base case
n=1
is trivial and the case
n=2
follows from
y_{2}=x_{N}>2
and
y_{1}=x_{N+1}\leq 2
. Assume it holds for some
n\geq 2
, then we have

\displaystyle y_{n+1}-\frac{(n+1)^{2}y_{1}}{14}

\displaystyle=f^{-1}(y_{n})-\frac{(n+1)^{2}y_{1}}{14}

\displaystyle>y_{n}+\sqrt{y_{n}}-\frac{(n+1)^{2}y_{1}}{14}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n^{2}y_{1}}{14}+\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}y_{1}}{14}}-\frac{(n+1)^{2}y_{1}}{14}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{y_{1}}}{14}((\sqrt{14}-2\sqrt{y_{1}})n-\sqrt{y_{1}}),

which is positive since
y_{1}=x_{N+1}\leq 2
and
n\geq 2
. Using
y_{N}>N^{2}y_{1}/14
, we thus have

(6.15)

N<\sqrt{\frac{14y_{N}}{y_{1}}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{14y_{N}}{2-\sqrt{2}}}\leq 5\sqrt{y_{N}}\leq 5\varepsilon^{-1/2}.

∎

6.2.
Construction of wave packets

This step is the same as Section

Fix

In particular, for every

High-low decomposition

For each

With this, decompose

The low part:

(3)

The

For dyadic numbers

(6.23)

P_{\theta,\lambda}:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{3}\xi_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\theta):|\xi_{1}|\leq 1,\frac{\lambda}{2}\delta^{-1}\leq|\xi_{2}|\leq\lambda\delta^{-1},K^{-1}\delta^{-1}\leq|\xi_{3}|\leq\delta^{-1}\right\}.

) through (

Claim 6.3
.

Either one of the following is correct:

•

High case: there exists a Borel set

(6.24)

6.4.
Analysis of high and mixed parts

Assume that we are in the high case of Claim

By the lower bound in (

(6.26)

\int|f_{\mathrm{high}}|^{p}d\mu+\sum_{\text{dyadic }\lambda\in[\delta^{1/2},K^{-1}]}|f_{\lambda}|^{p}d\mu\gtrapprox c^{p}\mu(\mathbb{R}^{3})\delta^{p(\varepsilon-1)}.

One good thing about the high case and the mixed cases is that we can use the uncertainty principle. More precisely, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
f
be a bounded integrable function and
\eta
be an
L^{1}
-normalised Schwartz function Fourier supported on a ball
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of radius
\delta^{-1}
. Let
a\in(0,d]
and
\mu
be a finite Borel measure such that
\rho_{a}(\mu)<\infty
. Then for each
p\in[1,\infty)
we have

(6.27)

\int|f*\eta(x)|^{p}d\mu(x)\lesssim\rho_{a}(\mu)\delta^{a-d}\int|f*\tilde{\eta}(x)|^{p}dx,

where
\tilde{\eta}
is a Schwartz function whose Fourier transform is supported on
2B
and equals
1
on
B
.

Proof of lemma.

Since
f*\tilde{\eta}*\eta=f*\eta
, it suffices to prove (
6.27
) with the integral on the right hand side replaced by
\int|f|^{p}dx
, and then replace
f
by
f*\tilde{\eta}
. Next, by Hölder’s inequality we have

|f*\eta(x)|^{p}\lesssim|f|^{p}*|\eta|(x)\cdot\|\eta\|_{L^{1}}^{p-1}\lesssim|f|^{p}*|\eta|(x),

and so we may assume
p=1
. By Minkowski’s inequality, it then suffices to prove

(6.28)

\int|\eta(y)|d\mu(y)\lesssim\rho_{a}(\mu)\delta^{a-d}.

To check that, use that from the support assumption on
\eta
one can write
\eta(y)=\delta^{-d}\eta_{0}(\delta^{-1}y)
where
\eta_{0}
is Fourier supported in a ball of radius
1
. Then

\int|\eta(y)|d\mu(y)\lesssim\delta^{-d}\left\{\int_{|y|\leq\delta}\|\eta_{0}\|_{\infty}d\mu(y)+\sum_{i\geq 0}\int_{|y|\sim\delta 2^{i}}|\eta(\delta^{-1}y)|d\mu(y)\right\}

so (
6.28
) follows by the rapid decay of
\eta_{0}
and the definition of
\rho_{a}(\mu)
.
∎

Using the lemma to bound the left hand side of (
6.26
) (strictly speaking,
f_{\mathrm{high}}
and

should be replaced by functions Fourier supported on a slightly larger slab, but that does not affect the estimates), we have

\int|f_{\mathrm{high}}|^{p}dx+\sum_{\text{dyadic }\lambda\in[\delta^{1/2},K^{-1}]}|f_{\lambda}|^{p}dx\gtrapprox c^{p}\mu(\mathbb{R}^{3})\rho_{a}(\mu)^{-1}\delta^{p(\varepsilon-1)+3-a}.

), (

) and (

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\delta^{3}\left(K^{2}\delta^{-p+1}\left(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\#\mathbb{T}_{\theta}\right)+\sum_{\text{dyadic }\lambda\in[\delta^{1/2},K^{-1}]}\delta^{-\varepsilon-p+1}\left(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\#\mathbb{T}_{\theta}\right)\lambda^{\frac{p}{2}-2}\right)

where in the second to last line we used (

\displaystyle:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:C^{-1}2^{j-1}\leq|\xi|\leq C2^{j},

(7.11)

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(\xi,\Gamma)\in[C^{-1}2^{-k-3/2}|\xi|,C2^{-k-1/2}|\xi|]\},

and for
j=k

(7.12)

C\Gamma_{j,j}:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:C^{-1}2^{j-1}\leq|\xi|\leq C2^{j},\mathrm{dist}(\xi,\Gamma)\in[0,C2^{-j-1/2}|\xi|]\}.

When
C=1
, we simply denote
\Gamma_{j,k}
. We also denote

C\Gamma_{j}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon C^{-1}2^{j-1}\leq|\xi|\leq C2^{j}\}.

Lemma 7.3
.

For all
j\geq k\geq 0
, we have

(7.13)

\Gamma_{j,k}\subseteq\bigcup_{\theta\in\Theta_{k}}P_{\theta,j,k}\subseteq A\Gamma_{j,k},

for some absolute constant
A
. Moreover, the family
\Lambda_{j,k}
has
O(1)
-overlap.

Proof.

The second inclusion follows directly from Lemma
7.2
if
A
is large enough. The
O(1)
-overlap follows from essentially the same proof of that of Part
2
of Lemma
5.5
in Section
5.11
.

To prove the first inclusion, let
\xi\in\Gamma_{j,k}
. Take
\xi^{\prime}:=r\mathrm{e}_{3}(\omega)\in\Gamma
that attains
\mathrm{dist}(\xi,\Gamma)
. Take
\theta\in\Theta_{k}
be closest to
\omega
, so
|\theta-\omega|\leq c2^{-k/2}
. It suffices to prove that
\xi\in P_{\theta,j,k}
.

We first consider the normal direction. For
j>k
, by Proposition
7.1
, we have

(7.14)

|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\omega)|=|(\xi-\xi^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\omega)|=\mathrm{dist}(\xi,\Gamma)\geq 2^{-k-3/2}|\xi|\geq 2^{j-k-5/2}.

Also,

(7.15)

|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\omega)|=|(\xi-\xi^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\omega)|\leq|\xi-\xi^{\prime}|\leq 2^{-k-1/2}|\xi|.

Thus by Taylor expansion,

\displaystyle|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)|

\displaystyle\geq|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\omega)|-|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\omega)||\omega-\theta|-2|\xi||\omega-\theta|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{j-k-5/2}-c2^{j-3k/2-1/2}-2c^{2}2^{j-k}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{j-k-10}.

Similarly, for
j\geq k
, we also get an upper bound
|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)|\leq 2^{j-k+10}
.

Now we come to the tangential direction. By Proposition
7.1
, we have

(7.16)

\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\omega)=(\xi-\xi^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\omega)+\xi^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\omega)=0.

By Taylor expansion,

(7.17)

|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)|\leq 2|\xi||\theta-\omega|\leq 2c2^{j-k/2}\leq 2^{-100}2^{j-k/2}.

Lastly, we consider the radial direction. By Proposition
7.1
, we have

(7.18)

|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}(\omega)|=|\xi^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}(\omega)|=|\xi^{\prime}|.

Also, recalling that
\mathrm{dist}(\xi,\Gamma)\leq 2^{-1/2}|\xi|
, we have

(7.19)

|\xi^{\prime}|\geq|\xi|-|\xi^{\prime}-\xi|\geq(1-2^{-1/2})|\xi|\geq 2^{j-5}.

Thus by Taylor expansion,

(7.20)

|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta)|\geq|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}(\omega)|-|\xi||\theta-\omega|\geq 2^{j-5}-c2^{j-k/2}\geq 2^{j-10}.

Similarly, we can get an upper bound
|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta)|\leq 2^{j+10}
.
∎

We have

(7.21)

\Gamma_{j}\subseteq\bigcup\Lambda_{j}\subseteq A\Gamma_{j}.

\Lambda_{j}

The inclusion follows by Lemma

. The bounded overlap of

established in Lemma

and the bounded overlap of
\{A\Gamma_{j,k}:0\leq k\leq j\}
.
∎

Lemma 7.5
.

The family of planks

covers

-overlap.

Proof.

The bounded overlap of

and the bounded overlap of

.

To prove the covering, by Lemma

(7.22)

To see this, given any

. Take

such that

7.2.
Construction of wave packets

Consider the collection
\Lambda
of (infinitely many) planks we constructed in (
7.9
).

We first define a smooth partition of unity adapted to the family
\Lambda
: for each
P\in\Lambda
, let
\psi_{P}
be a bump function that
\sim 1
on
P
and is supported in
2P
. Also, let
\psi_{0}
be a bump function adapted to the ball
B^{3}(0,1)
. Moreover, they can be chosen in a way that

(7.24)

\psi_{0}+\sum_{P\in\Lambda}\psi_{P}=1.

For technical issues, we need to introduce a tiny exponent
\beta>0
to be determined.

For each
P\in\Lambda
, let
\mathbb{T}_{P}
be a tiling of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by planks
T
nearly dual to
P=P_{\theta,j,k}
, that is, having dimensions

(7.25)

2^{k-j+k\beta},\quad 2^{k/2-j+k\beta},\quad 2^{-j+k\beta}

in the normal, tangential and radial directions, respectively. Strictly speaking, they are not duals due to the tiny factor
\beta
. This is intended so that we can use repeated integration by parts argument to prove Lemma
7.7
below.

For each
T\in\mathbb{T}_{P}
, we choose a Schwartz function
\eta_{T}
which
\sim 1
on
T
and is Fourier supported on the plank centred at
0
, with sides parallel to
P
but with dimensions
2^{j-k-k\beta}
,
2^{j-k/2-k\beta}
and
2^{j-k\beta}
, respectively, such that we also have

(7.26)

\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P}}\eta_{T}=1.

Now for each
P\in\Lambda
and each
T\in\mathbb{T}_{P}
, we define the wave packet

(7.27)

M_{T}\mu(x):=\eta_{T}(x)(\mu*\psi_{P}^{\vee})(x)=\eta_{T}(x)\int\psi_{P}^{\vee}(x-y)d\mu(y).

We record some estimates on the wave packets.

Lemma 7.6
.

For
P\in\Lambda_{j,k}
,
T\in\mathbb{T}_{P}
and any
\theta\in I_{0}
, we have

(7.28)

\begin{split}\norm*{\pi_{\theta\#}M_T\mu}_{L^{1}(\mathcal{H}^{2})}&\lesssim_{N,\beta}\int w_{T,N}(y)d\mu(y)\\
&\lesssim_{N,\beta}2^{-kN}\mu(\mathbb{R}^{3})+\mu(T),\quad\forall N\geq 1,\end{split}

where
w_{T,N}(x)
is any weight function adapted to
T
(refer to (
3.17
)).

Proof.

Given a continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}
of compact support and
\theta\in I_{0}
, we may compute, using the definition of push-forward measure via Riesz representation theorem

(7.29)

\pi_{\theta\#}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x+t\gamma(\theta))dt,\quad\forall x\in\pi_{\theta\#}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

Thus

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta\#}M_{T}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{T}\mu(x+t\gamma(\theta))dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int\eta_{T}(x+t\gamma(\theta))\psi_{P}^{\vee}(x+t\gamma(\theta)-y)d\mu(y)dt.

By Fubini’s theorem and writing
z=x+t\gamma(\theta)
, we have

(7.30)

\int|\pi_{\theta\#}M_{T}\mu(x)|d\mathcal{H}^{2}(x)=\int\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\eta_{T}(z)\psi_{P}^{\vee}(z-y)dzd\mu(y).

Since
\psi_{P}^{\vee}
is an
L^{1}
-normalized Schwartz function essentially supported on a slightly smaller rectangle than
T
(due to the
\beta
factor), an application of Proposition
3.6
(generalized to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in an apparent way) gives
|\psi_{P}^{\vee}*\eta_{T}(y)|\lesssim_{N}w_{T,N}(y)
for any choice of
N
. Thus the result follows.

∎

The next is an almost orthogonality lemma.

Lemma 7.7
.

For each
P\in\Lambda_{j,k}
, denote by
\theta_{P}
the angle in
I_{0}
such that
P=P_{\theta_{P},j,k}
. Then for any
\theta\in I_{0}
such that
|\theta-\theta_{P}|\geq C2^{-k/2}
(here
C
is a large enough absolute constant) and any
x\in\pi_{\theta}(B^{3}(0,1))
, we have

(7.31)

\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P}}|\pi_{\theta\#}M_{T}\mu(x)|\lesssim_{N}2^{-kN}|P|\mu(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\quad\forall N\geq 1.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that for any rectangle
T\in\mathbb{T}_{P}
centred at
c_{T}
and any
x\in\pi_{\theta}(B^{3}(0,1))
, we have

(7.32)

|\pi_{\theta\#}M_{T}\mu(x)|\lesssim_{\beta,N}2^{-kN}(1+|x-c_{T}|2^{j-k-k\beta})^{-N}|P|\mu(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\quad\forall N\geq 1.

Using the inverse Fourier transform formula, we compute directly that

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta\#}M_{T}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int\int\eta_{T}(x+t\gamma(\theta))\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\psi_{P}(\xi)e^{i(x+t\gamma(\theta))\cdot\xi}dtd\xi

\displaystyle\leq\int|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\psi_{P}(\xi)|\left|\int\eta_{T}(x+t\gamma(\theta)e^{it\gamma(\theta))\cdot\xi}dt\right|d\xi.

Using
\|\hat{\mu}\|_{\infty}\leq\mu(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, it suffices to prove that

(7.33)

\left|\int\eta_{T}(x+t\gamma(\theta))e^{it\gamma(\theta)\cdot\xi}dt\right|\lesssim_{\beta,N}2^{-kN}(1+|x-c_{T}|2^{j-k-k\beta})^{-N},\quad\forall N\geq 1.

To prove this, we first prove the following claim.

Claim 7.8
.

Let
\theta\in I_{0}
and
|\theta-\theta_{P}|\geq C2^{-k/2}
. If
C
is large enough, then we have

(7.34)

|\xi\cdot\gamma(\theta)|\gtrsim(\theta-\theta_{P})^{2}2^{j},\quad\forall\xi\in P.

Proof of claim.

Write
\xi=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\xi_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\theta_{P})\in P
. For
i=3
, by Taylor expansion at the centre
\theta_{P}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta_{P})\cdot\gamma(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta_{P})\cdot\bigg(\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta_{P})+\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta_{P})(\theta-\theta_{P})

\displaystyle+(-\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta_{P})+\uptau(\theta_{P})\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta_{P}))(\theta-\theta_{P})^{2}\bigg)+o((\theta-\theta_{P})^{2})

\displaystyle\sim(\theta-\theta_{P})^{2}.

Since
\xi_{3}\sim 2^{j}
, we have

(7.35)

|\xi_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta_{P})\cdot\gamma(\theta)|\gtrsim(\theta-\theta_{P})^{2}2^{j}.

We also have

(7.36)

|\xi_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta_{P})\cdot\gamma(\theta)|\lesssim 2^{j-k},

and

(7.37)

|\xi_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta_{P})\cdot\gamma(\theta)|\lesssim 2^{j-k/2}|\theta-\theta_{P}|.

If
C
is large enough, then

(7.38)

|\xi\cdot\gamma(\theta)|\sim|\xi_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta_{P})\cdot\gamma(\theta)|\gtrsim(\theta-\theta_{P})^{2}2^{j}.

∎

We also need to estimate the derivative of
\eta_{T}(x+t\gamma(\theta))
with respect to
t
. Without loss of generality we may write

\displaystyle\eta_{T}(x)

\displaystyle=a_{1}(2^{j-k-k\beta}(x-c_{T})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta_{P}))a_{2}(2^{j-k/2-k\beta}(x-c_{T})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta_{P}))

\displaystyle\cdot a_{3}(2^{j-k\beta}(x-c_{T})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta_{P})),

for some suitable bump functions
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
supported on
(-1,1)
. Thus for
x\in B^{3}(c_{T},2^{k-j+k\beta})
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{d}{dt}\eta_{T}(x+t\gamma(\theta))\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{j-k-k\beta}|\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta_{P})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)|+2^{j-k/2-k\beta}|\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta_{P})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)|

\displaystyle+2^{j-k\beta}|\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta_{P})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)|

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{j-k-k\beta}+2^{j-k/2-k\beta}|\theta-\theta_{P}|+2^{j-k\beta}|\theta-\theta_{P}|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{j-k\beta}|\theta-\theta_{P}|^{2},

and for
x\notin B^{3}(c_{T},2^{k-j+k\beta})
we have rapid decay in
x
since
\eta
is Schwartz.

With Claim
7.8
, we can apply integration by parts repeatedly to estimate the left hand side of (
7.33
). Each time we integrate by parts, we gain a factor
\lesssim 2^{-k\beta}
, from which the result follows.

∎

7.3.
Good part and bad part

The main idea is to divide the wave packets into two parts, called the good part and the bad part. We will prove an
L^{1}
estimate for the bad part and an
L^{2}
estimate for the good part.

Let
a>2
be as in Theorem
1.5
. Let
\varepsilon,a_{1}
be small numbers to be determined later.

For each
P\in\Lambda
, define the set of heavy planks in
\mathbb{T}_{P}

(7.39)

\mathbb{T}_{P,b}:=\left\{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P}:\mu(T)\geq 2^{-k\frac{a_{1}+1}{2}-a(j-k)}\right\},\quad\mathbb{T}_{P,g}:=\mathbb{T}_{P}\backslash\mathbb{T}_{P,b}.

Decompose
\mu=\mu_{b}+\mu_{g}:=\mu_{b}+\mu_{0}+\mu_{g1}+\mu_{g_{2}}
, where

(7.40)

\displaystyle\mu_{0}

\displaystyle:=\mu*\psi_{0}^{\vee},

(7.41)

\displaystyle\mu_{b}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\in[j\varepsilon,j]}\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,b}}M_{T}\mu,

(7.42)

\displaystyle\mu_{g_{1}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\in[j\varepsilon,j]}\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}M_{T}\mu,

(7.43)

\displaystyle\mu_{g_{2}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\in[0,j\varepsilon)}\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P}}M_{T}\mu.

We remark that the wave packets of
\mu_{b}
are those that have heavy mass and are not too far away from the cone
\Gamma
(as
k
is not too small).

Note that each
M_{T}\mu
is a function but
\mu_{g},\mu_{b}
are in general measures. We also remark that
\mu_{g}=\mu_{g,\beta,a,a_{1},\varepsilon}
and
\mu_{b}=\mu_{b,a,\beta,a,a_{1},\varepsilon}
depend on parameters
\beta,a,a_{1},\varepsilon
, but for simplicity we just omit them.

We now state the main estimates we want to prove.

Lemma 7.9

(Lemma 6 of
[
GGG+22
]
)
.

Let
a>2
,
\varepsilon\ll 1
and
a_{1}<2
. For all Borel measures
\mu
supported on the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\rho_{a}(\mu)\leq 1
, it holds that

(7.44)

\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\in[j\varepsilon,j]}\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,b}}\int\int|\pi_{\theta\#}M_{T}\mu|d\mathcal{H}^{2}d\theta\lesssim 1.

Let
\{\phi_{n}:n\geq 1\}
be an approximation to the identity in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and then define
\mu^{(n)}_{g}:=\mu_{g}*\phi_{n}
to be a mollified version of
\mu_{g}
, such that each
\mu^{(n)}_{g}
is a function.

Lemma 7.10

(Lemma 7 of
[
GGG+22
]
)
.

Let
a>2
. Then for
\varepsilon>0
and
2-a_{1}>0
small enough depending on
a
, all Borel measures
\mu
supported on the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\rho_{a}(\mu)\leq 1
, it holds that

(7.45)

\int\int|\pi_{\theta\#}\mu^{(n)}_{g}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{2}d\theta\lesssim 1,

where the constant is independent of
n
.

We now prove Theorem
1.5
assuming Lemmas
7.9
and
7.10
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Take
\varepsilon,2-a_{1}
sufficiently small such that both Lemmas
7.9
and
7.10
hold.

By Lemma
7.9
, in particular, for a.e.
\theta
we have

(7.46)

\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\in[j\varepsilon,j]}\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,b}}\int|\pi_{\theta\#}M_{T}\mu|d\mathcal{H}^{2}<\infty,

which implies that
\pi_{\theta\#}\mu_{b}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{2}
.

By Lemma
7.10
, the sequence of functions
\{\pi_{\theta\#}\mu^{(n)}_{g}\}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathcal{H}^{2}\times I_{0})
, and so by weak compactness, there exists an
L^{2}(\mathcal{H}^{2}\times I_{0})
function
h
such that
\pi_{\theta\#}\mu^{(n)}_{g}\to h
weakly in
L^{2}(\mathcal{H}^{2}\times I_{0})
passing to a subsequence. However, by properties of approximate identity we know that
\mu^{(n)}_{g}\to\mu_{g}
weakly in
L^{2}(\mathcal{H}^{2})
, whence
\pi_{\theta\#}\mu^{(n)}_{g}\to\pi_{\theta\#}\mu_{g}
weakly in
L^{2}(\mathcal{H}^{2}\times I_{0})
. By uniqueness of limits, we have
h=\pi_{\theta\#}\mu_{g}
, and so
\pi_{\theta\#}\mu_{g}
is actually a function in
L^{2}(\mathcal{H}^{2}\times I_{0})
. Since
\mathcal{H}^{2}|_{B^{2}(0,1)}\times I_{0}
is a finite measure space, Hölder’s inequality implies that

(7.47)

\int\int|\pi_{\theta\#}\mu_{g}|d\mathcal{H}^{2}d\theta\lesssim 1.

In particular, for a.e.
\theta\in I_{0}
we have

(7.48)

\int|\pi_{\theta\#}\mu|d\mathcal{H}^{2}<\infty,

and so
\pi_{\theta\#}\mu_{g}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{2}
. Hence
\pi_{\theta\#}\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{2}
.
∎

7.4.
Controlling bad part

We now prove Lemma
7.9
. The main ingredient is Corollary
6.6
. We have

\displaystyle\int\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\in[j\varepsilon,j]}\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,b}}\int|\pi_{\theta\#}M_{T}\mu|d\mathcal{H}^{2}d\theta

(7.49)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\in[j\varepsilon,j]}\int\int\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}}{{|\theta_{P}-\theta|<C2^{-k/2}}}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,b}}|\pi_{\theta\#}M_{T}\mu|d\mathcal{H}^{2}d\theta

(7.50)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\in[j\varepsilon,j]}\int\int\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}}{{|\theta_{P}-\theta|\geq C2^{-k/2}}}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,b}}|\pi_{\theta\#}M_{T}\mu|d\mathcal{H}^{2}d\theta.

7.4.1.
Far part

To bound (
7.50
), we use Lemma
7.7
to get

\displaystyle\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}}{{|\theta_{P}-\theta|\geq C2^{-k/2}}}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P}}|\pi_{\theta\#}M_{T}\mu(x)|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{N,\beta}(\#\Lambda_{j,k})2^{-kN+3j}\mu(\mathbb{R}^{3})

\displaystyle\lesssim_{N,\beta}2^{-kN+3j},

where we have used
\#\Lambda_{j,k}\lesssim 2^{k/2}
and
\mu(\mathbb{R}^{3})\lesssim 1
since
\rho_{a}(\mu)\leq 1
.

Thus we have

(
7.50
)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{N,\beta}\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\in[j\varepsilon,j]}\int\int 2^{-kN+3j}d\mathcal{H}^{2}d\theta

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\geq 0}2^{3j-3j\varepsilon N}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1,

if we choose
N>10\varepsilon^{-1}
.

7.4.2.
Near part

To estimate (
7.49
), we apply Lemma
7.6
and get

(7.51)

\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}}{{|\theta_{P}-\theta|\leq C2^{-k/2}}}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,b}}\int|\pi_{\theta\#}M_{T}\mu|d\mathcal{H}^{2}\lesssim_{\beta,N}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}}{{|\theta_{P}-\theta|\leq C2^{-k/2}}}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,b}}2^{-kN}+\mu(T).

The rapidly decaying term
2^{-kN}
can be dealt with easily, since we also have

(7.52)

\#\mathbb{T}_{P,b}\leq 2^{k\frac{a_{1}+1}{2}+a(j-k)}

by the heavy mass in our definition of
\mathbb{T}_{P,b}
. The remaining argument is divided into the following steps.

Step 1: Discretization.

To estimate the other term, we define

(7.53)

F(\theta,C):=\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}}{{|\theta_{P}-\theta|\leq C2^{-k/2}}}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,b}}\mu(T)=\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}}{{|\theta_{P}-\theta|\leq C2^{-k/2}}}}\mu(\cup\mathbb{T}_{P,b}).

It is easy to see that for
|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\leq C2^{-k/2}
, we have

(7.54)

F(\theta,C)\leq F(\theta^{\prime},2C).

Using this and denoting
\Theta^{\prime}_{k}
to be the
C2^{-k}
lattice on
[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\int F(\theta,C)d\theta

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{k}}\int_{\theta^{\prime}-C2^{-k-1}}^{\theta^{\prime}+C2^{-k-1}}F(\theta,C)d\theta

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{k}}\int_{\theta^{\prime}-C2^{-k-1}}^{\theta^{\prime}+C2^{-k-1}}F(\theta^{\prime},2C)d\theta

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta^{\prime}_{k}}2^{-k}F(\theta^{\prime},2C)

(7.55)

\displaystyle\sim 2^{-k}\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\mu(\cup\mathbb{T}_{P,b}),

where in the last line we have used that
\Theta_{k}
is a
c2^{-k/2}
lattice.

Step 2: Rescaling.

Now we seek to apply Corollary
6.6
with
\delta^{1/2}\sim 2^{-k/2}
, where we take
\Delta=\Theta_{k}
.

Let
B_{l}
be finitely overlapping balls of radius
2^{k-j+k\beta}
that cover
B^{3}(0,1)
. For each
\theta
and
l
we have

(7.56)

\mu(\cup\mathbb{T}_{P,b})\leq\sum_{B_{l}}\mu(\cup\mathbb{T}_{P,l,b}),

where

(7.57)

\mathbb{T}_{P,l,b}:=\{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,b}:T\cap B_{l}\neq\varnothing\}.

It is easy to see that
\cup\mathbb{T}_{P,l,b}\subseteq 2^{1000}B_{l}
where
2^{1000}B_{l}
denotes the concentric dilation of
B_{l}
; also
2^{1000}B_{l}
’s have finite overlap.

Now we perform a rescaling. Let
\lambda(x):=2^{-j+k-k\beta}x
and
\mu_{j,k}
be the measure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined by

(7.58)

\mu_{j,k}(A):=\mu(\lambda(A)).

Also, for each
l
, define
\tilde{B}_{l}=\lambda(2^{1000}B_{l})
and

(7.59)

\mu_{j,k,l}:=2^{a(j-k)}\mu_{j,k}1_{\tilde{B}_{l}},\quad\tilde{\mathbb{T}}_{P,l,b}:=\{\lambda(T):T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,l,b}\}.

We can check that the set
\cup\tilde{\mathbb{T}}_{P,l,b}
is a disjoint union of planks of dimensions
2^{-k}\times 2^{-k/2}\times 1
pointing in the directions
\mathbf{e}_{i}(\theta_{P})
,
i=1,2,3
respectively, and it is contained in
\tilde{B}_{l}
. If we project every plank along the direction
\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta_{P})
, then we obtain a disjoint family of planar rectangles of dimensions
2^{-k}\times 2^{-k/2}
pointing in the directions
\mathbf{e}_{i}(\theta_{P})
,
i=1,2
respectively.

Meanwhile, in view of the heavy mass as in the definition
\mathbb{T}_{P,b}
, we have
\mu_{j,k,l}(\lambda(T))\gtrsim 2^{-k\frac{a_{1}+1}{2}}
, and so we have

(7.60)

\#(\tilde{\mathbb{T}}_{P,l,b})\lesssim 2^{k\frac{a+1}{2}}\mu_{j,k,l}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

It is also direct to see that
\rho_{a}(\mu_{j,k,l})\lesssim 2^{ka\beta}
.

Step 3: Applying Corollary
6.6
.

Now we use Corollary
6.6
with
\delta^{1/2}=c2^{-k/2}
to get some small exponent
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
, depending only on
a,a_{1}
, such that

(7.61)

\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\pi_{\theta_{P}\#}\mu_{j,k,l}\left(\bigcup\pi_{\theta_{P}\#}\tilde{\mathbb{T}}_{P,l,b}\right)\lesssim 2^{-\frac{k}{2}-k\varepsilon^{\prime}+ka\beta}\mu_{j,k,l}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

But by direct computation,

(7.62)

\pi_{\theta_{P}\#}\mu_{j,k,l}\left(\bigcup\pi_{\theta_{P}\#}\tilde{\mathbb{T}}_{P,l,b}\right)=\mu_{j,k,l}\left(\bigcup\tilde{\mathbb{T}}_{P,l,b}\right)\gtrsim 2^{a(j-k)}\mu(\cup\mathbb{T}_{P,l,b}).

Summing over
B_{l}
and using (
7.56
), we thus have

(7.63)

\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\mu(\cup\mathbb{T}_{P,b})\leq\sum_{B_{l}}\mu(\cup\mathbb{T}_{P,l,b})\lesssim 2^{-\frac{k}{2}-k\varepsilon^{\prime}}\mu(\mathbb{R}^{3})\leq 2^{-\frac{k}{2}-k\varepsilon^{\prime}+ak\beta}.

Continuing estimating from (
7.51
) and using (
7.53
) and (
7.55
), we have

(7.64)

\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}}{{|\theta_{P}-\theta|\leq C2^{-k/2}}}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,b}}\mu(T)\lesssim 2^{ak\beta-k\varepsilon^{\prime}}.

Now it suffices to choose
\beta=\varepsilon^{\prime}/100
and sum over
k\in[j\varepsilon,j]
and then
j\in\mathbb{N}
. This concludes the
L^{1}
estimate of the bad part.

7.5.
Controlling good part

We now prove Lemma
7.10
. By the functional analytic proof of Theorem
1.5
, we may abuse notation and treat
\mu_{g}
as a function as well.

Fix
\theta
and
x\in\pi_{\theta\#}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. We have

(7.65)

\pi_{\theta\#}\mu_{g}(x)=\int\mu_{g}(x+t\gamma(\theta))dt.

With the coordinate system
\{\mathbf{e}_{i}(\theta):i=1,2,3\}
and slightly abusing notation, any
x\in\pi_{\theta\#}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
can be written as
(0,x_{2},x_{3})
. Thus the above equality can be rewritten as

(7.66)

\pi_{\theta\#}\mu_{g}(x_{2},x_{3})=\int\mu_{g}(t,x_{2},x_{3})dt.

Doing the Fourier transform in the
(\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3})
-plane, we have

(7.67)

(\pi_{\theta\#}\mu_{g})^{\wedge}(\eta_{2},\eta_{3})=\widehat{\mu_{g}}(0,\eta_{2},\eta_{3})=\widehat{\mu_{g}}(\eta_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+\eta_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta)).

By Plancherel’s theorem,

(7.68)

\int\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\pi_{\theta\#}\mu_{g}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{2}d\theta=\int\iint|\widehat{\mu_{g}}(\eta_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+\eta_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta))|^{2}d\eta d\theta.

By the triangle inequality, we will estimate
\mu_{0}
,
\mu_{g1}
and
\mu_{g2}
respectively.

We first settle the easiest case, namely, for
\mu_{0}
. We have
\widehat{\mu_{0}}=\widehat{\mu}\psi_{0}
. Since
\sup|\widehat{\mu}|\leq\mu(\mathbb{R}^{3})<\infty
, the integral over
|\eta|\lesssim 1
and
\theta\in I_{0}
is clearly finite.

7.5.1.
Preparation

To estimate the other two terms, we first perform a change of variables

(7.69)

\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})=\eta_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+\eta_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta),

which has Jacobian

(7.70)

|\det(\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_3,\eta_2 (-\mathbf e_1+\uptau\mathbf e_3)-\eta_3 \uptau\mathbf e_2)|=|\eta_{2}\det(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_3)|=|\eta_{2}|.

Thus we have

(7.71)

\int\iint|\widehat{\mu_{g}}(\eta_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+\eta_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta))|^{2}d\eta d\theta=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\widehat{\mu_{g}}(\xi)|^{2}|\eta_{2}|^{-1}d\xi.

We also need the following lemma.

Lemma 7.11
.

Let
P\in\Lambda_{j,k}
. If there exist
\theta\in I_{0}
and
(\eta_{2},\eta_{3})
satisfying

(7.72)

\eta_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+\eta_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta)\in\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{P}),

as well as
|\theta-\theta_{P}|\ll 1
, then it holds that

(7.73)

|\theta-\theta_{P}|\lesssim 2^{-\frac{k}{2}},\quad|\eta_{2}|\lesssim 2^{j-\frac{k}{2}},\quad|\eta_{3}|\sim 2^{j}.

Furthermore, if
j>k
, then it holds that

(7.74)

|\theta-\theta_{P}|\sim 2^{-\frac{k}{2}},\quad|\eta_{2}|\sim 2^{j-\frac{k}{2}},\quad|\eta_{3}|\sim 2^{j}.

Proof.

Write
\xi=\eta_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+\eta_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta)
. Since
\xi\in\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{P})=2P
, we have (without loss of generality)

(7.75)

\displaystyle|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta_{P})|\lesssim 2^{j-k},

(7.76)

\displaystyle|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta_{P})|\ll 2^{j-\frac{k}{2}},

(7.77)

\displaystyle\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta_{P})\sim 2^{j}.

In particular,
|\xi|\sim|\eta_{2}|+|\eta_{3}|\sim 2^{j}
. Assume towards contradiction that
|\theta-\theta_{P}|\gg 2^{-k/2}
. Using (
7.76
), by Taylor expansion for
\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta_{P})
at
\theta
we have

(7.78)

|\eta_{2}+\eta_{3}(\theta_{P}-\theta)+O(\theta_{P}-\theta)^{2}|\ll 2^{j-\frac{k}{2}}.

Using (
7.77
), by Taylor expansion for
\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta_{P})
at
\theta
we have

\displaystyle 2^{j-k}

\displaystyle\gtrsim|\xi\cdot[\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)+\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)(\theta_{P}-\theta)+(-\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)+\uptau(\theta)\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta))(\theta_{P}-\theta)^{2}

\displaystyle+|\xi|o((\theta_{P}-\theta)^{2})]|

\displaystyle=\eta_{2}(\theta_{P}-\theta)+\eta_{3}(\theta_{P}-\theta)^{2}(1/2+o(1)).

Using
|\theta-\theta_{P}|\gg 2^{-k/2}
, we have

(7.79)

|\eta_{2}+\eta_{3}(\theta_{P}-\theta)(1/2+o(1))|\ll 2^{j-\frac{k}{2}}.

Combining this with (
7.78
) and using the triangle inequality, we have

(7.80)

|\eta_{2}|\ll 2^{j-k/2},

and plugging this into (
7.78
) again, we get

(7.81)

\eta_{3}\lesssim|\theta_{P}-\theta|^{-1}2^{j-k/2}\ll 2^{j},

using
|\theta-\theta_{P}|\ll 1
. This leads to a contradiction since
|\eta_{2}|+|\eta_{3}|\sim 2^{j}
. Thus we must have
|\theta-\theta_{P}|\lesssim 2^{-k/2}
.

By Taylor approximation, it is easy to get
|\eta_{2}|\lesssim 2^{j-k/2}
and
\eta_{3}\sim 2^{j}
.

Lastly, we come to the case
j>k
. Assume towards contradiction that
|\theta-\theta_{P}|\ll 2^{-k/2}
. Then by Taylor expansion,

(7.82)

|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)-\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta_{P})|\leq|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta_{P})||\theta-\theta_{P}|\ll 2^{j-k},

and so
|\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)|\sim 2^{j-k}
, contradicting the assumption that
\xi\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)=0
. Thus
|\theta-\theta_{P}|\gtrsim 2^{-k/2}
. By Taylor approximation, it is easy to get
|\eta_{2}|\sim 2^{j-k/2}
and
\eta_{3}\sim 2^{j}
.

∎

7.5.2.
Estimate of
\mu_{g2}

For
\mu_{g2}
we have
k\leq j\varepsilon
, namely, the planks
P
are extremely far away from the cone
\Gamma
. We estimate (
7.68
) with
\mu_{g2}
in place of
\mu_{g}
. Since
k\geq j\varepsilon
, Lemma
7.11
implies that

(7.83)

|\eta_{2}|\gtrsim|\xi|^{1-\varepsilon/2}.

Thus, by (
7.71
), we have

(7.84)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\widehat{\mu_{g}}(\xi)|^{2}|\eta_{2}|^{-1}d\xi\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{\varepsilon/2-1}d\xi,

which is finite if we choose
\varepsilon
small enough that
a-\varepsilon/2>2
, since
\rho_{a}(\mu)\lesssim 1
and
a>2
(see, for instance, Theorems 2.7 and 3.10 of
[
Mat15
]
).

7.5.3.
Estimate of
\mu_{g1}

This part estimates the contribution from the planks that have light mass and are not too far away from the cone. By definition, we have

(7.85)

\widehat{\mu_{g}}(\xi)=\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\in[j\varepsilon,k]}\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\widehat{M_{T}\mu}

where we have

(7.86)

\widehat{M_{T}\mu}(\xi)=\widehat{\eta_{T}}*(\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\psi_{P}(\xi)),

which is supported on
3P
where
P\in\Lambda_{j,k}
. By Plancherel’s identity, we have

(7.87)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\widehat{\mu_{g}}(\xi)|^{2}|\eta_{2}|^{-1}d\xi=\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\in[j\varepsilon,k]}\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\widehat{M_{T}\mu}(\xi)\bigg|^{2}|\eta_{2}|^{-1}d\xi.

We further divide the argument into the following steps.

Step 1: Using orthorgonality.

Fix
k\in[j\varepsilon,k]
. We consider the cases
k<j
and
k=j
separately. In the former case, using Lemma
7.11
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\widehat{M_{T}\mu}(\xi)\bigg|^{2}|\eta_{2}|^{-1}d\xi

\displaystyle\sim 2^{-j+k/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\widehat{M_{T}\mu}(\xi)\bigg|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle=2^{-j+k/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}M_{T}\mu(x)\bigg|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\sim 2^{-j+k/2}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|M_{T}\mu(x)|^{2}dx.

Summing over
P\in\Lambda_{j,k}
, we thus have

(7.88)

\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\widehat{M_{T}\mu}(\xi)\bigg|^{2}|\eta_{2}|^{-1}d\xi\lesssim 2^{-j+\frac{k}{2}}\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|M_{T}\mu(x)|^{2}dx.

For
j=k
we have (
7.88
) holds as well. To see this, fix
P
and we go back to the form

(7.89)

\int\iint\bigg|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\widehat{M_{T}\mu}(\eta_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+\eta_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta))\bigg|^{2}d\eta d\theta.

By Lemma
7.11
, we must have
|\theta-\theta_{P}|\lesssim 2^{-j/2}
. For every such
\theta
, we apply Plancherel’s identity in the
(\eta_{2},\eta_{3})
plane to get

(7.90)

\iint\bigg|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\widehat{M_{T}\mu}(\eta_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+\eta_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta))\bigg|^{2}d\eta=\iint|F(x_{2},x_{3})|^{2}dx_{2}dx_{3},

where

\displaystyle F(x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\iint\widehat{M_{T}\mu}(\eta_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+\eta_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta))e(x_{2}\eta_{2}+x_{3}\eta_{3})d\eta_{2}d\eta_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}M_{T}\mu(y)\iint e(-y\cdot(\eta_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+\eta_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta)))dy

\displaystyle\cdot e(x_{2}\eta_{2}+x_{3}\eta_{3})d\eta_{2}d\eta_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}M_{T}\mu(y)\delta_{0}(x_{2}-y\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta))\delta_{0}(x_{3}-y\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta))dy

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{T}\mu(t\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)+x_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+x_{3}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta))dt.

Plugging back into (
7.90
), we get

\displaystyle\iint|F(x_{2},x_{3})|^{2}dx_{2}dx_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint F(x_{2},x_{3})\overline{F(x_{2},x_{3})}dx_{2}dx_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\iint\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{T}\mu(t\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)+x_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+x_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta))

\displaystyle\cdot\overline{M_{T^{\prime}}\mu(t^{\prime}\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)+x_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+x_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta))}dtdt^{\prime}dx_{2}dx_{3}.

For a fixed
T
, recall that
M_{T}\mu
is essentially supported on
T
. Since
|\theta-\theta_{P}|\lesssim 2^{-j/2}
, we can show that

(7.91)

|M_{T}\mu(t\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta)+x_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta)+x_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta))|\lesssim|\tilde{M}_{T}\mu(t\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta_{P})+x_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta_{P})+x_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta_{P}))|

where
\tilde{M}_{T}:=w_{T}(\mu*\psi_{P}^{\vee})
and
w_{T}
is a suitable weight function adapted to
T
. For fixed
x_{2},x_{3}
, we thus have
T,T^{\prime}
must only differ at most in the
\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta_{P})
position (otherwise we have rapid decay which can be treated as
0
; we leave the rigorous argument to the reader; cf. Section
3.8
). However, since we are in the case
k=j
and we may assume
T\subseteq B^{3}(0,1)
(otherwise we have rapid decay), we must have
T=T^{\prime}
. Thus we can continue estimating

\displaystyle\iint|F(x_{2},x_{3})|^{2}dx_{2}dx_{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{T}\mu(t\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta_{P})+x_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta_{P})+x_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta_{P}))dt\right|^{2}dx_{2}dx_{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|M_{T}\mu(t\mathbf{e}_{1}(\theta_{P})+x_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}(\theta_{P})+x_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}(\theta_{P}))\right|^{2}dtdx_{2}dx_{3},

where in the last line we have used the fact that
t
is essentially supported on the unit interval. Integrating in
\theta
, using the discretization and summing over
P\in\Lambda_{j,k}
, we have (
7.88
).

We continue to estimate (
7.88
) and do not distinguish
j>k
and
j=k
anymore. Thus it suffices to estimate the term

(7.92)

\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|M_{T}\mu(x)|^{2}dx.

Step 2: Preparation for refined Strichartz inequality.

We hope to apply Theorem
8.2
stated in Section
8
below. Before that we need to do some preparation work.

Write

(7.93)

f_{T}:=(\eta_{T}M_{T}\mu)*\psi_{P}^{\vee},

so that

(7.94)

\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|M_{T}\mu(x)|^{2}dx=\int\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}f_{T}d\mu.

Similar to the proof of Step 2 of Section
7.4.2
, we cover the unit ball by
O(1)
-overlapping small balls
B_{l}
of radius
2^{k-j+k\beta}
and let
\nu_{l}
be the restriction of
\mu
to
B_{l}
. By Cauchy-Schwarz, we have

(7.95)

\int\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}f_{T}d\mu\leq\sum_{l}\mu(B_{l})^{1/2}\left(\int\left|\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}f_{T}\right|^{2}d\nu_{l}\right)^{1/2}.

Let
\zeta_{j}
be a non-negative bump function such that
\widehat{\zeta_{j}}(\xi)=1
for
|\xi|\leq 2^{j+k\beta+10}
. By the Fourier support condition of
f_{T}
, we have

(7.96)

\int\left|\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}f_{T}\right|^{2}d\nu_{l}=\int\left|\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}f_{T}\right|^{2}d(\nu_{l}*\zeta_{j}).

By dyadic pigeonholing (see Section
5.10
for the technique), we can find a subset

(7.97)

\mathbb{W}_{l}\subseteq\bigcup_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}:T\cap B_{l}\neq\varnothing\}

such that
\norm*{f_T}_{2}
is a constant
N
up to a factor of
2
as
T
varies over
\mathbb{W}_{l}
, and

(7.98)

\int\left|\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}f_{T}\right|^{2}d(\nu_{l}*\zeta_{j})\lesssim j\int\left|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{W}_{l}}f_{T}\right|^{2}d(\nu_{l}*\zeta_{j}).

Now we take some
p\geq 2
to be determined. By Hölder’s inequality, we have

(7.99)

\int\left|\sum_{T\in\mathbb{W}_{l}}f_{T}\right|^{2}d(\nu_{l}*\zeta_{j})\leq\norm*{\sum_{T\in\mathbb W_l}f_T}_{p}^{2}\left(\int(\nu_{l}*\zeta_{j})^{\frac{p}{p-2}}\right)^{1-\frac{2}{p}}.

We can view
\sum_{T\in\mathbb{W}_{l}}f_{T}
as essentially supported on
B^{3}(0,1)
by the rapid decay outside. Thus, by dyadic pigeonholing again, there is some dyadic
M\geq 1
and a disjoint union
Y
of balls
Q
of radius
2^{-j}
, such that each
Q\subseteq Y
intersects
\sim M
planks
T
as they vary over
\mathbb{W}_{l}
, and that

(7.100)

\norm*{\sum_{T\in\mathbb W_l}f_T}_{p}^{2}\left(\int(\nu_{l}*\zeta_{j})^{\frac{p}{p-2}}\right)^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\lesssim j\norm*{\sum_{T\in\mathbb W_l}f_T}_{L^{p}(Y)}^{2}\left(\int_{Y}(\nu_{l}*\zeta_{j})^{\frac{p}{p-2}}\right)^{1-\frac{2}{p}}.

Combining all the above estimates, we have in particular

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\Lambda_{j,k}}\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{P,g}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|M_{T}\mu(x)|^{2}dx

(7.101)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\beta}2^{j\beta}\sum_{l}\mu(B_{l})^{1/2}\norm*{\sum_{T\in\mathbb W_l}f_T}_{L^{p}(Y)}\left(\int_{Y}(\nu_{l}*\zeta_{j})^{\frac{p}{p-2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}.

Step 3: Applying refined Strichartz inequality.

Now we apply Theorem
8.2
(to be proved later) to the rescaled functions
f_{T}(2^{-j}\cdot)
to get

\displaystyle\norm*{\sum_{T\in\mathbb W_l}f_T}_{L^{p}(Y)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\beta}2^{j\beta+(3j-\frac{3k}{2})(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\left(\frac{M}{\#\mathbb{W}_{l}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\left(\sum_{T\in\mathbb{W}_{l}}\norm*{f_T}_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\sim 2^{j\beta+(3j-\frac{3k}{2})(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\left(\frac{M}{\#\mathbb{W}_{l}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}(\#\mathbb{W}_{l})^{\frac{1}{2}}N

(7.102)

\displaystyle=2^{j\beta+(3j-\frac{3k}{2})(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}M^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}(\#\mathbb{W}_{l})^{\frac{1}{2}}N.

We also need to estimate the term involving
\nu*\zeta_{j}
. Using the assumption that
\rho_{a}(\mu)\leq 1
, we have
\rho_{a}(\nu)\leq 1
, and so by the rapid decay of
\zeta_{j}
outside
B^{3}(0,2^{-j-k\beta})
,

\displaystyle\left|\int\zeta_{j}(x-y)d\nu_{l}(y)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\nu_{l}(B^{3}(x,2^{-j-k\beta}))2^{3(j+k\beta)}\lesssim 2^{3(j+k\beta)-a(j-k\beta)}.

Hence by the light mass in the definition of
\mathbb{T}_{P,g}

9.1.
Proof of flat decoupling

We give a proof of Theorem
5.2
. First, by a simple tiling argument and the triangle and Hölder’s inequalities, we may assume
K=1
.

The idea is to use interpolation. The case
p=2
follows from Plancherel’s identity, and the case
p=\infty
follows from the triangle and Hölder’s inequalities. However, there are a few technical obstacles we need to consider. First, it is not of the form of a general sublinear operator since we have extra Fourier support conditions on each
f_{T}
. Also, on the right hand side of the decoupling inequality we have a mixed norm. The full detail is as follows.

Rigorous proof of flat decoupling.

For each
T\in\mathbb{T}
, let
\psi_{T}
be a Schwartz function such that
\widehat{\psi_{T}}
is supported on
CT
and equals
1
on
T
. Define the vector-input linear operator
L
by

L((f_{T})_{T\in\mathbb{T}})=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}f_{T}*\psi_{T},

where
f_{T}
are arbitrary functions (with no Fourier support assumption) in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Then by Plancherel’s identity, we have

(9.1)

\norm*{L((f_T)_{T\in\mathbb T})}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\norm*{\norm{f_T* \psi_T}_{L^2(\R^3)}}_{\ell^{2}(T\in\mathbb{T})}\lesssim_{C}\norm*{\norm{f_T}_{L^2(\R^3)}}_{\ell^{2}(T\in\mathbb{T})},

using Young’s inequality, since
\norm*{\psi_T}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\sim 1
. On the other hand, by the triangle and Hölder’s inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\norm*{L((f_T)_{T\in\mathbb T})}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle\leq(\#\mathbb{T})^{1/2}\norm*{\norm{f_T* \psi_T}_{L^\infty(\R^3)}}_{\ell^{2}(T\in\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{C}(\#\mathbb{T})^{1/2}\norm*{\norm{f_T}_{L^\infty(\R^3)}}_{\ell^{2}(T\in\mathbb{T})}.

Thus, using the interpolation theorem for mixed normed spaces (see, for instance, Theorem 2 of Section 7 of
[
BP61
]
), we have for every
2\leq p\leq\infty
that

(9.2)

\norm*{L((f_T)_{T\in\mathbb T})}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim_{C}(\#\mathbb{T})^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\norm*{\norm{f_T}_{L^p(\R^3)}}_{\ell^{2}(T\in\mathbb{T})}.

Lastly, given any
f_{T}
Fourier supported on
T
, we have
f_{T}=f_{T}*\psi_{T}
. Thus

(9.3)

\norm*{\sum_{T\in\mathbb T}f_T}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\norm*{\sum_{T\in\mathbb T}f_T*\psi_T}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim_{C}(\#\mathbb{T})^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\norm*{\norm{f_T}_{L^p(\R^3)}}_{\ell^{2}(T\in\mathbb{T})}.

∎

9.2.
Proof of decoupling for
C^{2}
curves

In this section, we roughly sketch the proof of Theorem 10 using the language of
[
Dem20
]
, and point out the changes we need to make when we only have
C^{2}
regularity. For simplicity of notation, we take
p=6
.

Remark.
Unless otherwise stated, an uncoloured reference without a hyperlink will mean a label in
[
Dem20
]
.

Below and henceforth, for each Schwartz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
and each interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
f_{I}
the Fourier restriction of
f
onto
I\times\mathbb{R}
, namely,
\widehat{f_{I}}:=\widehat{f}1_{I\times\mathbb{R}}
.

Theorem 9.1

(Bourgain-Demeter decoupling for planar
C^{2}
curves)
.

Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
-curve such that
|\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)|\sim 1
. Let
\delta\in 4^{-\mathbb{N}}
and let
N_{\gamma}(\delta)
be the (vertical)
\delta
-neighbourhood of the graph of
\gamma
over
[0,1]
, namely,

(9.4)

N_{\gamma}(\delta):=\{(t,\gamma(t)+s):t\in[0,1],|s|\leq\delta\}.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{\delta^{1/2}}
be a tiling of
[0,1]
into intervals of length
\delta^{1/2}
. Let
C>0
and denote by
D_{\gamma}(\delta)
the smallest constant such that the following inequality holds for all
f
Fourier supported on
N_{\gamma}(C\delta)
:

(9.5)

\norm*{f}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq D_{\gamma}(\delta)\norm*{\norm{f_I}_{L^6(\R^2)}}_{\ell^{2}(I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta^{1/2}})}.

Then the following holds: for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists some
C_{\varepsilon,\gamma,C}
such that

(9.6)

D_{\gamma}(\delta)\leq C_{\varepsilon,\gamma,C}\delta^{-\varepsilon},\quad\forall\delta\in 4^{-\mathbb{N}}.

The following corollary will be useful when the curve is given by a parametric equation.

Corollary 9.2
.

Let
\phi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a
C^{2}
-curve with nonzero velocity and nonzero curvature. Let
\delta\in 4^{-\mathbb{N}}
and let
N^{\prime}_{\phi}(\delta)
be the
\delta
-neighbourhood of the image of
\phi
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, namely,

(9.7)

N^{\prime}_{\phi}(\delta):=\{\phi(t)+s:t\in[0,1],|s|\leq\delta\}.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\delta^{1/2}}
be a covering of
N^{\prime}_{\phi}(C\delta)
by
O(1)
-overlapping rectangles
T
of dimensions
O(\delta^{1/2})\times O(\delta)
, such that
\cup\mathcal{T}_{\delta^{1/2}}\subseteq N^{\prime}_{\phi}(O(\delta))
. Then

(9.8)

\norm*{f}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}\norm*{\norm{\mathcal R_T f}_{L^6(\R^2)}}_{\ell^{2}(T\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta^{1/2}})},\quad\forall\varepsilon>0,

where
\mathcal{R}_{T}f:=(\hat{f}1_{T})^{\vee}
and the implicit constant also depends on
C
and all implicit constants in the big
O
notations above.

Proof.

Since
|\phi^{\prime}|=1
, by the implicit function theorem and a compactness argument, we can cut the image of
\phi
into
O(1)
many pieces, such that each piece after a suitable rotation becomes the graph of some
C^{2}
function
\gamma
with non-vanishing curvature. By the triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities, it suffices to consider each such piece. However, after rotation, we can view the tiling
\mathcal{T}_{\delta^{1/2}}
as a
O(1)
overlapping cover of
N_{\gamma}(\delta)
by rectangles of dimensions
\delta^{1/2}\times\delta
that are all contained in
N_{\gamma}(O(\delta))
. By a simple tiling argument, we may apply Theorem
9.1
to get our desired conclusion, since decoupling is invariant under rotations.
∎

Now we come to the proof of Theorem
9.1
. One of the most important modifications is for Proposition 10.2.

Let
0<a<1<A
be fixed constants, and denote by
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}(a,A)
the family of all
C^{2}
functions
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(9.9)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\{|\gamma(t)|,|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|,|\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)|\}\leq A,

\displaystyle\inf_{t\in[0,1]}|\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)|\geq a

Proposition 9.3

(Parabolic rescaling for
\mathcal{F}(a,A)
)
.

Let
\sigma\in[\delta,1]\cap 4^{-\mathbb{N}}
. For each
J\in\mathcal{I}_{\sigma^{1/2}}
, denote by
\mathcal{I}_{\delta^{1/2}}(J)
the collection of all intervals
I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta^{1/2}}
that are also contained in
J
.

Then there exists some
\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathcal{F}(a,A)
such that for every Schwartz function
f
Fourier supported on
N_{\gamma}(\delta)
, we have

(9.10)

\norm*{f_J}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq D_{\tilde{\gamma}}(\sigma^{-1}\delta)\norm*{\norm{f_I}_{L^6(\R^2)}}_{\ell^{2}(I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta^{1/2}}(J))}.

Proof.

Write
J=[c,c+\sigma^{1/2}]
and let

(9.11)

\widehat{g}(t,s):=\widehat{f}(\sigma^{1/2}t+c,\sigma s-\gamma(c)-\gamma^{\prime}(c)\sigma^{1/2}t),

which is supported on
N_{\tilde{\gamma}}(\sigma^{-1}\delta)
where

(9.12)

\tilde{\gamma}(t):=\sigma^{-1}\left[\gamma(\sigma^{1/2}t+c)-\gamma(c)-\gamma^{\prime}(c)\sigma^{1/2}t\right].

If
\gamma\in\mathcal{F}(a,A)
, we can check that
\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathcal{F}(a,A)
as well. Applying the definition of
D_{\tilde{\gamma}}(\sigma^{-1}\delta)
to
g
, we have

(9.13)

\norm*{g}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq D_{\tilde{\gamma}}(\sigma^{-1}\delta)\norm*{\norm{g_{\tilde I}}_{L^6(\R^2)}}_{\ell^{2}(\tilde{I}\in\mathcal{I}_{\sigma^{-1/2}\delta^{1/2}})}.

Reversing the change of variables and cancelling the same Jacobian factor on both sides, we obtain the desired result.
∎

The next modification will be for Proposition 10.14.

Fix
I_{1}=[0,1/4]
and
I_{2}=[1/2,1]
. Define
BD_{\gamma}(\delta)
to be the smallest constant such that the inequality

(9.14)

\norm*{|f_1f_2|^{1/2}}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq BD_{\gamma}(\delta)\prod_{i=1}^{2}\norm*{\norm{f_I}_{L^6(\R^2)}}^{1/2}_{\ell^{2}(I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta^{1/2}}(I_{i}))}

holds for all
f_{i}
with Fourier support on
N_{\gamma}(\delta)\cap(I_{i}\times\mathbb{R})
,
i=1,2
respectively. For our convenience, we also define

(9.15)

D(\delta):=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{F}(a,A)}D_{\gamma}(\delta),\quad BD(\delta):=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{F}(a,A)}BD_{\gamma}(\delta).

We also have the following bilinear analogue of Proposition
9.3
.

Proposition 9.4

(Bilinear parabolic rescaling for
\mathcal{F}(a,A)
)
.

Let
\sigma\in[\delta,1/16]\cap 4^{-\mathbb{N}}
. For
i=1,2
, intervals
J_{i}\in\mathcal{I}_{\sigma^{1/2}}(I_{i})
and every Schwartz function
f
Fourier supported on
N_{\gamma}(\delta)
, we have

(9.16)

\norm*{|f_{J_1}f_{J_2}|^{1/2}}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq BD(\sigma^{-1}\delta)\prod_{i=1}^{2}\norm*{\norm{f_I}_{L^6(\R^2)}}^{1/2}_{\ell^{2}(I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta^{1/2}}(J_{i}))}.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Proposition
9.3
, so we leave it as an exercise.
∎

Lemma 9.5

(Sub-multiplicativity of decoupling constants)
.

For
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in 4^{-\mathbb{N}}
we have

(9.17)

D(\delta_{1}\delta_{2})\leq D(\delta_{1})D(\delta_{2}),\quad BD(\delta_{1}\delta_{2})\leq BD(\delta_{1})BD(\delta_{2}).

Proof.

The first inequality follows from Minkowski’s inequality and Proposition
9.3
. The second inequality follows from Hölder’s inequality, Minkowski’s inequality and Proposition
9.4
.
∎

Proposition 9.6

(Reduction to bilinear decoupling)
.

For each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
C_{\varepsilon}
such that for all
\delta\in 4^{-\mathbb{N}}
we have

(9.18)

D(\delta)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}\max_{\sigma\in[\delta,1]\cap 4^{-\mathbb{N}}}BD(\sigma).

Proof.

Everything in the proof of Proposition 10.14 should remain unchanged (modulo difference in notation), except that we now use Proposition
9.4
for the family
\mathcal{F}(a,A)
.
∎

The content of Corollary 10.18 is unchanged.

Proposition 9.7

(Corollary 10.18)
.

Assume
\delta=4^{-2^{u}}
for some
u\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for each
1\leq s\leq u
,

(9.19)

BD(\delta)\lesssim_{s,\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon-2^{-s-2}}\prod_{l=1}^{s}D(\delta^{1-2^{-l}})^{2^{l-s-1}}.

Proof of Proposition
9.7
.

All arguments in
[
Dem20
]
leading to Corollary 10.18 are the same, except the paragraph right after (10.16). In this case, the longer side of each
T\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
is now pointing in the direction
(\gamma^{\prime}(c_{I}),-1)
. Since
|\gamma^{\prime\prime}|\sim 1
, when
|c_{I}-c_{\tilde{I}}|\sim 1
, the directions are also
\sim 1
separated. This ensures the bilinear Kakeya inequality in Theorem 10.21 is applicable. We also remark here that the analogue of the rigorous proof of Theorem 11.10 also remains unchanged.
∎

Now we come to the proof of Theorem
9.1
.

Proof.

Iterating Proposition
9.7
in the same way as in
[
Dem20
]
, we obtain
BD(\delta)\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}
when
\delta\in 4^{-2^{\mathbb{N}}}
.

We still need to prove
BD(\delta)\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}
for a general
\delta\in 4^{-\mathbb{N}}
. This part was not given in
[
Dem20
]
, so we give it here for completeness. The idea is a simple induction on scales argument.

Let
m\geq 1
and assume we have proved
BD(\delta)\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}
for every
\delta=4^{-n}
where
0\leq n<2^{m}
. We need to show
BD(\delta)\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}
for every
\delta=4^{-n}
where
2^{m}<n<2^{m+1}
. Then
n-2^{m}\in(0,2^{m})
, and so by Lemma
9.5
, we have

(9.20)

BD(\delta)=BD(4^{-2^{m}}4^{2^{m}-n})\leq BD(4^{-2^{m}})BD(4^{2^{m}-n})\lesssim_{\varepsilon}4^{2^{m}\varepsilon}4^{(n-2^{m})\varepsilon}=\delta^{-\varepsilon},

using both the result already established for
\delta\in 4^{-2^{\mathbb{N}}}
and the induction hypothesis.
∎

9.3.
Proof of decoupling for
C^{2}
cones

In this section, we give a proof of Theorem
5.1
. Although the most important idea of the proof is already given in the appendix of
[
GGG+22
]
, we would like to provide more details. We also refer the reader to Section 12.2 of
[
Dem20
]
as well as Section 8 of
[
BD15
]
.

We first prove Part
1
. For the covering part, let
\theta\in[0,1]
be arbitrary, we choose
\omega\in\delta^{1/2}\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,1]
that is closest to
\theta
. Then the result follows from Proposition
1.10
. The bounded overlap follows exactly from Part
2
of Lemma
5.5
. (Note the planks
A_{\omega,\delta^{1/2}}
are denoted
C_{1}P_{\omega,\delta^{1/2}}
there.)

9.3.1.
Preliminary reductions

To prove Part
2
, we use an iteration argument known as Pramanik-Seeger type iteration
[
PS07
]
. For simplicity of notation we only consider
p=6
. By splitting
\Gamma
into
\lesssim|\log K|
many pieces and rescaling each piece back to
1\leq\xi_{3}\leq 2
, we may assume
K=1
, which leads to
a loss of
O(K^{2}|\log K|)
by the triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities (so
K^{10}
is safe). Lastly, also by a simple tiling argument and triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities, we may assume the collection
\{A_{\omega,\delta^{1/2}}\}
is disjoint.

9.3.2.
Introducing intermediate scale

For
\delta\in 4^{-\mathbb{N}}
, we denote by
D(\delta)
the smallest constant such that for every family of Schwartz functions
\{f_{\omega}:\omega\in\Omega\}
where each
f_{\omega}
has Fourier support in
A_{\omega,\delta^{1/2}}
, we have

(9.21)

\left\|\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}f_{\omega}\right\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq D(\delta)\norm*{\norm{f_\omega}_{L^6(\R^3)}}_{\ell^{2}(\omega\in\Omega)}.

Our goal is to show that
D(\delta)\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}
for every
\varepsilon>0
.

To this end, we define an intermediate scale

(9.22)

\lambda:=\delta^{(1-\varepsilon)/2},

and consider the lattice
\Sigma=\lambda\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,1]
. The choice of
\lambda
will be clear soon. We then recall (
5.50
) (with
K=1
):

(9.23)

CP_{\sigma,\lambda}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{3}\xi_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\sigma):|\xi_{1}|\leq C\lambda^{2},|\xi_{2}|\leq C\lambda,C^{-1}\leq\xi_{3}\leq C\right\}.

Since
A_{\omega,\delta^{1/2}}\subseteq C_{1}P_{\omega,\lambda}
, by Lemma
5.8
, we know that the collection
\{C_{3}P_{\sigma,\lambda}:\sigma\in\Sigma\}
forms a
O(1)
-overlapping cover of
\cup_{\omega\in\Omega}A_{\omega,\delta^{1/2}}
.

Recall the notation
\omega\prec\sigma
, which means that
\omega-\sigma\in(\lambda/2,\lambda/2]
. Define for each
\sigma\in\Sigma
that

(9.24)

f_{\sigma}=\sum_{\omega\prec\sigma}f_{\omega},

which has Fourier support in
C_{3}P_{\sigma,\lambda}
by Part
2
of Lemma
5.8
. Also, we have

(9.25)

\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}f_{\omega}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma}f_{\sigma}.

Thus, we may first apply the definition of
D(\lambda^{2})
to get

(9.26)

\left\|\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}f_{\omega}\right\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq D(\lambda^{2})\norm*{\norm{f_\sigma}_{L^6(\R^3)}}_{\ell^{2}(\sigma\in\Sigma)}.

It remains to estimate each
\norm*{f_\sigma}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}
.

9.3.3.
Approximation by cylinder

We also encounter some technical difficulty here, namely, the curve
\mathbf{e}_{3}
is merely
C^{1}
by Lemma
1.8
since the torsion function
\uptau
may not be differentiable. This will hinder applications of Theorem
9.1
as well as multiple Taylor expansions.

To deal with this issue, we will reparametrize
\mathbf{e}_{3}
; note that the planks remain unchanged.
Let us be more precise. Recall by Lemma
1.8
that
\mathbf{e}^{\prime}_{3}=-\uptau e_{2}
. Put

(9.27)

s=s(\theta)=\int_{0}^{\theta}-\frac{1}{\uptau(t)}dt,

and redefine

(9.28)

\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{i}(\theta)=\mathbf{e}_{i}(s(\theta)),\quad i=1,2,3.

In this way, we obtain the new Frenet frame formula:

(9.29)

\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}_{1}=-\uptau^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}_{2},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}_{2}=\uptau^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{3},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}_{3}=\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{2}.

Thus
\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{3}
becomes
C^{2}
, at the cost that
\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{1}
may not necessarily be
C^{2}
.

For simplicity, we abuse notation and still use
\mathbf{e}_{i}
to mean
\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
.

Fix
\sigma\in\Sigma
. For simplicity of notation we will assume without loss of generality that

(9.30)

\mathbf{e}_{1}(\sigma)=(1,0,0),\quad\mathbf{e}_{2}(\sigma)=(0,1,0),\quad\mathbf{e}_{3}(\sigma)=(0,0,1).

Also write

(9.31)

\mathbf{e}_{i}=(e_{i1},e_{i2},e_{i3}).

Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. Since we allow a loss of the form
\delta^{-\varepsilon}
, we may cut
[0,1]
into smaller intervals of length
\delta^{\varepsilon}
. By the triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities, it suffices to assume all angles
\theta,\omega,\sigma\in[0,\delta^{\varepsilon}]
. For the same reason, we may partition each
A_{\omega,\delta^{1/2}}
and assume
|\xi_{3}-1|\leq\delta^{\varepsilon}
on
A_{\omega,\delta^{1/2}}
.

The key is the following geometric observation. It is more complicated than the argument outlined in the proof of Theorem 11 in
[
GGG+22
]
, since we have chosen to perform the approximation directly, instead of first reducing to the form
\mathbf{e}_{3}(t)=(1,t,\phi(t))
for some
\phi\in C^{2}
.

Lemma 9.8
.

For each
\omega\prec\sigma
, the plank
A_{\omega,\delta^{1/2}}
is contained in the
O(\delta)
neighbourhood of the part of the cylinder

(9.32)

\displaystyle R_{\sigma,\omega,\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(e_{31}(\theta),e_{32}(\theta),\xi_{3}):|\theta-\omega|\lesssim\delta^{1/2},|\xi_{3}-1|\lesssim\delta^{\varepsilon}\right\}.

Proof of Lemma
9.8
.

Let
\xi=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\xi_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\omega)\in A_{\omega,\delta^{1/2}}
. It suffices to find
\theta,\xi^{\prime}_{3}
such that
|\xi-(e_{31}(\theta),e_{32}(\theta),\xi^{\prime}_{3})|\lesssim\lambda^{2}\delta^{\varepsilon}
.

We Taylor expand
\mathbf{e}_{3}
with respect to the centre
\omega
. Using (
9.29
),
|\xi_{1}|\lesssim\delta
and
|\omega-\theta|\leq\delta^{1/2}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\xi_{1}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\omega)-(e_{31}(\theta),e_{32}(\theta),\xi_{3}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\xi_{2}e_{21}(\omega)+(\xi_{3}-1)e_{31}(\omega)+e_{21}(\theta-\omega)+O(\delta),

\displaystyle\xi_{2}e_{22}(\omega)+(\xi_{3}-1)e_{32}(\omega)+e_{22}(\omega)(\theta-\omega)+O(\delta)

(9.33)

\displaystyle\xi_{2}e_{23}(\omega)+\xi_{3}e_{33}(\omega)-\xi_{3}^{\prime}+O(\delta)\Big).

We thus choose
\xi^{\prime}_{3}=\xi_{2}e_{23}(\omega)+\xi_{3}e_{33}(\omega)
, which satisfies
|\xi^{\prime}_{3}-1|\lesssim\delta^{\varepsilon}
since
|e_{33}(\omega)-1|=|e_{33}(\omega)-e_{33}(\sigma)|\lesssim\delta^{\varepsilon}
. This makes the third coordinate of (
9.33
) of the order
O(\delta)
.

Next, we put

(9.34)

\theta=\omega+\frac{\xi_{2}e_{22}(\omega)-(\xi_{3}-1)e_{32}(\omega)}{e_{22}(\omega)},

which satisfies
|\theta-\omega|\lesssim\delta^{1/2}
. Indeed, the denominator is
\sim 1
since
e_{22}(\omega)\sim 1
. For the numerator, we have
|\xi_{2}|\lesssim\delta^{1/2}
and
|\xi_{3}-1|\leq\delta^{\varepsilon}
, and using Lemma
9.30
and (
9.29
), we get

(9.35)

|e_{32}(\omega)|=|e_{32}(\omega)-e_{32}(\sigma)|\lesssim|\omega-\sigma|\leq\lambda,

and so
|\theta-\omega|\lesssim\delta^{\varepsilon}\lambda\leq\delta^{1/2}
. This makes the second coordinate of (
9.33
) of the order
O(\delta)
.

It remains to estimate the first coordinate. Using (
9.34
), the first coordinate of (
9.33
) is within
O(\delta)
of

(9.36)

e_{22}(\omega)^{-1}(\xi_{3}-1)(e_{31}(\omega)e_{22}(\omega)-e_{21}(\omega)e_{32}(\omega)).

Now we recall
|\omega-\sigma|\leq\lambda
, and use (
9.29
), Taylor expansion and (
9.30
) to get

(9.37)

e_{31}(\omega)=e_{31}(\sigma)+e_{21}(\sigma)(\omega-\sigma)+O(\lambda^{2})=O(\lambda^{2}).

Similarly, we also have
e_{32}(\omega)=O(\lambda^{2})
. Thus (
9.36
) is bounded above by
O(\lambda^{2})
, and so the first coordinate of (
9.33
) is of the order
O(\lambda^{2}\delta^{\varepsilon})=O(\delta)
.
∎

9.3.4.
Applying decoupling for
C^{2}
curves

The most important consequence of Lemma
9.8
is that if we project every plank
R_{\sigma,\omega,\lambda}
onto
\mathbf{e}_{3}(\sigma)^{\perp}
, then we are essentially in the case of decoupling for
C^{2}
curves as in Corollary
9.2
. This is a general philosophy of decoupling called the projection principle, namely, the decoupling constant for a family of subsets is dominated by the decoupling constant for the same family of subsets under any fixed orthogonal projection. Using Corollary
9.2
and noting the
O(1)
overlap, the result is that

(9.38)

\left\|f_{\sigma}\right\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon^{2}}\norm*{\norm{f_\omega}_{L^6(\R^3)}}_{\ell^{2}(\omega\prec\sigma)}.

The choice of
\delta^{-\varepsilon^{2}}
but not
\delta^{-\varepsilon}
is necessary for the bootstrap inequality in the next subsection to give the desired conclusion.

We give more details of the proof of (
9.38
) here. Fix
z\in\mathbb{R}
. For each
\omega\prec\sigma
, define
g_{z,\omega}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
by

(9.39)

\widehat{g_{z,\omega}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f_{\omega}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})e(z\xi_{3})d\xi_{3}.

Then by Lemma
9.8
,
\widehat{g_{z,\omega}}
is supported on the
O(\delta)
neighbourhood of

(9.40)

\{(e_{31}(\theta),e_{32}(\theta)):|\theta-\omega|\lesssim\delta^{1/2}\},

which has
O(1)
overlap, by the same proof of Lemma
5.5
as in Section
5.11
. The planar curve
(e_{31}(\theta),e_{32}(\theta))
is
C^{2}
and has curvature comparable to
1
. Thus we may apply Corollary
9.2
to the family
\{g_{z,\omega}:\omega\prec\sigma\}
to obtain

(9.41)

\left\|\sum_{\omega\prec\sigma}g_{z,\omega}\right\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon^{2}}\norm*{\norm{g_{z,\omega}}_{L^6(\R^2)}}_{\ell^{2}(\omega\prec\sigma)}.

The desired inequality (
9.38
) then follows by Fubini’s theorem and Minkowski’s inequality.

9.3.5.
Iteration

Combining (
9.26
) and (
9.38
), we thus get

(9.42)

\left\|\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}f_{\omega}\right\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq D(\lambda^{2})C_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon^{2}}\norm*{\norm{f_\omega}_{L^6(\R^3)}}_{\ell^{2}(\omega\in\Omega)},\quad\forall\varepsilon>0,

which, by definition of
D(\delta)
, implies the following bootstrap inequality:

(9.43)

D(\delta)\leq D(\delta^{1-\varepsilon})C_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon^{2}},\quad\forall\varepsilon>0.

It suffices to iterate this bootstrap inequality. For
\delta\geq 1/2
we trivially have
D(\delta)\sim 1
. If
\delta<1/2
, we let
N
be the largest integer such that
\delta^{(1-\varepsilon)^{N}}<1/2
