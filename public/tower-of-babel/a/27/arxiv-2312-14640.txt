The scaling limit of the root component in the Wired Minimal Spanning Forest of the Poisson Weighted Infinite Tree
Omer Angel, and, Delphin Sénizergues

Abstract
In this paper we prove a scaling limit result for the component of the root in the Wired Minimal Spanning Forest (WMSF) of the Poisson-Weighted Infinite Tree (PWIT), where the latter tree arises as the local weak limit of the Minimal Spanning Tree (MST) on the complete graph endowed with i.i.d. weights on its edges. The limiting object ℳ ∞ \mathcal{M}^{\infty} can be obtained by aggregating independent Brownian trees using two types of gluing procedures: one that we call the Brownian tree aggregation process and resembles the so-called stick-breaking construction of the Brownian tree ; and another one that we call the chain construction, which simply corresponds to gluing a sequence of metric spaces along a line.

Introduction

Let
(V,E,w)
be a finite, connected, weighted graph, where
(V,E)
is the underlying graph and
w:E\rightarrow\mathopen{[}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}
is the weight function.
A spanning tree of
(V,E)
is a
tree that is a subgraph of
(V,E)
with vertex set
V
.
A minimal spanning tree (MST)
T
of
(V,E,w)
satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in T}w(e)=\min\left\{\sum_{e\in T^{\prime}}w(e)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}T^{\prime}\text{ is a spanning tree of }(V,E)\right\}.

This combinatorial optimization problem has been extensively studied in computer science, combinatorics and probability.
We refer to
[
3
, Section 1.1]
for a overview of the probabilistic results related to the MST problem.

In this paper we place ourselves in the following random setting: we consider
K_{n}
the complete graph on
n
vertices and assign i.i.d. weights to the edges distributed as uniform random variables in
\mathopen{[}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},1\mathclose{]}
.
In that case the edge-weights are almost surely distinct so there almost surely exist a unique MST, which we call
\mathrm{M}_{n}
.
We are interested in some asymptotic properties of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
and, in particular, in different ways of defining a notion of limit for this object.

The scaling limit of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
.

For a measured metric space
(X,d,\mu)
, we denote by
\scale(a,b;X)
the space
X
with distances scaled by
a
and measure scaled by
b
.
In
[
3
]
, the authors consider
\mathrm{M}_{n}
as random measured metric space, by endowing its set of vertices with the graph distance and the counting measure.
They obtain the following scaling limit convergence.
We shall recall the definition of the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology later in Section
2.1
.

{theorem}
[Theorem 1.1 of
[
3
]
]
Seen as a measured metric space, we have the following convergence in the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology

\displaystyle\mathrm{Scale}(n^{-\frac{1}{3}},n^{-1};\mathrm{M}_{n})\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{comp}},

where the limiting object
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{comp}}
is a compact random tree that has Minkowski dimension equal to
3
almost surely.

The limiting tree appearing in the statement of the theorem is not constructed in an explicit way in
[
3
]
and remains quite mysterious.
This object is believed to be universal in the sense that it is expected to arise as the scaling limit of the MST of random graphs that exhibit mean field behavior when their edges are endowed with i.i.d. weights: random regular graphs, random graphs with given degree sequence, inhomogeneous graphs, high dimensional hypercubes and more.
One step in this direction is the work
[
6
]
of Addario-Berry and Sen where they rigorously prove such a result for the uniform
3
-regular graph.
In the recent preprint
[
12
]
, Broutin and Marckert present an explicit construction of
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{comp}}
as the
convex minorant tree
obtained from a trajectory of Brownian motion with parabolic drift, together with a sequence of independent uniform random variable
(U_{i})_{i\geq 1}
on
\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},1\mathclose{)}
, and refer to the object
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{comp}}
as the
parabolic Brownian tree
.
This new construction allows them to get more insight on the structure of the object, and in particular, it allows them to prove that the Hausdorff dimension of the object is
3
.

The local weak limit.

Another way of considering a limit of the object
M_{n}
is to look at what happens locally around a random vertex.
Some general theorem of Aldous and Steele
[
9
]
ensures that for the appropriate
weighted
local topology (one that accounts for the weights of the edges as well as the structure of the graph), if the underlying weighted graph converges locally in that topology towards a limit, then the corresponding MST converges as well towards the Wired Minimal Spanning Forest (WMSF) of the limiting graph.
In the case of
M_{n}
, the underlying graph
K_{n}
endowed with appropriate i.i.d. weights on the edges is known to converge to the Poisson-Weighted Infinite Tree (PWIT) and so the weighted local limit of
M_{n}
is the WMSF of the PWIT.

In
[
2
]
, the same convergence is proved in the specific case of the object
M_{n}
and the structure of the limit is investigated in more depth.
Last, in the recent paper
[
25
]
, Nachmias and Pang prove that under some mild conditions, which apply in our case, this convergence in the weighted local topology transfers to a convergence in the usual local topology when one forgets the weights.
Note that in this case the limiting object is only a tree instead of being a forest: any vertex that is not in the root component of the WMSF is at distance infinity from the root when using the usual graph distance.

{theorem}
[
[
9
,
2
,
25
]
]
We have the convergence

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{n}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}\mathrm{M}^{\infty},

in the local weak topology, where
\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
is the root component of the WMSF of the PWIT.

The limiting tree is shown in
[
2
]
to be one-ended and having roughly cubic volume growth. These results are refined in
[
25
]
, where the authors also prove results about the random walk on such a graph; in particular they prove that the spectral dimension and typical displacement exponent are almost surely respectively
\frac{3}{2}
and
\frac{1}{4}
.

Towards a non-compact scaling limit.

The goal of this paper is to introduce
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
, a continuous non-compact version of the object
\mathrm{M}_{n}
the MST of
K_{n}
.
This should be reminiscent of the case of the uniform spanning tree
T_{n}
of
K_{n}
, which is simply a uniform labeled tree on
n
vertices.
It is well-known in that case that three limiting objects can be associated to
T_{n}
: its scaling limit called the
Brownian CRT
(after rescaling the distances by
n^{1/2}
), its local weak limit which is an infinite discrete tree called the
Kesten tree
; and its non-compact scaling limit called the
infinite Brownian CRT
.
This non-compact limit arises as several different limits: the scaling limit of the Kesten tree, the local limit of the Brownian CRT (i.e. what we see in the CRT when we zoom in around a random point), and as the scaling limit of
T_{n}
when the distances are rescaled by a quantity
o(n^{1/2})
.

In our case, the goal is to introduce the corresponding continuous non-compact object
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
for
M_{n}
and prove one of these convergences, namely the analogue of the first one, i.e. that
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
is the scaling limit of
\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
, see Figure
1
.
The two other convergences will be studied in a separate paper.

First we describe the objects of study.
Let
\mathbb{U}
be the Ulam-Harris tree; this is the tree with vertex set

V(\mathbb{U})=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{N}^{k}
, and for each
k\geq 1
and each vertex
v=(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, an
edge between
v
and its parent
(n_{1},\dots,n_{k-1})
.
For each
v=(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})\in\mathbb{U}
, let
(x_{i},i\geq 1)
be the atoms of a
homogeneous rate one Poisson process on
\mathopen{[}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}
, and for each
i\geq 1
give the edge from
v

to its child
v_{i}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{k},i)
the weight
W({v,v_{i}})=x
.
Writing
W=\{W(e),e\in E(\mathbb{U})\}
,
where
E(\mathbb{U})
is the edge set of
\mathbb{U}
, the Poisson-weighted infinite tree is the tree
\mathbb{U}
rooted at
\emptyset
, endowed with the weight function
W
, which we write as a triple
(\mathbb{U},\emptyset,W)
.

Invasion percolation, forward maximal edges and ponds.

Invasion percolation (also called Prim’s algorithm) on a rooted locally finite weighted graph with distinct weights is defined as follows: the algorithm grows a component from the root of the graph, adding at each step the lowest weight edge leaving the current component.
When performed on a finite connected weighted graph, this procedure yields the minimal spanning tree of that graph.
Performed on an infinite graph however, it yields an infinite tree whose set of vertices is a proper subset of the original graph, which we call the invasion percolation cluster of the root.

We consider
\mathrm{T}^{\infty}
the invasion cluster of the root of the weighted rooted tree
(\mathbb{U},\emptyset,W)
.
It is known that
\mathrm{T}^{\infty}
is almost surely one-ended and that it admits a decomposition into ponds, by cutting at so-called
forward maximal edges
.
We say that an edge
e\in E(\mathrm{T}^{\infty})
is forward maximal if it disconnects the root
\emptyset
from infinity and is such that if there exists
e^{\prime}\in E(\mathrm{T}^{\infty})
such that
e
is in the path joining
\emptyset
and
e^{\prime}
then
W(e^{\prime})<W(e)
. In terms of the invasion percolation process described above, this means that any edge explored after
e
will have a smaller weight than
e
.

Denote by
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>\dots
the respective weights of the successive forward maximal edges
e_{1},e_{2},\dots
in the order in which they are discovered by the invasion percolation process, and denote by
\mathscr{A}=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots\}
the set of all their values.
We can remove those edges in order to disconnect
\mathrm{T}^{\infty}
into finite connected components that we call
ponds
.
We denote those ponds by
(\mathrm{T}^{\bullet}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}}
, by indexing them by the weight of the corresponding forward maximal edge exiting the component, which we call the
type
(called
activation time
in
[
25
]
) of the vertices belonging to the corresponding pond.
Here, each
\mathrm{T}^{\bullet}_{\alpha}
is then a finite rooted tree, rooted at its vertex closest to the root and pointed at the vertex from which the next forward maximal edge exits. See Figure
2
for an illustration.

Invasion percolation from every vertex.

For any vertex
u\in V(\mathbb{U})
,
we can repeat the procedure above
from vertex
u
by just considering
T(u)
the invasion percolation cluster of the rooted weighted tree
\mathbb{U}(u)=(U,u,W)
, the PWIT re-rooted at vertex
u
.
Let
\widetilde{\mathrm{M}}
be the union of all the trees
(T(u),u\in V(\mathbb{U}))
.
The forest
\widetilde{\mathrm{M}}
is the so-called Wired Minimal Spanning Forest of the PWIT.
Now the tree
\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
, which we study in this paper, is defined as the connected component of the forest
\widetilde{\mathrm{M}}
containing the root
\emptyset
of
\mathbb{U}
.
It is almost surely one-ended, see
[
2
, Corollary 7.2]
.

The tree
\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
of course contains
\mathrm{T}^{\infty}=T(\emptyset)
, the invasion percolation cluster of the root.
Consider a vertex
u\in V(\mathbb{U})
that is such that
u\notin\mathrm{T}^{\infty}
and its invasion percolation cluster
T(u)
.
The tree
T(u)
can be disconnected along its own forward maximal edges in the same way as
T(\emptyset)
.
Now if we suppose that
u\in\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
then the facts that
T(\emptyset)
and
T(u)
are one-ended, as well as
\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
, imply that the ponds and forward maximal edges of
T(\emptyset)
and
T(u)
coincide except for a finite number of them: this ensures that the invasion percolation process from vertex
u
visits a finite number of ponds before starting to explore one of the ponds
(\mathrm{T}^{\bullet}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}}
.
In order to avoid confusion, for
u\in\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
, we call any of the forward maximal edges of
T(u)
that is not a forward maximal edge of
T(\emptyset)
a
back-edge
.
Any pond of
T(u)
that does not overlap with
\mathrm{T}^{\infty}
is denoted by
T_{\beta}
, where
\beta
is the weight of the first back-edge visited by invasion percolation started from that vertex.
Again we say that all the vertices in
T_{\beta}
have type
\beta
.

Now, consider
\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
and as before we disconnect it along the forward maximal edges
e_{1},e_{2}\dots
of
\mathrm{T}^{\infty}
.
We denote by
(\mathrm{M}^{\bullet}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}}
the obtained sequence of connected components.
Each of those component
\mathrm{M}^{\bullet}_{\alpha}
can actually be decomposed again into the union of
\mathrm{T}^{\bullet}_{\alpha}
and a set of trees
T_{\beta}
connected to each other along a tree structure by back-edges.
In fact,
\mathrm{M}^{\bullet}_{\alpha}
can be obtained from
\mathrm{T}^{\bullet}_{\alpha}
by running the so-called Poisson–Galton–Watson Aggregation Process which we recall in Section
3
.
The idea of this paper is to define a similar structure in the continuum and prove that it is indeed the scaling limit of
\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
, resp.
\mathrm{T}^{\infty}
.

Summary of the results

We define an infinite one-ended continuum random tree
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
jointly with
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
, which are the continuous analogues of respectively
\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
and

Joint construction of
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
.

We first explain how we can describe the object
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
from another object that we denote by
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
, which we then discuss below.
This is slightly analogous to the construction of Brownian motion by chaining of excursions sampled from the Ito measure, though in our construction each component has another one immediately following it (among other differences).
Let

•

\mathscr{P}
be a Poisson Point Process on
\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}
with intensity
\frac{1}{t}\mathrm{d}t
,

•

\left((\mathcal{T}^{\bullet}_{x},\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}_{x}),\ x\in\mathscr{P}\right)
a collection of random variables, where conditionally on
\mathscr{P}
they are independent and we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}^{\bullet}_{x},\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}_{x})\overset{(d)}{=}\left(\mathrm{Scale}(x^{-1},x^{-2};\mathcal{T}^{\bullet}),\ \mathrm{Scale}(x^{-1},x^{-3};\mathcal{M}^{\bullet})\right),

where
(\mathcal{T}^{\bullet},\mathcal{M}^{\bullet})
is a couple of random pointed rooted measured trees such that
\mathcal{T}^{\bullet}\subset\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
that we will describe in more details later on.

Then
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
is constructed from the sequence
(\mathcal{M}_{x}^{\bullet})_{x\in\mathscr{P}}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Chain}(\mathcal{M}_{x}^{\bullet},\ x\in\mathscr{P}).

The tree
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}
is constructed in a similar manner from
(\mathcal{T}_{x}^{\bullet})_{x\in\mathscr{P}}
and can be seen as a subset of
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
.
The
\mathrm{Chain}
construction will be defined in detail in Section
2
, but essentially consists in taking all the sequence of rooted pointed trees
(\mathcal{M}_{x}^{\bullet})_{x\in\mathscr{P}}
and gluing them head to tail in decreasing order of
x
-value.
The resulting space is completed by adding an extra point
\rho_{\infty}
which is characterized by
\rho_{\infty}:=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty,x\in\mathscr{P}}\rho_{x}
, where for any
x\in\mathscr{P}
, the point
\rho_{x}
denotes the common root of
\mathcal{T}^{\bullet}_{x}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}_{x}
.
The obtained space is an unbounded complete measured tree, rooted at
\rho_{\infty}
.

Notice the similar structure to that of
(\mathrm{T}^{\infty},\mathrm{M}^{\infty})
: the point process
\mathscr{P}
corresponds to the set
\mathscr{A}
of weights of forward maximal edges, the trees
(\mathcal{T}^{\bullet}_{x},\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}_{x})
for
x\in\mathscr{P}
take the place of the
(\mathrm{T}_{\alpha}^{\bullet},\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}^{\bullet})
for
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}
,
and the Chain construction mimics the way that we can get
\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
and
\mathrm{T}^{\infty}
from linking all the
(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}^{\bullet})_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}}
(resp. the
(\mathrm{T}_{\alpha}^{\bullet})_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}}
) using the forward maximal edges.

Convergence results.

Before discussing the construction of
(\mathcal{T}^{\bullet},\mathcal{M}^{\bullet})
, which are the building blocks of
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
, we state our scaling limit convergence result.
The result below is expressed in the local Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology, the definition of which is recalled in Section
2
.

{theorem}

The tree
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
is the scaling limit of
\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
for the local Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov topology.
More precisely

\displaystyle\mathrm{Scale}\left(r,\frac{r^{3}}{C_{0}};\mathrm{M}^{\infty}\right)\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{M}^{\infty},

where the constant
C_{0}
is defined later in (
47
). The above convergence happens jointly with

\displaystyle\mathrm{Scale}\left(r,r^{2};\mathrm{T}^{\infty}\right)\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{T}^{\infty}.

Note that invasion percolation on a
d
-regular tree and its scaling limit were investigated in
[
11
]
.
While the behavior does not depend significantly on
d
, the extension to the PWIT was only investigated later in
[
5
]
.
In
[
11
]
, the tree generated by the invasion percolation process started at the root is studied using its encoding via functions such as the contour function, height function or Lukasiewicz path.
The scaling limit is then described as well by its encoding by a height function that is described as the solution to some stochastic differential equation.
Heuristically, the obtained tree should have the same distribution as
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}
, up to some deterministic scaling, even though our description can seem quite different as first glance.

Construction of
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
from
\mathcal{T}^{\bullet}
: the Brownian tree aggregation process.

We now describe in more details the distribution of the pair
(\mathcal{T}^{\bullet},\mathcal{M}^{\bullet})
used to construct
\mathcal{T}^{\infty}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
.
They are seen as (nested) random metric spaces, endowed with a common root
\rho
and distinguished point
v
, and measures (one for each) that are not necessarily normalized to be probability measures.

First,
\mathcal{T}^{\bullet}
has the distribution of a Brownian tree with random mass
X
, where
X\overset{(d)}{=}\sqrt{2}\cdot\Gamma
where
\Gamma
has distribution
\mathrm{Gamma}(\frac{1}{2})
, endowed with a randomly chosen point
v
(on top of being rooted at some point
\rho
).
The construction of
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
from
\mathcal{T}^{\bullet}
then uses a process that we call the
Brownian tree aggregation process
(which is analogous to the Poisson-Galton-Watson aggregation process used in the discrete setting to describe the construction of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}^{\bullet}
for any
\alpha>1
).
A useful analogy for the reader is the stick-breaking construction of the CRT, where line segments of random lengths are glued at uniform points on the union of all past segments.
However, unlike that process,
there are infinitely many attached trees being added in any time interval.

Here is a first intuitive way of understanding the construction of
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
.
This is a growth process for a tree, started at time
t=1
(which turns out to be more convenient) with the
seed

\mathcal{T}^{\bullet}
.
Immediately after time
t=1
, independent Brownian trees arrive and are attached to the existing tree by having their root identified to a point already present.
At time
t
, attachments of trees of mass
y
to an element of mass
\mathrm{d}m
of the current structure arrive at rate

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}y^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2}y\right)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}m,

(1)

where each corresponding tree of mass
y
is a Brownian tree with the given mass (conditionally on all the rest).
The object
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
is the limit as
t\to\infty
of the process defined above, still rooted and pointed at respectively
\rho
and
v
.

The way that we define such a process formally deviates slightly from this intuition, and start with the process giving the total mass of the tree at time
t
.
We would like to define a pure jump Markov process
(\mathcal{W}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
in terms of its generator.
Since this Markov process is inhomogeneous in time, we actually give the generator of the process
((t,\mathcal{W}_{t}))_{t\geq 1}
, which is time-homogeneous.
The generator
\mathbf{A}
of the process
(t,\mathcal{W}_{t})
is so that for any smooth and compactly supported function
f:(0,\infty)\times\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}f(t,x)=\partial_{t}f(t,x)+x\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(f(t,x+y)-f(t,x)\right)\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2}y\right)}{y^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathrm{d}y.

(2)

This process represent the evolution of the total mass that is present at time
t
.
The process
(\mathcal{W}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is a subordinator and a pure jump process, and so has countably many jumps.
For every jump time
t
of the process, take an independent Brownian tree

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{t}

with mass given by the size of the jump
w_{t}:=\mathcal{W}_{t}-\mathcal{W}_{t-}
.
For every such times
t
, a point
X_{t}
is sampled proportionally to the mass measure on

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\bullet}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{1<s<t}\mathcal{T}_{t}.

(3)

The tree at time
t
is obtained by gluing the root of every
\mathcal{T}_{s}
, for all jump times
s\leq t
of the process
\mathcal{W}
, to the corresponding point
X_{s}
.
For
t=\infty
, we take the completion of the obtained space.
The fact that this construction makes sense and almost surely yields a compact metric space is proved in a more general setting in the corresponding section.

We should also mention how the measure on
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
is obtained:
The total mass of the tree constructed up to time
t
is
\mathcal{W}_{t}
and tends to infinity.
The measure on
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
arises as the scaling limit as
t\to\infty
of the mass-measure on the trees that have index less than
t
.
As it turns out, the correct normalization is
\frac{1}{t}
.
The measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
is defined as
\mu:=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\mu_{t}
, where
\mu_{t}
is the measure on the tree at time
t
, and the limit is a weak limit of measures.
The limiting measure on
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
has total mass given by

\displaystyle Y:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{W}_{t}}{t}.

More about that in Section
3
.

Thanks to the construction of
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
as an aggregation process, we can determine some of its geometric properties.

{theorem}

The random metric space
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
is well-defined as a random variable in
\mathbb{L}^{\bullet,c}
. Furthermore

(i)

Its mass and diameter have moments of all orders.

(ii)

It almost surely has Minkowski and Hausdorff dimension
3
.

(iii)

Its measure is almost surely non-zero, diffuse and has full support.

Note that, as a result, the non-compact random metric space
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
which is a countable union of copies of
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
also has Hausdorff dimension
3
and diffuse mass measure with full support.

1.3
Organization of this paper

We start in Section
2
by recalling some facts about the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology and introducing the two constructions called
\mathrm{Chain}
and aggregation process (
\mathrm{Aggreg}
) respectively.
These constructions are particularly important because we interpret all our objects in this framework.
In particular, we state some sufficient conditions for the convergence of such structures.
In Section
3
we then use these constructions to rigorously construct the trees
\mathcal{M}^{\bullet}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
. In particular, we prove Theorem
1.2
in Section
3.1
.
Then in Section
4
, we study the tree
\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
and decompose it in a way that use again the
\mathrm{Chain}
and
\mathrm{Aggreg}
constructions in an analogous way.

Constructions and convergences

In this section, we introduce two constructions from sequences of measured metric space. One of them is fairly simple and consists in linking a sequence of metric spaces in a linear manner: we call it the
chain
construction.
The other one is slightly more involved and consists of gluing some ordered family of metric spaces on top of each other in a random way: this is called an
aggregation process
. It is a natural extension of the so called "line-breaking" construction of random trees
[
7
,
8
,
16
]
, already extended to more general metric spaces
[
17
,
27
]
, where we do not constrain the blocks that we glue together to arrive in a discrete order anymore.

We state some properties of these two constructions (in particular convergence results) and we explain how to interpret our objects of interest in this setting.
These results will be the base of the rest of our arguments.

In this section we only expose results and give an idea of how they are used later in the paper.
The proofs of the results stated here will be postponed to the Appendix.

2.1
Reminder about the pointed GH and GHP topologies and local versions

In this section, we give a brief reminder about the definition of the Hausdorff, Gromov–Hausdorff and Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topologies on compact metric spaces, and their local equivalent on boundedly finite rooted metric spaces. We will mainly follow the exposition of
[
1
]
.
This section can be skipped at first reading.

Let
(X,d^{X})
be a Polish metric space and
{\mathcal{B}}(X)
be its set of Borel measurable subsets.
The diameter of

\diam(A)=\sup\left\{d^{X}(x,y)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x,y\in A\right\}.

For
A,B\in{\mathcal{B}}(X)

d_{\text{H}}^{X}(A,B)=\inf\{\epsilon>0;\ A\subset B^{\epsilon}\ \mathrm{and}\ B\subset A^{\epsilon}\},

the Hausdorff distance between
A
and
B

A^{\epsilon}=\left\{x\in X\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\inf_{y\in A}d^{X}(x,y)<\epsilon\right\}

(4)

is the
\epsilon

If

is compact, then the space of
compact subsets of
X

Let

denote the set of all finite Borel measures on
X

d_{\text{P}}^{X}(\mu,\nu)=\inf\left\{\epsilon>0\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mu(A)\leq\nu(A^{\epsilon})+\epsilon\text{ and }\nu(A)\leq\mu(A^{\epsilon})+\epsilon\ \text{ for any closed set }A\right\},

the Prokhorov distance between
\mu
and
\nu
.
It is well known, that

is a Polish metric space, and that the

is exactly the topology of weak

The notion of Prokhorov distance can be extended in the following way.
A Borel measure is said to be locally finite if the measure of any
bounded Borel set is finite.
Let
\mathrm{Mes}(X)

denote the set of all locally finite Borel measures on
X
. Let

\rho
be a distinguished element of
X
, which we call the

We consider the closed ball of radius

centered at

X^{(R)}=\left\{x\in X\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}d^{X}(\rho,x)\leq R\right\},

(5)

and for

its restriction

to

\mu^{(R)}(dx)=\mathbf{1}_{X^{(R)}}(x)\;\mu(dx).

(6)

If

and

d_{\text{gP}}^{X}(\mu,\nu)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp{-R}\left(1\wedge d^{X}_{\text{P}}\left(\mu^{(R)},\nu^{(R)}\right)\right)\ \differential R.

(7)

The function

is well defined and is a

Furthermore
(\mathrm{Mes}(X),d_{\text{gP}}^{X})
is a Polish metric
space, and the topology generated by

is exactly the
topology of vague convergence.
When there is no ambiguity on the metric space
(X,d^{X})
, we may write
d
,

, and
d_{\text{P}}
instead of

,

and

If

is a Borel-measurable map between two Polish metric

\Phi_{*}\mu

the image measure on

defined by

for any Borel set

.

2.1.1
Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov metric for compact spaces

Let

and

be two compact metric spaces. The Gromov-Hausdorff metric

and

and
\varphi^{\prime}:X^{\prime}\hookrightarrow Z

Now, we introduce the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov distance for compact

Let

(9)

\Phi:X\hookrightarrow Z
and
\Phi^{\prime}:X^{\prime}\hookrightarrow Z

common Polish metric space
(Z,d^{Z})

and

[Theorem 2.5 of

We sometimes need to endow our spaces with extra structure, like an additional distinguished point for example.

2.2
The chain construction

In this section, we present a construction that takes a family of compact measured metric spaces and glues them sequentially along a path.

2.2.1
Construction

Suppose that we are given a discrete set
\mathscr{N}\subset\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}
and a family
(\mathsf{L}_{t}^{\bullet},t\in\mathscr{N})
of rooted pointed measured compact length spaces indexed by the set
\mathscr{N}
, where

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}_{t}^{\bullet}=(L_{t},d_{t},\rho_{t},v_{t},\nu_{t}).

We would like to make sense of the object obtained by linking every
\mathsf{L}_{t}^{\bullet}
by identifying the distinguished point
v_{t}
to the root
\rho_{t^{\prime}}
of the next block in ascending order.
For the result to be well defined we make the following assumptions.

Assume that

(Ch1)

For any
A>0
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\mathscr{N}\cap\mathopen{(}A\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}}d_{t}(\rho_{t},v_{t})<\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{t\in\mathscr{N}\cap\mathopen{(}A\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}}\nu_{t}(L_{t})<\infty

(Ch2)

We have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathscr{N}\cap\mathopen{(}A\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}}\diam(\mathsf{L}_{t}^{\bullet})\underset{A\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}0.

(Ch3)

For any
\epsilon>0
, one of the two following conditions is satisfied:

(Ch3a)

\sum_{t\in\mathscr{N}\cap\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\epsilon\mathclose{)}}d_{t}(\rho_{t},v_{t})=\infty
,

(Ch3b)

\mathscr{N}\cap\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\epsilon\mathclose{)}
is finite.

We define
L_{\infty}=\{\rho_{\infty}\}
and consider

\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{t\in\mathscr{N}\cup\{\rho_{\infty}\}}L_{t}

which we endow with the pseudo-distance
d
defined as

d(x,y)=d(y,x)=\begin{cases}d_{t}(x,y)&\text{if }x,y\in L_{t},\\
d_{t}(x,v_{t})+\displaystyle\sum_{r\in\mathscr{N}\cap(s,t)}d_{r}(\rho_{r},v_{r})+d_{s}(\rho_{s},y)&\text{if }(x,y)\in L_{s}\times L_{t},\ t<s.\end{cases}

(10)

Then

is defined as

\displaystyle\left(\bigsqcup_{t\in\mathscr{N}\cup\{\rho_{\infty}\}}L_{t}\right)/\sim

where

is the equivalence relation generated by

if

.
The space
\mathrm{Chain}(\mathsf{L}_{t}^{\bullet},t\in\mathscr{N})
is seen as a rooted measured metric space after endowing the space with the projection of the distance
d
and the measure
\sum_{t\in\mathscr{N}}\nu_{t}
and the root
\rho_{\infty}

\thetheorem

(Ch2)

(Ch3)

A
.

(Ch3)

2.3
Aggregation processes

The second construction that we will use is that of
aggregation processes
.
In this case, we create a metric space by gluing together a (potentially infinite) collection of measured metric spaces
\left(\mathsf{B}_{t},\ t\in\mathscr{J}\right)
in a
random
manner.
The reader familiar with the stick-breaking construction of the CRT may wish to keep that in mind as a useful analogy.
Indeed, the stick breaking construction can be defined as a special case of an aggregation process as defined here.
However, unlike the stick breaking construction, here it is possible that infinitely many blocks are being glued within a finite time interval.
(For any
t<\infty
he blocks used up to time
t
still have finite total mass.)

2.3.1
Construction

Since the definition of this process is a bit more involved, we express it for a particular case where the blocks
\left(\mathsf{B}_{t},t\in\mathscr{J}\right)
that we glue together are random and satisfy some
ad hoc
assumptions that will be satisfied by the objects studied in this paper.
We start with

•

a
weight process

(W_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
, which is a càdlàg increasing pure-jump process, such that
W_{t_{0}}>0
, (we denote by
\mathscr{J}
its set of jump times),

•

a
seed

\mathsf{S}^{\bullet}=(S,\rho,v,d_{S},\nu_{S})

which is a compact, rooted, pointed, measured length space (

an element of
\mathbb{L}^{\bullet,c}
) and has total mass equal to

.

•

a

\eta=\left(\eta(t,w):\ t>t_{0},w>0\right)

, an object
\mathsf{B}(t,w)
sampled under the measure
\eta(t,w)
almost surely has mass

.

From these, we construct below some rooted (pointed) random metric space

\displaystyle\mathrm{Aggreg}((W_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}},\mathsf{S}^{\bullet},\eta).

For each jump time

we let

and we let

.
Then we sample a random

\eta(t,w_{t})
, independently for all
t\in\mathscr{J}
.
Then the object of interest in constructed from the collection
\left(\mathsf{B}_{t},t\in\mathscr{J}\right)

\displaystyle S\sqcup\bigsqcup_{t>t_{0}}B_{t},

by the appropriate identification of pairs of points:
For every jump time
t\in\mathscr{J}

X_{t}

\bigsqcup_{t_{0}\leq s<t}B_{s}

Define the equivalence relation

for each

The object of interest is then the

\displaystyle(A_{\infty},\rho,v,d):=\overline{\left(\mathsf{S}^{\bullet}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{t>t_{0}}\mathsf{B}_{t}\right)/\sim}

[

Intuition behind this description of

3.3
A priori estimates on the weight process

For any measurable subset

we define

\displaystyle M^{\mathscr{Q}}(E):=\sum_{(s,m,y)\in\mathscr{Q}}y\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{(s,m,y)\in E\}}.

(24)

In the time-space-mass interpretation of the construction appearing at the end of Section

corresponds to the total mass of points present in the set

We are interested in this quantity because of the result stated below, which states that if the process
\mathcal{W}

Lemma
\thetheorem
.

For two different times
t<t^{\prime}
and any two constants
A,B>0
, on the event where

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{t}\leq A\qquad\text{and}\qquad M^{\mathscr{Q}}(\mathopen{(}t\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t^{\prime}\mathclose{]}\times\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},A+B\mathclose{]}\times\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)})<B

we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{t^{\prime}}<A+B.

Proof.

Assume that we are on the event where the first condition is satisfied and where
\mathcal{W}_{t^{\prime}}>A+B
.
Then there exists some
t^{\prime\prime}=\inf\left\{t\leq s\leq t^{\prime}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{W}_{s}\geq A+B\right\}
. We then have

\displaystyle A+B\leq\mathcal{W}_{t^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}_{t}+\sum_{(s,m,y)\in\mathscr{Q}}y\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{m<\mathcal{W}_{s-},\ t<s\leq t^{\prime\prime}\}}

\displaystyle\leq A+M^{\mathscr{Q}}(\mathopen{(}t\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t^{\prime}\mathclose{]}\times\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},A+B\mathclose{]}\times\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)})<A+B,

which is a contradiction, so the event that we considered in the first place is empty.
This concludes the proof by contradiction.
∎

Using Campbell’s theorem for Poisson point processes (see for example

\lambda\ll t^{2}\end{subarray}}{=}\frac{\lambda}{t}+O\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{t^{3}}\right)

Proof.

We just replace

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda y)^{k}}{k!}\right)\cdot\frac{e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}y}}{y^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\pi}}\differential{y}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{W}_{t}^{2}-\mathcal{W}_{s}^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]

Recall the definition of

In what follows, we describe the tree

First, we have to recall a few fact about Poisson–Galton–Watson trees and their probability of being infinite.

4.1.1
Around the probability of extinction of Poisson–Galton–Watson trees

For any
\lambda\geq 1
we write
\theta(\lambda)=\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{\PGW(\lambda)}=\infty\right)
. The dual parameter
\lambda^{*}
is such that
\lambda e^{-\lambda}=\lambda^{*}e^{-\lambda^{*}}
.
We state a few useful facts about those quantities:

•

The value
\theta=\theta(\lambda)
is defined by the equation
1-\theta=e^{\lambda\theta}
.

•

For any
\lambda>1
we have
\lambda^{*}=\lambda(1-\theta(\lambda))
.

•

For any
\lambda>1
we have

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{\theta(\lambda)(1-\theta(\lambda))}{1-\lambda^{*}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\theta^{\prime}(\lambda)\underset{\lambda\rightarrow 1}{\rightarrow}2.

(27)

•

For
\epsilon>0
we have

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)^{*}=1-\epsilon+\frac{2}{3}\epsilon^{2}+O(\epsilon^{3}).

(28)

•

For any
\lambda>1
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1-\theta(\lambda)}{1-\lambda^{*}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda-1}.

(29)

•

For any
\lambda>1
we have

\displaystyle(\lambda-1)\frac{1-\theta(\lambda)}{(1-\lambda^{*})^{3}}\leq\frac{1}{(\lambda-1)^{2}}

(30)

The first four facts can be found in
[
25
]
and
[
31
, Corollary 3.19]
.
We only prove (
29
), since (
30
) is obtained is a very similar way.

Proof of (
29
).

For
\theta=\theta(\lambda)
, since
1-\theta=e^{\lambda\theta}
we have
\lambda=\frac{\log(1-\theta)}{\theta}
.
Then since
\lambda^{*}=\lambda(1-\theta)
we have
\lambda^{*}=\frac{(1-\theta)\log(1-\theta)}{\theta}
.
Proving the inequality (
29
) can then be reduced to proving

\displaystyle\frac{1-\theta}{1-\frac{(1-\theta)\log(1-\theta)}{\theta}}\leq\frac{1}{\frac{\log(1-\theta)}{\theta}-1},

which in turns corresponds to

\displaystyle 2(1-\theta)\log(1-\theta)-2\theta+\theta^{2}\leq 0.

Now we can just check that the right-hand-side is
0
for
\theta=0
and the derivative in
\theta
is negative for
\theta\in\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},1\mathclose{)}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Description as a branching process with types.

We can describe the trees
\mathrm{T}^{\infty}
and
\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
using a branching process in discrete time.
This branching process considers individuals with types in
\mathopen{(}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}
.
Additionally, individuals can be normal or special and there are two types of reproduction events that happen simultaneously: giving birth to individuals of smaller or equal type, and adopting individuals of larger type.

•

A normal individual of type
\alpha

–

gives birth to a Poisson number
\Poi(\alpha^{*})
of normal individuals of type
\alpha

–

This special child has the same type
\alpha

and has a different type with probability
(1-\alpha^{*})
.
In that case its type is taken in the interval
\mathopen{(}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\alpha\mathclose{)}

\mathrm{M}^{\infty}

\mathopen{(}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}
.
The tree
\mathrm{T}^{\infty}\subset\mathrm{M}^{\infty}
corresponds to the tree obtained by removing the adoption mechanism in this process.

. This process is intimately linked with a Poisson Point Process of intensity

, as the next lemma asserts.

Lemma
\thetheorem
.

The set of values
\mathscr{P}:=\left\{\mathcal{L}(t)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}t\in\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}\right\}

\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}

This fact comes easily from the characterization (

), the fact that

conditionally on the trajectory on the interval

4.4
Convergence of the seed
\mathrm{Scale}(r,r^{2};\mathrm{T}^{\bullet}_{1+r})

From Section
4.1.2
, a way to sample
\mathrm{T}^{\bullet}_{\alpha}
is to first sample a random variable
X_{\alpha}
with the Borel–Tanner distribution with parameter
\alpha
, which is such that for all
m\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{\alpha}=m\right)=\frac{\theta(\alpha)}{\theta^{\prime}(\alpha)}\frac{e^{-\alpha m}(\alpha m)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!},

and then let
\mathrm{T}^{\bullet}_{\alpha}
be a PGW tree conditioned to have
X_{\alpha}
vertices.
The vertex
v_{\alpha}
is then uniformly picked in
\mathrm{T}^{\bullet}_{\alpha}
.
Using Sterling’s approximation we have, for
m\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle m!=\sqrt{2\pi m}\cdot\left(\frac{m}{e}\right)^{m}\cdot(1+o(1)).

Then, for any
x\in\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}
and
r\rightarrow 0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1+r}=\lfloor xr^{-2}\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta(1+r)}{\theta^{\prime}(1+r)}\frac{e^{-r\lfloor xr^{-2}\rfloor}}{r^{-1}\sqrt{2\pi x}}(1+r)^{\lfloor xr^{-2}\rfloor}\cdot(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{x}{2}\right)\cdot(1+o(1))

and this proves that

\displaystyle r^{2}\cdot X_{1+r}\overset{(d)}{\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\rightarrow}}X

(38)

where
X
has density
\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{x}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\mathrm{d}x
on
\mathopen{(}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}
, which is the density of
\sqrt{2}\cdot G
where
G
is
\mathrm{Gamma}(\frac{1}{2})
-distributed. In fact the above convergence also takes place in
L^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]=1
and
\mathrm{Var}\left(X\right)=2
.

From there and from known results about the convergence of
\PGW
trees conditioned on their size towards to Brownian tree, it is easy to check that we have the convergence

\displaystyle\mathrm{Scale}(r,r^{2};\mathrm{T}^{\bullet}_{1+r})\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{T}^{\bullet},

(39)

where
\mathcal{T}^{\bullet}
is a Brownian tree with mass
\sqrt{2}\cdot\mathrm{Gamma}(\frac{1}{2})
-distributed, pointed at a point sampled at random using a normalized version of its weight measure.

4.5
Properties and convergence of the discrete weight process

4.5.1
Definition of the weight process as a Markov process

Recall the definition of the PGWA process started from at time
\alpha
from some seed tree
S_{\alpha}
.
The aggregate starts at
A(\alpha)=S_{\alpha}
and at any time
s\geq\alpha
, growing event happen at each vertex of the aggregate at rate
(1-\theta(s))
.
Whenever an growing event happens, a tree with distribution
\PGW(s^{*})
is attached to the corresponding vertex.
From this description, it is easy to check that the weight process
(W(s))_{s\geq\alpha}
defined as

W(s):=\#\{\text{vertices in $A(s)$ at time }s\},

is then a time-inhomogeneous Markov process such that at time
s
, a jump happens with rate
W(s)\cdot(1-\theta(s))
and the size of the corresponding jump is distributed as the number of vertices of a
\PGW(s^{*})
-distributed tree.
This entails that the process
((s,W(s))_{s\geq\alpha}
is a time-homogeneous Markov process with generator
A
characterized by the fact that, given any compactly supported smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle Af(s,x)

\displaystyle=\partial_{s}f+x\cdot(1-\theta(s))\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(f(s,x+k)-f(s,x)\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{\PGW(s^*)}=k\right).

(40)

Note that, by construction, this process satisfies the so-called
branching property
:
if we denote by
\left(W(s)\right)_{s\geq\alpha}
and
\left(W^{\prime}(s)\right)_{s\geq\alpha}
two independent processes with the same transitions as above, started from respectively
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, then their sum
\left(W(s)+W^{\prime}(s)\right)_{s\geq\alpha}
would still be a Markov process with the same transitions, started at
k_{1}+k_{2}
.

In the rest of the section, we will use
(W(s))_{s\geq\alpha}
to denote a general version of this Markov process started at time
\alpha
and write
(W^{\alpha}(s))_{s\geq\alpha}
for the version of this process started at time
\alpha
from the value
X_{\alpha}
, the number of vertices of
\mathrm{T}_{\alpha}^{\bullet}
, which is the version of the process that appears in the construction of
\mathrm{M}^{\bullet}_{\alpha}
, see Section
4.1.2
.

Moment computations

In order to compute the moments, we will follow the same strategy as in the proof of Lemma
3.4
,
and rely on the fact that for the functions

Expectation of

We follow the same steps as in the proof of Lemma

We get, using the same steps,

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[W(s)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right]\frac{(1-\theta(s))}{1-s^{*}}.

Integrating the above differential equation yields

Since the right-hand side tends to a finite limit as

we can write, denoting
W(\infty)
as the limit of the increasing process

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W(\infty)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right]=W(\alpha)\cdot\exp\left(\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\theta(\lambda))}{1-\lambda^{*}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right).

(43)

Assuming that

\displaystyle\exp\left(\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\theta(\lambda))}{1-\lambda^{*}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{\alpha}^{2}\frac{1}{\lambda-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)\cdot\exp\left(\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\theta(\lambda))}{1-\lambda^{*}}-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda\leq 2\}}}{\lambda-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\cdot\exp\left(\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\theta(\lambda))}{1-\lambda^{*}}-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda\leq 2\}}}{\lambda-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right),

(44)

where the last integrand is integrable over
\mathopen{[}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}
and so the second term in the above product is bounded above by the value

and converges to that value when

Remark
\thetheorem
.

From the computations above, we also get that for any

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{W(s)}{s-1}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\beta)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{W(\beta)}{\beta-1}\cdot\frac{\beta-1}{s-1}\cdot\exp\left(\int_{\beta}^{s}\frac{1-\theta(\lambda)}{1-\lambda^{*}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{W(\beta)}{\beta-1}\cdot\exp\left(\int_{\beta}^{s}\frac{1-\theta(\lambda)}{1-\lambda^{*}}-\frac{1}{\lambda-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right).

Since from (

Second moment of
W(s)
.

We apply the same method as above with

.
We use in the computation below the fact that for any parameter

the second moment for the size of a Poisson–Galton–Watson tree with parameter

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(W(s)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left[\left(W(s)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right](1-\theta(s))\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{\PGW(s^*)}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[W(s)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right](1-\theta(s))\mathbb{E}\left[\absolutevalue{\PGW(s^*)}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left[\left(W(s)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right]\frac{1-\theta(s)}{1-s^{*}}+\mathbb{E}\left[W(s)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right]\frac{1-\theta(s)}{(1-s^{*})^{3}}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(W(s)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right]=\exp\left(2\int_{\alpha}^{s}\frac{(1-\theta(\lambda))}{1-\lambda^{*}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)\cdot\left((W(\alpha))^{2}+\int_{\alpha}^{s}\mathbb{E}\left[W(\lambda)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right]\frac{1-\theta(\lambda)}{(1-\lambda^{*})^{3}}\ \differential\lambda\right)

and again, the above display has a limit when

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(W(\infty)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(2\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\theta(\lambda))}{1-\lambda^{*}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)\cdot\left((W(\alpha))^{2}+\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[W(\lambda)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right]\frac{1-\theta(\lambda)}{(1-\lambda^{*})^{3}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right).

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(W(\infty)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(2\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\theta(\lambda))}{1-\lambda^{*}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)\cdot\int_{\alpha}^{s}\mathbb{E}\left[W(\lambda)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right]\frac{1-\theta(\lambda)}{(1-\lambda^{*})^{3}}\ \differential\lambda

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(W(\infty)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W(\alpha)\right)\leq\frac{(C_{0})^{2}W(\alpha)}{(\alpha-1)^{4}}.

(46)

4.5.3
Convergence of the weight process

We introduce the following rescaled version of the process

\displaystyle\left(\mathsf{W}^{r}(t)\right)_{t\geq 1}=\left(r^{2}\cdot W^{1+r}(1+rt)\right)_{t\geq 1}.

We are going to compare this process to the process
(\mathcal{W}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
and we recall that
Y
is defined as the almost sure limit
Y:=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{W}_{t}}{t}
.

{proposition}

We have the joint convergence in distribution

\displaystyle\left(\left(\mathsf{W}^{r}(t)\right)_{t\geq 1},r\cdot\mathsf{W}^{r}(\infty)\right)\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\longrightarrow}\left((\mathcal{W}_{t})_{t\geq 1},C_{0}\cdot Y\right),

where

\displaystyle C_{0}:=\exp\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1-\theta(\lambda)}{1-\lambda^{*}}-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda\leq 2\}}}{\lambda-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right),

(47)

and the convergence of the process is in the sense of the Skorokhod convergence on every bounded interval.

Proof.

The proof works in two steps.
First we prove the convergence of
\left(\left(t,\mathsf{W}^{r}(t)\right)\right)_{t\geq 1}
towards
\left((t,\mathcal{W}_{t})\right)_{t\geq 1}
as Feller processes by proving the convergence of their generator and the convergence of their starting distribution.
Then in a second step we prove that this convergence holds jointly with that of
r\cdot\mathsf{W}^{r}(\infty)
.

Step 1: convergence of Feller processes via their generators.

Fix a smooth function
f
with compact support in
\mathopen{[}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
. We first write down the generator
\mathbf{A}^{r}
of the process
(t,\mathsf{W}^{r}(t))
applied to the function
f
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{r}f(t,x)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}f+\frac{x}{r^{2}}\cdot r\cdot(1-\theta(1+rt))\cdot\int_{u=1}^{\infty}(f(t,x+r^{2}x)-f(t,x))\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{\PGW((1+rt)^*)}=\lfloor u\rfloor\right)\differential u

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}f+(1-O(rt))\cdot x\cdot r^{-3}\cdot\int_{y=r^{2}}^{\infty}(f(t,x+y)-f(t,x))\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{\PGW((1+rt)^*)}=\lfloor r^{-2}y\rfloor\right)\differential y

(48)

For any
\lambda>0
and
k\geq 1
we have, using the cycle lemma,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{\PGW(\lambda)}=k\right)=\frac{1}{k}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)=k-1\right)=\frac{1}{k}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda k)=k-1\right)=\frac{e^{-\lambda k}(\lambda k)^{k-1}}{k!}.

(49)

For a bounded
t\geq 1
and
r
going to
0
, we have the expansion
(1+tr)^{*}=1-tr+\frac{2}{3}(tr)^{2}+O((tr)^{3})
.
We plug in
k=\lfloor xr^{-2}\rfloor
in the equality above and using Stirling’s approximation and the asymptotic expansion of the logarithm near
1
and get the following fact, the proof of which is postponed to the end of Section
4.5.3
.

Fact
\thetheorem
.

Let
T>0
. There exists constants
c,C>0
such that for
r\rightarrow 0
and uniformly for
t\in\mathopen{[}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},T\mathclose{]}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{\PGW((1+rt)^*)}=\lfloor r^{-2}y\rfloor\right)\left\{\begin{aligned} &=\frac{r^{3}\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2}x\right)}{y^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\pi}}(1+o(1))\quad&\text{uniformly for }y\in\mathopen{[}r^{\frac{3}{2}}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathclose{]}\\
&\leq\frac{r^{3}\exp\left(-c\frac{t^{2}}{2}y+C\right)}{y^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\pi}}\quad&\text{for }y\in\mathopen{[}r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}\end{aligned}\right.

Now, we can approximate the integral appearing on the right-hand-side of (
4.5.3
) by splitting it into three pieces. First,

\displaystyle r^{-3}\cdot\left|\int_{y=r^{2}}^{r^{\frac{3}{2}}}(f(t,x+y)-f(t,x))\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{\PGW((1+rt)^*)}=\lfloor r^{-2}y\rfloor\right)\differential y\right|

\displaystyle=r^{-1}\left|\int_{y=1}^{r^{-\frac{1}{2}}}(f(t,x+r^{2}y)-f(t,x))\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{\PGW((1+rt)^*)}=\lfloor y\rfloor\right)\differential y\right|

\displaystyle\leq r^{-1}\cdot\sup_{y\leq r^{-1/2}}|f(t,x+r^{2}y)-f(t,x)|\cdot 1\leq r^{\frac{1}{2}}\norm{\partial_x f}_{\infty}

which tends to
0
as
r\rightarrow 0
.
Also we get

\displaystyle r^{-3}\cdot\left|\int_{y=r^{-1/2}}^{\infty}(f(t,x+y)-f(t,x))\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{\PGW((1+rt)^*)}=\lfloor r^{-2}y\rfloor\right)\differential y\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{y=r^{-1/2}}^{\infty}\norm{\partial_x f}_{\infty}y\frac{\exp\left(-c\frac{t^{2}}{2}y+C\right)}{y^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\pi}}\differential y,

which also tends to
0
as

, uniformly in
t\in\mathopen{[}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},T\mathclose{]}
.
Last, we have

\displaystyle r^{-3}\cdot\int_{y=r^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{r^{-\frac{1}{2}}}(f(t,x+y)-f(t,x))\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{\PGW((1+rt)^*)}=\lfloor r^{-2}y\rfloor\right)\differential y

\displaystyle\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\rightarrow}\int_{0}^{\infty}(f(t,x+y)-f(t,x))\cdot\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2}y\right)}{y^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\pi}}\differential y

by dominated convergence, using again that
|f(t,x+y)-f(t,x)|\leq\norm{\partial_x f}_{\infty}\cdot y
.
Putting everything together we get that for any fixed function
f
that is smooth and compactly supported we have the convergence

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{r}f(t,x)

\displaystyle\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\rightarrow}\partial_{t}f+x\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}(f(t,x+y)-f(t,x))\cdot\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2}y\right)}{y^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\pi}}\differential y

and we recognize the limit as
\mathbf{A}f(t,x)
where
\mathbf{A}
is the generator of the process
((t,\mathcal{W}_{t}))_{t\geq 1}
as described in (
22
).
Recall also the convergence
\mathsf{W}^{r}(1)=r^{2}\cdot X_{1+r}\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\rightarrow}X=\mathcal{W}_{1}
from Section
4.4
.
Using a general result
[
20
, Theorem 17.25]
for the convergence of Markov process via their generators, we get the convergence of
\left(\left(t,\mathsf{W}^{r}(t)\right)\right)_{t\geq 1}
towards
\left((t,\mathcal{W}_{t})\right)_{t\geq 1}
for the Skorokhod topology on every bounded interval.

Step 2: convergence of the limiting value.

Fix some large
T
.
Conditionally on the value
\mathsf{W}^{r}(T)
, the computation (
4.5.2
) ensures that the limiting value

has conditional expectation given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[r\cdot\mathsf{W}^{r}(\infty)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathsf{W}^{r}(T)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[r^{3}\cdot W^{1+r}(\infty)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W^{1+r}(1+rT)\right]

\displaystyle=r^{3}\cdot\frac{W^{1+r}(1+rT)}{rT}\cdot\exp\left(\int_{1+rT}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\theta(\lambda))}{1-\lambda^{*}}-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda\leq 2\}}}{\lambda-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2}\cdot W^{1+r}(1+rT)}{T}\cdot\exp\left(\int_{1+rT}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\theta(\lambda))}{1-\lambda^{*}}-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda\leq 2\}}}{\lambda-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)

\displaystyle\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\rightarrow}C_{0}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{W}_{T}}{T},

where the convergence on the last line is in distribution, jointly with that of
\left(\mathsf{W}^{r}(t)\right)_{t\in\mathopen{[}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},T\mathclose{]}}
towards
\left(\mathcal{W}_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathopen{[}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},T\mathclose{]}}
.
The conditional variance of the same quantity can be bounded above using (
46
) as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(r\cdot\mathsf{W}^{r}(\infty)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathsf{W}^{r}(T)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left(r^{3}\cdot W^{1+r}(\infty)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}W^{1+r}(1+rT)\right)

\displaystyle\leq r^{6}\cdot(C_{0})^{2}\cdot W^{1+r}(1+rT)\cdot(rT)^{-4}

\displaystyle\leq\cst\cdot\frac{r^{2}\cdot W^{1+r}(1+rT)}{T^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\cst\cdot\frac{r^{2}\cdot W^{1+r}(1+rT)}{T}\cdot\frac{1}{T^{3}}

\displaystyle\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\rightarrow}\cst\frac{\mathcal{W}_{T}}{T}\cdot\frac{1}{T^{3}},

again, in distribution, jointly with the previous convergences.

As
T\rightarrow\infty
, we have
\frac{\mathcal{W}_{T}}{T}\rightarrow Y
and so the two last displays satisfy

\displaystyle C_{0}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{W}_{T}}{T}\underset{T\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}C_{0}\cdot Y\quad\text{and}\quad\cst\cdot\frac{\mathcal{W}_{T}}{T}\cdot\frac{1}{T^{3}}\underset{T\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}0.

This ensures that for large
T
and small

, the value
r\cdot\mathsf{W}^{r}(\infty)
is very concentrated around its conditional expectation at time

which is close to its
r\rightarrow 0
limit
C_{0}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{W}_{T}}{T}
, which in turn is close to its
T\rightarrow\infty
limit
C_{0}\cdot Y
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Fact
4.5.3
.

We use the Stirling formula
k!\underset{k\rightarrow\infty}{=}\exp\left(k\log k-k-\log(\sqrt{2\pi k})+o_{k}(1)\right)

\displaystyle\underset{k\rightarrow\infty}{=}\exp\left((\log\lambda-(\lambda-1))k-\log(\lambda)-\log(k^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\pi})+o_{k}(1)\right)

Now, we plug in

Setting

, for values
x\geq r^{\frac{3}{2}}

) we get that

and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{\PGW((1+tr)^*)}=\lfloor xr^{-2}\rfloor\right)=\frac{1}{x^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}x+O(t^{3}xr)+o(1)\right).

The result follows from this asymptotics.
∎

4.5.4
Control on the process.

We want bounds on the whole process

that hold at fixed

Denote

because the convergence (

For the second type of control, we use the same method as in Section

and assume that the process

Now reason on the event

and write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{2,r}\geq A,Z_{1,r}\leq A\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\Poi\left(A\int_{t=1}^{\infty}t(1-\theta((1+rt)^{*}))\differential t\int_{x=A\frac{\log^{2}t}{t^{2}}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|\PGW((1+rt)^{*})|\geq r^{-2}x\right)\right)\geq 1\right)

This entails that

Appendix B
Aggregation processes

In this section, we introduce a general version of an
aggregation process
.
We prove that under some reasonable assumptions that will be met in our applications, such a process yields a compact measured metric space in the limit and prove some properties of that limit (namely Hausdorff dimension, diffusivity of the measure).

This construction is very similar to the general line-breaking construction of Curien and Haas
[
14
]
, except that we glue trees instead of segments, and that the times of gluing here can be are dense instead of being discrete.
The ideas that we develop in this section are very similar to those already present in
[
27
]
in a similar context of gluing random metric spaces together.

In the next section, we prove the convergence of sequences of such process, under some reasonable assumptions, and this is the result that allows us to prove convergence results between different objects with this construction.

B.1

a

takes values in
\{t_{0}\}\cup\mathscr{J}\cap\mathopen{(}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{)}
.
Remark that for any
s\in\{t_{0}\}\cup\left(\mathscr{J}\cap\mathopen{(}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{)}\right)
we have, conditionally on the weight process,
\mathbb{P}\left(K_{t}=s\right)=\frac{w_{s}}{W_{t-}}
.

•

Then, conditionally on
K_{t}
, we let
X_{t}
be sampled on
\mathsf{B}_{K_{t}}
under a normalized version of the measure
\nu_{K_{t}}
, with
\mathsf{B}_{t_{0}}
interpreted as the seed
\mathsf{S}
.

We can easily check that defined in the way described above, the points
X_{t}
have the appropriate distribution.
The introduction of the random variables
(K_{t},t\in\mathscr{J})
allows us to define the following order relation
\prec
on
\{t_{0}\}\cup\mathscr{J}
defined as the order relation generated by
s\prec t
whenever
K_{t}=s
.

This relation encodes the tree structure along which the blocks
(\mathsf{B}_{t},\ t\in\mathscr{J})
are glued.
A way to picture this structure is to imagine a discrete (locally infinite) tree
\mathtt{T}
whose vertices are labelled by
\mathscr{J}
and such for any
t\in\mathscr{J}
the parent of the vertex labelled
t
is
K_{t}
.
We can also define the corresponding increasing tree process
(\mathtt{T}_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
defined so that for any
t
, the tree
\mathtt{T}_{t}
corresponds to the set of vertices of labels smaller than or equal to
t
.

From the construction, the process
(\mathtt{T}_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
is a form of weighted recursive tree as studied in
[
24
]
in the sense that for any
t\in\mathscr{J}
, the parent of vertex labelled
t
is chosen among
\{t_{0}\}\cup\mathscr{J}\cap\mathopen{(}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{)}
with probability proportional to their weight, independently for all
t\in\mathscr{J}
.
Some properties of weighted recursive trees remain true in this general setting, like the following proposition.

{proposition}

For any jump time
t\in\mathscr{J}
the random variables

\displaystyle(\mathbf{1}_{\{s\prec t\}},s\in\mathscr{J}\cap\mathopen{(}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{)})

are independent Bernoulli random variables with respective parameter
\frac{w_{s}}{W_{s}}
.

Proof.

See
[
14
, Section 2.2]
or
[
24
, Corollary 8]
.
∎

B.2
Bounds on diameter and compactness of
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}

In this section, we prove that under assumptions
(AG1)
and
(AG2)
the object
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}^{\bullet}
obtained from the aggregation process is almost surely compact and we provide some quantitative estimate on the tail of its diameter.
In this section, we omit the
\bullet
superscript on pointed objects to lighten the notation.

B.2.1
Bounding distance in the aggregate

Recall the definition of
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
as the metric gluing

\displaystyle(A_{\infty},\rho,v,d):=\overline{\left(\mathsf{S}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{t>t_{0}}\mathsf{B}_{t}\right)/\sim}

where every
\rho_{t}
is identified with the corresponding point
X_{t}
. Then for any for any jump
s\in\mathscr{J}
and any point
x\in\mathsf{B}_{s}
, by definition, the distance from
x
to the seed
\mathsf{S}
in
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
is bounded above by the sum of the contribution of all the block along the chain that links
\mathsf{B}_{s}
to
\mathsf{S}
in
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
. Hence, recalling that
K_{t}
is defined such that
X_{t}\in\mathsf{B}_{K_{t}}
,

\displaystyle d(x,\mathsf{S})=d(x,\rho_{s})+\sum_{t_{0}\prec u\prec s}d(X_{u},\rho_{K_{u}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t_{0}\prec u\preceq s}\diam(\mathsf{B}_{u}).

(55)

The idea is then to use the probabilistic description of the set
\left\{u\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}t_{0}\prec u\prec s\right\}
and the tails of the diameter of the blocks
\mathsf{B}_{u}
given by
(AG2)
.

For that, we first decompose the set of jump times
\mathscr{J}
into

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}=\mathscr{E}^{*}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})

where for any
\epsilon
we set
\mathscr{E}(\epsilon):=\left\{t>t_{0}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\epsilon\leq w_{t}<2\epsilon\right\}
and
\mathscr{E}^{*}=\left\{t>t_{0}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}w_{t}\geq 1\right\}
.

Now for
s\in\mathscr{J}
, for
x\in\mathsf{B}_{s}
, we decompose further the sum appearing on the right-hand-side of (
55
) according to the sizes of the different jumps involved in the sum

\displaystyle d(x,\mathsf{S})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t_{0}\prec u\preceq s}\diam(\mathsf{B}_{u})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t_{0}\prec u\preceq s}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\diam(\mathsf{B}_{u})\mathbf{1}_{\{u\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})\}}+\sum_{t_{0}\prec u\preceq s}\diam(\mathsf{B}_{u})\mathbf{1}_{\{u\in\mathscr{E}^{*}\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\underset{Y(k)}{\underbrace{\left(\max_{u\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})}\diam(\mathsf{B}_{u})\right)}}\underset{X(k)}{\underbrace{\left(\max_{r\in\mathscr{J}}\sum_{t_{0}\prec u\preceq r}\mathbf{1}_{\{u\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})\}}\right)}}+\underset{Z}{\underbrace{\sum_{u\in\mathscr{E}^{*}}\diam(\mathsf{B}_{u})}}

(56)

Remark that the last display does not depend on
x
nor
s
anymore, so that the Hausdorff distance
d_{H}(\mathsf{A}_{\infty},\mathsf{S})
is smaller than the last display.
Now it just remains to prove that the right-hand-side is almost surely finite under our assumptions.

The first part
X(k)
will be handled using concentration inequalities about sums of independent Bernoulli random variables, thanks to Proposition
B.1.2
.
For the second part
Y(k)
, we will use the tail behaviour for the diameter of the blocks that is ensured by
(AG2)
.
Also,
Z
is just a finite sum of random variables so it is almost surely finite.

B.2.2
Controls on the quantities
X(k)
and
Y(k)

We prove here a technical result that contains bounds for the quantities
X(k)
and
Y(k)
that appear on the right-hand-side of (
B.2.1
).

Lemma
\thetheorem
.

Under assumptions
(AG1)
and
(AG2)
, there exists constants
c,C
and independent random variables
X
and
Y
such that for all
k\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle X(k)\leq X\cdot(\log(Z_1 Z_2)+1)\cdot 7k\cdot\quad\text{ and }\quad Y(k)\leq Y\cdot k\cdot 2^{-\frac{k}{2}}

and such that for
x\geq C
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X\geq x\right)\leq C\cdot Z_{1}\cdot Z_{2}\cdot\exp\left(-cx^{2}\right)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq x\right)\leq C\cdot Z_{1}\cdot Z_{2}\cdot\exp\left(-cx\right).

As a preliminary result, we prove that deterministically, for all
k\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle|\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})|\leq Z_{1}\cdot Z_{2}\cdot 2^{2k}.

(57)

Proof of (
57
).

Thanks to
(AG1)
, there exists
Z_{1},Z_{2}
and some non-increasing function
\delta:\mathopen{[}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}\rightarrow\mathopen{[}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},1\mathclose{]}
so that for all
t\geq t_{0}
,

\displaystyle W_{t}\leq Z_{1}\cdot t\quad\text{ and }\quad w_{t}\leq Z_{2}\cdot t^{-2+\delta(t)}.

From the above display, if for any
\epsilon
we define
t_{\epsilon}
as

t_{\epsilon}=Z_{2}\cdot\epsilon^{-1}
, then for any
t>t_{\epsilon}
we have

\displaystyle w_{t}\leq Z_{2}\cdot t^{-2+\delta(t)}\leq Z_{2}\cdot t_{\epsilon}^{-1}<\epsilon.

This entails that
\mathscr{E}(\epsilon)\cap\mathopen{(}t_{\epsilon}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},\infty\mathclose{)}=\emptyset
.
Hence we have

\displaystyle W_{t_{\epsilon}}\geq\sum_{t\in\mathscr{E}(\epsilon)}w_{t}\geq|\mathscr{E}(\epsilon)|\cdot\epsilon.

And so we get

\displaystyle|\mathscr{E}(\epsilon)|\leq\epsilon^{-1}\cdot W_{t_{\epsilon}}\leq\epsilon^{-1}\cdot Z_{1}\cdot t_{\epsilon}\leq Z_{1}\cdot Z_{2}\cdot\epsilon^{-2}.

Applying the former to
\epsilon=2^{-k}
concludes the proof of (
57
).
∎

Now we turn to the proof of Lemma
B.2.2
.

Proof of Lemma
B.2.2
.

We first study the random variables
(X(k),\ k\geq 1)
.
For that we write, using a union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X(k)\geq x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{u\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})}\left(\sum_{v\preceq u}\mathbf{1}_{\{v\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})\}}\right)\geq x\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{v\preceq u}\mathbf{1}_{\{v\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})\}}\geq x\right)

But for any such
u
in the sum

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{v\preceq u}\mathbf{1}_{\{v\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})\}}\geq x\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{v\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})}\mathbf{1}_{\{v\preceq u\}}\geq x\right),

and all the
\mathbf{1}_{\{v\preceq u\}}
, for
v<u
are independent Bernoulli r.v. with parameters
\frac{w_{v}}{W_{v}}
, thanks to Proposition
B.1.2
.
The terms
\mathbf{1}_{\{v\preceq u\}}
for
v>u
are identically
0
and the term
\mathbf{1}_{\{u\preceq u\}}
is identically
1
, which we interpret as Bernoulli random variables of parameter
0
and
1
respectively.
We can bound the sum of the parameters of those Bernoulli random variables by

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{v\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})}\frac{w_{v}}{W_{v}}\leq 1+\sum_{v\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})}\frac{w_{v}}{\sum_{u\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})}w_{u}\mathbf{1}_{\{u\leq v\}}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})|}\frac{1}{i}

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\left(1+\log(|\mathscr E(2^{-k})|)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\log(Z_1Z_2)+4k\log 2+3

\displaystyle\leq(\log(Z_1Z_2)+1)\cdot 7k,

where we use the bound (
57
).
Then using the last display together with some classical estimates for sums of independent Bernoulli r.v. we get for any
y>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{v\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})}\mathrm{Ber}\left(\frac{w_{v}}{W_{v}}\right)\geq(1+y)(\log(Z_1Z_2)+1)\cdot 7k\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{3}y^{2}\cdot 7k\right)

so that for all
x>1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X(k)\geq((\log(Z_1 Z_2)+1)\cdot 7k)\cdot x\right)

\displaystyle\leq|\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})|\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{1}{3}\cdot(x-1)^{2}\cdot 7k\right)

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{eq:upper bound number of blocks of given scale}}{\leq}Z_{1}Z_{2}\cdot\exp\left(\left(-\frac{1}{3}\cdot(x-1)^{2}\cdot 7k\right)+2k\log 2\right).

We can find constant
c_{1},c_{2},C>0
so that the expression that appears in the exponential in the last display is bounded above for all
x\geq 1
and all
k\geq 1
by
(c_{1}-c_{2}\cdot x^{2})k+C
.
In the end, for all
x>1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X(k)\geq((\log(Z_1 Z_2)+1)\cdot 7k)\cdot x\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot Z_{1}\cdot Z_{2}\cdot\exp((c_1-c_2 x^2) \cdot k).

Introducing

\displaystyle X:=\sup_{k\geq 1}\left(\frac{X(k)}{(\log(Z_1 Z_2)+1)\cdot 7k}\right),

we can use an union-bound and write, for all
x
sufficiently large so that
(c_{1}-c_{2}x^{2})\leq-1
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X\geq x\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}C\cdot Z_{1}\cdot Z_{2}\cdot\exp((c_1-c_2 x^2) \cdot k)

\displaystyle=C\cdot Z_{1}\cdot Z_{2}\cdot\frac{\exp(c_1-c_2 x^2)}{1-\exp(c_1-c_2 x^2)}

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot Z_{1}\cdot Z_{2}\cdot\exp(-c_2 x^2),

by adequately changing the value of
C
at the last inequality.

Now let us turn to the study of
(Y(k),\ k\geq 1)
.
Recall that the blocks
(\mathsf{B}_{s})_{s\in\mathscr{J}}
are random and independent, and that they are such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\diam\mathsf{B}_{s}}{\sqrt{w_{s}}}\geq x\right)\leq C_{1}\exp\left(-C_{2}x\right).

Then, using a union-bound, the Markov inequality and (
57
) we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y(k)\geq x\cdot k\cdot 2^{\frac{k}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{u\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})}\diam(\mathsf{B}_{u})\geq x\cdot k\cdot 2^{-k/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq|\mathscr{E}(2^{-k})|\cdot C_{1}\exp\left(-C_{2}xk\right)

\displaystyle\leq Z_{1}\cdot Z_{2}\cdot C_{1}\cdot\exp\left((2\log 2-C_{2}x)k\right)

which is summable in
k
if we take
x
large enough (in a way that does not depend on
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
).
In the end, introducing

\displaystyle Y:=\sup_{k\geq 1}\left(\frac{Y(k)}{k\cdot 2^{\frac{k}{2}}}\right),

using a union bound we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq x\cdot k\cdot 2^{\frac{k}{2}}\right)\leq C\cdot Z_{1}\cdot Z_{2}\cdot\exp(-c x),

by adequately changing the values of the constants
c,C
.
This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

B.2.3
Proof of compactness of
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}

We now prove the following lemma.

Lemma
\thetheorem
.

Under
(AG1)
and
(AG2)
, the aggregation process (considered without measure) yields an almost surely compact metric space
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
.

Proof of Lemma
B.2.3
.

First, under the assumption of the lemma we can apply Lemma
B.2.2
to ensure that almost surely

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}^{\infty}X(k)Y(k)\leq 7XY(1+\log(Z_1 Z_2))\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{2}2^{-\frac{k}{2}}<\infty.

Recalling (
B.2.1
), this already ensure that the metric space
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
is bounded.
Since we want to prove the stronger statement that it is compact, we need to work a little bit more.
First introduce for any
k\geq 1
the set

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}^{(k)}:=\mathsf{S}\cup\bigcup_{t\in\mathscr{E}^{*}}\mathsf{B}_{t}\cup\bigcup_{i=0}^{k}\bigcup_{t\in\mathscr{E}(2^{-i})}\mathsf{B}_{t}

(58)

seen as a subset of
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
.
Since it is a finite union of compact sets, it is itself compact as well.

Then, from the same argument that led to (
B.2.1
), we can show that for any
1\leq k\leq\ell
we have the following upper-bound on the Hausdorff distance between the compact subsets
\mathsf{A}^{(k)}
and
\mathsf{A}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathsf{A}^{(k)},\mathsf{A}^{(\ell)})\leq 2\sum_{i\geq k+1}^{\infty}X(i)Y(i).

(59)

Now since the right-hand-side of the inequality almost surely tends to
0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
, this ensures that the sequence
(\mathsf{A}^{(k)})_{k\geq 0}
of compact subsets of the Polish space
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
is a Cauchy sequence for the Hausdorff distance, hence it converges.
We can then identify
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
as its limit, which is then indeed compact. This proves the claim of the lemma.
∎

To finish, we state a more quantitative result.

Lemma
\thetheorem
.

Under
(AG1)
and
(AG2)
, the aggregation process (considered without measure) is such that
for any
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathsf{A}_{\infty},\mathsf{S})^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C_{p}\cdot\left(Z_{1}Z_{2}^{3/2}+350(1+\log(Z_1 Z_2))(Z_{1}Z_{2})^{\frac{1}{p}}\right),

where
C_{p}
is a constant that only depends on
p
and on
\eta
but not on
(W_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
.

Proof.

Thanks (
B.2.1
) and the previous lemma we have,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathsf{A}_{\infty},\mathsf{S})

\displaystyle\leq Z+XY(1+\log(Z_1 Z_2))\cdot 7\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{2}2^{-\frac{k}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq Z+350\cdot XY(1+\log(Z_1 Z_2)).

Using the triangular inequality for the
L^{p}
norm we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathsf{A}_{\infty},\mathsf{S})^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}+350(1+\log(Z_1 Z_2))\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left(XY\right)^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}+350(1+\log(Z_1 Z_2))\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2p}\right]^{\frac{1}{2p}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2p}\right]^{\frac{1}{2p}}

where we used Cauchy-Schwarz in the second inequality.
Using the tails of
X
and
Y
that are so that for
x
larger than some constant

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X\geq x\right)\leq C\cdot Z_{1}\cdot Z_{2}\cdot\exp\left(-cx^{2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq x\right)\leq C\cdot Z_{1}\cdot Z_{2}\cdot\exp\left(-cx\right),

we can get by integrating that there exists a constant
C_{p}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2p}\right]^{\frac{1}{2p}}\leq C_{p}(Z_{1}Z_{2})^{\frac{1}{2p}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2p}\right]^{\frac{1}{2p}}\leq C_{p}(Z_{1}Z_{2})^{\frac{1}{2p}}.

Now for the term involving
Z
, without any additional assumption, we can use some crude bounds

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathscr{E}^{*}}\diam(\mathsf{B}_{u})=\sum_{u\in\mathscr{E}^{*}}\sqrt{w_{u}}\cdot\frac{\diam(\mathsf{B}_{u})}{\sqrt{w_{u}}}

\displaystyle\leq(\max_{u\in\mathscr{E}^{*}}\sqrt{w_{u}})\cdot\sum_{u\in\mathscr{E}^{*}}\frac{\diam(\mathsf{B}_{u})}{\sqrt{w_{u}}}.

In the last display, we have
(\max_{u\in\mathscr{E}^{*}}\sqrt{w_{u}})\leq\sqrt{Z_{2}}
and
\sum_{u\in\mathscr{E}^{*}}\frac{\diam(\mathsf{B}_{u})}{\sqrt{w_{u}}}
is a sum of independent variables with a common exponential tail containing
|\mathscr{E}^{*}|\leq Z_{1}Z_{2}
terms.
This ensures that, up to increasing the value of
C_{p}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq Z_{1}Z_{2}^{\frac{3}{2}}\cdot C_{p}.

Putting everything together concludes the proof of the lemma.

∎

B.3
Endowing
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
with a probability measure: definition of
\mu_{\infty}

Now that under assumptions
(AG1)
and
(AG2)
, Lemma
B.2.3
ensures that the object
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}:=\mathrm{Aggreg}((W_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}},\mathsf{S},\eta)
is almost surely well-defined as a compact random metric space, we want to endow it with some probability measure.
Recall the definition for any
t\geq t_{0}
of the measure
\mu_{t}

\displaystyle\mu_{t}:=\frac{1}{W_{t}}\sum_{t_{0}\leq s\leq t}\nu_{s},

where as usual we abuse notation by seeing every one of the blocks
\mathsf{B}_{s}
as a subset of
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
.
The measure process
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
takes values in the set of probability measures on
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
which is compact.
Endowing the set of probability measure on
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
with the so-called Lévy-Prokhorov distance yields a compact metric space that has the topology of weak convergence.

Elements of notation.

In what follows, we will need some elements of notation. For
t\geq t_{0}
:

•

We let
\mathsf{A}_{t}\subset\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
be defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}\cup\bigcup_{s\in\mathopen{(}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{]}}\mathsf{B}_{s},

the subset of
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
constituted by the blocks arrived at time
s\leq t
.

•

We let
p_{t}:\mathsf{A}_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathsf{A}_{t}
be the projection towards
\mathsf{A}_{t}
, which maps any
x\in\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
to the point
p_{t}(x)
which makes the distance
d(x,p_{t}(x))
minimal.

•

We let
\mathscr{D}(t)=\left\{s\in\mathscr{J}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}s\succeq t\right\}
, be the set of descendants of
t
, (recall the definition of
\preceq
from the beginning of the section).

•

We let
\mathsf{D}(t)=\overline{\bigcup_{s\in\mathscr{D}(t)}\mathsf{B}_{s}}
be the corresponding subset of
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
.

•

In a similar manner, for
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathsf{A}_{t}
we let
\mathscr{D}(t,\mathcal{C})=\left\{s\in\mathscr{J}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}p_{t}(\rho_{s})\in\mathcal{C}\right\}
.

•

Similarly, define
\mathsf{D}(t,\mathcal{C})=\mathcal{C}\cup\overline{\bigcup_{s\in\mathscr{D}(t,\mathcal{C})}\mathsf{B}_{s}}
.

{proposition}

The process of measures
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
on
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
converges weakly to a limit
\mu_{\infty}
.
Furthermore, a random point
Y_{\infty}
that has distribution
\mu_{\infty}
conditionally on
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
is such that the events

B.3.1
Generalized Pólya urns with weight process
(W_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}

We start by describing an urn process, a slightly more general version of the one studied by Pemantle in
[
26
]
.
Let
a,b
be two non-negative real numbers, with
a+b>0
,
and
t_{0}
be some real number and
(W_{s})_{s\geq t_{0}}
a non-increasing càdlàg pure jump process, whose jumps we call
(w_{s})_{s\in\mathscr{J}}
where
\mathscr{J}
is the set of jump times of the process
s\mapsto W_{s}
.

We refer to the
following process as a time-dependent Pólya urn starting at time
t_{0}
with
a
red balls and
b
black balls and weight process
(W_{s})_{s\geq t_{0}}
:

•

At time
t_{0}
, the urn contains
a
red balls and
b
black balls with
a+b=W_{t_{0}}
,

•

Then at every jump time
s
of the process, a ball is drawn at random and replaced in the urn, along with
w_{s}
additional balls of the same colour.
For any
s\geq t_{0}
we call
R_{s}
the proportion of red balls in the urn at time
s
.

From that description, it is unclear if the definition of the process makes sense because we could draw infinitely many times in the urn in every finite interval of time.
However, we can define a version of this whole process using a family
(V_{r})_{r\in\mathscr{J}}
i.i.d. random variables that are uniform on
\mathopen{[}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},1\mathclose{]}
:

\displaystyle R_{t}=R_{s}+\sum_{t<r\leq s}\frac{w_{r}}{W_{r}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{r}\leq R_{r-}\}}-R_{r-}\right).

(60)

In particular, with this definition we deterministically have for any
s\leq t
,

\displaystyle|R_{s}-R_{t}|\leq\sum_{t<r\leq s}\frac{w_{r}}{W_{r}}\leq\frac{1}{W_{t}}\cdot(W_{s}-W_{t})

which ensures that the function that we define is almost surely càdlàg. From (
60
), it is also immediate that this process is a martingale in its own filtration. Because its values are in
\mathopen{[}0\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},1\mathclose{]}
we automatically have the convergence of
R_{s}
to some random variable
R_{\infty}
almost surely and in all
L^{p}
for
p\geq 1
.

From
[
26
, Theorem 4]
, easily adapted to this setting, if the process
(R_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
starts with a positive value
R_{t_{0}}>0
, then we almost surely have

\displaystyle R_{\infty}>0.

(61)

Bounding the moments.

In order to get a bound on the moments of
R_{\infty}
, we use the following reasoning, which is the same as the one used in the proof of
[
27
, Lemma 5]
.
For any jump time
s
and integer
N\geq 2
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{s})^{N}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(R_{s-}+\frac{w_{s}}{W_{s}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{s}\leq R_{s-}\}}-R_{s-}\right)\right)^{N}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{W_{s-}}{W_{s}}R_{s-}+\frac{w_{s}}{W_{s}}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{s}\leq R_{s-}\}}\right)^{N}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left(\frac{W_{s-}}{W_{s}}\right)^{N}+N\left(\frac{W_{s-}}{W_{s}}\right)^{N-1}\frac{w_{s}}{W_{s}}\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{s-})^{N}\right]+\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-2}\binom{N}{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{s-})^{\ell+1}\right]\left(\frac{w_{s}}{W_{s}}\right)^{k-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{s-})^{N}\right]+\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-2}\binom{N}{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{s-})^{\ell+1}\right]\left(\frac{w_{s}}{W_{s}}\right)^{k-\ell}.

Hence, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{t})^{N}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{t_{0}})^{N}\right]+\sum_{t_{0}<s\leq t}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{s})^{N}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{s-})^{N}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{t_{0}})^{N}\right]+\sum_{t_{0}<s\leq t}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-2}\binom{N}{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{s-})^{\ell+1}\right]\left(\frac{w_{s}}{W_{s}}\right)^{k-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{t_{0}})^{N}\right]+\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-2}\binom{N}{\ell}\left(\sup_{s\geq t_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{s-})^{\ell+1}\right]\right)\left(\sum_{s\geq t_{0}}\left(\frac{w_{s}}{W_{s}}\right)^{N-\ell}\right).

(62)

This entails that we can have bounds on the moments of
R_{\infty}
recursively.
A useful inequality for those martingales is the following:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(R_{\infty}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}R_{t}\right)=\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{r>t}\frac{w_{r}}{W_{r}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{r}\leq R_{r-}\}}-R_{r-}\right)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}R_{t}\right)\leq R_{t}\cdot\sum_{r>t}\left(\frac{w_{r}}{W_{r}}\right)^{2}.

(63)

B.5
Upper-bound on Minkowski dimension

In this section, we provide an upper-bound on the Minkowki dimension of the metric space
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}

(i)

For any metric space

\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{\mathrm{Mink}}(X)=\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log N_{\epsilon}(X)}{-\log\epsilon}.

By monotonicity, it is sufficient to take the above limsup along a sequence

\frac{\log\epsilon_{k+1}}{\log{\epsilon_k}}\underset{k\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}1

Proof of Proposition
2.3.2
(i)
.

Using the same notation as in the last paragraph of the previous subsection, we want to construct a covering of
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
by balls of small radius.

k\geq 1
and set

.
Then we consider an

\mathsf{A}^{(k)}
defined in (
58
).
The number of balls needed is then bounded above by

\displaystyle N_{\epsilon_{k}}(\mathsf{S})+\sum_{t\in\mathscr{J}:w_{t}\geq 2^{-k}}N_{\epsilon_{k}}(\mathsf{B}_{t}).

Thanks to assumption
(AGMink)

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\mathscr{J}:w_{t}\geq 2^{-k}}C\cdot(1\vee w_{t}\epsilon_{k}^{-2})

depends on

and

\mathsf{A}_{\infty}

In the end, on an event of probability

i
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\mathopen{[}2^{i}t_{n}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},2^{i+1}t_{n}\mathclose{)}\cap\mathscr{S}^{\delta}}\frac{w_{t}}{W_{t}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{W_{2^{i+1}t_{n}}}\cdot\sum_{t\in\mathopen{[}2^{i}t_{n}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},2^{i+1}t_{n}\mathclose{)}\cap\mathscr{S}^{\delta}}w_{t}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{Z_{3}2^{i+1}t_{n}+o(2^{i+1}t_{n})}\cdot(Z_{3}\cdot 2^{i+1}t_{n}+o(2^{i}t_{n})-(Z_{3}\cdot 2^{i}t_{n}+o(2^{i+1}t_{n})))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}+o(1),

so that
\sum_{t\in\mathopen{[}t_{n}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t_{n+1}\mathclose{)}\cap\mathscr{S}^{\delta}}\frac{w_{t}}{W_{t}}\geq I_{n}(\frac{1}{2}+o(1))
.
Now, by definition of
I_{n}
and of
t_{n}
we have

\displaystyle I_{n}\sim\frac{\log(t_{n+1})-\log(t_n)}{\log 2}\sim\frac{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)^{n}}{\log 2}

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
This completes the proof.
∎

B.7
Convergence of tree aggregation processes

Finally in this section we prove Proposition
2.3.3
, which ensures that under the appropriate conditions, we have the convergence

\displaystyle\mathrm{Aggreg}((W_{t}^{r})_{t\geq t_{0}},\mathsf{S}^{\bullet,r},\eta^{r})\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\rightarrow}\mathrm{Aggreg}((W_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}},\mathsf{S}^{\bullet},\eta),

in distribution in
\mathbb{L}
.

Proof of Proposition
2.3.3
.

Recall the setting introduced in Section
2.3.3
.
We write, for every
r
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}^{r,\bullet}_{\infty}:=\mathrm{Aggreg}((W_{t}^{r})_{t\geq t_{0}},\mathsf{S}^{\bullet,r},\eta^{r})

and
\mathsf{A}^{\bullet}_{\infty}:=\mathrm{Aggreg}((W_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}},\mathsf{S}^{\bullet},\eta)
for the limit candidate.
We will keep the same notation for both constructions, simply adding a superscript
r
for the one that depends on the parameter
r
. For the whole proof below, we drop the
\bullet
superscript for readability.

Let
k\geq 1
and
t\geq t_{0}
.
We denote by
\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t}
the subset of
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
obtained by only keeping the seed and the blocks corresponding to jump times
s\in\mathscr{J}\cup\mathopen{(}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{]}
and whose weight
w_{s}
are larger than
2^{-k}
.
For simplicity, we assume that with probability
1
, no jump in
\mathscr{J}
has size exactly
2^{-k}
. (Otherwise we could just replace
2^{-k}
with another similar threshold that has that property).

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t}=\mathsf{S}\cup\underset{w_{s}>2^{-k}}{\bigcup_{s\in\mathscr{J}\cap\mathopen{(}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{]}}}\mathsf{B}_{s}.

We consider the finite measure
\mu_{t}^{(k)}:=\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t}}\cdot\mu_{t}
on
\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t}
to see it as a rooted, measured, compact metric space. We define
\mathsf{A}^{(k),r}_{t}
and
\mu_{t}^{(k),r}
similarly for
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}^{r}
.

First, let us show that for any fixed
k
and
t
then

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}^{(k),r}_{t}\rightarrow\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t}

(67)

in distribution for the GHP topology.

By assumption
(AGConv1)
, and using the Skhorokhod embedding theorem, we can couple
(W_{s}^{r})_{s\geq t_{0}}
and
(W_{s})_{s\geq t_{0}}
in such a way that
(W_{s}^{r})_{s\in\mathopen{[}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{]}}\rightarrow(W_{s})_{s\in\mathopen{[}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{]}}
for the Skorokhod topology.
This entails that the position and size of the jumps of
(W_{s}^{r})_{s\in\mathopen{[}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{]}}
converge to that of
(W_{s})_{s\in\mathopen{[}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{]}}
on that interval.

We enumerate the jump times
s_{0},s_{1},\dots
of the process
(W_{t})
on the interval
\mathopen{[}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},T\mathclose{]}
say in non-increasing order of the size of the associated jump.
The above convergence ensures that we can find times
s_{0}^{r},s_{1}^{r},\dots
to that, as
r\rightarrow 0
we have
s_{k}^{r}\rightarrow s_{k}
and
w_{s_{k}^{r}}^{r}\rightarrow w_{s_{k}}
and
W_{s_{k}^{r}}^{r}\rightarrow W_{s_{k}}
. (Note that for a given
r
and
k
, it is possible that
s_{k}^{r}
is not a jump time of the process).
Now we couple the constructions:

•

We couple the seeds
\mathsf{S}^{\bullet}
and
\mathsf{S}^{r,\bullet}
in such a way that their GHP distance is small (and tends to
0
as
r\rightarrow\infty
), as given by assumption
(AGConv2)
.

•

For any
k\geq 1
for which
s_{k}^{r}\in\mathscr{J}^{r}
, we couple the blocks
\mathsf{B}_{s_{k}}
and
\mathsf{B}_{s_{k}^{r}}^{r}
which have respective distribution
\eta(s_{k},w_{s_{k}})
and
\eta^{r}(s_{k}^{r},w_{s_{k}}^{r})
in such a way that their GHP distance is small.
This amounts to saying that we can isometrically embed
\mathsf{B}_{s_{k}}
and
\mathsf{B}_{s_{k}^{r}}^{r}
in the same metric space so that the Hausdorff distance of their image and Prokhorov distance of their push-forward measure is small.
This is possible thanks to assumption
(AGConv3)
.

•

We couple the uniform random variables
(U_{t},\ t\in\mathscr{J})
and
(U_{t}^{r},\ t\in\mathscr{J}^{r})
in such a way that
U_{s_{k}}=U_{s_{k}^{r}}
, whenever
s_{k}^{r}\in\mathscr{J}^{r}
. The rest of those can be chosen in an i.i.d. fashion.

•

From all the above, for any
k\geq 1
we almost surely have
K_{s_{k}}=K_{s_{k}^{r}}^{r}
when
r
is large enough.
In that case, we couple the random point
X_{s_{k}}
with
X_{s_{k}^{r}}^{r}
on respectively
\mathsf{B}_{s_{k}}
and
\mathsf{B}_{s_{k}^{r}}^{r}
, in such a way that
X_{s_{k}}
and
X_{s_{k}^{r}}^{r}
are close together.
This is possible because they are embedded in such a way that the Prokhorov distance between their respective measure is small.

Now, since
\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t}
and
\mathsf{A}^{(k),r}_{t}
are determined by a finite (though random) number of gluing events, they can be shown to be close to one another in GHP topology when
r
is large enough.
More precisely,
\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t}
only depends on the blocks and gluing events corresponding to all the jumps in

\displaystyle\left\{u\in\mathscr{J}\cap\mathopen{(}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{)}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}u\preceq s\text{ for some }s\in\mathscr{J}\cap\mathopen{(}t_{0}\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},t\mathclose{)}\text{ with }w_{s}>2^{-k}\right\},

and similarly for
\mathsf{A}^{(k),r}_{t}
.
Then the coupling of all those objects and quantities presented above ensures that the convergence (
67
) holds, (see
[
29
, Lemma 2.4]
for a similar statement only for the GH topology, the addition of measures is straightforward).

Now, in order to prove Proposition
2.3.3
, we just need to show that for any
\epsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{\mathrm{GHP}}(\mathsf{A}^{(k),r}_{t},\mathsf{A}^{r}_{\infty})\geq\epsilon\right)\underset{r\rightarrow 0}{\rightarrow}0,

as well as a similar statement
\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{\mathrm{GHP}}(\mathsf{A}^{(k),r}_{t},\mathsf{A}^{r}_{\infty})\geq\epsilon\right)=0
for the limit.
Indeed, for any
\epsilon>0
, it would suffice to first take
t
and
k
large enough so that

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{\mathrm{GHP}}(\mathsf{A}^{(k),r}_{t},\mathsf{A}^{r}_{\infty})\geq\frac{\epsilon}{3}\right)
and
\mathbb{P}\left(d_{\mathrm{GHP}}(\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t},\mathsf{A}_{\infty})\geq\frac{\epsilon}{3}\right)
are smaller than
\frac{\epsilon}{3}

and then take
r
small enough so that
d_{\mathrm{GHP}}(\mathsf{A}^{(k),r}_{t}\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{3}
with probability larger than
1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}
.
This would ensure that on the coupling that we determined above, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(d_{\mathrm{GHP}}(\mathsf{A}_{\infty},\mathsf{A}^{r}_{\infty})\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\epsilon
, so that
\mathsf{A}^{r}_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
in probability.

Now, we fix some
\epsilon>0
and we aim at showing that we can find
k
and
t
such that for
r
small enough we can couple
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
and
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}^{r}
in such a way that their GHP distance is smaller than
\epsilon
with probability at least
1-\epsilon
.
This will show that
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}^{r}
converges in distribution as
r\rightarrow 0
towards
\mathsf{A}_{\infty}
.

First,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t},\mathsf{A}_{\infty})\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}d_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathsf{A}^{(k)},\mathsf{A}_{\infty})\underset{k\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}0,

thanks to (
59
) so this term is small with arbitrary high probability, by taking
t
and
k
large enough.
The same is true for
d_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathsf{A}^{r,(k)}_{t},\mathsf{A}_{\infty}^{r})
using
(AGConv4)
.
Then

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu_{t}^{(k)},\mu_{\infty})\leq d_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu_{t}^{(k)},\mu_{t})+d_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu_{t},\mu_{\infty})\leq(1-\mu_{t}(\mathsf{A}_{t}^{(k)}))+d_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu_{t},\mu_{\infty}),

where the second term can be made small by taking a large
t
and the first one can be made small by taking a large
k
.
Last

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu_{t}^{(k),r},\mu_{\infty}^{r})\leq d_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu_{t}^{(k),r},\mu_{t}^{r})+d_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu_{t}^{r},\mu_{\infty}^{r})\leq(1-\mu_{t}^{r}(\mathsf{A}_{t}^{(k),r}))+d_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu_{t}^{r},\mu_{\infty}^{r}).

The first term is small if we take
t
and
k
large and then
r
large.

We just have to take care of the last term
d_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu_{t}^{r},\mu_{\infty}^{r})
.
For that, suppose that we have constructed
\mathsf{A}^{r,(k)}_{t}
and
\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t}
on the same probability space and that we are on the event where
d_{\mathrm{GHP}}(\mathsf{A}^{r,(k)}_{t},\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t})\leq\epsilon
. This entails that we can isometrically embed
\mathsf{A}^{r,(k)}_{t}
and
\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t}
in the same metric space with isometries
\phi
and
\phi^{\prime}
so that
d_{\mathrm{H}}(\phi(\mathsf{A}^{r,(k)}_{t}),\phi^{\prime}(\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t}))\leq\epsilon
and
d_{\mathrm{P}}(\phi_{*}\mu_{t}^{(k),r},\phi^{\prime}_{*}\mu_{t}^{(k)})\leq\epsilon
.
Now since
\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t}
is compact, it can be covered with a finite number
N(\epsilon)
of balls of radius
\epsilon
. If we abuse notation and consider
\mathsf{A}^{r,(k)}_{t}
and
\mathsf{A}^{(k)}_{t}
as embedded in the same metric space as above, then using the same centres but taking the radii to be
2\epsilon
instead of
\epsilon
allows us to cover
\mathsf{A}^{r,(k)}_{t}
as well.

This gives us a covering of
\mathsf{A}^{r,(k)}_{t}
of a certain number of pieces
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{r},\mathcal{C}_{2}^{r}\dots\mathcal{C}_{N}^{r}
which all have diameter smaller than
2\epsilon
, and where
N=N(\epsilon)
.
From there, we can show using estimates on the mass martingales that the
\mu_{\infty}^{r}
-mass of any of the
\mathsf{D}(t,\mathcal{C}^{r})
will not substantially deviate from their
\mu_{t}
-mass and this will prove that indeed those two measures are close in Prokhorov sense.

Then, for any
1\leq i\leq N(\epsilon)
we know that we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\infty}^{r}(\mathsf{D}(t,\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r}))=M_{t,\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r})\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu_{t}^{r}(\mathsf{D}(t,\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r}))=M_{t,t}(\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r})=\mu_{t}(\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r}).

The variance of
\mu_{\infty}^{r}(\mathsf{D}(t,\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r}))
is, thanks to (
63
) and Lemma
B.3.3
, bounded above by

\displaystyle\sum_{s>t}\left(\frac{w_{s}}{W_{s}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{(W_{t}^{r})^{2}}\cdot Z_{1}^{r}\cdot(Z_{2}^{r})^{2}\cdot t^{-1+\delta(t)}.

Note that using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}|M_{t,\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r})-\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t,\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r})\right]|\right)^{2}\leq N(\epsilon)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N}|M_{t,\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r})-\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t,\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r})\right]|^{2}.

Now using Markov’s inequality we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}|M_{t,\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r})-\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t,\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r})\right]|\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot N\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{Var}\left(M_{t,\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r})\right)

\displaystyle\leq N\cdot\epsilon^{-2}\cdot N\cdot\sum_{s\geq t}\left(\frac{w_{s}}{W_{s}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{-2}N^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{(W_{t}^{r})^{2}}\cdot Z_{1}^{r}\cdot(Z_{2}^{r})^{2}\cdot t^{-1+\delta(t)}.

and the last display tends to
0
in probability as
t\rightarrow\infty
, uniformly in
r
.
This ensures that with high probability as
t\rightarrow\infty
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\mu_{\infty}^{r}(\mathsf{D}(t,\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r}))-\mu_{t}^{r}(\mathsf{D}(t,\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r}))|\leq\epsilon.

This ensures that we can couple two random variables
X
and
Y
with respective distribution
\mu_{t}^{r}
and
\mu_{\infty}^{r}
in such a way that with probability at least
1-2\epsilon
, the points
X
and
Y
fall into the same
\mathsf{D}(t,\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r})
.
Since

\displaystyle\sup_{1\leq i\leq N}\diam(\mathsf{D}(t,\mathcal{C}_{i}^{r}))\leq 2\epsilon+2d_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathsf{A}_{t}^{r},\mathsf{A}_{\infty}^{r}),

this proves that the Prokhorov distance between
\mu_{t}^{r}
and
\mu_{\infty}^{r}
is smaller than
3\epsilon
with high probability as
t
is large.
This completes the proof.
∎
