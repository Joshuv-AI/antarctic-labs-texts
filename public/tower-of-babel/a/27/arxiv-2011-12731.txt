Lower Gaussian heat kernel bounds for the Random Conductance Model in a degenerate ergodic environment
Sebastian Andres, Noah Halberstam

Abstract
. We study the random conductance model on ℤ d \Z^{d} with ergodic, unbounded conductances. We prove a Gaussian lower bound on the heat kernel given a polynomial moment condition and some additional assumptions on the correlations of the conductances. The proof is based on the well-established chaining technique. We also obtain bounds on the Green’s function.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Model

We let
G=(\Z^{d},E_{d}),
where
E_{d}=\{\{x,y\}\in\Z^{d}\times\Z^{d}:|x-y|=1\},
be the
d
-dimensional lattice for a fixed dimension
d\geq 2
. We write
x\sim y
if
(x,y)\in E_{d}
. We consider the space of positive weightings on the edges of the graph,
\Omega=(0,\infty)^{E_{d}},
and for
\omega\in\Omega,
we access the weight at a particular edge
e\in E_{d}
by
\omega(e)
, which we will also refer to as the
conductance
on an edge
e
. For
x,y\in\Z^{d}
and
\omega\in\Omega
we set
\omega(x,y)=\omega(y,x)=\omega(\{x,y\})
if
\{x,y\}\in E_{d},
else
\omega(x,y)=0.

For any fixed
\omega,
we define measures
\mu^{\omega}
and
\nu^{\omega}
on
\Z^{d}
by

\mu^{\omega}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{y\sim x}\omega(x,y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu^{\omega}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{y\sim x}\frac{1}{\omega(x,y)}.

For any
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we denote by
\tau_{z}\!:\Omega\to\Omega
the space shift by
z
defined by

\displaystyle(\tau_{z}\,\omega)(\{x,y\})\;\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\;\omega(\{x+z,y+z\}),\qquad\forall\;\{x,y\}\in E_{d}.

We equip
\Omega
with a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}
. Further, we will denote by
\prob
a probability measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, and we write
\mean
for the expectation with respect to
\prob
. Throughout the paper we will assume that the conductances are stationary and ergodic.

Assumption 1.1

(Stationarity and ergodicity)
.

\prob
is stationary and ergodic with respect to translations of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, i.e.
\prob\circ\,\tau_{x}^{-1}\!=\prob\,
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\prob[A]\in\{0,1\}\,
for any
A\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\tau_{x}(A)=A\,
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

We now introduce the
random conductance model (RCM)
. For a given
\omega\in\Omega
, we consider the continuous time Markov chain
X=\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with generator

(\mathcal{L}^{\omega}f)(x)=\frac{1}{\mu^{\omega}(x)}\sum_{y\sim x}\omega(x,y)\,\big(f(y)-f(x)\big).

This stochastic process, also known as the
constant speed random walk
(CSRW), waits at
x
for an exponential time with mean
1
, and then chooses the next position
y\sim x
with probability
\omega(x,y)/\mu^{\omega}(x)
. We also recall that the Markov chain
X
is reversible with respect to
\mu^{\omega}
. We denote by
\mathrm{P}_{x}^{\omega}
the law of the walk starting at the vertex
x\in\Z^{d},
and by
\mathrm{E}_{x}^{\omega}
the expectation with respect to this law. For
x,y\in\Z^{d}
and
t>0,
we let
p^{\omega}(t,x,y)
be the transition density (or the heat kernel associated with
\mathcal{L}^{\omega}
) with respect to the measure
\mu^{\omega}
, i.e.

p^{\omega}(t,x,y)=\frac{\mathrm{P}_{x}^{\omega}[X_{t}=y]}{\mu^{\omega}(y)}.

1.2.
Main Results

The random conductance model has been the subject of extensive research for more than a decade,
see
[
19
,
38
]
for surveys of the model and references therein.
More recent results include the derivation of quenched functional central limit theorems
[
4
,
17
,
25
,
22
]
and local limit theorems
[
15
,
5
,
3
,
10
,
16
]
for the RCM with unbounded ergodic conductances under moment conditions.
In this paper we will focus on heat kernel estimates, see e.g.
[
24
,
13
,
18
,
15
,
14
,
29
,
21
,
6
,
7
]
for previous results.
In particular, we will obtain Gaussian type lower bounds on the heat kernel in the case of ergodic unbounded conductances.

It is known that Gaussian bounds do not hold in general: for example, under i.i.d. conductances with fat tails at zero, the heat kernel decay may be sub-diffusive due to a trapping phenomenon – see
[
18
,
21
]
. Moreover, in
[
5
, Theorem 5.4]
, it is proved that in the general ergodic setting, moment bounds on the conductances and their reciprocals are a necessary condition for upper and lower near-diagonal Gaussian bounds to hold. In
[
6
]
, this necessary condition is shown to be sufficient
for full upper Gaussian heat kernel bounds.

Gaussian lower bounds have been shown on i.i.d. percolation clusters in
[
13
]
, and for variable speed random walks under i.i.d. conductances bounded away from zero in
[
14
]
.
However, in the general ergodic setting, as of yet, Gaussian lower bounds have only been proved under the stronger condition of uniformly elliptic conductances
[
24
]
, i.e.
c^{-1}\leq\omega(e)\leq c
,
e\in E_{d}
, for some
c\geq 1
. In this paper we relax the uniform ellipticity assumption, substituting it for the combination of a polynomial moment condition together with an assumption concerning the correlations of the conductances, see Assumption
1.3
. It is unknown whether moment conditions by themselves should be sufficient for the lower bound to hold. The main available technique for proving lower bounds, the
chaining method
(see
[
28
]
), fails at present in this generality (see Section
1.3
below for a more in-depth discussion), while our assumptions are sufficient to ensure the functionality of this method. However, it seems that other techniques would be required in order to weaken these assumptions. One possible approach would be to use techniques from quantitative stochastic homogenization that lead to much stronger quantitative homogenization results for heat kernels and Green functions, see
[
11
, Chapters 8–9]
for details. This technique has been adapted to Bernoulli bond percolation clusters in
[
23
]
, and it is expected that it also applies to other degenerate models.

We will begin by recalling the already established Gaussian upper bound in
[
6
]
, for which we will need some more notation. For
A\subset\Z^{d}
non-empty and finite, and
p\in[1,\infty)
, we introduce space-averaged
l^{p}
norms on functions
\phi:A\rightarrow\R
by

\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{p,A}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\bigg(\frac{1}{\left\lvert A\right\rvert}\sum_{x\in A}\big|\phi(x)\big|^{p}\bigg)^{\!\frac{1}{p}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{\infty,A}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max_{x\in A}\left\lvert\phi(x)\right\rvert,

where
\left\lvert A\right\rvert
denotes the cardinality of the set
A
. For
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we denote by
B(x,r)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:|x-y|<r\}
balls in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
centered at
x
with respect to the graph distance, where
|x|\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|
for
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Suppose now that
\omega(e)\in L^{p}(\prob)
and
\omega(e)^{-1}\in L^{q}(\prob)
for any
p,q\geq 1
. Then, under Assumption
1.1
, the spatial ergodic theorem gives that,
\prob
-a.s., for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

\bar{\mu}_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mean[\mu^{\omega}(0)^{p}]=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\mu^{\omega}\right\rVert_{p,B(x,n)}^{p},\qquad\bar{\nu}_{q}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mean[\nu^{\omega}(0)^{q}]=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\nu^{\omega}\right\rVert_{q,B(x,n)}^{q}.

In particular, for
\prob
-a.e.
\omega
and each
x\in\Z^{d},
there exists
N_{1}(x)=N_{1}(\omega,x,p,q)\in\N
such that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq N_{1}(x)}\left\lVert\mu^{\omega}\right\rVert_{p,B(x,n)}^{p}\leq 2\bar{\mu}_{p},\qquad\sup_{n\geq N_{1}(x)}\left\lVert\nu^{\omega}\right\rVert_{q,B(x,n)}^{q}\leq 2\bar{\nu}_{q}.

We will choose
N_{1}(x)
to be the minimal such random variable. The Gaussian upper heat kernel bound is as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that Assumption
1.1
holds and suppose there exist
p,q\in(1,\infty]
with
1/p+1/q<2/d
such that
\omega(e)\in L^{p}(\pr)
and
\omega(e)^{-1}\in L^{q}(\pr)
for any
e\in E_{d}
. Then, there exist constants
c_{i}=c_{i}(d,p,q,\bar{\mu}_{p},\bar{\nu}_{q})
such that, for
\prob
-a.e.
\omega
, for any given
t
and
x
with
\sqrt{t}\geq N_{1}(x)
and all
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
the following hold.

(i)

If
|x-y|\leq c_{1}t
then

\displaystyle p^{\omega}(t,x,y)\;\leq\;c_{2}\,t^{-d/2}\,\exp\!\big(\!-c_{3}\,|x-y|^{2}/t\big).

(ii)

If
|x-y|\geq c_{1}t
then

\displaystyle p^{\omega}(t,x,y)\;\leq\;c_{2}\,t^{-d/2}\exp\!\big(\!-c_{4}\,|x-y|(1\vee\log(|x-y|/t))\big).

Proof.

See
[
6
, Theorem 1.6]
and a more general version with a streamlined proof in
[
7
, Theorem 3.2]
.

We now state the additional assumptions we require, followed by our main results. We will then discuss why these additional assumptions are needed and how they interact with the strategy of the proof.

For
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega
we write
\omega\leq\omega^{\prime}
if
\omega(e)\leq\omega^{\prime}(e)
for all
e\in E_{d}
. We say that a function
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is non-decreasing if
f(\omega)\leq f(\omega^{\prime})
whenever
\omega\leq\omega^{\prime}
.

Assumption 1.3
.

At least one of the following four conditions holds.

(A1)

(i)
FKG inequality.
For any finite set of edges
A\subset E_{d},
and any non-decreasing functions
f,g:\Omega\rightarrow\R
depending only on
\{\omega(e):e\in A\}
, we have

(1.2)

\cov(f,g)\geq 0,

whenever the covariance exists.

(ii)
Polynomial mixing.
There exist constants
\gamma>d^{2}
and
C_{\mathrm{mix}}\in(0,\infty)
such that for any non-decreasing function
f\in L^{2}(\prob)
depending only
on
\{\omega(0,y),|y|=1\}
, and any
x\in\Z^{d}\setminus\{0\}
,

\displaystyle\cov\big(f,f\circ\tau_{x}\big)\leq C_{\mathrm{mix}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pr)}^{2}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{-\gamma}.

(A2)

Spectral gap.

There exists
C_{\mathrm{sg}}\in(0,\infty)
such that

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mean\big[(f-\mean[f])^{2}\big]\leq C_{\mathrm{sg}}\sum_{e\in E_{d}}\mean\Big[\big(\partial_{e}f\big)^{2}\Big],

for any
f\in L^{2}(\prob)
. Here, the ‘vertical derivative’
\partial_{e}f
is defined as

\partial_{e}f(\omega):=\limsup\limits_{h\to 0}\frac{f(\omega+h\delta_{e})-f(\omega)}{h},

where
\delta_{e}:E_{d}\to\{0,1\}
stands for the Dirac function satisfying
\delta_{e}(e)=1
and
\delta_{e}(e^{\prime})=0
if
e^{\prime}\not=e
.

(A3)

Finite range dependence.
There exists a positive constant
\mathfrak{R}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for any
x\in\Z^{d}
, the collection of random variables
\big(\omega(\{x,x+e\}):\left\lvert e\right\rvert=1\big)
is independent of
\big(\omega(\{z,z+e\}):\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert\geq\mathfrak{R},\left\lvert e\right\rvert=1\big)
.

(A4)

Negative association.
For any finite set of edges
A\subset E_{d},
and any non-decreasing functions
f,g:\Omega\rightarrow\R
depending only on
\{\omega(e):e\in A\}
, we have

\cov(f,g)\leq 0,

whenever the covariance exists.

The FKG inequality was first (formally) investigated by Fortuin, Kastelyn and Ginibre in
[
30
]
in connection with correlation properties of Ising spin systems. The inequality is in fact a natural property of a very wide range of statistical mechanics models, including the random cluster model (with
q\geq 1
)
[
34
]
, Yukawa quantum field theory models
[
41
]
, Gaussian free fields
[
20
, Proposition 5.22]
and interlacement percolation
[
45
]
. We note that by
[
27
, Theorem 3.3]
, it is sufficient to check that (
1.2
) holds for bounded continuous non-decreasing functions. On the other hand, the opposite assumption of negative association (A4) also holds for some prominent models, including the uniform spanning tree, the random cluster model (with
q\leq 1
), and simple exclusion models, we refer to
[
42
]
also for more motivation and background for this condition. We note that in the case of Gaussian fields, pairwise positive and negative correlation are enough to imply the FKG inequality
[
36
]
and negative associativity
[
37
]
, respectively.

The spectral gap condition in (A2) and the finite range dependence in (A3) also appear as decorrelation assumptions in the context of quantitative stochastic homogenization, see for instance
[
33
]
for (A2), and
[
11
]
for (A3). In a sense, the spectral gap condition in (A2), introduced in
[
33
]
, can be interpreted as a quantified version of ergodicity, as it implies an optimal variance decay for the semigroup associated with the “process of the environment as seen from the particle” induced by the simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, cf.
[
32
, Proposition 1 and Remark 5]
.

Throughout the paper we write
c
to denote a positive constant which may change on each appearance, while constants denoted

will be the same through the paper.
The constants will depend only on

and

Example 1.7

(RCMs defined by Ginzburg-Landau
\nabla\phi
interface models)
.

One class of conductances satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
1.4
can be constructed from the Ginzburg-Landau
\nabla\phi
-interface model (see
[
31
]
), a well established model for an interface separating two pure thermodynamical phases. The interface is described by a random field of height variables
\phi=\{\phi(x);x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
sampled from a Gibbs measure formally given by
Z^{-1}\exp(-H(\varphi))\,\prod_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}d\varphi(x)
with formal Hamiltonian
H(\varphi)=\sum_{e\in E_{d}}V(\nabla\varphi(e))
and potential
V\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}_{+})
, which we suppose to be even and strictly convex. Note that in the special case
V(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
, the field
\phi
becomes a discrete Gaussian free field. In
d\geq 3
this can be made rigorous by taking the thermodynamical limit, while in dimension
d\geq 1
one considers the gradient process instead.
Then, thanks to the strict convexity we have the Brascamp-Lieb inequality, which allows one to show that any environment with random conductances of the form
\{\omega(x,y)=\lambda(\nabla\phi(e)),e\in E_{d}\}
for any positive, even, globally Lipschitz function
\lambda\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies the spectral gap condition in Assumption
1.3
-(A2), see
[
9
, Section 7]
for details.
The Brascamp-Lieb inequality also implies that exponential moments for gradient fields under the Gibbs measure exist (cf.
[
31
,
40
]
). Thus, the environment
\{\omega(e),e\in E_{d}\}
as chosen above also satisfies the required moment condition in Theorem
1.4
. The assumption of a strictly convex potential can be relaxed, see
[
10
]
.

The
\nabla\phi
interface model also satisfies the FKG inequality, see again e.g.
[
31
,
40
]
, and for models with
massive
Hamiltonians formally given by

\displaystyle H(\varphi)=\sum_{e\in E_{d}}V(\nabla\varphi(e))+\frac{m^{2}}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\phi(x)^{2},\qquad m>0,

we have exponential correlation decay, see
[
40
, Theorem B]
. In particular, Assumption
1.3
-(A1) holds, and Theorem
1.4
applies, for instance, to conductances of the form
\omega(x,y)=\exp(\phi(x)+\phi(y))
,
\{x,y\}\in E_{d}
.

1.3.
The Method

It is well known that Gaussian lower and upper bounds on the heat kernel are equivalent in many situations to a parabolic Harnack inequality (PHI), e.g. in the case of uniformly elliptic conductances, see
[
24
]
. Indeed, the PHI implies near-diagonal bounds which are then converted into off-diagonal bounds via the established
chaining method
(see e.g.
[
28
,
24
,
13
]
).

In our context, a PHI has been obtained in
[
5
]
. Unfortunately, due to the special structure of the constant in the PHI in the case of unbounded conductances (see (
3.2
) below), in particular its dependence on
\|\mu^{\omega}\|_{p,B(x,n)}
and
\|\nu^{\omega}\|_{q,B(x,n)}
, we cannot directly deduce off-diagonal Gaussian lower bounds from it.
In order to get effective Gaussian off-diagonal bounds using the chaining argument, one needs to apply the Harnack inequality on a number of balls with radius
n
over a distance of order
n^{2}
. In general, however, the ergodic theorem does not
give the required uniform control on the convergence of space-averages of stationary random variables over such balls (see
[
1
]
). Therefore, in order to obtain lower Gaussian bounds we will need to make use of one of the additional conditions on the correlations stated in Assumption
1.3
.
Specifically, in Proposition
2.2
we employ any one of these conditions to derive a certain concentration estimate. Then, in Proposition
3.2
(and Corollary
3.4
), we manipulate these to give us the desired uniform control on the space-averages of the conductances over the aforementioned chain of balls of radius
n.
Finally, we utilize this uniform control within the chaining argument to yield the desired Gaussian off-diagonal lower bound.

Near-diagonal heat kernel bounds can also be deduced from a local limit theorem, cf.
[
5
, Lemma 5.3]
. Recently, such local limit theorems have been derived for a more general class of RCMs in
[
3
,
10
]
via De Giorgi’s iteration technique, circumventing the need for a PHI. However, the bounds obtained from arguments in
[
3
,
10
]
involve random constants which are implicit functions of the averages
\|\mu^{\omega}\|_{p,B(x,n)}
and
\|\nu^{\omega}\|_{q,B(x,n)}
, while the chaining argument requires the more explicit dependence on the averages in the PHI in
[
5
]
. Note that in
[
5
]
the PHI has only been derived for the CSRW, so we obtain the lower heat kernel bounds in Theorem
1.4
for the CSRW only, while the upper bounds in
[
7
]
have been established for a general class of speed measures.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we first deduce some concentration estimates from the correlation decay conditions in Assumption
1.3
, which are then used in Section
3
to prove the lower Gaussian bounds in Theorem
1.4
. Finally, in Section
4
we show Theorem
1.6
.

2.
Concentration estimates under decorrelation assumptions

Recall that
\bar{\mu}_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mean[\mu^{\omega}(0)^{p}]
and
\bar{\nu}_{q}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mean[\nu^{\omega}(0)^{q}]
for any
p,q\in[1,\infty)
. In this section we will derive some moment estimates on the deviations of
\mu^{\omega}(x)
and
\nu^{\omega}(x)
from their means under Assumption
1.3
. For that purpose, we define the centred random variables

and

Our moment bounds on

Definition 2.1

For any

\displaystyle\mean\bigg[\Big|\sum_{x\in R}\Delta\mu_{p}^{\omega}(x)\Big|^{\eta}\bigg]\leq c\left\lvert R\right\rvert^{\theta}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mean\bigg[\Big|\sum_{x\in R}\Delta\nu_{q}^{\omega}(x)\Big|^{\eta}\bigg]\leq c\left\lvert R\right\rvert^{\theta}

for all hyper-rectangles

.

In the next proposition, which is the main result in this section, we gather and derive the relations between Assumption
1.3
and

Proposition 2.2
.

Let

be a hyper-rectangle. Suppose that Assumptions

and
1.3
hold, and that
\zeta<\gamma/d
if under (A1). There exist constants

such that if

,
\omega(e)^{-1}\in L^{q_{0}}(\pr)
for any

-moment bound holds.

We will prove Proposition
2.2
under each of the assumptions separately, referencing the necessary materials before incorporating them into the proof.
The following lemma is easily implied by
[

.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\{Y(x):x\in\Z^{d}\}
be a random field satisfying the FKG inequality, and which is stationary with respect to translation, and suppose that

\sum_{|x|\geq n}\cov\big(Y(0),Y(x)\big)=O(n^{-\nu})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\lvert Y(0)\right\rvert^{\eta+\delta}\Big]<\infty,

for some
\delta,\nu>0
and

. Then, for any hyper-rectangle

\mean\Bigg[\Big|\sum_{x\in R}Y(x)\Big|^{\eta}\bigg]=O(n^{\theta}),

for
\theta>\max\{\eta/2,\chi(1-d^{-1}\min\{1,\nu\delta/\chi\})/(\eta+\delta-2)\}
, where
\chi=\delta+(\eta+\delta)(\eta-2).

Proof.

We apply stationarity and the positivity of covariances due to the FKG inequality to
[
12
, Corollary 1]
to give the result. Indeed, by stationarity any hyper-rectangle can be shifted into
\N^{d}
, and positivity of the covariances allows us to bound the summation of covariances over
\N^{d}
by the summation of covariances over
\Z^{d}
.
∎

p_{0},q_{0}\geq 1
such that if
\omega(e)\in L^{p_{0}}(\prob)
and
\omega(e)^{-1}\in L^{q_{0}}(\prob)
, then
(1,1,\eta,\theta)
and
(\kappa p,\kappa q,\eta,\theta)
-moment bounds hold with
\eta-\theta\geq\zeta
.
This will allow us to apply Lemma
2.6
, Proposition
3.2
, and Corollary
3.4
as required.
We then set
N\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=N_{1}(0)\vee N_{3}\vee N_{4}
and combine the tail bounds in Lemma
2.6
and (
3.3
) to obtain that
N
satisfies the tail bound in (
1.4
).

Set again
D\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\lvert x\right\rvert
, and assume now that
t\geq N(D\vee 1)
.
We will split the proof into two cases,
D^{2}/t\leq 1/4
and
D^{2}/t>1/4
.

Case 1:

D^{2}/t\leq 1/4
. Then
x\in B(0,\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{t})
, so by Proposition
3.1
,

p^{\omega}(t,0,x)\geq\frac{c_{14}}{C_{\mathrm{PH}}}t^{-\frac{d}{2}}

with
C_{\mathrm{PH}}=C_{\mathrm{PH}}\big(\|\mu^{\omega}\|_{p,B(0,\sqrt{t})},\|\nu^{\omega}\|_{q,B(0,\sqrt{t})}\big)
.
Since
C_{\mathrm{PH}}\big(\|\mu^{\omega}\|_{p,B},\|\nu^{\omega}\|_{q,B}\big)
is increasing in
\left\lVert\mu^{\omega}\right\rVert_{p,B}
and
\left\lVert\nu^{\omega}\right\rVert_{q,B}
(cf. (
3.2
) above) and
t\geq N_{1}(0)
,

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{PH}}\big(\|\mu^{\omega}\|_{p,B(0,\sqrt{t})},\|\nu^{\omega}\|_{q,B(0,\sqrt{t})}\big)\leq C_{\mathrm{PH}}\Big(\big(2\bar{\mu}_{p}\big)^{1/p},\big(2\bar{\nu}_{q}\big)^{1/q}\Big),

and therefore
p^{\omega}(t,0,x)\geq ct^{-d/2}
.

Case 2:

D^{2}/t>1/4
. Set
r\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=t/D\geq 1\vee N_{3}\vee N_{4}
. We deploy the chaining setup as introduced right below Proposition
3.1
.
Recall that
s\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=Dr/k=t/k
with
\frac{12D}{r}\leq k\leq\frac{16D}{r}
so that
1\leq\frac{1}{16}r^{2}\leq s\leq\frac{1}{12}r^{2}
, and note that
k\geq 3
.
Then, for any collection of vertices
y_{0},\ldots y_{k}
with
y_{0}=0
,
y_{j}\in B_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq k-1
and
y_{k}=x
, we have
d(y_{i},y_{i+1})\leq r/8\leq\sqrt{s}/2
so that by Proposition
3.1
,

\displaystyle p^{\omega}(s,y_{i},y_{i+1})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{14}}{C_{\mathrm{PH}}\big(\|\mu^{\omega}\|_{p,B_{y_{j}}},\|\nu^{\omega}\|_{q,B_{y_{j}}}\big)}\,s^{-\frac{d}{2}}\geq\frac{c}{C_{\mathrm{PH}}\big(\|\mu^{\omega}\|_{p,B_{y_{j}}},\|\nu^{\omega}\|_{q,B_{y_{j}}}\big)}\,r^{-d},

with
B_{y_{j}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=B(y_{j},\sqrt{s})
. Further, recall the representation of
C_{\mathrm{PH}}
in (
3.2
) and that
\mathrm{P}_{y_{j}}^{\omega}[X_{s}=y_{j+1}]=p^{\omega}(s,y_{j},y_{j+1})\mu^{\omega}(y_{j+1})
. Hence, by the Markov property,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{0}^{\omega}\left[X_{t}=x\right]=\mathrm{P}_{0}^{\omega}\left[X_{ks}=x\right]\geq\mathrm{P}_{0}^{\omega}\left[X_{js}\in B_{j},1\leq j\leq k-1,X_{ks}=x\right]

\displaystyle\mspace{36mu}\geq\sum_{y_{1}\in B_{1},\dots,y_{k-1}\in B_{k-1}}\frac{c^{k}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}r^{-d}\mu^{\omega}(y_{j})\right)s^{-d/2}\mu^{\omega}(x)}{\exp\Big(c\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(1\vee\|\mu^{\omega}\|_{p,B_{y_{j}}}\big)^{\kappa}\big(1\vee\|\nu^{\omega}\|_{q,B_{y_{j}}}\big)^{\kappa}\Big)}

\displaystyle\mspace{36mu}\geq c^{k}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\|\mu^{\omega}\|_{1,B_{j}}\bigg)\,s^{-d/2}\mu^{\omega}(x),

where we used Corollary
3.4
in the last step.
In particular,

p^{\omega}(t,0,x)\geq c^{k}\,\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\|\mu^{\omega}\|_{1,B_{j}}\bigg)\,t^{-d/2}.

Now, by the harmonic-geometric mean inequality and Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\|\mu^{\omega}\|_{1,B_{j}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\geq\frac{k-1}{\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left\lVert\mu^{\omega}\right\rVert_{1,B_{j}}^{-1}}\geq\frac{c\,(k-1)}{\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left\lVert\nu^{\omega}\right\rVert_{1,B_{j}}}.

We use Proposition
3.2
(setting
p=q=1
,
y_{j}=z_{j}
, and replacing
B_{y_{j}}
with
B_{j}
) to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left\lVert\nu^{\omega}\right\rVert_{1,B_{j}}\leq c\,(k-1),

so that

\displaystyle\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\|\mu^{\omega}\|_{1,B_{j}}\bigg)\geq c^{k-1}.

Combining this with (
3
) yields
p^{\omega}(t,0,x)\geq c\,c_{18}^{k}t^{-d/2}
for some
c_{18}\in(0,1)
, which gives the bound (
1.5
) by the choice of
k
.
∎

4.
Green kernel estimates

In this final section we utilize Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
to establish Theorem
1.6
. We refer to
[
15
, Section 6]
for similar arguments.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

(i) First we deduce the upper bound (
1.6
) on the Green kernel. For any distinct
x,y\in\Z^{d}
, we decompose the integral as

(4.1)

g^{\omega}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\mu^{\omega}(x)}\int_{0}^{N_{1}(x)^{2}}\textrm{P}_{y}^{\omega}(X_{t}=x)\,\textrm{d}t+\int_{N_{1}(x)^{2}}^{N_{x,y}}p_{t}^{\omega}(x,y)\ \textrm{d}t+\int_{N_{x,y}}^{\infty}p_{t}^{\omega}(x,y)\ \textrm{d}t

with
N_{x,y}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=N_{1}(x)^{2}\vee(\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert/{c_{1}})
, where we used that
p_{t}^{\omega}(x,y)=p_{t}^{\omega}(y,x)
by the symmetry of the heat kernel. Using Theorem
1.2
we can bound the last two terms of (
4.1
) by

\displaystyle\int_{N_{1}(x)^{2}}^{N_{x,y}}p_{t}^{\omega}(x,y)\ \textrm{d}t\leq c_{2}e^{-c_{4}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-d/2}\ \textrm{d}t\leq c\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{2-d},

and

\displaystyle\int_{N_{x,y}}^{\infty}p_{t}^{\omega}(x,y)\ \textrm{d}t\leq c_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-d/2}\,e^{-c_{3}|x-y|^{2}/t}\ \textrm{d}t\leq c\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{2-d}.

It is left to bound the first term in the right hand side of (
4.1
). Recall that the random walk
X
spends i.i.d.
\mathrm{Exp}(1)
-distributed waiting times between its jumps. Set
\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=N_{1}(x)^{2}
and
r:=\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert\geq 1
. In particular, the random walk starting at
y
needs to perform at least
r
jumps to get to
x
. Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu^{\omega}(x)}\int_{0}^{\lambda}\textrm{P}_{y}^{\omega}(X_{t}=x)\,\textrm{d}t\leq\frac{\lambda}{\mu^{\omega}(x)}\textrm{P}_{y}^{\omega}\big(X_{t}=x\text{ for any }t\in[0,\lambda]\big)

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mspace{36mu}\leq\frac{\lambda}{\mu^{\omega}(x)}\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)\big([r,\infty)\big).

Here
\textrm{Pois}(\lambda)
denotes the Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda
, which we recall to have exponential tails (see e.g.
[
35
, Remark 2.6]
). So there exists
{N_{2}=N_{2}(\omega,x)}
such that for each
y\in\Z^{d}
with
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert\geq N_{2}(\omega,x)
the first term in (
4.1
) is bounded from above by
c\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{2-d}
, which completes the proof of (
1.6
).

(ii) This follows directly from Theorem
1.4
, which gives for
x,y\in\Z^{d}
with
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert>N(x)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{t}^{\omega}(x,y)\ \textrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\int_{N(x)|x-y|}^{\infty}c_{6}\,t^{-d/2}e^{-c_{7}|x-y|^{2}/t}\ \textrm{d}t\geq\int_{|x-y|^{2}}^{\infty}c_{6}\,t^{-d/2}e^{c_{7}|x-y|^{2}/t}\ \textrm{d}t

\displaystyle=|x-y|^{2-d}\int_{1}^{\infty}c_{6}\,t^{-d/2}\,e^{-c_{7}/t}\,\textrm{d}t=c\,|x-y|^{2-d}.

(iii) First, we carry out some preparation for the proof of the upper bound. In particular, we show the Green kernel has finite second moments. By the symmetry of the heat kernel and the on-diagonal part of the upper bound in Theorem
1.2
, note that

\displaystyle g^{\omega}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{N_{1}(x)^{2}}p^{\omega}_{t}(y,x)\ \mathrm{d}t+\int_{N_{1}(x)^{2}}^{\infty}p^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)\ \mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{N_{1}(x)^{2}}{\mu^{\omega}(x)}+cN_{1}(x)^{2-d}

\displaystyle\mspace{36mu}\leq cN_{1}(x)^{2}\nu^{\omega}(x),

where we used Jensen’s inequality in the last step.
Assuming that
\omega(e)\in L^{p_{0}}(\prob)
and
\omega(e)^{-1}\in L^{q_{0}}(\prob)
for suitable
p_{0},q_{0}\in(1,\infty)
, we apply Proposition
2.2
together with Lemma
2.6
(with
\zeta=d
) to obtain that
N_{1}\in L^{\beta}(\pr)
for any
\beta<d^{2}-1
. Thus, by Hölder’s inequality,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mean\big[g^{\omega}(x,y)^{\beta}\big]<\infty,

for any
\beta<(d^{2}-1)(q_{0}-1)/(2q_{0})
. Then, as
d\geq 3
, assuming
q_{0}>2
ensures that the second moment of the Green kernel exists.

We can now prove the upper bound of (
1.8
). To do so, we show that the random variable
N_{2}
introduced in (i) satisfies the tail bound

(4.4)

\displaystyle\prob\big[N_{2}>u]\leq c\,u^{2-d},\qquad\forall u\geq 1.

Indeed, if (
4.4
) holds true, then we obtain

\displaystyle\mean\big[g^{\omega}(x,y)\big]\leq c\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{2-d}+\mean\big[g^{\omega}(x,y)^{2}\big]^{1/2}\,\pr\big[N_{2}>|x-y|\big]\leq c\,\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{2-d},

where we used (
1.6
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the first step, and (
4.4
) and (
4.3
) in the second step.

In order to show (
4.4
), recall that
N_{2}
has been chosen as a value of
r
such that

\frac{\lambda}{\mu^{\omega}(x)}\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)\big([r,\infty)\big)\leq cr^{2-d}

with
\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=N_{1}(x)^{2}
. By Chernoff’s inequality (cf. e.g.
[
35
, Corollary 2.4 and Remark 2.6]
),
\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)\big([r,\infty)\big)\leq e^{-r+7\lambda}
for
r>7\lambda
. Hence,
N_{2}
can be chosen as a constant times
\lambda=N_{1}^{2}
, so the tail bound on
N_{2}
can be dominated by a tail bound on
N_{1}^{2}
, which is provided by Proposition
2.2
and Lemma
2.6
(with the choice
\zeta=d
) under suitable moment conditions on
\omega(e)
and
\omega(e)^{-1}
. More precisely,

\pr(N_{2}>u)\leq\pr(N_{1}^{2}>cu)\leq c\,u^{\frac{1-d^{2}}{2}}\leq c\,u^{2-d},\qquad u\geq 1,

since
(1-d^{2})/2<2-d
for
d\geq 3
, which completes the proof of (
4.4
).

Finally, we prove the lower bound of (
1.8
), which follows again from Theorem
1.4
. Choose
K\in(0,\infty)
such that
\pr(N(x)\leq K)=\pr(N(0)\leq K)\geq 1/2
, then

\mean\big[g^{\omega}(x,y)\big]\geq\mean\Big[\int_{N(x)}^{\infty}p_{t}^{\omega}(x,y)\ \textrm{d}t\Big]\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{K}^{\infty}c_{6}\,t^{-d/2}\,e^{-c_{7}|x-y|^{2}/t}\ \textrm{d}t.

If
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{2}\geq K
then we can bound the integral on the right hand side as in the proof of (
1.7
) to give (
1.8
).
On the other hand, there are only finitely many vertices
z\in B(0,\sqrt{K})
, and for each such
z
we have
\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\omega}(0,z)\right]>0
. Therefore,
\inf_{y\in B(x,\sqrt{K})}\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\omega}(x,y)\right]=\inf_{z\in B(0,\sqrt{K})}\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\omega}(0,z)\right]>0
. Thus,
we can adjust the constant
c_{10}
such that (
1.8
) also holds for
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{2}\leq K
.
∎
