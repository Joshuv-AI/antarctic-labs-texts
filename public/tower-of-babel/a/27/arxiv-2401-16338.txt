Limit theorems for compensated weighted sums and application to numerical approximations
Yanghui Liu

Abstract
. In this paper, we consider a “compensated” random sum that arises from numerical approximation of stochastic integrations and differential equations. We show that the compensated sum exhibits some surprising cancellations among its components, a property which allows to transform it into a Skorohod-type Riemann sum. We then establish limit theorem for the compensated sum based on study of the Skorohod-type Riemann sum. Our proof employs techniques from Malliavin calculus and rough path. We apply our limit theorem result to the Euler approximation method for stochastic integrals and additive stochastic differential equations, filling a notable gap in this area of research. We show that the Euler method converges to the solution at the rate ( 1 / n ) H + 1 / 2 (1/n)^{H+1/2} , and that this rate is exact in the sense that the asymptotic error distribution solves a linear differential equation.



1.
Introduction

Consider a stochastic process
(h^{(n)}_{k},k=0,\dots,n-1)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and an associated “weight” process
(y_{t},t\in[0,T])
.
Let

[0,T]
.
Suppose that
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}h^{(n)}_{k}
converges in distribution as
n\to\infty
, to a Wiener process for instance. What can be said about the asymptotic behavior of the weighted sum:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}y_{t_{k}}h^{(n)}_{k}?

This problem has drawn a lot of attention in the recent literature due to its essential roles in topics such as stochastic integration (see e.g.
[
3
,
4
,
22
,
33
]
), numerical approximation (see e.g.
[
13
,
15
,

]
), and parameter estimation (see e.g.
[

,
5
,
6
,
7
]
). The limit theorem results for (

) have been established in various settings (see e.g.

[
4
,
24
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
32
]
).

Inspired by these contributions, the purpose of this paper is to study a compensated weighted sum in the following form:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\big(y_{t_{k}}h_{k}^{(n),1}+y_{t_{k}}^{\prime}h_{k}^{(n),2}+\cdots+y_{t_{k}}^{(\ell-1)}h_{k}^{(n),\ell}\big)\qquad\text{for}\quad\ell\geq 2,

where
h^{(n),i}
are defined by:

\displaystyle h^{(n),i}_{k}=\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}x^{i}_{t_{k}u}du\qquad\text{with}\quad x^{i}_{t_{k}u}:=(x_{u}-x_{t_{k}})^{i}/i!\,

and
(x_{t},t\geq 0)
is a fBm with Hurst parameter
H<1/2
. Here
(y,y^{\prime},\dots,y^{(\ell-1)})
is a process “controlled” by
x
(see Definition
2.1
).
As an application of the compensated sum (
1.2
), we will investigate Euler’s numerical methods for stochastic integrals and additive stochastic differential equations driven by fBm. Our aim is to derive the exact rate and asymptotic error distribution of these numerical methods, and thus to fill a notable gap in this field of research.

A main challenge concerning the compensated weighted sum (
1.2
) arises from the cancellations present among individual weighted sums.
For example, consider the case of
\ell=2
in (
1.2
), namely the compensated sum:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\big(y_{t_{k}}h^{(n),1}_{k}+y_{t_{k}}^{\prime}h^{(n),2}_{k}\big).

It has been shown that both weighted sums in (
1.3

(1/n)^{2H}
(see
[
25
]
). Surprisingly, as we will see in this paper, the compensated sum (

stands for the minimum between

and

. In fact, we will show that the larger

in (

A main effort of this paper will be devoted to understanding the cancellations in (

In the following we state our main limit theorem result. The reader is referred to Theorem
3.5
for a more precise statement.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\ell H>1/2
. Then
the compensated sum in (
1.2
) converges stably to the conditional Gaussian random variable
c_{H}^{1/2}T^{H+1/2}\int_{0}^{T}y_{u}dW_{u}
as the mesh size of
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
tends to zero, where
W
is a Brownian motion independent of

and

is a constant depending on

only.

The proof of Theorem

is a combination of rough path techniques and Malliavin calculus. More specifically, we will show that the compensated sum can be reduced to some monomial-weighted sums based on the approach developed in
[

. These simpler compensated sums allow us to investigate the cancellations by applying the Malliavin calculus tools. Eventually, the asymptotic distribution of (

[

,

]
.

As a main application of Theorem

we consider the Euler method for additive stochastic differential equations (SDE for short).
Let

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(y_{u})du+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(u)dx_{u}\qquad t\in[0,T],

\displaystyle y^{(n)}_{t_{k}}+b(y^{(n)}_{t_{k}})(t-t_{k})+\sigma(t_{k})(x_{t}-x_{t_{k}})\qquad t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}].

The reader is referred to

On the other hand, the analysis of Euler method (

In this paper we will see that the convergence of Euler method (

In the following we state our asymptotic error distribution result for the Euler method (a more precise statement can be found in Theorem

Theorem 1.2
.

The process

converges in distribution to the solution of the linear differential equation:

\displaystyle U_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\partial b(y_{u})U_{u}du+c_{H}^{1/2}T^{H+1/2}\int_{0}^{t}\big[\partial b(y_{u})\sigma(u)-\sigma^{\prime}(u)\big]dW_{u}

t\in[0,T]
as

tends to infinity.

Our second application is focused on numerical approximation of the regular integral
\int_{0}^{t}y_{u}du
, stated as follows. The reader is referred to Theorem

Theorem 1.3
.

Let the assumptions be as in Theorem

\displaystyle n^{H+1/2}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}y_{u}d{u}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}y_{t_{k}}\cdot(t_{k+1}-t_{k})\Big)\to c_{H}^{1/2}T^{H+1/2}\int_{0}^{T}y_{u}^{\prime}dW_{u}

We first introduce a general notion of controlled rough process which will be used throughout the paper.

2.2.
Elements of Malliavin calculus

We briefly recall some basic facts of Malliavin calculus. We refer the reader to
[
31
,
34
]
for further details.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a separable Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}
and norm
|\cdot|_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{1/2}
. For
q\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes q}
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{\odot q}

q
th symmetric tensor product of
{\mathcal{H}}
. Let
X=\{X(h):h\in{\mathcal{H}}\}
be an isonormal Gaussian process over
{\mathcal{H}}
, namely,
X
is a centered Gaussian family defined on some probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},P)
such that
\mathbb{E}[X(g)X(h)]=\langle g,h\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}
for every
g,h\in{\mathcal{H}}
. Let
\mathit{S}
denote the set of random variables of the form

F=f(W(h_{1}),\dots,W(h_{n}))
,
where
n\geq 1
,
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, and

is a

-function such that

and its partial derivatives have at most polynomial growth. The

th Malliavin derivative of

is a random variable with values in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\odot p}
defined by:

\displaystyle D^{l}F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{l}=1}^{m}\frac{\partial^{l}f}{\partial x_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{i_{l}}}(X(h_{1}),\dots,X(h_{m}))h_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots h_{i_{l}}.

For any
p\geq 1
and any integer
k\geq 1
, we define the Sobolev space
\mathbb{D}^{k,p}
as the closure of
\mathit{S}
with respect to the norm:

\displaystyle\left\|F\right\|_{k,p}^{p}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|F\right|^{p}\right]+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\big[\|D^{l}F\|^{p}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes l}}\big].

We denote by
\delta^{\diamond,k}
the adjoint of the derivative operator

. We say

if there is a
\delta^{\diamond,k}(u)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that for any

the following duality relationship holds:

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\langle u,D^{k}F\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes k}})=\mathbb{E}[\delta^{\diamond,k}(u)F].

When

we simply write

.
Recall that for

we have the relation:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\delta^{\diamond,k}(h^{\otimes k})=H_{k}(\delta^{\diamond}(h)),

where

is called the Hermite polynomial of order

.

The following result is an elaboration of
[

, Proposition 1.3.1]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

and
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
. Then we have the relation:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\delta^{\diamond}(F_{1}h_{1})\delta^{\diamond}(F_{2}h_{2})]=\mathbb{E}[F_{1}F_{2}]\langle h_{1},h_{2}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}+\mathbb{E}[\langle DF_{1},h_{2}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\langle DF_{2},h_{1}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}].

Proof.

By linearity of relation (
2.8
) in
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
in order to show (
2.8
) it suffices to consider two cases: (i)
h_{1}=h_{2}
and (ii)

such that
|h_{1}|_{{\mathcal{H}}}=|h_{2}|_{{\mathcal{H}}}=1
.

According to
[
34
, Proposition 1.3.1]
, for

and a complete orthonormal system

of

we have the relation

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\delta^{\diamond}(u)\delta^{\diamond}(v)]=\mathbb{E}[\langle u,v\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}]+\mathbb{E}\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}[D_{e_{i}}\langle u,e_{j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\cdot D_{e_{j}}\langle v,e_{i}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}].

and let

be a complete orthonormal system of

. Take

, and

in (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}[D_{e_{i}}\langle u,e_{j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\cdot D_{e_{j}}\langle v,e_{i}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}]=\mathbb{E}\left[D_{e_{1}}\langle u,e_{1}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\cdot D_{e_{1}}\langle v,e_{1}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}[D_{h_{1}}F_{1}D_{h_{2}}F_{2}].

Substituting this into (

In Case (ii) we take

and

. As before, by taking

,

Let
x
be a standard fractional Brownian motion (fBm for short) with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
, that is

is a continuous Gaussian process with covariance function

\mathbb{E}[x_{s}x_{t}]=\frac{1}{2}(|s|^{2H}+|t|^{2H}-|s-t|^{2H})
.
Recall that the fBm

is almost surely
{\gamma}
-Hölder continuous for all
{\gamma}<H
.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be the completion of the space of indicator functions with respect to the inner product
\langle\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]},\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\mathbb{E}(\delta x_{uv}\delta x_{st})
.
In the following we state an estimate result for indicate functions which we make extensive use of.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\rho
be the function
\rho(x)=|x|\vee 1
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
There exists a constant
K>0
such that the following estimate holds:

(2.10)

\displaystyle\left|\langle\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]},\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K|v-u|^{2H}\cdot\left([|s^{\prime}|^{2H-1}+|t^{\prime}|^{2H-1}]\wedge 2^{2H-1}\right)

(2.11)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K|v-u|^{2H}\cdot\left(\rho(s^{\prime})^{2H-1}+\rho(t^{\prime})^{2H-1}\right)

for all
(s,t)
and
(u,v)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, where we denote
s^{\prime}=\frac{s-u}{v-u}
and
t^{\prime}=\frac{t-u}{v-u}
.

Proof.

By self-similarity and stationarity of increments of fBm we have

(2.12)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]},\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}=|v-u|^{2H}\langle\mathbf{1}_{[s^{\prime},t^{\prime}]},\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}.

An elementary estimation gives:

\displaystyle|\langle\mathbf{1}_{[s^{\prime},t^{\prime}]},\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\langle\mathbf{1}_{[s^{\prime},t^{\prime}]\cap[0,1]},\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}|+|\langle\mathbf{1}_{[s^{\prime},t^{\prime}]\setminus[0,1]},\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+1\leq 4\cdot 2^{2H-1}.

Substituting this into (
2.12
) we get:

(2.13)

\displaystyle\left|\langle\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]},\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\cdot 2^{2H-1}|v-u|^{2H}.

The estimate (
2.13
) shows that the relation (
2.10
) holds when
|s^{\prime}|^{2H-1}+|t^{\prime}|^{2H-1}\geq 2^{2H-1}
.

It remains to prove that (
2.10
) holds under the condition that
|s^{\prime}|^{2H-1}+|t^{\prime}|^{2H-1}\leq 2^{2H-1}
. Note that this condition implies that
|s^{\prime}|\geq 2
and
|t^{\prime}|\geq 2
, and so we must have either (i)
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\cap(0,1)=\emptyset
or (ii)

(0,1)\subset(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
.

Assume first that
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\cap(0,1)=\emptyset
. Then either
t^{\prime}\leq 2
or
s^{\prime}\geq 2
. When
t^{\prime}\leq 2
, we have

(2.14)

\displaystyle|\langle\mathbf{1}_{[s^{\prime},t^{\prime}]},\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}|\leq|\langle\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,t^{\prime}]},\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}|\lesssim|t^{\prime}|^{2H-1},

which together with (
2.12
) gives the estimate (
2.10
).
In the same way we can show that (
2.10
) also holds when
s^{\prime}\geq 2
.

Suppose now that
(0,1)\subset(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
. In this case we have:

(2.15)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{1}_{[s^{\prime},t^{\prime}]},\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\langle\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,s^{\prime}]},\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}+\langle\mathbf{1}_{[t^{\prime},\infty)},\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}.

Since
(-\infty,s^{\prime})\cap(0,1)=\emptyset
and
(t^{\prime},\infty)\cap(0,1)=\emptyset
we can apply (
2.14
) to the terms in the right-hand side of (
2.15
). This gives the estimate:

(2.16)

\displaystyle|\langle\mathbf{1}_{[s^{\prime},t^{\prime}]},\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}|\lesssim|s^{\prime}|^{2H-1}+|t^{\prime}|^{2H-1}.

The estimate (
2.16
) together with (
2.12
) gives the estimate (
2.10
).
In summary, we have obtained that (
2.10
) holds for both cases (i) and (ii). The proof is now complete.
∎

2.3.
Some lemmas

In this subsection we prove some technical lemmas. Our first result provides convenient enlargements for certain upper-bound estimates.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
A
be a function from
{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket)\times\mathbb{N}
to
\mathbb{R}
. Assume that there exists constants
{\alpha}>0
and
{\beta},{\gamma},K_{1}\geq 0
such that

(2.17)

\displaystyle A(s,t,n)\leq K_{1}(1/n)^{{\alpha}}(t-s)^{{\beta}}[\log(n(t-s))]^{{\gamma}}

for all
(s,t,n)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket)\times\mathbb{N}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a constant
K_{2}>0
such that

(2.18)

\displaystyle A(s,t,n)\leq K_{2}(1/n)^{{\alpha}-\varepsilon}(t-s)^{{\beta}+\varepsilon}

for all
(s,t,n)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket)\times\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, we have the convergence:

(2.19)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket)}n^{{\alpha}-\varepsilon}A(s,t,n)=0.

Proof.

Since
(\log x)^{{\gamma}}/x^{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
x\to\infty
, we can find a constant
K>0
such that

(2.20)

\displaystyle[\log(n(t-s))]^{{\gamma}}\leq K(n(t-s))^{\varepsilon}

for all
(s,t,n)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket)\times\mathbb{N}
.
Substituting (
2.20
) into the right-hand side of (
2.17
) we obtain (
2.18
). To see that (
2.19
) holds, replacing
\varepsilon
by
\varepsilon/2
in (
2.18
) to get:

\displaystyle A(s,t,n)\leq K_{2}(1/n)^{{\alpha}-\varepsilon/2}(t-s)^{{\beta}+\varepsilon/2}.

This gives

\displaystyle n^{{\alpha}-\varepsilon}A(s,t,n)\leq K_{2}(1/n)^{\varepsilon/2}(t-s)^{{\beta}+\varepsilon/2}\leq K_{2}(1/n)^{\varepsilon/2}T^{{\beta}+\varepsilon/2}.

Now sending
n\to\infty
we obtain the convergence (
2.19
).
∎

In the following we state an elementary result for
p
-series.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\rho
be the function defined by:
\rho(x)=|x|\vee 1
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Take
{\alpha}\leq 0
.

(i)

There exists a constant
K>0
such that the following estimate holds:

(2.21)

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\,sn\leq k<tn}\rho(k-k^{\prime})^{{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{cases}K(n(t-s))^{{\alpha}+1}&{\alpha}>-1\\
K\log(n(t-s))&{\alpha}=-1\\
K&{\alpha}<-1\end{cases}

for all
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T])
and
k^{\prime}\in[0,n]
.

(ii)
When
k^{\prime}\not\in[sn,tn)
and
{\alpha}<-1
we have the estimate:

(2.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\,sn\leq k<tn}\rho(k-k^{\prime})^{{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{cases}K\rho(k^{\prime}-ns)^{{\alpha}+1}&k^{\prime}<ns.\\
K\rho(k^{\prime}-nt)^{{\alpha}+1}&k^{\prime}\geq nt.\end{cases}

Proof.

We first consider the case when
k^{\prime}\in[sn,tn)
. Write:

(2.23)

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\,sn\leq k<tn}\rho(k-k^{\prime})^{{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\,k^{\prime}\leq k<tn}\rho(k-k^{\prime})^{{\alpha}}+\sum_{k:\,sn\leq k<k^{\prime}}\rho(k-k^{\prime})^{{\alpha}}.

Applying a change of variables to each sum in the right-hand side of (
2.23
) we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\,sn\leq k<tn}\rho(k-k^{\prime})^{{\alpha}}=\sum_{k:\,0\leq k<tn-k^{\prime}}\rho(k)^{{\alpha}}+\sum_{k:\,0<k\leq k^{\prime}-ns}\rho(k)^{{\alpha}}.

It follows that

(2.24)

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\,sn\leq k<tn}\rho(k-k^{\prime})^{{\alpha}}\leq 2\sum_{k:\,0\leq k<(t-s)n}\rho(k)^{{\alpha}}\leq 2\int_{1}^{(t-s)n}x^{{\alpha}}dx.

The estimate (
2.22
) then follows.

Assume now that
k^{\prime}<sn
. As before, a change of variables gives:

(2.25)

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\,sn\leq k<tn}\rho(k-k^{\prime})^{{\alpha}}=\sum_{k:\,sn-k^{\prime}\leq k<tn-k^{\prime}}\rho(k)^{{\alpha}}\leq\int_{sn-k^{\prime}}^{tn-k^{\prime}}x^{{\alpha}}dx.

It follows that the estimate (
2.21
) holds. In the same way we can prove (
2.21
) for
k^{\prime}\geq tn
. In summary, we have proved Lemma
2.7
(i).

To see that (
2.22
) is true, we note that when
{\alpha}<-1
the right-hand side of (
2.25
) can be bounded by
\int_{sn-k^{\prime}}^{\infty}x^{{\alpha}}dx
, and thus by
\rho(k^{\prime}-sn)^{{\alpha}+1}
.
The estimate (
2.22
) then follows. In the same way we can prove the estimate for
k^{\prime}>tn
.
∎

We end the section by stating the following classical result.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
U_{n},U_{\infty},V_{n,M},\widetilde{V}_{n,M}
,
M,n\in\mathbb{N}
be random variables with values in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and assume that
U_{n}=V_{n,M}+\widetilde{V}_{n,M}
for all
M,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that

(2.26)

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}P(V_{n,M}\leq x)=P(U_{\infty}\leq x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Denote
\widetilde{V}_{M,n}=(\widetilde{V}_{M,n}^{1},\dots,\widetilde{V}_{M,n}^{N})
and assume that

(2.27)

\displaystyle\limsup_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(|\widetilde{V}_{n,M}^{i}|\geq\varepsilon)\to 0\qquad\text{as $\varepsilon\to 0$ }

for all
i=1,\dots,N
.
Then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
P(U_{\infty}=x)=0
we have

(2.28)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P(U_{n}\leq x)=P(U_{\infty}\leq x).

Proof.

We first have the following elementary relation for
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
:

\displaystyle P(V_{M,n}\leq x-\varepsilon)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}P(|\widetilde{V}_{M,n}^{i}|\geq\varepsilon)\leq P(V_{M,n}+\widetilde{V}_{M,n}\leq x)

\displaystyle\leq P(V_{M,n}\leq x+\varepsilon)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}P(|\widetilde{V}_{M,n}^{i}|\geq\varepsilon).

Sending
n\to\infty
and then
M\to\infty
, and substituting the relation
V_{M,n}+\widetilde{V}_{M,n}=U_{n}
gives:

\displaystyle P(U_{\infty}\leq x-\varepsilon)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\limsup_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(|\widetilde{V}_{M,n}^{i}|\geq\varepsilon)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}P(U_{n}\leq x),

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(U_{n}\leq x)\leq P(U_{\infty}\leq x+\varepsilon)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\limsup_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(|\widetilde{V}_{M,n}^{i}|\geq\varepsilon).

Sending
\varepsilon\to\infty
and invoking relation (
2.27
) and that
P(U_{\infty}=x)=0
we obtain (
2.28
).
∎

\displaystyle n^{-H-1}\sum_{s_{j}\leq t_{k},t_{k^{\prime}}<s_{j+1}}\rho(k-k^{\prime})^{3H-2}.

\displaystyle n^{-H-1}(n^{3H}+n\log n)

as
n\to\infty
.

We turn to the estimate of
\widetilde{Z}_{2}^{n,j}
.
Note that

(3.74)

\displaystyle\sum_{s_{j}\leq t_{k^{\prime}}<s_{j+1}}\mu(k^{\prime}-k)-c_{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s_{j}\leq t_{k^{\prime}}<s_{j+1}}\mu(k^{\prime}-k)-\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mu(k^{\prime}-k)

\displaystyle\sum_{t_{k^{\prime}}<s_{j}}\mu(k^{\prime}-k)+\sum_{t_{k^{\prime}}\geq s_{j+1}}\mu(k^{\prime}-k).

n\to\infty

It is clear that we also have

In summary, we have shown that the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}^{(n)}_{2}+\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}{\mathcal{I}}_{11}^{jj}\to 0\qquad\text{as $n\to\infty$.}

It follows from (

Then by (

as

Remark 3.4

\displaystyle\delta{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}_{sut}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}r^{(j-1)}_{su}{\mathcal{E}}_{j}^{x}(u,t).

Applying Lemma
3.3
(i) to
{\mathcal{E}}_{j}^{x}(u,t)
in (
3.144
) and taking into account the estimate
|r^{(j-1)}_{su}|_{L_{2p}}\leq K(u-s)^{(\ell-j+1)H}
we obtain:

(3.145)

\displaystyle|\delta{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}_{sut}|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}|r^{(j-1)}_{su}|_{L_{2p}}\cdot|{\mathcal{E}}_{j}^{x}(u,t)|_{L_{2p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(u-s)^{(\ell-j+1)(H-\varepsilon)}(1/n)^{H+1/2}(t-u)^{(j-1)H+1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1/n)^{H+1/2}(t-s)^{\ell(H-\varepsilon)+1/2}.

Since
\ell H+1/2>1
, according to the discrete sewing lemma (see e.g.
[
23
, Lemma 2.7]
) the relation (
3.145
) implies that we have the estimate:

(3.146)

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}_{st}|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(1/n)^{H+1/2}(t-s)^{\ell(H-\varepsilon)+1/2}.

Step 2: Estimate of
{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}(s,t)
.

By Definition
2.1
we have

z^{(i-1)}_{t}=\sum_{j=i}^{\ell}z^{(j-1)}_{s}x^{j-i}_{st}+r^{(i-1)}_{st}

for
i=1,\dots,\ell
.
Substituting this relation into (
3.76
) we obtain:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=i}^{\ell}z^{(j-1)}_{s}{\mathcal{J}}_{s}^{t}(x^{j-i},h^{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}{\mathcal{J}}_{s}^{t}(r^{(i-1)},h^{i})

Exchanging the order of summations in
i
and
j
and invoking the definition of
{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}_{st}
we obtain:

(3.147)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}(s,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}z^{(j-1)}_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{j}{\mathcal{J}}_{s}^{t}(x^{j-i},h^{i})+{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}_{st}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}z^{(j-1)}_{s}{\mathcal{E}}^{x}_{j}(s,t)+{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}_{st}.

Taking
L_{p}
norm in both sides of (
3.147
) and
then applying the estimates (
3.79
) and (
3.146
) to the right-hand side we obtain:

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}(s,t)|_{L_{p}}\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}(1/n)^{H+1/2}(t-s)^{1/2+(L-1)H}

(3.148)

\displaystyle\qquad+(1/n)^{H+1/2}(t-s)^{\ell(H-\varepsilon)+1/2}.

It is readily checked that the right-hand side of (
3.148
) is bounded by
K(1/n)^{H+1/2}(t-s)^{1/2}
for some constant
K
. This completes the proof of Theorem
3.5
(i).

Step 3: Convergence of
{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}
.

Recall that
\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket
stands for the uniform parition of
[0,T]
:
0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
.
Take another partition of
[0,T]
:

\pi=\{0=s_{0}<\cdots<s_{N}=T\}
. For each subinterval
[s_{l},s_{l+1}]
,
l=0,\dots,N-1
take
M\in\mathbb{N}
and let
s_{l}=u_{0}^{l}<u_{1}^{l}<\cdots<u_{M}^{l}=s_{l+1}
be the uniform partition of
[s_{l},s_{l+1}]
. For convenience we will write
u^{l}_{j}=u_{j}
in the sequel.

Let
u_{j}^{\prime}\in\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket
,
j=0,\dots,M
be such that
\{t_{k}:u_{j}^{\prime}\leq t_{k}<u_{j+1}^{\prime}\}=\{t_{k}:u_{j}\leq t_{k}<u_{j+1}\}
.
By the definition of
{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}(s_{l},s_{l+1})
in (
3.76
) it is easy to see that

(3.149)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}(s_{l},s_{l+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}(u_{j},u_{j+1})=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}(u_{j}^{\prime},u_{j+1}^{\prime}).

Substituting the relation (
3.147
) into the right-hand side of (
3.149
) and then multiplying both sides of (
3.149
) by
n^{H+1/2}
we have

(3.150)

\displaystyle n^{H+1/2}{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}(s_{l},s_{l+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{H+1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u^{\prime}_{j}}{\mathcal{E}}^{x}_{i}(u^{\prime}_{j},u^{\prime}_{j+1})+n^{H+1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}_{u^{\prime}_{j}u^{\prime}_{j+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{n,M}^{l}+\widetilde{V}_{n,M}^{l},

where

(3.151)

\displaystyle V_{n,M}^{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{H+1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z_{u^{\prime}_{j}}{\mathcal{E}}^{x}_{1}(u^{\prime}_{j},u^{\prime}_{j+1})

(3.152)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{n,M}^{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{H+1/2}\sum_{i=2}^{\ell}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u^{\prime}_{j}}{\mathcal{E}}^{x}_{i}(u^{\prime}_{j},u^{\prime}_{j+1})+n^{H+1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}_{u^{\prime}_{j}u^{\prime}_{j+1}}.

In the following we consider the convergence of the components in (
3.151
)-(
3.152
). The convergence of
n^{H+1/2}{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}
will then follow by applying Lemma
2.8
.

Recall the estimate (
3.146
) for
{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}_{st}
. So we have

(3.153)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\big|\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}_{u_{j}^{\prime}u_{j+1}^{\prime}}\big|_{L_{p}}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\big|{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}_{u_{j}^{\prime}u_{j+1}^{\prime}}\big|_{L_{p}}\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}(1/n)^{H+1/2}(u_{j+1}^{\prime}-u_{j}^{\prime})^{\ell H+1/2}.

Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}(u_{j}^{\prime},u_{j+1}^{\prime})=(u_{j},u_{j+1})
,
sending
n\to\infty
and then
M\to\infty
in (
3.153
) gives:

(3.154)

\displaystyle\qquad\lim_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}n^{H+1/2}\big|\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}_{u_{j}^{\prime}u_{j+1}^{\prime}}\big|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}(u_{j+1}-u_{j})^{\ell H+1/2}=0,

where the last relation in (
3.154
) holds since the exponent
\ell H+1/2
is greater than
1
.

We turn to the convergence of
n^{H+1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}}^{x}_{i}(u_{j}^{\prime},u_{j+1}^{\prime})
in (
3.152
). By applying Theorem
3.3
(ii) we have the convergence:

(3.155)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\big|n^{H+1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}}^{x}_{i}(u_{j}^{\prime},u_{j+1}^{\prime})-\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}^{\prime}}Z^{(n),i}_{u_{j}^{\prime}u_{j+1}^{\prime}}\big|_{L_{p}}=0.

Note that by Definition
2.1
we have
|z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}^{\prime}}-z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}}|_{L_{p}}\to 0
.
On the other hand, according to relation (
3.1
) we have

Z^{(n),i}_{u_{j}^{\prime}u_{j+1}^{\prime}}=Z^{(n),i}_{u_{j}u_{j+1}}
.
It follows that we can replace
z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}^{\prime}}Z^{(n),i}_{u_{j}^{\prime}u_{j+1}^{\prime}}
by
z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}}Z^{(n),i}_{u_{j}u_{j+1}}
in (
3.155
). Namely, we have

(3.156)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\big|n^{H+1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}}^{x}_{i}(u_{j}^{\prime},u_{j+1}^{\prime})-\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}}Z^{(n),i}_{u_{j}u_{j+1}}\big|_{L_{p}}=0.

Applying Theorem
3.1
to
\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}}Z^{(n),i}_{u_{j}u_{j+1}}
in (
3.156
) we obtain the
{\mathcal{F}}^{x}
-stable convergence:

\displaystyle\Big(n^{H+1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}}^{x}_{i}(u_{j}^{\prime},u_{j+1}^{\prime}),\,l=0,\dots,N-1\Big)

(3.157)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\to\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}}Z^{i}_{u_{j}u_{j+1}},\,l=0,\dots,N-1\Big)\qquad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

The Itô isometry implies that

(3.158)

\displaystyle|\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}}Z^{i}_{u_{j}u_{j+1}}|_{L_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}|z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}}|^{2}\int_{u_{j}}^{u_{j+1}}(x^{i-1}_{u_{j}u})^{2}du

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}|u_{j+1}-u_{j}|^{2(i-1)H+1}.

Since the exponenet
2(i-1)H+1>1
for
i\geq 2
, it follows from (
3.158
) that we have:

(3.159)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}}Z^{i}_{u_{j}u_{j+1}}\to 0

in probability as
M\to\infty
. In summary of (
3.157
) and (
3.159
) we have shown that

(3.160)

\displaystyle n^{H+1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}}^{x}_{i}(u_{j}^{\prime},u_{j+1}^{\prime})\to 0

in distribution as
n\to\infty
and then
M\to\infty
for all
i=2,\dots,\ell
.

When
i=1
equation (
3.3
) becomes
Z^{1}_{u_{j}u_{j+1}}=c_{H}^{1/2}T^{H+1/2}(W_{u_{j+1}}-W_{u_{j}})
, and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}}Z^{i}_{u_{j}u_{j+1}}=c_{H}^{1/2}T^{H+1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z_{u_{j}}(W_{u_{j+1}}-W_{u_{j}}).

Sending
M\to\infty
we obtain the convergence in
L_{2}
to the Wiener integral:

(3.161)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z^{(i-1)}_{u_{j}}Z^{i}_{u_{j}u_{j+1}}\to c_{H}^{1/2}T^{H+1/2}\int_{s_{l}}^{s_{l+1}}z_{u}dW_{u}.

Step 4: Conclusion.

Let

V_{n,M}=(V_{n,M}^{l},x_{s_{l}s_{l+1}}^{1},l=0,\dots,N-1)
,
where
V_{n,M}^{l}
are defined in (
3.151
).
The convergence in (
3.157
) and (
3.161
) shows that we have

(3.162)

\displaystyle V_{n,M}\to\Big(c_{H}^{1/2}T^{H+1/2}\int_{s_{l}}^{s_{l+1}}z_{u}dW_{u},\,\,x_{s_{l}s_{l+1}}^{1},\,l=0,\dots,N-1\Big)

in distribution as
n\to\infty
and then
M\to\infty
.
On the other hand,
by applying the convergences (
3.154
) and (
3.160
) to (
3.152
) we obtain:

(3.163)

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(|\widetilde{V}^{l}_{n,M}|\geq\varepsilon)=0.

Let

\widetilde{V}_{n,M}=(\widetilde{V}_{n,M}^{0},\dots,\widetilde{V}_{n,M}^{N-1},0,\dots,0)
and
V_{n,M}
be defined as above. Then it follows from (
3.150
) that
V_{n,M}+\widetilde{V}_{n,M}
equals the f.d.d. of
({\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}^{z,n},x^{1})
, namely,

\displaystyle V_{n,M}+\widetilde{V}_{n,M}=(n^{H+1/2}{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}(s_{l},s_{l+1}),\,\,x_{s_{l}s_{l+1}}^{1},\,l=0,\dots,N-1).

According to
(
3.162
) and (
3.163
) the conditions (
2.26
) and (
2.27
) in Lemma
2.8
are satisfied. It follows from Lemma
2.8
that the f.d.d. of
(n^{H+1/2}{\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell},x^{1})
converges in distribution to that of
({\mathcal{E}}^{z},x^{1})
as
n\to\infty
. This proves Theorem
3.5
(ii).
∎

We end the section by stating convergence of Riemann sum for the regular integral
\int z_{u}du
, where
z
is a continuous path. The result is a consequence of Theorem
3.5
(i). Recall that
x
is a fBm with Hurst parameter
H<1/2
and
\ell
is the least integer such that
\ell H>1/2
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
{\bf z}=(z,z^{\prime},\dots,z^{(\ell)})
be a continuous process controlled by
(x,\ell+1,H)
almost surely. Let

\displaystyle U^{n}_{st}=n^{H+1/2}\big(\int_{s}^{t}z_{u}d{u}-\sum_{s\leq t_{k}<t}z_{t_{k}}\cdot{T}/{n}\big),\qquad(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T]).

Then the f.d.d. of
U^{n}
converges
{\mathcal{F}}^{x}
-stably to that of
{\mathcal{E}}^{z^{\prime}}
as
n\to\infty
, where
{\mathcal{E}}^{z^{\prime}}(s,t)=c_{H}^{1/2}T^{H+1/2}\int_{s}^{t}z_{u}^{\prime}dW_{u}
and
W
is a standard Brownian motion indpendent of
x
(see (
3.141
)).

Proof.

Let
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(\llbracket 0,T\rrbracket)
be such that
\{t_{k}:s^{\prime}\leq t_{k}<t^{\prime}\}=\{t_{k}:s\leq t_{k}<t\}
.
It is easy to see that we have

(3.164)

\displaystyle U^{n}_{st}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{H+1/2}\big(\sum_{s\leq t_{k}<t}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\delta z_{t_{k}u}du+\int_{s}^{s^{\prime}}z_{u}du-\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}z_{u}du\big).

Since
{\bf z}
is controlled by
(x,\ell+1,H)
(see Definition
2.1
) we have the relation
\delta z_{t_{k}u}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}z_{t_{k}}^{(i)}x^{i}_{t_{k}u}+r^{(0)}_{t_{k}u}
. Substituting this relation into (
3.164
) we obtain

(3.165)

\displaystyle U^{n}_{st}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{H+1/2}{\mathcal{E}}^{z^{\prime},n}_{\ell}(s,t)+R^{U},

where

(3.166)

\displaystyle R^{U}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{H+1/2}\big(\sum_{s\leq t_{k}<t}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}r^{(0)}_{t_{k}u}du+\int_{s}^{s^{\prime}}z_{u}du-\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}z_{u}du\big)

and recall that
{\mathcal{E}}^{z^{\prime},n}_{\ell}(s,t)
is defined in (
3.76
).

By the definition of controlled processes we can find a finite random variable
G
and a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
such that
|r^{(0)}_{t_{k}u}|\leq G(1/n)^{(\ell+1)H-\varepsilon}
for all
(t_{k},u)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(t_{k},t_{k+1})
,
k=0,\dots,n-1
. Applying this relation to
R^{U}
and taking into account that
\ell H>1/2
we obtain that
R^{U}\to 0
almost surely as
n\to\infty
. On the other hand, applying Theorem
3.5
(i) to
{\mathcal{E}}^{z^{\prime},n}_{\ell}(s,t)
we obtain that the f.d.d. of
{\mathcal{E}}^{z^{\prime},n}_{\ell}(s,t)
converges
{\mathcal{F}}^{x}
-stably to that of
{\mathcal{E}}^{z^{\prime}}
. Applying these two convergences to (
3.165
) we obtain the desired convergence of
U^{n}
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Euler method for additive SDEs

Let
y
be solution of additive differential equation:

(4.1)

\displaystyle y_{t}=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(y_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dx_{s}\qquad t\in[0,T],

where
y_{0}
is a constant vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
x
is a
1
-dimensional standard fBm with Hurst parameter
H<1/2
,
\sigma
and
b
are deterministic mappings from
t\in[0,T]
to
\sigma(t)=(\sigma_{1}(t),\dots,\sigma_{m}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and from
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
b(y)=(b_{1}(y),\dots,b_{m}(y))\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, respectively.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}:0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
be the uniform partition on
[0,T]
.
We consider the following Euler method for equation (
4.1
):

(4.2)

\displaystyle y_{t}^{(n)}=y_{t_{k}}^{(n)}+b(y^{(n)}_{t_{k}})(t-t_{k})+\sigma(t_{k})(x_{t}-x_{t_{k}}),\qquad t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]

for
k=0,1,\dots,n-1
.
Denote
\eta(t)=t_{k}
for
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
. Then (
4.2
) can also be written as:

(4.3)

\displaystyle y_{t}^{(n)}=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(y^{(n)}_{\eta(s)})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\eta(s))dx_{s}\,,\qquad t\in[0,T].

In this section we establish the exact rate of convergence and asymptotic error distribution for the Euler method
(
4.3
).

4.1.
Rate of convergence

In this subsection we show that under proper regularity conditions the Euler method converges with the rate
{H+1/2}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
y
be the solution of the equation (
4.1
) and
y^{n}
be the Euler method defined in (
4.2
). Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\ell H>1/2
and assume that
b\in C_{b}^{\ell+1}
and
\sigma\in C^{2}_{b}
.
Then

(i) There exists a constant
K>0
such that the following estimate holds:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\big|(y_{t}-y^{(n)}_{t})-(y_{s}-y^{(n)}_{s})\big|_{L_{2}}\leq K(1/n)^{H+1/2}(t-s)^{1/2}

for all
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T])
.

(ii) The process
n^{H+1/2}(y_{t}-y^{(n)}_{t})
,
t\in[0,T]
is tight on
C([0,T])
.

Remark 4.2
.

A natural question is whether the same rate of the (backward) Euler method holds for multidimensional fBm.
The proof of Theorem
4.1
suggests that this is indeed the case.
We leave the discussion to a future research.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

The proof is divided into several steps.

Step 1: Linear equation for
y-y^{(n)}
.
In this step we derive a linear equation for the error process
y-y^{(n)}
.
By taking difference between the equations (
4.1
) and (
4.3
) we have:

(4.5)

\displaystyle y_{t}-y^{(n)}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}[b(y_{s})-b(y^{(n)}_{\eta(s)})]ds+\int_{0}^{t}[\sigma(s)-\sigma(\eta(s))]dx_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}[b(y_{s})-b(y^{(n)}_{s})]ds+\int_{0}^{t}[b(y^{(n)}_{s})-b(y^{(n)}_{\eta(s)})]ds+\int_{0}^{t}[\sigma(s)-\sigma(\eta(s))]dx_{s}

for
t\in[0,T]
. Denote

\displaystyle b_{1}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}\partial b(y^{(n)}_{s}+\theta(y_{s}-y^{(n)}_{s}))d\theta,\qquad s\in[0,T],

where recall that
\partial b
is defined in (
1.6
).
Then similar to equation (
3.10
) we have the relation:

(4.6)

\displaystyle b(y_{s})-b(y^{(n)}_{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{b_{1}}(s)[y_{s}-y^{(n)}_{s}],

where the right-hand side stands for the matrix multiplication:

\displaystyle{b_{1}}(s)[y_{s}-y^{(n)}_{s}]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{1}{\partial_{j}b}(y^{(n)}_{s}+\theta(y_{s}-y^{(n)}_{s}))d\theta\cdot[y^{j}_{s}-y^{(n),j}_{s}],

and
y_{s}=(y_{s}^{1},\dots,y_{s}^{m})

and
y^{(n)}_{s}=(y_{s}^{(n),1},\dots,y_{s}^{(n),m})
.
Substituting (
4.6
) into (
4.5
) gives:

(4.7)

\displaystyle y_{t}-y^{(n)}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{b_{1}}(s)[y_{s}-y^{(n)}_{s}]ds+\int_{0}^{t}[b(y^{(n)}_{s})-b(y^{(n)}_{\eta(s)})]ds

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}[\sigma(s)-\sigma(\eta(s))]dx_{s},\qquad t\in[0,T].

Step 2: Fundamental solution of (
4.7
).

Let
{\Lambda}^{(n)}
be the
fundamental solution of the linear equation (
4.7
), namely,

(4.8)

\displaystyle{\Lambda}_{t}^{(n)}=\text{Id}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{1}(s){\Lambda}_{s}^{(n)}ds\,,\qquad t\in[0,T].

Let
{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{t}=({\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t})^{-1}
be the inverse matrix of
{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}
. It is well-known that
{\Gamma}^{(n)}
satisfies the equation:

(4.9)

\displaystyle{\Gamma}_{t}^{(n)}=\text{Id}-\int_{0}^{t}{\Gamma}_{s}^{(n)}b_{1}(s)ds,\qquad t\in[0,T].

Applying Gronwall’s inequality to (
4.8
)-(
4.9
) we obtain the following estimates:

(4.10)

\displaystyle|{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}-{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{s}|\leq Ke^{KT}(t-s)\qquad\text{and}\qquad|{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{t}-{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{s}|\leq Ke^{KT}(t-s)

for all
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T])
.

Step 3: Explicit representation of
y-y^{(n)}
.

By definition of
{\Lambda}^{(n)}
in (
4.8
) it is easy to verify that we have

(4.11)

\displaystyle y_{t}-y^{(n)}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{u}[b(y^{(n)}_{u})-b(y^{(n)}_{\eta(u)})]du

\displaystyle\qquad+{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{u}[\sigma(u)-\sigma(\eta(u))]dx_{u}.

From (
4.11
) we can write

(4.12)

\displaystyle(y_{t}-y^{(n)}_{t})-(y_{s}-y^{(n)}_{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}-{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{s})E(0,t)+{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{s}E(s,t)

for
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T])
,
where

(4.13)

\displaystyle E(s,t)=E_{1}(s,t)+E_{2}(s,t),

and

(4.14)

\displaystyle E_{1}(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{u}[b(y^{(n)}_{u})-b(y^{(n)}_{\eta(u)})]du

\displaystyle E_{2}(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{u}[\sigma(u)-\sigma(\eta(u))]dx_{u}\,.

In the following steps

Step 4: Estimate of

when

Step 4: A decomposition of

Step 7: Estimate of

Substituting (

(4.29)

\displaystyle|R^{b}|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle K(1/n)^{(\ell+1)H}(t-s)

By the definition of

\displaystyle|\tilde{r}^{b}_{\eta(u),u}|_{L_{p}}

This implies that

(4.30)

\displaystyle|\widetilde{R}^{b}|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K(1/n)(t-s)\qquad\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$, $(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T]).$}

Step 9: Estimate of

.

We define the processes:

(4.31)

\displaystyle z_{t}^{(i-1)}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{t}\partial^{i}b(y^{(n)}_{t})\sigma(t)^{\otimes i}

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{i}=1}^{m}{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{t}\partial^{i}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{i}}b(y^{(n)}_{t})\sigma_{j_{1}}(t)\cdots\sigma_{j_{i}}(t)

Note that

(4.32)

\displaystyle{(\sigma(\eta(u))x^{1}_{\eta(u)u})^{\otimes i}}/{i!}={\sigma(\eta(u))^{\otimes i}(x^{1}_{\eta(u)u})^{i}}/{i!}={\sigma(\eta(u))^{\otimes i}}x^{i}_{\eta(u)u}.

Substituting (

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle=

Let

,

4.2.
Asymptotic error distribution

With the previous preparations let us now consider asymptotic error distribution of the Euler method.
Let
{\Lambda}
be solution of the equation:

(4.44)

\displaystyle{\Lambda}_{t}=\text{Id}+\int_{0}^{t}\partial b(y_{s}){\Lambda}_{s}ds\,,\qquad t\in[0,T].

Let
{\Gamma}_{t}={\Lambda}_{t}^{-1}
be the inverse matrix of
{\Lambda}_{t}
. As in (
4.9
)-(
4.10
) we have the equation:

(4.45)

\displaystyle{\Gamma}_{t}=\text{Id}-\int_{0}^{t}{\Gamma}_{s}\partial b(y_{s})ds.

The Gronwall’s ienquality implies that we have the estimate:

\displaystyle|{\Lambda}_{t}-{\Lambda}_{s}|\leq Ke^{KT}(t-s),\qquad|{\Gamma}_{t}-{\Gamma}_{s}|\leq Ke^{KT}(t-s),\qquad(s,t)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2}([0,T]).

The following result shows that
({\Lambda},{\Gamma})
is the limiting process of
({\Lambda}^{n},{\Gamma}^{n})
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
{\Lambda}
,
{\Lambda}^{(n)}
,
{\Gamma}
and
{\Gamma}^{(n)}
be defined in (
4.8
), (
4.9
), (
4.44
), and (
4.45
), respectively. Then for
p\geq 1
there is a constant
K>0
such that the following estimate holds:

(4.46)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big(|{\Lambda}_{t}-{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}|_{L_{p}}+|{\Gamma}_{t}-{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{t}|_{L_{p}}\big)\leq K(1/n)^{H+1/2}.

Proof.

Take the difference between (
4.44
) and (
4.8
). We obtain:

(4.47)

\displaystyle{\Lambda}_{t}-{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\partial b(y_{s})({\Lambda}_{s}-{\Lambda}_{s}^{(n)})ds+\int_{0}^{t}(\partial b(y_{s})-b_{1}(s)){\Lambda}_{s}^{(n)}ds.

Applying
L_{p}
-norm in both sides of (
4.47
) gives:

(4.48)

\displaystyle|{\Lambda}_{t}-{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}|{\Lambda}_{s}-{\Lambda}_{s}^{(n)}|_{L_{p}}ds+\int_{0}^{t}|\partial b(y_{s})-b_{1}(s)|_{L_{2p}}ds.

Note that by applying the mean value theorem and then the estimate (
4.4
) we have:

(4.49)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}|\partial b(y_{s})-b_{1}(s)|_{L_{2p}}ds\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}|y_{s}-y_{s}^{(n)}|_{L_{2p}}ds\lesssim(1/n)^{H+1/2}.

Substituting (
4.49
) into (
4.48
) we obtain:

(4.50)

\displaystyle|{\Lambda}_{t}-{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}|{\Lambda}_{s}-{\Lambda}_{s}^{(n)}|_{L_{p}}ds+(1/n)^{H+1/2}.

It then follows from Gronwall’s inequality that relation (
4.46
) holds for
|{\Lambda}_{t}-{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}|_{L_{p}}
.
The estimate for
|{\Gamma}_{t}-{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{t}|
can be shown in the similar way and is omitted for conciseness.
∎

In the following we establish the asymptotic error distribution of the Euler method.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let the assumptions be as in Theorem
4.1
. Denote
U^{(n)}_{t}=n^{H+1/2}(y_{t}^{(n)}-y_{t})
,
t\in[0,T]
. Then we have the following convergence in distribution on
C([0,T])
:

(4.51)

\displaystyle(U^{(n)}_{t},x_{t},t\in[0,T])\to(U_{t},x_{t},t\in[0,T]),

where
U
is the solution of the linear equation:

(4.52)

\displaystyle U_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\partial b(y_{u})U_{u}du+c_{H}^{1/2}T^{H+1/2}\int_{0}^{t}\big[\partial b(y_{u})\sigma(u)-\sigma^{\prime}(u)\big]dW_{u}

and
W
is a Brownian motion independent of
x
and
c_{H}
is the constant defined in (
3.2
).

Proof.

Recall that we have shown the tightness of
U^{(n)}
in Theorem
4.1
(ii). In order to prove (
4.51
) it remains to show the convergence of the f.d.d. of
(U^{(n)},x)
. According to Corollary
4.3
this can be reduced to proving the convergence:

(4.53)

\displaystyle\qquad\big(n^{H+1/2}{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}\big({\mathcal{E}}^{z,n}_{\ell}(0,t)+E_{211}(0,t)\big),\,\,t\in[0,T]\big)\xrightarrow{stable\,\,f.d.d.}U\qquad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

Define the processes:
\bar{z}_{t}^{(i-1)}:={\Gamma}_{t}\partial^{i}b(y_{t})\sigma(t)^{\otimes i}
,

i=1,\dots,\ell
.
As in (
4.31
) it is easy to see that
(\bar{z},\bar{z}^{\prime},\dots,\bar{z}^{(\ell-1)})
is controlled by
(x,\ell,H)
in
L_{p}
for
p\geq 1
.
As a first step for the proof of (
4.53
), let us show that

(4.54)

\displaystyle n^{H+1/2}\big({\Lambda}_{t}{\mathcal{E}}^{\bar{z},n}_{\ell}(0,t)-{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}{\mathcal{E}}^{{z},n}_{\ell}(0,t)\big)\to 0\quad\text{in probability as $n\to\infty$.}

Note that by the definition of
{\mathcal{E}}^{\bar{z},n}_{\ell}(0,t)
and
{\mathcal{E}}^{{z},n}_{\ell}(0,t)
we have

\displaystyle{\Lambda}_{t}{\mathcal{E}}^{\bar{z},n}_{\ell}(0,t)-{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}{\mathcal{E}}^{{z},n}_{\ell}(0,t)

(4.55)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\int_{0}^{t}\big({\Lambda}_{t}{\Gamma}_{\eta(u)}\partial^{i}b(y_{\eta(u)})-{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{\eta(u)}\partial^{i}b(y^{(n)}_{\eta(u)})\big)\sigma^{\otimes i}x^{i}_{\eta(u)u}du.

Applying the mean value theorem and the estimates (
4.4
) and (
4.46
) we have:

\displaystyle\big|{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{\eta(u)}\partial^{i}b(y^{(n)}_{\eta(u)})-{\Lambda}_{t}{\Gamma}_{\eta(u)}\partial^{i}b(y_{\eta(u)})\big|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(1/n)^{H+1/2}.

Applying this to (
4.55
) gives:

\displaystyle|{\Lambda}_{t}{\mathcal{E}}^{\bar{z},n}_{\ell}(0,t)-{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}{\mathcal{E}}^{{z},n}_{\ell}(0,t)|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}(1/n)^{H+1/2}(1/n)^{iH}.

It follows that relation (
4.54
) holds.

Similar to (
4.54
), define

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{211}(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{s\leq t_{k}<t}{\Gamma}_{t_{k}}\sigma^{\prime}(t_{k})h^{1}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}.

Then we can show that

(4.56)

\displaystyle n^{H+1/2}\big|{\Lambda}^{(n)}_{t}E_{211}(0,t)-{\Lambda}_{t}\bar{E}_{211}(0,t)\big|\to 0\qquad\text{ in probability as $n\to\infty$.}

Now, according to Theorem
3.5
we have the f.d.d. stable convergence:

(4.57)

\displaystyle\big(n^{H+1/2}{\Lambda}_{t}({\mathcal{E}}^{\bar{z},n}_{\ell}(0,t)+\bar{E}_{211}(0,t)),\,\,x_{t},\,\,t\in[0,T]\big)\to(U_{t},\,\,x_{t},\,\,t\in[0,T])

as
n\to\infty
, where

(4.58)

\displaystyle U_{t}=c_{H}^{1/2}T^{H+1/2}{\Lambda}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}{\Gamma}_{u}\big[\partial b(y_{u})\sigma(u)-\sigma^{\prime}(u)\big]dW_{u}\,.

It is easy to verify that the process
U
defined in (
4.58
) satisfies equation (
4.52
).
Putting together relations (
4.54
), (
4.56
) and (
4.57
) we obtain the convergence in (
4.53
).
This completes the proof.
∎
