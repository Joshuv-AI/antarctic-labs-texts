A Structural Investigation of the Approximability of Polynomial-Time Problems
Karl Bringmann, Alejandro Cassis, Nick Fischer, Marvin Künnemann

Abstract
An extensive research effort targets optimal (in)approximability results for various NP -hard optimization problems. Notably, the works of (Creignou’95) as well as (Khanna, Sudan, Trevisan, Williamson’00) establish a tight characterization of a large subclass of MaxSNP , namely Boolean MaxCSP s and further variants, in terms of their polynomial-time approximability. Can we obtain similarly encompassing characterizations for classes of polynomial-time optimization problems ? To this end, we initiate the systematic study of a recently introduced polynomial-time analogue of MaxSNP , which includes a large number of well-studied problems (including Nearest and Furthest Neighbor in the Hamming metric, Maximum Inner Product, optimization variants of k k -XOR and Maximum k k -Cover). Specifically, for each k k , MaxSP k \text{{MaxSP}}_{k} denotes the class of O ⁡ ( m k ) O(m^{k}) -time problems of the form max x 1 , … , x k ⁡ # ⁡ { y : ϕ ⁡ ( x 1 , … , x k , y ) } \max_{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}\#\{y:\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},y)\} where ϕ \phi is a quantifier-free first-order property and m m denotes the size of the relational structure. Assuming central hypotheses about clique detection in hypergraphs and exact Max - 3 3 - SAT , we show that for any MaxSP k \text{{MaxSP}}_{k} problem definable by a quantifier-free m m -edge graph formula ϕ \phi , the best possible approximation guarantee in faster-than-exhaustive-search time O ⁡ ( m k − δ ) O(m^{k-\delta}) falls into one of four categories: • optimizable to exactness in time O ⁡ ( m k − δ ) O(m^{k-\delta}) , • an (inefficient) approximation scheme, i.e., a ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation in time O ⁡ ( m k − f ⁡ ( ε ) ) O(m^{k-f(\varepsilon)}) , • a (fixed) constant-factor approximation in time O ⁡ ( m k − δ ) O(m^{k-\delta}) , or • an m ε m^{\varepsilon} -approximation in time O ⁡ ( m k − f ⁡ ( ε ) ) O(m^{k-f(\varepsilon)}) . We obtain an almost complete characterization of these regimes, for MaxSP k \text{{MaxSP}}_{k} as well as for an analogously defined minimization class MinSP k \text{{MinSP}}_{k} . As our main technical contribution, we show how to rule out the existence of approximation schemes for a large class of problems admitting constant-factor approximations, under a hypothesis for exact Sparse Max - 3 3 - SAT algorithms posed by (Alman, Vassilevska Williams’20). As general trends for the problems we consider, we observe: (1) Exact optimizability has a simple algebraic characterization, (2) only few maximization problems do not admit a constant-factor approximation; these do not even have a subpolynomial-factor approximation, and (3) constant-factor approximation of minimization problems is equivalent to deciding whether the optimum is equal to 0 0 .

Introduction

For many optimization problems, the best known exact algorithms essentially explore the complete search space, up to low-order improvements. While this holds true in particular for NP-hard optimization problems such as maximum satisfiability, also common polynomial-time optimization problems like Nearest Neighbor search are no exception. When facing such a problem, the perhaps most common approach is to relax the optimization goal and ask for
approximations
rather than optimal solutions. Can we obtain an approximate value significantly faster than exhaustive search, and if so, what is the best approximation guarantee we can achieve?

These strong advances from both sides shift the algorithmic frontier and the frontier of conditional hardness towards each other. In fact, we view this as one of the fundamental tasks and uses of fine-grained algorithm design & complexity.

\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
as Polynomial-Time Analogue of
MaxSNP

Investigating
NP
-hard optimization problems, Papadimitriou and Yannakakis
[
71
]
introduced the class
MaxSNP
, which motivates the definition of
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
as a natural polynomial-time analogue (see
[
30
]
and Section
C.1
for details). As a general class containing prominent, constant-factor approximable optimization problems,
MaxSNP
was introduced to give the first evidence that
Max
-
3
-

does not admit a PTAS, by proving that
Max
-
3
-
SAT
belongs to the hardest-to-approximate problems in

Ideally, one would like to understand the approximability landscape in
MaxSNP
fully and give tight approximability results for each such problem. Major advances towards this goal have been achieved by Creignou
[
41
]
and Khanna, Sudan, Trevisan, and Williamson
[
62
]
who gave a complete classification of a large subclass of

either is polynomial-time optimizable or it does not admit a PTAS unless

s, the situation is more diverse with several equivalence classes needed to describe the result
[
62
]
.

We initiate the study of the same type of questions in the polynomial-time regime. Our aim is to achieve a detailed understanding of
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
and
\text{{MinSP}}_{k}
akin to the classification theorems achieved for
MaxCSP
s and
MinCSP
s
[
41
,
62
]
.

On Plausibility of the Hypotheses

As tight unconditional lower bounds for polynomial-time problems are barely existent, we give
conditional
lower bounds, based on established assumptions in fine-grained complexity theory, such as SETH (see
[
77
]
for an excellent survey). The essentially only exception is the only recently introduced
Sparse

Max
-
3
-
SAT
hypothesis
[
12
]

While it is possible that this hypothesis could ultimately be refuted, our classification describes the frontier of the current state of the art. At the very least, our conditional lower bound for approximation schemes reveals a rather surprising connection: To obtain an
approximation scheme
for
polynomial-time problems
such as Maximum
4

On the Use of Fast Matrix Multiplication

From our hardness parameters, it is easy to read off which of our algorithms require the use of fast matrix multiplication. In fact, this connection also works in the other direction: Since fast matrix multiplication techniques are at times deemed too sophisticated to be used in practice, we might want to disallow the use of these techniques. If we do this, it is plausible to assume that also Sparse

At the very least, this connection suggests that to close the remaining gap of our classification, we need to use fast matrix multiplication.

3.3.2
Minimization

There is an easier criterion for the hardness of approximating minimization problems. Namely, observe that giving a multiplicative approximation to a minimization problem
\text{{Min}}(\psi)
is at least as hard as testing if the optimal value is zero (recall that we refer to this problem as
\text{{Zero}}(\psi)
). More precisely, suppose that we are given an instance with
\mathrm{OPT}=0
. Then any multiplicative approximation must return an optimal solution.

It turns out that this is the only source of hardness for
\text{{Min}}(\psi)
problems. We show a fine-grained equivalence of deciding
\text{{Zero}}(\psi)
and approximating
\text{{Min}}(\psi)
within a constant factor:

Theorem 14

(Constant Approximation is Equivalent to Testing Zero)
.

Let
\psi
be a
\text{{MinSP}}_{k}
graph formula. Via a randomized reduction, there exists a constant-factor approximation algorithm for
\text{{Min}}(\psi)
in time
O(m^{k-\delta})
for some
\delta>0
if and only if
\text{{Zero}}(\psi)
can be solved in time
O(m^{k-\delta^{\prime}})
for some
\delta^{\prime}>0
.

To reduce approximating
\text{{Min}}(\psi)
to
\text{{Zero}}(\psi)
, we make use of
locality-sensitive hashing
(LSH); see
Appendix G
for details. This technique was for instance used to solve the Approximate Nearest Neighbors problem in Hamming spaces
[
53
]
, and was recently adapted by Chen and Williams to show that Minimum Inner Product can be reduced to
OV

[
38
]
. The latter result constitutes a singular known case for the general trend in
\text{{MinSP}}_{k}
revealed by

. In comparison, our reduction is simpler and more general, but gives weaker guarantees on the constant factor.

We specifically use LSH for a reduction from approximating
\text{{Min}}(\psi)
to the intermediate problem of

solutions to
\text{{Zero}}(\psi)
(
56

leads to an approximation scheme (

, we show that any
\text{{Zero}}(\psi)
problem with an

-time decider, for some

(

Since the hardness of

is completely classified

4
Discussion and Open Problems

Our investigation reveals all possible approximability types (in better-than-exhaustive-search time) for general classes of polynomial-time optimization problems, namely graph formulas in
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
and
\text{{MinSP}}_{k}
. Our results, which give an almost complete characterization, open up the following questions:

•

Can we extend our classification beyond graph formulas, i.e., when we allow more binary relations, or even higher-arity relations? Such settings include, e.g., generalizations of Max
k
-XOR from
\mathbb{F}_{2}
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, or variants of the densest subgraph problem on hypergraphs.

•

In our setting, each variable
x_{i}
ranges over a separate set
X_{i}
, also known as a
multichromatic
setting. We leave it open to transfer our results to the
monochromatic
setting (see e.g.
[
61
]
).

•

While we consider running times expressed in the input size (as usual in database contexts), it would also be natural to consider parameterization in the number
n
of objects in the relational structure, see
[
80
]
.

Besides these extensions, we ask whether one can close the remaining gap in our classification: Do formulas
\psi
with
H_{\mathrm{and}}(\psi)=2
and
H_{\mathrm{deg}}(\psi)\geq 3
admit an approximation scheme? As a specific challenge, we give the following open problem:

Open Problem 1
.

Is there an approximation scheme for Maximum Exact-
3
-Cover (or its minimization variant)? Specifically, can we prove or rule out that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is some
\delta>0
such that we can
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate Maximum Exact-3-Cover in time
O(m^{3-\delta})
?

It appears likely that showing existence of an approximation scheme for Maximum Exact-3-Cover would lead to a full characterization of
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
.

Finally, while we focused on the qualitative question whether or not exhaustive search can be beaten, a follow-up question is to determine precise
approximability-time tradeoffs
.
In this vein, consider the well-studied Maximum
k
-Cover problem: A simple linear-time greedy approach is known to establish a
(1-1/e)^{-1}
-approximation
[
55
]
. Subsequent lower bounds show that this is conditionally best possible in polynomial-time
[
44
]
and even
f(k)m^{o(k)}
-time (under
Gap-ETH
)
[
40
,
69
]
. On the other hand, for every fixed
k
, we show (1) existence of an approximation scheme, but (2) rule out an efficient one assuming SETH, i.e., for every
\delta>0
, there is some
\varepsilon>0
such that an
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation requires time
\Omega(m^{k-\delta})
.

Open Problem 2
.

Let
k\geq 2
. Can we determine, for every
1\leq\gamma\leq(1-1/e)^{-1}
, the optimal exponent
\alpha
of the fastest
\gamma
-approximation for Maximum
k
-Cover running in time
O(m^{\alpha\pm o(1)})
, assuming plausible fine-grained hardness assumptions?

Note that the extreme cases for
\gamma=1
and
\gamma=(1-1/e)^{-1}
are already settled and that
[
69
]
shows that for all immediate cases,
\alpha
must have a linear dependence on
k
, assuming
Gap-ETH
.

Appendix B
Hardness Assumptions

As usual in fine-grained complexity, our lower bounds are conditioned on certain hardness assumptions. In this section we introduce the relevant hypotheses and briefly argue for their plausibility. In our work we mostly depend on two popular hypotheses on polynomial-time problems, both of which are backed by hardness assumptions on fundamental, well-studied exponential-time problems. Whenever possible, we design our reductions to start from the (weaker) polynomial-time assumptions. To state all of our results, we rely on a relatively new assumption on sparse maximum satisfiability.

B.1
Orthogonal Vectors and SETH

One of the most prominent hypotheses in the area is the (

Appendix C
Comparison to Related Work

In this section, we give a more detailed comparison of similarities and differences between some related works in inapproximability and fine-grained complexity theory.

In particular, the close conceptual connection between
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
and
MaxSNP
is described in Section

. A comparison to the parameterized inapproximability result of Karthik, Laekhanukit, and Manurangsi
[

is given in Section

C.1
Correspondence of
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
and
MaxSNP

Let us review the definition of
MaxSNP
and compare it to
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
(see
[
30
]
for more details). The class

is motivated by Fagin’s theorem, and is defined as problems of the form
\exists S\,\forall\bar{y}\,\phi(\bar{y},G,S)
, where
G
is a given relational structure,
\exists S
ranges over a relational structure
S
and
\forall\bar{y}\,\phi(\bar{y},G,S)
is a
\forall^{*}
-quantified first-order property. Its natural optimization variant is
MaxSNP
, defined as the set of problems expressible as
\max_{S}\#\{\bar{y}:\phi(\bar{y},G,S)\}
.

As an analogue for
O(m^{k})
-time solvable problems,
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
is a variation of
MaxSNP
where the maximization over a relational structure
S
is replaced by maxmization over
k
variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
, and the counting is performed over a variable
y
.
14
14

14

An alternative would be to allow counting over
\ell
variables
y_{1},\dots,y_{\ell}
. This more general definition is pursued in
[
30
]
, but proven to be reducible to the case of
\ell=1
, as Maximum
k
-Inner Product (a problem for
\ell=1
) is shown to be complete for this more general class.
Let us remark that
[
30
]
choose the name “
\mathrm{SP}_{k}
” for “strict polynomial time

” in analogy to the name “strict NP” of
SNP
.

C.2
Comparison to Karthik, Laekhanukit and Manurangsi

Let us detail some interesting aspects of the work of Karthik, Laekhanukit and Manurangsi
[
60
]
. To obtain inapproximability results for Dominating Set, Maximum
k
-Inner Product and other problems, the authors use a framework of reductions from so-called Product Space Problems (PSPs) via MaxCover as an intermediate problem. It is insightful to highlight how these notions relate to our problem settings:

Connection to Product Space Problems (PSPs)

A Product Space Problem (PSP) is a very abstract problem formulation: Given a function
f:X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{k}\to\{0,1\}
, the corresponding product space problem
\mathrm{PSP}(f)
is to determine, given an input
A_{1}\subseteq X_{1},\dots,A_{k}\subseteq X_{k}
, whether there exists
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{k}
such that
f(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=1
. Karthik et al. use this notion to give a general and elegant reduction framework: Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of functions such that (1) each
f\in\mathcal{F}
has an “efficient” communication protocol (for the precise communication setting, we refer the reader to
[
60
]
) and (2) computing
\mathrm{PSP}(f)
for
f\in\mathcal{F}
is hard to solve under some fine-grained assumption
\mathcal{A}
. Then the framework immediately derives approximation hardness for MaxCover based on the assumption
\mathcal{A}
. In particular, this framework is used in
[
60
]
to obtain – via reduction from MaxCover – approximation hardness for Dominating Set and Maximum
k
-Inner Product under a diverse set of assumptions (such as SETH,
\mathrm{W}[1]\neq\mathrm{FPT}
and the
k
-SUM hypothesis).

Note that while PSPs defines a general family of problems, this notion would not be a suitable class to study in the context of our work: In a sense, it definition is too abstract/general for our purposes (one could not even enumerate all problems in this class, so characterizing all such problems appears hopeless). Our chosen classes,
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
and
\text{{MinSP}}_{k}
, provide problem classes defined by a simple
syntactic
definition – they are general enough to contain a diverse set of problems with very different approximability, while on the other hand, it is always clear which problems are members of this class (notably, for every
k
, we could enumerate the problems in
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
and
\text{{MinSP}}_{k}
). This opens up the possibility of a systematic classification, as performed in our work.

Is MaxCover in
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
?

Karthik et al. prove their inapproximability results
[
60
, Theorem 5.2]
already for a restricted setting of MaxCover that is closely related to our setting of
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
. Specifically, one can view this variant (also called
MaxCover with projection property
) as the following problem over vectors: Given sets
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}\subseteq\Sigma^{d}
, maximize the number of coordinates
y
such that
x_{1}[y]=x_{2}[y]=\cdots=x_{k}[y]
over all
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{k}
. For
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
, this is precisely a
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}

graph
formula studied in this paper as Maximum
k
-Agreement. For any constant
|\Sigma|
, we can express the corresponding Maximum
k
-Agreement problem still as a
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
formula, using additional binary relations to represent the larger alphabet (this is no longer a
graph
formula, see also the discussion in Section
4
). Importantly, Karthik et al.’s main inapproximability result for MaxCover
[
60
, Theorem 5.2]
assuming hardness of
\mathrm{PSP}(\mathcal{F})
produces instances for which
|\Sigma|
depends both on
2^{k}
and the efficiency of the communication protocol for
\mathcal{F}
. Thus, this result cannot be directly used for the
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k}
graph formulas studied in the present paper, but provides an important result for larger alphabet sizes. After a subsequent reduction to Maximum
k
-Inner Product
[
60
, Theorem B.3]
(see Section
J
), their result provides an important part of the basis for our hardness of approximation results given in Section
I
.

Appendix D
Vector Optimization Problems

As mentioned before, the predicates
E(x_{i},x_{j})
have only a minor impact on the complexity of both the model-checking problem of
\psi

[
31
, Theorem 7]
and the optimization problems
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
. In particular, as we will show, it suffices to classify the subtype of problems featuring no predicates
E(x_{i},x_{j})
. For convenience in the following parts of this paper, we will now make this notion precise.

Definition 28

(Vector Optimization Problems)
.

Let
\phi
be a Boolean function on
k
inputs, and let
\psi=\opt_{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}\counting_{y}\phi(E(x_{1},y),\ldots,E(x_{k},y))
. We refer to
\text{{Max}}(\psi)
and
\text{{Min}}(\psi)
as
vector (optimization) problems

\text{{VMax}}(\phi)
and
\text{{VMin}}(\phi)
. Occasionally, we write
\text{{VOpt}}(\psi)
to refer to both problems simultaneously.

In the context of vector problems, we often view the objects
x_{i}
as vectors and view
Y
as the set of coordinates. In addition, we define the
dimension

d:=|Y|
and, following standard notation, write
x_{i}[y]:=E(x_{i},y)
. Note that a vector problem instance is sparsely represented by one list of all nonzero entries in all vectors.

Recall that
\psi_{0}
(as defined in the beginning of
section 3
) is a Boolean function obtained from
\psi
by substituting each atom
E(x_{i},x_{j})
by
\mathit{false}
. Similar to
[
31
, Theorem 7]
, we obtain the following equivalence of general optimization problems
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
and vector optimization problems
\text{{VOpt}}(\psi_{0})
. Note that this equivalence holds in the strongest approximation-preserving way, and a similar statement holds for additive approximations. The proof of
Theorem 29
is postponed to the end of this section.

Theorem 29

(Equivalence of Vector Optimization Problems)
.

Let
\psi
be an
\text{{OptSP}}_{k}
graph formula, and let
c=c(m)\geq 1
.

•

If a
c
-approximation for
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
can be computed in time
T(m)
, then a
c
-approximation for
\text{{VOpt}}(\psi_{0})
can be computed in time
O(T(m))
.

•

If a
c
-approximation for
\text{{VOpt}}(\psi_{0})
can be computed in time
O(m^{k-\varepsilon})
for some
\varepsilon>0
, then a
c
-approximation for
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
can be computed in time
O(m^{k-\varepsilon^{\prime}})
for some
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
.

In the upcoming algorithms, to solve
\text{{VOpt}}(\phi)
it is often necessary to “brute-force” over a number of variables
x_{i}
to reduce
k
to some smaller
k^{\prime}
. However, in general this cannot be considered a self-reduction, as the resulting
k^{\prime}
-problem is not necessarily the same vector optimization problem
\text{{VOpt}}(\phi)
. As an example, consider the
3
-
XOR
problem,
\text{{VOpt}}(z_{1}\oplus z_{2}\oplus z_{3})
. After brute-forcing over all variables
x_{1}
, we are left with a combination of
\text{{VOpt}}(0\oplus z_{2}\oplus z_{3})
and
\text{{VOpt}}(1\oplus z_{2}\oplus z_{3})
, where in each coordinate one of these two constraints applies. As we regularly have to deal with these combined problems, we now define them formally.

Definition 30

(Hybrid Optimization Problems)
.

Let
\Phi
be a set of Boolean functions on
k
inputs. In a
hybrid optimization problem
\text{{VMax}}(\Phi)
, we are given an
(k+1)
-partite graph
X_{1}\mathbin{\cup}\dots\mathbin{\cup}X_{k}\mathbin{\cup}Y
, where each coordinate
y\in Y
is associated to a function
\phi_{y}\in\Phi
, and the task is to compute

\mathrm{OPT}=\max_{x_{1}\in X_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in X_{k}}\counting_{y\in Y}\phi_{y}(x_{1}[y],\dots,x_{k}[y]).

We similarly define
\text{{VMin}}(\Phi)
.

Our hardness parameters
H_{\mathrm{and}}
and
H_{\mathrm{deg}}
interplay nicely with hybrid problems: Let
\phi
be a
k
-
variable Boolean function of hardness, say
H_{\mathrm{and}}(\phi)\leq h
, and, for some
h\leq k^{\prime}\leq k
, let
\Phi
be the set of all
k^{\prime}
-variable Boolean functions
\phi^{\prime}
of hardness
H_{\mathrm{and}}(\phi^{\prime})\leq h
. Then
\text{{VOpt}}(\phi)
reduces to
n^{k-k^{\prime}}
instances of
\text{{VOpt}}(\Phi)
. Indeed, by the definition of
H_{\mathrm{and}}
, we can always find a set of
k-k^{\prime}
indices which we can brute-force over, such that any remaining formula is of hardness at most
h
. The same statement holds for
H_{\mathrm{deg}}
as well.

We finally give the missing proof of
Theorem 29
. The idea is a rather straightforward generalization of
[
31
, Theorem 7]
. We essentially exploit that there are not too many tuples
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
for which at least one of the edges
(x_{i},x_{j})
is present. As an important ingredient, we recycle a lemma already proven in
[
31
]
.

Lemma 31

(
[
31
, Lemma 22]
)
.

Given a nonempty set of index pairs
\emptyset\neq I\subseteq\binom{[k]}{2}
, we say that
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

respects

I
if, for any
i\neq j
, we have
E(x_{i},x_{j})
if and only if
\{i,j\}\in I
. We can compute
\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\rangle
for all vectors
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
respecting
I
in time
O(m^{k-1/2})
.

Proof of
Theorem 29
.

The first part is clear: The vector optimization problem
\text{{VOpt}}(\psi_{0})
is the special case of
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
, where we leave out all edges
E(x_{i},x_{j})
.

So let us focus on the second part and start with
\text{{Max}}(\psi)
. Let

\psi=\max_{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}\counting_{y}\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y).

For any set
I\subseteq\binom{[k]}{2}
of index pairs, let
\phi_{I}
denote the formula obtained from
\phi
after substituting every atom
E(x_{i},x_{j})
by
\mathit{true}
if
\{i,j\}\in I
, and by
\mathit{false}
otherwise. In particular,
\phi_{\emptyset}=\psi_{0}
. Furthermore, we define

\psi_{I}=\max_{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}\counting_{y}\left[\left(\bigwedge_{\{i,j\}\subseteq[k]}E(x_{i},x_{j})\iff\{i,j\}\in I\right)\land\phi_{I}(x_{1}[y],\dots,x_{k}[y])\right].

It suffices to separately solve the given
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
instance for all
I
, treated as an instance of
\text{{Opt}}(\psi_{I})
, respectively. Then
\mathrm{OPT}=\max_{I}\mathrm{OPT}_{I}
, where
\mathrm{OPT}_{I}
is the optimal value of the
\text{{Opt}}(\psi_{I})
instance. We distinguish two cases:

Case 1:
I\neq\emptyset
.

We claim that an exact solution for
\text{{Opt}}(\psi_{I})
can be computed in time
O(m^{k-1/2})
. Clearly, we only need to focus on tuples
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
that respect
I
, as any other tuple is of value
0
. There are at most
O(m^{k-1})
such tuples. Indeed, since we assumed that
I
is non-empty, there exists a pair of indices
\{i,j\}\in I
such that there exists an edge
E(x_{i},x_{j})
whenever
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
respects
I
. However, there are at most
m
pairs
(x_{i},x_{j})
satisfying that condition, and therefore at most
n^{k-2}m\leq O(m^{k-1})
relevant tuples
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
.

We can represent the value of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
in terms of the polynomial representation of
\phi_{I}
; in the language of
Appendix E
:

\mathrm{Val}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}<\ldots<i_{\ell}\\
0\leq\ell\leq k\end{subarray}}\widehat{\phi}_{I}(\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\})\cdot\langle x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{\ell}}\rangle.

Assuming that all inner products
\langle x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{\ell}}\rangle
are precomputed, we can determine the optimum value in time
O(m^{k-1})
by iterating over all tuples respecting
I
and applying the previous equation in each step. For
\ell<k
, we can easily compute all inner products
\langle x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{\ell}}\rangle
in time
O(m^{\ell})\leq O(m^{k-1})
. For
\ell=k
, we apply
31
to compute all inner products
\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\rangle
in time
O(m^{k-1/2})
.

Case 2:
I=\emptyset
.

Note that
\text{{Max}}(\psi_{\emptyset})
is not directly equivalent to
\text{{VMax}}(\psi_{0})
, since an arbitrary solution
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
for the latter does not necessarily meet the condition that none of the edges
(x_{i},x_{j})
be present. The following reduction enforces this constraint.

Computing a
c
-approximation of
\text{{Max}}(\psi)
can be reduced to the following gap problem: Given a target
t
, distinguish whether
\mathrm{OPT}\geq t
, or whether
\mathrm{OPT}<t/c
. Binary-searching over
t
then yields a
c
-approximation of the original problem, incurring only a logarithmic overhead. Despite its name, the definition of the gap problem also makes sense for the case
c=1
.

Let
\gamma>0
be a parameter to be fixed later. We call a vertex
x_{i}

heavy
if it is of degree
\geq m^{\gamma}
, and
light
otherwise. The first step is to eliminate all heavy vertices; there can exist at most
O(m/m^{\gamma})=O(m^{1-\gamma})
many such vertices
x_{i}
. Fixing
x_{i}
, we can solve the remaining problem in time
O(m^{k-1})
using the baseline algorithm. If a solution of value
\geq t/c
is detected, we accept. It thus takes time
O(m^{k-\gamma})
to safely remove all heavy vertices.

Next, partition each set
X_{i}
into several groups
X_{i,1},\ldots,X_{i,g}
such that the total degree of all vertices in a group is
\Theta(m^{\gamma})
, except for possibly the last nonempty groups. This is implemented by greedily inserting vertices into
X_{i.j}
until its total degree exceeds
m^{\gamma}
. As each vector inserted in that way is light, we can overshoot by at most
m^{\gamma}
. It follows that
g\leq O(m/m^{\gamma})=O(m^{1-\gamma})
.

We assume that
\text{{VMax}}(\phi_{\emptyset})
can be
c
-approximated in time
O(m^{k-\delta})
for some
\delta>0
. Then we continue as follows:

1.

For all combinations
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})\in[g]^{k}
, compute a
c
-approximation of the vector optimization problem
\text{{VMax}}(\psi_{0})
with input
X_{1,j_{1}},\ldots,X_{k,j_{k}}
. If the returned value is
\geq t/c
, then we call
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})
a
successful
combination.

2.

If there are more than
mn^{k-2}
successful combinations, we accept.

3.

Otherwise, for any successful combination
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})
, solve
\text{{Max}}(\psi)
on
X_{1,j_{1}},\ldots,X_{k,j_{k}}
exactly using the baseline algorithm, and accept if and only if one of these calls returns a value
\geq t/c
.

We begin with the correctness of the algorithm. First of all, whenever a solution
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
in
\text{{Max}}(\psi_{\emptyset})
is of value at least
t
, then the respective value in
\text{{VMax}}(\phi_{\emptyset})
is also at least
t
. It is therefore safe to only treat those subinstances in step 3 which we considered successful in step 1. It remains to argue why step 2 is correct. How many tuples
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
can be false positives, that is, be of value
\geq t/c
in
\text{{VMax}}(\phi_{\emptyset})
, but of value
<t/c
in
\text{{Max}}(\psi_{\emptyset})
? At most
mn^{k-2}
, since at least one edge
(x_{i},x_{j})
must exist for any false positive. Thus, if we witness
>mn^{k-2}
solutions of value
\geq t/c
in
\text{{VMax}}(\phi_{\emptyset})
, among these there exists at least one true positive.

Finally, let us bound the running time of the above algorithm. Recall that removing heavy vertices accounts for
O(m^{k-\gamma})
time. In step 1, the

algorithm is applied

times on instances of size

, which takes time

. Step 3 becomes relevant only if there are at most

successful combinations. For any such combination, the baseline algorithm takes time
O((m^{\gamma})^{k})=O(m^{\gamma k})
. The total running time is
O(m^{k-\gamma}+m^{k-\gamma\delta}+m^{k-1+\gamma k})
. By picking

, the claim follows.

This finishes the proof for the maximization variant. For minimizations, the proof is essentially analogous. We define

\psi_{I}=\min_{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}\counting_{y}\left[\left(\bigvee_{\{i,j\}\subseteq[k]}E(x_{i},x_{j})\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.98611pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.40282pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}\{i,j\}\in I\right)\lor\phi_{I}(x_{1}[y],\dots,x_{k}[y])\right]

∎

By carefully inspecting the proof of

and

are equivalent under additive approximations.

Let

For any Boolean function

E.1
Hardness Results

As the main tool for the hardness proof, we demonstrate how to construct
coordinate gadgets
for all problems classified as hard in
Theorem 32
. Our gadget definition is inspired by the standard reduction from
OV
to
MaxIP
.

Recall that a
restriction
of a Boolean function
\phi
is another Boolean function
\phi^{\prime}
obtained by substituting constant values for some inputs of
\phi
. In the following, we need a strictly more general concept:

Definition 37

(Unary Transformation)
.

Let
\phi(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
be a Boolean function and let
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{k}:\{0,1\}\to\{0,1\}
be unary functions. We call the composition
\phi(\tau_{1}(z_{1}),\ldots,\tau_{k}(z_{k}))
a
unary transformation
of
\phi
.

Whenever the unary functions
\tau_{i}
are either constant or the identity, a unary transformation of
\phi
is simply a restriction of
\phi
. However, unary transformations also allow to flip some of the inputs.

Lemma 38

(Coordinate Gadget)
.

Let
\phi(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
be a Boolean function of degree
\deg(\phi)=k
. Then there exist unary transformations
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{\ell}
of
\phi
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\phi_{j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}\cdot z^{[k]},

for some positive integers
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
. The collection
\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{\ell}\}
is called a
coordinate gadget
of
\phi
.

Proof.

We describe an inductive procedure maintaining a polynomial
g
which is invariantly a sum of unary transformations of
\phi
. Initially, we set
g:=\phi
and the goal is to eventually transform
g
into
\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}\cdot z^{[k]}
. We can assume that
\widehat{g}([k])<0
; otherwise start with
g:=\phi(1-z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{k})
. We proceed by eliminating unnecessary monomials one at a time:

If
g
does not have any monomial of degree
1\leq d\leq k-1
, then we stop and output
g
. Otherwise, pick one such monomial
\widehat{g}(S)\cdot z^{S}
of maximum degree. Moreover, let
T\subseteq[k]
be an inclusion-wise minimal set such that
S\subseteq T
and
\widehat{\phi}(T)\neq 0
(note that
T
exists since
\deg(\phi)=k
). Then, we construct the unary transformation
\phi^{\prime}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}):=\phi(\tau_{1}(z_{1}),\ldots,\tau_{k}(z_{k}))
with

\tau_{i}(z):=\begin{cases}z&\text{if $i\in S$,}\\
1&\text{if $i\in T\setminus S$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We have that
\widehat{\phi}^{\prime}(S)=\widehat{\phi}(T)\neq 0
. By additionally flipping an arbitrary input
z_{i}
,
i\in S
, we construct
\phi^{\prime\prime}
with
\widehat{\phi}^{\prime\prime}(S)=-\widehat{\phi}(T)
. Moreover, observe that
z^{S}
is the only nonzero monomial of degree
\geq|S|
in
\phi^{\prime\prime}
.

Next, we cancel the monomial
z^{S}
as follows: Assume without loss of generality that
\widehat{\phi}(T)>0
(and thus
\widehat{\phi}^{\prime\prime}(S)<0
) and also assume
\widehat{g}(S)>0
; the other cases are symmetric. We set

g^{\prime}:=\widehat{\phi}(T)\cdot g+\widehat{g}(S)\cdot\phi^{\prime\prime}.

Then
\widehat{g}^{\prime}(S)=\widehat{\phi}(T)\cdot\widehat{g}(S)+\widehat{g}(S)\cdot(-\widehat{\phi}(T))=0
, and any other monomial of degree
\geq|S|
remains untouched in
g^{\prime}
. Since the number of nonzero monomials of maximum degree (less than
k
) has decreased, the construction continues with
g^{\prime}
by induction. Here, we implicitly used that the coefficients
\widehat{\phi}(T)
and
\widehat{g}(S)
are integral, which can for example be seen from the inclusion-exclusion formula (
33
).

It remains to argue that
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
are positive integers. Recall that we started with
\widehat{g}([k])<0
and any function
\phi^{\prime\prime}
that was added in the process satisfies
\widehat{\phi}^{\prime\prime}([k])=0
. Hence,
\widehat{g}([k])<0
and thus
\beta_{2}>0
. Finally, since
g
is a sum of (polynomial extensions of) Boolean functions, it is clear that
0\leq g(1,\ldots,1)=\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}
. It follows that
\beta_{1}\geq\beta_{2}>0
.
∎

With the coordinate gadget in mind, the
k

(No Exact Optimization for

Proof.

We assume an

For the remaining part of

.

In both algorithms, we brute-force over some variables

and solve the remaining

Lemma 41

(Exact Optimization for
H_{\mathrm{deg}}(\phi)\leq 1
)
.

Let
\phi
be a Boolean function on
k

H_{\mathrm{deg}}(\phi)\leq 1<k
. Then
\text{{VOpt}}(\phi)
can be solved in time
O(m^{k-1})
.

Proof.

By
34

H_{\mathrm{deg}}(\phi)\leq 1
implies that there exist distinct variables

such that
\widehat{\phi}(S)=0
for all

in linear time. However, by our choice of

we know that
\deg(\phi_{y})\leq 1
for all

\max_{x_{1}\in X_{1}}\sum_{y\in Y}\widehat{\phi}_{y}(\{1\})\cdot x_{1}[y]+\max_{x_{2}\in X_{2}}\sum_{y\in Y}\widehat{\phi}_{y}(\{2\})\cdot x_{2}[y]+\sum_{y\in Y}\widehat{\phi}_{y}(\emptyset).

These summands can be computed independently by a single pass through the edge set and so the total running time is
O(n^{k-2}m)=O(m^{k-1})
. Note that the same approach works for minimization problems.
∎

We present the second algorithm as a reduction from

to the

Lemma 42

(Maximum-Weight

)
.

Assume that the

-
Uniform

Proof.

Let

(k-h)

\text{{VMax}}(z_{1}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\leftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\leftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.98611pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\leftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.40282pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\leftrightarrow}}}z_{2})

The following result about
Furthest Neighbor
occurs several times in the literature; in particular, it was proven by Alman, Chan and Williams
[
9
]
:

Theorem 52

(
[
9
, Theorem 1.5]
)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists some
\delta>0
such that we can compute an
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
Furthest Neighbor
in randomized time
\tilde{O}(nd+n^{2-\delta})
.

Their proof first uses a dimension reduction by locality-sensitive hashing in Hamming spaces. The improvement for the low-dimensional problem is then achieved by the polynomial method.

To state our reductions, recall the notion of a hybrid optimization problem as defined in
Appendix D
. As a first step, we show how to obtain an approximation scheme for the hybrid problem of
Furthest Neighbor
and its complement:

Lemma 53
.

Let
\Phi=\{z_{1}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\leftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\leftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.98611pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\leftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.40282pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\leftrightarrow}}}z_{2},z_{1}\leftrightarrow z_{2}\}
. There exists a randomized approximation scheme for
\text{{VMax}}(\Phi)
.

Proof.

We introduce some notation: Let
Y_{0}\subseteq Y
denote the set of coordinates
y
associated to
\phi_{y}(z_{1},z_{2})=(z_{1}\leftrightarrow z_{2})
and let
Y_{1}=Y\setminus Y_{0}
. Moreover, let
d_{0}=|Y_{0}|
and
d_{1}=|Y_{1}|
. For a vector
x
and a subset of dimensions
Y^{\prime}
, let
\|x\|_{Y^{\prime}}=|\{y\in Y^{\prime}:x[y]=1\}|
denote the Hamming weight of
x
restricted to
Y^{\prime}
.

Our goal is to get rid of all coordinates in
Y_{0}
, as then the given hybrid instance effectively becomes an instance of
Furthest Neighbor
. Following a naive approach, we would simply complement all entries
x_{1}[y]
,
y\in Y_{0}
. However, as
d_{0}
might be as large as
n
, we cannot afford to complement directly. Instead, we will first modify the instance to guarantee that
nd_{0}\leq O(m^{1.9})
. We have to further guarantee that
nd_{1}\leq O(m^{1.9})
to effectively apply the assumed
Furthest Neighbor
algorithm.

•

To prove that we can assume
nd_{1}\leq O(m^{1.9})
, suppose that otherwise
nd_{1}\geq m^{1.9}
. It follows that
m\leq(nd_{1})^{1/1.9}\leq n^{2/1.9}\leq n^{1.1}
. Let us call a vector
x_{i}

heavy
if
\|x_{i}\|_{Y_{1}}\geq n^{0.2}
, and
light
otherwise. Clearly, there can be at most
m/n^{0.2}\leq n^{1.1}/n^{0.2}=n^{0.9}
heavy vectors and thus it is feasible to explicitly enumerate all heavy vectors
x_{i}
and solve the remaining
\text{{MaxSP}}_{k-1}
problem exactly using the baseline algorithm in time
O(n^{0.9}m)=O(m^{1.9})
. In the remaining instance, there are only light vectors.

We now apply the following simple hashing scheme: Let
Y_{1}^{\prime}
be a set of size
|Y_{1}^{\prime}|=d_{1}^{\prime}:=n^{0.9}
and pick a random function
h:Y_{1}\to Y_{1}^{\prime}
. For every vector
x_{i}
, we construct a vector
x_{i}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{Y_{0}\mathbin{\cup}Y_{1}^{\prime}}
with
x_{i}^{\prime}[y^{\prime}]=\bigvee_{y\in h^{-1}(y^{\prime})}x_{i}[y]
for all
y^{\prime}\in Y_{1}^{\prime}
and
x_{i}^{\prime}[y]=x_{i}[y]
for all
y\in Y_{0}
. Notice that the value of any pair
(x_{1},x_{2})
can only decrease in the hashing step. Moreover, for any fixed pair
(x_{1},x_{2})
, with probability
1-2n^{2\cdot 0.2}/n^{0.9}=1-O(n^{-0.5})
, there occurs no collision in the hashing (i.e., no two
1
-entries are mapped to the same new coordinate) and thus the value of
(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})
is the same as the value for
(x_{1},x_{2})
. In particular, with probability
1-O(n^{-0.5})
, the optimal value does not change. In that way, we have effectively reduced
d_{1}=|Y_{1}|
to
n^{0.9}
, and thus
nd_{1}\leq n^{1.9}\leq O(m^{1.9})
.

•

Next, we prove that we can assume
nd_{0}\leq O(m^{1.9})
. So suppose that
nd_{0}\geq m^{1.9}
. Then
d_{0}\geq m^{1.9}/n\geq\Omega(n^{0.9})
. Moreover, there exist vectors
x_{1},x_{2}
with
\|x_{1}\|_{Y_{0}}\leq d_{0}^{0.9}
and
\|x_{2}\|_{Y_{0}}\leq d_{0}^{0.9}
(as otherwise
m>nd_{0}^{0.9}
and thus
m^{1.9}>nd_{0}
). But then the optimal value of this instance is very large: For most coordinates
y\in Y_{0}
,
x_{1}[y]=x_{2}[y]=0
, which is satisfying for
(z_{1}\leftrightarrow z_{2})
. Thus, the optimal value is at least
\mathrm{OPT}\geq d_{0}-d_{0}^{0.9}\geq(1-\Omega(n^{-0.09}))d_{0}\geq(1-\varepsilon)d_{0}
for any constant
\varepsilon>0
and sufficiently large
n
. Moreover, the contribution of all coordinates
y\in Y_{1}
is at most
2n^{0.2}
after eliminating all heavy vectors in the previous step. Therefore also
\mathrm{OPT}\leq d_{0}+2n^{0.2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)d_{0}
and we can safely return
x_{1},x_{2}
as an approximate optimal solution.

In combination, we can assume that
nd=n(d_{0}+d_{1})\leq O(m^{1.9})
. It is now feasible to complement all entries
x_{1}[y]
,
y\in Y_{0}
, in time
O(nd_{0})=O(m^{1.9})
. What remains is a pure
Furthest Neighbor
instance. Observe that we increased the sparsity by a lot, however, the assumed
Furthest Neighbor
is insensitive to the sparsity
m
. Running that algorithm takes time
O(nd+n^{2-\delta})=O(m^{1.9}+m^{2-\delta})=O(m^{2-\delta^{\prime}})
for
\delta^{\prime}:=\min\{\delta,0.1\}>0
.
∎

Lemma 54

Let
\phi
be a Boolean function on
k

•

If
H_{\mathrm{and}}(\phi)\leq 1
, then there exists a randomized approximation scheme for
\text{{VMin}}(\phi)
.

•

If
H_{\mathrm{and}}(\phi)\leq 2<k

\text{{VMin}}(\phi)

In contrast to the maximization case where we applied different techniques for each approximation regime, here all our results are a consequence of a general reduction from approximating
\text{{VMin}}(\phi)

\text{{VZero}}(\phi)

\text{{VZero}}(\phi)

Formally, we call an algorithm a

We start with the following technical lemma, which generalizes the baseline algorithm to the case where only a subset of all tuples
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
are considered candidate solutions.

Lemma 55
.

Let
\phi
be a Boolean function on
k
inputs, and let
\delta>0
. Given a
\text{{VMin}}(\phi)
instance, and a set of
O(n^{k-\delta})
candidates
C\subseteq X_{1}\times\dots\times X_{k}
, we can compute
\mathrm{Val}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
for all candidates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in C
in time
O(m^{k-\delta/3})
.

Proof.

The algorithm executes the following three steps:

•

We call a vector
x_{i}

heavy
if its Hamming weight exceeds
m^{\delta/3}
, and
light
otherwise. As the total Hamming weight of all vectors is at most
O(m)
, there can be at most
O(m^{1-\delta/3})
heavy vectors. Therefore, we can brute-force over all heavy vectors
x_{k}
and solve the remaining
\text{{MinSP}}_{k-1}
problem in time
O(m^{k-1})
using the baseline algorithm (which in fact computes all values
\mathrm{Val}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
in the same running time; see
[
30
, Theorem 22 of the full version]
). This step takes time
O(m^{k-\delta/3})
and afterwards, we can assume that all vectors
x_{k}
are light.

•

Next, call a tuple
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})
a
heavy prefix
if the number of vectors
x_{k}
such that
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is a candidate is at least
n^{1-2\delta/3}
. There can be at most
n^{k-1-\delta/3}
heavy prefixes, as otherwise
|C|>n^{k-\delta}
. Enumerating all such prefixes and solving the remaining
\text{{MinSP}}_{1}
problem by the baseline algorithm takes time
O(n^{k-1-\delta/3}m)=O(m^{k-\delta/3})
.

•

For any prefix
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1})
which is left, we can assume that there are at most
n^{1-2\delta/3}
completions
x_{k}
such that
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is a candidate. Moreover, recall that every such vector
x_{k}
is light, and therefore of Hamming weight at most
O(m^{\delta/3})
. Hence, we brute-force over all remaining prefixes and solve a
\text{{MinSP}}_{1}
problem of size
O(n^{1-2\delta/3}m^{\delta/3})
. Again, we use the baseline algorithm, so this step takes time
O(n^{k-1}n^{1-2\delta/3}m^{\delta/3})=O(m^{k-\delta/3})
as well. ∎

Lemma 56

(Approximation via Listing Zeros)
.

Let
\phi
be a variable Boolean function on
k\geq 2
inputs so that
L
solutions to
\text{{VZero}}(\phi)
can be listed in time
T(m,L)
. Then, for any
c>1
and
0<\gamma<k
, there is a randomized algorithm that with high probability computes a
c
-approximation of
\text{{VMin}}(\phi)
in time

\tilde{O}\!\left(m^{k-\frac{\gamma(1-1/c)}{3}}+\sum_{i=0}^{O(\log n)}\min\{2^{i},n^{\gamma/c}\}\cdot T\!\left(\!m,\frac{n^{k-\gamma(1-1/c)}}{2^{i}}\!\right)\!\right)\!.

Proof.

We will consider the following gap version of the
\text{{VMin}}(\phi)
problem: Along with the input, we are given an additional target
t
. The task is to compute an output satisfying the following requirements:

•

If
\mathrm{OPT}\leq t
, then return “
small
” and attach a solution of value at most
c\cdot t
.

•

If
\mathrm{OPT}>c\cdot t
, then return “
large
”.

•

Otherwise, we do not care about the output.

An algorithm for this gap problem translates into an approximation algorithm for
\text{{VMin}}(\phi)
by using binary search on
t
to determine
\mathrm{OPT}
up to a factor of
c
. For that reason, we focus on the gap problem for the remainder of this proof.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{|Y|}
be the given gap instance with target
t
, and let
R
and
S
be parameters to be specified later. Throughout the algorithm, we maintain a global counter
L
initialized to
L=8n^{k-\gamma(1-1/c)}+1
. The following steps are repeated
R
times:

1.

Sample
S
coordinates
y_{1},\ldots,y_{S}\in Y
uniformly at random. For a vector
x_{i}\in X_{i}
, we write
x_{i}^{\prime}
for the projected vector
(x_{i}[y_{1}],\ldots,x_{i}[y_{S}])\in\{0,1\}^{S}
and we set
X_{i}^{\prime}=\{x_{i}^{\prime}:x_{i}\in X_{i}\}
.

2.

List up to
L
solutions to the
\text{{VZero}}(\phi)
instance
(X_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{k}^{\prime})
. Afterwards, decrease
L
by the number of detected solutions.

3.

For every tuple
(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{k}^{\prime})
listed in the previous step, check whether
\mathrm{Val}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\leq c\cdot t
. In that case, report “
small
” and attach
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
.

If after all iterations no solution was detected, output “
large
”.

We start with the correctness argument. For a fixed round and a tuple
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
, we distinguish two cases:

•

If
\mathrm{Val}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\leq t
, then:
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\big[\mathrm{Val}(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{k}^{\prime})=0\big]\geq\big(1-\frac{t}{|Y|}\big)^{S}=:p_{1}^{S}
.

•

If
\mathrm{Val}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})>ct
, then:
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\big[\mathrm{Val}(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{k}^{\prime})=0\big]\leq\big(1-\frac{ct}{|Y|}\big)^{S}=:p_{2}^{S}
.

The strategy is to set
R
large enough such that any (fixed) solution
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
of value at most
t
is mapped to a tuple
(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{k}^{\prime})
of value zero in at least one round. It suffices to choose
R=p_{1}^{-S}
as then the probability of failing in all
R
rounds is bounded by
(1-p_{1}^{S})^{R}\leq 1/e
.

Next, we specify
S
in such a way that the number of false positives – that is, tuples
(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{k}^{\prime})
of value zero whereas
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
has large value – is bounded by
O(m^{k-1})
with good probability. Specifically, set

S:=\frac{\gamma\log n}{\log(1/p_{2})}=\frac{\gamma\log n}{\log(1-\frac{ct}{|Y|})^{-1}}\leq\frac{\gamma|Y|\log n}{ct}.

It follows that

R=p_{1}^{-S}=\left(1-\frac{t}{|Y|}\right)^{-S}\leq\left(1-\frac{t}{|Y|}\right)^{\textstyle-\frac{\gamma|Y|\log n}{ct}}\leq n^{\gamma/c}.

The expected number of false positives among all rounds is therefore bounded by
R\cdot n^{k}\cdot p_{2}^{S}\leq n^{\gamma/c}\cdot n^{k}\cdot n^{-\gamma}=n^{k-\gamma(1-1/c)}
. Using Markov’s inequality, the number of false positives is at most
8n^{k-\gamma(1-1/c)}
with probability at least
7/8
. Hence, it suffices to list
L=8n^{k-\gamma(1-1/c)}+1
many solutions to all
\text{{VZero}}(\phi)
instances together, in order find at least one true positive with probability
1-1/e-1/8>1/2
. By repeating the whole procedure
O(\log n)
times, we amplify the success probability to
1-n^{-\Omega(1)}
.

Finally, focus on the running time analysis. The first step runs in time
O(m)
per round. The second and third steps are more involved. To achieve the claimed time bound, we have to implement the second step slightly more efficiently: Instead of running the listing algorithm with the global capacity
L
directly, we use binary search to test for
2^{i}
solutions,
i=0,1,\ldots,\log L
. If in the
i
-th iteration there are strictly less than
2^{i}
solutions, we stop and continue as above. Among all
R
rounds, we execute that
i
-th step at most
L/2^{i}
times. Hence, the running time of step 2 is bounded by

O\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\log L}\min\left\{\frac{L}{2^{i}},R\right\}\cdot T(m,2^{i})\right)=O\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\log L}\min\{2^{i},R\}\cdot T\left(\!m,\frac{L}{2^{i}}\right)\right),

which is as stated, after plugging in the parameters from before. In the third step, across all rounds, we test at most
O(n^{k-\gamma(1-1/c)})
tuples
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
for their exact value
\mathrm{Val}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
. That task can be solved in time
O(m^{k-\gamma(1-1/c)/3})
using
55
.
∎

admits a listing algorithm in time
\tilde{O}(m^{k-\delta}+L)
for some

, then there is a randomized approximation scheme for
\text{{VMin}}(\phi)
.

Proof.

We use

with
\gamma=\delta

. The running time is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\tilde{O}\!\left(m^{k-\frac{\delta(1-1/(1+\varepsilon))}{3}}+\sum_{i=0}^{O(\log n)}\min\{2^{i},n^{\delta/(1+\varepsilon)}\}\cdot\!\left(\!m^{k-\delta}+\frac{n^{k-\delta(1-1/(1+\varepsilon))}}{2^{i}}\!\right)\!\right)\!

\displaystyle=\tilde{O}(m^{k-\frac{\delta(1-1/(1+\varepsilon))}{3}}+n^{\delta/(1+\varepsilon)}m^{k-\delta}+n^{k-\delta(1-1/(1+\varepsilon))})

\displaystyle=\tilde{O}(m^{k-\frac{\delta(1-1/(1+\varepsilon))}{3}}).

Notice that indeed

It remains to show how to list solutions to
\text{{VZero}}(\phi)
fast enough to apply Corollaries

and

. First we give a general algorithm, which can be then directly applied with

Lemma 59
.

Let
\phi
be a Boolean function on
k
inputs. If
\text{{VZero}}(\phi)
can be solved in time
O(m^{k-\delta})
for some
\delta>0
, then there is an algorithm listing
L
solutions of
\text{{VZero}}(\phi)
in time
T(m,L)=\tilde{O}(m^{k-\delta}\cdot L^{\delta/k})
.

Proof.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{Y}
be the given input. We write
m(X_{i})
for the total number of ones in all vectors in
X_{i}
. Each set
X_{i}
can be partitioned into sets
X_{i}=X_{i}^{1}\mathbin{\cup}\{\hat{x}_{i}\}\mathbin{\cup}X_{i}^{2}
where
m(X_{i}^{1})<m(X_{i})/2
and
m(X_{i}^{2})<m(X_{i})/2
in the following way: We greedily add elements to
X_{i}^{1}
until
m(X_{i}^{1})<m(X_{i})/2
but
m(X_{i}^{1}\mathbin{\cup}\{\hat{x}_{i}\})\geq m(X_{i})/2
for the next element
\hat{x}_{i}
. We take
\{\hat{x}_{i}\}
to be a set on its own and set
X_{i}^{2}=X_{i}\setminus(X_{i}^{1}\mathbin{\cup}\{\hat{x}_{i}\})
.

We can list all solutions
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
involving one of the middle elements
\hat{x}_{i}
in time
O(m^{k-1})
using the baseline algorithm. There are
2^{k}
many subproblems remaining, where each is of the form
(X_{1}^{j_{1}},\ldots,X_{k}^{j_{k}})
for
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in\{1,2\}
. Using the assumed efficient algorithm for
\text{{VZero}}(\phi)
, we test for each of these instances whether it contains a solution. For each subproblem which is a YES instance, continue recursively. If at some point we reported more than
L
solutions, stop.

Since we cannot miss solutions in the branching, the correctness is clear. For the running time, note that in each recursive call the sparsity was halved, and therefore the recursion depth is bounded by
O(\log m)
. Each individual call on the
i
-th recursion level takes time
O((m/2^{i})^{k-\delta})
and there are at most
\min\{L,2^{ik}\}
calls on that level. Hence, we can bound the total running time by

T(m,L)\leq\sum_{i=0}^{O(\log m)}\min\{L,2^{ik}\}\cdot O((m/2^{i})^{k-\delta})\leq\tilde{O}(m^{k-\delta}L^{\delta/k}).

The last inequality holds because there are
O(\log m)
summands and the largest one has
L=2^{ik}
.
∎

When
\phi
has and-hardness

Lemma 60
.

Let
\phi
be a Boolean function on

Proof.

This proof is similar to
[

, Lemma 11]
. Let us start with the case

; we later point out how to generalize. We distinguish several formulas

Case 0:
\phi
has 0 or 4 satisfying assignments.

Then

trivially accepts or rejects every input and we can list

solutions in time

.

Case 1:
\phi
has 1 satisfying assignment.

This case is contradictory since

.

Case 2:
\phi
has 2 satisfying assignments.

Case 2a:
\phi(z_{1},z_{2})=(z_{1}\leftrightarrow z_{2})
or
\phi(z_{1},z_{2})=(z_{1}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-6.06941pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\leftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-6.06941pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\leftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.98611pt\kern-5.10555pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\leftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.40282pt\kern-4.49992pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\leftrightarrow}}}z_{2})

In this case, any solution

requires that either

and

are equal in every coordinate, or exactly negations of each other. In any case, for each vector

such that

is a solution. Thus, it is easy to list all solutions in time
\tilde{O}(m)
.

Case 2b:
\phi
depends only on one input

.

Without loss of generality, assume that

. Then there can be at most one vector

which participates in a solution. It takes time

to identify this vector and to list all solutions

, for all

.

Case 3:
\phi
has 3 satisfying assignments.

Then
\phi
has only one falsifying assignment, say

. That means that the only possible solution

is that both
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are the all-ones vector. It easy to check whether such a pair exists in time
O(m)
.

Let
\Phi=\{\phi(z_{1},z_{2}):H_{\mathrm{and}}(\phi)\leq 1\}
. By combining the previous cases, we can also list

solutions of the hybrid problem

in time

. Indeed, we can list

in time

, and report only those solutions which are part of every individual solution set. There are two corner cases: If there are some coordinates associated to the constant
\mathit{false}
-function, then we return no solution at all, and if all coordinates are associated to the constant

-function, then we proceed as in Case 0.

Finally, assume that

. We show how to list

solutions to
\text{{VZero}}(\phi)
. Since

, there exists a set of

Appendix H
Additive Approximation Algorithms

We say that an algorithm computes an

c
-approximation
if it returns a solution whose value is contained in the interval
[\mathrm{OPT}-c,\mathrm{OPT}+c]
. Similarly as for multiplicative approximations, we can define approximation schemes in the additive setting.

Definition 61

(Additive Approximation Scheme)
.

Let
\psi
be an
\text{{OptSP}}_{k}
formula. We say that
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
admits an

if, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists some
\delta>0
and an algorithm computing an additive
\varepsilon|Y|
-approximation of
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
in time
O(m^{k-\delta})
.

We say that
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
admits an
efficient
additive approximation scheme if there exists some fixed constant
\delta>0
, such that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists an algorithm computing an additive
\varepsilon|Y|
-approximation of
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
in time
O(m^{k-\delta})
.

The complexity landscape of multiplicative approximations turned out to be rich, featuring natural problems in all major regimes of approximation guarantees. In contrast, we draw a significantly simpler picture for additive approximations.

Theorem 62

(Additive Approximation)
.

Let
\psi
be an
\text{{OptSP}}_{k}
graph formula.

•

There exists a randomized additive approximation scheme for
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
.

•

If
3\leq H_{\mathrm{deg}}(\psi)
or
H_{\mathrm{deg}}(\psi)=k
, then there exists no efficient additive approximation scheme for
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
unless the Sparse
Max
-
3
-
SAT

In summary, all problems
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
admit approximation schemes, but no efficient ones (except for the problems that can be optimized exactly).

In this section we will show the first item of

, i.e. that all problems
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
admit additive approximation schemes. We defer the proof of the second item to
subsection I.4
. In particular, we will show the following:

Lemma 63

(Additive Approximation Scheme)
.

Let
\phi
be an arbitrary Boolean function. There exists a randomized additive approximation scheme for
\text{{VOpt}}(\phi)
.

Combining
63
with the equivalence of
\text{{Opt}}(\psi)
and vector problems

(which also holds for additive approximations, see

The techniques used to obtain multiplicative approximations often differed substantially for maximization and minimization. As it turns out, to obtain additive approximation schemes we can treat both in a unified way. Indeed, to prove

to an instance of low-dimensional Maximum Inner Product (

and

(
[
11
]
)

For every
c>0
, there exists some

such that

.

As a first algorithmic step, we establish a simple dimension reduction:

Lemma 65
.

Let
\phi
be an arbitrary Boolean function. For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a randomized linear-time reduction, turning a given an instance of
\text{{VOpt}}(\phi)
on
n
vectors of dimension
d
into another
\text{{VOpt}}(\phi)
instance on
n
vectors of dimension

|\mathrm{OPT}/d-\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}/d^{\prime}|\leq\varepsilon

\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}

Proof.

Set
d^{\prime}:=c\varepsilon^{-2}\log n
for some sufficiently large constant
c
. Sample
d^{\prime}
coordinates from
Y
uniformly and independently at random, and let
X_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{k}^{\prime}
be the projections of the input vectors to these

x_{1}^{\prime}\in X_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{k}^{\prime}\in X_{k}^{\prime}

\mathrm{Val}(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{k}^{\prime})

\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathrm{Val}(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{k}^{\prime})]=\frac{d^{\prime}}{d}\mathrm{Val}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\left(\left|\frac{\mathrm{Val}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})}{d}-\frac{\mathrm{Val}(x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{k})}{d^{\prime}}\right|>\varepsilon\right)\leq 2\exp(-2\varepsilon^{2}d^{\prime})\leq O(n^{-2c}).

Thus, by a union bound over all tuples

, where
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}
is the optimal value of the new instance. As claimed, we can construct the instance in time

by once iterating over all one-entries, after subsampling the

Lemma 66
.

Let
\phi
be an arbitrary Boolean function. There exists a randomized additive approximation scheme for
\text{{VMax}}(\phi)
.

Proof.

Let
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k},Y)
be the given
\text{{VMax}}(\phi)

. In order to compute an additive

We focus only on one direction, the other one is analogous. Suppose there exists an additive approximation scheme for
\text{{VMax}}(\phi)

\varepsilon>0

\varepsilon d
-approximation for a given
\text{{VMin}}(\phi)

(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k},Y)

1.

Use the assumed additive approximation scheme to compute an additive

In this section we give a proof of the lower bound part of

Lemma 69

(No Subpolynomial-Factor Approximation for

Let

Now we give the full details. By

.

Lemma 76
