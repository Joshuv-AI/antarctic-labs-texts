On the intersections of finitely generated subgroups of free groups: reduced rank to full rank
Marco Linton

Abstract
. We show that the number of conjugacy classes of intersections A ∩ B g A\cap B^{g} , for fixed finitely generated subgroups A , B < F A,B<F of a free group, is bounded above in terms of the ranks of A A and B B ; this confirms an intuition of Walter Neumann. This result was previously known only in the case where A A or B B is cyclic by the w w -cycles theorem of Helfer and Wise and, independently, Louder and Wilton. In order to prove our main theorem, we introduce new results regarding the structure of fibre products of finite graphs. We work with a generalised definition of graph immersions so that our results apply to the theory of regular languages. For example, we give a new algorithm to decide non-emptiness of the intersection of two regular languages.



1.
Introduction

Given two combinatorial graph maps
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
and
\lambda:\Lambda\to\Delta
, we here study their fibre product
\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
. This explicitly constructed graph is the pullback in the category of combinatorial graphs. There are two main contexts in which this graph arises: free groups
[
26
]
and regular languages
[
24
]
. In the context of free groups, the topological structure of
\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
was intensely investigated in the lead up to the resolution of the strengthened Hanna Neuman conjecture (see in particular
[
26
,
23
,
27
,
5
,
16
]
). In the context of regular languages, the topological and combinatorial structure of
\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
has been studied with applications to free monoids (see
[
25
,
9
]
) and to the state complexity of minimal automata (see
[
1
,
10
]
). Here we give new results on the structure of the fibre product graph and provide applications in both contexts.

Our first result deals with the simpler case when
\Lambda\cong\sqcup S^{1}
. A cycle
\lambda:S^{1}\to\Delta
is
primitive
if it does not factor properly through any other cycle
S^{1}\to\Delta
. The
degree

\deg(\lambda)
will be the largest degree of a covering map
S^{1}\to S^{1}
that
\lambda
factors through. Recall that if
X
is a topological space, then
\beta_{i}(X)
is the
i^{\text{th}}
Betti number
of
X
. In
[
11
]
and
[
19
]
it was shown that if
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Lambda
is an immersion and
\lambda:S^{1}\to\Delta
is primitive, then
\core(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}S^{1})
consists of at most
\beta_{1}(\Gamma)
components. This improved upon other bounds given in
[
30
]
and resolved the
w
-cycles conjecture, first stated in
[
29
]
. If we allow
\lambda
to be imprimitive, then we would have at most
\beta_{1}(\Gamma)
distinct free homotopy classes of cycles instead. Allowing
\lambda
to also consist of arbitrarily many cycles, we prove the following result.

.

Let
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
and
\lambda:\mathbb{S}\to\Delta
be graph immersions with
\mathbb{S}=\sqcup S^{1}
. If
\lambda|_{S^{1}}
is primitive for each
S^{1}\subset\mathbb{S}
, then there are at most
\beta_{1}(\Gamma)
many components
S^{1}\subset\core(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\mathbb{S})
such that:

\deg(p_{\mathbb{S}}|_{S^{1}})\geq 12\cdot\beta_{1}(\Gamma),

where
p_{\mathbb{S}}:\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}
is the natural projection map.

From this theorem it follows that there is a unique map
s_{\Gamma}:\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
where
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
consists of a disjoint union of at most
\beta_{1}(\Gamma)
circles, such that, if
f:S^{1}\to\Gamma
is a primitive cycle satisfying
\deg(\gamma\circ f)\geq 12\cdot\beta_{1}(\Gamma)
, then
f
factors through
s_{\Gamma}
. In Theorem
7.2
we give an efficient algorithm to compute the map
s_{\Gamma}
. Theorems
4.20
and
7.2
are the culmination of several results proved in Section
4
on the full fibre product
\Gamma\times_{\Delta}S^{1}
. For example, we prove bounds on the number and the sizes of components of
\Gamma\times_{\Delta}S^{1}
, even when
\gamma
is not necessarily an immersion.

In Section
5
we allow
\Lambda
to be an arbitrary finite graph. As pointed out in
[
6
]
there is a precise multiplicative relation between the sizes of the edge sets of
\Gamma
and
\Lambda
and that of their fibre product. If
\Gamma
and
\Lambda
represent regular languages or subgroups of a free group, by passing to the core of their fibre product, relative to a collection of
marked vertices
, the subgroup or regular language in question is not changed. If
\Gamma,\Lambda
and
\Delta
are all isomorphic to
S^{1}
, then there is still a multiplicative relation, even after passing to the cores. We show that if we fix the topology of our graphs, this is essentially the only situation in which this relation can occur. The precise statement is as follows.

.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that the following holds. Let
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
and
\lambda:\Lambda\to\Delta
be immersions of graphs and let
s:\mathbb{S}=\core(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\times_{\Delta}\mathbb{S}_{\Lambda})\to\core(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)
be the natural map. Then

\left\lvert E(\core(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)-s(\mathbb{S}))\right\rvert\leq C\cdot(\beta_{1}(\Gamma)\cdot\beta_{1}(\Lambda))^{2}\cdot(\left\lvert E(\Gamma)\right\rvert+\left\lvert E(\Lambda)\right\rvert).

Finally, we also prove similar bounds to those provided by the
w
-cycles theorem in
[
11
]
and
[
19
]
for arbitrary pairs of forwards immersion.

.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that the following holds. Let
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
and
\lambda:\Lambda\to\Delta
be immersions of graphs. The natural map
\theta:\core(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)\to\Delta
consists of a disjoint union of at most:

C\cdot(\beta_{1}(\Gamma)\cdot\beta_{1}(\Lambda))^{2}

many free homotopy classes of connected graph maps.

We prove slightly more general versions of these theorems by also taking into account a set of marked vertices and relaxing the condition on the maps. The full statements of Theorems
5.7
and
5.12
also contain explicit upper bounds for their universal constants.

Now that we have stated our main results for the fibre product graph, we begin with some applications.

1.1.
Free groups

Since Stallings’ seminal paper
[
26
]
, using finite graphs and graph maps to represent free groups and their finitely generated subgroups has been very fruitful. Therefore, results for graphs and graph maps usually come with complementary results in the theory of free groups. Many examples of such results can be found in
[
13
]
. We now discuss the consequences of our theorems in the realm of free groups, starting with the following natural application of Theorem
4.20
.

.

Let
F
be a free group and
A<F
a finitely generated subgroup. Then there are at most
\rk(A)
many conjugacy classes of maximal cyclic subgroups
M<F
such that

12\cdot\rk(A)\leq[M:A\cap M]<\infty.

This has an immediate corollary. In
[
4
]
, Delgado, Ventura and Zhakarov introduce the notions of relative order and spectrum. Given a subset
S
of a group
G
, the
relative order

\mathcal{O}_{S}(g)
of an element
g\in G
, with respect to
S
, is the smallest integer
n\geq 1
such that
g^{n}\in S
. The
S
-spectrum
of
G
is the set
\mathcal{O}_{S}(G)=\cup_{g\in G}\mathcal{O}_{S}(g)
. It was shown in
[
13
]
that the relative spectrum of a finitely generated subgroup of a free group is bounded. In
[
4
]
, this result is generalised and the relative spectrum for other classes of groups is also studied. Using Theorem
6.4
, we may recover the result for free groups and also improve the known bound to one that, in some sense, only depends on the rank of the subgroup. Moreover, by Theorem
7.2
, we may efficiently compute an upper bound of the
A
-spectrum for finitely generated subgroups
A<F
of a free group.

.

Let
F
be a free group and
A<F
a finitely generated subgroup. Then there exists a set
N\subset\mathbb{N}
of cardinality bounded above by
13\cdot\rk(A)
, such that:

\mathcal{O}_{A}(F)=\bigcup_{n\in N}\bigcup_{d\mid n}d.

Our last result of this section is an application of Theorem
5.12
. It has been known since the 1950s by work of Howson
[
12
]
that the intersection of two finitely generated subgroups of a free group is again finitely generated. If
A
is a free group, recall that
\bar{\rk}(A)=\min{\{0,\rk(A)-1\}}
is the
reduced rank
of
A
. It was conjectured by Hanna Neumann that for any two finitely generated subgroups
A,B<F
of a free group, the inequality
\bar{\rk}(A\cap B)\leq\bar{\rk}(A)\cdot\bar{\rk}(B)
held
[
22
]
. A clean graph argument (see
[
23
]
) shows that in fact
\sum_{[A\cap B^{g}]}\bar{\rk}(A\cap B^{g})\leq 2\cdot\bar{\rk}(A)\cdot\bar{\rk}(B)
, where
[A\cap B^{g}]
denotes the conjugacy class of the subgroup
A\cap B^{g}
. In
[
23
]
, Walter Neumann formulated the strengthened Hanna Neumann conjecture, asserting that
\sum_{[A\cap B^{g}]}\bar{\rk}(A\cap B^{g})\leq\bar{\rk}(A)\cdot\bar{\rk}(B)
. In 2011, this long standing conjecture was settled independently by Mineyev
[
21
]
and Freidmann
[
8
]
.

In the same article in which the strengthened Hanna Neumann conjecture was proposed
[
23
]
, it was also shown that the larger sum
\sum_{[A\cap B^{g}]}\rk(A\cap B^{g})
is finite. The bound that one can derive from the argument depends on the size of generating sets for
A
and
B
. This, in turn, depends on a chosen embedding. However, Walter Neumann goes on to suggest that it may be possible to bound this larger sum solely in terms of the ranks of
A
and
B
.

Since then, most of the literature has focused on the strengthened Hanna Neumann conjecture. However, one improvement to the naive full rank bound in the case where
B=\langle w\rangle
is cyclic is offered by the
w
-cycles theorem (see
[
11
,
19
]
). Indeed, it is an immediate consequence that the inequality
\sum_{[A\cap\langle w\rangle^{g}]}\rk(A\cap\langle w\rangle^{g})\leq\rk(A)
holds. This result is even referred to as a rank 1 version of the Hanna Neumann theorem
[
11
]
. As a consequence of Theorem
5.12
, we prove a more general result for arbitrary finitely generated subgroups of a free group, confirming Walter Neumann’s intuition.

.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
, with the following property. For any free group
F
and any finitely generated subgroups
A,B<F
, we have:

\sum_{[A\cap B^{g}]}\rk(A\cap B^{g})\leq C\cdot(\rk(A)\cdot\rk(B))^{2}.

As demonstrated by Example
5.13
, a lower bound for a sharp upper bound for this sum is
\rk(A)\cdot\rk(B)
.

1.2.
Regular languages

Let
\Delta
be a rose graph with an edge for each element in some finite set
\Sigma
. Then to each regular language
L\subset\Sigma^{*}
, there is an associated unique minimal deterministic finite state automaton
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
, such that the language
L(\Gamma)
is precisely
L
. It is a classical fact that
L(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)=L(\Gamma)\cap L(\Lambda)

[
24
]
, thus providing an explicit deterministic quadratic time algorithm to compute the intersection of two regular languages. By considering finite sheeted covers of graphs we see that there are pairs of regular languages that require a quadratic number of states to express their intersection. Hence, this automaton cannot be improved. On the other hand, it is an open question as to whether the non-emptiness of
L(\Gamma)\cap L(\Lambda)
can be decided in subquadratic time
[
14
]
. This decision problem, extending to an input of
k
DFAs, is known as the
k
-DFA non-emptiness intersection problem (
k
-DFA-NEI). Restricting to unary alphabets,
2
-DFA-NEI is known to be solvable in deterministic linear time and
3
-DFA-NEI in subquadratic time
[
2
]
.

If the true complexity of DFA-NEI was strictly better than that of the Rabin-Scott algorithm, then several strong complexity results would be implied. For example, if
2
-DFA-NEI could be solved in subquadratic time, it would imply that
\text{NSPACE}[n+o(n)]\subset\text{DTIME}[2^{(1-\epsilon)\cdot n}]
for some
\epsilon>0
, SAT would be solvable in subexponential time and the strong exponential time hypothesis would be contradicted
[
3
]
. If
k
-DFA-NEI could be solved in
O(n^{o(k)})
time, we would also have
\text{NSPACE}[n]\subset\text{DTIME}[2^{o(n)}]

[
3
]
and
\text{NL}\neq\text{P}

[
28
]
.

We present a different approach to the non-emptiness intersection problem based on Theorem
5.7
. If we fix the topology of the automata, we obtain complexity bounds that depend linearly on the input size. Define
\partial\Gamma\subset V(\Gamma)
to be the vertices with indegree or outdegree equal to
0
.

.

For some
q>0
, there is an algorithm that, given as input two DFAs
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
and
\lambda:\Gamma\to\Delta
such that
\beta_{1}(\Gamma),\beta_{1}(\Lambda),\left\lvert\partial\Gamma\right\rvert,\left\lvert\partial\Lambda\right\rvert\leq m
and
\left\lvert E(\Gamma)\right\rvert,\left\lvert E(\Lambda)\right\rvert\leq n
, decides if
L(\Gamma)\cap L(\Lambda)
is non-empty in

O(m^{q}\cdot n)

time.

Note that if
\Delta
contains a single edge, then for every forwards graph immersion
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
we have
\beta_{1}(\Gamma)+\left\lvert\partial\Gamma\right\rvert\leq 2
. Thus, this can be seen as a direct generalisation of the unary case.

Theorem 2.1

The following are Lemmas 1 and 3 of

Let

Suppose

v,w\in\Sigma^{*}
. The set
\sub(v,w)
can be decomposed:

\sub(v,w)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\ap(p_{i},q_{i},r_{i})

where
k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{|v|}{|w|}\right\rfloor
and
p_{i}\cdot q_{i}\leq\left\lvert w\right\rvert
for all
i\leq k
.

We now move onto classifying prefix and suffix overlaps in a similar way. Since
\pref(w,v)=\{\left\lvert v\right\rvert-i\mid i\in\suff(v,w)\}
, it is only necessary to treat the
\suff(v,w)
case.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
v,w\in\Sigma^{*}
. If
i<j\in\suff(v,w)
, then

\ap\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\left\lvert v\right\rvert-i}{j-i}\right\rfloor,j-i,i\right)\subset\suff(v,w).

Proof.

If
i<j\in\suff(v,w)
, then
v[i:]=w[:\left\lvert v\right\rvert-i]
and
v[j:]=w[:\left\lvert v\right\rvert-j]
. In particular:

v[i:j]\cdot w[:\left\lvert v\right\rvert-i]=w[:\left\lvert v\right\rvert-i]\cdot w[\left\lvert v\right\rvert-j:\left\lvert v\right\rvert-i].

By Lemma
2.3
,
v[i:]
and
w[:\left\lvert v\right\rvert-i]
have period
j-i
and so
\ap\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\left\lvert v\right\rvert-i}{i-j}\right\rfloor,j-i,i\right)\subset\suff(v,w)
as claimed.
∎

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
v,w\in\Sigma^{*}
. The set of suffix indices of
w
in
v
can be decomposed as a union of arithmetic progressions:

\suff(v,w)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\ap(p_{i},q_{i},r_{i})

such that, for all
n\leq k
:

(1)

if
j\in\suff(v,w)
and
j\leq|v|-q_{n}
, then
j\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\ap(p_{i},q_{i},r_{i})
,

(2)

r_{n}\geq|v|-q_{n-1}
,

(3)

p_{n}\cdot q_{n}+r_{n}\geq|v|-q_{n}
,

(4)

q_{n}\leq\frac{q_{n-1}}{2}
,

(5)

v[r_{n}:r_{n}+q_{n}]
is primitive.

Furthermore,
k\leq\min{\{\left\lfloor\log_{2}{|v|}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\log_{2}{|w|}\right\rfloor\}}

Proof.

We will inductively define arithmetic progressions
\ap(p_{n},q_{n},r_{n})\subset\suff(v,w)
satisfying (
1
) to (
5
) in the statement. Once we have done this, (
1
) will imply the decomposition and (
4
) will imply the bound on
k
.

For the base case we will simply set
r_{1}=\min{\{\suff(v,w)\}}
,
p_{1}=0
and
q_{1}=\left\lvert v\right\rvert-r_{1}
. Now suppose
n\geq 2
. Let
j\in\suff(v,w)
be the smallest index not contained in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-1}\ap(p_{i},q_{i},r_{i})
. Let
l\in\suff(v,w)\cup\{\left\lvert v\right\rvert\}
minimise
\left\lvert l-j\right\rvert
. Inductively by (
1
), we have
j\geq\left\lvert v\right\rvert-q_{n-1}
and by (
3
) we have
p_{n-1}\cdot q_{n-1}+r_{n-1}\geq\left\lvert v\right\rvert-q_{n-1}
, hence
\left\lvert l-j\right\rvert\leq\frac{q_{n-1}}{2}
. So if we set
r_{n}=j
,
q_{n}=\left\lvert l-j\right\rvert
and
p_{n}=\left\lfloor\frac{\left\lvert v\right\rvert-r_{n}}{q_{n}}\right\rfloor
, then
\ap(p_{n},q_{n},r_{n})\subset\suff(v,w)
by Lemma
2.15
. This verifies the last four properties, so we turn our attention to (
1
).

If
l=|v|
, then we are done. Now assume that
l\neq\left\lvert v\right\rvert
and hence
p_{n}\geq 1
. Suppose there is some index
m\in\suff(v,w)
such that
|v|-q_{n-1}<m\leq|v|-q_{n}
and
m\notin\cup_{i=1}^{n}\ap(p_{i},q_{i},r_{i})
. By our choice of
j
, we must have
m>j
. Furthermore,
\left\lvert m-j\right\rvert\geq q_{n}
and
q_{n}\nmid\left\lvert m-j\right\rvert
. So in particular, there is some integer
i<p_{n}
such that
0<m-(i\cdot q_{n}+r_{n})<q_{n}
. We may assume that
m
was chosen to minimise
\left\lvert m-i\cdot p_{n}+r_{n}\right\rvert
over all valid choices of
i
. Then by Lemma
2.15
,
v[i\cdot q_{n}+r_{n}:]
has period
m-(i\cdot q_{n}+r_{n})
. Now by Theorem
2.1
we arrive at a contradiction to the minimality of
m
. Hence no such
m
exists and (
1
) holds.
∎

3.
Graphs

A directed graph is a tuple
\Gamma=(V,E,o,t)
where
V
is the set of
vertices
,
E
is the set of
edges
and
o,t:E\to V
are the
origin
and
target
maps respectively. We always assume our graphs are directed and so will refer to them simply as graphs. If
S\subset V
or
S\subset E
, then we denote by
\Gamma-S
the maximal subgraph of
\Gamma
that does not contain any vertices or edges in
S
. Define the
forwards star
of a vertex
v
in a graph
\Gamma
to be the set
\fstar(v,\Gamma)=\{e\in E(\Gamma)\mid o(e)=v\}
. We also define the set
\bstar(v,\Gamma)=\{e\in E(\Gamma)\mid t(e)=v\}
which we will call the
backwards star
. We then define the
indegree
of
v
to be
\ideg(v)=\left\lvert\bstar(v,\Gamma)\right\rvert
, the
outdegree
of
v
to be
\odeg(v)=\left\lvert\fstar(v,\Gamma)\right\rvert
and the
degree
of
v
to be
\deg(v)=\ideg(v)+\odeg(v)
.

With applications in mind, it is useful to allow an auxiliary subset
V^{m}(\Gamma)\subset V(\Gamma)
of
marked vertices
. We further decompose this set
V^{m}(\Gamma)=V^{i}(\Gamma)\cup V^{f}(\Gamma)
into
initial
and
final vertices
.

Definition 3.1
.

Denote by
\partial\Gamma\subset V(\Gamma)
the vertices
v
such that
\ideg(v)=0
or
\odeg(v)=0
.

Note that this is a different definition to the usual boundary of a graph.

Definition 3.2
.

Denote by
\bar{V}(\Gamma)\subset V(\Gamma)
the vertices
v
such that
\ideg(v)\neq 1
or
\odeg(v)\neq 1
.

A
graph map

\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
consists of a pair of maps
\gamma_{V}:V(\Gamma)\to V(\Delta)
and
\gamma_{E}:E(\Gamma)\to E(\Delta)
such that
\gamma_{V}\circ o=o\circ\gamma_{E}
and
\gamma_{V}\circ t=t\circ\gamma_{E}
. If
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
is a graph map of graphs with marked vertices, we require that
V^{i}(\Gamma)
maps to
V^{i}(\Delta)
and
V^{f}(\Gamma)
maps to
V^{f}(\Delta)
. We say a graph map
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
is a
forwards immersion
if the induced map
\gamma_{v}^{+}:\fstar(v,\Gamma)\to\fstar(\gamma(v),\Delta)
is injective for each
v\in V(\Gamma)
. We say
f
is an
immersion
if
\gamma_{v}^{+}
and
\gamma_{v}^{-}
are injective for all
v\in V(\Gamma)
.

A
segment
is a graph consisting of vertices
v_{0},...,v_{k}
and edges
e_{0},...,e_{k-1}
such that
o(e_{i})=v_{i}
,
t(e_{i})=v_{i+1}
for all
i<k
. A
path
is a graph map
f:I\to\Gamma
where
I
is a segment. The
length
of a path
f
will be defined as
|f|=|E(I)|=k
. Denote by

\displaystyle f(i)

\displaystyle=v_{i},

\displaystyle f[i]

\displaystyle=e_{i},

\displaystyle o(f)

\displaystyle=v_{0},

\displaystyle t(f)

\displaystyle=v_{k}.

Paths may also be considered as words over
E(\Gamma)
and thus we will be able to apply the same notation and results from Section
2
to paths. If
f
and
g
are two paths with
t(f)=o(g)
, then we will write
f*g
for their concatenation. A path
f
is
primitive
if there is no other path
w:I\to\Gamma
such that
f=w^{n}
for some
n\geq 2
. A path
f
is
simple
if
f(i)\neq f(j)
for all
i,j<\left\lvert f\right\rvert
. A path
f:I\to\Gamma
is a
loop
if
o(f)=t(f)
. We say that a path
f:I\to\Gamma
is
accepting
if
o(f)\in V^{i}(\Gamma)
and
t(f)\in V^{f}(\Gamma)
. A path
f:I\to\Gamma
is
admissible
if it is accepting or if it is a loop.

We say a path
f:I\to\Gamma

connects

o(f)
with
t(f)\in V(\Gamma)
. Connectedness generates a reflexive and transitive binary relation on
V(\Gamma)
. A graph is
connected
it has a single equivalence class under the symmetric closure of this binary relation. The
connected components
are the maximal connected subgraphs.

A
circle
is a graph consisting of vertices
v_{0},...,v_{k-1}
and edges
e_{0},...,e_{k-1}
such that
o(e_{i})=v_{i}
and
t(e_{i})=v_{i+1}
for all
i<k
and where the indices are taken mod
k
. A
cycle
is a graph map
f:S^{1}\to\Gamma
where
S^{1}
is a circle. Recall that
\deg(f)
is the largest degree of a covering map
S^{1}\to S^{1}
that
f
factors through. If
f:I\to\Gamma
is a path, we write
\hat{f}:S^{1}\to\Gamma
for the cycle obtained from
f
by identifying the vertices
\partial I
. The union of images of cycles in
\Gamma
is
the core
of
\Gamma
; we denote this by
\core(\Gamma)
. The
relative core

\rcore(\Gamma)
is the union of the images of all admissible paths.

Definition 3.3
.

Denote by
\bar{E}(\Gamma)
the collection of paths
e:I\to\Gamma
and cycles
f:S^{1}\to\Gamma
such that:

(1)

e_{E}
and
f_{E}
are injective,

(2)

e(I)\cap\bar{V}(\Gamma)=\{o(e),t(e)\}
and
f(S^{1})\cap\bar{V}(\Gamma)=\emptyset
.

We denote by
\bar{E}_{c}(\Gamma)\subset\bar{E}(\Gamma)
the subset corresponding to all the cycles.

See Figure
1
for an example of the elements
\bar{E}(\Gamma)
. Throughout, we may consider the elements of
\bar{E}(\Gamma)
as paths or cycles, or as subgraphs of
\Gamma
.

Figure 1
.
A graph
\Gamma
and the segments
\bar{E}(\Gamma)
.

We define an alternative version of our origin and target maps for graphs
\Gamma
such that
\bar{E}_{c}(\Gamma)=\emptyset
. If
e\in E(\Gamma)
and
\bar{e}\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)
is the unique element such that
e\subset\bar{e}(I)
, then we do this as follows:

\bar{o}:E(\Gamma)\to\bar{E}(\Gamma)\times\mathbb{N},

\bar{o}(e)=(\bar{e},i),

where
o(e)=\bar{e}(i)
. Then the map

\bar{t}:E(\Gamma)\to\bar{E}(\Gamma)\times\mathbb{N}

is defined similarly. We also extend these maps to non-trivial paths.

Definition 3.4
.

Suppose
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
is a graph map and
w:I\to\Delta
is a non-trivial path. Then we will denote by
\lift(\gamma,w)
the lifts of
w
to
\Gamma
.

Definition 3.5
.

Suppose
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
is a graph map and
w:I\to\Delta
is a path. Define
\slift(\gamma,w)\subset\lift(\gamma,w)
to be the lifts that do not contain any vertex
v\in\bar{V}(\Gamma)
in their image. Define
\olift(\gamma,w)\subset\lift(\gamma,w)
to be the lifts which do contain some
v\in\bar{V}(\Gamma)
in their image.

In Section
5.1
, we characterise these subsets for the fibre product graph.

The following lemma is a rephrasing of a standard fact about graphs using our notation.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph, then:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bar{V}(\Gamma)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\max{\{1,-2\cdot\chi(\Gamma)+2\cdot\left\lvert\partial\Gamma\right\rvert\}},

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)-\bar{E}_{c}(\Gamma)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\max{\{0,-3\cdot\chi(\Gamma)+2\cdot\left\lvert\partial\Gamma\right\rvert\}}.

3.1.
The fibre product graph

Let
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
and
\lambda:\Lambda\to\Delta
be two graph maps. Define the
fibre product graph
of
\Gamma
and
\Lambda
as the graph
\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
with vertex set:

V(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)=\{(u,v)\in V(\Gamma)\times V(\Lambda)\mid\gamma_{V}(u)=\lambda_{V}(v)\}

and edge set:

E(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)=\{(e,f)\in E(\Gamma)\times E(\Lambda)\mid\gamma_{E}(e)=\lambda_{E}(f)\}.

The origin and target maps
o,t:E(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)\to V(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)
will then be given by
o(e,f)=(o(e),o(f))
and
t(e,f)=(t(e),t(f))
. We will denote by
p_{\Gamma},p_{\Lambda}:\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda\to\Gamma,\Lambda
the natural projection maps. If our graphs have marked vertices, then we will define the marked vertices of
\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
to be

\displaystyle V^{i}(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\{(v,w)\in V^{i}(\Gamma)\times V^{i}(\Lambda)\mid\gamma(v)=\lambda(w)\},

\displaystyle V^{f}(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\{(v,w)\in V^{f}(\Gamma)\times V^{f}(\Lambda)\mid\gamma(v)=\lambda(w)\}.

In general, as pointed out in
[
6
]
, the following is satisfied:

\left\lvert E(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)\right\rvert=\sum_{e\in E(\Delta)}\left\lvert\gamma_{E}^{-1}(e)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\lambda_{E}^{-1}(e)\right\rvert.

If
\gamma
and
\lambda
are finite covers, then
\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
is equal to its core. Hence, a quadratic bound on the edges and vertices is best possible. The following lemma demonstrates a similar relationship between
\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert
,
\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Lambda)\right\rvert
and
\left\lvert\bar{E}(\rcore(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda))\right\rvert
when
\gamma
and
\lambda
are forwards immersions.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
and
\lambda:\Lambda\to\Delta
be forwards immersions of graphs. If
\Theta=\rcore(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)
, then

\left\lvert\bar{V}(\Theta)\right\rvert,\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Theta)-\bar{E}_{c}(\Theta)\right\rvert\leq 4\cdot\left(\left\lvert V^{i}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert V^{i}(\Lambda)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Lambda)\right\rvert\right).

Proof.

Let
V^{\prime}\subset V(\Theta)
be the vertices with outdegree two or more. By removing these vertices and taking the closure, we obtain a disjoint union of graphs
\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}\Theta_{i}
along with a surjective quotient map
q:\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}\Theta_{i}\to\Theta
. Each graph
\Theta_{i}
has the property that
\odeg(v)\leq 1
by construction. Furthermore,
q:\bar{V}(\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}\Theta_{i})\to\bar{V}(\Theta)
is surjective and
q:\bar{E}(\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}\Theta_{i})\to\bar{E}(\Theta)
is bijective. We have at most

\left\lvert V^{i}(\Theta)\right\rvert+\sum_{v\in V^{\prime}}\odeg(v)\leq\left\lvert V^{i}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert V^{i}(\Lambda)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Lambda)\right\rvert

many leaf vertices in
\sqcup_{i=1}^{n}\Theta_{i}
. Hence

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert\partial\Theta_{i}\right\rvert\leq 2\cdot\left(\left\lvert V^{i}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert V^{i}(\Lambda)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Lambda)\right\rvert\right).

Thus, since
\beta_{1}(\Theta_{i})\leq 1
for all
i
, the bounds follow by Lemma
3.6
.
∎

4.
The structure of
\Gamma\times_{\Delta}S^{1}

Let us fix a graph map
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
and a primitive loop
w:I\to\Delta
. A cycle
f:S^{1}\to\Gamma
is a
w
-cycle
if
f
factors through
\hat{w}
. A path
f:I\to\Gamma
will be called a
w
-path
if
\gamma\circ f
factors surjectively through
\hat{w}
. The projection of the
w
-path to
\Delta
can be factorised like a word:

\gamma\circ f=w^{\prime}*w^{n}*w^{\prime\prime}

where
w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime\prime}
are a proper suffix and a proper prefix of
w
respectively. We call this a
w
-factorisation of
f
. The following Lemma follows from Lemma
2.6
:

Lemma 4.1
.

If
f:I\to\Gamma
is a
w
-path, then it has a unique
w
-factorisation.

The
w
-length
of a
w
-path
f:I\to\Gamma
is the number
n=|f|_{w}
such that
\gamma\circ f=w^{\prime}*w^{n}*w^{\prime\prime}
is the
w
-factorisation of
f
. By Lemma
4.1
this is always well defined.

A
w
-path
f^{\prime}

right extends

f
, or is a
right extension of
f
, if
f
is a proper prefix of
f
. If
f
is a proper suffix of
f^{\prime}
, then we say
f^{\prime}

left extends

f
. A
w
-path is a
simple
w
-path
if

f(k+i\cdot|w|)\neq f(k+j\cdot|w|)

for all integers
i<j
and
k
, unless
k+j\cdot|w|=|f|
. We refer to
f
as
right maximal
if
f
is a simple
w
-path and there is no simple
w
-path
f^{\prime}
which right extends
f
. We similarly define
left maximal
. We refer to
f
as
maximal
if it is both left and right maximal. We also refer to
f
as
right submaximal
if either
f
is right maximal, or
f
is simple and
t(f)\in\bar{V}(\Gamma)\cup V^{f}(\Gamma)
. Similarly, we say
f
is
left submaximal
if either
f
is left maximal, or if
f
is a simple
w
-path and
o(f)\in\bar{V}(\Gamma)\cup V^{i}(\Gamma)
. Then
submaximal

w
-paths are those that are both left and right submaximal. Denote by
\wmax(\gamma,w)
the set of maximal
w
-paths in
\Gamma
and by
\subwmax(\gamma,w)
the set of submaximal
w
-paths in
\Gamma
.
Defining:

\displaystyle\swmax(\gamma,w)

\displaystyle=\slift(\gamma,w)\cap\wmax(\gamma,w),

\displaystyle\owmax(\gamma,w)

\displaystyle=\olift(\gamma,w)\cap\wmax(\gamma,w),

\displaystyle\ssubwmax(\gamma,w)

\displaystyle=\slift(\gamma,w)\cap\subwmax(\gamma,w),

\displaystyle\osubwmax(\gamma,w)

\displaystyle=\olift(\gamma,w)\cap\subwmax(\gamma,w),

we get decompositions

\displaystyle\wmax(\gamma,w)

\displaystyle=\swmax(\gamma,w)\sqcup\owmax(\gamma,w),

\displaystyle\subwmax(\gamma,w)

\displaystyle=\ssubwmax(\gamma,w)\sqcup\osubwmax(\gamma,w).

Remark 4.2
.

If
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
is a forwards immersion, then maximal right extensions of
w
-paths are unique. However, there may be several left maximal extensions if
\gamma
is not an immersion.

The following lemmas follow directly from the definitions, but we record them for later use.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
be a forwards immersion and
w:I\to\Delta
a primitive loop. Every
w
-path
f:I\to\Gamma
has a unique decomposition:

f=f_{s}*f_{c}^{n}*f_{c}^{\prime}

for some
n\geq 0
and where

(1)

f_{s}*f_{c}
is a simple
w
-path,

(2)

f_{c}
is a simple
w
-cycle,

(3)

f_{c}^{\prime}
is a proper prefix of
f_{c}
.

Furthermore,
f
is simple if and only if
n\leq 1
and
f_{c}^{\prime}=\epsilon
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
be a graph map and
w:I\to\Delta
a primitive loop. Let
f:I\to\Gamma
be an admissible path with a factorisation

f=f_{1}*f_{w}*f_{2}

with
f_{w}
a maximal
w
-path in
I
. Then if
f_{w}=f_{s}*f_{c}^{n}*f_{c}^{\prime}
is the factorisation from Lemma
4.3
,
f_{s}*f_{c}
is submaximal and
f_{c}^{\prime}
is a submaximal prefix of
f_{c}
.

Extending our definition of
w
-paths to graphs, we say a graph map
W\to\Delta
is a
w
-graph map
if it factors surjectively through the map
\hat{w}:S^{1}\to\Delta
. The graph
W
will then be called a
w
-graph
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
W
be a connected graph and
W\to S^{1}
a forwards immersion. Then
W
is one of the following:

(1)

A rooted tree,

(2)

a cycle with rooted trees attached along their root vertices.

Every
w
-path factors through
\Gamma\times_{\Delta}S^{1}
, but this is not quite a
w
-graph as it contains components that don’t surject onto
S^{1}
. So denote by
\Omega_{\gamma,w}\subset\Gamma\times_{\Delta}S^{1}
the subgraph consisting of the
w
-graph components. Call this
the
w
-graph of
\Gamma
and denote by
p_{w}:\Omega_{\gamma,w}\to S^{1}
the projection map. We will further define the subgraph
\Omega_{\gamma,w}^{k}\subset\Omega_{\gamma,w}
consisting of the components which support a
w
-path of
w
-length at least
k
.

The following Lemma is a graph version of Lemma
2.6
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
f:I\to\Gamma
be a path with
|f|\geq|w|
and
p_{\Gamma}:\Omega_{\gamma,w}\to\Gamma
the projection map, then:

\left\lvert\lift(p_{\Gamma},f)\right\rvert\leq 1.

Proof.

If
\tilde{f}:I\to\Omega_{\gamma,w}
is a lift of
f
, then
f
has a
w
-factorisation. If
|f|\geq|w|
, by Lemma
2.6
,
f
has a unique
w
-factorisation and so
o(p_{w}\circ\tilde{f})
is uniquely determined by this factorisation. By the construction of the fibre product graph, it follows that there can be at most one lift of
f
to
\Omega_{\gamma,w}
.
∎

4.1.
w
-paths

In this section we will fix a graph map
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
and a primitive loop
w:I\to\Delta
. The aim of this section will be to prove bounds on the number of
w
-lengths that
w
-paths in
\Gamma
can have.

First, we define a type of factorisation of paths relative to a subgraph.

Definition 4.7
.

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
be a subgraph and
f:I\to\Gamma
a path. If
f
does not factor through
\Gamma-\bar{V}(\Gamma)
, the
\Gamma^{\prime}
-factorisation
of
f
is the factorisation of the form:

f=e_{0}[-l:]*w_{1}*e_{1}*...*e_{n-1}*w_{n}*e_{n}[:m],

where:

(1)

o(w_{i})
,
t(w_{i})\in\bar{V}(\Gamma)
,

(2)

w_{i}
is supported in
\Gamma^{\prime}\cup\bar{V}(\Gamma)
,

(3)

e_{0}
,
e_{n}\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)
,

(4)

e_{1},...,e_{n-1}\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)-\bar{E}(\Gamma^{\prime})
,

(5)

n
is maximal possible among all such factorisations.

If
f
does factor through
\Gamma-\bar{V}(\Gamma)
, then by convention the
\Gamma^{\prime}
-factorisation of
f
will be
e_{0}[l:m]
for some
e_{0}\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)
.

The following lemma follows from the definition.

Lemma 4.8
.

\Gamma^{\prime}
-factorisations are unique.

Denote by
\bar{E}_{w}(\Gamma)\subset\bar{E}(\Gamma)
the elements
e
such that
|e|<|w|
. Then denote by

\Gamma_{w}=\cup_{e\in\bar{E}_{w}(\Gamma)}e

the subgraph of
\Gamma
induced by
\bar{E}_{w}(\Gamma)
.

Definition 4.9
.

We will call a vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
a
w
-sink
if there is a
w
-path
f:I\to\Gamma_{w}
satisfying the following:

(1)

o(f)
,
t(f)\in\bar{V}(\Gamma)
,

(2)

\gamma\circ f=w^{\prime}*w*w^{\prime\prime}
for some proper suffix
w^{\prime}
of
w
and proper prefix
w^{\prime\prime}
of
w
,

(3)

f(|w^{\prime}*w|)=v
.

Denote by
V_{w}(\Gamma)\subset V(\Gamma_{w})
the set of all
w
-sinks of
\Gamma
.

The
w
-sinks of a graph can be thought of as a set of vertices any sufficiently long
w
-path must visit. We now show that there cannot be too many of such vertices.

Lemma 4.10
.

There is a cyclic shift
w_{0}
of
w
such that:

\left\lvert V_{w_{0}}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\leq 2\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma_{w})\right\rvert.

Proof.

Since a cycle doesn’t have any
w
-sinks, we may assume that
\bar{E}_{c}(\Gamma)=\emptyset
. Now let
w=x*y
with
x\neq\epsilon
such that
w_{0}=y*x
is the cyclic shift of
w
from Theorem
2.17
with

\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\}=\{\gamma\circ e\mid e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma_{w})\}.

For each
v\in V_{w_{0}}(\Gamma)
, let
f_{v}:I\to\Gamma_{w}
be a
w_{0}
-path such that:
o(f_{v})
,
t(f_{v})\in\bar{V}(\Gamma_{w})
,
\gamma\circ f_{v}=w_{v}^{\prime}*w_{0}*w_{v}^{\prime\prime}
and
f_{v}(|w_{v}^{\prime}*w_{0}|)=v
. Denote by

\bar{t}\left(f_{v}\left[:\left\lvert w_{v}^{\prime}*w_{0}\right\rvert\right]\right)=(e_{v},i_{v})\in\bar{E}(\Gamma_{w})\times\mathbb{N}

Now by Theorem
2.17
, we have:

\left\lvert\{(e_{v},i_{v})\}_{v\in V_{w_{0}}(\Gamma)}\cap\{e\}\times\mathbb{N}\right\rvert\leq 2,

for all
e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma_{w})
, thus obtaining the desired inequality.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

If
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
is a forwards immersion, then:

\left\lvert V_{w}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\leq 2\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma_{w})\right\rvert.

Proof.

Since a cycle doesn’t have any
w
-sinks, we may assume that
\bar{E}_{c}(\Gamma)=\emptyset
. Now let
w=x*y
with
x\neq\epsilon
such that
y*x
is the cyclic shift of
w
from Theorem
2.17
with

\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\}=\{\gamma\circ e\mid e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma_{w})\}.

For each
v\in V_{w}
, let
f_{v}:I\to\Gamma_{w}
be a
w
-path such that:
o(f_{v})
,
t(f_{v})\in\bar{V}(\Gamma)
,
\gamma\circ f_{v}=w_{v}^{\prime}*w*w_{v}^{\prime\prime}
and
f_{v}(|w_{v}^{\prime}*w|)=v
. Denote by

\bar{t}(f_{v}[:\left\lvert w_{v}^{\prime}*x\right\rvert])=(e_{v},i_{v})\in\bar{E}(\Gamma_{w})\times\mathbb{N}

This is well defined since
x\neq\epsilon
. Since
\gamma
is a forwards immersion,
(e_{v},i_{v})=(e_{v^{\prime}},i_{v^{\prime}})
if and only if
v=v^{\prime}
. Now by Theorem
2.17
, we have:

\left\lvert\{(e_{v},i_{v})\}_{v\in V_{w}(\Gamma)}\cap\{e\}\times\mathbb{N}\right\rvert\leq 2,

for all
e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma_{w})
, thus obtaining the desired inequality.
∎

Using these bounds on
w
-sinks and
\Gamma_{w}
-factorisations, we prove something about general
w
-paths.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
f:I\to\Gamma
be a simple
w
-path that does not factor through
\Gamma-\bar{V}(\Gamma)
. Let

f=e_{0}[-l:]*w_{1}*e_{1}*...*e_{n-1}*w_{n}*e_{n}[:m],

be its
\Gamma_{w}
-factorisation. Then:

(1)

e_{i}=e_{j}
if and only if
i=j
for all
1\leq i,j<n
,

(2)

if
l\geq\left\lvert w\right\rvert
or
m\geq\left\lvert w\right\rvert
, then
e_{0}\neq e_{i}
or
e_{m}\neq e_{i}
for all
1\leq i<n
respectively,

(3)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert w_{i}\right\rvert_{w}\leq 4\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma_{w})\right\rvert
,

(4)

if
\gamma
is a forwards immersion, then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert w_{i}\right\rvert_{w}\leq 2\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma_{w})\right\rvert

Proof.

Since
f
is a simple
w
-path, it lifts to
\Omega_{\gamma,w}
as a simple path. As the lift is simple as a path, it can only traverse each edge in
\Omega_{\gamma,w}
at most once. Hence by Lemma
4.6
,
1
and
2
follow. Property
3
follows from Lemma
4.10
, the pigeonhole principle and the fact that
w^{2}
contains a copy of each cyclic shift of
w
as a subword. More directly,
4
follows from Lemma
4.11
.
∎

A direct consequence of Lemma
4.12
is that if
f:I\to\Gamma
is a simple
w
-path, then:

\left\lvert f\right\rvert_{w}\leq 4\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert+\sum_{e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)}\left\lfloor\frac{\left\lvert e\right\rvert}{\left\lvert w\right\rvert}\right\rfloor.

The following theorem is an important ingredient in our main results.

Theorem 4.13
.

Suppose
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
is a forwards immersion. There are at most
10\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert+3\cdot\left\lvert V^{i}(\Gamma)\right\rvert
integers
n\geq 1
such that there is a right maximal
w
-path
f\in\osubwmax(\gamma,w)
with
|f|_{w}=n
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\Gamma
is connected. If
\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert=1
, then Lemma
2.6
tells us there can be at most
2+\left\lvert V^{i}(\Gamma)\right\rvert
such integers
n
: one integer for the maximal
w
-prefix of
e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)
, one integer for the maximal
w
-suffix of
e
and one integer for each vertex in
V^{i}(\Gamma)
from which a
w
-path may begin. So from now on we assume that
\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\geq 2
. Let
f:I\to\Gamma
be a right maximal submaximal
w
-path and let

f=e_{0}[-l:]*w_{1}*e_{1}*...*w_{n}*e_{n}[:m]

be its
\Gamma_{w}
-factorisation. If
f
is a loop, we may assume that
o(f),t(f)\in\bar{V}(\Gamma)
. Since
f
is a
w
-path, each subpath
e_{i}
and
w_{i}
has a well defined
w
-factorisation. So let:

\displaystyle\gamma\circ e_{0}[-l:]

\displaystyle=w_{e_{0}}^{\prime}*w^{q_{0}}*w_{e_{0}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\gamma\circ e_{i}

\displaystyle=w_{e_{i}}^{\prime}*w^{q_{i}}*w_{e_{i}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\gamma\circ e_{n}[:m]

\displaystyle=w_{e_{n}}^{\prime}*w^{q_{n}}*w_{e_{n}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\gamma\circ w_{i}

\displaystyle=w_{i}^{\prime}*w^{k_{i}}*w_{i}^{\prime\prime}

be the relevant
w
-factorisations. Denote by
V_{f}\subset V(\Gamma)
the set of vertices that fall into one of the following categories:

(1)

v=e_{i}(\left\lvert w_{e_{i}}^{\prime}\right\rvert)
for some
1\leq i<n
, or,

(2)

v=e_{0}(-l+\left\lvert w_{0}^{\prime}\right\rvert)
if
l\geq|w|
, or,

(3)

v=e_{n}(\left\lvert w_{e_{n}}^{\prime}\right\rvert)
if
q_{n}\geq 1
, or,

(4)

v=w_{i}(\left\lvert w_{i}^{\prime}*w^{j}\right\rvert)
for some
i\geq 1
and
1\leq j\leq k_{i}
, or,

(5)

v=w_{1}(\left\lvert w_{1}^{\prime}\right\rvert)
if
l\geq|w|
, or,

(6)

v=w_{i}(\left\lvert w_{i}^{\prime}\right\rvert)
for some
i\geq 2
.

By the definition of
\Gamma_{w}
-factorisations, each vertex of type (
1
) and (
3
) is contained in some element
e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)-\bar{E}_{w}(\Gamma)
. Moreover, these are independent of
f
by Lemma
2.6
. Hence there can be at most
\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)-\bar{E}_{w}(\Gamma)\right\rvert
many vertices
v\in\bigcup_{f}V_{f}
of type (
1
) or (
3
), where the union is over all right maximal submaximal
w
-paths. Since either
o(e_{0}[-l:])\in V^{i}(\Gamma)
, or
e_{0}[-l:]
is a left maximal
w
-path, there can be at most
\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)-\bar{E}_{w}(\Gamma)\right\rvert+\left\lvert V^{i}(\Gamma)\right\rvert
many vertices of type (
2
). By Lemma
4.11
, there are at most
2\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}_{w}(\Gamma)\right\rvert
vertices of type (
4
) in
\bigcup_{f}V_{f}
. Since right extensions of
w
-paths are uniquely defined for forwards immersions, we get a bound of
\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)-\bar{E}_{w}(\Gamma)\right\rvert
for the vertices of type (
5
) and (
6
).

So putting all of this together we get:

\left\lvert\bigcup_{f}V_{f}\right\rvert\leq 3\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert+\left\lvert V^{i}(\Gamma)\right\rvert.

To each vertex
v\in\bigcup_{f}V_{f}
, we may associate an integer
k_{v}
that is the
w
-length of the right maximal
w
-path starting at
v
with
w
-factorisation beginning with
w
.

Now let
g:I\to\Gamma
be a right maximal submaximal
w
-path and let
g=w^{\prime}*w^{k}*w^{\prime\prime}
be its
w
-factorisation. Then by definition of
V_{g}\subset\bigcup_{f}V_{f}
, either
g(\left\lvert w^{\prime}\right\rvert)\in V_{g}
,
g(\left\lvert w^{\prime}*w\right\rvert)\in V_{g}
,
g(\left\lvert w^{\prime}*w^{2}\right\rvert)\in V_{g}
or
\left\lvert g\right\rvert_{w}\leq 2
. Hence
|g|_{w}=k_{v}
,
|g|_{w}=k_{v}+1
or
|g|_{w}=k_{v}+2
for some
v\in\bigcup_{f}V_{f}
, or
|g|_{w}\leq 2
. So
k
can be one of at most
3\cdot\left(3\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert+\left\lvert V^{i}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\right)+2
distinct integers. Since we assumed that
\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\geq 2
, we obtain the desired upper bound.
∎

Remark 4.14
.

If we wanted to relax the assumptions of Theorem
4.13
to encompass general graph maps, then the number of integers
n
needed could be exponential in
\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert
.

Theorem 4.15
.

Suppose that
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
is a forwards immersion and let
(u,v)\in V(\Gamma)\times V(S^{1})
. There is a regular language of the form:

L=(a^{n_{1}}+...+a^{n_{k}})\cdot(a^{n})^{*}\subset(a)^{*}

such that, for any path
f:I\to\Omega_{\gamma,w}
where
t(f)=(u,v)
and
p_{\Gamma}\circ f
is a left submaximal
w
-path, we have
a^{|f|_{w}}\in L
. Furthermore, we have that:

k\leq 10\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert+3\cdot\left\lvert V^{i}(\Gamma)\right\rvert.

Proof.

The fact that the regular language is of the given form is by Lemma
4.5
. If there is an
f:I\to\Omega_{\gamma,w}
such that
t(f)=(u,v)
,
p_{\Gamma}\circ f
is a left submaximal
w
-path and
p_{\Gamma}\circ f
does not contain any vertex in
\bar{V}(\Gamma)
in its image, then
k=1
and
n=0
. So, assuming that no such path exists, the proof of the bound on the number of integers is identical to that of Theorem
4.13
.
∎

Remark 4.16
.

We have stated Theorems
4.13
and
4.15
for left submaximal
w
-paths, but the analogous results for right submaximal paths also hold with virtually the same proof if we replace
V^{i}(\Gamma)
with
V^{f}(\Gamma)
.

4.2.
w
-cycles

The following theorem was conjectured in
[
30
]
in connection with coherence of one-relator groups and became known as the
w
-cycles conjecture. It was then proved independently in
[
11
]
and
[
19
]
.

Theorem 4.17
.

Let
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
be an immersion and
\hat{w}:S^{1}\to\Delta
a primitive cycle. Then:

\beta_{0}(\core(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}S^{1}))\leq\beta_{1}(\Gamma).

If we want to allow
\hat{w}
to be imprimitive, then it follows that the map
\core(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}S^{1})\to\Delta
consists of at most
\beta_{1}(\Gamma)
many free homotopy classes of cycles. We here prove a result transverse to this, Theorem
4.20
from the introduction.

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
be a graph map and
w:I\to\Delta
a primitive loop. Let
f:S^{1}\to\Gamma
be a primitive cycle with
\deg(\gamma\circ f)\geq 12\cdot\beta_{1}(\Gamma)
. Then there is some
e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)
such that
f
traverses
e
and
\left\lvert e\right\rvert\geq 2\cdot\left\lvert w\right\rvert
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\Gamma
is connected and core. If
\Gamma
is a cycle then the result is clear, so suppose that it is not. By Lemma
3.6
,
\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\leq 3\cdot(\beta_{1}(\Gamma)-1)
. Let
g:I\to\Gamma
,
w:I\to\Delta
be loops such that
\hat{g}=f
and
\gamma\circ g=w^{\deg(f)}
. Then
\left\lvert g\right\rvert_{w}>4\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert
. Now we simply apply the pigeonhole principle to Lemma
4.12
.
∎

Theorem 4.19
.

Let
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
be a forwards immersion of graphs. There is a unique graph map
s_{\Gamma}:\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
where
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
is a disjoint union of at most
\beta_{1}(\Gamma)
circles such that, if
f:S^{1}\to\Gamma
is a cycle satisfying:

\frac{\deg(\gamma\circ f)}{\deg(f)}\geq 12\cdot\beta_{1}(\Gamma),

then
f
factors through
s_{\Gamma}
.

Proof.

Each such cycle factors through a primitive cycle
g:S^{1}\to\Gamma
such that
\deg(\gamma\circ g)\geq 12\cdot\beta_{1}(\Gamma)
. Hence, let
s_{\Gamma}:\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\to\Gamma

and

, there corresponds an element in

in the following way:

\sub(e,f)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\ap(p_{i},q_{i},r_{i})

.

5.4.
Bounding the number of cycles

Topological methods have been used for a long time to study fibre products of immersions of undirected graphs (see
[
26
,
23
,
27
,
5
,
16
,
21
]
). By the Hanna Neumann Theorem, if
\gamma
and
\lambda
are immersions of undirected graphs, then
\core(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)
has at most
\chi(\Gamma)\cdot\chi(\Lambda)
many non cyclic components. However, handling the cyclic components requires a different approach. Let us first consider the simplest case when
\Gamma,\Lambda,\Delta\cong S^{1}
. Suppose
\deg(\gamma)=n\cdot p
and
\deg(\lambda)=n\cdot q
for integers
n,p,q\geq 1
with
\gcd(p,q)=1
. Then
\Theta=\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
has precisely
n
components. So the number of components in general of the fibre product cannot depend on the topology of the graphs as
n
was arbitrary. However, if
S_{1},S_{2}\subset\Theta
are any two distinct components, then

[\gamma\circ p_{\Gamma}|_{S_{1}}]=[\gamma\circ p_{\Gamma}|_{S_{2}}].

Hence, all of the components in
\Theta
represent the same conjugacy class of subgroup in
\pi_{1}(\Delta)
. We record a slight extension of this observation with the following Lemma.

Lemma 5.8
.

If
\Gamma\cong\Lambda\cong S^{1}
, then the canonical map
\theta:\core(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)\to\Delta
is the disjoint union of at most
\gcd(\deg(\gamma),\deg(\lambda))
many copies of a single free homotopy class of map
S^{1}\to\Delta
.

This simpler case suggests that the better quantity to count is the number of distinct homotopy classes of maps (of connected graphs) that
\theta
decomposes into. Indeed this line of approach is suggested in
[
23
]
in the context of free groups. The aim of this section is to provide general bounds for this quantity. The main non-trivial step is to bound the number of cycles in
\Theta^{s}
. The key observation needed is the following: each element of
\bar{E}_{c}(\Theta^{s})
must support some lift in
\lift(p_{\Gamma}^{s},e)
for some
e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)
such that
p_{\Lambda}^{s}\circ\tilde{e}\in\olift(\lambda,\gamma\circ e)
.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)
, then

\left\lvert\{\tilde{e}\in\lift(p_{\Gamma}^{s},e)\mid p_{\Lambda}^{s}\circ\tilde{e}\in\olift(\lambda,\gamma\circ e)\}\right\rvert\leq 500\cdot C_{\Gamma}\cdot C_{\Lambda}\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Lambda)\right\rvert.

Proof.

If
\tilde{e}\in\lift(p^{s}_{\Gamma},e)
such that
p_{\Lambda}^{s}\circ\tilde{e}\in\olift(\lambda,\gamma\circ e)
, then

E(\tilde{e})\subset\bigcup_{f\in\bar{E}(\Lambda)}(E(\Theta^{s}_{\sub}(e,f))\cup E(\Theta^{s}_{\pref}(e,f))\cup E(\Theta^{s}_{\suff}(e,f))).

By Propositions
5.4
and
5.5
:

\left\lvert E(\Theta^{s}_{\sub}(e,f))\cup E(\Theta^{s}_{\pref}(e,f))\cup E(\Theta^{s}_{\suff}(e,f))\right\rvert\leq 500\cdot C_{\Gamma\penalty\ }\cdot C_{\Lambda}\cdot\left\lvert e\right\rvert,

and so:

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\tilde{e}}E(\tilde{e})

6.
Free groups

Now that we have proved all of our results in the context of graphs, we can discuss applications of these results to free groups. We begin by making the connection with free groups explicit. An
involutive graph

\Gamma
is a graph, together with an involution
{}^{-1}:E(\Gamma)\to E(\Gamma)
such that
o(e)=t(e^{-1})
and
e^{-1}\neq e
for all
e\in E(\Gamma)
. An involutive graph map
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
must also satisfy

\gamma(e^{-1})=\gamma(e)^{-1}

for all
e\in E(\Gamma)
.

A path
f:I\to\Gamma
is
reduced
if there is no subsequent pair of edges
e_{1},e_{2}\in E(I)
such that
f(e_{1})=f(e_{2})^{-1}
. The core of an involutive graph is instead defined as the union of all the reduced cycles. The relative core is defined similarly. The set of (endpoint preserving homotopy classes of) reduced paths forms a groupoid, the fundamental groupoid
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)
. If we choose a basepoint
x\in V(\Gamma)
, then the set of (basepoint preserving homotopy classes of) reduced paths actually form a group, the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,x)
. By Stallings construction
[
26
]
, there is a bijection:

\{\gamma:(\Gamma,x)\to(\Delta,y)\mid\gamma\text{ based immersion of relatively core involutive graphs}\}\to\{A<\pi_{1}(\Delta,y)\}

given by the
\pi_{1}
functor. And so, as elucidated in detail in
[
13
]
, results for involutive graphs correspond directly to results for subgroups of free groups.

Since the graphs we have worked with were not involutive graphs, we will instead consider a slight modification of the classic construction. So let
\Gamma
be a graph equipped with an involution on its edges
{}^{-1}:E(\Gamma)\to E(\Gamma)
and its vertices
{}^{-1}:V(\Gamma)\to V(\Gamma)
such that

(1)

for every
e\in E(\Gamma)
, we have
o(e)=t(e^{-1})^{-1}
,

(2)

for all
e\in E(\Gamma)
,
(e^{-1})^{-1}=e
and
e^{-1}\neq e
,

(3)

for every
v\in V(\Gamma)
,
(v^{-1})^{-1}=v
.

We will call this a
fully involutive graph
. Note that by
1
, the involution
-1
can be extended to the set of paths: if
f:I\to\Gamma
is a path, then
f^{-1}
is also a path and
o(f)=t(f^{-1})
.

Let
\Gamma
be a fully involutive graph. We may also define reduced paths in
\Gamma
in the exact same way as we did for involutive graphs. However, the fundamental groupoid may not always be defined. If
x\in V(\Gamma)
such that
x^{-1}=x
, then by
1
, the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,x)
is well defined.

There is a standard formula relating the rank of the fundamental group of an involutive graph to its combinatorial data. We record this formula also for fully involutive graphs.

Lemma 6.1

Let

\rk(\Gamma,x)=\frac{\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)-\bar{E}_{c}(\Gamma)\right\rvert}{2}-\left\lvert\bar{V}(\Gamma)\right\rvert+1.

The quotient map
\Gamma\to\Gamma^{\prime}
which identifies all

with

We say a fully involutive graph

is obtained from a fully involutive graph

\displaystyle=o(e_{1}),

\displaystyle t(e_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=v^{\prime},

\displaystyle o(e_{1}^{\prime-1})

\displaystyle t(e_{1}^{\prime-1})

\displaystyle o(e_{2}^{\prime})

Theorem 7.1

Let

Proof.

We may find shortest loops

a pair of integers
(i,j)
in the following way: if

and

\eta_{\Lambda}:V(\Lambda)\to\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
for
\Lambda

\left\lvert p_{1}\circ\eta_{\Gamma}(V^{i}(\Gamma))\right\rvert\leq 2\cdot k
and
\left\lvert p_{1}\circ\eta_{\Lambda}(V^{i}(\Lambda))\right\rvert\leq 2\cdot k

is projection to the first factor. Both the sets

and

may also be computed in
O(n)
time.

For each pair

,

(a,c)\in\eta_{\Gamma}(V^{i}(\Gamma))
and
(b,c)\in\eta_{\Lambda}(V^{i}(\Lambda))

time for each choice of

. If there is no such pair, then

and
\core(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)
cannot contain an accepting path.

Now let
a
,

be a pair of such integers. With some minor modifications, we now essentially follow the proof of Theorem 2 in
[
2
]
. Let
\gamma\circ w_{\Gamma}=w^{m}
and

\displaystyle=(t\cdot q+r-b,c).

Thus, by iterating over all pairs

g:S^{1}\to S^{1}
such that
\gamma\circ f=\hat{w}\circ g
and
\deg(g)\geq 12\cdot\beta_{1}(\Gamma)
. By Lemma
4.18
, there is some element
e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)
such that
e
is a
w
-path and
\left\lvert e\right\rvert\geq 2\cdot\left\lvert w\right\rvert
. By Theorem
2.1
, it follows that
\per(e)=\left\lvert w\right\rvert
. So in order to find the cycles in
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
we may do the following. By Theorem
2.8
we may compute
\per(e)
for each
e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)
in
O(\left\lvert E(\Gamma)\right\rvert)
time. Then let
w_{e}:I\to e
denote a subpath of
e
of length exactly
\per(e)
. By Lemma
4.12
, we may decide in
O\left(\left\lvert E(\Gamma)\right\rvert+2\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert w_{e}\right\rvert\right)
time if this extends to a
w_{e}
-cycle. Since
\sum_{e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)}\left\lvert w_{e}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert E(\Gamma)\right\rvert
, we may do this for all
e\in\bar{E}(\Gamma)
in
O\left(\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert E(\Gamma)\right\rvert\right)
time. Moreover, we may also decide at the same time if the projections of the computed cycles to
\Delta
have degree greater than or equal to
12\cdot\beta_{1}(\Gamma)
. Thus, we may decide if they are cycles in
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
. Finally, by Proposition
2.9
, we may decide in
O\left(\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert^{2}\cdot\left\lvert E(\Gamma)\right\rvert\right)
time if any of the computed cycles are the same. Hence overall, the algorithm will require
O\left(\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert^{2}\cdot\left\lvert E(\Gamma)\right\rvert\right)
time to compute
s_{\Gamma}:\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
. Combined with Lemma
3.6
we obtain the result.
∎

The subgraph
s(\mathbb{S})\subset\Theta
in general has more than a linear number of states and transitions, providing an obstacle to a linear solution to the DFA non-empty intersection problem. Therefore, in order to get around this, we may like to learn something about the graph
s(\mathbb{S})
without actually computing it. We show that we may answer any question about connectivity of
s(\mathbb{S})
efficiently.

Proposition 7.3
.

There is an integer

such that there is a

g
lifts to

(2)

,

(3)

.

Proof.

Without loss we may assume that
\Gamma
and

are core graphs. By Theorem
7.2
, we may compute the graphs
s_{\Gamma}:\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
and

in

time. We set

\displaystyle V^{i}(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=\cup_{e^{\prime}\in E(s_{\Gamma}^{-1}(e))}o(e^{\prime}),

\displaystyle V^{i}(\mathbb{S}_{\Lambda})

\displaystyle V^{f}(\mathbb{S}_{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=s_{\Lambda}^{-1}(y).

Now by construction, the path

\mathbb{S}=\core(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\times_{\Delta}\mathbb{S}_{\Lambda})

and

. We may decide which pairs of components of
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
and

factor through the same cycle in

time using the algorithms from Theorem
2.8
and Proposition
2.9
. By Theorem
4.15
, if

and

are both

-cycles for some primitive loop

, there exist at most
10\cdot\left(\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Lambda)\right\rvert\right)\in O(m)
many segments of length

that cover

and

. Now the problem may be solved with the required complexity bounds by Theorem

∎

Proposition 7.4
.

7.3.
Regular languages

Let
\Sigma
be a finite set. A
finite state automaton over
\Sigma
is a graph
\Gamma
together with an initial vertex
V^{i}(\Gamma)=\{x\}
, a collection of final vertices
V^{f}(\Gamma)
and a labelling of its edges by elements of
\Sigma
. If
\Delta
is a graph with a single vertex and an edge for each element in
\Sigma
, then labellings of edges of
\Gamma
by elements of
\Sigma
are in a one-to-one correspondence with graph maps
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
. Thus, for our purposes, a finite state automaton will be a quadruple
(\Gamma,\gamma,x,V^{f})
, where
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
is a graph map with
\Delta
a connected graph with a single vertex. A
deterministic finite state automaton
(or
DFA
) is an automaton
(\Gamma,\gamma,x,V^{f})
such that
\gamma
is a forwards immersion. The
language
of the automaton
(\Gamma,\gamma,x,V^{f})
is the following collection of paths:

L(\Gamma)=\{\gamma\circ g\mid g:I\to\Gamma\text{ an accepting path}\}.

It is a classical result of Rabin-Scott
[
24
]
that, given two automata
(\Gamma,\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta,x,V^{f}(\Gamma))
,
(\Lambda,\lambda:\Lambda\to\Delta,y,V^{f}(\Lambda))
, we have:

L(\Gamma)\cap L(\Lambda)=L(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda).

If
\gamma
and
\lambda
are finite sheeted covers, then
\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
is a core graph and
V(\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)=V(\Gamma)\cdot V(\Lambda)
. So the optimal complexity bound for computing the automaton representing
L(\Gamma)\cap L(\Lambda)
is
O(n^{2})
.

On the other hand, it is an open problem if deciding if
L(\Gamma)\cap L(\Lambda)\neq\emptyset
can be done in subquadratic time. See
[
14
,
15
,
28
,
2
,
3
]
for results on this problem and its variations. Currently, the only known positive result for two DFAs is a linear time solution for automata over a unary alphabet, or rather, automata
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
where
\Delta
has only a single edge. This constraint on the edges of
\Delta
immediately puts constraints on the topology of
\Gamma
. Indeed, there are at most four distinct forms that
\Gamma
can take: a point, a segment, a circle or a circle with a segment attached. Thus, the
m
parameter is forced to be bounded above by
4
. Generalising the unary case, we show that for any fixed
m
, the DFA-NEI problem can be solved in linear time.

Theorem 7.5
.

There is an integer
q>0
such that there is a
O(m^{q}\cdot n)
time algorithm that, given as input two deterministic finite state automata
\gamma:\Gamma\to\Delta
and
\lambda:\Lambda\to\Delta
, decides if
L(\Gamma)\cap L(\Lambda)\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Let us first fix some notation. As
\Gamma
and
\Lambda
are automata, they both only have one initial vertex. So let
V^{i}(\Gamma)=\{x\}
and
V^{i}(\Lambda)=\{y\}
and denote by
V^{\prime}=(x,y)\cup\bar{V}(\Gamma)\times\bar{V}(\Lambda)
. Let
C
be the constant from Theorem
5.7
such that

\left\lvert E(\Theta^{s})\right\rvert\leq C\cdot m^{2}\cdot n.

Let
s_{\Gamma}:\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
and
s_{\Lambda}:\mathbb{S}_{\Lambda}\to\Lambda
be the graphs from Theorem
4.19
. By Theorem
7.2
we may assume that we have precomputed these graphs. We may also assume that we have computed the subsets
V_{\mathbb{S}}
and
E_{\mathbb{S}}
from Proposition
7.4
along with the partitions
V_{\mathbb{S}}=V_{1}\sqcup...\sqcup V_{c}
and
E_{\mathbb{S}}=E_{1}\sqcup...\sqcup E_{c}
.

Denote by
A\subset L(\Theta)
the set of paths
g:I\to\Theta
such that
t(g)\in V^{f}(\Theta^{s})
and by
B\subset L(\Theta)
the set of paths
g:I\to\Theta
such that
t(g)\in V^{f}(s(\mathbb{S}))
. Then, by definition of
\Theta^{s}
, we have
L(\Theta)=A\cup B
. Thus, determining if
L(\Theta)\neq\emptyset
is equivalent to determining if
A\neq\emptyset
or
B\neq\emptyset
.

The logical structure of the proof is as follows. Let
\Theta^{\circ}\subset\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
be the connected component containing the vertex
(x,y)
. We will first compute a graph
\Theta_{k}\subset\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
such that
\Theta_{k}
contains the subgraph
\Theta^{s}\cap\Theta^{\circ}
and satisfies
\left\lvert E(\Theta_{k})\right\rvert\in O(m^{q}\cdot n)
for some
q\geq 0
. It will also be shown that
V(\Theta_{k})\cap(V^{f}(\Gamma)\times V^{f}(\Lambda))\neq\emptyset
if and only if
A\neq\emptyset
. Thus we will be able to decide if
A\neq\emptyset
in
O(m^{q}\cdot n)
time for some
q\geq 0
. Then, we will show that using this graph and Theorem
7.1
we may also decide if
B\neq\emptyset
in
O(m^{q}\cdot n)
time for some
q\geq 0
.

Before showing how to compute
\Theta_{k}
we will need a useful procedure. We will call the following algorithm the
bounded forced path procedure
. Given as input a pair
(e,f)\in E(\Gamma)\times E(\Lambda)
with
\gamma(e)=\lambda(f)
and an integer
l
, do the following. Denote by
g:I\to\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
the path traversing only
(e,f)
. If
t(e,f)\in\bar{V}(\Gamma)\times\bar{V}(\Lambda)
or
l=1
, then we terminate and return the path
g
. If not, then
l\geq 2
and
\odeg(e)=1
or
\odeg(f)=1
. Since
\gamma
and
\lambda
are forwards immersions, there is at most one element
(e^{\prime},f^{\prime})\in\fstar((t(e),t(f)),\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)
. If no such element exists, then we also terminate and return the path
g
. If instead there is such a pair
(e^{\prime},f^{\prime})
, then let
g^{\prime}:I\to\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
be the output of the bounded forced path procedure with input
(e^{\prime},f^{\prime})
and
l-1
. Return the path
g*g^{\prime}
.

The following complexity bound follows from the fact that for all
u,v\in V(\Gamma)\times V(\Lambda)
, the set
\fstar((u,v),\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)
can be computed in
O(m)
time.

Lemma 7.6
.

The bounded forced path procedure runs in
O(m\cdot l)
time.

We are now ready to compute the graph
\Theta_{k}
as promised. We will in fact do more than this, we will compute the following finite sequences:

(1)

graphs
\Theta_{0},\Theta_{1},...,\Theta_{k}
with
\Theta_{i}\subset\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda
for all
i\geq 0

(2)

sets of edges
\ndir_{0},\ndir_{1},...,\ndir_{k}
,
\odir_{0},\odir_{1},...,\odir_{k}
with

\ndir_{i},\odir_{i}\subset\cup_{v\in V^{\prime}}\fstar(v,\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda),

such that
\ndir_{i}\cap\odir_{i}=\emptyset
and
\left\lvert\odir_{i}\right\rvert<\left\lvert\odir_{i+1}\right\rvert
for all
i\geq 0
.

By definition, we have
k\leq\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Gamma)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\bar{E}(\Lambda)\right\rvert
and so
k\in O(m^{2})
.

We begin the computation by letting
\text{ndir}_{0}=\fstar((x,y),\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)
,
\text{odir}_{0}=\emptyset
and
\Theta_{0}=\emptyset
. The set
\ndir_{i}
will contain the new directions that we need to explore at step
i+1
, meanwhile, the set
\odir_{i}
will contain the old directions that we have already explored before step
i+1
. So suppose now that we have computed
\Theta_{i-1}
,
\ndir_{i-1}
and
\odir_{i-1}
. If
\ndir_{i-1}=\emptyset
, then we are done and
k=i-1
. If not, choose some element
(e,f)\in\ndir_{i-1}
. We have precomputed
G
, so we first check if

(e,f)\in d(E(G))\subset\cup_{v\in V^{\prime}}\fstar(v,\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda).

We now have two cases to consider.

Case 1:

(e,f)\in E_{\mathbb{S}}
.

If
(e,f)\in E_{\mathbb{S}}
, then
(e,f)\notin E(\Theta^{s})
by definition. Now let
j
be the integer such that
(e,f)\in E_{j}
. By Proposition
7.4
, the vertices
V_{j}
are precisely the vertices in
\bar{V}(\Gamma)\times\bar{V}(\Lambda)
that can be reached by a path starting with
(e,f)
in
s(\mathbb{S})
. So let:

\displaystyle\odir_{i}

\displaystyle=\odir_{i-1}\cup(e,f)

\displaystyle\ndir_{i}

\displaystyle=(\ndir_{i-1}-(e,f))\cup\bigcup_{v\in V_{j}}(\fstar(v,\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)-(\fstar(v,\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)\cap\odir_{i}))

\displaystyle\Theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\Theta_{i-1}.

Case 2:

(e,f)\notin E_{\mathbb{S}}
.

Let
g
be the output of the bounded forced path procedure with input the pair
(e,f)
and the integer
C\cdot m^{2}\cdot n
. By Lemma
7.6
this may be done in
O(m^{3}\cdot n)
number of steps. Then we are left with two subcases.

Subcase 2.1:

t(g)\in\bar{V}(\Gamma)\times\bar{V}(\Lambda)
.

If this is the case, then we set:

\displaystyle\odir_{i}

\displaystyle=\odir_{i-1}\cup(e,f)

\displaystyle\ndir_{i}

\displaystyle=(\ndir_{i-1}-(e,f))\cup(\fstar(t(g),\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)-(\fstar(t(g),\Gamma\times_{\Delta}\Lambda)\cap\odir_{i}))

\displaystyle\Theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\Theta_{i-1}\cup g.

Subcase 2.2:

t(g)\notin\bar{V}(\Gamma)\times\bar{V}(\Lambda)
.

Then let
j
be the largest index such that
g(j)\in V^{f}(\Gamma)\times V^{f}(\Lambda)
. If no such integer exists, then set
j=0
. We update our sets as follows:

\displaystyle\odir_{i}

\displaystyle=\odir_{i-1}\cup(e,f)

\displaystyle\ndir_{i}

\displaystyle=\ndir_{i-1}-(e,f)

\displaystyle\Theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\Theta_{i-1}\cup g[:j].

We have the following properties of
\Theta_{k}
and
\odir_{k}
that follow from the construction:

(1)

\Theta^{s}\cap\Theta^{\circ}\subset\Theta_{k}
,

(2)

E(s(\mathbb{S}))\cap E(\Theta_{k})=\emptyset
,

(3)

for each
v\in\bar{V}(\Gamma)\times\bar{V}(\Lambda)
, there exists an accepting path
g:I\to\Theta
traversing
v
if and only if
v\in\bigcup_{(e,f)\in\odir_{k}}o(e,f)
.

By
1
, we have
(V^{f}(\Gamma)\times V^{f}(\Lambda))\cap V(\Theta_{k})\neq\emptyset
if and only if
A\neq\emptyset
. Hence, we have shown that may decide if
A\neq\emptyset
in
O(m^{q}\cdot n)
time for some
q\geq 0
. It remains to show that we can determine if
B\neq\emptyset
in
O(m^{q}\cdot n)
time for some
q\geq 0
.

Suppose that
B\neq\emptyset
. Then there is a pair of paths
g_{1},g_{2}:I\to\Theta
such that
o(g_{1})=(x,y)
,
t(g_{1})=o(g_{2})\in\bar{V}(\Gamma)\times\bar{V}(\Lambda)
,
t(g_{2})\in V^{f}(\Theta)
and
g_{2}
lifts to
s:\mathbb{S}\to\Theta
. By construction of
3
, it suffices to determine if there is a path
g:I\to\Theta
such that
o(g)\in\bigcup_{(e,f)\in\odir_{k}}o(e,f)
,
t(g)\in V^{f}(\Theta)
and
g
lifts to
s:\mathbb{S}\to\Theta
. In order to determine if such a path exists, we essentially repeat the algorithm from Proposition
7.3
. Let
(u,v)\in\bigcup_{(e,f)\in\odir_{k}}o(e,f)
and
S_{\Gamma}\subset\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
,
S_{\Lambda}\subset\mathbb{S}_{\Lambda}
a pair of connected components. Set

\displaystyle V^{i}(S_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=s_{\Gamma}^{-1}(u)\cap V(S_{\Gamma}),

\displaystyle V^{i}(S_{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=s_{\Lambda}^{-1}(v)\cap V(S_{\Lambda}),

\displaystyle V^{f}(S_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=s_{\Gamma}^{-1}(V^{f}(\Gamma))\cap V(S_{\Gamma}),

\displaystyle V^{f}(S_{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=s_{\Lambda}^{-1}(V^{f}(\Lambda))\cap V(S_{\Lambda}).

Then
\core(S_{\Gamma}\times_{\Delta}S_{\Lambda})
has an accepting path if and only if there is a path

such that

,

and

lifts to

. Thus, by iterating over all such vertices and pairs of components, we may decide if

. Now, just as in the proof of Proposition

, we may use Theorem

to obtain the required bounds.
∎
