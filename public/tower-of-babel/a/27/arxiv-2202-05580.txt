Central limit theorems for generalized descents and generalized inversions in finite root systems
Kathrin Meier, Christian Stump

Abstract
. We consider generalized inversions and descents in finite Weyl groups. We establish Coxeter-theoretic properties of indicator random variables of positive roots such as the covariance of two such indicator random variables. We then compute the variances of generalized inversions and descents in classical types. We finally use the dependency graph method to prove central limit theorems for general antichains in root posets and in particular for generalized descents, and then for generalized inversions.

Introduction

Permutation statistics have been studied in great detail.
Two of the most prominent examples are the following:
An
inversion
of a permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
is a pair
(ij)
with
1\leq i<j\leq n
and
\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)
, and a

is an inversion of distance
1
, this is, an inversion
(ij)

The

and the

on permutations then count inversions and descents, respectively,

\operatorname{inv}(\sigma)=\big|\{1\leq i<j\leq n\mid\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)\}\big|,\quad\operatorname{des}(\sigma)=\big|\{1\leq i\leq n-1\mid\sigma(i)>\sigma(i+1)\}\big|.

The associated random variables
{\mathcal{X}}_{\operatorname{inv}}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{\operatorname{des}}
are obtained by asking how many inversions or, respectively, descents does a permutation drawn uniformly at random from
\mathcal{S}_{n}
have.
Both of these random variables are well-studied, their mean values and variances are known and both satisfy central limit theorems, see for example
[
1
, Examples 5.3 & 5.5]
.

Generalizations of these classical results towards various directions have been considered.
The aim of this paper is to combine the follwoing two considerations.

One may define a

\displaystyle\operatorname{des}_{d}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\big|\{1\leq i\leq n-d\mid\sigma(i)>\sigma(i+d)\}\big|.

{\mathcal{X}}_{\operatorname{inv}_{d}}
have been computed in
[
3

for

[

.
Both papers then deduce central limit theorems for

-inversions

We study in this paper a natural way to jointly generalize these two contexts.
The defined notions of

We generalize the above results by exhibiting structural interactions between finite Coxeter groups and probabilistic notions.
Most notably, we show that

•

Using these interactions, we obtain central limit theorems in this generalized context.
Moreover, we obtain rates of convergence for certain growth behavior of the parameter

which generalize the above cases for the symmetric group.

2.2.
Main results

For
\beta\in\Phi^{+}
, one considers the Bernoulli random variable on elements chosen uniformly at random in
W
indicating whether or not the positive root
\beta
is an inversion,

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}(w)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }w(\beta)\in\Phi^{-}\ ,\\
0&\text{ if }w(\beta)\in\Phi^{+}\ .\end{cases}

In other words,
{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}
is the Bernoulli random variable given by

(2.2)

{\mathbb{P}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}=1]}=\frac{\big|\{w\in W\mid w(\beta)\in\Phi^{-}\}\big|}{|W|}\,,\qquad{\mathbb{P}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}=0]}=\frac{\big|\{w\in W\mid w(\beta)\in\Phi^{+}\}\big|}{|W|}\,.

Moreover, for a subset
\Psi\subseteq\Phi^{+}
we set
{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi}=\sum_{\beta\in\Psi}{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}
.

The main results of this paper concern central limits of this random variable for certain natural subsets of positive roots.
Before presenting these, we observe that the cardinality of
\Psi
must necessarily go to infinity for
{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi}
to have a central limit.
But the following example shows that this is not the only obstruction.

Example 2.3
.

Let
\Phi
be the root system of type
A_{n-1}
and consider

\Psi^{(n)}=\{e_{i}-e_{1}\mid 1<i\leq n\}\subseteq\Phi^{+}

of increasing cardinality.

for
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
.
This implies that
{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}
is uniformly distributed for all
n
and thus does not have a central limit.

A sequence
{\mathcal{X}}^{(n)}
of random variables is
asymptotically normal
if its normalized version converges in distribution towards the standard Gaussian distribution.
In symbols,

\frac{{\mathcal{X}}^{(n)}-{\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}^{(n)}]}}{{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}^{(n)}]}^{1/2}}\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

The first main theorem considers a further generalization of
d
-descents.
An
antichain
in the root poset is a set of positive roots that are pairwise incomparable.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\{\Phi^{(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of finite crystallographic root systems and let
\{\Psi^{(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of antichains in its root poset of
\Phi^{(n)}
of increasing cardinality,
|\Psi^{(n)}|\penalty\ \longrightarrow\penalty\ \infty
.
The corresponding random variable
{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}
is then asymptotically normal.
The rate of convergence in distribution is bounded by
|\Psi^{(n)}|^{-1/2}
.

Since the set
\Phi_{\operatorname{des}}^{(d)}
is an antichain for any
d
, we obtain a central limit theorem for
d
-descents as a direct consequence.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
\{\Phi^{(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of finite crystallographic root systems and let
\{d_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of integers such that
|\Phi_{\operatorname{des}}^{(d_{n})}|\longrightarrow\infty
.
The corresponding random variable
{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{des}}^{(d_{n})}}
is then asymptotically normal with rate of convergence in distribution being bounded by
|\Phi_{\operatorname{des}}^{(d_{n})}|^{-1/2}
.

Remark 2.8
.

The proof of Theorem
2.6
generalizes verbatim to any subset of roots that may be send to simple roots by an element of

.
If we allow any such subset of roots in a (not necessarily crystallographic) root system, we obtain asymptotic normality if

as given in

, Theorem 6.2]
.

.

Let
\{\Phi^{(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}

be a sequence of integers such that

.
The corresponding random variable

This generalizes the known rates for the symmetric group discussed at the end of Section

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
\Phi^{(n)}
be of rank

and let
\{\Phi_{i}\}
denote its irreducible components.
Set

•

to be the rank of the union of those components

for which
\operatorname{rk}(\Phi_{i})<d_{n}
,

•

r_{B}
to be the rank of the union of those components
\Phi_{i}
for which
d_{n}\leq\operatorname{rk}(\Phi_{i})\leq d_{n}^{2}
, and

•

r_{C}
to be the rank of the union of those components
\Phi_{i}
for which
d_{n}^{2}<\operatorname{rk}(\Phi_{i})
,

(and we observe that
r_{n}=r_{A}+r_{B}+r_{C}
).
We then have the following bounds on the rates of convergence:

•

if
r_{A}
grows linearly in
r_{n}
, we have the bound
r_{n}^{-1/2}
,

•

if
r_{B}
grows linearly in

and

grows faster than

, we have the bound
r_{n}\cdot d_{n}^{-3/2}
,

•

grows slower than

, we have the bound
r_{n}^{-1/2}\cdot d_{n}^{3/2}
.

In particular, this corollary shows that if
\Phi^{(n)}
is irreducible (of then necessarily classical type), we obtain rates of convergence in the same situations as for the symmetric group.

Based on a well-known Coxeter-theoretic result, the asymptotic normality in Theorem

We provide a more detailed alternative proof using the dependency graph method which also provides the proposed rate of convergence.

In addition, we provide explicit formulas for their variances in the classical types in Theorems

, and

.

Unfortunately, we do not have a uniform consideration for

Based on provided explicit formulas for their variances in the classical types in Theorems

,

,

, and

The situation of

-inversions could be considered as a special case of

in the root poset,
i.e.

such that

.
We neither found an example of a sequence of order ideals of increasing size that does not have a central limit, nor do we have any particular reason to expect them to always have a central limit.
We are therefore not aware of any natural subset generalizing

We propose this as an open question.

Do sequences of order ideals of increasing cardinality always have a central limit?

3.
Random inversions for finite root systems

The aim of this section is to understand how random variables
\{{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}\mid\beta\in\Phi^{+}\}
interact.
The two main result are Theorems
3.1
and
3.4
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For two positive roots
\beta,\gamma\in\Phi^{+}
, we have

\operatorname{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{\beta},{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma})=\pm\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2\cdot\operatorname{ord}(\beta,\gamma)}\right),

with the sign being positive if
\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle\geq 0
and negative if
\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle\leq 0
.
In particular,
{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}
are independent if and only if
\beta
and
\gamma
are orthogonal.

Remark 3.2
.

One may rephrase this statement into an expression avoiding the case distinction by casting it in terms of the angle between
\beta
and

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\Psi,\Psi^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi^{+}
.
If
\Psi
and
\Psi^{\prime}
are orthogonal, i.e.,
\operatorname{ord}(\beta,\gamma)=2
for all
\beta\in\Psi
and
\gamma\in\Psi^{\prime}
, then the sets
\{{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}\}_{\beta\in\Psi}
and
\{{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\Psi^{\prime}}
of random variables are independent.

The proofs of the two theorems are very similar.
We start with proving Theorem
3.1
by partitioning the Weyl group
W
depending on the behaviour on the positive roots
\beta,\gamma\in\Phi^{+}
.
For given signs
\varepsilon,\delta\in\{+,-\}
, we define the set

W^{(\varepsilon\delta)}(\beta,\gamma)=\big\{w\in W\mid\varepsilon\cdot w(\beta)\in\Phi^{+},\delta\cdot w(\gamma)\in\Phi^{+}\big\}\subseteq W

as those elements in the Weyl group that sends the given roots
\beta
and
\gamma
to positive or, respectively, negative roots depending on the given signs
\varepsilon,\delta
.
The core of the proof is the following relationship between the cardinalities of these four sets.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\beta,\gamma\in\Phi^{+}
.
Then

\displaystyle\big|W^{(++)}(\beta,\gamma)\big|

\displaystyle=\big|W^{(--)}(\beta,\gamma)\big|,

\displaystyle\big|W^{(+-)}(\beta,\gamma)\big|

\displaystyle=\big|W^{(-+)}(\beta,\gamma)\big|.

If moreover
\beta\neq\gamma
, then

\displaystyle\big|W^{(++)}(\beta,\gamma)\big|

\displaystyle=\big(\operatorname{ord}(\beta,\gamma)-1\big)\cdot\big|W^{(+-)}(\beta,\gamma)\big|\text{ if }\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle\geq 0,

\displaystyle\big|W^{(+-)}(\beta,\gamma)\big|

\displaystyle=\big(\operatorname{ord}(\beta,\gamma)-1\big)\cdot\big|W^{(++)}(\beta,\gamma)\big|\text{ if }\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle\leq 0.

In particular, all four sets have equal cardinality if and only if
\beta
and
\gamma
are orthogonal.

We first show that it is enough to restrict attention to the two-dimensional subspace
\operatorname{span}\{\beta,\gamma\}
of
V
and the parabolic subgroup of
W
acting on this subspace.
One considers, for a set
\Gamma\subseteq\Delta
of simple roots, the
parabolic subgroup

W_{\Gamma}=\langle s_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\Gamma\rangle\leq W

with corresponding root system
\Phi^{+}_{\Gamma}=\Phi^{+}\cap\,\operatorname{span}(\Gamma)
where the latter is the linear subspace of
V
generated by
\Gamma
.
It is well-known that the parabolic subgroup
W_{\Gamma}
comes with a
parabolic quotient

W^{\Gamma}
given by

W^{\Gamma}=\{w\in W\mid w(\alpha)\in\Phi^{+}\text{ for all }\alpha\in\Gamma\}.

Every element
w\in W
then has a unique decomposition
w=w^{\Gamma}\cdot w_{\Gamma}
with
w^{\Gamma}\in W^{\Gamma}
and
w_{\Gamma}\in W_{\Gamma}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\Delta
and let
w\in W
with parabolic decomposition
w=w^{\Gamma}\cdot w_{\Gamma}
.
For any
\beta\in\Phi^{+}_{\Gamma}
, it then holds that

w(\beta)\in\Phi^{+}\Longleftrightarrow w_{\Gamma}(\beta)\in\Phi^{+}.

Proof.

Since every element in
\Phi^{+}_{\Gamma}
is a non-negative linear combination of elements in
\Gamma
, the definition of the parabolic quotient immediately implies that
w^{\Gamma}(\Phi^{+}_{\Gamma})\subseteq\Phi^{+}
.
Thus, if
w_{\Gamma}(\beta)\in\Phi^{+}
then it is also in
\Phi^{+}_{\Gamma}
and therefore
w(\beta)=w^{\Gamma}(w_{\Gamma}(\beta))
is also in
\Phi^{+}
.
If on the other hand,
w_{\Gamma}(\beta)\notin\Phi^{+}
, then
w_{\Gamma}(\beta)\in-\Phi^{+}_{\Gamma}
.
Applying the previous argument to
-w_{\Gamma}(\beta)
then shows that
w(\beta)\in-\Phi^{+}=\Phi^{-}
.
∎

Looking at the definition of the random variable
{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}
in (
2.2
), this lemma shows that
{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}
only depends on the smallest parabolic root subsystem containing
\beta
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\Delta
with parabolic subgroup
W_{\Gamma}
and let
\beta\in\Phi^{+}_{\Gamma}
.
The random variable
{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}
is the same when considered on
W
and when considered on
W_{\Gamma}
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}
is a Bernoulli random variable, we check that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}=1]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big|\{w\in W\mid w(\beta)\in\Phi^{-}\}\big|}{|W|}=\frac{|W^{\Gamma}|\!\cdot\!\big|\{w\in W_{\Gamma}\mid w(\beta)\in\Phi^{-}\}\big|}{|W^{\Gamma}|\cdot|W_{\Gamma}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big|\{w\in W_{\Gamma}\mid w(\beta)\in\Phi^{-}\}\big|}{|W_{\Gamma}|}\qed

Proof of Proposition
3.5
.

The first two properties are implied by the observation that the map
w\mapsto w_{\circ}\cdot w
is a bijection on
W
interchanging inversions and non-inversions,

\operatorname{Inv}(w_{\circ}\cdot w)=\Phi^{+}\setminus\operatorname{Inv}(w)\,.

Let now be
\operatorname{span}\{\beta,\gamma\}
the linear subspace of
V
spanned by the two positive roots
\beta,\gamma\in\Phi^{+}
.
Then it is classical that there exists an element
w\in W
and simple roots
\Gamma=\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}\subseteq\Delta
such that

w\big(\operatorname{span}\{\beta,\gamma\}\big)=\operatorname{span}(\Gamma).

So in particular,
w(\beta),w(\gamma)\in\Phi^{+}_{\Gamma}
.
Since we are only interested in the cardinalities of the four sets and multiplying by
w
is a bijection on
W
, we may assume without loss of generality that
w=e
and
\beta,\gamma\in\Phi^{+}_{\Gamma}
.
It then holds by Lemma
3.6
that

W^{(\varepsilon,\delta)}(\beta,\gamma)=W^{\Gamma}\times W^{(\varepsilon,\delta)}_{\Gamma}(\beta,\gamma).

This shows that one only needs to check the properties

\displaystyle\big|W^{(++)}(\beta,\gamma)\big|

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{ord}{(s_{\beta}}{s_{\gamma})}-1)\cdot\big|W^{(+-)}(\beta,\gamma)\big|\text{ if }\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle\geq 0,

\displaystyle\big|W^{(+-)}(\beta,\gamma)\big|

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{ord}{(s_{\beta}}{s_{\gamma})}-1)\cdot\big|W^{(++)}(\beta,\gamma)\big|\text{ if }\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle\leq 0

for the dihedral types
A_{1}\times A_{1},A_{2},B_{2},C_{2},G_{2}
to conclude the proposition.
This check is an easy exercise, we consider the case of type
G_{2}
in the following example, the others are similar.
∎

Example 3.8
.

Consider the Weyl group

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\operatorname{Inv}(e)&=&\{\}&&\operatorname{Inv}(ststst)&=&\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\beta_{4},\beta_{5},\beta_{6}\}\\

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We calculate

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{\beta},{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma})\

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}\cdot{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}]}-{\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}]}\cdot{\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}]}={\mathbb{P}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}={\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}=1]}-\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{|W^{(--)}(\beta,\gamma)|}{|W|}-\frac{1}{4}\,.

By the first two equalities in Proposition
3.5
, this may be rewritten to

\operatorname{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{\beta},{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma})=\frac{|W^{(--)}(\beta,\gamma)|}{2\cdot\big(|W^{(--)}(\beta,\gamma)|+|W^{(+-)}(\beta,\gamma)|\big)}-\frac{1}{4}

If
\beta=\gamma
, we have

and obtain
\operatorname{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{\beta},{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma})=\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}=\tfrac{1}{4}
.

Let now
\beta\neq\gamma
and assume
\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle\leq 0
.
The forth equality in Proposition

then gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{\beta},{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\frac{|W^{(--)}(\beta,\gamma)|}{2\cdot(|W^{(--)}(\beta,\gamma)|+(\operatorname{ord}(\beta,\gamma)-1)\cdot|W^{(--)}(\beta,\gamma)|)}-\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\cdot\operatorname{ord}(\beta,\gamma)}-\frac{1}{4}.

The case of
\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle\geq 0
is analogous.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Let
\Psi,\Psi^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi^{+}
be orthogonal sets of roots as given in the assumption.
As in the proof of Corollary
3.7
, there exists an element
w\in W
and simple roots
\Gamma\subseteq\Delta
such that

w\big(\operatorname{span}(\Psi)\big)=\operatorname{span}(\Gamma)\,.

Since the inner product on
V
is
W
-invariant, we moreover have that
w(\Psi^{\prime})
is contained in the orthogonal complement of
\operatorname{span}(\Gamma)
.
Since
W=w\cdot W
, we may assume without loss of generality that
w=e\in W
is the identity element.

We use the unique (set) decomposition
W=W^{\Gamma}\cdot W_{\Gamma}
into a parabolic quotient and the parabolic subgroup generated by
\Gamma
to conclude the proof.
To this end, let
{\mathcal{X}}=\{{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}\}_{\beta\in\Psi}
and
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=\{{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\Psi^{\prime}}
.
We aim to show

{\mathbb{P}[{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=\delta]}={\mathbb{P}[{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=\delta\ |\ {\mathcal{X}}=\varepsilon]}

for any fixed outcomes
\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}^{\Psi},\delta\in\{0,1\}^{\Psi^{\prime}}
.
This follows from the observation that the outcome
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=\delta
only depends on the parabolic quotient
W^{\Gamma}
since
W_{\Gamma}
pointwise fixes
\Psi^{\prime}
, while the outcome
{\mathcal{X}}=\varepsilon
only depends on the parabolic subgroup
W_{\Gamma}
by Lemma
3.6

We make this argument precise in the following calculations.
To simplify notation, we write for

\varepsilon\cdot w(\Psi)\in\Phi^{-}\quad\text{ if for all }\beta\in\Psi\text{ we have }\begin{cases}w(\beta)\in\Phi^{-}\text{ if }\varepsilon(\beta)=1\,,\\
w(\beta)\in\Phi^{+}\text{ if }\varepsilon(\beta)=0\,,\end{cases}

and we analogously write
\delta\cdot w(\Psi^{\prime})\in\Phi^{-}
.
We then have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}[{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=\delta\ |\ {\mathcal{X}}=\varepsilon]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big|\{w\in W\mid\varepsilon\cdot w(\Psi)\in\Phi^{-},\ \delta\cdot w(\Psi^{\prime})\in\Phi^{-}\}\big|}{\big|\{w\in W\mid\varepsilon\cdot w(\Psi)\in\Phi^{-}\}\big|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big|\{w^{\Gamma}\in W^{\Gamma},w_{\Gamma}\in W_{\Gamma}\mid\varepsilon\cdot w_{\Gamma}(\Psi)\in\Phi^{-},\ \delta\cdot w^{\Gamma}(\Psi^{\prime})\in\Phi^{-}\}\big|}{|W^{\Gamma}|\cdot|\{w_{\Gamma}\in W_{\Gamma}\mid\varepsilon\cdot w_{\Gamma}(\Psi)\in\Phi^{-}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big|\{w\in W^{\Gamma}\mid\delta\cdot w(\Psi^{\prime})\in\Phi^{-}\}\big|}{|W^{\Gamma}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big|\{w\in W\mid\delta\cdot w(\Psi^{\prime})\in\Phi^{-}\}\big|}{|W|}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}[{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=\delta]}\,.\qed

4.
Central limit theorems

Equipped with the necessary Coxeter-theoretic properties from Section
3
, we use the dependency graph method to prove the claimed central limit theorems, together with bounds on rates of convergence where the used method is applicable.
The computations for
d
-inversions also depend on the concrete calculations of the variances for
d
-inversions given in Section
A
.

4.1.
The dependency graph method

Let
\{{\mathcal{X}}^{(n,j)}\}
with
n\geq 0
and
1\leq j\leq k_{n}
be a triangular array of random variables.
For fixed
n
, define the graph
G_{n}
to have vertices
\{1,\dots,k_{n}\}
and edges
\{i,j\}
with
i\neq j
if
{\mathcal{X}}^{(n,i)}
and
{\mathcal{X}}^{(n,j)}
are dependent.
It is called the
n
-th dependency graph
of this triangular array if for any two disjoined subsets
I,J\subseteq\{1,\dots,k_{n}\}
without edges between
I
and
J
in
G_{n}
, it holds that also the corresponding sets of random variables are independent.
In this case, we say that the triangular array
admits dependency graphs
of
dependency degree

(\delta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
where
\delta_{n}
is the maximal vertex degree of
G_{n}
.

We present the following theorem due to Janson
[
6
]
in a version tailor made for our situation.

Theorem 4.1

(​​
[
6
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Let
\{{\mathcal{X}}^{(n,j)}\}
with
n\geq 0
and
1\leq j\leq k_{n}
be a triangular array of Bernoulli random variables, admitting dependency graphs of dependency degree
(\delta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
.
Then the sequence
{\mathcal{X}}^{(n)}={\mathcal{X}}^{(n,1)}+\dots+{\mathcal{X}}^{(n,k_{n})}
is asymptotically normal if there exists an
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\frac{k_{n}\cdot\delta_{n}^{m-1}}{{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}^{(n)}]}^{m/2}}\longrightarrow 0\,.

Corollary 4.2

(​​
[
5
, Corollary 4.3 & Theorem 4.7]
)
.

If the assumption in the previous theorem is satisfied for
m=3
, we obtain a bound for the rate of convergence given by

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|{\mathbb{P}[{\mathcal{X}}^{(n)}\leq x]}-{\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{N}(0,1)\leq x]}\Big|\leq k_{n}\cdot\delta_{n}^{2}\cdot{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}^{(n)}]}^{-3/2}\longrightarrow 0\,.

We use Theorem
4.1
to prove asymptotic normality for antichains in Theorem
2.6
, directly implying the main result for
d
-descents in Corollary
2.7
.
Using then the concrete variances for
d
-inversions, we prove the second main result for
d
-inversions stated in Theorem
2.9
.

We close this section with introducing three notions for later usage.
For functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we write
f\lesssim g
, if there exists
\varepsilon>0
and an
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N
, it holds that
f(n)\leq\varepsilon g(n)
.
We moreover write
f\ll g
, if for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
N\in\mathbb{N}
with this property. Observe that
f\ll g\Leftrightarrow f(n)/g(n)\longrightarrow 0\,.

We finally write

f\approx g
for
f\lesssim g\lesssim f
.

4.2.
Central limit theorems for antichains in root posets and
d
-descents

Let
\{\Phi^{(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of finite crystallographic root systems and let
\{\Psi^{(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of antichains in the respective root posets.
We consider the random variable
{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}
as given in (
2.1
) and give two proofs of its asymptotic normality.
We use the dependency graph method above and then remark that one may also instead rely on the result for descents in
[
7
]
.
The dependency graph method yields in addition the proposed bound on the rate of convergence.

We start with collecting the following properties.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
be an antichain.
Then

•

\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle\leq 0
for all
\beta\neq\gamma
in
\Psi
,

•

there are at most
|\Psi|-1
many pairs of dependent random variables
{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta},{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}
for
\beta,\gamma\in\Psi
, and

•

the dependency degree
\delta
is at most
3
.

Proof.

All properties are clearly satisfied if
\Psi\subset\Delta
is a set of simple roots:
Any two simple roots have inner product less than or equal to zero and the dependency graph of
{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi}
for
\Psi\subset\Delta
equals the Coxeter graph of the parabolic subsystem generated by
\Psi
.
Since these properties are also invariant under the group action of
W
on
\Phi
, the conclusions follow from
[
9
]
where it was shown that any antichain in the root poset may be send to simple roots by an element in
W
.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

The variance of
{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}
grows linearly with the cardinality of the antichain, i.e.,

{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}]}\approx|\Psi^{(n)}|.

Proof.

Together with Lemma
4.3
, it follows from Theorem
3.1
that we may rewrite
{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}]}
as

{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}]}=\sum\limits_{\beta\in\Psi^{(n)}}{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}]}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta,\gamma\in\Psi^{(n)}\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{\beta},{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma})=\frac{1}{4}\left(|\Psi^{(n)}|-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta,\gamma\in\Psi^{(n)}\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{2}{\operatorname{ord}({\beta},{\gamma})}\Big)\right).

With
\operatorname{ord}(\beta,\gamma)\in\{2,3,4,6\}
, we immediately obtain
{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}]}\leq\frac{|\Psi^{(n)}|}{4}
.
We aim to also show the lower bound
{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}]}\geq\frac{|\Psi^{(n)}|}{12}
by showing that

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta,\gamma\in\Psi^{(n)}\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{2}{\operatorname{ord}({\beta},{\gamma})}\Big)\leq\frac{2}{3}|\Psi^{(n)}|\,.

It follows, as in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, from
[
9
]
that
\Psi^{(n)}
are the simple roots of a parabolic subsystem.
We may thus decompose
\Psi^{(n)}=\Delta_{1}\cup\dots\cup\Delta_{k}
into its irreducible components, meaning that
\Delta_{i}
contains the simple roots of on irreducible root system.
We therefore have
\operatorname{ord}(\beta,\gamma)=2
for
\beta\in\Delta_{i}
and
\gamma\in\Delta_{j}
with
i\neq j
, and also that for each
\Delta_{i}
, there are exactly
2|\Delta_{i}|-2
many pairs
(\beta,\gamma)\in\Delta_{i}^{2}
with
\operatorname{ord}(\beta,\gamma)\geq 3
and for at most two of them, say
(\beta,\gamma)
and
(\gamma,\beta)
, we have
\operatorname{ord}(\beta,\gamma)=\operatorname{ord}(\gamma,\beta)>3
.
We then obtain

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta,\gamma\in\Psi^{(n)}\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{2}{\operatorname{ord}({\beta},{\gamma})}\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta,\gamma\in\Delta_{i}\\
\beta\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{2}{\operatorname{ord}({\beta},{\gamma})}\Big)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{2}{3}\big(|\Delta_{i}|-2\big)+\frac{4}{3}\right)=\frac{2}{3}|\Psi^{(n)}|\,,

as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.6
.

The random variable
{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}
is a sum of Bernoulli random variables.
We have seen in Theorem
3.4
that this is a triangular array of random variables admits dependency graphs.
Lemma
4.3
showed that its dependency degree
\delta_{n}
is globally bounded by
3
.
We have moreover seen in Proposition
4.4
that the variance of
{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}
grows linearly with
k_{n}=|\Psi^{(n)}|
.
We therefore obtain that

\frac{k_{n}\cdot\delta_{n}^{m-1}}{{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}]}^{m/2}}\approx\big|\Psi^{(n)}\big|^{\tfrac{2-m}{2}}\longrightarrow 0

for any
m\geq 3
.

{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi^{(n)}}
is thus asymptotically normal by Theorem
4.1
and the bound on the rate of convergence is
|\Psi^{(n)}|^{-1/2}
as given by Corollary
4.2
.
∎

Without the rate of convergence, we may as well more easily reduce the situation of Theorem
2.6
to previously known results.

Remark 4.5
.

One may as well derive the asymptotic normality as claimed in Theorem
2.6
from the asymptotic normality in
[
7
]
as follows.
By
[
9
]
, any antichain in the root poset may be send to simple roots by an element in
W
.
We thus reduce the situation of
{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi}^{(n)}
to the case that
\Psi^{(n)}\subseteq\Delta^{(n)}
for the simple roots
\Delta^{(n)}
of the root system
\Phi^{(n)}
.
We have moreover seen in Corollary
3.7
that
{\mathcal{X}}_{\Psi}^{(n)}
may equivalently be considered inside

, and this case is treated in
[
7
, Theorem 6.2]
.
The approach used here uses different techniques and in particular yields the claimed rate of convergence.

4.3.
Central limit theorems for
d
-inversions

Let
\{\Phi^{(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of finite crystallographic root systems and let
\{d_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
a sequence of integer.
We consider the random variable

{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d_{n})}}=\sum_{\beta\in\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d_{n})}}{\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}\quad\text{with}\quad\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d_{n})}=\{\beta\in\Phi^{+}\mid 0pt{\beta}\leq d_{n}\}

and prove its asymptotic normality using the dependency graph method.
We in addition provide bounds on the rate of convergence where the method is applicable.
Based on the concrete calculations in Section
A
, we start with the following lemma giving lower bounds for the variance depending on the situation.

Lemma 4.6
.

There exists a global constant
\varepsilon>0
such that for any irreducible root system
\Phi
of rank
r
and any parameter
d
, we have

{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d+1)}}]}\geq{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d)}}]}>\begin{cases}\varepsilon\cdot r^{3}&\text{if }\hskip 6.0ptr\leq d\,,\\[5.0pt]
\varepsilon\cdot d^{3}&\text{if }\hskip 5.0ptd\leq r\leq d^{2}\,,\\[5.0pt]
\varepsilon\cdot r\cdot d&\text{if }\hskip 5.0ptd^{2}\leq r\,.\end{cases}

Proof.

The first property that the variance (weakly) increases in
d
can be checked by observing that the derivative in
d
of the involved polynomials—see Section
A
—is non-negative, or by concretely calculating the difference between two variances for consecutive parameters in
d
.
Since these calculations are not enlightening, we leave the details to the reader.

The inequality
{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d+1)}}]}\geq{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d)}}]}
may be used to obtain

{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d)}}]}\geq{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(k)}}]}

where

.
We therefore only need to treat the concrete variances for
2d\leq r
.

The first case is
r\leq d
and we obtain

{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d)}}]}\geq{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(r/2)}}]}>\varepsilon\cdot r^{3}\,.

The second case

is split into two subcases.
If

, we directly have

{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d)}}]}>\varepsilon\cdot d^{3}

and if

, we have

{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d)}}]}\geq{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(r/2)}}]}>\varepsilon\cdot r^{3}\geq\varepsilon\cdot d^{3}\,.

In the final case

, we have
2d\leq d^{2}\leq r
(the case

also fulfills the bound trivially).
Thus,

{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d)}}]}>\max{(rd,d^{3})}=rd\,.

where we used that in all classical types, there are terms
rd
and

with positive coefficients in the polynomial expressions for

.
All involved parameters
\varepsilon
may a priori differ, but—after also taking the finite number of exceptional groups into account—we may choose one global such
\varepsilon
.
∎

We decompose a given root system
\Phi=\Phi^{(n)}
depending on the parameter

into its irreducible components according to Lemma

\Phi=\Phi_{A}\cup\Phi_{B}\cup\Phi_{C}=\bigcup_{i\in A}\Phi_{i}\ \cup\ \bigcup_{i\in B}\Phi_{i}\ \cup\ \bigcup_{i\in C}\Phi_{i}

where we have that different irreducible components
\Phi_{i}
and
\Phi_{j}
for

live in orthogonal subspaces.
We moreover denote their respective ranks

and the indices are decomposed as

We set

and
r_{B}
and

analogously, and observe that

.

In the considered setup, we assume

.
This implies that also

.
We moreover assume without loss of generality that there exists positive roots for
\Phi^{(n)}
of height

in the root poset.
Otherwise

and we may replace

by

without modifying the considered random variable.
The root poset of

consists of the disjoint union of the root posets (in the sense that their Hasse diagrams are disjoint) of its irreducible components

We finally denote by

the

Proposition 4.7
.

We have

|\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d_{n})}|\lesssim r_{n}\cdot d_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\delta_{n}\lesssim d_{n}\,.

Proof.

The first property follows from the observation that the number of elements of a given height in the root poset is bounded by the rank.

For the second, let
\beta\in\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d_{n})}
.
We first discuss the situation that
\beta
is in an irreducible component of one of the classical types
A
–
D
.
In these cases, we have seen that
\beta
is of one of the respective forms
e_{i}-e_{j}
,
e_{i}
,
e_{i}+e_{j}
and a positive root
\gamma
is not orthogonal to
\beta
if and only if
\gamma
lives in the same irreducible component (so
\gamma
is of one the respective forms
e_{k}-e_{\ell}
,
e_{k}
,
e_{k}+e_{\ell}
) and
\beta
and
\gamma
share one of the used indices.
This implies that the number of roots not orthogonal to
\beta
is at most
4d_{n}
.
On the other hand, if
\beta
is in an irreducible component of exceptional type, then the number of roots not orthogonal to
\beta
is globally bounded.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.9
& Corollary
2.10
.

We write
\sigma_{n}^{2}={\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d_{n})}}]}
and analogously
\sigma_{i}^{2}
for the variance of the corresponding random variable of the irreducible component
\Phi_{i}
and decompose

\sigma_{n}^{2}=\sigma_{A}^{2}+\sigma_{B}^{2}+\sigma_{C}^{2}=\sum_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i\in B}\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i\in C}\sigma_{i}^{2}

according to the decomposition
\Phi^{(n)}=\Phi_{A}\cup\Phi_{B}\cup\Phi_{C}
where we used that the corresponding random variables of the irreducible components are independent because of the orthogonality between roots in different irreducible components, compare Theorem
3.1
.
By Lemma
4.6
, we moreover have a global constant
\varepsilon
with

\sigma_{A}^{2}=\sum_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}^{2}>\varepsilon\cdot r_{A}^{3},\quad\sigma_{B}^{2}=\sum_{i\in B}\sigma_{i}^{2}>\varepsilon\cdot|B|\cdot d^{3},\quad\sigma_{C}^{2}=\sum_{i\in C}\sigma_{i}^{2}>\varepsilon\cdot d\cdot r_{C}\,.

For the first inequality, we have used
\sigma_{i}^{2}>\varepsilon^{\prime}\sum_{i\in A}r_{i}^{3}\approx(\sum_{i\in A}r_{i})^{3}=r_{A}^{3}
.
The second and third inequalities are immediate from the lemma.

We aim to use Theorem
4.1
for
m=5
in general and for
m=3
where possible, we again emphasize that the dependency graph method is applicable by Theorem
3.4
.
Proposition
4.7
gives

\frac{|\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d_{n})}|\cdot\delta_{n}^{m-1}}{\sigma_{n}^{m}}\lesssim\frac{r_{n}\cdot d_{n}^{m}}{\sigma_{n}^{m}}\,.

We have already seen that
r_{n}=r_{A}+r_{B}+r_{C}\longrightarrow\infty
.
We thus consider the following three cases:

r_{A}\approx r_{n}
:
In this case, we have
r_{n}^{3}\lesssim\sigma_{n}^{2}
and thus

\frac{r_{n}\cdot d_{n}^{m}}{\sigma_{n}^{m}}\lesssim r_{n}^{1-\tfrac{3m}{2}}\cdot d_{n}^{m}\lesssim r_{n}^{1-\tfrac{m}{2}}\longrightarrow 0\text{ for }m=3\,.

r_{C}\approx r_{n}
:
In this case, we have
r_{n}\cdot d_{n}\lesssim\sigma_{n}^{2}
and thus

\frac{r_{n}\cdot d_{n}^{m}}{\sigma_{n}^{m}}\lesssim r_{n}^{1-\tfrac{m}{2}}\cdot d_{n}^{\tfrac{m}{2}}\lesssim r_{n}^{1-\tfrac{m}{4}}\longrightarrow 0\text{ for }m=5\,.

The latter approximation comes from the observation that
d_{n}^{2}<r_{i}
for any
i\in C
and thus
d_{n}^{2}\lesssim r_{n}
.
Indeed, if we even assume
d_{n}^{3}\ll r_{n}
, we obtain

r_{n}^{1-\tfrac{m}{2}}\cdot d_{n}^{\tfrac{m}{2}}\ll r_{n}^{1-\tfrac{m}{2}+\tfrac{m}{6}}=r_{n}^{1-\tfrac{2m}{6}}\,,

and thus

\frac{r_{n}\cdot d_{n}^{m}}{\sigma_{n}^{m}}\lesssim r_{n}^{1-\tfrac{m}{2}}\cdot d_{n}^{\tfrac{m}{2}}\longrightarrow 0\text{ for }m=3

in this case.

r_{B}\approx r_{n}
:
In this case, we have
|B|\cdot d_{n}^{3}\lesssim\sigma_{n}^{2}
and thus

\frac{r_{n}\cdot d_{n}^{m}}{\sigma_{n}^{m}}\lesssim r_{n}\cdot d_{n}^{-\tfrac{m}{2}}\big|B\,\big|^{-\tfrac{m}{2}}\,.

We may now compute

(4.1)

r_{n}\approx r_{B}=\sum_{i\in B}r_{i}\leq\sum_{i\in B}d_{n}^{2}=d_{n}^{2}\cdot|B|

where we used that
r_{i}\leq d_{n}^{2}
for all
i\in B
.
This gives

r_{n}\cdot d_{n}^{-\tfrac{m}{2}}\big|B\,\big|^{-\tfrac{m}{2}}\lesssim d_{n}^{2-\tfrac{m}{2}}\big|B\,\big|^{1-\tfrac{m}{2}}\longrightarrow 0\text{ for }m=5\,,

using (
4.1
) to show that either
d_{n}\rightarrow\infty
or
|B|\rightarrow\infty
.
If we even assume

, we obtain

r_{n}\cdot d_{n}^{-\tfrac{m}{2}}\big|B\,\big|^{-\tfrac{m}{2}}\longrightarrow 0\text{ for }m=3\,.\qed

The proof is provided in several steps.
We first partition the positive roots

Lemma A.2

We have

N_{a}\neq\emptyset\iff 1\leq a<n,\quad|N_{a}|=n-a\text{ for }1\leq a<n\,.

According to Theorem

Moreover, we have for

(A.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{{[ij]}},{\mathcal{X}}_{{[ik]}})=\operatorname{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{{[ji]}},{\mathcal{X}}_{{[ki]}})=\tfrac{1}{12},\quad\operatorname{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{{[ji]}},{\mathcal{X}}_{{[ik]}})=\operatorname{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{{[ij]}},{\mathcal{X}}_{{[ki]}})=-\tfrac{1}{12}\,.

Lemma A.3
.

For
1\leq a,b<n

\displaystyle\big|\{{[ij]},{[k\ell]}\in N_{a}\times N_{b}\mid i=k,j\neq k\}\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\{{[ij]},{[k\ell]}\in N_{a}\times N_{b}\mid i\neq k,j=\ell\}\big|

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n-\max\{a,b\}&a\neq b\\
0&a=b\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\big|\{{[ij]},{[k\ell]}\in N_{a}\times N_{b}\mid i=\ell\}\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\{{[ij]},{[k\ell]}\in N_{a}\times N_{b}\mid j=k\}\big|

\displaystyle=n-\min\{n,a+b\}

Proof.

First, we observe that the two sets in the first equality are empty for

For all other cases, one uses

Proof of Theorem
A.1
.

Combining Lemma
A.3

{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{des}}^{(d)}}]}=\tfrac{1}{4}|N_{d}|-\tfrac{2}{12}\big(n-\min(n,2d)\big)\,.

This is equivalent to the two cases in the formula for
{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{des}}^{(d)}}]}
.
On the other hand, we have

{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d)}}]}=\tfrac{1}{4}\sum\limits_{1\leq a\leq d}|N_{a}|+\tfrac{2}{12}\sum\limits_{1\leq a\neq b\leq d}\big(n-\max(a,b)\big)-\tfrac{2}{12}\sum\limits_{1\leq a,b\leq d}\big(n-\min(n,a+b)\big)\,.

The first sum comes from the terms
{\mathbb{V}[{\mathcal{X}}_{{[ij]}}]}
for all
{[ij]}\in\Phi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{(d)}
.
The second sum comes from the terms of the form
\operatorname{Cov}({\mathcal{X}}_{{[ij]}},{\mathcal{X}}_{{[ik]}})
.
It can be rewritten as

\sum\limits_{1\leq a\neq b\leq d}(n-\max(a,b))=2\cdot\sum\limits_{1\leq a<b\leq d}(n-b)\\
=2\cdot\Big(-\tfrac{1}{6}(d-1)d(2d-3n+2)\Big)\,.

For the third sum, first note that the summands are zero for
a+b\geq n

We may thus rewrite this as

\sum\limits_{1\leq a,b\leq d}(n-\min(n,a+b))=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a,b\leq d\\
a+b\leq n\end{subarray}}(n-(a+b))=\sum\limits_{1\leq a\leq d}\,\sum\limits_{1\leq b\leq\min(n-a,d)}(n-(a+b))\,.

Assume first
2d\leq n

Since

and thus

Assume next
2d\geq n

We further consider two cases

and

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{1\leq a,b\leq d}(n-\min(n,a+b))

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{1\leq a\leq n-d}\sum\limits_{1\leq b\leq d}(n-(a+b))+\sum\limits_{n-d<a\leq d}\sum\limits_{1\leq b\leq n-a}(n-(a+b))

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}d(n-2)(d-n)+\tfrac{1}{6}(2d-n)(d^{2}-dn+n^{2}-3n+2)

Combining the three sums under consideration, one obtains the stated variance.
∎
