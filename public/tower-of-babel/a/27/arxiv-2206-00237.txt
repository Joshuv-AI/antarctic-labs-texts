Matroids of Gain Signed Graphs
Laura Anderson, Ting Su, and Thomas Zaslavsky

Abstract
. A signed graph has edge signs. A gain graph has oriented edge gains drawn from a group. We define the combination of the two for the abelian case, in which each oriented edge of a signed graph has a gain from an abelian group, concentrating on the case of the additive group of a field. We develop the elementary graph properties, the associated matroid, and the vector and hyperplanar representations.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 05C22; Secondary 05B35, 52C35.

1.
Introduction

An
arrangement of hyperplanes
is a finite set of hyperplanes in some linear, affine, or projective space. Hyperplane arrangements formed a rather obscure corner of mathematics as recently as Grünbaum’s famous book of 1967
[
9
]
, in which they were the subject of a chapter, but subsequently they have expanded into many parts of combinatorics, combinatorial geometry, algebraic geometry, algebra, and even analysis. A fundamental combinatorial object associated to an arrangement is its intersection semilattice, which consists of all intersections of subarrangements (excluding empty sets in the affine case). Valuable information about the arrangement can be extracted from this semilattice; in particular, through the characteristic polynomial of the semilattice one obtains the number of regions in the complement of a real arrangement
[
21
]
and the cohomology of the complement of a complex arrangement
[
12
]
.

Certain special arrangements were long well known, and have been a basis for this expansion: the hyperplane arrangements
A_{n-1}^{*}
,
B_{n}^{*}=C_{n}^{*}
, and
D_{n}^{*}
dual to the classical root systems.
Greene observed that subarrangements of the first of these correspond to graphs and their matroids. The hyperplanes have equations of the form
x_{j}=x_{i}
, and such a hyperplane corresponds to an edge
v_{i}v_{j}
in the associated graph
[
8
]
. We call such a hyperplane
graphic
. Zaslavsky extended this notion to subarrangements of
B_{n}^{*}=C_{n}^{*}
and
D_{n}^{*}
, developing a matroid and coloring theory of signed graphs—graphs in which each edge is signed positive or negative
[
22
]
—that facilitates the calculation of the characteristic polynomial of the arrangement
[
23
]
. Here hyperplanes have equations of the forms
x_{j}=\pm x_{i}
and
x_{i}=0
, corresponding to signed edges.

As time passed new particular arrangements attracted interest. The Catalan arrangement (arising from combinatorial geometry) has hyperplanes of the form
x_{j}-x_{i}=0,\pm 1
for
i<j
; the Shi arrangement (arising from algebraic geometry) has hyperplanes of the form
x_{j}-x_{i}=0,1
and the Linial arrangement has hyperplanes
x_{j}-x_{i}=1
, both also for
i<j
. These examples led to the idea of a
deformation
of a root system arrangement: the hyperplanes may be translated (in multiple ways); for instance, the Catalan, Shi, and Linial arrangements are deformations of the
A_{n-1}^{*}
arrangement. (We call arrangements of hyperplanes of the form
x_{j}-x_{i}=c

affinographic
since they are affine deformations of graphic arrangements.) The same idea extends to the other classical root system arrangements, with hyperplanes of the form
x_{j}\pm x_{i}=c
, and there are variations such as the threshold arrangement with hyperplanes
x_{j}+x_{i}=1
.

To extract such valuable information as mentioned above one has to calculate the characteristic polynomial. This can be hard. Athanasiadis in a major paper
[
2
]
did that for many examples by using what he called the “finite field method”. Given an integral deformation of a rational linear arrangement, one treats it as an arrangement over a finite field of sufficiently large order—though in practice only prime fields are used. This retains the intersection semilattice and enables one to compute the characteristic polynomial by a counting process. Since there are infinitely many large primes, with sufficient ingenuity one obtains the polynomial for infinitely many prime arguments, thus determining it.

Meanwhile, Zaslavsky had generalized signed graphs, coloring, their hyperplane representations by subarrangements of
B_{n}^{*}
, and their matroids, to gain graphs
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
. In a gain graph, the edges of the graph are labelled by the elements of a group, invertibly, which means the group element depends on direction, being inverted when the direction is reversed. Signed graphs are the case where the group has order 2. A gain graph has a natural matroid, in fact, two of them, called the frame and lift matroids. Zaslavsky noticed that the integral affinographic arrangements studied by Athanasiadis—that is, arrangements with equations
x_{j}-x_{i}=c
with
c\in\mathbb{Z}
—are hyperplanar representations of gain graphs with gain group
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, the additive integers, and the lattice structure of the arrangement is given by the lift matroid of the gain graph
[
28
,
4
]
. Athanasiadis’ finite field method, which in practice uses only prime fields, is really a modular coloring method, in which the gain group is the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
for all sufficiently large moduli
m
; having a finite group enables the necessary counting process by way of coloring using color set
\mathbb{Z}_{m}

[
4
]
. This simplifies the theory for all integral affinographic arrangements as there is no need for
m
to be prime; it also strengthens the conclusions since it is possible, at least in principle, to determine all valid moduli
m
by knowing the gain graph. (These improvements do not reduce the need for ingenuity.) However, it leaves out Athanasiadis’ examples with hyperplanes of the form
x_{j}+x_{i}=c
in
[
2
, Theorems 3.10
et seq.
and 5.4–5.5]
, because those hyperplanes cannot be treated with gain graphs. (We call such arrangements
signed affinographic
because they are affine deformations of arrangements that represent signed graphs.) The problem is that, while
x_{j}-x_{i}=c
corresponds to an ordinary or positive edge with gain
c
,
x_{j}+x_{i}=c
corresponds to a negative edge with gain
c
. Neither signed nor gain graphs alone can handle this; it calls for a combination. How to produce the necessary combination remained an unsolved problem for some decades.

That is the problem we solve here. However, the immediate inspiration was different. Ohsugi and Hibi were led by an algebraic question to study the edge polytope of a graph
[
11
]
. This is the convex hull of vectors
\mathbf{b}_{i}+\mathbf{b}_{j}
, one for each edge
v_{i}v_{j}
in the graph. Part of their work involved finding the affine span of a set of such vectors
[
11
, Proposition 1.3]
. It was clear, from the viewpoint of signed graphs, that the affine span is a signed-graphic property; but what, exactly, is that property? We quickly realized that it requires a combination of signs and identically-
1
gains on the edges of the graph. This gave us the necessary hint, and from that we produced a successful definition of a gain signed graph and (the proof of success) a succession of theorems under the assumption that we have an abelian gain group, such as the additive group of a field. This paper reports our results. As a demonstration we prove Ohsugi and Hibi’s proposition in our new way in Example
3.6
and generalize it to bidirected graphs.

Terminological note.
Gain signed graphs are not the same as signed gain graphs. The inverse order of modifiers corresponds grammatically to the order of structures. A signed gain graph is a gain graph with added signs; the gain-graph structure does not involve the signs, though the sign structure might depend on the gains. We believe that trying to define signed gain graphs has previously been the barrier to a successful combination of signs and gains. It is the realization that the signs influence the gain structure, so one needs gains superimposed on a signed graph, that enabled us to succeed.

2.
Technical introduction

The object of study is a
gain signed graph
, which is a triple
\Upsilon=(\Gamma,\sigma,\varphi)
where
\Gamma=(V,E)
is a graph, the
signature
or sign function
\sigma
gives each edge an element of the sign set
\{+1,-1\}
, and the
gain function

\varphi
is an oriented labelling of edges from a group; that is, inverting the edge inverts the gain. The group will be the additive group of a field
\mathbf{K}
until at the end we retroactively generalize in Section
11
. All these terms must be explained further.

We assume acquaintance with the basics of matroid theory, such as in the beginning of Oxley
[
13
]
. As there are inconsistencies amongst common usage in graph theory, usage in matroid theory, and our special needs, we summarize some of our vocabulary here. The definitions will follow in this section.

Graph
: it may have links, loops, and half and loose edges, as well as multiple edges. We write
n:=|V|
.

Circle
: a connected, 2-regular subgraph or its edge set.

Circuit
: a matroid circuit.

Sign circuit
: a circuit of the frame matroid
\mathbf{F}(\Gamma,\sigma)
. Similarly for cocircuits.

Sign cycle
: a sign circuit oriented to have no source or sink.

\mathbf{F}(\Sigma)
: the frame matroid of a signed graph
\Sigma
.

A sign circuit is
neutral
in
\Upsilon
if it has gain 0.

\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
: the matroid of the gain signed graph
\Upsilon
.

\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
: the extended matroid of
\Upsilon
, with extra element
e_{\infty}
.

Hypercircuit, hypercocircuit
: a circuit or cocircuit of the matroid
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
or
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
, as appropriate.

\operatorname{Lat}M
: the geometric lattice of a matroid
M
.

2.1.
Graphs

The graph
\Gamma
always means
(V,E)
whose vertex set is
V=V(\Gamma)=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}
, with
n:=|V|
.
Our notion of a graph is unusual because we allow four kinds of edge: links, loops, half edges, and loose edges. Multiple edges are allowed. A link or loop has two
ends
, each of which is incident with one vertex, called an endpoint of the edge. (Note that an endpoint is a vertex while an end is part of an edge.) The edge is a link if these two end vertices are distinct and a loop if they coincide. A half edge has one end, which is incident with one vertex, and a loose edge has no ends. In notation we often write
e_{vw}
or
e_{ij}
to indicate the endpoints of a link or loop
e
,
e_{v}
or
e_{i}
for a half edge
e
, and
e_{\varnothing}
for a loose edge (although this notation does not distinguish parallel edges, which have the same endpoints).
We write
E^{2}
for the set of links and loops,
E^{0}
for that of loose edges, and
E^{1}
for that of half edges.
For an end of edge
e
incident with a vertex
v
we write
(v,e)
; this notation is technically ambiguous for a loop but that will rarely cause difficulty. We write
\operatorname{Ends}(\Gamma)
for the set of edge ends of
\Gamma
.

A
simple graph
is a graph that has only links and has no parallel edges.

The
components
of
\Gamma
are the usual connected components, not including loose edges. More precisely, they are the
vertex components
of
\Gamma
. The
edge components
are each loose edge and the vertex components that are not isolated vertices.

A
circle
is a connected 2-regular graph, or its edge set (widely known as a “cycle” or “circuit”, but we have different uses for those names). A
unicycle
is a circle or half edge that may have trees attached. A
handcuff
is a pair of circles with at most one common vertex together with a minimal connecting path (of length 0 if there is a common vertex).

A
cut
is a non-empty set that consists of all edges between a subset of
V
and its complement. A minimal cut is a
bond
. The two components of the bond-deleted graph that are joined by the bond are its
sides
.

The edge set induced by
X\subseteq V
is denoted by
E{:}X
; the induced subgraph is denoted by
\Gamma{:}X
. Suppose
\pi
is a partition or partial partition of
V
(that is, a partition of a subset); then the partition-induced subgraph is denoted by
\Gamma{:}\pi=(\bigcup\pi,E{:}\pi):=\bigcup_{B\in\pi}\Gamma{:}B
.

The
restriction
of
\Gamma
to an edge set
S
is the spanning subgraph
\Gamma|S:=(V,S)
.
The partition of
V
induced by the components of
\Gamma
is
\pi(\Gamma)
; for an edge set
S\subseteq E
the induced partition of
V
is
\pi(S):=\pi(\Gamma|S)=\pi(V,S)
. The number of components of
\Gamma
is
c(\Gamma)=|\pi(\Gamma)|
.
The set of vertices of edges of
S
is
V(S)
, and
c(S)
is the number of components of the spanning subgraph
\Gamma|S
. The
cyclomatic number
of
S
is

\xi(S)=|S|-|V|+c(S)=|S|-|V(\Gamma|S)|+c(\Gamma|S).

It is the number of edges that must be deleted in order to eliminate all circuits of the graphic matroid; this includes loose edges.

For a walk, say
W=u_{0}e_{1}u_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{l}u_{l}
(where the length
l\geq 0
), we write
W_{ij}
to denote that part of
W
beginning at
u_{i}
and ending at
u_{j}
. The
reverse
of that partial walk is
W_{ji}=W_{ij}^{-1}
. In particular,
W=W_{0l}
and
W^{-1}=W_{l0}
. The direction of a walk
W
is indicated by the ordered pair of initial and final vertices; note that this concept of direction is not the one common in directed graph theory and is different from the concept of orientation in Section
2.3
.

For sign circuits (to be defined shortly) we sometimes need an extension of the notion of walk. An
ultrawalk
is like a walk except that it may begin or end with a half edge. For example,
W=e_{0}u_{0}e_{1}u_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{l}u_{l}
is an ultrawalk if
e_{0}
is a half edge incident with
u_{0}
.

2.2.
Signed graphs

A
signed graph

\Sigma=(\Gamma,\sigma)
consists of a graph
\Gamma
and a function
\sigma:E\to\{+1,-1\}
, the
signature
or
sign function
, that is defined on all edges (this differs from the convention in previous articles such as
[
22
]
). Links and loops may be positive or negative. Half edges must be negative and loose edges must be positive. The set of edges with sign
\varepsilon
is
E^{\varepsilon}
.
A simple graph
\Gamma
gives rise to a signed graph in several ways: we may give all edges the same sign
\varepsilon
and we write that signed graph
\varepsilon\Gamma
, or we may double the edges with both signs, which is the graph
\pm\Gamma=(+\Gamma)\cup(-\Gamma)
, which is the union of two edge-disjoint signed graphs on the same vertex set
V=V(\Gamma)
.

We apply most graph notations to signed graphs; e.g., the restriction of a signed graph is
\Sigma|S:=(\Gamma|S,\sigma|_{S})
, more simply
(\Gamma|S,\sigma)
.

The sign of an edge set
S
is
\sigma(S):=
the product of the signs of edges in
S
. This is important for a circle
C
, which is either positive or negative. If an edge set
S\subseteq E
contains no negative circles and no half edges, it is
sign balanced
, and similarly for a subgraph. We write
b_{\Sigma}(S)
for the number of connected components of
S
, or more precisely of
\Sigma|S
, that are sign balanced.

A signed graph
\Sigma
is
sign antibalanced
if its negative,
-\Sigma
, is balanced.

The sign of a walk, say
W=u_{0}e_{1}u_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{l}u_{l}
, is
\sigma(W):=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\sigma(e_{i})
. Note that the sign of a walk is not necessarily the same as the sign of its edge set since the walk may repeat edges; but the two definitions do agree for a circle.
The same definition gives the sign of an ultrawalk: it is the product of the signs of its edges, accounting for multiplicity (recall that half edges are negative).

The
frame matroid

\mathbf{F}(\Sigma)
(
[
22
,
26
]
; called the bias matroid in
[
26
]
) is a matroid whose ground set is
E
and whose circuits are the sign circuits of
\Sigma
. To define a sign circuit, first we define a
negative figure
: it is any negative circle or half edge. A
sign circuit
is an edge set that is either a positive circle, or a loose edge, or a pair of negative figures that have exactly one common vertex (a
tight handcuff
), or a pair of disjoint negative figures together with a minimal connecting path (a
loose handcuff
)—minimal in the sense that it intersects the two circles only at its endpoints; the length is immaterial.
(We view a tight handcuff as having a connecting path of length
0
.)
In this definition any negative circle can be replaced by a half edge; thus we call a
negative figure
any negative circle or half edge.
The frame matroid’s rank function is
\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}(S)=n-b_{\Sigma}(S)
.

A fundamental operation on signed graphs is
switching
(which we call
sign switching
in the context of gain signed graphs). A
switching function
, briefly

, is any vertex function
\zeta:V\to\{\pm 1\}
. Switching
\Sigma
by
\zeta
means changing the signs of links and loops:
\Sigma=(\Gamma,\sigma)
becomes
\Sigma^{\zeta}=(\Gamma,\sigma^{\zeta})
whose sign function is
\sigma^{\zeta}(e_{vw}):=\zeta(v)\sigma(e)\zeta(w)
. The signs of loose and half edges do not change. Switching does not change the signs of closed walks and, most importantly, of circles.
A signed graph is balanced or antibalanced if and only if it can be sign switched to all positive or all negative
[
22
]
.

A switcher can also be regarded as a way of signing edges. We call
\zeta
a
sign potential
for an edge set
S
if
\sigma(e_{vw})=\zeta(v)^{-1}\zeta(w)
for every edge in
S
. (The inversion exists to suit the common notion of a potential, although for signs it is unnecessary.) For a subset

this is equivalent to

being identically

2.3.
Orientation

A signed graph, that is
\Sigma=(\Gamma,\sigma)
, is oriented differently from an ordinary, unsigned graph. An
orientation
of
\Sigma
is a mapping from edge ends to signs, say
\tau:\operatorname{Ends}\to\{+1,-1\}
, such that for a link or loop
e_{vw}
,
\tau(v,e)\tau(w,e)=-\sigma(e_{vw})
. In diagrams we interpret an end sign
+1
as an arrow pointing into the endpoint and sign
-1
as an arrow pointing from the endpoint into the edge. With this sign rule an oriented positive edge is an ordinary directed edge. An oriented negative edge, on the contrary, does not have a direction; it is either
introverted
, with both ends negative, or
extraverted
, with both ends positive. A half edge also has an orientation (at its single end); it is introverted if its end is negative and extraverted if it is positive.
A loose edge has no orientation (as it has no end). To denote an oriented signed graph and its orientation we write
\Sigma_{\tau}
. When we do not wish to specify a particular orientation of an edge we write
e
for one orientation and
e^{-1}
for the opposite orientation.

\tau=+-,\ -+,\ -+
. Middle: negative edges with
\tau=++,\ --,\ +,\ -
. Bottom: negative loops with
\tau=++,\ --
.

Orientations switch: if we switch
\Sigma
by
\zeta
, an orientation
\tau
becomes
\tau^{\zeta}
defined by
\tau^{\zeta}(v,e)=\zeta(v)\tau(v,e)
. Switching of an orientation applies to all edges (and is trivial for loose edges).

A walk in an oriented signed graph, say
W=u_{0}e_{1}u_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{l}u_{l}
, is
coherent
if the arrows line up at each vertex, that is,
\tau(u_{i},e_{i})=-\tau(u_{i},e_{i+1})
for each
i=1,2,\ldots,l-1
. (In any walk, each intermediate vertex
u_{i}
is either coherent, if
\tau(u_{i},e_{i})=-\tau(u_{i},e_{i+1})
, or incoherent. Coherence of a vertex in a walk is not altered by switching.) The same definition applies to any ultrawalk if among the intermediate vertices we include any vertex supporting a half edge.
A
closed walk
, in which
u_{l}=u_{0}
, is
coherent
if it is coherently oriented as an open walk and in addition it closes coherently, i.e.,
\tau(u_{l},e_{l})=-\tau(u_{0},e_{1})
; this implies that
\sigma(W)=+1
. A closed walk is
rooted coherent
if it is coherent as an open walk but not as a closed walk; this implies that
\sigma(W)=-1
; the
root
is
u_{0}
. To prove those sign statements, we show that a positive closed walk, however oriented, has an even number of incoherent vertices, while a negative circle has an odd number. The proof is a simple calculation (with subscripts modulo
l

\displaystyle\sigma(C)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\sigma(e_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{l}[-\tau(u_{i-1},e_{i})\tau(u_{i},e_{i})]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{l}[-\tau(u_{i},e_{i})\tau(u_{i},e_{i+1})]=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\iota(u_{i}),

where
\iota(u_{i})=+1
or
-1
if the vertex is, respectively, coherent or incoherent.

A
source
in an oriented signed graph is a vertex
v
such that all arrows point inward, i.e.,
\tau(v,e)=+1
for every edge end
(v,e)
at
v
. A
sink
is similar but all arrows point outward, i.e.,
\tau(v,e)=-1
for every edge end
(v,e)
at
v
.
There are exactly two ways to orient a sign circuit (other than a loose edge) so that none of its vertices is a source or sink, and they differ only by reversing the orientations of all the edges. We call such an edge set in an oriented signed graph a
sign cycle
.

A sign circuit
C
has one or two minimal covering ultrawalks, up to choice of direction and starting point, which we call
circuit walks
. The description can be complicated. For a circle the circuit walk goes once around the circle. For a tight handcuff whose negative figures are circles, it goes once around each circle, crossing over at their intersection vertex. For a loose handcuff whose negative figures are circles, it goes once around each circle and twice through the connecting path, once in each direction; this reduces to the circuit walk of a tight handcuff if the connecting path has length zero. For a handcuff in which one negative figure is a circle and the other is a half edge
h
, the circuit walk begins at
h
, goes through the connecting path (if any) to the circle, goes once around the circle, and returns along the connecting path to end with
h
. In a handcuff whose negative figures are both half edges, the circuit walk is an ultrawalk that begins with one half edge and proceeds to the other, where it ends. (The difference of the last type from the others is due to the fact that negative circles interfere with unimodularity while half edges do not. This oddity does not affect our theory. For a treatment of it see
[
5
]
.)

For all types, if
C
is oriented, it is a sign cycle if and only if one (equivalently, each) of these walks is coherent.

Now consider a circle. If it is positive, it can be coherently oriented, and then it is a sign cycle. If it is negative, it can be coherently oriented except at one arbitrarily chosen incoherent vertex
v
with incident edges
e,f
. Then we call it an
excycle

rooted at
v
if
\tau(v,e)=\tau(v,f)=+1
(i.e., the edge ends at
v
point towards
v
and thus out of the circle) and an
incycle
if the opposite. An incycle or excycle is the nearest a negative circle can be to a cycle. We also call an oriented half edge an incycle or excycle depending whether it is oriented into or out from its vertex.
The orientation of a sign cycle on a loose handcuff is such that one circle is an incycle, the other is an excycle, and the connecting path is a coherent path connecting the root of the excycle to the root of the incycle and, at its endpoints, coherent with the incident circle edges. For a tight handcuff sign cycle, the two roots are the common vertex and the incident edges in different circles are coherent with each other.

2.4.
Gains

2.4.1.
The gain function

The gain function
\varphi
is a mapping from oriented edges to an abelian group, written additively, such that the two orientations of the edge,
e
and
e^{-1}
, have mutually inverse gains, i.e.,
\varphi(e^{-1})=-\varphi(e)
(so when we specify a value for the gain
\varphi(e)
, we must also specify the orientation).
For a positive edge or a half edge, this means that the two directions of the edge have mutually inverse gains; but for a negative edge there is not a direction in the usual sense.
An edge with gain 0 is called
neutral
.
The set of neutral loose edges is written
E^{00}
.
Note that all edges have gains (unlike the definition in
[
26
]
).

In a slightly more formal definition, let the set of oriented edges be
\vec{E}
. The gain function is a mapping
\varphi
from
\vec{E}
to an additive abelian group with the property that
\varphi(e^{-1})=-\varphi(e)
. (It is important to remember that
e
and
e^{-1}
are the same object with opposite orientations.)

Reorienting
an edge in
\Upsilon
means negating the
\tau
values of that edge and inverting the gain of that edge as well. We define sign-switching to have no effect on gains.

The restriction of a gain signed graph to an edge set
S
is
\Upsilon|S:=(\Gamma|S,\sigma|_{S},\varphi|_{S})
, abbreviated as
(\Gamma|S,\sigma,\varphi)
.

Suppose we treat an unsigned graph as all positive; the pair
(\Gamma,\varphi)
is called a
gain graph
. The frame matroid theory of signed graphs was developed in
[
22
]
; that of gain graphs was developed in
[
26
]
. The former is a special case of the latter; but it turns out that this is the wrong way to look at it, at least for us. A gain signed graph is not a gain graph: the negative edges cannot be treated as edges of a gain graph. The reason for our treatment of negative edges will appear when we introduce the vector model in Section
3
.

Our gain group will be the additive group
\mathbf{K}^{+}
of a field
\mathbf{K}
whose characteristic is not equal to 2 (because we need
1\neq-1
), especially the real or complex numbers. We generalize this to an arbitrary abelian group in Section
11
. The most interesting such groups may be the additive group of integers and those of the integers modulo a natural number.

2.4.2.
Walk gains

We now define a crucial concept: the gain sum of a sign circuit
C
. First we define that of a walk or ultrawalk
W
. We want a definition that makes the gain of
W
invariant under reorientation, that agrees with the gain of a walk in a gain graph, and that agrees with the vector model in Section
3
; this implies a complicated definition that is not invariant under sign switching. We choose an orientation
\tau
of
\Sigma
. Then the gain of a walk
W=u_{0}e_{1}u_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{l}u_{l}
is

(2.1)

\displaystyle\varphi(W)

\displaystyle:=-\tau(u_{0},e_{1})\sum_{i=1}^{l}\varphi(e_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}[-\tau(u_{j},e_{j})\tau(u_{j},e_{j+1})]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{l}\varphi(e_{i})\sigma(W_{0,i-1})\tau(u_{i-1},e_{i}),

where
W_{0,i-1}
denotes the part of
W
from
u_{0}
to
u_{i-1}
. The factor
[-\tau(u_{0},e_{1})]
is a general sign correction that is positive if the first edge leaves
u_{0}
, negative if it enters
u_{0}
. The factors
[-\tau(u_{j},e_{j})\tau(u_{j},e_{j+1})]
are positive at a coherent vertex
u_{j}
and negative, thus reversing the signs with which following edge gains are added, at an incoherent vertex. That is,
\varphi(e_{i})
is added to the sum if it is preceded by an even number of incoherent vertices and subtracted if preceded by an odd number of incoherent vertices.

The gain of an ultrawalk is similar but we have to take account of some extreme cases, such as an ultrawalk that consists only of one half edge. Let
W=e_{0}u_{0}e_{1}u_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{l}u_{l}e_{l+1}
be an ultrawalk, in which
e_{0}
and
e_{l+1}
are half edges. Its gain is given by

(2.2)

\displaystyle\varphi(W)

\displaystyle:=\tau(u_{0},e_{0})\varphi(e_{0})+\varphi(W_{0l})-\varphi(e_{l+1})\sigma(W_{0l})\tau(u_{l},e_{l+1}),

where the first term is omitted if
W
has no initial half edge and the last term is omitted if
W
has no final half edge.

The gain of a walk of length
1
may not equal the gain of the edge
e_{1}
; that depends on the orientation of
e_{1}
. Considering the walk
u_{0}e_{1}u_{1}
, we see that

(2.3)

\varphi(u_{0}e_{1}u_{1})=-\tau(u_{0},e_{1})\varphi(e_{1}).

Similarly, the gain of an ultrawalk
e_{0}u_{0}
or
u_{0}e_{1}
is given respectively by

(2.4)

\varphi(e_{0}u_{0})=\tau(u_{0},e_{0})\varphi(e_{0})\text{ and }\varphi(u_{0}e_{1})=-\tau(u_{0},e_{1})\varphi(e_{1}).

Lemma 2.5
.

The gain of a walk or ultrawalk is invariant under reorientation and sign switching, except that it is negated by sign switching the initial vertex.

Proof.

Reorienting an edge
e_{i}
changes the sign of both
\varphi(e_{i})
and
\tau(u_{i-1},e_{i})
, so it has no effect on the term of
e_{i}
. For
j>i
the sign of
W_{0,j-1}
remains the same. Thus, there is no change to
\varphi(W)
.

Switching the initial vertex negates
\tau(u_{0},e_{1})
(and
\tau(u_{0},e_{0})
if there is an initial half edge or if
W
is closed so
u_{l}=u_{0}
and we can take
e_{0}=e_{l}
), so it negates the sum in (
2.1
). Switching the final vertex has no effect on the sum. Switching an internal vertex has no effect on coherence, so it does not change the sum.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

For a walk or ultrawalk
W
, the gain of
W^{-1}
is given by
\varphi(W^{-1})=-\sigma(W)\varphi(W)
.

Proof.

We compute for a walk:

\displaystyle\varphi(W)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{l}\varphi(e_{i})\sigma(W_{0,i-1})\tau(u_{i-1},e_{i})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{l}\varphi(e_{i})\sigma(W)\sigma(W^{-1}_{l,i})\sigma(e_{i})\tau(u_{i-1},e_{i})

\displaystyle=\sigma(W)\sum_{i=1}^{l}\varphi(e_{i})\sigma(W^{-1}_{l,i})[\tau(u_{i},e_{i})]=-\sigma(W)\varphi(W^{-1}).

For an ultrawalk with half edges at both ends,

\displaystyle\varphi(W)

\displaystyle=\tau(u_{0},e_{0})\varphi(e_{0})+\varphi(W_{0l})-\varphi(e_{l+1})\sigma(W_{0l})\tau(u_{l},i_{l+1})

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{0l})[\sigma(W_{0l})\tau(u_{0},e_{0})\varphi(e_{0})+\sigma(W_{0l})\varphi(W_{0l})-\varphi(e_{l+1})\tau(u_{l},i_{l+1})]

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{0l})[\varphi(e_{l+1})(-\tau(u_{l},i_{l+1})+\varphi(W_{l0})-\sigma(W_{0l})(-\tau(u_{0},e_{0}))\varphi(e_{0})]

\displaystyle=-\sigma(W)\varphi(W^{-1})

by (
2.2
), because for the formula in the direction of
W^{-1}
, the half-edge orientations reverse. For an ultrawalk with a half edge at only one end, omit one term in the preceding expressions.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

If
W
is the concatenation of walks,
W=W_{1}W_{2}
, then
\varphi(W)=\varphi(W_{1})+\sigma(W_{1})\varphi(W_{2})
. The same holds for the concatenation of any two ultrawalks
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
for which
W_{1}
ends and
W_{2}
begins at the same vertex.

Proof.

This follows directly from the second form of Equation
2.1
, with appropriate modifications for half edges.
∎

The complicated definitions (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) call for explanation. For simplicity assume
W
is a path and
\tau(u_{0},e_{0})=+1
(
e_{0}
leaves
u_{0}
). Consider first a path of positive edges, all oriented in the direction from the beginning at
u_{0}
to the end at
u_{l}
. Then
-\tau(u_{i-1},e_{i})=+1=\sigma(W_{0,i-1})
, so
\varphi(W)=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\varphi(e_{i})
, simply a sum, consistent with an ordinary gain graph. This consistency is what we want. Suppose, though, that some of the edges (still all positive) are oriented in reverse; then at a backward edge we have
-\tau(u_{i-1},e_{i})=-1
so the gain of
e_{i}
is subtracted, not added. Equivalently, we can view this as first reorienting the backward edges, which negates their gains, and then summing the revised gains as in the first case. Note that reorienting (say) the first backward edge
e_{i}
changes
u_{i-1}
from incoherent to coherent and reverses the coherence of
u_{i}
(this is for
i>1
). Thus, the number of incoherent vertices before
e_{i}
, and only before
e_{i}
, changes parity, while the gain changes sign. This implies that the sign we put on
\varphi(e_{i})
is determined by the number
p
of preceding incoherent vertices in
W
and the sum should contain
(-1)^{p}\varphi(e_{i})
, exactly as stated after Equation (
2.1
).

But what if our path
W
contains negative edges? We may switch it with a suitable sign-switching function
\zeta
to
W^{\zeta}
which is all positive; this does not change the gains of edges nor the state of coherence or incoherence at any vertex. We may choose
\zeta
not to switch the initial vertex, i.e., so that
\zeta(u_{0})=+1
. By Lemma
2.5

\varphi(W^{\zeta})=\varphi(W)
. That is, the sign of
\varphi(e_{i})
in the sum is still determined by the number of preceding incoherent vertices.

2.4.3.
Sign-cycle gains

Consider a positive circle
C
. Its gain
\varphi(C)
is the sum of its edge gains after sign switching and reorientation so it is an all-positive sign cycle. The only uncertainty is the sign of
\varphi(C)
, which depends on the choice of direction around
C
. There is not much else to say about it.

A handcuff
C
is a different story. First we need a more elementary concept.

A
rooted negative figure

(C_{1},v)
is a negative figure
C_{1}
(a circle or half edge) with a distinguished vertex
v
, the
root
.
If
C_{1}
is a circle, to compute its gain we orient it as an excycle rooted at
v
, i.e., so that
\tau(v,e)=-1
for both edges at
v
. Then the
gain of
(C_{1},v)
, written
\varphi(C_{1},v)
, is the gain of a minimal walk around
C
from
v
to
v
. By Lemmas
2.5
and
2.6
and since
C
is negative,
\varphi(C_{1},v)
is independent of the particular orientation and choice of direction, except that it negates if
\tau(v,e)=+1
for the edges at
v
.

If
C_{1}=\{e\}
, consisting of a half edge
e
at vertex
v
, orient it as an excycle, that is with
\tau(v,e)=-1
; the gain
\varphi(C_{1},v)
is the gain of the excycle
\{e\}
considered as an oriented ultrawalk, which by (
2.4
) is
\varphi(e)
since
(C_{1},v)
is an excycle.

Now, orient the edges of the handcuff
C
so it is a sign cycle. If its two negative figures are circles, choose a circuit walk
W
around
C
(which will necessarily be coherent). The gain of
C
is
\varphi(C):=\varphi(W)
. If one negative figure in
C
is a circle and the other is a half edge
e
, we choose
W
to be a circuit ultrawalk that begins and ends with
e
. If both negative figures are half edges, we choose
W
to be a circuit ultrawalk that begins at one half edge and ends at the other (this type is exceptional in that the connecting path is traversed only once).

For a loose edge
e
, its gain as a sign circuit is its gain as an edge.

In the following proposition we compute the gain of a sign circuit.

Proposition 2.8
.

The gain of a sign circuit
C
is well defined up to negation and choice of circuit walk.
For a positive circle
C
, it is the sum of the edge gains when
C
is oriented so every vertex is coherent.
If
C
is a handcuff with rooted negative figures
(C_{1},u_{1})
and
(C_{2},u_{2})
where the roots are the endpoints of the connecting path
P
, and
P
is oriented out of
u_{1}
, then

\varphi(C)=\varphi(C_{1},u_{1})-\varphi(C_{2},u_{2})-2\varphi(P_{u_{1}u_{2}})

(or its negative) if both negative figures are circles,

\varphi(C)=2\varphi(C_{1},u_{1})-\varphi(C_{2},u_{2})-2\varphi(P_{u_{1}u_{2}})

(or its negative) if
C_{1}
is a half edge and
C_{2}
is a circle, and

\varphi(C)=\varphi(C_{1},u_{1})-\varphi(C_{2},u_{2})-\varphi(P_{u_{1}u_{2}})

(or its negative) if both negative figures are half edges.

Proof.

If
C
is a positive circle, this is obvious.
Thus, assume
C
is a handcuff with connecting path
P=P_{u_{1}u_{2}}
from
u_{1}
to
u_{2}
. By sign switching make
P
positive. Note that
\varphi(C_{i})
for a negative circle is computed by taking a walk on it from
u_{i}
to
u_{i}
. For a half edge
\varphi(C_{i})
is computed by taking a walk from
u_{i}
.

First, consider the case where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are circles.
We apply Lemma
2.7
to
W=C_{1}PC_{2}P^{-1}
but we have to be careful about signs.
We get

\displaystyle\varphi(W)

\displaystyle=\varphi(C_{1})+\sigma(C_{1})\varphi(P)+\sigma(C_{1}P)\varphi(C_{2})+\sigma(C_{1}PC_{2})\varphi(P^{-1})

\displaystyle=\varphi(C_{1})-\varphi(P)-\varphi(C_{2})+\varphi(P^{-1})

\displaystyle=\varphi(C_{1})-\varphi(C_{2})-2\varphi(P)

because
\varphi(P^{-1})=-\varphi(P)
by Lemma
2.6
.

If we interchanged the roles of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, taking the walk
W^{\prime}=C_{2}P^{-1}C_{1}P
, we would get the negated gain
\varphi(C_{2})-\varphi(P^{-1})-\varphi(C_{1})+\varphi(P)=\varphi(C_{2})-\varphi(C_{1})+2\varphi(P)
; that explains the ambiguity of negation.

Second, consider the case where
C_{1}=\{e_{1}\}
for a half edge
e_{1}
and
C_{2}
is a circle.
Take the circuit ultrawalk
W=C_{1}u_{1}Pu_{2}C_{2}u_{2}P^{-1}u_{1}C_{1}
.
We initially walk on
C_{1}
from
e_{1}
to
u_{1}
, which is
C_{1}^{-1}
because
C_{1}
is an excycle for computing
\varphi(C_{1})
.
The calculation gives

\displaystyle\varphi(W)

\displaystyle=\varphi(C_{1}^{-1})+\sigma(C_{1})\varphi(P)+\sigma(C_{1}P)\varphi(C_{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\sigma(C_{1}PC_{2})\varphi(P^{-1})+\sigma(C_{1}PC_{2}P^{-1})\varphi(C_{1})

\displaystyle=-\sigma(C_{1})\varphi(C_{1})-\varphi(P)-\varphi(C_{2})+\varphi(P^{-1})+\varphi(C_{1})

by Lemma
2.6
applied to
C_{1}
,

\displaystyle=2\varphi(C_{1})-\varphi(C_{2})-2\varphi(P).

Finally, consider the case where
C_{1}=\{e_{1}\}
and
C_{2}=\{e_{2}\}
are both half-edge figures.
Take the circuit ultrawalk
W=e_{1}u_{1}Pu_{2}e_{2}
.
Then

\displaystyle\varphi(W)

\displaystyle=\varphi(C_{1}^{-1})+\sigma(C_{1})\varphi(P)+\sigma(C_{1}P)\varphi(C_{2})

\displaystyle=\varphi(C_{1})-\varphi(P)-\varphi(C_{2}).

Independence of the choice of circuit walk follows from the formulas for
\varphi(C)
.
∎

Now we can make the key definition.

Definition 2.9

(Hyperbalance)
.

A sign circuit is
neutral
if its gain is
0
.
An arbitrary edge set or subgraph is
hyperbalanced
if every sign circuit in it is neutral; otherwise it is
hyperfrustrated
.

Neutrality is the fundamental fact about an edge set. It is independent of orientation, because by Lemmas
2.5
and
2.6
neutrality of a sign circuit is independent of orientation. The list of neutral sign circuits is the essential datum upon which we base the definition of the matroid of a gain signed graph.

2.4.4.
Gain switching

Gains can be switched by a gain-switching function, briefly
gain switcher
, which is a function
\theta:V\to\mathbf{K}^{+}
(until Section
11
). The gain function
\varphi
switches to
\varphi^{\theta}
defined on an oriented edge
e=e_{vw}
by

\displaystyle\varphi^{\theta}(e)

\displaystyle:=\tau(v,e)\theta(v)+\varphi(e)+\tau(w,e)\theta(w)

\displaystyle=\varphi(e)+\tau(v,e)[\theta(v)-\sigma(e)\theta(w)].

For example, if
\theta
is a constant, then
\varphi^{\theta}(e)=\varphi(e)+\tau(v,e)(1-\sigma(e))\theta
.
For a half edge
e=e_{v}
,

\varphi^{\theta}(e):=\tau(v,e)\theta(v)+\varphi(e).

Gain switching has no effect on a loose edge.

Very similar is the concept of a
gain potential
for an edge set
S
, which is a mapping
\bar{\theta}:V\to\mathbf{K}^{+}
such that

\varphi(e_{vw})=\tau(v,e)\bar{\theta}(v)+\tau(w,e)\bar{\theta}(w)

for every link or loop in
S
,
\varphi(e_{v})=\tau(v,e)\bar{\theta}(v)
for every half edge in
S
, and
\varphi(e_{\varnothing})=0
for every loose edge in
S
. A gain potential for
S
is the negative of a gain switcher that switches
S
to all neutral gains. For a gain switcher
\theta
we define

\displaystyle E(\theta)

\displaystyle:=(\varphi^{\theta})^{-1}(0)

\displaystyle\,=\{e_{vw}\in E:\varphi(e_{vw})=-\tau(v,e)\theta(v)-\tau(w,e)\theta(w)\}

\displaystyle\qquad\cup\{e_{v}\in E:\varphi(e_{v})=-\tau(v,e)\theta(v)\}

\displaystyle\qquad\cup E^{00},

i.e., it is the largest edge set such that
-\theta
is a gain potential for these edges.
Combining gain and sign potentials,
E(\theta,\zeta):=E(\theta)\cap E(\zeta)
, which is the set of edges with sign determined by
\zeta
and gain by
\theta
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Switching the gains does not change the gain sum of a positive closed walk or an ultrawalk with a half edge at each end. More generally, switching the gains on a walk or ultrawalk
W
from
u_{0}
to
u_{l}
(
l\geq 0
) gives switched gain

\displaystyle\varphi^{\theta}(u_{0}e_{1}\cdots u_{l})

\displaystyle=-\theta(u_{0})+\varphi(W)+\sigma(W)\theta(u_{l}),

\displaystyle\varphi^{\theta}(e_{0}u_{0}e_{1}\cdots u_{l})

\displaystyle=-\theta(u_{0})+\varphi(W),

\displaystyle\varphi^{\theta}(u_{0}e_{1}\cdots u_{l}e_{l+1})

\displaystyle=\varphi(W)+\sigma(W_{0l})\theta(u_{l}),

\displaystyle\varphi^{\theta}(e_{0}u_{0}e_{1}\cdots u_{l}e_{l+1})

\displaystyle=\varphi(W_{0l}),

Proof.

We check by a calculation, which we present for an ultrawalk with a half edge at each end:

\displaystyle\varphi^{\theta}(W)

\displaystyle=\tau(u_{0},e_{0})[\varphi(e_{0})+\tau(u_{0},e_{0})\theta(u_{0})]

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{l}[\tau(u_{i-1},e_{i})\theta(u_{i-1})+\varphi(e_{i})+\tau(u_{i},e_{i})\theta(u_{i})]\sigma(W_{0,i-1})\tau(u_{i-1},e_{i})

\displaystyle\quad-[\tau(u_{l},e_{l+1})\theta(u_{l})+\varphi(e_{l+1})]\sigma(W_{0l})\tau(u_{l},e_{l+1})

\displaystyle=\theta(u_{0})-\sum_{i=1}^{l}[\theta(u_{i-1})+\tau(u_{i},e_{i})\tau(u_{i-1},e_{i})\theta(u_{i})]\sigma(W_{0,i-1})

\displaystyle\quad-\theta(u_{l})\sigma(W_{0l})+\varphi(W)

\displaystyle=\theta(u_{0})-\sum_{i=1}^{l}\theta(u_{i-1})\sigma(W_{0,i-1})+\sum_{i=1}^{l}\theta(u_{i})\sigma(W_{0i})-\theta(u_{l})\sigma(W_{0l})+\varphi(W)

\displaystyle=[\theta(u_{0})-\theta(u_{l})\sigma(W_{0l})]-\theta(u_{0})+\theta(u_{l})\sigma(W_{0l})+\varphi(W).

The two terms in square brackets apply when there is an initial half edge
e_{0}
(the first term) or a terminal half edge
e_{l+1}
(the second term). The stated formulas follow from this.

Evidently,
\varphi^{\theta}(W)
equals
\varphi(W)
when
W
is closed and positive and also when it begins and ends with a half edge.
∎

The effect of switching on the gain of a walk does not depend on orientation.

Theorem 2.11
.

Switching of signs and gains preserves the property of hyperbalance or hyperunbalance of every edge set.

Proof.

For sign switching this is obvious. The gain sum of a sign circuit equals the gain sum of a circuit walk on it, and by Lemma
2.10
switching preserves that sum, so a neutral or non-neutral sign circuit remains neutral or non-neutral, respectively.
∎

For stating the next lemma we define a

Lemma 2.12

Let

For connected

\theta_{0}=\varphi(T_{re})

We may assume

Consider an edge

v=u_{l-1}
, and
w=u_{l}
). The switched gain is given by

\displaystyle 0=\varphi^{\theta}(e)=\tau(u_{l-1},e)\theta(u_{l-1})+\varphi(e)+\tau(u_{l},e)\theta(u_{l}),

from which it follows that

\displaystyle\sigma(T_{rw})\theta(w)

\displaystyle=\sigma(T_{ru_{l-1}})\theta(u_{l-1})+\sigma(T_{ru_{l-1}})\tau(u_{l-1},e_{l-1,l})\varphi(e_{l-1,l})

\displaystyle=\theta(r)+\sum_{i=1}^{l}\sigma(T_{ru_{i-1}})\tau(u_{i-1},e_{i-1,i})\varphi(e_{i-1,i})

\displaystyle=\theta(r)-\varphi(T_{rw})

by (
2.1
). This gives the value of
\theta(w)
, with an arbitrary constant
\theta(r)
that we call
\theta_{0}
, provided there is no half edge in
T
.

If there is a half edge
e
at vertex

, its switched gain is
0=\varphi^{\theta}(e)=\tau(v,e)\theta(v)+\varphi(e),
hence
\theta(v)=-\tau(v,e)\varphi(e).
Inserting this into the general formula for
\theta(v)
gives the value of
\theta_{0}
:

\theta_{0}=\varphi(T_{rv})-\sigma(T_{rv})\tau(v,e)\varphi(e)=\varphi(T_{re})

by (

).
∎

It is easy to see that, if
T
has no half edge, any one choice of root gives all possible switching functions by varying
\theta_{0}
. A similar proof establishes that the gains on
T
can be set by gain switching to any desired values; we omit the details.
We mention that if

is all positive and is oriented as an out-arborescence from

, then
\varphi(T_{rv})
equals the sum of the gains of edges in
T_{rv}
.

Theorem 2.13
.

The gain signed graph
\Upsilon
is hyperbalanced if and only if its gains can be switched so all edges are neutral.

Proof.

Sufficiency is trivial. For necessity, we may assume
\Upsilon
is connected and, by suitably switching signs, has an all-positive spanning tree
T
. By reorientation make all negative edges (including half edges) introverted. By gain-switching as in Lemma
2.12
we can make all tree gains
0
.

First, we show all positive edges have gain
0
. Let
e
be such an edge, not in
T
. The unique circle
C_{T}(e)\subseteq T\cup\{e\}
is all positive, hence a sign circuit, hence it has gain
0
. From the definition,
\varphi(C_{T}(e))=\pm\varphi(e)
; that implies
\varphi(e)=0
.

Next, we show all negative links and loops have the same gain. Two such edges,
e
and
f
, form circles
C_{T}(e)
and
C_{T}(f)
, whose intersection may be either a path of positive length, or at most a single vertex. In the former case,
C_{T}(e)\cup C_{T}(f)
is a theta graph in which there is a positive circle
C
containing both negative edges. Take a walk
W=u_{0}eu_{1}\cdots u_{i-1}fu_{i}\cdots u_{0}
around
C
; then
\varphi(W)=-\tau(u_{0},e)\varphi(e)-\sigma(W_{0,i-1})\tau(u_{i-1},f)\varphi(f)=\varphi(e)-\varphi(f)
. As
C
is hyperbalanced,
\varphi(e)=\varphi(f)
. In the latter case,
T\cup\{e,f\}
contains a handcuff circuit
C
in which
e
and
f
belong to opposite circles; by Proposition
2.8
a circuit walk around
C
gives the same conclusion that
\varphi(e)=\varphi(f)
.

Consider two half edges,
e
and
f
. They are joined by a path
P
of length
l
in
T
with which they make an ultrawalk
W
, which is a circuit walk on the circuit
C=P\cup\{e,f\}
. As in the previous case, by Proposition
2.8

\varphi(W)=\varphi(e)-\varphi(f)
so
\varphi(e)=\varphi(f)
by hyperbalance. That is, all half edges have the same gain.

Now compare a half edge
e
to a negative link or loop
f
.
T\cup\{e,f\}
contains a handcuff circuit
C
in which
f
belongs to a negative circle and
\varphi(C)=2\varphi(e)-\varphi(f)=0
. Thus, the gain of a half edge equals half that of a negative link or loop.

Let the common value of
\varphi(e)
for all negative links and loops be
\varphi_{0}
and switch by the constant switching function
\theta\equiv\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{0}
; or if there are no such edges, let
\varphi_{1}
be the gain of a half edge and switch by the constant function
\theta\equiv\varphi_{1}
. This reduces the gains of all negative edges to
0
.

Hyperbalance implies that all loose edges are neutral. Thus, all edges have gain
0
after gain switching.

Finally, switch the signs back with the original sign switcher. This does not change the gains, which remain identically
0
.
∎

Now that we have three separate operations, two switchings and a reorientation, that preserve essential properties, it is desirable to know how they commute.
Sign and gain switching, on the other hand, do not commute. Define an action of
\zeta:V\to\{\pm 1\}
on
\theta:V\to\mathbf{K}^{+}
by
\theta^{\zeta}(v):=\zeta(v)\theta(v)
.

Proposition 2.14

(Commutation of Switching and Reorientation)
.

The commutation relations among sign switchers
\zeta
, gain switchers
\theta
, and reorientation functions
\rho
are
\zeta\theta=\theta^{\zeta}\zeta
,
\zeta\rho=\rho\zeta
, and
\theta\rho=\rho\theta
.

Proof.

We leave to the reader the proof that reorientation commutes freely with both switchings. Hint: Define a reorientation function
\rho:E\to\{\pm 1\}
such that
\varphi^{\rho}(e)=\rho(e)\varphi(e)
and
\tau^{\rho}(v,e)=\rho(e)\tau(v,e)
(assuming
v
is an endpoint of
e
), i.e.,
\rho
acts on
\varphi
and
\tau
by multiplication.

Under the action of
\zeta
, the orientation
\tau
required for gain switching changes to
\tau^{\zeta}:=\zeta\tau
, i.e.,
\tau^{\zeta}(v,e)=\zeta(v)\tau(v,e)
. The actions of
\theta\zeta
and
\zeta\theta
on
\varphi
are given by

\displaystyle\varphi^{\theta\zeta}(e_{vw}):=(\varphi^{\theta})^{\zeta}(e_{vw})

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\theta}(e_{vw})

because sign switching does not change gains,

\displaystyle=\tau(v,e)\theta(v)+\varphi(e_{vw})+\tau(w,e)\theta(w)

for
e=e_{vw}
, while

\displaystyle\varphi^{\zeta\theta}(e_{vw}):=(\varphi^{\zeta})^{\theta}(e_{vw})

\displaystyle=\tau^{\zeta}(v,e)\theta(v)+\varphi^{\zeta}(e_{vw})+\tau^{\zeta}(w,e)\theta(w)

\displaystyle=\zeta(v)\tau(v,e)\theta(v)+\varphi(e_{vw})+\zeta(w)\tau(w,e)\theta(w)

\displaystyle=\tau(v,e)\theta^{\zeta}(v)+\varphi(e_{vw})+\tau(w,e)\theta^{\zeta}(w)

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\theta^{\zeta}}(e_{vw})=\varphi^{\theta^{\zeta}\zeta}(e_{vw})

according to the preceding calculation.
Note that in
\varphi^{\theta^{\zeta}\zeta}
,
\zeta
acts on
\tau
after
\theta^{\zeta}
is applied to
\varphi
, while in
\varphi^{\zeta\theta}
,
\zeta
acts on
\tau
before
\theta^{\zeta}
is applied. The action on
\tau
is why
\zeta\theta=\theta^{\zeta}\zeta\neq\theta^{\zeta}
.
∎

Proposition
2.14
suggests that the full switching group, which combines both kinds of switching, might be a semidirect product
(\mathbf{K}^{+})^{V}\rtimes\{+1,-1\}^{V}
, but it is not; the associative law fails, as the reader can verify.

The extra point

In Section
4
we will define a matroid on the edge set of
\Upsilon
that has a one-point extension by an
extra point

e_{\infty}
, which is not part of the graph.
The extension is necessary for a full understanding of the matroid and its canonical hyperplane representation.

3.
The vector model

Our definition of the matroid of
\Upsilon
is modeled on a mental picture of vectors over a field

, so we begin the main work with that picture. We are in
\mathbf{K}^{1+n}
with coordinates

\{\mathbf{b}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{n}\}
. The

\pi_{0}:\mathbf{K}^{1+n}\to\mathbf{K}^{n}

-coordinate.

Here is the vector associated to an edge

\tau

\mathbf{z}(e)=\mathbf{z}_{\tau}(e):=\varphi(e)\mathbf{b}_{0}+\tau(v_{i},e)\mathbf{b}_{i}+\tau(v_{j},e)\mathbf{b}_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi(e)\\
0\\
\vdots\\
\tau(v_{i},e)\\
0\\
\vdots\\
\tau(v_{j},e)\\
0\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix},

where the nonzero rows are numbered 0,
i

e
is a loop, so
v_{i}=v_{j}

\tau

of the matrix form. Thus, a positive loop has
x_{i}=0
and a negative loop has

For a half edge

\mathbf{z}(e)=\mathbf{z}_{\tau}(e):=\varphi(e)\mathbf{b}_{0}+\tau(v_{i},e)\mathbf{b}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi(e)\\
0\\
\vdots\\
\tau(v_{i},e)\\
0\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}.

For a loose edge

\mathbf{z}(e)=\mathbf{z}_{\tau}(e):=\varphi(e)\mathbf{b}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi(e)\\
\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}

This defines a mapping
\mathbf{z}:E\to\mathbf{K}^{1+n}
.
We call the vectors corresponding to the edges of
\Upsilon
the

of
\Upsilon
. Note that
\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{x}

2.4

The extra point

corresponds to the vector

\mathbf{z}
to a mapping
\mathbf{z}:E_{\infty}\to\mathbf{K}^{1+n}

The
incidence matrix
of
\Upsilon
is the
(1+n)\times|E|
matrix that has a column for each edge, in which is placed the standard vector
\mathbf{z}(e)

We mention incidence matrices because they have many uses, such as to define flows on the graph, but those uses are outside the scope of this article.

Given a sign switcher
\zeta

Lemma 3.1
.

Some elementary properties of the vector representation are:

(1)

\mathbf{z}(e^{-1})=-\mathbf{z}(e)

(2)

Switching signs by

Given an orientation

\Sigma

(3.1)

Lemma 3.2

Proof.

The expressions for

Proposition 3.3

(I)

For a walk

(II)

We prove part (I). Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}(W)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathbf{z}(e_{k})\sigma(W_{0,k})\tau(u_{k},e_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\big[\varphi(e_{k})\mathbf{b}_{0}+\tau(u_{k-1},e_{k})\mathbf{b}_{i_{k-1}}+\tau(u_{k},e_{k})\mathbf{b}_{i_{k}}\big]\sigma(W_{0,k})\tau(u_{k},e_{k})

Corollary 3.5
.

A sign circuit is a circuit in the vector model if and only if it has gain
0
. Otherwise, it is linearly independent

By “in the vector model” we mean the vectors that represent the edges of the sign circuit.

Proof.

A proper subset
S
of a sign circuit
C
is independent in the frame matroid
\mathbf{K}(\Sigma)
and hence the set
\{\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(e):e\in S\}
of projected vectors is independent. It follows that
\{\mathbf{z}(e):e\in S\}
is independent.

The linear combination of vectors
\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(e)
for

that yields
\mathbf{0}
is unique (up to scaling) because the vectors are minimally dependent. Therefore, the vectors
\mathbf{z}(e)
can be dependent only if that linear combination has

3.3

∎

This is a partial solution to finding the circuits of the matroid
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
. The full answer is complicated, so we turn in the next section to the simpler question of rank.

Example 3.6

(Edge Points of an Edge Polytope
[
11
]
)
.

The example that led us to gains on signed graphs comes from algebra. For an edge
e_{ij}
in a graph
\Gamma
(all of whose edges are links and loops) its edge point is
\mathbf{b}_{i}+\mathbf{b}_{j}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
(which corresponds to the vector
\mathbf{z}(-e_{ij})=\mathbf{b}_{0}+\mathbf{b}_{i}+\mathbf{b}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
of the extraverted orientation of
-e_{ij}
). The convex hull
P
of the edge points, known as the
edge polytope
of
\Gamma
, is related to binomial ideals. Ohsugi and Hibi found that the affine dimension of the edge polytope (assuming
\Gamma
is connected) is
n-2
if
\Gamma
is bipartite and
n-1
if it is not
[
11
, Proposition 1.3]
. Restated in terms of signed graphs: the dimension is
n-2
if
-\Gamma
is balanced and 1 greater if it is not. As affine dimension is 1 less than matroid rank, this corresponds exactly to the rank of the frame matroid
\mathbf{F}(-\Gamma)
and should be deducible by matroid theory. In developing our theory we wanted to generalize to arbitrary oriented signed graphs, where the edge points of this example correspond to extraverted edges. For a point in affine space its vector has the extra coordinate
x_{0}=1
, thus the gains are identically 1. We found it easiest and most enlightening to allow arbitrary gains (as far as possible). Hence, gain signed graphs.

We give a matroidal proof of Ohsugi and Hibi’s Proposition 1.3. When all edges are negative, the sign-balanced circles are those of even length; thus, the sign circuits are the even circles and the handcuffs with two odd circles.
Taking gains identically 1 implies that every sign circuit is neutral so the gain signed graph
\Upsilon=(\Gamma,-1,1)
(that is, all edges being negative with gain 1) is hyperbalanced.
Now we anticipate the rank function of Section
4
. Let
b(S)
be the number of bipartite components of an edge set
S
(considered as a spanning subgraph). An edge set
S
has rank
n-b(S)
. For a connected graph, therefore,
\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(E)=n-1
if
\Gamma
is bipartite and
n
if not. The rank is the linear dimension of the vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, but since the edge vectors
\mathbf{b}_{i}+\mathbf{b}_{j}
are contained in the inhomogeneous hyperplane
\sum x_{i}=2
, their affine dimension is one less; that is,
\dim P=n-2
if
\Gamma
is bipartite and
n-1
otherwise.

Example 3.7

(Edge Points for a Bidirected Graph)
.

The edge points of Example
3.6
are the columns of the unoriented incidence matrix of the graph. (To define that matrix, reverse the previous sentence.) After the edge polytope was introduced, Matsui et al.
[
10
]
) considered the analog for an oriented incidence matrix, which is the incidence matrix of an all-positive signed graph. (To get that matrix, take an all-positive gain signed graph and delete the row of gains.) An oriented positive edge is an ordinary directed edge. Our theory enables us to state the affine dimension of the points corresponding to directed edges, which may differ from their linear dimension (see the next example). In fact, we can state a general theorem for the points obtained from any edges of any bidirected graph. Again, we anticipate Theorem
4.2
.

First we examine coherent and incoherent vertices of a closed walk in a bidirected graph. Let
W=u_{0}e_{1}u_{1}\cdots e_{l}u_{l}
be a closed walk; that is,
u_{0}=u_{l}
. For convenience, define
e_{l+1}=e_{1}
. The sign of
W
is

\sigma(W)=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\sigma(e_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\big[-\tau(u_{i-1},e_{i})\tau(u_{i},e_{i})\big]=(-1)^{l}\prod_{i=1}^{l}\tau(u_{i-1})\prod_{i=1}^{l}\tau(u_{i},e_{i}).

Define the incoherence sign for vertex
u_{i}
to be
\iota(u_{i})=+1
if
W
is coherent at
u_{i}
and
-1
if
W
is incoherent; that includes
u_{l}=u_{0}
with edges
e_{l}
and
e_{l+1}=e_{1}
. Thus,
\iota(u_{i})=-\tau(u_{i},e_{i})\tau(u_{i},e_{i+1})
. Then the product of all vertex signs of
W
is

\prod_{i=1}^{l}\iota(u_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\big[-\tau(u_{i},e_{i})\tau(u_{i},e_{i+1})\big]=(-1)^{l}\prod_{i=1}^{l}\tau(u_{i-1})\prod_{i=1}^{l}\tau(u_{i},e_{i})=\sigma(W).

In other words:

Lemma 3.8
.

The number of incoherent vertices in a closed walk
W
in a bidirected graph is even if and only if
W
is positive.

Now we define poise of a closed walk
W
. We assign the edges to two sets,
A
and
B
. We put
e_{1}
into set
A
and for each edge
e_{i}
in one of the sets, we put
e_{i+1}
into the same set if
u_{i}
is coherent and the opposite set if it is incoherent. This gives a well-defined bipartition of the edges of
W
into sets
A
and
B
if and only if both the number of changes of set, which equals the number of incoherent vertices, is even, i.e.,
W
is positive, and also a repeated edge is assigned to the same set in every appearance in
W
. We define
W
to be
poised
if the bipartition is well defined and
|A|=|B|
.

We also define poise for a walk
W
from a half edge to a half edge (which may be the same edge). We put the initial half edge into
A
and apply the same rule as before. We get a well-defined bipartition of the edges of
W
if and only if a repeated edge is assigned to the same set at every appearance. We say
W
is
poised
if the bipartition is well defined and
|A|=|B|
.

Now we can state the theorem about edge points. A sign circuit is poised if its circuit walk is poised; since a sign circuit walk is either positive or begins and ends at a half edge, this definition is independent of the choice of circuit walk.

Theorem 3.9

(Dimension of Bidirected Edge Points)
.

In a bidirected graph
\vec{\Sigma}
let
S\subseteq E
. The affine dimension of the point set
\mathbf{x}(S)
in
\mathbb{A}^{d}(\mathbb{R})
is
n-b_{\Sigma}(S)
if every sign circuit in
S
is poised and
n-b_{\Sigma}(S)+1
if not.

Proof.

An affine point
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
corresponds to the vector
\mathbf{b}_{0}+\mathbf{a}
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
. It is well known that the affine dimension of a set of affine points equals the linear dimension of the corresponding vectors. Thus, we are assigning gain
1
to every edge of
\vec{\Sigma}
, forming a gain signed graph
\Upsilon
. A sign circuit with these gains is neutral if and only if it is poised. Theorem
4.2
then gives the rank of an edge set:
\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(S):=n-b_{\Sigma}(S)+\delta_{\Upsilon}(S)
, where
\delta_{\Upsilon}(S)=0
if every sign circuit in
S
is poised, and otherwise is
1
. As we observe immediately after Theorem
4.2
, the rank of
S
equals the dimension of
\mathbf{z}(S)
, which equals the affine dimension of
\mathbf{x}(S)
by the relation between affine points and their corresponding vectors.
∎

Example 3.10

(Arc Adjacency Polytope
[
11
,
6
]
)
.

As a special case of the previous example, suppose all edges in
\Sigma
are positive; i.e.,
\vec{\Sigma}
is a directed graph
\vec{\Gamma}
. The sign circuits are the circles. A circle is poised if and only if it has equal numbers of directed edges in each direction. The convex hull of the set
\mathbf{x}(E)
of affine points of a symmetric digraph (where for every arc there is also the opposite arc) has been called its symmetric edge polytope
[
10
]
and also its adjacency polytope (e.g.,
[
6
]
); as in
[
6
]
we generalize this to the
arc polytope
of any directed graph (an arc being a directed edge). Let
c(\Gamma)
denote the number of connected components of
\vec{\Gamma}
.

Corollary 3.11

(Dimension of Arc Polytope)
.

For a directed graph
\vec{\Gamma}
, the affine dimension of its arc polytope is
n-c(\Gamma)
if every circle in
\vec{\Gamma}
is poised and
n-c(\Gamma)+1
if not.

Example 3.12

(Double Arc Adjacency Polytope
[
7
]
)
.

There is a second polytope that has been called the adjacency polytope. Again suppose we have a directed graph
\vec{\Gamma}=(V,E)
. We represent an arc
(v_{i},v_{j})
by the point
(\mathbf{b}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{j})\in\mathbb{A}^{2n}(\mathbb{R})
, thus in twice the dimension of the adjacency polytope; we call the convex hull of these
double arc points
the
double adjacency polytope
of
\vec{\Gamma}
. Chen and Mehta
[
7
]
introduced this polytope for the special case of a symmetric digraph (which they regarded as an undirected graph). Note that if we represent
(v_{i},v_{j})
by
f(v_{i},v_{j})=(-\mathbf{b}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{j})
, nothing essential changes in the structure of the set
f(E)
: dimension and convexity remain the same. We use
f
as the representation for the rest of this example.

We determine the dimension of a set of double arc points by a new digraph
\tilde{\Gamma}
, which is a vertex doubling of
\vec{\Gamma}
. The vertex set is
\tilde{V}=V_{+}\cup V_{-}
, where
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
are disjoint copies of
V
whose vertices corresponding to
v\in V
are respectively
v_{+}
and
v_{-}
, and for each arc
(v_{i},v_{j})
we create an arc
(v_{i-},v_{j+})
in
\tilde{\Gamma}
. Note that every circle in
\tilde{\Gamma}
is poised because its vertices alternate between
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
, so its arcs reverse direction at every vertex. Therefore, Corollary
3.11
applies and we have the following description of dimension:

Corollary 3.13

(Dimension of Double Arc Polytope)
.

The affine dimension of the double arc polytope of a digraph
\vec{\Gamma}
equals
2n-c(\tilde{\Gamma})
, which is also its linear dimension.

Completion of Proof.

The linear dimension is the same as the affine dimension because it equals the rank of the incidence matrix of
\vec{\Gamma}
, which is
2n-c(\tilde{\Gamma})
.
∎

Of course, this corollary can be applied to any subset of the arcs.

A special case of particular interest is that in which
\vec{\Gamma}
is symmetric and has all loops
(v_{i},v_{i})
. Then
c(\tilde{\Gamma})=c(\Gamma)
, which is a nice simplification.

4.
Matroid: rank

We want a combinatorial description of the matroid obtained by representing each edge of
\Upsilon
by a vector as in Section
3
.
The problem is to describe the linear dependence matroid of the vectors without reference to linear algebra. Thus, we want combinatorial formulas for the rank function, the circuits, the independent sets and bases, the closed sets (or flats), and the coatoms of the lattice of flats, whose complements are the matroid cocircuits. We call this the
matroid of
\Upsilon
and denote it by
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
. We base the matroid on its rank function.

The matroid has a natural one-point extension, which we denote by
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)=\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)\cup\{e_{\infty}\}
, to the extra point.
Whereas the ground set of
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
is
E=E(\Upsilon)
, that of
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
is
E_{\infty}=E\cup\{e_{\infty}\}
. We call
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
the
extended matroid
of
\Upsilon
.
It is a one-point coextension of the frame matroid
\mathbf{F}(\Sigma)
; that is,
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)/e_{\infty}=\mathbf{F}(\Sigma)
. (One purpose of the extra point is to implement this property of
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
.)
A subset of
E_{\infty}
is defined to be hyperfrustrated if it contains
e_{\infty}
; if not, it is a subset of
E
whose treatment is as we have already described. Hence, in the matroid the extra point behaves like a non-neutral loose edge (and we treat it as such in proofs), although its significance is different in that it implements the coextension of
\mathbf{F}(\Sigma)
.

The purpose of the extra point will become clearer in Section
5
. For the present we only mention that, if all edges are positive, so that
\Upsilon
is a gain graph
\Phi=(\Gamma,\varphi)
, then
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
is the lift matroid
\mathbf{L}(\Phi)
and
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
is the extended lift matroid
\mathbf{L}_{\infty}(\Phi)
(formerly written
\mathbf{L}_{0}
) of
[
26
, Section 3]
.

Definition 4.1
.

The
rank
of an extended edge set
S\subseteq E_{\infty}
is

\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(S):=n-b_{\Sigma}(S)+\delta_{\Upsilon}(S),

where

\delta_{\Upsilon}(S):=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if $S$ is hyperbalanced,}\\
1&\text{ if $S$ is hyperfrustrated.}\end{cases}

We define
\operatorname{rk}(\Upsilon)=\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(E)
.

For an extended edge set
S
,
\mathbf{z}(S)
denotes the multiset of vectors representing
S
, i.e.,
\mathbf{z}(S)=\{\mathbf{z}(e):e\in S\}
. The dimension of an arbitrary (multi)set of vectors means the dimension of its linear span.

Theorem 4.2
.

The function
\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}
defines a matroid on ground set
E_{\infty}
such that

\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(S)=\dim\mathbf{z}(S)

for every edge set
S
.

That matroid on ground set
E
is the matroid
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
, and its extension to
E_{\infty}
is the extended matroid
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
.
Thus, the vector model, i.e., the function
\mathbf{z}:E\to\mathbf{K}^{1+n}
, is a vector representation of this matroid.

Proof.

The first step is to dispose of the extra point. Observe that
\mathbf{z}(e_{\infty})
is a nonzero scalar multiple of
\mathbf{z}(e)
for any non-neutral loose edge
e
. Therefore, for the matroid we can treat
e_{\infty}
as another non-neutral loose edge.

We prove that
\dim\mathbf{z}(S)
satisfies Equation (
4.1
). Then the matroid exists, because
\dim
is a matroid rank function, and it is represented by
\mathbf{z}
.

Because projection cannot increase dimension, for every edge set
S
,

(4.1)

\dim\mathbf{z}(S)\geq\dim\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(S)=\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}(S)=n-b_{\Sigma}(S)

by
[
22
, Theorems 8B.1 and 5.1]
.

Case 1.
S
is hyperbalanced.

By the definition of hyperbalance, every sign circuit has gain sum 0. Thus, by Corollary
3.5
for every sign circuit
C\subseteq S
,
\mathbf{z}(C)
is a circuit in the vector model. It follows that
\mathbf{z}(R)
is dependent for every subset
R\subseteq S
that is dependent in the frame matroid
\mathbf{F}(\Sigma)
. That and (
4.1
) imply that the vector matroid of
\mathbf{z}(S)
is the same as the frame matroid
\mathbf{F}(\Sigma|S)
, so
\dim\mathbf{z}(S)=\dim\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(S)=\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}(S)=n-b_{\Sigma}(S)
.

Case 2.
S
is not hyperbalanced.

By definition, there exists a sign circuit
C\subseteq S
with gain sum nonzero. By Corollary
3.5
,
\mathbf{z}(C)
is independent.

Suppose first that
C=\{e\}
, so
e
is a positive loop or a loose edge. Then
\mathbf{z}(e)=\varphi(e)\mathbf{b}_{0}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Let
B
be a basis for
S
in
\mathbf{F}(\Sigma)
. No nontrivial linear combination of the vectors in
\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(B)
equals
\mathbf{0}
, so there is no way to express
\mathbf{z}(e)
as a linear combination of
\mathbf{z}(B)
. It follows that
\dim\mathbf{z}(B\cup\{e\})=\dim\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(B)+1=\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}(B)+1=n-b_{\Sigma}(S)+1
.

Now consider the general case,
|C|>1
.
Let
e\in C
and
T=C\smallsetminus e
. Extend
T
to a basis
B
of
\mathbf{F}(\Sigma|S)
; thus,
\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(B)
is independent so
\mathbf{z}(B)
is independent. We prove
\mathbf{z}(B\cup\{e\})
is independent.

Suppose by way of contradiction that it is dependent.
Then there exists a unique circuit
\mathbf{z}(A\cup\{e\})
in
\mathbf{z}(B\cup\{e\})
. The projection
\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(A\cup\{e\})
is therefore dependent, which means that
A\cup\{e\}
is dependent in
\mathbf{F}(\Sigma)
. It follows that
A\cup\{e\}
contains the unique sign circuit in
B\cup\{e\}
, i.e.,
C\subseteq A\cup\{e\}
. But equality cannot hold because
\mathbf{z}(C)
is independent; hence,
A\subset T
.

Because
\mathbf{z}(A\cup\{e\})
is a minimal dependent set, there is a unique linear combination
\mathbf{z}(e)=\sum_{f\in A}\alpha_{f}\mathbf{z}(f)
, where all
\alpha_{f}\neq 0
. Because
C
is a sign circuit, there is a unique linear combination
\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(e)=\sum_{g\in T}\beta_{g}\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(g)
, where all
\beta_{g}\neq 0
. So,
\sum_{f\in A}\alpha_{f}\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(f)=\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(e)=\sum_{g\in T}\beta_{g}\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(g)
. Now,
A,T\subseteq B
and
\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(B)
is an independent set in
\mathbf{K}^{n}
; consequently
A=T
and all
\alpha_{f}=\beta_{f}
. However, we saw that
A\supset T
. This contradiction proves that
\mathbf{z}(B\cup\{e\})
is independent, and since
|B|=\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}(S)
, we conclude that
\dim\mathbf{z}(B\cup\{e\})=\operatorname{rk}_{s}(S)+1=n-b_{\Sigma}(S)+1
.

Clearly,
\dim\mathbf{z}(S)\geq\dim\mathbf{z}(B\cup\{e\})=n-b_{\Sigma}(S)+1
. On the other hand,
\dim\mathbf{z}(S)\leq\dim\pi_{0}\mathbf{z}(S)+1=\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}(S)+1=\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}(B)+1=n-b_{\Sigma}(S)+1
. That proves the formula for
\dim\mathbf{z}(S)
in Case 2.
∎

5.
Matroid: closure and flats

Our next mission is to characterize the closed sets of a matroid of a gain signed graph. Recall that for a set
S
of elements of a matroid, the closure of
S
is
\{e:\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(S\cup\{e\})=\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(S)\}
.
Equivalently, the closure of
S
is
S\cup\{e:S\cup\{e\}\mbox{ contains a hypercircuit containing $e$}\}
, but we do not use this characterization since we have not yet found the hypercircuits.

In the matroids
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
and
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
the closure of
S
is called the
hyperclosure
, written
\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(S)
in
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
and
\operatorname{clos}_{\infty}
in
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
.
To define it we use the
gain balance-closure
operator in
\Upsilon
,
\operatorname{bcl}_{\Upsilon}
, defined by

\operatorname{bcl}_{\Upsilon}(S):=S\cup\{e\notin S:\exists\ \text{a neutral sign circuit $C$ such that }e\in C\subseteq S\cup\{e\}\}.

Note that
\operatorname{bcl}_{\Upsilon}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{clos}_{\Sigma}(S)
. Also,
\operatorname{bcl}_{\Upsilon}(S)\supseteq E^{00}
since a loose edge is a sign circuit.
It is easy to prove using a gain potential for
S
and Theorem
2.13
that:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
S\subseteq E
. If
S
is hyperbalanced, then
\operatorname{bcl}_{\Upsilon}(S)
is also hyperbalanced.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
S\subseteq E_{\infty}
. If
S
is hyperbalanced,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(S),

\displaystyle=\operatorname{bcl}_{\Upsilon}(S)

\displaystyle=[E(\theta){:}U_{\Sigma}(S)]\cup[E(\theta,\zeta){:}\pi_{\text{\rm b}}(S)]\cup E^{00}.

where
\zeta
is a sign potential for
S{:}U_{\Sigma}(S)^{c}
and
-\theta
is a gain potential for
S
. The closure is hyperbalanced and is the same in
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
and
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
.

If
S
is hyperfrustrated, then in
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)

(5.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(S)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{clos}_{\Sigma}(S)

\displaystyle=[E{:}U_{\Sigma}(S)]\cup[E(\zeta){:}\pi_{\text{\rm b}}(S)]\cup E^{0},

where
\zeta
is a sign switcher for
S{:}U_{\Sigma}(S)^{c}
. The closure
\operatorname{clos}_{\infty}(S)
in
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
is the same with the addition of
e_{\infty}
.

Note that if
X\in\pi_{\text{\rm b}}(S)
and
E{:}X
is sign balanced, then
E(\theta,\zeta){:}X=E(\theta){:}X
in the first part and
E(\zeta){:}X=E{:}X
in the second part so
\zeta
is not necessary for that component of
S
.

Proof.

Recall that we can treat
e_{\infty}
as a non-neutral loose edge, so it need not be considered separately.

Let us assume
S
is hyperfrustrated, so that
\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(S)=n-b_{\Sigma}(S)+1
.
Let
\zeta
be a sign switcher that switches
S{:}U_{\Sigma}(S)^{c}
to all positive.
Write
A:=[E{:}U_{\Sigma}(S)]\cup[E(\zeta){:}\pi_{\text{\rm b}}(S)]\cup E^{0}
.
It is clear that
S\subseteq A
; thus
\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(A)
and
\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(S)\leq\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(A)
. We want to show that
A
is closed and has the same rank as
S
.

For the rank, since
A
is hyperfrustrated,
\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(A)=n-b_{\Sigma}(A)+1
, so we should prove
b_{\Sigma}(A)=b_{\Sigma}(S)
. Consider
B\in\pi_{\text{\rm b}}(S)
. Then
E(\zeta){:}B
is sign balanced, and it is connected because
S{:}B
is connected. Therefore,
B
is contained in a block of
\pi_{\text{\rm b}}(A)
. But no edge of
A
connects two different sign-balanced components of
S
, so
B\in\pi_{\text{\rm b}}(A)
. This proves
b_{\Sigma}(A)\geq b_{\Sigma}(S)
, so that
\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(A)\leq\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}(S)
. That implies equality, so
A\subseteq\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(S)
and also
\pi_{\text{\rm b}}(A)=\pi_{\text{\rm b}}(S)
.

Now we prove that an edge
e\notin A
is also not in
\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(S)
. It is sufficient to prove that
b_{\Sigma}(A\cup\{e\})<b_{\Sigma}(A)
, since both
A
and
A\cup\{e\}
are hyperfrustrated. If
e
joins a sign-balanced component of
A
to another component, it reduces
b_{\Sigma}
. If it joins vertices in a sign-balanced component
A{:}B
or is a half edge in
E{:}B
, then
A{:}B\supseteq E(\zeta){:}B

\implies

e\notin E(\zeta)

\implies

(A\cup\{e\}){:}B
is sign-unbalanced; then adding
e
also reduces
b_{\Sigma}
. These are the only possibilities, because
E{:}U_{\Sigma}(S)\cup E^{0}\subseteq A
. That completes the proof that
A=\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(S)
.

If
S
is hyperbalanced, then we may restrict attention to
\Upsilon|E(\theta)
for some gain potential
-\theta
and either apply the same reasoning as in the previous case or simply appeal to Lemma
2.3
. The closure is hyperbalanced because it is contained in
E(\theta)
.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

The closed sets of
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
are those of the forms

(5.3)

[E{:}U]\cup[E(\zeta){:}\pi]\cup E^{0},

where
U\subseteq V
,
\pi
partitions
U^{c}
, and
\zeta
is a sign function on
U^{c}
, and

(5.4)

[E(\theta){:}U]\cup[E(\theta,\zeta){:}\pi]\cup E^{00},

where
\theta
is a gain switcher.
A closed set of type (
5.4
) is always hyperbalanced, and every hyperbalanced closed set has the form (
5.4
).

The closed sets of
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
are the same, if the set is hyperbalanced, but the same with the addition of
e_{\infty}
if the set is hyperfrustrated.

The first kind of closed set, (
5.3
), is the same as the second, hence hyperbalanced and redundant, if
\Upsilon
is hyperbalanced, but it is usually not hyperbalanced if
\Upsilon
is not hyperbalanced. It will be hyperbalanced if and only if all loose edges are neutral and
E{:}U
is hyperbalanced.

Theorems
5.2
and
5.3
demonstrate one function of the extra point
e_{\infty}
: it expresses hyperbalance in purely matroidal terms because an edge set
S\subseteq E
is hyperbalanced if and only if its closure in
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
does not contain
e_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Theorem
5.2
implies that every closed set has one of these two forms. We prove the converse.

Let
A
be the set in (
5.3
). The partition
\pi(A)
refines
\pi\cup\{U\}
. Thus, if
B_{A}\in\pi(A)
is contained in some
B\in\pi
, we have
E(\zeta){:}B_{A}\subseteq E(\zeta){:}B\subseteq A
. If
B_{A}\subseteq U
, then
E{:}B_{A}\subseteq E{:}U\subseteq A
. The conclusion is that
\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(A)=[E{:}U_{\Sigma}(A)]\cup[E(\zeta){:}\pi(A)]\cup E^{0}\subseteq A
, from which equality is obvious.

In type (
5.4
), we can restrict attention to
E(\theta)
, which is hyperbalanced, so its matroid is that of the signed graph
\Sigma(\theta)
. Then
A
is closed by Lemma
2.3
. An alternative is to repeat the proof for type (
5.3
) with
E(\theta)
in place of
E
and
E^{00}
in place of
E^{0}
.
∎

There is redundancy in Theorem
5.3
, inasmuch as a flat
A
may be representable with different choices of
U
,
\pi
,
\zeta
, and
\theta
. If
E{:}U
has components that are sign balanced, and in (
5.4
) hyperbalanced, then
U
can be made smaller and
\pi
larger. The smallest possible set
U
is
U_{\Sigma}(A)
. Once
U
is chosen, the most refined partition is
\pi(A{:}U^{c})
, but sometimes
\pi
may be chosen to combine components of
A{:}U^{c}
. Then given
U
and
\pi
, the switching function(s)
\zeta
and
\theta
are determined up to sign (for

) and (for
\theta
) translation on each component of

if

and

but not necessarily with other choices. This versatility in Theorem

is sometimes something to be careful about.

The flats of
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
form a geometric lattice, which we denote by
\operatorname{Lat}\Upsilon
.
There is also the semilattice of hyperbalanced flats, which we call
\operatorname{\operatorname{Lat}^{\text{\rm b}}}\Upsilon
; it is a geometric semilattice that corresponds to the intersection semilattice of the hyperplane representation of
\Upsilon
, so it is of particular interest; see Section
10
.

6.
Matroid: coatoms and cocircuits

The coatoms of
\operatorname{Lat}\Upsilon
, the lattice of closed sets, are of interest, but even more so their complements, the cocircuits of
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
, and also the hyperbalanced coatoms, which are the maximal elements of
\operatorname{\operatorname{Lat}^{\text{\rm b}}}\Upsilon
when
\Upsilon
is not hyperbalanced.
The coatoms and cocircuits are quite different depending on whether
\Upsilon
is hyperbalanced or not.
On the whole it is easier to describe the cocircuits (which we may call hypercocircuits to distinguish them from the sign cocircuits in
\mathbf{F}(\Sigma)
).

We begin with a pair of lemmas.

Lemma 6.1
.

Every basis of
\mathbf{F}(\Sigma)
is hyperbalanced.

Proof.

A basis cannot be hyperfrustrated because it does not contain any sign circuits.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose
\Upsilon
is hyperfrustrated. Then every maximal hyperbalanced edge set
A
is a coatom with
b_{\Sigma}(A)=b_{\Sigma}(E)
and
\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}A=\operatorname{rk}(\Upsilon)-1=n-b_{\Sigma}(E)
.

Proof.

A maximal hyperbalanced edge set
A
is closed in
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
, either by Theorem
5.2
, or simply because adding an edge to it makes it hyperfrustrated, which increases its rank.

By maximality
A
is connected and spanning in each component of
\Upsilon
. We use (
5.2
) to prove that, for any edge
e\notin A
,
\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(A\cup e)=E
, by proving that any other edge
f\notin A\cup e
is in
\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(A\cup e)
. If
f\in E^{0}
, then
f\in\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(A\cup e)
. Otherwise,
f
is in a component
\Upsilon{:}X
. If
(A\cup e){:}X
is sign unbalanced, then
X\subseteq U_{\Sigma}(A\cup e)
so
f\in E{:}U_{\Sigma}(A\cup e)\subseteq\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(A\cup e)
. If
(A\cup e){:}X
is sign balanced, we prove that
E{:}X
is sign balanced. By maximality of
A
,
(A\cup f){:}X
is hyperfrustrated for
f\in(E\smallsetminus A){:}X
, so
f
belongs to a non-neutral sign circuit in
A\cup f
, which can only be a positive circle because
A{:}X
contains no negative figure. Therefore,
f\in\operatorname{bcl}_{\Sigma}(A)
. Since
f
was any edge in
(E\smallsetminus A){:}X
, it follows that
E{:}X
is sign balanced. Therefore,
X\in\pi_{\text{\rm b}}(A\cup e)
and
E{:}X\subseteq E{:}\pi_{\text{\rm b}}(A\cup e)\subseteq\operatorname{clos}_{\Upsilon}(A\cup e)
.
∎

A
hyperbalancing set
for
\Upsilon
is an edge set whose deletion results in hyperbalance.

Note that, since
e_{\infty}
can be treated as a non-neutral loose edge, the extended matroid
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)

7.
Matroid: independence and bases

Knowing the rank function of the matroid
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
we can determine the independent sets and bases. They are complicated but they can be fully classified using the matroid invariant
nullity
, defined as
\operatorname{nul}_{\Upsilon}S:=|S|-\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}S
for a set
S
. In particular, a set is independent if and only if its nullity is 0.
For a gain signed graph nullity has a nice expression in terms of the cyclomatic number
\xi
and the quantity
u_{\Sigma}(S)
, the number of sign-unbalanced components of
S
. (Recall that this does not include loose edges;
u_{\Sigma}(S)=0
if
S
has only loose edges.)

Lemma 7.1
.

The nullity of an edge set
S
in
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{nul}_{\Upsilon}S

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\xi(S)+b_{\Sigma}(S)-c(S)&\text{if $S$ is hyperbalanced,}\\
\xi(S)+b_{\Sigma}(S)-c(S)-1&\text{if $S$ is hyperfrustrated}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\xi(S)-u_{\Sigma}(S)&\text{if $S$ is hyperbalanced,}\\
\xi(S)-u_{\Sigma}(S)-1&\text{if $S$ is hyperfrustrated}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The formula follows from the definition of nullity, the rank formula (
4.1
), and the definition of the cyclomatic number.
∎

The second version of the nullity formula shows that isolated vertices can be ignored in computing nullity.

We prepare for independence with a lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

An edge set that contains two hyperfrustrated edge components is dependent.

Proof.

Say
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are hyperfrustrated edge components of
S
. Then

\operatorname{nul}_{\Upsilon}S=\xi(S)-u_{\Sigma}(S)-1\geq[\xi(S_{1})-u_{\Sigma}(S_{1})]+[\xi(S_{2})-u_{\Sigma}(S_{2})]-1

since
\xi
and
u_{\Sigma}
are additive on components.
In order to be hyperfrustrated a component
S_{i}
must contain a positive circle, a handcuff with two negative figures, or a loose edge. In either case,
\xi(S_{i})-u_{\Sigma}(S_{i})>0
. It follows that
\operatorname{nul}_{\Upsilon}S>0
.
∎

A
unicycle
is a circle or a half edge that may have trees attached.

Theorem 7.3
.

The independent sets of
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
are the following types of edge set, considered as spanning subgraphs.

I.

Hyperbalanced: Every component is a tree or a sign-unbalanced unicycle, and it has no loose edges.

II.

Hyperfrustrated: Every component is a tree or a sign-unbalanced unicycle, except that there may be one component
S_{0}
that is either a sign-balanced unicycle, a loose edge, or a sign-unbalanced theta graph or a handcuff with at least one negative figure, possibly with attached trees, and in each case the unique sign circuit in

is non-neutral.

Lower rows: The possible hyperfrustrated component

Proof.

To find independent sets we assume
S
is independent and examine how
\operatorname{nul}_{\Upsilon}S
can equal 0.
The equation we have to solve is
\xi(S)-u_{\Sigma}(S)=0
or

is or is not hyperbalanced. By independence, any positive circle or loose edge must be non-neutral.

In the hyperbalanced case

and

. Therefore, there can be no positive circles or loose edges in

In the hyperfrustrated case the components are the same except that, because

that contains a sign circuit, which must be non-neutral since

is independent; thus
S_{0}
is hyperfrustrated. (By Lemma

there cannot be two such components.)
If

is sign balanced, it has one circle, which is positive (and is not neutral), so it is a unicycle, or else it is a non-neutral loose edge.

If

is sign unbalanced, it has cyclomatic number 2 and at least one negative figure. Thus it is either an unbalanced theta graph or an unbalanced handcuff, with possible pendant trees. In either case it contains exactly one sign circuit, which must not be neutral. Thus there is an edge

(in the sign circuit) such that

is hyperbalanced. That is,

If

is a unicycle. In order to contain a sign circuit it must be a sign-balanced unicycle. If

is an isthmus in

would be hyperbalanced; thus the components must be sign-unbalanced unicycles and

contains a handcuff sign circuit. The third possibility is that

is added to a unicyclic component of

to form

with

In every case,
S_{0}
contains a sign circuit that is the unique sign circuit in

hyperfrustrated.
∎

Corollary 7.4
.

Sign and gain switching and edge reorientation in
\Upsilon
do not change the matroid
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
and therefore do not change linearly dependent subsets among the vectors
\mathbf{z}(E)
.

Proof.

The matroid is determined by its independent sets. An independent set is characterized by its underlying graph, by the signs of its circles, and by the neutrality of its sign circuits. None of these is affected by switching or orientation. The only one of those assertions that is not obvious is the one about neutrality, but Lemma
2.10
says the gain of a sign circuit is not changed by gain switching.

The dependent vector sets are determined by
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
, according to Theorem

.
∎

We can characterize the maximal independent sets.

.

The bases of
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)

The contraction

Lemma 9.2
.

In

Definition 9.3

(Contraction of a Gain Signed Graph)
.

The contraction of an edge set

in

.
These are the two cases.

The first theorem says that minors are independent of the order of operations. In particular, a minor can be computed by deleting and contracting one edge at a time.

Theorem 9.4

Suppose

Proof of Theorem

The parts with deletions are routine. Signed graph minors obey the formulas in Theorem

[

Theorem 9.6
.

Suppose
S\subseteq E
. Then
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon\smallsetminus S)=\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)\smallsetminus S
and
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon/S)=\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)/S
.

Proof.

We prove the rank functions agree. This is trivial for deletion.

For contraction we compute the ranks in
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{M}/S
. With
A\subseteq E\smallsetminus S
, first from the definition of rank in a contraction matroid:

\displaystyle\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)/S}A

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbf{M}}(A\cup S)-\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbf{M}}(S)

\displaystyle=[n-b_{\Sigma}(A\cup S)+\delta_{\Upsilon}(A\cup S)]-[n-b_{\Sigma}(S)+\delta_{\Upsilon}(S)]

\displaystyle=b_{\Sigma}(S)-b_{\Sigma}(A\cup S)+\delta_{\Upsilon}(A\cup S)-\delta_{\Upsilon}(S),

and second from rank in the contracted gain signed graph:

\displaystyle\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon/S)}A

\displaystyle=|V(\Upsilon/S)|-b_{\Sigma/S}(A)+\delta_{\Upsilon/S}(A)

\displaystyle=b_{\Sigma}(S)-b_{\Sigma/S}(A)+\delta_{\Upsilon/S}(A).

Now we consider two cases.

If
S
is hyperbalanced, then
\delta_{\Upsilon/S}(A)=\delta_{\Upsilon}(A\cup S)
by Lemma
9.5
, so

\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)/S}A=b_{\Sigma}(S)-b_{\Sigma}(A\cup S)+\delta_{\Upsilon}(A\cup S)

and

\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon/S)}A=b_{\Sigma}(S)-b_{\Sigma/S}(A)+\delta_{\Upsilon}(A\cup S).

By Lemma
9.2

b_{\Sigma}(A\cup S)=b_{\Sigma/S}(A)
. Therefore, the ranks are equal.

If
S
is hyperfrustrated, then
\Upsilon/S=\Sigma/S
so
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon/S)=\mathbf{F}(\Sigma/S)
. The contracted matroid computation is

\displaystyle\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbf{M}(\Sigma)/S}A

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbf{M}}(A\cup S)-\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbf{M}}(S)

\displaystyle=[n-b_{\Sigma}(A\cup S)]-[n-b_{\Sigma}(S)]

\displaystyle=b_{\Sigma}(S)-b_{\Sigma}(A\cup S).

The computation in the contracted graph is

\displaystyle\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon/S)}A

\displaystyle=|V(\Sigma/S)|-b_{\Sigma/S}(A)

\displaystyle=b_{\Sigma}(S)-b_{\Sigma/S}(A).

\displaystyle=b_{\Sigma}(S)-b_{\Sigma}(A\cup S)

by Lemma
9.2
. Thus, the ranks are equal in this case as well.
∎

Corollary 9.7
.

For

, the contraction

is described in the following list:

(a)

If

is a link

and

(b)

If

(c)

If

10.
The hyperplane model

The biggest reason for gain signed graphs is that they encode popular hyperplane arrangements in a more manageable form.

10.1.
Affine hyperplanes

The hyperplane associated to an edge
e_{ij}
lies in the affine space
\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbf{K})
and is given by

\mathbf{h}(e):\tau(v_{i},e)x_{i}+\tau(v_{j},e)x_{j}=-\varphi(e).

(The affine space is distinguished from the vector space
\mathbf{K}^{n}
by having inhomogeneous as well as homogeneous subspaces.)
The hyperplane of a half edge
e
at
v_{i}
is
x_{i}=-\varphi(e)
. The “honorary hyperplane” of a loose edge is given by the equation
0=-\varphi(e)
, which is the
degenerate hyperplane

\mathbf{h}(e)=\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbf{K})
if
\varphi(e)=0
and the
phantom hyperplane

\varnothing
if
\varphi(e)\neq 0
. (That makes sense in the projectivization.) The honorary hyperplane of the extra point
e_{\infty}
is the phantom hyperplane.
Thus from a gain signed graph
\Upsilon
with gain group
\mathbf{K}^{+}
we get the hyperplane arrangement

\mathcal{A}[\Upsilon]:=\{\mathbf{h}(e):e\in E\}.

The intersection semilattice of this arrangement is

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}[\Upsilon]):=\{\textstyle\bigcap\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{A}[\Upsilon],\ \bigcap\mathcal{S}\neq\varnothing\}.

We list some arrangements of the type gain signed graphs are intended for, which we call
(signed) affinographic
since they are affine deformations of graphic or signed-graphic hyperplane arrangements.
By
\varepsilon e_{ij}
we denote an edge with endpoints
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
and sign
\varepsilon
, while by
\pm e_{ij}
we denote two edges with both signs, a positive edge
+e_{ij}
and a negative edge
-e_{ij}
. A simple graph
\Gamma
thus gives rise to a signed graph
\varepsilon\Gamma
, in which all edges have the same sign
\varepsilon
, and to
\pm\Gamma=(+\Gamma)\cup(-\Gamma)
, in which all edges are doubled with both signs.

(Af1)

Affine arrangements whose edge sets have forms like
\{(+e_{ij},g):i<j,\ -k\leq g\leq l\}
, known as deformations of the (all-positive) complete-graphic arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{n-1}
with edge set
\{(+e_{ij},0):i<j\}
.

(Af2)

Affine arrangements whose edge sets have forms like
\{(\pm e_{ij},g):i<j,\ -k\leq g\leq l\}\cup\{(-e_{ii},g),\ -k\leq g\leq l\}
, known as deformations of the complete signed-graphic arrangement
\mathcal{B}_{n}
with edge set
\{(\pm e_{ij},0):i<j\}\cup\{(-e_{ii},0)\}
.

(Af3)

Affine deformations whose edge sets have forms like
\{(\pm e_{ij},g):i<j,\ -k\leq g\leq l\}
, of the signed-graphic arrangement
\mathcal{D}_{n}
with edge set
\{(\pm e_{ij},0):i<j\}
.

(Af4)

An affinographic arrangement of Coxeter type, whose edge set is as in (Af
2
) or (Af
3
) with
k=0

[
2
, Theorem 3.5]
.

(Af5)

The sign-symmetric Shi arrangement, whose edge set is
\{(\pm e_{ij},g):i<j,\ g=0,1\}
, and a variety of similar arrangements
[
2
, Section 3]
.

(Af6)

The Shi threshold arrangement, whose edge set is
\{(-e_{ij},g):i<j,\ g=0,1\}

[
14
]
, and the similar arrangement in
[
2
, Theorem 5.4]
.

(Af7)

The Linial threshold arrangement with coordinate and shifted hyperplanes, whose edge set is
\{(-e_{ij},1):i<j\}\cup\{(-e_{i},g):g=0,1\}

[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.

(Af8)

The Catalan threshold arrangement, whose edge set is
\{(-e_{ij},g):i<j,\ g=0,\pm 1\}

[
15
]
.

(Af9)

A generalized threshold arrangement, whose edge set is
\{(-e_{ij},g):i<j,\ -k\leq g\leq l\}

[
3
]
.

10.2.
Projective hyperplanes

The link between this affine arrangement and the vector model in linear space is through the
projectivization
,

\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}}[\Upsilon]:=\{\mathbf{h}(e)_{\mathbb{P}}:e\in E\}\cup\{h_{\infty}\},

where
h_{\infty}
denotes the ideal or infinite hyperplane while
\mathbf{h}(e)_{\mathbb{P}}
is the extension of
\mathbf{h}(e)
into the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbf{K})
if
\mathbf{h}(e)
is not the phantom hyperplane and
\mathbf{h}(e)_{\mathbb{P}}=h_{\infty}
if
\mathbf{h}(e)
is the phantom hyperplane. (The affine part of
h_{\infty}
is
\varnothing
, which should explain the name “phantom hyperplane”.)
We define
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbb{P}}(e):=\mathbf{h}(e)_{\mathbb{P}}
in order to have the function
h_{\mathbb{P}}
that maps edges to projective hyperplanes.
The addition of the ideal hyperplane in the projectivization ensures that, in the real case, the regions remain the same, and in the complex case, the complement of the arrangement remains the same.

The intersection lattice of the projective arrangement is

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}}[\Upsilon]):=\{\textstyle\bigcap\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}}[\Upsilon]\}.

This is the lattice of closed sets of a matroid
\mathbf{M}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}})
whose ground set is the set of hyperplanes and whose rank function is
\operatorname{rk}\mathcal{S}=\operatorname{codim}\bigcap\mathcal{S}
.
The affine intersection semilattice
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}[\Upsilon])
, which is a meet subsemilattice of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}}[\Upsilon])
, is a
geometric semilattice
, as defined by Wachs and Walker
[
20
]
. One definition is that it consists of the flats of a geometric lattice that do not lie above a fixed atom; in our case the lattice is
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}}[\Upsilon])
and the atom is
h_{\infty}
.
The corresponding matroid-like structure is a
semimatroid
, defined subsequently by Ardila
[
1
]
.

The coordinates in projective space are homogeneous coordinates
[x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
(not all zero). Projecting the vector model in
\mathbf{K}^{1+n}
to homogeneous coordinates in
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbf{K})
, the hyperplane
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbb{P}}(e)
is the dual space of the projected vector
[\mathbf{z}(e)]
; that is,
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbb{P}}(e)=\{[\mathbf{y}]\in\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbf{K}):[\mathbf{z}(e)]\cdot[\mathbf{y}]=0\}
.

Theorem 10.1

(Hyperplane Representations)
.

The matroid
\mathbf{M}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}}[\Upsilon])
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
by the mapping
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbb{P}}
, which induces a lattice isomorphism
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}}[\Upsilon])\cong\operatorname{Lat}\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
and a semilattice isomorphism
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}[\Upsilon])\cong\operatorname{\operatorname{Lat}^{\text{\rm b}}}\Upsilon
.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
4.2
by vector-space duality.
∎

10.3.
Regions and polynomials

Consider a hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathbb{A}^{d}(\mathbf{K})
.
The
characteristic polynomial
of
\mathcal{A}
is

\displaystyle p(\mathcal{A};\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{A}:}\,{\bigcap\mathcal{S}\neq\varnothing}}(-1)^{|\mathcal{S}|}\lambda^{\dim\bigcap\mathcal{S}}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})}\mu(\hat{0},s)\lambda^{\dim s},

where
\hat{0}=\mathbb{A}^{d}(\mathbf{K})
, the bottom element of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
, and
\mu
is the Möbius function.

We define the
chromatic polynomial
of
\Upsilon
:

\displaystyle\chi_{\Upsilon}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{S\subseteq E:}(-1)^{|S|}\lambda^{n-\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}S}=\sum_{S\in\operatorname{Lat}\Upsilon}\mu(\varnothing,S)\lambda^{n-\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}S},

which is interpreted as
0
if
\Upsilon
has any neutral loose edges. A variant is

\chi_{\Upsilon_{\infty}}(\lambda)
, which is
\chi_{\Upsilon\cup\{e_{\infty}\}}(\lambda)
where
e_{\infty}
is interpreted as a non-neutral loose edge.
The
balanced chromatic polynomial
of
\Upsilon
is

\displaystyle\chi^{\text{\rm b}}_{\Upsilon}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{{S\subseteq E:}\,\text{hyperbalanced}}(-1)^{|S|}\lambda^{n-\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}S}=\sum_{S\in\operatorname{\operatorname{Lat}^{\text{\rm b}}}\Upsilon}\mu(\varnothing,S)\lambda^{n-\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}S},

which also is
0
if
\Upsilon
has neutral loose edges.
(Note that a graph has a chromatic polynomial, while an arrangement (or matroid) has a characteristic polynomial. One expects a chromatic polynomial to count colorings, as is the case with signed graphs and gain graphs; we hope to present such an interpretation separately.)

Lemma 10.2
.

Assuming that
\Upsilon
has no neutral loose edges, we have

p(\mathcal{A}[\Upsilon];\lambda)=\chi^{\text{\rm b}}_{\Upsilon}(\lambda)

and

p(\mathcal{A}_{\infty}[\Upsilon];\lambda)=\chi_{\Upsilon_{\infty}}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

This is a consequence of Theorem
10.1
and the semilattice expressions for the polynomials.
∎

11.
Abstract abelian gains

The theory we developed for an additive group of a field largely applies to any abelian gain group, except of course for the vector representation. We demonstrate that here by presenting a purely combinatorial proof that
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
and
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
are matroids. All the analysis in the preceding sections applies without change except that
\mathfrak{G}^{n}
may not be a vector space.

We had hoped to treat general groups, not necessarily abelian, in which the additive definitions become multiplicative and multipliers become exponents, but the failure of commutativity led to the failure of so many properties from Section
2
that we were unable even to establish that neutrality of sign circuits was independent of the method of calculating the gain. Thus, we leave open the problem of dealing with nonabelian gain groups.

An
abelian gain signed graph
is a triple
\Upsilon=(\Gamma,\sigma,\varphi)
where
\Gamma=(V,E)
is a graph, the
signature
or sign function
\sigma
gives each edge an element of the sign set
\{+1,-1\}
, and the
gain function

\varphi:\vec{E}\to\mathfrak{G}
is an oriented labelling of edges from an abelian group
\mathfrak{G}
, which we write additively so that, for instance,
\varphi(e^{-1})=-\varphi(e)
. All the formulas and results of Section
2
remain valid if we replace the group
\mathbf{K}^{+}
by
\mathfrak{G}
.

We define rank exactly as in Equation (
4.1
).
The task is to prove this is a matroid rank function without using a representation. Instead, we use the fact that
\mathbf{F}(\Sigma)
is known to be a matroid. (A side effect of this proof is that Theorem
4.2
becomes a proof that
\mathbf{z}
is a module representation of
\mathbf{M}(\Upsilon)
even when
\mathfrak{G}
is not the additive group of a field.) As before, we can treat the extra point
e_{\infty}
as if it were a non-neutral half edge, so we are simultaneously proving that
\mathbf{M}_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
is a matroid.

Theorem 11.1
.

The function
\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}
is a matroid rank function on
E(\Upsilon)
and
E_{\infty}(\Upsilon)
.

Proof.

We establish the fundamental properties of a matroid rank function on
E
, which are:

R1.

Normalization
:
\operatorname{rk}(\varnothing)=0
.

R2.

Unit Increase
: If
e\notin S\subset E
, then
\operatorname{rk}S\leq\operatorname{rk}(S\cup\{e\})\leq\operatorname{rk}S+1
.

R3.

Submodularity
: If
e,f\notin S\subset E
, then

\operatorname{rk}(S\cup\{e\})-\operatorname{rk}S\geq\operatorname{rk}(S\cup\{e,f\})-\operatorname{rk}(S\cup\{f\}).

R1 is obvious.

For R2, what needs to be proved is that

\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}S+\delta_{\Upsilon}(S)\leq\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}(S\cup\{e\})+\delta_{\Upsilon}(S\cup\{e\})\leq\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}S+\delta_{\Upsilon}(S)+1.

The left-hand inequality is true because
\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}
and
\delta_{\Upsilon}
are weakly increasing.
The right-hand inequality is true by property R2 of
\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}
if
\delta_{\Upsilon}(S)=\delta_{\Upsilon}(S\cup\{e\})
, so assume
S
is hyperbalanced and
S\cup\{e\}
is hyperfrustrated. Then
\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}(S\cup\{e\})=\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}S
by Lemma
11.2
. That proves R2.

Lemma 11.2
.

Suppose
S\subset E
is hyperbalanced,
e\in E
, and
S\cup\{e\}
is hyperfrustrated. Then
e\in\operatorname{clos}_{\Sigma}S
,
rk_{\Sigma}(S\cup\{e\})=\operatorname{rk}_{\Sigma}S
, and
rk_{\Upsilon}(S\cup\{e\})=\operatorname{rk}_{\Upsilon}S+1
.
