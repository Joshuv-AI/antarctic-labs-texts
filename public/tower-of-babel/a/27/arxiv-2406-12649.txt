Probabilistic Conceptual Explainers: Trustworthy Conceptual Explanations for Vision Foundation Models
Hengyi Wang, Shiwei Tan, Hao Wang

Abstract
Vision transformers (ViTs) have emerged as a significant area of focus, particularly for their capacity to be jointly trained with large language models and to serve as robust vision foundation models. Yet, the development of trustworthy explanation methods for ViTs has lagged, particularly in the context of post-hoc interpretations of ViT predictions. Existing sub-image selection approaches, such as feature-attribution and conceptual models, fall short in this regard. This paper proposes five desiderata for explaining ViTs -- faithfulness, stability, sparsity, multi-level structure, and parsimony -- and demonstrates the inadequacy of current methods in meeting these criteria comprehensively. We introduce a variational Bayesian explanation framework, dubbed ProbAbilistic Concept Explainers (PACE), which models the distributions of patch embeddings to provide trustworthy post-hoc conceptual explanations. Our qualitative analysis reveals the distributions of patch-level concepts, elucidating the effectiveness of ViTs by modeling the joint distribution of patch embeddings and ViT’s predictions. Moreover, these patch-level explanations bridge the gap between image-level and dataset-level explanations, thus completing the multi-level structure of PACE. •

Faithfulness:
The explanation should be faithful to the explained ViT and able to recover its prediction.

•

Stability:
The explanation should be stable for different perturbed versions of the same image.

•

Sparsity:
For each prediction’s explanation, only a small subset of concepts are relevant.

•

Multi-Level Structure:
There should be dataset-level, image-level, and patch-level explanations.

•

Parsimony:
There are a small number of concepts in total (see Appendix

Related Work

Vision Transformers.

Vision Transformers (ViTs) have revolutionized computer vision by adapting the Transformer architecture for image recognition. The pioneering ViT model processes images as sequences of patches, surpassing traditional convolutional networks in efficiency and performance
(
Dosovitskiy et al., 2020
)
. Subsequent innovations include the Swin Transformer
(
Liu et al., 2021
)
, which introduces a hierarchical structure with shifted windows, and the Data-efficient Image Transformers (DeiT)
(
Touvron et al., 2021
)
, which optimize training with a distillation token and teacher-student strategy. The CLIP model
(
Radford et al., 2021
)
extends ViT’s applicability by learning from natural language supervision, showcasing the architecture’s versatility and robustness in visual representation learning.

Visual Explanation Methods.

The landscape of visual explanation methods
(
Gilpin et al., 2018
;
Langer et al., 2021
;
Schwalbe & Finzel, 2023
)
in computer vision is diverse, encompassing both feature attribution and concept-based approaches. Prominent methods such as LIME and SHAP
(
Ribeiro et al., 2016
;
Lundberg & Lee, 2017
;
Simonyan et al., 2013
;
Li et al., 2021b
;
Shrikumar et al., 2017
)
provide insights by assigning importance scores to input features, enhancing the understanding of model decisions. Alongside these, concept-based explanations are also gaining popularity.
Inherent
methods
(
Chen et al., 2019
;
Alvarez Melis & Jaakkola, 2018
;
Koh et al., 2020
;
Kim et al., 2018
;
Chattopadhyay et al., 2024
;
Xu et al., 2024
)
learn and deduce concepts alongside the prediction model. These methods necessitate modifications to the models for explanations, posing challenges in scalability to new model architectures and increased computational demands.

To address these challenges,
post-hoc
methods
(
Yuksekgonul et al., 2022
;
Pan et al., 2021
;
Fel et al., 2023b
;
Sundararajan et al., 2017
;
Bach et al., 2015
;
Kindermans et al., 2016
;
Rohekar et al., 2024
;
Xie et al., 2022
;
Covert et al., 2022
;
Bennetot et al., 2022
)
deduce concepts from the existing prediction model without additional modifications. Given such advantages, our work focuses on the post-hoc setting.
These methods are pivotal in image-level explanation for ViTs, providing deeper insights into ViTs’ visual data processing. Nevertheless, their focus remains on image-level explanations, overlooking the multi-level structure within ViTs. They also fall short in other desiderata such as faithfulness/stability.
Some methods require additional text supervision or human-annotated labels, such as
(
Yang et al., 2023
;
Ben Melech Stan et al., 2024
;
Menon & Vondrick, 2022
;
Chefer et al., 2021b
;
Chefer et al., 2021a
;
Ming et al., 2022
;
Kim et al., 2023
;
Losch et al., 2019
)
. Therefore, these approaches are not applicable to our unsupervised learning setting.

In contrast, our PACE provides multi-level conceptual explanations that are faithful, stable, sparse, and parsimonious; this is verified by our empirical results in Sec.
4
.

Methodology

In this section, we formalize the definition of five desiderata for post-hoc conceptual explanations of ViTs and describe our PACE for achieving these desiderata.

Problem Setting and Notations

Consider a dataset comprising
M

J

(
Dosovitskiy et al., 2020
)

f(\cdot)
, which processes image
m
(represented by
{\bf I}_{m}
) and yields: (1) the predicted label
\widehat{\bf y}_{m}
with
N

{\bf e}_{mj}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(

is the hidden dimension), and (3) the attention weights
{\bf a}_{m}^{(h)}\triangleq[a_{mj}^{(h)}]_{j=1}^{J}
(
{\bf a}_{m}^{(h)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J}
) for each patch relative to the final layer’s ‘CLS’ token, where

h
. We define the mean attention weight across H heads as

1
). A typical post-hoc explainer, denoted as

{\bf e}_{m}

\widehat{\bf y}_{m}
, and optionally ViT parameters
{\bf P}
, producing a concept activation
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
(

g({\bf e}_{m},\widehat{\bf y}_{m},{\bf P})=\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
. See Appendix
D
for details. Note that while some methods do not inherently provide explanations with authentic concepts, the explanation activation
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
(or its suitably adapted version) can still be interpreted as a quasi-concept vector.

In contrast to typical post-hoc explainers that only provide image-level explanations
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
, our PACE provides multi-level conceptual explanations; for an image
m
, PACE provides
K

dataset-level
variables
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
(
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the Gaussian mean, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
the Covariance), an
image-level
variable
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
, and
J

patch-level
variables
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}\triangleq\{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}\}_{j=1}^{J}
(see details in Sec.
3.2
and Sec.
4
).

3.2
Definition of Trustworthy Conceptual Explanations

We formally define the five desiderata for trustworthy conceptual explanations for ViTs as follows (see Sec.
3.1
).

Definition 3.1

(
Trustworthy Conceptual Explanations
)
.

Consider a dataset
{\mathcal{D}}
with
M
images
{\bf I}_{m}
(
m\in{1,\dots,M}
), each consisting of
J
patches. For a given number of concepts
K
, a trustworthy conceptual explanation for an image
m
consists of
K

dataset-level
variables
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, an
image-level
variable
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
, and
J

patch-level
variables
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}\}_{j=1}^{J}
with the following properties:

(1)

Faithfulness
, which implies a strong relation between the concept activation
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
and the post-hoc label
\widehat{y}_{m}
derived from ViT predictions. In this paper, we measure linear faithfulness score by applying a logistic regression model
LR(\cdot)
, i.e.,
\widehat{y}_{m}=LR(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m})(1\leq m\leq M)
, and evaluating its accuracy (details in Sec.
4
).

(2)

Stability
, which is the consistency of explanations across different perturbed versions of the same image. For an image
{\bf I}_{m}
with the inferred
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
and its perturbed version
{\bf I}^{\prime}_{m}
with inferred
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m}
, stability is quantified by
\tfrac{\|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m}\|}{\|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}\|}
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the
L_{2}
norm.

(3)

Sparsity
, which involves the concept vector having a sparse representation, measured by the fraction of values nearing zero. Specifically, sparsity is defined as the proportion of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
’s entries nearing zero, i.e.,
\tfrac{1}{K}\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\mymathbb{1}(|\theta_{mk}|<\epsilon)
,
with a small threshold
\epsilon>0
.

(4)

Multi-Level Structure
,
which means that an ideal explainer should yield

K

dataset-level
variables
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
representing the mean and covariance of each concept in the dataset,
an
image-level
variable
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
for each image
m
, and a
patch-level
variable
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
for each patch
j
in image
m
.

(5)

Parsimony
, which involves using the minimal number of concepts
K
to produce clear and simple explanations for humans. Methods with flexible concept counts could use fewer concepts while maintaining other properties’ performance. In contrast, too many concepts usually lead to redundancy in conceptual explanations and a lack of compact representation.

In Definition
3.1
, Property (1) ensures the learned concepts convey essential information for predicting image labels from hidden embeddings. Property (2) guarantees robustness and generalization in face of perturbations. Property (3) reflects that each prediction usually only involves a small number of relevant concepts. Property (4) offers diverse and comprehensive multi-level conceptual explanations. Finally, Property (5) facilitates learning concepts efficiently, restricts the number of redundant concepts for meaningful explanations, and reduces humans’ cognitive load reading explanations.
Theorem
3.2
in Sec.
3.7
provides the theoretical guarantees for PACE in terms of these properties.

3.3
ProbAbilistic Conceptual Explainers (PACE)

Drawing inspiration from hierarchical Bayesian deep learning
(
Wang & Yeung, 2016
;
Wang & Yeung, 2020
;
Wang et al., 2016
;
CausalTrans
;
Wang & Yan, 2023
;
Xu et al., 2023
)
, we introduce a variational Bayesian framework, dubbed ProbAbilistic Concept Explainers (PACE), for post-hoc conceptual explanation of Vision Transformers (ViTs). To ensure PACE produces trustworthy concepts as defined in Definition
3.1
, PACE treats the explained ViT’s patch-level embeddings as observed variable and
design a hierarchical Bayesian model that generates these embeddings in a top-down manner, from dataset-level concepts through image-level concepts to patch-level embeddings.

Fig.
1
shows an overview of our PACE, where patch embeddings
{\bf e}_{m}
and ViT’s predicted label
\widehat{\bf y}_{m}
are treated as observable variables. Attention weights
{\bf a}_{m}
are considered as the virtual count for each patch; for example,
a_{mj}=0.2
means patch
j
is considered as
0.2J
patches, where
J
is the total number of patches in image
m
(see details below).PACE models the patch embedding distribution using a mixture of
K
Gaussians (
K
concepts), characterized by parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}

(1\leq k\leq K)
.
For image
m
, PACE provides three levels of conceptual explanations:
(1)
K

dataset-level
variables
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
representing the mean and covariance of each concept
k
in the dataset,
(2) an
image-level
variable
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
for each image
m
, and
(3)
J

patch-level
variable
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}\triangleq\{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}\}_{j=1}^{J}

for each patch
j
in image
m
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
.

•

.

•

Draw the predicted label
\widehat{\bf y}_{m}\sim\text{GLM}(\bar{\bf z}_{m},{\bf H})
.

•

For each pair of images
{\bf I}_{m}
and

and

Here
\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
is the parameter for the Dirichlet distribution

, and we define

\displaystyle\bar{\bf z}_{m}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{J}}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{J}{\bf z}_{mj}.

(1)

denotes a categorical distribution from a generalized linear model (GLM), given by

\displaystyle p(\widehat{\bf y}_{m}|{\bf H},\bar{\bf z}_{m})=\prod\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}[\tfrac{\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}_{n}^{T}\bar{\bf z}_{m})}{\sum\nolimits_{n^{\prime}}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}_{n^{\prime}}^{T}\bar{\bf z}_{m})}]^{\widehat{y}_{mn}},

(2)

where

is the number of classes, and

The function

defines a distribution over whether

is the perturbation of
{\bf I}_{m}
, where
y_{s}
is a binary label. Let

, and we have

\displaystyle p(y_{s}=1|\bar{\bf z}_{1:M},\bar{\bf z}^{\prime}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath})=\tfrac{\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\bf z}_{m}\circ\bar{\bf z}^{\prime}_{m}))}{\sum\nolimits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\bf z}_{m}\circ\bar{\bf z}_{f}))},

3.4
Inferring Conceptual Explanations using PACE

We begin by detailing the inference of image-level and patch-level explanations (i.e.,
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
and
\{{\bf z}_{mj}\}_{j=1}^{J}
) given the dataset-level concept parameters
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. We then discuss learning
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
later in Sec.
3.5
.

Inferring Patch-Level and Image-Level Concepts.

Given the dataset-level concept parameters
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, the patch embeddings
{\bf e}_{m}\triangleq[{\bf e}_{mj}]_{j=1}^{J}
, and the associated attention weights
{\bf a}_{m}\triangleq[a_{mj}]_{j=1}^{J}
, as well as the predicted label
\widehat{\bf y}_{m}
produced by the ViT, for each image
{\bf I}_{m}
, PACE infers the posterior distribution of the image-level concept explanation
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
, i.e.,
p(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}|{\bf e}_{m},{\bf a}_{m},\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K},\widehat{\bf y}_{m})
, and the posterior distribution of the patch-level concept explanation
{\bf z}_{mj}
, i.e.,
p({\bf z}_{mj}|{\bf e}_{m},{\bf a}_{m},\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K},\widehat{\bf y}_{m})
. Fig.
1
describes the inference process of PACE.

Variational Distributions.

The aforementioned posterior distributions are intractable; hence, we employ variational inference
(
Jordan et al., 1998
;
Blei et al., 2003
;
Chang & Blei, 2009
)
, using variational distributions
q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m})
and
q({\bf z}_{mj}|\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj})
to approximate them. This results in the following joint variational distribution:

\displaystyle q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\{{\bf z}_{mj}\}_{j=1}^{J}|\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m},\{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}\}_{j=1}^{J})

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m})\cdot\prod\nolimits_{j=1}^{J}q({\bf z}_{mj}|\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}),

(4)

where the variational parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}

are estimated by maximizing Eq.
5
(more details below).

Objective Function.

In line with the generative process outlined in Sec.
3.3
, for each image
m
sampled from the dataset, the optimal variational distributions are found by maximizing the following evidence lower bound (ELBO):

\displaystyle L({\bf e}_{mj}

\displaystyle,\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m},\widehat{\bf y}_{m},y_{s};\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath},\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K},{\bf H},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath})

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle L_{e}+L_{f}+L_{s},

(5)

\displaystyle L_{e}=~

\displaystyle L({\bf e}_{mj},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath},\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}),

\displaystyle L_{f}=~

\displaystyle L(\widehat{\bf y}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m};{\bf H}),

\displaystyle L_{s}=~

\displaystyle L(y_{s},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}).

(6)

This equation can be derived using log likelihood factorization of the variables in Fig.

(details provided in Appendix

Below we describe each term’s intuition:

1.

L_{e}=L({\bf e}_{mj},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath},\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K})
is the expected log likelihood of the joint distribution of patch embeddings
{\bf e}_{mj}
and the variational parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
.
This term models the generation of patch embeddings

in the ViT.

2.

L_{f}=L(\widehat{\bf y}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m};{\bf H})
is the expected log likelihood of the predicted label

given explanation
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
. This term reflects the

.

3.

is the expected log likelihood of the binary label
y_{s}
,
which indicates whether image
{\bf I}_{m}
(with its inferred concepts
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
) and
{\bf I}^{\prime}_{m}
(with its inferred concepts
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m}
) comes from the same image.
This term reflects the

.

Computing
L_{e}
.
We compute
L_{e}
as:

\displaystyle L_{e}=~

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log p({\bf e}_{mj},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath},\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K})]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{{k}}\phi_{mjk}a_{mj}\log{\mathcal{N}}({\bf e}_{mj}|\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k})+{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath})]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{{k}}\phi_{mjk}a_{mj}\{-\tfrac{1}{2}({\bf e}_{mj}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k})^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}^{-1}({\bf e}_{mj}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k})

\displaystyle-\log[(2\pi)^{d/2}|\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}|^{1/2}]\}+{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath})],

(7)

where the expectation is over the joint variational distribution in Eq.
4
.
d
is the dimension of the embedding
{\bf e}_{mj}
.

Computing
L_{f}
.
We compute
L_{f}
according to Eq.
2
:

\displaystyle L_{f}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\widehat{\bf y}_{m}|\bar{\bf z}_{m},{\bf H})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\widehat{y}_{mn}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m})-{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log(\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\bf z}_{m}))]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\widehat{y}_{mn}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m})-\log(\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m})),

(8)

where
N
is the number of classes for classification,
n
the class index. We approximate
\bar{\bf z}_{m}
by taking the average of
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
:
\bar{\bf z}_{m}\approx\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{J}}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{J}{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{mj}.
See Appendix
F.1
for details on how to approximate
\bar{\bf z}_{m}
. Eq.
8
implies that maximizing the log likelihood of the predicted class
\widehat{\bf y}_{m}
encourages a correlation between
\widehat{\bf y}_{m}
and the inferred patch-level concepts
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
, thereby enhancing PACE’s
faithfulness.

Computing
L_{s}
.

Inspired by contrastive learning
(
Chen et al., 2020
)
, for each original image
{\bf I}_{m}
, we first generate its perturbed image
{\bf I}^{\prime}_{m}
.
Then, with their associated patch-level concepts
\bar{\bf z}_{1:M}
and
\bar{\bf z}^{\prime}_{m}
from Eq.
1
, the
stability
term
L_{s}
is defined as the expected likelihood of the binary label
y_{s}
in Eq.
3
. Let
{\mathcal{F}}=\{1,..,M\}\backslash\{m\}
. We compute
L_{s}
as:

\displaystyle L_{s}=~

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log p(y_{s}=1|\bar{\bf z}_{1:M},\bar{\bf z}^{\prime}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath})]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\bf z}_{m}\circ\bar{\bf z}^{\prime}_{m})-{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log(\sum\nolimits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\bf z}_{m}\circ\bar{\bf z}_{f})))]

\displaystyle\approx~

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}\circ\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}_{m})-\log(\sum\nolimits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}\circ\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{f}))),

(9)

where
\circ
is the element-wise product. Eq.
9
indicates that maximizing the log likelihood of
y_{s}
encourages the inferred patch-level concepts from the original and perturbed patches (
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
from
{\bf I}_{m}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m}
from
{\bf I}^{\prime}_{m}
) to be similar, thus enhancing PACE’s
stability
against perturbations.
See detailed derivations of
L_{e}
,
L_{f}
, and
L_{s}
in Appendix
F.1
.

3.6
Summary of Learning and Inference of PACE

In summary, PACE is a variational Bayesian framework that consists of (1) the
learning stage
to train on the training set, and (2) the
inference stage
to explain on the test set.

For example, given a finetuned ViT classifier on a dataset, PACE explains it by (1) training the global parameters, i.e., the
dataset-level
concept centers
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k}
and covariance matrices
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}
(where
k=1,\dots,K
) as dataset-level explanations, on the training set (these are called
global
parameters because they are shared across all data points, e.g. images), and (2) inferring the
local
parameters, i.e., the
image-level
concepts (explanations)
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
and
patch-level
concepts (explanations)
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
, on the test set (these are called
local
parameters because each image has its own
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
).

Below, we discuss the learning and inference processes, respectively.

The Learning Stage.
In Sec.
3.5
, we describe the process of learning the
global parameters

\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}
(where
k=1,\dots,K
). As described in Alg.
1
, in each epoch
t
(
t=1,\dots,T
):

(1)

PACE first infers the
local
parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}
for each document
m
using Eq.
10
and Eq.
11
;

(2)

PACE then updates the
global
parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}
(where
k=1,\dots,K
) for the entire dataset using Eq.
12
and Eq.
13
.

The learning stage concludes at the
T^{th}
epoch. Note that the process above is iterative; it alternates between (1) updating the
local
parameters and (2) updating the
global
parameters.

The Inference Stage.
In Sec.
3.4
, we describe the process of inferring the
local
parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}
after the PACE learns the global parameters in the learning stage. Specifically, given the
global
parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}
(where
k=1,\dots,K
),

(1)

PACE initializes
local
parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}
;

(2)

Given the current
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}
, PACE updates the
local parameters

\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m}
using Eq.
10
;

(3)

Given the current
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}
, PACE updates the
local parameters

\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}
using Eq.
11
;

(4)

PACE repeats (2) and (3) until
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}
converge;

(5)

PACE then infers the image-level concept
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
using the learned variational distribution
q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m})
, which is a Dirichlet distribution. One can (roughly) think of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
as a normalized version of
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m}
.

3.7
Discussion and Theoretical Analysis

Our PACE addresses all five desiderata in Definition
3.1
:

•

Faithfulness
is encouraged by
maximizing the prediction
\widehat{\bf y}_{m}
’s likelihood, i.e.,
L_{f}
in Eq.
8
.

•

Stability
against perturbations is enhanced by maximizing the binary label
y_{s}
’s likelihood
L_{s}
in Eq.
9
.

•

Sparsity
is encouraged by the Dirichlet prior
p(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath})
that regularizes the inference of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
.

•

Multi-Level Structure
is intrinsically supported by our multi-level generative process in Sec.
3.3
.

•

Parsimony
is ensured by the flexibility in choosing the number of concepts
K
in PACE (see Appendix
A
).

Theorem
3.2
below further demonstrates that PACE’s inferred image-level and patch-level explanations,
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
and
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}\}_{j=1}^{J}
, align with the properties in Definition
3.1
.

Theorem 3.2

(
Mutual Information Maximization
)
.

The ELBO
in Eq.
5
is upper-bounded by the sum of (1) mutual information between contextual embeddings
{\bf e}_{m}
and multi-level explanation
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}\}_{j=1}^{J}
in Definition
3.1
, (2) mutual information between the predicted label
\widehat{\bf y}_{m}
and patch-level concept
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
, and (3) mutual information between the patch-level original concept
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
and perturbed concept
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m}
. Formally, with approximate posteriors
q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m})
and
q({\bf z}_{mj}|\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj})
, we have

\displaystyle L({\bf e}_{mj},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m},\widehat{\bf y}_{m},y_{s})

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle{I}({\bf e}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})+I(\widehat{\bf y}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})+I(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m})+C,

(14)

where the
C
is a constant.

The proof of Theorem
3.2
is provided in Appendix
E
.
Theorem
3.2
implies that maximizing the ELBO in Eq.
5
is equivalent to maximizing the sum of (1) mutual information between the contextual embeddings
{\bf e}_{m}
and
multi-level
conceptual explanations defined in Definition
3.1
, thereby ensuring the generated explanations are informative, (2) mutual information between the ViT prediction
\widehat{\bf y}_{m}
and patch-level concept
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
, thereby enhancing PACE’s
faithfulness
, and (3) mutual information between the patch-level original
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
and perturbed
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m}
, thereby enhancing PACE’s
stability
against perturbations.

4.3
Evaluation Metrics

With ViT-Base
(
Dosovitskiy et al., 2020
)
as the prediction model, we evaluate different methods against the five desiderata defined in Definition
3.1
:

•

(Linear) Faithfulness:

We fit a logistic regression (LR) model
\widehat{\bf y}_{m}=LR(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m})
to each dataset’s training set, with
\widehat{\bf y}_{m}
as the prediction of the ViT, and test the model’s accuracy on the test set. Higher accuracy indicates stronger (linear) faithfulness. Note that one can fit more complex models (e.g., nonlinear models such as neural networks) to evaluate nonlinear faithfulness; for simplicity, we focus on linear faithfulness.
Stability:

For each input image
{\bf I}_{m}
in the test set, we generate an augmented version
{\bf I}^{\prime}_{m}
(following
Chen et al. (2020)
), and compute the normalized difference between inferred concepts
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m}
, i.e.,
\tfrac{\|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m}\|}{\|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}\|}
. Lower values indicate stronger stability.

•

Sparsity:
We compute sparsity (with the threshold
\epsilon=0.1/K
) as
\tfrac{1}{K}\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\mymathbb{1}(|\theta_{mk}|<\epsilon)
, where K is the number of concepts.
For many concept-based explanation methods, including ours, the inferred activation typically normalizes to sum up to 1. If this is not the case, we normalize the explanation activation before calculating the sparsity score, ensuring a fair comparison.

•

Multi-Level Structure:
As highlighted in Sec.
1
, baseline models do not account for dataset-level and/or patch-level concepts, thereby possess
No
or
Partial
multi-level structure. In contrast, PACE is specifically designed to offer
Full
conceptual explanations at three levels: dataset, image, and patch. We will demonstrate in Sec.
4.5
that modeling embeddings’ distribution is instrumental in bridging three levels of ViT concepts.

•

Parsimony:
For the conceptual explanation methods PACE and CRAFT
(
Fel et al., 2023b
)
, we set the number of concepts
K=100
, to facilitate a fair comparison. Note that other baseline models’ number of concept
K
is constrained to the hidden dimension of ViT embeddings, i.e.,
K=768
; they therefore fall short in parsimony.

For details and the three other desiderata, see Appendix
A
.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we identify five desiderata
faithfulness
,
stability
,
sparsity
,
multi-level structure
, and
parsimony
when generating trustworthy concept-level explanations for ViTs. We develop the first general method, PACE, that is compatible with any transformer variants and satisfies these desiderata. Through both quantitative and qualitative evaluations, our method demonstrates superior performance in explaining post-hoc ViT predictions via visual concepts, outperforming state-of-the-art methods across various datasets. As a limitation, our approach assumes a fixed number of concepts (Similar to existing methods). Therefore future work could focus on developing PACE into a non-parametric explainer that automatically determines the number of concepts. Another limitation is that our approach requires access to hidden states and attention weights from the layers inside ViTs; we argue that this is an advantage because it allows our PACE to interpret vision foundation models’ internals thoroughly rather than simply their output superficially.

As we discuss in Definition
3.1
,

is defined as the proportion of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
’s entries nearing zero, i.e.,
\tfrac{1}{K}\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\mymathbb{1}(|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{mk}|<\epsilon)
,
with a small threshold
\epsilon>0
;
parsimony
is defined as the minimal number of concepts
K
to produce clear and simple explanations.
While sparsity is an
image-level
property, parsimony is
dataset-level
.

Example 1.

For example, first consider a dataset with four concepts and three images,
{\bf I}_{1}
,
{\bf I}_{2}
, and
{\bf I}_{3}
:

Theoretical Analysis

We provide the following proof of Theorem
3.2
. For convenience, let
\Omega=(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k=1}^{K},\Sigma_{k=1}^{K})
. We then introduce a helper joint distribution of the variables
{\bf e}_{m}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}
,
s({\bf e}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})=p({\bf e}_{m})q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}|{\bf e}_{m})
.

According to the definition of ELBO of Sec.
3.4
, in Eq.
5
and Eq.
6
, we only need to prove that

\displaystyle LHS=~

\displaystyle L_{e}+L_{f}+L_{s}

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle{I}({\bf e}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})+I(\widehat{\bf y}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})+I(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m})+C.

(16)

We split the proof into the following three separate part:

(1) The bound of
L_{e}
.
We have that

\displaystyle L_{e}={\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bf e}_{m})}[{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log p({\bf e}_{m}|\Omega,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})]]+{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}|\Omega)].

(17)

Since
{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}|\Omega)]\leq 0
, we are going to prove that

\displaystyle L_{e}\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bf e}_{m})}[{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log p({\bf e}_{m}|\Omega,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})]]\leq I_{s}({\bf e}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})-H({\bf e}_{m}).

(18)

In fact,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bf e}_{m})}[{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log p({\bf e}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m},\Omega)]]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bf e}_{m})}[{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log p({\bf e}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})]]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bf e}_{m})}[{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log\tfrac{q({\bf e}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})}{p({\bf e}_{m})}\tfrac{p({\bf e}_{m})p({\bf e}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})}{q({\bf e}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})}]]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bf e}_{m})}[{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log\tfrac{q({\bf e}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})}{p({\bf e}_{m})}]]+{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bf e}_{m})}[{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log p({\bf e}_{m})]]+{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bf e}_{m})}[{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log\tfrac{p({\bf e}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})}{q({\bf e}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})}]]

\displaystyle=I_{s}({\bf e}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})-H({\bf e}_{m})-{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[KL(q({\bf e}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})|p({\bf e}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}))]

\displaystyle\leq I_{s}({\bf e}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})-H({\bf e}_{m})-0,

(19)

where
H({\bf e}_{m})
is a constant.

(2) The bound of
L_{f}
.
With the constraint
-{\bf 1}\leq\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}_{n}\leq{\bf 1}(1\leq n\leq N)
, we have that

\displaystyle L_{f}=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\widehat{\bf y}_{m}|\bar{\bf z}_{m},{\bf H})]

\displaystyle\approx~

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\widehat{y}_{mn}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m})-\log(\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}))

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\widehat{y}_{mn}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m})

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{y}_{mn}\bar{\phi}_{mk}

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\sum_{\widehat{\bf y}_{m}}\sum_{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}}p(\widehat{\bf y}_{m},\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m})\log\tfrac{p(\widehat{\bf y}_{m},\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m})}{p(\widehat{\bf y}_{m})p(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m})}+C_{1}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle I(\widehat{\bf y}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})+C_{1},

(20)

where
C_{1}
is a constant.

(3) The bound of
L_{s}
.
With the constraint
{\bf 0}\leq\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}\leq{\bf 1}
, we have that

\displaystyle L_{s}=~

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log p(y_{s}=1|\bar{\bf z}_{1:M},\bar{\bf z}^{\prime}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath})]

\displaystyle\approx~

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}\circ\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}_{m})-\log(\sum\nolimits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}\circ\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{f})))

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}\circ\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}_{m})

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}\cdot\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}_{m}

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\sum_{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m}}\sum_{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m}}p(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m})\log\tfrac{p(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m})}{p(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m})p(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m})}+C_{2}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle I(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{m};\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{m})+C_{2},

(21)

where
C_{2}
is a constant.

Combining
(1\sim 3)
above concludes the proof.

Appendix F
Details on Learning PACE

F.1
Derivations of ELBO

Inferring
{\bf z}_{m}
.

According to Eq.
1
,

\displaystyle\bar{\bf z}_{m}=\tfrac{1}{J}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{J}{\bf z}_{mj},

(22)

where
{\bf z}_{mj}
can be approximate by a variational distribution parameterized by
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}
:

\displaystyle q({\bf z}_{mj}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj})=\text{Categorical}({\bf z}_{mj}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}),

(23)

which indicates that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[{\bf z}_{mj}]=\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}.

(24)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{\bf z}_{m}]=\tfrac{1}{J}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{J}{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{\bf z}_{mj}]=\tfrac{1}{J}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{J}\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj}=\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}.

(25)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\bar{\bf z}_{m}\approx\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}.

(26)

Computing
L_{e}
.

We can expand the ELBO in Eq.
7
as:

\displaystyle L_{e}=~

\displaystyle\log\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$\unboldmath}(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k})-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$\unboldmath}(\alpha_{k})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\alpha_{k}-1)(\Psi(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{mk})-\Psi(\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{k^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\phi_{mjk}(\Psi(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{mk})-\Psi(\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{mk^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\phi_{mjk}a_{mj}\{-\tfrac{1}{2}({\bf e}_{mj}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k})^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}^{-1}({\bf e}_{mj}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{k})-\log[(2\pi)^{d/2}|\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{k}|^{1/2}]\}

\displaystyle-\log\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$\unboldmath}(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{mk})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$\unboldmath}(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{mk})-\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{mk}-1)(\Psi(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{mk})-\Psi(\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$\unboldmath}_{mk^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\phi_{mjk}\log\phi_{mjk}.

(27)

We can interpret the meaning of each term of
L_{e}
as follows:

•

The sum of the first and the fourth terms, namely
{{\mathbb{E}}}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath})]-{{\mathbb{E}}}_{q}[\log q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m})]
, is equal to
-KL(q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m})|p(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath}))
, which is the negation of KL Divergence between the variational posterior probability
q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m})
and the prior probability
p(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath})
of the topic proportion
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}
for document
m
. Therefore maximizing the sum of these two terms is equivalent to minimizing the KL Divergence
KL(q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m})|p(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath}))
; this serves as a regularization term to make sure the inferred
q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m})
is close to its prior distribution
p(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$\unboldmath})
.

•

Similarly, the sum of the second and the last terms (ignoring the summation over the word index
j
for simplicity), namely
{{\mathbb{E}}_{q}}[\log p(z_{mj}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m})]-{{\mathbb{E}}_{q}}[\log q(z_{mj})]
is equal to
-KL(q(z_{mj})|p(z_{mj}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}))
, which is the negation of the KL Divergence between the variational posterior probability
q(z_{mj})
and the prior probability
p(z_{mj}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m})
of the word-level topic assignment
z_{mj}
for word
j
of document
m
. Therefore maximizing the sum of these two terms is equivalent to minimizing the KL Divergence
KL(q(z_{mj})|p(z_{mj}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m}))
; this serves as a regularization term to make sure the inferred
q(z_{mj})
is close to its “prior” distribution
p(z_{mj}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{m})
.

•

The third term
{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log p({\bf e}_{mj}|z_{mj},\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{z_{mj}},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{z_{mj}})]
is to maximize the log likelihood
p({\bf e}_{mj}|z_{mj},\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{z_{mj}},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{z_{mj}})
of every contextual embedding
{\bf e}_{mj}
(for word
j
of document
m
) conditioned on the inferred
z_{mj}
and the parameters
(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{z_{mj}},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}_{z_{mj}})
.

Computing
L_{f}
.

Eq.
8
is derived from employing Taylor’s expansion to Eq.
2
:

\displaystyle L_{f}=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\widehat{\bf y}_{m}|\bar{\bf z}_{m},{\bf H})]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\widehat{y}_{mn}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m})-{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log(\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\bf z}_{m}))]

\displaystyle\approx~

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\widehat{y}_{mn}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m})-\log(\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}+(1/2)\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}_{n}^{T}{\bf S}_{m}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}_{n})),

(28)

where
{\bf S}_{m}
is the covariance matrix of
\bar{\bf z}_{m}
.

We will see that for any entry of
{\bf S}_{m}
, i.e.
\forall x,y\in\{1,2,...,K\}
, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq{\bf S}_{m,xy}\leq\tfrac{1}{J^{2}}.

(29)

In our setting, the number of patches
J
in each image satisfies
J>100
, hence
{\bf S}_{m,xy}
is very close to zero.

We compute
{\bf S}_{m}
by definition:

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{m,xy}=~

\displaystyle Cov[\bar{\bf z}_{m}\bar{\bf z}_{m^{\prime}}]_{x,y}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[(\bar{z}_{mx}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}x}-{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{mx}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}x}])(\bar{z}_{my}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}y}-{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{my}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}y}])]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[(\bar{z}_{mx}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}x}-{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{mx}]{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}x}]])(\bar{z}_{my}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}y}-{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{my}]{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}y}]])]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[(\bar{z}_{mx}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}x}-\bar{\phi}_{mx}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}x})(\bar{z}_{my}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}y}-\bar{\phi}_{my}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}y})]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{mx}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}x}\bar{z}_{my}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}y}]-\bar{\phi}_{my}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}y}{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{mx}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}x}]-\bar{\phi}_{mx}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}x}{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{my}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}y}]+\bar{\phi}_{mx}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}x}\bar{\phi}_{my}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}y}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{mx}\bar{z}_{my}]{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}x}\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}y}]-\bar{\phi}_{mx}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}x}\bar{\phi}_{my}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}y}.

(30)

We then consider two different cases:

Case (1):
x=y
.

Then we have that

\displaystyle Cov[\bar{\bf z}_{m}\bar{\bf z}_{m^{\prime}}]_{x,y}=~

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{mx}^{2}]{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{m^{\prime}y}^{2}]-\bar{\phi}_{mx}^{2}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}y}^{2}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}_{mx}^{2}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}y}^{2}-\bar{\phi}_{mx}^{2}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}y}^{2}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle 0.

(31)

Case (2):
x\neq y
.

Note that

\displaystyle\bar{z}_{mx}\bar{z}_{my}=\tfrac{1}{J}\sum_{j}z_{mjx}\cdot\tfrac{1}{J}\sum_{j}z_{mjy}.

(32)

Given that
{\bf z}_{mj}
is a one-hot vector, we have

\displaystyle z_{mjx}\cdot z_{mjy}=0.

(33)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{mx}\bar{z}_{my}]=~

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{mx}]{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{z}_{my}]-\tfrac{1}{J^{2}}(\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\mathbb{E}}[z_{mjx}]{\mathbb{E}}[z_{mjy}])

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}_{mx}\bar{\phi}_{my}-\tfrac{1}{J^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\phi_{mjx}\phi_{mjy}.

(34)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle Cov[\bar{\bf z}_{m}\bar{\bf z}_{m^{\prime}}]_{x,y}=~

\displaystyle(\bar{\phi}_{mx}\bar{\phi}_{my}-\tfrac{1}{J^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\phi_{mjx}\phi_{mjy})(\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}x}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}y}-\tfrac{1}{J^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\phi_{m^{\prime}jx}\phi_{m^{\prime}jy})-\bar{\phi}_{mx}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}x}\bar{\phi}_{my}\bar{\phi}_{m^{\prime}y}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{J^{4}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\phi_{mjx}\phi_{mjy}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\phi_{m^{\prime}jx}\phi_{m^{\prime}jy}.

(35)

Since
{\bf 0}\leq\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}_{mj},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{mj}\leq{\bf 1}
, we have that

\displaystyle 0\leq{\bf S}_{m,xy}=Cov[\bar{\bf z}_{m}\bar{\bf z}_{m^{\prime}}]_{x,y}\leq\tfrac{1}{J^{2}}.

(36)

In summary, we can see that in either case, Eq.
29
holds.

Therefore we have

\displaystyle L_{f}\approx

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\widehat{y}_{mn}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m})-\log(\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$\unboldmath}^{T}_{n}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m})).

(37)

Computing
L_{s}
.

Similarly, by employ Taylor’s expansion of Eq.
3
, as well as Eq.
29
, we have that

\displaystyle L_{s}=~

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log p(y_{s}=1|\bar{\bf z}_{1:M},\bar{\bf z}^{\prime}_{m},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath})]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\bf z}_{m}\circ\bar{\bf z}^{\prime}_{m})-{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\log(\sum\nolimits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\bf z}_{m}\circ\bar{\bf z}_{f})))]

\displaystyle\approx~

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}_{m}-\log(\sum\nolimits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{f})+(1/2)\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}{\bf S}_{m}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}))

\displaystyle\approx~

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}\circ\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}_{m})-\log(\sum\nolimits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{T}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{m}\circ\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$\unboldmath}}_{f}))),

(38)

where the parameter
\beta
is learned jointly by gradient-based optimization algorithms, such as Adam.
