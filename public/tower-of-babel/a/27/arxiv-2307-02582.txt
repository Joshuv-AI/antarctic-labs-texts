Estimating the roughness exponent of stochastic volatility from discrete observations of the integrated variance
Xiyue Han, Alexander Schied

Abstract
We consider the problem of estimating the roughness of the volatility process in a stochastic volatility model that arises as a nonlinear function of fractional Brownian motion with drift. To this end, we introduce a new estimator that measures the so-called roughness exponent of a continuous trajectory, based on discrete observations of its antiderivative. The estimator has a very simple form and can be computed with great efficiency on large data sets. It is not derived from distributional assumptions but from strictly pathwise considerations. We provide conditions on the underlying trajectory under which our estimator converges in a strictly pathwise sense. Then we verify that these conditions are satisfied by almost every sample path of fractional Brownian motion (with drift). As a consequence, we obtain strong consistency theorems in the context of a large class of rough volatility models, such as the rough fractional volatility model and the rough Bergomi model. We also demonstrate that our estimator is robust with respect to proxy errors between the integrated and realized variance, and that it can be applied to estimate the roughness exponent directly from the price trajectory. Numerical simulations show that our estimation procedure performs well after passing to a scale-invariant modification of our estimator.

Introduction

Consider a stochastic volatility model whose price process satisfies

(1.1)

dS_{t}=\sigma_{t}S_{t}\,dB_{t},\qquad S_{0}=s_{0}>0,

where
B
is a standard Brownian motion and
\sigma_{t}
is a continuous and adapted stochastic process. Since the publication of the seminal paper
[
18
]
by Gatheral, Jaisson, and Rosenbaum, it has been widely accepted that the sample paths of
\sigma_{t}
often do not exhibit diffusive behavior but instead are much rougher. A specific example suggested in
[
18
]
is to model the log volatility by a fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. That is,

(1.2)

\sigma_{t}=\exp(X^{H}_{t}),

where
X^{H}
solves the following integral equation

(1.3)

X^{H}_{t}=x_{0}+\rho\int_{0}^{t}(\mu-X^{H}_{s})\,ds+W^{H}_{t},\qquad t\geq 0,

for a fractional Brownian motion
W^{H}
with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
. In this model, the ‘roughness’ of the trajectories of
X^{H}
is governed by the Hurst parameter
H
, and it was pointed
out
in
[
18
]
that rather small values of
H
appear to be most adequate for capturing the stylized facts of empirical volatility time series. Such a model is often referred to as the rough fractional stochastic volatility model.

In this paper, we include the case
H\geq 1/2
, in which the volatility process
\sigma

Since the publication of
[

]
, many alternative rough volatility models have been proposed, e.g., the rough Heston model
[

and the rough Bergomi model

The present paper contributes to the literature on rough volatility by considering the statistical estimation of the degree of roughness of the volatility process
\sigma_{t}
.
There are several difficulties that arise in this context.

The first difficulty consists in the fact that in reality the volatility process
\sigma_{t}
cannot be observed directly; only the asset prices
S
are known. Thus, one typically computes the quadratic variation of the log stock prices,

(1.4)

\langle\log S\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}_{s}\,ds,

which is also called the integrated variance, and then performs numerical differentiation to estimate proxies
\widehat{\sigma}_{t}
for the actual values of
\sigma_{t}
. The roughness estimation is then based on those proxy values
\widehat{\sigma}_{t}
. For instance, this two-step procedure is underlying the statistical analysis for empirical volatilities in
[
18
]
, where roughness estimates were based on proxy values
\widehat{\sigma}_{t}
taken from the Oxford-Man Institute of Quantitative Finance Realized Library. A problem with that approach is that estimation errors in the proxy values
\widehat{\sigma}_{t}
might substantially distort the outcomes in the final roughness estimation; see Fukasawa et al.
[
17
]
and Cont and Das
[
12
]
.

As a matter of fact, the quadratic variation (
1.4
) is usually approximated by a finite sum of the form
\sum_{i}(\log S_{t_{i}}-\log S_{t_{i-1}})^{2}
based on discrete observations
S_{t_{i}}
of the price process. This finite sum can be regarded as the (cumulative) realized variance of the price trajectory
S
. The bias caused by this error is emphasized in
[
17
]
, where it is assumed that the approximation errors are log-normally distributed and independent of the Brownian motion
B
in (
1.1
), and a Whittle-type estimator for the Hurst parameter is developed based on quasi-likelihood. Another attempt to tackle this measurement error is made by Bolko et al.
[
7
]
, where in a similar framework, the proposed estimator is based on the generalized method of moments approach. Chong et al.
[
10
,
11
]
substantially extend the previous results by alleviating the assumption on proxy errors and basing the volatility model on a semi-parametric setup, in which, with the exception of the Hurst parameter of the underlying fractional Brownian motion, all components are fully non-parametric. One of the conclusions from
[
7
,
17
,
10
]
is that the error arising from approximating the quadratic variation (
1.4
) with finite sum
\sum_{i}(\log S_{t_{i}}-\log S_{t_{i-1}})^{2}
could lead to a biased estimation result unless properly controlled.

Here, we analyze a new estimator for the roughness of the volatility process
\sigma
that is based directly on discrete observations of the quadratic variation (
1.4
). Our estimator has a very simple form and can be computed with great efficiency on large data sets. It is not derived from distributional assumptions, as most other estimators in the literature, but from strictly pathwise considerations that were developed in
[
21

23

As a consequence, our estimator does not actually measure the traditional Hurst parameter, which quantifies the autocorrelation of a stochastic process and hence does not make sense in a strictly pathwise setting. Instead, our estimator measures the so-called roughness exponent, which was introduced in
[
23
]
as the reciprocal of the critical exponent for the power variations of trajectories.
For fractional Brownian motion, this roughness exponent coincides with the Hurst parameter, but it can also be computed for many other trajectories, including certain fractal functions.

In

]

To incorporate the measurement errors between the integrated and realized variance into our estimation framework, we construct a new estimator

in

. It is worthwhile to point out that
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
and

admit very simple forms, and condition (

is robust with respect to proxy errors and readily applicable to financial time series from markets. Moreover, a key insight from
Section

We believe that the fact that our estimator is built on a strictly pathwise approach makes it very versatile and applicable also in situations in which trajectories are not based on fractional Brownian motion. As a matter of fact, our Examples

and

One disadvantage of our original estimator

The subsequent

2
Main results

Consider a stochastic volatility model whose price process satisfies

(2.1)

dS_{t}=\sigma_{t}S_{t}\,dB_{t},\qquad S_{0}=s_{0}>0,

where
B
is a standard Brownian motion and
\sigma_{t}
is a continuous and adapted stochastic process.
As explained in the introduction, our goal in this paper is to estimate the roughness of the trajectories
t\mapsto\sigma_{t}
directly from discrete, equidistant observations of the integrated variance,

(2.2)

\langle\log S\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}_{s}\,ds,

without having first to compute proxy values for
\sigma_{t}
via numerical differentiation of
t\mapsto\langle\log S\rangle_{t}
. This is important, because in reality the volatility
\sigma_{t}
is not directly observable and numerical errors in the computation of its proxy values might distort the roughness estimate (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
).

While our main results are concerned with rough stochastic volatility models based on fractional Brownian motion, a significant portion of our approach actually works completely trajectorial-wise, in a model-free setting; see
Section

3
. So let
x:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be any continuous function.
For
p\geq 1
, the
p^{\text{th}}
variation of the function
x
along the
n^{\text{th}}
dyadic partition is defined as

(2.3)

\langle x\rangle^{(p)}_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}|x((k+1)2^{-n})-x(k2^{-n})|^{p}.

If there exists
{R}\in[0,1]
such that

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\langle x\rangle^{(p)}_{n}=\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{for}\quad p>1/{R},\\
\infty&\quad\text{for}\quad p<1/{R},\end{cases}

we follow
[
23
]
in referring to
{R}
as the
roughness exponent
of
x
. Intuitively, the smaller
R
the rougher the trajectory
x
and vice versa. Moreover, if
x
is a typical sample path of fractional Brownian motion, the roughness exponent
{R}
is equal to the traditional Hurst parameter (see in
[
23
, Theorem 5.1]
). An analysis of general properties of the roughness exponent can be found in
[
23
]
. There, we also provide an estimation procedure for
{R}
from discrete observations of the trajectory
x
.
However, the problem of estimating
{R}
for a trajectory of stochastic volatility is more complex, because volatility cannot be measured directly; only asset prices and their integrated variance (
2.2
) can be observed. In our current pathwise setting, this corresponds to making discrete observations of

(2.4)

y(t)=\int_{0}^{t}g(x(s))\,ds,\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1,

where
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is sufficiently regular. For instance, in the rough fractional stochastic volatility model (
1.1
), (
1.2
), where log-volatility is given by a fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (
1.3
), we will take
x
as a trajectory of the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process and
g(t)=(e^{t})^{2}=e^{2t}
.

Let us now introduce our estimator. Suppose that for some given
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have the discrete observations
\{y(k2^{-n-2}):k=0,\dots,2^{n+2}\}
of the function
y
in (
2.4
). Based on these data points, we introduce the coefficients

(2.5)

\vartheta_{n,k}:=2^{3n/2+3}\left(y\Big(\frac{4k}{2^{n+2}}\Big)-2y\Big(\frac{4k+1}{2^{n+2}}\Big)+2y\Big(\frac{4k+3}{2^{n+2}}\Big)-y\Big(\frac{4k+4}{2^{n+2}}\Big)\right),

for
0\leq k\leq 2^{n}-1
.
Our estimator for the roughness exponent of the trajectory
g\circ x
is now given by

(2.6)

\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y):=1-\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\vartheta_{n,k}^{2}}.

This estimator was first proposed in
[
21
, Remark 2.2]
. In
Section

3.1
, we provide a detailed explanation of the rationale behind the estimator
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
and how it relates to the results in
[
21
,
23
]
. Our estimator
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
admits a simple form that is easy to calculate. More importantly, this simple nature straightforwardly leads to a new estimator
\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
, which can estimate the roughness exponent from realized variance; see
Section

2.3
.

2.1
Strong consistency theorems

We can now state our main results, which show the strong consistency of
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
when it is applied to
the situation in which
x
is a typical trajectory of fractional Brownian motion with possible drift. In the sequel,
W^{H}=(W^{H}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}
will denote a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H
, defined on a given probability space
(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}},\mathbb{P})
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For
H\in(0,1)
and a strictly monotone function
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, let
X_{t}:=g(W^{H}_{t})
and

Y_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}g(W^{H}_{s})\,ds,\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1.

Then, with probability one,
X
admits the roughness exponent
H
and we have
\lim_{n}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(Y)=H
.

The preceding theorem solves our problem of consistently estimating the roughness exponent for a rough volatility model with
\sigma_{t}^{2}=g(W^{H}_{t})
. However, empirical
volatility is mean-reverting, and that effect is not captured by this model. Therefore, it is desirable to replace the fractional Brownian motion
W^{H}
with a mean-reverting process such as the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.
This process
was first introduced in
[
9
]
as the solution of the integral equation

(2.7)

X^{H}_{t}=x_{0}+\rho\int_{0}^{t}(\mu-X^{H}_{s})\,ds+W^{H}_{t},\qquad t\in[0,1],

where
x_{0},\rho,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
are given parameters. The integral equation (
2.7
) can be uniquely solved in a pathwise manner. The fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process was suggested by Gatheral et al.
[
18
]
as a suitable model for log volatility, i.e.,
\sigma_{t}=e^{X^{H}_{t}}
. In our context, this model choice implies that we are making discrete observations of the process

\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}_{s}\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}e^{2X^{H}_{s}}\,ds,\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1.

The fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process can simply be regarded as a fractional Brownian motion with starting point
x_{0}
and adapted and absolutely continuous drift
\rho(\mu-X^{H}_{s})
, and so it falls into the class of stochastic processes considered in the following theorem, which we are quoting from
[
22
]
for the convenience of the reader in the following simplified form.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X
be given by

(2.8)

X_{t}:=x_{0}+W^{H}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{s}\,ds,\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1,

where
\xi
is progressively measurable with respect to the natural filtration of
W^{H}
and satisfies the following additional assumption.

•

If
H<1/2
, we assume that the function
t\mapsto\xi_{t}
is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. bounded in the sense that there exists a finite random variable
C
such that
|\xi_{t}(\omega)|\leq C(\omega)
for a.e.
t
and
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
\omega
.

•

If
H>1/2
, we assume that for
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
\omega
the function
t\mapsto\xi_{t}(\omega)
is Hölder continuous with some exponent
\alpha(\omega)>2H-1
.

Then the law of
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of
(x_{0}+W^{H}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
.

In the preceding theorem, the assumption that
\xi
is adapted to the natural filtration of
W^{H}
can be relaxed; see
[
22
]
for details. Moreover, suppose that
X
is an adapted solution to the fractional integral equation

X_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(X_{s})\,ds+W^{H}_{t},\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1,

where
b:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is locally bounded and, for
H>1/2
, locally Hölder continuous with some exponent
\alpha>2-1/H
. Then
[
22
, Theorem 1.5]
states that the law of
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is equivalent to the law of
(x_{0}+W^{H}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
. This applies in particular to the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process
X^{H}
defined in (
2.7
), where
b(x)=\rho(\mu-x)
.
The following result is now an immediate corollary of Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Suppose that
X
is as in
Theorem

2.2
and
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is strictly monotone. Then the stochastic process

g(X_{t})=g\bigg(x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{s}\,ds+W^{H}_{t}\bigg),\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1,

admits
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. the roughness exponent
H
. Moreover, for

Y_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}g(X_{s})\,ds
, we have
\lim_{n}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(Y)=H

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

By
Theorem

2.2
, adding a drift to fractional Brownian motion can also be regarded as changing the underlying probability law.
Corollary

2.3
can therefore also be stated as follows: The strong consistency of
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
observed in
Theorem

2.1
remains true after replacing the law of
W^{H}
with a law that arises in the context of
Theorem

2.2
. This invariance can be seen as robustness of
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
with respect to model misspecification. In addition, the strong consistency of our estimator is unaffected by the change of the nonlinear scale function
g
, which is yet another indication of the estimator’s robustness and versatility.

Next, we will further illustrate the robustness of our estimator by showing that the estimator
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
is a strongly consistent estimator for the Hurst parameter
H
associated with the rough Bergomi model
[
16
]
. To this end, consider a (re-scaled) Riemann–Liouville process
(Z^{H}_{t})_{t\geq 0}

Moreover, for given

, we denote
Z^{H,\nu}_{t}:=Z^{H}_{t+\nu}-Z^{H}_{\nu}

. The class of stochastic volatility models considered hereupon consists of volatility processes

that admit the following dynamics
2

2

Here we include the case
H\geq 1/2
, in which the trajectories of

and

(2.9)

\sigma_{t}=\exp(V^{H,\nu}_{t})\quad\text{and}\quad V^{H,\nu}_{t}:=\gamma Z^{H,\nu}_{t}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4H}t^{2H},\qquad t\in[0,1].

In particular, taking

Suppose that

2.2
A scale-invariant estimator

By definition, the roughness exponent is scale-invariant, but our estimator is not. To wit, for every trajectory
y\in C[0,1]
we have

\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(\lambda y)-\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y)=-\frac{\log_{2}|\lambda|}{n}\quad\text{for}\quad\lambda\neq 0.

Consequently, a scaling factor
\lambda
may either remove or introduce a bias into an estimate, and it can notably slow down or speed up the convergence of
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y)
. This will be illustrated by the simulation studies provided in
Section

2.4
.

A number of scale-invariant modifications of
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
can be constructed in a manner completely analogous to the definitions in
[
23
, Section 8]
. Here, we carry this out for the analogue of sequential scaling proposed in
[
23
, Definition 8.1]
. The underlying idea is fairly simple: We choose
m<n
and then search for that scaling factor
\lambda
that minimizes the weighted mean-squared differences
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{k}(\lambda y)-\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{k-1}({\lambda y})
for
k=m+1,\dots,n
. The intuition is that such an optimal scaling factor
\lambda
enforces the convergence of the estimates
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{k}(\lambda y)
.

Definition 2.5
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m}\geq 0
with
\alpha_{0}>0
.
For
n>m
, the
sequential scaling factor

\lambda_{n}^{s}
and the
sequential scale estimate

{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}(y)
are defined as follows,

(2.10)

\begin{split}\lambda^{s}_{n}&:=\mathop{\hbox{\rm arg\,min}}_{\lambda>0}\sum_{k=n-m}^{n}\alpha_{n-k}\Big(\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{k}(\lambda y)-\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{k-1}(\lambda y)\Big)^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}(y):=\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(\lambda^{s}_{n}y).\end{split}

The corresponding mapping
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}:C[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
will be called the
sequential scale estimator
.

Just as Proposition 8.3 in
[
23
]
, one can prove the following result.

Proposition 2.6
.

Consider the context of Definition
2.5
with fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m}\geq 0
such that
\alpha_{0}>0
.

(a)

The optimization problem (
2.10
) admits a unique solution for every function
y\in C[0,1]
. In particular, all objects in Definition
2.5
are well defined.

(b)

The sequential scale estimator
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}
can be represented as follows as a linear combination of
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n-m-1},\dots,\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
,

{\mathscr{R}}_{n}^{s}=\beta_{n,n}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}+\beta_{n,n-1}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n-1}+\cdots+\beta_{n,n-m-1}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n-m-1},

where

\beta_{n,k}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{c^{\textrm{\rm s}}_{n}n^{2}(n-1)}&\text{if $k=n$,}\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{\textrm{\rm s}}_{n}nk}\Big(\frac{\alpha_{n-k}}{k-1}-\frac{\alpha_{n-k-1}}{k+1}\Big)&\text{if $n-m\leq k\leq n-1$,}\\
\displaystyle\frac{-\alpha_{m}}{c^{\textrm{\rm s}}_{n}n(n-m)(n-m-1)}&\text{if $k=n-m-1$,}\end{cases}\quad\text{for}\quad c^{s}_{n}:=\sum_{k=n-m}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{n-k}}{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}.

(c)

The sequential scale estimator is scale-invariant. That is, for
n>m
,
y\in C[0,1]
, and
\lambda\neq 0
, we have
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}(\lambda y)={\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}(y)
.

(d)

If
y\in C[0,1]
and
R\in[0,1]
are such that there exists
\lambda\neq 0
for which
|\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(\lambda y)-R|=\mathcal{O}(a_{n})
as
n\uparrow\infty
for some sequence
(a_{n})
with
a_{n}=o(1/n)
, then
|{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}(y)-R|=\mathcal{O}(na_{n})
.

In particular, assertion
(d)
of
Proposition

2.6
establishes the consistency criteria and obtains the rate of convergence of the sequential scale estimator
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}
. By applying the above result, the following theorem verifies the strong consistency and provides the rate of convergence of
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}
for the fractional Brownian motion with drift. The proof of
Theorem

2.7
is provided in
Section

4.3
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha_{0}>0
and
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{m}\geq 0
be fixed. Let
X
be as in (
2.8
) and

Y_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}\,ds,\qquad t\in[0,1].

Then the following almost sure rate of convergence holds for the sequential scale estimator
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}
,

(2.11)

|{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}(Y)-H|=\mathcal{O}\big(2^{-n/2}\sqrt{\log n}\big).

Remark 2.8
.

We now investigate the impact of the integration operation on the estimation of the roughness exponent. Let
R^{s}_{n}
be the sequential scale estimator based on
\widehat{R}_{n}
as in
[
23
, Definition 8.1]
, suppose the process
X
as in (
2.8
) can be directly observed. It is shown in
[

, Corollary 8.4]
that when applying

to estimate the Hurst parameter for

, the following almost sure rate of convergence holds

(2.12)

|R^{s}_{n}(X)-H|=\mathcal{O}\big(2^{-n/2}\sqrt{\log n}\big).

By comparing the rates of convergence in (

2.3
Estimation of the roughness exponent from realized variance

As previously mentioned, the integrated variance (
1.4
) is usually approximated by a finite sum of the form
\sum_{i}(\log S_{t_{i}}-\log S_{t_{i-1}})^{2}
based on discrete observations
S_{t_{i}}
of the price process. It has been shown in the literature
[
7
,
17
]
that the error arising from the approximation of the integrated with the realized variance introduces an ‘extra’ noise to the true ‘signal’
\langle\log S\rangle
, often resulting in underestimating the roughness exponent of the hidden volatility process
\sigma
.

The error between the integrated and realized variance has been previously studied in the framework of roughness estimation. Those studies were pioneered by Fukasawa et al.
[
17
]
, where a Whittle-type estimator for the Hurst parameter is developed under the assumption that approximation errors are log-normally distributed and independent of the Brownian motion
B
in (
1.1
). Under a similar setting, Bolko et al.
[
7
]
proposed an estimator based on the generalized method of moments approach. Finally, Chong et al.
[
10
,
11
]
alleviated the assumption on proxy errors in a semi-parametric setup, in which, with the exception of the Hurst parameter of the underlying fractional Brownian motion, all components are fully nonparametric.

In this section, we will show that our estimator is very robust and effective in accommodating the proxy error between the realized and integrated variance. To this end, we continue to consider a stochastic volatility model whose price process satisfies

(2.13)

dS_{t}=\sigma_{t}S_{t}\,dB_{t},\qquad S_{0}=s_{0}>0,

where
B
is a standard Brownian motion and
\sigma_{t}
is a continuous and adapted stochastic process. We denote the integrated variance by

(2.14)

Y_{t}=\langle\log S\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}_{s}\,ds,\qquad t\in[0,1].

Furthermore, we let
(m_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a fixed increasing sequence, where
m_{n}\geq 1
can be regarded as the number of observed data points used to compute the realized variance over each interval
[k2^{-n},(k+1)2^{-n}]
. We denote

(2.15)

\widehat{Y}^{(n)}_{t}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor 2^{n}m_{n}t\rfloor}\left(\log S_{\frac{k}{2^{n}m_{n}}}-\log S_{\frac{k-1}{2^{n}m_{n}}}\right)^{2}.

The process
\widehat{Y}^{(n)}_{t}
is the realized variance calculated from the price process (
2.13
) with a mesh size
(2^{n}m_{n})^{-1}
. Let us define the proxy coefficients
\widetilde{\vartheta}_{n,k}
computed from
\widehat{Y}^{(n)}_{t}
,

(2.16)

\widetilde{\vartheta}_{n,k}:=2^{3n/2+3}\left(\widehat{Y}^{(n+2)}_{\frac{4k}{2^{n+2}}}-2\widehat{Y}^{(n+2)}_{\frac{4k+1}{2^{n+2}}}+2\widehat{Y}^{(n+2)}_{\frac{4k+3}{2^{n+2}}}-\widehat{Y}^{(n+2)}_{\frac{4k+4}{2^{n+2}}}\right).

By replacing
\vartheta_{n,k}
with
\widetilde{\vartheta}_{n,k}
, we can construct an estimator
\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
that directly estimates the roughness exponent from the realized variance as follows

\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(S):=1-\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\widetilde{\vartheta}^{2}_{n,k}},

where the notation
S
specifies price process that
\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
estimates from. Next, we denote

Z^{(n)}_{k}:=\left(\widehat{Y}^{(n)}_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}-\widehat{Y}^{(n)}_{\frac{k-1}{2^{n}}}\right)-\left(Y_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}-Y_{\frac{k-1}{2^{n}}}\right),\qquad 1\leq k\leq 2^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

To illustrate how to incorporate the proxy error into our estimation framework, we impose the following assumption on
Z^{(n)}_{k}
. To this end, we denote
{\mathscr{F}}^{\sigma}=\bigcup_{t\geq 0}{\mathscr{F}}^{\sigma}_{t}
, where
{\mathscr{F}}^{\sigma}_{t}=\sigma(\{\sigma_{s},s\in[0,t]\})
.

Assumption 2.9
.

The random variables
(Z^{(n)}_{k})
are independent conditionally on
{\mathscr{F}}^{\sigma}
, and

Z^{(n)}_{k}\Big|{\mathscr{F}}^{\sigma}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{2}{2^{n}m_{n}}\int_{\frac{k-1}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}\sigma_{s}^{4}\,ds\right).

Furthermore, we assume that
\mathbb{E}[\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{8}_{s}\,ds]<\infty
.

This assumption is similar to, though slightly stronger than, the assumptions employed in Fukasawa et al.
[
17
]
and Bolko et al.
[
7
]
. Intuitively, this assumption results from the central limit theorem approximation of the proxy error; see, e.g.,
[
3
]
. Under this assumption, we are able to show that
\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(S)
and
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(Y)
converge to the same value, provided that
m_{n}
grows fast enough, which is the content of the following theorem.

Theorem 2.10
.

Suppose that
2.9
holds and there exists
R\in(0,1)
such that
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(Y)\rightarrow R
almost surely. If

(2.17)

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}m_{n}>2R,

then
\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(S)\rightarrow R
with probability one.

Proof of
Theorem

2.10
.

We denote
\zeta_{n,k}=\widetilde{\vartheta}_{n,k}-\vartheta_{n,k}
, and subtracting (
2.5
) from (
2.16
) gives

\zeta_{n,k}=2^{3n/2+3}\left(-Z^{(n+2)}_{4k}+Z^{(n+2)}_{4k+1}+Z^{(n+2)}_{4k+2}-Z^{(n+2)}_{4k+3}\right).

Applying
2.9
to the above equation yields that

\zeta_{n,k}|{\mathscr{F}}^{\sigma}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{2^{2n+7}}{m_{n+2}}\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\sigma_{s}^{4}\,ds\right).

Hence, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[2^{(2R-2)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\zeta_{n,k}^{2}\right]&=2^{(2R-2)n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta_{n,k}^{2}\Big|{\mathscr{F}}^{\sigma}\right]\right]=2^{(2R-2)n}\left(\frac{2^{2n+7}}{m_{n+2}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\sigma_{s}^{4}\,ds\right]\\
&=\frac{2^{2Rn+7}}{m_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{4}_{s}\,ds\right]\longrightarrow 0.\end{split}

Furthermore, applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

(2.18)

\begin{split}\text{\rm Var}\left[2^{(2R-2)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\zeta_{n,k}^{2}\right]&=2^{(4R-4)n}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\hbox{\rm cov}\left[\zeta_{n,j}^{2},\zeta_{n,k}^{2}\right]\leq 2^{(4R-4)n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\sqrt{\text{\rm Var}\left[\zeta_{n,k}^{2}\right]}\right)^{2}\\
&=2^{(4R-4)n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\sqrt{\text{\rm Var}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta_{n,k}^{2}|{\mathscr{F}}^{\sigma}\right]\right]+\mathbb{E}\big[\text{\rm Var}[\zeta_{n,k}^{2}|{\mathscr{F}}^{\sigma}]\big]}\right)^{2}.\end{split}

It follows from Jensen’s inequality that

\begin{split}\text{\rm Var}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta_{n,k}^{2}|{\mathscr{F}}^{\sigma}\right]\right]&=\text{\rm Var}\left(\frac{2^{2n+7}}{m_{n+2}}\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\sigma_{s}^{4}\,ds\right)=\frac{2^{4n+14}}{m^{2}_{n+2}}\text{\rm Var}\left(\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\sigma_{s}^{4}\,ds\right)\\
&\leq\frac{2^{4n+14}}{m^{2}_{n+2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\sigma_{s}^{4}\,ds\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{2^{3n+14}}{m_{n+2}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\sigma_{s}^{8}\,ds\right].\end{split}

Furthermore, a similar argument yields

\mathbb{E}\big[\text{\rm Var}[\zeta_{n,k}^{2}|{\mathscr{F}}^{\sigma}]\big]=2\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{2^{2n+7}}{m_{n+2}}\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\sigma_{s}^{4}\,ds\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{2^{3n+15}}{m_{n+2}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\sigma_{s}^{8}\,ds\right].

Thus, we have

\begin{split}\text{\rm Var}\left[2^{(2R-2)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\zeta_{n,k}^{2}\right]&\leq\frac{2^{(4R-1)n+16}}{m^{2}_{n+2}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\sigma_{s}^{8}\,ds\right]}\right)^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{2^{4Rn+16}}{m^{2}_{n+2}}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\sigma_{s}^{8}\,ds\right]=\frac{2^{4Rn+16}}{m^{2}_{n+2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\sigma_{s}^{8}\,ds\right],\end{split}

which, by condition (
2.17
), converges to zero exponentially fast. Thus, by the fast
L_{2}
-convergence, we have
2^{(2R-2)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\zeta_{n,k}^{2}
converges to zero with probability one. From here, note that

2^{(2R-2)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\widetilde{\vartheta}^{2}_{n,k}=2^{(2R-2)n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\vartheta^{2}_{n,k}+\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\zeta^{2}_{n,k}+2\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\vartheta_{n,k}\zeta_{n,k}\right),

and it remains to show that the third term on the right-hand side converges to zero as
n\uparrow\infty
. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{(2R-2)n}\left|\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\vartheta_{n,k}\zeta_{n,k}\right|\leq\sqrt{\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{(2R-2)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\vartheta^{2}_{n,k}}\sqrt{\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{(2R-2)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\zeta^{2}_{n,k}}=0.

Hence, we get

\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{(2R-2)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\widetilde{\vartheta}^{2}_{n,k}=\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{(2R-2)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\vartheta^{2}_{n,k}.

Moreover, in an analogous manner, we can obtain that

\liminf_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{(2R-2)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\widetilde{\vartheta}^{2}_{n,k}=\liminf_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{(2R-2)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\vartheta^{2}_{n,k},

Taking logarithms on both sides of the above identities, dividing by
n
and letting
n\uparrow\infty
complete the proof.

∎

Theorem

2.10
provides a simple condition guaranteeing that the estimates
\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(S)
and
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(Y)
converge to the same limit as
n
approaches infinity. Particularly,
Theorem

2.10
specifies the number of data points required to compute the realized variance for each interval
[k2^{-n},(k+1)2^{-n}]
. For instance, if the volatility process
\sigma_{t}
admits a relatively small roughness exponent, the realized variance only needs to be computed on a relatively sparse mesh so that the estimator
\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
is a consistent estimator. This is because a rough ‘signal’
\sigma_{t}
is very persistent against the proxy error between the integrated and realized variance. In contrast, to achieve a consistent estimation for the roughness exponent for a relatively smooth volatility process
\sigma_{t}
, the proxy error between the integrated and realized variance has to be very small so that the actual roughness of the ‘signal’
\sigma_{t}
is not overshadowed by the ‘noise’
|Y_{t}-\widehat{Y}^{(n)}_{t}|
.

It is worth noting that the estimators
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
take remarkably simple forms. Moreover, condition (
2.17
) is straightforward to verify, making our estimation framework very practical and easy to implement in real-life applications. Furthermore, one conceptual contribution of
Theorem

2.10
is to show that estimating the ’roughness’ of the volatility process from price trajectory can be decomposed into two distinct tasks, even in a strictly pathwise manner. First, it involves constructing a consistent estimator from discrete observations of integrated variance. Second, one needs to analyze the perturbation caused by observation noise to determine the number of observed prices included in each realized variance calculation.

Pathwise estimation

In this section, we formulate conditions on a single trajectory

under which the estimates
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y)

. In

x

We start by summarizing some key results and concepts from
[

23
]

\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
.

3.1
The rationale behind the estimator
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}

Recall that the Faber–Schauder functions are defined as

e_{-1,0}(t):=t,\quad e_{0,0}(t):=(\min\{t,1-t\})^{+},\quad e_{m,k}(t):=2^{-m/2}e_{0,0}(2^{m}t-k)

for
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. It is well known that the restrictions of the Faber–Schauder functions to
[0,1]
form a Schauder basis for
C[0,1]
. More precisely, our function
x\in C[0,1]
can be uniquely represented as the uniform limit
x=\lim_{n}x_{n}
, where

(3.1)

x_{n}=x(0)+\left(x(1)-x(0)\right)e_{-1,0}+\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{m}-1}\theta_{m,k}e_{m,k},

and the Faber–Schauder coefficients
\theta_{m,k}
are given by

(3.2)

\theta_{m,k}=2^{m/2}\left(2x\Big(\frac{2k+1}{2^{m+1}}\Big)-x\Big(\frac{k}{2^{m}}\Big)-x\Big(\frac{k+1}{2^{m}}\Big)\right).

As a matter of fact, it is easy to see that the function
x_{n}
is simply the linear interpolation of
x
based on the supporting grid
\mathbb{T}_{n}=\{k2^{-n}:k=0,\dots,2^{n}\}
.

In
[
23
]
, we derived simple conditions under which the trajectory
x
admits a roughness exponent
{R}\in[0,1]
and also suggested a way in which
{R}
can be estimated from discrete observations of
x
. Specifically, it follows from Theorem 2.4 and Proposition 4.1 in
[
23
]
that if the Faber–Schauder coefficients satisfy the so-called reverse Jensen condition (see Definition 2.3 in
[
23
]
) and the sequence

(3.3)

\widehat{R}^{*}_{n}(x):=1-\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\theta_{n,k}^{2}}

converges to a finite limit
R
, then
x
admits the roughness exponent
R
.

Note that it is assumed in
[
23
]
that the trajectory
x
can be observed directly. This, however, is not the case in the context of our present paper, where

x
is the (squared) volatility in a stochastic volatility model. So let us suppose now
that we can only observe the values of the antiderivative
y(t)=\int_{0}^{t}x(s)\,ds
takes on the supporting grid
\mathbb{T}_{n+2}
. If we can interpolate the data points
\{y(t):t\in\mathbb{T}_{n+2}\}
by means of a piecewise quadratic function
y_{n+2}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then its derivative
y^{\prime}_{n+2}
will be a continuous and piecewise linear function with supporting grid
\mathbb{T}_{n+1}
and hence representable in the form

(3.4)

y^{\prime}_{n+2}=\hat{x}_{0}+\widehat{\theta}_{-1,0}e_{-1,0}+\sum_{m=0}^{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{m}-1}\widehat{\theta}_{m,k}e_{m,k}

for some initial value
\hat{x}_{0}
and certain coefficients
\widehat{\theta}_{m,k}
. Such a piecewise quadratic
C^{1}
-interpolation
y_{n+2}
exists in the form of the standard quadratic spline interpolation. Unfortunately, though, it is well known that quadratic spline interpolation suffers some serious drawbacks:

•

the initial value
\hat{x}_{0}
is not uniquely determined by the given data
\{y(t):t\in\mathbb{T}_{n+2}\}
;

•

the values
y_{n+2}(t)
depend in a highly sensitive manner on the choice of
\hat{x}_{0}
;

•

the values
y_{n+2}(s)
depend in a nonlocal way on the given data
\{y(t):t\in\mathbb{T}_{n+2}\}
, i.e., altering one data point
y(t)
may affect the value
y_{n+2}(s)
also if
s
is located far away from
t
.

In
[
21
]
, we investigate the analytical properties of the estimated
Faber–Schauder coefficients
\widehat{\theta}_{m,k}
defined in (
3.4
). It turns out that, when looking at quadratic spline interpolation through the lens of these coefficients, a miracle occurs.
To see what happens, let us recall from
[
21
, Theorem 2.1]
the formula for the Faber–Schauder coefficients of
y^{\prime}_{n+2}
for the generations
m=0,\dots,n
and for generation
n+1
,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{m,k}

\displaystyle=\scalebox{0.85}{\mbox{$\displaystyle 2^{n+m/2+3}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n+1-m}}(-1)^{j}\left(y\Big(\frac{k}{2^{m}}+\frac{j}{2^{n+2}}\Big)-y\Big(\frac{k}{2^{m}}+\frac{j-1}{2^{n+2}}\Big)+y\Big(\frac{k+1}{2^{m}}-\frac{j-1}{2^{n+2}}\Big)-y\Big(\frac{k+1}{2^{m}}-\frac{j}{2^{n+2}}\Big)\right)$}},

(3.6)

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{n+1,k}

\displaystyle=\scalebox{0.85}{\mbox{$\displaystyle-2^{(n+1)/2+2}\hat{x}_{0}-2^{3(n+1)/2+4}\sum_{j=1}^{2k}(-1)^{j}\left(y\Big(\frac{j}{2^{n+2}}\Big)-y\Big(\frac{j-1}{2^{n+2}}\Big)\right)$}}

\displaystyle+3\cdot 2^{3(n+1)/2+2}\left(y\Big(\frac{2k+1}{2^{n+2}}\Big)-y\Big(\frac{2k}{2^{n+2}}\Big)\right)-2^{3(n+1)/2+2}\left(y\Big(\frac{2k+2}{2^{n+2}}\Big)-y\Big(\frac{2k+1}{2^{n+2}}\Big)\right).

As one can see immediately from those formulas, the coefficients in generations
m=0,\dots n
are independent of
\widehat{x}_{0}
, whereas the coefficients in generation
n+1
contain the additive term
-2^{(n+1)/2+2}\hat{x}_{0}
, which translates any error made in estimating
\hat{x}_{0}
into a
2^{(n+1)/2+2}
-fold error for each final-generation coefficient.
Moreover, for
m=0,\dots n
, each
\widehat{\theta}_{m,k}
depends only on those data points
y(t)
for which
t
belongs to the closure of the support of the corresponding wavelet function
e_{m,k}
. Thus, the entire nonlocality of the function
y_{n+2}
arises from the coefficients in generation
n+1
, while the coefficients of all lower generations depend locally on the given data. We refer to
[
21
, Figure 2]
for an illustration.

The main results in
[
21
]
concern error bounds for the estimated Faber–Schauder coefficients
\widehat{\theta}_{m,k}
. Specifically, we found that the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the combined errors in generations
m=0,\dots n
is typically benign, whereas the error in the
final generation
m=n+1
can be larger than a factor of size
\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
times the error of all previous generations combined. While the exact error bounds from
[
21
]
will not be needed in our present paper, the proof of
Lemma

3.2
will rely on an algebraic representation of the error terms obtained in
[
21
, Lemma 3.2]
and stated in
Equation

3.12
below.

The above-mentioned facts make it clear that the coefficients in generations
m=0,\dots n
provide robust estimates for the corresponding true coefficients, while the estimates
\widehat{\theta}_{n+1,k}
are highly non-robust and should be discarded. It is now obvious that in estimating the roughness exponent of
x
from the data
\{y(t):t\in\mathbb{T}_{n+2}\}
, we should replace the true coefficients
\theta_{n,k}
in our formula (
3.3
) for
\widehat{R}^{*}_{n}(x)
with their estimates
\widehat{\theta}_{n,k}
. It remains to note that
\widehat{\theta}_{n,k}
is in fact equal to
\vartheta_{n,k}
defined in (
2.5
), so that we finally arrive at the rationale behind our estimator
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
.

The purpose of the following example is twofold. First, it serves as an illustration for the fact that our method is not limited to stochastic processes, but that it works also in nonparametric situations of extreme
model uncertainty, where only the trajectory is given and no information whatsoever on its distribution is available.
Second, it illustrates in a very concise and transparent manner why the final generation of estimated Faber–Schauder functions must be excluded from the estimation process.

Example 3.1
.

For
{R}\in(0,1]
, let
x^{R}\in C[0,1]
be the function with Faber–Schauder coefficients
\theta_{n,k}=2^{n(1/2-{R})}
. These functions belong to the well-studied class of fractal Takagi–Landsberg functions. It was shown in
[
28
, Theorem 2.1]
that
x^{R}
has the roughness exponent
{R}
. Moreover, for
y^{R}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}x^{R}(s)\,ds
, it was shown in
[
21
, Example 2.3]
that the robust approximation (
2.5
) based on discrete observations of
y^{R}
recovers exactly the Faber–Schauder coefficients of
x^{R}
. That is, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq k\leq 2^{n}-1
, we have

\vartheta_{n,k}=\theta_{n,k}=2^{n(\frac{1}{2}-{R})}.

It follows that

\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y^{R})=1-\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}2^{(1-2{R})n}}=1-\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}2^{(1-{R})n}={R}.

Hence, the estimator
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
is not only consistent but also exact in the sense that it gives the correct value
{R}
for every finite
n
.

Now we replace
\vartheta_{n,k}=\widehat{\theta}_{n,k}
with the final-generation estimates
\widehat{\theta}_{n+1,k}
as defined in (
3.6
). Note that this requires the choice of an initial value
\hat{x}_{0}
. The corresponding estimator is given by

\widehat{M}_{n}(y^{R}):=1-\frac{1}{n+1}\log_{2}\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n+1}-1}\widehat{\theta}_{n+1,k}^{2}}.

We get from
[
21
, Example 3.2]
that for
{R}<1/2
,

\widehat{\theta}_{n+1,k}=-2^{(n+1)/2+2}\hat{x}_{0}+\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}2^{m(\frac{1}{2}-{R})}=-2^{(n+1)/2+2}\hat{x}_{0}+\frac{2^{(n+1)(\frac{1}{2}-{R})}}{1-2^{\frac{1}{2}-{R}}}.

Hence,

\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n+1}-1}\widehat{\theta}_{n+1,k}^{2}}=2^{n+1}\left|-4\hat{x}_{0}+\frac{2^{-(n+1){R}}}{1-2^{\frac{1}{2}-{R}}}\right|.

It follows that

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\widehat{M}_{n}(y^{R})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\hat{x}_{0}\neq 0$,}\\
{R}&\text{if $\hat{x}_{0}=0$.}\end{cases}

This shows that the estimator
\widehat{M}_{n}
is extremely sensitive with respect to the estimate
\hat{x}_{0}
of the exact initial value
x(0)
, which in typical applications will be unknown. Even in the case that
x(0)
is known, the correct value
{R}
is only obtained asymptotically, whereas
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y^{R})={R}
for all finite
n
. These observations illustrate once again why we deliberately discard the final generation
\widehat{\theta}_{n+1,k}
of estimated Faber–Schauder coefficients.

3.2
Pathwise consistency of
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y)

Let us fix
x\in C[0,1]
and denote by
\theta_{m,k}
its Faber–Schauder coefficients (
3.2
). As before, we denote by
y(t)=\int_{0}^{t}x(s)\,ds
the antiderivative of
x
and by
\vartheta_{n,k}
the coefficients defined in (
2.5
). To be consistent with
[
21
]
, we introduce the following vector notation,

(3.7)

\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}:=\big(\theta_{n,0},\theta_{n,1}\cdots,\theta_{n,2^{n}}\big)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{n}=\big(\vartheta_{n,0},\vartheta_{n,1}\cdots,\vartheta_{n,2^{n}-1}\big)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}},

Then the estimators
\widehat{R}^{*}_{n}
and
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
defined in (
3.3
) and (
2.6
) can be written as

(3.8)

\widehat{R}_{n}^{*}(x)=1-\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}\|\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y)=1-\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}\|\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}.

Following
[
21
]
, we introduce the column vector
\bm{z}_{n}:=(z^{(n)}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq 2^{n}}
with components

(3.9)

z^{(n)}_{i}=2^{3n/2}\sum_{m=n}^{\infty}2^{-3m/2}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{m-n}-1}\theta_{m,k+2^{m-n}(i-1)}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-3m/2}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{m}-1}\theta_{m+n,k+2^{m}(i-1)}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq 2^{n}.

As observed in
[
21
]
, the infinite series in (
3.9
) converges absolutely if
x
satisfies an Hölder condition, and for simplicity, we are henceforth going to make this assumption.
For
1\leq i,j\leq 2^{n}
, we let furthermore

(3.10)

\bm{\eta}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\bm{r}&\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i=j\leq 2^{n},\\
\bm{0}_{1\times 4}&\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\neq j\leq 2^{n},\end{cases}

where
\bm{r}:=\frac{1}{4}(-1,+1,+1,-1)
, and
\bm{0}_{m\times n}
denotes the
m\times n
-dimensional zero matrix. Moreover, we denote

(3.11)

Q_{n}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c c c c c}\bm{\eta}_{1,1}&\bm{\eta}_{1,2}&\cdots&\bm{\eta}_{1,2^{n}-1}&\bm{\eta}_{1,2^{n}}\\
\bm{\eta}_{2,1}&\bm{\eta}_{2,2}&\cdots&\bm{\eta}_{2,2^{n}-1}&\bm{\eta}_{2,2^{n}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\bm{\eta}_{2^{n},1}&\bm{\eta}_{2^{n},2}&\cdots&\bm{\eta}_{2^{n},2^{n}-1}&\bm{\eta}_{2^{n},2^{n}}\\
\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n+2}}.

It was shown in
[
21
, Lemma 3.2]
that the error between the true and estimated Faber–Schauder coefficients can be represented as follows,

(3.12)

\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{n}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\bm{w}_{n},\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{w}_{n}=Q_{n}\bm{z}_{n+2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}.

Consider the following condition:

(3.13)

\text{There exists $\kappa\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\}$ such that}\quad\frac{\left\lVert{\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}}{\|\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}}\longrightarrow\kappa\quad\text{as $n\uparrow\infty$.}

We will see in
Proposition

4.2
that condition (
3.13
) is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. satisfied for fractional Brownian motion.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under condition (
3.13
), there exist
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and constants
0<\kappa_{-}\leq\kappa_{+}<\infty
such that

(3.14)

\kappa_{-}\|\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\leq\kappa_{+}\|\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}\quad\text{for all $n\geq n_{0}$.}

Proof.

Let
\kappa
be as in (
3.13
). Then, for any
\varepsilon<|\kappa-1|/2
, there exists
n_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
, we have
\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}(\kappa-\varepsilon)<\left\lVert{\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}<\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}(\kappa+\varepsilon)
. Using the representation (
3.12
) and applying the triangle inequality gives

\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}=\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}+\left\lVert{\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\leq(\kappa+\varepsilon+1)\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}.

On the other hand, we have

\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}=\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\geq\Big|\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-\left\lVert{\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\Big|\geq\big(|1-\kappa-\varepsilon|\wedge|1-\kappa+\varepsilon|\big)\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

By taking logarithms in (
3.14
),
Lemma

3.2
immediately yields the following result.

Proposition 3.3
.

Under condition (
3.13
), the limit
\lim_{n}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y)
exists if and only if
\lim_{n}\widehat{R}^{*}_{n}(x)
exists. Moreover, in this case,
\lim_{n}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y)=\lim_{n}\widehat{R}^{*}_{n}(x)
.

Example 3.4
.

In the situation of
Example

3.1
, we have seen that
\vartheta_{n,k}=\theta_{n,k}=2^{n(\frac{1}{2}-{R})}
. Applying the representation (
3.12
) yields that
\bm{w}_{n}=\bm{0}_{2^{n}\times 1}
. This implies

\lim_{n}\left\lVert{\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}/\|\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}=0
. That is,
x^{R}
satisfies condition (
3.13
).
Hence,
Proposition

3.3
applies, which gives an additional proof of the previously observed fact that
\lim_{n}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y)={R}
.

Example 3.5
.

Now we consider a variant of
Example

3.1
in which each Faber–Schauder coefficient is assigned a random sign. That is, we let
\theta_{n,k}=\zeta_{m,k}2^{n(\frac{1}{2}-R)}
, where
R\in(0,1)
and
(\zeta_{m,k})
is an i.i.d. sequence of
\{-1,+1\}
-valued random variables with symmetric Bernoulli distribution. Then

X^{R}_{t}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\theta_{m,k}e_{m,k}(t)

is a stochastic process whose sample paths belong to the class of signed Takagi–Landsberg functions; see, e.g.,
[
1
,
24
,
28
]
. It was shown in
[
28
, Theorem 2.1]
that all sample paths of
X^{R}
admit the roughness exponent
R
. Let
Y^{R}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}X^{R}_{s}\,ds
. Then
(
3.9
) and (
3.12
) yield that

w_{n,k}=2^{(n+2)(\frac{1}{2}-R)-2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-m(1+R)}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m+2}-1}\zeta_{m+n+2,i+2^{m+2}(k-1)},\qquad 1\leq k\leq 2^{n}.

Clearly,
w_{n,k}
is well-defined, as the series on the right-hand side is a Cauchy sequence. It is also clear that
w^{2}_{n,k}
is independent of
w^{2}_{n,j}
. We denote

\kappa_{R}:=2^{-4R}\frac{2^{1+2R}}{2^{1+2R}-1}\quad\text{and}\quad R^{*}:=\frac{\log\left(1+\sqrt{17}\right)}{2\log 2}-1.

One checks that
\kappa_{R^{*}}=1
and
\kappa_{R}\neq 1
for any
R\neq R^{\ast}
. Then,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[w^{2}_{n,k}\right]&=2^{(n+2)(1-2R)-4}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-2m(1+R)}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m+2}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta^{2}_{m+n+2,i+2^{m+2}(k-1)}\right]\\
&=2^{(n+2)(1-2R)-2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-m(1+2R)}=2^{(1-2R)n}\kappa_{R}.\end{split}

Furthermore, an analogous argument shows that for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\text{\rm Var}[w^{2}_{n,k}]=2^{(2-4R)n}\sigma^{2}_{R}
for some
\sigma^{2}_{R}>0
that does not depend on
n
. Hence, Chebyshev’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{\left\lVert{\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}}{\|\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\lVert{\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}}{\|\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}\right]\right|\leq\varepsilon\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\left|2^{(2R-2)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}w_{n,k}^{2}-\kappa_{R}\right|\leq\varepsilon\right]\leq 2^{-n}\varepsilon^{-2}.

Thus, it follows from the Borel–Cantelli lemma that
{\|\bar{\bm{w}}_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}}/{\|\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\|_{\ell_{2}}}\longrightarrow\sqrt{\kappa_{R}}
. Hence, condition (
3.13
) yields that
\lim_{n}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(Y^{R})\longrightarrow R
with probability one for any
R\neq R^{\ast}
.

In the following, we investigate the roughness exponent of the sum and composition of functions. Furthermore, we also formulate conditions under which the estimator
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
is consistent for the sum and composition of functions, respectively. These conditions are crucial in the proof of
Theorem

2.1
and
Theorem

2.4
. To wit, let
z
be a sample trajectory of the (re-scaled) Riemann–Liouville process

, then the volatility process

can be replicated pathwise by
\exp(\gamma z_{t}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4H}t^{2H})
for

. Given that
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
is a consistent estimator for

, then it remains to verify the following conditions to show that
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
is also a consistent estimator for
\sigma
.

We first establish conditions on
x
and
g
, under which

admit the same roughness exponent of
x
. To this end, we fix the following notation for Propositions
3.6
and
3.7
.

(3.15)

u(t)=g(x(t))\qquad\text{and}\qquad v(t)=\int_{0}^{t}u(s)\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}g(x(s))\,ds.

Proposition 3.6
.

If
x
admits the roughness exponent
{R}
,
g

, and

is nonzero on the range

.

Now we turn to the following question: Under which conditions do we have

, where

is as in (

)? The conditions we are going to introduce to answer this question are relatively strong. Nevertheless, they hold for the sample paths of fractional Brownian motion. In the following proposition, we need to work with various functions

,

,

\begin{split}\theta^{f}_{n,k}&=2^{n/2}\left(2f\Big(\frac{2k+1}{2^{n+1}}\Big)-f\Big(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\Big)-f\Big(\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}\Big)\right),\\
\vartheta^{f}_{n,k}&=2^{3n/2+3}\left(f\Big(\frac{4k}{2^{n+2}}\Big)-2f\Big(\frac{4k+1}{2^{n+2}}\Big)+2f\Big(\frac{4k+3}{2^{n+2}}\Big)-f\Big(\frac{4k+4}{2^{n+2}}\Big)\right).\end{split}

With this notation, the coefficients
\vartheta_{n,k}
in (

\vartheta_{n,k}^{y}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Suppose there exists
{R}\in(0,1)
such that the following conditions hold.

(a)

We have

(3.17)

0<\liminf_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{n(2{R}-2)}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(\vartheta^{y}_{n,k}\right)^{2}\leq\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{n(2{R}-2)}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(\vartheta^{y}_{n,k}\right)^{2}<\infty.

(b)

The function
x
is Hölder continuous with exponent
\alpha\in(2{R}/5,1]
.

Then, if
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is strictly monotone, we have
\lim_{n}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(v)={R}
.

Subsequently, we consider the following question: What is the roughness exponent of

, given that

admit the roughness exponent

and

.

Our final result in this section investigates the asymptotic behavior of the estimator

for the antiderivative of the sum of continuous functions. To this end, let

, and we denote the antiderivatives of

and

by

and

, respectively. In the following proposition, we continue to use a superscript to indicate from which function the Faber–Schauder coefficients will be computed from. In addition, for

,

2^{-n-m-2}(2j+1)
cannot be written in the form
\ell 2^{-n-m-1}
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Hence

\xi_{2j+1}^{(n,m+1,i)}=2\cdot 2^{n/2-m-1}=2^{n/2-m},

as the term
x(2^{-n-m-2}(2j+1))
does not appear in the linear combination (
4.10
) for
k=m
. Next, for
2^{k-n}(i-1)<j<2^{k-n}i
, the point
2^{-n-m-2}2j=2^{-n-m-1}j
can be written in the form
\ell 2^{-n-m-1}
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. It thus follows from (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) that

\xi^{(n,m+1,i)}_{2j}=\xi^{(n,m,i)}_{j}-2\cdot 2^{n/2-m-1}=2^{n/2-m},

as the term
x(2^{-n-m-1}j)
contributes to the representation of
\bm{z}^{(n,m)}_{i}
with
\xi^{(n,m,i)}_{j}=2^{n/2-m+1}
. Moreover, for
j=2^{k-n}(i-1)
or
j=2^{k-n}i
, we have

\xi_{j}^{(n,m+1,i)}=\xi_{j}^{(n,m,i)}-2^{n/2-m-1}=2^{n/2}(2^{-m}-2)-2^{n/2-m-1}=2^{n/2}(2^{-m-1}-2).

Last, for
j\leq 2^{m+2}(i-1)-1
or
j\geq 2^{m+2}i+1
, the term
x(2^{-n-m-2})
does not appear on right-hand side of (
4.13
). Thus, we have
\xi_{j}^{(n,m+1,i)}=0
. Comparing the above identities with (
4.11
) proves the case for
k=m+1
.
∎

Using
Lemma

4.1
, we can establish condition (
3.13
) for almost all sample paths of fractional Brownian motion, which is achieved in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.2
.

With probability one, the sample paths of fractional Brownian motion
W^{H}
satisfy condition (
3.13
).

To prove
Proposition

4.2
, we need to obtain the asymptotic behavior of the
\left\lVert{\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}
associated with a fractional Brownian motion
W^{H}
. Let
\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{n}
,
\bm{z}_{(n,k)}
, and
\bm{z}_{n}
be defined as in (
2.5
), (
4.9
), and (
3.9
) for the sample paths of
W^{H}
. It is clear that
\bm{z}_{n}
is well defined, since the sample paths of
W^{H}
satisfy a Hölder condition. Moreover, all three are Gaussian random vectors. Our next lemma characterizes the covariance structure of the Gaussian vector
\bm{z}_{n}
. To this end, consider the function
g_{H}:=h_{1}+h_{2}+h_{3}
, where the
h_{i}:\mathbb{N}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are defined as follows,

(4.14)

\begin{split}h_{1}({\varsigma})&=-2\left(2{\varsigma}^{2H}+|{\varsigma}-1|^{2H}+|{\varsigma}+1|^{2H}\right)\\
h_{2}({\varsigma})&=\begin{cases}\frac{8}{2H+1}\left(({\varsigma}+1)^{2H+1}-({\varsigma}-1)^{2H+1}\right)&\quad\text{for}\quad{\varsigma}\geq 1,\\
\frac{16}{2H+1}&\quad\text{for}\quad{\varsigma}=0,\\
\end{cases}\\
h_{3}({\varsigma})&=-\frac{8}{(2H+2)(2H+1)}\left(|{\varsigma}+1|^{2H+2}-2{\varsigma}^{2H+2}+|{\varsigma}-1|^{2H+2}\right).\end{split}

Furthermore, we introduce the Toeplitz matrix
G_{n}:=(g_{H}(|i-j|))_{1\leq i,j\leq 2^{n}}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the random vector
\bm{z}_{n}
is a well-defined zero-mean Gaussian vector with covariance matrix

\Gamma_{n}=(\gamma^{(n)}_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq 2^{n}}=2^{(1-2H)n}G_{n}.

Proof.

For
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
, let us denote

\Gamma_{(n,k)}=(\gamma^{(n,k)}_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq 2^{n}}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}_{(n,k)}\bm{z}_{(n,k)}^{\top}\right].

It suffices to show that the components
\gamma^{(n,k)}_{i,j}
converges to
\gamma^{(n)}_{i,j}
as
k\uparrow\infty
. Moreover, by symmetry, it suffices to consider the case
j\geq i
.
Lemma

4.1
yields

\gamma^{(n,k)}_{i,j}=\mathbb{E}\big[z^{(n,k)}_{i}z^{(n,k)}_{j}\big]=\sum_{\tau_{1}=0}^{2^{n+k+1}}\sum_{\tau_{2}=0}^{2^{n+k+1}}\xi^{(n,k,i)}_{\tau_{1}}\xi^{(n,k,j)}_{\tau_{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[W^{H}_{\frac{\tau_{1}}{2^{n+k+1}}}\cdot W^{H}_{\frac{\tau_{2}}{2^{n+k+1}}}\Big].

We also get from
Lemma

4.1
that

\sum_{\tau=0}^{2^{n+k+1}}\xi_{\tau}^{(n,k,i)}=0
and
\xi_{j}^{(n,k,i)}=0
for
j\leq 2^{k+1}(i-1)-1
or
j\geq 2^{k+1}i+1
. Hence, for
\varsigma:=j-i\geq 0
,

\begin{split}\gamma^{(n,k)}_{i,j}&=-\sum_{\tau_{1}=0}^{2^{n+k+1}}\sum_{\tau_{2}=0}^{2^{n+k+1}}\frac{\xi^{(n,k,i)}_{\tau_{1}}\xi^{(n,k,j)}_{\tau_{2}}}{2}\left|\frac{\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}{2^{n+k+1}}\right|^{2H}=-2^{-2Hn}\sum_{\tau_{1}=0}^{2^{k+1}}\sum_{\tau_{2}=0}^{2^{k+1}}\frac{\xi^{(n,k,i)}_{\tau_{1}}\xi^{(n,k,j)}_{\tau_{2}}}{2}\left|\frac{\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}{2^{k+1}}+\varsigma\right|^{2H}.\end{split}

Using once again (
4.11
) yields that

(4.15)

\gamma^{(n,k)}_{i,j}=2^{(1-2H)n}\big(h_{1,k}(\varsigma)+h_{2,k}(\varsigma)+h_{3,k}(\varsigma)\big),

where functions
h_{i,k}
are defined as follows,

\begin{split}h_{1,k}(\varsigma)&=-\frac{(2^{-k}-2)^{2}}{2}\left(2|\varsigma|^{2H}+|\varsigma-1|^{2H}+|\varsigma+1|^{2H}\right),\\
h_{2,k}(\varsigma)&=2^{-k}(2-2^{-k})\sum_{\tau=1}^{2^{k+1}-1}\Big(\left|\frac{\tau}{2^{k+1}}+\varsigma\right|^{2H}+\left|\frac{\tau}{2^{k+1}}+\varsigma-1\right|^{2H}+\left|\frac{-\tau}{2^{k+1}}+\varsigma\right|^{2H}+\left|\frac{-\tau}{2^{k+1}}+\varsigma+1\right|^{2H}\Big),\\
h_{3,k}(\varsigma)&=-2^{1-2k}\sum_{\tau_{1}=1}^{2^{k+1}-1}\sum_{\tau_{2}=1}^{2^{k+1}-1}\left|\frac{\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}{2^{k+1}}+\varsigma\right|^{2H}.\end{split}

Let us first consider the case
\varsigma\geq 1
. Then,

(4.16)

\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}h_{1,k}(\varsigma)=-2\left(2\varsigma^{2H}+(\varsigma-1)^{2H}+(\varsigma+1)^{2H}\right).

Furthermore,

\begin{split}\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}h_{2,k}(\varsigma)&=\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}2^{1-k}(2-2^{-k})\sum_{\tau=1}^{2^{k+1}-1}\Big(\left|\frac{\tau}{2^{k+1}}+\varsigma\right|^{2H}+\left|\frac{\tau}{2^{k+1}}+\varsigma-1\right|^{2H}\Big)\\
&=8\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}2^{-k-1}\sum_{\tau=1}^{2^{k+1}-1}\Big(\left|\frac{\tau}{2^{k+1}}+\varsigma\right|^{2H}+\left|\frac{\tau}{2^{k+1}}+\varsigma-1\right|^{2H}\Big)\\
&=8\left(\int_{\varsigma}^{\varsigma+1}t^{2H}\,dt+\int_{\varsigma-1}^{\varsigma}t^{2H}\,dt\right)=\frac{8\left((\varsigma+1)^{2H+1}-(\varsigma-1)^{2H+1}\right)}{2H+1}\end{split}

We also get in a similar way that

\begin{split}\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}h_{3,k}(\varsigma)&=-8\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}2^{-2-2k}\sum_{\tau_{1}=1}^{2^{k+1}-1}\sum_{\tau_{2}=1}^{2^{k+1}-1}\left(\frac{\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}{2^{k+1}}+\varsigma\right)^{2H}=-8\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\left(t-s+\varsigma\right)^{2H}\,ds\,dt\\
&=-\frac{8\left((\varsigma+1)^{2H+2}-2\varsigma^{2H+2}+(\varsigma-1)^{2H+2}\right)}{(2H+2)(2H+1)}.\end{split}

For the case
\varsigma=0
,
\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}h_{1,k}(0)=h_{1}(0)
as in (
4.16
). Next, we have

\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}h_{2,k}(0)=\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}2^{2-k}(2-2^{-k})\sum_{\tau=1}^{2^{k+1}-1}\left|\frac{\tau}{2^{k+1}}\right|^{2H}=16\int_{0}^{1}t^{2H}\,dt=\frac{16}{2H+1}.

Finally,

\begin{split}\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}h_{3,k}(0)&=\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}-2^{1-2k}\sum_{\tau_{1}=1}^{2^{k+1}-1}\sum_{\tau_{2}=1}^{2^{k+1}-1}\left|\frac{\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}{2^{k+1}}\right|^{2H}=-8\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|t-s|^{2H}\,dsdt\\
&=16\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{2H}\,dsdt=\frac{16}{(2H+1)(2H+2)}.\end{split}

Comparing the above equations with (
4.14
) completes the proof.
∎

Our next lemma investigates the limit of
2^{n(H-1)}\left\lVert{\bm{z}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}
as
n\uparrow\infty
by applying a concentration inequality from
[
4
, Lemma 3.1]
. In the form needed here, it states that if
\bm{Z}
is a centered Gaussian random vector with covariance matrix
C
,
T:=\sqrt{\text{trace}\,C}
, and
\gamma:=\sqrt{\left\lVert{C}\right\rVert_{2}}
, then there exists a universal constant
\kappa
independent of
C
such that

(4.17)

\mathbb{P}\Big[\big|\|\bm{Z}\|_{\ell_{2}}-T\big|\geq t\Big]\leq\kappa\exp\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{4\gamma^{2}}\Big)\qquad\text{for all $t>0$.}

Lemma 4.4
.

With probability one,

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{n(H-1)}\left\lVert{\bm{z}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}=2\sqrt{\frac{1-H}{H+1}}.

Proof.

It follows from (
4.14
) that

(4.18)

\begin{split}\sqrt{\text{trace}\,\Gamma_{n}}&=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\gamma^{(n)}_{i,i}}=2^{(1-H)n}\sqrt{g_{H}(0)}=2^{(1-H)n}\sqrt{-4+\frac{16}{2H+1}-\frac{16}{(2H+1)(2H+2)}}\\
&=2^{(1-H)n+1}\sqrt{\frac{1-H}{H+1}}.\end{split}

Let
\|\cdot\|_{p}
denote the
\ell_{p}
-induced operator norm. As shown in
Lemma

4.3
, the covariance matrix
\Gamma_{n}
is a symmetric Toeplitz matrix and so
\gamma^{(n)}_{i,j}=\gamma^{(n)}_{j,i}=\gamma^{(n)}_{1,|j-i+1|}
. Hence, we have
\left\lVert{\Gamma_{n}}\right\rVert_{1}=\left\lVert{\Gamma_{n}}\right\rVert_{\infty}
, and this gives

(4.19)

\begin{split}\left\lVert{\Gamma_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}&\leq\sqrt{\left\lVert{\Gamma_{n}}\right\rVert_{1}\left\lVert{\Gamma_{n}}\right\rVert_{\infty}}=\left\lVert{\Gamma_{n}}\right\rVert_{1}=\max_{1\leq j\leq 2^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}|\gamma^{(n)}_{i,j}|=\max_{1\leq j\leq 2^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}|\gamma^{(n)}_{1,|j-i+1|}|\\
&\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}|\gamma^{(n)}_{1,i}|\leq 2^{(1-2H)n+1}\sum_{\varsigma=0}^{2^{n}}|g_{H}(\varsigma)|,\end{split}

where the first inequality is a well-known bound for the spectral norm of a matrix; see, e.g.,
[
31
, proof of Theorem 2.3]
.
In the next step, we will show that
g_{H}(\varsigma)=\mathcal{O}(\varsigma^{2H-4})
as
\varsigma\uparrow\infty
. For
\varsigma\geq 3
, Taylor expansion yields
u_{1}\in(\varsigma-1,\varsigma)
and
u_{2}\in(\varsigma,\varsigma+1)
such that

\begin{split}(\varsigma-1)^{2H}&=\varsigma^{2H}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{(-1)^{i}\prod_{j=1}^{i}(2H-j+1)}{i!}\varsigma^{2H-i}+\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{4}(2H-i+1)}{4!}u_{1}^{2H-4},\\
(\varsigma+1)^{2H}&=\varsigma^{2H}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{i}(2H-j+1)}{i!}\varsigma^{2H-i}+\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{4}(2H-i+1)}{4!}u_{2}^{2H-4}.\end{split}

Note that

\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\left((-1)^{i}+1\right)\prod_{j=1}^{i}(2H-j+1)}{i!}\varsigma^{2H-i}=2H(2H-1)\varsigma^{2H-2},

and therefore, we have

(4.20)

h_{1}(\varsigma)=-2\left(4\varsigma^{2H}+2H(2H-1)\varsigma^{2H-2}+\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{4}(2H-j+1)}{4!}(u_{1}^{2H-4}+u_{2}^{2H-4})\right).

In the same way, we obtain

(4.21)

\displaystyle h_{2}(\varsigma)

\displaystyle=8\left(2\varsigma^{2H}+\frac{2(2H)(2H-1)}{3!}\varsigma^{2H-2}+\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{4}(2H-j+1)}{5!}(u_{3}^{2H-4}+u_{4}^{2H-4})\right),

(4.22)

\displaystyle h_{3}(\varsigma)

\displaystyle=-8\left(\frac{2}{2!}\varsigma^{2H}+\frac{2(2H)(2H-1)}{4!}\varsigma^{2H-2}+\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{4}(2H-j+1)}{6!}(u_{5}^{2H-4}+u_{6}^{2H-4})\right),

for some
u_{3},u_{5}\in(\varsigma-1,\varsigma)
and
u_{4},u_{6}\in(\varsigma,\varsigma+1)
. Since
u_{i}\geq\varsigma-1
, we get
u_{i}^{2H-4}\leq(\varsigma-1)^{2H-4}
. Summing up (
4.20
), (
4.21
) and (
4.22
) yields that
g_{H}(\varsigma)=\mathcal{O}(\varsigma^{2H-4})
as
\varsigma\uparrow\infty
, which with (
4.19
) implies that

(4.23)

\left\lVert{\Gamma_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{(1-2H)n}\left(g_{H}(0)+g_{H}(1)+\sum_{\varsigma=2}^{2^{n}}g_{H}(\varsigma)\right)=\mathcal{O}(2^{(1-2H)n})\quad\text{for}\quad H\in(0,1).

Therefore, for each
H\in(0,1)
, there exist
c_{H}\geq 0
and
n_{c,H}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq n_{c,H}
, we have
\left\lVert{\Gamma_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq c_{H}
. Thus, for
n\geq n_{c,H}
and any given
\varepsilon>0
, the concentration inequality (
4.17
) gives

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\left|2^{n(H-1)}\left\lVert{\bm{z}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-2\sqrt{\frac{1-H}{H+1}}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left\lVert{\bm{z}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-2^{(1-H)n+1}\sqrt{\frac{1-H}{H+1}}\right|\geq 2^{(1-H)n}\varepsilon\right)\\
&\leq\kappa\exp\left(-\frac{2^{n(2-2H)}\varepsilon^{2}}{\left\lVert{\Gamma_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}}\right)=\kappa\exp(-c^{-1}_{H}2^{n}\varepsilon^{2})\end{split}

The latter expression is summable in
n
for every
\varepsilon>0
, and so a Borel–Cantelli argument yields that
2^{n(H-1)}\left\lVert{\bm{z}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\rightarrow 2\sqrt{\frac{1-H}{H+1}}
with probability one as
n\uparrow\infty
.∎

In the following lemma, we will derive the asymptotic behavior of the norms of
\bm{w}_{n}
defined in (
3.12
).

Lemma 4.5
.

With probability one, we have

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{n(H-1)}\left\lVert{\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}=2^{-2H}\sqrt{\alpha(H)},

where
\alpha(H)=g_{H}(0)-\frac{1}{2}g_{H}(1)-g_{H}(2)+\frac{1}{2}g_{H}(3)
.

Proof.

Let us denote the covariance matrix of
\bm{w}_{n}
by
\Phi_{n}:=(\phi^{(n)}_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{2^{n}}=Q_{n}\Gamma_{n+2}Q^{\top}_{n}
. We first show that

\text{trace}\,\Phi_{n}=2^{(2-2H)n-4H}\alpha(H).

For the fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the following partition of the covariance matrix
\Gamma_{n+2}
,

(4.24)

\Gamma_{n+2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\Gamma^{\ast}_{1,1}&\Gamma^{\ast}_{1,2}&\cdots&\Gamma^{\ast}_{1,2^{n}}\\
\Gamma^{\ast}_{2,1}&\Gamma^{\ast}_{2,2}&\cdots&\Gamma^{\ast}_{2,2^{n}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\Gamma^{\ast}_{2^{n},1}&\Gamma^{\ast}_{2^{n},2}&\cdots&\Gamma^{\ast}_{2^{n},2^{n}}\end{array}\right],

where
\Gamma^{\ast}_{i,j}
are
4\times 4
-dimensional matrices. In particular, for
1\leq i\leq 2^{n}
, the diagonal partitioned matrices
\Gamma^{\ast}_{i,i}
are of the form:

\Gamma^{\ast}_{i,i}=2^{(1-2H)(n+2)}G_{2}=2^{(1-2H)(n+2)}\left[\begin{array}[]{c c c c}g_{H}(0)&g_{H}(1)&g_{H}(2)&g_{H}(3)\\
g_{H}(1)&g_{H}(0)&g_{H}(1)&g_{H}(2)\\
g_{H}(2)&g_{H}(1)&g_{H}(0)&g_{H}(1)\\
g_{H}(3)&g_{H}(2)&g_{H}(1)&g_{H}(0)\\
\end{array}\right].

Recall the definition of
\bm{\eta}_{i,j}
from (
3.10
), we get

\begin{split}\phi^{(n)}_{i,i}&=\left(\bm{\eta}_{i,1},\bm{\eta}_{i,2},\dots,\bm{\eta}_{i,2^{n}}\right)\Gamma_{n+1}\left(\bm{\eta}_{i,1},\bm{\eta}_{i,2},\dots,\bm{\eta}_{i,2^{n}}\right)^{\top}\\
&=\left(\bm{0}_{1\times 4},\dots,\bm{\eta}_{i,i},\dots,\bm{0}_{1\times 4}\right)\Gamma_{n+1}\left(\bm{0}_{1\times 4},\dots,\bm{\eta}_{i,i},\dots,\bm{0}_{1\times 4}\right)^{\top}\\
&=\bm{r}\Gamma^{\ast}_{i,i}\bm{r}^{\top}=2^{(1-2H)(n+2)}\bm{r}G_{2}\bm{r}^{\top}.\end{split}

To evaluate the last term in the above equation, we have

\bm{r}G_{2}\bm{r}^{\top}=\frac{1}{16}\bm{1}_{1\times 4}\left[\begin{array}[]{c c c c}g_{H}(0)&-g_{H}(1)&-g_{H}(2)&g_{H}(3)\\
-g_{H}(1)&g_{H}(0)&g_{H}(1)&-g_{H}(2)\\
-g_{H}(2)&g_{H}(1)&g_{H}(0)&-g_{H}(1)\\
g_{H}(3)&-g_{H}(2)&-g_{H}(1)&g_{H}(0)\\
\end{array}\right]\bm{1}_{4\times 1}=\frac{\alpha(H)}{4}.

Therefore, we have
\phi^{(n)}_{i,i}=2^{(1-2H)(n+2)-2}\alpha(H)
for every
1\leq i\leq 2^{n}
, and

\text{trace}\,\Phi_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\phi^{(n)}_{i,i}=2^{(2-2H)n-4H}\alpha(H).

In our next step, we shall show that
2^{2n(H-1)}\left\lVert{\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}
converges to
2^{-2H}\sqrt{\alpha(H)}
. First of all, it follows from
[
21
]
that
\left\lVert{Q_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}=1/4
, and due to (
4.23
), there exists a constant
c_{H}>0
such that

\left\lVert{\Phi_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert{Q_{n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\left\lVert{\Gamma_{n+2}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq c_{H}2^{(1-2H)n}.

For any given
\varepsilon>0
, the concentration inequality (
4.17
)
yields that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\left|2^{n(H-1)}\left\lVert{\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-2^{n(H-1)}\sqrt{\text{trace}\,\Phi_{n}}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|2^{n(H-1)}\left\lVert{\bm{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-\sqrt{2^{2-4H}\alpha(H)}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\\
&\leq\kappa\exp\left(-\frac{2^{n(2-2H)}\varepsilon^{2}}{\left\lVert{Q_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}}\right)=\kappa\exp(-c^{-1}_{H}2^{n}\varepsilon^{2}).\end{split}

From here, a Borel–Cantelli yields the assertion.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leq k\leq 2^{n}-1
and
0\leq j\leq 2^{m}-1
be given such that

\big|2^{n}j-2^{m}k\big|>2^{m\vee n}.

Then there exists a positive constant
\rho_{H}
such that

(4.26)

\big|\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},\theta_{m,j}\big)\big|\leq\rho_{H}\cdot 2^{-3(m+n)/2}\left(\left|\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}-\frac{j}{2^{m}}\right|\wedge\left|\frac{k}{2^{n}}-\frac{j+1}{2^{m}}\right|\right)^{2H-4}.

Proof.

For fixed
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote

(4.27)

\begin{split}f_{n,m}(s,t)&:=\hbox{\rm cov}\left(W^{H}_{s+\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}}-W^{H}_{s},W^{H}_{t+\frac{1}{2^{m+1}}}-W^{H}_{t}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|s-t+\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\right|^{2H}+\left|s-t-\frac{1}{2^{m+1}}\right|^{2H}-\left|s-t\right|^{2H}-\left|s-t+\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}-\frac{1}{2^{m+1}}\right|^{2H}\right).\end{split}

Recall that
\theta_{n,k}=2^{n/2}\big((W^{H}_{\frac{2k+1}{2^{n+1}}}-W^{H}_{\frac{2k}{2^{n+1}}})-(W^{H}_{\frac{2k+2}{2^{n+1}}}-W^{H}_{\frac{2k+1}{2^{n+1}}})\big)
, and this gives

(4.28)

\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},\theta_{m,j}\big)=2^{\frac{n+m}{2}}\int_{\frac{2k}{2^{n+1}}}^{\frac{2k+1}{2^{n+1}}}\int_{\frac{2j}{2^{m+1}}}^{\frac{2j+1}{2^{m+1}}}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{n,m}(s,t)}{\partial s\partial t}\,dsdt\leq 2^{-\frac{m+n}{2}-2}\sup_{(s,t)\in\mathcal{S}^{(n+1,m+1)}_{\frac{k}{2^{n}},\frac{j}{2^{m}}}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}f_{n,m}(s,t)}{\partial s\partial t}\right|,

where for fixed
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{S}^{(m,n)}_{a,b}
denotes the triangular area of the following form

\mathcal{S}^{(m,n)}_{a,b}:=\left[a,a+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right]\times\left[b,b+\frac{1}{2^{m}}\right]\qquad\text{for}\quad a,b\in\mathbb{R}.

To obtain an upper bound for the rightmost term in (
4.28
), let
\rho_{1}=2H|2H-1|
and
\rho_{2}=\prod_{i=0}^{3}|2H-i|
, then it follows

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}f_{n,m}(s,t)}{\partial s\partial t}\right|&=\frac{\rho_{1}}{2}\left||t-s|^{2H-2}+\left|s-t+\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}-\frac{1}{2^{m+1}}\right|^{2H-2}-\left|s-t+\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\right|^{2H-2}-\left|s-t-\frac{1}{2^{m+1}}\right|^{2H-2}\right|\\
&\leq\frac{\rho_{2}}{2}\int_{s}^{s+\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}}\int_{t}^{t+\frac{1}{2^{m+1}}}|u-v|^{2H-4}\,dudv\leq\frac{\rho_{2}}{2^{n+m+3}}\sup_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{S}^{(n+1,m+1)}_{s,t}}|u-v|^{2H-4}.\end{split}

Applying the above inequality to (
4.28
) yields

(4.29)

\begin{split}\big|\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},\theta_{m,j}\big)\big|&\leq\rho_{2}\cdot 2^{-\frac{3(m+n)}{2}-4}\sup_{(s,t)\in\mathcal{S}^{(n+1,m+1)}_{\frac{k}{2^{n}},\frac{j}{2^{m}}}}\sup_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{S}^{(n+1,m+1)}_{s,t}}|u-v|^{2H-4}.\end{split}

Since
\big|2^{n}j-2^{m}k\big|>2^{m\vee n}
, we have
\left[\frac{k}{2^{n}},\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}\right]\cap\left[\frac{j}{2^{m}},\frac{j+1}{2^{m}}\right]=\varnothing
. As a result, we have

\begin{split}\sup_{(s,t)\in\mathcal{S}^{(n+1,m+1)}_{\frac{k}{2^{n}},\frac{j}{2^{m}}}}\sup_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{S}^{(n+1,m+1)}_{s,t}}|u-v|^{2H-4}&\leq\sup_{(s,t)\in\mathcal{S}^{(n,m)}_{\frac{k}{2^{n}},\frac{j}{2^{m}}}}|s-t|^{2H-4}\\
&\leq\left(\left|\frac{k}{2^{n}}-\frac{j+1}{2^{m}}\right|\wedge\left|\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}-\frac{j}{2^{m}}\right|\right)^{2H-4}.\end{split}

Inserting the above inequality to (
4.29
) and taking
\rho_{H}=2^{-4}\rho_{2}
completes the proof.
∎

Recall that
\bm{w}_{n}=Q_{n}\bm{z}_{n+2}
, and let us denote
\bm{w}_{n}=\left(w_{n,0},w_{n,1},\cdots,w_{n,2^{n}-1}\right)^{\top}

Q_{n}

(4.30)

w_{n,i}=\bm{r}^{\top}\left(z^{(n+2)}_{4i+1},z^{(n+2)}_{4i+2},z^{(n+2)}_{4i+3},z^{(n+2)}_{4i}\right)^{\top}=\frac{1}{4}\left(-z^{(n+2)}_{4i+1}+z^{(n+2)}_{4i+2}+z^{(n+2)}_{4i+3}-z^{(n+2)}_{4i+4}\right).

Applying the bound in
Lemma

4.6
, we can obtain the following result, which concerns the upper bound between the Faber–Schauder coefficient
\theta_{n,k}
and Gaussian random variable
w_{n,i}

Lemma 4.7
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
0\leq i,k\leq 2^{n}-1
be given such that
|i-k|>1
. Then we have

(4.31)

\big|\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},w_{n,i}\big)\big|\leq\rho_{H}2^{(1-2H)n}\left(\left|{k-i-1}\right|\wedge\left|{k-i+1}\right|\right)^{2H-4}.

Proof.

We first prove for the case
i>k+1
. In this context, we have for
1\leq\delta\leq 4
,

\begin{split}\left|\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},z^{(n+2)}_{4i+\delta}\big)\right|&=2^{3(n+2)/2}\left|\hbox{\rm cov}\left(\theta_{n,k},\sum_{m=n+2}^{\infty}2^{-3m/2}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m-(n+2)}-1}\theta_{m,j+2^{m-(n+2)}(4i+\delta-1)}\right)\right|\\
&\leq 2^{3(n+2)/2}\sum_{m=n+2}^{\infty}2^{-3m/2}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m-(n+2)}-1}\left|\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},\theta_{m,j+2^{m-(n+2)}(4i+\delta-1)}\big)\right|\\
&\leq\rho_{H}\cdot 2^{3(n+2)/2}\sum_{m=n+2}^{\infty}2^{-3m-3n/2}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m-(n+2)}-1}\left(\frac{i-k-1}{2^{n}}+\frac{\delta-1}{2^{n+2}}+\frac{j}{2^{m}}\right)^{2H-4}.\end{split}

As
\delta\geq 1
,
j\geq 0
and
i>k+1
, then we have

\frac{i-k-1}{2^{n}}+\frac{\delta-1}{2^{n+2}}+\frac{j}{2^{m}}\geq\frac{i-k-1}{2^{n}}.

Hence, we have

\left|\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},z^{(n+2)}_{4i+\delta}\big)\right|\leq\rho_{H}\cdot 2^{-n+1}\sum_{m=n+2}^{\infty}2^{-2m}\left(\frac{i-k-1}{2^{n}}\right)^{2H-4}\leq\rho_{H}\cdot 2^{(1-2H)n}\left(i-k-1\right)^{2H-4}.

For the case
i<k-1
, an analogous argument yields that

\left|\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},z^{(n+2)}_{4i+\delta}\big)\right|\leq\rho_{H}\cdot 2^{3(n+2)/2}\sum_{m=n+2}^{\infty}2^{-3m-3n/2}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m-(n+2)}-1}\left(\frac{k-i}{2^{n}}-\frac{\delta-1}{2^{n+2}}-\frac{j+1}{2^{m}}\right)^{2H-4}

As
j\leq 2^{m-(n+2)}-1
,
\delta\leq 4
and
k>i+1
, we have

\frac{k-i}{2^{n}}-\frac{\delta-1}{2^{n+2}}-\frac{j+1}{2^{m}}\geq\frac{k-i-1}{2^{n}}

Hence, we have

\left|\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},z^{(n+2)}_{4i+\delta}\big)\right|\leq\rho_{H}\cdot 2^{-n+1}\sum_{m=n+2}^{\infty}2^{-2m}\left(\frac{k-i-1}{2^{n}}\right)^{2H-4}\leq\rho_{H}\cdot 2^{(1-2H)n}\left(k-i-1\right)^{2H-4}.

Combining the above bounds for different cases, we have that for
|i-k|>1
,

\left|\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},z^{(n+2)}_{4i+\delta}\big)\right|\leq\rho_{H}\cdot 2^{(1-2H)n}\left(\left|{k-i-1}\right|\wedge\left|{k-i+1}\right|\right)^{2H-4}.

Finally, applying (
4.30
) gives

\big|\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},w_{n,i}\big)\big|\leq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\delta=1}^{4}\left|\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},z^{(n+2)}_{4i+\delta}\big)\right|\leq\rho_{H}\cdot 2^{(1-2H)n}\left(\left|{k-i-1}\right|\wedge\left|{k-i+1}\right|\right)^{2H-4}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\text{trace}\,\Psi_{n+1}=2^{2-2H}\text{trace}\,\Psi_{n}.

Proof.

We begin with showing that the Faber–Schauder coefficients satisfy the following properties:

(4.32)

\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},\theta_{m,j}\big)=2^{2H-1}\hbox{\rm cov}\left(\theta_{n+1,k},\theta_{m+1,j}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},\theta_{m,j}\big)=\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k+i},\theta_{m,j+2^{m-n}i}\big).

To show that the first identity holds, recall that
W^{H}
is
H
-self-similar. That is,
(W^{H}_{\alpha t})\overset{d}{=}(\alpha^{H}W^{H}_{t})
. This then implies

\begin{split}\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},\theta_{m,j}\big)&=2^{\frac{n+m}{2}}\hbox{\rm cov}\left(2W^{H}_{\frac{2k+1}{2^{n+1}}}-W^{H}_{\frac{2k}{2^{n+1}}}-W^{H}_{\frac{2k+2}{2^{n+1}}},2W^{H}_{\frac{2j+1}{2^{m+1}}}-W^{H}_{\frac{2j}{2^{m+1}}}-W^{H}_{\frac{2j+2}{2^{m+1}}}\right)\\
&=2^{2H+\frac{n+m}{2}}\hbox{\rm cov}\left(2W^{H}_{\frac{2k+2}{2^{n+1}}}-W^{H}_{\frac{2k}{2^{n+2}}}-W^{H}_{\frac{2k+2}{2^{n+2}}},2W^{H}_{\frac{2j+1}{2^{m+2}}}-W^{H}_{\frac{2j}{2^{m+2}}}-W^{H}_{\frac{2j+2}{2^{m+2}}}\right)\\
&=2^{2H-1}\hbox{\rm cov}\left(\theta_{n+1,k},\theta_{m+1,j}\right).\end{split}

To prove the second identity, take
k^{\prime}=k+i
and
j^{\prime}=j+2^{m-n}i
, and we get
2^{-n}k-2^{-m}j=2^{-n}k^{\prime}-2^{-m}j^{\prime}
. Since
W^{H}
admits stationary increments, then
\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k},\theta_{m,j}\big)=\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n,k^{\prime}},\theta_{m,j^{\prime}}\big)
. Moreover, the above properties remain to hold for the covariance between
\theta_{n,k}
and
w_{n,k}
, that is,

(4.33)

\hbox{\rm cov}(\theta_{n,k},w_{n,j})=2^{2H-1}\hbox{\rm cov}(\theta_{n+1,k},w_{n+1,j})\quad\text{and}\quad\hbox{\rm cov}(\theta_{n,k},w_{n,j})=\hbox{\rm cov}(\theta_{n,k+i},w_{n,j+i}).

To prove the first identity in (
4.33
), note that for
1\leq\delta\leq 4

\begin{split}\hbox{\rm cov}\big(\theta_{n+1,k},z^{(n+3)}_{4j+\delta}\big)&=\hbox{\rm cov}\left(\theta_{n+1,k},\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-3m/2}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m}-1}\theta_{m+(n+3),i+2^{m}(4j+\delta-1)}\right)\\
&=2^{1-2H}\hbox{\rm cov}\left(\theta_{n,k},\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-3m/2}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m}-1}\theta_{m+(n+2),i+2^{m}(4j+\delta-1)}\right)=2^{1-2H}\big(\theta_{n,k},z^{(n+2)}_{4j+\delta}\big).\end{split}

In addition, let
k^{\prime}=k+i
and
j^{\prime}=j+i
, then we have

\begin{split}\hbox{\rm cov}(\theta_{n,k^{\prime}},z^{(n+2)}_{4j^{\prime}+\delta})&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-3m/2}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m}-1}\hbox{\rm cov}\left(\theta_{n,k^{\prime}},\theta_{m+(n+2),i+2^{m}(4j^{\prime}+\delta-1)}\right)\\
&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-3m/2}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m}-1}\hbox{\rm cov}\left(\theta_{n,k},\theta_{m+(n+2),i+2^{m}(4j+\delta-1)}\right)=\hbox{\rm cov}(\theta_{n,k},z^{(n+2)}_{4j+\delta}),\\
\end{split}

where the second equality follows from (
4.32
). Since
w_{n,i}=(-z^{(n+2)}_{4i+1}+z^{(n+2)}_{4i+2}+z^{(n+2)}_{4i+3}-z^{(n+2)}_{4i+4})/4
and covariance is a bilinear map, then (
4.33
) holds. Furthermore, an analogous argument will also lead to

(4.34)

\hbox{\rm cov}(w_{n,k},w_{n,j})=2^{2H-1}\hbox{\rm cov}(w_{n+1,k},w_{n+1,j})\quad\text{and}\quad\hbox{\rm cov}(w_{n,k},w_{n,j})=\hbox{\rm cov}(w_{n,k+i},w_{n,j+i}).

Finally, (
4.32
), (
4.33
) and (
4.34
) yield that

\begin{split}\text{trace}\,\Psi_{n+1}&=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n+1}-1}\psi^{(n+1)}_{k,k}=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n+1}-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\theta^{2}_{n+1,k}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[w^{2}_{n+1,k}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\theta_{n+1,k}w_{n+1,k}\right]\right)\\
&=2^{n+1+(1-2H)}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\theta^{2}_{n,0}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[w^{2}_{n,0}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\theta_{n,0}w_{n,0}\right]\right)=2^{2-2H}\text{trace}\,\Psi_{n}.\end{split}

This completes the proof.

Proposition 4.9
.

For
H\in(0,1

\tau_{H}>0

Proof.

Let us denote
\tau_{H}=\text{trace}\,\Psi_{0}
, it is clear that
\text{trace}\,\Psi_{n}=2^{(2-2H)n}\tau_{H}
. Furthermore, as shown in
Proposition

4.2
, as

\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[w^{2}_{n,0}\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[\theta^{2}_{n,0}\big]}=\frac{\alpha(H)}{2^{2+2H}-2^{4H}}<1.

This then implies that
\tau_{H}>0
. Furthermore, following from
Lemma

4.3

Lemma

4.5

Lemma

4.8

\Psi_{n}
is a symmetric Toeplitz matrix and so
\psi^{(n)}_{i,j}=\psi^{(n)}_{j,i}=\psi^{(n)}_{0,|j-i|}

\left\lVert{\Psi_{n}}\right\rVert_{1}=\left\lVert{\Psi_{n}}\right\rVert_{\infty}
, and applying inequalities (
4.23
), (
4.26
) and (
4.31
) yields that there exists a positive constant
\kappa_{H}>0
such that

\begin{split}\left\lVert{\Psi_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}&\leq\sqrt{\left\lVert{\Psi_{n}}\right\rVert_{1}\left\lVert{\Psi}\right\rVert_{\infty}}=\left\lVert{\Psi_{n}}\right\rVert_{1}=\max_{0\leq j\leq 2^{n}-1}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}|\psi^{(n)}_{i,j}|\leq 2\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}|\psi^{(n)}_{0,i}|\\
&\leq 2\left(|\psi^{(n)}_{0,0}|+|\psi^{(n)}_{0,1}|+\sum_{i=2}^{2^{n}-1}|\psi^{(n)}_{0,i}|\right)\leq\kappa_{H}2^{n(1-2H)},\end{split}

where the final inequality holds as it follows from the same inequalities that both
2^{n(2H-1)}|\mathbb{E}[\theta_{n,0}\theta_{n,i}]|
,
2^{n(2H-1)}|\mathbb{E}[w_{n,0}w_{n,i}]|

4.17

\lambda>0

n\in\mathbb{N}

\delta>0

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left[\left|2^{n(H-1)}\left\lVert{\frac{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{n}}{\sqrt{\tau_{H}}}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-1\right|\geq\delta\right]&=\mathbb{P}\left[\left|2^{n(H-1)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{n}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-\sqrt{\tau_{H}}\right|\geq\delta\sqrt{\tau_{H}}\right]\\

H-\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}\left(\frac{Y^{H}}{\sqrt{\tau_{H}}}\right)=(H-1)+\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}\left\lVert{\frac{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{n}}{\sqrt{\tau_{H}}}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}=\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}\left(2^{n(H-1)}\left\lVert{\frac{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{n}}{\sqrt{\tau_{H}}}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\right),

and now using the fact
\sqrt{1+x}-1\sim x/2
as
x\rightarrow 0
yields the result. This completes the proof.

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{n(2H-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left|\left(\frac{k+1+s}{2^{n}}\right)^{2H}-\left(\frac{k+s}{2^{n}}\right)^{2H}\right|^{2}\,ds\leq\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{-n}+\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\frac{2^{(2H-2)n}\vee 2^{(-1-2H)n}}{|4H-1|}=0.

Hence, it follows from

3.9
that

0<\liminf_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{n(2H-2)}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(\vartheta^{v}_{n,k}\right)^{2}\leq\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{n(2H-2)}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(\vartheta^{v}_{n,k}\right)^{2}<\infty,

and this validates condition
(a)
for the sample path
v
. To validate condition
(b)
in
Proposition

3.7
for the sample path
v
, recall that the sample path of a fractional Brownian motion is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for any
\alpha<H
almost surely, thus, the law equivalence (
4.35
) yields that the sample path
z
is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. Hölder continuous for any exponent
\alpha<H
. Hence, the sample path
v
is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. Hölder continuous with exponent
\alpha<H
. Therefore, the sample path
v
satisfies condition
(b)
, and the assertion follows from
Proposition

3.7
. This completes the proof.
∎

On the other hand, the proof for the case
\nu=0
is more complex, since the laws of
W^{H}
and
Z^{H,\nu}/\Gamma(H+1/2)
are no longer equivalent, but in fact, mutually singular
[
30
, Theorem 5.8]
. To prove the assertion, we will show that the difference between
W^{H}
and
Z^{H,\nu}/\Gamma(H+\frac{1}{2})
satisfies the conditions in
Proposition

3.8
. Then, the assertions in
Theorem

2.4
follow from
Proposition

3.7
. To this end, we write
Z^{H}:=Z^{H,0}
for simplicity, and recall that Mandelbrot & van Ness representation demonstrates that the fractional Brownian motion can be represented as

W^{H}_{t}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(H+\frac{1}{2})}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left[(t-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right]\,dB_{u}+\int_{0}^{t}(t-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\,dB_{u}\right),\qquad t\geq 0.

where
B
is a standard Brownian motion. Furthermore, consider the process
(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
defined by

(4.36)

\widetilde{X}^{H}_{t}:=\Gamma(H+\frac{1}{2})W^{H}_{t}-Z^{H}_{t}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left[(t-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right]\,dB_{u},\qquad t\in[0,1].

It was shown in
[
30
, Theorem 5.1]
that
\widetilde{X}^{H}
is well-defined. Our next lemma verifies condition
(b)
of
Proposition

3.9
for the process
\widetilde{X}^{H}
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
\widetilde{X}^{H}
be as in (
4.36
). Then, with probability one, we have

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{n(2H-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\widetilde{X}^{H}\rangle^{(2)}_{n}\left(\frac{s}{2^{n}}\right)\,ds=0.

Proof.

Rewriting (
3.18
) gives

(4.37)

\displaystyle\begin{split}2^{n(2H-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\widetilde{X}^{H}\rangle^{(2)}_{n}\left(\frac{s}{2^{n}}\right)\,ds&=2^{n(2H-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{k+1+s}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{k+s}{2^{n}}}\right)^{2}\,ds\\
&=2^{n(2H-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s+1}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\right)^{2}\,ds+2^{n(2H-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{k+1+s}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{k+s}{2^{n}}}\right)^{2}\,ds.\end{split}

In the following, we will prove this lemma by showing that the two rightmost terms in (
4.37
) converge to zero with probability one. To this end, note that for
0\leq s\leq t<\infty
, we have

\widetilde{X}^{H}_{t}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{s}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left[(t-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(s-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right]dB_{u}=\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left(\int_{s}^{t}(\tau-u)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\,d\tau\right)\,dB_{u}.

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality then yields

(4.38)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{t}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{s}\right)^{2}\right]&=\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left(\int_{s}^{t}(\tau-u)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\,d\tau\right)^{2}\,du\\
&\leq\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\cdot|t-s|\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{s}^{t}(\tau-u)^{2H-3}\,d\tau du\\
&=\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\cdot|t-s|\int_{s}^{t}\int_{-\infty}^{0}(\tau-u)^{2H-3}\,dud\tau\\
&=\frac{\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}{2-2H}\cdot|t-s|\int_{s}^{t}\tau^{2H-2}\,d\tau.\end{split}

In particular, the first equation of (
4.38
) yields that the function
s\mapsto\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{1+s}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\big)^{2}\big]
is decreasing. Let us now denote

c_{H}:=\int_{0}^{1}\left((1-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}\,du.

It was shown in
[
29
, Propositoin 2.3]
that
c_{H}<\infty
for
H\in(0,1)
. Furthermore, the process
\widetilde{X}^{H}
is
H
-self-similar, i.e.,
(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\alpha t})\overset{d}{=}(\alpha^{H}\widetilde{X}^{H}_{t})
, and this gives

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{1+s}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\right)^{2}\right]&\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{1}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{0}\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{1}{2^{n}}}\right)^{2}\right]=2^{-2Hn}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{1}\right)^{2}\right]\\
&=2^{-2Hn}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left[(1-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right]\,dB_{u}\right)^{2}\right]\\
&=2^{-2Hn}\int_{0}^{1}\left((1-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}\,du=2^{-2Hn}c_{H},\end{split}

Hence, applying the Fubini–Tonelli theorem, we have

2^{n(2H-1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{1+s}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\right)^{2}\,ds\right]=2^{n(2H-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{1+s}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\right)^{2}\right]\,ds\leq 2^{-n}c_{H},

and thus,
2^{n(2H-1)}\mathbb{E}\big[\int_{0}^{1}\big(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{1+s}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\big)^{2}\,ds\big]\rightarrow 0
as
n\uparrow\infty
.

In the next step, we are going to show that
\text{\rm Var}\big[2^{n(2H-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\big(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{1+s}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\big)^{2}\,ds\big]
converges to zero exponentially fast. Since
\widetilde{X}^{H}_{t}
is a centered Gaussian process, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{1+s}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\right)^{4}\right]=3\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{1+s}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\right)^{2}\right]^{2}\leq 3\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{1}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{0}\right)^{2}\right]^{2}\leq 2^{-4Hn+2}c^{2}_{H}.

Hence, by a direct application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the Fubini–Tonelli theorem,

\begin{split}\text{\rm Var}\left[2^{n(2H-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s+1}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\right)^{2}\,ds\right]&\leq 2^{n(4H-2)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s+1}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\right)^{2}\,ds\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\leq 2^{n(4H-2)}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s+1}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\right)^{4}\,ds\right]\leq 2^{-2n+2}c_{H}^{2}.\end{split}

Clearly, the rightmost term converges to zero exponentially fast. Thus, by fast
L_{2}
-convergence,
2^{n(2H-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\big(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s+1}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{s}{2^{n}}}\big)^{2}\,ds
converges to zero with probability one as
n\uparrow\infty
.

Next, we aim to show that the second term in (
4.37
) also converges to zero with probability one. To this end, we first of all show that for
\gamma>0
and almost every sample path
x
of
\widetilde{X}^{H}
, there exists
\kappa_{\gamma}>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq n_{0}
, we have

(4.39)

\left|x\left(\frac{k+1+s}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(\frac{k+s}{2^{n}}\right)\right|\leq\kappa_{\gamma}2^{-(1-\gamma)n}\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)^{H-1},\qquad s\in[0,1]\quad\text{and}\quad k\geq 1.

To this end, applying (
4.38
) yields that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{2^{-n}(H-\frac{1}{2})^{2}}{2-2H}\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\tau^{2H-2}\,d\tau\leq\frac{2^{-2n}(H-\frac{1}{2})^{2}}{2-2H}\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)^{2H-2}.

For any given
\gamma>0
, Chebyshev’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq 2^{n}-1}\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)^{1-H}|\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}|\geq 2^{-(1-\gamma)n}\right)&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)^{1-H}|\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}|\geq 2^{-(1-\gamma)n}\right)\\
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}\right)^{2}\right]\left(\dfrac{k}{2^{n}}\right)^{2-2H}}{2^{-(2-2\gamma)n}}\leq\frac{(H-\frac{1}{2})^{2}}{2-2H}\cdot 2^{-2\gamma n}.\end{split}

Clearly, the rightmost term in the above inequality is summable. Thus, the Borel–Cantelli lemma yields that for a typical sample path
x
, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(4.40)

\left|x\left(\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)\right|\leq 2^{-(1-\gamma)n}\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)^{H-1},\qquad k\geq 1.

To extend the above inequality to case
s\in[0,1]
as in (
4.39
), for given
s\in[0,1]
, consider an increasing sequence
(s_{j})_{j\geq n}
such that for
j\geq n

s_{n}=\frac{k}{2^{n}},\quad|s_{j+1}-s_{j}|\in\left\{0,\frac{1}{2^{j+1}}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{j}\uparrow\frac{k+s}{2^{n}}

In a similar manner, we can construct a sequence
(s^{\prime}_{k})_{k\geq n}
analogously that converges to
\frac{k+1+s}{2^{n}}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle x\left(\frac{k+1+s}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(\frac{k+s}{2^{n}}\right)=\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}x(s_{j+1})-x(s_{j})+x\left(\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)+\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}x(s^{\prime}_{j+1})-x(s^{\prime}_{j}).

Therefore, applying the triangular inequality gives

\displaystyle\left|x\left(\frac{k+1+s}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(\frac{k+s}{2^{n}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}|x(s_{j+1})-x(s_{j})|+\left|x\left(\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)\right|+\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}|x(s^{\prime}_{j+1})-x(s^{\prime}_{j})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\left(s_{j}\right)^{H-1}|s_{j+1}-s_{j}|^{H-1}+2^{-(1-\gamma)n}\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)^{H-1}+\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\left(s^{\prime}_{j}\right)^{H-1}|s^{\prime}_{j+1}-s^{\prime}_{j}|^{H-1}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)^{H-1}\left(\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}2^{-(1-\gamma)j}+2^{-(1-\gamma)n}+\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}2^{-(1-\gamma)j}\right)=\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)^{H-1}2^{-(1-\gamma)n}\left(1+\frac{2}{1-2^{-(1-\gamma)}}\right).

Taking
\kappa_{\gamma}=1+\frac{2}{1-2^{-(1-\gamma)}}
yields back (
4.39
). For given
H\in(0,1)
, we take
\gamma<1-H\vee\frac{1}{2}
. Then

\begin{split}2^{n(1-2H)}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}}\left|x\left(\frac{k+1+s}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(\frac{k+s}{2^{n}}\right)\right|&\leq\kappa_{\gamma}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}2^{n(1-2H)-2(1-\gamma)n}\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)^{2H-2}=\kappa_{\gamma}2^{n(2\gamma-1)}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}k^{2H-2}.\end{split}

Clearly, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}k^{2H-2}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}(1)&\qquad\text{for}\quad H\in(0,1/2),\\
\mathcal{O}(2^{(2H-1)n})&\qquad\text{for}\quad H\in(1/2,1).\end{cases}

Thus, we have
2^{n(2\gamma-1)}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}k^{2H-2}=\mathcal{O}(2^{n(2\gamma-1)}\vee 2^{n(2H+2\gamma-2)})
. Hence, for any
s\in[0,1]
, we have

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}2^{n(1-2H)}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}}\left|x\left(\frac{k+1+s}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(\frac{k+s}{2^{n}}\right)\right|=0.

Finally, the assertion follows from Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

For any
H\in(0,1)
, the process
\widetilde{X}^{H}
admits the roughness exponent
R=1
, and the Riemann–Liouville process
Z^{H}
admits the roughness exponent
R=H
with probability one.

Proof.

We begin with showing that
\widetilde{X}^{H}
admits the roughness exponent
R=1
almost surely. Let
x
be a typical sample path of
\widetilde{X}^{H}
. It suffices to show that
\langle x\rangle^{(p)}_{n}\downarrow 0
as
n\uparrow\infty
for any
p>1
. To this end, note that

\langle x\rangle^{(p)}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left|x\left(\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)\right|^{p}=|x\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(0\right)|^{p}+\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}\left|x\left(\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)\right|^{p}.

Clearly,
|x(2^{-n})-x(0)|\rightarrow 0
as
n\uparrow\infty
, as
x
is continuous. Hence, it suffices to prove that the second term converges to zero. For given
p>1
, we take
\gamma<(1-1/p)\wedge H
. Then (
4.40
) implies that there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n>n_{0}
, we have

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}\left|x\left(\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)\right|^{p}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}2^{-p(1-\gamma)n}\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)^{p(H-1)}=2^{(\gamma-H)pn}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}k^{pH-p}.\end{split}

It is clear that
\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}k^{pH-p}=\mathcal{O}(2^{(pH-p+1)n}\vee 1)
, and hence,

\sum_{k=1}^{2^{n}-1}\left|x\left(\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}\right)-x\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)\right|^{p}=\mathcal{O}(2^{(1+\gamma-p)n}\vee 2^{-(\gamma-H)p}).

As a result, the above term converges to zero as
n\uparrow\infty
, and this demonstrates the
\widetilde{X}^{H}
admits the roughness exponent
R=1
with probability one. Recall that it was shown in
[
23
, Theorem 5.1]
that
W^{H}
admits
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. the roughness exponent
H
. Together with the definition
Z^{H}=\Gamma(H+1/2)W^{H}-\widetilde{X}^{H}
,
Proposition

3.9
leads to the assertion.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2.4
for
\nu=0
.

We write
V^{H}:=V^{H,0}
and
Y^{H}:=Y^{H,0}
for simplicity. As
t\rightarrow t^{2H}
is of bounded variation, and
Z^{H}
admits the roughness exponent
H
with probability one.
Proposition

3.8
implies that the process
V^{H}=\gamma Z^{H}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4H}t^{2H}
admits the roughness exponent
H
. It then follows from
Proposition

3.6
that the process
g\circ V^{H}
admits the roughness exponent
H
for
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

To prove
\lim_{n}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(Y^{H})=H
, it suffices to show that any sample path of
Z^{H}
satisfies condition
(a)
of
Proposition

3.9
and condition
(b)
of
Proposition

3.7
with probability one. If so, an analogous application of
Proposition

3.9
as in the case
\nu=0
will yield that
V^{H}
satisfies condition
(b)
of
Proposition

3.7
. Moreover, together with the fact
t\mapsto t^{2H}
is
H
-Hölder continuous, we can obtain that
V^{H}
satisfies condition
(a)
of
Proposition

3.7
. Subsequently,
Proposition

3.7
will lead to
\lim_{n}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(Y^{H})=H
. It is implied by
Lemma

3.2
that condition
(a)
of
Proposition

3.9
is satisfied by sample paths of
W^{H}
with probability one. Furthermore,
Lemma

4.10
demonstrates that almost all sample paths of
\widetilde{X}^{H}
satisfy condition
(a)
of
Proposition

3.9
. As a result, condition
(a)
of
Proposition

3.7
is satisfied by the sample paths of
Z^{H}
with probability one.

Finally, integrating out the last term in (
4.38
) yields that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{X}^{H}_{t}-\widetilde{X}^{H}_{s}\right)^{2}\right]&=\frac{(H-\frac{1}{2})^{2}}{2-2H}\cdot|t-s|\int_{s}^{t}\tau^{2H-2}\,d\tau\\
&\leq\frac{(H-\frac{1}{2})^{2}}{(2-2H)(2H-1)}\left(t^{2H-1}-s^{2H-1}\right)|t-s|\leq\kappa_{H}|t-s|^{2H},\end{split}

for some
\kappa_{H}>0
. Here, the final inequality follows from
[
22
, Lemma 2.2]
. Hence, it follows from the Kolmogorov continuity theorem that the sample paths of
\widetilde{X}^{H}
are Hölder continuous for any exponent
\alpha<H
. Thus,
Z^{H}=\Gamma(H+\frac{1}{2})W^{H}-\widetilde{X}^{H}
is also
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for any
\alpha<H
. This verifies condition
(b)
of
Proposition

3.7
, and hence, we complete the proof.
∎
