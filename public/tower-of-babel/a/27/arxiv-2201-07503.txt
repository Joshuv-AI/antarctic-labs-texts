Dual-Code Bounds on Multiple Concurrent (Local) Data Recovery
Gianira N. Alfarano, Alberto Ravagnani, Emina Soljanin

Abstract
We are concerned with linear redundancy storage schemes regarding their ability to provide concurrent (local) recovery of multiple data objects. This paper initiates a study of such systems within the classical coding theory. We show how we can use the structural properties of the generator matrix defining the scheme to obtain a bounding polytope for the set of data access rates the system can support. We derive two dual distance outer bounds, which are sharp for some large classes of matrix families.

Introduction

Distributed computing systems rely on their storage layers to provide data access services for executing applications. Thus, the system’s overall performance depends on the underlying storage system’s data access performance.
Distributed storage systems strive to maximize the number of concurrent data access requests they can support with fixed resources. Replicating data objects according to their relative popularity and access volume helps achieve this goal. However, these quantities are often unpredictable. In emerging applications such as edge computing, the expected number of users and their data interests fluctuate, and data storage schemes should support such dynamics
[
1
]
. Erasure-coding has emerged as an efficient and robust form of redundant storage, which can
flexibly handle skews in the request rates.

Recent work on redundant distributed storage access
introduced the notion of the
service rate region
of a redundancy scheme that includes all data access requests that the system can serve

[

,

,

,

To understand this concept, consider a distributed system that stores
k
different data objects by encoding them into
n
and storing the
n
coded objects on
n
different nodes. Each of the
n
nodes can serve requests at a rate
\mu
(i.e., has service capacity
\mu
). The system can serve requests to access the
k

,
\lambda_{2}
, …,
\lambda_{k}
if each request can be routed to a group of nodes that can jointly fulfill the request, and the total request rate allocated to each node does not exceed its service capacity
\mu
. We call the set of such request vectors (
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
, …,
\lambda_{k}

The overview paper

postulated the service rate region as an important consideration in the design of erasure-coded distributed systems. It highlights several open problems that can be grouped into two broad threads: 1) characterizing the service rate region of a given code and finding the optimal request allocation, and 2) designing the underlying erasure code for a given service rate region. The paper argued that the presented problems not only require expertise from different areas, but have also already been addressed in those areas in some special forms and under different names. Moreover, it explained how some problems associated with the service rate region generalize previously studied distributed problems such as batch codes, codes with locality and availability, and private information retrieval

The numerous open problems described in

Our contribution.

The goal of this paper is to initiate a study of redundancy schemes within the classical coding theory.
We focus on characterizing the service rate region of a storage scheme defined by a rank
k
generator matrix
G
, which is
a convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Our ultimate goal is
to establish a series of inequalities that, when combined, cut out the service region.
This paper makes a first step towards this goal by showing

We also show that the

Distributed Coded Recovery Systems

In this section we establish the notation for
the rest of the paper, define distributed coded systems and their service rate region.

Notation 2.1
.

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{F}_{q}
denotes the finite field with
q
elements where
q
is a prime power.
We work with integers
n>k\geq 2
, a real number
\mu\geq 1
,
and a fixed matrix

G\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
of rank
k
. We assume that
G
has no all-zero column and
denote by
G^{j}

its
j
th column.

We consider coded distributed systems where
k
data objects are linearly encoded into
n
objects stored on
n
servers. Each server stores exactly one object and the objects are elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Such a distributed coded system is fully specified by a rank
k
matrix
G\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
, which we call the
generator matrix
of the system. If
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
is the
k
-tuple of objects to be stored,
then the
j
th server stores the
j
th component of the vector

(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\cdot G\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}.

We say
that the matrix
G
is
systematic
if its first
k
columns form the identity
k\times k
matrix.

We consider
k
-tuples
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
of rate requests to access the
k
objects. More precisely,
\lambda_{i}
is the rate request for the
i
th object.
Each of the
n
servers can serve request at the rate of at most
\mu\geq 1
. The parameter
\mu
is called the server’s
capacity
.

Each user gets assigned to a set of servers that, together, allow recovering the desired object. An object can be recovered from different server sets, which motivates the following terminology.

Definition 2.2
.

For
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, let

\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}:=\{R\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\mid e_{i}\in\langle G^{j}\mid j\in R\rangle\},

where
e_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
denotes the
i
th
standard basis vector and
\langle G^{j}\mid j\in R\rangle
is the span of the columns of
G
indexed by
R
. The elements of
\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}
are the
recovery sets
for the
i
th object.

Note that, in the above definition, we have

\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}\neq\emptyset
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. This is a simple consequence of the fact that
G
has rank
k
. Moreover,
R\neq\emptyset
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and
R\in\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}
. We use the superscript
“all” to indicate that
\mathscr{R}_{i}^{\textnormal{all}}
contains
all
the recovery sets for the symbol
i
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
k=2
,
n=4
,

and

G:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1&1\\
0&1&2&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2\times 4}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{\{1\},\{2,3\},\{2,4\},\{3,4\},\{2,3,4\}\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{\{2\},\{1,3\},\{1,4\},\{3,4\},\{1,3,4\}\}.

When designing a recovery system starting from
G
, not all recovery sets need to be considered.

Definition 2.4
.

A
recovery

G
-
system
is a
k
-tuple

\mathscr{R}=(\mathscr{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{R}_{k})
of subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
\mathscr{R}_{i}\subseteq\mathscr{R}_{i}^{\textnormal{all}}
and
\mathscr{R}_{i}\neq\emptyset
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

The service rate region of a recovery
G
-system
\mathscr{R}
is the set of all request rate tuples
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
that can be served by the system.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\mathscr{R}=(\mathscr{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{R}_{k})
be
a recovery
G
-system.
The
service rate region
associated with
\mathscr{R}
and
\mu
is the set of
all
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
for which there exists a collection of real numbers

\{\lambda_{i,R}\mid i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},\,R\in\mathscr{R}_{i}\}

with the following properties:

\displaystyle\sum_{R\in\mathscr{R}_{i}}\lambda_{i,R}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\textnormal{ for }1\leq i\leq k,

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathscr{R}_{i}\\
j\in R\end{subarray}}\lambda_{i,R}

\displaystyle\leq\mu\

\displaystyle\textnormal{ for }1\leq j\leq n,

(2)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,R}

\displaystyle\geq 0\

\displaystyle\textnormal{ for }1\leq i\leq k,\,R\in\mathscr{R}_{i}.

(3)

A collection
\{\lambda_{i,R}\}

of real numbers that satisfy the above three properties is called a
feasible allocation
for
(\mathscr{R},\mu)
. The service rate region associated with
\mathscr{R}
and

is denoted by

\Lambda(\mathscr{R},\mu)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}.

Remark 2.6
.

We have

\Lambda(\mathscr{R},\mu)=\mu\cdot\Lambda(\mathscr{R},1)

for any recovery

\mathscr{R}
. Therefore, we will simply call
\Lambda(\mathscr{R},1)
the

of

.

Our main goal is to establish outer bounds for the service rate region
\Lambda(\mathscr{R},1)
of a recovery

-system

, in the form of a

polytope.

Example 2.7
.

Let

.

3
Minimal Recovery Sets

A natural question is how the service rate region changes when changing the recovery
G
-system.
An immediate observation is the following. The result easily follows from Definition
2.5
and is therefore left to the reader.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that
\mathscr{R}=(\mathscr{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{R}_{k})
and

\mathscr{R}^{\prime}=(\mathscr{R}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{R}^{\prime}_{k})
are recovery
G
-systems with
\mathscr{R}^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq\mathscr{R}_{i}
for all

i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Then

\Lambda(\mathscr{R},1)\supseteq\Lambda(\mathscr{R}^{\prime},1)
. In particular,

\Lambda(\mathscr{R},\mu)\subseteq\Lambda(\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}},\mu)

for any recovery
G
-system
\mathscr{R}
.

The service rate region, however, does not change when selecting from
\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}
the recovery sets that are
minimal with respect to inclusion, in the following precise sense.

Definition 3.2
.

A set
R\in\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}
is

i
-minimal
if there is no
R^{\prime}\in\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}
with
R^{\prime}\subsetneq R
. We define
\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{min}}
as the recovery
G
-system defined, for all
i
, by

\mathscr{R}_{i}^{\textnormal{min}}:=\{R\in\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}\mid R\mbox{ is $i$-minimal}\}.

Proposition 3.3
.

We have

\Lambda(\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{min}},1)=\Lambda(\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}},1)
.

Therefore, when studying the service rate region of the system defined by
G
one may or may not restrict to the minimal recovery sets.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

The inclusion “
\subseteq
”
follows directly from Proposition
3.1
.
To prove the inclusion “
\supseteq
”, let
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\in\Lambda(\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}},1)
. By definition, there exist a collection of real numbers

\{\lambda_{i,R}\mid i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},\,R\in\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}\},

satisfying (
1
)–(
3
). We will show
that
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\in\Lambda(\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{min}},1)
.
In order to do so, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}

and
R\in\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}
we fix a set

\mbox{min}_{i}(R)\in\mathscr{R}_{i}^{\textnormal{min}}

such that
R\supseteq\mbox{min}_{i}(R)
.
For
Q\in\mathscr{R}_{i}^{\textnormal{min}}
define

\lambda_{i,Q}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}\\
\textnormal{min}_{i}(R)=Q\end{subarray}}\lambda_{i,R}.

Then
the numbers
\{\lambda_{i,Q}\mid i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},\,Q\in\mathscr{R}_{i}^{\textnormal{min}}\}
satisfy (
1
)–(
3
).
∎

4.1
First Dual Distance Bound

We next establish a connection between the recovery sets of a systematic coded system and the codewords of the code whose parity-check matrix is
G
. By combining this with the Total Capacity Bound, we obtain our First Dual Distance Bound.

Notation 4.3
.

In the sequel, we denote by

\mathscr{C}
the linear code generated by
G
, and by
\mathscr{C}^{\perp}
, its dual code. Note that
\mathscr{C}

is an
[n,k]_{q}
code. We let
d^{\perp}
be the minimum distance of
\mathscr{C}^{\perp}
.
Recall that the (
Hamming
)
support
of a vector
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is
\sigma(x):=\{1\leq i\leq n\mid x_{i}\neq 0\}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose that
G
is systematic.
Let
R\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
and
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. Then
R\in\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}
if and only if
R=\{i\}
or there exists a codeword
x\in\mathscr{C}^{\perp}
with

\sigma(x)\subseteq R\cup\{i\}

and
i\in\sigma(x)
.

Proof.

By definition,
R\in\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}
if and only if
the span of the columns of
G
indexed by
R\cup\{i\}

contains
e_{i}
. This happens if and only if

R=\{i\}
or there exists a linear combination of the columns of
G
indexed by
R
, where the
i
th column of
G
is taken with a nonzero coefficient, that gives zero. The latter condition is equivalent to the existence of a codeword
x\in\mathscr{C}^{\perp}
with
i\in\sigma(x)
and
\sigma(x)\subseteq R
.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

If
G
is systematic,

i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and

R\in\mathscr{R}^{\textnormal{all}}_{i}
, then
R=\{i\}
or
|R|\geq d^{\perp}-1
.

We are now ready to present the First Dual Distance Bound.

Theorem 4.6

(First Dual Distance Bound)
.

Suppose that
G
is systematic and let
\mathscr{R}
be a recovery
G
-system.
If
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})\in\Lambda(\mathscr{R},1)
, then

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(\min\{\lambda_{i},1\}+(d^{\perp}-1)\max\{0,\lambda_{i}-1\}\Bigr)\leq n.

Proof.

Let
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})\in\Lambda(\mathscr{R},1)
and let

\{\lambda_{i,R}\}
be a feasible allocation.
By Corollary
4.5
,
for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}

and every set
R\in\mathscr{R}_{i}
with
R\neq\{i\}
we have
|R|\geq d^{\perp}-1
. We can therefore rewrite and bound the LHS of (
4
) as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,\{i\}}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathscr{R}_{i}\\
R\neq\{i\}\end{subarray}}|R|\lambda_{i,R}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i,\{i\}}+(d^{\perp}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathscr{R}_{i}\\
R\neq\{i\}\end{subarray}}\lambda_{i,R}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i,\{i\}}+(d^{\perp}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i,\{i\}})

\displaystyle=(d^{\perp}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}-(d^{\perp}-2)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i,\{i\}}.

(5)

Since
G
has no all-zero column, we have
d^{\perp}\geq 2
. Therefore, since

\lambda_{i,\{i\}}\leq\min\{\lambda_{i},1\}
for all
i
, we can further say that the right-hand side of (
4.1
) is at least

\displaystyle(d^{\perp}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}-(d^{\perp}-2)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\min\{\lambda_{i},1\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(\min\{\lambda_{i},1\}+(d^{\perp}-1)\max\{0,\lambda_{i}-1\}\Bigr),

which, combined with (
4
),
gives the statement.
∎

We can apply Theorem
4.6
to families of systematic codes whose dual distance is known. For example, by considering the class of systematic MDS codes
we obtain the following result.

Corollary 4.7
.

Suppose that
G
is systematic and that it generates an MDS code.
Then for all

(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})\in\Lambda(\mathscr{R},1)
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(\min\{\lambda_{i},1\}+k\cdot\max\{0,\lambda_{i}-1\}\Bigr)\leq n.

The previous corollary on MDS codes is sharp whenever

4.2
Second Dual Distance Bound

The goal of this subsection is to identify and study
new, non-classical parameters of the matrix
G
that play a role in determining the associated service rate region. As an application, we obtain a second outer bound for the service rate region of a coded system,
which refines the First Dual Distance Bound of Theorem
4.6
under some assumptions.

Notation 4.9
.

In the sequel, for all

i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, we let

G_{i}=\Bigl(G\mid e_{i}^{\top}\Bigr)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times(n+1)}

be the matrix obtained from
G
by appending the
i
th standard basis vector as the

-th column.
Then
G_{i}
generates an
[n+1,k]_{q}
code, which we denote by
\mathscr{C}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}

\mathscr{C}_{i}^{\perp}
. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, we introduce the following sets and parameters:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}

\displaystyle:=\{\textnormal{wt}(x)\mid x\in\mathscr{C}_{i}^{\perp},\,n+1\in\sigma(x)\},

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}

\displaystyle:=|\Gamma_{i}|~~\text{and}~~\delta_{i}^{1}:=\min(\Gamma_{i}).

Moreover, we let

\delta_{i}^{2}:=\min\{\textnormal{wt}(x)\mid x\in\mathscr{C}_{i}^{\perp},\,n+1\in\sigma(x),\textnormal{wt}(x)>\delta_{i}^{1}\},

with the convention that
\delta_{i}^{2}=\delta_{i}^{1}
when
\gamma_{i}=1
.
Finally, we define

\omega_{i}:=\frac{|\{x\in\mathscr{C}_{i}^{\perp}:n+1\in\sigma(x),\,\textnormal{wt}(x)=\delta_{i}^{1}\}|}{q-1}.

The structural parameters defined in Notation

In this conference paper, we give preliminary evidence of this with a result that

We start by describing the recovery sets of

Proposition 4.10

Let

Proposition 4.14
.

Suppose that
G
is systematic and that

d^{\perp}\geq 3
. Then for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
we have
\delta_{i}^{1}=2
,
\omega_{i}=1
, and
\delta_{i}^{2}\geq d^{\perp}
.

Proof.

We only prove the result for
i=1
; the proof for the other indices is identical.
We denote by
\pi:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
the projection onto the first
n
coordinates.
That
\delta_{1}^{1}=2
easily follows from the definitions, since
the
1
st and the
(n+1)
th columns of
G_{1}
are equal.

We claim that
(1,0,\ldots,0,-1)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}
is the only codeword

x\in\mathscr{C}_{1}^{\perp}
(up to multiples) with
n+1\in\sigma(x)
and Hamming weight 2.
Let
x=(1,0,\ldots,0,-1)
and
suppose towards a contradiction that there exists
y\in\mathscr{C}_{i}^{\perp}
with

y_{n+1}=1
, Hamming weight 2, and linearly independent from
x
.
Then
\pi(x+y)\in\mathscr{C}^{\perp}
is non-zero and has weight at most 2, contradicting
d^{\perp}\geq 3
.
This shows that
\omega_{1}=1
.

It remains to show that
\delta_{1}^{2}\geq d^{\perp}
. Let
x\in\mathscr{C}_{1}^{\perp}
be the codeword defined above.

•

We start by showing that
\delta_{1}^{2}\geq 3
.
Take
y\in\mathscr{C}^{\perp}

with
\textnormal{wt}(y)=d^{\perp}\geq 3
.
Then
(y,0)+x\in\mathscr{C}_{i}^{\perp}
has weight at least
d^{\perp}\geq 3
.
Not all codewords of
\mathscr{C}_{1}^{\perp}
with
n+1
in their support have weight 2. Therefore
\delta_{1}^{2}\geq 3
by definition of
\delta_{1}^{2}
.

•

Let
y\in\mathscr{C}_{1}^{\perp}
be a codeword with
\textnormal{wt}(y)=\delta_{1}^{2}
and
n+1\in\sigma(y)
. By the previous item,
\textnormal{wt}(y)\geq 3
.

If
1\not\in\sigma(y)
, then
\pi(x+y)\in\mathscr{C}
is a non-zero codeword with the same weight as
y
. Therefore
\delta_{i}^{2}=\textnormal{wt}(y)\geq d^{\perp}
. On the other hand, if
1\in\sigma(y)
then
z:=(y_{1}+y_{n+1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathscr{C}^{\perp}
. Since
\textnormal{wt}(y)\geq 3
we have
z\neq 0
. Therefore

d^{\perp}\leq\textnormal{wt}(z)\leq\textnormal{wt}(y)=\delta_{1}^{2}
. ∎

The example illustrates that the service rate region of a
G
-system depends on
G
. How invertible operations on

5
Conclusions and Future Work

The service rate region is a new aspect of linear redundancy schemes, which measures their ability to provide simultaneous data recovery. The problem was studied in different frameworks. We initiates a study within the classical coding theory. This approach enabled us to derive some new results and recover some previously known bounds on the service rate region straightforwardly.
A liner redundancy system is defined by a matrix akin to a code generator matrix.
We believe that coding theory can be instrumental in identifying and using the matrix’s structural properties to construct polytopes that contain the service rate region by establishing a series of outer bounds that, when combined, determine the region.
