A Primal-Dual Framework for Symmetric Cone Programming
Jiaqi Zheng, Antonios Varvitsiotis, Tiow-Seng Tan, Wayne Lin

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a primal-dual algorithmic framework for solving Symmetric Cone Programs (SCPs), a versatile optimization model that unifies and extends Linear, Second-Order Cone (SOCP), and Semidefinite Programming (SDP). Our work generalizes the primal-dual framework for SDPs introduced by Arora and Kale [ 6 ] , leveraging a recent extension of the Multiplicative Weights Update method (MWU) to symmetric cones. Going beyond existing works, our framework can handle SOCPs and mixed SCPs, exhibits nearly linear time complexity, and can be effectively parallelized. To illustrate the efficacy of our framework, we employ it to develop approximation algorithms for two geometric optimization problems: the Smallest Enclosing Sphere problem and the Support Vector Machine problem. Our theoretical analyses demonstrate that the two algorithms compute approximate solutions in nearly linear running time and with parallel depth scaling polylogarithmically with the input size. We compare our algorithms against CGAL as well as interior point solvers applied to these problems. Experiments show that our algorithms are highly efficient when implemented on a CPU and achieve substantial speedups when parallelized on a GPU, allowing us to solve large-scale instances of these problems.



Keywords:
Symmetric Cone Programming, Approximation Algorithms, Geometric Optimization

Introduction

A
linear conic program
(LCP) is an optimization problem that minimizes a linear objective function over the intersection of a
convex cone
\mathcal{K}
and a finite number of halfspaces:

\begin{aligned} \max\quad&{\bm{c}}\bullet{\bm{x}}\\
\subto\quad&{\bm{a}}_{j}\bullet{\bm{x}}\leq b_{j}\quad\forall j\in[m]\\
&{\bm{x}}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}}0\end{aligned}\hskip 40.00006pt\begin{aligned} \min\quad&{\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}\\
\subto\quad&\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}^{*}}{\bm{c}}\\
&{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}\end{aligned}

(LCP)

Here
\mathcal{K}^{*}
denotes the dual cone of
\mathcal{K}
and
\succeq_{\mathcal{K}}
denotes the generalized inequality induced by the cone
\mathcal{K}
, i.e.
{\bm{x}}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}}{\bm{y}}
iff
{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{K}
.
Some of the most important convex optimization models are instances of LCPs with respect to appropriate convex cones. For example, LCPs over the
d
-dimensional non-negative orthant
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
correspond to
linear programs

(LPs), and
LCPs over the cone of
d\times d
positive semidefinite (PSD) matrices
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{+}
correspond to
semidefinite programs
(SDPs). Additionally, LCPs related to second-order cone
\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}

correspond to
second-order cone programs
(SOCPs). These optimization models – LPs, SDPs, and SOCPs – hold a prominent place in the realm of convex optimization, finding applications in a wide array of domains.

A
symmetric cone
is a closed convex cone that is self-dual and homogeneous.
The non-negative orthant, the cone of (real or Hermitian) PSD matrices, the second-order
cone, and Cartesian products thereof are all examples of symmetric cones, and a
symmetric cone program
(SCP) is simply an LCP over a symmetric cone.
Consequently, SCPs provide a unifying framework for studying LPs, SDPs, and SOCPs.
From an algorithmic standpoint, SCPs admit self-concordant barrier functions, which facilitate the development and analysis of interior point methods (IPMs)
[
20
,
35
]
.

Many approximation algorithms have been introduced in the literature for LPs and SDPs based on the Multiplicative Weight Update (MWU) method over the simplex, e.g.
[
39
,
4
]
, and its matrix variant over the set of density matrices, e.g.
[
6
]
. In this work, we focus on algorithms based on the MWU framework that are primal-dual, in the sense that, at the end of execution, we obtain a pair of primal/dual feasible solutions with an optimality gap bounded by the desired error tolerance. An important instance of this is the primal-dual SDP framework by Arora and Kale for SDPs
[
6
]
. MWU-based algorithms have a running time that is nearly linear in the input size and are amenable to parallelization due to the nature of the multiplicative update.

Motivated by the success of such methods for LPs and SDPs, in this work we try to
understand:

Can we develop primal-dual MWU-based algorithms for symmetric cone programs that have nearly linear time complexity and are effectively parallelizable?

In this work, we introduce the first primal-dual approximation framework for SCPs, which is detailed in Section
3
.
Our framework is motivated by, and generalizes, the primal-dual framework for SDPs developed by Arora and Kale
[
6
]
. To devise our framework, we rely on the recently introduced
symmetric cone multiplicative weight update
method (SCMWU)
[
13
]
that is designed for online optimization over symmetric cones.

In contrast to existing MWU-based algorithms designed solely for LPs or SDPs, our new framework can handle SOCPs and, additionally, SCPs with mixed conic constraints,
e.g., problems featuring both linear constraints and positive-semidefinite constraints.
Compared to IPMs, which require solving systems of linear equations at each iteration, our framework has significantly lower computational costs and can be effectively parallelized. Additionally, we offer geometric insights into our primal-dual framework that are also applicable to the previous MWU-based frameworks for LPs
[
39
]
and SDPs
[
6
]
.

As a practical demonstration of our framework we develop approximation algorithms for two computational geometry problems. The first one is the
smallest enclosing sphere
(SES) problem of finding the sphere of minimum radius that encloses an input set of spheres, and the second one is the
support vector machine
(SVM) problem of finding a hyperplane that separates two sets of data with maximum margin.
Our framework allows various degrees of freedom that necessitate further specification for each individual problem, which we provide in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
respectively.
When applied to the SES problem, our framework yields a parallelizable approach (dual-only algorithm in this context) that computes a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution with a
running time of
O(\frac{nd\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}})
,
where
n
represents the number of inputs and
d
is the dimensionality.
When applied to the SVM problem, our framework yields a parallelizable primal-dual algorithm that computes a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximate solution for SVM and a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution for its dual counterpart in
O(\frac{End\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}})
time,
where
n
is the number of data points,
d
is the dimensionality, and
E
is a instance-specific parameter that measures its difficulty.

In addition to the theoretical analyses, our algorithms for SES and SVM have been implemented in both sequential (CPU) and parallel (GPU) settings. Extensive experiments have been conducted to compare them with CGAL and IPM solvers in Section
5
. The results show that our algorithms are GPU-friendly and possess fairly large parallelism. For large-scale input instances, our GPU implementations outperform the commercial solvers.

Related work.

Plotkin, Shmoys, and Tardos
[
39
]
introduced a framework based on the classical MWU method for computing approximate solutions to packing and covering LPs with a running time that is nearly linear in the input size.
The framework of Plotkin et al.
[
39
]
was later employed by Klein and Lu
[
28
]
to devise fast approximation algorithms for the
Max Cut
problem, which was further extended by Arora et al.
[
4
]
for solving general SDPs.
These methods can be categorized as
primal-only
methods.
They can only find approximately feasible primal solutions that satisfy every constraint up to an
additive
error
\varepsilon
, and the running time is proportional to
1/\varepsilon^{2}
, which poses challenges when a strictly feasible solution is required or when
\varepsilon
is very small.
The problem is addressed when Arora and Kale
[
6
]
introduce their
primal-dual
SDP framework.
Their framework is based on the matrix multiplicative weight update method (MMWU) that has been discovered in different fields including optimization
[
8
]
, machine learning
[
44
]
, and online learning
[
47
]
.
It produces a pair of solutions to both primal and dual SDPs, which are strictly feasible and have multiplicative approximation ratios.
Together with specific combinatorial techniques, Arora and Kale also developed efficient approximation algorithms for NP-hard graph problems such as
Sparsest Cut
and
Balanced Separator
.

The MWU-based frameworks (for both LPs and SDPs) rely on the “width” of the problem, a parameter related to the largest entry or eigenvalue of the constraints.
As a closely related area of study, the literature also contains a range of sequential and parallel algorithms that are designed for specific families of LPs and SDPs, known as positive LPs (see, e.g.
[
30
,
50
,
2
]
) and positive SDPs
[
26
,
1
]
.
Unlike the MWU-based algorithms, the running time of these algorithms is typically independent of the width.
However, these width-independent algorithms often necessitate more sophisticated analysis, and most of the techniques developed in this line of research are not applicable to the generic problems.

Preliminaries

Symmetric cones and Euclidean Jordan algebras

A
symmetric cone
is a closed convex cone
\mathcal{K}
in a finite-dimensional inner product space
\mathcal{J}
that is self-dual (i.e.,
\mathcal{K}^{*}\triangleq\{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{J}:{\bm{y}}\bullet{\bm{x}}\geq 0,\forall{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{K}\}=\mathcal{K}
) and homogeneous (i.e., for any
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\in\mathbf{int}(\mathcal{K})

there exists an invertible linear transformation
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{J}
such that
\mathcal{T}({\bm{u}})={\bm{v}}
and
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{K})=\mathcal{K}
). Importantly, a symmetric cone can also be characterized as the cone of squares of an
Euclidean Jordan algebra
(EJA, e.g. see Proposition 2.5.8 in
[
46
]
):

\mathcal{K}=\{{\bm{x}}^{2}\triangleq{\bm{x}}\circ{\bm{x}}:\forall{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{J}\}.

Here, we recall that an EJA is a finite-dimensional vector space
\mathcal{J}
equipped with a bilinear product
\circ:\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{J}
that satisfies
{\bm{x}}\circ{\bm{y}}={\bm{y}}\circ{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{x}}^{2}\circ({\bm{x}}\circ{\bm{y}})={\bm{x}}\circ({\bm{x}}^{2}\circ{\bm{y}})
for all
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{J}
, an inner product
\bullet
that satisfies
({\bm{x}}\circ{\bm{y}})\bullet{\bm{z}}={\bm{x}}\bullet({\bm{y}}\circ{\bm{z}})
for all
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{J}
, and an identity element
{\bm{e}}
satisfying
{\bm{e}}\circ{\bm{x}}={\bm{x}}\circ{\bm{e}}={\bm{x}}
for all
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{J}
. Every EJA has a finite
rank
, which is defined as the largest degree of the minimal polynomial of any of its elements.

Every element
{\bm{x}}
of an EJA has a
spectral decomposition
, in the sense that there exists unique (up to ordering and multiplicities) real numbers
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r}
and a Jordan frame
\{{\bm{q}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{q}}_{r}\}
such that
{\bm{x}}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}{\bm{q}}_{i}
, where a Jordan frame is a collection of primitive idempotents
{\bm{q}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{q}}_{r}
that satisfy
{\bm{q}}_{i}\circ{\bm{q}}_{j}={\bm{0}}\ \forall i\neq j
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\bm{q}}_{i}={\bm{e}},
and
r
is the rank of the EJA. An element
{\bm{q}}\in\mathcal{J}
is an idempotent if
{\bm{q}}^{2}={\bm{q}}
and is said to be primitive if it is nonzero and cannot be written as the sum of two nonzero idempotents.
The
trace
of an EJA element with spectral decomposition
{\bm{x}}=\sum_{i}^{r}\lambda_{i}{\bm{q}}_{i}
is given by the sum of its eigenvalues

\mathbf{Tr}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{i}^{r}\lambda_{i}
.
In the following, the
inner product
of the EJA is denoted by
{\bm{x}}\bullet{\bm{y}}=\mathbf{Tr}({\bm{x}}\circ{\bm{y}})
.
The
cone of squares
of an EJA is then the set of elements with non-negative eigenvalues.

The characterization of symmetric cones as the cone of squares of EJAs, taken together with the classification result for finite-dimensional EJAs (e.g. see
[
19
]
), implies that all symmetric
cones over the real field are isomorphic to Cartesian products of
PSD cones and
second-order cones.
We now present some examples of symmetric cones, together with their algebraic properties which shall be important in our applications:

•

The non-negative orthant
\mathbbm{R}^{d}_{+}
.
The EJA product associated with
\mathbbm{R}^{d}_{+}
is the component-wise product
{\bm{x}}\circ{\bm{y}}=\mathbf{diag}({\bm{x}})\ {\bm{y}}
, and the associated identity element is
{\bm{e}}={\bm{1}}_{d}
. The rank of
\mathbbm{R}^{d}_{+}
is
d
, and the spectral decomposition of an element
{\bm{x}}
is
\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}{\bm{e}}_{i}
, where
{\bm{e}}_{i}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector. The trace is then
\mathbf{Tr}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}
.

•

The second-order cone
\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}
.
We consider
\mathbbm{R}^{d+1}
as an EJA with Jordan product
({\bm{u}};u_{0})\circ({\bm{v}};v_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(v_{0}{\bm{u}}+u_{0}{\bm{v}};{\bm{u}}^{T}{\bm{v}}+u_{0}v_{0})
. The corresponding cone of squares is the second-order cone

\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}\triangleq\{({\bm{u}};u_{0})\in\mathbbm{R}^{d+1}:\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}\leq u_{0}\}.

The rank of this EJA is 2 and its identity element is
{\bm{e}}=({\bm{0}};\sqrt{2})
.
For an element
({\bm{u}};u_{0})
of this EJA, its eigenvalues are
\lambda_{1,2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(u_{0}\pm\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2})
and the corresponding idempotents are
{\bm{q}}_{1,2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\pm{\bm{v}};1)
, where
{\bm{v}}=\frac{{\bm{u}}}{\|{\bm{u}}\|}
if
{\bm{u}}\neq{\bm{0}}
and any vector with unit
\ell_{2}
-norm otherwise.
The trace of an element
({\bm{u}};u_{0})
in this EJA is given by
\mathbf{Tr}({\bm{u}};u_{0})=\sqrt{2}u_{0}
.

•

The PSD cone
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{+}
.
The underlying EJA of the cone of
d\times d
Hermitian PSD matrices is the vector space
\mathbbm{R}^{d(d+1)/2}
endowed with the Jordan product
{\bm{x}}\circ{\bm{y}}={1\over 2}{\rm vec}({\bm{X}}{\bm{Y}}+{\bm{Y}}{\bm{X}})
, where
{\bm{X}}={\rm mat}({\bm{x}})
and
{\bm{Y}}={\rm mat}({\bm{y}})
.
The rank of the EJA is
d
and its identity element
{\bm{e}}={\rm vec}(I)
, where
I
is the identity matrix.
For an element
{\bm{x}}
in the EJA, its spectral decomposition is
{\bm{x}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}{\rm vec}({\bm{v}}_{i}{\bm{v}}_{i}^{T})
, where
\lambda_{i}
and
{\bm{v}}_{i}
are from the eigendecomposition of
{\rm mat}({\bm{x}})
. The trace of
{\bm{x}}
is simply
\mathbf{Tr}({\rm mat}({\bm{x}}))
.

•

Product cones.
The Cartesian products of symmetric cones are also symmetric cones. We denote the Cartesian product of
n
symmetric cones
\mathcal{K}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{K}_{n}
as
\mathcal{K}=\cproduct_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{K}_{i}
. (We will also use
\cproduct
to denote vector concatenations.) The rank/trace of a product cone is defined as the sum of the ranks/traces of its components. The spectral decomposition of the product cone follows from the decomposition of each component.

The spectral decomposition allows us to define the
Löwner extension
of
the exponentiation
function
\exp:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, which gives a function
that maps elements of the EJA with spectrum in
\mathbb{R}
to elements of the EJA as follows:

\mathbf{exp}:\quad\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}{\bm{q}}_{i}\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{r}\exp(\lambda_{i}){\bm{q}}_{i}\ \in\mathcal{J}.

Let
(\mathcal{J},\circ)
be an EJA and
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{J}
, the generalized Golden-Thompson inequality holds
[
42
]
:

\mathbf{Tr}(\mathbf{exp}({\bm{x}}+{\bm{y}}))\leq\mathbf{Tr}(\mathbf{exp}({\bm{x}})\circ\mathbf{exp}({\bm{y}})).

(1)

Moreover, for any element
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{J}
, let
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}{\bm{q}}_{i}
be its spectral decomposition. We define the infinity norm of
{\bm{x}}
as the maximum magnitude of its eigenvalues, namely
\|{\bm{x}}\|_{\infty}\triangleq\max_{i\in[r]}|\lambda_{i}|
.

Additional details on symmetric cones and EJAs can be found in
[
19
]
,
[
46
]
, and
[

]
.

Symmetric cone programming

A Symmetric Cone Program (SCP) corresponds to linear optimization over the intersection of a symmetric cone with an affine subspace. A pair of primal/dual SCPs over a symmetric cone
\mathcal{K}
(in standard form) is given by:

\begin{aligned} \max\quad&{\bm{c}}\bullet{\bm{x}}\\
\subto\quad&{\bm{a}}_{j}\bullet{\bm{x}}=b_{j}\quad\forall j\in[m]\\
&{\bm{x}}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}}0\end{aligned}\hskip 40.00006pt\begin{aligned} \min\quad&{\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}\\
\subto\quad&\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}}{\bm{c}}\end{aligned}

(2)

As a consequence of the classification of EJAs, symmetric cones provide a unified framework to study Linear Programs (LPs), Semidefinite Programs (SDPs), and Second-Order Cone Programs (SOCPs). Specifically, when
\mathcal{K}=\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
, SCPs correspond to LPs; when
\mathcal{K}
is a Cartesian product of second-order cones, they correspond to SOCPs; and when
\mathcal{K}
is the cone of Hermitian PSD matrices
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{+}
, they correspond to SDPs.

Symmetric cone multiplicative weights update (SCMWU)

SCMWU is a recently introduced algorithm for online linear optimization (OLO) over the trace-one slice of a symmetric cone
[
13
]
. It extends the seminal MWU method for OLO over the simplex
[
5
]
and the MMWU method for OLO over the set of density matrices
[
27
,
44
]
.

We now describe the online linear optimization framework where the SCMWU finds its application.
At each decision epoch
t
, a decision maker needs to choose a trace-one element
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}
within a symmetric cone
\mathcal{K}
. Once this choice is made, a linear loss function

{\ell}^{(t)}({\bm{p}})={{\bm{m}}}^{(t)}\bullet{\bm{p}}
, where
{{\bm{m}}}^{(t)}
is in the underlying EJA space of
\mathcal{K}
, is revealed.
Employing SCMWU, the next iterate is determined by

{{\bm{p}}}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\mathbf{exp}(-\eta\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}{{\bm{m}}}^{(\tau)})}{\mathbf{Tr}(\mathbf{exp}(-\eta\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}{{\bm{m}}}^{(\tau)}))},

(
SCMWU)

and
{{\bm{p}}}^{(1)}
is initialized as
\frac{{\bm{e}}}{r}
, where
r
represents the rank of the underlying EJA.

In the asymptotic limit
t\to+\infty
, the cumulative losses incurred by SCMWU become comparable to the losses of the best fixed action that the decision maker can make
in
hindsight.
This is commonly referred to as the “no-regret” property.
Here, we provide the regret bound for SCMWU presented in
[
13
]
for the sake of comprehensiveness.

.

be an EJA of rank

via the following steps:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Tr}({{\bm{w}}}^{(T+1)})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Tr}(\mathbf{exp}(-\eta\sum_{t=1}^{T}{{\bm{m}}}^{(t)}))

(3)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\mathbf{Tr}({{\bm{w}}}^{(T)}\circ\mathbf{exp}(-\eta{{\bm{m}}}^{(T)}))

\displaystyle={{\bm{w}}}^{(T)}\bullet\mathbf{exp}(-\eta{{\bm{m}}}^{(T)})

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}{{\bm{w}}}^{(T)}\bullet({\bm{e}}-\eta{{\bm{m}}}^{(T)}+\eta^{2}({{\bm{m}}}^{(T)})^{2})

Parallel computation

The
work-span
(or work-depth) model is a framework for conceptualizing and describing parallel algorithms.
In this model, a computation can be viewed as a directed acyclic graph (DAG). The work
W
of a parallel algorithm is the total number of operations, and the span
S
is the length of the longest path in the DAG.
Given
P
processors with shared memory, the computation can be executed in
W/P+O(S)
time with high probability
[
3
,
12
]
. Since
P
is often asymptotically much smaller than
W
, the running time is often dominated by the total work of the algorithm. On the other hand, the span measures the ability of a parallel algorithm to effectively utilize more processors to improve the performance
[
11
]
.

In this work, we will use the work-span model to analyze our algorithms under parallel settings.
A primitive problem that frequently arises as a subproblem when designing our parallel algorithms is the information aggregation problem. Given an array
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
of length
n
and an associative operator
\diamond
, we want to compute
x_{1}\diamond\dots\diamond x_{n}
. Let
\mathcal{I}
be the set of odd indices in the array. For each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, we compute
y_{\lceil i/2\rceil}=x_{i}\diamond x_{i+1}
(if
n
is odd, we let
y_{\lceil n/2\rceil}=x_{n}
). Then we get a new array
y
with length
\lceil n/2\rceil
and the problem becomes computing
y_{1}\diamond\dots\diamond y_{\lceil n/2\rceil}
. The problem can be solved recursively using the same approach until the length of the array is reduced to 1. The associated computational DAG of this process form a binary tree, where the total number of operations is
O(n)
and the longest path in the DAG (the height of the binary tree) is
O(\log n)
.
Therefore, under the setting of the work-span model, the information aggregation problem can be solved in
O(n)
work and
O(\log n)
span.

The information aggregation problem covers many basic operations such as summation and extremum, which are building blocks for our algorithms. Here we elucidate the process of parallelizing the SCMWU method within the work-span model, given its frequent status as the most computationally intensive component in our algorithms. Let
\mathcal{K}
be the Cartesian product of several primitive symmetric cones and let
\mathcal{J}
be the underlying EJA. The computation of the exponential of an element
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{J}
can be decomposed into problems of computing the exponentials of each of its components. Let
N
be the number of non-zero entries in
\mathbf{exp}({\bm{x}})
and let
r
be the rank of
\mathcal{J}
. Given the eigenvalues (or the trace) of each component of
\mathbf{exp}({\bm{x}})
, the computation of
\mathbf{Tr}(\mathbf{exp}({\bm{x}}))
is simply a summation problem that is solvable in
O(r)
work and
O(\log r)
span, and consequently the normalization of
\mathbf{exp}({\bm{x}})
can be performed in
O(N)
work and
O(1)
span.
By the classification theorem of symmetric cones, any symmetric cone over the real field is isomorphic to the Cartesian product of PSD cones and second-order cones. Thus to analyze the computation of
\mathbf{exp}({\bm{x}})
, it only remains analyzing the exponentials of elements in the spaces of PSD cones and second-order cones.
Let
\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}
be a second-order cone and let
({\bm{u}};u_{0})
be an element in the underlying space
\mathbbm{R}^{d+1}
. The spectral decomposition of
({\bm{u}};u_{0})
is given by
\lambda_{1,2}={1\over\sqrt{2}}(u_{0}\pm\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2})
and
{\bm{q}}_{1,2}={1\over\sqrt{2}}(\pm{\bm{v}};1)
, where
{\bm{v}}=\frac{{\bm{u}}}{\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}}
or any unit vector if
{\bm{u}}=0
. If
{\bm{u}}\neq 0
, To compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, the most intensive part is to compute
\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}
, which is again a summation problem with some simple additional arithmetic and can be computed in
O(d)
work and
O(\log d)
span. Once the spectral decomposition is obtained, the exponential can be directly computed in
O(d)
work and
O(1)
span.
In summary, an SCMWU step for
\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}
can be performed with
O(d)
work and
O(\log d)
span.
In
[
6
, Sec. 7]
, Arora and Kale proposed an approximation algorithm for computing the exponential of matrices that lie in the space of the PSD cone. Their algorithm utilizes the power-series expansion of the exponential and the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma and only involves matrix multiplications, which can be reduced to a series of inner products and summations and can be directly parallelized within the work-span model. Given a
d\times d
matrix, Arora and Kale’s algorithm can be parallelized in
{\sf poly}(d)
work and
{\sf polylog}(d)
span.

Primal-dual meta algorithm for SCPs

In this section, and throughout this work, we consider a pair of primal/dual SCPs of the form

\begin{aligned} \max\quad&{\bm{c}}\bullet{\bm{x}}+{\bm{c}}^{\prime}\bullet{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\\
\subto\quad&{\bm{a}}_{j}\bullet{\bm{x}}+{\bm{a}}_{j}^{\prime}\bullet{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=b_{j}\quad\forall j\in[m]\\
&{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{K},\ {\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\end{aligned}\hskip 30.00005pt\begin{aligned} \min\quad&{\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}\\
\subto\quad&\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}}{\bm{c}}\\
&\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{j}y_{j}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}{\bm{c}}^{\prime}\end{aligned}

(SCP)

where
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
are symmetric cones,
{\bm{c}},{\bm{a}}_{j}\in\mathcal{J}
,
{\bm{c}}^{\prime},{\bm{a}}_{j}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
, and
\mathcal{J},\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
are the EJAs
associated with
the cones
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
respectively. We assume throughout that (
SCP
) exhibit strong duality (e.g. see
[
31
]
), and use
{\sf OPT}
to denote the shared optimal value of the primal and dual problem.

Notice that the dual feasible region is defined by two generalized inequality constraints,

\mathcal{F}\ {\triangleq}\ \{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{a}_{j}y_{j}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}}\mathbf{c}\}\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{C}\ {\triangleq}\ \{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{j}y_{j}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\},

(6)

where
\mathcal{F}
is a constraint set that is “hard to deal with” while
\mathcal{C}
is a constraint set that is “easy to satisfy”. In our algorithm, the hard constraint set
\mathcal{F}
is relaxed to a halfspace while for the easy part we will need to to perform linear optimization over the intersection of the set
\mathcal{C}
and a given halfspace.

As a concrete example, consider the case where
\mathcal{K}=\mathbbm{R}^{n}_{+}
,
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}=\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
,
{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the
j
-th unit vector, and
{\bm{c}}^{\prime}={\bm{0}}
. Thehe pair of primal/dual SCPs is then just a pair of primal/dual LPs:

\max\{{\bm{c}}^{T}{\bm{x}}:{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}\leq{\bm{b}},\ {\bm{x}}\geq 0\}\text{ and }\min\{{\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}:{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{y}}-{\bm{c}}\geq 0,\ {\bm{y}}\geq 0\}.

Here, the dual feasible region is the intersection of a polyhedron
\mathcal{F}=\{{\bm{y}}:{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{y}}-{\bm{c}}\geq 0\}
corresponding to the hard set and the non-negative orthant
\mathcal{C}
, which corresponds to the easy set. Note that optimizing a linear function over the intersection of the non-negative orthant and a halfspace can be accomplished easily.

As a second example, we consider the case where
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
,
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}=\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
,
{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the
j
-th unit vector, and
{\bm{c}}^{\prime}={\bm{0}}
. In this setting, the pair of primal/dual SCPs defined above correspond to a pair of primal/dual SDPs:

\max\{{\bm{c}}\bullet{\bm{x}}:{\bm{a}}_{j}\bullet{\bm{x}}\leq b_{j}\ \forall j\in[m],\ {\bm{x}}\succeq 0\}\text{ and }\min\{{\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}:\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}\succeq{\bm{c}},\ {\bm{y}}\geq 0\}.

In this setting the hard part of the dual feasible region is carved out by a linear matrix inequality, i.e.
\mathcal{F}=\{{\bm{y}}:\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}\succeq{\bm{c}}\}
, while the easy part is again the non-negative orthant.
This is exactly the setting that Arora and Kale consider in their primal-dual framework for SDPs
[
6
]
.

Finally, our analysis necessitates that the SCPs adhere to the following assumption:

\exists j\in[m]\text{ such that }{\bm{a}}_{j}={\bm{e}}\text{ and }{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{j}\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}.

This property allows us to “project” an approximately feasible dual solution to a strict one.
For simplicity, we assume that this assumption is true for the last primal constraint, i.e.

{\bm{a}}_{m}={\bm{e}}\ \text{ and }\ {\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{m}\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}.

(7)

As a canonical example, this assumption holds when we have a bound
R
on the trace of primal solutions. Indeed, this case is captured by setting
{\bm{a}}_{m}={\bm{e}}
,
{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{m}\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
, and
b_{m}=R
.

In order to solve the
SCP
problem we reduce it to a feasibility problem, i.e. we
consider the following problem: can
the dual problem in (
SCP
) achieve a specified objective value
\alpha
?
More specifically, does
(1+\varepsilon)\alpha\geq{\sf OPT}
or
\alpha<{\sf OPT}
?

In addition to the answer, for the former case we also want to find a dual feasible solution to (
SCP
) with objective value at most
(1+\varepsilon)\alpha
, and for the latter case a primal feasible solution with objective value at least
\alpha
.
In the following, the decision problem, together with the problem of finding the primal/dual solutions, is referred to as the
\alpha
-feasibility test problem
,
\alpha
-FTP.
Given an algorithm that solves
\alpha
-FTP, the
SCP
problem can then be solved by employing a binary search (or other searching methods) to narrow the range of
\alpha
.
Therefore, in this section, we discuss mainly the algorithm for solving
\alpha
-FTP.

Define
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}=\{{\bm{y}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}:{\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}\leq\alpha\}
to be the
\alpha
-sublevel set of the dual objective function. Recalling the definitions of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{F}
(see (
6
)) we have that

\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\neq\varnothing\iff{\sf OPT}\leq\alpha.

Indeed, if
\alpha\geq{\sf OPT}
, the dual optimal solution lies in
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
, so it is nonempty.
Conversely, if
\alpha<{\sf OPT}
, all feasible solutions have value
>\alpha
, so we have
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}=\varnothing
.
Therefore,
\alpha
-FTP is equivalent to determine whether the intersection between
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is empty or not.
Since
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
are convex sets,
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}=\varnothing
is equivalent to saying that the hard constraint set
\mathcal{F}
can be separated from
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
, which can be certified by a separating hyperplane. Our meta-algorithm is designed to discover a separating hyperplane, thereby certifying that
\alpha<{\sf OPT}
. To this end, we relax the hard constraint set
\mathcal{F}
to a halfspace
\mathcal{H}_{+}
and test whether the intersection
\mathcal{H}_{+}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is empty. If this intersection is empty, then
\mathcal{H}
serves as a certificate that
\alpha<{\sf OPT}
. As long as the intersection is non-empty, we adjst the halfspace to ensure it contains the hard constraint set
\mathcal{F}
while also endeavoring to separate it from the average of all previous iterates. If we succeed to do this for enough times, we will find an approximate feasible solution.
The high-level outline of the algorithm is presented in the boxed environment below.

Primal-dual meta-algorithm for
\alpha
-FTP

Let
{\mathcal{H}}^{(1)}
be a hyperplane such that
\mathcal{F}
is contained in the closed positive halfspace,
{\mathcal{H}}^{(1)}_{+}
.

For each iteration
t
, we test whether the intersection of
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}
with
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is empty:

•

If yes, conclude that
\alpha<{\sf OPT}
, find a primal feasible solution to (
SCP
) with objective value at least
\alpha
, and terminate.

•

If no, find a point
{{\bm{y}}}^{(t)}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
. Let
{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}{{\bm{y}}}^{(\tau)}
, which is the mean of the past iterates.
Adjust
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
to get a new hyperplane
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t+1)}
(maintaining
\mathcal{F}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}^{(t+1)}_{+}
), with the goal of separating
{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}^{(t)}
from
\mathcal{F}
.

If the algorithm does not terminate within a sufficiently large number of iterations, output a dual feasible solution to (
SCP
) with objective value at most
(1+\varepsilon)\alpha
.

\alpha_{1}>{\sf OPT}>\alpha_{2}
.

Figure
1
illustrates three iterations (in two settings)
of our meta-algorithm in
both non-separable and separable cases.
In
the former case (the top row), where
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{1}}\not=\varnothing

and
\alpha_{1}>{\sf OPT}
, our algorithm would not find a separating hyperplane, and would thus
continue till
t
reaches some large number and
then output a dual feasible solution.
In the latter case, indicated in the bottom row, the algorithm identifies a separating hyperplane, concluding that
\alpha_{2}<{\sf OPT}
.

We designate this approach as a meta-algorithm because it contains several degrees of freedom that must be specified to develop a concrete algorithm for the
\alpha
-feasibility test problem. We will now discuss how each of the steps within the meta-algorithmic framework can be implemented.

Parameterizing the hyperplanes
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
.

For each iteration
t
, we need to find a halfspace
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}
satisfying
\mathcal{F}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}
.
Recall that
\mathcal{F}
is defined by the generalized inequality
\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}-{\bm{c}}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}}{\bm{0}}
. A generalized inequality is equivalent to an infinite number of halfspace constraints, i.e.

\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}-{\bm{c}}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}}{\bm{0}}\iff(\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}-{\bm{c}})\bullet{\bm{p}}\geq 0,\ \forall{\bm{p}}\in\mathcal{K}\text{ with }\mathbf{Tr}({\bm{p}})=1,

by the self-duality and homogeneous properties of symmetric cones.
Thus, it is a natural choice to consider hyperplanes
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
that are parametrized by trace-one vectors
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{K}
. That is, we define

{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}=\{{\bm{y}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}:(\textstyle\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}-{\bm{c}})\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}=0\},

and
\mathcal{F}
indeed lies in the closed positive halfspace
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}=\{{\bm{y}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}:(\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}-{\bm{c}})\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}\geq 0\}
.

Testing of separation.
Given a halfspace
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}
satisfying
\mathcal{F}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}
, we try to determine whether the intersection of
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}
and
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is empty.
If yes, then
\mathcal{F}
, which is contained in
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}
, also has no intersection with
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
and we can conclude that
\alpha<{\sf OPT}
.
On the other hand, if
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\neq\varnothing
, we also want to output a point
{{\bm{y}}}^{(t)}
inside the region as a witness to this fact.
As this decision problem is the core of the meta-algorithm, we abstract it as an
ORACLE
:

Given
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{K}
with
\mathbf{Tr}({{\bm{p}}}^{(t)})=1
, determine if there exists
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}
such that

(\textstyle\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}-{\bm{c}})\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}\geq 0,\ {\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}\leq\alpha\text{ and }{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{C}.

The
ORACLE
can be implemented in a variety of ways. One possible implementation of the
ORACLE
is to solve the following convex problem (with
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}
as the input at iteration
t
):

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\sum_{j}({\bm{a}}_{j}\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)})y_{j}-{\bm{c}}\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}

(8)

\displaystyle\subto

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}\leq\alpha,\quad{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{C}.

If the optimal value is negative, this means we have
(\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{y}_{j}-{\bm{c}})\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}<0
for all the points
{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
, in which case the entire region of
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is in the open negative halfspace
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{-}
and separated from
\mathcal{F}
in
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}
.
Thus, the
ORACLE
can output affirmatively that

{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is empty.
On the other hand, if the
optimal value of (
8
) is non-negative and
{{\bm{y}}}^{(t)}
is an optimal solution, we clearly have that
{{\bm{y}}}^{(t)}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
.

The complexity of each iteration of our algorithm depends on the complexity of implementing the
ORACLE
, which in turn depends on the “complexity” of the set
\mathcal{C}
.
Additionally, let
\mathcal{D}=\{{\bm{p}}\in\mathcal{K}:\mathbf{Tr}({\bm{p}})=1\}
be the set of all possible inputs to the
ORACLE
. We assume that for any input
{\bm{p}}\in\mathcal{D}
, the output
{\bm{y}}
of the
ORACLE
satisfies
\|\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}-{\bm{c}}\|_{\infty}\leq\rho
. The quantity
\rho
is designated as the
width
of the
ORACLE
. The number of iterations of our algorithm will depend on the width of the
ORACLE
as will be revealed in what follows.
These two considerations on the complexity of implementing the
ORACLE
and its width offer insights into the properties that the constraint set
\mathcal{C}
should possess to be considered easy.

Finding a primal solution.

Consider the pair of primal/dual optimization problems:

\begin{aligned} \min_{\bm{y}}\quad&{\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}\\
\subto\quad&\textstyle\sum_{j}({\bm{a}}_{j}\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)})y_{j}\geq{\bm{c}}\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}\\
&{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{C}\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} \max_{z,{\bm{x}}^{\prime}}\quad&({\bm{c}}\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)})z+{\bm{c}}^{\prime}\bullet{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\\
\subto\quad&({\bm{a}}_{j}\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)})z+{\bm{a}}_{j}^{\prime}\bullet{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=b_{j}\quad\forall j\in[m]\\
&z\geq 0,\ {\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\end{aligned}

(9)

denoted
P
and
D
, respectively.
Clearly, if the optimal value of (
8
) is negative (i.e. when the
ORACLE
outputs affirmatively),
the value of
P

is greater than
\alpha
. Then, by strong duality, the value of
D
is also greater than
\alpha
. Let
(\tilde{z};\tilde{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime})
be the optimal solution of
D
.
Since
\tilde{z}\geq 0
and
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{K}
,

(\tilde{z}{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)};\tilde{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime})
is
a primal feasible solution to (
SCP
) with value at least
\alpha
.

Adjusting the hyperplane
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
.

Consider the case when
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}
intersects
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
and the
ORACLE
returns
{{\bm{y}}}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
. Then, we need to adjust
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
to a new hyperplane
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t+1)}
. This is done by using the symmetric cone multiplicative weights update (SCMWU) method introduced in Section
2.3
to update the vector
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{K}
that parameterizes
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
.
Specifically, we define the loss vectors
{{\bm{m}}}^{(\tau)}=\frac{1}{\rho}(\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{y}^{(\tau)}_{j}-{\bm{c}})
for
\tau=1,\cdots,t
, where
{{\bm{y}}}^{(\tau)}
is the output of the
ORACLE
at the
\tau
-th iteration and
\rho
is the width of the
ORACLE
. The next vector
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t+1)}
is then obtained from (
SCMWU
). Note that since
\sum_{\tau}^{t}{{\bm{m}}}^{(\tau)}
equals
\frac{t}{\rho}(\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{\bar{y}}^{(t)}_{j}-{\bm{c}})
, where
{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}^{(t)}
is the mean of the past points, the next vector
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t+1)}
can be computed by:

{{\bm{p}}}^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{C}\cdot\mathbf{exp}\Big({-\eta t\over\rho}(\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{\bar{y}}^{(t)}_{j}-{\bm{c}})\Big),

(10)

where
C
normalizes
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t+1)}
to have trace one.

We have two notes to make here. First, for any
{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have
(\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{\bm{y}}-{\bm{c}})\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t+1)}\geq 0
and thus
\mathcal{F}
is still in
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t+1)}_{+}
. Second, here is the intuition on
how the new hyperplane
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t+1)}
endeavors to separate
{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}^{(t)}
and
\mathcal{F}
:
since the vectors

\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{\bar{y}}^{(t)}_{j}-{\bm{c}}
and

{{\bm{p}}}^{(t+1)}

commute,
let
\lambda_{k}
be the eigenvalues of
\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{\bar{y}}^{(t)}_{j}-{\bm{c}}
, we have:

(\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{\bar{y}}^{(t)}_{j}-{\bm{c}})\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\exp({-\eta t\lambda_{k}\over\rho}).

Hence, on the right-hand side above, significantly negative eigenvalues are given large positive weights
in an effort
to make the above summation negative. From a geometric perspective, the adjustment endeavors to place
{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}^{(t)}
in the open negative halfspace
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t+1)}_{-}\ \triangleq\ \{{\bm{y}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}:(\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}-{\bm{c}})\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t+1)}<0\}
so that it is separated from
\mathcal{F}
in
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t+1)}_{+}
.

Having outlined all the steps in the meta-algorithm, we are now ready to present our central theorem, specifying the required number of steps to reach a conclusion regarding whether
OPT
surpasses or falls short of our current estimate
\alpha
.

Theorem 2
.

Consider a pair of primal/dual
SCP
s over the symmetric cone
\mathcal{K}\cproduct\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
, where the rank of
\mathcal{K}
is
r
, and assumption (
7
) holds.
Fix an error parameter
\varepsilon>0
and a guess
\alpha>0
for the optimal value
OPT
.
Execute the primal-dual meta-algorithm for
T=\left\lceil\frac{4R^{2}\rho^{2}\ln r}{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}}\right\rceil
iterations, adjusting the hyperplane using SCMWU (as defined in (
10
)) with step size
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{\ln r}{T}}
and an
ORACLE
with width
\rho.
Then, we either correctly conclude that
{\sf OPT}>\alpha
, or, if
the algorithm does not terminate within
T
iterations we conclude that
{\sf OPT}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\alpha
and the vector

\tilde{{\bm{y}}}=\left({\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{1},\ldots,{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{m-1},{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{m}+\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{R}\right)

is a dual feasible solution to
SCP
with objective value
{\bm{b}}^{T}\tilde{{\bm{y}}}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\alpha
.

Proof.

Suppose that the algorithm does not terminate after
t=1,\ldots,T
iterations. By our choice of
{{\bm{y}}}^{(t)}
and
{{\bm{m}}}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{\rho}(\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{y}^{(t)}_{j}-{\bm{c}})
, the loss
{{\bm{m}}}^{(t)}\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}
for SCMWU is always non-negative. By the regret bound of SCMWU (Theorem
1
), we have:

0\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{\rho}(\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{y}^{(t)}_{j}-{\bm{c}})\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}\leq\lambda_{\min}({\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{\rho}(\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{y}^{(t)}_{j}-{\bm{c}})})+\eta T+\frac{\ln r}{\eta}.

Then, scaling both sides of the inequality by
\frac{\rho}{T}
, we have

0\leq\lambda_{\min}({\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{j}-{\bm{c}}})+\eta\rho+\frac{\rho\ln r}{\eta T},

(11)

and substituting
T=\left\lceil\frac{4R^{2}\rho^{2}\ln r}{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}}\right\rceil
and
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{\ln r}{T}}
into the above gives us

\lambda_{\min}({\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{j}-{\bm{c}}})+\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{R}\geq 0,

or equivalently

\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{j}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}}{\bm{c}}-\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{R}{\bm{e}}.

(12)

We now show
that the vector
\tilde{{\bm{y}}}=({\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{1},\ldots,{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{m-1},{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{m}+\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{R})
is dual feasible. First, to show that
\tilde{{\bm{y}}}\in\mathcal{F}
, we use (
12
)
and recall that by assumption (
7
) we have that
{\bm{a}}_{m}={\bm{e}}
. Then, (
12
) becomes:

\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}{\bm{a}}_{j}{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{j}+({\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{m}+\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{R}){\bm{e}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{a}}_{j}\tilde{y}_{j}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}}{\bm{c}}.

Next, we show that
\tilde{{\bm{y}}}\in\mathcal{C}
. For this, by definition of the
ORACLE
we have that
{{{\bm{y}}}}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{C}
, for all
t=1,\ldots,T
, and by averaging we get that
{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}^{(T)}\in\mathcal{C}
, i.e.

\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}^{\prime}{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{j}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}{\bm{c}}^{\prime}.

(13)

Then, by assumption (
7
) we have that
{\bm{a}}_{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
, and thus (
13
) implies that

\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{a}}_{j}^{\prime}\tilde{y}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}{\bm{a}}_{j}^{\prime}{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{j}+{\bm{a}}_{m}^{\prime}({\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{m}+{\epsilon\alpha\over R})\succeq_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}{\bm{a}}_{j}^{\prime}{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{j}+{\bm{a}}_{m}^{\prime}{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{m}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}{\bm{c}}^{\prime}.

Finally, by assumption (
7
) we have that
b_{m}=R
. Thus, the objective value of
\tilde{{\bm{y}}}
satisfies:

{\bm{b}}^{T}\tilde{{\bm{y}}}={\bm{b}}^{T}{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}^{(T)}+R\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{R}\leq\alpha+\varepsilon\alpha=(1+\varepsilon)\alpha,

which completes the proof.
∎

Remarks on
\mathcal{C}
.

The
iteration complexity
is quadratic in the width
\rho
of the
ORACLE
used. Thus, it is desirable to design an
ORACLE
with a small width. As
\mathcal{C}
is a constraint of the convex problem solved by the
ORACLE
,
\rho
in some way depends on the choice of
\mathcal{C}
. Therefore, to reduce
\rho
, we may shrink the space of
\mathcal{C}
by adding extra constraints to the problem. These constraints
should
be redundant to the original problem (
SCP
), but
strong enough
to restrict
the result of (
8
)
so that

ORACLE

has a
smaller
\rho
, as we will see in an example in Section
4.2
. Moreover, if it is not required to find a primal solution (by solving (
9
)), the choice of
\mathcal{C}
can be more aggressive:
we can replace
the set
\mathcal{C}
by any
convex set
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
that contains the dual optimal solution
{\bm{y}}^{*}
,
since whenever the
ORACLE
confirms that
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is empty we will have the correct outcome that

{\sf OPT}>\alpha
(since
y^{*}\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
) and

\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}=\varnothing
.

From feasibility to optimization.

Assume an upper/lower bound for the value of
{\sf OPT}
. (Otherwise, we can employ an exponential search or a constant-approximation algorithm to obtain one.) With our meta-algorithm, a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution to (
SCP
) can then be computed using a binary search. However, using the desired accuracy
\varepsilon
as the error tolerance in each binary search step results in an iteration complexity of
O(T\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
. Instead of doing that, we can choose the error tolerance
\varepsilon_{i}
adaptively for each search step by setting
\varepsilon_{i}\alpha_{i}
to be within a constant fraction of the size of its searching range. The total number of iterations is then dominated by the last step (with the smallest
\varepsilon_{i}\alpha_{i}=\Theta(\varepsilon{\sf OPT})
), and is improved to
O(T)
.
Assume we know that
{\sf OPT}>0
and lies in the range
[L_{0},U_{0}]
.
In the first step, we run our meta-algorithm, with
\alpha_{1}=L_{0}+{1\over 3}(U_{0}-L_{0})
and
\varepsilon_{1}={1\over 3\alpha_{1}}(U_{0}-L_{0})
. According to Theorem
2
, we either correctly conclude that this problem is infeasible, in which case
{\sf OPT}>\alpha_{1}
and the search range becomes:

L_{0}+{1\over 3}(U_{0}-L_{0})\leq{\sf OPT}\leq U_{0},

or we conclude that
{\sf OPT}\leq(1+\varepsilon_{1})\alpha_{1}=L_{0}+{2\over 3}(U_{0}-L_{0})
(and find a solution with this value), and the search range becomes:

L_{0}\leq{\sf OPT}\leq L_{0}+{2\over 3}(U_{0}-L_{0}).

Setting
L_{1}=L_{0}+{1\over 3}(U_{0}-L_{0}),\ U_{1}=U_{0}
if the first case materializes and
L_{1}=L_{0},\ U_{1}=L_{0}+{2\over 3}(U_{0}-L_{0})
if the second case materializes. Both cases can be summarized as follows:

L_{1}\leq{\sf OPT}\leq U_{1},\ \text{ where }(U_{1}-L_{1})={2\over 3}(U_{0}-L_{0}).

Adopting this iterative refinement approach, at the
i
-th step we set
\alpha_{i}=L_{i-1}+{1\over 3}(U_{i-1}-L_{i-1})
and
\varepsilon_{i}={1\over 3\alpha_{i}}(U_{i-1}-L_{i-1})
. For the resultant interval
[L_{i},U_{i}]
we have

(U_{i}-L_{i})=\Big({2\over 3}\Big)^{i}(U_{0}-L_{0}).

Thus, in
O\big(\log(\frac{U_{0}-L_{0}}{\varepsilon{\sf OPT}})\big)
iterations the range size
(U_{i}-L_{i})
will be less than the desired (additive) error
\varepsilon{\sf OPT}
. The very last dual solution will have value at most
(1+\varepsilon){\sf OPT}
, and a scaled version of the
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}
that parameterized the last separating hyperplane is a primal solution with value at least
(1-\varepsilon){\sf OPT}
. At each step of the binary search, we invoke Theorem
2
with
\varepsilon_{i}\alpha_{i}={1\over 3}\left({2\over 3}\right)^{i-1}(U_{0}-L_{0})
that decreases geometrically. The total number of iterations needed to solve all
\alpha_{i}
-feasibility test during the search is then upper bounded by the complexity needed for the very last feasibility test, which is
O\big(\frac{R^{2}\rho^{2}\ln r}{\varepsilon^{2}{\sf OPT}^{2}}\big)
.

Extension to minimization problems.

With slight changes, our meta-algorithm also works for minimization problems.
Consider the following variant of (
SCP
) where the primal problem is minimizing and the dual problem is maximizing:

\begin{aligned} \min\quad&{\bm{c}}\bullet{\bm{x}}+{\bm{c}}^{\prime}\bullet{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\\
\subto\quad&{\bm{a}}_{j}\bullet{\bm{x}}+{\bm{a}}_{j}^{\prime}\bullet{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=b_{j}\quad\forall j\in[m]\\
&{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{K},\ {\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\end{aligned}\hskip 30.00005pt\begin{aligned} \max\quad&{\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}\\
\subto\quad&\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}\preceq_{\mathcal{K}}{\bm{c}}\\
&\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{j}y_{j}\preceq_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}{\bm{c}}^{\prime}\end{aligned}

(14)

This time, given a specified objective value
\alpha
, the
\alpha
-feasibility test problem is to determine whether
(1-\varepsilon)\alpha\leq{\sf OPT}
or
\alpha>{\sf OPT}
.
For the former case, we provide a dual solution with objective value at least
(1-\varepsilon)\alpha
, and for the latter case we want a primal solution with value at most
\alpha
.
Define the constraint sets

\mathcal{F}\ {\triangleq}\ \{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{a}_{j}y_{j}\preceq_{\mathcal{K}}\mathbf{c}\}\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{C}\ {\triangleq}\ \{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{j}y_{j}\preceq_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\},

(15)

and the
\alpha
-superlevel set of the dual objective function
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}=\{{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:{\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}\geq\alpha\}
. Similar to the maximization problems, we have

\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\neq\varnothing\iff{\sf OPT}\geq\alpha.

Thus
\alpha
-FTP can be solved by checking the emptiness of the intersection of
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
.
Given a
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{K}
with
\mathbf{Tr}({{\bm{p}}}^{(t)})=1
, we parameterize the hyperplane
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
as

{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}=\{{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:({\bm{c}}-\textstyle\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j})\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}=0\},

so that the region
\mathcal{F}
is kept inside the closed positive halfspace
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}
. To test if the hyperplane
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
separates
\mathcal{F}
from
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
(or equivalently whether
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}=\varnothing
), the
ORACLE
for minimization problems becomes:

Given
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{K}
with
\mathbf{Tr}({{\bm{p}}}^{(t)})=1
, determine if there exists
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}
such that

({\bm{c}}-\textstyle\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j})\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}\geq 0,\ {\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}\geq\alpha\text{ and }{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{C}.

Similarly, the
ORACLE
can be implemented by solving the following convex problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{c}}\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}-\textstyle\sum_{j}({\bm{a}}_{j}\bullet{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)})y_{j}

(16)

\displaystyle\subto

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{b}}^{T}{\bm{y}}\geq\alpha,\quad{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{C}.

If the optimal value of the above problem is non-negative, and
{{\bm{y}}}^{(t)}
is the solution.
Let
\rho
be the width of the above
ORACLE
which is defined as an upperbound on
\|{\bm{c}}-\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}y_{j}\|_{\infty}
.
We define the loss vector
{{\bm{m}}}^{(t)}={1\over\rho}({\bm{c}}-\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{{\bm{y}}}^{(t)})
and update
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}
using SCMWU:

{{\bm{p}}}^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{C}\cdot\mathbf{exp}\Big({-\eta t\over\rho}({\bm{c}}-\sum_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}{\bar{y}}^{(t)}_{j})\Big),

(17)

where
C
normalizes
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t+1)}
to be trace-one.
If the value of problem (
16
) is negative, we know that
\alpha>{\sf OPT}
and a scaled version of
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}
serves as a primal solution of (
14
) with objective value at most
\alpha
.
The running time of the meta-algorithm for solving
\alpha
-FTP for the minimization problem is concluded as the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Consider problem (
14
) over the symmetric cone
\mathcal{K}\cproduct\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
, where the rank of
\mathcal{K}
is
r
, and assumption (
7
) holds.
Fix an error parameter
\varepsilon>0
and a guess
\alpha>0
for the optimal value
OPT
.
Execute the primal-dual meta-algorithm for
T=\left\lceil\frac{4R^{2}\rho^{2}\ln r}{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}}\right\rceil
iterations, adjusting the hyperplane using SCMWU (as defined in (
17
)) with step size
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{\ln r}{T}}
and an
ORACLE
with width
\rho
. Then, we either correctly conclude that
{\sf OPT}<\alpha
, or, if
the algorithm does not terminate within
T
iterations, we conclude that
{\sf OPT}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\alpha
and the vector

\tilde{{\bm{y}}}=\left({\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{1},\ldots,{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{m-1},{\bar{y}}^{(T)}_{m}-\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{R}\right)

is a dual feasible solution of (
14
) with objective value
{\bm{b}}^{T}\tilde{{\bm{y}}}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\alpha
.

Applications

We apply the meta-algorithm to derive novel algorithms
for
two geometry applications: the smallest enclosing sphere problem (Section
4.1
) and the support vector machine problem (a.k.a. polytope distance, Section
4.2
).
By
meta-algorithm
, we refer to the meta-algorithm for
\alpha
-FTP that incorporates the necessary search of a good
\alpha
to solve (
SCP
) or (
14
).
For each application, we also show the analysis of the algorithm in the parallel setting.

Smallest enclosing sphere

In the Smallest Enclosing Sphere (SES) problem, the goal is to find the sphere with the minimum radius that encloses all the spheres in the input set

\mathcal{I}=\{S({\bm{v}}_{1},\gamma_{1}),\dots,S({\bm{v}}_{n},\gamma_{n})\}
, where
S({\bm{v}}_{i},\gamma_{i})
is a sphere centered at
{\bm{v}}_{i}
with radius
\gamma_{i}
.
SES is an important problem arising in many branches of computer science such as computer graphics, machine learning, and operations research. In the past few decades, numerous methods have been proposed for solving the SES problem.
These methods can be grouped under two classes:
exact and approximation methods.
The exact methods (for example,
[
32
,
33
,
18
,
48
,
22
,
24
]
) mainly focus on solving the SES problem (for a set of points) in some fixed (low) dimension where the running time is usually linear in the input size
n
. However, when extending these exact methods to higher dimensions, the dependence on dimension could be sub-exponential or worse. On the other hand,
the approximation methods are considerably faster in general (high) dimensions, and can be readily adapted to diverse input types such as spheres and ellipsoids.
A notable set of approximation methods uses core-sets
[
7
,
29
,
14
,
49
,
36
]
, to compute an
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution using only a subset of the input points of size
O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
or
O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}})
, and the running time is usually linear in the input size, i.e.
O(\frac{nd}{\varepsilon})
or
O(\frac{nd}{\varepsilon^{2}})
, where
d
is the dimensionality of the problem.
Note that
the
SES problem can also be formulated as a SOCP. However, employing the interior point methods on SOCPs often requires solving a linear system in each iteration and results in a super-quadratic or quadratic dependence in the dimensionality: see, for example,
[
29
,
51
]
.

We now formulate the SES problem as an SCP and use our meta-algorithm to develop an algorithm with a nearly linear running time. Clearly, SES can be formulated as the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{u}},\gamma}

\displaystyle\gamma

(SES)

\displaystyle\subto

\displaystyle\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\gamma-\gamma_{i}\quad\forall i\in[n]

Since the constraint
\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\gamma-\gamma_{i}
can be interpreted as a generalized inequality constraint w.r.t.
\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}
,
the problem can be viewed as a SCP where the cone is the Cartesian product of
n
second-order cones,
\cproduct_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}
.
Let
({\bm{u}}^{*},\gamma^{*})
be the optimal solution to the above program, then the SES of
\mathcal{I}
is centered at
{\bm{u}}^{*}
and the optimal radius is
\gamma^{*}
.

In the algorithm, we use the constraint
\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}_{1}\|_{2}\leq\gamma-\gamma_{1}
to define the set
\mathcal{C}
and the remaining constraints to define the set
\mathcal{F}
.
Notice that the vector
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}
that parameterizes the hyperplane
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
lies in the product cone
\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}\mathcal{Q}^{d-1}
.
The running time of our algorithm is stated below.

Theorem 4
.

Applying the meta-algorithm to problem (
SES
) for
n
spheres in a
d
-dimensional space, we can compute an enclosing sphere with radius at most
(1+\varepsilon)\gamma^{*}
in
O\left(\frac{nd\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
time.

Proof.

Noting that

\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\gamma-\gamma_{i}\iff({\bm{u}};\gamma)-({\bm{v}}_{i};\gamma_{i})\in\mathcal{Q}^{d+1},

we can write the (
SES
) problem as an SOCP:

{\min_{{\bm{u}},\gamma}}\left\{\gamma:({\bm{u}};\gamma)\succeq_{\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}}({\bm{v}}_{i};\gamma_{i}),\ i=1,\ldots,n\right\}.

For the (
SES
) problem, distinguishing between easy and hard constraints is very natural. The “easy” portion is derived by keeping only one of the second-order cone constraints (for instance, the first one), while the “hard” part is defined by the intersection of all other constraints, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\{({\bm{u}};\gamma)\in\mathbbm{R}^{d+1}:\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}_{1}\|_{2}\leq\gamma-\gamma_{1}\},

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\{({\bm{u}};\gamma)\in\mathbbm{R}^{d+1}:\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\gamma-\gamma_{i},\ \text{for}\ i=2,\dots,n\}.

Following this splitting, we express the (
SES
) problem as a pair of primal/dual SCPs in the form of (
SCP
). Setting
\mathcal{K}=\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}
and
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}=\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}
, the dual formulation is given by:

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\underbrace{({\bf 0};1)}_{{\bm{b}}}}^{T}\underbrace{({\bm{u}};\gamma)}_{{\bm{y}}}

(18)

\displaystyle\subto

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}\underbrace{\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}({\bm{e}}_{j};0)}_{{\bm{a}}_{j}}\Big)+\gamma\underbrace{\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}({\bf 0};1)}_{{\bm{a}}_{d+1}}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}}\underbrace{\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}({\bm{v}}_{i};\gamma_{i})}_{{\bm{c}}}

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}\underbrace{({\bm{e}}_{j};0)}_{{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{j}}\Big)+\gamma\underbrace{({\bf 0};1)}_{{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{d+1}}\succeq_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}\underbrace{({\bm{v}}_{1};\gamma_{1})}_{{\bm{c}}^{\prime}}.

On the other hand, the primal formulation is given by:

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\bm{v}}_{i};\gamma_{i})\bullet{\bm{x}}_{i}

(19)

\displaystyle\subto

\displaystyle\underbrace{\Big(\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}({\bm{e}}_{j};0)\Big)}_{{\bm{a}}_{j}}\bullet{\bm{x}}+\underbrace{({\bm{e}}_{j};0)}_{{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{j}}\bullet{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=0,\ j=1,\ldots,d

\displaystyle\underbrace{\Big(\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}({\bf 0};1)\Big)}_{{\bm{a}}_{d+1}}\bullet{\bm{x}}+\underbrace{({\bf 0};1)}_{{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{d+1}}\bullet{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=1

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{x}}=({\bm{x}}_{1};\ldots;{\bm{x}}_{n-1})\in\mathcal{K},\ {\bm{x}}^{\prime}={\bm{x}}_{n}\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}

Examining (
19
) and (
18
) we see that assumption (
7
) is implicitly satisfied. Specifically, if we scale the last constraint in (
19
) by
\sqrt{2}
, we get an equivalent problem where the constraint becomes

\underbrace{\Big(\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}({\bf 0};\sqrt{2})\Big)}_{{\bm{a}}_{d+1}}\bullet{\bm{x}}+\underbrace{({\bf 0};\sqrt{2})}_{{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{d+1}}\bullet{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=\sqrt{2},

and we have
m=d+1
,
{\bm{a}}_{d+1}=\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}({\bf 0};\sqrt{2})={\bm{e}}
(the identity in
\mathcal{K}
) and
{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{d+1}=({\bf 0};\sqrt{2})\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
. Moreover, we see
b_{d+1}=R=\sqrt{2}
. Note that we don’t need to perform the scaling explicitly, but the scaled constraint helps in deriving the final result.

Defining
D=\max_{i\geq 2}\|{\bm{v}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{i}\|_{2}+\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{i}
, then a sphere centered at
{\bm{v}}_{1}

with radius
D
will enclose
\mathcal{I}
, so
\gamma^{*}\leq D
. The distance of the farthest pair of points in
\mathcal{I}
is at least
D
, so
\gamma^{*}\geq\frac{D}{2}
. To solve the SES problem, we can search for the optimal radius in the range
[\frac{D}{2},D]
.

Fix an error parameter
\varepsilon>0
and a guess
\alpha>0
for the optimal value
OPT
. Let
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}
be the vector that parameterizes
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}
in the
t
-th iteration: it lies in the product cone
\mathcal{K}=\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}
that corresponds to
\mathcal{F}
. Let
({{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}_{i};{s}^{(t)}_{i})\in\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}
, where
{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}_{i}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
and
{s}^{(t)}_{i}\in\mathbbm{R}
, be the
(i-1)^{th}
component of
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}
.
In other words,
{{\bm{p}}}^{(t)}=\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}({{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}_{i};{s}^{(t)}_{i})
.
The convex program that the
ORACLE
needs to solve to check whether
{\mathcal{H}}^{(t)}_{+}\cap\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}=\varnothing
(recall (
8
))
is given by:

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{u}},\gamma}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{n}({\bm{u}};\gamma)\bullet({{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}_{i};{s}^{(t)}_{i})-\sum_{i=2}^{n}({\bm{v}}_{i};\gamma_{i})\bullet({{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}_{i};{s}^{(t)}_{i})

\displaystyle\subto

\displaystyle\gamma\leq\alpha,\quad\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}_{1}\|_{2}\leq\gamma-\gamma_{1}.

Since
\sum_{i=2}^{n}{s}^{(t)}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbf{Tr}({{\bm{p}}}^{(t)})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
(see Section
2.1
), the problem can be simplified to:

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{u}},\gamma}

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}_{i}\right)^{T}{\bm{u}}+\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{2}}-\sum_{i=2}^{n}{\bm{v}}_{i}^{T}{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}_{i}-\sum_{i=2}^{n}\gamma_{i}{s}^{(t)}_{i}

(20)

\displaystyle\subto

\displaystyle\gamma\leq\alpha,\quad\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}_{1}\|_{2}\leq\gamma-\gamma_{1}

Notice that once
({{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}_{i};{s}^{(t)}_{i})
are fixed, the last two terms in the objective are constants, and increasing the value of
\gamma
only relaxes the constraint for

{\bm{u}}
, resulting in
a larger objective value. Thus, the optimal value of (
20
) will be achieved at
\gamma=\alpha
. Once
\gamma
is fixed, the problem boils down to optimizing a linear function over a ball, which admits a simple closed-form solution.

Next, we show that the width
\rho
of the
ORACLE
is at most
\frac{3D}{\sqrt{2}}
. As explained above, at each iteration the
ORACLE
returns a point
({\bm{u}};\alpha)
that satisfies

\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}_{1}\|_{2}\leq\alpha-\gamma_{1}.

(21)

The width then equals the maximum value of

\Big|\lambda_{k}\big(\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}({{\bm{u}}}-{\bm{v}}_{i};{\alpha}-\gamma_{i})\big)\Big|,

over all
{\bm{u}},k
that satisfy

\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}_{1}\|_{2}\leq\alpha-\gamma_{1}
and
k\in[r]
, which is

\max_{i\geq 2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\alpha-\gamma_{i}+\|{{\bm{u}}}-{\bm{v}}_{i}\|_{2}).

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\max_{i\geq 2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\alpha-\gamma_{i}+\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}_{i}\|_{2})\overset{(a)}{\leq}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\alpha+\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}_{1}\|_{2}+\max_{i\geq 2}\|{\bm{v}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{i}\|_{2})

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\alpha+\alpha-\gamma_{1}+\max_{i\geq 2}\|{\bm{v}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{i}\|_{2})\overset{(c)}{\leq}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(2\alpha+D)\overset{(d)}{\leq}\frac{3D}{\sqrt{2}},

where
(a)
is due to the triangle inequality,
(b)
follows from (
21
),
(c)
follows from the definition of
D
, and
(d)
is because
\alpha\leq D
.

By Theorem
2
we know that if we execute the primal-dual meta-algorithm for
T=\left\lceil\frac{4R^{2}\rho^{2}\ln r}{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}}\right\rceil
iterations, we either correctly conclude infeasibility or we find a solution with value
(1+\varepsilon)\alpha
. For the latter case, a sphere centered at
\bar{{\bm{u}}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}{{\bm{u}}}^{(t)}
with radius
(1+\varepsilon)\alpha
will enclose all spheres in
\mathcal{I}
.
As discussed above, for problem (
SES
), we have
R=\sqrt{2},\rho\leq\frac{3D}{\sqrt{2}}
and
r=2(n-1)
. The number of iterations
T
can be simplified to
\left\lceil\frac{36D^{2}\ln(2n-2)}{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}}\right\rceil
, which is
O\left(\frac{D^{2}\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}}\right)
.

We now analyze the overall running time of the algorithm. For the SES problem, since we obtained a natural lower and upper bound on the value of
\alpha
, where
L_{0}=\frac{D}{2}
and
U_{0}=D
. Therefore, the technique of reducing optimization problems to feasibility problems discussed in Section
3
applies. The total number of iterations required for solving the optimization problem is dominated by the number of iterations required for the last feasibility problem, which is
O(\frac{D^{2}\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}{\sf OPT}^{2}})
and can be further simplified to
O(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}})
because
{\sf OPT}\geq\frac{D}{2}
. The computation in each iteration of the algorithm includes:
(a)
Solving (
20
) for test of separation. This involves computing the coefficients of the objective function and testing the intersection of a halfspace and a hyperball, which requires
O(nd)
time.
(b)
Performing a SCWMU step for adjusting the hyperplane. This involves computing the loss vector and its spectral decomposition and performing the exponentiation and normalization step, which requires
O(nd)
time in total.
Combining the iteration complexity and the running time of each iteration, we conclude that the overall running time of our algorithm is
O(\frac{nd\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}})
.
∎

Theorem 5
.

The algorithm described in Theorem
4
can be parallelized under the setting of the work-span model.
The parallel algorithm requires
O\Big(\frac{nd\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)
work and has
O\Big(\frac{\log(nd)\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)
span.

Proof.

To parallelize the algorithm for the SES problem, we parallelize the computation during each iteration. The computation in each iteration of the algorithm involves computing the analytical solution of (
20
) and updating an element of the product cone
\cproduct_{i=2}^{n}\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}
via SCMWU, which only requires simple arithmetic and information aggregation operations (summations or extremum queries) on vectors, and can be trivially parallelized.
The work of each iteration of the parallel algorithm is the same as the time complexity of the sequential version, while the span (or parallel depth) becomes
O\left(\log(nd)\right)
.
∎

Support vector machine

The Support Vector Machine (SVM) is one of the most successful machine learning tools.
Its
efficacy is rooted in its inherent simplicity, coupled with exceptional generalization properties applicable to both classification and regression tasks.
The vanilla SVM problem
[
15
]
is to find a hyperplane that separates two sets of data points with maximum margin, which is closely related to the geometric problem of finding the closest points in two convex polytopes (a.k.a. polytope distance)
[
9
]
. The soft-margin variants of SVM, such as
C
-SVM
[
15
]
and
\nu
-SVM
[
40
]
, can also be interpreted as polytope distance (PD) problems for reduced polytopes
[
9
,
10
,
17
]
.
The algorithms designed for SVM are usually based on optimization techniques. A milestone in this direction is the proposal of the sequential minimal optimization (SMO) method
[
38
]
, which can be viewed as a block coordinate descent method and is widely used in practice. Another line of research focuses on solving the PD problem using geometric
approaches: remarkable
algorithms in this modality include the Gilbert-Schlesinger-Kozinec (GSK) method
[
21
]
and the Mitchell-Dem’yanov-Malozemov (MDM) method
[
34
]
. In
[
23
]
, the authors also proved that the size of core-set for the SVM/PD problem is
\Theta(\frac{E}{\varepsilon})
, where
E
is the excentricity of the problem
instance, which is a quantity that measures its difficulty.

In this section we study the SVM problem in the separable case. We
formulate the problem
as a pair of primal/dual SCPs and apply the meta-algorithm to develop an algorithm that produces solutions for both problems in nearly linear time.
Given two point sets
\mathcal{P}=\{{\bm{u}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{u}}_{n_{1}}\}
and
\mathcal{Q}=\{{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{n_{2}}\}
in
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
,
we define the matrices
{\bm{P}}=({\bm{u}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{u}}_{n_{1}})
and
{\bm{Q}}=({\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{n_{2}})
,
where
{\bm{u}}_{i}
is the
i
-th column of
{\bm{P}}
and, similarly,
{\bm{v}}_{i}
the
i
-th column of
{\bm{Q}}
. We can write
SVM as the optimization problem (e.g. see
[
41
]
):

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{w}},s_{1},s_{2}}

\displaystyle s_{1}+s_{2}

(SVM)

\displaystyle\subto

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{P}}^{T}{\bm{w}}-s_{1}{\bm{1}}\geq{\bm{0}}

\displaystyle-{\bm{Q}}^{T}{\bm{w}}-s_{2}{\bm{1}}\geq{\bm{0}}

\displaystyle\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}\leq 1

If

is
the optimal solution to the above problem,

the maximum margin of SVM is given by

while

is the normal vector of the separating hyperplane.
The counterpart of (

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\|{\bm{P}}{\bm{\mu}}-{\bm{Q}}{\bm{\gamma}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{1}}^{T}{\bm{\mu}}={\bm{1}}^{T}{\bm{\gamma}}=1

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\gamma}}\geq{\bm{0}}

In the following, we view (

14

The following lemma is useful for us to add additional easy constraints to the problem that help to design the

Let

Let

From the constraint

In the same way, one can prove that

Theorem 9
.

The algorithm described in Theorem
7
can be parallelized under the work-span model.
The parallel algorithm takes
O\Big(\frac{End\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)

O\Big(\frac{E\log(nd)\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)

Proof.

Similar to Theorem
5
, we parallelize the computation during each iteration of the algorithm for the SVM/PD problem.
This involves computing the analytical solution of (

\mathbbm{R}^{n}_{+}
. Under the work-span model, the work of the parallel algorithm is the same as the sequential time complexity, while the span of each iteration becomes

. When the searching range is small and a solution of

and
{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{(t)}
, which can be done in
O(n)
work and

O\Big(\frac{End\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)
and the span is
O\Big(\frac{E\log(nd)\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)
.
∎

For both CPU and GPU versions, we employ the same implementation strategies.
We examine the solution we obtained in each iteration. For the SES problem, letting

t
iterates, we compute

{f}^{(t)}\triangleq\max_{i\in[n]}\|{\bar{{\bm{u}}}}^{(t)}-{\bm{v}}_{i}\|_{2}+\gamma_{i}.

For the SVM problem, letting
{\bar{{\bm{w}}}}^{(t)}
be the first
t
iterates, we compute

{f}^{(t)}\triangleq\max\Big\{\textstyle\frac{1}{\|{\bar{{\bm{w}}}}^{(t)}\|_{2}}\displaystyle\big(\min_{{\bm{v}}\in\mathcal{P}}{\bm{v}}^{T}{\bar{{\bm{w}}}}^{(t)}-\max_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{Q}}{\bm{u}}^{T}{\bar{{\bm{w}}}}^{(t)}\big),\ 0\Big\}.

If the current result is already better than our guess
\alpha
(for SES, when the current radius
{f}^{(t)}
is smaller than
\alpha
; and for SVM, when the current margin
{f}^{(t)}
is larger than
\alpha
), we can terminate the process and get an affirmative conclusion for the
\alpha
-feasibility test problem. Moreover, instead of running the algorithms for a sufficiently large number of iterations as stated in their theoretical analyses, we use an early-stopping technique that is often used in practice, and terminate the process when the result is stabilized. Specifically, in each iteration, we check whether

\frac{|{f}^{(t)}-{f}^{(t-1)}|}{{f}^{(t-1)}}<\delta,

where
\delta
is a small constant (in our experiments,
\delta=10^{-4}
).
1
1

1

Note that when
{f}^{(t-1)}=0
, we don’t compute the ratio and the algorithm continues.

If the criterion has been satisfied consecutively (in our experiments, 10 consecutive iterations), the algorithm will be terminated.

Concluding remark

In this work, we introduce a novel primal-dual framework for symmetric cone programming (SCP)
that utilizes
a recent extension of the multiplicative weights update method to symmetric cones.
We applied this framework to devise parallel algorithms for smallest enclosing sphere and support vector machine (polytope distance).
Our implementation of these two algorithms, tested in both sequential and parallel settings, demonstrated excellent efficiency and scalability with large-scale inputs.
Looking forward, the versatility of SCP allows our framework to have broad applications in both theoretical and practical aspects.
Besides the width-dependent method proposed in this paper, width-independent algorithms for special classes of SCPs (such as positive SCPs) would also be an interesting topic to study.
