The Biot-Stokes coupling using total pressure: formulation, analysis and application to interfacial flow in the eye Journal: Computer Methods in Applied Mechanics and Engineering
Ricardo Ruiz-Baier, Matteo Taffetani, Hans D. Westermeyer, Ivan Yotov

Abstract
We consider a multiphysics model for the flow of Newtonian fluid coupled with Biot consolidation equations through an interface, and incorporating total pressure as an unknown in the poroelastic region. A new mixed-primal finite element scheme is proposed solving for the pairs fluid velocity - pressure and displacement - total poroelastic pressure using Stokes-stable elements, and where the formulation does not require Lagrange multipliers to set up the usual transmission conditions on the interface. The stability and well-posedness of the continuous and semi-discrete problems are analysed in detail. Our numerical study is framed in the context of applicative problems pertaining to heterogeneous geophysical flows and to eye poromechanics. For the latter, we investigate different interfacial flow regimes in Cartesian and axisymmetric coordinates that could eventually help describe early morphologic changes associated with glaucoma development in canine species.

Introduction

Poroelastic structures are found in many applications of industrial and scientific relevance. Examples include the interaction between soft permeable tissue and blood flow, or the study of the spatial growth of biofilm in fluids. When the interaction with a free fluid is considered, the mechanics of the fluid and poroelastic domains are coupled through balance of forces and continuity conditions that adopt diverse forms depending on the expected behaviour in the specific application (see, e.g.,
[
26
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
and the references therein). The particular problem we consider in this paper as motivation for the design of the finite element formulation is the interfacial flow of aqueous humour between the anterior chamber and the trabecular meshwork (which is a deformable porous structure) in the eye, and how such phenomenon relates to early stages of glaucoma.

Glaucoma encompasses a group of mechanisms that lead to decreased retinal function, impaired visual fields and blindness. The main risk factor for glaucoma in canines is an abnormal increase in the intra-ocular pressure (which under physiologically normal conditions is balanced between aqueous humour production and outflow to the venous drainage system
[
32
]
). We are interested in modelling the flow behaviour of aqueous humour within the anterior chamber and its interaction with the poroelastic properties of particular compartments in the drainage outlet located between the base of the iris and the limbus, which, in the dog eye and most other non-primate species consists of an array of thin tissue columns (pectinate ligaments)
[
46
]
which mark the boundary of the trabecular meshwork with the anterior chamber. Sketches of the regions of interest are depicted in Figure
1.1
. Our focus is on how the physical changes associated with pectinate ligament dysplasia, a change seen in all dogs with primary angle closure glaucoma, affect aqueous humour flow through this boundary. We stress that the ciliary cleft anatomy of all carnivorous mammals is fairly similar to that of the dog. In fact, this anatomy is preserved across much of herbivorous mammals as well (see, e.g.,
[
43
]
). Therefore, even though the dog is probably the most studied due to its status as a companion animal, this work likely applies to most carnivorous and herbivorous mammals.

The flow within the anterior chamber will be modelled by Navier-Stokes and Stokes’ law for Newtonian fluids, whereas the filtration of aqueous humour through the deformable trabecular meshwork and towards the angular aqueous plexus will be described by Darcy’s law. Pressure differences are generated by production (from the ciliary muscle) and drainage (to angular aqueous plexus and then linked to the veins at the surface of the sclera through collecting channels) of aqueous humour.

Other effects that could contribute to modification of the flow patterns and that we do not consider here, are thermal properties (buoyancy mechanisms due to temperature gradients from inner to outer cornea)
[
11
]
, cross-link interaction between fibrils in the cornea
[
30
]
, pressure changes due to phacodonesis (vibration of the lens while the head or eye itself moves) and Rapid Eye Movement during sleep
[
29
]
, and nonlinear flow conditions in the filtration region (incorporated in
[
37
]
through Darcy-Forchheimer models).

In contrast with
[
25
,
28
,
42
,
52
]
, here we consider that the coalescing of the pectinate ligaments results in marked changes in porosity properties of the anterior chamber - trabecular meshwork interface, which could eventually lead to progressive collapse of the ciliary cleft. We further postulate that these modifications of the tissue’s microstructure could be induced by forces exerted by the flow that concentrate at the interface between the dysplastic pectinate ligament and the anterior chamber, and which occur over a timescale much larger than that of the ocular pulsating flow. In fact, evidence of the compliance of the trabecular meshwork can be found in, e.g.,
[
35
]
. One of the earliest modelling works including a coupling between aqueous humour in the anterior chamber with complying structures is presented in
[
33
]
, where mechanical properties of the bovine iris were employed to set an elastic interface to represent blinking. Other fluid-structure interaction models have been recently developed in
[
54
]
, suggesting that flow conditions in the trabecular meshwork and the outlets could be largely affected by the changes of permeability in microstructure, and
[
3
]
, where poroelastic properties of the choroid and viscoelastic response of the vitreous body are used to set up a more complete 3D model of larger scale that discards a dedicated physiological description of the trabecular meshwork and considers instead a windkessel model.

In the general context of single phase fluid / poromechanical coupling, there are already a variety of finite element formulations starting from the work
[
44
]
, which focuses on the effects of secondary consolidation.
More recently, partitioned finite element formulations using domain decomposition and or Nitsche’s approach for single and double poroelastic layers in contact with a single phase fluid can be found in
[

,
16
,
17
,
39
]
.
Monolithic couplings have been analysed in
[
7
]
for a mixed Darcy formulation using a Lagrange multiplier to impose flux continuity (see also
[
5
]
for the extension to the case of non-Newtonian fluids), as well in
[
19
,
20
]
for a primal Darcy formulation. Ghost penalty methods have been employed for cut FEM methods valid in the regime of large deformations in
[
2
]
.

Here, and drawing inspiration from the formulation in
[
7
]
and
[
27
,
40
,
45
]
, we rewrite the poroelasticity equations using three fields (displacement, fluid pressure and total pressure). Compared to previous works
[

,
5
,
9
,
16
,
17
,
19
,
20
,
39
]
, which employ the classical displacement formulation, an advantage of the present approach, inherited from
[

]
, is that the formulation is free of poroelastic locking, meaning that it is robust with respect to the Lamé parameters of the poroelastic structure. This is of particular importance when we test variations of the flow response to changes in the material properties of the skeleton and when the solid approaches the incompressibility limit.
The present work also stands as an extension of the formulation recently employed in
[
50
]
(where only the case of intrinsic incompressible constituent in the poroelastic region were considered) to obtain approximate solutions for heterogeneous poroelasticity coupled with Stokes flow in channels (and using also heterogeneous elastic moduli); while the PDE analysis, numerical aspects, and applicability of the formalism to more realistic scenarios have not yet been addressed. In this work, under adequate assumptions, the analysis of the weak formulation is carried out, using a (time continuous) semi-discrete Galerkin approximation and a weak compactness argument. The well-posedness of the semi-discrete formulation is established using the theory of differential algebraic equations (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
) and using similar results to those obtained in
[
7
,
53
]
. A conforming mixed finite element scheme of general order is used. Furthermore, a fully discrete scheme based on backward Euler’s time discretisation is considered, and the unique solvability and convergence for the fully discrete scheme are established.

We have organised the contents of this paper in the following manner. Section
2
outlines the model problem, motivating each term in the balance equations and stating the interfacial and boundary conditions. Section
3
states the weak form of the governing equations in Cartesian and axisymmetric coordinates. Then, in Section
4
, we address the construction of the finite element scheme, the well-posedness of the continuous and discrete problems, the stability of the fully discrete system in matrix form. Section
5
states the fully-discrete scheme and presents the error estimates. In Section

One of the examples involves large displacements near the interface, in which case a harmonic extension operator is used to deform the fluid domain. We close with a summary, some remarks and a discussion on model generalisations in Section
7
.

2
Governing equations

Let us consider a spatial domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=2,3
disjointly split into
\Omega_{F}
and
\Omega_{P}
representing, respectively, the regions where a chamber filled with incompressible fluid and the deformable porous structure are located. We will denote by
\boldsymbol{n}
the unit normal vector on the boundary
\partial\Omega
, and by
\Sigma=\Omega_{F}\cap\Omega_{P}
the interface between the two subdomains. We also define the boundaries
\Gamma_{F}=\partial\Omega_{F}\setminus\Sigma
and
\Gamma_{P}=\partial\Omega_{P}\setminus\Sigma
, and adopt the convention that on
\Sigma
the normal vector points from
\Omega_{F}
to
\Omega_{P}
. See a rough sketch in Figure
2.1
, that represents the geometry of the anterior segment in the eye distinguishing between the anterior chamber
\Omega_{F}
and the trabecular meshwork
\Omega_{P}
. The domain is sketched as an axisymmetric region, for which more specific properties will be listed later on.

In presenting the set of governing equations for the coupled fluid - poroelastic system we first focus on the fluid domain, then on the poroelastic domain and, finally, on the initial, boundary and interfacial conditions.

2.1
Fluid domain

In the fluid domain
\Omega_{F}
, the problem is governed by the momentum and mass conservation equations. Defining the fluid velocity
\boldsymbol{u}
and the fluid pressure
p_{F}
, the resulting system is written as

\displaystyle\rho_{f}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u})-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits[2\mu_{f}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-p_{F}\mathbf{I}]

\displaystyle=\rho_{f}\boldsymbol{g}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega_{F}\times(0,T]$},

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega_{F}\times(0,T]$},

(2.1b)

where
\rho_{f},\mu_{f}
are the density and dynamic viscosity of the fluid (e.g. the aqueous humour if we think to the application for the filtration in the eye and thus
\Omega_{F}
is the anterior chamber),

\boldsymbol{g}
is the gravity acceleration,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{\tt t})

is the strain rate tensor and
\partial_{t}
indicates derivatives with respect to time.
Note that, in typical flow conditions of the eye anterior chamber, the Reynolds number is low (approximately 1.25, while the reduced Reynolds number is between 0.06-0.08. See, e.g.,
[
18
,
29
,
35
,
42
]
). Therefore,
for the stability analysis and for some of the numerical tests later on, we will restrict the fluid model to Stokes’ equations.

2.2
Poroelastic domain

The poroelastic domain
\Omega_{P}
is a biphasic material constituted by a linear elastic solid phase (potentially intrinsically compressible) and an intrinsically incompressible fluid phase. In the context of the eye poromechanics, the trabecular meshwork region occupying
\Omega_{P}
is constituted by three distinctive tissues, the uveal meshwork, the juxtacanalicular meshwork, and the corneoscleral network; they have different micromechanical properties that, from our modelling perspective, can be regarded as a single poroelastic domain with heterogeneous porosity distribution that, in turn, means possible heterogeneity in the material properties. In addition, we anticipate that, although the fluid viscosity is relevant at the scale of the pore, we assume that the fluid can be treated as inviscid at the macroscale. Calling
p_{P}
the fluid pressure and
\boldsymbol{d}
the solid displacement, here we introduce the formulation that assumes pressure and displacement as primary variables as presented, for example, in
[
24
]

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(C_{0}p_{P}+\alpha\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{d}\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\biggl({\frac{\kappa}{\mu_{f}}(\nabla p_{P}-\rho_{f}\boldsymbol{g})}\biggr)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega_{P}\times(0,T]$},

(2.2a)

\displaystyle-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits[2\mu_{s}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{d})+\lambda\left(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{d}\right)\mathbf{I}-\alpha p_{P}\mathbf{I}]

\displaystyle=\rho_{m}\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega_{P}\times(0,T]$}.

(2.2b)

The first equation can be derived from the conservation of mass for the fluid phase once employing the Darcy’s law and the relation between fluid content - pressure - hydrostatic deformation of the solid phase. The storage capacity
C_{0}
is related to the intrinsic compressibility of the solid phase, while
\kappa
is the permeability (assumed isotropic but heterogeneous).
The second equation is the conservation of the momentum for the mixture, where
\boldsymbol{f}
is a (possibly fluid pressure-dependent) body load,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{d})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{d}+\nabla\boldsymbol{d}^{\tt t})
is the
infinitesimal strain tensor,
\rho_{s}
is the density of the
porous matrix and
\rho_{m}
is the average density of the poroelastic body,
\lambda,\mu_{s}
are the Lamé constants of the solid; the term in the divergence in the left-hand side of (
2.2b
) is known as the effective stress or Terzaghi stress and the parameter
\alpha
, also known as Biot-Willis poroelastic coefficient, depends on the intrinsic compressibility of the solid phase (
\alpha=1
when the solid phase is intrinsically incompressible). We refer an interested reader to
[
24
]
or
[
23
]
for further details.

For sake of robustness of the formulation with respect to
\lambda
, we introduce the
total pressure

\varphi:=\alpha p_{P}-\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{d}
, as an additional unknown in the system (following
[
40
,
45
]
), and rewrite the pressure-displacement formulation in (
2.2
) in term of the solid displacement
\boldsymbol{d}
, the fluid
pressure
p_{P}
, and the total pressure
\varphi
, as

\displaystyle-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits[2\mu_{s}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{d})-\varphi\mathbf{I}]

\displaystyle=\rho_{m}\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega_{P}\times(0,T]$},

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\varphi-\alpha p_{P}+\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{d}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega_{P}\times(0,T]$},

(2.3b)

\displaystyle\bigl(C_{0}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\lambda}\bigr)\partial_{t}p_{P}-\frac{\alpha}{\lambda}\partial_{t}\varphi-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\biggl({\frac{\kappa}{\mu_{f}}(\nabla p_{P}-\rho_{f}\boldsymbol{g})}\biggr)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega_{P}\times(0,T]$}.

(2.3c)

In contrast with the formulations in
[
16
,
7
]
, here we do not employ the fluid velocity in the porous domain as a separate unknown. Consistently with
[
41
]
, in the framework of linear poroelasticity, the permeability depends on the pressure and the displacement fields only at higher orders; however, it can be heterogeneous, with a spatial distribution dependent, for example, on an initial porosity distribution. We thus simply write

\kappa=\kappa(\boldsymbol{x}).

(2.4)

Likewise, it is also possible to assume heterogeneity of the Lamé constants, as in
[
50
]
. In such a case, we need to assume that there exist constants
\lambda_{\min}
and

\lambda_{\max}
such that
0<\lambda_{\min}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{\max}
. In the analysis we show that the estimates are independent of
\lambda_{\max}
, i.e., the results are uniform in the almost incompressible limit, even for the heterogeneous case. Heterogeneous permeability and/or heterogeneous Lamé parameters will be used in some of the numerical examples of Section
6
.

2.3
Initial, boundary and transmission conditions

To close the system composed by (
2.1a
), (
2.1b
), (
2.2b
), (
2.2a

Without losing generality, we suppose that

\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\quad p_{P}(0)=p_{P,0}\quad\text{in $\Omega\times\{0\}$,}

(2.5)

while for the rest of the variables we will construct compatible initial data. In particular, and for sake of the energy estimates to be addressed in Section

On the boundary
\Gamma_{F}
of the fluid domain we can apply conditions on either the velocity, or the stress tensor; we thus decomposed it between
\Gamma_{F}^{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
\Gamma_{F}^{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
,
with
|\Gamma_{F}^{\boldsymbol{u}}|>0
,
where we impose, respectively, no slip velocities and zero normal total stresses as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{F}^{\boldsymbol{u}}\times(0,T]$},

(2.6a)

\displaystyle[2\mu_{f}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-p_{F}\mathbf{I}]\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{F}^{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\times(0,T]$}.

(2.6b)

Similarly, on the boundary
\Gamma_{P}
of the poroelastic domain we can prescribe conditions on either the displacement or the traction and either the pressure or the fluid flux
\mathbf{q}=-\frac{\kappa}{\mu_{f}}(\nabla p_{P}-\rho_{f}\boldsymbol{g})
. We thus divide the boundary into
\Gamma_{P}^{p_{P}}
and
\Gamma_{P}^{\boldsymbol{d}}
, with
|\Gamma_{P}^{p_{P}}|>0
and
|\Gamma_{P}^{\boldsymbol{d}}|>0
, where we apply, respectively,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\kappa}{\mu_{f}}(\nabla p_{P}-\rho_{f}\boldsymbol{g})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{P}^{\boldsymbol{d}}\times(0,T]$},

(2.7a)

\displaystyle[2\mu_{s}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{d})-\varphi\mathbf{I}]\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{and}\quad p_{P}

\displaystyle={0}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{P}^{p_{P}}\times(0,T]$}.

(2.7b)

Next, and following
[
9
,
16
,
44
,
49
]
, we consider transmission conditions on
\Sigma
accounting for the continuity of normal
fluxes, momentum conservation, balance of fluid normal stresses, and the so-called Beavers-Joseph-Saffman (BJS) condition for tangential fluid forces

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{d}-\frac{\kappa}{\mu_{f}}(\nabla p_{P}-\rho_{f}\boldsymbol{g})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Sigma\times(0,T]$},

(2.8a)

\displaystyle(2\mu_{f}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-p_{F}\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=(2\mu_{s}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{d})-\varphi\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Sigma\times(0,T]$},

(2.8b)

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{n}\cdot(2\mu_{f}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-p_{F}\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle={p_{P}}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Sigma\times(0,T]$},

(2.8c)

\displaystyle-{\boldsymbol{t}^{j}}\cdot(2\mu_{f}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-p_{F}\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\mu_{f}}{\sqrt{\kappa}}(\boldsymbol{u}-\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{d})\cdot{\boldsymbol{t}^{j},\quad 1\leq j\leq d-1}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Sigma\times(0,T]$},

(2.8d)

where
\gamma>0
is the slip rate coefficient (or tangential resistance parameter), and we recall that the normal
\boldsymbol{n}
on the interface is understood as pointing from the fluid domain
\Omega_{F}
towards the porous structure

, while
\boldsymbol{t}^{1}
stands for the
tangent vector on

(for the case of

3
Weak formulation

Apart from the nomenclature introduced at the beginning of the section, conventional notation will be adopted throughout the paper. For Lipschitz domains
\Xi
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for
s\in\mathbb{N}
,

we denote by

and use boldfaces to refer to vector-valued functions and function spaces, e.g.,

\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{V})}:=\int^{T}_{0}\|f(t)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{V}}\,\,\mathrm{d}t

3.1
Cartesian case

The initial step in deriving the finite element scheme consists in stating a weak form for (
2.1a
)-(
2.3c
). We proceed to
test these equations against suitable smooth functions and to integrate over the corresponding subdomain. After applying
integration by parts wherever adequate,
we formally end up with the following remainder on the interface

I_{\Sigma}=-\langle(2\mu_{f}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-p_{F}\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{\Sigma}+\langle(2\mu_{s}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{d})-\varphi\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle_{\Sigma}+\langle\frac{\kappa}{\mu_{f}}\nabla p_{P}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},q_{P}\rangle_{\Sigma},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Sigma}
denotes the duality pairing between the trace functional space space

and its dual
H^{-1/2}(\Sigma)
. Then, as in, e.g.,
[
36
]
, we proceed to use each of the transmission conditions (

)-(

, and

,
respectively. Consequently we have the following

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+a_{4}^{P}(p_{P},q_{P})-b_{2}^{P}(\partial_{t}\varphi,q_{P})=G(q_{P})

4
Well-posedness of the weak formulation

The following analysis is confined to the Cartesian case. Furthermore, we
focus on the quasi-static Biot-Stokes model, i.e., we neglect the
terms
a_{1}^{F}
and
c^{F}
in (
3.1a
), as this is the typical flow
regime for the application of interest. We also restrict our attention
to the case
\tilde{\alpha}=1
. The solvability analysis is based on a Galerkin
argument, where one considers the semi-discrete continuous in time formulation
with a discretisation parameter
h
. We establish that it has a unique solution
and derive stability bounds. Then, owing to a weak compactness argument, we pass to
the limit
h\to 0
and obtain existence and uniqueness of a weak solution.

4.1
Semi-discrete mixed finite element formulation

In addition to the assumptions stated before on the domain geometry, to avoid additional technicalities, we operate under the condition that
\Omega
is a polytope.
We denote by

a shape-regular family of finite element partitions of

The finite-dimensional subspaces for fluid velocity, fluid pressure, porous displacement,

Feasible choices are Taylor-Hood, the MINI element, Crouzeix-Raviart, Scott-Vogelius, Guzmán-Neilan, Bernardi-Raugel, equal-order stabilised methods (including total pressure projection stabilisation), divergence-conforming stabilised methods, and many others.

-norm. A more general case, with different polynomial degrees for the different finite element spaces, is addressed in Section

\begin{split}&2\mu_{f}\!\int_{\Omega_{F}}\!\!\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(e_{\boldsymbol{u},h}^{n}):\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(e_{\boldsymbol{u},h}^{n})+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\langle\frac{\gamma\mu_{f}}{\sqrt{\kappa}}(e_{\boldsymbol{u},h}^{n}-\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},h})\cdot\boldsymbol{t}^{j}\,,(e_{\boldsymbol{u},h}^{n}-\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},h})\cdot\boldsymbol{t}^{j}\rangle_{\Sigma}\\

where
\mathcal{L}^{n}

). Using the continuity of the bilinear forms

) in combination with Young’s inequality, we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{n}&\leq\epsilon\big(\|e_{\boldsymbol{u},h}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{F},h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{P},h}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|e_{\varphi,h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}\big)\\
&\quad+C_{\epsilon}\big(\|e_{\boldsymbol{u},I}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{F},I}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{P},I}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{p_{P},I}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}\\
&\quad+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\varphi,I}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|r_{\boldsymbol{d}}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}+\|r_{p_{P}}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|r_{\varphi}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}\big)+\mathcal{H}(\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},h}),\end{split}

where
\mathcal{H}(\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},h})
is the collection of terms on the right-hand side of (
5.6c
) with
w_{h}=\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},h}
. Multiplying (
5.7
) by
\Delta t
, summing for

,

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{\epsilon}\big(\|e_{\boldsymbol{u},I}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{F},I}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{P},I}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{p_{P},I}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\varphi,I}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|r_{\boldsymbol{d}}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}+\|r_{p_{P}}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|r_{\varphi}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}\big)+\mathcal{H}(\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},h})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+C\big(\|e_{\boldsymbol{d},h}^{0}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{P},h}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\alpha e_{p_{P},h}^{0}-e_{\varphi,h}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}\big),

\begin{split}&\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{k}b_{3}^{\Sigma}(e_{\boldsymbol{u},I}^{n},\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},h})=b_{3}^{\Sigma}(e_{\boldsymbol{u},I}^{k},e_{\boldsymbol{d},h}^{k})-b_{3}^{\Sigma}(e_{\boldsymbol{u},I}^{1},e_{\boldsymbol{d},h}^{0})-\Delta t\sum_{n=2}^{k}b_{3}^{\Sigma}(\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{u},I},e_{\boldsymbol{d},h}^{n-1})\\

(5.10)

Now, combining (

)–(

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{k}\Big(\|e_{\boldsymbol{u},h}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\|(e_{\boldsymbol{u},h}^{n}-\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},h})\cdot\boldsymbol{t}^{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}+\|e_{p_{P},h}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\|e_{\boldsymbol{d},h}^{k}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{P},h}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\alpha e_{p_{P},h}^{k}-e_{\varphi,h}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{k}\Big(\epsilon\big(\|e_{\boldsymbol{u},h}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{F},h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{P},h}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|e_{\varphi,h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\|e_{\boldsymbol{u},I}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{F},I}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{P},I}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|r_{\boldsymbol{d}}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}+\|r_{p_{P}}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|r_{\varphi}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{p_{P},I}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\varphi,I}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{u},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+C\Big(\Delta t\sum_{n=2}^{k}\big(\|e_{\boldsymbol{d},h}^{n-1}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}r_{\boldsymbol{d}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\|e_{\boldsymbol{d},h}^{0}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{P},h}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\alpha e_{p_{P},h}^{0}-e_{\varphi,h}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{k}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{1}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\|e^{k}_{\boldsymbol{u},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e^{k}_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|e^{k}_{p_{P},I}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|e^{k}_{\varphi,I}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|r^{k}_{\boldsymbol{d}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\|e^{1}_{\boldsymbol{u},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e^{1}_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|e^{1}_{p_{P},I}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|e^{1}_{\varphi,I}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|r^{1}_{\boldsymbol{d}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}\Big).

(5.11)

We continue with bounding

) and (

) imply

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{k}\|e_{p_{F},h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}\leq C\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{k}\Big(\|e_{\boldsymbol{u},h}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\|(e_{\boldsymbol{u},h}^{n}-\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},h})\cdot\boldsymbol{t}^{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}+\|e_{p_{P},h}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\|e_{\boldsymbol{u},I}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{F},I}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{P},I}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|r_{\boldsymbol{d}}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}\Big).

On the other hand,
the inf-sup condition (
4.1b
) and (
5.6c
) allow us to get

\begin{split}&\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{k}\|e_{\varphi,h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}\leq C\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{k}\Big(\|e_{p_{P},h}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\|(e_{\boldsymbol{u},h}^{n}-\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},h})\cdot\boldsymbol{t}^{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}+\|e_{\boldsymbol{d},h}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}\\
&\ +\|e_{\boldsymbol{u},I}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}+\|e_{p_{P},I}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|e_{\varphi,I}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}+\|r_{\boldsymbol{d}}^{n}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}\Big).\end{split}

(5.12)

Before combining (
5.1
)–(
5.12

the terms involving
\partial_{t}^{n}
on the right-hand sides
require special treatment. In particular, it holds for
\phi(t)
that

(\partial_{t}^{n}\phi)^{2}=\frac{1}{\Delta t^{2}}\Big(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\partial_{t}\phi\Big)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2},

implying, for
\phi:[0,T]\to V
, where

is a Banach space with norm
\|\cdot\|_{V}
, that

\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{k}\|\partial_{t}^{n}\phi\|_{V}^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\|\partial_{t}\phi\|_{V}^{2}.

We then have

\begin{split}&\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{k}\big(\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{p_{P},I}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\varphi,I}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{u},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}\big)\\
&\quad\leq C\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\big(\|\partial_{t}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}e_{p_{P},I}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}e_{\varphi,I}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}e_{\boldsymbol{u},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}^{2}\big).\end{split}

(5.13)

To bound the term
\|\partial_{t}^{n}\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}
in (
5.1
), for any
\phi(t)
we have,
using the integral mean value theorem and the mean value theorem,

(\partial_{t}^{n}\partial_{t}^{n}\phi)^{2}=\frac{1}{\Delta t^{4}}\Big(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t^{n}}\partial_{t}\phi-\int_{t_{n-2}}^{t^{n-1}}\partial_{t}\phi\Big)^{2}=\frac{1}{\Delta t^{2}}\big(\partial_{t}\phi(\xi^{n})-\partial_{t}\phi(\xi^{n-1})\big)^{2}=\partial_{tt}\phi(\xi),\quad\xi\in[t_{n-2},t_{n}].

Therefore it holds that

\Delta t\sum_{n=2}^{k}\|\partial_{t}^{n}\partial_{t}^{n}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}\leq C\mathop{\mathrm{ess\,sup}}\limits_{t\in(0,t_{k})}\|\partial_{tt}e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}^{2}.

Next, we need to bound the time discretisation error. Taylor’s expansion gives

thus, for
\phi:[0,T]\to V
,

\|r_{\phi}^{n}\|_{V}\leq C\Delta t\mathop{\mathrm{ess\,sup}}\limits_{t\in(t_{n-1},t_{n})}\|\partial_{tt}\phi\|_{V}\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{k}\|r_{\phi}^{n}\|_{V}^{2}\leq C\Delta t^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\|\partial_{tt}\phi\|_{V}^{2}.

Similarly,

\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{k}\|\partial_{t}^{n}r_{\phi}\|_{V}^{2}\leq C\Delta t^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\|\partial_{ttt}\phi\|_{V}^{2}.

Finally, we need a bound on the initial discretisation error. Recalling the construction of the discrete initial data in the proof of
Theorem
4.1
and the definition of the continuous initial data in the proof of Theorem
4.2
, we note that
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{0},p_{F,h}^{0})
is the Stokes elliptic projection of
(\boldsymbol{u}(0),p_{F,h}(0))
based on (

)–(
4.2b
) at

with
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{d}_{h}=0
and
the term
b_{2}^{\Sigma}(p_{P,0}-p_{P,h,0},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})
on the right-hand side. In addition,

(\boldsymbol{d}_{h}^{0},\varphi_{h}^{0})
is the elliptic projection of
(\boldsymbol{d}(0),\varphi(0))
based on the stable problem (
4.2c
)–(
4.2e
) at
t=0
with
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{d}_{h}=0
and the terms
b_{3}^{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{0},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})
,

b_{4}^{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},p_{P,0}-p_{P,h,0})
, and
b_{2}^{P}(\psi_{h},p_{P,0}-p_{P,h,0})
on the right-hand side. Classical finite element analysis for these two problems implies that

\begin{split}&\|e_{\boldsymbol{u},h}^{0}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}+\|e_{p_{F},h}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{F})}+\|e_{\boldsymbol{d},h}^{0}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}+\|e_{p_{P},h}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}+\|e_{\varphi,h}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}\\
&\quad\leq C\big(\|e_{\boldsymbol{u},I}^{0}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{F})}+\|e_{p_{F},I}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{F})}+\|e_{\boldsymbol{d},I}^{0}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega_{P})}+\|e_{p_{P},I}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}+\|e_{\varphi,I}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{P})}\big).\end{split}

Representative computational results

All routines have been implemented using the open source finite element library
FEniCS

[
4
]
, as well as the specialised module
multiphenics

10
]

The description provided here allows to speculate that a rearrangement of the microstructure could not drive a macroscopic change in the mechanics of the system within a short time horizon, since the flow and the pressure drop remains of the same order of magnitude; however, higher pressure and strain gradients could be the cause of a local remodelling of the tissue that, in the long term and in the nonlinear regime, could lead to a collapse of part of the pectinate ligaments.

7
Concluding remarks

We have introduced a new formulation for the coupling of Biot’s poroelasticity system using total pressure and free flow described by the Stokes and Navier-Stokes equations, and which does not require Lagrange multipliers to set up the interface conditions between the two subdomains. The well-posedness of the continuous problem has been proved, we have provided a rigorous stability analysis. A mixed finite element method is defined for the proposed formulation together with a corresponding reduction to the axisymmetric case and the proposed schemes are robust with respect to the first Lamé parameter. We have conducted the numerical validation of spatio-temporal accuracy and have also performed some tests of applicative relevance, studying the behaviour of poromechanical filtration in subsurface hydraulic fracture with challenging heterogeneous material parameters, and simulating interfacial flow through the trabecular meshwork of the eye.

Different formulations we will study next include conservative discretisations on both poroelasticity and free flow
[
13
,
34
,
38
]
.
We are currently working towards the design of monolithic block preconditioners being robust with respect to all material parameters, and whose construction hinges on adequate weighted Sobolev spaces and fractional interfacial norms. On the other hand, a crucial model extension corresponds to the regime of large deformations and the incorporation of remodelling mechanisms that would better explain the progressive consolidation of the interface and the shrinkage of the trabecular meshwork and associated ciliary cleft collapse seen in canines with glaucoma (using formalisms sharing similarities with the study of lamina cribrosa thickening
[
31
]
). There, it is also necessary to consider nonlinear variations on permeability depending on porosity, which in turn undergoes changes due to microstructural rearrangement
[
41
]
.
