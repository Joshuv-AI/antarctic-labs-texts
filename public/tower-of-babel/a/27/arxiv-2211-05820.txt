Vibropolaritonic Reaction Rates in the Collective Strong Coupling Regime: Pollak-Grabert-Hänggi Theory
Matthew Du, Yong Rui Poh, Joel Yuen-Zhou

Abstract
Following experimental evidence that vibrational polaritons, formed from collective vibrational strong coupling (VSC) in optical microcavities, can modify ground-state reaction rates, a spate of theoretical explanations relying on cavity-induced frictions has been proposed through the Pollak-Grabert-Hänggi (PGH) theory, which goes beyond transition state theory (TST). However, by considering only a single reacting molecule coupled to light, these works do not capture the ensemble effects present in experiments. Moreover, the relevant light-matter coupling should have been N \sqrt{N} times smaller than those used by preceding works, where N ≈ 10 6 − 10 12 N\approx 10^{6}-10^{12} is the ensemble size. In this work, we explain why this distinction is significant and can nullify effects from these cavity-induced frictions. By analytically extending the cavity PGH model to realistic values of N N , we show how this model succumbs to the polariton “large N N problem”, that is, the situation whereby the single reacting molecule feels only a tiny 1 / N 1/N part of the collective light-matter interaction intensity, where N N is large.



Introduction

Vibrational strong coupling (VSC) occurs when molecular vibrational
modes interact strongly with infrared photon modes, typically confined
in an optical cavity, thus forming new light-matter hybrid modes known
as vibrational polaritons
(
Long and Simpkins 2015
;
Shalabney et al. 2015
)
. With microcavities
such as Fabry-Pérot cavities, these interactions are only appreciable
in the presence of a macroscopic number of molecules, that is, VSC
is a collective effect
(
Campos-Gonzalez-Angulo and Yuen-Zhou 2020
;
Martínez-Martínez et al. 2019
)
.
Over the past decade, vibrational polaritons formed from microcavities
have been experimentally shown to influence (1) ground-state chemical
reactivities
(
Thomas et al. 2016
;
Lather et al. 2019
;
Hirai et al. 2020
;
Garcia-Vidal et al. 2021
;
Thomas et al. 2019
;
Lather and George 2021
;
Lather et al. 2022
;
Ahn et al. 2022
)

and (2) vibrational energy transfer processes
(
Dunkelberger et al. 2016
;
Xiang et al. 2018
;
Xiang et al. 2020
;
Pang et al. 2020
)
,
creating a field known as vibropolaritonic chemistry. These experiments,
conducted under the following conditions:
(
Yuen-Zhou et al. 2021
)

C
1.

N\approx 10^{6}-10^{12}
molecules
collectively
coupled to
the cavity,

C
2.

in the absence of optical pumping, that is, the reaction relies purely
on
thermal fluctuations
,

reported the following observations:

O
1.

The cavity may either
enhance
or
suppress
reaction rates;

O
2.

Rate modification by the cavity is optimum when the cavity mode is

resonant
with the reactant, spectator and/or solvent vibrational
modes, and

O
3.

occurs only for the cavity mode at normal incidence (
k=0
).

Unfortunately, there remains a dearth of theoretical models that successfully
explain all five features. In particular, the first class of transitions
has, in the absence of VSC, been well-explained by thermal adiabatic
rate models
(
Peters 2017
)
such as transition state theory (TST).
Along this vein, pioneering studies have attempted to incorporate
VSC effects into a classical TST model
(
Galego et al. 2019
;
Campos-Gonzalez-Angulo and Yuen-Zhou 2020
)
,
only to find that the activation energy remains unchanged once the
often-neglected dipole self-energy term of the photon mode is included

(
Li et al. 2021
)
(although this conclusion has been contested by models
that account for vibrational quantum effects
(
Yang and Cao 2021
)
). The
transmission prefactor may, however, be reduced by VSC
(
Li et al. 2021
)

through a dynamical caging effect similar to the Grote-Hynes theory

(
Grote and Hynes 1980
)
, yet this result fails to account for the collective,
resonance and
k=0
features present in experiments (i.e. features
C1, O2 and O3). This sparked a series of works that considered additional
effects such as anharmonicities
(
Hernández and Herrera 2019
;
Triana et al. 2020
;
Campos-Gonzalez-Angulo et al. 2021
;
Ribeiro et al. 2018
)
,
multiple cavity modes
(
Hoffmann et al. 2020
;
Ribeiro 2022
)
, inter-mode
energy redistributions
(
Schäfer et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022a
;
Wang et al. 2022b
;
Fischer et al. 2022
)
,
and disorders
(
Sidler et al. 2021
;
Botzung et al. 2020
)
. A summary of these
theoretical results will be presented in an upcoming perspective
(
Campos-Gonzalez-Angulo et al. 2022
)
.
Note that considerable efforts have also been devoted to the second
class of transitions
(
Campos-Gonzalez-Angulo et al. 2019
;
Vurgaftman et al. 2020
;
Phuc et al. 2020
;
Du et al. 2021
;
Du and Yuen-Zhou 2022
;
Poh et al. 2022
;
Cao 2022
)

and will not be discussed here.

This paper joins the wave of some recent works
(
Sun and Vendrell 2022
;
Lindoy et al. 2022
;
Philbin et al. 2022
)

that explored the possibility of cavity-induced frictions. Through
classical trajectory-based simulations over a range of bath frictions,
Sun and Vendrell reported cavity-mediated rate enhancements in the
low-friction regime that peak when the reacting vibrational mode is
resonant with the cavity mode
(
Sun and Vendrell 2022
)
(thereby fulfilling
observation O2). Their result is consistent with the Pollak-Grabert-Hänggi
(PGH) description
(
Pollak et al. 1989
)
, an analytical adiabatic rate
model that also considers weak energy exchange between the system
and bath modes. Here, system refers to a reactive mode, so stronger
system-bath couplings (also known as frictions) allow the reacting
system to more easily acquire energy from the bath to cross the barrier,
thereby accelerating the reaction. Note that the PGH theory is a non-Markovian
generalisation of the Kramers turnover model, first predicted by Kramers

(
Kramers 1940
)
and later solved by Mel’nikov and Meshkov
(
Mel’nikov and Meshkov 1986
)
.
Importantly, PGH theory goes beyond TST and is unlike the Grote-Hynes
theory
(
Grote and Hynes 1980
)
, which may be reduced to TST
(
Pollak 1986
)
.
In the context of VSC, Lindoy et al. interpreted the cavity mode as
an effective bath mode, which exerts cavity-induced friction on the
system
(
Lindoy et al. 2022
)
. As such, the cavity accelerates chemical
reactions when its coupling to the reactive mode is stronger than
that of the molecule’s inherent bath modes. In both aforementioned
works
(
Sun and Vendrell 2022
;
Lindoy et al. 2022
)
, the cavity was assumed to have
a single photon mode. Later, Philbin et al. extended the model to
an imperfect cavity with multiple confined modes
(
Philbin et al. 2022
)

and made qualitatively similar observations apart from sharper resonances
and weaker cavity effects.

These studies, while enlightening, were investigated for a single
(or few) molecule(s) interacting with light and are therefore not
fully representative of the ensemble effects observed in VSC involving
microcavities
(
Thomas et al. 2016
;
Lather et al. 2019
;
Hirai et al. 2020
;
Garcia-Vidal et al. 2021
;
Thomas et al. 2019
;
Lather and George 2021
;
Lather et al. 2022
;
Ahn et al. 2022
)

(see condition C1). In addition, their numerical results were reported
using experimental values of light-matter interactions belonging to
an entire molecular ensemble, even though the single-molecule interaction
would have been more accurate. This distinction is highly non-trivial:
the appropriate single-molecule light-matter coupling
g
is
\sqrt{N}

times smaller than the experimentally-measured collective coupling

g\sqrt{N}
, where
N
is the number of confined molecules estimated
to be
\approx 10^{6}-10^{12}

(
del Pino et al. 2015a
;
Daskalakis et al. 2017
)
. Clearly

g\not\approx g\sqrt{N}
. To demonstrate how collectivity changes
the effects of cavity-induced frictions, we analytically extend the
PGH model to include a macroscopic number of molecules
N
, each
of which interacts with a single cavity mode [Fig. (
1
)].
Using a
g\sqrt{N}
value representative of experimental data, we
find that the purported cavity-mediated rate enhancements quickly
and expectedly vanish with increasing
N
and are negligible for
realistic values of
N>20
. In particular, cavity-induced frictions
depend on
g
to leading order in
g\sqrt{N-1}
, a result familiar
to the community of collective VSC
(
Kansanen 2022
;
Poh et al. 2022
;
Yang and Cao 2021
)
.
Therefore, for a constant
g\sqrt{N-1}\approx g\sqrt{N}
, we find
that
g
and thus cavity-mediated frictions diminish with realistic
values of
N
. This is a reminder of the polariton “large
N

problem”
(
Martínez-Martínez et al. 2019
)
, that is, that any benefit
from the polaritons is often lost to the penalty of having a large
number (
N-1
) of non-reacting molecules compete with a single reacting
molecule for the cavity [Fig. (
2
)]. Finally,
we qualitatively argue why our observations remain valid even with
disorder and multiple cavity modes.

With reference to the five features described earlier, our model fully
addresses C1, C2 and O2 and partially addresses O1 by dealing only
with rate enhancements. Note that the collective effect described
by condition C1 is the novel part of this work.

Results and Discussions

Outside the cavity, the PGH model
(
Pollak et al. 1989
)
considers a
single reactive (system) mode of coordinate
R
coupled to a harmonic
thermal bath of coordinates
\left\{Q_{\alpha}\right\}
and frequencies

\left\{\omega_{\alpha}\right\}
. The potential of the reactive mode has
a barrier of height
E_{b}
separating a well from the continuum
[Fig. (
1
)]. A particle (such as a single molecule)
moves along its reactive and bath modes classically; it starts from
a metastable state in the well and, through energy exchange with the
thermal bath modes, harnesses sufficient energy to cross the barrier,
signifying a reaction of which the rate may be computed. Working in
mass-weighted coordinates, the Hamiltonian for this model is

\displaystyle H_{\text{PGH}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{R}^{2}}{2}+V\left(R\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\left[\frac{\dot{Q}_{\alpha}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\omega_{\alpha}Q_{\alpha}+\gamma_{\alpha}R\right)^{2}\right],

(1)

where
\left\{\gamma_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}
are the system-bath
couplings (or frictions), and
V\left(R\right)
is the potential along the
reactive coordinate
R
and is modelled harmonically near the barrier
and well bottom (with imaginary frequency
i\omega_{b}
and real
frequency
\omega_{v}
respectively;
\omega_{b},\omega_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
).
It is convenient to work in the normal mode basis around the barrier
region, which comprises one unstable mode
u
of imaginary frequency

i\Omega_{b}
(
\Omega_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
) and
\mathcal{N}

stable modes
\left\{s_{k}\right\}
of real frequencies
\left\{\Omega_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\right\}
.
This implies that (1) the reaction occurs along the unstable mode
since it has a barrier (of frequency
\Omega_{b}
), and (2) in the
weak system-bath coupling limit, the reactive mode is composed mostly
of the unstable mode such that contributions from the stable modes
may be used to characterise these system-bath couplings. Mathematically,
if we define
R=Q_{1}=\cdots=Q_{\mathcal{N}}=0
when the particle
is at the barrier and expand
R
in the stable-unstable mode basis
as

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=c_{00}u+\sum_{k=1}^{\mathcal{N}}c_{0k}s_{k}

(2)

with
c_{0k}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
k=0,1,\cdots,\mathcal{N}
and

\sum_{k=0}^{\mathcal{N}}c_{0k}^{2}=1
, then the total system-bath
coupling may be characterised by

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\frac{c_{0k}^{2}}{c_{00}^{2}}=\frac{1}{c_{00}^{2}}-1.

(3)

PGH theory focuses on the weak system-bath coupling limit (
\epsilon\ll 1
),
so we expect larger
\epsilon
to improve energy exchange between
system and bath modes and therefore increase the reaction rate. Finally,
by analysing the energy flow between the stable and unstable modes
during the particle’s path in the well (during which the stable and
unstable modes are no longer normal modes), the reaction rate is predicted
to be
(
Pollak et al. 1989
)

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=\kappa k_{\text{1DTST}},

(4)

where
k_{\text{1DTST}}=\frac{\omega_{v}}{2\pi}e^{-\beta E_{b}}

is the rate calculated using 1D transition state theory (1D-TST) and
is independent of system-bath couplings, and

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{b}}{\omega_{b}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\left[\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dy}{1+y^{2}}\,\ln\left(1-e^{-\left(\beta\Delta E\right)\frac{1+y^{2}}{4}}\right)\right]

(5)

is the transmission factor due to system-bath couplings and serves
as a proxy measure of the bath’s effects on the reaction rate. Here,

\beta=\left(k_{\text{B}}T\right)^{-1}
, with
T
as temperature. Also,

\Delta E
characterises the system-bath energy exchange and has
the form

\displaystyle\Delta E

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\frac{c_{0k}^{2}}{c_{00}^{2}}\left|\tilde{F}\left(\Omega_{k}\right)\right|^{2},

(6)

where
\tilde{F}\left(\Omega_{k}\right)
is the Fourier transform of the
effective force experienced by the unstable mode. The latter may be
solved analytically for a piecewise differentiable parabolic potential
of the form

\displaystyle V\left(R\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\omega_{v}^{2}\left(R-R_{\text{eq}}\right)^{2}&R\leq R^{\prime},\\
-\frac{1}{2}\omega_{b}^{2}R^{2}+E_{b}&R>R^{\prime},\end{cases}

(7)

where
R_{\text{eq}}=-\sqrt{2E_{b}\left(1/\omega_{v}^{2}+1/\omega_{b}^{2}\right)}

and
R^{\prime}=R_{\text{eq}}\omega_{v}^{2}/\left(\omega_{v}^{2}+\omega_{b}^{2}\right)

are derived from making both
V\left(R\right)
and
\partial_{R}V\left(R\right)
continuous
at
R=R^{\prime}
(thus giving two conditions that solve for two unknowns).
The result is

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{F}\left(\Omega_{k}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\left(R^{\prime}/c_{00}\right)^{2}\Omega_{b}^{2}\left(\omega_{\text{eff}}^{2}+\Omega_{b}^{2}\right)^{2}}{\Omega_{k}^{2}\left(\Omega_{k}^{2}-\omega_{\text{eff}}^{2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\Omega_{b}\sin\left(\Omega_{k}\tau/2\right)+\Omega_{k}\cos\left(\Omega_{k}\tau/2\right)\right]^{2},

(8)

where
\omega_{\text{eff}}=\sqrt{c_{00}^{2}\left(\omega_{v}^{2}+\omega_{b}^{2}\right)-\Omega_{b}^{2}}

is the effective well frequency experienced by the unstable mode,
and
\tau
is the system-bath interaction time obtained by solving
the coupled equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\cos\omega_{\text{eff}}\tau=\left(\Omega_{b}^{2}-\omega_{\text{eff}}^{2}\right)/\left(\Omega_{b}^{2}+\omega_{\text{eff}}^{2}\right),\\
\sin\omega_{\text{eff}}\tau=-\left(2\Omega_{b}\omega_{\text{eff}}\right)/\left(\Omega_{b}^{2}+\omega_{\text{eff}}^{2}\right).\end{cases}

Focusing on Eqs. (
4
), (
5
) and (
6
),
we find that stronger system-bath couplings, characterised by
\epsilon=\sum_{k=1}^{\mathcal{N}}c_{0k}^{2}/c_{00}^{2}
,
facilitate energy exchange
\Delta E
between the modes and also
modify the unstable mode barrier frequency
\Omega_{b}
. While the
former increases
\kappa
, the reaction rate relative to that from
1D-TST, the latter may change
\kappa
in either directions.

To apply PGH theory to VSC, Lindoy et al. considered the reactive
mode (with finite barrier like Eq. (
7
)) to be bilinearly
coupled to both the harmonic molecular bath and a single cavity mode

(
Lindoy et al. 2022
)
. This bilinear light-matter interaction originates
from the Pauli-Fierz nonrelativistic QED Hamiltonian
(
Flick et al. 2017
;
Rokaj et al. 2018
;
Schäfer et al. 2020
)

and may be interpreted as a cavity-induced friction in the PGH framework
(i.e. the cavity mode acts as an effective bath mode). Since the reactive
mode belongs to a single reacting molecule, its harmonic molecular
bath corresponds to its “solvent” environment. Chemical reactions
are rare events, and it is unlikely for two molecules to react simultaneously.
As such, to extend this model to the collective regime, we need to
include
N-1
other non-reacting molecules, each with its own harmonic
(and, therefore, non-reactive) vibrational mode also coupled bilinearly
to the
same
cavity mode and its own
separate
harmonic
molecular bath. (Alternatively, this model may be interpreted as explicitly
describing the set-up of “cooperative VSC”, whereby a small amount
of reactive species is placed in a sea of chemically inert molecules,
all of which couple to the cavity in the same fashion
(
Lather et al. 2019
;
Lather and George 2021
;
Wiesehan and Xiong 2021
;
Lather et al. 2022
)
.)

In the following paragraphs, we will show that, through a normal mode
transformation, we may rewrite the subsystem comprising the cavity
and
N-1
non-reactive molecules into a pair of polariton modes bilinearly
coupled to the reactive mode. Just like the single-molecule models

(
Lindoy et al. 2022
;
Sun and Vendrell 2022
;
Philbin et al. 2022
)
, these bilinear couplings
represent cavity-induced frictions, which accelerate the reaction
in the low-friction regime.

The Hamiltonian describing the above model is, in mass-weighted coordinates,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=T_{\text{r}}+V_{\text{r}}\left(R\right)+H_{\text{nr-c}}+H_{\text{r-c}}+H_{\text{bath}},

(9)

where
T_{\text{r}}=\dot{R}^{2}/2
is the kinetic energy of the reactive
mode with coordinate
R
,
V_{\text{r}}\left(R\right)
is the piecewise differentiable
parabolic potential of the reactive mode as described by Eq. (
7
)
(well frequency
\omega_{v}
, barrier frequency
\omega_{b}
),

\displaystyle H_{\text{nr-c}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\left(\frac{\dot{X}_{j}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\omega_{v}^{2}}{2}X_{j}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\dot{q}_{c}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\omega_{c}q_{c}+2g\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}X_{j}\right)^{2}

(10)

represents the couplings between the cavity mode (coordinate
q_{c}
,
frequency
\omega_{c}
) and
N-1
non-reactive vibrational modes
(coordinates
\left\{X_{j}\right\}
, frequency
\omega_{v}
), as well as
their kinetic and potential energies,

\displaystyle H_{\text{r-c}}

\displaystyle=\left(\omega_{c}q_{c}+2g\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}X_{j}\right)2gR+2g^{2}R^{2}

(11)

represents the couplings between the reactive mode and the subsystem
of cavity and
N-1
non-reactive vibrational modes, and
H_{\text{bath}}

represents all
N
sets of molecular bath modes that belong to the
single reactive and
N-1
non-reactive vibrational modes. As mentioned
earlier, this Hamiltonian may be derived from the cavity QED Hamiltonian
in the dipole gauge, under the cavity Born-Oppenheimer approximation,
after assuming all molecules to be in the adiabatic electronic ground
state
(
Flick et al. 2017
;
Rokaj et al. 2018
;
Schäfer et al. 2020
)
; more details may
be found in Ref.
(
Li et al. 2021
)
. Here, we consider only a single
cavity mode and assume that all
N-1
non-reactive vibrational modes
have the same spatial alignments and frequencies
\omega_{v}
as
the potential well of the reactive mode (the result remains qualitatively
unchanged under isotropic alignment of dipoles and will be explained
later). As such, all molecules couple equally to the cavity mode,
each with the same coupling amplitude of
g=-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}/\sqrt{4\epsilon_{0}\mathcal{V}}
,
where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is the polarisation unit vector of
the cavity mode,
\mathcal{V}
is the cavity’s effective quantisation
volume, and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}^{\prime}
is the linear change of the dipole
moment along each vibrational mode near its equilibrium position (well
bottom for the reactive mode), identical for all modes (i.e.
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{r}}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}^{\prime}

for all
j=1,\cdots,N-1
). While
H_{\text{nr-c}}+H_{\text{r-c}}

[Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
)], which describe
couplings to the reactive mode, is not yet in the form of
H_{\text{PGH}}

[Eq. (
1
)], a normal mode transformation will do
the trick. Exploiting the symmetries created by the assumptions above,
we can rewrite the
N-1
degenerate non-reactive vibrational modes
into a single bright mode with coordinate

\displaystyle Q_{\text{B}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}X_{j}

(12)

and
N-2
dark modes with coordinates

\displaystyle Q_{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}A_{j,\zeta}X_{j},

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=2,\cdots,N-1,

where the coefficients
\left\{A_{j,\zeta}\right\}
are real-valued for
all
j
and
\zeta
and satisfy the orthonormality conditions of

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}A_{j,\zeta}

\displaystyle=0

and

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{A_{j,\zeta}A_{j,\eta}}{N-1}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\zeta\eta},

with
\delta_{\zeta\eta}
representing the Kronecker delta. Then,

H_{\text{nr-c}}
[Eq. (
10
)] becomes

\displaystyle H_{\text{nr-c}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\zeta=2}^{N-1}\left(\frac{\dot{Q}_{\zeta}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\omega_{v}^{2}}{2}Q_{\zeta}^{2}\right)+\frac{\dot{Q}_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\omega_{v}^{2}}{2}Q_{\text{B}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\dot{q}_{c}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\omega_{c}q_{c}+2g\sqrt{N-1}Q_{\text{B}}\right)^{2},

(13)

i.e. only the bright mode has the correct symmetry to couple with
the cavity. Performing a normal mode transformation on these two modes
gives two polariton modes with coordinates
Q_{\pm}
and frequencies

\omega_{\pm}
(see Appendix 1). By expressing
H_{\text{nr-c}}

[Eq. (
13
)] and
H_{\text{r-c}}
[Eq. (
11
)]
in terms of the polariton modes, the Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{\text{eff}}+H_{\text{bath}}+\sum_{\zeta=2}^{N-1}\left(\frac{\dot{Q}_{\zeta}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\omega_{v}^{2}}{2}Q_{\zeta}^{2}\right),

(14)

where

\displaystyle H_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{R}^{2}}{2}+V_{\text{r}}\left(R\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\left[\frac{\dot{Q}_{\alpha}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\omega_{\alpha}Q_{\alpha}+2g_{\alpha}R\right)^{2}\right],

(15)

such that the subsystem of cavity and
N-1
non-reactive vibrational
modes forms a pair of effective polariton bath modes (coordinates

Q_{\pm}
) that interact with the reactive mode through couplings

g_{\pm}
. These system-polariton couplings
g_{\pm}
are analogous
to the cavity-induced friction
g
described by single-molecule models

(
Sun and Vendrell 2022
;
Lindoy et al. 2022
;
Philbin et al. 2022
)
(for instance, compare Eq.
(
15
) with Eq. (3) of Ref.
(
Lindoy et al. 2022
)
, which
denotes
g
by
\eta_{c}\sqrt{\omega_{c}/2}
). Note that the dark
modes (coordinates
Q_{2},\cdots,Q_{N-1}
) do not couple to the reactive
mode and may be neglected in our future analysis.

molecules, but only through the cavity, thus making this a second-order
process with intensities proportional to the product of the two sub-processes’
couplings:
g^{2}
and
g^{2}\left(N-1\right)
. This effect is captured
in the series expansion of system-polariton couplings
g_{\pm}
[Eq.
(
16
)] and is an important part of the polariton
“large
N
problem”.

g\sqrt{N-1}
, the system-polariton

g_{\pm}
(i.e. cavity-induced frictions), the total system-polariton

\epsilon
and the unstable mode barrier frequency
\Omega_{b}
,

\omega_{c}\approx\omega_{v}

\displaystyle g_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{\sqrt{2}}\pm\frac{g^{2}\sqrt{N-1}}{2\sqrt{2}\omega_{v}}+\mathcal{O}\left(g^{3}\left(N-1\right)\right),

and

\displaystyle\Omega_{b}

\displaystyle=\omega_{b}-\frac{2\omega_{b}g^{2}}{\omega_{v}^{2}+\omega_{b}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(g^{4}\left(N-1\right)\right)

(18)

(see Appendix 2, which uses Ref.
(
O’Leary and Stewart 1990
)
). The first
term in
g_{\pm}
represents the single-molecule light-matter coupling

g
, a first-order process that dominates reaction dynamics and characterises
the cavity-induced friction in single-molecule models
(
Lindoy et al. 2022
;
Philbin et al. 2022
)
.
The second term represents couplings between the reactive and
N-1

non-reactive vibrational modes, a second-order process characterised
by
g\left(g\sqrt{N-1}\right)
. Notice from the expansion coefficients that
light-matter coupling enhances the reaction rate through
\epsilon

and retards the reaction rate through
\Omega_{b}
, observations
that concur with the single-molecule analysis
(
Lindoy et al. 2022
)
.
Regardless, to leading order in
g\sqrt{N-1}
, all three parameters
depend only on
g
, the single-molecule coupling, and not on
N
.
For a fixed collective light-matter coupling
g\sqrt{N-1}\approx g\sqrt{N}

– the experimentally measurable parameter – the cavity’s effects
(including the friction
g_{\pm}
) diminish as
N
grows. Such perturbative
results have been observed previously
(
Poh et al. 2022
;
Kansanen 2022
;
Yang and Cao 2021
)

and is a reminder of the polariton “large
N
problem”
(
Martínez-Martínez et al. 2019
)
,
i.e. cavity effects under VSC are mostly characterised by the single-molecule
coupling
g
, a small parameter due to weak photon confinements in
microcavities, and should not be confused with the collective coupling

g\sqrt{N}
, which is much larger and hence experimentally observable
[Fig. (
2
)].

A few comments are now in order:

a.

We note that, by keeping
g\sqrt{N}
constant at an experimentally-feasible
value, it follows that
g
must be tiny as
N\rightarrow\infty
.
The importance of this work, however, is not what happens when
N

is infinitely large, but rather what happens when experimental values
of
N
is considered, which happens to be large at
\approx 10^{6}-10^{12}

(
del Pino et al. 2015a
;
Daskalakis et al. 2017
)
. Taking this approach allows us to
emphasise the errors incurred by single-molecule models
(
Sun and Vendrell 2022
;
Lindoy et al. 2022
;
Philbin et al. 2022
)
,
which disregard contributions from non-reacting molecules and even
treats the light-matter coupling as the collective one (i.e. incorrectly
set
g\approx g\sqrt{N}
). From another perspective, since
g\sqrt{N}\approx 10^{-2}\omega_{v}
,
experimental values of
g
should be estimated as
\approx\left(10^{-5}-10^{-8}\right)\omega_{v}
,
a tiny value due to weak photon confinements in microcavities.

b.

Even if
g
were to be kept constant at an
appreciable
value
(which,
we emphasise
, has only been reported in nanoplasmonic
cavities
(
Chikkaraddy et al. 2016
;
Bitton and Haran 2022
)
and does
not
coincide
with experimental observations of rate enhancements),
\kappa
still
decreases with increasing
N
since the growing ensemble reduces
the cavity’s catalytic efficiency towards the single reacting molecule.
This effect is noteworthy in showing how single-molecule models are
not only limited by their choice of
g
but also the absence of entropic
effects from an explicit consideration of the ensemble.

c.

Note that, unlike Refs.
(
Galego et al. 2019
;
Campos-Gonzalez-Angulo and Yuen-Zhou 2020
)
,
the discussion above remains unchanged if the reactive and
N-1

non-reactive vibrational modes have isotropic spatial alignments.
In that case, the single-molecule light-matter coupling
g=-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}/\sqrt{4\epsilon_{0}\mathcal{V}}

is replaced by a weighted coupling
-\sqrt{\langle\mu_{j}^{\prime 2}\rangle_{N-1}}/\sqrt{4\epsilon_{0}\mathcal{V}}
,
whereby
\langle\mu_{j}^{\prime 2}\rangle_{N-1}\equiv\left(N-1\right)^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right)^{2}

reflects the average vibrational mode alignment
-squared
and
thus does not vanish in the isotropic limit (
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}^{\prime}

is the linear change in the dipole moment of molecule
j
). Of course,
the cavity effects still vanish with realistic values of
N
.

d.

Also, given how the studied cavity effects depend on
g
and not

g\sqrt{N}
to leading order of the latter term (see Eqs. (
16
),
(
17
) and (
18
)),
it is unlikely that disorder will significantly affect the reaction
rate. Indeed, simulations conducted for
N\leq 3000
molecules showed
little rate modifications due to disorder.

e.

Interestingly, results from the single molecule model
(
Lindoy et al. 2022
)

remained qualitatively unchanged when multiple cavity modes were considered,
with the exception of sharper cavity resonances and weaker cavity
effects
(
Philbin et al. 2022
)
. As such, our collective VSC analysis
should also hold beyond the single cavity mode limit, provided that
the number of molecules per photon mode remains close to the values
of
N
here studied
(
del Pino et al. 2015b
;
Daskalakis et al. 2017
)
.

f.

Finally, our model and calculations do not show cavity enhancement
effects of friction due to spatial delocalisation of eigenstates
(
Cao 2022
)
.
We expect these effects to only increase reaction rates by a moderate
amount (as discussed in Supplementary Information S2.3 of Ref.
(
Du et al. 2018
)
).
Furthermore, these effects will only arise if there are additional
near-field electrostatic interactions among molecules, which have
been ignored in this model in light of how weak they are in the vibrational
regime.

Conclusion

In conclusion, thermal reaction rate models, such as the PGH theory,
offer a possible explanation for changes in chemical kinetics within
the single-molecule VSC model
(
Philbin et al. 2022
;
Lindoy et al. 2022
;
Sun and Vendrell 2022
)
.
Unfortunately, this explanation breaks down with collective VSC commonly
found in vibropolaritonic chemistry experiments. In this regime,
N\approx 10^{6}-10^{12}

molecules simultaneously couple to the cavity
(
del Pino et al. 2015a
;
Daskalakis et al. 2017
)
.
As such, the single reacting molecule experiences only a tiny
1/N

part of the experimentally-observed light-matter interaction, the
remaining of which is shared among the macroscopic number (
N-1
)
of non-reacting molecules, thus negating most rate effects due to
the cavity. Overall, there remains little satisfactory explanation
for rate modifications observed in vibropolaritonic chemistry experiments
and the search continues.

Appendix 1: Deriving polariton modes from the
subsystem of cavity and bright modes

Here, we outline the derivation of the polariton modes (coordinates

Q_{\pm}
) through a normal mode transformation of the cavity mode
and bright mode (coordinates
q_{c}
and
Q_{\text{B}}
respectively).
As an example, we work in the zero cavity detuning limit (
\omega_{c}=\omega_{v}
),
but the same principle applies for any general cavity frequency
\omega_{c}
.
Starting from
H_{\text{nr-c}}
[Eq. (
13
)]
and disregarding the dark modes, we define

\displaystyle H_{\text{nr-c}}^{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=H_{\text{nr-c}}-\sum_{\zeta=2}^{N-1}\left(\frac{\dot{Q}_{\zeta}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\omega_{v}^{2}}{2}Q_{\zeta}^{2}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{Q}_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\dot{q}_{c}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\omega_{v}^{2}}{2}Q_{\text{B}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\omega_{v}q_{c}+2g\sqrt{N-1}Q_{\text{B}}\right)^{2}

(20)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\dot{Q}_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\dot{q}_{c}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l=0,1}x_{k}\mathcal{H}_{kl}x_{l},

(21)

where
x_{0}=q_{c}
and
x_{1}=Q_{\text{B}}
, and diagonalise the
Hessian matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\omega_{v}^{2}&2\omega_{v}g\sqrt{N-1}\\
2\omega_{v}g\sqrt{N-1}&\omega_{v}^{2}+4g^{2}\left(N-1\right)\end{array}\right)

to get the polariton eigenvalues and (normalised) eigenvectors as

\displaystyle\omega_{\pm}^{2}

\displaystyle=\omega_{v}^{2}+2g^{2}\left(N-1\right)\pm 2g\sqrt{N-1}\sqrt{g^{2}\left(N-1\right)+\omega_{v}^{2}},

(24)

\displaystyle Q_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{g\sqrt{N-1}}{\omega_{v}}\pm\sqrt{1+\frac{g^{2}\left(N-1\right)}{\omega_{v}^{2}}}\right)\frac{q_{c}}{K_{\pm}}+\frac{Q_{\text{B}}}{K_{\pm}},

(25)

with normalisation constants

\displaystyle K_{\text{$\pm$}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2+\frac{2g^{2}\left(N-1\right)}{\omega_{v}^{2}}\mp\frac{2g\sqrt{N-1}}{\omega_{v}}\sqrt{1+\frac{g^{2}\left(N-1\right)}{\omega_{v}^{2}}}}.

(26)

Note that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}
give the square of the
polariton mode frequencies. Also, we have assumed
g\geq 0
without
loss of generality. We may then write
H_{\text{nr-c}}^{\text{eff}}

[Eq. (
20
)] in terms of these polariton normal
modes as

\displaystyle H_{\text{nr-c}}^{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\left(\frac{\dot{Q}_{\alpha}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\omega_{\alpha}^{2}}{2}Q_{\alpha}^{2}\right).

(27)

Next, noting from Eq. (
25
) that, after
(re-)normalisation
,

\displaystyle q_{c}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{g\sqrt{N-1}}{\omega_{v}}-\sqrt{1+\frac{g^{2}\left(N-1\right)}{\omega_{v}^{2}}}\right)\frac{Q_{-}}{K_{-}}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(-\frac{g\sqrt{N-1}}{\omega_{v}}+\sqrt{1+\frac{g^{2}\left(N-1\right)}{\omega_{v}^{2}}}\right)\frac{Q_{+}}{K_{+}},

(28)

\displaystyle Q_{\text{B}}

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{-}}{K_{-}}+\frac{Q_{+}}{K_{+}},

(29)

we write
H_{\text{r-c}}
[Eq. (
11
)] in terms of
the polariton modes to get

\displaystyle H_{\text{r-c}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\left(2g_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}Q_{\alpha}R+2g_{\alpha}^{2}R^{2}\right),

(30)

where

\displaystyle g_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{\omega_{\pm}K_{\pm}}\left(g\sqrt{N-1}\pm\sqrt{\omega_{v}^{2}+g^{2}\left(N-1\right)}\right),

(31)

are the system-polariton couplings and we have noted that
g_{+}^{2}+g_{-}^{2}=g^{2}
.
Combining Eqs. (
9
), (
19
),
(
27
) and (
30
) and completing
the squares give Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
).

Appendix 2: Series expansions of
g_{\pm}
,
\epsilon

and
\Omega_{b}

Here, we outline the approach taken to expand
g_{\pm}
,
\epsilon

and
\Omega_{b}
in orders of
g\sqrt{N-1}
. For simplicity, we
set the cavity detuning as zero, i.e.
\omega_{c}=\omega_{v}
. Then,

g_{\pm}
may be expanded directly from Eq. (
31
) to get
Eq. (
16
). Next, we find the unstable and stable
modes by performing a normal mode transformation on the Hamiltonian

H_{\text{eff}}
[Eq. (
15
)] near the barrier region.
Noting that
V\left(R\right)\approx-\frac{1}{2}\omega_{b}^{2}R^{2}+E_{b}

in this region [Eq. (
7
)], we define

\displaystyle H_{\text{eff}}^{\left(b\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{R}}{2}+\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\frac{\dot{Q}_{\alpha}^{2}}{2}+E_{b}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l=0,1,2}y_{k}K_{kl}^{\left(b\right)}y_{l},

(32)

with
y_{0}=R
,
y_{1}=Q_{-}
and
y_{2}=Q_{+}
, and diagonalise
the force constant matrix

\displaystyle K^{\left(b\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}-\omega_{b}^{2}+4g_{-}^{2}+4g_{+}^{2}&2g_{-}\omega_{-}&2g_{+}\omega_{+}\\
2g_{-}\omega_{-}&\omega_{-}^{2}&0\\
2g_{+}\omega_{+}&0&\omega_{+}^{2}\end{array}\right)

to get an unstable coordinate
u
with negative eigenvalue
-\Omega_{b}^{2}

(
\Omega_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
) and two stable coordinates
\left\{s_{k}\right\}

with positive eigenvalues
\left\{\Omega_{k}^{2}\right\}
(
\Omega_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
).
Since
K^{\left(b\right)}
is an arrowhead matrix, the secular equation and
modal matrix (which are used to find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors)
have simple forms
(
O’Leary and Stewart 1990
)
. In particular,
-\Omega_{b}^{2}

satisfy the following equation

\displaystyle\Omega_{b}^{2}

\displaystyle=\omega_{b}^{2}\left(1+\frac{4g_{-}^{2}}{\omega_{-}^{2}+\Omega_{b}^{2}}+\frac{4g_{+}^{2}}{\omega_{+}^{2}+\Omega_{b}^{2}}\right)^{-1},

(36)

which is solved using perturbation theory in orders of
g\sqrt{N-1}

to get Eq. (
18
). Also,
c_{00}
, the contribution
of
u
to
R
, can be found from the modal matrix as

\displaystyle c_{00}

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{2g_{-}\omega_{-}}{-\Omega_{b}^{2}-\omega_{-}^{2}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{2g_{+}\omega_{+}}{-\Omega_{b}^{2}-\omega_{+}^{2}}\right)^{2}}\right)^{-1},

(37)

which, using Eq. (
18
) and noting the definition
of
\epsilon
in Eq. (
3
), gives Eq. (
17
)
through a series expansion in
g\sqrt{N-1}
.
