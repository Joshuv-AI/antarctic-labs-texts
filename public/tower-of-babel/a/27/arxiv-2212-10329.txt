Abstract
. Owing to their interesting spectral properties, the synthetic crystals over lattices other than regular Euclidean lattices, such as hyperbolic and fractal ones, have attracted renewed attention, especially from materials and meta-materials research communities. They can be studied under the umbrella of quantum dynamics over Cayley graphs of finitely generated groups. In this work, we investigate numerical aspects related to the quantum dynamics over such Cayley graphs. Using an algebraic formulation of the “periodic boundary condition” due to Lück [Geom. Funct. Anal. 4 , 455–481 (1994)], we devise a practical and converging numerical method that resolves the true bulk spectrum of the Hamiltonians. Exact results on the matrix elements of the resolvent, derived from the combinatorics of the Cayley graphs, give us the means to validate our algorithms and also to obtain new combinatorial statements. Our results open the systematic research of quantum dynamics over Cayley graphs of a very large family of finitely generated groups, which includes the free and Fuchsian groups.

Introduction and Main Statements

Given a discrete group
G
and a finite subset
S\subset G
, its associated Cayley digraph
{\mathcal{C}}(G,S)
is the colored graph with vertex set

and directed edges from

, with one distinct color for each edge produced by

. The standard Cayley graph can be often represented as a geometric graph rendered in our physical space and, when this is possible, it encodes the entire algebraic information on

in a geometric fashion. Quite often, difficult algebraic and analytic problems can be solved using the geometries of the Cayley graphs
[
47
]
. For example, in this work, we examine the close relation between the spectral characteristics of the quantum dynamics over a Cayley graph and the combinatorics of the graph. In the same time, Cayley graphs are an abundant source of interesting lattices that can be used to systematically explore the world of discrete patterns
[
33
,
13
]
. In materials science, this is interesting because the current technologies are at a point where fabrications of such lattices are feasible at many different length scales. As such, we can observe and learn from the synthetic quantum or classical dynamics set in play by the degrees of freedom of these new physical systems. In practice, this amounts to placing quantum resonators at the nodes or edges of the lattices and coupling these resonators in a fashion that respects the symmetries of the graphs. For example, in a natural crystal, the quantum resonators are the atoms themselves and the crystal’s sites are determined by a decorated Cayley graph generated from an Euclidean space group. Synthetic hyperbolic crystals can and have been fabricated by rendering superconducting circuits over a Cayley graph generated from a Fuchsian group
[
32
]
. Classical resonators, rendered and weakly coupled over a Cayley graph, generate a dynamics that is akin to a quantum dynamics (see
[

,

,
60
,
17
]
for examples relevant to the present context). It was also shown
[

that any quantum dynamics over a Cayley graph can be reproduced with stochastic dynamics over a decorated version of the same graph.

The Hilbert space for quantum dynamics over a Cayley graph of a discrete group
G
is
\ell^{2}(G)
. It accepts the orthonormal basis
|g\rangle
,
g\in G
, and the group
G
acts on
\ell^{2}(G)
via either the right regular representation,
\pi_{R}(g)|g^{\prime}\rangle=|g^{\prime}g^{-1}\rangle
, or the left regular representation
\pi_{L}(g)|g^{\prime}\rangle=|gg^{\prime}\rangle
. The generic Hamiltonians take the form
H=\sum_{g,g^{\prime}\in G}w_{g^{\prime},g}\,|g^{\prime}\rangle\langle g|
and self-adjointness imposes the constraint
w_{g,g^{\prime}}=w_{g^{\prime},g}^{\ast}
. Furthermore, if the quantum dynamics is invariant against the right action of the group
G
, the coefficients of the Hamiltonian must display the additional constraint
w_{g^{\prime}h,gh}=w_{g^{\prime},g}
for any
g
,
g^{\prime}
and

from
G

3
, any such

can be generated as the left regular representation of the element
\sum_{q\in G}w_{q,1}\cdot q
from the group algebra
{\mathbb{C}}G
. This is how the group algebra enters the picture. The case
G={\mathbb{Z}}
, the group of integers, supplies simple examples. Indeed, the Cayley graph of
{\mathbb{Z}}
is the regular 1-dimensional lattice and a translational invariant Hamiltonian takes the form

H=\sum\nolimits_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum\nolimits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\big(t_{k}|n+k\rangle\langle n|+t^{\ast}_{k}|n\rangle\langle n+k|\big),

(1.1)

where the sum over
k
is finite. Using the shift operator
T=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|n+1\rangle\langle n|
, this Hamiltonian can be conveniently written as
H=\sum\nolimits_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\big(t_{k}T^{k}+t^{\ast}_{k}T^{-k}\big)
and, since
T|n\rangle=|n+1\rangle
, we see that

is just the left representation of 1, the generator of
{\mathbb{Z}}
. We will use this example below to demonstrate how periodic boundary conditions can be formulated in an algebraic manner.

In the era of topological quantum materials, the most interesting feature of a quantum dynamics is the structure of the gaps in its resonant spectrum, which will be referred to as bulk spectral gaps if the sample is infinite. Indeed, with or without synthetic gauge fields, these gaps can carry topological invariants, typically detected by the
K
-theory of the underlying algebra of physical observables
[
10
,
11
,
29
]
. Nontrivial topological invariants set in action interesting physics in the presence of lattice defects
[
55
]
. Therefore, one of the important tasks when studying the quantum dynamics over a graph is resolving the gaps in its bulk spectrum. In a laboratory or in a computer simulation, one can only deal with a finite graph and, without proper boundary conditions, the resonant spectrum is contaminated by boundary modes and the detection of bulk spectral gaps may become impossible. This is particularly acute for the free and Fuchsian groups, as well as for any other non-amenable group, for which the ratio between the sites located at the boundary and the total number of sites of a truncated Cayley graph does not decrease to zero with the size of the truncated graph (see the estimate in Remark
2.5
). In such cases, it is quite obvious that specialized boundary conditions are needed in order to converge the spectrum or the spectral density of a finite quantum model to its thermodynamic limit, and this is the subject of our paper.

Even before approaching this problem of convergence, there is the fundamental question about the nature of the bulk spectrum. Indeed, while the generators of the quantum dynamics over a Cayley graph are drawn from the group algebra
{\mathbb{C}}G
, in order to do spectral theory and functional calculus, this algebra needs to be completed to a
C^{\ast}
-algebra. For non-amenable groups, there are two distinct options, the full and the reduced group
C^{\ast}
-algebras
[
19
, Sec. VII2]
. We recall that, for a non-amenable group, a functorial relation exists only between the group and its full
C^{\ast}
-algebra. For example, the irreducible representations of the group are in one-to-one relation with the representations of the full group
C^{\ast}
-algebra
[
19
, p. 184]
, while the representations of the reduced group
C^{\ast}
-algebra are generally very poor. As such, the spectrum of an element from
{\mathbb{C}}G
depends on which of these two algebras is used (see Example
3.11
). The dynamics on
\ell^{2}(G)
produced by the Hamiltonians mentioned in second paragraph, and observed in the typical experiments, takes place in the reduced group
C^{\ast}
-algebra, as canonically embedded as a sub-algebra of
{\mathbb{B}}(\ell^{2}(G))
, the algebra of bounded operators on
\ell^{2}(G)
(see subsection
3.2
). Indeed, any operator produced by a continuous functional calculus with
H
stays in the reduced group
C^{\ast}
-algebra. In particular, this is the case for the resolvent
(H-z)^{-1}
which determines the spectrum of
H
. Hence, there is an additional constraint on the boundary conditions, in that they not only need to obstruct the boundary modes from appearing, but they also have to ensure that the finite models converge to infinite models drawn from the reduced
C^{\ast}
-algebra of the group. In our section
5
, the reader can find numerical examples where different boundary conditions drive the calculations into both mentioned
C^{\ast}
-algebras.

The above issues can be attacked from the combinatorial side and, for the particular case of free groups, McKay has provided in
[
46
]
an explicit criterion that ensures the correct spectral convergence for a sequence of finite graph approximations. The criterion is actually very simple: The finite graph approximations have to be regular and the ratio between their number of closed loops and their number of vertices needs to converge to zero. The effectiveness of this criterion was put to the test in
[
61
]
and the numerical evidence there is promising. Unfortunately, to our knowledge, this criterion does not afford a generalization to the case when group relations are present and finite closed loops appear in the graph. For these cases, we turned to the algebraic solution introduced by Lück in
[

]
(see also

]

\langle g|(H-z)^{-1}|g^{\prime}\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\phi_{n}(g)|(H_{n}-z)^{-1}|\phi_{n}(g^{\prime})\rangle,\quad Im(z)\neq 0.

(1.2)

\begin{pmatrix}1+a&b\\

(1.3)

Likewise,

2.
Finitely generated groups and their Cayley graphs

Finitely generated groups are groups that can be presented in terms of finite sets of generators and relations, that is, as quotients of free groups by normal subgroups (the normal closures of the relations). Thus, the point of departure for this class of groups is the class of free groups, which we consider first into some detail. A pedagogical introduction to the subject can be found in
[
35
]
, which we use below to state basic facts and to fix the terminology and notation.

2.2.
Finitely generated groups

Given any finite set

,

{\mathbb{F}}({\mathcal{G}})\simeq{\mathbb{F}}_{n}

Definition 2.8

The group generated by the finite set
{\mathcal{G}}
and finite set of relations

{\mathbb{F}}({\mathcal{G}})/\widehat{\mathcal{R}}

Remark 2.9
.

We will always assume that the generating set
{\mathcal{G}}
has the smallest cardinality possible. Hence, the ranks of the finitely generated groups will always coincide with the cardinal
|{\mathcal{G}}|
of the generating set.

2.3.
Cayley graphs

Cayley graphs encode the data of a group in a geometric fashion
[
47
]
. For example, word problems and other theoretical problems in group theory can be solved by inspecting these geometric objects
[
47
, Ch. 5]
. On the applied side, Cayley graphs supply systematic generalizations of the crystal lattices investigated in materials science. Hence they can be an abundant source of new dynamical effects, which is our main motivation for studying them.

Definition 2.13
.

Given a discrete group
G
and a finite subset

of
G

is the un-directed graph with vertex set

and edge set containing an
edge between

and

g\in G
and
s\in S
.

One should be aware that

can be any finite subset of

and that the geometry of the Cayley graph depends quite strongly on the choice of

. When the group has a standard presentation in terms of generators and relations, there is special graph
{\mathcal{C}}(G,{\mathcal{G}})
which we call here the standard Cayley graph and denote it simply by
{\mathcal{C}}(G)
. As we shall see, however, for a given model Hamiltonian, the useful Cayley graph is the one constructed from the group elements present in the expression of the Hamiltonian.

Remark 2.18

The standard Cayley graphs and diagraphs displayed above are distorted by our insistence to embed them in the 2-dimensional Euclidean plane. However, in the true world of these graphs, where only the connectivities count, they will appear entirely the same when observed from any of its vertices. Unfortunately, this is not always apparent from the geometric rendering of the graphs, yet it is a fact that can help us understanding the structure of these complex graphs. Note also that the standard graph of a finitely generated group is necessarily a regular graph whose degree equals the rank of the group.

3.
Quantum dynamics over Cayley graphs

3.1.
Patterns of quantum resonators

In our physical world, we can generate a quantum dynamics over a Cayley graph
{\mathcal{C}}(G)
by simply placing quantum resonators at either the vertices or the edges of the graph. For example, in an ordinary solid state crystal, the atoms can be viewed as quantum resonators sitting at the vertices of a graph generated from the appropriate space symmetry group. Synthetic quantum resonators, such as those in circuit quantum electrodynamics
[
12
]
, can be and have been rendered and coupled in other graph configurations
[
32
]
.

We assume now that identical single-state quantum resonators have been placed at the vertices of a generic Cayley graph labeled by the elements of a finitely generated group
G
. In the one particle sector, the collective dynamics of the resonators takes place inside the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(G)
of square summable sequences over the graph, spanned by the vectors
|g\rangle
,
g\in G
. The dynamics is generated by a bounded and self-adjoint operator over this Hilbert space, which can always be written as a strongly convergent expansion

H=\sum_{g,g^{\prime}\in G}w_{g^{\prime},g}\,|g^{\prime}\rangle\langle g|,\quad w_{g,g^{\prime}}=w_{g^{\prime},g}^{\ast}.

(3.1)

The parameters
w_{g^{\prime},g}\in{\mathbb{C}}
are often called the coupling coefficients.

Remark 3.1
.

The physical reality always shows that the coupling coefficients become increasingly small with the graph distance between
g
and
g^{\prime}
, to a point where they cannot be resolved experimentally anymore. We recall that the graph distance between two vertices is the number of edges in the shortest path or paths joining the two vertices. Thus, the physically relevant Hamiltonians always have finite range,
i.e.
there exists
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
w_{g,g^{\prime}}=0
if the graph distance between
g
and
g^{\prime}
exceeds
R
.

\Diamond

If the coupling coefficients are adjusted arbitrarily, then the dynamics on the Cayley graph is no different from the dynamics over any other infinite graph. Indeed, separable infinite Hilbert spaces are all isomorphic, hence their algebras of bounded operators are isomorphic too. If we want the dynamics to reflect the symmetry of the Cayley graph, as stressed out in our Remark
2.18
, the coupling coefficients must by insensitive to the translations of the lattice:

w_{g^{\prime}h,gh}=w_{g^{\prime},g},\quad\forall\ h,g,g^{\prime}\in G.

(3.2)

As we shall see, if this is indeed the case, then the dynamics of the resonators can be analyzed in a pure algebraic framework, canonically built from the group
G
alone.

The right action of the group
G
on itself induces the right regular representation
\pi_{R}
of
G
on
\ell^{2}(G)
:

U_{g}|g^{\prime}\rangle=|g^{\prime}g^{-1}\rangle,\quad g,g^{\prime}\in G.

(3.3)

One can verify that

U_{h}HU_{h}^{\ast}=\sum_{g,g^{\prime}\in G}w_{g^{\prime},g}\,|g^{\prime}h^{-1}\rangle\langle gh^{-1}|=\sum_{g,g^{\prime}\in G}w_{g^{\prime}h,gh}\,|g^{\prime}\rangle\langle g|=H,

(3.4)

for any
h\in G
. Thus, the enforcement of the equivariant relation (
3.2
) produces Hamiltonians that are symmetric w.r.t. the natural group action. Furthermore, Eq. (
3.2
) also implies that the coefficients only depend on the relative index
q=g^{\prime}g^{-1}
, which reduces the Hamiltonian to a very particular form, as the following formal manipulation shows:

H=\sum_{g,g^{\prime}\in G}w_{g^{\prime},g}\,|g^{\prime}\rangle\langle g|=\sum_{g,g^{\prime}\in G}w_{g^{\prime}g^{-1},1}\,|g^{\prime}g^{-1}g\rangle\langle g|,

(3.5)

or, if we change the notation
w_{q^{\prime}q^{-1},1}
to
w_{q}
,

H=\sum_{q\in G}\sum_{g\in G}w_{q}\,|qg\rangle\langle g|.

(3.6)

In the above expression, we can quickly identify the left regular representation
\pi_{L}
of the group on
\ell^{2}(G)
, induced by the left action of
G
on itself. The conclusion is that

H=\sum_{q\in G}w_{q}\,\pi_{L}(q).

(3.7)

In the light of Remark
3.1
, the above sum involves a finite number of terms. Note however that the functional calculus with
H
will generally produce sums with infinite terms. This is certainly the case for the resolvent
(z-H)^{-1}
.

Let us conclude with an observation about the relation Eq. (
3.4
) stating the invariance of the Hamiltonian w.r.t. the right action of the group: This relation has its origin in the associativity property of the group multiplication. Indeed,

\pi_{L}(q)\pi_{R}(h)|g\rangle=|q(gh^{-1})\rangle,\quad\pi_{R}(h)\pi_{L}(q)|g\rangle=|(qg)h^{-1}\rangle,

(3.8)

and the associativity ensures that the two results coincide.

3.2.
Operator algebraic frameworks of analyses

Given a discrete group
G
, its group algebra
{\mathbb{C}}G
consists of formal series

a=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}\,g,\quad\alpha_{g}\in{\mathbb{C}},

(3.9)

where all but a finite number of terms are zero. Addition and multiplication of such formal series work in the obvious way, using the group and algebraic structures of
G
and
{\mathbb{C}}
, respectively. In addition, there exists a natural
\ast
-operation

a^{\ast}=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}^{\ast}\,g^{-1},\quad(a^{\ast})^{\ast}=a,\quad(\alpha a)^{\ast}=\alpha^{\ast}a^{\ast},\ \alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}.

(3.10)

Hence,
{\mathbb{C}}G
is naturally a
\ast
-algebra.

Remark 3.2
.

The group algebra has a unit
1:=e
, the neutral element of the group. Also, note that the elements of the group become unitary elements in this
\ast
-algebra,
g^{\ast}g=gg^{\ast}=1
.

\Diamond

The linear map

{\mathcal{T}}:{\mathbb{C}}G\to{\mathbb{C}},\quad{\mathcal{T}}\Big(\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}\,g\Big)=\alpha_{e},

(3.11)

defines a positive faithful trace on
{\mathbb{C}}G
and a pre-Hilbert structure on
{\mathbb{C}}G
via

\langle a|b\rangle:={\mathcal{T}}(a^{\ast}b),\quad a,b\in{\mathbb{C}}G.

(3.12)

The completion of the linear space
{\mathbb{C}}G
under this pre-Hilbert structure supplies the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(G)
, already encountered in the previous subsection. Indeed, one can verify that
\{|g\rangle\}_{g\in G}
supplies an orthonormal basis for the so defined Hilbert space:

\langle g|g^{\prime}\rangle={\mathcal{T}}(g^{-1}g^{\prime})=\delta_{g,g^{\prime}},\quad g,g^{\prime}\in G.

(3.13)

The action of
{\mathbb{C}}G
on itself can be extended to the action of a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(G)
, and this supplies the left regular representation
\pi_{L}
of
{\mathbb{C}}G
inside the algebra
{\mathbb{B}}\big(\ell^{2}(G)\big)
of bounded operators over
\ell^{2}(G)
. Specifically,

\pi_{L}\Big(\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}\,g\Big)|g^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{g}\alpha_{g}|gg^{\prime}\rangle,\quad g^{\prime}\in G.

(3.14)

This is simply the canonical extension of the left regular representation
\pi_{L}
of the group to the algebra structure. By inspecting Eq. (
3.7
), we see that any Hamiltonian with finite coupling range can be generated as the left regular representation of an element from
{\mathbb{C}}G
, specifically,

H=\pi_{L}(h),\quad h=\sum_{g\in G}w_{g}\,g.

(3.15)

Example 3.3
.

The adjacency operator
\Delta
of the standard Cayley graph of a finitely generated group
\langle{\mathcal{G}}|{\mathcal{R}}\rangle
is defined as

\Delta=\pi_{L}(\delta),\quad\delta=\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}\cup{\mathcal{G}}^{-1}}g.

(3.16)

It will be used extensively in our computer experiments.

\Diamond

The group algebra
{\mathbb{C}}G
, however, is not sufficient for analyzing the quantum dynamics. For example, the resolvent
(H-z)^{-1}
or the projections onto the spectral bands of
H
cannot be generated from
{\mathbb{C}}G
via the left regular representation. For this, we need to complete
{\mathbb{C}}G
to a
C^{\ast}
-algebra. Since the groups we are dealing with are not amenable, their group algebras accept two distinct
C^{\ast}
-completions
[
19
, Ch. VII]
. The one that is relevant for the experiments we described in the previous subsection is the reduced group
C^{\ast}
-algebra
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
(see Remark
3.9
), generated by the completion of
{\mathbb{C}}G
with respect to the norm

\|a\|_{r}=\|\pi_{L}(a)\|_{{\mathbb{B}}(\ell^{2}(G))},

(3.17)

induced by the left-regular representation. The latter extends to a representation of
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
in
{\mathbb{B}}(\ell^{2}(G))
, which coincides with the GNS representation associated to the trace
{\mathcal{T}}

[
49
]
. On the other hand, the full group
C^{\ast}
-algebra
C^{\ast}(G)
is generated by the completion of
{\mathbb{C}}G
with respect to the norm

\|a\|_{f}=\sup\{\|\pi(a)\|_{{\mathbb{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{\pi})}\,,\ \pi\ \mbox{is a }\ast\mbox{-representation of }\ell^{1}(G)\}

(3.18)

where
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathbb{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{\pi})}
is the operator norm on the Hilbert space of the representation and
\ell^{1}(G)
is the Banach algebra of the formal series of the type (
3.9
) corresponding to absolutely summable sequences
\{\alpha_{g}\}_{g\in G}
(see
[
19
, Chs. VII and VIII]
for more details):

\ell^{1}(G)=\Big\{a=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}\,g,\ \|a\|_{1}:=\sum_{g\in G}|\alpha_{g}|<\infty\Big\}.

(3.19)

As we shall see, a lot of what we are going to say in this work depends on the interplay between the algebras we just mentioned. Therefore, we elaborate on this aspect. First, by construction,
\ell^{1}(G)
embeds into
C^{\ast}(G)
, that is, there exists an injective homomorphism
\mathfrak{i}:\ell^{1}(G)\to C^{\ast}(G)
. In the same time, the identity map on
{\mathbb{C}}G
can be extended to a
C^{\ast}
-algebra homomorphism

\lambda:C^{\ast}(G)\to C^{\ast}_{r}(G),

(3.20)

which is a
C^{\ast}
-algebra isomporphism if and only if the group
G
is amenable
[
19
, Th. VII.2.5]
. The composition
\lambda\circ\mathfrak{i}
is always injective, hence
\ell^{1}(G)
also embeds into
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
. For the free and Fuchsian groups, for example,
\lambda
fails to be injective, which means the common elements from
{\mathbb{C}}G
will have different spectra when the computations are performed in
C^{\ast}(G)
or
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
. The reader can consult
[
4
]
for a definition of spectrum in a pure algebraic setting and how spectra behave under algebra morphisms. Here, we mention the following phenomenon that is specific to
C^{\ast}
-algebras:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be
C^{\ast}
-algebras such that
{\mathcal{A}}
embeds into
{\mathcal{B}}
. Then
{\rm Spec}_{\mathcal{A}}(a)={\rm Spec}_{\mathcal{B}}(a)
for any
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
. In other words, while
a
can be considered either as an element of
{\mathcal{A}}
or an element of
{\mathcal{B}}
, when it comes to the spectrum, this distinction makes no difference.

We now come to some of the most powerful tools we have for group
C^{\ast}
-algebras, namely the functorial relation between the group and its
C^{\ast}
-algebra:

Proposition 3.5

(
[
50
]
)
.

Let
G
and
H
be discrete groups and
\rho:G\to H
be a group homomorphism. Then
\rho
lifts to an algebra homomorphism between
C^{\ast}(G)
and
C^{\ast}(H)
, which on
\ell^{1}(G)
acts as

\ell^{1}(G)\ni\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}\,g\mapsto\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}\,\rho(g)\in\ell^{1}(H).

(3.21)

Remark 3.6
.

According to the above statement, any morphism from
G
to a finite group produces a finite representation of the group
C^{\ast}
-algebra. In fact, all finite representations of
C^{\ast}(G)
can be generated in this way.

\Diamond

If we replace the full by the reduced group
C^{\ast}
-algebra, this functorial relation continue to exist for group morphisms with amenable kernels, in particular, for the injective ones. More precisely:

Proposition 3.7

(
[
14
]
, Prop. 2.5.9 & Cor. 2.5.12)
.

If
\rho:H\to G
is injective, then
\rho
extends to an injective morphism between the corresponding reduced group
C^{\ast}
-algebras. Furthermore, if
C^{\ast}_{r}(H)
is identified with its image in
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
, then there exists a conditional expectation
E_{G}^{H}:C^{\ast}_{r}(G)\to C^{\ast}_{r}(H)
which acts like
E_{G}^{H}(g)=0
if
g\notin H
and
E_{G}^{H}(g)=g
otherwise.

Corollary 3.8
.

The above statement can be paired with Proposition
3.4
to conclude that
{\rm Spec}_{C^{\ast}_{r}(H)}(h)={\rm Spec}_{C^{\ast}_{r}(G)}(h)
for
h\in C^{\ast}_{r}(H)
and
H
a subgroup of
G
.

Remark 3.9
.

We are now in a position from where we can explain why the physical experiments engage the reduced group
C^{\ast}
-algebra. Indeed, note that the trace
{\mathcal{T}}
is faithful, hence the left regular representation of
C_{r}^{\ast}(G)
is faithful. Thus
\pi_{L}
is an embedding of
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
into
{\mathbb{B}}(\ell^{2}(G))
, the
C^{\ast}
-algebra of bounded operators over the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(G)
of physical observations. Now, when we compute or measure the spectrum of a Hamiltonian
H=\pi_{L}(h)
, with
h\in{\mathbb{C}}G\subset C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
, we do so inside
{\mathbb{B}}(\ell^{2}(G))
, because in a laboratory we manipulate and observe wavefunctions from
\ell^{2}(G)
. By the above proposition, however, same results will be obtained if the computations are carried inside
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
and this is why we claim that the physical experiments engage
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
and not
C^{\ast}(G)
.

\Diamond

Remark 3.10
.

We recall that the resolvent set of an element
a
of a
C^{\ast}
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
consists of all those
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}
for which
h-\xi
is invertible in that algebra. The spectrum of
a
is the complement of its resolvent set. To decide if
\xi
is inside or not in this spectrum, one has to search all algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
and see if there is an element that can serve as the inverse of
h-\xi
. Now, since
h
belongs to
{\mathbb{C}}G
and the latter is part of both
C^{\ast}(G)
and
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
, there is the dilema in which of them to search for the inverse of
h-\xi
. The previous remark answer this question: This inverse should be searched for in
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
. But what happens if we search for it in
C^{\ast}(G)
? Proposition
3.4
requires an injective homomorphism from
{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
in order to hold. As we already mentioned, the homomorphism
\lambda
from Eq. (
3.20
) fails to be injective for the groups we study here, hence
C^{\ast}(G)
does not embed into
{\mathbb{B}}(\ell^{2}(G))
. This means that the search for the inverse of
h-\xi
inside
C^{\ast}(G)
will return different results and the spectrum of
H=\pi_{L}(h)
will not be correctly computed.

\Diamond

Example 3.11
.

Consider the adjacency operator (
3.16
) on the standard Cayley graph of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
. It is generated by an element
\delta
from
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
, which can be seen either as an element of
C^{\ast}(G)
or
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
. Let us consider the first alternative. Since
\delta
is a linear combination of four unitary operators, its spectrum must be contained inside the interval
[-4,4]
. Now the subgroup
[G,G]
of commutators sits as a normal subgroup inside
G
. The quotient group
G/[G,G]
is called the abelianization of
G
. Every 1-dimensional representation of
G
factors through it, in the sense that it can be obtained as a composition of the projection
G\to G/[G,G]
and a character of
G/[G,G]
. According to Proposition
3.5
, the morphism
G\to G/[G,G]
lifts to a morphism between the corresponding full group
C^{\ast}
-algebras (but not between the reduced
C^{\ast}
-algebras!). Thus, we can talk about the abelianization of the adjacency operator, which is just the discrete Laplace operator on the regular lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, whose spectrum can be easily computed as
[-4,4]
. From the behavior of the spectra under morphisms
[
4
]
, we conclude that the spectrum of the adjacency operator inside
C^{\ast}(G)
is the full interval
[-4,4]
. On the other hand, the spectrum of the adjacency operator inside
{\mathbb{B}}(\ell^{2}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}))
,
i.e.
the real physical one, is the interval
[-2\sqrt{3},2\sqrt{3}]

[
46
]
.

\Diamond

Remark 3.12
.

The above example also demonstrates that the 1-dimensional representations generate spurious spectrum. We can describe what is going on without appealing to any algebraic arguments. Indeed, as a general rule,
\xi
belongs to spectrum of
H
if there is a sequence
\{\psi_{k}\}
from
\ell^{2}(G)
such that
\|\psi_{k}\|=1
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|H\psi_{k}-\xi\,\psi_{k}\|=0
. Now, the abelianization of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
is just
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and suppose that, through the 1-dimensional representations of this abelian model, we find
\xi
and a sequence
\{\psi_{k}^{(1)}\}
in
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
which check the spectral criterion for the abelianization of
H
. Unfortunately, there is now way to pull this sequence back on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
and check the spectral criterion for
H
itself.

\Diamond

Brisk yet quite complete characterizations of the full and reduced
C^{\ast}
-algebras of the free group
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
can be found in
[
19
, Sec. VII.6]
. For reader’s convenience, we reproduce some of these results, together with some implications relevant for the present context:

a)

C^{\ast}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
accepts a discrete family
\pi_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, of finite dimensional representations such that
\oplus_{n\geq 1}\pi_{n}
is a faithful representation. They can be produced by a standard algorithm described in
[
19
, pg. 204]
. On the other hand,
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
is simple, hence it does not accept any finite dimensional representation.

b)

Both
C^{\ast}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
and
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
accept faithful traces. There are many such traces on
C^{\ast}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
but only one on
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
(the one appearing in Eq. (
3.11
)).

c)

Both
C^{\ast}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
and
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
do not not contain any non-trivial projections. As such, any Hamiltonian
h\in C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
displays a single spectral band, hence no spectral gaps. Of course, gaps in the Hamiltonian spectra can be open by tensoring with algebras of finite matrices, which amounts to layering the lattices of metamaterials.

Remark 3.13
.

All a-c) points apply also to
C^{\ast}({\mathcal{F}}_{2})
and
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{F}}_{2})
algebras
[
2
,
3
]
.

\Diamond

The following statements give a coarse characterization of the spectral properties of the elements of
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
, for a generic finitely generated group
G
:

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group. Then:

i)

If
w
is an element of infinite order, then the spectrum of
\pi_{L}(w)
, which is a unitary operator by default, is the full circle and it is absolutely continuous. Same for
\pi_{R}(w)
.

ii)

If the group contains elements of infinite order, then the spectrum of any
\pi_{L}(h)
,
h\in C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
is void of discrete spectrum. Consequently, the image in
{\mathbb{B}}(\ell^{2}(G))
of
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
through the left regular representation does not contain any compact operator.

iii)

If the group does not contain any element of finite order and obeys the Baum-Connes conjecture
[
7
]
, then the spectrum of any
\pi_{L}(h)
,
h=h^{\ast}\in C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
, is connected (i.e. there are no gaps in the spectrum).

Proof.

i) We will make use of the right cosets of the subgroup
W=\langle w\rangle
of
G
generated by
w
. Every such right coset is invariant w.r.t. the left action of
W
and it can be presented as
\{w^{n}g\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
, for some
g\in G
. If
C_{w}
denotes the set of the right cosets of
W
, then we have the decomposition of the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(G)
into invariant sub-spaces for the action of
\pi_{L}(w)
,
\ell^{2}(G)=\bigoplus_{C_{w}}\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
. Furthermore,
\pi_{L}(w)
acts as the shift operator on each of the invariant subspaces
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
. The first statement then follows from the spectral property of this particular operator. The case involving the right regular representation is proved similarly by considering the left cosets.

ii) According to i), the right regular representation of an element of infinite order, say
w
, has continuous spectrum. Hence, there are no invariant finite dimensional subspaces of
\ell^{2}(G)
for the action of
\pi_{R}(w)
. Therefore,
\pi_{L}(a)
cannot have discrete spectrum because the projection onto its corresponding Hilbert subspace will be finite dimensional and invariant to the action of
\pi_{R}(w)
.

iii) In the stated conditions,
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
contains no proper projections
[
7
]
. Thus, all self-adjoint elements have gapless spectrum.
∎

Remark 3.15
.

H. Kesten was the first to consider random walks on non-commutative groups and to investigate spectra of Markovian operators
[
30
,
31
]
. He computed the spectrum of a simple random walk on a free group and the corresponding spectral measure was found to be absolutely continuous. This complements the information we already supplied in Proposition
3.14
, for the case of free groups. It seems that the continuity of the spectrum is a common feature of the adjacency operators on Cayley graphs of finitely generated groups
[
48
]
. For infinitely generated groups, however, we already know that this is not always the case
[
27
]
.

\Diamond

Remark 3.16
.

C^{\ast}
-algebras are only stable w.r.t. functional calculus involving continuous functions. When Borel calculus is mentioned, we automatically place the discussion in the context of the group von Neumann algebra, which is the bicommutant of
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
, identified as the weak closure of the image of
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
in
{\mathbb{B}}(\ell^{2}(G))
. We refrained from discussing this von Neumann closure for two reasons: Firstly, we are mostly interested in the continuous functional calculus (
e.g.
the gap projections of a Hamiltonian can be produced by this calculus) and, secondly, because
K
-theories generally become irrelevant once one passes from
C^{\ast}
-algebras to their von Neumann closures.

\Diamond

3.3.
Exact results

Here we show that there exists a large class of Hamiltonians whose spectral properties can be deduced from combinatorial exercises on appropriate Cayley graphs. Some of the results reported in this subsection are already known for the adjacency operator (see
[
48
,
67
]
for surveys of the topic).

Now, let
S
be a finite symmetric subset of
G
(
i.e.

S^{-1}=S
), not containing the neutral element, and consider the self-adjoint element

h=\sum_{s\in S}s\in{\mathbb{C}}G.

(3.22)

The left regular representation of elements of this type supply the class we are interested in. The Hamiltonian (
3.22
) and its associated functional calculus can be naturally studied on the Cayley graph
{\mathcal{C}}(G,S)
. For this particular graph, we denote by
N_{g}(n)
the number of graph paths of length
n
from the site labeled by
g
to the origin labeled by the neutral element
e
.

Proposition 3.17
.

N_{g}(n)\leq|S|^{n}
.

Proof.

We observe that
h^{n}
, after expansion, produces the sum of all un-reduced words of length
n
that can be formed with letters from
S
. We also recall that
{\mathcal{T}}
returns the coefficient of the neutral element, hence
{\mathcal{T}}(wg)
, with
w
a word as we just described, returns one if
wg=e
and zero otherwise. The conclusion is that
{\mathcal{T}}(h^{n}g)
counts all the un-reduced words of length
n
that can be generated from
S
such that
wg=e
. These words are in one to one correspondence with the graph paths of length
n
starting at
g
and ending at
e
. Then

N_{g}(n)={\mathcal{T}}(h^{n}\,g)=\langle e|\pi_{L}(h)^{n}|g\rangle

(3.23)

and the seen matrix element can be bounded by
\|h\|_{r}^{n}
. The statement follows because each
s\in S
is represented by a unitary element, hence
\|h\|_{r}\leq|S|
.
∎

Proposition 3.18
.

The sequence
\{N_{g}(n)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
accepts a generating function, that is, a function
F_{g}(x):{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
given by a finite linear combination of right-continuous monotone non-decreasing functions, each of them constant outside the interval
J_{S}=\big[-|S|,|S|\big]
, and

N_{g}(n)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{n}\,{\rm d}F_{g}(x),\quad\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(3.24)

Proof.

Since
\|h\|_{r}\leq|S|
,
(z-h)^{-1}
accepts the norm convergent series in
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
for
|z|>|S|
,

(z-h)^{-1}=\frac{1}{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(h/z)^{n},

(3.25)

and same applies to its matrix elements. Therefore,

\langle e|\pi_{L}(z-h)^{-1}|g\rangle=\frac{1}{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{-n}\,{\mathcal{T}}\big(h^{n}\,g\big)=\frac{1}{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{-n}N_{g}(n).

(3.26)

From spectral theory of self-adjoint operators, we know that the left hand side is an analytic function on the domain
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\rm Spec}_{C^{\ast}_{r}(G)}(h)
, hence the right side can be analytically continued over the same domain. Furthermore, using the residue theorem,

N_{g}(n)=\frac{1}{2\pi\imath}\int_{\gamma}{\rm d}z\;z^{n}\,\langle e|\pi_{L}(z-h)^{-1}|g\rangle,

(3.27)

where
\gamma
is a curve in the complex plane encircling the domain
|z|<|S|
. But this curve can be deformed to surround the interval
J_{S}
infinitely close, and the result is

N_{g}(n)=\int_{J_{S}}{\rm d}x\;x^{n}\,\tfrac{1}{\pi}{\rm Im}\langle e|\pi_{L}(x+\imath 0^{+}-h)^{-1}|g\rangle.

(3.28)

The right hand side can be expressed in terms of the family of spectral projections
\{\bm{E}(x)|x\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
of
\pi_{L}(h)
. Indeed, if we recall Stone’s formula,

\langle e|\bm{E}(x)|g\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{x+0^{+}}{\rm d}t\;\tfrac{1}{\pi}{\rm Im}\langle e|\pi_{L}(t+\imath 0^{+}-h)^{-1}|g\rangle,

(3.29)

then we have

N_{g}(n)=\int_{J_{S}}x^{n}\,{\rm d}\langle e|\bm{E}(x)|g\rangle.

(3.30)

Lastly, we can use the polarization identity to re-write

\langle e|\bm{E}(x)|g\rangle=\tfrac{1}{4}\sum_{v\in X_{g}}\langle v|\bm{E}(x)|v\rangle,\quad X_{g}=\{e\pm g,e\pm\imath g\}\subset\ell^{2}(G),

(3.31)

hence as a finite linear combination of monotone non-decreasing functions that are right-continuous.
∎

Remark 3.19
.

For a free group,
N_{g}(n)
and
F_{g}(x)
were computed explicitly in
[
46
]
using combinatorial techniques, for
g=e
and
S
the generating set defined in Eq. (
2.3
), in which case
h
coincides with the adjacency operator from Eq. (
3.16
) for the standard Cayley graph of
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
. They take the form
N_{e}(n)=0
for
n={\rm odd}
,

N_{e}(n)=v\sum_{k=0}^{n/2}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
k\end{pmatrix}\frac{n-2k}{n}(v-1)^{k},\quad n={\rm even},

(3.32)

and

F_{e}(x)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{v}{2\pi}\left[\arcsin\frac{x}{2\sqrt{v-1}}-\frac{v-2}{v}\arctan\frac{(v-2)x}{v\sqrt{4(v-1)-x^{2}}}\right]

(3.33)

inside the interval
[-2\sqrt{v-1},2\sqrt{v-1}]
,
F(x)=0
if
x<-2\sqrt{v-1}
and
F(x)=1
if
x>2\sqrt{v-1}
. Here,
v
is the degree of the corresponding Cayley graphs. In particular, for the free group with two generators,
v=4
. Furthermore,
N_{g}(n)
can be easily derived from
N_{e}(n)
when
g
is drawn from the set of generators:

N_{X_{1}^{\pm 1}}(n)=N_{X_{2}^{\pm 1}}(n)=N_{e}(n+1)/v,\quad n={\rm odd},

(3.34)

and
N_{X_{1}^{\pm 1}}(n)=N_{X_{2}^{\pm 1}}(n)=0
for
n={\rm even}
. These exact results will be used to validate our numerical algorithms in subsection
5.2
.

\Diamond

Corollary 3.20
.

Let
H=\pi_{L}(h)
, with
h
as in Eq. (
3.22
), and
z\notin{\rm Spec}(H)
. Then the matrix elements of the Green’s function
(z-H)^{-1}
can be computed as:

\langle g|(z-H)^{-1}|g^{\prime}\rangle=\int\frac{{\rm d}F_{g^{\prime}g^{-1}}(x)}{z-x}.

(3.35)

Furthermore, if
\varphi
is a Borel function over the real line, then

\langle g|\varphi(H)|g^{\prime}\rangle=\int\varphi(x){\rm d}F_{g^{\prime}g^{-1}}(x).

(3.36)

In particular, the spectral density function can be computed as

F(x):=\langle e|\bm{E}(x)|e\rangle=F_{e}(x).

(3.37)

The above statements, together with the known expression of
F_{e}
from Eq. (
3.33
), supply the means to validate our numerical simulations of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
-crystals, reported in subsection
5.2
. Note, however, that the results of this section can be also used in the opposite direction. Indeed, if one is in possession of rapidly convergent simulations, then the combinatorial numbers
N_{g}(n)
can be computed either via Eq. (
3.23
) or as

N_{g}(n)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int x^{n}\,{\rm d}\langle e|\bm{E}_{N}(x)|\phi_{N}(g)\rangle,

(3.38)

where
\bm{E}_{N}(x)
are the spectral families of projections for the finitely approximated Hamiltonian and the limit is over the size of the approximation (see section
5
). We will see in section
5
that this is possible to some extent with the computer algorithms supplied in this work. When
N_{g}
is known, Eq. (
3.38
) supplies a test on the quality of the approximated spectral measure.

Converging finite approximations

4.1.
Coherent sequences of normal subgroups

Definition 4.1
.

A group
G
is called residually finite if, for all
g\in G
such that
g\neq 1
, there
exists a normal subgroup
N\triangleleft G
such that
g\neq N
and
G/N
is finite. Equivalently,
we could characterize this property by saying that for all
g\in G
such that
g\neq 1
,
there exists a finite group
H
and a homomorphism
\theta:G\to H
such that
\theta(g)\neq 1
in H.

.

As stated in our introductory remarks, any finitely generated group possessing a faithful representation into

for

4.2.
Approximation results of Lück

The group morphisms
\phi_{N}
induce group algebra morphisms
\phi_{N}:{\mathbb{C}}G\to{\mathbb{C}}H_{N}
, for which we will use the same symbols. Below, we reproduce the original result by Lück
[
36
]
, in a slightly updated formulation supplied in the monograph
[
37
, Ch. 13]
. Throughout this subsection, we work in the setting described in the previous subsection.

Proposition 4.3

(
[
36
,
37
]
)
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{C}}G
be a self-adjoint and positive element and let
\{\bm{E}(x)|x\in[0,\infty)\}
be the right continuous family of spectral projections of
\pi_{L}(f)
in
{\mathbb{B}}(\ell^{2}(G))
. Consider the associated spectral density function

F:[0,\infty)\to[0,1],\quad x\mapsto\langle e|\bm{E}(x)|e\rangle

(4.5)

and define
F_{N}
in the same way for
\phi_{N}(f)\in{\mathbb{C}}G_{N}
. Then

F(x)=\liminf F_{N}(x+\imath 0^{+})=\limsup F_{N}(x+\imath 0^{+}).

(4.6)

The above statement assures us that the diagonal matrix elements

\langle g|\varphi(\pi_{L}(f))|g\rangle=\int\varphi(x){\rm d}F(x)

(4.7)

can be computed with arbitrary precision using the finite approximations, for any Borel function
\varphi
. In fact, one can says so much more:

Corollary 4.4
.

Consider the settings of Proposition
4.3
. Then the off-diagonal matrix elements
\langle e|\pi_{L}(\varphi(f))|g\rangle
can be also computed with arbitrary precision from the finite approximations, for any complex valued continuous function
\varphi
.

Proof.

Since any complex valued function can be written as a linear combination of real positively valued continuous functions, it is enough to assume that
\varphi
is such a function. Furthermore, it is known that the matrix element we want to calculate can be approximated with arbitrary precision if we replace
\varphi
by polynomials. Now, let
p(x)
be a polynomial approximating
\sqrt{\varphi}
. We have

\langle e|\pi_{L}(p^{2}(f))|g\rangle=\langle\pi_{L}(p(f))e|\pi_{L}(p(f))g\rangle

(4.8)

and, by using the polarization identity, the above matrix element can be written as a sum involving the terms

\langle\pi_{L}(p(f))e\pm\pi_{L}(p(f))g|\pi_{L}(p(f))e\pm\pi_{L}(p(f))g\rangle=\langle e\pm g|\pi_{L}(p^{2}(f))|e\pm g\rangle

(4.9)

and

\langle\pi_{L}(p(f))e\pm\imath\pi_{L}(p(f))g|\pi_{L}(p(f))e\pm\imath\pi_{L}(p(f))g\rangle=\langle e\pm\imath g|\pi_{L}(p^{2}(f))|e\pm\imath g\rangle.

(4.10)

The conclusion is that
\langle e|\pi_{L}(p^{2}(f))|g\rangle
can be expressed in terms of

\langle e|\pi_{L}(v^{\ast}p^{2}(f)v)|e\rangle,\quad v\in\{e\pm g,e\pm\imath g\},

(4.11)

and each
v^{\ast}p^{2}(f)v
is a self-adjoint and positive element of
{\mathbb{C}}G
. As such, the diagonal elements of their left regular representations can be computed with arbitrary precision using their finite approximations

\phi_{N}(v^{\ast}p^{2}(f)v)=\phi_{N}(v^{\ast})\,p^{2}(\phi_{N}(f))\,\phi_{N}(v).

(4.12)

Using the polarization identity in reverse, we conclude that
\langle e|p^{2}(\phi_{N}(f))|\phi_{N}(g)\rangle
can approximate the original matrix element we started with arbitrary precision.
∎

Given the above statements, it is now justified to say that the entire continuous functional calculus in
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
with self-adjoint elements from
{\mathbb{C}}G
can be reproduced with arbitrary precision using the finite approximations supplied by the coherent sequence of subgroups
\{G_{N}\}
. In particular, the resolvent
(z-h)^{-1}
can be numerically computed with arbitrary precision for any
z\notin{\rm Spec}(h)
.

4.3.
An operator algebraic viewpoint

Looking back at the statement of Proposition
4.3
, it is impossible not to notice its pure algebraic nature, even though its proof was achieved by hard analysis. From the applied point of view, this is an extremely useful feature because, once we discover a coherent sequence of normal subgroups, there is no need for any other checks, like specific bounds on operators, etc.. Here, we describe a possible mechanism behind this feature, which can be of some guidance when dealing with finite approximations. For this, we reproduce a very general result concerning inverse limits:

]

C^{\ast}_{r}(\bar{G})
can be reproduced with arbitrary precision from the continuous functional calculus in
C^{\ast}_{r}(H_{N})
. Now, let
\{H_{N}\}
be the projective tower of normal subgroups of the residually finite group
G
, introduced in subsection
4.1
, and
f
be a positive element of
{\mathbb{C}}G
. Then the coherent sequence
\{\phi_{N}(f)\}
defines an element of
{\mathbb{C}}\bar{G}
. We recall that
G
embeds in
\bar{G}
, which implies that
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
embeds in
C^{\ast}_{r}(\bar{G})
via the canonical map
\phi
(cf. Proposition
3.7
). Obviously, the coherent sequence
\{\phi_{N}(f)\}
coincides with
\phi(f)
, hence
\{\phi_{N}(f)\}
lands in the image of
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
inside
C^{\ast}_{r}(\bar{G})
. Therefore, the continuous functional calculus of
f
in
C^{\ast}_{r}(G)
coincides with the continuous functional calculus of
\{\phi_{N}(f)\}
in
C^{\ast}_{r}(\bar{G})
. But the latter can be reproduced with arbitrary precision from the functional calculus with the finite approximations
\bar{\phi}_{N}(\{\phi_{N}(f)\})
, which coincide with
\phi_{N}(f)
. This is precisely the statement of Corollary
4.4
.

In the light of the above arguments, we see the engines behind the approximation results as being: 1) The general fact that they hold for any inverse system of groups, and 2) The functorial properties of the reduced group
C^{\ast}
-algebras.

5.1.
Misleading boundary conditions

Before we discuss the converging finite approximations, we exemplify what happens when non-specialized boundary conditions are used. In Fig.
5.1
, we report the spectral densities of the finite approximations of the adjacency operator
\Delta
introduced in Example
3.3
, generated with Dirichlet (or open) boundary conditions after generation
k=5,\ldots,9
of the Cayley graph is completed. We recall that, for finite models, the spectral density of a Hamiltonian
H
evaluated at
x\in{\mathbb{R}}\ {\rm Spec}(H)
amounts to counting the eigenvalues of
H
below
x
and dividing the result by the dimension of the Hilbert space. As seen in Fig.
5.1
, the spectral density converges as
k
is increased, but to a wrong limit. The explanation is that the open boundary of the graph introduces spurious spectrum, in particular, a large number of zero modes. As the size of the truncated graph grows, the spectral density becomes entirely dominated by the spurious spectrum introduced by the open boundary and this is why we see a convergence in Fig.
5.1
.

We can also try some ad-hoc “periodic boundary conditions” by folding the graph into itself. The simplest way to fold the graph, is to close the orbits discussed in subsection
2
and seen in Fig.
2.3
. For example, the blue arrow coming out of the node
aaa
will end in the node
AAA
, the green arow coming out from the node
bba
will end in the node
BBa
, while the blue arrow coming out of the node
baa
end in the same node
baa
, and so on. Note that these orbit foldings can be performed for any truncated lattice and, as such, we can investigate what happens as the size of the truncated lattice is increased. At the first sight, this seems like a correct choice because now the generators
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
act as permutations of the truncated Cayley graph and, due to the universality property of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
, the whole
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
group is being mapped into a subgroup
P
of the permutation group of the truncated lattice. From the functorial properties, this lifts to a morphism
\rho
between the full group
C^{\ast}
-algebras and, for example, we can be sure that
{\rm Spec}_{C^{\ast}(P)}(\rho(\delta))\subset{\rm Spec}_{C^{\ast}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})}(\delta)
, where
\delta
was introduced in Example
3.3
. The problem is, however, that

{\rm Spec}_{C^{\ast}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})}(\delta)\neq{\rm Spec}_{C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})}(\delta)={\rm Spec}(\Delta).

(5.1)

Indeed, the former consists of the whole interval
[-4,4]
, while the latter consists of the interval
[-2\sqrt{3},2\sqrt{3}]
(see Example
3.11
). As a result, the ad-hoc periodic boundary condition is of little use and, in fact, it can lead to misleading results. This is confirmed by the simulations reported in Fig.
5.2
, where we can see clearly that the spectrum computed with the ad-hoc periodic boundary condition spills over the expected spectral interval. This could have been even anticipated beforehand, because the ad-hoc periodic boundary conditions produce a growing number of closed loops with the increase in the size of the truncated graph and, according to
[
46
]
, this is exactly the scenario where the eigenvalue counting does not converges to the correct result. One can also think of the spectra seen in Fig.
5.2
as corresponding to sequences of (not faithful) finite representations of
\delta
, with the latter seen as an element of the full group
C^{\ast}
-algebra.

5.2.
Converging finite approximations for a free group

Figure 5.4.

The general principle that we are relying on is as follows. Suppose we are dealing with a subgroup of
{\rm GL}(n,R)
, where
R
is a ring with a unit. Then, if
J\subset R
is an ideal of
R
, then

\widetilde{\rm GL}(n,J):=\big\{[\delta_{ij}+a_{ij}]\in{\rm GL}(n,R),\ a_{ij}\in J\big\}

(5.2)

is a normal subgroup of
{\rm GL}(n,R)
. Indeed, the elements of
\widetilde{\rm GL}(n,J)
are of the form
I+Q
, with
I
the identity matrix and
Q
from the ideal
M_{n}(J)
of the ring of
n\times n
matrices
M_{n}(R)
. Then, we can easily see that the set (
5.2
) is stable against matrix multiplication and, furthermore, for any
M\in{\rm GL}(n,R)
,

M\cdot(I+Q)\cdot M^{-1}=I+M\cdot Q\cdot M^{-1},

(5.3)

which clearly belongs to
\widetilde{\rm GL}(n,J)
. Also, the class in
{\rm GL}(n,R)/\widetilde{\rm GL}(n,J)
of an element of
{\rm GL}(n,R)
can be computed as

[a_{ij}]\cdot\widetilde{\rm GL}(n,J)=[a_{ij}\,{\rm mod}\,J].

(5.4)

For example, in the case of
n=2
, we have

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1+J&J\\
J&1+J\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a+J&b+J\\
c+J&d+J\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a\,{\rm mod}\,J&b\,{\rm mod}\,J\\
c\,{\rm mod}\,J&d\,{\rm mod}\,J\end{pmatrix}.

(5.5)

We now invoke the matrix presentation of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
supplied in Eq. (
2.9
) and observe that, for
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
p>1
,

{\mathbb{F}}_{2}=G_{0}\triangleright G_{1}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}\cap\widetilde{\rm GL}(2,p{\mathbb{Z}})\triangleright G_{2}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}\cap\widetilde{\rm GL}(2,p^{2}{\mathbb{Z}})\triangleright\cdots

(5.6)

is a coherent sequence of normal subgroups such that
\cap\,G_{N}=\{e\}
. The quotient group
H_{N}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}/G_{N}
can be generated by producing free products of the generating matrices (
2.9
) and their inverses, applying
{\rm mod}\,p^{N}
on all the entries of these free products and, finally, deleting the duplicates. The outcome of this process is a finite set
H_{N}
of matrices. Generating the multiplication tables for
H_{N}
’s amounts to taking pairwise matrix products from
H_{N}
, taking the
{\rm mod}\,p^{N}
on the result and identifying this final result with an existing element from the set
H_{N}
. Once this information is gathered on a computer, the left regular representations of the elements of
{\mathbb{C}}H_{N}
can be obtained by standard procedures, in particular, that of the adjacency operators
\phi_{N}(\delta)
. The algorithm we just outlined is fully documented in
[
38
]
. Fig.
5.3
reports the spectral densities of
\pi_{L}(\phi_{N}(\delta))
for different
N
’s and
p=2
. One can clearly see a fast convergence of the numerical results to the exact spectral density, as the theory predicted. In fact, the mean squared deviations
MSD=0.0053,0.00076,0.000096,0.000025
for
N=3,4,5,6
, respectively, of the numerical estimates from the exact spectral density indicate that the convergence happens exponentially fast in
N
. Lastly, for completion, we report in Fig.
5.4
the spectral densities of
\pi_{L}(\phi_{1}(\delta)
for different values of
p
in Eq. (
5.6
). As opposed to the monotone trend seen in Fig.
5.3
, the quality of the numerical outputs seen in Fig.
5.4
fluctuates wildly with
p
. This is because the subgroups generated by this procedure do not form a coherent tower.

The simulations demonstrate that our proposed periodic boundary conditions lead to fast converging algorithms and, in the following, we demonstrate that in fact we can reach a point where we can use the spectral simulations to derive combinatorial informations of the Cayley graph. where

H_{N}
