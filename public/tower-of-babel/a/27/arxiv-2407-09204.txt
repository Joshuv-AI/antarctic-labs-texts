Electron bubbles in highly excited states of the lowest Landau level
David D. Dai, Liang Fu

Abstract
We study the entire energy spectrum of an electron droplet in the lowest Landau level. By exact diagonalization calculations, we find highly excited states in the middle of the spectrum that display unexpected density distribution and pair correlation. We show that these exceptional excited states contain tightly bound electron bubbles with local filling ν = 1 \nu=1 that form various ordered structures. Remarkably, these bubble excited states are shown to exist for both the 1 / r 1/r Coulomb interaction and the 1 / r 3 1/r^{3} dipole interaction. The experimental realization of bubble excited states in moiré materials under a magnetic field is also discussed.

Exact Diagonalization—
The position-space Hamiltonian for a droplet of
N
electrons confined to a plane and subject to a perpendicular magnetic field is:

H=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2m}\left[{\mathbf{\bm{p}}}_{i}-q{\mathbf{\bm{A}}}({\mathbf{\bm{r}}}_{i})\right]^{2}+\sum_{i<j}v\left(|{\mathbf{\bm{r}}}_{i}-{\mathbf{\bm{r}}}_{j}|\right),

(1)

where
{\mathbf{\bm{r}}}_{i}
and
{\mathbf{\bm{p}}}_{i}
are the position and canonical momentum respectively of the
i
th electron,
q
is the electron charge,
{\mathbf{\bm{A}}}({\mathbf{\bm{r}}})={\mathbf{\bm{B}}}\crossproduct{\mathbf{\bm{r}}}/2
is the symmetric gauge vector potential, and
v(r)
is the electron-electron interaction. We work in units where
\hbar=1

z_{i}=x_{i}+iy_{i},\bar{z}_{i}=x_{i}-iy_{i}

One conserved quantity is the total angular momentum

L_{\text{tot}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[z_{i}\partial_{i}-\bar{z}_{i}\bar{\partial}_{i}\right],

(2)

where
\partial_{i}

\left(\bar{\partial}_{i}\right)

\left(\bar{z}_{i}\right)

Since this term is proportional to the total angular momentum within the lowest Landau level, it does not change the eigenstates within a fixed total angular momentum sector.

Because the interaction only depends on the electrons’ relative distance but not on the center-of-mass position, the angular momentum of the center of mass

L_{\text{CM}}=z_{\text{CM}}\partialderivative{z_\text{CM}}-\bar{z}_{\text{CM}}\partialderivative{\bar{z}_\text{CM}},\quad z_{\text{CM}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}z_{i}

L_{\text{CM}}=0

This universality across interactions is remarkable given the lack of an energy gap protecting these excited states, but it can be understood from the structure of the bubble states. The compactness of the
\nu=1

1375_{1/r}

On the other hand, the

An electron-electron interaction that falls off too slowly with distance couples the intra-bubble degrees of freedom and the bubbles’ centers of mass. This disrupts and eventually breaks apart the bubbles. For
v(r)=-\ln(r)

Conclusion—
Our work initiates the study of highly-excited bubble states in the lowest Landau level.
Exact diagonalization calculations find eigenstates in the middle of the spectrum with unexpected density distribution and pair correlation function.
We show that these exceptional highly excited states contain bubbles of tightly bound electrons in a local
\nu=1
state, similar to the ground states of partially filled higher Landau levels.
Additional exact diagonalization calculations show that very similar bubble states exist for both the
1/r
Coulomb interaction and
1/r^{3}
dipole interaction.

The presence of a small number of eigenstates in the middle of the spectrum with exceptional pair correlation and density profile, combined with the remaining eigenstates being essentially featureless, is highly reminiscent of many-body scarring. Recently discovered in spin models
[
34
,
35
]
, many-body scars are exceptional eigenstates that have finite energy density yet have ground state-like properties such as area-law entanglement
[
36
,
35
]
in defiance of the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
. Scars have been found in a variety of models such as the PXP spin chain (modeling Rydberg atom arrays)
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
, spin-
1
AKLT model
[
40
,
41
]
, XY models
[
42
]
, Hubbard models
[
43
]
, and partially-filled Landau levels on a thin-torus (which map to spin chains)
[
44
]
.

In contrast, our work finds exceptional excited states of electrons in a continuum system, albeit with a finite number of electrons. We speculate that electron bubbles survive in the thermodynamic limit and may form a lattice, giving rise to quantum many-body scars. This is plausible for the same reason that our 6-electron bubble states are so robust against variations in the interaction: the compactness of the
\nu=1
state prevents changes in each bubbles’ internal structure, and the lattice symmetry renders the net force on each bubble zero. We leave the fate of the bubbles in the thermodynamic limit and other compelling questions to future studies.

It will be interesting to experimentally probe highly excited states in a Landau level droplet and search for signatures of electron bubbles. We propose that a periodic array of multi-electron molecules can be formed in semiconductor moiré superlattices, in which each molecule is locked to a moiré potential minimum as experimentally observed
[
45
,
46
]
. Applying a magnetic field may produce an array of multi-electron Landau level droplets
[
47
]
. We also envision that pumping this system with a light pulse and probing its long-lived response
[
48
,
49
]
may reveal spectroscopic signatures of electron bubbles such as their vibrational modes.
In a broader context, we hope that our work stimulates both the theoretical and experimental investigation of highly-excited non-thermal states in realistic condensed matter settings such as flat band systems.

Landau Levels in the Disk Geometry

II.1
Landau Level Raising and Lowering Operators

The position space Hamiltonian for
N
electrons confined to an infinite plane and subject to a perpendicular magnetic field is:

H=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2m}\left[{\mathbf{\bm{p}}}_{i}-q{\mathbf{\bm{A}}}({\mathbf{\bm{r}}}_{i})\right]^{2}+\sum_{i<j}v\left(|{\mathbf{\bm{r}}}_{i}-{\mathbf{\bm{r}}}_{j}|\right),

(S1)

where
{\mathbf{\bm{r}}}_{i}
and
{\mathbf{\bm{p}}}_{i}
are the position and canonical momenta respectively of the
i
th electron,
\hbar=1
,
q
is the electron charge, and
{\mathbf{\bm{A}}}({\mathbf{\bm{r}}})
is a vector potential generating a uniform perpendicular magnetic field. Throughout, we use the convention that Latin letters
i,j\ldots
index particles, Greek letters
\mu,\nu\ldots
index the in-plane direction, and
\epsilon_{\mu\nu}
is the Levi-Civita symbol in two dimensions. Additionally, we use the convention that
q
is positive, which is more natural and allows us to work with the complex coordinate
z=x+iy
instead of
z=x-iy
later.

The mechanical momentum (here, we drop the index
i
and work with only one particle, as the generalization to many particles is obvious) is:

{\mathbf{\bm{\pi}}}={\mathbf{\bm{p}}}-q{\mathbf{\bm{A}}}({\mathbf{\bm{r}}}).

(S2)

The mechanical momentum’s components satisfy the commutator:

\commutator{\pi_\mu}{\pi_\nu}=\frac{i}{l_{B}^{2}}\epsilon_{\mu\nu},

(S3)

where the magnetic length is defined as
l_{B}=1/\sqrt{qB}
. The mechanical momentum allows us to define the Landau level raising and lowering operators:

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\frac{l_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\pi_{x}+i\pi_{y}\right),

(S4)

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{l_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\pi_{x}-i\pi_{y}\right),

which satisfy:

\begin{gathered}\commutator{a}{a^\dagger}=1,\\
H=\frac{1}{2m}({\mathbf{\bm{p}}}-q{\mathbf{\bm{A}}})^{2}=\omega_{B}\left(a^{\dagger}a+\frac{1}{2}\right).\end{gathered}

(S5)

Above, we have defined the cyclotron frequency
\omega_{B}=qB/m
, which is the energy scale of the magnetic field. For weak interactions
V\ll\omega_{B}
, the majority of the physics occurs in the lowest Landau level where
a\ket{\psi}=0
.

II.2
Guiding Center Operators

Classically, a particle in a magnetic field follows a circular trajectory of angular frequency
\omega_{B}
around a fixed guiding center. The particle’s energy comes from the rotational kinetic energy around the guiding center, which depends on the orbital radius. Meanwhile, the Landau level’s degeneracy comes from the fact that there are many points around which the electron could orbit. In the quantum theory, the Landau level raising and lowering operators change the orbital radius and hence the energy. Analogously, we should be able to identify operators in the quantum theory that correspond to the guiding center, enabling us to move within the highly-degenerate Landau levels.

From the Lorentz force law, it is easy to see that a particle’s displacement from the guiding center is locked to its momentum:

R_{\mu}=r_{\mu}+l_{B}^{2}\epsilon_{\mu\nu}\pi_{\nu}.

(S6)

Above, we use
{\mathbf{\bm{\pi}}}
as it and not
{\mathbf{\bm{p}}}
corresponds to the physical velocity of the electron. The guiding center commutes with the mechanical momenta and thus the kinetic energy:

\displaystyle\commutator{\pi_\mu}{R_\nu}

\displaystyle=\commutator{\pi_\mu}{r_\nu+ l_B^2 \epsilon_{\nu\kappa}\pi_\kappa}

(S7)

\displaystyle=-i\delta_{\mu\nu}+l_{B}^{2}\epsilon_{\nu\kappa}\frac{i}{l_{B}^{2}}\epsilon_{\mu\kappa}

\displaystyle=-i\delta_{\mu\nu}+i\delta_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=0,

as expected from our classical intuition. However, the guiding center’s
x
-coordinate and
y
-coordinate do not commute:

\displaystyle\commutator{R_x}{R_y}

\displaystyle=\commutator{x + l_B^2 \pi_y}{y - l_B^2 \pi_x}

(S8)

\displaystyle=-il_{B}^{2}-il_{B}^{2}+l_{B}^{4}\frac{i}{l_{B}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-il_{B}^{2},

where we have used
\commutator{r_\mu}{\pi_\nu}=\commutator{r_\mu}{p_\nu}
.

Eq.
S8
allows us to bound the degree to which
R_{x}
and
R_{y}
may be simultaneously localized. Letting
\sigma(R_{\mu})
be the standard deviation of
R_{\mu}
, the AM-GM inequality implies that:

\sigma({\mathbf{\bm{R}}})=\sqrt{\sigma(R_{x})^{2}+\sigma(R_{y})^{2}}\geq\sqrt{2\sigma(R_{x})\sigma(R_{y})}.

(S9)

The Heisenberg uncertainty principle requires that:

\sigma(R_{x})\sigma(R_{y})\geq\frac{1}{2}|\expectationvalue{\comm{R_x}{R_y}}|=\frac{l_{B}^{2}}{2}.

(S10)

Thus, we have:

\sigma({\mathbf{\bm{R}}})\geq l_{B},

(S11)

or in other words, the guiding center may not be localized beyond the magnetic length.

Our next goal is to construct magnetic translation operators
T({\mathbf{\bm{a}}})
such that
T^{\dagger}({\mathbf{\bm{a}}}){\mathbf{\bm{R}}}T({\mathbf{\bm{a}}})={\mathbf{\bm{R}}}+{\mathbf{\bm{a}}}
. The commutator between
R_{x}
and
R_{y}
suggests the following guess for a translation operator:

T({\mathbf{\bm{a}}})=\exp(-i\frac{\epsilon_{\mu\nu}}{l_B^2}R_\mu a_\nu).

(S12)

In the following, two identities will be helpful. Assuming that
A
and
B
are linear operators such that
\commutator{A}{B}
commutes with
A
and
B
(this is true for us, because

and
R_{y}

\displaystyle e^{A}Be^{-A}

\displaystyle=B+\commutator{A}{B},

(S13)

\displaystyle e^{A}e^{B}

\displaystyle=e^{A+B+\frac{1}{2}\commutator{A}{B}}.

Then we have:

\displaystyle T^{\dagger}({\mathbf{\bm{a}}})R_{\kappa}T({\mathbf{\bm{a}}})

(S14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(+i\frac{\epsilon_{\mu\nu}}{l_B^2}R_\mu a_\nu)R_{\kappa}\exp(-i\frac{\epsilon_{\mu\nu}}{l_B^2}R_\mu a_\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{\kappa}+i\frac{\epsilon_{\mu\nu}}{l_{B}^{2}}a_{\nu}\commutator{R_\mu}{R_\kappa}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{\kappa}+i\frac{\epsilon_{\mu\nu}}{l_{B}^{2}}a_{\nu}(-il_{B}^{2}\epsilon_{\mu\kappa})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(R+a)_{k},

showing that the proposed operator performs the translation as desired.

An important feature of the magnetic translation operators is that they commute only up to a phase. We have:

\displaystyle T({\mathbf{\bm{a}}})T({\mathbf{\bm{b}}})

(S15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(\frac{1}{2}\comm{-i\frac{\epsilon_{\mu\nu}}{l_B^2}R_\mu a_\nu}{-i\frac{\epsilon_{\kappa\lambda}}{l_B^2}R_\kappa b_\lambda})T({\mathbf{\bm{a}}}+{\mathbf{\bm{b}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(-\frac{\epsilon_{\mu\nu} \epsilon_{\kappa\lambda}}{2l_B^4} a_\nu b_\lambda\comm{R_\mu}{R_k})T({\mathbf{\bm{a}}}+{\mathbf{\bm{b}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(-\frac{\epsilon_{\mu\nu} \epsilon_{\kappa\lambda}}{2l_B^4} a_\nu b_\lambda(-i\epsilon_{\mu\kappa}l_B^2))T({\mathbf{\bm{a}}}+{\mathbf{\bm{b}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(i\frac{\epsilon_{\nu\lambda}}{2l_B^2} a_\nu b_\lambda)T({\mathbf{\bm{a}}}+{\mathbf{\bm{b}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(i\frac{\v a \wedge\v b}{2l_B^2})T({\mathbf{\bm{a}}}+{\mathbf{\bm{b}}}),

where
{\mathbf{\bm{a}}}\wedge{\mathbf{\bm{b}}}=a_{x}b_{y}-a_{y}b_{x}
is the two-dimensional wedge product. Eq.
S15
yields the product of four translation operators around a loop:

T({\mathbf{\bm{a}}})T({\mathbf{\bm{b}}})T(-{\mathbf{\bm{a}}})T(-{\mathbf{\bm{b}}})=\exp(i\frac{\v a \wedge\v b}{l_B^2}),

(S16)

which is consistent with the Aharanov-Bohm phase accumulated over such a path.

II.3
Symmetric Gauge Operators

For our system, it is convenient to work in the symmetric gauge
{\mathbf{\bm{A}}}={\mathbf{\bm{B}}}\crossproduct{\mathbf{\bm{r}}}/2=\left(-By/2,Bx/2,0\right)
, which manifestly respects rotation symmetry. In the symmetric gauge, the natural coordinates are the complex
z=x+iy
and its conjugate
z^{*}=x-iy
. The derivative conversions are:

\displaystyle\partial

\displaystyle\equiv\partialderivative{z}=\partialderivative{x}\partialderivative{x}{z}+\partialderivative{y}\partialderivative{y}{z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partialderivative{x}-i\partialderivative{y}\right),

(S17)

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}

\displaystyle\equiv\partialderivative{\bar{z}}=\partialderivative{x}\partialderivative{x}{\bar{z}}+\partialderivative{y}\partialderivative{y}{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partialderivative{x}+i\partialderivative{y}\right),

\displaystyle\partialderivative{x}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{z}\partialderivative{z}{x}+\partialderivative{\bar{z}}\partialderivative{\bar{z}}{x}=\partial+\bar{\partial},

\displaystyle\partialderivative{y}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{z}\partialderivative{z}{y}+\partialderivative{\bar{z}}\partialderivative{\bar{z}}{y}=i(\partial-\bar{\partial}),

where
\partial
and
\bar{\partial}
treat
z
and
\bar{z}
as independent. Because
\left(\partial_{x}\right)^{\dagger}=-\partial_{x}
and
\left(\partial_{y}\right)^{\dagger}=-\partial_{y}
, we have
\partial^{\dagger}=-\bar{\partial}
. It is also helpful to remember that
A_{x}+iA_{y}=iBz/2
and
A_{x}-iA_{y}=-iB\bar{z}/2
.
In terms of the complex coordinates, the Landau level raising and lowering operators become:

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\frac{l_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(-i\partial_{x}-qA_{x}+\partial_{y}-iqA_{y}\right)

(S18)

\displaystyle=\frac{l_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(-2i\bar{\partial}-\frac{iqBz}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-i\sqrt{2}\left(l_{B}\bar{\partial}+\frac{z}{4l_{B}}\right),

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=-i\sqrt{2}\left(l_{B}\partial-\frac{\bar{z}}{4l_{B}}\right).

The (canonical) angular momentum is defined as:

L=xp_{y}-yp_{x},

(S19)

which may be rewritten in terms of the complex coordinates as:

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=-i\left(\frac{z+\bar{z}}{2}\right)\cdot i\left(\partial-\bar{\partial}\right)+i\left(\frac{z-\bar{z}}{2i}\right)\cdot\left(\partial+\bar{\partial}\right)

(S20)

\displaystyle=z\partial-\bar{z}\bar{\partial}.

Acting on a term,
L
counts the number of
z
’s minus the number of
\bar{z}
’s, which makes sense as each
z
(
\bar{z}
) contributes a factor of
+1
(
-1
) to the winding about the origin.

We desire operators that simultaneously raise or lower
L
while commuting with the Hamiltonian. The previously defined guiding center operators provide a natural way to do this, given that they commute with the Hamiltonian and may be combined in a way like
R_{x}+iR_{y}
to increase the wavefunction’s winding about the origin. Consider:

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2}l_{B}}\left(R_{x}-iR_{y}\right),

(S21)

\displaystyle b^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}l_{B}}\left(R_{x}+iR_{y}\right),

which in terms of the complex coordinates is:

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2}l_{B}}\left(x+l_{B}^{2}\pi_{y}-iy+il_{B}^{2}\pi_{x}\right)

(S22)

\displaystyle=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2}l_{B}}\left(\bar{z}+il_{B}^{2}\left[\pi_{x}-i\pi_{y}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2}l_{B}}\left(\bar{z}+il_{B}^{2}\left[-2i\partial+\frac{iqB\bar{z}}{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=-i\sqrt{2}\left(l_{B}\partial+\frac{\bar{z}}{4l_{B}}\right),

\displaystyle b^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=-i\sqrt{2}\left(l_{B}\bar{\partial}-\frac{z}{4l_{B}}\right).

Because
b
either kills a factor of
z
or creates a factor of
\bar{z}
when acting on a state, it is clear that
b
lowers the angular momentum by one, and likewise
b^{\dagger}
clearly acts as a raising operator.

It is helpful for later derivations to express
z
,
\bar{z}
,
\partial
,
\bar{\partial}
in terms of the
a
and
b
operators. From Eqs.
S18
and
S22
, a short derivation shows that:

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=i\sqrt{2}l_{B}\left(a-b^{\dagger}\right),

(S23)

\displaystyle\bar{z}

\displaystyle=-i\sqrt{2}l_{B}\left(a^{\dagger}-b\right),

\displaystyle\partial

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\sqrt{2}l_{B}}\left(a^{\dagger}+b\right),

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\sqrt{2}l_{B}}\left(a+b^{\dagger}\right).

Similarly, the angular momentum may be expressed as:

L=-a^{\dagger}a+b^{\dagger}b.

(S24)

For a many-particle system, the total angular momentum is:

L_{\text{tot}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}L_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(-a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{i}+b^{\dagger}_{i}b_{i}\right),

(S25)

where
a_{i}
denotes the Landau level lowering operator for particle
i
, and likewise for
b_{i}
. The many-body Hamiltonian is:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=T+V,

(S26)

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\omega_{B}\sum_{i}\left(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}v\left(\sqrt{\left[\bar{z}_{i}-\bar{z}_{j}\right]\left[z_{i}-z_{j}\right]}\right).

L_{\text{tot}}
commutes with
T
because
L_{\text{tot}}
preserves the total number of
a
-quanta. To see that
L_{\text{tot}}
commutes with
V
, first note that any complex coordinate
z_{i}
increments
L_{\text{tot}}
by one (from Eq.
S23
,
z_{i}
either creates a
b
-quanta or destroys an
a
-quanta), while any conjugate coordinate
\bar{z}_{i}
decrements
L_{\text{tot}}
, so any operator such as
V
with a balanced number of
z
’s and
\bar{z}
’s commutes with
L_{\text{tot}}
. Therefore, the total angular momentum is conserved quantity.

II.4
Center-of-Mass Angular Momentum

When there is no external confining potential, there is an additional conserved quantity: the center-of-mass angular momentum. Intuitively, this is conserved because all electrons can be displaced simultaneously without changing the energy. To show this mathematically, we transform into the center-of-mass frame, where we have the center-of-mass coordinate:

Z_{1}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}z_{i},

(S27)

along with
N-1
relative coordinates such as
z_{2}-z_{1},z_{3}-z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}-z_{1}
. Following
S19
, we may define the center-of-mass angular momentum operator:

L_{\text{CM}}=Z_{1}\partialderivative{Z_1}-\bar{Z}_{1}\partialderivative{\bar{Z}_1}.

(S28)

If one moves only the center-of-mass by a displacement
\Delta z
but does not alter the
N-1
relative coordinates, then each of the
z_{i}
must also displace by
\Delta z
. Therefore, we have
\partialderivative{z_i}{Z_1}=1
, allowing us to re-express Eq.
S28
as:

\displaystyle L_{\text{CM}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\left[z_{i}\partialderivative{z_j}\partialderivative{z_j}{Z_1}-\bar{z}_{i}\partialderivative{\bar{z}_j}\partialderivative{\bar{z}_j}{\bar{Z}_1}\right]

(S29)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{ij}\left(z_{i}\partial_{j}-\bar{z}_{i}\bar{\partial}_{j}\right).

In terms of the
a
and
b
ladder operators, the center-of-mass angular momentum is:

\displaystyle L_{\text{CM}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{ij}\left(z_{i}\partial_{j}-\bar{z}_{i}\bar{\partial}_{j}\right)

(S30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{ij}\bigg[-\frac{1}{2}\left(a_{i}-b^{\dagger}_{i}\right)\left(a^{\dagger}_{j}+b_{j}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(a^{\dagger}_{i}-b_{i}\right)\left(a_{j}+b^{\dagger}_{j}\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{ij}\left[-a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}\right].

We may express the center-of-mass angular momentum more concisely in terms of “center-of-mass ladder operators”:

\displaystyle a_{\text{CM}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}+\ldots+a_{N}}{\sqrt{N}},

(S31)

\displaystyle b_{\text{CM}}

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{1}+b_{2}+\ldots+b_{N}}{\sqrt{N}},

\displaystyle L_{\text{CM}}

\displaystyle=-a_{\text{CM}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{CM}}+b_{\text{CM}}^{\dagger}b_{\text{CM}}.

From Eq.
S30
, it is clear that
L_{\text{CM}}
does not alter the number of a
a
or
b
-quanta, so
L_{\text{CM}}
commutes with
L_{\text{tot}}
. Due to to translation invariance,
V
only depends on the differences between coordinates and thus can be expressed using only differences between ladder operators, such as
a_{ij}\equiv a_{i}-a_{j}
or
b_{ij}\equiv b_{i}-b_{j}
. It is easy to see that the difference operators
a_{ij}
and
b_{ij}
must always commute with the center-of-mass operators (which is an equal linear combination of all the single-particle operators), so
a_{\text{CM}}
and
b_{\text{CM}}
commute with
V
.
a_{\text{CM}}
does not commute with the kinetic energy
T
, but
a_{\text{CM}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{CM}}
does. Therefore, the “center-of-mass Landau level”
a_{\text{CM}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{CM}}
and center-of-mass angular momentum
-a_{\text{CM}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{CM}}+b_{\text{CM}}^{\dagger}b_{\text{CM}}
index symmetry sectors. Without Landau level mixing,
a^{\dagger}_{\text{CM}}a_{\text{CM}}=0
and is irrelevant.

III
Exact Diagonalization Matrix Elements

III.1
Single-Particle Basis

The unique normalized state satisfying
a\ket{n}=0
and
b^{\dagger}b\ket{n}=n\ket{n}
is:

\ket{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n!\,2^{n}}}z^{n}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4}},

(S32)

where we take
l_{B}=1
from here on for convenience. To see this rigorously, consider a wavefunction
\psi(z,\bar{z})=f(z,\bar{z})\exp(-|z|^2/4)
(one may always choose to parameterize a wavefunction in this form) that is annihilated by
a
:

\displaystyle-i\sqrt{2}\left(\bar{\partial}+\frac{z}{4}\right)\left(f(z,\bar{z})e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4}}\right)

\displaystyle=0

(S33)

\displaystyle\left[\bar{\partial}f(z,\bar{z})\right]e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4}}-f(z,\bar{z})\frac{z}{4}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4}}

\displaystyle+\frac{z}{4}\left(f(z,\bar{z})e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4}}\right)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}f(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle=0.

Thus, any wavefunction lying entirely in the LLL must be the product of a holomorphic function
f(z)
and a Gaussian envelope
\exp(-|z|^2/4)
. Demanding that we have an angular momentum eigenstate forces
f(z)
to to be of constant degree
f(z)\propto z^{n}
, and the normalization in Eq.
S32
is easily determined from standard integrals.

III.2
Two-Body Matrix Elements

To build the many-body Hamiltonian, we need the matrix elements between two-body product states:

V^{l}_{n_{1}n_{2}}=\bra{n_1 + l, n_2}v(|z_{1}-z_{2}|)\ket{n_1, n_2 + l},

(S34)

where the two-body product states are:

\ket{n_1, n_2}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{n_{1}!\,n_{2}!\,2^{n_{1}+n_{2}}}}z_{1}^{n_{1}}z_{2}^{n_{2}}e^{-\frac{|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}}{4}}.

(S35)

Here, we demonstrate a method for analytically calculating the matrix elements of any interaction in terms of the corresponding Haldane pseudopotential and hypergeometric functions.

To start, observe that the two-body problem in the LLL is exactly solvable because both the center-of-mass angular momentum and relative angular momentum are conserved by the electron-electron interaction. For a prescribed center-of-mass angular momentum
M
and relative angular momentum
m
, the only possible state is:

\begin{gathered}v=(z_{1}+z_{2})/\sqrt{2},\quad w=(z_{1}-z_{2})/\sqrt{2},\\
\ket{M, m}_{\text{rel}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{M!\,m!\,2^{M+m}}}v^{M}w^{m}e^{-\frac{|v|^{2}+|w|^{2}}{4}}.\end{gathered}

(S36)

The corresponding eigenvalue is independent of
M
and is known as the Haldane pseudopotential:

\displaystyle v_{m}

\displaystyle=\bra{M, m}_{\text{rel}}v(|z_{1}-z_{2}|)\ket{M, m}_{\text{rel}}

(S37)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi m!\,2^{m}}\int\text{d}^{2}w\left[v\left(\sqrt{2}|w|\right)|w|^{2m}e^{-\frac{|w|^{2}}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m!}\int_{u=\frac{w^{2}}{2}=0}^{u=\infty}\text{d}u\left[v\left(2\sqrt{u}\right)u^{m}e^{-u}\right].

In general,
u^{m}e^{-u}
is peaked near
u\approx m
, so
v_{m}\sim v(2\sqrt{m})
for large
m
. This is expected, as the amplitude of
\ket{M, m}_{\text{rel}}
is peaked when the electrons are a distance of
2\sqrt{m}
apart. For bosons, the minimum relative angular momentum channel is
m=0
, so for an interaction to be well-defined, it must diverge slower than
v(r)\propto 1/r^{2}
. For fermions, the minimum relative angular momentum channel is
m=1
, so for an interaction to be well-defined, it must diverge slower than
v(r)\propto 1/r^{4}
.

Using the definition of the gamma function, Eq.
S37
can be evaluated analytically for power-law interactions
v(r)=r^{\alpha}
:

v_{m}\left[v(r)=r^{\alpha}\right]=\frac{2^{\alpha}\Gamma(m+1+\frac{\alpha}{2})}{\Gamma(m+1)}.

(S38)

We may also consider logarithmic repulsion
v(r)=-\ln(r)
, for which the pseudopotential becomes:

\displaystyle v_{m}\left[v(r)=-\ln(r)\right]

(S39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{m!}\int_{u=\frac{w^{2}}{2}=0}^{u=\infty}\text{d}u\left[\ln\left(2\sqrt{u}\right)u^{m}e^{-u}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\ln(2)-\frac{1}{2\Gamma(m+1)}\int_{u=0}^{u=\infty}\text{d}u\left[\ln\left(u\right)u^{m}e^{-u}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\ln(2)-\frac{1}{2\Gamma(m+1)}\derivative{m}\left(\int_{u=0}^{u=\infty}\text{d}u\left[u^{m}e^{-u}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\ln(2)-\frac{1}{2}\psi(m+1),\quad\psi(m)=\derivative{m}\ln\Gamma(m).

Above,
\psi(m)
is known as the digamma function and may be computed using standard libraries.

Then to calculate
V^{l}_{n_{1}n_{2}}
, all that we need to do is express
\ket{n_1, n_2}
as a linear combination of the center-of-mass and relative angular momentum eigenstates
\ket{M, m}_{\text{rel}}
, in which the interaction is diagonal. This expansion may be preformed by substituting
z_{1}=(v+w)/\sqrt{2},z_{2}=(v-w)/\sqrt{2}
and expanding out using the binomial theorem:

\displaystyle\ket{n_1, n_2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{n_{1}!\,n_{2}!\,2^{n_{1}+n_{2}}}}\bigg(\frac{v+w}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg)^{n_{1}}\bigg(\frac{v-w}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg)^{n_{2}}e^{-\frac{|v|^{2}+|w|^{2}}{4}}

(S40)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{n_{1}!\,n_{2}!}\,2^{n_{1}+n_{2}}}(v+w)^{n_{1}}(v-w)^{n_{2}}e^{-\frac{|v|^{2}+|w|^{2}}{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{n_{1}!\,n_{2}!}\,2^{n_{1}+n_{2}}}\Bigg[\sum_{a=0}^{n_{1}}\binom{n_{1}}{a}v^{n_{1}-a}w^{a}\Bigg]\Bigg[\sum_{b=0}^{n_{2}}(-1)^{b}\binom{n_{2}}{b}v^{n_{2}-b}w^{b}\Bigg]e^{-\frac{|v|^{2}+|w|^{2}}{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{n_{1}!\,n_{2}!}\,2^{n_{1}+n_{2}}}\sum_{m=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a\leq n_{1}\\
0\leq b\leq n_{2}\\
a+b=m\end{subarray}}(-1)^{b}\binom{n_{1}}{a}\binom{n_{2}}{b}\right]v^{n_{1}+n_{2}-m}w^{m}e^{-\frac{|v|^{2}+|w|^{2}}{4}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\left[\frac{2\pi\sqrt{(n_{1}+n_{2}-m)!\,m!\,2^{n_{1}+n_{2}}}}{2\pi\sqrt{n_{1}!\,n_{2}!}\,2^{n_{1}+n_{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a\leq n_{1}\\
0\leq b\leq n_{2}\\
a+b=m\end{subarray}}(-1)^{b}\binom{n_{1}}{a}\binom{n_{2}}{b}\right]\ket{n_1 + n_2 - m, m}_{\text{rel}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\left[\sqrt{\frac{(n_{1}+n_{2}-m)!\,m!}{n_{1}!\,n_{2}!\,2^{n_{1}+n_{2}}}}\sum_{a=\max(m-n_{2},0)}^{\min(m,n_{1})}(-1)^{m-a}\binom{n_{1}}{a}\binom{n_{2}}{m-a}\right]\ket{n_1 + n_2 - m, m}_{\text{rel}}.

The combinatorial sum over
a
becomes difficult to evaluate numerically for high
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
due to the alternating sign
(-1)^{m-a}
. Luckily, the sum may be expressed in terms of a hypergeometric function:

\sum_{a=\max(m-n_{2},0)}^{\min(m,n_{1})}(-1)^{m-a}\binom{n_{1}}{a}\binom{n_{2}}{m-a}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{m}\binom{n_{2}}{m}{}_{2}F_{1}(-m,-n_{1};1-m+n_{2};-1)&m\leq n_{2}\\
(-1)^{n_{2}}\binom{n_{1}}{m-n_{2}}{}_{2}F_{1}(m-n_{1}-n_{2},-n_{2};1+m-n_{2};-1)&m>n_{2}\\
\end{cases}.

(S41)

Here,
{}_{2}F_{1}
is the Gauss hypergeometric function expressed as the following power series:

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},\quad(q)_{n}=\begin{cases}1&n=0\\
q(q+1)\cdots(q+n-1)&n>0\end{cases}.

(S42)

If one of the first two arguments is nonpositive, then the power series is finite:

{}_{2}F_{1}(-m,b;c;z)=\sum_{n=0}^{m}(-1)^{n}\binom{m}{n}\frac{(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}z^{n},

(S43)

where
m
is assumed positive. Eq.
S43
can be used to prove the correctness of Eq.
S41
. For the first case, where
m\leq n_{2}
, both the first and second argument of
{}_{2}F_{1}
are nonpositive:

\displaystyle(-1)^{m}\binom{n_{2}}{m}{}_{2}F_{1}(-m,-n_{1};1-m+n_{2};-1)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\binom{n_{2}}{m}\sum_{a=0}^{m}(-1)^{a}\binom{m}{a}\frac{(-n_{1})_{a}}{(1-m+n_{2})_{a}}(-1)^{a}

(S44)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\frac{n_{2}!}{m!\,(n_{2}-m)!}\sum_{a=0}^{\min(m,n_{1})}\frac{m!}{a!\,(m-a)!}\frac{(-1)^{a}\frac{n_{1}!}{(n_{1}-a)!}}{\frac{(1-m+n_{2}+a-1)!}{(1-m+n_{2}-1)!}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=\max(m-n_{2},0)}^{\min(m,n_{1})}(-1)^{m-a}\binom{n_{1}}{a}\binom{n_{2}}{m-a}.

For the second case, where
m>n_{2}
:

\displaystyle(-1)^{n_{2}}\binom{n_{1}}{m-n_{2}}{}_{2}F_{1}(m-n_{1}-n_{2},-n_{2};1+m-n_{2};-1)

(S45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n_{2}}\binom{n_{1}}{m-n_{2}}\sum_{b^{\prime}=0}^{\min(n_{2},n_{1}+n_{2}-m)}(-1)^{b^{\prime}}\binom{n_{2}}{b^{\prime}}\frac{(m-n_{1}-n_{2})_{b^{\prime}}}{(1+m-n_{2})_{b^{\prime}}}(-1)^{b^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n_{2}}\frac{n_{1}!}{(m-n_{2})!\,(n_{1}+n_{2}-m)!}\sum_{b^{\prime}=0}^{n_{2}+\min(0,n_{1}-m)}\frac{n_{2}!}{b^{\prime}!\,(n_{2}-b^{\prime})!}\frac{(-1)^{b^{\prime}}\frac{(n_{1}+n_{2}-m)!}{(n_{1}+n_{2}-m-b^{\prime})!}}{\frac{(1+m-n_{2}+b^{\prime}-1)!}{(1+m-n_{2}-1)!}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{b^{\prime}=0}^{n_{2}+\min(0,n_{1}-m)}(-1)^{n_{2}-b^{\prime}}\binom{n_{1}}{m-n_{2}+b^{\prime}}\binom{n_{2}}{n_{2}-b^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=m-n_{2}+b^{\prime}=\max(m-n_{2},0)}^{\min(m,n_{1})}(-1)^{m-a}\binom{n_{1}}{a}\binom{n_{2}}{m-a}.

Although it may appear that we have just wrapped a difficult sum into a convenient special function, there exist sophisticated algorithms for accurately calculating the hypergeometric function. In contrast, the original direct summation in Eq.
S40
is highly susceptible to precision loss and becomes unusable when
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
exceed 50.

Combining Eq.
S40
with Eq.
S41
yields:

\begin{gathered}\ket{n_1, n_2}=\sum_{m=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}C^{m}_{n_{1}n_{2}}\ket{n_1 + n_2 - m, m}_{\text{rel}},\\
C^{m}_{n_{1}n_{2}}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{m}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{n_{1}+n_{2}}}\binom{n_{1}+n_{2}-m}{n_{1}}\binom{n_{2}}{m}}{}_{2}F_{1}(-m,-n_{1};1-m+n_{2};-1)&m\leq n_{2}\\
(-1)^{n_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{n_{1}+n_{2}}}\binom{m}{n_{2}}\binom{n_{1}}{m-n_{2}}}{}_{2}F_{1}(m-n_{1}-n_{2},-n_{2};1+m-n_{2};-1)&m>n_{2}\\
\end{cases}.\\
\end{gathered}

(S46)

Then the Coulomb matrix elements may be calculated easily as:

V^{l}_{n_{1}n_{2}}=\bra{n_1 + l, n_2}v(|{\mathbf{\bm{r}}}_{1}-{\mathbf{\bm{r}}}_{2}|)\ket{n_1, n_2 + l}=\sum_{m=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}+l}v_{m}\left(C^{m}_{n_{1}+l,n_{2}}\right)^{*}C^{m}_{n_{1},n_{2}+l},

(S47)

where all terms in the sum are
O(1)
because the expansion coefficients
C^{m}_{n_{1}n_{2}}
must satisfy the normalization condition
\sum_{m=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}|C^{m}_{n_{1}n_{2}}|^{2}
.

Checking the orthogonality of different pairs of
\ket{n_1, n_2}
provides a convenient way to detect numerical error. In our code, no precision loss beyond machine precision is observed even as
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
approach
100
. We note that specifically for the Coulomb interaction
v(r)=1/r
, there exist other numerically stable ways of calculating the two-body matrix elements
[
50
]
. We have verified that our method is consistent with others in the literature up to machine precision.

III.3
Many-Body Matrix Elements

For
N
electrons, the many-body states are Slater determinants of symmetric gauge orbitals, which are stored in the code as a sorted list of occupied orbitals
\ket{i_1 < i_2 < \ldots< i_N}
. Throughout, we use capital Latin letters
I,J,\ldots
to represent Slater determinants. In our exact diagonalization calculations, we work within a given total angular momentum sector, so the sum
i_{1}+i_{2}+\ldots+i_{N}=L_{\text{tot}}
for each Slater determinant is constrained. We note that total angular momentum conservation means that the angular momentum of any individual electron is bounded: the maximum angular momentum that any electron may have is
l_{\text{max}}=L_{\text{tot}}-(0+1+\ldots+N-2)=L_{\text{tot}}-(N-1)(N-2)/2
, which is attained by the determinant
\ket{0, 1, 2, \ldots N - 2, l_\text{max}}
. This naturally renders both the single-particle basis and many-body Hilbert space finite. We note that one may not artificially cutoff the single-particle basis, as this is equivalent to enforcing a hard potential wall and thus spoils the center-of-mass angular momentum conservation.

Due to angular momentum conservation, a Slater determinant only has nonzero matrix element with itself or another Slater determinant connected by double replacement:

\bra{J}H\ket{I}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij\in I}\bra{ij}v\bigg[\ket{ij}-\ket{ji}\bigg],&I=J\\
\text{sgn}(\sigma)\bra{ab}v\bigg[\ket{ij}-\ket{ji}\bigg],&J=\sigma\left(I^{ab}_{ij}\right)\\
\end{cases}.

(S48)

Above,
I^{ab}_{ij}
denotes the Slater determinant constructed from replacing filled orbitals
i
and
j
with new orbitals
a
and
b
,
\sigma
is the permutation that sorts the resulting Slater determinant (permutations of the occupied orbitals do not generate distinct determinants), and
\text{sgn}(\sigma)
is the sign of said permutation. In the second case,
i
,
j
,
a
, and
b
must all be distinct.

To determine the action of the center-of-mass angular momentum lowering operator, we first put aside particle statistics temporarily. When working with
N
distinguishable particles, a natural many-body state is the tensor product:

\ket{l_1, l_2, \ldots, l_N}_{D}\equiv\ket{l_1}\otimes\ket{l_2}\otimes\ldots\otimes\ket{l_N},

(S49)

where the “
D
” subscript on the LHS clarifies that the particles are “distinguishable”. The action of the center-of-mass lowering operator on this state is:

\displaystyle b_{\text{CM}}\ket{l_1, l_2, \ldots, l_N}_{D}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{l_{1}}{N}}\ket{l_1 - 1, l_2, \ldots, l_N}_{D}

(S50)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{l_{2}}{N}}\ket{l_1, l_2 - 1, \ldots, l_N}_{D}

\displaystyle\ldots

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{l_{N}}{N}}\ket{l_1, l_2, \ldots, l_N - 1}_{D}.

The antisymmetrization operator, which converts tensor product states
\ket{l_1, l_2, \ldots, l_N}_{D}
into Slater determinants
\ket{l_1, l_2, \ldots, l_N}
, commutes with
b_{\text{CM}}
since
b_{\text{CM}}
treats all particles on equal footing. Thus, Eq.
S50
applies similarly to Slater determinants:

\displaystyle b_{\text{CM}}\ket{l_1, l_2, \ldots, l_N}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{l_{1}}{N}}\ket{l_1 - 1, l_2, \ldots, l_N}

(S51)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{l_{2}}{N}}\ket{l_1, l_2 - 1, \ldots, l_N}

\displaystyle\ldots

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{l_{N}}{N}}\ket{l_1, l_2, \ldots, l_N - 1},

where we identify Slater determinants containing duplicated orbitals with the zero vector.
