Quantum walk on the Bloch sphere
Liwei Duan

Abstract
A scheme for implementing the discrete-time quantum walk on the Bloch sphere is proposed, which is closely related to the SU(2) group. A spin cluster serves as the walker, whereas its location on the Bloch sphere is described by the spin coherent state. An additional spin that interacts with the spin cluster plays the role of a coin, whose state determines the rotation of the spin cluster. The Wigner function is calculated to visualize the movement of the walker on the Bloch sphere, with which the probability distribution and the standard deviation are also achieved. The quadratic enhancement of variance for the quantum walk on the Bloch sphere is confirmed. Compared to the ideal quantum walk on a circle, the walker’s states on the Bloch sphere are generally nonorthogonal, whose drawbacks can be eliminated by increasing the number of spins in the spin cluster.

Generally, the discrete-time quantum walk consists of a walker moving in some space, and a flipped coin whose state determines the movement of the walker. The position space

The walker’s state determines the location on the phase plane, which consists of all possible values of position and momentum variables. Previous theoretical studies mainly focus on ideal localized states for the walker in the phase space. There is no overlap, namely that states corresponding to different locations are orthogonal. II.1
Spin-coherent state and Bloch sphere

I begin by briefly reviewing the spin coherent state, which corresponds to a point on the surface of the Bloch sphere
Zhang et al. 1990
;
Gazeau
.
It can be written as

\displaystyle\left|{}\theta,\phi\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[\frac{\theta}{2}\left(e^{i\phi}\hat{J}_{-}-e^{-i\phi}\hat{J}_{+}\right)\right]\left|{}J,J\right>

(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos^{2J}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\exp\left[\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)e^{i\phi}\hat{J}_{-}\right]\left|{}J,J\right>,

where
\hat{J}_{\gamma}
(
\gamma=x,y,z
) are the collective spin operators and
\hat{J}_{\pm}=\hat{J}_{x}\pm i\hat{J}_{y}
are the corresponding ladder operators.
\hat{J}_{\gamma}
can also be regarded as the generators of the SU(2) group.
\left|{}J,J\right>
is a Dicke state which satisfies
\hat{J}_{z}\left|{}J,J\right>=J\left|{}J,J\right>
.
For the spin coherent state
\left|{}\theta,\phi\right>
, the expectation values of the collective spin operators
J_{\gamma}=\left<{}\theta,\phi\right|\hat{J}_{\gamma}\left|{}\theta,\phi\right>
are

\displaystyle\left(J_{x},J_{y},J_{z}\right)/J=\left(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta\right).

(2)

Therefore, it is located on the Bloch sphere with polar angle
\theta
and azimuthal angle
\phi

Zhang et al. 1990
. It should be noted that
Arecchi et al. 1972

\displaystyle\left|\langle{}\theta,\phi|{}\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}\rangle\right|=\cos^{2J}\frac{\Theta}{2},

(3)

where
\Theta
is the angle between the
(\theta,\phi)
and the
(\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})
directions and satisfies

\displaystyle\cos\Theta=\cos\theta\cos\theta^{\prime}+\sin\theta\sin\theta^{\prime}\cos\left(\phi-\phi^{\prime}\right).

(4)

Then, the spin coherent states are, in general, not orthogonal except for antipodal points (
\Theta=\pi
)
Zhang et al. 1990
. The orthogonality is achieved in the limit of
J\rightarrow\infty
, for arbitrary two spin coherent states with
\Theta\neq 0
.

A generic rotation on the Bloch sphere can be described by the rotating operator, defined by

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{\mathbf{n}}(\alpha)=e^{-i\alpha\mathbf{n}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{J}}},

(5)

which indicates a rotation by angle
\alpha
along the
\mathbf{n}
direction.
Without loss of generality,
\mathbf{n}=(0,0,1)
is chosen and the corresponding rotating operator is labeled as
\hat{R}_{z}(\alpha)
in what follows.
For each
\theta
, there exists a corresponding circle on the Bloch sphere. A set of equally displacing sites on the circle can be written as
\left|{}\theta,\phi_{n}=n\delta\phi\right>
,
with
n\in\left[-\frac{L}{2},\frac{L}{2}\right]
,
\delta\phi=2\pi/L
, and
L
as the total number of sites. From Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
), the overlap between different states
|\langle{}\theta,\phi_{m}|{}\theta,\phi_{n}\rangle|
is smallest and the quantum walk on the Bloch sphere can better mimic the ideal one when
\theta=\pi/2
. Therefore, I focus on
\theta=\pi/2
and the corresponding states are labeled as

\displaystyle\left|{}\phi_{n}\right>=\left|{}\theta=\frac{\pi}{2},\phi=n\delta\phi\right>,

(6)

which satisfy

\displaystyle\left|\langle{}\phi_{m}|{}\phi_{n}\rangle\right|=\left[\frac{\cos(m-n)\delta\phi+1}{2}\right]^{J}.

(7)

As an example, Fig.
1
(b) depicts
6

II.2
Physical implementation of the quantum walk on the Bloch sphere

Now I consider a universal model composed of two subsystems, which are described by the collective spin operators
\hat{\mathbf{J}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
. One subsystem (
\hat{\mathbf{J}}
) serves as a walker, whereas the other one (
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
) serves as a coin whose state determines the movement of the walker.
The total Hamiltonian can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{1}(t),

(8)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\kappa\hat{J}_{z}\otimes\hat{S}_{z},

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\hat{I}_{\mathrm{w}}\otimes\mathbf{h}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{S}}\delta\left(t-kT\right),

(10)

where
\kappa
is the interacting strength between two subsystems,
\mathbf{h}=\left(h_{x},h_{y},h_{z}\right)
corresponds to a pulse acted on the coin with period
T
and amplitude
h=\sqrt{h_{x}^{2}+h_{y}^{2}+h_{z}^{2}}
along the direction
\mathbf{h}/h
, and
\hat{I}_{\mathrm{w}}
is the identity matrix of the walker. Such a kind of Hamiltonian commonly appears in various systems, such as atom-light interaction systems
Kuzmich et al. 1998
;
Bao et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2021
, Bose–Einstein condensates
Jing et al. 2019
;
Huang et al. 2021
and magnetic clusters
Hines et al. 2005
, etc. In this paper, I take two subsystems as spin clusters, whereas other systems can be dealt with accordingly. In terms of the Pauli matrices
\hat{\sigma}_{\gamma}
, the collective spin operators can be written as
\hat{J}_{\gamma}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{i,\gamma}/2
and
\hat{S}_{\gamma}=\hat{\sigma}_{\gamma}/2
, where
N
is the total number of spins in the spin cluster of the walker.

The time evolution over one period is determined by

\displaystyle\hat{U}(T)=\hat{M}\cdot\hat{C},

(11)

with

\displaystyle\hat{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-i\hat{H}_{0}T\right)

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{z}(\kappa T)\otimes\left|{}\uparrow\right>\left<{}\uparrow\right|+\hat{R}_{z}(-\kappa T)\otimes\left|{}\downarrow\right>\left<{}\downarrow\right|,

\displaystyle\hat{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-i\hat{I}_{\mathrm{w}}\otimes\mathbf{h}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{S}}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{I}_{\mathrm{w}}\otimes\exp\left(-i\mathbf{h}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{S}}\right).

In each step of the quantum walk, one flips the coin and changes its state at first, which is determined by the coin-flip operator. Then, the walker shifts its location according to the coin’s state, which is determined by the conditional-shift operator. Based on the time evolution operator
\hat{U}(T)
, one can find that
\hat{C}
leads to a rotation of the coin state by angle
h
along the
\mathbf{h}/h
direction, which plays a role of the coin-flip operator. One of the most frequently employed coin-flip operators is the Hadamard gate
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
, with

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
1&-1\end{array}\right).

The Hadamard gate up to a global phase factor is achieved

\displaystyle\hat{C}=-i\hat{I}_{\mathrm{w}}\otimes\hat{H}_{\mathrm{c}},

(17)

by setting
\mathbf{h}=\left(\pi,0,\pi\right)/\sqrt{2}
.

\hat{M}
can be regarded as a conditional-shift operator. The interacting strength
\kappa
and period
T
are chosen such that
\kappa T=\delta\phi=2\pi/L
, which leads to the transfer of walker’s states depending on the coin, namely

\displaystyle\hat{M}\left|{}\phi_{n}\right>\otimes\left|{}\uparrow\right>=\left|{}\phi_{n+1}\right>\otimes\left|{}\uparrow\right>,

\displaystyle\hat{M}\left|{}\phi_{n}\right>\otimes\left|{}\downarrow\right>=\left|{}\phi_{n-1}\right>\otimes\left|{}\downarrow\right>.

(18)

Given that the initial state is
\left|{}\psi(0)\right>=\left|{}w\right>\otimes\left|{}c\right>
, with the walker and the coin initially at
\left|{}w\right>
and
\left|{}c\right>
respectively, the final state after
k
steps would be

\displaystyle\left|{}\psi(k)\right>=\left(\hat{M}\cdot\hat{C}\right)^{k}\left|{}\psi(0)\right>.

(19)

In what follows, the initial state is set to be
\left|{}w\right>=\left|{}\phi_{0}\right>
and
\left|{}c\right>=\left|{}\uparrow\right>
. If the overlap (Eq. (
7
)) between different walker’s states is ignored, the quantum walk on the Bloch sphere reduces to an ideal one with orthogonal walker’s states
Travaglione and Milburn 2002
.

III
Results and Discussions

The quantum walk is known for its ballistic spread quadratically faster than its classical counterpart which shows a diffusive spread. Because of the quantum interference effect, the variance of the quantum walk grows quadratically with the number of steps
k
(
\sigma^{2}\propto k^{2}
), compared to the linear growth (
\sigma^{2}\propto k
) for the classical random walk.

In order to demonstrate the quadratic enhancement, one needs first calculate the probability distribution. For an ideal quantum walk, different walker’s states are orthogonal. One can easily achieve the probability distribution as follows:

\displaystyle P_{I}(\phi_{n})=\left<{}\phi_{n}\right|\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{w}}(k)\left|{}\phi_{n}\right>,

(20)

where
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{w}}(k)=\mathrm{tr}_{{}c}\left({}\left|{}\psi(k)\right>\left<{}\psi(k)\right|\right)
is the reduced density matrix of the walker. Then, the standard deviation is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{I}=\sqrt{\left<{}\phi^{2}\right>-\left<{}\phi\right>^{2}},

(21)

with
\left<{}\phi^{l}\right>=\sum_{n}P_{I}(\phi_{n})\phi_{n}^{l}
.

However, the quantum walk on the Bloch sphere corresponds to a set of spin coherent states, which are generally nonorthogonal, as indicated in Eq. (
7
). Fortunately, the Wigner function can be viewed as a quantum analogy to the classical probability density, which is able to visualize the evolution of the walker in the phase space. Following the Stratonovich-Weyl correspondence
Stratonovich 1957
;
Várilly and Gracia-Bondía 1989
;
Davis et al. 2021
, the Wigner function for the SU(2) group can be defined as

\displaystyle W(\theta,\phi)=\mathrm{tr}\left({}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{w}}\hat{\Delta}(\theta,\phi)\right),

(22)

where the kernel can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\Delta_{j,m}\left|{}j,m;\mathbf{d}\right>\left<{}j,m;\mathbf{d}\right|,

(23)

\displaystyle\Delta_{j,m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{2j}\frac{2l+1}{2j+1}\left<\begin{array}[]{@{}cc|c@{}}j&l&j\\
m&0&m\end{array}\right>.

Here
\left|{}j,m;\mathbf{d}\right>
is the Dicke basis along
\mathbf{d}=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)
direction, which satisfies
\mathbf{d}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{J}}\left|{}j,m;\mathbf{d}\right>=m\left|{}j,m;\mathbf{d}\right>
.
\left<\begin{array}[]{@{}cc|c@{}}j&l&j\\
m&0&m\end{array}\right>
is the Clebsch-Gordan coefficient.

The Wigner function satisfies the normalization relation

\displaystyle\frac{2J+1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}W(\theta,\phi)\sin\theta d\theta d\phi=1,

(27)

and its marginal gives the probability distribution

\displaystyle P(\phi)=\frac{2J+1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}W(\theta,\phi)\sin\theta d\theta.

(28)

Then one can define the standard deviation
\sigma
(same as Eq. (
21
)), with

\displaystyle\left<{}\phi^{l}\right>=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}P(\phi)\phi^{l}d\phi.

(29)

Here I focus on the short-time evolution when
\sigma
is feasible to depict the quadratic enhancement of the quantum walk. For the long-time evolution, the Holevo standard deviation is more appropriate due to the periodic phase
Xue et al. 2008
.

IV
Conclusions and outlook

The phase plane associating with the Heisenberg-Weyl group and the Bloch sphere associating with the SU(2) group are two well-known phase spaces. The quantum walk on the phase plane has been studied extensively based on the harmonic oscillator. However, little attention has been paid to the quantum walk on the Bloch sphere, to the best of my knowledge.

In this paper, the discrete-time quantum walk in the phase space is generalized to the Bloch sphere. I focus on the spin cluster which serves as the walker, whereas other systems belonging to the SU(2) group follow the same pattern. The walker’s locations on the Bloch sphere are determined by the spin coherent states, which are generally nonorthogonal. If the number of spins in the spin cluster increases, the overlap between different states decreases, which finally leads to the ideal quantum walk with orthogonal walker’s states. To visualize the walking process on the Bloch sphere, the Wigner function is calculated. The probability distribution and the standard deviation are also calculated in virtue of the Wigner function, which confirm the quadratically growing variance, namely,
\sigma^{2}\propto k^{2}
.

The Bloch sphere serves as a new platform and offers more possibilities to study the quantum walk theoretically and experimentally. There are numerous related applications to be addressed. Here I just give three possibilities: (1) Macroscopic superposition, such as the spin cat state, can be found during the walking process. The macroscopic superposed states may exhibit sub-Planck phase-space structures, which can be used to achieve the Heisenberg-limited sensitivity in weak-force measurements
Zurek 2001
;
Toscano et al. 2006
;
Akhtar et al. 2021
. (2) One can extend the coin to include two spins. Two spins control the movement of the walker along a parallel and a meridian on the Bloch sphere separately, which leads to a two-dimensional quantum walk. (3) In the presence of the decoherence, the quantum walk tends to the classical random walk, which provides a new arena to study the quantum-to-classical transition
Travaglione and Milburn 2002
;
Sanders et al. 2003
;
Brun et al. 2003
;
Karski et al. 2009
. The possible applications of quantum walk on the Bloch sphere and the influence of decoherence deserve further studies, which are left to future research.
