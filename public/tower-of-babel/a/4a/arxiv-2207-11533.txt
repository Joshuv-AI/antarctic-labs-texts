Some results on N-pure ideals
Mohsen Aghajani

Abstract
. In this paper, we consider the N-pure notion. An ideal I I of a ring R R is said to be N-pure, if for every a ∈ I a\in I there exists b ∈ I b\in I such that a ⁡ ( 1 − b ) ∈ N ⁡ ( R ) a(1-b)\in N(R) , where N(R) is nil radical of R R . We provide new characterizations for N-pure ideals. In addition, N-pure ideals of an arbitrary ring are identified. Also, some other properties of N-pure ideals are studied. finally, we prove some results about the endomorphism ring of pure and N-pure ideals.

2.
Preliminaries

Here we mention some objects which is needed in the following. An ideal
I
of a ring
R
is said to be a pure ideal if the canonical ring homomorphism
\pi:R\longrightarrow R/I
is a flat homomorphism. Then
I
is a pure ideal if and only if
\operatorname{Ann}(a)+I=R
for all
a\in I
, if and only if for each
a\in I
there exists some
b\in I
such that
a(1-b)=0
.
An ideal
I
of a ring
R
is said to be a N-pure ideal if for each
a\in I
there exists
b\in I
such that
a(1-b)\in N(R)
where
N(R)
is the nil-radical of
R
, or equivalently for each
a\in I
there exist
n\geqslant 1
and
b\in I
such that
a^{n}(1-b)=0
. The following result characterizes the N-pure ideals.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
R
be a ring and
I
be an ideal of
R
. Then the following conditions are equivalent.

\mathbf{(i)}

I
is a N-pure ideal.

\mathbf{(ii)}
For every
a\in I
there exists
t\geqslant 1
such that
\operatorname{Ann}(a^{t})+I=R
.

\mathbf{(iii)}

\sqrt{I}=\{a\in R|\exists n\geqslant 1,\operatorname{Ann}(a^{n})+I=R\}
.

\mathbf{(iv)}

\sqrt{I}
is a N-pure ideal.

\mathbf{(v)}
There exists a unique pure ideal
J
such that
\sqrt{I}=\sqrt{J}
.

Proof.
See
[
1
, Theorem 2.6]
.
\blacksquare

Recall that a ring
R
is said to be a mid ring if for every
a\in R
,
\operatorname{Ann}(a)
is a N-pure ideal. If
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Spec}(R)
, we denote the natural ring homomorphism
R\longrightarrow S^{-1}R
by
\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}
where
S=R\setminus\mathfrak{p}
. Then a ring
R
is a mid ring if and only if
\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}
is a primary ideal for all
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Spec}(R)
, see
[
1
, Theorem 4.6]
. Finally, a ring
R
is called a Gelfand ring (pm-ring) if every prime ideal of
R
is contained in a unique maximal ideal.

3.
Main results

The next result identifies N-pure ideals in an arbitrary ring.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
I
is a N-pure ideal of a ring
R
, then

\sqrt{I}=\bigcap\limits_{\mathfrak{m}\in V(I)\cap\operatorname{Max}(R)}\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{m}}}.

Proof.
Let
a\in\sqrt{I}
. Then there exist
n\geqslant 1
and
b\in I
such that
a^{n}(1-b)=0
. If
\mathfrak{m}
is a maximal ideal containing
I
, then
1-b\notin\mathfrak{m}
and so
a\in\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{m}}}
. Now, let
a\in\bigcap\limits_{\mathfrak{m}\in V(I)\cap\operatorname{Max}(R)}\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{m}}}
. It suffices to show that
\operatorname{Ann}(a^{m})+I=R
for some
m\geqslant 1
, see
[
1
, Theorem 2.6(iv)]
. Assume
\operatorname{Ann}(a^{n})+I\neq R
for every
n\geqslant 1
. Then
J=\sum\limits_{n\geqslant 1}\big(\operatorname{Ann}(a^{n})+I\big)
is a proper ideal. Let
\mathfrak{n}
be a maximal ideal which contains
J
. Hence
a\notin\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{n}}}
which is a contradiction.
\blacksquare

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
R
be a ring and
I
be a N-pure ideal of
R
. If
\mathfrak{m}
is a maximal ideal of
R
containing
I
, then
I\subseteq\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{m}}}
.
\blacksquare

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
R
be a ring. If
I
is a proper N-pure ideal of
R
containing a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
, then
I=\mathfrak{p}
. Especially,
I
is a minimal prime ideal of
R
.

Proof.
Let
\mathfrak{p}\neq I
. Then there exists
a\in I\setminus\mathfrak{p}
. Since
I
is N-pure, then there are
b\in I
and
n\geqslant 1
such that
a^{n}(1-b)=0
. Hence
1-b\in\mathfrak{p}
and so
1\in I
. This is a contradiction. Thus we have
I=\mathfrak{p}
. Additionally, this implies that
I
is a minimal prime ideal of
R
.
\blacksquare

Corollary 3.4
.

The radical of a N-pure ideal
I
is the intersection of all
minimal prime ideals of
R
containing
I
.
\blacksquare

Let
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Spec}(R)
. By
\Lambda(\mathfrak{p})
we mean the set of all
\mathfrak{q}\in\operatorname{Spec}(R)
such that
\mathfrak{q}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
R
be a mp-ring and
I
be a proper ideal of
R
. Then

J=\bigcap\limits_{\mathfrak{m}\in V(I)\cap\operatorname{Max}(R)}\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{m}}}=\bigcap\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{m}\in V(I)\cap\operatorname{Max}(R)\\
\mathfrak{p}\in\Lambda(\mathfrak{m})\cap\operatorname{Min}(R)\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{p}

is a N-pure ideal of
R
.

Proof.
Let
a\in J
. Then for every maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
containing
I
there are
b_{\mathfrak{m}}\in R\setminus\mathfrak{m}
and
t_{\mathfrak{m}}\geqslant 1
such that
a^{t_{\mathfrak{m}}}b_{\mathfrak{m}}=0
. If
\mathfrak{m}
is a maximal ideal that does not contain
I
, then let
c_{\mathfrak{m}}\in I\setminus\mathfrak{m}
. Thus the ideal
K=\big(b_{\mathfrak{m}},c_{\mathfrak{m}}|\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{Max}(R)\big)
is the whole ring. Hence
1=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}b_{i}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}c_{j}
where
b_{i}=b_{\mathfrak{m_{i}}}\in R\setminus\mathfrak{m_{i}}
,
c_{j}=c_{\mathfrak{m_{j}}}\in I\setminus\mathfrak{m_{j}}
and
r_{i},s_{j}\in R
. Now, let
t=min\{t_{i}|i=1,\cdots,m\}
where
t_{i}=t_{\mathfrak{m_{i}}}
. Then we have
a^{t}(1-c)=0
where
c=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}c_{j}
. If
\mathfrak{m}
is a maximal ideal containing
I
, then
1-c\notin\mathfrak{m}
and so
a^{t}\in\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{m}}
. On the other hand, by
[
2
, Theorem 6.2(ix)]
, there exists
u\geqslant 1
such that
\operatorname{Ann}(a^{tu})+\operatorname{Ann}((1-c)^{u})=R
. Then
e+f=1
for
e\in\operatorname{Ann}(a^{tu})
and
f\in\operatorname{Ann}((1-c)^{u})
. Therefore,
a^{tu}(1-f)=0
and
f\in\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{m}}
. This yields that
J
is a N-pure ideal. The second equality holds by
[
1
, Theorem 3.3(iii)]
.
\blacksquare

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
R
be a ring and
I
be an ideal of
R
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

\mathbf{(i)}

I
is a N-pure ideal.

\mathbf{(ii)}
For each ideal
J
with
I\subseteq J
,
J
is a N-pure ideal if and only if
J/I
is N-pure.

Proof.

\mathbf{(i)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(ii)}:
If
J
is a N-pure ideal of
R
containing
I
, then it is clear to see that
J/I
is a N-pure ideal of
R/I
. Conversely, let
a\in J
. Then there exist
n\geqslant 1
and
b+I\in J/I
such that
(a+I)^{n}(1+I-(b+I))=0
. Thus
a^{n}(1-b)\in I
and so there are
m\geqslant 1
and
c\in I
such that
a^{mn}(1-b)^{m}(1-c)=0
. Hence
a^{mn}(1-bd)(1-c)=0
for some
d\in R
. So
a^{mn}(1-bd-c+bcd)=0
. Therefore
J
is a N-pure ideal, since
bd+c-bcd\in J
.

\mathbf{(ii)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(i)}:
Since the zero ideal is a N-pure ideal, then the assertion is obtained by setting
J=I
.
\blacksquare

Let
I
be an ideal of a ring
R
. Setting
S=1+I
. Then
S
is a multiplicative closed subset of
R
. In the following, we prove some results about relation between the ring
S^{-1}R
and pure and N-pure ideals. The following result provides a new characterization for N-pure ideals.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
R
be a ring,
I
be an ideal of
R
and
S=1+I
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

\mathbf{(i)}

I
is a N-pure ideal.

\mathbf{(ii)}

\operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R)=\{S^{-1}\mathfrak{p}|I\subseteq\mathfrak{p}\}
.

\mathbf{(iii)}

N(S^{-1}R)=S^{-1}\sqrt{I}
.

\mathbf{(iv)}

\sqrt{I}=\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\varphi}
where
\varphi:R\longrightarrow S^{-1}R
is the natural ring homomorphism.

\mathbf{(v)}

S^{-1}R/N(S^{-1}R)\cong R/\sqrt{I}
as
R
-algebras.

Proof.

\mathbf{(i)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(ii)}:
Let
S^{-1}\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R)
. Then we have
\mathfrak{p}\cap S=\emptyset
. We claim that
I\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
. Otherwise, there exists
a\in I\setminus\mathfrak{p}
. Thus there are
n\geqslant 1
and
b\in I
such that
a^{n}(1-b)=0
, because
I
is N-pure. So
1-b\in\mathfrak{p}
which is a contradiction. On the other hand, if
I\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
, then it is obvious that
S^{-1}\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R)
.

\mathbf{(ii)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(i)}:
It suffices to show that for each
a\in I
there exists
n\geqslant 1
such that
\operatorname{Ann}(a^{n})+I=R
, see
[
1
, Theorem 2.6(iii)]
. If not then the ideal
K=\sum\limits_{m\geqslant 1}\big(\operatorname{Ann}(a^{m})+I\big)
is a proper ideal. Let
\mathfrak{m}
be a maximal ideal containing
K
. This means that
a^{m}/1\neq 0
for all
m\geqslant 1
and so
a/1\notin N(S^{-1}R)
. Then there exists
S^{-1}\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Min}(S^{-1}R)
such that
a/1\notin S^{-1}\mathfrak{p}
. This is a contradiction because
I\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
. Hence
I
is a N-pure ideal.

\mathbf{(i)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(iii)}:
In general, we have
N(S^{-1}R)\subseteq S^{-1}\sqrt{I}
. Let
a/s\in S^{-1}\sqrt{I}
. Then there exist
b\in\sqrt{I}
and
t\in S
such that
a/s=b/t
. Hence there are
n\geqslant 1
and
c\in I
such that
b^{n}(1-c)=0
. So
a^{n}/s^{n}=b^{n}/t^{n}=0
. This implies
a/s\in N(S^{-1}R)
. Therefore,
N(S^{-1}R)=S^{-1}\sqrt{I}
.

\mathbf{(iii)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(i)}:
Let
a\in I
. Then
a/1\in N(S^{-1}R)
and so there exists
n\geqslant 1
such that
a^{n}/1=0
. Hence there exists
b\in I
such that
a^{n}(1+b)=0
. Thus
I
is a N-pure ideal.

\mathbf{(i)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(iv)}:
In general, we have
\operatorname{Ker}\varphi\subseteq I
. Let
a\in I
. Then there exist
n\geqslant 1
and
b\in I
such that
a^{n}(1-b)=0
. Hence,
a\in\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\varphi}
. So
\sqrt{I}=\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\varphi}
.

\mathbf{(iv)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(i)}:
It is obvious.

\mathbf{(iv)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(v)}:
Define
\psi:S^{-1}R\longrightarrow R/\sqrt{I}
with
\psi(a/s)=a+\sqrt{I}
. Then this map is an algebra homomorphism. Let
a/s\in\operatorname{Ker}\psi
. Thus
a\in\sqrt{I}
and so by hypothesis
a\in\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\varphi}
. So there exist
n\geqslant 1
and
b\in I
such that
a^{n}(1+b)=0
. Hence
a/s\in N(S^{-1}R)
. Therefore,
\operatorname{Ker}\psi=N(S^{-1}R)
. This implies that
S^{-1}R/N(S^{-1}R)\cong R/\sqrt{I}
.

\mathbf{(v)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(i)}:
By
[
1
, Theorem 2.6]
, it suffices to show that
\sqrt{I}
is a N-pure ideal. Let
a\in\sqrt{I}
. We claim that there is
n\geqslant 1
such that
\operatorname{Ann}(a^{n})+\sqrt{I}=R
. Otherwise, the ideal
\sum\limits_{m\geqslant 1}\big(\operatorname{Ann}(a^{m})+\sqrt{I}\big)
is a proper ideal. Hence it contained in a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
. Thus
\operatorname{Ann}(a^{m})\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
and so
a/1\notin N(S^{-1}R)
. This is a contradiction. Therefore,
I
is a N-pure ideal.
\blacksquare

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
R
be a ring,
I
and
J
be two ideals of
R
with
S=1+I
and
T=1+J
. If
\sqrt{I}=\sqrt{J}
and
I
is a N-pure ideal, then
S^{-1}R/S^{-1}\sqrt{I}\cong T^{-1}R/T^{-1}\sqrt{J}
as algebras.
\blacksquare

In the following result, the criterion (iii) is new.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
R
be a ring,
I
be an ideal of
R
and
S=1+I
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

\mathbf{(i)}

I
is a pure ideal.

\mathbf{(ii)}

S^{-1}R\cong R/I
as
R
-algebras.

\mathbf{(iii)}

S^{-1}I
is the zero ideal of
S^{-1}R
.

Proof.

\mathbf{(i)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(ii)}:
Define
\varphi:S^{-1}R\longrightarrow R/I
with
\varphi(a/s)=a+I
. Since
I
is a pure ideal, then
\varphi
is a well-define algebra homomorphism. Now, let
\varphi(a/s)=\varphi(b/t)
. Then we have
a+I=b+I
and so
ta+I=sb+I
. Thus
ta-sb\in I
. So there exists
c\in I
such that
(ta-sb)(1-c)=0
. Then
a/s=b/t
. This yields that
\varphi
is a monomorphism. Therefore,
\varphi
is an isomorphism.

\mathbf{(ii)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(i)}:
Let
a\in I
. It suffices to show that
\operatorname{Ann}(a)+I=R
. Otherwise, there exists a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
such that
\operatorname{Ann}(a)+I\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
. Then by hypothesis,
a/1\neq 0
. This is a contradiction. Hence
I
is a pure ideal.

\mathbf{(i)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(iii)}:
Let
a/s\in S^{-1}I
. Then
a/s=b/t
for some
b\in I
and
t\in S
. Thus there exists
c\in I
such that
b(1-c)=0
. Hence
a/s=0
. This yields that
S^{-1}I
is the zero ideal of
S^{-1}R
.

\mathbf{(iii)}\Rightarrow\mathbf{(i)}:
It is clear.

\blacksquare

Example 3.10
.

It is well-Known that only pure(=N-pure) ideals of an integral domain are zero ideal and the whole ring. Let
p
be a prime number. Then
p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
is not a Pure ideal of the ring
\mathbb{Z}
for
n\geqslant 1
. If
S=1+p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
, then
S^{-1}\mathbb{Z}
is an integral domain. If
q
is a prime number with
p\neq q
, then we have
q\mathbb{Z}\cap S\neq\emptyset
. Hence
\operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}\mathbb{Z})=\{0,S^{-1}p\mathbb{Z}\}
. In case
n=1
,
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
is a field while
S^{-1}\mathbb{Z}
is a non-field integral domain. As another example, let
R=\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathfrak{p}=2\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}
and
S=1+\mathfrak{p}
. It is easy to check that
\mathfrak{q}=3\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}
is a pure ideal of
R
. Hence, by
[
1
, Theorem 3.4(iv)]
and
[
1
, Corollary 3.5(iv)]
,
\mathfrak{p}
is a N-pure ideal of
R
which is not pure. Clearly,
\operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R)=\{S^{-1}\mathfrak{p}\}
. Then
S^{-1}R
is not an integral domain, since
S^{-1}\mathfrak{p}\neq 0
. But
R/\mathfrak{p}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
which is a field.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
\varphi:R\longrightarrow S
be a ring homomorphism. If
I
is a N-pure ideal of
R
, then
IS
is a N-pure ideal of
S
.

Proof.
Let
b\in IS
. Then there are
n\geqslant 1
,
a_{i}\in I
and
s_{i}\in S
such that
b=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}s_{i}
. Thus by
[
1
, Theorem 2.6(ii)]
, there exist
c\in I
and
t\geqslant 1
such that
a_{i}^{t}(1-c)=0
for
i=1,\cdots,n
. Then

b^{nt}(1-c)=\sum\limits_{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}i_{j}=nt}a_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots a_{n}^{i_{n}}s^{\prime}_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}}(1-c)=0.

Therefore,
IS
is a N-pure ideal of
S
.
\blacksquare

In the next result, we give the new short proof for
[
4
, Lemma 1.2]
in non-reduced case.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
R
be a Gelfand ring. Then
\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a pure ideal for every
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{Max}(R)
.

Proof.
Let
a\in\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{m}}
. Then there exists
b\in R\setminus\mathfrak{m}
such that
ab=0
. It is suffices to show that
\operatorname{Ann}(a)+\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{m}}=R
. Otherwise, there exists a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m^{\prime}}
such that
\operatorname{Ann}(a)+\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{m}}\subseteq\mathfrak{m^{\prime}}
.
Hence
\mathfrak{m}\neq\mathfrak{m^{\prime}}
, since
\operatorname{Ann}(a)\nsubseteq\mathfrak{m}
. But this is a contradiction by
[
2
, Theorem 4.3(ix)]
.
\blacksquare

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
R
be a ring. If
\mathfrak{p}
is a N-pure prime ideal of

, then
\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}}=\mathfrak{p}
.

\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}

and

and so

. This means that
\sqrt{\operatorname{Ker}\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}}=\mathfrak{p}
.
\blacksquare

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
R
be a ring and
I
be an ideal of
R
with
I\subseteq J(R)
. If
I
is a N-pure ideal, then

.

Let
a\in I
. Then there exist
n\geqslant 1
and

such that

, then

Recall that an ideal
I
of a ring
R
is said to be
\emph{strongly$\pi$-regular}
if for every
a\in I

there are
n\geqslant 1
and
b\in I
such that
a^{n}=a^{n+1}b
. It is clear that every strongly
\pi
-regular is a N-pure ideal. Also, it is well known
that a ring is zero-dimensional if and only if it is a strongly
\pi
-regular ring.

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then
R
is a zero-dimensional ring if and only if the N-pure ideals and the strongly
\pi
-regular ideals are the same.

Proof.
It is suffices to show that every N-pure ideal is a strongly
\pi
-regular ideal. Let
I
be a N-pure ideal and
a\in I
. Then by
[
1
, Theorem 3.4(iii)]
, there is
n\geqslant 1
and
c\in R
such that
a^{n}=a^{n+1}c
. Hence
I
is a strongly
\pi
-regular ideal. The converse case holds by
[
1
, Theorem 3.4(ii)]
and the fact that
R
is a N-pure ideal.
\blacksquare

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
R
be a ring and
I
be a finitely generated N-pure ideal of
R
. Then
\sqrt{I}=\sqrt{Ra}
for some
a\in I
.

Proof.
Let
\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}\}
be a generating set of
I
. Then there exist
t\geqslant 1
and
a\in I
such that
a_{i}^{t}(1-a)=0
for all
i=1,\cdots,n
by
[
1
, Theorem 2.6(ii)]
. Thus for all
i=1,\cdots,n
we have
a_{i}^{t}=a_{i}^{t}a
. Hence
a_{i}\in\sqrt{Ra}
for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. Therefore
I\subseteq\sqrt{Ra}
and so
\sqrt{I}=\sqrt{Ra}
.
\blacksquare

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
R
be a ring and
I
be a finitely generated N-pure ideal of
R
. Then there exist
n\geqslant 1
and
b\in I
such that
a^{n}(1-b)=0
for all
a\in I
.

Proof.
Let
I=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}Ra_{i}
. Then similar to the proof of
3.18
there exist
t\geqslant 1
and
b\in I
such that
a_{i}^{t}(1-b)=0
for all
i=1,\cdots,n
. Thus if
a=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}a_{i}
, then

a^{nt}=\sum\limits_{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}=nt}s_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}}a_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots a_{n}^{i_{n}}=\sum\limits_{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}=nt}s_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}}a_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots a_{n}^{i_{n}}b=a^{nt}b

since at least
i_{j}\geqslant t
for some
j
. Hence the assertion is proved.
\blacksquare

It is well known that the endomorphism ring of an arbitrary module is a noncommutative ring. In the following result, we prove that the endomorphism ring of arbitrary power of a pure ideal is commutative.

Theorem 3.20
.

Let
R
be a ring and
I
be a pure ideal of
R
. Then
\operatorname{End}(I^{n})
is a commutative ring for all
n\geqslant 1
.

Proof.
Let
n
be a fixed positive integer and
f,g\in\operatorname{End}(I^{n})
. If
a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}\in I
, then there exist
b_{i}\in I
such that
a_{i}=a_{i}b_{i}
for all
i=1,\cdots,n
. Setting
1-b=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}(1-b_{i})
, then we have
b\in I
and so
a_{i}=a_{i}b
for all
i
. Hence

\displaystyle fg(a_{1}\cdots a_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle fg(a_{1}\cdots a_{n}b^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(g(a_{1}\cdots a_{n}b^{n}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(a_{1}\cdots a_{n}g(b^{n}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(b^{n})f(a_{1}\cdots a_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(f(a_{1}\cdots a_{n}b^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle gf(a_{1}\cdots a_{n}b^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle gf(a_{1}\cdots a_{n}).

Then we have
fg=gf
, since
f
and
g
are stable under sum operation. Therefore
\operatorname{End}(I^{n})
is a commutative ring.
\blacksquare

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
R
be a ring and
I
be a principal N-pure ideal of
R
. Then there exists
n\geqslant 1
such that
\operatorname{End}(I^{i})
is a commutative ring for each
i\geqslant n
.

Proof.
Let
I=Ra
. Then there exist
n\geqslant 1
and
r\in R
such that
a^{n}(1-ra)=0
. It is obvious that

a^{i}=a^{i}(ra)^{i}
for each
i\geqslant n
. Now let
i
be a fixed integer with
i\geqslant n
and
f,g\in\operatorname{End}(I^{i})
. Then we have

\displaystyle fg(a^{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle fg(a^{i}(ra)^{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(g(a^{i}(ra)^{i}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(a^{i}g((ra)^{i}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g((ra)^{i})f(a^{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(f(a^{i}(ra)^{i}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle gf(a^{i}(ra)^{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle gf(a^{i})

Hence
fg=gf
and so
\operatorname{End}(I^{i})
is a commutative ring for each
i\geqslant n
.
\blacksquare
