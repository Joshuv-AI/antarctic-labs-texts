Abstract
. Let Z ⁡ ( N ) Z(N) denote the minimum number of zeros in [ 0 , 2 ​ π ] [0,2\pi] that a cosine polynomial of the form f A ​ ( t ) = ∑ n ∈ A cos ⁡ n ​ t f_{A}(t)=\sum_{n\in A}\cos nt can have when A A is a finite set of non-negative integers of size | A | = N |A|=N . It is an old problem of Littlewood to determine Z ⁡ ( N ) Z(N) . In this paper, we obtain the lower bound Z ⁡ ( N ) ⩾ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ N ) ( 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) Z(N)\geqslant(\log\log N)^{(1+o(1))} which exponentially improves on the previous best bounds of the form Z ⁡ ( N ) ⩾ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ log ⁡ N ) c Z(N)\geqslant(\log\log\log N)^{c} due to Erdélyi and Sahasrabudhe.



1.
Introduction

Littlewood posed the following problem in his 1968 paper “Some Problems in Real and Complex
Analysis”
[
8
, Problem 22]
.

Problem 1.1
.

If
A
is a set of non-negative integers of size
|A|=N
, what is the lower bound on the number of real zeros of
\sum_{n\in A}\cos nt
in a period
[0,2\pi]
? Possibly
N-1
, or not much
less.

The first progress on this problem was made by Borwein, Erdélyi, Ferguson and Lockhart
[
2
]
who proved the existence of cosine polynomials
\sum_{n\in A}\cos nt
with no more than
O(N^{5/6}\log N)
roots in a period, hence giving a counterexample to Littlewood’s suggested lower bound. Their construction was later optimised by Konyagin
[
7
]
and independently by Juškevičius and Sahasrabudhe
[
5
]
to obtain the better upper bound
O((N\log N)^{2/3})
. Let us write
Z(f_{A})
for the number of zeros in
[0,2\pi]
of the cosine polynomial

(1)

f_{A}(t)=\sum_{n\in A}\cos nt

where
A
is a set of non-negative integers. Obtaining lower bounds for
Z(f_{A})
seems to be a hard problem and it was only recently established that the number of such zeros grows to infinity as
|A|\to\infty
. This result was proved independently by Erdélyi
[
3
]
, and by Sahasrabudhe
[
10
]
who further obtained the explicit lower bound
Z(f_{A})\geqslant(\log\log\log|A|)^{1/2-o(1)}
. By combining the arguments of
[
3
]
and
[
10
]
, Erdélyi
[
4
]
later obtained the slight improvement
Z(f_{A})\geqslant(\log\log\log|A|)^{1-o(1)}
. We define

(2)

Z(N)=\min_{A\subset\mathbf{N}:|A|=N}Z(f_{A})

so that Littlewood’s Problem
1.1
is precisely to determine
Z(N)
. Then the best bounds prior to this work state that

(3)

(\log\log\log N)^{1-o(1)}\leqslant Z(N)\leqslant(N\log N)^{2/3},

and there remains a large gap between the upper and lower bounds. The methods of
[
3
,
4
,
10
]
further prove lower bounds on the number of zeros of a more general class of cosine polynomials. Let
S
be a finite set and let

(4)

g(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}\cos nt

be a cosine polynomial with coefficients
a_{n}\in S
. The following lower bound is proved in
[
4
]
(following similar theorems in
[
3
,
10
]
).

Theorem 1.2

(
[
4
]
, Theorem 2.1)
.

Let
S\subset\mathbf{Z}
be finite and
M(S)=\max_{s\in S}|s|
.
Let
g
be a cosine polynomial as in (
4
) with coefficients
a_{n}\in S
. Then the number of roots of
g
satisfies

Z(g)\geqslant\left(\frac{c}{1+\log M(S)}\right)\frac{\log\log\log|g(0)|}{\log\log\log\log|g(0)|}-1,

where
c>0
is an absolute constant.

In particular, this theorem shows that if
S\subset\mathbf{N}
is finite, then the number of zeros of a cosine polynomial
g
with coefficients in
S
tends to infinity with the degree
\deg(g)
. Interestingly, it is known that such a result does not hold for general
S\subset\mathbf{Z}
; in
[
10
]
it was shown to be false for
S=\{-1,0,1,2\}
. It was conjectured however that this is true for
S=\{-1,1\}
which is the simplest case to which Theorem
1.2
does not apply because
|g(0)|
may remain bounded as
\deg(g)\to\infty
. This conjecture was proved recently in
[
1
]
, thus providing a qualitative extension of Theorem
1.2
. The purpose of this paper is to obtain the following quantitative improvement over Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that the following holds. Let
S\subset\mathbf{Z}
be finite and
M(S)=\max_{s\in S}|s|
.
Let
g
be a cosine polynomial as in (
4
) with coefficients
a_{n}\in S
. Then the number of roots of
g
satisfies

Z(g)\geqslant\left(\frac{c}{1+\log M(S)}\right)\frac{\log\log|g(0)|}{\log\log\log|g(0)|}.

Applying this theorem with
S=\{0,1\}
clearly yields the following improved lower bound compared to (
3
) for Littlewood’s Problem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.4
.

We have that
Z(N)\geqslant(\log\log N)^{1-o(1)}
.

2.
Notation, prerequisites and organisation of the paper

\mathbf{N},\mathbf{Z}
and
\mathbf{R}
denote the sets of non-negative integers, the integers and the real numbers respectively. We use the asymptotic notation
f=O(g)
or
f\ll g
if there is a constant
C
such that
|f(x)|\leqslant Cg(x)
for all
x
. For a real number
t
, we use the notation
e(t)=e^{2\pi it}
. For a
1
-periodic function
g
we denote its
L^{1}
-norm by
\lVert g\rVert_{1}\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{1}|g(t)|\,dt
.

The rough approach in Erdélyi and Sahasrabudhe’s arguments is to first prove that cosine polynomials with few roots must be very structured, and then prove that structured polynomials still have many roots. In
Section 3
we prove a result which provides an exponential improvement for the number of roots of structured cosine polynomials. This is almost strong enough to obtain Theorem
1.3
by using Erdélyi’s structural result
[
4
, Lemma 3.9]
. To actually obtain Theorem
1.3
, we need to prove a slightly better structure theorem than is given in Erdélyi or Sahasrabudhe’s papers. This is achieved in
Section 4
with an argument that is based on the ideas in
[
4
,
10
]
. In
Section 5
we combine both results to yield the improved Theorem
1.3
.

A key role in the argument will be played by the following result known as ‘the Littlewood
L^{1}
conjecture’, which was established by Konyagin
[
6
]
and independently by McGehee, Pigno and Smith
[
9
]
who proved the following generalisation.

Theorem 2.1

(Littlewood’s
L^{1}
conjecture)
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k}
be complex numbers and
n_{1}<n_{2}<\dots<n_{k}
integers. Then

\left|\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}e(n_{j}t)\right|\right|_{1}\gg\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{|a_{j}|}{j}.

3.
L^{1}
bounds for structured trigonometric polynomials

In this section we prove the following theorem which provides an improved bound for the number of roots of structured trigonometric polynomials.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
S\subset\mathbf{Z}
be a finite set and
M(S)=\max_{s\in S}|s|
.
Let
g(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}\cos nt
be a cosine polynomial with coefficients
a_{n}\in S
. Suppose that
\deg g=N
and that we can partition
[0,N]=\cup_{j=1}^{K}I_{j}
into
K
intervals such that on each interval
I_{j}
, the coefficient sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in I_{j}}
is periodic with period
P
. If
d
is the number of sign changes of
g
in
[0,2\pi]
, then

(5)

dM(S)P^{2}\log(KP)\gg\log|g(0)|,

where the implied constant is absolute.

To begin, we may rescale the variable
t
by
2\pi
and write
g
in the following form

(6)

g(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}\cos(2\pi nt)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\hat{g}(n)e(nt)

where
e(x)\vcentcolon=e^{2\pi ix}
and
\hat{g}
denotes the Fourier transform of
g
. We define the related cosine polynomial

(7)

\tilde{g}(t)=g(t)\left(\frac{2}{P}\sum_{n=0}^{P-1}\cos 2\pi nt\right)^{2}

and record some important properties. Note that
\tilde{g}
and
g
have exactly the same
d
sign changes in
[0,1]
, and from the formula
2\cos\alpha\cos\beta=\cos(\alpha-\beta)+\cos(\alpha+\beta)
it follows that
\tilde{g}
is a cosine polynomial with coefficients in
(P^{-2}\cdot\mathbf{Z})\cap[-4M(S),4M(S)]
, recalling that
|a_{n}|\leqslant M(S)
by definition. It is also clear that
|\tilde{g}(0)|=4|g(0)|
. Finally, we analyse the structure of
\tilde{g}
. By assumption, we can write

g(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{n\in I_{j}}a_{n}\cos(2\pi nt)

where each of the sequences
(a_{n})_{n\in I_{j}}
is periodic with period
P
. For each
j\in[K]
, we split the interval
I_{i}=[u_{i},v_{i}]
into the interval
J_{i}=[u_{i}+2P,v_{i}-2P]
and the set of remaining elements
I_{i}\setminus J_{i}
(there may exist
i
for which
J_{i}
is empty). Define
S_{j}
to be the sum of any
P
consecutive terms in the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in I_{j}}
, so note that
S_{j}
is a constant only depending on
j
as
(a_{n})_{n\in I_{j}}
is
P
-periodic. Hence, if
j\in[K]
and
n\in J_{j}
, then (
7
) and the expansion

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2}{P}\sum_{n=0}^{P-1}\cos 2\pi nt\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{P^{2}}\left(1+2\sum_{m=-P+1}^{P-1}e(mt)+\sum_{m=-2P+1}^{2P-1}(2P-1-|m|)e(mt)\right),

give

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{g}}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2P^{2}}\left(a_{n}+2\sum_{m=-P+1}^{P-1}a_{n+m}+\sum_{m=-2P+2}^{2P-2}(2P-1-|m|)a_{n+m}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2P^{2}}\left(4P\sum_{m=0}^{P-1}a_{n+m}\right)=\frac{2S_{j}}{P}

since
J_{j}+[-2P,2P]\subset I_{j}
so that all the terms
a_{n+m}
appearing above are part of the periodic sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in I_{j}}
. Therefore we may write

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{2S_{j}}{P}\sum_{n\in J_{j}}\cos(2\pi nt)+\sum_{n\in[N+2P]\setminus\cup_{j}J_{j}}2\hat{\tilde{g}}(n)\cos(2\pi nt).

Note that the sum on the right hand side has at most
O(PK)
terms since there are
K
intervals
I_{j}
and each of these contributes at most
|I_{j}\setminus J_{j}|\leqslant 4P
terms to this second sum. Hence, if we consider all these terms as a sum over
O(PK)
intervals of length
1
, then we can find

(8)

\tilde{K}=K+O(PK)=O(PK)

intervals
\tilde{J}_{1},\tilde{J}_{2},\dots,\tilde{J}_{\tilde{K}}
partitioning
[N+2P]
and constants
c_{j}
which satisfy
c_{j}\in(P^{-2}\cdot\mathbf{Z})\cap[-4M(S),4M(S)]
so that

(9)

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{K}}c_{j}\sum_{n\in\tilde{J}_{j}}\cos(2\pi nt).

The proof of Proposition
3.1
comes down to finding two bounds for the
L^{1}
-norm of
\tilde{g}
.
The lower bound will follow from an application the Littlewood
L^{1}
conjecture
2.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\tilde{g}
be as in (
9
). Then

(10)

\lVert\tilde{g}\rVert_{1}\gg P^{-2}\log\frac{|g(0)|}{M(S)}.

Proof.

Recall that the coefficients
c_{j}
lie in
P^{-2}\cdot\mathbf{Z}
so that each non-zero
c_{j}
satisfies
|c_{j}|\geqslant P^{-2}
. The bound in Theorem
2.1
therefore immediately gives

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{g}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\gg P^{-2}\log\left(\sum_{j:c_{j}\neq 0}|\tilde{J}_{j}|\right).

We also have that
|c_{j}|\leqslant 4M(S)
which yields the following lower bound for the number of non-zero Fourier coefficients of
\tilde{g}
:

\displaystyle 4M(S)\sum_{j:c_{j}\neq 0}|\tilde{J}_{j}|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left|\sum_{j:c_{j}\neq 0}c_{j}|\tilde{J}_{j}|\right|

\displaystyle=|\tilde{g}(0)|.

Hence,
\sum_{j:c_{j}\neq 0}|\tilde{J}_{j}|\geqslant\frac{|\tilde{g}(0)|}{4M(S)}=\frac{|g(0)|}{M(S)}
since
\tilde{g}(0)=4g(0)
and plugging this in the lower bound for
\lVert\tilde{g}\rVert_{1}
above finishes the proof.
∎

We now move on to obtaining an upper bound for
\lVert\tilde{g}\rVert_{1}
. We assumed that
g
has
d
sign changes in
(0,1/2)
and hence so does
\tilde{g}
. Let us denote these by
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{d}
. If we write
\xi_{d+1}=1/2
, then as
\tilde{g}
is even we get

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{g}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1/2}|\tilde{g}(t)|\,dt

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{m}\int_{\xi_{m}}^{\xi_{m+1}}\tilde{g}(t)\,dt,

for some signs
\varepsilon_{m}\in\{-1,1\}
. We define the function
G(x)\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{x}\tilde{g}(t)\,dt
and hence obtain the following upper bound

(11)

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{g}\rVert_{1}\leqslant 4d\sup_{x\in[0,1/2]}|G(x)|.

This bound above can be quite wasteful for general
P
and
K
and could be improved, but the loss of a factor of
d
is unimportant for (
5
) in the situation provided by the structural result Proposition
4.4
where
P,\log K
are exponential in
d
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
D_{n}(t)=1/2+\sum_{m=1}^{n}\cos(mt)
be a Dirichlet kernel. Then uniformly for
x\in[0,\pi]
, we have

(12)

\int_{0}^{x}D_{n}(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\sin nt}{t}\,dt+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

The proof can be assembled by combining some results from chapter II of Zygmund’s book
[
11
]
.

Proof.

Let
D^{*}_{n}(t)=D_{n}(t)-1/2\cos(nt)
. Since
\int_{J}\cos(nt)\,dt=O(1/n)
uniformly for all intervals
J\subset[0,2\pi]
, it suffices to prove that
\int_{0}^{x}D^{*}_{n}(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\sin nt}{t}\,dt+O(1/n)
. We have the standard formula
D^{*}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\cot(t/2)\sin(nt)
, see
[
11
, (5.2)]
, and hence we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}D^{*}_{n}(t)-\frac{\sin(nt)}{t}\,dt=\int_{0}^{2\pi}H_{x}(t)\sin(nt)\,dt

where
H_{x}(t)\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\cot(t/2)-1/t&\text{if $t\in(0,x)$}\\
0&\text{if $t\in[x,2\pi]$}\end{cases}
. Let
f
be a function on
(0,2\pi)
whose total variation
V(f)
is bounded, then we can bound the Fourier coefficients of
f
in terms of
V(f)
as follows, see
[
11
, Theorem 4.12]
:

(13)

\hat{f}(n)\ll\frac{V(f)}{n}.

Using the fact that
H_{\pi}(t)
is bounded, vanishes at
t=0
and has bounded variation on
[0,\pi]
, we see that
V(H_{x})=O(1)
is uniformly bounded for all
x\in[0,\pi]
. Hence, from (
13
) we deduce that uniformly for
x\in[0,\pi]
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}H_{x}(t)\sin(nt)\,dt\ll|\hat{H_{x}}(n)|\ll n^{-1}.

∎

Let
\operatorname{Si}(x)\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt
denote the sine integral. Then by Lemma
12
we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}D_{n}(2\pi t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2\pi}\operatorname{Si}(2\pi nx)+O(1/n)

uniformly for all
x\in[0,1/2]
. Getting back to
\tilde{g}
, if we write the intervals
\tilde{J}_{j}=(n_{j-1},n_{j}]
, then we can rewrite (
9
) as

(14)

\tilde{g}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{K}}c_{j}(D_{n_{j}}(2\pi t)-D_{n_{j-1}}(2\pi t))

so that for
x\in[0,1/2]
we have

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\tilde{g}(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{K}}c_{j}\left(\operatorname{Si}(2\pi n_{j}x)-\operatorname{Si}(2\pi n_{j-1}x)\right)+O\left(\max_{j}|c_{j}|\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{K}}\frac{1}{n_{j}}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{K}}c_{j}\int_{2\pi n_{j-1}x}^{2\pi n_{j}x}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt+O\left(M(S)\log(\tilde{K})\right),

where we used that
\max|c_{j}|\leqslant 4M(S)
and that
n_{1}<n_{2}<\dots<n_{\tilde{K}}
to bound the error term. We have therefore shown that

(16)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,1/2]}|G(x)|\ll M(S)\sup_{x\in[0,\pi]}\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{K}}\left|\int_{n_{j-1}x}^{n_{j}x}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt\right|+O(M(S)\log\tilde{K}).

The first term in (
16
) will be bounded with the help of the next lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for all
y^{\prime}>y>1
we have

\left|\int_{y}^{y^{\prime}}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt\right|\leqslant Cy^{-1}\min\left(1,|y^{\prime}-y|\right).

Proof.

From the pointwise bound
|\frac{\sin t}{t}|\leqslant t^{-1}
we immediately deduce
\left|\int_{y}^{y^{\prime}}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt\right|\leqslant y^{-1}|y^{\prime}-y|.
For the other bound, assume that
a\pi,(a+1)\pi,\dots,b\pi
are the integer multiples of
\pi
in
(y,y^{\prime})
. The sequence
\left(\int_{j\pi}^{(j+1)\pi}t^{-1}\sin t\,dt\right)_{j}
is alternating in sign and strictly decreasing in absolute value, so

\displaystyle\left|\int_{y}^{y^{\prime}}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\int_{y}^{a\pi}t^{-1}\sin t\,dt\right|+\left|\sum_{j=a}^{b-1}\int_{j\pi}^{(j+1)\pi}t^{-1}\sin t\,dt\right|+\left|\int_{b\pi}^{y^{\prime}}t^{-1}\sin t\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\ll y^{-1}+\left|\int_{a\pi}^{(a+1)\pi}t^{-1}\sin t\,dt\right|+(y^{\prime})^{-1}\ll y^{-1}.

∎

Combining (
11
) and (
16
), we have shown so far that

(17)

\lVert\tilde{g}\rVert_{1}\ll dM(S)\sup_{x\in[0,\pi]}\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{K}}\left|\int_{n_{j-1}x}^{n_{j}x}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt\right|+dM(S)\log\tilde{K}.

The final step in proving an upper bound for
\lVert\tilde{g}\rVert_{1}
is bounding the first term above.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
x\in[0,\pi]
and let
n_{1}<n_{2}<\dots<n_{\tilde{K}}
be positive integers. Then

\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{K}}\left|\int_{n_{j-1}x}^{n_{j}x}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt\right|\ll 1+\log\tilde{K}.

Proof.

Let
x\in[0,\pi]
and
n_{1}<n_{2}<\dots<n_{\tilde{K}}
. For each
m\in\mathbf{N}
which satisfies
m<n_{\tilde{K}}x
we define

\displaystyle j_{m}\vcentcolon=\min\{j\in[\tilde{K}]:n_{j}x>m\}.

This produces a set of indices
\{j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{\tilde{k}}\}\subset[\tilde{K}]
where
\tilde{k}
is the largest integer less than
n_{\tilde{K}}x
. In other words, we define a partition of
[1,\tilde{K}]
into intervals
[1,j_{1}),[j_{1},j_{2}),\dots,[j_{\tilde{k}},\tilde{K}]
such that

\displaystyle n_{j}x\in\begin{cases}[0,1]&\text{ if $j\in[1,j_{1})$,}\\
(m,m+1]&\text{ if $j\in[j_{m},j_{m+1})$ for each $m=1,2,\dots,\tilde{k}$,}\\
(\tilde{k},\tilde{k}+1]&\text{ if $j\in[j_{\tilde{k}},\tilde{K}]$.}\end{cases}

Then we can bound the sum of interest as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{K}}\left|\int_{n_{j-1}x}^{n_{j}x}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{\sin t}{t}\right|\,dt+\left|\int_{n_{j_{1}-1}x}^{n_{j_{1}}x}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt\right|

(18)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=j_{1}}^{j_{\tilde{k}}}\left|\int_{n_{j}x}^{n_{j+1}x}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt\right|+\left|\int_{n_{j_{\tilde{k}}}x}^{n_{{\tilde{K}}}x}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt\right|,

noting that
n_{j_{1}-1}x\leqslant 1
by definition. The first term on the right hand side is
O(1)
, and the second and the fourth are each bounded by
2\sup_{y}|\operatorname{Si}(y)|
which is also
O(1)
. Indeed, it is a well-known fact that
\sup_{y\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\operatorname{Si}(y)\right|=O(1)
which can be proved by observing that
t^{-1}\sin t
is bounded on
\mathbf{R}
and by recalling the classical result that
\lim_{y\to\infty}\operatorname{Si}(y)=\frac{\pi}{2}
. Using Lemma
3.4
, the third term can be bounded by

(19)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=j_{1}}^{j_{\tilde{k}}}\left|\int_{n_{j}x}^{n_{j+1}x}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=j_{1}}^{j_{\tilde{k}}}\frac{1}{n_{j}x}\min(1,|n_{j+1}x-n_{j}x|).

Recall from the definition of the indices
j_{m}
that
n_{j}x\in(m,m+1]
for all
j\in[j_{m},j_{m+1})
and hence we can bound

\displaystyle\sum_{j=j_{m}}^{j_{m+1}-1}\frac{1}{n_{j}x}\min(1,|n_{j+1}x-n_{j}x|)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=j_{m}}^{j_{m+1}-1}\min(1,|n_{j+1}x-n_{j}x|)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{m}\left(1+\sum_{j=j_{m}}^{j_{m+1}-2}|n_{j+1}x-n_{j}x|\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{m}\left(1+\left((m+1)-m\right)\right)\ll\frac{1}{m}.

Finally, the intervals
[j_{1},j_{2}),[j_{2},j_{3}),\dots,[j_{\tilde{k}-1},j_{\tilde{k}}]
partition
[j_{1},j_{\tilde{k}}]
and hence we can bound the total sum in (
19
) by

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\tilde{k}}\sum_{j=j_{m}}^{j_{m+1}-1}\frac{1}{n_{j}x}\min(1,|n_{j+1}x-n_{j}x|)

We are now in a position to prove the main result of this section.

Proof of Proposition

5.
Proof of the main theorem

In this short section, we show how the results from Sections 3 and 4 may be combined to prove Theorem
