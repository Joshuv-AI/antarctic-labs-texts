Transient Waiting Time Distributions in Small Call Centres with Skills-Based Routing
Mark Fackrell, Hritika Gupta, Peter G. Taylor

Abstract
Many call centres are subject to service level agreements that stipulate that they must achieve targets in terms of the proportion of calls that are answered within a specified time. In order to manage a centre so that targets like these are met, we need to have a method of calculating the waiting time distributions experienced by customers. In this paper, we provide such a method for small call centres that employ skills-based routing. We first build the methodology for the single-skill case and then extend it to a multi-skill case. We model the call centre system as a continuous-time Markov chain and then make use of the Laplace transform to calculate the relevant quantities. We later demonstrate the use of this method to find the optimal routing policy in a certain class of policies. Keywords: Call centre, queueing, transient, waiting time.

Introduction

The term
call centre
is commonly used to describe a telephone-based human-service operation. A call centre provides tele-services, namely services in which customers and service agents are remote from each other
[
17
]
.

It is common for specialist call centre providers to manage centres for client companies, in which case the provider is usually responsible for meeting the provisions of a service level agreement. For instance, they may be required to answer at least
90\%
of all calls within
20
seconds and have no more than
3\%
abandonments measured over a month. The cost of human resources typically constitutes more than
60\%
of the overall operating expenses of a telephone service centre (
[
13
]
,
[
6
]
). Hence, a reasonable objective for a call centre service provider is to minimise staffing requirements while fulfilling its agreed targets. In a previous paper
[
16
]
, we discussed how to calculate the expected number of abandonments and the expected total waiting time in
(0,t)
. In this paper, we extend this analysis to calculate the waiting time distributions of the customers. This requires first modelling the waiting times of customers, conditional on the state that they observe on arrival and then taking expectations over the possible states.

In most call centres, customer inquiries are categorised based on their complexity. Likewise, agents are categorised according to their skill set. An arriving caller is first directed to an automatic
Interactive Voice Response (IVR)
system, which asks questions that enable it to classify what type of query the caller has. The task is then to allocate the call to a suitable agent. Since some agents have multiple skills that enable them to deal with more than one type of enquiry, this is not a straightforward decision and there is an opportunity for a call centre to increase its efficiency by making these decisions in an optimal manner. The rules governing the assignment of incoming calls are termed
policies
here, and different policies can lead to variations in the call centre’s performance.

In this paper, we develop a method to calculate the waiting time distributions in call centres that can be modelled as Markov chains under a transient setting without using any asymptotic regimes. It is important to use the transient setting because in a call centre, the call arrival rate changes throughout the day and the number of agents also changes with changes in shifts, hence the initial state of the system cannot be ignored while calculating performance measures.

Our study has been motivated by a problem brought to us by an industry partner that manages call centres for its clients. The particular centre that the industry partner was interested in is reasonably small, with around a dozen agents in total. Furthermore, there are four levels of agents of increasing expertise who are required to handle four levels of queries of increasing complexity. Agents at level
j
are able to handle queries from all levels less than or equal to
j
.

In
[
16
]
, we calculated the expected values of some performance measures for such a call centre when some defined call allocation policies are used. In this paper, we aim to extend this study by calculating the distribution function of the waiting time of customers which, as we observed above, is needed for meeting measures that are often inserted into service level agreements. It is more difficult to calculate distribution functions than expectations, especially for more than one type of caller. Depending on the policy, a customer may jump in front of other customers, hence the waiting time of a customer can be affected by other customers that arrive later.

We will focus on reservation policies. Under this class of policies, if there is an arrival of a level
j-1
caller, we first try to assign that call to a level
j-1
agent and if they all are busy, we check the number of level
j
agents available, and if this number is greater than or equal to a certain number
n_{j}
(which we define later in Subsection
4.1
), we assign the call to one of the available level
j
agents. Otherwise, the caller has to wait in the queue.

When a service is completed by a level
j
agent, that level
j
agent will first look for level
j
calls and answer them in first-come-first-served (FCFS) order, and if no level
j
calls are in waiting but level
j-1
calls are in waiting, then they will take the first level
j-1
call in the queue if the number of level
j
agents available is at least
n_{j}
. This implies that customers at the same level are served according to an FCFS discipline, but this does not apply across levels.

We model the system as a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC), assuming that the calls are arriving according to a Poisson process, and the service and abandonment times are exponentially distributed. The method we will use here was introduced in
[
7
]
.

We start by illustrating the method for a simple case where there is one type of caller and one class of agent in Section
2
. In Section
3

4

5
we give a numerical example utilising the method to find the optimal number of agents to be reserved at each level such that the percentage of callers served within
20

6
.

Four levels

Now, we will modify the method above to calculate the waiting time distributions when there are four types of calls and four classes of agents, which is equal to the number of levels our partner call centre uses.

. We will denote the index of an element by the state it corresponds to.

4.2.2
Transition rates

There will be a transition to a new state in the case of an arrival, a service or an abandonment at each level. The transition would be different if the service is completed by the same level agent as compared to if the service is completed by an agent one level higher. A service completed by an agent one level higher can also lead to different states depending on the current state of the system. For example, say a level 1 call is served by a level 2 agent and now the agent is free. If there is no level 2 call in waiting but there are more level 1 calls in waiting, the agent will take the first level 1 call in the queue which will lead to a transition from
(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
to
(a-1,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
(because a level 1 call was served but there was no change in the number of level 1 calls being served by level 2 agents). However if there is a level 2 call in waiting and the agent takes that call, it will lead to a transition from
(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
to
(a-1,a_{1}-1,b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
. Similarly a call arrival can also lead to different states depending on who serves that call. A detailed description of all the possible transitions is given in
[
16
]
.

4.2.3
Laplace Transforms

As we did in Section
2.1

Again, we can solve these equations numerically for a given set of parameters and then invert them using the Euler method.

For a given state
\mathbf{\nu_{0}}=(a_{0},a_{10},b_{0},b_{10},c_{0},c_{10},d_{0})
at time
0
, let the probability that the system is in state
(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
at a uniformly-distributed point during the time interval
(0,t)
be denoted by
r_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}},(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)}(t)
. Using an argument similar to that presented in Section 2.2, this can be calculated using

r_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}},(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)}(\mathbf{n},t)=\dfrac{(\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}}}(\mathbf{n},t))_{(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)}}{t}.

(11)

Next, following the procedure that we introduced in Section
2.3
, we need to calculate the probability that a caller is served within
y
time units given the state of the system when they arrive. This is different for different levels of caller. Below, we deal with each level in turn, starting with level 4 callers, because this is the simplest case.

4.3
The waiting time distribution for a level
4
caller

We can model the situation for a tagged level
4
caller as a CTMC with state space
\{(c_{1},d):c_{1}+d\leq\ell,c_{1}\leq k_{4}-n_{4}\}
because for a level
4
caller, the number of level
1
,
2
or
3
callers in the system is irrelevant. Its waiting time distribution is affected only by the number of level
3
callers being served by level
4
agents when it arrives. As soon as a level
4
agent is free, the arriving level
4
caller will have priority over callers at all other levels.

For
y,t>0
, let
\Xi^{4}_{c_{1},d}(\mathbf{n},y)
be the probability that a level
4
caller who arrives during the interval
(0,t)
is served within
y
time units given that the state of the system at their arrival is
(c_{1},d)
and the reservation vector is
\mathbf{n}
.

We can proceed in a similar way as Subsection
2.3
. The Laplace transform,
\tilde{\Xi}_{c_{1},d}^{4}(\mathbf{n},s)=0
for
c_{1}+d=\ell
(because if the system is full, the arriving caller is blocked and hence the probability of being served is
0
), and for
c_{1}+d<\ell

\tilde{\Xi}_{c_{1},d}^{4}(\mathbf{n},s)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{s}&d<k_{4}-c_{1}\\[6.88889pt]
\dfrac{c_{1}\mu_{3}^{\prime}\tilde{\Xi}_{c_{1}-1,d}^{4}(\mathbf{n},s)+\left((k_{4}-c_{1})\mu_{4}+(d-(k_{4}-c_{1}))\theta_{4}\right)\tilde{\Xi}_{c_{1},d-1}^{4}(\mathbf{n},s)}{c_{1}\mu_{3}^{\prime}+(k_{4}-c_{1})\mu_{4}+(d+1-(k_{4}-c_{1}))\theta_{4}+s}&d\geq k_{4}-c_{1}.\end{cases}

(12)

4.4
The waiting time distribution for a level
3
caller

We can model the situation of a tagged level
3
caller as a CTMC with state space
\{(b_{1},c,c_{1},d):b_{1}+c+d\leq\ell,b_{1}\leq k_{3}-n_{3},\min(c-k_{3},k_{4}-d-n_{4})\mathbbm{1}_{c>k_{3},d<k_{4}-n_{4}}\leq c_{1}\leq\min(k_{4}-n_{4},c)\}
because the waiting time of a level
3
caller will be affected by the total number of level
3
and level
4
callers, and also by the number of level
2
callers being served by level
3
agents. Also, the arrival of new level
3
callers will not change anything but the arrival of new level
4
callers is relevant (because level
4
callers have priority over level
3
callers for being served by level
4
agents). We will consider the transition rates accordingly.

For
y,t>0
, let
{\Xi}_{b_{1},c,c_{1},d}^{3}(\mathbf{n},y)
be the probability that a level
3
caller arriving during the interval
(0,t)
is served within
y
time units given that the state of the system at their arrival is
(b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
and the reservation vector is
\mathbf{n}
.

Similar to the last section, the Laplace transforms,
\tilde{\Xi}_{b_{1},c,c_{1},d}^{3}(\mathbf{n},s)=0
for
b_{1}+c+d=\ell
and otherwise

\tilde{\Xi}_{b_{1},c,c_{1},d}^{3}(\mathbf{n},s)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{s}&k_{3}-b_{1}>c-c_{1}\text{ or }k_{4}>d+c_{1}+n_{4}\\[12.91663pt]
\dfrac{\xi^{3}_{b_{1},c,c_{1},d}(\mathbf{n},s)}{\psi^{3}_{b_{1},c,c_{1},d}(s)}&k_{3}-b_{1}\leq c-c_{1}\text{ and }k_{4}\leq d+c_{1}+n_{4}\end{cases}

(13)

where

\begin{split}\xi^{3}_{b_{1},c,c_{1},d}(\mathbf{n},s)=&\lambda_{4}\mathbbm{1}_{b_{1}+c+d<\ell-1}\tilde{\Xi}_{b_{1},c,c_{1},d+1}^{3}+(k_{3}-b_{1})\mu_{3}\tilde{\Xi}_{b_{1},c-1,c_{1},d}^{3}(\mathbf{n},s)+\\
&b_{1}\mu_{2}^{\prime}\tilde{\Xi}_{b_{1}-1,c,c_{1},d}^{3}(\mathbf{n},s)+\\
&c_{1}\mu_{3}^{\prime}\tilde{\Xi}_{b_{1},c-1,c_{1}-(1-\mathbbm{1}_{c+1-c_{1}>k_{3}-b_{1},d=k_{4}-c_{1}-n_{4}}),d}^{3}(\mathbf{n},s)+\\
&\min(k_{4}-c_{1},d)\mu_{4}\tilde{\Xi}_{b_{1},c,c_{1}+\mathbbm{1}_{c+1-c_{1}>k_{3}-b_{1},d=k_{4}-c_{1}-n_{4}},d-1}^{3}(\mathbf{n},s)+\\
&(c-(k_{3}-b_{1}+c_{1}))\theta_{3}\tilde{\Xi}_{b_{1},c-1,c_{1},d}^{3}(\mathbf{n},s)+\\
&\max(0,d-(k_{4}-c_{1}))\theta_{4}\tilde{\Xi}_{b_{1},c,c_{1},d-1}^{3}(\mathbf{n},s)\end{split}

(14)

and

\begin{split}\psi^{3}_{b_{1},c,c_{1},d}(s)=&\lambda_{4}\mathbbm{1}_{b_{1}+c+d<\ell-1}+(k_{3}-b_{1})\mu_{3}+b_{1}\mu_{2}^{\prime}+c_{1}\mu_{3}^{\prime}+\min(d,k_{4}-c_{1})\mu_{4}+\\
&(c+1-(k_{3}-b_{1}+c_{1}))\theta_{3}+\max(0,d-(k_{4}-c_{1}))\theta_{4}+s.\end{split}

(15)

4.5
The waiting time distribution for a level
2
caller

The expression for the probability of being served within
y
time units for a level
2
caller (
{\Xi}_{a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d}^{2}(\mathbf{n},y)
) is similar to Subsection
4.4
except that we need to include two more variables in the state space, because now, we are concerned with all level
2
,
3
and
4
callers in addition to level
1
callers being served by level
2
agents. Accordingly, we include the relevant transition rates as well. The state space is given by
\{(a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)\}
such that

•

a_{1}+b+c+d\leq\ell
,

•

a_{1}\leq k_{2}-n_{2}
,

•

\min(b-k_{2},k_{3}-c-n_{3})\mathbbm{1}_{b>k_{2},c<k_{3}-n_{3}}\leq b_{1}\leq\min(b,k_{3}-n_{3})
and

•

\min(c-k_{3},k_{4}-d-n_{4})\mathbbm{1}_{c>k_{3},d<k_{4}-n_{4}}\leq c_{1}\leq\min(c,k_{4}-n_{4})
.

The expressions for the Laplace transforms are given in Appendix
B.1
.

4.6
The waiting time distribution for a level
1
caller

For a tagged level
1
caller the state space is the same as
S
. The transition rates are also similar to Subsection
4.2.2
except for some small changes, for example, the transition rate associated with the arrival of new level
1
callers will not be included. Let
{\Xi}_{a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d}^{1}(\mathbf{n},y)
be the probability that a level
1
caller arriving during the interval
(0,t)
is served within
y
time units given that the state of the system at their arrival is
(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
and the reservation vector is
\mathbf{n}
. The expressions for the Laplace transforms of these probabilities are given in Appendix
B.2
.

We can solve the Laplace transform equations for all four levels of callers and then invert them using the Euler method to obtain the required probabilities.

4.7
The waiting time distribution of any caller

For each type of caller, we can now use the law of total probability and the PASTA property to calculate the probability that an arriving caller is served within
y
time units by conditioning it on the state of the system when the caller arrives. Then we can use these probabilities and condition them on the type of arriving caller to obtain the general probability of being served within
y
time units for a caller at any level.

For a set of reservation parameters
{\bf n}
, let
P_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}}}^{j}(\mathbf{n},y,t)
be the probability that a level
j
caller who arrives during the interval
(0,t)
is served within
y
time units given that the initial state of the system at time 0 is
\mathbf{\nu_{0}}=(a_{0},a_{10},b_{0},b_{01},c_{0},c_{01},d_{0})
. Hence, for level 1 callers

P_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}}}^{1}(\mathbf{n},y,t)=\sum_{(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)\in S}\Xi_{a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d}^{1}(\mathbf{n},y)r_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}},(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)}(\mathbf{n},t),

(16)

for level 2 callers

P_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}}}^{2}(\mathbf{n},y,t)=\sum_{(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)\in S}\Xi_{a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d}^{2}(\mathbf{n},y)r_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}},(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)}(\mathbf{n},t),

(17)

for level 3 callers

P_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}}}^{3}(\mathbf{n},y,t)=\sum_{(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)\in S}\Xi_{b_{1},c,c_{1},d}^{3}(\mathbf{n},y)r_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}},(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)}(\mathbf{n},t),

(18)

and for level 4 callers

P_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}}}^{4}(\mathbf{n},y,t)=\sum_{(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)\in S}\Xi_{c_{1},d}^{4}(\mathbf{n},y)r_{{\mathbf{\nu_{0}}},(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)}(\mathbf{n},t).

(19)

Let
P_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}}}(\mathbf{n},y,t)
be the probability that a caller of any type who arrives during the interval
(0,t)
is served within
y
time units given that the state of the system at time 0 is
\mathbf{\nu_{0}}=(a_{0},a_{10},b_{0},b_{01},c_{0},c_{01},d_{0})
. Hence,

P_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}}}(\mathbf{n},y,t)=\dfrac{\Sigma_{j=1}^{4}\lambda_{j}P_{\mathbf{\nu_{0}}}^{j}(\mathbf{n},y,t)}{\Sigma_{j=1}^{4}\lambda_{j}}.

(20)

The limitation of this method is a state explosion problem that can occur if we want to analyse a system with high capacity in case of four types of calls or if we want to use it for a policy requiring a large number of state space variables. For four types of caller, we were able to run the code with
\ell=12
but not with
\ell=15
. We believe that the code can be parallelised and written more efficiently, in case a higher capacity is needed.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed a method to calculate the transient waiting time distributions for small call centres. In Section
5
, we provided a numerical example demonstrating how the method can be used to find the optimal allocation policy such that we maximise the probability of serving the customers within a given time duration.

This method can be extended further by adding more levels and capacity to the system, possibly using approximation methods. It can also be used for different structures of agents’ skill sets and other allocation policies.
