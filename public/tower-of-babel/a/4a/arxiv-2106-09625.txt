Poisson-Dirichlet asymptotics in condensing particle systems Thanks: S. Gabriel acknowledges financial support from EPSRC through grant EP/R513374/1. S. Grosskinsky is grateful to Technical University of Delft, where part of this research was carried out.
Paul Chleboun, Simon Gabriel, Stefan Grosskinsky

Abstract
. We study measures on random partitions, arising from condensing stochastic particle systems with stationary product distributions. We provide fairly general conditions on the stationary weights, which lead to Poisson-Dirichlet statistics of the condensed phase in the thermodynamic limit. The Poisson-Dirichlet distribution is known to be the unique reversible measure of split-merge dynamics for random partitions, which we use to characterize the limit law. We also establish concentration results for the macroscopic phase, using size-biased sampling techniques and the equivalence of ensembles to characterize the bulk distribution of the system.



1.
Introduction and results

1.1.
Mathematical setting and motivation

The results presented in this paper are motivated by the study of interacting particle systems. We consider finite systems consisting of
N
particles on
L
sites indexed by the set
\Lambda
, where
|\Lambda|=L
. For simplicity we take
\Lambda=\{1,\ldots,L\}
in the remainder of this paper.
The space of such particle configurations
\eta=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{L})
with
\eta_{x}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is given by

\Omega_{L,N}:=\left\{\eta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{L}:\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}=N\right\}\,,

which we equip with the discrete topology.
The dynamics of systems we consider are assumed to be irreducible Markov processes on
\Omega_{L,N}
, conserving only the quantities
N
and
L
. Thus, there exists a unique invariant distribution
\pi_{L,N}
of the system on
\Omega_{L,N}
, which is called the
canonical distribution
.

We will focus on models where the canonical distributions are of product form

\pi_{L,N}[d\eta]=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{L,N}}\prod_{x=1}^{L}w_{L}(\eta_{x})\,d\eta\ &\text{if }\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}=N\,,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1)

Here
d\eta
denotes the counting measure on
\Omega_{L,N}
and
(w_{L}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a sequence of positive weights, possibly depending on the system size
L
. The normalising constant, called
canonical partition function
, is given as

Z_{L,N}=\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{L,N}}\prod_{x=1}^{L}w_{L}(\eta_{x})\,.

Note that the weights
(w_{L}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are independent of the site
x
, thus the
\pi_{L,N}
are permutation invariant and in particular spatially homogeneous, so that single-site marginals
\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x}\in\cdot]
do not depend on
x
.

We are primarily interested in the limiting behaviour of
\pi_{L,N}
in the thermodynamic limit
N,L\to\infty
such that
\tfrac{N}{L}
converges to
\rho\;\geqslant\;0
, which we will subsequently abbreviate by
N/L\rightarrow\rho
.
Assume for now that the weak limit of the single-site marginals exists for all
\rho\;\geqslant\;0
,

\displaystyle\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x}\in\cdot\,]\to\nu_{\rho}[\,\cdot\,]\quad\text{as }N/L\rightarrow\rho\,,

(2)

and the limit is a probability measure on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
This implies in particular convergence of the expectations for bounded functions
f:\mathbb{N}_{0}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f(n)\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x}=n]\to\nu_{\rho}(f)\,,

where we write
\nu_{\rho}(f)
for the expectation of
f
under
\nu_{\rho}
. Sometimes we will also write
\nu_{\rho}(f(\eta_{x}))
for the corresponding expectation if needed for clarity.
Looking at a single site’s expected occupation number under
\pi_{L,N}
, we see that due to spatial homogeneity we have

\pi_{L,N}(\eta_{x})=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{y=1}^{L}\pi_{L,N}(\eta_{y})=\frac{1}{L}\pi_{L,N}\Big(\sum_{y=1}^{L}\eta_{y}\Big)=\frac{N}{L}\to\rho\,,

when taking the thermodynamic limit.
However, because the identity
f(n)=n
is an unbounded function,
we cannot guarantee that the particle density of the system is conserved in the limit
\nu_{\rho}
and
\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{x})
may be strictly smaller than
\rho
.
This phenomenon is known as condensation.

Definition 1.1

(Condensation)
.

A system characterised by spatially homogeneous canonical distributions
(\pi_{L,N})_{L,N}
exhibits
condensation
in the thermodynamic limit
N/L\rightarrow\rho
if
\nu_{\rho}
in (
2
) exists and

\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{x})<\rho=\lim_{N/L\rightarrow\rho}\pi_{L,N}(\eta_{x})\,.

Furthermore, we say that the system has a
condensation transition
with
critical density

\rho_{c}\;\geqslant\;0
if

\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{x})\begin{cases}=\rho&\quad\text{ if }\rho<\rho_{c}\,,\\
<\rho&\quad\text{ if }\rho>\rho_{c}\,.\end{cases}

In the context of stochastic particle systems, condensation means that a positive fraction of the total density
\rho
is not observed in the thermodynamic limit, since it concentrates on sites with diverging occupation numbers called the
condensed phase
. Clearly, the number of such sites has a vanishing volume fraction and does not contribute to the weak limit
\nu_{\rho}
, which describes the distribution of the
background
or
bulk phase
.

Condensation in homogeneous stochastic particle systems has been studied previously in great generality, partially reviewed e.g. in
[
5
,
14
,
21
]
. Early results are formulated in the context of zero-range processes in
[
8
,
13
,
20
]
and in
[
29
,
25
,
2
,
1
]
on a rigorous level, where the condensed phase concentrates on a single lattice site. Our goal here is to understand details of the condensed phase when it extends over more than one site and exhibits a non-trivial structure. Such structures have previously been observed as a result of spatial correlations
[
39
,
40
,
38
]
and as a result of
L
-dependent stationary weights, with a soft cut-off for site occupation numbers under zero-range dynamics
[
37
]
or in the inclusion process
[
28
]
.

On the level of particle configurations the condensed phase disappears in the thermodynamic limit due to its vanishing volume fraction. To study its structure, it is more useful to interpret a configuration as an ordered partition of the total mass.
For models of type (
1
) partitions and particle configurations are equivalent, since
\pi_{L,N}
is permutation invariant and the underlying lattice structure is irrelevant.

We will represent particle configurations rescaled by the total mass
N
as ordered partitions of the unit interval
[0,1]

on the set

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}:=\left\{p=(p_{i})_{i}\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}:\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}\;\leqslant\;1\quad\text{and}\quad p_{1}\;\geqslant\;p_{2}\;\geqslant\;\cdots\right\}\,.

(3)

We use the map
T=T(L,N):\Omega_{L,N}\to\overline{\nabla}
with

T(\eta):=\frac{1}{N}\left(\widehat{\eta}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\eta}_{L},0,\ldots\right)\,,

(4)

where
\widehat{\eta}=(\widehat{\eta}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\eta}_{L})
denotes the entries in
\eta
in decreasing order with
\widehat{\eta}_{1}\;\geqslant\;\widehat{\eta}_{2}\;\geqslant\;\ldots\;\geqslant\;\widehat{\eta}_{L}
. Since any permutation of entries in
\eta
yields the same partition in
\overline{\nabla}
, the map
T
is not injective. Thus, the push-forward measure of
\pi_{L,N}
under
T
on
\overline{\nabla}
is given by

\mu_{L,N}[dp]:=\pi_{L,N}\circ T^{-1}[dp]=\pi_{L,N}[d(Np)]\,|T^{-1}(\{p\})|\,,

(5)

with
Np
denoting any configuration in
\Omega_{L,N}
inducing the finite ordered partition
p\in\overline{\nabla}
.
Note that
\mu_{L,N}
concentrates on finite partitions with at most
L
non-zero entries and
T^{-1}(\{p\})=\emptyset
otherwise.
In fact, the
\mu_{L,N}
further concentrate on the subset where
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}=1
. However, this space is not compact, unlike
\overline{\nabla}
which is compact w.r.t. the product topology by Tychonoff’s theorem, ensuring existence of subsequential weak limits of
\mu_{L,N}
in the thermodynamic limit
N/L\to\rho
.
The objective of this article is to identify general assumptions on the weights
(w_{L})_{L}
, such that
for
\rho>0
large enough
\mu_{L,N}
converges weakly to a Poisson-Dirichlet distribution as
N/L\rightarrow\rho
. Details on this distribution are introduced in Section
2
.

The starting point of our analysis is the recent paper

[
28
]
in which weights of the form

w_{L}(n)=\frac{\Gamma(n+d)}{n!\Gamma(d)}\,,

(6)

with
d=d(L)\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\lim_{L\to\infty}dL=\theta\in(0,\infty)
,
are considered. Such weights emerge for example from the dynamics of the inclusion process introduced in
[
17
,
6
]
, which can also be applied in population genetics as a multi-species Moran model
[
34
]
, with the above scaling of the parameter
d
corresponding to a small mutation rate.
With weights (
6
) the system (
1
) exhibits a condensation transition with critical density
\rho_{c}=0
, leaving an empty bulk behind, i.e.
\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{x})=0
. For the condensed phase in this model we have

\displaystyle\mu_{L,N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\text{PD}(\theta)\,,\quad\text{ as }\,N/L\rightarrow\rho\,\text{ for all }\rho>0\,,

(7)

where PD
(\theta)
denotes the Poisson-Dirichlet distribution with parameter
\theta

[
28
, Theorem 1]
.
The proof uses the fact that
\pi_{L,N}
with weights (
6
) is a Dirichlet multinomial distribution which permits an exact, simple expression for the corresponding partition function
Z_{L,N}
. This leads to exact expressions for the distribution of size-biased marginals which characterize the Poisson-Dirichlet limit (cf. Section
2.1
).

Our main result provides a generalization to models with more general weights that do not lead to exact expressions for
Z_{L,N}
, and with non-trivial bulk distribution where
0<\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{x})<\rho
.
In our proof, we not only make use of the Poisson-Dirichlet distribution’s characterisation via size-biased sampling, which was essential for the arguments in
[
28
]
, but also use the characterisation as the unique reversible distribution under split-merge dynamics as explained in Section
2.1
.
This allows us to avoid explicit expressions or approximations of the partition function
Z_{L,N}
which are not always at hand.
Our approach is motivated by a recent paper by Ioffe and Tóth
[
27
]
, where the embedding of integer configurations into partitions of
[0,1]
was used to show convergence of cycle-length processes of stationary random stirring to the split-merge dynamics.

1.2.
Main results

We recall from (
1
) that the
\pi_{L,N}
are probability measures on
\Omega_{L,N}
given by

\displaystyle\pi_{L,N}[d\eta]=\frac{1}{Z_{L,N}}\prod_{x=1}^{L}w_{L}(\eta_{x})\,d\eta\,.

For our first result we fix a density
\rho>0
and choose
N,L\to\infty
such that
N/L\rightarrow\rho
.
We assume that

(A1)

w_{L}(n)\;\geqslant\;0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

and the limit

\lim_{L\to\infty}w_{L}(n)=:w(n)\;\geqslant\;0\quad\mbox{exists for all fixed }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,,

such that
w
is summable and non-trivial,

and a weak form of the equivalence of ensembles:

(A2)

The limiting probability distribution (
2
) exists and is of the form

\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{1}=n]\to\nu_{\rho}[n]=\frac{w(n)\phi^{n}}{\mathcal{Z}}\qquad\forall\,n\;\geqslant\;0\,,

for some
\phi>0
, and
\mathcal{Z}=\sum_{n}w(n)\phi^{n}\in(0,\infty)
is the corresponding normalising constant.

Remark 1.2
.

The equivalence of ensembles is the main mathematical framework to understand the large scale behaviour of statistical mechanics models, and in particular to show condensation as in Definition
1.1
(see Section
4
for details). Assumption
(A2)
has therefore been established for all homogeneous particle systems that are known to exhibit condensation (see citations above).
We will see in our second result Theorem
1.7
, that equivalence of ensembles and Assumption
(A2)
can be shown for a large class of models under slightly stronger assumptions on convergence of the stationary weights.

Remark 1.3
.

It is natural to ask if
\nu_{\rho}
, cf. (
2
), can always be described by an exponential change of measure w.r.t.
w
, as is assumed in
(A2)
.
One possible sufficient condition to recover the postulated form in
(A2)
is to require that

\nu_{\rho}
and the measure
w
defined by the limiting weights in
(A1)
are equivalent in the sense of measures, i.e.
\nu_{\rho}
and
w
have the same support,
and
w(0),\,w(1)>0
. Then we can define

\phi:=\frac{\nu_{\rho}[1]}{w(1)}\frac{w(0)}{\nu_{\rho}[0]}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{Z}=\frac{w(0)}{\nu_{\rho}[0]}\in(0,\infty)

and use a telescopic product argument for the ratio of partition functions to see that

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{L-1,N-n}}{Z_{L,N}}=\frac{Z_{L-1,N}}{Z_{L,N}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{Z_{L-1,N-k}}{Z_{L-1,N-k+1}}\to\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\phi^{n}\,.

Thus, we recover Assumption
(A2)
in this case, and a similar argument works in the degenerate case
w(0)>0
and
w(k)=0
for all
k\;\geqslant\;1
, which applies for the inclusion process with weights (
6
).
We believe that this connection between
\nu_{\rho}
and
w
holds under more general conditions, but a more detailed discussion is out of the scope of this paper.

In order to formulate our main result with simple notation, we assume without loss of generality that
\phi=\mathcal{Z}=1
, since we can absorb
\phi
and
\mathcal{Z}
into the weights by

\widetilde{w}_{L}(n):=\frac{w_{L}(n)\phi^{n}}{\mathcal{Z}}\quad\text{and }\quad\widetilde{w}(n):=\frac{w(n)\phi^{n}}{\mathcal{Z}}\,.

So
w
can be assumed to be the probability mass function of
\nu_{\rho}
, i.e.

\nu_{\rho}[\eta_{x}=n]=w(n)\quad\mbox{for all }n\;\geqslant\;0\,.

(8)

We are interested in the macroscopic part of the condensed phase, i.e. the distribution of occupation numbers
\eta_{x}
that scale linearly with the total mass
N
in the system when taking the thermodynamic limit.
The structure of this macroscopic phase will depend on the asymptotic behaviour of the stationary weights. In order to see Poisson-Dirichlet statistics in this phase, the weights
w_{L}(n)
must scale like
(nL)^{-1}
, for at least all
n
which are visible under macroscopic rescaling:

(A3)

there exists
\theta>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have

\sup_{\varepsilon N\;\leqslant\;n\;\leqslant\;N}\left|nw_{L}(n)L-\theta\right|\to 0\qquad\text{as }N/L\rightarrow\rho\,.

For the second part of Theorem
1.4
we also impose a second moment condition:

(A4)

The limit

\alpha^{2}:=\frac{1+\theta}{\rho}\lim_{N/L\rightarrow\rho}\frac{\pi_{L,N}(\eta_{x}^{2})}{N}\in[0,1]\quad\text{exists}\,.

(9)

In Section
3
we will see that

\alpha=\mu(\|p\|_{1})\,,\quad\mbox{with}\quad\|p\|_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}p_{j}\,,

i.e.
\alpha
coincides with the expected total mass fraction for each accumulation point
\mu
of the measures
(\mu_{L,N})_{L,N}
, and that the variance of
\|p\|_{1}
vanishes. Therefore, Assumption
(A4)
guarantees that the macroscopic phase is well defined in the thermodynamic limit, excluding fluctuations of mass towards other scales, and plays an important role when identifying the accumulation points
\mu
. This leads to our first main result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\rho>0
and
(w_{L})_{L}
be a sequence of weights satisfying

(A1)
-
(A3)
for some
\theta\in(0,1]
and let
\mu
be an accumulation point of
the laws of mass partitions
(\mu_{L,N})_{L,N}
defined in (
5
). Then

\mu=\text{PD}_{[0,\alpha_{\mu}]}(\theta)\quad\mbox{with}\quad\alpha_{\mu}=\mu\big(\|p\|_{1}\big)\in[0,1]\ ,

where PD
{}_{[0,\alpha^{\prime}]}(\theta)
denotes the Poisson-Dirichlet distribution with parameter
\theta
, concentrating on partitions of the interval
[0,\alpha^{\prime}]
, which depends on the accumulation point.
If in addition
(A4)
holds then

(\mu_{L,N})_{L,N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\text{PD}_{[0,\alpha]}(\theta)\,,\quad\text{ as }\quad N/L\rightarrow\rho\,.

Remark 1.5
.

(a) If
\theta=0
, assumption
(A3)
does not specify the leading order limiting behaviour of the weights. Our proof can cover this case, if we assume in addition that
n\mapsto nw_{L}(n)
is a regularly varying function
1
1

1

i.e.
nw_{L}(n)/\big(\lambda nw_{L}([\lambda n])\big)\to C\in(0,\infty)
for all
\lambda>0
as
n\to\infty
for large enough
L
(see (
29
) in the proof). This is consistent with choosing fixed weights of the form
w_{L}(n)=w(n)=n^{-b}/\mathcal{Z}
for
b>2
whenever
n>0
and
w_{L}(0)=1
.
Indeed, for this choice, as part of a larger class of sub-exponential weights, it is a well known result that the condensed phase consists of a single cluster
[
13
,
25
,
2
]
, which can be interpreted as a degenerate Poisson-Dirichlet distribution with
\theta=0
.

(b) The result also covers the case
\alpha=0
where the macroscopic phase is empty and its limiting distribution is trivial. This includes models that do not condense at all, or where the condensed phase concentrates on sub-macroscopic scales such as for certain models with spatial correlations
[
39
,
40
,
38
]
. Of course our result does not say anything interesting in this case, since there is no mass on the macroscopic scale.

(c) All results in this paper extend to arbitrary
\theta>0
under the assumption that PD
(\theta)
is the unique reversible distribution for the split-merge dynamics introduced in Section
2
. It seems widely accepted that this is indeed the case, though to the authors’ best knowledge no proof exists for
\theta>1
.

Assumption
(A3)
is the core premise which guarantees the Poisson-Dirichlet limit of the macroscopic phase, and is consistent with the scaling of weights (
6
) for the inclusion process

w_{L}(n)=\frac{\Gamma(n+d)}{n!\Gamma(d)}\simeq\frac{\theta}{n\,L}\,,\quad\text{if we set}\quad d=\frac{\theta}{L}\,.

This scaling implies that a size-biased sample of a macroscopic cluster has the stationary weights
nw_{L}(n)\simeq\theta/L
which are independent of
n
. That means, picking a particle uniformly at random, the size of its cluster is uniformly distributed. This is the trademark of Poisson-Dirichlet statistics, and the distribution can only be normalized in the scaling limit due to the factor
1/L
.
Thus, to get a non-trivial macroscopic phase with
\theta>0
, it is necessary that the weights
w_{L}
depend on the system size
L
.
The role of
\theta
and more details on size-biased sampling will be given in Section
2
.

Remark 1.6
.

A system where
(A1)
-
(A3)
are satisfied but
(A4)
is difficult to verify, is for example given by weights of the form

\displaystyle w_{L}(n)=w(n)\mathds{1}\{n\;\leqslant\;N^{1/2}\}+\frac{\theta}{n\,L}\mathds{1}\{n>N^{1/2}\}\,,

(10)

with
w
being an arbitrary probability mass function on
\mathbb{N}
(not necessarily of finite support).
In this case, to determine if
(A4)
holds,
careful evaluation of the second moment condition would be required that would depend on the choice of
w(n)
.
Theorem
1.4
still allows to characterise the macroscopic part of the condensate as Poisson-Dirichlet, with the catch that it is possibly trivial with
\alpha=0
.

Recall that in Theorem
1.4
we have fixed the density
\rho>0
and it provides a very general result that also includes trivial cases without condensation. But we had to assume the equivalence of ensembles in
(A2)
and regularity of the macroscopic phase in
(A4)
, which are not easy to check in general (if not established already for particular models). Strengthening the requirements
(A1)
and
(A3)
on the stationary weights
(w_{L})_{L}
, we can use Theorem
1.4
to show a stronger but more specialized result, including the equivalence of ensembles and regularity of the macroscopic phase in the conclusion.

Theorem 1.7
.

Assume
(w_{L})_{L}
is a sequence of non-negative weights satisfying the following two conditions:

(B1)

(w_{L})_{L}
converges in the sup-norm,
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, to a sequence
w
such that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w(n)=1

and either
w(0)=1
or

\displaystyle w(0)>0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sup_{n}[w(n-1)\wedge w(n)]>0\,.

(11)

(B2)

There exists some
\theta\in(0,1]
such that

\lim_{J\to\infty}\lim_{L\to\infty}\sup_{n>J}\left|nw_{L}(n)L-\theta\right|=0\,.

Then the system exhibits a condensation transition according to Definition
1.1
with critical density

\displaystyle\rho_{c}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}nw(n)\in[0,\infty)\,.

Furthermore, we have bulk density
\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{x})=\rho_{c}
for all
\rho\;\geqslant\;\rho_{c}
and

\displaystyle(\mu_{L,N})_{L,N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\text{PD}_{[0,\alpha]}(\theta)\,,\quad\text{ as }\quad N/L\rightarrow\rho\;\geqslant\;\rho_{c}\,,

with
\alpha=\alpha(\rho)=1-\frac{\rho_{c}}{\rho}
.

Remark 1.8
.

By assumption
(B2)
, there exists an
A\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\lim_{L\to\infty}\sup_{n>A}\left|nw_{L}(n)L-\theta\right|\;\leqslant\;C\quad\text{with an arbitrary constant }C>0\,.

So for each
n\;\geqslant\;A
we have
w_{L}(n)\to 0
as
L\to\infty
, and hence by assumption
(B1)

w(n)=0\quad\text{for all }n>A\,,

(12)

and the limiting distribution
w
can only have finite support.
In this sense, Theorem
1.4
is more general because it allows for arbitrary limiting distributions of possibly infinite support, at the cost of loosing control over intermediate scales. Recall (
10
) for an example.

Clearly, the restriction in
(B1)
that
w
can be interpreted as a probability mass function is for notational convenience, we could just assume summability.
Furthermore, with
\theta>0
all models covered by this result have a macroscopic phase with non-trivial structure, excluding systems where the latter concentrates on a single site which have been studied previously (see citations above).
Condition (
11
) is necessary to avoid lattice effects and establish the equivalence of ensembles for the bulk part of the distribution (see Proposition
A.1
).
In the special case that
w(0)=1
there is a simpler proof of the equivalence of ensembles result.

We want to stress that single-site-condensation in models is typically due to a strong enough attraction between particles, whereas for systems covered in Theorem
1.7
particle attraction alone is too weak, and condensation only occurs in combination with particle expulsion from the bulk as represented by condition (
12
) on the limiting weights.
This strict exclusion condition for occupation numbers larger than
A
in the limiting weights prevents clustering of particles on sub-macroscopic scales.
It should be possible to weaken this, but some form of bulk exclusion is essential for condensation with non-trivial macroscopic phase in models with stationary product measures. In Section
5
we provide an intuitive explanation of this in terms of dynamics of generic particle systems covered by our result.

1.3.
Key steps of the proofs

The essential steps of the proof of Theorem
1.4
may be summarised as follows.
By compactness we know that
(\mu_{L,N})_{L,N}
has weak accumulation points. In order to determine the limit points’ distributions, we prove that
(\mu_{L,N})_{L,N}
is approximately reversible w.r.t. a discrete split-merge dynamics. These discrete dynamics converge to the generator of the coagulation-fragmentation process with split-merge dynamics, which we will introduce in Section
2
, see (
13
).
Lastly, we use size-biased sampling together with a disintegration argument to prove that the corresponding limit points concentrate and therefore have a Poisson-Dirichlet law. This is due to the fact that the Poisson-Dirichlet distribution is the unique distribution which concentrates and is reversible w.r.t. the limiting split-merge dynamics mentioned above
[
41
,
36
]
. Here, we say that
\mu
concentrates if
\|p\|_{1}=\alpha

\mu
-a.s. for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
.

Essentially, Theorem
1.7
is a direct application of Theorem
1.4
. Additionally, the stronger assumptions allow us to establish the equivalence of ensembles in Appendix
A
, which in our case implies the condensation transition.
The proof is based on the application of a local central limit theorem (LCLT) which, together with a relative entropy bound, shows convergence of single-site marginals to a distribution independent of the particle density
\rho\;\geqslant\;\rho_{c}
.

The remainder of the paper will be structured as follows: in Section
2
we will give a short review on Poisson-Dirichlet distributions and size-biased sampling. Section
3
will focus on the proof of Theorem
1.4
whereas in Section
4

we state the proof of Theorem
1.7
.
Lastly, we discuss possible applications of Theorem
1.7
to a family of zero-range and generalized inclusion processes in Section
5
.
In Appendix
A
we give a brief introduction to grand-canonical ensembles before proving equivalence of ensembles for size-dependent weights under a sub-exponential growth condition.

2.
Background on partitions

2.1.
The Poisson-Dirichlet distribution

The Poisson-Dirichlet (PD) distribution is a one-parameter family of probability measures on the space of ordered partitions
\nabla=\nabla_{[0,1]}
of the unit interval, where for any
\alpha>0
we denote

{\nabla}_{[0,\alpha]}:=\left\{p=(p_{i})_{i}\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}:\|p\|_{1}=\alpha\quad\text{and}\quad p_{1}\;\geqslant\;p_{2}\;\geqslant\;\cdots\right\}\,.

Note that elements
p
in
\nabla
are not partitions themselves but induce partitions of the form
\{[0,p_{1}),[p_{1},p_{1}+p_{2}),\ldots\}

The family of measures was first introduced by Kingman
[
31

in the study of random distributions on countably infinite sets, motivated by Bayesian inference and decision theory.
Apart from the original construction as a limit of Dirichlet distributions,
the PD distribution can be more intuitively constructed via a stick-breaking procedure. Let
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots
be independent Beta

-distributed random variables and define

V_{1}:=U_{1}\,,\;\;V_{2}:=(1-U_{1})U_{2}\,,\;\;V_{3}:=(1-U_{1})(1-U_{2})U_{3}\,,\ldots,

i.e. we start with a stick of unit length and continue by breaking a random fraction of
U_{1}
apart. Then we do the same with the remaining part of the stick and iterate.
The resulting random vector

is said to be GEM(
\theta

[
23
,
24
]
Engen
[

and McCloskey

. Reordering the entries of

in decreasing order yields

which is known to be PD(
\theta
)-distributed (see e.g.
[

]
).

Note the two special cases,
\theta=1
where the

’s are uniformly distributed on the interval
[0,1]
, and
\theta=0
where the
U_{i}
’s are degenerated point-measures on one and hence

.
Clearly, the choice of the interval
[0,1]
is arbitrary and one can construct PD and GEM distributions on intervals
[0,\alpha]
for arbitrary

just by rescaling

p\sim PD_{[0,\alpha]}(\theta)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad p/\alpha:=(p_{1}/\alpha,p_{2}/\alpha,\ldots)\sim PD_{[0,1]}(\theta)\,,

and analogously for
GEM_{[0,\alpha]}(\theta)
.
Since its introduction in
[
31
]
the PD distribution emerged first in population biology
[
31
,
11
]
, before appearing in statistical mechanics
[
32
,
18
,
27
]
and interacting particle systems
[
28
]
. In particular, the statistics of cycle length distributions in spatial permutations can be linked to condensation phenomena in quantum-mechanical models, see e.g.
[

3

4

and references therein. While those models often involve weights that satisfy a decay condition similar to

, they consider a different scaling limit with only one diverging parameter, and our results are not directly applicable.

Besides its characterisation via the GEM-construction, the PD distribution was furthermore found to be the unique invariant (in fact reversible) measure on
\nabla
of the coagulation-fragmentation process with split-merge dynamics for
\theta\in(0,1]
.
This is a Markov process on the state space
\overline{\nabla}

with infinitesimal generator given by

\mathcal{G}_{\theta}f(p)=\sum_{i\neq j}p_{i}p_{j}\left[f(\widehat{M}_{ij}p)-f(p)\right]+\theta\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}\left[\int_{0}^{1}f(\widehat{S}_{i}^{u}p)du-f(p)\right]\,.

(13)

Here

denotes the operator that merges the parts

and

to a single block of size

and then reorders the partition to maintain the decreasing order. On the other hand,
\widehat{S}_{i}^{u}p
defines the operation of splitting

into two blocks of size

and

before reordering the resulting partition.

Proposition 2.1

(
[

]
)

For
\theta\in[0,1]
, the Poisson-Dirichlet distribution PD(
\theta

\nabla
with respect to

Since the generator in (

In general, the set

Originally, the split-merge process was constructed in discrete time, see

The uniqueness of the invariant measure when

bounds
\|(\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N,\varepsilon)}-\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(\varepsilon)})g\|_{\infty}
from above, we take the thermodynamic limit
N/L\to\rho
and conclude the first part of the lemma.

The second statement requires us to show that
\|(\mathcal{G}_{\theta}-\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(\varepsilon)})g\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
.
First, note that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{G}_{\theta}-\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(\varepsilon)})g(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{\infty}p_{i}p_{j}\mathds{1}\{p_{i}<\varepsilon\}\cup\{p_{j}<\varepsilon\}\left[g(\widehat{M}_{ij}p)-g(p)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\theta\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}^{2}\left[\int_{0}^{1}g(\widehat{S}_{i}^{u}p)du-g(p)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\theta\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}^{2}\mathds{1}\{p_{i}\;\geqslant\;2\varepsilon\}\left[\int_{\varepsilon}^{1-\varepsilon}g(\widehat{S}_{i}^{u}p)du-g(p)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{\infty}p_{i}p_{j}\mathds{1}\{p_{i}<\varepsilon\}\cup\{p_{j}<\varepsilon\}\left[g(\widehat{M}_{ij}p)-g(p)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\theta\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}^{2}\mathds{1}\{p_{i}<2\varepsilon\}\left[\int_{0}^{1}g(\widehat{S}_{i}^{u}p)du-g(p)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\theta\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}^{2}\mathds{1}\{p_{i}\;\geqslant\;2\varepsilon\}\left[\int_{0}^{1}g(\widehat{S}_{i}^{u}p)du-\int_{\varepsilon}^{1-\varepsilon}g(\widehat{S}_{i}^{u}p)du\right]\,.

The first two terms vanish by dominated convergence and for the last term we have the estimate

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}^{2}\mathds{1}\{p_{i}{\;\geqslant\;}2\varepsilon\}\left[\int_{0}^{1}g(\widehat{S}_{i}^{u}p)du-\int_{\varepsilon}^{1-\varepsilon}\!\!\!\!\!g(\widehat{S}_{i}^{u}p)du\right]\right|\;\leqslant\;2\varepsilon\|g\|_{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}^{2}\;\leqslant\;2\varepsilon\|g\|_{\infty}\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

The proof of Proposition
3.1
, requires the following key observation which states that
\mu_{L,N}
is approximately reversible w.r.t. the dynamics corresponding to
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N,\varepsilon)}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For every
f,g\in C_{b}(\overline{\nabla})
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have

\left|\mu_{L,N}(f\,\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N,\varepsilon)}g)-\mu_{L,N}(g\,\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N,\varepsilon)}f)\right|\to 0

(21)

in the thermodynamic limit
N/L\to\rho\;\geqslant\;0
.

We postpone the proof of Lemma
3.4
until after the one of Proposition
3.1
. Now we have everything to state the proof of this section’s main finding.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
:

Due to compactness of the space
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\overline{\nabla})
w.r.t. the topology induced by weak convergence, the sequence
(\mu_{L,N})
has weak accumulation points.
Let
\mu
be such an accumulation point and
(\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}})_{j}
a subsequence converging to it. Then for each
\epsilon\in(0,1)
we have

\begin{split}\left|\mu(f\mathcal{G}_{\theta}g)-\mu(g\mathcal{G}_{\theta}f)\right|&\;\leqslant\;\left|\mu(f\mathcal{G}_{\theta}g)-\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}(f\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N_{j},\varepsilon)}g)\right|\\
&\qquad+\left|\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}(f\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N_{j},\varepsilon)}g)-\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}(g\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N_{j},\varepsilon)}f)\right|\\
&\qquad+\left|\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}(g\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N_{j},\varepsilon)}f)-\mu(g\mathcal{G}_{\theta}f)\right|\,.\end{split}

The middle term on the r.h.s. vanishes due to Lemma
3.4
, whereas the first term can be estimated by

\begin{split}\left|\mu(f\mathcal{G}_{\theta}g)-\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}(f\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N_{j},\varepsilon)}g)\right|&\;\leqslant\;\left|\mu(f\mathcal{G}_{\theta}g)-\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}(f\mathcal{G}_{\theta}g)\right|\\
&\qquad+\left|\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}(f\mathcal{G}_{\theta}g)-\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}(f\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N_{j},\varepsilon)}g)\right|\,.\end{split}

(22)

Since
(\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}})_{j}
converges in distribution to
\mu
and
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}g
is bounded and continuous, see
[
35
, Lemma 4]
, the first term on the r.h.s. of (
22
) vanishes as
L
diverges. Also, by Lemma
3.3
, the second term on the right vanishes after taking the limit
L,N\to\infty
before
\varepsilon\to 0
, since

\displaystyle\left|\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}(f(\mathcal{G}_{\theta}-\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N_{j},\varepsilon)})g)\right|\;\leqslant\;\|f\|_{\infty}\|(\mathcal{G}_{\theta}-\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N_{j},\varepsilon)})g\|_{\infty}\,.

The same steps hold when applied to
\left|\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}(g\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{(N_{j},\varepsilon)}f)-\mu(g\mathcal{G}_{\theta}f)\right|
.
Overall, this yields

\mu(f\mathcal{G}_{\theta}g)=\mu(g\mathcal{G}_{\theta}f)

which finishes the proof.
∎

It only remains to state the proof of Lemma
3.4
.

Proof of Lemma
3.4
:

First, we note that we can write
\mu_{L,N}(f\mathcal{G}^{(N,\varepsilon)}g)
in terms of the canonical distribution
\pi_{L,N}
:

\displaystyle N(N-1)\mu_{L,N}(f\mathcal{G}^{(N,\varepsilon)}g)

(23)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{L,N}}f\left(\frac{\widehat{\eta}}{N}\right)\sum_{i\neq j}^{L}\widehat{\eta}_{i}\widehat{\eta}_{j}\mathds{1}\{\widehat{\eta}_{i},\widehat{\eta}_{j}\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon N\}\left[g\left(\widehat{M}_{ij}\left(\frac{\widehat{\eta}}{N}\right)\right)-g\left(\frac{\widehat{\eta}}{N}\right)\right]\pi_{L,N}(\eta)

\displaystyle+\theta\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{L,N}}f\left(\frac{\widehat{\eta}}{N}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{L}\widehat{\eta}_{i}\mathds{1}\{\widehat{\eta}_{i}\;\geqslant\;2\varepsilon N\}\sum_{k=\varepsilon N}^{\widehat{\eta}_{i}-\varepsilon N}\left[g\left(\widehat{S}_{i}^{k/\widehat{\eta}_{i}}\left(\frac{\widehat{\eta}}{N}\right)\right)-g\left(\frac{\widehat{\eta}}{N}\right)\right]\pi_{L,N}(\eta)\,,

where we multiplied with
N(N-1)
for convenience.

In the following it will be easier not to work with discrete partitions, but with corresponding particle configurations without worrying about the order of the corresponding sites.
Hence, we require a new notation to lift split and merge operations to the space of particle configurations: for
\eta\in\Omega_{L,N}
we define

\begin{split}M_{xy}\eta:=\eta+\eta_{y}(e^{(x)}-e^{(y)})\,,\\
S_{xy}^{k}\eta:=\eta+k(e^{(y)}-e^{(x)})\,,\\
S_{x}^{k}\eta:=\eta+k(e^{(L+1)}-e^{(x)})\,,\end{split}

where
e^{(x)}\in\Omega_{L,1}
denotes the configuration with a single particle at site
x
, i.e.
(e^{(x)})_{z}=\delta_{x,z}
.
The operator
S_{x}^{k}
is only necessary for the case of a full particle configuration
\eta
, i.e.
\#_{0}(\eta)=0
. Then, we simply append the additional block of particles at the end of the configuration, which then has length
(L+1)
. This arbitrary but convenient choice of course does not change the projection of the dynamics on the level of partitions.
As such we can rewrite (
23
) as

\displaystyle N(N-1)

\displaystyle\mu_{L,N}(f\mathcal{G}^{(N,\varepsilon)}g)=\pi_{L,N}\left(\widehat{f}\left(\eta\right)\sum_{x\neq y}^{L}\eta_{x}\eta_{y}\mathds{1}\{\eta_{x},\eta_{y}\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon N\}\left[\widehat{g}\left(M_{xy}\eta\right)-\widehat{g}\left(\eta\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\theta\,\pi_{L,N}\left(\mathds{1}\{\#_{0}(\eta)>0\}\widehat{f}\left(\eta\right)\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}\mathds{1}\{\eta_{x}\;\geqslant\;2\varepsilon N\}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times\left.\sum_{k=\varepsilon N}^{\eta_{x}-\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{L}\frac{\mathds{1}\{\eta_{y}=0\}}{\#_{0}(\eta)}\left[\widehat{g}\left(S_{xy}^{k}\eta\right)-\widehat{g}\left(\eta\right)\right]\right)

(24)

\displaystyle\quad+\theta\,\pi_{L,N}\left(\mathds{1}\{\#_{0}(\eta)=0\}\widehat{f}\left(\eta\right)\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}\mathds{1}\{\eta_{x}\;\geqslant\;2\varepsilon N\}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times\left.\sum_{k=\varepsilon N}^{\eta_{x}-\varepsilon N}\left[\widehat{g}\left(S_{x}^{k}\eta\right)-\widehat{g}\left(\eta\right)\right]\right)\,,

where
\widehat{f}:=f\circ T
and
\widehat{g}:=g\circ T
with the ordering map
T
given in (
4
).
Furthermore, we can see that the terms that depend on
\widehat{f}
and
\widehat{g}
only through the product
\widehat{f}(\eta)\widehat{g}(\eta)
cancel when taking the difference between
\mu_{L,N}(f\mathcal{G}^{(N,\varepsilon)}g)
and
\mu_{L,N}(g\mathcal{G}^{(N,\varepsilon)}f)
in (
21
). Additionally, the very last term in (
3.1
) is negligible since, using
(\eta_{x}-1)/(N-1)\;\leqslant\;1
and
\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}/N=1

\pi_{L,N}
-almost surely, we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\pi_{L,N}&\left(\mathds{1}\{\#_{0}(\eta)=0\}\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}(\eta_{x}-1)\right)\;\leqslant\;\pi_{L,N}\left[\#_{0}(\eta)=0\right]\,,\end{split}

which vanishes due to Lemma
3.2
because
w(0)>0
.

To simplify notation we introduce

V_{f,g}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}:=\pi_{L,N}\left(\widehat{f}\left(\eta\right)\sum_{x\neq y}^{L}\eta_{x}\eta_{y}\mathds{1}\{\eta_{x},\eta_{y}\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon N\}\widehat{g}\left(M_{xy}\eta\right)\right)

(25)

and

\displaystyle U_{f,g}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle:=\theta\,\pi_{L,N}\left(\mathds{1}\{\#_{0}(\eta)>0\}\widehat{f}\left(\eta\right)\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}\mathds{1}\{\eta_{x}\;\geqslant\;2\varepsilon N\}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\left.\sum_{k=\varepsilon N}^{\eta_{x}-\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{L}\frac{\mathds{1}\{\eta_{y}=0\}}{\#_{0}(\eta)}\widehat{g}\left(S_{xy}^{k}\eta\right)\right)\,.

(26)

We are then left to analyse

\displaystyle N(N-1)

\displaystyle(\mu_{L,N}(f\mathcal{G}^{(N,\varepsilon)}g)-\mu_{L,N}(g\mathcal{G}^{(N,\varepsilon)}f))

\displaystyle=V_{f,g}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}+U_{f,g}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}-V_{g,f}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}-U_{g,f}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}+o(N^{2})\,.

The goal is to compare
V_{f,g}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}-U_{g,f}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}
and
U_{f,g}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}-V_{g,f}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}
, respectively, and show that these differences vanish in the limit if divided by
N(N-1)
.
In order to prove this, we perform a change of measure, since both
V^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}
and
U^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}
are expectations with respect to
\pi_{L,N}
.

First, we note that the restriction of the merge map

M_{xy}\Big|_{\{\eta_{y}=k\}}:\Omega_{L,N}\cap\{\eta_{y}=k\}\to\Omega_{L,N}

is injective and therefore defines a bijection between the set
\Omega_{L,N}\cap\{\eta_{y}=k\}
and its image
M_{xy}(\Omega_{L,N}\cap\{\eta_{y}=k\})=\Omega_{L,N}\cap\{\eta_{y}=0,k\;\leqslant\;\eta_{x}\}
with inverse
S_{xy}^{k}
.
Therefore, the change of measure of
\pi_{L,N}
and its pushforward measure under
M_{xy}
for fixed
x,y,k
on the set
\Omega_{L,N}\cap\{\eta_{y}=k\}
is given by:

\begin{split}\frac{\pi_{L,N}}{\pi_{L,N}\circ M_{xy}}[\eta]&=\frac{\prod_{z=1}^{L}w_{L}(\eta_{z})}{\prod_{z=1}^{L}w_{L}((M_{xy}\eta)_{z})}=\frac{w_{L}((M_{xy}\eta)_{x}-k)w_{L}(k)}{w_{L}((M_{xy}\eta)_{x})w_{L}(0)}\,.\end{split}

(27)

This will allow us to perform a change of measure in the following sense.
Fix
x,y
and
k
, furthermore let
h_{y,k}
be a real valued function on
\Omega_{L,N}
with support in
\{\eta_{y}=k\}
.
By definition of
M_{xy}
, we can define
\widetilde{h}_{x,y,k}=h_{y,k}\circ M_{xy}^{-1}
which is zero outside of
\Omega_{L,N}\cap\{\eta_{y}=0,k\;\leqslant\;\eta_{x}\}
. We then have

\begin{split}\pi_{L,N}(h_{y,k}(\eta))&=\pi_{L,N}(\widetilde{h}_{x,y,k}(M_{xy}\eta))=\int_{\{\eta_{y}=k\}}\widetilde{h}_{x,y,k}(M_{xy}\eta)\;\pi_{L,N}[d\eta]\\
&=\int_{\{\xi_{y}=0,k\;\leqslant\;\xi_{x}\}}\widetilde{h}_{x,y,k}(\xi)\;\pi_{L,N}\circ M_{xy}^{-1}[d\xi]\\
&=\int_{\{\xi_{y}=0,k\;\leqslant\;\xi_{x}\}}\widetilde{h}_{x,y,k}(\xi)\frac{w_{L}(\xi_{x}-k)w_{L}(k)}{w_{L}(\xi_{x})w_{L}(0)}\;\pi_{L,N}[d\xi]\,.\end{split}

Before we apply the change of measure, we divide and multiply by
(M_{xy}\eta)_{x}
, decompose over
\{\eta_{y}=k\}
, and interchange the order of integration:

\displaystyle V_{f,g}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\neq y}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\pi_{L,N}\left((M_{xy}\eta)_{x}\widehat{f}\left(\eta\right)\widehat{g}\left(M_{xy}\eta\right)\frac{\eta_{x}\eta_{y}}{(M_{xy}\eta)_{x}}\mathds{1}\{k,\eta_{x}\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon N\}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathds{1}\{\eta_{y}=k\}\mathds{1}\{(M_{xy}\eta)_{y}=0\}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\neq y}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\pi_{L,N}\left((M_{xy}\eta)_{x}\widehat{f}\left(S_{xy}^{k}M_{xy}\eta\right)\widehat{g}\left(M_{xy}\eta\right)\frac{((M_{xy}\eta)_{x}-k)k}{(M_{xy}\eta)_{x}}\right.\mathds{1}\{k\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon N\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathds{1}\{k\;\leqslant\;(M_{xy}\eta)_{x}-\varepsilon N\}\mathds{1}\{(S_{xy}^{k}M_{xy}\eta)_{y}=k\}\mathds{1}\{(M_{xy}\eta)_{y}=0\}\bigg)\,,

where in the last step we expressed
\eta
in terms of
M_{xy}\eta
.
Recall now (
27
) and note that we can recover
\eta
from
M_{xy}\eta
and
k=\eta_{y}
. Therefore, the change of measure yields

\displaystyle V_{f,g}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\neq y}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\pi_{L,N}\left(\mathds{1}\{\#_{0}(\eta)>0\}\eta_{x}\widehat{f}\left(S_{xy}^{k}\eta\right)\widehat{g}\left(\eta\right)\frac{(\eta_{x}-k)k}{\eta_{x}}\frac{w_{L}(\eta_{x}-k)w_{L}(k)}{w_{L}(\eta_{x})w_{L}(0)}.\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathds{1}\{k\;\leqslant\;\eta_{x}-\varepsilon N\}\mathds{1}\{k\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon N\}\mathds{1}\{(S_{xy}^{k}\eta)_{y}=k\}\mathds{1}\{\eta_{y}=0\}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\pi_{L,N}\left(\mathds{1}\{\#_{0}(\eta)>0\}\widehat{g}\left(\eta\right)\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}\mathds{1}\{\eta_{x}\;\geqslant\;2\varepsilon N\}\sum_{k=\varepsilon N}^{\eta_{x}-\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{L}\frac{\mathds{1}\{\eta_{y}=0\}}{\#_{0}(\eta)}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\times\widehat{f}\left(S_{xy}^{k}\eta\right)\#_{0}(\eta)\frac{(\eta_{x}-k)k}{\eta_{x}}\frac{w_{L}(\eta_{x}-k)w_{L}(k)}{w_{L}(\eta_{x})w_{L}(0)}\bigg)\,.

Comparing now
V_{f,g}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}
to
U_{g,f}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}
in (
26
), the only discrepancy is the term

\begin{split}\#_{0}(\eta)\frac{(\eta_{x}-k)k}{\eta_{x}}\frac{w_{L}(\eta_{x}-k)w_{L}(k)}{w_{L}(\eta_{x})w_{L}(0)}=\frac{\#_{0}(\eta)}{L}\frac{k\,w_{L}(k)L}{w_{L}(0)}\frac{(\eta_{x}-k)\,w_{L}(\eta_{x}-k)}{\eta_{x}\,w_{L}(\eta_{x})}\,.\end{split}

(28)

First, note that by Assumption
(A3)

k\,w_{L}(k)L\frac{(\eta_{x}-k)w_{L}(\eta_{x}-k)}{\eta_{x}w_{L}(\eta_{x})}\to\theta\,,\quad\mbox{as }L\to\infty\,,

(29)

uniformly in
k
and
\eta
, since
\varepsilon N\;\leqslant\;k,\eta_{x}-k,\eta_{x}
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mu_{L,N}(f\mathcal{G}^{(N,\varepsilon)}g)-\mu_{L,N}(g\mathcal{G}^{(N,\varepsilon)}f)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\left|\left(V_{f,g}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}-U_{g,f}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}\right)+\left(U_{f,g}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}-V_{g,f}^{(L,N,\varepsilon)}\right)\right|+o(1)

\displaystyle\qquad\;\leqslant\;\frac{2\|f\|_{\infty}\|g\|_{\infty}\theta}{N(N-1)}\pi_{L,N}\left(\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}\right.\left.\sum_{k=\varepsilon N}^{\eta_{x}-\varepsilon N}\left|\frac{\#_{0}(\eta)}{L}\frac{1}{w_{L}(0)}-1\right|\right)+o(1)

\displaystyle\qquad\;\leqslant\;2\|f\|_{\infty}\|g\|_{\infty}\theta\,\pi_{L,N}\left(\left|\frac{\#_{0}(\eta)}{L}\frac{1}{w_{L}(0)}-1\right|\right)+o(1)\,,

where we used (
29
) before dropping all indicator functions in the first inequality.
Lastly, by Lemma
3.2
and Assumption
(A1)
we know that

\displaystyle\lim_{N/L\rightarrow\rho}\pi_{L,N}\left(\left|\frac{\#_{0}(\eta)}{L}\frac{1}{w_{L}(0)}-1\right|\right)=0\,,

which completes the proof.

∎

3.2.
Concentration and uniqueness of the limit

As already mentioned in the introduction,
the only invariant distribution w.r.t.
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
which concentrates on full partitions
\nabla_{[0,\alpha]}
, for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
, is the Poisson-Dirichlet distribution PD
{}_{[0,\alpha]}(\theta)
.
Hence, in order to prove that weak accumulation points of measures
(\mu_{L,N})_{L,N}
coincide, it is enough to show that each such weak limit
\mu
from Proposition
3.1
satisfies

\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}\right)=0

and there exists
\alpha_{\mu}\in[0,1]
such that
\mu\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}\right)=\alpha_{\mu}
for every limit
\mu
.

In the following we consider not only the
\ell^{1}
-norm but also the
\ell^{2}
-norm as a function on
\overline{\nabla}
and write

\|p\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|p\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|p_{i}|^{2}\,,

respectively. Note that the latter has the advantage, in contrast to
\|\cdot\|_{1}
, of being a continuous function on
\overline{\nabla}
with respect to the product topology.

First, we recall the first part of Lemma 5 in
[
35
]
where Mayer-Wolf et al. proved the following relationship between
\mu(\|p\|_{1}^{2})
and
\mu(\|p\|_{2}^{2})
, which is a natural consequence of the balance between expected split and merge rates for the stationary distribution
\mu
.

Lemma 3.5

(Mayer-Wolf et al.)
.

Let
\theta\in(0,1]
and let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\overline{\nabla})
be invariant w.r.t.
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
, then

\mu(\|p\|_{1}^{2})=(1+\theta)\mu(\|p\|_{2}^{2})\,.

Together with its counterpart in the following lemma, this immediately proves that each subsequential limit of
(\mu_{L,N})_{L,N}
concentrates on some
\nabla_{[0,\alpha_{\mu}]}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\theta\in(0,1]
and let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\overline{\nabla})
be an accumulation point of
(\mu_{L,N})_{L,N}
.
Then

\mu(\|p\|_{1})^{2}=(1+\theta)\mu(\|p\|_{2}^{2})\,.

Proof.

Since the statement is trivial for
\mu=\delta_{(0,0,\ldots)}
, we assume without loss of generality
\mu\neq\delta_{(0,0,\ldots)}
.
Let
(\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence converging weakly to
\mu
.
For such
\mu
we
first note that

\displaystyle\mu(\|p\|_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\to\infty}\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}(\|p\|_{2}^{2})=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{L_{j}}{N_{j}^{2}}\pi_{L_{j},N_{j}}(\eta_{1}^{2})\,,

(30)

since
\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}
converges weakly to
\mu
.
Rewriting the r.h.s. as size-biased expectation, cf. (
19
), yields in particular

\displaystyle\mu(\|p\|_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{1}{N_{j}}\pi_{L_{j},N_{j}}(\widetilde{\eta}_{1})=\lim_{j\to\infty}\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}(\widetilde{p}_{1})=\lim_{j\to\infty}\sigma(\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}})(q_{1})\,.

(31)

In the second to last step we used that the size-biased distribution is invariant under reordering of the configuration.
Furthermore, weak convergence of
\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}}
implies weak convergence of the size biased distributions by Lemma
2.2
, i.e.

\sigma(\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}})\to\sigma(\mu)

in the topology induced by weak convergence. Recall that under
\sigma(\mu)
, the first component in
q
is zero with probability

\sigma(\mu)[q_{1}=0]=\mu(1-\|p\|_{1})\,.

(32)

Since the projection map on the first component is continuous w.r.t. the product topology, we have
\sigma(\mu_{L,N})(q_{1})\to\sigma(\mu)(q_{1})
which yields with (
31
) and (
18
)

\mu(\|p\|_{2}^{2})=\sigma(\mu)(q_{1})=\sigma(\mu)(q_{1}\;|\;q_{1}>0)\sigma(\mu)[q_{1}>0]=\sigma^{\prime}(\mu)(q_{1})\mu(\|p\|_{1})\,,

(33)

where
\sigma^{\prime}(\mu)[q_{1}\in\cdot]
is the size-biased distribution conditioned on positive components defined.
Now, using (
17
) and disintegration of measures we write

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(\mu)(q_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{\overline{\nabla}}\|p\|_{1}\int_{\nabla}q_{1}\,\sigma_{p/\|p\|_{1}}[dq]\,\mu[dp]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\bar{\alpha}\int_{\overline{\nabla}}\int_{\nabla}q_{1}\,\sigma_{p/\|p\|_{1}}[dq]\,\mu^{(\bar{\alpha})}[dp]\,\mu[\|p\|_{1}\in d\bar{\alpha}]\,,

where
\mu^{(\bar{\alpha})}=\mu[\,\cdot\,|\|p\|_{1}=\bar{\alpha}]
denotes the measure
\mu
conditioned on
\nabla_{[0,\bar{\alpha}]}
, which is well defined almost surely w.r.t.
\mu[\|p\|_{1}\in d\bar{\alpha}]
.
Since
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
(
13
) conserves
\|p\|_{1}
,
\mu^{(\bar{\alpha})}
is also invariant for
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
and thus equal to PD
{}_{[0,\bar{\alpha}]}(\theta)
by Proposition
2.1
.
Hence,
\sigma(\mu^{(\bar{\alpha})})[dq]=\int_{\overline{\nabla}}\sigma_{p/\|p\|_{1}}[dq]\,\mu^{(\bar{\alpha})}[dp]
, as in (
16
), is the GEM distribution and therefore

\int_{\overline{\nabla}}\int_{\nabla}q_{1}\,\sigma_{p/\|p\|_{1}}[dq]\,\mu^{(\bar{\alpha})}[dp]=\frac{1}{1+\theta}\,,\quad\text{ for a.e. }\bar{\alpha}\in(0,1]\,.

Thus,

\sigma^{\prime}(\mu)(q_{1})=\frac{1}{1+\theta}\int_{0}^{1}\bar{\alpha}\;\mu[\|p\|_{1}\in d\bar{\alpha}]=\frac{1}{1+\theta}\mu(\|p\|_{1})

which, with (
33
), yields with

\mu(\|p\|_{2}^{2})=\frac{1}{1+\theta}\mu(\|p\|_{1})^{2}\,,

concluding the proof.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

It is interesting to note that Lemma
3.6
holds in greater generality, because all
k
-norms are continuous whenever
k>1
. We have

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mu}^{k}=\mu(\|p\|_{1})^{k}=\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(j+\theta)\right]\mu(\|p\|_{k}^{k})\qquad\text{for all }k\;\geqslant\;1\,.

This can be proven in exactly the same way as Lemma
3.6
, with the only difference that we replace
\mu(\|p\|_{2}^{2})
with
\mu(\|p\|_{k}^{k})
when
k>1
. The case
k=1
is trivial.

Lemmas
3.5
and
3.6
imply
\mu(\|p\|_{1}^{2})=\mu(\|p\|_{1})^{2}
, which is equivalent to

\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}(\|p\|_{1})=\mu(\|p\|_{1}^{2})-\mu(\|p\|_{1})^{2}=0\,.

(34)

Therefore, accumulation points
\mu
concentrate on
\nabla_{[0,\alpha_{\mu}]}
with
\alpha_{\mu}:=\mu(\|p\|_{1})
. To conclude the proof of Theorem
1.4
, it is left to show that
\alpha_{\mu}
is independent of the choice of
\mu
if assumption
(A4)
is satisfied.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
\theta\in(0,1]
.
By Proposition
3.1
, we know that under Assumptions
(A1)
-
(A3)
all weak accumulation points of
(\mu_{L,N})_{L,N}
are reversible w.r.t.
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
.
Furthermore, every such limit
\mu
of a subsequence
(\mu_{L_{j},N_{j}})_{j\;\geqslant\;1}
concentrates on
\nabla_{[0,\alpha_{\mu}]}
for some
\alpha_{\mu}\in[0,1]
as follows from (
34
).
Thus, Proposition
2.1
implies that
\mu
must be the Poisson-Dirichlet distribution on
[0,\alpha_{\mu}]
with parameter
\theta
, which proves the first statement.

The only control we have on
\alpha_{\mu}
from
(A1)
-
(A3)
is that

\alpha_{\mu}\;\leqslant\;1-\frac{\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{1})}{\rho}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{1})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}nw(n)

is the expected density in the bulk.
However, Assumption
(A4)
together with Lemma
3.6
implies uniqueness of the limit, since for every accumulation point
\mu
we have

\mu(\|p\|_{1})=\sqrt{(1+\theta)\mu(\|p\|_{2}^{2})}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\theta}{\rho}\lim_{N/L\rightarrow\rho}\frac{\pi_{L,N}(\eta_{x}^{2})}{N}}=\alpha\,,

(35)

where we used the identity (
30
) in the second equality.
This implies the second statement and concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.7

In this section we prove Theorem
1.7
, a specialized version of Theorem
1.4
which is better suited for application to condensation in particle systems.
In contrast to assumptions
(A1)
-
(A3)
, we require uniform convergence of the weights as well as stronger control on the weights in sub-
L
scales in
(B2)
. However, this allows us to drop assumption
(A4)
that was needed to guarantee the concentration of the macroscopic phase in Theorem
1.4
.
Thanks to the stronger assumptions, we can explicitly calculate the form of limiting single-site marginals, which will imply the equivalance of ensembles
(A2)
and the condensation transition.

In Appendix
A
we prove the equivalence of ensembles and deduce that the system defined by weights
(w_{L})_{L}
satisfying
(B1)
(and a growth condition on the weights which is weaker than
(B2)
) exhibits condensation for
\rho
large enough. We summarize this result here, a more general and detailed version is given in Proposition
A.1
.

Proposition 4.1

(Equivalence of ensembles)
.

Consider weights
(w_{L})_{L}
and
w
satisfying
(B1)
,
(B2)
with corresponding canonical measures
(\pi_{L,N})_{L,N}
as defined in (
1
).
Then the system exhibits a condensation transition in the thermodynamic limit
N/L\rightarrow\rho\;\geqslant\;0
(cf. Definition
1.1
) with critical density
\rho_{c}:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nw(n)
.
More precisely, we have convergence of single-site marginals

\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x}\in\cdot]\to\nu_{\rho}
such that

\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\rho&\text{if }\rho<\rho_{c}\,,\\
\rho_{c}&\text{if }\rho\;\geqslant\;\rho_{c}\,.\end{array}\right.

(36)

and
\nu_{\rho}=w
for
\rho\;\geqslant\;\rho_{c}
.

This establishes existence of the condensed phase for
\rho>\rho_{c}
, and the following result guarantees that it agrees with the macroscopic phase, and there is no mass on intermediate scales.

Proposition 4.2
.

Consider weights
(w_{L})_{L}
and
w
satisfying
(B1)
and
(B2)
with corresponding canonical measures
(\pi_{L,N})_{L,N}
. Then, for every
\rho>\rho_{c}

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{N/L\rightarrow\rho}\pi_{L,N}[\widetilde{\eta}_{1}>\varepsilon N]=1-\frac{\rho_{c}}{\rho}\,.

Proof.

We fix a density
\rho>\rho_{c}
.
Now, by definition of the first size-biased marginal, cf. (
19
), we have for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]

\displaystyle\pi_{L,N}[J<\widetilde{\eta}_{1}\;\leqslant\;\varepsilon N]=\frac{L}{N}\sum_{n=J+1}^{\varepsilon N}n\,w_{L}(n)\frac{Z_{L-1,N-n}}{Z_{L-1,N}}\,,

for
N
large enough. By Assumption
(B2)
, there exists a sufficiently large
J=J(\varepsilon)
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to\infty}\sup_{n\;\geqslant\;J}|n\,w_{L}(n)L-\theta|<\varepsilon\,.

On the other hand, applying Lemma
4.3
below yields

\displaystyle\limsup_{N/L\rightarrow\rho}\sup_{J<n\;\leqslant\;\varepsilon N}\frac{Z_{L-1,N-n}}{Z_{L-1,N}}\;\leqslant\;\Big(1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\rho_{c}/\rho}\Big)^{-1}\,.

Altogether,

\displaystyle\lim_{N/L\rightarrow\rho}\pi_{L,N}[J<\widetilde{\eta}_{1}\;\leqslant\;\varepsilon N]\;\leqslant\;\varepsilon\,(\theta+\varepsilon)\Big(1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\rho_{c}/\rho}\Big)^{-1}\,,

which vanishes as we take the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.
This completes the proof, since

\lim_{N/L\rightarrow\rho}\pi_{L,N}[\widetilde{\eta}_{1}\;\leqslant\;J]=\lim_{N/L\rightarrow\rho}\frac{L}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{J}n\,\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{1}=n]=\frac{\rho_{c}}{\rho}\,,

which is a direct implication of Proposition
4.1
.
∎

We now prove the key estimate used in Proposition
4.2
, which guarantees that the ratio of partition functions
Z_{L-1,N-n}/Z_{L,N}
does not blow up for
n=o(N)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume that
(B1)
and
(B2)
are both satisfied. Then we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{N/L\to\rho}\frac{Z_{L-1,(1-\kappa)N}}{Z_{L,N}}\;\leqslant\;\Big(1-\tfrac{\overline{\kappa}}{1-\rho_{c}/\rho}\Big)^{-1}\,,

for every
\kappa=\kappa(L)=O(1)
such that
\kappa\;\leqslant\;1-\tfrac{\rho_{c}}{\rho}
, where
\overline{\kappa}:=\limsup_{L\to\infty}\kappa(L)
.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, by
(B2)
and Remark
1.8
there exists
J\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{m\,w_{L}(m)}{n\,w_{L}(n)}\;\geqslant\;1-\varepsilon\quad\textrm{for all }m,n\;\geqslant\;J\,,

(37)

for all
L
sufficiently large (depending on
J
and
\varepsilon
).
Let
K=\lceil\kappa N\rceil
, by definition of the canonical measures we have

\displaystyle\pi_{L,N}(\eta_{1}\,;\,\eta_{1}>J)

\displaystyle\;\geqslant\;\pi_{L,N}(\eta_{1}\,;\,\eta_{1}>J+K)=\sum_{n>J+K}^{N}n\,w_{L}(n)\frac{Z_{L-1,N-n}}{Z_{L,N}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n>J}^{N-K}(n+K)\,w_{L}(n+K)\frac{Z_{L-1,N-K-n}}{Z_{L,N}}

\displaystyle\;\geqslant\;\big(1-\varepsilon\big)\sum_{n>J}^{N-K}n\,w_{L}(n)\frac{Z_{L-1,N-K-n}}{Z_{L,N}}\,,

where the last inequality follows from (
37
).
The sums above are all non-empty because
N-K>J
for
L,N
sufficiently large since
\kappa<1
.
Multiplying and dividing by
Z_{L,N-K}
we have

\displaystyle\pi_{L,N}(\eta_{1}\,;\,\eta_{1}>J)

\displaystyle\;\geqslant\;\big(1-\varepsilon\big)\frac{Z_{L,N-K}}{Z_{L,N}}\pi_{L,N-K}(\eta_{1}\,;\,\eta_{1}>J)\,.

(38)

By equivalence of ensembles (Proposition
4.1
), in the thermodynamic limit
N/L\to\rho\;\geqslant\;\rho_{c}
the single site marginals of
\pi_{L,N}
converge weakly to
\nu_{\rho}
which has mean
\rho_{c}
, so

\pi_{L,N}(\eta_{1}\,;\,\eta_{1}>J)=\frac{N}{L}-\pi_{L,N}(\eta_{1}\,;\,\eta_{1}\;\leqslant\;J)\to\rho-\rho_{J}\,,\quad\textrm{as }N/L\to\rho\,,

where
\rho_{J}
is given by
\nu_{\rho}(\eta_{1}\,;\,\eta_{1}\;\leqslant\;J)
.
Furthermore, by dominated convergence,
\rho_{J}\to\rho_{c}
as
J\to\infty
.
Now taking the thermodynamic limit in (
38
), followed by the limit
J\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{N/L\to\rho}\frac{Z_{L,(1-\kappa)N}}{Z_{L,N}}\;\leqslant\;\frac{\rho-\rho_{c}}{(1-\overline{\kappa})\rho-\rho_{c}}\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\,.

From the equivalence of ensembles in Proposition
4.1
, and the identity
\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{1}=0]=w_{L}(0)Z_{L-1,N}/Z_{L,N}
, we observe that
\lim_{N/L\to\rho}Z_{L-1,N}/Z_{L,N}=1
, which completes the proof, since we can choose
\varepsilon
arbitrarily small after taking
J\to\infty
.
∎

We are now ready to state the full proof of Theorem
1.7
which follows by putting together the statements of Theorem
1.4
, Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

The equivalence of ensembles implies the condensation transition with critical density
\rho_{c}
, and with its formulation in Proposition
A.1
, also Assumption
(A2)
is satisfied.
Next, we recover assumptions
(A1)
,
(A3)
and
(A4)
for a fixed choice of
\rho>\rho_{c}
.
Clearly,
(A1)
is a direct implication of
(B1)
, also
(A3)
follows immediately from
(B2)
. This already yields that the subsequential limits of the corresponding measures
\mu_{L,N}
are Poisson-Dirichlet distributions. It is only left to show that the macroscopic phase is non-trivial and indeed agrees with the condensed phase.
Recall from (
32
) that

\displaystyle\mu(\|p\|_{1})=\sigma(\mu)[q_{1}>0]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sigma(\mu)[q_{1}>\varepsilon]

and, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

\displaystyle\sigma(\mu)[q_{1}>\varepsilon]

\displaystyle=\lim_{N/L\rightarrow\rho}\mu_{L,N}[\widetilde{p}_{1}>\varepsilon]

\displaystyle=\lim_{N/L\rightarrow\rho}\pi_{L,N}[\widetilde{\eta}_{1}>\varepsilon N]\to 1-\frac{\rho_{c}}{\rho}\,,

which follows from Proposition
4.2
.
Hence, with (
35
) we conclude
(A4)
with
\alpha=\mu(\|p\|_{1})=1-\tfrac{\rho_{c}}{\rho}
. Altogether, we verified
(A1)
-
(A4)
and so can apply Theorem
1.4
.
∎

5.
Application to interacting particle systems and conclusion

As mentioned in the introduction, condensation transitions occur naturally and have been studied extensively for interacting particle systems, more precisely for stochastic lattice gases which model transport phenomena and conserve the number of particles. A large class of such models with state space
\Omega_{L,N}
has been introduced in
[
7
]
with infinitesimal generator of the form

\mathcal{L}^{(L)}f(\eta)=\sum_{x,y=1}^{L}p(x,y)u(\eta_{x},\eta_{y})\left[f(\eta^{xy})-f(\eta)\right]\,,

(39)

where
f\in C_{b}(\Omega_{L,N})
. Whenever
\eta_{x}>0
,
\eta^{xy}
denotes the configuration
\eta-e^{(x)}+e^{(y)}
where one particle moved from site
x
to site
y
. Recall that
e^{(x)}_{z}=\delta_{x,z}
.
The jump rate
u(n,m)\;\geqslant\;0
is a non-negative function of the occupation numbers
n
on the departure and
m
on the target site of a particle jump, and to avoid degeneracies we assume that
u(n,m)=0
if and only if
n=0
.
p(x,y)
denotes an irreducible probability kernel on
\{1,\ldots,L\}
and models the geometry of the underlying lattice. Systems have been studied, e.g. on regular lattices in various dimensions and with different boundary conditions, here we assume that the system is closed and conserves the total number of particles
\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}
.

For any fixed number of particles
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the operator
\mathcal{L}^{(L)}
defines an irreducible, continuous-time Markov process on the finite state space
\Omega_{L,N}
, which therefore has a unique invariant distribution
\pi_{L,N}
. It has been established in
[
7
,
16
]
that this distribution is indeed of product form and spatially homogeneous, cf. (
1
), under the conditions:

\frac{u(n+1,m)}{u(m+1,n)}=\frac{u(n+1,0)}{u(1,n)}\frac{u(1,m)}{u(m+1,0)}\qquad\forall n,m\;\geqslant\;0\,,

(40)

and at least one of the following

•

p(\cdot,\cdot)
is symmetric,

or

•

p(\cdot,\cdot)
is doubly stochastic, i.e.
\sum_{y}\big(p(x,y)-p(y,x)\big)=0
, and

u(n,m)-u(m,n)=u(n,0)-u(m,0)\qquad\forall n,m\;\geqslant\;0\,.

(41)

Then the stationary weights are

w(n)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{u(1,k-1)}{u(k,0)}\qquad\forall n\;\geqslant\;0\,,

(42)

which depend only on the jump rates
u
but not on the kernel
p
.

For the special case of zero-range dynamics, (
42
) simplifies further since
u(k,\cdot)=u(k)
, and (
40
) and (
41
) are fulfilled. In this case (
42
) leads to the
simple identification between stationary weights and rates

\displaystyle u(n)=\frac{w(n-1)}{w(n)}\qquad\forall n\;\geqslant\;1\,.

(43)

Due to this simple one-to-one correspondence between weights and transition rates, zero-range processes provide a generic framework of studying condensation transitions in interacting particle systems.

Another less restrictive simplification is to assume that
u(n,m)=u_{1}(n)u_{2}(m)
is of product form, which automatically satisfies (
40
) and always leads to factorized stationary measures under symmetric dynamics, i.e.
p(\cdot,\cdot)
is a symmetric transition kernel. The weights from (
42
) now take the form

w(n)=\bigg(\frac{u_{1}(1)}{u_{2}(0)}\bigg)^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{u_{2}(k-1)}{u_{1}(k)}\qquad\forall n\;\geqslant\;0\,.

(44)

Note that due to the conservation law the exponential factor
\Big(\frac{u_{1}(1)}{u_{2}(0)}\Big)^{n}
is usually omitted, since it cancels in the definition of
\pi_{L,N}
(
1
).
One particular example is the inclusion process with rates

u(n,m)=n\,(d+m)\quad\text{with }d>0\,,

(45)

which leads to the stationary weights (
6
).
If we set

d=d(L)>0\quad\text{with}\quad dL\to\theta>0\text{ as }L\to\infty\,,

(46)

the system exhibits a condensation transition with
\rho_{c}=0
and a Poisson-Dirichlet structure with PD
{}_{[0,1]}(\theta)
, see
[
28
, Theorem 1]
or (
7
) above. Theorem
1.7
recovers this result for
\theta\in(0,1]
. The restriction of
\theta
is solely due to the fact that the Poisson-Dirichlet distribution is (so far) only proven to be the unique invariant distribution for the split-merge process if
\theta\in(0,1]
.

Using the size-dependent parameter
d
as above, we will provide a few instructive examples of particle systems with size-dependent jump rates
u_{L}
of product form, where Theorem
1.7
applies with a non-trivial critical density
\rho_{c}>0
.
We start by fixing the weights

w_{L}(n)=\begin{cases}w(n)&\quad\text{ if }n\;\leqslant\;A\,,\\
\frac{d}{n}&\quad\text{ if }n>A\,,\end{cases}\quad\text{for some fixed }A\in\mathbb{N}\,,

(47)

where
d=d(L)
as in (
46
),
\theta\in(0,1]
and
w
is a probability mass function on the set
\{0,\ldots,A\}
. Let us fix for simplicity the uniform distribution with
w(n)=(1+A)^{-1}\mathds{1}\{n\;\leqslant\;A\}
.
Using (
43
), the corresponding rates of a zero-range process are given by
u_{2}(m)=1
and

u_{L}(n)=u_{1,L}(n)=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{ if }1\;\leqslant\;n\;\leqslant\;A\,,\\
d^{-1}\simeq L/\theta&\quad\text{ if }n=A+1\,,\\
\frac{n}{n-1}&\quad\text{ if }n>A+1\,.\end{cases}

(48)

This underlines the mechanism that leads to condensation in such systems: Sites with occupation numbers different from
A+1
are stable and eject particles at rates of order
1
. Sites with occupation number
A+1
are unstable and eject a particle at diverging rate of order
L
, creating a sharp threshold between bulk sites (
\eta_{x}\;\leqslant\;A
) and cluster sites (
\eta_{x}>A+1
). Note that with (
44
) the same effect could be achieved by a vanishing rate of arrival onto sites with occupation number
A
. If in the thermodynamic limit

N/L\to\rho>\rho_{c}=\frac{A}{2}\,,

i.e. the total density exceeds the expectation of the uniform bulk distribution, the system exhibits a condensation transition with mass fraction
1-\rho_{c}/\rho
in the condensate.
Heuristically, the excess mass is expelled from the bulk and accumulates in stable clusters with occupation numbers larger than
A+1
. The particular form of the rates for those clusters leads to a macroscopic phase with Poisson-Dirichlet statistics PD
{}_{[0,1-\rho_{c}/\rho]}(\theta)
, which follows from Theorem
1.7
and is illustrated in Figure
2
. Clearly, the weights
(w_{L})_{L}
converge uniformly to
w
and therefore satisfy both Assumption
(B1)
and
(B2)
. The same process with rates
du_{L}(n)
corresponds to asymptotically vanishing exit rates from stable sites, which is simply a time change and leads of course to the same stationary behaviour.

Figure 2
.
Simulation results at stationarity for a zero-range process with rates (
48
), parameters
\theta=A=\rho=1
and
\rho_{c}=1/2
.
The left boxplot displays
\|p\|_{2}^{2}
where
p=\tfrac{\widehat{\eta}}{N}
was sampled
200
-times for every system size, indicating convergence to
\tfrac{1}{8}
which agrees with
\mu_{L,N}(\|p\|_{2}^{2})\simeq(1+\theta)^{-1}(1-\tfrac{\rho_{c}}{\rho})^{2}
from Lemma
3.6
.
The right plot shows three samples of accumulated configurations in a system of size
N=L=1024
, where we clearly see occurance of large clusters, with a background density of
\rho_{c}=1/2
indicated by dotted lines.

We can also generalize the inclusion process dynamics (
45
) to stationary weights of the form (
47
). Consider a process with rates of product form
u_{L}(n,m)=u_{1,L}(n)u_{2,L}(m)
with

u_{1,L}(n)=u_{2,L}(n)=\begin{cases}d\simeq\theta/L&\quad\text{ if }1\;\leqslant\;n\;\leqslant\;A\,,\\
n&\quad\text{ if }n>A\,,\end{cases}

(49)

and
u_{2,L}(0)=d
.
It is easy to see from (
44
) that the stationary weights for this process are given by (
47
) and Theorem
1.7
applies. Heuristically, sites with occupation number up to
A
eject and attract particles at a slow rate
d
, and particles on sites with higher occupation numbers become “free” and leave independently at the same rate and also attract other particles, as a simple generalization of the standard inclusion interaction.
As a result, the dynamics in the condensed phase happen at a much higher rate than in the bulk. This separation of time scales leads to completely different dynamics than in the zero-range example above, even though both models share the same stationary distributions. We want to stress that due to the general nature of Assumptions
(B1)
and
(B2)
, Theorem
1.7
applies also to modifications of these examples and the particular form of the weights (
47
) is not important.

Understanding the dynamics of the condensed phase in these models is a very interesting question for future research, in particular the coarsening regime, where macroscopic clusters emerge from homogeneous initial conditions and approach stationarity by exchanging particles. Heuristically, their stationary mass partition can be understood as a balance between aggregation and fragmentation of macroscopic clusters.
Note that these dynamics are not described by split-merge processes, which we only use as an auxiliary tool to characterize PD distributions, but are rather of a diffusive nature. A diffusive model on partitions that has stationary PD distribution has been introduced in
[
11
]
, and it would be very interesting to study hydrodynamic scaling limits in this context.

As we have seen, the Poisson-Dirichlet structure in the macroscopic phase arises due to uniform stationary weights under size-biased sampling, which leads to particular rates in the zero-range process (
48
) or the generalized inclusion process (
49
) for large occupation numbers. In the context of the dynamics of interacting particle systems this is only one particular case, and it would be interesting to study the statistics of the condensed phase beyond Poisson-Dirichlet under a different scaling behaviour of the weights.

Appendix A
Equivalence of ensembles

In this section we prove that, under assumption
(B1)
and if the weights
(w_{L})_{L}
decay sub exponentially, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\log w_{L}(aL)\to 0\,,\quad\forall a>0\,,\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{2}w(n)<\infty\,,

(50)

we have equivalence of ensembles and condensation in the sense of weak convergence of finite dimensional marginals.
Note that this includes condensation in the sense of Definition
1.1
, and (
50
) is weaker than Assumption
(B2)
in Theorem
1.7
.
The result is in the same spirit as previous results on equivalence of ensembles and condensation in stochastic particle systems with stationary product measures (see for example
[
5
]
).
However, as far as we know, this is the first general result in this direction for models with size-dependent weights.

In order to state the result in more generality we first introduce some extra notation.
For a sequence of non-negative, non-trivial weights,
(w_{L}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, possibly depending on the system size
L
, we define a family of probability measures on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
by tilting the weights by a non-negative
fugacity
parameter
\phi\;\geqslant\;0
:

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}_{\phi,L}[dn]:=\frac{1}{z_{L}(\phi)}w_{L}(n)\phi^{n}dn\quad\mbox{with}\quad z_{L}(\phi):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w_{L}(n)\phi^{n}\,,

which is well defined for each
\phi\in D_{L}:=\{\phi\,:\,z_{L}(\phi)<\infty\}
.
The corresponding family of
grand-canonical distributions
is given by the product measures

\bar{\nu}_{\phi,L}^{\otimes L}[d\eta]=\frac{1}{z_{L}(\phi)^{L}}\prod_{x=1}^{L}w_{L}(\eta_{x})\phi^{\eta_{x}}d\eta\,,

(51)

which are defined on the configuration space
\Omega_{L}=\mathbb{N}_{0}^{L}=\bigcup_{N=0}^{\infty}\Omega_{L,N}
, where the total number of particles is arbitrary.
The expected number of particles per site (density)
R_{L}:D_{L}\to[0,\infty)
is a strictly increasing function of
\phi
,
with

\displaystyle R_{L}(\phi):=\bar{\nu}_{\phi,L}(\eta_{x})=\phi\,\partial_{\phi}\log z_{L}(\phi)\,,\quad\textrm{for }\phi\in D_{L}\,.

(52)

We denote the inverse of
R_{L}
by
\Phi_{L}
.
Furthermore, we define the variance of
\bar{\nu}_{\phi,L}

\displaystyle\sigma_{L}^{2}(\phi)=\bar{\nu}_{\phi,L}(\eta_{x}^{2})-R_{L}(\phi)^{2}\,,

which is finite for each
\phi
in the interior of
D_{L}
.
By construction, the canonical measures (
1
) on
\Omega_{L,N}
are given by conditioning any grand-canonical measure on the total number of particles, i.e.

\pi_{L,N}[d\eta]:=\bar{\nu}_{\phi,L}^{\otimes L}\left[d\eta\;\middle|\;\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}=N\right]=\frac{1}{Z_{L,N}}\prod_{x=1}^{L}w_{L}(\eta_{x})d\eta\,,

which is independent of
\phi\in D_{L}
.

By Assumption
(B1)
, the weights
(w_{L}(n))_{n,L}
converge uniformly in
n
as
L\to\infty
to a probability measure
(w(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
. We define the limiting grand-canonical measures by

\bar{\nu}_{\phi}[dn]:=\frac{1}{z(\phi)}w(n)\phi^{n}dn\quad\mbox{with}\quad z(\phi):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w(n)\phi^{n}\ .

Since
(w(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is normalised, these measures must exist at least for each
\phi\in[0,1]
, and
\bar{\nu}_{1}
corresponds to the weights
w
.
By analogy with (
52
), we define the function
R(\phi)=\bar{\nu}_{\phi}(\eta_{1})
, which is a strictly increasing function
R\colon[0,1]\to[0,\rho_{c}]
with

\rho_{c}=R(1)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}nw(n)\,,\quad\mbox{as given in Theorem \ref{THEO_SPECIALISED_MAIN}}\ .

We denote the inverse of
R
by
\Phi
, so that the average particle density under
\bar{\nu}^{\otimes{L}}_{\Phi(\rho)}
is
\rho
for all
\rho\;\leqslant\;\rho_{c}
.
Further, we denote the variance of
\bar{\nu}_{\phi}
by
\sigma^{2}(\phi)
which is finite for
\phi\in[0,1]
by the second moment condition in (
50
). Note that it may be possible that
R(\phi)<\infty
and
\bar{\nu}_{\phi}
is well defined also for
\phi>1
, but such measures are not accessible as limits of
\bar{\nu}_{\phi,L}
and do not play a role in the following.

Under Assumption
(B1)
and sub-exponential weights (
50
), it turns out that there is a condensation transition according to Definition
1.1
with critical density
\rho_{c}=R(1)
.
In this case, in the thermodynamic limit
N/L\rightarrow\rho
, all finite dimensional marginals of the canonical measures converge weakly to the limiting grand-canonical measures with density
\rho
if
\rho\;\leqslant\;\rho_{c}
, and with density
\rho_{c}
if
\rho\;\geqslant\;\rho_{c}
.
This implies that for
\rho>\rho_{c}
the excess mass must condense on a vanishing volume fraction in the thermodynamic limit.

Proposition A.1

(Equivalence of ensembles)
.

Consider non-negative weights
(w_{L})_{L}
satisfying
(B1)
with limit
w
. Furthermore, assume that
w_{L}
’s have sub-exponential tails, in the sense of (

Then for each
M\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with
|M|=m<\infty
, denoting the marginal of
\pi_{L,N}
on
M
by
\pi_{L,N}^{M}
, we have

\displaystyle\pi_{L,N}^{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\begin{cases}\bar{\nu}_{\Phi(\rho)}^{\otimes m}&\quad\text{ if }\rho<\rho_{c}\,,\\
\bar{\nu}_{1}^{\otimes m}&\quad\text{ if }\rho\;\geqslant\;\rho_{c}\,,\end{cases}

where
\rho_{c}=R(1)<\infty

Throughout the proof we assume further that condition (
11

\displaystyle w(0)>0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sup_{n}[w(n-1)\wedge w(n)]>0\,,

which implies that the variance,
\sigma^{2}(\phi)

\sum n^{2}w(n)\phi^{n}
, is positive for each
\phi\in(0,1]
.
The special case of
w(0)=1
is covered at the end of the proof.

We firstly observe that for each
\rho\in[0,\rho_{c}]

we can construct a sequence of size-dependent fugacities such that the mean of the size-dependent grand-canonical measures converges to
\rho
and the variance remains bounded.

R_{L}(\phi)

Lemma A.2
.

Under Assumption
(B1)
and if the weights
(w_{L})_{L}
are sub-exponential in the sense of (
50
), then for each
\varphi\in[0,1]
there exists a sequence
(\varphi_{L})_{L}
in
[0,1)
, with limit point
\varphi
, such that

\displaystyle\|w_{L}-w\|_{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-\varphi_{L})^{3}}\to 0\,,\quad\text{ as }L\to\infty\,,

(53)

and

\displaystyle R_{L}(\varphi_{L})\to R(\varphi)\in[0,\rho_{c}]\,,\quad\sigma^{2}_{L}(\varphi_{L})\to\sigma^{2}(\varphi)>0\,,\ \text{ and }\ z_{L}(\varphi_{L})\to z(\varphi)\,.

(54)

Proof.

For
\varphi\in[0,1)
we may choose
\varphi_{L}=\varphi
for each
L
.
For
\varphi=1
, we let
\varphi_{L}=1-\|w_{L}-w\|_{\infty}^{1/4}
.
Then
\varphi_{L}\to 1
as
L\to\infty
and condition (
53
) is satisfied since
\|w_{L}-w\|_{\infty}\to 0
by assumption
(B1)
.

It is only left to prove the convergence in (
54
), which is equivalent to showing that

\lim_{L\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{p}w_{L}(n)\varphi_{L}^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{p}w(n)\varphi^{n}\,,

for
p\in\{0,1,2\}
.
Since the weights converge uniformly, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{p}(w_{L}(n)-w(n))\varphi_{L}^{n}\bigg|\;\leqslant\;\|w_{L}(n)-w(n)\|_{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{p}\varphi_{L}^{n}\,.

Term-by-term differentiation of the geometric series yields for
p\in\{0,1,2\}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{p}\varphi_{L}^{n}=\frac{(p\,\vee 1)\varphi_{L}^{p}}{(1-\varphi_{L})^{p+1}}+\frac{\varphi_{L}}{(1-\varphi_{L})^{2}}\mathds{1}\{p=2\}\,.

Hence, using again (
53
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{p}w_{L}(n)\varphi_{L}^{n}\simeq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{p}w(n)\varphi_{L}^{n}\to\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{p}w(n)\varphi^{n}\,,

where we used dominated convergence in the last step.
∎

We will prove Proposition
A.1
by showing that the relative entropy between the single site marginal of
\pi_{L,N}
and
\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}
vanishes, where the limit density
R(\varphi)
is equal to
\rho
in the sub-critical case, and
\rho_{c}
in the super critical case.
Optimally, in the super-critical case, we would like to measure the relative entropy w.r.t. the limiting measure directly, however this is not possible since
\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x}\in\cdot\,]\ll w
is in general not satisfied and the relative entropy would be infinite.

The main tool we rely on in the proof of Proposition
A.1
is a local central limit theorem (see for example
[
10
, Theorem 1.2]
) which allows us to estimate the decay of the relative entropy.
For completeness we include the local limit theorem here. To state it, we first introduce the Bernoulli part decomposition
q
of a probability measure
\mathds{P}_{x,L}
on
{\mathbb{Z}}
as

\displaystyle q(\mathds{P}_{x,L}):=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathds{P}_{x,L}[n]\wedge\mathds{P}_{x,L}[n+1])\,.

Moreover, for a family of measures
(\mathds{P}_{x,L})_{1\;\leqslant\;x\;\leqslant\;L}
we define
Q_{L}:=\sum_{x=1}^{L}q(\mathds{P}_{x,L})
.

Lemma A.3

(
[
10
, Theorem 1.2]
Local central limit theorem)
.

Consider a triangular array of independent integer valued random variables
\eta_{x,L}
, for
1\;\leqslant\;x\;\leqslant\;L
, and
L\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\eta_{x,L}
has law
\mathds{P}_{x,L}
. Suppose there exist sequences
a_{L}
and
b_{L}
,
L\;\geqslant\;1
, such that

b_{L}\to\infty
,
\limsup_{L\to\infty}b_{L}^{2}/Q_{L}<\infty

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b_{L}}\sum_{x=1}^{L}(\eta_{x,L}-a_{L})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\,.

(55)

Then

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|b_{L}\,\mathds{P}_{L}\Big[\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x,L}=n\Big]-g\Big(\frac{n-a_{L}}{b_{L}}\Big)\right|\to 0\,,

where
\mathds{P}_{L}
denotes the product measure
\bigotimes_{x=1}^{L}\mathds{P}_{x,L}
and
g
the density of a standard normal.

To apply Lemma
A.3
we consider
independent random variables

\displaystyle(\eta_{x,L})_{1\;\leqslant\;x\;\leqslant\;L}\quad\text{ with law }\quad\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}^{\otimes L}\quad\mbox{for each }L\;\geqslant\;1\,,

(56)

where
(\varphi_{L})_{L\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence in
[0,1)
satisfying (
53
).
To apply Lemma
A.3
in the proof of Proposition
A.1
, we first verify the central limit theorem (
55
) for the
\eta_{x,L}
’s.

Lemma A.4
.

Consider
(\varphi_{L})_{L}
to be a sequence in
[0,1)
with limit point
\varphi\in[0,1]
satisfying (
53
) and (
54
).
Furthermore, let
(\eta_{x,L})_{1\;\leqslant\;x\;\leqslant\;L,L\in\mathbb{N}}
be as in (
56
) and define

\zeta_{x,L}:=\frac{\eta_{x,L}-R_{L}(\varphi_{L})}{\sqrt{L\,\sigma_{L}^{2}(\varphi_{L})}}\,.

Then
\sum_{x=1}^{L}\zeta_{x,L}
converges weakly to a standard normal, as
L
tends to infinity.

Proof.

We want to apply the Lindeberg-Feller central limit theorem, see
[
30
, Theorem 5.12]
: because the
\zeta_{x,L}
’s are centered and normalised, it suffices confirm that the following Lindeberg condition holds:

\textrm{For every $\varepsilon>0$, }\lim_{L\to\infty}\sum_{x=1}^{L}\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}(\zeta_{x,L}^{2}\mathds{1}\{|\zeta_{x,L}|>\varepsilon\})=0\,.

Since
R_{L}(\varphi_{L})
and
\sigma^{2}_{L}(\varphi_{L})
converge to positive numbers, we have that for
L
large enough

\displaystyle\sum_{x=1}^{L}\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}(\zeta_{x,L}^{2}\mathds{1}\{|\zeta_{x,L}|>\varepsilon\})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z_{L}(\varphi_{L})\,\sigma^{2}_{L}(\varphi_{L})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n-R_{L}(\varphi_{L}))^{2}w_{L}(n)\varphi_{L}^{n}\mathds{1}\Big\{|n-R_{L}(\varphi_{L})|>\varepsilon\sqrt{L\,\sigma^{2}_{L}(\varphi_{L})}\Big\}

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{z_{L}(\varphi_{L})\,\sigma^{2}_{L}(\varphi_{L})}\sum_{n=K_{\varepsilon,L}}^{\infty}n^{2}w_{L}(n)\varphi_{L}^{n}\,,

where
K_{\varepsilon,L}=\varepsilon\sqrt{L\,\sigma^{2}_{L}(\varphi_{L})}+R_{L}(\varphi_{L})
, which diverges like
\sqrt{L}
.
The denominator in the above expression converges to a positive constant.
For the numerator, we observe

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{n=K_{\varepsilon,L}}^{\infty}n^{2}(w_{L}(n)-w(n))\varphi_{L}^{n}\bigg|\;\leqslant\;\|w_{L}(n)-w(n)\|_{\infty}\sum_{n=K_{\varepsilon,L}}^{\infty}n^{2}\varphi_{L}^{n}\to 0\,,

(57)

where we used property (
53
) of the sequence
(\varphi_{L})_{L}
.
Thus, using the second-moment assumption on
w
in (
50
), we have, for each
\epsilon

\displaystyle\sum_{n=K_{\varepsilon,L}}^{\infty}n^{2}w_{L}(n)\varphi_{L}^{n}\simeq\sum_{n=K_{\varepsilon,L}}^{\infty}n^{2}w(n)\varphi_{L}^{n}\;\leqslant\;\sum_{n=K_{\varepsilon,L}}^{\infty}n^{2}w(n)\to 0\,,

as
L\to\infty
, since
K_{\epsilon,L}\to\infty
.
This concludes the Lindeberg condition.
∎

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

We first consider the sub-critical and critical case together, fix
\rho\in(0,\rho_{c}]
.
Let
(\varphi_{L})_{L}
be a sequence converging to
\varphi:=\Phi(\rho)\in(0,1]
satisfying (
53
) and (
54
).
We will measure the relative entropy between single-site marginals of
\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x}\in\cdot\,]
and
\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}
.

We start with an expression for the relative entropy between
\pi_{L,N}
and
\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}^{\otimes L}
which is used frequently in the proof of similar equivalence of ensembles results (see for example
[
5
]
),

\displaystyle\begin{split}H\left(\pi_{L,N}\mid\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}^{\otimes L}\right)&=\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{L,N}}\pi_{L,N}[\eta]\log\left(\frac{\pi_{L,N}[\eta]}{\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}^{\otimes L}[\eta]}\right)=\log\left(\frac{z_{L}(\varphi_{L})^{L}}{Z_{L,N}\varphi_{L}^{N}}\right)\\
&=-\log\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}^{\otimes L}\left[\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}=N\right]\,.\end{split}

Then, by subadditivity of the relative entropy we have for marginals

H\left(\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x}\in\cdot\,]\mid\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}\right)\;\leqslant\;-\frac{1}{L}\log\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}^{\otimes L}\left[\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}=N\right]\,.

(58)

We estimate the right-hand side using the local limit theorem in Lemma
A.3
with the specific choices of

\displaystyle a_{L}:=L\,R_{L}(\varphi_{L})\quad\text{and}\quad b_{L}:=\sqrt{L\,\sigma_{L}^{2}(\varphi_{L})}\,.

It follows from Lemma
A.4
that

\sum_{x=1}^{L}\zeta_{x,L}=\frac{\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x,L}-a_{L}}{b_{L}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\,.

Moreover,
b_{L}
diverges in the large
L
limit because
\lim_{L\to\infty}\sigma_{L}^{2}(\varphi_{L})=\sigma^{2}(\varphi)>0
.
Also,

\displaystyle\limsup_{L\to\infty}\frac{b_{L}^{2}}{Q_{L}}=\limsup_{L\to\infty}\frac{\sigma_{L}^{2}(\varphi_{L})}{q(\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L})}\;\leqslant\;\frac{\sigma^{2}(\varphi)}{\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}[w(n)\wedge w(n+1)]}\,,

where the final inequality follows by dominated convergence.
The right hand side is finite by assumption
(B1)
.
Therefore, we may apply the local limit theorem stated in Lemma
A.3
which yields

\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}^{\otimes L}\left[\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}=N\right]=O\left(L^{-1/2}\right)\,,

and coming back to (
58
)

H\left(\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x}\in\cdot\,]\mid\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}\right)\sim\frac{\log L}{L}\to 0\quad\mbox{as }L\to\infty\,.

With Pinsker’s inequality (see e.g.
[
22
, Lemma 6.2]
)
this implies for the total variation distance

\displaystyle d_{TV}\left(\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x}\in\cdot\,],\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}\right)\to 0\,.

(59)

Also, by (
54
) and uniform convergence of the weights,
\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}
converges weakly to
\bar{\nu}_{\varphi}
, which together with (
59
) implies

\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x}\in\cdot\,]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\bar{\nu}_{\Phi(\rho)}

as
N/L\to\rho\;\leqslant\;\rho_{c}
.

Finally, we conclude the super-critical case
\rho>\rho_{c}
using a large deviation estimate. Now let
\varphi_{L}
be a sequence converging to
1
and satisfying (
53
) and (
54
) so that
R_{L}(\varphi_{L})\to\rho_{c}
.
Then

\begin{split}-\frac{1}{L}\log\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}^{\otimes L}\left[\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}=N\right]&\;\leqslant\;-\frac{1}{L}\log\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}^{\otimes L\setminus\{1\}}\left[\sum_{x=2}^{L}\eta_{x}=N-\lfloor(\rho-\rho_{c})L\rfloor\right]\\
&\qquad-\frac{1}{L}\log\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}\left[\eta_{1}=\lfloor(\rho-\rho_{c})L\rfloor\right]\,,\end{split}

where the first term on the r.h.s. converges to zero by the local central limit theorem, since
(N-\lfloor(\rho-\rho_{c})L\rfloor)/L\to\rho_{c}
.
For the second term,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{L}\log\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}\left[\eta_{1}=\lfloor(\rho-\rho_{c})L\rfloor\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{1}{L}\log w_{L}(\lfloor(\rho-\rho_{c})L\rfloor)-(\rho-\rho_{c})\log\varphi_{L}+\frac{1}{L}\log z_{L}(\varphi_{L})\to 0\,,

where convergence follows from the sub-exponential assumption (
50
), and since
\varphi_{L}\to 1
and
z_{L}(\varphi_{L})\to z(1)\in(0,\infty)
.
It follows that
H\left(\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x}\in\cdot\,]\mid\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}\right)
vanishes and, for the same reason as in the sub-critical case,
\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x}\in\cdot\,]
converges weakly to
w=\bar{\nu}_{1}
.

Finally, to establish weak convergence of finite dimensional marginals; fix
n_{1},\ldots,n_{m}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
distinct indices, then

\displaystyle\pi_{L,N}

\displaystyle[\eta_{x_{1}}=n_{1},\eta_{x_{2}}=n_{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{L}(n_{1})Z_{L-1,N-n_{1}}}{Z_{L,N}}\frac{1}{Z_{L-1,N-n_{1}}}w_{L}(n_{2})\sum_{\xi\in\Omega_{L-2,N-n_{1}-n_{2}}}\left(\prod_{z}w_{L}(\xi_{z})\right)

\displaystyle=\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x_{1}}=n_{1}]\,\pi_{L-1,N-n_{1}}[\eta_{x_{2}}=n_{2}]\,.

This identity immediately generalises to

\displaystyle\pi_{L,N}[\eta_{x_{1}}=n_{1},\ldots,\eta_{x_{m}}=n_{m}]=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{L-j+1,N-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}n_{j}}[\eta_{x_{j}}=n_{j}]\,,

which, by taking the thermodynamic limit on the right hand side, completes the proof.

It is possible to drop the assumption that the variance of the limiting weights is positive in the case
w(n)=\mathds{1}\{n=0\}
.
If we fix
\rho>0
, we can use the same argument as in the super-critical case above and put all particles on a single site.
In this case the right hand side of (
58
) vanishes since

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{L}\log\bar{\nu}_{\varphi_{L},L}^{\otimes L}\left[\sum_{x=1}^{L}\eta_{x}=0\right]=-\log\nu_{\varphi_{L},L}\left[\eta_{x}=0\right]\to\log w(0)=0\,,

as
L\to\infty
. In this case we do not use the local central limit theorem.
Otherwise, the proof remains unchanged.
∎
