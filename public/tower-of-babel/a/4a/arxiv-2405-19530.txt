Causal fermion states in magnetic field in a relativistic rotating frame and electromagnetic radiation by a rapidly rotating charge
Matteo Buzzegoli, Kirill Tuchin

Abstract
We consider the Dirac field uniformly rotating with angular velocity Ω \Omega and also subject to the constant magnetic field 𝑩 {\bm{B}} directed along the rotation axis. The causal states are constrained to the interior of the light cylinder of radius c / Ω c/\Omega . When this radius is smaller than the system size, as in the quark-gluon plasma, the effect of the boundary on the fermion spectrum is critical. We derive the fermion spectrum and study its properties. We compute the intensity of the electromagnetic radiation emitted due to transitions between the fermion states. We study its dependence on energy and angular momentum for different values of the angular velocity and the magnetic field. Rotation has enormous impact on the electromagnetic radiation by the quark-gluon plasma with or without the magnetic field.

II
General solution to Dirac equation in magnetic field in rotating frame

It is instructive to begin with a brief review of the Dirac equation in the frame rotating with the constant angular velocity
{\bm{\Omega}}=\Omega\hat{{\bm{z}}}
. The constant magnetic field
{\bm{B}}=B\hat{{\bm{z}}}
is conveniently described in the symmetric gauge
A^{\mu}=(0,-By/2,Bx/2,0)
. The Dirac Hamiltonian
H
in the rotating frame is obtained from that in the stationary frame by adding the operator
-\Omega J_{z}
, where
J_{z}=-i\partial_{\phi}+\frac{i}{2}\gamma^{x}\gamma^{y}
is the angular momentum projection on the rotating axis:

\displaystyle H=\gamma^{0}{\bm{\gamma}}\cdot({\bm{p}}-q{\bm{A}})+\gamma^{0}M-\Omega J_{z}\,.

(1)

In view of the axial symmetry, we employ cylindrical coordinates
r
,
\phi
,
z
. In place of the radial coordinate
r
it will be advantageous to use a dimensionless variable
\rho=\frac{|qB|}{2}r^{2}
. The light-cylinder is located at
\rho=\rho_{R}
, where

\rho_{R}=\frac{|qB|R^{2}}{2}=\frac{|qB|}{2\Omega^{2}}.

(2)

We are seeking a solution to the Dirac equation in the standard representation in the form

\psi(t,r,\phi,z)={\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\epsilon Et}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}p_{z}z}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}m\phi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{1}(\rho){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
f_{2}(\rho){\rm e}^{+{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
f_{3}(\rho){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
f_{4}(\rho){\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi/2}\end{array}\right)\,,

(3)

where
p_{z}
is the momentum component along the rotation axis,
\epsilon=+/-
labels
particle/antiparticle wave functions, integer
m
is the magnetic quantum number
and the radial functions
f_{s}(\rho)
,
s=1,\ldots,4
must satisfy the boundary condition that we specify in the next section.
It is convenient to define the operators
[
43
]
:

\displaystyle R_{1}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{|qB|}{2}\rho}\left(2\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\rho}+\bar{\sigma}-\frac{m-\tfrac{1}{2}}{\rho}\right),\quad R_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{|qB|}{2}\rho}\left(2\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\rho}-\bar{\sigma}+\frac{m+\tfrac{1}{2}}{\rho}\right),

(4)

where
\bar{\sigma}=\text{sgn}\left(qB\right)
.
Substituting (
3
) into the Dirac equation and using (
4
) one finds the equations for the radial functions:

R_{1}R_{2}f_{2,4}(\rho)=-2|qB|\lambda f_{2,4}(\rho),\quad R_{2}R_{1}f_{1,3}(\rho)=-2|qB|\lambda f_{1,3}(\rho),

(5)

and the dispersion relation

2|qB|\lambda=(\epsilon E-\Omega\,m)^{2}-M^{2}-p_{z}^{2}\,,

(6)

where
M
is the fermion mass and the real parameter
\lambda
is the principal quantum number.

The solutions of (
5
), finite at the origin, are

\displaystyle f_{1,3}^{\bar{\sigma}}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{|qB|}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1,3}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho),\quad f_{2,4}^{\bar{\sigma}}=\sqrt{|qB|}{\rm i}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2,4}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho),

(7)

where
F_{1,2}
are given in terms of the confluent hypergeometric function:

\displaystyle F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{1}}{\Gamma(m+\frac{1}{2})}\,{\rm e}^{-\rho/2}\rho^{\frac{m-\tfrac{1}{2}}{2}}\,_{1}F_{1}\left(-\left(\lambda-\frac{1-\bar{\sigma}}{2}m-\frac{1-\bar{\sigma}}{2}\frac{1}{2}\right);\,m+\frac{1}{2};\,\rho\right),

(8a)

\displaystyle F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{2}}{\Gamma(m+\frac{3}{2})}\,{\rm e}^{-\rho/2}\rho^{\frac{m+\tfrac{1}{2}}{2}}\,_{1}F_{1}\left(-\left(\lambda-\frac{1-\bar{\sigma}}{2}m-\frac{3+\bar{\sigma}}{4}\right);\,m+\frac{3}{2};\,\rho\right),

(8b)

and
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
are the normalization constants such that

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\rho_{R}}{\rm d}\rho\,\left[F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho)\right]^{2}=&1,\quad\int_{0}^{\rho_{R}}{\rm d}\rho\,\left[F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho)\right]^{2}=1.\end{split}

(9)

In general,
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
depend on
m,\,\lambda,\,\bar{\sigma}
and
R
.
The gamma factors in (
8
) ensures that the functions are regular and real for every value of
m
and
\rho

[
44
]
.
Using (
4
) and (
8
) we can also show that

R_{1}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho)=-\sqrt{2|qB|}\lambda\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho),\quad R_{2}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho)=+\sqrt{2|qB|}\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho).

(10)

Employing these formulas it is straightforward to demonstrate that
F_{1,2}
solve
the Eqs. (
5
).
In summary, the general form of solutions to the Dirac equation
in the magnetic field in rotating frame is

\psi^{\rho_{R}}(t,r,\phi,z)={\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\epsilon Et}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}p_{z}z}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}m\phi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{|qB|}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
{\rm i}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho){\rm e}^{+{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{3}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
{\rm i}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{4}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho){\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi/2}\end{array}\right),

(11)

with
F_{1,2}^{\bar{\sigma}}
given by (
8
).
C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{s}
,
s=1,\ldots,4
are constant coefficients.

It is remarkable that eigenfunctions (
11
) and their derivatives are continuous across the light cylinder
r=1/\Omega
. This is a general feature of motion in the rotating frame. For example, a free classical particle in the rotating frame spirals out to infinity oblivious of the light cylinder. Of course, the very definition of the rigidly rotating frame breaks down at the light cylinder. In Sec.
IV
we cut off the physically meaningless region by imposing an appropriate boundary condition.

II.1
Transverse polarization states

The constants
C_{s}
are not completely fixed by the Dirac equation, which only requires
that the constants satisfy the algebraic system

\displaystyle(\varepsilon E-\Omega\,m\mp M)C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1,3}-\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}\sqrt{2|qB|}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{4,2}-p_{z}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{3,1}=

\displaystyle 0,

(12a)

\displaystyle(\varepsilon E-\Omega\,m\mp M)C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2,4}-\lambda\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}\sqrt{2|qB|}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{3,1}+p_{z}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{4,2}=

\displaystyle 0.

(12b)

Furthermore, imposing the normalization of the wave function function as

\int\psi^{\dagger}\psi{\rm d}^{3}x=1,

(13)

we find the constraint

\sum_{s=1}^{4}C^{\bar{\sigma}\,*}_{s}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{s}=1.

(14)

To fix the constants we can require the fermions to be in a certain polarization state so that the wave functions (
11
) are
the corresponding eigenfunctions. It is also convenient to choose a polarization
operator which commutes with the Hamiltonian. As in the case of unbounded solutions, i.e. when
R\to\infty
, a
commuting polarization operator is given by the spin magnetic moment

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{\mu}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{\mu}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{\mu}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{\mu}$}}=\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{\Sigma}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{\Sigma}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{\Sigma}$}}-\frac{{\rm i}\gamma_{0}\gamma_{5}}{2}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{\Sigma}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{\Sigma}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{\Sigma}$}}\times(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{p}$}}-q\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{A}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{A}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{A}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{A}$}})\,.

(15)

The spin magnetic moment component along the direction of the magnetic spin is a constant of motion.
Eigenstates of this operator are said to have the transverse polarization
and are obtained by solving the eigenvalue problem

\widehat{\mu}_{z}\,\psi=\zeta\,\sqrt{(E-\Omega\,m)^{2}-p_{z}^{2}}\;\psi,

(16)

with
\zeta=\pm 1
. This is equivalent to the set of equations for the constants
C_{s}

\begin{split}\left[(\varepsilon E-\Omega\,m)\mp\zeta\bar{E}\right]C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1,3}=&p_{z}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{3,1},\\
\left[(\varepsilon E-\Omega\,m)\pm\zeta\bar{E}\right]C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2,4}=&-p_{z}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2,4},\end{split}

(17)

where
\bar{E}=\sqrt{(\varepsilon E-\Omega\,m)^{2}-p_{z}^{2}}
. The solution of (
17
) can be written in the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}\\
C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}\\
C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{3}\\
C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{4}\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}B_{3}(A_{3}+A_{4})\\
B_{4}(A_{4}-A_{3})\\
B_{3}(A_{3}-A_{4})\\
B_{4}(A_{4}+A_{3})\end{array}\right)

(18)

such that

\frac{A_{3}+A_{4}}{A_{3}-A_{4}}=\frac{p_{z}}{(\varepsilon E-\Omega\,m)-\zeta\bar{E}}.

(19)

The coefficients are now obtained by solving Eqs. (
12
) and imposing the normalization condition (
14
).
From (
12
), and reminding that
\lambda
is non-negative, see Eq. (
6
), we obtain

\begin{split}\frac{B_{4}}{B_{3}}=&\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}N_{1}}{N_{2}}\zeta\sqrt{\frac{\bar{E}-\zeta M}{\bar{E}+\zeta M}}=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}N_{1}}{N_{2}}\zeta\frac{\sqrt{2|qB|\lambda}}{|\bar{E}+\zeta M|}.\end{split}

(20)

Imposing (
14
) the coefficients read:

C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1,3}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}B_{+}(A_{+}\pm\zeta A_{-}),\quad C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2,4}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}N_{1}}{N_{2}}B_{-}(A_{-}\mp\zeta A_{+}),

(21)

where

\begin{split}A_{\pm}=&\left(\frac{E-\Omega\,m\pm p_{z}}{E-\Omega\,m}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\\
B_{+}=&\left(\frac{2(\bar{E}+\zeta M)}{(1+\frac{\lambda N_{1}^{2}}{N_{2}^{2}})\bar{E}+(1-\frac{\lambda N_{1}^{2}}{N_{2}^{2}})\zeta M}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\\
B_{-}=&\left(\frac{2(\bar{E}-\zeta M)}{(1+\frac{\lambda N_{1}^{2}}{N_{2}^{2}})\bar{E}+(1-\frac{\lambda N_{1}^{2}}{N_{2}^{2}})\zeta M}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

(22)

The upper signs in (
21
) refer to
s=1,\,2
and the lower ones to
s=3,\,4
.

III
Solutions to Dirac equation with boundary condition at
R\to\infty

As we mentioned in the Introduction, when the angular velocity of rotation is so small that
\Omega\ll\sqrt{|qB|}
one can ignore the exponentially small value of the wave function at
r=R
and consider the light cylinder as located infinitely far from the rotation axis
r\to\infty
. We refer to this boundary condition and its results as the unbounded case. The boundary condition then requires the vanishing of all components of
\psi
at
\rho\to\infty
. This can only be realized if the radial quantum number

\displaystyle a=n+\bar{\sigma}\,m-\frac{1}{2}\,

(23)

is a non-negative integer
[
43
,
44
]
. We denoted the principal quantum number
\lambda=n=0,1,\ldots
to emphasize that it must be a non-negative integer.
At these values of the radial quantum quantum number, the hypergeometric functions (
8
) reduce, up to a phase factor, to the Laguerre functions

\displaystyle I_{n,a}(\rho)=\sqrt{\frac{a!}{n!}}{\rm e}^{-\rho/2}\rho^{\tfrac{n-a}{2}}L_{a}^{n-a}(\rho).

(24)

In particular, by multiplying the wave function (
11
) by an appropriate phase factor, we find from the definitions (
8
)

\lim_{R\to\infty}F_{1}=-\bar{\sigma}I^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho),\quad\lim_{R\to\infty}F_{2}=I^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho),

(25)

where

\displaystyle I^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho)=I_{n-\frac{1-\bar{\sigma}}{2},a}(\rho),\quad I^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho)=I_{n-\frac{1+\bar{\sigma}}{2},a}(\rho).

(26)

We now show that this solution to the Dirac equation in constant magnetic field in stationary frame is the same as the one obtained in
[
43
]
. For that purpose we move the phase
-\bar{\sigma}
from
F_{1}
in (
25
) to a newly defined coefficients
\tilde{C}_{1,3}^{\bar{\sigma}}=-\bar{\sigma}C_{1,3}^{\bar{\sigma}}
, such that the eigenfunctions of the Dirac equation read

\psi^{\infty}_{n,\,a,\,p_{z},\,\zeta}(t,r,\theta,\phi)={\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\epsilon Et}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}p_{z}z}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}m\phi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{|qB|}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{C}^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}I^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
{\rm i}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}I^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho){\rm e}^{+{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
\tilde{C}^{\bar{\sigma}}_{3}I^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
{\rm i}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{4}I^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho){\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi/2}\end{array}\right),\quad n\geq 0,\;a\geq 0,

(27)

which are in the same form as in
[
43
]
.
The corresponding energy spectrum is

E=\epsilon\sqrt{p_{z}^{2}+2n|qB|+M^{2}}+m\Omega.

It can be shown comparing Eq. (
25
) with (
8
) that in the infinitely large cylinder the normalization constants satisfy
nN_{1}^{2}/N_{2}^{2}=1
, taking advantage of that and using the coefficients of transverse polarization
(
21
) and (
22
) we obtain:

\tilde{C}^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1,3}=-\frac{\bar{\sigma}}{2\sqrt{2}}B_{+}(A_{+}\pm\zeta A_{-}),\quad C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2,4}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}B_{-}(A_{-}\mp\zeta A_{+}),

(28)

where

\displaystyle A_{\pm}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{E-\Omega\,m\pm p_{z}}{E-\Omega\,m}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(29a)

\displaystyle B_{\pm}=

\displaystyle\left(1\pm\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{(E-\Omega\,m)^{2}-p_{z}^{2}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(29b)

in agreement with the solution of
[
43
]
.

It is instructive to study how the unbounded modes are
approached as we increase the radius of the cylinder
R
. For the sake of clarity, we fix

\bar{\sigma}=+1
; the same analysis can be done for
\bar{\sigma}=-1
. In order to have a
wave function that vanish at infinity, we must choose
\lambda
and
a
as non-negative
integers. However, in a finite cylinder, solutions of the Dirac equation can also have
modes with
negative
a
. To study what happens to these modes
we evaluate the hypergeometric functions (
8
) for positive and negative integer values of
\lambda=n
and
a
; we find

\left.\frac{F_{1}^{+}(\rho)}{N_{1}}\right|_{\lambda=n}=\begin{cases}\sim{\rm e}^{-\rho/2}&n\geq 0,\,a\geq 0\\
=0&n\geq 0,\,a<0\\
\sim{\rm e}^{+\rho/2}&n<0\end{cases}

(30)

and

\left.\frac{F_{2}^{+}(\rho)}{N_{2}}\right|_{\lambda=n}=\begin{cases}\sim{\rm e}^{-\rho/2}&n\geq 1,\,a\geq 0\\
=0&n\geq 1,\,a<0\\
\sim{\rm e}^{+\rho/2}&n\leq 0\end{cases}

(31)

where
\sim
denotes the asymptotic behavior at
\rho\to\infty
. The increasing modes, namely those that do not vanish at infinity, are automatically discarded in the unbounded solutions and the spectrum is limited to the case
n\geq 0
and
a\geq 0
. Moreover, the radial functions are always increasing at infinity when
\lambda
is not an integer
[
44
]
:

\frac{F_{1}(\rho)}{N_{1}}\sim{\rm e}^{+\rho/2},\quad\frac{F_{2}(\rho)}{N_{2}}\sim{\rm e}^{+\rho/2},\quad\text{for }\lambda\notin\mathbb{Z}.

(32)

In a finite cylinder the increasing modes are generally allowed, and we must check what happens as we increase the size of the cylinder.

As anticipated, as the radius of the cylinder increases, the modes of the unbounded case are recovered. Indeed, for
n\geq 0
and
a\geq 0
, we have

\begin{split}\lim_{\rho_{R}\to\infty}F_{1}^{+}=&F_{1}^{+}(\lambda=n)=I_{n,a}(\rho),\quad n\geq 0,\;a\geq 0,\\
\lim_{\rho_{R}\to\infty}F_{2}^{+}=&F_{2}^{+}(\lambda=n)=I_{n-1,a}(\rho),\quad n\geq 1,\;a\geq 0.\end{split}

(33)

The case
n=0
is recovered if
F_{2}^{+}=0
, indeed
I_{n-1,a}(\rho)
is vanishing for
n=0
.

Consider now the modes that are present in the finite cylinder but are absent in the unbounded space. As noted, the radial functions corresponding to these mode are increasing exponentially with
\rho
. However, the functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are normalized to
1
when integrated over the dimensionless radial coordinate
\rho
. This implies that the constants
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
corresponding to an increasing radial function must fall to zero very rapidly with
\rho_{R}

\lim_{\rho_{R}\to\infty}N_{1,2}=0.

(34)

Eventually, at a large enough radius, the radial functions are significantly different from zero only close to the boundary of the cylinder

\lim_{\rho_{R}\to\infty}F^{+}_{1,2}\simeq\delta(\rho-\rho_{R}),

(35)

because for any finite
\rho
the radial function is brought to zero by its constant. To wit, the radius of the cylinder increases, these increasing modes, including
F_{2}^{+}
for
n=0
, are pushed and squeezed close to the boundary of the cylinder.
In the limit of infinite radius they can be considered as vanishing everywhere.

In this section, we showed that by imposing that the radial wave functions
f_{s}
are vanishing at
r\to\infty
, requires
\lambda
to be a non-negative integer
n
and
f_{s}
be proportional to the
generalized Laguerre polynomials (
26
). In the next section we impose the boundary condition at finite
r
and as a result
\lambda
is not an integer and
f_{s}
are not expressed in terms of the Laguerre polynomials.

IV
Boundary condition at finite
r=R

A light signal sent radially from any point in the region
r<R=1/\Omega
will never cross the light cylinder at
r=R
. Therefore, the wave functions (
27
) must be confined to the causally connected region
r\leq R
. We recently considered a similar problem in connection to the nonrelativistic spin zero bound state problem in rotating frame
[
40
]
. There we imposed the Cauchy boundary condition at
r=R
on the wave function that resulted in a peculiar dependence of the energy spectrum on
\Omega
. Whereas the state of the spin zero system is described by the single wave function, a nonrelativistic spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
state is a two-component spinor, which cannot satisfy the Cauchy boundary condition. This problem is well known since the early days of QCD when the models of hadron structure were developed: the color confinement requires that the quarks be localized within the hadron of a certain radius. Given two solutions of the Dirac equation
(\psi,\,\Phi)
the quantum theory is well posed if the Hamiltonian is a self-adjoint operator with respect to the inner product, that is
\langle\psi,\,\widehat{H}\Phi\rangle=\langle\widehat{H}\psi,\,\Phi\rangle
. In the considered system inside the finite cylinder the Hamiltonian is self-adjoint if the following condition is satisfied, see for instance
[
9
]
:

\displaystyle R\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!{\rm d}z\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\!{\rm d}\phi\,\bar{\psi}(R)\gamma^{r}\Phi(R)=

\displaystyle 0,

(36)

\displaystyle\partial_{r}(\bar{\psi}\Phi)(R)=

\displaystyle-\Theta(R),

(37)

where
\psi(R)=\psi(z,\,\phi,\,r=R)
,
\Theta(R)<\infty

and
\gamma^{r}=\not{n}=\hat{n}_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}=\hat{r}_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}
, with
\hat{n}=\hat{r}

the radial direction in cylindrical coordinates.
A consistent solution is to impose the MIT boundary conditions
[
45
,
46
]
that require vanishing of the outward-pointing component of the fermion current, that is the integrand of Eq. (
36
) is vanishing. The MIT boundary condition in the rotating frame were studied in
[
47
,
48
,
9
]
. Other types of boundary conditions have also been considered
[
9
,
49
,
24
]
.
In the semi-classical limit, it is more convenient to use the representation obtained in
[
50
]
performing a Foldy-Wouthuysen transformation on a curved background (including rotation), where in the nonrelativistic limit the spinor components related to the antiparticles are vanishing.

The MIT boundary conditions in a cylinder of radius
R
constrain the wave function to satisfy
[
45
,
46
]

{\rm i}\hat{r}^{\mu}\gamma_{\mu}\psi(R)={\rm i}\gamma_{r}\psi(R)=\alpha\psi(R),\quad\alpha=\pm

(38)

and

\partial_{r}\left(\bar{\psi}\psi\right)(R)<\infty,

(39)

where
\hat{r}
is the radial direction in cylindrical coordinates. This ensures that Eq. (
36
) is satisfied, hence that
the Hamiltonian is self-adjoint, and that the fluxes of energy, momentum, angular momentum
and charge vanish at the boundary
[
9
,
51
]
. According to the Hellinger–Toeplitz theorem
[
52
]
a self-adjoint Hamiltonian is bounded from below. As a result the vacuum state is well defined which is essential for obtaining meaningful physical results, see Fig.
2
and discussion in
[
5
,
6
]
. Imposing the condition (
38
) to the general solution of the Dirac equation (
11
), we obtain

\begin{cases}C_{1}^{\bar{\sigma}}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho_{R})+\alpha C_{4}^{\bar{\sigma}}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho_{R})=0,\\
C_{3}^{\bar{\sigma}}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho_{R})-\alpha C_{2}^{\bar{\sigma}}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho_{R})=0,\end{cases}

(40)

whence

F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho_{R})=-\alpha\frac{C_{4}^{\bar{\sigma}}}{C_{1}^{\bar{\sigma}}}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho_{R})=\alpha\frac{C_{2}^{\bar{\sigma}}}{C_{3}^{\bar{\sigma}}}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho_{R}),

(41)

where
\rho_{R}=|qB|R^{2}/2
.
Clearly, the boundary condition can only be satisfied by states such that
C_{2}^{\bar{\sigma}}/C_{3}^{\bar{\sigma}}=-C_{4}^{\bar{\sigma}}/C_{1}^{\bar{\sigma}}
.
In the transverse polarization states (
21
), it is straightforward to check that

\frac{C_{4}^{\bar{\sigma}}}{C_{1}^{\bar{\sigma}}}=-\frac{C_{2}^{\bar{\sigma}}}{C_{3}^{\bar{\sigma}}}=\frac{B_{4}}{B_{3}}=\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}\zeta\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{\bar{E}-\zeta M}{\bar{E}+\zeta M}}.

(42)

Then, the MIT boundary conditions applied to the transverse polarization states result in a single equation

\frac{F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho_{R})}{N_{1}}=-\alpha\zeta\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{\bar{E}-\zeta M}{\bar{E}+\zeta M}}\frac{F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho_{R})}{N_{2}}=-\alpha\zeta\sqrt{\frac{M^{2}+\lambda|qB|-\zeta\sqrt{M^{2}+2\lambda|qB|}}{|qB|}}\frac{F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho_{R})}{N_{2}},

(43)

where we remind

\bar{E}=\sqrt{(\varepsilon E-\Omega\,m)^{2}-p_{z}^{2}}=\sqrt{2\lambda|qB|+M^{2}}.

(44)

Equation (
43
) has an infinite countable number of roots
\{\lambda_{i}\},\,i=1,\,2,\,3,\,\dots
such that
\lambda_{i}<\lambda_{i+1}
.
Solving this equation for
\lambda
provides the complete set of normal modes (
11
)

\psi^{\rho_{R}}_{i,\,m,\,p_{z},\,\zeta}(t,r,\phi,z)={\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\epsilon Et}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}p_{z}z}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}m\phi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{|qB|}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
{\rm i}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho){\rm e}^{+{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{3}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{1}(\rho){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
{\rm i}C^{\bar{\sigma}}_{4}F^{\bar{\sigma}}_{2}(\rho){\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi/2}\end{array}\right)_{\lambda=\lambda_{i}(m,\zeta)},

(45)

and the energy spectrum for the Dirac particle in the finite cylinder, whose energy levels are

E_{i,m,p_{z}}=\sqrt{2\lambda_{i}|qB|+p_{z}^{2}+M^{2}}+\Omega\,m\,.

Fixing the value of
\rho_{R}
,

|qB|
,

and

V
Vanishing magnetic field

For fast rotation and small
\rho_{R}
, the effect of the magnetic field is a small correction to the predominant effect of rotation. It is instructive to compare this case with the one with a vanishing magnetic field. Indeed, a charged fermion embedded in a rotating system is accelerating because of its rotation and it will radiate even without the need of an external magnetic field. We compute the radiation of such a fermion in a finite cylinder with radius
R=1/\Omega
assuming MIT BC.

To obtain the complete set of normal modes and the energy spectrum we follow the same procedure described above with the Hamiltonian
H
given by Eq. (
1
) with vanishing gauge field. The results are the same except that the radial functions
F_{1,2}
are now given by Bessel functions and the transverse momentum
p_{T}
is not related to the magnetic field: in the limit of vanishing magnetic field
2\lambda|qB|\to\,p_{T}
. The general form of solutions to the Dirac equation with just rotation (JR), viz. in rotating frame and vanishing magnetic field, is
[
53
]

\psi^{\rm JR}(t,r,\phi,z)={\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\epsilon Et}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}p_{z}z}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}m\phi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}C^{\rm JR}_{1}F^{\rm JR}_{1}(r){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
(-{\rm i})C_{2}^{\rm JR}F^{\rm JR}_{2}(r){\rm e}^{+{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
C^{\rm JR}_{3}F^{\rm JR}_{1}(r){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\phi/2}\\
(-{\rm i})C^{\rm JR}_{4}F^{\rm JR}_{2}(r){\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi/2}\end{array}\right),

(48)

with
F^{\rm JR}_{1,2}
given by

\begin{split}F^{\rm JR}_{1}(r)=&N^{\rm JR}_{m-1/2}J_{m-1/2}(p_{T}R),\\
F^{\rm JR}_{2}(r)=&N^{\rm JR}_{m+1/2}J_{m+1/2}(p_{T}R),\end{split}

(49)

with
J
the Bessel function and with normalization constant

N^{\rm JR}_{\nu}=\sqrt{2}\Omega\left|J_{\nu}\left(\frac{p_{T}}{\Omega}\right)^{2}+J_{\nu+1}\left(\frac{p_{T}}{\Omega}\right)^{2}-\frac{2\nu\Omega}{p_{T}}J_{\nu}\left(\frac{p_{T}}{\Omega}\right)J_{\nu+1}\left(\frac{p_{T}}{\Omega}\right)\right|^{-1/2},

(50)

such that

\int_{0}^{1/\Omega}{\rm d}r\,r\left|F^{\rm JR}_{1,2}(r)\right|^{2}=1.

(51)

In the transverse polarization the coefficients
C^{\rm JR}_{s}
read

C^{\rm JR}_{1,3}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}B^{\rm JR}_{+}(A^{\rm JR}_{+}\pm\zeta A^{\rm JR}_{-}),\quad C^{\rm JR}_{2,4}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\frac{N^{\rm JR}_{m-1/2}}{N^{\rm JR}_{m+1/2}}B^{\rm JR}_{-}(A^{\rm JR}_{-}\mp\zeta A^{\rm JR}_{+}),

(52)

where

\begin{split}A^{\rm JR}_{\pm}=&\left(\frac{E-\Omega\,m\pm p_{z}}{E-\Omega\,m}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\\
B^{\rm JR}_{\pm}=&\left(\frac{2(\bar{E}\pm\zeta M)}{\left(1+\left(\frac{N^{\rm JR}_{m-1/2}}{N^{\rm JR}_{m+1/2}}\right)^{2}\right)\bar{E}+\left(1-\left(\frac{N^{\rm JR}_{m-1/2}}{N^{\rm JR}_{m+1/2}}\right)^{2}\right)\zeta M}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\end{split}

(53)

with
\bar{E}=\sqrt{(\varepsilon E-\Omega\,m)^{2}-p_{z}^{2}}=\sqrt{p_{T}^{2}+M^{2}}
.

Likewise, the MIT boundary condition is imposed in the same fashion as described for the case with magnetic field. At the end we obtain that the MIT BC impose a constrain expressed as in Eq. (
41
) with the hypergeometric function replaced with the Bessel functions; the constrain reads:

J_{m-1/2}\left(\frac{p_{T}}{\Omega}\right)-\alpha\zeta\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{p_{T}^{2}+M^{2}}-\zeta M}{\sqrt{p_{T}^{2}+M^{2}}+\zeta M}}J_{m+1/2}\left(\frac{p_{T}}{\Omega}\right)=0.

(54)

Solving this equation for the roots
\{p_{T,i}\}
provides the complete set of normal modes and the energy spectrum for the rotating Dirac particle in the finite cylinder.
As in the finite magnetic field case, the roots
p_{T}
depend on
\Omega
,
m
and
\zeta
.

VI
Differential Radiation Intensity

Thus far we investigated the fermion spectrum in rotating frame subject to the external magnetic field. We observed that it significantly differs from the familiar Landau levels. Thus its electromagnetic radiation must be quite distinct from the synchrotron radiation as indeed we found in
[
1
,
2
]
. It is convenient to study the electromagnetic radiation in the stationary laboratory frame where the fermion motion is a nonlinear combination of circular motion in the external magnetic field and another circular motion with angular velocity
\Omega
as the fermion is dragged along with the rotating medium. Unlike the fermion, the electromagnetic field does not rotate. Therefore, in the laboratory frame the photon wave function is given by the standard inertial frame solutions to the wave equation. This setup is described in detail in
[
2
]
where it is also elucidated using a classical model.

The intensity of electromagnetic radiation by a fermion of charge
q
in magnetic field
{\bm{B}}=B\hat{{\bm{z}}}

{\bm{\Omega}}=\Omega\hat{{\bm{z}}}
in the unbounded volume is given by
[
1
]

(56)

where
\theta
is the angle between the photon momentum and the rotation axis and the matrix elements
\langle\,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{j}$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}\,\rangle

\displaystyle\int\bar{\psi}^{\infty}_{n^{\prime},\,a^{\prime},\,p_{z}^{\prime},\,\zeta^{\prime}}({\bm{x}})\bm{\Phi}^{*}_{h,l,\omega}({\bm{x}})\cdot\bm{\gamma}\psi^{\infty}_{n,\,a,\,p_{z},\,\zeta}({\bm{x}})\,d^{3}x\equiv\langle\,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{j}$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}\,\rangle\delta_{m^{\prime},m-l}\frac{2\pi}{L}\delta(p_{z}-p_{z}^{\prime}-k_{z})\,,

with the fermion wave functions given by Eq. (
27

The primed quantities throughout the present and the remaining sections refer to the Landau levels of the final state unless indicated otherwise,

where

\displaystyle\int_{V(R)}\bar{\psi}^{\rho_{R}}_{i^{\prime},\,m^{\prime},\,p_{z}^{\prime},\,\zeta^{\prime}}({\bm{x}})\bm{\Phi}^{*}_{h,l,\omega}({\bm{x}})\cdot\bm{\gamma}\psi^{\rho_{R}}_{i,\,m,\,p_{z},\,\zeta}({\bm{x}})\,d^{3}x\equiv\langle\,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{j}$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}\,\rangle\delta_{m^{\prime},m-l}\frac{2\pi}{L}\delta(p_{z}-p_{z}^{\prime}-k_{z})\,.

\displaystyle\varphi^{*}_{z}(r)=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{\perp}}{k}J_{l}(k_{\perp}r).

\displaystyle f_{\lambda^{\prime},a^{\prime}}^{T}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{x}f_{\lambda,a}=

Writing the scalar product
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{\varphi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{\varphi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{\varphi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{\varphi}$}}^{*}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{\gamma}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{\gamma}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{\gamma}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{\gamma}$}}

and using (

\displaystyle\langle\,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{j}$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}\,\rangle=|qB|\int_{0}^{R}drr

and

\displaystyle W_{i,m,p_{z},\zeta}^{h}=

\displaystyle\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\sum_{i^{\prime},m^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime}}\int\omega^{2}\sin\theta d\omega\,d\theta\,\delta(\omega-E+E^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times\Big|\sin\theta\left[K_{4}\mathcal{J}_{4}-K_{3}\mathcal{J}_{3}\right]+K_{1}\left(h-\cos\theta\right)\mathcal{J}_{1}-K_{2}\left(h+\cos\theta\right)\mathcal{J}_{2}\Big|^{2}\,.

The total radiation intensity requires integration over

\omega=E-E^{\prime}
and

. Using (

\displaystyle\omega_{0}=\frac{E-m^{\prime}\Omega}{\sin^{2}\theta}\left\{1-\left[1-\frac{\mathcal{B}\sin^{2}\theta}{(E-m^{\prime}\Omega)^{2}}\right]^{1/2}\right\}\,,

(71)

where we defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=

\displaystyle 2(n-n^{\prime})|qB|-\Omega^{2}(m-m^{\prime})^{2}+2(E-m^{\prime}\Omega)\Omega(m-m^{\prime}).

(72)

Unlike the unbounded case, in a finite cylinder we cannot analytically sum over the fermion polarizations
\zeta
,
\zeta^{\prime}
because the dependence on
\zeta^{\prime}
is not only contained in the coefficients

, but also in the principal quantum number
\lambda^{\prime}
and other quantities. Furthermore, since the principal quantum number
\lambda_{i}(\zeta,\,m)
can be quite different for opposite polarizations, it is often misleading to compute the total radiation as an average of initial polarizations
\zeta=+
and
\zeta=-
. We will compute instead the total radiation at fixed initial polarization.
The total intensity (at a given polarization

W=\sum_{h,\zeta^{\prime}}W_{i,m,p_{z}=0,\zeta}^{h}=\frac{3}{2}\frac{W_{\mathrm{Cl}}}{E^{2}(qB)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}{\rm d}\theta\sum_{\zeta^{\prime},\,m^{\prime},\,i^{\prime}}\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}\sin\theta}{1+\frac{\omega_{0}\cos^{2}\theta}{E^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\Omega}}\Gamma^{0}_{i,m,\zeta}(i^{\prime},m^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime},\theta),

(73)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma^{0}_{i,m,\zeta}(i^{\prime},m^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime},\theta)=

\displaystyle\sum_{h}\langle\,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{j}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{j}$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{\Phi}$}}\,\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left[K_{1}^{2}\mathcal{J}_{1}^{2}+K_{2}^{2}\mathcal{J}_{2}^{2}-2K_{1}K_{2}\mathcal{J}_{1}\mathcal{J}_{2}\right.

\displaystyle+\cos^{2}\theta\left(K_{1}\mathcal{J}_{1}+K_{2}\mathcal{J}_{2}\right)^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\left(K_{3}\mathcal{J}_{3}-K_{4}\mathcal{J}_{4}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\left.+\sin(2\theta)(K_{1}\mathcal{J}_{1}+K_{2}\mathcal{J}_{2})(K_{3}\mathcal{J}_{3}-K_{4}\mathcal{J}_{4})\right],

(74)

and
W_{\mathrm{Cl}}
is the classical intensity in the ultrarelativistic limit

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{Cl}}=\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\frac{2(qB)^{2}E^{2}}{3}\,.

(75)

We use (

\frac{W}{W_{\mathrm{Cl}}}=\sum_{h,\,\zeta^{\prime}}\frac{W_{i,m,p_{z}=0,\zeta}^{h}}{W_{\mathrm{cl}}}=\frac{3}{2}\frac{1}{E^{2}(qB)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}{\rm d}\theta\sum_{\zeta^{\prime},\,m^{\prime},\,i^{\prime}}\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}\sin\theta}{1+\frac{\omega_{0}\cos^{2}\theta}{E^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\Omega}}\Gamma^{0}_{i,m,\zeta}(i^{\prime},m^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime},\theta),

(76)

with the energy

being the energy of the initial fermion

(77)

We will also compute the radiation intensity for a given photon’s total angular momentum

\frac{W(l)}{W_{\mathrm{Cl}}}=\sum_{h,\,\zeta^{\prime}}\frac{W_{i,m,p_{z}=0,\zeta}^{h}(l)}{W_{\mathrm{Cl}}}=\frac{3}{2}\frac{1}{E^{2}(qB)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}{\rm d}\theta\sum_{\zeta^{\prime},\,i^{\prime}}\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}\sin\theta}{1+\frac{\omega_{0}\cos^{2}\theta}{E^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\Omega}}\Gamma^{0}_{i,m,\zeta}(i^{\prime},m^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime},\theta)\,.

(78)

Since the analytical expression of the roots

are not known, the summation on

,

and

We will also compute the radiation intensity in the finite volume at vanishing magnetic field

VII
Numerical results

In this section we present the procedure and the results of the numerical calculation of the radiation intensity (
76
) and its angular momentum spectrum (
78
) for
\bar{\sigma}=
sgn
(qB)=-1
, e.g. an electron
q=-1
in the magnetic field pointing in the
z
-direction
B>0
. The radiation intensity for a positive charge can be easily obtained by simply inverting the sense of rotation. The calculation is performed for two values of magnetic field
qB=-0.2M^{2}
and
qB=0
and for a wide range of parameters: initial angular momentum
m
, angular velocity
\Omega
, and initial energy
E
of Eq. (
77
). The parameter that controls the influence of the boundary condition on the intensity is
\rho_{R}=|qB|/(2\Omega^{2})
. We observed that at
\rho_{R}=100
the system is in an intermediate stage where the boundary effects start to be significant. In addition, the calculation of radiation intensity can also be done in the unbounded case without breaking causality as long as the energy is low and the initial angular momentum is not large. In this case we compare the results obtained with unbounded solution and MIT BC.

In principle the summations in Eqs. (
76
) and (
78
) must be done for each quantum number of the final fermion such that its energy is lower than the initial one. This is not a huge number of states when the rotation is negative, see Fig.
3
, but the total number of final states can be very large for positive rotation, especially for small angular velocity, see Fig.
2
. However, since the emission of photons with a large angular momentum is generally disfavored by the integrals (
69
) inside the matrix element (
68
), we do not need to include final states with an angular momentum
m^{\prime}
that is far from the initial angular momentum
m
to obtain a result within a certain precision. Taking advantage of this feature the calculation has been performed as described in the following paragraphs.

Having fixed the magnetic intensity
qB
, the angular velocity
\Omega
and initial state with energy
E
, we solved the MIT BC (
43
) and we computed the energy levels of the final fermions up to the energy
E
with both polarizations
\zeta^{\prime}=\pm
and with an angular momentum
m^{\prime}
from
-|\bar{m}^{\prime}|
to
|\bar{m}^{\prime}|
. Since the largest initial angular momentum that we considered is
m=\pm 20.5
, we choose
|\bar{m}^{\prime}|=100.5
for
\rho_{R}=100
and
|\bar{m}^{\prime}|=50.5
for smaller values of
\rho_{R}
. We then counted the total number of final states
N_{f}
with
-|\bar{m}^{\prime}|\leq m^{\prime}\leq|\bar{m}^{\prime}|
and such that
E^{\prime}\leq E
and we computed the radiation intensity
W_{f}(\theta=\pi/2)
for each of those single final states at
\theta=\pi/2
where the intensity is at or close to its peak and we found the maximal state
\bar{f}
giving the maximum of radiation intensity
\overline{W}_{\bar{f}}=
Max
\{W_{f}(\theta=\pi/2)\}
.
Given that we want to perform the calculation up to a relative precision
\epsilon
, among all these final states we only included the states such that they had a relevant contribution up to the precision
\epsilon
. We considered a final state
f
a relevant state if it satisfies the following condition

\frac{W_{f}(\theta=\pi/2)}{\overline{W}_{\bar{f}}}N_{f}>\epsilon.

(79)

In other words, we are comparing the contribution from the state
f
and the maximal contribution
\bar{f}
. A state
f
is considered not relevant when the ratio between its contribution multiplied by the number of all considered states and the contribution from
\bar{f}
is smaller then our goal precision. In this way since
f
is a correction below the precision for
\bar{f}
, it is also a correction below the precision for the total intensity. If the values of
m^{\prime}
between the relevant states did not reached the extreme values
\pm|\bar{m}^{\prime}|
, the set of relevant states were considered enough to reach the precision
\epsilon
and we proceeded to compute the summations within those states and the integration over
\theta
, otherwise we decreased the precision and we repeated the procedure.

For
\rho_{R}<100
we were always able to reach a precision of
\epsilon=0.01
and the error bars in the plots are smaller than the marker size used to represent the data point. For
\rho_{R}=100
we were only able to reach the precision
\epsilon=0.01
when the initial energy was lower than
E\sim 2M
, for higher energies we decreased the precision when possible (and we show the error bars in the plots), however past a certain value the limit
|\bar{m}^{\prime}|=100.5
was not enough to obtain a meaningful result.

For negative rotation instead, at rapid rotation the radiation inverts the trend and starts increasing instead of decreasing because at large

VIII
Summary

In this paper we performed a detailed analysis of the electromagnetic radiation by a fermion embedded in a uniformly rapidly rotating medium and subject of the external magnetic field. We first obtained the exact solution of the Dirac equation in the finite cylinder with radius
R=c/\Omega
. These states, unlike the ones in an unbounded volume, do not violate causality and the energy spectrum is bounded from below, ensuring that summations over the complete set of states do not introduce nonphysical divergences. These were essential problems to solve if one wants to study rapidly rotating systems. Between several methods to impose a boundary condition (BC) at the light-cylinder, we used the MIT boundary condition because it guarantees that no conserved currents cross the light-cylinder. Another important difference with the unbounded case is that the integer Landau principal quantum number
n
becomes a real number whose value depends on other quantum numbers: the total angular momentum along the axis of the cylinder and the polarization, as well as on the size of the cylinder, fermion mass and the magnetic field strength.

Using these causal states we analytically computed the differential radiation intensity given by (
70
), the spectrum of the photon’s angular momentum (
76
) and the total intensity (
78
). The final analytical results still contain summations and integration that we performed numerically. In the unbounded case some of these sums and integrals can be carried out analytically, while in the finite volume that is not possible because the quantization of transverse momentum has to be obtained numerically from the MIT BC constrain (
43
) and depends on many parameters.

At slow rotation the synchrotron radiation intensity is suppressed for parallel
q\bm{B}
and
\bm{\Omega}
and is enhanced for the antiparallel configuration. Our main observation, illustrated in Fig.
7
and
13
, is that for fast rotation the effect of enhancement is reduced and that the intensity from the parallel configuration starts to be enhanced as well. The latter occurs simply because the radiation from the global rotation becomes large and dominates over the circular motion caused by the magnetic field. Instead, a likely explanation of the reduced enhancement for the antiparallel configuration is that as the angular velocity increases, the states get squeezed in a smaller volume to preserve causality. This causes all energy levels to rise, and as a result, a state with a given energy has less states to decay into, resulting in a lower emission probability compared to an emission in a larger volume.

We furthermore studied the angular momentum of the radiation. The results illustrated in Fig.
16
, indicates that the photon angular momentum tends to be aligned in the opposite direction of
q\bm{B}
and when
\bm{B}
and
\bm{\Omega}
are parallel becomes aligned to rotation only at very fast rotation. For moderate rotation the angular momentum is spread almost evenly in a wide range of values including very high values. At faster rotations the angular momentum spread is more peaked toward lower values.

The analysis presented in this work is general and can be applied to any rapidly rotating system. Our main interest and motivation is relativistic heavy-ion collisions, where estimates indicate that the magnetic length and the light-cylinder radius are of similar order of magnitude. The early time magnetic field is on the order of
eB\sim m_{\pi}^{2}=2\times 10^{4}\text{ MeV}^{2}
. In the quark-gluon plasma phase at temperature
T=300
MeV, considering a quark with thermal mass
M\sim T
, we obtain
qB/M^{2}\sim 0.2
, which is the value used in this work. The late time vorticity of the QGP, which gives the angular velocity of rotation, is measured through spin polarization
[
55
]
. Contrary to the magnetic field, which is larger at higher collision energies, the vorticity is higher at low energies. Estimates indicate that at low collision energy, the angular velocity
\Omega
can also be larger than the magnetic field
\sqrt{qB}
, see for instance Fig. 1 of
[
2
]
, while at high energies the magnetic field is definitely larger. However, the time evolution suppress the magnetic field much faster than the rotation, which is instead sustained by the conservation of angular momentum, and even in the high collision energy we expect a stage where the magnetic field is lower than the rotation. Thus it emerges that throughout most of plasma evolution
\rho_{R}=|qB|/{2\Omega^{2}}\leq 1
, implying that the effects analyzed in this work are expected to be very important.

The numerical computation of the photon radiation by the quark-gluon plasma is quite costly and we leave it for the future study. We would like to point out however, that in the limit of extremely fast rotation, when the light-cylinder radius
1/\Omega
is much shorter than the mean-free-path, which is in turn much shorter than the plasma size, the calculation significantly simplifies.
First, the magnetic field can be neglected, as we showed in this paper. Second, we argued in our recent article
[
56
]
that the electromagnetic spectrum can be computed by considering only the states localized on the light-cylinder surface. This may be the most realistic path toward the phenomenological modeling of the electromagnetic radiation by rotating quark-gluon plasma.
