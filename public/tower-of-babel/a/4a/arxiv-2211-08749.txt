A few more Lonely Runners
Avinash Bhardwaj, Vishnu Narayanan, Hrishikesh Venkataraman

Abstract
. Lonely Runner Conjecture , proposed by Jörg M. Wills and so nomenclatured by Luis Goddyn, has been an object of interest since it was first conceived in 1967: Given positive integers k k and n 1 , n 2 , … , n k n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{k} there exists a positive real number t t such that the distance of t ⋅ n j t\cdot n_{j} to the nearest integer is at least 1 k + 1 \frac{1}{k+1} , ∀ 1 ≤ j ≤ k \forall~~1\leq j\leq k . In a recent article, Beck, Hosten and Schymura described the Lonely Runner polyhedron and provided a polyhedral approach to identifying families of lonely runner instances. We revisit the Lonely Runner polyhedron and highlight some new families of instances satisfying the conjecture. In addition, we relax the sufficiency of existence of an integer point in the Lonely Runner polyhedron to prove the conjecture. Specifically, we propose that it suffices to show the existence of a lattice point of certain superlattices of the integer lattice in the Lonely Runner polyhedron .



1.
Introduction

The following was conjectured independently by Wills
[
21
]
and Cusick
[
11
]
.

Conjecture 1
.

Consider
k+1
pairwise-distinct real numbers
n_{0},n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
. For any
0\leq i\leq k
, there is a real number
t
such that the distance of
t(n_{j}-n_{i})
to the nearest integer is at least
\dfrac{1}{k+1}
, for all
0\leq j\leq k
,
j\neq i
.

Goddyn
[
5
]
termed the above as the
Lonely Runner Conjecture
, where he considered the real numbers to be the speeds of runners on a unit length circular track, all of whom start at the same time and at the same point on the track. Note that it is the
(n_{j}-n_{i})
that matters and not the
n_{i}
itself. Consequently, the speed of a runner can be subtracted from all the speeds. This leaves a runner stationary and possibly, some runners with negative speeds. However, running with a negative speed is equivalent to running in the opposite direction, with the same magnitude of speed. Combining the stationarity of a runner with the symmetry of the track, all resultant speeds can be assumed to be positive. A similar argument can be made for the time
t
as well. Thus, an equivalent version of the conjecture is:

Conjecture 2
.

(Lonely Runner Conjecture)

Given
k
positive real numbers
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{k}
, there is a non-negative real number
t
such that the distance of each
tn_{i},~1\leq i\leq k
to its nearest integer is at least
\dfrac{1}{k+1}
.

In the remainder of this paper, we refer to the
Lonely Runner Conjecture
in the form described in
2
. Furthermore, we refer to any
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{k})
as a
lonely runner instance
if
\mathbf{n}
satisfies the conjecture.

With minimal effort, the conjecture can be proven to be true for
k=1
. The
k=2
case was proven in the context of Diophantine approximation by Wills
[
21
]
. Using a similar approach, the
k=3
case was proven, first by Betke and Wills
[
4
]
, and then by Cusick
[
11
]
. The first proof for
k=4
was given using a view-obstruction problem approach, by Cusick and Pomerance
[
12
]
. A simpler proof was then given by Bienia et al.
[
5
]
by relating the conjecture to nowhere zero flows in regular matroids. Thinking of the conjecture as a covering problem, the
k=5
case was proven by Bohman, Holzman and Kleitman
[
7
]
. Later, Renault
[
18
]
provided a simpler proof for this, using a case-by-case analysis of all possible speed vectors. Subsequently, a study of the regular chromatic number of distance graphs, by Barajas and Serra
[
1
]
, led to a proof for
k=6
. The conjecture remains open for
k\geq 7
.

Henze and Malikiosis
[
15
]
were among the first ones to study the conjecture from a polyhedral theory perspective. They made use of the equivalence of - (i) well-known geometric problems, such as the motion of billiards balls in a cube avoiding an inner cube, and (ii) determining the covering radii of lattice zonotopes. They established, as the first unconditional proof, that it suffices to prove the conjecture for positive integral speeds.

An alternative approach to fixing
k
and attacking the problem is to identify families of speed vectors that are
lonely runner instances
. Such an endeavor was taken by Beck, Hosten and Schymura
[
3
]
. The authors introduced the
Lonely Runner polyhedron
and provided families of
lonely runner instances
, one in terms of the speeds of the fastest and slowest runners, and another in terms of the parities of the runners’ speeds. Subsequently, by considering the projection of the polyhedron on to the first one and two coordinates, they provide families of
lonely runner instances
by relating the speeds of the second and third fastest runners with the slowest.

Very recently, Rifford
[
19
]
employed a new method to approach the conjecture. In particular, he explored the problem of determining an upper bound on
t
, in terms of the number of rounds covered by the slowest runner, and described this in terms of a covering problem. A conjecture was proposed for the covering problem and it was proven to be true for all
2\leq k\leq 5
.

One related quantity, to
2

gap of loneliness
. Let

represent the distance of a real number
x
to the nearest integer.
The

\Gamma(k)=\inf_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}}\sup\limits_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\min\limits_{i\in[k]}d(tn_{i},\mathbb{Z})

An equivalent version of the
Lonely Runner Conjecture
states that
\Gamma(k)=\dfrac{1}{k+1}
. Dirichlet approximation theorem establishes that
\Gamma(k)\leq\dfrac{1}{k+1}
. Several efforts have been made to prove the converse in
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
19

and
[
20

[
20

proved that it suffices to show that the

holds for
\mathbf{n}
having all components at most
k^{O(k^{2})}
. In an attempt to prove the same, Tao proved that if all the components of a speed vector
\mathbf{n}
are at most

\mathbf{n}
is a

. Furthermore, it was noted that a desired goal is to increase the multiplier

to

[
6

proved the approximate version of this result when they showed that it is possible to get the multiplier arbitrarily close to

Assuming that

\mathbf{n}

Some Properties of Lonely Runner Polyhedra

The major construct in the proofs of Theorems
5
and
6
is the
Lonely Runner polyhedron
,
{P}(\mathbf{n})
, as defined in Section
1.1
. In particular,

{P}(\mathbf{n}):=\left\{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\dfrac{n_{i}-kn_{j}}{k+1}\leq n_{j}x_{i}-n_{i}x_{j}\leq\dfrac{kn_{i}-n_{j}}{k+1}~\text{for}~1\leq i<j\leq k\right\}

Proposition 11
.

P(\mathbf{n})
is symmetric about
-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},...,v_{k})
be an arbitrary point in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. With this,

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}+\mathbf{v}\in P(\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle\iff\dfrac{n_{i}-kn_{j}}{k+1}\leq n_{j}\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}+v_{i}\right)-n_{i}\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}+v_{j}\right)\leq\dfrac{kn_{i}-n_{j}}{k+1}~~\forall 1\leq i<j\leq k

\displaystyle\iff-\dfrac{(k-1)(n_{i}+n_{j})}{2(k+1)}\leq n_{j}v_{i}-n_{i}v_{j}\leq\dfrac{(k-1)(n_{i}+n_{j})}{2(k+1)}~~\forall 1\leq i<j\leq k

\displaystyle\iff-\dfrac{(k-1)(n_{i}+n_{j})}{2(k+1)}\leq-n_{j}v_{i}+n_{i}v_{j}\leq\dfrac{(k-1)(n_{i}+n_{j})}{2(k+1)}~~\forall 1\leq i<j\leq k

\displaystyle\iff\dfrac{n_{i}-kn_{j}}{k+1}\leq n_{j}\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}-v_{i}\right)-n_{i}\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}-v_{j}\right)\leq\dfrac{kn_{i}-n_{j}}{k+1}~~\forall 1\leq i<j\leq k

\displaystyle\iff-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}-\mathbf{v}\in P(\mathbf{n})

The result follows.
∎

A very important fact that we use to prove our main results is that any integer translate of a polyhedron, say
{P}+\mathbf{v}
,
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, contains the same number of integer points as the original polyhedron,
{P}
. To observe this, first note that
\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}=b
is a face of
{P}
if and only if
\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v})=b
is a face of
{P}+\mathbf{v}
. Then,
\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
satisfies
\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}=b
if and only if
\mathbf{l}+\mathbf{v}
satisfies
\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v})=b
. Due to this bijection between
{P}
and
{P}+\mathbf{v}
, the number of integer points in
{P}
and
{P}+\mathbf{v}
are equal.

We also utilize projection arguments to prove some of the results. Consider the projection of
{P}(\mathbf{n})
on to the first
m
coordinates,
{P}_{m}(\mathbf{n})
. If
\mathbf{n}
satisfies

contains an integer point

instead of proving that
{P}(\mathbf{n})
is not integer lattice-free. This is because,

would lie in
{P}(\mathbf{n})
.

Consider the projection of
{P}(\mathbf{n})
on to the first two coordinates,

is a polyhedron as well

\displaystyle\dfrac{n_{2}}{(k+1)n_{k}}-\dfrac{k}{k+1}\leq x_{2}\leq\dfrac{kn_{2}}{(k+1)n_{3}}-\dfrac{1}{k+1}

\displaystyle\dfrac{n_{1}-kn_{2}}{k+1}\leq n_{2}x_{1}-n_{1}x_{2}\leq\dfrac{kn_{1}-n_{2}}{k+1}

In addition to the generating inequalities of

1-b_{i}\in\left[\dfrac{1}{k+1},\dfrac{k}{k+1}\right]
.

We have,
\{n_{i}(1-t)\}\in\left[\dfrac{1}{k+1},\dfrac{k}{k+1}\right]
for all
i\in[k]
. Thus
1-t
is a suitable time as well.

A similar argument can be made by assuming
1-t
to be a suitable time, which shows
1-(1-t)=t
is a suitable time as well. The result follows.
∎

Theorem 8
.

\mathbf{n}
is a lonely runner instance iff there is a suitable time
t\leq\dfrac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Sufficiency follows from the definition of suitable time. To see the necessity, assume that
\mathbf{n}
is a
lonely runner instance
. Thus there is a suitable time
s\in[0,1]
. Consider the function

t=\begin{cases}s&\text{if }s\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\\
1-s&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

17
yields that both
s
and
1-s
are suitable times, implying that at least one of
s
and
1-s
is at most
\dfrac{1}{2}
. The result follows.
∎

6.
A new sufficiency condition

From a polyhedral theory perspective, the existence of an integer lattice point in
P(\mathbf{n})
is the best known sufficiency condition to prove the
Lonely Runner Conjecture
. In this section, we provide a new sufficiency condition to prove the conjecture.

Lemma 18
.

Consider
p\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. Then, the set of points,
\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p}
, defined by

\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p}:=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\mathbf{x}=\dfrac{a}{p}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b},0\leq a\leq p-1,a\in\mathbb{Z},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}\right\}

is a lattice.

Proof.

First, let
a=0
and
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}
. Then,
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p}
. Next, consider two points
\mathbf{y}=\dfrac{a_{1}}{p}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b_{1}}
and
\mathbf{z}=\dfrac{a_{2}}{p}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b_{2}}
, where
0\leq a_{1},a_{2}\leq p-1
,
a_{1}
,
a_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbf{b_{1}}
,
\mathbf{b_{2}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. Their sum is given by
\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z}=\left(\dfrac{a_{1}}{p}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b_{1}}\right)+\left(\dfrac{a_{2}}{p}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b_{2}}\right)=\dfrac{a_{1}+a_{2}}{p}\mathbf{n}+(\mathbf{b_{1}}+\mathbf{b_{2}})
. Note that
(a_{1}+a_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}
and
(\mathbf{b_{1}}+\mathbf{b_{2}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. If
0\leq a_{1}+a_{2}\leq p-1
, then
\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z}\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p}
. If not, then
p\leq a_{1}+a_{2}\leq 2p-2
. Then,
\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z}=\dfrac{a_{1}+a_{2}-p}{p}\mathbf{n}+(\mathbf{b_{1}}+\mathbf{b_{2}}+\mathbf{n})
, and thus,
\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z}\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p}
. Finally, let
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p}
. If
\mathbf{u}\neq\mathbf{v}
, then
\exists~i\in[k]~\text{s.t}~|u_{i}-v_{i}|\geq\dfrac{1}{p}
. As a result,
\mathcal{B}\left(\mathbf{v},\dfrac{1}{2p}\right)=\mathbf{v}
. Therefore,
\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p}
is a discrete additive subgroup or, equivalently, a lattice.
∎

Lemma 19
.

If
p_{1},p_{2},m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that
p_{2}=mp_{1}
, then
\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p_{1}}\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p_{2}}
.

Proof.

Consider
\mathbf{x}=\dfrac{a}{p_{1}}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b}\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p_{1}}
. Note that
\mathbf{x}=\dfrac{am}{mp_{1}}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b}=\dfrac{am}{p_{2}}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b}\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p_{2}}
, where
0\leq am\leq m(p_{1}-1)\leq mp_{1}-1=p_{2}-1
. Thus, we have
\mathbf{x}\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p_{2}}
and the result holds.
∎

The equation of any line in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n\geq 2
) can be represented using the symmetric form. If
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a point on the line and the direction ratios of the line are given by
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the equation (in symmetric form) of the line is

\dfrac{x_{1}-y_{1}}{a_{1}}=\dfrac{x_{2}-y_{2}}{a_{2}}=\ldots=\dfrac{x_{n}-y_{n}}{a_{n}}=t

where
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Other than in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there is no known condition to determine whether or not a line passes through an integer point. We now provide one such condition for the case where
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
(or equivalently
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
) and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.

Proposition 20
.

Let
l
be a line in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with direction ratios
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. If there exists
p\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
such that
l\cap\Lambda_{\mathbf{r},p}\neq\phi
, then
l\cap\Lambda_{\mathbf{r},1}\neq\phi
.

Proof.

Let
\dfrac{a}{p}\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{b}
be a point on
l
. Then the equation of this line is given by:

\dfrac{x_{1}-\left(\dfrac{a}{p}r_{1}+b_{1}\right)}{r_{1}}=\dfrac{x_{2}-\left(\dfrac{a}{p}r_{2}+b_{2}\right)}{r_{2}}=\ldots=\dfrac{x_{n}-\left(\dfrac{a}{p}r_{n}+b_{n}\right)}{r_{n}}

Equating the terms corresponding to
x_{i}
and
x_{n}
gives

\dfrac{x_{i}-\left(\dfrac{a}{p}r_{i}+b_{i}\right)}{r_{i}}=\dfrac{x_{n}-\left(\dfrac{a}{p}r_{n}+b_{n}\right)}{r_{n}}

On simplification, the equations can be written as:

x_{i}=\dfrac{r_{i}}{r_{n}}x_{n}+\dfrac{r_{n}b_{i}-r_{i}b_{n}}{r_{n}}

On substituting
x_{n}=b_{n}+cr_{n},c\in\mathbb{Z}
, we get
x_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}~~\forall 1\leq i\leq n
. Thus,
l
contains an integer point.
∎

Proposition 21
.

Any line in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with direction ratios
\mathbf{n}
is parallel to each of the facets of
P(\mathbf{n})
.

Proof.

Consider any facet of
P(\mathbf{n})
. The normal vector of this hyperplane is given by
\mathbf{N}=n_{j}\mathbf{e_{i}}-n_{i}\mathbf{e_{j}}
. Then,
\mathbf{N}\cdot\mathbf{n}=n_{i}(n_{j})+n_{j}(-n_{i})=0
. Since the dot product is zero, the normal vector of the hyperplane and the direction vector of the line are parallel to each other. The result follows since the above holds for each of the facets of
P(\mathbf{n})
.
∎

Remark 3
.

Let
l
be a line in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, with direction ratios
\mathbf{n}
. Then, due to
21
,
l\subseteq P(\mathbf{n})
or
l\cap P(\mathbf{n})=\emptyset
.

Theorem 10
.

If
P(\mathbf{n})\cap\left(\bigcup\limits_{p\geq 1}\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p}\right)\neq\emptyset
, then
P(\mathbf{n})\cap\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},1}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Consider any line with direction ratios
\mathbf{n}

P(\mathbf{n})\cap\left(\bigcup\limits_{p\geq 1}\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p}\right)\neq\emptyset
. Due to
20
and Remark
3
, we have
P(\mathbf{n})\cap\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},1}\neq\emptyset
.

serves as a new sufficiency condition that may be used to prove the conjecture. In order to understand the geometry of a lattice, it helps to have knowledge of the basis of the lattice. To this end, we attempt to identify a basis for
\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p}
in the following.

Proposition 22
.

Let
p
be such that
GCD(n_{i},p)=1
for some
i\in[k]
. Then,

B=\left\{\dfrac{m}{p}\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{d},\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{i-1},\mathbf{e}_{i+1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{k}\right\}

is a basis of
\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p}
where
m=n_{i}^{-1}~\textit{{mod}}~p
and
\mathbf{d}=\left(\Bigg\lfloor\dfrac{mn_{1}}{p}\Bigg\rfloor,\ldots,\Bigg\lfloor\dfrac{mn_{k}}{p}\Bigg\rfloor\right)
.

Proof.

WLOG assume that
GCD(n_{k},p)=1
. From this, we get that
mn_{k}=1+cp
where
c\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then,
d_{k}=c
and we get,

(12)

\dfrac{mn_{k}}{p}-d_{k}=\dfrac{1}{p}

Next, note that
0\leq m<p
. By letting
a=m
and
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{d}
in the definition of
\Lambda_{\mathbf{n},p}
, we get that
\left(\dfrac{m}{p}\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{d}\right)
is a lattice point. Moreover, it is easy to check that
\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{k-1}
are lattice points.

Now consider an arbitrary lattice point. Let it be
\left(\dfrac{a}{p}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b}\right)
. Assume that

(13)

\dfrac{a}{p}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b}=c_{1}\left(\dfrac{m}{p}\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{d}\right)+c_{2}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\ldots+c_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k-1}

where
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}
are real numbers. To prove the result, it suffices to show that these
c_{i}
s are, in fact, all integers.

Equating the
k
th coordinates of the left and right hand side of (
13
), we get:

\dfrac{an_{k}}{p}+b_{k}=c_{1}\left(\dfrac{mn_{k}}{p}-d_{k}\right)

On simplification, we then get:

c_{1}=\dfrac{\frac{an_{k}}{p}+b_{k}}{\frac{mn_{k}}{p}-d_{k}}=an_{k}+pb_{k}

where the second equality follows from (
12
). It is easy to see that
c_{1}
is an integer. Next, we equate the
i
th coordinate of the left and right hand sides of (
13
) for
i<k
. That gives:

\dfrac{an_{i}}{p}+b_{i}=\dfrac{mc_{1}n_{i}}{p}-c_{1}d_{i}+c_{i+1}

Substitute the expression obtained for
c_{1}
and simplify. We then get:

\dfrac{an_{i}}{p}+b_{i}=\dfrac{m(an_{k}+pb_{k})n_{i}}{p}-(an_{k}+pb_{k})d_{i}+c_{i+1}=\dfrac{amn_{i}n_{k}}{p}+mb_{k}n_{i}-(an_{k}+pb_{k})d_{i}+c_{i+1}

Writing the equation in terms of
c_{i+1}
gives:

\displaystyle c_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{an_{i}(1-mn_{k})}{p}+b_{i}-mb_{k}n_{i}+(an_{k}+pb_{k})d_{i}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{an_{i}(-pd_{k})}{p}+b_{i}-mb_{k}n_{i}+(an_{k}+pb_{k})d_{i}

\displaystyle=-an_{i}d_{k}+b_{i}-mb_{k}n_{i}+(an_{k}+pb_{k})d_{i}

where the second equality follows from (
12
). As before,
c_{i+1}
is an integer. Therefore, the result holds.
∎

Utilizing the basis in
22

\dfrac{1}{p}
. Since this volume is decreasing in
p
, one can expect that for a
good
basis of the lattice, the corresponding fundamental parallelepiped is contained in
\left[\frac{1}{k+1},\frac{k}{k+1}\right]^{k}
. Consequently, it can be shown that some translate of this fundamental parallelepiped is contained in
P(\mathbf{n})
.

7.
Conclusion

In this work, we revisited the
Lonely Runner Conjecture
, primarily through a polyhedral perspective. In addition to identifying new families of speed vectors that can be characterized as
lonely runner instances
, we propose a new sufficiency condition for a set of speeds to satisfy the conjecture.

There are several ways in which the conjecture has been or is being approached. However, more often than not, we have to deal with infinite sample spaces. We have tried to condense the sample space to a finite set through Conjecture
9
. We hope that this may provide promising new approaches to resolve the long standing conjecture.
