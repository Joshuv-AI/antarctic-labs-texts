Continuous Family of Equilibria of the 3D Axisymmetric Relativistic Vlasov-Maxwell System
Katherine Zhiyuan Zhang

Abstract
. We consider the relativistic Vlasov-Maxwell system (RVM) on a general axisymmetric spatial domain with perfect conducting boundary which reflects particles specularly, assuming axisymmetry in the problem. We construct continuous global parametric solution sets for the time-independent RVM. The solutions in these sets have arbitrarily large electromagnetic field and the particle density functions have the form f ± = μ ± ​ ( e ± ​ ( x , v ) , p ± ​ ( x , v ) ) f^{\pm}=\mu^{\pm}(e^{\pm}(x,v),p^{\pm}(x,v)) , where e ± e^{\pm} and p ± p^{\pm} are the particle energy and angular momentum, respectively. In particular, for a certain class of examples, we show that the spectral stability changes as the parameter varies from 0 0 to ∞ \infty .



1.
Introduction

We consider the 3D relativistic Vlasov-Maxwell (RVM) system on an axisymmetric bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We study a plasma with two species (ion and electron) with non-negative distribution functions
f^{\pm}(t,x,v)
, where
t\geq 0
,
x\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The system RVM is

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f^{\pm}+\hat{v}\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{\pm}\pm(\textbf{E}+\hat{v}\times\textbf{B})\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{\pm}=0,

(1.2)

\nabla_{x}\cdot\textbf{E}=\rho=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(f^{+}-f^{-})dv,\ \nabla_{x}\cdot\textbf{B}=0,

(1.3)

\partial_{t}\textbf{E}-\nabla_{x}\times\textbf{B}=-\textbf{j}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\hat{v}(f^{+}-f^{-})dv,\ \partial_{t}\textbf{B}+\nabla_{x}\times\textbf{E}=0.

In this system,
f^{\pm}(t,x,v)\geq 0
is the density distribution of ions (
+
) and electrons (
-
). We consider the plasma in a region
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, so that
x\in\Omega
is the particle position.
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the particle momentum,
\langle v\rangle=\sqrt{1+v^{2}}
is the particle kinetic energy, and
\hat{v}=v/\langle v\rangle
is the particle velocity. Also,
E
is the electric field,
B
is the internal magnetic field, so
\pm(\textbf{E}+\hat{v}\times\textbf{B})
is the total (internal) electromagnetic force. Moreover, the charge density
\rho
and the current density
j
are defined as

(1.4)

\rho=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(f^{+}-f^{-})dv,\qquad\textbf{j}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\hat{v}(f^{+}-f^{-})dv.

A key direction of study is to construct the stationary solutions (equilibria) of the RVM system on a bounded domain with varied boundary conditions.

In this paper, at the boundary we impose the specular condition (which means that
f^{\pm}
is even with respect to
v_{n}=v\cdot e_{n}
on
\partial\Omega
, with
e_{n}
being the outward normal vector of
\partial\Omega
at
x
):

(1.5)

f^{\pm}(t,x,v)=f^{\pm}(t,x,v-2(v\cdot e_{n}(x))e_{n}(x)),e_{n}(x)\cdot v<0,\ \forall x\in\partial\Omega,

as well as the perfect conductor boundary condition

(1.6)

\textbf{E}(t,x)\times n(x)=0,\ \textbf{B}(t,x)\cdot n(x)=0,\ \forall x\in\partial\Omega.

We assume axisymmetry in the problem. Therefore it is natural to use cylindrical coordinates here.

We aim to construct equilibrium solutions for the system (
1.1
) – (
1.3
) with the boundary conditions above. We consider equilibria with the form

f^{\pm}=\mu^{\pm}(e^{\pm}(x,v),p^{\pm}(x,v))

where
e^{\pm}
and
p^{\pm}
are the particle energy and angular momentum, respectively, and use a potential formulation together with the axisymmetry assumption of (
1.1
) – (
1.3
) so as to the equations satisfied by the equilibria can be reformulated as a coupled elliptic system (see (
2.12
) – (
2.14
) below). The resulting elliptic system is highly nonlinear on its right hand side.

Literature review

Investigation for time-independent RVM on a domain with physical boundaries has been carried out over the years. Rein

[

constructed large stationary solutions for problems with a specific type of axial symmetric domain. Poupaud
[

[

considered a specific parametrized family of (single species) particle density distribution, and gave sufficient conditions for the existence of bifurcation points corresponding to distribution functions of a certain form. Moreover, the lower dimensional models have also been considered. For example, Schaeffer
[

]
studied the stationary Vlasov-Maxwell equation (VM) in the 1.5D setting, when the spatial domain being the real line

, see also Guo
[

The RVM with the presence of an external magnetic field is also of physical concern. This type of problem is considered in Ben-Artzi-Holding

[

[

]

[

On the other hand, in plasma theory, an important goal is to study the stability properties of plasmas.
A pioneer result is the renowned Penrose’s sharp criterion derived in
[
19
]
, which is a sharp criterion on linear stability for a spatially homogeneous equilibrium of the Vlasov-Poisson system. In the subsequent papers
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
and
[

, analysis of the linear stability of a spatially inhomogeneous equilibrium was carried out in domains without spatial boundaries (i.e. whole space or periodic setting). The question of nonlinear stability is much more difficult, see, for example,
[
15
]
. Moreover, a topic of physical importance is to understand the stability properties of a confined plasma.
[
11
]
discussed the confinement of a tokamak plasma is discussed using some fluid models. For the microscopic model RVM in bounded domains, few rigorous studies are available.
[

studied the instability for confined RVM with symmetry on unbounded domains. In
[

1.2.
Description of main results

1.2.1.
Construction of global sets of equilibria

The main purpose of the paper is to construct equilibrium solutions for the system (
1.1
) – (
1.3
). Our strategy is to consider parametrized families of particle density distribution function for the equilibria, and to apply the global implicit function theorem (Theorem
C.2
) so as to obtain global solution sets that branch out from ”trivial” equilibrium solutions. The result is stated in Theorem
3.2
. Similar methods have been applied in varied PDEs, see, for example,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
27
]
.

We consider a general parametrized family of particle density distribution
f^{\pm}=\mu^{\pm}(e^{\pm}(x,v),p^{\pm}(x,v))
, and construct a
global continuum/loop
of equilibria branching out from the trivial or neutral steady states for the RVM on a general axisymmetric spatial domain with perfect conducting boundary which reflects particles specularly, assuming axisymmetry in the problem. Moreover, in the single species case, with some additional constraints, the global continuum/loop of equilibria we constructed contains a locally analytic curve that is
unbounded
, see Theorem
3.3
.

Our approach enables us to observe the structure of the solution set, and reveal more information about the change of the electromagnetic potential (and therefore the electromagnetic field) as the density distribution function changes in different ways. In the case when the solution set is unbounded, we make use of the maximum principles and elliptic estimates to investigate size properties of the equilibria constructed in Theorem
3.2
. In particular, we construct a continuum of equilibrium solutions with arbitrarily large electromagnetic field, see Proposition
4.2
. It is an open question whether the solutions obtained in this paper are the same as the ones constructed in the historical literature
[
22
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
26
]
, etc.. Our approach can also be applied in the case when an external magnetic field is present, see Remark
2.2
.

1.2.2.
Spectral stability property of equilibria in the solution set

We also investigate the change of spectral stability property along a family of solutions constructed in Theorem
3.2
using the result obtained in
[
28
]
. Specifically, we track down the solutions along the solution set as the parameter
K
changes from
0
to
\infty
, and show that the corresponding equilibrium solutions gradually become spectrally unstable as
K
grows large. The details are given in Section
5
, Proposition
5.3
and Theorem
5.4
.

1.2.3.
Example

In addition, we provide a single species example on which the results above can be applied, where the ion density distribution function is parametrized as

\mu^{K,+}(e,p)=a^{+}(K)\mu^{\pm}(e,Kp),

where
K\in[0,\infty)
is the parameter,
a^{+}(K)
and
\mu^{+}(e,p)
are fixed non-negative functions (of
K
and
(e,p)
, respectively) satisfying certain conditions. Unbounded solution sets of single species equilibria can then be obtained, solutions in these sets have arbitrarily large electromagnetic field (as
K
gets large). Moreover, the equilibria turn from spectrally stable to unstable as the parameter
K
changes from
0
to
\infty
.

Set up

Let us introduce the basic settings of the stationary RVM problem. We consider the 3D relativistic Vlasov-Maxwell (RVM) system (
1.1
) – (
1.3
) on the axisymmetric bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with axisymmetry in the problem, and use cylindrical coordinates

\Omega
is rotational invariant around the

\Omega

\mathcal{C}
in the plane
\{\varphi=0\}

\beta

(2.1)

r=\tilde{r}(\beta),\quad z=\tilde{z}(\beta),\quad\beta\in[0,1],\quad\tilde{r}>0\ \text{for all}\ \beta\in(0,1),\quad\tilde{r},\tilde{z}\in C^{1}(0,1),

with either

(2.2)

\tilde{r}(0)=\tilde{r}(1)>0,\quad\tilde{z}(0)=\tilde{z}(1),\quad\tilde{z}^{\prime}(0)=\tilde{z}^{\prime}(1),

or,

(2.3)

\tilde{r}(0)=\tilde{r}(1)=0,\quad\tilde{z}^{\prime}/\tilde{r}^{\prime}=0\ \text{at}\ \beta=0,1.

Rotating
\mathcal{C}
around the
z
-axis gives the boundary for the domain
\partial\Omega
.
In all these cases, the boundary
\partial\Omega
is
C^{1}
smooth. We denote

d:=\sup_{x\in\Omega}r(x).

Then
d<+\infty
.

Let
e_{r}
,
e_{\varphi}
and
e_{z}
be the unit vectors in the cylindrical coordinate system (see Appendix
A
), and
e_{n}
,
e_{tg}
to be the unit vectors in outward normal direction and tangential direction orthogonal to

on
\partial\Omega

\partial\Omega

e_{n}(x)=(\tilde{z}^{\prime}e_{r}-\tilde{r}^{\prime}e_{z})/\sqrt{\tilde{z}^{\prime 2}+\tilde{r}^{\prime 2}}
, and
e_{tg}(x)=(-\tilde{r}^{\prime}e_{r}-\tilde{z}^{\prime}e_{z})/\sqrt{\tilde{z}^{\prime 2}+\tilde{r}^{\prime 2}}

On the boundary, we assume the specular condition on the density function
f^{\pm}
in case the particle hits the boundary:

(2.4)

f^{\pm}(t,x,v)=f^{\pm}(t,x,v-2(v\cdot e_{n}(x))e_{n}(x)),\ e_{n}(x)\cdot v<0,\forall x\in\partial\Omega,\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

For the electric and magnetic fields, we put down the perfect conductor boundary condition:

(2.5)

\textbf{E}(t,x)\times e_{n}(x)=0,\quad\textbf{B}(t,x)\cdot e_{n}(x)=0,\forall x\in\partial\Omega.

We introduce the electric potential
\phi
and the magnetic potential
A
:

(2.6)

\textbf{E}=-\nabla\phi-\partial_{t}\textbf{A},\quad\textbf{B}=\nabla\times\textbf{A}

and impose the Coulomb gauge

(2.7)

\nabla\cdot\textbf{A}=0.

Remark 2.1
.

The choices of
\phi
and
A
are not unique. In fact, the choice of
A
can differ by the gradient of a harmonic function.

We use the cylindrical coordinates
(r,\varphi,z)
, and make the axisymmetry assumption:

(2.8)

\phi,\ A_{r},\ A_{z},\ A_{\varphi}\ \text{are independent of}\ \varphi.

Therefore,
f^{\pm}
does not depend
explicitly
on
\varphi
, although it might depend on it
implicitly
through the components of
v
. The Maxwell system can be reformulated as

(2.9)

-\Delta\phi=\rho,

(2.10)

(\partial_{t}^{2}-\Delta+\frac{1}{r^{2}})A_{\varphi}=j_{\varphi},

(2.11)

(\partial_{t}^{2}-\Delta)\tilde{\textbf{A}}+\partial_{t}\nabla\phi=\tilde{\textbf{j}},

where
\tilde{\textbf{A}}:=A_{r}e_{r}+A_{z}e_{z}
.

We consider an equilibrium field, with the potential denoted by
(\phi^{0},A_{\varphi}^{0},\tilde{\textbf{A}}^{0})
:

(2.12)

-\Delta\phi^{0}=\rho^{0},

(2.13)

(-\Delta+\frac{1}{r^{2}})A_{\varphi}^{0}=j_{\varphi}^{0},

(2.14)

(-\Delta)\tilde{\textbf{A}}^{0}=\tilde{\textbf{j}}^{0}.

We assume that the magnetic potential
\textbf{A}^{0}
satisfies

\textbf{A}^{0}=A^{0}_{\varphi}e_{\varphi},

which implies

B^{0}_{\varphi}=0.

The time-independent Maxwell system (
2.12
) – (
2.14
) is now reduced to

(2.15)

-\Delta\phi^{0}=\rho^{0},

(2.16)

(-\Delta+\frac{1}{r^{2}})A_{\varphi}^{0}=j_{\varphi}^{0}.

According to (
2.5
), we assume the following boundary conditions for the potentials, Section 4 in
[
28
]
for details:

(2.17)

\phi^{0}=0,\qquad A_{\varphi}^{0}=0,\qquad A_{tg}^{0}=0,\qquad\tilde{z}^{\prime}\partial_{r}A_{n}^{0}-\tilde{r}^{\prime}\partial_{z}A_{n}^{0}+\frac{\tilde{z}^{\prime}}{\tilde{r}}A_{n}^{0}=0,\qquad\forall x\in\partial\Omega.

Then the equilibrium field is

(2.18)

\textbf{E}^{0}=-\nabla\phi^{0}=-\frac{\partial\phi^{0}}{\partial r}e_{r}-\frac{\partial\phi^{0}}{\partial z}e_{z},

(2.19)

\begin{split}\textbf{B}^{0}&=-\frac{\partial A^{0}_{\varphi}}{\partial z}e_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(rA^{0}_{\varphi})}{\partial r}e_{z}.\\
\end{split}

The boundary condition (
2.17
) then becomes

(2.20)

\phi^{0}=0,\qquad A_{\varphi}^{0}=0,\qquad\forall x\in\partial\Omega.

We define the particle trajectories as

(2.21)

\dot{X}^{\pm}=\hat{V}^{\pm},\ \dot{V}^{\pm}=\pm\textbf{E}^{0}(X^{\pm})\pm\hat{V}^{\pm}\times\textbf{B}^{0}(X^{\pm})

with initial values
(X^{\pm}(0;x,v),V^{\pm}(0;x,v))=(x,v)

v_{tg}=v\cdot e_{tg}=\frac{-\tilde{z}^{\prime}v_{z}-\tilde{r}^{\prime}v_{r}}{\sqrt{\tilde{z}^{\prime 2}+\tilde{r}^{\prime 2}}}

and

with

v_{*}=-v_{n}e_{n}+v_{tg}e_{tg}+v_{\varphi}e_{\varphi}\ ,\ V^{\pm}_{*}=-V^{\pm}_{n}e_{n}+V^{\pm}_{tg}e_{tg}+V^{\pm}_{\varphi}e_{\varphi}.

Thus from the specular boundary condition, the trajectory can be continued by the rule

(2.23)

(X^{\pm}(s_{0}+;x,v),V^{\pm}(s_{0}+;x,v))=(X^{\pm}(s_{0}-;x,v),V_{*}^{\pm}(s_{0}-;x,v)).

Furthermore, we consider the following invariants along the particle trajectories given by (
2.21
)

(2.24)

\begin{split}&e^{\pm}(x,v)=\langle v\rangle\pm\phi^{0}(r,z),\ p^{\pm}(x,v)=r(v_{\varphi}\pm A^{0}_{\varphi}(r,z)).\\
\end{split}

The proof of the invariance of

\end{split}

3.
Global continuation

In this section, we consider the system (
2.15
) – (
2.16
). Let us consider a parametrized family of functions
\{\mu^{K,\pm}(e,p)\}_{K\in[0,+\infty)}
, which satisfy

Assumption 3.1
.

We assume the following:

1)
\mu^{K,\pm}(e,p)
are non-negative
C^{1}
functions, and

(3.1)

\begin{split}&|\mu^{K,\pm}(e,p)|+|\mu^{K,\pm}_{p}(e,p)|+|\mu^{K,\pm}_{e}(e,p)|\leq\frac{C_{\mu}(K)}{1+|e|^{\delta}},\ \delta>3.\\
\end{split}

2)
\mu^{K,\pm}(e,p)

K
in the
C^{1}
sense, i.e.
\frac{\partial\mu^{K,\pm}(e,p)}{\partial K}\in C^{0}
. Moreover,
\frac{\partial\mu^{K,\pm}(e,p)}{\partial K}|_{K=0}=0
.

3)
\mu^{0,+}(e,p)=\mu^{0,-}(e,p)=M^{0}(e,p)
for some non-negative function

satisfying the decay assumption (
3.1
).

4) The matrix operator

(3.2)

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\tilde{J}_{11}\ \tilde{J}_{12}\\
\tilde{J}_{21}\ \tilde{J}_{22}\\
\end{array}\right):\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{2}(\Omega)

is bounded with a bounded inverse. Here

(3.3)

\begin{split}&\tilde{J}_{11}(\delta u)=\delta u-(-\Delta+\frac{1}{r^{2}})^{-1}\big\{f_{1}(r)\delta u\big\},\\

\begin{split}\langle\mathcal{A}^{0,K}_{2}h,h\rangle_{L^{2}}&=1-\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{-1}p^{K,+}\mu^{K,+}_{p}(e^{K,+},p^{K,+})|h|^{2}dvdx\\
&+\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\mu^{K,+}_{p}(e^{K,+},p^{K,+})}{\langle v\rangle}rA^{K,0}_{\varphi}|h|^{2}dvdx+\|\mathcal{P}^{K,+}(\hat{v}_{\varphi}h)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{K,+}}\\
&:=1+I+II+III.\\
\end{split}

We are going to estimate the term
I
, and show that when
K
is large enough, it remains negative and dominates all the others. We compute

(5.18)

\begin{split}I&\leq-C^{\prime}_{\mu}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{-1}K^{1+m-\epsilon}|p^{K,+}|\langle p^{K,+}\rangle^{-\epsilon}\nu(e^{K,+})dv\ |h|^{2}dx\\
&\leq-C^{\prime}_{\mu}K^{1+m-\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}\int_{|p^{K,+}(x,v)|>1}\langle v\rangle^{-1}|p^{K,+}|^{1-\epsilon}\cdot 2^{-\epsilon/2}\nu(e^{K,+})dv\ |h|^{2}dx\\
&\leq-C^{\prime}_{\mu}K^{1+m-\epsilon}M,\\
\end{split}

where

M=2^{-\epsilon/2}\int_{r(x)\geq 1}\inf_{x\in\Omega,r(x)\geq 1}\Big[\int_{\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{3},|p^{K,+}(x,v)|>1\}}\langle v\rangle^{-1}|v_{\varphi}+A^{K,0}_{\varphi}(r,z)|^{1-\epsilon}\nu(e^{K,+})dv\Big]r^{1-\epsilon}|h|^{2}dx\ .

Noting that
\|(\phi^{K,0},A^{K,0}_{\varphi})\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim K^{-1+m}\leq 1/2
when
K
is large, we have

(5.19)

\begin{split}M&=2^{-\epsilon/2}\int_{r(x)\geq 1}\inf_{x\in\Omega,r(x)\geq 1}\Big[\int_{\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{3},|p^{K,+}(x,v)|>1\}}\langle v\rangle^{-1}|v_{\varphi}+A^{K,0}_{\varphi}(r,z)|^{1-\epsilon}\nu(e^{K,+})dv\Big]r^{1-\epsilon}|h|^{2}dx\\
&\geq 2^{-\epsilon/2}\int_{r(x)\geq 1}\inf_{x\in\Omega,r(x)\geq 1}\Big[\int_{\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{3},|v_{\varphi}|>2+\frac{1}{r(x)}\}}\langle v\rangle^{-1}(1+\frac{1}{r(x)})^{1-\epsilon}\nu(\langle v\rangle+\phi^{K,0})dv\Big]r^{1-\epsilon}|h|^{2}dx\\
&\geq 2^{-\epsilon/2}\int_{r(x)\geq 1}\inf_{x\in\Omega,r(x)\geq 1}\Big[\int_{\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{3},|v_{\varphi}|>3\}}\langle v\rangle^{-1}\nu(\langle v\rangle+\phi^{K,0})dv\Big]r^{1-\epsilon}|h|^{2}dx\\
&\geq 2^{-\epsilon/2}C_{\nu}\int_{r(x)\geq 1}\inf_{x\in\Omega,r(x)\geq 1}\Big[\int_{\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{3},|v_{\varphi}|>3\}}\langle v\rangle^{-1}\exp(-\langle v\rangle-\phi^{K,0})dv\Big]r^{1-\epsilon}|h|^{2}dx\\
&\geq 2^{-\epsilon/2}C_{\nu}\cdot 2\exp(-\frac{1}{2})\int_{r(x)\geq 1}\int_{\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{3},|v_{\varphi}|>3\}}\langle v\rangle^{-1}\exp(-\langle v\rangle)dvr^{1-\epsilon}|h|^{2}dx\\
&\geq 2^{-\epsilon/2}C_{\nu}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\int_{r(x)\geq 1}r^{1-\epsilon}|h|^{2}dx\\
&\geq 2^{-\epsilon/2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}d^{-\epsilon}C_{\nu}\int_{r(x)\geq 1}r|h|^{2}dx,\\
\end{split}

where
d=\sup_{x\in\Omega}r(x)>1
. From Remark 1, we have
|\nu(e^{K,+})|\leq\frac{\langle p^{K,+}\rangle^{\epsilon}}{C(1+|e^{K,+}|^{\delta})}
with
\delta>4
, which ensures that the integrals involved here are convergent. Hence

I\leq-H_{1}C_{1}C_{\nu}C^{\prime}_{\mu}K^{1+m-\epsilon},

where
H_{1}=\int_{r\geq 1}r|h|^{2}dx
,
C_{1}=2^{-1-\epsilon/2}b^{-\epsilon}
.

Let us then consider
II
.
II=0
if
A^{K,0}_{\varphi}=0
. If
A^{K,0}_{\varphi}\neq 0
, we have

\begin{split}II&\leq 20\pi b^{2}C_{\mu}(H_{1}+H_{2})\|A^{K,0}_{\varphi}\|_{L^{\infty}}\sup_{x}(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{-1}|\mu^{K,+}_{p}(e^{K,+},p^{K,+})|dv).\\
\end{split}

Using Lemma
5.9
and Assumption
5.1
, we estimate

(5.20)

\begin{split}II&\leq 20\pi b^{2}C_{\mu}(H_{1}+H_{2})C_{\mu}K^{-1+m}\cdot K^{1+m}\sup_{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{\langle v\rangle(1+|e^{K,+}|^{\delta})}dv\\
&\leq 20\pi b^{2}C_{\mu}(H_{1}+H_{2})C_{\mu}K^{2m}\sup_{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{\langle v\rangle(1+|\langle v\rangle+\phi^{K,0}(x)|^{\delta})}dv(1+K^{(-1+m)\delta})\\
&\leq 40\cdot 2^{\delta}\pi b^{2}(H_{1}+H_{2})C_{\mu}^{2}K^{2m}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{1+\langle v\rangle^{\delta}}dv\\
&\leq 40\cdot 2^{\delta}\pi b^{2}(H_{1}+H_{2})C_{\mu}^{2}K^{2m}\cdot 3\pi\\
&\leq 120\cdot 2^{\delta}\pi^{2}b^{2}(H_{1}+H_{2})C_{\mu}^{2}K^{2m}\\
\end{split}

where
H_{2}=\int_{\Omega}|h|^{2}dx=\|h\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
. Therefore the term
II
is
O(K^{2m})
.

For
III=\|\mathcal{P}^{K,+}(\hat{v}_{\varphi}h)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{K,+}}
, we use Assumption
5.1
to estimate

(5.21)

\begin{split}III=\|\mathcal{P}^{K,+}(\hat{v}_{\varphi}h)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{K,+}}&\leq\int_{\Omega}\sup_{v}|\mathcal{P}^{K,+}(\hat{v}_{\varphi}h)|^{2}dx\sup_{x}(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\mu^{K,+}_{e}(e^{K,+},p^{K,+})|dv)\\
&\leq K^{m}H_{2}\sup_{x}(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{C_{\mu}}{1+|\langle v\rangle+\phi^{K,0}(x)|^{\delta}}dv)\\
&\leq 2^{\delta}H_{2}C_{\mu}\cdot 2K^{m}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{1+\langle v\rangle^{\delta}}dv\\
&\leq 2^{\delta}H_{2}C_{2}C_{\mu}K^{m}.\\
\end{split}

Here
C_{2}=\frac{8}{3}\pi+\frac{4}{\delta-1}\pi^{2}
. Therefore
III=O(K^{m})
.

Combining together all the estimates above, as well as Lemma
5.8
, we conclude

(5.22)

\begin{split}&\langle\mathcal{L}^{0,K}h,h\rangle_{L^{2}}\\
&\leq 1-H_{1}C_{1}C_{\nu}C^{\prime}_{\mu}K^{1+m-\epsilon}+120\cdot 2^{\delta}\pi^{2}b^{2}(H_{1}+H_{2})C_{\mu}^{2}K^{2m}+2^{\delta}H_{2}C_{2}C_{\mu}K^{m}\\
&+256\pi^{2}c_{P}C_{\mu}^{2}H_{2}K^{2m}.\\
\end{split}

Hence
(\mu^{K,\pm},\phi^{K,0},A_{\varphi}^{K,0})
is spectrally unstable if
C_{\mu}
,
K
and
h
satisfies (
5.11
).

∎

Appendix A
Derivative Operators in the Cylindrical Coordinates

For the readers’ convenience, we provide the information of the derivatives under the cylindrical coordinates. Using
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
to denote the Cartesian coordinates and
(r,\varphi,z)
to be the cylindrical coordinates, we have

x_{1}=r\cos\varphi,\qquad x_{2}=r\sin\varphi,\qquad x_{3}=z

and therefore

e_{r}=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi,0),\qquad e_{\varphi}=(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi,0),\qquad e_{z}=(0,0,1).

Hence for any function
f(r,\varphi,z)
and any vector field
\textbf{A}(r,\varphi,z)
, we have

\nabla_{x}f=\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}e_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\varphi}e_{\varphi}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}e_{z},

\Delta_{x}f=\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(r\frac{\partial f}{\partial r})+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\varphi^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial z^{2}},

\nabla_{x}\cdot\textbf{A}=\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(rA_{r})}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial A_{\varphi}}{\partial\varphi}+\frac{\partial A_{z}}{\partial z},

\nabla_{x}\times\textbf{A}=(\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial A_{z}}{\partial\varphi}-\frac{\partial A_{\varphi}}{\partial z})e_{r}+(\frac{\partial A_{r}}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial A_{z}}{\partial r})e_{\varphi}+\frac{1}{r}(\frac{\partial(rA_{\varphi})}{\partial r}-\frac{\partial A_{r}}{\partial\varphi})e_{z}.

Appendix B
Key Invariants

In this section, we prove the invariance of
e^{\pm}(x,v)=\langle v\rangle\pm\phi^{0}(r,z)
and
p^{\pm}(x,v)=r(v_{\varphi}\pm A^{0}_{\varphi}(r,z))
(or
p^{\pm}(x,v)=r(v_{\varphi}\pm A^{0}_{\varphi}(r,z)\pm A_{\varphi,ext}(r,z))
when an external magnetic potential
\textbf{A}_{ext}=A_{\varphi,ext}(r,z)e_{\varphi}
is present) along the particle trajectories given in Section
2
:

(B.1)

\begin{split}&\dot{X}^{\pm}=\hat{V}^{\pm},\ \dot{V}^{\pm}=\pm\textbf{E}^{0}(X^{\pm})\pm\hat{V}^{\pm}\times\textbf{B}^{0}(X^{\pm}).\\
\end{split}

(Or

(B.2)

\dot{X}^{\pm}=\hat{V}^{\pm},\ \dot{V}^{\pm}=\pm\textbf{E}^{0}(X^{\pm})\pm\hat{V}^{\pm}\times\textbf{B}^{0}(X^{\pm})\pm\hat{V}^{\pm}\times\textbf{B}_{ext}(X^{\pm})

when the external magnetic potential is present.)

We only compute the

(B.3)

\begin{split}\dot{e}&=\hat{V}\cdot\dot{V}+\dot{X}\cdot\nabla\phi^{0}\\
&=\hat{V}\cdot(\textbf{E}^{0}+\hat{V}\times\textbf{B}^{0})+\hat{V}\cdot\nabla\phi^{0}\\
&=\hat{V}\cdot(-\nabla\phi^{0}+\hat{V}\times\textbf{B}^{0})+\hat{V}\cdot\nabla\phi^{0}\\
&=0,\end{split}

(B.4)

\begin{split}\dot{p}&=\dot{X}\cdot\nabla_{X}p+\dot{V}\cdot\nabla_{V}p\\
&=\dot{X}\cdot\nabla_{X}(r(v_{\varphi}+A^{0}_{\varphi}(r,z)))+\dot{V}\cdot\nabla_{V}(r(v_{\varphi}+A^{0}_{\varphi}(r,z)))\\
&=\dot{X}\cdot\nabla_{X}(rv_{\varphi})+\dot{X}\cdot\nabla_{X}(rA^{0}_{\varphi})+\dot{V}\cdot r\nabla_{V}v_{\varphi}\\
&=\dot{X}\cdot\nabla_{X}(rv_{\varphi})+\dot{X}\cdot A^{0}_{\varphi}e_{r}+\dot{X}\cdot r\frac{\partial A^{0}_{\varphi}}{\partial r}e_{r}+\dot{X}\cdot r\frac{\partial A^{0}_{\varphi}}{\partial z}e_{z}+r(\textbf{E}^{0}+\hat{V}\times\textbf{B}^{0})\cdot e_{\varphi}\\
&=\dot{X}\cdot\nabla_{X}(rv_{\varphi})+\dot{X}\cdot A^{0}_{\varphi}e_{r}+\dot{X}\cdot r\frac{\partial A^{0}_{\varphi}}{\partial r}e_{r}+\dot{X}\cdot r\frac{\partial A^{0}_{\varphi}}{\partial z}e_{z}\\
&+r(-\nabla\phi^{0}+\dot{X}\times\frac{\partial A^{0}_{\varphi}}{\partial z}e_{r}+\dot{X}\times\frac{1}{r}A^{0}_{\varphi}e_{z}+\dot{X}\times\frac{\partial A^{0}_{\varphi}}{\partial r}e_{z})\cdot e_{\varphi}\\
&=\dot{X}\cdot\nabla_{X}(rv_{\varphi})+\dot{X}\cdot A^{0}_{\varphi}e_{r}+\dot{X}\cdot r\frac{\partial A^{0}_{\varphi}}{\partial r}e_{r}+\dot{X}\cdot r\frac{\partial A^{0}_{\varphi}}{\partial z}e_{z}-r\frac{\partial A^{0}_{\varphi}}{\partial z}\dot{X}\cdot e_{z}-A^{0}_{\varphi}\dot{X}\cdot e_{r}-r\frac{\partial A^{0}_{\varphi}}{\partial r}\dot{X}\cdot e_{r}\\
&=\dot{X}\cdot\nabla_{X}(rv_{\varphi}).\\
\end{split}

(Here
r
,
\varphi
,
z
,
v_{r}
,
v_{\varphi}
,
v_{z}
are components of
X
and
V
.) In the computation above, we used that
\nabla\phi^{0}
does not have
e_{\varphi}
-component. We evaluate
\dot{X}\cdot\nabla_{X}(rv_{\varphi})
using the Cartesian coordinates:

(B.5)

\begin{split}\dot{X}\cdot\nabla_{X}(rv_{\varphi})&=\dot{X}\cdot\nabla_{X}(rV\cdot(-\frac{X_{2}}{r},\frac{X_{1}}{r},0))\\
&=\dot{X}\cdot\nabla_{X}(-X_{2}V_{1}+X_{1}V_{2})\\
&=\frac{1}{\langle V\rangle}(V_{1},V_{2},V_{3})\cdot(V_{2},-V_{1},0).\\
&=0\end{split}

Hence
\dot{p}=0
. Moreover, one can show in a similar way that
p(x,v)=r(v_{\varphi}+A^{0}_{\varphi}(r,z)+A_{\varphi,ext}(r,z))
is invariant along the particle trajectories satisfying the ODE
\dot{X}=\hat{V}
,
\dot{V}=\textbf{E}^{0}(X)+\hat{V}\times\textbf{B}^{0}(X)+\hat{V}\times\textbf{B}_{ext}(X)
. The invariance along the particle trajectories is now verified.

Moreover, we introduce the following maximum-principle-type result on elliptic equations.

Lemma C.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a
C^{1}
bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(
N\leq 4
). Assume
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
satisfies

(C.13)

\begin{split}&-\Delta u\geq\alpha(x)\zeta(u),\ \text{for}\ x\in\Omega,\\
&u\geq 0,\ \text{for}\ x\in\partial\Omega,\\
\end{split}

where
\alpha
is a non-negative function and
\zeta:[0,a_{\zeta})\rightarrow[0,+\infty)

(0<a_{\zeta}\leq+\infty)
is a non-decreasing
C^{1}
function such that
\zeta(0)>0
. Let
F(t):=\int^{t}_{0}\frac{ds}{\zeta(s)}
,
d_{\Omega}(x):=dist(x,\partial\Omega)
. Then

(C.14)

F(u(x))\geq\alpha_{x}(d_{\Omega}(x))\frac{d_{\Omega}(x)^{2}}{2N}\ \text{for all}\ x\in\Omega\text{ (a.e.) },

and equivalently (since
F(t)
is a non-decreasing function)

(C.15)

u(x)\geq F^{-1}(\alpha_{x}(d_{\Omega}(x))\frac{d_{\Omega}(x)^{2}}{2N})\ \text{for all}\ x\in\Omega\text{ (a.e.) }.

Here
\alpha_{x}(r):=\inf_{y\in B_{r}(x)}\alpha(y)
,
B_{r}(x)=\{y:|y-x|<r\}
.

Proof.

The proof can be adapted from Theorem 2.2 in
[
1
]
.
Let
\psi_{\alpha}
be the unique solution of the equation

\begin{split}&-\Delta u=\alpha(x),\ \text{for}\ x\in\Omega,\\
&u=0,\ \text{for}\ x\in\partial\Omega,\\
\end{split}

where
\alpha
is a non-negative function. Denote

\alpha_{x}(r):=\inf_{y\in B_{r}(x)}\alpha(y)\quad(0<r\leq d_{\Omega}(x)=dist(x,\partial\Omega))

Using the product rule for the divergence of product of a scalar valued function and a vector field,
F^{\prime}>0
and
F^{\prime\prime}\leq 0
we compute

\begin{split}\Delta F(u)&=div(\nabla F(u))=div(F^{\prime}(u)\nabla u)\\
&=\nabla(F^{\prime}(u))\cdot\nabla u+F^{\prime}(u)div(\nabla u)\\
&=F^{\prime\prime}(u)\nabla u\cdot\nabla u+F^{\prime}(u)\Delta u\\
&=F^{\prime\prime}(u)|\nabla u|^{2}+F^{\prime}(u)\Delta u\leq F^{\prime}(u)\Delta u.\\
\end{split}

Hence
-\Delta F(u)\geq F^{\prime}(u)(-\Delta u)
. Moreover,

\begin{split}D_{i}D_{j}F(u)&=D_{i}(F^{\prime}(u)D_{j}u)=F^{\prime\prime}(u)D_{i}uD_{j}u+F^{\prime}(u)D_{i}D_{j}u.\\
\end{split}

Notice that since
\zeta(s)
is in
C^{1}
,
F(t)=\int^{t}_{0}\frac{ds}{\zeta(s)}
is in
C^{2}
. Hence
F^{\prime}(u)D_{i}D_{j}u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Also,
D_{i}u
,
D_{j}u\in L^{4}(\Omega)
by Sobolev embedding, so
F^{\prime\prime}(u)D_{i}uD_{j}u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
by Hölder’s inequality. Thus,
F(u)\in H^{2}(\Omega)
.

Now, from
-\Delta u\geq\alpha(x)\zeta(u)
, we have

\begin{split}-\Delta F(u)\geq F^{\prime}(u)(-\Delta u)=\frac{1}{\zeta(u)}(-\Delta u)\geq\alpha(x)=-\Delta\psi_{\alpha}.\end{split}

Since we have
F(u)=\psi_{\alpha}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, by the maximum principle (see, for example,
[
10
]
, Theorem 8.1), we obtain
F(u(x))\geq\psi_{\alpha}(x)
in
\Omega
.

To estimate
\psi_{\alpha}
, we notice that for
y\in B_{d_{\Omega}(x)}(x)
,

-\Delta\psi_{\alpha}(y)=\alpha(y)\geq\alpha_{x}(d_{\Omega}(x)).

Consider the function

w(y)=\frac{d_{\Omega}(x)^{2}-|x-y|^{2}}{2N}.

This function satisfies
-\Delta w=1
in
B_{d_{\Omega}(x)}(x)
and
w=0
on
\partial B_{d_{\Omega}(x)}(x)
. Hence

-\Delta\psi_{\alpha}(y)\geq-\Delta(\alpha_{x}(d_{\Omega}(x))w(y)).

By maximum principle,

\psi_{\alpha}(y)\geq\alpha_{x}(d_{\Omega}(x))w(y).

Combining the results above, we obtain

F(u(x))\geq\alpha_{x}(d_{\Omega}(x))\frac{d_{\Omega}(x)^{2}-|x-y|^{2}}{2N}\ \text{if}\ |y-x|<d_{\Omega}(x).

Taking
x=y
, we have

F(u(x))\geq\alpha_{x}(d_{\Omega}(x))\frac{d_{\Omega}(x)^{2}}{2N}\ \text{for all}\ x\in\Omega\text{ (a.e.) }.
