On Block Cholesky Decomposition for Sparse Inverse Covariance Estimation


Abstract
The modified Cholesky decomposition is popular for inverse covariance estimation, but often needs pre-specification on the full information of variable ordering. In this work, we propose a block Cholesky decomposition (BCD) for estimating inverse covariance matrix under the partial information of variable ordering, in the sense that the variables can be divided into several groups with available ordering among groups, but variables within each group have no orderings. The proposed BCD model provides a unified framework for several existing methods including the modified Cholesky decomposition and the Graphical lasso. By utilizing the partial information on variable ordering, the proposed BCD model guarantees the positive definiteness of the estimated matrix with statistically meaningful interpretation. Theoretical results are established under regularity conditions. Simulation and case studies are conducted to evaluate the proposed BCD model.

1
Introduction

The estimation of covariance and inverse covariance matrices is of fundamental importance in the multivariate statistics with a broad spectrum of applications,
such as linear discriminant analysis
(
Clemmensen et al. 2011
)
, portfolio optimization
(
Deng and Tsui 2013
)
, and assimilation
(
Nino-Ruiz et al. 2019
)
.
In high-dimensional data, sparse estimation of an inverse covariance matrix has specially attracted great attention,
since it is closely related to a graphical model for inferring the conditional independence between variables of multivariate normal data.
However, estimation of a large inverse covariance matrix often encounters two challenges.
First, the estimated matrix needs to be positive definite for the valid statistical inferences.
Second, the number of parameters in the model increases quickly in a quadratic order in terms of the matrix dimensionality.

Existing studies on the inverse covariance estimation in the literature generally fall into two categories.
Denote the random variables of interest by
\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})^{\prime}
with mean
0
for simplicity and covariance matrix
\Sigma
.
The first category needs the pre-specification on the full ordering information of variables
X
.
That is, the variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}
have a natural ordering, which typically occurs in longitudinal data, time series, spatial data, spectroscopy and so forth.
In this situation, banded or tapering estimation for the high-dimensional matrices has been developed
(
Bickel and Levina 2008
)
, requiring an assumption that the variables are becoming weakly correlated as their positions in the ordering are far away.
Although these methods are straightforward and easy in computation, the resultant estimates may not be positive definite.
A better technique for estimating the inverse covariance matrix in this category is the modified Cholesky decomposition (MCD) introduced by
Pourahmadi 1999
.
It not only guarantees the positive definiteness of the estimated matrix,
but also utilizes the information of variable ordering, leading to an accurate estimate.
Moreover, this decomposition has a meaningful regression interpretation, allowing the use of the regularization for sparse estimation
(
Huang et al. 2006
;
Kang and Wang 2021
)
.
That is, the sparse pattern in the Cholesky-based matrix estimates can be induced by the sparsity in the Cholesky factors through linear regressions in the decomposition.

The second category considers that the information of variable ordering is not available.
In this case, one strategy is to identify a proper variable ordering based on a certain data driven mechanism,
transforming it into the estimation problem of the first category.
For example,
Wagaman and Levina 2009
determined the variable ordering by the Isomap algorithm and proposed an Isoband matrix estimate.

Dellaportas and Pourahmadi 2012
suggested using BIC criterion to seek for the variable ordering before applying the MCD technique.

Rajaratnam and Salzman 2013
recovered the variable ordering via the best permutation algorithm, which is very efficient for autoregressive model.
However, a potential drawback is obvious that the accuracy of such estimates relies on the accuracy of the estimated orderings of variables.
Furthermore, some real data, for example gene data and medical data, practically may not have a natural variable ordering,
which means that it is not adequate to find a variable ordering for such data.
Alternatively, another strategy in this category is to consider a permutation invariant estimation for inverse covariance matrix.
One popular method is the Graphical lasso (Glasso) proposed by
Meinshausen and Bühlmann 2006
and
Yuan and Lin 2007
, of which the algorithms and properties have been widely studied
(
Friedman et al. 2008
;
Lam and Fan 2009
;
Yuan 2010
)
.
Other works on the permutation invariant estimation for inverse covariance matrix can be found in
Xue et al. 2012
;
Wang et al. 2015
;
Cai et al. 2016
;
Van Wieringen 2019
;
Wang et al. 2020
, among many others.

By comparing two categories of estimation methods, it is seen that the MCD is an appropriate method for estimating the inverse covariance matrix when the information of variable ordering is fully available,
while the Glasso is suitable when the information of variable ordering is not available.
However, in many applications, there is only partial information of variable ordering available.
Here the partial information means that the variables can be divided into several groups (including one group) with the group ordering known but the variable ordering within each group unknown.
For example, in the multi-stage manufacturing process
(
Shi 2006
)
, each stage contains a set of variables and the stages have a natural ordering among themselves because of the nature of manufacturing process.
Thus, the full information of variable ordering is not available since the variables within the same stage may not have an ordering.
In this case the partial information of variable ordering is present since there is an ordering among stages.
To estimate the inverse covariance matrix of variables in such a multi-stage manufacturing process, neither the MCD method nor the Glasso method is adequate.
Another concrete example is the Covid-19 data in Case Studies Section.
The data were weekly collected from 37 continuous weeks at the beginning of the pandemic.
In each week, four variables which have no ordering among themselves are recorded.
Such four variables in a certain week are correlated with variables in the former weeks (see details in Case Studies).
Therefore the
4\times 37=148
variables can be naturally divided into 37 groups by calendar week, which forms a partial information of the variable ordination.

In this work, we fill in the gap to develop a block Cholesky decomposition (BCD) method for estimating the inverse covariance matrix given the partial information of variable ordering.
The proposed BCD method takes advantage of such partial information on the variable ordination to estimate the inverse covariance matrix via the joint estimation of a set of penalized multivariate regressions.
It guarantees the positive definiteness of the estimated sparse inverse covariance matrix with statistically meaningful interpretation.
Moreover, the proposed method provides a unified framework of estimating the inverse covariance matrix, where the MCD, the Glasso method, the
Witten et al. 2011
’s estimator and
Rothman et al. 2010a
’s estimator can be all considered as special cases of the proposed method.
The theoretical results suggest that the proposed model can have faster consistent rate than that of Glasso method when the partial information on the variable ordination is present.
The R codes of implementing the proposed model are available at https://github.com/xiaoningmike/BCD.

The remainder of this work is organized as follows.
Section
2
develops the proposed method along with its parameter estimation.
The asymptotically theoretical property is established in Section
3
under regularity conditions.
Sections
4
and
5
demonstrate the merits of the proposed model via simulations and two real data examples.
We conclude our work with some discussion in Section
6
.
All technical proofs are reported in the Appendix.

2
The Proposed Method

In this section, we describe the proposed methodology of the BCD method. Different from the MCD technique, the proposed BCD method only requires partial information of the variable ordination.

2.1
Block Cholesky Decomposition for Inverse Covariance Matrix

Suppose that the variables in
X
can be partitioned into
M
groups, with the
j
th group of variables denoted as
\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(j)}=(X^{(j)}_{1},X^{(j)}_{2},\ldots,X^{(j)}_{p_{j}})^{\prime},j=1,2,\ldots,M
, where
p_{j}
is the number of variables in the
j
th group, and
\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}=p
.
Assume that these
M
groups of variables have a natural ordering of
\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(1)},\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(2)},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(M)}
,
while there is not any ordering structure among variables
X^{(j)}_{1},X^{(j)}_{2},\ldots,X^{(j)}_{p_{j}}
within each group
\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(j)}
.
We call such ordering information of these
M
groups as the
partial information of the variable ordination
.
Note that when
M=1
, it reduces to the case that there is no ordering information for the
p
variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}
,
and
M=p
corresponds to the case that the
p
variables have a full ordering information.
In this work, we assume both of
p
and
M
can be diverged as the sample size goes to infinity.

Without loss of generality, we write
\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}=((\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(1)})^{\prime},(\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(2)})^{\prime},\ldots,(\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(M)})^{\prime})^{\prime}
with its inverse covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}^{-1}
.
The key idea of the BCD model is to decompose
\Omega
by
a block lower triangular matrix constructed from the multivariate regression coefficients when the variable group
\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(j)}
is regressed on its preceding variable groups

\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(1)},\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(2)},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(j-1)}
for
j=2,3,\ldots,M
.
Specifically, the BCD method considers a series of multivariate regressions

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(j)}=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{ji}\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(i)}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$\unboldmath}_{j}=\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}\mbox{\boldmath$Z$\unboldmath}^{(j)}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$\unboldmath}_{j},\ j=2,\ldots,M,

(2.1)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$\unboldmath}^{(j)}=((\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(1)})^{\prime},(\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(2)})^{\prime},\ldots,(\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(j-1)})^{\prime})^{\prime}
and

\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}=(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j1},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j,j-1})
with

\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{ji}
being the
p_{j}\times p_{i}
coefficient matrix.
Here
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$\unboldmath}_{j}
is the
p_{j}
-dimensional vector of error term for the
j
th multivariate regression with
E\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$\unboldmath}_{j}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}
and
Cov(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$\unboldmath}_{j})=\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}
.
Hence, we can construct a block lower triangular matrix
A
written as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}&\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}&\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}&\ldots&\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{21}&\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}&\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}&\ldots&\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{31}&\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{32}&\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}&\ldots&\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{M1}&\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{M2}&\ldots&\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{M,M-1}&\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}\end{array}\right),

with its
j
th diagonal element being a
p_{j}\times p_{j}
zero matrix,
and its lower left part composed of all the regression coefficient matrices in Equation (
2.1
).
Besides, define

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}=Cov(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$\unboldmath}_{j})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}Cov(\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(1)}),~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~j=1,\\
Cov(\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(j)}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{ji}\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(i)}),~j=2,3,\ldots,M.\end{array}\right.

Denote by
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}={\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{2},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{M})
the block diagonal covariance matrix of vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$\unboldmath}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$\unboldmath}_{1}^{\prime},\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$\unboldmath}_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$\unboldmath}_{M}^{\prime})^{\prime}
.
Thus the multivariate regressions in (
2.1
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$\unboldmath}=\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}-\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}=(\mbox{\boldmath$I$\unboldmath}-\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath})\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}\triangleq\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath},

(2.2)

where
I
represents the
p\times p
identity matrix, and
\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}=\mbox{\boldmath$I$\unboldmath}-\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}
is a unit block lower triangular matrix having ones on its diagonal.
The matrices
T
and
D
are called the block Cholesky factors.
By taking
Cov(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$\unboldmath})=Cov(\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath})
in Equation (
2.2
), we have
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}=\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}^{\prime}
, which consequently leads to

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$\unboldmath}^{-1}=\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath},

(2.3)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}^{-1}={\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{1}^{-1},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{2}^{-1},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{M}^{-1})
.
As a result, the BCD method reduces the challenge of modeling an inverse covariance matrix based on the partial information of the variable ordination into
the problem of estimating
(M-1)
multivariate linear regressions.
Because of the decomposition (
2.3
),
the general sparsity in
T
would induce some sparsity in
\Omega
(although it may not induce certain structured sparsity), which is easily implemented by the regularization on the linear regressions in (
2.1
).
The decomposition (
2.3
) also indicates that the BCD method can guarantee the positive definiteness of the resulting estimate of
\Omega
provided that
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{1}^{-1},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{2}^{-1},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{M}^{-1}
are all positive definite.
Note that there is no constraint required for parameters in matrix
T
.
We would like to remark that although the proposed model needs information on the group ordering, it is invariant to the permutation of variables within each group because the coefficient estimation of multivariate regressions in (
2.1
) is not affected by the ordering of variables in
\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(j)}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$\unboldmath}^{(j)}
.
This point is verified in the simulation study.
Moreover, it is seen that the MCD is a special case of the proposed BCD with
M=p
(i.e.,
p_{j}=1
for
j=1,2,\ldots,M
),
which represents the case where the
p
variables have a full ordering information.

2.2
Parameter Estimation

Denote by
\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}_{2},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}_{n}
the
n
independently and identically distributed observations from the multivariate normal distribution
\mathcal{N}_{p}(\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath},\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}^{-1})
.
Let
\mathbb{X}=(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}_{2},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}_{n})^{\prime}
be the
n\times p
data matrix.
Based on the partial information of the variable ordination,
we partition
\mathbb{X}
by columns and write
\mathbb{X}=(\mathbb{X}^{(1)},\mathbb{X}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}^{(M)})
,
where
\mathbb{X}^{(j)}
represents the
n\times p_{j}
sub-data matrix corresponding to the
j
th variable group
\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(j)}
.
Based on the methodology of BCD, we need to model a set of multivariate regressions in (
2.1
) to obtain the estimates of block Cholesky factors matrices
T
and
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}^{-1}
.
The negative joint log-likelihood function is expressed as

\displaystyle L(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}^{-1})=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left\{-\log|\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}|+{\rm tr}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$S$\unboldmath}_{\epsilon_{j}}\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}\right]\right\},

(2.4)

up to some constant.
The symbol
\mbox{\boldmath$S$\unboldmath}_{\epsilon_{j}}=\frac{1}{n}(\mathbb{X}^{(j)}-\mathbb{Z}^{(j)}\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{j})^{\prime}(\mathbb{X}^{(j)}-\mathbb{Z}^{(j)}\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{j})
, where
\mathbb{Z}^{(j)}=(\mathbb{X}^{(1)},\mathbb{X}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}^{(j-1)})
stands for the data matrix of the first
(j-1)
groups of variables.
By encouraging the sparsity in the estimates
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j}^{-1}
, we can obtain a sparse estimate
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}^{-1}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}
.
Thus, the penalized log-likelihood function in the following is considered for parameter estimation as

\displaystyle L_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}^{-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left\{-\log|\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}|+{\rm tr}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$S$\unboldmath}_{\epsilon_{j}}\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}\right]\right\}+\lambda_{1}\sum_{j=2}^{M}\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}\|_{1}+\lambda_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\|\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}\|_{1}^{-}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left\{-\log|\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}|+{\rm tr}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$S$\unboldmath}_{\epsilon_{j}}\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}\right]+\lambda_{1}\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}\|_{1}+\lambda_{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}\|_{1}^{-}\right\}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{j=1}^{M}\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}),

(2.5)

where
\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})=-\log|\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}|+{\rm tr}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$S$\unboldmath}_{\epsilon_{j}}\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}\right]+\lambda_{1}\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}\|_{1}+\lambda_{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}\|_{1}^{-}
with
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{1}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}
being zero matrix.
Here
\lambda_{1}\geq 0
and
\lambda_{2}\geq 0
are tuning parameters,
the matrix norm
||\mbox{\boldmath$B$\unboldmath}||_{1}=\sum_{i,j}|b_{ij}|
, and
||\mbox{\boldmath$B$\unboldmath}||_{1}^{-}=\sum_{i\neq j}|b_{ij}|
with
b_{ij}
being the elements of matrix
B
.

Note that different components
\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})
contain different parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}
.
Hence minimizing
L_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}^{-1})
is equivalent to minimizing each
\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})
separately,
which facilitates a parallel computing procedure for estimating
(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})
simultaneously to save computational time.
For each minimization of
\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})
, although the objective function is not convex with respect to
(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})
, it is a biconvex optimization
(
Gorski et al. 2007
)
.
That is,
\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})
is convex over
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}
when fixing
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}
, and is also convex over
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}
when fixing
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}
.
This property enables us to apply a coordinate descent algorithm to iteratively estimate
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}
by minimizing
\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})
for a given
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}=(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})_{\ast}
,
and estimate
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}
by minimizing
\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j},\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})
for a given
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}=(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j})_{\ast}

(
Rothman et al. 2010b
;
Sofer et al. 2014
)
.
Specifically, for a given
(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})_{\ast}
, we solve

\displaystyle\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j}[(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})_{\ast}]

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}}\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}|(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})_{\ast})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}}\{\frac{1}{n}{\rm tr}[(\mathbb{X}^{(j)}-\mathbb{Z}^{(j)}\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{j})(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})_{\ast}(\mathbb{X}^{(j)}-\mathbb{Z}^{(j)}\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{j})^{\prime}]+\lambda_{1}\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}\|_{1}\}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j}}\{\frac{1}{n}(\tilde{\mathbb{X}}^{(j)}-\tilde{\mathbb{Z}}^{(j)}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j})^{\prime}(\tilde{\mathbb{X}}^{(j)}-\tilde{\mathbb{Z}}^{(j)}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j})+\lambda_{1}\|\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j}\|_{1}\},

(2.6)

where
\tilde{\mathbb{X}}^{(j)}={\rm vec}[\mathbb{X}^{(j)}(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}})_{\ast}]
,
\tilde{\mathbb{Z}}^{(j)}=(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-\frac{1}{2}})_{\ast}\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{(j)}
and
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j}={\rm vec}[\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{j}]
.
Here vec denotes the vectorization operator and
\otimes
the Kronecker product, and their property
{\rm vec}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}\mbox{\boldmath$B$\unboldmath}\mbox{\boldmath$C$\unboldmath})=(\mbox{\boldmath$C$\unboldmath}^{\prime}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}){\rm vec}(\mbox{\boldmath$B$\unboldmath})
is applied.
The optimization problem (
2.2
) is thus a linear regression with Lasso penalty
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
.
The initial value for
(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})_{\ast}
is set to be the identity matrix.
On the other hand, for a given
(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j})_{\ast}
, we solve

\displaystyle\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j}^{-1}[(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j})_{\ast}]

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}}\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}|(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j})_{\ast})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}}\left\{-\log|\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}|+{\rm tr}\left[(\mbox{\boldmath$S$\unboldmath}_{\epsilon_{j}})_{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}\right]+\lambda_{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}\|_{1}^{-}\right\},

(2.7)

where
(\mbox{\boldmath$S$\unboldmath}_{\epsilon_{j}})_{\ast}=\frac{1}{n}[\mathbb{X}^{(j)}-\mathbb{Z}^{(j)}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{j})_{\ast}]^{\prime}[\mathbb{X}^{(j)}-\mathbb{Z}^{(j)}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{j})_{\ast}]
.
It has the same form as Glasso estimation.
Accordingly, the estimates
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j}^{-1}
are iteratively solved from optimization problems (
2.2
) and (
2.2
) until convergence.
After obtaining the estimates
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j}^{-1}
, we construct the block Choleksy factor estimate
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}
with
-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j}
as the
j
th block row and the identity matrix being the block diagonal.
The estimate
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}^{-1}
is constructed with
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j}^{-1}
as its
j
th block diagonal.
Then
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}^{-1}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}
is a sparse estimate of inverse covariance matrix under the partial information of variable ordination.
We briefly summarize the above estimation procedure in Algorithm 1.

Algorithm 1

Input: Data
\mathbb{X}
, tuning parameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
.

Output: Estimate
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
corresponding to
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
.

For
j=1
to
M
do

Step 0: Set an initial value of
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}=\mbox{\boldmath$I$\unboldmath}
.

Step 1: Given
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j;t}^{-1}
, solve
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}
in (
2.2
) by the Lasso technique.

Step 2: Given
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j;t}
, solve
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}
in (
2.2
) by the Glasso technique.

Step 3: Repeat Steps 1 and 2 till both
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j;t}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j}^{-1}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j;t}^{-1}
converge.

End

Step 4:
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}=\mbox{\boldmath$I$\unboldmath}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}^{-1}={\rm diag}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{1}^{-1},\ldots,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{M}^{-1})
, then
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}^{-1}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}
.

Here
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j;t}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j;t}
represent the estimates of
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}
in the
t
th iteration.
The convergence criteria are
||\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j;t}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j;t-1}||_{F}^{2}<\tau_{1}
and
||\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j;t}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j;t-1}||_{F}^{2}<\tau_{2}
,
where
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
are two pre-selected small quantities, and
||\cdot||_{F}
stands for the Frobenius norm.
Since the objective (
2.2
) is not joint convex, there is no guarantee of finding the global minimum.
However, Algorithm 1 uses a coordinate descent to compute a local solution of (
2.2
).
Steps 1 and 2 both ensure a decrease in the value of objective, leading to the convergence of
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}
.
In Step 3, we also set a maximum number of iterations as 100 in case that the parameter estimation is not empirically converged.
However, in the simulation we have tried, almost all the parameter estimates satisfy the convergence criteria quickly with several tens of iterations.

Note that there are two tuning parameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
in the objective function (
2.2
).
To choose their optimal values, studies in the literature often suggest cross-validation, information criteria, independent validation set mechanism and so forth.
In this work the BIC (Bayesian information criterion) proposed by Yuan and Lin (2007) is adopted to determine the optimal values of tuning parameters as follows

\displaystyle\mbox{BIC}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=-\log|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})|+{\rm tr}[\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$S$\unboldmath}]+\frac{\log n}{n}\upsilon(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})),

where
S
is the sample covariance matrix, and
\upsilon(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}))
represents the number of non-zeros in the lower triangular part of estimate
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
.
The optimal values of tuning parameters are selected to minimize BIC
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
.

At the end of this section, we would like to point out that the literatures studying the MCD often assume a certain type of sparse structure, e.g. banded structure, for the underlying inverse covariance matrix.
Then some sparse patterns in
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}
will lead to certain desired sparse structures of estimate
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}
.
However when the underlying matrix have a general unstructured sparsity, the relationship of sparsity between matrices
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}
is not very explicit.
Nonetheless, the sparsity in
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}
is also able to be induced by a sparse estimate
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}
empirically
(
Huang et al. 2006
;
Kang and Deng 2020
)
.
Moreover, we justify this point in the simulation by FSL criterion which evaluates the capability of catching the sparsity of underlying matrix.

2.3
Comparison and Connection with Several Existing Methods

The proposed BCD method obtains a sparse inverse covariance matrix estimate under the partial information of variable ordination.
Both of the Cholesky factors’ estimates
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}^{-1}
contain the sparsity resulting from a set of penalized multivariate regressions.
It makes close connection with several existing methods.

Firstly, we demonstrate that the MCD, Glasso,
Rothman et al. 2010a
and
Witten et al. 2011
are all special cases of the proposed method.
The MCD method for estimating
\Omega
is a special case when
M=p
, indicating that there is a full ordering information.
When data have partial ordering information, the MCD is not suitable.
The MCD needs a pre-specified full ordering before analyzing data, but the variables have no ordering within each group.
Therefore, one needs to identify an ordering before applying the MCD.
However, different orderings would lead to different estimates
(
Chang and Tsay 2010
)
, and an incorrectly identified ordering would result in an inaccurate estimate.
Additionally, the Glasso is a special case of the proposed BCD with
M=1
, implying that there is no ordering information.
Besides,
Rothman et al. 2010a
studied a banded estimate of
\Omega
via MCD.
Their approach can also be viewed as a special case of the proposed BCD model by
M=p
and regressing

\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(j)}
only on its several nearest previous group variables instead of all the previous group variables in Equation (
2.1
).
Furthermore, the proposed BCD method can be easily extended to estimate the inverse covariance matrix with a banded block structure.

In addition,
Witten et al. 2011
introduced a block diagonal inverse covariance estimation with
each block obtained by the Glasso on the corresponding group variables.
Their method assumed that variables in different groups are independent.
Thus their estimator is also a special case of the proposed BCD, in the sense that the block Cholesky factor
T
becomes the identity matrix under the independence assumption between group variables,
and each
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}
is then estimated from Glasso on
\mathbb{X}^{(j)}
.
The estimate
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}=\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}^{-1}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}
is the same as that in
Witten et al. 2011
.

Secondly, we compare several methods from perspective of ordering information.
When there is no ordering among variables, apart from Glasso which penalizes likelihood function, some papers investigated matrix estimation through penalized pseudo-likelihood.
They solve their optimization problems often in a column-by-column fashion, such as
Yuan 2010
;
Cai et al. 2011
;
Liu and Luo 2015
;
Liu and Wang 2017
and so forth.

Liu and Luo 2015
extended the idea of
Cai et al. 2011
and proposed SCIO estimator as

\displaystyle\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}}_{i}=\arg\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$S$\unboldmath}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}-\mbox{\boldmath$e$\unboldmath}^{\prime}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}+\lambda_{i}||\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}||_{1}\right\},

where
\mbox{\boldmath$e$\unboldmath}_{i}
is the
i
th column of the identity matrix,
\lambda_{i}>0
is a tuning parameter, and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$\unboldmath}}_{i}
is the estimate of the
i
th column of
\Omega
.
However the estimates obtained from a column by column fashion are not guaranteed to be positive definite, and a symmetrization step is also needed to make their estimates symmetric.
In the contrast, the proposed BCD estimate is itself symmetric and positive definite.

On the other hand, when variables have a full information on the ordering, two recent works
Yu and Bien 2017
and
Khare et al. 2019
proposed to estimate
\Omega
via the classical Cholesky decomposition
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}=\mbox{\boldmath$L$\unboldmath}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$L$\unboldmath}
, where the Cholesky factor
L
is a lower triangular matrix.
Both of such decomposition and the MCD induce the sparse estimates by the sparsity in the Cholesky factors.
The advantage of classical Cholesky decomposition is to directly result in a convex objective with respect to parameter
L
, hence guaranteeing a global convergence with an appropriate penalty.
But it lacks statistical meanings and interpretation as explicit as the MCD technique.

Yu and Bien 2017
assumed “local dependence” in the ordered data in the sense that the
j
th variable is correlated with its
K_{j}
nearest variables, where
K_{j}
can be different. That is, they assumed structured sparse pattern in the underlying
\Omega
, while the proposed BCD is suitable for a general or unstructured sparsity.

Khare et al. 2019
introduced CSCS estimator, which accommodates the unstructured sparsity by imposing a Lasso-type penalty on the likelihood function in terms of
L
, and developed a cyclic coordinate algorithm which leads to a closed form of solution for the estimates of each row of
L
.
To the best of our knowledge, few works have contributed to the inverse covariance estimation when variables have partial ordering information.

3
Theoretical Properties

In this section, we establish the asymptotically theoretical properties for the proposed BCD estimator.
To facilitate the presentation and proofs, we introduce some notation and make assumptions on the true model.
Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{0}=\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}_{0}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{0}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}_{0}
be the underlying inverse covariance matrix with its block MCD according to the group variables
\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(1)},\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(2)},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$X$\unboldmath}^{(M)}
.
Let
\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}_{j}=-\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}
,
and
\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}_{j0}
be the counterpart of
\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}_{j}
in the block Cholesky factor
\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}_{0}
.
That is,
\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}_{j0}
is the
j
th block row in the lower triangular part of matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}_{0}
.
Define
\mathcal{Z}_{T_{j}}=\{(i,k):(\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}_{j0})_{ik}\neq 0\}
as the collection of nonzero elements in the matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}_{j0}
.
Similarly, denote the counterpart of
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}
in the block Cholesky factor
D
by the matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j0}
, which is the
j
th block diagonal of matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{0}
.
Let
\mathcal{Z}_{D_{j}}=\{(i,k):i\neq k,(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j0}^{-1})_{ik}\neq 0\}
be the collection of nonzero off-diagonal elements in the matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{0}^{-1}
.
Denote by
s_{T_{j}}
and
s_{D_{j}}
the cardinality of
\mathcal{Z}_{T_{j}}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{D_{j}}
, respectively.
Let
s_{T}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}s_{T_{j}}
and
s_{D}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}s_{D_{j}}
.
In order to achieve the asymptotic consistent property of the proposed estimator,
a mild condition is needed that there exists a constant
\theta>0
such that the singular values of
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{0}
are bounded as

\displaystyle 1/\theta<\varphi_{p}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{0})\leq\varphi_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{0})<\theta,

(3.1)

where
\varphi_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$B$\unboldmath}),\varphi_{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$B$\unboldmath}),\ldots,\varphi_{p}(\mbox{\boldmath$B$\unboldmath})
represent the singular values of matrix
B
in a decreasing order.
This assumption is made to guarantee the positive definiteness of
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{0}
.
In addition, assume that there exist constants
0\leq C_{j}\leq 1
and
\gamma_{j}\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle p_{j}=\gamma_{j}p^{C_{j}}~~~\mbox{for}~~~j=1,2,\ldots,M.

(3.2)

Assumption (
3.2
) characterizes the relation between each
p_{j}
and
p
for high-dimensional data.
If
p
diverges to infinity,
C_{j}=0
corresponds to the case where
p_{j}
is fixed and controlled by
\gamma_{j}
, while
0<\tau_{c}\leq C_{j}\leq 1
implies that
p_{j}
also diverges, but not faster than
p
, where
\tau_{c}
is some positive arbitrarily small number.
Denote
\mathcal{Z}_{C}=\{j:0<\tau_{c}\leq C_{j}\leq 1\}
, the set of indices that the corresponding groups have a diverging number of variables.
Now, we present the main results in Theorems
1
and
2
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}_{1},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}_{n}
are
n
independently and identically distributed observations from
\mathcal{N}_{p}(\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath},\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}^{-1})
.
Let
(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})_{\ast}
and
(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j})_{\ast}
be any estimates of
\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}
obtained from the path of Algorithm 1.
Under (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), assume that the tuning parameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
satisfy
\lambda_{1}=O(\sqrt{\log(p)/n})
,
\lambda_{2}=O(\sqrt{\log(p)/n})
, then

(a) there exists a local minimum
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}}_{j}(=-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}_{j})
of
\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}|(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})_{\ast})
such that
\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}_{j}}-\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}_{j0}\|_{F}=O_{p}(s_{T_{j}}\log(\sum_{k=1}^{j}p_{k})/n)
.

(b) there exists a local minimum
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j}
of
\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}|(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j})_{\ast})
such that
\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}}-\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j0}\|_{F}=O_{p}((s_{D_{j}}+p_{j})\log p_{j}/n)
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}
be the estimate of
\Omega
obtained by Algorithm 1.
Assume all the assumptions in Theorem
1
hold, then we have

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{0}\|_{F}=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{s_{T}\log p+\sum_{j=1}^{M}(s_{D_{j}}+p_{j})\log p_{j}}{n}}\right).

Under the condition
s_{T}\log p+\sum_{j=1}^{M}(s_{D_{j}}+p_{j})\log p_{j}=o(n)
,

\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{0}\|_{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\rightarrow}}0
.

Theorem
1
provides asymptotic consistent rates of the estimators
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$T$\unboldmath}}_{j}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}_{j}
that are obtained by minimizing
\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j}|(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})_{\ast})
in (
2.2
) and minimizing
\ell_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1}|(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j})_{\ast})
in (
2.2
).
Theorem
2
establishes the property for the inverse covariance estimate
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}}
obtained from the proposed Algorithm 1, which demonstrates the consistent rate of our estimate in practice.
Moreover, we would like to have the following remarks.

First, from Theorem
1
we have
\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}^{-1}-\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{0}^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}-\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{0}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}}-\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j0}\|_{F}=O_{p}(\sum_{j=1}^{M}(s_{D_{j}}+p_{j})\log(p_{j})/n)
.
Such rate is sharper than the theoretical Glasso consistent rate
(\tilde{s}+p)\log(p)/n
(
\tilde{s}
is a measure of sparsity), which has been obtained in the literature
(
Lam and Fan 2009
)
, because of
\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}\log p_{j}\leq p\log p
resulting from
\prod_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}^{p_{j}}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{M}p_{\max}^{p_{j}}=p_{\max}^{p}\leq p^{p}
, where
p_{\max}=\max\{p_{j}\}^{M}_{j=1}
.
The equality holds only in the situation that all the
p
variables are in one group, where it is exactly a Glasso problem.
This implies that, as long as there are at least two groups of variables, the proposed BCD model with partial information of variable ordination is useful in reducing the consistent rate.

Second, the estimation of
D
is decomposed into
M
separate Glasso estimations in the proposed method.
According to the log sum inequality, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}\log p_{j}\geq p\log(p/M)
.
The equality holds when
p_{j}=p/M
for all
j=1,2,\ldots,M
, indicating that the lowest bound of the proposed estimator’s consistent rate would be achieved when each group has the equal number of variables.
In view of this, we would like to point out that:
(i) a larger number of variable groups leads to a smaller value of the lowest bound of the consistent rate;
(ii) the more evenly that
p
variables are assigned into
M
groups, the more closely that the consistent rate tends to the lowest bound.

Third, the requirement
s_{T}\log p+\sum_{j=1}^{M}(s_{D_{j}}+p_{j})\log p_{j}=o(n)
in Theorem
2
is a relatively weaker condition compared with the assumption for the Glasso model.
Note that
\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}-\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{0}\|^{2}_{F}\leq O_{p}(\sum_{j=1}^{M}(s_{D_{j}}+p_{j})\log(p_{\max})/n)=O_{p}((s_{D}+p)\log(p_{\max})/n)
, then we may require a stronger condition
s_{T}\log p+(s_{D}+p)\log p_{\max}=o(n)
.
Moreover, if we further loose the upper bound of
\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}}-\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{0}\|^{2}_{F}\leq O_{p}((s_{D}+p)\log(p)/n)
, the proposed BCD model needs an even stronger condition
(s_{T}+s_{D}+p)\log p=o(n)
, which however is the similar condition as that for the Glasso estimator.

In this section, we conduct simulation studies to evaluate the performance of the proposed BCD model (Prop) in comparison with several existing methods, including the MCD method, Glasso, SCIO
(
Liu and Luo 2015
)
and CSCS
(
Khare et al. 2019
)
.
The MCD, where
M=p
, reduces Equation (
2.1
) to
(p-1)
univariate linear regressions with their coefficients estimated by Lasso.
The Glasso and SCIO are two popular methods, but do not consider ordering information.
The CSCS is suitable for data with full ordering information and implemented via classical Cholesky decomposition.
The tuning parameters in the Glasso, SCIO and CSCS methods are selected based on BIC.
Besides, to examine whether the proposed model is permutation invariant within each group, we also implement the Prop

\Omega

.

5
Case Studies

5.1
Covid-19 Data

We further evaluate the performance of the proposed model through a real data example of Covid-19 pandemic, which is available on the official website of Virginia health department.
The data were weekly collected during May 29th, 2020 to February 6th, 2021 (37 weeks) from 34 districts in Virginia State such as Arlington, Fairfax, Richmond, Roanoke, Virginia Beach, etc.
For each week the data contain four variables: the accumulative number of cases, the accumulative number of people hospitalized, the accumulative number of deaths and the accumulative number of people taking the PCR (polymerase chain reaction) tests,
resulting in
4\times 37=148
variables.

Denote the collected data by
\mbox{\boldmath$N$\unboldmath}_{i}=(N_{i1},\ldots,N_{i148})^{\prime},i=1,...,34
,
and transform
y_{ij}=\sqrt{(N_{ij}+1/4)}
to make the data distribution close to normal
(
Brown et al. 2005
)
.
We then apply the proposed model as well as the MCD, SCIO, CSCS and Glasso methods to estimate the
148\times 148
inverse covariance matrix.
To conduct data analysis, the 148 variables are partitioned into 37 groups with each group naturally corresponding to a calendar week.
Hence it is seen that the data have a partial information of the variable ordination in a weekly scale.
To examine the performance of methods in comparison, we predict the accumulative number of cases, the accumulative number of people hospitalized, the accumulative number of deaths and the accumulative number of people taking PCR in the last 2 weeks
using the inverse covariance estimates obtained from the data in the first 35 weeks.
Specifically, let
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{i}=(y_{i1},\ldots,y_{i148})^{\prime}=(\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iE}^{\prime},\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iL}^{\prime})^{\prime}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iE}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iL}
represent the data in the first 35 weeks and last 2 weeks for the
i
th district,
i=1,2,\ldots,34
.
Hence
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iE}
contains
4\times 35=140
variables and
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iL}
contains
4\times 2=8
variables.
Accordingly, the mean vector and the inverse covariance matrix are divided as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{1}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{2}\end{array}\right]~~~\mbox{and}~~~\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{11},&\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{12}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{12}^{\prime},&\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{22}\end{array}\right].

Assuming multivariate normality, we have

\displaystyle E(\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iL}|\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iE})=\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{2}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{22}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$\unboldmath}_{12}^{\prime}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iE}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}_{1}).

(5.1)

The 34 observations are split into a training set and a testing set using the leaving-one-out mechanism.
That is, each observation is considered as a testing point with the rest 33 observations as training set.
The training set is used to estimate the mean vector
\mu
and the inverse covariance matrix
\Omega
.
The values of
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iE}=(y_{i1},\ldots,y_{i140})^{\prime}
in the testing data are used to predict
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iL}=(y_{i141},\ldots,y_{i148})^{\prime}
based on Equation (
5.1
).
For each variable in
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iL}
, define the average absolute prediction error (APE) by

\displaystyle\mbox{APE}_{j}=\frac{1}{34}\sum_{i=1}^{34}|\hat{y}_{ij}-y_{ij}|,~~~j=141,\ldots,148.

where
\hat{y}_{ij}
is the predicted value.

5.2
Call Center Data

In this section, we apply the proposed model to analyze the call center data from
Huang et al. 2006
.
The data set was collected from one of call centers in a major U.S. northeastern financial organization.
It recorded the time that every call arrives at the service queue from 7:00am until midnight in each day of 2002, except for 6 days when the data collecting
equipment was out of order.
The 17-hour period is divided into 102 10-minute intervals, and the number of calls during each time interval is counted.
Since the arrival patterns of calls are different between weekdays and weekends, the analysis focuses on weekdays here.
In addition, screening out some outliers that include holidays and days when the recording equipment was faulty, we are left with 239 observations.

Denote such data by
\mbox{\boldmath$N$\unboldmath}_{i}=(N_{i1},\ldots,N_{i102})^{\prime},i=1,...,239
, where
N_{ij}
is the number of calls received by the call center for the

j
th 10-minute interval on day
i
.
Do the transformation
y_{ij}=\sqrt{(N_{ij}+1/4)},i=1,\ldots,239,j=1,\ldots,102
to make the data distribution close to normal
(
Brown et al. 2005
)
.
We apply the compared methods to estimate the
102\times 102
inverse covariance matrix.
The 102 variables are partitioned into three groups with each group subsequently containing 30, 30 and 42 variables, corresponding to three time intervals of 7:00am - 12:00am, 12:00am - 5:00pm and 5:00pm - midnight.
To compare the performance of different methods, we predict the number of arriving calls later in a day based on the arrival patterns at earlier times of that day.
As in Section
5.1
, we use notation
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{i}=(y_{i1},\ldots,y_{i102})^{\prime}=(\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iE}^{\prime},\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iL}^{\prime})^{\prime}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iE}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iL}
measure the arrival patterns in the early and later times of day
i
.
To examine the performance of the proposed model,
the 239 observations are split into a training set which contains the first 205 data corresponding to dates from January to October, and a testing set with the rest 34 data corresponding to dates from November and December.
The values of
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iE},i=206,\ldots,239
in the testing data are used to predict
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iL}
based on Equation (
5.1
).
Two different settings of
\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}_{iE}
are considered as (A):

6
Discussion

In this work, we propose a block Cholesky decomposition (BCD) method for inverse covariance estimation when the partial information of variable ordination is available.
The proposed model adopts the MCD to induce the sparsity by imposing the regularization on the multivariate regressions.
The proposed method provides a unified framework for several existing methods.
including the MCD, the Glasso, and the estimators of
Rothman et al. 2010a
and
Witten et al. 2011
.
The theoretical results indicate that the proposed model enjoys a faster consistent rate than the Glasso if the data have the partial ordination among the variables.

There are several directions for future research.
First, the objective function (
2.2
) is not jointly convex, hence the theoretical global minimizer is not guaranteed.
A potential way to address this issue is to employ the classical Cholesky decomposition for the inverse covariance estimation
(
Yu and Bien 2017
;
Khare et al. 2019
)
, which leads to a convex optimization.
However, the statistical interpretation of the classical Cholesky decomposition may not be as explicit as the MCD, where the Cholesky factor matrix can be constructed from regressions.
It will be interesting to further investigate how to incorporate the partial information of variable ordering into the classical Cholesky decomposition.
Second, the proposed method can be extended to investigate the multivariate response regression, where the multivariate response variables have certain ordering information, such as data with outcomes collected in a time sequence order.
Third, the error bounds in Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 could be further improved.
Note that
(\mbox{\boldmath$D$\unboldmath}_{j}^{-1})_{\ast}
and
(\mbox{\boldmath$A$\unboldmath}_{j})_{\ast}
in Theorem 1 are generated on the path of Algorithm 1.
When they are close to the underlying true parameters, several terms of
o_{p}(1)
in the proof could be close to 0,
making a smaller constant term in the error bound.
