An intrinsical description of group codes
José Joaquín Bernal, Ángel del Río, Juan Jacobo Simón

Abstract
. A (left) group code of length n n is a linear code which is the image of a (left) ideal of a group algebra via an isomorphism 𝔽 ​ G → 𝔽 n \mathbb{F}G\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{n} which maps G G to the standard basis of 𝔽 n \mathbb{F}^{n} . Many classical linear codes have been shown to be group codes. In this paper we obtain a criterion to decide when a linear code is a group code in terms of its intrinsical properties in the ambient space 𝔽 n \mathbb{F}^{n} , which does not assume an “a priori” group algebra structure on 𝔽 n \mathbb{F}^{n} . As an application we provide a family of groups (including metacyclic groups) for which every two-sided group code is an abelian group code. It is well known that Reed-Solomon codes are cyclic and its parity check extensions are elementary abelian group codes. These two classes of codes are included in the class of Cauchy codes. Using our criterion we classify the Cauchy codes of some lengths which are left group codes and the possible group code structures on these codes.

1.
Characterizing group codes

In this section we give necessary and sufficient conditions for a subspace
C
of
\mathbb{F}^{n}

to be a (left) group code in terms of the group of permutation automorphisms of
C
. For this result we do not need to assume that
\mathbb{F}
is finite.

Let
S_{n}
denote the group of permutations on
n
symbols, that is the group of
bijections of
{\mathbb{N}}_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
onto itself. Recall that a subgroup
G
of
S_{n}
is

regular
if and only if it is transitive and has order
n
(equivalently,
|G|=n

and
\sigma(x)\neq x
for every
1\neq\sigma\in G
and
x\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
). We consider
S_{n}

acting by linear transformations on
\mathbb{F}^{n}
via the rule:

\sigma(e_{i})=e_{\sigma(i)}\quad(\sigma\in S_{n},i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}).

Two linear codes
C
and
C^{\prime}
are permutation equivalent if
\sigma\left(C\right)=C^{\prime}
for some
\sigma\in S_{n}
. Following
[
2
]
, we denote the group of permutation automorphisms of a linear code
C

by

{\rm PAut}(C)=\{\sigma\in S_{n}:\sigma(C)=C\}.

For a group
G
, let
Z(G)
denote the center of
G
and let
C_{G}(H)
denote the
centralizer of
H
in
G
, for each subgroup
H
of
G
.

We will need the following lemma which is possibly well known.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
H
be a regular subgroup of
S_{n}
and fix an element
i_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
. Let

\psi:H\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
be the bijection given by
\psi(h)=h(i_{0})
. Then there is an
anti-isomorphism
\sigma:H\rightarrow C_{S_{n}}(H)
, mapping
h\in H
to
\sigma_{h}
, where

\sigma_{h}(i)=\psi^{-1}(i)\left(h(i_{0})\right)\quad(i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}).

Moreover
\sigma_{h}=h
for every
h\in Z(H)
and so
Z(H)=Z(C_{S_{n}}(H))
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\psi
is a bijection, since
H
is regular. Furthermore

\psi^{-1}(i)(i_{0})=i
, for every
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
and
\psi^{-1}\left(h(i_{0})\right)=h
, for

h\in H
. Therefore, if
h,k\in H
then

\sigma_{h}k(i)=\sigma_{h}\left(k\left(\psi^{-1}(i)(i_{0})\right)\right)=\psi^{-1}\left(\left[k\psi^{-1}(i)\right](i_{0})\right)\left(h(i_{0})\right)=\left[k\psi^{-1}(i)\right]\left(h(i_{0})\right)=k\sigma_{h}(i).

This shows that
\sigma
maps
H
into
C_{S_{n}}(H)
.

Now we prove that
\sigma
is an anti-homomorphism. If
h,k\in H
and
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
then

\sigma_{hk}(i)=\psi^{-1}(i)hk(i_{0})=\psi^{-1}\left(\psi^{-1}(i)h(i_{0})\right)k(i_{0})=\sigma_{k}\left(\psi^{-1}(i)h(i_{0})\right)=\sigma_{k}(\sigma_{h}(i)).

If
\sigma_{h}=1
then
\psi^{-1}(i)(i_{0})=i=\sigma_{h}(i)=\psi^{-1}(i)h(i_{0})
, for every
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
. Thus
\psi^{-1}(i)=\psi^{-1}(i)h
, since
H
is regular, and hence
h=1
. This
shows that
\sigma
is injective.

Finally we prove that
\sigma(H)=C_{S_{n}}(H)
. Indeed, for each
x\in C_{S_{n}}(H)
let

h=\psi^{-1}(x(i_{0}))\in H
. Then
h(i_{0})=\psi^{-1}(x(i_{0}))(i_{0})=x(i_{0})=x\left(\left(\psi^{-1}(i)\right)^{-1}(i)\right)=\left(\psi^{-1}(i)\right)^{-1}x(i)
, for
every
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
. Hence
x(i)=\psi^{-1}(i)h(i_{0})=\sigma_{h}(i)
for every
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
we have
x=\sigma_{h}
.

The last calculation also shows that if
x\in Z(H)=H\cap C_{S_{n}}(H)
then
h=x=\sigma_{h}
.
∎

We are ready to present our intrinsical characterization of (left) group codes.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
C
be a linear code of length
n
over a field
\mathbb{F}
and
G
a finite group of order

n
.

(1)

C
is a left
G
-code if and only if
G
is isomorphic to a transitive subgroup of
S_{n}
contained in

{\rm PAut}(C)
.

(2)

C
is a
G
-code if and only if
G
is isomorphic to a transitive subgroup
H
of
S_{n}
such that
H\cup C_{S_{n}}(H)\subseteq{\rm PAut}(C)
.

Proof.

Given a bijection
\phi:E\rightarrow G
, we also use
\phi
to denote its linear
extension
\mathbb{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}G
, and we define
f=f_{\phi}:G\rightarrow S_{n}
by setting

e_{f(g)(i)}=\phi^{-1}(g\phi(e_{i}))
, for
g\in G
and
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
. Then
f
is a group
homomorphism.
Furthermore,
f(g)(i)=i
if and only if
\phi^{-1}(g\phi(e_{i}))=e_{i}
if and only if

g\phi(e_{i})=\phi(e_{i})
if and only if
g=1
. This shows that
f
is injective and
H=f(G)

is a regular subgroup of
S_{n}
. Let
e_{i_{0}}=\phi^{-1}(1)
. By Lemma
1.1
, there is
an anti-isomorphism
\sigma:H\rightarrow C_{S_{n}}(H)
, defined by

\sigma_{h}(i)=\psi^{-1}(i)h(i_{0})
, where
\psi:H\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
is the bijection given by

\psi(h)=h(i_{0})
. We will use several times the equality
\psi^{-1}(i)(i_{0})=i
, for
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
.

For every
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
one has

e_{i}=e_{\psi^{-1}(i)(i_{0})}=e_{f(f^{-1}\psi^{-1}(i))(i_{0})}=\phi^{-1}\left(f^{-1}\left(\psi^{-1}(i)\right)\cdot\phi(e_{i_{0}})\right)=\phi^{-1}f^{-1}\psi^{-1}(i).

Using this and the fact that
f:G\rightarrow H
is an isomorphism, one has the following
equalities for every
h\in H
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{h}(e_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{\sigma_{h}(i)}=e_{(\psi^{-1}(i)h)(i_{0})}=\phi^{-1}f^{-1}\psi^{-1}\left((\psi^{-1}(i)h)(i_{0})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi^{-1}f^{-1}(\psi^{-1}(i)h)=\phi^{-1}\left(f^{-1}(\psi^{-1}(i))\cdot f^{-1}(h)\right)=\phi^{-1}\left(\phi(e_{i})\cdot f^{-1}(h)\right).

This implies

(1.1)

\sigma_{h}(x)=\phi^{-1}(\phi(x)\cdot f^{-1}(h))

for every
x\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
and
h\in H
.

Assume now that
C
is a left
G
-code and let
\phi:E\rightarrow G
be a bijection such
that
\phi(C)
is a left ideal of
\mathbb{F}G
and
f=f_{\phi}
. By the previous paragraph

H=f(G)
is a regular subgroup of
S_{n}
. Furthermore, by the definition of
f
, if
h\in H
and
\alpha=\sum\alpha_{i}e_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
, then

h(\alpha)=\sum\alpha_{i}e_{h(i)}=\sum\alpha_{i}\phi^{-1}\left(f^{-1}(h)\cdot\phi(e_{i})\right)=\phi^{-1}\left(f^{-1}(h)\cdot\phi(\alpha)\right)
.
Hence
h(C)=\phi^{-1}\left(f^{-1}(h)\cdot\phi(C)\right)=C
, that is
H\subseteq{\rm PAut}(C)
. If, moreover,
\phi(C)
is a two-sided ideal of
\mathbb{F}G
and
x\in C_{S_{n}}(H)

then, by Lemma
1.1
, there is
h\in H
such that
x=\sigma_{h}
. Then
x(C)=\sigma_{h}(C)=\phi^{-1}\left(\phi(C)\cdot f^{-1}(h)\right)=C
, by (
1.1
). This gives
the sufficiency part of (1) and (2).

Conversely, assume that
G
is isomorphic to a regular subgroup
H
of
S_{n}
contained in

{\rm PAut}(C)
. One may assume without loss of generality that
G=H
. Let
\phi:E\rightarrow G
be the bijection given by
\phi^{-1}(g)=e_{g(1)}
. Then
h\phi(e_{g(1)})=hg=\phi(e_{(hg)(1)})=\phi(h(e_{g(1)}))
, for every
h,g\in G
. Therefore

h\phi(e_{i})=\phi(h(e_{i}))
, for every
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
, since
G
is transitive. Using this we
have
h\phi(C)=\phi(h(C))=\phi(C)
, because
G\subseteq{\rm PAut}(C)
. Thus
\phi(C)
is a
left ideal of
\mathbb{F}G
.

Assume, additionally that
C_{S_{n}}(G)\subseteq{\rm PAut}(C)
and let
f=f_{\phi}
. If
g\in G

then
f(g)(e_{i})=e_{f(g)(i)}=\phi^{-1}(g\phi(e_{i}))=e_{(g\phi(e_{i}))(1)}=g(e_{\phi(e_{i})(1)})=g(e_{i})
. Thus
f(g)=g
for every
g\in G
. By (
1.1
),
\phi(C)g=\phi(C)f^{-1}(g)=\phi(\sigma_{g}(C))=\phi(C)
, for every
g\in G
, and thus
\phi(C)
is
a two-sided ideal of
\mathbb{F}G
.
∎

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
C
be a linear code of length
n
over a field and let
\mathcal{G}
be a class of
groups.

(1)

C
is a left
\mathcal{G}
group code if and only if
{\rm PAut}(C)
contains a regular subgroup of
S_{n}
in

\mathcal{G}
.

(2)

C
is a
\mathcal{G}
group code if and only if
{\rm PAut}(C)
contains a regular subgroup
H
of
S_{n}
in

\mathcal{G}
such that
C_{S_{n}}(H)\subseteq{\rm PAut}(C)
.

2.
One-dimensional group codes

Even if one-dimensional codes are not relevant for applications, it is educating to see
how one can use Theorem
1.2
to decide which one-dimensional codes are
group codes.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a finite field,
n\geq 1
and
0\neq v\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
. Then
\mathbb{F}v
, the
one-dimensional subspace spanned by
v
, is a left group code if and only if the entries
of
v
are of the form
u,\xi u,\xi^{2}u,\dots,\xi^{h-1}u
, each of them appearing

s=n/h
times in
v
, for some
u\in\mathbb{F}^{*}
and
\xi
an
h
-th primitive root of unity.

Assume that
v
satisfies the above conditions and let
G
be a group. Then
\mathbb{F}v
is a
left
G
-code if and only if
G
has a normal subgroup
N
of order
s
such that
G/N

is cyclic (of order
h
).

Proof.

Write
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
. We start describing
{\rm PAut}(\mathbb{F}v)
. Let
\sigma\in S_{n}
. Then

\sigma\in{\rm PAut}(\mathbb{F}v)
if and only if
\sigma(v)=\lambda_{\sigma}v
, for some
(necessarily unique)
\lambda_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{F}^{*}
. Moreover,
\sigma\mapsto\lambda_{\sigma}
defines a group homomorphism
\lambda:{\rm PAut}(\mathbb{F}v)\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
. Let

\xi
be a generator of the image of
\lambda
and let
K=Ker\lambda
. Then

\sigma(v)=\xi v
and hence, if
u\in\mathbb{F}
appears exactly
s
times as an entry of
v

then
\xi u
also appears exactly
s
times as an entry of
v
. Therefore, after
permuting the entries of
v
, one may assume that
v
is of the form

\begin{array}[]{rcl}v&=&(u_{1},\dots,u_{1},\xi u_{1},\dots,\xi u_{1},\xi^{2}u_{1},\dots,\xi^{2}u_{1},\xi^{h-1}u_{1},\dots,\xi^{h-1}u_{1},\\
&&\hskip 5.69046ptu_{2},\dots,u_{2},\xi u_{2},\dots,\xi u_{2},\xi^{2}u_{2},\dots,\xi^{2}u_{2},\xi^{h-1}u_{2},\dots,\xi^{h-1}u_{2},\\
&&\hskip 5.69046pt\dots,\\
&&\hskip 5.69046ptu_{k},\dots,u_{k},\xi u_{k},\dots,\xi u_{k},\xi^{2}u_{k}\dots,\xi^{2}u_{k},,\xi^{h-1}u_{k},\dots,\xi^{h-1}u_{k}).\end{array}

for
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{F}
such that
u_{x}\neq\xi^{z}u_{y}
for every
1\leq x<y\leq k
and

z\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, and each
\xi^{z}u_{i}
appears

. Then

K=(S_{s_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{s_{1}})\times(S_{s_{2}}\times\dots\times S_{s_{2}})\times\dots\times(S_{s_{k}}\times\dots\times S_{s_{k}})
, where the

-th block has

of
S_{s_{i}}
and the
j
-th

is identified with the subgroup of
S_{n}
formed by
the permutations which fix the coordinates
k
for which

. Consider the
permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
given by

\sigma(k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}k+s_{i},&\text{if }h(s_{1}+\dots+s_{i-1})<k\leq h(s_{1}+\dots+s_{i-1})+(h-1)s_{i};\\
k-(h-1)s_{i},&\text{if }h(s_{1}+\dots+s_{i-1})+(h-1)s_{i}<k\leq h(s_{1}+\dots+s_{i}).\end{array}\right.

Clearly,
\sigma
has order
h
and
\lambda_{\sigma}=\xi
. Thus
{\rm PAut}(\mathbb{F}v)=K\rtimes\langle\sigma\rangle
. This provides the description of
{\rm PAut}(\mathbb{F}v)
.

Observe that, with the notation of the previous paragraph,
{\rm PAut}(\mathbb{F}v)
is transitive if
and only if
k=1
. In that case (up to a permutation of coordinates)

(2.2)

v=(u,\dots,u,\xi u,\dots,\xi u,\xi^{2}u,\dots,\xi^{2}u,\xi^{h-1}u,\dots,\xi^{h-1}u)

for some
u\in\mathbb{F}^{*}
and some
h
-th primitive root of unity
\xi
in
\mathbb{F}^{*}
and each

\xi^{j}u
is repeated
s=n/h
times as a coordinate of
v
.

If
\mathbb{F}v
is a left
G
-code, for
G
a group (of order
n
) then, by
Theorem

, we may assume that
G
is a transitive subgroup of
S_{n}

contained in

. Therefore

is as in (

) and

. This implies that

. If

. Thus

Conversely, assume that

) and let

be a group of order

, which we

is a left

-code.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

If a one-dimensional vector space is a left group code then it is a cyclic group code.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathbb{F}v
is a one-dimensional group code of length
n
. By
Proposition
2.1
,
v
has
h
different coordinates each of them repeated
s=n/h

times. Then
\mathbb{F}v
is a cyclic group code because the cyclic group of order
n
has a
subgroup of order
s
.
∎

3.
Group codes versus abelian group codes

In this section we present a class of groups
\mathcal{G}
, containing properly the class
of abelian groups, such that every
\mathcal{G}
group code is also an abelian group
code. We also show that for every non-abelian finite group
G
there is a finite field

\mathbb{F}
such that some left
G
-code over
\mathbb{F}
is not an abelian group code.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Assume that
G
has two abelian subgroups
A
and
B
such
that every element of
G
can be written as
ab
with
a\in A
and
b\in B
. Then every

G
-code is an abelian group code.

Proof.

Assume that
C
is a
G
-code of length
n
. Then, by Theorem
1.2
,
G

is isomorphic to a regular subgroup
H
of
S_{n}
such that
H\cup C_{S_{n}}(H)\subseteq{\rm PAut}(C)
. One may assume without loss of generality that
G=AB=H
.

Let
\sigma:G\longrightarrow C_{S_{n}}(G)
be the anti-isomorphism defined in Lemma

1.1
, with respect to some
i_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
. Set
A_{1}=\sigma(A)
and
B_{1}=\sigma(B)
.
Since
\sigma
is an anti-isomorphism,
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
are abelian groups and

C_{S_{n}}(G)=B_{1}A_{1}
.

Now, we consider the group
K=\langle A,B_{1}\rangle\leq\langle G,C_{S_{n}}(G)\rangle\leq{\rm PAut}(C)
. Then
K
is
abelian, since
A
and
B_{1}
are abelian subgroups,
A\subseteq G
and
B_{1}\subseteq C_{S_{n}}(G)
. By Theorem
1.2
, it is enough to prove that
K
has order

n
and the stabilizer in
K
of every element of
{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
is trivial. To prove the first it
is sufficient to show that
\left[K:A\right]=\left[G:A\right]
. By Lemma
1.1
,

B\cap Z(G)=\sigma(B\cap Z(G))=B_{1}\cap Z(C_{S_{n}}(G))
. Then
[B:B\cap Z(G)]=[B_{1}:B_{1}\cap Z(C_{S_{n}}(G))]
and
\left[B\cap Z(G):B\cap A\right]=\left[B_{1}\cap Z(C_{S_{n}}(G)):B_{1}\cap A\right]
. Thus
\left[G:A\right]=\left[AB:A\right]=\left[B:B\cap A\right]=\left[B_{1}:B_{1}\cap A\right]=\left[AB_{1}:A\right]=\left[K:A\right]
, as desired.

Finally, let
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n}
and
k\in K
with
k(i)=i
. Then
k=a\beta
for some
a\in A

and
\beta=\sigma_{b}\in B_{1}
with
b\in B
. Moreover,
i=g(i_{0})
for a unique
g\in G
.
Therefore
g(i_{0})=i=k(i)=a\beta g(i_{0})=a\sigma_{b}(\psi(g))=agb(i_{0})
, where
\psi

is the map defined in Lemma
1.1
. Thus
agb=g
, because
G
is regular. Set

g=a^{\prime}b^{\prime}
, for
a^{\prime}\in A
and
b^{\prime}\in B
. Then
a^{\prime}abb^{\prime}=aa^{\prime}b^{\prime}b=agb=g=a^{\prime}b^{\prime}
and hence

ab=1
. Thus
b^{-1}=a\in A\cap B\subseteq Z(G)
. Using the last part of
Lemma
1.1
once more, we have
\beta=\sigma_{b}=b
and so
a\beta=1
.
∎

Recall that a group
G
is metacyclic if it has a cyclic normal subgroup
N
such that

G/N
is cyclic. Sabin and Lomonaco
[
4
]
have proved that if
C
is a
G
-code of a
split metacyclic group
G
(i.e.
G
is a semidirect product of cyclic groups) then
C

is an abelian group code. Using the previous result this can be extended to arbitrary
metacyclic group codes.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
C
is a metacyclic group code then
C
is an abelian group code.

Theorem
3.1
provides a family of groups
\mathcal{G}
such that every
two-sided
\mathcal{G}
group code is also an abelian group code. We do not know any
example of a group code which is not an abelian group code. For left group codes the
situation is completely different.

Proposition 3.3
.

For every non-abelian group
G
and every prime
p
not dividing the order of
G
there is a left

G
-code over some field of characteristic
p
which is not an abelian group code.

Proof.

Assume that
p
is a prime not dividing
|G|
. If
c
is the number of conjugacy classes of
G
then the number of
ideals of
\mathbb{F}G
is at most
2^{c}
, for every field
\mathbb{F}
of characteristic
p
. Therefore, there is a bound on the
number of
G
-codes which is independent of the field. In particular, the number of abelian group codes of a given
length is bounded by a number not depending on the field used. However, if
G
is non-abelian, then there is a field

K
such that a simple factor of
KG
is isomorphic to
M_{m}(K)
for some
m\geq 2
. Then a simple factor of
\mathbb{F}G
is
isomorphic to
M_{m}(\mathbb{F})
for every field extension
\mathbb{F}/K
. Since the left ideals of
M_{m}(\mathbb{F})
generated by matrices of
the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&a&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&0\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
0&0&0&\dots&0\end{array}\right)\quad(a\in\mathbb{F})

are all different,
\mathbb{F}^{n}
has at least
|\mathbb{F}|
different left
G
-codes. Thus, the number
of left
G
-codes for the different fields of characteristic
p
increases with the order
of the field and so it is not limited.
∎

Example 3.4
.

Since the proof of Proposition
3.3
is not constructive it is worth to present an
example of a left group code which is not an abelian group code. Let
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{11}
be the
field with 11 elements. Every
2
-dimensional cyclic group code of length 6 over
\mathbb{F}
is
permutation equivalent to one of the following codes:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}C_{1}&=&\{\left(\lambda,\mu,\mu-\lambda,-\lambda,-\mu,\lambda-\mu\right)\mid\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{F}\},\\
C_{2}&=&\{\left(\lambda,\mu,-\mu-\lambda,\lambda,\mu,-\mu-\lambda\right)\mid\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{F}\},\\
C_{3}&=&\{\left(\lambda,\mu,\lambda,\mu,\lambda,\mu\right)\mid\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{F}\}.\end{array}

Then one can easily check that the subspace
C
of
\mathbb{F}^{6}
generated by

u=\left(2,5,-7,2,-7,5\right)
and
v=\left(4,-3,-1,-4,1,3\right)
is not an abelian
group code. However
C
is a left
S_{3}
-code. Indeed,
A=(1,2,3)(4,5,6)
and

B=(1,4)(2,6)(3,5)
belong to
{\rm PAut}(C)
, since
A(u)=5u+4v
,
B(u)=u
,
A(v)=5(v-u)
and

. Moreover,
\langle A,B\rangle
is a regular subgroup of
S_{6}
isomorphic to

and
the claim follows from Theorem
1.2
.

4.
Cauchy codes

In this section we give some necessary and sufficient conditions for some Cauchy codes to
be left group codes and describe the possible left group code structures on such codes.
Our main tool is a description of the group of permutation automorphisms due to Dür

[
1
]
(see also
[
2
]
) which we present in a slightly different form.

We first recall the definition of Cauchy codes. For that we need to introduce some notations. Let
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
denote
the projective line over
\mathbb{F}
. Each element of
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
is represented either by its homogeneous coordinates, denoted

[x,y]
, or by an element of
\mathbb{F}\cup\{\infty\}
in such a way that
x\in\mathbb{F}
and
[x,1]
represent the same element in

\overline{\mathbb{F}}
, and
\infty
and
[1,0]
also represent the same element in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
. We use the coordinatization map
\varphi:\overline{\mathbb{F}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{2}
defined as follows:

\varphi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(x,1),&\text{if }x\in\mathbb{F};\\
(1,0),&\text{if }x=\infty.\end{array}\right.

We evaluate a homogeneous polynomial
F\in\mathbb{F}[X,Y]
on an element
z\in\overline{\mathbb{F}}
by setting
F(z)=F(\varphi(z))
. For
an element
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in\overline{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
we denote the set of entries of
\alpha
by
L_{\alpha}
.

Definition 4.1
.

Given
1\leq k<n
,
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in\overline{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
, without repeated entries, and a map
f:L_{\alpha}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
, the
k
-dimensional
Cauchy code with location vector
\alpha
and scaling map
f
is the code

\mathcal{C}_{k}(\alpha,f)=\left\{(f(\alpha_{1})P(\alpha_{1}),\dots,f(\alpha_{n})P(\alpha_{n})):P\in\mathbb{F}[X,Y],\text{ homogeneous of degree }k-1\right\}.

Note that our notation is slightly different from that of
[
2
]
. The set
L_{\alpha}

is called the location set of
\mathcal{C}_{k}(\alpha,f)
.

Every Cauchy code is MDS and its dual is another Cauchy code (see
[
2
]
for details).
Moreover, if
\sigma\in S_{n}
then
\sigma(\mathcal{C}_{k}(\alpha,f))=\mathcal{C}(\sigma(\alpha),f)
and in
particular the class of Cauchy codes is closed under permutations.

Examples of Cauchy codes are the generalized Reed-Solomon codes which correspond to
Cauchy codes with location set included in
\mathbb{F}
. In particular, the
k
-dimensional narrow
sense Reed-Solomon codes are those Cauchy codes of the form
\mathcal{C}_{k}(\alpha,1)
with location
vector
\alpha=(1,\xi,\xi^{2},\dots,\xi^{q-2})
, for
\xi
a primitive element of
\mathbb{F}
.
Furthermore the parity check extension of the narrow sense Reed-Solomon code

\mathcal{C}_{k}(\alpha,1)
is the Cauchy code
\mathcal{C}_{k}(\overline{\alpha},1)
with

\overline{\alpha}=(1,\xi,\xi^{2},\dots,\xi^{q-2},0)
. (Here
1
denotes the constant map

x\mapsto 1
.)

As mentioned at the beginning of this section our aim is to obtain criteria to decide when
a Cauchy code
\mathcal{C}_{k}(\alpha,f)
is a left
G
-code. Since a linear code and its dual
have the same automorphism groups, they are (left)
G
-codes for the same groups
G
.
Furthermore the dual of a Cauchy code is a Cauchy code too and
\mathcal{C}_{1}(\alpha,f)
is the
one-dimensional code generated by
(f(\alpha_{1}),\dots,f(\alpha_{n}))
(see
[
2
]
for
details). This shows that the cases
k=1
and
k=n-1
reduce to Proposition
2.1
.
Thus, in the remainder of this section, we assume that
2\leq k\leq n-2
.

Let
\Gamma={\rm PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{F})
, the 2-dimensional projective general linear group. We identify

\Gamma
with the group of homographies of
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
, so that
{\rm GL}_{2}(\mathbb{F})
is considered
acting on the left on
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
by

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\end{array}\right)[x,y]=[ax+by,cx+dy].

For a subset
X
of
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
let
\Gamma_{X}=\{T\in\Gamma:T(X)=X\}
. For
a\in\overline{\mathbb{F}}
, let

\Gamma_{a}=\Gamma_{\{a\}}
. For example,
\Gamma_{\infty}
is formed by the classes
represented by upper triangular invertible matrices and

(4.3)

\Gamma_{\infty}\simeq N\rtimes H,\text{ with }N=\left\{U_{b}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&b\\
0&1\end{array}\right):b\in\mathbb{F}\right\}\text{ and }H=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right):a\in\mathbb{F}^{*}\right\}.

Moreover
N
is an elementary abelian
p
-group of order
q
and
H
is cyclic of order

q-1
.

For every
T\in{\rm GL}_{2}(\mathbb{F})
, let
\theta_{T}:\overline{\mathbb{F}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
be defined by

\theta_{T}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{second coordinate of }T(\varphi(z)),&\text{ if it is non-zero};\\
\text{first coordinate of }T(\varphi(z)),&\text{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Fix a subset
L
of
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
. If
T\in{\rm GL}_{2}(\mathbb{F})
then we denote the restrictions of

T:\overline{\mathbb{F}}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{F}}
and
\theta_{T}:\overline{\mathbb{F}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
to
L
as
T_{L}
and

\theta_{T,L}
respectively. If
f,g:L\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
then let
f\cdot g:L\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
be given by

(f\cdot g)(z)=f(z)g(z)\quad(z\in L).

We say that
f
and
g
are equivalent, denoted
f\equiv g
, if
g=\lambda f
for some

\lambda\in\mathbb{F}^{*}
.

The following theorem describes when two Cauchy codes are equal.

Theorem 4.2
.

[
2
, Theorem 2.6]

Let
2\leq k\leq n-2
and consider two
k
-dimensional Cauchy codes.

C=\mathcal{C}_{k}(\alpha,f)
and
C^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}_{k}(\alpha^{\prime},f^{\prime})
of length
n
. Then
C=C^{\prime}
if and only if there exists

T\in{\rm PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{F})
such that
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}=T(\alpha_{i})
and
f^{\prime}\circ T_{L_{\alpha}}\equiv\theta_{T,L_{\alpha}}^{k-1}\cdot f
.

In particular,
C
and
C^{\prime}
are permutation equivalent if and only if there exists
T\in{\rm PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{F})
such that
T(L_{\alpha})=L_{\alpha^{\prime}}
and
f^{\prime}\circ T_{L_{\alpha}}\equiv\theta_{T,L_{\alpha}}^{k-1}\cdot f
.

Note that the equivalence
f^{\prime}\circ T_{L_{\alpha}}\equiv\theta_{T,L_{\alpha}}^{k-1}\cdot f
, in Theorem
4.2
does not depend on the representative of
T
in
{\rm GL}_{2}(\mathbb{F})

used to calculate
\theta_{T}
, because
\theta_{\lambda T}=\lambda\theta_{T}
, for every

\lambda\in\mathbb{F}^{*}
.

Given
f:L\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
with
|L|=n
we denote

\Gamma_{k,f}=\{T\in\Gamma_{L}:f\circ T_{L}\equiv\theta_{T,L}^{k-1}\cdot f\}

Proposition 4.3
.

If
C
is a
k
-dimensional Cauchy code of length
n
with
2\leq k\leq n-2
and scaling
map
f:L\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
then the map
T\mapsto T_{L}
is a group isomorphism

\Gamma_{k,f}\rightarrow{\rm PAut}(C)
.

Proof.

Let
C=\mathcal{C}_{k}(\alpha,f)
with
L=L_{\alpha}
. If
\sigma\in S_{n}
then

\sigma(C)=\mathcal{C}_{k}(\sigma(\alpha),f)
. Using this and Theorem
4.2
and identifying

S_{n}
with the permutations of
L
one has

(4.4)

{\rm PAut}(C)=\left\{T_{L}:T\in\Gamma_{L,f}\right\}.

The map
T\mapsto T_{L}
is injective because
|L|=n>k\geq 2
and an element of
{\rm PGL}_{2}(K)

is uniquely determined by its action on three different elements of
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
.
∎

Now it is clear that we have the following consequence of Theorem
1.2
.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
L\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{F}}
with
n=|L|
and
2\leq k\leq n-2
. Let
C
be a Cauchy code with
scaling map
f:L\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
. Then
C
is a left
G
-code if and only if
G
is
isomorphic to a subgroup
H
of order
n
of
\Gamma_{k,f}
whose action by homographies
on
L
is transitive (equivalently,
h(x)\neq x
, for every
x\in L
and
1\neq h\in H
).

As an application of Corollary
4.4
we characterize when some Cauchy codes with
large location set are group codes.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
L
and
L^{\prime}
be subsets of
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
with
|L|=|L^{\prime}|\geq q-2
. Then every Cauchy code with
location set
L
is equal to a Cauchy code with location set
L^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By assumption
|\overline{\mathbb{F}}\setminus L|=|\overline{\mathbb{F}}\setminus L^{\prime}|\leq 3
. Since the action of
\Gamma
on

\overline{\mathbb{F}}
is triply transitive there is
T\in{\rm GL}_{2}(\mathbb{F})
such that
T(\overline{\mathbb{F}}\setminus L)=\overline{\mathbb{F}}\setminus L^{\prime}
and so
T(L)=L^{\prime}
. Therefore, by Theorem
4.2
, if
C
is a
Cauchy code with scaling map
f:L\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
then
C
is also a Cauchy code with
scaling map
(\theta_{T,L}^{k-1}\cdot f)\circ T_{L}^{-1}:L^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
.
∎

It is well known that the parity check extension of a narrow sense Reed-Solomon codes is a
q
-ary Cauchy
G
-code of length
q
, for

G
the
p
-elementary abelian group of order
q
(see e.g.
[
3
]
). Next theorem
shows that this is the only possible structure of
G
-code on a
q
-ary Cauchy code of
length
q
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
C
be a
q
-ary Cauchy code of length
q
and dimension
2\leq k\leq q-2
and let
G

be a group of order
q
. Then
C
is a left
G
-code if and only if it is permutation
equivalent to the parity check extended narrow sense Reed-Solomon code and
G
is
p
-elementary
abelian.

Proof.

One implication was proved by Landrock and Manz
[
3
]
. Conversely, let
C
be a
q
-ary Cauchy code of length
q
with
scaling map
f:L\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
. By Lemma
4.5
, one may assume, without loss of
generality, that
L=\mathbb{F}
. Then
\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}}=\Gamma_{\infty}=N\rtimes H
, with
N
and

H
as in (
4.3
). Thus
N
is a normal Sylow elementary abelian
p
-subgroup of

\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}}
and hence it is the only subgroup of order
q
of
\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}}
. Therefore,
if
C
is a
G
-code then
G\simeq N
. Moreover,
N
should be contained in

\Gamma_{k,f}
and this implies that
f\circ U_{b}\equiv f
, for every
b\in\mathbb{F}
, because

\theta_{U_{b}}(z)=1
for every
b\in\mathbb{F}
and
z\in\overline{\mathbb{F}}
. Thus, there is
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}^{*}

with
f\circ U_{b}=\lambda f
. So,
f(0)=f(pb)=(f\circ U_{b})((p-1)b)=\lambda f((p-1)b)=\dots=\lambda^{p}f(0)
and hence
\lambda^{p}=1
. Then
\lambda=1
, since
|\mathbb{F}^{*}|=q-1
is
relatively prime with
p
. Hence
f(b)=(f\circ U_{b})(0)=f(0)
, for every
b\in\mathbb{F}
, i.e.

f
is constant. Therefore
C
is permutation equivalent to the parity check extended
narrow sense Reed-Solomon code.
∎

Now we turn our attention to Reed-Solomon like Cauchy codes. It is well known that the

q
-ary Reed-Solomon codes are cyclic of length
q-1
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the field of
q
elements, and let
\xi
be a primitive element of
\mathbb{F}
.
Let
C
be a
k
-dimensional
q
-ary Cauchy code with scaling map
f:L\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}

and assume that
|L|=q-1
and
2\leq k\leq q-3
.

Then
C
is a left group code if and only one of the following conditions hold for some
(for all)
T\in{\rm PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{F})
with
T(L)=\mathbb{F}^{*}
:

(1)

there is an integer
m
such that

\left(\theta_{T,L}^{k-1}\cdot f\right)\circ T^{-1}_{L}
is equivalent to the map

f_{m}:\mathbb{F}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
given by
f_{m}(z)=z^{m}
for every
z\in\mathbb{F}^{*}
.

(2)

q
is odd and there are integers
m
and
m^{\prime}
such that
4m+2(k-1)\equiv 2m^{\prime}+k-1\equiv 0\text{ mod }(q-1)
and
\left(\theta_{T,L}^{k-1}\cdot f\right)\circ T^{-1}_{L}
is
equivalent to the map
f_{m,m^{\prime}}:\mathbb{F}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{*}
given by

f_{m,m^{\prime}}(\xi^{2t+r})=\xi^{2tm+rm^{\prime}},\quad\text{ for every }t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ and }r=0\text{ or }1.

Moreover, if
G
is a group of order
q-1
then
C
is a left
G
-code if and only if
either
G
is cyclic and condition (1) holds or
G
is a dihedral group and condition (2)
holds.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.5
(and Theorem
4.2
), it is enough to prove that if
L=\mathbb{F}^{*}
and
f(1)=1
then
C
is a
left
G
-code if and only if either
G
is cyclic and
f=f_{m}
, for some
m
, or
q-1
is even,
G

is the dihedral group of order
q-1
and
f=f_{m,m^{\prime}}
, for some
m
and
m^{\prime}
satisfying
the conditions of (2).

So assume that
L=\mathbb{F}^{*}
and
f(1)=1
. Let
D=\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}^{*}}=\Gamma_{\{0,\infty\}}\supseteq\Gamma_{k,f}
.
Then
D=\langle A,B\rangle
with

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\xi&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad B=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right).

So,
D
is a dihedral group of order

2(q-1)
. If
q
is even then the only subgroup of
D
of order
q-1
is
\langle A\rangle
.
Otherwise, that is if
q
is odd then, the only subgroups of
D
of order
q-1
are

\langle A\rangle
,
\langle A^{2},B\rangle
and
\langle A^{2},AB\rangle
. By Corollary
4.4
,
C
is a left

G
-code if and only if
G
is isomorphic to a subgroup
H
of order
q-1
of

\Gamma_{k,f}
which acts transitively on
\mathbb{F}^{*}
. However
B(1)=1
, and this excludes the
possibility
H=\langle A^{2},B\rangle
. Thus
C
is a left
G
-code if and only if either
G
is
cyclic and
A\in\Gamma_{k,f}
, or
q
is odd,
G
is the dihedral group of order
q-1

and
A^{2}
and
M=AB
belong to
\Gamma_{k,f}
.

Notice that
\theta_{A}(z)=1
for every
z\in\mathbb{F}
and
(f_{m}\circ A)(z)=f_{m}(\xi z)=\xi^{m}f_{m}(z)=\xi^{m}(\theta_{A}\cdot f_{m})(z)
for every
z\in\mathbb{F}
. Thus
A\in\Gamma_{k,f_{m}}
and
hence if
f=f_{m}
then
C
is a cyclic group code. Conversely, if
C
is a cyclic group
code then
A\in\Gamma_{k,f}
, that is
f\circ A_{\mathbb{F}^{*}}\equiv f
. Hence there is

m=0,1,\dots,q-2
such that
f(\xi z)=\xi^{m}f(z)
for every
z
. This implies, together
with the assumption
f(1)=1
, that
f(\xi^{i})=\xi^{im}
and so
f=f_{m}
.

Secondly assume that
q
is odd and let
G
be the dihedral group of order
q-1
. On the one hand, arguing as in the
previous paragraph one deduces that
A^{2}\in\Gamma_{k,f}
if and only if there is an integer
0\leq m_{1}<q-1
such that

f(\xi^{2t+r})=\xi^{tm_{1}}f(\xi)^{r}
, for every

and
r=0
or
1
. Moreover
m_{1}
is even because

1=f(1)=f\left(\xi^{2\frac{q-1}{2}}\right)=\xi^{\frac{q-1}{2}m_{1}}
. Set
m_{1}=2m
. On the other hand,
M(z)=\xi/z

and
\theta_{M}(z)=z
, for every
z\in\mathbb{F}^{*}
. Hence
M\in\Gamma_{k,f}
if and only if there is an integer
0\leq m^{\prime}<q-1
such that
f(\xi/z)=\xi^{m^{\prime}}z^{k-1}f(z)
, for every
z\in\mathbb{F}^{*}
. So, if
C
is a left
G
-code then there are
integers
m
and
m^{\prime}
such that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\xi^{m^{\prime}+(2t+r)(k-1)+2tm}f(\xi)^{r}&=&\xi^{m^{\prime}+(2t+r)(k-1)}f(\xi^{2t+r})=f\left(\frac{\xi}{\xi^{2t+r}}\right)=f(\xi^{2(-t)+(1-r)})\\
&=&\xi^{-2tm}f(\xi)^{1-r},\end{array}

Considering the two values

Remarks 4.8
.

Let
2\leq k\leq q-3
and let
E_{m}
and
E_{m,m^{\prime}}
denote
k
-dimensional Cauchy codes
with scaling maps
f_{m}
and
f_{m,m^{\prime}}
respectively. By Theorem
4.7
, every

k
-dimensional left group Cauchy code of length
q-1
is permutation equivalent to
either
E_{m}
, for some
m
, or
E_{m,m^{\prime}}
, for
m
and
m^{\prime}
, satisfying the conditions in
statement (2) of Theorem
4.7
. Using Theorem
4.2
one can decide when two of
these codes are permutation equivalent:

(1)

On the one hand, by Theorem
4.2
,
E_{m}

and
E_{m^{\prime}}
are permutation equivalent if and only if
f_{m^{\prime}}\equiv f_{m}\circ T_{\mathbb{F}^{*}}

for some
T\in\langle A,B\rangle
(see the first part of the proof of Theorem
4.7
). On the
other hand,
f_{m}\circ A\equiv f_{m}
and
f_{m}\circ B\equiv f_{-m}
. This proves that

E_{m}
and
E_{m^{\prime}}
are permutation equivalent if and only if
m^{\prime}\equiv\pm m\mod(q-1)
.

(2)

On the other hand, in the second case of Theorem
4.7
,
k
is necessarily odd and

f_{m,m^{\prime}}
is determined by the values of
2m
and
m^{\prime}
modulo
q-1
. The equation

2X+k-1\equiv 0\mod(q-1)
has a unique solution modulo
\frac{q-1}{2}
, namely

\frac{1-k}{2}
.

(a)

If
q\equiv-1\mod 4
then the equation
4X+2(k-1)\equiv 0\mod(q-1)
has also a unique
solution modulo
\frac{q-1}{2}
, namely
\frac{1-k}{2}
and hence, in this case,

2m\equiv 1-k\mod q-1
. Thus

is either
f_{\frac{1-k}{2},\frac{1-k}{2}}=f_{\frac{1-k}{2}}

f_{\frac{1-k}{2},\frac{q-k}{2}}=f_{\frac{q-k}{2}}
. We conclude

(b)

Otherwise, that is for

has a unique solution modulo
\frac{q-1}{4}
, namely
\frac{1-k}{2}
. Therefore
