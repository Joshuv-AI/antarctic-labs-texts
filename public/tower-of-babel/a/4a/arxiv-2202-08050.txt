COMPUTING DISCRETE INVARIANTS OF
VARIETIES IN POSITIVE CHARACTERISTIC

Abstract




Abstract.
We develop a method to compute the Ekedahl–Oort type of a curve
C
over a field
k
of characteristic
p
(which is the isomorphism type of the
p
-kernel group scheme
J[p]
, where
J
is the Jacobian of
C
). Part of our method is general, in that we introduce the new notion of a Hasse–Witt triple, which re-encodes in a useful way the information contained in the Dieudonné module of
J[p]
. For complete intersection curves we then give a simple method to compute this Hasse–Witt triple. An implementation of this method is available in Magma.

AMS 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification:
11G20, 14Q05, 14L15

Key words:

p
-kernel group schemes, Jacobians, Dieudonné modules, Ekedahl–Oort types

1. Introduction

An elliptic curve
E
over a field
k
of characteristic
p
can be either ordinary or supersingular. If
E
is given by a homogeneous cubic equation
f=0
then
E
is supersingular if and only if the coefficient of
(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})^{p-1}
in
f^{p-1}
is non-zero. The goal of the present paper (and its sequel
[
4
]
) is to generalize this, first to more general curves, and later to certain varieties of higher dimension.

For the rest of this introduction, assume the ground field
k
is algebraically closed; let
\sigma
be its Frobenius automorphism. For curves
C
of genus
g
there are
2^{g}
possibilities for the isomorphism class of the group scheme
J[p]
, the
p
-kernel of the Jacobian of
C
. This isomorphism class is often referred to as the Ekedahl–Oort type of
C
. The Ekedahl–Oort stratification on the moduli space
\mathscr{A}_{g}
of
g
-dimensional abelian varieties (say with principal polarization) has been studied in great detail, and the underlying theory has been refined so as to be able to handle more general Shimura varieties. It appears, however, that not so many results concerning explicit calculations are as yet available. The main question that we answer in this paper is how, for a complete intersection curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
given by homogeneous equations
f_{1}=\cdots=f_{n-1}=0
and
p
not dividing the degrees of these equations, we can calculate the Ekedahl–Oort type of
C
. (For hyperelliptic curves, which are more amenable to explicit calculation, this question was answered in
[
1
]
.)

At the heart of the paper lies a new approach to such questions, which in the second part of this work will be extended to varieties of higher dimensions, or better: to cohomology in degree
>1
. The Ekedahl–Oort type of a curve
C
can be calculated from the Dieudonné module of
J[p]
, which is just the first de Rham cohomology group
H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(C/k)
, equipped with semi-linear operations
F
and
V
. Thinking of
H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}
as being an extension of
Q=H^{1}(C,\mathscr{O}_{C})
by its dual
Q^{\vee}\cong H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1}_{C})
, we realize that the induced action of
F
on
Q
(which is just the classical Hasse–Witt operator) is relatively easy to calculate. Unlike the case of elliptic curves, however, for
g\geq 2
knowing the Hasse–Witt operator
\Phi\colon Q\to Q
is in general not enough to determine the Ekedahl–Oort type of
C
. The idea that we pursue is that there is not too much information missing. We make this precise in the notion of a Hasse–Witt triple.

By definition, a Hasse–Witt triple
(Q,\Phi,\Psi)
consists of a finite dimensional
k
-vector space
Q
equipped with a
\sigma
-linear endomorphism
\Phi\colon Q\to Q
and a
\sigma
-linear bijective map
\Psi\colon\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)\to\mathrm{Coker}(\Phi)^{\vee}
. To a Dieudonné module of a
p
-kernel group scheme
G
(more precisely: a polarized
p
-kernel of a
p
-divisible group) one can associate a Hasse–Witt triple. There is also an easy way to go back from a HW-triple to a Dieudonné module. In Theorem
2.8
we show that this gives a bijective correspondence between isomorphism classes of Dieudonné modules and isomorphism classes of HW-triples.

The main point of the paper, then, is that the Hasse–Witt triple associated to a curve can be efficiently computed, at least for complete intersection curves if
p
does not divide the degrees of the equations. (We expect that something similar can be done in case the curve
C
is given to us in a different way, e.g., as a branched cover of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.) Let us describe the main result in the case of a plane curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
. The general case, which is given in Theorem
3.14
, is entirely similar but is notationally a little more involved.

Theorem
.

Let
C=\mathscr{Z}(f)\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
be a plane curve of degree
d\geq 3
over an algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic
p
with
p\nmid d
. Let
\mathsf{S}=k[X_{0},X_{1},X_{2}]
, and define
\mathsf{T}=k[X_{0}^{\pm 1},X_{1}^{\pm 1},X_{2}^{\pm 1}]/L
, where
L
is the
k
-linear span of all monomials
X_{0}^{e_{0}}X_{1}^{e_{1}}X_{2}^{e_{2}}
for which at least one of the exponents
e_{i}
is non-negative. Let
\mathsf{S}=\oplus_{m\geq 0}\,\mathsf{S}_{m}
and
\mathsf{T}=\oplus_{m\leq-3}\,\mathsf{T}_{m}
be the natural gradings. Define

Q=\mathsf{T}_{-d}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad Q^{\prime}=\bigl\{\xi\in\mathsf{T}_{-2d}\bigm|\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial X_{j}}\cdot\xi=0\ \text{in $\mathsf{T}_{-d-1}$, for all $j=0,1,2$}\bigr\}\,.

The space
\mathsf{U}=\bigl\{\xi\in\mathsf{T}_{-3d+3}\bigm|\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial X_{j}}\cdot\xi=0\quad\text{for all $j=0,1,2$}\bigr\}
is
1
-dimensional. Choose
0\neq\mathsf{u}\in\mathsf{U}
. Then
\mathsf{S}_{d-3}\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}Q^{\prime}
by
g\mapsto g\cdot\mathsf{u}
, and the bilinear map
Q\times Q^{\prime}\to k
that sends
(q,g\cdot\mathsf{u})
to the coefficient of
X_{0}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}X_{2}^{-1}
in
g\cdot q
is a perfect pairing. Let
\theta\colon Q^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}Q^{\vee}
be the associated isomorphism.

Define

\Phi\colon Q\to Q\qquad\text{by}\quad\Phi[A]=\bigl[f^{p-1}\cdot A^{p}\bigr]

and define

\Psi\colon\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)\to Q^{\vee}\qquad\text{by}\quad\Psi[A]=\theta\bigl[f^{p-2}\cdot A^{p}\bigr]\,.

Then
(Q,\Phi,\Psi)
is a Hasse–Witt triple whose associated Dieudonné module is isomorphic to the Dieudonné module of the group scheme
J[p]
, where
J
is the Jacobian of
C
.

In conclusion, to compute the Ekedahl–Oort type of
C
it suffices to enhance the pair consisting of the space
H^{1}(C,\mathscr{O}_{C})
with its Hasse–Witt operator
\Phi
to a Hasse–Witt triple, and for the new ingredient, the operator
\Psi
, we have a formula which is just as elegant as, and in fact very similar to, the classical formula for
\Phi
.

2. Dieudonné modules and Hasse–Witt triples

2.1

Let
k
be a perfect field of characteristic
p>0
with Frobenius automorphism
\sigma\colon k\to k
. We are interested in Dieudonné modules
(M,F,V)
, where:

(1)

M
is a finite dimensional
k
-vector space;

(2)

F\colon M\to M
is
\sigma
-linear and
V\colon M\to M
is
\sigma^{-1}
-linear;

(3)

\mathrm{Ker}(F)=\mathrm{Im}(V)
and
\mathrm{Ker}(V)=\mathrm{Im}(F)
.

These are precisely the Dieudonné modules associated with
p
-kernels of
p
-divisible groups. The term
Dieudonné module
will henceforth refer to triples
(M,F,V)
satisfying (1)–(3). We usually denote such a Dieudonné module by the single letter
M
, leaving
F
and
V
implicit.

Let
M
be a Dieudonné module over
k
. By a
polarization
of
M
we mean a non-degenerate alternating bilinear form
b\colon M\times M\to k
such that

b\bigl(F(x),y\bigr)=b\bigl(x,V(y)\bigr)^{p}\qquad\text{for all $x$, $y\in M$.}

(2.1.1)

If such a polarization exists, the dimension of
M
is even, say
\dim_{k}(M)=2g
, and the subspaces
\mathrm{Ker}(F)\subset M
and
\mathrm{Ker}(V)\subset M
are maximal isotropic.

If
(M,F,V,b)
is a polarized Dieudonné module, the Verschiebung
V
can be recovered from the remaining ingredients by (
2.1.1
).

2.2

Fix an integer
g\geq 1
and let
(W,S)
denote the Weyl group of the reductive group
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}
. Concretely,

W=\bigl\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{2g}\bigm|\pi(i)+\pi(2g+1-i)=2g+1\quad\text{for all $i\in\{1,\ldots,2g\}$}\bigr\}

(
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
= symmetric group on
n
leters), and
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{g}\}
with
s_{i}=(i\quad i+1)\,(2g-i\quad 2g-i+1)
for
i<g
and
s_{g}=(g\quad g+1)
. Let
X=S\setminus\{s_{g}\}
, and let
W_{X}\subset W
be the subgroup generated by
X
. Then
W_{X}\cong\mathfrak{S}_{g}
and
W_{X}\backslash W
is a set of
2^{g}
elements. For every class in
[w]\in W_{X}\backslash W
there is a unique representative
\dot{w}\in W
of minimal length.

To a polarized Dieudonné module
M
with
\dim_{k}(M)=2g
we can associate an element
w(M)\in W_{X}\backslash W
. Here we only give a quick summary of how this is done; for details see
[
3
]
and
[
6
]
. The first step is to build a symplectic flag

\mathscr{D}_{\bullet}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\qquad(0)=\mathscr{D}_{0}\subsetneq\mathscr{D}_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathscr{D}_{r}=M\,,

in the following way: We start with
(0)\subset M
. Then we apply the operations
F
(taking the image under
F
) and
V^{-1}
(taking the pre-image under
V
) to each term, which yields
(0)\subset V^{-1}(0)=FM\subset M
. This procedure we iterate. After finitely many iterations the process stabilizes; define
\mathscr{D}_{\bullet}
to be the flag in
M
that is obtained. Put differently,
\mathscr{D}_{\bullet}
is the coarsest flag that is stable under
F
and
V^{-1}
, in the sense that for any term
\mathscr{D}_{i}
there are indices
a
and
b
such that
F\mathscr{D}_{i}=\mathscr{D}_{a}
and
V^{-1}\mathscr{D}_{i}=\mathscr{D}_{b}
.

This “canonical flag”
\mathscr{D}_{\bullet}
is not, in general, a full flag in
M
. Let
\tilde{\mathscr{D}}_{\bullet}
be a refinement of
\mathscr{D}_{\bullet}
to a full symplectic flag. Then the relative position of the flags
(0)\subset\mathrm{Ker}(F)\subset M
and
\tilde{\mathscr{D}}_{\bullet}
is an element

\mathrm{relpos}\bigl(\mathrm{Ker}(F),\tilde{\mathscr{D}}_{\bullet}\bigr)\in W_{X}\backslash W

(see
[
3
]
, Section 3) that turns out to be independent of how we choose the refinement
\tilde{\mathscr{D}}_{\bullet}
. Now define
w(M)=\mathrm{relpos}\bigl(\mathrm{Ker}(F),\tilde{\mathscr{D}}_{\bullet}\bigr)
.

The following result is based on a classification result for Dieudonné modules that was proven by Kraft
[
2
]
(unpublished) and was later re-obtained by Ekedahl and Oort, see
[
6
]
. The result as we state it can also be found (stated using a different encoding) in
[
7
]
, Section 9; it is a special case of the results in
[
3
]
.

2.3
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic
p
. Then
M\mapsto w(M)
gives a bijection

\Biggl\{\vbox{\hbox{isomorphism classes of polarized Dieudonn\'{e} modules}\hbox to232.52637pt{\hfil$(M,F,V,b)$ over\penalty\ $k$ with $\dim_{k}(M)=2g$\hfil}}\Biggr\}\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}W_{X}\backslash W\,.

2.4
.

While from a theoretical perspective the encoding in terms of Weyl group cosets is the most natural choice, there are other ways to encode the isomorphism class of a given polarized Dieudonné module. For a nice discussion, see
[
8
]
, Section 3. Let us note that it is very easy to read off from
w(M)
more basic invariants of
M
such as the
p
-rank or the
a
-number. For instance, if
\dot{w}\in W
is the (unique) representative of
w(M)
of minimal length then the
p
-rank of
M
equals the number of indices
i\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
such that
\dot{w}(i)=i+g
, and the
a
-number is given by the number of indices
i\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
such that
\dot{w}(i)\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
.

2.5

In the next section we will describe a method to compute, for certain classes of curves
C
over a field
k
of characteristic
p
, the isomorphism class of the group scheme
J[p]
over
{\bar{k}}
, where
J
is the Jacobian of
C
. This isomorphism class is referred to as the Ekedahl–Oort type of
C
. Our method is based on the observation that the Dieudonné module of
J[p]
can be reconstructed from some data that are easier to compute, as we shall now explain.

As before, let
k
be a perfect field of characteristic
p>0
. By a
HW-triple over
k
(short for “Hasse–Witt triple”) we mean a triple
(Q,\Phi,\Psi)
where

(1)

Q
is a finite dimensional
k
-vector space;

(2)

\Phi\colon Q\to Q
is a
\sigma
-linear map;

(3)

\Psi\colon\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}
is a
\sigma
-linear bijective map.

Here
\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}\subset Q^{\vee}
is the subspace given by

\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}=\bigl\{\lambda\in Q^{\vee}\bigm|\lambda(q)=0\quad\text{for all $q\in\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)$}\bigr\}\,.

(Instead of
\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}
we could also write
\mathrm{Coker}(\Phi)^{\vee}
.) The name alludes to the fact that, in the example of a curve
C
over
k
, the map
\Phi
that we will consider is the Hasse–Witt operator on the space
Q=H^{1}(C,\mathscr{O}_{C})
. Note that the spaces
\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)
and
\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}
have the same dimension, so to check bijectivity of a
\sigma
-linear map
\Psi\colon\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)\to\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}
, it suffices to establish either its injectivity or surjectivity.

To a polarized Dieudonné module
(M,F,V,b)
we can associate a HW-triple by taking
Q=M/\mathrm{Ker}(F)
with
\Phi
given by the composition

M/\mathrm{Ker}(F)\xrightarrow{\penalty\ F\penalty\ }M\rightarrow\kern-8.0pt\rightarrow M/\mathrm{Ker}(F)\,.

For
x\in\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)
we have
F(x)\in\mathrm{Ker}(F)\subset M
, and because
\mathrm{Ker}(F)
is a maximal isotropic subspace of
M
the linear map
b\bigl(-,F(x)\bigr)\colon M\to k
is an element of
Q^{\vee}=\bigl(M/\mathrm{Ker}(F)\bigr)^{\vee}\subset M^{\vee}
. Define
\Psi\colon\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)\to Q^{\vee}
by
\Psi(x)=b\bigl(-,F(x)\bigr)
. Note that
\Psi
is injective, because
b\bigl(-,F(x)\bigr)=0
implies that
F(x)=0
and
F
is injective on
Q=M/\mathrm{Ker}(F)
. Further, for
x\in\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)
and
y\in Q
we have
b\bigl(\Phi(y),F(x)\bigr)=0
because
\Phi(y)\in Q
is the class of
F(y)
modulo
\mathrm{Ker}(F)
and
\mathrm{Im}(F)\subset M
is an isotropic subspace. This shows that
\Psi
takes values in
\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}
. As remarked above, the injectivity of
\Psi
implies that it is bijective; so
(Q,\Phi,\Psi)
is indeed a HW-triple.

2.6

In the opposite direction, we can associate to a HW-triple
(Q,\Phi,\Psi)
a polarized Dieudonné module, well-determined up to isomorphism. This works as follows. Define
M=Q\oplus Q^{\vee}
, and let
b\colon M\times M\to k
be the form given by
b\bigl((q,\lambda),(q^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})\bigr)=\lambda^{\prime}(q)-\lambda(q^{\prime})
.

Write
R_{1}=\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)\subset Q
and choose a subspace
R_{0}\subset Q
which is a complement of
R_{1}
. Then we can define

F\colon M=R_{0}\oplus R_{1}\oplus Q^{\vee}\longrightarrow M=Q\oplus Q^{\vee}

by
F(r_{0},r_{1},\lambda)=\bigl(\Phi(r_{0}),\Psi(r_{1})\bigr)
, and we let
V
be the unique map such that (
2.1.1
) is satisfied. To describe
V
more explicitly, note that the maps

\Psi\colon R_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}=\bigl(Q/\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)\bigr)^{\vee}\subset Q^{\vee}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Phi\colon R_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)\subset Q

dualize to
\sigma^{-1}
-linear maps
\Psi^{\vee}\colon Q/\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}R_{1}^{\vee}
and
\Phi^{\vee}\colon Q^{\vee}/\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}=\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\vee}\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}R_{0}^{\vee}
; now

V\colon M=Q\oplus Q^{\vee}\longrightarrow M=Q\oplus R_{0}^{\vee}\oplus R_{1}^{\vee}

is given by

V(q,\lambda)=\bigl(0,\Phi^{\vee}(\lambda\bmod\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}),-\Psi^{\vee}(q\bmod\mathrm{Im}(\Phi))\bigr)\,.

As it is clear that
\mathrm{Im}(F)=\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)\oplus\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}=\mathrm{Ker}(V)
and
\mathrm{Ker}(F)=(0)\oplus Q^{\vee}=\mathrm{Im}(V)
, it follows that
(M,F,V,b)
is a polarized Dieudonné module.

2.7
.

In the construction of
2.6
, the isomorphism class of the polarized Dieudonné module
(M,F,V,b)
is independent of the choice of
R_{0}\subset Q
.

Proof.

Let
R_{0}^{\prime}\subset Q
be another complement of
R_{1}
, and let
F^{\prime}\colon M\to M
and
V^{\prime}\colon M\to M
be the associated Frobenius and Verschiebung on
M
. There exists a linear map
t\colon R_{0}\to R_{1}
such that
R_{0}^{\prime}\subset Q=R_{0}\oplus R_{1}
is the graph of
t
. Let
m=(q,\lambda)
be an element of
M
. If
q=(r_{0},r_{1})\in R_{0}\oplus R_{1}
then
q=\bigl(r_{0},t(r_{0})\bigr)+\bigl(0,r_{1}-t(r_{0})\bigr)
with
\bigl(r_{0},t(r_{0})\bigr)\in R_{0}^{\prime}
and
\bigl(0,r_{1}-t(r_{0})\bigr)\in R_{1}
. Because
R_{1}=\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)
we find that

F(m)=\bigl(\Phi(r_{0}),\Psi(r_{1})\bigr)\qquad\text{and}\qquad F^{\prime}(m)=\bigl(\Phi(r_{0}),\Psi(r_{1}-t(r_{0}))\bigr)\,.

The main point is now to show that there exists a self-dual linear map
u\colon Q\to Q^{\vee}
such that
u\circ\Phi=-\Psi\circ t
as maps from
R_{0}
to
Q^{\vee}
. Since the map
\Phi\colon R_{0}\to Q
is injective and
\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)=\Phi(R_{0})
, we can define a linear map
u_{0}\colon\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)\to\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}\subset Q^{\vee}
by the rule
u_{0}\bigl(\Phi(r_{0})\bigr)=-\Psi\bigl(t(r_{0})\bigr)
. As the domain of
u_{0}
is
\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)
and
u_{0}
takes values in
\mathrm{Im}(\Phi)^{\perp}
, we can find a self-dual
u\colon Q\to Q^{\vee}
that extends
u_{0}
. Now define
\alpha\colon M\to M
by
\alpha(q,\lambda)=(q,\lambda+u(q))
. Then one readily verifies that
\alpha
is a symplectic automorphism of
(M,b)
with
\alpha\circ F=F^{\prime}\circ\alpha
, and hence also
\alpha\circ V=V^{\prime}\circ\alpha
.
∎

2.8
.

Let
k
be a perfect field of characteristic
p
. Then the construction given in
2.5
gives a bijection

\Biggl\{\vbox{\hbox{isomorphism classes of polarized Dieudonn\'{e}}\hbox to194.44882pt{\hfil modules $(M,F,V,b)$ with $\dim_{k}(M)=2g$\hfil}}\Biggr\}\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}\Biggl\{\vbox{\hbox{isomorphism classes of HW-triples}\hbox to151.33237pt{\hfil$(Q,\Phi,\Psi)$ over\penalty\ $k$ with $\dim_{k}(Q)=g$\hfil}}\Biggr\}

whose inverse is given by the construction in
2.6
.

Proof.

Let
(Q,\Phi,\Psi)
be a HW-triple, choose a
R_{0}\subset Q
which is a complement of
R_{1}=\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)
, and define
(M,F,V,b)
as in
2.6
. Now it is a routine verification that if we apply the construction of
2.5
to
(M,F,V,b)
we recover the original HW-triple.

In the opposite direction, start with a polarized Dieudonné module
(M,F_{M},V_{M},b_{M})
, and let
(Q,\Phi,\Psi)
be the associated HW-triple. Define
N=Q\oplus Q^{\vee}
, and equip it with the alternating bilinear form
b_{N}
given by
b_{N}\bigl((q,\lambda),(q^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})\bigr)=\lambda^{\prime}(q)-\lambda(q^{\prime})
. Choose a complement
R_{0}\subset Q
of
R_{1}=\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)
, and let
F_{N}
and
V_{N}
be the associated Frobenius and Verschiebung on
N
, as in
2.6
.

Let
\mathrm{pr}\colon M\to Q
be the projection map, and choose a totally isotropic subspace
L\subset M
(with respect to the form
b_{M}
) such that
\mathrm{Ker}(F_{M})\cap L=(0)
. The composition
L\hookrightarrow M\xrightarrow{\mathrm{pr}}Q
is an isomorphism; write
j\colon Q\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}L
for the inverse. We then obtain an isometry
h\colon N\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}M
by sending
(q,\lambda)\in Q\oplus Q^{\vee}
to
x_{\lambda}-j(q)
, where
x_{\lambda}\in\mathrm{Ker}(F_{M})
is the unique element with
\lambda(y)=b_{M}\bigl(x_{\lambda},j(y)\bigr)
for all
y\in Q
.

Let
F_{Q}\colon Q\hookrightarrow M
be the map induced by
F_{M}
. Note that
F_{Q}(R_{0})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(F_{M})=(0)
, and because
F_{Q}(R_{0})\subset\mathrm{Im}(F_{M})
is an isotropic subspace of
M
, we may choose the maximal isotropic
L\subset M
as above in such a way that
F_{Q}(R_{0})\subset L
. We are done if we can show that with this choice of
L
we have
F_{M}\circ h=h\circ F_{N}
. For this, let
(q,\lambda)
be an element of
N=Q\oplus Q^{\vee}
, and write
q=r_{0}+r_{1}
. Then
(F_{M}\circ h)\bigl(q,\lambda\bigr)=-F_{M}\bigl(j(q)\bigr)=-F_{M}\bigl(j(r_{0})\bigr)-F_{M}\bigl(j(r_{1})\bigr)
. On the other hand,
F_{N}(q,\lambda)=\bigl(\Phi(r_{0}),\Psi(r_{1})\bigr)
, so that

(h\circ F_{N})\bigl(q,\lambda\bigr)=x_{\Psi(r_{1})}-j\bigl(\Phi(r_{0})\bigr)\,,

where
x_{\Psi(r_{1})}\in\mathrm{Ker}(F_{M})
is the unique element with
\Psi(r_{1})\bigl(y\bigr)=b\bigl(x_{\Psi(r_{1})},j(y)\bigr)
for all
y\in Q
. But if
\tilde{y}\in M
is any representative of
y
then we have
\Psi(r_{1})\bigl(y\bigr)=b\bigl(\tilde{y},F_{Q}(r_{1})\bigr)
by definition of
\Psi
, and since we may choose
\tilde{y}=j(y)
it follows that
x_{\Psi(r_{1})}=-F_{Q}(r_{1})=-F_{M}\bigl(j(r_{1})\bigr)
. (Note that indeed
-F_{Q}(r_{1})\in\mathrm{Ker}(F_{M})
because
r_{1}\in R_{1}=\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)
.) On the other hand,
F_{Q}(r_{0})
and
j\bigl(\Phi(r_{0})\bigr)
are two elements of
L
that under the projection to
Q
both map to
\Phi(r_{0})
; hence
j\bigl(\Phi(r_{0})\bigr)=F_{Q}(r_{0})
, which is the same as
F_{M}\bigl(j(r_{0})\bigr)
. This shows that indeed
F_{M}\circ h=h\circ F_{N}
.
∎

2.9

There is an equivalence relation on HW-triples that is weaker than isomorphy and that will be convenient for us to use. Namely, if
(Q_{1},\Phi_{1},\Psi_{1})
and
(Q_{2},\Phi_{2},\Psi_{2})
are two HW-triples, we call a
k
-linear bijective map
f\colon Q_{1}\to Q_{2}
an
equivalence
if there exist constants
c
,
d\in k^{*}
such that
\Phi_{2}\circ f=c\cdot(f\circ\Phi_{1})
and
\Psi_{2}\circ f=d\cdot(f^{\vee,-1}\circ\Psi_{1})
.

2.10
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic
p>0
. If two HW-triples over
k
are equivalent then they are in fact isomorphic.

Note that the assumption that
k
is algebraically closed cannot be dropped. The reader is invited to skip the proof upon first reading.

Proof.

Let
(Q,\Phi,\Psi)
be a HW-triple over
k
, and choose a decomposition
Q=R_{0}\oplus R_{1}
with
R_{1}=\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)
. For
c
,
d\in k^{*}
, let
M_{c,d}
be the polarized Dieudonné module associated, via the construction in
2.6
, with the HW-triple
(Q,c\cdot\Phi,d\cdot\Psi)
. By Theorem
2.8
, it suffices to show that
M_{c,d}\cong M_{1,1}
. The proof of this is based on the results of Kraft in
[
2
]
. As a first step we recall how to construct certain “standard Dieudonné modules”. Start with a natural number
q
, a map
X\colon(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})\to\{F,V\}
, and a
q
-tuple
a=(a_{i})
in
(k^{*})^{\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}}
. Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}}
be the standard basis of
k^{\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}}
. We give
M_{X,a}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=k^{\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}}
the structure of a Dieudonné module by declaring that
F(e_{i})=a_{i}\cdot e_{i+1}
if
X(i)=F
and
V(a_{i}\cdot e_{i+1})=e_{i}
if
X(i)=V
. In combination with the requirement that
\mathrm{Ker}(F)=\mathrm{Im}(V)
and
\mathrm{Ker}(V)=\mathrm{Im}(F)
, these rules uniquely determine the structure of a Dieudonné module; for instance, if
X(i)=V
then
V(a_{i}\cdot e_{i+1})=e_{i}
forces
F(e_{i})=0
. If
a_{i}=1
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
then we write
M_{X}
instead of
M_{X,a}
. Note that
M_{X,a}\cong M_{X}
for all
a\in(k^{*})^{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}
; an isomorphism
M_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}M_{X,a}
is obtained by
e_{i}\mapsto c_{i}\cdot e_{i}
for suitable constants
c_{i}
. We will need the more general form
M_{X,a}
later.

As we shall discuss next, every polarized Dieudonné module can be described in terms of the following two basic cases:

Case 1:
In the above construction, suppose
q=2r
is even and the function
X\colon(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})\to\{F,V\}
has the property that
X(i+r)=F\iff X(i)=V
. Choose
\zeta\in k^{*}
with
\zeta^{p^{r}}=-1
. Then the alternating bilinear form
b\colon M_{X}\times M_{X}\to k
defined by the property that
b(e_{i},e_{j})=0
if
j\neq i+r
and
b(e_{i},e_{i+r})=\zeta^{p^{i}}
is a polarization of
M_{X}
.

Case 2:
In the above construction, define
\check{X}\colon(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})\to\{F,V\}
by the rule
\check{X}(i)=F\iff X(i)=V
. Let
\{\check{e}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}}
denote the standard basis of
M_{\check{X}}
. Then the form
b\colon(M_{X}\oplus M_{\check{X}})\times(M_{X}\oplus M_{\check{X}})\to k
determined by the rules

b(e_{i},e_{j})=0=b(\check{e}_{i},\check{e}_{j})\,,\qquad b(e_{i},\check{e}_{j})=\delta_{ij}\,,\qquad b(\check{e}_{i},e_{j})=-\delta_{ij}

is a polarization of the Dieudonné module
M_{X}\oplus M_{\check{X}}
.

We will make use of the following facts:

(1)

Every Dieudonné module over
k
is isomorphic to a direct sum of Dieudonné modules of the form
M_{X}
.
This result is due to Kraft
[
2
]
.

(2)

If
N
is a Dieudonné module over
k
that admits a polarization
b\colon N\times N\to k
then this polarization is unique up to isomorphism. In other words: if
b^{\prime}
is another polarization of
N
then
(N,b)\cong(N,b^{\prime})
. This was proven by Oort; see
[
7
]
, Theorem 9.4.

(3)

Every polarized Dieudonné module is isomorphic to a direct sum of terms
(M_{X},b)
as in Case 1 and
(M_{X}\oplus M_{\check{X}},b)
as in Case 2. (This follows without much difficulty from the previous two facts.)

To conclude the proof we may now assume that
(Q,\Phi,\Psi)
is the HW-triple associated with a polarized Dieudonné module
(M_{X},b)
as in Case 1 or a polarized Dieudonné module
(M_{X}\oplus M_{\check{X}},b)
as in Case 2. We first treat Case 1. Let
c
,
d\in k^{*}
be given. We define a sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/2r\mathbb{Z}}
as follows. If
X(i)=X(i+1)=F
, let
a_{i}=c
. If
X(i)=F
and
X(i+1)=V
, let
a_{i}=d
. If
X(i)=V
then
X(i+r)=F
, so that
a_{i+r}
has already been defined; in this case let
a_{i}=a_{i+r}^{-1}
. One checks that the same form
b
as in Case 1 above defines a polarization on
M_{X,a}
and that the associated HW-triple is
(Q,c\cdot\Phi,d\cdot\Psi)
.

In Case 2 the calculation is similar but we need to introduce some more notation. Let
I=(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})\coprod(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})
and let
i\mapsto\bar{i}
be the involution of
I
that exchanges the two copies of
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
. Define a sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in I}
by starting with the rule that
a_{i}=c
if
X(i)=X(i+1)=F
and
a_{i}=d
if
X(i)=F
and
X(i+1)=V
. If
X(i)=V
then
X(\bar{i})=F
so that
a_{\bar{i}}
has already been defined, and we take
a_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(a_{\bar{i}})^{-1}
. Again one checks by direct calculation that the same form
b
as in Case 2 above defines a polarization on
M_{X,a}\oplus M_{\check{X},a}
and that the associated HW-triple is
(Q,c\cdot\Phi,d\cdot\Psi)
. This completes the proof.
∎

3. Complete intersection curves

In this section we describe a method to calculate the equivalence class of the Hasse–Witt triple associated with a complete intersection curve, assuming the characteristic of our field does not divide the degrees of the equations. When combined with the results of the previous section, this gives a way to compute the Ekedahl–Oort type of such curves.

All curves we consider are assumed to be complete and non-singular.

3.1

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic
p>0
with Frobenius automorphism
\sigma\colon k\to k
. Let
C/k
be a curve of genus
g\geq 1
, and let
J
be the Jacobian of
C
. Write
M=H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(C/k)
. As is well-known (see
[
5
]
, Section 5) we have a
\sigma
-linear Frobenius
F\colon M\to M
and a
\sigma^{-1}
-linear Verschiebung

such that
(M,F,V)
is the (contravariant) Dieudonné module of the
p
-kernel group scheme
J[p]
. The principal polarization of
J
gives rise to an alternating bilinear form
b\colon M\times M\to k
satisfying (
2.1.1
), so that
(M,F,V,b)
is a polarized Dieudonné module as in
2.1
.

For the discussion that follows, it is important to recall that we have natural isomorphisms
\mathrm{Ker}(F)\cong H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1}_{C})
and
M/\mathrm{Ker}(F)\cong H^{1}(C,\mathscr{O}_{C})
. The HW-triple associated with
M
, as in
2.5
is of the form
\mathscr{Q}_{C}=\bigl(H^{1}(C,\mathscr{O}_{C}),\Phi_{C},\Psi_{C}\bigr)
, where the operator
\Phi_{C}
is the classical Hasse–Witt operator on
H^{1}(C,\mathscr{O}_{C})
, induced by the absolute Frobenius on
\mathscr{O}_{C}
. The operator
\Psi_{C}
may be viewed as an injective
\sigma
-linear map
\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi_{C})\to H^{0}(C,\Omega^{1}_{C})
.

3.2

Let
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}^{n}
for some

, and let

for all

.

The following convention will be in force:

If a finite set

is written as

, it is assumed that
v_{1}<v_{2}<\cdots<v_{m}

(3.2.1)

(In particular,
m=|V|

We will compute cohomology groups using Čech cohomology with respect to the standard open cover

of
\mathbb{P}
. For

. If

is an abelian sheaf on

, the direct sum running over all subsets

of

are defined as in
[
9

Tag 01FH
]

Let

and

\mathsf{S}(C)=\bigoplus_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}\,H^{0}\bigl(C,\mathscr{O}_{C}(r)\bigr)=\mathsf{S}(\mathbb{P})/I_{C}\,,

where

\mathsf{T}(C)=(0\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I_{C})=\bigl\{t\in\mathsf{T}(\mathbb{P})\bigm|\text{$f_{i}\cdot t=0$ for all\penalty\ $i$}\bigr\}\,,

which is a graded

-module.

3.3

Let
\mathscr{E}=\oplus_{i=1}^{n-1}\,\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(-d_{i})
. Consider the Koszul complex

K_{\bullet}=K_{\bullet}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\qquad\wedge^{n-1}\mathscr{E}\longrightarrow\wedge^{n-2}\mathscr{E}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow\mathscr{E}\longrightarrow\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}}\,,

with

, the sum running over all subsets

and

The Koszul complex is a resolution of

. As the sheaves

have zero cohomology in degrees

, we obtain isomorphisms

(3.3.1)

Let
v_{r}\colon H^{1}\bigl(C,\mathscr{O}_{C}(r)\bigr)\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to15.0pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\;\,\sim\;\;}}\mathsf{T}(C)_{r-d}
be the isomorphism thus obtained. For later use, note that if

and

.

3.4

For the calculations that follow, we need to introduce some signs. The setting for this is that we consider a finite set of integers

and an integer

. Write

(with convention (

)), and let

. Then we let

3.5

A class in

can be represented by a cocycle

with

\alpha_{m}\in C^{m+1}(\wedge^{m}\mathscr{E})=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subset\{0,\ldots,n\}\\
|J|=m+2\end{subarray}}\;\bigoplus_{{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\\
|I|=m\end{subarray}}}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(-d_{I})\bigl(U_{J}\bigr)\,.

Let
\alpha_{m,I,J}
denote the component of

in

(3.5.1)

Under the left isomorphism in (
3.3.1
) (taking

) the class
[\alpha]\in H^{1}(\mathbb{P},K_{\bullet})
is sent to the class in

3.6

As in

For

4. A brief description of the Magma implementation

4.1

Jointly with Wieb Bosma (who wrote most of the code), we have implemented our method to calculate the Ekedahl–Oort type of a plane curve in Magma. One of the referees has kindly provided a cleaned-up version of the code, and has given permission to make it available on the author’s webpage. On their request we outline how the implementation works.

To use the code, run
Attach("EOType.txt")
. If a polynomial
f
in three variables over a field of characteristic
p
has been created, calling
EOType(f)
then returns the Ekedahl–Oort type of the plane curve defined by
f
. (It is assumed that
f
is homogeneous of degree
d\geq 3
with
p\nmid d
, and that the curve defined by it is smooth over the base field; if not, a message is returned.)

4.2 Overview.

After checking if the above conditions on
f
are satisfied,
EOType
successively calls three functions
HWtriple
,
DieudMod
, and
WeylGrElt
. The function
HWtriple
implements the theorem stated in the introduction. Next
DieudMod
converts the Hasse–Witt triple into a Dieudonné module, following the method explained in Section
2
. Finally,
WeylGrElt
computes the Weyl group coset that represents the isomorphism class of the Dieudonné module, under the correspondence given in Theorem
2.3
. More details about these functions are given below.

Two general comments: (a) We represent
\sigma
-linear maps by ordinary matrices, choosing bases for the spaces involved. E.g., in the code
APhi
and
APsi
are matrices that represent the maps
\Phi
and
\Psi
that are part of a Hasse–Witt triple. (b) Elements of the space
\mathsf{T}
(notation as in the theorem stated in the introduction) can be represented by Laurent polynomials, but this turns out to be inconvenient because Magma functions such as
MonomialCoefficient
are only available for polynomials. The solution we use is to multiply Laurent polynomials by a sufficiently high power of
(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})
to ensure that we obtain ordinary polynomials.

4.3 The function
HWtriple
.

This function takes as inputs a field
k
, an integer
d
and a polynomial
f
. It outputs a string
s
, a matrix
A(\Phi)
of size
g\times g
, where
g=(d-1)(d-2)/2
, a basis
\kappa=\{\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{h}\}
of the kernel of the
k
-linear map
k^{g}\to k^{g}
given by
A(\Phi)
, and a matrix
A(\Psi)
of size
g\times h
. (The integer
h
is not known a priori). The purpose of the string
s
is only to avoid unnecessary calculations: it will be assigned one of the values
ordinary
,
superspecial
or
interesting
. In the first two cases (which are detected as soon as we have the Hasse–Witt matrix
A(\Phi)
), no further work is required and we can directly output the Ekedahl–Oort type.

A basis for the space
Q
is given by the classes of the monomials
m_{i}^{-1}\cdot(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})^{-1}
, where
m_{1},\ldots,m_{g}
are all monomials in
k[X_{0},X_{1},X_{2}]
of degree
d-3
. These monomials are stored in a sequence called
Md
. Next the Hasse–Witt matrix
A(\Phi)
with respect to this basis is calculated. First we store
F=f^{p-2}
. (For
A(\Phi)
we need
f^{p-1}
; but we again need
f^{p-2}
later.) The matrix coefficient
A(\Phi)_{ij}
is the coefficient of
m_{j}^{p}\cdot(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})^{(p-1)}
in
f^{p-1}\cdot m_{i}=f\cdot F\cdot m_{i}
. If
A(\Phi)
is either invertible (ordinary case) or zero (superspecial case), we can stop.

If the curve is neither ordinary nor superspecial, we go on to store a basis
\kappa=\{\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{h}\}
of the kernel of the linear map
A(\Phi)\colon k^{g}\to k^{g}
. Note that
k^{g}
represents the space
Q
through the chosen basis of
Q
, and that a basis of the kernel of the
\sigma
-linear map
\Phi\colon Q\to Q
is given by the vectors
{}^{\tau}\kappa_{j}
, where
\tau
is the inverse of
\sigma
. Next we store bases for the spaces
\mathsf{T}_{-2d+2}
and
\mathsf{T}_{-3d+3}
. As explained above, we want to work with ordinary polynomials instead of Laurent polynomials; for this reason, the elements that we use are in fact
(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})^{3d-3}
times a basis. Then we calculate the partial derivatives
\partial f/\partial X_{i}
and we compute the matrix
Multdf
which represents the linear map
\mathsf{T}_{-3d+3}\to\mathsf{T}_{-2d+2}^{\oplus 3}
given by
\xi\mapsto\bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial X_{0}}\cdot\xi,\;\frac{\partial f}{\partial X_{1}}\cdot\xi,\;\frac{\partial f}{\partial X_{2}}\cdot\xi\bigr)
. By definition,
\mathsf{U}
is the kernel of this map. We choose a generator; but for the same reason as above, what we store is not a generator
\mathsf{u}
of
\mathsf{U}
but rather
\tilde{\mathsf{u}}=(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})^{3d-3}\cdot\mathsf{u}
, which in the code is called
utilde
. The elements
m_{i}\cdot\mathsf{u}
form a basis of the space
Q^{\prime}\cong Q^{\vee}
which is dual to the chosen basis
\{m_{i}^{-1}\cdot(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})^{-1}\}
of
Q
.

The final step of
HWtriple
is the calculation of the
g\times h
matrix
A(\Psi)
. If we write
\kappa_{j}
as a column matrix with entries
\kappa^{(j)}_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,g
), the
j
th column of the matrix
A(\Psi)
is obtained by solving

A(\Psi)_{1j}\cdot m_{1}\cdot\mathsf{u}+\cdots+A(\Psi)_{gj}\cdot m_{g}\cdot\mathsf{u}=f^{p-2}\cdot\Bigl(\kappa_{1}^{(j)}\cdot m_{1}^{-p}\cdot X^{-p\cdot\mathbf{1}}+\cdots+\kappa_{g}^{(j)}\cdot m_{g}^{-p}\cdot X^{-p\cdot\mathbf{1}}\Bigr)\,.

(4.3.1)

(This is an equation in
\mathsf{T}_{-2d}
, and
X^{-p\cdot\mathbf{1}}
means
(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})^{-p}
. Note that the
j
th column of the matrix
A(\Psi)
is the vector
\Psi({}^{\tau}\kappa_{j})
; as
\Psi\colon\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi)\to Q^{\prime}
is given by
[A]\mapsto[f^{p-2}\cdot A^{p}]
, this leads to equation (
4.3.1
) for the coefficients of
A(\Psi)
.)

Let
c=(2d-1)(2d-2)/2
, which is the number of monomials in
k[X_{0},X_{1},X_{2}]
of degree
2d-3
, and let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{c}
be those monomials. Because Magma’s default is to let matrices act from the right, (
4.3.1
) is written as the matrix equation

{}^{\mathsf{t}}A(\Psi)\cdot B=\kappa\cdot C\,,

(4.3.2)

where
B
and
C
are the matrices of size
g\times c
whose rows express the
m_{i}\cdot\mathsf{u}
(resp. the
f^{p-2}\cdot m_{i}^{-p}\cdot(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})^{-p}
) as vectors with respect to the basis
\{M_{j}^{-1}\cdot(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})^{-1}\}_{j=1,\ldots,c}
of
\mathsf{T}_{-2d}
, and where
\kappa
now is the matrix of size
h\times g
whose rows give the vectors
\kappa_{j}
. Concretely,
B_{ji}
is the coefficient of
(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})^{3d-4}
in
M_{i}\cdot m_{j}\cdot\tilde{\mathsf{u}}
, and
C_{ji}
is the coefficient of
(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})^{p-1}\cdot m_{j}^{p}
in
f^{p-2}\cdot M_{i}
. (Recall that
F=f^{p-2}
has been calculated before and that we have stored
\tilde{\mathsf{u}}=(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2})^{3d-3}\cdot\mathsf{u}
.) Then (
4.3.2
) is solved using Magma’s function
IsConsistent
.

4.4 The function
DieudMod
.

This function takes as input a field
k
, a positive integer
d
, a matrix
A(\Phi)
of size
g\times g
, where
g=(d-1)(d-2)/2
, a basis
\{\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{h}\}
of the kernel of the
k
-linear map
k^{g}\to k^{g}
given by
A(\Phi)
, and a matrix
A(\Psi)
of size
g\times h
. It outputs a matrix
A(F)
of size
2g\times g
whose columns are linearly independent.

We first find a subset
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{g-h}\}\subset\{1,\ldots,g\}
such that
\mathrm{Span}(e_{i};i\in I)
is a complement of
\{\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{h}\}
inside
k^{g}
. To obtain the
j
th column of the matrix
A(F)
, write the standard base vector
e_{j}
in the form

e_{j}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{g-h}\;a_{\mu}\cdot e_{i_{\mu}}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{h}\;b_{\nu}\cdot\kappa_{\nu}\,.

(4.4.1)

Then the output is the matrix
A(F)
given by

A(F)_{rj}=\begin{cases}\sum_{\mu=1}^{g-h}\;a_{\mu}\cdot A(\Phi)_{r,i_{\mu}}&r=1,\ldots,g\\
\sum_{\nu=1}^{h}\;b_{\nu}\cdot A(\Psi)_{2g+1-r,\nu}&r=g+1,\ldots,2g.\end{cases}

To explain why this is what we want, recall that the goal is to give the matrix of
F\colon M\to M
, where
M=Q\oplus Q^{\vee}
. (See Section
2.6
.) As
F
factors through the projection
M\to Q
, we only need to give the first
g
columns. We are identifying
Q
with
k^{g}
via the basis
\{m_{i}^{-1}\cdot X^{-\mathbf{1}}\}_{i=1,\ldots,g}
. The dual vector space
Q^{\vee}
is identified with
Q^{\prime}
via the choice of a generator
\mathsf{u}\in\mathsf{U}
, and the dual basis of
Q^{\prime}
is
\{m_{i}\cdot\mathsf{u}\}_{i=1,\ldots,g}
. However, as a preparation for the next step we want to use
e_{1},\ldots,e_{g},\check{e}_{g},\ldots,\check{e}_{1}
(note the order!) as a basis for
M=Q\oplus Q^{\vee}=(k^{g})\oplus(k^{g})^{\vee}
, where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{g}
is the standard basis of
k^{g}
.

By our choice of
I
, the space
R_{0}=\mathrm{Span}(e_{i})_{i\in I}
is a complement of
{}^{\sigma}R_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{Span}(\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{h})
inside
k^{g}
. Then
R_{0}
is also a complement of
R_{1}=\mathrm{Ker}(\Psi)=\mathrm{Span}({}^{\tau}\kappa_{1},\ldots,{}^{\tau}\kappa_{h})
, where we recall that
\tau=\sigma^{-1}
. With notation as in (
4.4.1
), the decomposition of
e_{j}
corresponding to
k^{g}=R_{0}\oplus R_{1}
is given by
e_{j}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{g-h}\;{}^{\tau}a_{\mu}\cdot e_{i_{\mu}}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{h}\;{}^{\tau}b_{\nu}\cdot{}^{\tau}\kappa_{\nu}
. This gives the stated formula for the coefficients of the matrix
A(F)
, where the lower half of each column is put upside down because we order the base vectors of
(k^{g})^{\vee}
as
\check{e}_{g},\ldots,\check{e}_{1}
.

4.5 The function
WeylGrElt
.

In this step we calculate (the minimal representative of) the Weyl group coset that, under the bijection in Theorem
2.3
, corresponds to our Dieudonné module. As outlined in Section
2.2
, we have to determine the canonical flag for this. Rather than using the
\sigma^{-1}
-linear Verschiebung, we build this flag using the operations
F
and
\perp
(taking orthogonal complement), as this is more convenient. (The result is the same.) For the conversion to a Weyl group element, we then follow
[
3
]
, Section 3.6. In the version of the code provided by the referee, most of the original function
WeylGrElt
has been moved to a new function called
EOseq
, and in addition to the Weyl group representative also the corresponding so-called “final type” is given.

The function
WeylGrElt
takes as input a field
k
, a positive integer
d
, and a matrix
A(F)
of size
2g\times g
whose columns are linearly independent. It outputs an element
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{2g}
(the symmetric group on
2g
letters), as well as a sequence of integers of length
2g+1
(the final type). There are several steps that are carried out. In steps (1)–(3) we (partially) fill a table, whose initial state is the following:

\begin{array}[]{| r || c | c | c | c | c | c | c | c | c |}\hline\cr i&0&1&2&\cdots&g-1&g&g+1&\cdots&2g\\
\hline\cr\mathrm{Basis}(i)&\emptyset&&&&&\text{the $g$ columns of $A(F)$}&&&\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{2g}\}\\
\hline\cr f(i)&0&&&&&&&&g\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

where
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{2g}\}
denotes the standard basis of
k^{2g}
. If
\mathrm{Basis}(i)
is defined, it consists of a set of
i
linearly independent vectors in
k^{2g}
, and if
f(i)
is defined, it is an integer with
0\leq f(i)\leq i
. The calculation involves finding the perpendiculars of certain subspaces
W\subset k^{2g}
with respect to the symplectic form on
k^{2g}
that is represented by the block matrix
\bigl({0\atop-\mathsf{J}}{\mathsf{J}\atop 0}\bigr)
, where
\mathsf{J}
denotes the anti-diagonal matrix of size
g\times g
with all anti-diagonal entries equal to
1
. In step (4) we will then define
f(i)
for all
i
, and in step (5) we convert the sequence
f
into a permutation.

In more detail, here is what happens.

(1)

Create a table as above.

(2)

Search for the first index
i
such that
\mathrm{Basis}(i)
is defined, but
f(i)
is not yet defined. If there is no such
i
(in the range
1,\ldots,2g
), go to step (4). If
\mathrm{Basis}(i)=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{i}\}
, calculate the vectors
A(F)\bigl({}^{\sigma}b_{j}\bigr)
(
j=1,\ldots,i
), and let
f(i)
be the dimension of their
k
-linear span.

(3)

If
\mathrm{Basis}\bigl(f(i)\bigr)
is already defined, again do step (2). If not, do the following:

•

Among the vectors
A(F)\bigl({}^{\sigma}b_{1}\bigr),\ldots,A(F)\bigl({}^{\sigma}b_{i}\bigr)
, find a maximal linearly independent subset, say
\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{f(i)}\}
, and store this collection as
\mathrm{Basis}\bigl(f(i)\bigr)
.

•

Find a basis for the space
\mathrm{Span}\bigl(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{f(i)}\bigr)^{\perp}
and store this as
\mathrm{Basis}\bigl(2g-f(i)\bigr)
.

After this, return to step (2).

(4)

If
f(i)
is defined for all
i
, go to step (5). Otherwise, find the first value
a
for which
f(a)
is still undefined, and let
b
be the next value for which
f(b)
is defined. It will be true that either
f(a-1)=f(b)
or that
f(b)=f(a-1)+(b-a+1)
; in the first case, set
f(a),f(a+1),\ldots,f(b-1)
all equal to
f(a-1)
, in the second case define
f(i)
for
a\leq i<b
by the rule
f(i)=f(a-1)+(i-a+1)
. Now repeat this step.

(5)

Let
j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{g}
be the values in
\{1,2,\ldots,2g\}
with the property that
f(j)=f(j-1)
. (There will be precisely
g
such values.) Let
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{g}
be the remaining values. Define a function
w\colon\{1,2,\ldots,2g\}\to\{1,2,\ldots,2g\}
by
w(j_{m})=m
and
w(i_{m})=g+m
. Finally, return the Weyl group element

\left[\begin{matrix}1&2&\cdots&g&g+1&\cdots&2g\\
w(1)&w(2)&\cdots&w(g)&w(g+1)&\cdots&w(2g)\end{matrix}\right]

(given as a product of cycles), and the final type, which is the sequence of numbers
f(i)
.

It should be noted that in the actual Magma implementation, the index
i
in our table runs from
1
to
2g+1
, rather than from
0
to
2g
; so everything is shifted by
1
.
