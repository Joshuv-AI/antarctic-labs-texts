Cover and Hitting Times of Hyperbolic Random Graphs
Marcos Kiwi, Markus Schepers, John Sylvester

Abstract
. We study random walks on the giant component of Hyperbolic Random Graphs (HRGs), in the regime when the degree distribution obeys a power law with exponent in the range ( 2 , 3 ) (2,3) . In particular, we first focus on the expected time for a random walk to hit a given vertex or visit, i.e. cover, all vertices. We show that, a.a.s. (with respect to the HRG), and up to multiplicative constants: the cover time is n ​ ( log ⁡ n ) 2 n(\log n)^{2} , the maximum hitting time is n ​ log ⁡ n n\log n , and the average hitting time is n n . We then determine the expected time to commute between two given vertices a.a.s., up to a small factor polylogarithmic in n n , and under some mild hypothesis on the pair of vertices involved. Our results are proved by controlling effective resistances using the energy dissipated by carefully designed network flows associated to a tiling of the hyperbolic plane, on which we overlay a forest-like structure. Keywords— Random walk, hyperbolic random graph, cover time, hitting time, average hitting time, target time, effective resistance, Kirchhoff index. AMS MSC 2010— 05C80, 60J10, 60G40.

Early results via non-rigorous methods indicated

(such as networks of acquaintances, citation networks, networks of autonomous systems, etc.). Many of these properties were later established formally, among these are power-law degree distribution

Many other fundamental parameters of the HRG model have been studied since its introduction (see the related work section), however notable exceptions are key quantities concerning the behaviour of random walks. This paper is a first step in redressing this situation. The random walk is the quintessential random process, and studies of random walks have proven relevant for algorithm design and analysis; this coupled with the aforementioned appealing aspects of the HRG model motivates this research.

The (simple) random walk is a stochastic process on the vertices of a graph, which at each time step uniformly samples a neighbour of the current vertex as its next state
[
3
,
55
]
. A key property of the random walk is that, for any connected graph, the expected time it takes for the walk to visit a given vertex (or to visit all vertices) is polynomial in the number of vertices in the graph. These times are known as the hitting and cover times, respectively. This ability of a random walk to explore an unknown connected graph efficiently using a small amount of memory was, for example, used to solve the undirected
s-t
connectivity problem in logarithmic space
[
4
]
. Other properties such as the ability to sample a vertex independently of the start vertex after a polynomial (often logarithmic) number of steps (mixing time) helped random walks become a fundamental primitive in the design of randomized and approximation algorithms
[
59
]
. In particular, random walks have been applied in tasks such as load balancing
[
68
]
, searching
[
36
]
, resource location
[
45
]
, property testing
[
26
]
, graph parameter estimation
[
9
]
and biological applications
[
39
]
.

One issue to keep in mind when working with HRGs is that for the most relevant range of parameters of the model (the range for which it exhibits the properties observed in ‘real-world’ networks) the graphs obtained are disconnected with probability that tends to
1
as the order of the graph goes to infinity. Quantities such as average hitting time and commute time are not meaningful for disconnected graphs, as they are trivially equal to infinity. However, again for the range of parameters we are interested in, Bode, Fountoulakis and Müller
[
13
]
showed that it is very likely the graph has a component of linear size. This result was then complemented by the first author and Mitsche
[
47
]
who showed that all other connected components were of size at most polylogarithmic in the order of the graph. This justifies referring to the linear size component as the
giant component
. With this work being among the first studies of characteristics of simple random walks in HRGs, it is thus natural and relevant to understand their behavior in the giant component of such graphs. This is the main challenge we undertake in this paper.

Among our main contributions are the determination of the order of growth (as a function of the parameters of the model) of the expectation of the target time and also of the hitting, cover and commute times of random walks on the giant component of HRGs. To achieve this, we appeal to a connection of the former to effective resistances in the graph
[
55

. The effective resistance has also found applications to graph clustering
[

1.1.
Main Results

Our main contributions are to determine several quantities related to random walks on the largest connected component
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
of the (Poissonized) hyperbolic random graph
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
. We refer to this component as the
giant
and note that it is known to have
\Theta(n)
vertices a.a.s.
[
13
]
.
The primary probability space we will be working in is the one induced by the HRG. We use
\mathbb{P}
for the associated measure. We also deal with the expected stopping times of random walks, and we use bold type (e.g.
\mathbf{E}
) for the expectation with respect to the random walk on a fixed graph. A sequence of events (w.r.t. the HRG) holds
asymptotically almost surely (a.a.s.)
if it occurs with
probability going to
1
as
n\to\infty
. We give brief descriptions of the objects we study here, for full definitions see Section
2
.

The
hitting time
of a vertex
v
from a vertex
u
in a graph
G
is the expected time it takes a random walk started from
u
to first visit
v
. Let
t_{\mathsf{hit}}(G)
denote the
maximum hitting time
, that is, the maximum over all pairs of vertices
u,v
in
V(G)
of the hitting time of
v
from
u
.

The
target time

t_{\odot}(G)
of a graph
G
(also known as
Kemeny’s constant
) is the expected time for a random walk to travel between two vertices chosen independently from the stationary distribution
\pi
, see Section
2.4
. So it can be seen as the weighted average of the hitting times, where the weights are implied by the stationary distribution, i.e., in the long-term, the target time is the most natural ‘average’ hitting time.
Our main result, which is also technically the most difficult to establish, shows that on the giant of the HRG the target time is of order
n
in expectation and a.a.s.

5
, and more generally Section

. Formally, we prove the following.

Theorem 1
.

For any
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
and
\nu>0
, if
\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
, then
t_{\mathsf{\odot}}(\mathcal{C})=\Theta(n)
a.a.s and in expectation.

The
cover time
t_{\mathsf{cov}}(G)
is the expected time for the walk to visit all vertices of
G
(taken from a worst case start vertex), see Section
2.4
. We show that both maximum hitting time
t_{\mathsf{hit}}(\mathcal{C})
and cover time
t_{\mathsf{cov}}(\mathcal{C})
concentrate on the giant of the HRG.

Theorem 2
.

For any
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
and
\nu>0
, if
\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
, then
a.a.s. and in expectation

t_{\mathsf{hit}}(\mathcal{C})=\Theta(n\log n)\qquad\text{and }\qquad t_{\mathsf{cov}}(\mathcal{C})=\Theta(n\log^{2}n).

The
commute time
between vertices
s
and
t
is the expectation of the number of steps
\tau_{s,t}
it takes the random walk to visit
t
starting from
s
and then return to
s
. Besides being of intrinsic interest, the commute time can be used as a measure of network search efficiency, proximity of data, and cluster cohesion. We give sharp estimates for the commute time between two additional vertices under mild hypotheses on these two vertices are added to the HRG.

Theorem 3
.

Consider
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
and
\nu>0
. Suppose
s,t
are vertices added to
\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
at points with radial coordinates
r_{s},r_{t}

If the degrees of
s
and
t
are
d(s)
and
d(t)
, respectively, and
d(s),d(t)\geqslant 1
, then the following holds:

(i)

If
(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha})R<r_{s},r_{t}\leqslant R-\frac{\ln(R/\nu)}{2(\alpha-1/2)(1-\alpha)}-\Theta(1)
, then a.a.s.,

\mathbf{E}_{s}\!\left(\,\tau_{s,t}\,\right)=\mathcal{O}\Big(n\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{d(s)^{4\alpha(1-\alpha)}}+\frac{1}{d(t)^{4\alpha(1-\alpha)}}\Big)\Big).

(ii)

If the angle formed at the origin between
s
and
t
is at least
\min\{\phi_{r_{s}},\phi_{r_{t}}\}
where
\phi_{r}:=o(e^{\alpha(R-r)}/n)

\mathbf{E}_{s}\!\left(\,\tau_{s,t}\,\right)=\Omega\Big(\frac{n}{(\ln n\cdot\ln\ln n)^{3}}\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{d(s)^{4\alpha(1-\alpha)}}+\frac{1}{d(t)^{4\alpha(1-\alpha)}}\Big)\Big).

There are several aspects of the preceding theorem that are worth stressing in order to better appreciate the mildness of the conditions required and the sharpness of the bounds.
Since the expected number of vertices of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
with radial coordinate at most
(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha})R
is
\mathcal{O}(1)
, the lower bound on the radial coordinates required
in Item (
i

is satisfied by almost all vertices of the HRG. On the other hand,
from the hitting time bound of Theorem
2
it follows that the commute time between any pair of vertices
s
and
t
of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
is
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
in expectation.
Vertices that do not satisfy the upper bound on the radial coordinates in Item (
i
) of Theorem
3
have, in expectation, degrees that are at most polylogarithmic in
n
so, even for these vertices, we have good (up to polylogarithmic factors) bounds on commute times.
To conclude our discussion of Theorem
3
, note that the restriction on the angle between vertices
s
and
t
in Item (
ii
) of Theorem
3
is satisfied a.a.s. for any random pair of points (angular coordinates chosen uniformly in
[0,2\pi)

r_{s},r_{t}\geqslant(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha})R
.

The
effective resistance

\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)
between two vertices
u,v
of a graph
G
is the energy dissipated by a unit current flow from
u
to
v
.
Effective resistances are of key interest when interpreting a graph as an electrical network with some electrical current flow through the edges, representing wires, each with a certain resistance.
The study of simple random walks corresponds to the case when all edges have unit resistances, see Section
2.5
for a formal definition.
The sum of all resistances in
G
is the
Kirchhoff index

\mathcal{K}(G)
, this has found uses in centrality
[
57
]
, noisy consensus problems
[
66
]
, and social recommender systems
[
75
]
.
Our main result here gives the expected order of the Kirchhoff index and shows that the expected effective resistance between two vertices of the giant chosen uniformly at random is bounded.

Theorem 4
.

For any
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
and
\nu>0
, if
\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
, then

\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{C})=\Theta(n^{2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{|V(\mathcal{C})|^{2}}\sum_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)=\Theta(1)

a.a.s. and in expectation.

The two results in the preceding theorem are very closely related but do not directly imply each other as
|V(\mathcal{C})|
, the size of the largest connected component, is a random variable that is not independent of the resistances.

Observe that the gap between maximum (as implied by Theorem
2
) and average effective resistance (given in Theorem
4
) differs slightly from the previously established gap for the HRG between maximum and average graph distance (i.e., between diameter and typical graph distance): for the graph distances in the giant, it has been known that the maximum distance, i.e. diameter, is
\Theta(\log n)

[
64
]
and the average, or typical, distance is
\Theta(\log\log n)

[
1
]
a.a.s. Now, our result on the maximum hitting time means that the maximum resistance between two vertices of the giant is
\Theta(\log n)
a.a.s., while the average resistance (over a uniform pair of vertices from the giant) is
\Theta(1)
a.a.s. by Theorem
4
.

Furthermore, note the interesting observation there are enough (polynomially many) pairs of vertices with resistance matching the maximum to ensure that the cover time is a factor
\Theta(\log^{2}n)
larger than the average hitting time, many random graphs (e.g., connected Erdős-Rényi, preferential attachment) are expanders and do not have this feature.

Finally, regarding the commute time result (Theorem
3
), in a graph with good expansion one would expect that for a pair of vertices
s,t\in V
we have
\mathbf{E}_{s}\!\left(\,\tau_{s,t}\,\right)
of the order
|E|\cdot\big(\tfrac{1}{d(s)}+\tfrac{1}{d(t)}\big)
, where
E
is the edge set of the graph. However, the HRG is not an expander and we show that, a.a.s. up-to polylogs for most pairs
s,t
in the giant of the HRG,
\mathbf{E}_{s}\!\left(\,\tau_{s,t}\,\right)
is instead of the order
|E|\cdot\big(\tfrac{1}{d(s)^{4\alpha(1-\alpha)}}+\tfrac{1}{d(t)^{4\alpha(1-\alpha)}}\big)
. In particular, for
1/2<\alpha<1
, the power on the inverse degrees is strictly less than one, so the commute times are penalised by the lack of expansion in the HRG.

1.2.
Proof Strategy

The overall approach of the paper is to derive the main results concerning random walks, in particular the target time, by proving corresponding results for effective resistances. Other standard approaches do not seem to readily provide as good results. To illustrate this, we next discuss how we tackle the determination of the cover time.

Over the last two decades, the cover time of many random graph models has been determined using Cooper and Frieze’s
first visit lemma
, see references in the following related work section or
[
62
]
. This result is based on expressing the probability that a vertex has been visited up-to a given time by a function of the return probabilities. One (simplified) condition required to easily apply the first visit lemma is that
t_{\mathsf{rel}}\cdot\max_{v\in V}\pi(v)=o(1)
, where
t_{\mathsf{rel}}(G):=\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{2}}
is the
relaxation time
of
G
, where
\lambda_{2}
is the second-largest eigenvalue of the transition matrix of the (lazy) random walk on
G
. However, inserting the best known bounds on
t_{\mathsf{rel}}
and
\max_{v\in V}\pi(v)
for the HRG, by
[
48
]
and
[
38
]
respectively, gives
t_{\mathsf{rel}}\cdot\max_{v\in V}\pi(v)\leqslant(n^{2\alpha-1}\log n)\cdot n^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}-1+o(1)}
which is not
o(1)
for any
1/2\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 1
.

Another key ingredient of the first visit lemma is good bounds on the expected number of returns to a vertex before the walk mixes, i.e.
\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\mathsf{mix}}}P_{v,v}^{t}
where
P_{x,y}^{t}
is the probability a (lazy) random walk from
x
is at vertex
y
after exactly
t
steps. Obtaining such bounds in the HRG appears challenging due to the large mixing time and irregular local structure of the HRG. This also effects arguably the most natural approach to obtaining bounds on the average hitting time, that is applying the formula
\pi(v)E_{\pi}[\tau_{v}]=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}[P_{v,v}^{t}-\pi(v)]
, see
[
3
, Lemma 2.11]
, as this involves the same sum (which only needs to be considered up to relaxation/mixing time).

Given the perceived difficulty in determining the cover time using the return probabilities as described above, the approach taken in this paper is to determine upper bounds on the cover times (see Theorem
2
) via the effective resistances
\{\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)\}_{u,v\in V}
, and similarly for the other studied quantities.
This relies on the intimate connection between reversible Markov chains and electrical networks, which arises as certain quantities in each setting are determined by the same harmonic equations.
Classically this connection has been exploited to determine whether random walks on infinite graphs are transient or recurrent
[
61
, Chapter 2]
, and more recently the effective resistance metric has been understood to relate the blanket times of random walks on finite graphs to the Gaussian free field
[
28
]
.
Among the random graph models whose cover times have been obtained via determination of effective resistances is the binomial random graph
[
41
,
71
]
and the geometric random graph
[
8
]
.
The main connection we shall use is that the commute time (sum of hitting times in either direction) between two vertices is equal to the number of edges times the effective resistance between the two points
[
17
,
72
]
. Luxburg et al.
[
73
]
recently refined a previous bound of Lovász
[
59
]
to give

(1)

\left|\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)-\frac{1}{d(u)}-\frac{1}{d(v)}\right|\leqslant\frac{t_{\mathsf{rel}}+2}{d_{\mathsf{min}}}\left(\frac{1}{d(u)}+\frac{1}{d(v)}\right),

for any non-bipartite graph
G

and
u,v\in V(G)
, where
d(w)
is the degree of the vertex
w
and
d_{\mathsf{min}}:=\min_{w\in V}d(w)
is the minimum degree. For the HRG with parameter
1/2<\alpha<1
, a.a.s. we have
t_{\mathsf{rel}}\geqslant n^{2\alpha-1}/(\log n)^{1+o(1)}

[
48
]
and the average degree is constant - thus (
1
) does not give a good bound.

The upper bounds in Theorems
2
through
4
rely on the fact that hitting times are bounded above by commute times and that commutes times are determined by effective resistances (via the Commute time identity).
We then exploit the fact that effective resistances can be expressed as the minimum of the energy dissipated by network flows.
A significant part of our efforts are therefore focused on designing low energy flows.
Our constructions rely
on a tiling of the hyperbolic plane. In this regard, it is similar to how various authors have obtained estimates of the size of the giant and upper bounds on the diameter of the HRG
[
31
,
64
]
. However, when constructing a desirable flow one often needs multiple paths (as opposed to just one when bounding the diameter) or else the energy dissipated by the flow could be too large to get a tight bound on the effective resistance. Abdullah et al.
[
1
]
showed that hyperbolic random graphs of expected size
\Theta(n)
have typical distances of length
\Theta(\log\log n)
(within the same component), in contrast we show that typical resistances are
\Theta(1)
.
The diameter of the HRG when
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
was only recently determined precisely
[
64
]
, though the lower bound, non-tight upper bounds, and the diameter for other values of
\alpha
, were established earlier
[
34
,
47
]
.
The tight
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
upper bound for the diameter of the giant of the HRG when
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1

[
64
]
was proved using a coupling with the Fountoulakis-Müller upper half-plane HRG model
[
31
]
and is also based on a tiling-construction.
The tiling on which we rely is closely related to the
Fountoulakis-Müller tiling of the half-plane model. In fact, our tiling is approximately equal to the latter (see the end of Section
3.1
for a detailed discussion). As for other instances in the literature where low energy flows are constructed in order to estimate effective resistances, the flows we construct elicit the combinatorial structure of the underlying graph, in our case, the bottlenecks (small edge-cuts) and expansion versus clustering trade-off of the HRG. We believe this by itself is of independent interest.

A technical complication that arises multiple times in our investigation (in particular in the proof of Theorem
1
) is the need to carefully control raw moments and higher order moments of the number of vertices within certain regions of the underlying geometric space. The arguments we used to do so might be useful in other studies concerning geometric graphs.

Organization

In Section

In Section

2.1.
Conventions:

Throughout, we use standard notions and notation concerning the asymptotic behavior of sequences.
If
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are two sequences of real numbers, we write
a_{n}=\mathcal{O}(b_{n})

to denote that for some constant
C>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that
|a_{n}|\leqslant C|b_{n}|
for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
.
Also, we write
a_{n}=\Omega(b_{n})
if
b_{n}=\mathcal{O}(a_{n})
, and
a_{n}=\Theta(b_{n})
if
a_{n}=\mathcal{O}(b_{n})
and
a_{n}=\Omega(b_{n})
. Finally, we write
a_{n}=o(b_{n})
if
a_{n}/b_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and
a_{n}=\omega(b_{n})
if
b_{n}=o(a_{n})
.

Unless stated otherwise, all asymptotics are as
n\to\infty
and all other parameters are assumed fixed. Expressions
given in terms of other variables that depend on
n
, for example
\mathcal{O}(R)
, are still asymptotics with respect to
n
. Most results in this paper assume that
1/2<\alpha<1
and
\nu>0
are fixed constants (independent of
n
), and the implied constants in
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
asymptotics will typically depend on
\alpha
and
\nu
. As we are interested in asymptotics, we only claim and prove inequalities for
n
sufficiently large. So, for simplicity, we always assume
n
sufficiently large. For example, we may write

n^{2}>5n
without requiring
n>5
.

An event, more precisely a family of events parameterized by
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
is said to hold
with
high probability (w.h.p.)
, if for every
c>0
the event holds with probability at least
1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-c})
. Recall that, in contrast, an event is said to hold
asymptotically almost surely (a.a.s.)
if the event holds with probability at least
1-o(1)
.

Remark 5
.

For convenience we will often make statements of the form ‘
X=\mathcal{O}(f(n))
a.a.s.’, for some function
f
. What is meant by this is that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

P(X<C\cdot f(n))\rightarrow 1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Likewise, we say
X=\Omega(f(n))
a.a.s. if there exists a constant
c>0
such that
P(X>c\cdot f(n))\rightarrow 1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

We follow standard notation, such as denoting the vertex and edge sets of
G
by
V(G)
and
E(G)
, respectively. We use
d_{G}(u,v)
to denote the graph distance between two vertices
u,v\in V(G)
, let
N(v):=\{u\in V\mid d_{G}(u,v)=1\}
denote the neighbourhood of a vertex, and let
d(v)=|N(v)|
.

We use

2.2.
Poisson Random Variables.

We work with a Poissonized model, where the number of points within a given region is Poisson-distributed. Thus, we will need some elementary results for Poisson random variables. The first is a (Chernoff type) large deviation bound.

Lemma 6
.

Let
P
have a Poisson distribution with mean
\mu
. Then, the following holds:

(i)

If
\delta>0
, then
\mathbb{P}(P\leqslant(1-\delta)\mu)\leqslant e^{-\delta^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\mu}
. In particular,
\mathbb{P}(P\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mu)\leqslant e^{-\frac{1}{8}\mu}
.

(ii)

If
\delta\geqslant e^{\frac{3}{2}}
, then

\mathbb{P}(P\geqslant\delta\mu)\leqslant e^{-\frac{1}{2}\delta\mu}
.

Proof.

The first part is a direct application of
[
6
, Theorem A.1.15]
.
For the second part, by the cited result, note that if
\chi>0
, then

\mathbb{P}(P\geqslant(1+\chi)\mu)\leqslant\big(e^{\chi}(1+\chi)^{-(1+\chi)}\big)^{\mu}.

Taking
\chi:=\delta-1
above (also noticing that by hypothesis
on
\delta
, we have
\chi>0
), we get

e^{\chi}(1+\chi)^{-(1+\chi)}=\frac{1}{e}(e/\delta)^{\delta}\leqslant e^{-\frac{1}{2}\delta}
and the desired conclusion follows.
∎

The following bound on the raw moments of Poisson random variables will be useful.

Lemma 7

(
[
10
, Theorem 2.5]
)
.

Let
X
be a Poisson random variable, and
\kappa\geqslant 1
. Then,

\mathbb{E}(X^{\kappa})\leqslant\left(\frac{\kappa}{\ln(1+\kappa)}\right)^{\kappa}\cdot\max\{\mathbb{E}(X),\;\mathbb{E}(X)^{\kappa}\}.

2.3.
The HRG model.

We represent the hyperbolic plane (of constant Gaussian curvature
-1
), denoted
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, by points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Elements of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
are referred to by the polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
of their representation as points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The point with coordinates
(0,0)
will be called the
origin
of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and denoted
O
.
When alluding to a point
u\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
we denote its polar coordinates by
(r_{u},\theta_{u})
.
The hyperbolic distance
d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(p,q)
between two points
p,q\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
is determined via
the Hyperbolic Law of Cosines as the unique solution of

(2)

\cosh d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(p,q)=\cosh r_{p}\cosh r_{q}-\sinh r_{p}\sinh r_{q}\cos(\theta_{p}-\theta_{q}).

In particular, the hyperbolic distance between the origin and a point
p
equals
r_{p}
. The ball of radius
\rho>0
centered at
p

will be denoted
B_{p}(\rho)
, i.e.,

B_{p}(\rho):=\{q\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\mid d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(p,q)<\rho\}.

We will work in the Poissonized version of the HRG model which we describe next.
For a positive integer
n
and positive constant
\nu
we consider a Poisson point process on the hyperbolic disk centered at the origin
O
and
of radius
R:=2\ln(n/\nu)
.
The intensity function at polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
for
0\leqslant r<R
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
equals

(3)

\lambda(r,\theta):=\nu e^{\frac{R}{2}}f(r,\theta)=nf(r,\theta)

where
f(r,\theta)
is the joint density function of independent random variables
\theta
and
r
, with
\theta
chosen uniformly at random in
[0,2\pi)

and
r
chosen according to the following density function:

f(r):=\frac{\alpha\sinh(\alpha r)}{\cosh(\alpha R)-1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{[0,R)}(r)\qquad\text{where $\mathbf{1}_{[0,R)}(\cdot)$ is the indicator of
$[0,R)$.}

The parameter
\alpha>0
controls the distribution: For
\alpha=1
, the distribution is uniform in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\cap B_{O}(R)
, for smaller values the vertices are distributed more towards the center of
B_{O}(R)
and for bigger values more towards the border, see Figure
1
for an illustration of instances of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
for two distinct values of
\alpha
.

f(r)=\alpha e^{-\alpha(R-r)}\cdot(1+\Theta(e^{-\alpha R}+e^{-2\alpha r}))\cdot\mathbf{1}_{[0,R)}(r)

We denote the point set of the Poisson process by
V

The probability space over graphs

\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)

Note in particular that
\mathbb{E}|V|=n
since

(4)

\int_{B_{O}(R)}\lambda(r,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}r=\nu e^{\frac{R}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)\,\mathrm{d}r=n.

The parameter
\nu
controls the average degree of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)

which, for
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}

(1+o(1))\frac{2\alpha^{2}\nu}{\pi(\alpha-1/2)^{2}}
(see
[

).

The Hyperbolic Law of Cosines turns out to be inconvenient to work with when computing distances in hyperbolic space.
Instead, it is more convenient to consider the maximum angle
\theta_{R}(r_{u},r_{v})
that two points
u,v\in B_{O}(R)
can
form with the origin
O
and still be within (hyperbolic) distance at most
R
provided
u
and
v
are at distance

r_{u}
and
r_{v}
from the origin, respectively.

Remark 9
.

Replacing in (
2
) the terms
d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(u,v)
by
R
and
\theta_{u}-\theta_{v}

by
\theta_{R}(r_{u},r_{v})
, taking partial derivatives on both sides with respect to
r_{u}
and
some basic arithmetic gives
that the mapping
r\mapsto\theta_{R}(r,r_{v})
is continuous and
strictly decreasing in
the interval
[0,R)
.
Since
\theta_{R}(r_{u},r_{v})=\theta_{R}(r_{v},r_{u})
, the same is true of the
mapping
r\mapsto\theta_{R}(r_{u},r)
. (See
[
49
, Remark 2.1]
for additional details.)

The following estimate of
\theta_{R}(r,r^{\prime})
, due to Gugelmann, Panagiotou & Peter is very useful and accurate
(especially when
R-(r+r^{\prime})
goes to infinity with
n
).

Lemma 10

(
[
38
, Lemma 6]
)
.

If
0\leqslant r\leqslant R
and
r+r^{\prime}\geqslant R
, then

\theta_{R}(r,r^{\prime})=2e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-r-r^{\prime})}(1+\Theta(e^{R-r-r^{\prime}}))
.

Clearly, if
r+r^{\prime}<R
, then
\theta_{R}(r,r^{\prime})=\pi
.

We will need estimates for measures of regions of the hyperbolic plane, and specifically for the measure of balls.
We denote by
\mu(S)
the measure of a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{2}
, i.e.,

\mu(S):=\int_{S}f(r,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\theta
.
The following approximation of the measures of the ball of radius

centered at the origin and the ball of radius
R
centered at

, both also due to Gugelmann et al., will be used frequently in our analysis.

Lemma 11

(
[
38
, Lemma 7]
)
.

For
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
,
p\in B_{O}(R)
and

\displaystyle\mu(B_{O}(r))

\displaystyle=e^{-\alpha(R-r)}(1+o(1)),

\displaystyle\mu(B_{p}(R)\cap B_{O}(R))

\displaystyle=\frac{2\alpha e^{-\frac{1}{2}r_{p}}}{\pi(\alpha-\tfrac{1}{2})}\big(1+\mathcal{O}(e^{-(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})r_{p}}+e^{-r_{p}})\big).

Moreover, if
0\leqslant\rho\leqslant R
and
r_{p}+\rho\geqslant R
, then

\mu(B_{p}(R)\cap B_{O}(\rho))=\Theta(e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-r_{p})-(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})(R-\rho)}/n).

Henceforth, the connected component that contains the vertex of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
that is closest to the origin will be referred to as the

of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
and denoted
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
.

Next, we state a result that is implicit in
[
13
]
(later refined in
[
31
]
) concerning the size
and the ‘geometric location’ of the giant component of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
.
In particular, it implies that a.a.s. the center component and the giant component of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
are the same.

Theorem 12

(
[
13
, Theorem 1.4]
,
[
49
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

If
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
, then a.a.s. the center component of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
contains every vertex in
B_{O}(\frac{R}{2})
, has size
\Theta(n)
, and all other connected components of

\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
are of size polylogarithmic in
n
.

The previous result partly explains why we focus our attention on simple random walks in the center component of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
. In the following remark we justify formally why we, henceforth, consider both the giant and the center component as being the same component, and consequently treat the latter as being the former.

Remark 13
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
be the event that the giant (the largest component) is equal to the center component
\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
, then
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S}_{n})=1-e^{-\Omega(n)}
by
[
13
]
. It follows immediately that all of our results holding a.a.s. for the center component also hold for the giant component. For statements of the form
\mathbb{E}(X(\mathcal{C}))
, where
X(G)
is a function of a graph satisfying
1\leqslant X(G)\leqslant|V(G)|^{\kappa}
for some fixed
\kappa\geqslant 1
, for example (non-trivial) cover/hitting times, the results also carry over. That is, if
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is the giant of the HRG, then
\mathbb{E}(X(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}))=(1+o(1))\mathbb{E}(X(\mathcal{C}))
. To see this, since
\mathbb{E}(|V(\mathcal{G})|^{\kappa})=\mathcal{O}(n^{\kappa})
by Lemma
7
, we have the following by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\mathbb{E}(X(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}))\leqslant\mathbb{E}(X(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}})+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}|V(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})|^{2\kappa}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S}_{n}^{c})}\leqslant\mathbb{E}(X(\mathcal{C}))+\mathcal{O}(n^{\kappa}e^{-\Omega(n)})=(1+o(1))\mathbb{E}(X(\mathcal{C})).

This also holds with the roles of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{C}
reversed, giving the result.

The condition
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
guarantees that
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
has constant average degree depending on
\alpha
and
\nu
only
[
38
, Theorem 2.3]
.
If on the contrary
\alpha\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, then the average degree grows with
n
.
If
\alpha>1
, the largest component of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
is sublinear in
n

[
13
, Theorem 1.4]
. For
\alpha=1
whether the largest component is of size linear in
n
depends on
\nu

[
13
, Theorem 1.5]
. Hence, the parameter range where
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
is where HRGs are always sparse, exhibit power law degree distribution with exponent between
2
and
3
and the giant component is, a.a.s., unique and of linear size. All these are characteristics ascribed to so-called ‘social’ or ‘complex networks’ which HRGs purport to model. Our main motivation is to contribute to understanding processes that take place in complex networks, many of which, as already discussed in the introduction, either involve or are related to simple random walks on such networks. Thus, we restrict our study exclusively to the case where
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
, but in order to avoid excessive repetition, we omit this condition from the statements we establish.

The last known property of the HRG that we recall is that, w.h.p. the center component has a number of edges that is linear in
n
.

Lemma 14

(
[
48
, Corollary 17]
)
.

For
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
, w.h.p.
|E(\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n))|=\Theta(n)
.

To conclude this section we point out that everything we do throughout this paper can be easily adapted to the case where
\mathbb{H}^{2}
has Gaussian curvature
-\eta^{2}
instead of
-1
and all stated results hold provided
\alpha
is in the range
(\eta/2,\eta)
and is replaced by
\alpha/\eta
everywhere.

2.4.
Random Walks

The simple random walk
(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
on a graph
G=(V,E)
is a discrete time random process on
V
which at each time moves to a neighbour of the current vertex
u\in V
uniformly with probability
1/d(u)
. We use
\mathbf{P}\left(\cdot\right):=\mathbf{P}^{G}\left(\cdot\right)
to denote the law of the random walk on a graph
G
(as opposed to
\mathbb{P}
for the random graph). For a probability distribution
\mu
on
V
we let
\mathbf{P}_{\mu}^{G}\!\left(\,\cdot\,\right):=\mathbf{P}^{G}\!\left(\,\cdot\mid X_{0}\sim\mu\,\right)
be the conditional probability of the walk on
G
started from a vertex sampled from
\mu
. In the case where the walk starts from a single vertex
u\in V
we write
u
instead of
\mu
, for example
\mathbf{E}_{u}^{G}\!\left(\,\cdot\,\right):=\mathbf{E}^{G}\!\left(\,\cdot\mid X_{0}=u\,\right)
. We shall drop the superscript
G
when the graph is clear from the context. We now define the
cover time

t_{\mathsf{cov}}(G)
of
G
by

t_{\mathsf{cov}}(G):=\max_{u\in V}\mathbf{E}_{u}^{G}\!\left(\,\tau_{\mathsf{cov}}\,\right),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\tau_{\mathsf{cov}}:=\inf\Big\{t\geqslant 0:\bigcup_{i=0}^{t}\{X_{i}\}=V(G)\Big\}.

Similarly, for
u,v\in V
we let
\mathbf{E}_{u}\!\left(\,\tau_{v}\,\right)
, where
\tau_{v}:=\inf\{t\geqslant 0\mid X_{t}=v\}
, be the
hitting time
of
v
from
u
. We shall use
\pi
to denote the
stationary distribution
of a simple random walk on a connected graph
G
, given by
\pi(v):=\frac{d(v)}{2|E|}
for
v\in V
. Let

t_{\mathsf{hit}}(G):=\max_{u,v\in V}\;\mathbf{E}_{u}^{G}\!\left(\,\tau_{v}\,\right),\quad\text{and}\quad t_{\odot}(G):=\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}\mathbf{E}_{u}^{G}\!\left(\,\tau_{v}\,\right)\pi(u)\pi(v),

denote the
maximum hitting time
and the
target time
, respectively. We define each of these times to be
0
if
G
is either the empty graph or consists of just a single vertex. The target time
t_{\odot}(G)
, also given by
\mathbf{E}_{\pi}^{G}\!\left(\,\tau_{\pi}\,\right)
, is the expected time for a random walk to travel between two vertices sampled independently from the stationary distribution
[
55
, Section 10.2]
. We will consider the random walk on the center component
\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
of the HRG and so each of the expected stopping times
t_{\mathsf{cov}}(\mathcal{C}),t_{\mathsf{hit}}(\mathcal{C})
and
t_{\odot}(\mathcal{C})
will in fact be random variables.

Formally, the commute time between vertices
u,v
is
\mathbf{E}_{u}\!\left(\,\tau_{u,v}\,\right)=\mathbf{E}_{u}\!\left(\,\tau_{v}\,\right)+\mathbf{E}_{v}\!\left(\,\tau_{u}\,\right)
.

2.5.
Electrical Networks & Effective Resistance

An electrical network,
N:=(G,C)
, is a graph
G
and an assignment of conductances
C:E(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
to the edges of
G
. For an undirected graph
G
we define
\vec{E}(G):=\left\{\vec{xy}\mid xy\in E(G)\right\}
as the set of all possible oriented edges for which there is an edge in
G
. For some
S,T\subseteq V(G)
, a
flow
from
S
to
T
(or
(S,T)
-flow
, for short) on
N
is a function
f:\vec{E}(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying
f(\vec{xy})=-f(\vec{yx})
for every
xy\in E(G)
as well as Kirchhoff’s node law for every vertex apart from those that belong to
S
and
T
, i.e.

\sum\limits_{u\in N(v)}f(\vec{uv})=0\qquad\text{for each $v\in V\setminus(S\cup T)$.}

A flow from
S
to
T
is called a
unit
flow if, in addition, its strength is
1
, that is,

\sum\limits_{s\in S}\sum\limits_{u\in N(s)}f(\vec{su})=1.

We say that the
(S,T)
-flow is
balanced
if

\sum_{u\in N(s)}f(\vec{su})=\!\!\sum_{u\in N(s^{\prime})}f(\vec{s^{\prime}u})\ \text{ and }\sum_{u\in N(t)}f(\vec{ut})=\!\!\sum_{u\in N(t^{\prime})}f(\vec{ut^{\prime}})\qquad\text{for all $s,s^{\prime}\in S$ and $t,t^{\prime}\in T$.}

When
S=\{s\}
and
T=\{t\}
we write
(s,t)
-flow instead of
(\{s\},\{t\})
-flow. Note that
(s,t)
-flows are always balanced. For the network
N:=(G,C)
and a flow
f
on
N
define the
energy dissipated
by
f
,
denoted
\mathcal{E}(f)
, by

(5)

\mathcal{E}(f):=\sum\limits_{e\in\vec{E}(G)}\frac{f(e)^{2}}{2C(e)},

For future reference, we state the following easily verified fact:

Claim 15
.

Let
N:=(G,C)
be an electrical network and
S,M,T\subseteq V(G)
.
If
f
and
g
are balanced
(S,M)
- and
(M,T)
-flows on
N
, respectively,
then

f+g
is a balanced
(S,T)
-flow on
N
and
\mathcal{E}(f+g)\leqslant 2(\mathcal{E}(f)+\mathcal{E}(g))
.
Moreover, if
f
and
g
are unit flows, so is
f+g
.

For
S,T\subseteq V(G)
, the
effective resistance
between

S
and
T
, denoted
\mathcal{R}_{C}\left(S\leftrightarrow T\right)
, is

(6)

\mathcal{R}_{C}\left(S\leftrightarrow T\right):=\inf\left\{\mathcal{E}(f)\mid f\text{ is a unit flow from $S$ to $T$}\right\}.

The set of conductances
C
defines a reversible Markov chain
[
61
, Chapter 2]
. In this paper we shall fix
C(e)=1
for all
e\in E(G)
as this corresponds to a simple random walk. In this case, we write
\mathcal{R}_{G}\left(S\leftrightarrow T\right)
(or
\mathcal{R}\left(S\leftrightarrow T\right)
if the graph is clear) instead of
\mathcal{R}_{C}\left(S\leftrightarrow T\right)
and
\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow t\right)
instead of
\mathcal{R}\left(\{s\}\leftrightarrow\{t\}\right)
. The following is well known.

Proposition 16

(
[
55
, Corollary 10.8]
)
.

The effective resistances form a metric space on any graph, in particular they satisfy the triangle inequality.

Choosing a single shortest path
P
between any two vertices
s,t
(if one exists) in a network (with
C(e)=1
for each
e\in E
) and routing a unit flow down the edges of
P
we obtain, straight from the definition (
6
) of
\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow t\right)
, the following basic but useful result.

Lemma 17

(
[
17
, Lemma 3.2]
)
.

For any graph
G=(V,E)
and
s,t\in V
, we have
\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow t\right)\leqslant d_{G}(s,t)
.

Another very useful tool is Rayleigh’s monotonicity law (RML).

Theorem 18

(Rayleigh’s Monotonicity Law
[
55
, Theorem 9.12]
)
.

If
\{C(e)\}_{e\in E}
and
\{C^{\prime}(e)\}_{e\in E}
are sets
of conductances on the edges of the same graph
G
and if
C(e)\geqslant C^{\prime}(e)
for all
e\in E
, then

\mathcal{R}_{C}\left(S\leftrightarrow T\right)\leqslant\mathcal{R}_{C^{\prime}}\left(S\leftrightarrow T\right)\qquad\text{for all $S,T\subseteq V(G)$}.

The following result allows us to relate hitting times to effective resistance.

Lemma 19

(
[
55
, Proposition 10.7]
)
.

For any connected graph
G
and any pair
u,v\in V(G)
,

\mathbf{E}_{u}\!\left(\,\tau_{u,v}\,\right)=2|E(G)|\cdot\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right).

The commute time identity gives us the following simple expression for the target time.

Lemma 20
.

For any connected graph
G
with
|V(G)|\geqslant 2
we have

t_{\odot}(G)=\frac{1}{4|E(G)|}\sum_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)\cdot d(u)d(v)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}|V(G)|.

Proof.

Observe that, by the definition of
t_{\odot}(\cdot)
and Lemma
19
, if
|V(G)|\geqslant 2
then

t_{\odot}(G)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}\mathbf{E}_{u}\!\left(\,\tau_{u,v}\,\right)\cdot\pi(u)\pi(v)=\frac{1}{{4}|E(G)|}\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)\cdot d(u)d(v).

For any two vertices
u,v\in V
we have
\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)\geqslant 1/(d(u)+1)+1/(d(v)+1)
by
[
41
, Lemma 1.4]
.
Thus, since
G
is connected,

\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)\cdot d(u)d(v)\geqslant{\big(\frac{1}{d(u)+1}+\frac{1}{d(v)+1}\big)}\cdot d(u)d(v)\geqslant{\frac{1}{2}(d(u)+d(v))}

and the result follows since
\sum_{w\in V(G)}d(w)=2|E(G)|
. ∎

3.
The Effective Resistance Between Typical Vertices of the Giant

The main goal of this section is to show that, in expectation, the effective resistance
between two uniformly chosen
vertices in the giant component of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)

is
\mathcal{O}(1)
.
By Theorem
12

it suffices to establish the claim for the center component of

\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
.

This section is organized in six subsections. In Section
3.1
, we partition hyperbolic space into tiles and associate a forest-like structure to the collection of tiles. In Section
3.2
, under some conditions on vertices
s,t
in the center component of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}
and a tiling, we construct a candidate unit
(s,t)
-flow
f_{s,t}
whose dissipated energy will give us an upper bound on the effective resistance between vertices
s
and
t
. Section
3.3
is devoted to establishing conditions under which one can guarantee the existence of the flow
f_{s,t}
, and to determining an upper bound on the energy it dissipates. Unfortunately, for most pairs of vertices
s,t
the existence of the flow
f_{s,t}
is not guaranteed. Significant additional effort is spent, in Section
3.4
, showing that for almost all pairs of vertices
s,t
belonging to the center component there are vertices
s^{\prime},t^{\prime}
for which: (i) the flow
f_{s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}
exists and its dissipated energy is relatively small, and (ii) the effective resistance between

and
s^{\prime}
is relatively small, and similarly for the resistance between
t^{\prime}
and
t
. In Section
3.5
, we use the flow constructed in the earlier subsections to determine the expectation of the average (among pairs of vertices) effective resistance, and the expectation of the target time of the giant of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
. Finally, in Section

, we show that both the expectation of the average of the effective resistances and the target time is concentrated.

3.1.
Tiling

We start by constructing an infinite tiling of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. A region of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
between two rays emanating from the origin
O
will be called a
sector
.
A sector centered at the origin with bisector
p
and (central) angle
\theta

will be denoted by
\Upsilon_{p}(\theta)
.
Formally,

\Upsilon_{p}(\theta):=\{q\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\mid-\tfrac{1}{2}\theta\leqslant\theta_{p}-\theta_{q}<\tfrac{1}{2}\theta\}.

(The reason for using closed-open angle intervals for defining sectors
is due to a minor technical convenience and mostly inconsequential for the
ensuing discussion.)

A
tiling
of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is a partition of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
into regions topologically equivalent to a disk.
The tiling will depend on a parameter
c
(a positive constant) and will be
denoted by
\mathcal{F}(c)
. There will be a distinguished collection of tiles, called
root
tiles, corresponding
to the elements of the equipartition of
B_{O}(\frac{R}{2})
into
N_{0}
sectors, hence each sector with
central angle
2\pi/N_{0}
, for

(7)

N_{0}:=\lceil 2\pi/\theta_{R}(\tfrac{1}{2}(R+c),\tfrac{1}{2}(R+c))\rceil\qquad\text{where $c>0$.}

Also, let

(8)

N_{i}:=2^{i}N_{0}\ \text{ and }\ \theta_{i}:=2\pi/N_{i}\qquad\text{for $i\in\mathbb{N}$}.

The rest of the tiling is specified recursively as follows:
For
i\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
and
j\in\{0,...,N_{i}-1\}

let
\{\Upsilon_{i,2j},\Upsilon_{i,2j+1}\}

be an equipartition of
\Upsilon_{i-1,j}

(thus each sector has central angle
\theta_{i}
and there is a total of
N_{i}=2N_{i-1}

sectors for a given
i
).
Also, for the same constant
c>0
as above, let

h_{-1}:=0,\qquad h_{0}=\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot R,\qquad h_{1}:=\tfrac{1}{2}(R+c),

and then recursively define

h_{i}:=\sup\{h\mid\theta_{R}(h,h)\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\theta_{R}(h_{i-1},h_{i-1})\}\quad\text{for $i>1$.}

The fact that
h_{i}
is well-defined is a direct consequence of the
continuity and monotonicity of the mapping
h\mapsto\theta_{R}(h,h)

(see Remark
9
).
In particular, observe that the sequence
(h_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is monotone increasing and unbounded. Moreover, since

\theta_{i}=2\pi/N_{i}=2\pi/(2^{i}N_{0})\leqslant\theta_{R}(h_{0},h_{0})/2^{i}=\theta_{R}(h_{i},h_{i})

we have
\theta_{i}\leqslant\theta_{R}(h_{i},h_{i})
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

We can now specify the rest of the tiling.
Let the
(i,j)
-tile
be defined by

(9)

T_{i,j}:=\Upsilon_{i,j}\cap(B_{O}(h_{i})\setminus B_{O}(h_{i-1}))\qquad\text{for $i\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}$ and $j\in\{0,...,N_{i}-1\}$.}

Note that (
9
) remains valid even for
i=0
.
See Figure
2
(a) for an illustration of the tiling.

It will also be convenient to introduce various terms that will simplify the exposition.
First, a tile
T_{i,j}
for which
j\in\{0,...,N_{i}-1\}
will be referred to as a
level
i

tile.
We say that
T_{i,j}
is the
parent
tile of both
T_{i+1,2j}
and
T_{i+1,2j+1}

and refer to the latter two tiles as
children
of tile
T_{i,j}
(root tiles are assumed to be their own parent).
We define the mapping
T\mapsto\pi(T)
that associates to tile
T
its parent tile and denote
the composition of
\pi
with itself
s
times by
\pi^{s}
.
A tile that belongs to
\{\pi^{s}(T)\mid s\in\mathbb{N}\}
will be called
ancestor
of
T
.
A tile
T
will be said to be a
descendant
of another tile
T^{\prime}
if the latter is an ancestor
of the former.
Note that by definition, a
tile is always an ancestor and a descendant of itself.
From a geometrical perspective, the ancestors of a given tile
T
are all those tiles that
intersect the line segment between the origin and any point in
T
.
We say that a tile
T^{\prime}
is a
sibling
of a tile
T
of level
i
, if the former is obtained
from the latter by rotation around the origin in either
-\theta_{i}
or
+\theta_{i}
.
If the rotation is in
-\theta_{i}
(respectively,
+\theta_{i}
) we say that
T^{\prime}
is a

clockwise
(respectively,
anti-clockwise
) sibling of
T
.

One last piece of terminology/notation that we will rely on is the following:
Let
\{\Upsilon^{0}_{i,j},\Upsilon^{1}_{i,j}\}
be the equipartition
of
\Upsilon_{i,j}
(along its bisector)
into two isometric sectors.
We let
T_{i,j}^{b}:=T_{i,j}\cap\Upsilon^{b}_{i,j}
for
b\in\{0,1\}
.
Note that
\{T^{0}_{i,j},T^{1}_{i,j}\}
is the ‘natural’ equipartition of

T_{i,j}
. We refer to the
T^{b}_{i,j}
’s as
half-tiles
.
Given a tile
T
we call

H
the
parent half-tile
of
T
if
it is a half-tile of the parent of
T
and a line segment
from the origin to any point in the interior of
T
intersects
H
.

We extend the concept of levels and of (clockwise/anti-clockwise) sibling to half-tiles (for the latter concept, the only difference now is that when dealing with level
i
half-tiles rotations are in
\pm\frac{1}{2}\theta_{i}
).
Clearly, a half-tile
H
has two sibling half-tiles, one of which is included in the tile containing
H
, say tile
T
, and the other one which is not contained in
T
. We refer to the former half-tile, i.e., to
T\setminus H

as the
twin
half-tile of
H
(or twin of
H
, for short).

Having described the tiling we will be working with, we can now compare it to the one introduced by Fountoulakis and Müller
[

.
In this article we work in the so-called

[

or

[

of hyperbolic space, in contrast to
[

where the
Poincaré half-plane model
is used.
Although the two tilings are approximately equal, ours is a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
instead of the

Since both representations are alternative models of hyperbolic space, both tilings can be cast in one or the other. Doing so, one gets that the similarity of both tilings increases the further towards infinity their tiles are, i.e., further from the origin in the Gans model and closer to the half-plane boundary in the Poincaré half-plane model. For points close to the origin in the former or far from the boundary in the latter representation, both tilings differ significantly, although this is irrelevant for the analyses performed either here or in
[

[

[
64
]
. We believe this explains why we can guarantee that most of our results hold with probability

for all

instead of the same probability but just for some

.

3.2.
Definition of Flows

We now turn our attention to the construction of a unit
(s,t)
-flow, where
s
and
t
are any two distinct vertices in the center component
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
.
As already discussed, the energy dissipated by such flows yields bounds on the effective
resistance between
s
and
t
.

Throughout, we assume
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
and let
\mathcal{G}:=\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
,
V:=V(\mathcal{G})
and
E:=E(\mathcal{G})
.
In addition, we assume a constant
c>0
is given which determines a tiling
\mathcal{F}(c)
as described
in Section
3.1
. All tiles and half-tiles mentioned should be understood with respect to
\mathcal{F}(c)
. Also, given a vertex
w
of
\mathcal{G}
we denote by
T(w)
and
H(w)
the (unique) tile and half-tile containing
w
, respectively.

In order to make the
(s,t)
-flow construction simpler to grasp and analyze, we build it as a sum of three different flows.
Specifically; a source flow
f_{s,H(s)}
, a middle flow
f_{H(s),H(t)}
from the half-tiles containing
s
to the one containing
t
, and a sink flow
f_{H(t),t}
, so that

(10)

f_{s,t}:=f_{s,H(s)}+f_{H(s),H(t)}+f_{H(t),t}.

The middle flow
f_{H(s),H(t)}
will be obtained as a particular instance of a balanced flow
f_{H_{s},H_{t}}
that we define between pairs of half-tiles
H_{s}
and
H_{t}
. The latter flow will also be useful later on when we consider local parameters of simple random walks in
\mathcal{G}
.

The term
f_{s,H(s)}
will be a
(\{s\},V\cap H(s))
-flow that equally distributes the unit of flow that is injected in
s
among all vertices in the half-tile
H(s)
to which
s
belongs.
Similarly, the term
f_{H(t),t}
will be a balanced
(V\cap H(t),\{t\})
-flow that sends towards
t
all the flow at vertices in the half-tile
H(t)
to which
t
belongs.
Formally, we define
f_{s,H(s)}:\vec{E}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathbb{R}
and
f_{H(t),t}:\vec{E}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

(11)

\displaystyle f_{s,H(s)}(\vec{sv})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{|V\cap H(s)|},\qquad\text{if $v\in H(s)$, $v\neq s$,}

\displaystyle f_{H(t),t}(\vec{ut})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{|V\cap H(t)|},\qquad\text{if $u\in H(t)$, $u\neq t$.}

The mapping
f_{s,H(s)}
is extended to all
\vec{E}(\mathcal{G})
so it satisfies the antisymmetric property of flows, i.e.,
f_{s,H(s)}(\vec{uv})=-f_{s,H(s)}(\vec{vu})
, and so it is null for all remaining oriented edges
\vec{uv}\in\vec{E}(\mathcal{G})
where
f_{s,H(s)}(\vec{uv})
remains undefined.
The mapping
f_{t,H(t)}
is similarly extended.

Note that the 2 terms
f_{s,H(s)}
and
f_{H(t),t}

end up being well-defined in the sense that no oriented edge is assigned multiple values.

Next, we define a flow
f_{H_{s},H_{t}}
from a half-tile
H_{s}
to a half-tile
H_{t}
. The flow
f_{H(s),H(t)}
will correspond to the particular case of
f_{H_{s},H_{t}}
where
H_{s}:=H(s)
and
H_{t}:=H(t)
.
However, half-tiles
H_{s},H_{t}
can be arbitrary (the sub-indices are now unrelated to vertices
s
and
t
, instead, they are used as labels of which half-tile is the source and which one the sink of the flow). We specify
f_{H_{s},H_{t}}
as a sum of three terms
f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
,
f^{\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
, and
f^{\prime\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
.
There are a few special cases that need to be considered depending on whether
H_{s}
and
H_{t}
are ancestors of one another. We consider a specific case below (
Case (i)
below).
In the other cases, among the 3 terms whose sum gives

f_{H_{s},H_{t}}
, some are null. We then discuss how to adapt the construction of
f_{H_{s},H_{t}}
to the other cases.

Case (i)
:

The half-tiles
H_{s}
and
H_{t}
do not share a common ancestor.

For
H_{w}\in\{H_{s},H_{t}\}
, let
H^{\prime}_{w}
be the half-tile contained in a root tile which intersects the ray between the origin and a point
in
H_{w}
. Note that the choice of
H^{\prime}_{w}
is independent of the point in
H_{w}
that is picked.
Clearly,
H^{\prime}_{w}
is a half-tile of an ancestor of the tile
that contains
H_{w}
.
If
H_{w}
belongs to a root tile, then
H_{w}
and
H^{\prime}_{w}
coincide.
The term
f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
will be defined so that it determines a balanced flow from
H_{s}
to
H^{\prime}_{s}
.
The underlying idea in specifying
f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
consists in pushing the flow from vertices in
H:=H_{s}
to vertices in its twin half-tile
H^{\prime}
, so that all vertices in the tile
T:=H\cup H^{\prime}
end up with the same amount of flow. Subsequently, flow is pushed through the oriented edges
from vertices in
T
to vertices in the parent half-tile of
T
, so all edges involved carry exactly
the same amount of flow, and then repeating the process
but taking
H
as the parent half-tile of
T
, and so on and so forth until
H
equals
H^{\prime}_{s}
.

Formally, we define
f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}:\vec{E}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathbb{R}
so that if
H\neq H^{\prime}_{s}
and
T
is the tile that contains
H
(thus,
T\setminus H
is the twin of
H
), then

(12)

f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}(\vec{uv}):=\frac{1}{|V\cap T|}\cdot\frac{1}{|V\cap H|}\qquad\text{for all $u\in V\cap H$ and $v\in V\cap(T\setminus H)$.}

If
T
is a non-root tile which is an ancestor of the tile containing
H_{s}
and
H
is the parent half-tile of
T
, then

(13)

f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}(\vec{uv}):=\frac{1}{|V\cap T|}\cdot\frac{1}{|V\cap H|}\qquad\text{for all $u\in V\cap T$ and $v\in V\cap H$.}

The value of
f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
is extended to all of
\vec{E}(\mathcal{G})
in the same way as
f_{s,H(s)}
was extended before.
Note that when
H_{s}
is contained in a root tile, we get that
f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
is zero everywhere.

The term
f^{\prime\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
is defined as
f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
but replacing
H_{s}
and
H^{\prime}_{s}
by
H_{t}
and
H^{\prime}_{t}
, respectively, and reversing the direction of the flow in each oriented edge (i.e., multiplying the value of the flow of every oriented edge by
-1
).

It only remains to specify
f^{\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
which corresponds to the balanced
(V\cap H^{\prime}_{s},V\cap H^{\prime}_{t})
-flow obtained by
equally distributing the flow at vertex
v\in V\cap H^{\prime}_{s}
among the oriented edges leaving
v
and incident to vertices in
V\cap H^{\prime}_{t}
.
Formally,
f^{\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}:\vec{E}(G)\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as follows:

(14)

f^{\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}(\vec{uv}):=\frac{1}{|V\cap H^{\prime}_{s}|}\cdot\frac{1}{|V\cap H^{\prime}_{t}|}\qquad\text{for all $u\in V\cap H^{\prime}_{s}$ and $v\in V\cap H^{\prime}_{t}$.}

This concludes Case (i) when the tiles containing
H_{s}
and
H_{t}
have no common ancestors, and thus we need to route flow through vertices belonging to
B_{O}(\frac{R}{2})
.
If the tiles containing
H_{s}
and
H_{t}
share a common ancestor, then we distinguish several cases.

Case (ii)
:

The half-tiles
H_{s}
and
H_{t}
belong to the same tile.

There are two subcases, one where
H_{s}
and
H_{t}
are twin half-tiles and the other when
H_{s}
and
H_{t}
are the same half-tile. In the former case, we set
f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
and
f^{\prime\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
to be zero everywhere and set the flow
f^{\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
as in Case (i). For the other subcase, we set
f_{H_{s},H_{t}}=0
, that is,
f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
,
f^{\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
and
f^{\prime\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
are set to zero everywhere.

Case (iii)
:

The half-tiles
H_{s}
and
H_{t}
belong to distinct tiles that share a common ancestor tile.

If
H_{s}
and
H_{t}
lie in a ray emanating from the origin, we can assume w.l.o.g. that
H_{t}
is closer to the origin than
H_{s}
. We let
H^{\prime}_{s}:=H(t)
and also
H^{\prime}_{t}:=H(t)
, set

f^{\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
and
f^{\prime\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
to be zero everywhere and let
f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
be again as in Case (i).
If
H_{s}
and
H_{t}
do not belong to half-tiles that lie along a ray emanating from the origin, then we set
H^{\prime}_{s}
and
H^{\prime}_{t}
as the two twin half-tiles whose union is the highest level tile among all common ancestor tiles of
H_{s}
and
H_{t}
and define
f_{H_{s},H_{t}}
as in Case (i), that is, we
f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
,
f^{\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
and
f^{\prime\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
are fixed as in Case (i).

In all cases, the 3 terms
f^{\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
,
f^{\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
,
f^{\prime\prime\prime}_{H_{s},H_{t}}
are well-defined.

Although our notation does not reflect it, the definition of
f_{s,t}
depends on the tiling
\mathcal{F}(c)
and thus implicitly on
c
. If we need to stress the dependency, then we will say
f_{s,t}
is
\mathcal{F}(c)
-
compatible
. Let
\mathcal{H}_{s,t}
be the collection of half-tiles
H
of
\mathcal{F}(c)
such that
H\in\{H^{\prime}_{s},H^{\prime}_{t}\}
or

H
is a half-tile of an ancestor of either
T(s)
or
T(t)
but not of both.
We now show that
f_{s,t}
as defined above is indeed a balanced unit
(s,t)
-flow.
Although the proof is straightforward, we include it for completeness.

Lemma 22
.

Let
c>0
be a constant that determines the tiling
\mathcal{F}(c)
. If every half-tile in
\mathcal{H}_{s,t}
contains a vertex of
\mathcal{G}:=\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
, then each non-zero
f\in\{f_{s,H(s)},f_{H(s),H(t)},f_{H(t),t}\}
is an
\mathcal{F}(c)
-compatible
balanced unit flow in
\mathcal{G}
, and so is

f_{s,t}:=f_{s,H(s)}+f_{H(s),H(t)}+f_{H(t),t}
.

Proof.

The hypothesis that
V\cap H\neq\emptyset
guarantees that none of the denominators that appear in (
12
)-(
14
) is null.

The claim is obvious for
f_{s,H(s)}
and
f_{H(t),t}
.
To show that it holds for
f_{H(s),H(t)}
we argue that the claim holds for
f^{\prime}_{H(s),H(t)}
(the case of
f^{\prime\prime\prime}_{H(s),H(t)}
is analogous and the one of
f^{\prime\prime}_{H(s),H(t)}
is obvious).
Assuming
f:=f^{\prime}_{H(s),H(t)}
is non-zero, the half-tiles

H(s)
and
H(t)
cannot belong to the same tile.

Let
V:=V(\mathcal{G})
,
H_{s}:=H(s)
and
H_{t}:=H(t)
.
We need to check that Kirchhoff’s node law is satisfied at every vertex
V\setminus(H_{s}\cup H^{\prime}_{s})
, that
the outgoing flow from every vertex
u
in
H_{s}
is
1/|V\cap H_{s}|
,
and that the flow into every vertex
u
in
H^{\prime}_{s}
is
1/|V\cap H^{\prime}_{s}|
.
Throughout the rest of this proof, we let
f(u)
denote the flow outgoing from
u
,
i.e.,
f(u):=\sum_{v\in N(u)}f(\vec{uv})
.

First, we verify that
f(u)=1/|V\cap H_{s}|
for all
u\in H_{s}
.
Let
T_{s}
be the tile containing
H_{s}
and
H
be the parent half-tile of
T_{s}
.
Observe that by (
12
)-(
13
), since by construction of the tiling
\mathcal{F}(c)
we have
N(u)\cap(T_{s}\setminus H_{s})=T_{s}\setminus H_{s}
and
N(u)\cap H=H
,
it follows that

\displaystyle f(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V\cap N(u)\cap(T_{s}\setminus H_{s})}f(\vec{uv})+\sum_{v\in V\cap N(u)\cap H}f(\vec{uv})

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V\cap(T_{s}\setminus H_{s})}\frac{1}{|V\cap T_{s}|}\cdot\frac{1}{|V\cap H_{s}|}+\sum_{v\in V\cap H}\frac{1}{|V\cap T_{s}|}\cdot\frac{1}{|V\cap H|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|V\cap T_{s}|}\cdot\frac{|V\cap(T_{s}\setminus H_{s})|}{|V\cap H_{s}|}+\frac{1}{|V\cap T_{s}|}.

Since
|V\cap T_{s}|=|V\cap H_{s}|+|V\cap(T_{s}\setminus H_{s})|
, it follows immediately that
f(u)=1/|V\cap H_{s}|
.

Similarly, if
u\in H^{\prime}_{s}
, one can verify that
f(u)=1/|V\cap H^{\prime}_{s}|
.

If
u\in V\setminus(H_{s}\cup H^{\prime}_{s})
, there are two subcases to consider.
In the first one,
u

3.3.
Existence of Flows and Energy Dissipated

Lemma
22
explains why we need to turn our attention to determining conditions under which
f_{s,t}
exists (i.e., every half-tile of
\mathcal{F}(c)
contains a vertex), and more importantly is a good flow
in the sense that it dissipates a small amount of energy.
Clearly, the
larger the number of vertices in each element of the half-tile collection
\mathcal{H}_{s,t}
, the smaller the energy dissipated by the flow. To make this statement precise, we establish several
intermediate results whose aim is to quantify the expected number of vertices
per half-tile involved in the definition of

, and upper bound the probability
that any of them contain far fewer than
the number of vertices one would expect.

Claim 23
.

For any

such that if
h_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}(R+c)

h_{i}:=\sup\{h\mid\theta_{R}(h,h)\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\theta_{R}(h_{i-1},h_{i-1})\}\qquad\text{for all $i>1$,}

|h_{j}-h_{i}-(j-i)\ln 2|\leqslant\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\theta_{i}}{\sqrt{e^{\varepsilon}}}\leqslant 2e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-2h_{i})}\leqslant\theta_{i}\sqrt{e^{\varepsilon}}\qquad\text{for all $j\geqslant i\geqslant 1$.}

Proof.

By Lemma
10
, there is a sufficiently large constant
c:=c(\varepsilon)>0

such that if

, then for all sufficiently large

2e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-a-b-\varepsilon)}\leqslant\theta_{R}(a,b)\leqslant 2e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-a-b+\varepsilon)}.

Hence, for

, by definition of
h_{s}
, we have

2^{j-i}e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-2h_{j}+\varepsilon)}\geqslant e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-2h_{i}-\varepsilon)}
,
and moreover

2^{j-i}e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-2h_{j}-\varepsilon)}\leqslant e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-2h_{i}+\varepsilon)}

h_{j}-(j-i)\ln(2)-\varepsilon\leqslant h_{i}\leqslant h_{j}-(j-i)\ln(2)+\varepsilon

Finally, observe that from the above discussion, and given that

for all

that

\theta_{i}\leqslant\theta_{R}(h_{i},h_{i})\leqslant 2e^{\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon}e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-2h_{i})}
.
Moreover, since
N_{0}\leqslant 1+2\pi/\theta_{R}(h_{0},h_{0})
and
\theta_{R}(h_{i},h_{i})=\theta_{R}(h_{0},h_{0})/2^{i}

\theta_{i}=2\pi/N_{i}=2\pi/(2^{i}N_{0})\geqslant\theta_{R}(h_{i},h_{i})\cdot\big(1+\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\theta_{R}(h_{0},h_{0})\big)^{-1}.

So, taking an even larger

and

to obtain the claimed lower bound on

.
∎

Remark 27

Since all vertices of

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{$T$ is robust}\mid\text{$\pi^{(i-\ell)}$(T) is robust})

be the parent half-tile of
\pi^{p}(T(s))
.
By (

\mathcal{E}(f^{\prime}_{H(s),H(t)})=\sum_{p=0}^{\ell_{s}-1}\frac{1}{|V\cap H_{p}(s)|}\cdot\frac{1}{|V\cap(\pi^{p}(T(s))\setminus H_{p}(s))|}+\sum_{p=0}^{\ell_{s}-1}\frac{1}{|V\cap\pi^{p}(T(s))|}\cdot\frac{1}{|V\cap H_{p+1}(s)|}.

Since
T(s)
is by assumption a robust tile, none
of its ancestors is faulty, so all
terms like
|V\cap\pi^{p}(T(s))|
,
|V\cap H_{p}(s)|
,
|V\cap(\pi^{p}(T(s))\setminus H_{p}(s))|
, are at least half
their expected value, in particular none of them is
0
.
By Claim
23
, we have
h_{\ell_{s}-p}\leqslant h_{\ell_{s}}-p\ln 2+\varepsilon
for every
0\leqslant p<\ell_{s}
,

h_{1}:=\frac{R}{2}+c
and
h_{0}:=\frac{R}{2}
.
Taking all the previous comments into account, using
Claim
24
and observing that
\pi^{p}(T(s))
is a
level
\ell_{s}-p
tile, we get,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f^{\prime}_{H(s),H(t)})=\sum_{p=0}^{\ell_{s}}\mathcal{O}\big(e^{-2(1-\alpha)(R-h_{\ell_{s}-p})}\big)=\mathcal{O}\Big(e^{-2(1-\alpha)(R-h_{\ell_{s}})}\sum_{p=0}^{\ell_{s}}\frac{1}{2^{2(1-\alpha)p}}\Big)=\mathcal{O}\big(e^{-2(1-\alpha)(R-h_{\ell_{s}})}).

Similarly, we have

\mathcal{E}(f^{\prime\prime\prime}_{H(s),H(t)})=\mathcal{O}(e^{-2(1-\alpha)(R-h_{\ell_{t}})})
.
Since
h_{\ell_{s}},h_{\ell_{t}}\geqslant h_{0}=\frac{R}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f_{H(s),H(t)})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(f^{\prime}_{H(s),H(t)})+\mathcal{E}(f^{\prime\prime}_{H(s),H(t)})+\mathcal{E}(f^{\prime\prime\prime}_{H(s),H(t)})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\big(e^{-(1-\alpha)(R-h_{\ell_{s}})}+e^{-(1-\alpha)(R-h_{\ell_{t}})}\big).

The desired conclusion follows since, as already observed,
both
H_{s}=H(s)
and
H_{t}=H(t)
are contained in

, hence by hypothesis
h_{\ell_{s}},h_{\ell_{t}}\leqslant\rho^{\prime}=R-\mathcal{O}(1)
.
∎

Remark 30
.

A careful inspection of the proofs of Lemma
22
and Proposition
29
reveals that we have actually shown that given two half-tiles
H_{s},H_{t}
at levels
\ell_{s},\ell_{t}
of the tiling
\mathcal{F}(c)
, respectively, if they are both contained in robust tiles,
then
f_{H_{s},H_{t}}
(as defined in Section
3.2
) is a balanced unit
(H_{s},H_{t})
-flow in
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
such that
\mathcal{E}(f_{H_{s},H_{t}})=\mathcal{O}\big(e^{-2(1-\alpha)(R-h_{\ell_{s}})}+e^{-2(1-\alpha)(R-h_{\ell_{t}})}\big)
.

By definition of effective resistance and the commute time identity, Proposition
29
gives bounds on the hitting time between vertices belonging to robust tiles contained in

.
By Lemma
26

even if we only take

So, we are yet far from being able to bound either the average resistance or target time of the center component. A key step in this direction that we undertake in the next section is to study the average (graph) distance between vertices in the center component and robust tiles.

3.4.
Bounds on Distance to Robust Tiles

In this section, we show that in expectation over
\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
, the graph distance between the vertices of
\mathcal{C}
located outside
B_{O}(\rho)
and a vertex belonging to a robust tile is
\mathcal{O}(1)
.
To achieve this goal, it will be useful to bound the probability that none of the level

\ell^{\prime}
descendants of a given tile
A
is robust.
Unfortunately, the events corresponding to nearby tiles being robust are not
independent, which complicates our task.
Henceforth, we say a collection of tiles
\mathcal{T}
is
frail

if each tile
T
that belongs to
\mathcal{T}
is frail.

Lemma 31
.

Let
\varepsilon
,
c:=c(\varepsilon)
be as in Lemma
28
,
C^{\prime}>0
sufficiently large, and
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
be given by (
18
).
If
A
is a level
i:=\ell^{\prime}-p\geqslant\ell
tile of the tiling
\mathcal{F}(c)
, then

\mathbb{P}(\text{$\pi^{-p}(\{A\})$ is frail}\mid\text{$A$ is robust})\leqslant\exp\big({-}\tfrac{1}{16}C^{\prime}e^{(1-\alpha)(h_{\ell^{\prime}}-h_{i})}\big).

Proof.

Let
P_{p}(A)
denote the probability in the statement. We claim that for
p\in\{0,...,\ell^{\prime}-i\}

P_{p}(A)\leqslant\exp({-}\tfrac{1}{16}C^{\prime}e^{(1-\alpha)(h_{\ell^{\prime}}-h_{\ell^{\prime}-p})}).

To prove the claim, we proceed by induction on
p
.
For
p=0
the result is obvious since then
\pi^{-p}(\{A\})=\{A\}
so
P_{p}(A)=0
.
Assume now that
p>0
. Observe that
\pi^{-1}(\{A\})=\{A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime}\}
where
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
are the two children of tile
A
. Note that
\pi^{-p}(\{A\})=\pi^{-(p-1)}(\{A^{\prime}\})\cup\pi^{-(p-1)}(\{A^{\prime\prime}\})
.
Since by definition a non-robust tile cannot have robust descendant tiles, it follows that
for
\pi^{-p}(\{A\})
to be frail given that
A
is robust it must happen that for every
T\in\{A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime}\}
either
T
is faulty or
\pi^{-(p-1)}(\{T\})
is frail conditioned on
T
being robust.
Formally,

P_{p}(A)\leqslant\prod_{T\in\pi^{-1}(\{A\})}\big(\mathbb{P}(\text{$T$ is faulty})+P_{p-1}(T)\big).

By the inductive hypothesis, Claim
25
, Item (
i
) of Lemma
28
(taking
C^{\prime}>32\ln 2
), and since
h_{\ell^{\prime}-p}\geqslant h_{\ell^{\prime}-(p-1)}-(\ln 2+\varepsilon)
,

P_{p}(A)\leqslant\big(3\exp\big({-}\tfrac{1}{16}C^{\prime}e^{(1-\alpha)(h_{\ell^{\prime}}-h_{\ell^{\prime}-(p-1)})}\big)\big)^{2}\leqslant 9\big(\exp\big({-}\tfrac{1}{16}C^{\prime}e^{(1-\alpha)(h_{\ell}^{\prime}-h_{\ell^{\prime}-p})}\big)\big)^{2^{\alpha}/e^{(1-\alpha)\varepsilon}}.

To conclude recall that
by hypothesis
\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\ln 2
, thus
2^{\alpha}/e^{(1-\alpha)\varepsilon}\geqslant 2^{\frac{1}{2}(3\alpha-1)}>1
,
so the induction claim follows provided
C^{\prime}
is large enough so

\frac{1}{16}C^{\prime}(2^{\frac{1}{2}(3\alpha-1)}-1)>\ln 9
.
∎

Consider a vertex
w
of
\mathcal{G}:=\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
which may or may not belong to the center component
\mathcal{C}

of
\mathcal{G}
.
In what follows, we abuse terminology and say
w
is robust if it belongs to a robust tile.
Let
w^{+}
(respectively,
w^{-}
) be any robust vertex in
V(\mathcal{G})\cap(B_{O}(h_{\ell^{\prime}})\setminus B_{O}(h_{\ell^{\prime}-1}))
which is clockwise (respectively, anti-clockwise) from
w
and closest in angular coordinate to
w
. It is not hard to see that both
w^{-}
and
w^{+}
exist and are distinct
w.h.p.
Observe that in our definition of
w^{-}
and
w^{+}
we do not require
w
being a robust vertex.
In fact,
w
might not even belong to the center component of
\mathcal{G}
.
In contrast,
vertices
w^{-}
and
w^{+}
necessarily belong to
\mathcal{C}
(since by definition, robust vertices always belong to the center component).
Henceforth, let
\Upsilon(w)
be the smallest sector whose closure contains both
w^{-}
and
w^{+}
.

Lemma 32
.

Let
w
be a vertex in the center component of
\mathcal{G}:=\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
and let
w^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{G})\cap B_{O}(\rho^{\prime})

be a robust vertex which is closest (in graph distance) to
w
.
Then,

d(w,w^{\prime})\leqslant 1+|V(G)\cap\Upsilon(w)|.

Proof.

Consider a shortest path
P_{w}
between
w
and
w^{\prime}
.
If the path
P_{w}
contains no vertex of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}
outside
\Upsilon(w)
, then

d(w,w^{\prime})\leqslant|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|
and we are done.
Assume then that
P_{w}
contains vertices in
V(\mathcal{G})\setminus\Upsilon(w)
.
Let
u
be the last (starting from
w
) vertex of
P_{w}
that belongs
to
\Upsilon(w)
. Also, let
v
be the first vertex of
P_{w}

that is not in
\Upsilon(w)
.
By definition of
\Upsilon(w)
, we can assume that the angular coordinate of
w^{+}
is between those
of
u
and
v
(the argument is similar if
w^{-}
was the vertex
‘in between’
u
and
v
, and thus we omit it).

For the ensuing discussion it will be convenient, given two points
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
, to denote by
\theta(q,q^{\prime})\in[0,\pi]
the angle spanned by
q
and
q^{\prime}
at the origin, i.e.,
\theta(q,q^{\prime})=\pi-|\pi-|\theta_{q}-\theta_{q^{\prime}}||
.

First, we consider the case where the radial coordinate of
v
is at least the one of
w^{+}
, i.e.,
r_{v}\geqslant r_{w^{+}}
. Since
u
and
v
are neighbours in
P_{w}
, then
\theta(u,v)\leqslant\theta_{R}(r_{u},r_{v})
. On the other hand,
by Remark
9
, we have
\theta_{R}(r_{u},r_{v})\leqslant\theta_{R}(r_{u},r_{w^{+}})
.
Since
\theta(u,w^{+})\leqslant\theta(u,v)
, it follows that
u
and
w^{+}
are neighbours in
\mathcal{G}
, contradicting the fact that
P_{w}
is a shortest path between
u
and a robust vertex in
B_{O}(\rho^{\prime})
(recall that by definition

w^{+}\in B_{O}(\rho^{\prime})
is a robust vertex).
Assume then that
r_{v}<r_{w^{+}}
, but suppose that
r_{u}\geqslant r_{w^{+}}
. Arguing as before, we now
deduce that
v
and
w^{+}
are neighbours in
\mathcal{G}
, so the only vertex that
P_{w}

has outside
\Upsilon(w)
is
v
. Hence,

d(w,w^{+})\leqslant 1+|V(P)\cap\Upsilon(w)|
. Since
V(P)\subseteq V(\mathcal{G})
and
recalling again that
w^{+}\in B_{O}(\rho^{\prime})

is robust, by minimality we have
d(w,w^{\prime})\leqslant d(w,w^{+})
.

We are left with the case where
r_{u},r_{v}<r_{w^{+}}
. Let
i
be the smallest integer such
that
r_{u},r_{v}<h_{i}
(thus,
\max\{r_{u},r_{v}\}\geqslant h_{i-1}
).
By definition of
w^{+}
, the tile
T(w^{+})
to which it belongs is at level
\ell^{\prime}
, thus
r_{u},r_{v}<r_{w^{+}}<h_{\ell^{\prime}}
so
i\leqslant\ell^{\prime}
.
Since all tiles intersecting the
line segment between the origin
O
and
w^{+}
are ancestors of
T(w^{+})
,
they must be robust tiles (because
T(w^{+})
is a robust tile). Hence, all
ancestor tiles of
T(w^{+})
are non-empty, in particular the one at level

i
, say
T
. Let
H
be the parent half-tile of
T

(hence,
H
belongs to a level
i-1
tile). Since
T
is
a robust tile, its parent is not faulty and thus
V(\mathcal{G})\cap H

is non-empty, so there exists some vertex
\widehat{w}\in V(\mathcal{G})\cap H
.
Since
H
is at level
i-1
, we have
r_{\widehat{w}}<h_{i-1}\leqslant\max\{r_{u},r_{v}\}
.
Note in particular that
\widehat{w}
is robust (because it belongs to the robust tile
T
) and lies in
B_{O}(\rho^{\prime})
(because
r_{\widehat{w}}<h_{i-1}\leqslant h_{\ell^{\prime}}\leqslant\rho^{\prime}
).
Observe that
\widehat{w}
is in between
u
and
v
, so

\theta(u,\widehat{w})+\theta(\widehat{w},v)=\theta(u,v)\leqslant\theta_{R}(r_{u},r_{v})
.
To conclude, we consider two cases:

(i)

Case
r_{\widehat{w}}<r_{v}
: By Remark
9

we get
\theta(u,\widehat{w})\leqslant\theta_{R}(r_{u},r_{v})\leqslant\theta_{R}(r_{u},r_{\widehat{w}})
implying
that
u\widehat{w}
is an edge of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}
and contradicting the minimality
of
P_{w}
.

(ii)

Case
r_{u}>r_{\widehat{w}}\geqslant r_{v}
: Once more, by
Remark
9
, we get

\theta(\widehat{w},v)\leqslant\theta_{R}(r_{u},r_{v})\leqslant\theta_{R}(r_{\widehat{w}},r_{v})
. It follows that
v\widehat{w}
is an edge of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}
, so by minimality,
P_{w}
contains a single vertex outside
\Upsilon(w)
, and thus

d(w,w^{\prime})\leqslant d(w,\widehat{w})\leqslant 1+|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|
.

The result follows.
∎

Corollary 33
.

Let
s,t\in V(\mathcal{C})
be vertices of the center component of
\mathcal{G}:=\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
.
If neither
s
nor
t
are located in
B_{O}(\rho)
, then w.h.p.,

\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow t\right)\leqslant|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(s)|+|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(t)|+\mathcal{O}(1).

If
s
but not
t
(respectively,
t
but not
s
) is located in
B_{O}(\rho)
, then the first (respectively, second) term in the right-hand side of the inequality above may be omitted, if both
s
and
t
are located in
B_{O}(\rho)
, then
\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow t\right)=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Furthermore, the following two bounds hold w.h.p.,

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(n)\cdot\sum_{w\in V(\mathcal{C})\setminus B_{O}(\rho)}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|+\mathcal{O}(n^{2}),

\displaystyle t_{\odot}(\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{w\in V(\mathcal{C})\setminus B_{O}(\rho)}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|\cdot d(w)+\mathcal{O}(n).

Proof.

Let
s^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}
be robust vertices in
V(\mathcal{G})\cap B_{O}(\rho^{\prime})
which are closest (in graph distance) to
s
and
t
, respectively.
Such vertices exist w.h.p. because
s
and
t
belong to the center component of
\mathcal{G}
, and since Lemma
26
guarantees that w.h.p.
V(\mathcal{G})\cap B_{O}(\rho)
is non-empty and all elements within are robust vertices.
Recalling that
\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow t\right)\leqslant\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow s^{\prime}\right)+\mathcal{R}\left(s^{\prime}\leftrightarrow t^{\prime}\right)+\mathcal{R}\left(t^{\prime}\leftrightarrow t\right)
by Proposition
16
and observing that by
Proposition
29
we know that

\mathcal{R}\left(s^{\prime}\leftrightarrow t^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{O}(1)
, we get that

\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow t\right)\leqslant\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow s^{\prime}\right)+\mathcal{R}\left(t^{\prime}\leftrightarrow t\right)+\mathcal{O}(1)
.
The desired conclusion follows applying Lemma
32
twice.
The last part of the statement holds because w.h.p., again by Lemma
26
,
every vertex
w\in V(\mathcal{G})\cap B_{O}(\rho)
is robust so
\Upsilon(w)=\{w\}
.

For the second claim let
S:=V(\mathcal{C})\setminus B_{O}(\rho)
and thus, by the first claim, w.h.p.

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\big(|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(u)|\mathbf{1}_{u\in S}+|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(v)|\mathbf{1}_{v\in S}+\mathcal{O}(1)\big)

\displaystyle=2|V(\mathcal{C})|\cdot\sum_{w\in S}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|+\mathcal{O}(|V(\mathcal{C})|^{2}).

Thus, since
|V(\mathcal{C})|=\mathcal{O}(n)
and
|V(\mathcal{C})|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})

, the second claim holds.

For the third claim, recall

\displaystyle t_{\odot}(\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{{4}|E(\mathcal{C})|}\sum_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\left(|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(u)|\mathbf{1}_{u\in S}+|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(v)|\mathbf{1}_{v\in S}+\mathcal{O}(1)\right)\cdot d(u)d(v)

Corollary
33

Lemma 34

In what follows take
\varepsilon
,

and

to be as in the statement of Lemma

. Let
\Upsilon^{+}(w)
and
\Upsilon^{-}(w)

(21)

\mathbb{P}(|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon^{+}(w)|\geqslant 2^{p}N_{0}n\theta_{\ell^{\prime}})\leqslant\mathbb{P}(|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Psi_{w}^{+}|\geqslant 2^{p}N_{0}n\theta_{\ell^{\prime}})+\mathbb{P}(\theta(w,w^{+})>2^{p}N_{0}\theta_{\ell^{\prime}}).

\mathbb{P}(|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Psi^{+}_{w}|\geqslant 2^{p}N_{0}n\theta_{\ell^{\prime}})\leqslant e^{-2^{p-1}N_{0}n\theta_{\ell^{\prime}}},\qquad\text{for $p<\ell^{\prime}$.}

be the level
\ell^{\prime}

may not belong to

.
Now, let

by the probability

is frail.
Specifically,

(24)

\mathbb{P}(\theta(w,w^{+})>2^{p}N_{0}\theta_{\ell^{\prime}})\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\text{$\pi^{-(p-1)}(\{A^{+}_{p-1}(w)\})$ is frail}).

We consider two cases. First, we assume
p\leqslant\ell^{\prime}-\ell
. Observe that
A^{+}_{p-1}(w)
is an
(\ell^{\prime}-(p-1)-\ell)
-th generation descendant of some tile at level
\ell
, contained in
B_{O}(\rho)
. By Lemma
26
, for any
d>0
we can take
C>0
sufficiently large so that, with probability
1-o(n^{-d})
, none of the tiles of
\mathcal{F}(c)
contained in
B_{O}(\rho)
is
faulty. Thus, from (
24
), we get

(25)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\theta(w,w^{+})\geqslant 2^{p}\theta_{\ell^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\text{$\pi^{-(p-1)}(\{A^{+}_{p-1}(w)\})$ is frail}\mid\text{$A^{+}_{p-1}(w)$ is robust})

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}(\text{$A^{+}_{p-1}(w)$ is frail}\mid\text{$\pi^{\ell^{\prime}-(p-1)-\ell}(A^{+}_{p-1}(w))$ is robust})+o(n^{-d}).

Note that
A_{p-1}^{+}(w)
is a level
\ell^{\prime}-p+1>\ell
tile, so by Lemma
31
and Item (
ii
) of Lemma
28

we can bound the two probabilities in (
25
).
It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\theta(w,w^{+})\geqslant 2^{p}\theta_{\ell^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\big({-}\tfrac{1}{16}C^{\prime}e^{(1-\alpha)(h_{\ell^{\prime}}-h_{\ell^{\prime}-p+1})}\big)+\exp\big({-}\tfrac{1}{23}C^{\prime}e^{(1-\alpha)(h_{\ell^{\prime}}-h_{\ell^{\prime}-p+1})}\big)+o(n^{-d})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\big(\exp\big({-}\tfrac{1}{23}C^{\prime}e^{(1-\alpha)(h_{\ell^{\prime}}-h_{\ell^{\prime}-p+1})}\big)\big)

(26)

\displaystyle=e^{-\Omega(2^{p(1-\alpha)})},

where the last equality holds since, for any
\varepsilon>0
, if
n
is large enough, by Claim
23
we have
h_{\ell^{\prime}}-h_{\ell^{\prime}-p+1}\geqslant(p-1)\ln 2-\varepsilon
. Thus, since
N_{0}\geqslant 1
, we can bound the second term in (
22
).
Recalling that above we established that
\theta_{\ell^{\prime}}=\Theta(1/n)
,
it follows that
N_{0}n\theta_{\ell^{\prime}}=\Theta(1)
and so the stated result also holds for
p\leqslant\ell^{\prime}-\ell
.

We still need to consider the case where
\ell^{\prime}-\ell<p<\ell^{\prime}
.
Now,
A_{p-1}^{+}(w)
is a level
\ell^{\prime}-p+1\leqslant\ell
tile, so
among its descendants there is at least one level
\ell
tile. In fact,
among the descendants of
A_{p-1}^{+}(w)
there are precisely

m:=2^{\ell+p-\ell^{\prime}-1}
tiles at level
\ell
. Let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of such
tiles. Let
\mathcal{E}_{T}
denote the event that none of the level
\ell^{\prime}
descendants of
T
is robust, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}_{T}:=\{\text{$\pi^{-(\ell^{\prime}-\ell)}(\{T\})$ is frail}\}
.
Since distinct
\mathcal{E}_{T}
’s are determined by what happens for disjoint collections of tiles, they are independent.
Thus, from (
24
) we deduce that

\mathbb{P}(\theta(w,w^{+})\geqslant 2^{p}\theta_{\ell^{\prime}})\leqslant\prod_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{P}(\text{$\pi^{-(\ell^{\prime}-\ell)}(T)$ is frail}).

Observing that w.h.p., by Lemma
26
, every tile
T
in
\mathcal{T}
is robust, from Lemma
31
we get

\mathbb{P}(\theta(w,w^{+})\geqslant 2^{p}\theta_{\ell^{\prime}})\leqslant\exp\big({-}\tfrac{|\mathcal{T}|}{16}C^{\prime}e^{(1-\alpha)(h_{\ell^{\prime}}-h_{\ell})}\big).

Since
|\mathcal{T}|=2^{\ell+p-\ell^{\prime}-1}\geqslant 2^{(1-\alpha)(\ell+p-\ell^{\prime}-1)}
,
arguing as was done above, we have
e^{(1-\alpha)(h_{\ell^{\prime}}-h_{\ell})}=\Theta(2^{(1-\alpha)(\ell^{\prime}-\ell)})
, so we again obtain that

(27)

\mathbb{P}(\theta(w,w^{+})\geqslant 2^{p}\theta_{\ell^{\prime}})\leqslant\exp\big(-\Omega(2^{-p(1-\alpha)})\big),

as claimed.
∎

To conclude this section, we establish a bound on the raw moments of
|V(\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n))\cap\Upsilon(w)|
. The derived estimate will be useful for bounding, in the following section, the expectation of the target time.

Lemma 35
.

For any fixed
\kappa\geqslant 1
and
\mathcal{G}:=\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
we have

\mathbb{E}\big(\sum_{w\in V(\mathcal{G})\setminus B_{O}(\rho)}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|^{\kappa}\big)=\mathcal{O}(n).

Proof.

An application of the Campbell-Mecke formula
[
54
, Theorem 4.4]
, and a recollection of the intensity function
\lambda(\cdot,\cdot)
from (
3
), yields the following

\mathbb{E}\big(\sum_{w\in V(\mathcal{G})\setminus B_{O}(\rho)}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon^{+}(w)|^{\kappa}\big)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{\rho}^{R}\mathbb{E}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon^{+}(w)|^{\kappa}\,\lambda(r_{w},\theta_{w})\,\mathrm{d}r_{w}\mathrm{d}\theta_{w}.

It suffices to show that for a fixed
w\in V(\mathcal{G})\setminus B_{O}(\rho)
, and any
\kappa\geqslant 1
, there exists a constant
\eta:=\eta_{\alpha,\nu,\kappa}>0
such that
\mathbb{E}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon^{+}(w)|^{\kappa}\leqslant\eta
.

Observe that for a non-negative random variable
X
and any
x_{0}>0
we have

\mathbb{E}X^{\kappa}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}(X^{\kappa}>x)\leqslant x_{0}^{\kappa}+\sum_{p\in\mathbb{N}}2^{p\kappa}x_{0}^{\kappa}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X^{\kappa}\geqslant 2^{p\kappa}x_{0}^{\kappa})=x_{0}^{\kappa}\cdot(1+\sum_{p\in\mathbb{N}}2^{p\kappa}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X\geqslant 2^{p}x_{0})),

taking
X:=\theta(w,w^{+})
and
x_{0}:=\kappa_{1}
to be the constant from Lemma
34
, the bound above gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|^{\kappa}

\displaystyle\leqslant(\kappa_{1})^{\kappa}\cdot\Big(1+\sum_{p\in\mathbb{N}}2^{p\kappa}\cdot\,\mathbb{P}(|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|\geqslant\kappa_{1}\cdot 2^{p})\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant(\kappa_{1})^{\kappa}\cdot\Big(2+\sum_{p=1}^{\ell^{\prime}-1}2^{p\kappa}\cdot e^{-\kappa_{2}\cdot 2^{p(1-\alpha)}}+\sum_{p\in\mathbb{N}}2^{p\kappa}\cdot e^{-\kappa_{2}\cdot 2^{p-1}}\Big)\leqslant\eta,

for some
\eta:=\eta_{\alpha,\nu,\kappa}
by the bound in Lemma
34
.
∎

3.5.
Average Resistance & Target Time

In this section we establish the convergence in expectation claims of two of our main contributions (Theorems
1
and
4
). First, we determine the expectation of the average (over pairs of vertices) effective resistance and then the expectation of the target time of the center component.

Recall that the Kirchhoff index
\mathcal{K}(G)
of a graph
G
is the sum of resistances in the graph, that is

\mathcal{K}(G)=\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right).

Proposition 36
.

If
\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
, then

\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{C}))=\Theta(n^{2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{|V(\mathcal{C})^{2}|}\sum_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)\Big)=\Theta(1).

Proof.

For the lower bound, observe that in any connected graph
G
with at most
\kappa|V(G)|
edges there are at least
|V(G)|/2
vertices with degree at most
4\kappa
. Thus, there are
\Omega(|V(G)|^{2})
pairs
u,v
with degree at most
4\kappa
that do not share an edge. It follows that
\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)\geqslant 1/d(u)+1/d(v)\geqslant 1/(2\kappa)
for each such pair, and so
\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)=\Omega(|V(G)|^{2}/(2\kappa))
. Recall that
|V(\mathcal{C})|=\Theta(n)
and
|E(\mathcal{C})|=\Theta(n)
a.a.s. by Lemmas
12
and
14
respectively, giving the lower bound for the expectation of
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{C})
and the average resistance.

For the upper bound, let
S:=V(\mathcal{C})\setminus B_{O}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{E}_{n}
be the event that

(28)

\sum_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)=\mathcal{O}(n)\cdot\sum_{w\in S}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|+\mathcal{O}(n^{2}),

and thus
\mathcal{E}_{n}
holds w.h.p. by Corollary
33
. Then, by (
28
) and Lemma
35
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}}\Big)=\mathcal{O}(n)\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{w\in S}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|\Big)+\mathcal{O}(n^{2})=\mathcal{O}(n^{2}).

Since
\mathcal{E}_{n}
holds w.h.p., we can assume that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c})=o(n^{-6})
. Thus, by Cauchy–Schwarz

\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\Big)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\Big(|V(\mathcal{C})|^{3}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\Big)\leqslant\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(|V(\mathcal{C})|^{6})\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c})}=o(1),

as
\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)<|V(\mathcal{C})|
, since the resistance between two vertices is bounded by their graph distance, and
\mathbb{E}(|V(\mathcal{C})|^{6})=\mathcal{O}(n^{6})
by Lemma
7
. Consequently,
\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{C}))=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
.

For the average resistance, let
\mathcal{V}_{n}:=\{|V(\mathcal{C})|\geqslant cn\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{n}
, where
c>0
is the constant implied by the lower bound in Lemma
14
. Then, by (
28
) and Lemma
35
with
\kappa=1
,

\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{|V(\mathcal{C})|^{2}}\sum_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{n}}\Big)\leqslant\mathcal{O}(1/n)\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{w\in S}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|\Big)+\mathcal{O}(1)=\mathcal{O}(1).

As
\mathcal{E}_{n}
holds w.h.p., we can assume
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{V}_{n}^{c})=o(n^{-2})
by Lemma
14
.
Arguing as for the sum of resistances, we have

\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)<|V(\mathcal{C})|
and
\mathbb{E}(|V(\mathcal{C})|^{2})=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
.
Thus,

\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{|V(\mathcal{C})|^{2}}\sum_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{n}^{c}}\Big)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\Big(|V(\mathcal{C})|\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{V}_{n}^{c}}\Big)\leqslant\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(|V(\mathcal{C})|^{2})\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{V}_{n}^{c})}=o(1),

and so combining the two bounds above gives us the result for average resistance.
∎

Proposition
36
gives the claim on the expectations in Theorems
4
. We will also use our bounds on the effective resistance to determine the target time, however first we must control sums of moments of degrees.

Lemma 37
.

For any fixed
1\leqslant\kappa<2\alpha
and
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}:=\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{v\in V(\mathcal{G})}d(v)^{\kappa}\Big)=\mathcal{O}(n).

Proof.

The number of neighbours
d(v)
of a vertex
v
, where
v
is at radial distance
r_{v}
from the origin, is Poisson-distributed with mean
\mu_{v}
which, by Lemma
11
, is given by

(29)

\mu_{v}:=n\cdot\mu(B_{v}(R)\cap B_{O}(R))=nc_{\alpha}e^{-\frac{1}{2}r_{v}}\big(1+\mathcal{O}(e^{-(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})r_{v}}+e^{-r_{v}})\big),

where
c_{\alpha}:=2\alpha/\pi(\alpha-1/2)
. Recall
R=2\ln(n/\nu)
. For any
v\in V
we have
r_{v}\leqslant R
and thus
\mu_{v}\geqslant\nu c_{\alpha}(1-o(1))
which, for any fixed
\nu>0
, is bounded from below by a fixed positive constant when
n
is sufficiently large. By Lemma
7
and (
29
), for any
\kappa\geqslant 1
, we have

(30)

\mathbb{E}(d(v)^{\kappa})\leqslant\left(\frac{\kappa}{\ln(1+\kappa)}\right)^{\kappa}\cdot\max\{\mu_{v},\;\mu_{v}^{\kappa}\}\leqslant c^{\prime}n^{\kappa}\cdot e^{-\frac{1}{2}r_{v}\kappa},

for some constant
c^{\prime}:=c_{\alpha,\kappa}^{\prime}
as
\alpha>1/2
. Recall the function
f(r)
in our definition of the intensity function
\lambda(r,\theta)
from (
3
), and observe that
f(r)=\mathcal{O}(e^{-\alpha(R-r)})
by Lemma
8
. Thus, by the Campbell-Mecke formula
[
54
, Theorem 4.4]
and (
30
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\sum_{w\in V(\mathcal{G})}d(v)^{\kappa}\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{R}\mathbb{E}(d(v)^{\kappa})\,\lambda(r_{v},\theta_{v})\,\mathrm{d}r_{v}\mathrm{d}\theta_{v}=\mathcal{O}(n^{\kappa+1}\cdot e^{-\alpha R})\cdot\int_{0}^{R}e^{(\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\kappa)r_{v}}\,\mathrm{d}r_{v}.

Now observe that, since
R=2\ln(n/\nu)
and
\alpha-\kappa/2>0
, the desired result follows since

\int_{0}^{R}e^{(\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\kappa)r_{v}}\,\mathrm{d}r_{v}=\left[\frac{e^{(\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\kappa)r_{v}}}{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\kappa}\right]_{0}^{R}=\mathcal{O}(e^{(\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\kappa)R})=\mathcal{O}(e^{\alpha R}n^{-\kappa}).\qed

We finally have all necessary ingredients to determine the expectation, over the choice of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}:=\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
, of the target time of the center component
\mathcal{C}
, thus establishing a claim from Theorem
1
.

Proposition 38
.

If
\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
, then

\mathbb{E}(t_{\odot}(\mathcal{C}))=\Theta(n)
.

Proof.

For the lower bound we have
\mathbb{E}\left(t_{\odot}(\mathcal{C})\right)\geqslant\mathbb{E}(|V(\mathcal{C})|)/2=\Omega(n)
Lemmas
20
and
12
.

For the upper bound, let
S:=V(\mathcal{C})\setminus B_{O}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{E}_{n}
be the event that

t_{\odot}(\mathcal{C})\leqslant\sum_{w\in S}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|\cdot d(w)+\mathcal{O}(n).

Recall that
1/2<\alpha<1
is fixed, so we can set
\kappa:=3/4+\alpha/2
; which satisfies
1<\kappa<2\alpha
, and has Hölder conjugate
1<\kappa/(\kappa-1)<\infty
. Thus, applying Hölder’s inequality gives

t_{\odot}(\mathcal{C})\leqslant\Big(\sum_{w\in S}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|^{\frac{\kappa}{\kappa-1}}\Big)^{\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa}}\cdot\Big(\sum_{w\in S}d(u)^{\kappa}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\kappa}}+\mathcal{O}(n).

Applying the version of Hölder’s inequality for expectations (with the same Hölder pair) yields

\mathbb{E}\big(t_{\odot}(\mathcal{C})\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}}\big)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{w\in S}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|^{\frac{\kappa}{\kappa-1}}\Big)^{\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{v\in S}d(v)^{\kappa}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\kappa}}+\mathcal{O}(n)=\mathcal{O}(n),

by the bounds on moments from Lemmas
35
and
37
. We now need to control what happens outside the ‘desirable’ event
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. By Lemma
19
, since the center component
\mathcal{C}
is connected,

t_{\odot}(\mathcal{C})\leqslant t_{\mathsf{hit}}\leqslant 2|E(\mathcal{C})|\cdot\max_{u,v\in V(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{R}\left(u\leftrightarrow v\right)\leqslant|V(\mathcal{G})|^{3}.

Recall that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c})=o(1/n^{6})
by Corollary
33
. Thus, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\mathbb{E}\big(t_{\odot}(\mathcal{C})\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\big)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big(|V(\mathcal{\mathcal{C}})|^{3}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\big)\leqslant\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(|V(\mathcal{\mathcal{C}})|^{6})\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c})}=o(1),

as
\mathbb{E}(|V(\mathcal{C})|^{6})=\mathcal{O}(n^{6})
by Lemma
7
, giving the result.
∎

3.6.
Concentration of Average Resistance & Hitting Time

In this section we prove concentration for the Kirchhoff constant
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{C})
, the average resistance, and the target time
t_{\odot}(\mathcal{C})
of the center component of the hyperbolic random graph (Theorems
1
and
4
). This will be achieved by proving concentration for sums of powers of degrees and numbers of points in sectors between robust tiles, since we can bound our quantities of interest in terms of these random variables by Corollary
33
. We control the former using the second moment method, for the latter we use the following extension of McDiarmid’s inequality proved by Warnke
[
74
]
.

Theorem 39

(
[
74
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Let
X=(X_{j})_{j\in[N]}
be a family of independent random
variables with
X_{j}
taking values in a set
\Lambda_{j}
.
Let
\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda:=\prod_{j\in[N]}\Lambda_{j}
be an event and
assume that the function
f:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}

satisfies the following
typical Lipschitz condition
:

(TL)

There are numbers
(c_{j})_{j\in[N]}
and

(d_{j})_{j\in[N]}
with
c_{j}\leq d_{j}
such that whenever

x,\widetilde{x}\in\Lambda
differ only in the

k
-th coordinate we have

|f(x)-f(\widetilde{x})|\;\leq\;\begin{cases}c_{k},&\;\text{if $x\in\Gamma$,}\\
d_{k},&\;\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For any numbers
(\gamma_{j})_{j\in[N]}
with
\gamma_{j}\in(0,1]

there is an event
\mathcal{B}:=\mathcal{B}(\Gamma,(\gamma_{j})_{j\in[N]})

satisfying

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B})\leqslant\mathbb{P}(X\notin\Gamma)\cdot\sum_{j\in[N]}\gamma_{j}^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda\setminus\mathcal{B}\subseteq\Gamma,

such that for
e_{j}:=\gamma_{j}(d_{j}-c_{j})
and any

t\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{P}(\{|f(X)-\mathbb{E}f(X)|\geqslant t\}\cap\mathcal{B}^{c})\leqslant 2\cdot\exp\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sum_{j\in[N]}(c_{j}+e_{j})^{2}}\Big).

To apply Theorem
39
it will be convenient to work in the binomial model for the HRG, as the total number of points is then fixed. In this model exactly
n
vertices are placed independently in
B_{O}(R)
according to the density
f(r,\theta)
given by (
3
), the same density as the Poisson model, and then two vertices are adjacent if they have distance at most
R
. Let
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
denote probability of an event in the binomial model of the HRG, and recall that
\mathbb{P}
denotes probability in the Poisson model. Observe that if we condition on the number of points in the Poission model being
n
the distribution is identical to that of the binomial process, that is
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid|V(\mathcal{G})|=n)
. Thus, since
\mathbb{P}(|V(\mathcal{G})|=n)=\frac{1}{n!}(n/e)^{n}=\Theta(n^{-1/2})
, for any event
\mathcal{E}
, we have

(31)

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E})\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})/\mathbb{P}(|V(\mathcal{G})|=n)=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}).

Conversely, for any event
\mathcal{E}_{n}
where
n
is the number of points in the binomial point process and the expected number of points in the corresponding Poisson point process, we have

(32)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n})\leqslant n^{-\omega(1)}+\sum_{k=n/2}^{3n/2}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{k}\;\big|\;|V(\mathcal{G})|=k)\cdot\mathbb{P}(|V(\mathcal{G})|=k)\leqslant n^{-\omega(1)}+\max_{n/2\leqslant k\leqslant 3n/2}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{E}_{k}),

since
\mathbb{P}(||V(\mathcal{G})|-n|>n/2)=n^{-\omega(1)}
by Lemma
6
.

Lemma 40
.

Let
T
and
T^{\prime}
be any two tiles in
B_{O}(R)\setminus B_{O}(h_{\ell})
. If the minimum angle between any two rays from
O
intersecting
T
and
T^{\prime}
respectively is strictly greater than
2\theta_{\ell}
, then
T
and
T^{\prime}
do not share any common ancestor tiles in
B_{O}(R)\setminus B_{O}(h_{\ell})
.

Proof.

Let
A
and
A^{\prime}
be the unique ancestors of
T
and
T^{\prime}
respectively that intersect
B_{O}(h_{\ell+1})\setminus B_{O}(h_{\ell})
. Observe that the internal angle spanned within either tile
A
or
A^{\prime}
is
\theta_{\ell}
. Thus, if
\phi
is the minimum angle between any two rays from
O
intersecting
T
and
T^{\prime}
, then since these rays must pass through
A
and
A^{\prime}
respectively, it follows that if
\phi-2\theta_{\ell}>0
, then
A
and
A^{\prime}
do not intersect. Since all ancestor tiles of
T
and
T^{\prime}
in
B_{O}(R)\setminus B_{O}(h_{\ell})
are descendants of
A
and
A^{\prime}
the result follows.
∎

Using the variant of McDiarmid’s inequality from Theorem
39
and Lemma
34
, we can derive the following result.

Lemma 41
.

For
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}:=\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
and any fixed
\kappa\geqslant 1
, one can take
C>0
suitable large so that the following holds w.h.p. for
\rho:=\rho(C)
as in (
16
),

\sum_{w\in V(\mathcal{G})\setminus B_{O}(\rho)}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|^{\kappa}=\mathcal{O}(n).

Proof.

We apply Theorem
39
to the binomial model for the HRG, where exactly
n
vertices are placed independently in
B_{O}(R)
with the same density as the Poisson model. Let
S:=V(\mathcal{G})\setminus B_{O}(\rho)
. Observe that if
X_{j}\in B_{O}(R)
is the position of vertex
j\in[n]
, and we let

X=(X_{j})_{j\in[n]}
, then

f(X):=\sum_{w\in S}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|^{\kappa}\geqslant 0,

is a function of the
n
independent random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
.

We begin by showing
f
satisfies condition (TL) for some event
\Gamma
and sequences
(c_{j})_{j\in[n]}
and
(d_{j})_{j\in[n]}
. Observe that since the graph has at most
n
vertices, for any
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
we have,

f(X)\leqslant\sum_{w\in V(\mathcal{G})}|V(\mathcal{G})|^{\kappa}\leqslant n^{1+\kappa},

thus we can take
d_{j}:=n^{1+\kappa}
for each
j\in[n]
. Fix
s:=(\log n)^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}
and define the event
\Gamma:=\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2}\cap\Gamma_{3}\subseteq(B_{O}(R))^{n}
as the intersection of the following three events:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle:=\big\{|V(\mathcal{G})\cap\Upsilon(w)|\leqslant s\text{ for each }w\in S\big\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{\text{each half tile $H$ intersecting $B_{O}(\rho)$ contains at least $\tfrac{3}{4}\cdot\mathbb{E}|V(\mathcal{G})\cap H|$ many points}\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{3}

\displaystyle:=\{\text{any sector of central angle at most $4\theta_{\ell}$ contains at most $s$ points}\}.

Claim 42
.

For any
x\in\Gamma
, and
\widetilde{x}\in(B_{O}(R))^{n}
that differs from
x
at a single coordinate, we have

Bound
1
[

The following lemma will help us deal with the intersection of balls of radius

Lemma 43
.

If

Moreover,

if and only
\theta_{q}-\theta_{p}\geqslant 2\pi e^{-\frac{1}{2}\min\{r_{p},r_{q}\}}

Proof.

Recall that
\theta_{R}(r,r^{\prime})
denotes the largest possible angle between two vertices at distances
r
and

from the origin, which are at hyperbolic distance at most
R
from each other.
Observe that, for

and

simultaneously.
Assuming, without loss of generality, that

9

we get

.
Observe that if

. Thus, we can assume

, and apply
[
44

to give

Lemma 45

Let

be any non-negative integer valued random variable satisfying
\mathbb{E}(X^{2A}\ln X)<\infty
. Then,
\operatorname{Var}(X^{a})
is non-decreasing in
0\leqslant a\leqslant A
.

Proof.

Observe that for
a\leqslant A
, each element of the sequence
(f_{n}(a))
, where
f_{n}(a):=\sum_{x=1}^{n}x^{2a}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X=x)
, is differentiable and
f_{n}
converges to
\mathbb{E}(X^{2a})<\infty
. Furthermore, the sequence
(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}a}f_{n}(a))_{n}
converges uniformly to
\sum_{x\geqslant 1}(2a\ln x)x^{2a}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X=x)=2a\cdot\mathbb{E}(X^{2a}\ln X)<\infty
. Thus, it follows from a standard convergence result
[
67
, Theorem 7.17]
that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}a}\operatorname{Var}(X^{a})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}a}\sum_{x\geqslant 1}x^{2a}\mathbb{P}(X=x)-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}a}\Big(\sum_{x\geqslant 1}x^{a}\mathbb{P}(X=x)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\geqslant 1}(2a\ln x)x^{2a}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X=x)-2\Big(\sum_{x\geqslant 1}(a\ln x)x^{a}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X=x)\Big)\cdot\Big(\sum_{x\geqslant 1}x^{a}\mathbb{P}(X=x)\Big)

\displaystyle=2a\cdot\big(\mathbb{E}(X^{2a}\ln X)-\mathbb{E}(X^{a}\ln X)\mathbb{E}(X^{a})\big)

\displaystyle\geqslant 0,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
\operatorname{Cov}(f(X),g(X))\geqslant 0
for increasing functions
f,g
, see for example
[
69
]
. Thus, since
\operatorname{Var}(X^{0})=0
,
\operatorname{Var}(X^{a})
is non-decreasing in
0\leqslant a\leqslant A
. ∎

We can now prove the main result of this section,
that is the a.a.s. convergence claims in Theorem
1
and
4
, which together with Propositions
36
and
38
establish the said theorems.

4.
Cover Time and Maximum Hitting Time

In this section, we first determine the asymptotic behavior
of the cover time of the giant component of the HRG.
To do so, we rely on several intermediate structural results concerning HRGs that were established
in the previous section. However, the arguments developed here are not flow based.
In particular, the ones concerning commute times rely on
extensions of Matthew’s Bound, a classical and useful method for bounding cover times.
This result is then complemented, by the determination of the maximum hitting time also for the giant.

As in the previous sections, we restrict our discussion exclusively to the parameter range where
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
.

4.1.
Upper Bounds on Hitting and Cover times

As mentioned above, to determine the cover time of the center component of the HRG we rely on Matthew’s bound (stated below) and one of its extensions (mentioned later). The bound and its extensions relate the cover time of a graph with its minimum and maximum hitting times.

Theorem 47
.

[
56
, Theorem 11.2]

For any finite irreducible Markov

t_{\mathsf{cov}}\leqslant t_{\mathsf{hit}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n}\right).

A well known relation between hitting times and diameter of graphs together with established results
concerning the diameter of the HRG allows us to easily derive upper bounds on
hitting and, via Matthew’s bound, also on the cover time of simple random walks on the center component of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
.

4.2.
Lower Bounds on Hitting and Cover times

The lower bounds in the classical version of Matthew’s bound
[
56

do not directly (or at least
not obviously) imply a
lower bound for the cover time of
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
matching the upper bound obtained in Proposition

Fortunately, several extensions of Matthew’s bound have been established. Among these,

Theorem 49

(
[

If

5.
Commute Times

In previous sections we analyzed parameters of simple random walks on the giant of the HRG, with a focus on parameters which depend on the graph as a whole and not the start and end vertices of the walks. In this section we give (under mild assumptions) tight estimates (up to polylogarithmic factors) for the commute time between a pair

of vertices added to
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
. The estimates depend on the radius of the added vertices.

We address the upper and lower bounds in two separate sections below. First, we establish the upper bound via the commute time identity and bounding effective resistances from above via the energy dissipated by a specially crafted network flow.
Then, we consider the lower bound, establishing it by exhibiting small edge-cuts whose removal leaves
u
and
v
in distinct connected components.
Put together, these two bounds yield Theorem
3
.

We start by giving a high level overview of the argument used to prove the upper bound, this relies on building a unit
(s,t)
-flow

close to the boundary. However, the closer to the boundary the neighbours are, the smaller the angle of the sector that contains them. In order for the flow to dissipate little energy it cannot traverse exceedingly large paths. However, in the HRG, geodesic paths tend to approach the origin (more so the larger the angle formed at the origin between the end vertices of the path). Unfortunately, the paths starting at vertices within a sector of small central angle merge as they approach the origin. So, there is a trade-off in designing low energy unit

\mathcal{E}(f_{w,H_{w}})=\mathcal{O}\big(e^{(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})(R-h_{\ell^{\prime}_{w}})}/e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-r_{w})}+(e^{R-h_{\ell^{\prime}_{w}}}e^{R-r_{w}})^{-(1-\alpha)}\big).

To conclude, note that by the definition of
\ell^{\prime}_{w}

(see (
51
)) and our estimates for the
h_{i}
’s (see Claim
23
),
either
R-h_{\ell^{\prime}_{w}}=(2\alpha-1)(R-r_{w})+\Theta(1)

or
h_{\ell^{\prime}_{w}}=\rho+\Theta(1)
.
Replacing in the last displayed equation, for the former case, we get

\mathcal{E}(f_{w,H_{w}})=\mathcal{O}(e^{-2\alpha(1-\alpha)(R-r_{w})})
and for the latter

\mathcal{E}(f_{w,H_{w}})=\mathcal{O}(\tfrac{1}{\ln n}\,e^{-(1-\alpha)(R-r_{w})}\big)
.
∎

The following result gives the desired upper bound.

Proposition 63
.

Let
\varepsilon,c:=c(\varepsilon)
be as in Claim
24
. Then, for every
d>0
there is a sufficiently large
C>0
such that for a pair of vertices
s,t
added to
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}:=\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
at points located within
B_{O}(\rho)\setminus B_{O}((1-\frac{1}{2\alpha})R)
where
\rho:=\rho(C)
is as in (
16
),
with probability
1-o(1/n^{d})
,
if
both
R-r_{s}
and
R-r_{t}
are at least

(R-\rho)/(2\alpha-1)
, then

\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow t\right)=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{d(s)^{4\alpha(1-\alpha)}}+\frac{1}{d(t)^{4\alpha(1-\alpha)}}\Big).

If
w\in\{s,t\}
is such that

w\in B_{O}(\rho)
and
(2\alpha-1)(R-r_{w})<R-\rho
,
then the claim holds but with
1/d(w)^{4\alpha(1-\alpha)}
above replaced by
\frac{1}{\ln n}\cdot 1/d(w)^{2\alpha}
.

Proof.

By definition of effective resistance, it suffices to show that with probability
1-o(1/n^{d})
there is an
f_{s,t}
unit
\mathcal{F}(c)
-compatible
(s,t)
-flow in
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}
such that
\mathcal{E}(f_{s,t})=\mathcal{O}(d(s)^{-4\alpha(1-\alpha)}+d(t)^{-4\alpha(1-\alpha)})
.

By Lemma
26
, there is a sufficiently large constant
C>0
such that for
\rho:=\rho(C)
as defined in (
16
), with probability
1-o(1/n^{d})
, every tile in
\mathcal{F}(c)
intersecting
B_{O}(\rho)
is non-faulty.
By hypothesis, if
w\in\{s,t\}
, then
w\in B_{O}(\rho)
.
Thus, there is a
f_{w,H_{w}}

\mathcal{G}
satisfying
the properties guaranteed by Lemma
62
.

By Remark
30
(following Proposition
29
) and since
H_{w}
intersects
B_{O}(\rho)
and is at level
\ell^{\prime}_{w}-k_{w}
, there is an
\mathcal{F}(c)
-compatible balanced unit
(H_{s},H_{t})
-flow
f_{H_{s},H_{t}}
in
\mathcal{G}
such that

\mathcal{E}(f_{H_{s},H_{t}})=\mathcal{O}(e^{-2(1-\alpha)(R-h_{\ell^{\prime}_{s}-k_{s}})}+e^{-2(1-\alpha)(R-h_{\ell^{\prime}_{t}-k_{t}})}).

Assuming

,
since
R-h_{0}=\frac{R}{2}>\alpha(R-r_{w})
if and only if
r_{w}>(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha})R
, by hypothesis,

e^{-2(1-\alpha)(R-h_{\ell^{\prime}_{w}-k_{w}})}=e^{-2(1-\alpha)(R-h_{0})}<e^{-2\alpha(1-\alpha)(R-r_{w})}.

Assuming
k_{w}<\ell^{\prime}_{w}
, then
by Claim

, we have
h_{\ell^{\prime}_{w}-k_{w}}=h_{\ell^{\prime}_{w}}-k_{w}\ln 2+\Theta(1)

and thus,

,

e^{-2(1-\alpha)(R-h_{\ell^{\prime}_{w}-k_{w}})}=\Theta\big((2^{k_{w}}e^{R-h_{\ell^{\prime}_{w}}})^{-2(1-\alpha)}\Big)=\Theta\big((e^{R-r_{w}}e^{R-h_{\ell^{\prime}_{w}}})^{-(1-\alpha)}\big).

Recalling that
R-h_{\ell^{\prime}_{w}}=(2\alpha-1)(R-r_{w})+\Theta(1)

.

Summarizing,

(54)

\mathcal{E}(f_{H_{s},H_{t}})=\mathcal{O}(e^{-2\alpha(1-\alpha)(R-r_{s})}+e^{-2\alpha(1-\alpha)(R-r_{t})}).

To conclude, let
f^{\prime}_{H_{t},t}
be the flow in
\mathcal{G}
obtained from

\mathcal{G}
, that is,
f^{\prime}_{H_{t},t}(\vec{uv})=-f_{t,H_{t}}(\vec{uv})
for all
\vec{uv}\in\vec{E}(\mathcal{G})
.
It is easy to check that

is indeed a
(V\cap H_{t},\{t\})
-flow, actually an
\mathcal{F}(c)
-compatible balanced unit flow.
By Claim
15
, we get that

f_{s,t}:=f_{s,H_{s}}+f_{H_{s},H_{w}}+f^{\prime}_{H_{t},t}
is
a unit
(s,t)
-flow in
\mathcal{G}
such that

\mathcal{E}(f_{s,t})=\mathcal{O}\big(\mathcal{E}(f_{s,H_{s}})+\mathcal{E}(f_{H_{s},H_{t}})+\mathcal{E}(f^{\prime}_{H_{t},t})\big)=\mathcal{O}(e^{-2\alpha(1-\alpha)(R-r_{s})}+e^{-2\alpha(1-\alpha)(R-r_{t})}).

where the last equality is a consequence of (
54
)
and Lemma
62
.
The desired conclusion follows from the well known fact (which is an immediate consequence of Lemma
11
) that the degree
d(w)
of every vertex
w\in V(\mathrm{\mathcal{G}})\cap B_{O}(\rho)
is, w.h.p.,
\Theta(e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-r_{w})})
.
∎

The preceding result together with the commute time identity (see Lemma
19
) and
the fact that the number of edges of the center component of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
is
\Theta(n)
(see Lemma
14
) yield the upper bound of Theorem
3
.

5.2.
Lower bound

As in the previous section, let
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}:=\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
, consider
s,t\in V(\mathcal{G})\cap B_{O}(R)
and let
\rho
be as in (
16
). The goal of this section is to obtain a lower bound for effective resistances matching, within polylogarithmic factors, the upper bound of Proposition
63
.

One classical approach to derive a lower bound on
\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow t\right)
is
to observe that the cardinality of a subset
F
of the edges of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}
whose
removal leaves
s
and
t
in separate connected components gives
a lower bound for
\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow t\right)
.
Indeed, the energy dissipated by any unit
(s,t)
-flow must be at least
1/|F|
, so by definition of effective resistance
\mathcal{R}\left(s\leftrightarrow t\right)\geqslant 1/|F|
, see the Nash-Williams inequality
[
61
, §2.5]
.
Clearly, the smaller the size of
F
, the better the lower bound.
To achieve this section’s goal, we thus focus on finding small
(s,t)
-edge-cuts in
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}
.
We start by identifying small edge-cuts that would isolate a vertex
v
added to
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}
at a point
p\in B_{O}(R)
from some vertex in the center component of
\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}
.

Throughout our ensuing discussion we assume the radial coordinate of
p
is
r_{p}=r
.
Also, we let
\omega:=\omega(n)
be a positive function going to infinity arbitrarily slowly and
define
\phi_{r}:=2\pi\nu e^{-\omega}/(n\mu(B_{O}(r)))
.

Claim 64
.

If
(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha})R\leqslant r<R
, then

\phi_{r}=2\pi(1+o(1))(\nu/n)e^{\alpha(R-r)-\omega}=\mathcal{O}(e^{-\omega})
.

Proof.

Direct consequence of our choice of
\phi_{r}
, the hypothesis on
r
and Lemma
11
.
∎

Let
\Upsilon_{p}
be the sector of central angle
\phi_{r}
on whose bisector
p
lies.
Let
V:=V(\mathcal{G})
and
v
be a vertex added at
p
.
We are interested in determining a good upper bound (that holds a.a.s.) for
the edge-cut in
\mathcal{G}
induced by
(\{v\}\cup V)\cap\Upsilon_{p}
, that is,
if for a graph
G
and a set of its nodes
S\subseteq V(G)
we define

\delta_{G}(S):=\{uv\in E(G)\mid u\in S,v\in V(G)\setminus S\}
, then we seek a
good upper bound for
|\delta_{\mathcal{G}}((\{v\}\cup V)\cap\Upsilon_{p})|
.
The following result reduces (under some mild conditions) the task of bounding
the edge-cut induced by
(\{v\}\cup V)\cap\Upsilon_{p}
to the one of bounding
the edge-cut induced by
V\cap\Upsilon_{p}
.

Lemma 65
.

If
p\in B_{O}(R)
is such that
(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha})R<r_{p}\leqslant R-\omega/(1-\alpha)(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})
,
then a.a.s.,

|\delta_{\mathcal{G}}((\{v\}\cup V)\cap\Upsilon_{p})|=|\delta_{\mathcal{G}}(V\cap\Upsilon_{p})|+\mathcal{O}(e^{2\alpha(1-\alpha)(R-r)}).

Proof.

Let
r:=r_{p}
.
By Lemma
10
and since
R=2\ln(n/\nu)
,

\theta_{R}(R,r)=2(1+o(1))e^{-\frac{1}{2}r}=2(\nu/n)(1+o(1))e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-r)}.

By hypothesis on
r
we have
\frac{1}{2}(R-r)\leqslant\alpha(R-r)-\omega/(1-\alpha)
which together with Lemma
10
implies
that
\theta_{R}(R,r)=o(\phi_{r})
.
On the other hand, by definition of
\theta_{R}(\cdot,\cdot)
, we
have
\theta_{R}(R-r,r)=\pi\geqslant\phi_{r}
.
Thus, by monotonicity and continuity of the mapping
x\mapsto\theta_{R}(x,r)

(see Remark
9
),
there is a unique
R-r\leq x\leq R
such that
\theta_{R}(x,r)=\phi_{r}
.

We claim that any
vertex
u\in V\setminus B_{O}(x)
at distance at most
R
from
v

must necessarily be in
\Upsilon_{p}
. Indeed,
since
\theta_{R}(r,\cdot)
is non-decreasing
(again by Remark
9
) we have

\theta_{R}(r_{p},r_{u})=\theta_{R}(r_{v},r_{u})\leqslant\theta_{R}(r,x)=\phi_{r}

and thus
u
is in

\Upsilon_{p}
(actually, in its closure).
Hence, if we denote by
E_{\mathcal{G}}(S,T):=\{uv\in E(\mathcal{G})\mid u\in S,v\in T\}
the set of edges of
\mathcal{G}
with one
end-vertex in
S
and the other in
T
, then we have

|\delta_{\mathcal{G}}((\{v\}\cup V)\cap\Upsilon_{p})|\leqslant|\delta_{\mathcal{G}}(V\cap\Upsilon_{p})|+|E_{\mathcal{G}}(\{v\},V\cap B_{O}(x))|.

Since
x+r\geq R
, by Lemma
10
,
we have
n\theta_{R}(r,x)=\Theta(e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-x)+\frac{1}{2}(R-r)})
.
On the other hand, by Claim
64
,

n\phi_{r}=\Theta(e^{\alpha(R-r)-\omega})
.
So
\theta_{R}(r,x)=\phi_{r}
implies that

R-x=(2\alpha-1)(R-r)+2\omega+\Theta(1)
.
By Lemma
11
,
since
|E_{\mathcal{G}}(\{v\},V\cap B_{O}(x))|

equals the number of vertices in
B_{O}(x)
at distance at most
R

from the location of
v
, the expectation of

|E_{\mathcal{G}}(\{v\},V\cap B_{O}(x))|
is

n\mu(B_{p}(R)\cap B_{O}(x))=\Theta(e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-r)-(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})(R-x)})=\Theta(e^{2\alpha(1-\alpha)(R-r)-(2\alpha-1)\omega})=\omega(1).

By hypothesis on
r
we have
2\alpha(1-\alpha)(R-r)\geqslant 2\alpha\omega/(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})\geqslant 2\alpha\omega
so the probability that

|E_{\mathcal{G}}(\{v\},V\cap B_{O}(x))|
exceeds its expectation by a factor
of at least
\delta:=e^{\frac{3}{2}}
is at most
e^{-\delta\omega/2}=o(1)
.
∎

Motivated by the previous result, we focus on bounding
|\delta_{\mathcal{G}}(V\cap\Upsilon_{p})|
from above.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{a}
be the annulus of points of
B_{O}(R)

at radial distance between
a
and
a+1
of the origin,
that is,
\mathcal{A}_{a}:=B_{O}(R)\cap(B_{O}(a+1)\setminus B_{O}(a))
.
Also, let
\mathcal{O}_{a,b}
be the collection of points in
\mathcal{A}_{a}
outside

\Upsilon_{p}
(that is, in
B_{O}(R)\setminus\Upsilon_{p}
)
that are at distance at most
R

from a point belonging to
\mathcal{A}_{b}\cap B_{O}(R)\cap\Upsilon_{p}
.
Analogously, let
\mathcal{I}_{a,b}
be the collection of points in
\mathcal{A}_{b}

inside
B_{O}(R)\cap\Upsilon_{p}
that are at distance at most
R

from any point belonging to
\mathcal{A}_{a}\cap B_{O}(R)\setminus\Upsilon_{p}
.
Clearly,

\delta_{\mathcal{G}}(V\cap\Upsilon_{p})=\bigcup_{a=0}^{\lfloor R\rfloor}\bigcup^{\lfloor R\rfloor}_{b=0}E_{\mathcal{G}}(V\cap\mathcal{O}_{a,b},V\cap\mathcal{I}_{a,b})neighbour{.}

Let
b_{min}=\lfloor r\rfloor
.
Observe that the expected number of vertices of
\mathcal{G}

that are located
in
\Upsilon_{p}\cap B_{O}(b_{min})
is

n\mu(\Upsilon_{p}\cap B_{O}(b_{min}))=\frac{\phi_{r}}{2\pi}n\mu(B_{O}(b_{min}))=\frac{\phi_{r}}{2\pi}n\mu(B_{O}(r))(1+o(1))e^{\alpha(b_{min}-r)}=\mathcal{O}(e^{-\omega})=o(1)

where the second equality is due to Lemma
11
and
the third equality is by Claim
64
and since

b_{min}\leqslant r
.
As the expected number of points in
\Upsilon_{p}\cap B_{O}(b_{min})
is
o(1)
, Markov’s inequality gives us that a.a.s.
V\cap\Upsilon_{p}\cap B_{O}(b_{min})=\emptyset
.
Similarly, for
a_{min}=\lceil(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha})R-\omega\rceil

it also holds that a.a.s.
V\cap B_{O}(a_{min})=\emptyset
.
From our ongoing discussion, we get that a.a.s.,

\delta_{\mathcal{G}}(V\cap\Upsilon_{p})=\bigcup_{a=a_{min}}^{\lfloor R\rfloor}\bigcup^{\lfloor R\rfloor}_{b=b_{min}}E_{\mathcal{G}}(V\cap\mathcal{O}_{a,b},V\cap\mathcal{I}_{a,b}),

so if we let
O_{a,b}=|V\cap\mathcal{O}_{a,b}|
and
I_{a,b}=|V\cap\mathcal{I}_{a,b}|
, then a.a.s.,

(55)

|\delta_{\mathcal{G}}(V\cap\Upsilon_{p})|\leqslant\sum_{a=a_{min}}^{\lfloor R\rfloor}\sum_{b=b_{min}}^{\lfloor R\rfloor}O_{a,b}\cdot I_{a,b}.

To upper bound the right-hand side of (
55
), we will need to estimate the expected value of
O_{a,b}
and
I_{a,b}
, or equivalently, compute
\mu(\mathcal{O}_{a,b})

and
\mu(\mathcal{I}_{a,b})
. We claim that if
a+b\geqslant R
, then

\mu(\mathcal{O}_{a,b})=\Theta\big(\theta_{R}(a,b)\cdot\mu(B_{O}(a))\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu(\mathcal{I}_{a,b})=\Theta\big(\min\{\phi_{r},2\theta_{R}(a,b)\}\cdot\mu(B_{O}(b))\big).

Indeed, observe that
\mathcal{O}_{a,b}
is contained in the union of two sectors (each one of central angle
\theta_{R}(a,b)
). By Lemma
11
we have
\mu(\mathcal{A}_{a})=\Theta(B_{O}(a))
.
The claimed formula for
\mu(\mathcal{O}_{a,b})
follows.
A similar argument holds for
\mu(\mathcal{I}_{a,b})
but taking into account that
\mathcal{I}_{a,b}
is contained in the sector
\Upsilon_{p}
of central angle
\phi_{r}
.

To bound the summation in (
55
), we will use the following technical claim which allows us to ignore summation terms corresponding to pairs
(a,b)
satisfying a specific constraint.

Claim 66
.

Let
\omega^{\prime}=\omega^{\prime}(n)
be a positive function that goes to infinity with
n
and let
\varphi:=\varphi(n)=2\ln(\omega^{\prime}R)

be such that
\varphi\geq 2\alpha\omega
.
With probability
\mathcal{O}(1/\omega^{\prime})
, there is pair
(c,d)
such that

a_{min}\leqslant c\leqslant R
,
b_{min}\leqslant d\leqslant R
,
and
R-d\leqslant(2\alpha-1)(R-c)-\varphi
, for which either
I_{c,d}\neq 0

or
O_{c,d}\neq 0
.

Proof.

Since
c\geqslant a_{min}\geqslant(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha})R-\omega
and by hypothesis

2(1-\alpha)R\leqslant c+d-2\alpha c-\varphi\leqslant c+d-(2\alpha-1)R
,
we have that
c+d\geq R
.
Thus,

n\max\{\mu(\mathcal{O}_{c,d}),\mu(\mathcal{I}_{c,d})\}=\Theta(n\theta_{R}(c,d)\mu(B_{O}(d))=\Theta(e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-d)-(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})(R-c)}).

Since
\mathbb{E}(O_{c,d})=n\mu(\mathcal{O}_{c,d})

(respectively,
\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{I}_{c,d})=n\mu(\mathcal{I}_{c,d})
), by Markov’s inequality, the probability that
O_{c,d}\neq 0
(respectively,
I_{c,d}\neq 0
) is
\mathcal{O}(e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-d)-(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})(R-c)})
.
By a union bound,
summing over
R-(2\alpha-1)(R-c)+\varphi\leqslant d\leqslant R
and then over the
at most
\lceil R\rceil=\mathcal{O}(\ln n)
possible values taken by
c
we
get that with probability
\mathcal{O}(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\varphi}\cdot\ln n)
there
exists a pair
(c,d)
such that
O_{c,d}\neq 0
(respectively,
I_{c,d}\neq 0
).
The claim follows by our choice of
\varphi
.
∎

Finally, we can bound the size of the edge-cut induced
by
(\{v\}\cup V(\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}))\cap\Upsilon_{p}
.

Lemma 67
.

Suppose
(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha})R<r<R-\omega/(1-\alpha)(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})
.
Then, a.a.s. it holds that

|\delta_{\mathcal{G}}((\{v\}\cup V)\cap\Upsilon_{p})|=\mathcal{O}(e^{2\alpha(1-\alpha)(R-r)}\cdot(\ln n)^{3}\cdot(\ln\ln n)^{2}).

Proof.

By Lemma
65
, it suffices to upper bound

|\delta_{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}(V\cap\Upsilon_{p})|
.
Let
\varphi
and
\omega^{\prime}
be as in Claim
66
.
Henceforth, let
\Omega
be the set of pairs
(a,b)
such that

a_{min}\leq a\leqslant\lfloor R\rfloor
,
b_{min}\leq b\leq\lfloor R\rfloor
,

R-b\geq(2\alpha-1)(R-a)-\varphi
, and

R-a\geq(2\alpha-1)(R-b)-\varphi
.

We first bound the sum of
O_{a,b}\cdot I_{a,b}

over the pairs
(a,b)\in\Omega
such that
2\theta_{R}(a,b)\leqslant\phi_{r}
.
By Claim
64
we know that
\phi_{r}=o(1)
,
so by Lemma
10
,
if
2\theta_{R}(a,b)\leqslant\phi_{r}
, then
a+b\geq R
and

\theta_{R}(a,b)=\Theta(e^{\frac{1}{2}(R-a-b)})
.
Moreover,
R-d\geqslant(2\alpha-1)(R-c)-\varphi
when
(c,d)\in\Omega
, so

\displaystyle n\mu(\mathcal{O}_{a,b})

\displaystyle=\Theta(e^{-(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})(R-a)+\frac{1}{2}(R-b)})=\Omega(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\varphi}),

\displaystyle n\mu(\mathcal{I}_{a,b})

\displaystyle=\Theta(e^{-(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})(R-b)+\frac{1}{2}(R-a)})=\Omega(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\varphi}).

By Lemma
6
and a union bound over the at most

\lceil R\rceil^{2}
pairs
(a,b)\in\Omega
such that
2\theta_{R}(a,b)\leq\phi_{r}
,
the probability that some such
O_{a,b}
(respectively,
I_{a,b}
)
exceeds its expectation by a factor
e^{\frac{1}{2}\varphi}\cdot 2\ln(\omega^{\prime}R^{2})
is at most
\mathcal{O}(1/\omega^{\prime})
.
Hence, a.a.s.,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)\in\Omega\text{ s.t. }\\
2\theta_{R}(a,b)\leqslant\phi_{r}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!O_{a,b}\cdot I_{a,b}

\displaystyle\leqslant 4e^{\varphi}(\ln(\omega^{\prime}R^{2}))^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)\in\Omega\text{ s.t. }\\
2\theta_{R}(a,b)\leqslant\phi_{r}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!n^{2}\mu(\mathcal{O}_{a,b})\mu(\mathcal{I}_{a,b})

\displaystyle=e^{\varphi}(\ln(\omega^{\prime}R^{2}))^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)\in\Omega\text{ s.t. }\\
2\theta_{R}(a,b)\leqslant\phi_{r}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\mathcal{O}(e^{(1-\alpha)(2R-a-b)}).

A direct consequence of Claim
64
is that

2\theta_{R}(a,b)\leqslant\phi_{r}\text{ if and only if }R-\tfrac{1}{2}(a+b)\leq\alpha(R-r)-\omega+\Theta(1).

Hence, summing over the pairs
(a,b)\in\Omega

such that
a+b=s
and then over the valid
values of
s\geqslant 2R-2\alpha(R-r)+2\omega+\Theta(1)

(observing that there are at most

\lfloor R\rfloor=\mathcal{O}(\ln n)
pairs

(a,b)\in\Omega
for which
a+b=s
for a given

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)\in\Omega\text{ s.t. }\\
2\theta_{R}(a,b)\leqslant\phi_{r}\end{subarray}}O_{a,b}\cdot I_{a,b}=\mathcal{O}(e^{2\alpha(1-\alpha)(R-r)-2(1-\alpha)\omega}\cdot\ln n\cdot e^{\varphi}(\ln(\omega^{\prime}R^{2}))^{2}).

Next, we bound the sum of
O_{a,b}\cdot I_{a,b}
over the pairs
(a,b)\in\Omega

such that
2\theta_{R}(a,b)>\phi_{r}
.
Observe that,

\displaystyle n\mu(\mathcal{O}_{a,b})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Theta(e^{\frac{R}{2}-\alpha(R-a)}),&\text{if $a+b\leq R$,}\\
\Theta(e^{-(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})(R-a)+\frac{1}{2}(R-b)}),&\text{if $a+b>R$.}\end{cases}

Since

we have

\frac{R}{2}-\alpha(R-a)\geqslant\frac{R}{2}-\alpha(R-a_{min})
.
Moreover, if

-(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})(R-a)+\frac{1}{2}(R-b)\geqslant\frac{R}{2}-\alpha(R-a_{min})

Since

n\mu(\mathcal{O}_{a,b})=\Omega(e^{-\alpha\omega})
independent of whether

a+b
is smaller or larger than

Concluding remarks

In this paper we determined the order of the maximum hitting time, cover time, target time and effective resistance between two uniform vertices, the results hold in expectation and a.a.s. (w.r.t. to the HRG). Our main finding to take away is that (in expectation) there are order
\log n
gaps between each of the quantities. This indicates that most vertices in the giant are well-connected to the center (vertices at distance at most
R/2
of the origin) of the HRG, but a significant proportion is not.
We also gave sharp estimates for commute times for pairs of vertices
s,t
added to the HRG for almost all the relevant positions at which
s,t
could be placed, provided the angular coordinates of the vertices satisfy some (mild) condition. We speculate that there could be settings in which the commute times between vertices whose angular distances are very close might be relevant. Our estimates do not hold in this case and might be worth deriving.

We restricted our study to the giant component of the graph in the regime
1/2<\alpha<1
. Even though this is arguably the most interesting regime it would still be interesting to determine the aforementioned quantities on the other smaller components or when
\alpha\notin(1/2,1)
. Another problem we leave open is to discover the leading constants hidden behind our asymptotic notation. If the expression for the clustering coefficient of the HRG
[
32
]
is anything to go by these constants may have very rich and complex expressions as functions of
\alpha
and
\nu
.

An interesting problem is to determine the order of the meeting time, that is, the expected time it takes two (lazy) random walks to occupy the same vertex when started from the worst case start vertices
[
43
]
. Finally, the mixing time of a (lazy) random walk on the giant HRG is known up to polylogarithmic factors by
[
48
]
. Closing this gap is of great importance, but it may well be quite challenging.
