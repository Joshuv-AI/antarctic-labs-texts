Transverse-electric surface plasmon polaritons in periodically modulated graphene Preprint: APS/123-QED
Zeeshan Ahmad, Sang Soon Oh, Egor A. Muljarov

Abstract
Transverse-electric (TE) surface plasmon polaritons are unique eigenmodes of a homogeneous graphene layer that are tunable with the chemical potential and temperature. However, as their dispersion curve spectrally lies just below the light line, they cannot be resonantly excited by an externally incident wave. Here, we propose a way of exciting the TE modes and tuning their peaks in the transmission by introducing a one-dimensional graphene grating. Using the scattering-matrix formalism, we show that periodic modulations of graphene make the transmission more pronounced, potentially allowing for experimental observation of the TE modes. Furthermore, we propose the use of turbostratic graphene to enhance the role of the surface plasmon polaritons in optical spectra.



I
Introduction

Surface plasmon polaritons (SPPs) in graphene have been an interesting area of theoretical and experimental research especially with the possibility of supporting SPPs with transverse-electric (TE) polarization in a graphene layer
Mikhailov and Ziegler 2007
. More recently, TE SPPs were shown to exist in a homogeneous layer of graphene for an extended frequency range at non-zero temperature
Ahmad et al. 2021
using a complex frequency approach that models an open system with temporal decay. Although the TE SPP frequency dispersion is very close to the light line due to the small magnitude of the conductivity of graphene, proportional to the fine-structure constant
Mikhailov and Ziegler 2007
, TE SPPs cannot be resonantly excited by an externally incident light because their dispersion curve lies below the light line.

Graphene is known for its tunability of optical conductivity by application of suitable gate voltage that induces sufficiently low but finite chemical potential
Lee et al. 2012
. This is because the electronic transitions occur near the K-point
Zhan et al. 2012
where the electronic dispersion is linear and the density of states vanishes. Devices like optical modulators
Bahadori-Haghighi et al. 2020
and polarizers
Bao et al. 2011
, as well as absorption enhancement devices
Maji and Das 2018
;
Guo et al. 2019
benefit from this tunability, which together with the existence of TE SPPs in graphene, provides exciting prospects for plasmonic applications
He et al. 2013
. In addition, structures with periodic graphene open the possibilities of generating topological plasmonic states
Kuzmin et al. 2018
;
Jin et al. 2017
;
Slipchenko et al. 2021
;
Taillefumier et al. 2011
when a magnetic field is applied.
Periodic plasmonic structures of graphene
Gonçalves et al. 2016
;
Cui et al. 2021
;
Rhouma et al. 2022
;
Xiong et al. 2019
, and even multilayer stacks of periodic graphene strips
Emani et al. 2015
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Rodrigo et al. 2017
;
Yan et al. 2012
;
He et al. 2014
have been already studied. The effect of stacking graphene-dielectric layers on the properties of transverse-magnetic (TM) SPPs has also been analyzed for up to ten graphene layers
Gong et al. 2020
.
Although periodic graphene grating structure has been studied for TM SPP modes
Gonçalves et al. 2016
;
Rhouma et al. 2022
, it has not been proposed for excitation and measurement of TE SPPs in graphene, to the best of our knowledge.

Various finite-element and finite-difference time-domain methods have been employed in many studies of plasmonic structures
Jin et al. 2017
;
Haddouche and Cherbi 2017
;
Maier et al. 2017
;
Jung et al. 2007
;
Shibayama et al. 2021
;
Zang et al. 2020
;
Rana et al. 2015
;
Olkkonen 2010
to investigate SPPs.
These fully numerical approaches are usually suitable for treating electromagnetic systems of relatively small sizes, but suffer from an increase in computation time for larger samples, since they require discretization of Maxwell’s equations in real space over entire samples.
For larger samples and extended periodic systems, expanding fields into Fourier harmonics presents a more suitable approach
Weiss et al. 2011
.
One can approximate structural details by expanding the solutions to Maxwell’s equations in the Fourier space
Anttu and Xu 2011
, organizing the obtained solutions as coefficients of Bragg diffraction orders, which is a more appropriate approach for an infinite system with translational symmetries presented in this work.
Although studies exist that consider periodic modulation of graphene at the atomic scale
Maksimova et al. 2012
, these are suitable for periods of the order of nanometers. For a modulation period of microns order, which is sufficiently large compared to the electronic wavelengths, we may approximate the optical conductivity of graphene as isotropic
Nikitin et al. 2012
and consider the periodic modulation on the order of wavelengths of electromagnetic waves.

In this paper, we theoretically show that the TE SPP modes can be excited by incident light with an angle close to normal incidence with the help of one-dimensional periodic modulation in graphene.
We demonstrate the excitation of the TE SPP modes as pronounced dips in the zeroth Bragg order transmission spectra. We also show that the in-plane wave number of the transmission dips are tunable with the grating period.
To enhance the transmission feature of the graphene grating, we propose the use of multilayer turbostratic graphene strips
Wei et al. 2019
;
Baek et al. 2017
.

To obtain the transmission spectra showing features due to the TE SPP modes, we employ the scattering-matrix formalism
Whittaker and Culshaw 1999
;
Tikhodeev et al. 2002
that is commonly used for systems periodically modulated in space. The fact that the width of a single- or multiple-layer graphene is much smaller than the SPP wavelength allows us to obtain an explicit analytical expression for the scattering matrix. The SPP modes, which are the eigen solutions of Maxwell’s equations satisfying outgoing wave boundary conditions, correspond to the poles of the scattering matrix for the grating system
Tikhodeev et al. 2002
;
Bykov and Doskolovich 2013
and manifest themselves as peaks and/or dips in transmission spectra. We have developed an analytical approximation for the scattering matrix, eigenmodes, and transmission, providing a proof that the features observed in optical spectra of periodically modulated graphene are in fact a manifestation of the TE SPP modes, which are unique to this kind of material.

II
Theoretical model

In this section, we first derive, in the spirit of the scattering-matrix formalism
Tikhodeev et al. 2002
, the transmission coefficients for a system containing a thin layer of a periodically modulated dispersive material surrounded by vacuum. Generalizations of the presented method to two-dimensional (2D) periodic modulation of a thin material layer and to systems with a substrate and/or superstrate are straightforward. The developed general formalism is then applied to a one-dimensional periodic array of single- and multiple-layer graphene strips, by using the available analytic expression for the conductivity of graphene. We also present here a useful approximation of the transmission at normal incidence, valid for the frequencies near the crossing point of the folded SPP mode dispersion, which allows us to analyse the optical response of the periodically modulated system in terms of its SPP modes.

In the following, we use the model of the permittivity which is described in the entire space as

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x,z;\omega)=1+\chi(\omega)\Lambda(x)\delta(z)\,,

(1)

where
\chi(\omega)
is the two-dimensional (2D) susceptibility of the infinitesimal layer,
\Lambda(x)=\Lambda(x+d)
is a periodic function with period
d
describing the spatial modulation of the layer, and
\delta(z)
is the Dirac delta function approximating the system as an infinitely thin layer. An illustration of the system, studied in this work, which is a periodic array of graphene strips, as well as the geometry of the electromagnetic field in the TE polarization are shown in Fig.
1
(a). The use of the
\delta
function is justified by the condition that the graphene thickness is much smaller than the wavelength of the electromagnetic field.

II.1
Scattering matrix, transmission, and the eigenmodes of an inifnitely thin periodically modulated layer

The scattering matrix, transmission and reflection coefficients, as well as the secular equation determining the dispersion of SPP modes are obtained in this section by solving Maxwell’s equations,

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=i\omega\mathbf{H}\,,

(2)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\varepsilon\mathbf{E}\,,

(3)

in which we assume non-magnetic materials and use the units in which the speed of light
c=1
. We also
assume a harmonic time dependence of the electromagnetic field in the form of
e^{-i\omega t}
and use the frequency- and position-dependent permittivity
\varepsilon(x,z;\omega)
introduced in Eq. (
1
). For zero component of the light wave number in the
y
-direction, solutions of Maxwell’s equations (
2
) and (
3
) split into TE and TM polarizations. We focus below on the TE polarization. The case of TM polarization for the same system is addressed in Appendix
A.3
.

For TE-polarized waves propagating in the
x
-direction [Fig.
1
(a)] with the wave number
q
, the magnetic and electric fields in Cartesian coordinates take the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{x}\\
0\\
H_{z}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbf{E}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
E_{y}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(4)

respectively.
The periodic grating profile
\Lambda(x)
can be written as a Fourier series,

\displaystyle\Lambda(x)=\sum_{n}V_{n}e^{ig_{n}x}\,,

(5)

where

g_{n}=\frac{2\pi n}{d}

(6)

are the reciprocal lattice vectors and
V_{n}
are Fourier coefficients of the periodic function
\Lambda(x)
. We can take the general electric-field solution to Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
) in the basis of the same Fourier harmonics,

\displaystyle E_{y}(x,z)=\sum_{n}e^{i(q+g_{n})x}\times\begin{cases}C_{n}e^{ik_{n}z}+D_{n}e^{-ik_{n}z}&z>0\,,\\
A_{n}e^{ik_{n}z}+B_{n}e^{-ik_{n}z}&z<0\,,\\
\end{cases}

(7)

where
A_{n}
,
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
, and
D_{n}
are the field coefficients of the incoming and outgoing waves of the Bragg order
n
, as depicted in Fig.
1
(b). The normal component of the light wave number for each Bragg order is given by

k_{n}=\sqrt{\omega^{2}-(q+g_{n})^{2}}\,,

(8)

with the square root in Eq. (
8
) chosen in such a way that either
{\rm Re}\,k_{n}>0
or
{\rm Im}\,k_{n}>0
, for any real frequency
\omega
and real wave number
q
, so the term
C_{n}e^{ik_{n}z}
in Eq. (
7
) describes, respectively, either a purely propagating in the
z
-direction or a purely evanescent wave. For complex frequencies, the choice of the square root is determined by a proper analytic continuation of Eq. (
8
) from the real
\omega
-axis into the complex frequency plane.

Integrating Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
) across the infinitesimal grating layer results in an infinite set of simultaneous equations for the amplitudes
A_{n}
,
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
, and
D_{n}
:

\displaystyle k_{n}(C_{n}\!-\!D_{n}\!-\!A_{n}\!+\!B_{n})

\displaystyle=-\omega^{2}\chi(\omega)\sum_{m}V_{n-m}(A_{m}\!+\!B_{m})\,,

\displaystyle C_{n}\!+\!D_{n}

\displaystyle=A_{n}\!+\!B_{n}\,,

(9)

see Appendix
A
for a derivation.

Equations (
9
) can be written in matrix form as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{C}\\
\mathbf{B}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{\mathbb{S}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{D}\\
\mathbf{A}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(10)

where
\mathbf{\mathbb{S}}
is the scattering matrix, and
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{C}
, and
\mathbf{D}
are vectors containing the field coefficients
A_{n}
,
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
, and
D_{n}
, respectively.
The scattering matrix can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathbb{S}}=\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbf{W}^{-1}\mathbf{U}&\mathbf{W}^{-1}\\
\mathbf{W}^{-1}&-\mathbf{W}^{-1}\mathbf{U}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(11)

where
\mathbf{W}^{-1}
is the matrix inverse of
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U}
,
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix, and
\mathbf{U}
is the matrix describing the coupling between diffraction orders, with the matrix elements given by

\displaystyle U_{nm}=\frac{\omega^{2}\chi(\omega)}{2ik_{n}}V_{n-m}\,,

(12)

see Appendix
A
for details.

For each Bragg channel
n
, the transmission coefficient
T_{n}
is given by the ratio of the energy of the diffracted wave of order
n
to the energy of an incoming wave (corresponding to
n=0
), which can be both evaluated from the normal component of the Poynting vector
Tikhodeev et al. 2002
, which yields

\displaystyle T_{n}=\left|\frac{k_{n}}{k_{0}}\,C^{2}_{n}\right|\,.

(13)

Here, it is assumed without loss of generality that

A_{n}=\delta_{n0}\,,\quad D_{n}=0\,,

(14)

where
\delta_{nm}
is the Kronecker symbol, and the expansion coefficients
C_{n}
in Eq. (
13
) are found by solving the linear algebraic equations (
10
) [or Eq. (
9
)] with the boundary conditions Eq. (
14
). Note that the above definition of the transmission coefficients, Eq. (
13
), is valid for so-called “open” Bragg channels only, for which

\displaystyle\omega^{2}>(q+g_{n})^{2}\,,

(15)

equivalent to
k_{n}
being real, see Eq. (
8
). For “closed” channels, the inequality Eq. (
15
) is not fulfilled, and the values of
T_{n}
correspond to the near-field coefficients for these channels.

All the electromagnetic modes of the system, including the SPP modes and their dispersion relations, i.e. the dependence of the mode frequency on the wave number, can be found from Eq. (
10
) by applying the outgoing boundary conditions,
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{D}=0
, for all diffraction orders. This is equivalent to solving
a secular equation

\displaystyle\text{Det[}\mathbf{\mathbb{S}}^{-1}(\omega;q)\text{]}=0\,,

(16)

or finding the poles of the scattering matrix
\mathbf{\mathbb{S}}(\omega;q)
in the complex frequency plane for any real wave number
q
.

II.2
Graphene conductivity and normalized frequencies and wave numbers

While the general method introduced in Sec.
II.1
is suitable for treating any thin periodically modulated layer, in what follows, we consider a graphene grating structure depicted in Fig.
1
.
In this case, the susceptibility is given by

\displaystyle\chi(\omega)=\frac{2iN\sigma(\omega)}{\omega},

(17)

where
\sigma(\omega)
is the 2D conductivity of a single homogeneous graphene layer and
N
is the number of parallel graphene layers stuck together in such a way that, on the one hand, the total thickness of the system is much smaller than the SPP wavelength, but on the other hand, all the layers are electronically decoupled and periodically modulated with the modulation function
\Lambda(x)
, the same for all layers. For the graphene strips shown in Fig.
1
, we take this periodic function in the following form

\displaystyle\Lambda(x)=\sum_{n}\Theta\left(\frac{b}{2}-|x-nd|\right)

(18)

where
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function,
d
is the period, and
b
is the width of the graphene strips.

In the long-wavelength limit, the single-layer frequency-dependent optical conductivity of graphene
\sigma(\omega)
is presented in Refs.
Mikhailov and Ziegler 2007
;
Falkovsky 2008
and is shown in Fig.
3
(a) for zero temperature. In terms of the notations used in Ref.
Ahmad et al. 2021
, it is given by

\displaystyle\sigma(\omega)=2\pi[\sigma_{\text{intra}}(\omega)+\sigma_{\text{inter}}(\omega)]\,,

(19)

and full expressions for
\sigma_{\text{intra}}
and
\sigma_{\text{inter}}
, the intraband and interband parts of the graphene conductivity, are derived in Ref.
Ahmad et al. 2021
. While the interband part, given by the Drude model, describes the physical mechanism of conductivity typical for normal metals, the interband part can be understood from the electronic band structure of graphene for a finite chemical potential
\mu
, as shown in Fig.
2
. The interband part
\sigma_{\text{inter}}
comes from optical transitions with energy greater than
2\mu
, contributing to a dip and a step-like feature at
\text{Re}\,\Omega=2
which can be seen in Fig.
3
(a), respectively, in the imaginary and real parts of the total conductivity Eq. (
19
).

Here and below we use for convenience dimensionless frequencies and wave numbers, all normalized to the chemical potential
\mu
as

\Omega=\frac{\hbar\omega}{\mu}\,,\quad Q=\frac{\hbar cq}{\mu}\,,\quad G_{n}=\frac{\hbar cg_{n}}{\mu}\,,\quad K_{n}=\frac{\hbar ck_{n}}{\mu}\,.

(20)

Equation (
8
) then modifies to
K_{n}=\sqrt{\Omega^{2}-(Q+G_{n})^{2}}
, and similar changes are made in all other equations. The periodicity of the structure generates the first Brillouin zone with the range
Q\in[-G_{1}/2,G_{1}/2]
, as depicted in Fig.
3
(b). The conductivity
\sigma(\omega)
is then called below
\sigma(\Omega)
, which is a function of the normalized frequency
\Omega
, but it also depends on a normalized inverse temperature
\mu\beta=\mu/k_{B}T
, where
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant.

Let us note finally that multilayer graphene structures generally have interlayer coupling which alters the electronic band structure near the K-point and thus qualitatively changes the optical conductivity spectrum.
However, it is known that turbostratically stacked graphene layers have negligible interlayer coupling
Min and MacDonald 2008
, simply factorizing the graphene conductivity
Baek et al. 2017
.
Such graphene stacks have already been synthesized
Garlow et al. 2016
;
Wei et al. 2019
.
We propose to use turbostratic multilayer graphene strips
Negishi et al. 2017
to enhance features in transmission due to the TE SPP mode, including the increase of its linewidth and the frequency gap in SPP mode dispersion. In this work, we obtain transmission spectra and approximate frequencies of the TE modes for both single-layer (
N=1
) and turbostratic (
N=10
) graphene grating by using the suitable number of graphene layers
N
in Eq. (
17
).

II.3
Controlling mode frequency and wave number

The conductivity spectrum of graphene has a key frequency
\text{Re}\,\Omega=2
where the interband dip occurs at low temperatures, see Fig.
3
(a). Since the scattering matrix depends on frequency
\omega=\mu\Omega/\hbar

and the SPP mode coupling due to the periodic modulation of the system depends on the grating period
d
,
one can control this coupling and the SPP mode properties by varying the chemical potential
\mu
and the period
d
. In fact, by tuning the grating period
d
, the edge of the first Brillouin zone
G_{1}/2
where the bands are folded also changes, leading to variations of the frequency of the TE SPP folded mode crossing at
Q=0
, as it is clear from Fig.
3
(b).
Within and above the micrometer range, the grating period has a negligible effect on the conductivity spectrum, since the photon wave numbers in this case are much smaller than the electronic wave numbers
Ahmad et al. 2021
— this regime of a negligible spatial dispersion is called above the long-wavelength limit of the conductivity. One can therefore independently choose a particular part of the conductivity spectrum shown in Fig.
3
(a) and the desired normalized coupled mode frequency, where the two TE SPP dispersion lines (which are close to the light lines) cross. These are, for example,
\Omega=1.667
and
\Omega=2
in Fig.
3
(a) and
\Omega=2
,
2.3
, and
2.6
in Fig.
3
(b). For a better description of this tuning process, we define the normalized interband detuning
\Delta=G_{1}-2
, between the crossing in dispersion at
\Omega=G_{1}=2\pi\hbar c/(\mu d)
and the interband dip in the imaginary part of conductivity at
\Omega=2
as illustrated in Fig.
3
(a).
As an example, for a chemical potential of
\mu
= 0.2 eV, the zero detuning
\Delta=0
would require the graphene stripe width to be in the order of micrometers. Since the dispersion of the TE mode is close to the light line
Mikhailov and Ziegler 2007
;
Ahmad et al. 2021
, we may approximate the TE mode dispersion as the folded light line and estimate the grating period required to tune the TE mode to a desired frequency
\Omega
, for any in-plane wave number
Q
.
In Fig.
3
(c) we show the dependence of the grating period
d
required for the TE SPP mode of the
n=-1
diffraction order to reach the frequency
\Omega=2
. For general
\Omega
and
Q
this condition is given by

\displaystyle d=\frac{\hbar c}{\mu}\,\frac{2\pi}{\Omega+Q}\,.

(21)

Note that this dependence is shown in Fig.
3
(c) in terms of different values of the chemical potential
\mu
in eV, unnormalized frequency
\omega
in Hz, and the angle of incidence
\theta
measured from the normal to the graphene plane and defined as
\sin\theta=Q/\Omega
. Clearly
|\theta|\leqslant 30^{\circ}
in this case, since
\Omega=G_{1}
and
|Q|\leqslant G_{1}/2
within the first Brillouin zone.

II.4
Role of the TE SPP modes in the transmission: Analytic approximation at normal incidence

To reveal the effects of periodic modulation on TE SPP modes and the role of these modes in the transmission, we focus in this section on the normal incidence and develop approximate analytical expressions for the transmission around the frequency Re
\Omega=G_{1}
. To do so, we truncate
\mathbf{W}
in the scattering matrix [Eq. (
11
)] to the lowest three diffraction orders,
n=0
and
n=\pm 1
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}=\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle 1+\frac{\Omega\tilde{V}_{0}}{K_{-1}}&\displaystyle\frac{\Omega\tilde{V}_{-1}}{K_{-1}}&\displaystyle\frac{\Omega\tilde{V}_{-2}}{K_{-1}}\\
\displaystyle\frac{\Omega\tilde{V}_{1}}{K_{0}}&\displaystyle 1+\frac{\Omega\tilde{V}_{0}}{K_{0}}&\displaystyle\frac{\Omega\tilde{V}_{-1}}{K_{0}}\\
\displaystyle\frac{\Omega\tilde{V}_{2}}{K_{1}}&\displaystyle\frac{\Omega\tilde{V}_{1}}{K_{1}}&\displaystyle 1+\frac{\Omega\tilde{V}_{0}}{K_{1}}\end{pmatrix},

(22)

where
\tilde{V}_{n}=N\sigma(\Omega)V_{n}
and
K_{n}\equiv\sqrt{\Omega^{2}-(Q+G_{n})^{2}}
.

The transmission coefficients are then given by the middle column entries of
\mathbf{W}^{-1}
,

\displaystyle T_{\pm 1}=\frac{F_{\pm 1}}{\left|\mathbf{W}\right|}\,,\quad T_{0}=\frac{F_{0}}{\left|\mathbf{W}\right|}\,,

(23)

where

\displaystyle F_{\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{K_{1}K_{-1}}\tilde{V}_{\mp 1}\tilde{V}_{\pm 2}-\left(1+\frac{\Omega}{K_{\mp 1}}\tilde{V}_{0}\right)\frac{\Omega}{K_{\pm 1}}\tilde{V}_{\pm 1}\,,

(24)

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{\Omega}{K_{-1}}\tilde{V}_{0}\right)\left(1+\frac{\Omega}{K_{1}}\tilde{V}_{0}\right)-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{K_{1}K_{-1}}\tilde{V}_{2}\tilde{V}_{-2}\,,

(25)

and the determinant of Eq. (
22
) is given by

\left|\mathbf{W}\right|=\left(1+\frac{\Omega}{K_{0}}\tilde{V}_{0}\right)F_{0}+\frac{\Omega}{K_{0}}\tilde{V}_{1}F_{-1}+\frac{\Omega}{K_{0}}\tilde{V}_{-1}F_{1}\,.

(26)

Note that the modulated TE SPP mode frequencies correspond to the zeros of this determinant.

For
Q=0
, corresponding to normal incidence, the approximate transmission coefficients obtained in Eq. (
23
), can be further simplified by using the fact that
K_{0}=\Omega
and
K_{\pm 1}=\sqrt{\Omega^{2}-G_{1}^{2}}
, that results in

\displaystyle F_{\pm 1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Omega\tilde{V}_{1}}{K_{1}}\,\mathcal{D}_{-}(\Omega)\,,

(27)

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{-}(\Omega)\,{\mathcal{D}_{+}}(\Omega)\,,

(28)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{W}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{-}(\Omega)\,\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\,,

(29)

where

\mathcal{D}_{\pm}(\Omega)=1+\frac{\Omega}{K_{1}}\left(\tilde{V}_{0}\pm\tilde{V}_{2}\right)\,,

(30)

and

\mathcal{B}(\Omega)=1+\tilde{V}_{0}+\frac{\Omega}{K_{1}}\left[\tilde{V}_{0}+\tilde{V}_{2}+\tilde{V}_{0}\left(\tilde{V}_{0}+\tilde{V}_{2}\right)-2\tilde{V}_{1}^{2}\right]\,.

(31)

Here, without loss of generality, we have made use of the fact that the Fourier coefficients
V_{n}
of the grating profile function
\Lambda(x)
are real, if
\Lambda(x)
is an even function of
x
; therefore
\tilde{V}_{n}=\tilde{V}_{-n}
. Thus, for
Q=0
, the transmission coefficients in Eq. (
23
) reduce to

T_{0}(\Omega)=\frac{{\mathcal{D}_{+}}(\Omega)}{\mathcal{B}(\Omega)}\,,\quad T_{\pm 1}(\Omega)=-\frac{\Omega}{K_{1}}\frac{\tilde{V}_{1}}{\mathcal{B}(\Omega)}\,.

(32)

As it is clear from Fig.
3
(b), the two folded dispersion lines originating from the unperturbed TE SPP of the homogeneous layer, corresponding to the
n=1
and
n=-1
Bragg channels and crossing at
\Omega\approx G_{1}
, produce in a periodically modulated system two perturbed TE SPP modes at each
Q
. The frequencies of these modes can be found from the zeros of the determinant Eq. (
26
), which factorizes at
Q=0
, according to Eq. (
29
). One mode, having frequency
\Omega_{d}
, is found from the condition
\mathcal{D}_{-}(\Omega_{d})=0
, which yields

\frac{\Omega_{d}}{G_{1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\displaystyle 1-\left(\tilde{V}_{0}-\tilde{V}_{2}\right)^{2}}}\,,

(33)

and is further referred to as
dark mode
, as it does not manifest itself in the normal-incidence transmission. In fact, since
F_{\pm 1}
and
F_{0}
are proportional to
\mathcal{D}_{-}(\Omega)
there is an exact cancellation of this factor in
T_{\pm 1}
and
T_{0}
. The other mode, having frequency
\Omega_{b}
, which we further call
bright mode
, is obtained from equating to zero the other factor in
|\mathbf{W}|
given by Eq. (
29
),
\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{b})=0
, which yields

\frac{\Omega_{b}}{G_{1}}=\frac{1+\tilde{V}_{0}}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\left(1+\tilde{V}_{0}\right)^{2}-\left[(1+\tilde{V}_{0})(\tilde{V}_{0}+\tilde{V}_{2})-\tilde{V}_{1}^{2}\right]^{2}}}\,,

(35)

and a similar approximate expression can be obtained also for
T_{\pm 1}(\Omega)
.

IV
Conclusions

We have shown that the complex-frequency transverse-electric surface plasmon polariton (TE SPP) modes in a graphene grating can be excited by external electro-magnetic waves with close-to-normal incidence. Specifically, a significant reduction in the zeroth diffraction-order transmission is observed near the interband transition frequency of graphene.
This reduction can be explained as a reflection of the energy of the incoming beam due to the resonant coupling to the TE SPP modes.
In addition, we have shown that the frequency and the in-plane wave number of TE SPP modes seen in transmission can be tuned by controlling the graphene chemical potential and grating period. Specifically, we have demonstrated that for zero detuning, that is the grating period is chosen so that the TE SPP mode energy matches the interband threshold of the graphene conductivity, the features in the optical spectra due to the TE SPPs are significantly enhanced.
Furthermore, by using turbostratic graphene layers with negligible interlayer interactions, we can enhance these features further, making them experimentally more accessible than in the single-layer graphene.

A.1
Solving Maxwell’s equations for an infinitely thin periodic layer

We start from Maxwell’s equations having the form

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{t}\mathbf{H}\,,

(36)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}\,,

(37)

where we assume non-magnetic materials, i.e. the permeability
\mu=1
everywhere in space, and use the units in which the speed of light
c=1
.
Assuming a harmonic dependence on time of the electro-magnetic field in the form of
e^{-i\omega t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=i\omega\mathbf{H}\,,

(38)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\varepsilon(\omega)\mathbf{E}\,.

(39)

For the TE polarization, the magnetic and electric fields can be expressed in terms of their Cartesian components as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{x}\\
0\\
H_{z}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbf{E}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
E_{y}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(40)

Maxwell’s equations then take the form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\partial_{z}E_{y}\\
0\\
\partial_{x}E_{y}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=+i\omega\begin{pmatrix}H_{x}\\
0\\
H_{z}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(41)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{y}H_{z}\\
\partial_{z}H_{x}-\partial_{x}H_{z}\\
-\partial_{y}H_{x}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}0\\
E_{y}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(42)

These can be rewritten as

\displaystyle-\partial_{z}E_{y}

\displaystyle=i\omega H_{x}\,,

(43)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}E_{y}

\displaystyle=i\omega H_{z}\,,

(44)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}H_{x}-\partial_{x}H_{z}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\varepsilon E_{y}.

(45)

As defined in Eq. (
1
), the permittivity in the whole space is given by
\varepsilon(x,z;\omega)=1+\chi(\omega)\Lambda(x)\delta(z)
, where
\chi(\omega)
is the 2D susceptibility of a thin (graphene) layer and
\Lambda(x)
is a periodic function describing its spatial modulation, which can be represented by its Fourier series as

\displaystyle\Lambda(x)=\sum_{n}V_{n}e^{ig_{n}x}\,.

(46)

Integrating Eqs. (
43
) and (
45
) over
z
across the point
z=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle-\left.E_{y}\right|_{z=0^{+}}+\left.E_{y}\right|_{z=0^{-}}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(47)

\displaystyle\left.H_{x}\right|_{z=0^{+}}-\left.H_{x}\right|_{z=0^{-}}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\chi(\omega)\Lambda(x)\left.E_{y}\right|_{z=0}\,,

(48)

where
0^{+(-)}
is a positive (negative) infinitesimal. For the TE polarization, it is easier to eliminate
H_{x}
and write equations in terms of the
E_{y}
component having the following general form

\displaystyle E_{y}(x,z)=\sum_{n}e^{i(q+g_{n})x}\times\begin{cases}C_{n}e^{ik_{n}z}+D_{n}e^{-ik_{n}z}&z>0\,,\\
A_{n}e^{ik_{n}z}+B_{n}e^{-ik_{n}z}&z<0\,.\\
\end{cases}

(49)

in which
g_{n}
and
k_{n}
are defined, respectively, by Eqs. (
6
) and (
8
), and
q
is the wave number along
x
.

Substituting Eq. (
43
) into Eq. (
48
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{z}E_{y}\right|_{z=0^{+}}

\displaystyle-\left.\partial_{z}E_{y}\right|_{z=0^{-}}=-\omega^{2}\chi(\omega)\Lambda(x)\left.E_{y}\right|_{z=0},

(50)

which after using the series Eqs. (
46
) and (
49
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{n}

\displaystyle ik_{n}(C_{n}-D_{n}-A_{n}+B_{n})e^{i(q+g_{n})x}

\displaystyle=-\omega^{2}\chi(\omega)\sum_{mn}e^{iqx}e^{ig_{m+n}x}V_{m}(A_{n}+B_{n}).

(51)

By shifting the indices of summation
n\xrightarrow{}n-m
in the right-hand side of Eq. (
51
), we find

\displaystyle\sum_{n}

\displaystyle ik_{n}(C_{n}-D_{n}-A_{n}+B_{n})e^{i(q+g_{n})x}

\displaystyle=-\omega^{2}\chi(\omega)\sum_{m,n}e^{iqx}e^{ig_{n}x}V_{m}(A_{n-m}+B_{n-m})\,,

(52)

and then equating the coefficients at
e^{i(q+g_{n})x}
in Eq. (
52
), we obtain

\displaystyle ik_{n}

\displaystyle(C_{n}-D_{n}-A_{n}+B_{n})

\displaystyle=-\omega^{2}\chi(\omega)\sum_{m}V_{m}(A_{n-m}+B_{n-m})\,.

(53)

Finally, shifting the indices again in the summation in Eq. (
53
), by substituting
m\xrightarrow{}-m+n
, we arrive at

ik_{n}(C_{n}\!-\!D_{n}\!-\!A_{n}\!+\!B_{n})\!=\!-\omega^{2}\chi(\omega)\sum_{m}V_{n-m}(A_{m}\!+\!B_{m})\,.

(54)

In addition to this, we find from the continuity of the electric field, Eq. (
47
), that

\displaystyle C_{n}+D_{n}=A_{n}+B_{n}\,.

(55)

An infinite set of simultaneous equations given by Eqs. (
54
) and (
55
) presents a general solution of Maxwell’s equation in TE polarization for the infinitely thin periodically modulated layer.

A.2
Deriving the scattering matrix

To build a scattering matrix, let us rearrange Eqs. (
54
) and (
55
) in the following way

\displaystyle C_{n}+B_{n}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m}\frac{\omega^{2}\chi(\omega)}{ik_{n}}V_{n-m}B_{m}

\displaystyle=A_{n}+D_{n}-\sum_{m}\frac{\omega^{2}\chi(\omega)}{ik_{n}}V_{n-m}A_{m}

(56)

\displaystyle C_{n}-B_{n}

\displaystyle=A_{n}-D_{n}

(57)

Thus, in terms of
2\times 2
matrices of diffraction-order blocks, the above equations can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{U}\\
\mathbf{I}&-\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}\\
\mathbf{B}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{U}\\
-\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{D}\\
\mathbf{A}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(58)

where vectors
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{C}
, and
\mathbf{D}
have components
A_{n}
,
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
, and
D_{n}
, respectively, matrix
\mathbf{U}
has the matrix elements

\displaystyle U_{nm}=\frac{\omega^{2}\chi(\omega)}{2ik_{n}}V_{n-m}\,,

(59)

and
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix.

The scattering matrix, relating the amplitudes of incoming and outgoing waves, is then given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathbb{S}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{U}\\
\mathbf{I}&-\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{U}\\
-\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}.

(60)

To further simplify it, we can write the inverse of the square matrix in Eq. (
60
) in terms of its blocks:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathbb{S}}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{W}^{-1}&\mathbf{W}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{U})\\
\mathbf{W}^{-1}&-\mathbf{W}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{U}\\
-\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix},

(61)

where
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{U}
. Finally, performing the matrix multiplication, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathbb{S}}=\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbf{W}^{-1}\mathbf{U}&\mathbf{W}^{-1}\\
\mathbf{W}^{-1}&-\mathbf{W}^{-1}\mathbf{U}\end{bmatrix}.

(62)

A.3
TM polarization

We note that TM polarization can be treated in exactly the same manner as TE polarization solved above, leading to the same result for the scattering matrix, provided that matrix
\mathbf{U}
is replaced with
\mathbf{U}^{\text{TM}}
having the following matrix elements:

\displaystyle U_{nm}^{\text{TM}}=-\frac{1}{2}ik_{n}\chi(\omega)V_{n-m}\,.

(63)

Also, in TM polarization, the vectors
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{C}
, and
\mathbf{D}
have the same meaning of the expansion coefficients in Eq. (
49
), but for
H_{y}(x,z)
instead, which in this case is the only non-zero component of the magnetic field.
