Abstract
. We deal with the as yet unresolved exponential stability problem for Beck’s Problem on a metric star graph with three identical edges. The edges are stretched Euler–Bernoulli beams which are simply supported with respect to the outer vertices. At the inner vertex we have viscoelastic damping acting on the slopes of the edges. We carry out a complete spectral analysis of the system operator associated with the abstract spectral problem in Hilbert space. Within this framework it is shown that the eigenvectors have the property of forming a Riesz (i.e. an unconditional) basis, which makes it possible to directly deduce the exponential stability of the corresponding C 0 C_{0} -semigroup using spectral information for the system operator alone. A physically interesting conclusion is that the particular choice of vertex conditions ensures the exponential stability even when the elasticity acting on the slopes of the edges is absent.



1.
Introduction

The small motions of a long, thin, uniform Euler–Bernoulli beam of finite length which is subjected to an external axial force negatively proportional to the bending moment can be modelled by the partial differential equation

(1.1)

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\bm{w}\left(s,t\right)-\gamma\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}\bm{w}\left(s,t\right)+\frac{\partial^{4}}{\partial s^{4}}\bm{w}\left(s,t\right)=0.

Here
\bm{w}\left(s,t\right)
is the deflection of the beam at position
s
and time
t
. In the case where the beam is compressed one has
\gamma<0
, otherwise, when
\gamma>0
, the beam is stretched. By supplementing (
1.1
) with the standard boundary conditions of clamped-free ends, one gets the physical problem known as
Beck’s Problem
, which is one of the oldest problems in the theory of elastic stability (see
[
2
,
5
,
32
]
). An analysis of the existence, location, multiplicity and asymptotics of the eigenvalues, i.e., a complete spectral analysis of the boundary-eigenvalue problem associated with (
1.1
), subject to damped, simply supported (pinned) boundary conditions has been given in
[
21
]
in the spatially variable compression/tension case.

Our primary interest in this paper lies with the dynamic behaviour and stability of a star-shaped network of such beams, with a dampening effect at the junction point of the network. This is an interesting problem which should find application in a varied assortment of mechanical and control problems on networks. Beck’s Problem, of course, is nothing new by itself, but the problem we consider is an interpretation as a network setup of Beck’s Problem with damped simple-elastic ends. Even in this comparatively simple case (the star-shaped network is an elementary example of a nontrivial network) we are not aware that the stability problem, let alone the exponential stability problem has been addressed previously. From the physical viewpoint the natural question the engineer will therefore ask (but which so far has escaped attention in the mathematical literature) is this:
Do the connectivity conditions coupling the beams cause all motions of the network to die out, possibly exponentially, as time progresses?

To explain our problem in a bit more detail, let us consider first (
1.1
) on each beam or what we call edge
e
:

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\bm{w}_{e}\left(s,t\right)-\gamma\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}\bm{w}_{e}\left(s,t\right)+\frac{\partial^{4}}{\partial s^{4}}\bm{w}_{e}\left(s,t\right)=0,\quad s\in e.

The existence and properties of solutions
\bm{w}_{e}
also depend on initial conditions, boundary conditions at the outer vertices of the network, and certain connectivity conditions at the inner vertex. We are going to consider an equilateral
3
-edge metric star graph, i.e. a metric star graph formed by three identical edges
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
,
e_{3}
, as illustrated in Fig.
1
, where each edge is of the same finite length and there is a single inner vertex at
a_{0}
. The boundary conditions at the outer vertices
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
,
a_{3}
correspond to the edges being simply supported at one end. At the inner vertex we have a combination of elasticity and viscous damping, or
viscoelastic damping
acting on the angles or slopes of the edges. It turns out that the question of stability essentially concerns (not so surprisingly) the viscous damping condition at the inner vertex but (surprisingly) not the elasticity condition, as long as
\gamma>0
.

Figure 1.
An equilateral
3
-edge metric star graph.

The problem with the system considered, and in fact with all continuum mechanical or distributed systems governed by partial differential equations, is that the spectral mapping theorem in general need not hold. This difficulty prevents use of only spectral information for the
system operator
(or, equivalently, for the corresponding spectral problem) to study the exponential stability of the corresponding strongly continuous semigroup for an abstract or operator formalism in
infinite-dimensional
state space. By exponential stability of the semigroup we mean that the norms of solutions of the corresponding first-order initial-value problem decay exponentially to zero as time goes to infinity; a formal definition will be given later. So it is clear from what has just been said that the mathematical question directly related to the question in the second paragraph then arises:
Does the spectrum of the system operator determine the decay rate of the semigroup?

As indicated above, the key to our approach to the stability problem is the abstract formulation in terms of operators. Let
{S}\left(t\right)
be a strongly continuous semigroup of bounded linear operators, in short
C_{0}
-semigroup, on the Hilbert space
\mathbb{X}
with infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{T}
(a closed linear operator with domain
\bm{D}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
dense in
\mathbb{X}
). The operator
\mathcal{T}
is the system operator in our contexts. In general, then, one always has by the Hille–Yoshida theorem that

(1.2)

\omega\left(\mathcal{T}\right)\geq s\left(\mathcal{T}\right),

where
\omega\left(\mathcal{T}\right)\coloneqq\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left\|{S}\left(t\right)\right\|
is the type,
s\left(\mathcal{T}\right)\coloneqq\sup\left\{\operatorname{Re}{\lambda}~\middle|~\lambda\in\sigma\left(\mathcal{T}\right)\right\}
is the spectral abscissa, and
\sigma\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
denotes the spectrum of
\mathcal{T}
. If equality holds in (
1.2
), then, as is well known,
\mathcal{T}
satisfies the
spectrum-determined growth assumption
. If

(1.3)

\sup\left\{\operatorname{Re}{\lambda}~\middle|~\lambda\in\sigma\left(\mathcal{T}\right)\right\}\leq-\varepsilon<0

for some constant
\varepsilon>0
, then the exponential stability of
S\left(t\right)
is a
direct consequence of the spectrum-determined growth assumption. Hence, in this case,
\varepsilon
is the decay rate and is intimately related to the spectral abscissa. Let us note that even if we can show that
\mathcal{T}
satisfies the spectrum-determined growth assumption, we must still face the problem of analysing and hence estimating its spectrum, which is a good deal more complicated given the network nature of the system. This means, effectively, that the object of the paper is twofold: prove that we have equality in (
1.2
) and, in particular, provide accurate estimates of the location and asymptotics of the spectrum of
\mathcal{T}
thereby show that condition (
1.3
) holds. This is the main undertaking of the paper and it is hoped that the methods we employ, and the results we obtain, will serve as a guide for general star graphs of beams. (The reader will undoubtedly notice that our results may in fact be extended, without additional effort, to graphs of more general topology. The symmetry of the graph about the vertices does probably play a role, however, when it comes to spectral analyses.)

Our results are naturally obtained in the context of the classical spectral problem

(1.4)

\mathcal{T}x=\lambda x,\quad x\in\bm{D}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)\subset\mathbb{X},\quad\lambda\in\mathbf{C},

for the root vectors
x
(eigen- and associated vectors or chains of eigen- and associated vectors). Here
\lambda
is the spectral parameter, and it is important to note that the system operator
\mathcal{T}
is not skewadjoint in general. We seek to prove the spectrum-determined growth assumption by proving that
\mathcal{T}
is a discrete operator (employing some old terminology of Dunford, see
[
8
, Definition XIX.1]
) – i.e. it has a compact resolvent and thus a discrete spectrum consisting only of normal eigenvalues (isolated eigenvalues of finite algebraic multiplicity, see Definition
4.3
) and its root vectors form a
Riesz basis
for the underlying Hilbert space
\mathbb{X}
. A bonus of using this “spectral approach” is that the Riesz basis property or Riesz basisness of the root vectors also gives the semigroup generating property and guarantees the representation of solutions of the initial-value problem as norm-convergent series of eigensolutions. All these considerations already show that in effect we reduce the study of exponential stability to using only spectral information for the system operator, as one would do in lumped parameter systems modelled by ordinary differential equations (cf., e.g.,
[
5
]
).

Function spaces and operators basic to the abstract formulation are defined in the section. We will use some terminology common in the literature of differential equations on networks or on metric graphs, and we recommend the reader consult the papers

[

,

]
. We should also note the very readable monograph
[

]
in which the authors present a variety of special examples from mechanics of metric graphs. In Section

we examine the well-posedness of the initial-value problem, which requires study of the semigroup generating property of the system operator
\mathcal{T}
. The heart of this work – also from a technical standpoint – lies in Sections

and

, where we establish the key results required for the proof of exponential stability in Section

. While Section

is devoted to a complete spectral analysis of

, in Section

and Xu and Yung

. The former is a well-known result on the completeness of root vectors of compact operators on a Hilbert space which are subject to certain nuclearity conditions. 2.
Problem formulation and preliminaries

As mentioned in the Introduction, our results will be obtained in the framework of abstract function spaces. We begin, however, with a detailed description of the physical model.

2.1.
The physical model

We define the graph
\mathscr{G}\coloneqq\left(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E}\right)
with

\mathscr{V}=\left\{a_{0}\right\}\cup\left\{a_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{3},\quad\mathscr{E}=\left\{e_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{3},

the vertex and edge sets, respectively. Each of the edges
e_{j}
connecting the inner vertex
a_{0}
to the outer vertices
a_{j}
is of unit length and is identified with the interval
s_{j}\in\left[0,1\right]
,
j=1,2,3
. The values
s_{j}=0
and
s_{j}=1
correspond to the inner and outer vertices, respectively. The graph
\mathscr{G}
thus defined is a metric graph.

Letting
\bm{w}_{j}\left(s_{j},t\right)
be the deflections of the edges
e_{j}
for
s_{j}\in\left[0,1\right]
at time
t\geq 0
from their equilibrium positions which we identify with
\mathcal{G}
, we suppose they satisfy the partial differential equation

(2.1)

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\bm{w}_{j}\left(s_{j},t\right)-\gamma\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s_{j}^{2}}\bm{w}_{j}\left(s_{j},t\right)+\frac{\partial^{4}}{\partial s_{j}^{4}}\bm{w}_{j}\left(s_{j},t\right)=0,\quad j=1,2,3.

Associated with (
2.1
) we have given initial conditions

(2.2)

\bm{w}_{j}\left(s_{j},0\right)=g_{j}\left(s_{j}\right),\quad\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{w}_{j}\left(s_{j},t\right)\right|_{t=0}=h_{j}\left(s_{j}\right),\quad s_{j}\in e_{j},\quad j=1,2,3,

where the known functions
g_{j}
,
h_{j}
are “suitably” smooth as specified later. The edges are assumed simply supported at the outer vertices
a_{j}
, so the boundary conditions at
s_{j}=1
are

(2.3)

\bm{w}_{j}\left(1,t\right)=\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s_{j}^{2}}\bm{w}_{j}\left(s_{j},t\right)\right|_{s_{j}=1}=0,\quad j=1,2,3.

At the inner vertex various connectivity conditions may be considered; we impose the following:

•

Deflection compatibility condition:

(2.4)

\bm{w}_{i}\left(0,t\right)=\bm{w}_{j}\left(0,t\right),\quad i,j=1,2,3.

•

Viscoelastic damping condition:

(2.5)

\left.\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s_{j}^{2}}\bm{w}_{j}\left(s_{j},t\right)-\alpha\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{j}}\bm{w}_{j}\left(s_{j},t\right)-\beta\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s_{j}\partial t}\bm{w}_{j}\left(s_{j},t\right)\right)\right|_{s_{j}=0}=0,\quad j=1,2,3.

•

Force balance condition:

(2.6)

\sum^{3}_{j=1}\left.\left(\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial s_{j}^{3}}\bm{w}_{j}\left(s_{j},t\right)-\gamma\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{j}}\bm{w}_{j}\left(s_{j},t\right)\right)\right|_{s_{j}=0}=0.

These conditions seem natural. Condition (
2.4
) ensures the continuity or compatibility of the deflections of the edges across the inner vertex, for otherwise the edges would not be joined to the network. Condition (
2.5
) recognises that the bending moments at the inner vertex are proportional to linear combinations of the slopes and the angular velocities at the inner vertex. It corresponds, more concretely, to the slopes at the vertex being acted on by elastic and viscous damping effects which are proportional, respectively, to
\alpha
and
\beta
. Condition (
2.6
) is important in that it balances the internal forces acting on the inner vertex, assuming that the edges undergo “nonfollowing” tension or compression proportional to
\gamma
. By this is meant that the external axial forces remain constant in direction along the axes of the edges, projected in the direction of the shear forces. It is crucial to understand that this assumption, although physically reasonable, makes the originally nonconservative Beck’s Problem conservative in the absence of dissipative effects (i.e. when
\beta=0
). Strictly speaking then, Beck’s Problem specialises to the classical problem of Euler, but we shall not stress this distinction between the two problems in the paper (see the books
[
5
,
32
]
and the survey
[
9
]
for further discussion of this point). Throughout the paper we will make the assumption that the edges are stretched for fixed
\gamma>0
and let the parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
vary. (We expect our techniques to apply similarly to the compression modification, i.e. when
\gamma<0
.)

The problem posed by (
2.1
)–(
2.6
) constitutes the initial/boundary-value problem which, we should stress, is considerably more complicated and does not fit precisely into the framework considered, e.g., in
[
29
]
or
[
30
, Section 4.2]
(as well as in the references contained therein). So in what follows we suggest an alternative approach to abstracting the initial/boundary-value problem and to verification of the necessary theoretical concepts.

2.2.
Operator formulation

Let us begin by putting
s
in place of the variable
s_{j}\in\left[0,1\right]
, and set

\bm{v}_{j}\left(s,t\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{w}_{j}\left(s,t\right),\quad\bm{x}_{j}\left(s,t\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{w}_{j}\left(s,t\right)\\[1.99997pt]
\bm{v}_{j}\left(s,t\right)\end{matrix}\right),\quad j=1,2,3,

and

\bm{x}\left(s,t\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{x}_{1}\left(s,t\right)\\[1.99997pt]
\bm{x}_{2}\left(s,t\right)\\[1.99997pt]
\bm{x}_{3}\left(s,t\right)\end{matrix}\right).

Denoting by
\bm{H}^{m}\left(0,1\right)
,
m\in\mathbf{N}_{0}\left(=\mathbf{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}\right)
, the Sobolev space with
\bm{L}_{2}\left(0,1\right)\coloneqq\bm{H}^{0}\left(0,1\right)
, we define the space

\hat{\bm{H}}^{2}\left(0,1\right)\coloneqq\left\{w\in\bm{H}^{2}\left(0,1\right)~\middle|~w\left(1\right)=0\right\}

which is a closed subspace of
\bm{H}^{2}\left(0,1\right)
. With the inner product

\left(w,\tilde{w}\right)=\int^{1}_{0}w^{\prime\prime}\left(s\right)\overline{\tilde{w}^{\prime\prime}\left(s\right)}\,ds+\alpha w^{\prime}\left(0\right)\overline{\tilde{w}^{\prime}\left(0\right)}

the space
\hat{\bm{H}}^{2}\left(0,1\right)
is a Hilbert space for
\alpha>0
. To proceed further it is necessary to define the metric spaces of three-component vectors in the following way:

\bm{L}_{2}\left(\mathscr{G}\right)\coloneqq\left\{v=\left(\begin{matrix}v_{1}\\[1.99997pt]
v_{2}\\[1.99997pt]
v_{3}\end{matrix}\right)~\middle|~v_{j}\in\bm{L}_{2}\left(0,1\right),\quad j=1,2,3\right\}

and

\hat{\bm{H}}^{2}\left(\mathscr{G}\right)\coloneqq\left\{w=\left(\begin{matrix}w_{1}\\[1.99997pt]
w_{2}\\[1.99997pt]
w_{3}\end{matrix}\right)~\middle|~\begin{gathered}w_{j}\in\hat{\bm{H}}^{2}\left(0,1\right),\\[1.99997pt]
w_{i}\left(0\right)=w_{j}\left(0\right),\quad i,j=1,2,3\end{gathered}\right\}.

With these we define by

(2.7)

\mathbb{X}=\hat{\bm{H}}^{2}\left(\mathscr{G}\right)\times\bm{L}_{2}\left(\mathscr{G}\right)\coloneqq\left\{x=\left\{x_{j}\right\}^{3}_{j=1}~\middle|~\begin{gathered}x_{j}=\left(\begin{matrix}w_{j}\\[1.99997pt]
v_{j}\end{matrix}\right)\in\hat{\bm{H}}^{2}\left(0,1\right)\times\bm{L}_{2}\left(0,1\right),\\[1.99997pt]
w_{i}\left(0\right)=w_{j}\left(0\right),\quad i,j=1,2,3\end{gathered}\right\}

the state space with the inner product

\mathinner{\langle x,\tilde{x}\rangle}\coloneqq\left(w,\tilde{w}\right)_{2}+\left(w,\tilde{w}\right)_{1}+\left(v,\tilde{v}\right)_{0},

where

\displaystyle\left(w,\tilde{w}\right)_{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(\int^{1}_{0}w_{j}^{\prime\prime}\left(s\right)\overline{\tilde{w}_{j}^{\prime\prime}\left(s\right)}\,ds+\alpha w^{\prime}_{j}\left(0\right)\overline{\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{j}\left(0\right)}\right),\quad\left(w,\tilde{w}\right)_{1}=\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{3}\int^{1}_{0}w_{j}^{\prime}\left(s\right)\overline{\tilde{w}_{j}^{\prime}\left(s\right)}\,ds,

\displaystyle\left(v,\tilde{v}\right)_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\int^{1}_{0}v_{j}\left(s\right)\overline{\tilde{v}_{j}\left(s\right)}\,ds.

The resulting norm in
\mathbb{X}
will be denoted by
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|
, as usual.

In
\mathbb{X}
we define the system operator
\mathcal{T}
on the domain

(2.8)

\bm{D}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)=\left\{x=\left\{x_{j}\right\}^{3}_{j=1}\in\mathbb{X}~\middle|~\begin{gathered}x_{j}=\left(\begin{matrix}w_{j}\\[1.99997pt]
v_{j}\end{matrix}\right)\in(\bm{H}^{4}\left(0,1\right)\cap\hat{\bm{H}}^{2}\left(0,1\right))\times\hat{\bm{H}}^{2}\left(0,1\right),\\[1.99997pt]
w^{\prime\prime}_{j}\left(1\right)=0,\quad w^{\prime\prime}_{j}\left(0\right)-\alpha w^{\prime}_{j}\left(0\right)-\beta v^{\prime}_{j}\left(0\right)=0,\quad j=1,2,3,\\
\sum_{j=1}^{3}\,(w^{(3)}_{j}\left(0\right)-\gamma w^{\prime}_{j}\left(0\right))=0\end{gathered}\right\}

by

(2.9)

\mathcal{T}{x}\coloneqq\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}v_{j}\\[1.99997pt]
-w_{j}^{(4)}+\gamma w^{\prime\prime}_{j}\end{matrix}\right)\right\}_{j=1}^{3}.

Then we can rewrite the initial/boundary-value problem described by (
2.1
)–(
2.6
) as an abstract first-order initial-value problem in
\mathbb{X}
in the form

(2.10)

\left\{\begin{gathered}\dot{x}\left(t\right)=\mathcal{T}{x}\left(t\right),\quad{x}\left(t\right)=\bm{x}\left(\,\cdot\,,t\right)=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{w}_{j}\left(\,\cdot\,,t\right)\\[1.99997pt]
\bm{v}_{j}\left(\,\cdot\,,t\right)\end{matrix}\right)\right\}^{3}_{j=1},\\
{x}\left(0\right)=x_{0}=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}g_{j}\\[1.99997pt]
h_{j}\end{matrix}\right)\right\}^{3}_{j=1}.\end{gathered}\right.

The initial state
x_{0}
is assumed to be suitably smooth in the sense that
x_{0}\in\bm{D}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
. We recall two basic notions from semigroup theory:

•

Let
{x}\left(t\right)={S}\left(t\right)x_{0}
, continuously differentiable and satisfying (
2.10
) in
\mathbb{X}
for all
t\in\mathbf{R}_{+}
, where
{S}\left(t\right)
is a
C_{0}
-semigroup on
\mathbb{X}
with infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{T}
. Then
{x}\left(t\right)
is a
classical
solution, or simply a solution of (
2.10
).

•

The
C_{0}
-semigroup generated by
\mathcal{T}
is said to be
exponentially
stable if and only if there are constants
M,\varepsilon>0
such that

(2.11)

\left\|{S}\left(t\right)\right\|\leq Me^{-\varepsilon t},\quad t\geq 0.

The goal in the paper now clearly becomes one of proving that for solutions of (
2.10
) we have (
2.11
). For this, according to our programme stated in the Introduction, we must check whether equality in (
1.2
) holds and condition (
1.3
) is satisfied. We must first however check to see if (
2.10
) is well posed.

3.
Well-posedness

The next two lemmas deal with properties of
\mathcal{T}
defined by (
2.8
), (
2.9
) and will be also of importance later in the proofs of the main theorems. We recall that, by definition,

\alpha,\beta\geq 0,\quad\gamma>0

throughout the whole paper.

Lemma 3.1
.

\mathcal{T}
has a compact inverse.

Proof.

We consider the equation

(3.1)

\mathcal{T}x=\tilde{x}

with
\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{X}
,
x\in\bm{D}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
, which is equivalent to

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} v_{j}&=\tilde{w}_{j},&j&=1,2,3,\\[1.99997pt]
-w^{(4)}_{j}+\gamma w^{\prime\prime}_{j}&=\tilde{v}_{j},&j&=1,2,3,\\[1.99997pt]
w_{j}\left(1\right)=w^{\prime\prime}_{j}\left(1\right)&=0,&j&=1,2,3,\\[1.99997pt]
w_{i}\left(0\right)&=w_{j}\left(0\right),&i,j&=1,2,3,\\[1.99997pt]
w^{\prime\prime}_{j}\left(0\right)-\alpha w^{\prime}_{j}\left(0\right)-\beta v^{\prime}_{j}\left(0\right)&=0,&j&=1,2,3,\\[1.99997pt]
\sum^{3}_{j=1}\,(w^{(3)}_{j}\left(0\right)-\gamma w^{\prime}_{j}\left(0\right))&=0.&&&\end{aligned}\right.

Integrating the differential equations in (
3.2
) twice from
0
to
1
, making use of the boundary conditions
w_{j}\left(1\right)=w^{\prime\prime}_{j}\left(1\right)=0
,
j=1,2,3
, we get

(3.3)

w^{\prime\prime}_{j}\left(s\right)-\gamma w_{j}\left(s\right)+(w^{(3)}_{j}\left(0\right)-\gamma w_{j}^{\prime}\left(0\right))\left(1-s\right)=-\tilde{V}_{j}\left(s\right),\quad j=1,2,3,

with the integral terms

\tilde{V}_{j}\left(s\right)=-\int^{1}_{s}dt\int^{t}_{0}\tilde{v}_{j}\left(r\right)dr.

The solutions of (
3.3
) can be directly computed to give

(3.4)

\begin{split}w_{j}\left(s\right)&=a_{j}\sinh\sqrt{\gamma}\left(1-s\right)+(w^{(3)}_{j}\left(0\right)-\gamma w_{j}^{\prime}\left(0\right))\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\int^{1}_{s}\left(1-r\right)\sinh\sqrt{\gamma}\left(s-r\right)dr\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\int^{1}_{s}\sinh\sqrt{\gamma}\left(s-r\right)\tilde{V}_{j}\left(r\right)dr,\quad j=1,2,3,\end{split}

where
a_{j}

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\lambda\mapsto\left(\lambda I-\mathcal{A}\right)
be a mapping from
\mathbf{C}
into the set of closed linear operators in
\mathbb{X}
. A number
\lambda\in\mathbf{C}
is said to belong to
\varrho\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
, the resolvent set of
\mathcal{A}
, provided
\lambda I-\mathcal{A}
has a closed and bounded inverse; we call the inverse
\left(\lambda I-\mathcal{A}\right)^{-1}
the resolvent of
\mathcal{A}
. If
\lambda\not\in\varrho\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
, then
\lambda
is said to be in the spectrum
\sigma\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
of
\mathcal{A}
. We say that a number
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbf{C}
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}
if the geometric eigenspace
\operatorname{Ker}\left(\lambda_{0}I-\mathcal{A}\right)\neq\left\{0\right\}
and there exists an eigenvector
x_{0}\left(\neq 0\right)
corresponding to
\lambda_{0}
such that (
1.4
) is satisfied. The set of all eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}
forms the point spectrum of
\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
{\lambda}_{0}\in\mathbf{C}
be an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}
. The geometric multiplicity of
\lambda_{0}
is the number of linearly independent eigenvectors in a system of chains of root vectors of
\mathcal{A}
corresponding to
\lambda_{0}
and is defined as
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Ker}\left(\lambda_{0}I-\mathcal{A}\right)
. The algebraic multiplicity of
{\lambda_{0}}
is the maximum value of the sum of the lengths of chains corresponding to the linearly independent eigenvectors and is defined as
\sup_{k\in\mathbf{N}}\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Ker}\left(\lambda_{0}I-\mathcal{A}\right)^{k}
. We call
{\lambda_{0}}
semisimple if it has equal geometric and algebraic multiplicities (i.e. there are no associated vectors corresponding to
\lambda_{0}
), and simple if it is semisimple and
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Ker}\left(\lambda_{0}I-\mathcal{A}\right)=1
.

Definition 4.3
.

If an eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbf{C}
is isolated and
\lambda_{0}I-\mathcal{A}
is a Fredholm operator (see
[
14
, Section IV.5.1]
for definition), then we call
\lambda_{0}
a normal eigenvalue. The set of all such eigenvalues is denoted by
\sigma_{0}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
.

The next result refers to the structure of the spectrum of
\mathcal{T}
and location of eigenvalues.

Theorem 4.1
.

The following statements hold:

(1)

\sigma\left(\mathcal{T}\right)=\sigma_{0}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
.

(2)

The number
\lambda=0
is not an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{T}
.

(3)

If
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{T}
, then
\overline{\lambda}
, the complex-conjugate of
{\lambda}
, is also an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{T}
. Hence
\sigma\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
is symmetric with respect to the real axis.

(4)

\sigma\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
lies in the closed left half-plane; when

is confined to the open left half-plane.

Proof.

Statements

and
2
follow from Lemma

. In fact from the lemma it follows that
\mathcal{T}
has a compact resolvent. So the spectrum of

consists of an infinite number of normal eigenvalues with no finite accumulation points (see
[
11

). Using that
\mathcal{T}
is a real operator establishes statement

1.4
) shows that

satisfies its conjugate spectral problem and is an eigenvector of
\mathcal{T}
which corresponds to the eigenvalue
\overline{\lambda}
. In order to establish statement

, we take the inner product of (

) with the corresponding

\mathinner{\langle\mathcal{T}x,x\rangle}=\lambda\left\|x\right\|^{2}.

The real part of this equation is

\operatorname{Re}\mathinner{\langle\mathcal{T}x,x\rangle}=\operatorname{Re}{\lambda}\left\|x\right\|^{2},

yielding by Lemma

This proves that the spectrum of
\mathcal{T}
lies in the closed left half-plane. We show that if

. Indeed, suppose

is a purely imaginary eigenvalue,

, with eigenvector

. Then from (

\operatorname{Re}\mathinner{\langle\mathcal{T}x,x\rangle}=0
. The proof of Lemma

then clearly implies, since

As

\left\{\begin{aligned} w^{(4)}_{j}-\gamma w^{\prime\prime}_{j}&=-\lambda^{2}w_{j},&j&=1,2,3,\\[1.99997pt]

(4.12)

\left\{\begin{split}\mu_{3}\cosh\mu_{1}\sum^{3}_{j=1}c_{j,1}+\mu_{1}\cosh\mu_{3}\sum^{3}_{j=1}c_{j,2}&=0,\\[1.99997pt]
\left[\mu^{2}_{1}\sinh\mu_{1}+\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\mu_{1}\cosh\mu_{1}\right]\sum^{3}_{j=1}c_{j,1}\hskip 68.99895pt&\\
+\left[\mu^{2}_{3}\sinh\mu_{3}+\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\mu_{3}\cosh\mu_{3}\right]\sum^{3}_{j=1}c_{j,2}&=0.\end{split}\right.

We divide the discussion of the solutions of (
4.12
) into two cases. Before going on to these, however, it will be convenient to derive a few intermediate results. Let
\Delta_{1}=\Delta_{1}\left(\lambda\right)
be the determinant of the coefficient matrix formed by (
4.12
). We then see easily that

(4.13)

\Delta_{1}\left(\lambda\right)=\mu^{3}_{3}\cosh\mu_{1}\sinh\mu_{3}-\mu^{3}_{1}\sinh\mu_{1}\cosh\mu_{3}+(\mu^{2}_{3}-\mu^{2}_{1})\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\cosh\mu_{1}\cosh\mu_{3}.

Let us set

(4.14)

\sum^{3}_{j=1}c_{j,2}=-b\mu_{1}\left[\mu_{1}\sinh\mu_{1}+\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\cosh\mu_{1}\right],

for some constant
b\neq 0
. Then

(4.15)

\sum^{3}_{j=1}c_{j,1}=b\mu_{3}\left[\mu_{3}\sinh\mu_{3}+\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\cosh\mu_{3}\right].

The compatibility condition
w_{i}\left(0\right)=w_{j}\left(0\right)\equiv w\left(0\right)
,
i,j=1,2,3
, implies, together with (
4.9
), that

(4.16)

c_{j,1}\sinh\mu_{1}+c_{j,2}\sinh\mu_{3}=w\left(0\right),\quad j=1,2,3.

So it follows on summing (
4.16
) over
j=1,2,3
and using (
4.14
), (
4.15
) that

\displaystyle\sinh\mu_{1}\left\{b\mu_{3}\left[\mu_{3}\sinh\mu_{3}+\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\cosh\mu_{3}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 68.99895pt-\sinh\mu_{3}\left\{b\mu_{1}\left[\mu_{1}\sinh\mu_{1}+\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\cosh\mu_{1}\right]\right\}=3w\left(0\right).

From this it follows that if we let
\Delta_{2}=\Delta_{2}\left(\lambda\right)
be another determinant given by

(4.17)

\Delta_{2}\left(\lambda\right)=(\mu^{2}_{3}-\mu^{2}_{1})\sinh\mu_{1}\sinh\mu_{3}+\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\left(\mu_{3}\sinh\mu_{1}\cosh\mu_{3}-\mu_{1}\cosh\mu_{1}\sinh\mu_{3}\right),

then

w\left(0\right)=\frac{b}{3}\Delta_{2}\left(\lambda\right).

Case 1.
We consider first the case of a nonzero solution pair
\sum^{3}_{j=1}c_{j,1}
,
\sum^{3}_{j=1}c_{j,2}
of (
4.12
). The necessary and sufficient condition for this is that
\Delta_{1}\left(\lambda\right)=0
. Solving the system of equations given by (
4.10
) and (
4.16
) for
c_{j,1}
,
c_{j,2}
,
j=1,2,3
, we have

(4.18)

\displaystyle c_{j,1}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{b\mu^{2}_{3}\left[\mu_{3}\sinh\mu_{3}+\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\cosh\mu_{3}\right]\cosh\mu_{1}}{3\mu_{3}\cosh\mu_{1}},

(4.19)

\displaystyle c_{j,2}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle-\frac{b\mu^{2}_{1}\left[\mu_{1}\sinh\mu_{1}+\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\cosh\mu_{1}\right]\cosh\mu_{3}}{3\mu_{1}\cosh\mu_{3}}.

Note that

\mu^{2}_{3}\left[\mu_{3}\sinh\mu_{3}+\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\cosh\mu_{3}\right]\cosh\mu_{1}=\Delta_{1}\left(\lambda\right)+\mu^{2}_{1}\left[\mu_{1}\sinh\mu_{1}+\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\cosh\mu_{1}\right]\cosh\mu_{3}.

Let

b=\frac{3c\mu_{1}\mu_{3}\cosh\mu_{1}\cosh\mu_{3}}{\mu^{2}_{1}\left[\mu_{1}\sinh\mu_{1}+\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\cosh\mu_{1}\right]\cosh\mu_{3}}.

It is easy to see then from (
4.18
), (
4.19
) that
c_{j,1}=c\mu_{1}\cosh\mu_{3}
,
c_{j,2}=-c\mu_{3}\cosh\mu_{1}
,
j=1,2,3
. Using these relations in (
4.9
), we obtain for the eigenfunctions

(4.20)

w_{j}\left(s\right)=c\left(\mu_{1}\cosh\mu_{3}\sinh\mu_{1}\left(1-s\right)-\mu_{3}\cosh\mu_{1}\sinh\mu_{3}\left(1-s\right)\right),\quad j=1,2,3.

Case 2.
We suppose now that (
4.12
) has only the zero solution, i.e.
\Delta_{1}\left(\lambda\right)\neq 0
.
From (
4.16
) we then get that
w\left(0\right)=0
. Nonzero solutions of the system of equations given by (
4.10
) and (
4.16
) exist if and only if
\Delta_{2}\left(\lambda\right)=0
. Then, because
\sinh\mu_{1}\neq 0
and
\sinh\mu_{3}\neq 0
, we have

c_{j,1}=-\frac{\sinh\mu_{3}}{\sinh\mu_{1}}\,c_{j,2},\quad j=1,2,3.

Setting

a_{j}=\frac{c_{j,1}}{\sinh\mu_{3}}=-\frac{c_{j,2}}{\sinh\mu_{1}},\quad j=1,2,3,

and recalling that here
\sum^{3}_{j=1}c_{j,1}=\sum^{3}_{j=1}c_{j,2}=0
, we arrive at

\sum^{3}_{j=1}a_{j}=\frac{1}{\sinh\mu_{3}}\sum^{3}_{j=1}c_{j,1}=-\frac{1}{\sinh\mu_{1}}\sum^{3}_{j=1}c_{j,2}=0.

In this case (
4.9
) becomes

w_{j}\left(s\right)=a_{j}\left(\sinh\mu_{3}\sinh\mu_{1}\left(1-s\right)-\sinh\mu_{1}\sinh\mu_{3}\left(1-s\right)\right),\quad j=1,2,3,\quad\sum^{3}_{j=1}a_{j}=0.

Let us write
w_{1,j}=a_{1,j}\phi
,
w_{2,j}=a_{2,j}\phi
,
j=1,2,3
, where

(4.21)

\left\{a_{1,1},a_{1,2},a_{1,3}\right\}=\left\{-2,1,1\right\},\quad\left\{a_{2,1},a_{2,2},a_{2,3}\right\}=\left\{0,1,-1\right\}

and
\phi
is given by

(4.22)

\phi\left(s\right)=\sinh\mu_{3}\sinh\mu_{1}\left(1-s\right)-\sinh\mu_{1}\sinh\mu_{3}\left(1-s\right).

The eigenvector
x
corresponding to
\lambda
can then be represented as

x=\hat{a}x_{1}+\hat{c}x_{2},\quad\hat{a},\hat{c}\in\mathbf{C},

with

x_{1}=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}w_{1,j}\\[1.99997pt]
\lambda w_{1,j}\end{matrix}\right)\right\}^{3}_{j=1},\quad x_{2}=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}w_{2,j}\\[1.99997pt]
\lambda w_{2,j}\end{matrix}\right)\right\}^{3}_{j=1}.

We summarise in the following result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let the characteristic exponents satisfying (
4.6
) be given as in (
4.7
) for
\lambda\in\mathbf{C}\backslash\left\{-\frac{\gamma}{2}\right\}
, and let the determinants
\Delta_{1}\left(\lambda\right)
and
\Delta_{2}\left(\lambda\right)
be given by (
4.13
) and (
4.17
), respectively. The spectrum of
\mathcal{T}
consists of two branches,

\sigma\left(\mathcal{T}\right)=\sigma^{(1)}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)\cup\sigma^{(2)}\left(\mathcal{T}\right),

where

\sigma^{(1)}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbf{C}\backslash\left\{-\frac{\gamma}{2}\right\}~\middle|~\Delta_{1}\left(\lambda\right)=0\right\},\quad\sigma^{(2)}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbf{C}\backslash\left\{-\frac{\gamma}{2}\right\}~\middle|~\Delta_{2}\left(\lambda\right)=0\right\}.

For each
\lambda\in\sigma^{(1)}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
the corresponding eigenvector is

x^{(1)}=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}w_{j}\\[1.99997pt]
\lambda w_{j}\end{matrix}\right)\right\}^{3}_{j=1},

where the
w_{j}
are given by (
4.20
). For each
\lambda\in\sigma^{(2)}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
the corresponding eigenvector is of the form

x^{(2)}=\hat{a}x_{1}+\hat{c}x_{2},\quad\hat{a},\hat{c}\in\mathbf{C},

with

x_{1}=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1,j}\phi\\[1.99997pt]
a_{1,j}\lambda\phi\end{matrix}\right)\right\}^{3}_{j=1},\quad x_{2}=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}a_{2,j}\phi\\[1.99997pt]
a_{2,j}\lambda\phi\end{matrix}\right)\right\}^{3}_{j=1},

where the
a_{1,j}
,
a_{2,j}
and
\phi
are given by (
4.21
) and (
4.22
), respectively.

Remark 4.1
.

With the choice of eigenvectors
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
as in the theorem, one can verify directly that they form an orthogonal basis for the eigensubspace. Indeed, taking the inner product of
x_{1}
with
x_{2}
yields

\displaystyle\mathinner{\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum^{3}_{j=1}a_{1,j}\overline{a_{2,j}}\left(\int^{1}_{0}\,\bigl|\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(s\right)\bigr|^{2}ds+\alpha\,\bigl|\phi^{\prime}\left(0\right)\bigr|^{2}\right)+\gamma\sum^{3}_{j=1}a_{1,j}\overline{a_{2,j}}\int^{1}_{0}\,\bigl|\phi^{\prime}\left(s\right)\bigr|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum^{3}_{j=1}a_{1,j}\overline{a_{2,j}}\int^{1}_{0}\,\bigl|\lambda\phi\left(s\right)\bigr|^{2}ds

\displaystyle=\left(\int^{1}_{0}\,\bigl|\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(s\right)\bigr|^{2}ds+\alpha\,\bigl|\phi^{\prime}\left(0\right)\bigr|^{2}+\gamma\int^{1}_{0}\,\bigl|\phi^{\prime}\left(s\right)\bigr|^{2}ds+\int^{1}_{0}\,\bigl|\lambda\phi\left(s\right)\bigr|^{2}ds\right)\sum^{3}_{j=1}a_{1,j}\overline{a_{2,j}}

which in view of the choice of constants
a_{1,j}
,
a_{2,j}
as in (
4.21
) obviously implies
x_{1}\perp x_{2}
.

4.2.
Eigenvalue asymptotics

Our main task in this subsection will be to investigate, by analysis of the asymptotic zeros of the determinants
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
given by (
4.13
) and (
4.17
), respectively, how the two branches
\sigma^{(1)}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
and
\sigma^{(2)}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
of eigenvalues of
\mathcal{T}
behave asymptotically. The standard procedure for such an undertaking originated in the work of Birkhoff
[
4
,
3
]
(and was further developed by Naimark, see
[
24
]
). Take, e.g., the branch
\sigma^{(1)}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
(the branch
\sigma^{(2)}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
could be treated similarly). The procedure involves the use of asymptotic expansions of
\Delta_{1}\left(\lambda\right)
when
\left|\lambda\right|
is large and subsequent use of Rouche’s theorem, applied to an appropriately chosen comparison function (whose zeros, or at least their proper enumeration and asymptotics are known), to count the number of the zeros of
\lambda\mapsto\Delta_{1}\left(\lambda\right)
in small neighbourhoods, say disks, around the zeros of the corresponding comparison function and verify that in each such disc there is exactly one zero of
\Delta_{1}
. An advantage of this method is that it requires no closed-form computation of
\Delta_{1}
. However, when the determinants may be written down in closed form, as in the cases here, one is led to adopt a more direct approach. In this vein, we use the explicit forms (
4.13
) and (
4.17
) and then proceed to derive the asymptotic zeros by means of a direct, but involved, computation.

In the course of our analysis it will frequently be useful to write

{\mu}_{3}-{\mu}_{1}=\left(i-1\right)\rho,\quad{\mu}_{1}+{\mu}_{3}=\left(1+i\right)\rho

for the (yet to be determined) parameter
\rho
, leading to the relationships

(4.23)

\mu_{3}=i{\rho},\quad\mu^{2}_{3}=-{\rho}^{2},\quad\mu^{3}_{3}=-i{\rho}^{3},\quad\mu_{1}={\rho},\quad\mu^{2}_{1}={\rho}^{2},\quad\mu^{3}_{1}={\rho}^{3},

and note that

(4.24)

\lambda=\mu_{1}\mu_{3}=i{\rho}^{2}.

We know from Theorem
4.1
that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{T}
lie in the left half-plane and occur in symmetric pairs
\lambda
,
\overline{\lambda}
, corresponding to those eigenvalues with nonzero imaginary part. So let us consider the sector
\arg\lambda\in\left[\frac{\pi}{2},\pi\right]
and, with the substitution (
4.24
), thus consider
\arg\rho\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{4}\right]
in what follows.

4.2.1.
Asymptotic zeros of
\Delta_{1}

The following two elementary formulae will be needed:

\displaystyle\sinh\left(x+y\right)=\sinh x\cosh y+\cosh x\sinh y,

\displaystyle\cosh\left(x+y\right)=\cosh x\cosh y+\sinh x\sinh y.

Let us use these to rewrite (
4.13
) as

(4.25)

\begin{split}2\Delta_{1}\left(\lambda\right)&=(\mu^{3}_{3}-\mu^{3}_{1})\sinh\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{3}\right)+(\mu^{3}_{1}+\mu^{3}_{3})\sinh(\mu_{3}-\mu_{1})\\
&\qquad+(\mu^{2}_{3}-\mu^{2}_{1})\left(\alpha+\lambda\beta\right)\left(\cosh(\mu_{1}+\mu_{3})+\cosh(\mu_{3}-\mu_{1})\right).\end{split}

The asymptotic zeros of
\Delta_{1}
are investigated in three steps.

Step 1.
We first determine the roots of the equation

\cosh\left(1+i\right)\rho+\cosh\left(i-1\right)\rho=0.

We may equivalently write that

-1=\frac{\cosh\left(i-1\right)\rho}{\cosh\left(1+i\right)\rho},

which in the sector
\arg\rho\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{4}\right]
has the roots

\rho_{n}=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi.

So in this case we have

(4.26)

\sinh\left(i-1\right)\rho_{n}=\sinh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n},\quad\cosh\left(i-1\right)\rho_{n}=-\cosh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}.

Step 2.
Let the sequence
\left\{\lambda_{n}\right\}
represent the zeros of
\Delta_{1}
, where the
\lambda_{n}
are considered in the neighbourhoods of the
i\rho_{n}^{2}
. Setting

\tilde{\rho}_{n}=\rho_{n}+\xi_{n},

where the
\xi_{n}
will be determined, writing
\tilde{\rho}_{n}
in place of
\rho
in (
4.23
) and (
4.24
), and substituting the relevant relationships in (
4.25
) we get

\displaystyle 2\Delta_{1}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=-\tilde{\rho}^{3}_{n}\left(1+i\right)\sinh\left(1+i\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}-\tilde{\rho}^{3}_{n}\left(i-1\right)\sinh\left(i-1\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad-2\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{n}\,(\alpha+i\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{n}\beta)\left(\cosh\left(1+i\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}+\cosh\left(i-1\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}\right).

The equation
2\Delta_{1}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)=0
then becomes

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\tilde{\rho}_{n}\left(1+i\right)\sinh\left(1+i\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}-\tilde{\rho}_{n}\left(i-1\right)\sinh\left(i-1\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad-2\,(\alpha+i\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{n}\beta)\left(\cosh\left(1+i\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}+\cosh\left(i-1\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}\right)

or, equivalently,

(4.27)

-\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{n}}{2\,(\alpha+i\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{n}\beta)}=\frac{\cosh\left(1+i\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}+\cosh\left(i-1\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}}{\left(1+i\right)\sinh\left(1+i\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}+\left(i-1\right)\sinh\left(i-1\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}}.

We now use (
4.27
) to determine the asymptotics of
\xi_{n}
. For
\rho_{n}
large we can simplify our calculations below by noting that

\tanh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}=\frac{\sinh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}}{\cosh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}}=1+{O}\,(e^{-2\rho_{n}})

so

(4.28)

\tanh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}\sim 1.

We proceed now to estimate the right-hand side of (
4.27
). Consider first the numerator. A straightforward calculation using (
4.26
) and (
4.28
) shows that for large
\rho_{n}

\displaystyle\cosh\left(1+i\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}+\cosh\left(i-1\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.5021pt\sim\cosh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}\left(\cosh\left(1+i\right)\xi_{n}-\cosh\left(i-1\right)\xi_{n}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 137.9979pt\left.+\sinh\left(1+i\right)\xi_{n}+\sinh\left(i-1\right)\xi_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 34.5021pt=2e^{\xi_{n}}\sinh i\xi_{n}\cosh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}.

Likewise for the denominator in the right-hand side of (
4.27
),

\displaystyle\left(1+i\right)\sinh\left(1+i\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}+\left(i-1\right)\sinh\left(i-1\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.5021pt\sim\cosh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}\left[\left(1+i\right)\left(\sinh\left(1+i\right)\xi_{n}+\cosh\left(1+i\right)\xi_{n}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 137.9979pt\left.-\left(i-1\right)\left(\sinh\left(i-1\right)\xi_{n}-\cosh\left(i-1\right)\xi_{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 34.5021pt=2e^{\xi_{n}}\left(\sinh i\xi_{n}+i\cosh i\xi_{n}\right)\cosh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}.

So the right-hand side of (
4.27
) becomes

\frac{\cosh\left(1+i\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}+\cosh\left(i-1\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}}{\left(1+i\right)\sinh\left(1+i\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}+\left(i-1\right)\sinh\left(i-1\right)\tilde{\rho}_{n}}\sim\frac{\sinh i\xi_{n}}{\sinh i\xi_{n}+i\cosh i\xi_{n}}

from which it is clear that
\xi_{n}
is bounded for large
n
. In fact, for large
n
we can now write (
4.27
) as

(4.29)

-\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{n}}{2\,(\alpha+i\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{n}\beta)}\sim\xi_{n}.

To see how the left-hand side of (
4.29
) behaves asymptotically we observe that

\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{n}}{\alpha+i\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{n}\beta}=-\frac{i}{\tilde{\rho}_{n}\beta}\left[1+\frac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{n}^{2}}\left(\frac{i\alpha}{\beta}\right)+\frac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{n}^{4}}\left(\frac{i\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{2}+\cdots\right]=-\frac{i}{\tilde{\rho}_{n}\beta}+{O}\,(\tilde{\rho}^{-3}_{n}),

wherein

\frac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{n}}=\frac{1}{\rho_{n}+\xi_{n}}=\frac{1}{\rho_{n}}\left[1+\left(-\frac{\xi}{\rho_{n}}\right)+\left(-\frac{\xi}{\rho_{n}}\right)^{2}+\cdots\right].

We may, therefore, replace (
4.29
) by

\frac{i}{2\rho_{n}\beta}-\frac{i\xi_{n}}{2\rho_{n}^{2}\beta}+{O}\,(\rho^{-3}_{n})\sim\xi_{n}

which leads to

\displaystyle\xi_{n}\sim\frac{i\rho_{n}}{i+2\rho_{n}^{2}\beta}+{O}\,(\rho^{-3}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\rho_{n}\beta}\left[1+\frac{1}{{\rho}_{n}^{2}}\left(-\frac{i}{2\beta}\right)+\frac{1}{{\rho}_{n}^{4}}\left(-\frac{i}{2\beta}\right)^{2}+\cdots\right]+{O}\,(\rho^{-3}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\rho_{n}\beta}+{O}\,(\rho^{-3}_{n}).

Step 3.
An easy calculation, taking into account that
\lambda_{n}=i\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{n}=i\left(\rho_{n}+\xi_{n}\right)^{2}
, shows that the eigenvalues belonging to the first branch
\sigma^{(1)}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)
of the spectrum have asymptotic representations

(4.30)

\lambda_{n}=-\frac{1}{\beta}+i\rho^{2}_{n}+{O}\,(\rho^{-2}_{n}),\quad\rho_{n}=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi,

for large
n
.

4.2.2.
Asymptotic zeros of
\Delta_{2}

We turn now to the asymptotic zeros of
\Delta_{2}
. Again we proceed in three steps.

Step 1.
First the roots of the equation

(4.31)

i\sinh\rho\cosh i\rho-\cosh\rho\sinh i\rho=0

are determined. A simple manipulation using

\displaystyle 2i\sinh\rho\cosh i\rho-2\cosh\rho\sinh i\rho

\displaystyle=i\left(\sinh\left(1+i\right)\rho-\sinh\left(i-1\right)\rho\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sinh\left(1+i\right)\rho-\sinh\left(i-1\right)\rho

\displaystyle=\left(i-1\right)\sinh\left(1+i\right)\rho-\left(1+i\right)\sinh\left(i-1\right)\rho

shows that (
4.31
) is equivalent to

i=\frac{\sinh\left(i-1\right)\rho}{\sinh\left(1+i\right)\rho}=-\frac{e^{-2i\rho}-e^{-2\rho}}{1-e^{-2\left(1+i\right)\rho}}=-e^{-2i\rho}+{O}\,(e^{-2\rho}).

The roots are

\rho_{n}=\left(n+\frac{1}{4}\right)\pi+\nu_{n},

where
\nu_{n}={O}\,(e^{-2\rho_{n}})
. In this case

(4.32)

\frac{\sinh\left(i-1\right)\rho_{n}}{\sinh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}}=i,\quad\frac{\cosh\left(i-1\right)\rho_{n}}{\cosh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}}=-i\,(1+{O}\,(e^{-2\rho_{n}})).

On the other hand, we know that

(4.33)

\tanh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}=1+{O}\,(e^{-2\rho_{n}}).

In the next step we will use (
4.32
) and (
4.33
) in our calculations.

Step 2.
Let the sequence
\left\{\lambda_{n}\right\}
represent the zeros of
\Delta_{2}
. We proceed as in Step
2
of the previous subsection, setting

\tilde{\rho}_{n}=\rho_{n}+\xi_{n},

writing
\tilde{\rho}_{n}
in place of
\rho
in (
4.23
), and using the relationships in (
4.17
) to obtain

\Delta_{2}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)=-2\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{n}\sinh\tilde{\rho}_{n}\sinh i\tilde{\rho}_{n}+(\alpha+i\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{n}\beta)\left(i\tilde{\rho}_{n}\sinh\tilde{\rho}_{n}\cosh i\tilde{\rho}_{n}-\tilde{\rho}_{n}\cosh\tilde{\rho}_{n}\sinh i\tilde{\rho}_{n}\right).

The equation
2\Delta_{2}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)=0
then reads

-4\tilde{\rho}_{n}\sinh\tilde{\rho}_{n}\sinh i\tilde{\rho}_{n}+(\alpha+i\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{n}\beta)\left(2i\sinh\tilde{\rho}_{n}\cosh i\tilde{\rho}_{n}-2\cosh\tilde{\rho}_{n}\sinh i\tilde{\rho}_{n}\right)=0,

which is equivalent to

(4.34)

\frac{2\tilde{\rho}_{n}}{\alpha+i\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{n}\beta}=\frac{2i\sinh\tilde{\rho}_{n}\cosh i\tilde{\rho}_{n}-2\cosh\tilde{\rho}_{n}\sinh i\tilde{\rho}_{n}}{2\sinh\tilde{\rho}_{n}\sinh i\tilde{\rho}_{n}}.

Let us estimate the right-hand side of (
4.34
) and begin with the numerator. Using (
4.32
) and (
4.33
), we obtain after straightforward calculations

\displaystyle 2i\sinh\tilde{\rho}_{n}\cosh i\tilde{\rho}_{n}-2\cosh\tilde{\rho}_{n}\sinh i\tilde{\rho}_{n}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.5021pt=\left(i-1\right)\cosh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}\left(\cosh\left(1+i\right)\xi_{n}-\cosh\left(i-1\right)\xi_{n}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 137.9979pt\left.+\sinh\left(1+i\right)\xi_{n}+\sinh\left(i-1\right)\xi_{n}\right)+{O}\,(e^{-\rho_{n}})

\displaystyle\hskip 34.5021pt=2e^{\xi_{n}}\left(i-1\right)\sinh i\xi_{n}\cosh\left(1+i\right)\rho_{n}+{O}\,(e^{-\rho_{n}}),

Likewise, considering the denominator in the right-hand side of (
4.34
),

The results developed in this section so far, together with Theorems

and

, prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.3
.

The spectrum of
\mathcal{T}
consists of two branches of an infinite sequence of normal eigenvalues, each sequence being symmetric about the real axis and accumulating only at infinity. Asymptotically, for large
n
, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{T}
split into the two branches of eigenvalues

\sigma\left(\mathcal{T}\right)=\{\lambda^{(1)}_{\pm n}{\}}^{\infty}_{n=0}\cup\{\lambda^{(2)}_{\pm n}{\}}^{\infty}_{n=0}

which can be properly enumerated (see Remark
4.2
) such that

\operatorname{Im}\lambda^{(2)}_{0}<\operatorname{Im}\lambda^{(1)}_{0}<\operatorname{Im}\lambda^{(2)}_{1}<\operatorname{Im}\lambda^{(1)}_{1}<\cdots<\operatorname{Im}\lambda^{(2)}_{n}<\operatorname{Im}\lambda^{(1)}_{n}<\operatorname{Im}\lambda^{(2)}_{n+1}<\operatorname{Im}\lambda^{(1)}_{n+1}<\cdots

and which, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, satisfy

\lambda^{(1)}_{\pm n}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\pm i\,(\rho^{(1)}_{n}{)}^{2}+{O}\,\bigl((\rho^{(1)}_{n}{)}^{-2}\bigr),\quad\rho^{(1)}_{n}=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi,

and

\lambda^{(2)}_{\pm n}=-\frac{2}{\beta}\pm i\,(\rho^{(2)}_{n}{)}^{2}+{O}\,\bigl((\rho^{(2)}_{n}{)}^{-2}\bigr),\quad\rho^{(2)}_{n}=\left(n+\frac{1}{4}\right)\pi+\nu_{n},\quad\nu_{n}={O}\,(e^{-2n\pi}).

Remark 4.2
.

The enumeration of eigenvalues is said to be proper if (i) the algebraic multiplicities are taken into account; (ii)
{\lambda}_{-n}=\overline{\lambda_{n}}
whenever
\operatorname{Im}{\lambda}_{n}\neq 0
; (iii)
0\leq\operatorname{Im}{\lambda}_{n}\leq\operatorname{Im}{\lambda}_{n+1}
; and (iv) there are two numbers
{\lambda}_{+0}
and
{\lambda}_{-0}
. Such an enumeration gives a sequence
\left\{{\lambda}_{n}\right\}
with
\left|\operatorname{Re}\lambda_{n}\right|\leq N<\infty
, some constant
N
, and no finite accumulation points, i.e.,
\operatorname{Im}\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Remark 4.3
.

We have obtained a strengthening of Remark
3.1
that is of independent interest: the elasticity parameter
\alpha
has no effect on the eigenvalue asymptotics, as it is at most within
{O}\,(n^{-2})
. So in terms of proving exponential stability, it makes no difference what value is chosen for
\alpha
.

5.
Completeness, minimality, and Riesz basisness

Attention is now turned to the completeness and minimality properties as well as to the Riesz basisness of the eigenvectors of

As we have already indicated in the Introduction, the proofs use operator results from the literature, which we collect here as a convenience for the reader. The first, Lemma

, is actually a sharper version of
[

[

, while
the third is based – in a slightly modified form – on

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a compact skewadjoint operator on a Hilbert space
\mathbb{X}
with
\operatorname{Ker}\mathcal{K}=\left\{0\right\}
, and let
\mathcal{S}
be a real operator on
\mathbb{X}
which has finite rank. Let

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{K}+\beta\mathcal{S}

for
\beta\geq 0
. Then the root vectors of the operator
\mathcal{A}
are complete in
\mathbb{X}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a compact operator on
\mathbb{X}
and
\operatorname{Ker}\mathcal{A}=\left\{0\right\}
. Then the root vectors of
\mathcal{A}
are minimal in
\mathbb{X}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a separable Hilbert space and let
\mathcal{A}
be the infinitesimal generator of a

-semigroup
{U}\left(t\right)
on
\mathbb{X}
. Suppose that the following conditions hold:

(1)

\sigma\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=\sigma^{(1)}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\cup\sigma^{(2)}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
where the branch
\sigma^{(2)}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=\{\lambda_{k}{\}}^{\infty}_{k=1}
, consisting entirely of normal eigenvalues;

(2)

\sup_{k\geq 1}\dim E\left(\lambda_{k},\mathcal{A}\right)\mathbb{X}<\infty
, the
E\left(\lambda_{k},\mathcal{A}\right)
being the eigenprojections associated with the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
; and

(3)

there is a real constant
\kappa
such that

\sup\,\{\operatorname{Re}{\lambda}~|~\lambda\in\sigma^{(1)}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\}\leq\kappa\leq\inf\,\{\operatorname{Re}{\lambda}~|~\lambda\in\sigma^{(2)}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\},

and

(5.1)

\inf_{k\neq j}\left|\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}\right|>0.

Then the following statements hold:

(1)

There exist two
{U}\left(t\right)
-invariant closed subspaces
\mathbb{X}_{1}
and
\mathbb{X}_{2}
, in the sense that
{U}\left(t\right)\mathbb{X}_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{X}_{1}
and
{U}\left(t\right)\mathbb{X}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{X}_{2}
, with the properties

(a)

\sigma\left(\mathcal{A}|_{\mathbb{X}_{1}}\right)=\sigma^{(1)}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
and
\sigma\left(\mathcal{A}|_{\mathbb{X}_{2}}\right)=\sigma^{(2)}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
; and

(b)

\left\{E\left(\lambda_{k},\mathcal{A}\right)\mathbb{X}_{2}\right\}^{\infty}_{k=1}
forms a Riesz basis of subspaces for
\mathbb{X}_{2}
, and

\mathbb{X}=\overline{{\mathbb{X}_{1}\oplus\mathbb{X}_{2}}}.

(2)

If

, then

\bm{D}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\subset\mathbb{X}_{1}\oplus\mathbb{X}_{2}\subset\mathbb{X}.

(3)

\mathbb{X}

.

Remark 5.1

It follows from the denseness of the domain of

6.
Exponential stability

We have collected in the previous sections all the ingredients necessary for a conclusive verification of exponential stability of the
C_{0}
-semigroup
{S}\left(t\right)
or, equivalently, of the norms of solutions of (

4.1

Theorem 6.1
.

If

, then the norms of the solutions to (
