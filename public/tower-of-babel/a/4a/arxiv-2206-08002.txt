The convergent Indian buffet process
Ilsang Ohn

Abstract
We propose a new Bayesian nonparametric prior for latent feature models, which we call the convergent Indian buffet process (CIBP). We show that under the CIBP, the number of latent features is distributed as a Poisson distribution with the mean monotonically increasing but converging to a certain value as the number of objects goes to infinity. That is, the expected number of features is bounded above even when the number of objects goes to infinity, unlike the standard Indian buffet process under which the expected number of features increases with the number of objects. We provide two alternative representations of the CIBP based on a hierarchical distribution and a completely random measure, respectively, which are of independent interest. The proposed CIBP is assessed on a high-dimensional sparse factor model. Keywords: Indian buffet process, latent feature models, completely random measure, sparse factor models.

1.1
Convergent Indian buffet process

Our proposed variant of the IBP, which we call the convergent Indian buffet process (CIBP) can be described by the following restaurant analogy.

Definition 1

(The restaurant analogy of the CIBP)
.

Let
\gamma>0
,
\alpha>0
and
\kappa\geq 0
. We call the stochastic process given below the restaurant analogy of
\texttt{{CIBP}}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa)
:

1.

The first customer tries
\texttt{{Poisson}}(\gamma\mathsf{B}(\alpha+1,\kappa+1)/\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+1))
dishes, where
\mathsf{B}(a,b)
denotes the beta function with parameters
a
and
b
.

2.

For every
j=2,\dots,p
, the
j
-th customer

•

tries each previously tasted dish independently according to

\texttt{{Bernoulli}}\mathinner{\left(\frac{m_{j,k}+\alpha}{j+\kappa+\alpha}\right)}

(1.1)

where
m_{j,k}
is the number of previous customers (before
j
-th customer) who have tried the
k
-th dish;

•

and tries

\texttt{{Poisson}}\mathinner{\left(\gamma\frac{\mathsf{B}(\alpha+1,\kappa+j)}{\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+1)}\right)}

(1.2)

new dishes.

The restaurant analogy leads to the binary matrix with the number of rows being the number of costumers and the number of columns being unbounded, where the
(j,k)
-th element of the binary matrix is equal to 1 if the
j
-th customer tried the
k
-th dish and 0 otherwise. We denote by
\texttt{{CIBP}}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa)
the distribution of the binary matrix induced by the above restaurant analogy. In this note, we discuss properties and construction of the CIBP.

2.1
Distribution of the number of features

In this section, we show that the number of features (i.e., dishes) under the CIBP follows a Poisson distribution with mean being fixed as the number of objects increases. Let
K^{+}
be the number of nonzero columns of
\Xi
, which represents the number of features. Formally, we can define

K^{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=K^{+}(\bm{\Xi})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}(\bm{\xi}_{\bullet k}\neq\mathbf{0}),

where
\bm{\xi}_{\bullet k}
denotes the
k
-th column of
\bm{\Xi}
. The following proposition describes the distribution of
K^{+}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
\bm{\Xi}\sim\texttt{{CIBP}}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa)
, then

K^{+}\sim\texttt{{Poisson}}\mathinner{\left(\gamma(1-\bar{\mathsf{B}}_{\alpha,\kappa+1}^{0,p})\right)},

(2.1)

where
\bar{\mathsf{B}}_{\alpha,\kappa+1}^{0,p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+p+1)/\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+1)\leq 1.

Moreover, the Poisson mean
\gamma(1-\bar{\mathsf{B}}_{\alpha,\kappa+1}^{0,p})
monotonically increases and converges to
\gamma

as
p\to\infty
, which, in particular, implies that
K^{+}
converges to the random variable
K\sim\texttt{{Poisson}}(\gamma)
in distribution.

Proof.

From the restaurant analogy of the CIBP, we have that

K^{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{P}K^{\textup{new}}_{j},\mbox{ where }K^{\textup{new}}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{ind}}}{{\sim}}\texttt{{Poisson}}\mathinner{\left(\gamma\frac{\mathsf{B}(\alpha+1,\kappa+j)}{\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+1)}\right)}

Therefore, by the additive property of independent Poisson random variables,

K^{+}\sim\texttt{{Poisson}}\mathinner{\left(\frac{\gamma}{\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathsf{B}(\alpha+1,\kappa+j)\right)}

From the identity
\mathsf{B}(x,y)-\mathsf{B}(x,y+1)=\mathsf{B}(x+1,y)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathsf{B}(\alpha+1,\kappa+j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathinner{\left\{\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+j)-\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+j+1)\right\}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+1)-\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+p+1),

which implies
2.1
. The fact that
\bar{\mathsf{B}}_{\alpha,\kappa+1}^{0,p}\leq 1
follows from that
\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+1)-\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+p+1)\geq 0
.

For the second assertion, note that

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{B}}_{\alpha,\kappa+1}^{0,p}=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\kappa+p+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\kappa+p+1)}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\kappa+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\kappa+1)}=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\frac{\kappa+j}{\alpha+\kappa+j},

where
\Gamma(\bullet)
denotes the gamma function. Since
\alpha>0
, it follows that
\bar{\mathsf{B}}_{\alpha,\kappa+1}^{0,p}\downarrow 0
as
p\to\infty
.
∎

2.3
Connection to the two-parameter IBP

The restaurant analogy of the two-parameter IBP with parameters
\omega

\kappa

\texttt{{Poisson}}(\omega)

\texttt{{Poisson}}(\omega\kappa/(j+\kappa))

\texttt{{IBP}}(\omega,\kappa)

By comparing the restaurant analogies of
\texttt{{CIBP}}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa)
and
\texttt{{IBP}}(\omega,\kappa)
, we then have the following proposition that connects these two stochastic processes.

Proposition 2.3
.

For two
p\times\infty
-dimensional binary matrices
\bm{\Xi}\sim\texttt{{CIBP}}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa)
and
\bm{\Xi}_{0}\sim\texttt{{IBP}}(\omega,\kappa)
,
\bm{\Xi}
converges to
\bm{\Xi}_{0}
in distribution as
\alpha\to 0
and
\gamma\alpha/\kappa\to\omega
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the means of the Bernoulli distribution in
1.1
and the Poisson distribution in
1.2
converge to the corresponding quantities for
\texttt{{IBP}}(\omega,\kappa)
, which can be derived as:

\frac{m_{j,k}+\alpha}{j+\kappa+\alpha}\to\frac{m_{j,k}}{j+\kappa}

as
\alpha\to\mathbf{0}
and

\displaystyle\gamma\frac{\mathsf{B}(\alpha+1,\kappa+j)}{\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+1)}

\displaystyle=\gamma\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\kappa+j)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\kappa+j+1)}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\kappa+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\kappa+1)}

\displaystyle=\gamma\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\kappa+j}\prod_{h=1}^{j}\frac{\kappa+h}{\alpha+\kappa+h}

\displaystyle\to\frac{\omega\kappa}{j+\kappa},

as
\alpha\to 0
and
\gamma\alpha/\kappa\to\omega
.
∎

Alternative representations

In this section, we provides two alternative representations of the CIBP. The first one is based on a hierarchical distribution of Poisson, Beta and Bernoulli distributions and the second one is based on random measures. The proofs of all the results in this section are deferred to
Section

5
.

3.1
Hierarchical representation

In this section we show that the CIBP is equivalent to the following hierarchical distribution.

Definition 2

(Hierarchical representation of the CIBP)
.

Let
\gamma>0
,
\alpha>0
and
\kappa\geq 0
. We call the probability distribution given below the hierarchical representation of
\texttt{{CIBP}}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa)
:

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\sim\texttt{{Poisson}}(\gamma),

(3.1)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{iid}}}{{\sim}}\texttt{{Beta}}\mathinner{\left(\alpha,\kappa+1\right)},\>k\in[K]

\displaystyle\xi_{jk}|\theta_{k}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{ind}}}{{\sim}}\texttt{{Bernoulli}}(\theta_{k})\>j\in[p],k\in[K].

To state the result rigorously, we need a concept of
lof-equivalence
classes. Under the latent feature model, the ordering of the features does not affect the likelihood of the data. Hence, we say that two
p\times\infty
dimensional binary matrices are equivalent if they are identical up to a permutation of columns. It is convenient to choose a representative of every equivalence class by the
left-ordering procedure
. The left-ordering procedure maps each
p\times\infty
dimensional binary matrix to its left-ordered version whose columns are ordered by the score
s_{k}
, which is defined by

s_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\xi_{jk}2^{p-j}

i.e., the colums are ordered so that
s_{1}\geq s_{2}\geq\cdots.
We call the equivalence class defined by the left-ordering procedure
lof-equivalence
class and we denote the lof-equivalence class of a binary matrix
\bm{\Xi}
by
[\bm{\Xi}]
.

We introduce useful notations. Let
\Delta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{0,1\}^{p}
which is a set of
p
-dimensional binary vectors and
\Delta_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Delta\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
where
\mathbf{0}
is the vector or zero.
For each
\mathbf{u}\in\Delta_{1}
, we define

\displaystyle K_{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}(\bm{\xi}_{\bullet k}=\mathbf{u}),

(3.2)

where
\bm{\xi}_{\bullet k}
denotes the
k
-th column of
\bm{\Xi}
. In words,
K_{\mathbf{u}}
is the number of columns equal to the binary vector
\mathbf{u}
. Note that
K^{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}(\bm{\xi}_{\bullet k}\neq\mathbf{0})=\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\Delta_{1}}K_{\mathbf{u}}
. Moreover, let

m_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\xi_{jk},

be the number of rows that have the
k
-th feature.

In the next proposition, we provide the explicit form of the probability mass function of the lof-equivalence class
[\bm{\Xi}]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

If a
p\times\infty
-dimensional random binary matrix
\Xi\equiv(\xi_{jk})_{j\in[p],k\in\mathbbm{N}}
follows the distribution in
3.1
, then

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}([\Xi])=\frac{\gamma^{K^{+}}}{\prod_{\mathbf{u}\in\Delta_{1}}K_{\mathbf{u}}!}\textup{e}^{-\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bar{\mathsf{B}}_{\alpha,\kappa+1}^{1,j-1}}\mathinner{\left[\prod_{k=1}^{K^{+}}\bar{\mathsf{B}}_{\alpha,\kappa+1}^{m_{k},p-m_{k}}\right]}.

(3.3)

From
Proposition

3.1
, we can show that the restaurant analogy and the hierarchical representation of the CIBP are equivalent.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose that a
p\times\infty
-dimensional binary matrix
\bm{\Xi}
follow the hierarchical distribution presented in
3.1
. Then the lof-equivalence class
[\Xi]
follows
\texttt{{CIBP}}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa)
.

3.2
Random measure representation

In this section, we provide another representation of the CIBP, which is based on random measures.

We first briefly review completely random measures. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
a Polish space with its Borel
\sigma
-field and let
(\mathfrak{M},\mathcal{M})
be a set of all measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
with its Borel
\sigma
-field. A completely random measure (CRM)
\mu
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
is a random measure such that
\mu(A_{1}),\dots,\mu(A_{k})
for all disjoint measurable sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
are mutually independent. Every CRM can be decomposed into three independent parts:

\displaystyle\mu=\mu_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\delta_{\omega_{k}}+\sum_{(q,\omega)\in\Phi}q\delta_{\omega}

where
\mu_{0}
is a non-random measure,
(\omega_{k})_{k\in[K]}
are fixed atoms in
\Omega
,
(q_{k})_{k\in[K]}
are independent random variables on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\Phi
is a Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega
. Here we only consider purely-atomic CRMs such that
\mu_{0}=0
. We write

\mu\sim\texttt{{CRM}}\mathinner{\left(\Lambda,(\omega_{k},P_{k})_{k\in[K]}\right)}

if
\mu
is the purely-atomic CRM represented by
\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\delta_{\omega_{k}}+\sum_{(q,\omega)\in\Phi}q\delta_{\omega}
with
q_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{ind}}}{{\sim}}P_{k}
for
k\in[K]
and
\mathsf{E}\Phi=\Lambda
for some probability measures
(P_{k})_{k\in[K]}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\Lambda
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega
. In particular, we write
\mu\sim\texttt{{CRM}}\mathinner{\left(\Lambda\right)}
if
\mu=\sum_{(q,\omega)\in\Phi}q\delta_{\omega}
with
\mathsf{E}\Phi=\Lambda
.

It is well known that the two-parameter IBP,
\texttt{{IBP}}(\alpha,\kappa+1)
with
\alpha>0
and
\kappa\geq 0
, has the following random measure representation:

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}_{j\bullet}|\mu

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{iid}}}{{\sim}}\texttt{{BeP}}(\mu),\>j\in[p]

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\sim\texttt{{BP}}(\kappa+1,\alpha\Lambda_{0})

for some smooth probability measure
\Lambda_{0}
, i.e.,
\Lambda_{0}(\Omega)=1
. Here,
\texttt{{BeP}}(\mu)
denotes the Bernoulli process with mean
\mu
, which is equivalent to
\texttt{{CRM}}(\Lambda_{\texttt{{BeP}}(\mu)})
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
with

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\texttt{{BeP}}(\mu)}(\textup{d}q,\textup{d}\omega)=\delta_{1}(\textup{d}q)\mu(\textup{d}\omega),

where
\delta_{1}
denotes a point mass at 1, and
\texttt{{BP}}(\kappa+1,\alpha\Lambda_{0})
denotes the Beta process which is equivalent to
\texttt{{CRM}}(\Lambda_{\textup{BP}(\theta,\gamma\Lambda_{0})})
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
with

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\texttt{{BP}}(\kappa+1,\alpha\Lambda_{0})}(\textup{d}q,\textup{d}\omega)=\alpha(\kappa+1)q^{-1}(1-q)^{\kappa}\textup{d}q\Lambda_{0}(\textup{d}\omega).

We introduce another stochastic process represented by a random measure, which will be shown to be related to the CIBP.

Definition 3

(Random measure representation of the CIBP)
.

Let
\gamma>0
,
\alpha>0
and
\kappa\geq 0
. We call the stochastic process given below the random measure representation of
\texttt{{CIBP}}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa)
:

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}_{j\bullet}|\mu

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{iid}}}{{\sim}}\texttt{{BeP}}(\mu),\>j\in[p]

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\sim\texttt{{CRM}}(\Lambda_{\texttt{{CIBP}}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa)})

with

\Lambda_{\texttt{{CIBP}}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa)}(\textup{d}q,\textup{d}\omega)=\frac{\gamma}{\mathsf{B}(\alpha,\kappa+1)}q^{\alpha-1}(1-q)^{\kappa}\textup{d}q\Lambda_{0}(\textup{d}\omega)

(3.5)

for some smooth probability measure
\Lambda_{0}
.

The next theorem shows that the hierarchical representation in
Definition

2
and random measure representation in
Definition

3
of the CIBP are equivalent.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\bm{\xi}_{1\bullet},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{p\bullet}
be random measures following the distribution given in
3.4
. Then the joint distribution of
\bm{\xi}_{1\bullet},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{p\bullet}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}(\bm{\xi}_{1\bullet},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{p\bullet})=\textup{e}^{-\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bar{\mathsf{B}}_{\alpha,\kappa+1}^{1,j-1}}\mathinner{\left[\prod_{k=1}^{K^{+}}\bar{\mathsf{B}}_{\alpha,\kappa+1}^{m_{k},p-m_{k}}\lambda_{0}(\omega_{k}^{*})\right]},

(3.6)

where there are
K^{+}
atoms
\omega_{1}^{*},\cdots,\omega_{K^{+}}^{*}
such that
m_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{\xi}_{j\bullet}(\omega_{k}^{*})\geq 1
for
k\in[K^{+}]
, and
\lambda_{0}
denotes the density of
\Lambda_{0}
.

The function
q\mapsto q^{\alpha-1}(1-q)^{\kappa}
is integrable on
[0,1]
, which means that there would be a finite number of features.

4
Application to Bayesian sparse factor models

In this section, we consider an application of the CIBP prior distribution to Bayesian estimation of the factor model.

4.1
Model and prior

We consider the following factor model where a
p
-dimensional random vector
\mathbf{Y}
is distributed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{z}\sim\texttt{{N}}_{p}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}),\quad\mathbf{Z}\sim\texttt{{N}}_{K}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),

(4.1)

with
K<p
,
\mathbf{B}
being a
p\times K
factor loading matrix,
\mathbf{Z}
a
K
-dimensional factor and
\sigma^{2}>0
a noise variance.

We consider the following prior on the loading matrix
\mathbf{B}
. Let
\beta_{jk}
be the
(j,k)
-th entry of the
p\times\infty
-dimensional loading matrix
\mathbf{B}
. We impose the prior distribution based on the CIBP distribution such that

\displaystyle\beta_{jk}|\xi_{jk}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{ind}}}{{\sim}}

\displaystyle(1-\xi_{jk})\delta_{0}+\xi_{jk}\texttt{{N}}(0,\tau),\>j\in[p],\>k\in[K]

\displaystyle\xi_{jk}|\theta_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{ind}}}{{\sim}}

\displaystyle\texttt{{Bernoulli}}\mathinner{\left(\theta_{k}\right)},\>j\in[p],\>k\in[K]

\displaystyle\theta_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{iid}}}{{\sim}}

\displaystyle\texttt{{Beta}}(\alpha,\kappa+1),\>k\in[K]

\displaystyle K\sim

\displaystyle\texttt{{Poisson}}(\gamma)

where
\kappa\geq 0
and
\tau>0
. That is, we impose
\texttt{{CIBP}}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa)
on the binary matrix
\bm{\Xi}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\xi_{jk})_{j\in[p],k\in\mathbbm{N}}
. We refer to the above distribution on
\mathbf{B}
as
\mathsf{SSCIBP}_{p}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa,\tau)
, which is an abbreviation of
spike and slab CIBP
.

4.2
Posterior computation

We provide an Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm for sampling from the posterior distribution under the
\mathsf{SSCIBP}_{p}(\gamma,\alpha,\kappa,\tau)
prior on
\mathbf{B}
and inverse Gamma prior
\texttt{{IG}}(a,b)
on
\sigma^{2}
. Let
K^{+}
be the number of nonzero columns of the loading matrix
\mathbf{B}
. The MCMC algorithm is as follows:

Sample
\beta_{jk}
for
j\in[p]
and
k\in[K^{+}]
.

The factor loading
\beta_{jk}
is sampled from the conditional posterior

\beta_{jk}|-\sim\begin{cases}\texttt{{N}}(\widehat{\beta}_{jk},\widehat{\tau}_{k})&\mbox{if }\xi_{jk}=1\\
\delta_{0}&\mbox{if }\xi_{jk}=0,\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{k}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathinner{\left(\sigma^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{ik}^{2}+\tau^{-1}\right)}^{-1}

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{jk}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\widehat{\tau}_{k}\mathinner{\left\{\sigma^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{ik}\mathinner{\left(Y_{ij}-\sum_{h\in[K^{+}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}h\neq k}Z_{ih}\beta_{jh}\right)}\right\}}.

Sample
\xi_{jk}
for
j\in[p]
and
k\in\mathbbm{N}
.

When we sample
(\xi_{jk}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k\in\mathbbm{N})
, we use the fact that the CIBP is exchangeable to assume that the
j
-th customer is the last customer to enter the restaurant. Therefore, for each
k\in[K^{*}]
,
\xi_{jk}
is sampled with probability

\frac{\Pi(\xi_{jk}=1|-)}{\Pi(\xi_{jk}=0|-)}=\frac{m_{j,k}+\alpha}{\kappa+p-m_{j,k}}\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{k}}{\tau}}\exp\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{2\widehat{\tau}_{k}}\widehat{\beta}_{jk}^{2}\right)},

where
m_{j,k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{l\in[p]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}l\neq j}\xi_{lk}
. We then sample
\xi_{jk}
for each of the infinitely many all-zero columns. To do this, we use the Metropolis–Hastings (MH) steps as follows. We propose
K_{j}^{*}\in\mathbbm{N}\cup\{0\}
and

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed the CIBP, a new three-parameter generalization of the IBP. The most notable property of the CIBP compared with the standard IBPs is that the distribution of the number of features converges to a fixed distribution as the number of objects grows. Due to this property, the CIBP prevents features that are unnecessary in interpretation and/or prediction. The proposed CIBP is assessed on a high-dimensional sparse factor model. We provided empirical evidence showing that the CIBP is better than the IBP in estimation of the number of factors.
