Inverse modeling of time-delayed interactions via the dynamic-entropy formalism Preprint: APS/123-QED
Elena Agliari, Francesco Alemanno, Adriano Barra, Michele Castellana, Daniele Lotito, Matthieu Piel

Abstract
Although instantaneous interactions are unphysical, a large variety of maximum entropy statistical inference methods match the model-inferred and the empirically-measured equal-time correlation functions. Focusing on collective motion of active units, this constraint is reasonable when the interaction timescale is much faster than that of the interacting units, as in starling flocks, yet it fails in a number of counter examples, as in leukocyte coordination (where signalling proteins diffuse among two cells). Here, we relax this assumption and develop a path integral approach to maximum-entropy framework, which includes delay in signalling. Our method is able to infer the strength of couplings and fields, but also the time required by the couplings to completely transfer information among the units. We demonstrate the validity of our approach providing excellent results on synthetic datasets of non-Markovian trajectories generated by the Heisenberg-Kuramoto and Vicsek models equipped with delayed interactions. As a proof of concept, we also apply the method to experiments on dendritic migration, where matching equal-time correlations results in a significant information loss.

The ability to move is a fundamental property of many living systems, ranging on different scales, from animals to cells
Locomotion2018
. As for the latter, advances in live imaging allowed scientists to collect big data for which statistical analysis has now become a robust and reliable tool
Meijering-2012
. These investigations suggest that cells often migrate in groups and communicate as they move. Establishing whether such interactions are present is, in many cases, of utmost importance to possibly control and anticipate the evolution of a system.
However, answering this question is notoriously difficult, especially when unit-to-unit communication is not supported by the existence of physical bonds, as in
juxtacrine interactions, but it is rather induced by some signaling pathways,
as in paracrine interactions.
To inspect these possible interactions, several methods have been introduced in the past few decades. Among these, inferential techniques have been designed to take as input some tracks of moving units, such as birds in flocks or migrating cells, and output the parameters which provide information about the motion and the existence of interactions among the units, and/or between the units and an external source, such a predator for a bird flock
Procaccini2011
, or a cancer cell producing signalling proteins for leukocytes
Alemanno2023
.

In particular, approaches based on the maximum entropy (ME) principle
jaynes1957information
have been proven successful in a broad variety of contexts, revealing interaction patterns resulting from amino-acid sequences in protein families
seno2008maximum
, interaction structures of genetic networks
lezon2006using
, effective interactions in networks of neurons
bialek2012statistical
, etc. The state of the art of this inverse modeling still exhibits some limitations which make it unsatisfactory at work on units coordination for collective motion.
Specifically, this approach usually forces the theoretical and experimental equal-time correlation functions to match, implicitly assuming instantaneous interactions among units, however, this assumption may not hold when the interaction is mediated by some chemicals which take some time to diffuse from the emitter to the receiver. For example, in cell-migration experiments, cell
i
may locally release a chemical compound along its migratory path, and cell
j
may cross the former path of
i
at a later time and thus feel a delayed interaction with
i
mediated by this compound. In this work, we introduce, develop and test a ME framework that allows unveiling not only time-lagged effective interactions but also, and more generally, interactions that arise indirectly as cell
i
alters the environment of cell
j
.

Let us consider the motion of
N
particles (i.e. units) in a
D
-dimensional space, and denote their trajectories by

\left\{\bm{x}_{i}^{t}\right\},\,~~1\leq i\leq N,~~~1\leq t\leq N_{\rm T}+1,

(1)

where
\bm{x}_{i}^{t}
is the
D
-dimensional position vector of particle
i
at time
t
, and the superscript index indicates instants of time separated by
\delta t
, for a total of
N_{\rm T}+1
temporal samples.
The velocity
\bm{v}_{i}^{t}
and direction of motion

of each particle

then read, respectively,

\bm{v}_{i}^{t}\equiv\frac{\bm{x}_{i}^{t+1}-\bm{x}_{i}^{t}}{\delta t},\quad\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\equiv\frac{\bm{v}_{i}^{t}}{\left|\bm{v}_{i}^{t}\right|}\ \text{and}\ \bm{m}_{t}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{i}^{t}

is the instantaneous mean alignment of the moving units at time

is interpreted as a configuration (at time

For practical purposes, we assume that the motion of these units may be temporally correlated up to a maximum temporal window of length

The two natural observables we use to quantify this temporal correlation are the temporally averaged magnetization
\bm{M}

\bm{M}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\bm{m}_{t},

(4)

We seek
P(\bm{s})
as the minimal probability measure such that the theoretical averages for the magnetization (
3
) and correlation function (
4
) match their empirical values and, accordingly, we obtain it following the ME principle

where

is the surface

To solve this optimization task, we introduce the Lagrangian multipliers
\bm{J}\equiv\{J_{\tau}\}_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}

Remarkably,

can be looked at as a Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution and, consistently, the argument of its exponential can be interpreted as a Hamiltonian. Thus, the Lagrange multipliers

Having obtained an analytical expression for

Before proceeding we note that, in the following, the theoretical average over

As averaging over the measure

The resulting distribution is referred to as

Denoting by

the related average, we get

\displaystyle\langle\bm{m}_{t}\rangle_{\rm A}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\prod_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\right)P_{\rm A}[\bm{s}]\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{i}^{t}=

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\bm{\mathcal{M}}\left(\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H}\right)&\textrm{ if }\tau_{\rm M}\leq t\leq N_{\rm T},\\

where

(9)

and

are hyperbolic Bessel functions of order

and

Next, we notice that

is factorized with respect to every variable

\langle\bm{m}_{t}\bm{m}_{t^{\prime}}\rangle_{\rm A}=\langle\bm{m}_{t}\rangle_{\rm A}\langle\bm{m}_{t^{\prime}}\rangle_{\rm A}
.
This allows us to recast the constraints in the ME extremization as

\displaystyle\bm{M}^{\rm E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\langle\bm{m}_{t}\rangle_{\rm A},

(10)

\displaystyle R^{\rm E}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\langle\bm{m}_{t}\rangle_{\rm A}\cdot\langle\bm{m}_{t-\tau}\rangle_{\rm A},~~1\leq\tau\leq\tau_{\rm M}.

(11)

The non-linearities in
\bm{J}
and
\bm{H}
introduced by the operator
\bm{\mathcal{M}}
make the direct evaluation of these parameters from Eqs. (
10
)-(
11
) non-feasible. To circumvent this difficulty, we can note that these equations are simultaneously fulfilled by requiring that

\langle\bm{m}_{t}\rangle_{\rm A}=\bm{m}_{t}^{\rm E},\qquad\tau_{\rm M}+1\leq t\leq N_{\rm T},

(12)

which, exploiting

formalism
, becomes

\bm{\mathcal{M}}\left(\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H}\right)=\bm{m}_{t}^{\rm E},\qquad\tau_{\rm M}+1\leq t\leq N_{\rm T},

(13)

and, finally, by introducing the inverse function
\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\equiv\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\left(\bm{m}_{t}^{\rm E}\right)
we can shift the non-linearity on the experimentally-determined term and get

\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H}=\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E},\qquad\tau_{\rm M}+1\leq t\leq N_{\rm T}.

(14)

Hence, the solution of the original integral over the past history of all the particles (
5
) is effectively approximated in terms of the solution of the linear system (
14
).

We stress that
Eq.

12
constitutes a stricter condition than Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
), because it stems from approximation (
7
) that removed degrees of freedom. To avoid that (
14
) is overconstrained, we include an additional and controllable term in it, i.e.,

\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H}+\sigma\bm{\epsilon}_{t}=\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}
, where
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0}_{D},\bm{1}_{D}\right)
and
\sigma\geq 0
is a further parameter to be determined: we have modified the constraint in such a way that the relation (
14
) does not hold deterministically. Thus,
the related log-likelihood

reads

\displaystyle l_{\rm A}(\bm{J},\bm{H},\sigma|\bm{s}^{\rm E})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\left|\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H}-\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\right|^{2}

(15)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}\right)\frac{D}{2}\log\left(2\pi\sigma^{2}\right).

We now obtain the optimal estimates for
\bm{J},\bm{H}
and
\sigma
. Setting
\nabla_{\bm{H}}l_{\rm A}=0
, we obtain

\bm{H}=\langle\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\rangle_{t}-\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}\right\rangle_{t},

where

denotes the time average, and, proceeding analogously for

, we get

\bm{J}=\bm{A}^{-1}\cdot\bm{B},

(17)

with

\displaystyle A_{\lambda\tau}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}\cdot\bm{m}_{t-\lambda}^{\rm E}\right\rangle_{t}-\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}\right\rangle_{t}\cdot\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\lambda}^{\rm E}\right\rangle_{t},

\displaystyle B_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\cdot\bm{m}_{t-\lambda}^{\rm E}\rangle_{t}-\langle\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\rangle_{t}\cdot\langle\bm{m}_{t-\lambda}^{\rm E}\rangle_{t}.

Finally, setting

, we obtain

\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{D}\left\langle\left|\bm{H}+\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}-\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\right|^{2}\right\rangle_{t}.

As detailed in the Appendix, an estimate of the errors for the inferred parameters can be obtained by using the Fisher information for the log-likelihood

\textrm{Var}\left(\bm{J},\bm{H},\sigma\right)\approx\textrm{diag}\left(\bm{I}^{-1}\right),

So far in our analysis, we assumed that
\tau_{\rm M}
is known, yet in most practical cases it is not. To estimate
\tau_{\rm M}
, we rely on the AIC

, which provides a tool for model selection, each model being characterised by a different value of
\tau_{\rm M}
. In general, given a dataset and a model

parameters and

observations, the quantity

\textrm{AIC}\equiv\frac{2n_{\rm p}}{n_{\rm o}}+\log\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)

(22)

assesses the quality of such a model for the data available. In fact, the AIC favors models with a small error
and
a small number of parameters.
In the analysis above, the parameters in
Eq.

22
are

n_{\rm o}=D\left(N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}\right),~~~~n_{\rm p}=\tau_{\rm M}+D+1,~~~~\epsilon^{2}=\frac{N_{\rm T}}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\sigma^{2},

thus

\textrm{AIC}=2\frac{\tau_{\rm M}+D+1}{D\left(N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}\right)}+\log\frac{N_{\rm T}~\sigma^{2}}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}.

(23)

In the following, after performing inference for multiple values of
\tau_{\rm M}

and determining

from

, we evaluate

23
for each inferred

\tau_{M}
) and we choose the effective

as that with the smallest value of

is then interpreted as the best inferred coupling.

c. A further last control test on this inference can be achieved simply by reshuffling the frames and feeding the ME method with this random permutation: in this case the coupling should disappear yet the field should be preserved and, indeed, this is the case, as shown in the Appendix (see Fig.
9
).

To summarize, we revised the standard ME method to encompass scenarios where communication among units is slow: the dependence over the whole history of any particle has to be considered and the emerging dynamics does not have to be any longer Markovian. While from a gnoseological perspective such general setting should always be preferable to versions where equal-time correlators are constrained, when communication among units is reasonably faster than the fastest timescale of their dynamics, standard ME and our method return the same outcome. Whatever the route, in a nutshell, we use the knowledge stemming from empirical correlations to construct an effective Hamiltonian model that correctly reproduces the observed motion (e.g. its statistical characterstics, at some prescribed order, depending on how much empirical information we relied upon and thus how many Lagrange multipliers we used). The interactions that we infer via maximum entropy should however be seen as effective interactions as, in principle, we do not know the model that generated the dynamics. For instance, when analysing trajectories generated by the Vicsek model, the details of the generating model do not enter in our analysis, nevertheless the inferred values of these interactions -despite being
effective
- carry valuable information about the motion (furthermore underlying model may not even exist, and in general it is now known).

First, we present our approach to generalize the maximum entropy (ME) method, standardly based on matching equal-time correlation, in order to cope with delay in signalling, then we validate our method on synthetic and real datasets. As for the synthetic ones, we focus on trajectories generated by the Heisenberg-Kuramoto model (HKM)
Alemanno2023

II
Dynamic maximum-entropy formalism

II.1
Problem formulation

Let us consider the motion of
N
particles in a
D
-dimensional space, and denote by

\left\{\bm{x}_{i}^{t}\right\},\,~~1\leq i\leq N,~~~1\leq t\leq N_{\rm T}+1

(28)

the set of trajectories, where
\bm{x}_{i}^{t}
is the position of the particle
i
at time
t
and the superscript index indicates instants of time separated by
\delta t
, for a total of
N_{\rm T}+1
temporal samples.
By using these collected positions
\bm{x}_{i}^{t}
, we can evaluate
the velocity
\bm{v}
and direction of motion
\bm{s}
of each particle
i
at time
t
:

\bm{v}_{i}^{t}\equiv\frac{\bm{x}_{i}^{t+1}-\bm{x}_{i}^{t}}{\delta t},\quad\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\equiv\frac{\bm{v}_{i}^{t}}{\left|\bm{v}_{i}^{t}\right|},\quad\,1\leq i\leq N,~~~1\leq t\leq N_{\rm T}.

(29)

Focusing on the collection of variables
\bm{s}\equiv\{\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\},\,1\leq i\leq N,\,1\leq t\leq N_{\rm T}
, a natural framework to determine a probability measure for these units is the path-integral formalism
agliari2020a
;
cavagna2014dynamical
: we
define the path entropy
S
as

S\left[P\right]=-\int\mathcal{D}\bm{s}P(\bm{s})\log P(\bm{s})

(30)

where

\mathcal{D}\bm{s}\equiv\prod_{t=1}^{N_{\rm T}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\bm{s}_{i}^{t}

is the integration measure and each
d\bm{s}_{i}^{t}
is a surface
element of the
(D-1)
-dimensional sphere.
The average alignment of the moving units at time
t
is given by

\bm{m}(\bm{s}^{t})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{i}^{t},

(31)

where

\bm{s}^{t}\equiv\{\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\},~1\leq i\leq N.

(32)

The latter can be interpreted as a configuration of soft spins that evolve in time, thus, we will refer to
\bm{m}(\bm{s}^{t})
as the magnetization at time
t
.
Furthermore, to quantify the direction along which the units move on average, we introduce the temporally averaged magnetization
\bm{M}
:

\bm{M}(\bm{s})\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\bm{m}(\bm{s}^{t}).

(33)

In our analysis, we assume that the motion of the units above can be temporally correlated up to a maximum temporal window whose length is
\tau_{\rm M}
with
0\leq\tau_{\rm M}<N_{\rm T}
. A natural observable to quantify this persistence is the two-point correlation function
R
with delay
\tau

\small R\left(\tau,\bm{s}\right)\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\,\bm{m}\left(\bm{s}^{t}\right)\cdot\bm{m}\left(\bm{s}^{t-\tau}\right),~~1\leq\tau\leq\tau_{\rm M}.

(34)

For the moment we suppose that the correct value for
\tau_{\rm M}
is known a priori; in the following sections, after having developed the necessary tools, we also provide a recipe for selecting
\tau_{\rm M}
in a self-consistent manner for any dataset.
Notice that the expression in (
34
) implements a “mean-field” correlation as the direction of each unit
i
is related to the direction of any other unit up to
\tau_{\rm M}
time steps before, regardless of their spatial distance.

Following the ME principle
jaynes1957information
, we seek
P
as the minimal probability measure whose average magnetization (
33
) and two-point correlation function (
34
) match their respective empirical values:

\displaystyle\max_{P}S[P]

(35)

subject to

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}\bm{s}P(\bm{s})\bm{M}(\bm{s})=\bm{M}^{\rm E},

(36)

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}\bm{s}P(\bm{s})R\left(\tau,\bm{s}\right)=R^{\rm E}(\tau),~1\leq\tau\leq\tau_{\rm M},

(37)

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}\bm{s}P(\bm{s})=1,

(38)

where
\bm{M}^{\rm E}
and
R^{\rm E}
are the empirical values of
the average magnetization and two-point correlation, respectively.

To solve
Eq.

35
, we use the method of Lagrangian
multipliers. The Lagrangian
S^{\star}
reads

\begin{split}S^{\star}\left[P,J,\bm{H}\right]=&-\frac{1}{N(N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M})}\int\mathcal{D}\bm{s}P[\bm{s}]\log P[\bm{s}]+\\
&+\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\left(\int\mathcal{D}\bm{s}P[\bm{s}]R\left(\tau,\bm{s}\right)-R^{\rm E}(\tau)\right)+\\
&+\bm{H}\cdot\left(\int\mathcal{D}\bm{s}P(\bm{s})\bm{M}(\bm{s})-\bm{M}^{\rm E}\right)+\\
&+\frac{\zeta}{N(N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M})}\left(\int\mathcal{D}\bm{s}P[\bm{s}]-1\right).\end{split}

(39)

where
\bm{J}\equiv\{J_{\tau}\}_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}},\bm{H}\equiv\{H_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{D},\zeta
play as Lagrangian multipliers and the normalization factor
\frac{1}{N(N_{T}-\tau_{M})}
ensures that
S^{\star}
is an intensive quantity with respect to
N
and
N_{T}
, and that the final expression for
P(\bm{s})
is well defined (vide infra).
The Lagrangian can be easily extremized with respect to
P
:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta S^{\star}}{\delta P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N(N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M})}(\log P(\bm{s})+1-\zeta)

(40)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}R\left(\tau,\bm{s}\right)+\bm{H}\cdot\bm{M}(\bm{s})=0

yielding

\small P(\bm{s})=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left\{N(N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M})\left[\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}R\left(\tau,\bm{s}\right)+\bm{H}\cdot\bm{M}(\bm{s})\right]\right\},

(41)

where the Lagrangian multipliers
\bm{J},\bm{H}
are determined implicitly by
Eqs.

36
,
37
and
38
, and
Z\equiv\exp\{(1-\zeta)/[N(N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M})]\}
.
By substituting
Eqs.

33
and
34
into
Eq.

41
,
we get

\small P(\bm{s})=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left[N\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\bm{m}\left(\bm{s}^{t}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\,\bm{m}\left(\bm{s}^{t-\tau}\right)+\bm{H}\right)\right].

(42)

Finally, by plugging
Eq.

31
into
Eq.

42
, we obtain

\small P(\bm{s})=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left[\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{j}^{t-\tau}+\bm{H}\right)\right],

(43)

where the normalization constant
Z
reads

\small Z=\int\mathcal{D}\bm{s}\exp\left[\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{j}^{t-\tau}+\bm{H}\right)\right].

(44)

We emphasize that the resulting
P(\bm{s})
can be interpreted as a Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution whose exponential defines an effective energy function that, consistently with thermodynamic principles, scales linearly with the number of degrees of freedom
N
and
N_{T}
. More generally, the structure of
P(\bm{s})
suggests that the direction of the
i
th particle at time
t

tends to be aligned with the direction given by
\bm{H}
– which plays as an external field – and that, if
J_{\tau}>0
(resp.
J_{\tau}<0
), the direction of the
i
th particle at time
t
tends to be aligned (resp. misaligned) with the average unit’s direction at time
(t-\tau)
– which plays as an internal field.

II.2
Solution method

In this section we present our strategy to obtain an estimate for the parameters
\bm{J}
and
\bm{H}
, starting from a sample of experimental data. We first recast the problem into a maximum likelihood setting, which allows us to implement a simplification in the expression for
P(\bm{s})
, yielding to an approximated expression denoted as
P_{A}(\bm{s})
. This approximation is made necessary by the prohibitive difficulty of averaging over the probability measure (
43
), since it involves handling simultaneously all the correlated variables over their past history, as standard in path-integral formulations. In fact, the idea is to replace the mean expectation of the internal field produced by the peer units and appearing in the exponent of the Boltzmann-Gibbs representation (
43
), with its empirical evaluation. As we will show, this makes the measure
P_{A}(\bm{s})
factorized in such a way that we can recast the Lagrangian constraints in an approximated linear system that is feasible for a straightforward evaluation of the parameters
\bm{J}
and
\bm{H}
.

Let us implement the plan. Suppose that we experimentally observe a system of
N
particles at regular time
intervals for a total of
N_{\rm T}+1
timepoints, then, according
to
Eq.

29
, we can evaluate the velocity
\bm{v}
and the direction
\bm{s}
for each particle and time point, overall collecting
the dataset:

\bm{s}^{\rm E}=\{\bm{s}_{i}^{{\rm E},t}\,|1\leq i\leq N,\,\,1\leq t\leq N_{\rm T}\},

(45)

hence, we can calculate

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{t}^{E}\equiv\bm{m}^{\rm E}(\bm{s}^{{\rm E},t})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{i}^{{\rm E},t},

(46)

\displaystyle\bm{M}^{E}\equiv\bm{M}^{\rm E}(\bm{s}^{\rm E})=\frac{1}{N_{T}-\tau_{M}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\bm{m}^{\rm E}(\bm{s}^{{\rm E},t}),

(47)

\displaystyle R^{E}(\tau)=\frac{1}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{M}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\bm{m}^{\rm E}(\bm{s}^{{\rm E},t})\cdot\bm{m}^{\rm E}(\bm{s}^{{\rm E},t-\tau}),

(48)

where the superscript E highlights that the quantity is evaluated by experimental data and in the last definition we wrote
R^{E}(\tau)\equiv R^{\rm E}(\tau,\bm{s}^{\rm E})
to ligthen notation.
In order to estimate the parameters
\bm{J}
and
\bm{H}
appearing in
Eq.

43
,
and thus build a model for the dataset
\bm{s}^{\rm E}
, we can use the
method of maximum likelihood
Cox2011
. The log-likelihood
l
of the model (
43
) given the dataset
\bm{s}^{\rm E}
is

\displaystyle l(\bm{J},\bm{H}|\bm{s}^{\rm E})\equiv\log P(\bm{s}^{\rm E}|\bm{J},\bm{H})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{i}^{{\rm E},t}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{j}^{E,t-\tau}+\bm{H}\right)

(49)

\displaystyle-\log\int\mathcal{D}\bm{s}e^{[\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\cdot(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{j}^{t-\tau}+\bm{H})]}.

The values of
\bm{J}
and
\bm{H}
that maximize
l(\bm{J},\bm{H}|\bm{s}^{\rm E})
represent the maximum likelihood estimate for the parameters. We therefore derive
l(\bm{J},\bm{H}|\bm{s}^{\rm E})
in
Eq.

49
and solve for

\frac{\partial l}{\partial\bm{J}}=\bm{0},\ \ \frac{\partial l}{\partial\bm{H}}=\bm{0}.

(50)

However, such a direct approach requires the evaluation of high-dimensional multi-variable integrals, like the one appearing in the last term of
Eq.

49
, which entail several numerical issues as for the stability of the solution and the computational time (e.g., in our experimental datasets the number of correlated variables, for which we have to perform integrals, is
\mathcal{O}({10}^{5})
, being
N_{\rm T}=150
and
N=300
).
Thus, we resort to an approximation: First, in
Eq.

43
we replace the internal field, acting on
\bm{s}_{i}^{t}
, with its empirical counterpart

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{j}^{t-\tau}\rightarrow\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E},

(51)

along the lines of the mean-field approximation in statistical mechanics.

With the substitution (
51
), pairs like
\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\bm{s}_{j}^{t-\tau}
no longer appear in (
49
), that is, in a statistical-mechanics jargon, the original two-body model has been recast into a one-body model. Also, with this passage, the dependence displayed by the probability distribution on the first tranche of trajectories, i.e.,
\bm{s}_{i}^{t},\;t=1,\cdots,\tau_{\rm M}
is lost, but it can be restored, at least formally, by introducing some constraints that set the missing variables to their experimental values, in such a way that
P_{\rm A}(\bm{s})
remains defined over the whole set of variables:

\displaystyle P(\bm{s})\rightarrow P_{\rm A}(\bm{s})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\rm A}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\prod_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}e^{[\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\cdot(\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H})]}\right\}

(52)

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{t=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}\delta\left(\bm{s}_{i}^{t}-\bm{s}_{i}^{{\rm E},t}\right)\right],

with

\small Z_{\rm A}\equiv\int\left(\prod_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\right)\exp\left[\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H}\right)\right],

(53)

where the subscript
\rm A
stands for
approximated
.
In this way, we are still able to evaluate all the momenta of the variables
\bm{s}
, including those involving
\bm{s}_{i}^{t},\;t=1,\cdots,\tau_{\rm M}
.

We denote by
\langle\rangle_{\rm A}
the average performed
with respect to
P_{\rm A}
, i.e., for the generic observable
F(\bm{s})
,

\langle F(\bm{s})\rangle_{\rm A}\equiv\int\mathcal{D}\bm{s}P_{\rm A}[\bm{s}]F(\bm{s}),

(54)

and note that

\displaystyle\langle\bm{m}_{t}\rangle_{\rm A}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\prod_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\right)P_{\rm A}(\bm{s})\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{s}_{i}^{t}\right)=

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\bm{\mathcal{M}}\left(\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H}\right)&\tau_{\rm M}\leq t\leq N_{\rm T}\\
\bm{m}_{t}^{\rm E}&1\leq t\leq\tau_{\rm M}\end{cases}

(55)

where the vector function
\bm{\mathcal{M}}
is defined as

\bm{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{x})\equiv\frac{\bm{x}}{\left|\bm{x}\right|}\frac{\mathcal{I}_{1}\left(\left|\bm{x}\right|\right)}{\mathcal{I}_{0}\left(\left|\bm{x}\right|\right)}

(56)

and
\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{I}_{1}
are hyperbolic Bessel functions (also known as modified Bessel function of the first kind) of order
0
and
1
, respectively
abramowitz1965handbook
.
In addition, we note that
P_{\rm A}
is factorized with respect to every variable
\bm{s}_{i}^{t}
, thus any average of products of magnetizations at
different times
t
and
t^{\prime}
equals a product
of averages:

\langle\bm{m}_{t}\bm{m}_{t^{\prime}}\rangle_{\rm A}=\langle\bm{m}_{t}\rangle_{\rm A}\langle\bm{m}_{t^{\prime}}\rangle_{\rm A}.

(57)

The approximation (
52
) and the property (
57
) allow us to rearrange
Eqs.

36
and
37
in the following form:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\langle\bm{m}_{t}\rangle_{\rm A}=\bm{M}^{\rm E}=\frac{1}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\bm{m}_{t}^{\rm E},

(58)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\langle\bm{m}_{t}\rangle_{\rm A}\cdot\langle\bm{m}_{t-\tau}\rangle_{\rm A}=R^{\rm E}(\tau)=

(59)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\bm{m}_{t}^{\rm E}\cdot\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E},~~1\leq\tau\leq\tau_{\rm M},

where we dropped the explicit dependence on
\bm{s}^{E,t}
to lighten the notation.
These equations are simultaneously fulfilled by requiring that

\langle\bm{m}_{t}\rangle_{\rm A}=\bm{m}_{t}^{\rm E},\qquad\tau_{\rm M}+1\leq t\leq N_{\rm T}.

(60)

In fact, given that Eq. (
60
) is automatically satisfied for
1\leq t\leq\tau_{\rm M}
, if Eq. (
60
) holds, then Eqs. (
58
)-(

) are satisfied too.

We stress that (
60
) is only a

58
and
59

Eqs.

58
and
59
. However, by moving from
Eqs.

58
and
59
to

\bm{H}
and
\bm{J}
. Specifically, in this problem, the amount of data available

is typically much larger than the number of parameters
D+\tau_{\rm M}
. Thus, this stricter condition does not imply any overfitting (we are introducing constraints and not parameters) which, on the other hand, could be controlled by tuning
\tau_{\rm M}
.

Exploiting (
55
),
Equation

can be rewritten as

\bm{\mathcal{M}}\left(\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H}\right)=\bm{m}_{t}^{\rm E},\qquad\tau_{\rm M}+1\leq t\leq N_{\rm T}.

(61)

Since
\bm{\mathcal{M}}
is a monotone function, its inverse function is well defined and we can rewrite (

) as

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}

\displaystyle\equiv\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\left(\bm{m}_{t}^{\rm E}\right),

(62)

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H}

\displaystyle=\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E},\qquad\tau_{\rm M}+1\leq t\leq N_{\rm T}.

(63)

We observe that the operator
\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}
is linear in the variables
\bm{J},\bm{H}
: this property turns out to be crucial for the following developments. Let us underline that (

) constitutes a linear system, whose solution provides us with the parameters

, but this system involves more constraints than parameters. More precisely, as a result of (

, and the number of constraints is now

. We can therefore enrich the model by stating that the relation (

.
This stochastic term turns the linear system (

) into an auto-regressive model

of order

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H}+\sigma\bm{\epsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E},\qquad\tau_{\rm M}+1\leq t\leq N_{\rm T},

\displaystyle\bm{\epsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0}_{D},\bm{1}_{D}\right),\quad\sigma\geq 0.

(65)

In other words, given the parameters

\bm{m}_{t}=\bm{\mathcal{M}}\left[\bm{H}+\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}+\sigma\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\right].

(66)

Now, the log-likelihood
l_{\rm A}(\bm{J},\bm{H},\sigma|\bm{s}^{\rm E})
related to the auto-regressive model is introduced as

\small-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\left|\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H}-\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\right|^{2}-\left(N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}\right)\frac{D}{2}\log\left(2\pi\sigma^{2}\right)

(67)

and, in the next section, we will extremize

to obtain an estimate for
\bm{J},\bm{H}
, and
\sigma
.

II.2.1
Analytical solution for the estimated parameters

As customary in maximum likelihood estimations, we evaluate the gradient of the log-likelihood
l_{\rm A}(\bm{J},\bm{H},\sigma|\bm{s}^{\rm E})
and set it to zero
in order to estimate
\bm{J},\bm{H}
and
\sigma
. To this goal, we first compute the derivatives of (
67
)

\displaystyle\small\nabla_{\bm{H}}l_{\rm A}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}{\sigma^{2}}\left\langle\bm{H}+\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}-\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\right\rangle_{t},

(68)

\displaystyle\small\nabla_{J_{\tau}}l_{\rm A}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}{\sigma^{2}}\left\langle\left(\bm{H}+\sum_{\lambda=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\lambda}\bm{m}_{t-\lambda}^{E}-\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\right)\cdot\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}\right\rangle_{t},

(69)

\displaystyle\small\nabla_{\sigma}l_{\rm A}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}{\sigma^{3}}\langle\left|\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}+\bm{H}-\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\right|^{2}\rangle_{t}-\left(N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}\right)\frac{D}{\sigma},

(70)

where

\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{t}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\sum_{t=\tau_{\rm M}+1}^{N_{\rm T}}\cdot

(71)

denotes the time average.

Setting (
68
) to zero and solving for
\bm{H}
, we get

\bm{H}=\langle\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\rangle_{t}-\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}\right\rangle_{t}.

(72)

Proceeding along the same lines, from (
69
), for
\bm{J}
we obtain

\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}A_{\lambda\tau}J_{\tau}=B_{\tau}\leftrightarrow\bm{J}\equiv\bm{A}^{-1}\cdot\bm{B},

(73)

where the
\tau_{\rm M}\times\tau_{\rm M}
matrix
A_{\lambda\tau}
and the
\tau_{\rm M}
-dimensional vector
B_{\lambda}
are

\displaystyle A_{\lambda\tau}

\displaystyle\equiv\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}\cdot\bm{m}_{t-\lambda}^{\rm E}\right\rangle_{t}-\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}\right\rangle_{t}\cdot\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\lambda}^{\rm E}\right\rangle_{t},

(74)

\displaystyle B_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\cdot\bm{m}_{t-\lambda}^{\rm E}\rangle_{t}-\langle\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\rangle_{t}\cdot\langle\bm{m}_{t-\lambda}^{\rm E}\rangle_{t}.

(75)

Finally, setting
\nabla_{\sigma}l_{\rm A}=0
, we obtain the variance

\sigma^{2}

of the auto-regressive process (
66
):

\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{D}\left\langle\left|\bm{H}+\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm M}}J_{\tau}\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}-\bm{G}_{t}^{\rm E}\right|^{2}\right\rangle_{t}.

(76)

II.2.2
Errors on the inferred measurements

In order to estimate the errors for the inferred parameters in the maximum likelihood estimation,
we exploit the Fisher information matrix (see e.g.,
Frieden-2004
).
We recall that, given some dataset
\bm{X}
where each observation
\bm{x}_{i}
is assumed to be identically and independently distributed according to a true underlying distribution, and being
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})
a model probability density function parametrized by
\bm{\theta}
, we can write the log-likelihood function as
l(\bm{\theta}|\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i})
, where
n
is the sample size.
Then, the (empirical) Fisher information matrix
\bm{I}
has elements given by

{\bm{I}}_{\theta_{a},\theta_{b}}\equiv-\int\exp[l(\bm{\theta}|\bm{x})]\frac{\partial^{2}l(\bm{\theta}|\bm{x})}{\partial\theta_{a}\partial\theta_{b}}d\bm{x},

(77)

namely its elements correspond to the expectation of the elements of the Hessian matrix of the log-likelihood.
Let us suppose that the true parameter is
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, and that the maximum-likelihood estimate of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}=\textrm{argmax}_{\bm{\theta}}l(\bm{\theta}|\bm{x})
.
Then, one can prove that
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{\theta}}_{0},{\bm{I}}^{-1}({\bm{\theta}}_{0}))
. Since when the sample size approaches infinity, the maximum-likelihood estimate approaches the true parameter (this is also known as the consistency property), we can write that the covariance matrix for the estimated parameters is just

\bm{\Sigma}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}}\underset{n\gg 1}{\approx}{\bm{I}}^{-1}({\bm{\theta}}^{*}).

(78)

We will use this result to provide the error estimates for our model. By (
67
) the
matrix elements of the Fisher information read

\displaystyle I_{H_{a},H_{b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}{\sigma^{2}}\delta_{ab},

(79)

\displaystyle I_{J_{\tau},\bm{H}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}{\sigma^{2}}\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}\right\rangle_{t},

(80)

\displaystyle I_{J_{\tau},J_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}{\sigma^{2}}\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}\cdot\bm{m}_{t-\lambda}^{E}\right\rangle_{t},

(81)

\displaystyle I_{\sigma,\sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}{\sigma^{2}}~2D,

(82)

\displaystyle I_{\sigma,J_{\tau}}

\displaystyle=I_{\sigma,\bm{H}}=0,

(83)

and (
77
) becomes

\bm{I}=\frac{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}\cdot\bm{m}_{t-\lambda}^{E}\right\rangle_{t}&\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\tau}^{\rm E}\right\rangle_{t}&\bm{0}\\
\left\langle\bm{m}_{t-\lambda}^{E}\right\rangle_{t}&{\bm{\delta}}&{\bm{0}}\\
\bm{0}&{\bm{0}}&2D\end{array}\right).

(84)

Finally, by substituting
Eq.

84
into
Eq.

78
, we obtain the
error estimates for
\bm{J},\bm{H}
and
\sigma
:

\textrm{Var}\left(\bm{J},\bm{H},\sigma\right)=\textrm{diag}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}}\right).

(85)

In the section dedicated to the model validation, we use this relation to test the goodness of our method on synthetic datasets.

II.2.3
Effective number of delayed interactions

So far, in our analysis, we assumed that the parameter
\tau_{\rm M}
is a known constant. In most practical inference scenarios, where one needs to find from scratch the best model for a given dataset, the number of the effective delayed interactions
\tau_{\rm M}
that must be considered is unknown: in what follows, we will provide a way to estimate it.

In order to tackle this problem, we rely on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)
aic_banks2017
, which is used in the literature to probe the flexibility of an auto-regressive model.
In fact, given a dataset and a model with a mean squared error
\epsilon^{2}
,
n_{\rm p}
parameters and
n_{\rm o}
observations, the quantity

\textrm{AIC}\equiv\frac{2n_{\rm p}}{n_{\rm o}}+\log\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)

(86)

estimates the prediction error of the model. Thus, having developed several models for a certain dataset, the AIC estimates the quality of each model relative to all others, hence providing a tool for model selection. Note that, by definition, the AIC favours those models yielding a small error
and
a small number of parameters.

In the analysis above, the parameters appearing in
Eq.

86
are

n_{\rm o}=D\left(N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}\right),~~~~n_{\rm p}=\tau_{\rm M}+D+1,~~~~\epsilon^{2}=\frac{N_{\rm T}}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\sigma^{2}

(87)

Thus,

\textrm{AIC}=2\frac{\tau_{\rm M}+D+1}{D\left(N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}\right)}+\log\left(\frac{N_{\rm T}~\sigma^{2}}{N_{\rm T}-\tau_{\rm M}}\right).

(88)

To summarize, the protocol followed in the next sections is the following: we will perform inference for multiple values of
\tau_{\rm M}
and we solve for the fields and the couplings via eq.s (
72
)(
73
). We further determine
\sigma^{2}
from
Eq.

76
, next, we will evaluate (
88
) for each of the inferred models, and we will select the value of
\tau_{M}
that implies the smallest estimate of
AIC
. Finally, the maximal
J_{\tau}
in the selected time window
\tau\in[0,\tau_{M}]
turns out to be the best estimate (inferred value) of the actual coupling (planted value).

III
Model validation

In the remaining of this Appendix, we test our inference method on synthetic and real datasets: we consider two synthetic datasets, generated from the HKM and from the VM, and a biological dataset, collected via time-lapse microscopy on dendritic cell migration in a chemoattractant field (see
Alemanno2023
for details).

In general, we first run the inference protocol on such datasets and obtain the parameters
\bm{J}
and
\bm{H}
, which we call
inferred
parameters, and then we evaluate the goodness of the inference process. As for the HKM and the VM, previously addressed, a validation is possibile by comparing the inferred parameters and those set in the model under consideration and denoted as
planted
parameters. The HKM is the direct formulation of the inverse problem developed in this paper, thus, in this setting,
inferred
and
planted
parameters must match even quantitatively (and their agreement is a
conditio sine qua non
to move to more challenging scenarios); in fact, in this case, inference is excellent. Conversely, for the VM, the inference process can be harder as this model is not the direct model of the delayed ME approach we developed, and, in order to investigate the problem meticulously, we face two versions of the delayed VM, namely, the topological and the metric ones. In both cases we have a set of units whose dynamics is non-Markovian and influenced by the trajectories of a subset of peers, made of
i
. the closest
n_{c}
units (regardless of their reciprocal distance) in the former version, or
ii
. the closest units within a certain interaction range
r
(regardless of their number) in the latter version.

Finally, the analysis of the biological datasets aims to be a proof of concept to highlight the non-Markovianity of biological dynamics: this is why we consider for the test study the migration of dendritic cells as the latter are expected to perceive signalling proteins that must diffuse from the emitter cell to the receiver one, thus, these interactions are natural prototypes of delayed interactions.

We anticipate that, beyond successfully testing our delayed-inference protocol on all the synthetic datasets, we obtained a remarkable result in inspecting the biological ones: the trajectory of a given dendritic cell at time
t
turns out to be influenced by those of the other cells for a past temporal window spanning back up to
\tau_{M}\sim\mathcal{O}(10)
time-steps, thus resulting in severe violation of the Markovian assumption.

III.3
Real dataset: dendritic cells in a chemokine gradient

Having successfully tested our delayed ME inference on heterogeneous synthetic datasets, we now apply it to an experimental dataset on cell migration as a proof of concept. This is because, from one side, there is a vast network of signalling proteins (released by other cells) affecting immune dynamics

From the other side, there is a vast literature that implements Markov processes as a natural starting point to model immunodynamics, see e.g.
NoMarkov1

;
NoMarkov3
;
Agliari-SciRep2014
;
Biselli-SciRep2017
;
agliari2020a

The results obtained for the two datasets are consistent and the unique picture that emerges can be streamlined as follows: the dynamics of a given leukocyte at a given time is influenced by the past actions of its peers for a very long time window (in these cases
\tau_{M}\sim 13,\ 14
), hence the trajectories that these cells paint while migrating are far from the Markovian limit. Moreover, along the dynamics, the inferred coupling are (mainly) negative in the recent past (
\tau=1,\ 2
) and (essentially) positive in the further past (
\tau\geq 3
) and the AIC criterion selects
\tau_{M}\sim 13,\ 14
. We speculate that a cell, during migration, may integrate signals it receives in time and, from this perspective, the present research seems to suggest that – at least in the present setting – dendritic cells may integrate signals for a temporal time-window of
\mathcal{O}(10)
time steps, but clearly a dedicated study has to deepen this aspect in a forthcoming paper.
