Linearly-Convergent FISTA Variant for Composite Optimization with Duality
Casey Garner 1 \text{Casey Garner}^{1}, Shuzhong Zhang 2 \text{Shuzhong Zhang}^{2}

Abstract
Many large-scale optimization problems can be expressed as composite optimization models. Accelerated first-order methods such as the fast iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm (FISTA) have proven effective for numerous large composite models. In this paper, we present a new variation of FISTA, to be called C-FISTA, which obtains global linear convergence for a broader class of composite models than many of the latest FISTA variants. We demonstrate the versatility and effectiveness of C-FISTA through multiple numerical experiments on group Lasso, group logistic regression and geometric programming models. Furthermore, we utilize Fenchel duality to show C-FISTA can solve the dual of a finite sum convex optimization model.

This paper presents three main contributions. The first contribution of this paper is the development of an accelerated version of FISTA which obtains global linear convergence for (

The second contribution of this paper is our utilization of Fenchel duality to develop dual models which can be solved via C-FISTA, and our leveraging of Fenchel duality theory

A Generalized Composite Optimization Algorithm

In this paper, our primary focus revolves around the composite optimization model,

\begin{array}[]{lll}&\min&H(\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}))+R(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in{\cal X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{array}

where
\mathcal{X}
is a closed, convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
H:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth, convex function,

R:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is convex but potentially non-smooth,
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is smooth and defined as
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\left(B_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}),\cdots,B_{m}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\right)^{\top}
with Jacobian,
\mathbf{J_{B}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
, at
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For convenience in our analysis we denote
F(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}):=H(\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}))+R(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
. A key assumption for feasible implementation is that the following proximal mapping with respect to
R
is efficiently computable:

\mbox{\rm Prox}_{tR}(\mbox{\boldmath$u$}):=\mbox{arg}\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in{\cal X}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\|^{2}+tR(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\right\},

where
t>0
. We now state the four main assumptions in our analysis:

(A0)

H
is strongly convex with parameter
\mu
and gradient Lipschitz with parameter
L
, and
H\circ\mbox{\boldmath$B$}
is convex over
{\cal X}
.

(A1)

B_{i}\in C^{2}({\cal X})
is Lipschitz continuous with parameter
\bar{L}_{i}\geq 0
, and has Lipschitz gradient constant
L_{i}\geq 0
for
i=1,\ldots,m
.

(A2)

There exists
\tau>0
such that
\tau\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\|^{2}\leq\|\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})-\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})\|^{2}
for all
\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\in\text{span}(\mathcal{X})
.

(A3)

There exists
\xi\geq 0
such that
\forall\;\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\in\text{span}(\mathcal{X})
with
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}:=\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})
,

\bigg|\nabla_{\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}}H(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}})^{\top}\left(\mathbf{J_{B}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})-\mathbf{J_{B}}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})\right)(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$})\bigg|\leq\xi\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\|^{2}.

Our analysis focuses on the proposed algorithm C-FISTA (Algorithm

). However, before stating the algorithm and proving our convergence results, we further frame the assumptions.

2.1
Discussion of Assumptions

In this section we further detail the assumptions and demonstrate how they compare to standard assumptions in the literature. Before expounding upon (A0) - (A3), we first make the essential observation that under these assumptions it is possible
H(\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}))
is not strongly convex in
x
. Letting
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}=\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
, we do see, however, that the descent inequality and the strongly convex inequality still hold for
H
with respect to
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}
and
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}
, i.e. for all
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}},\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\displaystyle H(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}})\leq H(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})+\nabla_{\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}}H(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})^{\top}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}-\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})+\frac{L}{2}\|\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}-\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}\|^{2},

\displaystyle H(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}})\geq H(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})+\nabla_{\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}}H(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})^{\top}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}-\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}-\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}\|^{2},

where
\nabla_{\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}}H(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}):=\nabla H(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})\big|_{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}=\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})}
and

\nabla_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}H(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})=\nabla_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}H(\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}))=\mathbf{J_{B}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})^{\top}\nabla_{\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}}H(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}).

For the remainder of the paper, we will reserve
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}
to denote
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
, that is
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}:=\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
.

Of the assumptions, (A0) and (A1), are the most straightforward. The first assumption is standard in the literature, and the second ensures
B
is sufficiently well-behaved in terms of its continuity and differentiability; however, no convexity assumptions are made directly on the component functions
B_{i}
.

Remark 1

From assumption (A1) we obtain a few important inequalities. First, utilizing the fact each
B_{i}
is Lipschitz continuous, it follows from the limit definition of the directional derivative that
\|\nabla B_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\|^{2}\leq\bar{L}_{i}^{2}
for all
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore, letting
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be fixed we see that,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{J_{B}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\|\mathbf{J_{B}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\|\;\bigg|\;\|\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\|=1\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\nabla B_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\right)^{2}}\;\bigg|\;\|\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\|=1\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\bar{L}_{1}^{2}+\ldots+\bar{L}_{m}^{2}}.

So, defining,

r:=\bar{L}_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\bar{L}_{m}^{2},

(6)

we have,

\|\mathbf{J_{B}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\|^{2}\leq r,\;\;\forall\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(7)

As a result of (
7
) we obtain a second important inequality for our analysis. Letting
\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by the differentiability of

,

\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})-\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{J_{B}}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}+t(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\right)(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$})dt,

which by the triangle inequality and (

) implies,

\|\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})-\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\|\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|\mathbf{J_{B}}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}+t(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\right)\|\cdot\|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|dt\leq\sqrt{r}\|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|,

and so,

\|\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}-\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}\|^{2}=\|\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})-\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\|^{2}\leq r\|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|^{2},\;\forall\;\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(8)

In regards to (A2), we note it is a condition which depends as much on the constraint set
{\cal X}
as it does on the function
B
. For example, if
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}
with
\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
singular, then (A2) will fail to hold if
\text{span}{({\cal X})}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
; however, it will be satisfied if
\text{span}{({\cal X})}\cap\text{null}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
. Additionally, this condition is fundamentally different than growth conditions about the set of optimal solutions such as given in

because it places no condition on the proximal mapping nor relates to the set of optimal solutions of (
1

Assumption (A3) by all appearances is the most opaque; however, (A3) is essentially equivalent to assuming
H\circ\mbox{\boldmath$B$}
is gradient Lipschitz continuous. If it is assumed
H\circ\mbox{\boldmath$B$}
is gradient Lipschitz with parameter
\hat{L}>0
, then (A3) follows with
\xi=\hat{L}+rL
. Similarly, if assumption (A3) holds, then
H\circ\mbox{\boldmath$B$}
is gradient Lipschitz with parameter
\xi+rL
. Therefore, this assumption is fundamentally a constraint on the gradient of
H\circ\mbox{\boldmath$B$}
which is unlike standard error bound or growth conditions found in the literature.

As a closing remark on the assumptions, which we will revisit following the proof of Theorem
2.1
, although (A2) and (A3) are stated in a global fashion they only need to hold near the iterates generated by Algorithm
1
. For the sake of our argument, we require our assumptions to hold on
\text{span}({\cal X})
; however, in practice this is unnecessary and we demonstrate this through several numerical experiments in Section
6
.

Remark 3

The condition
\tau\mu-\xi>0
essentially requires that the product of the first-order derivatives of
H
and the second-order derivative of
B
should not exceed the product of their curvatures. In some cases, this condition is easy to satisfy by variable-transformation, or scaling. For example, when
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
is homogeneous of degree
d_{1}>0
, i.e.
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\lambda\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\lambda^{d_{1}}\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
for any
x
and
\lambda>0
, and
R(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
is homogeneous with degree
d_{2}>0
, i.e.
R(\lambda\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\lambda^{d_{2}}R(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
. Then for any
\lambda>0
one can scale the variable as
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\prime}:=\lambda^{\frac{1}{d_{1}}}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}
, and the problem is turned equivalently into minimizing
H(\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\prime}))+R(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\prime})=H(\lambda\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}))+\lambda^{\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}}R(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
. After the above change of variables, the new objective has the modified parameters
\tau:=\lambda^{2}\tau
,
\mu:=\mu
, and
\xi:=\lambda\xi
. Therefore, in this situation by appropriately choosing
\lambda>0
one can always satisfy the condition
\tau\mu-\xi>0
.

As mentioned previously in Section
2.1
, the global nature of assumptions (A2) and (A3) are in many instances stronger than necessary. From the proof of Theorem
2.1
, we see these assumptions only need to hold locally about the
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}
sequences generated by C-FISTA. Enforcing the conditions to hold on the span of the constraint set ensures global linear convergence; however, even when the assumptions are not strictly satisfied on the span, C-FISTA still proves to be effective in practice. In Section
6
, we will demonstrate the effectiveness of C-FISTA on group Lasso and geometric programming models where the assumptions are not strictly met globally but asymptotic linear convergence is still achieved. Ultimately, the success of C-FISTA in these regimes demonstrates the current gap between theory and practice; further research should shrink this separation and provide a direction for future inquiry.

3
Motivation: Lasso, Logistic Regression & Geometric Programming

A class of motivating composite optimization models for which C-FISTA is applicable
are the various Lasso formulations. The standard Lasso model originating from Tibshirani
T96
,

\begin{array}[]{lll}&\min&\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\|^{2}+\gamma\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|_{1}\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{array}

is solvable via C-FISTA. All the assumptions for Algorithm
1
will be satisfied when
A
is of full-column rank, but, as we will see in Section
6.2.2
, C-FISTA demonstrates linear convergence when
A
is not of full-rank and the assumptions are almost but not completely met. The results from Section
2
further guarantee global linear convergence to the optimal solution even when there exists a constraint on
x
. For example, Algorithm
1
can solve the following constrained Lasso model,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\|^{2}+\gamma\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|_{1}\\
\mbox{s.t.}&l_{i}\leq x_{i}\leq u_{i},\,\,i=1,2,...,n.\end{array}

The utility of the Lasso model is detailed extensively in the literature; see
JOV9
;
QSG13
;
YLY11
;
YL7
;
ZZST20
. The success of the basic model spawned the development of several Lasso variants including: the group Lasso (GL) model
QSG13
;
YL7
,

\begin{array}[]{lll}&\min&\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\|^{2}+\gamma\sum_{j\in{\cal G}}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(j)\|\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{array}

where
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(j)
is a subvector of
x
corresponding to the indices in
j\in{\cal G}
where
{\cal G}
disjointly partitions
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, the sparse-group Lasso (SGL) model
SFHT13
;
ZZST20
,

\begin{array}[]{lll}&\min&\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\|^{2}+\gamma_{1}\sum_{j\in{\cal G}}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(j)\|+\gamma_{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|_{1}\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{array}

and the overlapping group and overlapping sparse-group Lasso (OSGL) models
JOV9
;
YLY11
;
ZZST20
which are of identical form to the group Lasso and sparse-group Lasso models respectively, expect
{\cal G}
no longer is a disjoint partition of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. As we will see in Section
5
, the subproblems required in C-FISTA for the Lasso models are solvable in closed form or by an efficient subroutine in the case of the overlapping group/sparse-group Lasso models with global linear convergence achieved in each variant.

Another model where C-FISTA has demonstrated linear convergence is the sparse-group logistic regression (SGLR) model
SLEP
;
MGB8
,

\begin{array}[]{lll}&\min&\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ln\left(1+\exp\left(-y_{i}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}_{i}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}+b\right)\right)\right)+\gamma_{1}\sum_{j\in{\cal G}}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(j)\|+\gamma_{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|_{1}\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},b\in\mathbb{R},\end{array}

where
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector containing only entries of
\pm 1
. The sparse-group logistic regression model has applications in various machine learning models especially in the area of classification, and the subproblems for the logistic regression model are in the same format as the sparse-group Lasso model detailed extensively in Section
5
.

A final motivating model is geometric programming. Geometric programming is a useful modeling paradigm which has numerous applications especially in electrical circuit design. A full description and tutorial on these models is provided by Boyd
et al.
in
GP
. A regularized subclass of geometric programs solvable via C-FISTA is,

\begin{array}[]{lll}&\min&\;\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}x_{1}^{a_{k1}}x_{2}^{a_{k2}}\ldots x_{n}^{a_{kn}}+R(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}),\\
&\mbox{s.t}&\;\;\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in{\cal X},\end{array}

where
c_{k}>0
,
\mathbf{a_{k}}:=(a_{k1},\ldots,a_{kn})^{\top}\leq 0
for all
k
, and
{\cal X}
is a closed and bounded convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
. The condition
\mathbf{a_{k}}\leq 0
ensures the model is convex. In solving this model, we decompose the objective such that
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\left(\ln(x_{1}),\ldots,\ln(x_{n})\right)^{\top}
. Allowing non-linear and non-convex component functions for

demonstrates the novelty of C-FISTA to escape strictly affine compositions showcasing a level of extended generality.

Before discussing how to compute subproblems for the Lasso models, we first discuss Fenchel duality in Section
4
and describe how C-FISTA can be utilized via a dual approach to solve a general constrained composite model.

4
A Dual Formulation and Algorithmic Approach

In this section, we outline the construction of a dual algorithm using Fenchel duality which generates an approximate primal solution from the dual solution at every iterate. To elucidate this approach we first provide a brief overview of standard convex analysis results regarding conjugate functions and Fenchel duality
RF70
;
Roos20
. Unless otherwise stated, we use the standard notation of Rockafellar
RF70
. For generality, we will consider the following constrained composite model:

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;\;h_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+h_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\cdots+h_{\ell}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})

(17)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\;\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in{\cal C}_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},\,j=1,2,...,r,

where the
h_{i}
’s are proper convex functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the
{\cal C}_{j}
’s are closed convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. To begin, let us introduce some basic notions in convex analysis.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed convex set. The
support function
of
S
is defined as,

f_{S}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}):=\max_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in S}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$x$},

and the
polar set
of
S
is,

S^{\circ}:=\{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\leq 1,\,\forall\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in S\}.

Thus, we can succinctly write,
S^{\circ}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\mid f_{S}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})\leq 1\}
. Let
g
be a convex function whose domain is contained in
S
. The
conjugate of
g
is defined as,

g^{*}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}):=\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in S}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-g(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\right],

where the function is assumed to take value
+\infty
anywhere outside its domain. In deriving the Fenchel dual of (
17
) we need some standard results from convex analysis.

Lemma 1

Suppose
f
is a proper convex function, then

-f^{*}(\mathbf{0})=\inf_{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}\;f(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}).

(18)

Lemma 2

(Theorem 16.4
RF70
) Suppose
g_{1},...,g_{r}
are proper convex functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the intersection of the relative interiors of the domains of the
g_{i}
’s is non-empty, then

\left(g_{1}+\cdots+g_{r}\right)^{*}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\inf_{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1}+\cdots+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{r}=x}\sum_{j=1}^{r}g_{j}^{*}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}).

(19)

Lemma 3

For a convex set
S
, suppose its indicator function is defined as,

I_{S}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\mbox{if $\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in S$};\\
+\infty,&\mbox{if $\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\not\in S$}.\end{array}\right.

Then, the conjugate of the indicator function is simply the support function of
S
;

I_{S}^{*}=f_{S}.

The proofs of Lemmas
1
and
3
follow directly from the definitions of conjugate and support functions. Thus, rewriting (
17
) in the equivalent form,

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle h_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+h_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\cdots+h_{\ell}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+I_{{\cal C}_{1}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\ldots+I_{{\cal C}_{r}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})

s.t.

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and utilizing Lemmas
1
,
2
, and
3
, the Fenchel dual of (
17
) is:

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle h_{0}^{*}(-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1}-\cdots-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{\ell}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{\ell+1}-\cdots-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{\ell+r})

(20)

\displaystyle+h_{1}^{*}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1})+\cdots+h^{*}_{\ell}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{\ell})+f_{{\cal C}_{1}}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{\ell+1})+\cdots+f_{{\cal C}_{r}}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{\ell+r})

s.t.

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,j=1,2,...,\ell+r.

Note, for the sake of presentation we have written the dual problem (
20
) in such a manner that the optimal solutions of (
17
) and (
20
) differ by a negative sign. To better understand the link between the primal and dual models, we demonstrate how the optimality conditions of (
17
) and (
20
) are linked. Assuming the conjugate functions of the
h_{i}
’s (
i=0,1,...,\ell
) are differentiable and denoting
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,\ell+r
to be optimal for (
20
), we have the first-order optimality conditions,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\nabla h_{0}^{*}(-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}^{*})+\nabla h_{i}^{*}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{i})=0,&i=1,...,\ell\\
\nabla h_{0}^{*}(-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}^{*})\in\partial f_{{\cal C}_{j}}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{\ell+j}),&j=1,...,r.\end{array}\right.

(21)

Denoting
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}:=\nabla h_{0}^{*}(-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}^{*})
, the optimality conditions imply
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}=\nabla h_{i}^{*}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, and so by Lemma 5 of
Z18
,
\nabla h_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})=\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{i}
, for
i=1,...,\ell
. Similarly, because
f_{{\cal C}_{j}}^{*}=I_{{\cal C}_{j}}
,
if
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}\in\partial f_{{\cal C}_{j}}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{\ell+j})
, then
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{\ell+j}\in\partial I_{{\cal C}_{j}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})
, which means that
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{\ell+j}
is a normal direction at
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}
:

(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{\ell+j})^{\top}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})\leq 0,\mbox{ for all $\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in{\cal C}_{j}$}.

Furthermore, by (
21
)

\nabla h_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})=-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}^{*}=-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\nabla h_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})-\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{\ell+j}.

(22)

Thus, the first-order optimality conditions for the dual problem implies (
22
), which is the optimality condition for the primal problem (
17
). To see this, note that

\nabla h_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\nabla h_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})=-\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{\ell+j},

and so,

\left(\nabla h_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\nabla h_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})\right)^{\top}\hskip-7.22743pt(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})=-\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{\ell+j}\right)^{\top}\hskip-3.61371pt(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})\geq 0,\,\,\forall\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\cap_{j=1}^{r}{\cal C}_{j}.

We now present an important relationship between the primal solution induced by the dual iterations which outlines a dual approach to solving (
17
).

Proposition 1

Suppose that
h_{0}
is a strongly convex function with strong convexity parameter
\mu
and gradient Lipschitz constant
L
. Let
\{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}\}
be a sequence converging to the dual solution
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}
. For each
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}
in the dual sequence, we recover a primal solution

\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k}:=\nabla h_{0}^{*}(-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}^{k})
such that,

\frac{1}{L}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}_{j}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{j}\right)\right\|\leq\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}\|\leq\frac{1}{\mu}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}_{j}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{j}\right)\right\|.

Proof

By the Fenchel duality relation, we know that
h_{0}^{*}
is also a strongly convex function with strong convexity parameter
1/L
and gradient Lipschitz constant
1/\mu
(Theorem 1
Z18
). Further, by the definition of
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k}
and previously stated results,

\nabla h_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k})=-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}^{k}
. Therefore,
\nabla h_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k})-\nabla h_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}^{*}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}^{k})
, and so the gradient Lipschitz condition on
h
yields,

\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}_{j}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{j}\right)\right\|=\|\nabla h_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k})-\nabla h_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*})\|\leq L\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}\|.

On the other hand, the gradient Lipschitz condition on
h^{*}
states,

\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}\|=\left\|\nabla h_{0}^{*}\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}^{k}\right)-\nabla h_{0}^{*}\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}^{*}\right)\right\|\leq\frac{1}{\mu}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\ell+r}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}_{j}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{*}_{j}\right)\right\|.

∎

Therefore, the above result gives us the framework to develop a general dual approach to solving (
17
). Applying any algorithm to solve the dual problem (
20
) we can recover a primal solution at each iteration; furthermore, the rate of convergence on the primal side for any algorithm will only differ by a fixed constant,
1/\mu
, in-comparison to the rate of convergence on the dual side.

The presented dual formulation is not novel; Han and Lou
HL88
and Fukushima
et al.

FHN96
have very similar derivations of the dual and many algorithms such as Dykstra’s projection algorithm
GM89
utilize Fenchel duality. The clarity provided by the previous result is that, under the assumption
h_{0}
is strongly convex with gradient Lipschitz, any algorithm which generates a converging dual solution will generate a primal solution with the same rate of convergence up to a constant factor. This is directly relevant to our proposed algorithm because the dual (
20
) is solvable via C-FISTA. Let
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\;\mbox{\boldmath$B$}:\mathbb{R}^{n(\ell+r)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
R:\mathbb{R}^{n(\ell+r)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that,

\displaystyle H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})

\displaystyle=h_{0}^{*}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}),

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})

\displaystyle=\left[-\mathbf{I}_{n}\;|\;\ldots\;|\;-\mathbf{I}_{n}\right]\mbox{\boldmath$x$},

\displaystyle R(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})

\displaystyle=h_{1}^{*}(\mathbf{E}_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\cdots+h^{*}_{\ell}(\mathbf{E}_{\ell}\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+f_{{\cal C}_{1}}(\mathbf{E}_{\ell+1}\mbox{\boldmath$x$})+\cdots+f_{{\cal C}_{r}}(\mathbf{E}_{\ell+r}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}),

where
\mathbf{I}_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix and
\mathbf{E}_{k}
is the
1\times(\ell+r)
block matrix with the identity matrix
\mathbf{I}_{n}
in the k-th position, e.g.
\mathbf{E}_{2}=\left[\mathbf{0}\;|\;\mathbf{I}_{n}\;|\;\mathbf{0}\;|\;\ldots\;|\;\mathbf{0}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n(\ell+r)}
, then we can rewrite (
20
) as,

\begin{array}[]{lll}&\min&H(\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}))+R(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(\ell+r)}.\end{array}

With this decomposition it is straightforward to specify the constants:
\mu^{\prime}
,
L^{\prime}
,
\tau^{\prime}
,
r^{\prime}
and
\xi^{\prime}
for C-FISTA. Assuming
h_{0}
is strongly convex with parameter
\mu
and gradient Lipschitz with constant
L
, it follows
\mu^{\prime}=1/L
and
L^{\prime}=1/\mu
. The linearity of
B
yields
\xi^{\prime}=0
, and the simple structure of the mapping gives
r^{\prime}=\|\left[-\mathbf{I}_{n}\;|\;\ldots\;|\;-\mathbf{I}_{n}\right]\|^{2}_{2}=(\ell+r)
. Lastly,
\tau^{\prime}=0
since the matrix defining
B
is singular; however, though
\tau^{\prime}=0
does not strictly satisfy assumption (A2), in practice C-FISTA will often converge for a range of small
\tau^{\prime}
values. This is demonstrated in Section
6.2.2
through multiple numerical experiments on an underdetermined group Lasso model and follows from our previous discussion on the overly strict nature of the assumptions which ensure global linear convergence.

A final note must be made in-regards to the proximal mapping step which would be required to solve the dual formulation. Computing
\mbox{\rm Prox}_{R}(\cdot)
in this case would depend on the definitions of
h_{i}^{*}
and
f_{C_{i}}
. This could prove difficult; however,
R
presents a natural decomposition such that only the individual proximal mappings of the functions
h_{i}^{*}
and
f_{C_{i}}
would be necessary. This substantially simplifies the procedure enabling tractable computations in many instances.

Therefore, we see that (
17
) under the proper convexity assumptions has a dual which in many cases is solvable via C-FISTA, and Proposition
1
demonstrates the primal solution is recoverable from the dual solution without losing linear convergence.

Besides using Fenchel duality to directly solve the dual model with C-FISTA and recover the primal solution, we can also utilize Fenchel duality theory to solve the intermediate subproblems in the primal implementation of C-FISTA. In the next section, we describe this process using the sparse-group Lasso model as an example.

5
Solving Subproblems with Fenchel Duality

One example of how duality can be leveraged to apply C-FISTA can be seen in how we solve the subproblems for the group and sparse-group Lasso models. In the sparse-group Lasso model, the main subproblem which needs to be solved to apply C-FISTA is of the form,

\begin{array}[]{lll}(S)&\min&\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$d$}\|^{2}+\gamma_{1}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|+\gamma_{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|_{1}\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{array}

with
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\geq 0
. Utilizing Lemmas
1
and
2
and standard conjugate functions
Roos20
, we see the Fenchel dual of
(S)
is,

\begin{array}[]{lll}(DS)&\min&\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}\|^{2}\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1}\|\leq\gamma_{1},\\
&&\|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma_{2}.\end{array}

When
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}
is fixed, consider the simple projection problem,

\min_{\|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1}\|\leq\gamma_{1}}\,\,\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1}\|,

whose solution is explicit:

\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1}^{*}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\mbox{\boldmath$d$}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2},&\mbox{ if $\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}\|\leq\gamma_{1}$};\\
-\gamma_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2})/\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}\|,&\mbox{ if $\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}\|>\gamma_{1}$}.\end{array}\right.

Therefore,

\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1}^{*}\|=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\mbox{ if $\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}\|\leq\gamma_{1}$};\\
\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}\|-\gamma_{1},&\mbox{ if $\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}\|>\gamma_{1}$}.\end{array}\right.

Thus, the dual problem
(DS)
is equivalent to
\min_{\|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma_{2}}\,\,(\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}\|-\gamma_{1})_{+}
, which can be solved through the associated model,

\begin{array}[]{lll}(DS)^{\prime}&\min&\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}\|^{2}\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma_{2}.\end{array}

Since the individual components of
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}
are decoupled in
(DS)^{\prime}
, the above problem can be reduced to solving
n
1-dimensional problems,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&(d_{i}+t)^{2}\\
\mbox{s.t.}&|t|\leq\gamma_{2},\end{array}

for
i=1,2,...,n
. Observe that the solutions for the above 1-dimensional models can be found using the following thresholding operator:

{{\rm Th}}_{\gamma_{2}}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\gamma_{2},&\mbox{if $t<-\gamma_{2}$};\\
t,&\mbox{if $-\gamma_{2}\leq t\leq\gamma_{2}$};\\
\gamma_{2},&\mbox{if $t>\gamma_{2}$}.\\
\end{array}\right.

Therefore, a solution for
(DS)
is
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}^{*}=\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-\mbox{\boldmath$d$})
, where
(\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-\mbox{\boldmath$d$}))_{i}={\rm Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-d_{i})
for all
i
, and,

\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1}^{*}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\mbox{\boldmath$d$}-\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-\mbox{\boldmath$d$}),&\mbox{ if $\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-\mbox{\boldmath$d$})\|\leq\gamma_{1}$};\\
-\gamma_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-\mbox{\boldmath$d$}))/\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-\mbox{\boldmath$d$})\|,&\mbox{ if $\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-\mbox{\boldmath$d$})\|>\gamma_{1}$}.\end{array}\right.

After solving
(DS)
with the optimal solution
(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}^{*})
, one recovers the optimal solution to
(S)
as
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}=\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1}^{*}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{2}^{*}
using the results from Section
4
. In particular,

\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\mbox{ if $\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-\mbox{\boldmath$d$})\|\leq\gamma_{1}$};\\
\frac{(\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-\mbox{\boldmath$d$})\|-\gamma_{1})}{\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-\mbox{\boldmath$d$})\|}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-\mbox{\boldmath$d$})\right),&\mbox{ if $\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}+\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}}(-\mbox{\boldmath$d$})\|>\gamma_{1}$}.\end{array}\right.

Hence, by Fenchel duality we obtain a closed form solution to
(S)
yielding an exact solution to the required subproblems to apply C-FISTA to the sparse-group Lasso model. Similar approaches can be taken to solve other potential subproblems which arise when applying C-FISTA. In the next section, we present the algorithms and numerical experiments for solving the models discussed in Section
3
.

6.1
Lasso Models

In this section we state how to apply C-FISTA to solve the various Lasso models described in Section
3
. In order to apply C-FISTA, the practitioner must select a decomposition of the objective function, i.e. the user must define
H
and
B
in (
1
). For the sake of exposition, in Sections
6.1.1
,
6.1.2
, and
6.1.3
, we decompose
H(\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}))=\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\|^{2}
as
H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\|^{2}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}
. By doing this we have
\xi=0
,
r=1
, and
\tau=1
. Therefore, the strong convexity constant
\mu
and gradient Lipschitz constant
L
for
H
are the only parameters which must be estimated to apply C-FISTA. This was the convention chosen for the algorithms presented in these sections as it enables more concise algorithmic descriptions; however, another viable decomposition is to have
H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\|^{2}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}
. This decomposition is utilized in Section
6.2.2
. Under this decomposition, one readily obtains the strong convexity and gradient Lipschitz constants as
\mu=L=1
. As for the other constants, the linearity of
B
yields
\xi=0
and
r=\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$A$})
. Hence, the only constant to estimate is
\tau
from assumption (A2). The ability to chose the decomposition for a particular problem is a benefit of C-FISTA. This freedom enables the practitioner to select the most beneficial and/or convenient decomposition for their model of interest.

We now begin our discussion with how to apply C-FISTA to the group Lasso formulation.

6.1.1
C-FISTA for Group Lasso

Applying C-FISTA to solve any model hinges on computing the proximal mapping in (
4
). This key subproblem in the group Lasso model is of the form,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$d$}\|^{2}+\gamma\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|\\
\mbox{s.t.}&\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{array}

(23)

which by the first-order optimality conditions has the simple closed-form solution,

\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}\|\leq\gamma;\\
\left(\frac{\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}\|-\gamma}{\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}\|}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$d$},&\text{ if }\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}\|>\gamma.\end{cases}

With the solution to the group Lasso subproblems (
23
), we write down how to solve the group Lasso model with C-FISTA in Algorithm
2
where
H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\|^{2}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}
.

Algorithm 2

C-FISTA for
(GL)

Input:
Constants
\mu
and
L
; penalty parameter
\gamma>0
; vector partition
\mathcal{G}
.

Step 0.
Choose any
(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{0},\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
k:=0
.

Step 1.
Let,

\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}:=\frac{1}{1+\theta}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k}+\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{k}.

Step 2.
For each
j\in\mathcal{G}
:

\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(j)^{k+1}:=\begin{cases}0,&\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}(j)\|\leq\gamma/L;\\
\left(\frac{\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}(j)\|-\gamma/L}{\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}(j)\|}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$d$}(j),&\|\mbox{\boldmath$d$}(j)\|>\gamma/L;\end{cases}

with
\mbox{\boldmath$d$}:=\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla H(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k})=\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}-\frac{1}{L}\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{\top}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$})
and corresponding subvectors
\mbox{\boldmath$d$}(j)
.

Step 3.

\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{k+1}:=(1-\theta)\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{k}+\theta\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}+{\alpha}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k+1}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}\right),

where
\theta=\sqrt{\mu/L}
and
\alpha=\sqrt{L/\mu}
.

Step 4.
Let
k:=k+1
; return to Step 1 until convergence.

Practical application of Algorithm
2
requires the user to determine bounds for the Lipschitz constant and strong convexity constant of
H
. For the group Lasso models
H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}):=\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\|^{2}
making such bounds readily available. By the definition of strong convexity we see
\mu=\lambda_{\min}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$A$})
. Similarly, using the definition of the Lipschitz constant, we see that
L\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$A$})
where
\lambda_{\min}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$A$})
and
\lambda_{\max}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$A$})
are the smallest and largest eigenvalues of
\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$A$}
respectively. For other strongly convex functions
H
on which C-FISTA is applicable, tight bounds for
L
and
\mu
might be unavailable. In these situations conservative estimates for these bounds or a backtracking scheme such as in
BT09
to estimate these parameters would be required. In this paper we do not focus on subroutines to estimate these bounds in our algorithms; however, the backtracking strategies applied in
BT09
;
CC19
;
FV19
could similarly be implemented in many instances of C-FISTA.

6.1.3
C-FISTA for Overlapping Sparse-Group Lasso

The key difference between the overlapping sparse-group Lasso model and those previously discussed is the removal of the prohibition that the groups of variables cannot intersect. Allowing overlapping groups of variables removes the ability to decouple the minimization in (
4
) into subproblems over the individual subvectors. In the sparse-group Lasso model, since the groups of variables do not overlap, we were able to show in Section
5
there was a closed form solution to the proximal mappings; however, with overlapping groups the key subproblem for implementing C-FISTA,

\min\;\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$d$}\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{L}\sum_{j\in{\cal G}}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(j)\|+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{L}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|_{1},

(24)

is no longer decomposable and a simple closed form solution is unobtainable; therefore, we must solve this subproblem numerically at each iteration in-order to apply C-FISTA to the overlapping sparse-group Lasso model. In
YLY11
the authors apply a variation of FISTA to solve the (OSGL) model. Our approach is distinct from their approach because we accelerate FISTA through the use of two sequences,
\{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}\}
and
\{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{k}\}
, while they use a single sequence performing a Nesterov-like acceleration. In order to apply their algorithm, the authors of
YLY11
developed an efficient subroutine,
overlapping.c
, to numerically solve (
24
). Their subroutine is provided in the SLEP software package
SLEP
. In our solving of the (OSGL) model with C-FISTA, we apply Algorithm
3
exactly in the same fashion as for the sparse-group Lasso model except when computing
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k+1}
in Step 2 we solve (
24
) with
overlapping.c
from the SLEP software package.

6.2.2
Underdetermined Group Lasso Numerical Tests

The second set of Lasso experiments focused on underdetermined data. In particular, the group Lasso model was studied with a rank deficient data matrix. With underdetermined data, the assumptions for Theorem
2.1
are not fully realized; however, as previously claimed and will be shown, in practice asymptotic linear convergence is still possible when the global assumptions (A2) and/or (A3) are not fully realized. To demonstrate this we conducted an extensive numerical experiment comparing FISTA, without backtracking, to C-FISTA on the group Lasso model with underdetermined data.

For the experiment, we let
H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\|^{2}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}
. Therefore, the strong convexity and gradient Lipschitz constants for
H
are
\mu=L=1
,
\xi=0
, and
r=\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$A$})
. The only remaining constant to compute is
\tau>0
which by (A2) needs to satisfy,

\tau\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\|^{2}\leq\|\mbox{\boldmath$A$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$})\|^{2},

for all
\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
A
is not of full-column rank, this assumption cannot hold; however, choosing a small positive value for
\tau
ensures the inequality will hold for a sufficient number of iterates generated by Algorithm
1
. This enables C-FISTA to maintain its convergence though assumption (A2) is not formally satisfied.

In our underdetermined group Lasso experiments, we generated synthetic data matrices and response vectors as in Section
6.2.1
. Randomly generated matrices with dimensions:
2500\times 5000,1200\times 5000
and
600\times 5000
were constructed, and each of these group Lasso problem sizes were solved under the three subvector groupings from Section
6.2.1
. We generated ten instances of these nine group Lasso settings and solved the models with FISTA and C-FISTA under three different parameter settings for
\tau
. Table
1
displays the results for these ninety numerical experiments. Each entry in the table provides the average number of iterations for the respective algorithms to converge to within
10^{-10}
of the optimal objective value.

From Table
1
we observe C-FISTA is convergent for a range of
\tau
values demonstrating a robustness to miss-specification. Of the different values for
\tau
selected, only the selection of
\tau=1
failed to converge after 20,000 iterations and this was only for the case
\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\in\mathbb{R}^{600\times 5000}
and the group variable size was 200. Second, we note C-FISTA outperformed FISTA consistently with the best algorithm for each setting being C-FISTA with either
\tau=0.1
or
\tau=1
. A visual representation of the performance comparison is given in Figure
2
. From the convergence plots we see C-FISTA would often display asymptotic linear convergence when the group sizes were 100 and 200 while FISTA would converge sub-linearly. When the group size was 10, irrespective of the dimension of the data matrix, C-FISTA and FISTA were comparable and demonstrated asymptotic linear convergence; however, with
\tau=1
, C-FISTA converged between 300 and 500 iterations sooner on average then FISTA in this setting. These experiments demonstrate that C-FISTA maintains linear convergence properties even when some of the stated assumptions are not strictly met and showcases the robustness of the parameter
\tau
to miss-specification.

6.3
C-FISTA for Sparse-Group Logistic Regression

In this section we describe how to apply C-FISTA to solve the sparse-group logistic regression model,

\begin{array}[]{lll}&\min&\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ln\left(1+\exp\left(-y_{i}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}_{i}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}+b\right)\right)\right)+\gamma_{1}\sum_{j\in{\cal G}}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(j)\|+\gamma_{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|_{1}\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},b\in\mathbb{R}.\end{array}

As in the Lasso formulations, a decomposition must be chosen to tackle the regression model with C-FISTA. For continuity with the previous discussion, we will utilize the decomposition
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}
and
H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ln(1+\exp(-y_{i}\langle(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}_{i}^{\top},1)^{\top},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\rangle)).
Under these definitions for
H
and
B
, the sparse-group Lasso and sparse-group logistic regression models only differ with respect to their strongly convex term, so the procedure for solving the sparse-group logistic regression model with C-FISTA is very similar to Algorithm
3
. Only two alterations to Algorithm
3
are required to solve the sparse-group logistic regression model. One, we must update
d
in Algorithm
3
to be,

\mbox{\boldmath$d$}=\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla H(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k})=\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}+\frac{1}{mL}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{y_{i}\exp(-y_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}_{i}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}+b))}{1+\exp(-y_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}_{i}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}+b))}\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath$a$}_{i}\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(25)

and second we require new estimates for the bounds of the Lipschitz and strong convexity constants
L
and
\mu
. Updating
d
is straightforward, but obtaining tight upper and lower bounds on the Lipschitz and strong convexity constants can become a challenging task as mentioned previously. Algorithm
4
presents C-FISTA for solving the sparse-group logistic regression model. In our implementation of Algorithm
4
, we fixed the Lipschitz and strong convexity parameters though backtracking procedures could be utilized.

Algorithm 4

C-FISTA for
(SGLR)

Input:
Constants
\mu
and
L
; penalty parameters
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}>0
; vector partition
\mathcal{G}
.

Step 0.
Choose any
(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{0},b^{0},\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Let
k:=0
.

Step 1.
Let

\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{k}:=\frac{1}{1+\theta}\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k}\\
b^{k}\end{pmatrix}+\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{k}.

Step 2.
For
j\in\mathcal{G}
: define
\mathbf{\Omega}(j):=\|\d{(}j)+\textbf{Th}_{\gamma_{2}/L}(-\d{(}j))\|
and,

\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(j)^{k+1}\hskip-1.4457pt:=\hskip-2.168pt\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\hskip-1.4457pt0,&\hskip-3.61371pt\mbox{ if $\mathbf{\Omega}(j)\leq\gamma_{1}/L$};\\
\hskip-5.05942pt\left(\frac{\|d(j)+{\rm Th}_{\gamma_{2}/L}(-d(j))\|-\gamma_{1}/L)}{\|d(j)+{\rm Th}_{\gamma_{2}/L}(-d(j))\|}\right)\left(d(j)+{\rm Th}_{\gamma_{2}/L}(-d(j))\right),&\hskip-3.61371pt\mbox{ if $\mathbf{\Omega}(j)>\gamma_{1}/L$};\end{array}\right.

where
d
is as defined in (
25
) and
d(j)
is the subvector of
d
corresponding to the subvector
x(j)
.

Step 3.

b^{k+1}=y^{k+1}_{n+1}+\frac{1}{mL}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{y_{i}\exp(-y_{i}(a_{i}^{\top}y^{k}+b))}{1+\exp(-y_{i}(a_{i}^{\top}y^{k}+b))}.

Step 4.

z^{k+1}:=(1-\theta)z^{k}+\theta y^{k}+{\alpha}\left[\begin{pmatrix}x^{k+1}\\
b^{k+1}\end{pmatrix}-y^{k}\right],

where
\theta=\sqrt{\mu/L}
and
\alpha=\sqrt{L/\mu}
.

Step 5.
Let
k:=k+1
; return to Step 1 until convergence.

Note, computing
b^{k+1}
at each iteration is simple relative to
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{k+1}
because the subprobem (
4
) is decomposable into a minimization over
x
and
b
respectively. Since the minimization over
b
contains no regularization terms, the first-order optimality conditions provide a simple update for
b
.

The astute observer will note
H
is strictly instead of strongly convex making
\mu=0
and leaving the necessary assumptions unsatisfied. Though this is technically true, C-FISTA is undeterred. As in the underdetermined group Lasso tests, this decomposition showcases the robustness of C-FISTA when the assumptions are not formally met. It is clear the sparse-group logistic regression model could be equivalently expressed as a constrained problem; therefore, by rewriting the model as an equivalent constrained problem,
H
would become strongly convex on the problem domain; however, as is seen in Figure

, C-FISTA outperforms both SLEP and FISTA without solving an equivalent model where the assumptions are formally satisfied.

6.5
C-FISTA for Geometric Programming

In this section we discuss how to solve an instance of the geometric programming model described in Section
3
. In particular, we focus on the group regularized geometric program,

\begin{array}[]{lll}&\min&\;\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}x_{1}^{a_{k1}}x_{2}^{a_{k2}}\ldots x_{n}^{a_{kn}}+\gamma\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(j)\|_{2},\\
&\mbox{s.t}&\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in{\cal X},\end{array}

(26)

where
{\cal X}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{0}\|_{2}\leq\Delta\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
with
\Delta>0
and
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\gamma>0
,
c_{k}>0
and
\mathbf{a_{k}}:=(a_{k1},\ldots,a_{kn})^{\top}\leq 0
for
k=1,\ldots,K
, and
\mathcal{G}
is a non-overlapping partition of the vector
x
. Letting,

H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}):=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}e^{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}_{k}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}},\;\;\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}):=\left(\ln(x_{1}),\ldots,\ln(x_{n})\right)^{\top},\;\;\;R(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}):=\gamma\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(j)\|_{2},

we can rewrite (
26
) in the format of our standard composite optimization model. From the characteristics of
{\cal X}
it follows
H
is gradient Lipschitz on the problem domain. Additionally,
H
will be strongly convex over the domain provided the vectors
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{K}\}
span
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note, this is a fairly weak condition if
K>n
. Additionally, similar to our underdetermined group Lasso discussion, although constants
r
and
\tau
do not exist to satisfy the necessary constraints over the span of the constraint set, such constants exist which satisfy the necessary inequalities over the constraint set. By Taylor’s theorem and simple bounding we can see,

\tau\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\|^{2}\leq\|\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})-\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})\|^{2}\leq r\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\|^{2},\;\;\forall\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\in{\cal X},

with
r=1/\min_{1\leq i\leq n}\left[(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{0})_{i}-\Delta\right]^{2}
and
\tau=1/\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left[(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{0})_{i}+\Delta\right]^{2}
. Thus, with these values for
r
and
\tau
, along with appropriate estimates for the other parameters
\mu,L
and
\xi
, C-FISTA can be employed to solve instances of this subclass of geometric programs. The only difference in implementing C-FISTA for this problem class is a constrained optimization model of the form given in (
23
) must be solved to compute the proximal mapping. In our numerical experiments, we apply a simple projected gradient method to solve this subproblem to within a specified tolerance. We now present the numerical results from the geometric programming model.

7
Conclusions

In this paper we developed an accelerated composite version of FISTA, C-FISTA, which handles the composite optimization model (
1
) and is a generalization of GFISTA
CC19
;
CP16
. We proved global linear convergence for C-FISTA without having a strongly convex objective function, and demonstrated through Fenchel duality the breadth of convex models which are solvable via C-FISTA in Section
4
. In Section
6
, we demonstrated through several numerical experiments C-FISTA was able to obtain linear convergence even in settings where the formal assumptions were not satisfied. Furthermore, C-FISTA outperformed ADMM, the software package SLEP and the seminal FISTA algorithm in both Lasso and logistic regression models.

The following lines of directions could be of interest as future research topics. First, the conditions underlying our global linear convergence results may be weakened. Second, one may further study possible
adaptive
schemes to implement C-FISTA without requiring the exact knowledge of the problem parameters. Finally, it is interesting to study how the new algorithm behaves beyond the scope of convex optimization.
