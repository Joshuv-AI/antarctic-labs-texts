Distributed Bayesian Learning of Dynamic States
Mert Kayaalp, Virginia Bordignon, Stefan Vlaski, Vincenzo Matta, Ali H. Sayed

Abstract
This work studies networked agents cooperating to track a dynamical state of nature under partial information. The proposed algorithm is a distributed Bayesian filtering algorithm for finite-state hidden Markov models (HMMs). It can be used for sequential state estimation tasks, as well as for modeling opinion formation over social networks under dynamic environments. We show that the disagreement with the optimal centralized solution is asymptotically bounded for the class of geometrically ergodic state transition models, which includes rapidly changing models. We also derive recursions for calculating the probability of error and establish convergence under Gaussian observation models. Simulations are provided to illustrate the theory and to compare against alternative approaches.



I
Introduction

Distributed inference refers to the problem where a collection of agents works collaboratively in order to estimate a hidden variable of interest. This problem is of fundamental importance to the design of distributed systems, as well to the study of opinion formation over social networks. The goal in the first case is to devise communication/computation-efficient algorithms that approach centralized levels of performance by relying solely on localized agent interactions. In comparison, the goal in the second case is to reason about behavioral phenomena occurring during social learning processes.

The hidden variable (also called state or hypothesis) that the agents are interested in tracking is time-varying in many scenarios, such as the position of a moving object, the concentration of air pollutants, and the product quality of a brand. In all situations, the agents will attempt to cooperatively track the dynamic state by using observations emitted by the underlying physical systems.

This setting is general enough and can be used in many engineering applications, including target tracking, environmental monitoring, and opinion formation over networks. For example, consider an economic network where the individual agents are trying to decide which currency (e.g., USD, EUR, CHF) is the best option to buy now. The optimal choice (true hypothesis) can be changing rapidly. Most of the literature on social learning ignores the dynamic nature of the truth, or assumes slow transition models.

In this work, we propose a networked filtering algorithm to track the state of a general hidden Markov model (HMM). We also analyze the performance and steady-state behavior of the resulting distributed strategy. In this process, we clarify questions about the benefit of cooperation and the nature of equilibria in social networks under dynamic environments. More specifically, the following is a list of the main contributions:

•

In Sec.
IV-A
, we propose an HMM filtering algorithm for multi-agent networks. The algorithm requires only one round of communication between agents per state change. Moreover, it utilizes the knowledge of the transition model, which allows it to track highly dynamic states.

•

In Sec.
V
, we study the deviation of the proposed algorithm from the optimal centralized strategy defined in Sec.
III-A
. Geometric ergodicity is the only assumption on the transition model. The specialization of the results to the single-agent case is also another contribution to the Bayesian filtering literature.

•

In Sec.
VI
, we provide recursive expressions for the probability of error across the network for the binary hypothesis testing case. Furthermore, under Gaussian data distributions, we obtain an asymptotic convergence result in
distribution
. The result implies that the agents attain steady-state probability of errors, which can vary across the agents depending on their centrality.

•

In Sec.
VII
, we support the theoretical results with simulations. Furthermore, we compare the proposed algorithm to alternative methods, which are described in Sec.
IV-B
.

III
Problem Formulation

We consider a network of
K

\mathcal{N}
, that are cooperating to track some
dynamic
state of nature, denoted by
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}
at time

with each other while respecting a graph topology restricting the communication. The belief

H

\theta\in\Theta=\{0,1,..,H-1\}

\mu_{k,i}(\theta)
represents the confidence level that agent
k
has at time
i
about
\theta
being the true hypothesis
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}
(which is also assumed to belong to the set
\Theta
). The true hypothesis is a random variable and it will be assumed to evolve according to some Markov chain, known to all agents. We use the following notation for the transition model:

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}(\theta_{i}|\theta_{i-1})\triangleq\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=\theta_{i}|\bm{\theta}_{i-1}^{\circ}=\theta_{i-1}).

(2)

The problem setting is as follows. At each time instant
i
, each agent
k
receives a partially informative observation
\bm{\xi}_{k,i}
about the true hypothesis
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}
. Conditioned on
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}
, the observation is distributed according to some likelihood function known to agent
k
, and denoted by
L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ})
. These agent-specific likelihoods can be probability density or mass functions depending on whether the observations are continuous or discrete. In the sequel, for ease of notation, we assume the observations are continuous. Nevertheless, our analysis is also valid for discrete observations with proper adjustments, e.g., by changing integrals to summations. Next, we state some common assumptions.

Assumption 1

(
Independent observations
[
8
,
32
]
)
.

Conditioned on the true state, the observations are independent over space. More specifically, let
\bm{\xi}_{i}\triangleq\{\bm{\xi}_{k,i}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, collect all observations from across the agents at time

. Then, the joint likelihood is given by,

\displaystyle L(\bm{\xi}_{i}|\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}).

(3)

∎

In this work, agents will be required to communicate only once with their neighbors per iteration. The underlying communication topology is assumed to satisfy the following condition.

Assumption 2

(
Strongly connected graph

[
8
,
32
,
24
]
)
.

The graph topology is strongly connected
[
34
]
, which means that there exists a path between any pair of agents
(k,\ell)
, and, moreover, there exists at least one agent

with a self-loop (i.e.,

). Under these conditions, the combination matrix

turns out to be primitive. Here, the entry

is the weight that agent

uses to scale information sent by agent
\ell
. This weight will be positive if, and only if, agent

is in the neighborhood of

\displaystyle A\mathds{1}_{K}=\mathds{1}_{K},\quad A=A^{\T}.

(4)

Strong connectivity causes the information to disperse throughout the entire network given sufficient iterations. When the true hypothesis is fixed (i.e.,

III-A

Optimal Centralized Belief Recursion

Let us denote the observation history of all agents across the network up to time
i
by
\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\triangleq\{\bm{\xi}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{i}
. Likewise, let us denote the posterior distribution (or belief), which is a probability mass function (pmf) due to the assumed finite state-space model, by the notation:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\star}(\theta_{i})\triangleq\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=\theta_{i}|\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{i}).

(5)

It is known that the above distribution satisfies the optimal Bayesian filtering recursion
[
12
]
:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\star}(\theta_{i})\propto L(\bm{\xi}_{i}|\theta_{i})\bm{\eta}_{i}^{\star}(\theta_{i}),

(6)

where
\bm{\eta}_{i}^{\star}(\theta_{i})
is the time-adjusted prior defined by

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{i}^{\star}(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=\theta_{i}|\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta_{i-1}\in\Theta}\mathbb{T}(\theta_{i}|\theta_{i-1})\bm{\mu}_{i-1}^{\star}(\theta_{i-1}).

(7)

Once the posterior is updated by (
5
), the state estimator at time
i
is obtained from the maximum a-posteriori construction:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{\star}\triangleq\argmax_{\theta_{i}\in\Theta}\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\star}(\theta_{i})

(8)

The main challenge with this solution method is that it requires a fusion center to gather all data from across time and agents. In Section
V
, we will examine how close the beliefs generated by the proposed decentralized algorithm will get to the above centralized posterior given by (
6
)–(
III-A
).

IV
Decentralized Bayesian Filtering

The centralized solution (
6
)–(
III-A
) can be disadvantageous for various reasons: (i) collecting all data at a single fusion location makes the system vulnerable with a single point of failure; (ii) the agents may be reluctant to share their raw data with a remote central processor for privacy or security reasons; and (iii) communications back and forth with a remote fusion center is costly. For these reasons, we pursue instead a decentralized solution that is able to approach the performance of the centralized solution. In the decentralized approach, agents will only share data with their immediate neighbors; actually, the agents will not be required to share their raw data but only their updated belief vectors. The resulting solution will be more robust to node or link failure and more communication efficient, and will lead to an effective solution method.

IV-A

Diffusion HMM Filtering

The streaming observations arriving at each agent are generally only partially informative about the true state of nature,
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}
. For this reason, agents will need to cooperate with their neighbors, thus leading to a learning mechanism that allows information to diffuse through the network for enhanced performance. To do so, we propose a
social
Bayesian filtering algorithm where cooperation among agents takes advantage of the notion of diffusion learning (see, e.g.,
[
34
,
36
]
).

Specifically, at each time instant
i
, every agent
k
first time-adjusts or evolves its belief from
i-1
, denoted by
\bm{\mu}_{k,i-1}(\theta_{i-1})
, via the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation
[
12
]
and generates an updated prior denoted by
\bm{\eta}_{k,i}(\theta_{i})
:

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{k,i}(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta_{i-1}\in\Theta}\mathbb{T}(\theta_{i}|\theta_{i-1})\bm{\mu}_{k,i-1}(\theta_{i-1})\qquad\text{(Evolve)}

(9)

This relation is motivated by the optimal update (
III-A
), except that optimal beliefs are replaced by their local versions at agent
k
. In the next step, agents seek to incorporate the information from their newly arrived private observations. This can be achieved by considering the following regularized optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\psi\in\Delta_{H}}\Big\{D_{\text{KL}}(\psi||\bm{\eta}_{k,i})-\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{\psi}\log L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta_{i})\Big\}

(10)

where
\Delta_{H}
is the probability simplex of dimension
H
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\psi}
is the expectation computed with respect to
\psi
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\psi}\log L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta_{i})\triangleq\sum_{\theta_{i}\in\Theta}\psi(\theta_{i})\log L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta_{i}).

(11)

The objective function in (
10
) consists of two terms. The first term is the KL-divergence term that penalizes the disagreement with the time-adjusted prior
\{\bm{\eta}_{k,i}\}
. The second term corresponds to the log-likelihood of the observation
\bm{\xi}_{k,i}
averaged over the hypotheses with respect to
\psi
. The cost in (
10
) then seeks to minimize disagreement with the prior while maximizing the likelihood of the observation; the two terms are coupled by a regularization parameter
\gamma>0
. As we show in the sequel, different special cases of the problem setting might necessitate different
\gamma
values, e.g.,
\gamma=K,\gamma=1
. Therefore, we continue with a general parameter
\gamma>0
. The objective function in (
10
) can be expanded as

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta_{i}\in\Theta}\psi(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle\Big(\log\frac{\psi(\theta_{i})}{\bm{\eta}_{k,i}(\theta_{i})}-\gamma\log L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta_{i})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta_{i}\in\Theta}\psi(\theta_{i})\log\frac{\psi(\theta_{i})}{\bm{\eta}_{k,i}(\theta_{i})(L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta_{i}))^{\gamma}}

(12)

The RHS of (
IV-A
) is a KL-divergence under a proper normalization. Minimizing it results in the following
local

\gamma
-scaled Bayesian adaptation step for each agent:

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle\propto(L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta_{i}))^{\gamma}\bm{\eta}_{k,i}(\theta_{i})\qquad\text{(Adapt)}

(13)

where
\gamma>0
scales the likelihood of the new observation against prior information. After agents independently obtain their intermediate beliefs
\bm{\psi}_{k,i}
according to (
9
) and (
13
), they exchange these beliefs with their neighbors. Each agent
k
will then need to fuse the beliefs received from the neighbors, and one way to do so is to seek the belief vector
\mu
that solves
[
37
,
38
]
:

\displaystyle\min_{\mu\in\Delta_{H}}\left\{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}a_{\ell k}D_{\text{KL}}(\mu||\bm{\psi}_{\ell,i})\right\}.

(14)

This objective function penalizes the average disagreement with the neighbors’ intermediate beliefs and it can be expanded as

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta_{i}\in\Theta}\mu(\theta_{i})\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}

\displaystyle a_{\ell k}\log\frac{\mu(\theta_{i})}{\bm{\psi}_{\ell,i}(\theta_{i})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta_{i}\in\Theta}\mu(\theta_{i})\log\frac{\mu(\theta_{i})}{\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}[\bm{\psi}_{\ell,i}(\theta_{i})]^{a_{\ell k}}}

(15)

where the term on the RHS of (
IV-A
) can be seen as a KL divergence under proper normalization, whose minimizer is given by the following geometric-average combination:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}\big(\bm{\psi}_{\ell,i}(\theta_{i})\big)^{a_{\ell k}}\quad\text{(Combine)}.

(16)

Exchanging and combining the beliefs repeatedly allows the local information to diffuse through the network. The complete procedure leads to the

Regularity condition).

The absolute log-likelihood functions are uniformly bounded over their support for all agents:

\displaystyle\big|\log L_{k}(\xi|\theta)\big|\leq C_{L},\qquad\forall k\in\mathcal{N},\theta\in\Theta.

(26)

∎

Assumption
4
implies that the likelihood functions do not get arbitrarily close to zero or arbitrarily large in their support. This ensures that each private signal
\xi
has bounded informativeness. For example, discrete signal space models or truncated Gaussian likelihoods satisfy this assumption.

Next, we introduce conditions on the transition model, which will play a crucial part in the analysis.

V-A

Transition Model

We assume that the transition Markov chain is
irreducible
and
aperiodic

[
42
, Chapter 2]
. This means that there exists a constant integer
n>0
such that for any two hypotheses
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{n}(\theta|\theta^{\prime})>0,

(27)

where
\mathbb{T}^{n}
is the
n
-fold application of the transition kernel. This condition also implies that the Markov chain is
ergodic
because the number of hypotheses
H
is finite
[
42
, Chapter 2]
. In other words, repeated application of the transition kernel
\mathbb{T}
will converge to a limiting distribution regardless of the initial input distribution. More formally, for any input distribution
\mu\in\Delta_{H}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\mathbb{T}^{n}(\theta|\theta^{\prime})\mu(\theta^{\prime})=\pi(\theta),

(28)

where
\pi
is the Perron vector of the
H\times H
transition matrix
T\triangleq[\mathbb{T}(\theta|\theta^{\prime})]
. In this work, we consider the
geometrically ergodic

[
12
, Chapter 2]
subclass of transition models. In the following, we define these models using the strong-data processing inequality (SDPI)
[
43
]
.

Strong-data processing inequality (SDPI)
[
43
]
: Consider any two discrete distributions over
\Theta
,
\mu^{a}
and
\mu^{b}
, satisfying
0<D_{\textup{KL}}(\mu^{a}||\mu^{b})<\infty
, and introduce their time-adjusted versions according to the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation as in (
17
):

\displaystyle\eta^{a}(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta_{i-1}\in\Theta}\mathbb{T}(\theta_{i}|\theta_{i-1})\mu^{a}(\theta_{i-1})

(29)

and similarly for
\eta^{b}
. Then, the SDPI states that:

\displaystyle D_{\textup{KL}}(\eta^{a}||\eta^{b})\leq\kappa_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{T})D_{\textup{KL}}(\mu^{a}||\mu^{b})

(30)

where
\kappa_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{T})\in[0,1]
is a contraction coefficient defined as

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{T})\triangleq\sup_{\mu^{a},\mu^{b}}\frac{D_{\textup{KL}}(\eta^{a}||\eta^{b})}{D_{\textup{KL}}(\mu^{a}||\mu^{b})}.

(31)

Observe that the coefficient is only dependent on the transition model, and is not a function of the input distributions. An upper bound on
\kappa_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{T})
is given by the Dobrushin’s contraction coefficient
[
44
,
43
,
12
]
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\kappa(\mathbb{T})\triangleq\sup_{\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime}\in\Theta}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\Big|\mathbb{T}(\theta|\theta^{\prime})-\mathbb{T}(\theta|\theta^{\prime\prime})\Big|\quad\in[0,1],

(32)

It is known that
[
43
]
:

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{T})\leq\kappa(\mathbb{T}),\qquad\kappa_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{T})=1\Longleftrightarrow\kappa(\mathbb{T})=1.

(33)

For example,

•

If the transition model is a binary symmetric channel:

\mathbb{T}(\theta_{i}|\theta_{i-1})=\begin{cases}1-\alpha,&\theta_{i}=\theta_{i-1}\\
\alpha,&\theta_{i}\neq\theta_{i-1}\end{cases},

(34)

then
\kappa(\mathbb{T})=|1-2\alpha|

[
43
]
. Notice that this is a symmetric function around the transition probability
\alpha=0.5
, e.g.,
\alpha=0.2
and
\alpha=0.8
yield the same coefficient.

•

The coefficient
\kappa(\mathbb{T})=0
if, and only if
[
12
, Ch. 2]
,

\mathbb{T}(\theta_{i}|\theta_{i-1})=\pi(\theta_{i}).

(35)

Note that this implies, for any
\mu\in\Delta_{H}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta_{i-1}\in\Theta}\mathbb{T}(\theta_{i}|\theta_{i-1})\mu(\theta_{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta_{i-1}\in\Theta}\pi(\theta_{i})\mu(\theta_{i-1})

\displaystyle=\pi(\theta_{i})

(36)

In other words, the transition kernel will output the same distribution
\pi(\theta)
regardless of the input distribution
\mu(\theta)
. Observe that the rapidly mixing binary symmetric channel with transition probability
\alpha=0.5
is an example of this case.

In general, for two input distributions
\mu^{a}
and
\mu^{b}
, the output distributions resulting from an
n
-fold application of the transition kernel, i.e.,

\displaystyle\eta_{n}^{a}(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta_{i-n}\in\Theta}\mathbb{T}^{n}(\theta_{i}|\theta_{i-n})\mu^{a}(\theta_{i-n}),

(37)

and similarly for
\eta_{n}^{b}
, satisfy the following SDPI:

\displaystyle D_{\textup{KL}}(\eta_{n}^{a}||\eta_{n}^{b})\leq(\kappa(\mathbb{T}))^{n}D_{\textup{KL}}(\mu^{a}||\mu^{b})

(38)

It is clear that if
\kappa(\mathbb{T})<1
, then the disagreement between any two input distributions will approach 0 exponentially fast. Transition models for which
\kappa(\mathbb{T})<1
are said to be geometrically ergodic
[
12
, Ch. 2]
. It is seen from (
38
) that the coefficient
\kappa(\mathbb{T})
is a measure of how rapidly the initial conditions are forgotten. In particular, as
\kappa(\mathbb{T})\to 0
, forgetting is faster.

Assumption 5

(
Transition model).

The transition model
\mathbb{T}
is assumed to be geometrically ergodic, i.e.,
\kappa(\mathbb{T})<1
. ∎

The class of geometric ergodic transition models comprises a large group of transition models. For instance, non-deterministic binary symmetric channels, i.e., with a transition probability
\alpha\in(0,1)
, are geometrically ergodic. Moreover, transition matrices with all positive elements, and in general, those that satisfy a minorization condition
[
12
, Theorem 2.7.4]
are examples of geometrically ergodic transition models. However, the geometrically ergodic class excludes some transition models such as the fixed hypothesis case, where
\kappa(\mathbb{T})=1
. We elaborate more on this issue in the sequel.

\displaystyle J_{k,i}

and

Our first result establishes that the disagreement between the centralized and distributed solutions is asymptotically bounded for all agents in the network.

Theorem 1

(

For each agent

,

and

\displaystyle\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{J}_{k,i}\leq\frac{2\kappa(\mathbb{T})\sqrt{K}\gamma\lambda C_{L}}{1-\kappa(\mathbb{T})}

where

.

Proof.

See Appendix

.
∎

Swift and random changes in the environment can prevent a strongly-connected network from approaching the performance of the centralized solution close enough—especially when the network is sparse and it takes more time for the information to diffuse to all agents than the rate at which the state is changing. Therefore, the bounds in Theorem

are not generally close to 0. The following remarks are now in place. Simulations that support these observations appear in Sec.
VII
.

•

Matching the centralized strategy
: The bounds (
41
) and (
42
) will be tight when the distributed solution reduces to the centralized implementation, which happens with a uniformly weighted fully-connected network (
a_{\ell k}=1/K

\forall\ell,k\in\mathcal{N}
,
\rho_{2}=0
), same initial priors (
\mu_{0}^{\star}=\mu_{k,0}
,
\forall k\in\mathcal{N}
), and
\gamma=K
as shown in (
•
‣
IV-A
). In this case, the upper bounds will become zero as expected.

•

Stability
: The bounds (
41
) and (
42
) are independent of the initial beliefs as long as Assumption
3
is satisfied. Indeed, geometric ergodicity is sufficient to asymptotically forget the initial conditions. In this way, the filter is robust to incorrect initializations.

•

Network connectivity
: Note that as
\gamma\to K
, the bounds become proportional to
\rho_{2}
, the mixing rate of the graph. This aspect emphasizes the benefit of cooperation. Highly-connected graphs, with small
\rho_{2}
, will be closer to the centralized solution while sparse networks or non-cooperative agents will have higher deviation.

•

Network size
: The bounds are also proportional to the number of agents. The disagreement between the centralized solution and the individual agents increases with the square-root of the network size. Note that this does not mean agents would perform worse if new agents join the network. It is the
relative
performance compared to the optimal solution, which has access to data from all agents, that could decrease.

•

Ergodicity
: Notice that when
\kappa(\mathbb{T})\to 0
, from the bound (
42
), it is obvious that
\widetilde{J}_{k,i}\to 0
. This is anticipated because if the coefficient
\kappa(\mathbb{T})=0
, the time-adjustment steps of the centralized and decentralized strategies (

) and (

) become

\displaystyle\eta_{i}^{\star}(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\sum_{\theta_{i-1}\in\Theta}\!\!\!\mathbb{T}(\theta_{i}|\theta_{i-1})\bm{\mu}_{i-1}^{\star}(\theta_{i-1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:pi_transition}}}{{=}}\pi(\theta_{i}),

\displaystyle\eta_{k,i}(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\sum_{\theta_{i-1}\in\Theta}\!\!\!\mathbb{T}(\theta_{i}|\theta_{i-1})\bm{\mu}_{k,i-1}(\theta_{i-1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:pi_transition}}}{{=}}\pi(\theta_{i}),

(43)

regardless of the input distributions
\bm{\mu}_{i-1}^{\star}(\theta_{i-1})
and
\bm{\mu}_{k,i-1}(\theta_{i-1})
. As a result,

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{k,i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:prior_risk}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{i-1}}D_{\textup{KL}}(\bm{\eta}_{i}^{\star}||\bm{\eta}_{k,i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:two_time_adj_priors}}}{{=}}0.

Therefore, the bound (

) captures the effect of the ergodicity of the transition model via the ergodicity coefficient

accurately. In particular, the results imply that the bound is tight for rapidly changing binary symmetric transition models where

.

•

Informativeness of observations
: Nonetheless, the bounds fall short in capturing the effect of the observations. For example, if the true hypothesis is fixed, then the transition model
\mathbb{T}
satisfies (

\displaystyle\eta^{a}(\theta_{i})

and similarly for

\displaystyle D_{\textup{KL}}(\eta^{a}||\eta^{b})=D_{\textup{KL}}(\mu^{a}||\mu^{b}),

and consequently,

. Since this transition model is not geometrically ergodic, the bounds do not cover this case. However, it is known from the standard social learning literature
[

,
26
,
24

]
that when the observations of the agents are informative enough, that is to say, the observations provide sufficient information about the underlying state
2
2

2

For several works, e.g.,
[

•

Single-agent case
: In fact, the distinction between the ergodicity of the transition model and the informativeness of the observations arises in the analysis of single-agent strategies as well. The stability of the algorithm would refer to the conditions under which a wrongly initialized belief converges to the true posterior distribution in HMMs. In the notation of this work, this is the special case of
\gamma=K=1
and
\rho_{2}=0
. Remember that in this special case the proposed diffusion HMM strategy is equivalent to a traditional optimal Bayes filter
[
12

. One criterion that the literature on the single-agent case uses is whether the total variation distance between the true posterior and the agent’s belief vanishes in the mean asymptotically, i.e., whether (setting the single-agent index
k=1
),

\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}\Big[\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\big|\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\star}(\theta)-\bm{\mu}_{1,i}(\theta)\big|\Big]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}0,

(47)

when the initial belief of the agent is not accurate, i.e., when

\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\circ}=\theta)=\mu_{0}^{\star}(\theta)\neq\mu_{1,0}(\theta).

(48)

The total variation distance in (
47
) can vanish because of two mechanisms: either the observations are sufficiently informative about the true state, or the transition model is sufficiently ergodic. Even though there are some works that focus on the informativeness of observations
[
45
]
, most of the results in the literature rely on ergodicity to establish stability
[
46
,
47
]
. Similarly, Theorem
1
also depends on the ergodicity of the transition model. In particular, reference
[
47
]
studies the stability via the Dobrushin coefficient

and concludes that as long as
\kappa(\mathbb{T})<1/2
, regardless of the observation model, (
47
) is satisfied. In comparison, our results are in terms of KL-divergences, but they can be expressed in terms of total variation distances if we use Pinsker’s inequality
[
48

. For the single-agent case, we get:

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\big|\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\star}(\theta)-\bm{\mu}_{1,i}(\theta)\big|\leq

\displaystyle\big(2D_{\textup{KL}}(\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\star}||\bm{\mu}_{1,i})\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(49)

If we take the expectations, this relation implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}\Big[\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\big|\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\star}(\theta)-\bm{\mu}_{1,i}(\theta)\big|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\!\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}\big(2D_{\textup{KL}}(\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\star}||\bm{\mu}_{1,i})\!\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\big(2J_{1,i}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(50)

where
(a)
follows from Jensen’s inequality. Finally, taking the limit of both sides, we arrive at

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\!\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}\Big[\!\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\!\big|\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\star}(\theta)-\bm{\mu}_{1,i}(\theta)\big|\!\Big]\!\!

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\lim_{i\to\infty}\big(J_{1,i}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\;0,

(51)

where
(b)
follows from the fact that the bound (

and
\rho_{2}=0
. This is a more general result, namely that the single-agent Bayesian filter is stable whenever

).

VI
Probability of Error and Convergence

The previous section analyzed the closeness of the diffusion HMM strategy to the optimal centralized solution. In this section, we study the error probability for each agent. There are a few results for the probability of error of HMM filtering, even in the single agent case. One notable result is
[
49
]
where error recursions are obtained for the single-agent binary hypothesis setting case. In a similar spirit, we will obtain recursive equations for the probability density functions (pdfs) that capture the stochastic behavior of the underlying system. This will not only provide recursive formulas for the error probabilities, but will also allow us to deduce asymptotic properties for the diffusion HMM strategy. Similar to
[
49
]
, for this section, we shift our focus to the binary hypothesis setting, i.e., throughout this section, we set
H=2
and
\Theta=\{0,1\}
. We do not need to restrict ourselves to bounded log-likelihood signal models in this section, as was the case in Assumption
4
. In this setting, the MAP-classifier at agent
k
at time instant
i
becomes

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{k,i}=\begin{cases}1,\quad\text{if}\quad\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(1)>\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(0)\\
0,\quad\text{if}\quad\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(0)\geq\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(1)\end{cases}.

(52)

This estimator is equivalent to

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{k,i}=\begin{cases}1,\quad\text{if}\quad\bm{w}_{k,i}>0\\
0,\quad\text{if}\quad\bm{w}_{k,i}\leq 0\end{cases}

(53)

in terms of the log-belief ratio
\bm{w}_{k,i}
defined as

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{k,i}\triangleq\log\frac{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(1)}{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(0)}.

(54)

As such, the probability of error for agent
k
at time instant

\displaystyle p_{k,i}\triangleq\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=1,\bm{w}_{k,i}\leq 0)+\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=0,\bm{w}_{k,i}>0).

(55)

Let

denote the probability density function of the joint variable

for agent

at time

. Note that the joint variable

mixes a discrete and a continuous random variable. Here, we use the general definition of density, i.e., density is the Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to a measure. The corresponding measure for

is the product measure of the counting measure for
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}
and Lebesgue measure for
\bm{w}_{k,i}

50
]

\displaystyle f_{k,i}(\theta,w_{k})dw_{k}=\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=\theta,\bm{w}_{k,i}\in(w_{k},w_{k}+dw_{k}))

In this way, the probability of error (

\displaystyle p_{k,i}

\displaystyle f_{i}(\theta,w)dw_{1}dw_{2}\cdots dw_{K}\triangleq\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=\theta,\bm{w}_{i}\in(w,w+dw)),

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{i}\triangleq\text{col}\{\bm{w}_{\ell,i}\}_{\ell=1}^{K},\quad w\triangleq\text{col}\{w_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{K}.

\displaystyle f_{k,i}(\theta,w_{k})=\int\!\!...\!\!\int f_{i}(\theta,w)dw_{1}\cdots dw_{k-1}dw_{k+1}\cdots dw_{K}.

is utilized by agent
k
as well as by its neighbors in the same iteration. On the other hand, for consensus, under Assumption
1
,

\displaystyle S_{i}^{(\theta)}(w,w^{\prime})dW

\displaystyle=\prod_{\ell=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\bm{w}_{\ell,i}\in(w_{\ell},w_{\ell}+dw_{\ell})|\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=\theta,\bm{w}_{i-1}=w^{\prime})

(69)

since the fresh data is used by the observing agent only. In other words, in the consensus implementation (
24
), each log-belief
\bm{w}_{k,i}
is a function of that agent’s observation
\bm{\xi}_{k,i}
only (given
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}
and
\bm{w}_{i-1}
) and does not depend on the observations at the other agents at that time. This distinction between diffusion and consensus can be seen from (
62
) and (
65
).

Now, marginalizing (
VI
) with respect to the state
\theta^{\prime}
and the log-belief ratio
w^{\prime}
at the earlier time instant yields the following temporal recursion for the joint density in (
58
):

\displaystyle f_{i}(\theta,w)dW=\sum_{\theta^{\prime}}\mathbb{T}(\theta|\theta^{\prime})\Bigg[\int_{w^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=\theta,\bm{w}_{i}\in(w,w+dw),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\bm{\theta}_{i-1}^{\circ}=\theta^{\prime},\bm{w}_{i-1}\in(w^{\prime},w^{\prime}+dw^{\prime}))\Bigg],

(70)

which implies that

\displaystyle f_{i}(\theta,w)\!=\!\sum_{\theta^{\prime}}\mathbb{T}(\theta|\theta^{\prime})\Bigg[\!\!\int_{w^{\prime}}S_{i}^{(\theta)}(w,w^{\prime})f_{i-1}(\theta^{\prime},w^{\prime})dW^{\prime}\Bigg].

(71)

To summarize, the probability of error at each time instant
i
can be computed by (i) using (
71
) to find the joint density at time
i
, (ii) marginalizing the joint density to find the agent-specific density by (
60
), and finally (iii) integrating the agent density as in (
57
).

Remark 2

(
Evaluation of integrals).

Finding closed-form expressions to the integral expressions (e.g., (
71
), (
60
)) might not be feasible. One can apply numerical integration methods such as Monte Carlo techniques
[
51
]
to compute the desired integrals. ∎

The analysis until here holds for general transition and likelihood models. However, the kernel
S_{i}^{(\theta)}(w,w^{\prime})
might not be obtained in closed form in general. Therefore, for stronger results, we focus on Gaussian likelihood models in the next section.

VI-A

Gaussian Likelihoods

Thus, let us consider now Gaussian models of the form:

\displaystyle L_{k}(\xi_{k,i}|\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\text{exp}\Big\{-\frac{(\xi_{k,i}-\zeta(1))^{2}}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}\Big\}

(72)

\displaystyle L_{k}(\xi_{k,i}|\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\text{exp}\Big\{-\frac{(\xi_{k,i}-\zeta(0))^{2}}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}\Big\}

where the means are assumed to satisfy
\zeta(0)=-\zeta(1)=\zeta
for a constant value
\zeta\neq 0
, and where the agent-specific variances satisfy
\sigma_{k}^{2}>0
. The corresponding log-likelihood ratio appearing in (
VI
) is then given by

\displaystyle\log\frac{L_{k}(\xi_{k,i}|\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=1)}{L_{k}(\xi_{k,i}|\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}=0)}=\frac{-2\zeta\xi_{k,i}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}},

(73)

and accordingly, the vector
\bm{\nu}_{i}
of
\gamma
-scaled LLRs across agents is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}_{i}=\text{col}\Big\{\frac{-2\gamma\zeta}{\sigma_{\ell}^{2}}\bm{\xi}_{\ell,i}\Big\}_{\ell=1}^{K}.

(74)

By Assumption
1
, the
\{\bm{\xi}_{\ell,i}\}_{\ell=1}^{K}
are independent random variables conditioned on
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}
. This implies that
\bm{\nu}_{i}
is a multivariate Gaussian random variable conditioned on the true hypothesis
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}
at time instant

,

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}_{i}\big|_{\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}}\sim\mathcal{G}\Big(\bm{\beta}^{(\theta_{i}^{\circ})},\Sigma\Big)

(75)

with mean

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{(\theta_{i}^{\circ})}\triangleq\text{col}\Big\{\frac{-2(-1)^{\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\circ}}\gamma\zeta^{2}}{\sigma_{\ell}^{2}}\Big\}_{\ell=1}^{K},

(76)

and covariance matrix

\displaystyle\Sigma\triangleq\text{diag}\Big\{\frac{4\gamma^{2}\zeta^{2}}{\sigma_{\ell}^{2}}\Big\}_{\ell=1}^{K}.

(77)

Next, we treat the consensus and diffusion cases separately. The consensus case is straightforward and useful to understand the diffusion case.

VI-B

Asymptotic Convergence

In addition to providing a way for calculating the error probabilities, the density evolution recursion (
71
) also allows us to show that agents exhibit a regular behavior in the limit. In particular, the distributions of the beliefs
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}
and log-belief ratios
\bm{w}_{k,i}
will converge to the distribution of some time-independent random variables. That is to say, they will converge
in distribution

[
52
, Chapter 17]
. A sequence (over time index
i
) of random variables
\bm{x}_{i}
converges to a limiting random variable
\bm{x}
in distribution if it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X})=\mathbb{P}(\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X})

(80)

for a set
\mathcal{X}
of
\bm{x}
, whose boundary has zero probability under the limiting distribution. We denote this by writing

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\bm{x}.

(81)

Although beliefs can demonstrate random behavior with fluctuations in the limit, convergence in distribution implies the existence of limiting statistics such as steady-state probability of errors, as shown in the following result.

Theorem 2

(
Asymptotic probability of error).

The diffusion and consensus HMM strategies (
17
)–(
19
) and (
24
) are asymptotically stable (in the sense of
[
53
]
) under binary classification, Gaussian likelihood models (
72
), and non-deterministic transition models (i.e.,
\mathbb{T}(\theta|\theta^{\prime})<1

\forall\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
). That is, the density function
f_{i}
satisfy

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\|f_{i}-f_{\infty}\|_{\textup{TV}}=0,

(82)

where
f_{\infty}
is a unique stationary density, and
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{TV}}
is the total variation norm defined with respect to the product measure, i.e., for any two densities
f
and
g

\|f-g\|_{\textup{TV}}\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\int\big|f(\theta,w)-g(\theta,w)\big|dW.

(83)

This result implies the convergence of the distribution of the log-belief ratios, and as a special case, the agent-specific probability of errors converge as well:

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{k,i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\bm{w}_{k,\infty},\qquad\lim_{i\to\infty}p_{k,i}=p_{k,\infty}.

(84)

Proof.

See Appendix
D
.
∎

In order to establish this result, we employ in Appendix
D
a known result from ergodic theory
[
53
, Theorem 5.7.4]
. As is common in ergodic theory, even though we can affirm that there exist limiting distributions; we do not know exactly what these distributions are. This task might be of formidable complexity in general.

Note that this result is in contrast to fixed-hypothesis social learning, where log-belief ratios do not converge, i.e.,
\bm{w}_{k,i}=\log\frac{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(1)}{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(0)}\to-\infty
(assuming w.l.o.g. that
\theta=0
is the fixed true hypothesis).

Theorem
2
states that the agents’ probability of error will approach a steady-state value despite the fact that the belief vectors can fluctuate randomly in the limit.

Corollary 1

(
Asymptotic beliefs).

Theorem
2
implies that beliefs of the agents converge in distribution. More formally,

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{k,i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\bm{\mu}_{k,\infty},

(85)

for a time-independent random variable
\bm{\mu}_{k,\infty}
.

Proof.

By definition (
54
),

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{k,i}=\log\frac{1-\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(0)}{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(0)}\Longleftrightarrow\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(0)=\frac{1}{1+\text{exp}\{\bm{w}_{k,i}\}}.

(86)

Since this is a continuous and non-degenerate transformation, by Theorem
2
and the continuous mapping theorem
[
54
]
it holds that
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\bm{\mu}_{k,\infty},
for some time-independent random variable
\bm{\mu}_{k,\infty}
. This implies that the statistics of the belief distribution also converge.
∎

Corollary
1
suggests that in general, the beliefs of agents will have random characteristics and will fluctuate in the long-run. This is in contrast to conventional social learning models where beliefs on the true
fixed
hypothesis converge to one almost surely
[
23
,
24
,
26
,
27
]
. In other words, all agents come to an agreement on the truth eventually. In comparison, in the current
dynamic
hypothesis scenario, agents do not even come to an agreement as shown in the next result.

Lemma 1

(
Network disagreement).

In general, the agents’ beliefs do not converge to the same random variable in distribution. Namely, for any agent pair
(\ell,k)
, the limiting variables
\bm{\mu}_{k,\infty}
and
\bm{\mu}_{\ell,\infty}
are not necessarily distributed according to the same distribution. Moreover, agents will have different performance in the long run, namely:

\displaystyle p_{k,\infty}\neq p_{\ell,\infty},\qquad J_{k,\infty}\neq J_{\ell,\infty}.

(87)

Proof.

We prove this by a counter-example in Appendix
E
.
∎

Lemma
1
implies that rapidly changing states prevent learning the truth with full confidence, as well as eventual network agreement, even under strongly-connected networks where information can flow thoroughly in all directions. Moreover, when the true state of nature is changing, agents can have different and non-vanishing asymptotic error probabilities. In traditional social learning, agents can have different and non-zero error probabilities in
finite-time
. But as time goes by, all probabilities of error vanish, i.e., they all become 0. So, unlike the traditional setting, the dynamic truth model gives rise to an equilibrium of
wise
and
unwise
agents in asymptotics. That is to say, some agents will be more successful in predicting the truth than others in steady-state. The agents’ error probabilities will be dependent on their observations’ informativeness and their relative location in the network. Indeed, this “wise agent phenomenon” is more in line with what we observe in real-world, as against to eventual agreement of agents on the correct hypothesis that traditional social learning literature concludes. This observation shows the importance of incorporating the changing behavior of the state of nature into social learning models.

As discussed before, most of the literature on learning over strongly-connected social networks conclude consensus across agents, although there are exceptions. The works
[
55
]
and
[
56
]
show that when there are
stubborn
agents in the network that never change their opinion, the beliefs in the long-run can fluctuate and vary, as in the current work. Moreover, if agents tend to communicate with other agents that think alike
[
57
,
58
]
; or if they tend to use their own beliefs as substitutes for others’ beliefs in the case of limited communication
[
59
]
, then opinion clusters can emerge. The current work gives evidence for another reason of disagreement, namely, the rapidly changing truth.

VIII
Concluding Remarks

In this work, we proposed a distributed and online state estimation algorithm for finite-state HMMs. Based on ergodicity of the underlying transition model, we provided asymptotic bounds on the disagreement between the distributed strategy and the optimal centralized strategy. We also examined the error probability in steady-state and established convergence in distribution under Gaussian observation models.

In addition to state estimation, the proposed algorithm can be used for the prediction of incoming data, by averaging the data-state likelihood functions with respect to the belief over states. More formally, for well-defined cases, agent
k
can estimate the incoming data at time
i
by using

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\xi}}_{k,i}

\displaystyle\triangleq\argmax_{\xi_{k,i}}\sum_{\theta_{i}}L_{k}(\xi_{k,i}|\theta_{i})\bm{\eta}_{k,i}(\theta_{i}).

(90)

Furthermore, the algorithm can also be used for continuous state estimation. This would require changing the summations over the states to integrals. These can be numerically tractable under some conditions. For instance, the exponential family of observation models can lead to closed-form formulas for the integral expressions, as in
[
10
]
.

Finally, the theoretical analysis in the current work utilizes the ergodicity of the transition model to establish performance bounds. A future challenge is to incorporate the informativeness of the observations as well.
