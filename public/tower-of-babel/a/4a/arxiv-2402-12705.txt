Abstract
For integers d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 and k ≥ d + 1 k\geq d+1 , the Distance Coloring problem asks if a given graph G G has a ( d , k ) (d,k) -coloring, i.e., a coloring of the vertices of G G by k k colors such that any two vertices within distance d d from each other have different colors. In particular, the well-known Coloring problem is a special case of Distance Coloring when d = 1 d=1 . For integers d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 and k ≥ d + 1 k\geq d+1 , the ( d , k ) (d,k) -Coloring Reconfiguration problem asks if there is a way to change the color of one vertex at a time, starting from a ( d , k ) (d,k) -coloring α \alpha of a graph G G to reach another ( d , k ) (d,k) -coloring β \beta of G G , such that all intermediate colorings are also ( d , k ) (d,k) -colorings. We show that even for planar, bipartite, and 2 2 -degenerate graphs, ( d , k ) (d,k) -Coloring Reconfiguration remains 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -complete for d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 and k = Ω ⁡ ( d 2 ) k=\Omega(d^{2}) via a reduction from the well-known Sliding Tokens problem. Additionally, on split graphs, there is an interesting dichotomy: the problem is 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -complete when d = 2 d=2 and k k is large but can be solved efficiently when d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 and k ≥ d + 1 k\geq d+1 . For chordal graphs, we show that the problem is 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -complete for even values of d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . Finally, we design a quadratic-time algorithm to solve the problem on paths for any d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 and k ≥ d + 1 k\geq d+1 . Keywords: Reconfiguration problem, d d -Distance k k -Coloring, Computational complexity, PSPACE-completeness, Polynomial-time algorithm

Introduction

For the last few decades,
reconfiguration problems
have emerged in various areas of computer science, including computational geometry, recreational mathematics, and constraint satisfaction
[
van13
,
Nis18
,
MN19
]
.
Given a
source problem

\mathcal{P}
(e.g.,
Satisfiability
,
Coloring
,
Independent Set
),
one can define its
reconfiguration variants
.
In such a variant, two
feasible solutions
(e.g., satisfying truth assignments, proper vertex-colorings, independent sets)
S
and
T
of
\mathcal{P}
are given along with a prescribed
reconfiguration rule
that usually describes a “small” change in a solution.
The question is to decide if there is a sequence of feasible solutions that transforms
S
into
T
, where each intermediate member is obtained from its predecessor by applying the reconfiguration rule exactly once.
Such a sequence, if it exists, is called a
reconfiguration sequence
.

1.1
Distance Coloring

The concept of
(d,k)
-coloring
(or
d
-distance
k
-coloring
) was introduced in 1969 by Kramer and Kramer
[
KK69a
,
KK69
]
.
For a graph
G=(V,E)
and integers
d\geq 1
and
k\geq d+1
, a
(d,k)
-coloring
of
G
is an assignment of
k
colors to the vertices of
G
such that no two vertices within distance
d
share the same color.
In particular, the classic
proper
k
-coloring
is the case when
d=1
.
The
(d,k)
-Coloring
problem, which asks if a given graph
G
has a
(d,k)
-coloring, is known to be
\mathsf{NP}
-complete for any fixed
d\geq 2
and large
k

[
McC83
,
LS95
]
.
In 2007, Sharp
[
Sha07
]
proved the following complexity dichotomy:
(d,k)
-Coloring
can be solved in polynomial time for
k\leq\lfloor 3d/2\rfloor
but is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard for
k>\lfloor 3d/2\rfloor
.
We refer readers to the survey
[
KK08
]
for more details on related
(d,k)
-Coloring
problems.

1.2
Coloring Reconfiguration

k
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
has been extensively studied in the literature
[
Nis18
,
MN19
,
Mah24
]
.
In

-
Coloring Reconfiguration
, we are given two proper
k
-colorings
\alpha
and
\beta
of a graph
G

there exists a way to recolor vertices one by one, starting from
\alpha
and ending at
\beta
,

k
-coloring.
It is well-known that

-
Coloring Reconfiguration
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete for any fixed

on bipartite graphs, for any fixed

on planar graphs,
and for

on bipartite planar graphs (and thus

-degenerate graphs)
[
BC09
]
.
A further note from Bonsma and Paulusma
[

]
shows that

-

is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete even on
(k-2)
-connected bipartite graphs for

.
Indeed, the problem remains
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete even on planar graphs of bounded bandwidth and low maximum degree
[

]
.
On the other hand, for

,
k
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
can be solved in linear

[
CvJ11
,

]
.
Additionally,

-
Coloring Reconfiguration
is solvable in polynomial time on planar graphs for

and on bipartite planar graphs for

[
BC09
,
van13
]
.
With respect to graph classes,
k
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
is solvable in polynomial time on
2
-degenerate graphs (which contains graphs of treewidth at most
two such as trees, cacti,
outerplanar graphs, and series-parallel graphs) and several subclasses of chordal graphs
[
HIZ19
,
BP19
]
.

1.5
On The Technical Contributions of Our Main Result

In this section, we outline the key technical challenges underlying our main result (
Theorem

1
).
Our primary contribution, presented in
Section

3
, establishes that for any
d\geq 2
and
k=\Omega(d^{2})
, the
(d,k)
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
problem remains
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete even when restricted to graphs that are bipartite, planar, and
2
-degenerate.

Recall that the
d
-th power
of a graph
G
, denoted by
G^{d}
, is the graph with
V(G^{d})=V(G)
and

E(G^{d})=\{uv\mid u,v\in V(G)\text{ and }\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq d\}
, where
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)
denotes the
distance
(i.e., the length of a shortest path)
between
u
and
v
in
G
.
It is well known that
\alpha
is a
(d,k)
-coloring of a graph
G
if and only if
\alpha
is a
(1,k)
-coloring of
G^{d}
.
At first glance, this relationship suggests a straightforward proof of
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-completeness for
(d,k)
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
: since the problem is already known to
be
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete for
d=1
and
k\geq 4

[
BC09
]
, one might attempt to reduce
(1,k)
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
to
(d,k)
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
.
However, we emphasize that
such a reduction fails
.

Let us take a closer look at this straightforward reduction.
For a valid polynomial-time reduction, given any instance
(G,\alpha,\beta)
of
(1,k)
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
, one would need to
construct a corresponding instance
(H,\alpha,\beta)
of
(d,k)
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
where
H
is a
d
-th root
of
G
(i.e.,
H^{d}\simeq G
).
The problem lies in finding such a root: deciding whether a graph
G
has a
d
-th root is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete for all fixed
d\geq 2
in chordal graphs and remains
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete even on bipartite graphs for all fixed
d\geq 3

[
LN10
]
.
Therefore, unless
{\mathsf{P}}={\mathsf{NP}}
, this reduction cannot be computed in polynomial time, invalidating this approach for proving
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-completeness.

Our approach follows that of Bonsma and Cereceda
[
BC09
]
for
d=1
but requires more technical insights.
We follow a two-step reduction approach.
First, we present a polynomial-time reduction from
List
(d,k)
-Coloring Reconfiguration
to
(d,k^{\prime})
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
(
Section

3.2
), where
k^{\prime}=O(nd)
.
Second, in
Section

3.4
, we establish that
List
(d,k)
-Coloring Reconfiguration
is indeed
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete on bipartite, planar, and
2
-degenerate graphs for
d\geq 2
and
k\geq 3(d+1)/2+3
if
d
is odd or
k\geq 3(d+2)/2+3
if
d
is even.
This second step involves introducing a variant of the well-known
Sliding Tokens
problem that remains
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete even on planar and
2
-degenerate graphs (
Section

3.3
), followed by a polynomial-time reduction from this problem to
List
(d,k)
-Coloring Reconfiguration
. Finally, (
Section

3.5
) we show that reducing the instance of
List
(d,k)
-Coloring Reconfiguration
as derived from the second part to
(d,k^{\prime})
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
using the same process as
Section

3.2
results in a reduction of the number of colors used from
k^{\prime}=O(nd)
to
O(d^{2})
.

Bonsma and Cereceda’s hardness proof also broadly contains the same two parts: (1) Reduction from
List
k
-Coloring Reconfiguration
to
k
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
and (2) Reduction from
Sliding Tokens
to
List
k
-Coloring Reconfiguration
.
Let us now discuss the technical differences between their approach and ours due to the nature of our problem.
In what follows, we assume that for
j\in\{1,2\}
, the graphs
G_{j}
and
G^{\prime}_{j}
are respectively from the original instance (of the base problem that they reduce from) and the new instance (of the target problem that they reduce to) in Reduction
(j)
.

In Reduction (1), each vertex
v
of
G_{1}
is assigned a list of colors
L(v)
(i.e., a subset of
\{1,\dots,k\}
).
In Bonsma and Cereceda’s reduction as well as ours, all vertices of
G_{1}
appear in
G^{\prime}_{1}
.
Bonsma and Cereceda construct, for each vertex
v
of
G_{1}
and a color
c\notin L(v)
, a corresponding
pre-colored frozen graph

F_{v,c}
(which they
called
a graph with
k
-frozen coloring
) whose vertices are colored with all
k
colors and no vertex in
F_{v,c}
can be recolored (hence the terminology
“frozen coloring”).
Moreover,
v
is joined to a vertex of
F_{v,c}
whose color is
c
.
Intuitively, this construction allows it to simulate the behavior of a list
(1,k)
-coloring of
G_{1}
by a
(1,k)
-coloring of the newly constructed graph

G^{\prime}_{1}
:
for any vertex
v
of
G_{1}
, as no vertex in
F_{v,c}
can be recolored,
v
never has color
c
in
G^{\prime}_{1}
,
which corresponds to the assumption that
c\notin L(v)
— the list of available colors for
v
in
G_{1}
.
The crucial point leading to the success of this construction is that “in
G^{\prime}_{1}
, recoloring any vertex
w\neq v(\in G_{1})
not in
F_{v,c}

is not affected by any vertex in
F_{v,c}
”.
When
d=1
, this property trivially holds as
v
acts as a ”separator”.
Some technical difficulties arise when
d\geq 2
.
More precisely, in
G^{\prime}_{1}
, suppose that
w\in G_{1}
is within distance
d
from a vertex
x
of
F_{v,c}
whose color is
c_{x}\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. This implies that
w

cannot be colored
c_{x}
in
G^{\prime}_{1}
.
But it can happen that
c_{x}\in L(w)
, which means
w
indeed can be colored
c_{x}
in
G_{1}
!
This is the reason why for the same
k
colours, it is highly unlikely
to simulate a list
(d,k)
-coloring of
G_{1}
by a
(d,k)
-coloring of
G^{\prime}_{1}
.
In other words, to achieve this simulation, in addition to the
k
colors given in the
(d,k)
-coloring of
G^{\prime}_{1}
, we need extra colors.

Another technical difficulty is that in each frozen graph by definition, all colors (original ones used in
G_{1}
and extra new ones added later) must be
used. Corresponding to each vertex in
G_{1}
we introduce new extra colors in
G^{\prime}_{1}
but every frozen graph (in
G^{\prime}_{1}
) corresponding to each vertex in
G_{1}
need to contain all these extra colors.
Moreover, no extra color can be used to color
v
in
G^{\prime}_{1}
.
Keeping these issues in mind, in
Section

3.2
, we present a non-trivial construction of these frozen graphs which overcomes these
technical difficulties.
Additionally, we also note that Bonsma and Cereceda’s constructed frozen graphs are either bipartite or planar, while ours are
bipartite, planar and even
1
-degenerate (they are essentially trees) as well.

In Reduction (2), Bonsma and Cereceda used the
Sliding Tokens
problem and simulated the behavior of a “token placement” (where each vertex has at most one token and no two tokens are adjacent)
of
G_{2}
by a list
k
-coloring of
G^{\prime}_{2}
using a gadget which they call
(a,b)
-forbidding path
. The instance of the
Sliding Tokens
problem consisted of triangles and edges connected together.
Intuitively, each token is allowed to move “inside” either a triangle
i
whose vertices are conventionally labelled
t_{i1},t_{i2},t_{i3}
or an edge
j
whose
vertices are conventionally labelled
e_{j1},e_{j2}
.
(Bonsma and Cereceda called them a
token triangle
and a
token edge
, respectively.)
Such a triangle or edge is “contracted” to a single vertex in
G^{\prime}_{2}
named either
t_{i}
or
e_{j}
, respectively.
The corresponding color list of
t_{i}
is
\{1,2,3\}
and that of
e_{j}
is
\{1,2\}
.
For example, sliding a token from
t_{i1}
to
t_{i2}
in a token triangle
i
of
G_{2}
may correspond to recoloring the corresponding vertex
t_{i}
of
G^{\prime}_{2}
from

1
to
2
.
Intuitively, for example, if the vertex
t_{ia}
of token triangle
i
and
t_{\ell b}
of triangle
\ell
are adjacent in
G_{2}
, one needs to guarantee that if one vertex has
a token then the other does not.
This means that the vertices
t_{i}
and
t_{\ell}
in
G^{\prime}_{2}
are never simultaneously colored
a
and
b
respectively.
To ensure such a requirement led to the concept of “
(a,b)
-forbidding paths”. Moreover, the internal vertices of these “
(a,b)
-forbidding paths” used new extra colors other than
\{1,2,3\}
, which were the colors used to color vertices of the form
t_{i}
and
e_{j}
. To make Bonsma and Cereceda’s construction work, one crucial point is that “recoloring an internal vertex
v
(which is not an endpoint) of a forbidding path
P
with such an extra color does
not affect any vertex in
V(G^{\prime}_{2})\setminus V(P)
”. This property trivially holds for
d=1
as these endpoints act as ”separators”.
However, maintaining the same instance
G^{\prime}_{2}
for
d\geq 2
requires many more extra colors, as two internal vertices of different forbidding paths may be within distance
d
from each
other in
G^{\prime}_{2}
.
In
Section

3.4
, we extend the “forbidding paths” concept and describe a non-trivial construction that overcomes the mentioned issues.

Additionally, we remark that our extension of both reductions, when independently considered,
may require a lot of extra colors.
By cleverly joining the gadgets, the number of colors may be reduced.
As we will show in
Section

3.5
, we are able to combine our constructions and argue that
O(d^{2})
extra colors are enough.

Finally, we want to state that for reasons above we feel it is highly unlikely to solve the problem using
d
colors. However, it remains an interesting open question if we can solve the problem using
o(d^{2})
colors. Moreover, our constructions also imply the
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-completeness on
2
-degenerate graphs for the case
d\geq 2
.
This result, along with the known result that the problem is in
{\mathsf{P}}
for
d=1
on
2
-degenerate graphs
[
HIZ19
]
, leads to an interesting complexity dichotomy.

Preliminaries

We refer readers to

]
for the concepts and notations not defined here.
Unless otherwise mentioned, we always consider simple, undirected, connected graphs.
For two vertices

of a graph
G
, we denote by

the

(i.e., the length
of the shortest path) between
u

and

.
We define

for a given graph

An

.

A

A

2.1

-Coloring

For two positive integers
d\geq 1
and
k\geq d+1
, a
(d,k)
-coloring
of a graph
G
is a function

\alpha\colon V(G)\to\{1,\dots,k\}
such that for any pair of
distinct vertices
u
and
v
,
\alpha(u)\neq\alpha(v)
if
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq d
.
In particular, a
(1,k)

In this section, we first revisit a variant of

3.3.1
Bonsma and Cereceda’s
Sliding Tokens
Variant

In a graph
G
, a
valid token configuration
is a set of vertices on which tokens are placed such that no two tokens are either
on the
same or adjacent vertices, i.e., each token configuration forms an
independent set
of
G
.
A
move
(or
\mathsf{TS}
-move
) between two token configurations of
G
involves sliding a single
token from one vertex to one
of its (unoccupied) neighbors.
A move must always result in a valid token configuration.
Given a graph
G
and two valid token configurations
T_{A},T_{B}
, the
Sliding Tokens
problem, first introduced by Hearn and Demaine
[
HD05
]
, asks if there is a
sequence of moves transforming
T_{A}
into
T_{B}
.
Such a sequence, if it exists, is called a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence
in
G
between
T_{A}
and
T_{B}
.

Bonsma and Cereceda
[
BC09
]
show that
Sliding Tokens
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete even when restricted to the following set of
(G,T_{A},T_{B})

instances.
For a more detailed explanation, we refer readers to the PhD thesis of Cereceda
[
Cer07
]
.

•

The graph
G
has three types of gadgets:
token triangles
(a copy of
K_{3}
),
token edges
(a copy of
K_{2}
), and

link edges
(a copy of
K_{2}
).
Token triangles and token edges are all mutually disjoint.
They are joined together by link edges in such a way that every vertex of
G
belongs to exactly one token triangle or one token
edge.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, for
d\geq 2
and
k\geq d+1
, we provided an initial exploration of the computational complexity of
(d,k)
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
and related problems on different graph classes.
Our primary contribution establishes that the problem remains
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete for any
d\geq 2
even when restricted to graphs that are simultaneously planar, bipartite, and
2
-degenerate.
Following the natural hierarchy of graph degeneracy, a logical next direction is to examine
1
-degenerate graphs (forests).
Notably, the complexity of
(d,k)
-
Coloring Reconfiguration
(
d\geq 2
) remains unresolved even for trees, and we have only partially addressed this question by developing a quadratic-time algorithm for paths (a subclass of trees).
