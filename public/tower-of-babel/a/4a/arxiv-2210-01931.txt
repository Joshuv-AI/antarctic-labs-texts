3/2 magic-angle quantization rule of flat bands in twisted bilayer graphene and relationship with the Quantum Hall effect Preprint: APS/123-QED
Leonardo A. Navarro-Labastida and Gerardo G. Naumis

Abstract
Flat band electronic modes in twisted graphene bilayers are responsible for superconducting and other highly correlated electron-electron phases. Although some hints were known of a possible connection between the quantum Hall effect and zero flat band modes, it was not clear how such connection appears. Here the electronic behavior in twisted bilayer graphene is studied using the chiral model Hamiltonian. As a result, it is proved that for high-order magic angles, the zero flat band modes converge into coherent Landau states with a dispersion σ 2 = 1 / 3 ​ α \sigma^{2}=1/3\alpha , where α \alpha is a coupling parameter that incorporates the twist angle and energetic scales. Then it is proved that the square of the hamiltonian, which is a 2 × 2 2\times 2 matrix operator, turns out to be equivalent in a first approximation to a two-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator. The interlayer currents between graphene’s bipartite lattices are identified with the angular momentum term while the confinement potential is an effective quadratic potential. By considering the zero mode equation, the boundary conditions and a scaling argument, a limiting quantization rule for high-order magic angles is obtained, i.e., α m + 1 − α m = 3 / 2 \alpha_{m+1}-\alpha_{m}=3/2 where m m is the order of the angle. From there, an equipartition and quantization of the kinetic, confinement and angular momentum contributions is found. All these results are in very good agreement with numerical calculations.



I
Introduction

In 2018 it was found experimentally that twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) presents strongly correlated electron-electron quantum phases leading for example to unconventional superconductivity and Mott insulator states
Cao et al. 2018
. More recently, trilayer twisted graphene has been found to be the most strongly interacting correlated material
Park et al. 2021
;
Shen et al. 2022
. Such remarkable discoveries presented a new paradigm in the so-called Moiré materials and unveiled the importance of two-dimensional (2D) materials to understand unconventional superconductivity in cuprates and heavy fermions systems, as they share similar quantum phase diagrams
Cao et al. 2018
;
Park et al. 2021
;
Song and Bernevig 2022
. TBG advantages are i) its simplicity, as they are made from a single chemical element, and ii) they have a high degree of manipulation that cuprates doesn’t have. In recent years, there has been a significant interest in these phases of matter from a fundamental point of view
Goodwin et al. 2019
;
Hridis et al. 2019
;
Kerelsky et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2020a
;
Ochoa and Fernandes 2021
;
Koshino and Nam 2020
;
Oka and Koshino 2021
;
Fujimoto and Koshino 2021
;
Dante et al. 2021
but also because they present a lot of possible electronic applications and quantum computing advantages
Giustino et al. 2020
;
Dante et al. 2021
. There is also an interesting connection between topological phases, edge states, semimetals, and fractional quantum Hall effect (FQHE)
Andrews and Soluyanov 2020
;
Liu et al. 2021a
;
Liu et al. 2021b
;
Ledwith et al. 2022
;
Ledwith et al. 2022
;
Xu and Balents 2018
;
Wu 2019
;
González and Stauber 2020
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Stauber et al. 2018
;
Pantaleón et al. 2022
;
Fu et al. 2020
. A recently paper establishes a connection between heavy fermion models and TBG
Song and Bernevig 2022
, opening the prospect of using heavy fermions physics to the superconducting physics of TBG and more strongly correlated phases.

The discovery of such phases was proceeded by the Bistritzer-Mac Donald (BM) theoretical observation that twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) develops flat bands at certain twisting angles which are called magic
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
. BM considered a continuum Dirac model in which the moiré periodicity between layers produces moiré Bloch´s bands
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
. The model is continuum in the sense that the interlayer potential between Carbon
\pi
orbitals is a smooth function of the spatial separation projected onto the graphene planes and also the hopping is local and periodic, allowing to apply the Bloch´s theorem for any rotation angle. For TBG it was demonstrated that non-Abelian gauge fields arise due to the coupling between layers in the low-energy regime
San-Jose et al. 2012
;
Gerardo et al. 2021
.

Flat band modes that arise at magic angles, also known as zero energy modes, have been investigated in many recent works
Tarnopolsky et al. 2019
;
Patrick et al. 2020
;
Ledwith et al. 2021
;
Jie et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Popov and Milekhin 2021
;
Liu et al. 2018
;
Onari and Kontani 2022
;
Herrera and Naumis 2021
;
De Beule et al. 2021
, and in particular, there were hints in the mathematics for a possible connection with the quantum Hall effect (QHE) and the lowest Landau level
Tarnopolsky et al. 2019
;
Jie et al. 2021
. There are interesting properties of the zero mode wave function
Jie et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Popov and Milekhin 2021
, in particular, the connection with the lowest Landau level reveals that TBG presents topological phases
Jie et al. 2021
;
Sheffer and Stern 2021
.

Importantly, the wave function is reminiscent of a quantum hall wave function because is described in terms of Jacobi theta functions such as in the quantum hall effect wave function
Tarnopolsky et al. 2019
;
Patrick et al. 2020
;
Ledwith et al. 2021
. This hidden wave function is important to understand because leads to particular localization properties, orbital current, density wave function distribution, and symmetries of the pseudo-magnetic gauge fields. Yet, exactly how this analogy arises was not clear as no connection between the quantum harmonic oscillator and the TBG hamiltonian was ever found. Tarnopolsky et. al. also found that magic angles were quantized but no explanation was provided for this fact
Tarnopolsky et al. 2019
. Thus there were
two open questions related to the same problem. The present work shows how these two questions relate to each other, and also answers them. Moreover, we find that in fact, the zero flat band modes converge into coherent Landau levels. As we will discuss, this is done by using boundary layer differential equations theory and squaring the Hamiltonian
Gerardo et al. 2021
;
Navarro-Labastida et al. 2022
, a process that has also been used in supersymmetry
Matsumoto et al. 2022
;
Mizoguchi et al. 2021

Roychowdhury et al. 2022

II
Chiral TBG and Squared TBG Hamiltonians

The chiral Hamiltonian of twisted bilayer graphene is a variant of the original Bistritzer-MacDonald Hamiltonian in which the
AA
tunneling is set to zero
Ledwith et al. 2021
. Here we use as basis the wave vectors
\Phi(\bm{r})=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}(\bm{r}),\psi_{2}(\bm{r}),\chi_{1}(\bm{r}),\chi_{2}(\bm{r})\end{pmatrix}^{T}
where the index
1,2
represents each graphene layer and
\psi_{j}(\bm{r})
and
\chi_{j}(\bm{r})
are the Wannier orbitals on each inequivalent site of the graphene’s unit cell.
The chiral Hamiltonian is given by
Tarnopolsky et al. 2019
;
Eslam et al. 2019
;
Patrick et al. 2020
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}&=\begin{pmatrix}0&D^{\ast}(-\bm{r})\\
D(\bm{r})&0\end{pmatrix}\\
\end{split}

(1)

where the zero-mode operator is defined as,

\begin{split}D(\bm{r})&=\begin{pmatrix}-i\bar{\partial}&\alpha U(\bm{r})\\
\alpha U(-\bm{r})&-i\bar{\partial}\end{pmatrix}\\
\end{split}

(2)

and,

\begin{split}D^{*}(-\bm{r})&=\begin{pmatrix}-i\partial&\alpha U^{*}(-\bm{r})\\
\alpha U^{*}(\bm{r})&-i\partial\end{pmatrix}\\
\end{split}

(3)

with
\bar{\partial}=\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y}
,
\partial=\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y}
. The potential is,

U(\bm{\bm{r}})=e^{-i\bm{q}_{1}\cdot\bm{r}}+e^{i\phi}e^{-i\bm{q}_{2}\cdot\bm{r}}+e^{-i\phi}e^{-i\bm{q}_{3}\cdot\bm{r}}

(4)

where the phase factor is
\phi=2\pi/3
and the vectors are given by
\bm{q}_{1}=k_{\theta}(0,-1)
,
\bm{q}_{2}=k_{\theta}(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\frac{1}{2})
,
\bm{q}_{3}=k_{\theta}(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\frac{1}{2})
, the moiré modulation vector is
k_{\theta}=2k_{D}\sin{\frac{\theta}{2}}
with

k_{D}=\frac{4\pi}{3a_{0}}
is the magnitude of the Dirac wave vector
and
a_{0}
is the lattice constant of monolayer graphene. The model contains only the parameter
\alpha
, defined as
\alpha=\frac{w_{1}}{v_{0}k_{\theta}}
where
w_{1}
is the interlayer coupling of stacking AB/BA with value
w_{1}=110
meV and
v_{0}
is the Fermi velocity with value
v_{0}=\frac{19.81eV}{2k_{D}}
.
The operators
\partial
and
\bar{\partial}
are dimensionless as the Hamiltonian Eq. (
1
) is written in using units where
v_{0}=1
,
k_{\theta}=1
. The twist angle only enters in the dimensionless parameter
\alpha
. The combinations
\bm{b}_{1,2}=\bm{q}_{2,3}-\bm{q}_{1}
are the moiré Brillouin zone (mBZ) vectors and also
\bm{b}_{3}=\bm{q}_{3}-\bm{q}_{2}
.
Using this basis for the reciprocal space lattice, some important high symmetry points of the moiré Brillouin zone are
\bm{K}=(0,0)
,
\bm{K^{\prime}}=-\bm{q}_{1}
, and
\bm{\Gamma}=\bm{q}_{1}
(see ref.
Navarro-Labastida et al. 2022
for a diagram).
For further use it is also convenient to define a set of unitary vectors
\bm{q}_{\mu}^{\perp}
perpendicular to the set
\bm{q}_{\mu}
and given by

\bm{q}_{1}^{\perp}=(1,0),\bm{q}_{2}^{\perp}=\big(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\big),\bm{q}_{3}^{\perp}=\big(-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\big)
.
The moiré vectors unitary cell are given by
\bm{a}_{1,2}=(4\pi/3k_{\theta})(\sqrt{3}/2,1/2)
. Observe that
\bm{q}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{a}_{1,2}=-\phi
for
\mu=1,2,3
.

In a previous work we showed how, by taking the square of
H
, it is possible to write the Hamiltonian as a
2\times 2
matrix
Gerardo et al. 2021
;
Navarro-Labastida et al. 2022
,

\begin{split}H^{2}&=\begin{pmatrix}-\nabla^{2}+\alpha^{2}|U(-\bm{r})|^{2}&\alpha A^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\\
\alpha A(\bm{r})&-\nabla^{2}+\alpha^{2}|U(\bm{r})|^{2}\end{pmatrix}\end{split}

(5)

where the squared norm of the potential is an effective trigonal confinement potential,

\begin{split}|U(\bm{r})|^{2}&=3+2\cos(\bm{b}_1\cdot\bm{r}-\phi)+2\cos(\bm{b}_2\cdot\bm{r}+\phi)\\
&+2\cos(\bm{b}_3\cdot\bm{r}+2\phi)\end{split}

(6)

and the off-diagonal term is,

\begin{split}A^{\dagger}(\bm{r})&=-i\sum_{\mu=1}^{3}e^{-i\bm{q}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{r}}(2\bm{q}_{\mu}^{\perp}\cdot\bm{\nabla}+1)\\
\end{split}

(7)

where
\bm{\nabla}^{\dagger}=-\bm{\nabla}
with
\bm{\nabla}=(\partial_{x},\partial_{y})
and
\mu=1,2,3
.

III
Zero-energy modes as coherent Landau states

Now we investigate the asymptotic limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
by numerically solving (see appendix C) the Schrödinger equation
\mathcal{H}\Psi(\bm{r})=E\Psi(\bm{r})
where
E
is the energy. As the potential is periodic, it satisfies Bolch’s theorem, and thus
\psi_{\bm{k},j}(\bm{r})=e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}u_{\bm{k},j}(\bm{r})
where
u_{\bm{k},j}(\bm{r})
has the periodicity of the lattice (see appendix A). The rotational
C_{3}
symmetry allows to further simplify the problem (see appendix B). In Fig.
1
we present
the zero mode wave function, corresponding to
E=0
at the reciprocal space point
\bm{k}=\Gamma
for the
m
th magic angles (
\alpha_{m}
) with
m=8
and
m=9
. The electronic maxima of the density form hexagons which are nearly localized at
\bm{r_{\mu}}\approx\pm\bm{q}_{\mu}
. Such observation is detailed in Fig.
1
. Moreover, the wave-function for other
\bm{k}
points follow the same behavior although the
\Gamma
point best captures the magic angle behavior
Navarro-Labastida et al. 2022
. In the limit of
\alpha_{m}\rightarrow\infty
we have verified that in fact, the electron density is almost localized at
\bm{q}_{\mu}
. Notice that here we are working with adimensional units but this suggests a connection with the QHE as solutions seem self-dual
Hofstadter 1976
, i.e., in real space are similar to those in reciprocal space with renormalized parameters.

Although there are expressions for the wave-function
Tarnopolsky et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2020b
;
Wang et al. 2021
at any
\bm{k}
point that hinted a relationship with the lowest Landau levels, they depend on the wave function at the
\bm{K}
point, i.e.,

\psi_{\bm{k},j}(\bm{r})=f_{\bm{k}}(z)\psi_{\bm{K},j}(\bm{r})

(8)

where
z=x+iy
and
f_{\bm{k}}(z)
is an analytic function which satisfy the boundary condition and turns out to be a Jacobi theta function. The form of the
\psi_{\bm{K},j}(\bm{r})
is not analytically known. Yet in Figs.
1
and
2
we see numerically that the electron wave function reaches an asymptotic limit almost invariant as
\alpha_{m}\rightarrow\infty
. In this limit, the localization centers for the
\bf{\Gamma}
point wave function seem to converge as seen in Figs.
1
,
2
and
3
. Such wave function tends to be localized in certain points of space which are not the stacking points AA, AB, and BA. In that sense, the solutions are very different from the first magic angle a fact that was explained elsewhere
Navarro-Labastida et al. 2022
. As seen in Fig.
3
, for other
\bm{k}
points different from
\bf{\Gamma}
the situation is quite similar, i.e., the wave functions are more localized as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
and approach the same localization center.

To understand how this limiting wave function arises, let us discuss the zero-mode equation
D(\bm{r})\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}(\bm{r}),\psi_{2}(\bm{r})\end{pmatrix}^{T}=0
for states in the flat-band.
Although not essential for the analysis, it is easier to understand the
\Gamma
point solution. For this case we have
that due to symmetry,
\psi_{2}(\bm{r})=i\mu_{\alpha}\psi_{1}(-\bm{r})
where
\mu_{\alpha}=\pm 1
depending on the magic angle parity
Tarnopolsky et al. 2019
.
Therefore, we obtain,

\bar{\partial}\psi_{1}(\bm{r})=\alpha\mu_{\alpha}U(\bm{r})\psi_{1}(-\bm{r})

(9)

\bar{\partial}\psi_{1}(-\bm{r})=-\alpha\mu_{\alpha}U(-\bm{r})\psi_{1}(\bm{r})

(10)

To solve the equation in the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty

we use the boundary layer theory of differential equations
Roscoe B. 2010
, i.e., whenever the gradients are small, we can neglect the derivative in Eqns. (
9
)-(
10
) when compared to the potential term. Then our solution must satisfy
\psi_{j}(r)\rightarrow 0
. The solution will be different from zero only inside the boundary layer, i.e., whenever
\bar{\partial}\psi_{j}(\bm{r})
is of order
\alpha U(\pm\bm{r})\psi_{j}(\bm{r})
. Taken into account the boundary layer we conclude that the solution must be strongly peaked around certain regions of space. Then is natural to seek the solution within continuous functions having a peak while keeping the form of Eq. (
8
).
We then propose a coherent Landau state ansatz for a given layer (and thus suppress the subindex
j
),

\begin{split}\psi(z,z^{*})&=f_{\lambda}(z)e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}|z|^{2}}\\
\end{split}

(11)

where
f_{\lambda}(z)
is an analytic function
Greiner 2001
,

\begin{split}f_{\lambda}(z)&=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\lambda^{*}z}e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\lambda\lambda^{*}}\end{split}

(12)

The parameter
\lambda
is the localization center (known as the guiding coordinates in the QHE problem
Girvin and Yang 2019
) and
\sigma
the standard deviation as the electronic density is a Gaussian,

\rho(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}e^{\frac{-|z-\lambda|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}

(13)

Notice how the Gaussian envelope in Eq. (
11
) ensures the boundary layer condition, i.e., the vanishing of the wave function whenever the gradient is small.

However, still we need to make remarks. As the equation involves
\psi(\bm{r})
and
\psi(-\bm{r})
, the solutions can be written as a sum of a symmetrized and antisymmetrized forms. Therefore, it will be a linear combination of the symmetrized/antisymmetrized wavefunctions,

\begin{split}\psi_{\pm}(z,z^{*})\approx e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}|z|^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(f_{\lambda}(z)\pm f_{-\lambda}(z))\\
\end{split}

(14)

provided that
\sigma\rightarrow\infty
to avoid overlap between the Gaussians centered at
\lambda
and
-\lambda
. A second reason to neglect the overlap effect around
z=0
is that
U(0)=0
and
\overline{\partial}\psi_{\pm}(z,z^{*})|_{z,z^{*}=0}=0
.

In what follows we will use our ansatz in the zero mode equation to prove how it satisfies the equation and to obtain
\sigma
.

Before doing so, observe that
\psi(r)
must transform according to the
C_{3}
symmetry group and this can be ensured by defining a
\lambda_{1}
such that,

\begin{split}\psi_{\pm}(z,z^{*})\approx\frac{1}{{\sqrt{6}}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{3}e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}|z|^{2}}(f_{\lambda_{\mu}}(z)\pm f_{-\lambda_{\mu}}(z))\\
\end{split}

(15)

where
\lambda_{2}=e^{i\phi}\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{3}=e^{-i\phi}\lambda_{1}
and the normalization constant was modified to account for two layers and the three
\lambda_{\mu}
. Concerning the boundary conditions, i.e., the Bloch’s theorem, we will discuss the subject after testing the solution for a unit cell.

As the numerical simulation indicates that the electronic density is localized on
\pm\bm{q}_{\mu}
,
this suggests to propose

\lambda_{\mu}=Q_{\mu}=q^{x}_{\mu}+iq^{y}_{\mu}

where
q^{x}_{\mu}
and
q^{y}_{\mu}
are the components of vector
\bm{q}_{\mu}=(q^{x}_{\mu},q^{y}_{\mu})
. Finally, the parameter
\sigma
will be determined by imposing the ansatz to satisfy Eqns. (
9
)-(
10
).

Now we test our ansatz in the zero mode equation. Using complex numbers and that
\bar{\partial}=2\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{*}}
, the zero mode equation can be rewritten as,

\begin{split}2\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{*}}\psi_{\pm}(z,z^{*})=\mu_{\alpha}\alpha_{m}U(z,z^{*})\psi_{\pm}(-z,-z^{*})\end{split}

(16)

where
\alpha_{m}
indicate a magic angle and
U(z,z^{*})
is the complex form of the coupling layer potential
U(\bm{r})
.

In the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
we can expand
U(z,z^{*})
locally around
Q_{\mu}
(see appendix C) where the boundary layer lies, therefore,

\begin{split}U(z,z^{*})\psi_{\pm}(-z,-z^{*})&\approx\frac{3z}{2}\psi_{\pm}(-z,-z^{*})\end{split}.

(17)

Next we use that the anti-holomorphic derivative of an analytic function is zero from where

\begin{split}\bar{\partial}\psi_{\pm}(z,z^{*})&=2\frac{\partial\psi_{\pm}(z,z^{*})}{\partial z^{*}}=-\frac{z}{2\sigma^{2}}\psi_{\pm}(z,z^{*})\end{split}

(18)

Finally, we combine the left-side of the zero mode equation, Eq. (
18
), with the right-hand side and use Eq.
(
17
) to obtain,

\begin{split}-\frac{z}{\sigma^{2}}\psi_{\mp}(z,z^{*})&\approx\mu_{\alpha}3\alpha_{m}z\psi_{\mp}(-z,-z^{*})\end{split}

(19)

where we see that the equation imposes the need of a symmetric or antisymmetric solution depending on the magic angle parity, given by the sign of
\mu_{\alpha}
. This can be numerically verified in Fig.
1
where we plot the symmetrized and antisymmetrized numerically obtained wavefunctions for finite
\alpha
. Observe that for one of the layers
\psi_{\pm}(\bm{r})=\psi_{1}(\bm{r})\pm\psi_{1}(\bm{-r})=\psi_{1}(\bm{r})\mp i\mu_{\alpha}\psi_{2}(\bm{r})
, the other layer is obtained from
\psi_{2}(\bm{r})\pm\psi_{2}(\bm{-r})
. The resulting symmetric/antisymmetric components of
\psi_{\pm}(\bm{r})
are purely real/imaginary respectively for odd/even
m
(see appendix
VI.4
).
Moreover, from Eq. (
19
) we obtain the width of the coherent Landau state,

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sigma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\alpha_{m}}}

(20)

To test these two results, in Fig.
2
we compare the evolution of the electronic density as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
for several magic angles, in this case for the axis
x=0
. The dashed line is the asymptotic solution given by Eq. (
15
) which does not contain any free parameter.

In Fig.
3
we show a log-log plot of
\sigma
versus
\alpha_{m}
as obtained by fitting Gaussians to the numerical results. The red line is the theoretical prediction given by Eq. (
20
) giving a very good agreement with the numerical data for higher order magic angles. In Fig.
3
b) we also plot the maximum position of the numerically obtained wavefunctions (
|r_{m}|
), confirming the tendency for localization seen in the inverse participation ratio

IV
Magic angle quantization rule and Quantum Hall effect: squared twisted graphene hamiltonian

Let us now prove why magic angles are quantized and the relation with the quantum harmonic oscillator. Consider the zero mode Eq. (
9
) applied to a symmetric or antisymmetric wave function. We can scale the equation by setting
\bm{r}^{\prime}=\bm{r}\alpha_{m}
and, for the time being, without caring for the boundary conditions, we have that,

\bar{\partial}^{\prime}\psi_{\pm}(\bm{r}^{\prime})=\mu_{\alpha}U(\bm{r}^{\prime}/\alpha_{m})\psi_{\pm}(-\bm{r}^{\prime})

(21)

which suggests that changing
\alpha_{m}
is akin to scale the unitary cell. However, we also need to preserve the boundary conditions. It turns out that if,

\alpha_{m}=3m

(22)

the boundary conditions of the potential
U(\bm{r})
are preserved in a bigger unitary cell since for example, the exponentials in the definition of
U(\bm{r})
become
e^{i\bm{q}_{1}\cdot\bm{a}_{1,2}^{\prime}}=e^{i3m\bm{q}_{1}\cdot\bm{a}_{1,2}}=e^{-i3m\phi}=1
. The same situation holds for the terms with
\bm{q}_{2}
and
\bm{q}_{3}
. Notice that the scaling by
3
appears due to the need to traverse three unitary cells in order to pick a phase
2\pi
in
U(\bm{r})
as for example
U(\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{1})=e^{-i\phi}U(\bm{r})
and
U(\bm{r}+\bm{a}_{2})=e^{-i\phi}U(\bm{r})
. This procedure is akin to consider a magnetic supercell as usually done in the Quantum Hall Effect and gives a possible explanation to the numerically observed change in the effective magnetic flux between magic angles
Jie et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
. The scaling argument explains why magic angles
\alpha_{m}
with a given parity are separated by
3
. Now if we take into account sequences with alternate parities (
\mu_{\alpha}=\pm 1

V
Conclusions

In conclusion, we showed that in the twisted bilayer hamiltonian, zero flat band modes converge into coherent Landau levels. The shape and dispersion of the zero-mode wavefunctions as a function of the twist angles was found and showed an excellent agreement with the numerical results. Then we proved that the squared twisted bilayer hamiltonian, up to first order, describes a quantum harmonic oscillator. Thereafter it was found that for high order magic angles, the strong confinement and the symmetry of the potential lead to solutions with well defined angular momentum. By using a scaling argument, this allows to obtain the
3/2
magic angle quantization rule which was observed numerically
Tarnopolsky et al. 2019
. Another important consequence is that the angular momentum can be related with interlayer currents between each graphene’s bipartite lattice which are thus quantized.

Let us add that in the chiral model, the interlayer tunneling in the region of AA stacking is artificially switched-off. Nevertheless, in the full description of the continuum limit for a more real twisted bilayer graphene model, there is a persistent localization of zero-energy wavefunction in the region of AA stacking
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
. However, the region of AA stacking is much reduced than AB stacking due to lattice relaxation in real samples with small twist angles
Tarnopolsky et al. 2019
. Consequently, in the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
the interlayer tunneling of AA stacking is neglected, and therefore the chiral limit is recovered. It is also possible to improve the presented study simply by taking into account the hopping between AA atoms at the individual graphene layers. This can be done in a more or less straightforward fashion by using the AA hopping as a perturbation parameter as done with random binary alloys in the band split regimen or in doped graphene
Kirkpatrick and Eggarter 1972
;
Naumis et al. 2002
;
Barrios-Vargas and Naumis 2013
.

Nevertheless, our work does not pretend to completely solve the problem. Instead, we showed that a famous TBG model can be transformed into an effective quantum Hall effect hamiltonian once the hamiltonian is squared. This approach is akin to a supersymmetric transformation which seems to play a role in the proposed equivalence between the squared TBG electron hamiltonian and a phonon hamiltonian for flexible systems
Gerardo et al. 2021
. Flat-band modes are thus mapped into zero-frequency floppy modes produced by a lack of mechanical constraints
Huerta and Naumis 2002
;
Huerta et al. 2004
;
Flores-Ruiz et al. 2010
;
Navarro-Labastida et al. 2021
;
Flores-Ruiz and Naumis 2011
;
Moukarzel and Naumis 2022
.

Due to the multiple mathematical and physical properties of coherent states, this opens many exciting technological and physical possibilities in moiré materials, as for example, the possibility of controlling coherency by manipulation of the twisting angle or the self-duality property of the wave functions
Hofstadter 1976
.
In particular, coherent modes and the strong electron-electron coupling can be used in quantum computation applications
Friis et al. 2015
or electron
analogues to coherent optical effects
Betancur-Ocampo et al. 2019
.

\begin{split}\psi_{\mp}(z,z^{*})&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(f_{\lambda}(z)e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}|z|^{2}}\mp f_{-\lambda}(z)e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}|z|^{2}})\\
&=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2\pi}}(e^{\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\lambda^{*}z-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\lambda\lambda^{*}-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}|z|^{2}}\\
&\mp e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\lambda^{*}z-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\lambda\lambda^{*}-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}|z|^{2}})\\
\end{split}

(64)

where
\lambda
is the center of the Gaussians. Is important to note that Eq. (
64
), satisfies the relation
\psi_{\mp}(-z,-z^{*})=\mp\psi_{\mp}(z,z^{*})
as we consider here the wavefunction as a sum of symmetrized/antisymmetrized functions that can be treated separately. We also have,

\begin{split}|\psi_{\mp}(z,z^{*})&|^{2}\approx\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}(2\pi)}(e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}|z-\lambda|^{2}}+e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}|z+\lambda|^{2}})\end{split}

(65)

where the overlap term between Gaussian’s is neglected in the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
. On the other hand, the coupling potential can be written as,

\begin{split}U(z,z^{*})=-2\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{*}}S(z,z^{*})\end{split}

(66)

where
\bar{\partial}=2\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{*}}
and,

\begin{split}S(z,z^{*})=\sum^{3}_{\mu=1}e^{-i(Q^{*}_{\mu}z+Q_{\mu}z^{*})/2}\end{split}

(67)

where
Q_{\mu}=q^{x}_{\mu}+iq^{y}_{\mu}
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{*}}S(z,z^{*})&=\frac{1}{2}\sum^{3}_{\mu=1}(-iQ_{\mu})e^{-i(Q^{*}_{\mu}z+Q_{\mu}z^{*})/2}\end{split}

(68)

Finally,
U(z,z^{*})
is the complex form of the coupling layer potential,

U(\bm{r})=U(z,z^{*})=\sum^{3}_{\mu=1}(iQ_{\mu})e^{-i(Q^{*}_{\mu}z+Q_{\mu}z^{*})/2}

(69)

Notice that such result can be obtained straightforward from the definition of
U(\bm{r})
and
z
and
z^{*}
, yet is illustrative to use the function
S(z,z^{*})
as this quantity appears in several commutators
Gerardo et al. 2021
.

Substituting Eqns. (
66
)-(
68
) in Eq. (
48
) for magic angles,

\begin{split}&2\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{*}}\psi_{\mp}(z,z^{*})=\pm\alpha_{m}(-2\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{*}}S(z,z^{*}))\psi_{\mp}(-z,-z^{*})\\
&=\pm\alpha_{m}(\sum^{3}_{\mu=1}(iQ_{\mu})e^{-i(Q^{*}_{\mu}z+Q_{\mu}z^{*})/2})\psi_{\mp}(-z,-z^{*})\end{split}

(70)

using the fact that in the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
the wave function is localized in
Q_{\mu}
and in other points is zero, we can expand (
70
) up to first order,

\begin{split}2\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{*}}\psi_{\mp}(z,z^{*})&\approx\pm\alpha_{m}\sum^{3}_{\mu=1}(iQ_{\mu})(1-i\frac{1}{2}(Q^{*}_{\mu}z\\
&+Q_{\mu}z^{*}))\psi_{\mp}(-z,-z^{*})\end{split}

(71)

and since
\sum_{\mu}Q_{\mu}=\sum_{\mu}Q^{2}_{\mu}=0
and
\sum_{\mu}|Q_{\mu}|^{2}=3
, it follows that,

\begin{split}2\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{*}}\psi_{\mp}(z,z^{*})\doteq\left(\frac{3\alpha_{m}}{2}\right)(-z)(\psi_{\mp}(z,z^{*}))\end{split}

(72)

since
\mp\psi_{\mp}(-z,-z^{*})=\psi_{\mp}(z,z^{*})
.
Normalizing, the final form of the ansatz wave function for
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
is,

\begin{split}\psi_{\pm}(z,z^{*})&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{\frac{3\alpha}{2\pi}}\sum^{3}_{\mu=1}(f_{Q_{\mu}}(z)e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}|z|^{2}}\\
&\pm f_{-Q_{\mu}}(z)e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}|z|^{2}})\end{split}

(73)

VI.4
Equivalence between
H^{2}
and the Quantum Harmonic Oscillator

From the square Hamiltonian at the zero flat band
H^{2}\Psi(\bm{r})=0
it follows that,

\begin{split}(-\nabla^{2}+\alpha^{2}|U(-\bm{r})|^{2})\psi_{1}(\bm{r})+\alpha A^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\psi_{2}(\bm{r})=0\end{split}

(74)

which can be rewritten in complex notation and by expanding
|U(-\bm{r})|^{2}
in the boundary layer,

\begin{split}(4p_{z}p^{*}_{z}+\left(\frac{3\alpha}{2}\right)^{2}|z|^{2})\psi(z,z^{*})+i\mu_{\alpha}\alpha A^{\dagger}(z,z^{*})\\
\times\psi(-z,-z^{*})=0\end{split}

(75)

where
A^{\dagger}(\bm{r})=A_{g}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})+A_{f}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})
, with the definitions
Navarro-Labastida et al. 2022
,

A_{g}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})=-2i\sum^{3}_{\mu=1}e^{-i\bm{q}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{r}}\bm{q}^{\perp}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{\nabla}

(76)

and

A_{f}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})=-i\sum^{3}_{\mu=1}e^{-i\bm{q}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{r}}.

(77)

We work first with
A_{g}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})

\begin{split}A_{g}^{\dagger}(z,z^{*})\psi(-z,-z^{*})&=-2i\sum^{3}_{\mu=1}e^{-\frac{i}{2}(Q^{*}_{\mu}z+Q_{\mu}z^{*})}(Q^{\perp,*}_{\mu}\partial_{z^{*}}\\
&+Q^{\perp}_{\mu}\partial_{z})\psi(-z,-z^{*})\end{split}

(78)

Now for
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, we expand the exponential up to first order and considering that
Q^{\perp}_{\mu}=iQ_{\mu}
, it follows that

\begin{split}A_{g}^{\dagger}(z,z^{*})\psi(-z,-z^{*})&=-2i\sum^{3}_{\mu=1}(1-\frac{i}{2}(Q^{*}_{\mu}z+Q_{\mu}z^{*}))\\
\times(iQ_{\mu}\partial_{z}&-iQ_{\mu}^{*}\partial_{z^{*}})\psi(-z,-z^{*})\end{split}

(79)

with
\sum^{3}_{\mu=1}Q_{\mu}=0
and
\sum^{3}_{\mu=1}|Q_{\mu}|^{2}=3
we have that,

\begin{split}A_{g}^{\dagger}(z,z^{*})\psi(-z,-z^{*})&=-3i(z\partial_{z}-z^{*}\partial_{z^{*}})\psi(-z,-z^{*})\\
&=3(zp_{z}-z^{*}p_{z^{*}})\psi(-z,-z^{*})\end{split}

(80)

Making a similar procedure for
A_{f}^{\dagger}(z,z^{*})
we obtain,

A_{f}^{\dagger}(z,z^{*})\psi(-z,-z^{*})\approx-3i\psi(-z,-z^{*})

(81)

Substituting Eq. (
80
) and Eq. (
81
) in Eq. (
75
),

\begin{split}(4p_{z}p^{*}_{z}+&\left(\frac{3\alpha}{2}\right)^{2}|z|^{2})\psi(z,z^{*})+3i\mu_{\alpha}\alpha(zp_{z}\\
&-z^{*}p_{z^{*}})\psi(-z,-z^{*})=-\mu_{\alpha}3\alpha\psi(-z,-z^{*})\end{split}

(82)

However the angular momentum in complex notation is defined as
L_{z}=i(zp_{z}-z^{*}p_{z^{*}})
, and defining the zero-point energy constant
E_{0}=3\alpha
, we have that,

\begin{split}(4p_{z}p^{*}_{z}+\left(\frac{3\alpha}{2}\right)^{2}|z|^{2})\psi(z,z^{*})+3\alpha L_{z}\mu_{\alpha}&\psi(-z,-z^{*})\\
=-\mu_{\alpha}E_{0}\psi(-z,-z^{*})\end{split}

(83)

for odd parity
\mu_{\alpha}=1
the solution is anti-symmetric
\psi(-z,-z^{*})=-\psi(z,z^{*})
and for even parity
\mu_{\alpha}=-1
the solution is symmetric
\psi(-z,-z^{*})=\psi(z,z^{*})
. Therefore, the equation is reduced as,

\begin{split}(4p_{z}p^{*}_{z}+\left(\frac{3\alpha}{2}\right)^{2}|z|^{2}-3\alpha L_{z})\psi(z,z^{*})=E_{0}\psi(z,z^{*})\end{split}

(84)

On the other hand, the quantum harmonic oscillator in complex notation is,

\begin{split}H_{zz^{*}}\psi(z,z^{*})=\left(\frac{2p_{z}p^{*}_{z}}{m}+\left(\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}\right)|z|^{2}\right)\psi(z,z^{*})\end{split}

(85)

from where we identified by comparison with Eq. (
84
) that
m=\frac{1}{2}
and
\omega=3\alpha
, therefore we can define a creation/annihilation operators associated as,

\begin{split}a_{z}=\sqrt{\frac{3\alpha}{4\hbar}}z+i\frac{2}{\sqrt{3\alpha\hbar}}p^{*}_{z}\end{split}

(86)

\begin{split}a^{\dagger}_{z}=\sqrt{\frac{3\alpha}{4\hbar}}z^{*}-i\frac{2}{\sqrt{3\alpha\hbar}}p_{z}\end{split}

(87)

from where,

\begin{split}a^{\dagger}_{z}a_{z}=\frac{3\alpha}{4\hbar}|z|^{2}+\frac{4p_{z}p^{*}_{z}}{3\alpha\hbar}+i\frac{2}{\sqrt{3\alpha\hbar}}\sqrt{\frac{3\alpha}{4\hbar}}(z^{*}p^{*}_{z}-zp_{z})\end{split}

(88)

multiplying both sides of Eq. (
88
) by
3\alpha\hbar
follows that

\begin{split}\hbar(3\alpha)a^{\dagger}_{z}a_{z}&=\left(\frac{3\alpha}{2}\right)^{2}|z|^{2}+4p_{z}p^{*}_{z}-3\alpha L_{z}\\
&=H_{zz^{*}}-3\alpha L_{z}\end{split}

(89)

from where we obtain an effective two-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian,

\begin{split}H_{zz^{*}}\psi(z,z^{*})=\left(\hbar\omega a^{\dagger}_{z}a_{z}+\omega L_{z}\right)\psi(z,z^{*})\end{split}

(90)

We can also define the conjugate operators,

\begin{split}a_{z^{*}}=\sqrt{\frac{3\alpha}{4\hbar}}z^{*}+i\frac{2}{\sqrt{3\alpha\hbar}}p_{z}\end{split}

(91)

\begin{split}a^{\dagger}_{z^{*}}=\sqrt{\frac{3\alpha}{4\hbar}}z-i\frac{2}{\sqrt{3\alpha\hbar}}p^{*}_{z}\end{split}

(92)

and it follows that the angular momentum is,

\begin{split}L_{z}=\frac{\hbar}{2}(a^{\dagger}_{z^{*}}a_{z^{*}}-a^{\dagger}_{z}a_{z})\end{split}

(93)

where
L^{\dagger}_{z}=L_{z}
is Hermitian and therefore Eq. (
90
) is rewritten as,

\begin{split}H_{zz^{*}}\psi(z,z^{*})=\hbar\omega\left(N_{zz^{*}}+1\right)\psi(z,z^{*})\end{split}

(94)

with
N_{zz^{*}}=\frac{1}{2}(a^{\dagger}_{z^{*}}a_{z^{*}}+a^{\dagger}_{z}a_{z})=\frac{1}{2}(N_{z}+N_{z^{*}})
with
N_{z}=a^{\dagger}_{z}a_{z}
and
N_{z^{*}}=a^{\dagger}_{z^{*}}a_{z^{*}}
in analogy to a 2D harmonic oscillator where
\omega=3\alpha
. The constant term
\hbar\omega
is the zero-point energy of the oscillator which in this case is exactly
E_{0}=3\alpha
and comes from the
A^{\dagger}_{f}
operator.
