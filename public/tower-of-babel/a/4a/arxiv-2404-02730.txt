Two new constructions in the theory of projection complexes
Patrick S. Nairne

Abstract
. In this paper we provide two new constructions that are useful for the theory of projection complexes developed by Bestvina, Bromberg, Fujiwara and Sisto. We prove that there exists a subtree of the projection complex which is quasiisometric to the projection complex. We use this subtree to form a tree of metric spaces, which is a subgraph of the quasi-tree of metrics spaces and quasiisometric to it. These constructions simplify the metric structure (up to quasiisometry) of the projection complex and the quasi-tree of metric spaces. As an application, we use these constructions to provide a shorter proof of Hume’s theorem that the mapping class group admits a quasiisometric embedding into a finite product of trees.

Introduction

1.1.
Results

Various metric spaces can be understood by considering projections of subspaces onto other subspaces. For example, due to work of Sisto, the metric of a relatively hyperbolic group can be understood by considering projections of peripheral cosets onto other peripheral cosets
[
Sis13
]
. Similarly, the metric on the mapping class group
MCG(S)
can be understood by considering the projection of curves in
S
onto curve complexes of subsurfaces of
S

[
MM99
]

[
MM00
]
. Bestvina, Bromberg and Fujiwara
[

]

\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})
and the

\mathcal{C}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})

and

A key result in the theory of projection complexes states that

[

[

]
. In this paper, I am able to prove this result more concretely.

There exists subtree

Furthermore this subtree

has a simple definition: having arbitrarily chosen a basepoint

is the union of all standard paths

Theorem 1.2

And again

2.
Background

2.1.
Projection complexes

The theory of projection complexes and quasi-trees of metric spaces is outlined thoroughly and properly in
[
BBFS19
]
, nonetheless in this section I briefly provide the necessary background.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{Y}}
be an indexing set such that for each
Y\in{\mathbb{Y}}
we have an associated geodesic metric space
{\mathcal{C}}(Y)
. Suppose also that for each
Y\in{\mathbb{Y}}
we have a function

\pi_{Y}:{\mathbb{Y}}\setminus\{Y\}\rightarrow\textrm{non-empty subsets of }{\mathcal{C}}(Y)

We call these functions
projections
. We then define
d_{Y}^{\pi}:{\mathbb{Y}}\setminus\{Y\}\times{\mathbb{Y}}\setminus\{Y\}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
by

d_{Y}^{\pi}(X,Z)=\textrm{diam}(\pi_{Y}(X)\cup\pi_{Y}(Z))

We call these functions the
projection distances
. The collection of maps
\{\pi_{Y}\}
satisfy the
projection axioms
if there exists a constant
\xi<\infty
, known as the
projection constant
, such that

(P3)

\textrm{diam}(\pi_{Y}(X))\leq\xi
for all distinct
X,Y\in{\mathbb{Y}}
;

(P4)

if
X,Y,Z\in{\mathbb{Y}}
are distinct and such that
d_{Y}^{\pi}(X,Z)>\xi
then
d_{X}^{\pi}(Y,Z)\leq\xi
;

(P5)

\{Y:d_{Y}^{\pi}(X,Z)>\xi\}
is finite for each pair
X,Z\in{\mathbb{Y}}\setminus\{Y\}
.

The axioms
(P3)
-
(P5)
are those that appear in "real-life". However we need them to satisfy an extra axiom in order to conduct the theory of projection complexes. The collection of maps
\{\pi_{Y}\}
satisfy the
strong projection axioms
if, in addition to
(P3)
-
(P5)
, the following condition also holds

(P4’)

if
X,Y,Z\in{\mathbb{Y}}
are distinct and such that
d_{Y}^{\pi}(X,Z)>\xi
then
\pi_{X}(Y)=\pi_{X}(Z)
.

Bestvina, Bromberg and Fujiwara prove that, given projections
\{\pi_{Y}\}
satisfying the projection axioms, we can adjust the projections
\pi_{Y}(X)
by a uniformly bounded amount so that the projections
\{\pi_{Y}\}
now satisfy the strong projection axioms with respect to some possibly larger projection constant
\theta
. This is the content of the following theorem (see
[
BBFS19
, Theorem 4.1]
).

Theorem 2.2

(Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara)
.

Suppose we have a collection of maps
\{\pi_{Y}\}
satisfying the projection axioms with projection constant
\xi
. Then there exists a new collection of projections

\{\pi_{Y}^{\prime}:{\mathbb{Y}}\setminus\{Y\}\rightarrow\textrm{non-empty subsets of }{\mathcal{C}}(Y)\}

such that if
d_{Y}(X,Z):=\textrm{diam}(\pi_{Y}^{\prime}(X)\cup\pi_{Y}^{\prime}(Z))
then

•

\pi_{Y}^{\prime}(X)\subseteq N_{\xi}(\pi_{Y}(X))
;

•

\{\pi_{Y}^{\prime}\}
now satisfy the strong projection axioms
(P3)
-
(P5)
and
(P4’)
with respect to the projection constant
\theta=11\xi
;

•

d_{Y}^{\pi}-2\xi\leq d_{Y}\leq d_{Y}^{\pi}+2\xi
.

Further, if all the metric spaces
{\mathcal{C}}(Y)
are graphs, and all the projections
\pi_{Y}(X)\subset{\mathcal{C}}(Y)
are collections of vertices, then
\pi_{Y}^{\prime}(X)\subset{\mathcal{C}}(Y)
is also a collection of vertices.

The
Further
part of the above theorem may be deduced from the definition of
\pi_{Y}^{\prime}(X)
in
[
BBFS19
, Definition 4.11]
. Indeed, one may write
\pi_{Y}^{\prime}(X)
as a union
\bigcup_{W}\pi_{Y}(W)
where the union is over a non-empty set.

Given a collection of projections
\{\pi_{Y}:Y\in{\mathbb{Y}}\}
with projection distances
d_{Y}:{\mathbb{Y}}\setminus\{Y\}\times{\mathbb{Y}}\setminus\{Y\}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
and projection constant
\theta
satisfying the
strong
projection axioms, we can now define standard paths, the projection complex and the quasi-tree of metric spaces.

Definition 2.3
.

For a constant
K\geq 0
, define

{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}(X,Z)=\{Y\in{\mathbb{Y}}:d_{Y}(X,Z)>K\}

and

{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]={\mathbb{Y}}_{K}(X,Z)\cup\{X,Z\}

In what follows, we will also use the notation

L(X,Z)=\lvert{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}(X,Z)\rvert

and

L[X,Z]=\lvert{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]\rvert

One should interpret the statement
Y\in{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}(X,Z)
as saying that
Y
is
between

X
and
Z
.

Definition 2.4
.

The
projection complex
is the graph
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})
with vertex set
{\mathbb{Y}}
and an edge between two vertices if
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}(X,Z)=\emptyset
.

We now state three results which describe the structure of the sets
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]
. These are Lemma 2.3, Proposition 2.4 and Corollary 2.6 of
[
BBFS19
]
respectively.

Lemma 2.5

(Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara)
.

For
K\geq 2\theta
, and
Y,Y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}(X,Z)
, the following conditions are equivalent

(1)

d_{Y}(X,Y^{\prime})>\theta
;

(2)

d_{Y^{\prime}}(Y,Z)>\theta
;

(3)

d_{Y}(Y^{\prime},Z)\leq\theta
;

(4)

d_{Y^{\prime}}(X,Y)\leq\theta
;

(5)

d_{Y}(Y^{\prime},W)=d_{Y}(Z,W)
for all
W\neq Y
;

(6)

d_{Y^{\prime}}(Y,W)=d_{Y^{\prime}}(X,W)
for all
W\neq Y^{\prime}
.

Given
K\geq 2\theta
and two distinct elements
Y,Y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}(X,Z)
, we write
Y<Y^{\prime}
if any of the above equivalent conditions hold. Write
X<Y
and
Y<Z
for all
Y\in{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}(X,Z)
and write
X<Z
.

Proposition 2.6

(Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara)
.

Suppose
K\geq 2\theta
. The relation
<
on
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]
defines a total order with least element
X
and greatest element
Z
. Further, if
Y<Y^{\prime}<Y^{\prime\prime}
in
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]
then
d_{Y^{\prime}}(Y,Y^{\prime\prime})=d_{Y^{\prime}}(X,Z)
.

Corollary 2.7

(Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara)
.

Let
K\geq 2\theta
. Suppose
Y,Y^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]
and for some
Y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Y}}
we have
d_{Y^{\prime}}(Y,Y^{\prime\prime})>K
. Then
Y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}(X,Z)
.

We now state some important facts about the graph
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})
. These are Lemma 3.1, Theorem 3.5, Lemma 3.6 and Corollary 3.7 from
[
BBFS19
]
respectively.

Lemma 2.8

(Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara)
.

Let
K\geq 2\theta
. Suppose
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]=\{X<X_{1}<\dots<X_{k}<Z\}
. Then

X\rightarrow X_{1}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow X_{k}\rightarrow Z

is a path in
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})
.

We refer to the sets
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]
as
standard paths
in
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})
. Note that for any
X,Z\in{\mathbb{Y}}
we have
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]={\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[Z,X]
as a set, and
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[Z,X]
is the path
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]

Theorem 2.9

(Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara)
.

For
K\geq 3\theta
,
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})
is a quasi-tree.

Lemma 2.10

(Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara)
.

Suppose
K\geq 2\theta
. Let
X,Y,Z
be distinct elements of
{\mathbb{Y}}
. Write
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Y]=\{X=Y_{0}<Y_{1}<\dots<Y_{k}<Y_{k+1}<Y\}

•

•

•

Suppose again that

(P5)

Definition 2.12
.

The

\mathcal{C}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})
is built by taking the disjoint union of the metric spaces

and then adding an edge of length
K
between every element of
\pi_{X}(Y)
and every element of
\pi_{Y}(X)
whenever there is an edge between
X
and
Y
in
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})

and

.

The name of

comes from the fact that crushing all the disjoint metric spaces

that make up

.

It is useful to extend the definitions of

,

and

over to elements

Definition 2.13
.

For

and
z\in{\mathcal{C}}(Z)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})
and

•

we define

\pi_{Y}(x)=\begin{cases}\pi_{Y}(X)&\text{if $Y\neq X$}\\
\{x\}&\textrm{if $Y=X$}\end{cases}
;

•

we define

where the diameter is taken with respect to the metric on

;

•

we define
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}(x,z)=\{Y\in{\mathbb{Y}}:d_{Y}(x,z)>K\}
.

We then have the following distance formula for

, Theorem 6.4]

(Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara)

If

, then for all

A quasiisometric subtree of the projection complex

Suppose we have a collection of projections
\{\pi_{Y}:Y\in{\mathbb{Y}}\}
with projection distances
d_{Y}:{\mathbb{Y}}\setminus\{Y\}\times{\mathbb{Y}}\setminus\{Y\}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
and projection constant

(P3)
-
(P5)
and

Definition 3.1
.

Let
B\in{\mathbb{Y}}
be a fixed basepoint. This corresponds to a vertex of
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})
. We can define the following subgraph of
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})

{\mathcal{T}}_{K}({\mathbb{Y}},B):=\bigcup_{X\in{\mathbb{Y}}}{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[B,X]

We will generally write

.

The subgraph

First we need a preliminary lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]=\{X=X_{0}<X_{1}<\dots<X_{k}<X_{k+1}=Z\}

Proof of

.

We begin by proving the following claim.

Claim
.

Suppose we have a standard path

connected by an edge (in

) to

Proof of claim.

Since the edge from

, we know that there exists some vertex

. Suppose for a contradiction that

and

. Then, by

,

and

. By

, we know that

to

.

Suppose in

we have a cycle

and

Theorem 3.4
.

The inclusion map
{\mathcal{T}}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})
is a quasiisometry.

Proof.

Evidently the inclusion map is Lipschitz; it remains to prove the lower bound of the quasiisometric inequality.

Consider the triple

. By
Lemma

2.10
, the path
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]
can be described as follows: an initial segment that is also initial within
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,B]
, a middle segment of length at most
2
, and a terminal segment that is also terminal in
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[B,Z]
. Let
Y^{\prime}
be the vertex at which
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]
diverges from
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,B]
and let
Y^{\prime\prime}
be the vertex at which
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]
joins
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[B,Z]
. Let
Y
be the vertex at which
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[B,X]
and
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[B,Z]
diverge. By
Lemma

2.10
we have

(1)

L[X,Y]\leq L[X,Y^{\prime}]+3\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qn.ub1}

and

(2)

L[Y,Z]\leq L[Y^{\prime\prime},Z]+3\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qn.ub2}

Now, by the definition of
{\mathcal{T}}
, we have that

(3)

d_{\mathcal{T}}(X,Z)=d_{\mathcal{T}}(X,Y)+d_{\mathcal{T}}(Y,Z)=L[X,Y]-1+L[Y,Z]-1\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qn.treedist}

We also know that

(4)

L[X,Z]\geq L[X,Y^{\prime}]+L[Y^{\prime\prime},Z]-1\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qn.lb1}

Hence

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})}(X,Z)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}(L[X,Z]-1)

by

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}(L[X,Y^{\prime}]+L[Y^{\prime\prime},Z]-1)

by (
4
)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}(L[X,Y]-3+L[Y,Z]-3-1)

by (
1
) and (
2
)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(d_{\mathcal{T}}(X,Z)-5)

by (
3
)

and so we are done.

4.
The tree of metric spaces

We can use
{\mathcal{T}}={\mathcal{T}}({\mathbb{Y}},B)

tree

Definition 4.1
.

For each pair

which are connected by an edge in

, fix an arbitrary element
p(X,Y)\in\pi_{X}(Y)\subset{\mathcal{C}}(X)
and an arbitrary element

Remark 4.2

All sensible logic suggests that we should be using the notation

Evidently, there exists a natural inclusion map
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbb{Y})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})

Theorem 4.3
.

The inclusion
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbb{Y})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})
is a quasiisometry.

We first need a lemma about the projection complex.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

be distinct vertices of
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{Y})
.

(1)

If

is an element of
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Y]
and
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]
but not
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[Y,Z]
then
d_{W}(Y,Z)\leq K
.

(2)

If

is an element of
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Y]
but not
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[X,Z]
and not
{\mathbb{Y}}_{K}[Y,Z]

.

Proof.

The first statement is immediate. For the second, note that
d_{W}(X,Y)\leq d_{W}(X,Z)+d_{W}(Z,Y)\leq 2K
.
∎

A new proof of Hume’s theorem

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
S_{g,n}
be a compact surface. Then
MCG(S_{g,n})
admits a quasiisometric embedding into a finite product of trees.

Proof.

Bestvina, Bromberg and Fujiwara
[
BBF15
, Theorem C]
prove that there exists a quasiisometric embedding of the mapping class group into a finite product of quasiitrees of metric spaces

MCG(S_{g,n})\rightarrow\prod_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{C}}_{K}({\mathbb{Y}}^{i})

where the metric spaces
{\mathcal{C}}(Y)\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{K}({\mathbb{Y}}^{i})
are curve complexes of subsurfaces of
S_{g,n}
. By
Theorem

1.2
, we know that
{\mathcal{C}}_{K}({\mathbb{Y}}^{i})
is quasiisometric to
{\mathcal{C}}_{K}^{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbb{Y}}^{i})
which is a
tree of curve complexes
. It can be proved using the results of
[
MM99
]

[
BF05
]

[
MS13
]

[
Buy05
]
that the curve complexes
{\mathcal{C}}(Y)
admit a quasiisometric embedding into a finite product of trees (see
[
Hum17
, Proposition 5.2]
and
