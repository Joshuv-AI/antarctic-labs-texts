Entropy for actions of free groups under bounded orbit-equivalence
Lewis Bowen, Yuqing (Frank) Lin

Abstract
The f f -invariant is a notion of entropy for probability-measure-preserving actions of free groups. We show it is invariant under bounded orbit-equivalence.



Keywords
: orbit equivalence,
f
-invariant

MSC
: 37A35

1
Introduction

The goal of this note is to prove that the
f
-invariant, which is a generalization of Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy to actions of free groups, is invariant under
\textrm{L}^{1}
-orbit-equivalence. We begin by quickly going over the history of entropy theory and orbit-equivalence to set notation and motivate the main result.

1.1
Notation

Throughout, we will denote standard probability spaces by
(X,\mu)
,
(Y,\nu)
, etc and their Borel sigma-algebras by
{\mathcal{B}}_{X},{\mathcal{B}}_{Y}
etc. If
G
is a countable group then a
pmp action
of
G
is a tuple
(X,\mu,T,G)
where
(X,\mu)
is a standard probability space and for every
g\in G
,
T^{g}:X\to X
is a measure-preserving transformation satisfying
T^{gh}=T^{g}\circ T^{h}
(pmp is short for probability-measure-preserving). In the special case that
G={\mathbb{Z}}
, a pmp action is also called a
pmp transformation
.

Let
(Y,\nu,U,H)
be another pmp action where
H
is a countable group,
(Y,\nu)
a standard probability space and
U=(U^{h})_{h\in H}
a pmp action of
H
on
Y
. Let
\Phi:(X,\mu)\to(Y,\nu)
be a measure-space isomorphism.

•

\Phi
is an
orbit-equivalence
(OE) if for a.e.
x\in X
,

\Phi(T^{G}x)=U^{H}\Phi(x)

where, for example,
T^{G}x=\{T^{g}x:~g\in G\}
is the
T^{G}
-orbit of
x
.

•

\Phi
is a
measure-conjugacy
if
G=H
and
\Phi(T^{g}x)=U^{g}\Phi(x)
for a.e.
x\in X
and every
g\in G
.

Measure-conjugacy implies orbit-equivalence and the converse is false in general.

1.2
Classical entropy theory

Classical entropy theory was introduced by Kolmogorov in order to classify Bernoulli shifts up to measure conjugacy
[
Kol58
,
Kol59
]
. Given a countable group
G
and a Borel probability space
(K,\kappa)
, the
Bernoulli shift over
G
with base
(K,\kappa)
is the product probability space
(K^{G},\kappa^{G})
with the
G
-action
G{\curvearrowright}K^{G}
given by

(g\cdot x)_{f}=x(g^{-1}f),\quad g,f\in G,x\in K^{G}.

For example, if
x
is a random element of
K^{G}
with law
\kappa^{G}
then
(x_{g})_{g\in G}
is a
G
-indexed i.i.d. process in which each variable
x_{g}
has law
\kappa
.

The
Shannon entropy
of the base space is defined by

H(K,\kappa)=-\sum_{k\in K}\kappa(k)\log\kappa(k)

if
\kappa
is concentrated on a countable set. Otherwise,
H(K,\kappa):=+\infty
.

Now suppose
(X,\mu)
is a standard probability space. An
observable
on
X
is a measurable map
\phi:X\to\mathtt{A}
where
\mathtt{A}
is a finite or countable set. If
\psi:X\to\mathtt{B}
is another observable, then the
join
of
\phi
and
\psi
is the observable

\phi\vee\psi:X\to\mathtt{A}\times\mathtt{B},\quad\phi\vee\psi(x)=(\phi(x),\psi(x)).

The
Shannon entropy
of the observable
\phi
with respect to the measure
\mu
is

H_{\mu}(\phi)=H(\mathtt{A},\phi_{*}\mu)=-\sum_{a\in\mathtt{A}}\mu(\phi^{-1}(a))\log\mu(\phi^{-1}(a)).

If
T:X\to X
is measure-preserving then the
entropy rate of
(X,\mu,T,\phi)
is

h_{\mu}(T,\phi):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}H_{\mu}(\phi\vee\phi\circ T\vee\cdots\vee\phi\circ T^{n-1}).

The
entropy rate
of
T
is defined to be the supremum of entropy rates
h_{\mu}(T,\phi)
over all finite Shannon entropy observables
\phi
:

h_{\mu}(T)=\sup\{h_{\mu}(T,\phi):~H_{\mu}(\phi)<\infty\}.

This is a measure-conjugacy invariant. Moreover, Kolmogorov proved the entropy rate of a Bernoulli shift over
{\mathbb{Z}}
is the same as the Shannon entropy of its base space
[
Kol58
,
Kol59
]
. This proves one direction of Ornstein’s Isomorphism Theorem
[
Orn70
]
: Bernoulli shifts over
{\mathbb{Z}}
are measurably conjugate if and only if they have the same base space entropy. All of these results were extended to actions of countably infinite amenable groups
[
OW80
,
OW87
]
. For example, if
G
is a countable amenable group then two Bernoulli shifts over
G
are measurably conjugate if and only if they have the same base space entropy.

1.3
Orbit equivalence (the amenable case)

In
[
Dye59
,
Dye63
]
, Dye proved that all ergodic aperiodic pmp transformations are orbit-equivalent (where aperiodic means that a.e. orbit is infinite). In
[
OW80
]
it was announced that all essentially free ergodic pmp actions of countably infinite amenable groups are OE. A complete proof appears in
[
CFW81
]
. In particular, entropy is not an OE-invariant.

1.4
Quantitative orbit equivalence

Suppose that
\Phi:X\to Y
is an orbit-equivalence as above. Also suppose both actions are essentially free. Then there are cocycles
\alpha:G\times X\to H
,
\beta:H\times Y\to G
defined by

U^{\alpha(g,x)}\Phi(x)=\Phi(T^{g}x),\quad T^{\beta(h,y)}\Phi^{-1}(y)=\Phi^{-1}(U^{h}y).

These cocycles satisfy the identities

\displaystyle\alpha(gh,x)=\alpha(g,T^{h}x)\alpha(h,x),

\displaystyle\beta(gh,y)=\beta(g,U^{h}y)\beta(h,y)

(1)

\displaystyle\alpha(\beta(g,y),\Phi^{-1}(y))=g,

\displaystyle\beta(\alpha(g,x),\Phi(x))=g.

(2)

Suppose
G
and
H
are both finitely generated groups. After choosing finite generating sets, we may let
|\cdot|_{G}:G\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
|\cdot|_{H}:H\to{\mathbb{R}}
denote word-length functions. The map
\Phi
is said to be an
\textrm{L}^{p}
- orbit equivalence
(
\textrm{L}^{p}
-OE) if for every
g\in G
and
h\in H
the functions

x\mapsto|\alpha(g,x)|_{H},\quad y\mapsto|\beta(h,y)|_{G}

are in
\textrm{L}^{p}(X,\mu)
and
\textrm{L}^{p}(Y,\nu)
respectively.
\textrm{L}^{1}
-OE is also called
integrable OE
and
\textrm{L}^{\infty}
-OE is also called
bounded OE
. These notions do not depend on the choice of finite generating sets. In
[
Aus16
]
, Austin proved that entropy is an
\textrm{L}^{1}
-orbit equivalence invariant for actions of finitely generated amenable groups.

The map
\Phi
is said to a
Shannon orbit equivalence
if for every
g\in G
and
h\in H
, the observables
\alpha(g,\cdot):X\to H
and
\beta(h,\cdot):Y\to G
have finite Shannon entropies. In
[
KL21
]
Kerr and Li proved that if each of
G
and
H
contain a w-normal amenable subgroup that is neither locally finite nor virtually cyclic then entropy for their actions is invariant under Shannon orbit equivalence, where entropy means maximum sofic entropy. The groups are not required to be amenable or finitely generated but they are required to be sofic. The statement is false for locally finite groups by a counterexample due to Vershik
[
Ver94
]
. In fact, Vershik’s counterexample is with a bounded orbit-equivalence (but using non-finitely generated groups).

1.5
Entropy for non-amenable groups

For a long time, there was no entropy theory for actions of non-amenable groups. Ornstein and Weiss exhibited an example of a factor map between Bernoulli shifts over a non-abelian free group in which the base space entropy of the source is smaller than the base space entropy of the target
[
OW87
]
. By contrast, entropy for amenable groups cannot increase under a factor map. In spite of this it is possible to define entropy for actions of non-amenable groups. Today there are several versions of entropy: sofic, Rokhlin, naive, etc (see
[
Bow20
]
for a survey). We will focus on the
f
-invariant which is a flavor of entropy specifically tailored to actions of free groups.

1.6
The
f
-invariant

Let
G=\langle S\rangle
denote the rank
r
free group with generating set
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\}
. Let
(X,\mu,T,G)
be a pmp action of
G
and
\phi:X\to\mathtt{A}
be an observable. For any subset
H\subset G
, let
\phi^{T,H}:X\to\mathtt{A}^{H}
be the join

\phi^{T,H}=\bigvee_{h\in H}\phi\circ T^{h^{-1}}.

Let
\mathrm{B}(e,\rho)\subset G
denote the ball of radius
\rho
centered at the identity in
G
with respect to the word metric (where
e\in G
denotes the identity). Write
\phi^{T,\rho}=\phi^{T,\mathrm{B}(e,\rho)}
.

Define

\displaystyle F_{\mu}(T,\phi)

\displaystyle=(1-2r)\mathrm{H}_{\mu}(\phi)+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathrm{H}_{\mu}(\phi^{T,\{e,s_{i}\}})

\displaystyle f_{\mu}(T,\phi)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\rho}F_{\mu}(T,\phi^{T,\rho})=\lim_{\rho\to\infty}F_{\mu}(T,\phi^{T,\rho}).

An observable
\phi
is
T
-generating
if the smallest
T^{G}
-invariant Borel sigma-algebra in which
\phi
is measurable is the full Borel sigma-algebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{X}
, up to sets of measure zero. If there exists an observable
\phi
which has finite Shannon entropy
H_{\mu}(\phi)<\infty
and is
T
-generating then the action
(X,\mu,T,G)
is said to be
finitely generated
. This terminology is justified by Seward’s generalization of Krieger’s Theorem
[
Sew19
]
which implies that if
(X,\mu,T,G)
is finitely generated then there exists a
T
-generating observable
\phi:X\to\mathtt{A}
such that
\mathtt{A}
is finite.

The main theorem of
[
Bow10d
]
is that if
(X,\mu,T,G)
is finitely generated then there is a number
f_{\mu}(T)\in[-\infty,\infty)
called the
f
-invariant
such that every
T
-generating finite Shannon entropy observable
\phi
satisfies
f_{\mu}(T,\phi)=f_{\mu}(T)
. If the action is not finitely generated then the
f
-invariant is not defined.

In
[
Bow10d
]
, it is shown that the
f
-invariant of a Bernoulli shift action equals the Shannon entropy of its base space. So the
f
-invariant distinguishes Bernoulli shifts. However, in
[
Bow11
]
it is shown that all Bernoulli shifts of a free group are OE. In particular, the
f
-invariant is not an OE-invariant.

The main theorem of this paper is:

Theorem 1.1
.

The
f
-invariant is invariant under bounded-orbit-equivalence. To be precise, suppose
G
is a free group and
(X,\mu,T,G)
,
(Y,\nu,U,G)
are finitely generated essentially free pmp actions of
G
. If these actions are bounded orbit-equivalent then
f_{\mu}(T)=f_{\nu}(U)
.

Remark 1
.

Belinskaya proved that if two ergodic aperiodic pmp transformations
T,U
are
\textrm{L}^{1}
-OE then they are either measurably conjugate or flip-conjugate (which means
T
is measurably conjugate to
U^{-1}
)
[
Bel68
]
. So if
G={\mathbb{Z}}
then Theorem
1.1
is trivial. This motivates the question: if
G,T,U
are as in Theorem
1.1
, then must
T
be measurably conjugate to
U\circ\alpha
for some automorphism
\alpha:G\to G
? A recent work-in-progress due to Matthieu Joseph shows the answer is ‘no’ by an explicit counterexample.

1.7
Related literature

About the problem of classifying Bernoulli shifts up to measure-conjugacy: Seward proved that if two probability spaces have the same Shannon entropy then the corresponding Bernoulli shifts are measurably conjugate, for any countably infinite group
[
Sew18
]
. The converse holds for sofic groups
[
Bow10b
,
KL11
]
.

About the problem of classifying Bernoulli shifts up to OE: if a group
G
is Bernoulli cocycle-superrigid, then it is immediate that if two Bernoulli shifts over
G
are OE then they are measurably-conjugate. This notion is implicit in ground-breaking work of Popa where it is proven that
G
is Bernoulli cocycle-superrigid if it contains an infinite normal subgroup
N
such that either (i) the pair
(G,N)
has relative property (T), or (ii)
N
is generated by (element-wise) commuting subgroups
H
and
K
, with
H
nonamenable and
K
infinite (
[
Pop07
, Theorem 0.1]
and
[
Pop08
, Theorem 4.1]
). Recent work show that a type of entropy called weak Pinsker entropy is OE-invariant for all essentially free pmp actions of Bernoulli cocycle-superrigid groups
[
BTD18
]
. On the other hand, there are two classes of groups for which it is known that all Bernoulli shifts are OE. These are countably infinite amenable groups
[
OW80
,
CFW81
]
and free products of amenable groups
[
Bow11
]
. It remain a very interesting open problem whether fundamental groups of closed surfaces of genus
\geq 2
have this property.

Kammeyer and Rudolph found a unified approach to Dye’s and Ornstein’s Theorem which also gives explicit restrictions on orbit-equivalence which imply entropy-invariance
[
KR02
]
, for actions of amenable groups.

Kerr and Li find conditions under which topological sofic entropy is preserved under continuous orbit equivalence in
[
KL20
]
.

Rudolph and Weiss proved that if
T
and
S
are orbit-equivalent pmp ergodic essentially free actions of amenable groups, then their entropies
relative to their orbit-change sigma-algebras
are equal
[
RW00
]
. This insight was developed into a technique for generalizing entropy-theory results for
{\mathbb{Z}}
-actions to actions of arbitrary amenable groups
[
Dan01
,
DP02
]
.

1.8
Outline of the proof and paper

The techniques we use are completely different from the works of Austin
[
Aus16
]
and Kerr-Li
[
KL21
]
,
[
KL20
]
.

By
[
Bow10a
]
, we know that the
f
-invariant is the exponential growth rate of the average number of approximate periodic points (or microstates) which are approximately equidistributed with respect to the given measure. This result is strengthened in §
2
for the special case in which the invariant measure
\mu
is supported on a subshift of finite type (SFT). In this case, we find that, to compute the
f
-invariant, it suffices to count actual periodic points in the SFT. This is very special to the free group. For example, the analogous statement is false for actions of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, because of the existence of SFTs without periodic points.

In §
3
we set notation for the rest of the paper. In particular, we assume
(X,\mu)=(Y,\nu)
and
T,U
are essentially free actions of
G
on
(X,\mu)
with the same orbits. There are cocycles
\alpha:G\times X\to G
,
\beta:G\times X\to G
defined by

U^{\alpha(g,x)}x=T^{g}x,\quad T^{\beta(g,x)}x=U^{g}x.

The idea now is to show that, after replacing the system
(X,\mu,T,G)
with a measurably conjugate system, we may assume
X
is a SFT. Moreover, we can design the SFT so that
\alpha(g,\cdot)
and
\beta(g,\cdot)
are continuous functions of
X
. Then any periodic point for the system
(X,\mu,T,G)
can be re-arranged to obtain a periodic point for
(X,\mu,U,G)
(and vice versa). Because the
f
-invariant is the exponential growth rate of approximately-equidistributed periodic points, this proves the main Theorem
1.1
. This is shown in §
4
and §
5
. The last section §
A
is devoted to open problems.

2
Symbolic dynamics

Let
\mathtt{A}
be a countable or finite alphabet,
\mathtt{A}^{G}
be the set of functions
x:G\to\mathtt{A}
and
G
act on
\mathtt{A}^{G}
by

(gx)(f)=x(g^{-1}f)\quad\forall f,g\in G,x\in\mathtt{A}^{G}.

Symbolic dynamics is the study of
G
-invariant measures and subspaces of
\mathtt{A}^{G}
.

Whenever we are working with symbolic dynamical systems we always use the left shift action described above and as seen above we do not use a symbol such as
T
for the action. We also write
f_{\mu}(\phi)
instead of
f_{\mu}(T,\phi)
for example and we denote the system by
(\mathtt{A}^{G},\mu,G)
.

In the first subsection below, we recall a formula for the
f
-invariant of an invariant measure
\mu
on
\mathtt{A}^{G}
in terms of counting periodic points. In the second subsection, we assume the support of
\mu
is contained in a subshift of finite type
Z\subset\mathtt{A}^{G}
and prove a formula for the
f
-invariant in terms of counting periodic points
which lie in
Z
. This second formula is crucial to our proof of Theorem
1.1
. It seems to be a very special fact about free groups. For example, the analogous statement fails for the group
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
for any
d\geq 2
because of the existence of subshifts of finite type which contain no periodic points.

2.1
The
f
-invariant via periodic points

In this section let
\mathtt{A}
be a finite set. First we approximate the action of
G
on itself by an action of
G
on a finite set. So let
\sigma:G\to\operatorname{sym}(n)
be a homomorphism into the symmetric group on
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Next we consider observables
\mathnormal{x}:[n]\to\mathtt{A}
whose local statistics approximate
\mu
. To make this precise, define the
pullback name of
\mathnormal{x}
at vertex
v\in[n]
by

\mathnormal{x}^{\sigma}_{v}\in\mathtt{A}^{G},\quad\mathnormal{x}^{\sigma}_{v}(g)=\mathnormal{x}(\sigma(g)^{-1}v).

We observe that the map
v\mapsto\mathnormal{x}^{\sigma}_{v}
is equivariant in the sense that

\mathnormal{x}^{\sigma}_{\sigma(g)v}=g\mathnormal{x}^{\sigma}_{v}

for any
g\in G
. In particular,
\mathnormal{x}^{\sigma}_{v}
is a periodic point of
\mathtt{A}^{G}
(that is, it has a finite
G
-orbit).

The
empirical distribution
of
\mathnormal{x}
is defined by

P^{\sigma}_{\mathnormal{x}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in[n]}\delta_{\mathnormal{x}^{\sigma}_{v}}\in\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}^{G})

where
\delta_{\mathnormal{x}^{\sigma}_{v}}
is the Dirac probability measure concentrated on
{\mathnormal{x}^{\sigma}_{v}}
and
\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}^{G})
is the space of all Borel probability measures on
\mathtt{A}^{G}
.

Informally, we consider
\mathnormal{x}
to be a good approximation to
\mu
if
P^{\sigma}_{\mathnormal{x}}
is close to
\mu
. To make this notion precise, recall that the weak* topology on
\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}^{G})
is the weakest topology with the following property: for every continuous function
f:\mathtt{A}^{G}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, the map
\mu\mapsto\int f~d\mu
is a continuous function on
\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}^{G})
with respect to the weak* topology. Thus a sequence
(\mu_{i})_{i}
weak* converges to a measure
\mu_{\infty}
if and only if: for every continuous
f:\mathtt{A}^{G}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
\int f~d\mu_{i}\to\int f~d\mu_{\infty}
as
i\to\infty
. By the Banach-Alaoglu Theorem,
\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}^{G})
is compact.

Now let
{\mathcal{O}}\subset\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}^{G})
be a weak* open neighborhood of
\mu
and define

\Omega(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}}):=\{\mathnormal{x}\in\mathtt{A}^{n}\,:\,P^{\sigma}_{\mathnormal{x}}\in{\mathcal{O}}\}.

Then
\Omega(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})
is the set of all observables on
[n]
whose empirical distributions are in
{\mathcal{O}}
.

For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
u_{n}=\operatorname{Unif}(\operatorname{Hom}(G,\operatorname{sym}(n)))
be the uniform probability measure on the set of homomorphisms from
G
to
\operatorname{sym}(n)
. The main result of
[
Bow10a
]
is the formula

\displaystyle f_{\mu}(\mathtt{A}^{G})=\inf_{{\mathcal{O}}\ni\mu}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\sigma\sim u_{n}}\lvert\Omega(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})\rvert.

(3)

The goal of the next section is to make a small but crucial change to the formula above in the special case in which the support of
\mu
is contained in a subshift of finite type
Z\subset\mathtt{A}^{G}
.

2.2
Subshifts of finite type

Definition 1

(Subshifts of finite type)
.

As above, let
G
be a countable group,
\mathtt{A}
a finite set and
G{\curvearrowright}\mathtt{A}^{G}
the left shift action:
(gx)(f)=x(g^{-1}f)
for
g,f\in G,x\in\mathtt{A}^{G}
. A subshift
Z\subset\mathtt{A}^{G}
is a closed
G
-invariant subspace. It has
finite type
if there exists a finite collection
{\mathcal{W}}
of maps
w:D_{w}\to\mathtt{A}
such that

1.

D_{w}\subset G
is finite for all
w\in{\mathcal{W}}
;

2.

Z
is the set of all
x\in\mathtt{A}^{G}
satisfying: for all
g\in G
and
w\in{\mathcal{W}}
, the restriction of
gx
to
D_{w}
is not equal to
w
.

So
{\mathcal{W}}
is a set of forbidden patterns and
Z
is the subshift of finite type (SFT)
determined by
{\mathcal{W}}
.

If
G=\langle s_{1},...,s_{r}\rangle
and for each
w\in{\mathcal{W}}
there exists an
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
such that the domain
D_{w}=\{e,s_{i}\}
, then
we say
Z
is a
nearest neighbor
subshift of finite type. In other words a nearest neighbor subshift forbids only certain edge patterns.

Definition 2
.

Let
Z\subset\mathtt{A}^{G}
be a subshift of finite type. With notation as in the previous section, let
\Omega_{Z}(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})
be the set of all
\mathnormal{x}\in\Omega(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})
such that
P^{\sigma}_{\mathnormal{x}}(Z)=1
. This occurs precisely when
\mathnormal{x}^{\sigma}_{v}\in Z
for all
v\in[n]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose
\mathtt{A}
is finite and
Z\subset\mathtt{A}^{G}
is a subshift of finite type. Let
\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}^{G})
be a
G
-invariant Borel probability measure concentrated on
Z
(meaning
\mu(Z)=1
). Let

f^{Z}_{\mu}(\mathtt{A}^{G})=\inf_{{\mathcal{O}}\ni\mu}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\sigma\sim u_{n}}\lvert\Omega_{Z}(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})\rvert.

Then

f_{\mu}(\mathtt{A}^{G})=f^{Z}_{\mu}(\mathtt{A}^{G}).

Because
\Omega_{Z}(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})\subset\Omega(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})
, it is immediate that
f^{Z}_{\mu}(\mathtt{A}^{G})\leq f_{\mu}(\mathtt{A}^{G})
. So it suffices to prove the opposite inequality.

Remark 2
.

Suppose
G
is a countable group and
Z\subset\mathtt{A}^{G}
is a subshift of finite type which has no periodic points (i.e., no points with finite
G
-orbit) but does admit an invariant probability measure. In this case, no analog of Theorem
2.1
can hold. The first proof that
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
admits such an SFT is due to Berger
[
Ber66
]
. For context, a subshift of finite type that has no periodic points is called
weakly aperiodic
. An SFT on which the group acts freely is called
strongly aperiodic
. There is an interesting line of research whose goal is to determine which groups admit weakly or strongly aperiodic SFTs (e.g.
[
AK13
,
CGS17
,
Coh17
,
Coh20
]
).

The proof of Theorem
2.1
will take up the rest of this section. We assume from now on that
G=\langle s_{1},...,s_{r}\rangle
is a free group of rank
r
and
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\}
.

2.2.1
Proof sketch

In the special case in which
\mu
is a Markov chain which assigns rational numbers to cylinder sets, the proof follows quickly from
[
Bow10c
,
Bow10a
]
. We obtain the full theorem from by approximating an arbitrary invariant measure by Markov chains. These approximating Markov chains are typically not nearest-neighbor and so we will have to work with general observables
\phi:\mathtt{A}^{G}\to\mathtt{C}
with respect to which a measure might be Markov.

We begin by introducing restricted versions of the
f
-invariant and the function
F
. Then we discuss Markov chains and establish a number of lemmas before finishing the proof.

2.2.2
Restricted versions of
f
and
F

We will make the following assumptions for the rest of this section

Proof of Lemma

(1).

We need an analogue of Lemma 2.3 from
[
Bow10a
]
. To motivate it, suppose that
\nu
is an invariant probability measure on
\mathtt{A}^{G}
. For
a,b\in\mathtt{A}
and

, define

W_{\nu}(a)=\nu(\{z\in\mathtt{A}^{G}:~z(e)=a\})

W_{\nu}(a,b;i)=\nu(\{z\in\mathtt{A}^{G}:~z(e)=a\textrm{ and }z(s_{i})=b\}).

Then
W_{\nu}
is a weight. Formally, a

is a function

W:\mathtt{A}\sqcup(\mathtt{A}\times\mathtt{A}\times[r])\to[0,1]

satisfying the conditions below.

1.

(Balanced). For every

, and every
a\in\mathtt{A}
,
W(a)=\sum_{b\in\mathtt{A}}W(a,b;i)=\sum_{b\in\mathtt{A}}W(b,a;i).

2.

(Normalized).
\sum_{a}W(a)=1
.

Given two weights
W_{1}
,
W_{2}
define
d(W_{1},W_{2})=\sum_{(a,b;i)\in\mathtt{A}\times\mathtt{A}\times[r]}|W_{1}(a,b;i)-W_{2}(a,b;i)|
.

A weight
W
is a
Y
-weight
if for every
(a,b;i)\in\mathtt{A}\times\mathtt{A}\times[r]
, if there does not exist
x\in X
with
\Phi(x)\in Y
,
x(e)=a
and
x(s_{i})=b
then
W(a,b;i)=0
. If the measure
\nu
is supported on
\Phi^{-1}(Y)
then
W_{\nu}
is a
Y
-weight.

A
Y
-weight as defined above induces an invariant transition system as defined in section 7 in
[
Bow10c
]
, and there it is shown that an invariant transition system induces a Markov process. In particular, for every
Y
-weight
W
, there is a unique Markov measure
\nu
such that
W_{\nu}=W
.

The equations defining
Y
-weights are linear equations with rational coefficients. Therefore, the subset of rational-valued

-weights is dense (with respect to the distance function defined above) in the space of all

-weights. It is straightforward to check that convergence of a sequence of weights implies convergence in weak* of the associated Markov measures and that a rational-valued

-weight corresponds to a Markov measure with rational probabilities. So this implies the lemma.

Also note that the above definitions and (
4

(5)

Note

We would like to say that the maps

and

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(h^{-1})^{-1}\phi(h^{-1}g),

\displaystyle(\mho^{h}\phi)(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h\phi(\phi^{-1}(h^{-1})g)=\left(\Theta^{\phi^{-1}(h^{-1})^{-1}}\phi\right)(g).

These two actions of

Because of the multiplication rules, the map

5
A subshift of finite type for bounded orbit-equivalences

The goal of this section is to show that there is a subshift of finite type which encodes bounded orbit-change maps. To begin, let
\mathtt{A}=G^{S\cup S^{-1}}
where
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\}
is the generating set. If
x\in\mathtt{A}^{G}
,
g\in G
and
s\in S\cup S^{-1}
then we write
x_{g}(s)=x(g)(s)\in G
to simplify notation.

Define
{\mathcal{E}}:\operatorname{sym}_{e}(G)\to\mathtt{A}^{G}
by

{\mathcal{E}}(\phi)_{h}(s)=\Theta^{h^{-1}}\phi(s)=\phi(h)^{-1}\phi(hs).

This map is an embedding in the sense that it is equivariant, continuous and injective. The equivariance means that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\Theta^{h}\phi)=h{\mathcal{E}}(\phi)

(10)

for
h\in G
,
\phi\in\operatorname{sym}_{e}(G)
. In fact
{\mathcal{E}}
is determined by this equivariance condition and the formula
{\mathcal{E}}(\phi)_{e}(s)=\phi(s)
.

If
x={\mathcal{E}}(\phi)
and
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in S\cup S^{-1}
then

\displaystyle\phi(s_{1}\cdots s_{n})=x_{e}(s_{1})x_{s_{1}}(s_{2})x_{s_{1}s_{2}}(s_{3})\cdots x_{s_{1}\cdots s_{n-1}}(s_{n}).

(11)

This is obtained via induction on
n
. This verifies that
{\mathcal{E}}
is injective. We have the following more general fact:

Lemma 5.1
.

If
\phi\in\operatorname{sym}_{e}(G)
,
x={\mathcal{E}}(\phi)
,
g\in G
and
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in S\cup S^{-1}
then

\displaystyle\phi(gs_{1}\cdots s_{n})=\phi(g)x_{g}(s_{1})x_{gs_{1}}(s_{2})\cdots x_{gs_{1}\cdots s_{n-1}}(s_{n}).

Moreover, for any
t\in G
and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\in S\cup S^{-1}
, the equation

\displaystyle\phi^{-1}(gt)=\phi^{-1}(g)t_{1}\cdots t_{m}

is true if and only if

t=x_{\phi^{-1}(g)}(t_{1})\cdots x_{\phi^{-1}(g)t_{1}\cdots t_{m-1}}(t_{m}).

Moreover, such a sequence is unique if we require
m=|t_{1}\cdots t_{m}|_{G}
.

Proof.

The first statement follows from (
11
) by writing
g\in G
as a word in
S\cup S^{-1}
.

To prove the second statement, suppose
\phi^{-1}(gt)=\phi^{-1}(g)t_{1}\cdots t_{m}
. Apply
\phi
to both sides, then apply the first statement to obtain

\displaystyle gt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(\phi^{-1}(g)t_{1}\cdots t_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle gx_{\phi^{-1}(g)}(t_{1})x_{\phi^{-1}(g)t_{1}}(t_{2})\cdots x_{\phi^{-1}(g)t_{1}\cdots t_{m-1}}(t_{m}).

After cancelling
g
from both sides, we obtain
t=x_{\phi^{-1}(g)}(t_{1})x_{\phi^{-1}(g)t_{1}}(t_{2})\cdots x_{\phi^{-1}(g)t_{1}\cdots t_{m-1}}(t_{m})
as required. The converse is obtained by following the same steps in reverse. Moreover,
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\in S\cup S^{-1}
are uniquely determined by two conditions:
m=|t_{1}\cdots t_{m}|
and

t_{1}\cdots t_{m}=\phi^{-1}(g)^{-1}\phi^{-1}(gt).

∎

For
\rho\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\mathtt{A}_{\rho}=\mathrm{B}(e,\rho)^{S\cup S^{-1}}
which we view as a subset of
\mathtt{A}
. Because
\mathtt{A}_{\rho}
is finite,
\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}
is compact. Also, let
\operatorname{sym}_{\rho}(G)\subset\operatorname{sym}_{e}(G)
be the subset of
\phi\in\operatorname{sym}_{e}(G)
such that

|(\Theta^{g^{-1}}\phi)(s)|_{G}=|\phi(g)^{-1}\phi(gs)|_{G}\leq\rho~\textrm{ and }~|(\Theta^{g^{-1}}\phi^{-1})(s)|_{G}=|\phi^{-1}(g)^{-1}\phi^{-1}(gs)|_{G}\leq\rho

for all
s\in S\cup S^{-1}
and
g\in G
. In other words, if
x={\mathcal{E}}(\phi)
and
y={\mathcal{E}}(\phi^{-1})
then

|x_{g}(s)|_{G}\leq\rho
and
|y_{g}(s)|_{G}\leq\rho
. In particular, if
\phi\in\operatorname{sym}_{\rho}(G)
, then
{\mathcal{E}}(\phi)\in\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}
.

The main theorem of this section is:

Theorem 5.2
.

For
\rho\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
{\mathcal{E}}(\operatorname{sym}_{\rho}(G))\subset\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}
is a subshift of finite type.

Proof.

Let
F=\mathrm{B}(e,\rho^{2}+1)\subset G
be the radius
\rho^{2}+1

{\mathcal{V}}\subset\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{F}
be the set of all maps
z:F\to\mathtt{A}_{\rho}
such that

(Axiom 1)

z_{e}(s)z_{s}(s^{-1})=e
for all
s\in S\cup S^{-1}
.

(Axiom 2)

For every
h\in G
with
|h|_{G}\leq\rho

s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in S\cup S^{-1}
with
|s_{1}\cdots s_{n}|_{G}=n\leq\rho^{2}+1

h=z_{e}(s_{1})z_{s_{1}}(s_{2})z_{s_{1}s_{2}}(s_{3})\cdots z_{s_{1}\cdots s_{n-1}}(s_{n}).

Moreover,
n\leq\rho|h|_{G}
.

Let
{\mathcal{W}}=\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{F}\setminus{\mathcal{V}}
be the complement of
{\mathcal{V}}
. Let
Z
be the subshift of finite type determined by
{\mathcal{W}}
. This means
Z
is the set of all
x\in\mathtt{A}^{G}
such that for every
g\in G
,
gx
restricted to

is in
{\mathcal{V}}
. We will show

Let

. We claim that

\displaystyle x_{g}(s)x_{gs}(s^{-1})=e

(12)

for all

and
g\in G

(g^{-1}x)_{e}(s)(g^{-1}x)_{s}(s^{-1})=e.

Equation (

(g^{-1}x)_{s}(s^{-1})=x_{gs}(s^{-1})

Claim 1
.
Z\supset{\mathcal{E}}(\operatorname{sym}_{\rho}(G))

Proof of Claim 1.

Let

. If

5.1

Define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}(\mho^{h}\phi)=h{\mathcal{F}}(\phi)

(19)

,

to periodic points witnessing the entropy of
(X,\mu,U,G)
.

and

\tau

\displaystyle\phi^{-1}_{w}(t)^{-1}\phi_{v}^{-1}(g)^{-1}=\phi_{v}^{-1}(gt)^{-1}.

(20)

where

\displaystyle t=\phi_{w}(t_{1}\cdots t_{m})=(x^{\sigma}_{w})_{e}(t_{1})(x^{\sigma}_{w})_{t_{1}}(t_{2})\cdots(x^{\sigma}_{w})_{t_{1}\cdots t_{m-1}}(t_{m}).

,

\displaystyle(x^{\sigma}_{w})_{t_{1}\cdots t_{i-1}}(t_{i})

\displaystyle(x^{\sigma}_{v})_{\phi^{-1}_{v}(g)t_{1}\cdots t_{i-1}}(t_{i}).

To prove the last statement, we check

{\mathcal{F}}(\phi_{v})_{g}(s)=g^{-1}\phi_{v}(\phi_{v}^{-1}(g)s)

(x^{\sigma}_{v})_{\phi_{v}^{-1}(g)}(s)=g^{-1}\phi_{v}(\phi_{v}^{-1}(g)s).

So
{\mathcal{F}}(\phi_{v})_{g}(s)=(x^{\sigma}_{v})_{\phi_{v}^{-1}(g)}(s)
. By definition of
x^{\sigma}_{v}
,
\tau
and
x^{\tau}_{v}
, we have

(x^{\sigma}_{v})_{\phi_{v}^{-1}(g)}(s)=x(\sigma(\phi_{v}^{-1}(g)^{-1})v)(s)=x(\tau(g^{-1})v)(s)=(x^{\tau}_{v})_{g}(s).

Since
g
and
s
are arbitrary,
{\mathcal{F}}(\phi_{v})=x^{\tau}_{v}
. Because
\phi_{v}={\mathcal{E}}^{-1}(x^{\sigma}_{v})
, this implies the last claim.

∎

Next we extend
{\mathcal{E}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}
as follows. Define
{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}},{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}:\operatorname{sym}_{e}(G)\times\mathtt{B}^{G}\to\mathtt{A}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G}
by

{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}(\phi,y)=({\mathcal{E}}(\phi),y),\quad{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}(\phi,y)=({\mathcal{F}}(\phi),y\circ\phi^{-1}).

We claim these maps have the following equivariance properties:

\displaystyle{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}\circ{\widetilde{\Theta}}^{h}=h{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}},\quad{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}\circ\tilde{\mho}^{h}=h{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}.

(21)

The first equality above is straightforward and left to the reader (use (
10
), (
9
)). Verifying the second equality above is also straightforward but a little long. We will use the notation
y\circ h
for the function which takes
g\in G
to
y(hg)
. For any
(\phi,y)\in\mathtt{A}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G}
, the definition of
\tilde{\mho}
and
{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}
give

\displaystyle{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}\circ\tilde{\mho}^{h}(\phi,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}(\mho^{h}\phi,\phi^{-1}(h^{-1})^{-1}y)={\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}(\mho^{h}\phi,y\circ\phi^{-1}(h^{-1}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\mathcal{F}}\mho^{h}\phi,y\circ\phi^{-1}(h^{-1})\circ(\mho^{h}\phi)^{-1}).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle h{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}(\phi,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h({\mathcal{F}}\phi,y\circ\phi^{-1})=(h{\mathcal{F}}\phi,y\circ\phi^{-1}\circ h^{-1}).

By (
19
), the first coordinates are equal. So it now suffices to show

y\circ\phi^{-1}(h^{-1})\circ(\mho^{h}\phi)^{-1}=y\circ\phi^{-1}\circ h^{-1}.

Equivalently, for all
g\in G
,

y(\phi^{-1}(h^{-1})(\mho^{h}\phi)^{-1}(g))=y(\phi^{-1}(h^{-1}g)).

Removing the
y
’s, it suffices to show

\phi^{-1}(h^{-1})(\mho^{h}\phi)^{-1}(g)=\phi^{-1}(h^{-1}g).

Multiply both sides by
\phi^{-1}(h^{-1})^{-1}
, to obtain the equivalent

(\mho^{h}\phi)^{-1}(g)=\phi^{-1}(h^{-1})^{-1}\phi^{-1}(h^{-1}g).

Now apply
\mho^{h}\phi
to both sides to obtain

g=(\mho^{h}\phi)(\phi^{-1}(h^{-1})^{-1}\phi^{-1}(h^{-1}g)).

This is a straightforward consequence of the definition of
\mho^{h}\phi
and so finishes our verification of (
21
).

Lemma 5.5
.

Keep notation as in Lemma
5.4
. Also let
y\in\mathtt{B}^{n}
. Then

{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}({\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{-1}(x^{\sigma}_{v},y^{\sigma}_{v}))=(x^{\tau}_{v},y^{\tau}_{v}).

Proof.

Recall the notation
\phi_{v}={\mathcal{E}}^{-1}(x^{\sigma}_{v})\in\operatorname{sym}_{e}(G)
. By definition of
{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}
,

{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{-1}(x^{\sigma}_{v},y^{\sigma}_{v})=(\phi_{v},y^{\sigma}_{v}).

By definition of
{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}
and Lemma
5.4
,

{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}({\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{-1}(x^{\sigma}_{v},y^{\sigma}_{v}))=(x^{\tau}_{v},y^{\sigma}_{v}\circ\phi_{v}^{-1}).

So it suffices to show
y^{\tau}_{v}=y^{\sigma}_{v}\circ\phi_{v}^{-1}
. For any
g\in G
,

y^{\tau}_{v}(g)=y(\tau(g^{-1})v),\quad y^{\sigma}_{v}\circ\phi_{v}^{-1}(g)=y(\sigma(\phi_{v}^{-1}(g)^{-1})v).

The definition of
\tau
gives
\tau(g^{-1})v=\sigma(\phi_{v}^{-1}(g)^{-1})v
. This finishes the proof.
∎

5.2
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Without loss of generality, we assume the standard hypotheses from §
3
. Because the actions
T
and
U
are boundedly orbit-equivalent, there is a
\rho\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
and every
s\in S\cup S^{-1}
,
\alpha(s,x)
and
\beta(s,x)
both have word-length
\leq\rho
.

Let
\nu={\widetilde{\Gamma}}_{*}\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\operatorname{sym}_{e}(G)\times\mathtt{B}^{G})
. Recall from §
4.1
that it suffices to show
f_{\nu}({\widetilde{\Theta}})=f_{\nu}(\tilde{\mho})
. Recall the maps
{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}
,
{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}
from §
5.1
. These maps are injective (mod null sets) because
{\mathcal{E}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}
are injective (mod null sets). They are also equivariant in the sense of (
21
). So it suffices to show
f_{{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}_{*}\nu}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})=f_{{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}_{*}\nu}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})
.

Let
Z={\mathcal{E}}(\operatorname{sym}_{\rho}(G))\subset\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}
and let
{\tilde{Z}}=Z\times\mathtt{B}^{G}
. Recall from §
2
that
\Omega(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})
is the set of all
x\in\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{n}\times\mathtt{B}^{n}
such that
P^{\sigma}_{x}\in{\mathcal{O}}
where
P^{\sigma}_{x}
is the empirical measure of
x
(with respect to
\sigma
). Also
\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})
is the set of
x\in\Omega(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})
such that
P^{\sigma}_{x}({\tilde{Z}})=1
.

Given an open subset
{\mathcal{O}}\subset\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})
, we define the following subsets of
\operatorname{Hom}(G,\operatorname{sym}(n))\times\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{n}\times\mathtt{B}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\Omega(n,{\mathcal{O}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{(\sigma,x,y):~(x,y)\in\Omega(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})\}

\displaystyle\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(n,{\mathcal{O}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{(\sigma,x,y):~(x,y)\in\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})\}

\displaystyle\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(n)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(n,\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})).

By Theorem
5.2

Z
is a subshift of finite type. Therefore,
{\tilde{Z}}
is also a subshift of finite type. By Theorem
2.1
and the formula
n!^{r}=\#\operatorname{Hom}(G,\operatorname{sym}(n))
,

\displaystyle f_{{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}_{*}\nu}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{{\mathcal{O}}\ni{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}_{*}\nu}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\sigma\sim u_{n}}\lvert\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(\sigma,{\mathcal{O}})\rvert

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{{\mathcal{O}}\ni{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}_{*}\nu}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{\#\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(n,{\mathcal{O}})}{n!^{r}}\right).

Define

\Upsilon_{n}:\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(n)\to\operatorname{Hom}(G,\operatorname{sym}(n))\times\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{n}\times\mathtt{B}^{n}

by
\Upsilon_{n}(\sigma,x,y)=(\tau,x,y)
where
\tau
is as defined in Lemma
5.4
. To prove the theorem, we will show that
\Upsilon_{n}
maps periodic points witnessing the
f
-invariant for
{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}_{*}\nu
to periodic points witnessing the
f
-invariant for
{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}_{*}\nu
. The first step is the following continuity property.

Claim 1
. Let
{\mathcal{U}}\subset\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})
be an open neighborhood of
{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}_{*}\nu
. Then there exists an open neighborhood
{\mathcal{O}}
of
{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}_{*}\nu
such that
\Upsilon_{n}(\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(n,{\mathcal{O}}))\subset\Omega(n,{\mathcal{U}}).

Proof of Claim 1.

The map
{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}:Z\to\operatorname{sym}_{e}(G)
is continuous by Lemma
5.1
. The continuity of
{\mathcal{F}},{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}
and
{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{-1}:{\tilde{Z}}\to\operatorname{sym}_{e}(G)\times\mathtt{B}^{G}
follows directly from their definitions. Therefore
{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}\circ{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{-1}:{\tilde{Z}}\to\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G}
is continuous and the induced map

({\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}\circ{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{-1})_{*}:\operatorname{Prob}({\tilde{Z}})\to\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})

is continuous. Since
{\mathcal{U}}\subset\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})
is an open neighborhood of
{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}_{*}\nu
, this implies that its pre-image in
\operatorname{Prob}({\tilde{Z}})
is an open neighborhood of
{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}_{*}\nu
. In particular, there exists an open neighborhood
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}\subset\operatorname{Prob}({\tilde{Z}})
of
{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}_{*}\nu
such that
({\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}\circ{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{-1})_{*}({\mathcal{O}}^{\prime})\subset{\mathcal{U}}
.

Because
{\tilde{Z}}
is closed in
\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G}
, the weak* topology on
\operatorname{Prob}({\tilde{Z}})
is the restriction of the weak* topology on
\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})
. So there is an open neighborhood
{\mathcal{O}}\subset\operatorname{Prob}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})
of
{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}_{*}\nu
such that
{\mathcal{O}}\cap\operatorname{Prob}({\tilde{Z}})\subset{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
.

Let
(\sigma,x,y)\in\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(n,{\mathcal{O}})
. Since
\Upsilon_{n}(\sigma,x,y)=(\tau,x,y)
, it suffices to show
(\tau,x,y)\in\Omega(n,{\mathcal{U}})
. Because
(\sigma,x,y)\in\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(n,{\mathcal{O}})
,
P^{\sigma}_{(x,y)}({\tilde{Z}})=1
and
P^{\sigma}_{(x,y)}\in{\mathcal{O}}
. So
P^{\sigma}_{(x,y)}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
. Thus
({\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}\circ{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{-1})_{*}P^{\sigma}_{(x,y)}\in{\mathcal{U}}
. However,

\displaystyle({\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}\circ{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{-1})_{*}P^{\sigma}_{(x,y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in[n]}({\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}\circ{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{-1})_{*}\delta_{(x^{\sigma}_{v},y^{\sigma}_{v})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in[n]}\delta_{(x^{\tau}_{v},y^{\tau}_{v})}=P^{\tau}_{(x,y)}.

Above we have used Lemma
5.5
to conclude
({\mathcal{F}}\circ{\mathcal{E}}^{-1})(x^{\sigma}_{v},y^{\sigma}_{v})=(x^{\tau}_{v},y^{\tau}_{v})
. So
P^{\tau}_{(x,y)}\in{\mathcal{U}}
which implies
(\tau,x,y)\in\Omega(n,{\mathcal{U}})
.

∎

Claim 2
.
\Upsilon_{n}
is injective.

Proof of Claim 2.

Suppose
\Upsilon_{n}(\sigma,x,y)=(\tau,x,y)
. Recall that
\phi_{v}={\mathcal{E}}^{-1}(x^{\sigma}_{v})\in\operatorname{sym}_{e}(G)
. By Lemma
5.4
,

\tau(g)v=\sigma(\phi^{-1}_{v}(g^{-1})^{-1})v.

Set
h=\phi^{-1}_{v}(g^{-1})^{-1}
. Then
g=\phi_{v}(h^{-1})^{-1}
. Thus

\sigma(h)v=\tau(\phi_{v}(h^{-1})^{-1})v.

Now suppose that
h\in S\cup S^{-1}
. By Lemma
5.1
with
g=e
,

\phi_{v}(h^{-1})=(x^{\sigma}_{v})_{e}(h^{-1})=x(v)(h^{-1}).

Thus

\sigma(h)v=\tau(x(v)(h^{-1})^{-1})v

for all
h\in S\cup S^{-1}
and
v\in[n]
. This shows that
\sigma
is determined by
\tau
and
x
. Thus
\Upsilon_{n}
is injective.
∎

If
{\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{U}}
are as in Claim 1, then Claim 2 implies

\#\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(n,{\mathcal{O}})=\#\Upsilon_{n}(\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(n,{\mathcal{O}}))\leq\#\Omega(n,{\mathcal{U}}).

So (
22
) implies

\displaystyle f_{{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}_{*}\nu}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{{\mathcal{O}}\ni{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}_{*}\nu}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{\#\Omega_{\tilde{Z}}(n,{\mathcal{O}})}{n!^{r}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{{\mathcal{U}}\ni{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}_{*}\nu}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{\#\Omega(n,{\mathcal{U}})}{n!^{r}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}_{*}\nu}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G}).

As mentioned above, since
{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}
and
{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}
are embeddings, this shows

f_{\nu}({\widetilde{\Theta}})=f_{{\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}}_{*}\nu}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})\leq f_{{\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}_{*}\nu}(\mathtt{A}_{\rho}^{G}\times\mathtt{B}^{G})=f_{\nu}(\tilde{\mho}).

By symmetry, the same argument with
T
and
U
switched shows the opposite inequality. This proves the theorem.

∎
