Quantum state testing beyond the polarizing regime and quantum triangular discrimination
Yupan Liu

Abstract
The complexity class Quantum Statistical Zero-Knowledge ( QSZK ) captures computational difficulties of the time-bounded quantum state testing problem with respect to the trace distance, known as the Quantum State Distinguishability Problem ( QSDP ) introduced by Watrous (FOCS 2002) . However, QSDP is in QSZK merely within the constant polarizing regime, similar to its classical counterpart shown by Sahai and Vadhan (JACM 2003) due to the polarization lemma (error reduction for SDP ). Recently, Berman, Degwekar, Rothblum, and Vasudevan (TCC 2019) extended the SZK containment for SDP beyond the polarizing regime via the time-bounded distribution testing problems with respect to the triangular discrimination and the Jensen-Shannon divergence. Our work introduces proper quantum analogs for these problems by defining quantum counterparts for triangular discrimination. We investigate whether the quantum analogs behave similarly to their classical counterparts and examine the limitations of existing approaches to polarization regarding quantum distances. These new QSZK -complete problems improve QSZK containments for QSDP beyond the polarizing regime and establish a simple QSZK -hardness for the quantum entropy difference problem ( QEDP ) defined by Ben-Aroya, Schwartz, and Ta-Shma (ToC 2010) . Furthermore, we prove that QSDP with some exponentially small errors is in PP , while the same problem without error is in NQP .

Introduction

The quantum state testing problem is generally about telling whether one quantum (mixed) state is close to the other with respect to a chosen distance-like measure, which is an instance of the emerging field of quantum property testing
[
MdW16
]
. This problem generalizes the classical question of testing whether two probability distributions are close, known as distribution testing
[
Can20
]
.
This problem typically focuses on the number of samples (sample complexity) needed to distinguish two states of interest. However, in this paper, we instead concentrate on understanding the computational complexity of the
time-bounded quantum state testing
problem. In particular, we can prepare two states of interest by performing polynomial-size quantum circuits and subsequently tracing out non-output qubits.

The
Quantum State Distinguishability Problem
(
QSDP
), defined by Watrous
[
Wat02
]
, is a time-bounded state testing problem with respect to the trace distance, serving as the quantum analog of the
Statistical Difference Problem
introduced by Sahai and Vadhan
[
SV03
]
.
This promise problem is essential in quantum complexity theory and quantum cryptography, closely linked to quantum statistical zero-knowledge (
QSZK
).
The input to this problem consists of the description of two polynomial-size quantum circuits
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
that prepare (the purification of) corresponding quantum (mixed) states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
, respectively.
In
QSDP
,
yes
instances are those in which the trace distance between these states is at least
\alpha
, while
no
instances are those in which the distance is at most
\beta
, where
0\leq\beta<\alpha\leq 1
.
Any input circuits that do not fit into either of these categories are considered outside the promise.
In this paper, we generalize the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
from constant to efficiently computable functions, denoting this version as
\textnormal{{QSDP}}[\alpha,\beta]
.

Error reduction for
SDP
, known as the
polarization lemma

[
SV03
]
, polarizes the statistical distance between two probability distributions. Put it differently, for any constants
\alpha
and
\beta
such that
\alpha^{2}>\beta
, the lemma constructs new distributions such that either very far apart (approaching
1
) for
yes
instances or very close (approaching
0
) for
no
instances, thereby reducing errors on both sides.
By employing the polarization lemma, the
SZK
containment for
\textnormal{{SDP}}[\alpha,\beta]
when
\alpha^{2}>\beta
, denoted as
constant polarizing regime
, is established in
[
SV03
]
. Furthermore, an analog of the direct product lemma for the Hellinger affinity leads to error reduction for
StoqMA
when the error for
yes
instances is negligible
[
Liu21
]
.

Sahai and Vadhan left an open problem concerning reducing error parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
beyond the constant polarizing regime, specifically considering the
non-polarizing regime
where
\alpha>\beta>\alpha^{2}
.
This challenge also extends to the quantum counterpart
QSDP
.
Recently, Berman, Degwekar, Rothblum, and Vasudevan
[
BDRV19
]
made significant progress in addressing this problem by examining the limitations of existing polarization approaches. As a result, they extended the

Why do parameter regimes matter?

Beyond our particular motivation to understand the polarization lemma for quantum distances, we would like to emphasize the general importance of the parameter regime. In computational complexity theory, we typically use
worst-case hardness
, where
a few

C
-hard instances are sufficient to identity that a computational problem
Prob
is hard for the class
C
. However, to demonstrating a
C
containment for
Prob
, we need to show this containment holds for
all
instances of
Prob
with completeness
c
and soundness
s
(the acceptance probability for
yes
instances and
no
instances, respectively), such that
c-s
is at least the designated (usually polynomial) precision.

Otherwise, we may risk having
a somewhat “fake” complete problem
. For instance, if a promise problem
Prob
is proven to be
QSZK
-hard and contained in
QSZK
for certain parameter regime, then we cannot rule out the possibility that those parameter regimes not yet known to be in
QSZK
may be inherently
QIP(2)
-hard.
This possibility suggests that
Prob
is not
QSZK
-complete unless
\textnormal{{QSZK}}{}=\textnormal{{QIP(2)}}{}
. Unfortunately, such parameter regime issues have appeared in many previous works, such as
[
Wat02
,
GHMW15
]
. To the best of our knowledge, the only
QSZK
-complete with a natural parameter regime of the containment is the
Quantum Entropy Difference Problem
introduced in
[
BASTS10
]
.

Resolving such parameter regime issues is often
technically challenging
. A specific example is the low-rank variant of
QSDP
, where the rank of states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
is at most polynomial in
n
. By leveraging rank-dependent inequalities between the trace distance and the Hilbert-Schmidt distance, such as
[
AS17
, Equation (1.31)]
or
[
CCC19
, Equation 6]
, we can show that this low-rank variant of
QSDP
is in
BQP
for certain parameter regime (with polynomial precision). This is achieved through a clever use of the SWAP test
[
BCWdW01
]
(similar to
Section

5.2
). However, a
BQP
containment for this problem under the natural regime, as established in
[
WZ24
]
, requires more sophisticated techniques.

for any states
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}
, and
\rho_{2}
.
This indicates that

is a

, with the same property also holding for triangular discrimination

]

2.1
Distances and divergences for classical probability distributions

In this subsection, we will review several commonly used classical distances and divergences.
We first begin by defining the statistical distance and the triangular discrimination.

{definition}

[Statistical distance]

The statistical distance between two distributions
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
is

\mathrm{SD}(p_{0},p_{1})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\|p_{0}-p_{1}\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|p_{0}(x)-p_{1}(x)|
where
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\mathrm{supp}\left(p_{0}\right)\cup\mathrm{supp}\left(p_{1}\right)
.

{definition}

[Triangular discrimination]

The triangular discrimination (a.k.a., Le Cam divergence) between two distributions
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
is
\mathrm{TD}(p_{0},p_{1})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{(p_{0}(x)-p_{1}(x))^{2}}{p_{0}(x)+p_{1}(x)}
.

It is noteworthy that
\mathrm{TD}
is a symmetrized version of the
\chi^{2}
divergence, namely
\mathrm{TD}(p_{0},p_{1})=\chi^{2}\!\left(p_{z}\big\|\frac{p_{0}+p_{1}}{2}\right)
for
z\in\{0,1\}
. We also know that
\mathrm{SD}^{2}(p_{0},p_{1})\leq\mathrm{TD}(p_{0},p_{1})\leq\mathrm{SD}(p_{0},p_{1})
, as presented in
[
Top00
]
.
Next, we proceed by defining the Jensen-Shannon divergence (
\mathrm{JS}
).

{definition}

[Jensen-Shannon divergence]

The Jensen-Shannon divergence between two distributions
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
is

\mathrm{JS}_{2}(p_{0},p_{1})\coloneqq\mathrm{H}\!\left(\frac{p_{0}+p_{1}}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{H}(p_{0})+\mathrm{H}(p_{1})\right]
,
where the Shannon entropy
\mathrm{H}(p)\coloneqq-\sum_{x}p(x)\log_{2}p(x)
.

The Jensen-Shannon divergence is a symmetrized version of the Kullback–Leibler divergence (also known as the relative entropy), namely
\mathrm{JS}_{2}(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm KL}\left(p\big\|\frac{p+q}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}{\rm KL}\left(q\big\|\frac{p+q}{2}\right)
by a simple calculation.

{proposition}

[Adapted from
[
FvdG99
,
Top00
]
]

For any distributions
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
,

\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{SD}(p_{0},p_{1})^{2v}}{\ln{2}\cdot 2v(2v-1)}=1-\mathrm{H_{2}}\left(\frac{1-\mathrm{SD}(p_{0},p_{1})}{2}\right)\leq\mathrm{JS}_{2}(p_{0},p_{1})\leq\mathrm{SD}(p_{0},p_{1}),

where the binary entropy function
\mathrm{H_{2}}(p)\coloneqq-p\log_{2}p-(1-p)\log_{2}(1-p)
.

Finally, we define the Hellinger distance and the inner product
\langle P|Q\rangle
between normalized non-negative vectors, also known as
Hellinger affinity
(or
Bhattacharyya coefficient
).

{definition}
[Squared Hellinger distance]

The squared Hellinger distance between two distributions
p
and
q
on
\mathcal{X}
is

\mathrm{H}^{2}(p,q)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}(\sqrt{p(x)}-\sqrt{q(x)})^{2}=1-\left\langle P|Q\right\rangle
, where
|{P}\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{x}\sqrt{p(x)}|{x}\rangle
and
|{Q}\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{x}\sqrt{p(x)}|{x}\rangle
.

Additionally, a simple observation
[
LC86
, Page 48]
indicates the squared Hellinger distance is very close to the triangular discrimination, namely
\mathrm{H}^{2}(p,q)\leq\mathrm{TD}(p,q)\leq 2\mathrm{H}^{2}(p,q)
.

2.2
Distances and divergences for quantum states

Now we will review relevant quantum distances and divergences.
Classical distances and divergences often have corresponding quantum versions, and sometimes even
multiple options
.
These distances usually reduce to the classical counterpart when quantum states
\rho_{0}=\mathrm{diag}(p_{0})
and
\rho_{1}=\mathrm{diag}(p_{1})
are diagonal. We recommend Section 3.1 in
[
BOW19
]
for a nice survey.

For any classical
f
-divergence, we can define a quantum analog through either converting arithmetic operations in the classical divergence to their matrix-theoretic counterparts, or considering the probability distributions induced by the same POVM, referred to as the
measured quantum
f
-divergence
. For a comprehensive overview of other quantum analogs of
f
-divergence and their relationships, we refer to
[
Hia21
]
. Consider a classical
f
-divergence
\mathrm{d}_{f}(\cdot,\cdot)
, the measured quantum
f
-divergence
\mathrm{D}_{f}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined as follows:

\mathrm{D}_{f}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=\sup_{\mathrm{POVM}\penalty\ \mathcal{E}}\left\{\mathrm{d}_{f}\left(p_{0}^{(\mathcal{E})},p_{1}^{(\mathcal{E})}\right)\right\}\text{ where }p_{z}^{(\mathcal{E})}\coloneqq\left(\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{z}E_{1}),\cdots,\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{z}E_{N})\right).

(2.1)

Put it differently, the quantum divergence is the maximum classical divergence achievable when the same POVM is applied to both states.

Quantum analogs of statistical distance and Hellinger distance.

We start with the trace distance, which is a metric. This distance has a maximum value of
1
occurring when
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
have orthogonal supports. Moreover, the trace distance is a measured version of the statistical distance in terms of
Equation

2.1

[
NC10
, Theorem 9.1]
.

{definition}
[Trace distance]
The trace distance between two quantum states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
is

\mathrm{td}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}|\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}|=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(((\rho_{0}-\rho_{1})^{\dagger}(\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}))^{1/2}).

Although the squared Hellinger distance is closely related to the inner product, there are several quantum analogs because of the non-commuting nature of matrices.
Based on the (Uhlmann) fidelity, we proceed with the
squared Bures distance
, which is the first quantum analog of
Section

2.1
since it is precisely the
measured squared Hellinger distance

[
FC94
]
.

{definition}
[Squared Bures distance]

The squared Bures distance between two states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
is
\mathrm{B}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\coloneqq 2(1-\mathrm{F}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}))
, where the fidelity
\mathrm{F}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\coloneqq\mathrm{Tr}|\sqrt{\rho_{0}}\sqrt{\rho_{1}}|
.

We also provide inequalities between the trace distance and the Bures distance.

{proposition}
[Adapted from
[
FvdG99
]
]

For any quantum states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
,

\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{B}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq\mathrm{td}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq\mathrm{B}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}).

Notice that matrices
(ABA)^{1/2}
and
A^{1/2}B^{1/2}A^{1/2}
are not generally equal. This fact suggests that the Uhlmann fidelity differs from the quantum Hellinger affinity
\mathrm{Q}_{1/2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\coloneqq\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{\rho_{0}}\sqrt{\rho_{1}})
. The latter infers the second quantum analog of
Section

2.1
:

{definition}

[Quantum squared Hellinger distance]
For any states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
, quantum squared Hellinger distance is
\mathrm{QH^{2}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{\rho_{0}}-\sqrt{\rho_{1}})^{2}=1-\mathrm{Q}_{1/2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}).

Additionally, it is noteworthy that
\mathrm{F}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\geq\mathrm{Q}_{1/2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
. We further recommend two comprehensive reviews
[
CS20
,
BGJ19
]
for summarizing different variants of the fidelity.

Quantum analogs of Jensen-Shannon Divergence.

We will encounter different flavors of quantum analogs of Jensen-Shannon divergence.
The study on quantum analogs of Jensen-Shannon Divergence (a.k.a., Shannon Distinguishability) could date back to the Holevo bound
[
Hol73
]
. We begin with a definition in
[
MLP05
]
and notice that
\textnormal{{QJS}}_{2}
is at most
1
.
8
8

8

Whereas quantum relative entropy is unbounded, by properties of von Neumann entropy (e.g., Theorem 11.8 in
[
NC10
]
), we know that
\textnormal{{QJS}}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq\mathrm{H_{2}}(1/2)=1
. Then the equality holds if and only if
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
have support on orthogonal subspaces.
It is noteworthy that
\sqrt{\textnormal{{QJS}}}
is a metric and, therefore satisfies the triangle inequality
[
Vir21
,
Sra21

{definition}

[Quantum Jensen-Shannon Divergence,
[
MLP05
]
]

The quantum Jensen-Shannon divergence between two quantum states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
is
\textnormal{{QJS}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\coloneqq S\left(\frac{\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}}{2}\right)-\frac{S(\rho_{0})+S(\rho_{1})}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{D}\left(\rho_{0}\big\|\frac{\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}}{2}\right)+\mathrm{D}\left(\rho_{1}\big\|\frac{\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}}{2}\right)\right],

where
S(\rho)\coloneqq-{\rm Tr}(\rho\ln\rho)
is the von Neumann entropy and
\mathrm{D}(\cdot\|\cdot)
is the quantum relative entropy.

The work of
[
FvdG99
]
studied a measured variant of the Jensen-Shannon divergence, also known as
quantum Shannon distinguishability
. Notably, there is no explicit formula for
\mathrm{QJS}^{\rm meas}
since it is a solution of some transcendental equation
[
FC94
]
. We can define the
Measured Quantum Jensen-Shannon Divergence

\mathrm{QJS}^{\rm meas}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
similarly in terms of
Equation

2.1
.
For convenience, we introduce
\textnormal{{QJS}}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\coloneqq\textnormal{{QJS}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})/\ln 2
and
\mathrm{QJS}^{\rm meas}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\coloneqq\mathrm{QJS}^{\rm meas}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})/\ln 2
with respect to the base-
2
(matrix) logarithmic function.

Interestingly, the quantum Jensen-Shannon divergence (
QJS
) is a special case of the Holevo
\chi
quantity (the right-hand side of the Holevo bound
[
Hol73
]
).
9
9

9

Following the notation in
[
NC10
, Theorem 12.1]
,
QJS
coincides with the Holevo
\chi
quantity for size-
2
ensembles with a uniform distribution.

Moreover,
QJS
is upper-bounded by the trace distance:

{lemma}

[Adapted from
[
BH09
]
]

For any states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
,
\textnormal{{QJS}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq\ln{2}\cdot\mathrm{td}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}).

It is noteworthy that the proof of
Section

2.2
is essentially adapting the demonstration of a corresponding result for classical distances (e.g.,
[
Vad99
, Claim 4.4.2]
).

Proof of
Section

2.2
.

We begin with the construction in
[
BH09
, Theorem 14]
. Consider a single qutrit register
B
with basis vectors
|{0}\rangle,|{1}\rangle,|{2}\rangle
.
Define
\tilde{\rho}_{0}
and
\tilde{\rho}_{1}
on
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{B}
as below, where
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{C}^{3}
is the Hilbert space corresponding to the register
B
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{0}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\tfrac{\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}-|\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}|}{2}\otimes\left|2\right\rangle\left\langle 2\right|+\tfrac{\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}+|\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}|}{2}\otimes|{0}\rangle\langle{0}|\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}\otimes\left|2\right\rangle\left\langle 2\right|+\sigma_{0}\otimes|{0}\rangle\langle{0}|,

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{1}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\tfrac{\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}-|\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}|}{2}\otimes\left|2\right\rangle\left\langle 2\right|+\tfrac{\rho_{1}-\rho_{0}+|\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}|}{2}\otimes|{1}\rangle\langle{1}|\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}\otimes\left|2\right\rangle\left\langle 2\right|+\sigma_{1}\otimes|{1}\rangle\langle{1}|.

Here,
\sigma_{0}
corresponds to the regime that
\rho_{0}
is “larger than”
\rho_{1}
(where
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
are “distinguishable”) and so does
\sigma_{1}
, whereas
\sigma_{2}
corresponds to the regime that
\rho_{0}
is “indistinguishable” from
\rho_{1}
.
One can see this construction generalizes the proof of the classical counterparts (i.e.,
[
Vad99
, Claim 4.4.2]
) to quantum distances.

Then it is left to show
\textnormal{{QJS}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq\textnormal{{QJS}}(\tilde{\rho}_{0},\tilde{\rho}_{1})=\mathrm{td}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
.
Using the data-processing inequality of the quantum relative entropy (e.g.,
[
Pet07
, Theorem 3.9]
), we obtain

\displaystyle\textnormal{{QJS}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})

\displaystyle=\textnormal{{QJS}}(\mathrm{Tr}_{B}(\tilde{\rho_{0}}),\mathrm{Tr}_{B}(\tilde{\rho_{1}}))

(2.2)

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{{QJS}}(\tilde{\rho}_{0},\tilde{\rho}_{1})

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{Tr}\left(\tfrac{\tilde{\rho}_{0}+\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\ln\tfrac{\tilde{\rho}_{0}+\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left(\tilde{\rho}_{0}\ln\tilde{\rho}_{0}\right)+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\tilde{\rho}_{1}\ln\tilde{\rho}_{1}\right)\right).

Here, the first line is because of
\mathrm{Tr}_{B}(\tilde{\rho}_{k})=\rho_{k}
for
k\in\{0,1\}
, and the third line owes to
\textnormal{{QJS}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=S(\frac{\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}}{2})-\frac{1}{2}(S(\rho_{0})+S(\rho_{1}))
for any states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
.
Notice that
\sigma_{0}\otimes|{0}\rangle\langle{0}|
,
\sigma_{1}\otimes|{1}\rangle\langle{1}|
, and
\sigma_{2}\otimes|{2}\rangle\langle{2}|
are orthogonal to each other, and
\ln(A+B)=\ln(A)+\ln(B)
when
A
and
B
are orthogonal (i.e.,
AB=BA=0
), we have derived that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\tfrac{\tilde{\rho}_{0}+\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\ln\tfrac{\tilde{\rho}_{0}+\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\right)=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma_{2}\ln\sigma_{2})+\sum_{k\in\{0,1\}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\tfrac{\sigma_{0}}{2}\ln\tfrac{\sigma_{0}}{2}\right),

(2.3)

\displaystyle\forall k\in\{0,1\},\penalty\ \mathrm{Tr}\left(\tilde{\rho}_{k}\ln\tilde{\rho}_{k}\right)=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma_{2}\ln\sigma_{2})+\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma_{k}\ln\sigma_{k}).

Plugging
Equation

2.3
into
Equation

2.2
, we finish the proof:

\textnormal{{QJS}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq\mathrm{Tr}\left[\tfrac{\sigma_{0}}{2}\left(\ln\sigma_{0}\!-\!\ln\tfrac{\sigma_{0}}{2}\right)\right]+\mathrm{Tr}\left[\tfrac{\sigma_{1}}{2}\left(\ln\sigma_{1}\!-\!\ln\tfrac{\sigma_{1}}{2}\right)\right]=\tfrac{\ln{2}}{2}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma_{0}+\sigma_{1})=\ln{2}\cdot\mathrm{td}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}),

where the third equality is due to
\sigma_{0}+\sigma_{1}=|\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}|
.
∎

Owing to the Holevo bound, we know that the quantum Jensen-Shannon divergence is at least its measured variant (
\mathrm{QJS}^{\rm meas}
):

{proposition}

[Quantum Jensen-Shannon divergence is at least its measured variant]

For any states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
,
\mathrm{QJS}^{\rm meas}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq\textnormal{{QJS}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
.

Proof.

We begin by stating an equivalent characterization of the classical Jensen-Shannon divergence (
\mathrm{JS}
), building upon its fundamental property (e.g.,
[
BDRV19
, Proposition 4.1]
):

{proposition}
[Mutual information characterization of Jensen-Shannon divergence]

For any distributions
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
, let
T
be a binary indicator variable that chooses the value of
x
according to
p_{0}
if
T=i
where
i\in\{0,1\}
, as well as let
X
be a random variable associated with a uniform mixture distribution between
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
. Then we obtain

\mathrm{JS}(p_{0},p_{1})=I(T;X)=\mathrm{H}(T)-\mathrm{H}(T|X)=1-\mathrm{H}(T|X)
.

We then observe that
\textnormal{{QJS}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
corresponds exactly to the Holevo
\chi
quantity
[
NC10
, Section 12.1.1]
for the ensemble
\{1/2,\rho_{0};1/2,\rho_{1}\}
, while
\mathrm{QJS}^{\rm meas}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
is equivalent to the accessible information of the same ensemble. This equivalence allows the proof to follow directly from the Holevo bound.
∎

Utilizing
Section

2.2
, we obtain a lower bound of
QJS
in terms of the trace distance:

{lemma}

[Adapted from
[
Hol73
,
FvdG99
]
]

For any states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
,

\textnormal{{QJS}}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\geq\mathrm{QJS}^{\rm meas}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\geq 1-\mathrm{H_{2}}\left(\tfrac{1-\mathrm{td}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}{2}\right)=\sum\nolimits_{v=1}^{\infty}\tfrac{\mathrm{td}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2v}}{\ln{2}\cdot 2v(2v-1)},

where the binary entropy
\mathrm{H_{2}}(p)\coloneqq-p\log_{2}(p)-(1-p)\log_{2}(1-p)
.

Proof.

We first fix some POVM measurement

\mathcal{E}=\{E_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{U}}
where
\mathcal{U}=\mathrm{supp}\left(\rho_{0}\right)\cup\mathrm{supp}\left(\rho_{1}\right)
.
And let
p_{z}^{(\mathcal{E})}
be the induced distribution with respect to the POVM
\mathcal{E}
of
\rho_{z}
for
z\in\{0,1\}
.
By utilizing the left-hand side inequality in
Section

2.1
, we have

\mathrm{QJS}^{\rm meas}_{\mathcal{E}^{*}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\geq\mathrm{QJS}^{\rm meas}_{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=\mathrm{JS}(p_{0}^{(\mathcal{E})},p_{1}^{(\mathcal{E})})\geq\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}\tfrac{\mathrm{SD}\big(p_{0}^{(\mathcal{E})},p_{1}^{(\mathcal{E})}\big)^{2v}}{2v(2v-1)},

(2.4)

where
\mathcal{E}^{*}
is an optimal measurement of
\mathrm{QJS}^{\rm meas}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
.
Let
g(x)\coloneqq\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2v}}{2v(2v-1)}
, then
g(x)
is monotonically increasing on
0\leq x\leq 1
.
Since
Equation

2.4
holds for arbitrary POVM
\mathcal{E}
, as well as the trace distance is the measured version of the statistical distance, we complete the proof by choosing the one that maximizes
\mathrm{td}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
.
∎

2.3

In this subsection, we will provide definitions of the (time-bounded) quantum state testing problem with respect to different distance-like measures, along with several useful results concerning these computational problems.
We start with a formal definition of the

.

[

acting on

{lemma}

\rho_{0}

Plugging

Next, we present the upper bound of QJS in terms of QTD, as detailed in

{lemma}

For any states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}

Proof.

We begin by noting that an upper bound for the quantum relative entropy in

\mathrm{D}(\rho_{0}\|\rho_{1})\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho_{0}^{1+\gamma}\rho_{1}^{-\gamma}-\rho_{0}\right)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho_{0}^{1+\gamma}\rho_{1}^{-\gamma}\right)-1\right]\text{ for }0<\gamma\leq 1.

(3.7)

Since the quantum Jensen-Shannon divergence is a symmetrized version of the quantum relative entropy, we deduce the following by setting
\gamma=1/2

\displaystyle\textnormal{{QJS}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}}\mathrm{D}\left(\rho_{z}\big\|\tfrac{\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho_{z}^{3/2}\left(\tfrac{\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}\right)-1\right]

•

If

and

3

Here, the last inequality holds for any

Therefore, by employing the same quantum circuits

Unitary construction using the SWAP test.

We utilize the SWAP test

and
\rho_{1}

(5.1)

, initialized to zero, leading to:

(5.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\coloneqq

(1)

Proof of
Theorem

5.1
(1)
.

Consider two
n
-qubit quantum states, denoted as
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
, defined in a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
according to
Section

2.3
.
We begin with the inequalities between the trace distance and the Hilbert-Schmidt distance
[
CCC19
, Equation 6]
):

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{HS}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq\mathrm{td}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{\mathrm{rank}(\rho_{0})\mathrm{rank}(\rho_{1})}{\mathrm{rank}(\rho_{0})+\mathrm{rank}(\rho_{1})}}\mathrm{HS}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq\tfrac{\sqrt{\dim\mathcal{H}}}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{HS}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}).

(5.4)

We present a hybrid classical-quantum algorithm
\mathcal{A}
as follows. First, we toss two random coins that the outcomes denoted as
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
. Subsequently, we apply the SWAP test on the corresponding states in the following manner:

•

If the first coin lands on heads (
r_{1}=1
), we perform the SWAP test on
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
. We accept if the final measurement outcome is
0
.

•

If the first coin lands on tails (
r_{1}=0
), we perform the SWAP test on two copies of
\rho_{r_{2}}
. We accept if the final measurement outcome is
1
.

Let
p^{(o)}_{\mathrm{SWAP}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
be the probability of the SWAP test on
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
where the final measurement outcome
o
.
We then obtain the acceptance probability of our algorithm
\mathcal{A}
:

\frac{1}{2}p^{(0)}_{\mathrm{SWAP}}(\rho_{0},\!\rho_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\!\!\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}}\!\!\frac{p^{(1)}_{\mathrm{SWAP}}(\rho_{i},\!\rho_{i})}{2}=\frac{1\!+\!\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{0}\rho_{1})}{4}+\!\!\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}}\!\frac{1\!-\!\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{2}_{i})}{8}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\mathrm{HS}^{2}(\rho_{0},\!\rho_{1})}{8}.

(5.5)

It suffices to show that algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is indeed a
PP
containment distinguishing
yes
instances from
no
instances within an inverse-exponential gap.
Combining
Equation

5.4
and
Equation

5.5
, we then analyze the acceptance probability:
For
yes

p^{\sf(Y)}_{\mathcal{A}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{HS}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\right)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{td}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\geq\frac{1}{2}-2^{-n-4}
because of
\mathrm{td}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\leq 2^{-n/2-1}
.
For
no
instances, notice that
\mathrm{td}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\geq 1-2^{-n/2-1}
, we have derived that

p^{\sf(N)}_{\mathcal{A}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{HS}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{\dim\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{td}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}-2^{-n-2}\cdot\big(1-2^{-\frac{n}{2}-1}\big)^{2}
.

Since
\textnormal{{PreciseBQP}}\subseteq\textnormal{{PP}}
(e.g., Lemma 3.3 in
[
GSS+22
]
), we then complete the proof by showing that the gap

p^{\sf(Y)}_{\mathcal{A}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})-p^{\sf(N)}_{\mathcal{A}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=2^{-2n-4}+2^{-n-2}\cdot(\tfrac{3}{4}-2^{-n/2})\geq 2^{-2n-4},

where the last inequality holds for

.
