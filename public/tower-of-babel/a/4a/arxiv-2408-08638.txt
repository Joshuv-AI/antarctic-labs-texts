Sampling effects on Lasso estimation of drift functions in high-dimensional diffusion processes
Chiara Amorino †, Francisco Pina, Mark Podolskij *

Abstract
In this paper, we address high-dimensional parametric estimation of the drift function in diffusion models, specifically focusing on a d d -dimensional ergodic diffusion process observed at discrete time points. We consider both a general linear form for the drift function and the particular case of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process. Assuming sparsity of the parameter vector, we examine the statistical behavior of the Lasso estimator for the unknown parameter. Our primary contribution is the proof of an oracle inequality for the Lasso estimator, which holds on the intersection of three specific sets defined for our analysis. We carefully control the probability of these sets, tackling the central challenge of our study. This approach allows us to derive error bounds for the l 1 l_{1} and l 2 l_{2} norms, assessing the performance of the proposed Lasso estimator. Our results demonstrate that, under certain conditions, the discretization error becomes negligible, enabling us to achieve the same optimal rate of convergence as if the continuous trajectory of the process were observed. We validate our theoretical findings through numerical experiments, which show that the Lasso estimator significantly outperforms the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) in terms of support recovery. Keywords: High dimensional statistics, drift parameter estimation, Lasso estimator, high frequency, concentration inequality. AMS 2010 subject classifications: Primary 62M05, 60G15; secondary: 62H12.

Introduction

Over the last few decades, there has been a growing interest in statistical inference for stochastic processes, driven by the extensive range of applications of these models across various fields. For instance, stochastic processes are employed in biology

Scholars have investigated both parametric and non-parametric inference methods, utilizing either continuous or discrete observations, and within various asymptotic frameworks.

In recent years, the rise in computational power has spurred significant interest in high-dimensional statistical models. Researchers have focused on understanding statistical challenges in contexts where the number of model parameters far exceeds the number of observations. For classical models such as linear regression, issues like constructing procedures that are both computationally efficient and exhibit optimal statistical performance (quantified by convergence rates) are now well understood. However, deep statistical results for high-dimensional modeling of continuous-time processes are limited, despite their significant application potential.

In the realm of high-dimensional statistics, several methods have been developed to obtain estimation results. One prevalent approach for conducting parametric inference within the context of large-scale systems is by assuming sparsity in the parameter. In our presented work, we will assume that the true parameter of

In the literature, a diverse array of methodologies aims to leverage the sparsity constraint of the parameter to significantly improve estimator performance. Specifically, we aim to investigate the statistical behavior of the Lasso (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) estimator for the unknown parameter
\theta_{0}

[
53

and has been widely used since.

The Lasso approach is indeed a valuable and well-studied method for model selection, with the significant advantage of simultaneously performing parameter estimation and variable selection (see
[
24
,
28
,
53
]
). It allows the dimensionality of the parameter space to change with the sample size, offering an advantage over classical information criteria such as AIC, BIC. Typically, the Lasso method involves minimizing an
l_{2}
norm subject to
l_{1}
norm constraints on the parameters, implying a least squares or maximum likelihood approach with additional constraints. A crucial property of the Lasso approach is the oracle property (see
[
25
]
), which ensures that the correct parameters are set to zero under the true data-generating model. Originally, the Lasso procedure was introduced for linear regression problems. However, in recent years, this approach has been applied to various fields of stochastic processes. For example, in
[
56
]
, the problem of shrinkage estimation of regressive and autoregressive coefficients is addressed, while
[
40
]
studies penalized order selection for
AR(\rho)
models. Furthermore,
[
8
]
demonstrates that the Bridge estimator, that is a generalization of the Lasso approach by using
l_{q}

[
14
]
.

Recently, regularized estimators have been applied to multidimensional diffusion processes and point processes, representing a new research topic in the field of statistics for stochastic processes. In the high-frequency framework, for instance, penalized selection procedures are used to uncover the underlying true model, as discussed in
[
18
,
20
,
38
,
51

. Moreover, in
[
19

In the setting of continuous observations of an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process and under sparsity constraints in the interaction matrix, high dimensional estimation of the drift parameter is investigated in
[

,
29
]

[

Our paper is closely related to the work
[
17
]
, which investigates high-dimensional drift estimation in the model (
1
) under continuous time observations. This naturally raises the question of the impact of discretization on the estimation procedure and whether it is possible to achieve similar results as in the continuous case, given certain conditions on the rate at which the discretization step approaches zero.

To address this, we concentrate our analysis on two primary settings. First, we investigate general linear models. Under appropriate assumptions on the drift function, this framework enables a clear identification of discretization effects and provides a deeper understanding of the role played by the various model parameters. In this context, the drift function
b_{\theta}
is expressed as

b_{\theta}=\phi_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\theta_{j}\phi_{j},

(3)

where
\phi_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
j=0,\dots,p
, and
\theta_{j}>0
for
j=1,\dots,p.

Second, we examine the case of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, a canonical model in the high-dimensional inference literature due to its analytical tractability and wide range of applications. This model requires a distinct mathematical approach, primarily because of the unique behavior of concentration inequalities in the OU setting.
In this context, we are able to prove an oracle inequality for the Lasso estimator (see Theorem
2.1
), which holds true on the intersection of three sets specifically tailored for our purposes. We will rigorously control the probability of these three sets in Section
6.2
, addressing one of the main challenges of our paper.
We will show that the first set arises from the martingale component of the stochastic differential equation, with a continuous version detailed in
[
17
]
. The second set results from discretization error. We will demonstrate that the dominance of either the discretization error or the martingale component will determine which set is more significant. Specifically, we will derive a condition involving
\Delta_{n}
,
T
,
p
,
s
and
d
that must be satisfied to ensure that discretization does not affect the convergence rate. The third set pertains to the model design and includes a version of the
compatibility condition
.

To prove our main results and control the probabilities of the aforementioned sets, it is essential to use concentration inequalities. As detailed in Section
3
, a crucial aspect of our analysis involves tracking the dependence on the step size
\Delta_{n}
, the dimension of the diffusion
d
, and the parameter
p
.
While concentration bounds have been widely studied in the literature, the explicit dependence on the discretization step appears to have received little attention. This gap motivates the development of two distinct concentration inequalities: one for the general linear model, derived using martingale decomposition techniques, and another for the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, obtained via Malliavin calculus. These results are stated in Section
3
.

Thanks to the approach summarized above, we are able to deduce error bounds for the
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
norms, evaluating the performance of the Lasso estimator we proposed. In particular, for the general linear drift, assuming that
(d\ln(p))/(n\Delta_{n})\rightarrow 0
, we have that if
1/(s^{2}dn\Delta_{n}^{2})\rightarrow 0
, then the error term dominates the convergence of the estimator and

\|\widehat{\theta}_{L}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{d}{k^{2}}\sqrt{s^{3}\Delta_{n}\ln(p)}\right).

If instead
1/(s^{2}dn\Delta_{n}^{2})\rightarrow\infty
, then the error term is negligible and

\|\widehat{\theta}_{L}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{d\ln(p)s}{n\Delta_{n}}}\right).

The constant
k
above depends on the model and, in the case where
p\leq d
, its magnitude is often
\sqrt{d}
. Therefore, when the discretization error is negligible, and recalling that
n\Delta_{n}=T
, we recover the same optimal rate as in the scenario where a continuous trajectory of the process was available (see
[
17
]
).
Our bounds are notably sharp, demonstrated by the logarithmic dependence on
p
.

It is also worth emphasizing that our condition for determining whether the contribution of the discretization error or the martingale part is dominant is given by the asymptotic behavior of
s^{2}dn\Delta_{n}^{2}
. Notably, when
d
,
s
and
p
are constants, the constraint for obtaining a negligible discretization error simplifies to
n\Delta_{n}^{2}\rightarrow 0
. This condition is consistent with classical assumptions in the literature for parameter estimation of diffusion processes (see Remark
2.5
below for details).

We then extend these conclusions to the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck case. As demonstrated in our analysis, the results remain essentially comparable, with the main distinction arising from the influence of the sparsity parameter. Due to the specific structure of the OU process, this parameter cannot be tracked as explicitly as in the general linear setting within our framework. Additionally, the use of a dedicated concentration inequality derived via Malliavin calculus represents one of the key methodological differences and directly impacts the resulting bounds. However, the convergence rates are equivalent across both models, with the sampling effect having the same impact in both cases.

Moreover, to illustrate our main results, we present numerical experiments for the linear model. Specifically, we evaluate the performance of the Lasso and the MLE in a high-dimensional situation, and compare the mean
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
error for different values of the dimension of the true parameter. Our results show that the proposed estimator performs well. In particular, we observe that the performance of the Lasso estimator, especially in terms of support recovery, is significantly superior to that of the maximum likelihood estimator, even for a relatively small dimension of the true parameter.

1.2.1
Assumptions for the general linear drift

As previously mentioned, we first present the results related to the general linear drift, that is, when the drift function in (
1
) takes the form given in (
3
). In this context, we assume the following:

Assumption 1
.

For all
j=0,\dots,p
, the functions
\phi_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
are uniformly Lipschitz in
p
and
d
, and we denote by
L_{j}:=\|\phi_{j}\|_{\text{\rm Lip}}
their Lipschitz norms.

Assumption 2
.

There exists a constant
M>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\theta\in\Theta
, we have that

\left\langle b_{\theta}(x)-b_{\theta}(y),x-y\right\rangle\geq M\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}.

Assumption

1
and
Assumption

2
imply that equation
(
1
)
admits a unique strong solution
X_{t}
, which is a homogeneous continuous Markov process with an invariant distribution (cf.
[
47
]
, Theorem 12.1). This solution has bounded moments of any order. Henceforth, we will assume that these moments are uniformly bounded in all quantities of interest.

Assumption 3
.

For all
i,j=1,\dots,p
the functions
\left\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\right\rangle:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are assumed to be Lipschitz continuous, with constants uniformly bounded in
p
and
d
, and we denote
L_{ij}:=\|\left\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\right\rangle\|_{\text{\rm Lip}}
.

Remark 1.1
.

Assumption

1
implies that the drift function
b_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the diffusion process is Lipschitz, and we denote its Lipschitz norm by
L
. Notably,
Assumption

1
implies that
L\leq(L_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\theta_{j}L_{j})
, indicating that the drift function is not uniformly Lipschitz in the variables of interest. However, we will utilize the Lipschitz property of
b
only in the proof of the concentration inequality in Section
3
, where the drift is evaluated at the true parameter value
\theta_{0}
. In this context,
b
has a Lipschitz constant
L_{0}+s\max_{k}\theta_{0}^{k}L_{k}
, which is uniformly bounded in
p
and
d
, under our assumptions.
Moreover, since
b_{\theta}
is of the form (
3
), the drift function is continuously differentiable in
\theta
and
\dot{b_{\theta}}(\cdot)
has polynomial growth. We also point out that, in terms of the drift function,
Assumption

3
implies that the functions
(\dot{b_{\theta}}^{\star}\dot{b_{\theta}})_{i,j}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are Lipschitz continuous.

Remark 1.2
.

It is important to note that the assumption of Lipschitz continuity for the functions
\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle
does not hold in the case of the OU process.
Assumption

3
plays a crucial role in deriving the concentration inequality that we employ in the general linear model. This significant difference motivates us to treat the OU process separately, leading to the use of alternative techniques, such as Malliavin calculus, to derive the corresponding concentration inequality.

Assumption 4
.

Given
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
\|\theta\|_{0}=s
and
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfying
\theta-\vartheta\in\mathcal{C}(s,3+4/\gamma)
(defined in (
5
)), for an arbitrary
\gamma>0
, there exists a constant

such that

\frac{(\theta-\vartheta)^{\star}I(\theta-\vartheta)}{\|\theta-\vartheta\|_{2}^{2}}>l.

Remark 1.3
.

Assumption

4
can be seen as a relaxation of the strong convexity condition of the Fisher information, particularly when the drift function takes the form given by (
3
). This assumption draws inspiration from similar conditions discussed in
[
13
,
32
]
. Our motivation stems from the need to provide results applicable to real high-dimensional settings where

.

Similar assumptions have also been considered in
[
17
]
. However, by leveraging the independence of the Fisher information with respect to the parameter and the geometric property of the Lasso error—which asserts that the estimation error lies within a subset of

as defined in (

]

Assumption

avoids imposing a condition of the type

for all

, which may not hold when the information matrix is degenerate. Instead, it replaces this with an identifiability condition on

for a specific subspace in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

1.2.2
Assumptions for the OU case

We now turn our attention to the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. Specifically, we consider a
d
-dimensional process defined as the solution to the stochastic differential equation

dX_{t}=-A_{0}X_{t}\,dt+dW_{t},\hskip 28.45274ptt\geq 0.

(6)

Here,
A_{0}\in\mathbb{A}\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{d\times d}
denotes the interaction matrix of the process, and

is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion.
In this setting, we make the following assumptions:

Assumption 5
.

The interaction matrix
A_{0}
is assumed to be diagonalizable, with eigenvalues
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{d}\in\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, all eigenvalues have strictly positive real parts, and we define

\mathfrak{m}:=\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\text{Re}(\tau_{i})>0.

It implies that

(7)

where the column vectors of

are the eigenvectors of

\mathfrak{p}_{0}:=\|P_{0}\|_{\text{op}}\|P_{0}^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}

Under

X_{t}=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\exp(-(t-s)A_{0})dW_{t}.

In this situation, it is well known that

X_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C_{\infty}),\hskip 28.45274ptC_{\infty}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp{(-sA_{0})}\exp{(-sA_{0}^{\star})}ds.

Assumption 6
.

The covariance matrix

Remark 1.4
.

Since the OU process has a linear drift, the interaction matrix
A_{0}

can be expressed in the general form (
3

denotes the

\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d^{2}}
. The drift can then be written as

A_{0}x=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{ij}\,\phi_{ij}(x),

where

denotes the

-th entry of

,
playing a role analogous to the coefficients

in (
3

Considering the functions
\phi_{ij}
as in (
8
), we can draw a parallel between the assumptions of the two cases. First, we observe that for the OU case,
Assumption

1

is directly satisfied with
L_{ij}=1
. On the other hand, both
Assumption

2
and
Assumption

5
capture the same stabilizing effect of the drift.
While
Assumption

2
expresses this through a monotonicity condition,

Assumption

5
formulates it in spectral terms, providing a more general condition that includes a wider class of interaction matrices
and aligns naturally with the classical OU literature. Finally, we note that
Assumption

4
and
Assumption

6
are also related, both reflecting the idea of identifiability. In this context,
Assumption

4
provides a more general formulation for linear models, as explained in Remark
1.3
,
while
Assumption

6
aligns with the standard assumptions used in the OU literature. Finally, unlike in the general linear model, the OU process does not satisfy
Assumption

3
(see Remark
1.2
). Therefore, these differences motivate us to present the results for the two models separately, in order to clearly convey and facilitate their understanding.

As in the linear case, we consider the setting where the process is observed at discrete time points
(X_{t_{i}})_{i=0,\dots,n-1}
, with
t_{j}:=jT/n
, where

denotes the time horizon and
\Delta_{n}:=T/n
the discretization step. Directly related with the observations of the process, we define the empirical covariance matrix of the OU as

C_{T}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{t_{i-1}}X_{t_{i-1}}^{\star},

(9)

2.1
Main results for the general linear case

We begin by considering the case in which the drift function satisfies the general linear model (

,

are all large, and we assume that the unknown parameter
\theta_{0}
satisfies the sparsity constraint (
2
).
In the idealized setting where the full trajectory of the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is observed, Girsanov’s theorem enables an explicit expression for the scaled negative log-likelihood of the drift parameter in (
1
), given by

L_{T}(\theta)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}b_{\theta}^{\star}(X_{t})\,dX_{t}+\frac{1}{2T}\int_{0}^{T}\|b_{\theta}(X_{t})\|^{2}\,dt.

(10)

However, our work is concerned with the more realistic scenario where the process is observed only at discrete time points. In such cases, the likelihood function cannot be directly employed to estimate the unknown parameter
\theta_{0}
. Various methods have been developed to approximate the likelihood in diffusion models (see, e.g.,
[
57
]
). We propose the following contrast function:

R_{T}(\theta):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\Delta X_{i}+\Delta_{n}\,b_{\theta}(X_{t_{i-1}})\right\|_{2}^{2},

(11)

where the norm denotes the standard Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This contrast function is motivated by a first-order approximation of the continuous-time likelihood (
10
). It offers several advantages: it is simple to implement, computationally efficient, and its structure yields a natural and interpretable estimator, making it well-suited for a wide range of practical applications.

Since our aim is to understand the statistical behaviour of the Lasso estimator, we define the discrete Lasso estimator as the penalised contrast function:

\widehat{\theta}_{L}:=\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\operatorname{arg}\!\operatorname{min}}\;\left(R_{T}(\theta)+\lambda\|\theta\|_{1}\right),

where
\lambda>0
is a tuning parameter. Our first goal is, using the properties of the Lasso estimator, to obtain an inequality that provides the basis for deriving an oracle inequality. For this purpose, we define the following bi-linear random form. Given
f,g
two smooth functions such that
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define

\langle f,g\rangle_{D}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{t_{i-1}})^{\star}g(X_{t_{i-1}})

(12)

and set
\|f\|_{D}:=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle_{D}}
the associated norm.

Given
n
observations of
(
1
)
, and considering that
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
is the true parameter of the diffusion drift, for any
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\in\Theta
we introduce two random functions

A(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(b_{\theta_{1}}(X_{t_{i-1}})-b_{\theta_{2}}(X_{t_{i-1}})\right)^{\star}\left(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\left(b_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t_{i-1}})-b_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})\right)ds\right),

(13)

G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(b_{\theta_{1}}(X_{t_{i-1}})-b_{\theta_{2}}(X_{t_{i-1}})\right)^{\star}\left(\Delta W_{i}\right).

(14)

The random function at (
13
) will play an essential role when evaluating the error associated to the discretization of the process. On the other hand,
(
14
)
can be seen as a martingale capturing the empirical behaviour of the increments of the underlying diffusion.

In this work, we prove an oracle inequality that holds true with high probability. To accomplish this, we will need to ensure some control over the probabilities of the following sets:

\mathcal{T}:=\left\{\left\|\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{b_{\theta^{\prime}}}(X_{t_{i-1}})^{\star}\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\lambda}{4}\right\},

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}:=\left\{\left\|\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{b_{\theta^{\prime}}}(X_{t_{i-1}})^{\star}\left(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\left(b_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})-b_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t_{i-1}})\right)ds\right)\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\lambda}{4}\right\},

(15)

\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime}:=\left\{\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\theta\|_{0}=s\\
\upsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\theta-\upsilon\in\mathcal{C}(s,3+4/\gamma)\end{subarray}}{\frac{\|b_{\theta}-b_{\upsilon}\|_{D}}{\|\theta-\upsilon\|_{2}}\geq k}\right\},

for a constant

The first set,

\mathcal{T}

Remark 2.5
.

As mentioned in the introduction, Corollary
2.4
indicates that the threshold determining whether the discretization error or the martingale part is dominant is given by the asymptotic behavior of
1/s^{2}dn\Delta_{n}^{2}
. When
d,s
and
p
are constants, this simplifies to
n\Delta_{n}^{2}\rightarrow 0
. This assumption aligns with the condition found in the literature for parameter estimation in classical SDEs, as initially introduced by
[
26
]
. This condition was later improved to
n\Delta_{n}^{3}\rightarrow 0
by
[
57
]
, thanks to a correction in the contrast function. Finally, Kessler
[
37
]
proposed a contrast function based on a Gaussian approximation of the transition density, which allowed for a weaker condition
n\Delta_{n}^{\gamma}\rightarrow 0
for any arbitrary integer
\gamma
. Similar developments have been made for classical SDEs with jumps in
[
3
,
4
,
31
,
50
]
.

One might wonder if, in our context, it would be possible to relax the condition on the discretization step similarly.
To begin with, it is important to highlight that the analysis of discretization error, as presented in Proposition
6.4
, can be significantly simplified when the drift is bounded. In such cases, applying the Itô formula leads us to the condition
1/(s^{2}dn\Delta_{n}^{3})\rightarrow\infty
to ensure that the martingale part is the main contribution, and the error due to discretization becomes negligible. Even with an unbounded drift, we believe that utilizing higher-order approximations and iterating the Itô formula (similar to the methods proposed in
[
3
]
and
[
37
]
) could yield a less stringent condition. However, this would introduce additional stochastic terms into our analysis, which would need to be controlled using concentration inequalities (see Section
3
), with explicit dependence on the variables of interest. This adjustment would significantly alter our approach, resulting in more complex computations and interpretations. Consequently, we have left this for future exploration.

Remark 2.6
.

If
p\leq d
, meaning that the dimension of the stochastic process is greater than or equal to the dimension of the parameter, it is reasonable to add a classical assumption about the minimum eigenvalue

of the empirical Fisher information matrix (
4
). Specifically, if we assume

, then by choosing

and remarking that the magnitude of

is usually of order

Therefore, in the case where the discretization error is negligible, we achieve the same optimal rate of convergence for the estimator as when the entire path of the process is observed (see
[
17
]
).

Remark 2.10

The key distinction between Corollary

First, the concentration inequality differs between the two cases, leading to variations in how the parameters are tracked. Second, in the general linear drift model, the inherent structure of the drift allows us to effectively exploit the sparsity of the vector, making it easier to track the influence of

3
The concentration inequalities

In order to control the sets on which our oracle inequality holds true, we will need to derive bounds for the probability of the sets
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime}
. Given the structure of these sets, it is evident that we will need to employ a concentration bound for functionals of the form

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(F(X_{t_{i}})-\mathbb{E}\left[F(X_{t_{i}})\right]\right),

(24)

where
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies specific assumptions, depending on the goal and the nature of the problem, and
X_{t_{1}},\dots,X_{t_{n}}
are observations of the stochastic differential equation
(
1
)
. In particular, due to the structure of our sets, we will provide a concentration inequality under the assumption that
F
is Lipschitz.

Concentration inequalities (CI) for additive functionals are crucial probabilistic tools in statistics and other areas as machine learning or mathematical finance. There is increasing focus on concentration inequalities as in general terms, they allow us to quantify the deviation of an estimator or a stochastic element from its expected value or a target quantity. While CI for independent or strong mixed data have been explored for many years (see
[
48
,
58
]
), deep results for additive functionals of diffusion processes are limited.
Remarkable results on this area are presented in
[
1
,
23
,
54
,
55
]
among others. To the best of our knowledge, none of them have explicitly tracked the influence of the discretization step
\Delta_{n}
of the observations, nor the dimension of the process. As understanding the role of
\Delta_{n}
is crucial for assessing the influence of various parameters in our high-dimensional setting, we derive two exponential concentration inequalities corresponding to the two drift structures considered in this work. The first one is established for the general linear drift under
Assumption

3
. Since the OU process does not satisfy this assumption, we develop a separate, tailored concentration inequality using tools from Malliavin calculus.

3.1
Concentration inequality for the general linear drift model

We begin by studying the general linear drift setting, where the concentration inequality we derive is inspired by the approach developed in Varvenne’s work
[
55
]
. In that paper, a similar strategy is applied in the (more involving) context of diffusions driven by fractional Brownian motion. Our contribution lies in simplifying this methodology to the standard Brownian setting, while explicitly and carefully tracking the influence of the discretization step
\Delta_{n}
, which plays a crucial role in our analysis. A key assumption for deriving the concentration inequality is the Lipschitz continuity of the contrast functional in (
24

3

For a fixed value

Let

be a Lipschitz function, we define the Lipschitz norm of

by

\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|F(x)-F(y)|}{d_{n}(x,y)}.

.

Assume that the drift function satisfies

1
-
Assumption

In addition, if

3.2
Concentration inequality for the OU case

In contrast to the general linear model, the OU process does not satisfy
Assumption

3
. Therefore, a different strategy must be adopted to present a suitable concentration inequality. In this case, our approach is inspired by the work of
[
16
]
, where techniques from Malliavin calculus are employed to handle a similar challenge.

We now present some preliminaries on Malliavin calculus. For a comprehensive treatment of the subject, we refer the reader to
[
43
]
.

Let
\mathbb{H}
be a real separable Hilbert space. We denote by
B:=\{B(h):h\in\mathbb{H}\}
an isonormal Gaussian process over
\mathbb{H}
, meaning that
B
is a centered Gaussian family with covariance structure

\mathbb{E}[B(h_{1})B(h_{2})]=\langle h_{1},h_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},\quad\text{for all }h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{H}.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the set of smooth cylindrical random variables of the form

F=f(B(h_{1}),\ldots,B(h_{n}))
, where
n\geq 1
,

is a
C^{\infty}
-function with compact support, and
h_{i}\in\mathbb{H}
.

The Malliavin derivative
DF
of

is defined as

DF:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\left(B(h_{1}),\ldots,B(h_{n})\right)h_{i}.

We introduce the norm

\|F\|_{1,2}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}[F^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|DF\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}],

and define
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
as the closure of
\mathcal{S}
with respect to this norm.

A key property of the Malliavin derivative

is that it satisfies a chain rule. Specifically, if
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable function with bounded partial derivatives (i.e.,
\varphi\in C_{b}^{1}
) and
(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})
is a vector of elements in
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
, then
\varphi(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}

D\varphi(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})DF_{i}.

The concentration inequality we will use follows from the following key result, which provides tail bounds for certain elements in the space
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}

Theorem 3.2
.

(

Assume that the random variable

5
Conclusions and outlook

Our work addresses the problem of estimating the drift parameters in high-dimensional diffusion processes observed at discrete time points. To this end, we propose the use of the Lasso estimator under a sparsity assumption on the unknown parameter. The theoretical analysis is centered around an oracle inequality for the Lasso estimator, presented in Theorem
2.1
. This result holds on the intersection of three specifically constructed sets: one related to the martingale component of the process (
\mathcal{T}
), another capturing the discretization error (
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
), and a third (
\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime}
)
involving the model design through a compatibility condition. Among these, the set
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, which accounts for the discretization bias, represents a central methodological contribution of our work, as it addresses a gap in the analysis of Lasso estimation for discretely observed diffusion processes.

We establish control over these sets using concentration inequalities developed in Propositions
3.1
and
3.3
. These results explicitly quantify the dependence on the discretization step, the model dimension, the number of parameters, and the sparsity level, thus providing a significant advancement in the theory for discretely observed systems. Under suitable asymptotic conditions, we show that the effect of discretization can become negligible, allowing the Lasso estimator to attain the same optimal convergence rates as in the ideal case of continuous observation. These findings are formally stated in Corollaries
2.3
and
2.8
, corresponding to the general linear model and the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, respectively.

In addition, numerical experiments presented in Section
4
validate our theoretical results. They demonstrate that the Lasso estimator performs well in both
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
error norms and significantly outperforms the maximum likelihood estimator in terms of support recovery.

A natural and promising direction for future research is the extension of our framework to nonlinear drift functions. While the oracle inequality (
16
) and the structure of the sets
\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime}

remain formally valid, the main challenge lies in the probabilistic control of these sets. In contrast to linear models, the derivative of the drift in the nonlinear setting may depend explicitly on the parameter, requiring uniform bounds. This, in turn, calls for more sophisticated tools such as Talagrand’s generic chaining technique. Although such methods have been successfully employed in continuous-time settings (see, e.g.
[
17
]
), their adaptation to the discrete-time, high-dimensional regime remains an open and technically demanding problem.

Another interesting avenue for future work is the exploration of alternative matrix structures in high-dimensional OU processes. While our current analysis assumes a full-rank interaction matrix, it would be valuable to investigate the implications of relaxing Assumption
5
to allow for low-rank structures. In such cases, traditional methods may no longer be adequate, and estimators incorporating nuclear norm penalization could provide a more appropriate framework for capturing the underlying low-rank dynamics.

1

\displaystyle\frac{\|b_{\theta}-b_{\upsilon}\|_{D}^{2}}{\|\theta-\upsilon\|_{2}^{2}}\geq l+\frac{H_{\theta-\upsilon}}{\|\theta-\upsilon\|_{2}^{2}},

where
H_{\theta-\upsilon}
has been defined in
(

Now, using properties of the supremum and the infimum, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left(\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\theta\|_{0}=s\\
\upsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\theta-\upsilon\in\mathcal{C}(s,3+4/\gamma)\end{subarray}}\frac{\|b_{\theta}-b_{\upsilon}\|_{D}^{2}}{\|\theta-\upsilon\|_{2}^{2}}\geq k^{2}\right)\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left(\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\theta\|_{0}=s\\
\upsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\theta-\upsilon\in\mathcal{C}(s,3+4/\gamma)\end{subarray}}\frac{H_{\theta-\upsilon}}{\|\theta-\upsilon\|_{2}^{2}}\geq-(l-k^{2})\right)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\theta\|_{0}=s\\
\upsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\theta-\upsilon\in\mathcal{C}(s,3+4/\gamma)\end{subarray}}\frac{-H_{\theta-\upsilon}}{\|\theta-\upsilon\|_{2}^{2}}<l-k^{2}\right)\geq

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\theta\|_{0}=s\\
\upsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\theta-\upsilon\in\mathcal{C}(s,3+4/\gamma)\end{subarray}}\frac{|H_{\theta-\upsilon}|}{\|\theta-\upsilon\|_{2}^{2}}\geq l-k^{2}\right).

Therefore, we can conclude that

\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left(\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\theta\|_{0}=s\\
\upsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\theta-\upsilon\in\mathcal{C}(s,3+4/\gamma)\end{subarray}}\frac{\|b_{\theta}-b_{\upsilon}\|_{D}^{2}}{\|\theta-\upsilon\|_{2}^{2}}\geq k^{2}\right)\geq 1-\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left(\sup_{u\in\mathcal{C}(s,3+4/\gamma)}\frac{|H_{u}|}{\|u\|_{2}^{2}}\geq l-k^{2}\right).

(58)

Now, to prove our statement, we derive a bound for the probability of the right part of inequality
(
58
)
.
For any

Working similarly to Lemma F.1 and F.3 from the supplementary material of

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left(\sup_{u\in\mathcal{C}(s,3+4/\gamma)}\frac{|H_{u}|}{\|u\|_{2}^{2}}\geq l-k^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left(\left(5+\frac{4}{\gamma}\right)^{2}\sup_{u\in cl\{\text{conv}(\mathcal{K}(s))\}}\frac{|H_{u}|}{\|u\|_{2}^{2}}\geq l-k^{2}\right)

and

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left(\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\theta\|_{0}=s\\

6.4.1
Proof of the concentration inequality for the general linear drift

We start by introducing the following notation.
Given two functions
f,g\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, let
a,b,c>0
such that
a<b<c
. Then, we define

f_{[a,b]}\sqcup g_{[b,c]}:=\begin{cases}f(t)\quad&\text{if}\quad a\leq t\leq b\\
g(t)\quad&\text{if}\quad b<t\leq c.\\
\end{cases}

Following the ideas in
[
55
]
, the proof is based on the decomposition of the functional
F_{X}=F(X_{t_{1}},\dots,X_{t_{n}})
into a sum of martingale increments, and derive conditional exponential moments for them.

Proof.

Let
W
be a
d-
dimensional Brownian motion defined on
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}\right).
Let us denote by
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
the natural filtration associated to
W
and by
\mathbb{P}_{W}
its distribution.

For a time
t_{k}>0
, we denote by
\tilde{w}_{s}
the Brownian motion for
t_{s}>t_{k}
and by
\mathbb{P}_{W}(d\tilde{w})
the distribution law of
\tilde{w}
conditioned to
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}
.

Given
F_{X}
, we start by constructing the associated martingale
M_{k}=\mathbb{E}[F_{X}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}]
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(M_{k}-M_{k-1}\right)=F_{X}-\mathbb{E}[F_{X}]
.
Under
Assumption

1
,
X_{t}
can be expressed as a measurable functional of the time
t
, of the initial condition
X_{0}
and of the Brownian motion, i.e.
X_{t}:=\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
X_{t}=\Psi(t,x,(W_{s})_{s\in[0,t]})=\Psi_{t}(x,(W_{s})_{s\in[0,t]})
. To streamline the notation in this proof and alleviate the complexity of the upcoming computations, we have opted to simplify by using
|\cdot|
to denote both the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the absolute value on
\mathbb{R}
.

For
k\geq 1
, we have that

\displaystyle|M_{k}-M_{k-1}|=|\mathbb{E}[F_{X}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}]-|\mathbb{E}[F_{X}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}]|\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\big|F\big(\Psi_{t_{1}}(X_{0},W_{[0,t_{1}]}),\dots,\Psi_{t_{k}}(X_{0},W_{[0,t_{k}]}),\Psi_{t_{k+1}}(X_{0},W_{[0,t_{k}]}\sqcup\tilde{w}_{[t_{k},t_{k+1}]}),\dots,

\displaystyle\Psi_{t_{n}}(X_{0},W_{[0,t_{k}]}\sqcup\tilde{w}_{[t_{k},t_{n}]})\big)

\displaystyle-F\big(\Psi_{t_{1}}(X_{0},W_{[0,t_{1}]}),\dots,\Psi_{t_{k-1}}(X_{0},W_{[0,t_{k-1}]}),\Psi_{t_{k}}(X_{0},W_{[0,t_{k-1}]}\sqcup\tilde{w}_{[t_{k-1},t_{k}]}),\dots,

\displaystyle\Psi_{t_{n}}(X_{0},W_{[0,t_{k-1}]}\sqcup\tilde{w}_{[t_{k-1},t_{n}]})\big)\big|\mathbb{P}_{W}(d\tilde{w})

\displaystyle\leq\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{j=k}^{n}\Big|\Psi_{t_{j}}\left(X_{0},W_{[0,t_{k}]}\sqcup\tilde{w}_{[t_{k},t_{j}]}\right)-\Psi_{t_{j}}\left(X_{0},W_{[0,t_{k-1}]}\sqcup\tilde{w}_{[t_{k-1},t_{j}]}\right)\Big|\mathbb{P}_{W}(d\tilde{w}).

(67)

Let us set
u:=j-k+1.
Then, for
u\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we define

I_{t_{u}}:=\Psi_{t_{u+k-1}}\left(X_{0},(W_{s})_{s\in[0,t_{k}]}\sqcup(\tilde{w}_{s})_{s\in[t_{k},t_{u+k-1}]}\right)

and

\overline{I_{t_{u}}}:=\Psi_{t_{u+k-1}}\left(X_{0},(W_{s})_{s\in[0,t_{k-1}]}\sqcup(\tilde{w}_{s})_{s\in[t_{k-1},t_{u+k-1}]}\right),

where
t_{u}=u\Delta_{n}
.

Using the notation we have introduced, we can express
I_{t_{u}},\overline{I_{t_{u}}}
as

I_{t_{u}}=X_{0}-\int_{0}^{t_{u}}b(I_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t_{k}}dW_{s}+\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{u+k-1}}d\tilde{w}_{s},

\overline{I_{t_{u}}}=X_{0}-\int_{0}^{t_{u}}b(\overline{I_{s}})ds+\int_{0}^{t_{k-1}}dW_{s}+\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{u+k-1}}d\tilde{w}_{s}.

Using the change of variable
\tilde{s}=s-t_{k-1}
and defining the Brownian motions independent of
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}
,
(W^{(k)}_{s})_{s\geq 0}:=\left(W_{s+k-1}-W_{k-1}\right)_{s\geq 0}
and
\tilde{w}^{(k)}:=(\tilde{w}_{s+k-1}-\tilde{w}_{k-1})_{s\geq 0};
we can express the difference of
I_{t_{u}}
and
\overline{I_{t_{u}}}
as

I_{t_{u}}-\overline{I_{t_{u}}}=\int_{0}^{t_{u}}\left(b(\overline{I_{s}})-b(I_{s})\right)ds+\int_{0}^{\Delta_{n}}d(W^{(k)}-\tilde{w}^{(k)})_{\tilde{s}}.

We will proceed to control the differences
|I_{t_{u}}-\overline{I_{t_{u}}}|
. To do that, we consider two different cases:
u\geq 2
and
1\leq u\leq 2
.

When
u\geq 2
, by
Assumption

2
, we have that

\frac{d}{dt}|I_{t_{u}}-\overline{I_{t_{u}}}|^{2}=2\left|I_{t_{u}}-\overline{I_{t_{u}}}\right|^{\star}\left(b(\overline{I_{t_{u}}})-b(I_{t_{u}})\right)\leq-2M|I_{t_{u}}-\overline{I_{t_{u}}}|^{2}.

(68)

Applying Gronwall inequality to
(
68
)
, we obtain that,
\forall t_{u}\in[t_{2},\infty),

|I_{t_{u}}-\overline{I_{t_{u}}}|\leq|I_{t_{2}}-\overline{I_{t_{2}}}|\exp{\left(-M(t_{u}-t_{2})\right)}.

(69)

We consider now the case
1\leq u\leq 2
. The Lipschitzianity of
b
gathered in
Assumption

1
ensures

\displaystyle|I_{t_{u}}-\overline{I_{t_{u}}}|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{t_{u}}\left(b(\overline{I_{s}})-b(I_{s})\right)ds+\int_{0}^{\Delta_{n}}d\left(W^{(k)}-\tilde{w}^{(k)}\right)_{\tilde{s}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq L\int_{0}^{t_{u}}\left|I_{s}-\overline{I_{s}}\right|ds+\left|\int_{0}^{\Delta_{n}}d\left(W^{(k)}-\tilde{w}^{(k)}\right)_{\tilde{s}}\right|.

Now, using the Gronwall lemma in its integral form,

\displaystyle|I_{t_{u}}-\overline{I_{t_{u}}}|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{0}^{\Delta_{n}}d\left(W^{(k)}-\tilde{w}^{(k)}\right)_{\tilde{s}}\right|+\int_{0}^{t_{u}}\left|\int_{0}^{\Delta_{n}}d\left(W^{(k)}-\tilde{w}^{(k)}\right)_{\tilde{s}}\right|L\exp(L(t_{u}-s))ds

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{0}^{\Delta_{n}}d\left(W^{(k)}-\tilde{w}^{(k)}\right)_{\tilde{s}}\right|\left(1+\int_{0}^{t_{u}}L\exp{(L(t_{u}-s))}ds\right)

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{\Delta_{n}}d\left(W^{(k)}-\tilde{w}^{(k)}\right)_{\tilde{s}}\right|\exp(Lt_{u}),

(70)

where in the last line we have computed the integral explicitly.
We observe that the martingale differences can be bounded in terms of
|I_{t_{u}}-\overline{I_{t_{u}}}|
, since according to (
67
),

|M_{k}-M_{k-1}|\leq\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\sum_{u=1}^{n-k+1}|I_{t_{u}}-\overline{I_{t_{u}}}|\mathbb{P}_{W}(d\tilde{w})\right).

Combining
(
70
)
for the case
u=1
and
(
69
)
for the case
u>1
, we observe that, for all
p\geq 1
we have that

\displaystyle|M_{k}-M_{k-1}|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}^{p}2^{p-1}\exp({Lp\Delta_{n}})\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|\int_{0}^{\Delta_{n}}d\left(W^{(k)}-\tilde{w}^{(k)}\right)_{\tilde{s}}\right|\mathbb{P}_{W}(d\tilde{w})\right)^{p}

\displaystyle+\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}^{p}2^{p-1}\left(\sum_{u=2}^{n-k+1}\exp(-M(t_{u}-t_{2}))\right)^{p}\left(\int_{\Omega}|I_{t_{2}}-\overline{I_{t_{2}}}|\mathbb{P}_{W}(d\tilde{w})\right)^{p}.

(71)

To simplify the notation, we define the following expressions:

\Psi_{n,k}:=\sum_{u=2}^{n-k+1}\exp(-M(t_{u}-t_{2}))\hskip 28.45274pt\text{ and }\hskip 28.45274ptG_{\Delta_{n}}^{(k)}:=\int_{0}^{\Delta_{n}}d\left(W^{(k)}-\tilde{w}^{(k)}\right)_{\tilde{s}}.

If we use the bound
(
70
)
for
|I_{t_{2}}-\overline{I_{t_{2}}}|
in
(
71
)
, we can express the martingale differences as

|M_{k}-M_{k-1}|^{p}\leq 2^{p}\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}^{p}\exp(Lpt_{2})\Psi_{n,k}^{p}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|G_{\Delta_{n}}^{(k)}\right|\mathbb{P}_{W}(d\tilde{w})\right)^{p}.

Now we proceed to find a bound for
\mathbb{E}[|M_{k}-M_{k-1}|^{p}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}-1}]
for
p\geq 2.
If we denote by
\{\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
the natural filtration associated to
W^{(k)}
, since
W^{(k)}
and
\tilde{w}^{(k)}
are independent from
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|M_{k}-M_{k-1}|^{p}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{p}\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}^{p}\exp(Lpt_{2})\Psi_{n,k}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|G_{\Delta_{n}}^{(k)}\right|\mathbb{P}_{W}(d\tilde{w})\right)^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=2^{p}\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}^{p}\exp(Lpt_{2})\Psi_{n,k}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left|G_{\Delta_{n}}^{(k)}\right|^{p}\Big|\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{t_{1}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=2^{p}\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}^{p}\exp(Lpt_{2})\Psi_{n,k}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|G_{\Delta_{n}}^{(k)}\right|^{p}\right].

(72)

To control the conditional expectations of the increments of the martingale
M_{k}
, we prove a bound for
\mathbb{E}[|G_{\Delta_{n}}^{(k)}|^{p}]
. We observe that
G_{\Delta_{n}}^{(k)}\overset{d}{=}\int_{0}^{\Delta_{n}}dW_{s}
, which implies that
G_{\Delta_{n}}^{(k)}\overset{d}{=}W_{\Delta_{n}}
being
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
a
d-
dimensional Brownian motion, and
W_{t}^{i}
the
i
th component of the process.
Since
-W_{\Delta_{n}}^{1}\overset{d}{=}W_{\Delta_{n}}^{1}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|G_{\Delta_{n}}^{(k)}\right|\geq x\right)

\displaystyle\leq d\left[\mathbb{P}\left(-\left(W_{\Delta_{n}}^{1}\right)\geq\frac{x}{\sqrt{d}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(W_{\Delta_{n}}^{1}\geq\frac{x}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right]\leq 2d\hskip 2.84544pt\mathbb{P}\left(W_{\Delta_{n}}^{1}\geq\frac{x}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

Using Markov’s inequality and choosing
\eta=x/(\sqrt{d}\Delta_{n})
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|G_{\Delta_{n}}^{(k)}\right|\geq x\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2d\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\eta W_{\Delta_{n}}^{1}\right)\right]\exp\left(-\eta xd^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=2d\exp\left(\frac{\eta^{2}\Delta_{n}}{2}-\frac{\eta x}{\sqrt{d}}\right)=2d\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2d\Delta_{n}}\right).

Now, using that
\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)dx
for a non-negative random variable, and making the change of variables
x=s^{p}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|G_{\Delta_{n}}^{(k)}\right|^{p}\right]\leq 2dp\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{p-1}\exp\left({-\frac{s^{2}}{2d\Delta_{n}}}\right)ds.

If we proceed with the following change of variables
\tilde{s}=s^{2}/(2d\Delta_{n})
, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|G_{\Delta_{n}}^{(k)}\right|^{p}\right]\leq dp\hskip 1.9919pt\Gamma\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)(2d\Delta_{n})^{\frac{p}{2}},

(73)

where
\Gamma(y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-s)s^{y-1}ds.

From
(
72
)
, we have that

\mathbb{E}[|M_{k}-M_{k-1}|^{p}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}-1}]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq 2\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}\exp(2L\Delta_{n})\Psi_{n,k}\sqrt{d\Delta_{n}}\left(2d\hskip 1.9919ptp\hskip 1.9919pt\Gamma\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Now, applying Lemma 3.1 of
[
55
]
and using the fact that
\mathbb{E}[M_{k}-M_{k-1}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}]=0
, we obtain that for any
\eta>0,

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\eta(M_{k}-M_{k-1})\right)|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}\right]\leq\exp{\left(16\eta^{2}\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}^{2}\exp(4L\Delta_{n})\Psi_{n,k}^{2}d^{2}\Delta_{n}\right)}.

Now we observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\eta M_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\eta M_{n-1})\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\eta(M_{n}-M_{n-1}))\right|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n-1}}]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\eta M_{n-1})\right]\exp{\left(16\eta^{2}\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}^{2}\exp(4L\Delta_{n})\Psi_{n,n}^{2}d^{2}\Delta_{n}\right)}.

Applying the same procedure recursively, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\eta\left(F_{Y}-\mathbb{E}[F_{Y}]\right))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\eta\left(M_{n}-M_{0}\right))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(16\eta^{2}\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}^{2}\exp(4L\Delta_{n})d^{2}\Delta_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Psi_{n,k}^{2}\right)}.

Since

\Psi_{n,k}=\sum_{u=2}^{n-k+1}\exp(-M(t_{u}-t_{2}))=\sum_{\tilde{u}=0}^{n-k-1}\exp(-\tilde{u}M\Delta_{n})\leq(1-\exp(-M\Delta_{n}))^{-1},

then
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Psi_{n,k}^{2}\leq n/(1-\exp(-M\Delta_{n}))^{2}
and we conclude that

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp{(\eta(F_{Y}-\mathbb{E}[F_{Y}]))}\right]\leq\exp{\left(\frac{16\eta^{2}d^{2}\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}^{2}n\Delta_{n}\exp(4L\Delta_{n})}{(1-\exp(-M\Delta_{n}))^{2}}\right)}.

Now, if we apply Markov’s inequality and set
\eta=\frac{r(1-\exp(-M\Delta_{n}))^{2}}{32n\Delta_{n}d^{2}\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}^{2}\exp(4L\Delta_{n})}
, we obtain that

\mathbb{P}\left(F_{Y}-\mathbb{E}[F_{Y}]>r\right)\leq\exp{\left(\frac{-r^{2}(1-\exp(-M\Delta_{n}))^{2}}{64d^{2}\|F\|_{\text{Lip}}^{2}n\Delta_{n}\exp(4L\Delta_{n})}\right)}.

Finally, in case the functional
F
is of the form
F_{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{t_{i}})
for a Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

6.4.2
Proof of the concentration inequality for the OU case

Proof.

For an element
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|v\|_{2}=1
, we define the centred Gaussian process
Y_{t}^{v}=v^{\star}X_{t}
whose covariance kernel is given by
\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{v}Y_{s}^{v}]=\rho_{v}(|t-s|)
, with
\rho_{v}(r):=v^{\star}\exp(-rA_{0})C_{\infty}v
.
We observe that
(Y_{t}^{v})_{t\in[0,T]}
can be considered as an isonormal Gaussian process indexed by a separable Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
whose scalar product is induced by the covariance kernel of
(Y_{t}^{v})_{t\in[0,T]}
. In particular, we can write
Y_{t}^{v}=B(h_{t})
and
\langle h_{t},h_{s}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\rho(|t-s|)
. Now, we define the quantity

Z_{n}^{v}:=v^{\star}(C_{T}-C_{\infty})v=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left((Y_{t_{i-1}}^{v})^{2}-\mathbb{E}[(Y_{t_{i-1}}^{v})^{2}]\right).

We note that
Z_{n}^{v}
is an element of the second order Wiener chaos. Hence,
Z_{n}^{v}
has a Lebesgue density and condition
(ii)
of Theorem
3.2
is satisfied. Moreover, since
L^{-1}Z_{n}^{v}=-(1/2)Z_{n}^{v}
, we have that

\displaystyle\langle DZ_{n}^{v},-DL^{-1}Z_{n}^{v}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\frac{1}{2}\langle DZ_{n}^{v},DZ_{n}^{v}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left\langle D(Y_{t_{i-1}}^{v}),D(Y_{t_{j-1}}^{v})\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|Y_{t_{i-1}}^{v}Y_{t_{j-1}}^{v}|\left|\rho_{v}\left(t_{i-1}-t_{j-1}\right)\right|\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|Y_{t_{i-1}}^{v}|^{2}\left|\rho_{v}\left(t_{i-1}-t_{j-1}\right)\right|.

Adding and substracting
\mathbb{E}[(Y_{t_{i-1}}^{v})^{2}]
we obtain

\displaystyle\langle DZ_{n}^{v},-DL^{-1}Z_{n}^{v}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(|Y_{t_{i-1}}^{v}|^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{t_{i-1}}^{v})^{2}\right]\right)\left|\rho_{v}\left(t_{i-1}-t_{j-1}\right)\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{t_{i-1}}^{v})^{2}\right]\left|\rho_{v}\left(|t_{i-1}-t_{j-1}|\right)\right|=I_{1}+I_{2}.

First, we work on
I_{1}
. Since
t_{i-1}=(i-1)\Delta_{n}
and the same applies to
t_{j-1}
, a change of variable leads us to

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq\frac{4}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(|Y_{t_{i-1}}^{v}|^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{t_{i-1}}^{v})^{2}\right]\right)\left|\rho_{v}\left(\Delta_{n}k\right)\right|=\frac{4}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}Z_{n}^{v}|\rho_{v}(\Delta_{n}k)|.

Similarly, expression
I_{2}
can be controlled as

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq\frac{4}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{t_{i-1}}^{v})^{2}\right]\left|\rho_{v}\left(\Delta_{n}k\right)\right|\leq\frac{4}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho_{v}(0)|\rho_{v}(\Delta_{n}k)|.

Using that
|\rho_{v}(\Delta_{n}k)|\leq\|\exp(-\Delta_{n}kA_{0})\|_{\text{op}}\|C_{\infty}\|_{\text{op}}\leq\mathfrak{p}_{0}\mathfrak{l}_{\max}\exp(-\mathfrak{m}\Delta_{n}k),
we observe that condition
(ii)
of Theorem
3.2

is satisfied since

\displaystyle\langle DZ_{n}^{v},-DL^{-1}Z_{n}^{v}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\frac{4}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}|\rho_{v}(\Delta_{n}k)|(Z_{n}^{v}+\rho_{v}(0))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{n}\mathfrak{p}_{0}\mathfrak{l}_{\max}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\exp{(-\Delta_{n}\mathfrak{m}k)}(Z_{n}^{v}+\mathfrak{l}_{\max})\leq\alpha Z_{n}^{v}+\beta,

for
\alpha=4n^{-1}\mathfrak{p}_{0}\mathfrak{l}_{\max}a
and
\beta=4n^{-1}\mathfrak{p}_{0}\mathfrak{l}_{\max}^{2}a
, where
a=(1-\exp(-(n+1)\mathfrak{m}\Delta_{n}))/(1-\exp(-\mathfrak{m}\Delta_{n})).

Therefore, result (
25
) follows.

When
\Delta_{n}\rightarrow 0
, bounding from the infinite sum and applying a first order Taylor expansion, we observe that

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\exp{(-\Delta_{n}\mathfrak{m}k)}\leq(1-\exp(-\mathfrak{m}\Delta_{n}))^{-1}\leq(\mathfrak{m}\Delta_{n})^{-1}
. Then, considering
a=(\mathfrak{m}\Delta_{n})^{-1}
completes the proof.
∎
