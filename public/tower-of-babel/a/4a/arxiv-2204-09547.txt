Dependent Optics
Pietro Vertechi

Abstract
A wide variety of bidirectional data accessors, ranging from mixed optics to functor lenses , can be formalized within a unique framework— dependent optics . Starting from two indexed categories, which encode what maps are allowed in the forward and backward directions, we define the category of dependent optics and establish under what assumptions it has coproducts. Different choices of indexed categories correspond to different families of optics: we discuss dependent lenses and prisms, as well as closed dependent optics. We introduce the notion of Tambara representation and use it to classify contravariant functors from the category of optics, thus generalizing the profunctor encoding of optics to the dependent case.



1
Introduction

Lenses
[
3
,
11
]
are composable, bidirectional data accessors. They can be thought of as a collection of two methods: a
{\mathrm{get}}
method, to access a particular field of a data structure, and a
{\mathrm{put}}
method, to build a new instance of the data structure with an updated field value. Lenses and their more recent generalization, optics, have been implemented and explored in the popular
Haskell
library
\mathrm{lens}

[
13
]
. The possible fields of application vary widely, from game theory
[
10
]
to automatic differentiation
[
9
]
.

The current formalization of optics
[
8
,
21
]
extends the original theory of lenses from Cartesian categories to arbitrary symmetric monoidal categories, or even
actegories
, thus including under a unique formalism a wide variety of data accessors. Unfortunately, this approach fails to include a distinct elegant generalization of lenses, namely
functor lenses

[
22
]
: every pseudofunctor
{\mathcal{R}}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{op}}\rightarrow\mathbf{Cat}
induces a fibration of categories
{\mathbf{Lens}}_{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
via the Grothendieck construction on the pointwise opposite of
{\mathcal{R}}
. Classical categories of lenses, as well as novel examples, can be obtained with this approach.

The aim of this work is to develop a common generalization of the theories of optics and functor lenses, via the theory of bicategories and pseudofunctors. In
section

2
we lay the fundamental definition of
dependent optic
and show that it encompasses both regular optics and functor lenses. We show under what conditions the category of dependent optics has coproducts. In
section

3
we give some examples of dependent optics—dependent (monoidal) lenses, dependent (monoidal) prisms, and closed dependent optics—where the key ingredient is the operation of tensoring (co)modules over a (co)monoid in a symmetric monoidal category. Finally, in
section

4
, we establish the notion of
Tambara representation
. We show that
\mathcal{D}
-valued Tambara representations are equivalent to contravariant functors from the category of optics to an arbitrary category
\mathcal{D}
, thus generalizing the profunctor encoding of optics to the dependent case.

The aim of this section is to establish a general definition of
dependent optics
which encompasses both previous generalizations of lenses—functor lenses and optics. The definition is entirely analogous to the definition of optics, but the monoidal actions are replaced by
\mathcal{B}
-indexed categories, where
\mathcal{B}
is a
bicategory

[
2
]
(see also
[
14
]
for a more modern treatment). We will consider the bicategory as a
category weakly enriched in categories
, hence the notation
\mathcal{B}(A,B)
, for
A,B\in\ob(\mathcal{B})
, will represent the category of morphisms from
A
to
B
.

To encode the data of a
\mathcal{B}
-indexed category
{\mathcal{L}}
, i.e. a pseudofunctor
{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{B}^{\textnormal{op}}\rightarrow\mathbf{Cat}
, we will use the following notation.
{\mathcal{L}}^{A}
for
A\in\ob(\mathcal{B})
denotes the category
{\mathcal{L}}(A)
and
f^{*}
for
f\in\ob(\mathcal{B}(A,B))
denotes the functor
{\mathcal{L}}(f)
. Throughout this manuscript, we will work with two pseudofunctors,
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{R}}
. To avoid ambiguities, we will use the notation
f^{*^{\prime}}
to denote
{\mathcal{R}}(f)
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a bicategory. Let
{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}
be
\mathcal{B}
-indexed categories. The category
{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}
of dependent optics has, as objects, triplets
(X,X^{\prime})^{A}
, with
A\in\ob(\mathcal{B})
,
X\in\ob({\mathcal{L}}^{A})
,
X^{\prime}\in\ob({\mathcal{R}}^{A})
. Morphisms between
(X,X^{\prime})^{A}
and
(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}
are given by the following coend:

{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A},(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right)=\int^{f\in\mathcal{B}(A,B)}{{\mathcal{L}}^{A}}(X,f^{*}Y)\times{{\mathcal{R}}^{A}}(f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime}).

(1)

To refer to specific morphisms explicitly, we denote by
{\left\langle{l}\,|\,{r}\right\rangle}
the morphism given by
l\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{A}}(X,f^{*}Y)
and
r\in{{\mathcal{R}}^{A}}(f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime})
, and we say that it has representative
f
.

More explicitly, morphisms in
{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A},(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right)
are equivalence classes of pairs
(l,r)
with
l\colon X\rightarrow f^{*}Y
and
r\colon f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime}\rightarrow X^{\prime}
, where
f\colon A\rightarrow B
is called the representative. The equivalence relation is generated by

\left({\mathcal{L}}(m)_{Y}\circ l,\,r\right)\sim\left(l,\,r\circ{\mathcal{R}}(m)_{Y^{\prime}}\right),

with
m\colon f\Rightarrow g
,
l\colon X\rightarrow f^{*}Y
and
r\colon g^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime}\rightarrow X^{\prime}
, where
f,g\colon A\rightrightarrows B
are parallel 1-morphisms in
\mathcal{B}
.

Remark 2
.

Here and in what follows we assume that the above coend exists, either because the category
\mathcal{B}(A,B)
is small (and small colimits exist in
\mathbf{Set}
), or because we can compute it explicitly.

The generalization of optics via
\mathcal{B}
-indexed categories, rather than monoidal actions, has been proposed in

]
, where composition of optics is explained in terms of Kan extensions. Here, we will adopt a direct, explicit approach. While the chosen formalisms are different, the two definitions have been shown to be equivalent in

Let
\theta,\theta^{\prime}
encode the coherence natural transformations for
{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}
respectively. In particular, we have natural isomorphisms

\theta_{A}\colon\id_{{\mathcal{L}}^{A}}\Rightarrow\id_{A}^{*}\quad\text{ and }\quad\theta_{f,g}\colon f^{*}\circ g^{*}\Rightarrow(g\circ f)^{*}.

\theta^{\prime}_{A}
and
\theta^{\prime}_{f,g}
are defined in an analogous way.
The identity optic is defined as follows:

\id_{(X,X^{\prime})^{A}}:={\left\langle{(\theta_{A})_{X}}\,|\,{(\theta_{A}^{\prime-1})_{X^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}.

(2)

The map

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\mathcal{L}}^{B}}(Y,g^{*}Z)\times{{\mathcal{R}}^{B}}(g^{*^{\prime}}Z^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\times{{\mathcal{L}}^{A}}(X,f^{*}Y)\times{{\mathcal{R}}^{A}}(f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime})}

Theorem 1
.

{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}
is a category.

Proof.

Checking the category axioms (unity and associativity) is tedious but straightforward. Here, we denote

\id_{A}^{*^{\prime}}\Rightarrow\id_{{\mathcal{R}}^{A}}
), as well as the identity coherence law for a pseudofunctor, keeping in mind that
\mathbf{Cat}
is a strict 2-category and hence has a trivial unitor. Given an optic
{\left\langle{l}\,|\,{r}\right\rangle}\colon(X,X^{\prime})^{A}\rightarrow(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}

\displaystyle{\left\langle{l}\,|\,{r}\right\rangle}\circ\id_{(X,X^{\prime})^{A}}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{(\theta_{\id_{A},f})_{Y}\circ\id_{A}^{*}(l)\circ(\theta_{A})_{X}}\,|\,{(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X^{\prime}}\circ\id_{A}^{*^{\prime}}(r)\circ(\theta^{\prime-1}_{\id_{A},f})_{Y^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\alpha_{f,g,h})_{W}\circ(\theta_{f;g,h})_{W}\circ(\theta_{f,g})_{h^{*}W}

\displaystyle=(\theta_{f,g;h})_{W}\circ f^{*}((\theta_{g,h})_{W}),

\displaystyle(\theta^{\prime-1}_{f,g})_{h^{*^{\prime}}W^{\prime}}\circ(\theta^{\prime-1}_{f;g,h})_{W^{\prime}}\circ{\mathcal{R}}(\alpha_{f,g,h}^{-1})_{W^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=f^{*^{\prime}}((\theta^{\prime-1}_{g,h})_{W^{\prime}})\circ(\theta^{\prime-1}_{f,g;h})_{W^{\prime}},

where
\alpha_{f,g,h}
is the associator of
\mathcal{B}
.
∎

Remark 3
.

Unlike the dependent lenses case, here we generally do not have a pseudofunctor
{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
. Such a pseudofunctor would be ill-defined on morphisms in
{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}
due to the equivalence relation imposed by the coend. To obviate this issue, a possible approach worthy of future exploration would be to define a bicategory of dependent optics where, instead of identifying equivalent 1-morphisms, we add 2-morphisms between them. See
[
4
]
for a purely bicategorical approach to dependent optics.

2.1
Comparison with existent constructions

Dependent optics simultaneously generalize both mixed optics
[
21
]
and functor lenses
[
22
]
. Intuitively, mixed optics are dependent optics where the bicategory
\mathcal{B}
has a unique object, whereas functor lenses are dependent optics where
\mathcal{B}
is a 1-category and the
\mathcal{B}
-indexed category
{\mathcal{L}}
is trivial.

Proposition 1
.

Mixed optics
[
21
, Def. 6.1.1]
are a particular case of dependent optics, where the source bicategory has a unique object.

Proof.

Let us consider two categories
\mathcal{C}_{L}
and
\mathcal{C}_{R}
acted on by a monoidal category
\mathcal{M}
. We can consider the bicategory
\mathbb{B}\mathcal{M}
obtained by delooping. Explicitly,
\mathbb{B}\mathcal{M}
has a unique object
*
with endomorphism category
\mathbb{B}\mathcal{M}(*,*)=\mathcal{M}
, where composition is given by the monoidal structure of
\mathcal{M}
. Then the action of
\mathcal{M}
on another category
\mathcal{C}
induces a pseudofunctor
\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbf{Cat}
. Under this correspondence, optics for the actions
\psi_{L}\colon\mathcal{M}\rightarrow[\mathcal{C}_{L},\mathcal{C}_{L}]
and
\psi_{R}\colon\mathcal{M}\rightarrow[\mathcal{C}_{R},\mathcal{C}_{R}]
are the same as optics for the corresponding pseudofunctors
{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}\colon\mathbb{B}\mathcal{M}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Cat}
, hence they are a special case of dependent optics with
\mathcal{B}=(\mathbb{B}\mathcal{M})^{\textnormal{op}}
.
∎

Proposition 2
.

Functor lenses, as defined in
[
22
]
, are a particular case of dependent optics, where the source bicategory
\mathcal{B}
is a category and the
\mathcal{B}
-indexed category
{\mathcal{L}}
is trivial.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a 1-category. In
[
22
]
, functor lenses are defined as the Grothendieck construction of the pointwise opposite of a
\mathcal{B}
-indexed category
{\mathcal{R}}
. Let
\bullet
be the terminal
\mathcal{B}
-indexed category. Then,

{\mathbf{Lens}}_{\mathcal{R}}\simeq{\mathbf{Optic}}_{\bullet,{\mathcal{R}}}.

Indeed, objects in
{\mathbf{Optic}}_{\bullet,{\mathcal{R}}}
are simply pairs
(A,X^{\prime})
, with
X^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{R}}^{A}
, as there always is a unique object in
\bullet^{A}
. As
\mathcal{B}
has no non-trivial 2-morphisms, we have

\int^{f\in\mathcal{B}(A,B)}{{\mathcal{R}}^{A}}(f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime})\simeq\mathop{\text{
\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}
\vphantom{$\prod$}
}}_{f\in\mathcal{B}(A,B)}{{\mathcal{R}}^{A}}(f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime}).

∎

2.2
Coproducts

One fundamental motivation for dependent lenses and, more generally, dependent optics is the lack of coproducts in categories of ordinary lenses or optics. This situation is much improved in the dependent case: for instance, adding coproducts to the category of lenses leads naturally to dependent lenses
[
4
]
. In the following proposition, we show that there are general conditions to ensure that the category
{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}
has coproducts.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a bicategory with finite coproducts. Let us assume that
{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}
turn finite coproducts in
\mathcal{B}
into finite products in
\mathbf{Cat}
. Then,
{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}
has finite coproducts.

Proof.

Let
\left((X_{i},X_{i}^{\prime})^{A_{i}}\right)_{i\in I}
be a finite family of objects in
{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}
. Let
A=\mathop{\text{
\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}
\vphantom{$\prod$}
}}_{i\in I}A_{i}
. Let
\iota_{i}\colon A_{i}\hookrightarrow A
be the inclusions. Let
X\in\ob({\mathcal{L}}^{A})
and
X^{\prime}\in\ob({\mathcal{R}}^{A})
be such that, for all
i\in I
,

\iota_{i}^{*}(X)\simeq X_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\iota_{i}^{*^{\prime}}(X^{\prime})\simeq X_{i}^{\prime}.

For all
(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\in\ob\left({\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}\right)
the following holds:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A},(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right)

\displaystyle=\int^{f\in\mathcal{B}(A,B)}{\mathcal{L}}^{A}(X,f^{*}Y)\times{\mathcal{R}}^{A}(f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq\int^{(f_{i})_{i\in I}\in\prod_{i\in I}\mathcal{B}(A_{i},B)}\prod_{i\in I}{\mathcal{L}}^{A_{i}}(X_{i},f_{i}^{*}Y)\times{\mathcal{R}}^{A_{i}}(f_{i}^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime},X_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq\prod_{i\in I}{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}\left((X_{i},X_{i}^{\prime})^{A_{i}},(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right),

where the last isomorphism is Fubini’s theorem for coends, hence
(X,X^{\prime})^{A}
is the coproduct of
\left((X_{i},X_{i}^{\prime})^{A_{i}}\right)_{i\in I}
.
∎

3
Examples

Different choices of bicategories and functors give rise to different types of optics, see
[
8
]
for an overview of the monoidal case, i.e.,
\mathcal{B}=(\mathbb{B}\mathcal{M})^{\textnormal{op}}
, as in
proposition

1
. Here, we discuss
dependent lenses

[
22
]
,
dependent prisms
, and generalizations thereof. We then show how the existence of a right adjoint to a given functor can be used to construct further classes of examples of dependent optics. More examples of dependent optics, such as polynomial optics, are described in
[
18
]
.

3.1
Dependent lenses

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a finitely complete category. Let
{\mathbf{Span}}_{\mathcal{C}}
be its bicategory of spans. Let
\mathcal{C}/{\text{\textendash}}
be the
{\mathbf{Span}}_{\mathcal{C}}
-indexed category of slices. More explicitly,

\mathcal{C}/{\text{\textendash}}\colon{\mathbf{Span}}_{\mathcal{C}}^{\textnormal{op}}\rightarrow\mathbf{Cat}

is a pseudofunctor that associates to each object
A\in\ob(\mathcal{C})
the slice category
\mathcal{C}/A
. Functoriality is given by pulling back and then pushing forward along the legs of the span.
We define the category of dependent lenses as follows:

{\mathbf{DLens}}_{\mathcal{C}}:={\mathbf{Optic}}_{\mathcal{C}/{\text{\textendash}},\mathcal{C}/{\text{\textendash}}}.

Objects in
{\mathbf{DLens}}_{\mathcal{C}}
are cospans
X\rightarrow A\leftarrow X^{\prime}
. Morphisms between two cospans
X\rightarrow A\leftarrow X^{\prime}
and
Y\rightarrow B\leftarrow Y^{\prime}
are given by

\int^{M\in\mathcal{C}/(A\times B)}{\mathcal{C}/A}(X,M\times_{B}Y)\times{\mathcal{C}/A}(M\times_{B}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime}).

(4)

Equation

4
can be visualized as follows. A class of homomorphisms in
{\mathbf{DLens}}_{\mathcal{C}}
with representative
A\leftarrow M\rightarrow B
is given by a pair of dotted arrows that make the following diagram commute.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M\times_{B}Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M\times_{B}Y^{\prime}}

Morphisms in
{\mathbf{DLens}}_{\mathcal{C}}
can be computed explicitly:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{DLens}}_{\mathcal{C}}\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A},(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right)

\displaystyle=\int^{M\in\mathcal{C}/(A\times B)}{\mathcal{C}/A}(X,M\times_{B}Y)\times{\mathcal{C}/A}(M\times_{B}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime})

(5)

\displaystyle\simeq\int^{M\in\mathcal{C}/(A\times B)}\mathop{\text{
\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}
\vphantom{$\prod$}
}}_{X\rightarrow Y}{\mathcal{C}/(A\times B)}(X,M)\times{\mathcal{C}/A}(M\times_{B}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq\mathop{\text{
\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}
\vphantom{$\prod$}
}}_{X\rightarrow Y}\int^{M\in\mathcal{C}/(A\times B)}{\mathcal{C}/(A\times B)}(X,M)\times{\mathcal{C}/A}(M\times_{B}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq\mathop{\text{
\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}
\vphantom{$\prod$}
}}_{X\rightarrow Y}{\mathcal{C}/A}(X\times_{B}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime}),

where the last isomorphism follows from the Yoneda reduction lemma
[
21
, Lm 1.2.2]
.

Even though this category is equivalent to the definition of dependent lenses via the Grothendieck construction in
[
22
]
, we believe it can have independent practical value. Encoding dependent lenses as maps

X\rightarrow M\times_{B}Y\quad\text{ and }\quad M\times_{B}Y^{\prime}\rightarrow X^{\prime}

can lead to a more efficient implementation of, for instance, reverse-mode automatic differentiation (as done in the
Julia
library
\mathrm{Diffractor}

[
9
]
), where the representative
M
is optimized to contain precisely the information about the input that is required to compute the backward map. Features of the input that are not needed can be discarded, and quantities computed in the forward map can be stored in
M
if they are useful for the backward map.

The documentation of the
\mathrm{Diffractor}
library
[
9
]
hints at the need for dependent optics. Indeed, a key motivation for this work was to build a rigorous dependently-typed framework for bidirectional data transformations that would allow for reverse-mode automatic differentiation with an explicit notion of representative.
However, to formalize the difference between our construction of dependent lenses and the one based on functor lenses, we would need to define the
bicategory
of dependent optics, where all the information about the representative is preserved (cp.
remark

3
).

Unlike lenses, categories of dependent lenses admit finite coproducts, provided that the base category is
lextensive

[
7
, Sect. 4.4]
.

Lemma 1
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a lextensive category, then the inclusion
\mathcal{C}\hookrightarrow{\mathbf{Span}}_{\mathcal{C}}
preserves coproducts.

Proof.

Let
A=\mathop{\text{
\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}
\vphantom{$\prod$}
}}_{i\in I}A_{i}
be a coproduct in
\mathcal{C}
. Then, for all
B\in\ob(\mathcal{C})
,

{\mathbf{Span}}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(A,B\right)=\mathcal{C}/(A\times B)\simeq\mathcal{C}/\mathop{\text{
\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}
\vphantom{$\prod$}
}}_{i\in I}\left(A_{i}\times B\right)\simeq\prod_{i\in I}\mathcal{C}/\left(A_{i}\times B\right)=\prod_{i\in I}{\mathbf{Span}}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(A_{i},B\right),

therefore
A
is the coproduct of
(A_{i})_{i\in I}
in
{\mathbf{Span}}_{\mathcal{C}}
.
∎

Proposition 4
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a lextensive category, then
{\mathbf{DLens}}_{\mathcal{C}}
has finite coproducts.

Proof.

By
lemma

1
,
{\mathbf{Span}}_{\mathcal{C}}
has finite coproducts, given by coproducts in
\mathcal{C}
. It is straightforward to show that
\mathcal{C}/{\text{\textendash}}
turns coproducts into products, as

\mathcal{C}/A=\mathcal{C}/\mathop{\text{
\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}
\vphantom{$\prod$}
}}_{i\in I}A_{i}\simeq\prod_{i\in I}\mathcal{C}/A_{i}.

Thanks to
proposition

3
,
{\mathbf{DLens}}_{\mathcal{C}}
has finite coproducts.
∎

3.2
Dependent monoidal lenses

The construction in
section

3.1
can be generalized to a symmetric monoidal category
(\mathcal{C},\otimes)
with reflexive equalizers that are preserved by the tensor product. This is analogous to the approach taken in
[
22
]
to generalize lenses to symmetric monoidal categories via commutative comonoids.

Let
B
be a comonoidal object in
\mathcal{C}
. Given a right
B
-comodule
M
and a left
B
-comodule
N
, we can define their tensor product over
B
as the following equalizer:

M\otimes_{B}N:=\mathrm{eq}(M\otimes N\rightrightarrows M\otimes B\otimes N).

The category of commutative comonoids in
\ob(\mathcal{C})
, denoted
{\mathbf{CComon}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}
, has finite limits: the pullback of two
B
-coalgebras
Y_{1},Y_{2}
is isomorphic to the tensor product
Y_{1}\otimes_{B}Y_{2}
(see
[
12
, C1.1 Lm. 1.1.8]
and subsequent discussion for the dual statement). Let
{\mathbf{CCoalg}}_{({\text{\textendash}})}
and
{\mathbf{Comod}}_{({\text{\textendash}})}
be the
{\mathbf{Span}}_{{\mathbf{CComon}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}}
-indexed categories of commutative coalgebras and comodules respectively, where functoriality is given by extension and restriction of scalars. We define the category of
dependent monoidal lenses
as follows:

{\mathbf{DLens}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}:={\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathbf{CCoalg}}_{({\text{\textendash}})},{\mathbf{Comod}}_{({\text{\textendash}})}}.

A computation analogous to the one in
eq.

5
yields the following explicit formula:

{\mathbf{DLens}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A},(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right)\simeq\mathop{\text{
\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}
\vphantom{$\prod$}
}}_{X\rightarrow Y}{\mathbf{Comod}}_{A}(X\otimes_{B}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime}),

where the morphism
X\rightarrow Y
varies among comonoid homomorphisms.

Proposition

4
can be generalized to the monoidal case, establishing sufficient conditions for the existence of finite coproducts in
{\mathbf{DLens}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}
. In the following proposition, we rely on the fact that, whenever
\mathcal{C}
has finite coproducts, the forgetful functor
{\mathbf{CMon}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
creates coproducts in
{\mathbf{CMon}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}
. See the proof of
[
16
, Prop. 1.2.14]
for the dual statement, which concerns limits of commutative monoids rather than colimits of commutative comonoids.

Proposition 5
.

Let
(\mathcal{C},\otimes)
be a symmetric monoidal category, with reflexive equalizers that are preserved by the tensor product. Let us assume that
\mathcal{C}
has finite coproducts, and that for all finite coproduct of commutative comonoids
A=\mathop{\text{
\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}
\vphantom{$\prod$}
}}_{i\in I}A_{i}
, the map

{\mathbf{Comod}}_{A}\rightarrow\prod_{i\in I}{\mathbf{Comod}}_{A_{i}},\text{ given by }M\mapsto(M\otimes_{A}A_{i})_{i\in I},

(6)

is an equivalence of categories. Then,
{\mathbf{DLens}}_{\mathcal{C}}
has finite coproducts.

Proof.

The equivalence in
eq.

6
is monoidal, hence the functor

{\mathbf{CCoalg}}_{A}\rightarrow\prod_{i\in I}{\mathbf{CCoalg}}_{A_{i}},\text{ given by }X\mapsto(X\otimes_{A}A_{i})_{i\in I},

(7)

is an equivalence. As
{\mathbf{CCoalg}}_{({\text{\textendash}})}={\mathbf{CComon}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}/{\text{\textendash}}
, the category
{\mathbf{CComon}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}
is lextensive. Indeed, in the presence of pullbacks along coproduct injections,
eq.

7
is a condition equivalent to extensivity, as shown in
[
15
, Prop. 1.3]
. Thanks to
lemma

1
, the category
{\mathbf{Span}}_{{\mathbf{CComon}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}}
has finite coproducts, given by coproducts in
\mathcal{C}
. It follows from
eqs.

6
and
7
that the functors
{\mathbf{Comod}}_{({\text{\textendash}})}
and
{\mathbf{CCoalg}}_{({\text{\textendash}})}
turn finite coproducts into products. Thanks to
proposition

3
,
{\mathbf{DLens}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}
has finite coproducts.
∎

3.3
Dependent (monoidal) prisms

Dependent prisms are dual to dependent lenses.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a finitely cocomplete category. Let
{\mathbf{Cospan}}_{\mathcal{C}}={\mathbf{Span}}_{\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{{op}}}}
be its bicategory of cospans. Let
{\text{\textendash}}/\mathcal{C}
be the
{\mathbf{Cospan}}_{\mathcal{C}}
-indexed category of coslices. We define the category of
dependent prisms
as follows:

{\mathbf{DPrism}}_{\mathcal{C}}:={\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\text{\textendash}}/\mathcal{C},{\text{\textendash}}/\mathcal{C}}.

Objects are given by spans
X\leftarrow A\rightarrow X^{\prime}
.
Morphisms between two spans
X\leftarrow A\rightarrow X^{\prime}
and
Y\leftarrow B\rightarrow Y^{\prime}
are given by

\int^{M\in(A\sqcup B)/\mathcal{C}}A/\mathcal{C}(X,M\sqcup_{B}Y)\times A/\mathcal{C}(M\sqcup_{B}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime}).

(8)

As is the case for dependent lenses, the coend in
eq.

8
can be computed explicitly:

{\mathbf{DPrism}}_{\mathcal{C}}\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A},(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right)\simeq\mathop{\text{
\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}
\vphantom{$\prod$}
}}_{Y^{\prime}\rightarrow X^{\prime}}{A/\mathcal{C}}(X,X^{\prime}\sqcup_{B}Y).

Dependent monoidal prisms are dual to dependent monoidal lenses. Given a symmetric monoidal category
(\mathcal{C},\otimes)
with reflexive coequalizers that are preserved by the tensor product, let
{\mathbf{CMon}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}
be the category of commutative monoids in
\ob(\mathcal{C})
. Let
{\mathbf{CAlg}}_{({\text{\textendash}})}
and
{\mathbf{Mod}}_{({\text{\textendash}})}
be the
{\mathbf{Cospan}}_{{\mathbf{CMon}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}}
-indexed categories of commutative algebras and modules respectively, where functoriality is given by extension and restriction of scalars. We define the category of
dependent monoidal prisms
as follows:

{\mathbf{DPrism}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}:={\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathbf{Mod}}_{({\text{\textendash}})},{\mathbf{CAlg}}_{({\text{\textendash}})}}.

Morphisms can be computed via the explicit formula

{\mathbf{DPrism}}_{\mathcal{C},\otimes}\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A},(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right)\simeq\mathop{\text{
\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}
\vphantom{$\prod$}
}}_{Y^{\prime}\rightarrow X^{\prime}}{A/\mathcal{C}}(X,X^{\prime}\otimes_{B}Y),

where the morphism
Y^{\prime}\rightarrow X^{\prime}
varies among monoid homomorphisms.

3.4
Closed dependent optics

Using a technique analogous to
coalgebraic optics

[
21
]
, it is sometimes possible to explicitly compute the coend in the
{\mathbf{Optic}}
category using a right adjoint technique.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a bicategory, and
{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}
be
\mathcal{B}
-indexed categories.
We say that
{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}
is a category of
closed dependent optics
if, for any
A,B\in\ob(\mathcal{B})
and
Y^{\prime}\in\ob({\mathcal{L}}(B))
, the functor
({\text{\textendash}})^{*}Y^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{B}(A,B)\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}^{A}
has a right adjoint
Y^{\prime}\triangleright{\text{\textendash}}\colon{\mathcal{R}}^{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(A,B)
.
Whenever that is the case,
eq.

1
can be greatly simplified.

\displaystyle\int^{f\in\mathcal{B}(A,B)}{\mathcal{L}}^{A}(X,f^{*}Y)\times{\mathcal{R}}^{A}(f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq\int^{f\in\mathcal{B}(A,B)}{\mathcal{L}}^{A}(X,f^{*}Y)\times\mathcal{B}(f,Y^{\prime}\triangleright X^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq{\mathcal{L}}^{A}(X,(Y^{\prime}\triangleright X^{\prime})^{*}Y).

A possible application of this technique is based on the bicategory of bimodules
[
2
, Ex. 2.5]

\mathbf{Bimod}
and on the
\mathbf{Bimod}
-indexed category
{\mathbf{Mod}}_{({\text{\textendash}})}
. There,
Y^{\prime}\triangleright X^{\prime}=[Y^{\prime},X^{\prime}]
, considered as an
(A,B)
-bimodule, hence morphisms in
{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathbf{Mod}}_{({\text{\textendash}})},{\mathbf{Mod}}_{({\text{\textendash}})}}
can be computed explicitly:

{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathbf{Mod}}_{({\text{\textendash}})},{\mathbf{Mod}}_{({\text{\textendash}})}}\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A},(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right)={\mathbf{Mod}}_{A}(X,[Y^{\prime},X^{\prime}]\otimes_{B}Y).

4
Tambara representations

Tambara modules
[
20
]
can be useful to define an
interface
for optics that does not depend on a choice of representative. Here, we adapt the notion of
generalized Tambara module
from
[
8
]
to the dependent case, and we generalize it to an arbitrary target category. For simplicity of notation, throughout this section we fix a bicategory
\mathcal{B}
and two
\mathcal{B}
-indexed categories
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{R}}
(with coherence isomorphisms
\theta,\theta^{\prime}
respectively), and we write
{\mathbf{Optic}}
instead of
{\mathbf{Optic}}_{{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{R}}}
.

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a category. A
\mathcal{D}
-valued Tambara representation consists of

•

a functor
P^{A}\colon\left({\mathcal{L}}^{A}\right)^{\textnormal{op}}\times{\mathcal{R}}^{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
, for each object
A
in
\mathcal{B}
,

•

a natural transformation
\zeta_{f}\colon P^{B}({\text{\textendash}},{\text{=}})\Rightarrow P^{A}(f^{*}{\text{\textendash}},f^{*^{\prime}}{\text{=}})
, for each morphism
f\colon A\rightarrow B
in
\mathcal{B}
,

where
\zeta_{f}
is extranatural in
f
and satisfies the equations

P^{A}(\theta_{A},\theta_{A}^{\prime{-1}})\circ\zeta_{\id_{A}}=\id_{P^{A}}\quad\text{ and }\quad P^{A}(\theta_{f,g},\theta_{f,g}^{\prime{-1}})\circ\zeta_{g\circ f}=(\zeta_{f})_{g^{*}({\text{\textendash}}),g^{*^{\prime}}({\text{=}})}\circ\zeta_{g},

for all
A,B,C\in\ob(\mathcal{B})
,
f\colon A\rightarrow B
, and
g\colon B\rightarrow C
.

Remark 4
.

As the target category is arbitrary,
P
does not correspond to a module over an enriched category, hence we find the name representation more appropriate.

In more graphical terms, when
\mathcal{D}=\mathbf{Set}
and thus
P^{A},P^{B}
are profunctors, the relationship between
P^{A}
,
P^{B}
, and
\zeta_{f}
can be visualized via the following 2-cell in
\mathbf{Prof}
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{L}}^{B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{R}}^{B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{L}}^{A}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{R}}^{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces|}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P^{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{*^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces|}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P^{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\zeta_{f}}

\mathbf{Set}
-valued Tambara representations can therefore be thought of as
lax
\mathcal{B}
-indexed profunctors
.

Definition 4
.

Morphisms between Tambara representations
(P,\zeta),(Q,\zeta^{\prime})
are natural transformations
\eta^{A}\colon P^{A}\Rightarrow Q^{A}
satisfying

\eta^{A}_{f^{*}({\text{\textendash}}),f^{*^{\prime}}({\text{=}})}\circ\zeta_{f}=\zeta^{\prime}_{f}\circ\eta^{B}.

(9)

\mathcal{D}
-valued Tambara representations and their morphisms form a category, which we denote
{\mathbf{Tamb}}_{\mathcal{D}}
.

Functors from
{\mathbf{Optic}}^{\textnormal{op}}
to an arbitrary category can be described explicitly, thanks to
theorem

2
. The rest of the section is devoted to proving that result, via some intermediate steps, and exploring its consequences.

4.1
The universal Tambara representation

We aim to establish that all Tambara representations can be expressed as a composition of a functor with a
universal Tambara representation

\iota^{\textnormal{op}}
. Here, we will define
\iota
and prove that
\iota^{\textnormal{op}}
is a Tambara representation. In
section

4.2
, we will show universality.

Definition 5
.

Let
A\in\ob(\mathcal{B})
. The functor
\iota^{A}\colon{\mathcal{L}}^{A}\times({\mathcal{R}}^{A})^{\textnormal{op}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{Optic}}
is defined as follows. Given an object
(X,X^{\prime})
,
\iota^{A}(X,X^{\prime}):=(X,X^{\prime})^{A}
. Given a morphism

(l,r)\colon(X_{0},X_{0}^{\prime})\rightarrow(X_{1},X_{1}^{\prime}),

where
l\colon X_{0}\rightarrow X_{1}
and
r\colon X_{1}^{\prime}\rightarrow X_{0}^{\prime}
, we define
\iota^{A}(l,r)
as the optic

{\left\langle{(\theta_{A})_{X_{1}}\circ l}\,|\,{r\circ(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X_{1}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}\colon(X_{0},X_{0}^{\prime})^{A}\rightarrow(X_{1},X_{1}^{\prime})^{A}

with representative
\id_{A}
.

The following lemmas will make it much easier to do computations with
\iota
and will allow us to prove that
\iota^{A}
is indeed a functor and that
\iota^{\textnormal{op}}
is a Tambara representation.

Lemma 2
.

Let
(X_{0},X_{0}^{\prime})^{A},(X_{1},X_{1}^{\prime})^{A}\in\ob({\mathbf{Optic}})
. Let
l_{1}\colon X_{0}\rightarrow X_{1}
and
r_{1}\colon X_{1}^{\prime}\rightarrow X_{0}^{\prime}
. Then, for all optic
{\left\langle{l_{2}}\,|\,{r_{2}}\right\rangle}
with domain
(X_{1},X_{1}^{\prime})^{A}
,

{\left\langle{l_{2}}\,|\,{r_{2}}\right\rangle}\circ\iota^{A}(l_{1},r_{1})={\left\langle{l_{2}\circ l_{1}}\,|\,{r_{1}\circ r_{2}}\right\rangle}.

(10)

Proof.

We use twice the fact that
\theta_{A}
(resp.
\theta^{\prime-1}_{A}
) is a natural transformation
\id_{{\mathcal{L}}^{A}}\Rightarrow\id_{A}^{*}
(resp.
\id_{A}^{*^{\prime}}\Rightarrow\id_{{\mathcal{R}}^{A}}
). Specifically,

\displaystyle\id_{A}^{*}(l_{2})\circ(\theta_{A})_{X_{1}}\circ l_{1}=(\theta_{A})_{X_{2}}\circ l_{2}\circ l_{1}=\id_{A}^{*}(l_{2}\circ l_{1})\circ(\theta_{A})_{X_{0}}

\displaystyle r_{1}\circ(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X_{1}^{\prime}}\circ\id_{A}^{*^{\prime}}(r_{2})=r_{1}\circ r_{2}\circ(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X_{2}^{\prime}}=(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X_{0}^{\prime}}\circ\id_{A}^{*^{\prime}}(r_{1}\circ r_{2}).

As a consequence,

\displaystyle{\left\langle{l_{2}}\,|\,{r_{2}}\right\rangle}\circ\iota^{A}(l_{1},r_{1})

\displaystyle={\left\langle{l_{2}}\,|\,{r_{2}}\right\rangle}\circ{\left\langle{(\theta_{A})_{X_{1}}\circ l_{1}}\,|\,{r_{1}\circ(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})}\right\rangle}_{X_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{l_{2}\circ l_{1}}\,|\,{r_{1}\circ r_{2}}\right\rangle}\circ{\left\langle{(\theta_{A})_{X_{0}}}\,|\,{(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X_{0}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{l_{2}\circ l_{1}}\,|\,{r_{1}\circ r_{2}}\right\rangle}.

∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
(Y_{1},Y_{1}^{\prime})^{B},(Y_{2},Y_{2}^{\prime})^{B}\in\ob({\mathbf{Optic}})
. Let
l_{2}\colon Y_{1}\rightarrow Y_{2}
and
r_{2}\colon Y_{2}^{\prime}\rightarrow Y_{1}^{\prime}
. Then, for all optic
{\left\langle{l_{1}}\,|\,{r_{1}}\right\rangle}
with codomain
(Y_{1},Y_{1}^{\prime})^{B}
and representative
f
,

\iota^{B}(l_{2},r_{2})\circ{\left\langle{l_{1}}\,|\,{r_{1}}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{f^{*}(l_{2})\circ l_{1}}\,|\,{r_{1}\circ f^{*^{\prime}}(r_{2})}\right\rangle}.

(11)

Proof.

As
f^{*}
and
f^{*^{\prime}}
are functors,
eq.

3
implies that

\displaystyle\iota^{B}(l_{2},r_{2})\circ{\left\langle{l_{1}}\,|\,{r_{1}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{(\theta_{B})_{Y_{2}}\circ l_{2}}\,|\,{r_{2}\circ(\theta^{\prime-1}_{B})_{Y_{2}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}\circ{\left\langle{l_{1}}\,|\,{r_{1}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{(\theta_{B})_{Y_{2}}}\,|\,{(\theta^{\prime-1}_{B})_{Y_{2}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}\circ{\left\langle{f^{*}(l_{2})\circ l_{1}}\,|\,{r_{1}\circ f^{*^{\prime}}(r_{2})}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{f^{*}(l_{2})\circ l_{1}}\,|\,{r_{1}\circ f^{*^{\prime}}(r_{2})}\right\rangle}.

∎

Proposition 6
.

For all
A\in\ob(\mathcal{B})
,
\iota^{A}
is a functor.

Proof.

We must verify that
\iota^{A}
preserves identity and composition of morphisms. Preservation of identity is straightforward, as

\iota^{A}(\id_{X},\id_{X^{\prime}})={\left\langle{(\theta_{A})_{X}}\,|\,{(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}=\id_{(X,X^{\prime})^{A}}.

Let us now consider morphisms

X_{0}\xrightarrow{l_{1}}X_{1}\xrightarrow{l_{2}}X_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad X_{2}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{r_{2}}X_{1}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{r_{1}}X_{0}^{\prime}.

Then, using
eq.

10
,

\displaystyle\iota^{A}(l_{2},r_{2})\circ\iota^{A}(l_{1},r_{1})

\displaystyle={\left\langle{(\theta_{A})_{X_{2}}\circ l_{2}}\,|\,{r_{2}\circ(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X_{2}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}\circ\iota^{A}(l_{1},r_{1})

\displaystyle={\left\langle{(\theta_{A})_{X_{2}}\circ l_{2}\circ l_{1}}\,|\,{r_{1}\circ r_{2}\circ(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X_{2}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\iota^{A}(l_{2}\circ l_{1},r_{2}\circ r_{1}).

∎

Proposition 7
.

\iota^{\textnormal{op}}
is a
{\mathbf{Optic}}^{\textnormal{op}}
-valued Tambara representation, whose associated natural transformations are
{\left\langle{\id_{f^{*}({\text{\textendash}})}}\,|\,{\id_{f^{*^{\prime}}({\text{=}})}}\right\rangle}^{\textnormal{op}}
(with representative
f
).

Proof.

By inverting all morphisms in
definition

3
, we work with the category
{\mathbf{Optic}}
rather than
{\mathbf{Optic}}^{\textnormal{op}}
. Let
f\colon A\rightarrow B
. First, we need to ensure that

{\left\langle{\id_{f^{*}({\text{\textendash}})}}\,|\,{\id_{f^{*^{\prime}}({\text{=}})}}\right\rangle}\colon\iota^{A}(f^{*}({\text{\textendash}}),f^{*^{\prime}}({\text{=}}))\Rightarrow\iota^{B}({\text{\textendash}},{\text{=}})

is a natural transformation. Let us consider morphisms
l\colon Y_{0}\rightarrow Y_{1}
and
r\colon Y_{1}^{\prime}\rightarrow Y_{0}^{\prime}
.
Then, by
eqs.

11
and
10
,

\iota^{B}(l,r)\circ{\left\langle{\id_{f^{*}Y_{0}}}\,|\,{\id_{f^{*^{\prime}}Y_{0}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{f^{*}(l)}\,|\,{f^{*^{\prime}}(r)}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{\id_{f^{*}Y_{1}}}\,|\,{\id_{f^{*^{\prime}}Y_{1}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}\circ\iota^{A}(f^{*}(l),f^{*^{\prime}}(r)).

Next, we must show that
\zeta_{f}
is extranatural in
f
. Let us consider
f,g\colon A\rightrightarrows B
and
m\colon f\Rightarrow g
. The following diagram commutes for all
Y\in\ob({\mathcal{L}}^{B})
,
Y^{\prime}\in\ob({\mathcal{R}}^{B})
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota^{A}(f^{*}Y,g^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota^{A}(f^{*}Y,f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota^{A}(g^{*}Y,g^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota^{B}(Y,Y^{\prime})}

This can be show by direct computation, using
eq.

10
:

\displaystyle{\left\langle{\id_{g^{*}Y}}\,|\,{\id_{g^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}\circ\iota^{A}({\mathcal{L}}(m)_{Y},\id_{g^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime}})

\displaystyle={\left\langle{{\mathcal{L}}(m)_{Y}}\,|\,{\id_{g^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{\id_{f^{*}Y}}\,|\,{{\mathcal{R}}(m)_{Y^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{\id_{f^{*}Y}}\,|\,{\id_{f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}\circ\iota^{A}(\id_{f^{*}Y},{\mathcal{R}}(m)_{Y^{\prime}}).

Finally, we need to show the coherence laws for Tambara representations. The identity law is straightforward, as

{\left\langle{\id_{\id_{A}^{*}X}}\,|\,{\id_{\id_{A}^{*^{\prime}}X^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}\circ\iota^{A}((\theta_{A})_{X},(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X^{\prime}})={\left\langle{(\theta_{A})_{X}}\,|\,{(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}=\id_{(X,X^{\prime})^{A}}.

Thanks to
eq.

10

{\left\langle{\id_{(g\circ f)^{*}Z}}\,|\,{\id_{(g\circ f)^{*^{\prime}}Z^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}\circ\iota^{A}((\theta_{f,g})_{Z},(\theta^{\prime-1}_{f,g})_{Z^{\prime}})={\left\langle{(\theta_{f,g})_{Z}}\,|\,{(\theta^{\prime-1}_{f,g})_{Z^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}.

Thanks to
eq.

3
,

{\left\langle{\id_{g^{*}Z}}\,|\,{\id_{g^{*^{\prime}}Z^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}\circ{\left\langle{\id_{f^{*}(g^{*}Z)}}\,|\,{\id_{f^{*^{\prime}}(g^{*^{\prime}}Z^{\prime})}}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{(\theta_{f,g})_{Z}}\,|\,{(\theta^{\prime-1}_{f,g})_{Z^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}.

Hence, the composition law holds and
\iota
is a
{\mathbf{Optic}}^{\textnormal{op}}
-valued Tambara representation.
∎

4.2
Tambara encoding

In this section, we establish that there is a functor
[{\mathbf{Optic}}^{\textnormal{op}},\mathcal{D}]\rightarrow{\mathbf{Tamb}}_{\mathcal{D}}
given by composition with the universal Tambara representation
\iota
. Furthermore, this functor is an isomorphism of categories, which we will show in
theorem

2
. This isomorphism will allow us to recover a classical
end formula
linking Tambara representations and optics. We start by showing that composition of a functor and a Tambara representation yields a Tambara representation.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
be arbitrary categories. There is a functor
[\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}]\times{\mathbf{Tamb}}_{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{Tamb}}_{\mathcal{D}}
given by composition.

Proof.

All conditions for Tambara representations are preserved by a functorial transformation. Given natural transformation
\mu\colon F\Rightarrow G
and a morphism of Tambara representations
\eta\colon P\Rightarrow Q
, it is straightforward to verify that the horizontal composition of
\mu
and
\eta
is a morphism of Tambara representations
F\circ P\Rightarrow G\circ Q
:

\displaystyle\mu_{Q^{A}(X,X^{\prime})}\circ F(\eta^{A}_{X,X^{\prime}})\circ F((\zeta_{f})_{Y,Y^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mu_{Q^{A}(X,X^{\prime})}\circ F(\eta^{A}_{X,X^{\prime}}\circ(\zeta_{f})_{Y,Y^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mu_{Q^{A}(X,X^{\prime})}\circ F((\zeta^{\prime}_{f})_{Y,Y^{\prime}}\circ\eta^{B}_{Y,Y^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=G((\zeta^{\prime}_{f})_{Y,Y^{\prime}}\circ\eta^{B}_{Y,Y^{\prime}})\circ\mu_{P^{B}(Y,Y^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=G((\zeta^{\prime}_{f})_{Y,Y^{\prime}})\circ G(\eta^{B}_{Y,Y^{\prime}})\circ\mu_{P^{B}(Y,Y^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=G((\zeta^{\prime}_{f})_{Y,Y^{\prime}})\circ\mu_{Q^{B}(Y,Y^{\prime})}\circ F(\eta^{B}_{Y,Y^{\prime}}).

∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a category. Let
\iota^{\textnormal{op}}
be the universal Tambara representation. Then the functor

{\text{\textendash}}\circ\iota^{\textnormal{op}}\colon[{\mathbf{Optic}}^{\textnormal{op}},\mathcal{D}]\rightarrow{\mathbf{Tamb}}_{\mathcal{D}}

is an isomorphism of categories.

Proof.

Let
P
be a
\mathcal{D}
-valued Tambara representation, with associated natural transformations
\zeta_{f}
. Let us define
\tilde{P}\colon{\mathbf{Optic}}^{\textnormal{op}}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
as follows. On objects,

\tilde{P}\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A}\right)=P^{A}(X,X^{\prime}).

To extend
\tilde{P}
to morphisms, we proceed as follows. As
\zeta_{f}
is extranatural in
f
, the map

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{A}(X,f^{*}Y)\times{\mathcal{R}}^{A}(f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime},X^{\prime})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{D}\left(P\left((Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right),P\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A}\right)\right)

\displaystyle{\left\langle{l}\,|\,{r}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\mapsto P^{A}(l,r)\circ(\zeta_{f})_{Y,Y^{\prime}}

induces a map

\tilde{P}\colon{\mathbf{Optic}}\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A},(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}\left(P\left((Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right),P\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A}\right)\right).

Preservation of identity and composition follows from the coherence laws of
definition

3
, hence
\tilde{P}
is a functor.

It is straightforward to verify that
\tilde{P}\circ\iota^{\textnormal{op}}=P
as Tambara representations. On objects,

(\tilde{P}\circ\iota^{\textnormal{op}})^{A}(X,X^{\prime})=\tilde{P}((X,X^{\prime})^{A})=P^{A}(X,X^{\prime}).

On morphisms, given
l\colon X_{0}\rightarrow X_{1}
and
r\colon X_{1}^{\prime}\rightarrow X_{0}^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle(\tilde{P}\circ\iota^{\textnormal{op}})^{A}(l,r)

\displaystyle=\tilde{P}({\left\langle{(\theta_{A})_{X_{1}}\circ l}\,|\,{r\circ(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X_{1}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle})

\displaystyle=P((\theta_{A})_{X_{1}}\circ l,r\circ(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X_{1}^{\prime}})\circ(\zeta_{\id_{A}})_{X_{1},X_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=P(l,r)\circ P((\theta_{A})_{X_{1}},(\theta^{\prime-1}_{A})_{X_{1}^{\prime}})\circ(\zeta_{\id_{A}})_{X_{1},X_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=P(l,r).

Finally,

\tilde{P}({\left\langle{\id_{f^{*}Y}}\,|\,{\id_{f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime}}}\right\rangle})=P^{A}(\id_{f^{*}Y},\id_{f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime}})\circ(\zeta_{f})_{Y,Y^{\prime}}=(\zeta_{f})_{Y,Y^{\prime}},

hence
\tilde{P}\circ\iota^{\textnormal{op}}=P
as Tambara representations, so
{\text{\textendash}}\circ\iota^{\textnormal{op}}
is surjective on objects.

Let
P,Q
be Tambara representations, with associated natural transformation families
\zeta,\zeta^{\prime}
respectively. Let
\eta\colon P\Rightarrow Q
be a morphism of Tambara representations. Then, we can define a natural transformation

\tilde{\eta}\colon\tilde{P}\Rightarrow\tilde{Q}\quad\text{ given by }\quad\tilde{\eta}_{(X,X^{\prime})^{A}}:=\eta^{A}_{X,X^{\prime}}.

Naturality can be verified via a direct computation:

\displaystyle Q({\left\langle{l}\,|\,{r}\right\rangle})\circ\tilde{\eta}_{(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}}

\displaystyle=Q^{A}(l,r)\circ(\zeta^{\prime}_{f})_{Y,Y^{\prime}}\circ\eta^{B}_{Y,Y^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=Q^{A}(l,r)\circ\eta^{A}_{f^{*}Y,f^{*^{\prime}}Y^{\prime}}\circ(\zeta_{f})_{Y,Y^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\eta^{A}_{X,X^{\prime}}\circ P^{A}(l,r)\circ(\zeta_{f})_{Y,Y^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\eta}_{(X,X^{\prime})^{A}}\circ P({\left\langle{l}\,|\,{r}\right\rangle}).

Here, we have used
eq.

9
and the fact that
\eta^{A}
is a natural transformation from
P^{A}
to
Q^{A}
. As
\tilde{\eta}_{\iota^{A}(X,X^{\prime})}=\tilde{\eta}_{(X,X^{\prime})^{A}}=\eta^{A}_{X,X^{\prime}}
, the functor
{\text{\textendash}}\circ\iota^{\textnormal{op}}
is full. Finally, if for all
A\in\ob(\mathcal{B}),X\in\ob({\mathcal{L}}^{A}),X^{\prime}\in\ob({\mathcal{R}}^{A})
,

\eta^{A}_{X,X^{\prime}}=\mu_{\iota^{A}(X,X^{\prime})}

with
\mu\colon\tilde{P}\Rightarrow\tilde{Q}
, then
\mu=\tilde{\eta}
. Hence, the functor
{\text{\textendash}}\circ\iota^{\textnormal{op}}
is faithful.
∎

Theorem

2
has wide practical applications, as it implies that a Tambara representation
P
can be used as an
interface
for optics. In other words, an optic
o\in{\mathbf{Optic}}\left((X,X^{\prime})^{A},(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}\right)
can be encoded as a family of morphisms

P^{B}(Y,Y^{\prime})\rightarrow P^{A}(X,X^{\prime}),

where
P
varies among
\mathcal{D}
-valued Tambara representations. This encoding has several practical advantages. It does not depend on a choice of representative, it simplifies composition of optics—replacing the rule in
eq.

3
with standard function composition—and it can be used to compose optics of different types, as discussed in
[
8
]
. Furthermore, the dependent version of a classical result—the
profunctor representation theorem

[
8
, Thm. 4.14]
—is a direct consequence of
theorem

2
, specialized to the case
\mathcal{D}=\mathbf{Set}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a locally small category, and let
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
denote its category of presheaves. Then, for all
S,T\in\ob(\mathcal{C})
,

\mathcal{C}(S,T)\simeq\int_{F\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}}\mathbf{Set}(F(T),F(S)).

(12)

Proof.

Let us consider the representable presheaf
\mathcal{C}({\text{\textendash}},T)
. Applying the Yoneda reduction lemma
[
21
, Lm. 1.2.2]
to the evaluation-at-
S
functor
\hat{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow\mathbf{Set}
, we obtain

\mathcal{C}(S,T)=\mathcal{C}({\text{\textendash}},T)(S)\simeq\int_{F\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}}\mathbf{Set}(\hat{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{C}({\text{\textendash}},T),F),F(S))\simeq\int_{F\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}}\mathbf{Set}(F(T),F(S)),

where in the last step we have used the Yoneda lemma to compute
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{C}({\text{\textendash}},T),F)
.
∎

Theorem 3
.

[
5
, Thm. 3.5]

Let
(X,X^{\prime})^{A}
and
(Y,Y^{\prime})^{B}
be objects in

By

. Hence,

, with

,

∎

5
Discussion

In this work, we developed a theory of
dependent optics
that simultaneously generalizes (mixed) optics and functor lenses. Natural examples of this construction arise from finitely complete (or finitely cocomplete) categories or, more generally, from symmetric monoidal categories with reflexive equalizers (or reflexive coequalizers) preserved by the tensor product. Motivated by the practical applicability of coproducts of dependent lenses
[
4
,
6
,
9
]
, we showed sufficient conditions under which the category of dependent optics admits finite coproducts. In
[
19
,
22
]
, it was shown that the category of functor lenses admits a monoidal structure whenever the underlying indexed category is monoidal. Although we did not pursue this direction here, we believe that the key ingredient used in
[
19
]
—the notion of
pseudomonoid
in the 2-category of indexed categories—can be adapted to our setting to obtain an analogous result for the category of dependent optics.

Aiming to mimic the
profunctor encoding

[
8
,
17
]
of optics, we defined a generalization of Tambara modules—
Tambara representations
. We showed that contravariant functors from
{\mathbf{Optic}}
to an arbitrary category
\mathcal{D}
can equivalently be described as
\mathcal{D}
-valued Tambara representations. Using this result, we established a
representative-free
interface for dependent optics, where each dependent optic is encoded as a polymorphic function, and recovered the
profunctor representation theorem

[
8
]
in our setting. In the future, it will be interesting to explore in which particular cases of dependent optics this general result can be specialized to yield simplified encodings, akin to the
van Laarhoven encoding
for lenses
[
21
]
.

A more general definition of optics,
fiber optics
, has been developed in
[
4
]
. Furthermore, the authors sketched a possible formalization of the
bicategory
of dependent optics to simultaneously generalize dependent lenses, mixed optics, and fiber optics. We believe that novel avenues of research can arise from the interplay between the two works. From the construction in
[
4
, Sect. 4.3]
, it is straightforward to see that fiber optics are a particular case of dependent optics, as developed here, and they can probably be used to unify many examples of dependent optics. Hopefully, the work done here can help fine-tune the technical details of the definition of the bicategory of dependent optics.

Even though they appear different on the surface, our definition of dependent optics and the notion of
compound optics

[
18
]
are equivalent (see
[
5
, Ex. 4.2]
). In our view, this has several beneficial consequences. On the one hand, our manuscript can be used to fill the gaps left in
[
18
]
, such as the study of the properties of the category of dependent optics or the generalization of Tambara modules and of the profunctor representation theorem. On the other hand, the approach taken in
[
18
]
offers a different perspective on dependent optics and their composition in terms of Kan extensions, which can help form an intuitive understanding of our direct, explicit definitions. Finally, the existence of two equivalent, independently-developed definitions supports the intuition that this is indeed a principled adaptation of optics to the dependent case.
