The representation function has been investigated a lot in the literature. See, for example,

Theorem 1.1

Let

(2)

Remark 1.2

1)
When

Suppose that there is a set

1.1.
Connections with Sidon sets

We shall see that there are some connections between problems about Sidon sets and the problem we are investigating in the case
D=N
.

Definition 1.1
.

(Sidon sets and
g
-Sidon sets)
A set of natural numbers
S
is called a Sidon set if the equation
a+b=c+d
has only the trivial solution
\{a,b\}=\{c,d\}
, where
a,b,c,d
are elements of the set
S
. A set
A
is called
g
-Sidon set if for any integer
x
we have:

r_{A+A}(x):=\#\{(a,b)\in A\times A:a+b=x\}\leq g.

Cilleruelo, Ruzsa and Vinuesa (2010)
[
1
]
proved the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Define
\beta_{g}(N):=\max\{|A|\}
where
A\subseteq[N]
and
A
is a
g
-Sidon set.
Then,

\sigma_{1}(g)\sqrt{gN}(1-o(1))\leq\beta_{g}(N)\leq\sigma_{2}(g)\sqrt{gN}(1+o(1)).

Moreover,

\lim_{g\to\infty}\sigma_{1}(g)=\lim_{g\to\infty}\sigma_{2}(g)=\sigma,\penalty\ for\penalty\ some\penalty\ constant\penalty\ \sigma.

We can translate our result in Theorem
1.1
into their language.

Corollary 1.4
.

Define
\alpha_{g}(N):=\max\{|A|\}
where
A\subseteq[N]
,
r_{A-A}(x)\leq g
for all non-zero

.
Then,

\sigma_{1}(g)\sqrt{gN}(1-o(1))\leq\alpha_{g}(N)\leq\sigma_{2}(g)\sqrt{gN}(1+o(1)).

Moreover,

\lim_{g\to\infty}\sigma_{1}(g)=\lim_{g\to\infty}\sigma_{2}(g)=1.

2.1.
Lower bound for
f_{D}(N,\alpha)

In this section we are going to use the basic technique, the principle of inclusion and exclusion, to find the lower bound.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any
2\leq D\leq N
and any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have

f_{D}(N,\alpha)\geq\frac{2(D\alpha-1)}{D(D-1)}N-\frac{2}{D}.

Proof.

Let
A\subset[N]
be any subset with
|A|\geq\alpha N
. We need to show that

M_{D}(A)\geq\frac{2(D\alpha-1)}{D(D-1)}N-\frac{2}{D}.

For any
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, define the translate
A+d
to be the set

A+d:=\{a+d:a\in A\}.

By the principle of inclusion and exclusion, we estimate the size of the union of
A+d
as
d
ranges over
0\leq d<D
as following:

(2.1)

\left|\bigcup_{0\leq d<D}(A+d)\right|\geq\sum_{0\leq d<D}|A+d|-\sum_{0\leq d<d^{\prime}<D}|(A+d)\cap(A+d^{\prime})|.

Since
A+d\subseteq[N+D-1]
for
0\leq d<D
, the left hand side above is at most
N+D-1
. On the other hand, the first sum on the right hand side is
D|A|
. Thus we obtain

\sum_{0\leq d<d^{\prime}<D}|(A+d)\cap(A+d^{\prime})|\geq D|A|-(N+D-1)\geq(D\alpha-1)N-(D-1).

There are a total of
D(D-1)/2
summands, and thus for some
0\leq d<d^{\prime}<D
we have

|(A+d)\cap(A+d^{\prime})|\geq\frac{2}{D(D-1)}((D\alpha-1)N-(D-1))=\frac{2(D\alpha-1)}{D(D-1)}N-\frac{2}{D}.

The conclusion follows by noting that
|(A+d)\cap(A+d^{\prime})|
is precisely
r_{A-A}(d^{\prime}-d)
, and
1\leq d^{\prime}-d<D
.
∎

2.2.
Upper bound for
f_{D}(N,\alpha)

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
2\leq D\leq N
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
be parameters satisfying

(2.2)

D(2-D\alpha)>1

and

(2.3)

N\geq\frac{D(D-1)(2D+1)}{D(2-D\alpha)-1}.

Then

(2.4)

f_{D}(N,\alpha)\leq\frac{2(D\alpha-1)}{D(D-1)}N+4.

Remark 2.4
.

For example, if
\alpha=o(1)
then these assumptions hold when
D\leq(2-c)/\alpha
for any absolute constant
c>0
such that
Dc>1
and
N
is much larger than
\alpha^{-2}
.

Proof.

We construct one subset
A
with particular structure such that it has density at least
\alpha
and
M_{D}(A)
attains the upper bound in (
2.4
).

We define a set
B\subseteq\{1,2,\cdots,D^{2}\}
as following:

(2.5)

B:=\{D,2D,3D,\cdots,D^{2}\}\cup\{1,D+2,2D+3,\cdots,(D-2)D+D-1\}

so that
|B|=2D-1
. Let
M=\lfloor N/D\rfloor
and let
k=\lceil(\alpha N-M)/(D-1)\rceil
so that

k\leq\frac{\alpha N-M}{D-1}+1\leq\frac{\alpha N-N/D}{D-1}+2=\frac{D\alpha-1}{D(D-1)}N+2.

By the lower bound on
N
, we have

kD\leq\frac{D\alpha-1}{D-1}N+2D\leq\frac{N}{D}-1\leq M.

Thus we may define
A_{1},A_{2}\subseteq[N]
by

(2.6)

A_{1}:=\{x+iD^{2}:x\in B,i=0,1,2,\cdots,k-1)\},\ \ A_{2}:=\{jD:kD<j\leq M\},

and then take
A=A_{1}\cup A_{2}
. Hence

|A|=k|B|+(M-kD)=kD+M-k\geq\alpha N,

where the last inequality follows by our choice of
k
.

We only need to show that
r_{A-A}(d)\leq 2k
holds for any
1\leq d<D
to complete our proof. For any
1\leq d<D
, if
d=a-a^{\prime}
for some
a,a^{\prime}\in A
, then we must have
a,a^{\prime}\in A_{1}
since any two distinct elements must have difference at least
D
if at least one of them is in
A_{2}
. Writing
a=x+iD^{2}
and
a^{\prime}=x^{\prime}+i^{\prime}D^{2}
with
x,x^{\prime}\in B
and
0\leq i,i^{\prime}<k
, we first observe that the possible values of
|i-i^{\prime}|
can only be
0
or
1
.

If
|i-i^{\prime}|\geq 2
, then by triangle inequality we have

\left|(x+iD^{2})-(x^{\prime}+i^{\prime}D^{2})\right|\geq|i-i^{\prime}|D^{2}-\max_{x,x^{\prime}\in B}|x-x^{\prime}|\geq D^{2}+1>D.

If
|i-i^{\prime}|=1
, by considering the structure of
B
we have if
\{x,x^{\prime}\}\neq\{D^{2},1\}
, then

\left|(x+iD^{2})-(x^{\prime}+i^{\prime}D^{2})\right|\geq D.

So one must have
\{x,x^{\prime}\}=\{D^{2},1\}
and
d=1
if
|i-i^{\prime}|=1
. As for
d=1
, if
i=i^{\prime}
, then one must have
x=(D-2)D+D-1
and
x^{\prime}=(D-1)D
. So
r_{A-A}(1)=k+(k-1)<2k
. As for
d\geq 2
, we showed that
i
and
i^{\prime}
must satisfy
i=i^{\prime}
which gives
d=x-x^{\prime}
. And the number of possible choices for
x,x^{\prime}
in
B
is not larger than
2
in this case. So
r_{A-A}(d)\leq 2k
for all
d\neq 1
. In conclusion, the result holds for all
1\leq d<D
.
∎

At the end of this subsection, we give an explanation for why the principle of inclusion and exclusion works.
Since for any
y

\in

(A+i)\cap(A+j)\cap(A+k)
, where
i,j,k\in\{0,1,2,\cdots,D-1\}
, there exists
x\in A
, such that
x+i,x+j,x+k
are all in
A
, i.e., they lie in an interval with length
D-1
. But this is impossible in our constructed example, where any interval with length
D-1
has at most
2
elements instead of
3
.

There is no point in the intersection of the
3
sets, so it is even impossible for
4
or more sets.
Hence the values of all the moments with order greater than two must be zero.

3.
Proof of the lower bound in Theorem
1.1

(3)

In this section, we use Fourier analytic tools to prove Theorem
3.1
which gives the lower bound of
f_{D}(N,\alpha)
for a wide range of
D
. The argument is similar to the proof of
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
2\leq D\leq N
, then

(3.1)

f_{D}(N,\alpha)\geq\frac{\alpha^{2}N^{2}}{N+D}-\frac{\alpha N+1}{D}.

In particular, if
D\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, we have

(3.2)

f_{D}(N,\alpha)\geq(1+o(1))\alpha^{2}N.

Proof.

Let
A
be any subset of
[N]
with density at least
\alpha
. We regard
A
as a subset of
\mathbb{Z}/(N+D)\mathbb{Z}
in the natural way.
To fix our notation, we define the convolution of
f,g
where
f,g:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
are two functions on the abelian group
G
.

f*g(x):=\sum_{y\in G}f(y)\overline{g(x-y)}.

And we use our traditional notation to get that

1_{A}*1\textordmasculine_{A}(x)=\#\{(a,a^{\prime})\in A\times A:a-a^{\prime}=x\},

where
f\textordmasculine(x):=f(-x)
.

We use
M_{D}(A)
to denote the quantity the same as in the case of integers before. It is not true that for general
x\in\mathbb{Z}/(N+D)\mathbb{Z}
,
1_{A}*1\textordmasculine_{A}(x)\leq M_{D}(A)
in modular version. However, for any
x
with
0<|x|\leq D
, in the modular version we still have

1_{A}*1\textordmasculine_{A}(x)\leq M_{D}(A).

Let
I
be the characteristic function of the set
\{1,2,\cdots,D\}
. Then we estimate the following quantity.

E:=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}/(N+D)\mathbb{Z}}1_{A}*1\textordmasculine_{A}(x)I*I\textordmasculine(x).

We first notice that each summand is zero when
|x|>D
. Combining this and what we discussed above, we have

E\leq|A|D+\sum_{0<|x|\leq D}1_{A}*1\textordmasculine_{A}(x)I*I\textordmasculine(x).

The right hand side can be further upper bounded by

|A|D+M_{D}(A)D^{2}.

On the other hand, we use Plancherel’s identity to get

E=(N+D)\sum_{r}|\hat{1}_{A}(r)|^{2}|\hat{I}(r)|^{2}\geq\frac{|A|^{2}D^{2}}{N+D}.

The above two inequalities give us the bound for
M_{D}(A)
for any subset
A
with
|A|\geq\alpha N
. For any
A
such that can make
M_{D}(A)=f_{D}(N,\alpha)
, we must have
|A|=\lceil\alpha N\rceil
. By substituting this into our expression, we get the lower bound.
Notice that if
D\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, the lower bound in (
3.1
) has main term
\frac{\alpha^{2}N^{2}}{N+D}
. This main term is a decreasing function of
D
. Therefore, under the assumption that
D\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, it is better to use the lower bound in (
3.1
) with
D=o(N)
and it gives (
3.2
). Since function
f_{D}(N,\alpha)
is an increasing function of
D
, with the assumption that
D\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, (
3.2
) holds no matter
D=o(N)
holds or not.
∎

Proof of the upper bound in Theorem

In this section we study the upper bound of

with given density
\alpha
and

. We consider the case

, i.e., we can regard it as restriction free on the range of differences of any two distinct elements in

Let
f_{N}(\alpha)
simplify the notation

we defined before in the case

.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. If
\alpha^{2}N\rightarrow\infty
as

, then

f_{N}(\alpha)=(1+o(1))\alpha^{2}N.

In the above condition, we need to prove the result for

The upper bound here can be proved by the deterministic method. It also can be proved by the probabilistic method, however, a shortcoming of probabilistic method here is we can not always avoid the non-essential needed restriction on the range of density
\alpha
that it be much larger than
N^{-1/2}(\log N)^{-1/2}
; at least the author does not know how to avoid this. So in this paper, we use the deterministic approach to prove asymptotic formulas for the function
f
with loose restriction on
\alpha
. Before we prove the upper bound in the case of integers, we first prove the following theorem which gives us the results in cyclic groups and would imply the results in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
N=p^{2}s
for some positive integer

and prime number
p
. Let
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
be a cyclic group and
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
We define

f_{N}(\alpha):=\min_{A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z},|A|\geq\alpha N}\max_{d\neq 0}r_{A-A}(d).

If

, then

f_{N}(\alpha)=(1+o(1))\alpha^{2}N.

On the other side, there is a similar result proved in generalized Sidon sets. See
[
1
, Theorem 1.6]
. We state it by using our notation.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
be a cyclic group and
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
We define

g_{N}(\alpha):=\min_{A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z},|A|\geq\alpha N}\max_{d}r_{A+A}(d).

Then as

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{g_{N}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{2}N}=1,

Here

r_{A+A}(d)=\{(a,a^{\prime})\in A\times A:d=a+a^{\prime}\}.

Similarly, we have the following in the case of difference sets.

(4.1)

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{f_{N}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{2}N}=1.

Proof of (

).

The lower bound of

can be regarded as a corollary of Theorem

to denote the maximum value of

for all possible non-zero

|A|^{2}
number of pairs

|A|
of them satisfy

. So we have the following

|A|^{2}-|A|\leq(N-1)M_{N}(A).

By noting the fact that

, and letting

This gives the lower bound of

. Combining this with Theorem

4.1.
Construction in

We prove that there is a set

has a relatively small upper bound and

Proposition 4.4
.

Given a positive integer
k
, for any odd prime number
p\geq 2k
and any
(a,b)\not\equiv(0,0)\pmod{p}
, there is a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}

.

In the context
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, we do our construction by pasting several disjoint sets together. The main techniques will be used are properties of quadratic equations.

First we define the set which plays an important role in this section.
For
u\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, define

(4.2)

A_{u}:=\{(x,x^{2}/u):x\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\}.

For any
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, we use the following notation to denote the representation function. For
uv\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, define

r_{A_{u}-A_{v}}(a,b):=\#\{((x,x^{2}/u),(y,y^{2}/v)):a\equiv x-y,b\equiv x^{2}/u-y^{2}/v\pmod{p}\}.

This is exactly the number of solutions to the equations

\begin{cases}a\equiv x-y\pmod{p}\\
b\equiv\frac{x^{2}}{u}-\frac{y^{2}}{v}\pmod{p}.\end{cases}

In the next lemma we study the possible values of
r_{A_{u}-A_{v}}(a,b)
, where
(a,b)\not\equiv(0,0)\pmod{p}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose
r_{A_{u}-A_{v}}(a,b)
is defined as above,
p
is an odd prime and
(a,b)\not\equiv(0,0)\pmod{p}
.

(1)

If
u\equiv v\pmod{p}
, then
r_{A_{u}-A_{v}}(a,b)\leq 1
.

(2)

If
u-v\equiv u^{\prime}-v^{\prime}\pmod{p}
and
(\frac{uvu^{\prime}v^{\prime}}{p})=-1
, then
r_{A_{u}-A_{v}}(a,b)+r_{A_{u^{\prime}}-A_{v^{\prime}}}(a,b)=2
for all
(a,b)\not\equiv(0,0)\pmod{p}
.

The symbol
(-)
we use in
(2)
is Legendre symbol.

Proof.

1) In the case
u\equiv v\pmod{p}
, it is clear that if
a\equiv 0\pmod{p}
then there is no solution since we required that
(a,b)\not\equiv(0,0)\pmod{p}
. Otherwise we have
x+y\equiv uba^{-1}\pmod{p}
which leads to a unique solution with the fact that
x-y\equiv a\pmod{p}
.

2) In the case that
u-v\equiv u^{\prime}-v^{\prime}\pmod{p}
and
(\frac{uvu^{\prime}v^{\prime}}{p})=-1
, we have
u\not\equiv v\pmod{p}
and
u^{\prime}\not\equiv v^{\prime}\pmod{p}
. It means that we are studying proper quadratic equations in this case. Substituting
y\equiv x-a\pmod{p}
into
b\equiv x^{2}/u-y^{2}/v\pmod{p}
, we have the quadratic equation

(u-v)x^{2}-2aux+ua^{2}+buv\equiv 0\pmod{p}

and the discriminant is

\Delta\equiv 4uv(a^{2}-(u-v)b)\pmod{p}.

The number of solutions can be written as

r_{A_{u}-A_{v}}(a,b)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }(\frac{\Delta}{p})=0.\\
2&\text{if }(\frac{\Delta}{p})=1.\\
0&\text{if }(\frac{\Delta}{p})=-1.\end{cases}

This can be simplified as

r_{A_{u}-A_{v}}(a,b)=1+\Big(\frac{\Delta}{p}\Big).

To calculate the sum of
r_{A_{u}-A_{v}}(a,b)
and
r_{A_{u}^{\prime}-A_{v}^{\prime}}(a,b)
, we need to calculate the corresponding sum
(\frac{\Delta}{p})+(\frac{\Delta^{\prime}}{p})
.
Notice that the product of these two is

\Big(\frac{\Delta}{p}\Big)\Big(\frac{\Delta^{\prime}}{p}\Big)=\Big(\frac{16uvu^{\prime}v^{\prime}((u-v)b-a^{2})((u^{\prime}-v^{\prime})b-a^{2})}{p}\Big)=-\Big(\frac{((u-v)b-a^{2})^{2}}{p}\Big).

In the last step we used the fact that
u-v\equiv u^{\prime}-v^{\prime}\pmod{p}
and
(\frac{uvu^{\prime}v^{\prime}}{p})=-1
. Now there are two possibilities. If
(u-v)b-a^{2}\equiv 0
, then both
(\frac{\Delta}{p})
and
(\frac{\Delta^{\prime}}{p})
equal to zero. If
(u-v)b-a^{2}\not\equiv 0
, then the product of
(\frac{\Delta}{p})
and
(\frac{\Delta^{\prime}}{p})
equals to
-1
. These two cases give us the same result

\Big(\frac{\Delta}{p}\Big)+\Big(\frac{\Delta^{\prime}}{p}\Big)=0,

which means that
r_{A_{u}-A_{v}}(a,b)+r_{A_{u}^{\prime}-A_{v}^{\prime}}(a,b)=2
.
∎

In the next lemma, the main technique we use is Weil’s Theorem.

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose
0\leq n\leq k-1
,
1\leq|l|\leq k-1
,
1\leq i,j\leq k
and
p
is an odd prime number. Let
S_{n}
define the sum

S_{n}:=\sum_{1\leq|l|\leq k-1}\left|\sum_{i-j=l}\Big(\frac{(n+i)(n+j)}{p}\Big)\right|.

Then we have the following upper bound for the sum of
S_{n}

\sum_{0\leq n\leq p-1}S_{n}\leq\sqrt{2p^{2}k^{2}(2k-1)+8p^{3/2}k^{4}}.

To estimate the sum, we first use the Cauchy inequality to rewrite the sum which roughly gives us a function with power
4
in the numerator of Legendre symbol, and then apply the Weil’s Theorem.

Proof.

First we use Cauchy inequality to get the following estimation.

\sum_{0\leq n\leq p-1}S_{n}=\sum_{n,l}\left|\sum_{i-j=l}\Big(\frac{(n+i)(n+j)}{p}\Big)\right|\leq\sqrt{2kp\sum_{n,l}\Big(\sum_{i-j=l}\Big(\frac{(n+i)(n+j)}{p}\Big)\Big)^{2}}.

The right hand side above can be written as

\sqrt{2kp\sum_{i-j=i^{\prime}-j^{\prime}}\sum_{n}\Big(\frac{(n+i)(n+j)(n+i^{\prime})(n+j^{\prime})}{p}\Big)}.

Let
f(n)=(n+i)(n+j)(n+i^{\prime})(n+j^{\prime})
. We estimate the quantity above by using Weil’s Theorem.

1) If
f(n)
is a constant multiple of a square, then we bound the value
\Big(\frac{f(n)}{p}\Big)
trivially by
1
. The number of quadruples
(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
that can make
f(n)
a constant multiple of a square is bounded by
k(k-1)+k^{2}
. The reasons are the following. First notice that if the restriction on the quadruple is
i+j^{\prime}=i^{\prime}+j
, then we have two possible cases, either
i=i^{\prime}
and
j=j^{\prime}
, or
i=j
and
i^{\prime}=j^{\prime}
.
These two cases both give
k^{2}
choices but there is an overlap that all of them are equal, i.e., we need to subtract
k
choices. In total, we have the following bound

\sum_{f\penalty\ is\penalty\ square}\sum_{n}\Big(\frac{f(n)}{p}\Big)\leq pk(2k-1).

2) If
f(n)
is not a constant multiple of a square, we can use Weil’s Theorem. For each quadruple
(i.j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
, we have the following,

\sum_{0\leq n\leq p-1}\Big(\frac{f(n)}{p}\Big)\leq(degf)\sqrt{p}=4\sqrt{p}.

The total number of quadruples under the restriction
i-j=i^{\prime}-j^{\prime}
is bounded by
k^{3}
. We also use this bound as our bound for the number of quadruples which make
f
satisfy Weil’s Theorem. Then we have the following

\sum_{f\penalty\ isn^{\prime}t\penalty\ a\penalty\ square}\sum_{n}\Big(\frac{f(n)}{p}\Big)\leq 4k^{3}\sqrt{p}.

Combining case
1)
and case
2)
, we have

\sum_{0\leq n\leq k-1}S_{n}\leq\sqrt{2pk(pk(2k-1)+4k^{3}\sqrt{p})}=\sqrt{2p^{2}k^{2}(2k-1)+8p^{3/2}k^{4}}.

∎

Now we will use the above two lemmas to prove Proposition
4.4
.

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

First we point out how the set
A
looks. Let

be the same as what we defined in (
4.2
), and

be defined as follows:

The crucial point is to find a suitable

be the set we are searching for.

First notice that the size of

is independent of

and

only intersect at point

for any

.
Next we study the representation function

. From the definition of

To study the right hand side above, we first parametrize the variables above. Write

,

2) If

\alpha(l)+\beta(l)=k-|l|,\penalty\ \penalty\ \alpha(l)-\beta(l)=\sum_{u-v=l}\Big(\frac{uv}{p}\Big).

Our strategy is to match pairs into pairs according to their values under Legendre symbol. Precisely, if two pairs have different signs, we can match them and then use the second part in Lemma

4.2.
