Tail Bounds on the Runtime of Categorical Compact Genetic Algorithm
\name Ryoki Hamano, \addr hamano_ryoki_xa@cyberagent.co.jp, \name Kento Uchida, \addr uchida-kento-fz@ynu.ac.jp, \addr shirakawa-shinichi-bg@ynu.ac.jp, \name Daiki Morinaga, \addr morinaga@bbo.cs.tsukuba.ac.jp, \addr akimoto@cs.tsukuba.ac.jp

Abstract
The majority of theoretical analyses of evolutionary algorithms in the discrete domain focus on binary optimization algorithms, even though black-box optimization on the categorical domain has a lot of practical applications. In this paper, we consider a probabilistic model-based algorithm using the family of categorical distributions as its underlying distribution and set the sample size as two. We term this specific algorithm the categorical compact genetic algorithm (ccGA). The ccGA can be considered as an extension of the compact genetic algorithm (cGA), which is an efficient binary optimization algorithm. We theoretically analyze the dependency of the number of possible categories K K , the number of dimensions D D , and the learning rate η \eta on the runtime. We investigate the tail bound of the runtime on two typical linear functions on the categorical domain: categorical OneMax (COM) and KVal . We derive that the runtimes on COM and KVal are O ⁡ ( D ​ ln ⁡ ( D ​ K ) / η ) O(\sqrt{D}\ln(DK)/\eta) and Θ ⁡ ( D ​ ln ⁡ K / η ) \Theta(D\ln K/\eta) with high probability, respectively. Our analysis is a generalization for that of the cGA on the binary domain.

Introduction

Discrete domain black-box optimization is often involved in real-world applications. Several discrete black-box optimization methods are developed to obtain the optimal solution with few evaluations of the objective function.
Among these methods, some well-performing methods are categorized as probabilistic model-based algorithms.
The probabilistic model-based algorithms repeat generating multiple samples from the underlying probability distribution and updating the distribution parameters.
Particularly, the compact genetic algorithm (cGA)
(
Harik et al.,, 1999
)
has been widely used as a probabilistic model-based algorithm for binary optimization and has shown efficient search performance in several situations
(
Dey and Mukhopadhyay,, 2022
;
Xing and Qu,, 2012
;
Ha et al.,, 2016
)
.

Information geometric optimization (IGO)
(
Ollivier et al.,, 2017
)
has been proposed as a unified framework of probabilistic model-based algorithms to reveal the key factor of the well-known probabilistic model-based algorithms.
According to the IGO framework, cGA can be explained as a stochastic natural gradient ascent with respect to (w.r.t.) the parameter of the Bernoulli distribution, which is the underlying distribution of samples.
An instance of IGO contains two hyperparameters, the learning rate

\eta
and the sample size
\lambda
, whose setting significantly affects the search efficiency. To obtain their recommended setting, several theoretical analyses investigate the relations between the search efficiency, the setting of hyperparameters, and the information of the search space, such as the number of dimensions

;
Akimoto and Ollivier,, 2013
;

.

The search efficiencies of discrete evolutionary algorithms are often measured by the runtime, also called the first hitting time. In this context, the runtime is the number of iterations required until the optimal solution is obtained for the first time.
The majority of the analyses in the discrete domain investigated binary optimization methods even though runtime analyses of probabilistic model-based evolutionary algorithms are actively studied. However, the applications of stochastic algorithms are not limited to binary optimization. For example,

1.1
Runtime Analysis of cGA

The works most related to our analysis are the runtime analyses of cGA.
In those analyses, OneMax and BinVal have been popularly investigated as representative linear pseudo-Boolean functions
(
Droste,, 2006
;
Witt,, 2018
;
Hasenöhrl and Sutton,, 2018
;
Sudholt and Witt,, 2019
;
Lengler et al.,, 2021
;
Doerr,, 2021
)
.
OneMax counts the number of ones in a binary variable as its evaluation value, while BinVal represents the value of a binary variable regarded as a binary unsigned integer.

The first analysis of cGA on these functions was done by
Droste, (2006)
.
This work investigated cGA without the border, which is often introduced to correct the distribution parameters to be within a certain range so that they are not fixed to
0
or
1
.
The theoretical analysis provides the upper bound
O(D/\eta)
and the lower bound
\Omega(\sqrt{D}/\eta)
of the expected first hitting time (under the condition of finite first hitting time) and basic tail bounds
O(D/\eta)
and
\Omega(\sqrt{D}/\ln D/\eta)
on any linear function, where the conditions for
\eta
and the tail probabilities are also provided.
Additionally, this paper demonstrates that the expectations and tail bounds of the runtime on OneMax and BinVal are different.
More precisely, on OneMax, the expected first hitting time is
O(\sqrt{D}/\eta)
and the probability of the runtime being
O(\sqrt{D}/\eta)
is at least
1/2
with the condition of
\eta=o(D^{-\frac{1}{2}})
.
On BinVal, the expected first hitting time is
\Omega(D/\eta)
and the probability of the runtime being
O(D/\eta)
is at least
1-\exp(-\Theta(\eta^{-1}))
.

These results were refined later, owing to two observations: the first was that the bounds and the conditions for
\eta
were improved.
Sudholt and Witt, (2019)
analyzed the cGA with borders
\{D^{-1},1-D^{-1}\}
and observed that the lower and upper bounds of the expected first hitting time were
\Omega(\sqrt{D}/\eta+D\ln D)
with
\eta^{-1}=\Omega(D^{c})
for some
c>0
and
O(\sqrt{D}/\eta)
with
\eta^{-1}=\Omega(\sqrt{D}\ln D)
, respectively.

The second observation was that the tail bound has a probability of at least
1-D^{-c}
for some
c>0
.
Referring to
(
Witt,, 2018
)
, the probability of an event is said to occur with high probability when the event occurs with the probability of at least
1-D^{-c}
.

Witt, (2018)
showed that the runtime on BinVal was
\Theta(D/\eta)
with a high probability when
\eta^{-1}=\Omega(D\ln D)
, and
\eta=D^{-c}
for some
c>0
.

1.2
Contributions

In this paper, we analyze a probabilistic model-based evolutionary algorithm that uses the family of categorical distributions as its underlying distribution with sample size
\lambda=2
.
We term this algorithm the categorical compact genetic algorithm (ccGA).
We select two typical linear functions on the categorical domain, categorical OneMax (COM) and
KVal
, as representative examples of simple objective functions and difficult objective functions. COM and
KVal
are the extensions of OneMax and BinVal on the binary domain, respectively.
As described in Section
1.1
, the expected runtimes and the tail bounds of the runtime on OneMax and BinVal are different w.r.t.
D
.
Our analysis investigates how the number of categories
K
affects the runtime of ccGA and the conditions on the learning rate to realize an efficient search on COM and
KVal
.
In the categorical domain, we say that an event occurs with high probability when it occurs with a probability of at least
1-D^{-c}-K^{-c}
for some
c>0
.
Moreover, our analysis shows the following results under a common assumption
\eta^{-1}=O((DK)^{c^{\prime}})
for some
c^{\prime}>0
.

•

With a high probability, the runtime of ccGA is
O(\sqrt{D}\ln(DK)/\eta)
when
\eta^{-1}=\Omega(\sqrt{D}K\ln(DK))
and is
\Omega((\sqrt{D}+\ln K)/\eta)
when
\eta^{-1}=\Omega(\sqrt{D}\ln D)
on COM (Theorem
5
and Theorem
6
).

•

With a high probability, the runtime of ccGA is
\Theta(D\ln(DK)/\eta)
when
\eta^{-1}=\Omega(DK^{2}\ln K\ln(DK))
on
KVal
(Theorem
7
and Theorem
8
).

Considering the order w.r.t.
K
, while we obtain the same tail bound
\Theta(\ln K/\eta)
on both COM and
KVal
, the conditions for the learning rate are different as
\eta^{-1}=\Omega(K\ln K)
for the upper tail bound on COM and
\eta^{-1}=\Omega(K^{2}(\ln K)^{2})
on
KVal
.

The detailed proofs of the non-trivial drift theorems are another contribution of this study.
The drift theorem provides the tail bound of the first hitting time or the bound of the expected first hitting time of the target stochastic process
\{X^{(t)}\}
based on the upper or lower bound of the conditional expectation of a single transition
\mathbb{E}[X^{(t+1)}-X^{(t)}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(t)}]
under the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}^{(t)}\}
, termed as
drift
.
We extend the existing drift theorems in two ways to make our analysis rigorous.
The first way is to consider the
\mathcal{F}^{(t)}
-measurable event where the bound of the drift is significantly larger or smaller than the bound under the complementary event. A tighter tail bound of the first hitting time is achieved compared to that of the existing drift theorem when the complementary event rarely occurs. The second way is to skip the iteration where the process stays at the same value, i.e.,
X^{(t+1)}=X^{(t)}
. If such a skip occurs frequently, our drift theorem shows a tighter tail bound. We first illustrate the explicit proofs of these drift theorems even though they were implicitly used in the runtime analyses of
(1,\lambda)
EA
(
Rowe and Sudholt,, 2014
)
and cGA
(
Friedrich et al.,, 2017
)
.

Categorical Compact Genetic Algorithm (ccGA)

In this paper, we focus on the optimization problems of
D
-dimensional categorical variable
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{D})^{\mathrm{T}}
whose
d
-th element
x_{d}
is represented by a
K
-dimensional one-hot vector. The
k
-th element of
x_{d}
is denoted as
x_{d,k}
. The domain of
x
is denoted as

\mathbb{K}^{D}_{K}=\left\{x\in\{0,1\}^{D\times K}\biggm|\sum_{k=1}^{K}x_{d,k}=1\quad\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptd\in\llbracket 1,D\rrbracket\right\}\kern 5.0pt,

(1)

where
\llbracket a,b\rrbracket=\{\lceil a\rceil,\lceil a\rceil+1,\cdots,\lfloor b\rfloor-1,\lfloor b\rfloor\}\subset\mathbb{N}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
a<b
.
We consider finding the maximizer of a given objective function
f:\mathbb{K}^{D}_{K}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle x^{\ast}=\mathop{\rm arg~max}\limits_{x\in\mathbb{K}^{D}_{K}}f(x)\kern 5.0pt.

(2)

We define ccGA as the optimization method for
\mathbb{K}^{D}_{K}
; the method is a probabilistic model-based algorithm with the family of categorical distributions
\{P_{\theta}\}
as its underlying distribution. The probability mass function of the categorical distribution is given by

\displaystyle P_{\theta}(x)=\prod^{D}_{d=1}\prod^{K}_{k=1}(\theta_{d,k})^{x_{d,k}}\kern 5.0pt,

(3)

where
\theta\in\Theta
is a distribution parameter. The element of
\theta
represents the probability of the corresponding category to be selected, i.e.,
\theta_{d,k}=\Pr(x_{d,k}=1)
. The domain
\Theta
is defined as

\displaystyle\Theta=\left\{\theta\in[0,1]^{D\times K}\biggm|\sum^{K}_{k=1}\theta_{d,k}=1\quad\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptd\in\llbracket 1,D\rrbracket\right\}\kern 5.0pt.

(4)

The distribution parameter is initialized as

\displaystyle\theta^{(0)}_{d,k}=\frac{1}{K}\quad\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptd\in\llbracket 1,D\rrbracket\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptk\in\llbracket 1,K\rrbracket\kern 5.0pt.

(5)

The ccGA algorithm is described as follows:
similar to the cGA, two samples
x,x^{\prime}
are generated from
P_{\theta^{(t)}}
in each iteration.
The superior sample
x^{\langle 1\rangle}
and inferior sample
x^{\langle 2\rangle}
are selected from
\{x,x^{\prime}\}
, based on the evaluation value, which satisfies
f(x^{\langle 1\rangle})\geq f(x^{\langle 2\rangle})
.
In the case of a tie,
x
and
x^{\prime}
are assigned to
x^{\langle 1\rangle}
and
x^{\langle 2\rangle}
, respectively, which is the same as the random selection.
The update rule of ccGA is summarized as

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}+\eta(x^{\langle 1\rangle}-x^{\langle 2\rangle})\kern 5.0pt,

(6)

where
\eta>0
is the learning rate.
The update procedure of ccGA is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

The update rule of ccGA is introduced in
(
Akimoto et al.,, 2019
)
as a stochastic natural gradient method using the family of categorical distributions.
Contrary to the update rule detailed in
(
Akimoto et al.,, 2019
)
, we do not restrict the domain of each element of
\theta
to be
[\ell,u]
for some
0<\ell<u<1
.
Instead, we assume that
\eta
satisfies
(\eta K)^{-1}\in\mathbb{N}
. We note that all elements of the distribution parameter are initialized to
1/K
and they are not changed after they become
0
or
1
because, in such cases, the corresponding elements of the samples
x,x^{\prime}
are always the same. Therefore, the distribution parameter
\theta^{(t)}_{d,k}
is not updated below
0
or above
1
without modification of the update rule in (
6
). This assumption eases the difficulty of the analysis.

To explain the justification of the update rule of ccGA, we mention that the relation between cGA and ccGA can be explained using the information geometric optimization (IGO)
(
Ollivier et al.,, 2017
)
. The IGO is a unified framework of stochastic search algorithms, and the IGO algorithm is instantiated by applying a family of probability distributions. The update in the IGO algorithm is a kind of stochastic natural gradient ascent
(
Amari,, 1998
)
.
The IGO framework recovers several well-known algorithms, and the update rules of cGA and ccGA are derived by applying the family of Bernoulli distributions and the family of categorical distributions with the sample size of two, respectively. It should be noted that when
K=2
, the update rule (
6

(7)

4

Our analysis focuses on objective functions with unique optimal solutions and investigates the tail bound of

We assume, without loss of generality (w.l.o.g.), that the categories of the optimal solution are the first categories in all dimensions, i.e.,

For ccGA, the first hitting time and the transition of the distribution parameter are related.
In our analysis, the transition of a function of the distribution parameter, called a potential function, is investigated to derive the tail bound of
T_{\mathrm{Hit}}

Our potential functions relate the product and the summation of the elements of the distribution parameter
\theta^{(t)}_{d,1}
corresponding to the optimal categories.
The following lemmas provide a useful relationship between the tail bound of
T_{\mathrm{Hit}}
and the tail bounds of the iteration where the product and the summation reach a given target value for the first time.

Consider the update of ccGA. Assume the objective function has a unique optimal solution

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle>1-\Pr(T_{2}\geq s)-(1-p)^{2u}\kern 5.0pt,

4
Generalization of Drift Theorems

Our analysis is based on the drift theorems that are used in previous research on runtime analyses of evolutionary algorithms
(
Witt,, 2018
;
Doerr and Goldberg,, 2010
)
.
The drift is the conditional expectation of a single-step difference of a target stochastic process with respect to a filtration
5
5

5

We denote a stochastic process
(X^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
adapted to a filtration
(\mathcal{F}^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
as
(X^{(t)},\mathcal{F}^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. In this manuscript, we use the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
(\theta^{(0)},\cdots\theta^{(t)})
as
\mathcal{F}^{(t)}
.
.
The drift theorem provides the tail bound and the bound of the expectation of the first hitting time using the upper or lower bound of the drift.
A tighter bound of the drift generally provides a stricter bound of the first hitting time.
In the following, we generalize the well-known drift theorems to allow different kinds of bounds of the drift and obtain a stricter bound than the existing drift theorems for analyzing ccGA.

4.1
Conditional Drift Theorems

The drift changes significantly depending on events measurable with respect to the filtration in some cases, including our analysis.
This makes the upper or lower bound of the drift we can obtain too small or too large to derive the tight tail bound of the runtime. To deal with this problem, we introduce the conditional drift theorems, which consider a sequence of (likely) events
(E^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
where the bounds of the drift are not significantly large or small.
We note that such an approach appears in previous research
(
Witt,, 2018
;
Friedrich et al.,, 2017
)
. Nevertheless, to our best knowledge, there is no proof with an explicit explanation. The conditional drift theorems lead to a rigorous proof for this approach.

We provide three kinds of conditional drift theorems that assume different drift bounds. Theorem
1
requires an upper bound of the drift under the event
E^{(t)}
to show an upper tail bound of the first hitting time, while Theorem
2
requires a lower bound of the drift to show a lower tail bound. Theorem
3
requires a negative multiplicative upper bound under the event
E^{(t)}
to show a lower tail bound.
These conditional drift theorems are derived from existing drift theorems. We introduce the conditional drift theorems based on the additive drift theorem
(
Kötzing,, 2014
, Theorems 8, 9)
and multiplicative drift theorem
(
Doerr and Goldberg,, 2010
, Theorem 1)
as follows:

Theorem 1
.

Consider a stochastic process
(X^{(t)},\mathcal{F}^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with
X^{(0)}\leq 0
. Consider a sequence of events
(E^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, where
E^{(t)}\in\mathcal{F}^{(t)}
and
E^{(t)}\supseteq E^{(t+1)}
. Let
m\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and

\displaystyle T=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:X^{(t)}\geq m\}\kern 5.0pt.

(16)

Then, for constants
c>0
and
\varepsilon\in[0,c]
, if it holds, for all
t<T
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{(t+1)}-X^{(t)}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(t)}]\mathbf{1}_{\{{E^{(t)}}\}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\mathbf{1}_{\{{E^{(t)}}\}}

(17)

\displaystyle|X^{(t+1)}-X^{(t)}|\mathbf{1}_{\{{E^{(t)}}\}}

\displaystyle<c\kern 5.0pt,

(18)

then it holds, for all
n\leq m/(2\varepsilon)
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(T<n\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2}}{8c^{2}n}\right)+\Pr(\bar{E}^{(n-1)})\kern 5.0pt.

(19)

Theorem 2
.

Consider a stochastic process
(X^{(t)},\mathcal{F}^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with
X^{(0)}\geq 0
. Consider a sequence of events
(E^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, where
E^{(t)}\in\mathcal{F}^{(t)}
and
E^{(t)}\supseteq E^{(t+1)}
. Let
m\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and

\displaystyle T=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:X^{(t)}\geq m\}\kern 5.0pt.

(20)

Then, for constants
c>0
and
\varepsilon\in[0,c]
, if it holds, for all
t<T
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{(t+1)}-X^{(t)}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(t)}]\mathbf{1}_{\{{E^{(t)}}\}}

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon\mathbf{1}_{\{{E^{(t)}}\}}

(21)

\displaystyle|X^{(t+1)}-X^{(t)}|\mathbf{1}_{\{{E^{(t)}}\}}

\displaystyle<c\kern 5.0pt,

(22)

then it holds, for all
n\geq 2m/\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(T\geq n\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{8c^{2}}\right)+\Pr(\bar{E}^{(n-1)})\kern 5.0pt.

(23)

Theorem 3
.

Consider a stochastic process
(X^{(t)},\mathcal{F}^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and a sequence of events
(E^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, where
E^{(t)}\in\mathcal{F}^{(t)}
and
E^{(t)}\supseteq E^{(t+1)}
.
Assume
X^{(t)}
takes the value on
\{0\}\cup[x_{\min},x_{\max}]

t\in\mathbb{N}

0<x_{\min}<x_{\max}<\infty
, and
X^{(t)}
remains zero after

\displaystyle T=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:X^{(t)}=0\}\kern 5.0pt.

Then, for a constant

, if it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{(t+1)}-X^{(t)}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(t)}]\mathbf{1}_{\{{E^{(t)}}\}}\leq-\varepsilon X^{(t)}\mathbf{1}_{\{{E^{(t)}}\}}\kern 5.0pt

(25)

for all
t<T
, it holds, for any

,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(T>\frac{r+\ln(X^{(0)}/x_{\min})}{\varepsilon}\right)\leq\exp\left(-r\right)+\Pr(\bar{E}^{(n-1)})\kern 5.0pt,

4.2
Drift Theorem for Skipping Processes

Next, we demonstrate an extension of the negative drift theorem
(
Kötzing,, 2016
;
Oliveto and Witt,, 2011
;
Oliveto and Witt,, 2012
)
for stochastic processes which often stay in the same state. By introducing the conditional probability of staying, we establish a novel drift theorem based on a bound of the drift that is given by the probability multiplied by a constant. We can obtain more detailed information about the drift compared to the original negative drift theorem, which bounds the drift by a constant value. This leads to a strict tail bound of the first hitting time in our analysis.

The derived drift theorem is as follows.

Theorem 4
.

Consider a stochastic process
(X^{(t)},\mathcal{F}^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with
X^{(0)}\leq 0
. Let
T
be a stopping time defined as

\displaystyle T=\min\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}:X^{(t)}\geq m\right\}\kern 5.0pt.

(27)

Assume that, for all
t
, there are constants
0<\varepsilon<m/2
and
0<c<m
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{(t+1)}-X^{(t)}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(t)}]

\displaystyle\leq-\varepsilon\Pr(X^{(t+1)}\neq X^{(t)}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(t)})

(28)

\displaystyle|X^{(t+1)}-X^{(t)}|

\displaystyle\leq c\qquad\text{w.p. 1}\kern 5.0pt.

(29)

Then, for all
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\Pr(T\leq n)\leq\frac{2mn}{\varepsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{m\varepsilon}{4c^{2}}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

(30)

The proof of Theorem
4
is shown in Section
D
in the appendix. Some existing works
(
Rowe and Sudholt,, 2014
;
Friedrich et al.,, 2017
)
reconstruct a Markov chain by ignoring the transition to the same state in the given Markov chain, called self-loops, and applied the negative drift theorem to the modified Markov chain. Although it seems intuitively correct, an explicit explanation was not provided. In contrast, we reformulate the theorem in
(
Fan et al.,, 2012
)
, which was applied to derive the negative drift theorem, to provide an unambiguous proof.

Runtime Analysis on Categorical OneMax

In the theoretical analysis of evolutionary algorithms for binary optimization, OneMax is often picked up as one of the simplest but most widely used benchmark functions to measure the search efficiency

In this section, we introduce a generalization of OneMax, termed Categorical OneMax (COM), defined in the categorical domain as

\displaystyle f_{\,\textsc{com}}(x)=\sum_{d=1}^{D}x_{d,1}\kern 5.0pt.

(31)

The function value of COM is the number of the optimal categories in the inputted categorical variable. Therefore, the distinction of the categories except for the optimal categories is not necessary for the analysis on COM because the function value is determined by whether the optimal categories are selected or not. This implies that the behavior of ccGA has some common properties with cGA optimizing OneMax. The next lemma shows the basic properties of the number of the optimal categories in samples, which are derived in a similar way to the analysis of cGA.

Lemma 3

Consider

under

under
E^{(t)}_{d}
and

, an upper bound of (

\displaystyle 4\eta X^{(t)}_{d}\cdot 2K/(c_{1}\sqrt{D}K\ln(DK))\kern 5.0pt.

(67)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{(t)}_{d}-X^{(t+1)}_{d}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(t)}]\mathbf{1}_{\{{E^{(t)}_{d}}\}}\geq\frac{\eta}{4\sqrt{D}}X^{(t)}_{d}\mathbf{1}_{\{{E^{(t)}_{d}}\}}\kern 5.0pt.

(68)

X^{(0)}_{d}=K-1

.

\displaystyle\Pr(T_{d}^{\prime}\geq u^{\prime})\leq(DK)^{-2}+4c_{3}D^{-2}K^{-4}\kern 5.0pt,

(71)

where the values in Theorem

.

Finally, we consider the probability that

, we derived

\displaystyle\Pr\left(T_{\mathrm{max}}<u\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{d=1}^{D}\Pr\left({T_{d}^{\prime}}\geq u^{\prime}\right)\geq 1-D^{-\frac{1}{2}}K^{-2}\geq 1-(DK)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\kern 5.0pt.

5.2
Lower Tail Bound of the Runtime on COM

5.2.1
Main Result and Outline of the Proof

In the analysis of the lower tail bound, we focus on the dynamics of
\sum^{D}_{d=1}\theta^{(t)}_{d,1}
and consider two phases in the optimization process.
The first phase is
\Theta(\ln K/\eta)
iterations and the second phase is the next
\Theta(\sqrt{D}/\eta)
iterations. In the first phase, we consider the event where
\sum^{D}_{d=1}\theta^{(t)}_{d,1}

with the initial state

. Then, in the second phase, we consider the event where
\sum^{D}_{d=1}\theta^{(t)}_{d,1}
is not updated over

from the end of the first phase. Separating the optimization process allows us to use different potential functions and different drift theorems, which eases the difficulty of the analysis. These potential functions are designed as functions of
\sum^{D}_{d=1}\theta^{(t)}_{d,1}
. Applying drift theorems twice (Theorem
1
and Theorem
9
) shows that the product event of the above two events occurs with high probability. Finally, the summation of lower tail bounds
\Theta(\ln K/\eta)
and
\Theta(\sqrt{D}/\eta)
leads to the lower tail bound of the runtime of ccGA on COM.

5.3
Discussion

The conditions of the learning rate in both of Theorem
5
and Theorem
6
are satisfied when
(\eta K)^{-1}\in\mathbb{N}
and

\displaystyle c\sqrt{D}K\ln(DK)\leq\eta^{-1}\leq(DK)^{c^{\prime}}

(108)

for properly chosen constants
c
and
c^{\prime}
. We note that the condition for the learning rate w.r.t.
K
is more severe than the condition w.r.t.
D
. This implies that, for the problems in a categorical domain, the order notations of the suitable learning rate settings in
D
and
K
may be different. With the condition (
108
), Theorem
5
and Theorem
6
show that the runtime of ccGA on COM is with high probability
O(\sqrt{D}\ln(DK)/\eta)
and
\Omega((\sqrt{D}+\ln K)/\eta)
, respectively. The upper and lower tail bounds of the runtime become
O(DK(\ln(DK))^{2})
and
\Omega((\sqrt{D}+\ln K)\sqrt{D}K\ln(DK))
when the learning rate is set as large as possible satisfying (
108
), i.e.,
\eta^{-1}\in\Theta(\sqrt{D}K\ln(DK))
.

Considering the case of
K=2
, Theorem
5
recovers the analysis result of the cGA optimizing OneMax on the binary domain
\mathbb{K}^{D}_{2}
, which is analyzed in
(
Sudholt and Witt,, 2019
, Theorem 5)
.
However, the target algorithm of the analysis in
(
Sudholt and Witt,, 2019
)
is the runtime of cGA with borders
\{1/D,1-1/D\}
.
Unfortunately, the upper bound in
(
Sudholt and Witt,, 2019
)
is
O(\sqrt{D}/\eta)
, which is smaller than ours with
K=2
by a factor of
\Theta(\ln D)
.
The analysis of the runtime which recovers this analysis result is one of our future works. Considering the lower tail bound in Theorem
6
with
K=2
, our result is consistent with the lower tail bound of the first hitting time of the cGA optimizing OneMax on the binary domain, which is derived in
(
Sudholt and Witt,, 2019
, Theorem 8)
.

When
K
is given by a polynomial in
D
, the upper and lower tail bounds of the runtime on COM are given by
\Theta(\sqrt{D}\ln D/\eta)
and
\Theta(\sqrt{D}/\eta)
, respectively, in which the ratio is given by
\Theta(\ln D)
. In contrast, when
\ln K\in\Theta(D^{c})
for some
c>1/2
, the upper tail bound is
O(D^{c+1/2}\ln D/\eta)
while the lower tail bound is
\Omega(D^{c}\ln D/\eta)
, in which the ratio is given by
\Theta(\sqrt{D})
. Therefore, the difference between the upper and lower tail bounds becomes large in order notation when
K
is super-polynomial in
D
.

Even when
K>2
, the behavior of ccGA on COM coincides with the behavior of cGA on OneMax on the binary domain with a biased initial distribution parameter as
\theta^{(0)}_{d,1}=1/K
and
\theta^{(0)}_{d,2}=(K-1)/K
. One may consider, therefore, our analysis is almost the same as the analysis of cGA.
To the best of our knowledge, the runtime analysis of cGA with a biased initial distribution parameter has not been performed.
This analysis result is useful since a comparison with the analysis of
KVal
described in Section
6
shows the relationship between
K
and the runtime may be the same between the different linear functions on the categorical domain.

6.2.1

The domino convergence

Then, we derive the upper bound of the conditional drift of the potential function in (

where

and

as

Then, Theorem

shows the upper bound of

with

.
Finally, applying Lemma

shows the main analysis result as follows.

6.3
Discussion

From Theorem
7
and Theorem
8
, we can see that the runtime of ccGA on
KVal
is
\Theta(D\ln K/\eta)
with high probability when the learning rate is set as
O(1/(DK^{2}\ln K\ln(DK)))
.
When
K=2
, Theorem
7
recovers the analysis result of
(
Witt,, 2018
)
, the runtime analysis of cGA on BinVal.
We note the derived bounds of the runtime on
KVal
are in the same order w.r.t.
K
,
\Theta(\ln K/\eta)
, as those on COM under the same learning rate setting.
However, when the learning rate is set as
\Theta(1/(DK^{2}\ln K\ln(DK)))
, the upper bound can be expressed as
O(D^{2}K^{2}(\ln K)^{2}\ln(DK))
, which is greater than that on COM.
This analysis result indicates that the dependency of
K
to the suitable setting of the learning rate differs among the class of the linear functions on the categorical domain, as well as
D
.

However, the lower tail bound on
KVal
is still not enough to show the difference in performance between COM and
KVal
w.r.t.
K
when using the ideal learning rate settings on each function. To prove this, we need to show that a too large learning rate leads to the inefficient optimization performance or the failure of the optimization, such as early convergence of the distribution parameter. This is discussed in
(
Witt,, 2018
, Theorem 3.4)
in the analysis of BinVal. The analysis of the condition for the learning rate is left as a future work.

In the analysis of cGA on BinVal,
(
Witt,, 2018
, Lemma 3.2)
shows
\theta^{(t)}_{d}
stays above a constant threshold
1/3
for all
d\in{\llbracket 1,D\rrbracket}
with high probability.
Ignoring constant factors in the threshold, this claim can be represented by combining Lemma
5
with
K=2
and Lemma
6
with
K=k=2
.
From another perspective, we extend
(
Witt,, 2018
, Lemma 3.2)
in two ways: one is to consider the lower tail bound of
\theta^{(t)}_{d,1}
as shown in Lemma

8
Conclusion

We investigated the runtime of ccGA, which is a probabilistic model-based algorithm with the family of the categorical distributions. We focused on two linear functions, COM and
KVal
, as representative examples of simple and relatively difficult linear functions. Our analysis results show that the runtime on COM is
O(\sqrt{D}\ln(DK)/\eta)
and
\Omega((\sqrt{D}+\ln K)/\eta)
with high probability when the learning rate satisfies
\eta\in O(1/(\sqrt{D}K\ln(DK))
and
\eta\in\Omega(1/(DK)^{c})
for some
c>0
. The runtime on
KVal
is
\Theta(D\ln K/\eta)
with high probability when
\eta\in O(1/(DK^{2}\ln K\ln(DK))
and
\eta\in\Omega(1/(DK)^{c})
.
These results recover the previous results of the analysis of cGA on
KVal
as a special case
K=2
in
(
Witt,, 2018
)
, while there is a gap of
\Theta(\ln D)
on COM compared to
(
Sudholt and Witt,, 2019
)
. We can confirm that the order of the tail bounds of the runtime on those functions is the same in
K
, unlike in
D
, when considering the same learning rate.
The analysis of the most efficient learning rates on COM and
KVal
is necessary to show the difference of the tail bounds of the runtimes, which is left as a future work.
Our analysis results indicate the recommended setting of the learning rate of ccGA, particularly when
D
and
K
are large. As the problems on the categorical domain appear in several research fields, the analysis of search efficiency is important.

As we delve into the conclusion of our study, it is important to introduce a new development.
A recent study by
Ben Jedidia et al., (2023)
tackled a mathematical runtime analysis on estimation-of-distribution algorithms (EDAs) for categorical variables, which are referred to as multi-valued variables in their work.
They showed that the multi-valued univariate marginal distribution algorithm (
r
-UMDA) solves the
r
-valued LeadingOnes problem in
O(r(\ln r)^{2}n^{2}\ln n)
function evaluations, where
n
and
r
are the number of dimensions and categories, respectively, corresponding to
D
and
K
in our paper.
Although the results are mostly disjoint from our work in that they only analyze the generalized version of UMDA, they also proposed the
r
-cGA, which is the generalized version of cGA and is mostly identical to ccGA.
While the
r
-cGA is proposed from a perspective different from that of the IGO, we should probably reconsider our notation, especially in terms of the number of categories.
Furthermore, it is also important to obtain additional insights through a comparison with the analysis by Ben Jedidia et al.

In this paper, we analyze the algorithm without the bound of the probabilities, which corresponds to the border in cGA. The effect of the bound is considered to be negligible since the results are derived under the condition that the probability of generating the optimal category does not become small. The analysis to deal strictly with the parameter correction by the bound is complex and is left for future work.
In addition, our analysis only covers the representative examples of the linear functions on the categorical domain. Therefore, the analysis of the runtime on the arbitrary linear function is one of our future works. We also consider the generalization of the sample size, particularly about the relation between the optimal sample size and
K
, as another future work.

(361)

\displaystyle T_{i}

\displaystyle=\min\{s\in\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket:Y^{(s)}_{i}\geq m\}

(362)

\displaystyle s_{i,r}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{s\in\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket:\sum^{s}_{j=0}\mathbf{1}_{\{{Y^{(j)}_{i}\neq Y^{(j-1)}_{i}}\}}\geq r\right\}\kern 5.0pt.

(363)

Considering the condition
\varepsilon<m/2
and the definition of
r
in (
361
), we have
r+1\leq 2m/\varepsilon
and
-1/r\leq-\varepsilon/(2m)
. Then

\displaystyle\Pr(T_{i}\leq\min\{n,s_{i,r}\}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(i)})

\displaystyle\leq(r+1)\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2}}{2rc^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{2m}{\varepsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{m\varepsilon}{4c^{2}}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

(364)

Further we derive an upper bound of the indicator of
T\leq n
using the indicator of
T_{i}\leq\min\{n,s_{i,r}\}
.
To simplify the notations, we introduce
\psi_{i}
and
\omega_{i}
defined as

\displaystyle\psi_{i}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}\leq\min\{n,s_{i,r}\}}\}}

(365)

\displaystyle\omega_{i}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}\kern 5.0pt.

(366)

We note
\omega_{0}=\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}
and
\omega_{i}=0
for
i\geq n-1
. Decomposing
\omega_{i}
shows

\displaystyle\omega_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}\leq\min\{n,s_{i,r}\}}\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>\min\{n,s_{i,r}\}}\}}\right)

(367)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}\leq\min\{n,s_{i,r}\}}\}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>\min\{n,s_{i,r}\}}\}}

(368)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}\leq\min\{n,s_{i,r}\}}\}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>\min\{n,s_{i,r}\}}\}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{{n\leq s_{i,r}}\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{{n>s_{i,r}}\}}\right)

(369)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}\leq\min\{n,s_{i,r}\}}\}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>n}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{n\leq s_{i,r}}\}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>s_{i,r}}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{n>s_{i,r}}\}}\kern 5.0pt.

(370)

The first term is given by
\psi_{i}
. The second term is
0
, since
T_{i}>n
and
T>i
means
T>n+i
, which is contradictory to
T\leq n
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\omega_{i}=\psi_{i}+\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>s_{i,r}}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{n>s_{i,r}}\}}\kern 5.0pt.

(371)

Moreover, when
T_{i}\geq s_{i,r}
, which holds
Y^{(s_{i,r})}_{i}<m
, we observe
X^{(s_{i,r}+i)}
becomes less than zero since

\displaystyle Y^{(s_{i,r})}_{i}=X^{(s_{i,r}+i)}+\varepsilon r\geq X^{(s_{i,r}+i)}+m\kern 5.0pt,

(372)

indicating

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>j}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{i,r}=j}\}}=\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>j}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i+j)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{i,r}=j}\}}\kern 5.0pt.

(373)

In addition, since
Y^{(s)}_{i}\geq X^{(s+i)}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>j}\}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>j}\}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\geq i}\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{{T<i}\}}\right)

(374)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>j}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\geq i}\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{{T<i}\}}

(375)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i+j}\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{{T<i}\}}\kern 5.0pt.

(376)

Therefore, we obtain an upper bound of the second term in (
371
) as

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>j}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{i,r}=j}\}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i+j)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{i}>j}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{i,r}=j}\}}

(377)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i+j)}\leq 0}\}}(\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i+j}\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{{T<i}\}})\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{i,r}=j}\}}

(378)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i+j)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i+j}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{i,r}=j}\}}

(379)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{X^{(i+j)}\leq 0}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{T>i+j}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{i,r}=j}\}}

(380)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\omega_{i+j}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{i,r}=j}\}}

(381)

Totally,

\displaystyle\omega_{i}\leq\psi_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\omega_{i+j}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{i,r}=j}\}}\kern 5.0pt.

(382)

Remaining
\omega_{0}=\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}
and
\omega_{n-1}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{{T\leq n}\}}

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{0}+\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n-1}\omega_{j_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{0,r}=j_{1}}\}}

(383)

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{0}+\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n-1}\psi_{j_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{0,r}=j_{1}}\}}+\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{n-1}\omega_{{j_{1}+j_{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{j_{1},r}=j_{2}}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{0,r}=j_{1}}\}}

(384)

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{0}+\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n-1}\psi_{j_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{0,r}=j_{1}}\}}+\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{n-1}\psi_{{j_{1}+j_{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{j_{1},r}=j_{2}}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{0,r}=j_{1}}\}}+\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n-1}\cdots\sum_{j_{l}=1}^{n-1}}_{\text{$l$ summations}}\psi_{\sum^{l}_{k=1}j_{k}}\prod^{l}_{k=1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{j_{k-1},r}=j_{k}}\}}+\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n-1}\cdots\sum_{j_{n-1}=1}^{n-1}}_{\text{$n-1$ summations}}\psi_{\sum^{n-1}_{k=1}j_{k}}\prod^{n-1}_{k=1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{j_{k-1},r}=j_{k}}\}}\kern 5.0pt.

(385)

Since
\prod^{l}_{k=1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{j_{k-1},r}=j_{k}}\}}
is
\mathcal{F}^{(\sum^{l}_{k=1}j_{k})}
measurable,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n-1}\cdots\sum_{j_{l}=1}^{n-1}\psi_{\sum^{l}_{k=1}j_{k}}\prod^{l}_{k=1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{j_{k-1},r}=j_{k}}\}}\right]

(386)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n-1}\cdots\sum_{j_{l}=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{\sum^{l}_{k=1}j_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(\sum^{l}_{k=1}j_{k})}\right]\prod^{l}_{k=1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{j_{k-1},r}=j_{k}}\}}\right]

(387)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n-1}\cdots\sum_{j_{l}=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{{T_{\sum^{l}_{k=1}j_{k}}\leq\min\{n,s_{{\sum^{l}_{k=1}j_{k}},r}\}}\}}\mid\mathcal{F}^{(\sum^{l}_{k=1}j_{k})}\right]\prod^{l}_{k=1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{j_{k-1},r}=j_{k}}\}}\right]

(388)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2m}{\varepsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{m\varepsilon}{4c^{2}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n-1}\cdots\sum_{j_{l}=1}^{n-1}\prod^{l}_{k=1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{j_{k-1},r}=j_{k}}\}}\right]

(389)

\displaystyle=\frac{2m}{\varepsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{m\varepsilon}{4c^{2}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n-1}\cdots\sum_{j_{l-1}=1}^{n-1}\prod^{l-1}_{k=1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{j_{k-1},r}=j_{k}}\}}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{j_{l}=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{j_{l-1},r}=j_{l}}\}}\right)}_{=\mathbf{1}_{\{{s_{j_{l-1},r}\in{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}}\}}\leq 1}\right]

(390)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2m}{\varepsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{m\varepsilon}{4c^{2}}\right)\kern 5.0pt,

(391)

where the last inequality is obtained by bounding the summations from above by
1
from the last summation by turn.
Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\psi_{0}]\leq\frac{2m}{\varepsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{m\varepsilon}{4c^{2}}\right)\kern 5.0pt,

(392)

we have

\displaystyle\Pr(T\leq n)\leq\frac{2mn}{\varepsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{m\varepsilon}{4c^{2}}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

(393)

This concludes the proof.
∎
