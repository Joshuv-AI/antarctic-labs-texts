Causal Estimation of Memorisation Profiles
Pietro Lesci, Clara Meister, Thomas Hofmann, Andreas Vlachos, Tiago Pimentel

Abstract
Understanding memorisation in language models has practical and societal implications, e.g., studying models’ training dynamics or preventing copyright infringements. Prior work defines memorisation as the causal effect of training with an instance on the model’s ability to predict that instance. This definition relies on a counterfactual: the ability to observe what would have happened had the model not seen that instance. Existing methods struggle to provide computationally efficient and accurate estimates of this counterfactual. Further, they often estimate memorisation for a model architecture rather than for a specific model instance. This paper fills an important gap in the literature, proposing a new, principled, and efficient method to estimate memorisation based on the difference-in-differences design from econometrics. Using this method, we characterise a model’s memorisation profile—its memorisation trends across training—by only observing its behaviour on a small set of instances throughout training. In experiments with the Pythia model suite, we find that memorisation (i) is stronger and more persistent in larger models, (ii) is determined by data order and learning rate, and (iii) has stable trends across model sizes, thus making memorisation in larger models predictable from smaller ones.

Introduction

Large language models (LMs) are often pretrained with a single pass on web-scale datasets
(
Raffel et al., 2020
;
Gao et al., 2020
;
Penedo et al., 2023
,
inter alia

Given the colossal size of these training sets, one may expect each individual instance to have little impact on the final model.
Yet, LMs can still reproduce entire sequences from their training set verbatim

Carlini et al., 2021
)
, suggesting that models can store, or
memorise
, precise knowledge about individual training instances.
In the era of large LMs, measuring memorisation is crucial for NLP practitioners; it has implications for copyright and data protection
(
Hu et al., 2022
;
Vyas et al., 2023
;
Lee et al., 2023
)
, for how models encode factual information
(
Cao et al., 2022
;
Tirumala et al., 2022
)
, and for understanding their training dynamics

;

One line of prior work has adopted a causal definition of
memorisation
: it is the causal effect of observing an instance during training on a model’s ability to correctly predict that instance
(
Feldman, 2020
)
.
Despite being an intuitive concept, quantification of this definition is not straightforward as it requires knowledge of a
counterfactual
:
1
1

1

Counterfactuals are thought experiments about what would have happened if a present condition were changed while keeping everything else unchanged
(
McCloskey, 2016
)
.

we must know how our model would have performed on a training instance had the model not been trained on it.
To overcome this challenge, prior work has proposed a variety of methods to estimate memorisation.
Some estimate it by training a model multiple times on different subsets of the data
(
Feldman and Zhang, 2020
;
Zheng and Jiang, 2022
, e.g.,)
, while others implicitly assume this counterfactual’s value to be negligible
(
Carlini et al., 2021
)
.
Both these approaches, however, have drawbacks:
the first computes memorisation for an architecture rather than a specific model, while the second relies on a strong assumption (we discuss this in detail in
§

5
).

In this paper, we first formalise
counterfactual memorisation
as the difference between two potential outcomes; notably, our formalisation generalises prior definitions of memorisation, allowing us to compare them within a unified framework.
We then draw from the econometrics literature
(
Callaway and Sant’Anna, 2021
)
and propose a new method which estimates memorisation using only observational data;
our method simply needs a model’s performance measurements (e.g., log-likelihood) on a subset of the training data throughout training.
The output of our method is what we term a
memorisation profile
: a model’s memorisation of training batches over the course of training.

Empirically, we use this method to analyse memorisation for models in the Pythia suite
(
Biderman et al., 2023b
)
and characterise their memorisation profiles; e.g.,

1

By studying these memorisation profiles we find that memorisation is stronger and more persistent in larger models.
Furthermore, both the learning rate and the position of an instance in the training set considerably impact how strongly that instance is memorised.
Finally, memorisation profiles are stable across model sizes; thus, we can predict memorisation in larger models from the memorisation observed in smaller ones.

Language Modelling

We start by providing some background on language modelling.

Let

At each iteration, we use a batch

.

2.2
Causal Analysis

Causal estimation is typically split into three steps.
First, we define a
causal estimand
, the target quantity we want to estimate.
Second, we state the assumptions needed to rewrite this causal estimand in terms of observable data, thus defining a
statistical estimand
; this process is called identification.
Finally, we define an
estimator
, a statistical procedure to approximate the statistical estimand.

To formally define memorisation as a causal estimand, we will use the
potential outcomes
framework of
Rubin (1974)
;
Rubin (2005)
.
4
4

4

For a comparison of causal frameworks, see
Ibeling and Icard (2023)
. For an introduction to causal inference, see
Pearl (2009)
and
Imbens and Rubin (2015)
.

This framework allows us to formally describe the causal effect of an intervention, or
treatment
, on some target quantity, or
outcome
.
In
§

1
, we defined memorisation as the causal effect of
training on
an instance on a model’s ability to
predict
it.
Thus, the act of training on
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
defines the treatment, while the model’s ability to predict an instance defines the outcome.

Since training is performed iteratively over batches, instances are treated at different timesteps.
Thus, we use a
treatment assignment variable

G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
to denote the step
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}
an instance is trained on.
Further, to quantify the ability of a model with parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}
to predict
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
, we use a performance function
\gamma
.
We then define the
outcome variable
as
Y_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{def}}}{{=}}}\gamma(\bm{\theta}_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
, and, unless noted otherwise, we set this performance function to be the log-likelihood of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
under
p_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\bm{\theta}_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}}}
:
\gamma(\bm{\theta}_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})=\log p_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\bm{\theta}_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
.
5
5

5

We experiment with other functions in the appendix.

To define memorisation we need to represent both observed—i.e.,
Y_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
—and counterfactual outcomes—i.e., the performance of the model on
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
had we not trained on it.
The potential outcomes notation
(
Splawa-Neyman, 1923
)
enables us to represent both types of outcomes consistently.

Definition 1
.

The
potential outcome
of an instance
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
at timestep
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}
under treatment assignment
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}
, denoted as
Y_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})
, is the value that the outcome would have taken if
G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
was equal to
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}
.

Since we only observe a single permutation of the data, we only see one specific treatment step for each instance, i.e.,
G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
.
Thus, the potential outcome of an instance is observed only for the actual treatment assignment
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}\!=\!G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
.
In this case, we can equate potential and observed outcomes, that is
Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})=Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
, a property called consistency
(
Cole and Frangakis, 2009
)
.
For any other treatment step
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}\mathop{\neq}G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
, the potential outcome is counterfactual and, thus, unobservable from the data.

3
Counterfactual Memorisation

Intuitively, counterfactual memorisation can be understood as the answer to the question: how would the model’s performance on instance
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
at timestep
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}
be different if we had not trained on it at timestep
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}
?
Using the potential outcomes notation, we formalise this definition as follows.

Definition 2
.

Counterfactual memorisation
is the causal effect of using instance
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
for training at the observed timestep
G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})\mathop{=}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}
on the model’s performance on this same instance at timestep
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}
:

\displaystyle\tau_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}\,\,\,\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{def}}}{{=}}}\underbrace{Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{performance on ${\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}$}\\
\text{when trained with ${\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}$}\end{subarray}}-\underbrace{Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{performance on ${\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}$}\\
\text{when not trained with ${\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}$}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!

(2)

In econometrics,
eq.

2
is called an individual treatment effect (ITE).
Notably, the first potential outcome in this equation,
Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})
, can be observed from the data since, by definition, we trained on
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
at timestep
G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})\mathop{=}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}
.
However, the second term,
Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})

To compute the ITE, we would need to estimate this counterfactual outcome for a specific instance, which is challenging due to unobserved factors and heterogeneity

Lu et al., 2018

While we would ideally estimate memorisation at the instance level, we focus on average effects instead, as is common in the econometrics literature

Definition 3
.

Expected counterfactual memorisation
is the average causal effect of using instances for training at timestep
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}

{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}

7
7

7

In econometrics,
eq.

3
is called an average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), as it is defined in terms of an expectation over

In other words, this expectation is taken with respect to the instance distribution conditioned on it being selected for training at step
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}
.
Assuming the training set is sampled i.i.d. and that its permutation is random (as discussed in
§

2.1

3

\displaystyle\tau_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{def}}}{{=}}}\expect_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}}\Big[Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})-Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})\mid G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})\mathop{=}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}\Big]

(3)

Together, the

Memorisation profiles and paths allow us to analyse a model’s memorisation patterns across different treatment and checkpoint steps.
Notably, there cannot be memorisation whenever

We term

when
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}>{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}

{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}={\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}

4.1
The Difference Estimator

Our first approach to estimate memorisation is straightforward and only requires the observed outcomes of a held-out validation set.
However, it relies on a strong identification assumption.

Assumption 1

(I.i.d. Dataset Sampling)
.

Instances
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
are independently and identically distributed, following
p({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
, and are randomly assigned to treatment groups
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}

Under this assumption, we have that

4.2
The Difference-in-Differences Estimator

We now introduce another causal estimator based on the difference-in-differences (DiD) design.
The intuition behind DiD is to use the time dimension to help with identification; DiD identifies a causal estimand using the difference in the
trends
over time of the outcome on treated vs. untreated instances.
In our specific setting, DiD relies on the assumption that changes in model performance over time would follow similar trends in different batches if they had not been used for training.
We formalise this assumption as follows.

Assumption 2

(Parallel Trends)
.

In the absence of training, the expected change in model performance across checkpoints would be the same regardless of treatment.
That is, for all
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c^{\prime}}\geq{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}\mathop{-}1
:

\displaystyle\expect_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}}\big[Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})-Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c^{\prime}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})\mid G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})\mathop{=}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}\big]

(8)

\displaystyle\qquad=\expect_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}}\big[Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})-Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c^{\prime}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})\mid\mathop{G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})\mathop{=}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty}}\big]

We need a second assumption before we can apply the DiD design to our setting.

Assumption 3

(No Anticipation)
.

Training has no effect before it happens.
That is, for all
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}<{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}

\displaystyle\expect_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}}\big[Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})\mid G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})\mathop{=}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}\big]

(9)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad=\expect_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}}\big[Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})\mid G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})\mathop{=}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}\big]

5.1
Previous Operationalisations of Counterfactual Memorisation

As mentioned before, estimating an instance’s memorisation
\tau_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}
is non-trivial due to the counterfactual component in its definition.
We avoid this issue by estimating expected memorisation
\tau_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}
instead.
Prior work
(
Feldman, 2020
;
Feldman and Zhang, 2020
;
Zhang et al., 2023
;
Lukasik et al., 2024
)
takes a different approach, comparing the performance of models trained with and without that instance.
In doing so, they average performance across training runs, measuring what we term
architectural counterfactual memorisation
.

Formally, let
\bm{\psi}
be a vector of variables responsible for training variance.
This includes, e.g., the data permutation induced by
\sigma
and the initial model parameters
\bm{\theta}_{0}
.
By defining a distribution
p(\bm{\psi})
over these variables, architectural memorisation can be defined as follows.

Definition 4
.

Architectural counterfactual memorisation
is the counterfactual memorisation
\tau_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}
when marginalising over training variables
\bm{\psi}
:

\displaystyle\tau_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{p(\bm{\psi})}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{def}}}{{=}}}\expect_{\bm{\psi}}\left[\tau_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}\mathop{\mid}G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})\mathop{\neq}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty}\right]

(12)

\displaystyle=\expect_{\bm{\psi}}\left[Y_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}))\mathop{-}Y_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})\mathop{\mid}G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})\mathop{\neq}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty}\right]

where
G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
in the first potential outcome depends on which batch the shuffling function
\sigma
puts
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
in.

Prior work has proposed a number of methods to estimate this value
(
Bachmann et al., 2022
;
Lin et al., 2022
;
Ilyas et al., 2022
;
Park et al., 2023
)
.
The simplest of these is to train several models while including or not
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
in the training set; these models are then used to approximate the expectation above.
We describe a statistical estimand and estimator for
\tau_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{p(\bm{\psi})}}
in
§

B.1
, discussing the assumptions needed by this approach.

Notably, this operationalisation has the advantage of estimating memorisation at the instance level.
However, it also has drawbacks—beyond just being computationally expensive to estimate.
These become apparent upon closer inspection of the definition of
\tau_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{p(\bm{\psi})}}
.
First, it does not provide insights into the effect of the checkpoint step or treatment step on memorisation;
this is because
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}
is hard-coded into
\tau_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{p(\bm{\psi})}}
’s definition and because it marginalises over permutations of the data.
While it is trivial to generalise this definition to other checkpoint steps
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}
or to specific
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}
, prior work has mainly focused on these choices, overlooking the impact of training dynamics on memorisation.
Further, and perhaps more importantly, marginalising over
p(\bm{\psi})
means that this metric quantifies memorisation for a model architecture, rather than for a specific model.

5.2
Influence Functions

Influence functions
(
Hampel, 1974
;
Cook and Weisberg, 1980
)
estimate—without re-training a model—how its parameters would change if an instance
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
was removed from the training set.
Specifically, the new set of parameters can be approximated as follows
(
Koh and Liang, 2017
)
:

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{-{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}\approx\bm{\theta}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}\,\mathrm{H}^{\mathop{-1}}_{\bm{\theta}}\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})

(13)

where
\mathrm{H}_{\bm{\theta}}
is the hessian of
\mathcal{L}
evaluated at
\bm{\theta}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}
.
This approximation is based on a first-order Taylor expansion of the training objective around
\bm{\theta}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}
, and should lead to small errors under the following assumptions:
(i) the loss function is strictly convex in
\bm{\theta}
, (ii)
\mathrm{H}_{\bm{\theta}}
is a positive-definite matrix, and (iii) the model has converged (i.e., the gradient is zero).
Given these assumptions, influence functions can be used to efficiently estimate the counterfactual term
Y_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})
in the definition of
\tau_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}
.
Specifically, we can measure the model’s performance using the updated parameters,
Y_{-{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{def}}}{{=}}}\gamma(\bm{\theta}_{-{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}};{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
, and equate
Y_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})=Y_{-{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
.
The influence function estimator of memorisation can then be written as:

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}^{\mathtt{infl}}=Y_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})-Y_{-{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})

(14)

We formalise this estimator and its statistical estimand in
§

B.2
.
Notably,
Zheng and Jiang (2022)
use a similar approach to estimate memorisation in a classification setting.

Influence functions thus provide a computationally efficient method to estimate instance-level counterfactual memorisation.
However, none of the required assumptions above is typically satisfied for LMs, which can lead to strong biases in this estimator
(
Basu et al., 2020
;
Bae et al., 2022
;
Schioppa et al., 2023
)
.
Moreover, assumptions (ii) and (iii) require
\bm{\theta}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}
to be locally optimal, meaning that this approach is not applicable for studying
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}<{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}
.
We therefore cannot use it to study how memorisation interacts with training dynamics.

5.3
Extractable Memorisation

Carlini et al. (2023)
defines memorisation as
(k,\!\ell)
-extractability; a string is
(k,\!\ell)
-extractable if the model correctly predicts
\ell
of its tokens given a prefix of
k
tokens.
This definition has recently gained much interest because of its relevance to copyright infringement and data protection.
Despite being seemingly different, we argue that extractable memorisation is an estimator for counterfactual memorisation.
Concretely, let performance
\gamma
be measured as whether a string is
(k,\!\ell)
-extractable.
Now, assume that a string is not
(k,\!\ell)
-extractable in the absence of training, i.e.,
Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})\mathop{=}0
.
Given this assumption, we can define an extractable memorisation estimator as:

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}^{\mathtt{extr}}=Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})

(15)

We formalise this estimator and its statistical estimand in
§

B.3
.

Extractable memorisation thus implicitly assumes that
Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})\mathop{=}0
.
Counterfactual memorisation, on the other hand, controls for this counterfactual.
Notably, assuming
Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})\mathop{=}0
may be reasonable when a string is long and complex; in this case, the chance that
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
would be in the model’s top-
1
beam (the output of greedy decoding) approaches zero.
However, this assumption may not be reasonable for shorter or less complex sequences. Rather, it may cause the resulting estimate to conflate memorisation with the intrinsic difficulty of predicting a string.

Residual memorisation estimates (defined in

as

Interestingly, while all macro-batches undergo some degree of instantaneous memorisation, it appears that many are then forgotten by the end of the first epoch, as shown by the statistically insignificant residual memorisation estimates.

We hypothesise that this recency effect can be explained by the learning rate schedule.
Specifically, when the learning rate is high, the optimisation process moves model parameters further in the locally optimal direction, thus

On the contrary, towards the end of the training process, when the learning rate is lower, previous information is
“forgotten”

Due to the cost of training large LMs, it is highly desirable to be able to make predictions about a trained model’s characteristics before undertaking training. One strategy is to derive insights from smaller models to inform the design of larger ones

Predictability across scales is visually apparent in

8
Conclusions

The memorisation of training data by neural networks has critical implications for privacy, copyright, and security. Thus, well-founded quantifications of memorisation, and corresponding accurate and efficient methods for their estimation are of great importance.
This work presents one such quantification and builds on the econometrics literature to derive an unbiased and efficient estimator of memorisation based on the difference-in-differences design.
We use this estimator to study the memorisation profiles of the Pythia model suite and find that memorisation is stronger and more persistent in larger models, determined by data order and learning rate, and stable across model sizes.

Limitations

This work estimates counterfactual memorisation in pretrained LMs.
Unfortunately, due to the costs associated with running large pretrained LMs—even in inference mode—we experimented with a limited number of models (the Pythia suite) trained in a single language (English).
Investigating whether other model architectures, training procedures, and natural languages result in similar memorisation profiles would be important to strengthen our conclusions.
Furthermore, when collecting the panel data needed to estimate memorisation, we subsampled the number of evaluated instances; this can significantly increase our estimators’ variance.

A.3
Variances of Estimators

We assume that all potential outcomes
Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})
have the same variance
\sigma^{2}
.
We now first look at the variance of the difference estimator.
To this end, let’s consider the variance of
\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\Big(\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|}\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})\right)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|^{2}}\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}\mathrm{Var}\Big(Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})\mid G({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}\Big)=\frac{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|\,\sigma^{2}}{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|^{2}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|}

(23)

This is simply the variance of estimating an expectation using the mean of
|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|
i.i.d. random variables, each with variance
\sigma^{2}
. We can similarly derive the variance of
\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})
.
The variance of
\widehat{\tau}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}^{\mathtt{diff}}
is then:

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\tau}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}^{\mathtt{diff}})=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty}}|}-2\,\mathrm{Cov}(\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}),\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty}))

(24)

Assuming batches
\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty}}
were drawn independently,
then the estimators
\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})
and
\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})
should also be independent.
Thus,
\mathrm{Cov}(\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}),\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty}))=0
.

We now look at the variance of the difference-in-differences estimator.
Let the correlation between
Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})
and
Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}-1}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})
be
\rho
.
These are, respectively, the potential outcomes of our model on a specific instance
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}
before and after training on it.
For shorthand, let
\Delta\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}=\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})-\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}-1}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})
and
\Delta\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty}=\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})-\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}-1}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})
.
We can show that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\Delta\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\Big(\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})-\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}-1}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})\Big)

(25a)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|}\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}{\Big(Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})-Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}-1}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})\Big)}\right)

(25b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|^{2}}\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}\bigg(\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{2}-2\,\mathrm{Cov}\Big(Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}),Y_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}-1}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})\Big)\bigg)

(25c)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|^{2}}\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}{(2\sigma^{2}-2\rho\sigma^{2})}=\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|}(1-\rho)

(25d)

We can derive the variance for
\Delta\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty}
in the exact same manner.
We thus have that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\tau}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}^{\mathtt{did}})=\mathrm{Var}(\Delta\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})+\mathrm{Var}(\Delta\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})-2\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g},\Delta\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})

(26)

Note that
\Delta\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}
and
\Delta\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty}
are estimated with independent samples, and thus,
\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g},\Delta\overline{Y}_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})=0
.
We can thus rewrite this estimator’s variance as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\tau}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}^{\mathtt{did}})=\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|}(1-\rho_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g})+\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{|\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}}|}(1-\rho_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty})

(27)

If we have
\rho_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}>0.5
and
\rho_{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\infty}>0.5
, then the variance of
\widehat{\tau}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}^{\mathtt{did}}
should be lower than that of the
\widehat{\tau}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}^{\mathtt{diff}}
.
This is a reasonable assumption since—for fixed timesteps
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}\mathop{-}1}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}
—there should be a strong relationship between a model’s performance on an instance before (
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}\mathop{-}1}
) and after (
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}
) it has been trained on due to factors such as vocabulary richness and grammatical structure.

B.2
Influence Functions

As mentioned in
§

5.2
, influence functions approximate
\bm{\theta}_{-{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}
using a first-order Taylor expansion of the training objective around
\bm{\theta}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}
.
This should lead to small errors under the following assumptions:
(i) the loss function is strictly convex in
\bm{\theta}
, (ii)
\mathrm{H}_{\bm{\theta}}
is a positive-definite matrix, and (iii) the model has converged
(
Koh and Liang, 2017
)
.
We make these assumptions explicit now.

Assumption 5

(Strict Convexity)
.

The loss function
\mathcal{L}
is strictly convex with respect to the parameters
\bm{\theta}
.

Assumption 6

(Local Optimality)
.

The parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}
locally minimise the loss function
\mathcal{L}
, meaning that the

\mathrm{H}_{\bm{\theta}}
is a positive-definite matrix and that gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}
is zero.

Given these assumptions, we can estimate the counterfactual term in
\tau_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}}
(in
eq.

2
) by computing the performance using the updated parameters
\bm{\theta}_{-{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}
.
As mentioned in the main text, we thus define
Y_{-{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})=\gamma(\bm{\theta}_{-{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}},\lx@scalerel@obj{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}T}}};{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\bm{x}})
and equate

On the next page in

We report the results for the entire training process—i.e., using all the available checkpoints:
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c}\in\{$0$,$1\text{\,}\mathrm{k}$,...,$143\text{\,}\mathrm{k}$\}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}g}\in\{$1\text{\,}\mathrm{k}$,...,$143\text{\,}\mathrm{k}$\}
.
We present the metrics from most coarse—i.e., average token accuracy (
Fig.

7a
)—to most fine-grained—i.e., sequence log-likelihood (
Fig.

7c
).

As shown in
Fig.

7
, different performance metrics result in distinct memorisation estimates. Specifically, finer-grained metrics—like sequence log-likelihood—allow us to detect smaller memorisation effects, and vice versa.
For example, average token accuracy, which is the most coarse-grained metric, mostly does not capture instantaneous memorisation for Pythia
410\text{\,}\mathrm{M}
. Instead, a finer-grained metric—like average token rank or sequence log-likelihood—detects additional effects.
Depending on the use-case different metrics might be appropriate.
For example, analogously to extractable memorisation
(
Carlini et al., 2021
)
, average token accuracy could be used to measure memorisation as it matches the specific use-case: detecting whether a model would generate a specific sequence when prompted with some of its tokens.
We chose sequence log-likelihood because it allows us to capture more fine-grained memorisation effects beyond the capability of the model to generate a specific sequence.
In particular, accuracy captures
“hard”
transitions in the model’s output by determining whether a token is the most likely in the model’s output distribution.
Log-likelihood, on the other hand, captures more nuanced impacts of training on an instance by assessing whether a token is more likely to be generated than it would be otherwise.
