Noise-like analytic properties of imaginary chaos
Juhan Aru, Guillaume Baverez, Antoine Jego, Janne Junnila

Abstract
. In this note we continue the study of imaginary multiplicative chaos μ β := exp ⁡ ( i ​ β ​ Γ ) \mu_{\beta}:=\exp(i\beta\Gamma) , where Γ \Gamma is a two-dimensional continuum Gaussian free field. We concentrate here on the fine-scale analytic properties of | μ β ​ ( Q ​ ( x , r ) ) | |\mu_{\beta}(Q(x,r))| as r → 0 r\to 0 , where Q ⁡ ( x , r ) Q(x,r) is a square of side-length 2 ​ r 2r centred at x x . More precisely, we prove monofractality of this process, a law of the iterated logarithm as r → 0 r\to 0 and analyse its exceptional points, which have a close connection to fast points of Brownian motion. Some of the technical ideas developed to address these questions also help us pin down the exact Besov regularity of imaginary chaos, a question left open in [ JSW20 ] . All the mentioned properties illustrate the noise-like behaviour of the imaginary chaos. We conclude by proving that the processes x ↦ | μ β ​ ( Q ⁡ ( x , r ) ) | 2 x\mapsto|\mu_{\beta}(Q(x,r))|^{2} , when normalised additively and multiplicatively, converge as r → 0 r\to 0 in law, but not in probability, to white noise; this suggests that all the information of the multiplicative chaos is contained in the angular parts of μ β ​ ( Q ​ ( x , r ) ) \mu_{\beta}(Q(x,r)) .



1.
Introduction and main results

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an open bounded simply connected domain and let
\Gamma
be the Gaussian free field in
D
with zero boundary condition. That is,
\Gamma
is a Gaussian, centred Schwartz distribution whose covariance is given by the Green function
G_{D}
of the Laplacian with Dirichlet boundary condition, in the sense that for any smooth test functions
\varphi
,
\psi
,

\mathbb{E}[(\Gamma,\varphi)(\Gamma,\psi)]=\int_{D\times D}\varphi(x)G_{D}(x,y)\psi(y)dx\,dy.

In this paper, we normalise the Green function so that
G_{D}(x,y)\sim-\log\left|x-y\right|
when
x-y\to 0
. For
\beta\in(0,\sqrt{2})
the imaginary chaos
\mu(x)
is formally defined as

e^{i\beta\Gamma(x)}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{x\in D\right\}}.

Because
\Gamma
is not defined pointwise, the exponential of
i\beta\Gamma
is
a priori
not well defined. A simple regularisation procedure helps out: for
\varepsilon>0
, let
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)
be the
\varepsilon
-circle average of
\Gamma
about
x\in D
; then
\mu
is defined as the limit

(1.1)

\mu(x)\qquad\coloneqq\qquad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-\beta^{2}/2}e^{i\beta\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{x\in D\right\}}

where the limit takes place in
L^{2}
in any negative Sobolev space
H^{s}
with
s<-1
; see
[
JSW20
, Section 3.1]
. In particular,
(\mu,\phi)
are well-defined random variables simultaneously for all smooth test functions
\phi
. Further, if
f=\mathbf{1}_{A}
is the indicator function of a Borel set
A\subset D
(or indeed any measurable bounded function), one can define
\mu(A)=(\mu,\mathbf{1}_{A})
as the
L^{2}
limit of
\varepsilon^{-\beta^{2}/2}\int_{D}fe^{i\beta\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}
.

Imaginary multiplicative chaos seems to have first come up in the context of directed polymers
[
DES93
]
, though now we could name it rather the total mass of imaginary multiplicative cascade. Imaginary multiplicative cascades, i.e. imaginary chaos for log-correlated fields with a hierarchical structure, were studied in
[
BJM10a
]
and subsequently in
[
BJ10
,
BJM10b
]
. A generalization to more general log-correlated fields came about in the context of complex chaos in
[
LRV15
]
. In
[
JSW20
]
the authors not only studied imaginary chaos as an interesting analytic object, but made a direct connection to the scaling limit of spins of the critical XOR-Ising model. Thereafter imaginary chaos has been studied in several papers - in
[
AJJ22
,
AJJ24
]
it was shown that
(\mu,f)
has a bounded smooth positive density on
\mathbb{C}
, in
[
AJ21
]
it was shown that imaginary chaos of the free field - in contrast to imaginary cascades - contains all the information of the underlying log-correlated field. Finally, due to its connections to Coloumb gases, imaginary chaos is now making appearance also in probabilistic constructions of 2D conformal field theories
[
GKR23
]
.

In this note we continue the study of imaginary multiplicative chaos.
We concentrate on the study of the process
(|\mu(Q(x,r))|)_{x\in D,r>0}
, where
Q(x,r)=x+[-r,r]^{2}
are squares of side length
2r
centred at
x
(squares are considered instead of discs so that we can refer to a result of
[
LSZ17
]
; see Theorem
1.6
below).
We will see that this process has a very noise-like character that does not exhibit in the
r\to 0
limit any non trivial macroscopic properties. Notice that for example the real part of
\mu(Q(x,r))
does give rise to the interesting macroscopic structure (
[
JSW20
]
), as probably does its angle. Imaginary multiplicative chaos is considerably harder to study than the real chaos due to the lack of monotonicity w.r.t. underlying field, and the cancellations that need to be taken into account carefully. For example, to prove the existence of density, one can use a simple Fourier argument in the case of real chaos, but one relies on Malliavin calculus in the case of imaginary chaos. In this note, we again need to combine (functional) analytic tools with probabilistic arguments; several small technical ideas and interplays arise, which we think might be of interest in studying random fields of similar nature.

We will next explain what is contained in the paper in terms of results, and also highlight some of the technical inputs going into proving these results.

1.1.
Monofractality

Our first main result proves a certain monofractality of the imaginary chaos
\mu
. To state this result, we first show that the process
(\mu(Q(x,r)))_{x\in D,r\geq 0}
has a version which is continuous. This is
a priori
not clear since
\mu
is not a random measure; see
[
JSW20
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Proposition 1.1

(Hölder regularity)
.

The map

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\{(x,r)\in D\times[0,1],Q(x,r)\subset D\}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{C}\\
(x,r)&\longmapsto&\mu(Q(x,r))\end{array}

possesses a continuous modification. Moreover, for all compact subsets
K
of
D
and
\alpha<1-\beta^{2}/4
, there exists a random constant
C_{K,\alpha}
, almost surely finite, such that for all
Q(x,r),Q(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\subset K
,

(1.2)

\left|\mu(Q(x,r))-\mu(Q(x^{\prime},r^{\prime}))\right|\leq C_{K,\alpha}(r\vee r^{\prime})^{\alpha}\left\|(x,r)-(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\right\|^{\alpha}.

As in the real case, also the imaginary chaos is believed to share a lot of properties with complex multiplicative cascades which have been thoroughly studied in
[
BJ10
,
BJM10a
,
BJM10b
]
. In particular, if we restrict ourselves to cascades built using complex exponentials of a normal distribution,
[
BJM10a
, Theorem 2.9]
establishes the monofractality of the cascade. Our first theorem claims that the same result holds for the imaginary chaos
\mu
.

Theorem 1.2

(Monofractality)
.

With probability one, simultaneously for all
x\in D
,

(1.3)

\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\left|\mu(Q(x,r))\right|}{\log r}=2-\beta^{2}/2.

A key technical input here is the existence and boundedness of density for imaginary chaos, i.e. the density of variables of the type
(\mu,f)
where
f
is a test function, first proved in
[
AJJ22
]
using Malliavin calculus. In Theorem
3.2
we revisit the original proof to bring out some quantitative bounds on the density that help us treat several simultaneous test functions at once.

We remark that the monofractality result contrasts sharply with the case of a two-dimensional real multiplicative
M_{\gamma}
defined by

M_{\gamma}(dx)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{\gamma^{2}/2}e^{\gamma\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)}dx

for
\gamma\in(0,2)
. Indeed, the quantity

\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\log M_{\gamma}(Q(x,r))}{\log r}

is known to depend heavily on the point
x
and takes values in
[(\sqrt{2}-\gamma/\sqrt{2})^{2},(\sqrt{2}+\gamma/\sqrt{2})^{2}]
(see
[
RV14
, Section 4]
for instance).

Remark 1.3
.

To get some intuition on Theorem
1.2
and its difference with respect to real chaos, we can consider near the origin the field
X
with correlations given by
\mathbb{E}\left[X(x)X(y)\right]=-\log\left|x-y\right|
. In that case, we can decompose
\mathbb{E}\left[X(x)X(y)\right]=-\log(\left|x-y\right|/r)+\log(1/r)
so that
X
is the independent sum
X=X_{r}+N_{r}
with

(X_{r}(rx),x\in\mathbb{R}^{2})\overset{\mathrm{(d)}}{=}(X(x),x\in\mathbb{R}^{2})\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad N_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\log(1/r)).

In particular,

\displaystyle\int_{Q(0,r)}e^{i\beta X(y)}dy=r^{2-\beta^{2}/2}e^{i\beta N_{r}}\int_{Q(0,1)}e^{i\beta X_{r}(ry)}dy,

where the
r^{-\beta^{2}/2}
comes from the renormalisation of
e^{i\beta X}
.
Therefore, in the case of imaginary chaos, the distribution of
r^{-2+\beta^{2}/2}\left|\int_{Q(0,r)}e^{i\beta X(y)}dy\right|
does not depend on
r
. In the case of real chaos, on the contrary,
the term
e^{\gamma N_{r}}
does not simplify and can be seen to be at the root of the multifractal behaviour.

1.2.
Law of the iterated logarithm and fast points

We next detail the study of the local behaviour of
\mu
by providing a law of the iterated logarithm for
\mu
.

Theorem 1.4

(Law of the iterated logarithm)
.

There exists
c^{*}(\beta)>0
depending only on
\beta
(see Theorem
1.6
below) such that for any given
x\in D
, we have

(1.4)

\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x,r))\right|}{r^{2-\beta^{2}/2}(\log\left|\log r\right|)^{\beta^{2}/4}}=c^{*}(\beta)^{-\beta^{2}/4}\quad\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

To prove this result, we again turn to the analytic toolbox and start off by making use of Sobolev embeddings to swap a uniform norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}
for a more tractable one. See the beginning of Section
4.2
for more details.

As a next step we study exceptional points where
\mu
has an atypical behaviour and breaks the law of the iterated logarithm. These points are an analogue of the fast points of Brownian motion
[
MP10
, Section 10.1]
- indeed, if one thinks of imaginary chaos as a noise-field,
then integrating it over squares would exactly give a 2D analogue for the definition of fast points. Whereas different in nature, in terms of being atypical, one can also recall here the notion of thick points of the GFF
\Gamma

[
HMP10
]
.

In the following, we will denote by
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(A)
the Hausdorff dimension of a set
A\subset\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem 1.5

(Exceptional points)
.

We have

(1.5)

\sup_{x\in D}\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x,r))\right|}{r^{2-\beta^{2}/2}\left|\log r\right|^{\beta^{2}/4}}=2^{\beta^{2}/4}c^{*}(\beta)^{-\beta^{2}/4}\quad\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

Moreover, if we define for
a>0
the set of
a
-fast points by

(1.6)

\mathcal{T}(a)\coloneqq\left\{x\in D:\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x,r))\right|}{r^{2-\beta^{2}/2}\left|\log r\right|^{\beta^{2}/4}}\geq a\right\},

then we have for all
0\leq a\leq\left(2/c^{*}(\beta)\right)^{\beta^{2}/4}
,

(1.7)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\left(\mathcal{T}(a)\right)=2-c^{*}(\beta)a^{4/\beta^{2}}\quad\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

The constant
c^{*}(\beta)
appearing in Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
comes from the asymptotics of the tails of the imaginary chaos derived in
[
LSZ17
, Corollary A.2]
. We now state this result precisely.
Consider the whole-plane GFF
\Gamma^{*}
and its associated imaginary chaos
\mu^{*}(y)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-\beta^{2}/2}e^{i\beta\Gamma^{*}_{\varepsilon}(y)}
, where
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}^{*}
is the
\varepsilon
-regularisation of
\Gamma^{*}
using circles averages. The whole-plane GFF
\Gamma^{*}
is a random generalised function modulo global additive constants. These additive constants disappear when one considers
\left|\int_{Q(0,1)}\mu^{*}(y)dy\right|
and so this latter object is a well-defined nonnegative real random variable.

Theorem 1.6

(
[
LSZ17
]
)
.

There exists a constant
c^{*}(\beta)>0
which may depend on
\beta
such that

(1.8)

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\int_{Q(0,1)}\mu^{*}(y)dy\right|>t\right)=\exp\left(-c^{*}(\beta)t^{4/\beta^{2}}+o\left(t^{4/\beta^{2}}\right)\right)\mathrm{~as~}t\to\infty.

The main result of
[
LSZ17
]
(Theorem 1 and Corollary 1.3 therein) actually provides extremely precise asymptotics for the moments of
\left|\int_{Q(0,1)}\mu^{*}(y)dy\right|^{2}
(which are closely related to 2D two-component plasma). The estimate (
1.8
) is then essentially shown to be a consequence of their moment estimates; see Appendix
B
for further comments and an alternative derivation.

1.3.
Exact Besov regularity of imaginary chaos

The fractal properties of imaginary chaos are tightly linked to its Besov regularity, a fact that can be interpreted in the broader context of the so-called “Frisch-Parisi conjecture” which relates more generally multifractal properties with Besov regularity (see e.g.
[
Jaf00
]
for an account).

In
[
JSW20
, Theorem 3.16]
, it was shown that for all
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
, it a.s. holds that
\mu
belongs to the Besov space
B^{s}_{p,q}(D)
if
s<-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}
and
\mu\not\in B^{s}_{p,q}(D)
if
s>-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}
. In this note, using ideas that we developed to prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
, we can settle the critical case
s=-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}
, i.e. we can decide for which values of
p,q\in[1,\infty]
it holds that
\mu\in B^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}_{p,q}(D)
. We also note that these values are the same as the ones found for the regularity of white noise
[
Ver10
]
. This is consistent with the fact that the imaginary chaos converges to white noise when
\beta\to\sqrt{2}

[
JSW20
]
.

Theorem 1.7

(Regularity of imaginary chaos)
.

Let
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
.
If
p<\infty
and
q=\infty
, then
\mu\in B^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}_{p,q,\loc}(D)
a.s. Otherwise,
\mu\not\in B^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}_{p,q,\loc}(D)
a.s.

1.4.
The limiting field of
(|\mu(Q(x,r))|)_{x,r}

We have seen that the absolute value of the imaginary chaos is monofractal. Here we ask the question of whether any interesting macroscopic information is left as
r\to 0
. For technical convenience, we work with the field
|\mu(Q(x,r)|^{2}
instead, and assume without loss of generality that
D
contains
[0,1]^{2}+B(0,2)
.

First, just because of scaling, this field, converges to
0
with no extra normalization. Further, when one normalizes by
\delta^{\beta^{2}-4}
, it converges to a constant field (this will actually follow from the proof below) and thus contains no interesting information. A natural question is whether one could regularize the process in a different manner and obtain an interesting limiting field. One option would be to look for instance at its oscillations around its mean.

We will show that, by subtracting the mean and renormalizing well, one indeed gets a non-trivial limit; however, the limiting field turns out to be just white noise. As the convergence takes place only in law and not in probability, it seems plausible to say that in the limit most of the information on the underlying GFF is lost. In fact, following the proof below, one could make it even precise, by showing that the same result also holds conditioned on any finite number of Fourier frequencies.

Theorem 1.8
.

The processes

(1.9)

W_{r}:=(r^{\beta^{2}-5}(|\mu(Q(x,r))|^{2}-\mathbb{E}(|\mu(Q(x,r))|^{2})))_{x\in[0,1]^{2}}

converge as
r\to 0
in distribution, but not in probability, in the space
H^{-2}_{0}([0,1]^{2})
, to a constant multiple of the White noise in
[0,1]^{2}
.

Although the path towards a proof is rather clear, given the independence properties noticed in earlier parts of the paper, it is technically non-trivial to push through. For example some symmetrization tricks are needed to identify cancellations and obtain precise enough estimates; see the paragraph following (
7.1
) for more details.

Finally, let us also mention a related result but with a different flavour from
[
JSW20
]
: when
\beta\to\sqrt{2}
, one can renormalise the imaginary chaos so that it converges to white noise.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notational convention:

In this article, we will use two different normalisations for the exponential of
i\beta\Gamma
:

(2.1)

e^{i\beta\Gamma(x)}\quad=\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-\beta^{2}/2}e^{i\beta\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad:e^{i\beta\Gamma(x)}:\quad=\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}e^{\beta^{2}\mathrm{Var}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x))/2}e^{i\beta\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)},

where
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
stands for the
\varepsilon
-circle average of
\Gamma
. The second normalisation is the so-called Wick normalisation and is distinguished from the first one by the two colons on both sides of the exponential.
These two normalisations lead to equivalent generalised functions in the sense that they are related by

(2.2)

e^{i\beta\Gamma(x)}\quad=\quad\CR(x,D)^{-\beta^{2}/2}:e^{i\beta\Gamma(x)}:.

These normalisations have their own advantages. For instance,
e^{i\beta\Gamma}
has a neat spatial Markov property, whereas the moments of
:e^{i\beta\Gamma}:
have a simpler expression.

2.2.
General moment bounds for imaginary chaos

We start this preliminary section by collecting some known estimates on the moments of the imaginary chaos. Consider a log-correlated field
X
defined on some 2D open set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. To be more specific and to be able to apply the existing literature, we assume that the covariance of
X
can be written as

-\log|x-y|+g(x,y)

where
g\in C^{2}(D\times D)\times L^{1}(D\times D)
is bounded from above.
For any
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in D^{N}
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{M})\in D^{M}
, denote by

(2.3)

\mathcal{E}(X;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\mathbb{E}[X(x_{i})X(x_{j})]-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq M}\mathbb{E}[X(y_{i})X(y_{j})]+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq N\\
1\leq j\leq M\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}[X(x_{i})X(y_{j})].

We will write
d\mathbf{x}=dx_{1}\dots dx_{N}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
f\in L^{\infty}(D)
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int:e^{i\beta X(x)}:f(x)dx\right|^{2N}\right]=\int_{D^{N}\times D^{N}}\,d\mathbf{x}\,d\mathbf{y}\,e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}(X;\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})}\prod_{i=1}^{N}f(x_{i})\overline{f(y_{i})}.

Proof.

See
[
JSW20
, Section 3.2]
.
∎

To bound the moments, we will moreover use the following Onsager inequality:

Lemma 2.2

(
[
JSW20
]
, Proposition 3.6 (i))
.

Let
K
be a compact subset of
D
and
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in D^{N}\times D^{N}
. Denote by
z_{i}=x_{i}
and
z_{N+i}=y_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,N
. Then

\mathcal{E}(X;\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})\leq-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\log\min_{i\neq j}|z_{i}-z_{j}|+CN

for some constant
C>0
depending only on
g
and
K
.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
JSW20
]
, Lemma 3.10)
.

Let
B(0,1)
be the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For all
\beta\in(0,\sqrt{2})
, there exists
C=C(\beta,d)>0
such that for all
p\geq 1
,

\int_{B(0,1)^{p}}d\mathbf{z}\exp\Big(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\log\min_{i\neq j}|z_{i}-z_{j}|\Big)\leq C^{p}p^{\beta^{2}p/4}.

Notation 2.4
.

For
x,y\in D
, we will denote by

(2.4)

G_{D}^{\prime}(x,y)=G_{D}(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}\log\CR(x,D)-\frac{1}{2}\log\CR(y,D).

For any
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in D^{N}
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{M})\in D^{M}
, denote by

(2.5)

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Gamma;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}G_{D}^{\prime}(x_{i},x_{j})-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq M}G_{D}^{\prime}(y_{i},y_{j})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq N\\
1\leq j\leq M\end{subarray}}G_{D}^{\prime}(x_{i},y_{j}).

By Lemma
2.1
and the difference of normalisation (
2.2
) between
\mu
and the Wick normalisation of
e^{i\beta\Gamma}
, we have for all test function
f:D\to\mathbb{R}
,

(2.6)

\mathbb{E}\left|\int_{D}\mu(x)f(x)\,dx\right|^{2}=\int_{D\times D}e^{\beta^{2}G_{D}^{\prime}(x,y)}f(x)f(y)\,dx\,dy

and, more generally, for all
N\geq 1
,

(2.7)

\mathbb{E}\left|\int_{D}\mu(x)f(x)\,dx\right|^{2N}=\int_{D^{N}\times D^{N}}e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Gamma;\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})}\prod_{j=1}^{N}f(x_{j})f(y_{j})\,d\mathbf{x}\,d\mathbf{y}.

2.3.
Moment bounds for rectangles

We will also need several times to control how the moments of imaginary chaos depend on the side-lengths of a rectangle. This is given by the following lemma:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
K
be a compact subset of
D
. There exists
C=C(K,\beta)
such that the following holds. For any
a,b\in(0,1]
, any
a\times b
rectangle
Q\subset K
and any
N\geq 1
, we have

\mathbb{E}|\mu(Q)|^{2N}\leq C^{N}(ab)^{(2-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2})N}N^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}N}.

Before proving Lemma
2.5
, we first recall the following recursive inequality which works for any log-correlated field:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\nu
be an imaginary chaos defined on some domain
D
(with the Wick normalisation) and
A\subset D
be a measurable set.
Let
m\geq 1
and
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}
be disjoint measurable subsets of
A
. Then for all
N_{1},\dots,N_{m}\geq 1
, we have

\frac{1}{(N!)^{2}}\mathbb{E}|\nu(A)|^{2N}\geq\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{(N_{j}!)^{2}}\mathbb{E}|\nu(A_{j})|^{2N_{j}},\quad\text{where}\quad N=N_{1}+\dots+N_{m}.

Proof.

See the proof of
[
JSW20
, Proposition 3.14]
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.5
.

It will be useful for us to compare the field
\Gamma_{D}
in
K
with a stationary log-correlated field.
Such a comparison is given by
[
AJJ22
, Theorem 4.5]
, which allows us to write in
K
the decomposition

(2.8)

\Gamma_{D}=Z+Y,

where
Z
and
Y
are two independent fields with the following properties.
The field
Y
is Hölder-regular.
On the other hand,

Z
is a field defined on the entire space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose covariance is of the form

(2.9)

\mathbb{E}Z(x)Z(y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}k(e^{u}(x-y))(1-e^{-\delta u})\,du

for some
\delta>0
and some rotationally invariant and compactly supported seed covariance
k\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
with
k(0)=1
(see
[
AJJ22
, Assumption 4.2]
for the list of properties satisfied by
k
).
In this proof, the property that will be the most useful for us is the fact that the law of
Z
is invariant under translations.

Let
Q
be a rectangle included in
K
. Let us first show that

(2.10)

\mathbb{E}|\mu(Q)^{2N}|\leq C^{N}\mathbb{E}\big|\int_{Q}:e^{i\beta Z(x)}:\,dx\big|^{2N}.

By independence of
Z
and
Y
and by Lemma
2.1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\mu(Q)^{2N}|

\displaystyle=\int_{Q^{N}\times Q^{N}}d\mathbf{x}\,d\mathbf{y}e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}(Z;\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\CR(x_{j},D)^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}\CR(y_{j},D)^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\beta Y(x_{1})}\dots e^{i\beta Y(x_{N})}e^{-i\beta Y(y_{1})}\dots e^{-i\beta Y(y_{N})}\right].

By Jensen’s inequality, the expectation in the integrand is at most 1. The remaining terms being nonnegative and
\sup_{x\in K}\CR(x,D)^{-\beta^{2}/2}
being finite, we deduce that
\mathbb{E}|\mu(Q)^{2N}|
is at most

C^{N}\int_{Q^{N}\times Q^{N}}d\mathbf{x}\,d\mathbf{y}e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}(Z;\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})}=C^{N}\mathbb{E}\big|\int_{Q}:e^{i\beta Z(x)}:\,dx\big|^{2N},

as stated in (
2.10
).

It is now enough to show that for any
a\times b
rectangle
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(2.11)

\mathbb{E}\big|\int_{Q}:e^{i\beta Z(x)}:\,dx\big|^{2N}\leq C^{N}(ab)^{(2-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2})N}N^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}N}.

The square
[0,1]^{2}
contains
m=\lfloor\frac{1}{a}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{1}{b}\rfloor
disjoint rectangles of size
a\times b
.
Then by Lemma
2.6
and stationarity we have

\frac{1}{((mN)!)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big|\int_{[0,1]^{2}}:e^{i\beta Z(x)}:\,dx\Big|^{2mN}\geq\Big(\frac{1}{(N!)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big|\int_{Q}:e^{i\beta Z(x)}:\,dx\Big|^{2N}\Big)^{m},

which gives us

(2.12)

\mathbb{E}\Big|\int_{Q}:e^{i\beta Z(x)}:\,dx\Big|^{2N}\leq\frac{(N!)^{2}}{((mN)!)^{2/m}}\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big|\int_{[0,1]^{2}}:e^{i\beta Z(x)}:\,dx\Big|^{2Nm}\Big)^{1/m}.

By applying
[
JSW20
, Theorem 1.3]
(or, equivalently, Lemmas
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
) we have

\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big|\int_{[0,1]^{2}}:e^{i\beta Z(x)}:\,dx\Big|^{2Nm}\Big)^{1/m}\leq C^{N}(Nm)^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}N}

for some constant
C>0
.
One easily checks by using Stirling’s formula that the remaining multiplicative factor on the right hand side of (
2.12
) is at most
C^{N}m^{-2N}
, concluding the proof of (
2.11
).
This finishes the proof of Lemma
2.5
.
∎

2.4.
Contribution of the harmonic extension to imaginary chaos

The proofs of some of our main results are based on the spatial Markov property which allows us to find a decomposition analogous to the one presented in Remark
1.3
. The difference here is that the field
N_{r}
corresponding to the harmonic part of the GFF is no longer constant. However, as it does not vary too rapidly we can recover an argument similar to Remark
1.3
. This is the content of the next lemma. We will need this lemma for both the GFF
\Gamma
in
D
with Dirichlet boundary conditions and for the whole-plane GFF
\Gamma^{*}
(see
[
MS17
, Proposition 2.8]
for the spatial Markov property for
\Gamma^{*}
).

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
x\in D
and
R_{0}>0
such that
Q(x,R_{0})\subset D
. Let
0<2r<R<R_{0}
. We decompose the GFF
\Gamma
in
D
and the whole-plane GFF
\Gamma^{*}
as the independent sums

\Gamma=\Gamma^{(R)}+h^{(R)}\quad\mathrm{~and~}\quad\Gamma^{*}=\Gamma^{(R)}+{h^{*}}^{(R)}

where
\Gamma^{(R)}
is a GFF in
Q(x,R)
and
h^{(R)}
and
{h^{*}}^{(R)}
are harmonic in
Q(x,R)
. By denoting
\mu^{(R)}
the imaginary chaos associated to
\Gamma^{(R)}
, there exist
c,c_{R_{0}}>0
which may depend on
\beta
and on
\beta,R_{0}
respectively such that for all
\lambda\geq(r/R)^{1/2}
,

(2.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left|\mu(Q(x,r))\right|-\left|\mu^{(R)}(Q(x,r))\right|\right|>\lambda r^{2-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{c_{R_{0}}}\exp\Big(-c_{R_{0}}\Big(\frac{R}{r}\lambda^{2}\Big)^{\frac{2}{\beta^{2}+1}}\Big),

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left|\mu^{*}(Q(x,r))\right|-\left|\mu^{(R)}(Q(x,r))\right|\right|>\lambda r^{2-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{c}\exp\Big(-c\Big(\frac{R}{r}\lambda^{2}\Big)^{\frac{2}{\beta^{2}+1}}\Big).

Proof of Lemma
2.7
.

We start by proving (
2.13
) whose proof is divided into two main steps. Firstly, we will give quantitative estimates saying that
h^{(R)}
does not vary too much on
Q(x,r)
. We will then use Markov’s inequality and compute moments of integrals of the imaginary chaos. This has the advantage of transforming oscillating integrals into integrals of nonnegative functions which are easier to handle.

We start by proving the following claim: there exists
C_{R_{0}}>0
which may depend on
R_{0}
s.t.

(2.15)

\sup_{y\in Q(x,r)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(h^{(R)}(y)-h^{(R)}(x)\right)^{2}\right]\leq C_{R_{0}}\frac{r}{R}.

An exact computation shows that for
y,z\in Q(x,r)
,

(2.16)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(h^{(R)}(y)-h^{(R)}(x)\right)^{2}\right]=2G_{Q(x,R)}(x,y)-2G_{D}(x,y)+\log\frac{\CR(x,D)\CR(y,D)}{\CR(x,Q(x,R))\CR(y,Q(x,R))}.

Let
\varphi:D\to\mathbb{D}
be a conformal map. By conformal invariance of the Green function,

\displaystyle 2G_{D}(x,y)-\log(\CR(x,D)\CR(y,D))

\displaystyle=2\log\frac{\left|1-\overline{\varphi(x)}\varphi(y)\right|}{\left|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\right|}+\log\left(\left|\varphi^{\prime}(x)\right|\left|\varphi^{\prime}(y)\right|\right)-\log\left(1-\left|\varphi(x)\right|^{2}\right)\left(1-\left|\varphi(y)\right|^{2}\right).

In the following, we will make computations which are justified because
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(D)
and
\varphi^{-1}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
. The error terms will thus depend on
R_{0}
.
We have

\log\left|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\right|=\log\left|\varphi^{\prime}(x)\right|+\log\left|x-y\right|+O(r)

and also,

\log\left|1-\overline{\varphi(x)}\varphi(y)\right|=\log\left(1-\left|\varphi(x)\right|^{2}\right)+O\left(\frac{\left|y-x\right|}{1-\left|\varphi(x)\right|^{2}}\right)=\log\left(1-\left|\varphi(x)\right|^{2}\right)+O(r).

Hence

2G_{D}(x,y)-\log(\CR(x,D)\CR(y,D))=\log\frac{1}{\left|x-y\right|}+O(r).

As
r<R/2
,
x
and
y
are in the bulk of
Q(x,R)
and we can show similarly that

2G_{Q(x,R)}(x,y)-\log(\CR(x,Q(x,R))\CR(y,Q(x,R)))=\log\frac{1}{\left|x-y\right|}+O\left(\frac{r}{R}\right).

Coming back to (
2.16
), it shows (
2.15
).

We now come back to the core of the proof of (
2.13
). In the rest of the proof, the constants may depend on
\beta
and on
R_{0}
.
Let
\lambda>0
.
By the triangle inequality, the probability on the left hand side of (
2.13
) is at most

(2.17)

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\int_{Q(x,r)}\mu^{(R)}(y)\left(e^{i\beta(h^{(R)}(y)-h^{(R)}(x))}-1\right)dy\right|>\lambda r^{2-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}\right).

Let
N\geq 1
.
Recall the notation
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Gamma^{(R)};\mathbf{y};\mathbf{z})
defined in (
2.5
).
As
\mu^{(R)}
and
h^{(R)}
are independent, we have by Lemma
2.1
,

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{Q(x,r)}\mu^{(R)}(y)\left(e^{i\beta(h^{(R)}(y)-h^{(R)}(x))}-1\right)dy\right|^{2N}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{Q(x,r)^{N}\times Q(x,r)^{N}}d\mathbf{y}\,d\mathbf{z}\,e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Gamma^{(R)};\mathbf{y};\mathbf{z})}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(e^{i\beta(h^{(R)}(y_{j})-h^{(R)}(x))}-1\right)\left(e^{-i\beta(h^{(R)}(z_{j})-h^{(R)}(x))}-1\right)\right].

By a generalised Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we can bound the expectation on the right hand side of the above display by

\max_{y\in Q(x,r)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|e^{i\beta(h^{(R)}(y)-h^{(R)}(x))}-1\right|^{2N}\right].

Denoting
\Gamma^{Q(0,1)}
the GFF in the unit square, we deduce by scaling that (
2.18
) is at most

\displaystyle C^{N}R^{(2-\beta^{2}/2)2N}\max_{y\in Q(x,r)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|e^{i\beta(h^{(R)}(y)-h^{(R)}(x))}-1\right|^{2N}\right]\int_{Q(0,\frac{r}{R})^{N}\times Q(0,\frac{r}{R})^{N}}d\mathbf{y}\,d\mathbf{z}\,e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Gamma^{Q(0,1)};\mathbf{y};\mathbf{z})}.

Using (
2.15
), we have

(2.19)

\max_{y\in Q(x,r)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|e^{i\beta(h^{(R)}(y)-h^{(R)}(x))}-1\right|^{2N}\right]\leq C^{N}\max_{y\in Q(x,r)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|h^{(R)}(y)-h^{(R)}(x)\right|^{2N}\right]\leq(N-1)!!\left(C\frac{r}{R}\right)^{N},

where
(N-1)!!
is the double factorial of
N-1
, i.e. the product of all positive integers smaller than
N-1
with the same parity as
N-1
.
Moreover, by Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
and by scaling, we can bound

\displaystyle\int_{Q(0,\frac{r}{R})^{N}\times Q(0,\frac{r}{R})^{N}}d\mathbf{y}\,d\mathbf{z}\,e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Gamma^{Q(0,1)};\mathbf{y};\mathbf{z})}\leq C^{N}\int_{Q(0,\frac{r}{R})^{2N}}d\mathbf{y}\prod_{j=1}^{2N}\left(\min_{k\neq j}\left|y_{k}-y_{j}\right|\right)^{-\beta^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\qquad=C^{N}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{(2-\beta^{2}/2)2N}\int_{Q(0,1)^{2N}}d\mathbf{y}\prod_{j=1}^{2N}\left(\min_{k\neq j}\left|y_{k}-y_{j}\right|\right)^{-\beta^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C^{N}N^{N\beta^{2}/2}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{(2-\beta^{2}/2)2N}.

Putting things together, it implies that there exists
C>0
such that the expectation in (
2.18
) is not larger than

(2.20)

(N-1)!!N^{N\beta^{2}/2}\left(C\frac{r}{R}r^{(2-\beta^{2}/2)2}\right)^{N}.

By Markov’s inequality and using that
(N-1)!!\leq C^{N}N^{N/2}
, we deduce that the probability on the left hand side of (
2.13
) is at most

\Big(C\frac{r}{R}\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\Big)^{N}N^{(\beta^{2}+1)N/2}.

We optimise in
N
and choose
N=\left\lfloor e^{-1}(\lambda^{2}\frac{R}{r})^{2/(\beta^{2}+1)}\right\rfloor
which leads to the estimate on the right hand side of (
2.13
).
It concludes the proof of (
2.13
).

The proof of (
2.14
) about the whole-plane GFF
\Gamma^{*}
is simpler since there is no boundary to deal with and follows from the same lines.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
0<2r<R
and denote by

and where

.

\varepsilon>0
.
The probability in (

1.8
) concerns the whole-plane GFF

3.
Hölder regularity and monofractality: proof of Proposition
1.1
and Theorem
1.2

We start this section by proving Proposition
1.1
.

Proof of Proposition
1.1
.

Fix a compact subset
K
of
D
and
N\geq 1
. If
A=Q(x,r)
and
A^{\prime}=Q(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})
are two squares included in
K
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mu(A)-\mu(A^{\prime})\right|^{2N}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mu(A\backslash A\cap A^{\prime})-\mu(A^{\prime}\backslash A\cap A^{\prime})\right|^{2N}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2N-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mu(A\backslash A\cap A^{\prime})\right|^{2N}+\left|\mu(A^{\prime}\backslash A\cap A^{\prime})\right|^{2N}\right].

A\backslash A\cap A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime}\backslash A\cap A^{\prime}
can be covered by finitely many
a\times b
rectangles with either
a\leq 2(r\vee r^{\prime})
and
b\leq C\left\|(x,r)-(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\right\|
, or the bounds swapped for
a
and
b
.
By Lemma
2.5
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mu(A)-\mu(A^{\prime})\right|^{2N}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{K,N}\left\{(r\vee r^{\prime})\left\|(x,r)-(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\right\|\right\}^{(2-\beta^{2}/2)N}.

With the help of Lemma
A.1
which is a slight modification of Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem, it shows that
(x,r)\mapsto\mu(Q(x,r))
restricted to the set
\{(x,r):Q(x,r)\subset K\}
possesses a continuous modification which satisfies (
1.2
). It concludes the proof.
∎

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

First, by applying (
1.2
) to
(x,r)
and
(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})=(x,0)
, we see that for all compact subset
K
of
D
,
\alpha<1-\beta^{2}/4
, there exists a random constant
C_{K,\alpha}
finite a.s. such that for all
Q(x,r)\subset K
,

|\mu(Q(x,r))|\leq C_{K,\alpha}r^{2\alpha}.

The lower bound of (
1.3
) then follows. It thus only remains to prove the upper bound of (
1.3
).
Let
\Gamma^{*}
be the whole-plane GFF and let
\mu^{*}
be the associated imaginary chaos. As with the the GFF
\Gamma
in the domain
D
, we can make sense of
\mu^{*}(Q(x,r))
simultaneously for all
x\in\mathbb{C}
and
r>0
. Moreover, for all compact sets
K\subset\mathbb{C}
and
\alpha<1-\beta^{2}/4
, there exists a random constant
C_{K,\alpha}
almost surely finite such that for all
Q(x,r),Q(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\subset K
,

(3.1)

\left|\mu^{*}(Q(x,r))-\mu^{*}(Q(x^{\prime},r^{\prime}))\right|\leq C_{K,\alpha}(r\vee r^{\prime})^{\alpha}\left\|(x,r)-(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\right\|^{\alpha}.

We are going to prove that almost surely, simultaneously for all
x\in D
,

(3.2)

\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\left|\mu^{*}(Q(x,r))\right|}{\log r}\leq 2-\beta^{2}/2.

It will be convenient to consider the whole-plane GFF instead of
\Gamma
because of its translation and scaling invariance.
We now explain how we conclude from (
3.2
) the proof of the upper bound of (
1.3
). By spatial Markov property (
[
MS17
, Proposition 2.8]
), we can couple the two GFFs
\Gamma^{*}
and
\Gamma
such that
\Gamma^{*}=\Gamma+h
and such that
\Gamma
and
h
are independent and
h
is harmonic in
D
. We claim that the map

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\{(x,r)\in D\times[0,1],Q(x,r)\subset D\}&\to&\mathbb{C}\\
(x,r)&\mapsto&e^{-i\beta h(x)}\mu^{*}(Q(x,r))-\mu(Q(x,r))\end{array}

possesses a continuous modification. Moreover, for all compact subsets
K
of
D
and
\alpha<3-\beta^{2}/2
, there exists a random constant
C_{K,\alpha}
almost surely finite such that for all
Q(x,r)\subset K
,

\left|e^{-i\beta h(x)}\mu^{*}(Q(x,r))-\mu(Q(x,r))\right|\leq C_{K,\alpha}r^{\alpha}.

The proof of this claim is very similar to the proof of Proposition
1.1
with the additional ingredient (which is similar to (
2.15
)) that for all compact subsets
K
of
D
, there exists
C_{K}>0
such that for all
Q(x,r)\subset K
,

\sup_{y\in Q(x,r)}\mathbb{E}\left[(h(x)-h(y))^{2}\right]\leq C_{K}r.

We omit the details of the proof of this claim. Since
\alpha
can be chosen larger than
2-\beta^{2}/2
, we now see that this claim and (
3.2
) imply the desired upper bound of (
1.3
).

The rest of the proof will consist in proving (
3.2
). Let us denote by

\xi=2-\beta^{2}/2.

Let
\delta>0
and
K
be an integer larger than
1/\delta-1
. We are going to bound the probability of the event that there exists
x\in D
such that

\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\left|\mu^{*}(Q(x,r))\right|}{\log r}\geq\xi+2\delta.

For such an
x
, we have for small enough
r>0
,

(3.3)

\left|\mu^{*}(Q(x,r))\right|\leq r^{\xi+\delta}.

Let
p\geq 1
,
z\in 2^{-2p}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be such that
\left|z-x\right|\leq 2^{-2p}
. For all
j=0,\dots,2K
, define
r_{j}:=2^{-p-j}
. By (
3.1
) with
\alpha>(\xi+\delta)/3
, there exists a random constant
C(\omega)
a.s. finite such that for all
j=0,\dots,2K
,

\displaystyle\left|\mu^{*}(Q(z,r_{j}))-\mu^{*}(Q(x,r_{j}))\right|\leq C(\omega)(r_{j}2^{-2p})^{\alpha}\leq C(\omega)r_{j}^{3\alpha}\leq r_{j}^{\xi+\delta}

if
p
is large enough. With (
3.3
), it implies that if
p
is large enough, for all
j=0,\dots,2K
,

\left|\mu^{*}(Q(z,r_{j}))\right|\leq 2r_{j}^{\xi+\delta}.

To put in a nutshell, we have shown that, up to an event of vanishing probability,

\displaystyle\left\{\exists x\in D,\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\left|\mu^{*}(Q(x,r))\right|}{\log r}\geq\xi+2\delta\right\}

\displaystyle\subset\bigcup_{n}\bigcap_{p\geq n}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in 2^{-2p}\mathbb{Z}^{2}\\
z\in D\end{subarray}}\left\{\forall j=0,\dots,2K,\left|\mu^{*}(Q(z,r_{j}))\right|\leq 2r_{j}^{\xi+\delta}\right\}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\subset\bigcap_{n}\bigcup_{p\geq n}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in 2^{-2p}\mathbb{Z}^{2}\\
z\in D\end{subarray}}\left\{\forall j=0,\dots,2K,\left|\mu^{*}(Q(z,r_{j}))\right|\leq 2r_{j}^{\xi+\delta}\right\}

the last inclusion being elementary and not specific to the events considered here.
The goal is now to show that the probability of the event (
3.4
) vanishes. Let
p
be a large integer and
z\in 2^{-2p}\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap D
. By translation and scaling invariance,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j=0,\dots,2K,\left|\mu^{*}(Q(z,r_{j}))\right|\leq 2r_{j}^{\xi+\delta}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j=0,\dots,2K,\left|\mu^{*}(Q(0,2^{-j}))\right|\leq 2\cdot 2^{-p\delta}2^{-(\xi+\delta)j}\right).

In Lemma
3.1
below the current proof, we show that the above right hand side term is at most
C_{K}2^{-2p\delta(K+1)}
for some
C_{K}>0
independent of
p
.
Since
K+1>1/\delta
, a union bound concludes that the probability of the event (
3.4
) vanishes. It concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mu^{*}
be the imaginary chaos associated to the whole-plane GFF. For all integers
K\geq 1
, there exists
C_{K}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j=0,\dots,2K,|\mu^{*}(Q(0,2^{-j}))|\leq\varepsilon\right)\leq C_{K}\varepsilon^{2(K+1)}.

Before proving Lemma
3.1
, we need to collect a density result following from
[
AJJ22
]
.
This result will feature a quantity
c_{1}(f)
associated to some complex-valued function
f:D\to\mathbb{C}
that we define now.
For
\delta>0
, let
\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}
be the collection of cubes of the form

(3.5)

[4k_{1}\delta,(4k_{1}+1)\delta]\times[4k_{2}\delta,(4k_{2}+1)\delta]

with
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Consider also the following cones of angle
\pi/4
:

(3.6)

\text{Cone}_{k}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\arg z\in[k\pi/8,\pi/4+8k\pi/8]\},

for

.

c_{1}(f)
is then defined by

(3.7)

c_{1}(f):=\liminf_{\delta\to 0}\delta^{2}\#\{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}:\exists k\in\{0,\dots,15\},\forall x\in Q,f(x)\in\text{Cone}_{k}\text{ and }|f(x)|\geq\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}/2\}.

Notice that

Theorem 3.2
.

Let

be a bounded simply connected domain and

be the GFF in

with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Let

be a compact subset of

has a smooth density

. Moreover, there exists

that may depend on

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

This is a version of

If

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Let
Q_{j}=Q(0,2^{-j})
,
j=0,\dots,2K
, and
Q_{2K+1}=\varnothing
.
We start with the elementary observation that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j=0,\dots,2K,\left|\mu^{*}(Q_{j})\right|\leq\varepsilon\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j=0,\dots,2K,\left|\mu^{*}(Q_{j}\backslash Q_{j+1})\right|\leq 2\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j=0,2,4,\dots,2K,\left|\mu^{*}(Q_{j}\backslash Q_{j+1})\right|\leq 2\varepsilon\right).

In the last step, we neglected the constraints on odd values of
j
so that the sets
Q_{j}\setminus Q_{j+1}
,
i=0,2,4,\dots,2K
, are now disjoint and well separated: the minimal distance between two such sets is at least
2^{-2K}
.
For
j=0,\dots,K
, let us define
A_{j}=Q_{2j}\setminus Q_{2j+1}
and
B_{j}=Q(0,2^{-2j+1/2})\setminus Q(0,2^{-2(j+1)+1/2})
. By construction, the
B_{j}
’s are disjoint and for all
j=0,\dots,K
,
A_{j}
is contained in
B_{j}
and is at a positive distance to
\partial B_{j}
. We apply Markov’s property inside each
B_{j}
: there exists a field
h
such that for all
j=0,\dots,K
,
\Gamma_{j}:=(\Gamma^{*}-h)\mathbf{1}_{B_{j}}
has the law of a GFF in
B_{j}
. Moreover,
\Gamma_{0},\dots,\Gamma_{K}
and
h
are mutually independent and
h
is harmonic inside each
B_{j}
.
Conditionally on
h
, we can now view
\mu^{*}(A_{j})
as the imaginary chaos
\mu_{j}
of
\Gamma_{j}
integrated against the test function

f_{j}(x):=e^{i\beta h(x)}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{x\in A_{j}\right\}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j=0,\dots,K,\left|\mu^{*}(A_{j})\right|\leq 2\varepsilon\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{K}\mathbb{P}\left(|(\mu_{j},f_{j})|\leq 2\varepsilon|h\right)\right],

where the expectation integrates out the randomness of
h
. By Theorem
3.2
, for all
j=0,\dots,K
and conditionally given
h
, the law of the random variable
(\mu_{j},f_{j})
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, the associated densities are uniformly bounded by some deterministic constant
C
times
c_{1}(f_{j})^{-8}
. We deduce that for all
j=0,\dots,K
,
\mathbb{P}\left(|(\mu_{j},f_{j})|\leq 2\varepsilon|h\right)\leq Cc_{1}(f_{j})^{-8}\varepsilon^{2}
, implying that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j=0,\dots,K,\left|\mu^{*}(A_{j})\right|\leq 2\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{K}c_{1}(f_{j})^{-8}\right]\varepsilon^{2(K+1)}

\displaystyle\leq C\prod_{j=0}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[c_{1}(f_{j})^{-8(K+1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{K+1}}\varepsilon^{2(K+1)},

where we used Hölder’s inequality (more precisely, a generalisation to finitely many variables) in the last inequality.

To conclude the proof, it remains to show that
\mathbb{E}\left[c_{1}(f_{j})^{-8(K+1)}\right]
is finite for all
j
.
Let
j\in\{0,\dots,K\}
and
x_{j}\in A_{j}
be at distance at least
2^{-10K}
to the boundary of
A_{j}
. Let
\eta\in(0,2^{-10K})
. Note that if
|h(x_{j})-h(y)|\leq 1/(100\beta)
for all
y\in B(x_{j},\eta)
, then there exists
k\in\{0,\dots,15\}
such that
f_{j}(y)\in\text{Cone}_{k}
for all
y\in B(x_{j},\eta)
and thus
c_{1}(f_{j})\geq c\eta^{2}
for some constant
c>0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(c_{1}(f_{j})\leq c\eta^{2}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{y\in B(x_{j},\eta)}|h(x_{j})-h(y)|>1/(100\beta)\Big).

Since
h-h(x_{j})
is a smooth Gaussian field inside each
A_{j}
, then, by local chaining inequalities (e.g.
[
VH14
, Proposition 5.35]
),
the above right hand side term is at most
Ce^{-c/\eta^{2}}
. This shows that the tails of
1/c_{1}(f_{j})
decays exponentially fast. In particular, all negative moments of
c_{1}(f_{j})
are finite, concluding the proof.
∎

4.
Law of the iterated logarithm: proof of Theorem
1.4

In this section, we will write

\varphi(r)\coloneqq r^{2-\beta^{2}/2}(\log\left|\log r\right|)^{\beta^{2}/4},\qquad r\in(0,1).

The goal of this section is to establish the law of the iterated logarithm (
1.4
) in Theorem
1.4
. The proof of this result will be decomposed into two main steps.
First, we will show that (
1.4
) holds along any geometric sequence of radii:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
x_{0}\in D
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
be fixed. Then

(4.1)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},\gamma^{n}))\right|}{\varphi(\gamma^{n})}=c^{*}(\beta)^{-\beta^{2}/4}\quad\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

We will then control the oscillations between two consecutive points in the geometric sequence:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
x_{0}\in D
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big[\sup_{r\in[\gamma^{n+1},\gamma^{n}]}\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},\gamma^{n}))-\mu(Q(x_{0},r))\right|>\varepsilon\varphi(\gamma^{n+1})\Big]<\infty.

Let us first see how the two lemmas imply Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
x_{0}\in D
.
For any
\gamma\in(0,1)
fixed, we can lower bound

\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},r))\right|}{\varphi(r)}\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},\gamma^{n}))\right|}{\varphi(\gamma^{n})}.

By Lemma
4.1
, the right hand side term is almost surely equal to
c^{*}(\beta)^{-\beta^{2}/4}
which provides the desired lower bound in Theorem
1.4
.
It remains to prove the upper bound. By Lemma
4.1
, to prove this upper bound, it is enough to show that for any
\varepsilon>0
,
there exists
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that we have

(4.2)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{r\in[\gamma^{n+1},\gamma^{n}]}\left|\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},r))\right|}{\varphi(r)}-\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},\gamma^{n}))\right|}{\varphi(\gamma^{n})}\right|\leq\varepsilon\quad\quad\text{a.s.}

To show (
4.2
), we note that, by the triangle inequality and the fact that
\varphi(r)
is increasing near
0
, we get for
r\in[\gamma^{n+1},\gamma^{n}]
and
n
large enough that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},r))\right|}{\varphi(r)}-\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},\gamma^{n}))\right|}{\varphi(\gamma^{n})}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\Big(\frac{1}{\varphi(r)}-\frac{1}{\varphi(\gamma^{n})}\Big)\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},\gamma^{n}))\right|+\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},r))\right|-\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},\gamma^{n}))\right|}{\varphi(r)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{\varphi(\gamma^{n})}{\varphi(\gamma^{n+1})}-1\Big)\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},\gamma^{n}))\right|}{\varphi(\gamma^{n})}+\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},\gamma^{n}))-\mu(Q(x_{0},r))\right|}{\varphi(\gamma^{n+1})}.

We conclude the proof by noticing that in the first term

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\varphi(\gamma^{n})}{\varphi(\gamma^{n+1})}-1=\gamma^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}-2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(\log\log\frac{1}{\gamma^{n}})^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}}}{(\log\log\frac{1}{\gamma^{n+1}})^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}}}-1=\gamma^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}-2}-1,

which can be made as small as desired by choosing
\gamma
close enough to
1
.
The second term is handled by Lemma
4.2
and Borel–Cantelli.
∎

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
.

4.1.
Proof of Lemma
4.1

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Fix
x_{0}\in D
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
and let
r_{n}\coloneqq\gamma^{n}
. We start by proving the lower bound which is the most difficult one. Consider the subsequence
(r_{n}^{\prime})
of
(r_{n})
given by
r_{n}^{\prime}=r_{k_{n}}
with
k_{n}=\left\lfloor Kn\log\log n\right\rfloor
for some
K>0
. In particular, if
K
is large enough, it satisfies

(4.3)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(r^{\prime}_{n-1}/r^{\prime}_{n})}{\log\log n}>\beta^{2}+1\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\left|\log r^{\prime}_{n}\right|}{\log n}=1.

As

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},r_{n}))\right|}{\varphi(r_{n})}\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},r_{n}^{\prime}))\right|}{\varphi(r_{n}^{\prime})},

it is enough to prove the lower bound along the subsequence
(r^{\prime}_{n})
.
In the following, we will decompose the GFF
\Gamma
as the independent sum of a GFF
\Gamma^{Q(x_{0},r^{\prime}_{n})}
in
Q(x_{0},r^{\prime}_{n})
plus a harmonic function
h^{Q(x_{0},r^{\prime}_{n})}
in
Q(x_{0},r^{\prime}_{n})
. We will denote
\mu^{(n)}
the imaginary chaos associated to
\Gamma^{Q(x_{0},r^{\prime}_{n})}
.
Lemma
2.7
together with (
4.3
) implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},{r^{\prime}_{n}}))\right|-\left|\mu^{(n-1)}(Q(x_{0},{r^{\prime}_{n}}))\right|\right|>{r^{\prime}_{n}}^{2-\beta^{2}/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{n}\exp\left(-c\left(\frac{r_{n-1}^{\prime}}{r_{n}^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{2}{\beta^{2}+1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{n}\exp\left(-c(\log n)^{2}\right)<\infty.

By Borel-Cantelli lemma, it implies that a.s.

(4.4)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},{r^{\prime}_{n}}))\right|}{\varphi(r_{n}^{\prime})}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|\mu^{(n-1)}(Q(x_{0},{r^{\prime}_{n}}))\right|}{\varphi(r_{n}^{\prime})}.

If
m\in\{n,n+1\}
and
t\in(1/2,2)
, Lemma
2.8
implies that

(4.5)

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\left|\mu^{(n-1)}(Q(x_{0},{r^{\prime}_{m}}))\right|}{{r^{\prime}_{m}}^{2-\beta^{2}/2}}>t^{\beta^{2}/4}c^{*}(\beta)^{-\beta^{2}/4}(\log n)^{\beta^{2}/4}\right)=n^{-t+o(1)}.

In particular, Borel-Cantelli lemma implies that a.s.

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},{r^{\prime}_{n}}))\right|}{\varphi(r_{n}^{\prime})}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|\mu^{(n-1)}(Q(x_{0},{r^{\prime}_{n}})\backslash Q(x_{0},{r^{\prime}_{n+1}}))\right|}{\varphi(r_{n}^{\prime})}.

Now, the random variables appearing on the right hand side are independent when we restrict ourselves to even or odd integers. Moreover, by (
4.5
) and the triangle inequality, they satisfy for
t\in(1/2,2)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\left|\mu^{(n-1)}(Q(x_{0},{r^{\prime}_{n}})\backslash Q(x_{0},{r^{\prime}_{n+1}}))\right|}{{r^{\prime}_{n}}^{2-\beta^{2}/2}}>t^{\beta^{2}/4}c^{*}(\beta)^{-\beta^{2}/4}(\log n)^{\beta^{2}/4}\right)=n^{-t+o(1)}.

With this and a simple use of the first and second Borel-Cantelli lemmas, we deduce that a.s.

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\\
n\mathrm{~even}\end{subarray}}\frac{\left|\mu^{(n-1)}(Q(x_{0},{r^{\prime}_{n}})\backslash Q(x_{0},{r^{\prime}_{n+1}}))\right|}{\varphi(r_{n}^{\prime})}=c^{*}(\beta)^{-\beta^{2}/4},

concluding the proof of the lower bound of (
1.4
).

The upper bound is much easier because for this bound we use the version of Borel–Cantelli lemma which does not require any independence. Consider
R_{0}>0
s.t.
Q(x_{0},R_{0})\subset D
. Since the ratio
r_{n}/R_{0}
decays much faster than
r^{\prime}_{n}/r^{\prime}_{n-1}
, we can repeat the previous arguments to show the analogue of (
4.4
):

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|\mu(Q(x_{0},r_{n}))\right|}{\varphi(r_{n})}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|\mu^{(R_{0})}(Q(x_{0},r_{n}))\right|}{\varphi(r_{n})}.

With Lemma
2.8
, we have for
t\in(1/2,2)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\left|\mu^{(R_{0})}(Q(x_{0},r_{n}))\right|}{r_{n}^{2-\beta^{2}/2}}>t^{\beta^{2}/4}c^{*}(\beta)^{-\beta^{2}/4}(\log n)^{\beta^{2}/4}\right)=n^{-t+o(1)}.

The previous probabilities are thus summable if
t>1
and Borel-Cantelli lemma concludes the proof of the upper bound of (
1.4
).
∎

4.2.
Proof of Lemma
4.2

This section is dedicated to the proof of Lemma
4.2
. Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
n\geq 1
. Define the function

(4.6)

F_{n}:r\in I_{n}\mapsto\mu(Q(x_{0},\gamma^{n})\setminus Q(x_{0},r)),\qquad\text{where}\qquad I_{n}:=[\gamma^{n+1},\gamma^{n}].

In Lemma
4.2
, we want to control the uniform norm of
F_{n}
. This norm is difficult to study directly. For instance, we do not know
a priori
how to compute its expectation. Our proof will be based on Sobolev embeddings which allow us to control this norm via more tractable norms. Let us first recall some definitions and some results on fractional Sobolev spaces. For more background on Sobolev spaces, we refer to
[
DNPV12
]
.

Sobolev spaces.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval.
For
s\in(0,1)
and
p\geq 1
, the Sobolev space
W^{s,p}(I)
is defined as

(4.7)

W^{s,p}(I):=\left\{f\in L^{p}(I):\left\|f\right\|_{W^{s,p}(I)}^{p}:=\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(I)}^{p}+\int_{I\times I}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{sp+1}}dxdy<\infty\right\}.

Recall also that for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the space of
\alpha
-Hölder continuous functions is defined as

(4.8)

C^{\alpha}(I):=\left\{f\in C^{0}(I):\left\|f\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(I)}:=\sup_{x\in I}|f(x)|+\sup_{x\neq y\in I}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}<\infty\right\}.

The Sobolev embedding we will rely on is the following (see e.g.
[
DNPV12
, Theorem 8.2]
):
when
sp>1
, then
W^{s,p}(I)\subset C^{\alpha}(I)
for
\alpha=s-1/p
. Moreover, there exists
C(s)>0
that may depend on
s
but not on
p
, nor on
I
, such that for all intervals
I
and for all
f\in W^{s,p}(I)
,

(4.9)

\sup_{x\neq y\in I}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\leq C(s)\left(\int_{I\times I}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{sp+1}}dxdy\right)^{1/p}\quad\quad(\alpha=s-1/p).

The fact that the constant
C(s)
can be chosen independently of
p
can be checked by following the proof of
[
DNPV12
, Theorem 8.2]
.
The fact that the constant can be chosen independently of the interval
I
follows directly by a scaling argument.

With the Sobolev inequality (
4.9
) at hand, the proof of Lemma
4.2
will be based on the following estimate:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
F_{n}
be the function defined in (
4.6
). For all
s\in(0,1-\beta^{2}/4)
, there exists
C>0
that may depend on
\beta
and
s
such that for all
n\geq 1
and even integers
p\geq 2
,

(4.10)

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{I_{n}\times I_{n}}\frac{|F_{n}(r)-F_{n}(r^{\prime})|^{p}}{|r-r^{\prime}|^{sp+1}}drdr^{\prime}\right]\leq C^{p}p^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}p}(1-\gamma)^{(1-\beta^{2}/4-s)p}\gamma^{(2-\beta^{2}/2)np}\gamma^{-spn+n}.

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

Let
s\in(0,1-\beta^{2}/4)
,
n\geq 1
and
p\geq 2
be an even integer.
By Lemma
2.5
, we have for all
r,r^{\prime}\in I_{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|F_{n}(r)-F_{n}(r^{\prime})|^{p}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mu(Q(x_{0},r\vee r^{\prime})\setminus Q(x_{0},r\wedge r^{\prime}))\right]\leq C^{p}((r\vee r^{\prime})|r-r^{\prime}|)^{(1-\beta^{2}/4)p}p^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}p}.

By Fubini, we deduce that the left hand side of (
4.10
) is at most

\displaystyle C^{p}p^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}p}\int_{I_{n}\times I_{n}}(r\vee r^{\prime})^{(1-\beta^{2}/4)p}|r-r^{\prime}|^{(1-\beta^{2}/4-s)p-1}drdr^{\prime}.

Recalling that
I_{n}=[\gamma^{n+1},\gamma^{n}]
, we bound
(r\vee r^{\prime})^{(1-\beta^{2}/4)p}
in the last integral above by
\gamma^{(1-\beta^{2}/4)np}
. The integral over
I_{n}\times I_{n}
of
|r-r^{\prime}|^{(1-\beta^{2}/4-s)p-1}
is then equal to

\frac{2}{(1-\beta^{2}/4-s)p((1-\beta^{2}/4-s)p+1)}(\gamma^{n}-\gamma^{n+1})^{(1-\beta^{2}/4-s)p+1}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed. Let
\gamma>0
be close to 1 and
n\geq 1
. Let
s\in(0,1-\beta^{2}/4)
be a fixed parameter (
s=1/2
would do) and
p\geq 2
be a large even integer that we will choose precisely later. Let
\alpha=s-1/p
.
We have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{r\in I_{n}}|F_{n}(r)|\geq\varepsilon\varphi(\gamma^{n+1})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{r\in I_{n}}|F_{n}(r)-F_{n}(\gamma^{n})|\geq\varepsilon\varphi(\gamma^{n+1})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{r\neq r^{\prime}\in I_{n}}\frac{|F_{n}(r)-F_{n}(r^{\prime})|}{|r-r^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}\geq\varepsilon\varphi(\gamma^{n+1})(\gamma^{n}-\gamma^{n+1})^{-\alpha}\right).

By Markov’s inequality, this is at most

(\varepsilon^{-1}(1-\gamma)^{\alpha})^{p}\varphi(\gamma^{n+1})^{-p}\gamma^{\alpha pn}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sup_{r\neq r^{\prime}\in I_{n}}\frac{|F_{n}(r)-F_{n}(r^{\prime})|}{|r-r^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}\right)^{p}\right].

Combining the Sobolev inequality (
4.9
) and Lemma
4.3
, we can bound the above expectation by

C^{p}p^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}p}(1-\gamma)^{(1-\beta^{2}/4-s)p}\gamma^{(2-\beta^{2}/2)np}\gamma^{-spn+n}.

Putting things together and recalling that
\varphi(\gamma^{n+1})=\gamma^{(2-\beta^{2}/2)np}\log((n+1)|\log\gamma|)^{\beta^{2}/4}
, we obtain that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{r\in I_{n}}|F_{n}(r)|\geq\varepsilon\varphi(\gamma^{n+1})\right)\leq(1-\gamma)^{-1}(C\varepsilon^{-1}(1-\gamma)^{1-\beta^{2}/4}(\log n)^{-\beta^{2}/4})^{p}p^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}p}.

We can now optimise in
p
. We choose
p=e^{-1}(C\varepsilon^{-1}(1-\gamma)^{1-\beta^{2}/4}(\log n)^{-\beta^{2}/4})^{-\frac{4}{\beta^{2}}}
(or rather an even integer close to this value), leading to the following bound:

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{r\in I_{n}}|F_{n}(r)|\geq\varepsilon\varphi(\gamma^{n+1})\right)\leq(1-\gamma)^{-1}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}e^{-1}(C\varepsilon^{-1}(1-\gamma)^{1-\beta^{2}/4})^{-\frac{4}{\beta^{2}}}\log n\right).

If
\gamma
is close enough to
1
, then the coefficient in front of
\log n
is larger than 1 making the right hand side term of the above display summable in
n
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

We can restrict ourselves to the bulk of

6.
Besov regularity of imaginary chaos: proof of Theorem
1.7

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.7
on the optimal Besov regularity of the imaginary chaos.
The proof is based on wavelet analysis and similar ideas were also used in
[
JSV19
]
. As a new ingredient in the boundary case where the smoothness index
s=-\beta^{2}/2
, we employ a law of large numbers type argument to show that the quantities
A_{j}
(see below) appearing in the wavelet version of the Besov norm converge almost surely to their nonzero mean.
On the other hand, the proof in the case
p=+\infty
will rely on what we have developed in the earlier sections of the current paper, especially Theorem
1.4
.
Let us first recall from
[
Mey93
, Chapter 6]
basic properties of wavelet bases and the characterisation of Besov spaces using them.

Wavelet bases and Besov spaces.

The wavelet bases we will use in 2D are defined in terms of a father wavelet
\phi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and three mother wavelets
\psi^{(\tau)}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
\tau\in\{0,1\}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
. For
j\geq 0
, let

\Lambda_{j}:=\{\lambda=2^{-j}k+2^{-j-1}\tau:k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},\tau\in\{0,1\}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}\}

and for
\lambda=2^{-j}k+2^{-j-1}\tau\in\Lambda_{j}
, let
\psi_{\lambda}(x)=2^{j}\psi^{(\tau)}(2^{j}x-k)
.
The father and mother wavelets are such that
\{\phi(\cdot-k),k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}\cup\bigcup_{j\geq 0}\{\psi_{\lambda},\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}\}

forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

The usefulness of wavelet bases stems from the fact that the wavelets can be chosen to have several desirable properties at the same time. In particular we will assume that
\supp\phi,\supp\psi^{\tau}\subset K
for some square
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

The localisation and smoothness properties allows one to characterise various function spaces in terms of the wavelet coefficients. The characterisation for Besov spaces that we will use is the following: If
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a distribution with the wavelet series

(6.1)

f(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma(k)\phi(x-k)+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}\alpha(\lambda)\psi_{\lambda}(x),

then a norm equivalent to the Besov norm
\|\cdot\|_{B_{p,q}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
, assuming
|s|<2
,
1
1

1

Here
2
comes from the fact that we chose our wavelets to be
C^{2}
. More generally one could choose them to be
C^{r}
and expand the valid range of
s
to
|s|<r
.
is given by

\|f\|_{B_{p,q}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\sim\|(\gamma(k))_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\|_{\ell^{p}}+\Big\|\Big\{2^{j(1-2/p+s)}\Big(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}|\alpha(\lambda)|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}\Big\}_{j\geq 1}\Big\|_{\ell^{q}}.

If
D
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the space
B_{p,q,\loc}^{s}(D)
is defined as the space of distributions
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that for all
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(D)
,
fu\in B_{p,q}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

The second tool we will use is a decomposition of the GFF.

Decomposition using exactly scaling field.
In
[
JSV19
, Section 2]
it was noted that to study the Besov-regularity of complex chaos it is enough to consider the so-called exactly scaling case. Let us briefly recall the details.

Let
X
be the log-correlated field on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the covariance

(6.2)

\mathbb{E}[X(x)X(y)]=\log^{+}\frac{R}{|x-y|}

for some
R>0
, where
\log^{+}t=\log(\max(t,1))
. This is indeed a covariance function in two dimensions, since one can write

\log^{+}\frac{R}{|x-y|}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(1-\sqrt{\frac{e^{u}|x-y|}{R}}\Big)_{+}\,du+2\Big(1-\sqrt{\frac{|x-y|}{R}}\Big)_{+},

which expresses the covariance kernel
\log^{+}\frac{1}{|x-y|}
as a sum (integral) of positive-definite kernels with covariance
(1-\sqrt{e^{u}|x-y|})_{+}
, see
[
RV10
]
for more details and references. The integral part corresponds to a so-called
\star
-scale invariant field. In particular one can find approximations
X_{s}(x)
,
s\in(0,R]
, of the field by cutting the integral at level
\log\frac{R}{s}
, leading to a process with the covariance structure

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{s}(x)X_{t}(y)]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\log\frac{R}{s\vee t}}\Big(1-\sqrt{\frac{e^{u}|x-y|}{R}}\Big)_{+}\,du+2\Big(1-\sqrt{\frac{|x-y|}{R}}\Big)_{+}

(6.3)

\displaystyle=\log^{+}\frac{R}{s\vee t\vee|x-y|}+2-2\sqrt{\frac{|x-y|}{s\vee t\vee|x-y|}}.

By (the proof of)
[
JSW19
, Theorem A]
we can for any
K\coloneqq\overline{U}\subset D
where
U\subset D
is open find a coupling such that
\Gamma|_{K}=X+G
, where
G
is an a.s. smooth Gaussian field. Note that for
\varepsilon<R/2
we have that
\mathbb{E}[X^{(\varepsilon)}(x)^{2}]=\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
, where
X^{(\varepsilon)}
is the
\varepsilon

X
. Hence when constructing the chaos for the field
X
there is no difference in Wick normalisation and renormalisation by
\varepsilon^{-\beta^{2}/2}
. It follows that if
\nu
is the chaos constructed using
X
, we have that for any
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
,
u\mu=e^{i\beta G}u\nu
almost surely. The exponent in front is smooth and hence a.s.

(6.4)

\mu\in B^{s}_{p,q,\loc}(D)\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\nu\in B^{s}_{p,q,\loc}(D).

The usefulness of the field
X
(whose covariance is given by (
6.2
)) stems from the fact that it satisfies the following scaling relation in law: For any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\varepsilon\leq 1
, we have
(X(\varepsilon x))_{x\in B(x_{0},R/2)}\sim(X(x)+N)_{x\in B(x_{0},R/2)}
, where
N
is a centered Gaussian with variance
\log(1/\varepsilon)
independent of
X

ensures that the covariance is a pure logarithm and does not get truncated. This implies in particular that if
U\subset B(0,R/2)

\int_{\varepsilon U}\nu(x)f(x)\,dx\sim\varepsilon^{2-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}e^{i\beta N(0,\log\varepsilon^{-1})}\int_{U}\nu(x)f(\varepsilon x)\,dx.

In what follows we will choose
R
to be so large that the supporting square
K
of the mother wavelets lies inside
B(0,R/2)
, which lets us employ the scaling relation when computing
\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda)|^{p}]
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.7
.

Proof of Theorem

.

Recall that we are interested in whether
\mu

where
s

The proof will deal with three cases separately.

Case 1

.
We want to show that
\mu\in B^{-\beta^{2}/2}_{p,\infty,\loc}(D)

Since the function

u\nu\in B^{-\beta^{2}/2}_{p,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

Note that, by compactness of the support of

(A_{j})_{j\geq 1}

Note also that, again by compactness of the support of

, for all

\alpha(\lambda)

is at most

(6.6)

2^{-2p/j}\Big(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}|\alpha(\lambda)|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}\leq C2^{-2p^{\prime}/j}\Big(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}|\alpha(\lambda)|^{p^{\prime}}\Big)^{1/p^{\prime}}.

This shows that it is actually enough to show that the sequence
(A_{j})_{j\geq 1}

p\geq 2

We will proceed in two steps by showing that

(6.7)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}[A_{j}^{p}])_{j\geq 1}\text{ is bounded,}

(6.8)

\displaystyle A_{j}^{p}-\mathbb{E}[A_{j}^{p}]\to 0\qquad\text{a.s.}

By Fubini, we have for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[A_{j}^{p}]=2^{j(1-2/p-\beta^{2}/2)p}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda)|^{p}]

and for all
\lambda=2^{-j}k+2^{-j-1}\tau\in\Lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda)|^{p}]=2^{jp}\mathbb{E}\left|\int\nu(x)u(x)\psi^{\tau}(2^{j}x-k)dx\right|^{p}.

Using the scaling property and translation invariance of

we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda)|^{p}]

\displaystyle=2^{jp}\mathbb{E}\left|\int\nu(x)u(x+2^{-j}k)\psi^{\tau}(2^{j}x)\,dx\right|^{p}

(6.9)

\displaystyle=2^{jp}2^{p(-2j+j\beta^{2}/2)}\mathbb{E}\left|\int\nu(x)u(2^{-j}(x+k))\psi^{\tau}(x)\,dx\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-j(1-\beta^{2}/2)p}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}|\nu(K)|^{2p}}\|u\|_{\infty}^{p}\|\psi^{\tau}\|_{\infty}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C2^{-j(1-\beta^{2}/2)p},

where
C>0
depends on
p,\psi
and
u
but not on
j
or
k
. Since
\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda)|^{p}]
vanishes for all
\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}
except for at most
O(2^{2j})
of them, this concludes that
(\mathbb{E}[A_{j}^{p}])_{j\geq 1}
is bounded as stated in (
6.7
).

We now move to the proof of (
6.8
).
Recall that
p=2N
is an even integer and define

S_{j}:=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}|\nu(u\psi_{\lambda})|^{2N},\quad\quad j\geq 1.

Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda_{j}
and set
D_{i}:=\supp\psi_{\lambda_{i}}
,
i=1,2
. Suppose that
D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\emptyset
, set
\Delta:=\sup\{|x-y|,\,x\in D_{1},y\in D_{2}\}
and
\delta:=\inf\{|x-y|,\,x\in D_{1},y\in D_{2}\}
.
Recall the notation
\mathcal{E}(\Gamma;\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})
defined in (
2.3
). For
m=1,2
,
\mathbb{E}[|\nu(u\psi_{\lambda_{m}})|^{p}]
equals

\displaystyle\int_{D_{m}^{N}\times D_{m}^{N}}d\mathbf{x}^{m}\,d\mathbf{y}^{m}e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}(X;\mathbf{x}^{m};\mathbf{y}^{m})}\prod_{k=1}^{N}u(x^{m}_{k})u(y^{m}_{k})\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x^{m}_{k})\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(y^{m}_{k})

and
\mathbb{E}[|\nu(u\psi_{\lambda_{1}})|^{p}|\nu(u\psi_{\lambda_{2}})|^{p}]
equals

\displaystyle\int_{D_{1}^{N}\times D_{1}^{N}\times D_{2}^{N}\times D_{2}^{N}}

\displaystyle d\mathbf{x}^{1}\,d\mathbf{y}^{1}\,d\mathbf{x}^{2}\,d\mathbf{y}^{2}e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}(X;\mathbf{x}^{1}\mathbf{x}^{2};\mathbf{y}^{1}\mathbf{y}^{2})}\prod_{m=1,2}\prod_{k=1}^{N}u(x^{m}_{k})u(y^{m}_{k})\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x^{m}_{k})\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(y^{m}_{k}).

In the last display we denoted by
\mathbf{x}^{1}\mathbf{x}^{2}\in D_{1}^{N}\times D_{2}^{N}
the concatenation of the vectors
\mathbf{x}^{1}\in D_{1}^{N}
and
\mathbf{x}^{2}\in D_{2}^{N}
. We deduce that
\mathrm{Cov}(|\nu(u\psi_{\lambda_{1}})|^{p};|\nu(u\psi_{\lambda_{2}})|^{p})
is at most

\displaystyle C^{N}\left\|u\right\|_{\infty}^{4N}\left\|\psi_{\lambda}\right\|_{\infty}^{4N}\int_{D_{1}^{N}\times D_{1}^{N}\times D_{2}^{N}\times D_{2}^{N}}d\mathbf{x}^{1}\,d\mathbf{y}^{1}\,d\mathbf{x}^{2}\,d\mathbf{y}^{2}\Big|e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}(X;\mathbf{x}^{1}\mathbf{x}^{2};\mathbf{y}^{1}\mathbf{y}^{2})}-\prod_{m=1,2}e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}(X;\mathbf{x}^{m};\mathbf{y}^{m})}\Big|.

Expanding
\mathcal{E}(\Gamma;\mathbf{x}^{1}\mathbf{x}^{2};\mathbf{y}^{1}\mathbf{y}^{2})
, we have for all
(\mathbf{x}^{1},\mathbf{y}^{1},\mathbf{x}^{2},\mathbf{y}^{2})\in D_{1}^{N}\times D_{1}^{N}\times D_{2}^{N}\times D_{2}^{N}
,

\mathcal{E}(X;\mathbf{x}^{1}\mathbf{x}^{2};\mathbf{y}^{1}\mathbf{y}^{2})=\sum_{m=1,2}\mathcal{E}(X;\mathbf{x}^{m};\mathbf{y}^{m})+\sum_{1\leq k,l\leq N}\log\frac{|x_{k}^{1}-x_{l}^{2}||y_{k}^{1}-y_{l}^{2}|}{|x_{k}^{1}-y_{l}^{2}||y_{k}^{1}-x_{l}^{2}|}.

By definition of
\Delta
and
\delta
and approximating the Green function by the log of the distance, the absolute value of the second sum on the right hand side is at most
2N^{2}\log\Delta/\delta
. We thus obtain that
\mathrm{Cov}(|\mu(u\psi_{\lambda_{1}})|^{p};|\mu(u\psi_{\lambda_{2}})|^{p})
is at most

\displaystyle C^{N}\Big(\Big(\frac{\Delta}{\delta}\Big)^{2N^{2}\beta^{2}}-1\Big)\left\|u\right\|_{\infty}^{4N}\left\|\psi_{\lambda}\right\|_{\infty}^{4N}\prod_{m=1,2}\int_{D_{m}^{N}\times D_{m}^{N}}d\mathbf{x}^{m}\,d\mathbf{y}^{m}e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}(X;\mathbf{x}^{m};\mathbf{y}^{m})}.

By scaling, the above product of integrals is at most
C_{N}2^{-(2-\beta^{2}/2)4jN}
. Since we also have
\left\|\psi_{\lambda}\right\|_{\infty}^{4N}\leq 2^{4jN}\left\|\psi\right\|_{\infty}^{4N}
, we have obtained that

(6.10)

\mathrm{Cov}(|\nu(u\psi_{\lambda_{1}})|^{p};|\nu(u\psi_{\lambda_{2}})|^{p})\leq C_{N}2^{-(1-\beta^{2}/2)4jN}\Big(\Big(\frac{\Delta}{\delta}\Big)^{2N^{2}\beta^{2}}-1\Big).

This is the main estimate we will need in order to bound:

(6.11)

\mathrm{Var}(S_{j})=\sum_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda_{j}}\mathrm{Cov}(|\mu(u\psi_{\lambda_{1}})|^{p};|\mu(u\psi_{\lambda_{2}})|^{p}).

Indeed, if
|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}|\geq 2^{-j/2}
, then the associated
\Delta
and
\delta
satisfy
\frac{\Delta}{\delta}=1+O(2^{-j/2})
and (
6.10
) shows that

\mathrm{Cov}(|\nu(u\psi_{\lambda_{1}})|^{p};|\nu(u\psi_{\lambda_{2}})|^{p})\leq C_{N}2^{-(1-\beta^{2}/2)4jN-j/2}.

The contribution of such
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
to the sum in (
6.11
) is thus at most
C_{N}2^{-j/2-4j(2-\beta^{2}/2)N+4j}=C_{p}2^{-j/2-2j(2-2/p-\beta^{2}/2)p}
, recalling that
p=2N
. On the other hand, if
|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}|\leq 2^{-j/2}
, then we simply use Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (
6.9
) to bound

\mathrm{Cov}(|\nu(u\psi_{\lambda_{1}})|^{p};|\nu(u\psi_{\lambda_{2}})|^{p})\leq C2^{-2pj(2-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2})}
. Since there are at most
C2^{3j}
couples
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
such that
|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}|\leq 2^{-j/2}
, this shows that the contribution of such couples to the sum in (
6.11
) is at most
C_{p}2^{-j-2j(2-2/p-\beta^{2}/2)p}
. Altogether, we have shown that

\mathrm{Var}(S_{j})\leq C2^{-j/2-2j(2-2/p-\beta^{2}/2)p}.

Applying the Chebychev inequality, and recalling that
A_{j}
is defined in (
6.5
), we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|A_{j}^{p}-\mathbb{E}[A_{j}^{p}]|\geq 2^{-j/8}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{j/4}\mathrm{Var}(A_{j}^{p})=2^{j/4}2^{2j(2-2/p-\beta^{2}/2)p}\mathrm{Var}(S_{j})\leq C2^{-j/4}.

Borel–Cantelli lemma then concludes the proof of (
6.8
). Together with (
6.7
), this shows that
(A_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is a.s. bounded, and thus
\mu u\in B^{-\beta^{2}/2}_{p,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
a.s., finishing the proof in this case.

Case 2
:
1\leq p<\infty,q<\infty
. We want to show that
\mu\not\in B_{p,q,\loc}^{-\beta^{2}/2}(D)
a.s. It is sufficient to prove that
u\nu\not\in B_{p,q}^{-\beta^{2}/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
a.s. for some specific choice of
u
, so let us pick
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
which is
1
in some square
Q\subset D
.
Arguing as in (
6.6
), it is enough to show that
u\nu\not\in B_{1,q}^{-\beta^{2}/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
where
p
has been replaced by 1. To conclude that
(A_{j})_{j\geq 1}
does not belong to
\ell^{q}
a.s., it is then enough to show that
(\mathbb{E}A_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is bounded away from 0 and that
A_{j}-\mathbb{E}[A_{j}]\to 0
a.s.
Let
j\geq 1
be large enough and
\lambda=2^{-j}k+2^{-j-1}\tau\in\Lambda_{j}
be such that the support of
\psi_{\lambda}
is included in
Q
. There are at least
c2^{2j}
such
\lambda
. By (
6.9
) we have

\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda)|]=2^{j(-1+\beta^{2}/2)}\mathbb{E}\left|\int\nu(x)\psi^{\tau}(x)\,dx\right|\geq C2^{j(-1+\beta^{2}/2)},

for some
C>0
and hence
\mathbb{E}A_{j}\geq C
for all
j
large enough.

Thus it remains to show that
A_{j}-\mathbb{E}[A_{j}]\to 0
a.s. Our basic strategy is the same as above, i.e. we will show the exponential decay of variance:
\mathbb{E}[|A_{j}-\mathbb{E}[A_{j}]|^{2}]\lesssim 2^{-j/2}
. We cannot however easily compute expectations such as
\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{1})||\alpha(\lambda_{2})|]
exactly since we are not looking at even powers, and therefore some extra care has to be taken in bounding the variance.

Let us begin by denoting
d(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
the distance between the supports of
\psi_{\lambda_{1}}
and
\psi_{\lambda_{2}}
and write

\mathbb{E}[|A_{j}-\mathbb{E}[A_{j}]|^{2}]=2^{-2j(1+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2})}\sum_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda_{j}}\mathbb{E}[(|\alpha(\lambda_{1})|-\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\lambda_{1})])(|\alpha(\lambda_{2})|-\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\lambda_{2})])]\eqqcolon S_{1}+S_{2}

where
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
consist of (nearly) diagonal and off-diagonal terms:

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=2^{-j(2+\beta^{2})}\sum_{d(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\leq c2^{-j}}\mathbb{E}[(|\alpha(\lambda_{1})|-\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\lambda_{1})])(|\alpha(\lambda_{2})|-\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\lambda_{2})])]

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=2^{-j(2+\beta^{2})}\sum_{d(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})>c2^{-j}}\mathbb{E}[(|\alpha(\lambda_{1})|-\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\lambda_{1})])(|\alpha(\lambda_{2})|-\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\lambda_{2})])].

Here
c>0
is a constant chosen so that
|x-y|\leq c2^{-j}
whenever
x,y\in\supp\psi_{\lambda}
for some
\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}
.
The diagonal terms can be handled by Cauchy–Schwarz, similarly to the Case 1 above, showing that each term is at most
C2^{-j(2-\beta^{2})}
and there are at most
C2^{2j}
terms, yielding that
S_{1}\lesssim 2^{-2j}
.

For the off-diagonal terms we will denote
r=d(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
and split
X
as the independent sum
X=X_{r}+\hat{X}_{r}
, where
X_{r}
is the
\star
-scale approximation with covariance (
6.3
). In particular we have
\mathbb{E}[\hat{X}_{r}(x)\hat{X}_{r}(y)]=0
when
|x-y|\geq r
and thus the field
\hat{X}_{r}
restricted to
\supp\psi_{\lambda_{1}}
is independent of itself restricted to
\supp\psi_{\lambda_{2}}
and hence,

S_{2}=2^{-j(2+\beta^{2})}\sum_{d(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})>c2^{-j}}\mathbb{E}[(\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{1})||\mathcal{F}_{r}]-\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{1})|])(\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{2})||\mathcal{F}_{r}]-\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{2})|])],

where
\mathcal{F}_{r}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
X_{r}
.

Note that for
m=1,2
we have

\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{m})||\mathcal{F}_{r}]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\int:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x)}::e^{i\beta\hat{X}_{r}(x)}:u(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\,dx\Big||\mathcal{F}_{r}\Big].

The key thing to notice is that
X_{r}(x)
will typically be almost like a constant in
\supp\psi_{\lambda_{m}}
if
r
is macroscopic and
j
is big, and hence it makes sense to write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{m})||\mathcal{F}_{r}]

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{r}(x_{0})^{2}]}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\int:e^{i\beta\hat{X}_{r}(x)}:u(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\,dx\Big|\Big]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\int:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x)}::e^{i\beta\hat{X}_{r}(x)}:u(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\,dx\Big||\mathcal{F}_{r}\Big]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\int:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x_{0})}::e^{i\beta\hat{X}_{r}(x)}:u(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\,dx\Big||\mathcal{F}_{r}\Big]

and similarly

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{m})|]

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{r}(x_{0})^{2}]}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\int:e^{i\beta\hat{X}_{r}(x)}:u(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\,dx\Big|\Big]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\int:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x)}::e^{i\beta\hat{X}_{r}(x)}:u(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\,dx\Big|\Big]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\int:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x_{0})}::e^{i\beta\hat{X}_{r}(x)}:u(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\,dx\Big|\Big].

By the triangle inequality we then have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{m})||\mathcal{F}_{r}]-\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{m})|]|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\int(:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x)}:-:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x_{0})}:):e^{i\beta\hat{X}_{r}(x)}:u(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\,dx\Big||\mathcal{F}_{r}\Big]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\int(:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x)}:-:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x_{0})}:):e^{i\beta\hat{X}_{r}(x)}:u(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\,dx\Big|\Big].

By Cauchy–Schwarz and Jensen’s inequality we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{1})||\mathcal{F}_{r}]-\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{1})|])(\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{2})||\mathcal{F}_{r}]-\mathbb{E}[|\alpha(\lambda_{2})|])]

\displaystyle\lesssim\prod_{m=1,2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\int(:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x)}:-:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x_{0})}:):e^{i\beta\hat{X}_{r}(x)}:u(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\,dx\Big|^{2}\Big]}.

We next compute the remaining second moment, getting

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\int(:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x)}:-:e^{i\beta X_{r}(x_{0})}:):e^{i\beta\hat{X}_{r}(x)}:u(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\,dx\Big|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\int(e^{\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{r}(x)X_{r}(y)]}-e^{\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{r}(x)X_{r}(x_{0})]}-e^{\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{r}(y)X_{r}(x_{0})]}+e^{\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{r}(x_{0})^{2}]})\cdot

\displaystyle\quad e^{\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}[\hat{X}_{r}(x)\hat{X}_{r}(y)]}u(x)u(y)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(y)\,dx\,dy.

Noting that
|x-y|\leq r
and applying (
6.3
) the integral is less than

C\int\big|e^{-2\beta^{2}\sqrt{\frac{|x-y|}{r}}}-e^{-2\beta^{2}\sqrt{\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}}}-e^{-2\beta^{2}\sqrt{\frac{|y-x_{0}|}{r}}}+1\big||x-y|^{-\beta^{2}}\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(x)\psi_{\lambda_{m}}(y)\,dx\,dy.

By Taylor expansion, the terms inside the first absolute value are at most
C2^{-j/2}r^{-1/2}
, while the rest of the integral is of order
2^{-j(2-\beta^{2})}
, giving an upper bound of
r^{-\frac{1}{2}}2^{-j(\frac{5}{2}-\beta^{2})}
.
Hence we see that

S_{2}\lesssim 2^{-j(2+\beta^{2})}\sum_{d(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})>c2^{-j}}r^{-\frac{1}{2}}2^{-j(\frac{5}{2}-\beta^{2})}\lesssim 2^{-\frac{9}{2}j}2^{4j}\int_{c2^{-j}}^{R}\sqrt{r}\,dr\lesssim 2^{-j/2},

which finishes the proof of this case.

Case 3
:
p=+\infty
. We want to show that
\mu\not\in B_{p,q,\loc}^{-\beta^{2}/2}(D)
a.s. It is sufficient to prove that
\mu u\not\in B_{p,q}^{-\beta^{2}/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
a.s. for some specific choice of
u
.
Consider for instance some nonnegative
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(D)
such that
u=1
on some compact set
K\subset D
whose interior is not empty.
Let
p^{\prime}\in[1,\infty]
and
q^{\prime}\in[1,\infty]
be the conjugates of
p
and
q
given by

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1

and

\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}=1
.
By Besov duality (see e.g.
[
Tri83
, Section 2.11]
), we have for any
f\in B_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}^{\beta^{2}/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

(6.12)

\left\|\mu u\right\|_{B_{p,q}^{-\beta^{2}/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\geq|\mu(uf)|/\left\|f\right\|_{B_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}^{\beta^{2}/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Since we currently consider the case
p=+\infty
, we will have
p^{\prime}=1
.
Let
x_{0}
be any given point in the interior of
K
and
\delta>0
be small enough so that
Q(x_{0},2\delta)\subset K
.
Let
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a nonnegative function which is equal to 1 in
Q(0,1)
and which vanishes outside of
Q(0,2)
. Define
f_{\delta}(x)=f((x-x_{0})/\delta)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We apply (
6.12
) to
f_{\delta}
.
We first claim that

(6.13)

\left\|f_{\delta}\right\|_{B_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}^{\beta^{2}/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C\delta^{2/p^{\prime}-\beta^{2}/2}.

Let us first assume that (
6.13
) holds and see how we conclude from this.
Recall that
p^{\prime}=1
.
Since
u=1
on the support of
f_{\delta}
, the duality estimate (
6.12
) applied to
f_{\delta}
yields

\left\|\mu u\right\|_{B_{p,q}^{-\beta^{2}/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\geq c\delta^{-2+\beta^{2}/2}|\mu(f_{\delta})|.

Using a very similar approach to what we did in the proof of Theorem
1.4
, we can show that

\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\delta^{-2+\beta^{2}/2}|\mu(f_{\delta})|=+\infty\qquad\text{a.s.}

The difference with the setup of Theorem
1.4
is that we are not integrating
\mu
against the uniform measures of concentric small squares, but against scaled versions of the nonnegative smooth test function
f
. The same line of argument can be applied in this case as well.
This shows that
\left\|\mu u\right\|_{B_{p,q}^{-\beta^{2}/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=+\infty
a.s.
We see from this argument the special role played by
p=+\infty,p^{\prime}=1
. Because of the scaling
\delta^{2/p^{\prime}}
in (
6.13
), the same reasoning would not have worked for any other value of
p
.

It remains to show (
6.13
). It is very likely that this is a standard fact, but we will derive it for the reader’s convenience. Recall that we denote by
\phi
and
\psi
the father and mother wavelets. For
j\geq 1
and
\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}
, let us denote by

\alpha_{\delta}(\lambda)=\int\psi_{\lambda}f_{\delta}.

Let
j\geq 1
. The number of
\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}
such that the supports of
\psi_{\lambda}
and
f_{\delta}
have a nonempty intersection is at most
2^{2j}\delta^{2}
. Let
\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}
be such. Because the integral of
\psi_{\lambda}
vanishes,

|\alpha_{\delta}(\lambda)|=\left|\int\psi_{\lambda}(f_{\delta}-f_{\delta}(x_{0}))\right|\leq\frac{\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}}{\delta}\int|\psi_{\lambda}(x)|\,|x-x_{0}|\,dx\leq C\frac{2^{-j}}{\delta}\int|\psi_{\lambda}|\leq C\frac{2^{-2j}}{\delta}.

If
j
is such that
2^{-j}\geq\delta
, we simply bound
|\alpha_{\delta}(\lambda)|\leq\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\psi_{\lambda}\right\|_{L^{1}}\leq C2^{-j}
. Overall, this shows that

\Big(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}|\alpha_{\delta}(\lambda)|^{p^{\prime}}\Big)^{1/p^{\prime}}\leq C2^{j(2/p^{\prime}-1)}\delta^{2/p^{\prime}}\min(2^{-j}\delta^{-1},1).

We conclude that

\displaystyle\Big\|\Big\{2^{j(1-2/p^{\prime}+\beta^{2}/2)}\Big(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}|\alpha_{\delta}(\lambda)|^{p^{\prime}}\Big)^{1/p^{\prime}}\Big\}_{j\geq 1}\Big\|_{\ell^{q^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\delta^{2q^{\prime}/p^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor|\log_{2}\delta|\right\rfloor}2^{jq^{\prime}\beta^{2}/2}+\delta^{2q^{\prime}/p^{\prime}-q^{\prime}}\sum_{j>\left\lfloor|\log_{2}\delta|\right\rfloor}2^{-jq^{\prime}(1-\beta^{2}/2)}\Big)^{1/q^{\prime}}\leq C\delta^{2/p^{\prime}-\beta^{2}/2}.

One can similarly control the coefficients coming from the father wavelet
\phi
to obtain the bound (
6.13
).
This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.7
.
∎

7.
Convergence to white noise: proof of Theorem
1.8

Throughout the section, we assume without loss of generality that
[0,1]^{2}+B(0,2)\subset D
.
Recall that, as defined in (
1.9
), we consider for all
r\in(0,1)
the process

W_{r}(z)\coloneqq r^{\beta^{2}-5}\big(|\mu(Q(z,r))|^{2}-\mathbb{E}|\mu(Q(z,r))|^{2}\big),\quad z\in[0,1]^{2}.

The goal of this section is to prove the convergence of
W_{r}
as
r\to 0
to the white noise as stated in Theorem
1.8
.
It is in fact technically more convenient to work with an integrated version of
W_{r}
that is defined, for
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
, by

B_{r}(x,y)\coloneqq\sqrt{A}\int_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}W_{r}(z)\,dz

where
A>0
is the normalising constant whose inverse is defined in (
7.1
) below.
The next result states that
(B_{r})_{r\in(0,1)}
converges to the Brownian sheet:

Theorem 7.1
.

The processes
(B_{r})_{r\in(0,1)}
converge in distribution in
C^{0}([0,1]^{2})
, but not in probability, to the Brownian sheet in
[0,1]^{2}
as
r\to 0
.

Let us first see how the convergence of
(W_{r})_{r\in(0,1)}
follows:

Proof of Theorem
1.8
, assuming Theorem
7.1
.

By definition, for all
x,y\in[0,1]^{2}
,
W_{r}(x,y)=A^{-1/2}\partial_{x}\partial_{y}B_{r}(x,y)
. Because
(B_{r})_{r\in(0,1)}
converges in distribution in
C^{0}([0,1]^{2})
, it also converges in distribution in
L^{2}([0,1]^{2})
. By duality, it implies that
(W_{r})_{r\in(0,1)}
converges in distribution in
H^{-2}_{0}([0,1]^{2})
to
A^{-1/2}
times the distributional derivative
\partial_{x}\partial_{y}
of the Brownian sheet. The latter is a constant multiple of the white noise.
∎

The lemmas quoted in the following proof will be stated and proved in the subsequent sections (Sections
7.1
,
7.2
and
7.3
).

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

By Lemma
7.2
we see that
(B_{r})_{r\in(0,1)}
is tight in
C([0,1]^{2})
.
To show convergence in distribution it is therefore enough to show the uniqueness of subsequential limits.
Assume thus that
B_{r_{k}}\to B
in
C([0,1]^{2})
for some sequence
r_{k}\to 0
. We note that by Lemma
7.5

B(s,t)-B(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
is independent of
B(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
whenever
s\geq s^{\prime}
and
t\geq t^{\prime}
. Together with the variance estimate
\mathbb{E}B(s,t)^{2}=st
established in Lemma
7.6
this is enough to characterise the Brownian sheet by
[
Zak81
, Proposition 5.4]
.

If the convergence were true in probability, then in particular
B_{r}(1,1)
would have to converge in probability, and indeed even in
L^{2}(\Omega)
by the uniform integrability coming from the moment bound obtained in Lemma
7.4
. This, however, is ruled out by Lemma
7.7
.
∎

Before stating and proving the intermediate lemmas mentioned in the previous proof, we would like to highlight a key feature of the proofs of this section.

Symmetrization trick.
In several places in this section, we will use “symmetrization tricks” that allow us to bound efficiently integrals with integrands whose signs are alternating. We give here a concrete simple example. For
x,y,u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let

\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)=\frac{|x-u|^{\beta^{2}}|y-v|^{\beta^{2}}}{|x-v|^{\beta^{2}}|y-u|^{\beta^{2}}}.

Let us consider the following integral:

(7.1)

A^{-1}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}dw\int_{Q(0,1)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,1)^{2}}du\,dv\frac{\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)-1}{|x-y|^{\beta^{2}}|u-v|^{\beta^{2}}}.

We wish to show that it is absolutely convergent as an integral over
w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The only possible source of divergence is when
|w|\to\infty
. Expanding
\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)-1
, we can show that for
x,y,u,v
as in the integral above,
|\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)-1|=O(|w|^{-2})
as
|w|\to\infty
. This estimate is not sufficient for the absolute integrability of the integral (we would get a logarithmic divergence), but we can exploit the symmetry of the integrand to show that it is in fact
O(|w|^{-4}
). Indeed, notice that swapping
x
and
y
changes
\mathbf{r}
to
1/\mathbf{r}
:
\mathbf{r}(y,x,u,v)=1/\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)
. Therefore, for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\displaystyle f(w)

\displaystyle:=\int_{Q(0,1)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,1)^{2}}du\,dv\frac{\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)-1}{|x-y|^{\beta^{2}}|u-v|^{\beta^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q(0,1)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,1)^{2}}du\,dv\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\beta^{2}}|u-v|^{\beta^{2}}}\Big(\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)+\frac{1}{\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)}-2\Big).

If
|w|
is large enough and if we denote by
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)
for
x,y,u,v
as in the above integral,
\mathbf{r}+1/\mathbf{r}-2=(\mathbf{r}-1)^{2}/\mathbf{r}
, so we get
f(w)=O(|w|^{-4})
as claimed. The fact that the integral is positive also follows from the fact
(\mathbf{r}-1)^{2}/\mathbf{r}
is nonnegative.

7.1.
Tightness of
(B_{r})_{r\in(0,1)}

In this section, we show the following tightness result:

Lemma 7.2
.

The family
(B_{r})_{r\in(0,1)}
is tight in
C^{0}([0,1]^{2})
.

Before proving this lemma, we first state and prove two intermediate results. The first one is a simple estimate on the Green function. The second one, on the other hand, provides a precise upper bound on the correlations of
W_{r}
which is the main technical ingredient needed in the proof of Lemma
7.2
. In both of these lemmas we need to bound integrals whose integrands do not have constant signs. The cancellations are important to take into account and we will do so using symmetrization tricks similar in flavour to the one below (
7.1
).

Lemma 7.3
.

There exists
C>0
such that for any
z_{1},w_{1},z_{2},w_{2}\in[0,1]^{2}
we have

|G(z_{1},z_{2})+G(w_{1},w_{2})-G(z_{1},w_{2})-G(w_{1},z_{2})|\leq C\frac{|z_{1}-w_{1}||z_{2}-w_{2}|}{r^{2}},

where
r=\min(|z_{1}-z_{2}|,|z_{1}-w_{2}|,|w_{1}-z_{2}|,|w_{1}-w_{2}|)
.

Proof.

Let
r
be as in the statement of lemma and denote by
\Gamma_{r}
the mollification of
\Gamma
with respect to some radially symmetric non-negative mollifier
\varphi
whose support is included in the unit ball. Since the Green function is harmonic, we have

\displaystyle|G(z_{1},z_{2})+G(w_{1},w_{2})-G(z_{1},w_{2})-G(w_{1},z_{2})|=|\mathbb{E}[(\Gamma(z_{1})-\Gamma(w_{1}))(\Gamma(z_{2})-\Gamma(w_{2}))]|

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{E}[(\Gamma_{r}(z_{1})-\Gamma_{r}(w_{1}))(\Gamma_{r}(z_{2})-\Gamma_{r}(w_{2}))]|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(\Gamma_{r}(z_{1})-\Gamma_{r}(w_{1}))^{2}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(\Gamma_{r}(z_{2})-\Gamma_{r}(w_{2}))^{2}}.

By Jensen’s inequality we further have

|\Gamma_{r}(z)-\Gamma_{r}(w)|^{2}\leq|z-w|^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{|z-w|}\int_{0}^{|z-w|}\Big|\nabla\Gamma_{r}\big(w+t\frac{z-w}{|z-w|}\big)\Big|^{2}\,dt\Big).

Lemma
7.3
will then follow from the following bound: there exists
C>0
such that for all
x\in[0,1]^{2}
and
r\in(0,\sqrt{2})
,

(7.2)

\mathbb{E}|\nabla\Gamma_{r}(x)|^{2}\leq Cr^{-2}.

We now prove (
7.2
). Let
x\in[0,1]^{2}
and
r\in(0,\sqrt{2})
.
We have

\mathbb{E}|\partial_{1}\Gamma_{r}(x)|^{2}=\int_{B(0,r)^{2}}G(x-y,x-z)\partial_{1}\varphi_{r}(y)\partial_{1}\varphi_{r}(z)dydz.

We want to bound the integral on the right hand side whose integrand does not have a constant sign. In order to take into account some cancellations, we will use the following
symmetrization trick
.
Since
\varphi
is radially symmetric,
2\mathbb{E}|\partial_{1}\Gamma_{r}(x)|^{2}
can be written as

(7.3)

\displaystyle\int_{B(0,r)^{2}}(G(x-y,x-z)-G(x+y,x-z))\partial_{1}\varphi_{r}(y)\partial_{1}\varphi_{r}(z)dydz

\displaystyle\leq r^{-6}\left\|\partial_{1}\varphi\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{B(0,r)^{2}}|G(x-y,x-z)-G(x+y,x-z)|dydz.

Recall that we can write
G(y,z)=-\log|y-z|+g(y,z)
where
g
is bounded in
[0,1]^{2}+B(0,\sqrt{2})
. Since

\int_{B(0,r)^{2}}\left|\log\frac{|y+z|}{|y-z|}\right|dydz=O(r^{4})

by scaling, this shows that
\mathbb{E}|\partial_{1}\Gamma_{r}(x)|^{2}
is bounded by
Cr^{-2}
for some
C>0
. One can obtain a similar bound for
\mathbb{E}|\partial_{2}\Gamma_{r}(x)|^{2}
concluding the proof of (
7.2
). Note that without the symmetrization trick, we would have obtained an extra
|\log r|
factor on the right hand side of (
7.2
). This finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 7.4
.

For any
N\geq 1
there exists
C_{N}>0
such that for any measurable subset
U\subset[0,1]^{2}
we have

(7.4)

\int_{U^{N}}|\mathbb{E}[W_{r}(z_{1})\dots W_{r}(z_{N})]|\,dz_{1}\dots dz_{N}\leq C_{N}|U|^{N/2}.

Proof.

We can split the integral (
7.4
) in several parts depending on which points are further than
4r
away from their closest neighbours. Without loss of generality it is therefore enough to check that for any
0\leq n\leq N
we have

\int_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}|\mathbb{E}[W_{r}(z_{1})\dots W_{r}(z_{n})W_{r}(w_{1})\dots W_{r}(w_{N-n})]|\lesssim|U|^{N/2},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n}

\displaystyle=\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{n},w_{1},\dots,w_{N-n})\in U^{N}:|w_{j}-z_{k}|\geq 4r\text{ for all }j,k

\displaystyle\wedge|w_{j}-w_{k}|\geq 4r\text{ for all }j\neq k

\displaystyle\wedge\text{for all }j\text{ we have }|z_{j}-z_{k}|\leq 4r\text{ for some }k\neq j\}.

Recalling the notation
G_{D}^{\prime}
defined in (
2.4
), we compute

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[W_{r}(z_{1})\dots W_{r}(z_{n})W_{r}(w_{1})\dots W_{r}(w_{N-n})]|

\displaystyle=r^{N\beta^{2}-5N}\Big|\mathbb{E}\int\prod_{j=1}^{n}\big(\mu(x_{j})\overline{\mu(y_{j})}-e^{\beta^{2}G^{\prime}(x_{j},y_{j})}\big)\prod_{j=1}^{N-n}\big(\mu(u_{j})\overline{\mu(v_{j})}-e^{\beta^{2}G^{\prime}(u_{j},v_{j})}\big)\Big|,

where the integral is over
x_{j},y_{j}\in Q(z_{j},r)
and
u_{k},v_{k}\in Q(w_{k},r)
with
1\leq j\leq n
,
1\leq k\leq N-n
.
Recall the notation
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Gamma;\cdot;\cdot)
defined in (
2.5
) and the formula (
2.7
). In the display below, we denote by
\mathbf{x}_{\pi}=(x_{j})_{j\in\pi}
and similar notations for
\mathbf{x}_{\tau}
,
\mathbf{y}_{\pi},\mathbf{y}_{\tau}
.
Expanding the two products and computing the expected value gives

\displaystyle r^{N\beta^{2}-5N}\Big|\int\sum_{\pi\subset\{1,\dots,n\}}\sum_{\tau\subset\{1,\dots,N-n\}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{j\in\pi}(\mu(x_{j})\overline{\mu(y_{j})})\prod_{j\notin\pi}(-e^{\beta^{2}G^{\prime}(x_{j},y_{j})})

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{j\in\tau}(\mu(u_{j})\overline{\mu(v_{j})})\prod_{j\notin\tau}(-e^{\beta^{2}G^{\prime}(u_{j},v_{j})})\Big]\Big|

\displaystyle=r^{N\beta^{2}-5N}\Big|\int\sum_{\pi\subset\{1,\dots,n\}}e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{\pi},\mathbf{y}_{\pi})}\prod_{j\notin\pi}e^{\beta^{2}G^{\prime}(x_{j},y_{j})}\sum_{\tau\subset\{1,\dots,N-n\}}(-1)^{|\pi|+|\tau|}e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}_{\tau},\mathbf{v}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot e^{\beta^{2}\sum_{j\in\pi,k\in\tau}(G^{\prime}(x_{j},v_{k})+G^{\prime}(y_{j},u_{k})-G^{\prime}(x_{j},u_{k})-G^{\prime}(y_{j},v_{k}))}\prod_{j\notin\tau}e^{\beta^{2}G^{\prime}(u_{j},v_{j})}\Big|.

Note next that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}_{\tau},\mathbf{v}_{\tau})+\sum_{j\in\pi,k\in\tau}(G^{\prime}(x_{j},v_{k})+G^{\prime}(y_{j},u_{k})-G^{\prime}(x_{j},u_{k})-G^{\prime}(y_{j},v_{k}))+\sum_{j\notin\tau}G^{\prime}(u_{j},v_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N-n}G^{\prime}(u_{j},v_{j})+\sum_{j,k\in\tau,j<k}\Delta_{j,k}+\sum_{j\in\pi,k\in\tau}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,k},

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq G^{\prime}(x_{j},v_{k})+G^{\prime}(y_{j},u_{k})-G^{\prime}(x_{j},u_{k})-G^{\prime}(y_{j},v_{k})\quad\text{for all }1\leq j\leq n,1\leq k\leq N-n

\displaystyle\Delta_{j,k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq G^{\prime}(u_{j},v_{k})+G^{\prime}(u_{k},v_{j})-G^{\prime}(u_{j},u_{k})-G^{\prime}(v_{j},v_{k})\quad\text{for all }1\leq j<k\leq N-n.

Thus we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[W_{r}(z_{1})\dots W_{r}(z_{n})W_{r}(w_{1})\dots W_{r}(w_{N-n})]|

\displaystyle=r^{N\beta^{2}-5N}\Big|\int\sum_{\pi\subset\{1,\dots,n\}}(-1)^{|\pi|}e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{\pi},\mathbf{y}_{\pi})+\beta^{2}\sum_{j\notin\pi}G^{\prime}(x_{j},y_{j})}e^{\beta^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N-n}G^{\prime}(u_{j},v_{j})}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\sum_{\tau\subset\{1,\dots,N-n\}}(-1)^{|\tau|}e^{\beta^{2}\sum_{j,k\in\tau,j<k}\Delta_{j,k}+\beta^{2}\sum_{j\in\pi,k\in\tau}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,k}}\Big|.

We now use once more a symmetrization trick by observing that the integrand is symmetric upon swapping the variables
u_{k}\leftrightarrow v_{k}
. Moreover doing the swap simply switches the sign of
\Delta_{j,k}
and
\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,k}
. Hence we can write the above as

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[W_{r}(z_{1})\dots W_{r}(z_{n})W_{r}(w_{1})\dots W_{r}(w_{N-n})]|

\displaystyle=2^{-(N-n)}r^{N\beta^{2}-5N}\Big|\int\sum_{\pi\subset\{1,\dots,n\}}(-1)^{|\pi|}e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{\pi},\mathbf{y}_{\pi})+\beta^{2}\sum_{j\notin\pi}G^{\prime}(x_{j},y_{j})}e^{\beta^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N-n}G^{\prime}(u_{j},v_{j})}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{N-n}}\sum_{\tau\subset\{1,\dots,N-n\}}(-1)^{|\tau|}e^{\beta^{2}\sum_{j,k\in\tau,j<k}(-1)^{\sigma_{j}+\sigma_{k}}\Delta_{j,k}+\beta^{2}\sum_{j\in\pi,k\in\tau}(-1)^{\sigma_{k}}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,k}}\Big|.

We will next focus on estimating the two inner sums in terms of the variables
\Delta_{j,k},\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,k}
, which by Lemma
7.3
are bounded by
C\frac{r^{2}}{|w_{j}-w_{k}|^{2}}\leq R
(resp. by
C\frac{r^{2}}{|z_{j}-w_{k}|^{2}}\leq R
) for some
R>0
. To this end we define the analytic function

\displaystyle f((\Delta_{j,k}),(\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,k}))\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{N-n}}\sum_{\tau\subset\{1,\dots,N-n\}}(-1)^{|\tau|}e^{\beta^{2}\sum_{j,k\in\tau,j<k}(-1)^{\sigma_{j}+\sigma_{k}}\Delta_{j,k}+\beta^{2}\sum_{j\in\pi,k\in\tau}(-1)^{\sigma_{k}}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,k}}

and consider its Taylor series around
0
. We have by the multinomial theorem that

\displaystyle f((\Delta_{j,k}),(\widetilde{\Delta}_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{N-n}}\sum_{\tau\subset\{1,\dots,N-n\}}(-1)^{|\tau|}\frac{\beta^{2\ell}}{\ell!}\big(\sum_{j,k}(-1)^{\sigma_{j}+\sigma_{k}}\Delta_{j,k}+\sum_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(-1)^{\sigma_{k^{\prime}}}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\big)^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{N-n}}\sum_{\tau\subset\{1,\dots,N-n\}}\sum_{\sum_{j,k}p_{j,k}+\sum_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}=\ell}\frac{(-1)^{|\tau|}\beta^{2\ell}}{\ell!}{\ell\choose(p_{j,k})_{j,k},(q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{j,k}(-1)^{p_{j,k}(\sigma_{j}+\sigma_{k})}\Delta_{j,k}^{p_{j,k}}\prod_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(-1)^{q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\sigma_{k^{\prime}}}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}^{q_{j,k}},

where the sums and products and index sets of variables are over
j,k\in\tau,j<k
and
j^{\prime}\in\pi,k^{\prime}\in\tau
. We may equivalently rephrase this condition by saying that the variables
p_{j,k}
and
q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
range over the whole index set with
1\leq j<k\leq N-n
and
1\leq j^{\prime}\leq n
,
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq N-n
, that their sum is
\ell
and with the extra condition that
p_{j,k}=0
if one of
j,k
is not in
\tau
and similarly
q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}=0
if
j^{\prime}\notin\pi
or
k^{\prime}\notin\tau
. Exchanging the two inner most sums then gives us

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{N-n}}\sum_{\sum_{j,k}p_{j,k}+\sum_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}=\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\subset\{1,\dots,N-n\}\\
p_{j,k}\neq 0\Rightarrow j,k\in\tau\\
q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\neq 0\Rightarrow k^{\prime}\in\tau\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{|\tau|}\beta^{2\ell}}{\ell!}{\ell\choose(p_{j,k})_{j,k},(q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{j,k}(-1)^{p_{j,k}(\sigma_{j}+\sigma_{k})}\Delta_{j,k}^{p_{j,k}}\prod_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(-1)^{q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\sigma_{k^{\prime}}}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}^{q_{j,k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{N-n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum_{j,k}p_{j,k}+\sum_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}=\ell\\
\forall 1\leq r\leq N-n\text{ exists non-zero }p_{j,r},p_{r,k}\text{ or }q_{j^{\prime},r}\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{N-n}\beta^{2\ell}}{\ell!}{\ell\choose(p_{j,k})_{j,k},(q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{j,k}(-1)^{p_{j,k}(\sigma_{j}+\sigma_{k})}\Delta_{j,k}^{p_{j,k}}\prod_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(-1)^{q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\sigma_{k^{\prime}}}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}^{q_{j,k}},

where we have used the fact that if the indices
j,k,k^{\prime}
of those
p_{j,k}
,
q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
that are non-zero span a set
S\subsetneq\{1,\dots,N-n\}
, then the sum over
\tau
can be written as a sum over the subsets of
\{1,\dots,N-n\}\setminus S
and for any non-empty finite set
A
we have
\sum_{\tau\subset A}(-1)^{|\tau|}=0
.

We note next that the sum over
\sigma
will be
0
unless every
\sigma_{j}
appears at least twice in the two products (indeed, if
\sigma_{j}
appears only once, the terms corresponding to
\sigma_{j}=0
and
\sigma_{j}=1
would be opposite of each other). Because the series is absolutely summable this means that we have the bound

|f((\Delta_{j,k}),(\widetilde{\Delta}_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}))|\leq C\sum_{p_{j,k},q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}}\prod_{j,k}\frac{r^{2p_{j,k}}}{|w_{j}-w_{k}|^{2p_{j,k}}}\prod_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\frac{r^{2q_{j,k}}}{|z_{j^{\prime}}-w_{k^{\prime}}|^{2q_{j,k}}},

where
C>0
is some constant that depends on
N
and
R
and the sum is over such
p_{j,k}
and
q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
for which

•

for all
1\leq r\leq N-n
we have that there exist at least two different pairs
(j,r),(r,k)
or
(j^{\prime},r)
such that the corresponding variables are non-zero, and

•

the variables
p_{j,k}
and
q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
are minimal in the sense that the above condition is not anymore satisfied after decreasing any of the variables
p_{j,k}
and
q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
by one.

One easily checks that there are only finitely many such configurations
p_{j,k}
,
q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
. In light of the above bound we see that it is enough to check that

\displaystyle r^{N\beta^{2}-5N}\int_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\int e^{\beta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{\pi},\mathbf{y}_{\pi})+\beta^{2}\sum_{j\notin\pi}G^{\prime}(x_{j},y_{j})}e^{\beta^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N-n}G^{\prime}(u_{j},v_{j})}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{j,k}\frac{r^{2p_{j,k}}}{|w_{j}-w_{k}|^{2p_{j,k}}}\prod_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\frac{r^{2q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}}}{|z_{j^{\prime}}-w_{k^{\prime}}|^{2q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}}}\lesssim|U|^{N/2}

where we have fixed
\pi\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
and a configuration
p_{j,k},q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
satisfying the conditions given above. As a reminder, here the first integral is over those points
z_{1},\dots,z_{n},w_{1},\dots,w_{N-n}\in U^{N}
that satisfy the nearest neighbour distance requirements given in the beginning and the second integral is again over
x_{j},y_{j}\in Q(z_{j},r)
and
u_{j},v_{j}\in Q(w_{j},r)
. The integral over
u_{j},v_{j}
is
O(r^{(N-n)(4-\beta^{2})})
and the integral over those
x_{j},y_{j}
for which
j\notin\pi
is similarly
O(r^{(n-|\pi|)(4-\beta^{2})})
. The integral over those
x_{j},y_{j}
for which
j\in\pi
is by a standard application of Onsager’s inequality and scaling
O(r^{|\pi|(4-\beta^{2})})
. Altogether we hence have an upper boundd

r^{-N}\int_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\prod_{j,k}\frac{r^{2p_{j,k}}}{|w_{j}-w_{k}|^{2p_{j,k}}}\prod_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\frac{r^{2q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}}}{|z_{j^{\prime}}-w_{k^{\prime}}|^{2q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}}}.

We will next bound the integrals over the
w_{j}
. It is helpful to view the dependencies as a graph with vertices
z_{1},\dots,z_{n},w_{1},\dots,w_{N-n}
such that there are
p_{j,k}
edges between
w_{j}
and
w_{k}
and
q_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
edges between
z_{j^{\prime}}
and
w_{k^{\prime}}
. The minimality condition of the configuration implies that for each of the edges one of the endpoints must have degree at most
2
. We will then proceed integrating over all of the vertices of the graph using the following operations:

(1)

Pick a vertex
w_{j}
with degree
2
that has a double edge to its neighbour
u
. Integrate
\frac{r^{4}\mathbf{1}_{|w_{j}-u|\geq 4r}}{|w_{j}-u|^{4}}
over
w_{j}
to obtain an upper bound of order
r^{2}
and remove
w_{j}
from the graph.

(2)

Pick a vertex
w_{j}
with degree
2
that has a double edge to its neighbour
w_{k}
. Integrate
\frac{r^{4}\mathbf{1}_{|w_{j}-u|\geq 4r}}{|w_{j}-u|^{4}}
over
w_{j}
and use Jensen’s inequality to obtain an upper bound of order
r\sqrt{|U|}
. Remove
w_{j}
from the graph.

(3)

Pick a vertex
w_{j}
with degree
2
that has two neighbours
u
and
v
and which is such that removing
w_{j}
does not increase the number of connected components of the graph when restricted to the
w
-vertices. Use the AM-GM inequality to replace the term
\frac{r^{4}}{|w_{j}-u|^{2}|w_{j}-v|^{2}}
with the term
\frac{r^{4}}{|w_{j}-u|^{4}}+\frac{r^{4}}{|w_{j}-v|^{4}}
, effectively replacing the graph with two new graphs where instead of two single edges
w_{j}
now has a double edge between either
u
or
v
.

(4)

Pick a vertex
w_{j}
with degree
1
and neighbour
u
. Integrate
\frac{r^{2}\mathbf{1}_{|w_{j}-w_{k}|}}{|w_{j}-u|^{2}}
over
u
and use Jensen’s inequality to obtain an upper bound of order
r\sqrt{|U|}
. Remove
w_{j}
from the graph.

(5)

Pick a vertex
w_{j}
with degree
0
. Integrating over
w_{j}
gives a bound of order
|U|
. Remove
w_{j}
from the graph.

We will now exhaust all the vertices
w_{j}
in the graph by using the above operations in the following manner: Start by picking a connected component of the graph. If this connected component contains any
z
-vertices, we simply use operations (2), (3) and (4), thereby obtaining an upper bound of
(r\sqrt{|U|})^{m}
, where
m
is the number of vertices
w_{j}
in the connected component. If the component does not contain any
z
-vertices, we will use one operation (1) (possibly after using (3) first) and then do operations (2), (3) and (4) until only a degree
0
-vertex remains so that we finish with one operation (5). The operations (1) and (5) together give us
r^{2}|U|
and the rest of the operations give
(r\sqrt{|U|})^{m-2}
, so we again get
(r\sqrt{|U|})^{m}
in total. Taking the product over all the components gives an upper bound of order
r^{N-n}|U|^{\frac{N-n}{2}}
.

Finally we will integrate over the
z_{j}
under the constraint that every
z_{j}
has another
x_{k}
no more than
4r
away from it. Here one may split the integral according to the nearest neighbour function as in the proof of
[
JSW20
, Lemma 3.10]
. Fixing a nearest neighbour function, recall that the nearest neighbour graph of
z_{j}
consists of components which are built from two trees whose roots are joined in a 2-cycle. We then get for each such component in a straightforward manner an upper bound of order
\min(r^{2},|U|)^{m-2}r^{2}|U|
, where
m\geq 2
is the number of vertices in the component. Since
\min(r^{2},|U|)\leq r\sqrt{|U|}
, we in fact have an upper bound of
r^{m}|U|^{\frac{m}{2}}
for the given component and taking the product over all the components gives an upper bound of order
r^{n}|U|^{\frac{n}{2}}
, which finishes the proof.
∎

Now that we have proved Lemma
7.4
,
Lemma
7.2
will follow quickly:

Proof of Lemma
7.2
.

By Kolmogorov’s tightness criterion (see e.g.
[
SV06
, Theorem 2.1.6]
) it is enough to check e.g. that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\mathbb{E}|B_{r}(z)-B_{r}(w)|^{6}\leq C|z-w|^{3}

for all
z,w\in[0,1]^{2}
and
r>0
. This on the other hand follows from Lemma
7.4
since

\mathbb{E}|B_{r}(z)-B_{r}(w)|^{6}\leq A^{3}\int_{U^{6}}|\mathbb{E}[W_{r}(z_{1})\dots W_{r}(z_{6})]|dz_{1}\dots dz_{6}\lesssim|U|^{3},

where
A
is defined in (
7.1
) and
where
U
is the symmetric difference between
[0,\operatorname{Re}(z)]\times[0,\operatorname{Im}(z)]
and
[0,\operatorname{Re}(w)]\times[0,\operatorname{Im}(w)]
and thus
|U|\lesssim|z-w|
.
∎

7.2.
Study of subsequential limits

Thanks to Lemma
7.2
, we now know that
(B_{r})_{r\in(0,1)}
is tight. In this section, we will study the properties of its subsequential limits. We will show in Lemma
7.5
the independence of their increments and compute in Lemma
7.6
the value of the variance.

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
s\geq s^{\prime}
and
t\geq t^{\prime}
and let
B
be a subsequential limit in distribution of
(B_{r})_{r}
. Then
B(s,t)-B(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
is independent of
B(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We first note that by continuity it is enough to check that for any
0<\varepsilon<\min(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
we have
(B(s,t)-B(s^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\bot B(s^{\prime}-\varepsilon,t^{\prime}-\varepsilon)
. Let us fix such
\varepsilon
and set
a\coloneqq s^{\prime}-\varepsilon
,
b\coloneqq t^{\prime}-\varepsilon
. Let us decompose our GFF as
\Gamma=\widetilde{\Gamma}+h
, where
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is a zero-boundary GFF in
\widetilde{D}:=[0,s^{\prime}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]\times[0,t^{\prime}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]
and
h
is harmonic in
\widetilde{D}
and independent of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
. We then define for all
r>0
the random variables

\widetilde{B}_{r}\coloneqq\sqrt{A}\int_{[0,a]\times[0,b]}\tilde{h}_{r}(z)\,dz,

where where
A
is defined in (
7.1
) and

\tilde{h}_{r}(z)\coloneqq r^{\beta^{2}-5}\Big(\big|\int_{Q(z,r)}e^{i\beta\widetilde{\Gamma}(x)}\,dx\big|^{2}-\mathbb{E}\big|\int_{Q(z,r)}e^{i\beta\widetilde{\Gamma}(x)}\,dx\big|^{2}\Big).

Notice that
\widetilde{B}_{r}
is independent of
B_{r}(s,t)-B_{r}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
for all
r<\varepsilon/2
. We will therefore be done if we can show that
\mathbb{E}|\widetilde{B}_{r}-B_{r}(a,b)|^{2}\to 0
as
r\to 0
.
In this proof, we will write for
x,y,u,v\in\widetilde{D}
,

\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\Gamma}(x)\widetilde{\Gamma}(y)]-\frac{1}{2}\log\CR(x,\widetilde{D})-\frac{1}{2}\log\CR(y,\widetilde{D})

and

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(x,u;y,v)=\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,y)+\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,v)+\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(u,y)+\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(u,v)-\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,u)-\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(y,v).

We have

\mathbb{E}|\widetilde{B}_{r}-B_{r}(a,b)|^{2}=A\int_{([0,a]\times[0,b])^{2}}dz\,dw\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{h}_{r}(z)-W_{r}(z))(\tilde{h}_{r}(w)-W_{r}(w))].

We will be done by dominated convergence theorem if we can show that
\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{h}_{r}(z)-W_{r}(z))(\tilde{h}_{r}(w)-W_{r}(w))]
is bounded and that for
z\neq w
it tends to
0
as
r\to 0
.

We will start by showing the second claim. Here it is enough to consider the four terms
\mathbb{E}\tilde{h}_{r}(z)\tilde{h}_{r}(w)
,
\mathbb{E}\tilde{h}_{r}(z)W_{r}(w)
,
\mathbb{E}W_{r}(z)\tilde{h}_{r}(w)
and
\mathbb{E}W_{r}(z)W_{r}(w)
separately. By looking at the proof of Lemma
7.4
we see that
\mathbb{E}\tilde{h}_{r}(z)\tilde{h}_{r}(w)
and
\mathbb{E}W_{r}(z)W_{r}(w)
will for small enough
r
have an upper bound of the form
\frac{r^{2}}{|z-w|^{4}}
. The mixed case
\mathbb{E}W_{r}(z)\tilde{h}_{r}(w)
can be reduced to the
\mathbb{E}\tilde{h}_{r}(z)\tilde{h}_{r}(w)
case as follows: We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}W_{r}(z)\tilde{h}_{r}(w)=r^{2\beta^{2}-10}\int_{Q(z,r)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,r)^{2}}du\,dv\mathbb{E}\big[(e^{i\beta\Gamma(x)}e^{-i\beta\Gamma(y)}-e^{\beta^{2}G^{\prime}(x,y)})

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0pt\cdot(e^{i\beta\widetilde{\Gamma}(u)}e^{-i\beta\widetilde{\Gamma}(v)}-e^{\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(u,v)})\big]

\displaystyle=r^{2\beta^{2}-10}\int_{Q(z,r)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,r)^{2}}du\,dve^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(x)-h(y))^{2}]}\mathbb{E}\big[(e^{i\beta\widetilde{\Gamma}(x)}e^{-i\beta\widetilde{\Gamma}(y)}-e^{\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,y)})

\displaystyle\quad\cdot(e^{i\beta\widetilde{\Gamma}(u)}e^{-i\beta\widetilde{\Gamma}(v)}-e^{\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(u,v)})\big]

\displaystyle=r^{2\beta^{2}-10}\int_{Q(z,r)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,r)^{2}}du\,dve^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(x)-h(y))^{2}]}e^{\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,y)+\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(u,v)}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\big(e^{\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,v)+\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(y,u)-\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,u)-\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(y,v)}-1\big).

Notice that the integral won’t change if we swap
x
and
y
in the integrand. Thus the above equals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}r^{2\beta^{2}-10}\int_{Q(z,r)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,r)^{2}}du\,dve^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(x)-h(y))^{2}]}e^{\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,y)+\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(u,v)}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\big(p(x,y,u,v)+p(x,y,u,v)^{-1}-2\big),

where

p(x,y,u,v)=e^{\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,v)+\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(y,u)-\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,u)-\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(y,v)}.

For any real number
r>0
we have
r+r^{-1}-2\geq 0
and
\mathbb{E}[(h(x)-h(y))^{2}]
is uniformly bounded in
[0,a]\times[0,b]
, so we see that

\mathbb{E}W_{r}(z)\tilde{h}_{r}(w)\lesssim\mathbb{E}\tilde{h}_{r}(z)\tilde{h}_{r}(w).

It remains to show the boundedness of
\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{h}_{r}(z)-W_{r}(z))(\tilde{h}_{r}(w)-W_{r}(w))]
. By Cauchy–Schwarz it is enough to show that
\mathbb{E}(\tilde{h}_{r}(z)-W_{r}(z))^{2}
is bounded. A direct but somewhat tedious computation shows that
\mathbb{E}(\tilde{h}_{r}(z)-W_{r}(z))^{2}
is equal to

\displaystyle r^{2\beta^{2}-10}\int_{Q(z,r)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(z,r)^{2}}du\,dv(e^{\beta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(x,u;y,v)}\mathfrak{A}+e^{\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(u,v)+\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,y)}\mathfrak{B})

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}=1-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(x)-h(y))^{2}]}-e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(u)-h(v))^{2}]}

\displaystyle+e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(x)-h(u))^{2}]+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(y)-h(v))2]-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(x)-h(y))^{2}]-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(u)-h(v))2]-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(x)-h(v))^{2}]-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(y)-h(u))^{2}]},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(x)-h(y))^{2}]}+e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(u)h(v))^{2}]}-e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(x)-h(y))^{2}]-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(h(u)-h(v))^{2}]}-1.

One easily checks that both in
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
all the exponents are
O(r)
. Thus by expanding the exponentials as series we see that
\mathfrak{B}=O(r^{2})
. For
\mathfrak{A}
we get

\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}h(x)h(v)+\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}h(y)h(u)-\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}h(x)h(u)-\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}h(y)h(v)+O(r^{2}),

but one also easily checks that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}h(x)h(v)+\mathbb{E}h(y)h(u)-\mathbb{E}h(x)h(u)-\mathbb{E}h(y)h(v)=-\mathbb{E}(h(x)-h(y))(h(u)-h(v))=O(r^{2}),

so in total
\mathbb{E}(\tilde{h}_{r}(z)-W_{r}(z))^{2}
is bounded by a constant times

\displaystyle r^{2\beta^{2}-8}\int_{B(z,r)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{B(w,r)^{2}}du\,dv(e^{\beta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x,y;u,v)}+e^{\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,y)+\beta^{2}\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(u,v)})\lesssim 1.

∎

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
B
be a subsequential limit in distribution of
B_{r}
. Then
\mathbb{E}B(s,t)^{2}=st
.

Proof.

Let
U=[0,s]\times[0,t]
. In this proof, we will denote by

\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)=\frac{|x-u|^{\beta^{2}}|y-v|^{\beta^{2}}}{|x-v|^{\beta^{2}}|y-u|^{\beta^{2}}}

and

g^{\prime}(x,y)=G(x,y)+\log|x-y|-\frac{1}{2}\log\CR(x,D)-\frac{1}{2}\log\CR(y,D),

for
x,y,u,v\in D
.
g^{\prime}
is smooth and vanishes on the diagonal. We will use several times the fact that swapping
x
and
y
changes
r
into
1/r
.
A direct computation shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}B_{r}(s,t)^{2}

\displaystyle=A\int_{U^{2}}dz\,dw\,r^{2\beta^{2}-10}\int_{Q(z,r)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,r)^{2}}du\,dv\frac{e^{\beta^{2}(g^{\prime}(x,y)+g^{\prime}(u,v))}}{|x-y|^{\beta^{2}}|u-v|^{\beta^{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\big(\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)e^{\beta^{2}(g^{\prime}(x,v)+g^{\prime}(y,u)-g^{\prime}(x,u)-g^{\prime}(y,v))}-1\big).

It is easy to see that in the limit as
r\to 0
we may replace
g^{\prime}(x,y)+g^{\prime}(u,v)
by
g^{\prime}(z,z)+g^{\prime}(w,w)=0
since the difference between the two is
O(r)
and the rest of the integral is bounded by arguments similar to Lemma
7.4
. Doing this substitution and by applying the symmetry upon swapping
x
and
y
(see the paragraph following (
7.1
) for more details) we get

\displaystyle\frac{A}{2}\int_{U^{2}}dz\,dw\,r^{2\beta^{2}-10}\int_{Q(z,r)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,r)^{2}}du\,dv\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\beta^{2}}|u-v|^{\beta^{2}}}\frac{((1+a)\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)-1)^{2}}{(1+a)\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)},

where
a=e^{\beta^{2}(g^{\prime}(x,v)+g^{\prime}(y,u)-g^{\prime}(x,u)-g^{\prime}(y,v))}-1=O(r^{2})
. Writing
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)
, we note that

\displaystyle\frac{(\mathbf{r}(1+a)-1)^{2}}{\mathbf{r}(1+a)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mathbf{r}-1)^{2}}{\mathbf{r}}-\frac{(\mathbf{r}-1)^{2}a}{\mathbf{r}(1+a)}+\frac{2a(\mathbf{r}-1)}{1+a}+\frac{\mathbf{r}a^{2}}{1+a}.

As
a=O(r^{2})
and
|\mathbf{r}-1|\lesssim\frac{r^{2}}{|z-w|^{2}}
, we see that only the first term
\frac{(\mathbf{r}-1)^{2}}{\mathbf{r}}
will remain in the limit. Undoing the symmetry trick, we thus have reduced the task to finding the limit of
A\int_{U^{2}}dz\,dwu_{r}(z,w)
, where

\displaystyle u_{r}(z,w)\coloneqq r^{2\beta^{2}-10}\int_{Q(z,r)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,r)^{2}}du\,dv\frac{\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)-1}{|x-y|^{\beta^{2}}|u-v|^{\beta^{2}}}.

We note that by the above considerations
|u_{r}(z,w)|=O(r)
when
|z-w|\geq r^{1/4}
. We may therefore restrict our focus to the case where
|z-w|<r^{1/4}
. Let us consider a fixed
z
and do the integral over
w
. By translation we may assume that
z=0
. We have by scaling

\displaystyle\int_{Q(0,r^{1/4})}dwr^{2\beta^{2}-10}\int_{Q(0,r)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,r)^{2}}du\,dv\frac{\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)-1}{|x-y|^{\beta^{2}}|u-v|^{\beta^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{Q(0,r^{-3/4})}dw\int_{Q(0,1)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,1)^{2}}du\,dv\frac{\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)-1}{|x-y|^{\beta^{2}}|u-v|^{\beta^{2}}}.

As we explained in the paragraph following (
7.1
), the integral w.r.t.
w
converges absolutely as
r\to 0
due to symmetry properties. Therefore, the right hand side of the above display converges as
r\to 0
to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}dw\int_{Q(0,1)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,1)^{2}}du\,dv\frac{\mathbf{r}(x,y,u,v)-1}{|x-y|^{\beta^{2}}|u-v|^{\beta^{2}}}.

This integral agrees with the inverse of the normalising constant
A
; see (
7.1
). The integral over
z
then gives
|U|=st
as required.
∎

7.3.
The convergence does not hold in probability

Lemma 7.7
.

The sequence
(B_{r}(1,1))_{r\in(0,1)}
is not Cauchy in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
r\to 0
.

Proof.

Let
0<r<s
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|B_{r}(1,1)-B_{s}(1,1)|^{2}=A\int_{([0,1]^{2})^{2}}dz\,dw\mathbb{E}[(W_{r}(z)-W_{s}(z))(W_{r}(w)-W_{s}(w))],

and thus we see that it is enough to show that for fixed
s
we have

\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{([0,1]^{2})^{2}}dz\,dw\mathbb{E}[W_{r}(z)W_{s}(w)]=0.

The expectation
\mathbb{E}[W_{r}(z)W_{s}(w)]
is equal to

\displaystyle r^{\beta^{2}-5}s^{\beta^{2}-5}\int_{Q(z,r)^{2}}dx\,dy\int_{Q(w,s)^{2}}du\,dve^{\beta^{2}G^{\prime}(x,y)+\beta^{2}G^{\prime}(u,v)}\big(e^{\beta^{2}(G^{\prime}(x,v)+G^{\prime}(y,u)-G^{\prime}(x,u)-G^{\prime}(y,v))}-1\big).

We will consider separately the cases where
|z-w|<r^{1/4}
and
|z-w|\geq r^{1/4}
. In the first case we note that since
G^{\prime}(x,v)-G^{\prime}(y,v)=O(r)
and the same holds for
G^{\prime}(y,u)-G^{\prime}(x,u)
, we have

|\mathbb{E}[W_{r}(z)W_{s}(w)]|\lesssim s^{-1}.

This gets multiplied by the measure of the set
|z-w|<r^{1/4}
, which is
O(\sqrt{r})
, and we get the claim.
In the second case we will use the bound from Lemma
7.3
and the symmetry trick of swapping
x
and
y
to get

|\mathbb{E}[W_{r}(z)W_{s}(w)]|\lesssim\frac{rs}{|z-w|^{4}},

whose integral over the set
|z-w|\geq r^{1/4}
is of the order
\sqrt{r}s
, which again goes to
0
as
r\to 0
.
∎

Appendix A
A variant of Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem

The following lemma is a variant of Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem. We provide a proof for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\left(B(x,r),x\in D,0\leq r\leq 1\right)
be a random process indexed by a three-dimensional set. Assume that there exist
N,\varepsilon,C>0
such that for every
x,x^{\prime}\in D,0\leq r,r^{\prime}\leq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B(x,r)-B(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\right|^{N}\right]\leq C\left\{(r\vee r^{\prime})\left\|(x,r)-(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\right\|\right\}^{3+\varepsilon}.

Then there is a modification
\tilde{B}
of
B
satisfying: for every configuration
\omega
and
for all
\alpha\in(0,\varepsilon/N)
, there exists
C_{\alpha}(\omega)\in(0,\infty)
such that for all
x,x^{\prime}\in D,0\leq r,r^{\prime}\leq 1
,

\left|\tilde{B}(x,r)-\tilde{B}(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\right|\leq C_{\alpha}(\omega)\left\{(r\vee r^{\prime})\left\|(x,r)-(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\right\|\right\}^{\alpha}.

Proof.

In this proof we will denote by
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{n}
(resp.
\mathcal{D}_{1}^{n}
) the set of two-dimensional (resp. one-dimensional) dyadic points of generation
n
lying in
D
(resp.
[0,1]
). We will write
\mathcal{D}_{2}=\cup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{D}_{2}^{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\cup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{D}_{1}^{n}
. Take
\alpha<\varepsilon/N
. If
x\in\mathcal{D}_{2}^{n},r\in\mathcal{D}_{1}^{n},r^{\prime}=r+2^{-n}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|B(x,r)-B(x,r^{\prime})\right|\geq(r\vee r^{\prime})^{\alpha}2^{-n\alpha}\right)\leq C\left((r\vee r^{\prime})2^{-n}\right)^{3+\varepsilon-\alpha N}\leq C2^{-n(3+\varepsilon-\alpha N)}.

By a union bound and because
\varepsilon-\alpha N>0
, we get that

\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists x\in\mathcal{D}_{2}^{n}:r\in\mathcal{D}_{1}^{n},\left|B(x,r)-B(x,r+2^{-n})\right|\geq(r+2^{-n})^{\alpha}2^{-n\alpha}\right)

is finite. By the Borel-Cantelli lemma, it implies that

\sup\left\{(r+2^{-n})^{-\alpha}2^{n\alpha}\left|B(x,r)-B(x,r+2^{-n})\right|:n\geq 1,x\in\mathcal{D}_{2}^{n},r\in\mathcal{D}_{1}^{n}\right\}

is almost surely finite (the quantity above is clearly finite for a finite number of
n
).
We can proceed in the same way for the first component to show that

\sup\left\{\frac{\left|B(x,r)-B(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\right|}{\left\{(r\vee r^{\prime})\left\|(x,r)-(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\right\|\right\}^{\alpha}}:\begin{array}[]{l}n\geq 1,x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{2}^{n},\\
r,r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{1}^{n},\\
\left\|(x,r)-(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\right\|_{L^{1}}=2^{-n}\end{array}\right\}

is almost surely finite. We denote this quantity by
K_{\alpha}
. Now consider
(x,r),(x,r^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}_{2}\times\mathcal{D}_{1}
. Assume for instance that
r<r^{\prime}
. Consider
p\geq 1
s.t.
2^{-p}\leq r^{\prime}-r\leq 2^{-p+1}
and
k
the smallest integer such that
r\leq k2^{-p}
. Let
\ell\geq 0
be s.t.
(x,r),(x,r^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}_{2}^{p+\ell}\times\mathcal{D}_{1}^{p+\ell}
. We can write
r
and
r^{\prime}
as

r=k2^{-p}-\varepsilon_{1}2^{-p-1}-\dots-\varepsilon_{\ell}2^{-p-\ell}\mathrm{~and~}r^{\prime}=k2^{-p}+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0}2^{-k}+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{1}2^{-p-1}+\dots+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\ell}2^{-p-\ell}

with
\varepsilon_{i},\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Define for all
i=0,\dots,\ell
,

r_{i}=k2^{-p}-\varepsilon_{1}2^{-p-1}-\dots-\varepsilon_{i}2^{-p-i}\mathrm{~and~}r^{\prime}_{i}=k2^{-p}+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0}2^{-k}+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{1}2^{-p-1}+\dots+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}2^{-p-i}.

In particular,
r_{i},r^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{1}^{p+i}
and
r_{i}-r_{i+1},r^{\prime}_{i+1}-r^{\prime}_{i}\in\{0,2^{-p-i-1}\}
. Notice that for all
i=0\dots\ell-1,r_{i}\vee r^{\prime}_{i}\leq r^{\prime}
. Similarly, there exists a sequence
(x_{i},i=0\dots 2\ell)
with
x_{2\ell}=x
such that

\displaystyle\forall i=0,\dots,\ell-1,x_{2i},x_{2i+1}\in\mathcal{D}_{2}^{p+i},\left\|x_{2i}-x_{2i+1}\right\|_{L^{1}}\in\{0,2^{-p-i}\}.

We can bound from above
\left|B(x,r)-B(x_{0},r_{0})\right|
by

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\left|B(x_{2i+2},r_{i+1})-B(x_{2i+1},r_{i+1})\right|+\left|B(x_{2i+1},r_{i+1})-B(x_{2i},r_{i+1})\right|+\left|B(x_{2i},r_{i+1})-B(x_{2i},r_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq 3K_{\alpha}(r^{\prime})^{\alpha}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}2^{-(p+i+1)\alpha}\leq 3\frac{2^{-\alpha}}{1-2^{-\alpha}}K_{\alpha}(r^{\prime})^{\alpha}2^{-p\alpha}.

The same bound holds true for
\left|B(x,r^{\prime})-B(x_{0},r^{\prime}_{0})\right|
and

\displaystyle\left|B(x,r)-B(x,r^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leq 6\frac{2^{-\alpha}}{1-2^{-\alpha}}K_{\alpha}(r^{\prime})^{\alpha}2^{-p\alpha}+\left|B(x_{0},r_{0},s_{0})-B(x_{0},r^{\prime}_{0},s_{0})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+6\frac{2^{-\alpha}}{1-2^{-\alpha}}\right)K_{\alpha}(r^{\prime})^{\alpha}2^{-p\alpha}\leq\left(1+6\frac{2^{-\alpha}}{1-2^{-\alpha}}\right)K_{\alpha}(r^{\prime})^{\alpha}(r^{\prime}-r)^{\alpha}.

A similar treatment can be applied when
x
varies as well. If we define for all
(x,r)\in\mathcal{D}_{2}\times\mathcal{D}_{1}
,

\tilde{B}(x,r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}B(x,r)&\mathrm{if~}K_{\alpha}<\infty\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{array}\right.,

and for all
(x,r)\in D\times[0,1]
,

\tilde{B}(x,r)=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\to(x,r)\\
(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}_{2}\times\mathcal{D}_{1}\end{subarray}}\tilde{B}(x^{\prime},r^{\prime}),

then
\tilde{B}
satisfies the requirements of the lemma.
∎

Appendix B
A tail estimate for
|\mu^{*}(Q(0,1))|

In this appendix, we give some details on the estimate (
1.8
) from
[
LSZ17
]
. Recall that we denote by
\mu^{*}
the imaginary chaos associated to the whole-plane GFF
\Gamma^{*}
. The main result of
[
LSZ17
]
is the existence of a constant
c^{**}(\beta)\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(B.1)

\mathbb{E}\left|\mu^{*}([0,1]^{2})\right|^{2N}=N^{\beta^{2}N/2}e^{c^{**}(\beta)N+o(N)},\qquad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty.

See
[
LSZ17
, Corollary 1.3]
.
Let us define the constant
c^{*}(\beta)>0
by

(B.2)

c^{*}(\beta)=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}e^{-1-2c^{**}(\beta)/\beta^{2}}\qquad\text{so that}\qquad c^{**}(\beta)=-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\Big(1+\log\frac{2c^{*}(\beta)}{\beta^{2}}\Big).

In
[
LSZ17
, Corollary A.2]
, the authors argue that (
B.1
) yields the following tail estimate:

(B.3)

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-4/\beta^{2}}\log\mathbb{P}\left(|\mu^{*}(Q(0,1))|>t\right)=-c^{*}(\beta).

The upper bound follows directly from (
B.1
) by optimising some Markov’s inequality. The lower bound however necessitates more care and the proof in
[
LSZ17
]
actually seems to contain a small gap.
Indeed, two random variables
X
and
Y
are considered (with the notations therein
X=Y_{\delta}-C_{2}
and
Y=|\int_{D}e^{i\beta h(x)}dx|^{2}
). These variables satisfy for all
k\geq 1
,
\mathbb{E}[X^{k}]\leq\mathbb{E}[Y^{k}]
. It is then inferred that
X
is stochastically dominated by
Y
in the sense that
\mathbb{P}(X>t)\leq\mathbb{P}(Y>t)
for all
t
. This implication is however erroneous in general as one can see with the example
X=1/3
a.s. and
Y
a Bernoulli random variable with
\mathbb{P}(Y=1)=1-\mathbb{P}(Y=0)=2/3
.
Since several main results of the current paper crucially use this tail estimate, we give an alternative elementary way of obtaining (
B.3
) from (
B.1
).

Let us also mention that we have corrected a typo in
[
LSZ17
]
where (
B.3
) is stated with
t^{2/\beta^{2}}
rather than
t^{4/\beta^{2}}
. Indeed, with their notations, the
2k
-th moment of the absolute value of the imaginary chaos is equal to
Z_{k,\beta^{2}}
rather than
Z_{k,2\beta^{2}}
, contrary to what is stated in
[
LSZ17
, Lemma A.1]
. Now, at the critical value
\beta=\sqrt{2}
, the tail (
B.3
) has a Gaussian behaviour. This is consistent with the fact that the imaginary chaos becomes a white noise at
\beta=\sqrt{2}
(see
[
LRV15
,
JSW20
]
).

We now explain our proof of the lower bound of (
B.3
).
Let
\nu
be the law of
\left|\mu^{*}(Q(0,1))\right|
,
\alpha=4/\beta^{2}
and
c^{*}=c^{*}(\beta)
be the constant defined in (
B.2
).
As mentioned, by a direct application of Markov’s inequality,
for all
d^{*}<c^{*}
, if
t
is large enough

\nu[t,\infty)\leq e^{-d^{*}t^{\alpha}}
.
We want to show the reverse inequality, i.e. that

(B.4)

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\nu[t,\infty)}{t^{\alpha}}\geq-c^{*}.

For all
\varepsilon>0
small enough and
d^{*}<c^{*}
, letting
k(t)
be the unique even integer in
[d^{*}\alpha t^{\alpha},d^{*}\alpha t^{\alpha}+2)
, we first claim that

(B.5)

\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k(t)}}{t^{k(t)}}\nu(dx)-\int_{(1-\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t}^{(1+\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t}\frac{x^{k(t)}}{t^{k(t)}}\nu(dx)\right|\leq e^{-(1+o(1))d^{*}(1+\varepsilon^{2}/4)t^{\alpha}},

where
o(1)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof of (
B.5
).

Since
-\log(1+u)+1+u=1+u^{2}/2+O(u^{3})
as
u\to 0
, there exists
u_{0}>0
s.t. for all
u\in(-u_{0},u_{0})
,
-\log(1+u)+1+u\geq 1+u^{2}/4
. We now fix
\varepsilon\in(0,u_{0})
and
d^{*}<c^{*}
. There exists
t_{0}>0
large enough such that for all
t\geq t_{0}
,
\nu[t,\infty)\leq\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{1}{Z}e^{-d^{*}x^{\alpha}}dx
where
Z
is the renormalising constant
Z=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-d^{*}x^{\alpha}}dx
. We thus have the stochastic domination

(B.6)

\nu(dx)\prec\frac{1}{Z}e^{-d^{*}x^{\alpha}}dx,\qquad\text{for}\quad x\geq t_{0}.

We decompose the left hand side of (
B.5
) into several parts depending on whether
x\leq t_{0}
,
x\in[t_{0},(1-\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t]
or
x\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t
. For the first part,
there exists
c>0
depending on
\alpha,d^{*},t_{0}
such that

\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\frac{x^{k(t)}}{t^{k(t)}}\nu(dx)\leq t_{0}^{k(t)}t^{-k(t)}\leq e^{-ct^{\alpha}\log t}.

Noticing that
x\mapsto x^{k(t)}e^{-d^{*}x^{\alpha}}
is increasing on
[0,(\frac{k(t)}{d^{*}\alpha})^{1/\alpha}]
and then decreasing, we also have:

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{(1-\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t}\frac{x^{k(t)}}{t^{k(t)}}\nu(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{Z}\int_{t_{0}}^{(1-\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t}\frac{x^{k(t)}}{t^{k(t)}}e^{-d^{*}x^{\alpha}}dx\leq\frac{1}{Z}(1-\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t(1-\varepsilon)^{k(t)/\alpha}e^{-d^{*}(1-\varepsilon)t^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=e^{-(1+o(1))\left(-d^{*}\log(1-\varepsilon)+d^{*}(1-\varepsilon)\right)t^{\alpha}}\leq e^{-(1+o(1))d^{*}(1+\varepsilon^{2}/4)t^{\alpha}}

because
\varepsilon<u_{0}
.
Similarly (essentially change
\varepsilon\leftarrow-\varepsilon
in the computations above)

\int_{(1+\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t}^{t^{2}}\frac{x^{k(t)}}{t^{k(t)}}\nu(dx)\leq e^{-(1+o(1))d^{*}(1+\varepsilon^{2}/4)t^{\alpha}}.

The integral from
t^{2}
to
\infty
is even smaller and putting things together, we have obtained (
B.5
).
∎

Let
\varepsilon>0
be small and
d^{*}<c^{*}
close enough to
c^{*}
so that
\varepsilon^{2}/4>\log(c^{*}/d^{*}).

By (
B.1
),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k(t)}}{t^{k(t)}}\nu(dx)

\displaystyle=t^{-k(t)}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}k(t)\log\frac{k(t)}{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(1+\log\frac{\alpha c^{*}}{2}\right)k(t)+o(k)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-(1+o(1))d^{*}\left(1+\log\frac{c^{*}}{d^{*}}\right)t^{\alpha}\right).

Because we have chosen
\varepsilon
and
d^{*}
so that
\varepsilon^{2}/4>\log(c^{*}/d^{*})
, combining the above estimate with (
B.5
) leads to

\int_{(1-\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t}^{(1+\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t}\frac{x^{k(t)}}{t^{k(t)}}\nu(dx)\geq\exp\left(-(1+o(1))d^{*}\left(1+\log\frac{c^{*}}{d^{*}}\right)t^{\alpha}\right).

In particular,

\displaystyle\nu\left[(1-\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t,\infty\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{-k(t)/\alpha}\int_{(1-\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t}^{(1+\varepsilon)^{1/\alpha}t}\frac{x^{k(t)}}{t^{k(t)}}\nu(dx)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-(1+o(1))d^{*}\left(1+\log\frac{c^{*}}{d^{*}(1-\varepsilon)}\right)t^{\alpha}\right).

We have thus shown that

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\nu[t,\infty)}{t^{\alpha}}\geq-\frac{d^{*}}{1-\varepsilon}\left(1+\log\frac{c^{*}}{d^{*}(1-\varepsilon)}\right).

By letting
d^{*}\to c^{*}
and then
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain (
B.4
) as wanted.
