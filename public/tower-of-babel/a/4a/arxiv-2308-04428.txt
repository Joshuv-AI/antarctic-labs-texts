Sample-Efficient Linear Representation Learning from Non-IID Non-Isotropic Data
Thomas T. Zhang, Leonardo F. Toso, James Anderson, Nikolai Matni

Abstract
A powerful concept behind much of the recent progress in machine learning is the extraction of common features across data from heterogeneous sources or tasks. Intuitively, using all of one’s data to learn a common representation function benefits both computational effort and statistical generalization by leaving a smaller number of parameters to fine-tune on a given task. Toward theoretically grounding these merits, we propose a general setting of recovering linear operators M M from noisy vector measurements y = M ​ x + w y=Mx+w , where the covariates x x may be both non-i.i.d. and non-isotropic. We demonstrate that existing isotropy-agnostic representation learning approaches incur biases on the representation update, which causes the scaling of the noise terms to lose favorable dependence on the number of source tasks. This in turn can cause the sample complexity of representation learning to be bottlenecked by the single-task data size. We introduce an adaptation, De-bias & Feature-Whiten ( DFW ), of the popular alternating minimization-descent scheme proposed independently in Collins et al. 2021 and Nayer and Vaswani 2022 , and establish linear convergence to the optimal representation with noise level scaling down with the total source data size. This leads to generalization bounds on the same order as an oracle empirical risk minimizer. We verify the vital importance of DFW on various numerical simulations. In particular, we show that vanilla alternating-minimization descent fails catastrophically even for iid, but mildly non-isotropic data. Our analysis unifies and generalizes prior work, and provides a flexible framework for a wider range of applications, such as in controls and dynamical systems.



1
Introduction

A unifying paradigm belying recent exciting progress in machine learning is learning a common feature space or
representation
for downstream tasks from heterogeneous sources. This forms the core of fields such as meta-learning, transfer learning, and federated learning. A shared theme across these fields is the scarcity of data for a specific task out of many, such that designing individual models for each task is both computationally and statistically inefficient, impractical, or impossible. Under the assumption that these tasks are similar in some way, a natural alternative approach is to use data across many tasks to learn a common component, such that fine-tuning to a given task involves fitting a much smaller model that acts on the common component. Over the last few years, significant attention has been given to framing this problem setting theoretically, providing provable benefits of learning over multiple tasks in the context of linear regression
(
Bullins et al. 2019
;
Du et al. 2020
;
Tripuraneni et al. 2021
;
Collins et al. 2021
;
Thekumparampil et al. 2021
;
Saunshi et al. 2021
)
and in identification/control of linear dynamical systems
(
Modi et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. These works study the problem of
linear representation learning
, where the data for each task is generated noisily from an unknown shared latent subspace, and the goal is to efficiently recover a representation of the latent space
\hat{\Phi}
from data across different task distributions. For example, in the linear regression setting, one may have data of the form

\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}={\theta^{(t)}}^{\top}\Phi\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}+\mathrm{noise},\quad\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\in\mathbb{R},\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}},\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{x}},

with
i=1,\dots,N
iid data points from
t=1,\dots,T
task distributions. Since the representation
\Phi
is shared across all tasks, one may expect the generalization error of an approximate representation
\hat{\Phi}
fit on
TN
data points to scale as
\frac{d_{x}r}{TN}
, where
d_{x}r
is the number of parameters determining the representation. This is indeed the flavor of statistical guarantees from prior work
(
Du et al. 2020
;
Tripuraneni et al. 2021
;
Thekumparampil et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, which concretely demonstrates the benefit of using data across different tasks.

However, existing work, especially beyond the scalar measurement setting, is limited in one or more important components of their analysis. For example, it is common to assume that the covariates
\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
are isotropic across all tasks. Furthermore, statistical analyses often assume access to an empirical risk minimizer, even though the linear representation learning problem is non-convex and ill-posed
(
Maurer et al. 2016
;
Du et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
.
Our paper addresses these problems under a unified framework of
linear operator recovery
, i.e. recovering linear operators
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times d_{x}}
from (noisy) vector measurements
y=Mx+w
, where the covariates
x
may not be independent or isotropic. This setting subsumes the scalar measurement setting, and encompasses many fundamental control and dynamical systems problems, such as linear system identification and imitation learning.
In particular, the data in these settings are incompatible with the common distributional assumptions (e.g., independence, isotropy) made in prior work.

Contributions:

Toward this end, our main contributions are as follows:

•

We demonstrate that naive implementation of local methods for linear representation learning fail catastrophically even when the data is iid but mildly non-isotropic. We identify the source of the failure as interaction between terms incurring biases in the representation gradient, which do not scale down with the number of tasks.

•

We address these issues by introducing two practical algorithmic adjustments,
De-bias & Feature-Whiten
(
DFW
), which provably mitigate the identified issues. We then show that
DFW
is necessary for gradient-based methods to benefit from the total size of the source dataset.

•

We numerically show our theoretical guarantees are predictive of the efficacy of our proposed algorithm, and of the key importance of individual aspects of our algorithmic framework.

Our main result can be summarized by the following descent guarantee for our proposed algorithm.

Theorem 1.1
(main result, informal)

Let
\hat{\Phi}
be the current estimate of the representation, and
\Phi_{\star}
the optimal representation. Running one iteration of
DFW
yields the following improvement

\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi}_{+},\Phi_{\star})\leq\rho\cdot\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi},\Phi_{\star})+\frac{C}{\sqrt{\text{\# tasks}\times\text{\# data per task}}},\quad\rho\in(0,1),\;C>0.

Critically, the second term of the right hand side scales jointly in the number of tasks and datapoints per task, whereas naively implementing other methods may be bottlenecked by a term that scales solely with the amount of data for a single task, which leads to suboptimal sample-efficiency.

Related Work

Multi-task linear regression:
Directly related to our work are results demonstrating the benefits of multi-task learning for linear regression

Regression Model.

Let a covariate sequence (also denoted a
trajectory
) be an indexed set
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
. We denote a distribution
\mathbb{P}_{x}
over covariate sequences, which we assume to have bounded second moments for all
i\geq 1
, i.e.
\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\right]
is finite for all
i\geq 1
.
Defining the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
where
\mathcal{F}_{i}:=\sigma(\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{i+1},\{w_{k}\}_{k=1}^{i})
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the covariates up to
i+1
and noise up to

, we assume that
\{w_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is a
\sigma_{w}^{2}
-subgaussian martingale difference sequence (MDS):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[v^{\top}w_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda v^{\top}w_{i}\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]\leq\exp\left(\lambda^{2}\left\|v\right\|^{2}\sigma_{w}^{2}\right)\;\text{a.s.}\;\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R},v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}.

Assuming a ground truth operator
M_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times d_{x}}
, our observation model is given by

y_{i}=M_{\star}x_{i}+w_{i},\quad i\geq 1,

for
y_{i}
the
labels
, and
w_{i}
the
label noise
. We further define
\Sigma_{x,N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}]
. When the marginal distributions of
x_{i},\;i\geq 1
are identical, we denote
\Sigma_{x}\equiv\Sigma_{x,N}
.

Multi-Task Operator Recovery.

We consider the following instantiation of the above linear operator regression model over multiple tasks. In particular, we consider heterogeneous data
\{(\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{},\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{})\}_{i=1,t=1}^{N,T}
, consisting of trajectories of length
N
, generated independently across
t=1,\dots,T
task distributions. For notational convenience, we assume that the length of trajectories
N
is the same across training tasks. For each task
t
, the observation model is

\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}=M^{(t)}_{\star}\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}+\mathopen{}w^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{},

(1)

where
M^{(t)}_{\star}=\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star}
admits a decomposition into a ground-truth representation
\Phi_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{x}}
common across all tasks
t\in[T]
and a task-specific weight matrix
\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times r}
,
r\leq d_{x}
. We denote the joint distribution over covariates and observations
\{\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{},\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\}_{i\geq 1}
by
\mathbb{P}_{x,y}^{(t)}
. We assume that the representation
\Phi_{\star}
is normalized to have orthonormal rows to prevent boundedness issues, since
\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}^{\prime}=\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}Q^{-1}
,
\Phi_{\star}^{\prime}=Q\Phi_{\star}
are also valid decompositions for any invertible
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
. To measure closeness of an approximate representation
\hat{\Phi}
to optimality, we define a subspace metric.

Definition 2.1
(Subspace Distance
(
Stewart and Sun 1990
;
Collins et al. 2021
)
)

Let
\Phi,\Phi_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{x}}
be matrices whose rows are orthonormal. Furthermore, let
\Phi_{\star,\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d_{x}-r)\times d_{x}}
be a matrix such that
\begin{bmatrix}\Phi_{\star}^{\top}&\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}
is an orthogonal matrix. Define the distance between the subspaces spanned by the rows of
\Phi
and
\Phi_{\star}
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(\Phi,\Phi_{\star})

\displaystyle:=\|\Phi\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}\|_{2}

(2)

In particular, the subspace distance quantitatively captures the alignment between two subspaces, interpolating smoothly between
0
(occurring iff
\mathrm{span}(\Phi_{\star})=\mathrm{span}(\hat{\Phi})
) and
1
(occurring iff
\mathrm{span}(\Phi_{\star})\perp\mathrm{span}(\hat{\Phi})
).
We define the task-specific stacked vector notation by capital letters, e.g.,

\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}=\begin{bmatrix}x^{(t)}_{1}&\cdots&x^{(t)}_{i}&\cdots&x^{(t)}_{N}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{x}}.

The goal of multi-task operator recovery is to estimate
\{\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\Phi_{\star}
from data collected across multiple tasks
\{(\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{},\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{})\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
t=1,\dots,T
. Some prior works
(
Maurer et al. 2016
;
Du et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
assume access to an empirical risk minimization oracle, i.e. access to

\{\hat{F}^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T},\hat{\Phi}\in\argmin_{\{F^{(t)}\},\Phi}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}-F^{(t)}\Phi\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\right\|^{2},

focusing on the statistical generalization properties of an ERM solution. However, the above optimization is non-convex even in the linear setting, and thus it is imperative to design and analyze efficient algorithms for recovering optimal matrices
\{\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\Phi_{\star}
.
To address this problem in the linear regression setting, various works, e.g.
FedRep

(
Collins et al. 2021
)
,
AltGD-Min

(
Nayer and Vaswani 2022
)
, propose an alternating minimization-descent scheme, where on a fresh data batch, the weights
\{\hat{F}^{(t)}\}
are computed on task-specific (“local”) data via least-squares, and an estimate of the representation gradient is subsequently computed with respect to task-specific data and averaged across tasks to perform gradient descent on the representation parameters.
This algorithmic framework is intuitive, and thus forms a reasonable starting point toward a provably sample-efficient algorithm in our setting.

3
Sample-Efficient Linear Representation Learning

We begin by describing the vanilla alternating minimization-descent scheme proposed in
Collins et al. 2021
. We show that in our setting with label noise and non-isotropy, interaction terms arise in the representation gradient, which cause biases to form that do not scale down with the number of tasks
T
. In §
3.2
, we propose alterations to the scheme to remove these biases, which we then show in §
3.3
lead to fast convergence rates that allow us to recover near-oracle ERM generalization bounds.

3.1
Perils of (Vanilla) Gradient Descent on the Representation

We begin with a summary of the main components of an alternating minimization-descent method analogous to
FedRep

(
Collins et al. 2021
)
and
AltGD-Min

(
Nayer and Vaswani 2022
)
. During each optimization round, a new data batch is sampled for each task:
\{(\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{},\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{})\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
t\in[T]
. We then compute
task-specific weights
\hat{F}^{(t)}
on the corresponding dataset, keeping the current representation estimate
\hat{\Phi}
fixed. For example,
\hat{F}^{(t)}
may be the least-squares weights conditioned on
\hat{\Phi}

(
Collins et al. 2021
)
. Define
\mathopen{}z^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}:=\hat{\Phi}\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
, and the empirical covariance matrices
\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}:=\tfrac{1}{N}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}
,
\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{z}\mathclose{}:=\tfrac{1}{N}\mathopen{}Z^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}Z^{(t)}\mathclose{}
. The least squares solution
\hat{F}^{(t)}
is given by the convex quadratic minimization

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{F}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}-F\mathopen{}z^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\begin{split}=F^{(t)}_{\star}\Phi_{\star}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}Z^{(t)}\mathclose{}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{z}\mathclose{})^{-1}+\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}Z^{(t)}\mathclose{}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{z}\mathclose{})^{-1},\end{split}

(3)

where we derive (
3.1
) through standard matrix calculus
(
Petersen et al. 2008
)
and expanding (
1
).
For each task, we then fix the weight matrix
\hat{F}^{(t)}
and perform a descent step with respect to the representation conditioned on the local data. The resulting representations are averaged across tasks to form the new representation. When the descent direction is the gradient, the update rule is given by

\displaystyle\overline{\Phi}_{+}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\hat{\Phi}-\frac{\eta}{2N}\nabla_{\Phi}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}-\hat{F}^{(t)}\hat{\Phi}\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\right\|^{2},\quad\overline{\Phi}_{+}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\overline{\Phi}_{+}^{(t)}

(4)

where
\eta>0
is a given step size. We normalize
\overline{\Phi}_{+}
to have orthonormal rows, e.g. by (thin/reduced) QR decomposition
(
Trefethen and Bau 2022
)
, to produce the final output
\hat{\Phi}_{+}
, i.e.
\overline{\Phi}_{+}=R\hat{\Phi}_{+}
,
R\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, leading to

\displaystyle R\hat{\Phi}_{+}

\displaystyle=\hat{\Phi}-\frac{\eta}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\left(\hat{F}^{(t)}\hat{\Phi}-F^{(t)}_{\star}\Phi_{\star}\right)\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}-\frac{\eta}{NT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}.

(5)

As in
Collins et al. 2021
, we right-multiply both sides of (
5
) by
\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}
, recalling
\|\Phi\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}\|_{2}=:\mathrm{dist}(\Phi,\Phi_{\star})
. Crucially,
Collins et al. 2021
assume
\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
has mean
0
and identity covariance, and
\mathopen{}w^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\equiv 0
across
i,t
.
Therefore, the label noise terms
\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}
disappear, and the sample covariance for each task
\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}
concentrates to identity. Under these assumptions, we get

\displaystyle\left\|R\hat{\Phi}_{+}\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\hat{\Phi}\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}-\frac{\eta}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\left(\hat{F}^{(t)}\hat{\Phi}-F^{(t)}_{\star}\Phi_{\star}\right)\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\underbrace{\left\|I-\frac{\eta}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\hat{F}^{(t)}\right\|}_{\text{Contraction term}}\mathrm{dist}\left(\hat{\Phi},\Phi_{\star}\right)+\underbrace{\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}-I_{d_{x}}\right\|\right)}_{\text{Covariance concentration term}}.

where we note
\Phi_{\star}\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}=0
. Under appropriate choice of
\eta
and bounding the effect of the orthonormalization factor
R
, linear convergence to the optimal representation can be established.
However, two issues arise when label noise
\mathopen{}w^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
is introduced and when
\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
has non-identity covariance.

1.

When
label noise

\mathopen{}w^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
is present, since
\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}
is computed on
\mathopen{}Y^{(t)}\mathclose{},\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}
, the gradient noise term is generally biased:
\frac{1}{NT}\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}]\neq 0
. Even in the simple case that all task distributions
\mathbb{P}^{(t)}_{x,y}
are identical,
\frac{\eta}{NT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}
concentrates to its bias, and thus for large
T
the size of noise term is bottlenecked at
\frac{\eta}{NT}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right\|\right]
. This critically causes the noise term to lose scaling in the
number of tasks

T
, even when the tasks are identical.

2.

When
\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
has
non-identity covariance
,
the decomposition into a contraction and covariance concentration term no longer holds, since generally
\Phi_{*}\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}]\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}\neq 0
. This causes a term whose norm otherwise concentrates around
0
in the isotropic case to scale with
\lambda_{\max}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})-\lambda_{\min}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})
in the worst case.
Unlike prior work that assumes identical distribution of covariates
\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
across tasks, this issue cannot be circumvented by whitening the covariates
\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
, as shifting the task-specific covariance factor to the operator
F^{(t)}_{\star}\Phi_{\star}\mathopen{}\Sigma_{x}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{1/2}
in general ruins the shared representation spanned by
\Phi_{\star}
.

This motivates modifying the representation update beyond following the vanilla stochastic gradient.

3.2
A Task-Efficient Algorithm:
De-bias & Feature-whiten

In the previous section, we identified two fundamental issues: 1. the bias introduced by computing the least squares weights and representation update on the same data batch, and 2. the nuisance term introduced by non-identity second moments of the covariates
\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
. Toward addressing the first issue, we introduce a “de-biasing” step, where each agent computes the least squares weights
\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}
and the representation update on
independent
batches of data, e.g. disjoint subsets of trajectories.
To address the second issue, we introduce a “feature-whitening” adaptation
(
LeCun et al. 2002
)
, where the gradient estimate sent by each agent is pre-conditioned by its inverse sample covariance matrix. Combining these two adaptations, the representation update becomes

\displaystyle R\hat{\Phi}_{+}

\displaystyle=\hat{\Phi}-\frac{\eta}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\left(\hat{F}^{(t)}\hat{\Phi}-F^{(t)}_{\star}\Phi_{\star}\right)-\frac{\eta}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}\left(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}\right)^{-1},

(6)

where we assume
\{\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\}
are computed on independent data using the aforementioned batching strategy. We comment that this “pre-conditioning by the inverse sample covariance” step bears striking resemblance to various algorithms applied to dynamical systems, e.g. Quasi-Newton method for Generalized Linear Models
(
Kowshik et al. 2021
)
, natural policy gradient for linear-quadratic systems
(
Fazel et al. 2018
)
. Curiously, the various motivations of this step differ entirely between all of these works; for example, the purpose of this step in our work arises even for independent data. When
\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{},\mathopen{}w^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
,
t=1,\dots,T
, are all mutually independent, then the first two terms of the update form the contraction, and the last term is an average of
zero-mean
least-squares-error-like terms over tasks, which can be studied using standard tools
(
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
;
Abbasi-Yadkori 2013
)
. This culminates in convergence rates that scale favorably with the number of tasks (§
3.3
).
To operationalize our proposed adaptations, let
D^{(t)}=\{(\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{},\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{})\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
t\in[T]
, be a dataset available to each agent. For the weights de-biasing step, we sub-sample trajectories
\mathcal{N}_{1}\subset[N],\;{\left|\mathcal{N}_{1}\right|}:=N_{1}
. For each agent, we compute least-squares weights from
\mathcal{N}_{1}
. We then sub-sample trajectories
\mathcal{N}_{2}\subset[N]\setminus\mathcal{N}_{1},\;{\left|\mathcal{N}_{2}\right|}=N_{2}
, and compute the task-conditioned representation gradients from
\mathcal{N}_{2}
.

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{(t)}=\argmin_{F}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{1}}\left\|\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}-F\mathopen{}z^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\right\|^{2},\quad\hat{}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}^{(t)}=\nabla_{\Phi}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{2}}\left\|\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}-\hat{F}^{(t)}\hat{\Phi}\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\right\|^{2}.

(7)

Lastly, each agent updates its local representation via a feature-whitened gradient step to yield
\bar{\Phi}_{+}^{(t)}
. The global representation update is computed by averaging the updated task-conditioned representations
\bar{\Phi}_{+}^{(t)}
and performing orthonormalization:

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}_{+}^{(t)}:=\hat{\Phi}-\eta\hat{}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}^{(t)}\left(\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x,\mathcal{N}_{2}}\right)^{-1},\quad R\hat{\Phi}_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{\Phi}_{+}^{(t)}\text{ s.t. }\hat{\Phi}_{+}^{\top}\hat{\Phi}_{+}=I_{r}

(8)

\displaystyle\iff\hat{\Phi}_{+},R

\displaystyle=\texttt{thin\_QR}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{\Phi}_{+}^{(t)}\right),

We summarize the full algorithm in
Algorithm

1
. The above de-biasing and feature whitening steps ensure that the expectation of the representation update (
6
) is a contraction (with high probability):

\displaystyle\begin{split}&R\hat{\Phi}_{+}\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}=\left(I-\frac{\eta}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right)\hat{\Phi}\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}-\frac{\eta}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}\left(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}\right)^{-1}\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}\\
\implies\;&\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi}_{+},\Phi_{\star})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|R^{-1}\left(I-\frac{\eta}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right)\right\|\right]\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi},\Phi_{\star}),\end{split}

(9)

where the task and trajectory-wise independence ensures that the variance of the gradient scales inversely in
NT
.

Remark 3.1

3.3
Algorithm Guarantees

We present our main result in the form of convergence guarantees for
Algorithm

1
.
We begin by defining a standard measure of dependency along covariate sequences via
\beta
-mixing.

Definition 3.1
(
\beta
-mixing)

Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued discrete-time stochastic process adapted to filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
.
We denote the stationary distribution
\nu_{\infty}
. We define the
\beta
-mixing coefficient

\beta(k):=\sup_{i\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\left\{x_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell=1}^{i}}\left[\left\|\mathbb{P}_{x_{i+k}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{i})-\nu_{\infty}\right\|_{\mathrm{tv}}\right],

(10)

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathrm{tv}}
denotes the total variation distance between probability measures.

Intuitively, the
\beta
-mixing coefficient measures how quickly on average a process converges to its stationary distribution along any sample path.
To instantiate our bounds, we make the following assumptions on the covariates.

Assumption 3.1
(Subgaussian covariates, geometric mixing)

Given number of tasks
T
and per-task samples
N
, we assume the marginal distributions of
\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
to be identical with zero-mean, covariance
\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}
, and to
\gamma^{2}
-subgaussian across all
i\in[N],t\in[T]
:

\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda v^{\top}\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\right)\right]\leq\exp\left(\lambda^{2}\left\|v\right\|^{2}\gamma^{2}\right)\quad\text{a.s. }\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R},v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}.

Furthermore, we assume the process
\{\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\}_{i\geq 1}
is geometrically
\beta
-mixing with
\beta^{(t)}(k)\leq\mathopen{}\Gamma^{(t)}\mathclose{}\mathopen{}\mu^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{k}
,
\mathopen{}\Gamma^{(t)}\mathclose{}>0
,
\mathopen{}\mu^{(t)}\mathclose{}\in[0,1)
, for each task
t\in[T]
. Lastly, we define
\tau_{\mathsf{mix}}^{(t)}:=\left(\frac{\log(\mathopen{}\Gamma^{(t)}\mathclose{}N/\delta)}{\log(1/\mathopen{}\mu^{(t)}\mathclose{})}\vee 1\right)
.

Notably, these assumptions subsume fundamental problems in learning over (stable) dynamical systems, in particular linear system identification and imitation learning, where non-iid and non-isotropy across tasks are unavoidable. We discuss these instantiations in depth in
Appendix

B
. As in prior work, our final convergence rates depend on a notion of task diversity.

Definition 3.2
(Task diversity)

We define the quantities

\displaystyle\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\min}:=\lambda_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\right),\quad\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\max}:=\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\right).

(11)

As hinted by
Equation

9
, our proof strategy boils down to bounding the various terms in the update rule of
DFW
; in particular, the “contraction factor”
I-\frac{\eta}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}
, the task-averaged noise term
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})^{-1}
, and lastly the effect of orthnormalization
R
. Starting with the contraction factor, we observe expanding the least-squares weights yields:

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}

\displaystyle=F^{(t)}_{\star}\Phi_{\star}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}Z^{(t)}\mathclose{}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{z}\mathclose{})^{-1}+\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}Z^{(t)}\mathclose{}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{z}\mathclose{})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star}\hat{\Phi}^{\top}+\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star}\hat{\Phi}_{\perp}^{\top}\hat{\Phi}_{\perp}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}Z^{(t)}\mathclose{}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{z}\mathclose{})^{-1}+\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}Z^{(t)}\mathclose{}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{z}\mathclose{})^{-1}.

Intuitively speaking, the least-squares weights decomposes into a term proportional to
\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}
, an error term arising from misspecification scaling with
\|\Phi_{\star}\hat{\Phi}_{\perp}^{\top}\|=\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi},\Phi_{\star})
, and a zero-mean least-squares error term scaling with
\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}
. Therefore, inverting bounds on the error terms into burn-in conditions, we get after some computation the following bound.

Lemma 3.1
(Contraction factor bound)

Let
3.1
hold. If the following burn-in conditions hold

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi},\Phi_{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{100}\sqrt{\frac{\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\min}\mathclose{}}{\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\max}\mathclose{}}}\max_{t}\kappa\hskip-1.5pt\left(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle N_{1}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\max_{t}\tau_{\mathsf{mix}}^{(t)}\cdot\max\left\{\gamma^{4}\left(r+\log(T/\delta)\right),\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\min}\mathclose{}^{-1}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}^{2}(d_{y}+r+\log(T/\delta))}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})}\right\},

then, for step-size satisfying
\eta\leq 0.956\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\max}\mathclose{}^{-1}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|I_{d_{x}}-\eta\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-0.954\eta\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\min}\mathclose{}\right).

Setting
\eta\approx\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\max}\mathclose{}^{-1}
, we observe that the bound on the contraction factor is approximately
1-c\frac{\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\min}\mathclose{}}{\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\max}\mathclose{}}
, which is the best one can hope for in the spectral norm. Furthermore, we note that past the burn-in, the contraction rate is independent of the data-size
N_{1}={\left|\mathcal{N}_{1}\right|}
used to compute the weights, implying that contraction holds as long as the least-squares weights are “good enough” (tying back to
Remark

3.1
), further implying
N_{1}
can be held fixed across rounds of
DFW
.

To bound the
DFW
noise term, we observe that for each task,
\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})^{-1}
is an
r\times d_{x}
matrix-valued self-normalized martingale
(
Abbasi-Yadkori 2013
)
. Importantly, by the de-biasing step, the weights
\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}
are mutually independent of of the processes
\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{},\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}
in the independent covariates setting. Similarly to the contraction factor, assuming a burn-in on
\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi},\Phi_{\star})
and
N_{1}
, the subgaussian constant of
\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}w^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
can be bounded by, say,
2\|\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\|_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}^{2}
. By realizability (
1
), we see that in the independent covariates setting,
\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X^{(t)}\mathclose{}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})^{-1}
is a
zero-mean
process that is bounded with high-probability. Therefore, applying a matrix Hoeffding inequality
(
Tropp 2011
)
across tasks
T
crucially yields a bound on the noise term that benefits from more tasks
T
.

Proposition 3.1
(Noise term bound)

Let
3.1
hold. Assume

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi},\Phi_{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{t}\frac{1}{100}\kappa\hskip-1.5pt\left(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle N_{1}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\max_{t}\tau_{\mathsf{mix}}^{(t)}\cdot\max\left\{\gamma^{4}\left(r+\log(T/\delta)\right),\max_{t}\frac{\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}^{2}}{\|\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\|^{2}\lambda_{\min}(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})}\left(d_{y}+r+\log(T/\delta)\right)\right\},

\displaystyle N_{2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\max_{t}\tau_{\mathsf{mix}}^{(t)}\cdot\gamma^{4}\left(d_{x}+\log(T/\delta)\right).

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\left(\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x,\mathcal{N}_{2}}\right)^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}\sqrt{\frac{d_{x}+\log(T/\delta)}{TN_{2}}\log\left(\frac{d_{x}}{\delta}\right)},

where
\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\tau_{\mathsf{mix}}^{(t)}\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}^{2}\|\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\|^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})}}
is the
task-averaged
noise-level.

The final piece of the proof lies in bounding the orthonormalization factor
R
. A key observation is that
RR^{\top}=(R\hat{\Phi}_{+})(R\hat{\Phi}_{+})^{\top}
, where
R\hat{\Phi}_{+}
is precisely the output of the preconditioned gradient step on
\hat{\Phi}
(
6
). Roughly speaking, we observe that the RHS of (
6
) is composed of three terms, the first of which
\hat{\Phi}
satisfies
\hat{\Phi}\hat{\Phi}^{\top}=I_{r}
, the second which scales with
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\hat{\Phi}-\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star}
, and the third that is the task-averaged self-normalized martingale term. Therefore, by re-invoking tools used in
Lemma

3.1
and
Proposition

3.1
, we find that under similar burn-in conditions, the orthogonalization factor is a small perturbation to identity, thus leaving the contraction rate and noise term essentially unaffected.

Lemma 3.2

Let
3.1
hold. Let the following burn-in conditions hold:

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi},\Phi_{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{100}\sqrt{\frac{\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\min}\mathclose{}}{\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\max}\mathclose{}}}\max_{t}\kappa\hskip-1.5pt\left(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle N_{1}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\max_{t}\tau_{\mathsf{mix}}^{(t)}\cdot\max\left\{\gamma^{4}\left(r+\log(T/\delta)\right),\;\;\overline{\sigma}_{\mathbf{F}}^{2}\left(d_{y}+r+\log(T/\delta)\right)\right\},

\displaystyle N_{2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\max_{t}\tau_{\mathsf{mix}}^{(t)}\cdot\max\left\{\gamma^{4}\left(d_{x}+\log(T/\delta)\right),\;\;\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\min}\mathclose{}^{-1}\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}^{2}}{T}(d_{x}+\log(T/\delta))\log\left(\frac{d_{x}}{\delta}\right)\right\},

where
\overline{\sigma}_{\mathbf{F}}^{2}:=\max\Big\{\max_{t}\frac{\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}^{2}}{\|\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\|^{2}\lambda_{\min}(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})},\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}^{2}}{\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\min}\mathclose{}\lambda_{\min}(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})}\Big\}
.
Then, given
\eta\leq 0.956\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\max}\mathclose{}^{-1}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have the following bound on the orthogonalization factor
R
:

\displaystyle\left\|R^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-0.0575\eta\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\min}\mathclose{}\right)^{-1/2}.

Putting all the pieces together, we now present our main result regarding the subspace distance improvement from running one iteration of
DFW
.

Theorem 3.1
(Main result)

Let
3.1
hold, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}^{2},\overline{\sigma}_{\mathbf{F}}^{2}
be as defined in
Proposition

3.1
and
Lemma

3.2
. Let the following burn-in conditions hold:

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi},\Phi_{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{100}\sqrt{\frac{\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\min}\mathclose{}}{\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\max}\mathclose{}}}\max_{t}\kappa\hskip-1.5pt\left(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle N_{1}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\max_{t}\tau_{\mathsf{mix}}^{(t)}\cdot\max\left\{\gamma^{4}\left(r+\log(T/\delta)\right),\;\;\overline{\sigma}_{\mathbf{F}}^{2}\left(d_{y}+r+\log(T/\delta)\right)\right\},

\displaystyle N_{2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\max_{t}\tau_{\mathsf{mix}}^{(t)}\cdot\max\left\{\gamma^{4}\left(d_{x}+\log(T/\delta)\right),\;\;\mathopen{}\lambda^{\mathbf{F}}_{\min}\mathclose{}^{-1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}^{2}}{T}}(d_{x}+\log(T/\delta))\log\left(\frac{d_{x}}{\delta}\right)\right\},

Then, given step-size satisfying

\hat{\Phi}_{+}
that satisfies with probability at least

Remark 3.2
(Initialization)

We note that Theorem
3.1
relies on the representation
\hat{\Phi}
being sufficiently close to
\Phi_{\star}
. We do not address this issue in this paper, and refer to
Collins et al. 2021
;
Tripuraneni et al. 2021
;
Thekumparampil et al. 2021
for initialization schemes in the iid linear regression setting. Our experiments suggest that initialization is often unnecessary, which mirrors the experimental findings in

Remark 3.3
(Burn-in dependence on
d_{x}
)

We remark that the burn-in requirement on
N_{2}
scales linearly with the covariate dimension
d_{x}
. This is larger than the
\Omega(r)
requirements in the algorithm/analysis in
Collins et al. 2021
;
Thekumparampil et al. 2021
, which we emphasize critically
impose isotropy across all covariates
. In the case of
DFW
, the
d_{x}
dependence arises for a fundamental reason: when
N_{2}<d_{x}
, then postmultiplying each task’s representation gradient by
(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})^{\dagger}
yields subspace distance error terms scaling as
\hat{\Phi}\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{range}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})}\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}
instead of
\hat{\Phi}\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}
. Crucially,
\hat{\Phi}\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{range}(\mathopen{}\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})}\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}
is an unbiased estimator of
\hat{\Phi}\Phi_{\star,\perp}^{\top}
only if
\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
is
isotropic
, and thus improvement in the subspace distance can only be guaranteed for non-isotropic
\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}
when
N_{2}\geq d_{x}
. We leave as an open question whether an efficient algorithm for linear representation learning can be posed/analyzed for non-isotropic covariates in the low per-task data regime
N<d_{x}
.

A key benefit of having the variance term in Theorem
3.1
scale properly in
N,T
is that we may construct representations on fixed datasets whose error scales on the same order as the oracle empirical risk minimizer by running
Algorithm

1
on appropriately partitioned subsets of a given dataset.

Corollary 3.1
(Approximate ERM)

Let the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
hold. Let
\hat{\Phi}_{0}
be an initial representation satisfying
\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi}_{0},\Phi_{\star})<\nu
, and define
\rho:=1-0.857\frac{\lambda_{\min}^{\mathbf{F}}}{\lambda_{\max}^{\mathbf{F}}}
. Let
\bm{D}:=\{\{(\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{},\mathopen{}y^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{})\}_{i=1}^{N}\}_{t\in[T]}
be a given dataset. There exists a partition of
\bm{D}
into independent batches
\bm{B}_{1},\dots,\bm{B}_{K}
, such that iterating
DFW
on
\bm{B}_{k}
,
k\in[K]
yields with probability greater than
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi}_{K},\Phi_{\star})^{2}\leq\tilde{}\mathcal{O}\left(C(\rho)\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}^{2}\frac{(d_{x}r+\log(1/\delta))}{NT}\right),

(12)

where
C(\rho)>0
is a constant depending on the contraction rate
\rho
.

We note that the RHS of (
12
) has the “correct” scaling: noise level
\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}^{2}
multiplied by # parameters of the representation, divided by the
total
amount of data
NT
.
In particular, given a fine-tuning dataset of size
N^{\prime}
sampled from a task
T+1
that shares the representation
\Phi_{\star}
, computing the least squares weights
\hat{F}^{(T+1)}
conditioned on
\hat{\Phi}_{K}
yields a high-probability bound (cf.
Section

A.4.1
) on the parameter error

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{F}^{(T+1)}\hat{\Phi}_{K}-M_{\star}^{(T+1)}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi}_{K},\Phi_{\star})^{2}+{\sigma_{w}^{(T+1)}}^{2}\frac{d_{y}r+\log(1/\delta)}{N^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\lesssim C(\rho)\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}^{2}(d_{x}r+\log(1/\delta))}{NT}+\frac{\mathopen{}\sigma_{w}^{(T+1)}\mathclose{}^{2}(d_{y}r+\log(1/\delta))}{N^{\prime}},

where we omit task-related quantities for clarity. We note that the above parameter recovery bound mirrors that of ERM estimates
(
Du et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, where we note the latter fine-tuning term scales with
d_{y}r
(the number of parameters in
F^{(T+1)}
) as opposed to
r
in the linear regression setting (
d_{y}=1
).

Discussion and Future Work

We propose an efficient algorithm to provably recover linear operators across multiple tasks to optimality from non-iid non-isotropic data, recovering near oracle empirical risk minimization rates. We show that the benefit of learning over multiple tasks manifests in a lower noise level in the optimization and smaller sample requirements for individual tasks. These results contribute toward a general understanding of representation learning from an algorithmic and statistical perspective. Some immediate open questions are: whether good initialization of the representation is necessary, and whether the convergence rate of
DFW
can be optimized e.g., through
\ell^{2}
-regularized weights
\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}
.
Resolving these questions has important implications for the natural extension of our framework: as emphasized in
(
Collins et al. 2021
)
, the alternating empirical risk minimization (holding representation fixed) and gradient descent (holding task-specific weights fixed) framework naturally extends to the nonlinear setting. Providing guarantees for nonlinear function classes is an exciting and impactful avenue for future work, which concurrent work is moving toward, e.g. for
2
-layer ReLU networks
(
Collins et al. 2023
)
and kernel ridge regression
(
Meunier et al. 2023
)
. It remains to be seen whether a computationally-efficient algorithm can be established for nonlinear meta-learning in the non-iid and non-isotropic data regime, while preserving joint scaling in number of tasks and data.

Lemma A.1
(cf.
Zhang et al. 2023

Let

be a
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
-valued process adapted to a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}

Let
\{w_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a
\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
-valued process adapted to
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\geq 2}

Suppose that

\sigma^{2}
-subgaussian

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[w_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]

\displaystyle=0,

(13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda v^{\top}w_{i})\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}\left\|v\right\|^{2}}{2}\right)\>\>\forall\mathcal{F}_{i}\text{-measurable }\lambda\in\mathbb{R},v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}.

(14)

For

,
let
\{M_{k}(\Lambda)\}_{k\geq 1}
be the

\displaystyle M_{k}(\Lambda)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle\Lambda x_{i},w_{i}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\|\Lambda x_{i}\right\|^{2}\right).

(15)

Then, the process
\{M_{k}(\Lambda)\}_{k\geq 1}

satisfies
\mathbb{E}[M_{k}(\Lambda)]\leq 1

In particular, this implies the following self-normalized martingale inequality that handles multiple matrix-valued self-normalized martingales simultaneously. This can be seen as an instantiation of the Hilbert space variant from

Proposition A.1
(cf.
Zhang et al. 2023

Fix

. For

,
let

be a
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}

and
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a filtration such that

\{x_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i\geq 1}
is adapted to
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i\geq 1}

\{w_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i\geq 1}
is adapted to
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i\geq 2}

\{w_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i\geq 1}

\sigma^{2}
-subgaussian martingale difference sequence.
Suppose that for all

For

Then, given any fixed

,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathopen{}w^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}^{\top}\left(V^{(t)}+\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{N}\right)^{-1/2}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq d_{y}\sigma^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\left(\frac{\det\left(V^{(t)}+\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{N}\right)}{\det(V^{(t)})}\right)+2\sigma^{2}\log(1/\delta)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathopen{}w^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}\mathopen{}x^{(t)}_{i}\mathclose{}^{\top}\left(V^{(t)}+\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{N}\right)^{-1/2}\right\|_{2}^{2}&\leq 4\sigma^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\left(\frac{\det\left(V^{(t)}+\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{N}\right)}{\det(V^{(t)})}\right)\\

Lemma A.3

Consider the quantities defined in

Definition A.1
(
\beta
-mixing)

Let
\{x_{i}\}_{t\geq 1}
be a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued discrete-time stochastic process adapted to filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
.
We denote the stationary distribution
\nu_{\infty}
. We define the
\beta
-mixing coefficient

\beta(k):=\sup_{t\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\left\{x_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell=1}^{t}}\left[\left\|\mathbb{P}_{x_{t+k}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{i})-\nu_{\infty}\right\|_{\mathrm{tv}}\right],

(20)

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathrm{tv}}
denotes the total variation distance between probability measures.

Intuitively, the
\beta
-mixing coefficient measures how quickly on average a process mixes to the stationary distribution along any sample path. To see how
\beta
-mixing is instantiated, let
\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
be a sample path from a
\beta
-mixing process. Consider the following subsampled paths formed by taking every
a
-th covariate of

X^{T}_{(j)}:=\left\{x_{i}:1\leq t\leq T,\;(t-1\;\mathrm{mod}\;a)=j-1\right\},\;\;j=1,\dots,a.

(21)

Let the integers
m_{1},\dots,m_{a}
and index sets
I_{(1)},\dots I_{(a)}
denote the sizes and indices of
X^{T}_{(1)},\dots,X^{T}_{(a)}
, respectively. Finally, let
X^{m_{j}}_{\infty}
denote a sequence of
m_{j}
iid draws from the stationary distribution
\nu_{\infty}
. The following is a key lemma in relating a correlated process to iid draws.

Lemma A.4
(
Kuznetsov and Mohri 2017
)

Let

be a real-valued Borel-measurable function satisfying
-M_{1}\leq g(\cdot)\leq M_{2}
for some
M_{1},M_{2}\geq 0
. Then, for all
j=1,\dots,a
.

\displaystyle{\left|\mathbb{E}[g(X_{\infty}^{m_{j}})]-\mathbb{E}[g(X_{(j)}^{T})]\right|}\leq(M_{1}+M_{2})m_{j}\beta(a).

In our analysis, we often instantiate
Lemma

A.4
with

as an indicator function on a success event. For appropriately selected block length
a
, we are thus able to relate simpler iid analysis on

to the original process

, accruing an additional factor in the failure probability. Lastly, we introduce a standard matrix concentration inequality.

Lemma A.5
(Matrix Hoeffding
[
Tropp 2011
]
)

Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a sequence of independent, random symmetric matrices, and let

be a sequence of fixed symmetric matrices. Assume each random matrix satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}\right]=0,\quad X_{t}^{2}\preceq B_{t}^{2}\text{ almost surely}.

Then for all
t\geq 0

\mathbb{P}\left[\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\right)\geq t\right]\leq d\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}\right),\quad\sigma^{2}:=\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}B_{t}^{2}\right\|.

In particular, for general rectangular

M_{t}^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}

\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t}\right]=0,\quad X_{t}^{2}\preceq B_{t}^{2}\text{ almost surely}.

\mathbb{P}\left[\sigma_{\max}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}M_{t}\right)\geq t\right]\leq(d_{1}+d_{2})\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}\right),\quad\sigma^{2}:=\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}B_{t}^{2}\right\|.

Let the conditions of

Proposition A.2
(Noise term bound)

Assume

\displaystyle N_{1}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\max\left\{\gamma^{4}\left(r+\log(T/\delta)\right),\max_{t}\frac{\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}^{2}}{C^{2}\|\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\|^{2}\lambda_{\min}(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})}\left(d_{y}+r+\log(T/\delta)\right)\right\},

\displaystyle N_{2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\gamma^{4}\left(d_{x}+\log(T/\delta)\right),

and
\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi},\Phi_{\star})\leq\max_{t}\frac{2}{5}C\kappa\hskip-1.5pt\left(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{}\right)^{-1}
, for fixed
C>0
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\left(\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x,\mathcal{N}_{2}}\right)^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}(1+C)\sqrt{\frac{d_{x}+\log(T/\delta)}{TN_{2}}\log\left(\frac{d_{x}}{\delta}\right)},

where
\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}^{2}\|\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\|^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})}}
is the
task-averaged
noise-level.

Proof of
Proposition

A.2
:
we set up for an application of the Matrix Hoeffding bound (
Lemma

A.5
). By union bounding over the task-specific noise bound

A.8
, we have with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
t\in[T]
simultaneously:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\left(\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x,\mathcal{N}_{2}}\right)^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+C)\frac{\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}\|F^{(t)}_{\star}\|}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})}}\sqrt{\frac{d_{x}+\log(T/\delta)}{N_{2}}},

given the assumed burn-in conditions hold. Therefore, by setting

\displaystyle B^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1)\frac{\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}\|F^{(t)}_{\star}\|}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})}}\sqrt{\frac{d_{x}+\log(T/\delta)}{N_{2}}}I_{d_{x}+r}

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}(B^{(t)})^{2}\right\|=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\mathopen{}\sigma^{(t)}_{w}\mathclose{}^{2}\|F^{(t)}_{\star}\|^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathopen{}\Sigma^{(t)}_{x}\mathclose{})}\right)(1+C)^{2}\frac{d_{x}+\log(T/\delta)}{N_{2}}

\displaystyle=T\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}^{2}(1+C)^{2}\frac{d_{x}+\log(T/\delta)}{N_{2}}

we invoke

A.5
and invert

The resulting Hoeffding bound yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\left(\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x,\mathcal{N}_{2}}\right)^{-1}\right\|\gtrsim\sqrt{T}\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}(1+C)\sqrt{\frac{d_{x}+\log(T/\delta)}{N_{2}}\log\left(\frac{d_{x}}{\delta}\right)}\right]\leq\delta

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}W_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}X_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\left(\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{x,\mathcal{N}_{2}}\right)^{-1}\right\|\gtrsim\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}(1+C)\sqrt{\frac{d_{x}+\log(T/\delta)}{TN_{2}}\log\left(\frac{d_{x}}{\delta}\right)}\right]\leq\delta

\hfill\blacksquare

We have bounded the noise term on the DFW representation update, demonstrating critically the bound on the noise term benefits from a scaling with the number of tasks
T
. The task-relevant quantity
\sigma_{\mathrm{avg}}
quantifies that the “noise level” of the problem is an
average
over the noise-levels of each task. We note that our application of Matrix Hoeffding is rather crude, and the above bound can likely be improved in terms of polylog
(1/\delta)
factors with stronger moment bounds on the matrix-valued self-normalized martingale terms, but this is out of the scope of this paper.

Returning to the choice of

,

implies no further system/task-specific dependence beyond the terms in

; however, this may translate into a stringent requirement on the burn-in

and the subspace distance

relaxes the burn-in and potentially renders the subspace distance requirement trivial, but manifests a condition number in the noise bound. We note that as we expect
\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\Phi},\Phi_{\star})
to decrease geometrically with iterations of DFW, the subspace distance requirement is only relevant for the first few iterations. In general, this intuitively captures the cost of ill-conditioned data distributions. We now move on to bounding the contraction term.

Bounding the Contraction Term

Let us define,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}

\displaystyle:=\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star}\left(I_{d_{x}}-\hat{\Phi}^{\top}\hat{\Phi}\right)\mathopen{}X_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}Z_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\left(\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{z,\mathcal{N}_{1}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}

\displaystyle:=\mathopen{}W_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}Z_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\left(\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}_{z,\mathcal{N}_{1}}\right)^{-1},

such that we may write (
22
) as
\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}=\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star}\hat{\Phi}^{\top}+\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}+\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}
. We expand

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}

\displaystyle=\hat{\Phi}\Phi_{\star}^{\top}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star}\hat{\Phi}^{\top}+\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}+\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{Sym}(\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star}\hat{\Phi}^{\top})+\mathrm{Sym}(\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star}\hat{\Phi}^{\top})+\mathrm{Sym}(\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}),

where
\mathrm{Sym}(A):=A+A^{\top}
.
We will make repeated use of the following matrix Cauchy-Schwarz-type lemma.

Lemma A.9

Let
A_{t},B_{t}
be real-valued matrices for
t=1,\dots,T
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}B_{t}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}A_{t}^{\top}\right\|^{1/2}\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}B_{t}^{\top}B_{t}\right\|^{1/2}.

Now taking the average over tasks
T
, we then may write

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\right)+\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right)+\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right)

\displaystyle+\left\|\mathrm{Sym}(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star}\hat{\Phi}^{\top})\right\|+\left\|\mathrm{Sym}(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star}\hat{\Phi}^{\top})\right\|+\left\|\mathrm{Sym}(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{})\right\|.

We observe that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{Sym}(A)\right\|

\displaystyle=\max_{\left\|u\right\|,\left\|v\right\|=1}u^{\top}Av+u^{\top}A^{\top}v

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|A\right\|,

and thus applying the above fact and
Lemma

A.9
on the cross terms yields

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}\hat{F}^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\right)+\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right)+\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right)

\displaystyle+2\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right\|^{1/2}\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\right\|^{1/2}+2\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right\|^{1/2}\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\right\|^{1/2}

\displaystyle+2\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}\Delta^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right\|^{1/2}\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}^{\top}\mathopen{}E^{(t)}\mathclose{}\right\|^{1/2}.

Using
Lemma

A.6
, we get the following upper bound on

A.10

Lemma A.14

Given
a_{1},a_{2},d\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, if
\varepsilon\geq a_{2}/a_{1}
, then the following holds:

\displaystyle\frac{1-a_{1}d}{\sqrt{1-a_{2}d}}<1-(1-\varepsilon)a_{1}d.

Additionally, as long as
\varepsilon\leq 1-\frac{1-\sqrt{1-a_{1}d}}{a_{1}d}
, then
1-(1-\varepsilon)a_{1}d\leq\sqrt{1-a_{1}d}
.

Proof of
Lemma

A.14
:
squaring the desired inequality and re-arranging some terms, we arrive at

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d}\left(1-\frac{(1-a_{1}d)^{2}}{(1-(1-\varepsilon)a_{1}d)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left(1-\frac{1-a_{1}d}{\underbrace{1-(1-\varepsilon)a_{1}d}_{<1}}\right)\underbrace{\left(1+\frac{1-a_{1}d}{1-(1-\varepsilon)a_{1}d}\right)}_{>1}.

To certify the above inequality, it suffices to lower-bound the RHS. Since
a_{1},a_{2},d\in(0,1)
, the last factor is at least
1
, such that we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\left(1-\frac{1-a_{1}d}{1-(1-\varepsilon)a_{1}d}\right)\left(1+\frac{1-a_{1}d}{1-(1-\varepsilon)a_{1}d}\right)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{d}\left(1-\frac{1-a_{1}d}{1-(1-\varepsilon)a_{1}d}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\frac{(1-\varepsilon)a_{1}d}{1-(1-\varepsilon)a_{1}d}

\displaystyle>\varepsilon a_{1}.

Therefore,
a_{2}\leq\varepsilon a_{1}
is sufficient for certifying the desired inequality. The latter claim follows by squaring and rearranging terms to yield the quadratic inequality:

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)^{2}a_{1}d-2(1-\varepsilon)+1\leq 0,

Setting
\lambda:=1-\varepsilon
, the solution interval is
\lambda\in\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-a_{1}d}}{a_{1}d},\frac{1+\sqrt{1-a_{1}d}}{a_{1}d}\right)
. The upper limit is redundant as it exceeds 1 and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, leaving the lower limit as the condition on
\varepsilon
proposed in the lemma.

A.4
Converting to Sample Complexity Bounds

To highlight the importance of the task scaling
T
in our descent guarantees, we demonstrate how to convert general descent lemmas to sample complexity guarantees.

Lemma A.15

For a sequence of positive integers
\{M_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{N}
, define
\{d_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
as a sequence of non-negative real numbers dependent on
\{M_{k}\}
that satisfy the relation

d_{k+1}\leq\rho\cdot d_{k}+\frac{C}{M_{k}},

for some
\rho\in(0,1)
and
C>0
. Let
d_{0}=\tau
. Given a positive integer
M
, we may partition
M=\sum_{k=1}^{K}M_{k}
, where

K:=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{2}{1+\rho}\right)^{-1}\frac{M\tau^{2}}{C}\left(\frac{1-\rho}{2}\right)^{3}+1\right\rfloor,

such that the following guarantee holds on
d_{K}
:

d_{K}\leq\tau\sqrt{\frac{2C}{M}\left(\frac{2}{1-\rho}\right)^{3}}.

The proof of
Lemma

A.15
follows by setting each
M_{k}
such that
\rho\cdot d_{k}+\frac{C}{M_{k}}=\left(\frac{1+\rho}{2}\right)d_{k}
, and setting
K
as the maximal
K
such that
\sum_{k=1}^{K}M_{k}\leq M
. Evaluating
d_{K}\leq\tau\left(\frac{1+\rho}{2}\right)^{K}
yields the result. For convenience, we do not consider burn-in times
M_{k}\geq M_{0}\;\forall k
or pseudo-linear dependence
\frac{C\mathrm{polylog}(M_{k})}{M_{k}}
. However, these will only lead to inflating
d_{K}
by a polylog
(M)
factor.

In essence,
Lemma

A.15
demonstrates how a fixed offline dataset of size
M
can be partitioned into independent blocks of increasing size such that the final iterate satisfies an approximate ERM bound scaling as
\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}
, inflated by a function of the contraction rate
\rho
. Instantiating
Lemma

A.15
with the problem parameters of Theorem
3.1
yields
Corollary

3.1
.

Case Study: Linear Dynamical Systems

To understand the importance of permitting non-isotropy and sequential dependence in multi-task data, we consider the fundamental setting of linear systems, which has served as a staple testbed for statistical and algorithmic analysis in recent years, since it lends itself to non-trivial yet tractable

[
Hu et al. 2023
,
Krauth et al. 2019
,
Tu and Recht 2018
,
Tu and Recht 2019
,

,

]
), as well as (online) statistical learning problems with temporally dependent covariates
[
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
,
Abbasi-Yadkori 2013
,
Simchowitz and Foster 2020
,
Dean et al. 2018
,
Dean et al. 2020
,
Agarwal et al. 2019a
,
Agarwal et al. 2019b
,
Ziemann et al. 2022
,
Ziemann and Sandberg 2022
,
Lee et al. 2023
,
Simchowitz et al. 2018
]
(see
[
Tsiamis et al. 2022
]
for a tutorial and literature review). In particular for our purposes, the dependence of contiguous covariates in a linear system is intricately connected to its
stability properties

[
Simchowitz et al. 2018
,
Jedra and Proutiere 2020
,
Tu et al. 2022
]
, such that we may instantiate the guarantees of
DFW
for non-iid data in an interpretable manner.

The standard state-space linear system set-up admits the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}&s[t+1]=A^{(h)}s[t]+B^{(h)}u[t]+w[t]\\
&w[t]\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{(h)}_{w}),\;\;s[0]\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{(h)}_{0}),\end{split}

(29)

where we preemptively index possibly task-specific quantities. We consider the following two common linear system settings: system identification and imitation learning.

B.1
Linear System Identification

In linear system identification, the aim is to estimate the system matrices
(A^{(h)},B^{(h)})
given state and input measurements
s_{t},u_{t}
. In particular, we may cast the sysID problem as the following regression:

s[t+1]=\begin{bmatrix}A^{(h)}&B^{(h)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}s[t]\\
u[t]\end{bmatrix}+w[t].

It is customary to consider exploratory signals that are iid zero-mean Gaussian random vectors
u[t]\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{(h)}_{u})

[
Simchowitz et al. 2018
,
Simchowitz and Foster 2020
,
Tsiamis et al. 2022
]
. In the stable system case,
\rho(A^{(h)})<1
, we can therefore evaluate the covariance of the
stationary
distribution of states
s[t]
induced by exploratory signal
u[t]\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{(h)}_{u})
by plugging in (
29
) into the following equation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,w}[s[t]s[t]^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u,w}\left[s[t+1]s[t+1]^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=A^{(h)}\mathbb{E}_{u,w}\left[s[t]s[t]^{\top}\right]{A^{(h)}}^{\top}+B^{(h)}\mathbb{E}\left[u[t]u[t]^{\top}\right]{B^{(h)}}^{\top}+\mathbb{E}\left[w[t]w[t]^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=A^{(h)}\mathbb{E}_{u,w}\left[s[t]s[t]^{\top}\right]{A^{(h)}}^{\top}+B^{(h)}\Sigma^{(h)}_{u}{B^{(h)}}^{\top}+\Sigma^{(h)}_{w}

Therefore, evaluating the stationary state covariance
\Sigma^{(h)}_{s}:=\mathbb{E}\left[s[\infty]s[\infty]^{\top}\right]
amounts to solving the Discrete Lyapunov Equation (
dlyap

\Sigma_{s}^{(h)}:=A^{(h)}\Sigma_{s}^{(h)}{A^{(h)}}^{\top}+B^{(h)}\Sigma^{(h)}_{u}{B^{(h)}}^{\top}+\Sigma^{(h)}_{w}.

In the notation introduced earlier in the paper, casting
y[t]\leftarrow s[t+1]
,
x[t]\leftarrow\begin{bmatrix}s[t]\\
u[t]\end{bmatrix}
,
M_{\star}^{(h)}\leftarrow\begin{bmatrix}A^{(h)}&B^{(h)}\end{bmatrix}
, we may instantiate multi-task linear system identification as a non-iid, non-isotropic linear operator recovery problem.

Definition B.1

Let the initial state covariance be the stationary covariance
\Sigma_{0}^{(h)}=\Sigma_{s}^{(h)}
, such that the covariance of the marginal covariate distribution satisfies

\displaystyle\Sigma_{x}^{(h)}:=\mathbb{E}\left[x[t]x[t]^{\top}\right]=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{s}^{(h)}&0\\
0&\Sigma_{u}^{(h)}\end{bmatrix},\;\text{for all }t\geq 0.

We make the above standard definition for the initial state distribution for convenience, as it ensures the marginal distributions of each state are identical. We note, however, given a different initial state distribution, the marginal state distribution converges exponentially quickly to stationarity, thus accumulating only a negligible factor to the final rates. We then make the following system assumptions to instantiate our representation learning guarantees.

Assumption B.1

We assume that for any task

the following hold:

1.

The operators share a rowspace

,

The state matrices have uniformly bounded spectral radii
\rho(A^{(h)})<\mu<1
. Subsequently, we assume there exists a constant
\Gamma^{\prime}>0
that satisfies

\|{A^{(h)}}^{k}\|_{2}\leq\Gamma^{\prime}\mu^{k},\;\;\text{for all }k\geq 0.

The existence of a uniform

is guaranteed by Gelfand’s Formula
[
Horn and Johnson 2012
]

The first assumption is satisfied, for example, when

and

individually admit low-rank decompositions. The second assumption translates to a quantitative bound on the mixing time of the covariates

by adapting a result from

(Adapted from

For each

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\nu_{0}}\left[\left\|\mathbb{P}_{s[k]}\left[\;\cdot\mid s_{0}=s\right]-\mathbb{P}_{s[k]}\right\|_{\mathrm{tv}}\right]\leq\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{0}}[\left\|s[0]\right\|^{2}]+\frac{\left\|\Sigma^{-1}\right\|_{\ast}}{1-\mu^{2}}}\cdot\mu^{k},

(30)

where

Lemma B.1

Following

and

B.2
Imitation Learning

In linear (state-feedback) imitation learning (IL), the aim is to estimate linear state-feedback controllers

\displaystyle u[t]=K^{(h)}_{\star}s[t]+z[t],\quad z[t]\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{z}^{(h)}),

\displaystyle s[t+1]

\displaystyle=A^{(h)}s[t]+B^{(h)}\left(K^{(h)}_{\star}s[t]+z[t]\right)+w[t]

We make the natural assumption that the expert controller
K_{\star}^{(h)}
stabilizes the system, i.e. the spectral radius of the closed-loop dynamics has spectral radius strictly less than 1:
\rho\left(A^{(h)}+B^{(h)}K_{\star}^{(h)}\right)<1
. As such, similar to the linear sysID setting, we may plug the above dynamics into the stationarity equation to yield the stationary covariance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[s[t]s[t]^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[s[t+1]s[t+1]^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(A^{(h)}+B^{(h)}K_{\star}^{(h)}\right)\mathbb{E}[s[t]s[t]^{\top}]\left(A^{(h)}+B^{(h)}K_{\star}^{(h)}\right)^{\top}+B^{(h)}\Sigma_{z}^{(h)}{B^{(h)}}^{\top}+\Sigma_{w}^{(h)}

\displaystyle\implies\Sigma_{s}^{(h)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{dlyap}\left(A^{(h)}+B^{(h)}K_{\star}^{(h)},B^{(h)}\Sigma_{z}^{(h)}{B^{(h)}}^{\top}+\Sigma_{w}^{(h)}\right).

Analogously to linear sysID, we make the following assumptions.

Assumption B.2

We assume that for any task
h
the following hold:

1.

The initial state covariance is set to the stationary covariance
\Sigma_{0}^{(h)}=\Sigma_{s}^{(h)}
, such that the marginal covariate distributions satisfy

\mathbb{E}\left[x[t]x[t]^{\top}\right]=\Sigma_{s}^{(h)}=:\Sigma_{x}^{(h)},\;\text{for all }t\geq 0.

2.

The controllers share a rowspace
M_{\star}^{(h)}\equiv K_{\star}^{(h)}=\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\Phi_{\star}
,
\mathopen{}F^{(t)}_{\star}\mathclose{}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times r}
,
\Phi_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{s}}
.

3.

The closed-loop dynamics have uniformly bounded spectral radii
\rho\left(A^{(h)}+B^{(h)}K_{\star}^{(h)}\right)<\mu<1
. Subsequently, we assume there exists a constant
\Gamma^{\prime}>0
that satisfies

\left\|\left(A^{(h)}+B^{(h)}K_{\star}^{(h)}\right)^{k}\right\|_{2}\leq\Gamma^{\prime}\mu^{k}.

The existence of uniform
\Gamma^{\prime}
is guaranteed by Gelfand’s Formula
[
Horn and Johnson 2012
]
.

By using a result almost identical to
Proposition

B.1
, we yield the following quantitative bound on the mixing time of covariates generated by stabilizing expert controllers.

Lemma B.2

Following
B.2
, the covariate process
\left\{x^{(h)}[t]\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is a mean-zero, stationary, geometrically
\beta
-mixing process with covariance
\Sigma_{x}^{(h)}=\Sigma_{s}^{(h)}
, where
\Sigma_{s}^{(h)}=\mathrm{dlyap}\left(A^{(h)}+B^{(h)}K_{\star}^{(h)},B^{(h)}\Sigma_{z}^{(h)}{B^{(h)}}^{\top}+\Sigma_{w}^{(h)}\right)
, and mixing-time bounded by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\beta(k)=\Gamma\mu^{k},\;\;\text{where}\\
&\Gamma:=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{2}\sqrt{\trace\left(\Sigma_{s}^{(h)}\right)+\frac{\left\|\Sigma^{-1}\right\|_{\ast}}{1-\mu^{2}}},\;\Sigma:=B^{(h)}\Sigma_{z}^{(h)}{B^{(h)}}^{\top}+\Sigma_{w}^{(h)}.\end{split}

(32)

Thus, instantiating
Lemma

B.1
in Theorem
A.2
gives us guarantees of
DFW
applied to multi-task linear imitation learning.

C.3
Imitation Learning

Our focus now turns to the problem of learning a linear quadratic regulator (LQR) controller, denoted as
K^{(T+1)}=F^{(T+1)}_{\star}\Phi_{\star}
, by imitating the behavior of
T
expert controllers
K^{(1)},K^{(2)},\ldots,K^{(T)}
. These controllers share a common low-rank representation and can be decomposed into the form
K^{(t)}=F^{(t)}_{\star}\Phi_{\star}
, where
F^{(t)}_{\star}
represents the task-specific weight and
\Phi_{\star}
corresponds to the common representation across all tasks. To achieve this, we exploit Algorithm
1
to compute a shared low-rank representation for all tasks by leveraging data obtained from the expert controllers. Within this context, we consider a discrete-time linear time-invariant dynamical system as follows:

\displaystyle x^{(t)}[i+1]=Ax^{(t)}[i]+Bu^{(t)}[i],\;\ i=0,1,\ldots,N-1,

with
n_{x}=4
states and
n_{u}=4
inputs, for all
t\in[T+1]
, where
u^{(t)}[i]=K^{(t)}x^{(t)}[i]+z^{(t)}[i]
, with
z^{(t)}[i]\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n_{u}})
being the input noise. In our current setting, rather than directly observing the state, we obtain a high-dimensional observation derived from an injective linear function of the state. Specifically, we assume that
y^{(t)}[i]=Gx^{(t)}[i]+w^{(t)}[i]
, where
G\in\mathbb{R}^{25\times 4}
represents the high-dimensional linear mapping. The injective linear mapping matrix
G
is generated by applying a
thin_svd
operation to a random matrix with values drawn from a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. This process ensures injectiveness with a high probability.

For this aforementioned multi-task imitation learning setting, we adopt a scheme in which we gather observations of the form
\{\{y^{(t)}[i],u^{(t)}[i]\}_{i=0}^{N-1}\}_{t=1}^{T}
from the initial
T
expert controllers to learn the controller
K^{(T+1)}
. These observations are obtained by following the dynamics:

\displaystyle y^{(t)}[i]=(\tilde{A}+\tilde{B}\tilde{K}^{(t)})y[i]+\tilde{B}z^{(t)}[i]+w^{(t)}[i]

with
\tilde{A}=GAG^{\dagger}
,
\tilde{B}=GB
,
\tilde{K}^{(t)}=K^{(t)}G^{\dagger}
, and process noise
w^{(t)}[i]\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{w})
.

The collection of stabilizing LQR controllers
K^{(1)},K^{(2)},\ldots,K^{(T+1)}
is generated by assigning different cost matrices, namely
R=\frac{1}{4}I_{n_{u}}
and
Q^{(t)}=\alpha^{(t)}I_{n_{x}}
, where
\alpha^{(t)}\in\texttt{logspace}(0,3,H)
. These matrices are then utilized to solve the Discrete Algebraic Ricatti Equation (DARE):
P^{(t)}=A^{\top}P^{(t)}A^{\top}+A^{\top}P^{(t)}B(B^{\top}P^{(t)}B+R)^{-1}B^{\top}P^{(t)}A+Q^{(t)}
, and compute
K^{(t)}=-(B^{\top}P^{(t)}B+R)^{-1}B^{\top}P^{(t)}A
, for all
t\in[T+1]
. Moreover, the system matrices
A
and
B
are randomly generated, with elements drawn from a uniform distribution. The trajectory length
N=75
remains consistent for all tasks. The shared representation is initialized by applying a random rotation to the true representation, denoted as
\Phi_{\star}=G^{\dagger}
.
