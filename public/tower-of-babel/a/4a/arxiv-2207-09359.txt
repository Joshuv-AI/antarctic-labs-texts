Grassmannians over rings and subpolygons
Michael Cuntz

Abstract
. We investigate special points on the Grassmannian which correspond to friezes with coefficients in the case of rank two. Using representations of arithmetic matroids we obtain a theorem on subpolygons of specializations of the coordinate ring. As a special case we recover the characterization of subpolygons in classic frieze patterns. Moreover, we observe that specializing clusters of the coordinate ring of the Grassmannian to units yields representations that may be interpreted as arrangements of hyperplanes with notable properties. In particular, we get an interpretation of certain Weyl groups and groupoids as generalized frieze patterns.

Introduction

The coordinate ring of a Grassmannian
\Gr(k,n)
is known to have a cluster structure
[
Sco06
]
.
When
k=2
, the clusters correspond to triangulations of an
n
-gon by non-intersecting diagonals.
Under this correspondence, Plücker coordinates
p_{i,j}
are edges and diagonals between vertices
i,j
; they satisfy the so-called Ptolemy relations. Specializing all variables of a cluster to some values in a commutative ring
R
we obtain a map
from the set of diagonals and edges of the
n
-gon to
R
.
Coxeter mentioned such a map in
[
Cox91
]
,
in
[
CHJ20
]
we call it a
frieze with coefficients
; it is a frieze pattern in the classical sense when the frozen variables (the edges of the
n
-gon) are mapped to
1

[
CC06
]
.

By the Laurent phenomenon, specializing all variables of a cluster to
1
gives a frieze with values in
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, it is then called a
Conway-Coxeter frieze
. Restricting the frieze to a subpolygon yields a frieze with values in the same ring. However, the frozen variables are not necessarily specialized to
1
since the edges of the subpolygon were originally diagonals. In
[
CHJ20
]
we raise the question to describe friezes with integer values that are obtained from subpolygons in Conway-Coxeter frieze patterns.
We give an answer to the case of triangles which is somewhat surprising at first sight:

Theorem

(
[
CHJ20
, Thm. 5.12]
)
.

A triple
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
appears as labels of a triangle
in some Conway-Coxeter frieze if and only if the following two conditions are
satisfied:

(1)

\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,c)=\gcd(a,c)
,

(2)

\nu_{2}(a)=\nu_{2}(b)=\nu_{2}(c)=0
or
|\{\nu_{2}(a),\nu_{2}(b),\nu_{2}(c)|>1

where
\nu_{2}(\cdot)
denotes the 2-valuation.

There is this strange appearance of
2
-valuations which was elucidated in our more general result:

Theorem

(
[
CH21
, Thm. 3.2]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a frieze with coefficients
on an
n
-gon over positive integers. Then
\mathcal{C}
appears as a subpolygon
of some Conway-Coxeter frieze if and only if the following conditions are
satisfied:

(1)

For any triangle
(a,b,c)
in
\mathcal{C}
we have

\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,c)=\gcd(a,c)
.

(2)

Let

be a prime number. Then for each
(p+1)
-subpolygon
\mathcal{D}

of

the labels of edges and diagonals in

are either
all not divisible by

or they do not all have the same

-valuation.

This explains why

-valuations play a role in the case of triangles.
However, the conditions still look mysterious.
In this paper, we generalize the question to
\SL_{k}
-friezes, viewed as in
[
MGOST14
]
as points on arbitrary Grassmannians
1

1

arbitrary

We call such a generalized frieze a

of the coordinate ring

.
It turns out that the

Let

Then

For the formulation and proof of the theorem we need some completely different notions and ideas (see Section
3
for details).
One can view a specialization of
\mathcal{A}_{k,n}
as a certain kind of
matroid
:
a matroid can be defined by prescribing subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
that are called linearly dependent.
If the matroid is the matroid of linear dependencies of some set of vectors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in R^{k}
, then this information can also be encoded by the
k
-subsets that have determinant
0
.
A specialization in our sense contains some more information, it prescribes the values of the Plücker coordinates and hence the values of all determinants. If a matroid is given by a set of vectors, then it is called representable.
We will call a set of vectors that represents a specialization of
\mathcal{A}_{k,n}
a
representation
.

Classically, the axioms of matroids model linear dependence of vectors in a vector space.
Since for frieze patterns over the integers we need linear dependence over
\mathbb{Z}
, we are working
with a structure similar to an arithmetic matroid instead
[
DM13
]
,
[
PP21
]
.
Conditions on the greatest common divisors like (1) also appear in the study of arithmetic matroids.

The proof of Theorem
4.6
uses representations and coordinates and is thus much easier to comprehend than our proofs in
[
CHJ20
]
and
[
CH21
]
: we argue with coordinate vectors instead of Plücker coordinates.

In the special case
R=\mathbb{Z}
one can additionally require that
\psi(t_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}})>0
for all
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}
. Such a specialization could then possibly come from an
\SL_{k}
-frieze pattern.
An
\SL_{k}
-frieze pattern is a
specialization with variables specialized to
\mathbb{N}
, frozen variables specialized to
1
, and
which has a representation
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}

such that the
x_{i}
have coprime coordinates, hence in particular it satisfies the condition of Theorem
4.6
.
Together with this theorem, the following second result characterizes subpolygons in
\SL_{k}
-frieze patterns (see Section
7
):

Theorem

(Thm.
7.1
)
.

Let
X=(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k\times n}
be a representation of a
specialization of
\mathcal{A}_{k,n}

with
|x_{i}|=1
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Assume that
|x_{i_{1}}\ldots x_{i_{k}}|>0
for all
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n
.
Then the specialization
may be extended to an
\SL_{k}
-frieze pattern,
i.e. a specialization
\psi
with positive integer values on the cluster variables and
in which all frozen variables are specialized to
1
.

The set of all clusters together with mutations gives a structure which has many features in common with a Weyl groupoid, see
[
CH11
]
for the case of rank two. But a connection in higher rank was nebulous so far.
In addition to the characterization of subpolygons in
\SL_{k}
-friezes, in Section
6
we also observe a relation between the cluster structure on the coordinate ring of a Grassmannian and the structure of a Weyl groupoid:
We consider specializations of the variables of a cluster to units.
It turns out that this produces arrangements of hyperplanes with special properties.
We exhibit some examples; for instance, we recover several crystallographic arrangements in rank three, these correspond to Weyl groupoids
[
Cun11
]
.
The following question remains:

Question 1.1
.

Which crystallographic arrangements are defined by representations of clusters in which we specialize all variables to
\pm 1
?

Since for fixed
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
the number of clusters is often infinite, on the other hand the number of matroids of rank
k
on
1,\ldots,n
is finite, it is conceivable that any matroid which has a representation is determined by the choice of units (in some ring of integers) for the variables of a fixed cluster.

2.
Grassmannian and Plücker coordinates

Let
R
be an integral domain and
K:=\Quot(R)
its field of fractions.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
U\leq K^{n}
be
a point on the
Grassmannian

\Gr(k,n)
,
i.e. a subspace of
K^{n}
of dimension
k
.
Let

v_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1,1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{1,n}\end{pmatrix},\ldots,v_{k}:=\begin{pmatrix}x_{k,1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{k,n}\end{pmatrix}

be a basis of
U
and
X=(x_{i,j})_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq k}

be the matrix with the coordinates of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
in its
rows
.
We write
X_{\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}}
for the matrix consisting of the columns
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
of
X
(where the labels are not required to be different or in increasing ordering), and set

p_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}:=\det(X_{\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}}).

These elements of
K
are called the
Plücker coordinates
of
U
. Indeed,
consider the element
v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{k}\in\bigwedge^{k}K^{n}
of the
k
-th exterior power of
K^{n}
. Then

v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{k}=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}=1}^{n}x_{1,i_{1}}e_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{1,i_{1}}e_{i_{k}}=\sum_{i_{1}\leq\ldots\leq i_{k}=1}^{n}p_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}e_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{i_{k}}

if
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
is the standard basis of
K^{n}
. It is easy to see that a base change on
U
yields the same
Plücker coordinates.

Now view the coordinates in
X
as variables in the polynomial algebra

P:=K[x_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i\leq k,1\leq j\leq n]

and consider the polynomial algebra

T:=K[t_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}\mid 1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n]=S({\bigwedge}^{k}K^{n}).

Let
p
be the homomorphism

p:T\rightarrow P,\quad t_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}\mapsto\det(X_{\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}})=p_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}.

We see that
\mathcal{A}_{k,n}:=T/\ker(p)
is the coordinate ring of
\Gr(k,n)
.

When
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
are not strictly increasing, we define

p_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}:=\sgn(\sigma)p_{i_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,i_{\sigma(k)}}

for a permutation
\sigma\in S_{k}
sorting the labels.
The following lemma is an easy exercise.

Lemma 2.1
.

The Plücker coordinates satisfy the following
Plücker relations
:

(1)

\sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^{r}p_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},j_{r}}p_{j_{0},\ldots,\hat{j}_{r},\ldots,j_{k}}=0

for all
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1}
and
j_{0},\ldots,j_{k}
.

In this paper, all indices
i
with
i>n
or
i<0
which should be within a range
\{1,\ldots,n\}
have to be reduced modulo
n
into this range.
Moreover, we write
[i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}]
for the ordered sequence with elements
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
in ascending order.

3.
Representations and specializations

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{k,n}
be the coordinate ring of
\Gr(k,n)
as defined above.
A homomorphism

\psi:\mathcal{A}_{k,n}\rightarrow K

is called a
specialization
of
\mathcal{A}_{k,n}
.

A
representation
of
\psi
is a matrix
X\in K^{k\times n}
such that

\psi(t_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}})=\det(X_{\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}})

for all
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n
.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that we do not require that a specialization takes the value
1
on frozen variables (see Section
5
).
The case when all frozen variables are specialized to
1
produces an
\SL_{k}
-frieze pattern as a representation of the specialization, see Example
5.1
or
[
BFG+21
]
.

Definition 3.3
.

An

\SL_{k}
-frieze pattern
is a specialization
\psi
to
\mathbb{Q}
with

\psi(t_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}})\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\quad\text{and}\quad\psi(t_{[i,\ldots,i+k-1]})=1

for all
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n
,
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Remark 3.4
.

Choose a subset
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\mid 1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n\}

and let
\Sigma
be the set of specializations with

\psi(t_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}})=0
for
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in\mathcal{M}
.
If
\Sigma
is not empty, then
\mathcal{M}
is a
matroid
.
The set of representations of all
\psi\in\Sigma
is the moduli space of representations of
\mathcal{M}
as a matroid.

The study of integral specializations (or specializations with values in a principal ideal domain) involves the following important volume function.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
R
be a principal ideal domain and
A\in R^{k\times r}
,
r\leq k
.
Then we denote by
|A|
a generator of the ideal generated by all
r\times r
minors of
A
.
By abuse of notation, we will write
|A|=a
if the ideals
(|A|)=(a)
are equal.

In particular, if
R=\mathbb{Z}
, then we can choose the non-negative integer

|A|=|\mathbb{Z}^{k}/(A\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{r})|.

Hence
|A|
is the product of the elementary divisors of
A
and is equal to the
volume
of the parallelotope spaned by the columns of
A
.

For
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m}\in R^{k}
, we will write

|\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{m}|
for the volume of the matrix with columns
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}
.
If
m=1
and
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
, then
|\beta|
is the greatest common divisor of the coordinates of
\beta
. We will write
|\beta|=1
if
|\beta|
is a unit.

If
m=k
and
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
,
then we will write
|\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{k}|
for the
determinant of the matrix with columns
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}
;
here we keep track of the signs.

Remark 3.6
.

An
arithmetic matroid
consists of a set
E=\{1,\ldots,n\}
together with
two maps
\rk
and
m
on subsets of
E
such that
\rk
may be viewed as the rank function of a matroid,
and, roughly speaking,
m
‘is’ the volume map defined above.
Of course, these maps have to satisfy certain axioms, see
[
PP21
, Def. 2.3]
for details.
Arithmetic matroids are used to investigate toric arrangements
[
DM13
]
.
Compared to our notion of a specialization, there are several crucial differences:
Representations of arithmetic matroids are in general chosen over the ring
R=\mathbb{Z}
because the map
m
takes values in
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
Moreover, our specializations are much more restrictive since we include for instance informations on the signs of volumes by prescribing the exact values of all determinants.
In contrast to our specializations, there are more representations for an arithmetic matroid in general
(see Theorem
3.7
below).

The following result is almost trivial in the case when the frozen variables are specialized to
1
(Example
5.1
). The situation is more subtle in the general case:

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
R
be a principal ideal domain,
K
its field of fractions, and

\psi
be a specialization of
\mathcal{A}_{k,n}
such that

\psi(t_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}})\in R\setminus\{0\}

for all
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n
.
Then the map
\psi
has a representation
X\in R^{k\times n}
.

Proof.

Denote
p_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}:=\psi(t_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}})
.
We proceed by induction over
k
.
If
k=1
then we may choose the representation
X=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
.
Now assume that
k>1
.
Let
\delta
be a greatest common divisor of
p_{1,i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}
for all
1<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n
.
Then there exist
\lambda_{i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}\in R
such that

\delta=\sum_{1<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n}\lambda_{i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}p_{1,i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}.

Using the Plücker relations (
1
) we get:

\displaystyle\delta\cdot p_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k}}p_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}\lambda_{i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}p_{1,i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k}}\sum_{\nu=1}^{k}(-1)^{\nu-1}p_{1,j_{1},\ldots,\hat{j}_{\nu},\ldots,j_{k}}\lambda_{i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}p_{j_{\nu},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=1}^{k}(-1)^{\nu-1}\left(\sum_{1<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k}}\lambda_{i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}p_{j_{\nu},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}\right)p_{1,j_{1},\ldots,\hat{j}_{\nu},\ldots,j_{k}}.

With

a_{j}:=\sum_{1<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k}}\lambda_{i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}p_{j,i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}

we obtain

(2)

p_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{k}(-1)^{\nu-1}a_{j_{\nu}}\frac{p_{1,j_{1},\ldots,\widehat{j_{\nu}},\ldots,j_{k}}}{\delta}.

Note that

(3)

\mathcal{A}_{k-1,n-1}\rightarrow K,\quad t_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1}}\mapsto\frac{p_{1,i_{1}+1,\ldots,i_{k-1}+1}}{\delta}\in R\setminus\{0\}

is a specialization of
\mathcal{A}_{k-1,n-1}
with Plücker coordinates in
R\setminus\{0\}
.
Hence by induction this has a representation
\tilde{X}\in R^{(k-1)\times(n-1)}
.
Now it suffices to check that

X=\begin{pmatrix}\delta&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{n}\\
0&&&\\
\vdots&&\tilde{X}&\\
0&&&\\
\end{pmatrix}

is a representation of
\psi
: Writing
X=(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})
, we get

|x_{1}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}|=p_{1,i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}
by (
3
)
for all
1<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n
and

|x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{k}}|=p_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}
by Equation (
2
)
for all
1<j_{1}<\ldots<j_{k}\leq n
.
∎

4.
Representations with volume one vectors

Our goal in Section
7
is to characterize specializations which are restrictions of
\SL_{k}
-friezes to subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
; note that
\SL_{k}
-friezes have representations in which all vectors have volume
1
.
Therefore the first step is to understand under which circumstances a representation exists such that all vectors have coprime entries.

Example 4.1
.

Let
\psi:\mathcal{A}_{2,3}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
,
\psi(t_{1,2})=\psi(t_{2,3})=\psi(t_{1,3})=3
.
Then the following matrices are both representations of
\psi
:

\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\
0&3&3\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}1&2&1\\
0&3&3\end{pmatrix}.

However, only the second one has the property that all columns have volume
1
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
R
be a principal ideal domain and

X=(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})\in R^{k\times n}
be such that

|x_{i}|=1
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then for all
1\leq i\leq n
, the ideals

(|x_{i}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}|\mid 1\leq i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n)=(|x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}|\mid 1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n)

coincide.

Proof.

The inclusion “
\subseteq
” is trivial. For the converse,
without loss of generality, after a base change over
R
we may assume that

x_{i}
is the first standard basis vector (remember that
|x_{i}|=1
). Denote
w_{j}
the vector
x_{j}
in which we have removed the first coordinate. Then

|x_{i}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}|=|w_{i_{2}}\cdots w_{i_{k}}|.

Hence

|x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}|=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}(-1)^{\ell}X_{1,i_{\ell}}|w_{i_{1}}\cdots\widehat{w_{i_{\ell}}}\cdots w_{i_{k}}|

is a linear combination of
|x_{i}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}|
,
i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k}
.
∎

Example 4.3
.

Let

\psi:\mathcal{A}_{2,3}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z},\quad\psi(t_{1,2})=\psi(t_{2,3})=\psi(t_{1,3})=2.

All ideals considered in Proposition
4.2
are then equal to
(2)
, so one could hope for a
representation of
\psi
in which the volumes of the columns are all equal to
1
.
In this case, we could choose the first column to be the first standard basis vector.
But then, this representation would be the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}1&a&b\\
0&2&2\end{pmatrix}

for some
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
a-b=1
.
Since
a
and
b
must have different parity, one of them is even, the corresponding column has volume
2
, and we get a contradiction.
Thus it is not possible to find a representation such that the volumes of the columns are all equal to
1
.

We will see in the theorem below that in this example, the explanation is that the projective line over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
has only
3
points.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
R
be a principal ideal domain and

\psi
be a specialization of
\mathcal{A}_{k,n}
such that

\psi(t_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}})\in R\setminus\{0\}

for all
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n
.
Assume that there is a prime
q\in R
with

q\in(p_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}\mid 1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n).

Then if
\psi
has a representation

X=(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})\in R^{k\times n}

such that
|x_{i}|=1
for all
1\leq i\leq n
,
then there is a representation

X^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{1}\cdots x^{\prime}_{n})\in R^{k\times n}

such that
|x^{\prime}_{i}|=1
and
X^{\prime}_{1,i}\in(q)
for all
1\leq i\leq n
,

Proof.

Let
F:=R/(q)
and
Y\in F^{k\times n}

|x_{j}\ldots x_{k-1}\>y\>x_{k}\ldots x_{j+k-2}|=z_{j}\leq a_{j,j}

and thus

\prod_{j=1}^{k}|x_{j}\ldots x_{k-1}\>y\>x_{k}\ldots x_{j+k-2}|\leq\det(\tilde{A})<\det(A)=\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}|x_{j}\ldots x_{j+k-1}|.

Including
y
, the new sequence satisfies our assumptions:
the new frozen variables are positive since
z_{j}>0
for all
j
;
the remaining variables are all positive because the new frozen variables extend
a previous cluster in which all variables were positive.
The product
D
has decreased and we are finished by induction.

We may now assume without loss of generality that
|\alpha_{i}|=1
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
, and moreover that
p_{1,\ldots,k}>1
. Since
\det(A)=\det(\tilde{A})
in this case, we have to be more careful with the choice of
y
.
Again, we choose a basis such that
A=\tilde{A}
is in Hermite normal form.
We compute the coordinates of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
with respect to this basis.
Recall (Equation
4
) that for
1\leq i<k
,

\alpha_{1}(x_{i})=\cdots=\alpha_{i}(x_{i})=0,\quad\alpha_{i+1}(x_{i})=(-1)^{i}p_{i,\ldots,i+k-1}.

Using
\alpha_{1}(x_{k})=p_{1,\ldots,k}
,
\alpha_{k}(x_{k})=0
,
the fact that
A
is upper triangular, and
a_{1,1}=1
(because
|\alpha_{1}|=1
), we get

(x_{1}\cdots x_{k})=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&\cdots&0&p_{1,\ldots,k}\\
\frac{p_{1,\ldots,k}}{a_{2,2}}&0&\cdots&\vdots&*\\
*&-\frac{p_{2,\ldots,k+1}}{a_{3,3}}&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0&*\\
*&\cdots&*&(-1)^{k}\frac{p_{k-1,\ldots,2k-2}}{a_{k,k}}&0\end{pmatrix},

and this should be a matrix in
\mathbb{Z}^{k\times k}
.
So
p_{i,\ldots,i+k-1}
is divisible by
a_{i+1,i+1}
for all
i
.
On the other hand,

\prod_{i=2}^{k}a_{i,i}=\det(A)=\pm\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}p_{i,\ldots,i+k-1},

which is only possible if
a_{i+1,i+1}=p_{i,\ldots,i+k-1}
for all
i
.
Now
0\leq a_{2,1}<a_{2,2}=p_{1,\ldots,k}
because
A
is in Hermite normal form.
Since
p_{1,\ldots,k}>1
and
|\alpha_{2}|=1
,
a_{2,1}=0
is excluded.
As before we look for a
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
such that
A\cdot y=z
has positive coordinates;
the form of
A
allows a
y
such that
0<z_{i}\leq a_{i,i}
for all
i
.
In particular,
a_{1,1}=1
, so we can choose
y_{1}=1
and get
z_{1}=1
.
For
y_{2}
we get the equation
a_{2,1}y_{1}+a_{2,2}y_{2}=z_{2}
, or

a_{2,1}+y_{2}\cdot p_{1,\ldots,k}=z_{2}.

With
y_{2}=0
we get
z_{2}=a_{2,1}<p_{1,\ldots,k}
.
Hence after choosing the remaining
y_{i}
,

\prod_{j=1}^{k}|x_{j}\ldots x_{k-1}\>y\>x_{k}\ldots x_{j+k-2}|=\prod_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}<\prod_{j=1}^{k}a_{j,j}=\det(A).

Including
y
in the sequence decreases the product
D
of all
p_{i,\ldots,i+k-1}
;
moreover, the new sequence satisfies the assumptions (
z_{j}>0
for all
j
and these extend a previous cluster),
so we are finished by induction.
∎
