Explicit Constructions of Finite Groups as Monodromy Groups
Ra-Zakee Muhammad, Javier Santiago and Eyob Tsegaye

Abstract
. In 1963, Greenberg proved that every finite group appears as the monodromy group of some morphism of Riemann surfaces. In this paper, we give two constructive proofs of Greenberg’s result. First, we utilize free groups, which given with the universal property and their construction as discrete subgroups of PSL 2 ⁡ ( ℝ ) \operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}) , yield a very natural realization of finite groups as monodromy groups. We also give a proof of Greenberg’s result based on triangle groups Δ ⁡ ( m , n , k ) \Delta(m,n,k) . Given any finite group G G , we make use of subgroups of Δ ⁡ ( m , n , k ) \Delta(m,n,k) in order to explicitly find a morphism π \pi such that G ≃ Mon ⁡ ( π ) G\simeq\operatorname{Mon}(\pi) .



1.
Introduction

Greenberg’s celebrated result
[
8
]
from 1963 shows than any finite group can be realized as the monodromy group of some morphism of Riemann surfaces. Over
40
years later, in
[
13
]
, Wolfart provided a method to make Greenberg’s ideas explicit for finite cyclic groups. Specifically, he utilizes surjections from triangle groups in order to construct the cyclic group as a monodromy group. Inspired by Wolfart, in this paper we seek to make Greenberg’s result explicit for all finite groups. That is, given any finite group
G
, our goal is to find an explicit morphism
\pi
with monodromy group
\operatorname{Mon}(\pi)
such that
G\simeq\operatorname{Mon}(\pi)
. On that account, we will be presenting two constructive proofs of Greenberg’s classical result.

We first present a natural construction arising from the theory of free groups, where we make use of the appearance of all groups
G
as quotient groups of free groups
[
5
]
, as well as the identification of the free group of rank
2
with the congruence subgroup
\Gamma(2)

[
7
]
.
Then we give a proof of our main result, which is a construction which utilizes surjections from appropriate subgroups of triangle groups. The formal construction is as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
S_{n}
be the symmetric group of degree
n
and let
\varphi\colon\Delta(2,n,n-1)\to S_{n}
be the homomorphism given by

\gamma_{0}\mapsto(1\;2),\quad\gamma_{1}\mapsto(1\;2\;\cdots\;n),\quad\gamma_{\infty}\mapsto(n\;\cdots\;3\;2).

Then,

(i)

\varphi
is a surjective group homomorphism. Moreover, if
n<5
, then
\varphi
is an isomorphism.

(ii)

If
G
is a subgroup of
S_{n}
, then
\Gamma=\varphi^{-1}(G)
and
\Gamma^{\prime}=\operatorname{ker}(\varphi)
are both Fuchsian groups.

(iii)

G\simeq\operatorname{Mon}(\pi)
, where
\pi\colon\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}\to\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
is the canonical projection and
G
,
\Gamma
, and
\Gamma^{\prime}
are as stated in (ii).

Note that by Cayley’s theorem, we may identify any finite group
G
as a subgroup of
S_{n}
for appropriate
n
. The organization of the paper is the following. In
Section

2
, we define the concepts of Fuchsian groups and triangle groups, in addition to giving a description of monodromy in simple algebraic terms. Moreover, we also go into detail on the explicit constructions of free groups
F_{r}
as subgroups of
\Gamma(2)
. In
Section

3
, we then give both constructive proofs of Greenberg’s result.

2.
Preliminaries

Recall
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, as a topological space, can be naturally identified with a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Consequently,
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is endowed with a quotient topology. In this paper, we will be interested in subgroups
\Gamma\leq\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that the topology induced in
\Gamma
is discrete.

Definition 2.1
.

[
7
]

A
Fuchsian group
is a subgroup
\Gamma\leq\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
there is a neighbourhood
V
of
\gamma
in
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
V\cap\Gamma=\{\gamma\}
.

By definition, it is clear that if
\Gamma
is a Fuchsian group and
\Gamma^{\prime}\leq\Gamma
, then
\Gamma^{\prime}
is also a Fuchsian group. This will be relevant when working with subgroups of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, since
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is a discrete subgroup of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. For more details regarding Fuchsian groups, see
[
10
,
9
,
6
]
.

As mentioned before, the goal of this paper is to explicitly realize any finite group
G
as the monodromy group of some branched cover. Now, on one hand, given a branched cover of Riemann surfaces, we may realize the monodromy group by lifting paths around the branch points. However, we may also take an algebraic point of view. In
[
7
]
, we see that monodromy groups can also be explicitly constructed from Fuchsian groups. Namely, given two Fuchsian groups
\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma
with
\Gamma^{\prime}\leq\Gamma
, we have
\displaystyle{\frac{\Gamma}{\bigcap_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\gamma^{-1}\Gamma^{\prime}\gamma}\simeq\operatorname{Mon}(\pi)}
, where
\pi\colon\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}\to\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
. Furthermore,
\displaystyle{\bigcap_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\gamma^{-1}\Gamma^{\prime}\gamma}
is the largest normal subgroup of
\Gamma
that is contained in
\Gamma^{\prime}
(also called the normal core). Therefore, if
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
, we have the following simple description of
\operatorname{Mon}(\pi)
.

Corollary 2.2
.

[
7
, Corollary 2.59]
Let
\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma
be Fuchsian groups. If
\Gamma^{\prime}\trianglelefteq\Gamma
, then
\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}\simeq\operatorname{Mon}(\pi)
, where
\pi\colon\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}\to\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
.

Making Greenberg’s result explicit is then a purely algebraic question: Given any finite group
G
, can we find Fuchsian groups
\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma
with
\Gamma^{\prime}\trianglelefteq\Gamma
such that
G\simeq\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}
?
Theorem

3.1
and
Theorem

1.1
provide two separate constructions that answer this question.

In
Theorem

3.1
, we take
\Gamma
to be a free group, whose rank depends on the number of generators of the finite group
G
. This construction is based on both the fact that
\Gamma(2)
is a free group generated by
A=\pm\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},B=\pm\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
2&1\end{bmatrix}\in\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

[
2
]
, as well as the classical result that the free group of rank two contains freely generated subgroups of rank
r
for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
. The following lemma gives an explicit construction of free groups of rank
r
as subgroups of
\Gamma(2)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
4
, Proposition 7.80]
Let
A=\pm\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
and
B=\pm\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
2&1\end{bmatrix}
and let
F_{r}=\langle\;B^{-j}AB^{j}\;|\;j=0,1,\dots,r-1\;\rangle
. Then
F_{r}
is a free subgroup of rank
r
in
\Gamma(2)
.

For
Theorem

1.1
we choose
\Gamma
to be a partiular subgroup of a triangle group.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
m,n,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then a group with presentation

\Delta(m,n,k)=\langle\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{\infty}\;|\;\gamma_{0}^{m}=\gamma_{1}^{n}=\gamma_{\infty}^{k}=\gamma_{0}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{\infty}=1\rangle

is called a
triangle group
.

An important result regarding triangle groups is the following:

Theorem 2.5
.

[
1
]

Let
m,n,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the triangle group
\Delta(m,n,k)
is a Fuchsian group.

Remark 2.6
.

[
7
]

Notice
\Gamma(2)
can be identified with the triangle group
\Delta(\infty,\infty,\infty)
.

In both
Theorem

3.1
and
Theorem

1.1
, we will construct a surjective homomorphism
\varphi
from
\Gamma
onto the finite group
G
, and
\operatorname{ker}(\varphi)
will be the natural choice for the normal subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

3.
Constructions

In this section, we will be using
Corollary

2.2
to explicitly realize finite groups
G
as monodromy groups. As discussed in
Section

2
, given a finite group
G
, our approach is to find a Fuchsian group
\Gamma
and a surjective homomorphism
\varphi\colon\Gamma\to G
such that
G\simeq\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}
, where
\Gamma^{\prime}=\operatorname{ker}(\varphi)
. The kernel of such a homomorphism
\varphi
is then a normal Fuchsian subgroup of
\Gamma
. As a result, we can then use
Corollary

2.2
to conclude
G\simeq\operatorname{Mon}(\pi)
, where
\pi\colon\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}\to\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
is the canonical map. Two separate constructions will be provided, one with free groups and one with subgroups of triangle groups.

3.1.
Free group construction

From group theory, we have that any group
G
is isomorphic to a quotient group of some free groups. Here, we will be using the structure of groups as quotients of free groups in order to explicitly express finite groups as monodromy groups.

Consider both
\Gamma(2)=\langle A,B\rangle
and
F_{r}
as constructed in
Lemma

2.3
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
be an
r
-generated finite group,
\Gamma=F_{r}
and
\varphi
a surjective group homomorphism
\varphi\colon\Gamma\to G
with
\Gamma^{\prime}=\ker\varphi
. Then,

(i)

\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
are both Fuchsian groups. Moreover,
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a free group of rank
1+\lvert G\rvert(r-1)
.

(ii)

G\simeq\operatorname{Mon}(\pi)
, where
\pi\colon\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}\to\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
is the canonical projection.

Proof.

(i)
By the construction in
Lemma

2.3
, we see that
\Gamma\leq\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, and thus, it is a Fuchsian group. Since
\Gamma^{\prime}=\operatorname{ker}(\varphi)
is a subgroup of the Fuchsian group
\Gamma
, it then follows that
\Gamma^{\prime}
is also a Fuchsian group. Notice further, by the Schreier index formula
[
11
]
, the rank of
\Gamma^{\prime}
is
1+\lvert G\rvert(r-1)
.

(ii)
Since
\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma
are Fuchsian groups, we then have the canonical map between Riemann surfaces
\pi\colon\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}\to\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
. Consequently, given that
\varphi
is surjective, we have
G\simeq\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}
, and from
Corollary

2.2
we can conclude
G\simeq\operatorname{Mon}(\pi)
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Given the group presentation for
G
, then
\operatorname{ker}(\varphi)
can be seen as smallest normal subgroup containing the relations in
G
.

Recall that the free group
\Gamma(2)
can be identified with the triangle group
\Delta(\infty,\infty,\infty)
. Thus, in
Theorem

3.1
, we can view
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}
as subgroups of
\Delta(\infty,\infty,\infty)
. We now give another proof of Greenberg’s result, this time using subgroups of the triangle group
\Delta(m,n,k)
with
m,n,k
finite.

3.2.
Triangle group construction

The specific triangle group we will be working with is
\Delta(2,n,n-1)
. As stated in
Theorem

1.1
, let

\varphi\colon\Delta(2,n,n-1)\to S_{n}

be the homomorphism given by

\gamma_{0}\mapsto(1\;2),\quad\gamma_{1}\mapsto(1\;2\;\cdots\;n),\quad\gamma_{\infty}\mapsto(n\;\cdots\;3\;2).

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

(i)
Since
S_{n}
is generated by the transposition
(1\textbf{ }2)
and the
n
-cycle
(1\textbf{ }2\textbf{ }\cdots\text{ }n)
and these are mapped to by
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
respectively,
\varphi
is a surjective map. Now, notice if
n\geq 5
, then
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n-1}<1
. In that case,
\Delta(2,n,n-1)
corresponds to a tiling of the hyperbolic plane. And so, it follows that
\Delta(2,n,n-1)
is an infinite group
[
12
]
. For the cases with
n<5
we go one by one. If
n=2
, then
\Delta(2,2,1)
is the cyclic group
C_{2}\simeq S_{2}
. If
n=3
, then
\Delta(2,3,2)
is the dihedral group
D_{3}\simeq S_{3}
. And finally, if
n=4
, then
\Delta(2,4,3)
is the octahedral group, which is isomorphic to
S_{4}

[
3
]
.

(ii)
Since
\Gamma=\varphi^{-1}(G)
and
\Gamma^{\prime}=\operatorname{ker}(\varphi)
are subgroups of
\Delta(2,n,n-1)
, by
Theorem

2.5
both
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}
are Fuchsian groups.

(iii)
Consider the restriction

\varphi\Big|_{\Gamma}\colon\Gamma\to G.

By the first isomorphism theorem,
G\simeq\Gamma/\operatorname{ker}\left(\varphi\Big|_{\Gamma}\right)
, and since
\Gamma^{\prime}=\ker\varphi=\operatorname{ker}\left(\varphi\Big|_{\Gamma}\right)
, we have
G\simeq\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}
. Consequently, given that both
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}
are Fuchsian groups, by
Corollary

2.2
, it then follows that

G\simeq\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}\simeq\operatorname{Mon}(\pi),

where
\pi\colon\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}\to\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

One may observe that the homomorphism
\varphi
in
Section

3.2
is really a projection map
F_{2}/N\to F_{2}/K
, where
N
and
K
are the normal closures of the relations defining
\Delta(2,n,n-1)
and
S_{n}
, respectively. Hence, the kernel of this map can be explicitly computed as
K/N
.
