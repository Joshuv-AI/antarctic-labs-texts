Unified construction of relativistic Hamiltonians
Wenjian Liu

Abstract
It is shown that four-component (4C), quasi-four-component (Q4C), and exact two-component (X2C) relativistic Hartree-Fock (HF) equations can be implemented in an unified manner, by making use of the atomic nature of the small components of molecular 4-spinors. A model density matrix approximation can first be invoked for the small-component charge/current density functions, which gives rise to a static, pre-molecular mean field (pmf) to be combined with the one-electron term. As a result, only the nonrelativistic-like two-electron term of the 4C/Q4C/X2C Fock matrix needs to be updated during the iterations. A ‘one-center small-component’ approximation can then be invoked in the evaluation of relativistic integrals. That is, all atom-centered small-component basis functions are regarded as extremely localized nearby the position of the atom to which they belong, such that they have vanishing overlaps with all small- or large-component functions centered at other nuclei. Under these approximations, the 4C, Q4C, and X2C mean-field and many-electron Hamiltonians share precisely the same structure and accuracy. Beyond these is the effective quantum electrodynamics Hamiltonian that can be constructed in the same way. Such approximations lead to errors that are orders of magnitude smaller than other sources of errors (e.g., truncation errors in the one- and many-particle bases as well as uncertainties of experimental measurements) and are hence safe to use for whatever purposes. The quaternion forms of the 4C, Q4C, and X2C equations are also presented in the most general way, based on which the corresponding Kramers-restricted open-shell (RKOHF) variants are formulated for ‘high-spin’ open-shell systems.

density represented in the basis
\{\chi^{L}_{\mu}\}

At this stage, the computational efficiency is gained only
in the matrix diagonalization step (by a factor of 8 in the case of
full diagonalization or by a factor of 4 in the case of partial diagonalization by iVI
Huang et al. 2017
;
Huang and Liu 2019
), which is very little
for a moderate basis. The real gain in efficiency
can only be achieved by further invoking a ‘model small component approximation’ (MSCA), which was proposed
Liu and Peng 2006
originally in the context
of density functional theory (DFT). Therein,
the molecular small-component density
\rho^{S}
is first approximated as the superposition of
the atomic ones (cf. Eq. (
62

\displaystyle\rho^{S}=\mathrm{Tr}[\Omega^{SS}\bar{\mathbf{P}}]\approx\tilde{\rho}^{S}=\sum_{A}\tilde{\rho}^{S}_{A}=\mathrm{Tr}[\Omega^{SS}\bar{\mathbf{P}}^{0}],\quad\Omega^{SS}_{pq}=\varphi_{p}^{S{\dagger}}\varphi_{q}^{S},\quad\bar{\mathbf{P}}^{0}=\sum_{A}^{\oplus}\mathbf{n}_{A},

(92)

such that the molecular density
\rho^{4c}
can be calculated as

\displaystyle\rho^{4c}

\displaystyle\approx\tilde{\rho}^{4c}=\rho^{L}+\tilde{\rho}^{S},\quad\rho^{L}=\mathrm{Tr}[\Omega^{LL}\bar{\mathbf{P}}],\quad\Omega^{LL}_{pq}=\varphi_{p}^{L{\dagger}}\varphi_{q}^{L}.

(93)

The matrix elements of the local Kohn-Sham (KS) potential
V_{\mathrm{eff}}[\rho^{4c}]

are then approximated as

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{p}^{L}|V_{\mathrm{eff}}[\rho^{4c}]|\varphi_{q}^{L}\rangle+\langle\varphi_{p}^{S}|V_{\mathrm{eff}}[\rho^{4c}]|\varphi_{q}^{S}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\langle\varphi_{p}^{L}|V_{\mathrm{eff}}[\tilde{\rho}^{4c}]|\varphi_{q}^{L}\rangle+\langle\varphi_{p}^{S}|V_{\mathrm{eff}}[\rho^{4c}_{\mathrm{mod}}]|\varphi_{q}^{S}\rangle,

(94)

\displaystyle\rho^{4c}_{\mathrm{mod}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A}\rho^{4c}_{A}=\mathrm{Tr}[(\Omega^{LL}+\Omega^{SS})\bar{\mathbf{P}}^{0}],

(95)

such that, under the LCA4S and MSCA, only the first iteration of the calculation is four-component but subsequent iterations are just two-component
(i.e., only
\rho^{L}
need to be updated), thereby justifying the name quasi-four-component (Q4C)
Liu and Peng 2006
.
Even the first iteration is much cheaper than a regular four-component iteration, for the sparsity associated with
\bar{\mathbf{P}}^{0}
renders
the evaluation of the two-electron matrix elements only at a fractional cost of a regular evaluation of the two-electron matrix elements.
It has been shown
Liu and Peng 2006
;
Liu 2017b
that Q4C-KS is indeed very accurate, e.g., with
errors being only a few milli-Hartrees for the energy levels of the innermost shells of E117
2

and completely negligible for molecular spectroscopic constants.
The MSCA
Peng et al. 2007
, which can also be termed ‘model density approximation’,
can trivially be generalized to a model density matrix (MDM) approximation to incorporate HF exchange.
That is, the molecular density matrix elements

IV
X2C

By definition, a two-component relativistic theory is a Schrödinger-like equation that describes only electrons relativistically.
In this sense, NESC
Dyall 1997
is not yet a genuine two-component relativistic theory, since the
eigenequation (
91
), along with the relativistic metric (
89
), determines only the large components of the M4Ss, whereas
the small components have to be constructed explicitly via relation (
90
).
That is, NESC is still within the Dirac picture,
unlike
P4C and Q4C. The real significance of NESC
lies in the initiative of matrix formulations of two-component relativistic theories.
That is, the starting point is the matrix DHF equation (
43
) instead of the operator DHF equation (
25
).
The former can be block-diagonalized exactly, so as to achieve exact decoupling of the PESs from the NESs,
ending up with an “exact two-component” (X2C)
X2C
relativistic theory for electrons.
This is different from approximate two-component (A2C) theories
Chang et al. 1986
;
van Lenthe et al. 1993
;
Hess 1986
;
Jansen and Hess 1989
that
are correct only to a finite order in relativity. The block-diagonalization of Eq. (
43
)
can be done in one step
Kutzelnigg and Liu 2005
;
Liu and Peng 2009
;
Liu and Kutzelnigg 2007
;
Iliaš and Saue 2007
, two steps
Barysz and Sadlej 2002
;
Ke et al. 2007
;
Jensen
, and multiple steps
Reiher and Wolf 2004a
;
Reiher and Wolf 2004b
;
Peng and Hirao 2009
.
It has been shown
Liu 2010
that
the three types of matrix formulations share the same decoupling condition and differ only in the renormalization.
There exist even closed mappings in between
Liu 2010
. Since
the initio free-particle transformation invoked in the two-step and multiple-step formulations
is only necessary to ensure variational stability and regularization of finite-order expansions
Hess 1986
;
Jansen and Hess 1989
but not needed
for an exact decoupling, and becomes very clumsy in the presence of magnetic interactions,
it is clear that it is the one-step formulation
Kutzelnigg and Liu 2005
;
Liu and Peng 2009
;
Liu and Kutzelnigg 2007
;
Iliaš and Saue 2007
that should be advocated.
It deserves to be mentioned that the same one-step matrix X2C equation
Kutzelnigg and Liu 2005
;
Liu and Peng 2009

can also be obtained by the (formal) Foldy-Wouthuysen (FW) transformation
Foldy and Wouthuysen 1950
of the operator DHF equation (
25
),
followed by making use of the RKB-RI to convert the operators therein
to matrix forms
Liu 2010
. That is, the matrix and operator (more precisely, operator-like) formulations of the one-step X2C are actually identical.
This applies also to the two-step
Barysz and Sadlej 2002
;
Ke et al. 2007
;
Jensen
and multiple-step
Reiher and Wolf 2004a
;
Reiher and Wolf 2004b
;
Peng and Hirao 2009

formulations: instead of the usual operator formulations followed by the use of the RKB-RI, the same matrix
equations therein can also be obtained
Li et al. 2012
by starting with the partitioned matrix DHF equation (
43
).
In essence, the use of the RKB-RI in the
unexpanded
one-step, two-step, and multiple-step operator formulations
is merely a formal step and does not introduce any error even with a finite RKB basis. This is different
from A2C approaches, where the use of the RKB-RI for their matrix representations is indeed an approximation
Liu 2020a
.

The one-step block-diagonalization of the matrix DHF equation (
43
) starts with
the formal relation (
90
) between the small- and large-component coefficients of the PESs, and
a similar relation

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{L}_{-}=\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{C}^{S}_{-}

(120)

between the small (upper)- and large (lower)-component coefficients of the NESs.
In terms of such relations, the following transformation matrix
\mathbf{U}
can be constructed
Liu 2010

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}^{LL}&\mathbf{U}^{LS}\\
\mathbf{U}^{SL}&\mathbf{U}^{SS}\end{pmatrix}=\bm{\Omega}_{D}\bm{\Omega}_{N}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{+}&\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{R}_{-}\\
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{R}_{+}&\mathbf{R}_{-}\end{pmatrix},

(121)

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{D}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\\
\mathbf{X}&\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{\Omega}_{N}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{+}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{R}_{-}\end{pmatrix},

(122)

where
\bm{\Omega}_{D}
does the decoupling, whereas
\bm{\Omega}_{N}

establishes the renormalization. Note that the matrix
\mathbf{U}

the matrix representation

V
Many-electron Relativistic Hamiltonians

Having determined the PESs (and NESs) by DHF, Q4C or X2C, a generic normal-ordered many-electron Hamiltonian
can be written down

\displaystyle H_{n}=H-\langle 0|H|0\rangle=F^{\mathrm{X}}_{pq}\{a^{p}_{q}\}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}g_{pq}^{rs}\{a^{pq}_{rs}\}_{n},\quad\mathrm{X=DHF,Q4C,X2C,eQED},

(193)

for post-HF calculations. Here,
|0\rangle
is a reference state, with respect to which the normal ordering
of the one-body (
a^{p}_{q}=a_{p}^{\dagger}a_{q}
) and two-body (
a^{pq}_{rs}=a_{p}^{\dagger}a_{q}^{\dagger}a_{s}a_{r}
) excitation operators has been made.
Like the 4C case, the two-body operator of Q4C incorporates automatically the full Coulomb-Gaunt/Breit interaction (
15
).
Since the large and small components of the spinor basis functions in Q4C share
the same expansion coefficients, the AO ERIs can be transformed
to the MO representation as a whole, instead of component-wise as in the 4C case.
When the AO ERIs refer to those of the renormalized basis functions (cf. Eq. (
145
)),
the two-body operator of X2C will also incorporate the full Coulomb-Gaunt/Breit interaction, which is equivalent to
working with the M4Ss recovered from the X2C spinors (cf. Eq. (
143
)). As such,
the index transformation of the two-body operator of X2C is as expensive as that of 4C and more expensive than that of Q4C.
The computation of X2C is simplified greatly by using only the untransformed two-body operator (i.e.,
\{g_{pq}^{rs}\}

are just the ERIs of the Coulomb interaction over the X2C spinors). However, X2C will then suffer from 2ePCEs in
electron-electron interaction, which are particularly prominent for deep-core properties
of heavy elements
Knecht et al. 2022
;
Halbert et al. 2021
. Not only so, the lack of genuine 2e-SO interaction
will fail to describe SO splittings between states differing by two electrons. The simplest remedy
Berning et al. 2000
;
Netz et al. 2021
of this failure is
to include the one-center 2e-SO AO ERIs (resulting from spin separation of
the transformed X2C two-body operator
Li et al. 2012
;
Li et al. 2014
) and transform them only to the active orbitals spanning the target states.
Beyond these no-pair relativistic Hamiltonians is the effective QED Hamiltonian
Liu and Lindgren 2013
;
Liu 2014a
, where the normal ordering is taken with respective
to the filled Dirac sea but meanwhile incorporating charge conjugation symmetry
Liu 2020a
. This
is the only correct and complete QED Hamiltonian
Liu 2023b
in the same form of Eq. (
193
). It is just
that the one-body operator further includes the effective potential describing vacuum polarization and electron self-energy
and that the orbital indices refer to both PESs and NESs. Unlike no-pair correlation,
the full QED correlation energy is independent of the ways of generating
the orbitals (like nonrelativistic full configuration interaction)
Liu 2014a
, for the
filled
NESs are also correlated therein.
Note that the frequency-dependent Breit interaction must be employed for this purpose.
For possible means of treating relativity, correlation, and QED effects simultaneously, see Ref.
15
.

The above spinor-based Hamiltonians are imperative for core properties of heavy atoms or valence properties involving
np
(
n\geq 5
) orbitals.
However, for chemical systems with moderate spin-orbit couplings, it is more appealing to invoke a two-step Hamiltonian
that treats scalar relativity and spin-orbit coupling separately. The
sf-X2C+so-DKHn variant
Li et al. 2012
;
Li et al. 2014
is arguably the best one for this purpose. The great advantage here lies in that
real-valued orbitals can be used, so as to facilitate the treatment of electron correlation, on top of which
spin-orbit couplings can further be added in one way or another. Methodologies and applications along this line
have recently been summarized
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Guo et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2023
and are hence not repeated here.

VI
Conclusions and outlook

It has been shown that the DHF, Q4C, and X2C equations can be recast into the same mean-field form by
making use of the MDM approximation for the small-component charge/current density functions.
They also share exactly the same relativistic integrals that can be simplified by using the 1CSC approximation.
As such, it is a matter of taste which variant is to be adopted for real-life applications. Nevertheless,
Q4C is manifestly most efficient due to the use of most compact bases and all-together integral transformation for subsequent
treatment of electron correlation. In particular, it has no 2ePCEs even in electron-election interaction
and is simpler than X2C for response properties. QED effects, including not only the one-body vacuum polarization and electron self-energy but also
the two-body correlation of NESs, can readily be accounted for in precision spectroscopic calculations.

The

\mathcal{T}=\begin{cases}K_{0}&\text{if one-component},\quad\mathcal{T}^{-1}=\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{T}\cr-\mathbbm{i}\sigma_{y}K_{0}&\text{if two-component},\quad\mathcal{T}^{-1}=\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}=-\mathcal{T}\cr-\mathbbm{i}[I_{2}\otimes\sigma_{y}]K_{0}&\text{if four-component},\quad\mathcal{T}^{-1}=\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}=-\mathcal{T},\end{cases}

(261)

where

represents complex conjugation. For a time-reversal symmetric (
t=1

t=-1

(

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}O_{\mu\nu}&O_{\mu\bar{\nu}}\\
O_{\bar{\mu}\nu}&O_{\bar{\mu}\bar{\nu}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}A_{\mu\nu}&B_{\mu\nu}\\
-tB^{*}_{\mu\nu}&tA^{*}_{\mu\nu}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{A}=h\mathbf{A}^{\dagger},\quad\mathbf{B}=-th\mathbf{B}^{T},

(262)

\displaystyle O_{\bar{\mu}\bar{\nu}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{T}\mu|O|\mathcal{T}\nu\rangle=\langle\mathcal{T}\mu|\mathcal{T}\mathcal{T}^{-1}O\mathcal{T}\nu\rangle=t\langle\mathcal{T}\mu|\mathcal{T}(O\nu)\rangle

\displaystyle=t\langle\mu|O|\nu\rangle^{*}=tA_{\mu\nu}^{*}=thA_{\nu\mu},

(263)

\displaystyle O_{\bar{\mu}\nu}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{T}\mu|O|\nu\rangle=\langle\mathcal{T}\mu|\mathcal{T}\mathcal{T}^{-1}O\mathcal{T}\mathcal{T}^{-1}\nu\rangle=t\langle\mathcal{T}\mu|\mathcal{T}(O\mathcal{T}^{-1}\nu)\rangle

\displaystyle=t\langle\mu|O|\mathcal{T}^{-1}\nu\rangle^{*}=-t\langle\mu|O|\bar{\nu}\rangle^{*}=-tB_{\mu\nu}^{*},

(264)

\displaystyle B_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\langle\mu|O|\bar{\nu}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{T}\mathcal{T}^{-1}\mu|\mathcal{T}\mathcal{T}^{-1}O\mathcal{T}\nu\rangle

\displaystyle=t\langle\mathcal{T}^{-1}\mu|O|\nu\rangle^{*}=-th\langle\nu|O|\bar{\mu}\rangle=-th(B^{T})_{\mu\nu}.

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}^{*}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{B}\\

(267)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}\otimes\Re\mathbf{A}+[\mathbbm{i}\sigma_{z}]\otimes\Im\mathbf{A}+[\mathbbm{i}\sigma_{y}]\otimes\Re\mathbf{B}+[\mathbbm{i}\sigma_{x}]\otimes\Im\mathbf{B},

\displaystyle=\Re\mathbf{A}+\check{i}\Im\mathbf{A}+\check{j}\Re\mathbf{B}+\check{k}\Im\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle\mbox{}^{\mathrm{Q}}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}\mbox{}^{\mathrm{Q}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n},

(296)

\displaystyle\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q=1}^{2}\mathbf{M}_{pq}e_{pq}=\sum_{p,q=1}^{2}e_{pq}\mathbf{M}_{pq}

(297)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\mbox{}^{i}\mathbf{M}e_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{3}e_{i}\mbox{}^{i}\mathbf{M},

(298)

\displaystyle\mbox{}^{\mathrm{0}}\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{M}_{11}+\mathbf{M}_{22}}{2},\quad\mbox{}^{1}\mathbf{M}=\frac{\mathbf{M}_{11}-\mathbf{M}_{22}}{2\mathbbm{i}},

\displaystyle\mbox{}^{2}\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{M}_{12}-\mathbf{M}_{21}}{2},\quad\mbox{}^{3}\mathbf{M}=\frac{\mathbf{M}_{12}+\mathbf{M}_{21}}{2\mathbbm{i}},

(299)

\displaystyle\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\mbox{}^{0}\mathbf{M}^{\dagger}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mbox{}^{i}\mathbf{M}^{\dagger}e_{i}.

(300)

Literally, for any matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}
there exists a unique biquaternion matrix
\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}

or vice versa, through the bijective map:

\displaystyle\psi(\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{}^{0}\mathbf{M}+\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{1}\mathbf{M}&\mbox{}^{2}\mathbf{M}+\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{3}\mathbf{M}\\
-\mbox{}^{2}\mathbf{M}+\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{3}\mathbf{M}&\mbox{}^{0}\mathbf{M}-\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{1}\mathbf{M}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mbox{}^{i}\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n},\quad\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}.

(301)

Note in passing that
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}
can further be separated into time-reversal symmetric (
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}
)
and antisymmetric (
h\bar{\mathbf{M}}
) components,

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{M}}+h\bar{\mathbf{M}}\Leftrightarrow\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}=\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}+h\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\bar{\mathbf{M}},

(302)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{M}}

\displaystyle=\psi(\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}})=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{}^{0}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}+\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{1}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}&\mbox{}^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}+\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{3}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\\
-\mbox{}^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}+\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{3}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}&\mbox{}^{0}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}-\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{1}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\mbox{}^{i}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}e_{i},

(303)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{M}}

\displaystyle=\psi(\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\bar{\mathbf{M}})=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{}^{0}\bar{\mathbf{M}}+\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{1}\bar{\mathbf{M}}&\mbox{}^{2}\bar{\mathbf{M}}+\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{3}\bar{\mathbf{M}}\\
-\mbox{}^{2}\bar{\mathbf{M}}+\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{3}\bar{\mathbf{M}}&\mbox{}^{0}\bar{\mathbf{M}}-\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{1}\bar{\mathbf{M}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\bar{\mathbf{M}}=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\mbox{}^{i}\bar{\mathbf{M}}e_{i},

(304)

where

\displaystyle\mbox{}^{0}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Re(\mathbf{M}_{11}+\mathbf{M}_{22}),\quad\mbox{}^{0}\bar{\mathbf{M}}=\frac{1}{2}\Im(\mathbf{M}_{11}+\mathbf{M}_{22}),

(305)

\displaystyle\mbox{}^{1}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Im(\mathbf{M}_{11}-\mathbf{M}_{22}),\quad\mbox{}^{1}\bar{\mathbf{M}}=-\frac{1}{2}\Re(\mathbf{M}_{11}-\mathbf{M}_{22}),

(306)

\displaystyle\mbox{}^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Re(\mathbf{M}_{12}-\mathbf{M}_{21}),\quad\mbox{}^{2}\bar{\mathbf{M}}=\frac{1}{2}\Im(\mathbf{M}_{12}-\mathbf{M}_{21}),

(307)

\displaystyle\mbox{}^{3}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Im(\mathbf{M}_{12}+\mathbf{M}_{21}),\quad\mbox{}^{3}\bar{\mathbf{M}}=-\frac{1}{2}\Re(\mathbf{M}_{12}+\mathbf{M}_{21}).

(308)

\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}
(
h\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\bar{\mathbf{M}}
) is usually called real (imaginary) part of
biquaternion
\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}
.
If
\mathbf{M}
is Hermitian (i.e.,
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}=\tilde{\mathbf{M}}^{\dagger}

and
\bar{\mathbf{M}}=-\bar{\mathbf{M}}^{\dagger}
),

\mbox{}^{0}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}
and
\mbox{}^{k}\bar{\mathbf{M}}
(

) would be symmetric, whereas

\mbox{}^{k}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}
(
k\in 1,2,3
) and
\mbox{}^{0}\bar{\mathbf{M}}
antisymmetric.
As an example, applying the
\mbox{}^{\mathrm{Q}}\mathbf{U}
transformation (

the time-reversal antisymmetric case of Eq. (

) leads directly to

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{O}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{O}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{z}_{p}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{z}_{p}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{z}_{p}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{z}_{p}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-\mathbbm{i}\epsilon_{p}e_{1}&0\\
0&-\mathbbm{i}\epsilon_{p}e_{1}\end{pmatrix},

(309)

with
\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{O}
given in Eq. (

Given a right eigenpair of

\displaystyle\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{X}_{p}=\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{X}_{p}\lambda_{p},\quad\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{X}_{p}=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\mbox{}^{i}\mathbf{X}_{p}e_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times 1},\quad\lambda_{p}\in\mathbb{C},

(310)

the corresponding eigenvector
\mathbf{Y}_{p}

\displaystyle\psi(\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M})\mathbf{Y}_{p}=\mathbf{Y}_{p}\lambda_{p},\quad\mathbf{Y}_{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 1},\quad\lambda_{p}\in\mathbb{C}

(311)

is simply the complex adjoint vector
\vec{\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{X}}_{p}
of
\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{X}_{p}

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{p}

\displaystyle=\vec{\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{X}}_{p}=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{}^{0}\mathbf{X}_{p}+\mathbbm{i}\mbox{}^{1}\mathbf{X}_{p}\\

\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},

which is in line with Eq. (

\displaystyle\psi(\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{X}_{p})\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\

\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}=\psi(\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{X})\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\

Conversely, given a complex eigenpair of Eq. (

(314)

which stems from the following identities,

\displaystyle\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{2n}\psi(\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M})\mathbf{E}_{2n}^{\dagger},\quad\psi(\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M})\mathbf{E}_{2n}^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}_{2n}=\mathbf{E}_{2n}^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}_{2n}\psi(\mbox{}^{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{M}),\quad\mathbf{E}_{2n}\mathbf{E}_{2n}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{I}_{n},

\displaystyle=(\mbox{}^{0}\mathbf{B}\mbox{}^{0}\mathbf{C}-\mbox{}^{i}\mathbf{B}\mbox{}^{i}\mathbf{C})+e_{i}(\mbox{}^{0}\mathbf{B}\mbox{}^{i}\mathbf{C}+\mbox{}^{i}\mathbf{B}\mbox{}^{0}\mathbf{C}+\epsilon_{ijk}\mbox{}^{j}\mathbf{B}\mbox{}^{k}\mathbf{C}),\quad i,j,k\in 1,2,3,
