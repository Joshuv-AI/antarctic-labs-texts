Iterative bounds on effective transport for advection diffusion in periodic flow fields
N. B. Murphy, D. Hallman, E. Cherkaev, J. Xin, and K. M. Golden

Abstract
Over three decades ago a Stieltjes integral representation for the effective diffusivity of a tracer in a steady fluid velocity field was developed, involving the spectral measure of a compact self-adjoint operator and the Péclet number of the flow. Rigorous bounds on the homogenized diffusivity could then be obtained from knowledge of the moments of the spectral measure. A recent extension to space-time periodic flows involves an unbounded self-adjoint operator. Though Padé approximants provide upper and lower bounds in terms of the moments, the lack of a general method for calculating them has significantly limited the utility of this approach. Here we develop an iterative method that enables an arbitrary number of moments, hence bounds, to be calculated analytically in closed form for spatially and space-time periodic flows. The known behavior of the effective diffusivity for a 2D steady cellular flow is accurately captured by high order upper and lower bounds. The bounds extend to 3D steady and time periodic flow fields away from the advection dominated regime where an open issue remains concerning the divergence of the bounds.

Introduction

The long-time large-scale behavior of transport of a passive tracer by an
incompressible fluid velocity field has been known for over a century to be
well-approximated by a diffusion process
[
58
]
via an effective
diffusivity matrix
\mathfrak{D}^{*}

[
59
]
. The effective diffusivity in general
depends on the local diffusivity of the fluid and the characteristics
of the velocity field such as its strength and geometry.
Estimating and computing
\mathfrak{D}^{*}

is an
example of
homogenization
,
an interdisciplinary area of applied mathematics with impacts throughout
science, engineering, medicine, and industry.
Problems in homogenization
[
5
]
, such as the effective
electrical or thermal conductivity of a host medium
with spheres inside,
or the effective viscosity of a fluid with spherical particles,
have a long and storied history, with scientific giants such as
Einstein and Maxwell having worked on them
in the dilute limit.

A major advance in the theory of homogenization was the
introduction of variational methods to derive
rigorous bounds on the homogenized coefficients for
multiphase composite materials, given knowledge
of the coefficients of the constituents, their
relative volume fractions, and isotropy
of the composite microstructure
[
25
]
. For two component media,
Hashin and Shtrikman
[
25
]
showed that their bounds are optimal
for electrical/thermal
conductivity, dielectric constant,
magnetic permeability and diffusivity, and are attainable
by coated sphere geometries

[
25
,
44
,
62
]
.
Other microstructures such as hierarchical laminates
can also
attain the bounds

[
44
,
62
]
.

When we consider a composite material interacting not
with a static applied field, but with a wave in the
long wavelength or quasistatic limit, however, then the parameters
describing the medium and its response are complex,
such as the complex permittivity or complex viscoelasticity.
The real bounds
[
25
]

no longer apply.
Extending rigorous bounds on homogenized
coefficients of composite media to the complex case
led to another major advance. Independently,
Bergman
[
6
]
and
Milton
[
43
]
introduced a
representation formula for effective parameters
which separated the component parameters from the
microstructural geometry, and does not rely on a variational principle.
They obtained the first bounds on
complex parameters for two phase composites,
finding complex versions of the bounds in
[
25
]
, as well as simpler bounds that only assume
knowledge of the component parameters and the relative volume fractions

[
6
,
43
,
44
,
62
]
.
Subsequently, using functional analysis and linear operator theory, Golden and
Papanicolaou
[
20
]
laid the mathematical foundations of the

analytic continuation method
. They proved that the effective parameters
can be represented as Stieltjes integrals involving the spectral measure of a
self-adjoint operator which contains all the geometrical information about the
microstructure, and established a rigorous extremal framework for the bounding
procedure. In further work they obtained the first rigorous bounds on complex
parameters for multiphase materials using techniques of several complex
variables
[
21
,
23
]
, and developed an iterative
method for incorporating more and more geometrical information into tighter and
tighter bounds in the two component case
[
23
,
42
]
.
We note that since the seminal findings in
[
25
]
,
the principal impact
of the works outlined above in applications
have been the bounds on effective properties. Prior to these rigorous
results, scientists and engineers relied primarily on
trial and error in the lab to figure out what the best possible
values for effective properties could be and what types of
composite geometries could achieve these extremal properties.

The Stieltjes framework in
[
20
]

was later adapted by Avellaneda and Majda to the effective diffusivity of a passive scalar in
an incompressible fluid flow

[
2
,
3
]
.
They systematically exploited analogies with the
theory of composite materials to guide their approach and results

[
3
]
, and
introduced a Stieltjes integral representation
for
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
in steady flows, via a spectral measure
\mu

of a compact self-adjoint operator

[
2
,
3
]
,
which was further developed in
[
47
]
. Recently this
result was extended to space-time periodic flows, via a spectral
measure for an unbounded self-adjoint operator
[
46
]
. The
integral representation
separates
the Péclet number from the
geometry and dynamics of the fluid flow, which is encoded into the
spectral measure through its
moments.

In
[
3
]
is
an analog of the
Hashin-Shtrikman upper bound for velocity fields with isotropic statistics,
obtained from knowledge of only the mass of the measure, which is 1.
A velocity field having structure analogous to a hierarchical laminate (or shear
structure) is argued to attain the isotropic bound. The theory of Padé
approximants for Stieltjes functions provides a nested sequence of rigorous
bounds for the diagonal components of the matrix
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
, given in terms of the
moments of the measure
[
4
]
. The bounds get tighter as
more moments are incorporated and can converge to the true value of
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
for
certain values of the Péclet number
[
4
]
. However,
while a sequence of tighter bounds on the effective diffusivity in terms of the
higher moments of the spectral measure are found in
[
3
]
,

the numerical values of these moments are assumed to be known, without
calculation
, and then the bounds are obtained in terms of these moments.
In the theory of composite materials, the usefulness of
rigorous bounds in applications depends on
being able to compute the bounds for a class of microstructures
of interest. Similarly, the impact of Stieltjes integral
representations for the effective diffusivity on the broad range
of advection diffusion problems across science and engineering
can be enhanced by the development of a systematic approach
to computing the moments of the spectral measure.
For over three decades,
the lack of a way to calculate the measure moments for general fluid
velocity fields has hindered the progress of rigorous
bounds for
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
,
which can serve as
benchmarks in important areas of application.

Here, we develop an iterative method that, in principle, enables an arbitrary
number of measure moments to be calculated analytically in
closed form

for any spatially periodic or space-time periodic fluid velocity field
represented as a finite trigonometric Fourier series. This, in turn, enables an
arbitrary number of nested bounds to be calculated for such flows.
The
framework facilitates new analytical bounds that are not available from existing
numerical methods.
This iterative method is implemented into a numerical
algorithm using the Maple and Python-SymPy symbolic math toolboxes which can be
used to calculate measure moments in closed form for such flows up to a given
order only limited by computational resources. Moreover, we extend this
numerical algorithm to MATLAB, which enables hundreds of moments to be computed
using floating point arithmetic. We incorporate the moment values into an
existing numerical algorithm
padeapprox

[
24
]
which computes Padé approximants in a
robust, stable way, and then compute several nested bounds for the diagonal
components of the matrix
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
for some model steady and space-time periodic
flows. High order bounds accurately capture the known

[
14
,
15
]
asymptotic behavior of
the effective diffusivity for a steady cell-flow as a function of Péclet
number in the advection-dominated
regime
[
46
,
47
]
. Adding a space-time periodic
term to the fluid velocity field of this steady flow results in an appreciable
enhancement of
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
, shown both in the Padé approximate bounds here and
in numerical results involving direct computation of the spectral measure

\mu

[
46
]
. In the fluid setting, a notable weakness of

\mathfrak{D}^{*}
value, see Fig. 1 of

[
3

2

3
, and Fig.

4

In sea ice dynamics,

2
Effective transport by
advection-diffusion

The density
\phi
of a cloud of passive tracer particles diffusing along
with molecular diffusivity
\varepsilon
and being advected by an incompressible
velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfies the advection-diffusion equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi(t,\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{u}(t,\boldsymbol{x})\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\phi(t,\boldsymbol{x})+\varepsilon\Delta\phi(t,\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\phi(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(1)

for
t>0
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here, the initial density
\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})

and the fluid velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}
are assumed given, and
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfies

\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\boldsymbol{u}=0
. In equation (
1
),
\varepsilon>0
is the
molecular diffusion constant,
\partial_{t}
denotes partial differentiation with
respect to time
t
, and
\Delta=\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}=\nabla^{2}
is the
Laplacian. Moreover,

\boldsymbol{\psi}\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\,T}\overline{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
, where

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\,T}
denotes transposition of the vector
\boldsymbol{\psi}
and

\overline{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
denotes component-wise complex conjugation, with

\boldsymbol{\psi}\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\boldsymbol{\psi}=|\boldsymbol{\psi}|^{2}
. Later, we will extensively use this form of
the dot product over complex fields, with built in complex conjugation. However,
we emphasize that all quantities considered in this section are

real-valued
.

In our analysis of the effective diffusivity matrix
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
, it is beneficial to
use non-dimensional parameters. We therefore assume that equation (
1
)
has been non-dimensionalized as follows. Let
\ell
and
\tau
be typical length
and time scales associated with the problem of interest. Mapping to the
non-dimensional variables
t\mapsto t/\tau
and
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{x}/\ell

finds that
\phi
satisfies the advection diffusion equation in (
1

with a non-dimensional molecular diffusivity and fluid velocity field,

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mapsto\tau\varepsilon/\ell^{\,2},\quad\boldsymbol{u}\mapsto\tau\,\boldsymbol{u}/\ell.

(2)

This non-dimensionalization demonstrates that the
fluid velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}
is divided by a quantity with dimensions
of velocity and the molecular
diffusivity is divided by a quantity with dimensions of velocity
multiplied by spatial length. A detailed discussion of
various non-dimensionalizations involving the Strouhal number, the
Péclet number, and the periodic Péclet number is given
in
[
39
,
37
]

convenient to choose the rescaled

\boldsymbol{u}
and
\varepsilon
in a way that captures information about the
fluid velocity field. However, it is also convenient to choose
these rescaled variables in a way that
separates
the rescaled

\varepsilon
from the
geometry and dynamics
of
\boldsymbol{u}
; this leads to
mathematically and physically meaningful
properties of rigorous bounds for
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
which follow from the
analytic structure of Stieltjes integral representations for

\mathfrak{D}^{*}

[
3
,
4
]
— discussed in
Section
2.2

We accomplish both of these goals as follows. Define the dimensional fluid
velocity field by
\boldsymbol{u}=u_{0}\boldsymbol{v}
, where the parameter
u_{0}
has dimensions of
velocity and represents the “
flow strength
” of
\boldsymbol{u}
which is
independent of the geometry and dynamics of
\boldsymbol{u}
which, in turn, is
encapsulated in the non-dimensional vector field
\boldsymbol{v}
. With these
definitions, we choose reference scales
\tau
and
\ell
in
equation (
2
) to satisfy
u_{0}=\ell/\tau
so that

\boldsymbol{u}\mapsto\boldsymbol{v}
and
\varepsilon\mapsto\varepsilon/u_{0}\,\ell\,
. For
example, for
BC
-flow
[
9
]
, we define the dimensional fluid
velocity field by
\boldsymbol{u}=u_{0}\,(C\cos{y},B\cos{x})
, where the flow strength

u_{0}\in(0,\infty)
is chosen to be independent of the non-dimensional parameters

B,C\in[0,1]
which determine the streamline geometry of
\boldsymbol{u}
in

\boldsymbol{v}=(C\cos{y},B\cos{x})

An example of a non-dimensional parameter that compares the rate of scalar
advection to the rate of diffusion is the Péclet number.
We define it by the ratio
\mbox{{Pe}}=\ell u_{0}/\varepsilon
, although other
definitions have been
used

,
37
,
37
]
.
Therefore, our choice of the rescaled
\varepsilon
satisfies

\mbox{{Pe}}=1/\varepsilon
. The advection and diffusion dominated regimes are
characterized by
\mbox{{Pe}}\gg 1
and
\mbox{{Pe}}\ll 1
, respectively.

The

between

and the geometry of the flow is
important for rigorous upper and lower Padé approximant bounds for

\mathfrak{D}^{*}

[

]
discussed in Section

Padé approximants of

are given in terms of ratios of

[
4
]

P(z)/Q(z)
, where

,

0<z<\infty
, and the coefficients of these polynomials depend on the moments of
a
spectral measure
that, in turn, depend on the fluid velocity field

\boldsymbol{u}

[
3
]
. For example, when
\boldsymbol{u}
is given by

BC
-flow the moments of the measure depend on the parameters
B
and
C
. Our
numerical investigations have shown if the non-dimensionalization of

1
) is chosen in a way that the variable
z
also depends on
the flow geometry through the ratio
B/C
, then this gives rise to

positive real
roots for the polynomials
P(z)
and
Q(z)
. This, in turn,
gives rise to positive real roots and poles in the (rigorous) Padé
approximant bounds for
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
, which is not physically or mathematically
consistent with the known behavior of

\mathfrak{D}^{*}

[
14
,
50
,
9
,
37
]
.
This demonstrates the importance of
parameter separation
between
z
and
the flow geometry for Padé approximant bounds for
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
.

This way of non-dimensionalizing equation (
1
) is also
convenient in the case of a time-dependent fluid velocity
field
[
46
]
, where the parameter
u_{0}
again
represents the flow strength and the vector field
\boldsymbol{v}
encapsulates
the
geometric and dynamical
properties of the flow. For example, the
space-time periodic flow with velocity field

\boldsymbol{u}=u_{0}(\,(C\cos{y},B\cos{x})+\theta\cos{t}\,(\sin{y},\sin{x})\,)

has dynamical behavior exhibiting Lagrangian
chaos
[
9
,
46
]
. Here, the flow

is independent of the parameters

which determine the geometric and dynamical

. This choice of non-dimensionalization gives a

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{\phi}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}[\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\bar{\phi}(t,\boldsymbol{x})],\quad\bar{\phi}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(3)

Equation (

) follows from the assumption that the initial tracer

\phi_{0}
varies slowly relative to the variations of the fluid velocity

\boldsymbol{u}

[
38
,
15
,
37
]
.
This information is incorporated into equation (

,

,
37
]

\displaystyle\phi(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\phi_{0}(\delta\boldsymbol{x}).

(4)

Anticipating that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{x}/\delta,\quad\tau=t/\delta^{2}.

The rescaled form of equation (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi^{\delta}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=\delta^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}(t/\delta^{2},\boldsymbol{x}/\delta)\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\phi^{\delta}(t,\boldsymbol{x})+\varepsilon\Delta\phi^{\delta}(t,\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\phi^{\delta}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}),

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\;\sup_{0\leq t\leq t_{0}}\,\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{\delta}(t,\boldsymbol{x})-\bar{\phi}(t,\boldsymbol{x})|=0,

2.1
Hilbert space

In this section we provide an abstract Hilbert space formulation of
the effective parameter problem for advection-diffusion that was
proposed in
[
50
]
, based
on
[
8
]
, and generalized to the setting
of a space-time periodic fluid velocity field in
[
46
]
.
To fix ideas, consider the following sets
\mathcal{T}=[0,T]
and

\mathcal{V}=\otimes_{j=1}^{d}[0,L]
which define the space-time period cell

\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{V}
. Now consider the Hilbert spaces

L^{2}(\mathcal{T})
and
L^{2}(\mathcal{V})
of Lebesgue measurable scalar functions over
the complex field
\mathbb{C}
that are also square
integrable
[
17
]
. Define the associated
Hilbert spaces
\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{T}}
,
\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{V}}
, and

\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{T}\mathcal{V}}=\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{T}}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{V}}
of periodic functions, where

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=\big\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{T})\,|\,\psi(t)=\psi(t+T)\big\},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle=\big\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{V})\,|\,\psi(\boldsymbol{x})=\psi(\boldsymbol{x}+L\boldsymbol{e}_{j}),\ j=1,\ldots,d\big\},

and the
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
are standard basis
vectors.

More specifically, denote time average over
\mathcal{T}
by

\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{T}}
, space average over
\mathcal{V}
by

\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}
, and space-time average over
\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{V}
by

\langle\cdot\rangle
. The space-time average
\langle\cdot\rangle
, induces a
sesquilinear inner-product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
given by

\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle=\langle\psi\;\overline{\varphi}\rangle
, with

\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle=\overline{\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle}
. This

\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{T}\mathcal{V}}
–inner-product, in turn, induces a norm
\|\cdot\|
given by

\|\psi\|=\langle\psi,\psi\rangle^{1/2}

[
17
]
. The set
of space-time periodic Lebesgue measurable functions
\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{T}\mathcal{V}}
satisfying

\|f\|<\infty
is a (complete) Hilbert space
[
17
]
.
Similarly, the space and time averages,
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}
and

\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{T}}
, induce sesquilinear inner-products,

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{T}}
, that
induce norms,
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{T}}
, associated with the
Hilbert spaces
\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{T}}
.

To treat temporal dependence, we define the space
\mathscr{A}_{\mathcal{T}}
of functions that
are absolutely continuous
[
56
,
52
]
on the interval
\mathcal{T}
,
having derivative
belonging to
L^{2}(\mathcal{T})
, and the space
\tilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\mathcal{T}}
of
absolutely continuous
\mathcal{T}
–periodic functions with time derivatives
belonging to
L^{2}(\mathcal{T})
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=\{\psi\in\mathscr{A}_{\mathcal{T}}\,|\,\psi(0)=\psi(T)\},

(13)

which is
not
a Hilbert space but is
instead an everywhere dense subset of the Hilbert space

\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{T}}

[
56
]
. To treat spatial dependence, we now define
the Sobolev space
\mathscr{H}^{1,2}_{\mathcal{V}}
which is itself a Hilbert
space
[
8
,
16
,
41
]
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{1,2}_{\mathcal{V}}=\big\{\psi\in\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{V}}\ |\ \|\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\psi\|_{\mathcal{V}}<\infty,\ \langle\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}=0\big\}.

(14)

The condition
\langle\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}=0
in (
14
) is required to
eliminate non-zero constant
\psi
, which satisfies

\|\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\psi\|_{\mathcal{V}}=0
. The
\mathscr{H}^{1,2}_{\mathcal{V}}
–norm
\|\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\cdot\|_{\mathcal{V}}

is induced by the
\mathscr{H}^{1,2}_{\mathcal{V}}
–inner-product:

\|\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\psi\|_{\mathcal{V}}=\langle\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\psi\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}^{1/2}
.

Finally, define the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
and its everywhere dense subset
\mathscr{F}

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{T}}\otimes\mathscr{H}^{1,2}_{\mathcal{V}},\qquad\mathscr{F}=\tilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\mathcal{T}}\otimes\mathscr{H}^{1,2}_{\mathcal{V}}.

(15)

Due to the presence of
\tilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\mathcal{T}}
in the definition of the function
space
\mathscr{F}
, it is
not
a complete Hilbert space, and is instead an
everywhere dense subset of the complete Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
. Recall that

\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes space-time average over
\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{V}
and

\boldsymbol{\psi}\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{T}\,\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
. The sesquilinear

\mathscr{H}
–inner-product is given by

\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle_{1,2}=\left\langle\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\psi\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\varphi\right\rangle
with associated norm
\|\cdot\|_{1,2}
given by
\|\psi\|_{1,2}=\langle|\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\psi|^{2}\rangle^{1/2}
. We emphasize that in the case of a
time-dependent fluid velocity field, it is necessary that
\psi\in\mathscr{H}
satisfy

\langle\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}=0
, as required by the definition of
\mathscr{H}^{1,2}_{\mathcal{V}}

in (
14
). Otherwise,

\|\cdot\|_{1,2}=|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{V}|^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{V}}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,|\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\cdot|^{2}
is not a norm, since a strictly positive function

\psi(t,\boldsymbol{x})=\psi(t)
on
\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{V}
satisfies
\|\psi\|_{1,2}=0
, where

|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{V}|
denotes Lebesgue measure of the set
\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{V}
. In the case
of a time-independent fluid velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})
we set

\mathscr{H}\equiv\mathscr{F}\equiv\mathscr{H}^{1,2}_{\mathcal{V}}
, and

\langle\cdot\rangle=\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}
.

3
Iterative moment method

In this section we provide an iterative method which may be used to
calculate, in principle, an arbitrary number of moments for spectral
measures associated with the effective diffusivity for spatially and
space-time periodic fluid velocity fields.
The spectral theorem shows that the mass
\mu^{0}_{jk}
and the moments

\mu^{n}_{jk}
,
n=1,2,3,\ldots
, of the spectral measure
\mu_{jk}
are given by

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{jk}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\mu_{jk}(\lambda)=\langle g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{1,2}\,,\qquad\mu^{n}_{jk}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\;\mathrm{d}\mu_{jk}(\lambda)=\langle M^{n}g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{1,2}\,,

where
M=-\imath A
and the operator
M^{n}
is defined through
composition. Antisymmetric properties of differentiation and symmetric
properties of the operator
(-\Delta)^{-1}
in the sesquilinear

L^{2}(\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{V})
inner-product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}

show that the mass
\mu^{0}_{jk}
of
the measure
\mu_{jk}
is given by
[
46
]

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{jk}=\langle g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{1,2}=\langle\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}(-\Delta)^{-1}u_{j}\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}(-\Delta)^{-1}u_{k}\rangle=\langle(-\Delta)^{-1}u_{j},u_{k}\rangle_{2}=\langle g_{j},u_{k}\rangle_{2}\,.

(20)

Similarly, denoting the

material derivative

D_{t}=\partial_{t}+\boldsymbol{u}\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}
for time-dependent
\boldsymbol{u}
and

D_{t}=\boldsymbol{u}\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}
for time-independent
\boldsymbol{u}
, with

A=(-\Delta)^{-1}D_{t}
, the anti-symmetry of
A
in the inner-product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{1,2}
yields
[
46
]

\displaystyle\mu^{1}_{jk}

\displaystyle=\langle Mg_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{1,2}=-\imath\,\langle D_{t}g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{2}\,,

(21)

\displaystyle\mu^{2}_{jk}

\displaystyle=\langle M^{2}g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{1,2}=\langle Ag_{j},Ag_{k}\rangle_{1,2}=\langle D_{t}g_{j},Ag_{k}\rangle_{2}\,.

Higher moments are found in a similar way, for
n=1,2,3,\cdots
,

\displaystyle\mu^{2n+1}_{jk}

\displaystyle=\langle M^{2n+1}g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{1,2}=-\imath\,\langle A^{n+1}g_{j},A^{n}g_{k}\rangle_{1,2}=-\imath\,\langle D_{t}A^{n}g_{j},A^{n}g_{k}\rangle_{2}\,,

(22)

\displaystyle\mu^{2n+2}_{jk}

\displaystyle=\langle M^{2n+2}g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{1,2}=\langle A^{n+1}g_{j},A^{n+1}g_{k}\rangle_{1,2}=\langle D_{t}A^{n}g_{j},A^{n+1}g_{k}\rangle_{2}\,.

From equation (
22
) and the asymmetry of
A
, we have

\mu^{2n+1}_{jk}=0
for all
n=0,1,2,\ldots

[
46
]
.
Moreover,
\mu^{2n}_{kj}=\mu^{2n}_{jk}
as
g_{k}
,
D_{t}g_{k}
,
A^{n}g_{k}
, and
D_{t}A^{n}g_{k}
are real-valued functions for all
n=1,2,\ldots
. In summary,

\displaystyle\mu^{2n}_{kj}=\mu^{2n}_{jk}\,,\qquad\mu^{2n+1}_{jk}=0\,.

(23)

Two key properties of the operators
(-\Delta)^{-1}
,
D_{t}
, and

A=(-\Delta)^{-1}D_{t}
are that they are
linear
and complex exponentials
are eigenfunctions of these operators. We consider spatially and space-time
periodic fluid velocity fields that can be written in terms of a finite number
of Fourier modes. For space-time periodic fluid velocity fields

\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

basis
[
17
]
for

\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=\exp[\imath(\ell t+\boldsymbol{k}\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\boldsymbol{x})]

\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

time-independent flow,
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})

functions

\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}=\boldsymbol{a}_{0,\boldsymbol{k}}=\boldsymbol{a}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\{\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}

of the operators

{\nabla}

[
46

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}=\imath\ell\,\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,,\qquad\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}=\imath\boldsymbol{k}\,\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,,\qquad(-\Delta)^{-1}\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}=|\boldsymbol{k}|^{-2}\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,.

Similarly, the basis functions
\{\phi_{\boldsymbol{k}}\}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\boldsymbol{a}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=(a^{1}_{\boldsymbol{k}},\ldots,a^{d}_{\boldsymbol{k}})

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}},\phi_{\ell^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\rangle_{2}=\langle\phi_{\ell-\ell^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\,\delta_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\,,

\langle\cdot\rangle

\langle\phi_{\boldsymbol{k}},\phi_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\rangle_{2}=\langle\phi_{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\,.
For

Denoting

\displaystyle\mu^{2n+1}_{jk}=-\imath\,\langle D_{t}g_{j}^{[n]},g^{[n]}_{k}\rangle_{2}\,,\qquad\mu^{2n+2}_{jk}=\langle D_{t}g_{j}^{[n]},g^{[n+1]}_{k}\rangle_{2}\,,

By linearity, it suffices to

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{-1}\phi_{\ell^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}

(28)

\displaystyle D_{t}\phi_{\ell^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(\partial_{t}+\boldsymbol{u}\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$})\phi_{\ell^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\imath\ell^{\prime}\,\phi_{\ell^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}+[\boldsymbol{u}\,\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\,\imath\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}]\,\phi_{\ell^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\imath\ell^{\prime}\,\phi_{\ell^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}-\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\imath[\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\,]\,\phi_{\ell^{\prime}+\ell,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=\imath\ell^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}|^{-2}\,\phi_{\ell^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}-\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\imath[\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\,]\,|\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{k}|^{-2}\,\phi_{\ell^{\prime}+\ell,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{k}}\,.

It follows from equations (

and (

that

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{jk}=\langle(-\Delta)^{-1}u_{j},u_{k}\rangle_{2}=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{-2}\,a^{\,j}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,\bar{a}^{\,k}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,,

To make this explicit, we write

\displaystyle u_{j}=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}a^{\,j}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,,

\displaystyle g_{j}=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}b^{\,j}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,,

\displaystyle D_{t}g_{j}=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\,j}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,,

\displaystyle Ag_{j}=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}d^{\,j}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,.

Therefore,
equations (

),
(

),

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{jk}=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}b^{\,j}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,\bar{a}^{\,k}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,,\qquad\mu^{1}_{jk}=-\imath\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\,j}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,\bar{b}^{\,k}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,,\qquad\mu^{2}_{jk}=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\,j}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,\bar{d}^{\,k}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,.

In a similar way, for each

\displaystyle D_{t}g_{j}^{[n]}=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\,j\,,\,n}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,,\qquad g_{j}^{[n]}=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}d^{\,j\,,\,n}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,\phi_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}\,,

4
Bounds for effective diffusivity

In this section we discuss Padé approximant bounds for Stieltjes functions
with a positive measure, and how the bounds are determined by the moments of the
measure. Padé approximants
\{[N-1/N]\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
and

\{[N/N]\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
form rigorous lower and upper bounds for the Stieltjes
function
f(z)

\displaystyle[N-1/N](z)\leq f(z)\leq[N/N](z)\,,\qquad f(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu(\lambda)}{1+z\lambda^{2}}\,,

(40)

with
positive
measure
\mu
, which can converge for certain values of

z

[
4
]
. To simplify notation, for the remainder of this
section, we will focus on a single diagonal component of the (symmetric part of
the) effective diffusivity,
\mathfrak{D}^{*}=\mathfrak{D}^{*}_{kk}
, in
(
19
) and the associated Stieltjes function
f(z)
,
with
z=\varepsilon^{-2}
,

\mathfrak{D}^{*}=\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon^{-2}f(\varepsilon^{-2}))
, and positive
spectral measure
\mu=\mu_{kk}
with moments
\mu^{n}
.
Since all of the terms of the expression in
equation (
19
) for
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
are positive, we have the
following rigorous lower and upper bounds for

\mathfrak{D}^{*}_{kk}

[
4
]

\displaystyle\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon^{-2}[N-1/N](\varepsilon^{-2}))\leq\mathfrak{D}^{*}(\varepsilon)\leq\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon^{-2}[N/N](\varepsilon^{-2}))\,.

(41)

The theory of Padé approximants for
f(z)
follows by expanding

1/(1+z\lambda^{2})
in a geometric series and writing
f(z)
as a

series of Stieltjes

[
4
]

\displaystyle f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\,z^{n}\,,\qquad c_{n}=(-1)^{n}\,\mu^{2n},\qquad\mu^{2n}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lambda^{2n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\lambda)\,.

(42)

The
L
,
N
Padé approximant
of
f(z)
is given by

\displaystyle[L/N](z)=\frac{P^{[L/N]}(z)}{Q^{[L/N]}(z)}\,,

(43)

where

P^{[L/N]}(z)
is a polynomial of degree of at most
L
and

Q^{[L/N]}(z)
is a polynomial of degree of at most
N
. The

formal
power series for
f(z)
in
equation (
42
) and the condition

f(z)-P^{[L/N]}(z)/Q^{[L/N]}(z)=O(z^{L+N+1})

determines the coefficients of
P^{[L/N]}(z)
and
Q^{[L/N]}(z)
and these
polynomials can be written in terms of determinants involving the
c_{n}

[
4
]
, which are given in terms
of the measure moments
\mu^{2n}
.

The iterative moment method described in Section
3

enables many moments, hence Padé approximant bounds to be calculated for

\mathfrak{D}^{*}
. In Appendix
B
we describe our
numerical implementation of
the iterative moment method. Moments for the fluid velocity fields in equations
(
34
)–(
39
) were calculated exactly
in closed form using the symbolic mathematics software Maple and Python-SymPy,
and to floating-point precision using MATLAB and Python-NumPy, with relative
differences between the two implementations less than
10^{-14}
for all moments
computed. In Section
C
we utilize the
moments calculated in Appendix
B
to
calculate Padé approximant bounds for Stieltjes functions associated with

\mathfrak{D}^{*}_{kk}
. The resultant software repository, Janus, is publicly available on
GitHub. We discuss these results.

4.1
Bound accuracy for steady and dynamic flows

The Padé bounds presented in Sections

computed from spectral measure moments

2n=0,\ldots,60

scaling procedure that reduces the dynamic range of the moment sequence. Full
details of the moment computation, complexity analysis, and Padé
stabilization procedures are given in Appendix

5
Conclusions

The effective diffusivity
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
in spatially and space-time
periodic fluid flows has a Stieltjes function representation involving a
spectral measure
\mu
of a self-adjoint operator and the Péclet number

Pe
of the flow.
A non-dimensionalization
separates
the geometry and dynamics of the
fluid flow (encoded in
\mu
) from the strength of the flow in
Pe

(reciprocal of the molecular diffusivity
\varepsilon
).
Padé approximants for
Stieltjes functions provide rigorous nested upper and lower bounds for
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
.
With more
\mu
-moments incorporated, the bounds get tighter and
converge to

\mathfrak{D}^{*}
at

\mbox{{Pe}}\ll 1
.
Theoretical and numerical barriers of
Padé approximants
limit the accuracy of bounds for

\mathfrak{D}^{*}
in the advection dominated regime (
\mbox{{Pe}}\gg 1
), an open issue since

[
3
]
.

An iterative method was introduced to compute any number of

\mu
-moments.
Our method was implemented in Maple and Python-SymPy to calculate
dozens of moments of steady and space-time periodic flows in closed form.
The method was also implemented in MATLAB and Python-NumPy to compute hundreds
of moments to floating point precision, in excellent
agreement with the exact values.
For 2D steady (BC) flow,
the

\mathfrak{D}^{*}\sim\varepsilon^{1/2}
(
\varepsilon\ll 1
) behavior
was
captured by
our bounds
down to
\varepsilon\approx 0.053
. Bound differences
indicate that
\mathfrak{D}^{*}
was
computed within
0.01
for
\varepsilon\gtrapprox 0.053
, with similar type of
findings for 3D steady (ABC/Kolmogorov) flows and their space-time periodic
extensions. The iterative moment method developed here applies to spatially
periodic and space-time periodic velocity fields admitting finite Fourier series
representations; extension to random velocity fields, where statistical
symmetries may allow analogous iterative strategies for computing spectral
measure moments, is left for future research.

A.1
Moments of shear flow

We now consider the special case of shear flow. The fluid velocity field for
shear flow is time-independent. In 2D shear flow in the
x
-direction has a
fluid velocity
\boldsymbol{u}=(0,\zeta(x))
and
\boldsymbol{u}=(\xi(y),0)
for flow in the

y
-direction, where in the current context
\xi
and
\zeta
are arbitrary
mean-zero functions which are expressible by finite Fourier series, as shown in
equation in (
25
). Examples of 3D shear flow fluid velocity
fields are
\boldsymbol{u}=(\xi(y,z),0,0)
,
\boldsymbol{u}=(0,\zeta(x,z),0)
, and

\boldsymbol{u}=(0,0,\gamma(x,y))
, where
\gamma
is also an arbitrary function of the
same type as
\xi
and
\zeta
.

The key property of these simple shear flows is that only one component of the
fluid velocity field is non-zero, the
i
th component say, and that component is
a function which is independent of
x_{i}
, the
i
th component of
\boldsymbol{x}
. This
property and equations (
24
)
and (
30
) imply
\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}g_{j}=0
for all

j=1,\ldots,d
, hence
D_{t}g_{j}=\boldsymbol{u}\mbox{\boldmath${\cdot}$}\mbox{\boldmath${\nabla}$}g_{j}=0
for all
j=1,\ldots,d

which, in turn, implies that
Ag_{j}=(-\Delta)^{-1}D_{t}g_{j}=0
for all

j=1,\ldots,d
which, in turn,
implies that
A^{n}g_{j}=0
and
D_{t}A^{n}g_{j}=0
for all
j=1,\ldots,d
and

n=1,2,\ldots\,
. Therefore, by
equations (
20
)–(
22
) we have

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{jk}=\langle g_{j},u_{k}\rangle_{2}\,,\qquad\mu_{jk}^{n}=0\,,\quad j,k=1,\ldots,d\,,\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\;.

(44)

Let’s first focus on the spectral measure
\mu_{kk}
for some
k=1,\ldots,d
,
which is a
positive
measure. It’s clear that the only positive Stieltjes
measure with all moments having value zero is a
\delta
-measure concentrated
at
\lambda=0
,
\delta_{0}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)
, where
\delta_{a}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)
is the

\delta
-measure concentrated at
\lambda=a
. We therefore have the following
result regarding the positive measure
\mu_{kk}
for simple shear flow,

\displaystyle\mu_{kk}=\mu_{kk}^{0}\,\delta_{0}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)\,.

(45)

We now show the signed measures
\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}
and
\mbox{Im}\,\mu_{jk}
,

j\neq k
, also satisfy (
45
) in a weak sense. By the
Jordan decomposition theorem
[
17
]
there exist
unique
positive measures
\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{+}
and
\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{-}
such that

\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}=\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{+}-\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{-}
and

\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{+}\perp\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{-}
, and similarly for the signed
measure
\mbox{Im}\,\mu_{jk}
.
This and (
44
) imply the moments of these measures
satisfy

\displaystyle[\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{+}]^{n}=[\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{-}]^{n}\,,\quad[\mbox{Im}\,\mu_{jk}^{+}]^{n}=[\mbox{Im}\,\mu_{jk}^{-}]^{n}\,,\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots\,,

(46)

where
[\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{+}]^{n}
and
[\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{-}]^{n}
are the
n
th moments
of the measures
\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{+}
and
\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{-}
, for example.
Consequently, (
44
) implies for all polynomial

P(\lambda)
satisfying
P(0)=0
that

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}P(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}(\lambda)=0\,,\qquad j\neq k\,.

(47)

Since
\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{+}\perp\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{-}
, it is clear that this can
only be the case if
\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}=\mbox{Re}\,\mu_{jk}^{0}\,\delta_{0}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)
.
A similar argument establishes that

\mbox{Im}\,\mu_{jk}=\mbox{Im}\,\mu_{jk}^{0}\,\delta_{0}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)
. Hence, we have that

\displaystyle\mu_{jk}=\mu_{jk}^{0}\,\delta_{0}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)\,,\quad j\neq k\,.

(48)

This argument can be strengthened for the setting of a time-independent
fluid velocity field,
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})
. In this case, the self-adjoint
operator
M
has bounded spectrum

\Sigma\subseteq[-\|M\|,\|M\|]

[
51
,
56
]
.
The Stone
Weierstrass theorem
[
17
]
then extends the
result in (
47
) to functions
\xi(\lambda)

continuous on the closed interval
[-\|M\|,\|M\|]
satisfying

\xi(0)=0
, thus (
48
) holds in this strengthened
sense.

Appendix B
Numerical implementation of moment calculations

In Section
3
we provided a theoretical foundation for
how the moments
\mu^{n}_{jk}
of the spectral measure
\mu_{jk}
can be
calculated iteratively in terms of the Fourier coefficients
\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell,\boldsymbol{k}}

of the fluid velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}
. To illustrate the method, in Appendix

A
we provided detailed calculations for all
the moments of shear flow, as well as the mass and first few moments for 2D
steady BC flow and a time-modulated BC flow. In this section, we discuss our
results utilizing the iterative moment method to calculate many moments for some
2D and 3D time-independent (steady) flows and time-dependent (dynamic) flows.

The moments were calculated exactly, in closed form using the symbolic
mathematics software tools Maple and Python-SymPy, and calculated to floating-point
precision using the numerical linear algebra software tools MATLAB and NumPy,
comparing the results to ensure consistency. The resultant GitHub software
repository, Janus, is publically available. The Python library SymPy has the
symbolic capabilities of Maple within an efficient programmatic environment not
provided by Maple. The library Numpy contains the numerical capabilities of
MATLAB with an efficient programmatic environment that is more flexible than
MATLAB. For this reason, Python was chosen as the primary implementation, while
the Maple and MATLAB results are used for verification of the Python results. We
refer to the NumPy implementation as
decimal
and the SymPy
implementation as
heuristic
.

Using the Python library, the moments were calculated for all six fluid velocity
fields: 2D steady BC-flow, 2D steady cats-eye flow, 3D steady ABC-flow, 3D
steady Kolmogorov flow, 2D space-time periodic BC-flow, and 3D space-time
periodic Kolmogorov flow described above. The code was programmed to either
calculate 60 moments or stop after the previous moment took longer than 5 hours
on a Macbook laptop with an Apple M3 Pro chip and 18 GB of RAM. The relative
differences between the
decimal
and
heuristic
results were
less than
10^{-14}
for all moments calculated by both methods, confirming
consistency between the two implementations. The moments calculated in closed
form using Maple were the same as those calculated using Python-SymPy and SymPy
was able to calculate dozens more than Maple. Those computed using MATLAB were
consistent with the Python results to within
10^{-14}
relative difference for
all moments calculated. The computational efficiency of the
decimal

algorithm enabled even more moments to be calculated, while monitoring roundoff
error by ensuring the odd moments, which are theoretically zero, remained
sufficiently small etc.

B.1
Moments for steady flows

In Figure
5
we display
2n=60
computed
diagonal components of the spectral measure moments,
\mu^{2n}_{kk}
,

k=1,\ldots,d
, using the
decimal
implementation for 2D steady BC-flow,
2D steady cats-eye flow, 3D steady ABC-flow, and 3D steady Kolmogorov flow.
Since the operator
M
is compact for steady flows, the moments decrease
exponentially with order, with exponential decay rate
s
shown. The moments are
plotted in semi-log scale to illustrate their exponential decay
\mu^{2n}_{kk}\sim 10^{2sn}
. The exponential decay rate
s
is shown in each panel, which is
determined by a linear fit to the logarithm of the last five moments computed.
Consistent with the flow symmetries for the unit amplitude parameters, the
moments satisfy
\mu^{2n}_{jj}=\mu^{2n}_{kk}
,
j,k=1,\ldots,d
.

The computational cost of the iterative moment method for 2D steady BC-flow is
determined by the growth of the Fourier mode count at each step. BC-flow has
velocity components
u_{1}=C\cos(y)
and
u_{2}=B\cos(x)
, each having
K_{v}=2

Fourier modes after conversion to complex exponential form. Writing
b^{j}_{n}=A^{n}g_{j}
for
A=(-\Delta)^{-1}D_{t}
and
g_{j}=(-\Delta)^{-1}u_{j}
, the iterates are
initialized with
b^{j}_{0}=g_{j}
, which has
N_{0}=2
nonzero Fourier coefficients
(both with eigenvalue
|\boldsymbol{k}|^{-2}=1
). Let
N_{n}
denote the number of nonzero
Fourier coefficients of the function
b^{j}_{n}=Ab^{j}_{n-1}
at step
n
; since

(-\Delta)^{-1}
acts diagonally on Fourier modes and does not change the mode
count,
N_{n}
is also the number of nonzero coefficients of
c_{n}^{j}=D_{t}b^{j}_{n-1}
. The Janus
heuristic
procedure, which uses exact symbolic
arithmetic, confirms the closed-form growth law

\displaystyle N_{n}=n(n+3),\qquad n\geq 1.

(71)

This quadratic growth law,
N_{n}=O(n^{2})
, arises from the separability of
BC-flow:
u_{1}
depends only on
y
and
u_{2}
only on
x
, so each
D_{t}

application shifts modes
(i,j)
by
(\pm 1,0)
or
(0,\pm 1)
, populating an
expanding diamond-shaped region in Fourier space after cancellations.

At each step
n
, three operations each cost
O(N_{n})=O(n^{2})
: (i)
the material-derivative
c_{n}^{j}=D_{t}b^{j}_{n-1}
, a Fourier-space
convolution of
N_{n-1}
modes with
K_{v}=2
velocity modes; (ii) the
pointwise inverse Laplacian
d_{n}^{j}=(-\Delta)^{-1}c_{n}^{j}
, which divides each
mode coefficient by
|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}
; and (iii) the inner products computing

\mu^{2n}_{jk}
and verifying
\mu^{2n-1}_{jk}=0
.
The total cost of step
n
is therefore
O(n^{2})
.

The
decimal
and
heuristic
procedures share this loop structure
but differ in arithmetic. To mitigate roundoff error, in the
decimal

procedure all operations use multi-precision floating-point (
evalf
with
50 significant digits by default), and Fourier modes with coefficients below

10^{-30}
are pruned after each step; because each coefficient is a
fixed-width float whose precision does not grow with
n
, every arithmetic
operation costs
O(1)
, and the
O(n^{2})
such operations at step
n
therefore
contribute
O(n^{2})
total work. In the
heuristic

procedure all arithmetic is exact rational (SymPy expressions); no mode pruning
occurs, but rational coefficient denominators grow rapidly with
n
(the
denominators of
\mu^{26}
, for example, exceed
10^{120}
), so each rational
multiply costs
O(L)
or
O(L\log L)
where
L=O(n^{2})
is the bit-length,
making the heuristic cost per step
O(n^{4}\log n)
—paying a significant
computational cost for exact symbolic arithmetic.

Both procedures execute
M
iteration steps to compute the
M+1
even moments

\mu^{0},\mu^{2},\ldots,\mu^{2M}
(the mass
\mu^{0}
is computed before the
main loop; each loop step
k=1,\ldots,M
computes
\mu^{2k}
and verifies
that
\mu^{2k-1}=0
). The total floating-point work for the
decimal

procedure is therefore
\sum_{k=1}^{M}O(k^{2})=O(M^{3})
,
the same asymptotic scaling as eigendecomposition of an
M\times M
real
symmetric matrix. The from-scratch cost to compute a single even moment

\mu^{2n}
is
O(n^{3})
; the odd moment
\mu^{2n-1}
is a byproduct of the
same step at no additional asymptotic cost.

The cases
B=0
or
C=0
are degenerate, reducing BC-flow to a unidirectional
shear flow in the
x
- or
y
-direction, respectively. When
C=0
, the velocity
simplifies to
u_{1}=0
,
u_{2}=B\cos(x)

\partial_{y}\cos(x)=0

\mu^{2n}_{jk}=0

Section
A.1
. The case
B=0
is analogous.

The complexity analysis for 2D steady cat’s eye flow with amplitude parameter

A=0
is structurally identical, using the same
steady_2D
module and
the same three-operation loop. The key difference is that each velocity
component has
K_{v}=4
Fourier modes (versus
K_{v}=2

modes. Direct computation confirms that the mode count

\displaystyle N_{n}=\begin{cases}n(n+1)&n\text{ odd},\\
n(n+2)&n\text{ even},\end{cases}\qquad n\geq 1.

(72)

These values are strictly smaller than the corresponding BC-flow counts

at every

O(n^{2})

O(n^{2})

\mu^{2n}

O(n^{3})
, and
the total cost of all
M+1

The amplitude

Section

.

independent of the specific value of

within this interval. The per-step cost
is therefore
O(n^{2})
, the from-scratch cost of
\mu^{2n}
is
O(n^{3})
, and the
total cost of all
M+1
even moments is
O(M^{3})
, the same asymptotic scaling as

For 3D steady ABC-flow the same three-operation loop is executed by the Python

Appendix C
Numerical implementation of Padé approximants

Given the large number moments
\mu^{2n}
obtainable, exactly in closed form,
from the iterative moment method discussed in Appendix

B
, it might seem that Padé
approximants for
\mathfrak{D}^{*}_{kk}
of any order could be attainable. However, even in
the absence of rounding errors on a computer, the theoretical treatment of
Padé approximants is subject to the appearance of seemingly spurious
pole-zero pairs or “Froissart doublets” in arbitrary locations that prevent
pointwise convergence
[
24
]
. Such anomalies
become common in the presence of rounding errors or other forms of noise

[
24
]
. Hence, computing the Padé
approximants directly in terms of the determinants
[
4
]

is essentially ill-posed and becomes unreliable for large polynomial degree.

Padé approximation uses information about the series of Stieltjes
f(z)
in
(
42
) at a single point
z=0
in the complex plane to gain
information about regions of the complex plane away from
z=0
— similar to
analytic continuation — and becomes more ill-posed further away from
z=0
.
This is a fundamental limitation of using Padé approximant bounds to study
the effective diffusivity in the advection dominated regime, where

\varepsilon\ll 1
, i.e.,
z\gg 1
. Despite this, we will show upper and lower
Padé bounds for
\mathfrak{D}^{*}_{kk}
approach the same value for fairly small values
of
\varepsilon
. Moreover, for the 2D steady BC-cell-flow and cat’s eye
cell-flow, Padé approximants capture the known asymptotic behavior

\mathfrak{D}^{*}_{kk}\sim\varepsilon^{1/2}
for

\varepsilon\ll 1

[
14
]
.

Such issues associated with numerical computation of Padé approximants are
addressed in
[
24
]
, where a MATLAB function

padeapprox
was released, which is freely available as part of

Chebfun

[
60
]
, which provides robust Padé
approximants via singular value decomposition (SVD)

[
24
]
. This numerical method does not enable
Padé approximants of an arbitrary order to be robustly computed, but instead
truncates the Padé approximants to an order that ensures numerical
stability, hence numerically dependable results. Incorporating additional
moments to achieve higher order Padé approximants simply results in the same
truncated Padé order, as shown in Figure
7
. This
automatic truncation is accomplished by introducing a tolerance
tol

that is used to zero out singular values less than
tol
. For most
purposes involving problems perturbed just by rounding errors,
tol

=10^{-14}
is a reasonable value
[
24
]
. This
regularization can be circumvented by setting
tol
=0, bypassing the
numerical stability enabled by a nonzero value of
tol

[
24
]
. We reimplemented the
padeapprox

function in Python, which is available in the Janus GitHub repository, and used
it to compute Padé approximants for the effective diffusivity
\mathfrak{D}^{*}_{kk}

for the various flows. Extensive unit testing ensures that the MATLAB and Python
implementations produce consistent results with very low relative differences.
Inputs to the
padeapprox
function include the desired degrees of the
numerator/denominator polynomials, in our case
N/N
and
N-1/N
, a vector of
the moments
\mu^{2n}
, and
tol
. Numerator and denominator polynomials
of actual degree
\alpha
and
\beta
computed are returned, as shown in Figure

7
.

Bounds for offdiagonal components of the effective diffusivity

In this section we provide bounds for the offdiagonal components
\mathfrak{D}^{*}_{jk}
,

j\neq k
, of the effective diffusivity tensor. Denote the Stieltjes function

f(z)
in (
40
) explicitly in terms of the measure by

f(z;\mu)
. By the linearity of Stieltjes integrals

[
54
,
56
]
, we have
f(z;\mu+\nu)=f(z;\mu)+f(z;\nu)
and the moments of the measure
\mu+\nu
are the
sums of the moments of
\mu
and
\nu
,

(\mu+\nu)^{n}=\int\lambda^{n}\mathrm{d}(\mu+\nu)(\lambda)=\mu^{n}+\nu^{n}
. Recall from Section

2.1
that the spectral measure
\mu_{jk}=\mu_{g_{j},g_{k}}
for
the self-adjoint operator
M
is defined in terms of the function of bounded
variation
\mu_{jk}(\lambda)=\langle Q(\lambda)g_{j},g_{k}\rangle_{1,2}
, where

Q(\lambda)
is the resolution of the identity operator,

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{1,2}
is the
\mathscr{H}
-inner-product, and
g_{j}
and

g_{k}
are members of the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
. The polarization identity for
spectral measures follows from the linearity of this functional in each
argument, giving

\displaystyle\mu_{g_{j},g_{k}}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\mu_{g_{j}+g_{k}}-\mu_{g_{j}-g_{k}}+i(\mu_{g_{j}+ig_{k}}-\mu_{g_{j}-ig_{k}})\right),

(73)

where we have denoted
\mu_{\phi}=\mu_{\phi,\phi}
for brevity.
It follows that

\displaystyle f(z;\mu_{g_{j},g_{k}})

\displaystyle=f_{r}^{+}(z)-f_{r}^{-}(z)+i(f_{i}^{+}(z)-f_{i}^{-}(z)),

(74)

\displaystyle f_{r}^{+}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}f(z;\mu_{g_{j}+g_{k}}),\qquad f_{r}^{-}(z)=\frac{1}{4}f(z;\mu_{g_{j}-g_{k}}),

\displaystyle f_{i}^{+}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}f(z;\mu_{g_{j}+ig_{k}}),\qquad f_{i}^{-}(z)=\frac{1}{4}f(z;\mu_{g_{j}-ig_{k}}).

The functions
f_{r}^{+}(z)
,
f_{r}^{-}(z)
,
f_{i}^{+}(z)
, and
f_{i}^{-}(z)
are all
Stieltjes functions associated with
positive
spectral measures for the
self-adjoint operator
M

\displaystyle[N-1/N]^{+}_{r}\leq f_{r}^{+}(z)\leq[N/N]^{+}_{r},\qquad[N-1/N]^{-}_{r}\leq f_{r}^{-}(z)\leq[N/N]^{-}_{r}.

(75)

It follows that

\displaystyle[N-1/N]^{+}_{r}-[N/N]^{-}_{r}\leq f(z;\text{Re}\mu_{jk})\leq[N/N]^{+}_{r}-[N-1/N]^{-}_{r},

(76)

\displaystyle[N-1/N]^{+}_{i}-[N/N]^{-}_{i}\leq f(z;\text{Im}\mu_{jk})\leq[N/N]^{+}_{i}-[N-1/N]^{-}_{i}.

From

\langle g_{j}+g_{k},g_{j}+g_{k}\rangle=\langle g_{j},g_{j}\rangle+2\text{Re}\langle g_{j},g_{k}\rangle+\langle g_{k},g_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\mu_{g_{j}+g_{k}}^{n}=\mu_{g_{j}}^{n}+2\text{Re}\mu_{g_{j},g_{k}}^{n}+\mu_{g_{k}}^{n},\qquad\mu_{g_{j}-g_{k}}^{n}=\mu_{g_{j}}^{n}-2\text{Re}\mu_{g_{j},g_{k}}^{n}+\mu_{g_{k}}^{n}.

(77)

\langle g_{j}-ig_{k},g_{j}-ig_{k}\rangle=\langle g_{j},g_{j}\rangle+2\text{Im}\langle g_{j},g_{k}\rangle+\langle g_{k},g_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\mu_{g_{j}+ig_{k}}^{n}=\mu_{g_{j}}^{n}-2\text{Im}\mu_{g_{j},g_{k}}^{n}+\mu_{g_{k}}^{n},\qquad\mu_{g_{j}-ig_{k}}^{n}=\mu_{g_{j}}^{n}+2\text{Im}\mu_{g_{j},g_{k}}^{n}+\mu_{g_{k}}^{n}.

(78)

\mu_{g_{j}-ig_{k}}

\mu_{jk}^{n}
