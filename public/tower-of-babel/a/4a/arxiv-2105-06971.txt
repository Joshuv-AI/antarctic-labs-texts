Dynamical mean-field theory of the Anderson–Hubbard model with local and non-local disorder in tensor formulation
A. Weh, Y. Zhang, A. Östlin, H. Terletska, D. Bauernfeind, K.-M. Tam, H. G. Evertz, K. Byczuk, D. Vollhardt, L. Chioncel

Abstract
To explore correlated electrons in the presence of local and non-local disorder, the Blackman–Esterling–Berk method for averaging over off-diagonal disorder is implemented into dynamical mean-field theory using tensor notation. The impurity model combining disorder and correlations is solved using the recently developed fork tensor-product state solver, which allows one to calculate the single particle spectral functions on the real-frequency axis. In the absence of off-diagonal hopping, we establish exact bounds of the spectral function of the non-interacting Bethe lattice with coordination number Z Z . In the presence of interaction, the Mott insulating paramagnetic phase of the one-band Hubbard model is computed at zero temperature in alloys with site- and off-diagonal disorder. When the Hubbard U U parameter is increased transitions from an alloy band-insulator through a correlated metal into a Mott insulating phase are found to take place.



I
Introduction

The electronic structure and transport properties of real materials are strongly influenced by the Coulomb interaction between the electrons and the presence of disorder
Lee and Ramakrishnan 1985
;
Mott 1990
;
Belitz and Kirkpatrick 1994
. In particular, both electronic correlations and randomness are driving forces behind a transition from a metallic to an insulating state due to the localization of electrons (“metal-insulator transition” (MIT)).
While the Mott–Hubbard MIT is caused by the repulsive interaction between the electrons
Mott 1949
;
Mott 1990
;
Imada et al. 1998
, the Anderson MIT is a result of coherent backscattering of non-interacting electrons from randomly distributed impurities
Anderson 1958
;
Lee and Ramakrishnan 1985
;
Evers and Mirlin 2008
;
Abrahams 2010
. The interplay between interactions and static
disorder gives rise to many unusual and often unexpected phenomena
Lee and Ramakrishnan 1985
;
Belitz and Kirkpatrick 1994
;
Abrahams et al. 2001
;
Abrahams 2010
;
Dobrosavljevic et al. 2012
;
Nandkishore and Huse 2015
;
Abanin et al. 2019
.
The simplest model of disordered interacting electrons is the Anderson–Hubbard model, obtained by supplementing a single-band Hubbard model with local and/or non-local disorder.
If the disorder acts only locally, i.e., via random local potentials (“diagonal disorder”), this model is able to describe substitutionally disordered binary alloys.
However, in general disorder also affects the amplitudes for hopping between two sites — especially when the bandwidths of the host and dopant are very different — leading to additional “off-diagonal disorder”.
In analytic calculations local disorder is easier to treat and was studied extensively
Ziman 1979
.
In particular, the coherent potential approximation (CPA)
Soven 1967
;
Taylor 1967
;
Velický et al. 1968
;
Elliott et al. 1974
;
Economou 2006
;
Gonis 1992

provides the best single-site approximation for non-interacting systems with local disorder.
For that reason the simultaneous investigation of diagonal disorder within the CPA and of interacting electrons with local (Hubbard) interaction within the dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT)
Metzner and Vollhardt 1989
;
Georges and Krauth 1992
;
Georges et al. 1996
;
Kotliar and Vollhardt 2004

fit together particularly well, since both DMFT
Metzner and Vollhardt 1989
;
Georges et al. 1996
and CPA
Vlaming and Vollhardt 1992
;
Janiš and Vollhardt 1992
become exact in the limit of infinite spatial dimensions or lattice coordination number.

The treatment of the Anderson–Hubbard model with off-diagonal disorder received somewhat less attention
Milovanović et al. 1989
;
Dobrosavljević and Kotliar 1993
;
Dobrosavljević and Kotliar 1994
;
Ulmke and Scalettar 1997
;
Denteneer et al. 2001
;
Miranda and Dobrosavljevic 2012
.
In particular, Dobrosavljević and Kotliar
Dobrosavljević and Kotliar 1993
;
Dobrosavljević and Kotliar 1994
investigated this model within DMFT by employing a functional integral representation for quantum averages and the replica method for disorder averaging.
Thereby they were able to study Hubbard models with arbitrary disorder on Bethe lattices, as well as models on an arbitrary lattice with a special distribution of the off-diagonal disorder.
In this way they studied the formation of local moments and the Mott transition in disordered systems.

In 1971 Blackman, Esterling, and Berk (BEB)
Blackman et al. 1971
;
Esterling 1975
showed that off-diagonal disorder can, in principle, be incorporated into the CPA framework, such that both diagonal and off-diagonal disorder are tractable within a single–site approximation.
In the absence of electronic interactions the BEB formalism was incorporated into the dynamical cluster approximation within the typical medium cluster theory
Terletska et al. 2014

and applied to multi-band systems
Zhang et al. 2016
.

In our paper we extend these investigations of disordered systems by including a local (“Hubbard”) interaction between the electrons.
To this end we investigate the Anderson–Hubbard model with diagonal and off-diagonal disorder within the CPA in the BEB formulation
Blackman et al. 1971
;
Esterling 1975
in a tensor formulation
Koepernik et al. 1997
;
Koepernik et al. 1998
, while correlation effects are treated within the DMFT
Georges et al. 1996
.
We compute the spectral function and discuss the occurrence of the MIT in the presence of diagonal and off-diagonal disorder.
For these purposes an accurate zero-temperature many-body solver on the real energies is used,
the so-called fork tensor-product state solver
Bauernfeind et al. 2017
.
We observe successive alloy-insulator to metal and metal to Mott-insulator transitions with increasing values of the Hubbard
U
parameter.
A similar transition scenario was previously discussed for models with diagonal disorder solved within DMFT, but using finite temperature solvers such as the Hirsch-Fye algorithm
Ulmke et al. 1995
or the perturbative non-crossing approximation
Lombardo et al. 2006
.
Contrary to the diagonal disorder model in which the CPA solution provides a common bandwidth for all alloy components, the presence of off-diagonal elements causes the formation of different effective bandwidths for alloy components.

The paper is organized as follows.
In
Section

II
the BEB theory of the multi-component Anderson–Hubbard model is formulated,
and the computational scheme is discussed.
In
Section

III
numerical results for a single-band Bethe lattice are presented.
Comparison with earlier results on DMFT+CPA allow us to identify effects specifically due to off-diagonal disorder.
Finally, conclusions and a summarizing discussion are presented in
Section

IV
.

II
Blackman–Esterling–Berk theory for the Anderson–Hubbard model

In the simplest case an alloy consists of two types of atoms,
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
, with diagonal substitutional disorder,
such that only the site-diagonal elements of the Hamiltonian vary stochastically according to the atomic species occupying the given site
Soven 1967
;
Taylor 1967
;
Velický et al. 1968
.
The physical quantities of interest, for instance the spectral function, are those averaged over the possible disorder realizations.
Therefore, the idea of the CPA is to replace the ensemble with random configurations by a periodic system with “average” atoms,
whose properties are determined self-consistently.
The CPA finds a natural description in the language of scattering theory.
Assuming the origin to be occupied by atoms of type
\mathsf{A}
or
\mathsf{B}
and all other sites by average atoms,
the scattering by the atom at the origin is easily computed.
The self-consistency condition of the CPA,
which expresses that the scattering at the origin vanishes on average,
then allows one to compute the coherent Green’s function for the average atom.

The BEB formalism
Blackman et al. 1971
;
Esterling 1975
is a generalization of the CPA such that it becomes applicable also to off-diagonal disorder.
Similar to CPA it was formulated for a tight-binding model in which the hopping matrix elements depend on the species of atoms occupying the two sites connected by the hopping.
For the above binary alloy example,
the BEB hopping matrix elements are
t_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}},t_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}
and
t_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}}
.
When the hopping matrix elements are equal the BEB formalism reduces to the CPA
Soven 1967
;
Taylor 1967
,
and for the binary alloy case the scalar CPA equation becomes a
2\times 2
matrix equation.
An in-depth analysis of the BEB method was performed in a tight-binding formalism by Gonis and Garland
Gonis and Garland 1977
using locators, propagators, and a variational technique proving the analyticity of the BEB-CPA Green’s function.
A realistic multiband formulation of the BEB-CPA was introduced more than three decades ago by Papaconstantopoulos, Gonis, and Laufer
Papaconstantopoulos et al. 1989
.
Then Koepernik
et al.

Koepernik et al. 1997
developed the BEB-CPA extension within a full potential local-orbital approach, and more recently a similar implementation was made within a pseudopotential approach
Herbig et al. 2017
.
Shvaika
Shvaika 2003
found a connection between the Falicov–Kimball model with correlated hopping and the BEB-CPA by rewriting the Hamiltonian as a
2\times 2
matrix.
For Hamiltonians with interactions the BEB-CPA was employed by Burdin and Fulde
Burdin and Fulde 2007
to study the interplay between the Kondo effect and disorder.
In an attempt to address localization in strongly disordered electronic systems,
the typical medium theory
Dobrosavljević and Kotliar 1993
was combined with the dynamical cluster approximation including effects induced by off-diagonal disorder
Terletska et al. 2014
.
However, this approach did not include electronic interactions.

In the present paper we extend the BEB formalism to
interacting
electrons such that it can be applied to
the multi-component Anderson–Hubbard model;
localization effects will not be addressed.
The model is defined in
Section

II.2
.
The corresponding DMFT equations are solved using the recently developed fork tensor-product state solver
Bauernfeind et al. 2017
.

II.1
Configurational averages and notation

In the conventional approach to systems with random variables (diagonal and/or off-diagonal) the Green’s function is first expanded and then an average over an appropriate set of terms is performed.
By contrast, the BEB method treats both diagonal- and off-diagonal randomness on equal footing by employing an extended representation, which will be discussed below.
The Green’s functions are then evaluated using conventional expansion techniques.
The formalism introduced by Koepernik
et al.

Koepernik et al. 1997
;
Koepernik et al. 1998
is particularly suitable for the BEB approach.
For this reason, we adopt the notation introduced in Refs.
Koepernik et al. 1997
;
Koepernik et al. 1998
.

We consider an alloy consisting of
M
types of atoms (“alloy components”) denoted by the index
\alpha
.
Every lattice site
i
is uniquely mapped to a particular component
\alpha
as expressed by

i\mapsto\alpha.

(1)

While this notation corresponds, in principle, to that of Ref.
Koepernik et al. 1998
,
we reverse the direction of the arrow to focus on the alloy components rather than the lattice sites.
To address multiple sites we use the notation

(i,j\mapsto\alpha,\beta)\coloneqq(i\mapsto\alpha)\land(j\mapsto\beta)
.
We refer to a specific mapping of the
N

M
components as a “configuration” (
\config
) or “disorder realization”,
and denote the set of all possible configurations by
{{\mathbb{C}}}=\{\config\}
.
As the specific configuration of a sample measured in an experiment is unknown,
we average over all possible configurations.
Since the concentrations
c^{\alpha}
of the different components
\alpha

we restrict the average to configurations with these concentrations,
denoted by
{{\mathbb{C}}}|_{\{c^{\alpha}\}}
.
In the absence of additional information we further assume that the probability of all physical configurations is the same:

P(\config)=1/\absolutevalue{\RestConfSet}\quad\forall\config\in{\mathbb{C}|_{\{c^{\alpha}\}}}.

(2)

For a random variable
X

(3)

This situation corresponds to the case of substitutional disorder.

II.2
Multi-component Anderson–Hubbard model

For a specific configuration the Anderson–Hubbard Hamiltonian reads

\hat{H}=-\sum_{ij\sigma}t_{ij}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j\sigma}+\sum_{i\sigma}(v_{i}-\mu)\hat{n}_{i\sigma}+\sum_{i}U_{i}\hat{n}_{i\uparrow}\hat{n}_{i\downarrow},

(4)

with the on-site energy
v_{i}
, the local Hubbard interaction
U_{i}
, and the amplitude
t_{ij}
for hopping between sites
i
and
j
.
The hopping parameters are Hermitian
t_{ij}=t^{*}_{ji}
and off-diagonal, with
t_{ii}=0
.
The Hamiltonian can be written in the compact matrix form

\hat{H}=\sum_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{H}_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}^{\intercal}_{\uparrow}\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_{\downarrow},

(5)

where we introduced
N\times 1
matrices to represent the operators.
The rows of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\sigma}}
are the annihilation operators
\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{i\sigma}
,
and the rows of
\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_{\sigma}
are the number operators
\hat{n}_{i\sigma}
.
The one-particle Hamiltonian matrix reads
{(\boldsymbol{H})}_{ij}=-t_{ij}+\delta_{ij}(v_{i}-\mu)
.
Here and in the following we suppress the spin-index
\sigma
unless explicitly needed, to simplify the notation.
The local interaction is written as a matrix
{(\boldsymbol{U})}_{ij}=\delta_{ij}U_{i}
.

The magnitude of the hopping parameters
t_{ij}
depends on the alloy components located on sites
i
and
j
, respectively,
which are referred to as “terminal points”.
In the following we employ the “terminal-point approximation”
Koepernik et al. 1997
;
Koepernik et al. 1998

which assumes that parameters with terminal points
i,j,k,\dots
depend only on the components located at
i,j,k,\dots

and not on the components surrounding these sites.
Thus, for a specific configuration (disorder realization)
every parameter
v_{i},U_{i},t_{ij}
takes a value depending on the component occupying the respective site or sites.
In the representation of the BEB we denote these configuration-specific values by an underline and a superscript indicating the component.
For instance, if site
i
is occupied by component
\alpha
(
i\mapsto\alpha
)
the parameter
v_{i}
takes the value
\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha}
.
For
i,j\mapsto\alpha,\beta
,
we have
v_{i}=\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha}
,
U_{i}=\underline{\mathrm{U}}^{\alpha}
, and
t_{ij}=\underline{\mathrm{t}}^{\alpha\beta}(\absolutevalue{\vb{r}_{i} - \vb{r}_{j}})
.
This will now be formalized.
Denoting the set of sites
i
occupied by the component
\alpha
by

\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}\coloneqq\{i|i\mapsto\alpha\},

(6)

the terminal point approximation can be expressed conveniently using the indicator function

1_{\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}}(i)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\mbox{\quad if\quad}i\in\mathbb{S}^{\alpha},\\
0&\mbox{\quad if\quad}i\not\in\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}.\end{cases}

(7)

The identity
\sum_{\alpha}1_{\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}}(i)=1
holds since
every site must be occupied by exactly one component.
Thus, the parameters read

\displaystyle v_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}1_{\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}}(i)\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha},

(8)

\displaystyle t_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta}1_{\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}}(i)\underline{\mathrm{t}}^{\alpha\beta}(\absolutevalue{\vb{r}_{i} - \vb{r}_{j}})1_{\mathbb{S}^{\beta}}(j),

\displaystyle H_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta}1_{\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}}(i)\underline{\mathrm{H}}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}1_{\mathbb{S}^{\beta}}(j),

\displaystyle U_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}1_{\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}}(i)\underline{\mathrm{U}}^{\alpha},

with
\underline{\mathrm{H}}_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{ij}\delta^{\alpha\beta}(\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha}-\mu)-\underline{\mathrm{t}}^{\alpha\beta}(\absolutevalue{\vb{r}_{i}- \vb{r}_{j}})
.
We further note,
that the conditional expectation value of the parameters equals the underlined component variables:

\Expval(v_{i}|i\mapsto\alpha)=\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha},\quad\Expval(t_{ij}|i,j\mapsto\alpha,\beta)=\underline{\mathrm{t}}^{\alpha\beta},\dots

(9)

The dependence on the components is therefore shifted from the parameters into the indicator function
1_{\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}}(i)
.
Depending on the component occupying a site, the indicator function selects the corresponding parameter from a finite set of choices.
We note that for elements diagonal in lattice sites
i
,
one has
1_{\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}}(i)1_{\mathbb{S}^{\beta}}(i)=1_{\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}}(i)\delta^{\alpha\beta}
, i.e.,
they are diagonal in the components.

II.2.2
Effective medium in the extended space

As in CPA,
in the BEB formalism one calculates an effective local Green’s function
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}(z)
from an effective medium
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{S}}}(z)
,
which approximates the average local Green’s function
\Expval(\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{G}}}_{ii}(z))
.
We consider only substitutional disorder without structural disorder, i.e.,
the lattice structure is assumed to be fixed.
Therefore, we decompose the hopping tensor
\underline{\mathrm{t}}^{\alpha\beta}(\absolutevalue{\vb{r}_{i} - \vb{r}_{j}})
into its component part,
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\beta}
, and its lattice part,
t(\absolutevalue{\vb{r}_{i} - \vb{r}_{j}})
:

\underline{\mathrm{t}}^{\alpha\beta}(\absolutevalue{\vb{r}_{i} - \vb{r}_{j}})\eqqcolon\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\beta}\,t(\absolutevalue{\vb{r}_{i} - \vb{r}_{j}}).

(18)

Depending on the component of the endpoints,
the matrix elements
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\beta}
scale the amplitudes for hopping on a given lattice structure by a dimensionless factor.
In the following we refer to
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\beta}
simply as “dimensionless hopping parameter”.
We perform the lattice Fourier transform for the hopping matrix elements as

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{T}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{ij}t(\absolutevalue{\vb{r}_{i} - \vb{r}_{j}})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{j})}=\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{T}}}\epsilon_{k}.

(19)

For a given effective medium
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{S}}}(z)
,
the effective local Green’s function reads

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}(z)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}{[\mathds{1}z-\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{S}}}(z)-\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{T}}}\epsilon_{k}]}^{-1}.

(20)

The effective medium as well as the effective local Green’s function are represented by
M\times M
matrices in the components.
Being local quantities they no longer carry lattice indices.
The effective medium
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{S}}}(z)
is determined by demanding that the averaged
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{t}}
-matrix vanishes:

\displaystyle\Expval(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{t}}(z))\overset{!}{=}0,

(21)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{t}}(z)\coloneqq-{[\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\chi}}}{[\mathds{1}-\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\chi}}}(\mathds{1}z-\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{S}}}(z)+\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{v}}})\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\chi}}}]}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\chi}}}+\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}(z)]}^{-1}.

(22)

II.3
Inclusion of electronic interactions and the BEB+DMFT self-consistency loop

We treat the local Hubbard interaction within the dynamical mean-field theory
Metzner and Vollhardt 1989
;
Georges et al. 1996
;
Kotliar and Vollhardt 2004
,
which assumes a local self-energy
\Sigma_{ij}(z)=\delta_{ij}\Sigma_{ii}(z)
;
this property becomes exact in the limit of infinite coordination number.
The problem of interacting disordered electrons may equally be viewed as a system of non-interacting particles moving in an effective local, energy dependent potential
\Sigma_{ii}(z)
; for details see Ref.
Janiš and Vollhardt 1992
;
Vollhardt 2010
.
The DMFT self-consistency equations
Georges et al. 1996
are equivalent to a fixed-point problem
which can be expressed by a functional
\widehat{\Sigma}
:
Given a self-energy
\Sigma_{ii}
and the resulting local Green’s function
G_{ii}(\Sigma_{ii})

this functional provides a new self-energy
\widehat{\Sigma}\big[G_{ii}(\Sigma_{ii}),\Sigma_{ii}\big]
,
such that the DMFT self-energy is determined self-consistently by the fixed-point

\Sigma_{ii}=\widehat{\Sigma}\big[G_{ii}(\Sigma_{ii}),\Sigma_{ii}\big].

(23)

Within the CPA,
the local Green’s function for a given self-energy
G_{ii}(\Sigma_{ii})
is replaced by the conditional average
\Expval(G_{ii}(\Sigma_{ii})|i\mapsto\alpha)=\underline{\mathrm{g}}_{\text{loc}}^{\alpha\alpha}(\Sigma_{ii})/c^{\alpha}
, see
Eqs.

17
and
20
.
Thus, the self-energy
\widehat{\Sigma}\big[\underline{\mathrm{g}}_{\text{loc}}^{\alpha\alpha}(\Sigma_{ii})/c^{\alpha},\Sigma_{ii}\big]
depends on the component
\alpha
.
Consequently, the self-energy at the fixed-point depends on the component
\alpha
, but not on the explicit site
i
:

\Sigma^{\alpha}=\widehat{\Sigma}\big[\underline{\mathrm{g}}_{\text{loc}}^{\alpha\alpha}(\Sigma^{\alpha})/c^{\alpha},\Sigma^{\alpha}\big].

(24)

This allows one to introduce the BEB+DMFT self-consistency
which we will discuss next.

By merging the BEB formalism with DMFT a two-fold self-consistency arises,
one for the BEB and one for the DMFT corresponding to the fixed-point
Eq.

24
.
The self-consistency equation of the BEB formalism is pointwise in the frequencies and is therefore much simpler than the self-consistency condition of the DMFT,
where frequencies mix due to the energy exchange caused by the interaction between the electrons.
We view the former self-consistence as an internal part of the full self-consistency loop.
In the BEB method we calculate an effective local Green’s function
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}(z)
,
Eq.

20
.
The effective medium
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{S}}}(z)
and, therefore, the effective local Green’s function have to be calculate self-consistently from
Eqs.

21
and
22
.
This condition simplifies to

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}^{-1}(z)=\overline{\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}}^{-1}(z)

(25)

with the diagonal matrix

{\overline{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}}^{\alpha\beta}(z)=\frac{c^{\alpha}\delta^{\alpha\beta}}{{(\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}^{-1})}^{\alpha\alpha}(z)+\underline{\mathrm{S}}^{\alpha\alpha}(z)+\mu-\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha}-\Sigma^{\alpha}(z)},

(26)

where
\Sigma^{\alpha}(z)
is the DMFT self-energy for the component
\alpha
.
The self-consistent
Eq.

25
can be solved with standard root-search algorithms
or by simple iteration.
In practice, we use an implementation of the BEB formalism without interactions and merely shift the on-site energy
\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha}\rightarrow\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha}+\Sigma^{\alpha}(z)
.
An efficient evaluation of the BEB self-consistency equation is discussed in
Appendix

B
;
an implementation is provided in Ref.
Weh and Östlin 2021
.
To emphasize the dependence on the self-energy
we denote the self-consistently determined effective local Green’s function for a given self-energy
Eq.

25
by
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}(z,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(z))
.

With the BEB self-consistency condition
Eq.

25
for the local Green’s function
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}(z,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(z))
,
the combined algorithm corresponds to the conventional DMFT self-consistency condition

Eq.

24
,
where the local Green’s function,
calculated from the lattice Hilbert transform,
is now replaced by the average

\Expval(G_{ii}|i\mapsto\alpha)=\underline{\mathrm{g}}_{\text{loc}}^{\alpha\alpha}(z,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(z))/c^{\alpha}.

The reciprocal concentration factor can be avoided by introducing a renormalized indicator tensor,
which leads to a slightly modified BEB self-consistency as elaborated in
Appendix

C
.
We have to solve a separate impurity problem for every component
\alpha
.
Starting from an initial guess for the DMFT self-energy
\Sigma^{\alpha}(z)
for every component,
the BEB+DMFT scheme is the following:

1.

Calculate the effective local Green’s function
Eq.

20
using
Eqs.

25
and
26
, which yields

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}(z,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(z)),

(27a)

2.

calculate the hybridization function

\Delta^{\alpha}(z)=z+\mu-\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha}-\Sigma^{\alpha}(z)-c^{\alpha}/\underline{\mathrm{g}}_{\text{loc}}^{\alpha\alpha}(z,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(z)),

(27b)

for every component
\alpha
,

3.

solve the impurity problem for the self-energy

\Sigma^{\alpha}(z)=\Sigma[\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha},\underline{\mathrm{U}}^{\alpha},\Delta^{\alpha}]

(27c)

for every component
\alpha
,

4.

repeat from step 1 until self-consistency is reached.

The hybridization function can also be expressed in terms of BEB quantities using the self-consistency condition
Eq.

25
:

\Delta^{\alpha}(z)=z-\underline{\mathrm{S}}^{\alpha\alpha}(z)-{(\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}^{-1})}^{\alpha\alpha}(z,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(z)).

(28)

This is different from CPA+DMFT,
where only one unique hybridization functions exists independent of the alloy components.
Analogous to the non-disordered case,
an expression for the hybridization function of the Bethe lattice in terms of the local Green’s function
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}(z,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(z))
is given in
Appendix

D
.

Central to the DMFT problem is the impurity solver which provides the local dynamic self-energy
Eq.

27c
.
To this end, we employ a tensor network based zero temperature solver,
the fork tensor-product state (FTPS) solver
Bauernfeind et al. 2017
.
The FTPS impurity solver is a Hamiltonian-based method
which discretizes the hybridization function
Eq.

27b
using a large number of bath sites.
We use
249
sites per spin resulting in a median energy distance of
0.03D
,
where the half-bandwidth
D
sets our energy scale.
We calculate the ground state
\ket{GS}
of the finite size impurity problem using the density matrix renormalization group (DMRG)
White 1992
;
Schollwöck 2005
.
Subsequently, we perform the time evolution using the time dependent variational principle (TDVP)
Haegeman et al. 2011
;
Lubich et al. 2015
;
Haegeman et al. 2016
;
Bauernfeind and Aichhorn 2020
.
To obtain the retarded time impurity Green’s function
{G}^{\text{ret}}(t)
,
the states
\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\sigma}\ket{GS}
,
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\ket{GS}
, as well as their adjoint states are time-evolved,
where
\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\sigma}
(
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}
) is the annihilation (creation) operator of the impurity site.
For DMRG we chose a truncated weight of
10^{-15}
and a maximal bond-dimension of
100
.
We perform the TDVP using time-steps of
0.1/D
up to a maximal time
t_{\text{max}}=150/D

with a truncated weight of
10^{-9}
and a maximal bond-dimension of
150
.
The convergence with respect to these parameters is checked.

We can calculate the Green’s function
{G}^{\text{ret}}(t)
only up to a maximal time,
and we have (small) finite size effects due to the discretization of the bath.
Therefore, we cannot evaluate the retarded Green’s function directly on the real-frequency axis
G(\omega+i0^{+})
.
Instead we calculate it on a parallel contour
G(\omega+i\eta)
shifted by a fixed finite
\eta>0
;
this corresponds to the Laplace transform:

G(\omega+i\eta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\e^{i(\omega+i\eta)t}{G}^{\text{ret}}(t)\eqqcolon\Fourier_{\eta}[{G}^{\text{ret}}(t)](\omega).

(29)

The shift
\eta
acts as a broadening for the Green’s function
G(z)
as can be seen from the Cauchy integral formula

2\pi iG(\omega+i\eta)=\oint\!dz\frac{G(z)}{z-\omega-i\eta}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!d\omega^{\prime}\frac{G(\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime}-\omega-i\eta^{-}},

(30)

with
\eta^{-}=\eta-0^{+}
, where
0^{+}
is a positive infinitesimal.
We write the Green’s function on the real axis in terms of the shifted Fourier transform

\displaystyle G(\omega+i0^{+})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\eta^{\prime}\searrow 0}\Fourier_{{\eta}^{\prime}}[{G}^{\text{ret}}(t)](\omega)=\Fourier_{\eta}[\e^{t\eta^{-}}{G}^{\text{ret}}(t)](\omega)

(31)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{1}{k!}{(\eta^{-})}^{k}\Fourier_{\eta}[t^{k}{G}^{\text{ret}}(t)](\omega).

The second equality replaces the limit
{\eta}^{\prime}\searrow 0
using
\lim_{{\eta}^{\prime}\searrow 0}\exp(-{\eta}^{\prime} t)=\exp(-\eta t)\exp(\eta^{-} t)

(33)

(34)

where

II.4
General properties of the BEB formalism

We shortly review some properties of the BEB formalism
Gonis and Garland 1977
;
Gonis 1992
;
Koepernik et al. 1998
.
First, the BEB formalism
is equivalent to the CPA when off-diagonal disorder is absent.
This limit was already proven in the original formulation
Blackman et al. 1971
;
Esterling 1975
.
Since the BEB theory includes the off-diagonal disorder in the single-site approximation,
the Herglotz property
Müller-Hartmann 1973
;
Mills and Ratanavararaksa 1978
of the CPA is preserved in the BEB as well
Gonis and Garland 1977
.

Second, for a non-interacting tight-binding Hamiltonian
the density of states (DOS) is non-zero only within certain energy ranges, determined by the Hamiltonian matrix elements.
This is also holds for the CPA
Velický et al. 1968
.
Koepernick
et al.

Koepernik et al. 1998
found the same
in their numerical study of one-dimensional chains using the BEB formalism.
Likewise, we find no violations of this property in our numerical results.
In the following subsection we derive exact bounds for the spectral function of a Bethe lattice with coordination number
Z
using the BEB formalism in the limit of independent alloy components, i.e., when there is no hopping between different components.

II.5
Limit of independent components

We consider the limit of vanishing hopping between different components.
If the hopping is diagonal in the components,
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\beta}\propto\delta^{\alpha\beta}
,
the BEB effective medium
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{S}}}(z)
is also diagonal in the components
and the self-consistency equations
Eq.

25
decouple.
In this case, the effective local Green’s function
Eq.

20
can be readily calculated,
since the matrix inverse is the reciprocal of the diagonal elements

\displaystyle\underline{\mathrm{g}}_{\text{loc}}^{\alpha\beta}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}\frac{\delta^{\alpha\beta}}{z-\underline{\mathrm{S}}^{\alpha\alpha}(z)-\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\alpha}\epsilon_{k}}

(35)

\displaystyle=\delta^{\alpha\beta}g_{0}^{\alpha}\big(z-\underline{\mathrm{S}}^{\alpha\alpha}(z)\big).

Here,
g_{0}^{\alpha}
is the lattice Hilbert transform
g_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}\frac{1}{z-\epsilon_{k}}
;
the superscript
\alpha
indicates that the bandwidth is scaled by
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\alpha}
:

g_{0}^{\alpha}(z)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}\frac{1}{z-\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\alpha}\epsilon_{k}}=\frac{1}{\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\alpha}}g_{0}(z/\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\alpha}).

(36)

For the component
\alpha
,
the decoupled self-consistency
Eq.

25
reads

0=\frac{\overline{c}^{\alpha}}{g_{0}^{\alpha}(z-\underline{\mathrm{S}}^{\alpha\alpha})}+\underline{\mathrm{S}}^{\alpha\alpha}-\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha},

(37)

with the concentration complement
\overline{c}^{\alpha}=1-c^{\alpha}\geq 0
.
For a Bethe lattice with coordination number
Z
and the lattice Hilbert transform
Economou 2006

\displaystyle g_{0}(z,Z)=2(Z-2)/\left[z\left(Z-2+Z\sqrt{1-D^{2}/z^{2}}\right)\right],

(38)

where
D
is the half-bandwidth,
the self-consistency condition is an algebraic equation and can be solved analytically.
The BEB effective medium reads

\underline{\mathrm{S}}^{\alpha\alpha}(z,Z)=\frac{(Z-2)\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha}c^{\alpha}+Z\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha}+(Z+2)c^{\alpha}z-Zz-2{(c^{\alpha})}^{2}z-Z\overline{c}^{\alpha}s\sqrt{{(z-\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha})}^{2}-c^{\alpha}{(D^{\alpha})}^{2}\frac{Z-c^{\alpha}}{Z-1}}}{2c^{\alpha}(Z-c^{\alpha})},

(39)

where
s
is the sign
s=\sign(\real(z-v^{\alpha}))
,
and
D^{\alpha}
is the half-bandwidth scaled by
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\alpha}
;
this is the retarded solution.
A conjugate solution exists with
-s
and therefore with a plus sign in front of the square root.

We are interested in the bandwidth of the resulting component spectrum

A^{\alpha}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{c^{\alpha}\pi}\imaginary g_{0}^{\alpha}(\omega+i0^{+}-\underline{\mathrm{S}}^{\alpha\alpha}(\omega+i0^{+})).

(40)

For non-interacting systems,
the Gershgorin circle theorem
Gershgorin 1931
gives the
maximal
spectral bounds

\absolutevalue{z - \cmp*{v}^{\alpha}}\leq D^{\alpha}.

(41)

In the limit
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\beta}\propto\delta^{\alpha\beta}
,
we can make a more precise statement and derive exact spectral bounds as will be discussed below.
The spectral function can only vanish when the imaginary part of the effective medium vanishes.
Thus, for non-interacting systems,
we need to check where the argument of the square root is negative.
One finds an imaginary part and therefore spectral weight for

\absolutevalue{z - \cmp*{v}^{\alpha}}<\sqrt{c^{\alpha}\frac{Z-c^{\alpha}}{Z-1}}D^{\alpha}.

(42)

Therefore, for the Bethe lattice with coordination number
Z
and
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\beta}\propto\delta^{\alpha\beta}
,
the bandwidth is reduced due to concentration by a factor
\sqrt{c^{\alpha}(Z-c^{\alpha})/(Z-1)}
.
We then obtain the effective bandwidth

D^{\alpha}_{\text{eff}}=\sqrt{c^{\alpha}\frac{Z-c^{\alpha}}{Z-1}}\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\alpha}D.

(43)

Our numerical results in
Section

III
were obtained for a semicircular DOS, i.e.,
the Bethe lattice with infinite coordination number
Z\rightarrow\infty
.
In this limit one finds an effective bandwidth

D^{\alpha}_{\text{eff}}=\sqrt{c^{\alpha}}\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\alpha}D.

(44)

The same factor
\sqrt{c}
was found in Ref.
Byczuk et al. 2004
in the CPA (
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}}=\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}=\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}}=1
)
in the limit of high disorder strength
(\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\mathsf{B}}-\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\mathsf{A}})/D=\delta\gg\max(1,U/D)
.
While the parameters in these limits are different, both describe the same physics, namely the decoupling of components.
Indeed, the components decouple not only for
vanishing hopping between the components
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}=0
,
but also in the case of a large separation in energy (
\delta\gg 1
).

For coordination number
Z=2
another interesting limit of the Bethe lattice is obtained;
this is the one-dimensional lattice
Economou 2006
, where

g^{1\text{D}}_{0}(z)=g_{0}(z,Z=2)=1/\left[z\sqrt{1-D^{2}/z^{2}}\right].

(45)

The spectral bounds are given by

D^{\alpha}_{\text{eff}}=\sqrt{c^{\alpha}(2-c^{\alpha})}\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\alpha}D.

(46)

Therefore, for the one-dimensional lattice and
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\beta}\propto\delta^{\alpha\beta}
the bandwidth is reduced by the factor
\sqrt{c^{\alpha}(2-c^{\alpha})}
.

III
Numerical results

The above formalism is now used to study the effect of off-diagonal disorder in the Anderson–Hubbard model at zero temperature.
We employ a Bethe lattice with infinite coordination number, whose half-bandwidth
D
sets the energy scale.
Furthermore we consider a discrete binary random alloy distribution with components
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
.

In all applications we consider the case of half-filling on average
\Expval(n_{i})=1
;
this leads to a fixed chemical potential which we choose as
\mu=0

In the following subsection we fix the alloy component concentration and study the change in the spectral function starting with the non-interacting case and equal atomic potentials.
The alloy component spectral functions are the concentration-weighted conditional spectral functions

\underline{\mathrm{A}}^{\alpha}(\omega)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{\pi}\imaginary\underline{\mathrm{g}}_{\text{loc}}^{\alpha\alpha}(\omega)=-\frac{c^{\alpha}}{\pi}\imaginary\Expval(G_{ii}(\omega)|i\mapsto\alpha).

(47)

The average spectral functions are given by the trace

\Expval(A(\omega))=\sum_{\alpha}\underline{\mathrm{A}}^{\alpha}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\imaginary\Tr\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}(\omega).

(48)

\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}>1
favors a band gap.
On the other hand, for
U=6D

a large
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}
favors metallicity.
For
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}\leq 1
the spectral function is gapped — similar to the result obtained in the Hubbard-I approximation
Hubbard 1963
.

IV
Summary

We presented a methodological framework for the study of interacting electrons in the presence of diagonal and off-diagonal disorder.
The formalism allows one to explore
a multi-component system of electrons with random on-site interactions and/or potentials as well as random hopping amplitudes.
For this purpose the Blackman, Esterling, Berk (BEB) formalism for averaging over off-diagonal disorder was combined with the dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT).
We introduced a tensor notation inspired by Koepernik
et al.

Koepernik et al. 1997
;
Koepernik et al. 1998
, by which the randomness of the components
is transferred to local indicator tensors defined in an extended space.
In this representation,
the problem can be solved in a single-site approximation, analogous to the coherent potential approximation (CPA).
The computational procedure, including the two-fold self-consistency and the impurity solver
Bauernfeind et al. 2017
, were discussed in detail.

In the limit of zero intercomponent-hopping
an analytic solution for the BEB approximation of the non-interacting Bethe lattice with the general coordination
Z
was provided.
We established the exact bounds of the spectral function, and showed that these lie within the maximal bound given by the Gershgorin circle theorem
Gershgorin 1931
.

The first application of the BEB+DMFT formalism in tensor formulation was the computation of spectral functions.
In particular, we discussed the changes in the spectral function for increasing dimensionless hopping amplitude
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}
and interaction strengths U.
For alloy components with the same local interaction,
we found a pseudo-gap in the spectral function of the alloy component with lower concentration.
For larger values of
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}
this is accompanied by quasiparticles of the dominant alloy component.
The pseudo-gap and the quasiparticle character were inferred by analyzing the respective self-energies.
We found a rather similar behavior in the case of alloy components with different interaction strengths.
For the discussion of both diagonal and off-diagonal disorder we studied equal alloy concentrations and equal alloy component bandwidths for a rather large scattering strength
\delta=3D
.
Increasing
U
while keeping
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}\leq 1.0
fixed
the electronic system was found to undergo two transitions: One from the non-correlated alloy insulator to a metallic state, and then from the metal to a correlated (Mott) insulator.
For larger values of
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}
the formation of quasiparticles signals the appearance of a metallic state, which eventually disappears again within the Mott insulating phase for large enough values of
U
.

Finally, we note that at the CPA+DMFT level our results are in agreement with previous model-Hamiltonian based publications.
As a matter of fact the BEB formulation is applicable to band structure schemes
Koepernik et al. 1997
;
Koepernik et al. 1998
;
Herbig et al. 2017
also for non-orthogonal basis sets.
The present formulation can be naturally extended following the CPA+DMFT
Östlin et al. 2018
methodology with similar computational costs.
Work along this line is in progress.

Appendix B
Efficient evaluation of the local Green’s function and its inverse

To solve the BEB self-consistency equation,
we need to repeatedly evaluate the effective local Green’s function

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}(z)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}{[\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}(z)-\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{T}}}\epsilon_{k}]}^{-1},

(60)

with
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}(z)=\mathds{1}z-\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{S}}}(z)
,
or rather its inverse
{(\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}})}^{-1}
.
A naive evaluation would be computational costly,
since one needs to invert a matrix for every
k
-point and every frequency point.
While this is feasible for small matrices,
it has the potential risk of inaccurate
k
-summations (or integrations),
especially for a DOS with singularities as for a one-dimensional or square lattice.
Therefore, we employ an algorithm based on the
compact
singular value decomposition (SVD) of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{T}}}

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{T}}}=\boldsymbol{U\sigma V}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{U\sigma}^{1/2}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}\eqqcolon\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{\dagger},

(61)

which we use to split the matrix;
here we partitioned the singular values symmetrically as
\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}=\boldsymbol{U\sigma}^{1/2}
,

\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{\dagger}=\sigma^{1/2}\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}
.
It is important to use the compact SVD as we will explicitly use the inverse
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-1}
.
Numerically, the need to truncate small singular values arises.
We note that for the binary alloy
the rank-1 case, where the SVD has to be truncated, is given for a hopping matrix of the type

\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}}&\sqrt{\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}}\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}}}\\
\sqrt{\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}}\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}}}&\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}}\end{pmatrix}\!=\!\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}}}\\
\sqrt{\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}}}\end{pmatrix}\!\!\!\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}}}&\sqrt{\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}}}\end{pmatrix}.

(62)

This is the structure of the hopping matrix discussed by Shiba
Shiba 1971
.
Another prominent rank-1 example is the CPA limit with
\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}}=\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}=\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}}=1
.
The matrix inverse
{[\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}(z)-\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{T}}}\epsilon_{k}]}^{-1}
is calculated using the Woodbury matrix identity
Higham 2002
.
Furthermore, we calculate the eigendecomposition

\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}^{-1}(z)\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}=\boldsymbol{P}(z)\boldsymbol{d}(z)\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}(z),

(63)

where
\boldsymbol{d}(z)
is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues.
The
k
-dependent Green’s function can be expressed as

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{G}}}(z,k)=\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{P}{\left[\boldsymbol{d}-\frac{\mathds{1}}{\epsilon_{k}}\right]}^{-1}\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}^{-1},

(64)

where we did not write the
z
-dependence explicitly.
We note that only the term in the square bracket depends on
k
.
Since it contains only diagonal matrices, the matrix inverse only involves the reciprocal matrix elements.
We look at a particular diagonal element with the
z
-dependent eigenvalue
\boldsymbol{d}_{ii}(z)=\lambda_{i}(z)

-{\left[\lambda_{i}(z)-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{k}}\right]}^{-1}=\frac{1}{1/\lambda_{i}(z)-\epsilon_{k}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{2}(z)}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}(z)}.

(65)

It is straightforward to perform the
k
-summation,
as we have the standard form of the lattice Hilbert transform

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}\frac{1}{z-\epsilon_{k}}\eqqcolon g_{0}(z),

(66)

evaluated at
1/\lambda_{i}
.
For simple lattices like the Bethe lattice we know the analytic expression for
g_{0}
;
a numerical integration can then be avoided.
For the local Green’s function we obtain the lengthy expression

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}=\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{P}{[\boldsymbol{d}^{-1}g_{0}(\boldsymbol{d}^{-1})-\mathds{1}]}\boldsymbol{d}^{-1}\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}^{-1},

(67)

where again the
z
-dependence was not written out.
For the self-consistency equation of the BEB formalism one only needs the inverse of
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}
.
The Woodbury matrix identity yields the simpler expression,

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}^{-1}(z)=\\
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}(z)+\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{P}(z)\left[\frac{1}{g_{0}\big(1/\boldsymbol{d}(z)\big)}-1/\boldsymbol{d}(z)\right]\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}(z)\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{\dagger},

(68)

where we explicitly noted the inverse of the diagonal matrices by reciprocal matrix elements.

Considering that the main cost of a naive evaluation of
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}^{-1}
arises from the matrix inversion,
this amounts to
N_{z}(N_{k}+1)
matrix inversions,
where
N_{z}
is the number of frequency points and

N_{k}
is the number of
k
-points required for the integration.
The alternative algorithm proposed here requires, on the other hand,

N_{z}
matrix inversions due to the calculation of
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}^{-1}(z)
,
and another
N_{z}
matrix diagonalizations in the compact space of the SVD.
In practice, the calculations were well behaved,
and we encountered no numerical problems regarding the diagonalization of
Eq.

63
.

For the common case of full rank
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{T}}}
,
we can use the unitarity
\boldsymbol{U}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{U}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{V}^{-1}

to simplify the formulas further.
We obtain the simple formulas for the local Green’s function

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}(z)=\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{P}(z)g_{0}\big(1/\boldsymbol{d}(z)\big)\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}(z)\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{U}^{\dagger}

(69)

and its inverse

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}^{-1}(z)=\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{P}(z)\frac{1}{g_{0}\big(1/\boldsymbol{d}(z)\big)}\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}(z)\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{\dagger}.

(70)

Furthermore, we can directly calculate the matrix diagonalization of

\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}(z)\boldsymbol{V\sigma}^{-1/2}=\boldsymbol{P}(z)\boldsymbol{d}^{-1}(z)\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}(z),

(71)

avoiding the need of the
N_{z}
matrix inversions for
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\xi}}}(z)
.

Appendix C
BEB self-consistency equation with renormalized indicator tensors

With
\alpha\neq\beta
, the high-frequency expansion of the effective medium yields

\displaystyle\underline{\mathrm{S}}^{\alpha\beta}(z)

\displaystyle=-\epsilon_{(1)}\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\beta}+\order{z^{-1} },

(72)

\displaystyle\underline{\mathrm{S}}^{\alpha\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{\alpha}-1}{c^{\alpha}}z+\frac{\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha}-\mu+\overline{c}^{\alpha}\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\alpha}\epsilon_{(1)}}{c^{\alpha}}+\order{z^{-1}},

(73)

where
\epsilon_{(1)}=\int\mathrm{d}\epsilon\rho(\epsilon)\epsilon
is the first moment of the DOS,
which vanishes for lattices with a symmetric DOS,

\underline{\mathrm{v}}
incorporates the static part of the self-energy
\Sigma^{\alpha}(z)
,
and
\overline{c}^{\alpha}=1-c^{\alpha}
is the concentration complement.
The diagonal of the effective medium,
\underline{\mathrm{S}}^{\alpha\alpha}(z)
, has a contribution which grows linearly in
z
,
and the on-site energies are multiplied by the inverse of the concentration.
The origin of this peculiar structure is evident from
Eq.

17

and the definition
Eq.

20
.
Unlike the diagonal elements of a one-particle Green’s function which behave like
1/z
for large
z
,
the effective local Green’s function
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{g}}}_{\text{loc}}(z)
behaves like
\boldsymbol{c}/z
.
The definition in terms of the effective medium, however, has the regular form of
{[\mathds{1}z+\dots]}^{-1}
.
This can be resolved by introducing a renormalized version of the component space.
Instead of the indicator tensor
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\eta}}}
,
Eq.

10
,
we use the concentration-scaled indicator tensor

{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{\alpha}_{ij}=\sqrt{c^{\alpha}}1_{\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}}(i)\delta_{ij}

(74)

and the Moore–Penrose inverse
Moore 1920
;
Penrose 1955

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+}
(which is in this case the left-inverse, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+}\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}=\mathds{1}
) of its equivalent matrix representation.
The components of the Moore–Penrose inverse read

{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}^{+}}^{\penalty\ \alpha}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c^{\alpha}}}1_{\mathbb{S}^{\alpha}}(i)\delta_{ij}&\mbox{\quad if\quad}c^{\alpha}>0,\\
0&\mbox{\quad if\quad}c^{\alpha}=0.\end{cases}

(75)

We can express the projector
Eq.

13
with the
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}
tensor:

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\chi}}}=\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma\gamma}}}^{+}.

(76)

For the renormalized BEB formalism,
we define the component Green’s function and the Hamiltonian matrix in terms of
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}
and the inverse
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{G}}}}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{(\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+})}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{G}(z)\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+},

(77)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}

\displaystyle\eqqcolon{\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}}\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{H}}}}{\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}}^{\intercal}.

Compared to the definitions in
Section

II.2
,
the Green’s function and the Hamiltonian are scaled by the concentration.
This can be conveniently demonstrated in the locator expansion

\boldsymbol{G}(z)=\boldsymbol{g}(z)+\boldsymbol{g}(z)\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{G}(z),

(78)

where
\boldsymbol{g}(z)={[\mathds{1}z-\boldsymbol{v}]}^{-1}
is the locator and
{(\boldsymbol{T})}_{ij}=t_{ij}
.
Sandwiching this equation by
{(\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+})}^{\intercal}
and
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+}
,
we obtain

{(\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+})}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{G}(z)\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+}={(\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+})}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{g}(z)\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+}\\
+{(\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+})}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{g}(z)\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+}{\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}}\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{\intercal}{(\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+})}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{G}(z)\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+},

(79)

where we inserted the identity
\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}^{+}\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\gamma}}}=\mathds{1}
.
This can be written in terms of the renormalized component quantities

\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{G}}}}(z)=\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{g}}}}(z)+\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{g}}}}(z)\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{T}}}}\,\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{G}}}}(z).

(80)

Compared to the regular BEB formalism
the Green’s functions are scaled with the reciprocal concentration,
and the hopping matrix with the concentration
\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{T}}}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}=\sqrt{c^{\alpha}}\underline{\mathrm{t}}^{\alpha\beta}(\absolutevalue{\vb{r}_{i} - \vb{r}_{j}})\sqrt{c^{\beta}}
.
The renormalized component Green’s function relates now to the one-particle Green’s function in the following way:

\Expval(\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{G}}}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij})=\begin{cases}{}\Expval(G_{ii}(z)|i\mapsto\alpha)\delta^{\alpha\beta}&\mbox{\quad if\quad}i=j,\\
\sqrt{c^{\alpha}c^{\beta}}\Expval(G_{ii}(z)|i,j\mapsto\alpha,\beta).&\mbox{\quad if\quad}i\neq j\end{cases}

(81)

There is no more concentration prefactor for the local Green’s function,
which is the central quantity of the BEB formalism, since it is a local theory.
The renormalized version of the self-consistency equation
Eqs.

25
and
26

reads

0=\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{g}}}}_{\text{loc}}^{-1}(z)-\overline{\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{g}}}}}^{-1}(z),

(82)

with the diagonal matrix

\overline{\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{g}}}}^{\alpha\beta}(z)=\frac{c^{\alpha}\delta^{\alpha\beta}}{{(\boldsymbol{\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{g}}}}_{\text{loc}}^{-1})}^{\alpha\alpha}+\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}^{\alpha\alpha}-\overline{c}^{\alpha}z+c^{\alpha}(\mu-\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha}-\Sigma^{\alpha})}.

(83)

With
\alpha\neq\beta
, the high-frequency expansion of the renormalized effective medium yields

\displaystyle\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}^{\alpha\beta}(z)

\displaystyle=-\epsilon_{(1)}\sqrt{c^{\alpha}}\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\beta}\sqrt{c^{\beta}}+\order{z^{-1}}

(84)

\displaystyle\underline{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}^{\alpha\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle=\underline{\mathrm{v}}^{\alpha}-\mu+\overline{c}^{\alpha}\underline{\mathrm{T}}^{\alpha\alpha}\epsilon_{(1)}+\order{z^{-1}}.

(85)

The scaling removes the contribution proportional to
z
,
and for a symmetric DOS the static part is simply the on-site energy of the components.
Furthermore, the static part remains finite with vanishing concentration.

Hybridization of the Bethe lattice

Eq.

.

given in
