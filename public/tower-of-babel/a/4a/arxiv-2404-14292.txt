Navier-Stokes equations for nearly integrable quantum gases
Maciej Łebek, Miłosz Panfil

Abstract
The Navier-Stokes equations are paradigmatic equations describing hydrodynamics of an interacting system with microscopic interactions encoded in transport coefficients. In this work we show how the Navier-Stokes equations arise from the microscopic dynamics of nearly integrable 1 ​ d 1d quantum many-body systems. We build upon the recently developed hydrodynamics of integrable models to study the effective Boltzmann equation with collision integral taking into account the non-integrable interactions. We compute the transport coefficients and find that the resulting Navier-Stokes equations have two regimes, which differ in the viscous properties of the fluid. We illustrate the method by computing the transport coefficients for an experimentally relevant case of coupled 1 ​ d 1d cold-atomic gases.



Hydrodynamics is a universal theory describing phenomena of transport and applicable to systems of sizes ranging from nuclear through biological up to cosmological scales
[

,

When viewed as an emergent theory, it assumes a huge reduction of the degrees of freedom present at the microscopic scale. The high-energy degrees of freedom are effectively integrated out which leads to dissipation effects present in the Navier-Stokes (NS) equations and captured by the transport coefficients. These are non-universal quantities depending on the details of a microscopic theory. In the present work we show how the NS equations arise from quantum-many body dynamics for systems that are nearly integrable. We also derive expressions for the transport coefficients which treat the integrable part of the interactions in an exact and non-perturbative way.

Generalized hydrodynamics:

The GHD is a theory of hydrodynamics for integrable models, such as integrable spin chains or 1d Bose gas, where an extensive number of local conservation laws are present. Whereas the theory is fairly new
[
21
,
22
]
(with earlier developments in
[
38
]
), it has been studied quite extensively (e.g. see the collections of reviews
[
23
]
) and was verified numerically
[
39
,
40
,
26
,
41
]
and experimentally
[
11
,
42
,
14
]
. Recently it was also shown how to extend its applicability, by the logic of
Boltzmann collision integral
, to the cases when the integrability is weakly broken
[
43
,
29
,
44
,
14
,
30
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
.

The GHD assumes a local relaxation of the system to the GGE
[
50
,
51
]
which in its microcanonical picture is described by the rapidity distribution
\rho_{\rm p}\equiv\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda;x,t)
slowly varying in space and time. The rapidity distribution evolves through ballistic propagation of particles accompanied by integrable scattering processes leading to diffusive spreading and non-integrable scatterings captured by the collision integral, as described by the GHD equation

\partial_{t}\rho_{\rm p}+\partial_{x}\left(v_{\rho}\rho_{\rm p}\right)+\mathfrak{f}\partial_{\lambda}\rho_{\rm p}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm p}\right)+\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\rm p}],

(1)

with effective velocity
v_{\rho}
, the diffusion kernel
\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}
and the Boltzmann collision integral
\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\rm p}]
. The effective velocity fulfills the integral equation
[
22
,
25
]

v_{\rho}(\lambda)=\lambda+2\pi\int{\rm d}\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda^{\prime})(v_{\rho}(\lambda^{\prime})-v_{\rho}(\lambda))
, where
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)
is model-dependent scattering kernel. The diffusion kernel
[
26
,
28
]
can be found in the Appendix A. We also consider external potential
U(x)
and associated force term
\mathfrak{f}=-\partial_{x}U

[
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
]
. The index
\rho
signifies that the effective velocity and diffusion kernel are functionals of
\rho_{\rm p}
and are space-time dependent. This renders the GHD equations highly nonlinear. Furthermore, the Boltzmann integral can be evaluated from the matrix elements of the perturbing operator in the spirit of the Fermi’s golden rule
[
29
,
30
]
. However, for the large scale dynamics the crucial aspect are their collision invariants.

In what follows we quite generally assume that there are
3
collision invariants corresponding to the total particle number, total momentum and energy such that

\int{\rm d}\lambda\,{h}_{j}(\lambda)\,\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\rm p}](\lambda)=0,\quad j=0,1,2.

(2)

where
{h}_{j}(\lambda)
are single-particle eigenvalues of the conserved charges of the underlying integrable model,

{q}_{j}=\langle{h}_{j}\rangle\equiv\int{\rm d}\lambda\,{h}_{j}(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda).

(3)

Functions
{h}_{j}(\lambda)
for a Galilean invariant theory are
{h}_{j}(\lambda)=\lambda^{j}/j!
. The
3
collisions invariants determine the nature of the stationary state of (
1
) to be a homogeneous thermal (boosted) state and the corresponding
\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)
follows from the standard construction of the Thermodynamic Bethe Ansatz
[
56
,
57
]
, which we recall in the Appendix B.

Macroscopic conservation laws:

The
3
collision invariants lead to macroscopic conservation laws. These are conveniently formulated using hydrodynamic fields

u(x,t)=\frac{{q}_{1}(x,t)}{{q}_{0}(x,t)},\quad e(x,t)=\frac{{q}_{2}(x,t)}{{q}_{0}(x,t)}-\frac{1}{2}u(x,t)^{2},

(4)

and we additionally rename the particle
density field
\varrho(x,t)={q}_{0}(x,t)
. We will refer to the set of hydrodynamic fields as
\{\varrho_{\alpha}\}={\varrho,u,e}
. To this end, we multiply the GHD eq. (
1
) by
{h}_{j}(\lambda)
, integrate over
\lambda
and use the property (
2
). Following this procedure for
j=0,1,2
we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}(\varrho u),\qquad\partial_{t}(\varrho u)=-\partial_{x}(\varrho u^{2}+\mathcal{P})+\mathfrak{f}\varrho,

(5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho e)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}(u\varrho e+\mathcal{J})-\mathcal{P}\partial_{x}u,

with
\mathcal{P}(x,t)
local pressure and
\mathcal{J}(x,t)
local heat current.
The pressure and the heat current have two contributions. One intrinsic to the diffusion term present in the GHD and given by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{D}}={h}_{1}\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm p}/2
and
\mathcal{J}_{\mathfrak{D}}={h}_{2}\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm p}/2
. The second one resembles the standard expressions from the kinetic theory,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{v}=\langle(\lambda-u)v(\lambda)\rangle,\quad\mathcal{J}_{v}=\frac{1}{2}\langle(\lambda-u)^{2}(v(\lambda)-u)\rangle.

(6)

The derivation of the conservation laws together with the formulas for
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{J}
is elementary and presented in
[
58
]
. We only note that in deriving the first conservation law the identity
\langle v\rangle=\langle{h}_{1}\rangle
, a consequence of the Galiliean invariance, is operatorial. This property establishes the velocity field
u
a relevant hydrodynamic degree of freedom.

The equations (
5
) are not closed because the pressure and heat current are functions of the full distribution
\rho_{\rm p}
rather than just its
3
moments encoded through fields
\varrho
,
u
and
e
. To circumvent this problem, in the phenomenological treatment one assumes the following expressions

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=P-\zeta\partial_{x}u,\qquad\mathcal{J}=-\kappa\partial_{x}T,

(7)

in terms of the hydrodynamic fields. Here
P=P(\varrho,e)
is the hydrostatic pressure,
\zeta
is the bulk viscosity and
\kappa
is the thermal conductivity, respectively. The temperature field and the energy field are related through the thermodynamic identity
T^{-1}=(\partial s/\partial e)_{\varrho}
, where
s
is thermodynamic entropy per particle
[
56
,
50
,
57
]
. This leaves the theory with two phenomenological constants
\zeta
and
\kappa
called
transport coefficients
. In this way we obtain the one-dimensional version of NS equations
[
59
,
60
,
61
]
, for the fields
\varrho(x,t)
,
u(x,t)
and
e(x,t)
. In the following we confirm this phenomenological picture and provide explicit formulas for the transport coefficients.

Chapman-Enskog method:
The standard procedure to justify the phenomenological picture and to turn the hydrodynamic equations into a closed set is the Chapman-Enskog (ChE) method. In the process it also provides a systematic computation of the transport coefficients from the microscopic theory. Originally it was formulated in the context of classical kinetic theory
[
37
,
1
]
. Here we adapt it to the GHD case. The ChE method assumes the time evolution of
\rho_{\rm p}
is determined by the hydrodynamic fields, namely
\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda,x|\varrho_{\alpha}(x,t))
, called
normal solutions
. With this assumption the GHD equations (
1
) can be rewritten as

\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\rho_{\rm p}}{\partial\varrho_{\alpha}}\partial_{t}\varrho_{\alpha}+\partial_{x}\left(v_{\rho}\rho_{\rm p}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm p}\right)+\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\rm p}].

(8)

As the external potential does not influence the determination of the transport coefficients we set it here to zero
[
62
]
. In what follows it will be useful to have a compact notation for the conservation laws. We write
\partial_{t}\varrho_{\alpha}=D_{\alpha}[\{\varrho_{\beta}\}]
where
D_{\alpha}[\{\varrho_{\beta}\}]
can be read out from eq. (
5
).
With this we find the ChE equation

\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\rho_{\rm p}}{\partial\varrho_{\alpha}}D_{\alpha}+\partial_{x}\left(v_{\rho}\rho_{\rm p}\right)=\frac{\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm p}\right)+\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathcal{I}}}\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\rm p}],

(9)

which is an equation for
\rho_{\rm p}
given the
\{\varrho_{\alpha}\}
and contains only spatial derivatives.
The effective velocity and diffusion are now determined fully from the knowledge of
\{\varrho_{\alpha}\}
.
In writing eq. (
9
) we rescaled
x
and
t
by hydrodynamic scales
t_{\rm h},l_{\rm h}
introducing two small parameters
\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{I}}
into the equation. They can be understood as generalizations of Knudsen numbers, ie. ratios of characteristic diffusion (collision integral) length-scales to
l_{\rm h}
.
In ChE method we treat the problem order by order in
\delta
’s writing

\rho_{\rm p}=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}^{m}\,\delta_{\mathcal{I}}^{n}\,\rho_{\rm p}^{(m,n)},

(10)

from which follow similar expansions for
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{J}
. Truncating the expansions of
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{J}
leads to the closure of (
5
). The resulting hydrodynamics depends on the truncation order. At the leading order eq. (
9
) gives
\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\rm p}^{(0,0)}]=0
.
The solution is the locally varying boosted thermal state and this is the Euler scale hydrodynamics. Specifically, explicit computations show that the hydrodynamic pressure
\mathcal{P}
reduces then to the hydrostatic pressure
\mathcal{P}^{(0,0)}=P(\varrho,e)

[
56
,
58
,
9
]
, and the heat current vanish identically
\mathcal{J}^{(0,0)}=0
. The hydrodynamic equations at the Euler scale are

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}(\varrho u),\qquad\partial_{t}(\varrho u)=-\partial_{x}(\varrho u^{2}+P),

(11)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho e)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}(u\varrho e)-P\partial_{x}u,

and are fully determined from the thermodynamics of the system encoded in the equation of state
P=P(\varrho,e)
. We thus partially confirm the phenomenological picture of eq. (
7
) in which
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{J}
, beside the hydrostatic pressure
P
, contain derivative terms which do not enter at the Euler scale. This renders the Euler scale dynamic reversible as can be also witnessed by the conservation of entropy. Indeed, simple computations show that the thermodynamic entropy density
\mathsf{s}(x,t)

obeys the continuity equation
\partial_{t}\mathsf{s}+\partial_{x}(v\mathsf{s})=0

[
58
]
.

At the first orders in both parameters
\delta_{\mathfrak{D}},\delta_{\mathcal{I}}
we recover the full NS hydrodynamics and find explicit expressions for the transport coefficients.
As announced earlier there are two contributions. The one from the GHD diffusion amounts to evaluating
\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{D}}
and
\mathcal{J}_{\mathfrak{D}}
on a boosted thermal state. The second contribution is due to the collision integral and is more technically involved.
The transport coefficients that we find are
\zeta=\zeta_{\mathcal{I}}+\zeta_{\mathfrak{D}}
and
\kappa=\kappa_{\mathcal{I}}+\kappa_{\mathfrak{D}}
with

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle={h}_{1}\mathcal{B}\phi_{\zeta},\quad\zeta_{\mathfrak{D}}=\beta{h}_{1}\mathfrak{D}\mathcal{C}{h}_{1}/2,

(12)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle={h}_{2}\mathcal{B}\phi_{\kappa},\quad\kappa_{\mathfrak{D}}=\beta^{2}{h}_{2}\mathfrak{D}\mathcal{C}{h}_{2}/2,

where
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
are kernels of the current and charge susceptibility matrices of the integrable model
[
63
,
64
]
, explicitly written in the Appendix A.
Additionally,
\beta\equiv 1/T
and functions
\phi_{\kappa,\zeta}(\lambda)
are found from the following integral equations

\Gamma\phi_{\kappa,\zeta}=\chi_{\kappa,\zeta},

(13)

where
\Gamma=-(\delta\mathcal{I}/\delta\rho_{\rm p})\mathcal{C}
evaluated in a thermal state, and

\displaystyle\chi_{\zeta}=\sqrt{\beta\varrho}\,\mathcal{B}h_{1}-\sqrt{\beta/\varkappa_{T}}\,\mathcal{C}(h_{0}+\sqrt{c_{P}/c_{V}-1}\,h_{2}),

(14)

\displaystyle\chi_{\kappa}=\beta\sqrt{\varrho c_{V}}\,\mathcal{B}(h_{2}-\sqrt{c_{P}/c_{V}-1}\,h_{0}).

Isothermal compressibility
\varkappa_{T}
and specific heats
c_{P},c_{V}
are those of the unperturbed, integrable model. As we show in
[
58
]
, thermodynamic quantities are represented as explicit functionals of the underlying thermal state with density
\varrho
and temperature
T
.
Functions
h_{n}(\lambda)
are charges constructed with Gram-Schimdt orthonormalization of
{h}_{n}(\lambda)
using
hydrodynamic scalar product

(g|h)\equiv g\mathcal{C}h
.
The integral equations (
13
) are supplemented with additional conditions
({h}_{i}|\phi_{\kappa,\zeta})=0,\,\,i=0,1,2
, which render their solutions unique. Equations (
13
) naturally generalize ChE integral equations
[
37
]
to interacting, nearly integrable models.
In the process we confirm that there are no other gradient terms in
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{J}
beside the two given in the phenomenological expression (
7
).

Both contributions to the transport coefficients have a feature known from the standard kinetic theory
[
37
,
65
]
. Namely, they are a simple generalization of coefficients found in a linearized hydrodynamics. Therefore, in what follows we focus on the linearized theory, which also has the advantage of revealing the physical ingredients and defer the further computations within the ChE method to a longer publication
[
62
]
.

Linearized hydrodynamics:
We linearize the GHD equations (
1
) around the homogeneous thermal state such that
\rho_{\rm p}(x,t,\lambda)=\rho_{\rm th}(\lambda)+\delta\rho_{\rm p}(x,t,\lambda)
,

\partial_{t}\delta\rho_{\rm p}+\mathcal{A}_{\rm th}\partial_{x}\delta\rho_{\rm p}=\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{D}_{\rm th}\partial_{x}^{2}\delta\rho_{\rm p}+\left(\frac{\delta\mathcal{I}}{\delta\rho_{\rm p}}\right)_{\rm th}\delta\rho_{\rm p},

(15)

where
\mathcal{A}
is the kernel of flux Jacobian matrix
[
63
,
25
]
. The linearized GHD equation (
15
) leads to an eigenequation for perturbations of conserved charges
\delta q_{n}(x,t)=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,h_{n}(\lambda)\delta\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda;x,t)
, for more details see the Appendix C. We then consider a time evolution of a single mode
\delta q_{n}(x,t)\sim\exp(i k x - \Lambda(k) t)\delta q_{n}(k)
.
For small
k
a standard perturbation theory methods apply and we find two sound modes and a heat mode (thermal mode) with dispersions (we neglect
\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
terms)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\pm}(k)

\displaystyle=\pm iv_{s}k+\frac{1}{2\varrho}\bigg[\left(\frac{1}{c_{V}}-\frac{1}{c_{P}}\right)\kappa+\zeta\bigg]k^{2},

(16)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm th}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{\varrho c_{P}}k^{2},

which agree with dispersion relations of linearized NS equations. The sound velocity
is equal to thermodynamic formula
v_{s}=1/\sqrt{\varrho\varkappa_{S}}
, where
\varkappa_{S}
is the adiabatic compressibility of the integrable model calculated for the thermal background state
\rho_{\rm th}
.
The transport coefficients
\zeta
and
\kappa
are given by (
12
) computed with the same state
\rho_{\rm th}
. The details are referred to
[
58
]
.

The corresponding eigenmodes are related to the hydrodynamic fields
\varrho
,
u
and
T

\displaystyle\delta\varrho=\varrho\sqrt{T\varkappa_{T}}\,\delta q_{0},\,\,\,\,\delta u=\sqrt{T/\varrho}\,\delta q_{1},\,\,\,\,\delta T=T/\sqrt{\varrho c_{V}}\,\delta q_{2},

(17)

where we took
\varrho(x,t)=\varrho+\delta\varrho(x,t)
,
u(x,t)=0+\delta u(x,t)
and
T(x,t)=T+\delta T(x,t)
. Therefore the first
3
orthonormalized charges are equivalent to the hydrodynamic degrees of freedom. Expressing heat and sound modes in terms of
\delta\varrho,\delta u,\delta T
we recover precisely the modes of NS equations
[
58
]
.

Application to the Lieb-Liniger model:

We now apply the introduced formalism to a situation relevant to experiments with cold atomic gases. We consider a setup of two 1d Bose gases, described by the integrable Lieb-Liniger (LL) model
[
66
]
, coupled by a long-range interaction which breaks the integrability. The integrable part of the Hamiltonian is
H_{\rm int}=H_{1}+H_{2}
with

H_{i}=\int{\rm d}x\,\psi_{i}^{\dagger}(x)\left(-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+\frac{c}{2}\,\psi_{i}^{\dagger}(x)\psi_{i}(x)\right)\psi_{i}(x),

(18)

where we work in the units
\hbar=m=1
and
\psi_{i}(x),\psi_{i}^{\dagger}(x)
are canonical bosonic fields. The integrability breaking coupling between the tubes is given by

V_{2}=\int{\rm d}x{\rm d}yV(x-y)\hat{\rho}_{1}(x)\hat{\rho}_{2}(y)
,
with
\hat{\rho}_{i}(x)=\psi_{i}^{\dagger}(x)\psi_{i}(x)
and
V(x-y)
interaction potential. In an experimental realizations with cold-atomic gases it originates from dipole-dipole interactions and its strength is tunable
[
12
,
16
]
.

Of special interest is the case when the two tubes are initially in the same state. Then they stay the same throughout the dynamics and the system can be described by a single equation (
1
). The collision integral based on form-factor approach
[
67
,
68
,
69
]
and the resulting relaxation for the homogeneous initial state was studied in
[
30
]
. For the computation of the transport coefficients we need
\mathcal{I}
linearized around a thermal state, c.f.(
13
). The derivation is presented in
[
58
]
. The result for the matrix elements of
\Gamma
in the basis
\{h_{j}\}
is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{nm}\!=\frac{2\pi}{\tau}\!\int{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}\,g_{nm}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\left(\mathcal{T}^{\rm dr}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\right)^{2}|v_{12}|,

(19)

with the integration measure
{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)(1-n(\lambda)){\rm d}\lambda
, the filling function
n(\lambda)
,
v_{i}=v(\lambda_{i})
,
v_{12}=v_{1}-v_{2}
, and

g_{nm}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\int{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\,(k^{\prime}(\lambda))^{2}\frac{\mathcal{S}[h_{n}^{\rm Dr}]\mathcal{S}[h_{m}^{\rm Dr}]}{(v-v_{1})^{2}(v-v_{2})^{2}}.

(20)

Here
\mathcal{T}
is the differential scattering kernel, for the LL model
\pi\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})=c/(c^{2}+(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})^{2})
and
{\rm dr}
and
{\rm Dr}
are dressing operations
[
70
]
,
k(\lambda)
is the Dressed momentum while
S[\varphi]\equiv S[\varphi](\lambda,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is

S[\varphi]=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}}{k^{\prime}(v_{1}-v_{2})}+\frac{\varphi_{1}^{\prime}}{k_{1}^{\prime}(v_{2}-v)}+\frac{\varphi_{2}^{\prime}}{k_{2}^{\prime}(v-v_{1})}.

(21)

The timescale
\tau
is related to the strength of inter-tube coupling
\tau^{-1}=\tilde{V}^{2}m/(4\hbar^{3})
, where
\tilde{V}^{2}=\int dk\tilde{V}^{2}(k)k^{2}
and
\tilde{V}(k)
is Fourier transform of
V(x)
.

The transport coefficients are then determined from (
12
) and the numerical solution is shown in Fig.
2
. We focus on the strong coupling regime of the LL model in the nomenclature of
[
71
]
where both interaction and thermal fluctuations are important. We find that there are two competing behaviors. When intratube interactions dominate the resulting fluid has relatively small viscosity and large thermal conductivity. Instead, when intertube interactions dominate then both viscosity and thermal conductivity are of the same order. In the limit of noninteracting integrable models,
c\to 0
or
c^{-1}\to 0
, the bulk viscosity vanishes and thermal conductivity diverges
[
58
]
as expected from the classical kinetic theory of weakly interacting models
[
59
]
.

Outlook:
In this work we have shown that the NS equations universally arise for
1d
quantum systems with weakly broken integrability and with Galilean invariance, assuming the GHD equation supplemented with the collision integral. We have provided an explicit equations (
13
) for the transport coefficients linking the effective hydrodynamic description with the exact microscopic thermodynamics of the integrable model. Unlike the standard approach in the kinetic theory, we treat an important part of the interactions non-perturbatively. One consequence of this is a non-zero viscosity, which in the derivation of the NS equations based on the free theory, is identically zero
[
37
,
60
]
.
The resulting fluid has two regimes. One dominated by the integrability breaking collisions with relatively small viscosity, and the second, dominated by the integrable collisions with thermal conductivity and viscosity of the same order.

It is well-known that transport in one dimension with conserved momentum becomes anomalous upon introducing noise to the system
[
72
,
73
,
74
,
75
]
. The linearized framework with
U(x)=0
presented here together with initial results for GHD with noise
[
36
]
open possibility for further explorations in this direction.

Recently, the NS equations were also found for the low temperature dynamics of the GHD without an explicit integrability breaking terms
[
76
]
. The effective projection to the three lowest conserved densities is attributed there to the low temperatures and holds at intermediate timescales. The transport coefficients are then of the form
\zeta_{\mathfrak{D}}
and
\kappa_{\mathfrak{D}}
taken in the
T\to 0
limit, namely they origin from the integrable collisions.

Our results apply also to classical integrable Galilean-invariant models, whose dynamics is described by (
1
). In such cases collision integrals may follow from BBGKY equations
[
77
]
or be constructed phenomenologically, as in the relaxation time approximation
[
31
]
. The methods introduced here can be also directly generalized to models without the Galilean invariance, e.g. spin chains.

Appendix A: Integral kernels–
For a linear operator
\mathcal{O}
we introduce the following notation for its action on arbitrary functions
\psi,\varphi

\displaystyle(\mathcal{O}\psi)(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}\mu\mathcal{O}(\lambda,\mu)\psi(\mu),

(22)

\displaystyle\psi\mathcal{O}\varphi

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}\lambda{\rm d}\mu\mathcal{O}(\lambda,\mu)\psi(\lambda)\varphi(\mu),

(23)

and use the standard notation for kernel multiplication

(\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2})(\lambda,\mu)=\int{\rm d}\nu\mathcal{O}_{1}(\lambda,\nu)\mathcal{O}_{2}(\nu,\mu).

(24)

The kernels for operators
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
appearing in the main text are as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}v(1-n\mathcal{T}),

(25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm p}fv(1-\mathcal{T}n)^{-1},

(26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm p}f(1-\mathcal{T}n)^{-1}.

(27)

where
f
is the statistical factor
[
25
]
(for fermionic statistics
f=1-n
). In the definitions above
\rho_{\rm p},n,f,v
should be viewed as diagonal kernels and we have
(\mathcal{T}\psi)(\lambda)=\int{\rm d}\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\psi(\lambda^{\prime})

\mathfrak{D}=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm tot}^{-1}\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}\rho_{\rm tot}^{-1}(1-n\mathcal{T}),

with

\displaystyle W(\theta,\alpha)=\rho_{\rm p}(\theta)f(\theta)\left(\mathcal{T}^{\rm dr}(\theta,\alpha)\right)^{2}|v(\theta)-v(\alpha)|,

\mathcal{T}^{\rm dr}(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})=\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})+\int{\rm d}\lambda^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime\prime})n(\lambda^{\prime\prime})\mathcal{T}^{\rm dr}(\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime}).

(30)

Appendix B: Boosted thermal states in the Thermodynamic Bethe Ansatz–
We discuss here the basics of Thermodynamic Bethe Ansatz (TBA)
[
50
,
51
]
for a model with fermionic statistics (for generic case see
[
58
]
) and give a precise meaning of boosted thermal states within this framework. The result of the TBA is that averages (of local operators or products thereof) in the grand canonical ensemble or generalized Gibbs ensemble are thermodynamically equal to expectation values computed in an eigenstate with rapidity distribution
\rho_{\rm p}
. This rapidity distribution follows from the solving the TBA equations that we now summarize. We start with bare pseudo-energy
\epsilon_{0}
which takes the following form

\epsilon_{0}(\lambda,x,t)=\sum_{n}{h}_{n}(\lambda)\beta_{n}.

(31)

The pseudo-energy
\epsilon
and total density of states
\rho_{\rm tot}
is then determined from the generalized TBA equations

\displaystyle\epsilon(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{0}(\lambda)-\int{\rm d}\mu\,\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\mu)\log\left(1+e^{-\epsilon(\mu)}\right),

(32)

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}+\int{\rm d}\mu\,\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\mu)n(\mu)\rho_{\rm tot}(\mu).

(33)

Solving the first equation yields
\epsilon(\lambda)
which enters the filling function

n(\lambda)=\frac{1}{1+e^{\epsilon(\lambda)}},

(34)

and in turn allows to solve the second equation. The total density
\rho_{\rm tot}
determines then the particles’ density according to
\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)=n(\lambda)\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda)
.

For thermal state
\bar{\rho}_{\rm p}(\lambda)
with chemical potential
\mu
and temperature
T
one has
\bar{\beta}_{0}=-\mu/T,\,\bar{\beta}_{1}=0,\,\bar{\beta}_{2}=1/T,\,\bar{\beta}_{n>2}=0
. The Galilean invariance implies that after boosting by velocity
u
the state is a TBA state with
\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)=\bar{\rho}_{\rm p}(\lambda-u)
and with Lagrange multipliers
\beta_{0}=\bar{\beta}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\bar{\beta}_{2},\,\beta_{1}=-u\bar{\beta}_{2},\,\beta_{2}=\bar{\beta}_{2},\,\beta_{n>2}=0
.

Appendix C: Further informations on the linearized equations–
We elaborate here on the computations involving the linearized Boltzmann equation. As indicated in the main text the structure of the resulting equations is simpler if we work in the orthonormal basis of the conserved charges with respect to the hydrodynamic scalar product
(g|h)
. Standard computations using the methods of Thermodynamic Bethe Ansatz yield, see
[
58
]
,

\partial_{t}\delta q_{n}+A_{nm}\partial_{x}\delta q_{m}-D_{nm}\partial_{x}^{2}\delta q_{m}=-\Gamma_{nm}\delta q_{m},

(35)

where we assume a summation over the repeated indices. Moreover,
A_{nm}
,
D_{nm}
and
\Gamma_{nm}
are now interpreted as matrix elements of operators associated with effective velocity, diffusion and collision integral evaluated in the orthonormal basis. We observe that
D_{0n}=D_{n0}=0
which is a consequence of Markovianity of the GHD diffusion operator
[
26
,
58
]
, while
\Gamma_{jn}=0
for
j,n=0,1,2
which reflects the collision invariants (
2
).
The linearized GHD equations (
35
) can be now solved perturbatively in gradients of
\delta q_{n}
. This is the easiest to formulate by going to the Fourier space and considering a time evolution of a single mode
\delta q_{n}(x,t)\sim\exp(i k x - \Lambda(k) t)\delta q_{n}(k)
. This leads to the eigenvalue problem

\left(\Gamma_{nm}+ikA_{nm}+k^{2}D_{nm}\right)\delta q_{m}(k)=\Lambda(k)\delta q_{n}(k),

(36)

with
\Lambda(k)
the dispersion relations for different modes. Among them there are
3
gapless modes related to the
3
conservation laws respected by the collision integral. The problem can be treated analytically in small
k
limit using standard perturbation theory around the spectrum of
\Gamma
operator. The three zero modes of
\Gamma
yield gapless dispersion relations (
16
) of heat and sound modes. At the first order in
k
the only non-trivial coefficient is sound velocity
v_{s}=\left(A_{1,0}^{2}+A_{2,1}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
, which agrees with the standard thermodynamic formula
v_{s}=1/\sqrt{\varrho\varkappa_{S}}
, see
[
58
]
. At the second order in
k
after lengthy calculation we recover subleading corrections as in (
16
) with transport coefficients which are determined from (
12
). These derivations are presented in
[
58
]
.

I
Effective velocity, diffusion operator and susceptibility matrices

We consider a Galilean-invariant model with energy
E(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}
and momentum
p(\lambda)=\lambda
. We set the mass to unity. As in the main text we introduce functions
{h}_{j}(\lambda)=\lambda^{j}/j!
related to ultra-local charges.
The effective velocity
[
25
]
is given by
v(\lambda)=(E^{\prime})^{\rm dr}(\lambda)/(p^{\prime})^{\rm dr}(\lambda)
where the dressing operation of an arbitrary function
g(\lambda)
is implemented by the following integral equation

g^{\rm dr}(\lambda)=g(\lambda)+\int{\rm d}\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})n(\lambda^{\prime})g^{\rm dr}(\lambda^{\prime}),

(I.1)

and
\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})
is a symmetric scattering kernel, which depends on the model. For instance, in the case of Lieb-Liniger model
[
66
]
it reads
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)=\frac{c}{\pi}\frac{1}{c^{2}+\lambda^{2}}
, where
c
is the coupling constant. We also defined the occupation function
n(\lambda)=\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)/\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda)
, where total density of states
\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda)
is

\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}+\int\differential\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda).

(I.2)

It can be shown that the effective velocity fulfils the following integral equation
[
25
]

v(\lambda)={h}_{1}(\lambda)+2\pi\int\differential\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda^{\prime})\left(v(\lambda^{\prime})-v(\lambda)\right).

(I.3)

With this, it is straightforward to check the identity used in the main text

\int\differential\lambda v(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)=\int\differential\lambda{h}_{1}(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda).

(I.4)

Indeed, we have

\int\differential\lambda v(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)=\int d\lambda{h}_{1}(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)+2\pi\int\differential\lambda\differential\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda^{\prime})\left(v(\lambda^{\prime})-v(\lambda)\right)=\int\differential\lambda{h}_{1}(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda),

(I.5)

where the integral
\int\differential\lambda\differential\lambda^{\prime}(\ldots)
vanished due to the symmetry of the kernel
\mathcal{T}(-\lambda)=\mathcal{T}(\lambda)
.

In addition to the Euler-scale term involving the effective velocity, there is also the diffusion term
[
26
]

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\mathfrak{D}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm p}\right)
with
(\mathfrak{D}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm p})(\theta)=\int\differential\alpha\mathfrak{D}(\theta,\alpha)\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm p}(\alpha)
. The diffusion kernel is

\mathfrak{D}=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm tot}^{-1}\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}\rho_{\rm tot}^{-1}(1-n\mathcal{T}),\qquad\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(\theta,\alpha)=\delta(\theta-\alpha)w(\theta)-W(\theta,\alpha),

(I.6)

and

\displaystyle W(\theta,\alpha)=\rho_{\rm p}(\theta)f(\theta)\left(\mathcal{T}^{\rm dr}(\theta,\alpha)\right)^{2}|v(\theta)-v(\alpha)|,\qquad w(\theta)=\int{\rm d}\alpha W(\alpha,\theta),

(I.7)

where
f(\theta)
denotes statistical factor
[
25
]
defined in (
IV.5
), which depends on the particles’ statistics. The dressed scattering kernel fulfills

\mathcal{T}^{\rm dr}(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})=\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})+\int\differential\lambda^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime\prime})n(\lambda^{\prime\prime})\mathcal{T}^{\rm dr}(\lambda^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{\prime}).

(I.8)

Note also that our definition of
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}
differs from the standard one
[
26
]
by a factor of
\rho_{\rm tot}^{2}(\theta)
.

From the Galilean invariance follows that diffusion kernel has left eigenvector with zero eigenvalue
[
26
]
,

\int{\rm d}\theta\mathfrak{D}(\theta,\alpha)=0.

(I.9)

This implies that the local density of particles is conserved by the diffusion term. Moreover from Markovianity of the diffusion kernel, there is a corresponding right eigenvector with zero eigenvalue, namely

\int{\rm d}\alpha\mathfrak{D}(\theta,\alpha)(\mathcal{C}{h}_{0})(\alpha)=0,

(I.10)

where
\mathcal{C}
is the charge susceptibility matrix defined as
[
25
,
63
]

\mathcal{C}=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm p}f(1-\mathcal{T}n)^{-1}.

(I.11)

We have used here the fact
[
25
]
that
\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda)={h}_{0}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)/(2\pi)
. For later use, we also introduce here the current susceptibility matrix
\mathcal{B}

[
25
,
63
]

\mathcal{B}=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm p}fv(1-\mathcal{T}n)^{-1},

(I.12)

we will discuss
matrix elements
of the operators
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
in more detail in Sec.
IV
.

II
Hydrodynamic conservation laws

We derive here the conservation laws for the 3 hydrodynamic modes. We adopt notation from the main text for the expectation values of charges

{q}_{n}=\int\differential\lambda{h}_{n}(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda).

(II.1)

We start with the GHD equation including the diffusion, force term
\mathfrak{f}=-\partial_{x}U(x)
and the Boltzmann scattering integral

\partial_{t}\rho_{\rm p}+\partial_{x}\left(v_{\rho}\rho_{\rm p}\right)+\mathfrak{f}\partial_{\lambda}\rho_{\rm p}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm p}\right)+\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\rm p}].

(II.2)

To obtain the conservation laws we multiply the GHD equation by
{h}_{j}(\lambda)
and integrate over
\lambda
.

•

Mass conservation: for
j=0
we obtain

\partial_{t}\langle{q}_{0}\rangle+\partial_{x}\langle{q}_{1}\rangle=0,

(II.3)

where we used that both the diffusion and the collision term conserve the particles’ density. This equation reflects the conservation of particles’ density. We have also used that

\int{\rm d}\lambda\,v(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,{h}_{1}(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda),

(II.4)

•

Momentum conservation: for
j=1
we obtain

\partial_{t}\langle{q}_{1}\rangle+\partial_{x}\langle{q}_{1}v\rangle=-\partial_{x}\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{D}}+\mathfrak{f}\varrho,

(II.5)

where we defined

\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{D}}=-\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\lambda{\rm d}\mu\,{h}_{1}(\lambda)\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}(\lambda,\mu)\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm p}(\mu).

(II.6)

The average in the second term can be decomposed into two contributions: the convective flow and the pressure term. To this end we define the velocity field

u=\frac{\langle{q}_{1}\rangle}{\langle{q}_{0}\rangle},

(II.7)

and introduce rapidity variable
\xi
defined with respect to
u
,
\lambda=u+\xi
. From the definition of
u
it then follows that

\int{\rm d}\xi\,\xi\rho_{\rm p}(u+\xi)=0,

(II.8)

which is easily shown by transforming back to rapidity
\lambda
. We then have

\displaystyle\langle{q}_{1}v\rangle=\int{\rm d}\lambda v(\lambda)\lambda\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)=\int{\rm d}\xi(u+\xi)v(u+\xi)\rho_{\rm p}(u+\xi)=\frac{\langle{q}_{1}\rangle^{2}}{\langle{q}_{0}\rangle}+\mathcal{P}_{v},

(II.9)

where we defined the pressure by

\mathcal{P}_{v}=\int{\rm d}\xi\,\xi v(u+\xi)\rho_{\rm p}(u+\xi)=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,(\lambda-u)v(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda).

(II.10)

We will later see that for a system locally in the thermal equilibrium state this reduces to the hydrostatic pressure
P
.

Summing up, the continuity equation reads

\partial_{t}\langle{q}_{1}\rangle+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\langle{q}_{1}\rangle^{2}}{\langle{q}_{0}\rangle}+\mathcal{P}\right)=\mathfrak{f}\varrho,

(II.11)

with
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{v}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{D}}
.

•

Energy conservation: Finally, for
j=2
we find

\partial_{t}\langle{q}_{2}\rangle+\partial_{x}\langle{q}_{2}v\rangle=-\partial_{x}\left(u\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{D}}+\mathcal{J}_{\mathfrak{D}}\right)+\mathfrak{f}\varrho u,

(II.12)

where we have defined

u\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{D}}+\mathcal{J}_{\mathfrak{D}}=-\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\lambda{\rm d}\mu\,{h}_{2}(\lambda)\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}(\lambda,\mu)\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm p}(\mu).

(II.13)

The average on the left hand side of (
II.12
) can be again divided into various contributions. Transforming to
\xi
variable we have

\langle{q}_{2}v\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\xi(u+\xi)^{2}v(u+\xi)\rho_{\rm p}(u+\xi)=\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\langle{q}_{1}\rangle+u\mathcal{P}_{v}+\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\xi\xi^{2}v(u+\xi)\rho_{\rm p}(u+\xi).

(II.14)

The remaining integral can be still divided into two contributions according to

\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\xi\xi^{2}v(u+\xi)\rho_{\rm p}(u+\xi)=\frac{1}{2}u\int{\rm d}\xi\,\xi^{2}\rho_{\rm p}(u+\xi)+\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\xi\,\xi^{2}(v(u+\xi)-u)\rho_{\rm p}(u+\xi).

(II.15)

The first integral, by going back to
\lambda
variable, becomes

\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\xi\,\xi^{2}\rho_{\rm p}(u+\xi)=\langle{q}_{2}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\langle{q}_{1}\rangle^{2}}{\langle{q}_{0}\rangle},

(II.16)

whereas the second defines the
\mathcal{J}_{v}
contribution to the heat current

\mathcal{J}_{v}=\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\xi\xi^{2}(v(u+\xi)-u)\rho_{\rm p}(u+\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\lambda(\lambda-u)^{2}(v(\lambda)-u)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda).

(II.17)

The total heat current is
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}_{v}+\mathcal{J}_{\mathfrak{D}}
.
Finally we obtain

\partial_{t}\langle{q}_{2}\rangle+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\langle{q}_{1}\rangle\langle{q}_{2}\rangle}{\langle{q}_{0}\rangle}+\frac{\langle{q}_{1}\rangle}{\langle{q}_{0}\rangle}\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{J}\right)=\mathfrak{f}\varrho u.

(II.18)

Summarizing we obtain the following set of equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle{q}_{0}\rangle+\partial_{x}\langle{q}_{1}\rangle=0,\qquad\partial_{t}\langle{q}_{1}\rangle+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\langle{q}_{1}\rangle^{2}}{\langle{q}_{0}\rangle}+\mathcal{P}\right)=\mathfrak{f}\varrho,

(II.19)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle{q}_{2}\rangle+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\langle{q}_{1}\rangle\langle{q}_{2}\rangle}{\langle{q}_{0}\rangle}+\frac{\langle{q}_{1}\rangle}{\langle{q}_{0}\rangle}\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{J}\right)=\mathfrak{f}\varrho u.

(II.20)

The continuity equations written above are for moments of the particles distribution. More standard form is found if we express them in terms of the velocity field
u
and internal energy field
e
defined by

e=\frac{\langle{q}_{2}\rangle}{\langle{q}_{0}\rangle}-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}.

(II.21)

Introducing also
\varrho(x,t)=\langle{q}_{0}\rangle
we find

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+\partial_{x}(\varrho u)=0,\qquad\partial_{t}(\varrho u)+\partial_{x}\left(\varrho u^{2}+\mathcal{P}\right)=\mathfrak{f}\varrho,

(II.22)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\varrho e+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\varrho\right)+\partial_{x}\left(u\varrho e+\frac{1}{2}u^{3}\varrho+u\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{J}\right)=\mathfrak{f}\varrho u.

(II.23)

This can be still simplified using that

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\left(u^{2}\varrho\right)=\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\partial_{x}\left(\varrho u\right)-u\partial_{x}\left(\varrho u^{2}+\mathcal{P}\right)+\mathfrak{f}\varrho u.

(II.24)

Substituting this into the energy conservation law we obtain the final set of equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+\partial_{x}(\varrho u)=0,\qquad\partial_{t}(\varrho u)+\partial_{x}\left(\varrho u^{2}+\mathcal{P}\right)=\mathfrak{f}\varrho,

(II.25)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\varrho e\right)+\partial_{x}\left(u\varrho e+\mathcal{J}\right)+\mathcal{P}\partial_{x}u=0,

(II.26)

reported in the main text.

III
Thermal boosted state

In this Section we study thermal boosted states. We show that a thermal boosted state characterized by chemical potentials
\beta_{0}
,
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
can be related to a thermal state with zero momentum and appropriately chosen
\bar{\beta}_{0}
,
\bar{\beta}_{2}
. Moreover, we will show that heat current
\mathcal{J}_{v}
vanishes in such case and that the pressure
\mathcal{P}_{v}
of a thermal boosted state is equal to the hydrostatic pressure
P
.

We start with the free energy density
[
25
]

\mathsf{f}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\differential\lambda F[\epsilon(\lambda)],

(III.1)

where function
F[\epsilon(\lambda)]
depends on the particle’ statistics. For example, for fermionic case relevant for Lieb-Liniger model
[
56
]

F[\epsilon(\lambda)]=-\log(1+e^{-\epsilon})
. The pseudoenergy
\epsilon(\lambda)
is determined from equation

\epsilon(\lambda)=\epsilon_{0}(\lambda)+\int\differential\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda)F[\epsilon(\lambda^{\prime})],

(III.2)

where for thermal boosted state the bare pseudoenergy reads

\epsilon_{0}(\lambda)=\beta_{0}{h}_{0}(\lambda)+\beta_{1}{h}_{1}(\lambda)+\beta_{2}{h}_{2}(\lambda).

(III.3)

In a thermal state, on the other hand
\beta_{0}=-\mu/T,\,\beta_{1}=0,\,\beta_{2}=1/T
with
\mu,T
equal to chemical potential and temperature, respectively (we set
k_{B}=1
).

We move now to discuss properties of a thermal boosted state, let
\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)
be its distribution function.
Then its first moment is non-zero,

u=\frac{\int{\rm d}\lambda\,\lambda\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)}{\int{\rm d}\lambda\,\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)}.

(III.4)

Define
\bar{\rho}_{\rm p}(\lambda)=\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda+u)
. We will show that
\bar{\rho}_{\rm p}(\lambda)
is a thermal state and find expressions for
\bar{\beta}_{0}
and
\bar{\beta}_{2}
in terms of the chemical potentials of the boosted state. This can be argued on the basis of the Galilean invariance of the theory or computed explicitly. We follow the latter path.

Consider the total density
\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda)
, it obeys the integral relation (
I.2
).
Using the definition of
\bar{\rho}_{\rm p}(\lambda)
it is easy to show that the corresponding total density is given by
\bar{\rho}_{\rm tot}(\lambda)=\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda+u)
. From this follows that the occupation functions and dressed pseudo-energies
\epsilon(\lambda)
and
\bar{\epsilon}(\lambda)
obey the same relation
\bar{\epsilon}(\lambda)=\epsilon(\lambda+u)
. Inspecting equation (
III.2
) we finally find that also
\bar{\epsilon}_{0}(\lambda)=\epsilon_{0}(\lambda+u)
. Writing this relation explicitly we have

\bar{\beta}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\bar{\beta}_{2}\lambda^{2}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}(\lambda+u)+\frac{1}{2}\beta_{2}(\lambda+u)^{2}.

(III.5)

Matching the terms with the same power of
\lambda
we find

\bar{\beta}_{0}=\beta_{0}+u\beta_{1}+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\beta_{2},\quad\bar{\beta}_{2}=\beta_{2},\quad\beta_{1}+u\beta_{2}=0.

(III.6)

The third equality is always fulfilled and gives
u=-\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}
.

The last property follows from the observation that for
\lambda=u
function
\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)
has the maximum. On the other hand

\frac{\partial\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}\sim\frac{\partial\epsilon(\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}.

(III.7)

Function
\epsilon^{\prime}(\lambda)
obeys a linear integral equation

\epsilon^{\prime}(\lambda)=\epsilon_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)+\int{\rm d}\mu\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\mu)n(\mu)\epsilon^{\prime}(\mu).

(III.8)

which follows from (
III.2
) and the identity
[
25
]

n=\frac{{\rm d}F}{{\rm d}\epsilon}

(III.9)

and because of its linearity it maps zero to zero hence we need to solve
\epsilon_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)=0
. Using
\epsilon_{0}(\lambda)=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\lambda+\frac{1}{2}\beta_{2}\lambda^{2}
we readily find the relation between
u
and the chemical potentials.

We can also show using (
I.3
) that the effective velocity transforms as
\bar{v}(\lambda)+u=v(\lambda+u)
. This has a consequence that
v(u)=u
where we used that
\bar{v}(0)=0
.

With these expressions we can show that the hydrodynamic pressure of a thermal boosted state reduces to the hydrostatic pressure
P
, see
[
9
]
and the end of the present section. Hence the bulk viscosity vanishes is such case. We have

\mathcal{P}_{v}=\int{\rm d}\xi\,\xi v(\xi+u)\rho_{\rm p}(\xi+u)=\int{\rm d}\xi\,\xi(\bar{v}(\xi)+u)\bar{\rho}_{\rm p}(\xi)=\int{\rm d}\xi\,\xi\bar{v}(\xi)\bar{\rho}_{\rm p}(\xi)=P.

(III.10)

For the heat current we have

\mathcal{J}_{v}=\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\xi\xi^{2}(v(u+\xi)-u)\rho_{\rm p}(u+\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\xi\xi^{2}\bar{v}(\xi)\bar{\rho}_{p}(\xi)=0,

(III.11)

because
\bar{v}(\xi)
is an odd function while
\bar{\rho}_{p}(\xi)
is even.

The entropy of the boosted state is equal to the entropy of the unboosted state. Recall the formula for the entropy density

\mathsf{s}=\int{\rm d}\lambda\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda)g(n(\lambda)),

(III.12)

where function
g(n)
depends on particles’ statistics
[
25
,
26
]
. For the fermionic models we have
g[n]=n\log n+(1-n)\log(1-n)
. In general
g=\epsilon(\lambda)n(\lambda)-F[\epsilon(\lambda)]
.
Using now the relations between the particle distribution and filling function of the boosted and unboosted state we see that the entropies are indeed equal.

Expression for the hydrostatic pressure in terms of
v(\lambda)

Let us close this section by further validating equation (
III.10
). Our aim is to show that indeed

P=\int\differential\lambda\lambda v(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)

(III.13)

where
\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)
is thermal state, is the hydrostatic pressure as defined in thermodynamics, see eq. (
IV.10
) of the next section. To this end we generalize the computations for the Lieb-Liniger model presented in
[
9
]
.

We start by noting that
\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}{h}_{0}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)
and
v(\lambda)={h}_{1}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)/{h}_{0}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)
, hence

P=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\differential\lambda\,\lambda\,n(\lambda){h}_{1}^{\rm dr}(\lambda).

(III.14)

Note that we have also shown at this point that
P={j}_{1}
, where
{j}_{1}
is the momentum current (compare with formula for current expectation value in
[
25
]
). To get
{h}_{1}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)
we look at (
III.8
). For thermal state it reads

\epsilon^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{T}{h}_{1}(\lambda)+\int\differential\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})n(\lambda^{\prime})\epsilon^{\prime}(\lambda^{\prime}).

(III.15)

Comparing with the definition of dressing operation (
I.1
) we conclude that
{h}_{1}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)=T\epsilon^{\prime}(\lambda)
. Moreover, using (
III.9
) and integrating by parts we get

P=-\frac{T}{2\pi}\int\differential\lambda F[\epsilon(\lambda)]=-T\mathsf{f}

(III.16)

in agreement with (
IV.10
).

IV
Thermodynamics and hydrodynamic matrices

In this section we will relate the matrix elements of hydrodynamic matrices
[
63
]

A
,
B
and
C
to the thermodynamics of the integrable model. This will be very helpful in linking results from linearized GHD with thermodynamic quantities, which appear naturally in the Navier-Stokes equations.

We start by defining the free energy flux
[
25
]
, in addition to the free energy density
\mathsf{f}
defined in (
III.1
),

\mathsf{g}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\differential\lambda E^{\prime}(\lambda)F[\epsilon(\lambda)],

(IV.1)

where the pseudoenergy
\epsilon(\lambda)
satisfies (
III.2
).

For a generic GGE state, the bare pseudoenergy includes higher conserved charges
[
50
]
. The simplest choice being the ultra-local charges
{q}_{j}
,

\epsilon_{0}(\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta_{j}{h}_{j}(\lambda).

(IV.2)

The expectation values of the charges in the GGE state follow then from derivatives of the free energy density with respect to the generalized temperatures. In a similar fashion, the expectation values of the currents are given by the derivatives of the free energy flux,

{q}_{i}=\frac{\partial\mathsf{f}}{\partial\beta_{i}},\qquad{j}_{i}=\frac{\partial\mathsf{g}}{\partial\beta_{i}}.

(IV.3)

The hydrodynamic matrices
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
are susceptibilities of the currents and charges

\mathcal{B}_{i,j}=-\frac{\partial{j}_{i}}{\partial\beta_{j}}=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)f(\lambda)v(\lambda){h}_{i}^{\rm dr}(\lambda){h}_{j}^{\rm dr}(\lambda),\qquad\mathcal{C}_{i,j}=-\frac{\partial{q}_{i}}{\partial\beta_{j}}=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)f(\lambda){h}_{i}^{\rm dr}(\lambda){h}_{j}^{\rm dr}(\lambda).

(IV.4)

The matrices defined above are clearly symmetric. Above we defined

f(\lambda)=-\frac{{\rm d}^{2}F/{\rm d}\epsilon^{2}}{{\rm d}F/{\rm d}\epsilon},

(IV.5)

which is called the statistical factor
[
25
]
. For instance, in the case of fermionic statistics one finds
f(\theta)=1-n(\theta)
.
We also note that for Galilean invariant systems there is an useful relation
\mathcal{B}_{1,0}=\mathcal{C}_{1,1}
.

The hydrodynamic matrices depend on the choice of the basis for the ultra-local charges. For the thermodynamics it is natural to work with matrix elements of the ultralocal charges
{h}_{n}(\lambda)
as defined above. Instead, for the hydrodynamics, it is more convenient to work with the orthonormalized basis obtained from the ultra-local charges through the Gram-Schmidt method with the inner product (
V.6
). For
h_{n}(\lambda)
denoting the single-particle eigenvalues of the resulting charges, the hydrodynamic matrices are

B_{i,j}=-\frac{\partial j_{i}}{\partial\beta_{j}}=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)f(\lambda)v(\lambda)h_{i}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)h_{j}^{\rm dr}(\lambda),\qquad C_{i,j}=-\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial\beta_{j}}=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)f(\lambda)h_{i}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)h_{j}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)=\delta_{i,j}.

(IV.6)

In the remainder of this section we will relate matrix elements of
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
to thermodynamic quantities. We focus on the Gibbs state with a thermal
\epsilon_{0}(\lambda)
function,

\epsilon_{0}(\lambda)=-\frac{\mu}{T}{h}_{0}(\lambda)+\frac{1}{T}{h}_{2}(\lambda).

(IV.7)

We also introduce the notation

\varrho={q}_{0}=\int\differential\lambda{h}_{0}(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda),\qquad\mathsf{e}={q}_{2}=\int\differential\lambda{h}_{2}(\lambda)\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda),\qquad\mathsf{s}=\int\differential\lambda\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda)g[n(\lambda)],

(IV.8)

for particle, energy and entropy density, respectively. Moreover, by

s=\varrho^{-1}\mathsf{s},\qquad e=\varrho^{-1}\mathsf{e},

(IV.9)

we denote entropy and energy per particle. For Gibbs state the free energy
\mathsf{f}
takes the form of the grand potential per unit length divided by temperature and can be written as

\mathsf{f}=-\frac{\mu}{T}\varrho+\frac{1}{T}\mathsf{e}-\mathsf{s}=-\frac{P}{T}.

(IV.10)

With this, we compute various thermodynamic quantities. We start with the sound velocity.

IV.1
Sound velocity

The sound velocity is related to the adiabatic compressibility
\varkappa_{S}
and defined as (we work with unit mass)

v_{s}^{2}=\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\varrho}\right)_{s}=\frac{1}{\varrho\varkappa_{S}},\qquad\varkappa_{S}=\frac{1}{\varrho}\left(\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial P}\right)_{s}.

(IV.11)

To compute the relevant derivative, we realize that
P,\varrho
and
s
depend only on two thermodynamic parameters, which specify the state:
\mu,T
. Equivalently, they depend on
\beta_{0},\beta_{2}
. We may thus write

\differential s=\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}\differential\beta_{0}+\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\beta_{2}}\right)_{\beta_{0}}\differential\beta_{2},

(IV.12)

and similarly for
\differential\varrho
and
\differential P
. To keep the entropy fixed
\differential\beta_{0}
and
\differential\beta_{2}
must be related through

\differential\beta_{0}=-\frac{\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\beta_{2}}\right)_{\beta_{0}}}{\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}}\differential\beta_{2},

(IV.13)

which leads to the following formula

\left(\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial P}\right)_{s}=\frac{\left(\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\beta_{2}}\right)_{\beta_{0}}-\left(\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial\beta_{2}}\right)_{\beta_{0}}\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}}{\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\beta_{2}}\right)_{\beta_{0}}-\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\beta_{2}}\right)_{\beta_{0}}\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}}.

(IV.14)

The derivatives of the entropy can be computed from the expression for free energy (
IV.10
). The entropy per particle equals to

s=\frac{\beta_{0}\varrho+\beta_{2}\mathsf{e}-\mathsf{f}}{\varrho},

(IV.15)

and the two required derivatives are

\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}=\frac{\mathcal{C}_{0,0}(\beta_{2}\mathsf{e}-\mathsf{f})-\mathcal{C}_{2,0}\beta_{2}\varrho}{\varrho^{2}},\qquad\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\beta_{2}}\right)_{\beta_{0}}=\frac{\mathcal{C}_{2,0}(\beta_{2}\mathsf{e}-\mathsf{f})-\mathcal{C}_{2,2}\beta_{2}\varrho}{\varrho^{2}}.

(IV.16)

We use now the relation
{j}_{1}=P
, valid for Galilean-invariant models, see (
III.14
). Together with
P=-T\mathsf{f}=-\mathsf{f}/\beta_{2}
they yield

\mathcal{B}_{1,0}=-\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}=\frac{\varrho}{\beta_{2}}=T\varrho,\qquad\mathcal{B}_{2,1}=-\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\beta_{2}}\right)_{\beta_{0}}=\frac{\beta_{2}\mathsf{e}-\mathsf{f}}{\beta_{2}^{2}},

(IV.17)

which gives

\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{B}_{1,0}^{2}}\left(\mathcal{C}_{0,0}\mathcal{B}_{2,1}-\mathcal{C}_{2,0}\mathcal{B}_{1,0}\right),\qquad\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\beta_{2}}\right)_{\beta_{0}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{B}_{1,0}^{2}}\left(\mathcal{C}_{2,0}\mathcal{B}_{2,1}-\mathcal{C}_{2,2}\mathcal{B}_{1,0}\right).

(IV.18)

We now go back to (
IV.14
) and (
IV.11
) to find the final expression for the adiabatic compressibilty

\varkappa_{S}=T\frac{\mathcal{C}_{2,2}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-\mathcal{C}_{2,0}^{2}}{\mathcal{B}_{1,0}^{2}\mathcal{C}_{2,2}+\mathcal{B}_{2,1}^{2}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-2\mathcal{B}_{1,0}\mathcal{B}_{2,1}\mathcal{C}_{2,0}},

(IV.19)

from which the final expression for the sound velocity follows

v_{s}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathcal{B}_{1,0}}\frac{\mathcal{B}_{1,0}^{2}\mathcal{C}_{2,2}+\mathcal{B}_{2,1}^{2}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-2\mathcal{B}_{1,0}\mathcal{B}_{2,1}\mathcal{C}_{2,0}}{\mathcal{C}_{2,2}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-\mathcal{C}_{2,0}^{2}}}.

(IV.20)

We have analytically confirmed that this general formula reduces to known formulas for free fermions
v_{s}^{\rm Fermi}=\sqrt{3T\frac{f_{3/2}(e^{\mu/T})}{f_{1/2}(e^{\mu/T})}}
where
f_{\alpha}(x)
are Fermi functions and for hard-rods
[
1
]

v_{s}^{\rm HR}=\sqrt{3T/(1-a\varrho)^{2}}
, where
a
is rod’s length. For the Lieb-Liniger model we confirmed with derivatives computed numerically from TBA.

IV.2
Specific heats, isothermal compressibility and additional identities

We calculate in a similar fashion several more thermodynamic quantities, which are useful for purposes of this work.

Isothermal compressibility:
The isothermal compressibility is defined as

\varkappa_{T}=\frac{1}{\varrho}\left(\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial P}\right)_{T},

(IV.21)

and standard manipulations give

\varkappa_{T}=\frac{1}{\varrho}\frac{\left(\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}}{\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}}=\frac{1}{\varrho}\frac{\mathcal{C}_{0,0}}{\mathcal{B}_{1,0}}.

(IV.22)

Specific heat at constant volume:
The definition is

c_{V}=\left(\frac{\partial e}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}=\frac{1}{\varrho}\left(\frac{\partial\mathsf{e}}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}.

(IV.23)

Expressing
\differential T
with
\differential\beta_{0}
and
\differential\beta_{2}
we obtain

c_{V}=\frac{1}{\varrho}\frac{-\left(\frac{\partial\mathsf{e}}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}\left(\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial\beta_{2}}\right)_{\beta_{0}}+\left(\frac{\partial\mathsf{e}}{\partial\beta_{2}}\right)_{\beta_{0}}\left(\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}}{-T^{2}\left(\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial\beta_{0}}\right)_{\beta_{2}}},

(IV.24)

which can now be expressed with the hydrodynamic matrices as

c_{V}=\frac{1}{T}\frac{\mathcal{C}_{2,2}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-\mathcal{C}_{2,0}^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{0,0}\mathcal{B}_{1,0}}.

(IV.25)

Specific heat at constant pressure:
The definition is

c_{P}=T\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}\right)_{P}.

(IV.26)

Proceeding in an analogous way as in the previous cases we find

c_{P}=\frac{1}{T}\frac{\mathcal{B}_{1,0}^{2}\mathcal{C}_{2,2}+\mathcal{B}_{2,1}^{2}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-2\mathcal{B}_{1,0}\mathcal{B}_{2,1}\mathcal{C}_{2,0}}{\mathcal{B}_{1,0}^{3}}.

(IV.27)

As a check for our expressions, we also observe that the two compressibilities and two specific heats are not independent from each other. Instead, they obey the thermodynamic identity

\varkappa_{S}=\frac{c_{V}}{c_{P}}\varkappa_{T}.

(IV.28)

One can easily verify that this identity holds for the expressions given in (
IV.19
), (
IV.22
), (
IV.25
) and (
IV.27
).

Expressions for the matrix elements of
A
:

The sound velocity may be rewritten with
A
matrix elements in GS-orthonormalized basis. These matrix elements are defined in (
V.14
) and appear explicitly in the equation (
VII.2
), from which dispersion relations are determined. It is thus useful to write thermodynamic quantities in terms of
A
matrix in this basis

A_{1,0}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{B}_{1,0}}{\mathcal{C}_{0,0}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varrho\varkappa_{T}}},\qquad A_{2,1}=\frac{\mathcal{B}_{2,1}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-\mathcal{B}_{1,0}\mathcal{C}_{2,0}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{0,0}\mathcal{B}_{1,0}(\mathcal{C}_{2,2}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-\mathcal{C}_{2,0}^{2})}}=\frac{T}{\varrho^{2}c_{V}}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varrho\varkappa_{T}}}\sqrt{c_{P}/c_{V}-1}.

(IV.29)

Above we have used a general thermodynamic identity
[
37
]

c_{P}-c_{V}=\frac{T}{\varrho^{2}}\frac{\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}^{2}}{\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\varrho}\right)_{T}}.

(IV.30)

In the perturbation theory, we will find that speed of sound becomes

v_{s}=\sqrt{A_{1,0}^{2}+A_{2,1}^{2}},

(IV.31)

which agrees with (
IV.20
). Moreover, we observe that

\frac{A_{2,1}^{2}}{A_{1,0}^{2}}=c_{P}\left(\frac{1}{c_{V}}-\frac{1}{c_{P}}\right)=\frac{c_{P}}{c_{V}}-1.

(IV.32)

Additional identities:

For analysis of the structure of heat and sound modes the following identities are useful

\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\frac{\mathcal{B}_{2,1}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-\mathcal{B}_{1,0}\mathcal{C}_{2,0}}{\mathcal{C}_{0,0}},\qquad\frac{1}{\varrho}\left(\mathsf{e}+P-T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}\right)=\frac{\mathcal{C}_{2,0}}{\mathcal{C}_{0,0}}.

(IV.33)

They can be derived with the methods presented above. Lastly, let us comment that the method presented in this section gives thermodynamic quantities as explicit functionals (that is, combinations of matrix elements of hydrodynamic matrices) of the thermal rapidity distribution
\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)
. This representation is convenient and may be useful for applications which go beyond the scope of this work.

V
Derivation of the linearized equation for charge perturbations

We again invoke the GHD equation supplemented with the collision integral term, this time with
U(x)=0
,

\partial_{t}\rho_{\rm p}+\partial_{x}\left(v_{\rho}\rho_{\rm p}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm p}\right)+\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\rm p}].

(V.1)

Our aim here is to first linearize this equation and then rewrite it as a continuity equation for the densities of local charges.
To this end we linearize it around a stationary uniform thermal state
\rho(x,t)=\rho_{\rm th}+\delta\rho_{\rm p}(x,t)

getting equation

\partial_{t}\delta\rho_{\rm p}+\partial_{x}\left((1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}v_{\rho}(1-n\mathcal{T})\delta\rho_{\rm p}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{D}_{\rm th}\partial_{x}^{2}\delta\rho_{\rm p}+\left(\frac{\delta\mathcal{I}}{\delta\rho_{\rm p}}\right)_{\rm th}\delta\rho_{\rm p}.

(V.2)

The second term in the equation above (linearized expression for effective velocity term) was derived in
[
63
]
.

Motivated by the structure of
\Gamma
matrix that we consider (see Sec.
IX
), we wish to change the degrees of freedom from a perturbation of quasiparticle distribution to a perturbation of the bare pseudoenergy
\epsilon_{0}
. To establish a relation between them, we start with

\rho_{\rm p}=n\rho_{\rm tot},\qquad\delta\rho_{\rm p}=\delta n\rho_{\rm tot}+n\delta\rho_{\rm tot}.

(V.3)

The perturbation occupation function may be related to the perturbation of pseudoenergy via (
III.9
), hence
\delta n=-fn\delta\epsilon
. Moreover from (
I.2
) we have
\delta\rho_{\rm tot}=\mathcal{T}\delta\rho_{\rm p}
and from (
III.2
) we get
\delta\epsilon=(1-\mathcal{T}n)^{-1}\delta\epsilon_{0}
. Combining, we find the following relation

\delta\rho_{\rm p}=-(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm p}f\delta\epsilon=-(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm p}f(1-\mathcal{T}n)^{-1}\delta\epsilon_{0}=-\mathcal{C}\delta\epsilon_{0}.

(V.4)

where the charge susceptibility
\mathcal{C}
operator was introduced in (
I.11
) and the operator
(1-\mathcal{T}n)^{-1}
implements the dressing (
I.1
). Function
\delta\epsilon_{0}(\lambda)
can be expanded as

\delta\epsilon_{0}(\lambda)=\sum_{m}(h_{m}|\delta\epsilon_{0})h_{m}(\lambda),

(V.5)

where
h_{m}(\lambda)
form an orthonormal set of functions with respect to the hydrodynamic scalar product
(\cdot|\cdot)
, see also
[
25
]
,

(h|g)=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)f(\lambda)h^{\rm dr}(\lambda)g^{\rm dr}(\lambda).

(V.6)

In practice,
h_{m}
are constructed by the Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization of the ultra-local charges with single particle eigenvalue
{h}_{m}(\lambda)=\lambda^{m}/m!
. The most important first three functions are

h_{0}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{0,0}}}{h}_{0}(\lambda),\qquad h_{1}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{1,1}}}{h}_{1}(\lambda),\qquad h_{2}(\lambda)=\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{C}_{0,0}}{\mathcal{C}_{2,2}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-\mathcal{C}_{2,0}^{2}}}\left({h}_{2}(\lambda)-\frac{\mathcal{C}_{2,0}}{\mathcal{C}_{0,0}}{h}_{0}(\lambda)\right).

(V.7)

The expansion coefficients are directly related to the change in the expectation value of the
m
-th conserved charge,
(h_{m}|\delta\epsilon_{0})=-\delta q_{m}
, where

\delta q_{m}=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,h_{m}(\lambda)\delta\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda).

(V.8)

This relation follows from the following identity
[
25
]
(here dot-product is
h\cdot g=\int{\rm d}\lambda h(\lambda)g(\lambda)
)

(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}ng\cdot h=g\cdot n(1-\mathcal{T}n)^{-1}h=g\cdot nh^{\rm dr},

(V.9)

which follows from the symmetry of the kernel
\mathcal{T}
. With the help of this identity we find

\displaystyle\delta q_{m}

\displaystyle=-\int{\rm d}\lambda\,h_{m}(\lambda)(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm p}f(1-\mathcal{T}n)^{-1}\delta\epsilon_{0}

\displaystyle=-\int{\rm d}\lambda\,\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)f(\lambda)h_{m}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)\delta\epsilon_{0}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)=-(h_{m}|\delta\epsilon_{0}),

(V.10)

where we used in the second step with
h=h_{m}
and
g=\rho_{\rm p}f\delta\epsilon_{0}^{\rm dr}/n
. This allows us to write

\delta\rho_{\rm p}=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm p}f(1-\mathcal{T}n)^{-1}\sum_{m}h_{m}\delta q_{m}.

(V.11)

Functions
h_{n}
allow us to reformulate the GHD equations as equations for the charge perturbations. We multiply the GHD equation (
V.2
) by
h_{n}(\lambda)
, plug (
V.11
) and integrate over
\lambda
taking advantage of the orthonormality of functions
h_{n}
. Moreover, we introduce
\Gamma
operator

\Gamma=\frac{\delta\mathcal{I}}{\delta\epsilon_{0}}=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{I}}{\delta\rho_{\rm p}}\mathcal{C}.

(V.12)

The result is

\partial_{t}\delta q_{n}+\sum_{m}A_{nm}\partial_{x}\delta q_{m}=\sum_{m}D_{nm}\partial_{x}^{2}\delta q_{m}-\sum_{m}\Gamma_{nm}\delta q_{m},

(V.13)

with

\displaystyle A_{nm}

\displaystyle=B_{nm}=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)f(\lambda)v(\lambda)h_{n}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)h_{m}^{\rm dr}(\lambda),

(V.14)

\displaystyle D_{nm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\lambda\,{\rm d}\mu\,h_{n}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)\rho_{\rm tot}^{-1}(\lambda)\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}_{\rm th}(\lambda,\mu)\rho_{\rm tot}^{-1}(\mu)\rho_{\rm p}(\mu)f(\mu)h_{m}^{\rm dr}(\mu),

(V.15)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{nm}

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,{\rm d}\mu\,h_{n}(\lambda)\Gamma(\lambda,\mu)h_{m}(\mu).

(V.16)

Furthermore, we can take into account that
\Gamma
operator hides the Dressing operation
[
29
,
30
,
70
]
. The Dressing operation is defined through (where
F(\mu|\lambda)
is backflow function),

f^{\rm Dr}(\lambda)=f(\lambda)-\int{\rm d}\mu\,n(\mu)F(\mu|\lambda)f^{\prime}(\mu),

(V.17)

such that the collision integral is (note that operator
\mathbb{F}
effectively implements Dressing)

\Gamma(\lambda,\mu)=\int{\rm d}\nu\,{\rm d}\nu^{\prime}\,\mathbb{F}(\lambda,\nu)\Gamma_{0}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\mathbb{F}(\mu,\nu^{\prime}),\qquad\mathbb{F}(\lambda,\nu)=\delta(\lambda-\nu)+\partial_{\lambda}(n(\lambda)F(\lambda|\nu)),

(V.18)

where
\Gamma_{0}
is the kernel of linearized bare collision integral. This Dressing operation may be shifted to
h_{n}(\lambda)
so finally we get

\Gamma_{nm}=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,{\rm d}\mu\,h_{n}^{\rm Dr}(\lambda)\Gamma_{0}(\lambda,\mu)h_{m}^{\rm Dr}(\mu).

(V.19)

Let us comment on general properties of the matrix elements listed above. Matrix elements
A_{nm}
are non-zero where
n,m
are of different parity, instead
\Gamma_{nm}
and
D_{nm}
are non-zero only when
n,m
have the same parity. We also have
\Gamma_{nm}=0
for
n,m=0,1,2
, which reflects the
3
collision invariants, whereas
D_{0,n}=D_{n,0}=0
which follows from the Markovianity of the diffusion kernel discussed in Section
I
.

For the spectrum of
\Gamma
operator we assume that it is non-negative and gapped. The eigenvectors correspond to either even or odd functions of rapidities. We confirm these properties for the collision integral analyzed in Sec.
IX
.

VI
Linearized Navier-Stokes equations and transport coefficients

We linearize the Navier-Stokes equations, which can be understood as (
II.25
) with the phenomenological expressions

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=P-\zeta\partial_{x}u,\qquad\mathcal{J}=-\kappa\partial_{x}T,

(VI.1)

for the dynamic pressure and heat current. In this section, we do not specify
\zeta
and
\kappa
. Our aim is to focus on general structure of N-S equations and find relation between the spectrum of the hydrodynamic modes and the transport coefficients.

We start by introducing the temperature field to the Navier-Stokes equations. For this, we note the following general thermodynamic equality, which can be derived from basic principles
[
37
]

\delta T=\frac{1}{c_{V}}\delta e-\frac{1}{c_{V}}\left(\frac{P}{\varrho^{2}}-\frac{T}{\varrho^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}\right)\delta\varrho.

(VI.2)

One-dimensional version of Navier-Stokes equations
[
59
,
61
]
for the fields
\varrho(x,t)
,
u(x,t)
and
T(x,t)
reads then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}(\varrho u),

(VI.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=-u\partial_{x}u-\frac{1}{\varrho}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\varrho}\right)_{T}\partial_{x}\varrho-\frac{1}{\varrho}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}\partial_{x}T+\frac{\zeta}{\varrho}\partial_{x}^{2}u,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}T

\displaystyle=-\frac{T}{\varrho c_{V}}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}\partial_{x}u-u\partial_{x}T+\frac{\kappa}{\varrho c_{V}}\partial_{x}^{2}T+\frac{\zeta}{\varrho c_{V}}(\partial_{x}u)^{2},

where
\kappa,\zeta
are thermal conductivity and bulk viscosity, respectively. Linearizing around a homogenous thermal state

\varrho(x,t)=\varrho+\delta\varrho(x,t),\qquad u(x,t)=0+\delta u(x,t),\qquad T(x,t)=T+\delta T(x,t),

(VI.4)

one gets

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta\varrho

\displaystyle=-\varrho\partial_{x}\delta u,

(VI.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta u

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\varrho}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\varrho}\right)_{T}\partial_{x}\delta\varrho-\frac{1}{\varrho}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}\partial_{x}\delta T+\frac{\zeta}{\varrho}\partial_{x}^{2}\delta u,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta T

\displaystyle=-\frac{T}{\varrho c_{V}}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}\partial_{x}\delta u+\frac{\kappa}{\varrho c_{V}}\partial_{x}^{2}\delta T.

Moving to the
k
-space and anticipating the time-dependence as

\delta\varrho(x,t)=\int\differential ke^{ikx-\Lambda t}\delta\varrho(k),\qquad\delta u(x,t)=\int\differential ke^{ikx-\Lambda t}\delta u(k),\qquad\delta T(x,t)=\int\differential ke^{ikx-\Lambda t}\delta T(k),

(VI.6)

we get an eigenproblem with non-Hermitian matrix. As the matrix is non-Hermitean the left- and right-eigenvectors are different. This problem be cured by changing the variables as

\delta\varrho=\sqrt{T\varrho/(\partial P/\partial\varrho)_{T}}\,\delta\tilde{\varrho}=\varrho\sqrt{T\varkappa_{T}}\,\delta\tilde{\varrho},\qquad\delta u=\sqrt{T/\varrho}\,\delta\tilde{u},\qquad\delta T=T/\sqrt{\varrho c_{V}}\,\delta\tilde{T}.

(VI.7)

In these variables, the relevant matrix becomes symmetric. Explicitly, when looking for eigenvalues
\Lambda
we will deal with

\text{det}\begin{pmatrix}-\Lambda&i\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varrho\varkappa_{T}}}k&0\\
i\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varrho\varkappa_{T}}}k&-\Lambda+\frac{\zeta}{\varrho}k^{2}&i\sqrt{\frac{T}{\varrho^{2}c_{V}}}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}k\\
0&i\sqrt{\frac{T}{\varrho^{2}c_{V}}}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}k&-\Lambda+\frac{\kappa}{\varrho c_{V}}k^{2}\end{pmatrix}=0.

(VI.8)

We use now the general thermodynamic identities (
IV.28
), (
IV.30
) and
\frac{c_{P}}{c_{V}}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\varrho}\right)_{T}=v_{s}^{2}

[
37
]
and find the following dispersion relations

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\pm}(k)=\pm iv_{s}k+\frac{1}{2\varrho}\bigg[\left(\frac{1}{c_{V}}-\frac{1}{c_{P}}\right)\kappa+\zeta\bigg]k^{2}+O(k^{3}),\qquad\Lambda_{\rm th}(k)=\frac{\kappa}{\varrho c_{P}}k^{2}+O(k^{3}).

(VI.9)

These dispersion relations correspond to two sound modes and one heat mode, respectively. We observe that in the case of vanishing bulk viscosity
\zeta=0
, the ratio of the quadratic coefficients is
(c_{P}/c_{V}-1)/2
and therefore is universal. Depends only on the thermodynamics of the underlying gas but not on the integrability breaking terms. Vanishing bulk viscosity is an expected result for an ideal gas perturbed with weak interactions
[
59
]
. We will see that in our setup the bulk viscosity vanishes in the Tonks-Girardeau (
c\rightarrow\infty
) and in
c\to 0
limits, see Sec.
VIII
.

In addition to the eigenvalues, we can find eigenvectors. In the limit of
k\to 0
we have

\delta_{\pm}(k\to 0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{c_{V}}{c_{P}}}\delta\tilde{\varrho}\pm\delta\tilde{u}+\sqrt{\frac{c_{P}-c_{V}}{c_{P}}}\delta\tilde{T}\right),\qquad\delta_{\rm th}(k\to 0)=-\sqrt{\frac{c_{P}-c_{V}}{c_{P}}}\delta\tilde{\varrho}+\sqrt{\frac{c_{V}}{c_{P}}}\delta\tilde{T}.

(VI.10)

The general structure revealed in this section will be our reference point for perturbative treatment of (
V.13
). It will allow us to identify Navier-Stokes hydrodynamics appearing in GHD equation supplemented with a collision term.

VII
Perturbation theory

As discussed in the main text the linearized problem (
V.13
) is solved by Fourier transform followed by perturbative solution in
k
. The dynamics of a single mode is

\delta q_{n}(x,t)\sim e^{ikx-\Lambda(k)t}\delta q_{n}(k).

(VII.1)

Substituting this expression to the linearized GHD equations we obtain the following spectral problem

\left(\Gamma_{nm}+ikA_{nm}+k^{2}D_{nm}\right)\delta q_{m}(k)=\Lambda(k)\delta q_{m}(k),

(VII.2)

where summation over the repeated indices is assumed. We approach the problem perturbatively assuming that
k
is small. The spectrum consists then of gapped and gapless modes. The value of the gap is determined by the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of
\Gamma
. The gapped modes decay in time and become irrelevant at sufficiently large time scales. On the other hand, there are also gapless modes that are related to the collision invariants of
\Gamma
. In the logic of the perturbation theory in
k
the spectrum of the gapless modes has then the following structure

\Lambda(k)=ak+\omega k^{2}+\mathcal{O}(k^{3}),

(VII.3)

and importantly the linear coefficients depend only on
A
and therefore are universal, whereas the second order coefficients depend on both the collision integral and the GHD diffusion. The dependence on
\Gamma
enters through the second-order perturbation theory from coupling of the zero-modes with the dispersive ones. On the other hand, the dependence on
D
, comes directly from the first order perturbation theory because of the
k^{2}
factor standing in front of it.

For the purpose of perturbation theory calculations it is convenient to introduce bra-ket notation such that
\Gamma_{nm}=\langle h_{n}|\Gamma|h_{m}\rangle
and similarly for the other operators. More concretely,

\langle f|g\rangle=\int{\rm d}\lambda f(\lambda)g(\lambda),\qquad K|f\rangle=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,K(\cdot,\lambda)f(\lambda),

(VII.4)

with matrix elements understood in this notation as

A_{nm}=\langle h_{n}|A|h_{m}\rangle=\langle h_{n}|\mathcal{A}\mathcal{C}|h_{m}\rangle,\qquad D_{nm}=\langle h_{n}|D|h_{m}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle h_{n}|\mathfrak{D}\mathcal{C}|h_{m}\rangle,\qquad\Gamma_{nm}=\langle h_{n}|\Gamma|h_{m}\rangle.

(VII.5)

Where
\mathcal{A}=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}v(1-n\mathcal{T})

[
25
,
63
]
and operators
\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{D}
are discussed in Sec.
I
. Operator
\Gamma
is discussed in Sec.
V
with a concrete example in
IX
. Moreover, for the basis
h_{n}
introduced in Sec.
V
, the orthonormality condition is
\langle h_{n}|\mathcal{C}|h_{m}\rangle=\delta_{n,m}
.

With this notation,
|h_{i}\rangle
with
i=0,1,2
are eigenstates of
\Gamma
with zero eigenvalues. We denote by
|g_{\beta}\rangle
the eigenstates with non-zero eigenvalue
\gamma_{\beta}\neq 0
,
\Gamma|g_{\beta}\rangle=\gamma_{\beta}|g_{\beta}\rangle
. Whenever summation over
\beta
appears, it means summation over eigenvectors with non-zero eigenvalue. On the other hand, index
\alpha
always corresponds to thermal and sound modes.

VII.1
Perturbation theory at order
k

At this order we can neglect the GHD diffusion. The linear part of the dispersion relation of the hydrodynamic modes is then found by solving the eigenproblem in the degenerate zero subspace of the collision integral. The problem reduces to diagonalizing the following matrix

ikA_{\rm trunc}=ik\begin{pmatrix}0&A_{1,0}&0\\
A_{1,0}&0&A_{2,1}\\
0&A_{2,1}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(VII.6)

We find in total three modes with eigenvalues
\Lambda_{\pm}=\pm iv_{s}k
(sound modes), and
\Lambda_{\rm th}=0
(heat mode). The sound velocity given by the following formula, in agreement with thermodynamic sound velocity, see Sec.
IV
,

v_{s}=\sqrt{A_{1,0}^{2}+A_{2,1}^{2}}=1/\sqrt{\varrho\varkappa_{S}},

(VII.7)

and associated eigenvectors are (normalization factor is
\mathcal{N}=\sqrt{1+(A_{2,1}/A_{1,0})^{2}}
)

|h_{\pm}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\mathcal{N}}\bigg(\frac{A_{1,0}}{A_{2,1}}|h_{0}\rangle\pm\mathcal{N}|h_{1}\rangle+|h_{2}\rangle\bigg),\qquad|h_{\rm th}\rangle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\bigg(-|h_{0}\rangle+\frac{A_{1,0}}{A_{2,1}}|h_{2}\rangle\bigg).

(VII.8)

Therefore, in the first order of the perturbation theory, the degeneracy has been completely lifted. It is worth emphasizing here that the found eigenvalues (yielding the speed of sound) are not eigenvalues of the full matrix
A
. These are eigenvalues of matrix
A_{\rm trunc}
truncated to subspace of charges
{q_{0},q_{1},q_{2}}
conserved by the perturbation
\Gamma
.
Observe that eigenvectors (
VII.8
) may be written entirely in terms of the specific heats, using the relations between hydrodynamic matrices and thermodynamics derived in Sec.
IV
,

|h_{\pm}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{c_{V}}{c_{P}}}|h_{0}\rangle\pm|h_{1}\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{c_{P}-c_{V}}{c_{P}}}|h_{2}\rangle\bigg),\qquad|h_{\rm th}\rangle=-\sqrt{\frac{c_{P}-c_{V}}{c_{P}}}|h_{0}\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{c_{V}}{c_{P}}}|h_{2}\rangle.

(VII.9)

This result can be compared with known formulas for the modes in the classical three-dimensional ideal gas. For the ideal gas
c_{P}/c_{V}=5/3
and our formulas above reproduce results for sound and heat modes, when these values are substituted, see
[
59
]
. Furthermore using (
VII.9
), we also find useful relations for the sound modes in terms of the heat mode

|h_{\pm}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{c_{P}-c_{V}}{2c_{V}}}|h_{\rm th}\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{c_{P}}{2c_{V}}}|h_{0}\rangle\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|h_{1}\rangle,

(VII.10)

and heat mode in terms of ultra-local basis functions

|h_{\rm th}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varrho T^{2}c_{P}}}\left(|{h}_{2}\rangle-\frac{\mathcal{B}_{2,1}}{\mathcal{B}_{1,0}}|{h}_{0}\rangle\right).

(VII.11)

Gapless modes are equal to the modes of Navier-Stokes equations

We complete considerations at this level of perturbation theory by noting that the found heat and sound modes coincide with the modes of Navier-Stokes equations (
VI.10
).
For the purpose of this part, the bra-ket notation (which simplifies perturbation theory calculations) is not the most convenient. We therefore explicitly write sound and heat modes as

\delta q_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{c_{V}}{c_{P}}}\delta q_{0}\pm\delta q_{1}+\sqrt{\frac{c_{P}-c_{V}}{c_{P}}}\delta q_{2}\bigg),\quad\delta q_{\rm th}=-\sqrt{\frac{c_{P}-c_{V}}{c_{P}}}\delta q_{0}+\sqrt{\frac{c_{V}}{c_{P}}}\delta q_{2}.

(VII.12)

Already here we can see that these are exactly the modes of Navier-Stokes equation (
VI.10
), after the following identification is made

\delta q_{0}=\delta\tilde{\varrho},\qquad\delta q_{1}=\delta\tilde{u},\qquad\delta q_{2}=\delta\tilde{T}.

(VII.13)

We can also observe the truncated matrix (
VII.6
) expressed with thermodynamic quantities (
IV.29
) coincides with the relevant matrix (
VI.8
) for linearized Navier-Stokes equations, when terms of order
k^{2}
are neglected (these terms do not influence the eigenvectors in
k\to 0
limit)

ikA_{\rm trunc}=ik\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varrho\varkappa_{T}}}&0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varrho\varkappa_{T}}}&0&\sqrt{\frac{T}{\varrho^{2}c_{V}}}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}\\
0&\sqrt{\frac{T}{\varrho^{2}c_{V}}}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\varrho}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(VII.14)

The relation (
VII.13
) can also be justified from the very definition of the charges
q_{0},q_{1},q_{2}
.
To show this, we start with perturbations to ultra-local charges
\delta{q}_{0},\delta{q}_{1},\delta{q}_{2}
, see (
V.7
) and find

\delta{q}_{0}=\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{0,0}}\delta q_{0},\qquad\delta{q}_{1}=\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{1,1}}\delta q_{1},\qquad\delta{q}_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{C}_{2,2}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-\mathcal{C}_{2,0}^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{0,0}}}\delta q_{2}+\frac{\mathcal{C}_{2,0}}{\sqrt{C_{0,0}}}\delta q_{0}.

(VII.15)

Perturbations of ultra-local charges can be easily connected to perturbations of the fields
\delta\varrho,\delta u,\delta e
via relations (
II.7
) and (
II.21
)

\delta\varrho=\delta{q}_{0}\qquad\delta u=\frac{1}{\varrho}\delta{q}_{1}\qquad\delta e=\frac{1}{\varrho}\delta{q}_{2}-\frac{{q}_{2}}{\varrho^{2}}\delta{q}_{0}.

(VII.16)

Lastly, to go to
\delta\varrho,\delta u,\delta T
we need (
VI.2
). Combining all three transformations together with thermodynamic formulas from Sec.
IV
(in particular, (
IV.33
)), we find

\delta\varrho=\sqrt{T\varrho\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\varrho}\right)_{T}^{-1}}\,\delta q_{0}=\varrho\sqrt{T\varkappa_{T}}\,\delta q_{0},\qquad\delta u=\sqrt{T/\varrho}\,\delta q_{1},\qquad\delta T=T/\sqrt{\varrho c_{V}}\,\delta q_{2}.

(VII.17)

These relations are obviously equivalent to (
VII.13
). Summing up, we have shown that perturbations of charges
\delta q_{0},\delta q_{1},\delta_{2}
are equal to perturbations of rescaled hydrodynamic fields.

VII.2
Perturbation theory at order
k^{2}

We move now to calculations at the
k^{2}
order. As already seen in (
VI.9
), at this level we should expect transport coefficients
\zeta,\kappa
appearing in corrections to the heat and sound modes. The perturbation theory will provide us with explicit expressions for transport coefficients.

The corrections of the order
k^{2}
have two origins. There are second-order corrections coming from the coupling between the hydrodynamic modes and dispersive modes through the collision integral; we denote them
\omega_{\alpha}^{\rm coll}
. There are also corrections coming from the GHD diffusion denoted
\omega_{\alpha}^{\rm diff}
. Here
\alpha=\pm,{\rm th}
specifies one of the hydrodynamic modes. Thus, the general structure is

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm th}(k)

\displaystyle=\left(\omega_{\rm th}^{\rm diff}+\omega_{\rm th}^{\rm coll}\right)k^{2}+\mathcal{O}(k^{3}),

(VII.18)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm\pm}(k)

\displaystyle=\pm iv_{s}k+\left(\omega_{\rm\pm}^{\rm diff}+\omega_{\rm\pm}^{\rm coll}\right)k^{2}+\mathcal{O}(k^{3}).

The corrections due to the diffusion take a simple form. They are given by the first order perturbation theory in the diffusion operator
\omega_{\alpha}^{\rm diff}=\langle h_{\alpha}|D|h_{\alpha}\rangle
. Because the diffusion operator is zero between eigenstates of different parity and
|h_{0}\rangle
is its zero eigenvector (both left and right) there are only
2
non-zero matrix elements
D_{1,1}
and
D_{2,2}
. In terms of them we find

\displaystyle\omega_{\rm th}^{\rm diff}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{V}}{c_{P}}D_{2,2},

(VII.19)

\displaystyle\omega_{\pm}^{\rm diff}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}D_{1,1}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{c_{P}-c_{V}}{c_{P}}D_{2,2}.

Using the decomposition (
VII.10
) we also find

\omega_{\pm}^{\rm diff}=\frac{c_{P}-c_{V}}{2c_{V}}\omega_{\rm th}^{\rm diff}+\frac{1}{2}D_{1,1}.

(VII.20)

The second-order correction to the eigenvalues of the hydrodynamic modes come from the standard formula from the perturbation theory

\omega_{\alpha}^{\rm coll}=\sum_{\beta}\frac{\langle h_{\alpha}|A|g_{\beta}\rangle\langle g_{\beta}|A|h_{\alpha}\rangle}{\gamma_{\beta}},\qquad\alpha=\pm,{\rm th},

(VII.21)

where the sum does not include the kernel of operator
\Gamma
.
Inspecting the formula we find
\omega_{+}^{\rm coll}=\omega_{-}^{\rm coll}
. This follows from the fact, that
\Gamma
operator has eigenfunctions which are either symmetric or antisymmetric functions of rapidities.

Note that action of
A
on
|h_{0}\rangle
gives a mode proportional to
|h_{1}\rangle
(this can be verified directly at the level of matrix elements (
V.14
)) and therefore the middle term from the decomposition (
VII.10
) does not contribute to
\omega_{\pm}^{\rm coll}
because
\langle g_{\beta}|h_{1}\rangle=0
from the definition of
|g_{\beta}\rangle
. In the expression for
\omega_{\pm}^{\rm coll}
there appear also mixed terms of the form
\langle h_{\rm th}|A|g_{\beta}\rangle\langle g_{\beta}|A|h_{1}\rangle
. Such terms vanish because
A
is non-zero only for states of different parity. This allows us to write

\omega_{\pm}^{\rm coll}=\frac{c_{P}-c_{V}}{2c_{V}}\omega_{{\rm th}}^{\rm coll}+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{1}^{\rm coll},\qquad\omega_{1}^{\rm coll}=\sum_{\beta}\frac{(\langle g_{\beta}|A|h_{1}\rangle)^{2}}{\gamma_{\beta}}.

(VII.22)

.

VII.3
Transport coefficients

We can finally read off the transport coefficients by comparing spectra (
VI.9
) of the linearized Navier-Stokes with the results of the perturbation theory. We find

\displaystyle\kappa^{\rm coll}

\displaystyle=\varrho c_{P}\omega_{\rm th}^{\rm coll},\qquad\zeta^{\rm coll}=\varrho\omega_{1}^{\rm coll},

(VII.23)

\displaystyle\kappa^{\rm diff}

\displaystyle=\varrho c_{V}D_{2,2},\qquad\zeta^{\rm diff}=\varrho D_{1,1}.

with
\kappa=\kappa^{\rm coll}+\kappa^{\rm diff}
and
\zeta=\zeta^{\rm coll}+\zeta^{\rm diff}
.

We confirm these formulas on Fig.
1
, which presents small-
k
part of the spectrum found in numerical diagonalization of (
VII.2
). Our results agree with numerics in the samll-
k
regime, as expected. The dispersion relations for larger number of modes and for higher values of
k
are presented in Fig.
2
. For small
k
the spectrum consists of Navier-Stokes modes and gapped modes. For high enough
k
, the spectrum is those of unperturbed GHD. This corresponds to operator
\Gamma
being negligible in the eigenproblem (
VII.2
), as compared to
A
and
D
. In between, there is a region which cannot be described neither by Navier-Stokes hydrodynamics, nor the pure diffusive GHD. Therefore, by numerically solving (
VII.2
) we are able to quantatively capture the crossover from Navier-Stokes hydrodynamics to GHD.

Equations (
VII.23
) together with (
VII.22
) provide also an efficient way to evaluate the transport coefficients numerically. The procedure is the following. For a given thermal equilibrum state, we construct an orthonormal basis
\{|h_{i}\rangle\}
using the hydrodynamic scalar product
(f|g)
. This allows us to compute the matrix elements
A_{nm}
,
\Gamma_{nm}
and
D_{nm}
. The matrix
\Gamma_{nm}
can be now diagonalized numerically. We obtain the spectrum
\gamma_{\beta}
but also expressions for
|g_{\beta}\rangle
in terms of linear combinations of
|h_{i}\rangle
’s. This allows us to evaluate the matrix elements
\langle g_{\beta}|A|h_{\alpha}\rangle
and
\omega_{\alpha}^{\rm coll}
. The remaining ingredient is the heat capacity
c_{P}
which can be computed from the hydrodynamic matrices
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
according to (
IV.27
). The numerical solution requires truncating the infinite basis to a finite one. In Fig.
1
we show that it is sufficient to take around
20
basis functions.

Let us note that contributions
\kappa^{\rm diff}
and
\zeta^{\rm diff}
can be also written as matrix elements in the ultra-local basis, as in the main text. Using relations (
V.7
), properties of the diffusion kernel and adopting notation of matrix elements of diffusion operator (
V.14
) as in the main text we find that indeed

\kappa^{\rm diff}=\frac{1}{2T^{2}}{h}_{2}\mathfrak{D}\mathcal{C}{h}_{2},\qquad\zeta^{\rm diff}=\frac{1}{2T}{h}_{1}\mathfrak{D}\mathcal{C}{h}_{1}.

(VII.24)

Importantly
\kappa^{\rm diff},\zeta^{\rm diff}\geq 0
, due to the positivity of operator
\mathfrak{D}\mathcal{C}

[
26
]
. Moreover, it is easy to observe that
\omega_{1}^{\rm coll},\omega^{\rm coll}_{\rm th}\geq 0
and hence
\kappa^{\rm coll},\zeta^{\rm coll}\geq 0
. We thus conclude that transport coefficients found by us are non-negative, which assures that entropy production in the Navier-Stokes dynamics is positive
[
59
]
.

In the next section we will confirm that
\kappa^{\rm coll}
and
\zeta^{\rm coll}
found in the second-order perturbation theory (
VII.23
) can be written as generalized Chapman-Enskog expressions given in the main text.

VIII
Integral equations for
\kappa^{\rm coll}
and
\zeta^{\rm coll}
transport coefficients

Expressions for the collisional transport coefficients involve sums over all non-zero eigenstates of the
\Gamma
operator. In the following, we will show how they can be rewritten as integral equations, which are identical to the integral equations found in the Chapman-Enskog procedure.
We start with the thermal conductivity related to
\omega_{\rm th}^{\rm coll}
. As mentioned earlier for any
\beta
we have
\langle h_{0}|A|g_{\beta}\rangle=0
. Using explicit form (
VII.11
) of
|h_{\rm th}\rangle
in ultra-local basis we find

\langle h_{\rm th}|A|g_{\beta}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varrho T^{2}c_{P}}}\langle{h}_{2}|A|g_{\beta}\rangle,

(VIII.1)

and thus we have

\omega_{\rm th}^{\rm coll}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varrho T^{2}c_{P}}}\sum_{\beta}\frac{\langle{h}_{2}|A|g_{\beta}\rangle\langle g_{\beta}|A|h_{\rm th}\rangle}{\gamma_{\beta}}.

(VIII.2)

For the heat mode the sum can be easily extended to the whole space by noting that
A|h_{\rm th}\rangle
is orthogonal to the kernel of
\Gamma
. For
|h_{0}\rangle,|h_{2}\rangle
it is clear from symmetry properties of
A
. For
|h_{1}\rangle
we can show
\langle h_{1}|A|h_{\rm th}\rangle=0
, which be seen from explicit computation using (
VII.11
). This allows us to write

\omega_{\rm th}^{\rm coll}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varrho T^{2}c_{P}}}\langle{h}_{2}|A\Gamma^{-1}_{\epsilon}A|h_{\rm th}\rangle,

(VIII.3)

where we used the completeness of the basis which implies that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sum_{m}|g_{m}\rangle\frac{1}{\gamma_{m}-\varepsilon}\langle g_{m}|=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\Gamma-\varepsilon\mathcal{C}}\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Gamma^{-1}_{\epsilon},

(VIII.4)

where
m
extends now over all the eigenstates.
Now, we can formally introduce vector
|\phi_{\kappa}\rangle
such that,

\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-1}A|h_{\rm th}\rangle=\frac{T}{\sqrt{\varrho c_{P}}}|\phi_{\kappa}\rangle,\qquad\omega_{\rm th}^{\rm coll}=\frac{1}{\varrho c_{P}}\langle{h}_{2}|A|\phi_{\kappa}\rangle.

(VIII.5)

To find
|\phi_{\kappa}\rangle
we can go to quasiparticle representation. We look for a function
\eta_{\kappa}(\lambda)
which fulfills the following integral equation

\Gamma_{\epsilon}\phi_{\kappa}=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm p}f\eta_{\kappa},

(VIII.6)

with function
\eta_{\kappa}(\lambda)
related to heat mode and given by

\eta_{\kappa}(\lambda)=\frac{\sqrt{\varrho c_{P}}}{T}v(\lambda)h_{\rm th}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)=\frac{\sqrt{\varrho c_{V}}}{T}\left(h_{2}-\sqrt{\frac{c_{P}-c_{V}}{c_{V}}}h_{0}\right)^{\rm dr}(\lambda).

(VIII.7)

For
\epsilon\neq 0
, the operator
\Gamma_{\epsilon}
is invertible and the solution to (
VIII.6
) is unique. However, we would like to explicitly take the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, which will invalidate uniqueness of the solution. This is clear from the existence of collision invariants of
\Gamma
- for any particular solution
\bar{\phi}_{\kappa}
to the equation (
VIII.6
) with
\epsilon=0
the function

\bar{\phi}_{\kappa}+\sum_{i=0}^{2}a_{i}{h}_{i}

(VIII.8)

is an another solution (coefficients
a_{i}
are arbitrary). Therefore, before we set
\epsilon=0
, let us multiply (
VIII.6
) with collision invariants
{h}_{i}(\lambda)
where
i=0,1,2
and integrate over
\differential\lambda
. This leads to the conditions

\langle{h}_{i}|\mathcal{C}|\phi_{\kappa}\rangle=({h}_{i}|\phi_{\kappa})=0,

(VIII.9)

which we keep after taking
\epsilon\to 0
. The conditions above make the solution unique by fixing the constants
a_{i}
in the general solution.

In the main text we shorten the notation introducing
\chi_{\kappa}=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm p}f\eta_{\kappa}
, which explicitly reads

\chi_{\kappa}=\frac{\sqrt{\varrho c_{V}}}{T}\mathcal{B}(h_{2}-\sqrt{c_{P}/c_{V}-1}\,h_{0}).

(VIII.10)

The integral equation can be then written as
\Gamma\phi_{\kappa}=\chi_{\kappa}
, as in the main text. Finally, thermal conductivity
\kappa^{\rm coll}
is given by

\kappa^{\rm coll}=\int\differential\lambda\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)f(\lambda)v(\lambda){h}_{2}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)\phi_{\kappa}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)={h}_{2}\mathcal{B}\phi_{\kappa}.

(VIII.11)

We focus now on
\zeta^{\rm coll}
. We proceed similarly, with the exception that now
\langle g_{\beta}|A|h_{1}\rangle\neq 0
, therefore we have to properly regularize matrix elements before we introduce representation of inverse
\Gamma
. We start with expression

\langle g_{\beta}|A|h_{1}\rangle,

(VIII.12)

which enters the sum for
\zeta^{\rm coll}
. We can add to it two zero terms, namely:

\langle g_{\beta}|A|h_{1}\rangle+a\langle g_{\beta}|h_{0}\rangle+b\langle g_{\beta}|h_{2}\rangle.

(VIII.13)

We want
a,b
to be such that the vector
|w_{1}\rangle=A|h_{1}\rangle+a|h_{0}\rangle+b|h_{2}\rangle
is orthogonal to
|h_{0}\rangle,|h_{1}\rangle,|h_{2}\rangle
. We find
a=-A_{1,0},b=-A_{2,1}.
Thus, we write (we have used here that
|h_{1}\rangle=1/\sqrt{\varrho T}|{h}_{1}\rangle
, see (
V.7
) and Sec.
IV
)

\omega_{1}^{\rm coll}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varrho T}}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\langle{h}_{1}|A\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-1}|w_{1}\rangle.

(VIII.14)

As previously, we introduce a vector
|\phi_{\zeta}\rangle
such that
\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-1}|w_{1}\rangle=\sqrt{T/\varrho}|\phi_{\zeta}\rangle
. In quasiparticle representation finding such vector corresponds to solving the following integral equation

\Gamma_{\epsilon}\phi_{\zeta}=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm p}f\eta_{\zeta},

(VIII.15)

with function (we have used here (
IV.29
))

\eta_{\zeta}(\lambda)=\sqrt{\frac{\varrho}{T}}\left[v(\lambda)h_{1}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varrho\varkappa_{T}}}\left(h_{0}+\sqrt{\frac{c_{P}-c_{V}}{c_{V}}}h_{2}\right)^{\rm dr}(\lambda)\right].

(VIII.16)

Similarly as for thermal conductivity we set
\epsilon\to 0
by imposing additional conditions
({h}_{i}|\phi_{\zeta})=0,\,\,i=0,1,2
. We shorten the notation and introduce
\chi_{\zeta}=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}\rho_{\rm p}f\eta_{\zeta}
, which explicitly reads

\chi_{\zeta}=\sqrt{\frac{\varrho}{T}}\mathcal{B}h_{1}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{T\varkappa_{T}}}\,\mathcal{C}(h_{0}+\sqrt{c_{P}/c_{V}-1}\,h_{2}).

(VIII.17)

The integral equation is then
\Gamma\phi_{\zeta}=\chi_{\zeta}
and the bulk viscosity
\zeta^{\rm coll}
finally is

\zeta^{\rm coll}=\int\differential\lambda\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)f(\lambda)v(\lambda){h}_{1}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)\phi_{\zeta}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)={h}_{1}\mathcal{B}\phi_{\zeta}.

(VIII.18)

In this way, we have shown that results from perturbation theory can be reformulated as generalized Chapman-Enskog integral equations, given in the main text. One of the advantages of integral equation approach is simple analysis of the non-interacting limit of the underlying integrable model. This is done in the next section.

Non-interacting limit of transport coefficients

In this section we look more closely on the limit of non-interacting integrable model with trivial dressings. Transport coefficients (
VII.23
) have contributions from collision integral and from diffusion operator. In the non-interacting limit, diffusion operator vanishes together with the associated contributions to the transport coefficients. We thus turn to contributions from collision integral, which in principle may be non-zero even in the limit of free integrable model. Obviously, we still consider non-zero integrability breaking term
\mathcal{I}
.

In the case of trivial dressings, the following symmetries appear on the level of hydrodynamic matrices

\mathcal{B}_{1,0}=2C_{2,0},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{2,1}=2\mathcal{C}_{2,2}.

(VIII.19)

Let us look how these affect expressions for
\eta_{\kappa,\zeta}(\lambda)
given by (
VIII.7
), (
VIII.16
). It is convenient to represent these functions in the ultra-local basis first, we find

\displaystyle\eta_{\kappa}(\lambda)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{2}}v(\lambda)\left({h}_{2}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)-\frac{\mathcal{B}_{2,1}}{\mathcal{B}_{1,0}}{h}_{0}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)\right),

(VIII.20)

\displaystyle\eta_{\zeta}(\lambda)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\left(v(\lambda){h}^{\rm dr}_{1}(\lambda)+\frac{\mathcal{B}_{2,1}\mathcal{C}_{2,0}-\mathcal{B}_{1,0}\mathcal{C}_{2,2}}{\mathcal{C}_{2,2}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-\mathcal{C}_{2,0}^{2}}{h}_{0}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)-\frac{\mathcal{B}_{2,1}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-\mathcal{B}_{1,0}\mathcal{C}_{2,0}}{\mathcal{C}_{2,2}\mathcal{C}_{0,0}-\mathcal{C}_{2,0}^{2}}{h}_{2}^{\rm dr}(\lambda)\right),

(VIII.21)

where we have expressed thermodynamic quantities with hydrodynamic matrices, see Sec.
IV
. Moreover, we have represented GS-orthonormalized vectors (
V.7
) with ultra-local counterparts. Let us consider a limit, where dressing can be neglected and hence
v(\lambda)={h}_{1}(\lambda)
. Taking into account also the symmetries (
VIII.19
), we observe

\displaystyle\eta_{\kappa}(\lambda)\to

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{T^{2}}{h}_{1}(\lambda)\left({h}_{2}(\lambda)-\frac{\mathcal{B}_{2,1}}{\mathcal{B}_{1,0}}{h}_{0}(\lambda)\right),

(VIII.22)

\displaystyle\eta_{\zeta}(\lambda)\to

\displaystyle\,0.

(VIII.23)

The rate on which
\eta_{\zeta}(\lambda)
approaches zero with vanishing coupling depends on the model. This might be important in cases where
\Gamma
vanishes in the free limit as well. When
\eta_{\zeta}(\lambda)
approaches zero faster than
\Gamma
with the interaction coupling, the bulk viscosity tends to 0. This is the case for collision integral studied by us. On the other hand, as
\eta_{\kappa}(\lambda)
remains finite in the non-interacting limit, the
\Gamma
operator, which vanishes in the free limit leads then to infinite thermal conductivity. This is a known result for non-interacting gas
[
59
]
and we observe it also for collision integral considered by us (see Fig. 2 in the main text).

IX
Collision integral for the coupled Lieb-Liniger models

In this supplementary material we derive the linearized collision integral for the two coupled Lieb-Liniger models. The tubes are coupled with intertube interaction potential
V_{\rm inter}(x-y)=V_{0}\,V(x-y).
The resulting collision integral has all the necessary properties which, we have assumed about the
\Gamma
operator and will allow us to illustrate our general formalism in an experimentally relevant situation.

Generally speaking there are two contributions to the collision integral
\mathcal{I}
. The direct contributions comes from the very scattering events caused by the perturbing potential. But there is also an indirect contribution, that arises because a modification to the distribution of rapidities in one place (in the rapidity space) reshuffles rapidities elsewhere due to the interaction present in the Lieb-Liniger model. The two contributions together appear as a dressing of the bare scattering integral
\mathcal{I}_{0}

\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\rm{p},1},\rho_{\rm{p},2}](\lambda)=\tau_{0}^{-1}\int\differential\mu\mathbb{F}(\lambda,\mu)\mathcal{I}_{0}[\rho_{\rm{p},1},\rho_{\rm{p},2}](\mu),

(IX.1)

where
\mathbb{F}
is backflow operator introduced in (
V.18
) and timescale
\tau_{0}^{-1}=16E_{F}/(\hbar\pi^{2})\times\gamma_{\rm inter}^{2}=\frac{2V_{0}^{2}m}{\hbar^{3}}
where
E_{F}
is Fermi energy of a single tube and
\gamma_{\rm inter}=V_{0}m/(\varrho\hbar^{2})
where
\varrho
is the density of the gas
[
30
]
.

As discussed in
[
30
,
46
]
in the homogeneous case when two tubes are in the same state the leading contribution to the collision integral comes from processes of type
(1,2)
and
(2,1)
. These are processes in which
1
particle-hole pair is created in the first tube and
2
particle-hole pair is created in the second tube or
vice verse
,
\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\rm{p}}]=\mathcal{I}^{(1,2)}[\rho_{\rm{p}}]+\mathcal{I}^{(2,1)}[\rho_{\rm{p}}]+(\dots)
, where
(\dots)
stands for processes of higher order in momentum transferred between the tubes.

The bare collision integral for these processes is
[
30
,
46
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}[\rho_{\rm p}](\lambda)=

\displaystyle\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{(2!)^{2}}\int\differential p_{1,1}\differential h_{1,1}\differential p_{1,2}\differential h_{1,2}\differential p_{2}\differential h_{2}\,\left(\delta(\lambda-p_{1,1})+\delta(\lambda-p_{1,2})+\delta(\lambda-p_{2})\right)\,\tilde{V}^{2}\big(k_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})\big)\times

(IX.2)

\displaystyle|F_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})|^{2}|F_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})|^{2}\,\delta\big(k_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})+k_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})\big)\,\delta\big(\omega_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})+\omega_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})\big)\times

\displaystyle\Big(J_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})\,J_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})-(\mathbf{h}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{p})\Big),

where
\tilde{V}(k)
is Fourier transform of the interaction potential with
\tilde{V}(k)=\tilde{V}(-k)
,
F_{n\rm ph}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{h})
are density operator form factors and

k_{n\rm ph}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{h})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(k(p_{i})-k(h_{i})\right),\qquad\omega_{n\rm ph}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{h})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\omega(p_{i})-\omega(h_{i})\right).

(IX.3)

where
k(\lambda)={h}_{1}^{\rm Dr}(\lambda)
and
\omega(\lambda)={h}_{2}^{\rm Dr}(\lambda)
. Finally,
J
factors are

J_{n\rm ph}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{h})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{\rm p}(h_{i})\rho_{\rm h}(p_{i}).

(IX.4)

We linearize now the collision integral around a thermal state. Any thermal state is a stationary state fulfilling the condition

J_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})\,J_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})-J_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{1})\,J_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{h}_{2},\mathbf{p}_{2})=0,

(IX.5)

on a
4
-dimensional manifold given by
\omega_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})+\omega_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})=0
and
k_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})+k_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})=0
.
To expand the collision integral around the thermal state it is useful to switch from the distribution functions to the pseudoenergy
\epsilon(\lambda)
for which we have

\rho_{\rm p}(\lambda)=\frac{\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda)}{1+e^{\epsilon(\lambda)}},\qquad\rho_{\rm h}(\lambda)=\frac{\rho_{\rm tot}(\lambda)}{1+e^{-\epsilon(\lambda)}}.

(IX.6)

The ratio of the
J
-factors has then a simple form

\frac{J_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})\,J_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})}{J_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{1})\,J_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{h}_{2},\mathbf{p}_{2})}=\exp\left(\epsilon(p_{1,1})-\epsilon(h_{1,1})+\epsilon(p_{1,2})-\epsilon(h_{1,2})+\epsilon(p_{2,1})-\epsilon(h_{2,1})\right),

(IX.7)

and the argument of the exponential vanishes for thermal states for which
\epsilon(\lambda)=\beta_{2}\omega(\lambda)+\beta_{0}
.

To linearize the collision integral we consider a deviation from the thermal state
\epsilon\to\epsilon+\delta\epsilon
and look at
\mathcal{I}[\epsilon+\delta\epsilon]
. To understand the structure of
\mathcal{I}[\epsilon+\delta\epsilon]
we analyze first
\epsilon+\delta\epsilon
. From (
III.8
) we conclude that

\epsilon^{\prime}(\lambda)=(\epsilon_{0}^{\prime})^{\rm dr}(\lambda).

(IX.8)

Using now the relation
[
70
]
between large and small dressing operations
(f^{\rm Dr})^{\prime}(\lambda)=(f^{\prime})^{\rm dr}(\lambda)
and integrating we find

\epsilon(\lambda)=(\epsilon_{0})^{\rm Dr}(\lambda)+const

(IX.9)

The same reasoning can be applied to the perturbed state, we get

\epsilon(\lambda)+\delta\epsilon(\lambda)+\mathcal{O}((\delta\epsilon_{0})^{2})=(\epsilon_{0}+\delta\epsilon_{0})^{{\rm Dr}+\delta{\rm Dr}}(\lambda)+const,

(IX.10)

where by
{\rm Dr}+\delta{\rm Dr}
we denoted Dressing implemented by the state with occupation function
n+\delta n
. The expression above may be expanded according to

\epsilon(\lambda)+\delta\epsilon(\lambda)=\epsilon_{0}^{Dr+\delta Dr}(\lambda)+(\delta\epsilon_{0})^{Dr+\delta Dr}+const=\epsilon_{0}^{Dr+\delta Dr}(\lambda)+(\delta\epsilon_{0})^{Dr}(\lambda)+const+O((\delta\epsilon_{0})^{2}).

(IX.11)

With this, we evaluate
\mathcal{I}[\epsilon+\delta\epsilon]
. The result is

\mathcal{I}_{0}[\rho]=\Gamma_{0}[\rho_{\rm th}](\delta\epsilon_{0})^{\rm Dr}+\mathcal{O}((\delta\epsilon)^{2}),

(IX.12)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(\lambda,\mu)=

\displaystyle\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{8}\int\differential p_{1,1}\differential h_{1,1}\differential p_{1,2}\differential h_{1,2}\differential p_{2}\differential h_{2}\,G(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})\times

(IX.13)

\displaystyle\Big(\delta(\lambda-p_{1,1})-\delta(\lambda-h_{1,1})+\delta(\lambda-p_{1,2})-\delta(\lambda-h_{1,2})+\delta(\lambda-p_{2})-\delta(\lambda-h_{2})\Big)\times

\displaystyle\Big(\delta(\mu-p_{1,1})-\delta(\mu-h_{1,1})+\delta(\mu-p_{1,2})-\delta(\mu-h_{1,2})+\delta(\mu-p_{2})-\delta(\mu-h_{2})\Big),

with

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})=

\displaystyle\tilde{V}^{2}\big(k_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})\big)|F_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})|^{2}|F_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})|^{2}J_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})\,J_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})\times

\displaystyle\delta\big(k_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})+k_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})\big)\,\delta\big(\omega_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})+\omega_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})\big).

(IX.14)

Note that
G(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})=G(\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{2},\mathbf{p}_{2})
, namely function
G
is
\rm ph
-symmetric. This implies that operator
\Gamma_{0}
is symmetric,
\Gamma_{0}(\lambda,\mu)=\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\lambda)
leading to
\Gamma_{mn}=\Gamma_{nm}
. It is also easy to observe that
\Gamma_{nn}\geq 0
, hence
\Gamma
is positive semi-definite.

What is more,
\Gamma_{0}
has an additional symmetry. By performing change of variables in the integrals (changing sign in each one) we get
\Gamma_{0}(\lambda,\mu)=\Gamma_{0}(-\lambda,-\mu)
. This property leads to certain selection rules once we consider the action of
\Gamma
in the basis of ultra-local conserved charges.

The linearized collision integral has also three left eigenvectors with zero eigenvalues
[
46
]
. They reflect the fact that the total number of particles, the total momentum and the total energy are conserved by the perturbation,

\int{\rm d}\lambda{h}_{0}^{\rm Dr}(\lambda)\Gamma_{0}(\lambda,\mu)=\int{\rm d}\lambda{h}_{1}^{\rm Dr}(\lambda)\Gamma_{0}(\lambda,\mu)=\int{\rm d}\lambda{h}_{2}^{\rm Dr}(\lambda)\Gamma_{0}(\lambda,\mu)=0,

(IX.15)

where to derive the last two properties we used conservation laws under the integral.

We consider now matrix elements of
\Gamma
in the basis of the ultra-local conserved charges. The matrix elements are defined according to eq. (
V.14
),

\Gamma_{nm}=\tau_{0}^{-1}\int\differential\lambda\differential\mu\,h_{n}^{\rm Dr}(\lambda)\Gamma_{0}(\lambda,\mu)h_{m}^{\rm Dr}(\mu).

(IX.16)

From the construction of our basis (see Sec.
V
), functions
h_{n}
are either symmetric or antisymmetric (and dressings do not change this property). This implies that
\Gamma_{nm}=0
whenever functions
h_{n}
and
h_{m}
have different parity. According to the above discussion on the zero eigenvectors we also have
\Gamma_{nm}=0
for
n=0,1,2
. Moreover, it is straightforward to observe that
\Gamma_{nm}=0
for
m=0,1,2
as well.

Small momentum limit

The matrix elements
\Gamma_{nm}
read explicitly

\displaystyle\Gamma_{nm}=

\displaystyle\tau_{0}^{-1}\times\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{8}\int\differential p_{1,1}\differential h_{1,1}\differential p_{1,2}\differential h_{1,2}\differential p_{2}\differential h_{2}\,G(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})\times

(IX.17)

\displaystyle\Big(h_{n}^{\rm Dr}(p_{1,1})-h_{n}^{\rm Dr}(h_{1,1})+h_{n}^{\rm Dr}(p_{1,2})-h_{n}^{\rm Dr}(h_{1,2})+h_{n}^{\rm Dr}(p_{2})-h_{n}^{\rm Dr}(h_{2})\Big)\times

\displaystyle\Big(h_{m}^{\rm Dr}(p_{1,1})-h_{m}^{\rm Dr}(h_{1,1})+h_{m}^{\rm Dr}(p_{1,2})-h_{m}^{\rm Dr}(h_{1,2})+h_{m}^{\rm Dr}(p_{2})-h_{m}^{\rm Dr}(h_{2})\Big).

We will find now a much simpler expression for them in the small momentum limit.
To this end we express the integrand in terms of center of mass rapidities and the momentum transferred between the tubes.

First, we change the integration variables to the center-of-mass rapidities. These are defined through
\lambda=(p+h)/2
and
\alpha=p-h
for each particle-hole excitation. The Jacobian of the transformation from
(p,h)
to
(\lambda,\alpha)
is
1
hence this amounts to simply replacing the integration measure by
\differential\lambda_{1,1}\differential\lambda_{1,2}\differential\lambda_{2}\differential\alpha_{1,1}\differential\alpha_{1,2}\differential\alpha_{2}
. We then assume that the main contribution to the integral comes from the small momentum excitations. Therefore, the integrand can be expanded in small
\alpha
’s. Specifically, the energy-momentum constraints become

k^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,1})\alpha_{1,1}+k^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,2})\alpha_{1,2}+k^{\prime}(\lambda_{2})\alpha_{2}=0,

(IX.18a)

\omega^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,1})\alpha_{1,1}+\omega^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,2})\alpha_{1,2}+\omega^{\prime}(\lambda_{2})\alpha_{2}=0.

(IX.18b)

Solving them for
\alpha_{1,1}
and
\alpha_{1,2}
gives

\displaystyle\bar{\alpha}_{1,1}=-\alpha_{2}\frac{k^{\prime}(\lambda_{2})}{k^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,1})}\frac{v(\lambda_{2})-v(\lambda_{1,2})}{v(\lambda_{1,1})-v(\lambda_{1,2})},\qquad\bar{\alpha}_{1,2}=-\alpha_{2}\frac{k^{\prime}(\lambda_{2})}{k^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,2})}\frac{v(\lambda_{2})-v(\lambda_{1,1})}{v(\lambda_{1,2})-v(\lambda_{1,1})}.

(IX.19)

We can now use the following property of
\delta

\delta(f(x,y))\delta(g(x,y))=\frac{\delta(x-\bar{x})\delta(y-\bar{y})}{|\det J|},\qquad J=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}f(x,y)&\partial_{y}f(x,y)\\
\partial_{x}g(x,y)&\partial_{y}g(x,y)\end{pmatrix},

(IX.20)

where
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a zero of
(f(x,y),g(x,y))
. This gives for the
\delta

\delta\big(k_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})+k_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})\big)\,\delta\big(\omega_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})+\omega_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})\big)=\frac{\delta(\alpha_{1,1}-\bar{\alpha}_{1,1})\delta(\alpha_{1,2}-\bar{\alpha}_{1,2})}{k^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,1})k^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,2})|v(\lambda_{1,1})-v(\lambda_{1,2})|}.

(IX.21)

After these preparational steps we evaluate the integrals over
\alpha_{1,1}
and
\alpha_{1,2}
with the help of the Dirac
\delta

\displaystyle\Gamma_{nm}=

\displaystyle\tau_{0}^{-1}\times\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{8}\int\differential\lambda_{1,1}\differential\lambda_{1,2}\differential\lambda_{2}\differential\alpha_{2}\,\frac{G(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})}{k^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,1})k^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,2})|v(\lambda_{1,1})-v(\lambda_{1,2})|}\times

(IX.22)

\displaystyle\Big((h_{n}^{\rm Dr})^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,1})\bar{\alpha}_{1,1}+(h_{n}^{\rm Dr})^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,2})\bar{\alpha}_{1,2}+(h_{n}^{\rm Dr})^{\prime}(\lambda_{2})\alpha_{2}\Big)\times

\displaystyle\Big((h_{m}^{\rm Dr})^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,1})\bar{\alpha}_{1,1}+(h_{m}^{\rm Dr})^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,2})\bar{\alpha}_{1,2}+(h_{m}^{\rm Dr})^{\prime}(\lambda_{2})\alpha_{2}\Big).

The second step is to evaluate the integral over
\alpha_{2}
which is proportional to the momentum transferred between the tubes. To this end we observe that the combinations of functions appearing in the last two lines of
\Gamma_{nm}

\bar{\alpha}_{1,1}\varphi(\lambda_{1,1})+\bar{\alpha}_{1,2}\varphi(\lambda_{1,2})+\alpha_{2}\varphi(\lambda_{2})=\frac{\alpha_{2}k^{\prime}(\lambda_{2})}{v(\lambda_{1,1})-v(\lambda_{1,2})}\mathcal{S}[\varphi],

\mathcal{S}[\varphi]=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,1})}{k^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,1})}(v(\lambda_{1,2})-v(\lambda_{2}))+\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,2})}{k^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,2})}(v(\lambda_{2})-v(\lambda_{1,1}))+\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda_{2})}{k^{\prime}(\lambda_{2})}(v(\lambda_{1,1})-v(\lambda_{1,2})).

(IX.24)

We note that
\mathcal{S}[\varphi]
is zero whenever any two of the
3

\lambda_{1,1}
,
\lambda_{1,2}
or
\lambda_{2}
are equal. Moreover, it also vanishes identically for constant

In the limit of small momentum transfer between the tubes we can also simplify function

. The density factors and the form-factors are independent of
\alpha_{2}
in this limit. The density factors are

while the form-factors yield

,
68
,

]

\displaystyle F_{1\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})

\displaystyle=k^{\prime}(\lambda_{2}),

(IX.25)

\displaystyle F_{2\rm ph}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi k^{2}\mathcal{T}^{\rm dr}(\lambda_{1,1},\lambda_{1,2})}{k^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,1})k^{\prime}(\lambda_{1,2})\bar{\alpha}_{1,1}\bar{\alpha}_{1,2}}=-2\pi\mathcal{T}^{\rm dr}(\lambda_{1,1},\lambda_{1,2})\frac{(v(\lambda_{1,1})-v(\lambda_{1,2}))^{2}}{(v(\lambda_{2})-v(\lambda_{1,2}))(v(\lambda_{2})-v(\lambda_{1,1}))},
