Abstract
. We show that the one-parameter family of special solutions of P II , the second Painlevé equation, constructed from the Airy functions, as well as associated solutions of P XXXIV and S II , can be expressed via the recurrence coefficients of orthogonal polynomials that appear in the analysis of the Hermitian random matrix ensemble with a cubic potential. Exploiting this connection we show that solutions of P II that depend only on the first Airy function Ai \mathrm{Ai} (but not on Bi \mathrm{Bi} ) possess a scaling limit in the pole free region, which includes a disk around the origin whose radius grows with the parameter. We then use the scaling limit to show that these solutions are monotone in the parameter on the negative real axis.

1.
Introduction

At the beginning of the 20th century, it was shown by Painlevé and Gambier that among the second order differential equations of the form

q^{\prime\prime}=F(q^{\prime},q,z),

where
F
is rational in
q,q^{\prime}
and analytic in
z
, there are exactly
50
canonical equations whose solutions do not possess movable branch points. Among these,
44
can either be reduced to linear equations, equations solved in terms of elliptic functions, or to the remaining
6
equations. In this work we are interested in what is now known as the second Painlevé equation, see (
2
). It was shown by Gambier
[
18
]
that P
II
has a one-parameter family of solutions that are expressible in terms of the Airy functions and their derivatives. These special function solutions appear in many contexts. For example, in Hermitian random matrix ensembles as the asymptotic analysis of eigenvalue behavior (in a suitable double scaling limit as the size of the matrices tends to infinity and near the edge of the spectrum) makes use of a correlation kernel that in particular cases can be written in terms of Airy solutions of P
II
and P
XXXIV
, see
[
21
]
. Another appearance of these functions in random matrix theory comes from Hermitian ensembles with cubic potential. The cubic random matrix model, given by the probability distribution

\tilde{Z}_{N}^{-1}(u)e^{-N\mathrm{Tr}(M^{2}/2-uM^{3})}dM

on
N\times N
Hermitian matrices
M
, where
u
is a parameter, has been investigated in physical literature by Brézin, Itzykson, Parisi, and Zuber
[
9
]
and Bessis, Itzykson, and Zuber
[
4
]
. There it was recognized that the free energy of this model must possess an expansion in the parameter
N
. The
N^{-2g}
coefficient of this expansion, as a function of
u
, possesses a Taylor series around the origin, whose coefficients count the number of the three-valent graphs on a Riemann surface of genus
g
. These considerations were later made rigorous by the middle two authors of the present work
[
5
]
. Further investigations of this model were carried out by Huybrechs, Kuijlaars, and Lejon in
[
20
,
19
]
and by the authors in
[
6
,
7
,
3
]
, see also
[
1
,
2
]
for the numerical studies. The main tool of investigation in these works was the connection to non-Hermitian orthogonal polynomials with respect to the cubic weight
\exp(-z^{3}/3+tz)
(parameter
t
is connected to the parameter
u
), whose moments can be expressed via the Airy functions
\mathrm{Ai}(z)
and
\mathrm{Bi}(z)
as well as their derivatives. In this work we utilize this connection to show what the results of
[
7
]
can say about the solutions of P
II
that depend only on
\mathrm{Ai}(z)
and its derivatives. The techniques of
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
3
]
can be extended to study all special solutions and we shall do so in a subsequent publication.

2.
Airy Solutions of P
II

Each of the Painlevé equations P
I
–P
VI
can be written as a Hamiltonian system

\frac{dq}{dz}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{dp}{dz}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q},

see
[
24
,
23
,
22
]
. In the case of P
II
, the Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{II}}
is equal to

H_{\mathrm{II}}(q,p,z;\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}p^{2}-\left(q^{2}+\frac{z}{2}\right)p-\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\right)q,

where
\alpha
is a parameter. Hence, the Hamiltonian system becomes

(1)

q^{\prime}=p-q^{2}-z/2\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptp^{\prime}=2pq+\alpha+1/2.

Eliminating
p
from (
1
) gives P
II
:

(2)

q^{\prime\prime}=2q^{3}+zq+\alpha,

while eliminating
q
from (
1
) yields P
XXXIV
:

(3)

p^{\prime\prime}=\frac{(p^{\prime})^{2}-(\alpha+1/2)^{2}}{2p}+2p^{2}-zp.

Notice that the first equation (
1
) allows one to express
p
through
q,q^{\prime}
and
z
while the second equation allows to express
q
through
p,p^{\prime}
. These connection formulae provide one-to-one correspondence between solutions of P
II
and P
XXXIV
. Morever, if
p_{\alpha+1/2}(z),q_{\alpha+1/2}(z)
are solutions of (
1
) and

(4)

\sigma_{\alpha+1/2}(z):=H_{\mathrm{II}}\big(q_{\alpha+1/2}(z),p_{\alpha+1/2}(z),z;\alpha\big),

then this function solves S
II
, the Jimbo-Miwa-Okamoto
\sigma
-form of P
II
:

(5)

(\sigma^{\prime\prime})^{2}+4(\sigma^{\prime})^{3}+2\sigma^{\prime}(z\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)=(\alpha/2+1/4)^{2}.

Conversely, if
\sigma_{\alpha+1/2}(z)
solves (
5
), then the functions

(6)

q_{\alpha+1/2}(z)=\frac{2\sigma_{\alpha+1/2}^{\prime\prime}(z)+\alpha+1/2}{4\sigma_{\alpha+1/2}^{\prime}(z)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptp_{\alpha+1/2}(z)=-2\sigma_{\alpha+1/2}^{\prime}(z),

solve (
2
) and (
3
), respectively, see
[
24
,
23
,
22
,
25
]
.

It is known from the work of Gambier, see
[
18
]
, that (
2
), and therefore (
3
) and (
5
) via the correspondence (
1
), (
4
), and (
6
), has a one-parameter family of solutions expressible through the Airy functions and their derivatives if and only if
\alpha+1/2
is an integer. Recall that the standard Airy functions can be given by their integral representations

(7)

\mathrm{Ai}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{L_{1}-L_{2}}e^{-V(s;z)}ds,\hskip 10.00002ptV(s;z):=-\frac{s^{3}}{3}+sz,

where
L_{k}:=\big\{xe^{\pi\mathrm{i}(-1+2k/3)}:x\in(0,\infty)\big\}
,
k\in\{0,1,2\}
, are rays oriented towards the origin, and

(8)

\mathrm{Bi}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\int_{L_{0}-L_{1}}+\int_{L_{0}-L_{2}}\right)e^{-V(s;z)}ds.

When
\alpha=1/2
, it can be shown that (
2

q_{1}(z;\lambda)=-\frac{d}{dz}\log\left(C_{1}\,\mathrm{Ai}\left(-2^{-1/3}z\right)+C_{2}\,\mathrm{Bi}\left(-2^{-1/3}z\right)\right),

where
\lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}
and

are any numbers such that
C_{2}/C_{1}=\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
C_{1}=0
when
\lambda=\infty
(the branch of the logarithm is not important as we immediately take

derivative). The solutions

corresponding to
\alpha=n-1/2
,
n\geq 1

q_{n+1}(z;\lambda)=-q_{n}(z;\lambda)-\frac{2n}{2q_{n}^{2}(z;\lambda)+2q_{n}^{\prime}(z;\lambda)^{2}+z}.

The solutions for non-positive values of
n
are then obtained via
q_{n}(z;\lambda)=-q_{-n+1}(z;\lambda)
. However, the above formula is not the most convenient way of expressing these solutions. To this end, define
\tau_{0}(z;\lambda)\equiv 1
and

(9)

\tau_{n}(z;\lambda):=\mathrm{det}\left[\frac{d^{j+k}}{dz^{j+k}}\left(C_{1}\,\mathrm{Ai}\left(-2^{-1/3}z\right)+C_{2}\,\mathrm{Bi}\left(-2^{-1/3}z\right)\right)\right]_{j,k=0}^{n-1}

when
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, see
[

,
25
,
10
]
, the solutions
q_{n}(z;\lambda)

n\geq 1
, and the corresponding functions

(10)

\begin{cases}q_{n}(z;\lambda)&\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dz}\log\frac{\tau_{n-1}(z;\lambda)}{\tau_{n}(z;\lambda)},\\
p_{n}(z;\lambda)&\displaystyle=-2\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}\log\tau_{n}(z;\lambda),\\
\sigma_{n}(z;\lambda)&\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dz}\log\tau_{n}(z;\lambda).\end{cases}

3.
Complex Cubic Ensemble of Random Matrices

Consider the unitary ensemble of random matrices with the cubic potential

V(M)=-\frac{1}{3}M^{3}+tM,

where
t
is a complex parameter. The partition function of this ensemble is formally defined by the matrix integral over the space of
N\times N
Hermitian matrices:

\int_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}e^{-N\mathrm{Tr}\left(-\frac{1}{3}M^{3}+tM\right)}dM.

Then the formal partition functions of the eigenvalues becomes

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ldots\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(s_{j}-s_{k})^{2}\,\prod_{j=1}^{N}e^{-NV(s_{j};t)}ds_{1}\ldots ds_{N},

where
V(s;t)
was defined above in (
7
). This expression is formal because the integrals are divergent and need regularization. To achieve it, let

\Gamma=\Gamma(\lambda):=\alpha_{0}L_{0}+\alpha_{1}L_{1}+\alpha_{2}L_{2},

where the rays
L_{k}
were defined right after (
7
) and
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
are complex parameters such that

(11)

\alpha_{0}=\frac{\lambda}{\pi},\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha_{1}=-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha_{2}=-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}},

when
|\lambda|<\infty
and
\alpha_{0}=1/\pi
,
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=-1/2\pi
when
\lambda=\infty
. Let

Z_{N}(t):=\int_{\Gamma}\ldots\int_{\Gamma}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(s_{j}-s_{k})^{2}\,\prod_{j=1}^{N}e^{-NV(s_{j};t)}ds_{1}\ldots ds_{N},

which is well defined (the integrals are convergent) for all values
t\in\mathbb{C}
, where we understand
\int_{\Gamma}
as
\sum\alpha_{k}\int_{\Gamma_{k}}
. As shown in
[
5
]
, the topological expansion of the free energy

F_{N}(t)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\log Z_{N}(t)

of the cubic ensemble of random matrices is connected to the enumeration of regular graphs of degree 3 on Riemann surfaces. The partition function
Z_{N}(t)
can also be expressed as

(12)

Z_{N}(t)=N!D_{N-1}(t,N),\hskip 10.00002ptD_{n}(t,N):=\left[\int_{\Gamma}s^{i+j}e^{-NV(s;t)}ds\right]_{i,j=0}^{n}.

The entries of the matrix defining
D_{n}(-t,N)
can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}s^{k}e^{-NV(s;-t)}ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{(k+1)/3}}\int_{\Gamma}s^{k}e^{-V\left(s;-N^{2/3}t\right)}ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{k+1/3}}\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\left(\int_{\Gamma}e^{-V\left(s;-N^{2/3}t\right)}ds\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{k+1/3}}\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\left(\alpha_{0}\pi\,\mathrm{Bi}\left(-N^{2/3}t\right)+(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})\pi\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{Ai}\left(-N^{2/3}t\right)\right),

where we used expressions (
7
) and (
8
) as well as the condition
\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=0
. That is, upon setting
z=(\sqrt{2}N)^{2/3}t
, we get that

\int_{\Gamma}s^{k}e^{-NV(s;-t)}ds=\frac{2^{k/3}}{N^{(k+1)/3}}\frac{d^{k}}{dz^{k}}\left(\alpha_{0}\pi\,\mathrm{Bi}\left(-2^{-1/3}z\right)+(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})\pi\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{Ai}\left(-2^{-1/3}z\right)\right).

This formula and the Hankel structure of the determinant
D_{n}(-t,N)
then imply that

(13)

D_{n}(-t,N)=\frac{2^{n(n+1)/3}}{N^{(n+1)(n+2)/3}}\tau_{n+1}(z;\lambda),\hskip 10.00002ptz=(\sqrt{2}N)^{2/3}t,

where
\tau_{n}(z;\lambda)
was defined in (
9
). In particular, if we take
n=N
, the last two representations in (
10
) can be rewritten in terms of the free energy
F_{N}(t)
as

\begin{cases}p_{N}(z;\lambda)&\displaystyle=-2N^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}F_{N}\left(-(\sqrt{2}N)^{-2/3}z\right),\\
\sigma_{N}(z;\lambda)&\displaystyle=N^{2}\frac{d}{dz}F_{N}\left(-(\sqrt{2}N)^{-2/3}z\right).\end{cases}

The cases of particular interest to us in this note are
\lambda=0,-\mathrm{i},\mathrm{i}
. These cases are essentially the same as they correspond to taking the seed function in (
9
) to be

\mathrm{Ai}\left(-2^{-1/3}z\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{Ai}\left(-2^{-1/3}ze^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{Ai}\left(-2^{-1/3}ze^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}\right)

since
\mathrm{Ai}(z)\pm\mathrm{i}\mathrm{Bi}(z)=2e^{\pm 2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}\mathrm{Ai}(ze^{\mp 2\pi\mathrm{i}/3})
, see
[
13
, Equation (9.2.1)]
. It is worth pointing out that, in these special cases, the functions
\tau_{n+1}
also appear in the study of the scaling limits of the largest eigenvalue in the GUE ensemble, see
[
17
, Proposition 28]
.

The above representations can be further rewritten utilizing the connection to orthogonal polynomials. Let
P_{n}(s;t,N)
be a non-identically zero polynomial of degree at most
n
such that

(14)

\int_{\Gamma}s^{k}P_{n}(s;t,N)e^{-NV(s;t)}ds=0,\hskip 10.00002ptk\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}.

Due to the non-Hermitian character of the above relations, it might happen that a polynomial satisfying (
14
) is non-unique. In this case we understand by
P_{n}(z;t,N)
the monic non-identically zero polynomial of the smallest degree (such polynomial is always unique). The standard determinantal representation of orthogonal polynomials yields that

(15)

h_{n}(t,N):=\int_{\Gamma}P_{n}^{2}(s;t,N)e^{-NV(s;t)}ds=\frac{D_{n}(t,N)}{D_{n-1}(t,N)},

where
D_{-1}(t,N)\equiv 1
. Observe that
D_{n}(t,N)
is an entire function of
t
and therefore each
h_{n}(t,N)
is meromorphic in
\mathbb{C}
. Hence, given
n
, the set of the values
t
for which there exists
k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
such that
h_{k}(t,N)=0
is countable with no limit points in the finite plane. Outside of this set the standard argument using (
14
) shows that

(16)

sP_{n}(s;t,N)=P_{n+1}(s;t,N)+\beta_{n}(t,N)P_{n}(s;t,N)+\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,N)P_{n-1}(s;t,N),

and by analytic continuation this relation extends to those values of
t
for which we have that
D_{n-1}(t,N)D_{n}(t,N)\neq 0
(that is,
n+1
-st and
n
-th polynomials appearing in (
16
) have the prescribed degrees), where

(17)

\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,N)=\frac{h_{n}(t,N)}{h_{n-1}(t,N)}.

Finally, denote by
p_{n,n-1}(t,N)
the coefficient of
P_{n}(s;t,N)
next to
s^{n-1}
, where
p_{0,-1}(t,N):=0
. It easily follows from (
16
) that

(18)

\beta_{n}(t,N)=p_{n,n-1}(t,N)-p_{n+1,n}(t,N).

In what follows, when it is important to us to stress that the quantities appearing in (
12
), (
14
), (

), (

), and (

) depend on
\lambda
, we shall use superscript
(\lambda)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix
N\geq 1
. Given
\lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}
, it holds for each
n\geq 1
that

(19)

\begin{cases}q_{n}(z;\lambda)&\displaystyle=-(N/2)^{1/3}\,\beta_{n-1}^{(\lambda)}\left(-(\sqrt{2}N)^{-2/3}z,N\right),\\
p_{n}(z;\lambda)&\displaystyle=-2(N/2)^{2/3}\,\gamma_{n}^{(\lambda)}\left(-(\sqrt{2}N)^{-2/3}z,N\right)^{2},\\
\sigma_{n}(z;\lambda)&\displaystyle=-(N/2)^{1/3}\,p_{n,n-1}^{(\lambda)}\left(-(\sqrt{2}N)^{-2/3}z,N\right).\end{cases}

Proof.

Since
\lambda,N
are fixed, we do not indicate the dependence on them of all the quantities related to orthogonal polynomials. A combination of (
10
), (
13
), and (
15
) immediately yields that

q_{n}(z;\lambda)=-\frac{d}{dz}\log h_{n-1}\left(-(\sqrt{2}N)^{-2/3}z\right).

Since
P_{n}(s;t)
is a monic polynomial, its partial
t
derivative is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
, which is, of course, orthogonal to
P_{n}(s;t)
. Hence, we get from (
15
), (
14
), and (
18
) that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}h_{n}(-t)

\displaystyle=N\int_{\Gamma}sP_{n}^{2}(s;-t)e^{-NV(s;-t)}ds

\displaystyle=N\int_{\Gamma}\big(s^{n+1}+p_{n,n-1}(-t)s^{n}\big)P_{n}(s;-t)e^{-NV(s;-t)}ds

\displaystyle=N\int_{\Gamma}P_{n+1}(s;-t)P_{n}(s;-t)e^{-NV(s;-t)}ds+N\beta_{n}(-t)h_{n}(-t)

(20)

\displaystyle=N\beta_{n}(-t)h_{n}(-t),

from which the formula for
q_{n}(z;\lambda)
follows. Now, we get from (
15
) that

\log D_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\log h_{k}(t).

The above expression and (
20
), together with (
10
) and (
13
), establish formula for
\sigma_{n}(z;\lambda)
since

(21)

\frac{d}{dt}\log D_{n-1}(-t)=N\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\beta_{k}(-t)=-Np_{n,n-1}(-t).

Finally, we get from orthogonality relations (
14
) that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{\Gamma}P_{n}(s;-t)P_{n-1}(s;-t)e^{-NV(s;-t)}ds\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\left(NsP_{n}(s;-t)-p_{n,n-1}^{\prime}(-t)s^{n-1}\right)P_{n-1}(s;-t)e^{-NV(s;-t)}ds

(22)

\displaystyle=Nh_{n}(-t)-p_{n,n-1}^{\prime}(-t)h_{n-1}(-t),

which, together with (
10
), (
13
), and (
17
), gives the expression for
p_{n}(z;\lambda)
.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Given
\lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}

) easily yield (

).

\lambda\in\{0,-\mathrm{i},\mathrm{i}\}
, with non-vanishing derivative there. Set

O_{(\lambda)}:=t(\Omega_{(\lambda)}),

see Figure
2
(right panel). The inverse map
x_{\lambda}(t)
exists and is holomorphic in
O_{(\lambda)}
. One can readily check that
O_{(\pm\mathrm{i})}=e^{\pm 2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}O_{(0)}
and

(25)

x_{0}(t)=e^{\pm 2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}x_{\pm\mathrm{i}}\left(te^{\pm 2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}\right).

Clearly, the branch
x_{0}(t)
is positive on

(it was shown in

that
\partial O_{(0)}
intersects the real line at

at the origin.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\lambda\in\{0,-\mathrm{i},\mathrm{i}\}
. For all
n
large enough it holds that

\begin{cases}\displaystyle(2/n)^{1/3}q_{n}\left(-(\sqrt{2}n)^{2/3}t;\lambda\right)&=-x_{\lambda}(t)+\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-1}\big),\\
\displaystyle(2/n)^{2/3}p_{n}\left(-(\sqrt{2}n)^{2/3}t;\lambda\right)&=1/x_{\lambda}(t)+\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-2}\big),\\
\displaystyle 2^{1/3}n^{-4/3}\sigma_{n}\left(-(\sqrt{2}n)^{2/3}t;\lambda\right)&=x_{\lambda}(t)-(2x_{\lambda}(t))^{-2}+\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-2}\big),\end{cases}

uniformly on closed subset of
\{t\in O_{(\lambda)}:|x_{\lambda}(t)|<\sqrt{n}/\delta\}
in the first formula and of
\{t\in O_{(\lambda)}:|\mathrm{arg}(t-t_{\lambda})-\theta_{\lambda}|>\delta\}
in the other two formulae, where
\delta>0
is any,
\theta_{0}=\pi
,
\theta_{\pm\mathrm{i}}=\mp\pi/3
, and
t_{\lambda}=e^{2\pi\lambda/3}t_{0}
.

Proof.

It holds for
\lambda\in\{-\mathrm{i},\mathrm{i}\}
that

P_{n}^{(0)}(s;t,N)=e^{2\pi\lambda n/3}P_{n}^{(\lambda)}\left(se^{-2\pi\lambda/3};te^{2\pi\lambda/3},N\right),

see (
14
). This implies that

\begin{cases}\gamma_{n}^{(0)}(t,N)^{2}&=e^{4\pi\lambda/3}\gamma_{n}^{(\lambda)}\big(te^{2\pi\lambda/3},N)^{2},\\
\beta_{n}^{(0)}(t,N)&=e^{2\pi\lambda/3}\beta_{n}^{(\lambda)}\big(te^{2\pi\lambda/3},N),\\
p_{n,n-1}^{(0)}(t,N)&=e^{2\pi\lambda/3}p_{n,n-1}^{(\lambda)}\big(te^{2\pi\lambda/3},N).\end{cases}

It has been shown in
[
7
, Theorem 4.5]
that for
|n-N|\leq N_{0}
it holds that

(26)

\begin{cases}\gamma_{n}^{(-\mathrm{i})}(t,N)^{2}&\sim-1/(2x_{-\mathrm{i}}(t))+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}G_{k}(t;n-N)N^{-k},\\
\beta_{n}^{(-\mathrm{i})}(t,N)&\sim x_{-\mathrm{i}}(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}B_{k}(t;n-N)N^{-k},\end{cases}

where the expansions hold uniformly on compact subsets of
O_{(-\mathrm{i})}
and closed subsets of

O_{(-\mathrm{i}),\delta}:=\{t\in O_{(-\mathrm{i})}:|\mathrm{arg}(t-t_{-\mathrm{i}})-\theta_{-\mathrm{i}}|>\delta\}

in the case of
\gamma_{N}^{(-\mathrm{i})}(t,N)
(the actual condition of separation from
\partial O_{(-\mathrm{i})}
is more refined in
[
7
, Theorem 4.5]
, see
[
7
, Definition 4.1]
). The functions
G_{k}(t;n-N)
and
B_{k}(t;n-N)
are analytic in
O_{(-\mathrm{i})}
and
G_{2k-1}(t;0)\equiv 0
. It is also implicitly contained in
[
7
, Section 8.2]
that

(27)

p_{n,n-1}^{(-\mathrm{i})}(t,N)=-nx_{-\mathrm{i}}(t)+N(2x_{-\mathrm{i}}(t))^{-2}+\mathcal{O}\big(N^{-1}\big),

where the error term is again analytic in
O_{(-\mathrm{i})}
, uniform on compact subsets of
O_{(-\mathrm{i})}
and closed subsets of
O_{(-\mathrm{i}),\delta}
when
n=N
. The second and third claims of the theorem now follow from Theorem
3.1
by taking
N=n
in (
27
) and in the first formula of (
26
). The first claim follows by replacing
n
with
n-1
in the second line of (
26
) and taking
N=n
there, except this proves it only on compact subsets of
O_{(\lambda)}
. To overcome this difficulty, write

(28)

\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,n)=-1/(2x_{\lambda}(t))+E_{\gamma_{n}}(t),

where we suppress the dependence on
\lambda
of the recurrence coefficients as emphasizing it is no longer important. The error term
E_{\gamma_{n}}(t)
is such that

|E_{\gamma_{n}}(t)|\leq C_{F}/n^{2},\hskip 10.00002ptt\in F,

for each closed subset
F\subset O_{(\lambda),\delta}
and any
\delta>0
. Notice that
F_{\epsilon}=\{t:\mathrm{dist}(t,F)\leq\epsilon\}
belongs to
O_{(\lambda),\delta^{\prime}}
for some appropriate choices of
\epsilon,\delta^{\prime}>0
. Applying Cauchy integral formula on circles of radius
\epsilon
around every point of
F
yields that
E_{\gamma_{n}}^{\prime}(t)
satisfies the same type of bound as
E_{\gamma_{n}}(t)
, possibly with a different constant. Now, let us write

(29)

\beta_{n-1}(t,n)=x_{\lambda}(t)+E_{\beta_{n-1}}(t).

It follows from Theorem
3.1
and the second relation in (
1
) that

2n\beta_{n-1}(t,n)\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,n)+n=(\gamma_{n}^{2})^{\prime}(t,n).

Therefore, we get from (
28
) and (
29
) that

E_{\beta_{n-1}}(t)\big(1-2x_{\lambda}(t)E_{\gamma_{n}}(t)\big)=2x_{\lambda}^{2}(t)E_{\gamma_{n}}(t)-\frac{1}{2n}\left(\frac{x^{\prime}_{\lambda}(t)}{x_{\lambda}(t)}+2x_{\lambda}(t)E_{\gamma_{n}}^{\prime}(t)\right).

Because
x^{3}-xt-1=0
, we have that
x^{\prime}=x^{2}/(2x^{3}+1)
for any branch
x(t)
. Since we already have the estimate of
E_{\beta_{n-1}}(t)
on compact subsets of
O_{(\lambda)}
, we can assume without loss of generality that
|x_{\lambda}(t)|\geq 1
. Thus, it holds that

E_{\beta_{n-1}}(t)\big(1-\mathcal{O}_{\delta}\big(n^{-3/2}\big)\big)=\mathcal{O}_{\delta}\big(n^{-1}\big)

uniformly on closed subsets
O_{(\lambda)}
for which
1\leq|x_{\lambda}(t)|\leq\sqrt{n}/\delta
. This, of course, finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Theorem
4.1
yields the following corollary, which gives pole and zero free regions around the origin in the
t
plane for the Painlevé II functions. The results in
[
12
]
in this direction are asymptotic in
z
without uniformity in the parameter
n
, but in our case we can show that the radius of these domains increases with
n
, something that is consistent with previous numerical experiments.

Corollary 4.2
.

For each
t_{*}>0
such that
\{|t|\leq t_{*}\}\subset O_{(\lambda)}
there exists
n_{*}
such that the functions
p_{n}(z;\lambda)
,
q_{n}(z;\lambda)
and
\sigma_{n}(z;\lambda)
are analytic and non-vanishing in
\big\{|z|\leq(\sqrt{2}n)^{2/3}t_{*}\big\}
for all
n\geq n_{*}
.

The asymptotic behavior of the above quantities in the complement of
\overline{O}_{(-\mathrm{i})}
was studied in
[
3
]
. However, the results of
[
3
, Theorem 4.2]
on asymptotic behavior of
\beta_{n}
and
\gamma_{n}^{2}
are not complete, not in the least due to the observed presence of their poles in this region.

5.
Monotonicity of Airy Solutions

It was conjectured by Clarkson in
[
10
]
, see also
[
11
]
, that

(30)

q_{n+1}(z;0)<q_{n}(z;0)

is true for every
z\leq 0
and every
n\geq 1
. In light of Corollary
3.2
, this claim is equivalent to
\beta_{n}(t,1)>\beta_{n-1}(t,1)
being true for
t\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
. Since
[
11
]
uses slightly different notation, let us point out that

D_{n-1}(t,1)=2^{n(n-1)/3}\mathrm{det}\left[\frac{d^{j+k}}{dt^{j+k}}\mathrm{Ai}(t)\right]_{j,k=0}^{n-1},

where everything related to orthogonal polynomials corresponds to
\lambda=0
in (
11
), see (
13
) and (
9
) (
C_{1}=1
and
C_{2}=0
). This quantity was labeled by
\Delta_{n}(t)
in
[
11
, Equation (5.2)]
. Hence, it follows from (
21
), (
22
), and (
17
) as well as (
20
) and (
15
) that

(31)

\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,1)=\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\log D_{n-1}(t,1)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\beta_{n}(t,1)=\frac{d}{dt}\log\frac{D_{n-1}(t,1)}{D_{n}(t,1)}.

These quantities were labeled as
-a_{n}(t)
and
b_{n-1}(t)
in
[
11
, Equation (5.1)]
. In
[
11
]
, these functions were studied since they are solutions of an
alternative discrete Painlevé I system
:

\begin{cases}\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,1)+\gamma_{n+1}^{2}(t,1)+\beta_{n+1}^{2}(t,1)=t\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,1)\big(\beta_{n+1}(t,1)+\beta_{n}(t,1)\big)=-n,\end{cases}

see
[
15
]
. Using rescaling formulae (
24
), the above relations can be rewritten as

\begin{cases}\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,N)+\gamma_{n+1}^{2}(t,N)+\beta_{n+1}^{2}(t,N)=t\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,N)\big(\beta_{n+1}(t,N)+\beta_{n}(t,N)\big)=-n/N,\end{cases}

which are also known as
discrete string equations
, see
[
8
]
. It was conjectured in
[
11
, Conjectures 3.2 and 5.3]
that

(32)

0<\beta_{n}^{\prime}(t,1)<1/(2\sqrt{t})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\sqrt{t}<\beta_{n}(t,1)<\beta_{n+1}(t,1)

for all
t>0
and
n\geq 0
. It was also shown that the first inequality in (
32
) implies the last inequality there and therefore (
30
). To see this, observe that it follows from the difference of the successive top string equations, (
31
), and the bottom string equation that

\beta_{n+1}(t,1)-\beta_{n}(t,1)=\frac{\gamma_{n-1}^{2}(t,1)-\gamma_{n+1}^{2}(t,1)}{\beta_{n+1}(t,1)+\beta_{n}(t,1)}=-(\beta_{n}^{\prime}(t,1)+\beta_{n-1}^{\prime}(t,1))\frac{\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,1)}{n}.

Now, it was shown in
[
19
, Equation (4.17)]
that

D_{n+1}(t,1)=-(n+1)D_{n-1}(t,1)\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{D_{n}^{2}(s,1)}{D_{n-1}^{2}(s,1)}ds

for
n\geq 0
, where
D_{0}(t,1)=\mathrm{Ai}(t)
and
D_{-1}(t,1)\equiv 1
. Let
\iota_{1}<0
be the largest real zero of
\mathrm{Ai}(t)
. Then it follows from the above relation and (
15
) that
\mathrm{sgn}\,h_{n}(t,1)=(-1)^{n}
for
t\in(\iota_{1},\infty)
. This, of course, means that
\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,1)<0
there by (
17
), which yields the desired implication on
(\iota_{1},\infty)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

There exists
n_{0}>0
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
it holds that

\beta_{n-1}^{\prime}(t,1)>0,\hskip 10.00002ptt\in[\iota_{1},\infty).

In particular, (
30
) holds for
z\leq-2^{1/3}\iota_{1}
and all
n\geq n_{0}
.

Proof.

It was shown in
[
11
, Lemma 3.3]
that
\beta_{n}^{\prime}(t,1)>0
for
t\geq 2^{-2/3}n^{4/3}
. Thus, we are only interested in
t\in[\iota_{1},2^{-2/3}n^{4/3}]
. It follows from (
24
) that

\beta_{n-1}(t,1)=n^{1/3}\beta_{n-1}(n^{-2/3}t,n).

Hence, it is sufficient for us to show that
\beta_{n}^{\prime}(t,n)>0
for
t\in F_{n}:=[-\epsilon,n^{2/3}]
, where
\epsilon>0
and small. To this end, recall that
x^{\prime}=x^{2}/(2x^{3}+1)
. Observe also that
x_{0}(t)>0
for
t\geq-\epsilon
(this is the branch that is equal to
1
at
0
and is positive for
t\geq 0
). Hence,
x_{0}^{\prime}(t)>0
for
t\geq-\epsilon
and therefore
x_{0}(t)
is increasing there. Notice that
x_{0}(t)<2\sqrt{t}
for
t\geq 1
(constant
2
is by no means optimal). Indeed, otherwise we would have that

1=x_{0}(t)\big(x_{0}^{2}(t)-t\big)>6t^{3/2}\geq 6,

which is impossible. Thus,
F_{n}
lies within the set where
|x_{0}(t)|\leq 2n^{1/3}<2n^{1/2}
. Moreover, there exists an open set
U_{n}\supset F_{n}
satisfying
\mathrm{dist}(\partial U_{n},F_{n})\geq c>0
that also lies within
\{t:|x_{0}(t)|\leq 2n^{1/2}\}
. As in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we can write

\beta_{n-1}(t,n)=x_{0}(t)+E_{\beta_{n-1}}(t),

where
E_{\beta_{n-1}}(t)
is analytic in
O_{(0)}
and satisfies (through the use of Cauchy integral formula)

|E_{\beta_{n-1}}^{\prime}(t)|\leq K/n,\hskip 10.00002ptt\in F_{n},

for some constant
K>0
. Since we can take
\epsilon
small enough so that
2x_{0}^{3}(-\epsilon)>1
, it then holds that

\beta_{n-1}^{\prime}(t,n)=x_{0}^{\prime}(t)+E_{\beta_{n-1}}^{\prime}(t)>\frac{1}{4x_{0}(t)}-\frac{K}{n}\geq\frac{1}{4x_{0}(n^{2/3})}-\frac{K}{n}>\frac{n^{2/3}-8K}{8n}

for
t\in F_{n}
, which finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎
