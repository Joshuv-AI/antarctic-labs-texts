Rainbow Thresholds
Tolson Bell, Alan Frieze, Trent G. Marbach

Abstract
We extend a recent breakthrough result relating expectation thresholds and actual thresholds to include some rainbow versions.



1
Introduction

It has long been observed that the threshold for the existence of various combinatorial objects in random graphs and hypergraphs occurs close to where the expected number of such objects tends to infinity. This informal observation has been given rigorous validation in recent breakthrough papers. First of all, Frankston, Kahn, Narayanan and Park
[
13
]
showed that under fairly general circumstances, the threshold for the existence of combinatorial objects is within a factor of
O(\log n)
of the point where the expected number of objects begins to take off. In a follow up paper, Kahn, Narayanan and Park
[
15
]
tightened their analysis for the case of the square of a Hamilton cycle and solved the existence problem up to a constant factor: a remarkable achievement, given the complexity of proofs of earlier weaker results. A key notion in this analysis is that of
spread
, see (
1
), first used in the paper of Alweiss, Lovett, Wu and Zhang
[
1
]
that made significant progress in the resolution of the
Sunflower Conjecture
of Erdős and Rado.

Spiro
[
19
]
describes a refinement of the notion of spread. Espuny Díaz and Person
[
6
]
generalised the approach of
[
15
]
to handle some questions on spanning structures from Frieze
[
8
]
.

There has been considerable research on random graphs where the edges have been randomly colored. Most notably several authors have considered the existence of rainbow colored combinatorial objects. A set of colored edges will be called
rainbow
if each edge has a different color. Improving on earlier results of Cooper and Frieze
[
4
]
and Frieze and Loh
[
10
]
, Ferber and Krivelevich
[
7
]
showed that w.h.p. at the threshold for Hamiltonicity, randomly coloring the edges of
G_{n,p}
with
n+o(n)
colors yields a rainbow Hamilton cycle. Our aim in this short paper is to show that the proof in
[
13
]
can be modified to incorporate rainbow questions. We begin by summarising the results of the papers
[
15
]
and
[
13
]
.

A hypergraph
{\mathcal{H}}
(thought of as a set of edges) is
r
-bounded if
e\in{\mathcal{H}}
implies that
|e|\leq r
. The most important notion comes next. For a set
S\subseteq X=V({\mathcal{H}})
we let
\langle S\rangle=\left\{T:\;S\subseteq T\subseteq X\right\}
denote the subsets of
X
that contain
S
. Let
\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle=\bigcup_{H\in{\mathcal{H}}}\langle H\rangle
be the collection of subsets of
X
that contain an edge of
{\mathcal{H}}
. We say that
{\mathcal{H}}
is
\kappa
-spread if we have the following bound on the number of edges of
{\mathcal{H}}
that contain a particular set
S
:

|{\mathcal{H}}\cap\langle S\rangle|\leq\frac{|{\mathcal{H}}|}{\kappa^{|S|}},\quad\forall S\subseteq X.

(1)

In our proof, we also use the slightly weaker definition that
{\mathcal{H}}
is
(K,\kappa)
-spread if

|{\mathcal{H}}\cap\langle S\rangle|\leq\frac{K|{\mathcal{H}}|}{\kappa^{|S|}},\quad\forall S\subseteq X.

(2)

Let

denote a random
m
-subset of
X
and
X_{p}
denote a subset of

where each

is included independently in

with probability

. The following theorem is from

]

Theorem 1
.

Let

be an

-bounded,

-spread hypergraph and let

(3)

then w.h.p.

contains an edge of

. Here w.h.p. assumes that

.

Remark 1
.

Let

and

denote the number of edges of

that are contained in

. Then, assuming that

Suppose now that the elements of

We see that our additional restriction (i) is satisfied by many important applications and that in these cases, it implies the other extra condition, (ii).

Lemma 3
.

With

Proof.

Let

.

For

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be as in Theorem

\kappa
-spread hypergraph on a set

of size

such that

{\mathcal{H}}^{*}=\left\{S^{*}\subseteq X^{*}:\xi(S^{*})\in{\mathcal{H}},\text{ and }c(x^{*})\neq c(y^{*})\text{ and }\xi(x^{*})\neq\xi(y^{*})\text{ for any distinct }x^{*},y^{*}\in S^{*}\right\},

be the set of possible rainbow edges of

We follow the basic idea of
[
13
]
and
[
18
]
and account for the coloring. We choose a small randomly colored random set

, throw away the
H^{*}
that are not compatible with

, and replace the other

with

to become relatively smaller. We then repeat the argument with respect to the hypergraph consisting of these

piece by piece and find members of
{\mathcal{H}}^{*}
which are mostly contained in
W^{*}
. After

iterations, one of those pieces is likely to be fully contained in a larger

. It is important to realise that the edge sets of the hypergraphs encountered in these iterations are multi-sets, i.e., the same edge can be repeated many times. Recall that asymptotics refer to

Let
\ell_{0}
be the least

and let
\ell
be the least
i>\ell_{0}
such that
r/2^{i}<1
. Note that
r_{\ell}\geq 1/2
. Let
p_{i}=C_{i}/\kappa
where

\displaystyle C_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}C_{0}&i\leq\ell_{0}\\
\frac{\log r}{\log\log r}&\ell_{0}<i\leq\ell\end{cases};\qquad\varepsilon_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{(i+1)^{2}}&1\leq i\leq\ell_{0}.\\
\frac{1}{(\log\log r)^{2}}&i>\ell_{0}.\end{cases};\qquad r_{i}=\frac{r}{2^{i}}\text{ for }i\geq 0.

\displaystyle K_{i}

\displaystyle=K\prod_{j=1}^{i}\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon_{j})\left(1-p_{j}\right)^{r_{j}}}.

Here
C_{0}
is some sufficiently large constant. Note that

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}r_{j}p_{j}\leq\frac{2C_{0}r}{\kappa}+\sum_{j=\ell_{0}+1}^{\ell}\frac{\log r}{\kappa\log\log r}=\frac{2C_{0}r}{\kappa}+o(1)\text{ and }\prod_{i=\ell_{0}+1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{(\log\log r)^{2}}}=e^{o(1)}.

So, using
1/(1-x)\leq e^{2x}
for small
x
, we obtain

1\leq K_{i}\leq\exp\left\{2\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(t+1)^{2}}+o(1)+2\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}r_{j}p_{j}\right\}\leq A,

(11)

where
A=B+e^{3C_{0}/L}
for some absolute constant
B
. (We have used the fact that
\kappa
-spread can be interpreted as
(1,\kappa)
-spread.)

Let
X_{0}=X
and
X_{0}^{*}=X\times[q]
. For
i=1,\dots,\ell
, let
X_{i}=X_{i-1}\setminus\xi(W_{i}^{*})
, where
W^{*}_{i}
is chosen uniformly at random from
{\mathcal{E}}^{*}_{Np_{i}}
on
X_{i-1}^{*}
(that is,
\left\{W^{*}_{i}\in{\mathcal{E}}^{*}_{Np_{i}}:W_{i}^{*}\subseteq X_{i-1}^{*}\right\}
). Let

{\mathcal{H}}_{i}^{*}=\{T^{*}(H^{*},W^{*}_{i}):\mbox{$H^{*}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{i-1}^{*}$, $H^{*}\sim W^{*}_{i}$, $|T^{*}|\leq r_{i}$}\}.

Thus
{\mathcal{H}}_{i}^{*}
is an
r_{i}
-bounded collection of subsets of
X_{i}
.

Note that
\exists H^{*}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{:}H^{*}\subseteq W_{i}^{*}
whenever some
T^{*}(H^{*},W_{i}^{*})=\emptyset
for
H^{*}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{i-1}^{*}
. As
r_{\ell}<1
, eventually we will either have some
T^{*}(H^{*},W_{i}^{*})=\emptyset
or
|{\mathcal{H}}_{i}^{*}|=0
. So to prove the claim, we just need to show

\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{H}}^{*}_{\ell}=\emptyset)=o(1),

(12)

(where the
\mathbb{P}
refers to the entire sequence
W^{*}_{1}\dots W^{*}_{\ell}
).

Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
be the event that
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
does not occur, where
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
is the
{\mathcal{H}}^{*}_{i}
equivalent of the event
{\mathcal{S}}
in Lemma
5
and let
{\mathcal{A}}_{\leq i}=\bigcap_{j=0}^{i}{\mathcal{A}}_{j}
.

\text{If ${\mathcal{A}}_{\leq\ell}$ occurs then }|{\mathcal{H}}^{*}_{\ell}|\geq|{\mathcal{H}}^{*}|\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(1-\varepsilon_{i})(1-p_{i})^{r_{i}}>0.

(13)

Lemma
5
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{A}}_{\leq i}\mid{\mathcal{A}}_{\leq i-1})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\left\{-\frac{\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\kappa^{2}q(1-p)}{16e^{3}N}\right\}-\frac{2K_{i}}{C_{i}^{r_{i}/3}\varepsilon_{i}\left(1-\frac{C_{i}}{\kappa}\right)^{r_{i}}}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\left\{-\frac{\kappa^{2}q}{400(i+1)^{4}N}\right\}-\frac{2A}{C_{i}^{r_{i}/3}\varepsilon_{i}\left(1-\frac{C_{0}}{\kappa}\right)^{r}}>0

\displaystyle\geq 1-r^{-1/400}-\frac{2A}{C_{i}^{r_{i}/3}\varepsilon_{i}\left(1-\frac{C_{0}}{\kappa}\right)^{r}}>0.

Note that
C_{i}^{r_{i}/3}\varepsilon_{i}\geq C_{0}^{\frac{1}{3}\log r}/\log^{2}r=\Omega(r)
for
i\leq\ell_{0}
and
C_{i}^{r_{i}/3}\varepsilon_{i}\geq(\log r/\log\log r)^{1/3}/(\log\log r)^{2}=\Omega(\log^{1/4}r)
for
\ell_{0}<i\leq\ell
.

So,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{A}}_{\leq\ell})

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{i=1}^{\ell_{0}}\left(1-r^{-1/400}-O\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)\right)\prod_{i=\ell_{0}+1}^{\ell}\left(1-\frac{e^{-C_{0}/L}}{(i+1)^{2}(1-e^{-C_{0}/L})}-O\left(\frac{1}{\log^{1/4}r}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(1-o(1)).

Applying (
12
) and (
13
) completes the proof of Theorem
2
.
