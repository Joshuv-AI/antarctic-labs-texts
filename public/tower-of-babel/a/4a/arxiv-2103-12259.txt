A stabilized computational nonlocal poromechanics model for dynamic analysis of saturated porous media
Shashank Menon, Xiaoyu Song

Abstract
In this article we formulate a stable computational nonlocal poromechanics model for dynamic analysis of saturated porous media. As a novelty, the stabilization formulation eliminates zero-energy modes associated with the original multiphase correspondence constitutive models in the coupled nonlocal poromechanics model. The two-phase stabilization scheme is formulated based on an energy method that incorporates inhomogeneous solid deformation and fluid flow. In this method, the nonlocal formulations of skeleton strain energy and fluid flow dissipation energy equate to their local formulations. The stable coupled nonlocal poromechanics model is solved for dynamic analysis by an implicit time integration scheme. As a new contribution, we validate the coupled stabilization formulation by comparing numerical results with analytical and finite element solutions for one-dimensional and two-dimensional dynamic problems in saturated porous media. Numerical examples of dynamic strain localization in saturated porous media are presented to demonstrate the efficacy of the stable coupled poromechanics framework for localized failure under dynamic loads.

In this article, we formulate and implement a stabilized coupled nonlocal poromechanics model for dynamic strain localization in saturated porous media. As a novelty, the stabilization terms eliminates zero-energy modes associated with the multiphase correspondence constitutive models in the coupled nonlocal framework for modeling saturated porous media under dynamic loads. The stabilization terms are formulated based on the energy method in which the nonlocal formulations of skeleton strain energy and fluid flow dissipation energy equate to their local formulations in line with the classical poromechanics for saturated porous media. Specifically, the method incorporates non-homogeneous solid deformation and fluid flow around a material point. The stabilized coupled nonlocal poromechanics model is numerically solved using a hybrid Lagrangian-Eulerian meshless method with an implicit time integration scheme. Parallel computing is also adopted for computational efficiency. The coupled stabilized formulation is validated by comparing numerical results with analytical and finite element solutions for one-dimensional and two-dimensional dynamic problems in saturated porous media. Numerical examples of dynamic strain localization in saturated porous media are presented to demonstrate the efficacy of the stable coupled poromechanics framework for localized failure under dynamic loads. We note that the stabilized coupled nonlocal model for saturated porous media can be readily extended to model dynamic problems in unsaturated porous media.

The contribution of this article includes (1) a proof of the zero-energy modes associated with the original multiphase correspondence constitutive principle for modeling porous media through multiphase peridynamic states, (2) a remedy based on the energy method to remove the multiphase zero-energy modes for dynamic analysis, and (3) an implicit numerical implementation of the proposed stabilized dynamic non-local poromechanics model and its validation. For sign convention, the assumption in continuum mechanics is followed, i.e., for solid skeleton, tensile force/stress is positive and compression is negative, and for fluid compression is positive and tension is negative.

2.3
Determination of stabilization parameters

In this part the parameters in the stabilized terms will be derived based on the energy equivalence between peridynamics and classical theory for the solid skeleton and fluid phase.
For the solid phase, it is postulated that the stored elastic energy in the solid skeleton from peridynamics equals to the elastic energy from the classical poromechanic theory at the same material point

For simplicity, a microelastic peridynamic model

and
\xi=|\boldsymbol{\xi}|
.
The micro-potential function is a measure of the elastic strain energy stored in a single bond of the solid skeleton due to its deformation. The total strain energy density at point
\boldsymbol{x}
is expressed as

\mathscr{W}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{H}}w(\eta,\xi)\text{d}V^{\prime},

(35)

where the factor of
1/2
means that each endpoint of a bond between two solid material points owns only half the energy in this bond. Let
f
be the magnitude of
\boldsymbol{f}
as

f(\eta,\xi)=\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial\eta}.

(36)

We further assume a homogeneous solid skeleton under isotropic extension. It follows

\eta=\mathscr{C}_{1}\xi,

(37)

where
\mathscr{C}_{1}
is a constant for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Thus
f
can be written as

f=C\mathscr{C}_{1}=C\eta/\xi,

(38)

where
C
is the constant defined previously.
It follows from (
36
) and (
38
) that

\displaystyle w=C\eta^{2}/(2\xi)=C\lambda^{2}\xi/2.

(39)

Substituting (
39
) into (
35
) gives

\mathscr{W}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\dfrac{1}{2}\left(C\mathscr{C}_{1}^{2}\xi\right)\text{d}V^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\delta}\dfrac{1}{2}\left(C\mathscr{C}_{1}^{2}\xi\right)(4\pi\xi^{2})\text{d}{\xi}=\frac{\pi C\delta^{4}}{4}\mathscr{C}_{1}^{2}.

(40)

The elastic strain energy of the solid skeleton at material point
\boldsymbol{x}
from the classical elastic theory under isotropic deformation is

\widetilde{\mathscr{W}}=\frac{1}{2}\overline{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}:{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=\frac{1}{2}({K^{e}}{\epsilon_{v}})(\epsilon_{v})=\frac{1}{2}{K^{e}}(3\mathscr{C}_{1})^{2}=\frac{9K^{e}}{2}\mathscr{C}_{1}^{2},

(41)

where
K^{e}
is the classical elastic bulk modulus and
\epsilon_{v}
is the elastic volumetric strain. Combining (
40
) and (
41
) leads to an expression for
C
under three-dimensional condition as

C_{3d}=\frac{18K^{e}}{\pi\delta^{4}}.

(42)

For the fluid phase, the pairwise fluid flow density
f
at material point
\boldsymbol{x}
in the bond-based peridynamics can be determined from a fluid dissipation micropotential
w^{f}
through

f_{w}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\frac{\partial w^{f}}{\partial\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),

(43)

where
\Phi=p^{\prime}_{w}-p_{w}
is the fluid pressure difference between material points
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
.
The dissipation micropotential
w^{f}
represents the dissipation potential along a bond between two material points and is a function of the fluid potential scalar state
\Phi
of that bond. Then the total dissipation potential at point
\boldsymbol{x}
is a summation over all the micropotentials in the family of this point.

\mathscr{V}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{H}}w^{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x})\text{d}V^{\prime},

(44)

where similar to the solid phase the factor of
1/2
means that each endpoint of a bond between two solid material points owns only half the energy in this bond.
The pairwise fluid flow density at
\boldsymbol{x}
is assumed as

f({\bf x^{\prime}},{\bf x})=K_{p}\frac{\Phi}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}|},

(45)

where
K_{p}
is the peridynamic hydraulic micro-conductivity. It follows from (
) and (
43
) that fluid flow dissipation micro-potential can be written as

w^{f}=\frac{1}{2}K_{p}\frac{\Phi^{2}}{|\boldsymbol{\xi|}}.

(46)

The peridynamic hydraulic micro-conductivity can be related to the classical hydraulic conductivity by equating the peridynamic fluid dissipation potential to the classical fluid dissipation potential at point
\boldsymbol{x}
. For simplicity, we assume a linear pressure field in a body,
p_{w}=\mathscr{C}_{2}(\boldsymbol{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})
for a three-dimensional case.
Thus, the fluid pressures at material points
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
are written as

p_{w}=\mathscr{C}_{2}(\boldsymbol{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})\;\;\text{and}\;p^{\prime}_{w}=\mathscr{C}_{2}(\boldsymbol{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}).

(47)

It follows that

w^{f}=K_{p}\frac{1}{2}\mathscr{C}^{2}_{2}\xi.

(48)

Substituting (
47
) into (
46
) and conducting integration in a sphere with radius
\delta
centered at
\boldsymbol{x}
lead to

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\delta}\left(\frac{1}{2}K_{p}\mathscr{C}^{2}_{2}\xi\right)(4\pi\xi^{2})\text{d}\xi=\frac{\pi K_{p}\delta^{4}}{4}\mathscr{C}^{2}_{2}.

(49)

Assuming a homogeneous body and isotropic fluid flow, the classical fluid dissipation energy through Darcy’s law at material point
\boldsymbol{x}
is expressed as

\widetilde{\mathscr{V}}=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}p_{w})k_{w}\boldsymbol{1}({\boldsymbol{\nabla}p_{w}}),

(50)

where
k_{w}
is the hydraulic conductivity of saturated porous media. It follows from (
50
) and (
47
) that the classical fluid dissipation energy at material point
\boldsymbol{x}
is

\widetilde{\mathscr{V}}=\frac{3}{2}k_{w}\mathscr{C}^{2}_{2}.

(51)

Combining (
49
) and (
51
) gives the hydraulic micro-conductivity under three-dimensional condition as

K_{p,3d}=\frac{6k_{w}}{\pi\delta^{4}}.

(52)

3
Numerical implementation

3.1
Spatial discretization

The equations (
32
) and (
33
) are spatially discretized by a hybrid Lagrangian-Eulerian meshfree scheme, as shown in Figure
2
. In this method, a porous continuum material is discretized into a finite number of mixed material points (i.e., mixed solid skeleton and pore water material points). Each material point has two types of degree of freedom, the displacement and the pore water pressure. The uniform grid is used to spatially discretize the problem domain in which all material points have an identical size.

Figure 2:
Schematic of a Lagrangian-Eulerian meshless spatial discretization scheme of material point
i
and its neighboring material points (solid points in red and fluid points in blue).

It is assumed that inertia loading has no impact on the fluid flow
[
1
]
and water is incompressible. Let
\mathscr{N}_{i}
be the number of material points in the horizon of a material point
i
. The spatially discretized equations at material point
i
can be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{s}_{i}+\rho_{i}\boldsymbol{g}

\displaystyle=\rho_{i}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i},

(53)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i}+\mathcal{Q}_{i}+\mathcal{Q}^{s}_{i}

\displaystyle=0,

(54)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\left[\underline{\omega}J_{i}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{(i)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{F}}_{(i)}^{-T}\boldsymbol{K}_{(i)}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{(ij)}-\underline{\omega}J_{j}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{(j)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{F}}_{(j)}^{-T}\boldsymbol{K}_{(j)}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{(ji)}\right]V_{j},

(55)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{s}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\frac{GC}{\omega_{0}}\left[\underline{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}_{(ij)}^{s}-\underline{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}_{(ji)}^{s}\right]V_{j},

(56)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\left[\underline{\omega}(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{(j)}-\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{(i)}){\boldsymbol{K}}_{(i)}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{(ij)}\right]V_{j},

(57)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\left[\underline{\omega}{\boldsymbol{q}}_{(i)}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{(i)}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{(ij)}-\underline{\omega}{\boldsymbol{q}}_{(j)}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{(j)}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{(ji)}\right]V_{j},

(58)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{s}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\frac{GK_{p}}{\omega_{0}}\left[\underline{\mathcal{R}}^{w}_{(ij)}-\underline{\mathcal{R}}^{{}^{\prime}w}_{(ji)}\right]V_{j}.b

(59)

We define the linear assembly operator
\mathscr{A}

[
47
]
. Let
\mathscr{N}
be the number of the total material points. The global discretization form of the coupled equations can be written as

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{s}_{i}+\rho_{i}\boldsymbol{g}\right)V_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{A}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}(\rho_{i}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})V_{i},

(60)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\left(\dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i}+\mathcal{Q}_{i}+\mathcal{Q}^{s}_{i}\right)V_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{A}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}0_{i}.

(61)

3.2
Time integration and linearization

We formulate a fully implicit scheme to integrate the coupled system of equations in time
[
47
]
.
At

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}^{u}_{n+1}=\left.\mathscr{A}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\left(\rho_{i}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{s}_{i}-\rho_{i}\boldsymbol{g}\right)V_{i}\right|_{n+1},

(62)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}^{p}_{n+1}=\left.\mathscr{A}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\left(\dot{\mathcal{V}}_{i}+\mathcal{Q}_{i}+\mathcal{Q}^{s}_{i}\right)V_{i}\right|_{n+1}.

(63)

The Newmark method
[
48
,
1
]
is adopted. In the temporal domain a second-order scheme is used to integrate the momentum balance equation and a first-order scheme is applied to integrate the mass balance equation. At time step
n
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
,
\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}
,
\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}
,
\boldsymbol{p}_{n}
, and
\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n}
are known. Let

\Delta\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n+1}=\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n+1}-\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n}

can be written as follows,

\displaystyle\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}+\Delta\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n+1},

(64)

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}+\Delta t\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}+\beta_{2}\Delta t\Delta\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n+1},

(65)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u}_{n}+\Delta t\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}+\frac{(\Delta t)^{2}}{2}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}+\beta_{1}\frac{(\Delta t)^{2}}{2}\Delta\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n+1},

(66)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{p}_{n}+\Delta t\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n}+\beta_{3}\Delta t\Delta\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n+1},

(67)

where
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}\in[0,1]
are numerical integration parameters. For unconditional stability,

\beta_{1}\geqslant\beta_{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{2},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\beta_{3}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}.

(68)

Substituting (
64
), (
65
), (
66
), and (
67
) into (
62
) and (
63
),
\Delta\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n+1}
and
\Delta\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n+1}
, can be solved by Newton’s method as follows. Let

be the iteration number.

\begin{Bmatrix}\boldsymbol{r}^{u,k+1}\\
\boldsymbol{r}^{p,k+1}\end{Bmatrix}=\begin{Bmatrix}\boldsymbol{r}^{u,k}\\
\boldsymbol{r}^{p,k}\end{Bmatrix}+\mathcal{A}^{k}\begin{Bmatrix}\delta\Delta\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k+1}\\
\delta\Delta\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}^{k+1}\end{Bmatrix}\approx\begin{Bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{Bmatrix},

(69)

where

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=\left.\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}^{u}}{\partial\Delta\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}}&\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}^{u}}{\partial\Delta\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}}\\
\vskip-8.61108pt\\
\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}^{p}}{\partial\Delta\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}}&\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}^{p}}{\partial\Delta\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}}\end{bmatrix}\right|^{k}_{n+1}.

(70)

By solving (

\begin{Bmatrix}\delta\Delta\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k+1}\\
\delta\Delta\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}^{k+1}\end{Bmatrix}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}\begin{Bmatrix}\boldsymbol{r}^{u,k}\\
\boldsymbol{r}^{p,k}\end{Bmatrix}.

(71)

Finally, we have

\begin{Bmatrix}\Delta\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k+1}\\
\Delta\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}^{k+1}\end{Bmatrix}=\begin{Bmatrix}\Delta\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\\
\Delta\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}^{k}\end{Bmatrix}+\begin{Bmatrix}\delta\Delta\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k+1}\\
\delta\Delta\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}^{k+1}\end{Bmatrix}.

(72)

3.4
Linearization of correspondence material models

We present the material-point level linearization of correspondence solid and fluid models implemented in this study. Now, the differentiation of the total force density at a material point
i
with respect to a deformation state
\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}
reads,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{i}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}\left(\underline{\boldsymbol{T}}_{ij}-\underline{\boldsymbol{T}}_{ji}\right)V_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\left[\underline{\omega}\left(\left\{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{P}_{(i)}}{\partial\widetilde{\boldsymbol{F}}_{(i)}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{\boldsymbol{F}}_{(i)}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}\right\}(\boldsymbol{K}_{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ij}\right)-\underline{\omega}\left(\left\{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{P}_{(j)}}{\partial\widetilde{\boldsymbol{F}}_{(j)}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{\boldsymbol{F}}_{(j)}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}\right\}(\boldsymbol{K}_{(j)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ji}\right)\right]V_{j},

(111)

where
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{P}_{(i)}}{\partial\widetilde{\boldsymbol{F}}_{(i)}}
can be determined through the return mapping algorithm following the lines in computational plasticity (e.g.,
[
50
,
51
,
52
]
, and others)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\boldsymbol{F}}_{(i)}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}

\displaystyle=\underline{\omega}\left[\frac{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\xi}_{il}V_{l}\right](\boldsymbol{K}_{(i)})^{-1}=\underline{\omega}\left[\boldsymbol{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\xi}_{il}V_{l}\right](\boldsymbol{K}_{(i)})^{-1},

(112)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\boldsymbol{F}}_{(j)}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}

\displaystyle=\underline{\omega}\left[\frac{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{li}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\xi}_{li}V_{i}\right](\boldsymbol{K}_{(j)})^{-1}=\underline{\omega}\left[-\boldsymbol{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\xi}_{li}V_{i}\right](\boldsymbol{K}_{(j)})^{-1}.

(113)

The linearization of the stabilization force with respect to a deformation state
\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}
reads,

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{s}_{i}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\left[\underline{\omega}\frac{GC}{\omega_{0}}\left\{\frac{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{ij}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}-\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{\boldsymbol{F}}_{(i)}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ij}\right)\right\}-\underline{\omega}\frac{GC}{\omega_{0}}\left\{\frac{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{ji}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}-\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{\boldsymbol{F}}_{(j)}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ji}\right)\right\}\right]V_{j},

(114)

where
\frac{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{ij}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}
and
\frac{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{ji}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{il}}
is nonzero only if
j=l
.
Next, the momentum balance linearized with respect to the fluid pressure reads,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{i}}{\partial{p}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\frac{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{T}}_{ij}}{\partial p_{i}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\underline{\omega}(\boldsymbol{K}_{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ij}V_{j},

(115)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{i}}{\partial{p}_{j}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{T}}_{ji}}{\partial p_{j}}=-\underline{\omega}(\boldsymbol{K}_{(j)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ji}V_{j}.

(116)

Similarly, for the mass balance, the derivative of fluid flow density with respect to a pressure potential state reads,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathcal{Q}}_{i}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}\left(\underline{\mathcal{Q}}_{ij}-\underline{\mathcal{Q}}_{ji}\right)V_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\left[\underline{\omega}\left\{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}_{i,w}}{\partial\widetilde{\gradient{\Phi}}_{i}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{\gradient{\Phi}}_{i}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}\right\}(\boldsymbol{K}_{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ij}-\underline{\omega}\left\{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{q}_{j,w}}{\partial\widetilde{\gradient{\Phi}}_{j}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{\gradient{\Phi}}_{j}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}\right\}(\boldsymbol{K}_{(j)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ji}\right]V_{j},

(117)

where

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\gradient{\Phi}}_{i}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}

\displaystyle=\left[\underline{\omega}\frac{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{il}V_{j}\right](\boldsymbol{K}_{(i)})^{-1}=\underline{\omega}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{il}(\boldsymbol{K}_{(i)})^{-1}V_{j},

(118)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\gradient{\Phi}}_{j}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}

\displaystyle=\left[\underline{\omega}\frac{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{li}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{li}V_{i}\right](\boldsymbol{K}_{(j)})^{-1}=-\underline{\omega}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{li}(\boldsymbol{K}_{(j)})^{-1}V_{i}.

(119)

The linearization of the stabilization flow density with respect to a deformation state
\underline{\Phi}_{il}
reads

\frac{\partial{\mathcal{Q}}^{s}_{i}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}_{i}}\left[\underline{\omega}\frac{GK_{p}}{\omega_{0}}\left\{\frac{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{ij}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}-\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{\gradient{\Phi}}_{i}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ij}\right)\right\}-\underline{\omega}\frac{GK_{p}}{\omega_{0}}\left\{\frac{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{ji}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}-\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{\gradient{\Phi}}_{j}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ji}\right)\right\}\right]V_{j},

(120)

where
\frac{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{ji}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}
and
\frac{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{ji}}{\partial\underline{\Phi}_{il}}
are nonzero only if
j=l
.

4.1
Correspondence material models

For the material models, we implement two classical material models for the solid skeleton and the classical Darcy’s law for fluid flow through the extended correspondence principle with stabilization. Following the lines in continuum mechanics
[
50
]
, the total strain
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
can be determined from the nonlocal deformation gradient at a material point.
In examples 1 and 2, the solid skeleton is modeled by an isotropic elastic constitutive relationship, which reads

\overline{\sigma}_{ij}=\mathcal{C}^{e}_{ijkl}\varepsilon^{e}_{kl},

(121)

where
i,j,k,l=1,2,3
,
\mathcal{C}^{e}_{ijkl}
is the isotropic elastic tensor, and
\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}
is the elastic strain tensor. The isotropic elastic tensor is written as

\mathcal{C}^{e}_{ijkl}=K^{e}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+2\mu^{e}(\mathcal{I}_{ijkl}-1/3\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}),

(122)

where
\mu^{e}
is the shear modulus and
\mathcal{I}_{ijkl}
is the rank-four identity tensor.

In example 3, the critical-state based elastoplastic model for saturated soils
[
53
,
54
]
is adopted and numerically implemented through the celebrated return-mapping algorithm in computational plasticity
[
50
,
55
]
. In this material model, the total strain tensor is additively decomposed into the elastic strain tensor
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{e}
and the plastic strain tensor
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{p}
as

\varepsilon_{ij}={\varepsilon}^{e}_{ij}+{\varepsilon}^{p}_{ij},

(123)

Given the elastic strain tensor, the effective stress can be computed by equation (
121
). The yield function
f
is written as

f(\overline{p},q,p_{c})=(\overline{p}-p_{c})\overline{p}+(q/M)^{2}\leqslant 0,

(124)

where
\overline{p}
is the effective mean stress,
q
is the equivalent shear stress,
M
is the slope of the critical state line, and
{p}_{c}
is the preconsolidation pressure. As a hardening law,
p_{c}
evolves with plastic volumetric strain.

\dot{p}_{c}=\dfrac{-p_{c}}{{\lambda}-{\kappa}}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}_{11}+\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}_{22}+\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}_{33}),

(125)

where
\lambda
and
{\kappa}
are the swelling index and the compression index, respectively. The plastic strain is determined below by assuming the associative flow rule.

\dot{{\varepsilon}}^{p}_{ij}=\dot{\gamma}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{{\sigma}}_{ij}},

(126)

where
\dot{\gamma}
is the non-negative plastic multiplier. It is noted that advanced constitutive models for geomaterials can be applied in the formulated peridynamics poromechanics in this article by the recently proposed multiphase correspondence principle (see
[
24
]
).

5
Closure

In this article we propose a stabilized computational nonlocal poromechanics model for dynamic strain localization in saturated porous media. The stabilized coupled nonlocal model is solved using a Lagrangian-Eulerian meshless method with an implicit time integration scheme. Parallel computing is adopted for computing efficiency. As a new contribution, we present a theoretical proof of the zero-energy modes associated with the multiphase correspondence principle. We propose a remedy based on the energy method to circumvent zero-energy modes in the solid deformation and fluid flow. We present a method to determine the stabilization parameter
G
for both the solid deformation and fluid flow process. The stabilized coupled nonlocal model for saturated porous media under dynamic loading can be readily extended to model dynamic problems in unsaturated porous media. We have validated the coupled stabilized nonlocal formulation by comparing numerical results with analytical and finite element solutions for dynamic problems in saturated porous media. Numerical examples are conducted to demonstrate the robustness of the coupled nonlocal dynamic model for dynamic strain localization analysis of saturated porous media.
