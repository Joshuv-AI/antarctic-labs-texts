Estimating odds and log odds with guaranteed accuracy
Luis Mendo

Abstract
Two sequential estimators are proposed for the odds p / ( 1 − p ) p/(1-p) and log odds log ⁡ ( p / ( 1 − p ) ) \log(p/(1-p)) respectively, using independent Bernoulli random variables with parameter p p as inputs. The estimators are unbiased, and guarantee that the variance of the estimation error divided by the true value of the odds, or the variance of the estimation error of the log odds, are less than a target value for any p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) . The estimators are close to optimal in the sense of Wolfowitz’s bound. Keywords: Estimation, odds, log odds, mean-square error, efficiency. MSC2010: 62F10, 62L12.

Introduction

Consider a probability
p\in(0,1)
, and let
\gamma=p/(1-p)
and
\phi=\log\gamma
respectively denote the
odds
and
log odds
associated with
p
. These are commonly used measures of the probability of an event, which find application in many fields including clinical trials, epidemiology, economics, sociology or data analysis. Also, log odds are inherent to logistic regression, which is a prevalent tool in medical and social sciences
(
Agresti, 2002
;
Norton and Dowd, 2018
)
,
and is widely used in machine learning
(
Bishop, 2006
)
.

For an estimator
\hat{\gamma}
of
\gamma
, a usual measure of accuracy is the
mean-square error
(MSE),
\E[(\hat{\gamma}-\gamma)^{2}]
, or its square root, known as root-mean-square error (RMSE). Either of these measures of error is most meaningful in a
relative
sense. For example, an RMSE equal to
0.5
may be adequate if the true odds are
\gamma=9
(corresponding to
p=0.9
), but unacceptable if
\gamma=0.25
(
p=0.2
). It is reasonable to require that the RMSE be proportional to the true (unknown) value of
\gamma
; or equivalently that the MSE be proportional to
\gamma^{2}
. The estimator
\hat{\gamma}
should ideally guarantee
\E[(\hat{\gamma}-\gamma)^{2}]/\gamma^{2}
smaller than a
prescribed
value, irrespective of
\gamma
.

When estimating the log odds
\phi
, on the other hand, the error should not be understood in a relative sense, because the logarithm already has a normalizing effect, as it transforms ratios into differences. Specifically, a difference
\delta
between
\phi
and an estimate
\hat{\phi}
thereof corresponds to a deviation by a factor
\exp\delta
between
\gamma
and its estimate
\hat{\gamma}
defined as
\exp\hat{\phi}
. That is, relative error in estimating
\gamma
corresponds to absolute (non-normalized) error in estimating
\phi
.

This paper deals with the problem of estimating
\gamma
and
\phi
from independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations of a Bernoulli distribution with parameter
p
. Sequential estimators of
\gamma
and
\phi
are presented that achieve a specified level of accuracy, defined as relative or absolute MSE respectively. The sequential character of the estimators cannot be avoided, because a fixed sample size cannot satisfy these conditions for all
p
. The proposed estimators are based on
inverse binomial sampling

Haldane, 1945
;
Mikulski and Smith, 1976
;
Lehmann and Casella, 1998
, chapter 2
, and have a number of desirable features, in addition to guaranteeing a target accuracy: they are simple to compute; they are unbiased; and their performance is close to optimal, in the sense that the relationship between accuracy and average sample size is only slightly worse than that given by Wolfowitz’s bound
(
Wolfowitz, 1947
)
.

The problems of estimating the odds and the log odds, as well as related parameters such as the ratio of two probabilities or the corresponding odds ratio,
have drawn a great deal of attention over the years. However, to the author’s knowledge, no estimator has been proposed that can guarantee a specified level of accuracy as discussed above, i.e. in a relative sense for the odds or in an absolute sense for the log odds, for all values of
p
. A review of existing results in this area is given next.

Early works by
Gart and Zweifel (1967)
and
Wells and Donner (1987)
focus on analysing the bias and mean-square error of several estimators of the odds under binomial (i.e. fixed-size) sampling.
Siegmund (1982)
studies the asymptotic properties of estimators of the odds and the odds ratio.
Sungboonchoo et al. (2021)
propose estimators of the odds with exponentially decreasing bias, using either binomial or inverse binomial sampling; and provide asymptotic confidence intervals for estimators of the odds ratio.
Ngamkham et al. (2016)
and
Ngamkham (2018)
consider interval estimation of the ratio of two probabilities, when each of the two populations is sampled independently using either a fixed sample size or inverse binomial sampling, and give asymptotic expressions for confidence intervals with a desired coverage probability. Similarly,
Tian et al. (2008)
discuss the estimation of the ratio of two probabilities using independent inverse binomial sampling for each population, and review several approaches for obtaining approximate confidence intervals.

Cho (2007)
estimates the probability ratio under a loss function defined as the sum of squared error and cost proportional to the number of observations, with samples taken in pairs, one from each population; and
proposes a sequential procedure which is asymptotically optimal when the cost per sample is small.
Cho (2013)
presents a sequential estimator for a probability ratio when the proportion of sample sizes is specified, and studies its asymptotic properties.
Kokaew et al. (2021)
;
Kokaew et al. (2023)
consider correlation between observations of the two populations, assuming sample sets of fixed size; and propose estimators of the probability ratio or its logarithm, for which they derive asymptotic confidence intervals.
Agresti (1999)
analyses confidence intervals for the log odds ratio when independent binomial sampling is applied to each population, and
Bandyopadhyay et al. (2017)
address the more general setting where the two populations are sampled using different combinations of binomial and inverse binomial sampling.

The following terminology and simple facts will be used throughout the paper. The two possible values of the Bernoulli distribution will be referred to as “success” and “failure”, which occur with probabilities
p
and
1-p
respectively. In inverse binomial sampling, given
r\in\mathbb{N}
, a sequence of i.i.d. Bernoulli variables with parameter
p
is observed until the
r
-th success is obtained. The required number of samples,
N
, has a
negative binomial
distribution:

\Pr[N=n]=\binom{n-1}{r-1}p^{r}(1-p)^{n-r},\ \ \ n\geq r.

(1)

(An alternative definition, not used in this paper, considers the negative binomial distribution as that of
N-r
.) For this distribution
Haldane, 1945
;
Ross, 2010
, chapter 4
,

\displaystyle\E[N]

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{p},

(2)

\displaystyle\E\left[\frac{1}{N-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{r-1},

(3)

\displaystyle\Var[N]

\displaystyle=\frac{r(1-p)}{p^{2}}.

(4)

Estimation of odds

The estimator of
\gamma=p/(1-p)
to be proposed is based on estimating
p
and
1/(1-p)
separately by means of inverse binomial sampling. Specifically, given
r_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
,
r_{1}\geq 3
, a random number
N_{1}
of samples are observed until
r_{1}

successes
occur. Then
\hat{p}_{1}=(r_{1}-1)/(N_{1}-1)
is an unbiased estimator of
p
as a consequence of (
3
), and its variance satisfies the bound
(
Pathak and Sathe, 1984
)

\Var[\hat{p}_{1}]\leq\frac{p^{2}(1-p)}{r_{1}-2+2p}.

(5)

Similarly, for
r_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, a second, independent set of samples is taken until
r_{2}

failures
are observed. The number of samples
N_{2}
is a negative binomial random variable with parameters
r_{2}
and
1-p
. Replacing
r
and
p
in (
2
) and (
4
) by
r_{2}
and
1-p
, it follows that
\hat{d}_{2}=N_{2}/r_{2}
is an unbiased estimator of
1/(1-p)
, and

\Var[\hat{d}_{2}]=\frac{\Var[N_{2}]}{r_{2}^{2}}=\frac{p}{r_{2}(1-p)^{2}}.

(6)

Given
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
r\geq 2
, the estimator of
\gamma
is defined as

\hat{\gamma}=\hat{p}_{1}\hat{d}_{2}

(7)

where
\hat{p}_{1}
and
\hat{d}_{2}
are obtained as above with
r_{1}=r+1
and
r_{2}=r-1
. Let
N=N_{1}+N_{2}
denote the total number of samples used by this estimator.

Theorem 1
.

For
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
r\geq 2
,
p\in(0,1)
, the estimator
\hat{\gamma}
given by (
7
) has the following properties:

\displaystyle\E[\hat{\gamma}]

\displaystyle=\gamma

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{\Var[\hat{\gamma}]}{\gamma^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{r-1}\left(1-\frac{p(1-p)}{r-1+2p}\right)

(9)

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{r-1}

(10)

\displaystyle\E[N]

\displaystyle=\frac{r+1-2p}{p(1-p)}.

(11)

Proof.

The estimators
\hat{p}_{1}
and
\hat{d}_{2}
are statistically independent, because they are based on two separate sets of samples. Therefore

\E[\hat{\gamma}]=\E[\hat{p}_{1}]\E[\hat{d}_{2}]=\frac{p}{1-p}=\gamma,

(12)

which proves (
8
).

Similarly, using (
5
) and (
6
) with
r_{1}=r+1
,
r_{2}=r-1
,

\begin{split}\Var[\hat{\gamma}]&=\E[\hat{p}_{1}^{2}]\E[\hat{d}_{2}^{2}]-\frac{p^{2}}{(1-p)^{2}}\\
&=\left(\Var[\hat{p}_{1}]+p^{2}\right)\left(\Var[\hat{d}_{2}]+\frac{1}{(1-p)^{2}}\right)-\frac{p^{2}}{(1-p)^{2}}\\
&\leq\left(\frac{p^{2}(1-p)}{r-1+2p}+p^{2}\right)\left(\frac{p}{(r-1)(1-p)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(1-p)^{2}}\right)-\frac{p^{2}}{(1-p)^{2}}\\
&=\frac{p^{2}}{(1-p)^{2}}\left(\frac{p(1-p)}{(r-1+2p)(r-1)}+\frac{1-p}{r-1+2p}+\frac{p}{r-1}\right)\\
&=\frac{(r-1+p+p^{2})p^{2}}{(r-1)(r-1+2p)(1-p)^{2}}\\
&=\frac{p^{2}}{(r-1)(1-p)^{2}}\left(1-\frac{p(1-p)}{r-1+2p}\right)<\frac{p^{2}}{(r-1)(1-p)^{2}},\end{split}

(13)

as in (
9
) and (
10
).

The average number of samples is obtained as

\E[N]=\E[N_{1}]+\E[N_{2}]=\frac{r+1}{p}+\frac{r-1}{1-p}=\frac{r+1-2p}{p(1-p)},

(14)

in agreement with (
11
).
∎

From Theorem
1
it stems that a relative error as low as desired can be guaranteed, irrespective of
p
, by choosing
r
large enough. For example, to achieve an RMSE smaller than
20\%
of the true value of
\gamma

it is sufficient to take
r=26
, according to (
10
). Guaranteeing better accuracy values requires larger
r
, which in turn causes the average sample size to increase, as given by (
11
). As a continuation of the example, decreasing the target RMSE by a factor of
1/\sqrt{2}
, i.e. reducing the target MSE to one half, requires an increase of
r
from
26
to
51
, with the result that
\E[N]
is approximately doubled.

To assess how good the trade-off between accuracy and average sample size is, it is meaningful to use Wolfowitz’s bound, which is a generalization of the Cramér-Rao inequality for sequential estimators
Wolfowitz, 1947
;
Ghosh et al., 1997
, section 4.3
. The bound indicates the lowest variance that an estimator can have for a given average sample size, provided that certain (mild) regularity conditions are satisfied by the distribution function of the observations, the sequential procedure and the estimator. When the observations are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with parameter
p
and the parameter to be estimated,
\theta
, is a differentiable function of
p
, Wolfowitz’s bound for an unbiased estimator
\hat{\theta}
is
(
DeGroot, 1959
, lemma 2.7)

\Var[\hat{\theta}]\E[N]\geq\left(\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}p}\right)^{2}p(1-p).

(15)

The
efficiency
of
\hat{\theta}
, which will be denoted as
\eta(\hat{\theta})
, can then be defined as Wolfowitz’s bound for
\Var[\hat{\theta}]\E[N]
divided by the actual value of that product achieved by
\hat{\theta}
:

\eta(\hat{\theta})=\frac{({\mathrm{d}\theta}/{\mathrm{d}p})^{2}\,p(1-p)}{\Var[\hat{\theta}]\E[N]}.

(16)

Taking
\theta=\gamma
,
\hat{\theta}=\hat{\gamma}
, which implies that
\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}p=1/(1-p)^{2}
and that
\Var[\hat{\gamma}]
and
\E[N]
satisfy (
9
) and (
11
) respectively, (
16
) becomes

\displaystyle\eta(\hat{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{(1-p)^{3}\Var[\hat{\gamma}]\E[N]}

(17)

\displaystyle>\frac{r-1}{r+1-2p}\left(1+\frac{p(1-p)}{r-1+p+p^{2}}\right)

(18)

\displaystyle>\frac{r-1}{r+1}.

(19)

Thus, the efficiency of
\hat{\gamma}
approaches
1
for large
r
.

Figure
1
compares, for several values of
r
, the lower bound (
18
) with the actual efficiency, obtained by Monte Carlo simulation. Specifically, for each
p
,
10^{8}
values of the estimate
\hat{\gamma}
are simulated using Bernoulli random variables with parameter
p
; the sample variance of
\hat{\gamma}
is computed; and this is used instead of the true variance in (
17
) to obtain
\eta(\hat{\gamma})
. As can be seen, the bound is quite tight, especially for
p
near
0
or
1
or for
r
not too small.

Estimation of log odds

The estimator of
\phi=\log(p/(1-p))
, to be defined precisely later, will be based on estimating
\log p
and
\log(1-p)
separately using inverse binomial sampling. Thus the estimation of
\log p
is analysed next.

Given
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
r\geq 2
, and denoting by
N
the number of samples required to obtain
r

\log p
can be obtained from
r

DeGroot (1959, theorem 4.1)

q=1-p

g(q)

q=0

\hat{\theta}(N)

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{(r-1)!\,c_{r,N-r}}{(N-1)!},

(20)

\displaystyle c_{r,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{d}q^{k}}\left.\frac{g(q)}{(1-q)^{r}}\right\rfloor_{q=0}.

(21)

Let the
n

H_{n}=1+1/2+\cdots+1/n

Proposition 1
.

For

20

\hat{\theta}(N)=-\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{r+1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{N-1}\right)=-H_{N-1}+H_{r-1}.

(22)

Proof.

Let

denote the rising factorial,

. Direct computation using (
21

shows that

, and for

c_{r,k}=-r^{(k-1)}+(r+k-1)c_{r,k-1}.

(23)

From (

) and (
23

\hat{\theta}(r)=0
, and for

\begin{split}\hat{\theta}(n)&=\frac{(r-1)!\,(-r^{(n-r-1)}+(n-1)c_{r,n-r-1})}{(n-1)!}\\

(24)

which is equivalent to (
22
).

4
Discussion and further work

Two sequential estimators have been proposed to estimate the odds
p/(1-p)
and the log odds
\log(1/(1-p))
, respectively, from i.i.d. Bernoulli observations with parameter
p
. The estimators are unbiased, and guarantee that the relative variance (for odds) or the variance (for log odds) are below a desired value, irrespective of
p
. As far as the author is aware, no previously known estimators of the odds or log odds have this property. The guaranteed accuracy level is determined by the parameter
r
. Larger values of
r
ensure better accuracy, at the cost of increasing the average sample size. Thus the parameter
r
can be used to control the trade-off between these two magnitudes. The performance of the estimators is close to optimal in the sense of Wolfowitz’s bound, with efficiency values greater than
(r-1)/(r+1)
and
(r-5/4)/r
for each estimator respectively.

The problem of estimating the odds or log odds is relevant in many areas of science and engineering. The proposed estimators can be used in any application, as long as the observations are i.i.d. Bernoulli variables.

Each of the two estimators is based on applying inverse binomial sampling separately with parameters
p
and
1-p
, where the latter is obtained by swapping the role of successes and failures in the Bernoulli samples. This approach might appear wasteful, because information about both
p
and
1-p
could in principle be inferred using only one of the two sample sets. However, since the two probabilities are complementary, it is never the case that the two separate procedures require a large average number of samples at the same time; and indeed performance turns out to be close to optimal, as stated.

On the other hand, using a single set of samples to infer
p/(1-p)
or its logarithm would pose some problems. As follows from
DeGroot (1959, theorem 4.1)
,
there is no unbiased estimator of these parameters in inverse binomial sampling. For
(1-p)/p
an unbiased estimator does exist; but then it is not possible to guarantee a relative MSE, because as
p
grows the sample size decreases, when it should increase so as to reduce the MSE. By similar arguments, an unbiased estimator of
\log((1-p)/p)
exists but is not useful either. For fixed-size sampling there are no unbiased estimators of any of these quantities
(
DeGroot, 1959
, section 4)
; and in any case, with a fixed sample size it is not possible to guarantee a desired relative MSE for odds, or a desired MSE for log odds. Moreover, regardless of the rule used for deciding the sample size, using a single sample set can make the error analysis more difficult. Namely, if the estimator is expressed as the product or sum of two estimators (as has been done in Sections
2
and
3
), those two estimators are not statistically independent, because they are based on the same observations.

The approach used in this paper could in principle be extended to estimating the probability ratio (or relative risk)
p_{1}/p_{2}
or the odds ratio
p_{1}(1-p_{2})/(p_{2}(1-p_{1}))
, as well as their logarithms, for two populations with parameters
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. For instance, to estimate
p_{1}/p_{2}
with guaranteed relative MSE, two inverse binomial sampling procedures would be used for estimating
p_{1}
and
1/p_{2}
respectively; and error bounds analogous to those derived in the paper are easy to obtain. However, an important limitation of using this method with two populations is that it does not provide control on the proportion of samples taken from each population. Although it is possible to choose different pairs of
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
that guarantee approximately the same estimation accuracy, the choice of
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
does not determine the distribution of
N_{1}/N_{2}
, or the ratio
\E[N_{1}]/\E[N_{2}]
, as these depend also on
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
. Thus, each value of the unknown ratio
p_{1}/p_{2}
would require a different choice of
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
in order to attain a given
\E[N_{1}]/\E[N_{2}]
and a desired accuracy.

Having control on
N_{1}/N_{2}
, or at least on
\E[N_{1}]/\E[N_{2}]
, when sampling from two populations is often important in applications. For example, if observations are taken in pairs, one sample from each population, then necessarily
N_{1}=N_{2}
. Further work is needed to devise a method that can incorporate this type of restriction while guaranteeing a given accuracy. One possible approach is to use two-stage sampling:
a small sample set of each population is taken to produce an initial, rough estimate of
p_{1}/p_{2}
; using that information the sizes of a second sample set of each population are obtained (for example, using two inverse binomial sampling procedures with
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
computed from the estimated
p_{1}/p_{2}
); and from these new sample sets the final estimation of the parameter of interest is obtained.

Another generalization of this work would be to allow for the observations to be statistically dependent. In this more general setting, a natural assumption is that the sequence of observations is exchangeable. This means that its joint distribution of any order is invariant to permutations, i.e. the statistical properties of the sequence are not affected by changes in the position of its elements. Then, the correlation between any pair of samples is the same, and is always non-negative
(
Kingman, 1978
)
.
In these conditions, the estimators proposed in this paper are not necessarily unbiased, and their variance is expected to be larger than in the i.i.d. case, because, for the same sample size, positively correlated observations are “less informative” than independent ones. Alternatively, dependence between samples can be modelled by assuming that the Bernoulli variables form a Markov chain, where the transition matrix is determined by the usual parameter
p
and an additional parameter that controls the correlation between consecutive samples
(
Lindqvist, 1978
)
. This will, again, introduce bias and affect the variance of the estimators.
