Disagreement coupling of Gibbs processes with an application to Poisson approximation
Günter Last, Moritz Otto

Abstract
We discuss a thinning and an embedding procedure to construct finite Gibbs processes with a given Papangelou intensity. Extending the approach in [ 18 , 17 ] we will use this to couple two finite Gibbs processes with different boundary conditions. As one application we will establish Poisson approximation of point processes derived from certain infinite volume Gibbs processes via dependent thinning. As another application we shall discuss empty space probabilities of certain Gibbs processes.



Keywords:
Gibbs process, disagreement coupling, Poisson approximation,
Papangelou intensity, Poisson thinning, Poisson embedding, empty space probabilities

AMS MSC 2010:
60G55, 60D05, 60K35

1
Introduction

In the seminal paper
[
38
]
the authors
introduced
disagreement percolation
for discrete
Markov random fields to control boundary effects and
to establish new uniqueness criteria for Gibbs measures on graphs.
The idea is to locally couple two fields with different
boundary conditions and to control the disagreement
with a stochastically dominating percolation process.
In
[
18
,
17
]
the method was developed for Gibbs processes
in continuum. The first aim of this paper is to establish

disagreement coupling
in great generality using modern
point process theory. This way we shall also close a gap
left in
[
18
,
17
]
. The second main aim is to combine this
coupling with a recent result from
[
6
]

(generalizing a classical result from
[
1
]
)
to obtain Poisson approximation of point processes
derived of Gibbsian functionals.
We shall also discuss empty space probabilities of Gibbs processes.

Gibbs processes form an important class of point processes.
In (mathematical) physics they describe the thermodynamical behavior
of interacting particles; see
[
34
]
. As a mathematical model they are much more
versatile than the completely independent Poisson process
(see e.g.
[
9
]
). They are also quite popular
in spatial statistics; see e.g.
[
30
,
7
]
.
Getting a handle on distributional properties of Gibbs processes is not easy.
However, starting with the seminal paper
[
35
]
, the last decade
has seen some efforts to understand some asymptotic
properties of functionals of Gibbs processes in infinite volume, at least
if the process is in a certain sense
close to a Poisson process. Our paper aims at adding
to this development.

Let us shortly summarize the structure and main results of the paper.
Section
2
collects some basic facts on Gibbs processes based on
the classical point process approach from
[
31
]
and

[
27
]
. The state space is assumed to be Borel but is
otherwise not required to have any topological properties. The main
result of Section
4
is the thinning representation
from Theorem
4.3
which extends a result in

[
17
]
to general Borel spaces. Our proof is based on Lebesgue–Stieltjes calculus and
is different from the one given in
[
17
]
. In
Section
5
we prove with Theorem
5.1
a
version of the thinning representation based on embedding into a
Poisson process on a suitable product space. This result is quite
convenient for coupling purposes and should be compared with the
classical Poisson embedding of marked point processes based on
stochastic intensities; see
[
5
]
. This embedding does not
require any boundedness assumptions on the Papangelou intensity.

In Section
6
we formulate and study the disagreement
coupling of two (local) Gibbs processes with the same Papangelou
intensity but different boundary conditions.
To this end we consider a
symmetric and measurable relation on the state space equipping each
point configuration with a graph structure. At each point of the
state space the Papangelou intensity is then assumed to depend only on
the cluster connected to this point.
Theorem
6.3
generalizes Theorem 3.1 in

[
17
]
and shows that the points of disagreement of two
recursively defined point processes are connected to the boundary
conditions. The main part of the proof (which seems to be missing in

[
17
]
) is devoted to checking that the
coupled processes have the desired Gibbs distribution.
Our main tool here is a basically well-known spatial Markov
property of the Poisson process. We work here
with a rather general definition of a stopping
set given in the Appendix of
[
24
]
.
We have chosen the terminology “disagreement coupling”
as opposed to “disagreement percolation” because this coupling
might be potentially useful also beyond a percolation setting.
In fact, Theorem
6.3
does not require the
absence of percolation in the dominating Poisson process.
But indeed, so far all current applications of disagreement coupling
require the absence of percolation. The main example
are proofs of uniqueness of certain Gibbs distributions;
see
[
18
,
38
,
17
]
.
A very recent result in this area
is
[
3
]
, a paper that makes crucial use of
the general setting in Theorem
6.3
.
In
[
2
]
disagreement coupling was used to
establish exponential decorrelation of certain Gibbs processes.

In Section
7
we discuss empty space probabilities
of Gibbs processes. In particular we show that a large class
of Gibbs processes are Poisson-like as defined in
[
35
]
.
This property is needed in the proof of Theorem
10.1

but we believe that it is of importance in its own right.

Section
8
contains some (basically well-known) material on Palm distributions of
a Gibbs process which is needed later. In Theorem
9.1
we
prove a bound on the total variation distance between an appropriately
scaled thinning of a marked Gibbs process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
bounded Papangelou intensity and a Poisson process. Our proof adapts a
coupling technique for Poisson approximation from

[
32
, Theorem 3.3]
to Gibbs processes.
In doing so it exploits the
disagreement coupling studied in Section
6
for a
Gibbs process and its Palm version.
Similarly as in
[
38
,
17
]
we assume that
a random graph defined on the points of a dominating Poisson
process does not percolate. In fact we need an exponentially
small cluster size; see (
9.13
).
This condition appears to be weaker than subcriticality
of an associated spatial branching type process, as assumed
in
[
35
]
; see
[
2
]
for a short discussion.
Our general bound involves expectations with respect to
the Gibbs distribution. This cannot be avoided.
Still, apart from subcriticality of the dominating percolation model,
our total variation bound is more explicit and more general than the one
presented in
[
36
]
.
We demonstrate this in Section
10
by applying
Theorem
9.1
to Matérn
type I thinnings of Gibbs processes. In this situation
the bound from Theorem
9.1
becomes very
concrete.

2
Gibbs processes

Let
({\mathbb{X}},{\mathcal{X}})
be a Borel space equipped with a

\sigma
-finite measure
\lambda
and define
the set ring
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}:=\{B\in{\mathcal{X}}:\lambda(B)<\infty\}
.
Let
{\mathbf{N}}\equiv{\mathbf{N}}({\mathbb{X}})
be the space of all measures on
{\mathbb{X}}

which are
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
-valued on
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
(where
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}:={\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
) and
let
{\mathcal{N}}\equiv{\mathcal{N}}({\mathbb{X}})
denote the smallest
\sigma
-field
such that
\mu\mapsto\mu(B)
is measurable for
all
B\in{\mathcal{X}}
. A
point process
is a random
element
\eta
of
{\mathbf{N}}
, defined over some fixed
probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{P})
.
The
intensity measure
of
\eta
is the
measure
\mathbb{E}[\eta]
defined by
\mathbb{E}[\eta](B):=\mathbb{E}\eta(B)
,
B\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
For any
\sigma
-finite measure
\nu
on
{\mathbb{X}}
we let

\Pi_{\nu}
denote the distribution of a
Poisson process

(see e.g.
[
25
]
) on
{\mathbb{X}}

with intensity measure
\nu
. Of particular interest for
us are the distributions
\Pi_{\alpha\lambda_{B}}
, where
\alpha\geq 0

and
\lambda_{B}:=\lambda(B\cap\cdot)
is the restriction of

\lambda
to some
B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
. We use the latter notation for any
measure on
{\mathbb{X}}
. For each
B\in{\mathcal{X}}
we define

{\mathbf{N}}_{B}:=\{\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}:\mu(B^{c})=0\}=\{\mu_{B}:\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}\}
.
For
\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}
we write
x\in\mu
if
\mu(\{x\})>0
.
If
\mu(\{x\})\in\{0,1\}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
then

\mu
is called
simple
.

Let
\kappa\colon{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be measurable.
A point process
\xi
on
{\mathbb{X}}
is called a
Gibbs process

with
Papangelou intensity
(PI)
\kappa
if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int f(x,\xi)\,\xi(\mathrm{d}x)\Bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int f(x,\xi+\delta_{x})\kappa(x,\xi)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg],

(2.1)

for each measurable
f\colon{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
The latter are the
GNZ equations
named after Georgii, Nguyen and Zessin

[
13
,
31
]
.

In the present generality it is not known, whether Gibbs processes
exist. Partial answers (under varying assumptions on
\kappa
) are given
for instance in
[
34
,
26
,
10
,
12
,
20
]
.
A considerably more challenging topic is the distributional uniqueness of
a Gibbs process. From a mathematical point of view not much is known in this
regard. We refer to
[
9
,
20
]
for recent surveys.

Equation (
2.1
) can be generalized. For
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define
a measurable function
\kappa_{m}\colon{\mathbb{X}}^{m}\times{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
by

\displaystyle\kappa_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},\mu):=\kappa(x_{1},\mu)\kappa(x_{2},\mu+\delta_{x_{1}})\cdots\kappa(x_{m},\mu+\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{m-1}}).

Note that
\kappa_{1}=\kappa
.
If
\xi
is a Gibbs process with PI
\kappa
then
we have for each
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and for each measurable
f\colon{\mathbb{X}}^{m}\times{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\bigg[\int f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},\xi)\,\xi^{(m)}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}))\bigg]

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},\xi+\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{m}})\kappa_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},\xi)\,\lambda^{m}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}))\bigg],

where
\mu^{(m)}
is the
m
-th factorial measure of
\mu
; see
[
25
]
.
This follows by induction, using that

\displaystyle\kappa_{m+1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m+1},\mu)=\kappa_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},\mu)\kappa(x_{m+1},\mu+\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{m}}).

For each
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
let
\tilde{\kappa}_{m}\colon{\mathbb{X}}^{m}\times{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

be the symmetrization of
\kappa_{m}
in the first
m
arguments.
The
Hamiltonian

H\colon{\mathbf{N}}\times{\mathbf{N}}\to(-\infty,\infty]

(based on
\kappa
) is defined by

\displaystyle H(\mu,\psi):=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $\mu({\mathbb{X}})=0$},\\
-\log\tilde{\kappa}_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},\psi),&\text{if $\mu=\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{m}}$},\\
\infty,&\text{if $\mu({\mathbb{X}})=\infty$}.\end{cases}

(2.3)

For
B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
the
partition function

Z_{B}\colon{\mathbf{N}}\to[0,\infty]
is defined by

\displaystyle Z_{B}(\psi):=\int e^{-H(\mu,\psi)}\,\Pi_{\lambda_{B}}(\mathrm{d}\mu),\quad\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}.

(2.4)

Since
H(0,\psi)=0
for all finite
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}
we have that

\displaystyle Z_{B}(\psi)\geq e^{-\lambda(B)},\quad B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}.

Note that the right-hand side is positive.
For
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}

the
Gibbs measure

\Pi_{B,\nu}
on
{\mathbf{N}}
is defined by

\displaystyle\Pi_{B,\nu}:=Z_{B}(\nu)^{-1}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{\mu\in\cdot\}e^{-H(\mu,\nu)}\,\Pi_{\lambda_{B}}(\mathrm{d}\mu)

(2.5)

provided that
Z_{B}(\nu)<\infty
. If
Z_{B}(\nu)=\infty
we set
\Pi_{B,\nu}:=\Pi_{\lambda_{B}}
.
This measure is concentrated on
{\mathbf{N}}_{B}
.

It was proved in
[
27
,
31
]
that
if
\xi
is a Gibbs process with PI
\kappa
then

\kappa_{m}(\cdot,\xi)
is almost surely symmetric for each
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{B}(\xi_{B^{c}})<\infty)=1,\quad B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0},

(2.6)

and, for each measurable
f\colon{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(\xi_{B})\mid\xi_{B^{c}}]=\int f(\mu)\,\Pi_{B,\xi_{B^{c}}}(\mathrm{d}\mu),\quad B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0},

(2.7)

where relations involving conditional expectations are assumed
to hold almost surely.
These are the
DLR-equations
; see
[
34
,
21
,
26
]
.
Note that (
2.7
) implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi(B)=0\mid\xi_{B^{c}})=e^{-\lambda(B)}Z_{B}(\xi_{B^{c}})^{-1},\quad B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}.

(2.8)

A natural requirement on
\kappa
is the
cocycle formula

\displaystyle\kappa(x,\mu)\kappa(y,\mu+\delta_{x})=\kappa(y,\mu)\kappa(x,\mu+\delta_{y}),

(2.9)

which should hold at least
for
\lambda^{2}\otimes\Pi_{\lambda}
-a.e.
(x,y,\mu)
.
(Otherwise one cannot hope for the existence of a Gibbs process.)
It then follows for each
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
that

\displaystyle\kappa_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},\mu)=\tilde{\kappa}_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},\mu),\quad

(2.10)

for
\lambda^{m}\otimes\Pi_{\lambda}
-a.e.
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},\mu)
.
Even though not stated in the present generality, the following result was proved
in the seminal work
[
31
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that
\kappa
satisfies
(
2.9
) for each
\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}
and for
\lambda^{2}
-a.e.
(x,y)
.
Assume that
\xi
is a point process satisfying (
2.6
)
and (
2.7
). Then
\xi
is a Gibbs process with PI
\kappa
.

In fact, the proof of Theorem
2.1
shows the following.

Corollary 2.2
.

Assume that
\lambda({\mathbb{X}})<\infty
.
Assume also that
\kappa
satisfies the assumption of Theorem
2.1

and that
Z_{\mathbb{X}}:=\int e^{-H(\mu,0)}\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)<\infty
.
Then

\displaystyle Z_{{\mathbb{X}}}^{-1}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{\mu\in\cdot\}e^{-H(\mu,0)}\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)

(2.11)

is the distribution of a Gibbs process with PI
\kappa
.

For
B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
and
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}
we
define
\kappa_{\psi}\colon{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
by

\displaystyle\kappa_{\psi}(x,\mu):=\kappa(x,\psi+\mu)

(2.12)

Let
\kappa_{B,\psi}
denote the restriction of
\kappa_{\psi}
to
B\times{\mathbf{N}}_{B}
.
Corollary
2.2
shows that
the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{B}\in\cdot\mid\xi_{B^{c}})

is almost surely a Gibbs process with PI
\kappa_{B,\xi_{B^{c}}}
;
see also
[
2
, Lemma 2.5]
.

In Section
6
we shall need the following property of Gibbs measures.
Let
B,C\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
with
C\subset B

and suppose that
\nu\in N_{{\mathbb{X}}\setminus B}
satisfies
Z_{B}(\nu)<\infty
.
Then
Z_{B\setminus C}(\nu+\mu_{C})<\infty
for
\Pi_{B,\nu}
-a.e.
\mu

and

\displaystyle\Pi_{B,\nu}=\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{\mu_{C}+\mu^{\prime}\in\cdot\}\,\Pi_{B\setminus C,\nu+\mu_{C}}(\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime})\,\Pi_{B,\nu}(\mathrm{d}\mu).

(2.13)

This is the DLR-equation for a Gibbs process
with PI
\kappa_{B,\nu}
. It can be proved directly, using
the definition (
2.5
) of a Gibbs measure and the fact that
a Poisson process on
B
is the sum of two independent
Poisson processes on
C
and
B\setminus C
.

Finally we introduce some (partially standard) notation used later in the paper.
Let
\nu
be a signed measure on some measurable
space
({\mathbb{Y}},{\mathcal{Y}})
which is locally finite in a suitable sense.
For instance
\nu
could be the difference of two measures
on
{\mathbb{X}}
which are finite on
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
.
Then
\nu
can be uniquely written as the difference of two
mutually singular locally finite measure, whose sum is the
total variation measure
|\nu|
of
\nu
. The total mass

\|\nu\|:=|\nu|({\mathbb{Y}})
(which could be infinite)
is the
total variation norm
of
\nu
.
If
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{N}}({\mathbb{X}})
then also
|\mu-\mu^{\prime}|\in{\mathbf{N}}({\mathbb{X}})
.
If
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
are locally finite measure on
{\mathbb{Y}}
, then we can define
as in
[
6
]

\displaystyle\mathbf{d_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):=\sup\{|\mu(A)-\mu^{\prime}(A)|:A\in{\mathcal{Y}},\mu(A)<\infty,\mu^{\prime}(A)<\infty\}.

(2.14)

It is not hard to show that

\displaystyle\mathbf{d_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|\leq 2\mathbf{d_{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime});

see
[
6
, (2.2)]
for the case
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{N}}
.
If
\mu({\mathbb{Y}})=\mu^{\prime}({\mathbb{Y}})<\infty
(and in particular if
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
are probability measures)
then the second inequality above becomes an identity.

3
Assumptions and Examples

In this paper a measurable function

\kappa\colon{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
will always denote
a PI of a Gibbs process.
To simplify the presentation we shall always assume (sometime without further
mentioning) that
\kappa
satisfies the cocycle
identity (
2.9
) for all
(x,y,\mu)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}\times{\mathbf{N}}
.
Very much as in the literature we will make two types
of assumptions on
\kappa
, namely stability
assumptions and assumptions on the local dependence of
\kappa(x,\mu)
on

\mu
.
We say that
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}
is
stable on
W\in{\mathcal{X}}
if

\displaystyle Z_{B}(\psi_{B^{c}})<\infty,\quad B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}\cap W,

(Dom1)

where
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}\cap W:=\{B\cap W:B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}\}.

If this holds for
W={\mathbb{X}}
then we say that

\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}
is
stable
.
We say that
\kappa
is
locally stable

if there exists a measurable function

\alpha\colon{\mathbb{X}}\to(0,\infty)
satisfying

\displaystyle\kappa(x,\mu)\leq\alpha(x),\quad(x,\mu)\in{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}({\mathbb{X}}),\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{B}\alpha(x)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty,\quad B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}.

(Dom2)

Remark 3.1
.

A sufficient condition for
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}

to be stable on
W\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
is as follows. Suppose that

\alpha_{W,\psi}\colon W\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is measurable and satisfies

\int_{W}\alpha_{W,\psi}(x)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty
.
If

\displaystyle\sup\{\kappa(x,\mu_{W}+\psi_{W^{c}}):\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}\}\leq\alpha_{W,\psi}(x),\quad x\in W,\,\mu\in{\mathbf{N}},

(3.1)

then the definition of
Z_{B}
shows that (Dom1) holds. In particular, if
\kappa
is locally
stable, then all
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}
are stable.

To state our second type of assumptions we assume
that
\sim
is a symmetric relation on
{\mathbb{X}}
such
that
\{(x,y):x\sim y\}
is a measurable subset of
{\mathbb{X}}^{2}
.
Given
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
A\subset{\mathbb{X}}
we write
x\sim A
if there exists

z\in A
such that
x\sim z
.
We say that
x,z\in{\mathbb{X}}
are
connected
via
A
if there
exist
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in A
such that

z_{i}\sim z_{i+1}
for
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
where
z_{0}:=x
and
z_{n+1}:=z
.
We use this terminology also for counting measures instead of
A
.
Define the
cluster

C(x,\mu)\in{\mathbf{N}}
of
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
in
\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}
by

\displaystyle C(x,\mu):=\int{\mathbf{1}}\{y\in\cdot\}{\mathbf{1}}\{\text{$y\neq x$ and $x$ and $y$ are connected via $\mu$}\}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}y).

We will consider the assumption

\displaystyle\kappa(x,\mu)=\kappa(x,C(x,\mu)),\quad(x,\mu)\in{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}.

(Loc1)

For
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
we let
N_{x}:=\{y\in{\mathbb{X}}\setminus\{x\}:y\sim x\}
.
A stronger version of (Loc1) is

\displaystyle\kappa(x,\mu)=\kappa(x,\mu_{N_{x}}),\quad(x,\mu)\in{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}.

(Loc2)

Of course, in such a general setting the assumptions (Loc1) and (Loc2)
do not put a restriction on
\kappa
. It is the specific choice of
\sim
together
with further assumptions on
\lambda
, which will make them meaningful.

The following examples will illustrate the forgoing assumptions.

Example 3.2
.

(Gibbs processes with pair potential)

Suppose that
U
is a
pair potential
on
{\mathbb{X}}
that
is a measurable and symmetric function

U\colon{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbb{X}}\to(\infty,\infty]
.
We assume that there exists
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j}U(x_{i},x_{j})\geq-Cn,\quad x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in{\mathbb{X}},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

This is a classical stability assumption on a pair potential; see
[
34
]
.
Let
\alpha>0
and assume that
\kappa
is given by

\displaystyle\kappa(x,\mu)=\alpha\exp\bigg[-\int U(x,y)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}y)\bigg],\quad x\in{\mathbb{X}},\,\mu\in\mathbf{N},

whenever the integral in the right-hand side exists.
Let
W\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
and
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}
. A simple calculation shows
that
Z_{W}(\psi_{W^{c}})<\infty
if

\displaystyle\int_{W}\exp\bigg[-\int U(x,y)\,\psi(\mathrm{d}y)\bigg]\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty.

(3.2)

Assume moreover that
U
is bounded from below
and let
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}
satisfy (
3.2
). Then it is easy to see
that
\psi
is stable on
W
and also that
\psi
is a regular boundary
condition for
W
.
Define
x\sim y\Leftrightarrow U(x,y)\neq 0
.
Then (Loc2) holds. If
U\geq 0
, then (Dom2) holds.

The existence of Gibbs processes with a pair potential
was shown under varying assumptions. We refer here to
the seminal work
[
33
,
34
]
(treating the case
{\mathbb{X}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
),
to
[
20
]
(working on a complete separable
metric space and assuming
U\geq 0
) and
to
[
12
]
(working on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
but allowing for
infinite range pair potentials which may take negative values).

Example 3.3
.

(Strauss process)

Suppose that
\mathbb{X}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and

\lambda=\lambda_{d}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
for some probability measure

\mathbb{Q}
on
[0,\infty)
. Define
(x,r)\sim(y,s)\Leftrightarrow\|x-y\|\leq r+s
. Consider for

\alpha>0
and
\beta\in[0,1]
the PI

\displaystyle\kappa(x,r,\mu):=\alpha\beta^{\mu(N_{(x,r)})},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},r>0,\mu\in\mathbf{N}.

A Gibbs process
\xi
with this PI is called Strauss
process. For
\beta=0
this is a hard-core process. It
is easy to see that
\kappa
satisfies (Dom2) and (Loc2).

Example 3.4
.

(Area interaction process)

As for the Strauss process, let
\mathbb{X}:={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and

\lambda:=\lambda_{d}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
for some probability measure

\mathbb{Q}
on
[0,\infty)
. Define
(x,r)\sim(y,s)\Leftrightarrow\|x-y\|\leq r+s
.
For
(x,r)\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}
define

\displaystyle V(x,r,\mu):=\lambda_{d}\bigg(B(x,r)\setminus\bigcup_{(y,s)\in\mu_{N_{(x,r)}}}B(y,s)\bigg).

Consider for
\alpha>0
and
\beta\in[0,1]
the PI

\displaystyle\kappa(x,r,\mu):=\alpha\beta^{V(x,r,\mu)},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},r>0,\mu\in\mathbf{N}.

Then (Loc2) holds.
Gibbs processes with this PI are called area interaction
processes. Their
existence is shown in
[
33
]
for
\mathbb{Q}
with finite support and in

[
8
]
for dimension
d\leq 2
.
If
\mathbb{Q}([r_{1},\infty))=1
for some
r_{1}>0
, then
(Dom2) is satisfied.

Example 3.5
.

(Continuum random cluster model)

Let
\mathbb{X}:={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and

\lambda:=\lambda_{d}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
for some probability measure

\mathbb{Q}
on
[0,\infty)
. Define

(x,r)\sim(y,s)\Leftrightarrow\|x-y\|\leq r+s
. Let
\alpha>0
,

q>0
and
k(x,r,\mu)
denote the number of connected components in

\mu
connected to
(x,r)
in
\mu+\delta_{(x,r)}
and define

\displaystyle\kappa(x,r,\mu)=\alpha q^{1-k(x,r,\mu)},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},r>0,\mu\in\mathbf{N}.

It is clear that (Loc1) holds. If
q\geq 1
then (Dom2) holds. Moreover it was shown in
[
11
]

that in both cases there exists a Gibbs process

\xi
with PI
\kappa
. If there are
0<r_{1}<r_{2}<\infty
such that

, then there exists a constant
c_{d}
(depending on
the dimension

) such that
\kappa(x,r,\mu)\leq q^{1-c_{d}}
and

Example 3.6
.

(Widom-Rowlinson model)
For
m\geq 2
let

\mathbb{M}:=\{1,\dots,m\}
,

\mathbb{X}:={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\mathbb{M}

, where

\mathbb{Q}
is a probability measure on

and

denotes uniform distribution on

. Define

\displaystyle(x,r,\ell)\sim(y,s,k)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\|x-y\|\leq r+s\,\text{ and }\ell\neq k,\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,r,s\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\,\ell,k\in\mathbb{M}.

and let

\displaystyle\kappa(x,r,\ell,\mu)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $\mu(N_{(x,r,\ell)})>0$},\\
\alpha,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If we think of the elements of

as colors, the

[

]

4.2]

4
Thinning presentation of finite Gibbs processes

In this section we assume that the measure
\lambda
is finite and diffuse.
We consider a measurable function
\kappa\colon{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

satisfying the stability assumption (Dom2).
We know from
[
15
]
(see
[
2
]
for the infinite case)
that a Gibbs process with PI
\kappa
is stochastically
dominated by a Poisson process with intensity measure
\alpha\lambda
,
defined by
\alpha\lambda(\mathrm{d}x):=\alpha(x)\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)
.
In this section we shall reestablish this result
by means of a rather explicit thinning construction, introduced in

[
17
, Proposition 4.1]
.
(The proof was amended in the latest preprint version of that paper.)
We work here in greater generality
using fundamental properties of Poisson processes
and standard techniques from Lebesgue–Stieltjes calculus.

We need to introduce some notation. By definition of a Borel space there
exists an injective measurable mapping
\varphi\colon{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

such that
\varphi({\mathbb{X}})
and the inverse mapping

\varphi^{-1}\colon\varphi({\mathbb{X}})\to{\mathbb{X}}
are measurable.
We introduce a total order
\leq
on
{\mathbb{X}}
by writing

x\leq y
if
\varphi(x)\leq\varphi(y)
. We write

x<y
if
x\leq y
and
x\neq y
. As usual we can then
define
(x,y]:=\{z\in{\mathbb{X}}:x<z\leq y\}
. Other intervals
are defined analogously. For instance we write

(-\infty,y):=\{z\in{\mathbb{X}}:z<y\}
.
Let
{\mathbf{N}}^{*}\equiv{\mathbf{N}}^{*}(\mathbb{X})

denote the set of all simple and finite elements
of
{\mathbf{N}}
.
It is easy to see that the mapping

(x,\mu)\mapsto(\mu_{(-\infty,x)},\mu_{(-\infty,x]})
is measurable
on
{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}^{*}
. We abbreviate
\mu_{x}:=\mu_{(-\infty,x)}
.

Assume that
\kappa\colon{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}^{*}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

is a measurable function satisfying the cocycle assumption
(
2.9
) for all
(x,y,\mu)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}\times{\mathbf{N}}^{*}
.
We define the partition functions by (
2.4
).
Define a function
p\colon{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}^{*}\to[0,1]
by

\displaystyle p(x,\psi):=\kappa(x,\psi_{x})\frac{Z_{(x,\infty)}(\psi_{x}+\delta_{x})}{Z_{(x,\infty)}(\psi_{x})},\quad(x,\psi)\in{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}^{*},

(4.1)

where
\infty/\infty:=0
. Since

\displaystyle Z_{(x,\infty)}(\psi_{x}+\delta_{x})

\displaystyle=\int e^{-H(\mu_{(x,\infty)},\psi+\delta_{x})}\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu),

\displaystyle Z_{[x,\infty)}(\psi_{x})

\displaystyle=\int e^{-H(\mu_{(x,\infty)},\psi)}\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu),

the function
p
is measurable. It turns out that it also satisfies
(Dom2).

Lemma 4.1
.

We have that

\displaystyle p(x,\psi)\leq\alpha(x),\quad(x,\psi)\in{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}^{*}.

(4.2)

Proof:
Let
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}^{*}
.
By the multivariate Mecke equation (
[
25
, Theorem 4.4]
),

\displaystyle\kappa(x,

\displaystyle\psi_{x})Z_{(x,\infty)}(\psi_{x}+\delta_{x})=\kappa(x,\psi_{x})\int e^{-H(\mu,\psi_{x}+\delta_{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\lambda_{(x,\infty)}}(\mathrm{d}\mu)

\displaystyle=\kappa(x,\psi_{x})e^{-\lambda((x,\infty))}\left(1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\int_{\mathbb{X}^{m}}\kappa_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},\psi_{x}+\delta_{x})\,\lambda_{(x,\infty)}^{m}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}))\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda((x,\infty))}\left(\kappa(x,\psi_{x})+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\int_{\mathbb{X}^{m}}\kappa_{m+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},x,\psi_{x})\,\lambda_{(x,\infty)}^{m}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}))\right).

Since

\kappa_{m+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},x,\psi_{x})=\kappa(x,\psi_{x}+\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{m}})\kappa_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},\psi_{x})

and
\kappa(x,\cdot)\leq\alpha(x)
by (Dom2), the above is
bounded by

\displaystyle\alpha(x)e^{-\lambda((x,\infty))}\left(1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\int_{\mathbb{X}^{m}}\kappa_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},\psi_{x})\,\lambda_{(x,\infty)}^{m}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}))\right)

which equals
\alpha(x)Z_{(x,\infty)}(\psi_{x})
. This proves (
4.2
).
∎

Let us define a kernel
K_{\kappa,\alpha}
from
{\mathbf{N}}^{*}
to
{\mathbf{N}}^{*}
by

\displaystyle K_{\kappa,\alpha}(\mu,\cdot):=\sum_{\psi\leq\mu}{\mathbf{1}}\{\psi\in\cdot\}\prod_{x\in\psi}\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi)\prod_{x\in\mu-\psi}\big(1-\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi)\big),\quad\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}^{*}.

(4.3)

The next lemma shows that
K_{\kappa,\alpha}
is a probability kernel.

Lemma 4.2
.

We have that
K_{\kappa,\alpha}(\mu,\mathbf{N}^{*})=1
for all

\mu\in\mathbf{N}^{*}
.

Proof:
We use induction on the number of point of
\mu
.
Obviously,
K_{\kappa,\alpha}(0,\mathbf{N}^{*})=1
, where

0
is the zero measure.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y\in\mathbb{X}
with

x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}<y
. Define
\mu:=\delta_{x_{1}}+\dots+\delta_{x_{n}}
.
From the definition of
K_{\kappa,\alpha}
we obtain that
we have

\displaystyle K_{\kappa,\alpha}(\mu+\delta_{y},\mathbf{N}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\psi\leq\mu+\delta_{y}}\prod_{x\in\psi}\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi)\prod_{x\in\mu+\delta_{y}-\psi}(1-\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\psi\leq\mu}\Bigg(\prod_{x\in\psi+\delta_{y}}\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi+\delta_{y})\prod_{x\in\mu-\psi}(1-\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi+\delta_{y}))

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\prod_{x\in\psi}\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi)\prod_{x\in\mu+\delta_{y}-\psi}\left(1-\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi)\right)\Bigg).

Since
p(x,\mu)=p(x,\mu_{x})
for all
(x,\mu)\in{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}^{*}
and

x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}<y
, we obtain that the above equals

\displaystyle\sum_{\psi\leq\mu}\Bigg(\alpha(y)^{-1}p(y,\psi+\delta_{y})\prod_{x\in\psi}\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi)\prod_{x\in\mu-\psi}(1-\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi))

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+(1-\alpha(y)^{-1}p(y,\psi))\prod_{x\in\psi}\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi)\prod_{x\in\mu-\psi}(1-\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi))\Bigg).

Since
p(y,\psi+\delta_{y})=p(y,\psi)
, this equals

\displaystyle\sum_{\psi\leq\mu}\prod_{x\in\psi}\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi)\prod_{x\in\mu-\psi}(1-\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\psi))=K_{\kappa,\alpha}(\mu,\mathbf{N}^{*}).

This finishes the induction.∎

The following thinning representation is the main result
of this section. It extends
[
17
, Proposition 4.1]

to general Borel spaces.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that
\lambda
is finite and diffuse
and that (Dom2) holds. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{\kappa,\alpha}:=\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{\psi\in\cdot\}\,K_{\kappa,\alpha}(\mu,\mathrm{d}\psi)\,\Pi_{\alpha\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)

(4.4)

is the distribution of a Gibbs process with PI
\kappa
.

Proof:
The proof will use Corollary
4.5
, to be proved later.
Let
f\colon{\mathbf{N}}^{*}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be measurable. Then

\displaystyle\iint f(\psi)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\kappa,\alpha}(\mathrm{d}\psi)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{\psi({\mathbb{X}})=n\}f(\psi)\,K_{\kappa,\alpha}(\mu,\mathrm{d}\psi)\,\Pi_{\alpha\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu).

Since
\alpha\lambda
is diffuse, a Poisson process with this distribution is
simple (
[
25
, Proposition 6.9]
), so that the above equals

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}

\displaystyle\iint f(\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}}){\mathbf{1}}\{x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}\}\prod^{n}_{i=1}\alpha(x_{i})^{-1}p(x_{i},\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}})

\displaystyle\times\,\prod_{x\in\mu-(\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}})}\big(1-\alpha(x_{i})^{-1}p(x,\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}})\big)\,\mu^{(n)}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))\,\Pi_{\alpha\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu),

where, for
n=0
, we interpret
\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}}
as the zero measure.
By the multivariate Mecke equation (
[
25
, Theorem 4.4]
), this equals

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}

\displaystyle\int f(\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}})\prod^{n}_{i=1}p(x_{i},\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}})P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\,\lambda_{n}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})),

where
\lambda_{n}
is the restriction of
\lambda^{n}
to
\{x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}\}
and

\displaystyle P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):=\int\prod_{x\in\mu}(1-\alpha(x)^{-1}p(x,\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}}))\,\Pi_{\alpha\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu).

By
[
25
, Exercise 3.6]
we have that

\displaystyle P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\exp\Big[-\int p(x,\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)\Big],

so that

\displaystyle\int f(\psi)

\displaystyle\,\Pi_{\kappa,\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\psi)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\int f(\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}})\prod^{n}_{i=1}p(x_{i},\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}})

\displaystyle\times\exp\Big[-\int p(x,\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)\Big]\,\lambda_{n}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})).

By assumption (Dom2) and Remark
3.1
all elements
of
{\mathbf{N}}^{*}
are stable.
Inserting above the result of Corollary
4.5
and
the definition (
4.1
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\int f(\psi)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\kappa,\alpha}(\mathrm{d}\psi)

\displaystyle=Z_{{\mathbb{X}}}(0)^{-1}e^{-\lambda({\mathbb{X}})}\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\int f(\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}})\kappa_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},0)

\displaystyle\quad\times{\mathbf{1}}\{x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}\}\,\lambda^{n}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})),

where
\kappa_{0}\equiv 1
. Since
\lambda
is diffuse
and
\kappa_{n}(\cdot,0)
is symmetric
\lambda^{n}
-a.e., we obtain that

\displaystyle\int f(\psi)\,\mathbb{Q}_{\kappa,\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\psi)=Z_{{\mathbb{X}}}(0)^{-1}\int f(\mu)e^{-H(\mu,0)}\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu).

Therefore the assertion follows from Corollary
2.2
.∎

The following lemma provides useful information on the function
p
.
It does not require assumption (Dom2).
Note that
\psi_{-\infty}=0
and
Z_{\emptyset}(\psi)=1

for each
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}^{*}
. We set
(\infty,\infty):=\emptyset
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}^{*}
is stable
and let
z,w\in{\mathbb{X}}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
such that
z<w
. Then

\displaystyle\int{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in(z,w]\}p(x,\psi)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\lambda(z,w]+\log Z_{(z,\infty)}(\psi_{(-\infty,z]})-\log Z_{(w,\infty)}(\psi_{(-\infty,w)})

(4.5)

\displaystyle\quad+\int\big(\log Z_{(x,\infty)}(\psi_{x}+\delta_{x})-\log Z_{(x,\infty)}(\psi_{x})\big)\,\psi_{(z,w)}(\mathrm{d}x).

Proof:
For
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}^{*}
we abbreviate

Z_{x}(\psi):=Z_{(x,\infty)}(\psi)
.
Since
\lambda
is diffuse, we have
\Pi_{\lambda}(\{\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}:\mu(\{x\})\geq 1\})=0
.
Therefore we obtain from definition (
2.4
)
that
Z_{x}(\psi)=Z_{[x,\infty)}(\psi)
. Since
\psi
is stable, this is a
finite number. In the following we write

\lambda(x,\infty):=\lambda((x,\infty))
and similar for other intervals.
By definition,

\displaystyle Z_{x}(\psi_{x})

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda(x,\infty)}+\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\int e^{-H(\mu_{(x,\infty)},\psi_{x})}{\mathbf{1}}\{\mu(x,\infty)=n\}\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda(x,\infty)}+\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\iint e^{-H(\mu_{[y,\infty)},\psi_{x})}{\mathbf{1}}\{y\in(x,\infty)\}

\displaystyle\quad\times{\mathbf{1}}\{\mu(x,y)=0,\mu(y,\infty)=n-1\}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}y)\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu),

where we have used that
\Pi_{\lambda}
is the distribution of a simple
point process. By the Mecke equation this equals

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda(x,\infty)}+\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}

\displaystyle\iint e^{-H(\mu_{(y,\infty)}+\delta_{y},\psi_{x})}{\mathbf{1}}\{y\in(x,\infty)\}

\displaystyle\quad\times{\mathbf{1}}\{\mu(x,y)=0,\mu(y,\infty)=n-1\}\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y),

where we have used that a Poisson process with a diffuse
intensity measure does not have fixed atoms.
Using the complete independence of a Poisson process and then
the formula for empty space probablities, we obtain that

\displaystyle Z_{x}(\psi_{x})=e^{-\lambda(x,\infty)}+\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{y\in(x,\infty)\}e^{-\lambda(x,y)}e^{-H(\mu_{(y,\infty)}+\delta_{y},\psi_{x})}\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y).

By the definition of the Hamiltonian we have that

\displaystyle e^{-H(\mu_{(y,\infty)}+\delta_{y},\psi_{x})}=\kappa(y,\psi_{x})e^{-H(\mu_{(y,\infty)},\psi_{x}+\delta_{y})},

so that

\displaystyle Z_{x}(\psi_{x})=e^{-\lambda(x,\infty)}+e^{\lambda(x)}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{y\in(x,\infty)\}e^{-\lambda(y)}\kappa(y,\psi_{x})Z_{y}(\psi_{x}+\delta_{y})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y),

(4.6)

where
\lambda(y):=\lambda(-\infty,y]
.

We now argue that we can assume that
{\mathbb{X}}={\mathbb{R}}
.
Consider the Borel isomorphism

\varphi\colon{\mathbb{X}}\to U:=\varphi({\mathbb{X}})
, interpreted as a mapping
from
{\mathbb{X}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
.
We define a diffuse measure
\lambda^{\prime}
on
{\mathbb{R}}

by
\lambda^{\prime}:=\lambda\circ\varphi^{-1}
(the image of
\lambda
under

\varphi
). Recall that
{\mathbf{N}}^{*}({\mathbb{R}})
denotes the set of all finite and simple counting
measures on
{\mathbb{R}}
and define
a measurable mapping
\kappa^{\prime}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbf{N}}^{*}({\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

by
\kappa^{\prime}(u,\mu):={\mathbf{1}}\{u\in U\}\kappa(\varphi^{-1}(u),\mu_{U}\circ\varphi)
.
(If
\mu_{U}=\delta_{s_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{s_{n}}
then

\mu_{U}\circ\varphi=\delta_{\varphi^{-1}(s_{1})}+\cdots+\delta_{\varphi^{-1}(s_{n})})
.)
Then
\kappa^{\prime}
satisfies the cocycle assumption (
2.9
)
and we denote the associated partition functions by

Z^{\prime}_{B}
for
B\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}})
(the system of Borel subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}
).
It is easy to show that

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}_{B}(\mu)=Z_{\varphi^{-1}(B)}(\mu_{U}\circ\varphi),\quad\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}^{*}({\mathbb{R}}),\,B\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}).

(4.7)

Let
\mu:=\psi\circ\varphi^{-1}
and suppose that (
4.5
)
holds with
({\mathbb{X}},\lambda,\kappa,\psi)
replaced with

({\mathbb{R}},\lambda^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime},\mu)
. This means
that for all
s,t\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}

\displaystyle\int{\mathbf{1}}\{u\in(s,t]\}p^{\prime}(u,\mu)\,\lambda^{\prime}(\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\prime}(s,t]+\log Z^{\prime}_{(s,\infty)}(\mu_{(-\infty,s]})-\log Z^{\prime}_{(t,\infty)}(\mu_{(-\infty,s]})

\displaystyle\quad+\int\big(\log Z^{\prime}_{(u,\infty)}(\mu_{u}+\delta_{u})-\log Z^{\prime}_{(u,\infty)}(\mu_{u})\big)\,\mu_{(s,t)}(\mathrm{d}u),

where
p^{\prime}
is defined in terms of
\kappa^{\prime}
as
p
in terms of
\kappa
.
Applying this formula with
(s,t):=(\varphi(z),\varphi(w))

(where
(\varphi(-\infty),\varphi(\infty)):=(-\infty,\infty)
) and using
(
4.7
), yields (
4.5
).

From now on we will assume that
{\mathbb{X}}={\mathbb{R}}
.

a\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
we set
\psi^{+}_{a}:=\psi_{[a,\infty)}
.
Let
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
such that

\psi(a,b)=0
. Then

\displaystyle\psi_{x}=\psi^{+}_{a},\quad x\in(a,b].

Since the left-hand side of (

) is finite,
so is the right-hand side.
This shows that the function
x\mapsto f(x):=Z_{x}(\psi_{x})

from
(a,b]
to
{\mathbb{R}}
is continuous and of totally bounded
variation. Now we use Lebesgue-Stieltjes calculus;
see e.g.
[
23
, Appendix A4]
.
By the product rule and
d_{x}e^{-\lambda(x,\infty)}=-e^{-\lambda(x,\infty)}\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

we have that

\displaystyle df(x)

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda(x,\infty)}\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)+e^{\lambda(x)}\bigg[\int^{\infty}_{x}e^{-\lambda(y)}\kappa(y,\psi^{+}_{a})Z_{y}(\psi^{+}_{a}+\delta_{y})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)\bigg]\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle\quad-\kappa(x,\psi_{a})Z_{x}(\psi^{+}_{a}+\delta_{x})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x),

where we note that
\kappa(x,\psi_{a})Z_{a}(\psi^{+}_{a}+\delta_{x})<\infty

for

-a.e.
x\in[a,b]
. Therefore,

\displaystyle df(x)

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda(x,\infty)}\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)+\big(f(x)-e^{-\lambda(x,\infty)}\big)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)-\kappa(x,\psi_{x})Z_{x}(\psi^{+}_{a}+\delta_{x})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=f(x)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)-\kappa(x,\psi_{a})Z_{x}(\psi^{+}_{a}+\delta_{x})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x),

that is

\displaystyle dZ_{x}(\psi_{x})=Z_{x}(\psi_{x})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)-\kappa(x,\psi_{x})Z_{x}(\psi_{x}+\delta_{x})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x),\quad\text{on $(a,b]$}.

(4.8)

Therefore we obtain from the definition (
4.1
) that

\displaystyle\int^{b}_{a}p(x,\psi)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\lambda(a,b]-\int^{b}_{a}Z_{x}(\psi_{x})^{-1}\,dZ_{x}(\psi_{x})

\displaystyle=\lambda(a,b]+\log Z_{a}(\psi^{+}_{a})-\log Z_{b}(\psi^{+}_{a}),

(4.9)

where we have used
[
23
, Corollary A4.11]
.
Note that

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to-\infty}Z_{z}(\psi^{+}_{z})=Z_{\mathbb{X}}(0),\qquad\lim_{z\to\infty}Z_{z}(\psi^{+}_{z})=1.

There exist
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in{\mathbb{X}}

such that
x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}
and
\psi=\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}}
.
If

, then the assertion (

) follows from (

).
Hence we can assume that

.
By (

) we have for each

,

\displaystyle\int^{x_{i+1}}_{x_{i}}p(x,\psi)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)=\lambda(x_{i},x_{i+1}]+\log Z_{x_{i}}(\psi_{(-\infty,x_{i}]})-\log Z_{x_{i+1}}(\psi_{(-\infty,x_{i}]}),

(4.10)

where

and
x_{n+1}:=\infty
.

Let
z,w\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
z<w
. Then there exist
j,k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}

with
j\leq k
such that
z\in[x_{j},x_{j+1})
and
w\in[x_{k},x_{k+1})
.
For
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
we set
\psi^{i}:=\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{i}}=\psi_{(-\infty,x_{i}]}
.
Let us first assume that
x_{k}<w
.
From (
4
) and (
4.10
) we then derive that

\displaystyle\int^{w}_{z}

\displaystyle p(x,\psi)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)=\int^{x_{j+1}}_{z}p(x,\psi)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)+\sum^{k-1}_{i=j+1}\int^{x_{i+1}}_{x_{i}}p(x,\psi)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)+\int^{w}_{x_{k}}p(x,\psi)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\lambda(z,w]+\log Z_{z}(\psi^{j})-\log Z_{x_{j+1}}(\psi^{j})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum^{k-1}_{i=j+1}\log Z_{x_{i}}(\psi^{i})-\sum^{k-1}_{i=j+1}\log Z_{x_{i+1}}(\psi^{i})+\log Z_{x_{k}}(\psi^{k})-\log Z_{w}(\psi^{k}).

Reordering terms yields,

\displaystyle\int^{w}_{z}

\displaystyle p(x,\psi)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\lambda(z,w]+\log Z_{z}(\psi^{+}_{z})-\log Z_{w}(\psi_{w})+\sum^{k}_{i=j+1}\log Z_{x_{i}}(\psi^{i})-\sum^{k-1}_{i=j}\log Z_{x_{i+1}}(\psi^{i})

\displaystyle=\lambda(z,w]+\log Z_{z}(\psi^{+}_{z})-\log Z_{w}(\psi_{w})+\sum^{k}_{i=j+1}\big(\log Z_{x_{i}}(\psi^{i})-\log Z_{x_{i}}(\psi^{i-1})\big).

This is equivalent to (
4.5
). If
w=x_{k}
we have that

\displaystyle\int^{w}_{z}

\displaystyle p(x,\psi)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)=\lambda(z,w]+\log Z_{z}(\psi^{j})+\sum^{k-1}_{i=j+1}\log Z_{x_{i}}(\psi^{i})-\sum^{k-1}_{i=j}\log Z_{x_{i+1}}(\psi^{i})

\displaystyle=\lambda(z,w]+\log Z_{z}(\psi^{j})-\log Z_{x_{k}}(\psi^{k-1})+\sum^{k-1}_{i=j+1}\big(\log Z_{x_{i}}(\psi^{i})-\log Z_{x_{i+1}}(\psi^{i})\big),

which is again equivalent to (
4.5
).
∎

The following corollary has been crucial in the proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Suppose that
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}^{*}
is stable. Then

\displaystyle\exp\bigg[-\int p(x,\psi)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg]=e^{-\lambda({\mathbb{X}})}Z_{{\mathbb{X}}}(0)^{-1}\prod_{y\in\psi}\frac{Z_{(y,\infty)}(\psi_{y})}{Z_{(y,\infty)}(\psi_{y}+\delta_{y})}.

(4.11)

In particular
\int p(x,\psi)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty
.

Proof:
Taking in Lemma
4.4

z=-\infty
and
w=\infty

yields the first assertion.
To prove the second, we take
y\in\psi
. Then

Z_{(y,\infty)}(\psi_{y}+\delta_{y})=Z_{(y,\infty)}(\psi_{(-\infty,y]})
.
Since
\psi
is stable, this is finite.
Therefore the right-hand side of (
4.11
) does not vanish and the result
follows. ∎

5

As in Section

(

In this section we construct a (finite) Gibbs process by a recursively
defined embedding
into a Poisson process on

with intensity

, where

denotes

. To the best of our knowledge it has

As in Theorem

Let

Using the total ordering on

we define for each

a mapping

as follows. Let

. If
\int p(x,0)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)=\infty
, then we set

If

\displaystyle\psi(\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}:t\leq p(x,0)\})<\infty

\displaystyle x_{1}(\psi):=\min\{x\in{\mathbb{X}}:\text{there exists $t\geq 0$ such that $(x,t)\in\psi$ and $t\leq p(x,0)$}\},

(5.1)

where

.

for all

If

and

\displaystyle\int{\mathbf{1}}\{x_{n}(\psi)<x\}p(x,\delta_{x_{1}(\psi)}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}(\psi)})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)=\infty,

\displaystyle x_{n+1}(\psi)

(5.2)

\displaystyle:=\min\{x>x_{n}(\psi):\text{there exists $t\geq 0$ s.t.\ $(x,t)\in\psi$ and
$t\leq p(x,\delta_{x_{1}(\psi)}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}(\psi)})$}\}.

Define the

\displaystyle T(\psi):={\mathbf{1}}\{\tau(\psi)<\infty\}\sum^{\tau(\psi)}_{n=1}\delta_{x_{n}(\psi)},

Theorem 5.1

Assume that
\lambda
is finite
and diffuse and that
\Pi_{\lambda}
-a.e.
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}^{*}
are stable.
Assume that
\Phi
is a Poisson process on
{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with

. Then

is a Gibbs process
with PI

.

Proof:
Given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in{\mathbb{X}}
and

we define

\displaystyle A_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):=\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}:x_{i}<x<x_{i+1},t\leq p(x,\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{i}})\},

where we set
x_{0}:=-\infty
and
x_{n+1}:=\infty

that
-\infty<y<\infty
for each

.
For

we set

.
Let

\displaystyle C_{n}:=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n}:\delta_{x_{1}}\in H,\delta_{x_{1}}+\delta_{x_{2}}\in H,\ldots,\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}}\in H\},

where

By definition of

\displaystyle T(\psi)(B)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{t_{1}\leq p(x_{1},0),\ldots,t_{n}\leq p(x_{n},\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n-1}})\}{\mathbf{1}}\{x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}\}

(5.4)

\displaystyle\times{\mathbf{1}}\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})\in C_{n-1},\psi(B_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))=0\}{\mathbf{1}}\{x_{i}\in B\}\,\psi^{(n)}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},t_{1},\ldots,x_{n},t_{n})).

We next prove that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x_{n}(\Phi)=-\infty)=0,\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

First we note that

For

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(

\displaystyle x_{n+1}(\Phi)=-\infty)\leq\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(T(\Phi)({\mathbb{X}})=k,x_{k+1}(\Phi)=-\infty).

. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(

\displaystyle x_{n+1}(\Phi)=-\infty)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int{\mathbf{1}}\{t_{1}\leq p(x_{1},0),\ldots,t_{n}\leq p(x_{n},\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n-1}})\}{\mathbf{1}}\{x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\mathbf{1}}\{\Phi(B_{n-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))=0,(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\notin C_{n}\}\,\Phi^{(n)}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},t_{1},\ldots,x_{n},t_{n}))\bigg].

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[

\displaystyle\int p(x_{1},0)\cdots p(x_{n},\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n-1}}){\mathbf{1}}\{x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\mathbf{1}}\{\Phi(B_{n-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}))=0,(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\notin C_{n}\}\,\lambda^{n}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int p(x_{1},0)\cdots p(x_{n},\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n-1}}){\mathbf{1}}\{x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}\}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathbb{P}(\Phi(B_{n-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}))=0){\mathbf{1}}\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\notin C_{n}\}\,\lambda^{n}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})).

Our assumptions allow to apply Corollary

Now we take a measurable

\displaystyle f(\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}})p(x_{1},0)\cdots p(x_{n},\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n-1}}){\mathbf{1}}\{x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}\}

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{P}(\Phi(B_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))=0)\,\lambda^{n}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})),

Whenever

\displaystyle=\prod^{n}_{i=0}\mathbb{P}(\Phi(A_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))=0)

\displaystyle=\prod^{n}_{i=0}\exp\bigg[-\int{\mathbf{1}}\{x_{i}<x<x_{i+1}\}p(x,\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{i}})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg].

The following lemma has been used in the preceding proof.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
W\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
and suppose that

\xi
is a Gibbs process on
W
. Let
S
be a graph-measurable
mapping from
\Omega
into
{\mathcal{X}}\cap W
.
Then

\int\mathbb{P}(\xi_{S}+\mu_{W\setminus S}\in\cdot)\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)\ll\Pi_{\lambda_{W}}
.

Proof.

Let
f\colon{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be measurable such that

\int f(\mu)\,\Pi_{\lambda_{W}}(\mathrm{d}\mu)=0
.
Since a Poisson process is completely independent
and
\lambda(S)\leq\lambda(W)<\infty
we have

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int f(\xi_{S}+\mu_{W\setminus S})\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[e^{\lambda(S)}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{\mu(S)=0\}f(\xi_{S}+\mu_{W\setminus S})\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\lambda(W)}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int f(\xi_{S}+\mu_{W})\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)\bigg].

By
[
19
, Theorem 1.1]

(applying to general Borel spaces),

\mathbb{P}(\xi_{S}\in\cdot)\ll\mathbb{P}(\xi\in\cdot)
. Hence
we also have
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{S}\in\cdot)\ll\Pi_{\lambda_{W}}

and we let
g\colon{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
denote the corresponding density.
Then

\displaystyle I\leq e^{\lambda(W)}\iint g(\psi_{W})f(\psi_{W}+\mu_{W})\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\psi).

Noting that

\displaystyle\iint f(\psi_{W}+\mu_{W})\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\psi)=\int f(\mu_{W})\,\Pi_{2\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\mu)

and that
\Pi_{2\lambda_{W}}
and
\Pi_{\lambda_{W}}
are equivalent,
we obtain that
I=0
. This concludes the proof.
∎

7
Bounds for empty space probabilities

In this section we apply the previous results to obtain upper bounds
for empty space probabilities of a Gibbs process.
These probabilities are important chracteristics of
a point process. In fact, they determine the distribution of a simple
point process (see e.g.
[
25
]
) and have
many applications, for instance in stochastic geometry (see e.g.
[
7
]
)
or in quantifying the clustering of a point
process (see
[
4
]
).
We shall need an upper bound in the proof of Theorem
10.1
.

A naive approach to bound the empty space
probability for a given boundary condition from above would be to
bound the partition function from below and to exploit the relation
(
2.8
). This is often not very promising since the partition
function is hardly accessible. Our approach is different. We start
with the embedding representation of a (finite) Gibbs process from
Section 5 that involves the function
p
from (
4.1
). This
enables us to use bounds for fractions of partition functions (which
are often considerably easier to find than bounds for the partition
function itself) to obtain bounds for empty space probabilities.

Let
({\mathbb{X}},{\mathcal{X}})
be a Borel space equipped with a
\sigma
-finite and
diffuse measure
\lambda
.
We consider a Gibbs process
\xi
on

{\mathbb{X}}
with PI
\kappa
satisfying the cocycle condition (
2.9
)
and assume that (Dom2) and (Loc1) hold.

Let
\eta
be a Poisson process
on
{\mathbb{X}}
with intensity measure
\alpha\lambda

and let
B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
.
By
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
(and
[
2
, Lemma 2.5]
)
the process
\xi_{B}
,
conditioned on
\xi_{B^{c}}
, is stochastically dominated by

\eta
. In particular

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi(B)=0\mid\xi_{B^{c}})\geq e^{-(\alpha\lambda)(B)},\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

(7.1)

The next result provides an inequality in the converse direction.
For
B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
we set

\displaystyle B_{\xi}:=\{x\in B:x\not\sim\xi_{B^{c}}\}=B\setminus\bigcup_{y\in\xi_{B^{c}}}N_{y}.

Theorem 7.1
.

Suppose that
\xi
is a Gibbs process whose
PI
\kappa
satisfies (Dom2) and (Loc1).
Let
B\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi(B)=0\mid\xi_{B^{c}})\leq\exp\bigg[-\int{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B_{\xi}\}e^{-(\alpha\lambda)(N_{x}\cap B)}\kappa(x,0)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg],\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

(7.2)

Proof:
We have mentioned at the end of Section
2

that the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{B}\in\cdot\mid\xi_{B^{c}})

is that of a Gibbs process with PI
\kappa_{B,\xi_{B^{c}}}
.
By assumption (Dom2) and Remark
3.1

we can apply Theorem
5.1
(with
\kappa=\kappa_{B,\xi_{B^{c}}}

and
{\mathbb{X}}=B
) to construct this Gibbs distribution.
By definition of the embedding operator and the properties
of the underlying Poisson process we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi(B)=0\mid\xi_{B^{c}})=\exp\bigg[-\int{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B\}p_{B,\xi_{B^{c}}}(x,0)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg],\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

(7.3)

Recall from (
4.1
) that

\displaystyle p_{B,\xi_{B^{c}}}(x,0)=\kappa(x,\xi_{B^{c}})\frac{Z_{B_{x}}(\xi_{B^{c}}+\delta_{x})}{Z_{B_{x}}(\xi_{B^{c}})},\quad x\in B,

where
B_{x}=B\cap(x,\infty)
and the intervals are defined with respect to the order
(induced by the Borel isomorphism) on
{\mathbb{X}}
.
Assume that
x\in B_{\xi}
.
If
\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}_{B}
satisfies
\mu(N_{x})=0
, then assumption (Loc1)
shows that

\displaystyle H(\mu,\xi_{B^{c}}+\delta_{x})=H(\mu,\xi_{B^{c}}).

(7.4)

Therefore

\displaystyle Z_{B_{x}}(\xi_{B^{c}}+\delta_{x})

\displaystyle\geq\int{\mathbf{1}}\{\mu(N_{x})=0\}e^{-H(\mu,\xi_{B^{c}})}\,\Pi_{\lambda_{B_{x}}}(\mathrm{d}\mu),

Since a Poisson process is completely independent
we obtain that

\displaystyle Z_{B_{x}}(\xi_{B^{c}}+\delta_{x})

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\lambda(N_{x}\cap B_{x})}\int e^{-H(\mu_{N_{x}^{c}},\xi_{B^{c}})}\,\Pi_{\lambda_{B_{x}}}(\mathrm{d}\mu)

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda(N_{x}\cap B_{x})}Z_{B_{x}\setminus N_{x}}(\xi_{B^{c}}).

Using the definition of the Hamiltonian we further obtain that

\displaystyle Z_{B_{x}}(\xi_{B^{c}})

\displaystyle=\int e^{-H(\mu_{N_{x}^{c}},\xi_{B^{c}})}e^{-H(\mu_{N_{x}},\xi_{B^{c}}+\mu_{N_{x}^{c}})}\,\Pi_{\lambda_{B_{x}}}(\mathrm{d}\mu)

\displaystyle\leq\int e^{-H(\mu_{N_{x}^{c}},\xi_{B^{c}})}\prod_{y\in\mu_{N_{x}}}\alpha(y)\,\Pi_{\lambda_{B_{x}}}(\mathrm{d}\mu),

where we have used our assumption (Dom2).
By the independence properties of a Poisson process this implies

\displaystyle Z_{B_{x}}(\xi_{B^{c}})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigg[\int{\mathbf{1}}\{y\in N_{x}\cap B_{x}\}(\alpha(y)-1)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)\bigg]Z_{B_{x}\setminus N_{x}}(\xi_{B^{c}}).

Therefore we obtain for
x\in B_{\xi}
that

\displaystyle p_{B,\xi_{B^{c}}}(x,0)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\bigg[-\lambda(N_{x}\cap B_{x})+\int{\mathbf{1}}\{y\in N_{x}\cap B_{x}\}(1-\alpha(y))\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)\bigg]\kappa(x,0)

\displaystyle\geq e^{-(\alpha\lambda)(N_{x}\cap B)}\kappa(x,0).

Inserting this into (
7.3
) gives the result. ∎

To the best of our knowledge, Poisson approximation of (derived) Gibbs

Let
\mu

Recall that the

Proof of Theorem

Lemma 9.4

\displaystyle{\mathbf{1}}\{B(o,u){\mathrel{\to}^{\eta}}B(o,u+v)^{c})\}

W_{n}:=[0,n^{1/d}]^{d}
,

u_{n}:=\sup_{x\in[0,1]^{d}}u_{n}(n^{1/d}x)
and

v_{n}:=2c_{2}^{-1}\log n
, where
c_{2}
is the constant from Corollary

9.7
. We let
g
and
f
(in Remark
9.3
) depend on
n
as well. For

(x,r,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{Y}\times\mathbf{N}
let

\displaystyle g_{n}(x,r,\mu):=g_{n}(x,\mu):={\mathbf{1}}\{\mu(B(x,u_{n}(x))\times{\mathbb{Y}})=0\}

(10.6)

and
f_{n}(x,r):=(n^{-1/d}x,r)
. Obviously,

satisfies the

8.2
) as well as (

). As Remark

9.6
shows, (
9.13
) holds.

For all
n
with

) that for all

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\chi_{n}](B)

\displaystyle=\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{(n^{-1/d}x,r)\in B\cap([0,1]^{d}\times\mathbb{Y})\}\mathbb{E}[g_{n}(x,r,\xi)]\kappa(x,r,0)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathbb{Q}(\mathrm{d}r)

\displaystyle=n\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{(x,r)\in B\cap([0,1]^{d}\times\mathbb{Y})\}\mathbb{E}[g_{n}(n^{1/d}x,r,\xi)]\kappa(o,r,0)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathbb{Q}(\mathrm{d}r).

Hence, for those
n
it holds that

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}[\chi_{n}]-\mathbb{E}[\nu]\|\leq\sup_{x\in[0,1]^{d}}|c-n\mathbb{P}(\xi(B(n^{1/d}x,u_{n}(n^{1/d}x))\times{\mathbb{Y}})=0)|\int\kappa(o,r,0)\,\mathbb{Q}(\mathrm{d}r),

which vanishes by (
10.2
).

Since we assumed that (Dom2) holds for some constant

, we find from (

)
that for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g_{n}(x,r,\xi)\kappa(x,r,\xi)]\leq 2\alpha cn^{-1}.

(10.7)

Hence, the term

in Corollary

is for those
n
bounded by

\displaystyle 8(\alpha c)^{2}n^{-2}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{\|x-y\|\leq 2u_{n}+4c_{2}^{-1}\log n\}{\mathbf{1}}\{x,y\in[0,n^{1/d}]^{d}\}\,\mathrm{d}(x,y)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{8(\alpha c)^{2}\kappa_{d}(2u_{n}+4c_{2}^{-1}\log n)^{d}}{n}.

(10.8)

For

\displaystyle 2\alpha^{2}\iint\mathbb{P}((\xi+\delta_{(y,s)})(B(x,u_{n}(x))\times\mathbb{Y})=0,\,(\xi+\delta_{(x,r)})(B(y,u_{n}(y))\times\mathbb{Y})=0)

Note that the probability in the integrand of

is zero if

. For

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi(B(x,u_{n}(x))\cup B(y,u_{n}(y)))=0).

Since the ball

, (

) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\xi(B(x,u_{n}(x)))=0,\xi\Big(B\Big(y+\frac{u_{n}(y)}{2}\frac{y-x}{\|y-x\|},\frac{u_{n}(y)}{2}\Big)\Big)=0\Big)

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\Big[g_{n}(x,r,\xi)\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\xi\Big(B\Big(y+\frac{u_{n}(y)}{2}\frac{y-x}{\|y-x\|},\frac{u_{n}(y)}{2}\Big)\Big)=0\,\Bigl|\,\xi_{B(y+\frac{u_{n}(y)}{2}\frac{y-x}{\|y-x\|},\frac{u_{n}(y)}{2})^{c}}\Big)\Big].

from

). Thus we conclude that

\displaystyle F_{2}(u_{n},v_{n})\leq 2\alpha^{2}c\kappa_{d}(2u_{n}+4c_{2}^{-1}\log n)^{d}\sup_{y\in[0,1]^{d}}e^{-c_{0}(u_{n}(n^{1/d}y)/2-2R)^{d}}.

Recalling the definition of the constant

for some constant

Now the assertion follows from (
