Computing p p -adic heights on hyperelliptic curves
Stevan Gajović, J. Steffen Müller

Abstract
. We describe an algorithm to compute the local Coleman–Gross p p -adic height at p p on a hyperelliptic curve. Previously, this was only possible using an algorithm due to Balakrishnan and Besser, which was limited to odd degree. While we follow their general strategy, our algorithm is significantly faster and simpler and works for both odd and even degree. We discuss a precision analysis and an implementation in SageMath. Our work has several applications, also discussed in this article. These include various versions of the quadratic Chabauty method, and numerical evidence for a p p -adic version of the conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton–Dyer in cases where this was not previously possible.



1.
Introduction

Algorithms for the computation of
p
-adic heights on Jacobian varieties
curves have recently played a crucial
part in explicit methods for the computation of integral and
rational points on curves via the quadratic Chabauty
method. Such heights also appear in

p
-adic analogues of the conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton–Dyer.
In this work, we restrict to the case of the Jacobian
J

of a hyperelliptic curve

X\colon y^{2}=f(x)\,,\quad f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]\;\;\text{squarefree}

over
\mathbb{Q}
and a prime
p
of good reduction for
X
.
Denote by

h\colon J(\mathbb{Q})\times J(\mathbb{Q})\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
the
p
-adic height pairing constructed
by Coleman and Gross
[
CG89
]
(depending on various
choices).
Then
h
is bilinear, and is a sum of local height pairings
h_{v}(\cdot,\cdot)

for each finite prime
v\in\mathbb{Z}
. These are defined on pairs of divisors on

X\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{v}
of degree 0 with disjoint support, and the construction
depends on whether
v\neq p
or
v=p
.
The former are defined in terms of intersection theory on arithmetic
surfaces and can be computed using algorithms discussed in
[
Hol12
,
Mül14
,
vBHM20
]
.
Our work focuses on the latter; these are given
in terms of
p
-adic analysis.
More precisely, one has
h_{p}(D_{1},D_{2})=\int_{D_{2}}\omega_{D_{1}}
, where

\omega_{D_{1}}
is a certain differential of the third kind on
X\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}
whose residue divisor is
D_{1}
, and the integral is a Coleman integral

[
Col85
]
,
[
CdS88
]
.

When
X
has an odd degree model over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, one can compute
h_{p}

using work of
Balakrishnan and Besser
[
BB12
]
.
Based on their strategy, we describe in Sections
4
,
5
and
6

an algorithm to compute
h_{p}
that does not
require
X
to have an odd degree model over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and
is much simpler and faster than the one from
[
BB12
]
(see the
timings in §
8.1
).
We use it

•

to obtain a vast speed-up of the quadratic Chabauty method for
rational points in some situations (see §
1.1
);

•

to develop a new
quadratic Chabauty method for integral points on even degree
hyperelliptic curves over number fields that is simpler than previous
instances of this method
(see §
1.2
and
[
GM23
]
) and crucially relies
on our new algorithm;

•

to give numerical evidence for the

p
-adic Birch and Swinnerton–Dyer conjecture from
[
BMS16
]

in cases where this was not previously possible
(see §
1.3
and Section
9
).

We now sketch our method.
Assume that
X
is given by an even degree model (we will see in Section
4
that this is sufficient), and, for simplicity, that
f
is monic. Denote by
\infty_{-}
and

\infty_{+}
the two points at infinity, namely

\infty_{\pm}=(1:\pm 1:0)
on the model of
X
given by the closure of
y^{2}=f(x)
in the weighted
projective plane
\mathbb{P}_{1,g+1,1}
.
Since the local heights are bi-additive, it suffices to compute
h_{p}(P-Q,R-S)

for distinct points
P,Q,R,S
.
As in
[
BB12
]
we assume, for simplicity and for implementation
reasons (see Remark
8.1
)
that these points are all defined over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.

Our method crucially depends on the existence of a nontrivial
degree-0 divisor supported at infinity, namely
D_{\infty}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\infty_{-}-\infty_{+}
.
We show in Sections
5
and
6
that the computation of
h_{p}(P-Q,R-S)
can be reduced essentially to the
computation of Coleman integrals of the differentials

\omega_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{x^{i}dx}{y},\quad i=0,\ldots,2g\,.

For
P-Q=D_{\infty}
, this reduction is straightforward, since the residue
divisor of
\omega_{g}
is
D_{\infty}
(see
Section
5
).
Using a change of variables and further reductions to divisors of a specific shape,
we also reduce the computation of the relevant integrals
\int\omega_{P-Q}
for most other divisors
P-Q
to the computation of
\int\omega_{g}
(see Section
6
).
Coleman integrals of the differentials
\omega_{i}

can be computed easily using Balakrishnan’s algorithm

[
Bal15
]
, which is implemented in
SageMath

[
The23
]
.

No nontrivial degree 0 divisor supported at
\infty
exists when
f
has odd degree.
Instead, the authors of
[
BB12
]
compute Coleman
integrals of differentials of the third kind using a clever but
more complicated
approach that involves high precision computations in
local coordinates of points over high degree extensions of

\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.
Our algorithm in fact extends to odd degree, by transforming to an even degree model.
We give some timings in §
8
; these show that our algorithm
is indeed faster than the one from
[
BB12
]
.

A
SageMath
-implementation of our algorithm is discussed
in
section

8
and can be obtained
from
https://github.com/StevanGajovic/heights_above_p
.
We give a precision analysis in Section
7
.
For our algorithm, we assume that the points
P,Q,R,S
satisfy a mild condition
(see Condition 1 in Definition
4.1
, and see §
8.2.3
on how this
requirement may be weakened).

1.1.
Application I: Quadratic Chabauty for rational points

The quadratic Chabauty method for rational points was introduced by
Balakrishnan and Dogra in
[
BD18
]
. In its simplest form,
it can be used to
compute the rational points on certain curves
X/\mathbb{Q}
of genus
g>1

having Mordell–Weil rank

r\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\operatorname{rk}J(\mathbb{Q})=g
and Picard number
\operatorname{rk}\mathrm{NS}(J)>1
,
where
J
is the Jacobian variety of
X
.
The idea is to fix a prime
p
of good reduction such that
J(\mathbb{Q})
has finite index in
J(\mathbb{Q}_{p})

and to use quadratic relations in the image
of the abelian logarithm
\log\colon J(\mathbb{Q})\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}\to\mathrm{H}^{0}(X_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{\vee}
coming from
p
-adic heights to write down a locally analytic function
\rho\colon X(\mathbb{Q}_{p})\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
which has only
finitely many zeroes and vanishes along
X(\mathbb{Q})
. This is similar to the
method of Chabauty–Coleman, which uses linear relations and requires

r<g
.
The approach of Balakrishnan and Dogra fits into Kim’s non-abelian
Chabauty framework
[
Kim05
]
and
[
Kim09
]
, which
aims to generalize Chabauty–Coleman using
p
-adic Hodge theory
and arithmetic fundamental groups.
Their results have been
generalized
[
BD20
]
, reinterpreted
[
EL23
,
BMS21
]
and
applied to several explicit examples (see for instance
[
BDM+19
,
BBB+21
,
AAB+23
]
).

To apply the quadratic Chabauty method in practice, one needs to
solve for the
p
-adic height pairing
as a bilinear pairing.
Two methods for this step are discussed in
[
BDM+23
, §3.3, §3.5.4]
: The first one uses Nekovář’s construction and the machinery of
[
BDM+19
]
, but it requires that
X
has “enough” rational points.
The second method uses that
the global
p
-adic height
h
can be written a linear combination of products of
Coleman integrals of holomorphic forms as in
[
BBM16
]
(see
also §
1.2
below). To find the coefficients, we need to evaluate these products and the height pairing
h
in sufficiently many (pairs of) points. For this purpose we can use the techniques of the present article.

For instance, the Atkin–Lehner quotient

X_{0}^{+}(107)\colon y^{2}=x^{6}+2x^{5}+5x^{4}+2x^{3}-2x^{2}-4x-3

has precisely the six rational points
(\pm 1,\pm 1),\infty_{\pm}
.
In
[
BDM+23
]
, this is proved using quadratic Chabauty for the prime

p=61
. Code for this computation can be found
at
https://github.com/steffenmueller/QCMod
.
The fairly large prime
p=61
was chosen for two reasons: First,
it is expected that
\rho
vanishes in further
p
-adic points
in addition to the rational ones. To show that these
are not rational, one can use the Mordell–Weil
sieve
[
BS10
]
, but for this to have a reasonable chance of
success, the prime
p
needs to be chosen carefully.
Second, to apply the algorithm from
[
BB12
]
, a prime
p
such that
X\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}
has
an odd degree model over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is required.
The first condition is also satisfied for
p=7
, but the second one is not. We use the methods of Sections
4
,
5
, and
6
to compute
X_{0}^{+}(107)(\mathbb{Q})
via a combination of the quadratic Chabauty method and the Mordell–Weil sieve as in
[
BD18
,
BDM+19
,
BDM+23
]
for
p=7
.
On
a single core of a 4-core 2.6 GHz Intel i7-6600U CPU with 8GB RAM, the
entire computation took 47 seconds, in contrast with about 40
minutes for
p=61
using the
Magma

[
BCP97
]
implementation of
the algorithm from
[
BB12
]
.

Recent work of Duque-Rosero, Hashimoto and Spelier
[
DRHS23
]
makes the
geometric quadratic Chabauty method due to Edixhoven and Lido
[
EL23
]

explicit by reinterpreting it in terms of Coleman–Gross
p
-adic heights. The work described in
the present article can also be used to apply their algorithm in practice.

1.2.
Application II: Quadratic Chabauty for integral
points

Prior to the work of Balakrishnan–Dogra, a quadratic Chabauty method to
compute the integral points on hyperelliptic curves
X
of odd degree had been developed by
Balakrishnan, Besser and the
second author in
[
BBM16
]
.
This method also requires that
r=g
and that the closure of

J(\mathbb{Q})
has finite index in
J(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
, but has no assumption on

\operatorname{rk}\mathrm{NS}(J)
.
The main result of
[
BBM16
]
is the existence of a nontrivial locally
analytic function
\rho\colon\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, where

\mathcal{U}=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}[x,y]/(y^{2}-f(x))
, and a finite set
T\subset\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, both computable, such that
\rho
takes values in
T
on

\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{Z})
.
The construction of
\rho
is fairly technical: it uses Besser’s
p
-adic Arakelov theory, an extension of local Coleman–Gross heights to pairs of
divisors with common support, and double Coleman integrals from tangential base points.
It was further extended in
[
BBBM21
]
to
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-integral points on certain hyperelliptic curves
X
of odd degree over number fields
K
.
The explicit methods based on this approach developed in
[
BBM16
,
BBM17
,
BBBM21
]
require the computation of local and global
p
-adic heights,
and hence our algorithm leads to a considerable speed-up of these methods. However, these methods are all limited to curves with a global odd degree model.

We develop in
[
GM23
]
a quadratic Chabauty
method for integral points on even degree hyperelliptic curves over
\mathbb{Q}

and more general number fields. Using the present article, this can be made explicit,
and we discuss two examples in
[
GM23
]
, including an example over

\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{7})
that is not amenable to any other existing technique (as far
as we can tell). The idea is to
decompose the linear functional
\lambda(P)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}h(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},P)
into local terms and to write it as a sum of
single Coleman integrals.
A noteworthy feature of this new quadratic Chabauty
method is its simplicity; no double integrals,
p
-adic Arakelov theory,
tangential base points or

p
-adic Hodge theory are required.

1.3.
Application III:
p
-adic BSD

Another
application of our algorithm is the numerical verification of Conjecture
9.1
, a
p
-adic Birch and Swinnerton–Dyer
conjecture for modular abelian varieties of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}
-type with good ordinary reduction, in examples.
So far, this has only been done for elliptic curves and for
Jacobians of hyperelliptic curves that have an odd degree model over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}

(see
[
BMS16
]
). Our implementation makes it possible to drop this
condition.
We discuss how to numerically verify Conjecture
9.1
and give
a worked example (for
J_{0}^{+}(67)
and
p=11
) in Section
9
.

2.
Coleman–Gross heights

Following
[
BB12
, Section 2]
, we introduce the
p
-adic height
pairing constructed by Coleman and Gross in
[
CG89
]
. Let
p
be a prime number and let
K
be a number
field. Let
X/K
be a
smooth projective geometrically integral curve of genus
g>0
, with good reduction at all
primes above
p
, and let
J/K
denote its Jacobian.
For a non-archimedean place
v
of
K
we write
K_{v}
for the completion of
K
at

v
,
\mathcal{O}_{v}
for its ring of integers and
\pi_{v}
for a uniformizer, and we denote

X_{v}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}X\otimes K_{v}
.

The
p
-adic height depends on the
following data, which we fix:

(a)

A continuous idèle class character

\ell\colon\mathbb{A}_{K}^{\ast}/K^{\ast}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}

such that the local characters
\ell_{\mathfrak{p}}
induced by
\ell
, for
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
, do not vanish
on
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}_{K_{\mathfrak{p}}}
.

(b)

For each
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
a choice of a subspace
W_{\mathfrak{p}}\subset\operatorname{H^{1}_{dR}}(X_{\mathfrak{p}}/K_{\mathfrak{p}})

complementary to the space of holomorphic forms.

The condition in (a) means that the local character
\ell_{\mathfrak{p}}
is ramified,
which we assume. We require this to define the
local component of the height using Coleman integration, see Definition

2.6

height if the local character
\ell_{\mathfrak{p}}
is unramified, using
intersection theory exactly as for primes
\mathfrak{q}\nmid p

[
BBBM21
, §2.2]
.
We use this greater generality in
[
GM23
]
.

\mathfrak{q}\nmid p
, we have
\ell_{\mathfrak{q}}(\mathcal{O}_{K_{\mathfrak{q}}}^{\ast})=0
for continuity reasons, which implies
that
\ell_{\mathfrak{q}}
is completely determined by
\ell_{\mathfrak{q}}(\pi_{\mathfrak{q}})
. On the other hand, for

\mathfrak{p}\mid p

Coleman–Gross define the height pairing on

3.3.
The cup product matrix

Since all
\eta_{i}
are holomorphic on
U
,
the cup product matrix on
C
, which we denote by

, is given by

m_{ij}=\operatorname{Res}_{\infty_{+}}\left(\eta_{j}\int\eta_{i}\right)+\operatorname{Res}_{\infty_{-}}\left(\eta_{j}\int\eta_{i}\right)=2\operatorname{Res}_{\infty_{+}}\left(\eta_{j}\int\eta_{i}\right)\,.

The final equality holds because if

are local coordinates of one point at infinity, for example induced by

(s_{x},-s_{y})
are local coordinates of the other one, and

is an even function in

4.1.
Reduction using bi-additivity

Any divisors

with disjoint support can be written as

for pairwise distinct points

Using the bi-additivity of the local height pairing, we can reduce the computation of

for distinct points

applied by Balakrishnan and Besser for odd degree.

4.2.
Conditions on points

Let
\iota\colon C\rightarrow C
denote the hyperelliptic involution.

Recall that for

we denote by

the residue disc containing it.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that

satisfy

\{D(P_{1}),D(P_{2}),D(\iota(P_{1})),D(\iota(P_{2}))\}\cap\{D(P_{3}),D(P_{4})\}=\emptyset.

We say that they satisfy
Condition 1’
if

\{\iota(P_{1}),\iota(P_{2})\}\cap\{P_{3},P_{4}\}=\emptyset.

Note that Condition 1 is strictly stronger than Condition 1’.
Our strategy for computing

[
BB12
]

This strategy requires that Condition 1’ is satisfied for the points

In the following, we assume that Condition 1 is satisfied for the points

for practical purposes; concretely, for the computation of Step (

would belong to a residue disc at infinity. Since

[

of even degree Coleman integration in

assumes that this is not the case, we would not be able to compute this integral. Similarly, in Section
6
, we use a change of variables to compute a certain Coleman integral, which would lead to the same issue. We explain in
section

how to circumvent this issue in theory, thus weakening Condition 1 to Condition 1’, but we have not attempted to implement this.

In fact, Balakrishnan’s implementation of the Coleman integral

assumes that

and

So

Definition 4.2
.

We say that two distinct affine points

satisfy

if they are in the same residue disc or if

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(y(P_{1})),\operatorname{ord}_{p}(y(P_{2}))\in\{0,\infty\}.

We emphasize that the only step in computing
h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},R-S)
that depends on the points
R,S\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is the computation of Coleman integrals with endpoints
R
and
S
. Hence, if we want to compute several different local heights of the type
h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},R-S)
(for example, for linear quadratic Chabauty in
[
GM23
]
), it is more efficient to complete all previous steps once and use them as input.

6.
The affine case

Our goal is to compute
h_{p}(P-Q,R-S)
for four distinct affine points
P,Q,R,S\in C(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
.
To this end, we describe Steps (
i
) to
(
iv
) of Algorithm
4.3
in this case.
We assume that
P,Q,R,S
satisfy Assumption
1
.
As explained in §
8
, our implementation in

SageMath
assumes only Condition 1 for
P,Q,R,S
.

We first use the same trick as in
[
BB12
]
; we write a degree zero divisor as a sum of a symmetric and an antisymmetric one.

Lemma 6.1
.

\displaystyle h_{p}(P-Q,R-S)=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\log_{p}\left(\dfrac{x(R)-x(P)}{x(R)-x(Q)}\cdot\dfrac{x(S)-x(Q)}{x(S)-x(R)}\right)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2}h_{p}(P-\iota(P),R-S)-\dfrac{1}{2}h_{p}(Q-\iota(Q),R-S).

Proof.

From

\operatorname{div}\left(\dfrac{x-x(P)}{x-x(Q)}\right)=P+\iota(P)-Q-\iota(Q)\,,

we find

P-Q=\dfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{div}\left(\dfrac{x-x(P)}{x-x(Q)}\right)+\dfrac{1}{2}(P-\iota(P))-\dfrac{1}{2}(Q-\iota(Q))\,.

Recall from Proposition
2.7
that we have

h_{p}\left(\operatorname{div}\left(\dfrac{x-x(P)}{x-x(Q)}\right),R-S\right)=\log_{p}\left(\dfrac{x(R)-x(P)}{x(R)-x(Q)}\cdot\dfrac{x(S)-x(Q)}{x(S)-x(P)}\right)\,.

The claim follows from additivity.
∎

Condition 1 for
P,Q,R,S
implies that the right hand side in
Lemma
6.1
is defined.
However, the much weaker condition
\{x(R),x(S)\}\cap\{x(P),x(Q)\}=\emptyset
actually suffices. This is precisely Condition 1’. We will explain in §
8.2.3
how to
weaken Condition 1 to Condition 1’ in such a way that
Lemma
6.1
continues to hold (though we did not implement
this).
In fact, Lemma
6.1
is the reason why we cannot weaken Condition
1’, even in theory.

From now on, we will restrict attention to
h_{p}(P-\iota(P),R-S)
,
where
P
is a non-Weierstrass point.
In this case,
Step (
i
)
follows from the following result,
which can be viewed as an explicit version of
[
BB12
, Proposition
5.13]
.

Proposition 6.2
.

The differential form

\omega^{\prime}=\dfrac{y(P)}{x-x(P)}\dfrac{dx}{y}

satisfies
\operatorname{Res}(\omega^{\prime})=P-\iota(P)
.

Proof.

To prove this, we use that the differential
\dfrac{dx}{y}
is holomorphic on
C
. The only possible poles of
\omega^{\prime}
are at
P
and
\iota(P)
.
At these points,
t=x-x(P)
is a uniformizer, so

\omega^{\prime}=\dfrac{y(P)}{y}\dfrac{dt}{t},

which immediately gives
\operatorname{Res}_{P}(\omega^{\prime})=1
and
\operatorname{Res}_{\iota(P)}(\omega^{\prime})=-1
.
∎

For
Step (
ii
)
we follow and improve
[
BB12
, §5.2]
.
Let
\omega^{\prime}
be a form as in Proposition
6.2
and write

\psi(\omega^{\prime})=\sum_{i=0}^{2g-1}u_{i}\eta_{i}
. Then Proposition
2.9
implies

\langle\omega^{\prime},\eta_{j}\rangle=\psi(\omega^{\prime})\cup[\eta_{j}]=\sum_{i=0}^{2g-1}u_{i}([\eta_{i}]\cup[\eta_{j}])\,.

Recalling that
M
is the cup product matrix, we have

(6.1)

\begin{pmatrix}u_{0}&u_{1}&\cdots&u_{2g-1}\end{pmatrix}^{t}=-M^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\langle\omega^{\prime},\eta_{0}\rangle&\langle\omega^{\prime},\eta_{1}\rangle&\cdots&\langle\omega^{\prime},\eta_{2g-1}\rangle\end{pmatrix}^{t}.

By the same argument as in
[
BB12
, Proposition 5.12]
, we have

\langle\omega^{\prime},\eta_{j}\rangle=-\int_{\iota(P)}^{P}\eta_{j}-\operatorname{Res}_{\infty_{+}}\left(\omega^{\prime}\int\eta_{j}\right)-\operatorname{Res}_{\infty_{-}}\left(\omega^{\prime}\int\eta_{j}\right)\,,

since
\infty_{\pm}\notin\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{Res}(\omega^{\prime}))
.
Because
P
and
\iota(P)
(as well as
Q
and
\iota(Q)
) are endpoints of
Coleman integrals, we use our assumption that
Condition 2 is satisfied for
P
and, by symmetry,
Q
.
We can simplify this
further as follows:

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\omega^{\prime}
be a form as in Proposition
6.2
. We have
\operatorname{Res}_{\infty_{+}}\left(\omega^{\prime}\int\eta_{j}\right)=\operatorname{Res}_{\infty_{-}}\left(\omega^{\prime}\int\eta_{j}\right)=0
for
0\leq j\leq 2g-1
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the statement for
\infty_{+}
and for
j\geq g
.
Consider the uniformizer
t=\frac{1}{x}
at
\infty_{+}
. Then we have
\operatorname{ord}_{t}(y)=-g-1
. Using
dx=-t^{-2}dt
we compute

\operatorname{ord}_{t}\left(\dfrac{\omega^{\prime}}{dt}\right)=\operatorname{ord}_{t}\left(-\dfrac{y(P)}{t^{-1}-x(P)}\dfrac{t^{-2}}{y}\right)=-2-(-1+(-g-1))=g,\quad\text{and}

\operatorname{ord}_{t}\left(\int\eta_{j}\right)=\operatorname{ord}_{t}\left(\int\dfrac{t^{-j-1}t^{-2}dt}{t^{-g-1}}\right)=\operatorname{ord}_{t}\left(\int t^{g-j-2}dt\right)=g-j-1.

Thus

\operatorname{ord}_{t}\left(\dfrac{\omega^{\prime}\int\eta_{j}}{dt}\right)=2g-1-j\,,

so
\omega^{\prime}\int\eta_{j}
is holomorphic at
\infty_{+}
because
j\leq 2g-1
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

We can prove a similar statement in the odd degree case. Namely, in the
setting of
[
BB12
, Proposition 5.12]
, we also have

\operatorname{Res}_{\infty}\left(\omega^{\prime}\int\eta_{j}\right)=0
for
0\leq j\leq 2g-1
and for the same definition of
\omega^{\prime}
.
This can be used to simplify the computation of the global symbols
in
[
BB12
, §5.2]
.

We compute
u_{0},\ldots,u_{g-1}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
in Step (
iii
) as in
Lemma
4.4
.

The main task of
Step (
iv
)
is the computation of the integral

\int_{S}^{R}\dfrac{y(P)}{x-x(P)}\dfrac{dx}{y}\,.

If
R
and
S
are in the same residue disc, then the integral
is tiny and can be computed directly. In the remaining cases, it can be computed using a change of variables that translates the desired
integral to the integral
\int\omega_{g}

considered in Section
5
.
Namely, consider

(6.2)

\displaystyle\tau\colon C

\displaystyle\to C^{\prime}\colon y^{\prime 2}=\dfrac{1}{y(P)^{2}}x^{\prime 2g+2}f\left(x(P)+\frac{1}{x^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\left(\dfrac{1}{x-x(P)},\dfrac{-y}{y(P)(x-x(P))^{g+1}}\right)\,.

Since
C^{\prime}
is defined by an even degree model, and since

\tau(P)=\infty_{-}\in C^{\prime}
and
\tau(\iota(P))=\infty_{+}\in C^{\prime}
, the
differential
\frac{y(P)}{x-x(P)}\frac{dx}{y}
is mapped to

\frac{x^{\prime g}dx^{\prime}}{y^{\prime}}
. This is also consistent with the fact that
\operatorname{Res}(\frac{y(P)}{x-x(P)}\frac{dx}{y})=P-\iota(P)
and
\operatorname{Res}(\frac{x^{\prime g}dx^{\prime}}{y^{\prime}})=\infty_{-}-\infty_{+}
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Denote
R^{\prime}=\tau(R)
,
S^{\prime}=\tau(S)
. Then we have

h_{p}(P-\iota(P),R-S)=\int_{S^{\prime}}^{R^{\prime}}\omega_{g}-u_{0}\int_{S}^{R}\eta_{0}-\cdots-u_{g-1}\int_{S}^{R}\eta_{g-1}.

Proof.

This follows from

(6.3)

\int_{S}^{R}\dfrac{y(P)}{x-x(P)}\dfrac{dx}{y}=\int_{S^{\prime}}^{R^{\prime}}\dfrac{x^{\prime g}dx^{\prime}}{y^{\prime}}\,,

for which we use the change of variables formula for
Coleman integrals (see
[
CdS88
, Section 2]
.
∎

Lemma
6.5
reduces Step (
iv
) to Step (
iv
)
from Section
5
.

Remark 6.6
.

In Lemma
6.5
, the points
R,S\in C(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
, and
R^{\prime},S^{\prime}\in C^{\prime}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
are endpoints of Coleman integrals.
Hence we
require Condition 2 for
R,S
, and we require
Condition
1 for
P,Q,R,S
. The division by
y(P)
in the
definition of the map
\tau
makes the assumptions
p\nmid y(P)
and
p\nmid y(Q)

necessary, since otherwise
C^{\prime}
could have non-integral coefficients, so we keep Condition 2 also for
P,Q
.

For convenience, we summarize our algorithm to compute

h_{p}(P-Q,R-S)
, where all points are affine. This makes
Algorithm
4.3
more precise in this case.

Algorithm 6.7
.

(Computation of
h_{p}(P-Q,R-S)
for
affine
P,Q,R,S
)

Input:
The same input as for Algorithm
4.3
, with all points

P,Q,R,S
affine and satisfying Assumption
1
.

Output:
The local height
h_{p}(P-Q,R-S)
.

(1)

Find a differential
\omega^{\prime}
such that
\operatorname{Res}(\omega^{\prime})=P-\iota(P)
. Define
\omega^{\prime}
as in Proposition
6.2
.

(2)

Compute
\psi(\omega^{\prime})
.

(3)

Compute the unique coefficients
u_{0},\ldots,u_{g-1}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}

such that

\omega\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\omega^{\prime}-\sum^{g-1}_{i=0}u_{i}\eta_{i}
satisfies
\operatorname{Res}(\omega)=P-Q
and
\psi(\omega)\in W
.

(4)

Compute the Coleman integrals
\int_{S}^{R}\omega^{\prime}
and
\int_{S}^{R}\eta_{i}
for

i=0,\ldots,g-1
and compute
h_{p}(P-\iota(P),R-S)=\int^{R}_{S}\omega^{\prime}-\sum^{g-1}_{i=0}u_{i}\int^{R}_{S}\eta_{i}
.

(5)

Similarly, compute
h_{p}(Q-\iota(Q),R-S)
.

(6)

Return

\dfrac{1}{2}\left(h_{p}(P-\iota(P),R-S)-h_{p}(Q-\iota(Q),R-S)+\log_{p}\left(\dfrac{x(R)-x(P)}{x(R)-x(Q)}\cdot\dfrac{x(S)-x(Q)}{x(S)-x(P)}\right)\right).

Remark 6.8
.

Alternatively, one could potentially compute
\int^{R}_{S}\omega^{\prime}
using

[
BB12
, Algorithm 4.8]
. Define
\alpha\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\phi^{*}\omega^{\prime}-p\omega^{\prime}
and
\beta
to be a differential whose residue
divisor is
R-S
. Then the desired integral can be expressed as

(6.4)

\int_{S}^{R}\omega^{\prime}=\dfrac{1}{1-p}\cdot\left(\psi(\alpha)\cup\psi(\beta)+\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\operatorname{Res}_{P}\left(\alpha\int\beta\right)-\int_{\phi(S)}^{S}\omega-\int_{R}^{\phi(R)}\omega\right),

where
\mathcal{P}
is the subset of
C(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p})
consisting of the
Weierstrass points and the
poles of
\alpha
. See Equation (14) in
[
BB12
]
.
While this formula is only proved there for odd degree hyperelliptic curves, the extension
to even degree is immediate. This leads to a more general, but more complicated algorithm.

Remark 6.9
.

We can also completely reduce the affine case
to the infinite case discussed in Section
5
.
Recall that it suffices to compute heights of the form
h_{p}(P-\iota(P),R-S)
.
Using the same notation and the map
\tau\colon C\rightarrow C^{\prime}
as in Lemma

6.5
, and the model-independence of local heights (see Corollary

2.11
), this amounts to computing
h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},R^{\prime}-S^{\prime})

on
C^{\prime}
.

7.
Precision analysis

In this section we analyze the precision of our algorithms.
The algorithms in §
3.1
and §
3.3
depend on the precision of local
coordinates. In both cases, we need to compute residues of differentials at
points at infinity. The computations in §
3.3
clearly require more precision than
§
3.1
. We want to compute

\operatorname{Res}_{\infty_{-}}\left(\eta_{j}\int\eta_{i}\right)
for
0\leq i,j\leq 2g-1
.
Recall that
t=\frac{1}{x}
is a uniformizer at
\infty_{-}
and that for
g\leq i\leq 2g-1
we have

\eta_{i}=\left(\frac{1}{t^{i-g+2}}+\cdots\right)dt
. The term that requires the largest precision is

\operatorname{Res}_{\infty_{-}}\left(\eta_{2g-1}\int\eta_{2g-1}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{t^{g+1}}+\cdots\right)dt\cdot\left(\frac{1}{gt^{g}}+\cdots\right).

Hence we need sufficient
t
-adic precision to compute the coefficient of

t^{g-1}
of
\eta_{2g}
, which amounts to computing the first
2g+1
coefficients of
y=y(t)
.

Using Harrison’s extension of Kedlaya’s algorithm
[
Har12
]
, we can
compute the matrix of Frobenius acting on
[\omega_{0}],\ldots,[\omega_{2g}]\subset\operatorname{H^{1}_{MW}}(U)^{-}
to any desired precision
p^{n}
. We assume
that we have already computed the basis differentials

\eta_{0},\ldots,\eta_{2g-1}
in §
3.1
to the same
precision
p^{n}
. Then it is clear that the results of §
3.2

and §
3.4
are correct to
precision
p^{n}
as well.

We now analyze precision for Algorithm
4.3
.
Step (
i
) is exact, so there is no loss of precision.

Step (
iii
) only involves a linear change of variables by Lemma
4.4
, and the loss of precision in this step is bounded by the valuation of the determinant of the corresponding matrix, which can be precomputed.

The precision in Step (
iv
) depends on the precision of the Coleman integrals and the coefficients computed in (
iii
). We compute the integrals using Algorithm 4 from
[
Bal15
]
. As stated in loc. cit., the same precision estimates as in
[
BBK10
]
apply. Briefly:

(a)

By
[
BBK10
, Proposition 18]
, the tiny integrals in
[
Bal15
, Equation (3.2)]
are computed correctly to
\min\{n,k+1-\lfloor\log_{p}(k+1)\rfloor
digits of precision, where the points are accurate to
n
digits and we truncate the local expansions of the differentials at precision
t^{k}
.

(b)

As in
[
BBK10
]
, the evaluation of the functions
h_{i}
at the
endpoints does not lead to any loss in precision. Indeed, according to
Harrison’s algorithm, the functions
h_{i}
are finite sums of terms

c_{i,j}x^{i}y^{j}
, where
i\in\mathbb{Z},i\geq 0
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. If the
c_{i,j}

are computed modulo
p^{n}
, then
h_{i}(P)
is correct to at least
the same precision.

(c)

By
[
BBK10
, Proposition 19]
, if we denote
m^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\operatorname{ord}_{p}\det(\Phi-I)
, and the previous computations are correct modulo
p^{n}
, then the Coleman integrals are computed correctly modulo
p^{n-m^{\prime}}
.

Remark 7.1
.

By
[
Har12
, Section 3]
, a working precision of
n+\lfloor\log_{p}(2n)\rfloor+1
digits suffices to compute both the matrix of Frobenius and the functions
h_{i}
to precision
p^{n}
.

Step (
ii
) is the only step where we need to distinguish cases. We first deal with the approach from Section
5
. Suppose that the matrix of Frobenius and the differentials
\eta_{0},\ldots,\eta_{2g-1}
of

\operatorname{H^{1}_{dR}}
are correct modulo
p^{n}
. Denoting
m\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\operatorname{ord}_{p}\det(\operatorname{Frob}-pI)
, we compute
\psi(\omega^{\prime})
in terms of
\eta_{0},\ldots,\eta_{2g-1}
correctly modulo
p^{n-m}
.

It remains to analyze the precision for Step (
ii
) in Section
6
; more precisely, this is Step (
2
) in Algorithm
6.7
). We first compute the global symbols
\langle\omega^{\prime},\eta_{j}\rangle=-\int_{\iota(P)}^{P}\eta_{j}
.
and then apply (
6.1
).
Hence, if all computations so far are correct modulo
p^{n}
, and we denote
m^{\prime\prime}=\operatorname{ord}_{p}\det(M)
, where
M
is the cup product matrix, the coordinates of
\psi(\omega^{\prime})
in Step (
ii
) are computed accurately modulo
p^{n-m^{\prime\prime}}
.

All three matrices
\operatorname{Frob}-pI
,
\Phi-I
, and
M
depend only on the curve. Thus, we may precompute their determinants and predict which starting
precision is necessary to reach a specified target precision.
For the approach discussed in Remark
6.9
, we also need to
compute
\det(\operatorname{Frob}-pI)
and
\det(\Phi-I)
for the curve
C^{\prime}
, which depends on the point
P
.

In addition, Step (
6
) in Algorithm
6.7
requires an evaluation of
\log_{p}
, but since
P,Q,R,S
satisfy Assumption
1
, this does not lead to any loss of precision.

Remark 7.2
.

Our precision analysis assumes that the matrix of Frobenius
on
\operatorname{H^{1}_{MW}}(U)
is
p
-adically integral.
As shown in
[
Har12
, Lemma 3.4, Conclusion]
this is the
case when
p>g
, so we assume this, as
in
[
Bal15
]
, whenever needed.

Remark 7.3
.

In the precision analysis above, we assume Assumption
1
. As discussed
in §
8.2.1
and §
8.2.2
,
Condition 2 is not necessary if we assume Condition 1, and in order to reduce
to the situation treated above, only symmetry and
tiny integrals are required.
Hence, we do not need an additional
precision analysis in this more general situation.

Implementation

We have implemented Algorithm

in
SageMath
. For simplicity,

, whenever needed (see Remark

),

\log_{p}

discussed in the present article and in
[

[

•

Balakrishnan’s algorithm from
[

to compute

when

available from

and
S
are
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-points in

and
6

(P,Q)

for

)
of Section

Equation (
6.4
). The latter would require

[

that can have degree as large as

, or even larger.
While this would lead to a more general implementation than our present one, we expect it to be
significantly slower.
We have also implemented the approach discussed in
Remark
6.9
in
SageMath
. For a single height computation,

Remark 8.1
.

The main reason for assuming that our points are defined over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is
that for even degree hyperelliptic curves, no implementation of Coleman

is currently available. Once such

For instance, Best

]
has implemented Coleman integration for superelliptic

for odd degree hyperelliptic curves. Notably, his implementation
works for unramified extensions of

. The special case of an extension to

would allow us to extend our algorithm in practice to points

defined over unramified extensions of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.
Moreover, in forthcoming work, Best, Kaya and Keller will extend the general

[

and give

8.2.
Implementation - special cases

We now explain how to compute
h_{p}(P-Q,R-S)
when Condition 2 is not
satisfied for either
(P,Q)
or
(R,S)
. Recall that we still assume that
P,Q,R,S\in C(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
satisfy Condition 1.

8.2.1.
Weierstrass and infinite discs

We can deal with the case that
R
or
S
belongs to a Weierstrass disc as
follows. Suppose that
D(R)
contains a Weierstrass point
T
and that

\omega
has no singularity at
T
. This is satisfied in our applications of Algorithm
4.3
.
Then

\int_{S}^{R}\omega=\int_{S}^{T}\omega+\int_{T}^{R}\omega
. The second integral is tiny,
so we can compute it. Since we integrate odd forms, we can also compute the first integral, using
\int_{S}^{T}\omega=\frac{1}{2}\int_{S}^{\iota(S)}\omega
(see
[
BBK10
, Lemma 16]
).
We could therefore replace
Condition 2 by the weaker condition that

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(y(R))\geq 0
and
\operatorname{ord}_{p}(y(S))\geq 0
.

By symmetry, it follows that we can compute
h_{p}(P-Q,R-S)
if

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(y(P)),\operatorname{ord}_{p}(y(Q))\geq 0
or if
\operatorname{ord}_{p}(y(R)),\operatorname{ord}_{p}(y(S))\geq 0
.
Lemma
6.1
implies that it is enough to compute heights of
the type
h_{p}(P-\iota(P),R-\iota(R))
. Condition 1 implies that
it is not possible to have both
\operatorname{ord}_{p}(y(P))<0
and
\operatorname{ord}_{p}(y(R))<0
at
the same time. Hence, without loss of generality, the only remaining case
is when we have
\operatorname{ord}_{p}(y(P))\geq 0
,
\operatorname{ord}_{p}(y(R))<0
, and
R\in D(\infty_{-})
. Then

h_{p}(P-\iota(P),R-\iota(R))=h_{p}(P-\iota(P),\infty_{-}-\infty_{+})\\
+h_{p}(P-\iota(P),R-\infty_{-})-h_{p}(P-\iota(P),\iota(R)-\infty_{+})\,.

We find
h_{p}(P-\iota(P),\infty_{-}-\infty_{+})
using the method from
Section
5
. The integrals that show up in Step
(
ii
) and Step (
iv
) when computing
h_{p}(P-\iota(P),R-\infty_{-})
and
h_{p}(P-\iota(P),\iota(R)-\infty_{+})
are tiny, so we can compute all heights on the right hand side.

Hence we only have to
assume Condition 1 when the points
P,Q,R,S
are affine.
We have implemented this approach in
SageMath
.

8.2.2.
Points at infinity

When exactly one of the points
P,Q,R,S
(satisfying Condition 1) is a point at infinity, we compute
the local height using a similar strategy. Namely, we have

\displaystyle h_{p}(\infty_{-}-Q,R-S)=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\log_{p}\left(\dfrac{x(S)-x(Q)}{x(R)-x(Q)}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+\left.h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\infty_{+},R-S)-h_{p}(Q-\iota(Q),R-S)\right).

The case
P=\infty_{+}
is analogous, since
h_{p}(\infty_{+}-Q,R-S)=h_{p}(\infty_{-}-\iota(Q),\iota(R)-\iota(S))
.
We have also implemented this case in
SageMath
.

8.2.3.
Weakening Condition 1 to Condition 1’

We now explain how Condition 1 may be weakened. However, we have
not implemented this, so in our implementation
we assume Condition 1.

Assume that the points
P,Q,R,S
do not satisfy Condition 1, but do
satisfy Condition 1’.
By Remark
6.9
, it is enough to consider the approach in
Section
5
, where Condition 1
is required to perform Step (
iv
).
One possible approach is to use the existing
Magma
implementation of the algorithm
for Coleman integrals on general
curves due to Balakrishnan and
Tuitman
[
BT20
]
. This algorithm directly computes Coleman integrals with
respect to a basis of
\operatorname{H^{1}_{dR}}(C/\mathbb{Q}_{p})
. However, it currently cannot be used
to compute integrals of differentials that are not essentially of the second kind; in particular,
we cannot compute integrals of the form
\int^{R}_{S}\omega_{g}
directly. Instead, we could
follow the strategy from
[
BB12
]
to carry out Step (
iv
), by using the differential
\alpha=\phi^{*}(\omega_{g})-p\omega_{g}
. which is essentially of the second kind. Hence

\int_{R}^{S}\alpha
can be computed using the existing
Magma
implementation even when
R
or
S
belong to
D(\infty_{-})

or
D(\infty_{+})
.
We obtain the required integral from

\int_{S}^{R}\omega_{g}=\dfrac{1}{1-p}\left(\int_{S}^{R}\alpha-\int_{\phi(S)}^{S}\omega_{g}-\int_{R}^{\phi(R)}\omega_{g}\right).

The integrals
\int_{\phi(S)}^{S}\omega_{g}
and
\int_{R}^{\phi(R)}\omega_{g}
are tiny integrals,
hence they can be computed easily.

Remark 8.2
.

As remarked in Step (
iv
) of Section
6
, if the
endpoints of a Coleman integral are in the same residue disc, we can
compute it as a tiny integral. Hence it would be also possible to weaken
Condition 1 by allowing that
R
and
S
(or by symmetry,
P
and

Q
) are in the same residue disc. But as this is a minor improvement
comparing to weakening Condition 1 to Condition 1’ and
was not necessary for our applications, we have not implemented this.
