Control of high-dimensional collective dynamics by deep neural feedback laws and kinetic modelling


Abstract
Modeling and control of agent-based models is twice cursed by the dimensionality of the problem, as both the number of agents and their state space dimension can be large. Even though the computational barrier posed by a large ensemble of agents can be overcome through a mean field formulation of the control problem, the feasibility of its solution is generally guaranteed only for agents operating in low-dimensional spaces. To circumvent the difficulty posed by the high dimensionality of the state space a kinetic model is proposed, requiring the sampling of high-dimensional, two-agent sub-problems, to evolve the agents’ density using a Boltzmann type equation. Such density evolution requires a high-frequency sampling of two-agent optimal control problems, which is efficiently approximated by means of deep neural networks and supervised learning, enabling the fast simulation of high-dimensional, large-scale ensembles of controlled particles. Numerical experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach in the control of consensus and attraction-repulsion dynamics.

Introduction

Collective behaviour in agent-based models (ABMs) is of evergrowing interest across various disciplines, including mathematics, physics, biology, and economics. ABMs enable the description of complex phenomena through a general paradigm that combines endogenous interactions between agents with external influences. Their applicability spans diverse areas, such as social sciences
[
46
,
29
]
, robotics, and computer science
[
22
]
. A fundamental topic of interest in ABMs is the study of pattern formation and self-organization
[
54
,
8
]
. However, beyond self-organization, a fascinating topic arises in relation to the design of external signals or controls to influence a system and inducing a prescribed collective behaviour
[
19
]
.

Agent-based models encode pairwise agent-to-agent interactions through a balance of attraction and repulsion forces acting over first or second-order dynamics, while the influence of the external world on the system is expressed as a suitable control signal. To make matters more concrete, let us consider a second-order system with
N
agents in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the state of the
i
-th agent is encoded by the pair
s_{i}=(x_{i},v_{i})\in\Omega_{x}\times\Omega_{v}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, representing position and velocity, respectively, evolving according to transport-interaction dynamics of the form

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=v_{i}(t)

(1)

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}P(x_{i}(t),x_{j}(t))(v_{j}(t)-v_{i}(t))+u_{i}(t)\,,\qquad i=1,...,N.

Here,
P(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes an interaction kernel, while
u_{i}(t)
is a control signal influencing agent
i
. The ensemble of control signals is denoted by
\mathbf{u}(t)=(u_{1}(t),\ldots,u_{N}(t))^{\top}
. The core of the self-organization behaviour of the free dynamics resides in
P
, which can induce clustering, polarization or alignment, among many others. This self-organization behaviour can be modified by the influence of an external control law. In the framework of dynamic optimization, this control is synthesized by minimizing a cost functional which rewards the convergence of the system towards a cooperative goal, e.g. consensus

\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)=\underset{\mathbf{u}(\cdot)}{\textrm{argmin}}\bigg\{\mathcal{J}_{N}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}):=\dfrac{1}{N}\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\|v_{i}-\bar{v}\|^{2}+\gamma\,\ell(u_{i})\,dt\bigg\}\,,\qquad\gamma>0\,,

(OCP)

where the first term in the cost is promoting consensus towards the target velocity
\bar{v}
, while
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{0\}
is a convex function penalizing the energy spent by the control
u_{i}
.

The solution of the optimal control problem defined by minimizing (
OCP
) subject to (
1
) is
twice cursed
by the dimensionality of the problem:
solving the OCP becomes prohibitively expensive for large values of
N
(as in swarm robotics or collective animal behaviour where hundreds or thousands of agents are present), as well as for high-dimensional state spaces (i.e.
d\gg 1
, as in portfolio optimization). A partial remedy to this problem comes from statistical mechanics by assuming the number of agents
N\to\infty
, so that the dynamics of the individual-based problem can be approximated by a mean field equation

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=-\nabla_{v}\cdot\bigg((\mathcal{P}[f]+u)f\bigg)\,,

(2)

where
f(t,x,v)
is the probability density of having an agent with state
(x,v)
at time
t
, and the mean field interaction force is given by the non-local operator

\mathcal{P}[f](t,x,v):=\int\limits_{\Omega_{x}\times\Omega_{v}}P(x,x_{*})(v_{*}-v)f(t,x_{*},v_{*})dx_{*}\,dv_{*}\,.

(3)

A direct transcription of the objective (
OCP
) leads to the mean field optimal control problem
[
28
]

\underset{u}{\textrm{min}}\;\mathcal{J}(f,u):=\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}\!\!\int\limits_{\Omega_{x}\times\Omega_{v}}\!\!\left(\|v-\bar{v}\|^{2}+\gamma\,\ell(u)\right)\,f(t,x,v)\,dx\,dv\,dt\,.

(MFOC)

Even though the formulation using (
2
) alleviates the curse of dimensionality with respect to
N
, it leads to a PDE-constrained optimization problem over
2d+1
dimensions, which becomes prohibitively expensive already for moderate values of
d
. The solution of high-dimensional mean field optimal control problems has been addressed using deep learning techniques in
[
55
]
in the case of linear dynamics, however, the nonlinear case remains open. In this paper, we propose the synthesis of a feedback control for (
2
) which is a suboptimal solution to (
MFOC
). In order to overcome the obstacle posed by the treatment of
high-dimensional agents with nonlinear interactions,
we resort to modeling the evolution of the agents density
f
from a kinetic viewpoint, reformulating the mean field controlled dynamics as a Povzner-Boltzmann type equation

\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\lambda\mathcal{Q}_{\eta,u}(f,f)(t,x,v)\,,

(4)

where the operator
\mathcal{Q}
takes into account the gain and the loss of particles in
(x,v)
at time
t
, due to the motion of individuals via free transport and the velocity changes resulting from the controlled interaction dynamics, see
[
52
,
48
]
. If we consider the interaction dynamics in (
1
), reduced to the case of
N=2
particles with velocities
(v,v_{*})
, by denoting as
(v^{\prime},v_{*}^{\prime})
the update of those velocities after a forward Euler step of length
\eta
, we have

\mathcal{Q}_{\eta,u}(f,f)(t;x,v)=\int\limits_{\Omega_{x}\times\Omega_{v}}\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{J}_{\eta}}f(t,x,^{\prime}v)f(t,x_{*},^{\prime}v_{*})-f(t,x,v)f(t,x_{*},v_{*})\,dx_{*}\,dv_{*}\,,

(5)

where
(^{\prime}v,^{\prime}v_{*})\longmapsto(v,v_{*})
are the pre-interaction velocities that generate the couple
(v,v_{*})
, while
\mathcal{J}_{\eta}
is the Jacobian of the binary interactions map
(v,v_{*})\longmapsto(v^{\prime},v_{*}^{\prime})
.

We will show that the alternative mesoscopic description of ABMs via eq. (
4
) is consistent with a sub-optimal, controlled solution of the constrained mean field PDE (
2
), when assuming high frequency
\lambda
and weak interactions
\eta
between particles, similarly to the
grazing-collision
limit in kinetic theory
[
38
]
. The convenience of such kinetic formulation relies on the reduced computational cost required for its solution via
direct simulation Monte Carlo
(DSMC) methods,
[
4
]
. Such a sampling-based technique approximates the solution of the Boltzmann eq.(
4
) by computing a collection of binary sub-problems for couples of agents sampled from the population density function
f(t,\cdot)

[
48
]
.

Figure 1

\mathbf{s}_{(i)}:=(x_{(i)},v_{(i)})
the state of the
i-
th agent.

Assuming a high interaction frequency between agents requires, at each evolution step, the solution of a large number of reduced 2-agent (
OCP
). For efficiency purposes, we rely on supervised learning to build an
Artificial Neural Networks
(ANN) approximating the solution of the binary OCP, thus circumventing its online solution at every sampling instance. For training, we generate synthetic data via the solution of the binary OCP using a discrete time
state-dependent Riccati equation
(SDRE) approach
[
32
,
63
]
. The overall procedure is outlined in Figure
1
.

Related literature and contributions

Simulation and control of high-dimensional ABMs is a longstanding and challenging topic. The modeling of such systems has been extensively studied within the kinetic research community, aiming at reducing the computational complexity of simulations
[
12
,
13
,
24
]
. The formulation of a kinetic description for evolutionary models has inspired a flourishing literature in ABMs
[
16
,
49
,
58
,
59
]
.
In this direction, the authors of
[
5
]
proposed a Monte Carlo approach based on binary collision dynamics
[
48
]
, inspired from plasma physics. The proposed methodology addresses the integration of the mean field formulation of ABMs by means of a Boltzmann scheme, allowing to retrieve the mean field evolution of the system as a limit of 2-agents sub-problems. Such complexity reduction has motivated the extension of this numerical scheme to fit the optimal control framework
[
4
,
50
,
51
]
.

In
[
2
,
4
]
, consistency was shown between the feedback controlled, non-local, mean field equation (
2
) and the homogeneous non-linear Boltzmann equation (
4
) with controlled binary interactions in the quasi-invariant scaling, and where the control action has been optimized by means of
model predictive control
(MPC)
[
53
]
, or via
dynamic programming
(DP)
[
11
]
. Similar approaches can be found also in
[
6
]
for leader-follower multi-agent systems. In the MPC case, the controller is to be considered sub-optimal, as it is designed to optimize the OCP up to a reduced horizon. DP instead, leads to optimal solutions for the binary OCP, but it requires the solution of a first-order nonlinear Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) PDE cast in the state-space of the system, which can be of arbitrarily high dimension.

Over the last years, the solution of high-dimensional HJB-PDEs has been addressed with a number of different numerical approaches
[
10
,
25
,
44
,
30
,
45
]
, and a flourishing literature on Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) . ANN methods are differentiated between unsupervised learning techniques
[
21
,
39
,
40
,
55
,
43
]
and supervised ones
[
33
,
1
,
62
]
. Examples of deep learning algorithms for solving PDEs can be found in
[
61
]
for high dimensional Boltzmann equations, or in
[
36
,
56
]
for more general applications.

The main contributions of our work, inspired by the aforementioned results, can be summarized as follows:

•

Aiming at a further reduction in computational complexity with respect to
[
4
,
2
]
, we rely on data-driven approximation models fitted in a supervised learning fashion over synthetic data for the reduced binary OCP. For this, we consider candidate approximation models of both feed-forward (FNN) and recurrent (RNN) neural network type.
The architecture of such fitted approximation models conveniently allows for batch evaluations of data, meaning that at each time step the controls can be computed for all the sampled 2-agents sub-systems at once. The precision of the fitted models and their efficiency as the dimensionality of the problem increases have been assessed through numerical tests.

•

The dataset guiding the ANNs training phase is collected from synthetic data, obtained from the solution of the infinite horizon (
OCP
) reduced to the binary case. Unlike the approaches taken in
[
2
]
, we circumvent the solution of the HJB equation associated to the binary OCP, which would be unfeasible even in moderate agent dimensions. Instead, we rely on a procedure for synthesizing nonlinear feedback controls that combines elements from both DP and MPC: the State-Dependent Riccati equation approach. In particular, we will focus on discrete time settings
[
27
,
18
]
.

•

A first, intuitive, choice is to consider the feedback control as target variable of the approximation task, as done in
[
53
,
1
,
33
,
35
,
26
]
. Furthermore, we also train networks for approximating the whole of the controlled right hand side of the discrete-time binary controlled dynamics. For uncontrolled systems, approximation techniques has been studied for detection and approximation of interaction kernels
P(\cdot,\cdot)

[
14
,
37
]
. However, to our knowledge, this work has not been yet extended to the controlled framework.

The rest of the paper has been organized as follows. In Section 2 we construct an approximation for the sub-optimal control of the mean field PDE (
2
) from a kinetic viewpoint. This leads to a Boltzmann description of the dynamics (
4
), which is then proven to converge to its mean field counterpart when performing a proper scaling of the frequency and strength of interactions. In Section 3 we discuss how to approximate the evolution of system according to the Boltzmann dynamics for the distribution of agents via Monte Carlo simulation: from the current system configuration, we sample a pool of agents, which are then randomly coupled according to statistics of interaction. The post-interaction agents are then considered, so that their sampling distribution models the updated population density. Following this scheme, the evolution of the system is tracked at discrete times. This requires the computation of the feedback control in discrete-time settings, which is addressed in Section 4 by means of a discrete time state-dependent Riccati Equation approach. In Section 5, we discuss a supervised learning approximation to the solution of the binary OCP. Numerical tests are presented in Section 6.

2
A Boltzmann formulation for the control of mean field dynamics

The accurate modeling of self-organization phenomena and optimal control in ABMs requires a large number of interacting individuals, implying the need for the solution of a very high dimensional optimal control problem, which often comes at a prohibitively expensive computational cost. An alternative way to address this problem is to model instead the distribution function
f(t,x,v)
describing the density of individuals having state variable
(x,v)\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
at time
t\geq 0
. The evolution of
f(t,x,v)
can be characterized by a kinetic equation accounting for the motion of individuals undergoing pairwise interactions, as modeled in (
8
). Thus, the mean field dynamics can be retrieved by suitable scaling of the interactions, also referred as
quasi-invariant scaling
, or grazing collision limit
[
48
,
60
]
. In particular, the quasi-invariant limit consists of considering an interaction regime where low intensity interactions occur with high frequency. In this regime, the density
f(t,\cdot,\cdot)
is expected to converge pointwise in time to the solution of the mean field controlled PDE (
2
), which corresponds to a sub-optimal solution of the mean field optimal control problem (
MFOC
).

2.1
Binary controlled dynamics

We denote by
(x,v),(x_{*},v_{*})\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
the position and velocity states of two agents in the population, and we assume that they modify their velocity states according to binary interaction maps
(v,v_{*})\mapsto(v^{\prime},v_{*}^{\prime})
as follows

\displaystyle v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=v+\eta P(x,x_{*})(v_{*}-v)\,+\eta u,

(6)

\displaystyle v_{*}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=v_{*}+\eta\,P(x_{*},x)(v-v_{*})+\eta u_{*}\,,

where
\eta
is the strength of interaction,
\mathbf{u}=(u,u_{*})^{\top}
is the forcing term associated to interaction between agents.
The goal of such external influence is to steer a couple of agents toward consensus. In particular, we will address the optimal control
\bar{u}
as the solution of an infinite horizon binary optimal control problem, as follows

\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=\underset{\mathbf{u}\in U_{\textrm{adm}}}{\textrm{argmin}}\bigg\{\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}\|v-\bar{v}\|^{2}+\|v_{*}-\bar{v}\|^{2}+\gamma\,\big(\mathcal{G}(u)+\mathcal{G}(u_{*})\big)\,dt\bigg\},\,

(7)

where
U_{adm}=L^{\infty}([0,+\infty[;\mathbb{R}^{2d})
is the space of admissible controls. The numerical procedure for the feedback control synthesis will be addressed in section
4
.

Furthermore, we model the evolution in time of
f(t,x,v)
with a kinetic integro-differential equation of Boltzmann type
[
2
,
47
,
52
]

\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(t,x,v)=\lambda\mathcal{Q}_{\eta,u}(f,f)(t,x,v)\,,

(8)

where the parameter
\lambda
encodes the interaction frequency, and the operator
Q_{\eta,u}(f,f)
accounts for the gain and loss of particles with state
(x,v)
at time
t

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\eta,u}(f,f)=\mathcal{Q}^{+}_{\eta,u}(f,f)-\mathcal{Q}^{-}_{\eta,u}(f,f).

(9)

In particular,
we can express respectively the gain and loss operators as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\eta,u}^{+}(f,f)(t,x,v)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{J}_{\eta,u}}f(t,x,^{\prime}v)f(t,x_{*},^{\prime}v_{*})\,dx_{*}\,dv_{*}\,,

(10a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\eta,u}^{-}(f,f)(t,x,v)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,x,v)f(t,x_{*},v_{*})\,dx_{*}\,dv_{*}\,,

(10b)

where
(^{\prime}v,^{\prime}v_{*})\longmapsto(v,v_{*})
are the pre-interaction velocities that generate the couple
(v,v_{*})
, while
\mathcal{J}_{\eta,u}
is the Jacobian of the binary interactions map (
6
).
To avoid the presence of the jacobian we can
introduce a test function
\varphi(x,v)\in C^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and express the Povzner-Boltzmann operator in the weak form as

\displaystyle\lambda\langle\mathcal{Q}_{\eta,u}(f,f),\varphi\rangle=\lambda\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left(\varphi(x,v^{\prime})-\varphi(x,v)\right)ff_{*}\,dx_{*}\,dv_{*}\,dx\,dv,

(11)

where
v^{\prime}
denotes the post-interaction velocity as in (
6
), and and we adopted the shorten notation
f=f(t,x,v)
, and
f_{*}=f(t,x_{*},v_{*})
.

Remark 2.1

Here we considered the unbounded domain in space and velocity, where the operator (
9
) accounts interactions with constant collision frequency
\lambda
, analogously to the Boltzmann equation for Maxwell molecules. In general, the interaction frequency is ruled by a non-linear kernel, where in the Povzner-Boltzmann model it reads as follows

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{Q}_{\eta,u},\varphi\rangle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}}B(x,x_{*},v,v_{*})\left(\varphi(x,v^{\prime})-\varphi(x,v)\right)f\,f_{*}\,dx_{*}\,dv_{*}\,dx\,dv,

(12)

where the kernel
B(\cdot)
in the Povzner approach
[
52
]
considers non-local interactions among particles.

If we consider a bounded domain, further conditions should be included in (
8
). Of particular interest is the case, when the velocity space is bounded, e.g.
v,v_{*}\in\Omega_{v}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this situation, we can directly impose in (
12
) that the boundary is satisfied introducing a non-linear interaction kernel
B
such as
B\equiv\chi(v^{\prime}\in\Omega_{v})\chi(v^{\prime}_{*}\in\Omega_{v})
. This latter choice automatically satisfies the boundaries, however such nonlinear kernel causes major difficulties when we are interested in studying asymptotic properties of the model (
8
), such as mean field approximations. Alternatively, following the approach proposed in
[
57
]
, one can ask that the discrete interaction (
6
) preserves the boundary. In this setting, such requirement is possible by properly designing the admissible space of controls
U_{\textrm{adm}}
in such a way that the boundary cannot be violated.
We also observe that the control acts by forcing the state within an admissible position in the bounded domain. Thus, to preserve numerically the bounds a possible strategy is to require a sufficiently small penalty parameter
\gamma>0
in the cost functional (
7
), as for example shown in
[
59
]
, where an explicit form of the control is obtained.

2.2
Consistency with the mean field formulation

Here we focus on the consistency of the Boltzmann operator (
9
) with a mean field controlled dynamics of the type (
2
), in particular introducing a
quasi-invariant optimality limit
we can regularize such operator, considering a regime where interactions strength is low and frequency is high. This technique, analogous to the grazing collision limit in plasma physics, has been thoroughly studied in
[
60
]
and specifically for first order models in
[
20
,
7
]
, and allows to pass from Boltzmann equation (
8
) to a mean field equation
[
4
,
6
]
.
In what follows we consider the change of notation for the control in the binary dynamics (
6
)

u\to u_{\eta}(x,v,x_{*},v_{*}),\quad u_{*}\to u_{\eta}(x_{*},v_{*},x,v)

to give explicit dependence on the parameters and the state variables, since we focus on feedback type controls, and we introduce the following assumptions

(i)

the system (
6
) constitutes an invertible changes of variables from
(v,w)
to
(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})
;

(ii)

there exists an integrable function
u(x,v,x_{*},v_{*})
such that the following limit is well defined

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\to 0}u_{\eta}(x,v,x_{*},v_{*})=u(x,v,x_{*},v_{*}).

(13)

Hence, in accordance with
[
16
]
and with
[
2
]
, where an analogous of this argument is furnished for the controlled dynamics in stochastic settings, we state the following theorem

Theorem 2.1

Consider the Boltzmann-type equation (
8
), with
\eta,\lambda>0
and the control
u_{\eta}\in U_{\textrm{adm}}
, where
U_{\textrm{adm}}
is the class the admissible controls.
Furthermore, assume the kernel function
F_{\eta}(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d})
for all
\eta>0
, where

F_{\eta}(x,v,x_{*},v_{*})=P(x,x_{*})(v_{*}-v)+u_{\eta}(x,v,x_{*},v_{*}),

and define the parameter
\varepsilon>0
to introduce the quasi-invariant scaling as follows

\eta=\varepsilon,\qquad\lambda={1/\varepsilon},

(14)

which links the strength and the frequency of the interactions in the Boltzmann-type equation (
8
).

Thus, if
f^{\varepsilon}(x,v,t)
is a solution for the scaled equation (
8
),
for
\varepsilon\to 0

f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)
converges pointwise, up to a subsequence, to
f(t,x,v)
where
f
satisfies the following controlled mean field equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=-\nabla_{v}\cdot\left((\mathcal{P}[f]+\mathcal{U}[f])f\right)

(15)

with initial data
f^{0}(x,v)=f(0,x,v)
and
where
\mathcal{P}[\cdot]
represents the interaction kernel (
3
) and the control is such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}[f](t,x,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(x,v,x_{*},v_{*})f(t,x_{*},v_{*})\,dx_{*}\,dv_{*}

(16)

where
u(x,v,x_{*},v_{*})
is defined as the limiting value in (
13
).

We report the proof of this result in the
A
, as a reformulation of the result proposed in
[
2
]
.

2.3
Full state controlled binary dynamics

A further generalization consists in introducing a binary interaction dynamics where, differently from the previous section, we consider a binary exchange of information for the full states of the agents
{s}=(x,v)^{\top},~{s}_{*}=(x_{*},v_{*})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.
The interacting process defining

({{s}},{{s}}_{*})\to({s}^{\prime},{{s}^{\prime}_{*})}
is described by a binary rules of the following type

\displaystyle{s}^{\prime}

\displaystyle={s}+\eta G({s},{s}_{*})+\eta{H}u

(17)

\displaystyle{s}_{*}^{\prime}

\displaystyle={s}_{*}+\eta G({s}_{*},{s})+\eta{H}u_{*}

where the controls are respectively
u=u({s},{s_{*}}),u_{*}=u_{*}(s,s_{*})
, and the operators
G
and
H
are defined accordingly to (
6
) as follows

\displaystyle G({s},{s}_{*})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{0}_{d}&\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
\mathbb{0}_{d}&-P(x,x_{*})\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
v\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{0}_{d}&\mathbb{0}_{d}\\
\mathbb{0}_{d}&P(x,x_{*})\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{*}\\
v_{*}\end{pmatrix},\qquad{H}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{0}_{d}\\
\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}.

(18)

Hence, the kinetic density
f(t,{s})
evolves according to a Boltzmann-type model of the following type

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f(t,s)=\lambda\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\eta,u}(f,f)(t,s),

(19)

with frequency
\lambda>0
, and where, differently from the formulation in (
8
), the transport term is now encoded in the interaction operator
\hat{Q}(f,f)(t,s)
.
The interaction operator
\hat{Q}(f,f)(t,s)
is now defined as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\eta,u}(f,f)(t,s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left(\frac{1}{\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\eta,u}}f(t,^{\prime}s)f(t,^{\prime}s_{*})-f(t,s)f(t,s_{*})\right)\,ds,

(20)

where
\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\eta,u}
is the jacobian of the binary rule (
17
), with
(^{\prime}s,^{\prime}s_{*})
the precollisional states.

In this framework, we can retrieve the consistency with mean field model (
15
) via Theorem
2.1
, considering now as a kernel function the following

F_{\eta}(s,s_{*})=\eta G({s},{s}_{*})+\eta{H}u(s,s_{*}),

(21)

and assuming
F_{\eta}(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d};\mathbb{R}^{2d})
.
Then, in the quasi-invariant scaling (
14
), and taking the limit
\varepsilon\to 0

we expect pointwise convergence of the kinetic model (
19
) to the following mean field equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f(t,s)=-\nabla_{{s}}\cdot\left(f(t,s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left(G({s},{s}_{*})+Hu({s},{s}_{*})\right)f(t,{s}_{*})\,d{s}_{*}\right).

(22)

Recalling that
\nabla_{{s}}=(\nabla_{x},\nabla_{v})^{\top}
and that the operators
G,H
are defined as in (
18
), we have that (
22
) is equivalent to the mean field equation (
15
).

This result follows the same steps as the proof of Theorem (

), in this regard

We stress that the main differences, with respect to the results of the previous section, consist in treating the transport term as part of the interaction dynamics, and requiring that (

3
Asymptotic Monte Carlo methods for constrained mean field dynamics

We provide a fast simulation numerical scheme for the Povzner-Boltzmann-type controlled equation, in the asymptotic regime, reminiscent of Direct Simulaton Monte Carlo methods (DSMCs), used in plasma physics,
[
13
,
12
,
5
]
and later adapted to collective dynamics
[
5
,
57
]
.

First of all, considering a splitting of the transport and collisional term of the Boltzmann-type equation (
8
) in the asymptotic regime in two different steps
[
5
]
:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f=-v\cdot\,\nabla_{x}f\qquad\qquad&\text{\emph{transport}}\\[2.15277pt]
\partial_{t}f={\varepsilon}^{-1}\,\mathcal{Q}_{\eta,u}(f,f).\qquad\qquad&\text{\emph{interaction}}\end{cases}

(23)

The purpose of the splitting scheme (
23
) is to focus the discussion on the convergence of the collisional term, since the free transport process coincides with the mean field formulation and the Boltzmann one.

In the previous section, consistency between the time evolution of the agents’ population has been proven between the mean field model for the population density function, and a scaled Boltzmann description for the dynamics. The latter modeling of
f(t,x,v)
is guided by the reduced microscopic binary interactions (
6
) between agents. This motivates the resorting to Monte Carlo simulation techniques for the approximation of the population density function under the Boltzmann formulation
[
48
,
47
,
5
,
2
]
.

The evolution (
23
) of
f
in
[0,T]
can be modeled by means of a Forward Euler method, for which we define a time step
\Delta t
, and discrete times
t_{n}=n\Delta t
for
n=0,\ldots,N_{T}

{\partial_{t}f}(t_{n},x,v)\,\approx\frac{f(t_{n+1},x,v)-f(t_{n},x,v)}{\Delta t}.

(24)

In the spirit of DSMC methods, we can design a stochastic simulation scheme where we sample
N_{s}
agents from
f^{0}
and we approximate the time variation of
\partial_{t}f
as the evolution of the sampled distribution according to the post transport/interaction states.

Concerning the free transport process, the time evolution amounts to the exact free flow at time
t_{n}
of sample particles

\left\{(x^{n}_{i},v^{n}_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{s}}
and evolves as follows

x^{n+1}_{i}=x^{n}_{i}+\Delta tv^{n}_{i}\,\qquad i=1,\ldots,N_{s}.

(25)

The collisional term in the Boltzmann-type equation (
23
), can be rewritten in dependence of the gain and loss components of the operator
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,u}
:

\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,u}^{+}(f,f)(t,x,v)-\rho f(t,x,v)\right)\,,

(26)

where
\rho>0
represent the total mass

\rho=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(t,x_{*},v_{*})\,dx_{*}\,dv_{*}\,.

(27)

Assuming
f
to be a probability density function we will consider
\rho=1
. The Forward Euler scheme for (
26

f^{n}=f(t_{n},x,v)
reads

f^{n+1}=\bigg(1-\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon}\bigg)f^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon}\,\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,u}^{+}(f^{n},f^{n})\,,

(28)

since

is a probability density, thanks to mass conservation
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,u}^{+}
is again probability density function. Moreover, under the restriction
\Delta t\leq\varepsilon
, also

is also a probability density. Equation (

) can be interpret as follows: an agent with state
(x,v)
has probability
(1-\Delta t/\varepsilon)\in[0,1]
to avoid collision with other agents in each time interval
[t,t+\Delta t]
. When, instead, the collision does happen (event with probability
\Delta t/\varepsilon\in[0,1]
), the evolution follows the interaction law
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,u}^{+}
described by the scaled binary interaction, then a sampled pair of agents at time
t_{n}

(x,v):=(x_{i}^{n},v_{i}^{n}),\qquad(x_{*},v_{*}):=(x_{j}^{n},v_{j}^{n})

evolves according to (

\displaystyle v^{n+1}_{i}

\displaystyle=v^{n}_{i}+{\varepsilon}\,P\left(x^{n}_{i},x^{n}_{j}\right)\left(v^{n}_{j}-v^{n}_{i}\right)+\varepsilon\,u\left(x^{n}_{i},v^{n}_{i},x^{n}_{j},v^{n}_{j}\right),

(29)

\displaystyle v^{n+1}_{j}

\displaystyle=v^{n}_{j}+{\varepsilon}\,P\left(x^{n}_{j},x^{n}_{i}\right)\left(v^{n}_{i}-v^{n}_{j}\right)+\varepsilon\,u\left(x^{n}_{j},v^{n}_{j},x^{n}_{i},v^{n}_{i}\right).

In what follows we consider the asymptotic regime with
\Delta t=\varepsilon
, where the binary interactions (
29

28

is of paramount importance to approximate consistently the mean-field model (
15

2.1

N_{s}
and the size of
\varepsilon
, indeed it is possible to see that below a certain threshold

there is no further improvement in approaching the mean field model. We refer the reader to
[

,

]
for a detailed discussion of these methods, and to
[
15
]
for a rigorous convergence analysis of stochastic particle dynamics in this framework.

The asymptotic Monte-Carlo algorithm can be formalized as the procedure outlined in Algorithm

[

]
. This approach differs from the conventional Nanbu scheme in plasma physics, which was originally developed for free particle systems lacking external control inputs, and it does not require the introduction of a mesh.

Up to this moment, the discussion overlooked the derivation of the forcing terms

in the interaction (

4
Infinite horizon optimal control of binary dynamics

In this section we study the solution of the infinite horizon optimal control problem for the reduced 2-agent system.

We recall some fundamental notions on optimal control for discrete-time systems
[
41
]
, to then present its numerical approximation using a discrete-time State-Dependent Riccati Equation approach.

We begin by reformulating (
29
) in a general control-affine discrete time systems of the form:

\mathbf{z}(t_{n+1})=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{z}(t_{n}))+\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{z}(t_{n}))\mathbf{u}(t_{n})\,,

(30)

where
\mathbf{z}(t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}
and
\mathbf{u}(t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mu}
denote the state of the system and the control signal at time
t_{n}=n\Delta t
, such that
\mathbf{z}(t_{n}):=(s^{n},s^{n}_{*})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\kappa},
with
\kappa=d
or
2d
depending on whether the agent dynamics are of first or second order, respectively. The control vector is defined as
\mathbf{u}(t_{n}):=(u^{n},u_{*}^{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mu}\,,
with
\mu=2d
.

The state-to-state map
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{z}):\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mu}
and the control operator
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{z}):\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}\to\mathbb{R}^{\kappa\times\mu}
are assumed to be
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{\kappa})
, satisfying
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{0}_{\kappa})=\mathbf{0}_{\kappa}
and
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{0}_{\kappa})=\mathbf{0}_{\kappa\times\mu}
. Note that binary systems of the form (
29
) fit this setting.
Given
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{\kappa\times\kappa}
,
Q\succeq 0
and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{\mu\times\mu}
,
R\succ 0
, we are interested in the infinite horizon optimal control problem

\underset{\mathbf{u}(\cdot)}{\min\,}\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{s}):=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{z}(t_{n})^{\top}Q\mathbf{z}(t_{n})\,+\,\mathbf{u}(t_{n})^{\top}R\mathbf{u}(t_{n})\,,

(31)

subject to the dynamics (
30
) with initial state
\mathbf{z}(t_{0})=\mathbf{s}
. We look for a solution
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
to (
31
) in feedback form, that is, an optimal control map
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}:\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mu}
which is expressed a function of the state,
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t_{n})=\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{z}(t_{n}))
. The computation of an optimal feedback law follows a dynamic programming argument, for which we define
V(\mathbf{s}):\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}\to\mathbb{R}
as the optimal cost-to-go departing from
\mathbf{s}
:

V(\mathbf{s})=\underset{\mathbf{u}(\cdot)}{\min}\;\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{s})\,,

(32)

where
V
satisfies the Bellman equation

V(\mathbf{s})=\underset{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mu}}{\textrm{min}}\bigg(\mathbf{s}^{\top}Q\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{u}^{\top}R\mathbf{u}+V\big(\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{s})+\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{s})\mathbf{u}\big)\bigg)\,,\qquad\text{for all }\;\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}\,.

(33)

From this point onwards, as we work globally in the state space,
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{s}
are treated indistinctly.

In order to better describe the difficulties related to the solution of this optimal control problem, we first focus on the linear quadratic case, where the optimal solution is computed via the discrete-time linear quadratic regulator (LQR). The optimality of the feedback law is ensured by a direct link between the LQR solution and the dynamic programming one, obtained from equation (
33
). In non-linear settings, the parallelism between the nonlinear QR and DP is broken, together with the optimality of the solution.

4.1
Discrete-time Linear Quadratic Regulator

The discrete time linear quadratic problem is a particular instance of the optimal control problem (
31
) when
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{s})=A\mathbf{s},\;A\in\mathbb{R}^{\kappa\times\kappa}
, and
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{s})=B\in\mathbb{R}^{\kappa\times\mu}
. Under these assumptions, we make the ansatz
V(\mathbf{s})=\mathbf{s}^{\top}\Pi\mathbf{s}
, so that the Bellman equation (
33
) becomes

\mathbf{s}^{\top}\Pi\,\mathbf{s}=\underset{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mu}}{\textrm{min}}\bigg(\mathbf{s}^{\top}Q\,\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{u}^{\top}R\,\mathbf{u}+(A\mathbf{s}+B\mathbf{u})^{\top}\Pi\,(A\mathbf{s}+B\mathbf{u})\bigg)\qquad\text{for all }\;\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}\,.

(34)

Solving the equation above leads to an optimal feedback of the form

\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{s})=-(R+B^{\top}\Pi\,B)^{-1}B^{\top}\Pi\,A\mathbf{s}\,,

(35)

where
\Pi\in\mathbb{R}^{\kappa\times\kappa}
is the unique positive definite solution of the discrete-time algebraic Riccati equation (DARE):

\Pi=Q+A^{\top}\Pi\,A-A^{\top}\Pi\,B(R+B^{\top}\Pi\,B)^{-1}B^{\top}\Pi\,A\,.

(36)

Remark 4.1

Consider a discrete first-order binary system as in (
29
) with constant interaction kernel
P(x,x_{*})=p
, with individual states
v,v_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, target
\tilde{v}=0
and
\varepsilon=\Delta t
. In this case, the optimal control problem (
31
) reads

\underset{\mathbf{u}(\cdot)}{\min}~\Delta t\sum\limits_{n=0}^{+\infty}\|v^{n}\|^{2}+\|v_{*}^{n}\|^{2}+\gamma\,(\|u^{n}\|^{2}+\|u_{*}^{n}\|^{2})

(37)

\text{s.t. }\;\begin{cases}v^{n+1}&=v^{n}\,+{\Delta t}\big(p(v_{*}^{n}-v^{n})+u^{n}\big)\\
v_{*}^{n+1}&=v_{*}^{n}+{\Delta t}\big(p(v^{n}-v_{*}^{n})+u_{*}^{n}\big)\,.\end{cases}

(38)

This formulation corresponds to a linear-quadratic control problem by setting
\mathbf{s}=(v,v_{*})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{u}=(u,u_{*})^{\top}
and

A=\mathbb{I}_{2d}+\Delta t\tilde{A}\,,\quad\tilde{A}=\begin{pmatrix}-p&p\\
p&-p\end{pmatrix}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d},\qquad B=\Delta t\mathbb{I}_{2d}\qquad Q=\Delta t\mathbb{I}_{2d},\qquad R=\Delta t\gamma\mathbb{I}_{2d}.

Similarly as in Theorem
2.1
, we are interested in the asymptotic limit for
\Delta t\to 0
, in which case (
36
) becomes

2\tilde{A}\Pi-\frac{1}{\gamma}\Pi^{2}+\mathbb{I}_{2d}=\mathbb{0}_{2d}.

(39)

Exploiting to the symmetric structure of the two agent interaction,
\Pi
can be reduced to diagonal and off-diagonal components, see e.g.
[
31
,
3
]
, obtaining the following

\Pi=\begin{pmatrix}\pi_{\textsc{d}}&\pi_{\textsc{o}}\\
\pi_{\textsc{o}}&\pi_{\textsc{d}}\end{pmatrix}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d}\,,\qquad\pi_{\textsc{d}}=\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}\left(1-2\sqrt{\gamma}p+\sqrt{1+4\gamma p^{2}}\right)\,,\quad\pi_{\textsc{o}}={\sqrt{\gamma}}-\pi_{\textsc{d}}\,.

(40)

The limiting optimal feedback law
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{s})=(\tilde{u},\tilde{u}_{*})
is given by

\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{s})=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\Pi\mathbf{s}=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\begin{pmatrix}\pi_{\textsc{d}}v+\pi_{\textsc{o}}v_{*}\\
\pi_{\textsc{o}}v+\pi_{\textsc{d}}v_{*}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(41)

from where it follows that the individual feedback laws are given by

\tilde{u}(v,v_{*})=-\frac{1}{\gamma}(\pi_{\textsc{d}}v+\pi_{\textsc{o}}v_{*})=\tilde{u}_{*}(v_{*},v),

Equivalently, notice that the discretized control computed in(
35
) in this case reads as follows

\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{\Delta t}(\mathbf{s})=-(\Delta t\gamma\mathbb{I}_{2d}+\Delta t^{2}\Pi)^{-1}\Delta t\Pi(\mathbb{I}_{2d}+\Delta t\tilde{A})\mathbf{s}

and in the limit for
\Delta t\to 0
we obtain exactly the same expression as in (
41
).

In the limiting kinetic equation this corresponds to the mean field control model

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=-\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(f\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}(p(v_{*}-v)-\frac{1}{\gamma}(\pi_{\textsc{d}}v+\pi_{\textsc{o}}v_{*}))f(t,x_{*},v_{*})\ dx_{*}\ dv_{*}\right)\,.

(42)

Notice that the structure of
~\Pi\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
in (
40
) implies isotropic action of the control in the
d
-dimensional state space.

4.2
Discrete-time Non-Linear Quadratic Regulator

It is feasible to apply LQR to a linearization of the system (
38
). However, if we want to compute a law accounting for nonlinearities, then we compromise the convenient connection between the DARE and the Bellman equation. Moreover, the computational cost required for the solution consistently increases.

In order to extend LQR to the nonlinear case, we begin by re-arranging the difference equation (
30
) in semilinear state-dependent form

\mathbf{z}(t_{n+1})=A(\mathbf{z}(t_{n}))\mathbf{z}(t_{n})+B(\mathbf{z}(t_{n}))\mathbf{u}(t_{n})\,,

(43)

where
\mathcal{A(\mathbf{z})}=A(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{z}
and
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{z})=B(\mathbf{z})
.
Note that this semi-linearization is not unique for systems of order greater than
1
. In what follows, we further assume pointwise controllability, i.e.
\forall\mathbf{z}\in\Omega
the pair
(A(\mathbf{z}),B(\mathbf{z}))
is controllable.

Similarly to the LQR design, the feedback control policy can be calculated as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{z}(t_{n}))

\displaystyle=-K(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{z}

(44)

\displaystyle K(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\big(B(\mathbf{z})^{\top}\Pi(\mathbf{z})B(\mathbf{z})+R\big)^{-1}B(\mathbf{z})^{\top}\Pi(\mathbf{z})A(\mathbf{z})

where the argument
\mathbf{z}
for the operators denotes dependency of the current state
\mathbf{z}(t_{n})
with discrete time scale
\Delta t
, and
\Pi(\mathbf{z})
is the solution of a DARE with state-dependent coefficients (DSDRE):

\Pi(\mathbf{z})=Q+A(\mathbf{z})^{\top}\Pi(\mathbf{z})\,A(\mathbf{z})-A(\mathbf{z})^{\top}\Pi(\mathbf{z})\,B(\mathbf{z})(R+B(\mathbf{z})^{\top}\Pi(\mathbf{z})\,B(\mathbf{z}))^{-1}B(\mathbf{z})^{\top}\Pi(\mathbf{z})\,A(\mathbf{z})\,.

(45)

As discussed in
[
65
]
, similarly to the continuous-time case, the discrete-time SDRE is derived by assuming a quadratic ansatz for the value function in the Bellman equation and by freezing the matrix
\Pi(\mathbf{z})
at the current state, thus neglecting its variation between time steps.
The state dependency of the feedback operator
K(\mathbf{z})=K(\mathbf{z}(t_{n}))
suggests the need for consecutive sequential solutions
\Pi(\mathbf{z})=\Pi(\mathbf{z}(t_{n}))
of (
45
) at every discrete time
t_{n}
along a trajectory. Thus, for high dimensions of the state space
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the exact solution of the DSDRE (
45
) comes at a cumbersome computational cost. Between the several numerical approaches that have been proposed to address this, we cite Taylor series method
[
9
]
and interpolation for
\Pi(\mathbf{z})

[
32
]
, while we refer the interested reader to
[
42
]
for a more exhaustive review. In what follows, we rely on a discrete version of the SDRE approach proposed in
[
32
]
.

4.3
Discrete-time SDRE approach

We aim at circumventing the computational challenge of solving (
45
) at each time step, by realizing the DSDRE feedback law in a model predictive control fashion: given the current state
\mathbf{z}(t_{n})
of the system, we assume the operator
\Pi(\mathbf{z})
to be a positive definite matrix in
\Pi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, meaning that (
45
) reduces to its algebraic form (
36
), where the state dependencies in (
45
) are neglected by accordingly freezing all the operators at the current configuration.
The resulting feedback variable leads to a suboptimal approximation of the controlled trajectory between
t_{n}
and
t_{n+1}
, after which the procedure is repeated by freezing the system at the updated state
\mathbf{z}(t_{n+1})
.

Algorithm 1

\mathbf{z}(0)\leftarrow\mathbf{s}
;
initial condition

for

do

\mathbf{s}\leftarrow\mathbf{z}(t_{n})
;

,
\;B\leftarrow B(\mathbf{s})
;
freeze the system accordingly

solve (
45
) for
{\Pi}
;

\mathbf{u}\leftarrow-(R+B^{\top}\Pi B)^{-1}B^{\top}\Pi A\mathbf{s}
;

;

\Delta t

The main computational bottleneck still persisting with this approach is the availability of a sufficiently fast solver for (
36
). Efficiency in retrieving the DARE solution is key in the settings under consideration. The consistency between the mean field and the kinetic dynamics only holds for
\varepsilon\ll 1
which in turn is associated with high frequency sampling, i.e. large numbers of couple of interacting particles in the Monte Carlo simulation. At each time step, we aim at conveniently approximate the controlled post-interaction states for a large number of paired agents, living in an arbitrarily high dimensional state space
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We address this task by means of supervised learning approximation, relying on models within the family of Artificial Neural Networks.

5
Neural networks and supervised learning approximation of feedback laws

The computational method proposed in this paper models the time evolution of the population density
f(t,x,v)
according to the Forward Euler scheme (
28
), where the updated density is to be approximated by the sampling distribution of
controlled
post-interaction states in a Monte Carlo fashion.

In this section, we provide an efficient approximating feedback map for the controlled binary dynamics via Feedforward and Recurrent Neural Networks (FNN/RNN). We briefly define these architectures and supervised learning framework for training
[
23
]
. After that, we will focus on the problem of interest, discussing synthetic data generation for feedback laws.

5.1
Feedforward Neural Networks

Figure 2

These architectures approximate general functions
\psi
through a sequence of layered transformations
l_{1},\ldots,l_{K}
, resulting in
\tilde{\psi}=\psi_{\theta}\approx\psi
where

\psi_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})=l_{K}\circ\ldots\circ l_{2}\circ l_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad l_{k}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma_{k}(\mathbf{W}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}+\mathbf{b}_{k}),

(46)

with information flowing from the input nodes to the output ones in a unidirectional path, avoiding any cycles or loops, as shown in Figure
2
. Each layer applies a nonlinear activation function
\sigma_{k}
element-wise to a linear transformation of its input
\mathbf{x}_{k}
. Assuming the
k
-th layer to have
n_{k}
neurons, the parameters
\mathbf{W}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k-1}\times n_{k}}
and
\mathbf{b}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
represent the weight matrix and bias vector for the
k
-th layer, respectively. We use the same activation function for all neurons within a given layer, with
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{K}
being the identity function.

In a supervised learning environment, the trainable parameters
\theta=\{\mathbf{W}_{k},\mathbf{b}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are then to be computed as minimizers of a suitable
loss function
measuring the approximation error within a set of sampled data (
training set
)
\mathcal{T}=\{\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\psi_{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{s}}
where
\psi_{(i)}:=\psi(\mathbf{x}_{(i)})
:

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=\underset{{\theta}}{\textrm{argmin}}\;\mathcal{L}(\psi,\psi_{{\theta}})\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\psi,\psi_{{\theta}})=\underset{\mathcal{T}}{\sum}l(\psi_{(i)},\psi_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)}))\,.

(47)

In particular, we consider

\mathcal{L}(\psi,\psi_{{\theta}})=MSE(\psi,\psi_{{\theta}})\,,\qquad l(\psi_{(i)},\psi_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)}))=\dfrac{\|\psi_{(i)}-\psi_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)})\|^{2}}{N_{s}}\,.

(48)

where we denoted by MSE the mean squared error as follows

MSE(\psi,\psi_{\theta})=\dfrac{1}{N_{t}}\underset{\mathcal{T}_{v}}{\sum}\dfrac{\|\psi_{(j)}-\psi_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{(j)})\|^{2}}{\|\psi_{(j)}\|^{2}}.

The number of layers
K
, the number of neurons per layer, the set of activation functions
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k=2}^{K-1}
in the hidden layers, and eventual other parameters represent further degrees of freedom defining the
architecture

\Theta
of the FNN. They are to be computed as result of the
hyper-parameter tuning phase
, which amounts to choose, between trained models
\psi_{\theta}[\Theta]
having different architectures, the one performing better to the error measure

\Theta^{*}=\underset{\tilde{\Theta}}{\textrm{argmin}}\;\sqrt{MSE(\psi,\psi_{\theta}[\Theta])},

(49)

where
\mathcal{T}_{v}=\{\mathbf{x}_{(j)},\psi_{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N_{v}}
is the

such that
\mathcal{T}\nsubseteq\mathcal{T}_{v}
.

5.2
Recurrent Neural Networks

Recurrent neural networks are a class of models which can be obtained from FNNs by allowing loop connections between layers. This architecture is particularly well-suited for processing sequential data. However, given that our input lacks temporal structure, we focus on
one-to-one

Here we extend FNNs into RNNs by allowing hidden layers to be one-to-one

(LSTM) cells which – for every input
\mathbf{x}
– generate output
h
according to the following system of equations

\begin{aligned} i&=\rho\big(W_{i}\,\mathbf{x}+b_{i}\big)\\

o&=\rho\big(W_{o}\,\mathbf{x}+b_{o}\big)\\
h&=o\odot\sigma\big(c\big)\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} &\text{\emph{input gate}}\\
&\text{\emph{candidate value}}\\
&\text{\emph{cell value}}\\
&\text{\emph{output gate}}\\
&\text{\emph{final output}}\end{aligned}

(50)

where (assuming
\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
), the weight matrices
W_{i},\,W_{c},\,W_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times n_{k+1}}
and the bias vectors
b_{i},\,b_{c},\,b_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k+1}}
are the trainable parameters,
\sigma(\cdot)
is the activation function, and
\rho(\cdot)
is the recurrent activation function. The number
\kappa
denotes the number of neurons in the cell. A visual representation of the flux of information through a LSTM cell can be found in Figure

The training and architecture selection for this kind of RNNs consists of the same procedure described in (

to output

, with parameters
\mathbf{W}_{k}=(W_{i},W_{c},W_{o})^{\top}
and
\mathbf{b}_{k}=(b_{i},b_{c},b_{o})^{\top}
.

5.3
Synthetic data generation

Both the training and hyper-parameter tuning phases for the neural network approximation rely on the availability of datasets containing input data points coupled with the corresponding target function evaluations. Since our goal is to approximate the feedback law for the reduced binary controlled dynamics, we consider two distinct approaches:

Control approximation
: The first approach treats the feedback control law
\mathbf{u}
, i.e. we build a network
\mathbf{u}_{\theta}(\mathbf{s},\Delta t)\approx\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{s},\Delta t)
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
is the optimal control obtained from the SDRE approach described in Section
4
.

State update approximation
: In this case, the neural network directly learns the mapping from current states to their controlled updates, i.e.,
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{\theta}(\mathbf{s},\Delta t)\approx\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{s})+\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{s})\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{s},\Delta t)
, bypassing the explicit computation of the control law.

The training loss for both approaches uses the mean squared error (MSE) as defined in equation (
47
). In Section
6
, we compare these two approaches in numerical tests, examining their precision and computational efficiency.

To create training data and establish a benchmark for precision, we solve the optimal control problem using the discrete-time SDRE approach. The difference equations for the binary system at the discrete time
t_{n}
hold as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x^{n+1}\,=x^{n}+\Delta t\,v^{n}\\
x_{*}^{n+1}\,=x_{*}^{n}+\Delta t\,v_{*}^{n}\end{cases}

(51)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}v^{n+1}\,=v^{n}\,+{\Delta t}\big[P(x^{n},x_{*}^{n})(v_{*}^{n}-v^{n})+u(x^{n},x_{*}^{n},v^{n},v_{*}^{n})\big]\\
v_{*}^{n+1}=v_{*}^{n}+{\Delta t}\big[P(x_{*}^{n},x^{n})(v^{n}-v_{*}^{n})+u_{*}(x^{n},x_{*}^{n},v^{n},v_{*}^{n})\big]\end{cases}

being
(x,v),(x_{*},v_{*})\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
the states for a couple of interacting agents.
We introduce the notation
\mathbf{s}=(x,x_{*},v,v_{*})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4d}
,
\mathbf{u}=(u,u_{*})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, for which the difference equation reads as in (
30
)

\mathbf{s}^{n+1}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{s}^{n})+\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{s}^{n})\mathbf{u}^{n}\,,

(52)

for
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{s})=(\mathbb{I}_{4d}+\Delta t\,G(\mathbf{s}))\mathbf{s}
and
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{s})=\Delta t\,H(\mathbf{s})
, with
G
and
H
defined as in (
18
).
From this, comes the
\Delta t
-dependency of the feedback law
\mathbf{u}
: the DSDRE parameters
\mathcal{A},\,\mathcal{B}
are dependent of the time-step, as will be the DARE solution associated to the system frozen at the current configuration.

Remark 5.1

As the controlled density evolution is carried by a Monte Carlo type method, the computational efficiency of evaluating a batch of controlled particle interactions is crucial. Our choice of control/state architectures is motivated by its favorable computational complexity. The evaluation cost of a single point through FNNs is

\|\mathbf{v}-\bar{v}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{v}-M\mathbf{v}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}-M\mathbf{v}\|^{2}

Conclusions

We have developed a novel computational method for mitigating the curse of dimensionality arising in the optimal control of large-scale, high-dimensional, agent-based models. The key ingredients of the proposed approach are: the use of a kinetic model to reduce the complexity associated to the particle ensemble to the sampling of two-agent subproblems, and the synthesis of control laws for the two-agent system by means of deep neural networks, supervised learning, and a discrete-time state-dependent Riccati equation approach.

Our numerical experiments validate the effectiveness of the approach in controlling consensus and attraction-repulsion dynamics in high-dimensional interacting particle systems. The use of neural network approximation models for fast feedback synthesis allows for a speedup of 2 to 3 orders of magnitude compared to solving a binary optimal control problem every time two interacting agents are sampled.

The proposed framework can be extended to construct a deep neural network that directly predicts the post-interaction states, or to different control problems, provided that a solver for synthetic data generation is available; moreover, the same approach can be applied to other types of dynamics, as long as they can be simulated or approximated through suitable numerical methods

Potential future research directions include the generalization of the proposed approach to the
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
robust control framework, which could further enhance the applicability of the method to real-world scenarios where robustness to uncertainties in the agents’ interaction forces is crucial. Additionally, exploring the integration of adaptive time-stepping techniques with the neural network approximation models could lead to faster and more accurate simulations of the controlled agent-based system. Finally, investigating the scalability of the proposed approach to even higher-dimensional problems and larger agent populations could enable applications in consensus-based optimization and mean field limits of neural networks.
