Hereditary completeness of Exponential systems { e λ n ​ t } n = 1 ∞ \{e^{\lambda_{n}t}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} in their closed span in L 2 ​ ( a , b ) L^{2}(a,b) and Spectral Synthesis
Elias Zikkos, Gajath Gunatillake

Abstract
Suppose that { λ n } n = 1 ∞ \{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} is a sequence of distinct positive real numbers satisfying the conditions inf { λ n + 1 − λ n } > 0 , \{\lambda_{n+1}-\lambda_{n}\}>0, and ∑ n = 1 ∞ λ n − 1 < ∞ . \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{-1}<\infty. We prove that the exponential system { e λ n ​ t } n = 1 ∞ \{e^{\lambda_{n}t}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} is hereditarily complete in the closure of the subspace spanned by { e λ n ​ t } n = 1 ∞ \{e^{\lambda_{n}t}\}_{n=1}^{\infty} in the space L 2 ​ ( a , b ) L^{2}(a,b) . We also give an example of a class of compact non-normal operators defined on this closure which admit spectral synthesis.



Mathematics Subject Classification: 30B60, 30B50, 47A10, 46C05.

Keywords: Müntz-Szász theorem, Exponential Systems, Closed Span, Hereditary completeness, Spectral Synthesis, Compact Operators.

1
Introduction

Let
(a,b)
be a bounded interval on the real line and let
L^{2}(a,b)

be the space of square integrable complex valued functions on
(a,b)
,
equipped with the norm

||f||_{L^{2}(a,b)}:=\left(\int_{a}^{b}|f(x)|^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and endowed with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle:=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\overline{g(x)}\,dx.

Let
\Lambda
denote the sequence of distinct positive real numbers
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}.
Assume that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}<\infty,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda_{n+1}-\lambda_{n}\geq c,

(1.1)

for some positive constant
c
and all
n.

Consider the associated exponential system
\{e^{\lambda_{n}t}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\Lambda
.
We will denote this system by
E_{\Lambda}
.
Let
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
denote the closed span of
E_{\Lambda}
in
L^{2}(a,b).

Since the series
\sum\lambda_{n}^{-1}
converges, it follows from the Müntz-Szász theorem that

E_{\Lambda}
is
not\,\,complete
in
L^{2}(a,b)
, that is,
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
is a proper subspace of
L^{2}(a,b)

(see
[
7
, Theorem 6.1]
,
[
4
, Theorem 4.2.6]
, and
[
6
]
).
Moreover,
E_{\Lambda}
is a
minimal
system, that is, each function
e^{\lambda_{n}t}

does not belong to the closed span of the remaining vectors of
E_{\Lambda}
in
L^{2}(a,b)

(see
[
7
, relation (1.9)]
).

It is known that a family
\{f_{n}\}
of functions in a separable Hilbert space
\cal{H}
is minimal, if and only if
it has a
biorthogonal
sequence in
\cal{H}
(see
[
5
, Lemma 3.3.1]
): in other words,
there exists a sequence
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\cal{H}
so that

\langle g_{n},f_{m}\rangle=\begin{cases}1,&m=n,\\
0,&m\not=n.\end{cases}

We note that a system in
\cal H
which is both complete and minimal is called
exact
. An exact system has a unique biorthogonal family
in
\cal H
, however this family is not necessarily complete in
\cal H
(see
[
10
]
).

Clearly now the system
E_{\Lambda}
has a unique biorthogonal family

\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
, assuming relation
(\ref{LKcondition})
.
We will show that this family is complete in
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
, thus
the system
E_{\Lambda}
is a
Markushevich\,\,basis
in
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
. In fact, we will prove that

E_{\Lambda}
is a
strong\,\,Markushevich\,\,basis
in
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
(Theorem
1.1
).
This means that
for any disjoint union of two sets
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
, such that

\mathbb{N}=N_{1}\cup N_{2}
, the closed span of the mixed system

\{e^{\lambda_{n}t}:\,\,n\in N_{1}\}\cup\{g_{n}:\,\,n\in N_{2}\}

in
L^{2}(a,b)
is equal to
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
. We point out that the phrase “strong Markushevich basis”
is used interchangeably with the phrase
hereditarily\,\,complete\,\,system
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let the sequence
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfy the condition
(\ref{LKcondition})
.
Then the exponential system
E_{\Lambda}
is
\bf{hereditarily\,\,complete}
in the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
.

Our work has been inspired by
[
1
]
where it was proved that a complete and minimal exponential system

\{e^{i\lambda_{n}t}:\,\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
in
L^{2}(-a,a)
is hereditarily complete up to one-dimensional effect
(
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
). That is, if
\{g_{n}:\,\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is the unique biorthogonal family
to the system
\{e^{i\lambda_{n}t}:\,\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
in
L^{2}(-a,a)
, then
for any disjoint union of two sets
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
, such that

\mathbb{N}=N_{1}\cup N_{2}
, the orthogonal complement in
L^{2}(a,b)
to the closed span of the mixed system

\{e^{i\lambda_{n}t}:\,\,n\in N_{1}\}\cup\{g_{n}:\,\,n\in N_{2}\},

is at most one dimensional.
The latter phenomenon is possible since
there exists an exact exponential system
\{e^{i\lambda_{n}t}\}
in
L^{2}(-\pi,\pi)
which is not hereditarily complete
(
[
1
, Theorem 1.3]
).

Remark 1.1
.

We point out that our exponential system
E_{\Lambda}
is not complete in
L^{2}(a,b)
but it is hereditarily complete in
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
.

The notion of hereditary completeness is closely related to the
Spectral\,\,Synthesis
problem for linear operators

[
8
,
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
We say that a bounded linear operator
T
in a separable Hilbert space
H
having a set of eigenvectors which is complete in
H
,
admits
Spectral\,\,Synthesis
if for any invariant subspace
A
of
T
, the set of eigenvectors of
T
contained in
A

is complete in
A
.
It follows by the results obtained by J. Wermer in
[
9
]
that an operator which is
\bf both
compact and normal
admits spectral synthesis. However, if one of the two conditions does not hold, then the conclusion might not be true.
In particular, Wermer gave an example of a bounded normal operator which does not admit spectral synthesis
[
9
, Theorem 2]
, while
A. S. Markus, amongst others, proved that there exist compact operators which do not admit spectral synthesis
(see
[
8
, Theorem 4.2]
).

Markus
[
8
, Theorem 4.1]
also showed that a compact operator with a trivial kernel and non-zero simple eigenvalues with corresponding eigenvectors
\{f_{n}\}
, admits spectral synthesis if and only if the family
\{f_{n}\}
is hereditarily complete in
\cal H
.
Motivated by this result, and since our system
E_{\Lambda}
is hereditarily complete in
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
,
we present in Section 4 a class of compact but not normal operators on
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
which admit spectral synthesis.

2
Properties of the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)

Subject to the condition
(\ref{LKcondition})
,
Luxemburg and Korevaar proved in
[
7
, Theorem 8.2]
that a function

f
belongs to the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
if and only if

f(x)=g(x)
almost everywhere on
(a,b)
, where
g
is an analytic function in the half plane
\Re z<b
,
admitting the Dirichlet series representation

g(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}e^{\lambda_{n}z}\qquad a_{n}\in\mathbb{C},\quad\forall\,\,z\in\Re z<b,

with the series converging uniformly on compact subsets of the half plane
\Re z<b
.

Let us rewrite the above result using another notation.
Consider the space of square integrable functions on
(a,b)

that admit a Dirichlet series representation

f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}e^{\lambda_{n}z}

in the half-plane
\Re z<b
, converging uniformly on compacta.
Denote this space by
L^{2}(a,b,E_{\Lambda})
, that is

L^{2}(a,b,E_{\Lambda}):=\left\{f\in L^{2}(a,b):\,\,f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}e^{\lambda_{n}z}\quad\forall\,\,z\in\Re z<b\right\}.

The Luxemburg-Korevaar result is now restated as follows.

Theorem A
.

Let
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of numbers satisfying the condition
(\ref{LKcondition})
.
Then

{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)=L^{2}(a,b,E_{\Lambda}).

The above result depends heavily on deriving the distance lower bound
(\ref{distanceresult})
:
let
e_{n}(x):=e^{\lambda_{n}x}
and let
E_{\Lambda_{n}}:=E_{\Lambda}\setminus\{e_{n}\}
,
that is
E_{\Lambda_{n}}
is the system
E_{\Lambda}
excluding the function
e^{\lambda_{n}x}
.
Denote by
D_{n}
the
\bf Distance
between
e_{n}

and the closed span of
E_{\Lambda_{n}}
in
L^{2}(a,b)
,

D_{n}:=\inf_{g\in\overline{\text{span}}(E_{\Lambda_{n}})}||e_{n}-g||_{L^{2}(a,b)}.

It was shown (
[
7
, relation (1.9)]
) that for every
\epsilon>0
, there is a positive constant
m_{\epsilon}

which does not depend on
n\in\mathbb{N}
, but only on
\Lambda
and the segment
(a,b)
, so that

D_{n}\geq m_{\epsilon}\cdot e^{(b-\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}.

(2.1)

3
Hereditary Completeness: Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section we prove our hereditary completeness result by first obtaining the Fourier type series representation

(\ref{representationf})
for functions in
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let the sequence
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfy the condition
(\ref{LKcondition})
and
consider the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
. Then the following hold.

(I) There exists a family of functions

r_{\Lambda}=\{r_{n}:\,\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)

so that it is the unique biorthogonal sequence to
E_{\Lambda}
in
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
.
For every
\epsilon>0
there is a constant
m_{\epsilon}>0
, independent of
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so that

||r_{n}||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\leq m_{\epsilon}e^{(-b+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}},\qquad\forall\,\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.1)

(II)
For each
f
in
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)

there exists a function
g
analytic in the half-plane
\Re z<b
,
so that

f(x)=g(x)\qquad\text{almost everywhere on}\quad(a,b),

with
g
admitting the Fourier-type Dirichlet series representation

g(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle f,r_{n}\rangle\cdot e^{\lambda_{n}z},

(3.2)

converging uniformly on compact subsets of the half-plane
\Re z<b
.

Proof.

(I)
Recall the distance result
(\ref{distanceresult})
and the system
E_{\Lambda_{n}}
.
Now, since
L^{2}(a,b)
is a Hilbert space, it then follows that there exists a unique element
in
\overline{\text{span}}(E_{\Lambda_{n}})
in
L^{2}(a,b)
,that we denote by
\Phi_{n}
,
so that

||e_{n}-\Phi_{n}||_{L^{2}(a,b)}=\inf_{g\in\overline{\text{span}}(E_{\Lambda_{n}})}||e_{n}-g||_{L^{2}(a,b)}=D_{n}.

The function
e_{n}-\Phi_{n}
is orthogonal to all the elements of the closed span of
E_{\Lambda_{n}}

in
L^{2}(a,b)
, hence to
\Phi_{n}
itself.
Therefore

\langle e_{n}-\Phi_{n},e_{n}-\Phi_{n}\rangle=\langle e_{n}-\Phi_{n},e_{n}\rangle.

Hence

(D_{n})^{2}=\langle e_{n}-\Phi_{n},e_{n}\rangle.

Next, we define

r_{n}(x):=\frac{e_{n}(x)-\Phi_{n}(x)}{(D_{n})^{2}},\qquad\text{thus}\qquad||r_{n}||_{L^{2}(a,b)}=\frac{1}{D_{n}}.

It then follows that
\langle r_{n},e_{n}\rangle=1
and
r_{n}
is orthogonal to all the
elements of the system
E_{\Lambda_{n}}
.
Thus
\{r_{n}:\,\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is biorthogonal to the system
E_{\Lambda}
.
Since
\Phi_{n}\in\overline{\text{span}}(E_{\Lambda_{n}})
in
L^{2}(a,b)
it follows that

r_{n}\in\overline{\text{span}}(E_{\Lambda})
in
L^{2}(a,b)
.
Moreover, since
||r_{n}||_{L^{2}(a,b)}=1/D_{n}
, then from
(\ref{distanceresult})
we obtain
(\ref{rnkbound})
.

(II)
From Theorem
A
we know that if
f
belongs to
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
then
f

is extended analytically to the half-plane
\Re z<b
, admitting the series representation

f(z)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}c_{m}e^{\lambda_{m}z}
for some coefficients
c_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
, converging uniformly on compacta.
We will show below that

c_{n}=\langle f,r_{n}\rangle\qquad\text{for\,\, all}\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Fix some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\displaystyle\langle f,r_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\overline{r_{n}(x)}\cdot\left(\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}c_{m}e^{\lambda_{m}x}\right)\,dx+\int_{a}^{b}\overline{r_{n}(x)}\cdot\left(\sum_{m=1}^{n}c_{m}e^{\lambda_{m}x}\right)\,dx

(3.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\overline{r_{n}(x)}\cdot\left(\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}c_{m}e^{\lambda_{m}x}\right)\,dx+c_{n}

with the last step valid due to the biorthogonality.

We claim that

\int_{a}^{b}\overline{r_{n}(x)}\cdot\left(\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}c_{m}e^{\lambda_{m}x}\right)\,dx=0.

(3.4)

Indeed, since
f\in L^{2}(a,b)
, then the Dirichlet series

Q_{n}(z):=f(z)-\sum_{m=1}^{n}c_{m}e^{\lambda_{m}z}=\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}c_{m}e^{\lambda_{m}z}

also belongs to
L^{2}(a,b)
. It then follows from Theorem
A
that
Q_{n}
belongs
to the closed span of the exponential system

E_{\Lambda(n+1)}:=E_{\Lambda}\setminus\{e^{\lambda_{m}x}:\,m=1,2,\dots,n\}=\{e^{\lambda_{m}x}:\,m\geq n+1\}

in
L^{2}(a,b)
. Thus, for every
\epsilon>0
, there is a function
f_{\epsilon}
in the span of
E_{\Lambda(n+1)}

so that
||Q_{n}-f_{\epsilon}||_{L^{2}(a,b)}<\epsilon
. From biorthogonality we have

\int_{a}^{b}\overline{r_{n}(x)}\cdot f_{\epsilon}(x)\,dx=0.

Combining this with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{a}^{b}\overline{r_{n}(x)}\cdot Q_{n}(x)\,dx\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{a}^{b}\overline{r_{n}(x)}\cdot(Q_{n}(x)-f_{\epsilon}(x))\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon\cdot||r_{n}||_{L^{2}(a,b)}.

The arbitrary choice of
\epsilon
implies that
(\ref{zerozero1})
is true.

Finally, replacing
(\ref{zerozero1})
in
(\ref{cnk})

shows that
\langle f,r_{n}\rangle=c_{n}
, that is
(\ref{representationf})
holds.

∎

3.1
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Let us write the set
\mathbb{N}
as an arbitrary disjoint union of two sets
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
.
In order to obtain the hereditary completeness of
E_{\Lambda}
in
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
,
we must show that the closed span of the mixed system

E_{1,2}:=\{e_{n}:\,\,n\in N_{1}\}\cup\{r_{n}:\,\,n\in N_{2}\},

in
L^{2}(a,b)
is equal to
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
.

Denote by
W_{\Lambda_{1,2}}
the closed span of
E_{1,2}
in
L^{2}(a,b)
.
Obviously
W_{\Lambda_{1,2}}
is a subspace of
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
.
Let
W^{\perp}_{\Lambda_{1,2}}
be the orthogonal complement of
W_{\Lambda_{1,2}}
in
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
, that is

W^{\perp}_{\Lambda_{1,2}}=\{f\in{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b):\,\,\langle f,g\rangle=0\quad\text{for\,\, all}\,\,g\in W_{\Lambda_{1,2}}\}.

Now, if
f\in W^{\perp}_{\Lambda_{1,2}}\subset{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
then by
(\ref{representationf})
we have

f(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle f,r_{n}\rangle\cdot e^{\lambda_{n}t},\quad\text{almost everywhere on}\,\,(a,b)

and of course
\langle f,r_{n}\rangle=0
for all
n\in N_{2}
.
Thus,

f(t)=\sum_{n\in N_{1}}\langle f,r_{n}\rangle\cdot e^{\lambda_{n}t},\quad\text{almost everywhere on}\,\,(a,b).

Since the above Dirichlet series
f
is analytic in the half-plane
\Re z<b

and belongs to
L^{2}(a,b)
, it follows from Theorem
A
that
f
belongs to the closed span of the exponential system

E_{\Lambda,N_{1}}:=\{e_{n}:\,\,n\in N_{1}\}

in
L^{2}(a,b)
. Hence, for every
\epsilon>0
there is a function
g_{\epsilon}
in
\text{span}(E_{\Lambda,N_{1}})
so that

||f-g_{\epsilon}||_{L^{2}(a,b)}<\epsilon
. Write

\langle f,f\rangle=\langle f,f-g_{\epsilon}\rangle+\langle f,g_{\epsilon}\rangle.

Since
f\in W^{\perp}_{\Lambda_{1,2}}
then
\langle f,e_{n}\rangle=0
for all
n\in N_{1}
, thus

\langle f,g_{\epsilon}\rangle=0
. Therefore,

||f||^{2}_{L^{2}(\gamma,\beta)}=\langle f,f\rangle=\langle f,f-g_{\epsilon}\rangle\leq||f||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\cdot||f-g_{\epsilon}||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\leq||f||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\cdot\epsilon.

Hence

||f||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\leq\epsilon.

Clearly this means that
f(x)=0
almost everywhere on
(a,b)
, hence
W^{\perp}_{\Lambda_{1,2}}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
Thus
W_{\Lambda_{1,2}}={\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
, meaning that the exponential system
E_{\Lambda}

is hereditarily complete in the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
.
The proof of Theorem
1.1
is now complete.

4
Compact operators on
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
admitting Spectral Synthesis

Our goal in this section is to present a class of compact but not normal operators on
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
that admit Spectral Synthesis.
The operators constructed will have the properties presented below, a result obtained by Markus.

Theorem B
.

[
8
, Theorem 4.1]

Let
T:\cal{H}
\to\cal{H}
be a compact operator such that

(i) its kernel is trivial and

(ii) its non-zero eigenvalues are
\bf simple
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the corresponding sequence of eigenvectors.
Then
T
admits
\bf{Spectral\,\,Synthesis}
if and only if
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\bf Hereditarily\,\,complete
in
\cal{H}
.

4.1
A class of operators on
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)

Let
f
be a function in the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
. Then from Lemma
3.1
we have

f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle f,r_{n}\rangle\cdot e_{n}(z),

with the series converging uniformly on compact subsets of the half-plane
\Re z<b
.
From relation
(\ref{rnkbound})
, for every
\epsilon>0
there exists some
m_{\epsilon}>0
, independent of
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so that

|\langle f,r_{n}\rangle|\leq||f||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\cdot m_{\epsilon}e^{(-b+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}.

Let
\delta>0
and choose a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of distinct non-zero complex numbers such that

|u_{n}|\leq e^{-\delta\lambda_{n}}.

(4.1)

Clearly
u_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Example 4.1
.

For
\delta>0
, we may take
u_{n}=e^{-\delta\lambda_{n}}
.

It follows from above that for every
0<\epsilon<\delta
,
there exists some
m_{\epsilon}>0
, independent of
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
, so that

|\langle f,r_{n}\rangle\cdot u_{n}|\leq||f||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\cdot m_{\epsilon}e^{(-b-\delta+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}.

(4.2)

Now, define

T(f(z)):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle f,r_{n}\rangle\cdot u_{n}\cdot e^{\lambda_{n}z}.

(4.3)

One deduces from
(\ref{upperbound})
that
T(f(z))

is a function analytic in the half-plane
\Re z<b+\delta
, converging uniformly on compact subsets of this half-plane, thus on
the interval
[a,b]
as well. The uniform convergence on
[a,b]
implies that

(i)

T(f(z))
belongs to the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
.

(ii)
The series
T(f)
converges in the
L^{2}(a,b)
norm.

Remark 4.1
.

It also follows from
(\ref{upperbound})-(\ref{operator})
that there exists some
N>0
so that

||T(f)||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\leq N||f||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\quad\text{for\,\, all}\quad f\in{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b).

Therefore,
T:{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)\to{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
defines a Bounded Linear Operator.
We denote by
T^{*}
its Adjoint operator.

We will show that

Theorem 4.1
.

The operator
T:{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)\to{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
defined in
(\ref{operator})
is compact, not normal, and admits spectral synthesis.

Proof.

Our result follows from Lemmas
\ref{compactoperator}-\ref{notnormal}
, Theorem
B
, and the fact that the system
E_{\Lambda}
is hereditarily complete in
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
.
∎

We remark that if we choose
u_{n}=e^{-\delta\lambda_{n}}
as in Example
4.1
, then
T(f(z))
is equal to the translation
f(z-\delta)
,
which is well defined on
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
since any function in this space extends analytically to the half-plane
\Re z<b
. Thus we have

Corollary 4.1
.

For any
\delta>0
, the shift operator
T_{\delta}(f):=f(z-\delta)
defined on
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)

is compact, not normal, and admits spectral synthesis.

4.2
Auxiliary results: Lemmas
4.1
,
4.2
,
4.3
, and
4.4

In the following four lemmas, we prove that the operator
T
is compact, we obtain various properties of
T

as those stated in Theorem
B
, and we show that
T
is not normal.
Finally, a Fourier type series representation is derived for the adjoint
T^{*}
.

If
L
is a bounded operator on
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
, we use
\|L\|
to
denote the operator norm of
L
which is the supremum of the set

\{\|L(f)\|_{L^{2}(a,b)}:f\in{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b),\|f\|_{L^{2}(a,b)}=1\}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The operator
T
is compact.

Proof.

Let
e_{n}(x)=e^{\lambda_{n}x}.

Define
T_{m}
on
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
by

T_{m}(g)(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{m}\langle g,r_{n}\rangle u_{n}e_{n}(x).

Let
f
be a unit vector in
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
. It is easy to see that

\|(T-T_{m})(f)\|_{L^{2}(a,b)}\leq\sum_{n=m+1}^{\infty}\|\langle f,r_{n}\rangle u_{n}e_{n}\|_{L^{2}(a,b)}.

We have that
\|\langle f,r_{n}\rangle u_{n}e_{n}\|_{L^{2}(a,b)}\leq m_{\epsilon}e^{(-b-\delta+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}\|e_{n}\|_{L^{2}(a,b)}.

It is easy to see that
\|e_{n}\|_{L^{2}(a,b)}<e^{\lambda_{n}b}.

Thus,
\|\langle f,r_{n}\rangle u_{n}e_{n}\|_{L^{2}(a,b)}\leq m_{\epsilon}e^{(-\delta+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}.
Therefore,

\|(T-T_{m})(f)\|_{L^{2}(a,b)}\leq m_{\epsilon}\sum_{n=m+1}^{\infty}e^{(-\delta+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}.

Since
\delta>\epsilon,
the series above converges. Hence,
\|T-T_{m}\|
tends to zero as
m
tends to infinity.
Thus, the finite rank operators
\{T_{m}\}
converge to
T
in the uniform operator topology. Therefore,

T
is compact.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

The following are true about the operator
T
and its adjoint.

1.

\{u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are eigenvalues of
T
and
\{e_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are the corresponding eigenvectors.

2.

\{\overline{u_{k}}\}
are eigenvalues of
T^{*}
and
\{r_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are the corresponding eigenvectors.

3.

The kernel of
T
is trivial.

4.

The spectrum of
T
is
\displaystyle{\{0\}\cup\{u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}}
.

5.

Each eigenvalue of
T
is simple.

Proof.

1. Due to the biorthogonal relation between
E_{\Lambda}
and
r_{\Lambda}
, we see in
(\ref{operator})
that

T(e_{k})=u_{k}e_{k}.

2. For fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\langle T^{*}r_{k}-\overline{u_{k}}r_{k},e_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\overline{u_{k}}\langle r_{k},e_{n}\rangle+\langle r_{k},Te_{n}\rangle

(4.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\overline{u_{k}}\langle r_{k},e_{n}\rangle+\langle r_{k},u_{n}e_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\overline{u_{k}}\langle r_{k},e_{n}\rangle+\overline{u_{n}}\langle r_{k},e_{n}\rangle.

If
n=k
then
(\ref{zero})
equals zero, and the same holds for all
n\not=k

due to the biorthogonal relation between
r_{\Lambda}
and
E_{\Lambda}
.
Thus
\langle T^{*}r_{k}-\overline{u_{k}}r_{k},e_{n}\rangle=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, hence

T^{*}r_{k}-\overline{u_{k}}\cdot r_{k}=\mathbf{0}.

3. We now show that the kernel of
T
is the zero set.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\langle Tf,r_{n}\rangle=\langle f,T^{*}r_{n}\rangle=\langle f,\overline{u_{n}}r_{n}\rangle.

If
Tf=0
then one has
\langle f,r_{n}\rangle=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since the family
\{r_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

is complete in the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
, then
f=\mathbf{0}
. Hence the kernel of
T
is the zero function.

4. Next we prove that
\{u_{k}\}
are the only non-zero eigenvalues.
Suppose that

Tf=\lambda f
for some
\lambda\notin\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
f\not=\mathbf{0}
. Then,

\displaystyle\lambda\langle f,r_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Tf,r_{n}\rangle,

\displaystyle=\langle f,T^{*}r_{n}\rangle,

\displaystyle=\langle f,\overline{u_{n}}r_{n}\rangle,

\displaystyle=u_{n}\langle f,r_{n}\rangle.

(4.5)

Thus,

(\lambda-u_{n})\cdot\langle f,r_{n}\rangle=0

for all
n.
But
\lambda\notin\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
therefore
\langle f,r_{n}\rangle=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since the family
\{r_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is complete in the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
, then
f=\mathbf{0}
.
This contradiction shows that
\{u_{k}\}
are the only non-zero eigenvalues.
Since
T
is compact it follows that the spectrum of
T
is

\displaystyle{\{0\}\cup\{u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}}.

5. Next we prove that each eigenvalue of
T
is simple.
Suppose that
Tf=u_{k}f
for some
u_{k}\in\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and some
f\in{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
.
Then, using the same computation in (
4.5
) above, we get

(u_{k}-u_{n})\langle f,r_{n}\rangle=0\qquad

for all
n.
But if
n\not=k,
then
u_{n}\not=u_{k}
, thus,
\langle f,r_{n}\rangle=0
for all
n\not=k
. Since

f(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle f,r_{n}\rangle\cdot e_{n}(t),

then we get
f(t)=\langle f,r_{k}\rangle e_{k}
. But this means that
u_{k}
is simple.

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

The operator
T
is not normal.

Proof.

Suppose that
T
is a normal operator, thus

TT^{*}(e_{k})=T^{*}T(e_{k})\qquad\text{for\,\,all}\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

But any two eigenvectors that correspond to different eigenvalues of a normal operator are orthogonal.
Clearly this is not the case here for the set of eigenvectors
e_{n}=e^{\lambda_{n}t}
of
T
.
∎

And finally, biorthogonality of
\{e_{n}\}
and
\{r_{n}\}
allows us to compute
T^{*}
explicitly.

Lemma 4.4
.

The adjoint operator
T^{*}
of
T
admits the representation

T^{*}f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle f,e_{n}\rangle\cdot\overline{u_{n}}\cdot r_{n}(z),

(4.6)

with the series converging uniformly on compact susbets of the half-plane
\Re z<b
. The series also converges in the

L^{2}(a,b)
norm.

Proof.

Given
f\in{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
,

(I) We first prove that the series

g_{f}(z):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle f,e_{n}\rangle\cdot\overline{u_{n}}\cdot r_{n}(z)

(4.7)

converges uniformly on compact susbets of the half-plane
\Re z<b
.

(II)
We then show that
g_{f}
belongs to the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
and
g_{f}
converges in the

L^{2}(a,b)
norm.

(III)
And finally we prove that

\langle Th,f\rangle=\langle h,g_{f}\rangle\qquad\text{for\,\, all}\quad h\in{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b).

(I)
Consider first an arbitrary compact subset
K
of the half plane
\Re z<b
,
such that
\Re z\leq A
for all
z\in K
for some
A<b
. From the Fourier-type Dirichlet series representation

(\ref{representationf})
, we have

r_{n}(z)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\langle r_{n},r_{m}\rangle e^{\lambda_{m}z}

with the series converging uniformly on
K
. Also, from
(\ref{rnkbound})
,
for every
\epsilon>0
there exists a positive constant
M_{\epsilon}
so that

|\langle r_{n},r_{m}\rangle|\leq M_{\epsilon}\cdot e^{(-b+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}\cdot e^{(-b+\epsilon)\lambda_{m}}.

Take
0<\epsilon<b-A
thus
-b+\epsilon+A<0
. Then

|r_{n}(z)|\leq M_{\epsilon}\cdot e^{(-b+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}\cdot\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}e^{(-b+\epsilon)\lambda_{m}}\cdot e^{A\lambda_{m}}\quad\text{for\,\, all}\quad z\in K.

Since
-b+\epsilon+A<0
, this means that there is some positive constant
P_{K}
which depends on the set
K
,
and not on
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so that

|r_{n}(z)|\leq P_{K}\cdot M_{\epsilon}\cdot e^{(-b+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}\quad\text{for\,\, all}\quad z\in K.

Now, from
(\ref{un})
, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and since
e_{n}(t)=e^{\lambda_{n}t}
, one gets

|\langle f,e_{n}\rangle\cdot\overline{u_{n}}|\leq||f||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\cdot e^{\lambda_{n}b}\cdot e^{-\delta\lambda_{n}}.

Combining the above bounds shows that

|\langle f,e_{n}\rangle\cdot\overline{u_{n}}\cdot r_{n}(z)|\leq P_{K}\cdot M_{\epsilon}\cdot||f||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\cdot e^{(-\delta+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}\quad\text{for\,\, all}\quad z\in K.

Since
\epsilon<\delta
, the above shows that the series
g_{f}

(\ref{seriesgf})
converges uniformly on
K
.

(II)
Next, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
let

V_{n}(z):=\langle f,e_{n}\rangle\cdot\overline{u_{n}}\cdot r_{n}(z),

thus

g_{f}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}V_{n}(z).

It then follows from relations
(\ref{rnkbound})
and
(\ref{un})
, that for every
\epsilon\in(0,\delta)
,
there is a positive constant
M_{\epsilon}
, so that

\displaystyle||V_{n}(t)||_{L^{2}(a,b)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\langle f,e_{n}\rangle|\cdot|u_{n}|\cdot||r_{n}||_{L^{2}(a,b)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{\epsilon}\cdot||f||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\cdot e^{\lambda_{n}b}\cdot e^{-\delta\lambda_{n}}\cdot e^{(-b+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}.

Thus

||V_{n}(t)||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\leq M_{\epsilon}\cdot||f||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\cdot e^{(-\delta+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}.

Since
\epsilon<\delta
then

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}||V_{n}(t)||_{L^{2}(a,b)}<\infty.

(4.8)

Consider now the function

H(t):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|V_{n}(t)|=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\langle f,e_{n}\rangle\cdot\overline{u_{n}}|\cdot|r_{n}(t)|\qquad\forall\,\,t\in(a,b).

(4.9)

From
(\ref{Vnt})
and the Minkowski inequality, we see that
H
belongs to the space
L^{2}(a,b)
.
Clearly
|g_{f}(t)|\leq H(t)
for all
t\in(a,b)
, thus
g_{f}
belongs to
L^{2}(a,b)
as well.

We also claim that
g_{f}
converges in the
L^{2}(a,b)
norm. Indeed, we have

\left|\left(g_{f}(t)-\sum_{n=1}^{M}V_{n}(t)\right)\right|^{2}=\left|\left(\sum_{n=M+1}^{\infty}V_{n}(t)\right)\right|^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{n=M+1}^{\infty}|V_{n}(t)|\right)^{2}\leq H^{2}(t).

Moreover,
|g_{f}(t)-\sum_{n=1}^{M}V_{n}(t)|^{2}\to 0
as
M\to\infty

for all
t\in(a,b)
.
Then by the Lebesgue Convergence Theorem we have

||g_{f}-\sum_{n=1}^{M}V_{n}||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\to 0\qquad\text{as}\qquad M\to\infty.

(4.10)

Finally, it is clear that each
V_{n}
belongs to the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
.
Together with
(\ref{tozeroU})
yields that
g_{f}
also belongs to the space
{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
.

(III)
Next, let
h\in{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
. It follows from
(\ref{rnkbound})
and
(\ref{un})
that

|\overline{f(t)}\cdot\langle h,r_{n}\rangle\cdot u_{n}\cdot e_{n}(t)|\leq m_{\epsilon}e^{(-\delta+\epsilon)\lambda_{n}}\cdot||h||_{L^{2}(a,b)}\cdot|f(t)|\qquad\text{for\,\, all}\quad t\in(a,b).

This justifies below interchanging the integral sign with the summation sign

\displaystyle\langle Th,f\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\overline{f(t)}\cdot\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle h,r_{n}\rangle\cdot u_{n}\cdot e_{n}(t)\right)\,dt

(4.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle h,r_{n}\rangle\cdot u_{n}\cdot\int_{a}^{b}\overline{f(t)}\cdot e_{n}(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle h,r_{n}\rangle\cdot u_{n}\cdot\langle e_{n},f\rangle.

Consider then the function
H
as in
(\ref{H})
with
H\in L^{2}(a,b)
as discussed before.
For any
M\in\mathbb{N}
, one has

|\sum_{n=1}^{M}\langle f,e_{n}\rangle\cdot\overline{u_{n}}\cdot r_{n}(t)|\leq|H(t)|\qquad\text{for\,\, all}\quad t\in(a,b).

From this upper bound and the Lebesgue Convergence Theorem, we can interchange below the integral sign with the summation sign
to get

\displaystyle\langle h,g_{f}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}h(t)\cdot\overline{\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle f,e_{n}\rangle\cdot\overline{u_{n}}\cdot r_{n}(t)\right)}\,dt

(4.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle e_{n},f\rangle\cdot u_{n}\cdot\int_{a}^{b}h(t)\overline{r_{n}(t)}\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle e_{n},f\rangle\cdot u_{n}\cdot\langle h,r_{n}\rangle.

But
(\ref{check2})
is identical to
(\ref{check})
, thus we conclude that

\langle Th,f\rangle=\langle h,g_{f}\rangle
. Clearly this holds for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b)
and therefore

\langle h,T^{*}f\rangle=\langle h,g_{f}\rangle\qquad\text{for\,\, all}\quad h\in{\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}}(a,b).

By the uniqueness of the adjoint operator
T^{*}
, the formula
(\ref{adjoint})
for
T^{*}
is valid.
∎
