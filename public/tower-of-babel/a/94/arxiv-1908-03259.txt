Abstract
. Steiner and Schwarz symmetrizations, and their most important relatives, the Minkowski, Minkowski-Blaschke, fiber, inner rotational, and outer rotational symmetrizations, are investigated. The focus is on the convergence of successive symmetrals with respect to a sequence of i i -dimensional subspaces of ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n} . Such a sequence is called universal for a family of sets if the successive symmetrals of any set in the family converge to a ball with center at the origin. New universal sequences for the main symmetrizations, for all valid dimensions i i of the subspaces, are found, by combining two groups of results. The first, published separately, provides finite sets ℱ {\mathcal{F}} of subspaces such that reflection symmetry (or rotational symmetry) with respect to each subspace in ℱ {\mathcal{F}} implies full rotational symmetry. In the second, proved here, a theorem of Klain for Steiner symmetrization is extended to Schwarz, Minkowski, Minkowski-Blaschke, and fiber symmetrizations, showing that if a sequence of subspaces is drawn from a finite set ℱ {\mathcal{F}} of subspaces, the successive symmetrals of any compact convex set converge to a compact convex set that is symmetric with respect to any subspace in ℱ {\mathcal{F}} appearing infinitely often in the sequence. It is also proved that for Steiner, Schwarz, and Minkowski symmetrizations, a sequence of i i -dimensional subspaces is universal for the class of compact sets if and only if it is universal for the class of compact convex sets, and Klain’s theorem is shown to hold for Schwarz symmetrization of compact sets.

1.
Introduction

Around 1836, Jakob Steiner, in attempting to prove the isoperimetric inequality for convex bodies in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, introduced the process that became known as Steiner symmetrization. (Definitions of Steiner and other important symmetrizations, and some of their basic properties, can be found in Section
4
below.) Its most useful feature is that there are sequences of directions such that the corresponding successive Steiner symmetrals of a convex body always converge to a ball of the same volume. Nowadays, this is employed in standard proofs of not only the isoperimetric inequality, but other potent geometric inequalities besides: the Blaschke-Santaló, Brunn-Minkowski, Busemann random simplex, and Petty projection inequalities, to name but a few. Certain of these inequalities, such as the isoperimetric and Brunn-Minkowski inequalities, hold for more general sets, such as sets of finite perimeter. Many are affine invariant and therefore, at least in the convex geometry context, even more powerful; the Petty projection inequality, for example, is far stronger than the isoperimetric inequality. Several have more general, and sometimes even stronger,
L_{p}
and Orlicz versions. For a sample, the reader can consult
[
1
,
17
,
23
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
. Moreover, the impact of these inequalities extends far beyond geometry, since they often lead quickly to analytic versions; for example, the classical Sobolev inequality is equivalent to the isoperimetric inequality, and the Brunn-Minkowski and Petty projection inequalities yield, respectively, the Prékopa-Leindler inequality and Zhang’s remarkable affine Sobolev inequality
[
52
]
. This transition is explained at length in
[
18
]
and provides a portal to very wide applications to other areas such as probability and multivariate statistics, PDEs, and mathematical economics and finance. For further information and references, see
[
19
, Chapter 9]
,
[
22
, Chapter 9]
, and
[
41
, Chapter 10]
.

Nor is Steiner symmetrization the only symmetrization process by which valuable geometric and analytic inequalities can be established. Minkowski and Schwarz symmetrization can be used to prove Urysohn-type inequalities and others; see, for example,
[
15
,
21
,
38
]

[
39
]
, which stimulated further applications to PDEs, potential theory, and mathematical physics. Here one finds inequalities for other set functions, such as the Poincaré-Faber-Szegő inequality for capacity, the Faber-Krahn inequality for the first eigenvalue of the Laplacian, and Pólya’s inequality for torsional rigidity. The literature is vast and we can only point to
[

,

,

,

,

Most of the results in

(see Sections

\diamondsuit

-universal. This allowed the same conclusion to be drawn, in

Steiner-universal sequences are far from rare. In

-dimensional subspaces whose unit normal vectors are dense in

and involves concatenating finite sequences of symmetrizations, some with respect to a deterministic choice of directions and some with respect to a random choice. Random Steiner symmetrizations that produce Steiner-universal sequences almost surely have been described by Burchard and Fortier
[
11

[
47
]
,
[

It is important to bear in mind that in general the limit of a sequence of successive Steiner symmetrals of a convex body may not exist; see
[
3
, Example 2.1]
. However, a remarkable theorem of Klain
[
29
, Theorem 5.1]
reveals another source of Steiner-universal sequences. This states that if a sequence
(H_{m})
of
(n-1)
-dimensional subspaces is drawn from a finite set
{\mathcal{F}}
of such subspaces, the successive Steiner symmetrals of any compact convex set, with respect to the subspaces in
(H_{m})
, will converge to a compact convex set that is symmetric with respect to each subspace in
{\mathcal{F}}
that occurs infinitely often in
(H_{m})
. From this, Klain
[
29
, Corollary 5.4]
is able to conclude that if the unit normal vectors to the subspaces in
{\mathcal{F}}
contains an irrational basis and each element of the basis occurs infinitely often as a unit normal vector to some

, then
(H_{m})
is Steiner-universal.

A major goal of this paper is to obtain more information about universal sequences of subspaces. In Theorem
5.6
, we adapt an argument from
[
3
]
to extend Klain’s theorem to fiber symmetrization for
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, defined by (
3
) below. This little-known but fundamental symmetrization reduces to Steiner symmetrization when
i=n-1
. Fiber symmetrization becomes Minkowski symmetrization only when
i=0
, but nevertheless we prove in Theorem
5.7
that Klain’s theorem also holds for Minkowski symmetrization when
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, and indeed for any symmetrization with Properties 1, 4, 7, and 9 from Section
4
. In Theorem
5.11
, we prove a version of Klain’s theorem for Schwarz symmetrization; in this case the limit compact convex set is rotationally symmetric with respect to each subspace in
{\mathcal{F}}
that occurs infinitely often in
(H_{m})
. We also show that the same result holds for Minkowski-Blaschke symmetrization. Since Klain’s theorem does not apply when
i=0
, our results extend the theorem to all the main subspace symmetrizations in the literature except Blaschke symmetrization. The latter is somewhat of an oddball, lacking even the monotonicity property (see
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
), and we pay no attention to it in this paper beyond stating Problem
8.4
.

Using our extensions of Klain’s theorem, we are able to exhibit new universal sequences when
{\mathcal{E}}={\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
. Theorem
6.1
does this for Minkowski symmetrization, for
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and Theorem
6.9
serves the same purpose for Schwarz and Minkowski-Blaschke symmetrization. Moreover, Theorem

and
[

, Corollary 8.2]
yield, for all

, weakly

-universal sequences for any
i
-symmetrization process

satisfying Properties 1, 4, and 7 in Section

, except for isolated results such as that of Tonelli
[

]
for Schwarz symmetrization when

.

The transition from extensions of Klain’s theorem to the existence of universal sequences requires an extra step. From Klain’s theorem we know that the limit compact convex set is symmetric with respect to each subspace in the finite set
{\mathcal{F}}
. Therefore if
{\mathcal{F}}
can be chosen so that reflection symmetry (or, for Schwarz and Minkowski-Blaschke symmetrization, rotational symmetry) in each subspace in
{\mathcal{F}}
implies full rotational symmetry, then the limit compact convex set must be an origin-symmetric ball. Hence, in the case of reflection symmetry, the problem is to find a finite set
\mathcal{F}
such the closure of the subgroup of

generated by the reflections in the subspaces in
\mathcal{F}
acts transitively on
S^{n-1}
. Here we apply results from our paper
[
5
]
, summarized in Section

, that build on earlier work of
Burchard, Chambers, and Dranovski
[
10
]
(see also
[
14
]
).

In Section
7
, we examine symmetrization of compact sets. Steiner, Schwarz, and Minkowski symmetrization make sense for compact sets, and some convergence results are available in this setting; see, for example,
[
3
]
,
[
43
]
,
[
46
]
,
[
50
]
, and
[

]
. To these we add the observation, in Theorem

, that Klain’s theorem holds for the Schwarz symmetrization of compact sets. By
[

, Examples 2.1 and 2.4]
, with the sequence (

) of reals there chosen so that their sum converges, we know that the limit of a sequence of successive Steiner symmetrals of a compact set may exist but be non-convex. Thus there is no obvious direct relationship between convergence for convex bodies and convergence for compact sets. Nevertheless, in Theorems
7.3
and
7.4
, we prove that if

, any Schwarz-universal (or Minkowski-universal) sequence for the class of convex bodies is also Schwarz-universal (or Minkowski-universal, respectively) for the class of compact sets. Here we regard Schwarz symmetrization for

as Steiner symmetrization. These results, together with those mentioned above, show that the eight properties (weakly) Steiner-universal for convex bodies, (weakly) Minkowski-universal for convex bodies, (weakly) Steiner-universal for compact sets, and (weakly) Minkowski-universal for compact sets are all equivalent. A tool in the proofs of Theorems
7.3
and
7.4
is the notion of the Kuratowski limit superior or inferior of a sequence of sets.

In addition to the principal results discussed above, we prove a new containment result relating various symmetrals, Lemma
4.1
; an analog of
[
4
, Theorem 8.1]
for Schwarz symmetrization, Theorem
6.10
; and a characterization of Schwarz symmetrization in terms of its properties, Theorem

Throughout the paper we provide, whenever we can, examples to show that the various assumptions we make are necessary. The final Section
8
lists some problems left open by our study.

For most applications, for example in establishing geometric inequalities, basic convergence results for symmetrizations suffice. We end this introduction, however, with a nod to a quite different line of investigation, namely, determining the least number of successive symmetrals required to transform a set into one in some sense close to an origin-symmetric ball. Such results on rates of convergence often require very delicate analysis, as evidenced by the deep work of Bourgain, Klartag, Lindenstrauss, Milman, and others, and have obvious applications to geometric asymptotic analysis, for example. See
[
30
]
,
[
31
]
, and the references given there. A worthwhile endeavor, but one that must wait for future research, would be to determine rates of convergence for symmetrizations in the general setting considered here.

4.
i
-Symmetrization and
i
-symmetrization processes

Let

and let

be fixed. Let

be a class of nonempty compact sets in

denote the subclass of members of

(with respect to

is appropriate for the properties concerned and that they hold for all

is the reflection of

in

1. (
Monotonicity
or

(or

, where

,

, and

3. (

)
\diamondsuit_{H}^{2}K=\diamondsuit_{H}(\diamondsuit_{H}K)=\diamondsuit_{H}K
.

4. (
Invariance on
H
-symmetric sets
)

.

5. (
Invariance on
H
-symmetric spherical cylinders
) If

, where

\diamondsuit_{H}K=K
.

6. (
Projection invariance
)
(\diamondsuit_{H}K)|H=K|H
.

7. (

H
of
H
-symmetric sets
)  If

and

, then
\diamondsuit_{H}(K+y)=\diamondsuit_{H}K
.

8. (

)
\diamondsuit_{H}K

.

9. (

)
\diamondsuit_{H}K_{m}\to\diamondsuit_{H}K
as

whenever

as

in the Hausdorff metric (cf.

).

The six main symmetrizations of interest for this paper are defined and their properties summarized below. In each, we take

Let

Let

. The

of

(2)

M_{H}K=\frac{1}{2}K+\frac{1}{2}R_{H}K.

(Recall that

is the reflection of

Let

h_{K}(u),&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Thus the support function

of

at

is replaced by the average of
h_{K}
over the subsphere of
S^{n-1}
orthogonal to

and containing

[

, p. 58]
and note that we have simplified and corrected the definition given there. We can extend the definition to

if we interpret it to mean that

in this case.

Let
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
. The
fiber symmetral

F_{H}K
of

is defined by

(3)

\displaystyle F_{H}K

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{x\in H}\left(\frac{1}{2}(K\cap(H^{\perp}+x))+\frac{1}{2}R_{H}(K\cap(H^{\perp}+x))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{x\in H}\left(\frac{1}{2}(K\cap(H^{\perp}+x))+\frac{1}{2}(R_{H}K\cap(H^{\perp}+x))\right).

See
[
4
, p. 58]
. Thus each non-degenerate section of

by an

-dimensional subspace orthogonal to

is the Minkowski symmetral of the corresponding section of

.

Let

. The

of

is such that for each
(n-i)
-dimensional plane

orthogonal to
H
and meeting
K
, the set

is a (possibly degenerate)
(n-i)
-dimensional ball with center in
H
and radius equal to that of the (possibly degenerate) largest
(n-i)
-dimensional ball contained in

. The

of

is the intersection of all rotationally symmetric with respect to

contains

.

The Minkowski, Minkowski-Blaschke, fiber, inner rotational, and outer rotational symmetrizations are all important and very natural relatives of Steiner and Schwarz symmetrizations. Indeed, there is a remarkable duality
[
4
, Theorem 7.1]
between Steiner and Minkowski symmetrization when
i=n-1
, which extends to one between fiber and Minkowski symmetrization when

: The fiber (Steiner, if
i=n-1
) symmetral of
K\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}
is the
union
of all
H
-symmetric compact convex sets such that some translate orthogonal to
H

is contained in

K
, while the Minkowski symmetral of
K
is the
intersection
of all
H
-symmetric compact convex sets such that some translate orthogonal to
H

contains

K
. The inner and outer rotational symmetrizations, introduced in
[

, Section 5]
, display exactly the same duality in a rotationally symmetric setting. Minkowski-Blaschke symmetrization extends to
i\in\{1,\dots,n-2\}
the case

of Minkowski symmetrization in a different, but nevertheless direct, way to using (
2

[

).

Some of the applications of the symmetrizations discussed in the previous paragraph stem from containment relations, to which we now turn our attention. We begin with the following new result, which reduces to the known fact that
S_{H}K\subset M_{H}K
when
i=n-1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
H\in{\mathcal{G}}(n,i)
,
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, and
K\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}
, then
S_{H}K\subset\overline{M}_{H}K\subset O_{H}K
.

Proof.

For the right-hand inclusion, let
L
be rotationally symmetric with respect to
H
and such that
K\subset L+y
, where
y\in H^{\perp}
. Since
\overline{M}_{H}
is monotonic and invariant under translations of
H
-symmetric sets orthogonal to
H
, we obtain

\overline{M}_{H}K\subset\overline{M}_{H}(L+y)=\overline{M}_{H}L=L,

where the previous equality follows directly from the definition of
\overline{M}_{H}
and the fact that
L
is rotationally symmetric with respect to
H
. Therefore
\overline{M}_{H}K\subset L
and hence
\overline{M}_{H}K\subset O_{H}K
by the definition of
O_{H}
.

For the left-hand inclusion, let
x\in H
. By the definition of
S_{H}K
, we have

(S_{H}K)\cap(H^{\perp}+x)=s_{x}(B^{n}\cap H^{\perp})+x,

for some
s_{x}\geq 0
satisfying

(4)

V_{n-1}(K\cap(H^{\perp}+x))=V_{n-1}(s_{x}(B^{n}\cap H^{\perp})+x).

By the definition of
\overline{M}_{H}K
, we have that for
u\in S^{n-1}
,
h_{\overline{M}_{H}K}(u)
is the average of
h_{K}(v)
over
v\in S^{n-1}\cap(H^{\perp}+u)
. It follows that

\overline{M}_{H}(K\cap(H^{\perp}+x))=t_{x}(B^{n}\cap H^{\perp})+x\subset H^{\perp}+x,

for some
t_{x}\geq 0
satisfying

(5)

V_{1}(K\cap(H^{\perp}+x))=V_{1}(t_{x}(B^{n}\cap H^{\perp})+x).

By Urysohn’s inequality (
[
19
, Theorem 9.3.2, p. 365]
with

or
[

, (7.21), p. 382]
) in

, the inequalities (

) and (

) imply that
s_{x}\leq t_{x}
. It follows, by the monotonicity of
\overline{M}_{H}
, that

(S_{H}K)\cap(H^{\perp}+x)\subset\overline{M}_{H}\left(K\cap(H^{\perp}+x)\right)\subset(\overline{M}_{H}K)\cap(H^{\perp}+x)

and hence that
S_{H}K\subset\overline{M}_{H}K
.
∎

5.
Extensions and variants of Klain’s theorem

The goal of this section is to establish some extensions and variants of Klain’s theorem
[

, Theorem 5.1]
for Steiner symmetrization, the special case of Theorem
5.6
below corresponding to
i=n-1
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be a class of compact sets closed under intersections with
o
-symmetric balls and let
f:{\mathcal{B}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a set function for which

(9)

\Omega_{f}(K)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(K\cap rB^{n})e^{-r^{2}}\,dr

exists for each
K\in{\mathcal{B}}
. We call
\Omega_{f}
the
f
-layering function
on
{\mathcal{B}}
. When
{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
and
f=V_{n}
, we write
\Omega_{V_{n}}=\Omega
for the usual layering function (see, for example,
[
29
, p. 344]
). Klain
[
29
, Theorem 3.1]
proved the following result for Steiner symmetrization.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be a class of compact sets closed under intersections with
o
-symmetric balls and let
f:{\mathcal{B}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be an increasing set function. Let
H\in{\mathcal{G}}(n,i)
,
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, and suppose that
{\diamondsuit_{H}}:{\mathcal{B}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{B}}_{H}
is monotonic, invariant on
o
-symmetric balls, and does not decrease
f
. If
K\in{\mathcal{B}}
, then

(10)

\Omega_{f}(K)\leq\Omega_{f}(\diamondsuit_{H}K).

If
f
is strictly increasing on
{\mathcal{B}}
, then equality in (
10
) implies that
\diamondsuit_{H}(K\cap rB^{n})=(\diamondsuit_{H}K)\cap rB^{n}
for each
r>0
.

Proof.

Since
\diamondsuit_{H}
is monotonic and invariant on
o
-symmetric balls, we have

(11)

\diamondsuit_{H}(K\cap rB^{n})\subset(\diamondsuit_{H}K)\cap rB^{n}

for all
r>0
. Since
\diamondsuit_{H}
does not decrease
f
and
f
is increasing, it follows that

(12)

f(K\cap rB^{n})\leq f(\diamondsuit_{H}(K\cap rB^{n}))\leq f((\diamondsuit_{H}K)\cap rB^{n})

for
r>0
and (
10
) is then a consequence of the definition (
9
) of
\Omega_{f}
.

Suppose that
f
is strictly increasing on
{\mathcal{B}}
,
K\in{\mathcal{B}}
, and equality holds in (
10
). Then, in view of (
12
), we clearly have
f(K\cap rB^{n})=f((\diamondsuit_{H}K)\cap rB^{n})
for almost all
r>0
and hence, since
f
is increasing, for all
r>0
. This implies that (
12
) holds with equality. Consequently, since
f
is strictly increasing on
{\mathcal{B}}
, we deduce from (
11
) that
\diamondsuit_{H}(K\cap rB^{n})=(\diamondsuit_{H}K)\cap rB^{n}
for each
r>0
.
∎

Example 5.2
.

The equality condition in Lemma
5.1
is not enough for our purposes: We would like to have equality in (
10
) if and only if
\diamondsuit_{H}K=K
. However, this is not true in general, even when
{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
. To see this, let
H\in{\mathcal{G}}(n,i)
,
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, and define
\diamondsuit_{H}
as follows. Choose
u\in S^{n-1}\cap H
and let
R=\{tu:t\geq 0\}
. If

, let

be the spherical cap of

with center

and the same

-dimensional Hausdorff measure as

, and let

. This process is sometimes called spherical cap symmetrization. Then define

.

If

with

and

.

Now let

is an

-symmetric translate of

, so

for each

.∎

Note that the

-symmetric cylinders nor invariant under translations orthogonal to

of

-symmetric sets. Since the symmetral of a ball is an

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. When
\diamondsuit_{H}=F_{H}
, fiber symmetrization with respect to
H
, equality holds in (
10
) with
{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
and
f=V_{n}
if and only if
F_{H}K=K
. Hence the corresponding conclusion also holds when
\diamondsuit_{H}
is monotonic, invariant on
H
-symmetric sets, and invariant under translations orthogonal to
H
of
H
-symmetric sets. In particular, it holds when
\diamondsuit_{H}=M_{H}
, Minkowski symmetrization with respect to
H
.

Proof.

Let
K\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}
, let
r>0
, and let
x\in H
. If equality holds in (
10
) with
{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
and
f=V_{n}
, then by Lemma
5.1
, we have
F_{H}(K\cap rB^{n})=(F_{H}K)\cap rB^{n}
. Using the definition (
3
) of
F_{H}
and the Brunn-Minkowski inequality in
H^{\perp}+x
, we obtain

\displaystyle V_{n-i}((F_{H}K)\cap rB^{n}\cap(H^{\perp}+x))^{1/(n-i)}=V_{n-i}(F_{H}(K\cap rB^{n})\cap(H^{\perp}+x))^{1/(n-i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{n-i}\left(\frac{1}{2}(K\cap rB^{n}\cap(H^{\perp}+x))+\frac{1}{2}R_{H}(K\cap rB^{n}\cap(H^{\perp}+x))\right)^{1/(n-i)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}V_{n-i}(K\cap rB^{n}\cap(H^{\perp}+x))^{1/(n-i)}+\frac{1}{2}V_{n-i}(R_{H}(K\cap rB^{n}\cap(H^{\perp}+x)))^{1/(n-i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{n-i}(K\cap rB^{n}\cap(H^{\perp}+x))^{1/(n-i)}

and hence

(13)

V_{n-i}((F_{H}K)\cap rB^{n}\cap(H^{\perp}+x))\geq V_{n-i}(K\cap rB^{n}\cap(H^{\perp}+x)).

Equality holds if and only if

is homothetic to

with respect to

.

Suppose that

. Then, since

is invariant on
H
-symmetric sets,

is not
H
-symmetric. It follows that there is an

is not
H

with respect to

. From the previous paragraph, we conclude that strict inequality holds in (
13
). By Fubini’s theorem, we obtain

and so, by continuity and (
9
),
\Omega(F_{H}K)>\Omega(K)
. This proves the first claim in the lemma.

Suppose that
\diamondsuit_{H}
is monotonic, invariant on
H
-symmetric sets, and invariant under translations orthogonal to
H
of
H
-symmetric sets. By
[
4
, Corollary 7.3]
,

. Therefore
\Omega(K)\leq\Omega(F_{H}K)\leq\Omega(\diamondsuit_{H}K)
, by Lemma

. If

, then
\Omega(F_{H}K)=\Omega(K)
. Consequently,

, implying that

is
H
-symmetric and hence that
\diamondsuit_{H}K=K
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,n-2\}
. When
\diamondsuit_{H}=S_{H}
, Schwarz symmetrization with respect to
H
, equality holds in (
10
) with
{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
and

if and only if

.

Proof.

Let
K\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}
and let

. By identifying

with
{\mathbb{R}}^{n-i}
, choose a Steiner-universal sequence
(H_{m})
such that

with

,

. Let

,

. Then the Steiner symmetrals

as

Suppose that equality holds in (

, i.e.,

. Let

. By Lemma

Lemma 5.5
.

Let

. When

, equality holds in (

The following result generalizes Klain’s theorem
[
29
, Theorem 5.1]
for Steiner symmetrization, which corresponds to the special case when
i=n-1
. The proof uses ideas from
[
3
]
.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and let
(H_{m})
be a sequence chosen from a finite set
{\mathcal{F}}=\{U_{1},\dots,U_{k}\}\subset{\mathcal{G}}(n,i)
. Then, for every
K\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}
, the successive fiber symmetrals

(14)

K_{m}=F_{H_{m}}\cdots F_{H_{1}}K

converge to a compact convex set
L
as
m\to\infty
. Furthermore,
L
is
U_{j}
-symmetric for each
U_{j}\in{\mathcal{F}}
that appears infinitely often in
(H_{m})
.

Proof.

A few preliminary observations will be useful.
Let
H\in{\mathcal{G}}(n,i)
and let
K,L\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}
, where
L
is
H
-symmetric. Note that
F_{H}
does not decrease volume. Indeed, the definition (
3
) of
F_{H}
shows that it is equivalent to Minkowski symmetrization in each
(n-i)
-dimensional plane orthogonal to
H
. It follows that

V_{n-i}(K\cap(H^{\perp}+x))\leq V_{n-i}((F_{H}K)\cap(H^{\perp}+x))

for each
x\in H
, since Minkowski symmetrization preserves
V_{1}
and does not decrease
V_{j}
for
j>1
(see
[
4
, p. 58]
). Then Fubini’s theorem yields

(15)

V_{n}(K)\leq V_{n}(F_{H}K).

Furthermore

(16)

V_{n}(K\cap L)\leq V_{n}((F_{H}K)\cap L)

holds because

F_{H}(K\cap L)\subset(F_{H}K)\cap F_{H}L=(F_{H}K)\cap L,

since
F_{H}
is monotonic and invariant on
H
-symmetric sets. Hence, by (
15
) with
K
replaced by
K\cap L
,

V_{n}(K\cap L)\leq V_{n}(F_{H}(K\cap L))\leq V_{n}((F_{H}K)\cap L).

We follow Klain’s argument. Dropping an initial segment
(K_{m})_{m\leq N}
of the sequence
(K_{m})
defined by (
14
) and possibly replacing
{\mathcal{F}}
by one of its subsets, we may assume, without loss of generality, that each subspace in
{\mathcal{F}}
appears infinitely often in the sequence
(H_{m})
. The main idea is to construct a subsequence along which the subspaces
U_{j}\in{\mathcal{F}}
appear in a particular order. With each index
m
, we associate a permutation
\pi_{m}
of
\{1,\dots,k\}
that indicates the order in which the subspaces
U_{1},\dots,U_{k}
appear for the first time among those
U_{j}
with
j\geq m
. Since there are only finitely many permutations, we can pick a subsequence
(H_{m_{p}})
such that the permutation
\pi_{m_{p}}
is the same for each
p
. By relabeling the subspaces, we may assume that this permutation is the identity. Passing to a further subsequence, we may assume that every subspace in
{\mathcal{F}}
appears in each segment
H_{m_{p}},H_{m_{p}+1},\dots,H_{m_{p+1}-1}
.

By Blaschke’s selection theorem, there is a subsequence (again denoted by
(K_{m_{p}})
) that converges in the Hausdorff metric to some

L\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}
. We note for later use that the entire sequence
\bigl(V_{n}(K_{m})\bigr)
is increasing, by (
15
), and hence convergent.
Moreover,

(17)

\lim_{m\to\infty}V_{n}(K_{m})=\lim_{p\to\infty}V_{n}(K_{m_{p}})=V_{n}(L).

Now assume that
K\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
.

We show by induction that
L
is
U_{j}
-symmetric for
j=1,\dots,k
. For
j=1
, observe that
H_{m_{p}}=U_{1}
for each
p
. Therefore
K_{m_{p}}
is
U_{1}
-symmetric for each
p
and the same is true for
L
. Suppose that
L
is
U_{r}
-symmetric for
r=1,\dots,j-1
. Let
m^{\prime}_{p}
be the index where
U_{j}
appears for the first time after
H_{m_{p}}
. Then for
m_{p}+1\leq m\leq m^{\prime}_{p}-1
,
H_{m}=U_{r}
for some
r=1,\dots,j-1
, so we can apply the inductive hypothesis and (
16
), successively with
H
equal to one of the latter subspaces, to obtain

(18)

V_{n}(K_{m_{p}}\cap L)\leq V_{n}((F_{H_{m^{\prime}_{p}-1}}\cdots F_{H_{m_{p}+1}}K_{m_{p}})\cap L)=V_{n}(K_{m^{\prime}_{p}-1}\cap L).

Since
(K_{m_{p}})
converges to
L
, (
17
) implies that the left-hand side of (
18
) converges to
V_{n}(L)
. Therefore the right-hand side converges to
V_{n}(L)
, which implies that

V_{n}(K_{m^{\prime}_{p}-1}\Delta L)=V_{n}(K_{m^{\prime}_{p}-1})+V_{n}(L)-2V_{n}(K_{m^{\prime}_{p}-1}\cap L)

converges to zero. Then, because
K_{m^{\prime}_{p}-1}\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
, it follows that
K_{m^{\prime}_{p}-1}
converges to
L
as
p\to\infty
. Now we use this, Lemma
5.1
(with
{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{K}}^{n}
,
f=V_{n}
, and
\diamondsuit_{H}=F_{H}
), the continuity of fiber symmetrization, and the continuity of the functional
\Omega
, to obtain

(19)

\Omega(L)\leq\Omega(F_{U_{j}}L)=\lim_{p\to\infty}\Omega(F_{U_{j}}K_{m^{\prime}_{p}-1})=\lim_{p\to\infty}\Omega(K_{m^{\prime}_{p}}).

Since
\bigl(\Omega(K_{m})\bigr)
is an increasing sequence, by Lemma
5.1
, and since it contains the subsequence
\bigl(\Omega(K_{m_{p}})\bigr)
which converges to
\Omega(L)
because
\Omega
is continuous, the first and last term in (
19
) are equal, so equality holds throughout. By Lemma
5.3
,
F_{U_{j}}L=L
. Therefore
L
is
U_{j}
-symmetric and this concludes the inductive step.

It remains to prove that the entire sequence converges. Since
L
is
U_{j}
-symmetric for
j=1,\dots,k
, we have, by the same reasoning as in (
18
) and the lines following it, that

V_{n}(K_{m_{p}}\cap L)\leq V_{n}(K_{m}\cap L),

for each
m\geq m_{p}
; moreover, since
V_{n}(K_{m_{p}}\cap L)\to V_{n}(L)
as
p\to\infty
, we also have
V_{n}(K_{m}\cap L)\to V_{n}(L)
as
m\to\infty
. Since
K_{m}\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
, this implies that
K_{m}
converges to
L
as
m\to\infty
. This completes the proof when
K\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
.

Suppose that
\dim K<n
. Fiber symmetrization is invariant under translations, so we may assume that
o\in K
. Then
o\in K_{m}
for all the successive symmetrals
K_{m}
of
K
. Since fiber symmetrization with respect to a subspace
H
corresponds to Minkowski symmetrization in each plane
H^{\perp}+x
,
x\in H
, it follows that
{\mathrm{aff}}\,K_{m}\subset{\mathrm{aff}}\,K_{m+1}
for all
m
. Then there is an
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
{\mathrm{aff}}\,K_{m}={\mathrm{aff}}\,K_{M}
for all
m\geq M
. Consequently, we may as well assume, by replacing
K
by
K_{M}
if necessary, that each
K_{m}
is contained in a subspace
S
and
\dim K_{m}=\dim S=k
, say. The cases
k=0
and
k=1
are trivial, and if
k\geq 2
, the previous argument can be repeated, with
n
replaced by
k
, by identifying
S
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
.
∎

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and let
\diamondsuit
be an

-symmetrization process on
{\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
. Suppose that for each

,

H

Proof.

We first check that the proof of Theorem

with the four stated properties. Indeed, the first three properties imply that for each

6.
Universal and weakly-universal sequences

In
[
29
, Corollary 5.4]
, Klain proves the following result:
Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
be a set of directions in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
that contains an irrational basis for
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Suppose that
(u_{m})
is a sequence of directions such that each
u_{m}
belongs to
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}
and each element of the irrational basis appears infinitely often in
(u_{m})
. Then
(u_{m})
is weakly Steiner-universal.

Weakly Steiner-universal is equivalent to Steiner-universal, as we know. Thus Klain’s result provides specific Steiner-universal sequences, the novel feature being that only a finite set of directions are used.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}

7.
Symmetrals of compact sets

In this section, we consider symmetrals of compact sets, paying special attention to Steiner, Schwarz, and Minkowski symmetrization. The definitions of
S_{H}K
and
M_{H}K
given for
K\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}
in Section
4
apply equally to
K\in{\mathcal{C}}^{n}
.

It is known (see
[
3
, Examples 2.1 and 2.4]
) that in general the limit of a sequence of successive Steiner symmetrals of a compact convex set may not exist. Also, the limit of a sequence of successive Steiner symmetrals of a compact set may exist but be non-convex; this is shown by
[
3
, Example 2.1]
with the sequence (
\alpha_{m}
) of reals chosen so that their sum converges.

In
[
3
, Theorem 6.1]
, it is proved that Klain’s theorem
[
29
, Theorem 5.1]
holds for compact sets, i.e., the same statement holds when the initial set is an arbitrary compact set. Volčič
[
51
]
showed that any sequence
(u_{m})
dense in
S^{n-1}
can be ordered so that the resulting sequence is Steiner-universal, even if the initial set is an arbitrary compact set.

Despite this progress, many problems concerning symmetrals of compact sets remain open, even for Steiner symmetrization; see, for example,
[
3
, p. 1708]
,
[
51
, p. 1691]
, and Section
8
.

Our first contribution is the following extension of the first part of Theorem
5.11
. (The second part would require a suitable extension of the definition of Minkowski-Blaschke symmetrization, which we shall not pursue here.)

Theorem 7.1
.

Klain’s theorem
[
29
, Theorem 5.1]
(cf. Theorem
5.6
) holds for Schwarz symmetrization of compact sets, where the limit set
L
is rotationally symmetric with respect to each

that appears infinitely often in
(H_{m})

Proof.

We shall only give a sketch, indicating the necessary observations that allow the proof for Steiner symmetrization from
[

to be modified.

Let

and let

. Let

be nonempty and let
E_{\delta}=E+\delta B^{n}
for

and
\delta>0
. The main argument requires the following four preliminary observations. Firstly, the monotonicity of
S_{H}
implies that
S_{H}(K\cap L)\subset S_{H}K\cap S_{H}L
. This, the equality
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(S_{H}K\setminus S_{H}L)={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(S_{H}K)-{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(S_{H}K\cap S_{H}L)
, and the fact that
S_{H}
preserves volume yield

(24)

{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(S_{H}K\setminus S_{H}L)\leq{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(K\setminus L).

Secondly, it follows that
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(S_{H}K\triangle S_{H}L)\leq{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(K\triangle L)
and hence that
S_{H}
is continuous on
{\mathcal{C}}^{n}
in the symmetric difference metric. Thirdly, the inclusion

(25)

(S_{H}K)_{\delta}\subset S_{H}K_{\delta}

is the special case
L=\delta B^{n}
of
S_{H}K+S_{H}L\subset S_{H}(K+L)
, which in turn follows from the Brunn-Minkowski inequality applied to intersections of
K
and

with translates of

. Fourthly,

(26)

{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(S_{H}K_{\delta}\setminus rB^{n})={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(K_{\delta}\setminus rB^{n})~{\text{for all}}~\delta,r>0\Rightarrow S_{H}K=K.

To see this, let

. Then

{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(K_{\delta}\setminus rB^{n})\geq{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(S_{u^{\perp}}K_{\delta}\setminus rB^{n})\geq{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(S_{H}S_{u^{\perp}}K_{\delta}\setminus rB^{n})={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(S_{H}K_{\delta}\setminus rB^{n}),

where the inequalities follow from (

) and the equality from

. The hypothesis in (

) implies that equality holds throughout. Then, from
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(K_{\delta}\setminus rB^{n})={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(S_{u^{\perp}}K_{\delta}\setminus rB^{n})
and
[

, we get

and since

was arbitrary, the desired conclusion

in (

With these preliminary observations in hand, a few substitutions allow the main argument of
[

to be followed without difficulty. Of course Steiner symmetrization with respect to a sequence of directions must be replaced by Schwarz symmetrization with respect to a sequence of subspaces. Otherwise, it is only necessary to appeal to (

), (

) wherever the proof of

uses
[

,
[

, and
[

, respectively.

For the remaining results in this section, we need some definitions. The
Kuratowski limit superior
of a sequence
(A_{m})
of sets in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the closed set

{\mathrm{Ls}}\,_{m\to\infty}A_{m}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\forall\varepsilon>0,~B(x,\varepsilon)\cap A_{m}\neq\emptyset~{\text{for infinitely many }}m\}

and the

of
(A_{m})
is the closed set

{\mathrm{Li}}\,_{m\to\infty}A_{m}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\forall\varepsilon>0,~B(x,\varepsilon)\cap A_{m}\neq\emptyset~{\text{for sufficiently large }}m\}.

It is not hard to check that

. Also, if
(A_{m})
is uniformly bounded, then

exists if and only if
{\mathrm{Li}}\,_{m\to\infty}A_{m}={\mathrm{Ls}}\,_{m\to\infty}A_{m}
. See
[

, where these notions are credited to P. Painlevé.

K\in{\mathcal{C}}^{n}
and suppose that the sequence
(\diamondsuit_{1,m}K)
, defined via (
8
), is uniformly bounded. Let
r>0
be minimal such that
{\mathrm{Ls}}\,_{m\to\infty}\diamondsuit_{1,m}K\subset rB^{n}
. Let
f:{\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}\to[0,\infty)
be continuous and strictly increasing. If
\diamondsuit_{H}
is monotonic on
{\mathcal{C}}^{n}
and
f
-preserving on
{\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
for each
H\in{\mathcal{G}}(n,i)
, then
rS^{n-1}\subset{\mathrm{Li}}\,_{m\to\infty}\diamondsuit_{1,m}K
.

Proof.

Let
K\in{\mathcal{C}}^{n}
and
r>0
satisfy the assumptions of the lemma and let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
L={\mathrm{Ls}}\,_{m\to\infty}\diamondsuit_{1,m}K
and let
I={\mathrm{Li}}\,_{m\to\infty}\diamondsuit_{1,m}K
. We claim that there is an
m_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

(27)

\diamondsuit_{1,m}K\subset L+\varepsilon B^{n}

for
m\geq m_{0}
. Indeed, if this is not true, then there exists a subsequence
(m_{j})
and points

x_{m_{j}}\in(\diamondsuit_{1,m_{j}}K)\setminus(L+\varepsilon B^{n})

for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Since
(\diamondsuit_{1,m}K)
is uniformly bounded, there is a subsequence of
(x_{m_{j}})
converging to some
z
. But then
z\in L
and
z\not\in L+\varepsilon B^{n}
, which is impossible.

If the conclusion of the lemma is false, there is an
x\in(rS^{n-1})\setminus I
and hence an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and subsequence
(m_{k})
such that

(28)

B(x,\varepsilon_{0})\cap\diamondsuit_{1,m_{k}}K=\emptyset

for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Let
J
be a closed half-space such that
x\not\in J
and

(29)

(rB^{n})\setminus B(x,\varepsilon_{0})\subset J.

Let
C=(rB^{n})\cap J
. As
f
is continuous and strictly increasing, we can choose
t>0
small enough so that
x\not\in C+tB^{n}
and

(30)

f(C+tB^{n})<f(rB^{n}).

By (
29
),
(rB^{n})\setminus B(x,\varepsilon_{0})\subset C\setminus B(x,\varepsilon_{0})
and hence

(31)

((r+t^{\prime})B^{n})\setminus B(x,\varepsilon_{0})\subset(C+tB^{n})\setminus B(x,\varepsilon_{0})

for sufficiently small
t^{\prime}>0
. It then follows from (
27
) with
\varepsilon=t^{\prime}
, the assumption
L\subset rB^{n}
, (
28
), and (
31
) that

(32)

\diamondsuit_{1,m_{k}}K\subset C+tB^{n}

for
m_{k}\geq m_{0}
. Now if
p>m_{k}\geq m_{0}
, (
32
) and the monotonicity of
\diamondsuit_{H}
on
{\mathcal{C}}^{n}
yields

(33)

\diamondsuit_{1,p}K=\diamondsuit_{m_{k}+1,p}(\diamondsuit_{1,m_{k}}K)\subset\diamondsuit_{m_{k}+1,p}(C+tB^{n})\to sB^{n}

as
p\to\infty
, where
s
is independent of
k
, since
C+tB^{n}\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
and
(H_{m})
is
\diamondsuit
-universal. As
\diamondsuit_{H}
is
f
-preserving on
{\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
and
f
is continuous there, we deduce from (
30
) and (
33
) that

f(sB^{n})=f(\diamondsuit_{{m_{k}+1,p}}(C+tB^{n}))=f(C+tB^{n})<f(rB^{n})

for
p\geq m_{k}\geq m_{0}
, so
s<r
because
f
is strictly increasing on
{\mathcal{K}}^{n}_{n}
. This and (
33
) imply that
\diamondsuit_{1,p}K\subset((r+s)/2)B^{n}
for sufficiently large
p
and consequently
L\subset((r+s)/2)B^{n}
. Since
(r+s)/2<r
, this contradicts the minimality of
r
.
∎

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and let
(H_{m})
be a Schwarz-universal sequence in
{\mathcal{G}}(n,i)
. If
K\in{\mathcal{C}}^{n}
and
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the successive Schwarz symmetrals
S_{l,m}K
of
K
, defined by (
8
) with
\diamondsuit=S
, converge to
rB^{n}
as
m\to\infty
, for some
r=r(K)
independent of
l
. In other words,
(H_{m})
is also Schwarz-universal for compact sets.

Proof.

It is enough to consider the case when
l=1
, because the fact that Schwarz symmetrization preserves volume on
{\mathcal{C}}^{n}
means that the constant
r
in the statement of the theorem must satisfy
V_{n}(rB^{n})={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(K)
. Accordingly, let
L={\mathrm{Ls}}\,_{m\to\infty}S_{1,m}K
and let
I={\mathrm{Li}}\,_{m\to\infty}S_{1,m}K
. Let
r\geq 0
be minimal such that
L\subset rB^{n}
. If
r=0
, then
L=\{o\}
and by (
27
), the result holds with
r=0
. Otherwise,
r>0
and we may apply Lemma
7.2
with
f=V_{n}
to conclude that
rS^{n-1}\subset I
. It will suffice to prove that
I=rB^{n}
, since this implies that
I=L
.

Suppose that
I\neq rB^{n}
. Then there is an
x_{0}\in rB^{n}\setminus I
and hence an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and a subsequence
(m_{k})
such that

(34)

B(x_{0},\varepsilon)\cap S_{1,m_{k}}K=\emptyset

for
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. As
I\subset rB^{n}
is compact, there is an
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
such that

(35)

B(x_{0},\varepsilon)\subset rB^{n}\setminus(I+\varepsilon B^{n})\subset(r-\varepsilon)B^{n}.

Choose
v_{1},\dots,v_{p}\in rS^{n-1}
such that

rS^{n-1}\subset\cup\{B(v_{i},\varepsilon/2):i=1,\dots,p\}.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
. Since
v_{i}\in I
, there is an
n_{i}
such that for each
m\geq n_{i}
, there is an
x_{i}\in B(v_{i},\varepsilon/2)\cap S_{1,m}K
. Let
n_{0}=\max\{n_{i}:i=1,\dots,p\}
and choose
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
m_{k}\geq n_{0}
.

Let
x_{0}=y_{0}+z_{0}
, where
y_{0}\in H_{m_{k}}
and
z_{0}\in H_{m_{k}}^{\perp}
. Let
v_{0}=y_{0}+w_{0}\in rS^{n-1}
, where
w_{0}\in H_{m_{k}}^{\perp}
and
x_{0}\in[y_{0},v_{0}]
. Since
v_{0}\not\in B(x_{0},\varepsilon)
by (
35
), we have
\|x_{0}-v_{0}\|>\varepsilon
. Moreover,
z_{0}\in[o,w_{0}]
and
\|z_{0}-w_{0}\|=\|x_{0}-v_{0}\|
. Consequently,

(36)

\|w_{0}\|>\|z_{0}\|+\varepsilon.

Choose
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
such that
v_{0}\in B(v_{i},\varepsilon/2)
. Since
{m_{k}}\geq n_{0}
, there is an
x_{i}\in B(v_{i},\varepsilon/2)\cap S_{1,{m_{k}}}K
and hence
\|v_{0}-x_{i}\|\leq\varepsilon
. Let
x_{i}=y_{i}+w_{i}
, where
y_{i}\in H_{m_{k}}
and
w_{i}\in H_{m_{k}}^{\perp}
. Then
\|y_{0}-y_{i}\|\leq\varepsilon
and
\|w_{0}-w_{i}\|\leq\varepsilon
, and from the latter and (
36
), we conclude that
\|w_{i}\|>\|z_{0}\|
. It follows that

(B(y_{i},\|w_{i}\|)\cap(H_{m_{k}}^{\perp}+y_{i}))\cap B(x_{0},\varepsilon)\neq\emptyset.

However,
B(y_{i},\|w_{i}\|)\cap(H_{m_{k}}^{\perp}+y_{i})\subset S_{1,{m_{k}}}K
, because
x_{i}=y_{i}+w_{i}\in S_{1,{m_{k}}}K
and
S_{1,{m_{k}}}K
is a Schwarz symmetral with respect to
H_{m_{k}}
. This contradicts (
34
) and completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and let
(H_{m})
be a Minkowski-universal sequence in
{\mathcal{G}}(n,i)
. If
K\in{\mathcal{C}}^{n}
and
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the successive Minkowski symmetrals
M_{l,m}K
of
K
, defined by (
8
) with
\diamondsuit=M
, converge to
rB^{n}
as
m\to\infty
, for some
r=r(K)
independent of
l
. In other words,
(H_{m})
is also Minkowski-universal for compact sets.

Proof.

It is enough to consider the case when
l=1
. Indeed, since
{\mathrm{conv}}\,(A+B)={\mathrm{conv}}\,A+{\mathrm{conv}}\,B
for arbitrary sets
A
and
B
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, Minkowski symmetrization preserves the mean width of convex hulls. Then, for any
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
, if
M_{l,m}K
converges to
rB^{n}
, we have that
M_{l,m}({\mathrm{conv}}\,K)
also converges to
rB^{n}
. But
r
must satisfy
V_{1}(rB^{n})=V_{1}({\mathrm{conv}}\,K)
, so
r
is independent of
l
.

Let
L={\mathrm{Ls}}\,_{m\to\infty}M_{1,m}K
and let
I={\mathrm{Li}}\,_{m\to\infty}M_{1,m}K
. Let
r\geq 0
be minimal such that
L\subset rB^{n}
. If
r=0
, then
L=\{o\}
and by (
27
), the result holds with
r=0
. Otherwise,
r>0
and we may apply Lemma
7.2
with
f=V_{1}
to conclude that
rS^{n-1}\subset I
. It will suffice to prove that
I=rB^{n}
, since this implies that
I=L
.

Suppose that
I\neq rB^{n}
. Then there is an
x\in rB^{n}\setminus I
and hence an
\varepsilon>0
and a subsequence
(m_{k})
such that

(37)

B(x,\varepsilon)\cap M_{1,m_{k}}K=\emptyset

for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Choose
v_{1},\dots,v_{p}\in rS^{n-1}
such that

rS^{n-1}\subset\cup\{B(v_{i},\varepsilon/2):i=1,\dots,p\}.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
. Since
v_{i}\in I
, there is an
n_{i}
such that for each
m\geq n_{i}
, there is an
x_{i}\in B(v_{i},\varepsilon/2)\cap M_{1,m}K
. Let
n_{0}=\max\{n_{i}:i=1,\dots,p\}
and choose
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
m_{k}>n_{0}
.

Let
y,z\in rS^{n-1}
be such that
x=(y+z)/2
.
Choose
i,j\in\{1,\dots,p\}
such that
y\in B(v_{i},\varepsilon/2)
and
R_{H_{m_{k}}}z\in B(v_{j},\varepsilon/2)
. Since
{m_{k}}>n_{0}
, there are
x_{i}\in B(v_{i},\varepsilon/2)\cap M_{1,{m_{k}}-1}K
and
x_{j}\in B(v_{j},\varepsilon/2)\cap M_{1,{m_{k}}-1}K
. Then
x_{i}\in B(y,\varepsilon)
and
x_{j}\in B(R_{H_{m_{k}}}z,\varepsilon)
; the latter implies that
R_{H_{m_{k}}}x_{j}\in B(z,\varepsilon)
.

Let
q=(x_{i}+R_{H_{m_{k}}}x_{j})/2
. Then
q\in B(x,\varepsilon)
and
q\in M_{H_{m_{k}}}(M_{1,{m_{k}}-1}K)=M_{1,{m_{k}}}K

since
x_{i}\in M_{1,{m_{k}}-1}K
and
R_{H_{m_{k}}}x_{j}\in R_{H_{m_{k}}}(M_{1,{m_{k}}-1}K)
. It follows that
B(x,\varepsilon)\cap M_{1,{m_{k}}}K\neq\emptyset
, which contradicts (
37
) and completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
7.3
with
