Two problems on the distribution of Carmichael’s lambda function
Paul Pollack

Abstract
. Let λ ⁡ ( n ) \lambda(n) denote the exponent of the multiplicative group modulo n n . We show that when q q is odd, each coprime residue class modulo q q is hit equally often by λ ⁡ ( n ) \lambda(n) as n n varies. Under the stronger assumption that gcd ⁡ ( q , 6 ) = 1 \gcd(q,6)=1 , we prove that equidistribution persists throughout a Siegel–Walfisz-type range of uniformity. By similar methods we show that λ ⁡ ( n ) \lambda(n) obeys Benford’s leading digit law with respect to natural density. Moreover, if we assume GRH, then Benford’s law holds for the order of a a mod n n , for any fixed integer a ∉ { 0 , ± 1 } a\notin\{0,\pm 1\} .

Introduction

If
f
is a naturally-occurring integer-valued arithmetic function, it is reasonable to ask how the values of
f
are distributed in arithmetic progressions. Several results of this nature are collected in Narkiewicz’s monograph
[
Nar84
]
. In particular, Chapter IV of that reference describes in detail an easily-applicable criterion of Delange (appearing originally as
[
Del69
, Theorem 1]
) deciding, for each additive function
f
and each modulus
q
, whether
f
is uniformly distributed (UD) modulo
q
.

The situation for multiplicative functions is more subtle. Here Euler’s
\varphi
-function is a convenient reference point. It has long been known that for each fixed positive integer
q
, the relation
q\mid\varphi(n)
holds for almost all
n
, meaning for all but
o(x)
values of
n\leq x
as
x\to\infty
. (This, and much more, follows from arguments given below.) That is, the class of
0
modulo
q
‘hogs’ almost all the values of
\varphi(n)
, ruling out uniform distribution mod
q
except in the trivial case
q=1
. This phenomenon prompted Narkiewicz in
[
Nar67
]
to introduce a weaker notion of equidistribution:
f
is weakly uniformly distributed mod
q
(or
WUD
mod
q
) if (a)
\gcd(f(n),q)=1
for infinitely many
n
, and (b) for each coprime residue class
a\bmod{q}
,

(1.1)

\#\{n\leq x:f(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}\}\sim\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\#\{n\leq x:\gcd(f(n),q)=1\},\quad\text{as~$x\to\infty$}.

In the same paper, Narkiewicz gives a criterion for weak uniform distribution that may be applied to multiplicative functions that are ‘polynomial-like’ (in a sense we will not describe here). As applications, he classifies the moduli
q
for which
d(n)
(the divisor function), and
\varphi(n)
, are WUD mod
q
. For instance,
\varphi(n)
is WUD mod
q
precisely when
\gcd(q,6)=1
. For a full development of the theory of weak uniform distribution of polynomial-like multiplicative functions (incorporating later refinements by Narkiewicz and collaborators), see Chapters V and VI of the previously mentioned monograph
[
Nar84
]
.

The central object of study in this paper is Carmichael’s
\lambda
-function
[
Car19
]
, which is a close cognate of Euler’s function. Whereas
\varphi(n)
gives the
order
of the unit group
U(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
, Carmichael’s function
\lambda(n)
describes its
exponent
; that is,
\lambda(n)
is the smallest positive integer for which
a^{\lambda(n)}\equiv 1\pmod{n}
for each
a
coprime to
n
. The
\lambda
function is not multiplicative but is what might be called
lcm-multiplicative
:

(1.2)

\lambda(\mathrm{lcm}[m,n])=\mathrm{lcm}[\lambda(m),\lambda(n)],\quad\text{for all positive integers $m,n$.}

As known already to Gauss,
\lambda(p^{k})=\varphi(p^{k})
whenever
p^{k}
is an odd prime power,
\lambda(1)=\lambda(2)=1
,
\lambda(4)=2
, and
\lambda(2^{k})=2^{k-2}
for all
k\geq 3
; these values, along with the relation (
1.2
), determine
\lambda(n)
for all inputs
n
.

Several statistical properties of
\lambda
, such as its average, typical, and lower order, are investigated by Erdős, Pomerance, and Schmutz in
[
EPS91
]
. One takeaway from their study is that despite having similar definitions,
\varphi
and
\lambda
can behave quite differently. To give just one example: It is classical that
\varphi(n)\gg n/\log\log{(3n)}
for all
n
, whereas it is shown in
[
EPS91
]
that there is a sequence of
n
tending to infinity along which
\lambda(n)\leq(\log{n})^{O(\log\log\log{n})}
. As far as we are aware, there have not been prior investigations into the distribution of
\lambda(n)
in residue classes. It is easy to prove that
\lambda(n)
is even whenever
n\geq 3
, and so
\lambda(n)
cannot be WUD mod
q
if
2\mid q
. In our first theorem we prove that this is the only obstruction.

Theorem 1.1
.

\lambda
is WUD mod
q
for all odd
q
. In fact, as
x\to\infty
,

(1.3)

\#\{n\leq x:\lambda(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}\}\sim\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\#\{n\leq x:\gcd(\lambda(n),q)=1\},

uniformly for coprime residue classes
a\bmod{q}
with
q
odd and
q\leq\log\log\log{x}
.

The range of uniformity in Theorem
1.1
is rather modest (to put it mildly). If we assume that
3\nmid q
, we can do much better.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix
A>0
. As
x\to\infty
, the relation (
1.3
) holds uniformly for moduli
q\leq(\log{x})^{A}
with
\gcd(q,6)=1
.

Our next two theorems are of a slightly different nature. To set the stage, fix an integer

. Let

be a positive integer, and let

be a positive real number. We say that

begins with

in base

are the base

. A sequence

is uniformly distributed modulo

. It is useful to rephrase this conclusion using Weyl’s criterion. For each integer

, for each

. This criterion was recently used in

to study Benford behavior of sequences described by multiplicative functions. For example, it was shown there that the sequences
\{\varphi(n)\}
and

(with

.

.

Let

. For each positive integer

, let

. In our final theorem we show that, under the assumption of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis

with

(conditional on GRH)
.

Fix an integer

with

. For each fixed nonzero real number

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\

We conclude this introduction with a word about the proofs. The UD and WUD criteria of Delange and Narkiewicz alluded to in the introduction are proved by recasting the respective problems in terms of mean values of multiplicative functions of modulus not exceeding
1
. (For additive functions, this involves composing with an additive character, while for multiplicative functions one uses Dirichlet characters.) The authors of
[
CLPSR
]
adopt a similar perspective in their work on Benford’s law (note that if
f
is positive-valued and multiplicative, then
f^{i\theta}
is a multiplicative function of modulus
1
, for every real
\theta
). Such an approach allows one to bring to bear powerful tools such as Halász’s theorem.

Since
\lambda
is not multiplicative, we must take a different tack. In recent work with Singha Roy
[
PSR
]
, we proposed an alternative method of proving UD and WUD theorems. This was used to show (among other things) that for
f=\varphi
, the relation (
1.1
) holds uniformly for
q\leq(\log{x})^{A}
with
\gcd(q,6)=1
. Theorem
1.2
is proved in §
3
using these same ideas. Here it is important that the contribution of large primes to
\lambda(n)
can usually be computed as if

were multiplicative; see the discussion following (

).

The arguments for Theorem
1.2
fail to establish weak uniform distribution when

but do show (as described in §
4
) that a failure of (

) in this case entails the nonuniform distribution of
\lambda(n)
mod

(among

, thus proving Theorem

. This requires bringing in certain anatomical facts of the kind that frequently arise when studying Euler’s
\varphi
-function. For instance, it may be instructive to compare the proof of our Lemma

with that of Theorem 8 in

.

Theorems

and

are proved in §

. The proof of Theorem

is a fairly straightforward adaptation of the arguments in §

. The proof of Theorem

to denote the greatest common divisor of

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

Proof.

By the Chinese remainder theorem, we can assume

is a prime power with

\frac{1}{\varphi(p^{k})}\sum_{\chi\bmod{p^{k}}}\left(\sum_{a\bmod p^{k}}\chi_{0}(a)\chi(a-1)\right)^{J}\bar{\chi}(r).

Here the principal character

contributes
\varphi_{2}(p^{k})^{J}/\varphi(p^{k})

\sum_{a\bmod p^{k}}\chi_{0}(a)\chi(a-1)=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod p^{k}\\

The residue classes

\frac{1}{\varphi(p^{k})}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\bmod{p^{k}}\\

We begin the proof of Theorem

We may assume that

4.
Interlude

Our proof of Theorem

does not yield weak uniform distribution when

. However, one can show by these methods that the only obstruction to weak uniform distribution mod

arises from an obstruction modulo

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix

, and

In the remainder of this section we describe how to adapt the proof of Theorem
1.2
to obtain Proposition

. Note that by Lemmas

and
3.2

it suffices to establish the “convenient version” of (

), where

is restricted to covenient values in both sets.

We start from (

to
\lambda(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}
, up to an error of

. The new twist is that when

, the sets

are no longer uniformly of size
\sim\varphi_{2}(q)^{J}/\varphi(q)
. In fact, since each

has

.

Suppose we are in the case that

\#\mathcal{V}_{m,\textbf{d}}(q_{0})=(1+o(1))\varphi_{2}(q_{0})^{J}/\varphi(q_{0})

We can recast the compatibility condition by by referring back to (

Here we have used that each

Combining the observations of the previous two paragraphs, we find that (

\frac{(2+o(1))}{\varphi(q)}\left[\frac{1}{J!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\

The bracketed expression is precisely analogous to (

with

.

(d^{\prime},2q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{g(d^{\prime})}{\varphi(d^{\prime})}+\frac{4}{3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}\text{ $w$-smooth}\\
d^{\prime}\equiv-\epsilon\pmod{3}\\
(d^{\prime},2q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{g(d^{\prime})}{\varphi(d^{\prime})}.

Hence, with

denoting the nontrivial character mod

\Bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\text{ $w$-smooth}\\
d\equiv 1\pmod{3}\\
(d,q)=1,~2\mid d\end{subarray}}\frac{g(d_{\mathrm{odd}})}{\varphi(d)}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\text{ $w$-smooth}\\
d\equiv-1\pmod{3}\\
(d,q)=1,~2\mid d\end{subarray}}\frac{g(d_{\mathrm{odd}})}{\varphi(d)}\Bigg|=\frac{2}{3}\Bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}\text{ $w$-smooth}\\
(d^{\prime},2q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(d^{\prime})g(d^{\prime})}{\varphi(d^{\prime})}\Bigg|.

We develop the sum on

as an Euler product, noting that

:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}\text{ $w$-smooth}\\
(d^{\prime},2q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(d^{\prime})g(d^{\prime})}{\varphi(d^{\prime})}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq w\\
p\nmid 2q\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{\chi(p)g(p)}{p-1}\left(1-\frac{\chi(p)}{p}\right)^{-1}\right).

The

th term in our right-hand product has size
1+\chi(p)/p+O(1/p^{2})
. Hence, the product is
\ll\exp(\sum_{p\leq w,~p\nmid 2q}\chi(p)/p)
, which is
\ll\log_{2}{(3q)}\exp(\sum_{p\leq w}\chi(p)/p)\ll\log_{2}{(3q)}
, using in the last step the convergence of
\sum_{p}\chi(p)/p
. It follows that the difference between (
5.8
) and half of (
5.9
) is

\ll\log_{2}(3q)\cdot W\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
(\lambda(m),q)=1\\
L_{m}<x/m\end{subarray}}(\mathrm{Li}(x/m)-\mathrm{Li}(L_{m}))\ll\log_{2}(3q)\cdot W\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
(\lambda(m),q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{x\sqrt{\log_{3}{x}}}{m\log{x}}\\
\ll\frac{x}{(\log{x})^{1-\alpha}}\exp(O((\log_{2}{(3q)})^{2}))\cdot W\log_{2}{(3q)}\sqrt{\log_{3}{x}}.

As

and

, this last expression is
o(N/(\log_{3}{x})^{1/3})
and so in particular
o(N)
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

6.1.
Proof of Theorem

By Lemma

are convenient. Furthermore, among convenient

As in §

(Here and below, we allow implied constants to depend on the fixed parameter
\theta

will show that the accumulation of errors terms is, in total,

W\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
L_{m}<x/m\end{subarray}}\lambda(m)^{i\theta}I_{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\text{ $w$-smooth}\\
2\mid d\end{subarray}}\frac{g(d_{\mathrm{odd}})}{\varphi(d)d^{i\theta}},

with

as in (

The sum on

, for each odd

(cf.
[

Collecting estimates shows that

\ll W\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
L_{m}<x/m\end{subarray}}|I_{m}|\ll W\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
L_{m}<x/m\end{subarray}}\frac{x}{m\log(x/m)}\ll\frac{xW\sqrt{\log_{3}{x}}}{\log{x}}\sum_{m\leq x}\frac{1}{m}\ll xW\sqrt{\log_{3}{x}}\ll\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log_{3}{x}}}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
