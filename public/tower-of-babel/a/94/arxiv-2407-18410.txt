IDEAL characterization of vacuum pp-waves
Igor Khavkine, David McNutt, Lode Wylleman

Abstract
An IDEAL characterization of a particular spacetime metric, g 0 g_{0} , consists of a set of tensorial equations T ⁡ [ g ] = 0 T[g]=0 arising from expressions constructed from the metric, g g , its curvature tensor and its covariant derivatives and which are satisfied if and only if g g is locally isometric to the original metric g 0 g_{0} . Earlier applications of the IDEAL classification of spacetimes relied on the construction of particular scalar polynomial curvature invariants as an important step in the procedure. In this paper we investigate the well-known class of vacuum pp-wave spacetimes, where all scalar polynomial curvature invariants vanish, and determine the applicability of an IDEAL classification for these spacetimes. We consider a modification of the IDEAL approach which permits a corresponding extension of the Stewart-Walker lemma. With this change, we are able to construct invariants and IDEAL-ly classify all of the vacuum pp-wave solutions which admit a two- or higher-dimensional isometry group, with the exception of one case.

2
IDEAL characterization

As was alluded to in the Introduction, an IDEAL characterization of a
spacetime is a local invariant condition that uses only the metric tensor and
quantities that can be covariantly obtained from it. More specifically,
an ideal characterization of a geometry
(M,g_{0})
is a list of tensorial
equations

\mathbf{T}_{k}[g]=0,\quad k=1,2,\ldots,N,

(1)

constructed covariantly out of a metric
g
and its derivatives
(concomitants of the Riemann tensor) that are satisfied on a small
neigborhood of a point if and only if that neighborhood is isomorphic to
a neighborhood of a point of
(M,g_{0})
. In this case, we say that the
tensor identities (
1
) characterize the local
isometry class of
(M,g_{0})
. We also allow the possibility that
g_{0}
is
a family of (possibly non-isometric) metrics and the tensor
identities (
1
) imply that
g
is locally isometric
to some member of that family. In such cases, we speak of an IDEAL
characterization of the whole family.

The most classical example is of spaces of locally constant curvature,
characterized by the identity

R_{abcd}=\alpha\left(g_{ac}g_{bd}-g_{ad}g_{bc}\right),

(2)

where
\alpha
is a constant; in relativity it is re-expressed via the
cosmological constant
\Lambda=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}\alpha
, in dimensions
n>2
.
Of course
\alpha=0
corresponds to the locally flat case. The

locally
attribute is important because (
2
) as any
IDEAL characterization (
1
) is a fundamentally local
condition, which is insensitive to the global topology of the spacetime.
Other notable geometries that have been given IDEAL characterizations
include Schwarzschild
[
12
]
(including in higher
dimensions
[
25
]
) and Kerr
[
13
]
black holes, as well
as FLRW
[
6
]
cosmological solutions. A growing list of other
examples can be gleaned from the references in the Introduction.

For the sake of terminology, let
IDEAL tensors
refer to the
left-hand sides of (
1
). If
v
is a vector field
and
\mathbf{T}[g]
is tensor covariantly constructed from the metric, then the
identity (sometimes known as the
Stewart-Walker
lemma

[
34
]
)
\dot{\mathbf{T}}_{g}[\mathcal{L}_{v}g]=\mathcal{L}_{v}\mathbf{T}[g]
, where

\mathbf{T}[g+h]=\mathbf{T}[g]+\dot{\mathbf{T}}_{g}[h]+O(h^{2})
, immediately links an
IDEAL characterization to linear gauge invariant observables. Namely, if

\mathbf{T}[g]
is one of the IDEAL tensors for
g
, then
\dot{\mathbf{T}}_{g}[\mathcal{L}_{v}g]=0
implies that
\dot{\mathbf{T}}_{g}[h]
is invariant under local
linearized gauge transformations (diffeomorphisms) of linearized gravity
about the background
g
. In fact, linearizing all the IDEAL tensors for
a given
g
yields a good candidate for a complete set of linear gauge
invariants on that background
[
23
,
17
,
24
]
. We
consider this connection to linear gauge invariants through
linearization to be one of the important applications of IDEAL
characterizations.

Later, we will find that the limitations on how IDEAL tensors
\mathbf{T}[g]

are to be constructed to be slightly restrictive. For instance,
\mathbf{A}[g]

and
\mathbf{B}[g]
may be two such tensors, of equal valence say, that become
proportional,
\mathbf{A}[g]=X[g]\mathbf{B}[g]
, for some subclass of metrics. Taking
derivatives and tensor products of
\mathbf{A}[g]
and
\mathbf{B}[g]
, it might be
possible to impose some additional limited set of polynomial conditions
on the scalar
X[g]
and its derivatives, but non-polynomial conditions
would certainly be impossible. That is a significant limitation while
trying to characterize all isometry classes of pp-wave spacetimes.
However, supposing that the proportionality factor
X[g]
exists, and
using it in further non-polynomial conditions seems to be sufficient to
do the job. A natural question then: is this step in the
spirit of IDEAL characterizations?

The E in the acronym stands for Explicit, so with
X[g]
being only
implicitly defined, the strict answer seems to be No. However, if
X[g]

exists, it is defined uniquely. Moreover, as we shall prove below,
when
X[g]
is defined, it still satisfies the Stewart-Walker lemma, thus
preserving the connection with linear gauge invariants that we described
above. Let us make the last statement more precise with the following
lemma, where a scalar
X[g]
is just a special case of a tensor field

\mathbf{X}[g]
.

Lemma 1

(extended Stewart-Walker)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
be tensors covariantly constructed
from the metric. Suppose the background metric
g_{ab}
is chosen so
that
\mathbf{B}\neq 0
and the relation
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{B}
holds for some tensor
\mathbf{X}
. Then there exists a
differential operator
\dot{\mathbf{X}}[h]
acting on metric
perturbations
h_{ab}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{v}\mathbf{X}=\dot{\mathbf{X}}[\mathcal{L}_{v}g]
for any vector field
v^{a}
.

Proof.

Let us denote the linearization by a dot, as in
\mathbf{A}_{g+h}=\mathbf{A}_{g}+\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{g}[h]+O(h^{2})
. In general,

\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{X}
may all have different
numbers of indices (including no indices), say
\mathbf{A}_{c_{1}\cdots c_{k}:a_{1}\cdots a_{j}}
and
\mathbf{B}_{b_{1}\cdots b_{j}}
, with

\mathbf{X}_{c_{1}\cdots c_{k}}
, and where
:
is used only to visually
separate groups of indices. Let us define the operation

(\mathbf{A}\wedge\mathbf{B})_{c_{1}\cdots c_{k}:a_{1}\cdots a_{j}:b_{1}\cdots b_{j}}=\mathbf{A}_{c_{1}\cdots c_{k}:a_{1}\cdots a_{j}}\mathbf{B}_{b_{1}\cdots b_{j}}-\mathbf{A}_{c_{1}\cdots c_{k}:b_{1}\cdots b_{j}}\mathbf{B}_{a_{1}\cdots a_{j}}.

(3)

By elementary linear algebra, the relation
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{B}
between
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
holds iff

\mathbf{A}\wedge\mathbf{B}
= 0. It is also convenient to note the
antisymmetry identity
(\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{B})\wedge\mathbf{C}=-(\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{C})\wedge\mathbf{B}
. Linearizing the
identity
\mathbf{A}\wedge\mathbf{B}=0
about the chosen background
metric
g_{ab}
, we find (dropping all
g
subscripts)

\dot{\mathbf{A}}\wedge\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{A}\wedge\dot{\mathbf{B}}=0.

(4)

Using antisymmetry and bilinearity of
\wedge
, as well as the background
relation
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{B}
, we arrive at

(\dot{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{X}\otimes\dot{\mathbf{B}})\wedge\mathbf{B}=0,

(5)

which implies that there must exist an operator
\dot{\mathbf{X}}[h]

such that
\dot{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{X}\otimes\dot{\mathbf{B}}=\dot{\mathbf{X}}\otimes\mathbf{B}
. The notation
\dot{\mathbf{X}}
is
just formal, since
\mathbf{X}
itself is not defined for arbitrary
background metrics.

Next, we fix a vector field
v^{a}
and recall the Stewart-Walker lemma,

\mathcal{L}_{v}\mathbf{A}=\dot{\mathbf{A}}[\mathcal{L}_{v}g]
and
\mathcal{L}_{v}\mathbf{B}=\dot{\mathbf{B}}[\mathcal{L}_{v}g]
. Taking the Lie derivative of the relation
between
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
obtains the relation

0=\mathcal{L}_{v}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{B})\\
=(\dot{\mathbf{A}}[\mathcal{L}_{v}g]-\mathbf{X}\otimes\dot{\mathbf{B}}[\mathcal{L}_{v}g])-(\mathcal{L}_{v}\mathbf{X})\otimes\mathbf{B}=(\dot{\mathbf{X}}[\mathcal{L}_{v}g]-\mathcal{L}_{v}\mathbf{X})\otimes\mathbf{B},

(6)

which implies that
\mathcal{L}_{v}\mathbf{X}=\dot{\mathbf{X}}[\mathcal{L}_{v}g]
,
since
\mathbf{B}\neq 0
.
∎

When
\mathbf{X}[g]
is defined implicitly as in Lemma
1
, we
will nevertheless refer to the corresponding
\dot{\mathbf{X}}_{g}[h]
as the

linearization
of
\mathbf{X}[g]
about the metric
g_{ab}
. Thus, since
the connection to linear gauge invariants through linearization is
important, for the purposes of this work a characterization of a spacetime geometry will still be considered to be in the spirit of
IDEAL characterization if it is IDEAL
except
for explicitly using
an implicitly defined tensor like
\mathbf{X}[g]
.

In light of the extended Stewart-Walker lemma, we will refer to a tensors defined implicitly from two or more IDEAL tensors as

Definition 1

(conditional invariants)
.

Given two tensors
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
which are covariantly constructed from the metric, if there is a tensor of lower rank,
{\bf X}
, such that
{\bf A}={\bf X}\otimes{\bf B}
, then
{\bf X}
is a
conditional invariant (tensor)
. If the rank is zero, then
X
is a
conditional invariant (scalar)
.

Conditional invariants, tensors
{\bf X}
or scalars
X
, will be determined from tensors constructed from the curvature tensor and its covariant derivatives.

Vacuum pp-waves

Plane-fronted gravitational waves with parallel rays,
in short

, can be defined as follows.

Definition 2
.

A pp-wave is a spacetime that admits a covariantly
constant null vector field,
\ell_{a}
:

\nabla_{a}\ell_{b}=0,\quad\ell^{a}\ell_{a}=0.

(7)

These spacetimes made their first appearance in the work of

. Subsequently, they have been widely

[

. For the rest of this section, we
restrict to

vacuum pp-wave has the line element
[

\mathrm{d}{s}^{2}=2\mathrm{d}\zeta\mathrm{d}\bar{\zeta}-2\mathrm{d}{u}\mathrm{d}{v}-2H\mathrm{d}{u}^{2},\quad H\equiv H(u,\zeta,\bar{\zeta})=f(\zeta,u)+\bar{f}(\bar{\zeta},u),

(8)

where
u,v
are two real coordinates and
\zeta=x+iy
is a
convenient complex coordinate, while
H(u,\zeta,\bar{\zeta})
is real
harmonic in
\zeta
and jointly smooth, justifying its presentation in
terms of the complex holomorphic in
\zeta
and jointly smooth

f(\zeta,u)
,
f_{,\bar{\zeta}}=0
. The
1
-forms

\bm{\ell}=\mathrm{d}{u},\quad\mathbf{n}=\mathrm{d}{v}+H\mathrm{d}{u},\quad\mathbf{m}=\mathrm{d}\zeta,\quad\bar{\mathbf{m}}=\mathrm{d}\bar{\zeta}

(9)

constitute a complex null coframe dual to the compex null frame of
vector fields

\ell=-\partial_{v},\quad n=-\partial_{u}+H\partial_{v},\quad m=\partial_{\bar{\zeta}},\quad\bar{m}=\partial_{\zeta},

(10)

normalized as
\ell^{a}n_{a}=n^{a}\ell_{a}=-1
,
m^{a}\bar{m}_{a}=\bar{m}^{a}m_{a}=1
, and all other contractions vanishing.

After fixing the form (
8
) of the metric, the residual
gauge freedom (coming from coordinate transformations or
underdeterminedness of the parameters) consists of the continuous
transformations

\displaystyle\zeta^{\prime}=e^{i\beta}(\zeta+h(u)),\quad u^{\prime}=(u+u_{0})/a,\quad v^{\prime}=a\,[v+\partial_{u}(h(u)\bar{\zeta}+\bar{h}(u)\zeta)+g(u)],

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\zeta^{\prime},u^{\prime})=a^{2}\,\left[f(\zeta,u)-\partial_{u}^{2}\bar{h}(u)\zeta+\tfrac{1}{2}\left|\partial_{u}\bar{h}(u)\right|^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{u}g(u)+ic(u)\right],

(11a)

where
\beta
,

,

are real constants,

and

are

is a smooth complex function, and the

\zeta^{\prime}=\bar{\zeta},\quad v^{\prime}=v,\quad u^{\prime}=u,\quad f^{\prime}=\bar{f},

(11b)

where
\bar{f}(\zeta,u)=\overline{f(\bar{\zeta},u)}
. The free choice
of

or
c(u)
and
g(u)
, respectively, allows us to adjust
both
\partial_{u}^{2}\bar{h}(u)
and
\frac{1}{2}\left|\partial_{u}\bar{h}(u)\right|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{u}g(u)+ic(u)
to be

The above line element (

\mathbf{K}
is a conditional invariant tensor and is the main geometric quantity that we will use

\mathbf{K}\cdot{\bf A}=A_{a_{1}...a_{k}}K^{a_{k}}.

The simplest invariant is just

\displaystyle\left(I_{\mathrm{a}}^{(2)}\right)^{4}\mathbf{T}

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{a}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\frac{1}{2}}}{|f_{,\zeta\zeta}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}(\ln f_{,\zeta\zeta})_{,u},

The vacuum pp-waves in four dimensions (4D) are characterized by the following

Theorem 7

(pp-wave isometry classes)
.

Consider a point
x\in M
, where
(M,g)
belongs to one of the highly
symmetric regular (Definition
5
) vacuum pp-wave
geometries from Proposition
3
. Then there exists a neighborhood of
x
such
that this neighborhood is isometric to exactly one of the classes listed
in Table
1
and
Figure
1
. These classes are identified by
invariant parameters and/or a signature function
F
defined on a domain

U
. The domain
U
needs to be chosen to correspond to the needed
neighborhood of
x
. Limiting values of the invariant parameters allow
for transitions between different classes according to the flowchart in
Figure
1
.

Proof.

We will proceed by going through the flowchart in
Figure
1
step by step, in the order of the
numbers indicated next to each node. In the corresponding numbered part
of the proof, we give some invariant conditions that select a subclass
of formfactor functions
f(\zeta,u)
. These subclasses are then broken
up into individual isometry classes under some non-degeneracy
conditions. The isometry classes and their invariant parameter
corresponding to that node in Figure
1
are
precisely recorded by representative formfactors in
Table
1
. Then, to be exhaustive, the ways
in which the formfactors
f(\zeta,u)
fail to satisfy the non-degeneracy
conditons, if any, are broken up into cases, which correspond to the
arrows leaving the given node in Figure
1
and
are discussed separately. This way, we show that all of the highly
symmetric formfactor families listed in (
44
) are exhausted
by our analysis.

The functional forms of
f(u,\zeta)
listed in (
44
) are
not all mutually exclusive and each one may contain multiple
individual isometry classes within it. Besides
G_{1}
, the most generic subclasses
are
G_{2a}
,
G_{2b}
,
G_{2c}
and
G_{5}
, each containing functional
degrees of freedom. The remaining subclasses will be specializations
thereof. Invariant parameters that label an isometry class will appear
in relations among differential invariants (
classifying
or

signature
relations). We know that we have isolated an individual
isometry class when the integration parameters of the corresponding set
of classifying relations can be completely absorbed by residual gauge
freedom. A combination of
f(\zeta,u)
and its derivatives can be
checked to be invariant under residual gauge freedom by direct
inspection or, as a shortcut, by realizing it as a component of one of
the tensor or conditional curvature invariants that we have defined in
Section
3
.

To start, note that
f_{,\zeta\zeta}=0
is equivalent to
\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}=0
(
16
), which together with the vacuum condition
implies that the spacetime is locally Minkowski. Hence we can presume
below that
f_{,\zeta\zeta}\neq 0
.

1.

The invariant relation

(\ln f_{,\zeta\zeta})_{,\zeta}=\frac{f_{,\zeta\zeta\zeta}}{f_{,\zeta\zeta}}=0

(57)

integrates to
f(\zeta,u)=A(u)\zeta^{2}+A_{1}(u)\zeta+A_{0}(u)
, where
we can set
A_{1}(u)=A_{0}(u)
by gauge freedom, which lands us in the

G_{5}
subclass. Rewriting
A(u)=e^{B(u)}
, the combinations
\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}
and
\ddot{B}e^{-\operatorname{Re}B}
are invariant under the
continuous gauge symmetries. The discrete gauge symmetry
\zeta\mapsto\bar{\zeta}
changes the signs of
\operatorname{Im}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}

and
\operatorname{Im}\ddot{B}e^{-\operatorname{Re}B}
, while
u\mapsto-u
changes the sign of

\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}
. Generically,
\partial_{u}(\operatorname{Re}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B})\neq 0
, so there exists a signature function

F=(F_{\operatorname{Re}},F_{\operatorname{Im}})\colon U\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with

F_{\operatorname{Im}}(y)\geq 0
, such that

\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}\ddot{B}e^{-\operatorname{Re}B}\\
(\operatorname{Im}\dot{B})^{2}e^{-\operatorname{Re}B}\end{pmatrix}=F\left(\operatorname{Re}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}\right).

(58)

Given any particular solution
B_{F}(u)
, symmetries dictate that the
general 3-parameter solution is

B(u)=B_{F}(a(u-u_{0}))+2\ln a+2i\beta\quad\text{or}\quad\overline{B_{F}(a(u-u_{0}))}+2\ln a-2i\beta,

(59)

where the integration constants
a,\beta,u_{0}
and the complex
conjugaton can be absorbed by corresponding gauge transformations. Hence
the signature function
F
completely describes the corresponding
isometry class, which we label by
G_{5^{\circ};F}
. The choice
F_{\operatorname{Re}}(y)=y^{2}/2
implies
\operatorname{Re}B=-2\ln|u-u_{0}|+\ln 4\alpha
, which implies
constant
\operatorname{Re}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}=\pm\alpha^{-1/2}
, which
must be excluded to be consistent with our genericity assumption.

The only possibilities left to consider are those with constant

\operatorname{Re}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}\neq 0
and
\operatorname{Re}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}=0
.

2.

The condition

\operatorname{Re}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}=0

(60)

integrates to a constant
\operatorname{Re}B(u)=2\ln a
. Generically then,

\partial_{u}(\operatorname{Im}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B})\neq 0
, so there exists a
signature function
F\colon U\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, with

F(-y)=-F(y)
because both
\operatorname{Im}B
and
-\operatorname{Im}B
must be solutions, such
that

\operatorname{Im}\ddot{B}e^{-\operatorname{Re}B}=F\left(\operatorname{Im}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}\right).

(61)

Given any particular solution
\operatorname{Im}B_{F}(u)
, symmetries dictate that the
general 2-parameter solution is

B(u)=i\operatorname{Im}B_{F}(a(u-u_{0}))+2\ln a+2i\beta\quad\text{or}\quad-i\operatorname{Im}B_{F}(a(u-u_{0}))+2\ln a-2i\beta.

(62)

All the integration constants
a,\beta,u_{0}
and the complex
conjugation can be absorbed by corresponding gauge transformations.
Hence, the signature function
F
completely describes the corresponding
isometry class, which we label by
G_{5^{\prime};\infty,F}
. The choice
F(y)=0

implies
\operatorname{Im}B=2a\lambda(u-u_{0})
, which implies constant

\operatorname{Im}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}=2\lambda
, which must be excluded to
be consistent with our genericity assumption.

The only possibility left to consider is that with constant
\operatorname{Im}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}
.

3.

Let us parametrize

\operatorname{Re}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}=\pm\alpha^{-1/2}

(63)

with constant
\alpha>0
, which integrates to
\operatorname{Re}B(u)=-2\ln|u-u_{0}|+\ln 4\alpha
with integration constant
u_{0}
, with the
+
and
-

signs corresponding to
u<u_{0}
and
u>u_{0}
respectively. Generically then,

\partial_{u}(\operatorname{Im}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B})\neq 0
, so there exists a
signature function
F\colon U\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, with

F(-y)=-F(y)
because both
\operatorname{Im}B
and
-\operatorname{Im}B
must be solutions, such
that

\operatorname{Im}\ddot{B}e^{-\operatorname{Re}B}=F\left(\operatorname{Im}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}\right).

(64)

Given any particular solution
\operatorname{Im}B_{F}(u-u_{0})
, symmetries dictate that the
general 2-parameter solution is

B(u)=i\operatorname{Im}B_{F}(a(u-u_{0}))-2\ln|u-u_{0}|+\ln 4\alpha+2i\beta\\
\text{or}\quad-i\operatorname{Im}B_{F}(a(u-u_{0}))-2\ln|u-u_{0}|+\ln 4\alpha-2i\beta.

(65)

All the integration constants
a,\beta,u_{0}
and the complex
conjugation can be absorbed by corresponding gauge transformations.
Hence, the signature function
F
completely describes the corresponding
isometry class, which we label by
G_{5^{\prime};\alpha,F}
. The choice
F(y)=-y/(2\alpha^{1/2})
implies
\operatorname{Im}B=2\kappa\ln|u-u_{0}|
, which implies
constant
\operatorname{Im}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}=\kappa\alpha^{-1/2}
, which
must be excluded to be consistent with our genericity assumption.

The only possibility left to consider is that with constant
\operatorname{Im}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}
.

Taking the invariant parameter limit
\alpha\to\infty
, accompanied by
the gauge parameter limit
u_{0}\to\infty
, such that
4\alpha/u_{0}^{2}\to 1
, we recover the
G_{5^{\prime};\infty,F}
subclass.

4.

The condition

\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}=2i\lambda\iff\operatorname{Re}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}=0,\quad\operatorname{Im}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}=2\lambda

(66)

integrates to
B(u)=2\ln a+2ia\lambda|u-u_{0}|
with integration
constants
a
,
u_{0}
, which can be absorbed by the corresponding
continuous gauge freedom. Under
\zeta\mapsto\bar{\zeta}
,
\lambda\mapsto-\lambda
, so we can choose to represent the isometry class
with
\lambda\geq 0
. Hence, the above condition already completely
describes the corresponding isometry class, which we label by

G_{6b;\lambda}
.

5.

The condition

\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}=(i\kappa\pm 1)\alpha^{-1/2}\\
\iff\operatorname{Re}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}=\pm\alpha^{-1/2},\quad\operatorname{Im}\dot{B}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}B}=\kappa\alpha^{-1/2},

(67)

with
\alpha>0
, integrates to

B(u)=i\beta+\ln 4\alpha+2(i\kappa\operatorname{sgn}(u-u_{0})-1)\ln|u-u_{0}|

(68)

with integration constants
\beta
,
u_{0}
, which can be absorbed by the
corresponding continuous gauge freedom. The
+
and
-
signs correspond
to
u<u_{0}
and
u>u_{0}
respectively, with the differrence between the
two cases absorbed by the
u\mapsto-u
residual gauge transformation,
after fixing
u_{0}=0
. Without loss of generality, we can then assume
that
u>0
. Under
\zeta\mapsto\bar{\zeta}
,
\kappa\mapsto-\kappa
,
so we can choose to represent the isometry class with
\kappa\geq 0
.

(IDEAL characterization of pp-waves)

5
Discussion

We have investigated the necessity of traditional scalar polynomial curvature invariants in the IDEAL approach to the classification of spacetimes, by considering the class of vacuum pp-wave spacetimes. These spacetimes belong to the larger class of VSI spacetimes for which all polynomial scalar curvature invariants vanish.

To hold with the primary principles of the IDEAL approach, we relaxed the constraint on the construction of scalar invariants by including conditional invariant tensors and more specifically, conditional invariant scalars as permitted quantities. A conditional invariant tensor or scalar arises when two IDEAL tensors are proportional. With this modification, we are able to characterize all of the highly-symmetric vacuum pp-wave spacetimes in an IDEAL manner and more finely classify these spacetimes into formfactor classes.

For a given highly symmetric vacuum pp-wave formfactor, there may be parameters or functions that must be fixed to specify a particular metric. This presents the problem of sub-classification within a given formfactor class and whether the IDEAL approach presented here is applicable. We have attempted to determine a minimal set of IDEAL quantities that will pick out the parameters and functions in the metric formfactor for each of the possible symmetry sub-classes. This is possible to do for almost all of the formfactor classes, except in the case of
G_{3d;\kappa,\gamma}
where the constant
\gamma
only appears in the form of
e^{2i\gamma}
in every conditional invariant. Thus, the two cases where
e^{i\gamma}=\pm 1
cannot be distinguished using an IDEAL approach. In every other subclass, it is possible to uniquely characterize a solution in terms of IDEAL quantities.
