Landau theory for finite-time dynamical phase transitions
Jan Meibohm, Massimiliano Esposito

Abstract
We study the time evolution of thermodynamic observables that characterise the dissipative nature of thermal relaxation after an instantaneous temperature quench. Combining tools from stochastic thermodynamics and large-deviation theory, we develop a powerful theory for computing the large-deviation statistics of such observables. Our method naturally leads to a description in terms of a dynamical Landau theory, a versatile tool for the analysis of finite-time dynamical phase transitions. The topology of the associated Landau potential allows for an unambiguous identification of the dynamical order parameter and of the phase diagram. As an immediate application of our method, we show that the probability distribution of the heat exchanged between a mean-field spin model and the environment exhibits a singular point, a kink, caused by a finite-time dynamical phase transition. Using our Landau theory, we conduct a detailed study of the phase transition. Although the manifestation of the new transition is similar to that of a previously found finite-time transition in the magnetisation, the properties and the dynamical origins of the two turn out to be very different.

More generally, our analysis reveals that finite-time dynamical phase transitions occur in a variety of ways for different observables within the same system. This hints towards the existence of such transitions in a much wider range of setups and it indicates that finite-time dynamical phase transitions of different nature are an integral part of the non-equilibrium statistical mechanics of relaxation. The extended Hamiltonian method including the dynamical Landau theory we develop here is a powerful tool for the identification and classification of finite-time dynamical phase transitions of systems in the thermodynamic or weak-noise limit.

Background

In this section, we review the relevant background for our analysis. First, we define the Curie-Weiss model, a mean-field version of the Ising model, describe its equilibrium properties, and how we model its dynamics. We then explain the standard Hamiltonian method
[
80
,

,
81
]
and how it is used to analyse the statistics of the magnetisation of the model after a temperature quench. The post-quench dynamics of the magnetisation, previously discussed in Ref.
[
71
]
, exhibits a finite-time dynamical phase transition whose properties we review at the end of the section.

2.1
Curie-Weiss model

The Curie-Weiss model is a simplified caricature of a magnet where
N\to\infty
Ising spins
\sigma_{i}=\pm 1
at sites
i=1,\ldots,N
are coupled by an infinite-range, ferromagnetic interaction of strength
J/N>0
. The system is immersed in a heat bath at inverse temperature
\beta=1/(k_{\text{B}}T)
. Due to the mean-field nature of the interaction, we can write all microstates with equal numbers
N_{\pm}
of spins in the states
\pm 1
in terms of the total magnetisation
M=N_{+}-N_{-}
. The constrained free energy
F(M)
of the system at a given magnetisation
M
reads
[
27
]

\displaystyle F(M)=E(M)-\beta^{-1}S^{\text{int}}(M)\,,

(1)

where
E
the denotes internal energy

\displaystyle E(M)=-\frac{J}{2N}\left(M^{2}-N\right)\,.

(2)

An additional coupling
-HM
to an external field
H
is omitted here. In this field-free version of the model, the internal energy
E(M)
is entirely due to ferromagnetic interactions between the spins.

The dimensionless internal entropy
S^{\text{int}}(M)=\ln\Omega(M)
in (
1
) originates from the microscopic degeneracy of
M
:

\displaystyle\Omega(M)=\frac{N!}{[(N+M)/2]![(N-M)/2]!}\,.

(3)

State changes of the system are induced by thermal fluctuations of the heat bath, modelled by a stochastic dynamics for
M
. An arbitrary spin flip leads to a change
M\to M_{\pm}\equiv M\pm 2
in the magnetisation. The probability
P(M,t)
for finding the system in state
M
at time
t
obeys the evolution equation

\displaystyle\dot{P}(M,t)=\sum_{\eta=\pm}\left[W_{\eta}(M_{-\eta})P(M_{-\eta},t)-W_{\eta}(M)P(M,t)\right]\,,

(4)

with Arrhenius-type rates
W_{\pm}(M)
for the transitions
M\to M_{\pm}
, given by

\displaystyle W_{\pm}(M)=\frac{N\mp M}{2\tau}\exp\left[-\beta E_{\pm}(M)/2\right]\,.

(5)

Here,
\tau
denotes the microscopic relaxation time for a single spin flip and
E_{\pm}(M)=E(M_{\pm})-E(M)=-2J(\pm M+1)/N
is the change of internal energy
E
during the transition
M\to M_{\pm}
. The algebraic prefactor
(N\mp M)/2=N_{\mp}
in (
5
) reflects that all
N_{\mp}
microscopic transitions
\mp 1\to\pm 1
are equivalent. Furthermore, the transition rates obey the spin-flip symmetry
W_{\pm}(M)=W_{\mp}(-M)
and the detailed-balance condition
[
86
]

\displaystyle\frac{W_{\pm}(M)}{W_{\mp}(M_{\pm})}=\frac{P^{\text{eq}}(M_{\pm})}{P^{\text{eq}}(M)}=\exp\left[-\beta F_{\pm}(M)\right]\,,

(6)

where

\displaystyle P^{\text{eq}}(M)=Z(\beta)^{-1}\exp\left[-\beta F(M)\right]\,,

(7)

denotes the equilibrium distribution with partition function
Z(\beta)
. In (
6
), the change in the constrained free energy
F_{\pm}(M)
due to the transition
M\to M_{\pm}
reads
F_{\pm}(M)=F(M_{\pm})-F(M)
.

2.2
Thermodynamic equilibrium

We now take the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
. To this end, we define the intensive magnetisation
m\equiv M/N
per spin and the constrained free-energy density

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}(\beta,m)\equiv\lim_{N\to\infty}F(Nm)/N=\mathscr{E}(m)-\beta^{-1}\mathscr{S}^{\text{int}}(m),

(8)

with internal energy density

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}(m)=-Jm^{2}/2

(9)

and internal entropy per spin

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}^{\text{int}}(m)=-\sum_{\eta=\pm}\frac{1+\eta m}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+\eta m}{2}\right)\,.

(10)

The equilibrium distribution in (
7
) written as a function of
m=M/N
, takes the large-deviation form
[
35
,
83
,
84
]

\displaystyle P^{\text{eq}}(m)\smilefrown\exp\left[-N\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)\right]\,,

(11)

with equilibrium rate function
[
71
]

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)=\beta\left[\mathscr{F}(\beta,m)-\mathscr{\bar{F}}(\beta)\right]\,.

(12)

Here, the equilibrium free energy

\displaystyle\mathscr{\bar{F}}(\beta)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta N}\ln Z(\beta)=\min_{m}\mathscr{F}(\beta,m)

(13)

arises as a consequence of the normalisation of
P^{\text{eq}}(M)
in (
7
), which ensures that
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)
vanishes at its minima
\pm\bar{m}(\beta)
, i.e.,
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}[\bar{m}(\beta)]=0
. The magnetisation
\bar{m}(\beta)
at the minima reflects the typical, most likely magnetisation that occurs at inverse temperature
\beta
in the thermodynamic limit. According to (
11
), the probabilities for fluctuations of
m
away from
\pm\bar{m}(\beta)
are exponentially suppressed in
N
at a rate given by
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)
.

The shape of
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)
reflects the macroscopic behaviour of the system at equilibrium. This macroscopic behaviour is driven by the tendency of the system to minimise its free energy, which results in a competition between energetic and entropic contributions in Eq. (
8
) as
\beta
is varied. At small inverse temperatures, the entropy term
-\beta^{-1}\mathscr{S}^{\text{int}}(m)
, minimised at
m=0
, dominates. In this case,
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)
has a unique minimum at
\bar{m}(\beta)=0
, meaning that the contributions of up and down spins cancel each other and the system is said to be in a disordered state. Upon increasing
\beta
, however, the energy term
\mathscr{E}(m)
in Eq. (
8
), minimised at
m=\pm 1
, becomes important and
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)
changes its shape. In particular, at the critical inverse temperature
\beta_{\text{c}}=1/J
,
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)
passes from single-well shape into that of a symmetric double-well, reflecting a continuous equilibrium phase transition
[
28
]
. Consequently, for
\beta>\beta_{\text{c}}
the magnetisation
\bar{m}(\beta)
becomes finite and the system is said to be ordered, because
\bar{m}(\beta)>0
implies that a dominant fraction of spins is aligned in either direction.

(a) shows the phase diagram of the Curie-Weiss model at vanishing external field, determined by the different topologies of
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)
as
\beta
is varied.

The phase diagram exhibits two phases: a disordered single-mode (SM) phase (solid black line) for

Objects like

or

. The corresponding Landau theory

, aims to describe phase transitions by postulating phenomenological Landau potentials based on the microscopic symmetries of the problem. For the simple model we describe here, the functions
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)
and

can be derived explicitly, see (

).

The concept of Landau theory has proven useful also in non-equilibrium contexts

68

69

70
,

]
, where it serves to identify different non-equilibrium behaviours and dynamical order parameters. In Sec.

2.3
Post-quench dynamics

At time
t<0
we initialise the system in the CE phase at inverse temperature
\beta>\beta_{\text{c}}
, where
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}(m)
has double-well shape and
\bar{m}(\beta)>0
. At
t=0
, we impose an instantaneous temperature quench
\beta\to\beta_{\text{q}}
into the SM phase, i.e.,
\beta_{\text{q}}<\beta_{\text{c}}
. Such a quench is said to be “disordering” as it forces the system to cross the phase boundary between the SM and CE phases [orange arrow in Figure
1
(a)], inducing an order-to-disorder phase transition in the long-time limit
[
71
]
. Ergodicity ensures that
P(m,t)\to P^{\text{eq}}_{q}(m)\smilefrown\exp[-N\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}(m)]
as
t\to\infty
, where
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}(m)
is the equilibrium rate function given in (
12
), but at final inverse temperature
\beta_{\text{q}}
. For
t>0
, the time dependence of
P(m,t)
is characterised by the large-deviation form

\displaystyle P(m,t)\smilefrown\text{e}^{-NV(m,t)}\,,

(14)

with time-dependent rate function
V(m,t)
, whose evolution follows from an appropriate limit of (
4
). This limit is analysed using the standard Hamiltonian method
[
32
,
33
,
80
,

]

2.3.1
Standard Hamiltonian method

When the transition rates
W_{\pm}
are extensive in
N
, i.e.,

\displaystyle w_{\pm}(q)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{W_{\pm}(Nq)}{N}\,,\qquad 0<w_{\pm}(q)<\infty\,,

(15)

then (
4
) transforms into a Hamilton-Jacobi equation for
V(m,t)

[
80
,
81
]
,

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{t}V(m,t)+\mathscr{H}[m,\partial_{m}V(m,t)]\,,

(16)

by substituting (
14
) into (
4
) and taking the large-
N
limit. For the Curie-Weiss model, (
15
) is satisfied and the dynamical Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}
reads
[
85
,
71
]

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}(q,p)

\displaystyle=w_{+}(q)\left(\text{e}^{2p}-1\right)+w_{-}(q)\left(\text{e}^{-2p}-1\right)\,,

(17)

including the
N
-scaled transition rates

\displaystyle w_{\pm}(q)=\frac{1\mp q}{2\tau}\exp\left[\mp\beta_{\text{q}}\mathscr{E}^{\prime}(q)\right]=\frac{1\mp q}{2\tau}\exp\left(\pm\beta_{\text{q}}Jq\right)\,.

(18)

The initial condition of (
16
) is given by the equilibrium rate function
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}
before the quench,

\displaystyle V(m,0)=\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)\,.

(19)

Solutions to the Hamilton-Jacobi equation (
16
) are expressed in terms of the characteristics
q(s)
and
p(s)=\partial_{m}V[q(s),s]
,
0\leq s\leq t
, that solve the Hamilton equations
[
87
]

\displaystyle\dot{q}(s)=\partial_{p}\mathscr{H}(q,p)\,,\quad\dot{p}(s)=-\partial_{q}\mathscr{H}(q,p)\,,

(20)

with the condition that
q(s)
must end at
m
at the given (observation) time
t
:

\displaystyle q(t)=m\,.

(21a)

The initial condition for
p(s)
at
s=0
follows from (
19
):

\displaystyle p(0)=\partial_{m}V[q(0),0]=\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}m}\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}[q(0)]\,.

(21b)

In order to solve the Hamilton-Jacobi equation (
20
), we evaluate the total derivative of
V[q(s),s]
along
q(s)
:

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}s}V[q(s),s]=\partial_{m}V[q(s),s]\dot{q}(s)+\partial_{t}V[q(s),s]=p(s)\dot{q}(s)-\mathscr{H}[q(s),p(s)]\,,

(22)

where we have used the definition of
p(s)
and (
16
). Integrating (
22
) from
0
to
t
and using the boundary conditions (
21
), one obtains
V(m,t)
as an integral over the characteristics
[q(s),p(s)]_{0\leq s\leq t}

\displaystyle V(m,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}s\left[p\dot{q}-\mathscr{H}(q,p)\right]+\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}[q(0)]\,.

(23)

Because of the parity symmetry
m\to-m
of the problem, the dynamical Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}
in (
17
) is invariant under inversion
\mathscr{H}(q,p)=\mathscr{H}(-q,-p)
of
q
and
p
. Furthermore,
\mathscr{H}
satisfies a shift-inversion symmetry in
p
, with respect to the equilibrium rate function
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}
at inverse temperature
\beta_{\text{q}}
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}(q,p)=\mathscr{H}\left[q,-p+\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}m}\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}(q)\right]\,,

(24)

which follows from detailed balance
[
88
]
. For
p=0
, (
24
) directly yields
\mathscr{H}(q,0)=\mathscr{H}[q,\text{d}\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}(q)/\text{d}m]=0
, which implies, together with (
16
), that the equilibrium rate function
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}
at quenched inverse temperature
\beta_{\text{q}}
is invariant under time evolution.

Each pair
[q(s),p(s)]_{0\leq s\leq t}
of characteristics is associated with a fluctuation path that realises
q(t)=m
for given
m
and
t
. While the coordinate
q(s)
corresponds to the change in the magnetisation
m
as function of time, the conjugate coordinate
p(s)
quantifies, roughly speaking, the fluctuations of the environment required to realise
q(t)=m
. In particular, characteristic pairs with
p(s)=0
correspond to the typical macroscopic relaxation dynamics
\dot{q}(s)=2[w_{+}(q)-w_{-}(q)]
,
\dot{p}(s)=0
, i.e., the dynamics of the minima of
V(m,t)
.

When there are multiple pairs
[q(s),p(s)]_{0\leq s\leq t}
of characteristics that solve (
20
) for same boundary conditions (
21
), one must pick the pair that minimises
V(m,t)
, since the probabilities of all other solutions are exponentially suppressed
[
71
]
. The minimising characteristic
q(s)
represents the most probable way to realise the magnetisation
q(t)=m
at time
t
, called the
optimal fluctuation
for the event.

Finite-time dynamical phase transition in magnetisation

A sudden switch in the optimal fluctuation that realises vanishing magnetisation

at the critical time

gives rise to a finite-time dynamical phase transition associated with the magnetisation

in the Curie-Weiss model

,

. This switch can be visualised using the phase portrait of the Hamiltonian dynamics (

The dynamics (

) occurs along the level lines (shown as the brown lines) of

and has a single fixed point (white bullet) at

\displaystyle q_{\text{FP}}=p_{\text{FP}}=0\,,

(25)

where
\dot{q}=\dot{p}=0
. For infinite observation times (

), trajectories may approach the fixed point asymptotically along the stable manifold

\displaystyle p_{\text{s}}(q)=0\,,

(26)

shown as the black line with inwards-pointing arrows in Figure

\displaystyle p_{\text{u}}(q)=\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}m}\mathscr{V}_{\text{q}}^{\text{eq}}(q)=-\beta_{\text{q}}q+\frac{1}{2}[\log(1+q)-\log(1-q)]\,,

At small observation times

which resides at the fixed point (

3
Extended Hamiltonian method for observables

To study the finite-time statistics of observables other than the magnetisation
m
in the thermodynamic limit, we must generalise the Hamiltonian method described in Sec.
2.3.1
. Although we focus on thermodynamic observables in the Curie-Weiss model, the theory we develop here is generally applicable to systems with well-defined thermodynamic or weak-noise limits and for a larger class of observables (specified below). Every observable in the extended Hamiltonian method is connected to its own dynamical Landau potential. This potential is a powerful tool for the identification and the classification of dynamical phases and phase transitions, associated with a given observable.

3.1
Thermodynamic observables

The thermodynamic observables of the Curie-Weiss model and their statistics are the main objects of our study. In order to define them, we use the framework of stochastic thermodynamics
[
36
,
82
,
39
]
to endow the stochastic dynamics of the model with a thermodynamic interpretation. This allows us to identify fluctuating thermodynamic observables at the microscale, that are consistent with macroscopic thermodynamics in the limit of large system size.

Because the energy of the system does not change during the temperature quench, the work done on the system vanishes. Hence, the thermodynamics of the quench is determined by the statistics of the heat per spin
\mathscr{Q}
, released by the system into the environment, and the total entropy
\Sigma
produced (per spin) in the relaxation process.

The dimensionless released heat
\mathscr{Q}
is given by the negative energy change of the system, multiplied by
\beta_{\text{q}}
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}(m,m^{\prime})=-\beta_{\text{q}}[\mathscr{E}(m)-\mathscr{E}(m^{\prime})]\,.

(28)

Here,
m^{\prime}
and
m
denote two magnetisations before and after the quench, respectively. Both
m^{\prime}
and
m
are random variables which depend on the thermal fluctuations of the heat baths before and after the quench. In our dimensionless formulation,
\mathscr{Q}
is equal to the change in environment entropy per spin,
\mathscr{Q}=\Sigma^{\text{env}}
.

The probability distribution
P(\mathscr{Q},t)
of
\mathscr{Q}
is constrained by a detailed fluctuation relation
[
89
,
36
]

\displaystyle\frac{P(\mathscr{Q},t)}{P(-\mathscr{Q},t)}=\exp\left[-N(\beta/\beta_{\text{q}}-1)\mathscr{Q}\right]\,,

(29)

which relates the negative and positive branches of
P(\mathscr{Q},t)
. We prove (
29
) for the present setup in
A
.

The total entropy production
\Sigma
is the sum of the entropy changes of the environment
\Sigma^{\text{env}}
and of the system
\Sigma^{\text{sys}}
:

\displaystyle\Sigma(m,m^{\prime},t)=\Sigma^{\text{sys}}(m,m^{\prime},t)+\Sigma^{\text{env}}(m,m^{\prime})\,.

(30)

The change in system entropy
\Sigma^{\text{sys}}
, in turn, decomposes further into

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{sys}}(m,m^{\prime},t)=V(m,t)-\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m^{\prime})+\mathscr{S}^{\text{int}}(m)-\mathscr{S}^{\text{int}}(m^{\prime})\,.

(31)

Here, the last two terms represent the change in internal entropy (
10
) of the spin system. The first two terms constitute the change in state entropy
[
90
]
, related to the changing probability distribution during the relaxation after the quench. Using (
28
), (
31
) and (
8
), we conveniently rewrite (
30
) as

\displaystyle\Sigma(m,m^{\prime},t)=V(m,t)-\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m^{\prime})-\beta_{\text{q}}[\mathscr{F}(m)-\mathscr{F}(m^{\prime})]\,.

(32)

In this formulation, the total entropy production is the sum of the negative change in free energy density (last two terms) and the change in state entropy at time
t
(first two terms). Note that due to the latter contribution,
\Sigma(m,m^{\prime},t)
depends explicitly on time, while
\mathscr{Q}(m,m^{\prime})
does not.

3.2
Statistics of thermodynamic observables

Because of the instantaneous nature of the temperature quench
\beta\to\beta_{\text{q}}
at
t=0
, the observables
\mathscr{Q}
and
\Sigma
can be written as differences of state functions. More precisely,
\mathscr{Q}
and
\Sigma
in (
28
) and (
32
) are differences of
-\beta_{\text{q}}\mathscr{E}
and
V-\beta_{\text{q}}\mathscr{F}
, respectively, evaluated at
(m,t)
and
(m^{\prime},0)
. Observables of this kind depend only on the initial and final states,
m^{\prime}
and
m
, and on time
t
, but are otherwise independent of the specific path
m(s)_{0\leq s\leq t}
taken by the dynamics.

Based on this observation, we now develop a general theory for the large-deviation statistics of such observables that applies to systems subject to quenches of the temperature or of other external parameters. This includes, but is not limited to, the thermodynamic observables
\mathscr{Q}
and
\Sigma
.

We define the moment-generating function
G(k,t)
of an intensive state-variable difference

\displaystyle\Delta\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{A}(m,t)-\mathscr{A}(m^{\prime},0)\,,

(33)

by

\displaystyle G(k,t)=\langle\text{e}^{Nk\Delta\mathscr{A}}\rangle\,.

(34)

Note again that the explicit time dependence of
\mathscr{A}
in (
33
) is absent for
\mathscr{Q}
but present in
\Sigma
. Conditioning (
34
) on the initial and final magnetisations
m^{\prime}
and
m
, we write

\displaystyle G(k,t)=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\langle\text{e}^{Nk\Delta\mathscr{A}}|m,t;m^{\prime},0\rangle P(m,t;m^{\prime},0)\,,

(35)

where
P(m,t;m^{\prime},0)
denotes the joint probability of observing
m
at time
t
and
m^{\prime}
at vanishing initial time. Because the observable
\Delta\mathscr{A}
in (
35
) depends only on
m
,
m^{\prime}
, and
t
, the conditioning renders
\Delta\mathscr{A}
deterministic, so that
\langle\text{e}^{Nk\Delta\mathscr{A}}|m,t;m^{\prime},0\rangle=\text{e}^{Nk\Delta\mathscr{A}}
. Furthermore, we write the joint probability in (
35
) as
P(m,t;m^{\prime},0)=P_{\text{q}}(m,t|m^{\prime},0)P^{\text{eq}}(m^{\prime})
, where
P_{\text{q}}(m,t|m^{\prime},0)
denotes the probability of observing magnetisation
m
at time
t

conditional
on starting with
m^{\prime}
at time
t=0
. The subscript
q
emphasises that the dynamics is due to the heat bath at quenched inverse temperature
\beta_{\text{q}}
. After these manipulations, (
35
) reads

\displaystyle G(k,t)=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\text{e}^{Nk[\mathscr{A}(m,t)-\mathscr{A}(m^{\prime},0)]}P_{\text{q}}(m,t|m^{\prime},0)P^{\text{eq}}(m^{\prime})\,,

(36)

We now define the “
k
-tilted” initial probability distribution

\displaystyle P_{k}(m^{\prime},0)\equiv Z_{k}^{-1}\text{e}^{-Nk\mathscr{A}(m^{\prime},0)}P^{\text{eq}}(m^{\prime})\,,

(37)

with
Z_{k}
obtained from normalisation. Summing over
m^{\prime}
in (
36
) we then arrive at

\displaystyle G(k,t)=Z_{k}\langle\text{e}^{Nk\mathscr{A}}\rangle_{k}\,,

(38)

where
\langle\ldots\rangle_{k}
denotes the average with respect to the time-evolved,
k
-tilted distribution

\displaystyle P_{k}(m,t)=\sum_{m^{\prime}}P_{\text{q}}(m,t|m^{\prime},0)P_{k}(m^{\prime},0)\,.

(39)

Equation (
38
) has the advantage that the observable
\mathscr{A}(m,t)
is independent of the initial state
m^{\prime}
, in distinction to
\Delta\mathscr{A}
in (
34
), which depends on both
m^{\prime}
and
m
.

3.3
Thermodynamic limit and Landau potential

In the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
, the probability distribution of an intensive observable
\Delta\mathscr{A}
typically takes a large-deviation form
[
35
,
83
,
84
]

\displaystyle P(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)\smilefrown\text{e}^{-NI(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)}\,,

(40)

with non-negative rate function
I(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)\geq 0
, analogous to
V(m,t)
in (
14
). The location of the vanishing minimum of
I(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)
is given by the typical, most probable value of
\Delta\mathscr{A}
, which coincides with its mean
\langle\Delta\mathscr{A}\rangle
, i.e.,
I(\langle\Delta\mathscr{A}\rangle,t)=0
, in the thermodynamic limit. Away from this minimum, the rate function quantifies the exponentially suppressed probabilities of deviations from the typical behaviour, thus generalising the central-limit theorem
[
35
,
83
,
84
]
. In other words, the rate function
I(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)
provides us with the time-dependent statistics of
\Delta\mathscr{A}
, to leading exponential order in the thermodynamic limit.

It is convenient to use the scaled cumulant-generating function

\displaystyle\Lambda(k,t)\equiv\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\ln G(k,t)\,,

(41)

to obtain
I(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)
by Legendre transform
[
35
,
83
,
84
]

\displaystyle I(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)=\max_{k}\{k\Delta\mathscr{A}-\Lambda(k,t)\}\,.

(42)

The scaled cumulants of
\Delta\mathscr{A}
are given by the derivatives of
\Lambda(k,t)
, evaluated at
k=0
. In particular, the mean is given by the slope at
k=0
,

\displaystyle\langle\Delta\mathscr{A}\rangle=\partial_{k}\Lambda(0,t)\,.

(43)

For
\Delta\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{Q}
, the detailed fluctuation relation (
29
) implies a symmetry for
\Lambda(k,t)
about the inflection point
k_{0}=(\beta/\beta_{\text{q}}-1)/2
:

\displaystyle\Lambda(k+k_{0},t)=\Lambda(-k+k_{0},t)\,.

(44)

In order to derive an expression for
\Lambda(k,t)
, we take the thermodynamic limit of (
38
) using the large-deviation form

\displaystyle P_{k}(m,t)\smilefrown\text{e}^{-NV_{k}(m,t)}

(45)

for the
k
-tilted probability distribution, with
k
-tilted rate function
V_{k}(m,t)
. In the limit
N\to\infty
, the sums in (
38
) and (
39
) turn into integrals that we evaluate by a saddle-point approximation. We collect the exponential terms and substitute them into (
41
), which yields

\displaystyle\Lambda(k,t)=-\min_{m}\left\{W_{k}(m,t)\right\}\,,

(46)

where

\displaystyle W_{k}(m,t)=-k\mathscr{A}(m,t)+V_{k}(m,t)-\zeta_{k}\,.

(47)

Equation (
46
) expresses
\Lambda(k,t)
as the negative minimum of the potential function
W_{k}(m,t)
. We show in Sec.
4
that
W_{k}(m,t)
takes the role of a dynamical Landau potential. The
k
-dependent constant

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\ln Z_{k}\,,

(48)

in (
47
) originates from the normalisation of the tilted rate function
V_{k}(m,t)
, but it cancels in the expressions of
W_{k}(m,t)
and
\Lambda(k,t)
, as we shall see in (
53
) and (
56
) below.

From (
39
), we observe that the
k
-tilted rate function
V_{k}(m,t)
obeys, up to different boundary conditions, the same Hamilton-Jacobi equation (
16
) as the “untilted” magnetisation rate function
V(m,t)=V_{k=0}(m,t)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{t}V_{k}(m,t)+\mathscr{H}[m,\partial_{m}V_{k}(m,t)]\,.

(49)

The initial condition for
V_{k}(m,t)
follows from the large-deviation form of
P_{k}(m,0)
in (
37
):

\displaystyle V_{k}(m,0)=k\mathscr{A}(m,0)+\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)+\zeta_{k}\,.

(50)

As a consequence, the time evolution of
W_{k}(m,t)
is also dictated by (
49
) through (
47
).

Before we proceed, we find it instructive to compute
I(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)
at
t=0
with what we have derived so far. Substituting the boundary condition (
50
) into (
47
) at
t=0
, we find
W_{k}(m,0)=\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)
. Equation (
46
) then gives
\Lambda(k,0)=0
for all
k
. Performing the Legendre transform (
42
), we find
P(\Delta\mathscr{A},0)=\delta(\Delta\mathscr{A})
, where
\delta(x)
denotes the Dirac delta function. This shows that the state-difference observable
\Delta\mathscr{A}
in (
33
) is initially zero with unit probability for all observables
\mathscr{A}
, as expected.

3.4
Post-quench dynamics of
W_{k}(m,t)

At finite time
t>0
after the quench,
V_{k}(m,t)
is the solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation (
49
) with initial condition (
50
). Equation (
49
) is solved by a
k
-dependent family of characteristics
[q_{k}(s),p_{k}(s)]_{0\leq s\leq t}
that are solutions to the Hamilton equations (
20
) with
k
- and
m
-dependent boundary conditions:

\displaystyle p_{k}(0)=k\partial_{m}\mathscr{A}[q_{k}(0),0]+\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}m}\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}[q_{k}(0)]\,,\quad q_{k}(t)=

\displaystyle m\,.

(51)

An expression for
V_{k}(m,t)
is then given by the
k
-tilted analogue of (
23
),

\displaystyle V_{k}(m,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}s\left[p_{k}\dot{q}_{k}-\mathscr{H}(q_{k},p_{k})\right]+V_{k}[q_{k}(0),0]\,.

(52)

Using (
47
), we can now write
W_{k}(m,t)
in terms of the characteristics
[q_{k}(s),p_{k}(s)]_{0\leq s\leq t}
as

\displaystyle W_{k}(m,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}s\left[p_{k}\dot{q}_{k}-\mathscr{H}(q_{k},p_{k})\right]+\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}[q_{k}(0)]-k\{\mathscr{A}[q_{k}(t),t]-\mathscr{A}[q_{k}(0),0]\}\,.

(53)

Hence, using (
53
) one computes
W_{k}(m,t)
from the solutions
[q_{k}(s),p_{k}(s)]_{0\leq s\leq t}
of (
20
) with boundary conditions (
51
), for given
m
,
k
and
t
. In order to obtain
\Lambda(k,t)
from
W_{k}(m,t)
according to (
46
), we must then take an additional, error-prone minimum over
m
. Hence, although (
53
) is useful for computing the Landau potential
W_{k}(m,t)
, it is not the ideal starting point for the evaluation of
\Lambda(k,t)
. In the next section, we derive a method for computing
\Lambda(k,t)
, that avoids evaluating
W_{k}(m,t)
for all
m
and only requires a one-dimensional
k
grid.

3.5
Post-quench dynamics of
\Lambda(k,t)

Equation (
53
) provides an expression for
W_{k}(m,t)
for given
m
and
t
. The scaled cumulant-generating function
\Lambda(k,t)
in (
46
), however, requires only the value
W_{k}(m^{*}_{k},t)
where
W_{k}(m,t)
acquires its minimum, i.e.,
\partial_{m}W_{k}(m^{*}_{k},t)=0
. The minimum value
W_{k}(m^{*}_{k},t)
can be obtained directly by imposing
\partial_{m}W_{k}(m^{*}_{k},t)=0
as a boundary condition, in addition to (
50
). Written as a condition for
V_{k}(m,t)
, one finds from (
47
):

\displaystyle\partial_{m}V_{k}(m_{k}^{*},t)=k\partial_{m}\mathscr{A}(m_{k}^{*},t)

(54)

at time
t
. This boundary condition leads to yet another family of characteristics
[q^{*}_{k}(s),p^{*}_{k}(s)]_{0\leq s\leq t}
, which, again, obey the Hamilton equations (
20
), but now with
m
-independent boundary conditions

\displaystyle p^{*}_{k}(0)=

\displaystyle k\partial_{m}\mathscr{A}[q^{*}_{k}(0),0]+\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}m}\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}[q^{*}_{k}(0)]\,,

(55a)

\displaystyle p^{*}_{k}(t)=

\displaystyle k\partial_{m}\mathscr{A}[q_{k}^{*}(t),t]\,.

(55b)

These boundary conditions ensure that
q_{k}^{*}(t)=m_{k}^{*}
is an extremum of
W_{k}(m,t)
. Using this set of characteristics
[q^{*}_{k}(s),p^{*}_{k}(s)]_{0\leq s\leq t}
, we directly express
\Lambda(k,t)
as

\displaystyle\Lambda(k,t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}s\left[p^{*}_{k}\dot{q}^{*}_{k}-\mathscr{H}(q^{*}_{k},p^{*}_{k})\right]-\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}[q^{*}_{k}(0)]+k\{\mathscr{A}[q^{*}_{k}(t),t]-\mathscr{A}[q^{*}_{k}(0),0]\}\,.

(56)

In the particular case
\Delta\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{Q}
, that we study in detail in Sec.
4
, the shift-inversion symmetry (
24
) of
\mathscr{H}
, combined with the boundary conditions (
55
), implies a time-reversal symmetry for characteristics below and above
k_{0}
,

\displaystyle q^{*}_{k+k_{0}}(s)=q^{*}_{-k+k_{0}}(t-s)\,,\qquad p^{*}_{k+k_{0}}(s)=-p^{*}_{-k+k_{0}}(t-s)+\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}[q^{*}_{-k+k_{0}}(t-s)]\,.

(57)

This is a generalisation of the detailed fluctuation relation (
44
) to the level of optimal fluctuations, which can be seen by recovering (
44
) from (
56
) and (
57
).

Depending on whether we intend to compute
W_{k}(m,t)
or
\Lambda(k,t)
, we solve the Hamilton equations (
20
) with either boundary conditions, (
51
) or (
55
), by a shooting method
[
71
]
. This returns families of characteristics on a grid of
k
(and
m
) values, enabling us to evaluate either
W_{k}(m,t)
in (
47
), or
\Lambda(k,t)
in (
56
) on this grid.

3.6
Long-time limit

In the long-time limit, the evaluation of
\Lambda(k,t)
simplifies considerably. This is seen most directly by taking the long-time limit of
G(k,t)
in (
36
), where the conditional probability converges to the equilibrium probability distribution
P^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}
at the quenched inverse temperature
\beta_{\text{q}}
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}P_{\text{q}}(m,t|m^{\prime},0)=P^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}(m)
. We can then write the limit
G_{\infty}(k)\equiv\lim_{t\to\infty}G(k,t)
as

\displaystyle G_{\infty}(k)=\langle\text{e}^{Nk\mathscr{A}_{\infty}(m)}\rangle^{\text{eq}}_{q}\langle\text{e}^{-Nk\mathscr{A}(m^{\prime},0)}\rangle^{\text{eq}}\,,

(58)

where
\mathscr{A}_{\infty}(m)\equiv\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathscr{A}(m,t)
;
\langle\ldots\rangle^{\text{eq}}
and
\langle\ldots\rangle^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}
denote averages with respect to the equilibrium distributions
P^{\text{eq}}
and
P^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}
, respectively. Taking the thermodynamic limit, we use the large-deviation forms of these distributions and evaluate the integrals over
m
and
m^{\prime}
in the saddle-point approximation. This leads us to an expression for
\Lambda_{\infty}(k)\equiv\lim_{t\to\infty}\Lambda(k,t)
in terms of a maximisation over initial and final states
m
and
m^{\prime}
, given by

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\infty}(k)=\max_{m}\{k\mathscr{A}_{\infty}(m)-\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}_{q}(m)\}+\max_{m^{\prime}}\{-k\mathscr{A}(m^{\prime},0)-\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m^{\prime})\}\,.

(59)

To connect this to our previous results, we write (
59
) as a function of the initial and final points,
q_{k}^{*}(0)
and
q_{k}^{*}(\infty)
, of an infinite-time optimal fluctuation:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\infty}(k)=k\mathscr{A}_{\infty}[q_{k}^{*}(\infty)]-\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}[q_{k}^{*}(\infty)]-k\mathscr{A}[q_{k}^{*}(0),0]-\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}[q_{k}^{*}(0)]\,.

(60)

In order for the optimal fluctuation to fulfil the boundary conditions (
55
) in infinite time, it must initiate on the stable manifold (
26
), pass through the fixed point at
(q_{\text{FP}},p_{\text{FP}})=(0,0)
, and either stay there [when
q_{k}^{*}(\infty)=0
], or end on the unstable manifold (
27
) [when
q_{k}^{*}(\infty)\neq 0
]. Combining (
26
) with (
55a
) at
q=q_{k}^{*}(0)
and (
27
) with (
55b
) at
q=q_{k}^{*}(\infty)
, we find that the initial and end points must satisfy

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle k\partial_{m}\mathscr{A}[q_{k}^{*}(0),0]+\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}m}\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}[q_{k}^{*}(0)]\,,

(61a)

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle k\partial_{m}\mathscr{A}_{\infty}[q_{k}^{*}(\infty)]-\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}m}\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}[q_{k}^{*}(\infty)]\,.

(61b)

In case there are several solutions to (
61
), we must pick the combination of
q_{k}^{*}(0)
and
q_{k}^{*}(\infty)
for which the right-hand side of (
60
) takes its maximum value. This approach leads to explicit expressions for the scaled cumulant-generating function
\Lambda_{\infty}(k)
and for the initial and final points of
q_{k}^{*}(s)_{0\leq s\leq\infty}
in the infinite-time limit.

3.7
Rate function

Finally, we compute the rate function
I(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)
from
\Lambda(k,t)
using the Legendre transform in (
42
). To this end, we evaluate

\displaystyle\Delta\mathscr{A}(k^{*})=\partial_{k}\Lambda(k^{*},t)=\mathscr{A}[q^{*}_{k^{*}}(t),t]-\mathscr{A}[q^{*}_{k^{*}}(0),0]\,,

(62)

which gives an implicit equation for the value
k^{*}
, where the right-hand side of (
42
) acquires its maximum. The second equality in (
62
) follows by taking a
k
derivative of (
56
):

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\Lambda(k,t)=\mathscr{A}[q^{*}_{k}(t),t]-\mathscr{A}[q^{*}_{k}(0),0]+\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}s\left[\frac{\delta\Lambda}{\delta p^{*}_{k}}\frac{\partial p^{*}_{k}}{\partial k}+\frac{\delta\Lambda}{\delta q^{*}_{k}}\frac{\partial q^{*}_{k}}{\partial k}\right]\,.

(63)

The integral in (
63
) vanishes due to the variational principle
\delta\Lambda=0
, which implies
\delta\Lambda/\delta q_{k}^{*}=\delta\Lambda/\delta p_{k}^{*}=0
, see
B
.

By inverting (
62
) we obtain the function
k^{*}(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)
and express (
42
) as

\displaystyle I(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)=k^{*}(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)\Delta\mathscr{A}-\Lambda[k^{*}(\Delta\mathscr{A},t),t]\,.

(64)

Equation (
64
) is the sought-after expression for the rate function
I(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)
in (
40
), that completes the extended Hamiltonian method for the calculation of the finite-time statistics of observables in the thermodynamics limit.

To summarise, while the standard Hamiltonian method, outlined in Sec.
2.3.1
, only yields the statistics of the state (magnetisation
m
for the Curie-Weiss model), the extended Hamiltonian method developed here represents a significant generalisation. It computes the finite-time large-deviations of arbitrary
\Delta\mathscr{A}
in the thermodynamic limit, whenever
\Delta\mathscr{A}
can be written as a difference of state functions. For the post-quench dynamics we analyse here, the thermodynamic observables
\mathscr{Q}
and
\Sigma
fall into this category. Furthermore, the method automatically provides the dynamical Landau potential
W_{k}(m,t)
given in (
53
), a powerful tool of the identification and classification of finite-time dynamical phase transitions.

We note that since our method uses generating functions and the Legendre transform,
I(\Delta\mathscr{A},t)
obtained from (
64
) is always convex. In cases where the underlying rate function has a non-convex part, such as, e.g.,
V(m,t)
in Figure
2
(b), (
64
) returns its convex hull, and the information stored in the non-convex part is lost. Non-convex parts in rate functions are signalled by non-differentiable points
[
84
]
in the scaled-cumulant generating function
\Lambda(k,t)
in (
41
). As a simple but non-trivial example of
\Delta\mathscr{A}
where this non-convexity matters, one may consider the magnetisation itself,
\Delta\mathscr{A}=m
, so that
\mathscr{A}(m,t)=m
and
\mathscr{A}(m^{\prime},0)=0
. In this case, the scaled cumulant generating function
\Lambda(k,t)
has a sharp kink at
k=0
, because the magnetisation rate function
V(m,t)
is non-convex between its minima for all finite times, see Figure
2
(b). The rate function
I(m,t)
obtained from (
64
) then gives the convex hull of
V(m,t)
, which is flat around the origin and, in particular, misses the kink of
V(m,t)
at
m=0
.

Fortunately,
\Lambda(k,t)
is oftentimes differentiable in its domain, and (
64
) returns the exact rate function. This is the case for thermodynamic observables of the Curie-Weiss model, so that non-convexity is of no concern here.

4.1
Characterisation of phase transition

We now give a more detailed characterisation of the dynamical phase transition in terms of the dynamical Landau potential
W_{k}(m,t)
defined in Sec
3.4
. This allows us to establish the continuous nature of the transition, to obtain an explicit expression for the critical time
t^{\mathscr{Q}}_{\text{c}}
and the critical exponent, and to provide an intuitive explanation for the occurrence of the transition in terms of a switch in the optimal fluctuations.

To get started, we first establish a connection between the locations of the kink in the
k
and
\mathscr{Q}
spaces. We take a
k

44
), and evaluate at

:

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\Lambda(k_{0},t)=-\partial_{k}\Lambda(k_{0},t)=0\,.

(66)

By (

is connected to the value of the observable
\mathscr{Q}
generated by the

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\Lambda(k_{0},t)=\mathscr{Q}(k_{0},t)=0\,.

(67)

Inverting this equation, we find

, establishing that

is the optimal fluctuation that generates
\mathscr{Q}=0
for all times

. In particular, this means that we can write the dynamical order parameter

for
\mathscr{Q}=0

m_{t}(0)=q^{*}_{k_{0}}(t)
and that the dynamical Landau potential of the transition at
\mathscr{Q}=0
is given by
W_{k_{0}}(m,t)
.

After identifying
W_{k_{0}}(m,t)
as our object of study, we compute it with the method described in Sec.
3.4
: We solve the Hamilton equations (
20
) with boundary conditions (
51
) to obtain a one-parameter family of characteristics on a fine

-grid. From (
52

V_{k_{0}}(m,t)
on this grid. In the last step, we compute
W_{k_{0}}(m,t)
using (
47
).

4.1.2
Critical time

With all necessary methods in place, we now compute the critical time
t^{\mathscr{Q}}_{\text{c}}
for the finite-time dynamical phase transition. When

4.1.4
Optimal fluctuations

Finally, the origin of the dynamical phase transition can be viewed from the perspective of the optimal fluctuations that generate
\mathscr{Q}=0
. To improve our intuition for these fluctuations, it is useful to consider how the condition
\mathscr{Q}=0
constrains their dynamics.

According to (
62
), the optimal fluctuation
q^{*}_{k_{0}}(s)_{0\leq s\leq t}
for
\mathscr{Q}=0
must satisfy

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}[q^{*}_{k_{0}}(t)]=\mathscr{E}[q^{*}_{k_{0}}(0)]\,,

(77)

i.e., the internal energy before the quench and at time
t
must coincide. For
\mathscr{E}(m)=-\beta_{\text{q}}Jm^{2}/2
, (
77
) translates into

\displaystyle q^{*}_{k_{0}}(t)=\pm q^{*}_{k_{0}}(0)\,.

(78)

Hence, the requirement that
\mathscr{Q}=0
forces the initial and end points of the optimal fluctuations to agree up to a sign.

The constraint (
78
) on the optimal fluctuation gives a simple qualitative explanation of why the dynamical phase transition occurs at a finite time, by considering the most likely ways to achieve
\mathscr{Q}=0
at short and long times: For short times
t\ll\tau
, the most likely way is to start and end close to the most likely initial condition
q^{*}_{k_{0}}(0)\approx\bar{m}(\beta)>0
, because the relaxation dynamics can be sustained for short times at low probabilistic cost. For long times
t\gg\tau
and
\Delta\bar{\beta}>0
, by contrast, the system is more likely to start at vanishing magnetisation, at high initial probabilistic cost, because it is also the most likely
final
magnetisation, i.e.,
\bar{m}(\beta_{\text{q}})=0
. In other words, although the initial probabilistic cost of
q^{*}_{k_{0}}(0)\approx 0
is high, the system may then stay close to the origin for an arbitrary amount of time at no additional cost.

According to this argument one expects different optimal fluctuations for short and long times, implying a transition between the two behaviours at some intermediate time, given by the critical time
t^{\mathscr{Q}}_{\text{c}}
.

For
\Delta\bar{\beta}<0
, the probability of initiating (and staying) at
m=0
is always too low, compared to starting (and ending) somewhere in the middle ground between to a likely initial condition and an unlikely final condition.

At finite
N
, the variable sign in (
78
) gives rise to the premature transition observed in the numerics in Figure
8
(a). This is, because at any finite
N
, trajectories that initiate close to
m=0
have two possibilities that occur with similar probability: At time
t
they may end up at either the positive or negative value of their initial magnetisation. Trajectories that initiate far away from the origin, close to the initial minima of
\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(m)
, say, have effectively only one possibility,
q^{*}_{k_{0}}(t)=q^{*}_{k_{0}}(0)
, because the probability of trajectories crossing the origin and ending up at their negative initial magnetisation,
q^{*}_{k_{0}}(t)=-q^{*}_{k_{0}}(0)
, is exponentially suppressed. As a result, the probability to start and end close to the origin is enhanced at finite
N
.

This effect becomes smaller as
N
increases, since trajectories for which both possibilities in (
78
) are of similar probability, recide closer and closer to the origin. In the thermodynamic limit, only the
+
constraint in (
78
) survives, so that the optimal fluctuations always obey
q^{*}_{k_{0}}(t)=q^{*}_{k_{0}}(0)
, see (
57
). Based on this argument, we have checked that the premature transition in Figure
8
(a) is absent when we enforce
q^{*}_{k_{0}}(t)=q^{*}_{k_{0}}(0)
also at finite
N
.

From our direct numerical simulations for
\Delta\bar{\beta}>0
, we visualise the optimal fluctuations by conditioning the trajectories on
\mathscr{Q}=0
at times smaller and larger than
t^{\mathscr{Q}}_{\text{c}}
. In order to emulate the thermodynamic limit, we now enforce
q^{*}_{k_{0}}(t)=q^{*}_{k_{0}}(0)
, instead of admitting both signs in (
78
). 5
Conclusions

Combining elements from stochastic thermodynamics and large-deviation theory, we derived a powerful extension of the Hamiltonian method for computing the time-dependent statistics of thermodynamic observables after an instantaneous temperature quench. The approach proves particularly effective for the analysis of finite-time dynamical phase transitions, as it naturally gives rise to a dynamical generalisation of Landau theory. The corresponding dynamical Landau potential allows for an unambiguous identification of the dynamical order parameter and of the associated dynamical phases in the phase diagram. Our theory applies to systems with underlying stochastic dynamics that admit well-defined thermodynamic or weak-noise limits.

We introduced our approach using the Curie-Weiss spin model as a concrete, non-trivial example of a system with an equilibrium phase transition. For disordering quenches across the phase boundary, the magnetisation
m
of this system was shown to exhibit a finite-dynamical phase transition in Ref.
[
71
]
. Using our new method, we conducted a detailed analysis of the statistics of the heat
\mathscr{Q}
released into the environment after such a disordering quench.

In a finite region of the parameter space, our investigation revealed another finite-time dynamical phase transition associated with this observable. The transition manifests itself in a finite-time kink in the probability distribution of
\mathscr{Q}
and classifies as continuous, with mean-field critical exponent, similar to the transition for
m

[
71
]
. Apart from these similarities, however, the two transitions exhibit very different properties.

On the trajectory level, we showed that the new finite-time dynamical phase transition associated with
\mathscr{Q}
is related to a constraint on the initial and end points of individual trajectories. The most likely ways to satisfy the constraint differ in the short- and long-time limits. This implies the occurrence of a sudden switch in the optimal, most likely fluctuation at finite time and thus provides a qualitative explanation for the occurrence of the phase transition. At finite
N
, we argued that the constraint posed on the fluctuations is effectively weaker for trajectories that reside close to
m=0
, which explains a premature phase transition at
t<t^{\mathscr{Q}}_{\text{c}}
observed in our direct numerical simulations.

The extended Hamiltonian method presented here opens the door to a complete, finite-time analysis of the stochastic thermodynamics of systems subject to quenches of either the temperature or other external parameters. Our analysis of the finite-time statistics of the released heat
\mathscr{Q}
reveals that multiple finite-time dynamical phase transitions associated with different observables and generated in different ways occur in the relaxation dynamics of the same system, as a consequence of different constraints posed on the dynamics at the trajectory level. This indicates that finite-time dynamical phase transitions with distinct properties are an integral part of far-from-equilibrium relaxation processes, that occur in a wide range of physical situations. The dynamical Landau theory we propose for the study of these transitions has proven powerful in identifying the distinct time-dependent phases and for classifying them in terms of well-known equilibrium categories. We are confident that our methods will be useful in the study of finite-time dynamical phase transitions in other models and for a variety of observables.

As for the Curie-Weiss model, the next logical step is to investigate the finite-time statistics of entropy production in response to quenches. This would give a more detailed account of the finite-time dynamics of dissipation and provide further insights into the irreversibility of relaxation processes in the thermodynamic limit. The analysis is slightly more involved in this case, because the observable depends explicitly on time, leading to time-dependent constraints on the trajectories. Notwithstanding, the theory developed here applies without further limitations.

An important generalisation our method is the inclusion of steady and time-dependent driving. This enables the study of dynamical observables not only in non-equilibrium steady states but also the transient relaxation towards them. For example, the characteristic kink in the rate function of entropy production found at steady state in Refs.
[
49
,
50
,
91
]
could have formed in the transient, as a consequence of a finite-time dynamical phase transition. An analysis of this and related problems with our methods would provide new insights into how known dynamical phase transitions are generated.

Finally, the fact that our theory applies in the thermodynamic limit, raises the question how finite
N
as well as critical fluctuations (in both space and time
[
71
]
) affect finite-time dynamical phase transitions. In equilibrium, finite-
N
corrections are known to potentially alter the location of the critical point and even change the order of phase transitions
[
26
]
. Our numerical simulations in Figure
8
(a) indicate that such corrections could also occur for finite-time dynamical phase transitions. How precisely these finite-
N
corrections and critical fluctuations, responsible for corrections to mean-field critical exponents at equilibrium
[
26
,
27
]
, affect finite-time dynamical phase transitions, remains an intriguing open question.

Fluctuation relation for
\mathscr{Q}

We prove a fluctuation relation for
\mathscr{Q}
, (
29
) in the main text. The proof given here is based on the following observation: In an equilibrium state at inverse temperature
\beta_{\text{q}}

P^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}(m,t;m^{\prime},0)
obeys detailed balance
[
86

\displaystyle P^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}(m,t;m^{\prime},0)=P^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}(m^{\prime},t;m,0)\,.

(79)

The subscript
q
indicates that both
m

m^{\prime}
are sampled from the equilibrium distribution at inverse temperature
\beta_{\text{q}}
. Conditioning on the initial equilibrium state, we now write

\displaystyle P^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}(m,t;m^{\prime},0)=

\displaystyle P_{\text{q}}(m,t|m^{\prime},0)P_{\text{q}}^{\text{eq}}(m^{\prime})\,,

(80a)

\displaystyle P^{\text{eq}}_{\text{q}}(m^{\prime},t;m,0)=

\displaystyle P_{\text{q}}(m^{\prime},t|m,0)P_{\text{q}}^{\text{eq}}(m)\,.

(80b)

Using this and (
79

\displaystyle P_{\text{q}}(m^{\prime},t|m,0)=

\displaystyle P_{\text{q}}(m,t|m^{\prime},0)\frac{P_{\text{q}}^{\text{eq}}(m^{\prime})}{P_{\text{q}}^{\text{eq}}(m)}\,,

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{\text{q}}(m,t|m^{\prime},0)\text{e}^{-N\beta_{\text{q}}[\mathscr{F}(\beta_{\text{q}},m^{\prime})-\mathscr{F}(\beta_{\text{q}},m)]}\,.

(82)

Swapping the summation indices

\displaystyle G(k,t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\text{e}^{-Nk\beta_{\text{q}}\left[\mathscr{E}(m^{\prime})-\mathscr{E}(m)\right]}P_{\text{q}}(m^{\prime},t|m,0)P^{\text{eq}}(m,0)\,.

(83)

Using (

\displaystyle G(k,t)=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\text{e}^{-N\beta_{\text{q}}(-k+\beta/\beta_{\text{q}}-1)[\mathscr{E}(m)-\mathscr{E}(m^{\prime})]}P_{\text{q}}(m,t|m^{\prime},0)P^{\text{eq}}(m^{\prime},0)\,.

(84)

Comparing with (
36
), and shifting

k_{0}=(\beta/\beta_{\text{q}}-1)/2

\displaystyle G(k+k_{0},t)=G(-k+k_{0},t)\,,

(85)

which shows that

is symmetric about

. For the probability distribution

\displaystyle P(\mathscr{Q},t)=P(-\mathscr{Q},t)\text{e}^{-N(\beta/\beta_{\text{q}}-1)\mathscr{Q}}\,,

(86)

leading to

\displaystyle I(\mathscr{Q},t)-I(-\mathscr{Q},t)=(\beta/\beta_{\text{q}}-1)\mathscr{Q}\,,

(87)

for the rate function.

\Lambda(k,t)

Here we prove a variational principle for
\Lambda(k,t)
valid for all
k

\delta\Lambda(k,t)

q^{*}_{k}(s)

\delta\Lambda(k,t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}s\big[\delta p^{*}_{k}\dot{q}^{*}_{k}+p^{*}_{k}\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}s}\delta q^{*}_{k}-\partial_{q}\mathscr{H}(q^{*}_{k},p^{*}_{k})\delta q^{*}_{k}-\partial_{p}\mathscr{H}(q^{*}_{k},p^{*}_{k})\delta p^{*}_{k}\big]\\

(89)

Applying the Hamilton equations (
20
) together with the boundary conditions (
55
) we readily obtain
\delta\Lambda(k,t)=0
. Equation (
89
) can be conveniently written as

\displaystyle\delta\Lambda(k,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}s\left[\frac{\delta\Lambda}{\delta p^{*}_{k}}\delta p^{*}_{k}+\frac{\delta\Lambda}{\delta q^{*}_{k}}\delta q^{*}_{k}\right]\,,

(90)

where the variational derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\Lambda}{\delta p^{*}_{k}}=

\displaystyle-\dot{q}^{*}_{k}+\partial_{p}\mathscr{H}(q^{*}_{k},p^{*}_{k})\,,

(91a)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\Lambda}{\delta q^{*}_{k}}=

\displaystyle\dot{p}^{*}_{k}+\partial_{q}\mathscr{H}(q^{*}_{k},p^{*}_{k})+\delta(s-t)\left[-p^{*}_{k}+k\partial_{q}\mathscr{A}(q^{*}_{k},s)\right]

(91b)

\displaystyle+\delta(s)\left[p^{*}_{k}-k\partial_{q}\mathscr{A}(q^{*}_{k},s)-\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}q}\mathscr{V}^{\text{eq}}(q^{*}_{k})\right]\,,

vanish individually, i.e.,
