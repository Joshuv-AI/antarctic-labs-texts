Infinite-dimensional Dubrovin-Frobenius manifolds and the Stokes phenomenon
Guido Carlet, Francisco Hernández Iglesias

Abstract
. We study the Dubrovin equation of the infinite-dimensional 2D Toda Dubrovin-Frobenius manifold at its irregular singularity. We first revisit the definition of the canonical coordinates, proving that they emerge naturally as generalized eigenvalues of the operator of multiplication by the Euler vector field. We then show that the formal solutions to the Dubrovin equation with exponential type behaviour at the irregular singular point are not uniquely determined by their leading order, but instead depend on an infinite number of parameters, contrary to what happens in the finite-dimensional case. Next, we obtain a large family of solutions to the Dubrovin equation given by integrals along the unit circle of certain combinations of the superpotentials. Observing that such a family is not complete and has trivial monodromy, we study a larger family of weak solutions obtained via Borel resummation of some distinguished formal solutions. These resummed solutions naturally appear in monodromy-related pairs, finally allowing us to compute the infinite-dimensional analogue of the Stokes matrices.



1.
Introduction

Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds were introduced by B. Dubrovin in
[
Dub96
]
to provide a coordinate-free description of the WDVV associativity equations
[
Wit90
,
DVV91
]
of two-dimensional topological field theory.
While on the one hand Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds provide the leading invariant in the reconstruction of higher-genus generating functions of several enumerative objects, on the other hand they have proven valuable in the classification and study of a large class of integrable hierarchies with one spatial variable
[
DZ01
]
.

The program of extending the tools of Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold theory to integrable hierarchies in two spatial variables, that is,
2+1
integrable systems, started in
[
CDM11
]
with the definition of an infinite-dimensional Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold
M_{0}
associated with the dispersionless limit of the bi-Hamiltonian structure
[
Car05
]
of the 2D Toda lattice due to Ueno and Takasaki
[
UT84
]
.
In
[
CM15
]
the Dubrovin equation of
M_{0}
was derived and studied, in particular by obtaining a Levelt basis of solutions near its regular singular point at
\zeta\sim 0
. This yields a canonical basis of Hamiltonian densities for the principal hierarchy of
M_{0}
, which constitutes a non-trivial extension of the dispersionless 2D Toda lattice.

In recent years, several other examples of infinite-dimensional Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds have been constructed. In
[
WZ14
]
a family
M^{n,m}_{0}
of infinite-dimensional Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds, all of them underlying the dispersionless 2D Toda lattice and coinciding with
M_{0}
for
n=m=1
, was defined. A similar infinite family for the dispersionless two-component BKP hierarchy was discussed in
[
WX12
]
. Other remarkable examples are the infinite-dimensional Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold associated with the dispersionless KP hierarchy, defined in
[
Rai12
]
, and a family of infinite-dimensional Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds underlying the Whitham hierarchy, recently obtained in
[
MWZ21
]
.

The existence of a theory in full genera associated with these infinite-dimensional Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds is still not clear. In this direction, a partial cohomological field theory of infinite rank has been recently defined in
[
BRZ21
]
. Its associated Hamiltonian integrable hierarchy, in a certain reduction, has been shown to coincide with the KP hierarchy.

In this paper we continue the study of the Frobenius manifold
M_{0}
associated with the 2D Toda hierarchy.

First, we revisit the definition of the canonical coordinates introduced in
[
CDM11
]
, showing that the continuous family
u_{p}
has to be supplemented by a finite number of discrete coordinates
u_{i}
,
\bar{u}_{i}
given by the critical values of the two superpotentials
\lambda
,
\bar{\lambda}
, in analogy to the usual description of canonical coordinates for finite-dimensional Frobenius manifolds given by a superpotential. To give a better justification for the somewhat ad hoc definition of the canonical coordinates
u_{p}
, we study the spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{U}
of multiplication by the Euler vector field. We show that the continuous canonical coordinates
u_{p}
coincide with the generalized eigenvalues of
\mathcal{U}
, while the standard eigenvalues are given by the critical values
u_{i}
,
\bar{u}_{i}
.
To give a more accurate and rigorous description of the tangent and cotangent spaces to
M_{0}
, here we make a distinction between the cotangent space and its representable (via the metric) subspace. This is necessary to deal with the non-representable differentials of several basic functionals on
M_{0}
, including those of the canonical coordinates.

We then consider the Dubrovin equation at its irregular singularity at
\zeta\sim\infty
. We reformulate it as an equation on the cotangent space to
M_{0}
, rather than on its representable subspace, to allow for sufficiently large families of solutions. We study the formal solutions of the Dubrovin equation at the irregular singularity, remarkably finding that such formal solutions are not uniquely determined by their leading order, unlike in the finite-dimensional case, but depend on a large set of parameters.

Our final aim is to describe the Stokes phenomenon for the irregular singularity of the Dubrovin equation and, in particular, to compute its Stokes matrices. We obtain an infinite family of solutions given by integrals along the unit circle and compute their asymptotics. We are however faced with the problem that such a family has trivial monodromy around
\zeta\sim\infty
and cannot be considered as the analogue of a fundamental solution in the finite-dimensional case or, in other words, it is not complete. To solve this problem, we apply the theory of resurgent functions to certain formal solutions for which we have an explicit description, namely those obtained as asymptotic series from the integral solutions. What we find in the resummation process is a large family of solutions which are nevertheless weak, i.e., they do not extend to linear functionals defined on the whole tangent space. For such a family, we explicitly compute the Stokes matrices. For simplicity, this last part of the paper is conducted restricting to a two-dimensional locus in
M_{0}
where the superpotentials have a particularly simple form.

Organization of the paper

In Section
2
we recall the definition of the 2D Toda Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold
M_{0}
given in
[
CDM11
,
CM15
]
.
In Section
3
we revisit the canonical coordinates and prove they coincide with the (generalized) eigenvalues of the operator
\mathcal{U}
of multiplication by Euler vector field.
In Section
4
we derive the Dubrovin equation on the cotangent spaces.
In Section
5
we find the formal solutions to the Dubrovin equation at
\infty
.
In Section
6
we study an infinite, albeit incomplete, family of integral solutions to the Dubrovin equation with suitable asymptotic expansions at
\infty
.
Finally, in Section
7
we apply the resurgence procedure to the formal solutions which arise as asymptotic expansions of the integral solutions, obtaining this way a family of weak solutions parametrized by the unit circle
S^{1}
. These solutions appear naturally in monodromy-related pairs, allowing us to study the Stokes phenomenon in a similar fashion to the finite-dimensional case. The section ends with the explicit computation of the infinite-dimensional analogue of the Stokes matrices.

2.
The 2D Toda Dubrovin-Frobenius manifold

In this section, we recall the definition and some properties of the 2D Toda Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold from
[
CDM11
,
CM15
]
.

2.1.
The manifold
M
and its tangent bundle

Let
D_{0}
be the closed unit disc in the Riemann sphere,
D_{\infty}
the closure of its complement and
S^{1}=D_{0}\cap D_{\infty}
the unit circle. For a compact subset
K
of the Riemann sphere, we denote by
\mathcal{H}(K)
the space of holomorphic functions on
K
, i.e., functions which extend holomorphically to an open neighborhood of
K
.

We define the infinite-dimensional manifold
M
as the affine space

(2.1)

\displaystyle M=\{(\lambda(z),\bar{\lambda}(z))\in z\mathcal{H}(D_{\infty})\oplus\frac{1}{z}\mathcal{H}(D_{0})\ |\ \lambda(z)=z+O(1)\}.

A point
\hat{\lambda}=(\lambda(z),\bar{\lambda}(z))\in M
can be represented by the Laurent series at
\infty
and
0
of its components

(2.2)

\displaystyle\lambda(z)=z+\sum_{k\leqslant 0}u_{k}z^{k},\qquad\bar{\lambda}(z)=\sum_{k\geqslant-1}\bar{u}_{k}z^{k}.

We identify the tangent space at a point
\hat{\lambda}
with the vector space underlying the affine space
M

(2.3)

\displaystyle T_{\hat{\lambda}}M=\mathcal{H}(D_{\infty})\oplus\frac{1}{z}\mathcal{H}(D_{0}).

2.2.
The manifold
M_{0}

We define
M_{0}

as the open subset of
M
consisting of the pairs
(\lambda(z),\bar{\lambda}(z))
satisfying the following conditions:

(T1)

The leading coefficient
\bar{u}_{-1}
of
\bar{\lambda}(z)
is nonzero.

(T2)

The derivative of
w(z):=\lambda(z)+\bar{\lambda}(z)
does not vanish on
S^{1}
.

(T3)

The curve parameterized by
w(z)
for
z\in S^{1}
is positively oriented, non-selfintersecting and encircles the origin
w=0
.

(T4)

The map
\sigma(z):=\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(z)}{\lambda^{\prime}(z)+\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)}
has non-vanishing derivative on
S^{1}
.

(T5)

The functions
\lambda^{\prime}(z)
,
\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)
are non-vanishing for
z\in S^{1}
; equivalently, the curve
\sigma:S^{1}\to\mathbb{C}
does not pass through the points
0
and
1
.

Remark 2.1
.

These conditions were introduced in the literature in different places
[
CDM11
,
WZ14
,
CM15
]
mainly to avoid non-generic cases and to simplify some of the definitions and the proofs. Conditions
(T2)
and
(T3)
are used in the definition of the metric and the flat coordinates. Conditions
(T4)
and
(T5)
are used in the definition of canonical coordinates and in the computation of the spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{U}
.

2.3.
The
w
-coordinates

Sometimes it is more convenient to represent
M_{0}
as a two-dimensional bundle over the space
M_{\textrm{red}}\subset\mathcal{H}(S^{1})
of parameterized simple analytic curves:

\displaystyle M_{0}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow M_{\textrm{red}}\oplus\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle(\lambda(z),\bar{\lambda}(z))

\displaystyle\longmapsto(w(z),v,u),

where
w(z)=\lambda(z)+\bar{\lambda}(z)
,
v=\bar{u}_{0}=(\bar{\lambda})_{0}
and
e^{u}=\bar{u}_{-1}=(\bar{\lambda})_{1}
. The map can be inverted by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\lambda(z)=w_{\leqslant 0}(z)+z-v-e^{u}z^{-1},\qquad\bar{\lambda}(z)=w_{\geqslant 1}(z)-z+v+e^{u}z^{-1}.

We refer to the triples
(w(z),v,u)
as
w
-coordinates. In these coordinates the tangent vectors are represented as elements of
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}
via the map

(2.5)

\begin{split}T_{\hat{\lambda}}M=\mathcal{H}(D_{\infty})\oplus\frac{1}{z}\mathcal{H}(D_{0})&\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}\\
(X(z),\bar{X}(z))&\longmapsto(W(z),X_{v},X_{u}),\end{split}

where

(2.6)

\displaystyle W(z)=X(z)+\bar{X}(z),\quad X_{v}=\bar{X}_{0},\quad X_{u}=e^{-u}\bar{X}_{-1},

(2.7)

\displaystyle X(z)=W_{\leqslant 0}(z)-X_{v}-e^{u}X_{u}z^{-1},\quad\bar{X}(z)=W_{\geqslant 1}(z)+X_{v}+e^{u}X_{u}z^{-1}.

Remark 2.2
.

Recall that the projections
(\hskip 2.84526pt)_{\geqslant p}:\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\rightarrow z^{p}\mathcal{H}(D_{0})
,
(\hskip 2.84526pt)_{\leqslant p-1}:\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\rightarrow z^{p-1}\mathcal{H}(D_{\infty})
and
(\hskip 2.84526pt)_{p}:\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
are defined by

(2.8)

\displaystyle(f)_{\geqslant p}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geqslant p}f_{k}z^{k}=\frac{z^{p}}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{|z|<|w|}\frac{w^{-p}f(w)}{w-z}dw,

(2.9)

\displaystyle(f)_{\leqslant p-1}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\leqslant p-1}f_{k}z^{k}=-\frac{z^{p}}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{|z|>|w|}\frac{w^{-p}f(w)}{w-z}dw,

(2.10)

\displaystyle(f)_{p}

\displaystyle=f_{p}=\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{|z|=1}f(z)z^{-p}\frac{dz}{z},

where
f(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{k}z^{k}
and
p\in\mathbb{Z}
.

2.4.
The metric and the contangent bundle

On the tangent spaces we define a symmetric non-degenerate bilinear form
\eta
, called the metric, by

(2.11)

\eta(\hat{X},\hat{Y})=\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{X(z)Y(z)}{z^{2}w^{\prime}(z)}dz+X_{v}Y_{u}+X_{u}Y_{v},

where
\hat{X},\hat{Y}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
are represented as triples in
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
By explicitly constructing the flat coordinates, it was proved in
[
CDM11
]
that the metric
\eta
is flat.

The cotangent space
T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
is defined as the algebraic dual to the tangent space, i.e., as the space
(T_{\hat{\lambda}}M)^{*}
of all linear functionals on
T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
.
The metric defines an injection
\eta_{*}
of
T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
into
T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
by

(2.12)

\hat{X}\mapsto\eta_{*}(\hat{X})=\eta(\hat{X},\cdot).

A cotangent vector
\xi\in T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
that is in the image of
\eta_{*}
is called representable, and we denote
\xi\in T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M^{\mathrm{rep}}
.

Remark 2.3
.

In this work we take a rather different approach to the cotangent bundle compared to
[
CDM11
,
CM15
]
. This is motivated by the fact that we need to consider functionals on
M_{0}
whose differentials are not representable. For example, the differentials
d\lambda(p)
,
d\bar{\lambda}(p)
and
du_{p}
are not representable.

2.5.
The associative product

The product on the tangent spaces is defined by

(2.13)

\displaystyle\hat{X}\cdot\hat{Y}=

\displaystyle\Bigg(X(z)\left(Y_{>0}(z)-(zw^{\prime}(z))_{>0}\frac{Y(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}+\frac{Y(z)}{w^{\prime}(z)}+\frac{e^{u}}{z}\left(\frac{Y(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}+Y_{u}\right)+Y_{v}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+zw^{\prime}(z)\bigg(\left(X_{>0}(z)\frac{Y(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{<0}-\left(X_{\leqslant 0}(z)\frac{Y(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{\geqslant 0}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{e^{u}}{z}X_{u}\left(\frac{Y(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}+Y_{u}\right)\left.+X_{v}\frac{Y(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right),

\displaystyle\left(e^{u}(X(z)+zw^{\prime}(z)X_{u})\left(\frac{Y(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}+Y_{u}\right)\right)_{1}-e^{u}X_{u}Y_{u}+X_{v}Y_{v},

\displaystyle\left(X(z)\frac{Y(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{0}+X_{u}Y_{v}+X_{v}Y_{u}\Bigg)

for
\hat{X},\hat{Y}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
represented as triples in
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
It was proved in
[
CDM11
]
that the product is commutative, associative, with a unit vector field given by
e=(-1,1)
or, equivalently, by
e=(0,1,0)
.
Moreover, it is compatible with the metric
\eta
, namely

(2.14)

\eta(\hat{X}\cdot\hat{Y},\hat{Z})=\eta(\hat{X},\hat{Y}\cdot\hat{Z})

for any
\hat{X},\hat{Y},\hat{Z}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
. If follows that
\eta(\hat{X},\hat{Y})=\xi(\hat{X}\cdot\hat{Y})
for
\xi\in T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M^{\mathrm{rep}}
, with
\xi=\eta_{*}(e)=du
.

Remark 2.4
.

Expression (
2.13
) for the product of tangent vectors corrects a sign mistake in the literature, cf.
[
CM15
, Lemma 15]
.

Finally, the Euler vector field is defined by

(2.15)

E=(\lambda(z)-z\lambda^{\prime}(z),\bar{\lambda}(z)-z\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)),\qquad\text{or}\qquad E=(w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z),v,2).

In
[
CDM11
]
it is proved that

Theorem 2.5
.

(M_{0},\eta,\cdot,e,E)
is an infinite-dimensional Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold of charge
d=1
.

2.6.
The operators
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}

The operator
\mathcal{U}:T_{\hat{\lambda}}M\rightarrow T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
of multiplication by the Euler vector field is defined on each tangent space as
\mathcal{U}(\hat{X})=E\cdot\hat{X}
. Using (
2.13
) one obtains

(2.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\hat{X})=

\displaystyle\Bigg((w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))\left(X_{>0}(z)-(zw^{\prime}(z))_{>0}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}+\frac{X(z)}{w^{\prime}(z)}+\frac{e^{u}}{z}\left(\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}+X_{u}\right)+X_{v}\right)+

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+zw^{\prime}(z)\Bigg(\left((w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))_{>0}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{<0}-\left((w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))_{\leqslant 0}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{\geqslant 0}

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt+2\frac{e^{u}}{z}\left(\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}+X_{u}\right)+v\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\Bigg),

\displaystyle\left(e^{u}(w(z)+zw^{\prime}(z))\left(\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}+X_{u}\right)\right)_{1}-2e^{u}X_{u}+vX_{v},

\displaystyle\left((w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{0}+2X_{v}+vX_{u}\Bigg).

The grading operator
\mathcal{V}:T_{\hat{\lambda}}M\rightarrow T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
is defined as

(2.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=\frac{1}{2}-\nabla E,

where
\nabla
is the Levi-Civita connection of the metric
\eta
. Explicitly, see
[
CM15
]
, it is given by

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\hat{X})=\left(-\frac{X(z)}{2}+z\partial_{z}\left(X(z)\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right),-\frac{X_{v}}{2},\frac{X_{u}}{2}\right).

2.7.
At a special point

To simplify computations, we will specialize certain constructions to a two-dimensional submanifold of
M_{0}
given by the points
\hat{\lambda}_{0}
of the form

(2.19)

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}(z)=z-v-e^{u}z^{-1},\qquad\bar{\lambda}_{0}(z)=v+e^{u}z^{-1}

or, written as a triple,

(2.20)

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{0}=(z,v,u).

Notice that conditions
(T1)
-
(T5)
are satisfied if
|e^{u}|\not=1
.

At
\hat{\lambda}_{0}
the operators
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
have the simpler form

(2.21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\hat{X})

\displaystyle=\left((v+2e^{u}z^{-1})X(z)+2e^{u}X_{u},\ 2e^{u}X_{1}+vX_{v},\ 2X_{v}+vX_{u}\right),

(2.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\hat{X})

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{X(z)}{2}+zX^{\prime}(z),-\frac{X_{v}}{2},\frac{X_{u}}{2}\right).

3.
Spectrum of
\mathcal{U}
and canonical coordinates

In this section, we compute the spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{U}
at an arbitrary point of the Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold and we show that the generalized eigenvalues correspond to the continuous canonical coordinates introduced in
[
CDM11
]
, while the discrete spectrum is given by the critical values of
\lambda
and
\bar{\lambda}
.

3.1.
Canonical coordinates

For a semisimple finite dimensional Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold with superpotential
\lambda(z)
, the canonical coordinates are typically given by the critical values of
\lambda(z)
. In the case of the infinite dimensional Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold
M_{0}
, however, it is not immediately clear what should take the place of the critical values, since one expects an infinite number of canonical coordinates and, instead of a single superpotential, there are two:
\lambda(z)
and
\bar{\lambda}(z)
.

In
[
CDM11
]
it was suggested to consider the following linear combination of the two superpotentials

(3.1)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\sigma}(z)=\sigma\bar{\lambda}(z)+(\sigma-1)\lambda(z)\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1})

for a parameter
\sigma\in\mathbb{C}
. One should then look for the critical points of
\lambda_{\sigma}(z)
that are located on
S^{1}
.
The condition
\lambda_{\sigma}^{\prime}(z)=0
for
z\in S^{1}
defines a curve
\Sigma=\{\sigma(z)|\ z\in S^{1}\}
, parameterized by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sigma(z)=\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(z)}{\lambda^{\prime}(z)+\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)}\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1}),

which is holomorphic on
S^{1}
as the denominator is non-vanishing for
\hat{\lambda}\in M_{0}
, and is non-singular, i.e.
\sigma^{\prime}(z)\not=0
, if and only if

(3.3)

\lambda^{\prime}(z)\bar{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(z)-\lambda^{\prime\prime}(z)\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)\not=0

for
z\in S^{1}
. For non self-intersecting
\Sigma
, we define the (continuous part of the) canonical coordinates at the point
\hat{\lambda}
as the set of critical values

(3.4)

u_{\sigma}=\lambda_{\sigma}(z(\sigma))

for
\sigma\in\Sigma
, where
z(\sigma):\Sigma\to S^{1}
is the inverse of
\sigma(z)
, which is a critical point of
\lambda_{\sigma}(z)
. Since
\Sigma
is parameterized by
z\in S^{1}
, we might as well index these coordinates by
p\in S^{1}
, denoting
u_{p}=u_{\sigma(p)}=\lambda_{\sigma(p)}(p)
.

In the following, we show that this seemingly ad hoc definition of canonical coordinates emerges naturally from the spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{U}
. Indeed, the generalized eigenvalues of
\mathcal{U}
are exactly given by the canonical coordinates defined above.

The operator
\mathcal{U}
turns out to also have standard eigenvalues, which are given by the critical values of the superpotentials
-\lambda(z)
and
\bar{\lambda}(z)
on their respective domains of definition,
D_{\infty}
and
D_{0}
. More precisely, consider a point of
M_{0}
at which
\lambda(z)
, resp.
\bar{\lambda}(z)
, has
n
, resp.
\bar{n}
, critical points in
D_{\infty}
, resp.
D_{0}
.
We define the following critical values:

(3.5)

\displaystyle u_{i}=-\lambda(z_{i}),\qquad

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(z_{i})=0,\qquad

\displaystyle z_{i}\in D_{\infty},\qquad

\displaystyle i=1,\dots,n,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{i}=\bar{\lambda}(\bar{z}_{i}),\qquad

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(\bar{z}_{i})=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\bar{z}_{i}\in D_{0},\qquad

\displaystyle i=1,\dots,\bar{n}.

The canonical coordinates on
M_{0}
are given by the set of all critical values as defined above:

(3.7)

\{u_{p},u_{i},\bar{u}_{j}\}_{p\in S^{1},i=1,\dots,n,j=1,\dots,\bar{n}}.

The differentials of the discrete canonical coordinates
u_{i}
,
\bar{u}_{j}
are

(3.8)

du_{i}=-d\lambda(z_{i}),\qquad d\bar{u}_{i}=d\bar{\lambda}(\bar{z}_{i}),

which can be represented as vectors in
T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
via the injection
\eta_{*}
as follows

(3.9)

\displaystyle du_{i}=\left(zw^{\prime}(z)\frac{z_{i}}{z-z_{i}},\frac{e^{u}}{z_{i}},1\right),\qquad d\bar{u}_{i}=\left(zw^{\prime}(z)\frac{\bar{z}_{i}}{z-\bar{z}_{i}},\frac{e^{u}}{\bar{z}_{i}},1\right).

We will show below that these differentials are actually the eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalues
u_{i}
and
\bar{u}_{j}
of
\mathcal{U}
.

It turns out that the generalized eigenvectors of
\mathcal{U}
, corresponding to the continuous family of canonical coordinates
u_{p}
, are given by

(3.10)

\displaystyle du_{p}:=d\lambda_{\sigma}(p)|_{\sigma=\sigma(p)}=(\sigma(p)-1)d\lambda(p)+\sigma(p)d\bar{\lambda}(p),

for
p\in S^{1}
.

Remark 3.1
.

Notice that in the previous definition we have slightly abused the notation, since the last formula does not represent the differential of
u_{p}
, but the differential of
\lambda_{\sigma}(z)
for fixed
\sigma
, later evaluated at
\sigma=\sigma(p)
. This is consistent with the fact that, as in the case of discrete canonical coordinates, the critical point should be allowed to vary as we differentiate along the Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold, but on the contrary it would be fixed at a point of
S^{1}
if we differentiated directly
u_{p}
.

Remark 3.2
.

The formula for the continuous canonical coordinates might be understood as the Legendre transform of the function
\lambda(w)=\lambda(z(w))
, where
z(w)
is the inverse of the function
w(z)=\lambda(z)+\bar{\lambda}(z)
defined on
S^{1}
. Denote by
w(\sigma)
the inverse of

(3.11)

\sigma(w)=\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial w}(w)=\lambda^{\prime}(z(w))z^{\prime}(w)=\left.\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(z)}{\lambda^{\prime}(z)+\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)}\right|_{z=z(w)}.

The Legendre transform of
\lambda(w)
is indeed

(3.12)

\displaystyle\sigma w(\sigma)-\lambda(w(\sigma))

\displaystyle=\sigma w(z(\sigma))-\lambda(w(z(\sigma)))=

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\left[\sigma(\lambda(z)+\bar{\lambda}(z))-\lambda(z)\right]_{z=z(\sigma)}=\lambda_{\sigma}(z(\sigma))=u_{\sigma},

where
z(\sigma)
is the inverse of (
3.2
).

3.2.
Spectrum of
\mathcal{U}

Let us consider the operator
\mathcal{U}
of multiplication by the Euler vector field
E
, see (
2.16
), at an arbitrary point
\hat{\lambda}
in
M_{0}
:

(3.14)

\mathcal{U}:T_{\hat{\lambda}}M\to T_{\hat{\lambda}}M.

The generalized spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{U}
is defined as the spectrum of the transpose

(3.15)

\mathcal{U}^{*}:T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M\to T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M,

defined by
<\mathcal{U}^{*}\xi,\hat{X}>=<\xi,\mathcal{U}\hat{X}>
for all
\hat{X}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
.
Explicitly, we say that
\xi\in T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
is a generalized eigenvector corresponding to the generalized eigenvalue
\mu
if

(3.16)

<\xi,\mathcal{U}\hat{X}>=\mu<\xi,\hat{X}>

for all
\hat{X}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
.
Since
\mathcal{U}
is symmetric w.r.t. the metric
\eta
, a standard eigenvector
\hat{X}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
with eigenvalue
\mu
is mapped by the injection
\eta_{*}
to a generalized eigenvector for the same eigenvalue
\mu
.

Notice that a family
E\subset T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
of cotangent vectors defines a map from
T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
to the space of functions over
E
. We say that
E
is complete if such map is injective, i.e., it defines an isomorphism of
T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
with the space of functions
E^{\prime}
given by its image.

Proposition 3.3
.

At an arbitrary point
\hat{\lambda}
of
M_{0}
the spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{U}
is given by

(1)

the eigenvalues
u_{i}
with eigenvectors
du_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
,

(2)

the eigenvalues
\bar{u}_{j}
with eigenvectors
d\bar{u}_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,\bar{n}
, and

(3)

the generalized eigenvalues
u_{p}
with generalized eigenvectors
du_{p}
for
p\in S^{1}
.

Moreover, the set of all eigenvectors
\{du_{p},du_{i},d\bar{u}_{j}\}
is a complete family in
T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
.

Actually, the completeness of the set of eigenvectors is realized via an explicit isomorphism

\displaystyle\Psi:T_{\hat{\lambda}}M

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\bar{n}}

(3.17)

\displaystyle\hat{X}

\displaystyle\longmapsto(\langle du_{z},\hat{X}\rangle,\langle du_{i},\hat{X}\rangle,\langle d\bar{u}_{j},\hat{X}\rangle).

Corollary 3.4
.

The operator
\mathcal{U}
in the representation given by
\Psi
, i.e.
U:=\Psi\mathcal{U}\Psi^{-1}
, is diagonal

\displaystyle U:\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\bar{n}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\bar{n}}

(3.18)

\displaystyle\hat{Y}=(Y(z),\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,n},\{\bar{Y}_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,\bar{n}})

\displaystyle\longmapsto U(\hat{Y})=(u_{z}Y(z),\{u_{i}Y_{i}\}_{i},\{\bar{u}_{j}\bar{Y}_{j}\}_{j}).

We now proceed to prove Proposition
3.3
first by an explicit approach at the special point in the following section, then in the general case in §
3.5
. In §
3.4
we prove a key lemma that will also be used in later sections.

3.3.
Proof at the special point

At the special point
\hat{\lambda}_{0}=(z,v,u)
, the operator
\mathcal{U}
takes the simpler form (
2.21
). This allows us to give an explicit proof of the proposition. It is evident in this proof that the formula for the canonical coordinates emerges from and is uniquely determined by the form of the operator
\mathcal{U}
. The first part of Proposition
3.3
can be restated as

Lemma 3.5
.

The operator
\mathcal{U}
acting on
T_{\hat{\lambda}_{0}}M\cong\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}
has the following eigenvalues and eigenvectors

(3.19)

\displaystyle u_{\pm}=v\pm 2{\mathrm{i}}e^{u/2},\qquad du_{\pm}=(\pm z(z\pm{\mathrm{i}}e^{u/2})^{-1},\mp 1,{\mathrm{i}}e^{-u/2}),

iff
|e^{u}|>1
and the following generalized eigenvalues and eigenvectors

(3.20)

\displaystyle u_{p}=v+\frac{2e^{u}}{p},\qquad\langle du_{p},\hat{X}\rangle=\frac{e^{u}}{p^{2}}X(p)+X_{\geqslant 1}(p)+X_{v}+\frac{e^{u}}{p}X_{u},

for
p\in S^{1}
.

Proof.

First, let us compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The equation
\mathcal{U}(\hat{X})=\mu\hat{X}
takes the explicit form

(3.21)

\displaystyle(v+2e^{u}z^{-1})X(z)+2e^{u}X_{u}=\mu X(z),

(3.22)

\displaystyle 2e^{u}X_{1}+vX_{v}=\mu X_{v},

(3.23)

\displaystyle 2X_{v}+vX_{u}=\mu X_{u}.

For
\mu=v
the system becomes

(3.24)

\displaystyle z^{-1}X(z)+X_{u}=0,

(3.25)

\displaystyle X_{1}=0,

(3.26)

\displaystyle X_{v}=0.

The first equation implies that the only possibly non-zero coefficient of the Laurent expansion
X(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}X_{k}z^{k}
is
X_{1}
, which is zero by the second equation. Thus,
X_{u}
also vanishes and
\hat{X}=0
, so
\mu=v
is not an eigenvalue. Therefore, we can assume
\mu\not=v
.

Let
p=\frac{2e^{u}}{\mu-v}
. The system becomes

(3.27)

\displaystyle(z-p)X(z)=p^{3}e^{-u}zX_{1},

(3.28)

\displaystyle pX_{1}=X_{v},

(3.29)

\displaystyle p^{2}e^{-u}X_{1}=X_{u}.

We rewrite the first equation as

(3.30)

\displaystyle\frac{X(z)}{z}=\frac{p^{3}e^{-u}X_{1}}{z-p}.

If
|p|=1
, the function
X(z)
defined as above would have a single pole at
p
, so it would not be an element of
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})
. Extracting the zeroth coefficient of the Laurent expansion of the left-hand side yields

(3.31)

\displaystyle 1=\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{p^{3}e^{-u}}{z-p}\frac{dz}{z}.

If
|p|<1
, the two poles of the integrand lie inside the unit circle, so the integral vanishes and the equation admits no solutions. If
|p|>1
, we obtain
e^{u}=-p^{2}
, which has two solutions iff
|e^{u}|>1
, namely
p_{\pm}=\mp{\mathrm{i}}e^{u/2}
, which correspond to the eigenvalues
u_{\pm}
and the eigenvectors
du_{\pm}
.

Let us now compute the generalized eigenvalues. Let
\rho=\mu-v
, then the generalized eigenvalue equation takes the form

(3.32)

\displaystyle\langle\omega_{z},(2e^{u}z^{-1}-\rho)X(z)+2e^{u}X_{u}\rangle+\langle\omega_{v},2e^{u}X_{1}-\rho X_{v}\rangle+\langle\omega_{u},2X_{v}-\rho X_{u}\rangle=0,

for a functional
\omega=\omega_{z}+\omega_{v}+\omega_{u}
. If
\rho=0
, then the previous equation becomes

(3.33)

\displaystyle\langle\omega_{z},2e^{u}z^{-1}X(z)+2e^{u}X_{u}\rangle+\langle\omega_{v},2e^{u}X_{1}\rangle+\langle\omega_{u},2X_{v}\rangle=0.

Choosing
\hat{X}=(0,X_{v},0)
implies
\omega_{u}=0
. Choosing
\hat{X}=(0,0,X_{u})
implies
\omega_{z}
is zero on constants. Then choosing
\hat{X}=(X_{1}z,0,0)
shows that
\langle\omega_{z},2e^{u}z^{-1}X(z)\rangle=\langle\omega_{z},2e^{u}X_{1}\rangle=0
because the argument is constant, so we can conclude that
\omega_{v}=0
. Finally, we choose
\hat{X}=(X(z),0,0)
, which shows
\omega_{z}=0
. Therefore, we can assume
\rho\not=0
.

Let
p=\frac{2e^{u}}{\rho}
. Substituting in the equation above, we obtain

(3.34)

\displaystyle\left\langle\omega_{z},\left(\frac{p}{z}-1\right)X(z)+pX_{u}\right\rangle+\langle\omega_{v},pX_{1}-X_{v}\rangle+\langle\omega_{u},e^{-u}pX_{v}-X_{u}\rangle=0.

Choosing
\hat{X}=(0,0,X_{u})
implies
\omega_{u}=p\langle\omega_{z},1\rangle
. Choosing
\hat{X}=(0,X_{v},0)
implies
\omega_{v}=e^{-u}p\omega_{u}=e^{-u}p^{2}\langle\omega_{z},1\rangle
. Substituting and setting
\hat{X}=(X(z),0,0)
yields

(3.35)

\displaystyle\left\langle\omega_{z},\left(\frac{p}{z}-1\right)X(z)\right\rangle+e^{-u}p^{3}X_{1}\langle\omega_{z},1\rangle=0.

Consider first the case
|p|\not=1
. Multiplication by
\left(\frac{p}{z}-1\right)
is then invertible in
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})
, so we obtain

(3.36)

\displaystyle\langle\omega_{z},X(z)\rangle=-e^{-u}p^{3}\left(\frac{z}{p-z}X(z)\right)_{1}\langle\omega_{z},1\rangle.

Clearly
\omega_{z}=0
iff
\langle\omega_{z},1\rangle=0
. Thus, we can assume
\langle\omega_{z},1\rangle\not=0
and, without loss of generality, take
\langle\omega_{z},1\rangle=1
. Setting
X(z)=1
gives the equation

(3.37)

\displaystyle 1=-e^{-u}p^{3}\left(\frac{z}{p-z}\right)_{1}=-e^{-u}p^{3}\left(\frac{1}{p-z}\right)_{0}.

If
|p|<1
, the right-hand side vanishes, so there is no solution. If
|p|>1
, the equation becomes
p^{2}=-e^{u}
, which admits the solutions
p_{\pm}=\mp{\mathrm{i}}e^{u/2}
when
|e^{u}|>1
.
The generalized eigenvectors
\omega_{\pm}
associated with
p_{\pm}
have eigenvalues
u_{\pm}
and correspond to the eigenvectors
du_{\pm}
computed above,
more precisely
\eta_{*}du_{\pm}=-{\mathrm{i}}e^{-u/2}\omega_{\pm}
.

Finally, let us consider the case
|p|=1
. One can check that the functional given by

(3.38)

\displaystyle\langle\omega_{z},X(z)\rangle=e^{-u}p^{2}X_{\geqslant 1}(p)+X(p)

satisfies
\langle\omega_{z},1\rangle=1
and equation (
3.35
). Let us now show that it is the only solution for fixed
p
with
|p|=1
. Let
\alpha_{z}
be a solution of (
3.35
) with
\langle\alpha_{z},1\rangle=0
. Then
\alpha_{z}
is zero on the subspace
\left(\frac{p}{z}-1\right)\mathcal{H}(S^{1})
, which is the subspace of
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})
of functions vanishing at
z=p
. Therefore,

(3.39)

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{z},X(z)\rangle=\langle\alpha_{z},X(p)\rangle+\langle\alpha_{z},(X(z)-X(p))\rangle=X(p)\langle\alpha_{z},1\rangle=0,

so
\alpha_{z}=0
. Now let
\omega^{\prime}_{z}
be a solution of (
3.35
) with
\langle\omega^{\prime}_{z},1\rangle\not=0
. We can renormalize it and consider the case
\langle\omega^{\prime}_{z},1\rangle=1
. Then
\omega_{z}-\omega^{\prime}_{z}
is a solution of (
3.35
) vanishing on 1, so it must be identically zero, hence
\omega^{\prime}_{z}=\omega_{z}
. The result follows by noting that
\omega=e^{-u}p^{2}du_{p}
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Notice that in this case we have

(3.40)

\langle du_{p},\hat{X}\rangle=\left(\left(\frac{e^{u}}{p^{2}}+1\right)X\right)_{\geqslant 1}+\frac{e^{u}}{p^{2}}X_{\leqslant 0}+\frac{e^{u}}{p}(X_{1}+X_{u})+(X_{v}+e^{u}X_{2}).

In the case
|e^{u}|<1
, one can easily check that knowing
Y(p)=\langle du_{p},\hat{X}\rangle
is sufficient to reconstruct
\hat{X}
, showing completeness. However, in the case
|e^{u}|>1
, we also need to know
Y_{\pm}=\langle du_{\pm},\hat{X}\rangle
to invert (
3.2
). In Section
3.5
, we will give a general formula for
\Psi^{-1}
.

3.4.
A key lemma

The following lemma will be used in the general proof of Proposition
3.3
and also in Section
6
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\hat{X}=(X(z),X_{v},X_{u})\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}
. The function

(3.41)

\displaystyle\langle d\lambda_{\sigma}(z),\mathcal{U}\hat{X}\rangle-\lambda_{\sigma}(z)\langle d\lambda_{\sigma}(z),\hat{X}\rangle+z\lambda^{\prime}_{\sigma}(z)\left\langle d\lambda_{\sigma}(z),\left(\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}X(z),0,-X_{u}\right)\right\rangle

is a scalar multiple of
z\lambda_{\sigma}^{\prime}(z)
, namely it is equal to

(3.42)

\displaystyle z\lambda_{\sigma}^{\prime}(z)\left[\left(\left(1-\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)X(z)\right)_{0}-X_{v}\right].

Proof.

Let us rewrite
\lambda_{\sigma}(z)
as a triple in
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}

(3.43)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\sigma}(z)

\displaystyle=(\sigma-1)w(z)+w_{\geqslant 1}(z)-z+v+\frac{e^{u}}{z},

(3.44)

\displaystyle\langle d\lambda_{\sigma}(z),\hat{X}\rangle

\displaystyle=(\sigma-1)X(z)+X_{\geqslant 1}(z)+X_{v}+\frac{e^{u}}{z}X_{u}.

We proceed componentwise. Let
E(z)
denote expression (
3.41
), and let us expand
E(z)
for
\hat{X}=(X(z),0,0)

\displaystyle E(z)=

\displaystyle(\sigma-1)(w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))\left(X_{\geqslant 1}(z)-(zw^{\prime}(z))_{\geqslant 1}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}+\frac{X(z)}{w^{\prime}(z)}+\frac{e^{u}}{z}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(\sigma-1)zw^{\prime}(z)\left(\left((w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))_{>0}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{<0}-\left((w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))_{\leqslant 0}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{\geqslant 0}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(\sigma-1)\left(2\frac{e^{u}}{z}X(z)+vX(z)\right)+\left(2\frac{e^{u}}{z}X(z)+vX(z)\right)_{\geqslant 1}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left((w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))\left(X_{\geqslant 1}(z)-(zw^{\prime}(z))_{\geqslant 1}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}+\frac{X(z)}{w^{\prime}(z)}+\frac{e^{u}}{z}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)\right)_{\geqslant 1}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(zw^{\prime}(z)\left(\left((w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))_{>0}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{<0}-\left((w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))_{\leqslant 0}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{\geqslant 0}\right)\right)_{\geqslant 1}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle e^{u}\left((w(z)+zw^{\prime}(z))\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{1}+\frac{e^{u}}{z}\left((w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{0}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left((\sigma-1)w(z)+w_{\geqslant 1}(z)-z+v+\frac{e^{u}}{z}\right)\left((\sigma-1)X(z)+X_{\geqslant 1}(z)\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left((\sigma-1)zw^{\prime}(z)+(zw^{\prime}(z))_{\geqslant 1}-z-\frac{e^{u}}{z}\right)\left((\sigma-1)\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}X(z)+\left(\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}X(z)\right)_{\geqslant 1}\right).

It is immediate to see that the terms with
(\sigma-1)^{2}
,
v
, and
(\sigma-1)e^{u}
cancel out. First, we simplify the rest of the terms with
e^{u}
, which equal

\displaystyle-\frac{e^{u}}{z}\left(\left(1-\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)X(z)\right)_{0}.

Second, one can similarly see that the terms with
(\sigma-1)
equal

\displaystyle(\sigma-1)zw^{\prime}(z)\left(\left(1-\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)X(z)\right)_{0}.

Third, we split the remaining terms of
E(z)
into two groups, the first one being

\displaystyle zX_{\geqslant 1}(z)-z\left(\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}X(z)\right)_{\geqslant 1}+\left(\frac{w(z)}{w^{\prime}(z)}X(z)\right)_{\geqslant 1}-(zX(z))_{\geqslant 1}

\displaystyle=-z\left(\left(1-\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)X(z)\right)_{0}.

Finally, we are left with

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle w_{\geqslant 1}(z)X_{\geqslant 1}(z)+(w(z)X_{\geqslant 1}(z))_{\geqslant 1}+(zw^{\prime}(z))_{\geqslant 1}\left(\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}X(z)\right)_{\geqslant 1}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left((zw^{\prime}(z))_{\geqslant 1}\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}X(z)\right)_{\geqslant 1}-(zw^{\prime}(z)X(z))_{\geqslant 1}+\left((zw^{\prime}(z))_{\geqslant 1}X(z)\right)_{\geqslant 1}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(zw^{\prime}(z)\left(\left((w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))_{>0}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{<0}-\left((w(z)-zw^{\prime}(z))_{\leqslant 0}\frac{X(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)_{\geqslant 0}\right)\right)_{\geqslant 1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(zw^{\prime}(z))_{\geqslant 1}\left(\left(1-\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)X(z)\right)_{0}.

Putting everything together,

(3.45)

\displaystyle E(z)

\displaystyle=\left((\sigma-1)zw^{\prime}(z)+(zw^{\prime}(z))_{\geqslant 1}-z-\frac{e^{u}}{z}\right)\left(\left(1-\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)X(z)\right)_{0}

\displaystyle=z\lambda_{\sigma}^{\prime}(z)\left(\left(1-\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}\right)X(z)\right)_{0}.

Let
\hat{X}=(0,1,0)
. In this case, it is immediate to see

(3.46)

\displaystyle E(z)=-z\lambda_{\sigma}^{\prime}(z).

Finally, for
\hat{X}=(0,0,1)
, it is also a straightforward computation to check

(3.47)

\displaystyle E(z)=0,

concluding the proof.
∎

3.5.
Proof of Proposition
3.3
and Corollary
3.4

Let
\hat{\lambda}=(\lambda(z),\bar{\lambda}(z))\in M_{0}
be such that
\lambda(z)
has
n
critical points in the interior of
D_{\infty}
and
\bar{\lambda}(z)
has
\bar{n}
critical points in the interior of
D_{0}
. We take
\hat{\lambda}
to be generic, i.e., none of the critical points is degenerate.

The fact that the functionals
d\lambda(z_{i})
,
d\bar{\lambda}(\bar{z}_{i})
and
d\lambda_{\sigma}(p)
for
\sigma=\sigma(p)
are generalized eigenvectors of
\mathcal{U}
simply follows from Lemma
3.7
.
Indeed, let
z_{i}
be one of the critical points of
\lambda(z)
, i.e.
\lambda^{\prime}(z_{i})=0
; substituting
\sigma=0
and
z=z_{i}
in (
3.41
), we get at once that

(3.48)

\langle d\lambda(z_{i}),\mathcal{U}\hat{X}\rangle=-\lambda(z_{i})\langle d\lambda(z_{i}),\hat{X}\rangle

for all
\hat{X}
, namely
d\lambda(z_{i})
is a generalized eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue
u_{i}=-\lambda(z_{i})
. Similarly, setting
\sigma=1
and
z=\bar{z}_{i}
, resp.
\sigma=\sigma(p)
and
z=p
, we obtain the analogous statement for
d\bar{\lambda}(\bar{z}_{i})
and
\bar{u}_{i}
, resp.
(d\lambda_{\sigma}(p))|_{\sigma=\sigma(p)}
and
u_{p}
.
By (
3.8
) and (
3.10
), we have

(3.49)

du_{i}=d\lambda(z_{i}),\qquad d\bar{u}_{i}=d\bar{\lambda}(\bar{z}_{i}),\qquad du_{p}=d\lambda_{\sigma}(p)|_{\sigma=\sigma(p)}.

One can easily check that
du_{i}
and
d\bar{u}_{i}
are representable as (
3.9
), therefore they are eigenvectors.

Let us now prove that this family of generalized eigenvectors is complete. For that, we will prove that the map

(3.50)

\displaystyle\Psi:T_{\hat{\lambda}}M

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\bar{n}}

\displaystyle\hat{X}

\displaystyle\longmapsto(\langle du_{p},\hat{X}\rangle,\langle du_{i},\hat{X}\rangle,\langle d\bar{u}_{j},\hat{X}\rangle)

defines an isomorphism of vector spaces.
Let us consider tangent vectors as pairs
\hat{X}=(X(z),\bar{X}(z))\in\mathcal{H}(D_{\infty})\oplus\frac{1}{z}\mathcal{H}(D_{0})
, and let

(3.52)

Y(p)=\langle du_{p},\hat{X}\rangle,\qquad Y_{i}=\langle du_{i},\hat{X}\rangle,\qquad\bar{Y}_{i}=\langle d\bar{u}_{i},\hat{X}\rangle.

Explicitly,

(3.53)

\displaystyle Y(p)=\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(p)}{\lambda^{\prime}(p)+\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(p)}\bar{X}(p)-\frac{\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(p)}{\lambda^{\prime}(p)+\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(p)}X(p),\qquad Y_{i}=-X(z_{i}),\qquad\bar{Y}_{i}=\bar{X}(\bar{z}_{i}).

It is enough to observe that the inverse
\Psi^{-1}
is given by

(3.54)

\displaystyle\bar{X}(p)

\displaystyle=\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(p)\left[\mu_{\hat{Y}}(p)+\left(\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(p)+\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(p)}{\lambda^{\prime}(p)\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(p)}Y(p)\right)_{\geqslant 1}\right],

(3.55)

\displaystyle X(p)

\displaystyle=-\lambda^{\prime}(p)\left[-\mu_{\hat{Y}}(p)+\left(\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(p)+\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(p)}{\lambda^{\prime}(p)\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}(p)}Y(p)\right)_{\leqslant 0}\right],

where

(3.56)

\displaystyle\mu_{\hat{Y}}(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{Y_{i}}{z_{i}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(z_{i})}\frac{p}{z_{i}-p}-\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}\frac{\bar{Y}_{i}}{\bar{z}_{i}\bar{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(\bar{z}_{i})}\frac{p}{\bar{z}_{i}-p}.

Let
\hat{Y}=(Y(p),Y_{i},\bar{Y}_{i})\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\bar{n}}
and
\hat{X}=\Psi^{-1}\hat{Y}
.
Corollary
3.4
follows from observing that

(3.57)

\Psi\mathcal{U}\hat{X}=(\langle du_{p},\mathcal{U}\hat{X}\rangle,\langle du_{i},\mathcal{U}\hat{X}\rangle,\langle d\bar{u}_{j},\mathcal{U}\hat{X}\rangle)=(u_{p}Y(p),u_{i}Y_{i},\bar{u}_{j}\bar{Y}_{j}).

To conclude, we notice that the (generalized) eigenvalues of
\mathcal{U}
coincide with those of
U
, the eigenvectors being related by the isomorphism
\Psi
.
Notice that, since
\lambda_{\sigma}^{\prime}(p)=0
for

,
we have

(3.58)

\frac{du_{p}}{dp}=\sigma^{\prime}(p)w(p).

Therefore, because of axioms
(T3)
and
(T4)
,

is non-vanishing on

.
This implies that the generalized eigenspaces are only those given in the proposition, see the following remarks for further details.

Remark 3.8
.

Consider the operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\bar{n}}
given in Corollary
3.4
, namely

(3.59)

U(X(z),X_{i},\bar{X}_{j})=(u_{z}X(z),u_{i}X_{i},\bar{u}_{j}\bar{X}_{j})

for
u_{z}\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1})
.
Clearly, the set of generalized and standard eigenvalues corresponds to the set of
u_{p}
,
u_{i}
and
\bar{u}_{j}
, namely

Lemma 3.9
.

The spectrum of
U
is given by

\{u_{p},u_{i},\bar{u}_{j}\}_{p\in S^{1},i=1,\dots,n,j=1,\dots,\bar{n}}
.

Proof.

From the diagonal form of
U
, it is immediately clear that the standard eigenvalues are
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,n}
and
\{\bar{u}_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,\bar{n}}
with eigenvectors
(0,e_{i},0)
and
(0,0,e_{j})
, respectively, where
e_{i}
denotes the canonical basis vector which is
1
at the
i
-th entry and
0
everywhere else.

In order to find its generalized eigenvalues, we look for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
0\not=\xi\in T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
such that

(3.60)

\displaystyle\langle\xi,\left((u_{z}-\lambda)X(z),(u_{i}-\lambda)X_{i},(\bar{u}_{j}-\lambda)\bar{X}_{j}\right)\rangle=0,\qquad\forall\hat{X}\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\bar{n}}.

Consider the decomposition
\xi=(\xi_{z},\xi_{i},\bar{\xi}_{j})
given by

(3.61)

\displaystyle\langle\xi,\hat{X}\rangle=\langle\xi_{z},X(z)\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\langle\xi_{i},e_{i}\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{n}}\bar{X}_{j}\langle\bar{\xi}_{j},e_{j}\rangle.

For
p\in S^{1}
, one can check that
u_{p}
is a generalized eigenvalue with generalized eigenvector
(\textrm{ev}_{p},0,0)
, where the functional
\textrm{ev}_{p}
is defined by

(3.62)

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{ev}_{p},X(z)\rangle=X(p).

Finally, let
\lambda\not=u_{i},\bar{u}_{j},u_{p}
for any
i,j,p
. Since
\lambda\not=u_{i},\bar{u}_{j}
, then we have
\xi_{i}=\bar{\xi}_{j}=0
for all
i,j
, so we are left with

(3.63)

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{z},(u_{z}-\lambda)X(z)\rangle=0,\qquad\forall X\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1}).

Since
\lambda\not=u_{p}
for any
p\in S^{1}
, then multiplication by
(u_{z}-\lambda)
is an invertible operator in
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})
, so
\xi_{z}=0
, hence
\lambda
is not an eigenvalue.
∎

Let us now compute the dimension of the (generalized) eigenspaces. Since
\frac{du_{z}}{dz}
does not vanish on
S^{1}
, we have the following

Lemma 3.10
.

Suppose exactly
s+k+\ell
generalized eigenvalues coincide, namely

(3.64)

\displaystyle u_{p_{1}}=\dots=u_{p_{s}}=u_{i_{1}}=\dots=u_{i_{k}}=\bar{u}_{j_{1}}=\dots=\bar{u}_{j_{\ell}}.

Then the corresponding eigenspace is
s+k+\ell
dimensional.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
denote (
3.64
). Then the generalized eigenspace of
\lambda
splits into two subspaces, the
(k+\ell)
-dimensional subspace corresponding to the standard eigenvectors
\{(0,e_{i_{r}},0),(0,e_{j_{l}},0)\}_{r=1,\dots,k}^{l=1,\dots,\ell}
mentioned before, and the subspace given by
\xi=(\xi_{z},0,0)
with
\xi_{z}
satisfying equation (
3.63
).
Let us compute the latter for
s\geqslant 1
. By (
3.58
) and axioms
(T3)
and
(T4)
, the function
\frac{du_{z}}{dz}
does not vanish on
S^{1}
, so
u_{z}-\lambda
does not have double zeros on
S^{1}
, i.e.,

(3.65)

\displaystyle u_{z}-\lambda=(z-p_{1})\dots(z-p_{s})g(z),

where
g(z)
is a non-vanishing holomorphic function on
S^{1}
. Therefore, since multiplication by
g(z)
is invertible on
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})
, equation (
3.63
) becomes

(3.66)

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{z},(z-p_{1})\dots(z-p_{s})X(z)\rangle=0,\qquad\forall X\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1}),

or, equivalently,
\xi_{z}
vanishes on the subspace of
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})
given by functions with zeros at the distinct points
p_{1},\dots,p_{s}
. It is clear that the functionals
\textrm{ev}_{p_{1}},\dots,\mathrm{ev}_{p_{s}}
defined in (
3.62
) are linearly independent and solve (
3.66
). Let us show that they span the whole space of solutions of (
3.66
).

For that, we need the following decomposition formula: for any
X\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1})
,
s\geqslant 1
, we can write

(3.67)

\displaystyle X(z)=X(p_{1})+(z-p_{1})Y_{1}+(z-p_{1})(z-p_{2})Y_{2}+\dots+(z-p_{1})\dots(z-p_{s})Y_{s}

where
Y_{<s}\in\mathbb{C}
,
Y_{s}\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1})
. This statement can be easily proved by induction. For
s=1
, it is clear by taking

(3.68)

\displaystyle Y_{1}(z)=\frac{1}{z-p_{1}}\left(X(z)-X(p_{1})\right).

Assuming it holds for
s-1\geqslant 1
, we write

(3.69)

\displaystyle X(z)

\displaystyle=X(p_{1})+(z-p_{1})Y_{1}+(z-p_{1})(z-p_{2})Y_{2}+\dots+(z-p_{1})\dots(z-p_{s-1})Y_{s-1}(z)

\displaystyle=X(p_{1})+(z-p_{1})Y_{1}+(z-p_{1})(z-p_{2})Y_{2}+\dots+(z-p_{1})\dots(z-p_{s-1})Y_{s-1}(p_{s})

\displaystyle+(z-p_{1})\dots(z-p_{s})\frac{Y_{s-1}(z)-Y_{s-1}(p_{s})}{z-p_{s}},

where we have split

(3.70)

\displaystyle Y_{s-1}(z)=Y_{s-1}(p_{s})+(z-p_{s})\frac{Y_{s-1}(z)-Y_{s-1}(p_{s})}{z-p_{s}}.

Applying (
3.67
), we write

(3.71)

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{z},X(z)\rangle

\displaystyle=X(p_{1})\langle\xi_{z},1\rangle+Y_{1}\langle\xi_{z},z-p_{1}\rangle+\dots+Y_{s-1}\langle\xi_{z},(z-p_{1})\dots(z-p_{s-1})\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\xi_{z},(z-p_{1})\dots(z-p_{s})Y_{s}(z)\rangle.

The last summand vanishes because
\xi_{z}
satisfies equation (
3.66
). Therefore,
\xi_{z}
is completely determined by the numbers

(3.72)

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{z},1\rangle,\langle\xi_{z},z\rangle,\dots,\langle\xi_{z},z^{s-1}\rangle,

so the space of solutions of (

) is at most
s
-dimensional, hence it must be the span of
\textrm{ev}_{p_{1}},\dots,\mathrm{ev}_{p_{s}}

∎

Remark 3.11
.

Notice that relaxing the axioms

and

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{ev}_{p_{i}}^{(m)},X(z)\rangle=\frac{d^{m}}{dz^{m}}\bigg|_{z=p_{i}}X(z),\qquad m=0,\dots,N_{i}-1.

4.
Dubrovin equation

It is well known that the geometric structure of a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold is (almost) completely encoded in the flatness of the so-called deformed flat connection
\widetilde{\nabla}
, which is an extension to
M_{0}\times\mathbb{C}^{*}
of the Levi-Civita connection of the metric
\eta
obtained by deforming it using the associative product on the tangent bundle.
In our case, if
\nabla
denotes the Levi-Civita connection of the metric
\eta
, then the deformed flat connection
\widetilde{\nabla}
on
M_{0}\times\mathbb{C}^{*}
is defined by
[
CM15
]

(4.1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{\hat{X}}\hat{Y}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\hat{X}}\hat{Y}+\zeta\hat{X}\cdot\hat{Y},

(4.2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{\frac{d}{d\zeta}}\hat{X}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\zeta}\hat{X}+\mathcal{U}(\hat{X})-\frac{1}{\zeta}\mathcal{V}(\hat{X}),

(4.3)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{\hat{X}}\frac{d}{d\zeta}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\nabla}_{\frac{d}{d\zeta}}\frac{d}{d\zeta}=0,

for
\hat{X},\hat{Y}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
, where the operators
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
are given by (
2.16
) and (
2.18
), respectively.

In Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold theory, one is interested in looking for differentials
dy\in T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
that are covariantly constant w.r.t. the deformed flat connection
\widetilde{\nabla}
. A basis of solutions adapted to
\zeta\sim 0
provides a family of so-called deformed flat coordinates, the coefficients of which define the Hamiltonian densities of the principal hierarchy associated with the Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold. See
[
DZ01
]
for the general construction and
[
CM15
]
for the derivation of the principal hierarchy of
M_{0}
.

In this paper we focus on the Dubrovin equation, namely the flatness equation in the
\frac{d}{d\zeta}
direction, corresponding to (
4.2
) in the definition of the deformed flat connection.
The covariant derivative w.r.t.
\frac{d}{d\zeta}

on an element of the cotangent space
\alpha\in T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
depending on the deformation parameter
\zeta
is given by

(4.4)

\widetilde{\nabla}_{\frac{d}{d\zeta}}(\alpha)=\partial_{\zeta}\alpha-\mathcal{U}^{*}\alpha+\frac{1}{\zeta}\mathcal{V}^{*}\alpha,

where
\mathcal{U}^{*}
and
\mathcal{V}^{*}
denote the trasposes of
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
. In other words, the cotangent vector
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\frac{d}{d\zeta}}(\alpha)
is defined by

(4.5)

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\nabla}_{\frac{d}{d\zeta}}(\alpha),\hat{X}\rangle=\partial_{\zeta}\langle\alpha,\hat{X}\rangle-\langle\alpha,\left(\mathcal{U}-\frac{1}{\zeta}\mathcal{V}\right)\hat{X}\rangle,

for all
\hat{X}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
.

The Dubrovin equation
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\frac{d}{d\zeta}}(\alpha)=0
is therefore given by

(4.6)

\partial_{\zeta}\langle\alpha,\hat{X}\rangle=\langle\alpha,\left(\mathcal{U}-\frac{1}{\zeta}\mathcal{V}\right)\hat{X}\rangle,

for all
\hat{X}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
.
We look for deformed flat functionals
y(\zeta):M_{0}\times\mathbb{C}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, namely those whose differential
dy(\zeta)\in T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
is covariantly constant w.r.t.
\widetilde{\nabla}
. In particular, they are solutions of the Dubrovin equation (
4.6
).

As expected, if the cotangent vector
\alpha
is representable,
\alpha=\eta_{*}\hat{Z}
for
\hat{Z}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
, then (
4.6
) is written as

(4.7)

\eta(\partial_{\zeta}\hat{Z},\hat{X})=\eta(\hat{Z},\left(\mathcal{U}-\frac{1}{\zeta}\mathcal{V}\right)\hat{X}),

which implies, since
\mathcal{U}
is symmetric and
\mathcal{V}
antisymmetric with respect to the metric
\eta
, the usual form of Dubrovin equation

(4.8)

\displaystyle\partial_{\zeta}\hat{Z}=\left(\mathcal{U}+\frac{1}{\zeta}\mathcal{V}\right)\hat{Z},

cf.
[
CM15
, equation 20b]
.

5.
Formal solutions

Let us solve equation (
4.6
) perturbatively at
\infty
. Recall that in the finite-dimensional case, the Dubrovin equation has a fundamental formal solution of the form (see
[
Dub99
]
)

(5.1)

\displaystyle\Xi(\zeta)=\Psi^{-1}R(\zeta)e^{U\zeta},\quad R(\zeta)=\mathrm{Id}+R_{1}\zeta^{-1}+R_{2}\zeta^{-2}+\dots,

where
\Psi
denotes the change of coordinates matrix from flat to normalized canonical. In the infinite-dimensional case, there is no natural analogue of the fundamental matrix
\Xi
, but nonetheless we can write functionals that generalize its columns, given by

(5.2)

\displaystyle\xi_{j}(\zeta)=e^{\zeta u_{j}}\left(v_{j}^{0}+v_{j}^{1}\zeta^{-1}+\dots\right),

where
u_{j}
is the
j
-th canonical coordinate and
v_{j}^{k}
are constant column vectors.

Proposition 5.1
.

The following statements hold at any point
\hat{\lambda}\in M_{0}
:

(1)

For each discrete canonical coordinate
u_{i}
, there exists a unique representable formal solution of the Dubrovin equation (
4.6
) of the form

(5.3)

\displaystyle\xi_{i}^{\textrm{formal}}(\zeta)=e^{\zeta u_{i}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r_{i}^{k}\zeta^{-k},\qquad r_{i}^{0}=du_{i},\ r_{i}^{k}\in(T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M)^{\textrm{rep}}.

(2)

For each discrete canonical coordinate
\bar{u}_{i}
, there exists a unique representable formal solution of the Dubrovin equation (
4.6
) of the form

(5.4)

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}_{i}^{\textrm{formal}}(\zeta)=e^{\zeta\bar{u}_{i}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\bar{r}_{i}^{k}\zeta^{-k},\qquad\bar{r}_{i}^{0}=d\bar{u}_{i},\ \bar{r}_{i}^{k}\in(T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M)^{\textrm{rep}}.

(3)

For each
p\in S^{1}
, the Dubrovin equation (
4.6
) admits formal solutions of the form

(5.5)

\displaystyle\xi_{p}^{\textrm{formal}}(\zeta)=e^{\zeta u_{p}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r_{p}^{k}\zeta^{-k},\qquad r_{p}^{0}=du_{p},\ r_{p}^{k}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}}^{*}M.

These solutions are given by the functionals

(5.6)

\displaystyle r_{p}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{k}a_{p}^{n}A^{*}_{p}(k-1-\mathcal{V})^{*}A_{p}^{*}(k-2-\mathcal{V})^{*}\dots A_{p}^{*}(n-\mathcal{V})^{*}du_{p},\qquad a_{p}^{0}=1,

where
A_{p}
is the left-inverse of
u_{p}-\mathcal{U}
with
A_{p}(0,1,0)=0
, and depend on the choice of complex constants
a_{p}^{n}\in\mathbb{C}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 1}
.

Proof.

To prove items
1
and
2
, let us solve (
4.8
) perturbatively at
\infty
. First, we apply the change of variables
\hat{Y}=\Psi dy
, where
\Psi
is defined as in Proposition
3.3
, and obtain

(5.7)

\displaystyle\hat{Y}_{\zeta}=\left(U+\frac{1}{\zeta}V\right)\hat{Y},

where
V=\Psi\mathcal{V}\Psi^{-1}
and
U=\Psi\mathcal{U}\Psi^{-1}
, which takes the diagonal form (
3.18
). We propose an Ansatz of the form

(5.8)

\displaystyle\hat{Y}_{i}^{\textrm{formal}}=e^{\zeta u_{i}}\left(\hat{Y}_{i}^{0}+\hat{Y}_{i}^{1}\zeta^{-1}+\dots\right),\qquad\hat{Y}_{i}^{k}\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\bar{n}},

which yields the recursion

(5.9)

\displaystyle(u_{i}-U)(\hat{Y}_{i}^{0})=0,

(5.10)

\displaystyle(u_{i}-U)(\hat{Y}^{k+1}_{i})=(k+V)(\hat{Y}_{i}^{k}),\qquad k\geqslant 0.

We will show that these equations have a unique solution up to normalization. Let
e_{i}
denote the canonical basis vector which is
1
at the
i
-th entry and
0
everywhere else. Then
\ker(u_{i}-U)=\langle(0,e_{i},0)\rangle
, so we choose

(5.11)

\displaystyle\hat{Y}^{0}_{i}=(0,-z_{i}^{2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(z_{i})e_{i},0),

which satisfies

(5.12)

\displaystyle\Psi^{-1}(\hat{Y}^{0}_{i})=du_{i}.

Let us move on to the next equation, namely

(5.13)

\displaystyle(u_{i}-U)(\hat{Y}_{i}^{1})=V(\hat{Y}_{i}^{0}).

This equation is solvable if and only if
V(0,e_{i},0)\in\textrm{im}(u_{i}-U)
. Note the diagonal form of
U
(
3.18
) allows us to decompose the space as
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\bar{n}}=\ker(u_{i}-U)\oplus\textrm{im}(u_{i}-U)
, so it is enough to show that the projection of
V(0,e_{i},0)
to the subspace
\ker(u_{i}-U)
is
0
. We prove it as an auxiliary lemma:

Lemma 5.2
.

The operator

(5.14)

\displaystyle V=\Psi\mathcal{V}\Psi^{-1}:\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\bar{n}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\bar{n}}

satisfies

(5.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}\circ V(0,e_{i},0)=0,\qquad

\displaystyle i=1,\dots,n

(5.16)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{j}\circ V(0,0,e_{j})=0,\qquad

\displaystyle j=1,\dots,\bar{n},

where
\mathbb{P}_{i}
is the projection to the
i
-th entry of the second component, and
\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{j}
is the projection to the
j
-th entry of the third component.

Proof.

The proofs of (
5.15
) and (
5.16
) are analogous, so we only perform the former. First, we compute

(5.17)

\displaystyle\Psi^{-1}(0,e_{i},0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z_{i}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(z_{i})}\left(\frac{zw^{\prime}(z)}{z_{i}-z},-\frac{e^{u}}{z_{i}^{2}},-\frac{1}{z_{i}}\right)=-\frac{1}{z_{i}^{2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(z_{i})}du_{i},

(5.18)

\displaystyle\hat{Y}=\mathcal{V}\Psi^{-1}(0,e_{i},0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z_{i}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(z_{i})}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{zw^{\prime}(z)}{z_{i}-z}+\frac{zw(z)}{(z_{i}-z)^{2}},\frac{1}{2}\frac{e^{u}}{z_{i}^{2}},-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{z_{i}}\right).

To conclude the proof, we have to show that

(5.19)

\displaystyle\langle du_{i},\hat{Y}\rangle=-Y_{\leqslant 0}(z_{i})+Y_{v}+\frac{e^{u}}{z_{i}}Y_{u}=0.

It is clear that
Y_{v}+\frac{e^{u}}{z_{i}}Y_{u}=0
. Let us compute

(5.20)

\displaystyle Y_{\leqslant 0}(z_{i})=\frac{1}{z_{i}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(z_{i})}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{z_{i}}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{|x|=1}\frac{w^{\prime}(x)}{(z_{i}-x)^{2}}dx+\frac{z_{i}}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{|x|=1}\frac{w(x)}{(z_{i}-x)^{3}}dx\right)=0,

where we have used integration by parts.
∎

As an immediate corollary,
V(\hat{Y}_{i}^{0})\in\textrm{im}(u_{i}-U)
and equation (
5.13
) admits solutions for
\hat{Y}_{i}^{1}
. Regarding uniqueness, it is clear that two different solutions of (
5.13
) must differ by an element of
\ker(u_{i}-U)
. Therefore, we write

(5.21)

\displaystyle\hat{Y}^{1}_{i}=\hat{R}^{1}_{i}+a^{1}_{i}\hat{Y}^{0}_{i},\qquad\hat{R}^{1}_{i}\in\textrm{im}(u_{i}-U),\ a_{i}^{1}\in\mathbb{C}.

Consider the second equation

(5.22)

\displaystyle(u_{i}-U)(\hat{Y}^{2}_{i})=(1+V)(\hat{Y}^{1}_{i}),

which has a solution for
\hat{Y}^{2}_{i}
if and only if
(1+V)(\hat{Y}^{1}_{i})\in\textrm{im}(u_{i}-U)
, which happens when

(5.23)

\displaystyle a_{i}^{1}\hat{Y}_{i}^{0}+\mathbb{P}_{i}\circ V(\hat{R}^{1}_{i})=0.

This fixes uniquely the constant
a_{i}^{1}
and ensures that (
5.22
) has a solution, which, as before, can be written as
\hat{Y}^{2}_{i}=\hat{R}^{2}_{i}+a_{i}^{2}\hat{Y}_{i}^{0}
. Iterating gives a unique
\hat{Y}_{i}^{\textrm{formal}}
, which concludes the proof of item
1
of the proposition. The proof of item
2
is completely analogous, and we will not do it explicitly here.

To prove item
3
, we insert the Ansatz (
5.5
) in the Dubrovin equation (
4.6
) and obtain the following recursion for the functionals
r^{k}_{p}
:

(5.24)

\displaystyle\langle r_{p}^{0},(u_{p}-\mathcal{U})\hat{X}\rangle=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\hat{X}\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}

(5.25)

\displaystyle\langle r_{p}^{k+1},(u_{p}-\mathcal{U})\hat{X}\rangle=\langle r_{p}^{k},(k-\mathcal{V})\hat{X}\rangle,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\hat{X}\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2},\ k\geqslant 0

Equation (
5.24
) is the eigenspace equation for the eigenvalue
u_{p}
. By the results of Section
3
, we have
r_{p}^{0}=du_{p}
. Note that
u_{p}-\mathcal{U}
is injective (one can directly see this from the diagonal form (
3.18
), noting
(T4)
excludes the degenerate case of all the canonical coordinates
u_{p}
being equal), but it fails to be surjective, as

(5.26)

\displaystyle\textrm{im}(u_{p}-\mathcal{U})=\{\hat{Y}\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}|\ \langle du_{p},\hat{Y}\rangle=0\}

is a subspace of
\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}
of codimension 1. Therefore, the operator
u_{p}-\mathcal{U}
admits left-inverses. Let
B_{p},B^{\prime}_{p}
be two left-inverses of
u_{p}-\mathcal{U}
. Then

(5.27)

\displaystyle(B_{p}-B^{\prime}_{p})(\hat{X})

\displaystyle=(B_{p}-B^{\prime}_{p})\left(\left(\hat{X}-\langle du_{p},\hat{X}\rangle(0,1,0)\right)+\langle du_{p},\hat{X}\rangle(0,1,0)\right)

\displaystyle=\langle du_{p},\hat{X}\rangle(B_{p}-B^{\prime}_{p})(0,1,0),

where we have used that
\hat{X}-\langle du_{p},\hat{X}\rangle(0,1,0)\in\textrm{im}(u_{p}-\mathcal{U})
. Therefore, a left-inverse of
u_{p}-\mathcal{U}
is completely determined by its action on
(0,1,0)
, and we choose
A_{p}
to be the one with
A_{p}(0,1,0)=0
. Back to the system (
5.25
), it is now clear that the recursively defined functionals

(5.28)

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{p}^{0}=du_{p},

(5.29)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{r}_{p}^{k+1},\hat{X}\rangle=\langle\hat{r}_{p}^{k},(k-\mathcal{V})A_{p}\hat{X}\rangle

solve it. Written in terms of transpose operators, the functionals

(5.30)

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{p}^{k}=A_{p}^{*}(k-1-\mathcal{V})^{*}A_{p}^{*}(k-2-\mathcal{V})^{*}\dots A_{p}^{*}(-\mathcal{V})^{*}du_{p}

give a formal solution of the form (
5.5
) to equation (
4.6
). Let us now study the uniqueness of solutions. Let
r_{p}^{0}=du_{p},r_{p}^{1},\dots,r_{p}^{k}
be given, and suppose both
s^{k+1}_{p}
and
t^{k+1}_{p}
solve (
5.25
) for
r^{k+1}_{p}
. Then

(5.31)

\displaystyle\langle s_{p}^{k+1}-t^{k+1}_{p},(u_{p}-\mathcal{U})\hat{X}\rangle=0,

so
s_{p}^{k+1}-t^{k+1}_{p}
must be a scalar multiple of
du_{p}
. Thus, the most general solution of the next recursive step is

(5.32)

\displaystyle r^{k+1}_{p}=A_{p}^{*}(k-\mathcal{V})^{*}r_{p}^{k}+a_{p}^{k+1}du_{p},

with
a_{p}^{k+1}\in\mathbb{C}
. From (
5.32
) we can deduce the most general form of the functionals, (
5.6
), thus completing the proof.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Since
A_{p}(0,1,0)=0
and
\langle du_{p},(0,1,0)\rangle=1
, it is easy to write the functionals of any given formal solution (
5.5
) in the form (
5.6
) by setting

(5.33)

\displaystyle a_{p}^{k}=\langle r_{p}^{k},(0,1,0)\rangle.

Remark 5.4
.

At the special point
\hat{\lambda}_{0}
, we can compute the operator
A_{p}
explicitly

(5.34)

\displaystyle A_{p}\hat{X}=\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{-u}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{z}\right)^{-1}(X(z)-X(p)),\ -\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}X(p)+Y_{u}\right),\ -\frac{1}{2}e^{-u}X(p)\right)

Remark 5.5

(Uniqueness of formal solutions)
.

Let us explain why the functionals
r^{k}_{i}
in the expansion of
\xi_{i}^{\textrm{formal}}
are uniquely determined, whereas
r_{p}^{k}
in the expansion of
\xi_{p}^{\textrm{formal}}
are not. Assume we have
r^{0}_{i},r^{1}_{i},\dots,r^{k-1}_{i}
and let
t^{k}_{i}
be such that

(5.35)

\displaystyle\langle t^{k}_{i},(u_{i}-\mathcal{U})\hat{X}\rangle=\langle r^{k-1}_{i},(k-1-\mathcal{V})\hat{X}\rangle.

Then the general solution of

(5.36)

\displaystyle\langle r^{k}_{i},(u_{i}-\mathcal{U})\hat{X}\rangle=\langle r^{k-1}_{i},(k-1-\mathcal{V})\hat{X}\rangle

is given by
r^{k}_{i}=t^{k}_{i}+a^{k}_{i}du_{i}
. To fix the constant
a^{k}_{i}
we consider the next equation

(5.37)

\displaystyle\langle r^{k+1}_{i},(u_{i}-\mathcal{U})\hat{X}\rangle=\langle t^{k}_{i}+a^{k}_{i}du_{i},(k-\mathcal{V})\hat{X}\rangle,

and choose
\hat{X}
to be the vector representative of
du_{i}
, i.e.,
\eta_{*}(\hat{X})=du_{i}
(here we do not denote
\hat{X}=du_{i}
as usual because it might lead to confusion). In particular,
\hat{X}\in\ker(u_{i}-\mathcal{U})
, which gives

(5.38)

\displaystyle a^{k}_{i}=-\frac{\langle t^{k}_{i},(k-\mathcal{V})\hat{X}\rangle}{\langle du_{i},(k-\mathcal{V})\hat{X}\rangle}.

Note that the denominator does not vanish since
\mathcal{V}(\hat{X})\in\textrm{im}(u_{i}-\mathcal{U})
and
\hat{X}\notin\textrm{im}(u_{i}-\mathcal{U})
. On the other hand, it is impossible to repeat this procedure to fix the constants appearing in
\xi_{p}^{\textrm{formal}}
, as the operator
(u_{p}-\mathcal{U})
is injective.

6.
Integral solutions and their asymptotics

In this section, we find a family of solutions to the Dubrovin equation defined in terms of an exponential integral along the unit circle in the complex plane. We derive the asymptotic behaviour of such solutions at
\zeta\sim\infty
, obtaining this way formal solutions in the sense of the previous section.

6.1.
Integral solutions

We define a family of functionals
y_{\sigma}(\zeta)
on
M_{0}\times\mathbb{C}
and we prove explicitly that their differentials
dy_{\sigma}(\zeta)
solve the Dubrovin equation (
4.6
).

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathbb{C}
and consider the functionals

(6.1)

\displaystyle y_{\sigma}(\zeta)=\frac{\zeta^{-1/2}}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{|z|=1}e^{\zeta\lambda_{\sigma}(z)}\frac{dz}{z}.

Their differentials
dy_{\sigma}(\zeta)
are solutions of the Dubrovin equation (
4.6
).

Proof.

The differentials
dy_{\sigma}(\zeta)\in T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
are given by

(6.2)

\displaystyle\langle dy_{\sigma}(\zeta),\hat{X}\rangle=\frac{\zeta^{1/2}}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{|z|=1}e^{\zeta\lambda_{\sigma}(z)}\langle d\lambda_{\sigma}(z),\hat{X}\rangle\frac{dz}{z}.

Plugging (
6.2
) in (
4.6
) yields

\displaystyle\langle dy_{\sigma}(\zeta),\left(\mathcal{U}-\frac{1}{\zeta}\mathcal{V}\right)\hat{X}\rangle-\langle dy_{\sigma}(\zeta),\hat{X}\rangle_{\zeta}=

(6.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta^{1/2}}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{|z|=1}e^{\zeta\lambda_{\sigma}(z)}\Big(\langle d\lambda_{\sigma}(z),\mathcal{U}\hat{X}\rangle-\lambda_{\sigma}(z)\langle d\lambda_{\sigma}(z),\hat{X}\rangle+

\displaystyle\qquad+z\lambda_{\sigma}^{\prime}(z)\left\langle d\lambda_{\sigma}(z),\left(\frac{w(z)}{zw^{\prime}(z)}X(z),0,-X_{u}\right)\right\rangle\Big)\frac{dz}{z},

which vanishes by Lemma
3.7
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

The differentials
dy_{\sigma}(\zeta)
are actually representable, see Remark
6.5
below. We can therefore use Proposition 20 in
[
CM15
]
to prove that they are covariantly constant w.r.t. the full deformed flat connection
\widetilde{\nabla}
.

6.2.
Asymptotics

Let us study the asymptotics of the solutions
dy_{\sigma}(\zeta)
for
|\zeta|\to\infty
. The usual approach to find the asymptotics of integrals of the form (
6.1
) or (
6.2
) is by applying the steepest descent method, first by expressing the path of integration as a combination of the steepest descent paths passing through the critical points of the superpotential
\lambda_{\sigma}(z)
, and then by computing saddle point asymptotics, see e.g.
[
Wit11
]
.

In our case, however, for generic values of
\sigma
, the path of integration cannot be deformed away from the domain of definition of
\lambda_{\sigma}(z)
, namely a neighbourhood of
S^{1}
. We will therefore restrict our analysis to those values of
\sigma
such that the critical points of
\lambda_{\sigma}(\zeta)
belong to
S^{1}
, and to
\sigma=0
and
1
, for which
\lambda_{\sigma}
coincides with
-\lambda
and
{\bar{\lambda}}
, respectively.

Let us consider a point
\hat{\lambda}=(\lambda,\bar{\lambda})
in the Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold
M_{0}
such that
\lambda
and
\bar{\lambda}
have
n
and
\bar{n}
critical points, respectively. Denote by
z_{i}
and
\bar{z}_{j}
the critical points and by
u_{i}
and
\bar{u}_{j}
the critical values of
-\lambda
and
\bar{\lambda}
, respectively, as in (
3.5
)-(
3.6
). Recall that we define a curve
\Sigma
via the function
\sigma(z)
on
S^{1}
, see (
3.2
). For every
\sigma\in\Sigma
the superpotential
\lambda_{\sigma}(z)
has a finite number of critical points
p_{1},\dots,p_{s}
, which are non-degenerate because of (
3.3
).

For
\sigma
belonging to the curve
\Sigma
, the path of integration
S^{1}
passes through the points
p_{1},\dots,p_{s}
. For
|\zeta|\to\infty
in a generic direction in the
\zeta
-plane the asymptotics of the integral will be dominated by the saddle point asymptotics of one of such points.
More precisely, let us consider the lines in
\mathbb{C}
passing through the origin and given by
\Re(\zeta(u_{p_{i}}-u_{p_{j}}))=0
for
i,j=1,\dots,s
.
These lines divide the
\zeta
-plane in sectors
S(u_{p_{j}})
for
j=1,\dots,s
such that in the sector
S(u_{p_{j}})
the exponential
e^{\zeta u_{p_{j}}}
has the dominant asymptotic behaviour as
|\zeta|\to\infty
.

For
\sigma=0
, the critical points
z_{i}
of the exponent
-\lambda(z)
belong to the exterior of the unit disc. In this case, however, the integrand is holomorphic in
D_{\infty}
, so we can deform the path of integration in such a way that it passes through all the critical points. As above, in each of the sectors
S(u_{j})
for
j=1,\dots,n
determined by removing the lines
\Re(\zeta(u_{i}-u_{j}))=0
for
i,j=1,\dots,n
from
\mathbb{C}
, the critical value
u_{j}
will determine the asymptotics.

Similarly, for
\sigma=1
, the path of integration can be deformed in such a way that it passes through all the critical points
\bar{z}_{i}
in the interior of
D_{0}
and the critical value
\bar{u}_{j}
will dominate the asymptotics in a sector
S(\bar{u}_{j})
, among the sectors obtained by removing the lines
\Re(\zeta(\bar{u}_{i}-\bar{u}_{j}))=0
for
i,j=1,\dots,\bar{n}
from the
\zeta
-plane.

For any
\hat{X}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
we have the following asymptotic behaviour.

Proposition 6.3
.

For
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
p=p_{j}
one of the critical points of
\lambda_{\sigma}
, we have

(6.4)

\displaystyle\langle dy_{\sigma},\hat{X}\rangle\sim e^{\zeta u_{p}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-k\right)}\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{p}\frac{\langle d\lambda_{\sigma}(z),\hat{X}\rangle}{(\lambda_{\sigma}(z)-u_{p})^{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{dz}{z}\ \zeta^{-k},\ |\zeta|\to\infty,\ \zeta\in S(u_{p}).

For
\sigma=0
and
z_{j}
one of the critical points of
\lambda
, we have

(6.5)

\displaystyle\langle dy_{0},\hat{X}\rangle\sim-e^{\zeta u_{j}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-k\right)}\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{z_{j}}\frac{\langle d\lambda(z),\hat{X}\rangle}{(-\lambda(z)-u_{j})^{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{dz}{z}\ \zeta^{-k},\ |\zeta|\to\infty,\ \zeta\in S(u_{j}).

For
\sigma=1
and
\bar{z}_{j}
one of the critical points of
\bar{\lambda}
, we have

(6.6)

\displaystyle\langle dy_{1},\hat{X}\rangle\sim e^{\zeta\bar{u}_{j}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-k\right)}\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{\bar{z}_{j}}\frac{\langle d\bar{\lambda}(z),\hat{X}\rangle}{(\bar{\lambda}(z)-\bar{u}_{j})^{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{dz}{z}\ \zeta^{-k},\ |\zeta|\to\infty,\ \zeta\in S(\bar{u}_{j}).

In the above formulas the symbol
\oint_{z}
denotes integration along a small counterclockwise simple path around
z
.

Proof.

Expression (
6.4
) follows from Lemma
A.1
applied to
\langle dy_{\sigma},\hat{X}\rangle
. For (
6.5
), note the integrand of
\langle dy_{0},\hat{X}\rangle

7.
Resurgence and Stokes phenomena

In this section, we study the Stokes phenomenon at the irregular singularity
\zeta\sim\infty
of the Dubrovin equation.

In the finite dimensional case
[
Dub99
]
, the Dubrovin equation (written in the normalized canonical frame) has a unique formal fundamental solution of the following form

(7.1)

Y_{\mathrm{formal}}(\zeta)=(\mathrm{Id}+R_{1}\zeta^{-1}+R_{2}\zeta^{-2}+\dots)e^{\zeta U},

where
U=\diag(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})
with
u_{i}\not=u_{j}
for
i\not=j
.
An admissible line
\ell
through the origin in
\mathbb{C}
is given by the choice of its positive direction
\phi
such that it satisfies
\Re(e^{{\mathrm{i}}\phi}(u_{i}-u_{j}))\not=0
for any
i\not=j
.
It can be shown that, given a choice of admissible line
\ell
in
\mathbb{C}
, there exists a unique fundamental solution
Y_{\mathrm{right}}
(resp.
Y_{\mathrm{left}}
) which is asymptotic to
Y_{\mathrm{formal}}
for
\zeta\sim\infty
on the open sector
\Pi^{\epsilon}_{\textrm{right}}
(resp.
\Pi^{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{left}}
) of opening slightly larger than
\pi
containing the right (resp. left) half-planes separated by
\ell
.
The Stokes matrices
S_{\pm}
relate such fundamental solutions on the intersection
\Pi^{\epsilon}_{\textrm{right}}\cap\Pi^{\epsilon}_{\textrm{left}}=\Pi^{\epsilon}_{+}\cup\Pi^{\epsilon}_{-}
, namely

(7.2)

Y_{L}(\zeta)=Y_{R}(\zeta)S_{\pm},\qquad\zeta\in\Pi^{\epsilon}_{\pm},

where
\Pi^{\epsilon}_{+}
, resp.
\Pi^{\epsilon}_{-}
, is the sector containing the direction
\phi
, resp.
\phi+\pi
.

Notice that the columns of a fundamental solution give a basis of solutions of the Dubrovin equation. In the infinite-dimensional case, we might consider the family of integral solutions
\{dy_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma\in\Sigma\cup\{0,1\}}
obtained in the previous section.
Such family, however, is not complete and moreover has trivial monodromy, see Remarks
6.6
and
6.7
, therefore it cannot be used to obtain the analogues of the Stokes matrices.
To find a larger family of solutions we adopt a different strategy, using resurgence theory to associate a family of “weak” solutions to a family of formal solutions like those studied in Section
5
.
More precisely, we consider the family of formal solutions given by the asymptotic expansions of the integral solutions and we apply to it the Borel resummation procedure.

Resurgence theory
[
Har73
,
Eca81
,
Ram93
,
Bal94
]
provides a method to associate analytic functions to formal series which are not convergent. The resummation procedure of a formal power series
\varphi(\zeta)=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}a_{k}\zeta^{-k}
can be summarized, for our aims, in three steps: computation of the sum of its Borel transform
\widehat{\varphi}(\chi)
obtained by the substitution
\zeta^{-k}\mapsto\chi^{k}/k!
, analytic continuation and identification of the resurgent structure of
\widehat{\varphi}(\chi)
, namely of its behaviour at singular points, and resummation to a function
s_{\theta}(\varphi)(\zeta)
via Laplace transform.
For recent expositions of these methods we refer the reader to
[
Mar21
,
Sau14
,
Dor19
,
MSS03
]
.

For simplicity, we restrict to the special point
\hat{\lambda}_{0}
in
M_{0}
. We also require
|e^{u}|<1
so that there are no discrete canonical coordinates to consider.

7.1.
Weak solutions

Recall that the cotangent space
T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
at a point
\hat{\lambda}\in M_{0}
is given by the algebraic dual of
T_{\hat{\lambda}}M
. Given a cotangent vector
\xi\in T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}}M
, we define its coefficients as the numbers
\langle\xi,e_{\hat{m}}\rangle
obtained by acting on the elements
e_{\hat{m}}\in\mathcal{H}(S^{1})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}
, given by

(7.3)

e_{m}=(z^{m},0,0),\quad e_{v}=(0,1,0),\quad\text{and}\quad e_{u}=(0,0,1),

where
\hat{m}\in\mathbb{Z}\cup\{v,u\}
.
In general, an arbitrary choice of coefficients
C_{\hat{m}}
does not define a cotangent vector
\xi
with
C_{\hat{m}}=\langle\xi,e_{\hat{m}}\rangle
.
However, it always defines an element in
T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}_{0}}M^{\mathrm{weak}}
, which is the algebraic dual of

\displaystyle T_{\hat{\lambda}_{0}}M^{\mathrm{test}}=\{\hat{X}=(X(z),X_{v},X_{u})\in T_{\hat{\lambda}_{0}}M|\ X(z)\in\mathbb{C}[z,z^{-1}]\}\cong\mathbb{C}[z,z^{-1}]\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2},

namely the subset of
T_{\hat{\lambda}_{0}}M
consisting of Laurent polynomials in
z
, by the formula

(7.4)

\displaystyle\left\langle\xi,(X(z),X_{v},X_{u})\right\rangle=\sum_{n}X_{n}C_{n}+X_{v}C_{v}+X_{u}C_{u}

for
X(z)=\sum X_{n}z^{n}\in\mathbb{C}[z,z^{-1}]
.

The motivation for introducing
T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}_{0}}M^{\mathrm{weak}}
is that we are going to obtain solutions to the Dubrovin equation by Borel resummation of the coefficients of the formal integral solutions, which will turn out not to be in
T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}_{0}}M
.
Notice that, since the operators
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
at the special point preserve the subspace
T_{\hat{\lambda}_{0}}M^{\mathrm{test}}
, it is possible to define weak solutions to the Dubrovin equation (
4.6
), i.e.,
\xi=\xi(\zeta)\in T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}_{0}}M^{\mathrm{weak}}
such that

(7.5)

\displaystyle\langle\xi,\hat{X}\rangle_{\zeta}=\langle\xi,\left(\mathcal{U}-\frac{1}{\zeta}\mathcal{V}\right)\hat{X}\rangle,\qquad\forall\hat{X}\in T_{\hat{\lambda}_{0}}M^{\mathrm{test}}.

7.2.
Formal integral solutions

At the special point, we can give an explicit formula for the coefficients of the formal solutions corresponding to the asymptotic expansion of the integral solutions obtained in Section
6
, namely (recall Proposition
6.3
)

(7.6)

\displaystyle\langle dy_{p}^{\mathrm{formal}},\hat{X}\rangle=e^{\zeta u_{p}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-k\right)}\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{p}\frac{\langle d\lambda_{\sigma}(z),\hat{X}\rangle}{(\lambda_{\sigma}(z)-u_{p})^{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{dz}{z}\ \zeta^{-k}.

Lemma 7.1
.

The coefficients of the formal integral solutions are given by

(7.7)

\displaystyle\langle dy_{p}^{\textrm{formal}},e_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{\zeta u_{p}}\frac{1}{2}\sigma(p)p^{m}\sqrt{\frac{p}{e^{u}}}\varphi_{p}^{m}(\zeta),\qquad

\displaystyle m\geqslant 1

(7.8)

\displaystyle\langle dy_{p}^{\textrm{formal}},e_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{\zeta u_{p}}\frac{1}{2}(\sigma(p)-1)p^{m}\sqrt{\frac{p}{e^{u}}}\varphi_{p}^{m}(\zeta),\qquad

\displaystyle m\leqslant 0

(7.9)

\displaystyle\langle dy_{p}^{\textrm{formal}},e_{v}\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{\zeta u_{p}}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{p}{e^{u}}}\varphi_{p}^{0}(\zeta),

(7.10)

\displaystyle\langle dy_{p}^{\textrm{formal}},e_{u}\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{\zeta u_{p}}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{e^{u}}{p}}\varphi_{p}^{-1}(\zeta),

where

(7.11)

\displaystyle\varphi_{p}^{m}(\zeta)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-k\right)}\binom{m+k-1/2}{2k}\left(\frac{p}{\zeta e^{u}}\right)^{k}.

Proof.

The coefficients in (
7.6
) are proportional to the integrals

(7.12)

\displaystyle I_{k,m}=\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{p}\frac{z^{m}}{(\lambda_{\sigma(p)}(z)-\lambda_{\sigma(p)}(p))^{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{dz}{z}.

Since at the special point
\sigma(p)=1+\frac{e^{u}}{p^{2}}
, we have that

(7.13)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\sigma(p)}(z)-\lambda_{\sigma(p)}(p)=\frac{e^{u}}{z}\left(\frac{z}{p}-1\right)^{2},

therefore

(7.14)

\displaystyle I_{k,m}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{2k+1}}{e^{(k+1/2)u}}\ \frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{p}\frac{z^{m+k-1/2}}{(z-p)^{2k+1}}dz=\frac{p^{2k+1}}{e^{(k+1/2)u}}\ \frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{0}\frac{(z+p)^{m+k-1/2}}{z^{2k+1}}dz.

Expanding the numerator and computing the residue at 0 gives

(7.15)

\displaystyle I_{k,m}=\binom{m+k-1/2}{2k}\left(\frac{p}{e^{u}}\right)^{k+1/2}p^{m},

from which the lemma follows immediately.
∎

Remark 7.2
.

Notice that (
7.11
) is a nowhere convergent formal power series in
\zeta^{-1}
.

7.3.
Borel transform and resurgent structure

Recall that a formal power series
\varphi(\zeta)=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}a_{k}\zeta^{-k}
at
\zeta\sim\infty
is called Gevrey-
1
if
|a_{k}|\leqslant C^{k}k!
for all
k>0
for some positive constant
C
.
In such case, its Borel transform, namely the series

(7.16)

\widehat{\varphi}(\chi)=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{a_{k}}{k!}\chi^{k},

is convergent in a neighbourhood of
\chi\sim 0
.

The formal integral solutions
\varphi_{p}^{m}(\zeta)
are are clearly Gevrey-
1
and we can explicitly identify their Borel transform:

Proposition 7.3
.

The Borel transform of
\varphi^{m}_{p}(\zeta)
converges for
|\chi|\leqslant\left|\frac{4e^{u}}{p}\right|
and is given by

(7.17)

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}^{m}_{p}(\chi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\ {}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-m,\frac{1}{2}+m;1;\frac{p\chi}{4e^{u}}\right),

where
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
denotes the Gauss hypergeometric function.

Proof.

Applying
\zeta^{-k}\rightarrow\chi^{k}/k!
to (
7.11
) yields

(7.18)

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}^{m}_{p}(\chi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-k\right)}\binom{m+k-1/2}{2k}\left(\frac{p\chi}{e^{u}}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{-1/2}{k}\binom{m+k-1/2}{2k}\left(\frac{p\chi}{e^{u}}\right)^{k}.

The desired result follows immediately from Lemma
B.1
, see Appendix
B.2
.
∎

Let us now consider the so-called resurgent structure of the Borel transform.
The Borel transform
\widehat{\varphi}^{m}_{p}(\chi)
has a singularity at
\chi_{p}=4e^{u}/p
corresponding to the logarithmic branch point at
z=1
of the hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;a+b;z)
, see (
B.20
).
Near the singularity it takes the form

(7.19)

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}^{m}_{p}(\chi_{p}+\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{\pi}\log\left(-\frac{p\xi}{4e^{u}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\ {}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-m,\frac{1}{2}+m,1;-\frac{p\xi}{4e^{u}}\right)+f_{\mathrm{reg}}(\xi)

where
f_{\mathrm{reg}}(\xi)
is holomorphic near
\xi\sim 0
. Here we have used the identity

(7.20)

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-m\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+m\right)=\frac{\pi}{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}+m\right)\pi}=(-1)^{m}\pi.

It is important to notice that the function multiplying the logarithm

(7.21)

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}^{m}_{-p}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\ {}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-m,\frac{1}{2}+m,1;-\frac{p\xi}{4e^{u}}\right),

is actually the Borel transform of the formal solution
\varphi^{m}_{-p}(\zeta)
above for a different sign of
p
.

7.4.
Borel resummation

The Borel resummation
s_{\theta}(\varphi)(\zeta)
of the formal series
\varphi(\zeta)
is defined as the Laplace transform of its Borel transform

(7.22)

s_{\theta}(\varphi)(\zeta)=\zeta\int_{C^{\theta}}\widehat{\varphi}(\chi)e^{-\zeta\chi}d\chi,

where the integral is along a ray
C^{\theta}=e^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta}\mathbb{R}_{+}
that does not contain any singularity of
\widehat{\varphi}(\chi)
.
The function
s_{\theta}(\varphi)(\zeta)
is holomorphic on the sector in
\mathbb{C}^{*}
given by those
\zeta
such that
|e^{-\zeta\chi}|\to 0
for
\chi\to\infty
along
C^{\theta}
and it is asymptotic to the formal series
\varphi(\zeta)
for
\zeta\sim\infty
. The above integral representation of the (possibly multivalued) analytic continuation of
s_{\theta}(\varphi)(\zeta)
also holds outside the sector, provided the path of integration is deformed accordingly.

Denote
C^{\theta_{\mathrm{St}}}
the ray passing through the logarithmic singularity of
\widehat{\varphi}_{p}^{m}(\chi)
at
\chi_{p}=4e^{u}/p
, corresponding to
\theta_{\textrm{St}}=\arg e^{u}-\arg p
.
For any ray
C^{\theta}=e^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta}\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\theta\not=\theta_{\textrm{St}}
, the Borel resummation

(7.23)

\displaystyle s_{\theta}(\varphi_{p}^{m})(\zeta)=\zeta\int_{C^{\theta}}\widehat{\varphi}_{p}^{m}(\chi)e^{-\zeta\chi}d\chi

defines an analytic function in the sector where the real part of the exponential is negative, i.e., the half-plane

(7.24)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\theta}=\left\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}|\ -\theta-\frac{\pi}{2}<\arg\zeta<-\theta+\frac{\pi}{2}\right\}.

Moreover,

(7.25)

\displaystyle s_{\theta}(\varphi^{m}_{p})(\zeta)\sim\varphi^{m}_{p}(\zeta),

for
|\zeta|\rightarrow\infty
in the sector
\Pi_{\theta}
.

Denote
s(\varphi_{p}^{m})(\zeta)
the multivalued analytic continuation of
s_{\theta}(\varphi_{p}^{m})(\zeta)
on
\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Notice that one obtains the same function by analytically continuing
s_{\theta^{\prime}}(\varphi_{p}^{m})(\zeta)
for
\theta^{\prime}\not=\theta
in the appropriate direction.

Observe that
s(\varphi_{p}^{m})(\zeta)
is asymptotic to
\varphi^{m}_{p}(\zeta)
for
|\zeta|\rightarrow\infty
in any sector where it is given by an integral representation as above.
Denoting
\theta_{0}=\theta_{\mathrm{St}}+\pi
, this happens whenever
\theta\not=\theta_{\mathrm{St}}
, namely when
\theta\in(\theta_{0}-\pi,\theta_{0}+\pi)
.
Therefore

(7.26)

s(\varphi^{m}_{p})(\zeta)\sim\varphi^{m}_{p}(\zeta)

for

(7.27)

\zeta\in\bigcup_{\theta\in(\theta_{0}-\pi,\theta_{0}+\pi)}\Pi_{\theta},

i.e. when
\zeta
belongs to the sector of opening
3\pi
given by

(7.28)

-\theta_{0}-\frac{3\pi}{2}<\arg\zeta<-\theta_{0}+\frac{3\pi}{2}.

The monodromy of the multivalued function
s(\varphi_{p}^{m})
is determined by the resurgent structure of the Borel transform. Indeed, for
\zeta\in\Pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{St}}}
we have

(7.29)

s(\varphi_{p}^{m})(e^{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\zeta)-s(\varphi_{p}^{m})(\zeta)=\zeta\int_{\mathcal{H}}\widehat{\varphi}_{p}^{m}(\chi)e^{-\zeta\chi}d\chi,

where
\mathcal{H}
is the clockwise Hankel contour around the singular point
\chi_{p}=4e^{u}/p
coming from infinity along the direction
\theta_{\mathrm{St}}
. By substituting (
7.19
) and performing the change of variable of integration
\chi=\chi_{p}+\xi
, we find it equals

(7.30)

-(-1)^{m}e^{-\zeta\frac{4e^{u}}{p}}\zeta\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\log\left(-\frac{p\xi}{4e^{u}}\right)\widehat{\varphi}_{-p}^{m}(\xi)e^{-\zeta\xi}d\xi,

where
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is the Hankel contour
\mathcal{H}
translated to 0. This in turn is equal to

(7.31)

-2{\mathrm{i}}(-1)^{m}e^{-\zeta\frac{4e^{u}}{p}}\zeta\int_{C^{\theta_{\textrm{St}}}}\widehat{\varphi}_{-p}^{m}(\xi)e^{-\zeta\xi}d\xi.

Therefore, the monodromy in
\zeta
of
s(\varphi_{p}^{m})
is given by

(7.32)

s(\varphi_{p}^{m})(e^{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\zeta)-s(\varphi_{p}^{m})(\zeta)=2{\mathrm{i}}(-1)^{m+1}e^{-\zeta\frac{4e^{u}}{p}}s_{\theta_{\textrm{St}}}(\varphi_{-p}^{m})(\zeta).

Let us now explicitly compute the function
s(\varphi_{p}^{m})(\zeta)
. Letting
\chi=te^{i\theta}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and using the Laplace transform formula (
B.21
), we have

(7.33)

\begin{split}s_{\theta}(\varphi^{m}_{p})(\zeta)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{i\theta}\zeta\int_{0}^{\infty}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-m,\frac{1}{2}+m,1;\frac{e^{i\theta}p}{4e^{u}}t\right)e^{-e^{i\theta}\zeta t}dt\\
&=\frac{2}{{\mathrm{i}}\pi}\sqrt{\frac{e^{u}}{p}}\ e^{-\zeta\frac{2e^{u}}{p}}\zeta^{\frac{1}{2}}K_{m}\left(-\zeta\frac{2e^{u}}{p}\right),\end{split}

where
K_{m}(z)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind, see Appendix
B.1
.
Clearly this identity extends to the analytic continuations of the functions on the plane cut at
e^{-i\theta_{\textrm{St}}}\mathbb{R}_{+}
, therefore we have

(7.34)

\displaystyle s(\varphi^{m}_{p})(\zeta)=\frac{2}{{\mathrm{i}}\pi}\sqrt{\frac{e^{u}}{p}}\ e^{-\zeta\frac{2e^{u}}{p}}\zeta^{\frac{1}{2}}K_{m}\left(-\zeta\frac{2e^{u}}{p}\right).

Remark 7.4
.

Equation (
7.34
) is actually an identity between functions defined on the universal covering of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
and formula (

) for the asymptotics of
K_{n}(z)
on a sector of opening
3\pi
induces the asymptotic formula (

).

Let us now define the resummed weak functionals
ds_{p}(\zeta)\in T^{*}_{\hat{\lambda}_{0}}M^{\mathrm{weak}}
for

by replacing in (

)-(
7.10
) the formal series
\varphi_{p}^{m}
with their Borel resummations

:

(7.35)

\displaystyle\langle ds_{p}(\zeta),e_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\mathrm{i}}\pi}\left(\frac{e^{u}}{p^{2}}+1\right)p^{m}e^{\zeta v}\zeta^{1/2}K_{m}\left(-\zeta\frac{2e^{u}}{p}\right),\qquad

\displaystyle m\geqslant 1

Let

Recall their formal asymptotics as

\displaystyle ds_{p}(\zeta)

is of argument
\theta
with
\theta\not=\theta_{0}+\frac{\pi}{2}\ \mathrm{mod}\ \pi

For a small
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\Pi^{\epsilon}_{\textrm{right}}=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ \theta-\pi-\epsilon<\arg\zeta<\theta+\epsilon\},

\displaystyle\Pi^{\epsilon}_{\textrm{left}}=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ \theta-\epsilon<\arg\zeta<\theta+\pi+\epsilon\}.

The intersection of

\displaystyle S_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

7.6.

Let us fix

\displaystyle\Pi_{\textrm{right}}=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ \theta-\pi<\arg\zeta<\theta\},

and

Theorem 7.11

The families of solutions

and
Y_{\textrm{left}}

\left(Y_{\mathrm{right/left}}(\zeta)\right)_{p}\sim dy^{\mathrm{formal}}_{p}(\zeta)

\left(Y_{\mathrm{left}}(\zeta)\right)_{p}=\left(Y_{\mathrm{right}}(\zeta)\right)_{p}-2\begin{cases}0,&\arg p\in(\arg e^{u}+\theta+\frac{\pi}{2},\arg e^{u}+\theta+\frac{3\pi}{2})\\
\left(Y_{\mathrm{right}}(\zeta)\right)_{e^{\pi{\mathrm{i}}}p},&\arg p\in(\arg e^{u}+\theta-\frac{\pi}{2},\arg e^{u}+\theta+\frac{\pi}{2})\end{cases}

for
\zeta\in\Pi_{+}^{\epsilon}

(7.75)

\left(Y_{\mathrm{left}}(\zeta)\right)_{p}=\left(Y_{\mathrm{right}}(\zeta)\right)_{p}-2\begin{cases}\left(Y_{\mathrm{right}}(\zeta)\right)_{e^{-\pi{\mathrm{i}}}p},&\arg p\in(\arg e^{u}+\theta+\frac{\pi}{2},\arg e^{u}+\theta+\frac{3\pi}{2})\\

Remark 7.12
.

We can formally express the relation between
Y_{\textrm{right}}
and
Y_{\textrm{left}}
in terms of kernels

by writing

\left(Y_{\mathrm{left}}(\zeta)\right)_{p}=\int_{S^{1}}\left(Y_{\mathrm{right}}(\zeta)\right)_{q}(S_{\pm})_{qp}dq,

in

with argument in

The kernels representing the analogues of the Stokes matrices are then written as

(7.77)

\displaystyle(S_{+})_{qp}

\displaystyle=\delta(q-p)-2\chi(q)\delta(q-e^{\pi{\mathrm{i}}}p),

(7.78)

\displaystyle(S_{-})_{qp}

\displaystyle=\delta(q-p)-2\chi(p)\delta(p-e^{\pi{\mathrm{i}}}q),

Notice that the two kernels

Appendix A
Saddle point asymptotics

Let us recall the proof of the following lemma, which can be seen as a simple application of Perron’s method
[
Won89
]
to our particular case.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
f
and
g
be holomorphic functions defined in a neighbourhood of a point
z^{\prime}
where
f
has a simple critical point and let
\mathcal{C}
be a path passing through
z^{\prime}
such that the real part of
e^{{\mathrm{i}}\psi}f(z)
restricted to
\mathcal{C}
has a maximum at
z^{\prime}
. Then the function of
\zeta
defined by

(A.1)

\mathcal{I}=\zeta^{1/2}\int_{\mathcal{C}}e^{\zeta f(z)}g(z)dz

admits the asymptotic expansion

(A.2)

\mathcal{I}\sim e^{\zeta f(z^{\prime})}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}d_{n}\zeta^{-n},

for
\zeta=|\zeta|e^{{\mathrm{i}}\psi}
,
|\zeta|\to+\infty
, with

(A.3)

d_{n}={\mathrm{i}}(-1)^{n}\Gamma(n+1/2)\res_{z=z^{\prime}}\frac{g(z)}{(f(z)-f(z^{\prime}))^{n+1/2}}dz.

Proof.

By shifting the variable of integration and renaming
f
and
g
we can assume that
f
and
g
are analytic in a neighborhood of
z=0
with
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0
and
f^{\prime\prime}(0)\not=0
. We write
f(z)=cz^{2}+O(z^{3})
with
c=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
.

By deforming the path
\mathcal{C}
we can make it coincide with a steepest descent path in a sufficiently small neighborhood of the critical point. We can moreover restrict the integral to a part of the path arbitrarily close to the critical point without changing the asymptotic expansion, as the difference will be exponentially vanishing.

We will therefore assume that
\mathcal{C}
is steepest descent path defined as the preimage of the path
\chi(t)=-e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\psi}t
for
t\in[0,T]
via
f(z)
with the appropriate orientation. Denote by
\mathcal{C}_{+}
the part of the path
\mathcal{C}
leaving the critical point and by
\mathcal{C}_{-}
the one arriving at the critical point.

Let
w(z)
be the unique square root of
c^{-1}f(z)
with
w(z)=z+O(z^{2})
.
The function
w(z)
is biholomorphic, so we can use it to change variable of integration; denoting
z(w)
the inverse, we get

(A.4)

\mathcal{I}=\zeta^{1/2}\int_{\mathcal{C}}e^{\zeta cw^{2}}s(w)dw,

where
s(w)=\frac{g(z(w))}{w^{\prime}(z(w))}
is holomorphic at
w=0
with Taylor expansion
s(w)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}s_{n}w^{n}
.

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
be the path
\eta(t)=-e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\psi}c^{-1}t
for
t\in[0,T]
. Let
\sqrt{\eta}
be the branch of the square root that maps
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
to
\mathcal{C}_{+}
(we choose a branch cut for the square root that does not coincide with
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
). The other branch
-\sqrt{\eta}
maps
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
to
-\mathcal{C}_{-}
. Splitting the integral in the two parts corresponding to
\mathcal{C}_{+}
and
\mathcal{C}_{-}
and changing the variable of integration with
w=\sqrt{\eta}
and
w=-\sqrt{\eta}
respectively, we obtain

(A.5)

\mathcal{I}=\zeta^{1/2}\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}}e^{\zeta c\eta}\tilde{s}(\eta)\frac{d\eta}{\sqrt{\eta}},

where

(A.6)

\tilde{s}(\eta)=\frac{1}{2}\left(s(\sqrt{\eta})+s(-\sqrt{\eta})\right)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}s_{2n}\eta^{n}.

The integral is explicitly given by

(A.7)

\mathcal{I}=\zeta^{1/2}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-|\zeta|t}t^{-1/2}a(t)dt,

with
a(t)=\sqrt{-\frac{e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\psi}}{c}}\tilde{s}\left(-\frac{e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\psi}}{c}t\right)
.

According to Watson’s Lemma (see Proposition 2.1 in
[
Mil06
]
) we have the following asymptotic expansion as
|\zeta|\to\infty

(A.8)

\int_{0}^{T}e^{-|\zeta|t}t^{-1/2}a(t)dt\sim\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\Gamma(n+1/2)\frac{a^{(n)}(0)}{n!}|\zeta|^{-n-1/2},

for any complex valued smooth function
a(t)
defined in a neighborhood of
[0,T]
.
Clearly
a^{(n)}(0)=n!s_{2n}\left(-\frac{e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\psi}}{c}\right)^{n+1/2}
, so we obtain the asymptotic expansion

(A.9)

\mathcal{I}\sim{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\Gamma(n+1/2)\frac{s_{2n}}{c^{n+1/2}}(-\zeta)^{-n}.

We can finally compute the coefficients
s_{n}
as residues

(A.10)

s_{n}=\res_{w=0}\frac{g(z(w))}{w^{\prime}(z(w))}\frac{dw}{w^{n+1}}=\res_{z=0}\frac{g(z)}{w(z)^{n+1}}dz.

Expressing
w(z)
as square root of
c^{-1}f(z)
we obtain the desired result.
∎

Remark A.2
.

We choose the branches of the roots of
c
and
e^{{\mathrm{i}}\psi}
such that the sign in the final expression is
+1
.

B.1.

In this appendix, we go over the definition and some properties of the modified Bessel functions. For more details, we refer the reader to
[

, Sections 10.25-10.46]
. The modified Bessel functions of the first kind are defined by

(B.1)

\displaystyle I_{\nu}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(k+\nu+1\right)k!}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2k+\nu}.

The modified Bessel functions of the second kind are defined by

(B.2)

\displaystyle K_{\nu}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{I_{-\nu}(z)-I_{\nu}(z)}{\sin(\pi\nu)},\qquad\nu\notin\mathbb{Z},

(B.3)

\displaystyle K_{m}(z)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\rightarrow m}K_{\mu}(z),\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}.

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
I_{n}(z)
is entire and
K_{n}(z)
is multivalued with a branch cut on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
. Its multivaluedness becomes clear from the expansion at
z=0

(B.4)

\displaystyle K_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(n-k-1)!}{k!}\left(-\frac{1}{4}z^{2}\right)^{k}+(-1)^{n+1}\log\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)I_{n}(z)

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\psi(k+1)+\psi(n+k+1)\right)\frac{\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2k}}{k!(n+k)!},

where

(B.5)

\displaystyle\psi(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k+z-1}\right)-\gamma,

(B.6)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}-\log n\right).

The following properties will be used in the text:

(B.7)

\displaystyle I_{n}(-z)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}I_{n}(z),

(B.8)

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=I_{n-1}(z)-\frac{n}{z}I_{n}(z),

(B.9)

\displaystyle K^{\prime}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=-K_{n-1}(z)-\frac{n}{z}K_{n}(z).

The monodromy of
K_{n}(z)
is given by

(B.10)

\displaystyle K_{n}(ze^{m\pi{\mathrm{i}}})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{mn}K_{n}(z)-(-1)^{n(m-1)}m\pi{\mathrm{i}}I_{n}(z),

(B.11)

\displaystyle K_{n}(ze^{m\pi{\mathrm{i}}})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n(m-1)}mK_{n}(ze^{\pi{\mathrm{i}}})-(-1)^{nm}(m-1)K_{n}(z).

It is also useful to keep in mind their asymptotic expansions for large

\displaystyle I_{n}(z)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\left(\frac{ez}{2n}\right)^{n},

(B.13)

\displaystyle K_{n}(z)\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\left(\frac{2n}{ez}\right)^{n},

and for large

\displaystyle I_{n}(z)

\displaystyle|\arg z|<\frac{3}{2}\pi,\ |z|\rightarrow\infty,

The Gauss hypergeometric function is defined by the power series

(B.18)

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)^{n}(b)^{n}}{n!(c)^{n}}z^{n},

in the disk

and by analytic continuation elsewhere, where

(B.19)

\displaystyle(q)^{n}=q(q+1)\dots(q+n-1)

denotes the rising factorial. At
z=1

(B.20)

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,a+b;z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}\log(1-z)\ {}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,a+b;1-z)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)^{k}(b)^{k}}{k!^{2}}\left(2\psi(k+1)-\psi(a+k)-\psi(b+k)\right)(1-z)^{k},

where the function
\psi
is defined by (
B.5
)–(
B.6
).

PBM92

(B.21)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{}_{2}F_{1}(a,1-a;c;-\omega x)e^{-qx}dx=\frac{q^{\frac{1}{2}-c}}{\sqrt{\pi\omega}}\Gamma(c)e^{\frac{q}{2\omega}}K_{a-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{q}{2\omega}\right),

Finally, we state and prove a technical lemma necessary for the Borel resummation procedure performed in Section

.

Lemma B.1
.

For
|z|<4
, the power series

(B.22)

\displaystyle f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{-1/2}{k}\binom{m+k-1/2}{2k}z^{k}

converges and coincides with the Gauss hypergeometric function

(B.23)

\displaystyle f(z)={}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-m,\frac{1}{2}+m,1;\frac{z}{4}\right).

Proof.

We write

(B.24)

\displaystyle\binom{-\frac{1}{2}}{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{k}(\frac{1}{2})^{(k)}}{k!},

(B.25)

\displaystyle\binom{m+k-\frac{1}{2}}{2k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(m+\frac{1}{2})^{(k)}(m-\frac{1}{2})_{(k)}}{(2k)!},

where
(a)^{(n)}
is the rising factorial and
(a)_{(n)}
is the falling factorial, given by

(B.26)

\displaystyle(a)_{(n)}

\displaystyle=a(a-1)(a-2)\dots(a-n+1),

(B.27)

\displaystyle(a)^{(n)}

\displaystyle=a(a+1)(a+2)\dots(a+n-1).

Using the property

(B.28)

\displaystyle(a)^{(n)}=(-1)^{n}(-a)_{(n)},

we can write

(B.29)

\displaystyle f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\frac{1}{2})^{(k)}(m+\frac{1}{2})^{(k)}(\frac{1}{2}-m)^{(k)}}{(2k)!k!}z^{k}.

Comparing this expression to the power series expansion (
B.18
) of the hypergeometric function reduces the lemma to proving the identity

(B.30)

\displaystyle 4^{k}k!(1/2)^{(k)}=(2k)!,\qquad k=0,1,\dots,

which, after noting
(2k)!=k!(k+1)^{(k)}
, further reduces to

(B.31)

\displaystyle(k+1)^{(k)}=4^{k}(1/2)^{(k)}.

To prove (
B.31
), we proceed via induction. For
k=0,1
it is clear that it holds. Assume it is true for
k\geqslant 1
. Then

(B.32)

\displaystyle(k+2)^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=(k+2)^{(k)}(2k+2)=\frac{(k+1)^{(k+1)}}{k+1}(2k+2)=2(k+1)^{(k+1)}=

\displaystyle=2(k+1)^{(k)}(2k+1)=4^{k}(1/2)^{(k)}2(2k+1)=

\displaystyle=4^{k+1}(1/2)^{(k)}(k+1/2)=4^{k+1}(1/2)^{(k+1)}.

∎
