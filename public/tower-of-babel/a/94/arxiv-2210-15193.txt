A framework of distributionally robust possibilistic optimization
Romain Guillaume, Adam Kasperski, Paweł Zieliński

Abstract
In this paper, an optimization problem with uncertain constraint coefficients is considered. Possibility theory is used to model uncertainty. Namely, a joint possibility distribution in constraint coefficient realizations, called scenarios, is specified. This possibility distribution induces a necessity measure in a scenario set, which in turn describes an ambiguity set of probability distributions in a scenario set. The distributionally robust approach is then used to convert the imprecise constraints into deterministic equivalents. Namely, the left-hand side of an imprecise constraint is evaluated by using a risk measure with respect to the worst probability distribution that can occur. In this paper, the Conditional Value at Risk is used as the risk measure, which generalizes the strict robust, and expected value approaches commonly used in literature. A general framework for solving such a class of problems is described. Some cases which can be solved in polynomial time are identified.



Keywords
:
robust optimization; possibility theory; fuzzy optimization; fuzzy intervals; conditional value at risk

1
Introduction

Modeling uncertainty in optimization problems has been a subject of extensive research in recent decades. This is due to the fact that in most applications, the exact values of input data in optimization models are unknown before a solution must be determined. Two basic questions arise while dealing with this uncertainty. The first is about the model of uncertainty adopted. The answer depends on the knowledge available about the input data. If the true probability distribution for uncertain parameters is known, then various stochastic models can be used (see, e.g.,
[
20
]
). For example, the uncertain constraints can be replaced with the so-called
chance constraints
, in which we require that they be satisfied with a given probability
[
7
]
.
On the other hand, when only the set of possible realizations of the parameters (called a
scenario set
) is available, the
robust
approach is widely utilized (see, e.g.,
[
2
]
). In the
strict robust
approach
the constraints must be satisfied for all possible realizations of input data (scenarios). This strong requirement can be
softened by introducing a
budget
, i.e. some limit on the amount of uncertainty
[
4
]
.

In practice, an intermediate case often arises. Namely, partial information about the probability distributions is known, which leads to the so-called
distributionally robust
models. The left-hand side of an uncertain constraint is then evaluated by means of some risk measure with respect to the worst probability distribution that can occur. Typically, the expected value is chosen as the risk measure. In this case, the decision-maker is risk neutral. Information about the distribution parameters, such as the mean or covariance matrix, can be reconstructed from statistical data, resulting in a class of
data-driven problems

[
3
]
. On the other hand, some information about the distributions can be derived
from
the knowledge of experts or past experience. In this case, a
possibilistic uncertainty model can be appropriate (see, e.g.,
[
19
]
).

In this paper, we will show how the
possibility theory

[
13
]
can be used in the context of distributionally robust optimization. Possibility theory offers a framework for dealing with uncertainty. In many practical situations, it is possible to say which realizations of the problem parameters are more likely to occur than others. Therefore, the common uncertainty sets used in robust optimization are too inaccurate. Also, focusing on the worst parameter realizations may lead to a large deterioration of the quality of the computed solutions (the large price of robustness). On the other hand, due to the lack of statistical data or the complex nature of the world, precise probabilistic information often cannot be provided. Then, the possibility theory, whose axioms are less rigorous than the axioms of probability theory, can be used to handle uncertainty.
In this paper, we will use some relationships between possibility and probability theories, which allows us to define a class of admissible probability distributions for the problem parameters. Namely, a possibility distribution in the set of parameter realizations induces a
necessity measure
in this set, which can be seen as a lower bound on the probability measure
[
11
]
.
We will further take into account the risk aversion of decision-makers by using a risk measure called
Conditional Value at Risk
(CVaR for short)
[
29
]
. CVaR is a convex coherent risk measure that possesses some favorable computational properties.
Using it, we can generalize the strict robust, and expected value-based approaches.
In this paper, we show a general framework for solving a problem with uncertain parameters
under the assumed model of uncertainty based on the above possibility-probability relationship. We also propose a method of constructing a joint possibility distribution for the problem parameters, which leads to tractable reformulations that can be solved efficiently by some standard off-the-shelf solvers. This paper is an extended version of
[
17
]
, where one particular uncertainty model was discussed.

The approach considered in this paper belongs to the area of
fuzzy optimization
in which many solution concepts have been proposed in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
25
]
). Generally, fuzzy sets can be used to model uncertainty or preferences in optimization models. In the former case (used in this paper), the membership function of a fuzzy set is interpreted as a possibility distribution for the values of some uncertain quantity
[
13
]
. This interpretation leads to various solution concepts (see, e.g.
[
25
,
22
]
for surveys). For example, fuzzy chance constraints can be used in which a constraint with fuzzy coefficients should be satisfied with the highest degree of possibility or necessity
[
24
]
. Furthermore, the uncertain objective function with fuzzy coefficients can be replaced with a deterministic equivalent by using some defuzzification methods
[
28
]
. Some recent applications of fuzzy robust optimization methods can be found, for example, in
[
23
,
27
]
. Our approach is new in this area. It uses a link between fuzzy and stochastic models established by the possibility theory. This allows us to use well-known stochastic methods under the fuzzy (possibilistic) model of uncertainty. Our model is illustrated with two examples, namely continuous knapsack and portfolio selection problems. Its validation on real problems is an interesting area of further research.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we consider an uncertain constraint and present various methods of converting such a constraint into deterministic equivalents. In Sections
3
and
4
, we recall basic notions of possibility theory. In particular, we describe some relationships between possibility and probability theories, which allow us to build a model of uncertainty in which a joint possibility distribution is specified in the set of scenarios. In Section
5
we discuss some
tractable cases of our model, which can be converted into linear or second-order cone programming problems.
In Section
6
, we characterize the complexity of basic classes of optimization problems, such as linear programming or combinatorial ones. Finally, in Section
7
, we illustrate our concept with two computational examples - the continuous knapsack and portfolio selection problems.

2
Handling uncertain constraints

Consider the following
uncertain linear constraint
:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}\leq b,

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{X}
is a vector of decision variables with a domain
\mathbb{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}=(\tilde{a}_{j})_{j\in[n]}
is a vector of uncertain parameters,
\tilde{a}_{j}
is
a real-valued uncertain variable
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
is a prescribed bound.
A particular realization
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{j})_{j\in[n]}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
is called a
scenario

and the set of all possible scenarios, denoted by
\mathcal{U}
, is called the
scenario set
or
uncertainty set
.
Intuitively, the uncertain constraint (
1
) means that
\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}\leq b
is satisfied by
a solution
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{X}
of an optimization problem for some or all scenarios
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{U}
,
depending on a method of handling the uncertain constraints.

Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})
be the set of all probability distributions in
\mathcal{U}
. In this paper, we assume that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
is a random vector with probability distribution in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})
. The true probability distribution
{\rm P}
for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
can be unknown or only partially known. The latter case can be expressed by providing an
ambiguity set of probability distributions

\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})
,
so that
{\rm P}
is only known to belong to
\mathcal{P}
with high confidence.
There are many ways of constructing the set
\mathcal{P}
. Most of them are based on statistical data available for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
, which enables us to estimate the moments (expected values or covariance matrix) or the distribution shape (see, e.g.,
[
9
,
32
,
15
]
). In the absence of statistical data, one can try
to estimate
\mathcal{P}
using experts’ opinions. In Sections
3
and
4

we will show how to construct an ambiguity set of probability distributions
\mathcal{P}
by
using a link between fuzzy and stochastic models established by possibility theory.

In order to convert (
1
) into a deterministic equivalent, one can use the following

distributionally robust constraint

[
32
,
9
]
:

\sup_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}}\E_{{\rm P}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]\leq b

(2)

or equivalently

\E_{{\rm P}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]\leq b\;\forall\,{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P},

where
\E_{{\rm P}}[\cdot]
is the expected value with respect to the probability distribution
{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}
. If
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})
, then (
2
) reduces to the following
strict robust constraint

[
2
]
:

\sup_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}\leq b.

(3)

Indeed, if
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U})
, then probability equal to 1 is assigned to scenario
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{U}
maximizing
\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}
(i.e. to a worst scenario for
\boldsymbol{x}
). The strict robust constraint is known to be very conservative, as it can lead to a large deterioration of the quality of the solutions computed. One can limit the number of scenarios by adding a budget to
\mathcal{U}

[
4
]
to overcome this drawback. This budget narrows the set
\mathcal{U}
to scenarios that are at some prescribed distance to a given
nominal scenario

\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{U}
. We thus assume that scenarios far from
\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}
are unlikely to occur.

We can generalize (
2
) by introducing a convex non-decreasing function
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and considering the following constraint:

\sup_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}}\E_{{\rm P}}[g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})]\leq b.

(4)

For example, the function
g
can be interpreted as a
disutility
for the values of
\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}
, where
\boldsymbol{a}
is a scenario in
\mathcal{U}
. In particular,
g
can be of the form
g(y)=y
, in which case we get (
2
).
Notice that
g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})
is a function of the random variable
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}
, so it is
also
a random variable.

By using the expectation in (
4
), one assumes risk neutrality of decision-makers.
In order to take an individual decision-maker’s risk aversion into account, one can replace the expectation with the
Conditional Value At Risk
(called also
Expected Shortfall
)
[
29
]
. If
{\rm X}
is a real random variable, then
the Conditional Value At Risk with respect to the probability distribution
{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}
is defined as follows:

{\rm CVaR}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\epsilon}[{\rm X}]=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}(t+\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\E_{{\rm P}}[{\rm X}-t]_{+}),

(5)

where
\epsilon\in[0,1)
is a given risk level and
[x]_{+}=\max\{0,x\}
. Notice that
{\rm CVaR}_{\mathrm{P}}^{0}[{\rm X}]={\rm E}_{\mathrm{P}}[{\rm X}]
. On the other hand, if
\epsilon\rightarrow 1
, then
{\rm CVaR}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\epsilon}[{\rm X}]
is equal to the maximal value that
{\rm X}
can take. We will consider the following constraint:

\sup_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}}{\rm CVaR}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\epsilon}[g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})]\leq b

(6)

or equivalently, by using (
5
):

\sup_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}}\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}(t+\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\E_{{\rm P}}[g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})-t]_{+})\leq b.

(7)

The parameter
\epsilon\in[0,1)
models risk aversion of
decision-makers. If
\epsilon=0
, then (
7
) is equivalent to (
4
). On the other hand, when
\epsilon\rightarrow 1
, then (
7
) becomes

\sup_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{U^{\prime}}}g(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})\leq b,

(8)

where
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
is the subset of scenarios that can occur with a positive probability for some distribution in
\mathcal{P}
. Thus, the approach based on
the Conditional Value At Risk generalizes (
3
) and (
4
).
Moreover,
the CVaR criterion can be used to establish a conservative approximation of the chance constraints
[
26
]
, namely

{\rm CVaR}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\epsilon}[g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})]\leq b\Rightarrow{\rm P}(g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})\leq b)\geq\epsilon.

(9)

Using condition (
9
) we conclude that a solution feasible to (
6
) is also feasible to the following
robust chance constraint
:

\inf_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}}{\rm P}(g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})\leq b)\geq\epsilon.

(10)

Optimization models with (
10
) can be hard to solve.
They are typically nonconvex, whereas the models with (
6
) can lead to more tractable optimization problems.

3
Possibility distribution-based model for uncertain constraints

Let
\Omega
be a set of alternatives. A primitive object of possibility theory (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
) is a
possibility distribution

\pi:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
, which assigns to each element
u\in\Omega
a
possibility degree

\pi(u)
. We only assume that
\pi
is
normal
, i.e. there is
u\in\Omega
such that
\pi(u)=1
. The possibility distribution can be built by using available data or experts’ opinions (see, e.g.,
[
13
]
). A possibility distribution
\pi
induces the following
possibility
and
necessity measures
in
\Omega
:

\displaystyle\Pi(A)

\displaystyle=\sup_{u\in A}\pi(u),\;A\subseteq\Omega,

(11)

\displaystyle{\rm N}(A)

\displaystyle=1-\Pi(A^{c})=1-\sup_{u\in A^{c}}\pi(u),\;A\subseteq\Omega,

(12)

where
A^{c}=\Omega\setminus A
is the complement of event
A
.
In this paper we assume that
\pi
represents uncertainty in
\Omega
, i.e. some knowledge about the uncertain quantity
\tilde{u}
taking values in
\Omega
.
We now recall, following
[
11
]
, a probabilistic interpretation of the pair
[\Pi,{\rm N}]
induced by the possibility distribution
\pi
.
Define

\mathcal{P}_{\pi}=\{{\rm P}:\forall A\text{ measurable }{\rm N}(A)\leq{\rm P}(A)\}=\{{\rm P}:\forall A\text{ measurable }\Pi(A)\geq{\rm P}(A)\}.

(13)

In this case
\sup_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}}{\rm P}(A)=\Pi(A)
and
\inf_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}}{\rm P}(A)={\rm N}(A)
(see
[
11
,
14
,
8
]
). Therefore, the possibility distribution
\pi
in
\Omega
encodes a family of probability measures in
\Omega
. Any probability measure
{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
is said to be
consistent with

\pi
and for any event
A\subseteq\Omega
the inequalities

{\rm N}(A)\leq{\rm P}(A)\leq\Pi(A)

(14)

hold.
A detailed discussion on the expressive power of (
14
) can also be found in
[
30
]
. Using the assumption that
\pi
is normal, one can show that
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
is not empty. In fact, the probability distribution such that
{\rm P}(\{u\})=1
for some
u\in\Omega
with
\pi(u)=1
is in
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
. To see this, consider two cases. If
u\notin A
, then
{\rm N}(A)=0
and
{\rm P}(A)\geq{\rm N}(A)=0
. If
u\in A
, then
{\rm P}(A)=1\geq{\rm N}(A)
.

Assume that
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,1]
is a
joint possibility distribution
for the vector of uncertain parameters
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
in the constraint (
1
). The value of
\pi(\boldsymbol{a})
,
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is the possibility degree for scenario
\boldsymbol{a}
.
A detailed method of constructing
\pi
will be shown in Section
4
. By (
13
), the possibility distribution
\pi
induces
an ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
of probability distributions
for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
, consistent with
\pi
.
Define

\mathcal{C}(\lambda)=\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\pi(\boldsymbol{a})\geq\lambda\},\;\lambda\in(0,1].

(15)

The set
\mathcal{C}(\lambda)
contains all scenarios
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, whose possibility of occurrence is at least
\lambda
.
We will also use
\mathcal{C}(0)
to denote the
support
of
\pi
, i.e the smallest subset (with respect to inclusion)
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
{\rm N}(\mathcal{C}(0))=1
. By definition (
15
),
\mathcal{C}(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in[0,1]
, is a family of nested sets, i.e.
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{2})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{1})
if
0\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq 1
. We will make the following, not particularly restrictive, assumptions about
\pi
:

Assumption 1
.

1.

There is a scenario
\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\pi(\hat{\boldsymbol{a}})=1
.

2.

\pi
is continuous in
\mathcal{C}(0)
.

3.

The support
\mathcal{C}(0)
of
\pi
coincides with the scenario set
\mathcal{U}
and is a compact set.

4.

The sets
\mathcal{C}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
are closed and convex.

5.

The sets
\mathcal{C}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in[0,1)
have non-empty interiors.

Assumption
1
.1 is a normalization one that must be satisfied by any possibility distribution. There is at least one scenario
\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}
, called a
nominal scenario
, whose possibility of occurrence is equal to 1. Assumption
1
.2 will allow us to represent the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
as a family of nested confidence sets. Assumption
1
.3 means that the compact set
\mathcal{C}(0)
contains all possible scenarios, i.e. realizations of the uncertain vector
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
, so it coincides with the scenario set
\mathcal{U}
. The last two assumptions will allow us to construct some linear or second-order cone reformulations of the constraint (
6
).

The assumptions about
\pi
imply the following equation:

{\rm N}(\mathcal{C}(\lambda))=1-\lambda,\;\lambda\in[0,1].

So, the degree of necessity (certainty) of
\mathcal{C}(\lambda)
(event “any
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{C}(\lambda)
”)
equals
1-\lambda
.
Hence,
\mathcal{C}(\lambda)
is a confidence set, with the confidence (necessity) degree equal to
1-\lambda
.
The continuity of
\pi
allows us also to establish the following formulation of the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
(see
[
18
]
):

\mathcal{P}_{\pi}=\{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U}):{\rm P}(\mathcal{C}(\lambda))\geq{\rm N}(\mathcal{C}(\lambda))=1-\lambda,\lambda\in[0,1]\}.

Thus
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
is represented
by a family of confidence sets
\mathcal{C}(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in[0,1]
, with confidence (necessity) degrees

1-\lambda
.

In order to construct a tractable reformulation of the constraint (
6
), we will use the following approximation of
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
. Let
\ell
be a positive integer and fix
\Lambda=\{0,1,\dots,\ell\}
. Define
\lambda_{i}=i/\ell
for
i\in\Lambda
. Consider the following ambiguity set:

\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi}=\{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U}):{\rm P}(\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{i}))\geq{\rm N}(\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{i}))=1-\lambda_{i},i\in\Lambda\}.

(16)

Set
\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi}
is a discrete approximation of
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
. It is easy to verify that
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi}
for any
\ell>0
(if
{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
, then
{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi}
). By fixing sufficiently large constant
\ell
, we obtain an arbitrarily close approximation of
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
.
The following theorem is the key result of the paper:

Theorem 1
.

The constraint (
6
) for the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi}
, i.e.

\sup_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi}}{\rm CVaR}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\epsilon}[g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})]\leq b,

(17)

is equivalent to the following semi-infinite system of constraints:

\displaystyle w+\sum_{i\in\Lambda}(\lambda_{i}-1)v_{i}\leq(b-t)(1-\epsilon)

(18)

\displaystyle w-\sum_{j\leq i}v_{j}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall i\in\Lambda

(19)

\displaystyle w-\sum_{j\leq i}v_{j}+t\geq g(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})\;\;\;\forall\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{i}),

\displaystyle\forall i\in\Lambda

(20)

\displaystyle v_{i}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall i\in\Lambda

(21)

\displaystyle w,t\in\mathbb{R}.

(22)

Proof.

Using (
7
) for

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi}
, we can express the left-hand side of (
17
)
as follows:

\max_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi}}\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left(t+\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\int_{\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})}[g(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})-t]_{+}\;{\rm d}\,\mathrm{P}(\boldsymbol{a})\right).

The function
t+\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\int_{\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})}[g(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})-t]_{+}\;{\rm d}\,\mathrm{P}(\boldsymbol{a})
is real-valued, linear in
\mathrm{P}
and convex in
t
(see
[
9
]
). Hence, by the minimax theorem, it is equivalent to

\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left(t+\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\max_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi}}\int_{\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})}[g(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})-t]_{+}\;{\rm d}\,\mathrm{P}(\boldsymbol{a})\right).

In consequence, the inequality (
17
) holds if and only if there is
t
such that

\max_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi}}\int_{\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})}[g(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})-t]_{+}\;{\rm d}\,\mathrm{P}(\boldsymbol{a})\leq(b-t)(1-\epsilon).

(23)

Taking into account the form of the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi}
(see (
16
))
the left-hand side of (
23
) can be expressed as the following
problem of moments
:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\max&\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})}[g(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})-t]_{+}\;{\rm d}\,\mathrm{P}(\boldsymbol{a})\\
&\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})}\boldsymbol{1}_{[\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{i})]}\;{\rm d}\,\mathrm{P}(\boldsymbol{a})\geq 1-\lambda_{i}&\forall i\in\Lambda\\
&\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})}{\rm d}\,\mathrm{P}(\boldsymbol{a})=1\\
&\mathrm{P}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\end{array}

(24)

where
\boldsymbol{1}_{[A]}
denotes the indicator function of an event
A

and

\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the set of all non-negative measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The dual of the problem of moments takes the following form (see, e.g.,
[
21
]
):

\begin{array}[]{llll}\min&\displaystyle w+\sum_{i\in\Lambda}(\lambda_{i}-1)v_{i}\\
&\displaystyle w-\sum_{i\in\Lambda}\boldsymbol{1}_{[\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{i})]}v_{i}\geq[g(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})-t]_{+}&\forall\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})\\
&v_{i}\geq 0&\forall i\in\Lambda\\
&w\in\mathbb{R},&\end{array}

(25)

where
v_{i}
,
i\in\Lambda
, and
w
are dual variables that correspond to
the constraints of the primal problem (
24
).
Strong duality
implies that the optimal objective values of (
24
) and (
25
) are the same
(see, e.g.,
[
21
, Theorem 1, Corollary 1]
).
Define

\overline{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda_{i})=\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{i})\setminus\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{i+1}),\;i\in\{0,\dots,\ell-1\}

and
\overline{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda_{\ell})=\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{\ell})
.
Hence
\overline{C}(\lambda_{i})
,
i\in\Lambda
, form a partition of support
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})
into
\ell+1
disjoint sets.
The model (
25
) can then be rewritten as follows:

\begin{array}[]{llll}\min&\displaystyle w+\sum_{i\in\Lambda}(\lambda_{i}-1)v_{i}\\
&\displaystyle w-\sum_{j\in\Lambda}\boldsymbol{1}_{[\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{j})]}v_{j}\geq[g(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})-t]_{+}&\forall\boldsymbol{a}\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda_{i}),&\forall i\in\Lambda\\
&v_{i}\geq 0&&\forall i\in\Lambda\\
&w\in\mathbb{R},&\end{array}

(26)

which is equivalent to

\begin{array}[]{llll}\min&\displaystyle w+\sum_{i\in\Lambda}(\lambda_{i}-1)v_{i}\\
&\displaystyle w-\sum_{j\leq i}v_{j}\geq[g(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})-t]_{+}&\forall\boldsymbol{a}\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda_{i}),&\forall i\in\Lambda\\
&v_{i}\geq 0&&\forall i\in\Lambda\\
&w\in\mathbb{R}&\end{array}

(27)

\begin{array}[]{llll}\min&\displaystyle w+\sum_{i\in\Lambda}(\lambda_{i}-1)v_{i}\\

&\displaystyle w-\sum_{j\leq i}v_{j}+t\geq g(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})&\forall\boldsymbol{a}\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda_{i}),&\forall i\in\Lambda\\

Remark 1

The tractability of the formulation (

Deciding efficiently if (

4
Constructing a joint possibility distribution for an uncertain constraint

In this section, we will propose a method to construct a joint possibility distribution
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,1]
for
the vector of uncertain parameters
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
in the constraint (
1
).
We will also provide a decomposition of
\pi
into a family of confidence sets, according to (
15
), which will allow us to deal with (
30
) and, consequently, with the formulation (
18
)-(
22
).

Choose a component
\tilde{a}_{j}
of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}=(\tilde{a}_{1},\dots,\tilde{a}_{n})
, where
\tilde{a}_{j}
is
a real-valued uncertain variable.
Suppose that the true value of
\tilde{a}_{j}
is known to belong to the closed interval
[\hat{a}_{j}-\underline{a}_{j},\hat{a}_{j}+\overline{a}_{j}]
, where
\hat{a}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
is the nominal (expected) value of
\tilde{a}_{j}
and
\underline{a}_{j},\overline{a}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
represent the maximum deviations of the value of
\tilde{a}_{j}
from
\hat{a}_{j}
.
The hyperrectangle

\mathcal{I}=[\hat{a}_{1}-\underline{a}_{1},\hat{a}_{1}+\overline{a}_{1}]\times\dots\times[\hat{a}_{n}-\underline{a}_{n},\hat{a}_{n}+\overline{a}_{n}]

(the Cartesian product of the intervals) contains all possible values of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
. In practice,
\mathcal{I}
is often an overestimation of the scenario set
\mathcal{U}
, which is due to correlations among the components of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
. In particular, it can be highly improbable that all components of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
will take extreme values at the same time
[
4
]
. Let
\delta(\boldsymbol{a})
be a distance of scenario
\boldsymbol{a}
from the nominal scenario
\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}
. We will use the following distance function:

\delta(\boldsymbol{a})=||\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{a}-\hat{\boldsymbol{a}})||_{p},

(31)

where
||\cdot||_{p}
is the
L_{p}
-norm,
p\geq 1
, and
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a given matrix which can be used to model some interactions among the components of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
. In the simplest case,
\boldsymbol{B}
is an identity
n\times n
matrix and
\delta(\boldsymbol{a})=||(\boldsymbol{a}-\hat{\boldsymbol{a}})||_{p}
. Let
\overline{\delta}\geq 0
be a parameter, called a
budget
, which denotes the maximum possible deviation of the values of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
from
\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}
. It stands for a limit on the amount of uncertainty for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
. The idea of the budgeted uncertainty has been proposed in
[
4
]
.
Let

\mathcal{D}=\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\delta(\boldsymbol{a})\leq\overline{\delta}\}

be the set of all scenarios whose distance from the nominal scenario does not exceed
\overline{\delta}
.
We will assume that the scenario set is defined as
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{D}
.
Hence
\Pi(\mathcal{U}^{c})=0
and, by (
12
),
{\rm N}(\mathcal{U})=1
.

\mu_{\tilde{a}_{j}}

\tilde{a}_{j}
and

Without any additional information, the scenario set
\mathcal{U}
induces the following joint possibility distribution for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
:
\pi(\boldsymbol{a})=1
if
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\pi(\boldsymbol{a})=0

for each

and

(also

) contains all probability distributions in

. Constraint (

) is then equivalent to the strict robust constraint (

We will now extend this idea to refine the model of uncertainty. We will make a reasonable assumption that scenarios closer to the nominal scenario
\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}
will be more possible than those with a greater distance from
\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}
.
Let
\pi_{\tilde{a}_{j}}
be a possibility distribution for uncertain parameter
\tilde{a}_{j}
with the support
[\hat{a}_{j}-\underline{a}_{j},\hat{a}_{j}+\overline{a}_{j}]
and
\pi_{\tilde{a}_{j}}(\hat{a}_{j})=1

\pi_{\tilde{a}_{j}}

and strictly decreasing in
[\hat{a}_{j}+\underline{a}_{j}]
.
Following
[
13
]
,
one can identify
\pi_{\tilde{a}_{j}}
with a membership function

of a fuzzy interval in

that models
\tilde{a}_{j}
, namely
\pi_{\tilde{a}_{j}}=\mu_{\tilde{a}_{j}}
. We denote it by

\tilde{a}_{j}=\braket{\hat{a}_{j},\underline{a}_{j},\overline{a}_{j}}_{z_{1}-z_{2}}
and
its membership function
\mu_{\tilde{a}_{j}}

can be of the following form (see Figure

\mu_{\tilde{a}_{j}}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lllll}(1+\frac{x-\hat{a}_{j}}{\underline{a}_{j}})^{\frac{1}{z_{1}}}&\text{for}\;x\in[\hat{a}_{j}-\underline{a}_{j},\hat{a}_{j}]\\

Note that,

Similarly, let

can be identified with a membership function

A detailed interpretation of the possibility distribution of a real-valued uncertain variable and some methods to obtain it from the possessed knowledge are described in

.

Let us now define the following joint possibility distribution for scenarios in

6
Solving optimization problems with uncertain coefficients

In this section, we apply the solution concept described previously to various optimization problems. We will characterize the complexity of the resulting deterministic counterparts.
Consider the following optimization problem with uncertain constraints and objective function:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\widetilde{\min}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}\\
&\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}\leq b_{i}&\forall i\in[m]\\
&\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{X},\end{array}

(86)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}=(\tilde{c}_{j})_{j\in[n]}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}=(\tilde{a}_{ij})_{j\in[n]}
,
i\in[m]
, are uncertain objective function and constraint coefficients. The right-hand constraint sides
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i\in[m]
, are assumed to be precise (we will show later how to deal with uncertain right-hand sides).
The uncertainty of each coefficient is modeled by a fuzzy interval whose membership function is regarded as a possibility
distribution for its values (according to the interpretation provided in Section
3
).
The set
\mathbb{X}
specifies the domain of decision variables
\boldsymbol{x}
.

Using the solution concept proposed in this paper, we can transform (
86
) into the following deterministic equivalent:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\min&h\\
&\displaystyle\sup_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi_{0}}}{\rm CVaR}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\epsilon}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]\leq h&\\
&\displaystyle\sup_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi_{i}}}{\rm CVaR}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\epsilon}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]\leq b_{i}&\forall i\in[m]\\
&h\in\mathbb{R}&\\
&\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{X},&\end{array}

(87)

where
\pi_{0}
and
\pi_{i}
,
i\in[m]
, are joint possibility distributions
induced by the possibility distributions of the objective function and the constraint coefficients, respectively,
according to (
34
). If the right-hand sides of the constraints are also uncertain in (
86
), then
the constraints in (
87
) can be rewritten as

\sup_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi_{i}}}{\rm CVaR}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\epsilon}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}-\tilde{b}_{i}x_{0}]\leq 0
,
where
x_{0}
is an auxiliary variable such that
x_{0}=1
.

The solutions to (
87
) have the following interpretation. Recall that
the parameter
\epsilon\in[0,1)
is provided by the decision-maker
and reflects her/his attitude toward risk. When
\epsilon=0
, then CVaR becomes the expectation
(which models risk neutrality). This case leads to solutions
\boldsymbol{x}
to (
87
)
that guarantee a long-run performance.
For a greater value of
\epsilon
(more risk aversion is taken into account), more attention is paid to the worst outcomes and
solutions
\boldsymbol{x}
to (
87
) become more robust, i.e., guarantee better worst-case performance.

The model (
87
) has
m+1
CVaR constraints, which can be represented as systems of linear or second-order cone constraints (see Section
5
).
Hence, if
\mathbb{X}
is a polyhedral set described by a system of linear constraints, then (
87
) becomes
linear programming problems (for the
L_{1}
or
L_{\infty}
norms) or
a second order cone optimization problem (for the
L_{2}
-norm).
Both cases can be solved efficiently by standard off-the-shelf solvers.

Corollary 4
.

If
\mathbb{X}
is a polyhedral set, then (
87
) is polynomially solvable for the
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
, or
L_{\infty}
norms, used in (

Consider the following special case of (
87

m=0
:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\min&\displaystyle\sup_{{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}_{\pi_{0}}}{\rm CVaR}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\epsilon}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{c}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]\\
&\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{X}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}.\end{array}

(88)

Hence, (
88

a combinatorial optimization problem with uncertain costs.
If the
L_{2}
-norm is used to define the deviation function (

, then (

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{X}}\max_{\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{c}^{T}\boldsymbol{x},

[

As a consequence, we get the following:

Exploring the complexity of (

8
Conclusions

In this paper, we have proposed a framework for handling uncertain constraints in optimization problems. We have applied fuzzy intervals to model uncertain constraint coefficients. Under the possibilistic interpretation, the membership functions of the fuzzy intervals induce a joint possibility distribution in the set of constraint coefficients realizations (scenario set). Furthermore, this joint possibility distribution characterizes an ambiguity set of probability distributions in the scenario set. We have applied the distributionally robust approach to convert imprecise constraints into deterministic equivalents. For this purpose, we have used the CVaR risk measure, which allows us to take decision-makers’ risk aversion into account. We have shown how the concept can be applied to various optimization problems with uncertain constraints and objective functions. Most of the resulting deterministic optimization problems are polynomially solvable, which makes the approach applicable in practice. An interesting research direction is to characterize the problem complexity for the class of combinatorial problems.
