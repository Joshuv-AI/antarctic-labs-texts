Quantum memory assisted entangled state verification with local measurements
Siyuan Chen, Wei Xie, Ping Xu, Kun Wang

Abstract
We consider the quantum memory assisted quantum state verification task, where an adversary prepare independent multipartite entangled states and send to the local verifiers, who then store several copies in the quantum memory and measure them collectively to make decision. We establish an exact analytic formula for optimizing two-copy state verification, where the verifiers store two copies, and give a globally optimal two-copy strategy for multi-qubit graph states involving only Bell measurements. When the verifiers can store arbitrarily many copies, we present a dimension expansion technique that designs efficient verification strategies for this case, showcasing its application to efficiently verifying GHZ-like states. These strategies become increasingly advantageous with growing memory resources, ultimately approaching the theoretical limit of efficiency. Our findings demonstrate that quantum memories enhance state verification efficiency, sheding light on error-resistant strategies and practical applications of large-scale quantum memory-assisted verification.

In QSV, we consider a quantum device designed to produce a multipartite pure state

\displaystyle\max_{\left\langle\psi\right|\sigma_{j}\left|\psi\right\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon}\tr[\Omega\sigma_{j}]=1-(1-\lambda_{2}(\Omega))\varepsilon,

Quantum memory assisted state verification.
—In this verification strategy,

n

First, they store
k

Then, they measure their local copies in

This “store-and-measure” strategy is vividly illustrated

The test will be repeated

We designate this quantum memory-assisted strategy as an

Its crucial distinction from standard verification strategies,

lies in the latter’s absence of quantum memory assistance.
In our notation, these standard strategies fall under the category of
(n,1,d)

In the good case, the overall state stored in the quantum memories
admits a tensor product structure:
\left|\Psi\right\rangle:=\bigotimes_{r=1}^{k}\left|\psi\right\rangle^{(r)}
,
where the superscript

represents the
r
-th copy in the quantum memory.
The verifiers perform a local binary measurement

such that state

passes the test with certainty.
In the bad case, we assume that the
k
states produced by the quantum device
are independent, indicating that the fake state
in the composite space
\mathcal{H}^{nk}
has the form

\displaystyle\xi=\bigotimes_{r=1}^{k}\sigma^{(r)},

(3)

where each
\sigma^{(r)}
satisfies
\left\langle\psi\right|\sigma^{(r)}\left|\psi\right\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon
.
Correspondingly, the maximal probability that the fake state
\xi

in the bad case can pass the test is

\displaystyle p(\Omega):=\max_{\left\langle\psi\right|\sigma^{(r)}\left|\psi\right\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon}\tr\left[\Omega\left(\bigotimes_{r=1}^{k}\sigma^{(r)}\right)\right].

(4)

The minimum required number of measurements to saturate the worst-case failure probability,
denoted as
M_{m}(\Omega)
, is given by
M_{m}(\Omega)=\ln\delta/\ln p(\Omega)
.
Thus, the total number of copies consumed by the verification strategy
\Omega
satisfies

\displaystyle N_{m}(\Omega)=kM_{m}(\Omega)=\frac{k\ln\delta}{\ln p(\Omega)}.

(5)

The verifiers’ objective is to design efficient memory-assisted strategies

that minimize the number of copies consumed.

Two-copy verification strategy.
—We analytically solve the maximization problem
in Eq. (
4
) for the case of
k=2
,
yielding an exact analytic formula for optimizing two-copy state verification.
First of all, we simplify the form of the optimisation in Eq. (
4
).
Regarding the permutation invariant nature of the verifiers, we show that
it is best to consider verification strategies that are symmetric with respect to
the two state copies; i.e.,
\mathbb{F}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}\Omega\mathbb{F}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}=\Omega
,
where
\mathbb{F}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}
is the swap operator between the first and second copy.
Regarding Eq. (
4
), we make the following useful observations:
(a) It suffices to optimize over pure fake states; and
(b) If the quantum device is not too bad, i.e.,
there exists an
insurance infidelity

\varepsilon_{\max}\left(\Omega\right)\geq\varepsilon
,depending on verification strategy,
such that
\left\langle\psi\right|\sigma\left|\psi\right\rangle\geq 1-\varepsilon_{\max}\left(\Omega\right)
for all
\sigma
,
it is then suffices to consider fake states
\sigma
for which
\left\langle\psi\right|\sigma\left|\psi\right\rangle=1-\varepsilon
.
We prove these observations in Appendix
A
,
where we elaborate the significance and bounds of
the insurance infidelity parameter
\varepsilon_{\max}\left(\Omega\right)
.
Note that for
\Omega
satisfying certain conditions, such as the strategy for graph states described below,
\varepsilon_{\max}(\Omega)\gg\varepsilon
, thus this insurance can be tested almost at no cost.
We introduce the following two projectors:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{s}:=\frac{\mathbb{F}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}+\mathbb{I}_{12}}{2},\quad\mathbb{P}_{\psi}:=\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|\otimes(\mathbb{I}-\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|),

(6)

which are useful in deriving the analytic formula.
Note that
\mathbb{P}_{s}
is the projector onto the symmetric subspace of
\mathcal{H}^{n}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{n}
.
For any symmetric two-copy verification strategy

define the doubly projected operator
\Omega_{\star}:=2\mathbb{P}_{\psi}\mathbb{P}_{s}\Omega\mathbb{P}_{s}\mathbb{P}_{\psi}
.
Let
\lambda_{\star}(\Omega)
be the maximal eigenvalue of the projected operator
\Omega_{\star}
.
We show that,
\lambda_{\star}
is the intrinsic property of
\Omega
which underpins

\Omega

The proof can be found in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 1
.

When
\lambda_{\star}(\Omega)<1
and
the existence of insurance fidelity
\varepsilon_{\max}\left(\Omega\right)
is guaranteed, it holds that

\displaystyle p(\Omega)=1-2(1-\lambda_{\star}(\Omega))\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1.5}).

(7)

Correspondingly, the sample complexity of
\Omega
is given by

\displaystyle N_{m}(\Omega)=\frac{2\ln\delta}{\ln p(\Omega)}\approx\frac{1}{(1-\lambda_{\star}(\Omega))\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}.

(8)

Comparing Eqs. (
2
) and (
8
),
we see that it is
\lambda_{\star}(\Omega)
, instead of
\lambda_{2}(\Omega)
,
that determines the sample complexity of
\Omega
in the memory assisted scenario.
For the tensor product of single-copy globally optimal strategies,

\Omega_{g}=\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|^{\otimes 2}
, we find that
\lambda_{\star}(\Omega_{g})=0
,
implying a sample complexity of
1/\varepsilon\ln 1/\delta
.
This confirms that, in this specific case, quantum memory assistance cannot surpass
the ultimate bound established by entangled measurements.
Similarly, for a tensor product strategy
\Omega=\Omega_{l}\otimes\Omega_{l}
,
where
\Omega_{l}
is any single-copy local verification strategy and quantum memories are absent,

\lambda_{\star}(\Omega)=\lambda_{2}(\Omega_{l})
, reducing precisely to the single-copy case.
These examples demonstrate the alignment of our findings with existing results.
Extending Theorem
1
for arbitrary
k

is possible through generalized versions of
\mathbb{P}_{s}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\psi}
.
However, two-copy verification strategies already showcase the potential to achieve
globally optimal efficiency as we will show in the following examples.
Moreover, the fidelity and coherence time requirements of quantum memory devices
become increasingly stringent with larger

,
potentially hindering their feasibility for practical applications beyond a certain threshold.

Dimension expansion.
—In the two-copy verification,
we analytically solved the maximization problem in Eq. (
4
),
relating the verification efficiency to an intrinsic property of
\Omega
.
However, it is demanding to generalize the result to larger

.
Inspired by the observation that every

-tensor state
\left|\Psi\right\rangle

can be equivalently viewed as a single

-partite state with local dimension

, we present the dimension expansion method that construct

-QSV protocol
according to existing

This “dimension expansion” from

to

leverages quantum memory
and establish an equivalence between an

Concretely, we relax the maximization problem in Eq. (

by considering any quantum state

\displaystyle p(\Omega)\leq\max_{\left\langle\Psi\right|\xi\left|\Psi\right\rangle\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{k}}\tr[\Omega\xi]=1-(1-\lambda_{2}(\Omega))\varepsilon^{\prime},

(13)

where

Because

\displaystyle N_{m}(\Omega)\leq\frac{1}{(1-\lambda_{2}(\Omega))\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}=:N_{{\rm de},k}(\Omega).

When investigating quantum memory assisted state verification,

(ii) Trust: the quantum memories are faithful without experimental error.
If either property is violated, the

In this correlated case, a weaker verification task determines whether

The constraint then relaxes to

leading to

as evident from

13
) and (
14
).
This signifies

as a fundamental upper bound on

GHZ-like states.​​​​

Conclusions.
—We have proposed a theoretical framework to
quantitatively analyze the performance boost offered by quantum memories in quantum state verification.
Our work demonstrates that memory-assisted verification strategies significantly
outperform non-assisted ones,
with a remarkable finding that even just two copies suffice to
achieve the theoretical limit of verification efficiency.
This superiority lies in the extended storage capacity,
enabling the verifier to perform powerful entangled measurements within the memory.

Many questions remain open.
Specifically, the analytic formula for two-copy verification and the optimal two-copy strategy
for graph states might be generalized to wider scenarios
with larger amount of quantum memories and arbitrary quantum states.
However, deriving such solutions will likely require innovative techniques
due to increased computational demands and higher state dimensions.

\varepsilon_{r},\varepsilon_{r}^{\prime}\in[\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{\max}\left(\Omega\right)]\end{subarray}}\left\langle\sigma\sigma^{\prime}\right|\Omega\left|\sigma\sigma^{\prime}\right\rangle=1-2(1-\lambda_{\star}(\Omega))\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1.5}),

(52)

Proof.

We establish an additional critical maximum value for the operator
\Omega
and the quantum state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\star}(\Omega)=\max_{\left|\psi^{\perp}\right\rangle}\left(\left\langle\psi\psi^{\perp}\right|\Omega\left|\psi\psi^{\perp}\right\rangle+\left\langle\psi^{\perp}\psi\right|\Omega\left|\psi\psi^{\perp}\right\rangle\right).

(53)

According to Proposition
2
, the existence of insurance infidelity guarantees that
\xi_{\star}(\Omega)+\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{\star}(\Omega)<1
. Consequently,
\lambda_{\star}(\Omega)\leq\xi_{\star}(\Omega)+\frac{\gamma_{\star}(\Omega)}{2}<1
.

Given the insurance infidelity
\varepsilon_{\max}\left(\Omega\right)
and the set
R
defined in Proposition
2
, we observe that the set
S=\{(\varepsilon_{r},\varepsilon_{r}^{\prime})|\varepsilon_{r},\varepsilon_{r}^{\prime}\in[\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{\max}\left(\Omega\right)]\}
satisfies

. Therefore,
p(\Omega)
, being the maximum value within the region

with respect to variables
\psi^{\perp}
,
\psi^{\prime\perp}
,
\varepsilon_{r}
, and
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r}
, is attained solely at the constraint
(\varepsilon_{r},\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r})=(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
.

Further optimization over
\psi
and
\psi^{\perp}
is as follows:

\displaystyle p_{\max}

\displaystyle=1-\max_{\left|\psi^{\perp}\right\rangle,\left|\psi^{\prime\perp}\right\rangle}[(1-R-B)+(1-R^{\prime}-B^{\prime})]\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1.5})

(54)

\displaystyle=1-2(1-\lambda_{\star}(\Omega))\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1.5}).

(55)

Again, given that
[\Omega,\mathbb{F}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}]=0
, we can further simplify the expression of
\lambda_{\star}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\star}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\max_{\left|\psi^{\perp}\right\rangle}\left\langle\psi\psi^{\perp}\right|(\mathbb{F}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}+\mathbb{I}_{12})\Omega\left|\psi\psi^{\perp}\right\rangle

(56)

\displaystyle=\max_{\left|\Phi\right\rangle}\left\langle\Phi\right|\mathbb{P}_{\psi}(\mathbb{F}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}+\mathbb{I}_{12})\Omega\mathbb{P}_{\psi}\left|\Phi\right\rangle

(57)

\displaystyle=\max_{\left|\Phi\right\rangle}\left\langle\Phi\right|2\mathbb{P}_{\psi}\mathbb{P}_{s}\Omega\mathbb{P}_{s}\mathbb{P}_{\psi}\left|\Phi\right\rangle,

(58)

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\psi}=\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|\otimes(\mathbb{I}-\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|),\qquad\mathbb{P}_{s}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{F}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}+\mathbb{I}_{12}).

(59)

Then,
\lambda_{\star}(\Omega)
is the maximum eigenvalue of the operator
\Omega_{\star}=2\mathbb{P_{\psi}}\mathbb{P}_{s}\Omega\mathbb{P}_{s}\mathbb{P}_{\psi}
.
∎

Appendix B
Verification and information-preserving channel

To construct the two-copy verification strategy, we consider
the case where the verifiers first implement the local operation and classical communication (LOCC) channel
\Lambda
.
This channel treats the second copies as if they were an ideal graph state
and utilizes this entanglement resource to implement a series of non-local gates to the first copy.
These gates are designed to perform unitary rotations, transforming an identical graph state into the specific state
\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle
. Following this channel, everyone measures their first copies on the computational basis
\{\left|0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\right|,\left|1\right\rangle\!\left\langle 1\right|\}
and passed the test if the results are all
0
.
For simplicity, we use
\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle_{n}=\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle
to denote the basis state of
n
-qubits.
For the expected state
\left|G\right\rangle\otimes\left|G\right\rangle
, it holds
\Lambda(\left|G\right\rangle\!\left\langle G\right|\otimes\left|G\right\rangle\!\left\langle G\right|)=\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\bm{0}\right|_{n}\otimes\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\bm{0}\right|_{n}
.
To assess the efficiency for fake states, In this Appendix,
we reformulate the optimization tasks in terms of information-preserving channels and establish the relation between channels
and measurement operators as
\Omega_{g}=\Lambda^{\dagger}(\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\bm{0}\right|_{n}\otimes\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\bm{0}\right|_{n})
.
We need the following lemma, which follows directly from
(
Chitambar et al. 2014
, Theorem 2)
.

Lemma 2
.

Any LOCC measurement strategy can be decomposed and consequently implemented through a LOCC channel within the same Hilbert space, followed by a measurement in the computational basis with a specific selection of binomial measurement results that yield the “pass” outcome.

One could set arbitrary binary string to the binomial measurement results with a “pass” outcome. However, the following theorem states that for a specific choice,
\{0\cdots 0\}
, this strategy could formulate all the semi-optimal one-way strategies
Yu et al. 2019
.

Theorem 4
.

Any semi-optimal one-way strategy
Yu et al. 2019
can be constructed as
a one-way LOCC channel followed by a binomial passing choice represented as
0\cdots 0
.

Proof.

For a semi-optimal one-way strategy with target state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
, Alice chooses a measurement
\left|v_{i}\right\rangle\!\left\langle v_{i}\right|
with results
i=0,\cdots,n
. Subsequently, Bob performs measurements on
\left|u_{t|i}\right\rangle\!\left\langle u_{t|i}\right|
where
\left|u_{0|i}\right\rangle=\left\langle v_{i}\middle|\psi\right\rangle
. In accordance with this, we define a unitary matrix
U_{i}
such that
\left|0\right\rangle=U_{i}\left|u_{0|i}\right\rangle
. The one-way LOCC channel can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Lambda(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}M_{i}\rho M_{i}^{\dagger},

(64)

\displaystyle M_{i}

\displaystyle=\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle v_{i}\right|\otimes U_{i}.

(65)

Subsequently, if Alice and Bob apply this channel first and then both measure on
\left|0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\right|,\left|1\right\rangle\!\left\langle 1\right|
with the pass results represented by
\left|00\right\rangle
, they will get the same passing probability for any fake state
\sigma
.
∎

Thus we consider all the strategies that set the "pass" binomial measurement results as
\{0\cdots 0\}
and gives the channel optimization task below:

Theorem 5

(Channel optimization)
.

Fix the choice of "pass" binomial measurement results as
\{0\cdots 0\}
. Let’s assume that
n
independent parties share a state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
. A LOCC channel
\Lambda
is optimal for verification if and only if it satisfies the following condition:

1.

\Lambda(\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|)=\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\cdots 0\right|
.

2.

Any other LOCC channel
\Lambda^{\prime}
satisfied the first condition will cancel more information on the difference between
\sigma
and
\left|\psi\right\rangle
compared to
\Lambda
. In other words,

\displaystyle\tr[\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|\sigma]

\displaystyle\leq\tr[\Lambda(\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|)\Lambda(\sigma)]

(66)

\displaystyle\leq\tr[\Lambda^{\prime}(\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|)\Lambda^{\prime}(\sigma)].

(67)

This information-preserving channel, along with the verification strategy
\{\Omega,\mathbb{I}-\Omega\}
constructed by this channel, then satisfies:

1.

\Omega=\Lambda^{\dagger}(\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\cdots 0\right|)
.

2.

M_{i}\left|\psi\right\rangle=c_{i}\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle
. Here
M_{i}
is the Kraus operator of channel
\Lambda
,
c_{i}
is a constant parameter.

3.

\Lambda^{\dagger}(\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\cdots 0\right|)\left|\psi\right\rangle=\sum_{i}c_{i}M_{i}^{\dagger}\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle=\left|\psi\right\rangle
.

Proof.

We consider the strategy in which verifiers manipulate channel
\Lambda
first and pass with all qubits in the result
\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\cdots 0\right|
. For any fake state
\sigma
, the passing probability is expressed as:

\displaystyle p(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\left\langle 0\cdots 0\right|\Lambda(\sigma)\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle

(68)

\displaystyle=\tr[\Lambda(\sigma)\Lambda(\left|\psi\cdots\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\cdots\psi\right|)]=\tr[\sigma\Lambda^{\dagger}(\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\cdots 0\right|)].

(69)

Consequently, we have derived the first conclusion that
\Omega=\Lambda^{\dagger}(\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\cdots 0\right|)
. For any other channels, due to the second condition in the theorem, it must satisfy:

\displaystyle p(\Lambda^{\prime})=\tr[\Lambda^{\prime}(\sigma)\Lambda^{\prime}(\left|\psi\cdots\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\cdots\psi\right|)]\geq p(\Lambda).

(70)

A larger passing probability implies that the
\Omega^{\prime}
generated by
\Lambda^{\prime}
will have less power to discern the fake state compared to
\Lambda
, showcasing the optimality of the channel construction within this fixed passing binomial choice. We suppose
M_{i}\left|\psi\right\rangle=c_{i}\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle
. Then the first condition becomes:

\displaystyle|c_{i}|^{2}\sum_{i}\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\Phi_{i}\right|=\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\cdots 0\right|.

(71)

Given that
\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\cdots 0\right|
is a pure state and lies at the boundary of the convex set, it implies that
\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle=\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle
must be satisfied, which proves the second conclusion. Regarding the last conclusion, we prove it with the calculations below:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\dagger}(\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\cdots 0\right|)\left|\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}M_{i}^{\dagger}\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\cdots 0\right|M_{i}\left|\psi\right\rangle

(72)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}c_{i}M_{i}^{\dagger}\left|0\cdots 0\right\rangle

(73)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}M_{i}^{\dagger}M_{i}\left|\psi\right\rangle=\left|\psi\right\rangle.

(74)

In the last equality, we use the trace one condition on the channel where
\sum_{i}M_{i}^{\dagger}M_{i}=\mathbb{I}
.
∎

F.3
Comparision with non-adaptive measurements based stabilizer state verification

It is well-known that stabilizer states can be verified efficiently non-adaptively,
as shown in the Theorems 6 and 7 in the work by Pallister
et al.

Pallister et al. 2018
.
To justify the improvement through using Bell measurements, we noted that there are two critical factors to adjust the efficiency of a verification strategy. One, as mentioned by you, is the sample complexity
N(\Omega)
, and the other is the number of measurement settings. In fact, research
Li et al. 2021
has shown the minimum number of measurement settings required to
non-adaptively verify bipartite pure states.

For the first stabilizer verification strategy by Pallister
et al.

(
Pallister et al. 2018
, Theorem 6)
,
if
n
is the number of qubits,
one randomly chooses one stabilizer from the
2^{n}-1
stabilizers of the stabilizer state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
and projects onto the positive eigenspace of this stabilizer. This strategy has an efficiency of
{(2^{n}-1)}/{2^{n-1}}\times\varepsilon^{-1}\ln{\delta}^{-1}
. Indeed, our strategy only has a constant sample complexity improvement because
{(2^{n}-1)}/{2^{n-1}}<2
for all
n
. However, this strategy requires
2^{n}-1
measurement settings, which becomes particularly challenging when it is difficult or slow to switch measurement settings, as is the case in many practical scenarios. In our strategy, only one measurement setting is required.

The second strategy by Pallister
et al.

(
Pallister et al. 2018
, Theorem 7)
improves the number of measurement settings.
It chooses one stabilizer from
n
stabilizer generators of
\left|\psi\right\rangle
and projects onto the positive eigenspace of this generator.
This strategy requires only
n
measurement settings.
However, the sample complexity now becomes
N=n\varepsilon^{-1}\ln\delta^{-1}
.
This complexity scales linearly with the number of qubits
n
, thus concluding a
\mathcal{O}(n)
improvement in sample complexity for our strategy.

To the best of our knowledge, there is still no strategy that can use constant measurement settings and constant sample complexity to verify stabilizer states. We thus expect the existence of quantum memory or collective measurements can help to find a strategy with both better sampling complexity and fewer measurement settings.

F.4
Comparision with quantum state verification in the adversarial scenario

In the standard quantum state verification
Pallister et al. 2018
,
the adversary can only prepare
independent
multipartite entangled states and send to the local verifiers.
After receiving a quantum state, the verifiers conduct local measurments and make decision based on the measurement outcomes.
From the quantum memory perspective, neither the adversary nor the verifiers have access to quantum memory.

In quantum state verification in the adversarial scenario
Zhu and Hayashi 2019
,
the adversary is much more powerful and can produce an arbitrarily correlated or even entangled state
among many state copies, which is applicable to the case of
nonindependent
sources.
After receiving the multipartite quantum state in many copies, the verifiers conduct local measurments and make decision based on the measurement outcomes.
Since the verifiers have no quantum memory, they have to conduct measurements qudit by qudit
but cannot conduct measurements across many qudits.
From the quantum memory perspective, the adversary possesses a quantum memory but the verifiers do not have quantum memory.

Our work generalizes the standard quantum state verification and is converse to the quantum state verification in the adversarial scenario:
In the verification task under our consideration, the adversary does not possess quantum memory but the verifiers have access to quantum memory.
Intuitively, we can expect that the verifiers can acomplish the verification tasks more efficiently since they
can store the quantum states and measure them collectively to make decision.
The main contribution of our work is that we rigorously justify this intuition by establishing
a mathematical framework and proposing various strategy construction techniques to accomplish this task.

In the most general case, we can consider quantum state verification where both the adversary and
the verifiers have access to different amount of quantum memory, quantified by the number of qudits that they can
store before processing. This corresponds to quantum state verification in the experimental and practical situations.
We leave this interesting problem as future work.
