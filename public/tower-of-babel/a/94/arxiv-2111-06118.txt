Effective Hamiltonians in the Quantum Rabi Problem
P. Gartner 1, V. Moldoveanu 1

Abstract
We revisit the theoretical description of the ultrastrong light-matter interaction in terms of exactly solvable effective Hamiltonians. A perturbative approach based on polaronic and spin-dependent squeezing transformations provides an effective Hamiltonian for the quantum Rabi model up to the second order in the expansion parameter. The model consistently includes both rotating and counter-rotating terms, going therefore beyond the rotating wave approximation. Analytical and numerical results show that the proposed Hamiltonian performs better than the Bloch-Siegert model when calculating operator averages (e.g. the mean photon number and number of excitations). This improvement is due to a refined calculation of the dressed states within the present model. Regarding the frequency shift induced by the qubit-photon interaction, we find a different sign from the Bloch-Siegert value. This influences the eigenstates structure in a non-trivial way and ensures the correct calculation of the number of excitations associated to a given dressed state. As a consistency check, we show that the exactly solvable independent boson model is reproduced as a special limit case of the perturbative Hamiltonian.



I
Introduction

The physics of strongly coupled qubit-photon hybrid systems has become a steadily developing field
Kockum
;
RMP
which
challenges the existing theoretical methods. In particular, as the field-matter coupling strength
increases, the Jaynes-Cummings model
JCM
, which neglects the so-called off-resonant transitions
within the rotating wave approximation (RWA), become
s
less accurate.

More general models taking fully into acount the interaction between a two-level system (e.g.  a superconducting qubit
or a quantum dot) and a boson mode (cavity photon or transmission line resonator) are defined by the class of Hamiltonians

H=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\sigma_{z}+\omega a^{\dagger}a+g_{1}(a^{\dagger}\sigma+a\sigma^{\dagger})+g_{2}(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\dagger}+a\sigma)\,,

(1)

where the quasi-spin notation is used for the qubit and
a,a^{\dagger}
are bosonic operators of the single-mode field.
The corresponding energies are
\varepsilon
and
\omega
respectively, and
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are the coupling strengths for the resonant (rotating)
and off-resonant (counter-rotating) processes. We take
\hbar=1
throughout. The Jaynes-Cummings (JC) Hamiltonian is then recovered
from Eq.(
1
)
by taking
g_{2}=0
, while the quantum Rabi model (QRM)
Meystre
;
Walls-Milburn
corresponds to
g_{1}=g_{2}=g
:

H_{R}=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\sigma_{z}+\omega a^{\dagger}a+g\sigma_{x}(a^{\dagger}+a)\,.

(2)

Generally, the problems defined by Eq.(
1
) are not exactly soluble, one notable exception being
the quantum Rabi Hamiltonian whose spectrum was calculated
Braak1
;
Braak2
;
Wang
as zeroes of recursively defined new
special functions or in terms of confluent Heun functions
Zhong
. The mathematics is quite involved both for the
eigenvalues and for eigenvectors.
In view of this situation, in the literature one prefers to devise
effective Hamiltonians
RMP
to obtain approximate eigenvalues and eigenvectors, in suitably restricted domains of the parameter
space defined by
\{\varepsilon,\omega,g\}
.
Also, simple, analytic effective descriptions are strongly preferred when including additional interactions to
describe optical, transport
Cirio
;
DMG
or relaxation processes
DeLiberato
;
Beaudoin
;
Settineri
.

A useful test for these approximations consists in inspecting limit cases in which exact solutions are known for the full
Hamiltonian. One such case is the JC model mentioned above, in which the RWA brings the Hamiltonian to a
2\times 2

block-diagonal form. A second one is the independent boson model (IBM)
Mahan
which is extensively used for quantum optical
and transport calculations involving carrier interaction with photons, phonons
Krummheuer
or vibrons
Mitra
.
In the present case the IBM limit is obtained by taking
\varepsilon=0
in the QRM, Eq. (
2
), and the corresponding Hamiltonian is
diagonalized by a displacement of the bosonic oscillator centers brought in by the Lang-Firsov, or polaronic unitary transform.

In deriving approximate effective Hamiltonians for the quantum Rabi problem one usually employs a limited expansion
of various unitary transforms in terms of a suitable small parameter. An example is provided by the dispersive Hamiltonian

Zueco
;
Blais
obtained by a unitary transform aiming at diagonalizing the qubit-boson interaction, expanded to second order in
the parameter
\lambda=g/|\varepsilon-\omega|
, in the weak coupling limit
\lambda\ll 1
.
The resulting effective Hamiltonian reproduces the JC spectrum to second order in
\lambda
, i.e. within
the domain of parameters in which it was derived. Moreover, it recovers the exact result in the IBM
limit.

Another popular effective Hamiltonian is the Bloch-Siegert (BS) one
Klimov-Chumakov
;
Rossatto
. It
treats the QRM problem up to second order in the parameter
\lambda=g/(\varepsilon+\omega)
and brings it to a renormalized
form of the JC Hamiltonian. Two succesive unitary transforms are used in the process and higher order off-resonant terms are discarded
as rapidly oscillating, in the spirit of the RWA.
Supposedly, the validity domain of this approach is roughly
g/\omega\lesssim 0.3
and corresponds to the so-called perturbative
ultra strong coupling regime (pUSC)
RMP
. The perturbative criteria for the BS Hamiltonian was recently used

Rossatto
to establish a spectral classification of the field-matter coupling regimes in the QRM. On the experimental side the
Bloch-Siegert shift was recently observed in strongly coupled qubit-oscillator systems
Diaz-BS
. An alternative improvement of
the JC model relies on the Van Vleck perturbation theory to the second order in the qubit-field coupling
Hausinger
.

Surprisingly, a simple analysis shows that the IBM limit is
not
recovered by the BS Hamiltonian, despite the fact
that taking
\varepsilon=0
does not preclude the expansion parameter, now equal to
g/\omega
, to remain small. Still, the BS model
is tacitly accepted as the main candidate to describe the ultra-strong coupling regime.

In view of this situation, in what follows we show how to obtain an effective Hamiltonian for
the QRM, in the same perturbative conditions as for the BS model, i.e. up to the second order in
\lambda
, having
the same simple, renormalized JC structure and which additionally meets the test
of recovering the exact IBM result for
\varepsilon=0
.
Moreover, it will become obvious that the procedure is
purely perturbative
, i.e. it does not have to
rely on discarding off-resonant terms.

As the qubit energy
\varepsilon
approaches resonance the spectra of the two effective models become more similar.
Nevertheless, we show that important differences remain in the dressed states and
are inherited by the expectation values provided by these states. Especially the excitation number turns out to be sensitive to
these differences.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The perturbative effective Hamiltonian is derived in Section II.
A detailed comparison of this perturbative Hamiltonian with the Bloch-Siegert model is presented and discussed in Section III.
Their spectra, average photon number, population inversion and number of excitations are obtained and compared both analytically and
numerically in subsections III A, III B, and III C. We also compare our result with the so called generalized
rotating wave Hamiltonian (GRWA) obtained by Irish
Irish
in the deep strong coupling case (subsection III D).
This regime is defined by large values
of the coupling,
g/\omega\approx 1
and beyond, but the numerical evidence
Yu
;
Xie
;
Zhang2016
shows that this approach
gives good results at lower couplings too. Therefore comparison against it is relevant. Besides, the GRWA Hamiltonian meets
the IBM limit test. Section IV is left for conclusions.

II
An IBM-compatible effective Hamiltonian

We consider unitary transforms of the form
e^{S}
, with
S^{\dagger}=-S
and their expansion in iterated
commutators with their generators
S
. Then any operator
A
transforms as

A_{S}=e^{S}A\,e^{-S}=A+\left[S,A\right]+\frac{1}{2!}\left[S,\left[S,A\right]\right]+\dots

(3)

Now let us rewrite the Rabi Hamiltonian of Eq.(
2
) as

H_{R}=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\sigma_{z}+H_{IBM}\,,

(4)

in which we separate the free qubit contribution and the independent boson model part.
The linear part in
H_{IBM}
can be absorbed in the quadratic term by a shift of the bosonic operators, produced by the generator

S=\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)\,\sigma_{x}\,

(5)

with the value of
\lambda
to be chosen later.
Using

[S,a^{\dagger}]=-\lambda\,\sigma_{x}\,,\qquad[S,a]=-\lambda\,\sigma_{x}\,,

(6)

it is clear that the canonical transformation amounts to a simple shift

a^{\dagger}_{S}=a^{\dagger}-\lambda\,\sigma_{x}\,,\qquad a_{S}=a-\lambda\,\sigma_{x}\,.

(7)

As a result, the transformed Hamiltonian becomes

H_{IBM,S}=\omega a^{\dagger}a+(g-\lambda\omega)(a^{\dagger}+a)\sigma_{x}+(\lambda^{2}\omega-2\lambda g)\,.

(8)

Choosing
\lambda=g/\omega
removes the interaction term and diagonalizes the IBM
Hamiltonian.

H_{IBM,S}=\omega a^{\dagger}a-g^{2}/\omega\,.

(9)

Therefore the IBM problem alone is exactly solvable. The solution
consists of the oscillator keeping its frequency
\omega
, but shifted by
g/\omega
,
the shift direction being decided by the eigenstate of
\sigma_{x}

The presence of the free qubit part in Eq.(
4
) spoils this simple scenario.
Both the frequency and the shift are modified, and we show below that in these
circumstances a different choice for the value of
\lambda
is more appropriate.

Obviously,
\sigma_{x}
is left unchanged by the IBM transform. In contrast, the
change in
\sigma_{z}
is rather complex. One can easily check that:

\displaystyle[S,\sigma_{z}]=

\displaystyle-2\,\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)\,i\sigma_{y}\,,

\displaystyle[S,i\sigma_{y}]=

\displaystyle-2\,\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)\,\sigma_{z}\,.

(10)

This means that successive commutators generate alternatively the
\sigma_{z}
and

i\sigma_{y}
operators, and each step brings in an additional factor
-2\,\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)
. The
\sigma_{z}
operator collects the even
terms, which add up to a hyperbolic cosine function, while the
i\sigma_{y}
terms
generate a hyperbolic sine function.
The net result is

\displaystyle H_{R,S}=\omega a^{\dagger}a+(g-\lambda\omega)(a^{\dagger}+a)\sigma_{x}+(\lambda^{2}\omega-2\lambda g)

\displaystyle+\,\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\left\{\cosh[2\,\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)]\,\sigma_{z}-\sinh[2\,\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)]\,i\sigma_{y}\right\}\,.

(11)

Up to now no approximation is involved. A simpler Hamiltonian, valid to
the second order in
\lambda
, is obtained by expanding

\cosh(x)\approx 1+x^{2}/2
and
\sinh(x)\approx x
.
Taking into account that
\sigma_{x}=\sigma^{\dagger}+\sigma
and
i\sigma_{y}=\sigma^{\dagger}-\sigma
,
this expansion leads to an effective Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
of the form

\displaystyle H_{R,S}\approx H^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\sigma_{z}+\omega a^{\dagger}a+(\lambda^{2}\omega-2\lambda g)

\displaystyle+(g-\lambda\omega+\lambda\varepsilon)(a^{\dagger}\sigma+a\sigma^{\dagger})+(g-\lambda\omega-\lambda\varepsilon)(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\dagger}+a\sigma)

\displaystyle+\lambda^{2}\varepsilon(a^{\dagger}-a)^{2}\sigma_{z}\,.

(12)

Now it is clear that an appropriate choice for
\lambda
is

\lambda=\frac{g}{\varepsilon+\omega}\,

(13)

which cancels out the off-resonant term linear in the bosonic operators. This
is the same small parameter as the one used in the Bloch-Siegert formalism

Klimov-Chumakov
.
The effective Hamiltonian has now the expression

\displaystyle H^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\sigma_{z}+\omega a^{\dagger}a-(2\,\lambda g-\lambda^{2}\omega)

\displaystyle+2\lambda\varepsilon(a^{\dagger}\sigma+a\sigma^{\dagger})+\lambda^{2}\varepsilon(a^{\dagger}-a)^{2}\sigma_{z}\,.

(14)

Finally, one has to handle the last, quadratic term. To this end one
considers a second unitary transform, with the generator

S^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\eta(a^{\dagger\,2}-a^{2})\sigma_{z}\,.

(15)

This
\sigma_{z}
-dependent squeezing transform
Comm-Zhang
leads to

[S^{\prime},a^{\dagger}]=-\eta\,a\,\sigma_{z}\,,\qquad[S^{\prime},a]=-\eta\,a^{\dagger}\sigma_{z}\,,

(16)

in which again succesive commutators generate alternatively the same two
operators. One gets a Bogolyubov-type transform

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}_{S^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\cosh\eta\,a^{\dagger}-\sinh\eta\,a\,\sigma_{z}\,,

\displaystyle a_{S^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-\sinh\eta\,a^{\dagger}\,\sigma_{z}+\cosh\eta\,a\,.

(17)

Different Hamiltonian pieces change accordingly, for instance the photon number operator

\displaystyle a_{S^{\prime}}^{\dagger}a_{S^{\prime}}=\cosh 2\eta\,a^{\dagger}a+\sinh^{2}\eta

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sinh 2\eta(a^{\dagger}-a)^{2}\sigma_{z}-\frac{1}{2}\sinh 2\eta(2a^{\dagger}a+1)\sigma_{z}\,,

(18)

and

\displaystyle(a_{S^{\prime}}^{\dagger}-a_{S^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=(\cosh\eta+\sinh\eta\,\sigma_{z})(a^{\dagger}-a)\,,

\displaystyle(a_{S^{\prime}}^{\dagger}-a_{S^{\prime}})^{2}\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle=[\cosh 2\eta\,\sigma_{z}+\sinh 2\eta](a^{\dagger}-a)^{2}\,.

(19)

From Eqs.(
18
),(
19
) above the quantities in
H^{\prime}_{S^{\prime}}
containing
(a^{\dagger}-a)^{2}
are identified as:

\displaystyle X_{z}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}\left(\lambda^{2}\varepsilon\cosh 2\eta-\frac{\omega}{2}\sinh 2\eta\right)(a^{\dagger}-a)^{2}\,,

(20)

\displaystyle X_{0}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\varepsilon\sinh 2\eta(a^{\dagger}-a)^{2}\,.

(21)

The terms contained in
X_{z}
cancel out by choosing
\eta
so that

\frac{1}{2}\sinh 2\eta=\frac{\lambda^{2}\varepsilon}{\omega}\cosh 2\eta\,.

(22)

The condition makes
\eta
of second order in
\lambda
and, neglecting higher
contributions, one has

\eta=\frac{\lambda^{2}\varepsilon}{\omega}\,.

(23)

In this case the term
X_{0}
in Eq.(
21
) is of fourth order in
\lambda
and is discarded.

The terms in the Hamiltonian Eq.(
14
) not considered up to now are
the free qubit energy, which commutes with
S^{\prime}
, and the JC interaction

2\lambda\,\varepsilon(a^{\dagger}\sigma+a\sigma^{\dagger})
, which is already a first order quantity, and
therefore its transformation by
S^{\prime}\sim\lambda^{2}
brings in changes of at least
third order. Therefore both are kept unchanged.

Finally, expanding systematically the hyperbolic functions to second order in

\lambda
(i.e. first order in
\eta
)
and spelling out the expressions of
\lambda
and
\eta
in terms of the
model parameters, leaves us with
H^{\prime}_{S^{\prime}}\approx H_{P}
where
the perturbative IBM-compatible effective Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H_{P}=

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(1-\frac{2g^{2}}{(\varepsilon+\omega)^{2}}\right)\sigma_{z}+\left(\omega-\frac{2g^{2}\varepsilon}{(\varepsilon+\omega)^{2}}\,\sigma_{z}\right)\,a^{\dagger}a

\displaystyle+2\,\frac{g\,\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\omega}(a^{\dagger}\sigma+a\,\sigma^{\dagger})-\frac{g^{2}}{\varepsilon+\omega}\,\frac{2\,\varepsilon+\omega}{\varepsilon+\omega}\,.

(24)

This expression of the effective Hamiltonian
H_{P}
is the main result
of the paper.

It is now obvious that for
\varepsilon=0
,
H_{P}
recovers exactly the
IBM result, Eq. (
9
) and, moreover, this holds for all values of the coupling constant
g
.

III
Comparing effective Hamiltonians

The Bloch-Siegert and the perturbative effective Hamiltonian
given by Eq. (
24
) have both the structure of a renormalized JC model

H=\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon+\Omega)\sigma_{z}+(\omega+\Omega\sigma_{z})a^{\dagger}a+\gamma(a^{\dagger}\sigma+a\sigma^{\dagger})-\delta\,.

(25)

The difference comes from the parameters
\Omega,\gamma
and
\delta
. They are given
by
Rossatto

\Omega=\frac{g^{2}}{\omega+\varepsilon},\quad\gamma=g,\quad\delta=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{g^{2}}{\omega+\varepsilon}\,

(26)

for
H=H_{BS}
, and by

\Omega=-\frac{g^{2}}{\omega+\varepsilon}\,\frac{2\varepsilon}{\omega+\varepsilon},\quad\gamma=g\,\frac{2\varepsilon}{\omega+\varepsilon},\quad\delta=\frac{g^{2}}{\omega+\varepsilon}\,\frac{\omega+2\varepsilon}{\omega+\varepsilon}\,

(27)

for
H=H_{P}
.

The JC structure of Eq.(
25
) reduced the problem to
2\times 2
blocks,
indexed by the photon number
n
. In the corresponding basis

\left\{\ket{n,\uparrow},\ket{n+1,\downarrow}\right\}
the
n
-th block has the form

\displaystyle{\hat{h}}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}\sigma_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{n}\sigma_{z}+\gamma\sqrt{n+1}\sigma_{x}

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}\sigma_{0}+R_{n}(\cos\theta_{n}\sigma_{z}+\sin\theta_{n}\sigma_{x})\,,

where
\sigma_{0}
is the
2\times 2
unit matrix and we introduced

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega\,(n+1/2)-\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}+\delta\right)

(29)

\displaystyle R_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Delta_{n}^{2}/4+\gamma^{2}(n+1)}\,,

(30)

with
\Delta_{n}=\varepsilon-\omega+2\,\Omega\,(n+1)
acting as an effective,
n
-dependent detuning. The angle

\theta_{n}
is defined by the relations
\cos\theta_{n}=\Delta_{n}/(2R_{n})
and

\sin\theta_{n}=\gamma\sqrt{n+1}/R_{n}
. Note that
\Omega/2+\delta
turns out to take the
same value
g^{2}/(\omega+\varepsilon)
in both effective Hamiltonians considered, therefore
the middle points of their spectra coincide.

It is known that the spectrum of
{\hat{h}}_{n}
is
E_{n,\pm}=\alpha_{n}\pm R_{n}
with the
eigenvectors (i.e. dressed states) given by the linear combination

\ket{\varphi_{n,\pm}}:=u_{\pm}\ket{n,\uparrow}+v_{\pm}\ket{n+1,\downarrow}

(31)

where
(u_{+},v_{+})=(\cos\theta_{n}/2,\sin\theta_{n}/2)
for the higher state
\ket{\varphi_{n,+}}
and

(u_{-},v_{-})=(\sin\theta_{n}/2,-\cos\theta_{n}/2)
for the lower state
\ket{\varphi_{n,-}}
. To complete
the picture we also calculated give the energy of the ground state
\ket{0,\downarrow}

as
E_{0}=-\varepsilon/2-(\Omega/2+\delta)
.

By examining Eqs.(
26
) and (
27
), it is clear that the differences
between the two models are more pronounced for small values of
\varepsilon
. As already mentioned, at

\varepsilon=0
,
H_{P}
recovers the exact IBM result, while
H_{BS}
does not.

On the other hand at resonance,
\varepsilon=\omega
, the parameters of the two models coincide,
except for
the sign of

\Omega
. The latter has no bearing on the spectra, which therefore are the same for
the two models, but it modifies the structure of the eigenstates. Indeed, at resonance the sign
of
\Omega
carries over to a sign change in
\cos\theta_{n}
which in turn leads to exchanging
\cos\theta_{n}/2

and
\sin\theta_{n}/2
.
In other words, even though at resonance the spectra of
H_{BS}
and
H_{P}
coincide, because of the
sign difference in
\Delta_{n}
the attribution of the eigenvalues is flipped:
for example, the weight of the two basis vectors
\ket{n,\uparrow}
and
\ket{n+1,\downarrow}
in the
upper state
\ket{\varphi_{n,+}}
of the BS Hamiltonian is the same as for the lower state

\ket{\varphi_{n,-}}
of the perturbative Hamiltonian. Further consequences of this fact are
analyzed in subsection
III.3
.

To resume the situation, the two models predict different spectra and states, as suggested by these
two examples. Intermediate values for
\varepsilon\in[0,\omega]
are expected to lead to similar conclusions.
Let us also remark that
\Omega
is interpreted as a
bosonic frequency shift which depends on the qubit state, and detecting the former gives
information about the latter
RMP
. Therefore the sign difference means that this way
of reading the qubit state works in opposite directions in the predictions of the two models.

Still, these arguments are merely heuristic. Eigenstates are not directly comparable,
H_{P}
and

H_{BS}
having been obtained by different unitary transforms, i.e. the two effective Hamiltonians
work in different bases. The relevant comparison is on basis-independent quantities, to which we turn below.

III.2
Operator averages. Photon population

When calculating the expectation value of an operator on a given eigenstate of the
effective Hamiltonian, one has to take into acccount the unitary transforms that
lead to it. The operators should be ’rotated’ by the same procedure as the
Hamiltonian.

In the case of
H_{P}
, we use again the two generators
S=\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)\sigma_{x}
and

S^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\eta(a^{\dagger\,2}-a^{2})\sigma_{z}
, with the parameters given in Eqs.(
13
) and (
23
).
The observable we are interested in is the photon number
\hat{n}=a^{\dagger}a
. From Eq.(
7
)
we obtain by the first transform

a^{\dagger}_{S}a_{S}=a^{\dagger}a-\lambda(a^{\dagger}+a)\sigma_{x}+\lambda^{2}\,.

(32)

The second step involves
S^{\prime}
and is apparently more complicated but it gets simpler by limiting
the expansion to second order in
\lambda
and taking into account that
\eta\sim\lambda^{2}
.
For instance, it is clear that the second term in Eq.(
32
) remains unchanged.
Indeed, changes are at least of first power of
\eta
and with the prefactor
\lambda
they
are third order quantities.

For the first term
a^{\dagger}a
one uses Eq.(
18
) expanded to second order in
\lambda
, with the net result that
after both transforms one has

\hat{n}_{S,S^{\prime}}\approx\hat{n}-\eta(a^{\dagger\,2}+a^{2})\sigma_{z}-\lambda(a^{\dagger}+a)\sigma_{x}+\lambda^{2}\,.

(33)

This is the operator to be averaged on the eigenstates of the form given in
Eq.(
31
). Things turn out to be further simplified by noting that
operators like
a^{2},a\sigma
and their hermitic conjugates do not contribute to
the expectation value, since they do not connect vectors belonging to the same two-dimensional subspace.

To conclude, one has

\displaystyle\left<\hat{n}\right>_{P,\pm}=

\displaystyle\bra{\varphi_{n,\pm}}[a^{\dagger}a-\lambda(a^{\dagger}\sigma+a\sigma^{\dagger})+\lambda^{2}]\ket{\varphi_{n,\pm}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u^{2}_{\pm}n+v^{2}_{\pm}(n+1)-2\lambda u_{\pm}v_{\pm}\sqrt{n+1}+\lambda^{2}\,,

(34)

where the index
P
recalls that the average is calculated w.r.t. the dressed states of
H_{P}
. Obviously,

\left<\hat{n}\right>_{P,\pm}
depends on the specific two-dimensional subspace
n
as well but we drop
this additional index for the simplicity of writing.
Replacing
u_{\pm}
and
v_{\pm}
leads to the values

\displaystyle\left<\hat{n}\right>_{P,\pm}=\,n+\frac{1}{2}(1\mp\cos\theta_{n})\mp\lambda\sin\theta_{n}\sqrt{n+1}+\lambda^{2}\,.

(35)

A negative sign of
\Omega
means a lower
\cos\theta_{n}
value, and this encourages a higher
photon population in the upper state.
For the ground state one obtains
\left<{\hat{n}}\right>_{P,0}=\lambda^{2}
.

The prediction of the Bloch-Siegert Hamiltonian for the same quantity is evaluated by the same procedure.
Some differences arise though, beginning with the first generator which now reads

\tilde{S}=\lambda(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\dagger}-a\sigma)
with the result that now

a_{\tilde{S}}^{\dagger}a_{\tilde{S}}=a^{\dagger}a-\lambda(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\dagger}+a\sigma)+\lambda^{2}\sigma\sigma^{\dagger}-\lambda^{2}a^{\dagger}a\sigma_{z}\,.

(36)

The second transform has the same form
\tilde{S}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\eta(a^{\dagger\,2}-a^{2})\sigma_{z}

with
\eta\sim\lambda^{2}
.

As before, the terms containing
\lambda
remain unchanged
up to the second order, and the first term
a^{\dagger}a
is modified by a combination of

a^{2}
and
a^{\dagger\,2}
, which do not contribute to the expectation value. The new
feature is that no contribution comes from the second term in Eq.(
36
) above.
Therefore one is lead to

\displaystyle\left<\hat{n}\right>_{BS,\pm}=

\displaystyle\bra{\varphi_{n,\pm}}[a^{\dagger}a+\lambda^{2}\sigma\sigma^{\dagger}-\lambda^{2}a^{\dagger}a\sigma_{z}]\ket{\varphi_{n,\pm}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,u^{2}_{\pm}(n-\lambda^{2}n)+v^{2}_{\pm}(n+1+\lambda^{2}(n+2))\,.

(37)

As a result one has

\displaystyle\left<\hat{n}\right>_{BS,\pm}=\,n+\frac{1}{2}+\lambda^{2}\mp\left(\frac{1}{2}+\lambda^{2}(n+1)\right)\cos\theta_{n}\,.

(38)

For the ground state one finds
\left<\hat{n}\right>_{BS,0}=\lambda^{2}
.

III.3
Operator averages. Population inversion and number of excitations

More clearcut differences exist between the two models than suggested by the above
results, especially at resonance. We show this by performing the same comparison of expectation
values, this time for the population inversion operator
\sigma_{z}
and for the number of excitations

\hat{N}_{x}=\hat{n}+1/2(1+\sigma_{z})
.

For
H_{P}
the change of
\sigma_{z}
under the first unitary transform is obtained as in
the derivation of Eqs.(
10
),(
11
).

\displaystyle\sigma_{z,S}=

\displaystyle\,\cosh[2\,\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)]\,\sigma_{z}-\sinh[2\,\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)]\,i\sigma_{y}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\,\sigma_{z}+2\lambda^{2}(a^{\dagger}-a)^{2}\sigma_{z}-2\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)i\sigma_{y}\,.

(39)

The expression remains unchanged by the second transform, which commutes with
\sigma_{z}
.
The terms containing
\lambda
are left unchanged, as discussed in the previous cases.
Also, as before, the operators
a^{2},a^{\dagger\,2}
and the off-resonant ones

a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\dagger},a\sigma
do not contribute to the expectation value. We are left with

\sigma_{z,S,S^{\prime}}=\,\sigma_{z}+2\lambda(a^{\dagger}\sigma+a\sigma^{\dagger})-2\lambda^{2}(2a^{\dagger}a+1)\sigma_{z}\,,

(40)

which by straightforward calculations leads to

\displaystyle\left<\sigma_{z}\right>_{P,\pm}

\displaystyle=(u^{2}_{\pm}-v^{2}_{\pm})[1-2\lambda^{2}(2n+1)]

\displaystyle+\,4\lambda u_{\pm}v_{\pm}\sqrt{n+1}+4\lambda^{2}v^{2}\,.

(41)

Specifically for the two states one obtains

\displaystyle\left<\sigma_{z}\right>_{P,\pm}=

\displaystyle\,\pm\cos\theta_{n}[1-4\lambda^{2}(n+1)]

\displaystyle\pm

\displaystyle 2\lambda\sin\theta_{n}\sqrt{n+1}+2\lambda^{2}\,.

(42)

A negative value for
\cos\theta_{n}
hints to a higher population inversion on the lower state.
For the ground state one finds
\left<\sigma_{z}\right>_{P,0}=-1+2\lambda^{2}
.

Finally, we address the same problem for
H_{BS}
. The transform of
\sigma_{z}
under

\tilde{S}=\lambda(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\dagger}-a\sigma)
is complicated, but for the second order in
\lambda
the
quantities
[\tilde{S},\sigma_{z}]
and
[\tilde{S},[\tilde{S},\sigma_{z}]]
are sufficient. It is easy to check that

\displaystyle[\tilde{S},\sigma_{z}]=

\displaystyle\,-2\lambda(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\dagger}+a\sigma)\,,

\displaystyle[\tilde{S},a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\dagger}+a\sigma]=

\displaystyle\,2\lambda(a^{\dagger}a\sigma_{z}-\sigma\sigma^{\dagger})\,.

(43)

This leads to the result

\sigma_{z,\tilde{S}}=\sigma_{z}-2\lambda(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\dagger}+a\sigma)-2\lambda^{2}(a^{\dagger}a\sigma_{z}-\sigma\sigma^{\dagger})\,,

(44)

which remains unchanged under the second unitary transform. Thus

\displaystyle\left<\sigma_{z}\right>_{BS,\pm}=

\displaystyle\bra{\varphi_{n,\pm}}[\sigma_{z}-2\lambda^{2}(a^{\dagger}a\sigma_{z}-\sigma\sigma^{\dagger})]\ket{\varphi_{n,\pm}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(u_{\pm}^{2}-v_{\pm}^{2})(1-2\lambda^{2}n)+4\lambda^{2}v_{\pm}^{2}\,.

(45)

Specifically, for the two states one has

\displaystyle\left<\sigma_{z}\right>_{BS,\pm}=

\displaystyle\,\pm\cos\theta_{n}\,(1-2\lambda^{2}n)+2\lambda^{2}(1\mp\cos\theta_{n})\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\pm\cos\theta_{n}\,[1-2\lambda^{2}(n+1)]+2\lambda^{2}\,.

(46)

For the ground state one finds
\left<\sigma_{z}\right>_{BS,0}=-1+2\lambda^{2}
.

\hat{N}_{x}
is now easily done for both effective Hamiltonians by assembling
Eqs.(

N_{x}^{\pm}=\langle\varphi_{n,\pm}|{\hat{N}}_{x}|\varphi_{n,\pm}\rangle=n+1+2\lambda^{2}\mp 2\lambda^{2}(n+1)\cos\theta_{n}\,.

(47)

III.4
The generalized RWA effective Hamiltonian

A successful effective Hamiltonian suitable in the deep strong coupling (DSC) regime defined by
g/\omega\geqslant 1\,

RMP
was
proposed by Irish
Irish
. This so called generalized RWA (GRWA) Hamiltonian follows from
H_{R}
(see Eq.(
2
)) by the
IBM transform. The free qubit part of the Hamiltonian then couples all pairs of shifted oscillator states.

To be specific, the model uses a single canonical transform, generated by

S=\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)\sigma_{x}
, this time with
\lambda=g/\omega
. As a result one obtains, as
for Eq.(
11
)

\displaystyle H_{R,S}=\omega a^{\dagger}a-g^{2}/\omega

\displaystyle+\,\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\left\{\cosh[2\,\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)]\,\sigma_{z}-\sinh[2\,\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)]\,i\sigma_{y}\right\}\,.

(48)

It is lengthy but rather straighforward to expand the hyperbolic functions as
the even and odd part of the exponential (see Xie
et al.

Xie
but
beware of typos)

\displaystyle e^{2\,\lambda(a^{\dagger}-a)}

\displaystyle=f_{0}(\hat{n},\lambda)+

\displaystyle\sum_{q>0}\left[a^{\dagger\,q}f_{q}(\hat{n},\lambda)+(-1)^{q}f_{q}(\hat{n},\lambda)a^{q}\right]\,,

(49)

where the functions of
\hat{n}
are defined, as usual, by their action
on the elements of the Fock basis
f_{q}(\hat{n},\lambda)\ket{n}=f_{q}(n,\lambda)\ket{n}
.
They are related to the generalized Laguerre polynomials
L_{n}^{(q)}(x)
by

f_{q}(n,\lambda)=e^{-2\lambda^{2}}(2\lambda)^{q}\,\frac{n!}{(n+q)!}\,L^{(q)}_{n}(4\lambda^{2})\,.

(50)

This translates into the following expression for
H_{R,S}

\displaystyle H_{R,S}=\,

\displaystyle\omega\,a^{\dagger}a-g^{2}/\omega+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}f_{0}(\hat{n},\lambda)\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{q>0,\rm{even}}\,\left[a^{\dagger\,q}f_{q}(\hat{n},\lambda)+f_{q}(\hat{n},\lambda)a^{q}\right]\}\,\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{q>0,\rm{odd}}\,\left[a^{\dagger\,q}f_{q}(\hat{n},\lambda)-f_{q}(\hat{n},\lambda)a^{q}\right](\sigma-\sigma^{\dagger})\,.

(51)

No approximation is involved so far. The
f_{0}
term is diagonal, while terms with
higher
q
-values are further away from the diagonal in the Fock basis.

The idea of the GRWA is to keep, besides the diagonal terms, only the off-diagonal
ones with
q=1
and which, moreover, are also
retained in the RWA, as not counter-rotating. More precisely, one has

\displaystyle H_{{\rm GRWA}}=

\displaystyle\,\omega\,a^{\dagger}a-g^{2}/\omega+\,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,f_{0}(\hat{n},\lambda)\,\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,[a^{\dagger}\sigma f_{1}(\hat{n},\lambda)+f_{1}(\hat{n},\lambda)\,a\sigma^{\dagger}]\,,

(52)

which reduces the Hamiltonian to the familiar JC structure, renormalized by

\hat{n}
-dependent factors. Thus the problem becomes
2\times 2
block-diagonal and
therefore solvable.

Obviously, this approach is not a systematic expansion to
\lambda^{2}
. Indeed,
f_{q}\sim\lambda^{q}

and therefore terms with
f_{2}
should have been kept as second order contribution. Instead
they were discarded as off-resonant, as prescribed by the RWA argument.

IV
Conclusions

The effective Hamiltonian given by Eq.(
24
) was derived from the quantum Rabi model systematically
to second order in the small parameter
\lambda=g/(\varepsilon+\omega)
. No other approximations (like discarding
terms as off-resonant) are involved. In this sense the proposed model is purely perturbative.
The expansion parameter
\lambda
is the same as in the Bloch-Siegert theory. In contrast to the latter,
the present formalism recovers exactly the IBM Hamiltonian in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.
The proposed model allows analytical calculations of various operator averages
which are used for a detailed comparison with the predictions of the BS approach.

Even in the cases when the spectra of two models are close, we find that the main difference from the BS results
is seen in the structure of the dressed eigenstates. The source of this dissimilarity is traced back to the different
sign of the frequency shift induced by the qubit-field coupling. This is furher reflected in the expectation values
of relevant observables, like the excitation number, and their attribution to the appropriate eigenstates.

The energy spectrum and the dressed state structure associated to
the full quantum Rabi model are well approximated by the IBM-compatible effective Hamiltonian in the resonant
and off-resonant perturbative USC regime.
Our analysis shows that recovering the independent-boson model in the limit

\varepsilon\to 0
is a valuable test for effective Hamiltonian approaches to the quantum Rabi model.
