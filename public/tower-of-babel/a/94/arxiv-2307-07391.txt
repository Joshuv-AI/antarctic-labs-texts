On the irrationality of moduli spaces of projective hyperkähler manifolds
Daniele Agostini, Ignacio Barros, Kuan-Wen Lai

Abstract
The aim of this paper is to estimate the irrationality of moduli spaces of hyperkähler manifolds of types K3 [n] , Kum n , OG6, and OG10. We prove that the degrees of irrationality of these moduli spaces are bounded from above by a universal polynomial in the dimension and degree of the manifolds they parametrize. We also give a polynomial bound for the degrees of irrationality of moduli spaces of ( 1 , d ) (1,d) -polarized abelian surfaces.



1  Introduction

Perhaps the coarsest invariant measuring birational complexity is the Kodaira dimension and the computation of this invariant in the moduli context has been a guiding question in the past decades. A much finer, but also harder to compute, collection of invariants measuring birational complexity go by the name of
measures of irrationality
. One of them, called the
degree of irrationality
, is defined for a variety
X
as the minimal possible degree of a dominant rational map

X\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\dim(X)}.

This invariant, denoted as
\operatorname{irr}(X)
, was first introduced in
[
33
]
and received a revived attention after
[
5
]
. Notice that
\operatorname{irr}\left(X\right)=1
if and only if
X
is rational. In this sense,
\operatorname{irr}\left(X\right)
measures how far is
X
from being rational. Deciding whether a variety
X
is rational is a famously hard problem in algebraic geometry, suggesting that the first approach to study
\operatorname{irr}(X)
is to find bounds.

In the moduli context, Donagi proposed to find bounds on measures of irrationality for classical moduli spaces such as those of curves
\mathcal{M}_{g}
and principally polarized abelian varieties
\mathcal{A}_{g}
; see
[
5
]
*Problem 4.4. These spaces are of general type when
g
is large enough
[
47
,
22
,
36
]
, so their degrees of irrationality are at least
2
for large
g
. To the best of our knowledge, there is no known upper bound on their degrees of irrationality.

In this paper, we continue our study on the irrationality of various modular varieties initiated in
[
1
]
. Our main objects of study are moduli spaces of
(1,d)
-polarized abelian surfaces
\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}
and moduli spaces of projective hyperkähler manifolds
\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda,2d}^{\gamma}
of known deformation types. Similar to moduli spaces of curves and principally polarized abelian varieties, components of
\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda,2d}^{\gamma}
become of general type when certain invariants grow; see
[
41
,
16
,
45
,
14
]
for the former and
[
15
,
17
,
18
,
30
,
3
]
for the latter. Our first main result gives bounds for degrees of irrationality of
\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
independent of
d
such that

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{8+\varepsilon}.

Further, for special series of
d
we have the following bounds:

•

If
d
is squarefree, then

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{4+\varepsilon}.

•

If
d
is a perfect square, then

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{2+\varepsilon}.

•

Fix
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
which satisfy
4ac-b^{2}<0
. Suppose that
d
is squarefree and of the form
d=aX^{2}-bXY+cY^{2}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}=C_{\varepsilon}(a,b,c)>0
independent of
d
such that

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{2+\varepsilon}.

Our second main result concerns moduli spaces of projective hyperkähler manifolds. The first examples of such manifolds, introduced in
[
6
]
, are Hilbert schemes of points on K3 surfaces and generalized Kummer varieties. A hyperkähler manifold
X
is said to be of K3
[n]
-type or Kum
n
-type if it is deformation equivalent respectively to the former or the latter. In addition, there are two sporadic examples in dimensions
10
and
6
constructed in
[
42
,
43
]
. In this case,
X
is of OG10 and OG6-type respectively. Up to now, every known projective hyperkähler manifold is one of these four types.

For a projective hyperkähler manifold
X
, its second cohomology group
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
carries a bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)
which turns it into a lattice known as the
Beauville–Bogomolov–Fujiki lattice
. If the manifold
X
is of one of the known deformation types, then this lattice is isomorphic to one of the lattices
\Lambda_{\mathrm{K3}^{[n]}}
,
\Lambda_{\mathrm{Kum}_{n}}
,
\Lambda_{\mathrm{OG10}}
,
\Lambda_{\mathrm{OG6}}
. If
\Lambda
is one of these lattices, we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda,2d}^{\gamma}
the moduli space of pairs
(X,H)
, where
X
is a projective hyperkähler manifold with
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong\Lambda
, and
H
is a primitive polarization on
X
of degree
(c_{1}(H),c_{1}(H))=2d
and divisibility
\gamma
(recall that the
divisibility
of
x\in\Lambda
is the positive generator of the ideal
(x,\Lambda)\subset\mathbb{Z}
). Note that the dimension of
X
, which equals
2n
for some integer
n
, can be read off
\Lambda
. The existence of such moduli spaces follows from
[
49
]
and their irreducible components are birational to orthogonal modular varieties due to the Torelli theorem
[
48
]
(see also
[
31
]
). This lays the foundation for our second main result about their degrees of irrationality.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that, for every irreducible component
Y\subset\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda,2d}^{\gamma}
, it holds that

\operatorname{irr}(Y)\leq C\cdot(n\cdot d)^{19}.

If we consider only
Kum
n
-type hyperkähler manifolds, then this bound can be refined as

\operatorname{irr}(Y)\leq C\cdot(n\cdot d)^{11}.

Furthermore, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}
such that for every irreducible component
Y\subset\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda,2d}^{\gamma}
, it holds that

•

\operatorname{irr}(Y)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{14+\varepsilon}
if we consider only
OG10
-type hyperkähler manifolds,

•

\operatorname{irr}(Y)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{6+\varepsilon}
if we consider only
OG6
-type hyperkähler manifolds.

There are special series of
n
and
d
for which one can considerably improve the bound; see Theorems
3.4
,
3.9
,
3.12
, and
3.17
.

Our approach is the same as in
[
1
]
but with several additional challenges. First of all, the Torelli theorem states that every irreducible component
Y\subset\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda,2d}^{\gamma}
admits an open embedding into an orthogonal modular variety associated with an even lattice
M
of signature
(2,m)
for some
m>0
. Hence the degree of irrationality of
Y
coincides with that of
\Omega(M)\big/\Gamma
, where
\Omega(M)
is the period domain of
M
and
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{O}^{+}(M)
is an arithmetic group.
Here
M
and
\Gamma
depend on the deformation type, the degree, the divisibility, as well as on the irreducible component
Y
. In each setting, this gives a collection of lattices
\Lambda_{n,d}
of signature
(2,m)
and arithmetic groups
\Gamma_{n,d}\subset\mathrm{O}^{+}\left(\Lambda_{n,d}\right)
.

Assume that there exists an even lattice
\Lambda_{\#}
of signature
(2,m^{\prime})
independent of
n,d
and embeddings
\Lambda_{n,d}\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
for all
n,d
. Assume further that each
\Gamma_{n,d}
is
extendable
, that is, each
g\in\Gamma_{n,d}
can be extended as an isometry
g_{\#}\in\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda_{\#})
preserving
\Lambda_{n,d}
whose restriction to
\Lambda_{n,d}
recovers
g
. These assumptions induce morphisms of quasiprojective varieties

\quad\mathcal{P}_{n,d}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Omega(\Lambda_{n,d})\big/\Gamma_{n,d}\overset{f_{n,d}}{\longrightarrow}\Omega(\Lambda_{\#})\big/\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda_{\#})=:\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}

(1.1)

of finite degree onto their images
Z_{n,d}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}
.
In particular, there is the immediate inequality

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{P}_{n,d}\right)\leq\deg(f_{n,d})\cdot\operatorname{irr}\left(Z_{n,d}\right).

The cycles
[Z_{n,d}]

are examples of
special cycles
(also referred to as
Kudla cycles
). They can be arranged in a generating series which turns out to be the Fourier expansion of a modular form. More precisely, if we fix an embedding

\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N}

and let
\overline{Z}_{n,d}
be the closure of
Z_{n,d}
in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
, then Kudla’s modularity conjecture
[
27
,
28
]
, proved in
[
28
,
50
,
10
]
, implies that the integers
\deg\left(\overline{Z}_{n,d}\right)
are coefficients of the Fourier expansion of a Siegel modular form of weight
\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{rk}(\Lambda_{\#})
. By taking a projection onto a general linear subspace
\mathbb{P}^{\dim(Z_{n,d})}\subset\mathbb{P}^{N}
, one can conclude that
\operatorname{irr}(Z_{n,d})\leq\deg(\overline{Z}_{n,d})
and this can be estimated via
standard bounds on the growth of coefficients of Siegel modular forms.

There are two main challenges: the first one is to find an appropriate
\Lambda_{\#}
together with embeddings
\Lambda_{n,d}\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
such that the
\Gamma_{n,d}
are extendable. The second one is to bound
\deg(f_{n,d})
. The general strategy was developed in
[
1
]
and the bulk of the paper is devoted to overcoming these two challenges.

Outline

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we set up notations and give bounds on degrees of irrationality for special cycles. Then we establish bounds on degrees of maps onto their images between orthogonal modular varieties induced by lattice embeddings. In Section
3
, we apply the results in the previous section to study the irrationality of moduli spaces of projective hyperkähler varieties of known deformation types. Finally in Section
4
, we study the irrationality of the moduli space of
(1,d)
-polarized abelian surfaces and also revisit the case of K3 surfaces.

2  Period spaces, special cycles, and irrationality

The aim of this section is to introduce some upper bounds on the degrees of irrationality of period spaces to be needed in later sections. The lemmas and notations established along the way will also be used later.

2.1  Degrees of irrationality of special cycles

Let us start by reviewing the main results in
[
1
]
about degrees of irrationality of special cycles on orthogonal Shimura varieties. Let
M
be an even lattice of signature
(2,m)
for some
m>0
(the assumption of
[
1
]
that
m
is even was made for simplicity and is not necessary) and identify the dual lattice
M^{\vee}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\operatorname{Hom}(M,\mathbb{Z})
as the space of vectors
v\in M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
satisfying
(v,M)\subset\mathbb{Z}
. The discriminant group of
M
will be denoted as
D(M)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}M^{\vee}/M
; recall that its order is equal to the absolute value of the discriminant
\operatorname{disc}(M)
. Let us further define
\mathrm{O}(M)
to be the group of isometries of
M
. Then the isometries which preserve the orientation of one (and thus all) positive
2
-plane in
M\otimes\mathbb{R}
form an index two subgroup
\mathrm{O}^{+}(M)\subset\mathrm{O}(M)
.

The period domain
\Omega(M)
is defined as one of the two components of

\left\{[w]\in\mathbb{P}(M\otimes\mathbb{C})\;\middle|\;(w,w)=0,\;(w,\overline{w})>0\right\}.

We consider this together with natural action of a finite index subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{O}^{+}(M)
. Then, the
period space

\mathcal{P}_{M}(\Gamma)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Omega(M)/\Gamma

is an orthogonal Shimura variety that is quasi-projective, cf.
[
2
]
. More precisely, the restriction of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(M\otimes\mathbb{C})}(-1)
to
\Omega(M)
descends to a
\mathbb{Q}
-line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
\mathcal{P}_{M}(\Gamma)
such that the space
H^{0}(\mathcal{L}^{\otimes k})
, where
k>1
is such that
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes k}
is a line bundle, can be identified with the space
\mathrm{Mod}_{k}(\Gamma,1)
of modular forms for
\Gamma
of weight
k
and trivial character. A projective model of
\mathcal{P}_{M}(\Gamma)
is then given by the Baily–Borel compactification

\overline{\mathcal{P}_{M}(\Gamma)}^{BB}=\mathrm{Proj}\left(\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}H^{0}(\mathcal{L}^{\otimes k})\right)=\mathrm{Proj}\left(\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{Mod}_{k}(\Gamma,1)\right).

It is normal and the complement of
\mathcal{P}_{M}(\Gamma)
has dimension at most one.

For every
t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
and
\gamma\in D(M)
, we consider the formal sum of hyperplane sections

\sum_{\frac{1}{2}(v,v)=t,\;v\equiv\gamma}v^{\perp}\;\subset\;\Omega(M)

where the sum runs through all
v\in M^{\vee}
and

v^{\perp}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\left\{[w]\in\Omega\left(M\right)\;\middle|\;(w,v)=0\right\}.

Under the map
\Omega\left(M\right)\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}_{M}\left(\Gamma\right)
, the formal sum descends to a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier divisor
Y_{t,\gamma}\subset\mathcal{P}_{M}(\Gamma)
called a
Heegner divisor
. Let us fix a projective embedding
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{M}(\Gamma)}^{BB}\subset\mathbb{P}
and let
\overline{Y}_{t,\gamma}\subset\overline{\mathcal{P}_{M}(\Gamma)}^{BB}
be the closure of
Y_{t,\gamma}
. By fundamental results of Kudla–Millson
[
26
]
and Borcherds
[
8
]
, for any
\gamma
the series

\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}}{\rm{deg}}\left(\overline{Y}_{t,\gamma}\right)q^{t}

is the Fourier expansion of a scalar-valued modular form of weight
\frac{m}{2}+1
, see also
[
11
]
for details. The growth of the Fourier coefficients of such a series are bounded by
C\cdot t^{\frac{m}{2}}
for some positive constant
C>0
. Since the degree of irrationality of a variety is at most the degree of the variety under a projective embedding, the same argument as in the proof of
[
1
]
*Theorem 2.6 yields:

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume
m\geq 3
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
and
\gamma\in D(M)
, it holds that

{\rm{irr}}\left(\overline{Y}_{t,\gamma}\right)\leq C\cdot t^{\frac{m}{2}}.

As a higher codimensional analogue, for each
r
-tuple

\underline{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{r})\in\left(M^{\vee}\right)^{\oplus r},

one can consider the linear subspace
\langle\underline{v}\rangle\subset\mathbb{P}(M\otimes\mathbb{C})
spanned by
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
and the moment matrix
Q(\underline{v})=\frac{1}{2}((v_{i},v_{j}))
. Fix a semi-positive symmetric matrix
T\in{\rm{Sym}}_{r}\left(\mathbb{Q}\right)_{\geq 0}
and an
r
-tuple of classes in the discriminant group
\underline{\gamma}\in D(M)^{\oplus r}
. Then the formal sum

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q(\underline{v})=T,\;\underline{v}\equiv\underline{\gamma}\end{subarray}}\langle\underline{v}\rangle^{\perp}\subset\Omega\left(M\right),

which runs through all

\underline{v}\in\left(M^{\vee}\right)^{\oplus r},

descends to a cycle
Z_{T,\underline{\gamma}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{M}\left(\Gamma\right)
. Borcherds’ result on Heegner divisors has a generalization known as Kudla’s modularity conjecture, which is proved in a series of works
[
27
,
28
,
50
,
10
]
. Using this result, we obtained in
[
1
]
*Theorem 6.2 the following bound.

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that
1\leq r\leq m-2
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
T\in{\rm{Sym}}_{r}(\mathbb{Q})_{>0}
and
\underline{\gamma}\in D(M)^{\oplus r}
, it holds that

{\rm{irr}}\left(Z_{T,\underline{\gamma}}\right)\leq C\cdot{\rm{det}}\left(T\right)^{1+\frac{m}{2}}.

2.2  Morphisms induced by lattice embeddings

Let
\Lambda
and
\Lambda_{\#}
be even lattices of signatures
(2,m)
and
(2,m^{\prime})
with
3\leq m\leq m^{\prime}
. Suppose there is an embedding
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
, not necessarily primitive. We say that a finite index subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda)
is
extendable
with respect to this embedding if for every
g\in\Gamma
, there exists
g_{\#}\in\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda_{\#})
such that
g_{\#}(\Lambda)\subset\Lambda
and
g_{\#}|_{\Lambda}=g
. In this setting, we have a natural map

\textstyle{\psi_{\Gamma}\colon\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)=\Omega(\Lambda)/\Gamma\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{{\mathcal{P}}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Omega(\Lambda_{\#})/{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{\#})}

(2.1)

with finite fibers. This map is actually a morphism of algebraic varieties due to Borel’s extension theorem
[
9
]
*Theorem 3.10.

In the following, we are going to bound the degree of
\psi_{\Gamma}
onto its image. To do so we consider the
stable orthogonal group

\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}(\Lambda)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\left\{g\in\mathrm{O}(\Lambda)\;\middle|\;g\text{ acts trivially on }D(\Lambda)\right\}.

and define

\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda)\cap\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}(\Lambda).

Note that this is a finite index subgroup of
\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

There exists an injection

\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)\hookrightarrow\widetilde{{\mathrm{O}}}^{+}(\Lambda_{\#})

given by extending
g\in\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)
to
\Lambda_{\#}
as the identity on
\Lambda^{\perp\Lambda_{\#}}
. In particular, the group
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)
is extendable with respect to the embedding
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
.

Proof.

By the proof of
[
19
]
*Lemma 7.1, one can embed
{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)
into
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}(\Lambda_{\#})
by extending
g\in\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)
to
\Lambda_{\#}
as the identity on
\Lambda^{\perp\Lambda_{\#}}
. Note that a positive
2
-plane in
\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{R}
corresponds to a positive
2
-plane in
\Lambda_{\#}\otimes\mathbb{R}
. Hence the extension of
g
is orientation-preserving, that is, it lies in
\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda_{\#})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma
2.3
allows us to insert
\Gamma=\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)
into
\psi_{\Gamma}
, which defines a morphism

\textstyle{\widetilde{\psi}^{+}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{+}_{\Lambda}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Omega(\Lambda)/\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{{\mathcal{P}}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}=\Omega(\Lambda_{\#})/{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{\#}).}

Our first step is to bound the degree of this map onto its image assuming
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
is a primitive embedding. Recall that the points of
\Omega(\Lambda)
correspond to
Hodge structures of K3 type
on the lattice
\Lambda
. For each
[v]\in\Omega(\Lambda)
, the
transcendental lattice

T(v)\subset\Lambda
is the minimal primitive sub-Hodge structure (in particular
T(v)\subset\Lambda
primitive) of the Hodge structure induced by
[v]
with
(T(v)\otimes\mathbb{C})^{2,0}=\mathbb{C}v
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For a very general
[v]\in\Omega(\Lambda)
, we have
T(v)=\Lambda
.

Proof.

For a very general
[v]\in\Omega(\Lambda)
, the hyperplane section
v^{\perp}\subset\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{C}
contains no element from
\Lambda
because
\Lambda
consists of countably many points. In this situation, the Hodge structure on
\Lambda
determined by
[v]
satisfies
\Lambda^{1,1}=\{0\}
, whence
T(v)=(\Lambda^{1,1})^{\perp}=\Lambda
.
∎

The following lemma shows that transcendental lattices are unchanged under primitive embeddings of lattices.

Lemma 2.5
.

Consider a primitive embedding
\iota\colon\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
. For every
[v]\in\Omega(\Lambda)
, we have

\iota(T(v))=T(\iota(v)).

That is, the transcendental lattice on
\Lambda
determined by
[v]
is mapped isomorphically onto the transcendental lattice on
\Lambda_{\#}
determined by
[\iota(v)]
.

Proof.

The Hodge substructure
\iota(T(v))\subset\Lambda_{\#}
has
(2,0)
-part spanned by
\iota(v)
. This implies that

\iota(T(v))\supset T(\iota(v))

due to the minimality of
T(\iota(v))
. Now,
T(\iota(v))
appears as a Hodge substructure of
\iota(\Lambda)
with
(2,0)
-part spanned by
\iota(v)
, so the containment is an equality due to the minimality of
\iota(T(v))
.
∎

The next lemma gives a slight extension of
[
40
]
*Lemma 4.3.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose that
[v_{1}],[v_{2}]\in\Omega(\Lambda)
have the same image under
\widetilde{\psi}^{+}
. Then there exists
g\in{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{\#})
such that there is an identification
g(T(v_{1}))=T(v_{2})
of Hodge structures.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
, we can view
T(v_{1})
(resp.
T(v_{2})
) as the transcendental lattice of the Hodge structure on
\Lambda_{\#}
defined by
v_{1}
(resp.
v_{2}
). By hypothesis, there exists
g\in{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{\#})
such that
g([v_{1}])=[v_{2}]
, so it induces an isomorphism between the Hodge structures on
\Lambda_{\#}
defined by
[v_{1}]
and
[v_{2}]
. Thus
g(T(v_{1}))=T(v_{2})
as they are minimal Hodge substructures associated to
g([v_{1}])=[v_{2}]
.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose that
[v_{1}],[v_{2}]\in\Omega(\Lambda)
are very general points such that there are identities
T(v_{1})=T(v_{2})=\Lambda
as lattices, and assume that they are mapped to the same point under
\widetilde{\psi}^{+}
. Then there exists
g\in\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda)
such that
g([v_{1}])=[v_{2}]
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.6
, there exists
g\in\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{\#})
such that
g(T(v_{1}))=T(v_{2})
as Hodge structures (so that
g([v_{1}])=[v_{2}]
). The hypothesis
T(v_{1})=T(v_{2})=\Lambda
implies that the sublattice
\Lambda\subset\Lambda_{\#}
is preserved by
g
, which gives an element
g|_{\Lambda}\in\mathrm{O}(\Lambda)
. We need to check that
g|_{\Lambda}\in\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda)
. Since it maps
[v_{1}]
to
[v_{2}]
, it takes the positive
2
-plane in
\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{R}
spanned by
\mathrm{Re}(v_{1}),\mathrm{Im}(v_{1})
to the positive
2
-plane spanned by
\mathrm{Re}(v_{2}),\mathrm{Im}(v_{2})
, and preserves their natural orientations. Hence
g|_{\Lambda}\in\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda)
. ∎

Lemma 2.8
.

When the embedding
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
is primitive, the degree of
\widetilde{\psi}^{+}
onto its image is less than or equal to
|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda))|
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.7
, a very general fiber of
\widetilde{\psi}^{+}
is contained in an orbit of
\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda)
acting on
\Omega(\Lambda)/\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)
. Such an orbit has cardinality at most
|\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda)/\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)|
, which is in turn less or equal to
|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda))|
, so the statement follows.
∎

We want to extend the previous lemma to the situation when
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
is not necessarily primitive. In this case, we let
\Lambda_{s}\subset\Lambda_{\#}
be the saturation of
\Lambda
. This is again an even lattice of signature
(2,n)
and the embedding
\Lambda_{s}\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
is primitive.

Lemma 2.9
.

When the embedding
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
is not necessarily primitive, the degree of
\widetilde{\psi}^{+}
onto its image is less than or equal to
[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s}):\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\cdot|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{s}))|
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
2.3
, there are injections

\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)\hookrightarrow\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s})\hookrightarrow\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{\#}).

This induces maps

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{+}_{\Lambda}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{+}_{\Lambda_{s}}\to\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}

whose composition gives
\widetilde{\psi}^{+}
. The first map has fibers of cardinality at most
[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s}):\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]
; the second map has degree onto its image bounded by
|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{s}))|
due to Lemma
2.8
. This proves the claim.
∎

We can finally give the bound for the map
\psi_{\Gamma}
in (
2.1
).

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
be an embedding and
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda)
be any extendable subgroup of finite index. Let also
\Lambda_{s}
be the saturation of
\Lambda
in
\Lambda_{\#}
. Then the degree of
\psi_{\Gamma}
onto its image is less than or equal to

[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda):\Gamma\cap\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\cdot[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s}):\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\cdot|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{s}))|.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{\Gamma}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Gamma\cap\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)
. Then the map

\psi_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}\colon\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda}(\widetilde{\Gamma})\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}

factors as

\textstyle{\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda}(\widetilde{\Gamma})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\psi_{\Gamma}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}.}

Therefore, the degree of
\psi_{\Gamma}
onto its image is bounded by that of
\psi_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}
. On the other hand,
\psi_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}
also factors as

\textstyle{\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda}(\widetilde{\Gamma})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{+}_{\Lambda}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\widetilde{\psi}^{+}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}.}

The first arrow is surjective with degree
\leq[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda):\widetilde{\Gamma}]
. By Lemma
2.9
, the degree of
\widetilde{\psi}^{+}
onto its image is bounded by
[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s}):\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\cdot|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{s}))|
. Hence the claim follows.
∎

In order to apply the above lemma, we need to bound each factor. For a lattice
M
denote by
\ell(M)
the minimal number of generators of the discriminant group
D(M)
. Then an element in
\mathrm{O}(D(M))
is determined by the images of the generators. Hence there is a bound

|\mathrm{O}(D(M))|\leq|\operatorname{Aut}(D(M))|\leq|\operatorname{disc}(M)|^{\ell(M)}.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
be a not necessarily primitive embedding and
\Lambda_{s}\subset\Lambda_{\#}
be the saturation of
\Lambda
. Then we have

|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{s}))|\leq|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|^{\ell(\Lambda)}.

Proof.

There is a chain of finite index embeddings

\Lambda\subset\Lambda_{s}\subset\Lambda_{s}^{\vee}\subset\Lambda^{\vee}.

In particular, we have equalities

D(\Lambda_{s})=\Lambda_{s}^{\vee}/\Lambda_{s}=(\Lambda_{s}^{\vee}/\Lambda)/(\Lambda_{s}/\Lambda),

which is a quotient of
\Lambda_{s}^{\vee}/\Lambda
by a finite index subgroup. Notice also that
\Lambda_{s}^{\vee}/\Lambda\subset\Lambda^{\vee}/\Lambda=D(\Lambda)
. These facts imply

\ell(\Lambda_{s})\leq\ell(\Lambda)
and
\mathrm{disc}(\Lambda_{s})\leq\mathrm{disc}(\Lambda)
. Combining these inequalities gives

|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{s}))|\leq|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda_{s})|^{\ell(\Lambda_{s})}\leq|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|^{\ell(\Lambda)},

as desired.
∎

Let us bound another term in Lemma
2.10
.

Lemma 2.12
.

It holds that

[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s}):\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\leq[\Lambda_{s}:\Lambda]^{\operatorname{rk}(\Lambda)}\cdot|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda))|.

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{O}(\Lambda_{s},\Lambda)
be the group of isometries
g\in\mathrm{O}(\Lambda_{s})
such that
g(\Lambda)\subset\Lambda
. Then

[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s}):\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]=[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s}):\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s})\cap\mathrm{O}(\Lambda_{s},\Lambda)]\cdot[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s})\cap\mathrm{O}(\Lambda_{s},\Lambda):\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)].

By construction,
\mathrm{O}(\Lambda_{s},\Lambda)
can be seen as a subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(\Lambda)
, so the second factor on the right hand side of the above equality is bounded by
[\mathrm{O}(\Lambda):\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\leq|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda))|
. For the first factor, observe that for every
g\in\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s})
the image
g(\Lambda)
is a sublattice of
\Lambda_{s}
of index
[\Lambda_{s}:\Lambda]
; furthermore,
g(\Lambda)=\Lambda
if and only if
g\in\mathrm{O}(\Lambda_{s},\Lambda)
. This means that
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s})
acts on the set
\Sigma
of sublattices of
\Lambda_{s}
of index equal to
[\Lambda_{s}:\Lambda]
and with
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s})\cap\mathrm{O}(\Lambda_{s},\Lambda)
as the stabilizer of
\Lambda
. Thus
[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s}):\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s})\cap\mathrm{O}(\Lambda_{s},\Lambda)]\leq|\Sigma|\leq[\Lambda_{s}:\Lambda]^{\operatorname{rk}(\Lambda)}
, where the second inequality comes from
[
51
]
*Remark 3.4.
∎

2.3  An even positive lattice of rank two

There is a special lattice that will play an important role in our application, so we collect below some useful facts about it. Let
a,d
be positive integers such that
a+d=4t
for some integer
t
. Then the lattice is given by

Q_{(a,d)}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{pmatrix}2a&-a\\
-a&2t\end{pmatrix}.

(2.2)

Denote by
z_{1},z_{2}\in Q_{(a,d)}
the basis vectors with respect to the above matrix. Consider the reflection isometry along
z_{1}
given by

\sigma_{z_{1}}\colon Q_{(a,d)}\longrightarrow Q_{(a,d)}\colon x\longmapsto x-2\frac{(x,z_{1})}{(z_{1},z_{1})}z_{1}.

Our goal is to find an embedding of
Q_{(a,d)}
into certain lattices that are defined independently of
a
and
d
such that
\sigma_{z_{1}}
is extendable.

In the following, we will consider the sublattice

W=\left<w_{1},w_{2}\right>\subset Q_{(a,d)}\quad\text{where}\quad w_{1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}z_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad w_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}2z_{2}+z_{1}.

Notice that
z_{1}=w_{1}
and
z_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(w_{2}-w_{1})
, so
W
has index two in
Q_{(a,d)}
. Moreover, we have
w_{1}^{2}=2a
,
w_{2}^{2}=2d
, and
(w_{1},w_{2})=0
, so
W
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}(2a)\oplus\mathbb{Z}(2d)
. Our strategy for finding a desired embedding for
Q_{(a,d)}
starts with finding an embedding for
W
with nice properties. We will need Lagrange’s four square theorem, which states that any nonnegative integer
a
can be written as the sum of four squares,
a=a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}+a_{4}^{2}
. It turns out that
a
can be written as the sum of four coprime squares if and only if
8
does not divide
a
: see
[
13
]
*Theorem 1 and also
[
29
]
.

Lemma 2.13
.

Suppose that both
a
and
d
are not divisible by
4
. Then there exists a primitive embedding
Q_{(a,d)}\hookrightarrow E_{8}
such that
\sigma_{z_{1}}
is extendable.

Proof.

Because
a
and
d
are not divisible by
4
, by
[
13
]
*Theorem 1 each of them can be written as a sum of four coprime squares:

a=a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}+a_{4}^{2}

and

d=b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}+b_{3}^{2}+b_{4}^{2}.

The hypothesis also implies that the
a_{i}
(resp. the
b_{i}
) cannot be all even or all odd. We claim that, upon rearranging the indices, we can assume
b_{i}\pm a_{i}
is odd for all
i
. Indeed, we can write
a+d=4t
as

\sum_{i=1}^{4}a_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{4}b_{j}^{2}\equiv 0\mod 4.

A direct check shows that the number of even
a_{i}
is the same as the number of odd
b_{i}
. Hence, upon rearranging the indices, we can assume that
a_{i}
is even if and only if
b_{i}
is odd for each
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, which implies that
b_{i}\pm a_{i}
is odd for each
i
.

Consider now the vector space
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{8}\mathbb{Q}e_{i}
with the standard Euclidean product. Then the lattice
E_{8}
can be realized as the sublattice in
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{8}\mathbb{Q}e_{i}
whose coordinates are either all integers or all half integers such that the sum of all coordinates is even. There is an embedding
W\hookrightarrow E_{8}
given by

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle=a_{1}(e_{1}-e_{5})+a_{2}(e_{2}-e_{6})+a_{3}(e_{3}-e_{7})+a_{4}(e_{4}-e_{8}),

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle=b_{1}(e_{1}+e_{5})+b_{2}(e_{2}+e_{6})+b_{3}(e_{3}+e_{7})+b_{4}(e_{4}+e_{8}).

Now we have

z_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(w_{2}-w_{1}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{b_{i}-a_{i}}{2}e_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{b_{i}+a_{i}}{2}e_{i+4}

which belongs to
E_{8}
since the
b_{i}\pm a_{i}
are all odd. Hence the embedding extends to an embedding
Q_{(a,d)}\hookrightarrow E_{8}
. To check that
\sigma_{z_{1}}
extends, consider the involution
\sigma\colon E_{8}\to E_{8}
defined by
\sigma(e_{i})=e_{4+i}
for
i=1,2,3,4
. Then
\sigma(w_{1})=-w_{1}=\sigma_{z_{1}}(w_{1})
and
\sigma(w_{2})=w_{2}=\sigma_{z_{1}}(w_{2})
, so that
\sigma(z_{1})=-z_{1}=\sigma_{z_{1}}(z_{1})
and
\sigma(z_{2})=z_{2}+z_{1}=\sigma_{z_{1}}(z_{1})
. This shows that
\sigma
is an extension of
\sigma_{z_{1}}
.

Let us prove that the embedding
Q_{(a,d)}\hookrightarrow E_{8}
is primitive. Every
x\in E_{8}\cap(Q_{(a,d)}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
can be written as

x=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\left(n_{i}+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)e_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}\left(n_{i+4}+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)e_{i+4}=\frac{A}{B}z_{1}+\frac{C}{D}z_{2}

where
n_{i},n_{i+4}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\delta\in\{0,1\}
,
A,B,C,D\in\mathbb{Z}
with
A,B
coprime and
C,D
coprime. Expanding
z_{1},z_{2}
in
e_{1},\dots,e_{8}
and comparing the coefficients gives

n_{i}+\frac{\delta}{2}=\frac{A}{B}a_{i}+\frac{C}{2D}(b_{i}-a_{i}),\quad n_{i+4}+\frac{\delta}{2}=-\frac{A}{B}a_{i}+\frac{C}{2D}(b_{i}+a_{i}),\quad\text{ for }i=1,\dots,4

which implies

n_{i}+n_{i+4}+\delta=\frac{C}{D}b_{i},\quad n_{i}-n_{i+4}=\left(\frac{2A}{B}-\frac{C}{D}\right)a_{i},\quad\text{ for }i=1,\dots,4.

(2.3)

The first set of equations in (
2.3
) implies that
D
divides all the
b_{i}
. Since the
b_{i}
are coprime, we get
D=1
. The second set of equations shows that
B
divides
2a_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,4
. Since the
a_{i}
are coprime, we conclude that
B=1
or
B=2
. If
B=1
, then
x\in Q_{(a,d)}
and we are done. If
B=2
, then
A
is odd. In this case, adding the two sets of equations in (
2.3
) together modulo
2
gives

\delta\equiv C(b_{i}-a_{i})+a_{i}\equiv C+a_{i}\mod 2

where the second equality holds as
b_{i}-a_{i}
are all odd. Hence
a_{i}\equiv\delta-C\mod 2
, so the
a_{i}
are all even or all odd, which is a contradiction. Therefore, the embedding
Q_{(a,d)}\hookrightarrow E_{8}
is primitive. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.14
.

Suppose that one, and hence both, of
a
and
d
are divisible by
4
. Then there exists an embedding
Q_{(a,d)}\hookrightarrow A_{1}^{\oplus 10}
such that
\sigma_{z_{1}}
is extendable. Moreover, if
Q_{(a,d),s}
is the saturation, then
[Q_{(a,d),s}:Q_{(a,d)}]=2
.

Proof.

Let us write
\frac{a}{4}-1=a_{1}^{2}+\dots+a_{4}^{2}
and
\frac{d}{4}-1=b_{1}^{2}+\dots+b_{4}^{2}
using Lagrange’s four square theorem. Then there is an embedding
W\hookrightarrow A_{1}^{\oplus 10}
defined by

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle=2a_{1}e_{1}+2a_{2}e_{2}+2a_{3}e_{3}+2a_{4}e_{4}+2e_{5},

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle=2b_{1}e_{6}+2b_{2}e_{7}+2b_{3}e_{8}+2b_{4}e_{9}+2e_{10},

where
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{10}\}
is the canonical basis for
A_{1}^{\oplus 10}
. Since
w_{2}-w_{1}
is divisible by
2
, this extends to an embedding of
Q_{(a,d)}
by taking
z_{1}=w_{1}
and
z_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(w_{2}-w_{1})
. The reflection
\sigma_{z_{1}}=\sigma_{w_{1}}
extends to the involution
\sigma\colon A_{1}^{\oplus 10}\to A_{1}^{\oplus 10}
defined by
\sigma(e_{i})=-e_{i}
and
\sigma(e_{5+i})=e_{5+i}
for
i=1,\dots,5
. Finally, one can verify that the saturation is given by
Q_{(a,d),s}=\left<\frac{1}{2}z_{1},z_{2}\right>
. Indeed, if
p,q\in\mathbb{Q}
are such that

p\left(\frac{1}{2}z_{1}\right)+qz_{2}\in A_{1}^{\oplus 10}
, then the coefficient of
e_{10}
is
q
, showing that
q\in\mathbb{Z}
, and the coefficient of
e_{5}
is
q-p
, showing that
p\in\mathbb{Z}
. This proves the last assertion.
∎

Now consider the lattice
\mathbb{Z}(2a)\oplus\mathbb{Z}(2d)
with canonical basis
\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
and let
\sigma_{z_{1}}
denote the reflection along
z_{1}
. With a similar strategy, we can find an embedding for this lattice independent of
a
and
d
such that
\sigma_{z_{1}}
extends.

Lemma 2.15
.

There exists a primitive embedding
\mathbb{Z}(2a)\oplus\mathbb{Z}(2d)\hookrightarrow A_{1}^{\oplus 10}
such that
\sigma_{z_{1}}
extends. When
8\nmid a
and
8\nmid d
, there exists a primitive embedding
\mathbb{Z}(2a)\oplus\mathbb{Z}(2d)\hookrightarrow A_{1}^{\oplus 8}
such that
\sigma_{z_{1}}
extends

Proof.

Write
a-1
and
d-1
as sums of four squares so that
a=a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}+a_{4}^{2}+1
and
d=b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}+b_{3}^{2}+b_{4}^{2}+1
. Then the embedding of
\mathbb{Z}(2a)\oplus\mathbb{Z}(2d)
into
A_{1}^{\oplus 10}
is defined by

z_{1}=a_{1}e_{1}+\dots+a_{4}e_{4}+e_{5},\qquad z_{2}=b_{1}e_{5}+\dots+b_{4}e_{9}+e_{10}.

One can check directly that this is a primitive embedding. Moreover,
\sigma_{z_{1}}
extends to the involution
\sigma\colon A_{1}^{\oplus 10}\to A_{1}^{\oplus 10}
defined by
\sigma(e_{i})=-e_{i}
and
\sigma(e_{5+i})=e_{5+i}
for
i=1,\dots,5
.

If
8
divides neither
a
nor
d
, we can write them as the sum of four coprime squares
a=a_{1}^{2}+\dots+a_{4}^{2}
and
d=b_{1}^{2}+\dots+b_{4}^{2}
. Then the embedding into
A_{1}^{\oplus 8}
is defined by

z_{1}=a_{1}e_{1}+\dots+a_{4}e_{4},\qquad z_{2}=b_{1}e_{5}+\dots+b_{4}e_{8},

which is again primitive. Furthermore,
\sigma_{z_{1}}
extends to the involution
\sigma\colon A_{1}^{\oplus 8}\to A_{1}^{\oplus 8}
defined by
\sigma(e_{i})=-e_{i}
and
\sigma(e_{4+i})=e_{4+i}
for
i=1,\dots,4
.
∎

2.4  Degrees of irrationality of period spaces

Putting the previous results together allows us to generalize the techniques in
[
1
]
*Section 6. Suppose that
\Lambda
is an even lattice of signature
(2,m)
with an embedding into an even lattice
\Lambda_{\#}
of signature
(2,m^{\prime})
, where
1\leq m^{\prime}-m\leq m^{\prime}-2
. Consider an arithmetic subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda)
which is extendable to
\Lambda_{\#}
. We are going to bound the degree of irrationality of the period space
\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}(\Lambda)
in terms of this embedding. Let us first deal with the case when the embedding is primitive.

Lemma 2.16
.

Suppose that the embedding
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
is primitive and consider the natural map

\psi_{\Gamma}\colon\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{P}}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}.

Then there exists a constant
C
depending only on
\Lambda_{\#}
such that with
Y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
, we have the inequalities

\displaystyle\operatorname{irr}(\psi_{\Gamma}(Y))\leq C\cdot|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|^{1+\frac{m^{\prime}}{2}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{irr}(Y)\leq C\cdot[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda):\Gamma\cap\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\cdot|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda))|\cdot|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|^{1+\frac{m^{\prime}}{2}}.

Proof.

Choose a basis
\underline{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{m^{\prime}-m})
for the orthogonal complement
\Lambda^{\perp\Lambda_{\#}}
with moment matrix
T=\left(\frac{1}{2}(w_{i},w_{j})\right)
. Then
\Omega(\Lambda)=\langle\underline{w}\rangle^{\perp}\subset\Omega(\Lambda_{\#})
. Thus
\psi_{\Gamma}(Y)
appears as a Zariski open subset of a component of the special cycle
Z_{T,0}\subset{\mathcal{P}}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}
. By Lemma
2.2
, there exist a constant
C^{\prime}
depending only on
\Lambda_{\#}
such that

\operatorname{irr}(\psi_{\Gamma}(Y))\leq C^{\prime}\cdot|\det(T)|^{1+\frac{m^{\prime}}{2}}.

Note that (cf.
[
25
]
*Section 14.0.2)

\det(T)=\frac{1}{2^{m^{\prime}-m}}\cdot|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda^{\perp})|=\frac{1}{2^{m^{\prime}-m}}\cdot\frac{|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda_{\#})|}{|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|}\cdot\left[\Lambda_{\#}:\Lambda\oplus\Lambda^{\perp}\right]^{2}.

From
[
19
]
*Section 7, (36), one can deduce that

\left[\Lambda_{\#}:\Lambda\oplus\Lambda^{\perp}\right]\leq|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|.

Hence

\left|\det(T)\right|\leq|{\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda_{\#})}|\cdot|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|

By setting
C\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}C^{\prime}\cdot|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda_{\#})|^{1+\frac{m^{\prime}}{2}}
, we obtain the first inequality

\operatorname{irr}(\psi_{\Gamma}(Y))\leq C\cdot|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|^{1+\frac{m^{\prime}}{2}}.

By Lemma
2.10
, we have

\operatorname{irr}(Y)\leq\operatorname{irr}(\psi_{\Gamma}(Y))\cdot[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda):\Gamma\cap\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\cdot|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda))|.

Combining this with the previous inequality gives us the second inequality.
∎

Now let us deduce a bound when the embedding is not necessarily primitive.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
\Lambda_{s}\subset\Lambda_{\#}
be the saturation of
\Lambda
and assume that
[\Lambda_{s}:\Lambda]\leq D
for some constant
D
. Let also
\ell(\Lambda)
be the minimum number of generators for
D(\Lambda)
. Then there exists a constant
C
depending only on
\Lambda_{\#}
and
D
such that with
Y=\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
, it holds that

\operatorname{irr}(Y)\leq C\cdot[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda):\Gamma\cap\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\cdot|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|^{1+\frac{m^{\prime}}{2}+2\ell(\Lambda)}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.10
, the degree of
\psi_{\Gamma}\colon\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}
onto its image is bounded by

[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda):\Gamma\cap\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\cdot[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s}):\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\cdot|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{s}))|.

Lemma
2.11
shows that
|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{s}))|\leq|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|^{\ell(\Lambda)}
. Combining this with Lemma
2.12
gives

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{s}):\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\cdot|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{s}))|

\displaystyle\leq[\Lambda_{s}:\Lambda]^{m+2}\cdot|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda))|\cdot|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|^{\ell(\Lambda)}

\displaystyle\leq D^{m+2}\cdot|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|^{2\ell(\Lambda)}\leq D^{m^{\prime}+2}\cdot|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|^{2\ell(\Lambda)}.

As a result, we obtain

\operatorname{irr}(Y)\leq\operatorname{irr}(\psi_{\Gamma}(Y))\cdot[\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda):\Gamma\cap\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)]\cdot D^{m^{\prime}+2}\cdot|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|^{2\ell(\Lambda)}.

Recall from the proof of Lemma
2.11
that

|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda_{s})|\leq|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda)|.

To bound
\operatorname{irr}(\psi_{\Gamma}(Y))
, first notice that
\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)=\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{s}}(\Gamma)
, so we can consider
\psi_{\Gamma}
as a map from
\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{s}}(\Gamma)
to
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\Lambda_{\#}}
. Then Lemma
2.16
shows that there exists
C^{\prime}>0
depending only on
\Lambda_{\#}
such that

Let

\textstyle{Y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

*Lemma 8.1. This allows us to bound the degree of irrationality of

3.3  Hyperkähler manifolds of OG10 type

Now consider hyperkähler manifolds

Lemma 3.11
.

Consider the subgroup
\operatorname{Mon}^{2}(\Lambda,h)\subset\mathrm{O}^{+}(\Lambda_{h})
.

1.

If
\gamma=3
, then
\operatorname{Mon}^{2}(\Lambda,h)=\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{h})

into

If
\gamma=1

with

. In this case, the monodromy group has the form
\operatorname{Mon}^{2}(\Lambda,h)=\langle\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{h}),\sigma_{v}\rangle
where

is the reflection with respect to any vector

with

\Lambda_{h}
into
\Lambda_{\#}=U^{\oplus 2}\oplus E_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}\oplus A_{2}(-1)\oplus A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 5}

\operatorname{Mon}^{2}(\Lambda,h)
is extendable. Furthermore, if

can be replaced by
A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 4}

Proof.

The formulas for
\operatorname{Mon}^{2}(\Lambda,h)
and

in both cases are part of
[
18
]
*Theorem 3.1 and the proof of
[
18
]
*Lemma 4.4. Let us find the lattice
\Lambda_{\#}
and prove the extendability case-by-case.

1.

Suppose that
\gamma=3
. First note that
Q_{h}(-1)
has signature
(0,3)
, so it can be embedded as a primitive sublattice of

(see
[
25
]
*Theorem 14.1.15). This induces a primitive embedding

\operatorname{Mon}^{2}(\Lambda,h)=\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{h})
in this case, it is extendable for every such embedding.

2.

Suppose that
\gamma=1
. Using Lagrange’s four square theorem, we can write
d=a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}+a_{4}^{2}+1
for some integers

. This induces a primitive embedding of
\mathbb{Z}\ell
into

which maps a generator to

into

, thus induces a primitive embedding of

into
\Lambda_{\#}=U^{\oplus 2}\oplus E_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}\oplus A_{2}(-1)\oplus A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 5}

for

and choose

[
18
]
*Lemma 4.4). We regard

as an element in

and consider the corresponding reflection

\widetilde{\sigma}_{v}:x\mapsto x-2\frac{(x,v)}{(v,v)}v.

Note that

and for any

,

is divisible by

. Then
\widetilde{\sigma}_{v}
is integral, that is,
\widetilde{\sigma}_{v}\in{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda_{\#}\right)

on

. If furthermore

, then
[

]
*Theorem 1 shows that

is the sum of

\mathbb{Z}\ell
into

.

∎

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{OG10},\;2d}^{\gamma}
be the moduli space of hyperkähler manifolds of type OG10 with a primitive polarization of degree
2d
and divisibility
\gamma
. For both
\gamma=1
and

, this moduli space is irreducible; see
[
38
,
39
]
or
[
46
]
*Proposition 3.4. In the non-split (

) case,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{OG10},\;2d}^{\gamma}
is non-empty if and only if

, cf.
[
18
]
*Lemma 3.4. Moreover, all moduli spaces

are of general type with finitely many exceptions
[
18
,
3
]
. We can now get a polynomial bound on their degrees of irrationality.

Theorem 3.12
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
independent of
\gamma,d
such that

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{OG10},\;2d}^{\gamma}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{14+\varepsilon}.

Moreover if
\gamma=1
the bound can be refined to
\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{OG10},\;2d}^{1}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{\frac{27}{2}+\varepsilon}
for all
d
and to
\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{OG10},\;2d}^{1}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{13+\varepsilon}
for all
d
not divisible by
8
.

Proof.

One uses Lemma
3.11
, proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
3.4
, in the case
n=2
or
\gamma\geq 3
.
The only different step here is the estimate of
|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{h}))|
. We need to prove that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
C^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{h}))|<C^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{\varepsilon}
.

If
\gamma=3
, then
2d=18t-6
for some integer
t
. The discriminant group
D\left(\Lambda_{h}\right)\cong D\left(Q_{h}(-1)\right)
is cyclic of order
2d/3
, see
[
18
]
*Theorem 3.1. Further, in this case,
Q_{h}(-1)
is given by

Q_{h}(-1)=\begin{pmatrix}-2&1&0\\
1&-2&-1\\
0&-1&-2t\end{pmatrix},

see
[
3
]
*Section 8. Let
\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3}\}
be a basis for
Q_{h}(-1)
and
r=2d/3
. Then
\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2}-3\delta_{3}
has divisibility
r
, and
\alpha=\frac{\delta_{1}+2\delta_{2}-3\delta_{3}}{r}+\Lambda_{h}
is a generator for
D\left(Q_{h}(-1)\right)
. An element
g\in\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{h}))
is given by
\alpha\mapsto a\cdot\alpha
, with

(\alpha,\alpha)\cdot\left(a^{2}-1\right)=\frac{-9}{2d}(a^{2}-1)=\frac{-3}{r}(a^{2}-1)\in\mathbb{Z}.

Note that
r\equiv-2\mod 3
, in particular
a^{2}\equiv 1
modulo
r
. Looking at the decomposition
r=p_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots p_{s}^{k_{s}}
, where
p_{i}
’s are distinct primes, by the Chinese remainder theorem, we see that the number of solutions to
x^{2}\equiv 1
modulo
r
is bounded by
2^{\rho(r)+1}
, where
\rho(r)
is the number of distinct prime factors of
r
. Hence, there are at most
2^{\rho(r)+1}\leq 2^{\rho(2d)+1}\leq 2^{\rho(d)+2}
possible values for
a
modulo
r
. Then we proceed as in the proof of Theorem
3.4
.

If instead
\gamma=1
, then we have
D(\Lambda_{h})=D(\mathbb{Z}\ell)\oplus D(A_{2}(-1))
where the direct sum is with respect to the
\mathbb{Q}\big/2\mathbb{Z}
-valued quadratic form. If
(\ell,e_{1},e_{2})
is a canonical basis of
\mathbb{Z}\ell\oplus A_{2}(-1)
, then
D(\Lambda_{h})
is generated by
\frac{\ell}{2d},\frac{2}{3}e_{1}+\frac{1}{3}e_{2}
modulo
\Lambda_{h}
.
An element
A\in\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{h}))
is determined by the images of the generators,

A\left(\frac{\ell}{2d}\right)=a_{11}\left(\frac{\ell}{2d}\right)+a_{21}\left(\frac{2}{3}e_{1}+\frac{1}{3}e_{2}\right),\quad A\left(\frac{2}{3}e_{1}+\frac{1}{3}e_{2}\right)=a_{12}\left(\frac{\ell}{2d}\right)+a_{22}\left(\frac{2}{3}e_{1}+\frac{1}{3}e_{2}\right)

hence it can be represented by a
2\times 2
matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1,2}
with
a_{1j}\in\{0,1,\ldots,2d-1\}
, and
a_{2j}\in\{0,1,2\}
. The fact that
A
preserves the quadratic form implies that

A^{t}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2d}&0\\
0&\frac{2}{3}\end{pmatrix}A\;\equiv\;\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2d}&0\\
0&\frac{2}{3}\end{pmatrix}\;\mod\;\mathbb{Z}.

Which can be written down explicitly as

\frac{1}{2d}a_{11}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}a_{21}^{2}\equiv\frac{1}{2d},\quad\frac{1}{2d}a_{11}a_{12}+\frac{2}{3}a_{21}a_{22}\equiv 0,\quad\frac{1}{2d}a_{12}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}a_{22}^{2}\equiv\frac{2}{3}\quad\mod\mathbb{Z}

(3.11)

Suppose first that
3\nmid d
. Then multiplying the equations of (
3.11
) by
6d
we get

3\cdot a_{11}^{2}+4d\cdot a_{21}^{2}\equiv 3,\quad 3\cdot a_{11}a_{12}+4d\cdot a_{21}a_{22}\equiv 0,\quad 3\cdot a_{12}^{2}+4d\cdot a_{22}^{2}\equiv 4d\quad\mod 6d\cdot\mathbb{Z}

Looking at the last equations modulo
d
and using that
3
is coprime to
d
, we get

a_{11}^{2}\equiv 1\qquad\text{and}\qquad a_{11}\cdot a_{12}\equiv 0\mod d.

In particular,
a_{12}\equiv 0
modulo
d
, so it must belong to
\{0,d\}
. Again, since the number of solutions to
x^{2}\equiv 1
modulo
d
is bounded by
2^{\rho(d)+1}
, there are at most
4\cdot 2^{\rho(d)}
possible values for
a_{11}
modulo
2d
. Since
a_{21},a_{22}
can take at most three possible values, all these facts yield
|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda))|\leq 2\cdot(4\cdot 2^{\rho(d)})\cdot 3\cdot 3=72\cdot 2^{\rho(d)}
. If instead
d=3r
, we can multiply equations (
3.11
) by
6r
and use a similar reasoning. As a consequence, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we see that there exists
C^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}>0
such that
\left|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{h}))\right|\leq C^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{\varepsilon}
.
∎

3.4  Hyperkähler manifolds of OG6 type

Let
X
be a hyperkähler manifold of dimension
2n
deformation equivalent to O’Grady’s six-dimensional example
[
43
]
. Then the group
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is isomorphic to the lattice
\Lambda=\Lambda_{\rm OG6}:=U^{\oplus 3}\oplus A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 2}
, which is an even lattice of signature
(3,5)
. In this case, we have that (cf.
[
34
]
)

\operatorname{Mon}^{2}(\Lambda)={\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda).

Let
h\in\Lambda
be a primitive class with
(h,h)=2d>0
. Then its divisibility is either
\gamma=1
or
\gamma=2
, and the lattice
\Lambda_{h}
has signature
(2,5)
and discriminant
|\operatorname{disc}(\Lambda_{h})|=\frac{8d}{\gamma^{2}}
. We will denote the standard bases of the first two copies of
U
by
\{e,f\}
and
\{e_{1},f_{1}\}
and the canonical basis of
A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 2}
by
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
.

Lemma 3.13
.

Up to the action of
\operatorname{Mon}^{2}(\Lambda)
, the class
h
and the orthogonal complement
\Lambda_{h}
have the following form:

1.

If
\gamma=1
, then
h=e+df
and
\Lambda_{h}=U^{\oplus 2}\oplus\langle e-df\rangle\oplus A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 2}
.

2.

If
\gamma=2
, then
h=2(e+tf)-w
for some
w\in\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{1}+v_{2}\}
and some integer
t
. In this case, we have
\Lambda_{h}=U^{\oplus 2}\oplus Q_{h}(-1)
where

\displaystyle Q_{h}(-1)

\displaystyle=\langle f-v_{1},e-tf,v_{2}\rangle,

\displaystyle\text{if }w=v_{1},

\displaystyle Q_{h}(-1)

\displaystyle=\langle f-v_{2},e-tf,v_{1}\rangle,

\displaystyle\text{if }w=v_{2},

\displaystyle Q_{h}(-1)

\displaystyle=\langle f-v_{1},f-v_{2},e-tf\rangle,

\displaystyle\text{if }w=v_{1}+v_{2}.

Proof.

Every element
h\in\Lambda
can be written as
av-w
with
v\in U^{\oplus 3}
primitive, and
w\in A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 2}
. Since
h
is primitive, by Eichler’s criterion, we can assume
w\in\{0,v_{1},v_{2},v_{1}+v_{2}\}
. If
\gamma=1
, then
w=0
and
a=1
. Then, using Eichler’s criterion as in the proof of Lemma
3.5
,
h
can be taken as
e+df
.

If
\gamma=2
, then
w\in\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{1}+v_{2}\}
. Further,

2
divides
a
and since
\Lambda
is even,
(v,v)\in 2\mathbb{Z}
. Then

2d=(h,h)=a^{2}(v,v)+(w,w)=8t+(w,w),

for a certain integer
t
. Then

d=\begin{cases}4t-2&\hbox{ if }w=v_{1}+v_{2},\\
4t-1&\hbox{ if }w\in\{v_{1},v_{2}\}.\end{cases}

Using Eichler’s criterion again, we can take
h=2(e+tf)-w
with
w\in\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{1}+v_{2}\}
for
\gamma=2
. Based on this, the lattice
\Lambda_{h}
can be computed explicitly.
∎

The following lemma is here for the sake of completeness, cf.
[
4
]
*Lemma 7.1.

Lemma 3.14
.

The group
{\rm{Mon}}^{2}\left(\Lambda,h\right)
is at most a degree two extension of
\widetilde{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda,h\right)
. More concretely,

{\rm{Mon}}^{2}\left(\Lambda,h\right)=\left\langle\widetilde{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda,h\right),\sigma\right\rangle,

where
\sigma
is the identity if
w\in\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
, and
\sigma=\sigma_{\kappa}\in{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda,h\right)
is the reflection along
\kappa=v_{1}-v_{2}
if
\gamma=1
or
\gamma=2
and
w=v_{1}+v_{2}
.

Proof.

Since
\Lambda
is indefinite of rank
8
, the map
{\rm{O}}\left(\Lambda\right)\longrightarrow{\rm{O}}\left(D\left(\Lambda\right)\right)
is surjective, see
[
37
]
*Theorem 1.14.2. Further,
D\left(\Lambda\right)\cong\left(\mathbb{Z}\big/2\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\oplus 2}
is generated by
\{\frac{v_{1}}{2},\frac{v_{2}}{2}\}
. One notes that if
g\in{\rm{O}}\left(D\left(\Lambda\right)\right)
, then
g={\rm{Id}}
or
g:\frac{v_{1}}{2}\mapsto\frac{v_{2}}{2},\frac{v_{2}}{2}\mapsto\frac{v_{1}}{2}
. In particular

\left[{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda,h\right):\widetilde{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda,h\right)\right]\leq 2.

One can check that
\sigma_{\kappa}\in{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda,h\right)
fixes
h
and does not act as the identity on
D(\Lambda)
. Assume
\gamma=1
, and
\kappa=v_{1}-v_{2}
. Since
h\in(v_{1}-v_{2})^{\perp}
, see Lemma
3.13
, then
\sigma_{k}(h)=h
, that is,
\sigma_{k}\in{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda,h\right)
. Further,

\sigma_{k}\left(\frac{v_{1}}{2}\right)=\frac{v_{1}}{2}-2\frac{\left(\frac{v_{1}}{2},\kappa\right)}{(\kappa,\kappa)}\kappa=\frac{v_{2}}{2}.

In particular
\sigma_{k}\not\in\widetilde{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda,h\right)
. The case
\gamma=2
and
w=v_{1}+v_{2}
is analogous. Finally, assume
\gamma=2
and
w=v_{1}
. Let
g\in{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda,h\right)
. Then
g(h)=h
and
g\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)=\frac{h}{2}
.
In particular, from Lemma
3.13
we have
g\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)=g(e+tf)-g\left(\frac{v_{1}}{2}\right)=(e+tf)-\frac{v_{1}}{2}
which implies
g\left(\frac{v_{1}}{2}\right)\equiv\frac{v_{1}}{2}\mod\Lambda
. Therefore, there is no element in
{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda\right)
fixing
h
and acting as
\frac{v_{1}}{2}\mapsto\frac{v_{2}}{2}
on the discriminant of
\Lambda
. In particular, every such
g
acts as the identity on
D(\Lambda)
. This shows the equality
{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda,h\right)=\widetilde{\rm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda,h\right)
. The argument is the same if
w=v_{2}
.
∎

Lemma 3.15
.

We have the equality

\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda,h)=\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{h}).

Proof.

The inclusion
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{h})\subset\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda,h)
follows from Lemma
2.3
. To prove the converse, let us first show that, if
x\in\Lambda_{h}^{\vee}
, then
x\in\mathbb{Q}\cdot h+\Lambda^{\vee}
; since
\Lambda_{h}\subset\Lambda^{\vee}
, it suffices to check this for those
x\in\Lambda_{h}^{\vee}
whose classes generate
D(\Lambda_{h})
. Let us proceed case-by-case:

1.

Assume first that
\gamma=1
. Then in the notation of Lemma
3.13
one can compute that

D(\Lambda_{h})=\left\langle\frac{1}{2d}(e-df),\frac{1}{2}v_{1},\frac{1}{2}v_{2}\right\rangle

Observe that
\frac{1}{2}v_{1},\frac{1}{2}v_{2}\in\Lambda^{\vee}
and
\frac{1}{2d}(e-df)=\frac{1}{2d}(e+df)-f=\frac{1}{2d}h-f
with
f\in\Lambda
.

2.

Assume now that
\gamma=2
. Due to Lemma
3.13
, we can assume
h=2(e+tf)-\omega
with
\omega\in\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{1}+v_{2}\}
. If
\omega=v_{1}
, then one can verify using the same lemma that

D(\Lambda_{h})=D(Q_{h}(-1))=\left\langle\frac{1}{2d}h-\frac{1}{2}v_{1},\frac{1}{d}h-f,\frac{1}{2}v_{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle\frac{1}{2d}h-\frac{1}{2}v_{1},\frac{1}{2}v_{2}\right\rangle

where the third equality comes from the relation
\frac{1}{d}h-f=2\left(\frac{1}{2d}h-\frac{1}{2}v_{1}\right)-(f-v_{1})
and the fact that
f-v_{1}\in Q_{h}(-1)
. Observe that the generators are all contained in
\mathbb{Q}\cdot h+\Lambda^{\vee}
. The case
\omega=v_{2}
can be solved in a similar way. If instead
\omega=v_{1}+v_{2}
, then one can verify that

\displaystyle D(\Lambda_{h})=D(Q_{h}(-1))

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{1}{2d}h-\frac{1}{2}v_{1},\frac{1}{2d}h-\frac{1}{2}v_{2},\frac{1}{d}h-f\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{1}{2d}h-\frac{1}{2}v_{1},\frac{1}{2d}h-\frac{1}{2}v_{2}\right\rangle.

Observe that these generators are contained in
\mathbb{Q}\cdot h+\Lambda^{\vee}
.

This completes the proof for the inclusion
\Lambda_{h}^{\vee}\subset\mathbb{Q}\cdot h+\Lambda^{\vee}
.
Now, pick any
g\in\mathrm{O}(\Lambda,h)\cap\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda)
and
x\in\Lambda_{h}^{\vee}
. Then
x\in\mathbb{Q}\cdot h+\Lambda^{\vee}
, which allows us to write
x=\alpha\cdot h+y
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
and
y\in\Lambda^{\vee}
. It follows that

g(x)-x=g(\alpha\cdot h)+g(y)-\alpha\cdot h-y=g(y)-y\in\Lambda.

We also have

(g(x)-x,h)=(g(x),h)-(x,h)=(x,g^{-1}(h))-(x,h)=(x,h)-(x,h)=0.

These two relations imply that
g(x)-x\in\Lambda_{h}
. Hence
g\in\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{h})
. This finishes the proof of the equality

\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{h})=\mathrm{O}(\Lambda,h)\cap\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda).

∎

As a consequence we have the following extendability result:

Corollary 3.16
.

In all cases listed in Lemma
3.13
there is a primitive embedding
\Lambda_{h}\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\#}
such that the group
{\rm{Mon}}^{2}\left(\Lambda,h\right)
extends.

1.

If
\gamma=1

\Lambda_{\#}=U^{\oplus 2}\oplus A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 7}
and if

8

.

2.

If
\gamma=2
and
w\in\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
, then
\Lambda_{\#}=U^{\oplus 2}\oplus E_{8}(-1)
.

3.

If

and

, then
\Lambda_{\#}=U^{\oplus 2}\oplus A_{3}(-1)\oplus A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 4}

Proof.

By Lemmas
3.14
and
3.15
it suffices to show that
\sigma_{\kappa}
extends to
\Lambda_{\#}
is all cases. We proceed case-by-case:

1.

By Lemma

and, as in the proof of Lemma
3.11

(resp.

Further, note that if we see

(3.12)

then since

2.

Note that

∎

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{OG6},\;2d}^{\gamma}

Theorem 3.17
.

For any

If instead

Finally, if
\gamma=1
and

is not divisible by
8
,
the bound can be further refined to

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{OG6},\;2d}^{\gamma}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{5+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

The proof uses Corollary
3.16
and is analogous to the proof of Theorem
3.4
. The only difference is the estimate of

. We want to prove that for any
\varepsilon>0

such that

.

In the case

3.12
: an element

is determined by what it does to the generators

In particular,

4  Moduli spaces of abelian surfaces and K3 surfaces

This section consists of two parts. First, we study the irrationality of moduli spaces
\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}
of
(1,d)
-polarized abelian surfaces. Then we revisit our study in
[
1
]
about the irrationality of moduli spaces
\mathcal{F}_{d}
of primitively polarized K3 surfaces.

4.1  Abelian surfaces of type (1, d)

Our strategy in bounding the degree of irrationality of
\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}
starts by realizing it as a double cover of an appropriate period space using the construction of Gritsenko and Hulek
[
20
]
. The construction will provide a bound for the degree of irrationality when
d
is squarefree. Our main task here is to reduce the general case to this one via a geometric argument by O’Grady
[
41
]
.

Let
\mathbb{H}_{2}
be the Siegel upper-half space of
2\times 2
symmetric complex matrices
\tau
with positive definite imaginary part. Consider the usual action of the symplectic group
\operatorname{Sp}(4,\mathbb{Q})
on
\mathbb{H}_{2}
and the arithmetic group

\Gamma_{1,d}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}&\mathbb{Z}&\mathbb{Z}&d\mathbb{Z}\\
d\mathbb{Z}&\mathbb{Z}&d\mathbb{Z}&d\mathbb{Z}\\
\mathbb{Z}&\mathbb{Z}&\mathbb{Z}&d\mathbb{Z}\\
\mathbb{Z}&\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}&\mathbb{Z}&\mathbb{Z}\end{pmatrix}\right\}\cap{\rm{Sp}}\left(4,\mathbb{Q}\right).

Then we have
\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}=\mathbb{H}_{2}/\Gamma_{1,d}
. Consider the Euclidean lattice
L=\mathbb{Z}e_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathbb{Z}e_{4}
where
(e_{i},e_{i})=1
and
(e_{i},e_{j})=0
for
i\neq j
. Then the space of bivectors
L\wedge L
forms a rank six lattice, where the pairing
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}
is defined by requiring that

x\wedge y=(x,y)\cdot(e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge e_{3}\wedge e_{4})\;\in\;\bigwedge^{4}L.

Fix
w_{d}:=e_{1}\wedge e_{3}+de_{2}\wedge e_{4}\in L\wedge L
and consider the
integral paramodular group of level
d

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1,d}=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}(L)\,|\,(\wedge^{2}g)(w_{d})=w_{d}\}.

Then the orthogonal complement
\Lambda_{d}=\left(w_{d}\right)^{\perp}\subset L\wedge L
has the form

\Lambda_{d}\cong U^{\oplus 2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\ell\qquad\text{where}\qquad(\ell,\ell)=2d.

The group
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1,d}
acts naturally on the lattice
\Lambda_{d}
via the group homomorphism

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1,d}\longrightarrow\mathrm{O}(\Lambda_{d}),\qquad g\longmapsto(\wedge^{2}g)|_{\Lambda_{d}}.

It turns out that

\Gamma_{1,d}=\mathbb{I}_{d}^{-1}\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1,d}\mathbb{I}_{d}

where
\mathbb{I}_{d}=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,d)
. In particular, the group
\Gamma_{1,d}
acts on
\Lambda_{d}
via the homomorphism

\Gamma_{1,d}\longrightarrow\mathrm{O}(\Lambda_{d}),\qquad g\longmapsto\wedge^{2}\left(\mathbb{I}_{d}g\mathbb{I}_{d}^{-1}\right).

(4.1)

It is proved in
[
20
]
*Lemma 1.1 that the image of this map lies inside
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}\left(\Lambda_{d}\right)
. In the case that
d
is squarefree, one of the key results in
[
20
]
asserts the existence of an index two extension

\Gamma_{1,d}\subset\Gamma_{1,d}^{+}\quad\text{together with a map}\quad\Gamma_{1,d}^{+}\longrightarrow\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda_{d}\right)

which induces a birational morphism from
\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}^{+}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{H}_{2}\big/\Gamma_{1,d}^{+}
to the quotient
\Omega(\Lambda_{d})/\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{d})
. This implies the following statement.

Proposition 4.1

(
[
20
]
*Proposition 1.4)
.

Assume that
d
is squarefree. Then there exists a dominant morphism of degree two

\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}\longrightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{+}_{\Lambda_{d}}=\Omega(\Lambda_{d})\big/\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}\left(\Lambda_{d}\right).

This allows us to bound the degree of irrationality of
\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}
using
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{+}_{\Lambda_{d}}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose that
d
is squarefree. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
independent of
d
such that

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{4+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

From Proposition
4.1
, we see that

\operatorname{irr}(\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)})\leq 2\operatorname{irr}(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\Lambda_{d}}).

Let us bound the latter using Lemma
2.16
. Recall that
\Lambda_{d}\cong U^{\oplus 2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}(-2d)
. Since
d
is squarefree, it is not divisible by
8
, and thus can be written as a sum of four coprime squares
d=\sum_{i=1}^{4}a_{i}^{2}
. This induces a primitive embedding of
\Lambda_{d}
into
\Lambda_{\#}=U^{\oplus 2}\oplus A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 4}
. As
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{d})
is always extendable, Lemma
2.16
shows that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\operatorname{irr}(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{+}_{\Lambda_{d}})\leq C\cdot|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{d}))|\cdot|\operatorname{disc}\Lambda_{d}|^{4}=2^{4}\cdot C\cdot|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{d}))|\cdot d^{4}.

Because
D(\Lambda_{d})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2d\mathbb{Z}
, we see that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}>0
such that
|\mathrm{O}(D(\Lambda_{d}))|\leq C^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{\varepsilon}
. Putting all the inequalities together yields the desired bound.
∎

In order to extend this result to the general case, let us recall a geometric construction due to O’Grady
[
41
]
. Let
(A,L)\in\mathcal{A}_{(1,n^{2}k)}
be an abelian surface with a polarization of type
(1,n^{2}k)
. Then the natural map
\phi_{L}\colon A\to A^{\vee}=\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(A)
satisfies
\operatorname{Ker}\phi_{L}\cong\left(\mathbb{Z}/n^{2}k\mathbb{Z}\right)^{2}
and the subgroup of
n
-torsion points
J\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\operatorname{Ker}\phi_{L}[n]
is isomorphic to
\left(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\right)^{2}
. Now consider the quotient
f\colon A\to B=A/J
. Then
B
has a natural polarization
M
of type
(1,k)
which satisfies
f^{*}M=L
. This yields a map

\mathcal{A}_{(1,n^{2}k)}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}_{(1,k)}.

(4.2)

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}
independent of
n
and
k
such that the degree of this map is at most
C_{\varepsilon}\cdot(nk)^{4+\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Let
\phi_{M}\colon B\longrightarrow B^{\vee}
be the isogeny induced by
M
. As in the proof of
[
41
]
*Proposition 5.1, the image
H=f(\operatorname{Ker}\phi_{L})
is a subgroup of
B[nk]
isomorphic to
\left(\mathbb{Z}/nk\mathbb{Z}\right)^{2}
such that
\phi_{M}(H)
equals the kernel of
f^{*}\colon B^{\vee}\to A^{\vee}
. In particular, since
f\colon A\to B
is the dual of
f^{*}\colon B^{\vee}\to A^{\vee}=B^{\vee}/\phi_{M}(H)
, we see that we can reconstruct
A
from
\phi_{M}
and
H
. This shows that the cardinality of the fiber of (
4.2
) over
(B,M)
is bounded by the number of subgroups
H\subset B[nk]
isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z}/nk\mathbb{Z})^{2}
. Since
B[nk]\cong(\mathbb{Z}/nk\mathbb{Z})^{4}
, this number can be explicitly computed: if
nk
is a prime power
p^{r}
, then
[
12
]
*Equation (1) and
[
7
]
*Theorem 8.1 show that it equals
p^{4r-4}\cdot(p^{2}+1)\cdot(p^{2}+p+1)\leq C\cdot p^{4r}
for a certain
C>1
independent of
p
. If we write
nk=p_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdot\dots\cdot p_{s}^{r_{s}},s=\rho(nk)
, as a product of pairwise distinct primes, then the Chinese Remainder Theorem implies that the number of subgroups is bounded by
C^{\rho(nk)}\cdot(nk)^{4}
, and
C^{\rho(nk)}={\rm O}((nk)^{\varepsilon})
for all
\varepsilon>0
. Indeed, if
C\leq 2^{N}
for fixed
N
, then
C^{\rho(nk)}\leq\left(2^{\rho(nk)}\right)^{N}={\rm O}((nk)^{N\cdot\varepsilon})={\rm O}((nk)^{\varepsilon})
.
∎

With this we can get a universal bound. For a positive integer
d
factored into primes as
d=p_{1}^{2h_{1}+r_{1}}\cdot\dots\cdot p_{s}^{2h_{s}+r_{s}}
where
h_{i}\geq 0
and
r_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, then we define

k(d)=p_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots p_{s}^{r_{s}}.

Theorem 4.4
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
independent of
d
such that

\operatorname{irr}(\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)})\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot\left(d^{2+\varepsilon}\cdot k(d)^{6+\varepsilon}\right)

In particular, since
k(d)\leq d
, a universal polynomial bound is given by

\operatorname{irr}(\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)})\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{8+2\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
4.3
with
n=\left(d/k(d)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
k=k(d)
, we get a map

\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}_{(1,k(d))}

whose degree is at most
C^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\left(n\cdot k(d)\right)^{4+\varepsilon}=C^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\cdot(d\cdot k(d))^{2+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon}
.
Since
k(d)
is squarefree, Proposition
4.2
gives for each
\varepsilon>0
a constant
C^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{A}_{(1,k(d))}\right)\leq C^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon}\cdot k(d)^{4+\varepsilon}.

Hence, with
C_{\varepsilon}=C^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\cdot C^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon}
we see that for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
independent of
d
such that

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot\left(d^{2+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon}\cdot k(d)^{6+\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{8+2\varepsilon}.

This completes the proof.
∎

One can improve the above bound for infinitely many series of
d
as in the case of K3 surfaces treated in
[
1
]
. One instance of this is when
k(d)
is fixed, e.g. when
d
is a square. We can also consider quadratic series on
d
. Consider positive integers
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
4ac-b^{2}<0
. This defines a negative definite rank two lattice

Q(-1)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{pmatrix}-2a&b\\
b&-2c\end{pmatrix}.

(4.3)

Proposition 4.5
.

Assume
d
is a perfect square. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
independent of
d
such that

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{2+\varepsilon}.

Further, fix
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
which satisfy
4ac-b^{2}<0
. Suppose that
d
is squarefree and has the form
d=aX^{2}-bXY+cY^{2}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}=C_{\varepsilon}(a,b,c)>0
such that

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{A}_{(1,d)}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{2+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

The first bound follows from Theorem
4.4
with
k(d)=1
. For the second bound, the assumption on
d
implies that there exists a primitive embedding of
\mathbb{Z}(-2d)
into
Q(-1)
and consequently a primitive embedding of
U^{\oplus 2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}(-2d)
into
U^{\oplus 2}\oplus Q(-1)
. Then the proof proceeds as in Proposition
4.2
.
∎

4.2  Revisiting the case of K3 surfaces

Let
\mathcal{F}_{2d}
be the moduli space of K3 surfaces with a primitive polarization of degree
2d
. Recall that
\mathcal{F}_{2d}
is birational to

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}^{+}_{\Lambda_{d}}=\Omega(\Lambda_{d})/\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{d})

where
\Lambda_{d}
is the lattice

\Lambda_{d}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}U^{\oplus 2}\oplus E_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\ell,\qquad(\ell,\ell)=-2d.

Let
Q(-1)
be a negative definite lattice. Then every primitive embedding of
\mathbb{Z}(-2d)
into
Q(-1)
induces a primitive embedding of
\Lambda_{d}
into
U^{\oplus 2}\oplus E_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}\oplus Q(-1)
. Since the stable orthogonal group
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}^{+}(\Lambda_{d})
is extendable with respect to such an embedding, we can use Lemma
2.16
to bound
\operatorname{irr}(\mathcal{F}_{2d})
. The general bound
[
1
]
*Theorem 1.1 is obtained by taking
Q(-1)=E_{8}(-1)
. The bounds
[
1
]
*Theorem 1.2 are obtained by choosing
Q(-1)=A_{2}(-1)
in the case of associated special cubic fourfolds,
Q(-1)=A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 2}
in the case of associated special Gushel–Mukai fourfolds, and

Q(-1)=\begin{pmatrix}-2&0\\
0&-2n\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{or}\quad\begin{pmatrix}-2&-1\\
-1&-\frac{n+1}{2}\end{pmatrix}

in the case of associated special hyperkähler fourfolds. We can get bounds for various series of
d
by choosing suitable
Q(-1)
.

Proposition 4.6
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for any
d
not divisible by
8
one has

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{F}_{2d}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{12+\varepsilon}.

If
d
is not congruent to
0,4,7\mod 8
, then

\operatorname{irr}\left(\mathcal{F}_{2d}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{\frac{23}{2}+\varepsilon}.

If
a,b,c
are positive integers such that
4ac-b^{2}<0
, then

{\rm{irr}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{2d}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\cdot d^{10+\varepsilon}

for all
d
of the form
d=aX^{2}-bXY+cY^{2}
with
X
and
Y
coprime.

Proof.

Since
d
is not divisible by
8
, it can be written as a sum of four coprime squares
d=\sum_{i=1}^{4}a_{i}^{2}
. This induces a primitive embedding of
\mathbb{Z}(-2d)
into
A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 4}

and one can get from Lemma
2.16
the first bound. For the second bound, it follows from
[
21
]
*Korollar 1 (see also
[
3
]
*Section 2) that if
d
is large enough and
d\not\equiv 0,4,7\mod 8
, then it can be expressed as the sum of three coprime squares. In particular, there exists a primitive embedding
\mathbb{Z}\ell\hookrightarrow A_{1}(-1)^{\oplus 3}
which leads to the second bound using Lemma
2.16
. In the last case, we have a primitive embedding of
\mathbb{Z}(2d)
into the lattice
Q(-1)
defined by (
4.3
), which yields the desired bound from Lemma
2.16
.
∎

Remark 4.7
