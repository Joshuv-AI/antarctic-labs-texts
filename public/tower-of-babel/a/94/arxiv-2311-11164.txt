Mitigating Exposure Bias in Discriminator Guided Diffusion Models
Eleftherios Tsonis, Paraskevi Tzouveli, Athanasios Voulodimos

Abstract
Diffusion Models have demonstrated remarkable performance in image generation. However, their demanding computational requirements for training have prompted ongoing efforts to enhance the quality of generated images through modifications in the sampling process. A recent approach, known as Discriminator Guidance, seeks to bridge the gap between the model score and the data score by incorporating an auxiliary term, derived from a discriminator network. We show that despite significantly improving sample quality, this technique has not resolved the persistent issue of Exposure Bias and we propose SEDM-G++, which incorporates a modified sampling approach, combining Discriminator Guidance and Epsilon Scaling. Our proposed approach outperforms the current state-of-the-art, by achieving an FID score of 1.73 on the unconditional CIFAR-10 dataset.

The computational cost associated with training new score models from the ground up has initiated research endeavors which employ pre-existing score models and enhance the quality of generated samples via refinements in the sampling procedure.

In an effort loosely inspired by Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Kim
et al
.

Kim et al. 2023

The method incorporates a correction term into the model score, guided by the discriminator’s feedback, which helps steer the sample generation towards more realistic paths.

The iterative sampling chain in diffusion models is long, usually requiring thousands of steps due to the Gaussian assumption of reverse diffusion, which only holds for small step sizes
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
. This leads to the exposure bias problem, illustrated by Ning
et al
.

Ning et al. 2023b

\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
. However, during inference, the model relies on the previously generated sample,
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
. This distinction between
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
results in a difference between
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
, where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the model’s noise prediction. This disparity between the two predictions results in error accumulation and deviations in the sampling process, known as ”sampling drift”
Li et al. 2023
. Ning
et al
.

Ning et al. 2023a
propose an effective method,
Epsilon Scaling
for alleviating exposure bias, which is incorporated directly into the sampling process and requires no training or fine-tuning of the model.

The preceding research prompts us to inquire whether Discriminator Guidance is effective in mitigating the accumulation of exposure bias in the sampling process. Our findings indicate that, despite notable enhancements in sample quality, Discriminator Guidance is ineffective in alleviating exposure bias in Diffusion Models. We propose SEDM-G++, which incorporates a modified sampling approach, combining Discriminator Guidance and Epsilon Scaling. Fig.

We investigate exposure bias in discriminator guided diffusion models.

•

We propose SEDM-G++, which incorporates a sampling approach combining Discriminator Guidance and Epsilon Scaling.

•

Our proposed method
improves sample quality across the board and outperforms the current state-of-the-art, by achieving an FID score of 1.73 on the unconditional CIFAR-10 dataset.

Background

3.1
Diffusion Models Framework

In DDPMs, Ho
et al
.

Ho et al. 2020
define the forward diffusion process as the gradual addition of noise to a clean datum, until structure is destroyed entirely and the datum is transformed into pure noise. Consider the data distribution
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
. The forward process is essentially a Markov chain, which generates a sequence of random variables
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots\boldsymbol{x}_{T}
with transition kernel
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})
. A variance schedule
\{\beta_{t}\in(0,1)\}_{t=0}^{T}
, such that the noise perturbing the data at each discrete timestep
t
is an isotropic Gaussian with variance
\beta_{t}
, is inherent to the DDPM framework. The Markov chain is defined by the transition kernel:

q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})={\cal N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t};\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1},\beta_{t}\boldsymbol{I})

(1)

The joint distribution of
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots\boldsymbol{x}_{T}
, conditioned on
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
is:

q(\boldsymbol{x}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})

(2)

A significant quality of the forward diffusion process is that
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
, drawn from
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})
at timestep
t
, can be directly expressed as a linear combination of
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
and a noise variable
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim{\cal N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
:

\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{a}}_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{a}_{t}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}

(3)

where
\bar{a}_{t}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-\beta_{i})
and
\alpha_{t}=1-\beta_{t}
.

For the reverse process, seeing as estimating
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
is unfeasible, a learnable transition kernel
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is utilised instead, to approximate the conditional probabilities. The kernel takes the following form:

p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1};\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t),\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t))

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
denotes model parameters. By sampling
{\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\sim{\cal N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})}
and iteratively using Eq.
4
to run the reverse diffusion process, we obtain a sample from
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
.

The success of the sampling process depends on training the reverse Markov chain to match the time reversal of the forward Markov chain. This involves adjusting parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
to make the joint distribution of the reverse Markov chain
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T})=p(\boldsymbol{x}_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
closely approximate the joint distribution of the forward process
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T})=q(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})
(Eq.
2
). This is accomplished by minimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
and
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
:

D_{KL}(q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\,||\,p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t}))

(5)

Ho
et al
.
show that it is possible to directly compare
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
against forward process posteriors, which are tractable when conditioned on
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}

Ho et al. 2020
:

q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})={\cal N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1};\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}),\tilde{\beta}_{t}\boldsymbol{I}),

(6)

where:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\beta_{t}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}

(7)

\tilde{\beta}_{t}=\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\beta_{t}

(8)

By plugging Eq.
3
into Eq.
7
we derive that given
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
,
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
must predict the following quantity:

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a_{t}}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{a}_{t}}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon_{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)\right)

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon_{\theta}}
is the approximate prediction for noise factor
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
based on
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
.

3.2
Stochastic Differential Equations

Song
et al
.

Song et al. 2021
demonstrated that DDPMs can be regarded as discretizations of stochastic differential equations (SDEs), thus providing a unifying perspective on previous approaches. The forward diffusion process is described by the forward SDE:

d\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)dt+g(t)d\boldsymbol{w},

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
is the drift coefficient,
g(t)
is the diffusion coefficient and
t
is a continuous time variable in
[0,T]
.

Anderson
Anderson 1982
demonstrated that reversing a diffusion process yields another diffusion process, which operates backward in time and is described by the reverse-time stochastic differential equation:

d\boldsymbol{x}=\big[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)-g^{2}(t)\nabla\log{q(\boldsymbol{x})}\big]d\bar{t}+g(t)d\bar{\boldsymbol{w}},

(11)

where
d\bar{t}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}
denote the infinitesimal reverse-time and reverse-time Brownian motion, respectively. Consequently, the continuous-time generative process is formulated as:

d\boldsymbol{x}=\big[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)-g^{2}(t)\boldsymbol{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\big]d\bar{t}+g(t)d\bar{\boldsymbol{w}},

(12)

Here, the score network’s estimation target
\boldsymbol{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
corresponds to the actual data score
\nabla\log{q(\boldsymbol{x})}
.

3.3
Discriminator Guidance

We investigate Exposure Bias in the discriminator guided
EDM-G++
(Kim
et al
.

Kim et al. 2023
). Their approach keeps the score function frozen and trains a discriminator network in isolation. The absence of adversarial training makes convergence simpler and faster to achieve, compared to GANs.
When producing samples using the reverse-time SDE:

d\boldsymbol{x}=[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)-g(t)^{2}\boldsymbol{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\infty}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)]dt+g(t)d\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}

(13)

Kim
et al
.

Kim et al. 2023
show that the generative process might diverge from the reverse-time data process if the local optimum
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\infty}
of the score network
\boldsymbol{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\infty}}
is different to the global optimum
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}
. However, augmenting the score function by a
correction term
can bridge the gap between the two processes.

The reverse-time SDE with the adjusted score becomes:

d\boldsymbol{x}=[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)-g(t)^{2}(\boldsymbol{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\infty}}+\boldsymbol{c}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\infty}})(\boldsymbol{x},t)]dt+g(t)d\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}

(14)

for
\boldsymbol{c}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\infty}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\nabla\log\frac{p_{r}^{t}(\boldsymbol{x})}{p^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\infty}}(\boldsymbol{x})}
. Seeing as this correction term is intractable, Kim
et al
.

Kim et al. 2023
train a neural discriminator
d_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{*}}
at all noise levels
t
and use it to estimate the correction term
\boldsymbol{c}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\infty}}
:

\boldsymbol{c}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\infty}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\approx\boldsymbol{c}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\infty}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\nabla\log\frac{d_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)}{1-d_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)}

(15)

Thus, they define the discriminator guided reverse SDE as:

d\boldsymbol{x}=[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)-g(t)^{2}(\boldsymbol{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\infty}}+\boldsymbol{c}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})(\boldsymbol{x},t)]dt+g(t)d\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}.

(16)

The Exposure Bias problem has been uncovered in traditional DDPMs, DDIMs and other diffusion models
Ning et al. 2023b
;
Ning et al. 2023a
;
Li et al. 2023
but not yet in the Discriminator Guidance approach.

3.4
Prediction Error leads to Exposure Bias

The exposure bias problem in diffusion models, highlighted by Ning
et al
.

Ning et al. 2023b
, refers to the mismatch between the model’s input data during the training and the inference phase. During training, the ground truth training sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
is available to the model, with the training distribution being
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
. In the inference phase, the model can only rely on the previously generated sample,
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
. The sampling distribution can be denoted as
q(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1},\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{t+1})
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{t+1}
is the prediction the model makes for
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
given
\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1}
, using Eq.
3
, following notation by Ning
et al
.

Ning et al. 2023a
. This results in a discrepancy between
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
.

Xiao
et al
.

Xiao et al. 2021
observed that the sampling distribution
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
is parameterized as:

p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{x}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
represents the predicted
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
. The sampling process involves predicting
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
using
\boldsymbol{\epsilon_{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)
and deriving the estimation
\boldsymbol{x}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
using Eq.
3
. Then,
\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}
is generated based on the ground truth posterior
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
, by replacing
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
with
\boldsymbol{x}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. However,
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
holds only if
\boldsymbol{x}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
. In practice,
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\neq q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, as the network makes prediction errors when estimating
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
and, as a result,
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
does not have the same variance as
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
.

.

Ning et al. 2023a
analytically calculate the discrepancy between the training and sampling distribution in DDPMs. They model

3.5
Epsilon Scaling

Ning
et al
.

Ning et al. 2023a
propose scaling down the predicted noise factor
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(where
s
denotes the noise factor predicted in the sampling stage) by a factor
\lambda_{t}
at time step
t
as a way to reduce Exposure Bias. Their approach is based on the assumption that the accuracy of the prediction
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
can be enhanced if we are able to shift the input
(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},t)
away from the unreliable vector field (depicted as the orange curve in Fig.
2
and
3
) and towards the dependable vector field (represented by the green curve in Fig.
2
and
3
).

Their approach is rooted in the following observation:
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(where
t
denotes to the noise factor predicted in the training stage) both originate from the same input
\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\sim{\cal N}(0,\boldsymbol{I})
at time step
t=T
. However, starting from time step
T-1
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
(the input for
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
) begins to deviate from
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
(the input for
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{t}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
) due to the
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot)
error made in the previous time step. This iterative process continues throughout the sampling chain, leading to exposure bias. Therefore, we can bring
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
closer to
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
by reducing the overestimated magnitude of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Their sampling method only differs from Eq.
9
in the
\lambda_{t}
term and can be expressed as:

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a_{t}}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{a}_{t}}}\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon_{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)}{\lambda_{t}}\right)

(19)

As a result, epsilon scaling is a plug-in method which requires no retraining or fine-tuning of the original score model and adds no overhead computational cost.

.

4.2
Proposed Framework

We use the EDM model, proposed by Karras
et al
.

Karras et al. 2022
, as a score estimator due to the detailed way in which it was designed. Through careful network design and fine-tuning of hyperparameters, EDM achieves a substantial quality enhancement, reducing the FID score on the CIFAR-10 dataset to 1.97, demonstrating notable progress at the time. Moreover, seeing as Exposure Bias exhibits a strong correlation with FID score
Ning et al. 2023a
, we assume that in comparison to other networks, the vanilla EDM model demonstrates a reduced accumulation of exposure bias.

When it comes to the discriminator network, we follow the setup demonstrated by Kim
et al
.

Kim et al. 2023
. The discriminator network is comprised of the encoders of two U-Net structures. The first U-Net encoder is frozen and the second one is fine-tuned, to accelerate training. We leave the exploration of other discriminator architectures, such as Vision Transformers, as future work.

In Sec.
2

An intriguing observation pertains to the fact that the FID gain achieved through Epsilon Scaling in the Euler sampler is more pronounced compared to the case of the Heun sampler. This is in line with the observations of Ning
et al
.

Ning et al. 2023a

Secondly, the corrective steps integrated into the Heun solver serve to mitigate exposure bias by readjusting the drifted sampling trajectory back to the precise vector field. This is also evident in Fig.
2
and
3
. In the case of the Heun solver, any prediction error (the root cause of exposure bias) incurred during each Euler step is rectified during the subsequent correction step (Fig.
3
), leading to a reduction in exposure bias. This exposure bias perspective offers a comprehensive explanation for the superior performance of the Heun solver in diffusion models.

6
Conclusion

We explore the effectiveness of Discriminator Guidance in addressing exposure bias accumulation during the sampling process in diffusion models. Our findings reveal that, despite notable improvements in sample quality, Discriminator Guidance falls short in mitigating exposure bias. In response, we introduce SEDM-G++, a novel approach that integrates a modified sampling technique, incorporating both Discriminator Guidance and Epsilon Scaling. Applying this method to the pre-trained EDM model, we demonstrate its consistent ability to enhance sample quality while reducing exposure bias. This improvement is observed across various ODE solvers, a range of numbers of timesteps employed, and different hyperparameter settings. Our proposed approach outperforms the current state-of-the-art, achieving an FID score of 1.73 on the unconditional CIFAR-10 dataset.
