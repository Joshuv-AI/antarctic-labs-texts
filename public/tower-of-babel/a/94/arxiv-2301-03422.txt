Centralizing maps on the ring of strictly upper triangular matrices
Jordan Bounds

Abstract

1.
Introduction

Let
R
be a ring with center
Z(R)
. We say that a map
f:R\rightarrow R
is
centralizing
if
[f(x),x]\in Z(R)
for all
x\in R
where
[a,b]=ab-ba
denotes the standard commutator on
R
. If
[f(x),x]=0
for all
x\in R
, then we say that
f
is
commuting
. Investigations into commuting maps were initiated by Posner
[
15
]
in 1957 when he proved that a noncommutative prime ring cannot exhibit a nonzero commuting derivation. Analogous results have since been established in numerous settings (see, for example,
[
2
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
13
,
14
,
16
]
).

The first general result in the study of commuting maps is due to Bres̆ar
[
5
]
when it was shown that an additive commuting map
f
over a simple unital ring
R
must be of the so-called
standard form
:

f(x)=\lambda x+\mu(x)

for some
\lambda\in Z(R)
and additive
\mu:R\rightarrow Z(R)
. Following this early finding, researchers have extensively examined the properties of commuting maps which satisfy certain conditions in various rings and algebras. The reader is referred to the survey paper by Bres̆ar
[
7
]
for details on the early development of the theory of commuting maps along with an overview of classical results in the field.

Particular interest has been placed in examining the structure of commuting maps over matrix rings and algebras with notable results being established in the cases of upper and strictly upper triangular matrices
[
1
,
3
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. In 2000, Beidar, Bres̆ar, and Chebotar
[
1
]
proved that a linear commuting map defined on
T_{r}=T_{r}(F)
, the algebra of
r\times r
upper triangular matrices with entries in a field
F
, must be of the standard form. The author showed in 2016
[
3
]
that linear commuting maps defined on
N_{r}=N_{r}(F)
, the ring of strictly upper triangular matrices with entries in a field
F
of characteristic zero, are almost of the standard form by proving the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem 1 in
[
3
]
)
.

Let
N_{r}=N_{r}(F)
be the ring of
r\times r
strictly upper triangular matrices,
r\geq 4
, over a field
F
of characteristic zero and suppose
f:N_{r}\rightarrow N_{r}
is a linear commuting map. Then there exist
\lambda\in F
and an additive map
\mu:N_{r}\rightarrow\Omega
such that

f(A)=\lambda A+\mu(A)

for all
A\in N_{r}
where
\Omega=\{ae_{1,r-1}+be_{1,r}+ce_{2,r}:a,b,c\in F\}
and
e_{i,j}
denotes the standard matrix unit.

While not explicitly explored in
[
3
]
, it is possible to obtain some additional information about the map
\mu
present in the above theorem. In particular, it is possible to show that
\mu
must be of the form

\mu(X)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&ax_{1,2}&0\\
&0&\cdots&0&ax_{r-1,r}\\
&&\ddots&&0\\
&&&&\vdots\\
&&&&0\end{pmatrix}+\zeta(X)

for some
a\in F
and additive
\zeta:N_{r}\rightarrow Z(N_{r})
, the details of which are given here in Section
3
. Our primary goal in the present paper, however, is to modify the methods utilized in
[
3
]
to prove the following statement.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f:N_{r}\rightarrow N_{r}
be a linear map such that
[f(x),x]\in Z(N_{r})
for all
x\in N_{r}
. Then there exist
\lambda\in F
and additive
\mu:N_{r}\rightarrow\Omega
such that
f(x)=\lambda x+\mu(x)
for all
x\in N_{r}
.

We begin with some preliminary details and provide a complete proof of Theorem
1.2
in Section
2
. We then conclude with a description of linear commuting maps
\mu:N_{r}\rightarrow\Omega
in Section
3
.

2.
Centralizing Maps on
N_{r}

Throughout the remainder of this paper we will assume
r\geq 4
is an integer and
F
is a field of characteristic zero. Let
e_{i,j}
denote the matrix with
ij
-th entry 1 and all other entries 0. We use
N_{r}=N_{r}(F)
to denote the ring of
r\times r
strictly upper triangular matrices with entries in
F
. It is well known that
N_{r}
is a nilpotent ring in which the identity
A^{r}=0
holds for all
A\in N_{r}
. Additionally, the center of
N_{r}
is the set
Z=Z(N_{r})=\{ae_{1,r}:a\in F\}
.

From the definition it is clear that every commuting map is necessarily centralizing. It is known that, under mild conditions, the converse is true in prime rings and centralizing maps must be commuting in this setting. This is also the case in
T_{r}=T_{r}(F)
for if we consider a map
f:T_{r}\rightarrow T_{r}
, then given any
X\in T_{r}
the matrix
[f(X),X]
will necessarily have all zero entries along the main diagonal. Given that
Z(T_{r})=\{aI_{r}:a\in F\}
, any centralizing map on
T_{r}
must then be commuting. However, there do exist maps on
N_{r}
which are centralizing but not commuting, as the following example illustrates.

Example 2.1
.

Define
p:N_{r}\rightarrow N_{r}
by

p(A)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&1&a_{1,r}\\
&0&\cdots&0&1\\
&&0&\cdots&0\\
&&&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&&&0\end{pmatrix}

where
A=(a_{i,j})
. Then
p
satisfies

[p(A),A]=(a_{r-1,r}-a_{1,2})e_{1,r}\in Z(N_{r}),

hence
p
is centralizing. However, the value
a_{r-1,r}-a_{1,2}
is not identically 0, meaning
p
is not commuting.

It should be noted that the subset
\Omega=\{ae_{1,r-1}+be_{1,r}+ce_{2,r}:a,b,c\in F\}\subset N_{r}
given in the statement of Theorem
1.1
is essential for the conclusion in
[
3
]
(see Example 2 of the same paper). As Example
2.1
illustrates above,
\Omega
remains essential in this setting as well as the given map
p
has its image contained in
\Omega
.

In proving Theorem
1.2
, we will proceed via a manner similar to that in
[
3
]
. As such, we will utilize the sets

S_{1}=\{D^{-1}JD:D=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}e_{i,i},\ d_{i}\in F,\ \text{with}\ \prod\limits_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\neq 0\}

and

S_{2}=\{T_{i,j}^{-1}JT_{i,j}:T_{i,j}=I_{r}-e_{i,j}\}

where
J=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r-1}e_{i,i+1}
and
I_{r}
is the
r\times r
identity matrix. Note that the elements
A\in S_{1}
are all of the form
A=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r-1}a_{i}e_{i,i+1}
where
\prod\limits_{i=1}^{r-1}a_{i}\neq 0
. Setting
S=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
we have that
\text{span}(S)=N_{r}
by Lemma 5 in
[
3
]
.

It is also convenient to utilize the following lemma which is an immediate consequence of Theorem 3.2.4.2 of
[
12
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
A=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r-1}a_{i}e_{i,i+1}\in N_{r}
such that
\prod\limits_{i=1}^{r-1}a_{i,i+1}\neq 0
. Then the centralizer of
A
in
N_{r}
is given by
C_{A}=\{\alpha_{1}A+\alpha_{2}A^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{r-1}A^{r-1}:\alpha_{i}\in F\}
.

Let
f:N_{r}\rightarrow N_{r}
be a linear centralizing map. Our first step in proving Theorem
1.2
is establishing an analog of Lemma
2.2
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r-1}a_{i}e_{i,i+1}\in S_{1}
. Then there exist
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r-3}\in F
and
\mu(A)\in\Omega
such that
f(A)=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-3}\alpha_{t}A^{t}+\mu(A)
.

Proof.

Suppose
f(J)=(x_{i,j})
. By assumption,

(2.1)

[f(J),J]=ze_{1,r}

for some
z\in F
. Fix
t
with
1\leq t\leq r-3
. It follows from
2.1
that
x_{i,i+t}-x_{i+1,i+t+1}=0
for all
i=1,\dots,r-t-1
. Therefore, there exists
\lambda_{t}\in F
such that
\lambda_{t}=x_{1,1+t}=x_{2,2+t}=\cdots=x_{r-t,r}
. Repeating this process with each possible value of
t
yields

f(J)=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-3}\lambda_{t}J^{t}+\mu(J)

for some
\mu(J)\in\Omega
.

Now let
A=B^{-1}JB\in S_{1}
and define
h:N_{r}\rightarrow N_{r}
by
h(X)=Bf(B^{-1}XB)B^{-1}
. Since
f
is centralizing we have

(2.2)

B[f(B^{-1}XB),B^{-1}XB]B^{-1}\in Z

for all
X\in N_{r}
. Expanding (
2.2
) reveals

B[f(B^{-1}XB),B^{-1}XB]B^{-1}=Bf(B^{-1}XB)B^{-1}X-XBf(B^{-1}XB)B^{-1}=[h(X),X],

hence
h
is a centralizing map on
N_{r}
. As a result, there exist
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r-3}\in F
and
\nu(J)\in\Omega
such that
h(J)=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-3}\alpha_{t}J^{t}+\nu(J)
. Thus,

\displaystyle Bf(A)B^{-1}

\displaystyle=Bf(B^{-1}JB)B^{-1}

\displaystyle=h(J)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-3}\alpha_{t}J^{t}+\nu(J)

.

Setting
\mu(A)=B^{-1}\nu(J)B
it follows that

\displaystyle f(A)

\displaystyle=B^{-1}(\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-3}\alpha_{t}J^{t}+\nu(J))B

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-3}\alpha_{t}B^{-1}J^{t}B+\mu(A)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-3}\alpha_{t}(B^{-1}JB)^{t}+\mu(A)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-3}\alpha_{t}A^{t}+\mu(A).

∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A\in S
. Then there exist
\lambda_{A}\in F
and
\mu(A)\in\Omega
such that
f(A)=\lambda_{A}A+\mu(A)
.

Proof.

Let
W_{1}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r-1}ie_{i,i+1}
and
W_{2}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r-1}(r-i)e_{i,i+1}
. It follows from Lemma
2.3
that there exist
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r-3},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r-3}\in F
and
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\Omega
such that
f(W_{1})=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-3}\alpha_{t}W_{1}^{t}+\mu_{1}
and
f(W_{2})=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-3}\beta_{t}W_{2}^{t}+\mu_{2}
.

Clearly
W_{1}+W_{2}=rJ\in S_{1}
, hence applying Lemma
2.3
and the linearity of
f
we obtain

(2.3)

\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-3}\gamma_{t}(rJ)^{t}+\mu_{3}=f(rJ)=f(W_{1})+f(W_{2})=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-3}(\alpha_{t}W_{1}^{t}+\beta_{t}W_{2}^{t})+\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}

for some
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{r-3}\in F
and
\mu_{3}\in\Omega
. Noting that

W_{1}^{t}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r-t}i(i+1)\cdots te_{i,i+t-1}\ \text{and}\ W_{2}^{t}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r-t}(r-i)(r-(i+1))\cdots(r-t)e_{i,i+t-1}

and equating entries on both sides of (
2.3
) we immediately see that
\mu_{3}=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}
and
2\gamma_{1}=\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}
.

For
1<t<r-2
the following equations hold:

(2.4)

\gamma_{t}r^{t}=\alpha_{t}t!+\beta_{t}\frac{r!}{(r-t)!}

(2.5)

\gamma_{t}r^{t}=\alpha_{t}(t+1)!+\beta_{t}\frac{(r-1)!}{(r-(t+1))!}

(2.6)

\gamma_{t}r^{t}=\alpha_{t}\frac{r!}{(r-t)!}+\beta_{t}t!

Equating (
2.4
) with (
2.6
) we have
\frac{r!}{(r-t)!}(\alpha_{t}-\beta_{t})=t!(\alpha_{t}-\beta_{t})
. Since
r!=t!(r-t)
can only occur when
t=1
or
r
, it must be the case that
\alpha_{t}=\beta_{t}
for
1<t<r-2
. Equating (
2.4
) with (
2.5
) yields
\alpha_{t}(t!+\frac{r!}{(r-t)!})=\alpha_{t}((t+1)!+\frac{(r-1)!}{(r-(t+1))!})
. As
r!\neq(t+1)!(r-t)!+(r-t)(r-1)!-t!(r-t)!
for our choice of
t
, it follows that
\alpha_{t}=0=\beta_{t}
for
1<t<r-2
. Therefore,
f(J)=1/r\cdot f(rJ)=1/r\cdot\left(\gamma_{1}rJ+\mu_{3}\right)=\gamma_{1}J+\mu(J)
where
\mu(J)=1/r\cdot\mu_{3}
.

Given
A=B^{-1}JB\in S
, define
h:N_{r}\rightarrow N_{r}
by
h(X)=Bf(B^{-1}XB)B^{-1}
. As in the proof of Lemma
2.3
,
h(X)
is a centralizing map and so there exists
\lambda\in F
and
\nu_{J}\in\Omega
such that
h(J)=\lambda J+\nu(J)
. Therefore,
f(A)=B^{-1}h(J)B=\lambda A+\mu(A)
where
\mu(A)=B^{-1}\nu(J)B
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
A,B\in S_{1}
be linearly independent. As
f
is centralizing, we have
[f(A+B),A+B]\in Z
. Expanding via linearity of
f
then yields

[f(A),A]+[f(A),B]+[f(B),A]+[f(B),B]\in Z,

hence
[f(A),B]+[f(B),A]\in Z
. By Lemma
2.4
we have
f(A)=\lambda_{A}A+\mu(A)
and
f(B)=\lambda_{B}B+\mu(B)
for some
\lambda_{A},\lambda_{B}\in F
and
\mu(A),\mu(B)\in\Omega
. Thus,

[\lambda_{A}A,B]+[\mu(A),B]+[\lambda_{B}B,A]+[\mu(B),A]\in Z.

As it is clear that
X\Omega\subset Z
and
\Omega X\subset Z
for any
X\in N_{r}
, it follows that

[\lambda_{A}A,B]+[\lambda_{B}B,A]=(\lambda_{A}-\lambda_{B})[A,B]\in Z.

But
A,B\in S_{1}
, so

[A,B]=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r-2}a_{i,i+1}b_{i+1,i+2}e_{i,i+2}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r-2}b_{i,i+1}a_{i+1,i+2}e_{i,i+2}

which lies in
Z
if and only if
[A,B]=0
. This is not possible due to Lemma
2.2
as we chose
A,B
to be linearly independent. It must then be the case that
\lambda_{A}=\lambda_{B}
. Extending to a basis for
S_{1}
we obtain
\lambda\in F
such that
f(X)=\lambda X+\mu(X)
for all
X\in S_{1}
.

Now let
C=T_{i,j}^{-1}JT_{i,j}\in S_{2}
and
W_{1}=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-1}te_{t,t+1}
. Via a similar argument we obtain

[\lambda W_{1},C]+[\lambda_{C}C,W_{1}]\in Z,

hence
(\lambda-\lambda_{C})[W_{1},C]\in Z
. A simple calculation reveals

[W_{1},C]=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{r-2}(-e_{t,t+2})-(i-2)e_{i-2,j}+(i+j-1)e_{i-1,j+1}-(j+1)e_{i,j+2},

which does not lie in
Z
for any valid choice of
i,j
. Thus
\lambda_{C}=\lambda
. Extending to a basis for
N_{r}
via the linearity of
f
we obtain
f(X)=\lambda X+\mu(X)
for all
X\in N_{r}
. Additivity of
\mu
follows from observing that

\lambda(X+Y)+\mu(X+Y)=f(X+Y)=\lambda(X+Y)+\mu(X)+\mu(Y).

∎

3.
The Map
\mu

Let
f:N_{r}\rightarrow N_{r}
be a commuting map. Then by Theorem
1.1
we can find
\lambda\in F
and additive
\mu:N_{r}\rightarrow\Omega
such that

(3.1)

f(x)=\lambda x+\mu(x)

for all
x\in N_{r}
. Rearranging (
3.1
) we obtain
\mu(x)=f(x)-\lambda x
for all
x\in N_{r}
, hence it becomes immediately clear that

(3.2)

a\mu(x)=a(f(x)-\lambda x)=f(ax)-\lambda ax=\mu(ax)

for all
a\in F
. Additionally, the commuting map assumption on
f
indicates that

(3.3)

0=[f(x),x]=[\lambda x+\mu(x),x]=[\mu(x),x].

Therefore,
\mu
is itself a linear commuting map on
N_{r}
.

Consider the map
g:N_{r}\rightarrow N_{r}
defined by

g(X)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&x_{1,2}&0\\
&0&\cdots&0&x_{r-1,r}\\
&&0&\cdots&0\\
&&&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&&&0\end{pmatrix}.

As shown by the author in Example 2 of
[
3
]
,
g(x)
is a commuting linear map whose image is contained in
\Omega
. Note that given any
a\in F
and additive map
\eta:N_{r}\rightarrow F
, the map
h(X):=ag(X)+\eta(X)
will define an additive commuting map whose image lies in
\Omega
. We claim that
\mu
must be of this form.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mu:N_{r}\rightarrow\Omega
be a linear commuting map. Then there exist
a\in F
and additive
\zeta:N_{r}\rightarrow F
such that

\mu(X)=a(x_{1,2}e_{1,r-1}+x_{r-1,r}e_{2,r})+\zeta(X)

for all
X\in N_{r}
.

Proof.

Since
\mu
is linear there exist polynomials
\mu_{i}\in F[x_{i,j}]
,
i=1,2,3
, such that

\mu(X)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&\mu_{1}(X)&\mu_{3}(X)\\
&0&\cdots&0&\mu_{2}(X)\\
&&\ddots&&0\\
&&&&\vdots\\
&&&&0\end{pmatrix}

for all
X\in N_{r}
and each monomial involved in
\mu_{i}
has degree one. As
\mu
is also commuting we have

(3.4)

0=[\mu(X),X]=(\mu_{1}(X)x_{r-1,r}-x_{1,2}\mu_{2}(X))e_{1,r}

for all
X\in N_{r}
.
Thus,
\mu_{1}(X)x_{r-1,r}=x_{1,2}\mu_{2}(X)
, meaning there must exist
a\in F
such that
\mu_{1}(X)=ax_{1,2}
and
\mu_{2}(X)=ax_{r-1,r}
for all
X\in N_{r}
. Defining
\zeta:N_{r}\rightarrow Z(N_{r})
by
\zeta(X)=\mu_{3}(X)e_{1,r}
we obtain

\mu(X)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&ax_{1,2}&0\\
&0&\cdots&0&ax_{r-1,r}\\
&&\ddots&&0\\
&&&&\vdots\\
&&&&0\end{pmatrix}+\zeta(X)=a(x_{1,2}e_{1,r-1}+x_{r-1,r}e_{2,r})+\zeta(X)

for all
X\in N_{r}
. To see that
\zeta
is additive, note that

0=\mu(X+Y)-\mu(X)-\mu(Y)=\zeta(X+Y)-\zeta(X)-\zeta(Y).

∎

Utilizing Theorem
3.1
we obtain the following immediate corollary to Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
N_{r}=N_{r}(F)
be the ring of
r\times r
strictly upper triangular matrices,
r\geq 4
, over a field
F
of characteristic zero and suppose
f:N_{r}\rightarrow N_{r}
is a linear commuting map. Then there exist
\lambda,a\in F
and a linear map
\zeta:N_{r}\rightarrow Z(N_{r})
such that

f(X)=\lambda X+a(x_{1,2}e_{1,r-1}+x_{r-1,r}e_{2,r})+\zeta(X)

for all
X\in N_{r}
.

Remark 3.3
.

For a simple unital ring
R
, a commuting map
f
must be of the standard form
f(x)=\lambda x+\mu(x)
for some
\lambda\in Z(R)
and additive
\mu:R\rightarrow Z(R)
. Thus, Corollary
3.2
indicates that a commuting map
f
on
N_{r}
is of the standard form if and only if
a=0
.
