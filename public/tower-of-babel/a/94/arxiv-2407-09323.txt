Abstract
. We obtain polynomial decay rates for C 0 \displaystyle C_{0} -semigroups, assuming that the resolvent grows polynomially at infinity in the complex right half-plane. Our results do not require the semigroup to be uniformly bounded, and for unbounded semigroups we improve upon previous results by, for example, removing a logarithmic loss on non-Hilbertian Banach spaces.

Primary 47D06. Secondary 35B40, 42B37, 46B20

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Setting

We study the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the abstract Cauchy problem

(1.1)

\displaystyle\dot{u}(t)

\displaystyle=Au(t)\quad(t\geq 0),

\displaystyle u(0)

\displaystyle=x,

on a Banach space
\displaystyle X
. We assume that (
1.1
) is well posed, so that the solution operators form a
\displaystyle C_{0}
-semigroup
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}\subseteq\mathcal{L}(X)
of bounded operators, with generator
\displaystyle A
. Throughout, we will consider
\displaystyle A
satisfying
\displaystyle\overline{{\mathbb{C}}_{+}}\subseteq\rho(A)
, where
\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}_{+}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid\Real(z)>0\}
and
\displaystyle\rho(A):={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma(A)
is the resolvent set of
\displaystyle A
. Under these assumptions, there are two well-known flavors of results that relate information about the resolvent operators
\displaystyle R(\lambda,A):=(\lambda-A)^{-1}
,
\displaystyle\lambda\in\rho(A)
, to asymptotic behavior of the semigroup orbits.

Firstly, the classical Gearhart–Huang–Prüss–Greiner theorem
[
13
,
17
,
23
]
says that, if
\displaystyle X
is a Hilbert space, then the semigroup
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
is uniformly stable, and all orbits decay exponentially to zero, if and only if

(1.2)

\sup_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}}\|R(\lambda,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}<\infty.

Versions of this theorem on non-Hilbertian Banach spaces were discovered later
[
36
,
37
,
35
]
. Here an assumption such as (
1.2
) typically guarantees exponential decay only for sufficiently smooth initial data, with the degree of smoothness depending on the geometry of the underlying Banach space. It is relevant to note that all these results make no a priori assumptions on the growth of the semigroup; only spectral information is required.

On the other hand, a more recent line of research considers the setting where the resolvent is not bounded on the right half-plane, but instead blows up along the imaginary axis at a specified rate. In this case the semigroup is not uniformly stable, and one can at best hope to obtain uniform decay rates for sufficiently smooth initial data. Semigroups with these properties arise naturally in the study of the damped wave equation

(1.3)

\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)=\Delta_{g}u(t,x)-a(x)\partial_{t}u(t,x)\quad((t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times M),

on a Riemannian manifold
\displaystyle(M,g)
, where
\displaystyle a\in C(M)

[
24
,
25
,
21
,
7
,
1
,
8
]
. A succession of results in semigroup theory
[
3
,
5
,
6
,
4
,
29
]
has elucidated the relationship between the rate of resolvent blowup and the rate of decay of classical solutions to (
1.1
), in the case where the semigroup is a priori assumed to be uniformly bounded. The latter assumption is in turn satisfied if the damping function
\displaystyle a
in (
1.3
) is non-negative.

However, when considering functions
\displaystyle a
in (
1.3
) that change sign, the associated semigroup need not be uniformly bounded and one may encounter unexpected spectral behavior (see e.g.
[
26
,
30
]
).
Moreover, polynomially growing semigroups appear naturally in the analysis of Schrödinger operators with unbounded potentials
[
10
,
15
]
, perturbed wave equations
[
14
,
22
]
, delay differential equations
[
33
]
and hyperbolic equations on non-Hilbertian Banach spaces
[
9
,
28
]
.

Hence it is natural to wonder what can be said when one combines some of the difficulties of both the lines of research mentioned above, that is, if the semigroup
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
is not uniformly bounded and the resolvent is not uniformly bounded on the right half-plane. This is the setting that will be considered in this article.

1.2.
Previous work

Throughout, we suppose that
\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\subseteq\rho(A)
and that there exist
\displaystyle\beta,C\geq 0
such that

\|R(\lambda,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\leq C(1+|\lambda|)^{\beta}\quad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

It then follows that
\displaystyle\overline{{\mathbb{C}}_{+}}\subseteq\rho(A)
, but unless
\displaystyle\beta=0
, i.e. unless (
1.2
) holds, the resolvent might blow up along the imaginary axis, with polynomial rate at most
\displaystyle O(|\lambda|^{\beta})
. As in the work for uniformly bounded semigroups mentioned above, one hopes to derive polynomial rates of decay for semigroup orbits with sufficiently smooth initial data.

In this regard, it was first shown in
[
3
]
that, on general Banach spaces, for each
\displaystyle\rho\geq 0
and
\displaystyle\tau>(\rho+1)\beta+1
one has

(1.4)

\|T(t)x\|_{X}\lesssim t^{-\rho}\|x\|_{D((-A)^{\tau})}

for all
\displaystyle t\geq 1
and
\displaystyle x\in D((-A)^{\tau})
.
Later,
[
31
]
improved this estimate under additional geometric assumptions on the underlying Banach space. Namely, if
\displaystyle X
has Fourier type
\displaystyle p\in[1,2]
(see Section
2.1
), then (
1.4
) holds for each
\displaystyle\tau>(\rho+1)\beta+\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}
. Moreover, if
\displaystyle p=2
, i.e. if
\displaystyle X
is a Hilbert space, then one may let
\displaystyle\tau=(\rho+1)\beta
. However, it was left as an open question whether one may also let
\displaystyle\tau=(\rho+1)\beta+\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}
for Banach spaces with Fourier type
\displaystyle p\in[1,2)
(see also
[
27
, Appendix B]
).

Recently, it was shown in
[
32
]
that the results from
[
31
]
regarding (
1.4
) can in fact be improved. More precisely, for each
\displaystyle\rho>0
and
\displaystyle\sigma>\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}

one has

(1.5)

\|T(t)x\|_{X}\lesssim t^{-\rho}\log(t)^{\sigma}\|x\|_{D((-A)^{\tau})}

for
\displaystyle t\geq 2
and
\displaystyle x\in D((-A)^{\tau})
, where
\displaystyle\tau=(\rho+1)\beta+\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}
. That is, for
\displaystyle\rho>0
and
\displaystyle p\in[1,2)
, (
1.5
) attains the missing endpoint exponent from
[
31
]
, up to a logarithmic loss. In fact,
[
32
]
combined methods from
[
31
]
with ones from the theory for bounded semigroups in
[
4
]
and considered resolvents with more general growth behavior, but specializing to polynomially growing resolvents leads to (
1.5
).

Finally, it is important to emphasize that the results from
[
31
]
and
[
32
]
are far from optimal if the semigroup
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
is uniformly bounded. Indeed, in this case
[
5
]
yields, on general Banach spaces and for all
\displaystyle\rho\geq 0
,

(1.6)

\|T(t)x\|_{X}\lesssim t^{-\rho}\log(t)^{\rho}\|x\|_{D((-A)^{\tau})}

for
\displaystyle t\geq 2
and
\displaystyle x\in D((-A)^{\tau})
, where
\displaystyle\tau=\rho\beta
. Moreover, by
[
6
]
, if
\displaystyle X
is a Hilbert space then the logarithmic factor in (
1.6
) can be removed, yielding (
1.4
) for
\displaystyle\tau=\rho\beta
.
On the other hand, for unbounded semigroups on Hilbert spaces and for
\displaystyle\rho=0
one cannot in general expect to obtain (
1.4
) for
\displaystyle\tau<(\rho+1)\beta
, as follows from an example of Wrobel (see
[
38
, Example 4.1]
and
[
31
, Example 4.20]
). We also refer to
[
31
, Section 4.7.1]
for an application to polynomially growing semigroups of the combination of (
1.6
) and a rescaling argument.

1.3.
Main result

For
\displaystyle\tau>0
and
\displaystyle q\in[1,\infty]
, we will work with the real interpolation space
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,q):=(X,D(A^{m}))_{\tau/m,q}
, where
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}
with
\displaystyle m>\tau
is arbitrary (see also (
2.7
)). Moreover, we refer to (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) for the definitions of Hardy–Littlewood type and Hardy–Littlewood cotype, respectively. The following is our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\displaystyle A
be the generator of a
\displaystyle C_{0}
-semigroup
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
on a Banach space
\displaystyle X
with Fourier type
\displaystyle p\in[1,2]
. Suppose that
\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\subseteq\rho(A)
, and that there exist

\displaystyle\beta>0
and

\displaystyle C\geq 0
such that

(1.7)

\|R(\lambda,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\leq C(1+|\lambda|)^{\beta}\quad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

Let
\displaystyle\rho\geq 0
and set
\displaystyle\tau:=(\rho+1)\beta+\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}
. Then there exists a
\displaystyle C_{\rho}\geq 0
such that

(1.8)

\|T(t)x\|_{X}\leq C_{\rho}t^{-\rho}\|x\|_{D_{A}(\tau,p)}

for all
\displaystyle t\geq 1
and
\displaystyle x\in D_{A}(\tau,p)
.
If
\displaystyle\rho>0
, then (
1.8
) also holds with
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,p)
replaced by
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,q)
for any
\displaystyle q\in[1,\infty]
, or by
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\tau})
.

Suppose, additionally, that
\displaystyle p>1
and that
\displaystyle X
has Hardy–Littlewood type
\displaystyle p
or Hardy–Littlewood cotype
\displaystyle p^{\prime}
. Then, for
\displaystyle\rho=0
, (
1.8
) also holds with
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,p)
replaced by
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\tau})
.

The first two statements of Theorem
1.1
are contained in the main text as Theorem
3.3
, while the last statement is Theorem
4.2
.

Given that any Banach space has Fourier type
\displaystyle p=1
, the first part of Theorem
1.1
applies to general Banach spaces. For
\displaystyle p\in(1,2]
, the assumptions on
\displaystyle X
in Theorem
1.1
are satisfied in particular if
\displaystyle X
is isomorphic to a closed subspace of
\displaystyle L^{r}(\Omega)
, for
\displaystyle\Omega
a measure space and
\displaystyle r=p
or
\displaystyle r=p^{\prime}
(see Section
2.1
).

For
\displaystyle p\in[1,2)
, the first part of Theorem
1.1
improves (
1.5
) by removing the logarithmic factor for
\displaystyle\rho>0
, and it yields an endpoint result for
\displaystyle\rho=0
.
The second part of Theorem
1.1
in turn fully extends (
1.4
) to
\displaystyle\rho=0
and
\displaystyle\tau=\beta+\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}
, under additional geometric assumptions. Also note that, for all
\displaystyle p\in[1,2]
and
\displaystyle\rho>0
, (
1.8
) involves a larger space of initial data than considered in
[
31
]
and
[
32
]
, since
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\tau})\subseteq D_{A}(\tau,\infty)
. On the other hand, for
\displaystyle\rho=0
, (
1.8
) complements the main result of
[
31
]
on Hilbert spaces, since in general one neither has
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\tau})\subseteq D_{A}(\tau,2)
nor
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,2)\subseteq D((-A)^{\tau})
.

The exponent
\displaystyle\tau
in Theorem
1.1
is sharp for
\displaystyle p=2
and
\displaystyle\rho=0
, as noted above, and for general
\displaystyle p\in[1,2]
as
\displaystyle\beta\to 0
, as follows from a modification of an example of Arendt concerning exponential stability (see
[
2
, Example 5.1.11]
and
[
37
, Section 4]
). We do not know whether, for a general Banach space with Fourier type
\displaystyle p\in[1,2)
and for
\displaystyle\rho=0
, (
1.8
) also holds with
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,p)
replaced by
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\tau})
.

For any
\displaystyle C_{0}
-semigroup
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
there exists an
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(1.9)

\|T(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\lesssim e^{\omega t}\quad(t\geq 0).

As already noted, only the case
\displaystyle\omega>0
will be of interest in this article. However, it follows from (
1.9
) that (
1.7
) holds whenever
\displaystyle\Real(\lambda)\geq\omega_{0}
, for
\displaystyle\omega_{0}>\omega
. Moreover, (
1.7
) directly extends to
\displaystyle\lambda\in i\mathbb{R}
as well.
Hence (
1.7
) is in fact an assumption on the growth of the resolvent as
\displaystyle\lambda
tends to infinity in the strip
\displaystyle\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid 0\leq\Real(\lambda)\leq\omega_{0}\}
.

One may weaken assumption (
1.7
) somewhat, by requiring instead that

(1.10)

\|R(\lambda,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\lesssim(1+|\lambda|)^{\beta_{0}}\quad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+})

for
some

\displaystyle\beta_{0}>0
, and that

\|R(i\xi,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\lesssim(1+|\xi|)^{\beta}\quad(\xi\in\mathbb{R}).

Then the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
still holds, and the specific value of
\displaystyle\beta_{0}
in (
1.10
) plays no role. Indeed, the place in the proof of Theorem
1.1
where one genuinely uses polynomial resolvent bounds for
\displaystyle\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
is in the proofs of Theorems
3.2
and
4.2
, to obtain a dense subset of initial values for which the semigroup orbits are integrable,
and there the value of
\displaystyle\beta_{0}
is irrelevant. Instead, as in the theory for uniformly bounded semigroups, to obtain concrete rates of decay we work with the behavior of the resolvent on the imaginary axis.

As in
[
31
,
32
]
, our techniques in principle also allow for
\displaystyle A
to have a singularity at zero. More precisely, one could suppose that (
1.7
) holds for
\displaystyle|\lambda|\geq 1
, and that there exists an
\displaystyle\alpha>0
such that
\displaystyle\|R(\lambda,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\lesssim|\lambda|^{-\alpha}
for
\displaystyle|\lambda|<1
. In this case one has to assume additionally that
\displaystyle-A
is an injective sectorial operator (see Remark
2.3
), and the initial values have to be restricted to the range of a suitable fractional power of
\displaystyle-A
. For simplicity, we will not consider such a setting in this article.

1.4.
The strategy of the proof

Our approach is similar to that in
[
31
]
(see also
[
27
]
), applying Fourier multiplier theory to the resolvent on the imaginary axis. However, whereas
[
31
]
mostly involved Fourier multipliers from
\displaystyle L^{p}(\mathbb{R};Y)
to
\displaystyle L^{q}(\mathbb{R};X)
for suitable
\displaystyle 1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
and
\displaystyle Y\subseteq X
, in the present article we proceed differently.

Namely, the first part of Theorem
1.1
is proved using Proposition
2.2
, which considers multipliers between the Besov space
\displaystyle B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};Y)
and
\displaystyle L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};X)
, for suitable values of
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle s
. Working with such multipliers allows us to obtain endpoint estimates. In turn, Besov spaces are intimately connected to the real interpolation method, and in Proposition
2.5
we show that real interpolation spaces can also be used effectively to cancel out resolvent growth, as is required to satisfy the conditions of our Fourier multiplier theorems. This somewhat different approach also necessitates other changes to the setup from
[
31
]
.

On the other hand, for the second part of Theorem
1.1
we consider Fourier multipliers between weighted spaces
\displaystyle L^{p}(\mathbb{R},w;Y)
and
\displaystyle L^{q}(\mathbb{R},v;X)
, for suitable weights
\displaystyle w
and
\displaystyle v
. This setting allows us to obtain endpoint results involving fractional domains, at the cost of having to make a priori assumptions about the mapping properties of the Fourier transform between such weighted spaces.

1.5.
Organization

In Section
2
we collect some preliminaries on the vector-valued Fourier transform, vector-valued Besov spaces, and interpolation spaces associated with semigroup generators. In Section
3
we then prove the first part of Theorem
1.1
, and in Section
4
we prove the final statement in Theorem
1.1
.

1.6.
Notation and terminology

The natural numbers are
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
, and
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. We write
\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}_{+}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid\Real(z)>0\}
for the open complex right half-plane.

For
\displaystyle p\in[1,\infty]
and
\displaystyle w:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
measurable, we denote by
\displaystyle L^{p}(\mathbb{R},w;X)
the Bochner space of equivalence classes of strongly measurable,
\displaystyle p
-integrable,
\displaystyle X
-valued functions on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
with respect to the weight
\displaystyle w
, endowed with the norm

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R},w;X)}:=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|f(x)\|_{X}^{p}w(x)\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{1/p}

for
\displaystyle f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R},w;X)
. We simply denote this space by
\displaystyle L^{p}(\mathbb{R};X)
when
\displaystyle w\equiv 1
. For
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, the weight
\displaystyle w_{\gamma}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is given by

(1.11)

w_{\gamma}(x):=|x|^{\gamma}\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}).

The Hölder conjugate
\displaystyle p^{\prime}\in[1,\infty]
of
\displaystyle p\in[1,\infty]
is defined by
\displaystyle 1=\tfrac{1}{p}+\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}
. We write
\displaystyle{\mathbf{1}}_{S}
for the indicator function of a set
\displaystyle S
.

The space of bounded operators between complex Banach spaces
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
is
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X):=\mathcal{L}(X,X)
.
The domain of a closed operator
\displaystyle A
on
\displaystyle X
is
\displaystyle D(A)
.

We use the notation
\displaystyle f(s)\lesssim g(s)

to indicate that
\displaystyle f(s)\leq Cg(s)
for all
\displaystyle s
and a constant
\displaystyle C\geq 0
independent of
\displaystyle s
, and similarly for
\displaystyle f(s)\gtrsim g(s)
and
\displaystyle f(s)\eqsim g(s)
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we first collect some basic definitions involving the vector-valued Fourier transform, and then we introduce Besov spaces and state two results which will be needed to prove the first half of Theorem
1.1
. Finally, we collect background on interpolation spaces and we prove two key results about them.

2.1.
The Fourier transform

Let
\displaystyle X
be a Banach space. The class of
\displaystyle X
-valued Schwartz functions on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
is denoted by
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R};X)
, and the space of
\displaystyle X
-valued tempered distributions by
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R};X)
. The Fourier transform of
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R};X)
is denoted by
\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}f
or
\displaystyle\widehat{f}
. If
\displaystyle f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};X)
then

{\mathcal{F}}f(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\xi t}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\quad(\xi\in\mathbb{R}).

One says that
\displaystyle X
has
Fourier type

\displaystyle p\in[1,2]
if
\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}:L^{p}(\mathbb{R};X)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};X)
is bounded.
Every Banach space
\displaystyle X
has Fourier type
\displaystyle 1
, and
\displaystyle X
has Fourier type
\displaystyle 2
if and only if
\displaystyle X
is isomorphic to a Hilbert space (see
[
20
]
).

We say that
\displaystyle X
has
Hardy–Littlewood type

\displaystyle p\in(1,2]
if

(2.1)

{\mathcal{F}}:L^{p}(\mathbb{R};X)\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R},w_{p-2};X)

is bounded, where
\displaystyle w_{\gamma}
is defined in (
1.11
) for
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,
\displaystyle X
has
Hardy–Littlewood cotype

\displaystyle q\in[2,\infty)
if

(2.2)

{\mathcal{F}}:L^{q}(\mathbb{R},w_{q-2};X)\to L^{q}(\mathbb{R};X)

is bounded. Note that, if
\displaystyle X={\mathbb{C}}
, then (
2.1
) is the Hardy–Littlewood inequality. In the latter case, and in fact for any Hilbert space
\displaystyle X
, (
2.1
) holds for all
\displaystyle p\in(1,2]
, and (
2.2
) for all
\displaystyle q\in[2,\infty)
.

If
\displaystyle X
has Fourier type
\displaystyle p_{0}\in(1,2]
, then
\displaystyle X
has Hardy–Littlewood type
\displaystyle p
for all
\displaystyle p\in(1,p_{0})
, and Hardy–Littlewood cotype for all
\displaystyle q\in(p_{0}^{\prime},\infty)
(see
[
11
, Proposition 3.5]
). Also, if
\displaystyle X
is a Banach lattice which is
\displaystyle p
-convex and
\displaystyle p
-concave with
\displaystyle p\in(1,\infty)
, then
\displaystyle X
has Fourier type
\displaystyle p
and Hardy–Littlewood type
\displaystyle p
if
\displaystyle p\leq 2
, and Fourier type
\displaystyle p^{\prime}
and Hardy–Littlewood cotype
\displaystyle p
if
\displaystyle p\geq 2
(see
[
12
, Proposition 2.2]
and
[
11
, Proposition 6.9]
). This holds in particular if
\displaystyle X
is isomorphic to a closed subspace of
\displaystyle L^{p}(\Omega)
, for
\displaystyle\Omega
any measure space. For more on the relation between the notions of Fourier type, Hardy–Littlewood (co)type, and convexity and concavity in Banach lattices, we refer to
[
11
]
.

Let
\displaystyle Y
be a Banach space and
\displaystyle m:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{L}(Y,X)
. We say that
\displaystyle m
is
\displaystyle X
-strongly measurable
if
\displaystyle\xi\mapsto m(\xi)y
is a strongly measurable
\displaystyle X
-valued map for every
\displaystyle y\in Y
. In this article we will consider
\displaystyle m
which have the additional property that there exist
\displaystyle\alpha,C_{\alpha}\geq 0

such that
\displaystyle\|m(\xi)\|_{\mathcal{L}(Y,X)}\leq C_{\alpha}(1+|\xi|)^{\alpha}
for all
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}
. In this case, we may set

T_{m}f:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(m\widehat{f}\,)\quad(f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R};X)).

Then
\displaystyle T_{m}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R};X)\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R};Y)
is the
Fourier multiplier operator
with
symbol

\displaystyle m
.

2.2.
Besov spaces

Throughout this article, fix an inhomogeneous Littlewood–Paley sequence
\displaystyle(\phi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\subseteq C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
. That is, one has
\displaystyle\phi_{1}(\xi)=0
if
\displaystyle|\xi|\notin[1/2,2]
,
\displaystyle\phi_{k}(\xi)=\phi_{1}(2^{-k+1}\xi)
for each
\displaystyle k>1
and
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\phi_{k}(\xi)=1\quad(\xi\in\mathbb{R}).

Let
\displaystyle p,q\in[1,\infty]
and
\displaystyle s\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the Besov space
\displaystyle B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R};X)

consists of all
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R};X)
such that
\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}(\phi_{k})*f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R};X)
for each
\displaystyle k\geq 0
, and such that

\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R};X)}:=\|(2^{ks}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}(\phi_{k})*f)_{k\geq 0}\|_{\ell^{q}(L^{p}(\mathbb{R};X))}<\infty.

Then
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R};X)\subseteq B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R};X)

, by
[
19
, Proposition 14.4.3]
. Moreover, if
\displaystyle p,q<\infty
, then
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R};X)
is a dense subset of
\displaystyle B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R};X)
. Finally, we will use the simple observation that

(2.3)

B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R};X)\subseteq B^{r}_{p,1}(\mathbb{R};X)

for all
\displaystyle p,q\in[1,\infty]
and
\displaystyle s,r\in\mathbb{R}
with
\displaystyle s>r
, and that

(2.4)

B^{0}_{p,1}(\mathbb{R};X)\subseteq L^{p}(\mathbb{R};X)\subseteq B^{0}_{p,\infty}(\mathbb{R};X)

for all
\displaystyle p\in[1,\infty]
(see e.g.
[
19
, Proposition 14.4.18]
).

The following lemma will be used in the proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle p\in[1,\infty)
,
\displaystyle q\in[1,\infty]
and
\displaystyle s\in(0,1/p)
. Then there exists a
\displaystyle C\geq 0
such that
\displaystyle{\mathbf{1}}_{(0,\infty)}f\in B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R};X)
for all
\displaystyle f\in B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R};X)
, and

\|{\mathbf{1}}_{(0,\infty)}f\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R};X)}\leq C\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R};X)}.

Proof.

For
\displaystyle p>1
, the statement in fact holds for
\displaystyle s\in(-1/p,1/p)
, as is shown in
[
19
, Corollary 14.6.35]
. In the proof of the latter result, one can see that for
\displaystyle s\in(0,1/p)
one may also allow
\displaystyle p=1
.
∎

Finally, the following Fourier multiplier result,
[
19
, Proposition 14.5.7]
, is one of the key ingredients in the proof of the first half of Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
be Banach spaces with Fourier type
\displaystyle p\in[1,2]
, and let
\displaystyle m:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{L}(Y,X)
be
\displaystyle X
-strongly measurable, with
\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}}\|m(\xi)\|_{\mathcal{L}(Y,X)}<\infty
. Then

T_{m}:B^{1/p-1/p^{\prime}}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};Y)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};X)

is bounded.

2.3.
Interpolation spaces

Let
\displaystyle A
be a linear operator on a Banach space
\displaystyle X
.
For
\displaystyle\omega\in(0,\pi)
, set
\displaystyle S_{\omega}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}\mid|\arg(z)|<\omega\}
.
Then
\displaystyle-A
is a
sectorial operator
if there exists an
\displaystyle\omega\in(0,\pi)
such that
\displaystyle\sigma(-A)\subseteq\overline{S_{\omega}}
, and

(2.5)

\sup\{\|\lambda(\lambda+A)^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\,|\,\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\overline{S_{\omega^{\prime}}}\}<\infty

for each
\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}\in(\omega,\pi)
. If
\displaystyle-A
is a sectorial operator, then
the fractional power
\displaystyle(-A)^{\alpha}
is well defined for each
\displaystyle\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, cf.
[
16
, Chapter 3]
. If, additionally,
\displaystyle A
is injective, then
\displaystyle(-A)^{\alpha}
is well defined for all
\displaystyle\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Note that
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\beta})\subseteq D((-A)^{\alpha})
whenever
\displaystyle\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}
satisfies
\displaystyle\Real(\beta)>\Real(\alpha)
.

Remark 2.3
.

Throughout this article, as in Theorem
1.1
, we will consider
\displaystyle C_{0}
-semigroups
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
with generator
\displaystyle A
such that
\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\subseteq\rho(A)
and

(2.6)

\|R(\lambda,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\leq C(1+|\lambda|)^{\beta}\quad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}),

where
\displaystyle\beta,C\geq 0
are independent of
\displaystyle\lambda
. Under these assumptions,
\displaystyle-A
is a sectorial operator of angle
\displaystyle\pi/2
. Indeed, the semigroup generation property implies that
\displaystyle\|R(\lambda,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\lesssim 1/\Real(\lambda)
for
\displaystyle\Real(\lambda)
large (as follows from (
1.9
)), which gives a uniform bound in (
2.5
) for
\displaystyle|\lambda|
large if
\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}>\pi/2
. On the other hand, (
2.6
) implies that
\displaystyle\overline{{\mathbb{C}}_{+}}\subseteq\rho(A)
, which in turn yields the required bound in (
2.5
) for
\displaystyle|\lambda|
small.

Let
\displaystyle-A
be a sectorial operator on a Banach space
\displaystyle X
, and let
\displaystyle\tau\in(0,\infty)
and
\displaystyle q\in[1,\infty]
. Then the real interpolation space associated with
\displaystyle A
,
\displaystyle\tau
and
\displaystyle q
is

(2.7)

D_{A}(\tau,q):=(X,D((-A)^{\alpha}))_{\tau/\alpha,q},

where
\displaystyle\alpha\in(\tau,\infty)
is arbitrary. It follows from reiteration that
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,q)
is independent of the choice of
\displaystyle\alpha
. In particular, one has

D_{A}(\tau,q)=(X,D((-A)^{m}))_{\tau/m,q}=(X,D(A^{m}))_{\tau/m,q}

whenever
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}
satisfies
\displaystyle m>\tau
. By basic properties of interpolation spaces,
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,q)\subseteq D_{A}(\sigma,r)
if
\displaystyle\sigma<\tau
, or if
\displaystyle\sigma=\tau
and
\displaystyle r\geq q
. By
[
16
, Corollary 6.6.3]
,

(2.8)

D_{A}(\tau,1)\subseteq D((-A)^{\tau})\subseteq D_{A}(\tau,\infty)

for all
\displaystyle\tau>0
. Also,
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\alpha})
is a dense subset of
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,q)
for all
\displaystyle\alpha>\tau
and
\displaystyle q<\infty
, by
[
16
, Theorem 6.6.1]
.

Finally, if
\displaystyle X
has Fourier type
\displaystyle p\in[1,2]
and
\displaystyle A
is injective, then both
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\tau})
and
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,p)
also have Fourier type
\displaystyle p
, for all
\displaystyle\tau>0
. Indeed,

\displaystyle(-A)^{\tau}:D((-A)^{\tau})\to X
is an isomorphism, while for
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,p)
the statement follows from (
2.7
) by interpolation (see also
[
18
, Proposition 2.4.17]
).

The following proposition, connecting interpolation spaces to the Besov spaces from the previous subsection, will play a key role in the proof of part of Theorem
3.2
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\displaystyle A
be the generator of a
\displaystyle C_{0}
-semigroup
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
on a Banach space
\displaystyle X
, and suppose that
\displaystyle-A
is a sectorial operator. Let
\displaystyle M\geq 0
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
\displaystyle\|T(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\leq Me^{(\omega-1)t}
for all
\displaystyle t\geq 0
, and let
\displaystyle p\in[1,\infty)
,
\displaystyle q\in[1,\infty]
and
\displaystyle s\in(0,1/p)
. Then there exists a constant
\displaystyle C\geq 0
such that
\displaystyle[t\mapsto{\mathbf{1}}_{(0,\infty)}(t)e^{-\omega t}T(t)x]\in B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R};X)
for all
\displaystyle x\in D_{A}(s,q)
, with

\|[t\mapsto{\mathbf{1}}_{(0,\infty)}(t)e^{-\omega t}T(t)x]\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R};X)}\leq C\|x\|_{D_{A}(s,q)}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle J:X\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R};X)
be the bounded linear operator given by
\displaystyle Jx(t):=e^{-\omega|t|}T(|t|)x
, for
\displaystyle x\in X
and
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\displaystyle(Jx)^{\prime}(t)=-\text{sign}(t)e^{-\omega|t|}T(|t|)(\omega-A)x
for
\displaystyle x\in D(A)
and
\displaystyle t\neq 0
, the restricted operator
\displaystyle J:D(A)\to W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R};X)
is bounded. Real interpolation (see
[
19
, Theorem 14.4.31]
) shows that
\displaystyle J:D_{A}(s,q)\to B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R};X)
is bounded as well. Now the proof is concluded by applying Lemma
2.1
.
∎

In turn, the following proposition will be crucial for the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\displaystyle A
be be the generator of a
\displaystyle C_{0}
-semigroup
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
on a Banach space
\displaystyle X
. Suppose that
\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\subseteq\rho(A)
,
and that there exist
\displaystyle\beta>0
and
\displaystyle C\geq 0
such that

(2.9)

\|R(\lambda,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\leq C(1+|\lambda|)^{\beta}\quad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

Then
\displaystyle i\mathbb{R}\subseteq\rho(A)
, and

\sup\{\|R(i\xi,A)^{k}\|_{\mathcal{L}(D_{A}((n+1)\beta,q),X)}\mid\xi\in\mathbb{R},k\in\{0,\ldots,n+1\}\}<\infty

for all
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\displaystyle q\in[1,\infty]
.

Proof.

The required statement is trivial for
\displaystyle k=0
and

\displaystyle|\xi|<1
, so henceforth we will consider
\displaystyle k>0
and
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}
with
\displaystyle|\xi|\geq 1
.

By basic properties of resolvents, it follows from (
2.9
) that
\displaystyle i\mathbb{R}\subseteq\rho(A)
and

(2.10)

\|R(i\xi,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\leq C(1+|\xi|)^{\beta}\quad(\xi\in\mathbb{R}).

As noted in Remark
2.3
,
\displaystyle-A
is a sectorial operator. Moreover,
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\alpha})=D((1-A)^{\alpha})
for any
\displaystyle\alpha>0
, by
[
19
, Proposition 15.2.12]
. Hence, combining (
2.10
) and
[
31
, Proposition 3.4]
yields

(2.11)

\sup_{|\xi|\geq 1}\|R(i\xi,A)(-A)^{-\beta}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}<\infty.

Then, for
\displaystyle k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\displaystyle|\xi|\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|R(i\xi,A)^{k}\|_{\mathcal{L}(D((-A)^{n\beta}),X)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\eqsim\|R(i\xi,A)^{k}(-A)^{-n\beta}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\|R(i\xi,A)^{k}(-A)^{-k\beta}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\|(-A)^{-(n-k)\beta}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\lesssim 1.

Together with (
2.10
), this implies

(2.12)

\|R(i\xi,A)^{k}\|_{\mathcal{L}(D((-A)^{n\beta}),X)}\lesssim(1+|\xi|)^{\beta}\quad(k\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}).

On the other hand, another application of
[
31
, Proposition 3.4]
shows that

\|R(i\xi,A)(-A)^{-\beta-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\lesssim(1+|\xi|)^{-1}.

This, combined with (
2.11
), yields

(2.13)

\displaystyle\|R(i\xi,A)^{k}\|_{\mathcal{L}(D((-A)^{(n+1)\beta+1}),X)}\eqsim\|R(i\xi,A)^{k}(-A)^{-(n+1)\beta-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}

\displaystyle\leq\|R(i\xi,A)(-A)^{-\beta}\|^{k-1}_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\|R(i\xi,A)(-A)^{-\beta-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\|(-A)^{-\beta}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}^{n-k+1}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|\xi|)^{-1},

for all
\displaystyle k\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
and
\displaystyle|\xi|\geq 1
.

Now, by (
2.8
), (
2.12
) and (
2.13
), we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|R(i\xi,A)^{k}\|_{\mathcal{L}(D_{A}(n\beta,1),X)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|\xi|)^{\beta},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|R(i\xi,A)^{k}\|_{\mathcal{L}(D_{A}((n+1)\beta+1,1),X)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|\xi|)^{-1},

for
\displaystyle|\xi|\geq 1
and
\displaystyle k\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
. Finally, since

D_{A}((n+1)\beta,q)=(D_{A}(n\beta,1),D_{A}((n+1)\beta+1,1))_{\frac{\beta}{1+\beta},q},

interpolating these estimates yields
\displaystyle\sup_{|\xi|\geq 1}\|R(i\xi,A)^{k}\|_{\mathcal{L}(D_{A}((n+1)\beta,q),X)}<\infty
.
∎

3.
Polynomial stability on real interpolation spaces

This section is devoted to the proof of the first half of Theorem
1.1
. To this end, we need two preliminary results.

We first require the following extension of
[
31
, Proposition 3.2]
to the mixed Besov-Lebesgue setting.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle A
be the generator of a
\displaystyle C_{0}
-semigroup
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
on a Banach space
\displaystyle X
, and let
\displaystyle Y
be a Banach space that is continuously embedded in
\displaystyle X
. Suppose that
\displaystyle i\mathbb{R}\subseteq\rho(A)
, and that there exist
\displaystyle\beta>0
and
\displaystyle C\geq 0
such that

(3.1)

\|R(i\xi,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(Y,X)}\leq C(1+|\xi|)^{\beta}\quad(\xi\in\mathbb{R}).

Let
\displaystyle p\in[1,\infty)
and
\displaystyle s\in[0,\infty)
be such that either
\displaystyle s>0
, or
\displaystyle s=0
and
\displaystyle p=1
, and suppose that there exist
\displaystyle q\in[1,\infty]
and
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(3.2)

T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{j}}:B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};Y)\to L^{q}(\mathbb{R};X)

is bounded for each
\displaystyle j\in\{n-1,n\}\cap\mathbb{N}
.
Then

T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{n}}:B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};Y)\to L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};X)

is bounded.

We only assume (
3.1
) to guarantee that the Fourier multiplier operator in (
3.2
) is
well defined; the specific value of
\displaystyle\beta
plays no role here.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to that of
[
31
, Proposition 3.2]
. For the convenience of the reader, we provide the argument. It suffices to show that there exists a
\displaystyle C\geq 0
such that

(3.3)

\sup_{k\leq\sigma\leq k+1}\|T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{n}}f(\sigma)\|_{X}\leq C\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};Y)}

for every
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R};Y)
and
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}
, since
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R};Y)
is a dense subset of
\displaystyle B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};Y)
.

For each
\displaystyle j\in\{n-1,n\}\cap\mathbb{N}
there exists a
\displaystyle K_{j}\geq 0
independent of
\displaystyle f
such that

(3.4)

\|T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{j}}f\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R};X)}\leq K_{j}\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};Y)}.

Hence there exists a
\displaystyle t\in[k-1,k]
such that

(3.5)

\|T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{j}}f(t)\|_{X}\leq K_{j}\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};Y)}.

Now let
\displaystyle\tau\in[0,2]
. One can check that

(3.6)

T(\tau)T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{n}}f(t)=T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{n}}f(t+\tau)-\int_{0}^{\tau}T(r)T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{n-1}}f(t+\tau-r)\mathrm{d}r.

Hence, by (
3.5
), Hölder’s inequality and (
3.4
), for
\displaystyle n>1
one has

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{n}}f(t+\tau)\|_{X}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lesssim\|T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{n}}f(t)\|_{X}+\int_{0}^{\tau}\|T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{n-1}}f(t+\tau-r)\|_{X}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq K_{n}\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};Y)}+\tau^{1/{q^{\prime}}}\|T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{n-1}}f\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R};X)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};Y)}.

This implies (
3.3
) for
\displaystyle n>1
.

Finally, for
\displaystyle n=1
, by the assumptions on
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle s
as well as (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), one has
\displaystyle B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};Y)\subseteq B^{0}_{p,1}(\mathbb{R};Y)\subseteq L^{p}(\mathbb{R};Y)
. Hence Hölder’s inequality gives

\int_{0}^{\tau}\|f(t+\tau-r)\|_{X}\mathrm{d}r\lesssim\int_{0}^{\tau}\|f(t+\tau-r)\|_{Y}\mathrm{d}r\leq\tau^{1/p^{\prime}}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R};Y)}\lesssim\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};Y)}.

By combining this with (
3.6
) in the same manner as before, one obtains (
3.3
).
∎

We will also rely on the following version of
[
31
, Theorem 4.6]
in the mixed Besov-Lebesgue setting.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle A
be the generator of a
\displaystyle C_{0}
-semigroup
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
on a Banach space
\displaystyle X
. Suppose that
\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\subseteq\rho(A)
, and that there exist
\displaystyle\beta>0

and
\displaystyle C\geq 0

such that

(3.7)

\|R(\lambda,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\leq C(1+|\lambda|)^{\beta}\quad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

Let
\displaystyle\gamma>0
,
\displaystyle p\in[1,\infty)
and
\displaystyle s\in(0,1/p)
, and suppose that there exist
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\displaystyle q\in[1,\infty]
such that

(3.8)

T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{k}}:B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};D_{A}(\gamma,p))\to L^{q}(\mathbb{R};X)

is bounded for each
\displaystyle k\in\{n-1,n,n+1\}\cap\mathbb{N}
. Then there exists a
\displaystyle C_{n}\geq 0
such that

(3.9)

\|T(t)x\|_{X}\leq C_{n}t^{-n}\|x\|_{D_{A}(\gamma+s,p)}

for all
\displaystyle t\geq 1
and
\displaystyle x\in D_{A}(\gamma+s,p)
.

Note that (
3.7
) automatically extends to all
\displaystyle\lambda\in\overline{{\mathbb{C}}_{+}}
, so (
3.8
) is well defined. Also, as in Proposition
3.1
, the specific value of
\displaystyle\beta
in (
3.7
) plays no role.

Proof.

We want to show that
\displaystyle\|T(t)x\|_{X}\leq C_{n}t^{-n}\|x\|_{D_{A}(\gamma+s,p)}
for all
\displaystyle t\geq 1
and
\displaystyle x\in D_{A}(\gamma+s,p)
. Since
\displaystyle D(A^{l})
is dense in
\displaystyle D_{A}(\gamma+s,p)
whenever
\displaystyle l\in\mathbb{N}
satisfies
\displaystyle l>\gamma+s
, we may suppose throughout that
\displaystyle x\in D(A^{l})
for some large
\displaystyle l
.
Hence, setting
\displaystyle g(t):=t^{n}{\mathbf{1}}_{(0,\infty)}(t)T(|t|)x
for
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{R}
, by
[
31
, Proposition 4.3]
we may suppose that
\displaystyle g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};X)
. In turn,
[
31
, Lemma 3.1]
then implies that
\displaystyle\widehat{g}(\xi)=n!R(i\xi,A)^{n+1}x
for all
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.

Next, note that
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
restricts to a
\displaystyle C_{0}
-semigroup on
\displaystyle D_{A}(\gamma,p)
, the generator of which is the part of
\displaystyle A
in
\displaystyle D_{A}(\gamma,p)
, which has domain
\displaystyle D_{A}(\gamma+1,p)
. In particular, we may fix
\displaystyle M\geq 1
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\displaystyle\|T(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}(D_{A}(\gamma,p))}\leq Me^{(\omega-1)t}
for all
\displaystyle t\geq 0
. Set
\displaystyle f(t):={\mathbf{1}}_{(0,\infty)}(t)e^{-\omega t}T(|t|)x
. Then

(3.10)

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};D_{A}(\gamma,p))}\leq M\|x\|_{D_{A}(\gamma,p)}\lesssim\|x\|_{D_{A}(\gamma+s,p)}.

Moreover,

(3.11)

\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};D_{A}(\gamma,p))}\lesssim\|x\|_{(D_{A}(\gamma,p),D_{A}(\gamma+1,p))_{s,p}}\lesssim\|x\|_{D_{A}(\gamma+s,p)},

as follows from Proposition
2.4
and
[
34
, Theorem 1.10.2]
. Also, again by
[
31
, Lemma 3.1]
,
\displaystyle\widehat{f}(\xi)=R(\omega+i\xi,A)x
for all
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, if we set

m(\xi):=n!(R(i\xi,A)^{n}+\omega R(i\xi,A)^{n+1}),

then
\displaystyle m(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)=\widehat{g}(\xi)
.

By combining all this, we see that

(3.12)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq 0}\|t^{n}T(t)x\|_{X}

\displaystyle=\|T_{m}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};X)}

\displaystyle\leq n!\big(\|T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{n}}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};X)}+\omega\|T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{n+1}}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};X)}\big).

For
\displaystyle n>0
, one can apply (
3.8
) and Proposition
3.1
to the final line, and then use (
3.11
) as well, to obtain

\sup_{t\geq 0}\|t^{n}T(t)x\|_{X}\lesssim\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};D_{A}(\gamma,p))}\lesssim\|x\|_{D_{A}(\gamma+s,p)}.

For
\displaystyle n=0
, the same reasoning can be used for the second term in brackets in (
3.12
), while for the first term one can directly rely on (
3.10
), since
\displaystyle T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{0}}f=f
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the first part of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\displaystyle A
be the generator of a
\displaystyle C_{0}
-semigroup
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
on a Banach space
\displaystyle X
with Fourier type
\displaystyle p\in[1,2]
. Suppose that
\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\subseteq\rho(A)
, and that there exist

\displaystyle\beta>0
and
\displaystyle C\geq 0
such that

\|R(\lambda,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\leq C(1+|\lambda|)^{\beta}\quad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}).\\

Let
\displaystyle\rho\geq 0
and set
\displaystyle\tau:=(\rho+1)\beta+\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}
. Then there exists a
\displaystyle C_{\rho}\geq 0
such that

(3.13)

\|T(t)x\|_{X}\leq C_{\rho}t^{-\rho}\|x\|_{D_{A}(\tau,p)}

for all
\displaystyle t\geq 1
and
\displaystyle x\in D_{A}(\tau,p)
.
Moreover, if
\displaystyle\rho>0
, then (
3.13
) also holds with
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,p)
replaced by
\displaystyle D_{A}(\tau,q)
for any
\displaystyle q\in[1,\infty]
, or by
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\tau})
.

Proof.

We first consider the case where
\displaystyle\rho\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Recall that, since
\displaystyle X
has Fourier type
\displaystyle p
, so does

\displaystyle D_{A}((\rho+1)\beta,p)
.
Moreover, by Proposition
2.5
,

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}}\|R(i\xi,A)^{k}\|_{\mathcal{L}(D_{A}((\rho+1)\beta,p),X)}<\infty

for all
\displaystyle k\in\{0,\ldots,\rho+1\}
. Hence Proposition
2.2
implies that

T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{k}}:B^{1/p-1/p^{\prime}}_{p,p}(\mathbb{R};D_{A}((\rho+1)\beta,p))\to L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};X)

is bounded for every
\displaystyle k\in\{0,\ldots,\rho+1\}
. Finally, Theorem
3.2
yields (
3.13
).

To extend (
3.13
) to general
\displaystyle\rho\geq 0
we proceed as follows. Fix
\displaystyle t\geq 1
and
\displaystyle\rho>0
. Let
\displaystyle\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be such that
\displaystyle\rho_{0}<\rho<\rho_{1}
, and let
\displaystyle\theta\in(0,1)
be such that
\displaystyle\rho=(1-\theta)\rho_{0}+\theta\rho_{1}
. Set
\displaystyle\tau_{i}:=(\rho_{i}+1)\beta+\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}
for
\displaystyle i\in\{0,1\}
. Then, by what we have already shown,

\|T(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}(D_{A}(\tau_{i},p),X)}\leq C_{\rho_{i}}t^{-\rho_{i}}

for some constant
\displaystyle C_{\rho_{i}}\geq 0
independent of
\displaystyle t
. Now, due to reiteration, real interpolation with parameters
\displaystyle\theta
and
\displaystyle q\in[1,\infty]
gives

\|T(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}(D_{A}(\tau,q),X)}\leq C_{\rho}t^{-\rho}

for some
\displaystyle C_{\rho}\geq 0
independent of
\displaystyle t
. This proves both (
3.13
) and the final statement of the theorem, since
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\tau})\subseteq D_{A}(\tau,\infty)
.
∎

4.
Polynomial stability on fractional domains

This section is devoted to the proof of the final statement in Theorem
1.1
.

The following proposition will play the same role in this section that Proposition
2.2
did in the previous section. For
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, recall the definition of the weight
\displaystyle w_{\gamma}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
from (
1.11
).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle p,q\in[1,\infty]
,
\displaystyle r\in[1,\infty)
,
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle\delta\in(-\infty,1/r^{\prime})
.
Let
\displaystyle Y
be a Banach space such that

(4.1)

{\mathcal{F}}:L^{p}(\mathbb{R};Y)\to L^{r}(\mathbb{R},w_{\gamma r};Y)

is bounded, and let
\displaystyle X
be a Banach space such that

(4.2)

{\mathcal{F}}:L^{r}(\mathbb{R},w_{\delta r};X)\to L^{q}(\mathbb{R};X)

is bounded.
Let
\displaystyle m:\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathcal{L}(Y,X)
be an
\displaystyle X
-strongly measurable map for which there exists a
\displaystyle C\geq 0
such that
\displaystyle\left\|m(\xi)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(Y,X)}\leq C|\xi|^{\gamma-\delta}
for all
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
\displaystyle T_{m}:L^{p}(\mathbb{R};Y)\to L^{q}(\mathbb{R};X)
is bounded.

Proof.

Simply combine the assumptions on
\displaystyle X
,
\displaystyle m
and
\displaystyle Y
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|T_{m}f\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R};X)}\lesssim\|m\widehat{f}\,\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R},w_{\delta r};X)}\lesssim\|w_{\delta}w_{\gamma-\delta}\widehat{f}\,\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R};Y)}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R};Y)}

for all
\displaystyle f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R};Y)
. Note that
\displaystyle L^{r}(\mathbb{R},w_{\delta r};X)\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R};X)
, since
\displaystyle\delta<1/r^{\prime}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the last statement in Theorem
1.1
, as a special case of the following result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle A
be the generator of a
\displaystyle C_{0}
-semigroup
\displaystyle(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
on a Banach space
\displaystyle X
with Fourier type
\displaystyle p\in(1,2]
, and suppose that
\displaystyle X
has Hardy–Littlewood type
\displaystyle p
or Hardy–Littlewood cotype
\displaystyle p^{\prime}
. Suppose that
\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\subseteq\rho(A)
, and that there exist

\displaystyle\beta>0
and
\displaystyle C\geq 0
such that

\|R(\lambda,A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}\leq C(1+|\lambda|)^{\beta}\quad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

Let
\displaystyle\rho\geq 0
and set
\displaystyle\tau:=(\rho+1)\beta+\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}
. Then there exists a
\displaystyle C_{\rho}\geq 0
such that

\|T(t)x\|_{X}\leq C_{\rho}t^{-\rho}\|x\|_{D((-A)^{\tau})}

for all
\displaystyle t\geq 1
and
\displaystyle x\in D((-A)^{\tau})
.

Note that Theorem
4.2
is independent of Theorem
3.3
in the special case where
\displaystyle\rho=0
. For
\displaystyle\rho>0
the conclusion of Theorem
4.2
already follows from Theorem
3.3
.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to that of
[
31
, Theorem 4.9]
, and as such also similar to the proof of Theorem
3.3
. We will indicate what the key steps are.

By interpolation, cf.
[
31
, Lemma 4.2]
, it suffices to consider
\displaystyle\rho\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then, using arguments as before but relying on
[
31
, Proposition 3.4]
instead of Proposition
2.5
, one can show that

(4.3)

\|R(i\xi,A)^{k}\|_{\mathcal{L}(D((-A)^{\tau}),X)}\lesssim(1+|\xi|)^{-(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}

for all
\displaystyle k\in\{1,\ldots,\rho+1\}
and an implicit constant independent of
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.

Next, note that
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\tau})
has the same Fourier type and Hardy–Littlewood type and cotype as
\displaystyle X
, because
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle D((-A)^{\tau})
are isomorphic. In particular, if
\displaystyle X
has Hardy–Littlewood type
\displaystyle p
, then one may apply Proposition
4.1
with
\displaystyle r=p
,
\displaystyle q=p^{\prime}
,
\displaystyle\gamma=\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\tfrac{1}{p}
and
\displaystyle\delta=0
. On the other hand, if
\displaystyle X
has Hardy–Littlewood cotype
\displaystyle p^{\prime}
, then one can apply Proposition
4.1
with
\displaystyle q=r=p^{\prime}
,
\displaystyle\gamma=0
and
\displaystyle\delta=\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}
. In both cases, it follows from (
4.3
) that

T_{R(i\cdot,A)^{k}}:L^{p}(\mathbb{R};D((-A)^{\tau}))\to L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};X)

is bounded for all
\displaystyle k\in\{1,\ldots,\rho+1\}
. Now
[
31
, Theorem 4.6]
concludes the proof.
∎
