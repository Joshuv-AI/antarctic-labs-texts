Avoiding Monochromatic Solutions to 3-term Equations
Kevin P. Costello, Gabriel Elvin

Abstract
Given an equation, the integers [ n ] = { 1 , 2 , … , n } [n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\} as inputs, and the colors red and blue, how can we color [ n ] [n] in order to minimize the number of monochromatic solutions to the equation, and what is the minimum? The answer is only known for a handful of equations, but much progress has been made on improving upper and lower bounds on minima for various equations. A well-studied characteristic an equation, which has its roots in graph Ramsey theory, is to determine if the minimum number of monochromatic solutions can be achieved (asymptotically) by uniformly random colorings. Such equations are called common . We prove that no 3-term equations are common and provide a lower bound for a specific class of 3-term equations.

c
are relatively prime.
Fix a coloring
f:[n]\to\{-1,1\}
, and let

R=f^{-1}(\{1\})
and
B=f^{-1}(\{-1\})

denote the red and blue elements, respectively.
We will actually show there are
\Omega(n^{2})
monochromatic
solutions just among the multiples of
c

R^{\prime}=R\cap c\mathbb{Z}
and
B^{\prime}=B\cap c\mathbb{Z}
.
Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be small, positive constants
possibly depending on
a
and
c
to
be determined later.

Claim 6
.

If
|R^{\prime}|\leq C_{1}n
for sufficiently small

, then there
are
\Omega(n^{2})
blue solutions.

Proof.

There are
\Omega(n^{2})
total solutions involving only
multiples of
c
.
Since each number in
R^{\prime}
is present in at most
3n
solutions,
by assumption there are at most
3C_{1}n^{2}
solutions with
an input from
R^{\prime}
.
If we make
C_{1}
small enough,
this still leaves
\Omega(n^{2})
solutions with inputs
exclusively from
B^{\prime}
.
∎

By this claim, we may assume
|R^{\prime}|,|B^{\prime}|\geq C_{1}n
.
Now we will cover some necessary notation.
Let
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
.
The
sum set
of two sets
X
and
Y
,
denoted
X+Y
, is

X+Y=\{x+y\,:\,x\in X,y\in Y\}.

(21)

The basic outline of our argument is as follows:
if the sum sets
R^{\prime}+R^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}+B^{\prime}
are both large,
then they will
have a nontrivial intersection,
and if one of these sum sets is small,
then
[n]\cap c\mathbb{Z}
will contain large
monochromatic arithmetic progressions,
and both cases imply there will be
\Omega(n^{2})

monochromatic solutions.
Rather than use sum sets,
We will use the
robust
sum sets
defined in
[
15
]
:
given a subset
\Gamma\subseteq X\times Y
,
let

X+_{\Gamma}Y\coloneqq\{x+y\,:\,(x,y)\in\Gamma\}.

(22)

For
A\in\{R^{\prime},B^{\prime}\}
,
let
\Gamma=\Gamma(A)
be the set
of all pairs in
A\times A
whose sum
has at least
C_{2}n
distinct
representations as a sum of pairs, i.e.

\Gamma=\{(a_{1},a_{2})\in A\times A\,:\,|\{(b_{1},b_{2})\in A\times A\,:\,b_{1}+b_{2}=a_{1}+a_{2}\}|\geq C_{2}n\}.

(23)

We will first show that if
the robust sum sets in question are large,
then we will have
\Omega(n^{2})
monochromatic solutions.
Let
\epsilon>0
. We leave it arbitrary for now,
but later we will pick a specific
\epsilon

which depends on
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.

Claim 7
.

If
|A+_{\Gamma(A)}A|\geq(2+\epsilon)|A|

for

, then

|(R^{\prime}+_{\Gamma(R^{\prime})}R^{\prime})\cap(B^{\prime}+_{\Gamma(B^{\prime})}B^{\prime})|=\Omega(n),

(24)

which implies there are
\Omega(n^{2})
monochromatic solutions.

Proof.

We have

|R^{\prime}+_{\Gamma(R^{\prime})}R^{\prime}|+|B^{\prime}+_{\Gamma(B^{\prime})}B^{\prime}|\geq(2+\epsilon)(|R^{\prime}|+|B^{\prime}|)=(2+\epsilon)\left\lfloor\frac{n}{c}\right\rfloor,

and since
A+_{\Gamma(A)}A\subseteq c\mathbb{Z}\cap[2n]

(which has only
\lfloor 2n/c\rfloor
elements),
(
24
) follows
from the Inclusion-Exclusion Principle.

Note that by construction

v\in(R^{\prime}+_{\Gamma(R^{\prime})}R^{\prime})\cap(B^{\prime}+_{\Gamma(R^{\prime})}B^{\prime})

corresponds to at least
C_{2}n

monochromatic solutions: if
v
is colored red,
each distinct representation will correspond
to a red solution, and similarly if
v
is colored blue.
Since there are
\Omega(n)
such

,
we have
\Omega(n^{2})
monochromatic solutions.
∎

Because of the above claim,
we may now assume that one of the robust sum sets
is not too large. Without loss of generality,
suppose

|R^{\prime}+_{\Gamma(R^{\prime})}R^{\prime}|<(2+\epsilon)|R^{\prime}|.

(25)

We are now in a position to use the previously
mentioned structure theorem
[

]
.
Rather than state the theorem verbatim,
we state only what we need for this scenario.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\epsilon>0
. Suppose
|R^{\prime}|\geq\max\{3,2\epsilon^{-1/2}\}
,
and let
\Gamma\subseteq R^{\prime}\times R^{\prime}
be a subset with

|\Gamma|\geq(1-\epsilon)|R^{\prime}|^{2}

|R^{\prime}+_{\Gamma}R^{\prime}|<(1+\theta-11\epsilon^{1/2})|R^{\prime}|
, where
\theta=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
,
then there is an arithmetic progression
P
with

|P|\leq|R^{\prime}+_{\Gamma}R^{\prime}|-(1-5\epsilon^{1/2})|R^{\prime}|
,

|R^{\prime}\cap P|\geq(1-\epsilon^{1/2})|R^{\prime}|
.

With (
25
),
R^{\prime}+_{\Gamma}R^{\prime}

is small enough to fit the corresponding assumption to the theorem.
We also have an appropriate lower bound on
\Gamma
.
To see this, note the following claim.

Claim 9
.

There are at most
\epsilon|R^{\prime}|^{2}
pairs in
R^{\prime}\times R^{\prime}-\Gamma
,
where
\epsilon=\frac{2C_{2}}{cC_{1}^{2}}
.

Proof.

Since
R^{\prime}+R^{\prime}
contains only multiples of
c
and lies inside
[2n]
,

|R^{\prime}+R^{\prime}|\leq 2n/c
. By the definition of
\Gamma
,
each of these elements leads to at most
C_{2}n
pairs
that are not in
\Gamma
.
Therefore, since
|R^{\prime}|\geq C_{1}n
,

|R^{\prime}\times R^{\prime}-\Gamma|\leq(C_{2}n)(2n/c)\leq\frac{2C_{2}}{cC_{1}^{2}}|R^{\prime}|^{2}.

∎

By this claim,

|\Gamma|=|R^{\prime}\times R^{\prime}-\Gamma|\geq|R^{\prime}|^{2}-\epsilon|R^{\prime}|^{2}=(1-\epsilon)|R^{\prime}|^{2},

as required.

Note that the line of reasoning
from Claim
6
up to this point
is valid for
B^{\prime}
as well
(with the same choice for
\epsilon
),
and that once
C_{1}
is fixed (by Claim
6
)
this choice of
\epsilon
can be made arbitrarily small by
decreasing

.

By Theorem
8
, we can now say that

R^{\prime}
strongly resembles an
arithmetic progression.
To be precise, we must first introduce a bit of notation:
if
f=o_{\epsilon}(1)
, then

f\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
.
Now the strong resemblance

has to

means

|R^{\prime}\cap P|=(1-o_{\epsilon}(1))|R^{\prime}|.

(26)

With so much information about the coloring
(at least on the multiples of
c
),
we can now find a specific progression
which contains the desired amount of monochromatic solutions.

Claim 10
.

There exists an arithmetic progression
Q=\{dk\,:\,1\leq k\leq\lfloor n/d\rfloor\}

with
|Q\cap A|=(1-o_{\epsilon}(1))|Q|

for some
A\in\{R,B\}
.

Proof.

If
P=\{a+dk\}
has these properties,
then we are done, so suppose it does not.
We may assume
0<a<d
.
Then the progression
Q=\{dk\}
is
contained almost entirely in
B^{\prime}

(since it’s almost entirely disjoint
from

and everything is still a multiple
of
c
).
To be precise,

|Q\cap P|=o_{\epsilon}(1)
,
so
|Q\cap B^{\prime}|=(1-o_{\epsilon}(1))|Q|
.
∎

Finally, if
x,y\in Q
, then

z=\frac{x+y}{c}=\frac{dk_{1}+dk_{2}}{c}=d\left(\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{c}\right),

which is in

k_{1}+k_{2}\in c\mathbb{Z}
,
with at most a constant number of exceptions.
This will happen about

of the time, so

\text{\# of monochr. solns. in $Q$}=\frac{1}{c}(1-o_{\epsilon}(1)|Q|)^{2}=\frac{1}{c}(1-o_{\epsilon}(1))\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)^{2}=\Omega(n^{2}).

Therefore, every equation of the form
ax+ay-cz=0

has
\Omega(n^{2})
monochromatic solutions
regardless of how
[n]
is colored,
proving Theorem

.

4
Conclusion and New Directions

We have shown that all
3
-term equations are uncommon over
[n]
.
For any single equation
a_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}x_{k}=0
over
an abelian group
A
whose order is relatively prime to each
a_{i}
,
a full classification is known.

Theorem 11

(
[
19
]
)
.

An equation is uncommon over
A
if and only if
k
is even
and has no canceling partition.

A
canceling partition
of an equation is
a partition of the coefficients into pairs

\{a_{i},a_{j}\}
such that
a_{i}+a_{j}=0
.
Over
[n]
, we expect the following to be true.

Conjecture 12
.

An equation is common over
[n]
if and only if
k
is even
and has a canceling partition.

Note that if this were true,
equations with
k
even would behave the same over
A
and
[n]
,
while equations with
k
odd would behave differently.
With this paper, the conjecture is now confirmed for
k=3
.
Much is still unknown, but we can also definitively say that
equations with
k
even and no canceling partition are uncommon over
[n]
.
This is simply because they are uncommon over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
if
p

is a large enough prime (
p>\max\{|a_{i}|\}
) by Theorem
11
,
and Lemma
2
implies they are also uncommon over
[n]
.
Appendix
A
provides a proof for the only type of equation
known to be common over
[n]
:

x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k/2}=x_{k/2+1}+\cdots+x_{k}\;\;(k\text{ even}).

(27)

For instance, it is not even known if
x+2y=z+2w

is common.

Aside from these types of classification problems
(and ones which address systems of equations as in
[
10
]
),
improving upper and lower bounds on minima remains widely open.
Furthermore, all these questions and more can be asked about
colorings of more than 2 colors.

Appendix A
Monochromatic Solutions
for Additive Tuples

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
even.
Here we prove all
additive tuples
, equations of the form

x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k/2}=x_{k/2+1}+\cdots+x_{k},

(28)

are common over
[n]
,
i.e. the minimum fraction of monochromatic solutions
the same as what is expected from uniformly random colorings:
2^{1-k}
.
Let
p
be a prime which is larger than
kn/2
.
We identify
[n]
with the subset
S=\{1,2,\dots,n\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{p}

and note that by our choice of
p
any solution to (
28
)
over
S
is also a solution over the integers.

Let
\mathds{1}_{S}
be the indicator function of
S
, and
recall the definition of the Fourier transform from
Section
2.1
:

\widehat{\mathds{1}_{S}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\mathds{1}_{S}(t)e^{-2\pi i\xi t/p}.

It is standard (see, for example, Equation (4.14) in
[
16
]
) that the number of solutions to (
28
) in
S
is given by

p^{k-1}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}_{S}}(t)\right|^{k}.

Now suppose we have a partition of
S
into a red set
R
and a blue set
B
.
The total number of monochromatic solutions to

x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k/2}=x_{k/2+1}+\cdots+x_{k}
is then given by

p^{k-1}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}_{R}}(j)\right|^{k}+p^{k-1}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}_{B}}(j)\right|^{k}.

Using the inequality
x^{k}+y^{k}\geq 2^{1-k}(x+y)^{k}
,
which is valid for all real
x,y
and
k\geq 2
even
(by Jensen’s inequality), we get that the number of monochromatic solutions is at least

\displaystyle(p/2)^{k-1}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\left(|\widehat{\mathds{1}_{R}}(j)|+|\widehat{\mathds{1}_{B}}(j)|\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(p/2)^{k-1}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}_{R}}(j)+\widehat{\mathds{1}_{B}}(j)\right|^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{1-k}\left(p^{k-1}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}_{S}}(j)\right|^{k}\right).

In other words, the number of monochromatic solutions is always at least a
2^{1-k}
fraction of the total number of solutions.
