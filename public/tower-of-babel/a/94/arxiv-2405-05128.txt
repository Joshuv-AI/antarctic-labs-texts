Degree of the Grassmannian as an affine variety
Lek-Heng Lim, Ke Ye

Abstract
. The degree of the Grassmannian with respect to the Plücker embedding is well-known. However, the Plücker embedding, while ubiquitous in pure mathematics, is almost never used in applied mathematics. In applied mathematics, the Grassmannian is usually embedded as projection matrices Gr ( k , ℝ n ) ≅ { P ∈ ℝ n × n : P 𝖳 = P = P 2 , tr ( P ) = k } \Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cong\{P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:P^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}=P=P^{2},\;\tr(P)=k\} or as involution matrices Gr ( k , ℝ n ) ≅ { X ∈ ℝ n × n : X 𝖳 = X , X 2 = I , tr ( X ) = 2 k − n } \Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cong\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}=X,\;X^{2}=I,\;\tr(X)=2k-n\} . We will determine an explicit expression for the degree of the Grassmannian with respect to these embeddings. In so doing, we resolved a conjecture of Devriendt, Friedman, Reinke, and Sturmfels about the degree of Gr ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ n ) \Gr(2,\mathbb{R}^{n}) and in fact generalized it to Gr ⁡ ( k , ℝ n ) \Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n}) . We also proved a set-theoretic variant of another conjecture of theirs about the limit of Gr ⁡ ( k , ℝ n ) \Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n}) in the sense of Gröbner degeneration.



1.
Introduction

The standard way to embed a Grassmannian in an ambient space is the celebrated Plücker embedding,
\pi:\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{P}(\Skew^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
,
\spn(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\mapsto[v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k}]
. The Plücker embedding has many appealing features, e.g., its mean curvature vanishes and so its image is a minimal submanifold
[
2
,
5
]
; in addition it is a
minuscule embedding

[
37
]
. However, there are several difficulties if one attempts to use the image of the Plücker embedding as a model for the Grassmannian in applied mathematics. One issue is that while it maps subspaces to antisymmetric
k
-tensors, it does so only up to scaling, i.e., the image of
\pi
is a projective variety. This presents a problem as equivalence classes can be tricky to implement well in software, and is the whole reason why one needs a model in applied mathematics for the Grassmannian that realizes abstract subspaces as concrete objects with coordinates. Another issue is the exceedingly high dimension
\binom{n}{k}
of the ambient space
\mathbb{P}(\Skew^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, compared to its intrinsic dimension of
k(n-k)
. For instance, while one might get around the first issue by further embedding
\mathbb{P}(\Skew^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
into
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
m=\binom{\binom{n}{k}+1}{2}
(note also that this is only possible over
\mathbb{R}
but not over
\mathbb{C}
), the high dimension
m
becomes a liability when one needs to perform computations.

As a result, in areas connected to applications such as coding theory
[
8
,
12
]
, machine learning
[
15
]
, optimization
[
52
,
56
]
, and statistics
[
10
]
, the Grassmannian is typically modeled as a set of projection matrices:

(1)

\displaystyle\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle\cong\{P\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):P^{2}=P,\;\tr(P)=k\};

or, more recently, as a set of involution matrices
[
34
]
:

(2)

\displaystyle\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle\cong\{X\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):X^{2}=I_{n},\;\tr(X)=2k-n\}

within the vector space of real symmetric matrices
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Even without taking into account constraints that further limit dimension, points on the Grassmannian are now realized as
n\times n
symmetric matrices, a far lower dimensional ambient space compared to that in the Plücker embedding. It is worth noting that the model in (
1
) is not limited to applied areas but is also common in geometric measure theory
[
42
]
and differential geometry
[
46
]
.

By “the degree of Grassmannian” in the title, we meant the degree of either (
1
) or (
2
) as defined by Hilbert polynomials of their projective closures, which notably applies to arbitrary fields
[
25
]
. It is easy to see that (
1
) and (
2
) have the same degree but (
2
) is defined by simpler equations (see Section
3
). Henceforth we will adopt the model (
2
) but our results will apply to (
1
) as well.

To determine the value of the degree of (
2
), it is easier to work over
\mathbb{C}
and use the fact that the degree of (
2
) is equal to that of its
complex locus

(3)

\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{X\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):X^{2}=I_{n},\;\tr(X)=2k-n\}.

This is also the approach used in
[
14
]
.

However the complex locus is not the only complex geometric object that may be associated with
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Indeed the
complex Grassmannian
(in the involution model)

(4)

\Gr(k,\mathbb{C}^{n})=\{X\in\Herm^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):X^{2}=I_{n},\;\tr(X)=2k-n\}

is arguably a more natural object. While (
3
) defines a complex affine variety in the
complex
vector space of complex symmetric matrices
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, (
4
) defines a real affine variety in the
real
vector space of Hermitian matrices
\Herm^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

The authors of
[
14
]
favor (
3
) over (
4
) but did not provide a rationale. In Section
3
, we will show that the former possesses a special property — (
3
) gives a
minimal algebraic complexification
, which is unique among all complexifications if one exists. On the other hand (
4
) gives a nonminimal complexification.

As our title suggests, our main goal is to establish an explicit expression for the degree of the Grassmannian as an embedded variety, which we accomplish in Section
4
. We will prove a closed-form combinatorial formula for the degree of (
3
) in Theorem
4.3
and highlight some of its consequences. Notably, Corollary
4.4
resolves
[
14
, Conjecture 5.7]
and Corollaries
4.5
and
4.6
confirm the numerical values in
[
14
, Proposition 5.5]
(that the authors computed with
Macaulay2
and
HomotopyContinuation.jl
). In Section
5
, we will characterize the boundary points of the projective closure of (
3
) in Theorem
5.5
and thereby resolve a set-theoretic version of
[
14
, Conjecture 5.8]
.

1.1.
Degree for practitioners

We add a few words for computational and applied mathematicians who may use the models (
1
) or (
2
) in their works but who may not be familiar with the notion of
degree of an algebraic variety

[
25
,
23
]
. As the name implies, it is a notion that generalizes the degree of a single polynomial to more than one polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, or, equivalently, to the variety cut out by these polynomials
V\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:f_{1}(x)=\dots=f_{k}(x)=0\}
.

Geometrically, the degree counts the number of intersection points of the variety with a generic linear space of complementary dimension. Roughly speaking, it counts the number of solutions of a linear system
Ax=b
on a variety
V\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
. If
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
has full rank,
b\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, and
V\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a variety of codimension
m
and degree
d
, then by definition there will be at most
d
solutions
x\in V
. The degree of a variety is also a measure of how complicated the variety is. For example, hypersurfaces of degree one or two are easily understood whereas degree three or higher hypersurfaces are still mysterious
[
26
]
.

The degree is an invariant of a variety but it depends on the embedding. So the notion is especially pertinent to practitioners as algebraic varieties in applications are usually explicitly embedded in some ambient spaces like
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
,
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
,
\Skew^{k}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, etc. Our results on the degree of Grassmannian, while primarily of theoretical interest, have some practical implications. For example, if we optimize a generic degree-
p
polynomial function on
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the models (
1
) or (
2
), then the number of critical points is bounded above by
p^{k(n-k)}d
where
d
is the degree of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in (
3
).
For another example, the aforementioned problem of solving a system of linear equations on a variety
V\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
arises in
unlabeled sensing

[
54
]
where
V
is a set of
n!
points; this could conceivably be extended to
V=\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

The last section will involve the notion of a
projective closure
of an affine variety. This is a standard procedure to turn an affine variety in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
into a projective variety in
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})
by adding “points at infinity.” Taking projective closure preserves degree, a fact that is often used in the calculation of degrees.

2.
Notations and background

We write
\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
for the set of nonnegative integers and
\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle++}
for the set of positive integers throughout. For easy reference, we recall three results from linear algebra, representation theory, and combinatorics that we will need later.

2.1.
Linear algebra

While a real symmetric matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable, a complex symmetric matrix is only similar to a block diagonal matrix under conjugation by complex orthogonal matrices. We will use the following result from
[
21
, p. 13]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Canonical form for complex symmetric matrices)
.

Let
A\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. There exists
Q\in\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})
so that

A=Q\diag(\lambda_{1}I_{q_{1}}+S_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}I_{q_{m}}+S_{m})Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},

a block diagonal matrix with diagonal blocks of the form

S_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(I_{q_{j}}-iJ_{q_{j}})N_{q_{j}}(I_{q_{j}}+iJ_{q_{j}})\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{q_{j}}),\quad j=1,\dots,m,

where
I_{q}
is the
q\times q
identity matrix and

J_{q}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\dots&0&1\\
0&0&\dots&1&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&1&\dots&0&0\\
1&0&\dots&0&0\end{bmatrix}\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{q}),\quad N_{q}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}0&1&\dots&0&0\\
0&0&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&0&1\\
0&0&\dots&0&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times q}

are the
q\times q
exchange matrix and nilpotent matrix respectively.
Here
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}
, not necessarily distinct, are eigenvalues of
A
, and
q_{1}+\dots+q_{m}=n
.

2.2.
Representation theory

Irreducible
\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-modules are indexed by non-increasing sequences
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m})
of
m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
integers such that
\lambda_{m}\geq 0
if
n=2m+1
and
\lambda_{m-1}\geq|\lambda_{m}|
if
n=2m
. Let
\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}
be the irreducible
\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-module indexed by
\lambda
. Then its dimension
[
22
, Proposition 3.1.19]
is given by

(5)

\dim\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}=\begin{cases}\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\dfrac{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}-i+j}{j-i}\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq j\leq m}\dfrac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}+n-i-j}{n-i-j}&\text{if }n=2m+1,\\[17.22217pt]
\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\dfrac{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}-i+j}{j-i}\dfrac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}+n-i-j}{n-i-j}&\text{if }n=2m.\end{cases}

Let
m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
and
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the standard basis vectors. Then the
fundamental weights
of
\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})
are
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by

\omega_{i}=\begin{cases}e_{1}+\dots+e_{i}&\text{if }n=2m+1\text{ and }1\leq i\leq m-1,\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+\dots+e_{m})&\text{if }n=2m+1\text{ and }i=m,\\[4.30554pt]
e_{1}+\dots+e_{i}&\text{if }n=2m\text{ and }1\leq i\leq m-2,\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+\dots+e_{m-1}-e_{m})&\text{if }n=2m\text{ and }i=m-1,\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+\dots+e_{m-1}+e_{m})&\text{if }n=2m\text{ and }i=m.\end{cases}

2.3.
Combinatorics

The
dominance partial ordering

\succeq
on the set of partitions is defined by

\lambda\succeq\mu\quad\iff\quad|\lambda|=|\mu|\text{ and }\sum_{j=1}^{i}\lambda_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mu_{j}\text{ for each }i=1,\dots,m.

The following expression may be found in
[
4
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
m,p,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle++}
and
\delta\coloneqq(m-1,\dots,1,0)
. Then

\int_{|x|\leq 1,\;x_{1}\geq\dots\geq x_{m}\geq 0}\prod_{1\leq i\leq m}x_{i}^{p}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(x_{i}^{2}-x_{j}^{2})^{d}\,dx=\frac{\sum_{\lambda\succeq d\delta}A_{\lambda,p,d}B_{\lambda,d}C_{\lambda,d}}{\Gamma(m(p+1+d(m-1))+1)},

where

A_{\lambda,p,d}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Gamma(\lambda_{i}+p+1+d(m-i)/2),\quad B_{\lambda,d}=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}+d(j-i+1)/2)}{\Gamma(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}+d(j-i)/2)},

and, for
\lambda\succeq d\delta\coloneqq\bigl(d(m-1),d(m-2),\dots,d,0\bigr)
,
C_{\lambda,d}
is the coefficient of the Jack symmetric functions
J_{\lambda}^{(\frac{2}{d})}(x)
in the expansion

\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(x_{i}+x_{j})^{d}=\sum_{\lambda\succeq d\delta}C_{\lambda,d}J_{\lambda}^{(\frac{2}{d})}(x).

It is perhaps worth noting that Jack symmetric functions, first introduced in
[
27
]
as a generalization of Schur functions, have profound connections to combinatorics and representation theory
[
19
,
41
,
53
,
9
,
18
,
31
]
, and are closely related to various forms of the Selberg integral
[

,
28
,
4
]
.

3.
Complex locus of the real Grassmannian

A reason we favor our
involution model
(
2
) over the
projection model
(
1
) is that we find
X^{2}=I
more convenient to handle than
P^{2}=P
. Since the projection model (
1
) is easily seen to be a scaled and translated copy of the involution model (
2
), they have the same degree. Henceforth we will assume the form in (
2
). We begin by deriving its complex locus, showing that it is indeed given by (
3
) as expected. To that end, we will need to determine the ideal of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Proposition
3.1
below is the involution model analogue of
[
14
, Theorem 5.1]
for the projection model; and we give an elementary proof with classical invariant theory, avoiding the scheme theory used in
[
14
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
k,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle++}
with
k\leq n
, let
\mathscr{I}_{k,n}
be the ideal generated by
2k-n-\tr(X)
and
I_{n}-X^{2}
. Then
\mathscr{I}_{k,n}=\mathscr{I}(\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n}))
.

Proof.

Clearly
\mathscr{I}_{k,n}\subseteq\mathscr{I}(\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n}))\subseteq\mathbb{R}[\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})]
. Let
\V_{n,k}(\mathbb{R})
be the Stiefel variety of
k
orthonormal frames in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The coordinate ring of
\V_{n,k}(\mathbb{R})
is
\mathbb{R}[\V_{n,k}(\mathbb{R})]=\mathbb{R}[Y]/\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle
, where
Y=(y_{i\ell})
is the
n\times k
matrix with indeterminate entries
y_{i\ell}
,
i=1,\dots,n
,
\ell=1,\dots,k
.
Since
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cong\V_{n,k}(\mathbb{R})/\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})
, the coordinate ring may be determined as a ring of
\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})
-invariants,

\mathbb{R}[\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})]\simeq\mathbb{R}[\V_{n,k}(\mathbb{R})]^{\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})}=(\mathbb{R}[Y]/\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle)^{\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})},

where the action of
\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})
is given by

\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})\times(\mathbb{R}[Y]/\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle)\to\mathbb{R}[Y]/\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle,\quad(Q,f(Y))\mapsto f(YQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}).

We consider the map

\varphi:\mathbb{R}[X]/\langle\tr(X)-k,X-X^{2}\rangle\to(\mathbb{R}[Y]/\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle)^{\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})},\quad\varphi(X)=YY^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}.

It is straightforward to verify that
\varphi
is well-defined. The domain of
\varphi
may also be written as

\mathbb{R}[x_{ij}:i,j=1,\dots,n]\!\!\biggm/\!\!\!\biggl\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ii}-k,x_{ij}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}x_{i\ell}x_{\ell j}:i,j=1,\dots,n\biggr\rangle

and in which case
\varphi
takes
x_{ij}
to
\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}y_{i\ell}y_{j\ell}
,
i,j=1,\dots,n
. We claim that

(\mathbb{R}[Y]/\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle)^{\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})}
is generated by

z_{ij}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}y_{i\ell}y_{j\ell},\quad i,j=1,\dots,n,

with relations

z_{ij}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}z_{i\ell}z_{\ell j},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{ii}=k,\quad i,j=1,\dots,n,

from which it follows that
\varphi
is an isomorphism. The construction of these generators and relations is routine and we will just present a sketch: Let
G_{1}
be the finite subgroup of
\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})
consisting of all signed permutation matrices and
G_{2}\coloneqq\bigl\{\begin{bmatrix}Q&0\\
0&I_{k-2}\end{bmatrix}\in\O_{k}(\mathbb{R}):Q\in\O_{2}(\mathbb{R})\bigr\}
. Note that
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
generates
\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})
. We first determine
(\mathbb{R}[Y]/\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle)^{G_{1}}
using the Reynold operator
[
50
]
. By further imposing
G_{2}
-invariance on the elements of
(\mathbb{R}[Y]/\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle)^{G_{1}}
, we see that

(\mathbb{R}[Y]/\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle)^{\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})}=(\mathbb{R}[Y]/\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle)^{G_{1}}\cap(\mathbb{R}[Y]/\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle)^{G_{2}}

is generated by
z_{ij}
,
i,j=1,\dots,n
. To obtain the relations among these generators, let
f
be a polynomial in
z_{ij}
,
i,j=1,\dots,n
, and write
Z=[z_{ij}]
. Observe that if
f(Z)=0
, then
f(Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y)\in\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle
and is invariant under both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. Since

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{ii}-k=\sum_{p=1}^{k}\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{ip}y_{ip}-1\biggr)

\displaystyle\in\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle,

\displaystyle z_{ij}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}z_{i\ell}z_{\ell j}=\sum_{p=1}^{k}\sum_{q=1}^{k}y_{ip}\biggl(\delta_{pq}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}y_{\ell p}y_{\ell q}\biggr)y_{jq}

\displaystyle\in\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle,

we have

f(Z)\in\biggl\langle z_{ij}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}z_{i\ell}z_{\ell j},\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{ii}-k\biggr\rangle=\langle Z-Z^{2},\tr(Z)-k\rangle.

Hence
\varphi
gives an isomorphism

(\mathbb{R}[Y]/\langle I_{k}-Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y\rangle)^{\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})}\simeq\mathbb{R}[X]/\langle X-X^{2},\tr(X)-k\rangle.

This implies that the ideal of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
via the embedding

j_{\proj}:\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad\mathbb{V}\mapsto VV^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}

is generated by
k-\tr(X)
and
X-X^{2}
. Here
V
is any representative of
\mathbb{V}
in
\V_{n,k}(\mathbb{R})
. Since the involution model is obtained by composing
j_{\proj}
with a translation, i.e.,

j_{\inv}:\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad\mathbb{V}\mapsto 2VV^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}-I_{n},

we conclude that the ideal of
j_{\inv}(\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n}))
in
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is generated by
2k-n-\tr(X)
and
I_{n}-X^{2}
.
∎

It follows from Proposition
3.1
that the complex locus of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is given by (
3
), i.e., replacing
\mathbb{R}
by
\mathbb{C}
in (
2
). By Lemma
2.1
, we may write
X\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
as

X=Q\diag(\lambda_{1}I_{q_{1}}+S_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}I_{q_{m}}+S_{m})Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}

for some
Q\in\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and symmetric matrices
S_{1},\dots,S_{m}
as defined therein. Thus
X^{2}=I_{n}
if and only if
m=n
,
q_{j}=1
, and
\lambda_{j}=\pm 1
,
S_{j}=0
,
j=1,\dots,n
. This observation leads to the following description of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})
act on
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
by conjugation. Then
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the
\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-orbit of
\diag(I_{k},-I_{n-k})
and we have isomorphisms

(6)

\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cong\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})/(\O_{k}(\mathbb{C})\times\O_{n-k}(\mathbb{C}))\cong\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})/\S(\O_{k}(\mathbb{C})\times\O_{n-k}(\mathbb{C})),

where

\S\bigl(\O_{k}(\mathbb{C})\times\O_{n-k}(\mathbb{C})\bigr)\coloneqq\{(X,Y)\in\O_{k}(\mathbb{C})\times\O_{n-k}(\mathbb{C}):\det(XY)=1\}.

It follows that the coordinate ring

(7)

\mathbb{C}[\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})]\simeq\mathbb{C}[\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})/\S(\O_{k}(\mathbb{C})\times\O_{n-k}(\mathbb{C}))].

We will next show that the complex locus in (
3
) has a rather unique property.
Recall that a complexification
[
32
]
of a real manifold
M
is a complex manifold
M_{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying
M\subseteq M_{\mathbb{C}}
and
M=\{x\in M_{\mathbb{C}}:\tau(x)=x\}
for some
conjugation

\tau
, i.e., an anti-holomorphic involution such that for every fixed point
x\in M_{\mathbb{C}}
of
\tau
, there is a holomorphic coordinate system
(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
around
x
with
\tau(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=(\overline{z}_{1},\dots,\overline{z}_{n})
. A complexification
M_{\mathbb{C}}
is
minimal
if the inclusion
M\subseteq M_{\mathbb{C}}
is a homotopy equivalence. It is well-known that any real manifold
M
admits a minimal analytic complexification
[
33
,
55
]
and any compact real manifold
M
can be realized as the set of real points of some algebraic variety
[
45
]
. However, the combination of these two statements is false: It is not true that any compact real manifold admits a minimal algebraic complexification.

Although the complex locus of a compact real variety is obviously an algebraic complexification, it is not necessarily minimal. For example,
M^{\varepsilon}=V((x^{2}+2y^{2}-1)(2x^{2}+y^{2}-1)+\varepsilon)
is a disjoint union of four ovals in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for small
\varepsilon>0

[
49
, Chapter 48]
. Its complex locus
M^{\varepsilon}_{\mathbb{C}}
is a Riemann surface of genus three with four points removed
[
30
, Section 6]
. Thus
M^{\varepsilon}_{\mathbb{C}}
is homotopic to the one point union of nine circles, from which we may conclude that the inclusion
M^{\varepsilon}\hookrightarrow M^{\varepsilon}_{\mathbb{C}}
is not a homotopy equivalence. This example indicates that the homogeneous space structure of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in Lemma
3.2
is essential below.

Proposition 3.3

(Minimal algebraic complexification)
.

The complex locus
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in (
2
) is a minimal affine algebraic complexification of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The complex Grassmannian
\Gr(k,\mathbb{C}^{n})
in (
4
) is a non-minimal complexification of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

Recall that as Lie groups,
\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is the complexification of
\SO_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. By the isomorphism (
6
) and the fact that
\S(\O_{k}(\mathbb{C})\times\O_{n-k}(\mathbb{C}))\cap\SO_{n}(\mathbb{R})=\S(\O_{k}(\mathbb{R})\times\O_{n-k}(\mathbb{R}))
, the first statement is a direct consequence of the proof of
[
33
, Theorem 5.1]
. On the other hand, the complex Grassmannian
\Gr(k,\mathbb{C}^{n})\cong\U(n)/(\U(k)\times\U(n-k))
, so
\pi_{1}\bigl(\Gr(k,\mathbb{C}^{n})\bigr)=0
. Since
\pi_{1}(\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n}))=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, the natural inclusion
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow\Gr(k,\mathbb{C}^{n})
cannot be a homotopy equivalence.
∎

Note that the conjectures in
[
14
]
concern real Grassmannians. This is ultimately our main reason for favoring the complex locus
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
over the complex Grassmannian
\Gr(k,\mathbb{C}^{n})
, as the degree of the real Grassmannian
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
equals that of its complex locus
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. To elaborate, we will need a more formal definition of degree than that given in Section
1.1
: For a
p
-dimensional affine variety
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, its degree, denoted as
\deg V
, is defined as the number of intersection points of
V_{\mathbb{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{m}
, a complex affine variety of complex dimension
p
, with a generic
(m-p)
-dimensional subspace in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. See, for example,
[
14
, p. 13]
and
[
6
, p. 2]
. It turns out that
\deg V
can be determined by the leading coefficient of the Hilbert polynomial of the projective closure of
V_{\mathbb{C}}

[
25
, p. 52]
, and consequently,

(8)

\deg V=p!\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{\dim\mathbb{C}[V]_{d}}{d^{p}}.

We do not know if the results in our article extend to complex Grassmannians. We suspect not, since the geometry of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{C}^{n})
and
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
are vastly different. Indeed, there are more fundamental differences than that revealed by Proposition
3.3
. Firstly,
\Gr(k,\mathbb{C}^{n})
is compact whereas
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is not. Secondly,
\Gr(k,\mathbb{C}^{n})
is a
real
affine variety in the
real
linear space
\Herm^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
but
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a complex affine variety in the complex linear space
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Although
\Gr(k,\mathbb{C}^{n})
, as the image of the Plücker embedding, is also a complex
projective
variety, here we are mainly interested in the degree of the Grassmannian as an affine variety, as our title indicates.

Another useful consequence of Lemma
3.2
is that it allows one to completely determine the decomposition of the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})]
, which is an
\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-module, into a direct sum of irreducible
\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-submodules
[
22
, Corollary 12.3.15]
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle++}
with
k\leq n/2
. Then

(9)

\mathbb{C}[\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})]\simeq\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{k,n}}\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}

where
\Lambda_{k,n}
is generated by
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
, the fundamental weights of
\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, as follows:

\Lambda_{k,n}=\begin{cases}\spn_{\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\{2\omega_{1},\dots,2\omega_{k}\}&\text{if }n=2m+1\text{ and }k\leq m-1,\\
\spn_{\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\{2\omega_{1},\dots,2\omega_{m-1},4\omega_{m}\}&\text{if }n=2m+1\text{ and }k=m,\\
\spn_{\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\{2\omega_{1},\dots,2\omega_{k}\}&\text{if }n=2m\text{ and }k\leq m-2\text{ or }k=m,\\
\spn_{\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\{2\omega_{1},\dots,2\omega_{m-2},2\omega_{m-1}+2\omega_{m}\}&\text{if }n=2m\text{ and }k=m-1.\end{cases}

4.
Degree of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})

The involution model of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is linearly isomorphic to its projection model:
X=2P-I
and
P=(I+X)/2
takes one back and forth between (
1
) and (
2
). As a result, the degree of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the involution model is identical to that in the projection model and we have in effect resolved
[
14
, Conjecture 5.7]
, reproduced below for easy reference and formally stated as Corollary
4.4
to our main result Theorem
4.3
.

Conjecture 4.1

(Devriendt, Friedman, Reinke, and Sturmfels)
.

The degree of
\Gr(2,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the projection model is
2\binom{2n-4}{n-2}
.

As we noted earlier, the involution model of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
as defined in (
2
) has degree equal to that of its complex locus
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
as defined in (
3
). Given that
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a subvariety of
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, its coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})]
is a quotient ring of the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})]
. For any
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
, we will write
\mathbb{C}[\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})]_{d}
for the subspace of
\mathbb{C}[\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})]
comprising functions that are restrictions of polynomials of degree at most
d
in
\mathbb{C}[\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})]
. As a consequence of (
8
) and Proposition
3.4
, we have the following:

Corollary 4.2

(Degree of Grassmannian as a limit)
.

Let
k,n,\Lambda_{n,k}
be as in Proposition
3.4
. Then for any
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
,

(10)

\mathbb{C}[\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})]_{d}\simeq\bigoplus_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr\lambda&\in\Lambda_{k,n}\\
|\lambda|&\leq 2d\crcr}}}\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}.

Here if
n=2k
, then
|\lambda|\coloneqq\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{k-1}+|\lambda_{k}|
. The degree of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is therefore given by

(11)

d_{k,n}=p!\lim\limits_{d\to\infty}\frac{1}{d^{p}}\sum\limits_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr\lambda&\in\Lambda_{k,n}\\
|\lambda|&\leq 2d\crcr}}}\dim\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}

where
p\coloneqq k(n-k)
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle++}
with
k\leq n/2
. We introduce the shorthand

(12)

\alpha_{k,n}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\dfrac{2^{k(n-k-1)}}{\prod_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr 1&\leq i\leq k\\
i&<j\leq\frac{n}{2}\crcr}}}(j-i)(n-j-i)}&\text{if $n$ is even and $k\leq n/2-1$},\\[17.22217pt]
\dfrac{2^{k(n-k)}}{\prod_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr 1&\leq i\leq k\\
i&<j\leq\frac{n-1}{2}\crcr}}}(j-i)(n-i-j)\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}(n-2i)}&\text{if $n$ is odd},\\[21.52771pt]
\dfrac{2^{k(k-1)+1}}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(j-i)(2k-j-i)}&\text{if $n=2k$}.\end{cases}

We now prove our main result.

Theorem 4.3

(Degree of Grassmannian)
.

For positive integers
k\leq n
, the degree of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is the same as that of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(n-k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. For
k\leq n/2
, this value is given by

(13)

d_{k,n}=\alpha_{k,n}\sum_{\lambda\succeq\delta_{k}}A_{\lambda,k}B_{\lambda,k}C_{\lambda,k},

where
\delta_{k}=(k-1,\dots,1,0)
,

A_{\lambda,k}\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Gamma(n-2k+1+\lambda_{i}+(k-i)/2),\quad B_{\lambda,k}\coloneqq\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}+(j-i+1)/2)}{\Gamma(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}+(j-i)/2)},

and, for
\lambda\succeq\delta_{k}
,
C_{\lambda,k}
is the coefficient of Jack symmetric functions
J_{\lambda}^{(2)}(x)
in the expansion

\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(x_{i}+x_{j})=\sum_{\lambda\succeq\delta_{k}}C_{\lambda,k}J_{\lambda}^{(2)}(x).

Proof.

The equality between degrees of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(n-k,n)
, also found in
[
34
, Equation 8]
and
[
14
, Corollary 5.6]
, follows from the isomorphism
\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
,
A\mapsto I_{n}-A
.

Recall that if
k<n/2
, a partition
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m})
lies in
\Lambda_{k,n}
if and only if
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
and
\mu_{k+1}=\dots=\mu_{m}=0
; whereas for
n=2k
,
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k})
lies in
\Lambda_{k,2k}
if and only if
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}
are of the same parity and
\mu_{1}\geq\dots\geq\mu_{k-1}\geq|\mu_{k}|
. With this in mind, we consider three cases with respect to the values of
n
and
k
.

Case I:

n=2m
,
k\leq m-1
.  By (
5
), we have

\displaystyle\dim\mathbb{V}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}}{j-i}+1\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{i}+\mu_{j}}{n-j-i}+1\Bigr)\prod_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr 1&\leq i\leq k\\
k+1&\leq j\leq m\crcr}}}\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{i}}{j-i}+1\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{i}}{n-j-i}+1\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\frac{\mu^{2}_{i}-\mu^{2}_{j}}{(j-i)(n-j-i)}\prod_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr 1&\leq i\leq k\\
k+1&\leq j\leq m\crcr}}}\frac{\mu^{2}_{i}}{(j-i)(n-j-i)}+\text{ lower order terms}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D_{k,n}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(\mu^{2}_{i}-\mu^{2}_{j})\biggl[\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}\mu_{i}\biggr]^{n-2k}+\text{ lower order terms}

where

D_{k,n}\coloneqq{\prod\limits_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr 1&\leq i\leq k\\
i&< j \leq m\crcr}}}(j-i)(n-j-i)}.

By (
11
), we have

\displaystyle d_{k,n}

\displaystyle=p!\lim\limits_{d\to\infty}\frac{1}{d^{p}}\sum\limits_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr\lambda&\in\Lambda_{k,n}\\
|\lambda|&\leq 2d\crcr}}}\dim\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{p}p!}{D_{k,n}}\lim\limits_{d\to\infty}\frac{1}{(2d)^{p}}\sum\limits_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr\lambda&\in\Lambda_{k,n}\\
|\lambda|&\leq 2d\crcr}}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(\lambda^{2}_{i}-\lambda^{2}_{j})\biggl[\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}\lambda_{i}\biggr]^{n-2k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{p}p!}{D_{k,n}}\lim\limits_{d\to\infty}\frac{1}{(2d)^{k}}\sum\limits_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr\lambda&\in\Lambda_{k,n}\\
|\lambda|&\leq 2d\crcr}}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\biggl[\biggl(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{2d}\biggr)^{2}-\biggl(\frac{\lambda_{j}}{2d}\biggr)^{2}\biggr]\biggl[\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{2d}\biggr]^{n-2k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{p}p!}{D_{k,n}}\lim\limits_{d\to\infty}\frac{1}{(2d)^{k}}\sum\limits_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr 2dt&\in\Lambda_{k,n}\\
|t|&\leq 1\crcr}}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(t_{i}^{2}-t_{j}^{2})\biggl[\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}t_{i}\biggr]^{n-2k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{p-k}p!}{D_{k,n}}\int_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr|t|&\leq 1\\
0&\leq t_{k}\leq\dots\leq t_{1}\leq 1\crcr}}}\biggl[\biggl(\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}t_{i}\biggr)^{n-2k}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(t_{i}^{2}-t_{j}^{2})\biggr]dt.

Case II:

n=2m+1
,
k\leq m
.  The dimension formula (
5
) gives

\displaystyle\dim\mathbb{V}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}}{j-i}+1\Bigr)\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq j\leq m}\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{i}+\mu_{j}}{n-i-j}+1\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}}{j-i}+1\Bigr)\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq j\leq k}\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{i}+\mu_{j}}{n-i-j}+1\Bigr)\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq k<j\leq m}\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{i}}{j-i}+1\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{i}}{n-i-j}+1\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}-\mu_{j}^{2}}{(j-i)(n-i-j)}\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq k}\frac{2\mu_{i}}{n-2i}\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq k<j\leq m}\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{(j-i)(n-i-j)}+\text{lower order terms}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{k}}{E_{k,n}}\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(\mu_{i}^{2}-\mu_{j}^{2})\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq k}\mu_{i}^{2(m-k)+1}+\text{lower order terms},

where

E_{k,n}\coloneqq\prod\limits_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr 1&\leq i\leq k\\
i&< j \leq m\crcr}}}(j-i)(n-i-j)\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq k}(n-2i).

By (
11
) and the same calculation as in
Case I
, we obtain

d_{k,n}=\frac{2^{p}p!}{E_{k,n}}\int_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr|t|&\leq 1\\
0&\leq t_k \leq\ldots\leq t_1 \leq 1\crcr}}}\biggl[\biggl(\prod_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\biggr)^{n-2k}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(t_{i}^{2}-t_{j}^{2})\biggr]dt.

Case III:

n=2k
.  We recall that in this case,
|\mu|=\mu_{1}+\dots+\mu_{k-1}+|\mu_{k}|
. Let

F_{k,2k}\coloneqq\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(j-i)(2k-j-i).

By (
11
), we have

\displaystyle d_{k,2k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(k^{2})!}{F_{k,2k}}\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{1}{d^{k^{2}}}\sum_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr\lambda&\in\Lambda_{k,n}\\
|\lambda|&\leq 2d\crcr}}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(\lambda^{2}_{i}-\lambda^{2}_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{k^{2}-k}(k^{2})!}{F_{k,2k}}\int_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr t_{1}+\dots+t_{k-1}+|t_{k}|&\leq 1\\
|t_{k}|\leq t_{k-1}\leq\dots\leq t_{1}&\leq 1\crcr}}}\biggl[\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(t_{i}^{2}-t_{j}^{2})\biggr]dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{k^{2}-k+1}(k^{2})!}{F_{k,2k}}\int_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr|t|&\leq 1\\
0&\leq t_{k}\leq\dots\leq t_{1}\leq 0\crcr}}}\biggl[\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(t_{i}^{2}-t_{j}^{2})\biggr]dt.

Applying Lemma
2.2
to the last integral in each of the three cases yields the required expression in (
13
).
∎

By Theorem
4.3
, it is immediate that for
k=1
, we get
d_{1,n}=2^{n-1}
, which is also obtained in
[
14
, Corollary 5.6]
via a geometric argument. For
k=2
, we confirm the value conjectured in
[
14
, Conjecture 5.7]
(and verified numerically for
n\leq 10
therein):

Corollary 4.4

(Degree of
\Gr(2,\mathbb{R}^{n})
)
.

For
n\geq 3
, we have

d_{2,n}=2\binom{2n-4}{n-2}.

Proof.

For
k=2
, we have
\delta_{2}=(1,0)
and
\lambda\succeq\delta_{2}
if and only if
\lambda=(1,0)
. Moreover,

A_{(1,0),2}=\Gamma(n-\tfrac{3}{2})\Gamma(n-3),\quad B_{(1,0),2}=\frac{\Gamma(2)}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})},

and since
J^{(2)}_{(1)}(x)=x_{1}+x_{2}
, we get
C_{(1,0),2}=1
. Hence we obtain from (
13
) that
d_{2,n}=2^{n-1}(2n-5)!!/(n-2)!=2\binom{2n-4}{n-2}
.
∎

For
k=3
and
4
, we may also simplify the expression in (
13
) to obtain more explicit ones for
d_{3,n}
and
d_{4,n}
. They confirm the values obtained numerically for
n\leq 10
in
[
14
, Proposition 5.5]
.

Corollary 4.5

(Degrees of
\Gr(3,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\Gr(4,\mathbb{R}^{n})
)
.

For
n\geq 5
, we have

d_{3,n}=\frac{(8n-25)(2n-9)!!}{(n-2)!}2^{2n-6}.

For
n\geq 7
, we have

d_{4,n}=\frac{(32n^{2}-288n+634)(2n-13)!!(2n-9)!!}{(n-2)!(n-4)!}2^{2n-6}.

Proof.

For
k=3
, we have
\delta_{3}=(2,1)
and
\lambda\succeq\delta_{3}
if and only if
\lambda=(2,1)
or
(1,1,1)
. Moreover,

\displaystyle A_{(2,1),3}

\displaystyle=\Gamma(n-2)\Gamma(n-\tfrac{7}{2})\Gamma(n-5),\quad B_{(2,1),3}=\frac{\Gamma(2)}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{7}{2})}{\Gamma(3)}\frac{\Gamma(2)}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})}=\frac{15}{4\sqrt{\pi}},

\displaystyle A_{(1,1,1),3}

\displaystyle=\Gamma(n-3)\Gamma(n-\tfrac{7}{2})\Gamma(n-4),\quad B_{(1,1,1),3}=\frac{\Gamma(1)}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})}\frac{\Gamma(1)}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})}{\Gamma(1)}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}},

and since

\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}(x_{i}+x_{j})=J^{(2)}_{(2,1)}+\frac{1}{2}J^{(2)}_{(1,1,1)},

we get
C_{(2,1),3}=1
,
C_{(1,1,1),3}=\frac{1}{2}
. Hence we obtain the expression for
d_{3,n}
from (
13
). The expression for
d_{4,n}
is similarly obtained.
∎

As these calculations reveal, if not for the fact that the coefficients
\{C_{\lambda,k}:\lambda\succeq\delta_{k}\}
are implicitly defined, our expression for
d_{k,n}
in (
13
) will be fully explicit, as
\alpha_{k,n}
,
A_{\lambda,k}
,
B_{\lambda,k}
are all explicitly given. While in general there is no explicit formula for the coefficients
C_{\lambda,k}
in

\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(x_{i}+x_{j})=\sum_{\lambda\succeq\delta_{k}}C_{\lambda,k}J_{\lambda}^{(2)}(x),

they are trivial to compute algorithmically. As described in
[
41
, page 326]
, a Jack symmetric function
J_{\lambda}^{(2)}(x)
can be expanded as a linear combination of monomial symmetric functions using the recursive Gram–Schmidt process, which in turn yields the values of
\{C_{\lambda,k}:\lambda\succeq\delta_{k}\}
. They may also be calculated efficiently in floating point arithmetic to high accuracy
[
13
]
.

Each expression in Corollaries
4.4
and
4.5
is a product of factorials, double factorials,
2
-powers, and a polynomial in
n
. We will show that this holds true in general for
d_{k,n}
. In fact, the first three quantities can be determined explicitly. As in Theorem
4.3
, we may assume
k\leq n/2
without loss of generality.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle++}
with
k\leq n/2
. Then there exists a polynomial
P_{k}
of degree at most
\binom{k}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lfloor i/2\rfloor
such that

(14)

d_{k,n}=\alpha_{k,n}\frac{\prod_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k-1}{2}\rfloor}(n-2k+2j)!\prod_{j=1}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}(2(n-2k+2j)-1)!!}{2^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor(n-2k+\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1)}}P_{k}(n),

where
\alpha_{k,n}
is as in (
12
). Moreover, for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle++}
, the sequence
(d_{k,n})_{n=2k}^{\infty}
is completely determined by its first
\binom{k}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lfloor i/2\rfloor
terms.

Proof.

The existence of the polynomial
P_{k}
and the expression (
14
) are a direct consequence of Theorem
4.3
. First observe that
C_{\lambda,k}
is independent of
n
. By plugging
\Gamma(m)=(m-1)!
and
\Gamma\bigl((2m+1)/2\bigr)=(2m-1)!!\sqrt{\pi}/2^{m}
into (
13
) and regrouping terms, we obtain (
14
). If the first
\binom{k}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lfloor i/2\rfloor
terms in
(d_{k,n})_{n=2k}^{\infty}
are known, then the subsequent values of
P_{k}(n)
can be uniquely determined by polynomial interpolation, and thereby determining the corresponding values of
d_{k,n}
via (
14
).
∎

We recall from
[
43
, Theorem 5.13]
that the degree of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the Plücker embedding is

(15)

\widehat{d}_{k,n}\coloneqq\frac{(k(n-k))!}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}j(j+1)\cdots(j+n-k-1)}.

Corollary
4.6
then allows us to compare
d_{k,n}
with
\widehat{d}_{k,n}
for any fixed
k
.

Proposition 4.7

(Comparison with degree of Plücker embedding)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
be fixed. Then
d_{1,n}/\widehat{d}_{1,n}=2^{1-n}
,
d_{2,n}/\widehat{d}_{2,n}=\frac{1}{2}(n-1)^{-1}
, and, for
k\geq 3
,

(16)

d_{k,n}/\widehat{d}_{k,n}=O\bigl((2/k)^{kn}n^{k^{2}}\bigr).

Proof.

The values for
k=1,2
follow from Corollary
4.4
and the discussion before it. For
k\geq 3
, it follows from (
12
), (
14
), and (
15
) that

d_{k,n}/\widehat{d}_{k,n}=O\biggl(\frac{n^{k^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{k/2}(2n-4k+4j-1)!}{(kn-k^{2})!}\biggr).

Applying Stirling’s formula gives us (
16
).
∎

Proposition
4.7
shows that for any fixed
k\geq 3
, the degree of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the involution model is exponentially smaller than its degree with respect to the Plücker embedding, i.e.,
d_{k,n}/\widehat{d}_{k,n}
decreases to
0
exponentially as
n\to\infty
. The practical implication is that the involution model for
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is geometrically much simpler than the Plücker embedding, and low-degree objects are always preferred in computations.

5.
Projective closure of the Grassmannian

Our main goal in this section is to prove a set-theoretic version of
[
14
, Conjecture 5.8]
, reproduced below for easy reference.

Conjecture 5.1

(Devriendt, Friedman, Reinke, and Sturmfels)
.

In the sense of Gröbner degeneration with respect to the monomial order given by total degree, the limit of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
\{X\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):X^{2}=0\}
. Furthermore, the initial ideal is given by
\initial(\mathscr{I}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,n})=\langle X^{2},\tr(X)\rangle
.

The notion of Gröbner degeneration is discussed in
[
3
,
16
,
11
]
. By definition,
\initial(\mathscr{I}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,n})
is the limit of
\mathscr{I}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,n}
with respect to the Gröbner degeneration. So Conjecture
5.1
may be rephrased as

(17)

Z(\initial(\mathscr{I}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,n}))=\{X\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):X^{2}=0\},\quad\initial(\mathscr{I}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,n})=\langle X^{2},\tr(X)\rangle

where
Z(\mathscr{I})
denotes the variety defined by the ideal
\mathscr{I}
.

We will prove a set-theoretic variant of Conjecture
5.1
. Instead of the limit of the ideal
\mathscr{I}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,n}
, we will give the limit points of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in
\mathbb{P}(\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\oplus\mathbb{C})
. Theorem
5.5
shows, among other things, that the set of limit points
\partial\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is exactly the conjectured
Z(\initial(\mathscr{I}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,n}))
in (
17
), i.e.,

\partial\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\mathbb{R}^{n}){\mathrel{\to}^{\text{Thm.~\ref{thm:set}}}}\{X\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):X^{2}=0\}{\mathrel{\to}^{\text{Conj.\ \ref{conj:limit}}}}Z(\initial(\mathscr{I}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,n})).

In fact, Theorem
5.5
shows that the first equality holds with
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
replaced by any
k\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.

Conjecture
5.1
and Theorem
5.5
are both about the limiting behavior of
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
with “limit” interpreted respectively in the sense of Gröbner degeneration and in the sense of topology. In this regard (
21
) in Theorem
5.5
may be viewed as a set-theoretic version of (
17
) or Conjecture
5.1
.

More generally Theorem
5.5
gives the polynomial equations defining
\overline{\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}
. Such set-theoretic descriptions of a variety are a common step towards the (usually more difficult) ideal-theoretic descriptions. Notable examples include the set-theoretic Salmon conjecture for
\sigma_{4}(\mathbb{P}^{3}\times\mathbb{P}^{3}\times\mathbb{P}^{3})

[
38
,
48
,
1
,
20
]
, the set-theoretic Eisenbud–Koh–Stillman conjecture for
\sigma_{r}(v_{d}(Z))

[
17
,
51
,
7
]
, the set-theoretic Landsberg–Weyman conjecture for
\tau(\mathbb{P}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{n_{k}})

[
39
,
47
]
, the set-theoretic description of
v_{d}(Z)

[
44
,
24
]
, among yet other similar endeavors. Here
\sigma_{r}(Z)
,
v_{d}(Z)
, and
\tau(Z)
denote the
r
th secant, degree-
d
Veronese, and tangential variety of a smooth projective variety
Z
respectively.

What we wrote in the beginning of Section
4
also applies to this section, that is, it makes no difference whether we use the projection model (
1
) or the involution model (
2
) as they only differ by a linear change of coordinates. So while Conjecture
5.1
was stated in
[
14
]
for the projection model, we may use the involution model below.

We begin by introducing some notations. Let
\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}
denote the
projective closure
of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
, i.e., its closure in the projective space
\mathbb{P}(\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\oplus\mathbb{C})
. Note that the Euclidean closure and Zariski closure are equal in this case. The projective variety defined by homogenized generators of the ideal of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is

(18)

\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\bigl\{[X:t]\in\mathbb{P}(\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\oplus\mathbb{C}):X^{2}-t^{2}I_{n}=0,\;\tr(X)-(2k-n)t=0\bigr\}.

Clearly, we have

\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}\subseteq\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})

and that

\partial{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}\setminus\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})=\bigl\{[X:t]\in\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}:t=0\bigr\}.

Let
L_{\infty}
denote the hyperplane at infinity, i.e.,

(19)

L_{\infty}=\bigl\{[X:t]\in\mathbb{P}(\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\oplus\mathbb{C}):t=0\bigr\}.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
S=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}i&1\\
1&-i\end{bmatrix}
. For any
[X:0]\in\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L_{\infty}
, there exist some integer
d\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
and matrix
Q\in\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that

X=Q\begin{bmatrix}S\otimes I_{d}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}.

Here
S\otimes I_{d}=\diag(S,\dots,S)
is a block diagonal matrix with
d
diagonal blocks.

Proof.

Clearly
\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L_{\infty}=\{X\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):X^{2}=0,\;\tr(X)=0\}
. A complex symmetric matrix has a decomposition

X=Q\diag(\lambda_{1}I_{q_{1}}+S_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}I_{q_{m}}+S_{m})Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}

for some
Q\in\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and symmetric matrices
S_{1},\dots,S_{m}
as in Lemma
2.1
. If
X^{2}=0
, then

0=(\lambda_{j}I_{q_{j}}+S_{j})^{2}=\lambda_{j}^{2}I_{q_{j}}+2\lambda_{j}S_{j}+S_{j}^{2},\quad j=1,\dots,m.

A direct calculation shows that
\lambda_{1}=\dots=\lambda_{m}=0
and for each
j=1,\dots,m
, we must have either
(i)
q_{j}=1
and
S_{j}=0
, or (ii)
q_{j}=2
and
S_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}i&1\\
1&-i\end{bmatrix}
.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

The variety
\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L_{\infty}
is a union of sets
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
given by

(20)

Z_{d}\coloneqq\biggl\{[X:0]\in\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n}):X=Q\begin{bmatrix}S\otimes I_{d}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},\;Q\in\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})\biggr\},\quad d=1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor.

Moreover,
\dim Z_{d}=d(n-d)
for each
d=1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.

Proof.

From (
18
) and (
19
),

\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L_{\infty}=\bigl\{[X:0]\in\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n}):X^{2}=0\bigr\}.

It follows from Lemma
5.2
that we have a disjoint union (denoted by
\sqcup
henceforth)

\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L_{\infty}=\bigsqcup_{d=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}Z_{d}

where
Z_{d}
is the orbit of
X_{0}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}S\otimes I_{d}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}
with respect to the adjoint action of
\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Let
G_{0}
be the stabilizer group of
X_{0}
. Its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
is given by

\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\{A\in\mathfrak{so}_{n}(\mathbb{C}):AX_{0}=X_{0}A\}.

We partition
A\in\mathfrak{g}_{0}
as

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1,1}&\cdots&A_{1,d}&A_{1,d+1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
A_{d,1}&\cdots&A_{d,d}&A_{d,d+1}\\
A_{d+1,1}&\cdots&A_{d+1,d}&A_{d+1,d+1}\\
\end{bmatrix}

where
A_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
,
A_{i,d+1}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times(n-2d)}
,
A_{d+1,j}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-2d)\times 2}
,
A_{d+1,d+1}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-2d)\times(n-2d)}
,
i,j=1,\dots,d
. Then
AX_{0}=X_{0}A
gives us

A_{ij}S=SA_{ij},\quad SA_{i,d+1}=0,\quad A_{d+1,j}S=0,\quad i,j=1,\dots,d;

and therefore

\dim\mathfrak{g}_{0}=2\binom{d}{2}+d(n-2d)+\binom{n-2d}{2}=\binom{n}{2}+d^{2}-nd.

It follows from the orbit-stabilizer theorem that
\dim Z_{d}=\dim\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})-\dim G_{0}=nd-d^{2}
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

For each
d=1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, we have
\overline{Z}_{d}=Z_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Z_{d}
. The set
Z_{d}
is connected (resp. irreducible) unless
n
is even and
d=n/2
, in which case
Z_{d}
has two connected (resp. irreducible) components.

Proof.

It follows from (
20
) that any
[X:0]\in\overline{Z}_{d}
must satisfy
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}=X
,
X^{2}=0
,
\rank(X)\leq d
. Thus
\overline{Z}_{d}\subseteq Z_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Z_{d}
by Lemma
2.1
. For the reverse inclusion, take any
[X:0]\in Z_{j}
,
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, with

X=Q\begin{bmatrix}S\otimes I_{j}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}.

Define

A\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}i&-1\\
-1&-i\end{bmatrix},\quad B\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&i\\
i&-1\end{bmatrix},

and, for each
\varepsilon>0
,

C_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon(\varepsilon+2i)}}\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon+i&1\\
1&-(\varepsilon+i)\end{bmatrix},\quad D_{\varepsilon}\coloneq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon(\varepsilon+2i)}}\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon&\sqrt{2\varepsilon i}\\
\sqrt{2\varepsilon i}&-\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}.

Since
A
and
B
are symmetric matrices and
A^{2}=B^{2}=0
, it follows from Lemma
2.1
that
A=Q_{1}SQ_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
and
B=Q_{2}SQ_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
for some
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\O_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and
S=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}i&1\\
1&-i\end{bmatrix}
. Since
C_{\varepsilon}
and
D_{\varepsilon}
are symmetric orthogonal matrices, we have

\displaystyle X_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneqq Q\begin{bmatrix}D_{\varepsilon}\otimes I_{j}&0&0\\
0&C_{\varepsilon}\otimes I_{d-j}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}S\otimes I_{d}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}D_{\varepsilon}\otimes I_{j}&0&0\\
0&C_{\varepsilon}\otimes I_{d-j}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=Q\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\varepsilon i+2\sqrt{2\varepsilon i}+2}{\varepsilon+2i}A\otimes I_{j}&0&0\\
0&\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+2i}B\otimes I_{d-j}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=V\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\varepsilon i+2\sqrt{2\varepsilon i}+2}{\varepsilon+2i}S\otimes I_{j}&0&0\\
0&\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+2i}S\otimes I_{d-j}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},

where
V\coloneqq Q\diag(Q_{1}\otimes I_{j},Q_{2}\otimes I_{d-j},I_{n-d})\in\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Hence
[X_{\varepsilon}:0]\in Z_{d}
for any
\varepsilon>0
and

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\;[X_{\varepsilon}:0]=[X:0]\in Z_{j}.

The required inclusion
\overline{Z}_{d}\supseteq Z_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Z_{d}
follows. Let
I_{n-1,1}\coloneqq\diag(1,\dots,1,-1)\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. We have the disjoint union of cosets

\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})=\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})\sqcup\bigl(I_{n-1,1}\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})\bigr).

This yields a decomposition
Z_{d}=Z^{0}_{d}\cup Z^{1}_{d}
where

\displaystyle Z^{0}_{d}

\displaystyle=\biggl\{[X:0]\in\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n}):X=Q\begin{bmatrix}S\otimes I_{d}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},\;Q\in\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})\biggr\},

\displaystyle Z^{1}_{d}

\displaystyle=\biggl\{[X:0]\in\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n}):X=QI_{n-1,1}\begin{bmatrix}S\otimes I_{d}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}I_{n-1,1}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},\;Q\in\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})\biggr\}.

Since
\SO_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is connected, both
Z^{0}_{d}
and
Z^{1}_{d}
are connected. If
1\leq d<n/2
, then

I_{n-1,1}\begin{bmatrix}S\otimes I_{d}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}I_{n-1,1}=\begin{bmatrix}S\otimes I_{d}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix};

so
Z^{0}_{d}=Z^{1}_{d}
and
Z_{d}
is connected. If
n
is even and
d=n/2
, then
Z^{0}_{d}\cap Z^{1}_{d}=\varnothing
and so
Z_{d}
has exactly two connected components. Observe also that ‘connected’ may be replaced by ‘irreducible’ throughout.
∎

We recall from Proposition
3.1
that
\mathscr{I}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,n}
is the ideal of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Since
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is an irreducible affine variety, this ideal is radical and prime. However, it is speculated in
[
14
]
that the conjectured limit
\langle X^{2},\tr(X)\rangle
in Conjecture
5.1
may fail to be prime. This follows immediately from Proposition
5.4
: If
n
is even, then

Z\bigl(\langle X^{2},\tr(X)\rangle\bigr)=\{X\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):X^{2}=0\}\cong\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L_{\infty}=Z_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Z_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=\overline{Z}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}

has two irreducible components.

Theorem 5.5

(Projective closure of the Grassmannian)
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle++}
with
k\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
and let

Z_{1},\dots,Z_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
be as in (
20
). Then

\displaystyle\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\sqcup Z_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Z_{k},

\displaystyle\partial\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle=Z_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Z_{k}=\overline{Z}_{k}.

In particular, we have

(21)

\partial\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{[X:0]\in\mathbb{P}(\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\oplus\mathbb{C}):X^{2}=0\}.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}\cap L_{\infty}=Z_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Z_{k}
. By Lemma
5.3
, we already have

\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L_{\infty}=Z_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Z_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

Since
\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}\subseteq\Gr^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we must have
\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}\cap L_{\infty}\subseteq Z_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Z_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
. Since
Z_{d}
is an
\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-orbit, either
Z_{d}\subseteq\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}\cap L_{\infty}
or
Z_{d}\cap\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}\cap L_{\infty}=\varnothing
, for any
d=1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. By Lemma
5.3
,
\dim Z_{d}=d(n-d)>k(n-k)
if
n/2\geq d>k
. Thus
Z_{d}
is not contained in
\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}\cap L_{\infty}
if
n/2\geq d>k
, from which we deduce that
\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}\cap L_{\infty}\subseteq Z_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Z_{k}
.

For the reverse inclusion, set
X_{d}=\begin{bmatrix}S\otimes I_{d}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}
and we will construct an approximation of
[X_{d}:0]
by elements in
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\oplus\mathbb{C}\bigr)
for each
d=1,\dots,k
. Let

S_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon+i&1\\
1&-(\varepsilon+i)\end{bmatrix},\quad T_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon&\sqrt{2\varepsilon i}\\
\sqrt{2\varepsilon i}&-\varepsilon\end{bmatrix},\quad 0<\varepsilon<1.

Then

S_{\varepsilon}^{2}=T_{\varepsilon}^{2}=\frac{\varepsilon(\varepsilon+2i)}{4}I_{2},\quad\tr(S_{\varepsilon})=\tr(T_{\varepsilon})=0,\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}S_{\varepsilon}=S,\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}T_{\varepsilon}=0.

Now let

X_{d}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}S_{\varepsilon}\otimes I_{d}&0&0\\
0&T_{\varepsilon}\otimes I_{k-d}\\
0&0&\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\varepsilon(\varepsilon+2i)}I_{n-2k}\end{bmatrix}\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})

so that

X_{d}(\varepsilon)^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon(\varepsilon+2i)}{4}I_{n}=0,\qquad\tr(X_{d}(\varepsilon))-\frac{1}{2}(n-2k)\sqrt{\varepsilon(\varepsilon+2i)}=0.

Hence we have

\biggl[X_{d}(\varepsilon):\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon(\varepsilon+2i)}}{2}\biggr]\in\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\oplus\mathbb{C})

and

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\biggl[X_{d}(\varepsilon):\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon(\varepsilon+2i)}}{2}\biggr]=[X_{d}:0]

as required.
∎

As a consequence of Theorem
5.5
, we have the following set-theoretic characterization of
\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}
.

Corollary 5.6

(Equations of projective closure)
.

For positive integers
k\leq n
, the projective closure of
\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is given by

(22)

\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\bigl\{[X:t]\in\mathbb{P}(\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\oplus\mathbb{C}):\\
X^{2}-t^{2}I_{n}=0,\;\rank(X+tI_{n})\leq k,\;\rank(X-tI_{n})\leq n-k\bigr\}.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.5
, each
[X:t]\in\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}
must satisfy the three conditions in (
22
). Conversely, if
t=0
and
[X:0]\in\mathbb{P}(\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\oplus\mathbb{C})
satisfies the three conditions in (
22
), then setting
d\coloneqq\rank(X)\leq\min\{k,n-k\}
gives us
[X:0]\in Z_{d}\subseteq\overline{\Gr_{\mathbb{C}}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})}
. If
t\neq 0
, we may assume
t=1
. If
[X:1]\in\mathbb{P}(\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\oplus\mathbb{C})
satisfies the three conditions in (
22
), then the eigenvalues of
X\in\Sym^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\cap\O_{n}(\mathbb{C})
must be
1
and
-1
with multiplicities at least
k
and
n-k
respectively — but this compels them to be exactly
k
and
n-k
.
∎

6.
Conclusion

We would like to add a word about the
quadratic model

[
40
]
of the Grassmannian, i.e.,

The degree and projective closure are arguably the two most fundamental properties of an embedded variety from an algebraic geometric perspective. This article provides complete characterizations for
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
embedded via (
1
) or (
2
). There is also the notion of Euclidean distance degree for an embedded variety and we have determined its value for
\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
in
[

]

1
) and (
2

\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})

\O_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. From a differential geometric perspective, these embeddings call for a study of their second fundamental forms, which may be found in
