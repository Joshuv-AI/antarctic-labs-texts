C γ C^{\gamma} well-posedness of some non-linear transport equations
J.C. Cantero

Abstract
Given k : ℝ n ∖ { 0 } → ℝ n k:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}^{n} a kernel of class C 2 C^{2} and homogeneous of degree 1 − n 1-n , we prove existence and uniqueness of Hölder regular solutions for some non-linear transport equations with velocity fields given by convolution of the density with k k . The Aggregation, the 3D quasi geostrophic, and the 2D Euler equations can be recovered for particular choices of k k . AMS 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35Q35 (primary) 35F20, 35B30 (secondary). Keywords: non-linear transport equation, vortex patches, Euler equation, quasi-geostrophic equation, compressible flow.



1
Introduction

Let
\rho(x,t)
a scalar quantity usually known as the
density
and let
v(x,t)
a vector field called
velocity
both depending on the position
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and on the time
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The
(homogeneous) transport equation
for the pair (
\rho
,
v
) is the partial differential equation
defined by

(1)

\begin{cases}\rho_{t}+v\cdot\nabla\rho=0,\\
\rho(\cdot,0)=\rho_{0}.\end{cases}

Given a velocity field
v
and a point
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we set, whenever it is well defined, the
flow map

\begin{split}X(\alpha,\cdot):\mathbb{R}&\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
t&\rightarrow X(\alpha,t)\end{split}

as the solution of the ordinary differential equation

(2)

\begin{cases}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}X(\alpha,t)=v(X(\alpha,t),t),\\
X(\alpha,0)=\alpha.\end{cases}

This map indicates the position at time
t
of the particle that was initially at
\alpha
and that has moved following the velocity field at every moment. It is also called the
trajectory
of the particle initially at
\alpha
. If
\rho(\cdot,t)
is smooth enough, then a straightforward computation allow us to check that
\rho(X(\alpha,t),t)=\rho_{0}(\alpha).

That means that the density at time 0 and at position
\alpha
takes the same value as the density evaluated at
t
and at the future position of
\alpha
at time
t
. So, it can be said that
\rho
is transported with the flow defined by the velocity field
v
. This is a good reason to call (
1
) a
transport equation
.

For a fixed time, the functions
\rho
and
v
in (
1
) can be related by some functional
T
so that
v(\cdot,t)=T(\rho(\cdot,t))
and often
T
can be expressed as a convolution with a given kernel. In this situation, the equation is not lineal for sure. The most important example of a transport equation of this kind is the Euler equation in the plane. Let
N(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln(\left|x\right|)
be the fundamental solution of the Laplacian and let

K_{BS}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{2\pi\left|x\right|^{2}}(-x_{2},x_{1})=\nabla^{\perp}N(x).

We call
K_{BS}
the Biot-Savart kernel. Let
\omega(\cdot,t)
denote the vorticity, which is the scalar curl of the velocity field
v(x,t)
, i.e.,

\omega(x,t)=\partial_{1}v_{2}(x,t)-\partial_{2}v_{1}(x,t).

Then the vorticity formulation of the Euler equation is

\begin{cases}\omega_{t}+v\cdot\nabla\omega=0,\\
v(\cdot,t)=K_{BS}\ast\omega(\cdot,t),\\
\omega(\cdot,0)=\omega_{0},\end{cases}

which is a non-linear transport equation for
(\omega,v)
.

Another example in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see
[
BLL
, Section 4.2]
for more details of its derivation) is the aggregation equation when the initial condition
\rho_{0}
is the characteristic function of some domain
D_{0}
,
\chi_{D_{0}}
. We will call solutions for this type of initial data
density patches
. Let
w_{n}
the volume of the
n
-dimensional unit ball and set

(3)

N(x)=-\frac{1}{n(n-2)w_{n}}\frac{1}{\left|x\right|^{n-2}},\,\,\,\,\,n\geq 3,

the fundamental solution of the Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In this case, given
K_{Ag}=-\nabla N
we get

\begin{cases}\rho_{t}+v\cdot\nabla\rho=0,\\
v(\cdot,t)=K_{Ag}\ast\rho(\cdot,t),\\
\rho(\cdot,0)=\rho_{0}=\chi_{D_{0}}.\end{cases}

We would like to remark that the original aggregation equation is not a transport equation. The equivalence with the equation above is just for the initial condition equal to the characteristic function of a domain, as mentioned above.

In the spirit of generalizing these example equations we will consider throughout this paper a kernel
k:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, homogeneous of degree
-(n-1)
and of class
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}).
That is, for such
k
, we consider the following general transport equation:

\begin{cases}\rho_{t}+v\cdot\nabla\rho=0,\\
v(\cdot,t)=k\ast\rho(\cdot,t),\\
\rho(\cdot,0)=\rho_{0}=\chi_{D_{0}}.\end{cases}

Our goal is to prove a well-posedness result for the transport equation and for the kernel
k
in some space of functions that will be defined in a moment. We would like to anticipate that the divergence of
v
is an important quantity appearing in the computations and in the proofs we are going to develop. For the Euler equation the divergence vanishes everywhere for any time and for the aggregation equation the divergence at a given time
t
is equal to
-\rho(\cdot,t)
. Owing to the special simple form of the divergence the proofs of well-posedness in the above two cases are relatively fluent.

The well-posedness will be proved in spaces of Hölder smooth functions. We define them now.

Definition 1
.

Given
0<\gamma<1
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
let

\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|f(x)\right|\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\left|f\right|_{\gamma}=\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}}{{x\neq y}}}\frac{\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|}{\left|x-y\right|^{\gamma}}.

We define the norm

\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{\gamma}:=\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|f\right|_{\gamma}.

For
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
x\to F(x)=(f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{d}(x))
, we define

\left|\left|F\right|\right|_{\gamma}:=\sup_{i=1,\ldots,d}\left|\left|f_{i}\right|\right|_{\gamma}

and then the space

C^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})=\left\{f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{\gamma}<\infty\right\}.

Finally, we define

\left|F\right|_{1,\gamma}=\left|F(0)\right|+\left|\left|\nabla F\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|\nabla F\right|_{\gamma},

where

\left|\left|\nabla F\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}=\sup_{i=1,\ldots,d}\left(\sup_{j=1,\ldots,n}\left|\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}f_{i}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\right),

\left|\nabla F\right|_{\gamma}=\sup_{i=1,\ldots,d}\left(\sup_{j=1,\ldots,n}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}f_{i}\right|_{\gamma}\right).

We define the Hölder space
C^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})
as

C^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})=\left\{f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{1,\gamma}<\infty\right\}.

When it is clear enough, we will just write
C^{1,\gamma}
.

Additionally, we define
C^{\gamma}_{c}
as the space of functions in
C^{\gamma}
which are also compactly supported.

We are ready to anticipate the main theorem of this paper.

Theorem 2
.

Let
N
the fundamental solution of the Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
k\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\};\mathbb{R}^{n})
a kernel homogeneous of degree
1-n
. For
0<\gamma<1
, if
\rho_{0}\in C^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, then the transport equation

\begin{cases}\rho_{t}+v\cdot\nabla\rho=0,\\
v(\cdot,t)=k\ast\rho(\cdot,t),\\
\rho(\cdot,0)=\rho_{0},\end{cases}

has a unique weak solution
\rho(\cdot,t)\in C^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
for any time
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

The reason we have chosen this space is double. Firstly, the result was proved for the Euler equation (see
[
MB
, Chapter 4]
) and for the Aggregation kernel (see
[
CGK
, Theorem 5.3]
). Secondly, we wanted to be sure about having well-posedness in the smooth case before moving to other situations (for instance, density patches). In fact, if one wants to deal with the Yudovich problem, that is, proving well-posedness for an initial data in
L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}
, a strategy would be first to smoothen the initial data via convolution with a mollifier, then apply the smooth Theorem presented here, check some compactness properties, take limit of the solutions of the mollified equations and verify that they indeed satisfy the original equation. So, the result presented here can be seen as the first necessary step to prove existence of weak solutions of the equation.

The fact that the divergence is as general as the situation envisaged allows makes the known argument much more involved; some differences arise and overcoming them requires often a delicate treatment. We will stress this fact in the next sections whenever those differences appear.

Remark 5

The proof of Lemma

3
Local Theorem

As in the case of Euler equation, a good way to prove an existence and uniqueness result is by dealing with an, in some sense, equivalent equation rather than the one presented in Theorem
2
. Recall that
\rho
is transported with the flow, i.e.,
\rho(x,t)=\rho_{0}(X^{-1}(x,t))
for
X^{-1}(\cdot,t)
the inverse of the flow
X(\cdot,t)
. Therefore by (
10
) we have

\left|\left|\rho(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{\gamma}\leq\left|\left|\rho_{0}\right|\right|_{\gamma}\left(1+\left|X(\cdot,t)\right|_{1,\gamma}^{\gamma(2n-1)}\right).

Thus,
\rho(\cdot,t)\in C^{\gamma}
provided
X(\cdot,t)\in C^{1,\gamma}
.

Furthermore, eventually we will need to control the measure of the support of
\rho(\cdot,t)
. In order to do it, we have the next lemma, which will be also needed in the following section. Note that in the zero divergence case there is no need to control the support of
\rho(\cdot,t)
since its measure is conserved with time and therefore it is equal to the measure of the support of
\rho_{0}
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
(\rho,v)
be a solution of (
1
) and let
X
be the flow map associated to
v(\cdot,t)
as in (
2
). Then

m(\text{supp}(\rho(\cdot,t)))\leq c(n)m(\text{supp}(\rho_{0}))\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}^{n}.

Proof.

Given
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\mathbb{1}_{A}
be the function taking value
1
in
A
and
0
otherwise. Then

m(\text{supp}(\rho(\cdot,t)))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbb{1}_{\text{supp}(\rho(\cdot,t))}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Taking the change of variables
x=X(\alpha,t)
we get

m(\text{supp}(\rho(\cdot,t)))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbb{1}_{\text{supp}(\rho(\cdot,t))}(X(\alpha,t))\det DX(\alpha,t)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha.

Since
\rho
is transported with the flow, it is clear that
X(\alpha,t)\in\text{supp}(\rho(\cdot,t))
if and only if
\alpha\in\text{supp}(\rho_{0})
. Thus,

m(\text{supp}(\rho(\cdot,t)))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbb{1}_{\text{supp}(\rho_{0})}(\alpha)\det DX(\alpha,t)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha.

Taking absolute value on the previous equation and having into account that
\left|\left|\det DX(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\leq c(n)\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}^{n}
we get

m(\text{supp}(\rho(\cdot,t)))\leq c(n)m(\text{supp}(\rho_{0}))\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}^{n}.

∎

We can focus then on proving existence, uniqueness and regularity for
X
. We know
X
satisfies (
2
) and then, as
v(\cdot,t)=k\ast\rho(\cdot,t)
, we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t}(\alpha,t)=v(X(\alpha,t),t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k(X(\alpha,t)-x^{\prime})\rho(x^{\prime},t)\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}.

Applying a change of variables
x^{\prime}=X(\alpha^{\prime},t)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t}(\alpha,t)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k(X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t))\rho(X(\alpha^{\prime},t))\,\det[DX(\alpha^{\prime},t)]\,\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\prime}=\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k(X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\,\det[DX(\alpha^{\prime},t)]\,\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\prime},\end{split}

where, in the last equality, we have used that
\rho
is conserved along the flow.

Consequently, we have an ordinary differential equation (ODE) for
X
. A standard way to prove existence and uniqueness for an ODE is to apply Picard-Lindelöf’s theorem. It can be stated as follows.

Theorem 7

(Picard-Lindelöf)
.

Let
O\subseteq B
be an open subset of a Banach space
B
and let
F:O\to B
be a locally Lipschitz continuous mapping.

Then given
X_{0}\in O
, there exists a time
T>0
such that the ordinary differential equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t}=F(X),\phantom{aaaaaa}X(\cdot,t=0)=X_{0}\in O,

has a unique (local) solution
X\in C^{1}\left[(-T,T);O\right]
.

So, in order to apply Theorem
7
, we first need an equation of type
\frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t}=F(X)
. As we have seen, we have it for

(16)

F(X(\alpha,t)):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k(X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\,\det[DX(\alpha^{\prime},t)]\,\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\prime}.

Then we need a Banach space
B
and an open subspace of
B
such that the flow maps
X(\cdot,t)
belong to
O_{M}
. We also need a functional
F
mapping
O_{M}
to
B
being this map locally Lipschitz continuous and satisfying that
F(X(\alpha,t))
is equal to (
16
). Let
B=C^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

(17)

O_{M}=B\cap\left\{X:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,\frac{1}{M}<\sup_{\alpha\neq\beta}\frac{\left|X(\alpha)-X(\beta)\right|}{\left|\alpha-\beta\right|}<M\right\}.

Then we have:

•

O_{M}
is non-empty:
Id\in O_{M}\,\,\forall M>1
.

•

It is an open set since it is the preimage of the open set
(\frac{1}{M},M)
for some norm function (which is continuous).

•

If
X\in O_{M}
, then the image of
X
is open because
X
is locally a diffeomorphism and it is also closed because it is complete (
X
is a bilipschitz function) . Then the image of
X
is the whole space and so
X
is a homeomorphism.

After this, we have to check the hypothesis in Picard-Lindelöf’s theorem. Since computations of derivatives of
F(X)
will be needed, we first look how distributional derivatives of our kernels are.

Lemma 8
.

Given
k=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
k\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
homogeneous of degree
1-n
, we have, distributionally, for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

\partial_{i}k_{j}=\text{p.v. }\partial_{i}k_{j}+c_{ij}\delta_{0},

where

(18)

c_{ij}=\int_{\partial B(0,1)}k_{j}(s)s_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(s).

Proof.

Let
f
be such that
k_{j}\ast f
makes perfect sense and
\varphi
a
C^{\infty}
and compactly supported test function. Then, computing the directional derivative
\partial_{i}
,

\begin{split}\langle\partial_{i}(k_{j}\ast f),\varphi\rangle&=-\langle k_{j}\ast f,\partial_{i}\varphi\rangle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k_{j}(x-y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right\}\partial_{i}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k_{j}(x-y)\partial_{i}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right\}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(y,\varepsilon)}k_{j}(x-y)\partial_{i}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right\}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(y,\varepsilon)}\partial_{i}[k_{j}(x-y)\varphi(x)]\,\mathrm{d}x\right\}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\\
&+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(y,\varepsilon)}\partial_{i}k_{j}(x-y)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right\}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(y,\varepsilon)}\partial_{i}[k_{j}(x-y)\varphi(x)]\,\mathrm{d}x\right\}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\\
&+\langle\text{p.v. }\partial_{i}k_{j}\ast f,\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\langle\text{p.v. }\partial_{i}k_{j}\ast f,\varphi\rangle,\end{split}

for
g(y):=-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(y,\varepsilon)}\partial_{i}[k_{j}(x-y)\varphi(x)]\,\mathrm{d}x
. Applying Stokes’ Theorem to the integral defining
g(y)
we get

\begin{split}g(y)&=-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(y,\varepsilon)}\partial_{i}[k_{j}(x-y)\varphi(x)]\,\mathrm{d}x=\\
&=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\partial B(y,\varepsilon)}k_{j}(x-y)\varphi(x)n_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x),\end{split}

where
n_{i}(x)
is the
i
-th component of
n(x)
, the exterior normal vector to the surface
\partial B(y,\varepsilon)
at the point
x
. Subtracting and adding
k_{j}(x-y)\varphi(y)n_{i}(x)
in the integrand we have

(19)

\begin{split}g(y)&=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\partial B(y,\varepsilon)}k_{j}(x-y)(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))n_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)+\\
&+\varphi(y)\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\partial B(y,\varepsilon)}k_{j}(x-y)n_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x),\end{split}

The first integral vanishes when taking the limit by the continuity of
\varphi
. Hence, we can write
g(y)=\varphi(y)h(y)
and we need to compute the value of the integral in
h(y)
. By a change of variable
x=y+\varepsilon s
,
s\in\partial B(0,1)
we get

\begin{split}h(y)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\partial B(0,1)}k_{j}(\varepsilon s)n_{i}(y+\varepsilon s)\varepsilon^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(s)=\int_{\partial B(0,1)}k_{j}(s)s_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(s)=:c_{ij},\end{split}

by making use of the homogeneity of the kernel
k_{j}
and the fact that
n(x)=x
for
x\in\partial B(0,1)
. Since
k
is locally integrable out of the origin, then
c_{ij}
is a well defined quantity independent of
y
. Then
g(y)=c_{ij}\varphi(y)
and therefore,

\langle\partial_{i}(k_{j}\ast f),\varphi\rangle=\langle\text{p.v. }\partial_{i}k_{j}\ast f,\varphi\rangle+\langle c_{ij}\delta_{0}\ast f,\varphi\rangle,

which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We are now in position to show that
F:O_{M}\to B
.

Proposition 9
.

Let
O_{M}
as defined in (
17
). Then, the functional
F
defined by

(20)

F(X)(\alpha)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\,\det[DX(\alpha^{\prime})]\,\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\prime}

maps
O_{M}
to
C^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

Let
X\in O_{M}
. In order to prove the proposition, we need to verify

(21)

\left|\left|F(X)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}+\sup_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}\left(\left|\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{i}}F(X)\right|\right|_{\gamma}\right)<\infty.

If we consider the change of variables
x^{\prime}=X(\alpha^{\prime})
in (
20
) we get, for the
j
-th component

(22)

\begin{split}F_{j}(X)(\alpha)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k_{j}(X(\alpha)-x^{\prime})\rho_{0}(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}=\\
&=(k_{j}\ast(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}))(X(\alpha)).\end{split}

Let
R=\textrm{m}(\textrm{supp }(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}))^{1/n}
. Then, as in Lemma
6
, we have, for
R_{0}=\textrm{m}(\textrm{supp }(\rho_{0}))^{1/n}
that

R\leq c_{n}R_{0}\left|\left|\nabla X\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}.

Since the kernel
k
satisfies (
11
) then by (
12
) in Lemma
4
we have

\left|\left|F(X)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\leq cR\left|\left|\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}=cR\left|\left|\rho_{0}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\leq c_{n}R_{0}\left|\left|\nabla X\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|\left|\rho_{0}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}},

which is bounded for
X\in C^{1,\gamma}
and
\rho_{0}\in C^{\gamma}_{c}
.

We focus then on the norms of derivatives of
F(X)
. We write
\partial_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{i}}
. We have, by definition of the norm,
\left|\left|\partial_{i}F(X)\right|\right|_{\gamma}=\sup_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}\left|\left|\partial_{i}F_{j}(X)\right|\right|_{\gamma}
. We work then with
\partial_{i}F_{j}(X)
for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
An application of the chain rule, combined with Lemma
18
, yields

(23)

\begin{split}\partial_{i}&F_{j}(X)(\alpha)=\nabla(k_{j}\ast(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}))(X(\alpha))\cdot\partial_{i}X(\alpha)=\\
&=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\partial_{r}(k_{j}\ast(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}))(X(\alpha))\partial_{i}X_{r}(\alpha)=\\
&=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left(c_{rj}\rho_{0}(\alpha)+\text{p.v.}(\partial_{r}k_{j}\ast(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}))(X(\alpha))\right)\partial_{i}X_{r}(\alpha)=\\
&=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left(c_{rj}\rho_{0}(\alpha)+S_{rj}(\alpha)\right)\partial_{i}X_{r}(\alpha),\end{split}

where
S_{rj}(\alpha):=\text{p.v.}(\partial_{r}k_{j}\ast(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}))(X(\alpha))
and
\cdot
stands for the usual scalar product.

Since
\rho_{0},\partial_{i}X_{r}\in C^{\gamma}
and
C^{\gamma}
is an algebra, then it suffices to control the
C^{\gamma}
norm of
S_{rj}
. Clearly, hypothesis in Lemma
4
are satisfied if
P=\partial_{r}k_{j}
. Then we set
\varepsilon=\left|\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}\right|_{\gamma}^{1/\gamma}
and apply bound (
13
) in Lemma
4
to get

(24)

\left|\left|S_{rj}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\leq c\left\{1+\max(1,\frac{1}{\gamma}\ln(R\left|\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}\right|_{\gamma}))\left|\left|\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\right\}

where
R=\textrm{m}(\textrm{supp }(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}))^{1/n}
. As previously,
R
is bounded by
c_{n}R_{0}\left|\left|X\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}
. Since both
\left|\left|\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}
and
\left|\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}\right|_{\gamma}
are bounded above by
\left|\left|\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}\right|\right|_{\gamma}
and taking also into account that

\left|\left|\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}\right|\right|_{\gamma}\leq\left|\left|\rho_{0}\right|\right|_{\gamma}(1+\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}^{\gamma(2n-1)})<\infty,

then we have that the right hand side of (
24
) is finite. On the other hand, by (
14
) we have

\left|S_{rj}\right|_{\gamma}\leq c\left|\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}\right|_{\gamma}\left|\left|\nabla X\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma}\leq c\left|\left|\rho_{0}\right|\right|_{\gamma}(1+\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}^{\gamma(2n-1)})\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}^{\gamma}.

Then, as we argued before, the
C^{\gamma}
norm of
\partial_{i}F_{j}(X)
is finite for any
i,j
so the supremum in (
21
) is finite as well, completing the proof of the proposition.
∎

Then we have that
F
satisfies the first hypothesis in Picard-Lindelöf’s theorem. It remains to check that
F
is locally Lipschitz. We claim (and prove later) that if the directional derivative
F^{\prime}(X)
is bounded as a linear operator between
O_{M}
and
B
then
F
is locally Lipschitz. So, first of all we have to compute this directional derivative. An auxiliary lemma is useful for this computation and we need to give a previous definition to write it.

Definition 10
.

Given
A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we define
A_{i,j}^{c}\in M_{(n-1)\times(n-1)}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as the submatrix of A obtained by erasing the
i
-th row and the
j
-th column .

The following lemma is not needed whenever the velocity field is divergence free, as in the Euler equation. In that case,
\det(DX(\cdot,t))\equiv 1
and consequently the functional in (
20
) does not contain the determinant inside the integral. In the general case, as we will see later, an expression for the sum of determinants will be necessary.

Lemma 11
.

Given
X,Y:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
differentiable homeomorphisms.

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\det(DX+\varepsilon DY)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(-1)^{i+j}\partial_{j}Y_{i}\det(DX_{i,j}^{c}).

Proof.

First we use a formula for the determinant of a sum of square matrices. The proof can be found in
[
Ma
, pp. 162-163]
. Let
A,B\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
and let
\alpha,\beta
strictly increasing integer sequences chosen from
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Let
\left|\alpha\right|
(resp.
\left|\beta\right|
) the number of elements of
\alpha
(resp.
\beta
). If
\left|\alpha\right|=\left|\beta\right|
then let
A[\alpha|\beta]\in M_{\left|\alpha\right|\times\left|\alpha\right|}(\mathbb{R})
the submatrix of
A
lying in rows
\alpha
and columns
\beta
and
B[\alpha|\beta]\in M_{(n-\left|\alpha\right|)\times(n-\left|\alpha\right|)}(\mathbb{R})
the submatrix of
B
lying in rows complementary to
\alpha
and columns complementary to
\beta
. Let
s(\alpha)
(resp.
s(\beta)
) the sum of the integers in
\alpha
(resp.
\beta
). Then

(25)

\det(A+B)=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha,\beta}}{{\left|\alpha\right|=\left|\beta\right|=r}}}(-1)^{s(\alpha)+s(\beta)}\det(A[\alpha|\beta])\det(B[\alpha|\beta]).

Note that for a matrix
M\in M_{s\times s}(\mathbb{R})
and a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
we have
det(c\cdot M)=c^{s}\det(M)
. Then setting
A=DX
and
B=\varepsilon DY
in (
25
) we get

\det(DX+\varepsilon DY)=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\varepsilon^{n-r}\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|=\left|\beta\right|=r}(-1)^{s(\alpha)+s(\beta)}\det(DX[\alpha|\beta])\det(DY[\alpha|\beta]).

Differentiating with respect to
\varepsilon
the previous equation and setting
\varepsilon=0
make some terms vanish and, in consequence,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\det(DX+\varepsilon DY)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|=\left|\beta\right|=n-1}(-1)^{s(\alpha)+s(\beta)}\det(DX[\alpha|\beta])\det(DY[\alpha|\beta]).

Note that a strictly increasing sequence taking
n-1
elements of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
is a sequence avoiding just one of them. So

\alpha=(1,\ldots,i-1,i+1,\ldots,n),\,\,\,\,\beta=(1,\ldots,j-1,j+1,\ldots,n),

for
i,j=1,\ldots,n
. Then
s(\alpha)+s(\beta)=n(n+1)-(i+j)
and hence
(-1)^{s(\alpha)+s(\beta)}=(-1)^{i+j}
. For these special sequences we can simplify and write
DY[\alpha|\beta]=\partial_{j}Y_{i}
and
DX[\alpha|\beta]=DX_{i,j}^{c}
as in Definition
10
.

Summing up,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\det(DX+\varepsilon DY)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(-1)^{i+j}\partial_{j}Y_{i}\det(DX_{i,j}^{c})

and the lemma is proved.

∎

We already have the tools to compute the directional derivative of
F
.

Proposition 12
.

Let
X\in O_{M},Y\in B
. For
F=(F_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
defined in (
20
) we have
F_{j}^{\prime}(X)Y=
I+II where

\begin{split}\text{I}:&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla k_{j}(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))\cdot(Y(\alpha)-Y(\alpha^{\prime}))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\det(DX)(\alpha^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\prime},\\
\text{II}:&=\sum_{r,s=1}^{n}(-1)^{r+s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k_{j}(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\partial_{s}Y_{r}(\alpha^{\prime})\det(DX_{r,s}^{c})(\alpha^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\prime}.\end{split}

Proof.

Let
j=1,\ldots,n
. Consider
X\in O_{M}
and
Y\in B
. Firstly, we apply the chain rule to see

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}&\left(k_{j}(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime})+\varepsilon(Y(\alpha)-Y(\alpha^{\prime})))\right)_{\varepsilon=0}=\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}k_{j}(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))(Y_{i}(\alpha)-Y_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}))=\\
&=\nabla k_{j}(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))\cdot(Y(\alpha)-Y(\alpha^{\prime})),\end{split}

where
\cdot
is the usual scalar product. Thus having into account the above computation and applying Lemma
11
we get

\begin{split}&(F_{j}^{\prime}(X)Y)(\alpha)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}(F_{j}(X+\varepsilon Y)(\alpha))_{\varepsilon=0}=\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla k_{j}(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))\cdot(Y(\alpha)-Y(\alpha^{\prime}))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\det(DX)(\alpha^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\prime}+\\
&+\sum_{r,s=1}^{n}(-1)^{r+s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k_{j}(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\partial_{s}Y_{r}(\alpha^{\prime})\det(DX_{r,s}^{c})(\alpha^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\prime},\end{split}

as we wanted to prove.
∎

Remark 13
.

Note that there is no need to write principal value in the term I of the proposition because the singularity of
\nabla k_{j}
when
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
is compensated with the term
Y(\alpha)-Y(\alpha^{\prime})
.

The directional derivative computed in Proposition
12
has been decomposed as the sum of two terms. The second one is very similar to the one treated in Proposition
9
, but the first one looks different. It can be written as an integral with respect to a kernel which is not of convolution type and so its derivatives may be tricky to handle. In the following lemma, which is somehow technical, we compute exactly those derivatives.

Lemma 14
.

Let
I
defined in Proposition
12
. Then
I=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}(\alpha)
where

I_{i}(\alpha):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{i}k_{j}(X(\alpha)-X(\alpha^{\prime}))(Y_{i}(\alpha)-Y_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\det(DX)(\alpha^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\prime},

and its distributional derivatives are
\partial_{l}I_{i}(\alpha)=\nabla\tilde{I}_{i}(X(\alpha))\cdot\partial_{l}X(\alpha)

where

\begin{split}&\partial_{k}\tilde{I}_{i}(x)=\\
&=\text{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{k}\partial_{i}k_{j}(x-x^{\prime})(Y_{i}(X^{-1}(x))-Y_{i}(X^{-1}(x^{\prime})))(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1})(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+\\
&+\partial_{k}[Y_{i}\circ X^{-1}](x)\text{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{i}k_{j}(x-x^{\prime})(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1})(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+\\
&+c_{k}(\nabla[Y_{i}\circ X^{-1}](x)\cdot\xi_{k})(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1})(x),\end{split}

where
\xi_{k}
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
depending on
k
.

Proof.

Consider
\alpha=X^{-1}(x)
, then after the change of variables
\alpha^{\prime}=X^{-1}(x^{\prime})
, we have

\begin{split}\tilde{I}_{i}(x)&=I_{i}(X^{-1}(x))=\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{i}k_{j}(x-x^{\prime})(Y_{i}(X^{-1}(x))-Y_{i}(X^{-1}(x^{\prime})))\rho_{0}(X^{-1}(x^{\prime}))\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}.\end{split}

Following the scheme in
[
MB
, p. 165]
for the Euler equation, let
R(x,x^{\prime})=\partial_{i}k_{j}(x-x^{\prime})(Y_{i}(X^{-1}(x))-Y_{i}(X^{-1}(x^{\prime})))
. Firstly, we compute the partial distributional derivative with respect to
x_{k}
of
R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})
. In order to do that, note previously that, given
h>0
and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

\left|a\right|=1
, we have, by Taylor expansion of
Y\circ X^{-1}
,

(26)

\begin{split}\lim_{h\to 0}R(x+&ah,x)h^{n-1}=\\
&=\lim_{h\to 0}\partial_{i}k_{j}(ah)(Y_{i}(X^{-1}(x+ah))-Y_{i}(X^{-1}(x)))h^{n-1}=\\
&=\lim_{h\to 0}\partial_{i}k_{j}(a)h^{-n}h^{n-1}(\nabla[Y_{i}\circ X^{-1}](x)\cdot ah+o(h))=\\
&=\partial_{i}k_{j}(a)\nabla[Y_{i}\circ X^{-1}](x)\cdot a.\end{split}

We then compute the distributional derivative of
R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})
. Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
a test function. Then

\begin{split}\langle&\partial_{x_{k}}R(\cdot+x^{\prime},x^{\prime}),\varphi\rangle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{x_{k}}\varphi(x)R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x=\\
=&\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,\varepsilon)}\varphi(x)\partial_{x_{k}}R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x-\\
-&\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,\varepsilon)}\partial_{x_{k}}\left[\varphi(x)R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\right]\,\mathrm{d}x.\\
\end{split}

Applying Stokes’s theorem, and since
\varphi
has compact support we obtain

\begin{split}\langle&\partial_{x_{k}}R(\cdot+x^{\prime},x^{\prime}),\varphi\rangle=p.v.\langle\partial_{x_{k}}R(\cdot+x^{\prime},x^{\prime}),\varphi\rangle+\\
&+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\partial B(0,\varepsilon)}\varphi(x)R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})n_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x),\end{split}

where
n_{k}(x)
is the
k
-th component of the unitary normal vector to
\partial B(0,\varepsilon)
at the point
x
.
We apply the observation made in (
26
) to conclude that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\partial B(0,\varepsilon)}\varphi(x)R(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})n_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)=\varphi(0)\nabla[Y_{i}\circ X^{-1}](x^{\prime})\cdot\xi_{k},

where the
l
-th component of
\xi_{k}
is

(\xi_{k})_{l}=\int_{\partial B(0,1)}\partial_{i}k_{j}(a)n_{k}(a)\,a_{l}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(a).

Therefore, distributionally

\partial_{x_{k}}R(\cdot+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})=\text{p.v.}\partial_{x_{k}}R(\cdot+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})+(c_{k}\nabla[Y_{i}\circ X^{-1}](x^{\prime})\cdot\xi_{k})\delta_{0}.

Then, since
\partial_{x_{k}}R(x,x^{\prime})=\partial_{x_{k}}[R(\cdot+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})](x-x^{\prime})
we finally get for
H(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}R(x,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}
,

\begin{split}\partial_{k}H(x)&=\text{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{k}R(x,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+\\
&+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\delta_{0}(x-x^{\prime})c_{k}\nabla[Y_{i}\circ X^{-1}](x^{\prime})\cdot\xi_{k}f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}=\\
&=\text{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{k}\partial_{i}k_{j}(x-x^{\prime})(Y_{i}(X^{-1}(x))-Y_{i}(X^{-1}(x^{\prime})))f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+\\
&+\partial_{k}[Y_{i}\circ X^{-1}](x)\text{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{i}k_{j}(x-x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+\\
&+c_{k}\nabla[Y_{i}\circ X^{-1}](x)\cdot\xi_{k}f(x).\end{split}

The proof is completed setting
f=\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}
in the previous expression and applying the chain rule to
I_{i}(\alpha)=\tilde{I}_{i}(X(\alpha))
.

∎

Remember that our goal is to bound the
C^{1,\gamma}
norm of the map
\alpha\to F^{\prime}(X)Y(\alpha)
in such a way that we get

\left|F^{\prime}(X)Y\right|_{1,\gamma}\leq c\left|Y\right|_{1,\gamma}

for
c
depending maybe on
n,\rho_{0}
and
X
in order to have that
F^{\prime}(X)
is bounded as a linear operator.
Note that the first term in
\partial_{k}\tilde{I}_{i}
in Lemma
14
is an integral containing
\partial_{k}\partial_{i}k_{j}
, which is a hypersingular kernel. Nevertheless, the term
Y(\alpha)-Y(\alpha^{\prime})
will, in some sense, kill this excess of singularity. We quantify this effect in the following Lemma.

Lemma 15
.

Let
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
H\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
, be a kernel homogeneous of degree
-n-1

such that
H_{1}^{i}(x)=x_{i}H(x)
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, define a CZO of convolution type. Let
g\in C^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
and
f\in C^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
. Then for

Tf(x)=\text{p.v. }\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}H(x-x^{\prime})(g(x)-g(x^{\prime}))f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

we have

\left|\left|Tf\right|\right|_{\gamma}\leq c\left|g\right|_{1,\gamma}\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{\gamma},

for
c
depending on
\text{m(supp(}f))
.

Proof.

Since
g\in C^{1,\gamma}
we can write its Taylor series centered at
x^{\prime}
as

g(x)=g(x^{\prime})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}g(x^{\prime})(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})+R(x,x^{\prime})

with
\left|R(x,x^{\prime})\right|\leq c\left|g\right|_{1,\gamma}\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|^{1+\gamma}
. Now, if we add and subtract some term we obtain

\begin{split}Tf(x)=&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}H(x-x^{\prime})(g(x)-g(x^{\prime})-\nabla g(x^{\prime})\cdot(x-x^{\prime}))f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{p.v. }&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})H(x-x^{\prime})\partial_{i}g(x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}=:T_{1}f(x)+T_{2}f(x),\end{split}

The kernel
H_{g}(x,x^{\prime}):=H(x-x^{\prime})(g(x)-g(x^{\prime})-\nabla g(x^{\prime})\cdot(x-x^{\prime}))
satisfies the bound
\left|H_{g}(x,x^{\prime})\right|\leq\frac{c\left|g\right|_{1,\gamma}}{\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|^{n-\gamma}}
and its gradient

\begin{split}\nabla_{x}H_{g}(x,x^{\prime})&=\nabla H(x-x^{\prime})(g(x)-g(x^{\prime})-\nabla g(x^{\prime})(x-x^{\prime}))+\\
&+H(x-x^{\prime})(\nabla g(x)-\nabla g(x^{\prime}))\end{split}

satisfies
\left|\nabla_{x}H_{g}(x,x^{\prime})\right|\leq\frac{c\left|g\right|_{1,\gamma}}{\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|^{n+1-\gamma}}
.
To check that
T_{1}f
belongs to
C^{\gamma}
we use an usual argument. We can see
\left|T_{1}f(x)\right|\leq c\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|g\right|_{1,\gamma}
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
\left|\left|T_{1}f\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\leq c\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|g\right|_{1,\gamma}
. Now, let
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
B:=B(x_{1},3\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|)
. We decompose

\begin{split}T_{1}f(x_{1})-T_{1}f(x_{2})&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B}(H_{g}(x_{1},x^{\prime})-H_{g}(x_{2},x^{\prime}))f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+\\
&+\int_{B}H_{g}(x_{1},x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}-\int_{B}H_{g}(x_{2},x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}.\end{split}

Then, by the Mean Value Theorem and the bounds for
H_{g}
and
\nabla_{x}H_{g}
, we have

\begin{split}&\left|T_{1}f(x_{1})-T_{1}f(x_{2})\right|\leq c\left|g\right|_{1,\gamma}\Big\{\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B}\frac{\left|f(x^{\prime})\right|}{\left|x_{1}-x^{\prime}\right|^{n+1-\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+\\
&+\int_{B}\frac{\left|f(x^{\prime})\right|}{\left|x_{1}-x^{\prime}\right|^{n-\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+\int_{B}\frac{\left|f(x^{\prime})\right|}{\left|x_{2}-x^{\prime}\right|^{n-\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\Big\}\leq\\
&\leq c\left|g\right|_{1,\gamma}\left\{\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|^{\gamma-1}+\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|^{\gamma}\right\}\leq\\
&\leq c\left|g\right|_{1,\gamma}\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|^{\gamma}.\end{split}

So
\left|T_{1}f\right|_{\gamma}\leq c\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|g\right|_{1,\gamma}
. To finish we need to bound
T_{2}f
. If we set
H_{1}^{i}(x)=x_{i}H(x)
, which is a CZO of convolution type by hypothesis, then since
T_{2}f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{1}^{i}\ast(f\partial_{i}g)
, by Lemma
4
we have

\left|\left|T_{2}f\right|\right|_{\gamma}\leq c\left|\left|f\nabla g\right|\right|_{\gamma}\leq c\left|g\right|_{1,\gamma}\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{\gamma},

finishing the proof of the lemma.

∎

The constant
c
in Lemma
15
is finite whenever
f
is compactly supported, but we know this is true by Lemma
6
.
In a few words, Lemma
6
states that
\rho(\cdot,t)
is compactly supported when it is transported by a flow with bounded gradient as it happens, in particular, if
X\in O_{M}
. We are ready to check that
F^{\prime}(X)
is bounded. As promised, taking into account that boundedness, we can verify that the second hypothesis in Picard-Lindelöf holds for
F
.

Proposition 16
.

Let
O_{M}
as defined in (
17
). Then, the functional
F:O_{M}\to C^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
defined in (
20
) is locally Lipschitz.

Proof.

First of all, by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, given
X_{1},X_{2}\in O_{M}
,

\begin{split}\left|F(X_{1})-F(X_{2})\right|_{1,\gamma}&=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}F(X_{1}+\varepsilon(X_{2}-X_{1}))\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\right|_{1,\gamma}=\\
&=\left|\left(\int_{0}^{1}F^{\prime}(X_{1}+\varepsilon(X_{2}-X_{1}))\cdot(X_{2}-X_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\right)\right|_{1,\gamma}\leq\\
&\leq\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\left|\left|F^{\prime}(X_{1}+\varepsilon(X_{2}-X_{1}))\right|\right|_{B\to B}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\right\}\left|X_{2}-X_{1}\right|_{1,\gamma},\end{split}

where
F^{\prime}(X)
is the operator defined by
Y\to F^{\prime}(X)Y
. So, provided
\left|\left|F^{\prime}(X)\right|\right|_{B\to B}<\infty
for every
X\in B
then the integral in the previous expression is finite and therefore
F
is Lipschitz. Thus, it suffices to prove this boundedness in order to prove the Proposition.

By Proposition
12
we have seen that every component of
F^{\prime}(X)Y
can be written as the sum of two terms
I
and
II
. The arguments in Proposition
9
can be repeated for each element appearing in the sum in which
II
is decomposed, just by changing the role of
\rho_{0}
to
\rho_{0}\partial_{s}Y_{r}
. Then we can conclude, similarly, that

\left|II\right|_{1,\gamma}\leq c(n,\rho_{0})\left|Y\right|_{1,\gamma}\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}^{n}.

Hence, we just have to work with the first term,
I
, and bound its
C^{1,\gamma}
norm. Before considering derivatives, note that
I
can be compared to any derivative of
II
, so also in similar fashion to Proposition
9
, we get

\left|\left|I\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\leq c(n,\rho_{0})\left|Y\right|_{1,\gamma}\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}^{n}.

We then need to consider derivatives of
I
. By Lemma
14
we write
I=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}
and also
\partial_{l}I_{i}(\alpha)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{k}\tilde{I}_{i}(X(\alpha))\partial_{l}X_{k}(\alpha)
. Since
C^{\gamma}
is an algebra and also, by (
9
), we have

\begin{split}\left|\left|\partial_{i}I_{i}\right|\right|_{\gamma}&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\left|\partial_{k}\tilde{I}_{i}\circ X\right|\right|_{\gamma}\left|\left|\partial_{l}X_{k}\right|\right|_{\gamma}\leq\\
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\left|\partial_{k}\tilde{I}_{i}\right|\right|_{\gamma}(1+\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}^{\gamma})\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}.\end{split}

Now we focus on
\left|\left|\partial_{k}\tilde{I}_{i}\right|\right|_{\gamma}
. We consider the expression given by Lemma
14

\begin{split}&\partial_{k}\tilde{I}_{i}(x)=\\
&=\text{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{k}\partial_{i}k_{j}(x-x^{\prime})(Y_{i}(X^{-1}(x))-Y_{i}(X^{-1}(x^{\prime})))(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1})(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+\\
&+\partial_{k}[Y_{i}\circ X^{-1}](x)\text{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{i}k_{j}(x-x^{\prime})(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1})(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}+\\
&+c_{k}(\nabla[Y_{i}\circ X^{-1}](x)\cdot\xi_{k})(\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1})(x)=A(x)+B(x)+C(x).\end{split}

A straightforward repetition of the arguments done before let us verify that

\left|\left|C\right|\right|_{\gamma}\leq c(n,\rho_{0})\left|Y\right|_{1,\gamma}\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}^{m},

where
m
is a finite constant depending on
\gamma
and
n
.
Also, as in Proposition
9
, we get

\left|\left|B\right|\right|_{\gamma}\leq c(n,\rho_{0})\left|Y\right|_{1,\gamma}\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}^{m}.

The term
A
is more involved than the rest in the decomposition of
\partial_{k}\tilde{I}_{i}
. Nevertheless, we claim that we can apply Lemma
15
for
H=\partial_{k}\partial_{i}k_{j}
,
g=Y_{i}\circ X^{-1}
and
f=\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}
to obtain also

\left|\left|A\right|\right|_{\gamma}\leq c(n,\rho_{0},R)\left|Y\right|_{1,\gamma}\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}^{m},

where
R=\text{m(supp(}\rho_{0}\circ X^{-1}))=\text{m(supp(}\rho))
. We conclude that

\left|I\right|_{1,\gamma}\leq c(n,\rho_{0},R)\left|Y\right|_{1,\gamma}\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}^{n},

where
c(n,\rho,R)
is finite by Lemma
6
.

Summing up, for any
X\in B
and any
Y\in O_{M}
we have seen

\left|F^{\prime}(X)Y\right|_{1,\gamma}\leq c(n,\rho_{0},R)\left|X\right|_{1,\gamma}^{m}\left|Y\right|_{1,\gamma},

so
F^{\prime}(X)
is bounded as a linear operator from
O_{M}
to
B
and then the Proposition is proved.
∎

Remark 17
.

We have been able to apply Lemma
15
since the kernels

x_{i}\partial_{j}\partial_{l}k
are CZO. In general, if
k
satisfies the hypothesis in Lemma
4
then its second derivatives
\partial_{j}\partial_{l}k
satisfies Lemma
15
. It is clear that
\partial_{j}\partial_{l}k
is homogeneous of degree
-n-1
. Also, if
i\neq j
(and similarly if
i\neq l
),

\int_{\left|w\right|=1}w_{i}\partial_{j}\partial_{l}k(w)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(w)=\int_{\left|w\right|=1}\partial_{j}[w_{i}\partial_{l}k(w)]\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(w)=0.

The last integral vanishes for similar reasons as explained in Remark
5
. Otherwise, if
i=j=l
,

\begin{split}\int_{\left|w\right|=1}&w_{i}\partial_{i}^{2}k(w)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(w)=\\
&=\int_{\left|w\right|=1}\partial_{i}[w_{i}\partial_{i}k(w)]\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(w)-\int_{\left|w\right|=1}\partial_{i}k(w)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(w)=0\end{split}

since both integrals in the right vanishes, similarly as before.

Finally, since all the hypothesis in Theorem
7
are verified, we prove the existence result for the trajectory maps.

Theorem 18
.

Let
\rho_{0}\in C^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
. Then there exists
T^{*}>0
such that the ordinary differential equation

\begin{cases}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}X(\alpha,t)=F(X(\alpha,t)),\\
X(\alpha,0)=\alpha,\end{cases}

for

F(X(\alpha,t))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k(X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\,\det[DX(\alpha^{\prime},t)]\,\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\prime},

has a unique solution
X(\cdot,t)\in C^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
t\in(-T^{*},T^{*})
.

Proof.

Let
B=C^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and let
O_{M}
defined in (
17
). Then, by Propositions
9
and
16
the functional
F
satisfies the hypothesis of Picard-Lindelöf’s theorem
7
and therefore we conclude that the statement holds.
∎

Given the flow map
X(\cdot,t)
we can define the solution to (
1
) in an unique way: since the velocity field is smooth enough, any solution of the transport equation (
1
) can be described through the trajectories. So we have the main result of this section: well-posedness in the Hölder class for the transport equation and for the class of kernels described above.

Theorem 19
.

Let
\rho_{0}\in C^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
. Let
k\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\};\mathbb{R}^{n})
be homogeneous of degree
1-n
. Then there exists
T^{*}>0
such that the transport equation

(27)

\begin{cases}\rho_{t}+v\cdot\nabla\rho=0,\\
v(\cdot,t)=k\ast\rho(\cdot,t),\\
\rho(\cdot,0)=\rho_{0}.\end{cases}

has a unique solution
\rho(\cdot,t)\in C^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
,
v(\cdot,t)\in C^{1+\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
t\in(-T^{*},T^{*})
.

Proof.

By Theorem
18
up to time
T^{*}
there exists a unique solution
X(\cdot,t)\in C^{1+\gamma}
. Let
\tilde{\rho}\in C^{\gamma}_{c}
and
\tilde{v}\in C^{1+\gamma}
satisfying (
27
). Then we can find a trajectory
\tilde{X}(\cdot,t)
associated to
\tilde{v}(\cdot,t)
such that
\tilde{\rho}
is transported by
\tilde{X}
. By the uniqueness of trajectory by Theorem
18
then
\tilde{X}=X
. Then
\tilde{\rho}(\cdot,t)=\rho_{0}(\tilde{X}^{-1}(\cdot,t))=\rho_{0}(X^{-1}(\cdot,t))=\rho(\cdot,t)
and hence, by convoluting the density with the kernel
k
we can see that
\tilde{v}=v
.
∎

4
Global Theorem

We want to show that the solution defined in Theorem
18
does exist for any time, that is, we want to show that
T^{*}=\infty
. In order to do that, we need to invoke a Continuation Theorem which gives us a necessary condition for that to happen. The theorem is stated as in
[
MB
, p. 148]
and a proof for a general version of it can be found in
[
LL
, p. 161]
. We would like to remark that it is valid since we have been able to state the problem with a functional
F
which does not depend explicitly on time.

Theorem 20
.

In the situation of Theorem
7
the unique solution
X\in C^{1}((-T,T);O)
either exists globally in time or
T
is finite and
X(t)
leaves the open set
O
as
\left|t\right|
approaches
T
.

In a nutshell, we need to check that at time
T^{*}
the flow
X(\cdot,T^{*})
still belongs to
O_{M}
. As we will see later, it is enough to verify that the
C^{1,\gamma}
norm of
X(\cdot,T^{*})
is a priori bounded. The following lemma is an auxiliary result needed in order to achieve bounds that allow us to prove that boundedness.

Lemma 21
.

Let
X(\cdot,t)
defined in (
2
). Then for the inverse flow at time
t
, we have
X^{-1}(\cdot,t)=\tilde{X}(0,t,\cdot)
, where
\tilde{X}(s,t,x)
is the solution of the integro-differential equation

\tilde{X}(s,t,x)=x-\int_{s}^{t}v(\tilde{X}(r,t,x),r)\,\mathrm{d}r.

Proof.

We define a generalized flow map
\hat{X}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

(28)

\hat{X}(s,t,x)=x+\int_{s}^{t}v(\hat{X}(s,r,x),r)\,\mathrm{d}r.

Then
\hat{X}(s,t,x)
is the position at time
t
of the particle that was at the position
x
at time
s
. It is clear that
X(x,t)=\hat{X}(0,t,x).

First, we can check that
\hat{X}
has the semigroup structure

(29)

\hat{X}(s,t,x)=\hat{X}(\tau,t,\hat{X}(s,\tau,x)).

By definition of
\hat{X}
in (
28
), the right hand side of (
29
) can be expressed as

(30)

\hat{X}(\tau,t,\hat{X}(s,\tau,x))=\hat{X}(s,\tau,x)+\int_{\tau}^{t}v(\hat{X}(\tau,u,\hat{X}(s,\tau,x)),u)\,\mathrm{d}u.

We differentiate (
30
) with respect to
\tau
to get

\begin{split}\partial_{\tau}(\hat{X}(\tau,t,\hat{X}(s,\tau,x)))&=\partial_{\tau}\hat{X}(s,\tau,x)-v(\hat{X}(\tau,\tau,\hat{X}(s,\tau,x)),\tau)=\\
&=v(\hat{X}(s,\tau,x),\tau)-v(\hat{X}(s,\tau,x),\tau)=0.\end{split}

So
\hat{X}(\tau,t,\hat{X}(s,\tau,x))
does not depend on
\tau
and hence

\hat{X}(\tau,t,\hat{X}(s,\tau,x))=\hat{X}(\tau,t,\hat{X}(s,\tau,x))|_{\tau=t}=\hat{X}(t,t,\hat{X}(s,t,x))=\hat{X}(s,t,x),

which proves equation (
29
).

Secondly, we want to see that
\hat{X}
satisfies a transport equation. Differentiating (
29
) with respect to
s
we obtain

(31)

\begin{split}\partial_{s}\hat{X}(s,t,x)&=\nabla\hat{X}(\tau,t,\hat{X}(s,\tau,x))\partial_{s}\hat{X}(s,\tau,x)=\\
&=-\nabla\hat{X}(\tau,t,\hat{X}(s,\tau,x))v(\hat{X}(s,s,x),s)=\\
&=-\nabla\hat{X}(\tau,t,\hat{X}(s,\tau,x))v(x,s),\end{split}

and putting
\tau=s
in (
31
)

(32)

\partial_{s}\hat{X}(s,t,x)+v(x,s)\nabla\hat{X}(s,t,x)=0.

Thus,
\hat{X}(\cdot,t,\cdot):\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies a transport equation.

Then, we define a map
\tilde{X}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
via

(33)

\tilde{X}(s,t,x)=x-\int_{s}^{t}v(\tilde{X}(r,t,x),r)\,\mathrm{d}r.

We want to check that for any
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
and any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the maps
\hat{X}
and
\tilde{X}
are inverse in the following sense

(34)

\hat{X}(s,t,\tilde{X}(s,t,x))=x.

In order to prove (
34
) we differentiate its left hand side with respect to
s

\partial_{s}\hat{X}(s,t,\tilde{X}(s,t,x))+\nabla\hat{X}(s,t,\tilde{X}(s,t,x))\partial_{s}\tilde{X}(s,t,x).

By (
33
) we can see that
\partial_{s}\tilde{X}(s,t,x)=v(\tilde{X}(s,t,x),s)
and then the above expression can be written as

\begin{split}\partial_{s}&\hat{X}(s,t,\tilde{X}(s,t,x))+v(\tilde{X}(s,t,x),s)\nabla\hat{X}(s,t,\tilde{X}(s,t,x))=\\
&=\left[\partial_{s}\hat{X}(\cdot,t,\cdot)+v(\cdot,\cdot)\nabla\hat{X}(\cdot,t,\cdot)\right](x,\tilde{X}(s,t,x))=0\end{split}

since
\hat{X}(\cdot,t,\cdot)
satisfies the transport equation (
32
).

Thus, the left hand side of (
34
) does not depend on
s
and hence

\hat{X}(s,t,\tilde{X}(s,t,x))=\hat{X}(t,t,\tilde{X}(t,t,x))=x,

as we wanted to prove.

Taking
s=0
we get
\hat{X}(0,t,\tilde{X}(0,t,x))=X(\tilde{X}(0,t,x),t),
so
X(x,t)^{-1}=\tilde{X}(s,t,x)|_{s=0}
and the Lemma is proved.
∎

As a consequence of the previous Lemma, we see that the flow map
X(\cdot,t)
and its inverse
X^{-1}(\cdot,t)
share the same regularity properties since its integral expressions are similar. We will use this fact later on.

The next lemma is a well known and very classical fact in the theory of ordinary differential differential equations. For its proof see, for instance,
[
Pa
, p. 13]
.

Lemma 22

(Gronwall Lemma)
.

Let
u
and
f
be continuous and nonnegative functions defined on
I=[a,b]
, and let
n
be a continuous, positive and non-decreasing function defined on
I
. If

u(t)\leq n(t)+\int_{a}^{t}f(s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

for
t\in I
, then

u(t)\leq n(t)\exp\left(\int_{a}^{t}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

We are ready to give a first condition ensuring the a priori
C^{1,\gamma}
boundedness of the flow map at any time. The following bounds are very similar to those obtained for the Euler equation. The only difference that appears in the proof is the fact that the measure of the support of
\rho(\cdot,t)
is not constant over time, because the divergence is not zero in general.

Proposition 23
.

Let
X(\cdot,t)
be the solution given in Theorem
18
and
c(n)
a constant depending on the dimension
n
. Then, for a certain function
G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
with
G(t)<\infty
whenever

\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty
, we have the following inequalities

\begin{split}&\left|X(0,t)\right|\leq c(n)m(\text{supp}(\rho_{0}))^{1/n}\left|\left|\rho_{0}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s,\\
&\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s\right),\\
&\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}\leq G(t)\exp\left(c\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s\right).\end{split}

In particular,
\left|X(\cdot,t)\right|_{1,\gamma}
is bounded provided
\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s
also is.

Proof.

By definition (
2
) we have, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

X(\alpha,t)=\alpha+\int_{0}^{t}v(X(\alpha,s),s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Setting
\alpha=0
and taking absolute value we get

\left|X(0,t)\right|=\left|\int_{0}^{t}v(X(0,s),s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|v(X(\cdot,s),s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Since
X(\cdot,s)
is an homeomorphism then
\left|\left|v(X(\cdot,s),s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}=\left|\left|v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}
and therefore we simply have

\left|X(0,t)\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Now let
R(s)=m(\text{supp}(\rho(\cdot,s)))^{1/n}
. Then as
v(\cdot,s)=k\ast\rho(\cdot,s)
by (
12
) we get the bound

\left|\left|v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\leq cR(s)\left|\left|\rho(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}=cR(s)\left|\left|\rho_{0}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}},

where the last equality stands provided
\rho
is transported with the flow and so, the
L^{\infty}
norm is conserved in time.

By Lemma
6
we have a control for
R(s)
and then

\left|X(0,t)\right|\leq c(n)m(\text{supp}(\rho_{0}))^{1/n}\left|\left|\rho_{0}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

In order to achieve bounds on derivatives of the flow map, we compute the partial derivative with respect to
\alpha_{i}
(denoted as
\partial_{i}
from now on) of the
j
-th component of (
2
). By the chain rule, we get

(35)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\partial_{i}X_{j}(\alpha,t))&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial v_{j}(X(\alpha,t),t)}{\partial X_{k}(\alpha,t)}\partial_{i}X_{k}(\alpha,t)=\\
&=\nabla v_{j}(X(\alpha,t))\cdot\partial_{i}X(\alpha,t),\end{split}

where
\cdot
stands for the scalar product between vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Taking
L^{\infty}
norm on (
35
) and considering supremum over
i,j
we get

(36)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}&\leq\left|\left|\nabla v(X(\cdot,t),t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}=\\
&=\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}.\end{split}

Therefore, by direct integration on (
36
) we have the desired bound

(37)

\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

Finally, taking the
\left|\cdot\right|_{\gamma}
seminorm and considering supremum over
i,j
on (
35
) we have

(38)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}&\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}\leq\sup_{i,j}\left|\nabla v_{j}(X(\cdot,t))\cdot\partial_{i}X(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}\leq\\
&\leq c\left(\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}+\left|\nabla v(X(\cdot,t),t)\right|_{\gamma}\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\right),\end{split}

where we have used inequality (
4
) to bound the
\left|\cdot\right|_{\gamma}
seminorm of a product.

By (
8
) we have

(39)

\left|\nabla v(X(\cdot,t),t)\right|_{\gamma}\leq\left|\nabla v(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma}.

Also, by (
13
),

(40)

\left|\nabla v(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}\leq c\left|\rho(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}\leq c\left|\rho_{0}\right|_{\gamma}\left|\left|\nabla X^{-1}(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma},

where we have used that
\rho(\cdot,t)=\rho_{0}(X^{-1}(\cdot,t))
.
Using the equation for
X^{-1}(\cdot,t)
described in Lemma
21
and similarly as done in (
36
) we have

(41)

\left|\left|\nabla X^{-1}(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

Combining inequalities (
36
), (
39
), (
40
), (
41
), we get a bound for (
38
)

(42)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}&\leq c\left(\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}+\right.\\
&+\left.\left|\rho_{0}\right|_{\gamma}\exp\left((1+2\gamma)\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\right).\end{split}

Setting

\begin{split}g(t):=c\left|\rho_{0}\right|_{\gamma}\exp\left((1+2\gamma)\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\end{split}

and
G(t):=\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
, and then applying Lemma
22
to (
42
) we get

\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}\leq G(t)\exp\left(c\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s\right),

which completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Having proved the inequalities in Proposition
23
we can see that, in fact, the
C^{1,\gamma}
norm of the flow is finite for any time which was our first goal.

Proposition 24
.

Let
X(\cdot,t)
be the solution given in Theorem
18
. Then
\left|X(\cdot,t)\right|_{1,\gamma}
is finite for any time.

Proof.

By Proposition
23

\left|X(\cdot,t)\right|_{1,\gamma}
is finite provided
\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|\nabla v(\cdot,s)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}s
is. Then it suffices to check that this integral is bounded for any time. Let
i,j\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
. By Lemma
18
we have

(43)

\partial_{i}v_{j}(\cdot,t)=c_{ij}\rho(\cdot,t)+p.v.\partial_{i}k_{j}\ast\rho(\cdot,t),

where
c_{ij}
are defined in (
18
). Let
\varepsilon=\left|\rho(\cdot,t)\right|_{\gamma}^{-1/\gamma}
and let

Theorem 25
.

Let
\rho_{0}\in C^{\gamma}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
. Then the ordinary differential equation

\begin{cases}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}X(\alpha,t)=F(X(\alpha,t)),\\
X(\alpha,0)=\alpha,\end{cases}

for

F(X(\alpha,t))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k(X(\alpha,t)-X(\alpha^{\prime},t))\rho_{0}(\alpha^{\prime})\,\det[DX(\alpha^{\prime},t)]\,\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\prime}

has a unique solution
X(\cdot,t)\in C^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
for any time
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We want to apply Theorem
20
in order to ensure the globalness of the solution
X(\cdot,t)
. So, we need to check that, for any given
T
, the map

X(\cdot,T)
belongs to

where

O_{M}=B\cap\left\{X:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,\frac{1}{M}<\sup_{\alpha\neq\beta}\frac{\left|X(\alpha)-X(\beta)\right|}{\left|\alpha-\beta\right|}<M\right\}.

Let us first prove that we can avoid to check that the condition for
M
is satisfied at time
T
. By the Mean Value Theorem,

\left|X(\alpha,t)-X(\beta,t)\right|\leq\left|\left|\nabla X(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|\alpha-\beta\right|\leq\left|X(\cdot,t)\right|_{1,\gamma}\left|\alpha-\beta\right|,

and also since we can express
\alpha=X^{-1}((X(\alpha,t),t))
and
\beta=X^{-1}((X(\beta,t),t))
,

\left|\alpha-\beta\right|\leq\left|\left|\nabla X^{-1}(\cdot,t)\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|X(\alpha,t)-X(\beta,t)\right|\leq\left|X(\cdot,t)\right|_{1,\gamma}^{2n-1}\left|X(\alpha)-X(\beta)\right|.

Consider now

such that

\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}\max\{\left|X(\cdot,t)\right|_{1,\gamma},\left|X(\cdot,t)\right|_{1,\gamma}^{2n-1}\}\leq M^{\prime}<\infty.

Such an

\left|X(\cdot,t)\right|_{1,\gamma}

. For this choice of

for every time

.

Throughout the proofs of Proposition

and
16

M
. Due to this independence we can modify

to

without changing neither the solution nor the maximal time of existence given by Picard-Lindelöf’s theorem.

Then as soon as
