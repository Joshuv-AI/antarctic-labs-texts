A Spectral Sequence for a Graded Linear Map
L.M. Bates, M. Bendersky, R.C. Churchill

Abstract
. We apply the method of spectral sequences to study classical problems in analysis. We illustrate the method by finding polynomial vector fields that commute with a given polynomial vector field and finding integrals of polynomial Hamiltonian systems. For the later we describe the integrals for the Henon-Heiles Hamiltonian which arises in celestial mechanics. The unifying feature is that these problems seek elements in the kernel of a linear operator. The spectral sequence approach emphasizes the obstructions constructed from cokernel of the operator to finding elements in the kernel.

1.
Introduction

In
[
BeC
]
a spectral sequence was introduced to study the problem of computing normal forms for the elements of a graded Lie algebra. The approach offered a fairly efficient method for specifying these
forms in a number of classical
contexts,
[
BeC1
]
,
[
BeCC
]
, each of which could be formulated in terms of the kernel of an adjoint representation. The spectral sequence provided a systematic investigation of this kernel through a chain of approximations.

In this paper we indicate how those methods can be extended beyond normal forms. Specifically, we are concerned with uncovering elements of the kernel of a linear mapping
\,f:M\rightarrow N\,
between modules over a commutative ring. The key conceptual results are elementary: the work comes in applications when attempting to verify the hypotheses.The calculations involved in the examples can be extensive, and
one can view the role of the spectral sequence as a means for organizing the work. In particular,
we will see that certain modules constructed from the cokernel of the relevant linear mapping can be viewed as obstructions to continuing the calculations.
The spectral sequence approach can then be applied to such problems as constructing commuting polynomial vector fields, and determining independent integrals of Hamiltonian vector fields.

The method involves associating one of two spectral sequences with
\,f
,  and these sequences will come as no surprise to those familiar with such entities. However, to make the results accessible to a wider audience we do not assume familiarity with such sequences. In particular we point out that many of the notions associated to a spectral sequences appear with different names in other context. We illustrate this point with examples in appendix (
A
) of problems such as characterizing a Gröbner basis for a polynomial ideal and solving polynomial differential equations.

Throughout the paper rings are assumed both commutative and unitary, elements of rings are termed “scalars.” “Module” always means “unitary left module,” and explicit reference to the underlying ring is sometimes omitted.

Our notation and terminology for spectral sequences is essentially that found in
[
Mac
, Chapter XI, §3 and §8]
.

Lemma 2.4

(Euler’s Formula)
.

Suppose
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a differentiable homogeneous function of degree
r
one has

(i)

\dfrac{dF}{dx}\cdot x=rF(x)\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

where

(ii)

\dfrac{dF}{dx}(x)\cdot x\eqqcolon\Big[\dfrac{df_{i}}{dx_{j}}\Big]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{array}\right].

Proof :
By assumption one has

(iii)

F(\lambda x)=\lambda^{r}F(x).

By differentiating with respect to
\lambda
we have:

\dfrac{dF(\lambda x)}{d\lambda}=\dfrac{dF}{dx}(\lambda x)\frac{d}{d\lambda}\lambda x=\dfrac{dF}{dx}(\lambda x)\cdot x=r\lambda^{r-1}F(x),

whereupon (i) is obtained by setting
\lambda=1
.
\square

Corollary 2.5
.

Suppose
t,s\in\mathbb{N}
and
F\in\mathcal{L}_{t},G\in\mathcal{L}_{s}
. Then

{\rm ad}(F)(G)=\displaystyle{}\frac{1}{1+t}\,\Big(\dfrac{dG}{dx}\,\dfrac{dF}{dG}-\displaystyle{}\ell\,\dfrac{dF}{dX}\,\dfrac{dG}{dx}\Big)\cdot x,\quad\text{where}\quad\ell:=\frac{1+t}{1+s}.

Proof.

From Lemma
2.4
one has

(i)

F=\dfrac{1}{t+1}\cdot\dfrac{dF}{dx}\cdot x\mbox{ and }G=\dfrac{1}{s+1}\cdot\dfrac{dG}{dx}\cdot x.\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

The corollary now follow immediately from (i) and the equalities below:

\begin{array}[]{lllllll}{\rm ad}(F)(G)&=&\dfrac{dG}{dx}F-\dfrac{dF}{dx}G\\
&=&\displaystyle{}\frac{1}{1+t}\,\dfrac{dG}{dx}\,\dfrac{dF}{dG}\cdot x-\displaystyle{}\frac{1}{1+s}\,\dfrac{dF}{dX}\,\dfrac{dG}{dx}\cdot x\\
&=&\displaystyle{}\frac{1}{1+t}\,\Big(\dfrac{dG}{dx}\,\dfrac{dF}{dG}\cdot x-\displaystyle{}\ell\,\dfrac{dF}{dX}\,\dfrac{dG}{dx}\cdot x\Big),\quad\text{where}\quad\ell:=\frac{1+t}{1+s},\\
&=&\displaystyle{}\frac{1}{1+t}\,\Big(\dfrac{dG}{dx}\,\dfrac{dF}{dG}-\displaystyle{}\ell\,\dfrac{dF}{dX}\,\dfrac{dG}{dx}\Big)\cdot x,\quad\text{where}\quad\ell:=\frac{1+t}{1+s}.\end{array}

\square

Corollary 2.6
.

The following statements are equivalent.

(a)

({\rm ad}\,F)(G)=0
, i.e.
\,G\in\ker({\rm ad}\,F)
.

(b)

\dfrac{dG}{dx}F=\dfrac{dF}{dx}G.

(c)

\big(\dfrac{dG}{dx}\dfrac{dF}{dx}-\ell\dfrac{dF}{dx}\dfrac{dG}{dx}\big)\cdot x=0
, where

\ell:=\displaystyle{}\frac{t+1}{s+1}
.

(d)

M(x)\cdot x=0

\,M=M(x)=\dfrac{dG}{dx}(x)\dfrac{dF}{dx}(x)-\ell\dfrac{dF}{dx}(x)\dfrac{dG}{dx}(x)

(e)

If
\ \dfrac{dF}{dx}\,
is invertible, an additional equivalent condition is

\left(\left(\dfrac{dF}{dx}\right)^{-1}\,\dfrac{dG}{dx}\,\dfrac{dF}{dx}\right)\cdot x=\ell\,\dfrac{dG}{dx}\cdot x.

\square

Note that the matrix

Appendix B
Hamiltonian Vector Fields

In this appendix we gather information about Hamiltonian vector fields. The collection
\mathcal{H}
of formal power series in the variables
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{2n})
, assumed to begin with quadratic terms is a real Lie algebra with bracket given by the Poisson bracket.

(B.1)

\{H,K\}:=\langle JH_{x},K_{x}\rangle,

where
J
denotes the canonical matrix
\left[\begin{matrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{matrix}\right]
(
I
being the
n\times n
identity matrix),
H_{x}
and
K_{x}
are the respective formal gradients of
H
and
K
, regarded as column vectors and
\langle,\rangle
is the column-vector adaptation of the usual inner product.

The Lie algebra
\mathcal{H}
becomes a graded Lie algebra if for each
i\geq 0
we take
\mathcal{H}_{i}\subset\mathcal{H}
to be the collection of homogeneous polynomials of degree
i+2
.

When viewed as a vector field
JH_{x}
in (
B.1
) is generally expressed as
X_{H}
. A centralizer
K\in\mathcal{H}
of
H
is known classically as a
first integral
of
X_{H}
.

With this notation Hamilton’s equation for a
Hamiltonian (function)

H:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
becomes

(B.2)

\dot{x}=JH_{x}(x)\mbox{ where }\dot{x}=\frac{dx}{dt}.

The connection between Hamilton’s equation and (
B.1
) is given by:

(B.3)

\frac{d}{dt}K(x(t))=\frac{dK}{dx}\frac{dx}{dt}=\langle K_{x},JH_{x}\rangle=\{H,K\}.

From which we see that
K
is a centralizer of (
B.1
) if and only if
K
is constant along solutions, which in turn is the case if and only if
K\in ker(ad(H))
.

When dealing with Hamiltonian systems it is useful to keep the following properties of the canonical matrix in mind:

(B.4)

J^{2}=-I\quad J^{\tau}=-J=J^{-1}.

(B.5)

Properties of Hamiltonians

(a)

(Conservation of Energy)
H
is an integral of
X_{H}
.

This follows because

\{H,H\}=\langle H_{x},JH_{x}\rangle=0

(b)

(Functional Dependence) Suppose
K:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a rational function, i.e. assume
K\in\mathbb{R}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{2n})
and suppose there is a rational function
g(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}(x,t)
such that

(i)

\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial s}\neq 0,

and

(ii)

g(K,H)=0

Then
K
is an integral of
X_{H}
.

By (ii) we have
\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial s}K_{x}+\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial t}H_{x}=0
. Taking the inner product with
JH_{x}
and using (a) above then gives

0=\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial s}\langle K_{x},JH_{x}\rangle+\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial t}\langle H_{x},JH_{x}\rangle=\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial s}\langle K_{x},JH_{x}\rangle.

whereupon from (i) we conclude that
\langle K_{x},JH_{x}\rangle=0
.

(c)

For any non-zero integer,
m
, the function
K=H^{m}
is a first integral of
X_{H}
: take
g(s,t)=s^{m}-t
in (b).

(d)

If
K
is a first integral of
X_{H}
then for every positive integer
m

\{H,K^{m}\}:=mK^{n-1}\langle JH_{x},K_{x}\rangle=0.

.

It follows that
K^{m}
is a first integral of
X_{H}
as well.

(e)

Suppose
H:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Hamiltonian function of the form

H=\frac{1}{2}\langle x,Ax\rangle
, where
A
is symmetric and invertible. Define
B:=-JA^{-1}J
and
K:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
by
K=\frac{1}{2}\langle x,Bx\rangle
. The
K
is a first integral of
X_{H}
.

To see this recall that for
Q(x)=\langle x,Tx\rangle
where
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a linear operator with usual basis matrix,
M
we have

Q_{x}=\dfrac{dQ}{dx}=(M+M^{\tau})x.

In particular since
A
and
B
are both symmetric

H_{x}=Ax\quad K_{x}=Bx
and we have

\begin{array}[]{llll}\langle K_{x},JH_{x}\rangle&=&\langle Bx,JAx\rangle\\
&=&\langle x,BJAx\rangle\\
&=&\langle x,(-JA^{-1}J)JAx\rangle\\
&=&\langle x,JA^{-1}Ax\rangle\\
&=&\langle x,Jx\rangle\\
&=&0\par\end{array}

(B.6)

\{H,K\}=-\dfrac{dH}{dx}J(\dfrac{dK}{dx})^{\tau},\quad\mbox{ where }\tau\mbox{ is the matrix }\mbox{ transpose. }

This is immediate from:

\begin{array}[]{lllll}\{H,K\}&=&\{JH_{x},K_{x}\}\\
&=&\langle H_{x},-JK_{x}\rangle\quad\mbox{ ( because }J^{\tau}=-J)\\
&=&-\langle(\frac{dH}{dx})^{\tau},J(\frac{dK}{dx})^{\tau}\\

For each

the mapping

is a real linear operator. In addition we have

The operator

We remark that an associative algebra with a Lie bracket such that

is a derivation is called a Poisson algebra.

Proof :
for any solution,

\begin{array}[]{llll}ad(H)(K_{1}K_{2})&=&\{K_{1}K_{2},H\}\\

Remark B.8
.

The Hamiltonian system (

(i)

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\dot{q}_{i}&=&\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}\\
\dot{p}_{i}&=&-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}}\end{array}

Or more succinctly as

(ii)

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\dot{q}&=&H_{p}\\
\dot{p}&=&-H_{q}\end{array}

where

(iii)

x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{2n})=:(q_{1},q_{2},\cdots,q_{n},p_{1},p_{,}\cdots,p_{n}).

The
q_{i}
and
p_{i}
are called

. The
q_{i}
are the
position variables
and the
p_{i}
are the
momentum variables
.

An important special case occurs when

3.2
). In this context equations (
ii
) become

(B.9)

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\dot{q}&=&p\\
\dot{p}&=&-V_{q}\end{array}

which are obviously equivalent to Newton’s equations

\ddot{q}=-V_{q}

for a particle of unit mass moving in a conservative force field with potential

.
