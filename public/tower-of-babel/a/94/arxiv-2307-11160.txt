Time-optimal multi-qubit gates: Complexity, efficient heuristic and gate-time bounds
P. Baßler, M. Heinrich, M. Kliesch

Abstract
Multi-qubit entangling interactions arise naturally in several quantum computing platforms and promise advantages over traditional two-qubit gates. In particular, a fixed multi-qubit Ising-type interaction together with single-qubit 𝖷 \operatorname{\mathsf{X}} -gates can be used to synthesize global 𝖹𝖹 \operatorname{\mathsf{ZZ}} -gates ( 𝖦𝖹𝖹 \operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}} gates). In this work, we first show that the synthesis of such quantum gates that are time-optimal is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard. Second, we provide explicit constructions of special time-optimal multi-qubit gates. They have constant gate times and can be implemented with linearly many 𝖷 \operatorname{\mathsf{X}} -gate layers. Third, we develop a heuristic algorithm with polynomial runtime for synthesizing fast multi-qubit gates. Fourth, we derive lower and upper bounds on the optimal 𝖦𝖹𝖹 \operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}} gate-time. Based on explicit constructions of 𝖦𝖹𝖹 \operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}} gates and numerical studies, we conjecture that any 𝖦𝖹𝖹 \operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}} gate can be executed in a time O ⁡ ( n ) \LandauO(n) for n n qubits. Our heuristic synthesis algorithm leads to 𝖦𝖹𝖹 \operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}} gate-times with a similar scaling, which is optimal in this sense. We expect that our efficient synthesis of fast multi-qubit gates allows for faster and, hence, also more error-robust execution of quantum algorithms.

Introduction

Any quantum computation requires to decompose its logical operations into the platform’s native instruction set.
The performance of the computation depends heavily on the available instructions and their implementation in the quantum hardware.
In particular for early quantum devices a major challenge is posed by their short decoherence times, which limits the runtime of a quantum computation significantly.
Therefore, it is not only necessary to optimize the number of native instructions but also their execution time.

Ising-type interactions give rise to an important and rich class of Hamiltonians ubiquitous in several quantum computing platforms
[

,

Previously, we have utilized these Ising-type interactions in a new synthesis method

In particular, we have considered the problem of synthesizing time-optimal multi-qubit gates on a quantum computing platform that supports the following basic operations:

(I)

single-qubit rotations can be executed in parallel, and

(II)

it offers a fixed Ising-type interaction.

The corresponding synthesis of global
\operatorname{\mathsf{ZZ}}
-gates (
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}
gates) is given by the minimization of the overall gate time, which can be written as a

(

[
6
]

This

It has been unclear whether such multi-qubit gates can be synthesized in a computationally efficient way while keeping the gate time optimal.

In this work, we prove that this synthesis problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard and provide a close-to-optimal efficient heuristic solution.
To establish this hardness result, we draw the connection between the synthesis problem and graph theory.
The
cut polytope
is defined as the convex hull of binary vectors representing the possible cuts of a given graph
[
7
]
.
We provide a polynomial time reduction of the membership problem of the cut polytope to the synthesis of time-optimal multi-qubit gates.
Since this membership problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
[
8
]

\mathsf{NP}
-hard.
This is akin to the
\mathsf{NP}

4

We provide several ways to circumvent the hardness of time-optimal
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}
-gates.
First, we provide explicit constructions of time-optimal multi-qubit gates realizing nearest-neighbor coupling under physically motivated assumptions.
Such constructed nearest-neighbor multi-qubit gates exhibit a constant gate time and can be implemented with only linearly many single-qubit gate layers.
We then use these ideas to define relaxations of the underlying linear program, leading to a hierarchy of polynomial-time algorithms for the synthesis of fast multi-qubit gates.
By increasing the level in the hierarchy, this heuristic approach can be adapted to provide substantially better approximations to the optimal solution at the cost of higher polynomial runtime.
For a small number of qubits, numerical experiments show that the so-obtained gate times are close to the optimal solution and come with significant runtime savings.

Among others, we prove bounds on the minimal multi-qubit gate time, and conjecture that it scales at most linear with the number of qubits.
This claim is supported by a class of explicit constructions of time-optimal multi-qubit gates achieving the linear upper bound.
Moreover, we provide numerical evidence that these explicit solutions in fact yield the longest gate time for a small number of qubits.

We expect our results to be useful for the implementation of time-optimal multi-qubit gates in
NISQ

NISQ

The polynomial-time heuristic algorithm makes it possible to efficiently synthesize fast multi-qubit gates for a growing number of qubits.
The here considered multi-qubit gates have been useful in a number of different applications, some of which we investigated in previous work

Furthermore, they are the main building blocks for a class of

More recently, it was shown that quantum memory circuits and boolean functions can be implemented with a constant number of

Moreover, there is an ancilla-free construction of multi-qubit Clifford circuits using at most

As noted in Ref.

Synthesizing multi-qubit gates with Ising-type interactions

In this section, we give a short introduction to the synthesis of time-optimal multi-qubit gates. For more details we refer to the first two sections of Ref.
[
6
]
.

On the abstract quantum computing platform with
n
qubits specified by the requirements
(I)
and
(II)
above, interactions between the qubits are generated by an Ising-type Hamiltonian

H_{ZZ}\coloneqq-\sum_{i<j}^{n}J_{ij}\sigma_{z}^{i}\sigma_{z}^{j}\,,

(1)

where
\sigma_{z}^{i}
is the Pauli-Z operator acting on the
i
-th qubit.
Note, that diagonal terms, where
i=j
, are excluded since they only change the Hamiltonian by an energy offset.
By
J
we denote the symmetric matrix with entries
J_{ij}
in the upper triangular part and vanishing diagonal.
We call
J
the
physical coupling matrix
.

Conjugating the Hamiltonian
H_{ZZ}
with
\operatorname{\mathsf{X}}
gates on the qubits indicated by the binary vector
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
yields

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}^{\boldsymbol{b}}H_{ZZ}\sigma_{x}^{\boldsymbol{b}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i<j}^{n}J_{ij}\sigma_{x}^{b_{i}}\sigma_{x}^{b_{j}}\sigma_{z}^{i}\sigma_{z}^{j}\sigma_{x}^{b_{i}}\sigma_{x}^{b_{j}}

(2)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i<j}^{n}J_{ij}(-1)^{b_{i}}(-1)^{b_{j}}\sigma_{z}^{i}\sigma_{z}^{j}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i<j}^{n}J_{ij}m_{i}m_{j}\sigma_{z}^{i}\sigma_{z}^{j}\eqqcolon H(\boldsymbol{m})\,,

where we define the
encoding

\boldsymbol{m}\coloneqq(-1)^{\boldsymbol{b}}
entry wise.
The sign of the interaction between qubit
i
and
j
is given by
m_{i}m_{j}\in\{-1,+1\}
.
We call
H(\boldsymbol{m})
the
encoded Hamiltonian
.

Given time steps
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}}\geq 0
during which the encoding
\boldsymbol{m}
is used, we consider unitaries of the form

\prod_{\boldsymbol{m}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}}H(\boldsymbol{m})}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}}H(\boldsymbol{m})}\eqqcolon\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}H}\,,

(3)

where we used that the diagonal Hamiltonians
H(\boldsymbol{m})
mutually commute.
For all possible encodings
\boldsymbol{m}\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
we collect the time steps
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}}
in a vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}
and interpret
t=\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}}
as the total gate time of the unitary
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}H}
, implemented by the sequence of unitaries (
3
).
Moreover, we use the symmetry

(-\boldsymbol{m})(-\boldsymbol{m})^{T}=\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{T}

(4)

to reduce the degrees of freedom in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
from
2^{n}
to
2^{n-1}
by adding up
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}}+\lambda_{-\boldsymbol{m}}
to a single time step.

The so generated unitary is the time evolution operator under the
total Hamiltonian

H=-\sum_{i<j}^{n}A_{ij}\sigma_{z}^{i}\sigma_{z}^{j}\,,

(5)

where we have defined the
total coupling matrix

A\coloneqq J\circ\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}}\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{T}\,,

(6)

and used the linearity of the Hadamard (entry-wise) product
\circ
.
By construction,
A
is a symmetric matrix with vanishing diagonal.
Let us define the
\binom{n}{2}
-dimensional subspace of symmetric matrices with vanishing diagonal by

\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\left\{M\in\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}M_{ii}=0\ \forall i\in[n]\right\}\,.

(7)

For
A\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we define an associated multi-qubit gate
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A)
, where
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}
stands for “global
ZZ
interactions”,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\sum_{i<j}^{n}A_{ij}\sigma_{z}^{i}\sigma_{z}^{j}\right)\,.

(8)

To determine which matrices can be decomposed as in
Eq.

6
, we denote the non-zero index set of a symmetric matrix as
\operatorname{nz}(M)\coloneqq\{(i,j)\,|\,M_{ij}\neq 0,i<j\}
.
Then, the subspace of matrices
A\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that can be decomposed as in
Eq.

6
is exactly given by the condition
\operatorname{nz}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{nz}(J)
, which we assume to hold from now on.
Thus, all-to-all connectivity enables to decompose any coupling matrix
A\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
but is not strictly required by our approach.
We call the number of encodings
\boldsymbol{m}
needed for the decomposition the
encoding cost
of
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A)
, and
\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}}
the
total
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}
time
.
Note, that both quantities depend on the chosen decomposition.

It is convenient to abstract the following analysis from the physical details given by
J
.
For a matrix
A\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\operatorname{nz}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{nz}(J)
, its possible decompositions are in one-to-one correspondence with the decompositions of the matrix
M\coloneqq A\oslash J\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
where

(A\oslash J)_{ij}\coloneqq\begin{cases}A_{ij}/J_{ij},&i\neq j\text{ and }J_{ij}\neq 0\,,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(9)

We further define the linear operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2^{n-1}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(10)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}}\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{T},

represented in the standard basis by a matrix

V\in\{-1,+1\}^{\binom{n}{2}\times(2^{n-1})}\,.

(11)

Let
\boldsymbol{v}:\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}
be the (row-wise) vectorization of the upper triangular part of the matrix input such that the columns of
V
are given by
\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{T})
.
Our objective is to minimize the total gate time and the amount of
\boldsymbol{m}
’s needed to express the matrix
M\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
To this end we formulate the following
LP
(
LP

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle}{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\quad\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

(12)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{}}{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}}\quad

\displaystyle V\boldsymbol{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}(M){},\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{n-1}}_{\geq 0}{}\,,

where
\boldsymbol{1}=(1,1,\dots,1)
is the all-ones vector such that
\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}}
.
A
feasible solution
is an assignment of the variables that fulfills all constraints of the optimization problem and an
optimal solution
is a feasible solution which also minimizes/maximizes the objective function.
Throughout this paper, we indicate optimal solutions by an asterisk, e.g.
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
.
Note, that the
LP
(
12
) has a feasible solution for any symmetric matrix
M
with vanishing diagonal
[
6

.
The theory of linear programming then guarantees the existence of an optimal solution with at most
\binom{n}{2}
non-zero entries
[
6

.

A standard tool in convex optimization is
duality

[
14
]
which will be used in
Section

7
.
The dual
LP
to the
LP
(
12
) reads as follows:

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle}{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle}{\mathrm{maximize}}}\quad\langle M,\boldsymbol{y}\rangle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

(13)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{}}{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}}\quad

\displaystyle V^{T}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle\leq\boldsymbol{1}{},\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}{}\,,

with the inner product
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle:\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\langle M,\boldsymbol{y}\rangle\mapsto\boldsymbol{v}(M)^{T}\boldsymbol{y}

Here, inequalities between vectors are to be understood entry-wise.
A simple, but important fact is the following:
If
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
is an optimal solution to the primal

(

\boldsymbol{y}
to the dual

(
13
) provides a

as
\langle M,\boldsymbol{y}\rangle\leq\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
.
Moreover, the feasibility of the

(

if

4
Synthesizing time-optimal GZZ gates is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard

In this section, we investigate the complexity of solving the gate synthesis problem stated as
LP
(
12
).
We observe that
LP
(
12
) is an optimization over the convex cone generated by

\mathcal{E}_{n}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{T}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{m}\in\{-1,+1\}^{n},m_{n}=+1\right\}\,,

(16)

which is the set of outer products generated by all possible encodings
\boldsymbol{m}
.
Due to the symmetry
Eq.

4
we can uniformly fix the value of one entry of
\boldsymbol{m}
.
We chose the convention
m_{n}=+1
.
In the literature
\mathcal{E}_{n}
is also known as the
elliptope
of rank one matrices
[
17
]
.
In the following, we consider the polytope

\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{E}_{n})\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=1,\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\ \right\}\,,

(17)

and show the connection to graph theory, in particular to the cuts of graphs.

Definition 1

(cut polytope
[
7
]
)
.

Let
K_{n}=(V_{n},E_{n})
be a complete graph with
n
vertices.
Denote
\delta(X)
the set of all edges with one endpoint in
X\subset V_{n}
and the other endpoint in its complement
\bar{X}
,
i.e.,
\delta(X)
defines the cut between
X
and
\bar{X}
.
Let
\chi^{\delta(X)}\in\{0,1\}^{|E_{n}|}
denote the characteristic vector of a cut, with
\chi_{e}^{\delta(X)}=1
if
e\in\delta(X)
and
\chi_{e}^{\delta(X)}=0
otherwise.
We define the
cut polytope
as the convex hull of the characteristic vectors

\operatorname{CUT}_{n}\coloneqq\operatorname{conv}\left\{\chi^{\delta(X)}\in\{0,1\}^{|E_{n}|}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}X\subseteq V_{n}\right\}\,.

(18)

Lemma 2
.

For all
n
,
\operatorname{CUT}_{n}
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{E}_{n})
.

Proof.

For each
X\subset V_{n}
we set
m_{i}=+1
if
i\in X
and
m_{i}=-1
if
i\in\bar{X}
.
Note, that there are
2^{n-1}
different pairs of
X
and
\bar{X}
.
We then have
m_{i}m_{j}=-1
if
i\in X
and
j\in\bar{X}
(or the other way around), and
m_{i}m_{j}=+1
if
i,j\in X
or
i,j\in\bar{X}
.
So the characteristic vector can be written as
\chi_{e}^{\delta(X)}=(1-m_{i}m_{j})/2
for each edge
e\in E_{n}
connecting vertices
i
and
j
.
This is clearly a bijective affine map between the vertices and thus
\operatorname{CUT}_{n}
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{E}_{n})
.
∎

Problem 5

.

The constructions of this section yield a qubit-independent total
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}

Moreover, we show that some

However, most of these constructions assume constant values for the elements of the band-diagonal of the physical coupling matrix

These assumptions are relaxed throughout this section providing explicit optimal solutions for physically relevant cases.
These results build the foundation for the heuristic algorithm for fast

By

we denote the
\ell_{p}
-norm of a symmetric matrix

\ell_{p}
-norm of a vector

containing all lower/upper triangular matrix elements in some order.
First, we proof a lower bound on the optimal total
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}

Lemma 7
.

For any
M\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the optimal objective function value of the
LP
(
12
) is lower bounded by

\lVert M\rVert_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\,.

(19)

Proof.

The lower bound can be verified by the fact that the matrix representation

\pm 1
and that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
is non-negative.
Thus, it holds that

.
∎

Next, we provide calculation rules for the coupling matrix
A\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of the
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A)
gate. These rules are inherited from matrix exponentials.
Let
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
then

(i)

\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A_{1}+A_{2})=\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A_{1})\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A_{2})
,

(ii)

\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A_{1}\oplus A_{2})=\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A_{1})\otimes\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A_{2})
and

(iii)

\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A_{1}\circ A_{2})=\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A_{3})
, where the coupling matrices can be decomposed as

A_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}_{k}}\boldsymbol{m}_{k}\boldsymbol{m}_{k}^{T},\quad A_{2}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{d_{2}}\beta_{\boldsymbol{v}_{k}}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}^{T},\quad A_{3}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{d_{3}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{w}_{k}}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{T}\,,

with
d_{3}=d_{1}d_{2}
,
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}=(\boldsymbol{m}_{i}\circ\boldsymbol{v}_{j})_{k=d_{2}i+j}
and
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{w}_{k}}=(\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}_{i}}\beta_{\boldsymbol{v}_{j}})_{k=d_{2}i+j}
for
j=1,\dots,d_{2}
and
i=0,\dots,d_{1}-1
.

In
Item

(iii)
we can also express
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta}
with the Kronecker product.
Note, that these generated
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}
gates are not necessarily time-optimal.

We denote the
k\times k
identity matrix by
\mathds{1}_{k}
, the
k

\boldsymbol{1}_{k}
and the
k\times k
matrix of ones with vanishing diagonal by

.
With the next two lemmas, we bound the encoding cost and total gate time for special cases of
Item

(i)
and
Item

(ii)
, where the total gate time is constant.
These results will provide a basis for all other constructions.
The following result constructs total coupling matrices
A\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
on arbitrary subsets of qubits.

Lemma 8
.

Let the coupling matrix
J
be constant, i.e.
J_{ij}=c\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
for all
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
.
For any
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}
and
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
the
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A)
with the matrix

(20)

with

\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}

\varphi/c
.
This total gate time is optimal.

Proof.

W.l.o.g. we set
c=\varphi=1
.
We have
n\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{s}k_{i}
qubits.
Note, that

only contributes to an entry in the diagonal of
A

\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A)
gate.
We denote the

and the matrix consisting of its first

The orthogonality of the columns of any Hadamard matrix yields

Replacing each

(H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{k}})^{T}H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-d\mathds{1}_{n}=d\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}E_{k_{i}}\,.

(21)

We set the diagonal elements on the left-hand side to zero, since they only contribute to an energy offset in the Hamiltonian.
Take each row of

as a possible vector
\boldsymbol{m}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}

to construct the total coupling matrix

A=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\operatorname{rows}(H_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{d\times n})}\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{T}\,,

Clearly, we have

\varphi

\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}

\varphi/c

Furthermore, this total
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}

7

∎

6
Efficient heuristic for fast GZZ gates

In this section, we build on the results of
Lemma

8
to derive a heuristic algorithm for synthesizing
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A)
gates with low total gate time for any
A\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
This algorithm runs in polynomial time as opposed to the general
LP
(
12
), which we have shown in
Theorem

6
to be
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.
The runtime saving is due to the restriction of the elliptope
\mathcal{E}_{n}
in
Eq.

16
, with exponential many elements, to a set with polynomial many elements.
This restriction yields a polynomial sized
LP
which can be solved in polynomial time.
In practice, the simplex algorithm has a runtime that scales polynomial in the problem size
[
25
]
.

Recall the modified Hadamard matrix
H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
defined in the proof of
Lemma

8
, where we used the rows of
H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
as encodings
\boldsymbol{m}
to generate block diagonal target coupling matrices under some assumptions.
Here,
s
is the number of block matrices on the diagonal of the target matrix,
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
contains the dimensions for each block and
d=2^{\lceil\log_{2}(s)\rceil}
is the required number of encodings to construct such a block diagonal matrix.
From now on, we only consider
\boldsymbol{k}=(j,1\dots 1)\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
such that

(H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{k}})^{T}H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-d\mathds{1}_{n}=d\left(E_{j}\oplus E_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus E_{1}\right)\,,

(34)

see
Eq.

21
.
The requirement that
\sum_{i}k_{i}=n
implies that such a vector
\boldsymbol{k}
has
s=n-j+1
entries.
Permuting the columns of
H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
results in the same permutation of the rows and columns of the right-hand side of
Eq.

34
.
We denote the set of all column-permuted
H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
by
\mathcal{C}^{(j)}
.
A specific element of
\mathcal{C}^{(j)}
is denoted by
H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{r}}
, where
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{j}
is an ordered multi-index
r_{1}<\dots<r_{j}
indicating which columns of
H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{r}}
are identical.
For example,
\boldsymbol{r}=(2,5,6)
indicates that the columns of
H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{r}}
with indices
2
,
5
and
6
are identical, i.e. replacing column
5
and
6
with column
2
.
This notation will be useful later.

Definition 12
.

For any
j\in\{2,3,\dots,n\}
, we define the restricted elliptope

\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{T}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{m}\text{ is a row of }H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{r}}\in\mathcal{C}^{(j)}\right\}.

(35)

Further we define

\mathcal{E}_{n}^{[j]}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=2}^{j}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(i)}\,.

(36)

We choose the definition in
Eq.

35
similar as in
Eq.

16
.
Next, we show the size scaling of the restricted elliptopes.
This directly translates to the size and runtime of the heuristic synthesis optimization.

Proposition 13
.

For any
j\in\{2,3,\dots,n\}
, the number of different encodings
\boldsymbol{m}
generating the restricted elliptope
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{[j]}
scales as
\LandauO(n^{j+1})
.

Proof.

Note, that
|\mathcal{C}^{(j)}|=\binom{n}{j}
since there are
j
duplicate columns in
H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{r}}
.
The binomial coefficient can be bounded by
\binom{n}{j}\leq n^{j}/j!
.
Since there are
d=2^{\lceil\log_{2}(n-j+1)\rceil}<2(n-j+1)
rows of
H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{r}}
we have a rough upper bound of the number different encodings generating the restricted elliptope,
\big\lvert\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}\big\rvert\leq d\binom{n}{j}<2(n-j+1)\binom{n}{j}<2n^{j+1}/j!
.
The first inequality is due to possible redundant encodings in the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}
.
Similarly, we can upper bound
\big\lvert\mathcal{E}_{n}^{[j]}\big\rvert\leq\sum_{i=2}^{j}\big\lvert\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(i)}\big\rvert<2\sum_{i=2}^{j}n^{i+1}/i!
which is a polynomial of order
j+1
.
∎

We denote the convex cone generated by a set
V
by

\operatorname{cone}(V)\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}v_{i}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\lambda_{i}\geq 0\text{, }v_{i}\in V\right\}\,.

(37)

With that, we are ready to present the main result of this section.

Theorem 14
.

\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(2)})=\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

W.l.o.g. we can assume
d=n
and denote
H^{d\times d}_{\boldsymbol{r}}\in\mathcal{C}^{(2)}
by
H^{d}_{(r_{1},r_{2})}
with the property

(H^{d}_{(r_{1},r_{2})})^{T}H^{d}_{(r_{1},r_{2})}-d\mathds{1}_{d}=d\,e_{(r_{1},r_{2})}\,,

(38)

where
e_{(r_{1},r_{2})}
is an element of the standard basis for symmetric matrices with vanishing diagonal.
By
Eq.

38
it holds
\HTL(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n})\subseteq\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(2)})
, i.e., symmetric matrices with non-negative entries are in the convex cone.

It is left to show that
\HTL(\mathbb{R}_{<0}^{n})\subseteq\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(2)})
, i.e., that also symmetric matrices with negative entries are in the convex cone.
To show this inclusion we define
H^{d}_{(r_{1},-r_{2})}
similar as
H^{d}_{(r_{1},r_{2})}
except the duplicate column at
r_{2}
is multiplied by
-1
such that

(H^{d}_{(r_{1},-r_{2})})^{T}H^{d}_{(r_{1},-r_{2})}-d\mathds{1}_{d}=-d\,e_{(r_{1},r_{2})}\,.

(39)

We have to show that for each row
\boldsymbol{m}\in\operatorname{rows}(H^{d}_{(r_{1},-r_{2})})
there exist
\tilde{r}_{1}
and
\tilde{r}_{2}
such that
\boldsymbol{m}\in\operatorname{rows}(H^{d}_{(\tilde{r}_{1},\tilde{r}_{2})})
.
This can be verified straightforwardly for

by checking all rows.
W.l.o.g. we show that the hypothesis holds for any
H^{2d}_{(r_{1},-r_{2})}
by assuming it holds for
H^{d}_{(r_{1},-r_{2})}
.
The Sylvester-Hadamard matrix is constructed inductively according to

H^{2d}=\begin{pmatrix}H^{d}&H^{d}\\
H^{d}&-H^{d}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(40)

We consider three cases for
H^{2d}_{(r_{1},-r_{2})}
.

Case 1.

For a

with identical columns, up to minus sign, at indices

or

\tilde{r}_{1},\tilde{r}_{2}\in[d]
or
\tilde{r}_{1},\tilde{r}_{2}\in[d+1,2d]
respectively.

Case 2.

Considering the first
d
rows of
H^{2d}_{(r_{1},-r_{2})}
with identical columns, up to minus sign, at indices
r_{1}\in[d]
and
r_{2}\in[d+1,2d]
.
This case is equivalent to
Case 1.
with

since only the column at
r_{2}
is negated.

Case 3.

Considering the last
d
rows of
H^{2d}_{(r_{1},-r_{2})}
with identical columns, up to minus sign, at indices
r_{1}\in[d]
and
r_{2}\in[d+1,2d]
.
These rows are included in the last
d
rows of
H^{2d}_{(\tilde{r}_{1},\tilde{r}_{2})}
with
\tilde{r}_{1},\tilde{r}_{2}\in[d+1,2d]
and
\tilde{r}_{2}=r_{2}
since the column
r_{2}
is negated which is equivalent to just duplicating a column of
-H^{d}
.

We have shown that for each row
\boldsymbol{m}\in\operatorname{rows}(H^{d}_{(r_{1},-r_{2})})
there exist
\tilde{r}_{1}
and
\tilde{r}_{2}
such that
\boldsymbol{m}\in\operatorname{rows}(H^{d}_{(\tilde{r}_{1},\tilde{r}_{2})})
.
Therefore,
\HTL(\mathbb{R}_{<0}^{n})\cup\HTL(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n})=\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(2)})
.
The last equality follows from the definition of
\operatorname{cone}
and
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(2)}
.
∎

7
Bounds on the total GZZ time

Our main analytic result (
Theorem

15
) is that the optimal total
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}
time
\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
is lower and upper bounded by the norms
\lVert M\rVert_{\ell_{\infty}}
and
\lVert M\rVert_{\ell_{1}}
, respectively.
Note, that for a dense matrix
M
, its norm
\lVert M\rVert_{\ell_{1}}
scales quadratic with the number of qubits
n
.
We conjecture an improved upper bound on the total
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}
time for dense
M
that scales at most linear with the number of qubits
n
.
We support this conjecture with explicit solutions for the
LP
(
12
) reaching this bound for any
n
and numerical results validating the conjecture for
n\leq 8
.

Theorem 15
.

The optimal total gate time of
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A)
with
A\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is lower and upper bounded by

\lVert M\rVert_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\leq\lVert M\rVert_{\ell_{1}}\,,

(42)

where
M\coloneqq A\oslash J
.
Equality holds for the lower bound for all matrices
M=C\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{T}
for any
\boldsymbol{m}\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
and
C\geq 0
.

Proof.

The lower bound has been shown in
Lemma

7
.
Equality in the lower bound holds for
M=C\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{T}
by setting
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}}=C=\lVert M\rVert_{\ell_{\infty}}
and
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}=0
for all
\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\neq\boldsymbol{m}
.
We use the explicit construction of the standard basis elements for symmetric matrices from the proof of
Theorem

14
to show the upper bound.
To be precise, we have

\frac{|M_{ij}|}{d}(H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{r}})^{T}H^{d\times n}_{\boldsymbol{r}}-d\mathds{1}_{n}=M_{ij}e_{(i,j)}\,,

(43)

where
\boldsymbol{r}=(i,j)
if
M_{ij}\geq 0
or
\boldsymbol{r}=(\tilde{i},\tilde{j})
if
M_{ij}<0
as in
Eqs.

38
and
39
, respectively. We define
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(i,j)}
with the entries
\lambda^{(i,j)}_{\boldsymbol{m}}\coloneqq\frac{|M_{ij}|}{d}\,[\boldsymbol{m}\in\operatorname{rows}(H^{d}_{\boldsymbol{r}})]
. According to
Lemma

8
we have
\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(i,j)}=|M_{ij}|
.
Adding
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(i,j)}
for all
i<j
yields the upper bound
\lVert M\rVert_{\ell_{1}}
.
∎

These bounds get tighter the sparser
M
is.
If
M
has only one non-zero value, then clearly
\lVert M\rVert_{\ell_{\infty}}=\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}=\lVert M\rVert_{\ell_{1}}
.
Furthermore, these bounds also hold for the heuristic, which we presented in
Section

6
.

Next, we state our conjecture that the optimal gate time of
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A)
scales at most linear with the number of qubits.

Conjecture 16
.

The optimal gate time of
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}(A)
with
A\in\HTL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is tightly upper bounded by

\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\leq\lVert M\rVert_{\ell_{\infty}}\cdot\begin{cases}n\,,&\text{for odd }n\,,\\
n-1\,,&\text{for even }n\,.\end{cases}

(44)

Hence, it provides a tighter bound for dense
M
than
Theorem

15
.
To support the claim of
16
we first construct explicit dual and primal feasible solutions for the case
M=-E_{n}
for the
LP
’s (
12
) and (
13
), respectively.
Then optimality is given by showing equality of the objective function values of the primal and dual problem.
We further show that the case
M=-E_{n}
leads to the same objective function value as
M=-\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{T}
for any
\boldsymbol{m}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
.
Finally, we provide numerical evidence that the conjecture holds for

.

For practical purposes it is important to keep in mind that the platform given

Explicit solutions for

The following lemma will be used in the proof of the explicit feasible solution of the dual problem for

being of the form

.
We can identify
\boldsymbol{m}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}

Lemma 17
.

It holds that

\displaystyle P_{\boldsymbol{b}}\coloneqq\sum_{i<j}^{n}(-1)^{b_{i}\oplus b_{j}}=\binom{n}{2}-2|\boldsymbol{b}|(n-|\boldsymbol{b}|)\,,

(45)

for any binary vector
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.
We denote the Hamming weight by
|\boldsymbol{b}|
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{m}=(-1)^{\boldsymbol{b}}
.
If the Hamming weight
|\boldsymbol{b}|
vanishes, then
\boldsymbol{m}=(+1,\dots,+1)
and
P_{\boldsymbol{b}}=\binom{n}{2}
which is the maximal value.
If
|\boldsymbol{b}|\neq 0
,
\boldsymbol{m}
contains
|\boldsymbol{b}|
entries
-1
, such that the upper triangular part of
\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{T}
contains a rectangle of
-1

|\boldsymbol{b}|
and width

so the total amount of
-1
’s is
|\boldsymbol{b}|(n-|\boldsymbol{b}|)

Therefore,

P_{\boldsymbol{b}}=\binom{n}{2}-2|\boldsymbol{b}|(n-|\boldsymbol{b}|)\,.

(46)

∎

Lemma 18

(explicit dual feasible solution)
.

Let

, then there is an explicit feasible solution

to the dual

\langle-E_{n},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle=\begin{cases}n\,,&\text{for odd }n\,,\\
n-1\,,&\text{for even }n\,.\end{cases}

We assume that

.

n-1\,,&\text{for even }n\,.\end{cases}

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{}}{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}}\quad

9
Conclusion

We investigated the time-optimal multi-qubit gate synthesis introduced in Ref.
[
6
]
.
We show that synthesizing time-optimal multi-qubit gates in our setting is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.
However, we also provide explicit solutions for certain cases with constant gate time and a polynomial-time heuristics to synthesize fast multi-qubit gates.
Our numerical simulations suggest that these heuristics provide good approximations to the optimal
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}
gate time.
Furthermore, tight bounds on the scaling of the optimal multi-qubit gate times were shown.
More precisely, we showed that the optimal multi-qubit gate time scales at most as
\lVert A\oslash J\rVert_{\ell_{1}}
, the
\ell_{1}
-norm of the element-wise division of the total and physical coupling matrices
A
and
J
, respectively.
We also conjectured that the optimal
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}
gate time scales at most linear with the number of qubits.
Our results are practical to estimate the execution time of a given circuit, where the entangling gates are implemented as
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}
gates.
The execution time is a crucial parameter, in particular, in the
NISQ
era since it is limiting the length of a gate sequence due to finite coherence time.

It is our hope to proof the conjectured linear scaling of the optimal
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}
gate time in the near future.
Moreover, we would like to test and verify our proposed time-optimal multi-qubit gate synthesis methods in an experiment.
Depending on the quantum platform we would like to develop adapted error mitigation schemes for the
\operatorname{\mathsf{GZZ}}
gates and investigate their robustness against errors.

A.1
Concave program

A convex linear program in standard form minimizes a convex objective function over a convex set.
But
LP
(
67
)
maximizes
a convex objective function over a convex set.
Such optimizations are called
concave programs
.
It is known that the maximum is attained at the extreme points of the polytope
V^{T}\boldsymbol{y}\leq\boldsymbol{1}
and therefore might have many local optima
[
29
]
.
There are several equivalent sufficient conditions for optimality
[
30
]
.
Here, we investigate one in detail.
First, we define the
conjugate function
for a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

f^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\sup\left\{\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}-f(\boldsymbol{y})\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}(f)\right\}\,,

(69)

with
\mathcal{D}(f)
the domain of
f
.
Furthermore, we need the
support function

h_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for a closed convex set
A

h_{A}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\sup\left\{\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{y}\in A\right\}\,.

(70)

Then the sufficient optimality condition in our case is

\lVert\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}=\sup\left\{h_{A}(\boldsymbol{x})-(\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\ell_{1}})^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}\right\}\,,

(71)

with
A=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}V^{T}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{1}\right\}
.
The conjugate function of
\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}
is

(\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\ell_{1}})^{*}=\begin{cases}0\,,&\text{if }\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq 1\,,\\
\infty\,,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(72)

Then we have

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{h_{A}(\boldsymbol{y})\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq 1\right\}

(73)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq 1,V^{T}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}V^{T}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{1}\right\}\,,

which is the same formulation as the original
LP
(
67
).

\displaystyle(-\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\ell_{1}})^{*}

A.3

By invexity we mean that the objective and constraint functions of the optimization problem are

Definition 22

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{}}{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}}\quad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle-g(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq\eta(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{*})^{T}\nabla g(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})

.
