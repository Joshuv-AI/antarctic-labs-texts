Abstract
. The moduli space of planar polygons with generic side lengths is a closed, smooth manifold. Mapping a polygon to its reflected image across the X X -axis defines a fixed-point-free involution on these moduli spaces, making them into free ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} -spaces. There are some important numerical parameters associated with free ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} -spaces, like index and coindex. In this paper, we compute these parameters for some moduli spaces of polygons. We also determine for which of these spaces a generalized version of the Borsuk-Ulam theorem hold. Moreover, we obtain a formula for the Stiefel-Whitney height in terms of the genetic code, a combinatorial data associated with side lengths.

1.
Introduction

The Borsuk-Ulam (BU) theorem has been an object of central attraction in topology.
It states that any continuous map from the
d
-sphere
{S}^{d}
to the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}

must identify a pair of antipodal points.
Recently, the BU theorem has been studied for many different complexes with a free
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-action.
For instance, Musin
[
15
]
considered PL-manifolds, Gonçalves et al.
[
7
]
considered finite dimensional CW-complexes with a free cellular involution.

For a topological space
X
with a fixed-point-free involution
\nu
, we say that
(X,\nu,d)
is a BU-triple if for every continuous map
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
there exists
x\in X
such that
f(x)=f(\nu(x))
.
The index of a free
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-space
X
is the smallest number
n
such that there is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant map from
X
to
S^{n}
.
It is known that
if the index of
X
is
d
then
(X,\mu,d)
is always a BU-triple and so is
(X,\mu,t)
for any
t\leq d
.
One way of determining BU-triples is to compute a index and other related numerical quantities.
The main focus of the paper is to try and calculate the exact value of the index for a class of spaces.
In some cases where the exact value is not possible to calculate we provide bounds in terms of other related quantities which we now define.

In
[
7
]
, the authors used the index and the Stiefel-Whitney height (see
Definition

2.1
) to find BU-triples.
There is also the dual notion of index of a free
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-space, the coindex.
A
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-space is called
tidy
if all these three numerical parameters coincide; otherwise it is called
non-tidy
.
Many authors have considered the problem of finding free
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-spaces that are tidy.
For more discussion about the index, coindex and tidy spaces, the reader is referred to the book of Matoušek
[
13
, Section 5.3]
and Csorba’s thesis
[
1
]
.

In this article, we investigate the tidyness and existence of BU-triples among the class of moduli spaces of planar polygons with a free cellular involution.
The
moduli space of planar polygons
associated with a length vector
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
, denoted by
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
, is the collection of all closed piecewise linear paths in the plane up to orientation preserving isometries with side lengths
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n}
. Equivalently, we can define
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
as

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}=\{(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n})\in(S^{1})^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}v_{i}=0\}/\mathrm{SO}_{2},

where
S^{1}
is the unit circle and the group of orientation preserving isometries
\mathrm{SO}_{2}
acts diagonally.
The moduli space of planar polygons (associated with
\alpha
) viewed upto isometries is defined as

\overline{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha}=\{(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n})\in(S^{1})^{n}:\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}v_{i}=0\}/\mathrm{O}_{2},

where the group of all isometries of the plane
\mathrm{O}_{2}
acts diagonally.
If we choose a length vector
\alpha
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pm\alpha_{i}\neq 0
then the moduli spaces
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
and
\overline{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha}
are closed, smooth manifolds of dimension
n-3
.
Such length vectors are called
generic
.
In this paper the length vectors are assumed to be generic unless stated otherwise.

Moduli spaces of planar polygons have been studied extensively.
See, for example, Farber’s book
[
5
]
, which contains many results that express topological invariants of the moduli space in terms of the combinatorial data associated with the length vector.
Hausmann and Knutson
[
8
]
computed the mod
2
cohomology ring of
\overline{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha}
.
Using this presentation of the cohomology ring, Kamiyama
[
12
]
computed the height of the Stiefel-Whitney class for (generic)
\alpha=(1,\dots,1,r)
, where
r
and
n
have the same parity.
Panina
[
16
]
showed that the orientation preserving moduli spaces admit a CW-structure with free
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-action.
It is therefore natural to look for BU triples among these spaces and also identify which one of these are tidy.
The aim of this article is to achieve these two goals.

The paper is organized as follows.
In
Section

2
we collect relevant results about
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-spaces and polygon spaces.

Section

3
contains results about those moduli spaces for which the genetic code of the length vector contains exactly one gene.
Then in
Section

4
we consider the two genes case and mainly focus on deriving an expression for the Stiefel-Whitney height.
The formula we obtain generalizes a result of Don Davis.
Finally, in
Section

5
we tackle the class of quasi-equilateral polygons.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
X
be a topological space with a free
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-action and consider the
n
-sphere,
S^{n}_{a}
, with the antipodal action. Then we have the following numerical data associated with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-spaces.

Definition 2.1
.

The
coindex
of
X
is

\mathrm{coind}(X):=\mathrm{max}\{n\geq 0:\exists~\textit{$\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-map $S^{n}_{a}\to X$}\}.

The
index
of
X
is

\mathrm{ind}(X):=\mathrm{min}\{n\geq 0:\exists~\textit{$\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-map $X\to S^{n}_{a}$}\}.

The Stiefel-Whitney
height
of
X
is

\mathrm{ht}(X):=\mathrm{sup}\{n\geq 0:(w_{1}(X))^{n}\neq 0\},

where
w_{1}(X)
is the first Stiefel-Whitney class of the double cover
X\to X/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

For a free
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-space
X
, the following inequality relates these three parameters:

(1)

0\leq\mathrm{coind}(X)\leq\mathrm{ht}(X)\leq\mathrm{ind}(X)\leq\mathrm{dim}(X).

Below is an equivalent formulation of BU-triples in terms of above defined numerical parameters.
The proof of this equivalence can be found in
[
7
]
.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
7
, Theorem 3.4]
)
.

Let
X
be an
m
-manifold with a free
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
action
\tau
.
Then
(X,\tau,m)
is a BU-triple if and only if
\mathrm{ind}(X)=\mathrm{ht}(X)=m
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
X
be an
m
-manifold with a free
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
action
\tau
.
If
\mathrm{ht}(X)=m-1
then
\mathrm{ind}(X)
is also
m-1
and
(X,\tau,m-1)
is a BU-triple. Moreover, if
\mathrm{ht}(X)<m
, then
\mathrm{ind}(X)<m
.

We now turn to spaces that are of central interest in the present article.
As introduced in
Section

1
, let
\alpha
be a generic
n
-length vector.
Let
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
be the corresponding moduli space of planar polygons whose side lengths are specified by
\alpha
.
Observe that
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
admits an involution
\tau
defined by

(2)

\tau(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n})=(\bar{v}_{1},\bar{v}_{2},\dots,\bar{v}_{n}),

where
\bar{v}_{i}=(x_{i},-y_{i})
and
v_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
.
This involution sends a polygon to its reflected image across the
X
-axis.
Since
\alpha
is generic,
\tau
is a fixed point free involution.

Since the diffeomorphism type of a moduli space does not depend on the ordering of the side lengths of polygons, we assume that
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n})
satisfies
\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}\leq\dots\leq\alpha_{n}
.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a length vector
\alpha
, a subset
I\subset[n]
is called
\alpha
-
short
if

\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}<\sum_{j\not\in I}\alpha_{j}

and
long
otherwise.

Definition 2.5
.

For a length vector
\alpha
, consider the collection of subsets of
[n]
:

S_{n}(\alpha)=\{J\subset[n]:\text{ $n\in J$ and $J$ is short}\},

and a partial order
\leq
on
S_{n}(\alpha)
by
I\leq J
if
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{t}\}
and
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{t}\}\subseteq J
with
i_{s}\leq j_{s}
for
1\leq s\leq t
.
The
genetic code
of
\alpha
is the set of maximal elements of
S_{n}(\alpha)
with respect to this partial order.
If
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k}
are the maximal elements of
S_{n}(\alpha)
with respect to
\leq
then the genetic code of
\alpha
is denoted by
\langle A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\rangle
.

Remark 2.6
.

Let the
n
-tuple
\alpha=(1,\dots,1,n-2)
be a length vector.
Then the genetic code of
\alpha
is
\langle n\rangle
.
It follows from
[
10
, Example 6.5]
that
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}\cong S^{n-3}
and
\overline{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha}\cong\mathbb{R}P^{n-3}
.

If
S
is a gene, then
S\setminus\{n\}
is a
gee
.
We set
[n-1]=\{1,2,\dots,n-1\}
.
An
S\subset[n-1]
is a
subgee
if
S\leq T
for some gee
T
.
Many topological invariants of these moduli spaces have been expressed in terms of the genetic code.
We state here a result describing the cohomological algebra of
H^{*}(\overline{M}_{\alpha};\mathbb{Z}_{2})
in terms of the genetic information.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
8
, Corollary 9.2]
)
.

The cohomology ring
H^{\ast}(\overline{M}_{\alpha};\mathbb{Z}_{2})
is generated by classes
R,V_{1},

V_{2},\dots,V_{n}\in H^{1}(\overline{M}_{\alpha};\mathbb{Z}_{2})
subject to the following relations:

(R1)

RV_{i}
+
V_{i}^{2}
, for
i\in[n]
.

(R2)

V_{S}:=\displaystyle\prod_{i\in S}V_{i}
, unless
S
is a subgee.

(R3)

For every subgee
S
with
|S|\geq n-d-2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{T\cap S=\emptyset}R^{d-|T|}V_{T},

where
T
is a subgee.

3.
Length vectors with monogenic codes

A genetic code with only one gene is called
monogenic
.
This section deals with computations of coindex, index and height of certain planar polygon spaces having monogenic code.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\langle\{n\}\rangle
be the genetic code of
n
-length vector
\alpha
. Then
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
is tidy.

Proof.

For a length vector with genetic code
\langle\{n\}\rangle
, it is well known that
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
is homeomorphic to
S^{n-3}
. The identity map on
S^{n-3}
implies that the coindex of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
is at least
n-3
. By Borsuk-Ulam theorem, there does not exist a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant map from
S^{n-2}\to S^{n-3}
. Therefore,
\mathrm{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})=n-3\leq\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})\leq\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})=n-3
. Hence
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
is tidy.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

If the size of the smallest gee in the genetic code corresponding to an
n
-length vector
\alpha
is
l
, then
\emph{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})\geq n-3-l
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
and
S\subset[n]
be the smallest gee of the genetic code of
\alpha
.
Consider the shorter length vector

\alpha(S)=(\alpha_{i_{1}},\dots,\alpha_{i_{k}},\alpha_{n}+\sum_{j\in S}\alpha_{j}),

where
S^{c}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k},n\}
.
Since
S\cup\{n\}
is a gene of the genetic code of
\alpha
, the set
S\cup\{n\}\cup\{i_{s}\}
is long for each
1\leq s\leq k
.
Therefore,
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha(S)}\cong S^{n-l-3}
as
\alpha(S)
is a
(n-l-3)
-length vector.
Note that any polygon with side lengths given by length vector
\alpha(S)
can be considered as a polygon with side lengths given by
\alpha
whose sides indexed by
S
are parallel.
This gives a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant embedding of
S^{n-l-3}
in
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
.
This proves the lemma.
∎

The above result will be used throughout this article.
In particular, this helps in the case of length vector
\alpha
with a gene of size
2
.
First we collect two results we will need.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
be a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-space such that
\mathrm{ind}(X)=\mathrm{coind}(X)=n
for
n\geq 1
.
Then the homotopy group
\pi_{n}(X)
cannot be a torsion group.
In fact,
\pi_{n}(X)
has an infinite cyclic quotient.

Proof.

Since the index and the coindex of
X
are the same, we have

S^{n}\to X\to S^{n}

such that both the maps are
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
equivariant.
The composition gives us a self-map which is antipodal-preserving.
Recall that such a map has odd degree.
Therefore the induced map on
\pi_{n}(S^{n})
is a non-trivial homomorphism with infinite cyclic image that factors through
\pi_{n}(X)
.
∎

We now consider the homotopy type of the universal cover of some polygon spaces.
It is proved in
[
11
, Theorem B]
that for the genetic code
\langle\{n-1,n\}\rangle
then the the universal cover of the corresponding polygon space is homotopic to wedge of infinitely many spheres.
The first step in the proof is to realize that the moduli space is homeomorphic to the connected sum of
n-1
copies of
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-4}
. Then, one uses the topological Bass-Serre theory to construct the universal cover.
Finally, one realizes that the universal cover is exhausted by wedge of
(n-4)
-spheres.
We note that even though the statement of
[
11
, Theorem B]
mentions only one genetic code the proof applies to all genetic codes of the type
\langle\{b,n\}\rangle
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\langle\{b,n\}\rangle
be the genetic code of a length vector
\alpha
and
n\geq 6
.
Then the universal cover of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
has the homotopy type of wedge of infinitely many spheres of dimension
n-4
.

Proof.

Note that as a consequence of
[
9
, Proposition 2.10]
the moduli space corresponding to the genetic code
\langle\{b,n\}\rangle
is homeomorphic to the connected sum
\sharp_{b}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{m-1})
.

The universal cover of a connected sum (of sphere products, in particular) can be constructed using topological Bass-Serre theory. We refer the reader to
[
6
,
14
,
17
]
, for more details.

We sketch the construction here.
Without loss of generality we assume that there are only two summands.
It is useful to consider the connected sum as a handlebody, i.e., first take a copy of
\mathbb{S}^{n-3}
minus the interior of four disjoint embedded balls, say
C_{1},C_{2},D_{1},D_{2}
, denote this space by
P
.
Then attach a handle connecting boundaries of
C_{i}
’s with
D_{i}
’s to obtain a space homeomorphic to
M:=(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-4})\sharp(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-4})
.
Note that
\pi_{1}(M)
is the free group of rank
2
with generators
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
.
In order to construct the universal cover
\tilde{M}
take a copy of
P
for each element
g
in the fundamental group and call it
gP
.
Finally, attach handles between
C_{i}
of
gP
and
D_{i}
of
g\alpha_{i}P
for each
1\leq i\leq 2
.

Observe that if
S
is a finite subset of
\pi_{1}(M)
, then the union of
gP
for
g\in S
is a compact, simply-connected space that gives an exhaustion of
\tilde{M}
.
This space is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{n-3}
minus finitely many disjoint embedded balls; hence it is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of finitely many
\mathbb{S}^{n-4}
’s.
The homology of
\tilde{M}
is the direct limit of homologies of wedge of spheres which is isomorphic to the homology of wedge of infinitely many spheres.
Since both the spaces are simply connected this homology isomorphism is indeed a homotopy equivalence.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\langle\{b,n\}\rangle
be the genetic code of a length vector
\alpha
and
n\neq 6
. Then

\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})=n-4
if
b
is odd, and
n-3
if
b
is even, while
\mathrm{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})=n-4
always. Therefore
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
is tidy iff
b
is odd.

Proof.

Since the genetic code is
\langle\{b,n\}\rangle
, the collection of gees is
\{\{b\}\}
, and hence

\{\emptyset,\{1\},\{2\},\dots,\{b\}\}

is the set of all the subgees.
For
i\neq j
, the relation (R2) of
Theorem

2.7
implies that
V_{i}V_{j}=0
if
i\neq j
.
Let
m=n-3
and
d\geq m
. Consider the subgee
S=\{1\}
, then subgees disjoint from
S
are
\emptyset,\{2\},\{3\},\dots,\{b\}
.
Using the relation (R3) of
Theorem

2.7
, we get

R^{d}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{b}R^{d-1}V_{i}=0.

For the subgee
S=\{j\},\ 1\leq j\leq b
, above equation gives:

(3)

R^{d}+\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i=1}^{b}R^{d-1}V_{i}=0.

Comparing these relations with each other, we get that
RV_{1}=RV_{2}=RV_{3}=\dots=RV_{b}
. Thus,
Equation

3
implies that
R^{d}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{b}R^{d-1}V_{1}=(b-1)R^{d-1}V_{1}.

Hence, for
d=m
, we have

(4)

R^{m}=(b-1)R^{m-1}V_{1}.

By
Lemma

3.2
, we see that
\mathrm{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})\geq m-1
. From
Equation

1
, we get

m-1\leq\mathrm{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})\leq\mathrm{ht}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})\leq\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})\leq\dim(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})=m.

We now analyse
Equation

4
further.
Since
R^{m-1}V_{1}
spans
H^{m}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha},\mathbb{Z}_{2})
we have
R^{m}=0
if and only if
(b-1)\equiv 0
(mod 2) implying that
b
is odd.
Thus for odd values of
b
, the Stiefel-Whitney height
\mathrm{ht}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})=m-1
.
By
Proposition

2.2
we have that
(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha},\tau,m)
is not a BU-triple and hence,
\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})=m-1
.
Hence
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
is tidy whenever
b
is an odd integer.

Now let
b
be even.
Then we get that
\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})=\mathrm{ht}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})=m
.
Therefore to find whether
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
is tidy or not, it suffices to find if
\mathrm{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})
is
m-1
or
m
. We first observe that the space
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}\cong\sharp_{b}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{m-1})
.
If
m=2
, then
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}\cong\sharp_{b}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1})
and hence is not tidy (see
[
13
, Section 5.3, page 100]
).

Let
m\geq 4
and
U(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})
denote the universal cover of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
which has the homotopy type of wedge of infinitely many copies of
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
’s (
Lemma

3.4
).
Let
\Lambda
denote the set indexing the spheres in the wedge product.
Since the homotopy group of a wedge sum of spheres is a colimit of the homotopy groups in that dimension taken over all finite subsets, we have

\displaystyle\pi_{m}(U(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}))

\displaystyle=\pi_{m}(\displaystyle\vee_{\Lambda}\mathbb{S}^{m-1})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{colim}_{\text{fin }F\subset\Lambda}\pi_{m}(\displaystyle\vee_{F}\mathbb{S}^{m-1})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{colim}_{\text{fin }F\subset\Lambda}\displaystyle\oplus_{F}(\pi_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{m-1}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{colim}_{\text{fin }F\subset\Lambda}\displaystyle\oplus_{F}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}).

Hence
\pi_{m}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})=\pi_{m}(U(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}))
is a torsion group and consequently
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
can’t be tidy (
Lemma

3.3
).
Therefore,
m-1=\mathrm{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})<\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})=m
.
Hence
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
is non-tidy for even values of
b
, and tidy for odd values of
b
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

We note two observations here.

(1)

Let
\Sigma_{g}
be the orientable surface of genus
g
. Recall that, for
1\leq g\leq 4
if the genetic code of a length vector
\alpha(g)
is
\langle\{g,5\}\rangle
then
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha(g)}\cong\Sigma_{g}
. Therefore,
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha(g)}
is tidy if and only if
g=1,3
.

(2)

Let
\alpha=(1,1,\dots,1,n-4)
be an
n
-length vector, then the only short subsets containing
n
are
\{n\}
and
\{n-1,n\}
.
Therefore the genetic code in this case is
\langle\{n-1,n\}\rangle
. By
Proposition

3.5
, the corresponding
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
is tidy if
n
is even and non-tidy otherwise.

Consider the following genetic codes:

(1)

G_{1}=\langle\{1,\dots,n-4,n\}\rangle
,

(2)

G_{2}=\langle\{1,\dots,n-5,n-3,n\}\rangle
,

(3)

G_{3}=\langle\{1,\dots,n-5,n-2,n\}\rangle
,

(4)

G_{4}=\langle\{1,\dots,n-5,n-1,n\}\rangle
.

Let
\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}}
be the planar polygon space corresponding to
G_{i}
.
It follows from
[
9
, Proposition 2.1]
that
\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}}\cong(S^{1})^{n-2}\times\mathrm{M}_{\alpha(i)}
where the genetic code of
\alpha(i)
is
\langle\{i,5\}\rangle
for
1\leq i\leq 4
.
The free
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-action on
\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}}
is given by an
Equation

2
.

With the above notations, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 3.7
.

For
1\leq i\leq 4
the space
\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}}
is tidy if and only if
i=1,3
.

Proof.

Note that for each
1\leq i\leq 4
, the projection
\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}}\to\mathrm{M}_{\alpha(i)}
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-map.
Therefore,
\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}})\leq\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha(i)})
.
Since
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha(i)}\cong\Sigma_{i}
,
\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}})\leq 2
for
1\leq i\leq 4
. It is easy to see that

\mathrm{ind}(\Sigma_{i})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $i$ is an odd integer, }\\[10.0pt]
2&\text{if $i$ is an even integer.}\end{cases}

Therefore,

\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}})\leq\begin{cases}1&\text{if $i=1,3$, }\\[10.0pt]
2&\text{if $i=2,4$.}\end{cases}

Let
\alpha(G_{i})
be the length vector corresponding to the genetic code
G_{i}
.
Note that
J=\{1,\dots,n-4\}
is a short subset with respect to each genetic code
G_{i}
.
Therefore, we can reduce
\alpha(G_{i})
to the length vector

\alpha(G_{i},J)=(\alpha_{m-3},\alpha_{m-2},\alpha_{m-1},\alpha_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-4}\alpha_{i}).

Observe that,
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha(G_{i},J)}\cong S^{1}
. Therefore, we have an
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant embedding of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha(G_{i},J)}
in
\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}}
.
This gives

1\leq\mathrm{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}}),\textit{ for }1\leq i\leq 4.

Now it is clear that
\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}}
is tidy if
i=1,3
.

For
i=2,4
we now prove that
\mathrm{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}})=1
and
\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}})=2
.
It follows from
[
2
, Theorem 2.3]
that the
2\leq\mathrm{ht}(\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}}))
for
i=2,4
.
Therefore,

\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}})=2,\text{ for }i=2,4.

Now it follows from

Proposition

3.5
that

\mathrm{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}})=1,\text{ for }i=2,4.

Therefore,
\mathrm{M}_{G_{i}}
is not tidy for
i=2,4
. This proves the proposition.
∎

4.
Formula for the Stiefel-Whitney height

In this section, we deal with the
n
-length vectors that correspond to the genetic code having two genes with one gene of size 2.
We begin with obtaining a formula for
R^{n-3}
, when the genetic code is
\langle\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k},n\},\{b,n\}\rangle
, where
g_{1}<g_{2}<\dots<g_{k}<b
. This formula is a generalization of Davis’ formula
[
3
, Theorem 1.4]
.

Let us fix the following notations for the sake of simplicity of writing.

(1)

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
be the set of non-negative integers, and

S_{k}=\{(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{k}:\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}b_{k-j}\leq i\text{ for }1\leq i\leq k\}.

(2)

Let
B=(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{k}
. Denote
B_{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}
.

(3)

Let
J=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{r}\}
be a set of distinct positive integers such that
j_{i}\leq g_{k}
for
1\leq i\leq r
.
Let
\theta(J)=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{k})
, where

\theta_{i}=|\{j\in J:g_{i-1}<j\leq g_{i}\}|.

Throughout this article,
|A|
denotes the cardinality of the set
A
, and we take
g_{0}=0
.

It is easy to see that
J
is a subgee dominated by
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\}
if and only if
\theta(J)\in S_{k}
.
With the above notations, Davis proved the following result.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
3
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

Let
\langle\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k},n\}\rangle
be the genetic code of
\alpha
and
a_{i}=g_{k+1-i}-g_{k-i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
.
Let
\phi:H^{n-3}(\overline{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha};\mathbb{Z}_{2})\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}
be the Poincare-duality isomorphism and
J
be a subgee of cardinality
r
. Then

\phi(R^{n-3-r}V_{J})=\sum_{B}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\binom{a_{i}+b_{i}-2}{b_{i}},

where

B\in\{(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{k}:\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}=k-r,\ (b_{1},\dots,b_{k})+\theta(J)\in S_{k}\}.

We extend the above result for the genetic code
\langle\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k},n\},\{b,n\}\rangle
with
g_{1}<\dots<g_{k}<b
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\langle\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k},n\},\{b,n\}\rangle
be the genetic code of
\alpha
and
a_{i}=g_{k+1-i}-g_{k-i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
.
Then

R^{n-3}=\sum_{B}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\binom{a_{i}+b_{i}-2}{b_{i}}+(b-g_{k}),

where
B\in S_{k}
with
B_{+}=k
.

To prove this theorem, we need some further notations and some more results which we derive now.

(1)

The collection
\mathcal{S}_{i}=\{P\subseteq[n-1]:P\text{ is a subgee and }|P|=i\}
.

(2)

For a subgee
P
, its support set is
\mathrm{supp}(P)=\{s\in[n-1]:s\notin P\text{ and $P\cup\{s\}$ a subgee}\}.

(3)

For a subgee
P
and
s\in\mathrm{supp}(P)
,
\mathcal{S}_{i}(P,s)=\{S\in\mathcal{S}_{i}:P\subseteq S,~s\notin S\}
.

(4)

For
1\leq i\leq k
, the set
(g_{i-1},g_{i}]:=\{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}:g_{i-1}<j\leq g_{i}\}
and
(g_{k},b]
is defined similarly. These sets are referred to as
blocks.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
P
be a subgee and
\{s,s^{\prime}\}\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(P)
. If
s
and
s^{\prime}
are from the same block, then for each
t\in[k]

|\mathcal{S}_{t}(P,s)|=|\mathcal{S}_{t}(P,s^{\prime})|.

Proof.

Let
t\in[k]
be fixed. Define
f:\mathcal{S}_{t}(P,s)\to\mathcal{S}_{t}(P,s^{\prime})
by

f(S)=\begin{cases}S&\text{if $s^{\prime}\notin S$, }\\[10.0pt]
(S\setminus\{\ s^{\prime}\})\cup\{s\}&\text{if $s^{\prime}\in S$.}\end{cases}

It is easy to see that
f
is a bijection.
∎

The next result shows that the conclusion of
Proposition

4.3
holds even if
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{supp}(P)
and are not in the same block, provided
|P|=k-1
.

Proposition 4.4
.

If
P\in\mathcal{S}_{k-1}
and
\{s,s^{\prime}\}\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(P)
, then

|\mathcal{S}_{k}(P,s)|=|\mathcal{S}_{k}(P,s^{\prime})|.

Proof.

We observe that both
P\cup\{s\}
and
P\cup\{s^{\prime}\}
are subgees of cardinality
k
, and thus

\mathcal{S}_{k}(P,s)\setminus(P\cup\{s^{\prime}\})=\mathcal{S}_{k}(P,s^{\prime})\setminus(P\cup\{s\}).

This proves the result.
∎

In
[
4
, Corollary 1.6]
, the author proved that for the monogenic code
\langle\{g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{k},n\}\rangle
the image of
R^{m-|J|}V_{J}
under

(the Poincare duality isomorphism) is
1
whenever the subgee
J
is of cardinality
k
. Observe that the same proof works for any genetic code whenever
J
is of maximum cardinality. Here we reproduce the proof in our case for completeness.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\langle\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k},n\},\{b,n\}\rangle
be the genetic code of a length vector
\alpha
and
J
be a subgee of cardinality
k
. Then
\phi(R^{m-|J|}V_{J})=1
.

Proof.

Note that the product
V_{J}
is non-zero since it is non-zero in the quotient
H^{*}(\overline{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha};\mathbb{Z}_{2})/\langle R\rangle
, which is an exterior face ring (see
[

, Corollary 1.6]
).
By Poincaré duality, there must be an

such that
X\cdot V_{J}=1
and thus
\phi(X\cdot V_{J})=1
.
Since
J
is maximal, if
X_{i}
contains a factor
V_{t}
such that

, then
X_{i}\cdot V_{J}=0
. Now using the relation

of

2.7
, we can replace

by

, whenever
t\in J
.
Therefore if

can be replaced by

. Since
\phi(X\cdot V_{J})=1
, number of such

∎

Corollary 4.9
.

If

and

\mathcal{S}_{k}(P,s)\setminus(P\cup\{s^{\prime}\})=\mathcal{S}_{k}(P,s^{\prime})\setminus(P\cup\{s\}).

Proposition 4.10
.

If
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
, then
R^{m-k}V_{S}=R^{m-k}V_{S^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Let

be the gee

. We will show that for any

R^{m-k}V_{T}=R^{m-k}V_{\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k-1},i_{k}\}}.

Similar argument implies that

By continuing this way, we get

Therefore, for any two subgees of size

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
P
be a subgee with
|P|=r
and

. If

, then

R^{m-r-1}V_{P\cup\{s\}}=R^{m-r-1}V_{P\cup\{s^{\prime}\}},

whenever

and
s^{\prime}
are in the same block.

Proof.

We prove this in decreasing order of

. From Corollary

, the result holds for

.
Let
|P|=r
and result holds for all subgees of cardinality bigger than

. Let
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{supp}(P)
. Using

4.6
, we get

\sum_{r+1\leq i\leq k}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{i}(P,s)}R^{m-i}V_{S}\right)=\sum_{r+1\leq j\leq k}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{j}(P,s^{\prime})}R^{m-j}V_{S}\right).

Using induction and

4.3
, we get

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{r+1}(P,s)}R^{m-t-1}V_{S}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{r+1}(P,s^{\prime})}R^{m-t-1}V_{S}.

The result now follows from
Proposition

4.4
.
∎

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
G=\langle\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\},\{b,n\}\rangle

and

{supp}(P)\neq\emptyset
.
Then the value of

under the corresponding Poincare duality isomorphisms from

remains same.

Proof.

Observe that if

4.5
.
Now we assume that

. In this case,

\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\}
.
Note that the collections of subgees of cardinality greater than

The following is now an immediate consequence of the above result and

4.1
.

Corollary 4.13
.

Let
\langle\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k},n\},\{b,n\}\rangle
be the genetic code of
\alpha
and
a_{i}=g_{k+1-i}-g_{k-i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
.
Let
P
be a subgee of cardinality
r\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}
dominated by
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\}
. Then

\phi(R^{m-r}V_{P})=\sum_{B}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\binom{a_{i}+b_{i}-2}{b_{i}},

where

B\in\{(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{k}:\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}=k-r,\textit{ and }(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})+\theta(P)\in S_{k}\}.

We now have all the necessary machinery for
Theorem

4.2
. So we next see its proof.

Proof of
Theorem

4.2
.

Without loss of generality, we omit the powers of
R
just to simplify the notations.
Comparing
E_{\{g_{k}\}}
with
E_{\{b\}}
we get.

(8)

V_{b}=V_{g_{k}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|S|\geq 2\\
g_{k}\in S\end{subarray}}V_{S}.

Let
P=\{g_{k}\}
and
s=g_{k-1}
.
Then
Lemma

4.6
gives

V_{g_{k}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|S|\geq 2\\
g_{k}\in S,~g_{k-1}\notin S\end{subarray}}V_{S}.

Comparing it with
Equation

8
, we get

(9)

V_{b}=V_{\{g_{k},g_{k-1}\}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|S|\geq 3\\
\{g_{k},g_{k-1}\}\subseteq S\end{subarray}}V_{S}.

Recall that
a_{j}=g_{k+1-j}-g_{k-j}
for
1\leq j\leq k
.
To get a more explicit expression for the monomials
V_{S}
, in the above equation, we note the following.
Any subgee
S
of cardinality at least
3
and containing
\{g_{k},g_{k-1}\}
corresponds to a
k
-typle
C:=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
such that
C+(1,1,0,\dots,0)
belongs to
S_{k}
.
In order to expand each
V_{S}
as a product of generating classes we note that exactly
c_{j}
members can be chosen from the block
(g_{k-j},g_{k-j+1}]
and then use
Lemma

4.11
to get the following expression.

V_{b}=V_{\{g_{k},g_{k-1}\}}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-2}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C+(1,1,0\dots,0)\in S_{k}\\
C_{+}=i\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{j=3}^{k}\binom{a_{j}}{c_{j}}V_{g_{k}}V_{g_{k-1}}V_{g_{k-j+1}}V_{g_{k-j+1}-1}\dots\right)\right).

Allowing
C_{+}=0
enables us to rewrite the above equation as

(10)

V_{b}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C+(1,1,0\dots,0)\in S_{k}\\
C_{+}=i\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{j=3}^{k}\binom{a_{j}}{c_{j}}V_{g_{k}}V_{g_{k-1}}V_{g_{k-j+1}}V_{g_{k-j+1}-1}\dots\right)\right).

From

4.13
we get,

\begin{split}V_{b}&=\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C+(1,1,0\dots,0)\in S_{k}\\

&=\sum_{C+(1,1,0\dots,0)\in S_{k}}\left(\prod_{j=3}^{k}\binom{a_{j}}{c_{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B+C+(1,1,0\dots,0)\in S_{k}\\
B_{+}=k-2-C_{+}\end{subarray}}~\prod_{q=1}^{k}\binom{a_{q}+b_{q}-2}{b_{q}}\right).\end{split}

For
P=\{1\}
, the relation
(R3)
of
Theorem

2.7
gives

R^{m}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|S|\geq 1\\
1\notin S\end{subarray}}V_{S}=\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|S|\geq 1\\

If
j_{1},j_{2}\in(g_{k},b]

.
Hence all

.

(11)

R^{m}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|S|\geq 1\\

Note that
C+(1,1,0,\dots,0)\in S_{k}

Let

Hence, using

R^{m}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S_{k}\\

.

The following is a straightforward corollary of the above result.

Corollary 4.14
.

Assuming the notations introduced in the proof of the previous result, and let
\langle\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k},n\},\{b,n\}\rangle
be the genetic code of
\alpha
. If

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S_{k}\\
B_{+}=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\binom{a_{i}+b_{i}-2}{b_{i}}+(b-g_{k})\equiv 0\ \mathrm{(mod\ 2)},

then
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
is tidy.

Proof.

By
Theorem

4.2
, we have that

R^{m}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B_{+}=k\\
B\in S_{k}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\binom{a_{i}+b_{i}-2}{b_{i}}+(b-g_{k})=0.

Since the size of the smallest gee in
\langle\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k},n\},\{b,n\}\rangle
is
1
,
Lemma

3.2
implies that

n-4\leq\mathrm{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})\leq\mathrm{ht}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})\leq\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})\leq n-3.

Using
[
7
, Proposition 2.2]
,
the height of the Stiefel-Whitney class is full if and only if the index is full. Here
R^{m}=0
, thus
\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})
is not full. In particular,
\mathrm{ind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})\leq n-4.
This proves the result.
∎

For an illustration of
Theorem

4.2
, we give an example where we compute the height of the Stiefel-Whitney class.

Example 4.15
.

Suppose that the genetic code is
\langle\{2,4,n\},\{b,n\}\rangle
and
b\geq 5
. Note that the collection of subgees is

(12)

\bigg\{\emptyset,\{1\},\{2\},\dots,\{b-1\},\{b\},\{1,2\},\{1,3\},\{1,4\},\{2,3\},\{2,4\}\bigg\}.

For the subgee
\{1,2\}
, the relation (R3) of
Theorem

2.7
gives

R^{m}=\sum_{i=3}^{b}R^{m-1}V_{i}.

Using Proposition
4.10
and
Equation

12
, we get

(13)

R^{m}=(b-2)R^{m-1}V_{b}=b-2.

We now compute the value of
R^{m}
using
Theorem

4.2
. Note that only possible values of
B\in S_{2}
are
(1,1)
and
(2,0)
.
Therefore,
Theorem

4.2
gives

R^{m}=\binom{1}{1}\binom{1}{1}+\binom{0}{0}\binom{2}{2}+b-4=b-2,

as desired.

5.
The case of quasi-equilateral polygon spaces

The planar polygon space
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
associated with the length vector
\alpha=(1,\dots,1,r)
is called a
quasi-equilateral
planar polygon space.
Suppose
r
is a natural number. Then it is easy to see that
\alpha
is generic if both
r
and
n
have the same parity.
Moreover, if
r>n-1
then
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}=\emptyset
.
We make the following observations.

(1)

If
r=n-2
, then the genetic code of
\alpha
is
\langle\{n\}\rangle
. It follows from
[
9
, Example 2.6]

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}\cong S^{n-3}
and
\overline{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha}\cong\mathbb{R}P^{n-3}
.

(2)

If
r=n-4
, then the genetic code of
\alpha
is
\langle\{n-1,n\}\rangle
. Then
[
9
, Example 2.12]
gives that
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}\cong\sharp_{n-1}(S^{1}\times S^{n-4})
and
\overline{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha}\cong\sharp_{n}\mathbb{R}P^{n-3}
. Here
\sharp_{n}X
represents the connected sum of
n
copies of a space
X
.

From
Theorem

2.7
, the cohomology ring

H^{\ast}(\overline{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha};\mathbb{Z}_{2})
for
\alpha=(1,\dots,1,r)
is generated by classes
R,V_{1},

V_{2},\dots,V_{n-1}\in H^{1}(\overline{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha};\mathbb{Z}_{2})
subject to the following relations:

(R1)

RV_{i}
+
V_{i}^{2}=0
, for
i\in[n-1]
,

(R2)

V_{S}=0
if
|S|\geq\frac{n-r}{2}
,

(R3)

For
L\subseteq[n-1]
such that
|L|\geq\frac{n+r}{2}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq L}R^{|L-S|-1}V_{S}=0.

Recall that the class
R
coincides with the first Stiefel-Whitney class of the double cover
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}\to\overline{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha}
.
Kamiyama
[
12
]
computed the height of
R
in terms of the values of
n
and
r
.
Before stating this result, we recall the following notations from
[
12
]
.

Notations:

(1)

D(n):=n-2
,
e(n,r):=\frac{n-r}{2}-1
. Note that
e(n,r)
is the largest size of the gee (here it is smallest as well since the genetic code has a single gene).

(2)

k(n,r):=\max\bigg\{i:0\leq i\leq e(n,r)-1,~\binom{D(n)-e(n,r)+i}{i}\equiv 1(\text{mod }2)\bigg\}
.

(3)

\phi(n,r):=\begin{cases}n-3,&\text{ if }\binom{D(n)}{e(n,r)}\equiv 1(\text{mod }2),\\[10.0pt]
\frac{n+r}{2}+k(n,r)-2,&\text{ if }\binom{D(n)}{e(n,r)}\equiv 0(\text{mod }2).\end{cases}

Theorem 5.1

(
[
12
, Theorem A]
)
.

Let
h(n,r)
be the height of the class
R
.
Then, for all
n\geq 4
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r\leq n-2
with the same parity as
n
,
h(n,r)=\phi(n,r)
.

We use
Theorem

5.1
to establish bounds on the index and the coindex of the corresponding
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha}
’s by computing their
h(n,r)
.
For that, we first give an algorithm to effectively and inductively compute the Stiefel-Whitney height of the corresponding quasi-equilateral planar polygon spaces.

Let
e(n,r)=k
and that
2^{t}\leq k<2^{t+1}
for some natural number
t
. For
1\leq j\leq k+1
, denote the sets
\{n-2:\binom{n-2-(j-1)}{k-(j-1)}\equiv 1\text{ (mod $2$)}\}
(mod
2^{t+1}
) and
\{n-2:\phi(n,r)=n-3+(j-1)\}
(mod
2^{t+1}
) by
A_{j}
and
B_{j}
respectively. For simplicity of notations, let
C_{j}=\sqcup_{i=1}^{j}B_{i}
for any
1\leq j\leq k+1
. Our aim now is to inductively determine the set
B_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq k+1
, starting with
B_{1}
. The following are some easy observations

(i)

A_{1}=B_{1}=C_{1}
.

(ii)

B_{j}=A_{j}\setminus C_{j-1}
.

We first explain the method for computing
A_{1}
and thereafter we give arguments for computing
A_{j}
inductively, which will be enough to compute
B_{j}
and
C_{j}
(from previous observations). Let
(k)_{2}=k_{t}k_{t-1}\dots k_{1}k_{0}
be the binary representation of the number
k
,
i.e.
,

k=2^{t}k_{t}+2^{t-1}k_{t-1}+\dots+2k_{1}+k_{0}.

Clearly,
k_{0},\dots,k_{t}\in\{0,1\}.
Let
I_{k}
be the indexing set corresponding to
k
that collects the location of non-zero
k_{i}
’s in an increasing order,
i.e.
,
I_{k}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{s}\}\subseteq[t]\cup\{0\}
with
i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{s}=t
, so that
k_{l}=1
if and only if
l\in I_{k}
.

We notice that the height is full,
i.e.
,
\phi(n,r)=n-3
, if and only if
\binom{n-2}{k}\equiv 1\ (\text{mod 2})
.
Using Lucas theorem, it suffices to know that in binary representations, each position in
(n-2)_{2}
is at least as much as
(k)_{2}
, position-wise,
i.e.
,

(14)

\alpha_{l}\geq k_{l}\text{ for all }0\leq l\leq t,

where
\alpha_{l}
is the coefficient of
2^{l}
in the binary representation of
n-2
.
Since
|I_{k}|=s
, there are
2^{t-s+1}
choices for
n-2
to satisfy
Equation

14
modulo
2^{t+1}
. These are exactly the places where
k_{i}
’s are zero. Let these values of
n-2
be
\{n_{1},\dots,n_{t-s+1}\}\text{ modulo }2^{t+1}
,
i.e.
,
n-2\equiv p
for some
p\in\{n_{1},\dots,n_{t-s+1}\}\text{ (mod $2^{t+1}$)}
. Thus,
B_{1}=A_{1}=\{n_{1},\dots,n_{t-s+1}\}
.

We now explain the algorithm of computing
A_{j+1}
, for
1\leq j\leq k
, from
A_{j}
. Let
I_{k-(j-1)}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{d}\}
, where
p_{d}\leq t
. Recall that
I_{k-(j-1)}
collects the location of
1
’s in the binary representation of
k-(j-1)
in an increasing order. We do the computations depending upon whether
k-(j-1)
is even or odd.

Case 1:

k-(j-1)
is odd.

We see that if
k-(j-1)
is odd, then
p_{1}=0
. Therefore,

I_{k-j}=I_{k-j+1}\setminus\{1\}=\{p_{2},p_{3},\dots,p_{d}\}.

Using Lucas theorem, we see that there are a total of
2^{t+1-(d-1)}
values that
n-2-j
can take to satisfy the equation
\binom{n-2-j}{k-j}\equiv 1\text{ (mod $2$)}
. It is now easy to see that
\binom{n-2-j}{k-j}\equiv 1\text{ (mod $2$)}
if and only if
n-2-j\equiv q\text{ (mod }2^{t+1})
where
q\in A_{j}\cup\{\ell-1:\ell\in A_{j}\}
. Hence,

(15)

\begin{split}A_{j+1}&=A_{j}\cup\{q+1:q\in A_{j}\}\text{ (mod }2^{t+1}),\text{ and}\\
B_{j+1}&=A_{j+1}\setminus C_{j}=\{q+1:q\in A_{j}\}\text{ (mod }2^{t+1}).\end{split}

Case 2:

k-(j-1)
is even.

If
k-(j-1)
is even, then
p_{1}>0
.
Therefore

I_{k-j}=\{0,1,\dots,p_{1}-1,p_{2},\dots,p_{d}\}.

This gives us that the values of
n-2-j
that satisfy
\binom{n-2-j}{k-j}\equiv 1\text{ (mod $2$)}
are
n-2-j\equiv q
(mod
2^{t+1}
), for some
q\in\{\ell_{1}-1:\ell_{1}\in A_{j},~\ell_{1}\equiv 0\text{ (mod }2^{p_{1}})\}\cup\{\ell_{2}\in A_{j}:\ell_{2}\equiv 2^{p_{1}}-1\text{ (mod }2^{p_{1}})\}
. Therefore,

(16)

\begin{split}A_{j+1}=&\big\{\{\ell_{1}:\ell_{1}\in A_{j},~\ell_{1}-(j-1)\equiv 0\text{ (mod }2^{p_{1}})\}\\
&\cup\{\ell_{2}+1:\ell_{2}\in A_{j},~\ell_{2}-(j-1)\equiv 2^{p_{1}}-1\text{ (mod }2^{p_{1}})\}\big\}\text{ (mod }2^{t+1}),\text{ and}\\
B_{j+1}&=\{\ell_{2}+1:\ell_{2}\in A_{j},~\ell_{2}-(j-1)\equiv 2^{p_{1}}-1\text{ (mod }2^{p_{1}})\}\big\}\text{ (mod }2^{t+1}).\end{split}

We repeat these steps to complete the analysis up till
\phi(n,r)=1
.

,

,

.

of 3, we see that

and

.
Using the above algorithm, there are

.
Therefore,

, whenever

,

,

.
As per the notation in the above algorithm,

.

We now compute when

.
Since

is odd,

To find the values of

Here

Finally, the values of

6.
Concluding remarks

In most of the results of this paper, we have found the exact value of the index for various planar polygon spaces.
However, in some cases, we could only find a lower bound for the index (for instance, see
Table

1
). It would be interesting to see if in those cases the height is equal to the index.

In
Lemma

3.2
, we showed that
\mathrm{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})\geq n-k-3
for any generic
n
-length vector
\alpha
with the smallest gee of size
k
. Based on our computations, we believe that this bound is tight. Therefore, we conjecture the following.

Conjecture 6.1
.

Let
\alpha
be a generic
n
-length vector. If the size of the smallest gee is
k
, then

\mathrm{coind}(\mathrm{M}_{\alpha})=n-k-3.

In the proof of
Proposition

3.5
, we showed that the
6.1
is true for
\alpha=\{b,n\}
if
n\neq 6
.
