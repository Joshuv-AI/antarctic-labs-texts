Truncated LinUCB for Stochastic Linear Bandits
Yanglei Song, Meng Zhou

Abstract
This paper considers contextual bandits with a finite number of arms, where the contexts are independent and identically distributed d d -dimensional random vectors, and the expected rewards are linear in both the arm parameters and contexts. The LinUCB algorithm, which is near minimax optimal for related linear bandits, is shown to have a cumulative regret that is suboptimal in both the dimension d d and time horizon T T , due to its over-exploration. A truncated version of LinUCB is proposed and termed “Tr-LinUCB", which follows LinUCB up to a truncation time S S and performs pure exploitation afterwards. The Tr-LinUCB algorithm is shown to achieve O ⁡ ( d ​ log ⁡ ( T ) ) O(d\log(T)) regret if S = C ​ d ​ log ⁡ ( T ) S=Cd\log(T) for a sufficiently large constant C C , and a matching lower bound is established, which shows the rate optimality of Tr-LinUCB in both d d and T T under a low dimensional regime. Further, if S = d ​ log κ ​ ( T ) S=d\log^{\kappa}(T) for κ > 1 \kappa>1 , the loss compared to the optimal is an extra log ⁡ log ⁡ ( T ) \log\log(T) factor, which does not depend on d d . This insensitivity to overshooting in choosing the truncation time of Tr-LinUCB is of practical importance.

1.1
Our contributions

First, we construct explicit problem instances, for which the cumulative regret of the LinUCB algorithm is both
\Omega(d^{2}\log^{2}(T))
and
O(d^{2}\log^{2}(T))
, and thus prove that LinUCB is suboptimal for stochastic linear bandits in both the dimension
d
and the horizon
T
.
The suboptimality of LinUCB is because the path-wise upper confidence bounds in LinUCB, based on the self-normalization principle
[
43
]
, is wider than the actual order of statistical error in estimating the arm parameters; see subsection
3.4
.

Second, in view of its over-exploration, we propose to truncate the duration of the LinUCB algorithm, and call the proposed algorithm “Tr-LinUCB". Specifically, we run LinUCB up to a truncation time
S
, and then perform pure exploitation afterwards. For Tr-LinUCB, if the truncation time
S=Cd\log(T)
for a large enough
C
, its cumulative regret is
O(d\log(T))
; more importantly, in practice, if we choose
S=d\log^{\kappa}(T)
for some
\kappa>1
, the regret is
O(d\log(T)\log\log(T))
. Thus unlike OLS, whose regret would be linear in the number of forced sampling, the cost of overshooting for Tr-LinUCB, i.e.,
S
being a larger order than the optimal, is a multiplicative
\log\log(T)
factor, that does not depend on
d
. The practical implication is that Tr-LinUCB is insensitive to the selection of the truncation time
S
if we err on the side of overshooting. Extensive experiments, including on several real-world datasets, corroborate our theory.

Third, we establish a matching
\Omega(d\log(T))
lower bound on the worst-case regret over concrete families of problem instances,
and thus show the rate optimality of Tr-LinUCB, with a proper truncation time, in both the dimension
d
and horizon
T
, for such families.
More specifically, the characterization of the optimal dependence on
d
, in both the upper and lower bounds, appears novel, holds under the low dimensional regime
d=O(\log(T)/\log\log(T))
, and relies on an assumption on contexts that relates the expected instant regret to the second moment of the arm parameters estimation error; see condition
3.1
. Under this assumption, by similar arguments, it can be shown that the OLS algorithm proposed by
[
23
]
also achieves
O(d\log(T))
regret. Thus our contribution in this regard should be understood as proposing and working with such a condition,
and verifying it for concrete problem instances, e.g., when contexts have a log-concave Lebesgue density. Without this condition, we establish
O(d^{2}\log(2d)\log(T))
upper bound for Tr-LinUCB, similar to that for OLS
[
8
]
, which, however, may not be tight (in
d
) for any family of problem instances. As discussed above, the main practical advantage of Tr-LinUCB is its insensitivity to tuning parameters.

Finally, we note that the elliptical potential lemma
[
37
, Lemma 19.4]
, which is the main tool for the analysis of LinUCB
[
1
,
38
,
57
,
26
,
38
]
, does not lead to the
O(\log(T))
upper bound for Tr-LinUCB, and a tailored analysis is required to handle the dependence among observations induced by sequential decision making, and to show that information accumulates at a linear rate in time for each arm.

1.2
More on stochastic linear bandits

In the formulation (
1
), under the “large margin" condition (for
K=2
) that

\mathbb{P}(|(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{X}_{1}|\leqslant\tau)=O(\tau^{\alpha})
with
\alpha>1
, the optimal regret is
O(1)
, achieved by the Greedy algorithm
[
9
, Corollary 1]
and the LinUCB algorithm
[
57
,
26
, Remark 8.4]
. We note that if

is fixed, and

has a continuous component with a bounded density, then the margin condition (i.e.,
\alpha=1
) holds, and thus it has a wider applicability.
Under the high dimensional regime,

Bastani and Bayati 2020
extends the OLS algorithm by replacing the least squares estimator by Lasso, which achieves a
O(s_{0}^{2}\log^{2}(T))
regret if
\log(d)=O(\log(T))
, where

is the number of non-zero elements in
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}

conjectures

Next, we discuss a more general version of stochastic linear bandits. Specifically, at each time
t\geqslant 1
, based on previous observations, a decision maker chooses an action
A_{t}
from a possibly infinite action set

, and receives a reward

\epsilon_{t}
is a zero mean observation noise. To see how the formulation in (
1

, and each

When the size of action set

K<\infty

\Omega(d\sqrt{T})
(resp.
\Omega(\sqrt{dT})
) lower bound, and is achieved, up to a logarithmic factor in
T
(resp.
T
and
K
), by, e.g.,
Dani, Hayes and
Kakade 2008
,
Abbasi-Yadkori, Pál and
Szepesvári 2011

Problem Formulation and Tr-LinUCB Algorithm

As discussed in the introduction, we consider
2\leqslant K<\infty
arms, and assume that the sequence of contexts
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t}:t\geqslant 1\}
are i.i.d.
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-random vectors, which may or may not contain an intercept. Recall that at each time
t\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, one observes the context
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
, and the potential outcome,
Y_{t}^{(k)}
, for arm
k\in[K]
is given by equation (
1
). If arm
k
is selected at time
t
, the realized reward
Y_{t}
is
Y_{t}^{(k)}
. For simplicity, we assume that
(\boldsymbol{X}_{t};\epsilon_{t}^{(k)},k\in[K])

t\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
are independent and identically distributed as a generic random vector
(\boldsymbol{X};\epsilon^{(k)},k\in[K])
. Thus a problem instance is determined by arm parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}:k\in[K]\}
, and the distribution of this generic random vector.

We assume that the time horizon
T\geqslant\max\{d,16\}
is known, and then an admissible rule is described by a sequence of measurable functions
\pi_{t}:(\mathbb{R}^{d}*[K]*\mathbb{R})^{t-1}*\mathbb{R}^{d}*\mathbb{R}\to[K]
for
t\in[T]

\pi_{t}

\boldsymbol{X}_{t}

\textup{Unif}(0,1)

\xi_{t}
, that is,

\displaystyle A_{t}=\pi_{t}(\{\boldsymbol{X}_{s},A_{s},Y_{s}:s<t\},\;\boldsymbol{X}_{t},\;\xi_{t}),\qquad Y_{t}=Y_{t}^{(A_{t})},\quad\text{ for }t\in[T],

(2)

where
\{\xi_{t}:t\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\}
are i.i.d., independent from all potential observations
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t},Y_{t}^{(k)}:k\in[K],t\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\}
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\sigma(0)
; for each
t\in[T]
, denote by
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{s},A_{s},Y_{s}:s\in[t])

t
, and by
\mathcal{F}_{t+}:=\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{t},\boldsymbol{X}_{t+1},\xi_{t+1})
the information set during the decision making at time
t+1

A_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{(t-1)+}

.

We evaluate the performance of an admissible rule in (

\displaystyle R_{T}(\{\pi_{t}:t\in[T]\}):=\sum_{t\in[T]}\mathbb{E}[\hat{r}_{t}],\quad\text{ where }\;\;\hat{r}_{t}:=\max_{k\in[K]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{X}_{t})-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{A_{t}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{X}_{t}.

(3)

(C.I) There exist absolute positive constants

, almost surely.

2.1
The proposed Tr-LinUCB Algorithm

As discussed in the introduction, the exploration of the popular LinUCB algorithm
[
40
,
1
,
37
,
26
,
57
]
is excessive, which leads to its suboptimal performance. We propose to stop the LinUCB algorithm early, and perform pure exploitation afterwards; we call the proposed algorithm “Tr-LinUCB", where “Tr" is short for “Truncated".

The Tr-LinUCB algorithm assumes that the constants
m_{\theta}
and
\sigma^{2}
in
2
known, and requires user provided parameters
\lambda>0
and
S\leqslant T
, where
\lambda
is used in estimating the arm parameters by ridge regression, and
S
denotes the truncation time of LinUCB. Specifically, let
\mathbb{V}_{0}^{(k)}=\lambda\mathbb{I}_{d}
and
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{0}_{d}
for
k\in[K]
. At each time
t\in[T]
, it involves two steps.

1.

(Arm selection) If
t\leqslant S
, we follow the LinUCB algorithm, by selecting the arm that maximizes upper confidence bounds for potential rewards; otherwise, we select an arm greedily. Specifically,
A_{t}=\arg\max_{k\in[K]}\text{UCB}_{t}(k)I(t\leqslant S)+((\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1}^{(k)})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{X}_{t})I(t>S)
, where

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{UCB}_{t}(k):=(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1}^{(k)})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{X}_{t}+\sqrt{\beta_{t-1}^{(k)}}\|\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\|_{(\mathbb{V}_{t-1}^{(k)})^{-1}},\qquad\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1}^{(k)}=(\mathbb{V}_{t-1}^{(k)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{t-1}^{(k)},\quad\text{and}\\
&\sqrt{\beta_{t-1}^{(k)}}=m_{\theta}\sqrt{\lambda}+\sigma\sqrt{2\log(T)+\log\left({\text{det}(\mathbb{V}_{t-1}^{(k)})}/{\lambda^{d}}\right)}.\end{split}

(4)

3.1
Assumptions

In this subsection, we collect assumptions and their discussions. For each

and

Assume that for some absolute positive constants

(C.II)
\mathbb{P}\left(|(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{X}|\leqslant\tau\right)\leqslant L_{0}\tau

(C.III)
\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}I\left(\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{U}_{{\ell_{0}}}^{(k)}\right)\right]\right)\geqslant\ell_{0}^{2}

(C.IV)
\mathbb{P}(|\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{X}|\leqslant\ell_{1})\leqslant 1/4
for all
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
.

(C.V)

\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\|\geqslant L_{1}^{-1}
and
for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
,
\mathbb{E}[|\boldsymbol{u}_{*}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{X}|I(\text{sgn}(\boldsymbol{u}_{*}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{X})\neq\text{sgn}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{X}))]\leqslant L_{1}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{*}-\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}
, where
\boldsymbol{u}_{*}=(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})/\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\|

The first two conditions are standard in the literature; see, e.g.,
Goldenshluger and
Zeevi 2013
,
Bastani and Bayati 2020
,
Bastani, Bayati and
Khosravi 2021
, and the discussions therein. In particular, the condition

is known as the “margin condition", requiring that the probability of
\boldsymbol{X}
falling within
\tau
distance to the boundary

is upper bounded by
L_{0}\tau
; note that since we can always increase

,
3.1
is in force only for small

. The condition
3.1
is known as the “positive-definiteness condition", which requires roughly that each arm is optimal by at least

with a positive probability, and that conditional on this event, the context
\boldsymbol{X}
spans
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; note that we use

on both sides of

\ell_{0}
becomes smaller.

The condition

requires that the projection of
\boldsymbol{X}
onto any direction is not concentrated about zero. We refer to it as “absolute continuity condition", as justified by the following lemma. Specifically, it holds with some constant
\ell_{1}
, depending on

\boldsymbol{X}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, after maybe removing the intercept. If its Lebesgue density is log-concave, then

is dimension free, i.e., independent of
d
. Note that a density

is log-concave, if
\log(p)
is a concave function.
In the following lemma, if the context
\boldsymbol{X}=(1,({\boldsymbol{X}}^{(-1)})^{\prime})^{\prime}
has an intercept, let

and
\tilde{d}=d-1

and

Let

(i)

(ii)

7
Conclusion

In this work, we consider the stochastic linear bandit problem in a low-dimensional regime, where the covariate dimension
d
is much smaller than the time horizon
T
. We show that the LinUCB algorithm is suboptimal in this setting due to over-exploration, and propose a truncated variant, Tr-LinUCB, which switches to pure exploitation after a specified time
S
. Through theoretical analysis and simulations, we demonstrate that Tr-LinUCB is robust to the choice of
S
. Furthermore, we characterize the minimax rate for concrete families of problem instances and show that Tr-LinUCB achieves minimax optimality. Although the setup is classical, the optimal dependence on
d
established here is, to our knowledge, novel.

As for future directions, it is of interest to consider the stochastic high-dimensional sparse linear bandit problem, where the minimax rate remains unknown. In addition, we plan to extend the framework to generalized linear models and to settings with unknown observation noise.

random variable that is independent from
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
. There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for any
\mathbb{R}^{d}
random vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{n}\in\sigma(\mathcal{H}_{n},\xi)
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}/{\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}\|}\right\|^{2}\right]\geqslant\sigma^{2}{(d-1)^{2}}/{(n\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}\|^{2}]+Cd^{2}\sigma^{2})}.

Proof.

We follow the approach in
[
21
]
.
The conditional density of
\boldsymbol{D}=(\boldsymbol{Z}_{1},Y_{1})
, given
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}
, is

f(\boldsymbol{D};\boldsymbol{\theta})=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-1/2}\exp\left(-{(Y_{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{Z}_{1})^{2}}/{(2\sigma^{2})}\right)
. The Fisher information matrix for
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\partial\log f(\boldsymbol{D};\boldsymbol{\Theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)^{\prime}\left(\frac{\partial\log f(\boldsymbol{D};\boldsymbol{\Theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\right]=\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}^{\prime})\epsilon_{1}^{2}\right].

In particular,
\text{trace}(\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}\|^{2}]/\sigma^{2}
. Further, the information for the prior
{\rho}_{d}(\cdot)
in (
12
) is

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{{\rho}_{d}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\partial\log\rho_{d}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}(\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}\|)}{\tilde{\rho}(\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}\|)}-\frac{d-1}{\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}\|}\right)^{2}\right].

Since
\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}\|\geqslant 2^{-1}
and
\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}\|
has the Lebesgue density
\tilde{\rho}(\cdot)
, we have
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\rho_{d}}\leqslant Cd^{2}
. Finally, let
\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{\theta}/\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|
for
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(1/2,1)
. Then for
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(1/2,1)
,

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}=\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|}-\frac{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{3}}\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}=\frac{d-1}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|}\geqslant d-1.

Now by
[
21
, Theorem 1]
with
B(\cdot)=C(\cdot)=\mathbb{I}_{d}
, and since there always exists a non-random Bayes rule, we have

\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\|^{2}]\geqslant{(d-1)^{2}}/{(n\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}\|^{2}]/\sigma^{2}+Cd^{2})}
.
∎

Remark 5
.

The random variable
\xi
in the above theorem is used to model additional information that is independent from data.

E.3
About log-concave densities

Let
p:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
be a probability density function with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say
p
is log-concave if
\log(p):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[-\infty,\infty)
is concave, and is isotropic if
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Z}]=\boldsymbol{0}_{d}
and
\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{Z})=\mathbb{I}_{d}
for a random vector
\boldsymbol{Z}
with the density
p
. We consider upper semi-continuous log-concave densities, just to fix a particular version.
In the main text, we apply the following lemmas for
m=1
or
2
.

Lemma E.3
.

Let
m\geqslant 1
be an integer. There exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
m
, such that for any isotropic, log-concave densities
p
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, (i)
\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}p(\boldsymbol{x})\leqslant C
; (ii)
p(\boldsymbol{x})\geqslant C^{-1}
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\leqslant C^{-1}
; (iii)
p(\boldsymbol{x})\leqslant C\exp(-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|/C)
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Proof.

For (i) and (ii), see
Lovász and
Vempala 2007
. We focus on (iii) for
m\geqslant 2
, and note that the
m=1
case follows from the same argument. Let
\boldsymbol{Z}
be an
m
-dimensional random vector with an
arbitrary
isotropic, log-concave densities
p
.

Fix any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{S}^{m-1}
. Let
\mathbb{U}=[\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{u}_{m-1}]
be an
m
-by-
(m-1)
matrix such that each column has length
1
and is orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{v}
, and columns are orthogonal to each other, i.e.,
\mathbb{U}^{\prime}\mathbb{U}=\mathbb{I}_{m-1}
and
\mathbb{U}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}_{m-1}
.

Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{Z}}=\mathbb{U}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{Z}
, and denote by
\tilde{p}
its density.
Then
\tilde{p}
is a log-concave density on
\mathbb{R}^{m-1}

[see
49
, Proposition 2.5]
. By definition,
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{Z}}]=\boldsymbol{0}_{m-1}
and
\text{Cov}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{Z}})=\mathbb{I}_{m-1}
; thus,
\tilde{p}
is isotropic.

By part (i) and (ii), there exists a constant
C_{1}>1
, depending only on
m
, such that

\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{0}_{m-1})\leqslant C_{1}
,
C_{1}^{-1}\leqslant p(\boldsymbol{0}_{m})\leqslant C_{1}
. Further, by a change-of-variable, for any
r>0
,

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{0}_{m-1})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(\tau\boldsymbol{v})d\tau\geqslant r\inf_{\tau\in[0,r]}p(\tau\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle r\inf_{\tau\in[0,r]}p(r\boldsymbol{v})^{\tau/r}p(\boldsymbol{0}_{m})^{1-\tau/r}\geqslant r\min\{p(\boldsymbol{0}_{m}),p(r\boldsymbol{v})\},

where the second to the last inequality is due to the log-concavity of
p
.
Thus for
r^{*}=2C_{1}^{2}
,
p(r^{*}\boldsymbol{v})\leqslant 1/(2C_{1})\leqslant p(\boldsymbol{0}_{m})/2
. Then again due to the log-concavity of
p
, for any
r>r^{*}
,

\displaystyle p(r^{*}\boldsymbol{v})\geqslant p(r\boldsymbol{v})^{r^{*}/r}p(\boldsymbol{0}_{m})^{1-r^{*}/r}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;p(r\boldsymbol{v})\leqslant p(\boldsymbol{0}_{m})(1/2)^{r/r^{*}}.

Since
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
is arbitrary, we have
p(\boldsymbol{x})\leqslant C_{1}2^{-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|/r^{*}}
for
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|>r^{*}
. Since
p
is also arbitrary, increasing
C
if necessary, the proof is complete.
∎

Next, we consider projections of “high-dimensional" log-concave random vectors onto low dimensional spaces.

Lemma E.4
.

Let
L>1
be a real number.
Let
d\geqslant 2
be an integer, and
\boldsymbol{Z}
an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
random vector with a log-concave density and the property that

\|\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Z}]\|\leqslant L
and the eigenvalues of
\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{Z})
are between
[L^{-1},L]
. Let
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
be two unit vectors such that
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{w}=0
. Denote by
p_{\boldsymbol{u}}
the density of
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{Z}
, and by
p_{\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}}
the joint density of
(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{Z})
.
There exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
L
(in particular, not on
d
), such that

(i)

p_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\tau)\leqslant Ce^{-|\tau|/C}
for
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
;

(ii)

p_{\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\leqslant Ce^{-\sqrt{\tau_{1}^{2}+\tau_{2}^{2}}/C}
for
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
;

(iii)

if, in addition,
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Z}]=0
, then
p_{\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\geqslant C^{-1}
if
\max\{|\tau_{1}|,|\tau_{2}|\}\leqslant C^{-1}
.

Proof.

By
Samworth 2018
,
p_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
p_{\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}}
are log-concave densities on
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
respectively. Further, let
U=\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{Z}
and
W=\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{Z}
. Then
U
has density
p_{\boldsymbol{u}}
, and
(U,W)
has the joint density
p_{\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}}
.

Since
\|\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Z}]\|\leqslant L
(resp.
=0
),
\max\{|\mathbb{E}[U]|,|\mathbb{E}[V]|\}\leqslant L
(resp.
=0
). Further, since the eigenvalues of
\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{Z})
are between
[L^{-1},L]
,
\text{Var}(U)
and the eigenvalues of
\text{Cov}(U,W)
are between
[L^{-1},L]
. Then the proof is complete due to Lemma
E.3
and change-of-variables.
∎

E.4
Elementary lemmas

Lemma E.5
.

For any
a\geqslant 9
and
b>0
, if
t\geqslant a+2b\log(a+b)
, then
a+b\log(t)\leqslant t
.

Proof.

Define
t_{0}=a+2b\log(a+b)
, and
f(t)=t-a-b\log(t)
. Since
f^{\prime}(t)=1-b/t
and
f^{\prime}(t_{0})>0
, it suffices to show that
f(t_{0})\geqslant 0
. Note that
f(t_{0})=2b\log(a+b)-b\log(a+2b\log(a+b))\geqslant 2b\log(a+b)-\max\{b\log(2a),b\log(4b\log(a+b))\}
. Since
a\geqslant 9
, we have
(a+b)^{2}\geqslant\max\{2a,4b\log(a+b)\}
, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma E.6
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}_{d}\}
. Then
\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}/{\|\boldsymbol{u}\|}-{\boldsymbol{v}}/{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|}\right\|\leqslant{2\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\|}/{\|\boldsymbol{u}\|}
.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\boldsymbol{u}}{\|\boldsymbol{u}\|}-\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|}\right\|\leqslant\left\|\frac{\boldsymbol{u}}{\|\boldsymbol{u}\|}-\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{\|\boldsymbol{u}\|}\right\|+\left\|\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{\|\boldsymbol{u}\|}-\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|}\right\|\leqslant\frac{\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{u}\|}+\frac{|\|\boldsymbol{u}\|-\|\boldsymbol{v}\||}{\|\boldsymbol{u}\|}.

Then the proof is complete by another application of the triangle inequality.
∎

Lemma E.7
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
be an integer, and
C,m_{Z}>0
.
Let
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a random vector that has a Lebesgue density
p
such that
\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(\boldsymbol{z})\leqslant C
. Further, assume
\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|\leqslant m_{Z}
. Then there exists a constant
\tilde{C}>0
, depending only on
d,C,m_{Z}
, such that the Lebesgue density of
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{Z}
is bounded by
\tilde{C}
for any
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
.

Proof.

Fix
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. There exist
\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{u}_{d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathbb{U}=[\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{u}_{2};\ldots;\boldsymbol{u}_{d}]

is an orthonormal matrix. Then the density of
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{Z}
is: for
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,

f_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\tau)=\int_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}p(\mathbb{U}^{-1}[\tau,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}]^{\prime})d\boldsymbol{x}.

Since
\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|\leqslant m_{Z}
and
p(\cdot)\leqslant C
, we have

f_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\tau)\leqslant\int_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{B}_{d-1}(m_{Z})}p(\mathbb{U}^{-1}[\tau,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}]^{\prime})d\boldsymbol{x}\leqslant C\text{Vol}(\mathcal{B}_{d-1}(m_{Z})),

where
\mathcal{B}_{d-1}(r)=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\leqslant r\}
is the Euclidean ball with radius
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, and

\text{Vol}(\mathcal{B}_{d-1}(r))
is its the Lebesgue volume. Since the upper bound does not depend on
\boldsymbol{u}
, the proof is complete.
∎
