A hydrodynamical model of nematic liquid crystal films with a general state of orientational order
Lucas Bouck, Ricardo H. Nochetto, Vladimir Yushutin

Abstract
We develop a Q-tensor model of nematic liquid crystals occupying a stationary surface which represents a fluidic material film in space. In addition to the evolution due to Landau–de Gennes energy the model includes a tangent viscous incompressible flow along the surface. A thermodynamically consistent coupling of a two-dimensional flow and a three-dimensional Q-tensor dynamics is derived from the generalized Onsager principle following the Beris–Edwards system known in the flat case. The main novelty of the model is that it allows for a flow of an arbitrarily oriented liquid crystal so the Q-tensor is not anchored to the tangent plane of the surface, and also obeys an energy law . Several numerical experiments explore kinematical and dynamical properties of the novel model.

Introduction

Modeling of materials with orientational order is a challenging task. There is a variety of approaches including particle theories as well as continuum director field and Q-tensor theories which may be customized by specifying the energy landscape to accommodate different types of phase transitions.

We aim to extend the Beris–Edwards model to stationary curved surfaces. For an example of a situation in which the model is relevant, one may think of a liquid crystal material confined between two parallel surfaces which may be far enough apart to fit normally oriented rod-like particles but are sufficiently close so Q-tensor states are constant along the thickness. The main challenge is thus to establish a thermodynamically consistent energy law for a curved surface while still having a generically oriented Q-tensor.

2.1.  External surface derivatives

Here we introduce some standard operators of
calculus on embedded surfaces

Although the concept is certainly not novel, in this paper we will call such operators

Consider a closed surface
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}=\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}\in{}C^{1}(\Omega_{\delta})^{n\times{}n}\,,\quad\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}\in{}C^{1}(\Omega_{\delta})^{n\times{}n}\,,\quad\mathbf{B}=\nabla\mathbf{n}\in{}C(\Omega_{\delta})^{n\times{}n}\,,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{\delta}

Consider a tensor (scalar, vector, matrix) field

Essentially, this condition prescribes a

We stress that the external derivative

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\nabla{}f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{e}_{j})\partial_{j}f\,,

\displaystyle\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=(\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{P}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{P}\mathbf{e}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{e}_{j}\otimes{}\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u}_{j}\,,

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{P}\mathbf{e}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{e}_{j}\otimes\nabla_{M}(\mathbf{A}^{T})_{j}\,,\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{\delta}\,.

\displaystyle=(\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{P}\mathbf{e}_{j})\partial_{j}\mathbf{u}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{v})\mathbf{e}_{j}\,,

2.2.  Covariant surface derivatives

Here we discuss covariant operators on the surface
\Gamma
, considered as an isometric embedding of a
C^{2}
Riemannian manifold, which are intrinsic in the sense that they only depend on the Riemannian structure and the surface values of the argument
if it belongs to the tangent plane
. Intuitively, the covariant derivative of a tangent object measures the tangent part of the change of the object in a tangent direction.

For each
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\Gamma
the subspace
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0})\}
is identified with the tangent space of the manifold at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. A linear operator
\mathbf{A}
is called
tangent
if the normal
\mathbf{n}
is in its kernel and its range belongs to the tangent plane, or
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}
.
Given a tangent vector
\mathbf{v}
at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
consider a regular curve
\gamma:(a,b)\rightarrow\Gamma
,
\gamma(t_{0})=\mathbf{x}_{0}
,
t_{0}\in(a,b)
such that
\gamma^{\prime}(t_{0})=\mathbf{v}
.
In the following we assume that all tangent planes of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are identified with itself as usual so the addition of tensors from different points is meaningful.
The tangent component of the variation along the curve defines the action of the covariant directional derivative:

\displaystyle(\nabla_{\Gamma}^{T}f(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\hskip 35.56593pt\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{0}}\frac{1}{t-t_{0}}\left(f(\gamma(t))-f(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right)

\displaystyle(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\left(\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{0}}\frac{1}{t-t_{0}}\left(\mathbf{u}(\gamma(t))-\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right)\right)

\displaystyle(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\left(\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{0}}\frac{1}{t-t_{0}}\left(\mathbf{A}(\gamma(t))-\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right)\right)\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0})

which, by extending the tensor fields normally and applying (
2.2
), can be shown to be equivalent to the following expressions for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma

\displaystyle(\nabla_{\Gamma}^{T}f)\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{M}^{T}f)\mathbf{v}\,,\qquad(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u})\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{P}(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u})\mathbf{v}

(2.8)

\displaystyle(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{A})\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{A})\mathbf{v}\mathbf{P}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{A}_{j})\mathbf{v}\otimes{}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{e}_{j}\,.

Note that
\nabla_{\Gamma}^{T}\!f
is a short notation for
(\nabla_{\Gamma}f)^{T}
.
Finally, we give the definition of the
covariant surface derivative
, which applies to fields that are not necessarily constant along the normal direction:

Definition 2.5

(covariant derivatives)
.

For a scalar field
f
, a vector field
\mathbf{u}
, and a matrix field
\mathbf{A}
on
\Omega_{\delta}
the covariant surface derivative is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Gamma}{}f

\displaystyle=\nabla_{M}{}f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{e}_{j})\partial_{j}f\,,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\partial_{j}\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{P}\mathbf{e}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{e}_{j}\otimes{}\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u}_{j}\,,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}(\partial_{j}\mathbf{A})\mathbf{P}\otimes\mathbf{P}\mathbf{e}_{j}\,.

In fact, it can be shown
[
25
]
that for points
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma
the covariant derivatives (
2.9
) of tensors extended from
\Gamma
are independent of the chosen extension.

The covariant divergence of a vector field
\mathbf{u}
and of
a matrix field
\mathbf{A}
is defined as:

\displaystyle{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle={\rm tr}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u})\,,\quad{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{A}=\left({\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{A}^{T})_{1},\,{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{A}^{T})_{2},\,{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{A}^{T})_{3}\right)^{T}\,,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{\delta}\,,

(2.10)

namely the divergence of
\mathbf{A}
is computed by rows.
However, because of the cyclic property of traces we have

\displaystyle{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}={\rm tr}(\mathbf{P}\nabla\mathbf{u}\mathbf{P})={\rm tr}(\nabla\mathbf{u}\mathbf{P})={\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{M}\mathbf{u}\,,\qquad{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{A}={\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{M}\mathbf{A}\,.

(2.11)

Definition 2.6

(covariant strain-rate and spin tensors)
.

For a given velocity field
\mathbf{v}
on
\Gamma
we define the
covariant strain-rate
and
covariant spin
tensors, respectively, as follows:

\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{v})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{v}+\nabla_{\Gamma}^{T}\mathbf{v})\,,\qquad\mathbf{W}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{v})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{v}-\nabla_{\Gamma}^{T}\mathbf{v}).

(2.12)

These tensors correspond to symmetric and antisymmetric tangent parts of the instant deformation of a tangent plane due to the flow
\mathbf{v}
. Essentially, formulas in (
2.12
) as well as the
\nabla_{\Gamma}
operator represent objects intrinsic to
\Gamma
which one may compute using the Riemannian structure only.

Finally we note the relation
of the
external
and covariant rates because of (
2.1
) and (
2.9
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}_{M}(\mathbf{v})\mathbf{P}\,,\qquad\mathbf{W}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{W}_{M}(\mathbf{v})\mathbf{P}\,.

(2.13)

3
Kinematics of Q-tensors on surfaces

In this section we discuss kinematic properties of the model of surface flows of liquid crystals developed in this paper. The kinematic properties are introduced by defining the dependence of the state variables, which are the momentum and the Q-tensor, on the prescribed deformation of the domain, caused by a vector field
\mathbf{v}
in the absence of any forces. The resulting operators, if set equal to zero, are called
passive transport
equations. For example, the passive transport of a scalar field
f
, e.g. the density, along a tangent flow
\mathbf{v}
on a surface
\Gamma
is usually given by

\displaystyle\dot{f}=\partial_{t}{}f+(\nabla_{\Gamma}^{T}f)\mathbf{v}=\partial_{t}{}f+(\nabla_{M}^{T}f)\mathbf{v}=0

(3.1)

which attaches scalar values to the flow
\mathbf{v}
.
Similarly, we will define a notion of surface transport of the linear momentum and a notion of surface transport of generically oriented Q-tensor fields based on the derivatives introduced in previous sections. While the former is well-established in the literature
[
25
]
, the latter is new. The definition of the Q-tensor passive transport will be motivated by kinematic assumptions with a clear physical meaning: a tangent eigenvector is embedded into a surface flow similar to the flat case in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a normal eigenvector has to stay normal along the flow.

3.1.  Momentum transport

We assume the density
\rho
is constant and often omitted it in this section for clarity. To express the
rate of change of the linear momentum field
\rho\mathbf{u}
in the ambient space, we need to use the Euclidean parallel transport equation (
2.1
) of a velocity field
\mathbf{u}
normally extended from
\Gamma
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u})\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(\mathbf{u}_{T}+u_{N}\mathbf{n})+(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u}_{T}+u_{N}\nabla_{M}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{n}\otimes\nabla_{M}{}u_{N})\mathbf{v}

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{T}+(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{n}(\partial_{t}{}u_{N}+(\nabla_{M}^{T}{}u_{N})\mathbf{v})+u_{N}(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{n})\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\left[\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{T}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{v}\right]+\mathbf{N}(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{v}+\dot{u}_{N}\mathbf{n}+u_{N}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\left[\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{T}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{v}\right]-(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{n}+\dot{u}_{N}\mathbf{n}+u_{N}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}\,,

where we split the velocity
\mathbf{u}
into the normal and tangent components as in (
B.4
), and used (
B.2
) and Proposition
A.1
.
We consider films which are stationary in space, whence the surface
\Gamma
does not evolve in time and the velocity
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{T}+u_{N}\mathbf{n}
is tangent to
\Gamma
, i.e.
u_{N}=0
. We express the transport of linear momentum by setting
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}_{T}
in (
3.2
).
What remains in (
3.2
) is the
tangential material acceleration

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{T}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{u}_{T}

(3.3)

and the normal centripetal acceleration
-\mathbf{u}_{T}\cdot\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_{T}
. Since we require
u_{N}=0
the centripetal acceleration has to be balanced by the reaction forces which enforce the constraint that the surface does not evolve. This suggests that the tangent part of the rate of change,
\mathbf{P}(\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u})=\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{T}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{u}_{T}
, should represent the passive transport of momentum in a surface model. This idea is summarized in the following assumption.

Assumption 1

(kinematics of momentum)
.

The passive transport of the linear momentum field
\rho\mathbf{u}=\rho\mathbf{u}_{T}
along the velocity
\mathbf{u}_{T}

is the parallel transport (
3.3
), i.e.:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{T}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{u}_{T}=0\,.

Based on this kinematic assumption on how the momentum
\rho\mathbf{u}
is transported in the absence of any forces we define the surface material derivative, which suits the Assumption
1
, as follows:

Definition 3.1
.

The
surface material derivative

\dot{\mathbf{u}}
of a vector field
\mathbf{u}:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
along a given tangent vector field
\mathbf{v}
is a vector field with the following normal and tangent components:

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}\dot{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\dot{u}_{N}\mathbf{n}\,,\quad\mathbf{P}\dot{\mathbf{u}}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{T}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{v}\,.

Remark 3.2
.

The passive transport
\dot{\mathbf{u}}=0
by the surface material derivative given in Definition
3.1
has the following properties. The normal component
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}=u_{N}
of the vector field
\mathbf{u}
is transported by (
3.1
) as a scalar field. Consequently, if at the initial moment of time the vector field
\mathbf{u}
is tangent, then it remains tangent along the passive flow by a vector field
\mathbf{v}
. The tangent component
\mathbf{u}_{T}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}
satisfies the
Riemannian parallel transport
equation

\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{T}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{v}=0.

Finally we would like to show that the passive transport of the velocity field along itself has the property of preserving the kinetic energy and the linear momentum in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We start with a property of the convective term, which is well known in flat domains.

Lemma 3.3

(vanishing of the convective term)
.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{T}
,
\mathbf{v}_{T}
,
\mathbf{w}_{T}
be tangent vector field on a closed surface
\Gamma
. Then
trilinear convective form satisfies

\displaystyle((\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{v}_{T})\mathbf{w}_{T},\mathbf{u}_{T})_{\Gamma}+((\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{w}_{T},\mathbf{v}_{T})_{\Gamma}=-(\mathbf{v}_{T}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{T},{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{T})_{\Gamma}\,.

and it vanishes provided
{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{T}=0
and
\mathbf{u}_{T}=\mathbf{v}_{T}
, namely

\displaystyle((\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{w}_{T},\mathbf{u}_{T})_{\Gamma}=0\,.

(3.4)

Proof.

Using Lemma
B.5
(covariant integration by parts) followed by (
2.11
) and Proposition
A.1
(product rules) for normally extended
\mathbf{u}^{e}_{T},\mathbf{v}^{e}_{T},\mathbf{w}^{e}_{T}
we deduce

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\big({\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}((\mathbf{v}_{T}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{w}_{T}),1\big)_{\Gamma}=\big((\mathbf{v}_{T}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{T}){\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{M}\mathbf{w}_{T}+\mathbf{w}_{T}\cdot\nabla_{M}(\mathbf{u}_{T}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{T}),1\big)_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\big((\mathbf{v}_{T}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{T}){\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{T}+\mathbf{u}_{T}\cdot(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{v}_{T})\mathbf{w}_{T}+\mathbf{v}_{T}\cdot(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{w}_{T},1\big)_{\Gamma}\,.

Invoking (
2.9
), namely
\mathbf{P}\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u}=\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}
, and reordering yields the assertion.
∎

Corollary 3.4

(preservation of kinetic energy and linear momentum)
.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{T}
be a tangent and incompressible velocity field that is passively transported, namely
\mathbf{u}
satisfies
{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T}=0
and
\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{T}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{u}_{T}=0
, for sufficiently smooth initial condition on a closed surface
\Gamma
. Then the kinetic energy
\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma}\rho\mathbf{u}_{T}^{2}
and the total linear momentum
\int_{\Gamma}{\rho}\mathbf{u}_{T}
are preserved over time.

Proof.

Taking into account that density
\rho
is constant we compute

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\rho}{2}\mathbf{u}_{T}^{2}=\rho\big(\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{T},\mathbf{u}_{T}\big)_{\Gamma}=-\rho\big((\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{u}_{T},\mathbf{u}_{T}\big)_{\Gamma}=0\,,

according to (
3.4
). To treat the vector of total linear momentum, we consider its
x
-component

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{\Gamma}{\rho}\mathbf{u}_{T}\cdot{}\mathbf{e}_{x}\right)=\rho\big(\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{T},\mathbf{e}_{x}\big)_{\Gamma}=-\rho\big((\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{u}_{T},\mathbf{e}_{x}\big)_{\Gamma}=-\rho\big(\nabla_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u}_{T}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{x}),\mathbf{u}_{T}\big)_{\Gamma}=0

where used Lemma
B.5
(covariant integration by parts) with
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{T}
and
f=\mathbf{u}_{T}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{x}
at the last step. Other components of
\mathbf{u}_{T}
are dealt with similarly.
This completes the proof.
∎

3.2.  Q-tensor transport

Although many objective rates are available for modeling of Q-tensor flows even in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we aim to choose one, the corotational derivative (
3.5
), and show how it should be adapted for the case of a fixed surface
\Gamma
resulting in Definition
3.7
.

Modeling flows of liquid crystal material in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
often involves the following objective rates of change
[
55
]
,
[
60
]

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}+(\nabla\mathbf{q})\mathbf{v}-\frac{(\nabla\mathbf{v}-\nabla^{T}\mathbf{v})}{2}\mathbf{q}\,,\quad\partial_{t}\mathbf{Q}+(\nabla\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{Q}\frac{\nabla\mathbf{v}-\nabla^{T}\mathbf{v}}{2}-\frac{\nabla\mathbf{v}-\nabla^{T}\mathbf{v}}{2}\mathbf{Q}

(3.5)

which are the
corotational derivatives
of a vector field
\mathbf{q}
and of a matrix field
\mathbf{Q}
along the flow
\mathbf{v}
. These corotational derivatives express the rate of change of tensors with respect to the (Lagrangian) coordinate system embedded in the fluid, and sometimes they should be chosen over the parallel Euclidean transport to model the physics adequately. For example, one uses the parallel Euclidean transport
\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+(\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{v}
to express the rate of change of the non-material momentum vector
\rho\mathbf{u}
; while if one works with the rate of change of the Q-tensor, which provides the statistical description of the liquid crystal orientation, the objective rate (
3.5
) should be used.
We refer to
[
44
, Section 10]
for discussion of different time derivatives for vectors and
Q
-tensors on surfaces.

Remark 3.5
.

A zero corotational derivative (
3.5
) of a vector field
\mathbf{q}
means that the vector field is embedded in the flow
\mathbf{v}

in the following kinematical sense
[
55
]
: it is transported parallelly in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by the flow
\mathbf{v}
and
, in addition,
is rotated along it by the spin tensor
\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathbf{v}-\nabla^{T}\mathbf{v})
.

To make this point concrete, we consider
{\bf X}(t)
to satisfy
\frac{d}{dt}{\bf X}(t)=v(t,{\bf X}(t))
. Computing the time derivative of
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}(t)=\mathbf{q}(t,{\bf X}(t))
yields

\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}+(\nabla\mathbf{q})\mathbf{v}=\frac{(\nabla\mathbf{v}-\nabla^{T}\mathbf{v})}{2}\mathbf{q}=\frac{(\nabla\mathbf{v}-\nabla^{T}\mathbf{v})}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}.

At the same time, a zero corotational derivative (
3.5
) of a Q-tensor field
\mathbf{Q}
means that its eigenframe is embedded in the flow
\mathbf{v}
: the eigenvectors are transported and rotated as vectors
by (
3.5
)
and the eigenvalues are transported as scalars by (
3.1
).

In this paper the flow
\mathbf{v}
is two-dimensional and is tangent to a surface
\Gamma
, but the Q-tensor is three-dimensional. This raises the question of what rate of change should be utilized to model the passive motion of Q-tensors along the surface flow.

It is natural to try the following transport equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{Q}+(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{Q}\frac{\nabla_{M}\mathbf{v}-\nabla_{M}^{T}\mathbf{v}}{2}-\frac{\nabla_{M}\mathbf{v}-\nabla_{M}^{T}\mathbf{v}}{2}\mathbf{Q}=0\,,

(3.6)

where all the derivatives correspond to Cartesian derivatives of the normal extensions of arguments. Unfortunately, the Q-tensor of type
\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}
is not in the kernel of the operator (
3.6
). In fact, using (
2.3
), (
2.2
) and Lemma
B.1
we compute

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{M}(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}))\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n})\frac{\nabla_{M}\mathbf{v}}{2}+\frac{\nabla_{M}^{T}\mathbf{v}}{2}(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{P}\mathbf{e}_{j})\partial_{j}(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{n}\otimes({\nabla_{M}^{T}\mathbf{v}})\mathbf{n}+(\nabla_{M}^{T}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}\right)

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{n})\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{n}+(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{n})\otimes\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{n}\otimes(-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v})+(-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v})\otimes\mathbf{n}\right)=\frac{1}{2}{}\left(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}\right)

whence
\mathbf{B}=0
or, equivalently, the surface has to be flat. We have just shown that transport of a
Q-tensor by (
3.6
) does not keep alignment with respect to the normal direction.
These considerations suggest that there are many ways to define the transport of a Q-tensor field and that we need to choose one based on some kinematic assumptions similar to Assumption
1
.
In fact, to transport a three-dimensional tensor we need a three dimensional spin tensor which cannot be provided by a two-dimensional surface flow.

At a point
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma
consider an orthonormal basis
\mathbf{t}_{1},\mathbf{t}_{2},\mathbf{n}
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
where the first two vectors belong to the tangent plane of
\Gamma
.
A general three-dimensional skew-symmetric
spin tensor

\mathbf{W}
can be represented in this local basis with the help of a tangent vector
\mathbf{w}_{T}=(w_{2}\mathbf{t}_{1}-w_{1}\mathbf{t}_{2})
and a tangent tensor
\mathbf{W}_{T}=w_{3}(\mathbf{t}_{2}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{1}-\mathbf{t}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{2})
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=w_{3}(\mathbf{t}_{2}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{1}-\mathbf{t}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{2})+w_{2}(\mathbf{t}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{1})+w_{1}(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{2}-\mathbf{t}_{2}\otimes\mathbf{n})

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{P}+(w_{2}\mathbf{t}_{1}-w_{1}\mathbf{t}_{2})\otimes\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{n}\otimes(w_{2}\mathbf{t}_{1}-w_{1}\mathbf{t}_{2})=\mathbf{W}_{T}+\mathbf{w}_{T}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{w}_{T}\,.

Motivated by the three-dimensional corotational derivatives (
3.6
) we are in position to determine the structure of the
surface corotational derivatives of Q-tensors
. Consider a vector field
\mathbf{v}
tangent to
\Gamma
, a matrix field
\mathbf{Q}
and its eigenvector field
\mathbf{q}
. A surface corotational derivative along the flow
\mathbf{v}
can be expected to be given by

\displaystyle\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}+(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{q})\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{q}\,,\qquad\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{Q}}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{Q}+(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Q})\,,

(3.8)

where the spin tensor
\mathbf{W}
is yet to be defined via
{\mathbf{w}_{T}}
and
{\mathbf{W}_{T}}
in (
3.7
).
For completeness, we define the corotational derivative of a scalar field by its material derivative (
3.1
),

\overset{\circ}{f}=\dot{f}.

We specify
\mathbf{W}_{T}
and
\mathbf{w}_{T}
in (
3.7
) by making the following assumption.

Assumption 2

(kinematics of Q-tensors)
.

The tangent vector
\mathbf{w}_{T}
and the tangent spin tensor
\mathbf{W}_{T}
are such that the normal eigenvector
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{n}
of a conforming Q-tensor is in the kernel of the passive transport operator (
3.8
) along a tangent flow
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}_{T}
. Also, the passive transport of a tangent eigenvector
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{t}
of a conforming Q-tensor field is a combination of the parallel transport (
3.3
) and the instant rotation by the covariant spin tensor (
2.12
):

\displaystyle\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{n}}=0\,,\qquad\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{t}}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{t}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{t})\mathbf{u}_{T}-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{T}-\nabla_{\Gamma}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{T})\mathbf{t}\,.

In view of Remark
3.5
,
\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{t}}=0
means that the tangent vector field
\mathbf{t}
is also embedded in the flow
\mathbf{u}_{T}
but this time in the sense of the Riemannian structure on
\Gamma
.
This rate of change
\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{t}}
of a tangent vector field
\mathbf{t}
is known as
surface Jaumann derivative

[
44
]
.
From this modelling assumption on how the eigenframe of a conforming Q-tensor is transported, we immediately find what should
\mathbf{w}_{T}
and
\mathbf{W}_{T}
be like.

Lemma 3.6

(characterization of spin tensor)
.

In order to satisfy Assumption
2
, the spin tensor
\mathbf{W}
in (
3.8
) should be given by (
3.7
) with

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}_{T}}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_{T}\,,\qquad{\mathbf{W}_{T}}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}_{T}}-\nabla_{\Gamma}^{T}{\mathbf{u}_{T}})

(3.9)

Proof.

Indeed, using the structure of (
3.8
) and the definition of the shape operator
\mathbf{B}=\nabla_{M}\mathbf{n}
we compute

\displaystyle\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\mathbf{n}+(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{n})\mathbf{u}_{T}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_{T}-\mathbf{w}_{T}

and, because
\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{n}}
should vanish, we have to have
\mathbf{w}_{T}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_{T}
in (
3.7
). Similarly, using Definition
3.1
, we compute

\displaystyle\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{t}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\mathbf{t}+(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{t})\mathbf{u}_{T}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{t}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{t}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{t})\mathbf{u}_{T}+\mathbf{N}(\nabla\mathbf{t})\mathbf{u}_{T}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=\dot{\mathbf{t}}+\mathbf{N}(\nabla\mathbf{t})\mathbf{u}_{T}-\mathbf{W}_{T}\mathbf{t}+(\mathbf{w}_{T}\cdot\mathbf{t})\mathbf{n}=\dot{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{W}_{T}\mathbf{t}-(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{B}\mathbf{t})\mathbf{u}_{T}+(\mathbf{w}_{T}\cdot\mathbf{t})\mathbf{n}=\dot{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{W}_{T}\mathbf{t}

which leads to the choice
\mathbf{W}_{T}=\mathbf{W}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u}_{T})
to suit Assumption
2
. Consequently, the spin tensor (
3.7
) can be expressed in terms of the covariant spin tensor
\mathbf{W}_{\Gamma}
from (
2.12
) or of the
external
spin tensor from
\mathbf{W}_{M}
(
2.5
) as follows (using (
B.3
) in the last equality)

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{\Gamma}({\mathbf{u}_{T}})+\mathbf{B}{\mathbf{u}_{T}}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{B}{\mathbf{u}_{T}}=\mathbf{W}_{\Gamma}({\mathbf{u}_{T}})+\mathbf{W}_{*}({\mathbf{u}_{T}})=\mathbf{W}_{M}({\mathbf{u}_{T}})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{W}_{*}({\mathbf{u}_{T}})

(3.10)

where the
star spin tensor

\mathbf{W}_{*}
is defined for a given velocity
\mathbf{u}_{T}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{*}({\mathbf{u}_{T}}):=\mathbf{B}{\mathbf{u}_{T}}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{B}{\mathbf{u}_{T}}\,.

(3.11)

This concludes the proof.
∎

Based on the Assumption
2
(kinematics of Q-tensors) on how the eigenframe of a conforming Q-tensor is transported in the absence of any forces we define the surface corotational derivative of
general
Q-tensors in the following definition.

Definition 3.7

(
external surface corotational
derivative
)
.

The
surface corotational derivatives

\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{q}}
of a vector field
\mathbf{q}:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{Q}}

Remark 3.12
.

Three cases appear in the statement because eigenvectors are not determined uniquely if some of the eigenvalues coincide. For example, in the case of two equal eigenvalues, one can discuss the transport of the corresponding planar eigenspace, but eigenvectors that form this eigenspace may undergo arbitrary deformations without affecting

Proof.

We start with the case of distinct eigenvalues. The spectral decomposition
\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{q}_{k}(\mathbf{x})\otimes\mathbf{q}_{k}(\mathbf{x}))
holds for all
\mathbf{x}\in U(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(\mathbf{q}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{k}\right)\dot{\lambda}_{k}+\lambda_{k}\left(\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{q}_{k}}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{k}+\mathbf{q}_{k}\otimes\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{q}_{k}}\right)

To show the necessity we contract the result with
\mathbf{q}_{i}
from the right and then with
\mathbf{q}_{j}
from the left:

\displaystyle\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{q}_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{q}}_{i}+\dot{\lambda}_{i}\mathbf{q}_{i}+\sum_{k=1,k\neq{}i}^{3}\lambda_{k}(\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{q}_{k}}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{i})\mathbf{q}_{k}

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{j}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{q}_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}\mathbf{q}_{j}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{q}}_{i}+\dot{\lambda}_{i}\mathbf{q}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{i}+\sum_{k=1,k\neq{}i}^{3}\lambda_{k}(\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{q}_{k}}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{i})(\mathbf{q}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{k})

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{i}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{q}_{i}

\displaystyle=\dot{\lambda}_{i}\,,\qquad{}\mathbf{q}_{j}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{q}_{i}=(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})\mathbf{q}_{j}\cdot\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{q}}_{i}

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{m}(\mathbf{q}_{m}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{m})+\lambda(\mathbf{q}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{i}+\mathbf{q}_{j}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{j})=\lambda_{m}(\mathbf{q}_{m}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{m})+\lambda(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{q}_{m}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{m})

Remark 3.13

The preceding surface corotational derivatives

from the surface

is described below.

\mathbf{N}
and the shape operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}

in the normal direction

\displaystyle=(v^{i})^{e}\mathbf{t}_{i}^{e}\,,\qquad{}\mathbf{v}^{r}=(1+d\kappa_{i}^{e})(v^{i})^{e}\mathbf{t}^{e}_{i}=\mathbf{v}^{e}+d\,\mathbf{B}^{e}\mathbf{v}^{e}

The principle is formulated without referring to dimensions of the system and its environment, and it appears to be suitable for modelling of embedded surfaces. We refer to

We briefly outline these abstract steps

Step 1:

Kinematics

\Gamma
to be the tangent momentum field
\rho\mathbf{u}
and the Q-tensor field
\mathbf{Q}

Step 3:

Evolution laws.

Reversible quantities

Step 5:

4.1.  Generalized Onsager principle

In this section we apply the generalized Onsager principle following the steps outlined above. After the last step the resulting system is simplified using Lemma

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}:=\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}+\mathbf{S}_{*}\,,\qquad{}\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}:=\mathbf{W}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})\,,\quad{}\mathbf{S}_{*}:=\mathbf{W}_{*}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}_{*}(\mathbf{u})\,.

(4.5)

This, together with the fact that
(\mathbf{u},(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u})_{\Gamma}=0
according to (
3.4
), yields the following expression for the rate of change of total energy

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{\rm{total}}[\mathbf{Q},\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{u}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\big(-\mathbf{H}:\partial_{t}\mathbf{Q}+\rho\mathbf{u}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}\big)

(4.6)

\displaystyle=-\left(\mathbf{H},\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{Q}}\right)_{\!\!\Gamma}+\bigg(\rho\mathbf{u},\dot{\mathbf{u}}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}+\bigg(\mathbf{H},(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{S}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{Q}]\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}.

Step 3: Evolution laws.

Following the classical Beris–Edwards model in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
we postulate that the surface model is driven by abstract evolution equations on the surface
\Gamma
with the kinematics derived from Assumptions
1
(kinematics of momentum) and
2
(kinematics of Q-tensors). Note that only the structure is postulated while the required new quantities (
\mathbf{a}^{d}_{T}
,
\mathbf{F}^{d}
,
\mathbf{a}^{r}
,
\mathbf{f}_{T}^{r}
) are yet-to-be-determined. We formulate these abstract evolution laws as follows.

Assumption 3

(evolution laws)
.

The thermodynamics of the surface Beris–Edwards model has both a dissipative and a Hamiltonian structure
[
61
]
. The dissipative structure is due to a symmetric tangent stress
\mathbf{a}^{d}_{T}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d}\mathbf{P}
and a symmetric tensor
\mathbf{F}^{d}
. The reversible Hamiltonian structure is due to a skew-symmetric stress
\mathbf{a}^{r}
and a tangent force
\mathbf{f}_{T}^{r}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{f}_{T}^{r}
. Motivated by the structure of the three-dimensional Beris–Edwards model, we propose the kinematic equations

\displaystyle\rho\dot{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{a}^{d}_{T}+\mathbf{a}^{r})+\mathbf{f}_{T}^{r}\,,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}^{d}\,;

the terms
\mathbf{f}_{T}^{r}
and
\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d}
in the surface momentum equation are assumed to be tangent to the surface
\Gamma
because the model
involves a two-dimensional viscous flow along
\Gamma
which is expected to be recovered upon setting
\mathbf{Q}=0
in (
4.7
); also, see Remark
4.6
.

Remark 4.1

(alternative definition of surface divergence and evolution laws)
.

As mentioned in Remark
2.3
, our definition of
{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}
follows that in differential geometry. However, suppose we posit the evolution laws to be

\displaystyle\rho\dot{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\widehat{{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}}(\mathbf{a}^{d}_{T}+\mathbf{a}^{r})+\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{T}^{r}\,,

\displaystyle\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}^{d}\,,

where
\widehat{{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}}
is defined as the
L^{2}(\Gamma)
adjoint of
\nabla_{M}
as motivated by the integration-by-parts formula in Lemma
B.7
(covariant integration by parts).
The procedure outlined in Steps 4 and 5 below would yield exactly the same surface Beris-Edwards system as (
4.13
) and (
4.14
). The resulting
\widehat{\bf f}_{T}^{r}
would be slightly simpler than
{\mathbf{f}}_{T}^{r}
because of using the
L^{2}(\Gamma)
adjoint
\widehat{{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}}
, but the right-hand sides of the equations for
\dot{\mathbf{u}}
would be identical.

Our next task is to combine Assumption
3
with (
4.6
).
We first invoke Lemma
B.7
, i.e.

\bigg(\mathbf{u},\rho\dot{\mathbf{u}}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}=-\bigg(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u},\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d}+\mathbf{a}^{r}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}+\bigg(({\rm tr}\mathbf{B})(\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d}+\mathbf{a}^{r})\mathbf{n},\mathbf{u}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}+\bigg(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{f}_{T}^{r}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}\,,

and take into account the symmetry and tangentiality of
\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d}\mathbf{P}
to write
\big(\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d},\nabla_{M}\mathbf{u}\big)_{\Gamma}=\big(\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d},\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})\big)_{\Gamma}
because of (
2.5
) and (
2.13
), as well as the skew-symmetry of
\mathbf{a}^{r}
and
(
2.5
) to obtain

\displaystyle\bigg(\mathbf{u},\rho\dot{\mathbf{u}}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}=\bigg(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{f}_{T}^{r}+({\rm tr}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{a}^{r}\mathbf{n}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}-\bigg(\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{a}^{d}_{T}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}-\bigg(\mathbf{W}_{M}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{a}^{r}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}\,.

(4.8)

Similarly, (
4.7
) yields

\left(\mathbf{H},\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{Q}}\right)_{\!\!\Gamma}=\bigg(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{F}^{d}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}\,,

and, in view of
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{S}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{Q}]=\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{u})
, we see that

\bigg(\mathbf{H},(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{S}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{Q}]\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}=\bigg(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{H}:\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}-\bigg(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{u})\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}\,.

We define the skew-symmetric
Ericksen
stress
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
and the
Leslie
force
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
to be

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{H}-\mathbf{H}\mathbf{Q},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=-\mathbf{H}:\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q};

(4.9)

the latter is tangent to
\Gamma
due to (
2.7
), whereas the former is unrelated to
\Gamma
. Consequently,

\bigg(\mathbf{H},(\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{S}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{Q}]\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}=-\bigg(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}+\bigg(\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{u})\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}\,.

Using (
3.10
) for
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{T}
tangential to
\Gamma
, namely
\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{W}_{M}(\mathbf{u})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{W}_{*}(\mathbf{u})
, we deduce

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{u})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:\mathbf{W}_{M}+\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{u}

because
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
and
\mathbf{W}_{*}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}
being antisymmetric yield

\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:\mathbf{W}_{*}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{n})={\rm tr}(\mathbf{n}\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\Sigma})={\rm tr}(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n})=\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{u}.

Hence the rate of change of the total energy
E_{\rm{total}}[\mathbf{Q},\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{u}]
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{\rm{total}}[\mathbf{Q},\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{u}]

\displaystyle=-\bigg(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{F}^{d}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}-\bigg(\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{a}^{d}_{T}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}

(4.10)

\displaystyle+\bigg(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{f}_{T}^{r}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda}+({\rm tr}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{a}^{r}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}-\bigg(\mathbf{W}_{M}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{a}^{r}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\bigg)_{\!\!\Gamma}\,,

where the dissipative and reversible terms are collected in separate lines.

Remark 4.2
.

Only the dissipative terms in the first line of (
4.10
) should contribute to the energy rate
\frac{d}{dt}E_{total}
as identified by Assumption
3
. Two reversible terms from the second line should cancel with each other for any possible dynamics of the system. From the perspective of theory of constitutive modeling, this gives rise to a plethora of possible models where the functional dependence of
\mathbf{a}^{r}
and
\mathbf{f}_{T}^{r}
on
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{Q}
varies even in the flat case. We will require in the Step 4 that each term in the second line vanishes separately. This modeling choice is consistent with the classical Beris–Edwards system in flat domains. One could try to attribute this choice to the principle of frame indifference, but it is beyond the scope of this paper.

Step 4: Reversible quantities.

To find the reversible quantities
\mathbf{a}^{r}
and
\mathbf{f}_{T}^{r}
we recall that these terms should not contribute to the time derivative of the total energy (
4.10
). Some of the reversible quantities in (
4.10
) are paired with the velocity
\mathbf{u}
which represents a uniform motion of an infinitesimal material volume while others are paired with
\mathbf{W}_{M}(\mathbf{u})
which represents a rotation of an infinitesimal volume.
As discussed in Remark
4.2
, none of these conjugated pairs should produce mechanical work, whence the last two terms in (
4.10
) vanish for any
\mathbf{u}
:

\mathbf{a}^{r}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\qquad\mathbf{f}_{T}^{r}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}-(\mathbf{B}+({\rm tr}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}\,.

(4.11)

Consequently, the reversible quantities in (
4.7
) are fully determined.

Step 5: Dissipative quantities.

To find the dissipative quantities
\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d}
and
\mathbf{F}^{d}
we make an additional assumption regarding the nonequilibrium thermodynamics of the model in the form of
the least action principle
(see
[
57
]
for details)
First, we define the dissipation functional

\displaystyle\Phi[\mathbf{F}^{d},\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d}]:=\int_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{|\mathbf{F}^{d}|^{2}}{2M}+\frac{|\mathbf{a}^{d}_{T}|^{2}}{4\mu}\right)

where the mobility
M
and the viscosity
\mu
are material constants. According to the least action principle, dissipative quantities should minimize the expression
\frac{d}{dt}E_{\rm{total}}+\Phi
at every time during the evolution to be thermodynamically consistent. Therefore, its first variation must vanish

\displaystyle\delta_{(\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d},\mathbf{F}^{d})}\left(\frac{d}{dt}E_{\rm{total}}[\mathbf{Q},\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{u}]+\Phi[\mathbf{F}^{d},\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d}]\right)=0\,.

In view of the first line of (
4.10
), we discover that the tensors
\mathbf{a}_{T}^{d}
and
\mathbf{F}^{d}

satisfy

-\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\mathbf{a}^{d}_{T}=0\,,\quad-\mathbf{H}+\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{F}^{d}=0\,.

(4.12)

Consequently, the dissipative quantities in (
4.7
) are fully determined.

We have just finished the five steps of the generalized Onsager principle and are now
ready to write the ensuing system of equations on
\Gamma
. Inserting (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) into (
4.7
) yields

\displaystyle\rho\dot{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=2\mu\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})+\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}-\big(\mathbf{B}+({\rm tr}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{P}\big)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}\,,

(4.13)

\displaystyle\overset{\circ}{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle=M\mathbf{H}\,,

(4.14)

which by construction enjoys the following energy structure.

Proposition 4.3

(energy law)
.

The system of equations (
4.13
)-(
4.14
) on the surface
\Gamma
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{\rm{total}}[\mathbf{Q},\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{u}]=-2\mu\|\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})\|_{\Gamma}^{2}-M\|\mathbf{H}\|_{\Gamma}^{2}\,.

(4.15)

Proof.

Simply replace (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) into (
4.10
).
∎

A further simplication of (
4.13
) is in order. We express the tangential force
\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
due to the Ericksen stress
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
defined in (
4.9
) in terms of the
tangent Ericksen stress

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}=\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}
and a remainder.
We resort to Lemma
B.6
to relate
{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
to
{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}+{\rm tr}(\mathbf{B})\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{T}\mathbf{n}\,.

(4.16)

We observe that the term
{\rm tr}(\mathbf{B})\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}
in (
4.16
) cancels with the last term in
(
4.13
), while the skew-symmetry
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{T}
of the Ericksen stress implies

\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{T}\mathbf{n}=2\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}
. We thus end up with the following reduced form of the momentum equation

\rho\dot{\mathbf{u}}=2\mu\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})+\mathbf{f}_{E}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}-\mathbf{f}_{*}\,,

(4.17)

with the
Ericksen force

\mathbf{f}_{E}
and
star force

\mathbf{f}_{*}
defined by

\mathbf{f}_{E}:=\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma},\qquad\mathbf{f}_{*}:=2\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}.

(4.18)

Both forces are tangent to
\Gamma
,
\mathbf{f}_{E}
due to the projection
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{f}_{*}
because the range of the shape operator
\mathbf{B}
is contained in the tangent plane at each point of
\Gamma
. It is worth realizing that thermodynamics consistency requires the presence of the novel force
\mathbf{f}_{*}
in (
4.17
). If the surface
\Gamma
is flat, e.g. a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then
\mathbf{B}=0
and
\mathbf{f}_{*}=0
. Moreover,
\mathbf{f}_{*}
vanishes again provided
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}=0
as it would happen if both
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{H}
are assumed to be conforming and flat-degenerate. The relaxation of these assumptions is the main contribution of this paper.

We further explore the extraction of the tangent part
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}
from
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
in (
4.16
), that leads to the Ericksen force
\mathbf{f}_{E}
of (
4.18
). In fact, we present a simple characterization of
\mathbf{f}_{E}
, which is of independent interest and quite useful to understand simulations in Section
6.2.2
.

Lemma 4.4

(characterization of the Ericksen force)
.

There exists a scalar function
\theta
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{E}=\mathbf{n}\times\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\,.

(4.19)

Proof.

Consider the right-handed basis of principal directions
\mathbf{t}_{1},\mathbf{t}_{2},\mathbf{n}
at a point
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma
. Any second order tensor
\mathbf{A}
may be represented in this basis via dyads as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\mathbf{t}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}(a_{3i}\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{i}+a_{i3}\mathbf{t}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{n})+a_{33}\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}

for some components
a_{ij}
,
1\leq i,j\leq 3
.
Since the surface Ericksen stress
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}=\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}
is tangent, its representation does not include any dyads involving
\mathbf{n}
. Moreover, skew-symmetry
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}=-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}^{T}
implies

a_{11}=a_{22}=0
and
a_{21}=-a_{12}=\theta
, where
\theta
is the only non-trivial component of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}
and is a function of
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma
. Consequently,

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}=\theta\left(\mathbf{t}_{2}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{1}-\mathbf{t}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{2}\right)\,,

and the second order tensor
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathbf{n}}:=\mathbf{t}_{2}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{1}-\mathbf{t}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{t}_{2}
maps
\mathbf{t}_{1}
to
\mathbf{t}_{2}
and
\mathbf{t}_{2}
to
-\mathbf{t}_{1}
. It is thus a rotation by
\frac{\pi}{2}
around the axis
\mathbf{n}
or, simply, the cross product operator
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathbf{n}}\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{a}
according to the corkscrew rule. It turns out that
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathbf{n}}
admits the following
cross product
matrix representation in terms of the canonical

Cartesian
basis
\mathbf{e}_{x},\mathbf{e}_{y},\mathbf{e}_{z}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}=\theta\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathbf{n}}=\theta\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathbf{n}_{z}&\mathbf{n}_{y}\\
\mathbf{n}_{z}&0&-\mathbf{n}_{x}\\
-\mathbf{n}_{y}&\mathbf{n}_{x}&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(4.20)

Note that the function
\theta
and the normal
\mathbf{n}
fully describe the surface Ericksen stress
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}
. Moreover, using (
2.11
), (
2.3
) together with Proposition
A.1
(product rules), we calculate

\displaystyle{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}={\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{M}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}={\mathop{\rm div}}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}^{e}={\mathop{\rm div}}\,(\theta^{e}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathbf{n}}^{e})=\theta^{e}{\mathop{\rm div}}\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{e}_{\mathbf{n}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{e}_{\mathbf{n}}\nabla\theta^{e}\,.

We finally observe that
{\mathop{\rm div}}\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{e}_{\mathbf{n}}=-\textrm{curl}\,\mathbf{n}^{e}=0
, because
\mathbf{n}^{e}=\nabla d
, to obtain that
{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}=\mathbf{n}\times\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta
and that
\mathbf{f}_{E}=\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}
is given by (
4.19
) as asserted.
∎

We point out that the orientation of
\mathbf{n}
is not unique. If we change
\mathbf{n}
to
-\mathbf{n}
, then we also have to exchange
\mathbf{t}_{1}
with
\mathbf{t}_{2}
to have a right-handed basis and this flips the sign of
\theta
; hence the representation of (
4.19
) is well defined. Moreover, since
{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}=\mathbf{n}\times\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta
is already tangent to
\Gamma
, we deduce that the projection
\mathbf{P}
in the definition
\mathbf{f}_{E}=\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}
is superfluous.

4.2.  Surface Beris–Edwards model

We are now in a position to present the novel model of fluidic liquid crystal films. Let a closed surface
\Gamma
represent the liquid crystal film. The liquid crystal may be generally oriented in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, but the material flows tangentially to
\Gamma
so that
\Gamma
does not change over time.
The incompressible flow is described by the tangential velocity
\mathbf{u}
, which is assumed to be divergence-free
{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}=0
. Therefore, the density
\rho
is constant and the scalar pressure field
p
enforces
{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}=0
on
\Gamma
.

The new model combines the equations
4.14
and (
4.17
) with the expressions (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) and the constitutive relations (
4.9
) and (
4.18
). Given initial conditions
\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{0}
, the model reads: find symmetric and traceless matrix fields
\mathbf{H},\mathbf{Q}
as well as tangent velocity
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}
and scalar pressure
p
on
\Gamma
such that for all times the following system of PDEs is satisfied on
\Gamma

\displaystyle{\bf H}+\mathcal{P}F^{\prime}[\mathbf{Q}]

\displaystyle=L\,\mathcal{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{M}\nabla_{M}{\mathbf{Q}}\,,

(4.21)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bf Q}+(\nabla_{M}{\bf Q}){\bf u}

\displaystyle=M{\bf H}+({\bf S}_{\Gamma}+{\bf S}_{*})\,,

\displaystyle\rho\big(\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}+\nabla_{\Gamma}p\big)

\displaystyle=2\mu\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})-(\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}+\mathbf{f}_{*})\,,

\displaystyle{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0\,,

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{u}]=\mathbf{W}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})\,,

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{S}_{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}_{*}[\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{u}]=\mathbf{W}_{*}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}_{*}(\mathbf{u})\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}[\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{H}]=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{H}-\mathbf{H}\mathbf{Q}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{H}]=\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{H}]=-\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}{\bf\Sigma}_{\Gamma}+\mathbf{H}:\nabla{}_{M}\mathbf{Q}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{f}_{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}_{*}[\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{H}]=2\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}\,.

Here
\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}
is the corotation (
4.5
) by the covariant spin tensor
\mathbf{W}_{\Gamma}
in (
2.12
),
\mathbf{S}_{*}
is the corotation (
4.5
) by the star spin tensor
\mathbf{W}_{*}
in (
3.11
),
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is the Ericksen stress (
4.9
) with the tangent part
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Gamma}=\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}
,
\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}
is the tangent
surface Beris–Edwards
force which consists of the Ericksen force
\mathbf{f}_{E}=\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}{\bf\Sigma}_{\Gamma}
in (
4.18
) and the Leslie force
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=-\mathbf{H}:\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q}
in (
4.9
), and
\mathbf{f}_{*}
is the star force
from

(
4.18
).
The first variation of the double-well potential
F[\mathbf{Q}]
in (
1.3
) is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}F^{\prime}[\mathbf{Q}]=a{\bf Q}-b{\bf Q}^{2}+\frac{b}{3}\text{tr}({\bf Q}^{2}){\bf I}+c\,{\rm tr}({\bf Q}^{2}){\bf Q}\,,

(4.22)

where
\mathcal{P}
is the projection onto the subspace of traceless and symmetric matrices. The operator
\mathcal{P}
acts likewise on the variation
{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{M}\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q}
of the elastic energy.
The surface Beris–Edwards model (
4.21
) obeys the energy law (
4.15
) by construction.

Remark 4.5
.

We would like to stress that the star corotation tensor
\mathbf{S}_{*}
and the star force
\mathbf{f}_{*}
distinguish our model from the model in
[
41
]
where
\mathbf{Q}
is assumed to be conforming to the surface with a prescribed eigenvalue in the normal direction. These terms guarantee the thermodynamical consistency of our model for a non-flat surface. We demonstrate the behavior of conforming and non-conforming Q-tensors in our numerical experiments of Section
6
.

Remark 4.6
.

If we disregard the Q-tensor equations and the coupling force
\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}+\mathbf{f}_{*}
we will be left with the well-known surface Navier–Stokes system
[
25
]
which models an incompressible surface flow driven by inertia.

5
Representation of Q-tensors on surfaces

In this section we define the notions of uniaxiality and flat-degeneracy, along with the biaxiality parameter
\beta[\mathbf{Q}]
which relates them. We also introduce the non-conformity parameter
r_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
. These parameters will be instrumental in describing and visualizing the numerical experiments in Section
6
.

5.1.  Biaxiality parameter

We start with a simple definition: unit vector fields
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
will be called
director
fields.

Definition 5.1

(flat-degeneracy)
.

A Q-tensor
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is
flat-degenerate
if one of its eigenvalues is zero, say
\lambda_{2}=0
whence
\lambda_{1}=-\lambda_{3}
. Therefore, if
r:=2\lambda_{1}
, then
\mathbf{Q}
reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}=\lambda_{1}\left(\mathbf{q}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{1}-\mathbf{q}_{3}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{3}\right)=r\left(\mathbf{q}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{q}_{2}}\right)\,,

(5.1)

where
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{q}}:=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{q}\otimes\mathbf{q}
is the projector onto the orthogonal plane to the director
\mathbf{q}
.

This definition is consistent with (
1.5
).
A flat-degenerate state of the form (
5.1
) is essentially a two-dimensional Q-tensor state in the plane orthogonal to
\mathbf{q}_{2}
. Another important class of three-dimensional Q-tensor states is given by the following definition
[
34
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
55
]
.

Definition 5.2

(unixiality)
.

A Q-tensor
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is
uniaxial
if it may be represented as

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}=s\left(\mathbf{q}\otimes\mathbf{q}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)

(5.2)

for some director
\mathbf{q}
and order parameter
s
. Otherwise the Q-tensor is
biaxial
.

The following biaxiality parameter
[
31
]
relates the notions of flat-degeneracy and uniaxiality of Q-tensors, and allows for a classification of Q-tensor fields useful in simulations.

Definition 5.3

(biaxiality parameter)
.

For a non-zero Q-tensor
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
the
biaxility parameter
is the real number

\displaystyle\beta[\mathbf{Q}]=1-6\frac{({\rm tr}{}\mathbf{Q}^{3})^{2}}{({\rm tr}{}\mathbf{Q}^{2})^{3}}\,.

(5.3)

It is well known that the vanishing of
\beta[\mathbf{Q}]
indicates that
\mathbf{Q}
is uniaxial
[
31
]
, but the opposite limit, when it is equal to one, indicates the flat-degeneracy of liquid crystal state as shown in the following Lemma.

Lemma 5.4

(properties of biaxiality parameter)
.

The biaxiality parameter satisfies

0\leq\beta[\mathbf{Q}]\leq 1\qquad\forall\,\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\,,\quad\mathbf{Q}\neq 0.

(5.4)

The minimal value,
\beta[\mathbf{Q}]=0
, corresponds to
\mathbf{Q}
being uniaxial whereas the maximal value,
\beta[\mathbf{Q}]=1
, corresponds to
\mathbf{Q}
being flat-degenerate.

Proof.

We exploit the spectral decomposition (
1.1
) to write
\mathbf{Q}^{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\lambda_{k}^{j}(\mathbf{q}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{k})
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
, whence
{\rm tr}\mathbf{Q}^{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\lambda_{k}^{j}
.
In view of the definition (
5.3
), we have to prove

0\leq 6\frac{\big({\rm tr}\mathbf{Q}^{3}\big)^{2}}{\big({\rm tr}\mathbf{Q}^{2}\big)^{3}}\leq 1.

The leftmost inequality is trivial. The rightmost one entails the following tedious computation.
Since
{\rm tr}\mathbf{Q}=0
we let
\lambda_{2}=-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{3}
and rewrite the desired traces in terms of
\alpha=\lambda_{1}^{-1}\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\mathbf{Q}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}^{2}\big(1+(1+\alpha)^{2}+\alpha^{2}\big)=2\lambda_{1}^{2}(1+\alpha+\alpha^{2}),

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\mathbf{Q}^{3}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}^{3}\big(1-(1+\alpha)^{3}+\alpha^{3}\big)=-3\lambda_{1}^{3}\alpha(1+\alpha),

because
\mathbf{Q}\neq 0
implies either
\lambda_{1}\neq 0
or
\lambda_{3}\neq 0
.
Consequently, we obtain the asserted inequality

\big({\rm tr}\mathbf{Q}^{2}\big)^{3}-6\big({\rm tr}\mathbf{Q}^{3}\big)^{2}=2\lambda_{1}^{6}\big(2+3\alpha-3\alpha^{2}-2\alpha^{3}\big)^{2}=2\lambda_{1}^{6}(1-\alpha)^{2}(2\alpha+1)^{2}(\alpha+2)^{2}\geq 0.

Moreover, this explicit expression reveals that
\beta[\mathbf{Q}]=0
is equivalent to either

\alpha=1,\alpha=-\frac{1}{2}
or
\alpha=-2
. This in turn corresponds to
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{3}
,

\lambda_{1}=-2\lambda_{3}
(i.e.
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}
) or
\lambda_{3}=-2\lambda_{1}
(i.e.
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}
). According to the characterization of a uniaxial Q-tensor after Definition
5.2
, we deduce that
\beta[\mathbf{Q}]=0
is equivalent to
\mathbf{Q}

being uniaxial.

In contrast,
\beta[\mathbf{Q}]=1
is equivalent to
{\rm tr}\mathbf{Q}^{3}=0
which reduces to either
\alpha=0
or

\alpha=-1
. This in turn reads either
\lambda_{3}=0
(or symmetrically
\lambda_{1}=0
) or
\lambda_{3}=-\lambda_{1}
(i.e.
\lambda_{2}=0
). According to Definition
5.1
, we infer
that
\beta[\mathbf{Q}]=1
if and only if
\mathbf{Q}
is flat degenerate. This completes the proof.
∎

Finally, the following result is proved in
[
31
, Proposition 1]
, but we state it as a lemma for further reference in Section
6
.

Lemma 5.5

(minimizer of
F
)
.

Let the parameters
a,b,c
of the double-well potential
F
in (
1.3
) satisfy

a<0\,,b>0\,,c>0
. Then
F[\mathbf{Q}]
is minimized by a uniaxial state (
5.2
) with
s
given by

\displaystyle s_{+}=\frac{b+\sqrt{b^{2}-24ac}}{4c}\,.

(5.5)

5.2.  Non-conformity parameter

In this section we turn to the representation of Q-tensors on surfaces and introduce a scalar field which quantifies the notion of non-conformity of Q-tensors. In fact, we extend the representation of conforming Q-tensors of
[
39
, equation (5)]
to arbitrary
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
.

Strictly speaking, since we are concerned with general Q-tensors there is no a priori relation between the surface
\Gamma
and the eigenframe of
\mathbf{Q}
. Nevertheless, we would like to split the general state of the Q-tensor into liquid crystal states related to the normal and tangent subspaces to
\Gamma
.

Given the unit normal
\mathbf{n}
to
\Gamma
and a tangent director field
\mathbf{q}_{\Gamma}
, i.e.
\mathbf{q}_{\Gamma}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
, we consider sums
\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}+\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}
of arbitrary traceless matrices of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}=n_{\Gamma}\left(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{P}\right)=\frac{3}{2}n_{\Gamma}\left(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\,,\quad{}\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}=s_{\Gamma}\left(\mathbf{q}_{\Gamma}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{\Gamma}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{P}\right)\,,

(5.6)

where
\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}
is an uniaxial homeotropic Q-tensor (normal to
\Gamma
), and
\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}
is a flat-degenerate Q-tensor tangent to
\Gamma
.
Note that
\beta[\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}]=0
and
\beta[\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}]=1
and that both
\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}
and
\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}
are always conforming.
Clearly, an arbitrary Q-tensor
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
cannot be represented by such sums if its eigenframe does not include the normal vector. Therefore, for
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
we define its traceless conforming (normal and tangential) components
\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
and
\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
by minimizing the residual with respect to
n_{\Gamma}
and
\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}
:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{n_{\Gamma},\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}}|\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}{}|^{2}\,,\qquad\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}{}=\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}-\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}\,.

(5.7)

Lemma 5.6

(homeotropic decomposition of
\mathbf{Q}
on
\Gamma
)
.

An arbitrary Q-tensor
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
admits the orthogonal decomposition
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]+\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]+\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
into three traceless symmetric tensors, where
\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
is a uniaxial
Q
-tensor given by (
5.6
) with

n_{\Gamma}=\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}
,
\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
is a flat-degenerate tangent Q-tensor, and
\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]

has minimal Frobenius norm. Moreover, they satisfy

|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}=|\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]|^{2}+|\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]|^{2}+|\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]|^{2}
and are given by the expressions

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]

\displaystyle=n_{\Gamma}\left(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{P}\right)\,,\quad\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}+\frac{n_{\Gamma}}{2}\mathbf{P}\,,

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{Q}-n_{\Gamma}\mathbf{I})\mathbf{n}\otimes{}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{n}\otimes{}(\mathbf{Q}-n_{\Gamma}\mathbf{I})\mathbf{n}\,.

Proof.

We expand the residual

\displaystyle|\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}{}|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}-n_{\Gamma}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T}-\frac{n_{\Gamma}}{2}\mathbf{P}):(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}-n_{\Gamma}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T}-\frac{n_{\Gamma}}{2}\mathbf{P})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{Q}-2\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}+\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}:\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}-2n_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}):\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T}-n_{\Gamma}\mathbf{P}:(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma})+\frac{3}{2}n_{\Gamma}^{2}\,,

and compute its first variations. Since
n_{\Gamma}
is scalar, we readily have

0=\frac{\partial}{\partial{}n_{\Gamma}}|\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}{}|^{2}=-2\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T}+{\rm tr}{}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma})+3n_{\Gamma}\,.

On the other hand, since
\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}
is traceless and tangent to
\Gamma
, a general variation of
\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}
reads
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{P}-\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}\big(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{P}\big)\mathbf{P}
for an arbitrary symmetric matrix
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
. Consequently, a tedious computation of
\frac{\partial}{\partial{}\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}}|\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}{}|^{2}:\mathbf{C}
using that
{\rm tr}\mathbf{P}=2
yields

0=\frac{\partial}{\partial{}\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}}|\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}{}|^{2}=-2\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}-{\rm tr}{(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P})}\mathbf{P}+2\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}\,.

Exploiting that
{\rm tr}(\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma})=0
, these equations give the optimal values

n_{\Gamma}=\frac{2}{3}\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}-\frac{1}{3}{\rm tr}{}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P})\,,\qquad\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}-\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}{(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P})}\mathbf{P}\,.

Since

\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}=\big(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}\big)\mathbf{Q}\big(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}\big)=\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}+\big(\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}\big)\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}\,,

and
\mathbf{Q}
is traceless, we deduce
{\rm tr}\big(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}\big)=-\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}

whence
n_{\Gamma}=\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}
and the expressions for
\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}
and
\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}
in (
5.8
) follow immediately. Moreover,
\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
and
\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
are orthogonal because

\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]:\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]=n_{\Gamma}\Big(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{P}\Big):\Big(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}+\frac{n_{\Gamma}}{2}\mathbf{P}\Big)=\frac{n_{\Gamma}}{2}\big(\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}-n_{\Gamma}\big)=0\,,

whence the minimization property (
5.7
) is equivalent to the orthogonality of
\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
and
\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]+\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Now we are in a position to introduce a quantitative measure of non-conformity for an arbitrary Q-tensor
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Since both
\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
and
\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
are conforming to
\Gamma
, possible non-conformity of
\mathbf{Q}
is dictated by the solution
\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
of the minimization problem (
5.7
). The relation
|\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]|\leq|\mathbf{Q}|
motivates the forthcoming definition.

Definition 5.7

(non-conformity parameter)
.

The
non-conformity parameter

r_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
of an arbitrary
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
on
\Gamma
is the fraction
0\leq r_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]\leq 1
defined by

\displaystyle r_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]:=\frac{|\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}{}[\mathbf{Q}]|}{|\mathbf{Q}|}\,.

(5.9)

Remark 5.8
.

We see that
r_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]=0
if and only if
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]+\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]
or equivalently
\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]=0
. In contrast,
r_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]=1
if and only if
\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]=\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]=0
or equivalently
n_{\Gamma}=0
and

\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{R}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}\,.

Therefore, if
r_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]=1
we infer that
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}
is tangent to
\Gamma
because
n_{\Gamma}=\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}=0
and
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{q}
is perpendicular to
\Gamma
for any tangent vector
\mathbf{q}
because
0=\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{q}
.

Remark 5.9
.

If
\mathbf{Q}
is conforming to
\Gamma
, i.e.
r_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]=0
, and its normal component
\mathbf{N}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]=0
, then
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{T}_{\Gamma}[\mathbf{Q}]

is flat degenerate. Therefore, according to Lemma
5.4
,
\mathbf{Q}
is biaxial and the biaxiality parameter

\beta[\mathbf{Q}]=1
is maximal.

5.3.  Enforcing Conformity: The Hess-Osipov Energy

We consider enforcing conformity through penalization. Natural penalizations are the following physically justified energies, which can be found in
[
39
, Eq. (4)]
and
[
19
, Eq. (8)]
, and are closely tied to the energies found in
[
18
, Eq. (4)]
and
[
50
, Eq. (7)]
:

\displaystyle E_{pen}[\mathbf{Q}]:=\gamma\int_{\Gamma}|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}|^{2},\quad E_{norm}[\mathbf{Q}]:=\alpha\int_{\Gamma}\big|\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}-\delta\big|^{2}.

(5.10)

The energy
E_{pen}[\mathbf{Q}]
with penalty parameter
\gamma
weakly enforces that
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}
be normal. In fact,
in the limit
\gamma\rightarrow\infty
, the energy
E_{pen}[\mathbf{Q}]
is minimized provided
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}=0
or equivalently if
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}
is normal. This is the strong form of conformity according to (
1.4
).

On the other hand, the energy
E_{norm}[\mathbf{Q}]
with penalty parameter
\alpha>0
enforces a value
\delta
of orientational order in the normal direction. For conforming Q-tensors the value
n_{\Gamma}=\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}
is the eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Q}
in the normal direction
\mathbf{n}
according to Lemma
5.6
(homeotropic decomposition of
\mathbf{Q}
on
\Gamma
). However, unless
\mathbf{n}
is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}
, the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
only penalizes the deviation of
\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}
from
\delta
, which may vary along
\Gamma
.

The role of (
5.10
) will be computationally explored in Section
6.3.1
and Section
6.3.2
.

6.3.1. Normal anchoring penalization

As discussed in Section
5.3
,
the Landau–de Gennes energy (
4.1
) of a liquid crystal film may include, in some applications
[
18
,
19
,
39
,
50
]
, the energy
E_{norm}[\mathbf{Q}]
with penalty parameter
\alpha>0
defined in (
5.10
), which enforces a value
\delta
of orientational order in the normal direction

\displaystyle E_{norm}[\mathbf{Q}]=\alpha\int_{\Gamma}\big|\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}-\delta\big|^{2}\,.

(6.17)

The dynamics of the Beris–Edwards system is dictated by the competition of several energies: the double-well potential
F[\mathbf{Q}]
promotes uniaxial states with order parameter
s_{+}
; the elastic energy
L|\nabla_{M}\mathbf{Q}|^{2}/2
promotes uniform states in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
; and the energy
E_{norm}[\mathbf{Q}]
promotes a certain degree of orientational order, but does not affect the conformity.
A rigid condition of the form
\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}=c
, as discussed in Section
1
, is often a modelling assumption postulated along with the conformity assumption. If this condition is relaxed but the conformity assumption is still applied, then
\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}
is an additional scalar variable representing the normal orientational order. One could model a transition from the conforming flat-degenerate state (
6.15
) with
\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}=0
to the conforming uniaxial state with
\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}=\delta
enforcing conformity of
\mathbf{Q}
for all intermediate times. However, our simulations show that our Beris-Edwards model find non-conforming intermediate states more energetically favorable.

We simulate the full surface Beris–Edwards system (
4.21
) with initial conditions
\mathbf{u}(0)=0
and
\mathbf{Q}(0)=\mathbf{Q}_{0}
given by (
6.15
), as well as the augmented Landau–de Gennes energy (
4.1
) by (
6.17
). This leads to the following variant of (
4.21
)a

{\bf H}+\mathcal{P}(F^{\prime}[\mathbf{Q}]+E_{norm}^{\prime}[\mathbf{Q}])=L\,\mathcal{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{M}\nabla_{M}{\mathbf{Q}}\,,

(6.18)

where
E_{norm}^{\prime}[\mathbf{Q}]
is the variational derivative of
E_{norm}
. We choose the parameters

a=-1,\,b=1,\,c=1;\quad M=1,\,L=1,\,\rho=0.1,\,\mu=0.1;\quad\alpha=10,\,\delta=\frac{2}{3}s_{+}=1.0

in (
1.3
), (
4.21
) and (
6.17
) respectively. We report on Figure
10
the numerical results for time evolution of the augmented surface Beris–Edwards system (

)–(
6.18
). The Q-tensor relaxes from the flat-degenerate state with
\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}=0
to the uniaxial state with
\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{n}=\frac{2}{3}s_{+}
passing through non-conforming states. The biaxiality parameter
\beta[\mathbf{Q}]
is uniform at the beginning and end of the simulation, with values
\beta[\mathbf{Q}]=1
(biaxial) to

(uniaxial) respectively, and exhibits alternating and space-dependent values in between. The energy landscape is complex with non-conforming intermediate states.

7
Conclusions

This paper derives and explores a novel model of liquid crystal films with general orientational order.
For a given smooth, stationary and closed surface
\Gamma
, the main contributions are:

•

Non-conforming Q-tensors
: We develop a new notion of Q-tensor kinematics on surfaces, which hinges on Assumption
1
. We introduce the surface corotational derivative of Q-tensors (
3.13
) to transport a generically oriented Q-tensor field. This allows for transport of Q-tensors such that the unit normal vector
\mathbf{n}
to
\Gamma
is not an eigenvector (non-conformity). In this vein, Assumption
2
dictates how the eigenframe of a conforming Q-tensor is transported.

•

Energy law
: We invoke the generalized Onsager principle to derive a model with an energy structure that mimics the Beris–Edwards model in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We impose Assumption
3
to define the structure of the evolution laws. The derivation employs extrinsic calculus in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, thereby avoiding surface parametrizations and making finite element discretizations in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
readily available. The surface model contains three distinct forces: the Leslie force
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
and
Ericksen force
\mathbf{f}_{E}
, which already exist in flat domains, as well as the new star force
\mathbf{f}_{*}
which is responsible for thermodynamics consistency for non-conforming Q-tensors.

Simulations
: We conduct a systematic computational study of the surface Beris–Edwards model to unravel the role of several forces and mechanisms. This includes

–

experiments with the “dry” surface Landau–de Gennes (gradient flow dynamics without linear momentum) to examine the novel Q-tensor kinematics;

–

experiments that illustrate the role of the three forces
\boldsymbol{\Lambda},\mathbf{f}_{E}
and
\mathbf{f}_{*}
and their profiles for a Q-tensor configuration with a degree
+1
defect;

–

experiments of the dynamics connecting two confoming states which undergo more energetically favorable non-conforming intermediate states;

–

conforming dynamics of four
+\frac{1}{2}
defects enforced via penalization that lead to a regular tetrahedral structure consistent with
[
41
]
.

–

simulations of the instability of a radially symmetric, homeotropic Q-tensor on a unit sphere due to insufficient anchoring, which showcases the effect of non-conformity.

The relaxation of the conformity assumption, via a thermodynamically consistent model, and computational exploration of its consequences are the main novelties of our work.
