Martingale representation for degenerate diffusions
A. S. Üstünel

Abstract




Abstract:
Let
(W,H,\mu)
be the classical Wiener space
on
{\rm I\!R}^{d}
. Assume that
X=(X_{t})
is a diffusion process satisfying
the stochastic differential equation
dX_{t}=\sigma(t,X)dB_{t}+b(t,X)dt
, where

\sigma:[0,1]\times C([0,1],{\rm I\!R}^{n})\to{\rm I\!R}^{n}\otimes{\rm I\!R}^{d}
,

b:[0,1]\times C([0,1],{\rm I\!R}^{n})\to{\rm I\!R}^{n}
,
B
is an
{\rm I\!R}^{d}
-valued
Brownian motion. We suppose that the weak
uniqueness of this equation holds for any initial condition. We
prove that any square integrable martingale
M
w.r.t. to the filtration

({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X),t\in[0,1])
can be represented as

M_{t}=E[M_{0}]+\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}(X)\alpha_{s}(X).dB_{s}

where
\alpha(X)
is an
{\rm I\!R}^{d}
-valued process adapted to

({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X),t\in[0,1])
, satisfying

E\int_{0}^{t}(a(X_{s})\alpha_{s}(X),\alpha_{s}(X))ds<\infty
,
a=\sigma^{\star}\sigma

and
P_{s}(X)
denotes a measurable version of the orthogonal projection
from
{\rm I\!R}^{d}
to
\sigma(X_{s})^{\star}({\rm I\!R}^{n})
. In particular, for any
h\in H
, we have

(0.1)

E[\rho(\delta h)|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)]=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}(P_{s}(X)\dot{h}_{s},dB_{s})-\frac{1}{2}\>\int_{0}^{1}|P_{s}(X)\dot{h}_{s}|^{2}ds\right)\,,

where
\rho(\delta h)=\exp(\int_{0}^{1}(\dot{h}_{s},dB_{s})-\frac{1}{2}\>|H|_{H}^{2})
. In the case the process
X
is adapted to the Brownian
filtration, this result gives a new development as an
infinite series of the
L^{2}
-functionals of the degenerate
diffusions. We also give an adequate notion of “innovation
process” associated to a degenerate diffusion which corresponds to
the strong solution when the Brownian motion is replaced by an adapted
perturbation of identity. This latter result gives the solution
of the causal Monge-Ampère equation.

Keywords: Entropy, degenerate diffusions, martingale representation,
relative entropy, innovation process, causal Monge-Ampère
equation.

1.
Introduction

The representation of random variables with the stochastic integrals
with respect to some basic processes has a long history and also it
has very important applications, e.g., in signal theory, filtering,
optimal control, finance, stochastic differential equations, in
physics, etc. Let us recall the question: assume that we are given a
certain semimartingale
X
indexed by
[0,1]
for example. Let

{\mathcal{F}}(X)=({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X),t\in[0,1])
denote its filtration. The question is under
which conditions can we represent any martingale adapted to the
filtration of
X
as a stochastic integral w.r.t. a fixed martingale
of the filtration
{\mathcal{F}}(X)
? For the case of Wiener process, this
question has a very long history and it is almost impossible to give
an exact account of the contributing works. To our
knowledge, it has been answered for the first time in the work of K. Itô,
cf.
[
10
,
15
]
. In
[
2
]
, C. Dellacherie has given a different
point of view to prove the representation theorem for the Wiener and
Poisson processes, based on the uniqueness of their laws. The case of nondegenerate
diffusion processes has been elucidated in
[
12
]
, also studied in

[
3
,
11
]
(cf. also the references there) with also some remarks
about the degenerate case. The general case, using the notion of
multiplicity is given in
[
1
]
.

Although the nondegenerate case
is completely settled, the degenerate case has not been solved in a
definitive way, in the sense that a unique minimal martingale with
respect to which the above mentioned property of representation holds,
has not been discovered. In this work we are doing exactly this: we
prove that for a degenerate diffusion whose law is unique, there
exists a minimal martingale with respect to which every square
integrable,
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)
-measurable functional can be represented as a
stochastic integral of an
{\mathcal{F}}(X)
-adapted process.
To do this we first prove the density of a class of

{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)
-measurable stochastic integrals in
L^{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X))
using the method
launched by C. Dellacherie, then we show that a
sequence from this class which is approximating any element of
L^{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X))

defines an
L^{2}
-converging sequence of processes with values in the range of the
adjoint of the diffusion coefficient, i.e., with values in

\sigma^{\star}(t,X)({\rm I\!R}^{n})
. Because of the degeneracy of
\sigma
, we
can not determine the limit of this sequence but its image under the
orthogonal projection
P_{t}(X):{\rm I\!R}^{d}\to\sigma^{\star}(t,X)({\rm I\!R}^{n})
and
its limit is perfectly well-defined and in this way we see that the
minimal martingale for the representation of the functionals of the
diffusion (Itô process) is nothing but
dm_{t}=P_{t}(X)dB_{s}
in its
infinitesimal Itô form, where
B
is the Brownian motion governing
the process
X
. Note that even the adaptability of
(m_{t},t\in[0,1])

to the filtration
{\mathcal{F}}(X)
is not evident. This result of
representation gives also existence of non-orthogonal chaos
representation of the elements of
L^{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X))
as the multiple
ordered integrals with respect to the martingale
(m_{t},t\in[0,1])
of elements of
\{L^{2}(C_{n},dt_{1}\times\cdots\times dt_{n}),n\geq 1\}
, where

C_{n}=\{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}:t_{1}>\ldots>t_{n}\}
.

define the notion of innovation process associated to a degenerate
diffusion process

with respect to the sigma algebra

, where

is the solution of the diffusion stochastic differential

.

The results of the third section is then applied to the solution of

2.
Stochastic integral representation of functionals of
diffusions

Let
X=(X_{t},t\in[0,1])
be a weak solution of the following
stochastic differential equation:

(2.2)

dX_{t}=b(t,X)dt+\sigma(t,X)dB_{t},\,X_{0}=x,

where
B=(B_{t},t\in[0,1])
is an
{\rm I\!R}^{d}
-valued Brownian motion and

\sigma:[0,1]\times C([0,1],{\rm I\!R}^{n})\to L({\rm I\!R}^{d},{\rm I\!R}^{n})
and
b:[0,1]\times C([0,1],{\rm I\!R}^{n})\to{\rm I\!R}^{n}
are measurable maps, adapted to the
natural filtration of
C([0,1],{\rm I\!R}^{n})
and of linear
growth. Recall that the classical theorem of Yamada-Watanabe says that the strong
uniqueness implies the uniqueness in law of the above SDE,
cf.
[
16
,
9
]
. Hence weak uniqueness is easier to obtain in the
applications. In this section we shall assume the weak
uniqueness of the equation and prove the martingale representation
property for the case where
\sigma
may be degenerate.

More precisely, let
({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X),t\in[0,1])

be the filtration of
X
and let us denote by
K
the set of

{\rm I\!R}^{n}
-valued,
({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X),t\in[0,1])
-adapted processes
\alpha(X)
,
s.t.

E\int_{0}^{1}(a(s,X){\alpha}_{s}(X),{\alpha}_{s}(X))ds<\infty\,,

where
a(s,w)=\sigma(s,w)\sigma^{\star}(s,w),\,s\in[0,1],\,w\in C([0,1],{\rm I\!R}^{n})
.

Theorem 1
.

The set
\Gamma=\{N\in L^{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)):\,N=E[N]+\int_{0}^{1}({\alpha}_{s}(X),\sigma(s,X)dB_{s}),\,{\alpha}\in K\}
is
dense in
L^{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X))
.

Proof:
Suppose that there is some
M\in L^{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X))
which is orthogonal (in

L^{2}
) to
\Gamma
. Using the
usual stopping technique, we can assume that the corresponding

({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X),t\in[0,1])
-martingale is bounded and positive whose expectation is equal to
one. The orthogonality implies that

(M_{t}(f(X_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}Lf(s,X)ds),t\in[0,1])
is again a (local) martingale for
any smooth function
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}
, where
L
is defined as

(2.3)

Lf(t,X)=\frac{1}{2}\>\sum_{i,j}a_{i,j}(t,X)\partial_{i,j}f(X_{t})+\sum_{i}b_{i}(t,X)\partial_{i}f(X_{t})\,.

This implies, with Proposition IV.2.1 of
[
9
]
, that, under the
measure
M\cdot P
,
X
is again a weak solution of
2.2
.
By the uniqueness in law, we get
X(M\cdot P)=X(P)
, since
M
is

{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)
-measurable, we should have
M=1
. Hence the functionals
of the diffusion which are orthogonal to the above set of stochastic
integrals are almost surely constant. Consequently, the set
\Gamma

is total in
L^{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X))
.

The following is the
extension of the martingale representation
theorem to the functionals of degenerate diffusions
:

Theorem 2
.

Denote by
P_{s}(X)
a measurable version of the orthogonal projection
from
{\rm I\!R}^{d}
onto
\sigma(s,X)^{\star}({\rm I\!R}^{n})\subset{\rm I\!R}^{d}
and let
F\in L^{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X))
be any random variable with zero expectation. Then
there exists a process
\xi(X)\in L_{a}^{2}(dt\times dP;{\rm I\!R}^{d})
, adapted to

({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X),t\in[0,1])
, such that

F(X)=\int_{0}^{1}(P_{s}(X)\xi_{s}(X),dB_{s})_{{\rm I\!R}^{d}}=\int_{0}^{1}(\xi_{s}(X),P_{s}(X)dB_{s})_{{\rm I\!R}^{n}}

a.s.

Conversely, any stochastic integral of the form

\int_{0}^{1}(P_{s}(X)\xi_{s}(X),dB_{s})\,,

where
\xi(X)
is an
({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X),t\in[0,1])
-adapted, measurable
process with
E\int_{0}^{1}|P_{s}(X)\xi_{s}(X)|^{2}ds<\infty
, gives rise to an

{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)
-measurable random variable.

Proof:
From Theorem
1
, there exists a sequence
(F_{n}(X),n\geq 1)\subset\Gamma
, where

\Gamma
is defined in the statement of Theorem
1
,
converging to
F(X)
in
L^{2}
. We can suppose that
E[F_{n}(X)]=0
, for
any
n\geq 1
. Hence

F_{n}(X)=\int_{0}^{1}(\gamma^{n}_{s}(X),\sigma(s,X)dB_{s})_{{\rm I\!R}^{d}}=\int_{0}^{1}(\sigma^{\star}(s,X)\gamma^{n}_{s}(X),dB_{s})_{{\rm I\!R}^{d}}\,.

As explained above
\gamma^{n}
is an
({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X),t\in[0,1])
-adapted,
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
-valued
process satisfying

E\int_{0}^{1}(a(X_{s})\gamma^{n}_{s}(X),\gamma^{n}_{s}(X))ds<\infty
.
Since
F_{n}(X)\to F(X)
in
L^{2}
,

\lim_{n,m\to\infty}E\int_{0}^{1}|\sigma^{\star}(s,X)\gamma^{n}_{s}(X)-\sigma^{\star}(s,X)\gamma^{m}_{s}(X)|^{2}ds=0\,.

Let
\alpha^{n}_{s}(X)=\sigma^{\star}(X_{s})\gamma^{n}_{s}(X)
, as

P_{s}(X)\alpha^{n}_{s}(S)=P_{s}(X)\sigma^{\star}(X_{s})\gamma^{n}_{s}(X)=\sigma^{\star}(X_{s})\gamma^{n}_{s}(X)
,

(P_{s}(X)\alpha^{n}_{s}(X),n\geq 1)
converges to some
\xi_{s}(X)
in

L_{a}^{2}(ds\times dP;{\rm I\!R}^{d})
. As
P_{s}(X)
is an orthogonal projection,

(P_{s}(X)\alpha_{s}^{n}(X),n\geq 1)
converges to
P_{s}(X)\xi_{s}(X)
also in

L_{a}^{2}(ds\times dP;{\rm I\!R}^{d})
. Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}P_{s}(X)\xi_{s}(X).dB_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n}\int_{0}^{1}P_{s}(X)\alpha^{n}_{s}(X).dB_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n}\int_{0}^{1}(\gamma^{n}_{s}(X),\sigma(s,X)dB_{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n}F_{n}(X)=F(X)\,.

Let now
G\in L^{2}(P)
be given by
G=\int_{0}^{1}(P_{s}(X)\eta_{s}(X),dB_{s})

and assume that it is not
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)
-measurable. Then
G-E[G|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)]
is
orthogonal to
L^{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X))
. It follows from the first part
of the theorem that we can represent
E[G|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)]
as

\int_{0}^{1}(P_{s}(X)\xi_{s}(X),dB_{s})
. Let us define
h=\eta-\xi
, then the
orthogonality mentioned above implies that

E\left[\int_{0}^{1}(P_{s}(X)h_{s},dB_{s}).\int_{0}^{1}(\alpha_{s}(X),\sigma(s,X)dB_{s})\right]=0

for any
({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X),t\in[0,1])
-adapted, measurable
\alpha
such
that
E\int_{0}^{1}(a(s,X)\alpha_{s},\alpha_{s})ds<\infty
. Consequently

P_{s}(X)h_{s}=0

ds\times dP
-a.s., hence
G=E[G|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)]
P-a.s.

Remark:
Let
\eta
be an adapted process such that
\eta_{s}
belongs to
the orthogonal complement of
\sigma^{\star}({\rm I\!R}^{n})
in
{\rm I\!R}^{d}

ds\times dP
-a.s. Then
\eta+\xi
can also be used to represent

F(X)
. Hence
\xi(X)
is not unique but
P(X)\xi(X)
is always unique.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\dot{u}\in L^{2}(dt\times dP,{\rm I\!R}^{d})
be adapted to the Brownian filtration, then we have

(2.4)

E\left[\int_{0}^{1}(\dot{u}_{s},dB_{s})|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)\right]=\int_{0}^{1}(E[P_{s}(X)\dot{u}_{s}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(X)],dB_{s})

almost surely.

Proof:
We have to prove first that the right hand side of (
2.4
)
is
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)
-measurable. We know from Theorem
2
that
the left side of (
2.4
) can be represented as

E\left[\int_{0}^{1}(\dot{u}_{s},dB_{s})|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)\right]=\int_{0}^{1}P_{s}(X)\xi_{s}(X).dB_{s}

for some
\xi\in L_{a}^{2}(dt\times dP;{\rm I\!R}^{d})
. Let

F(X)=\int_{0}^{1}P_{s}(X)\alpha_{s}(X).dB_{s}
be any element of

L^{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X))
with
\alpha(X)\in L_{a}^{2}(dt\times dP;{\rm I\!R}^{d})
. We have

\displaystyle E\left[\left(\int_{0}^{1}\dot{u}_{s}.dB_{s}\right)F(X)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\int_{0}^{1}(\dot{u}_{s},P_{s}(X)\alpha_{s}(X))ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\int_{0}^{1}(P_{s}(X)E[\dot{u}_{s}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(X)],P_{s}(X)\alpha_{s}(X))ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\left(\int_{0}^{1}P_{s}(X)\xi_{s}(X).dB_{s}\right)\,F(X)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\int_{0}^{1}(P_{s}(X)\xi_{s}(X),P_{s}(X)\alpha_{s}(X))ds\,,

hence
P_{s}(X)\xi_{s}(X)=P_{s}(X)E[\dot{u}_{s}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(X)]

ds\times dP
-a.s., in particular

\int_{0}^{1}(E[P_{s}(X)\dot{u}_{s}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(X)],dB_{s})

is
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)
-measurable. The above identification assures then the
validity of the relation (
2.4
).

Corollary 1
.

Let
h\in H^{1}([0,1],{\rm I\!R}^{d})
(i.e., the Cameron-Martin space), denote by

\rho(\delta h)
the Wick exponential

\exp(\int_{0}^{1}(\dot{h}_{s},dB_{s})-\frac{1}{2}\>\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{h}_{s}|^{2}ds)
,
then we have

E[\rho(\delta h)|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)]=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}(P_{s}(X)\dot{h}_{s},dB_{s})-\frac{1}{2}\>\int_{0}^{1}|P_{s}(X)\dot{h}_{s}|^{2}ds\right)\,.

Theorem 4
.

•

Assume that
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X)\subset{\mathcal{F}}_{t}(B)
for any
t\in[0,1]
, where

({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(B),t\in[0,1])

F=E[F]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{C_{n}}(f_{n}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}),dm_{s_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes dm_{s_{n}})

where

.

•

,

3.
Innovation Process

In this section
we assume that the SDE
2.2
has unique strong
solution
. To fix the ideas, we can suppose that
\sigma
and
b
satisfy the
following kind of Lipschitz condition on the path space: for

\xi,\,\eta\in C([0,1],{\rm I\!R}^{n})
:

|\gamma(t,\xi)-\gamma(t,\eta)|\leq K\sup_{s\leq t}|\xi(s)-\eta(s)|

for
\gamma
being equal either to
\sigma
or to
b
with corresponding
Euclidean norms at the left hand side.

Assume that
\dot{u}\in L^{2}(dt\times dP,{\rm I\!R}^{d})
is a process adapted to
the filtration of Brownian motion, let
U=(U_{t},t\in[0,1])
be defined
as
U_{t}=B_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\dot{u}_{s}ds
. We denote by
X^{U}
the strong
solution of the equation

\displaystyle dX^{U}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(t,X^{U})dU_{t}+b(t,X^{U})dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(t,X^{U})(dB_{t}+\dot{u}_{t}dt)+b(t,X^{U})dt

Since
B
is the canonical Brownian motion, we
have
X_{t}^{U}=X_{t}\circ U
a.s. Moreover, if
\eta\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
is any
vector, we have

\displaystyle(\sigma(t,X^{U})\dot{u}_{t},\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\dot{u}_{t},\sigma(t,X^{U})^{\star}\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(P_{t}(X^{U})\dot{u}_{t},\sigma(t,X^{U})^{\star}\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\sigma(t,X^{U})P_{t}(X^{U})\dot{u}_{t},\eta)\,,

where
P_{t}(X^{U})
denotes the orthogonal projection from
{\rm I\!R}^{d}
onto

\sigma(t,X^{U})^{\star}({\rm I\!R}^{n})
. Hence, in order to define a reasonably
useful concept of innovation, we should estimate the perturbation

\dot{u}
simultaneously w.r.t. the both projections, i.e., with
respect to the conditional expectation
E[\cdot|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(X^{U})]
(which
is a projection) and also w.r.t.
P_{s}(X^{U})
: Let
Z=(Z_{t},t\in[0,1])

(to avoid the ambiguity we shall also use the notation
Z^{U}
if necessary)

Theorem 6
.

Let
(M_{t},t\in[0,1])

(P,({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X^{U}),t\in[0,1]))

({\mathcal{F}}_{t}(X^{U}),t\in[0,1])

\beta(X^{U})

4.
Entropy Calculation and Monge-Ampère Equation

Assume that
l(X)
is a probability density measurable
w.r.t.
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X)
, i.e.,
E[l(X)]=1
and with finite entropy:

E[l(X)\log l(X)]<\infty
. We want to find a process
U=B+u=B+\int_{0}^{\cdot}\dot{u}_{s}ds
which is an adapted
perturbation of the Brownian motion
B
such that

l(X)=\frac{dX^{U}(P)}{dX(P)}\circ X\,.

This problem is called the causal Monge-Ampère problem. To simplify the calculations, we shall assume that
l\circ X
is

P
-a.s. strictly positive. Assume that
such a
U
(hence
u
) exists and that
u
satisfies the Girsanov
theorem, i.e.,
E[\rho(-\delta_{B}u)]=1
. Then the Girsanov theorem
implies that

(4.9)

l\circ X^{U}\,E[\rho(-\delta u)|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X^{U})]=1

P
-a.s., which is the causal version of the Monge-Ampère
equation. From Theorem
2
,
l\circ X
can be represented
as

l\circ X=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}P_{s}(X)\dot{v}_{s}(X)\cdot dB_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\>\int_{0}^{1}|P_{s}(X)\dot{v}_{s}(X)|^{2}ds\right)\,,

where
(P_{s}(X)\dot{v}_{s}(X),s\in[0,1])
is adapted to the filtration of
X
and

\int_{0}^{1}|P_{s}(X)\dot{v}_{s}(X)|^{2}ds<\infty

P
-a.s.
Besides, since
U
is a Brownian motion under the
probability
\rho(-\delta u)\,dP
, it follows from Theorem

2
that
l\circ X^{U}
can be represented as

(4.10)

l\circ X^{U}=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}P_{s}(X^{U})\dot{v}_{s}(X^{U})\cdot dU_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\>\int_{0}^{1}|P_{s}(X^{U})\dot{v}_{s}(X^{U})|^{2}ds\right)\,.

Inserting the right hand side of (
4.10
) and
E[\rho(-\delta u)|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(X^{U})]
which
is already calculated in Theorem
5
in the Monge-Ampère
equation (
4.9
) and then taking the
logarithm of the final expression, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}P_{s}(X^{U})\left(E[\dot{u}_{s}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(X^{U})]+\dot{v}_{s}(X^{U})\right)\cdot dZ_{s}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\>\int_{0}^{1}\left(|P_{s}(X^{U})\dot{v}_{s}(X^{U})+P_{s}(X^{U})E[\dot{u}_{s}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(X^{U})]|^{2}\right)ds=0\,.

This relation implies that

(4.11)

P_{s}(X^{U}_{s})\left(\dot{v}_{s}(X^{U})+E[\dot{u}_{s}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(X^{U})]\right)=0

ds\times dP
-almost surely, which is a quite elaborate nonlinear equation. From the Monge-Ampère equation (
4.9
) we can calculate the
relative entropy between
X^{U}(P)
and
X(P)
, denoted by

H(X^{U}(P)|X(P))
:

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that
l
is an
X(P)
-almost surely strictly positive
density. There exists some
\dot{u}\in L^{2}(ds\times dP)
with
E[\rho(-\delta u)]=1
with

\frac{dX^{U}(P)}{dX(P)}=l

if and only if

P_{s}(X^{U}_{s})\left(\dot{v}_{s}(X^{U})+E[\dot{u}_{s}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(X^{U})]\right)=0.

In this case we also have

H(X^{U}(P)|X(P))=\frac{1}{2}\>E\int_{0}^{1}|P_{s}(X^{U})E[\dot{u}_{s}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(X^{U})]|^{2}ds=\frac{1}{2}\>E\int_{0}^{1}|P_{s}(X^{U})\dot{v}_{s}(X^{U})|^{2}ds\,.

Proof:

\displaystyle H(X^{U}(P)|X(P))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\log\frac{dX^{U}(P)}{dX(P)}dX^{U}(P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\log\frac{dX^{U}(P)}{dX(P)}\circ X^{U}dP

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\log l\circ X^{U}dP

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\>E\int_{0}^{1}|P_{s}(X^{U})E[\dot{u}_{s}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(X^{U})]|^{2}ds\,,

provided that
\dot{u}\in L^{2}(ds\times dP)
and the first equality
follows, the second one is a consequence of the relation
(
4.11
), it can be also proven directly from the Girsanov
theorem.

Proposition 2
