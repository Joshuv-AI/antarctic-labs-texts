Observing hidden neuronal states in experiments
Dmitry Amakhin, Anton Chizhov, Guillaume Girier, Mathieu Desroches, Jan Sieber, Serafim Rodrigues

Abstract
In this article, we revisit voltage-clamp electrophysiology, a feedback-control method that has successfully been applied to study neuronal (non)linear current-voltage relationships. Yet its full scope has not been explored to date. We reinterpret the voltage-clamp protocol under slow variation of the feedback reference signal using the language of multiple-timescale dynamical systems theory. Gauged under this new epistemic step, we unveil non-observable (hidden) states. More specifically, the experimental measurements from voltage clamp can be put into correspondence with predictions of both stable and unstable states from dynamical systems theory. We demonstrate a general protocol for constructing systematically experimental steady-state bifurcation diagrams for electrophysiologically active cells. We perform our experiments on entorhinal cortex neurons, both excitatory (pyramidal neurons) and inhibitiory (interneurons). Moreover, employing a current-clamp open-loop electrophysiology protocol with slow variation of an applied electrical current drives the neuron to dynamically transition between its observable rest states and its observable spiking states, hence revealing experimental dynamic bifurcations. Overlaying the two aforementioned protocols produces a map of all these neuronal states and effectively leads to an experimental validation of the classical slow-fast dissection method introduced by J. Rinzel in the 1980s and routinely applied ever since in order to analyse slow-fast neuronal models. Our approach opens doors to observing further complex hidden states with more advanced control strategies, allowing to control real cells beyond pharmacological manipulations.



Introduction.–

When characterising the dynamics of nonlinear systems, one fundamental criterion for a model is if its stable states such as stationary solutions or periodic orbits match experimental observations. The ability to validate models is, thus, greatly expanded by experimental tools with the capacity to unveil non-observable (sensitive or dynamically unstable) states that are otherwise inaccessible to standard measurements. The combination of observable and non-observable states gives access to an experimental equivalent of parameter-dependent families of stable and unstable states in a model, which are usually referred to as a bifurcation diagram.

This article applies a new experimental technique of using feedback control to find unstable states to electrophysiology experiments on neuronal cells. Our aim is to support systematic validation of neuron models by comparing bifurcation diagrams and observing their between-cell variability. We focus on unstable parts of input-dependent families of steady-state solutions obtained by feedback-controlled experiments and compare them with indirect evidence from standard measurements from open-loop experiments. This extends recent work of Ori

Our technique is simplification of the so-called

The objective is to compute experimental bifurcation diagrams, that is, both families of stable and unstable states (either stationary or periodic) together with bifurcation points joining such families. Importantly, the control is noninvasive in the sense that it vanishes once an equilibrium or periodic of the uncontrolled system has been reached; the control is decreased iteratively using a Newton’s method. In our case, the approach is simpler and does not require the use of Newton iterations since we consider a slow ramp on the control target. Hence we exploit de presence of two timescales in the experimental procedure in order to obtain directly an approximation of the steady-state bifurcation curve, and we explain why such a bifurcation curve is obtained at low cost; see below.

Conclusion.–

Tracking non-observable (hidden) states and their stability boundaries in experimental settings bridges the gap between real-world phenomena and nonlinear science. Specifically, closed-loop control methods with slow variations of feedback reference signals enable to dissect the underlying states of multi-scale complex systems. Following this strategy, we demonstrate the feasibility of tracking a family of neuronal steady states (stable and unstable) via variations of reference signal and reparameterizing the obtained curve by feedback current. Crucially, the method is versatile in that it applies to different cell types, both pyramidal (PY) neurons (like in Fig.
1
), of excitability type I or II (Figs.
3
(a) and
4
), and inhibitory interneurons (Fig.
3
(b)).

These results can be extended to more complex neuronal states (e.g., limit cycles) by combining two recent advances, namely: 1.
dynamic-clamp
electrophysiology
Sharp et al. 1993
;
Marder et al. 1996
;
Chizhov et al. 2014
;
Ori et al. 2020
, which allows for a two-way real-time communication between a neuronal tissue (e.g., neuron, network or neuronal sub-processes) and a computer simulation of a component of the neuronal tissue. This introduces the full range of feedback control theory and engineering techniques into electrophysiology; 2.
Control-based continuation for experiments (CBCE
, which combines feedback control theory and pseudo-arclength numerical continuation for tracking solution branches of nonlinear systems directly from noisy experimental data
Sieber et al. 2008
. Indeed, CBCE method has been successfully applied in various experimental systems, for instance, in mechanical vibration and buckling experiments
Renson et al. 2019
, pedestrian flow experiments
Panagiotopoulos et al. 2022
, cylindrical pipe flow simulations
Willis et al. 2017
and feasibility studies for synthetic gene networks
de Cesare et al. 2022
;
Blyth et al. 2023
. Noteworthy, the methodology presented in this work can be seen as a special case of CBCE. Future work will focus on employing the full CBCE to track more complex neuronal states. This is crucial as it will allow to validate computational models by comparing their numerical bifurcation diagrams with experimental ones. This approach will have direct impact in experimental labs enabling biologists to have access to novel states with which to carry out new experimental paradigms. We envisage this extended protocol could be used to develop next-generation closed-loop deep-brain stimulation devices to treat certain brain diseases such as epilepsy
Breakspear et al. 2006
, where it could help monitor control the excitability threshold of key neural populations in real time.

We hypothesise that these intermittent fluctuations are currents (possibly mediated by voltage-gated sodium and/or calcium channels) generated in distal neuronal sites (e.g., dendrites, axons) due to non-ideal voltage clamp (i.e., space-clamp error due to failure to achieve isopotential across the entire neuronal cell).

This can be understood by first comparing I-V relationships of voltage-gated channels of neurons in slice preparations versus isolated neurons, and subsequently inferring what occurs to isolated voltage-gated channels that are poorly controlled due to space-clamp error. Neurons in slice preparations have lower input resistance and higher membrane capacitance when compared to an isolated neuron
Kay and Wong 1986
. We observed the same effect with our entorhinal cortical neurons, which in slice preparations had
R\in\left]150,350\right[M\Omega
and
C\approx 100
pF, while in a previous study (unpublished) the isolated case had
R\in\left]500,1000\right[M\Omega
and
C\in\left]10,30\right[
pF. Thus, in slice preparations the typically observed voltage range in which the I-V relationship has a negative slope (e.g., for sodium channels) is quite large
Sherman et al. 1999
. In ideal conditions (i.e., when voltage-gated channels are more or less clustered within a neuronal site, for instance, close to the soma), space-clamp error is minimised. In this case, when the membrane voltage is slowly increased then individual negative peak currents of voltage-gated channels are successively activated (i.e., a kind of averaging effect occurs due to the clustering of channels), which give rise to a wide voltage range in which the I-V relationship has a negative slope. In other words, the current peaks are distributed across the voltage range of the I-V relationship
Sherman et al. 1999
.

Noteworthy, the fast sodium current peaks occur during this negative slope phase because the voltage dependence of the transient sodium channel activation (which mostly mediate these peaks) is similar to the activation of the persistent sodium channels. The poorly voltage-clamped transient sodium channels mediate the fast negative currents, while the transient ones mediate a slow negative current, both of which appear in the same voltage range
Fleidervish and Gutnick 1996
. In contrast, isolated neurons typically lose most of their thin dendritic branches (i.e., abbreviated morphology), hence voltage-gated channels (e.g., sodium current) display only one negative peak threshold current under a step depolarisation
Kay and Wong 1986
;
Golding et al. 1999
. From these observations, it is reasonable to assume that in non-ideal conditions (i.e., space-clamp error) there are isolated channels furthest from the cell soma or electrode contact (e.g., distal dendrites with thin branches, or dendritic spines with increased resistance of the cytoplasm, or distal sodium channels)
Duménieu et al. 2017
;
Petersen et al. 2015
. These isolated channels have I-V relationships with negative slope but with a shorter voltage range and distinct activation threshold. Thus, under a VC protocol the mismatch between the hold voltage (
V_{\mathrm{h}}
) and the real-membrane voltage (
V
) of distal voltage-gated channels can be very large, resulting in the observed intermittent fluctuations. Additionally to the above mentioned factors, the delay of the feedback (associated with the amplifier, which is in the order tens of nanoseconds) is likely to play a role in these observed intermittent fluctuations. However, from a theoretical standpoint, a fixed delay can induce a Hopf bifurcation, and therefore, robust oscillations. So instead, we envisage a varying delay, due to possibly latent processes, could create this effect. Of course, to really address this point, further experiments are required.

To simulate the above hypothesis we added additional instantaneous currents (i.e., to model the effect of non-ideally clamped channels as external forcing that correspond to non-homogeneous terms); see Section

We hypothesized that the mismatch between the ascending and descending I-V relationships can be attributed to the activity of the slow inactivating sodium and calcium ion channels. These slowly inactivating sodium and calcium channels are active at the ascending ramp, whereas high depolarization at the descending ramp inactivates them such that they are ineffective and thus do not contribute to the S-shaped I-V relationship. It has previously been reported that persistent sodium channels are present in deep entorhinal cortex neurons, and that current through these channels can be detected even when the rate of membrane depolarization is very low
Agrawal et al. 2001
. A prepulse to depolarized voltages inactivates persistent sodium channels of the neocortical neurons
Fleidervish and Gutnick 1996
. T-Type calcium channels are also known to produce the “window” currents due to the overlap of the activation and steady-state inactivation curves
Amarillo et al. 2014
. In order to check this hypothesis we used QX314 (6 mM) and Cd
2+
(50 µM), which are the blockers of the voltage-gated sodium and calcium ion channels, respectively. To quantify the effects of the applied substances on the mismatch between the ascending and descending I-V relationships we introduced the I-V slope ratio (Fig.
S5
(c)). It was calculated as the ratio of the minimal slope of the ascending I-V relationship to the slope of the descending I-V relationship at -35 mV. In control conditions, the I-V slope ratio was negative due to a strong mismatch between the I-V relationships (Fig.
S5
(b,c), CTRL). When the pipette solution containing QX314 was used, the observed mismatch between the I-V relationships was much less pronounced (Fig.
S5
(b), middle panel), resulting in the significantly higher I-V slope ratio. With QX314 in the pipette solution and Cd
2+
in the ACSF, both the ascending and descending I-V relationships were monotonically increasing functions (Fig.
S5
(b), right panel), resulting in positive values of the I-V slope ratios. The results obtained suggest that the negative slope of the ascending I-V relationship is mainly mediated by persistent sodium channels and to a lesser extent by voltage-gated calcium channels.

In the model the channel properties depend sensitively on the maximal conductances (e.g.,
g_{\mathrm{Ca}}m_{\infty}
for calcium in eq. (
8
) of the main manuscript). A small change in these parameters in eq. (
8
) removes the two fold points from the steady-state curve (in a so-called cusp bifurcation), resulting in a change from type-I to type-II behavior.

S5
Equilibrium analysis of the general conductance-based model for the
\mathbf{V}
-clamped protocol

For a fixed hold voltage
V_{\mathrm{h}}
a general conductance-based model, written using indices
j
for
{n_{\mathrm{c}}}
channels and rates
r_{j}(V)=1/\tau_{j}(V)
, reads (see main text, equation (
1
)):

\displaystyle C\dot{V}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{c}}}I_{j}(x_{j},V)+g_{\mathrm{c}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}-V),

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{j}

\displaystyle=(x_{\infty,j}(V)-x_{j})r_{j}(V).

(S2)

This section states a sufficient criterion for the uniqueness and linear stability of equilibria for system (
S2
). Equilibria are at

x_{\mathrm{eq},j}=x_{\infty,j}(V_{\mathrm{eq}})
, where
V_{\mathrm{eq}}
satisfies

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{eq}}

\displaystyle=V_{\mathrm{h}}-\frac{1}{g_{\mathrm{c}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{c}}}I_{j}(x_{\infty,j}(V_{\mathrm{eq}}),V_{\mathrm{eq}})=:\operatorname{alg}_{\operatorname{fix}}(V_{\mathrm{eq}}).

(S3)

We denote the
{n_{\mathrm{c}}}\times 1
vectors (
j=1,\ldots,{n_{\mathrm{c}}}
)

\displaystyle I_{\infty,j}

\displaystyle=I_{j}(x_{\infty,j}(V_{\mathrm{eq}}),V_{\mathrm{eq}}),

\displaystyle x_{\infty,j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=x_{\infty,j}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{eq}}),

\displaystyle I_{x,j}

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}I_{j}(x_{\infty,j}(V_{\mathrm{eq}}),V_{\mathrm{eq}}),

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{eq},j}

\displaystyle=r_{j}(V_{\mathrm{eq}}),

\displaystyle I_{V,j}

\displaystyle=\partial_{2}I_{j}(x_{\infty,j}(V_{\mathrm{eq}}),V_{\mathrm{eq}}),

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{eq},j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=r_{j}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{eq}}),

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{eq},j}

\displaystyle=\tau_{j}(V_{\mathrm{eq}}).

The derivative of
\operatorname{alg}_{\operatorname{fix}}(V)
in
V=V_{\mathrm{eq}}
is (using the notation
\bm{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\mathsf{T}}
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{alg}_{\operatorname{fix}}(V_{\mathrm{eq}})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{g_{\mathrm{c}}}[I_{x}^{\mathsf{T}}x_{\infty}^{\prime}+I_{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{1}]=:\mu_{\mathrm{c}},

which is dimensionless. Consequently, a sufficient condition for the
uniqueness of the fixed point
V_{\mathrm{eq}}
is
|\mu_{\mathrm{c}}|<1
, or, which is guaranteed by the estimate

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{c}}:=\frac{1}{g_{\mathrm{c}}}[\|I_{x}.x_{\infty}^{\prime}\|_{1}+\|I_{V}\|_{1}]<1,

where
(.)
means element-wise multiplication (so,
v.w=\diag(v)w
), and
\|v\|_{1}:=|v_{1}|+\ldots+|v_{n_{\mathrm{c}}}|
is the
1
-norm of a vector
v
. The
condition
m_{\mathrm{c}}<1
is also sufficient for linear stability of

(V_{\mathrm{eq}},x_{\mathrm{eq},(\cdot)})
: the Jacobian of
(
S2
) in
(V_{\mathrm{eq}},x_{\mathrm{eq},(\cdot)})
is

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{eq}}:=\begin{bmatrix}-(g_{\mathrm{c}}+I_{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{1})/C,&-I_{x}^{\mathsf{T}}/C\\[2.15277pt]
r_{\mathrm{eq}}.x_{\infty}^{\prime},&-\diag(r_{\mathrm{eq}})\end{bmatrix},

(note that the matrix entries are in
row order
1\times 1
,
1\times{n_{\mathrm{c}}}
,
{n_{\mathrm{c}}}\times 1
and

{n_{\mathrm{c}}}\times{n_{\mathrm{c}}}
, and that the term

\diag(r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime})x_{\infty}(V_{\mathrm{eq}})
cancels in the bottom
left entry). By rescaling the
x_{j}
to
x_{j}/(x_{\infty,j}^{\prime}(1+\delta)

with an arbitrary
\delta>0
we can make the lower
{n_{\mathrm{c}}}
rows diagonally dominant. This gives then in the upper right entry
-(I_{x}.x_{\infty}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}(1+\delta)/C
:

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{scal}}:=\begin{bmatrix}-(g_{\mathrm{c}}+I_{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{1})/C,&-\frac{1+\delta}{C}(I_{x}.x_{\infty}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\\[2.15277pt]
r_{\mathrm{eq}}/(1+\delta),&-\diag(r_{\mathrm{eq}})\end{bmatrix},

Typically, the derivative of
I_{j}
with respect to
V
is non-negative (so,
I_{V,j}\geq 0
).
Hence, the matrix satisfies Gershgorin’s theorem for some
\delta>0
if

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{c}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{c}}}I_{V,j}

\displaystyle>\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{c}}}|I_{x,j}x_{\infty,j}^{\prime}|\mbox{,\quad or\quad}g_{\mathrm{c}}+\|I_{V}\|_{1}>\|I_{x}.x_{\infty}^{\prime}\|_{1}.

This is equivalent to
m_{\mathrm{c}}<1
. The fixed-point relation (
S3
) also ensures that

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{h}}(1-m_{\mathrm{c}})\leq V_{\mathrm{eq}}\leq\frac{V_{\mathrm{h}}}{1-m_{\mathrm{c}}},

which gives a bound on the relationship between
V_{\mathrm{h}}
and
V_{\mathrm{eq}}
, that becomes sharper for larger
g_{\mathrm{c}}
. Hence, for conductance-based models with clamped voltage
V_{\mathrm{h}}
the difference between the clamp voltage
V_{\mathrm{h}}
and the unique stable equilibrium potential across the membrane is of order
m_{\mathrm{c}}
(so gets small for large gain
g_{\mathrm{c}}
).

S6
Conditions for validity of estimate for systematic bias due to dynamic ramp of voltage

Estimate (
5
) in the main text provides an approximation to first order in
\varepsilon
for the bias between steady-state current,
I_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})
, for fixed hold voltage
V_{\mathrm{h}}
, and the actually measured current
I_{\mathrm{vc}}(t)
when the hold voltage is changed slowly with
\dot{V}_{\mathrm{h}}=\varepsilon\Delta_{V}
:

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}(t))-I_{\mathrm{vc}}(t)\approx I_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})\dot{V}_{\mathrm{h}}/\alpha.

(S4)

In this expression
\alpha
is the decay rate toward the equilibrium
I_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})
at fixed
V_{\mathrm{h}}
and
I_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})
is the slope of the equilibrium (steady-state) curve
I_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})
we are interested in. In practice, we estimate

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}}(t))

\displaystyle\approx I_{\mathrm{vc}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}}(t)),

\displaystyle\alpha^{-1}

\displaystyle\approx\tau_{1/2}/\ln 2,

(S5)

where
I_{\mathrm{vc}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})
is the measured
(I,V)
-curve during voltage clamp runs (VC) and
\tau_{1/2}
is the time of decay to half of the transients during large fluctuations of measured
I_{\mathrm{vc}}
during VC runs (see Section
S3
and Fig.
S4
). The estimates (
S5
) are themselves accurate to first order in
\varepsilon
, such that their bias is only a second-order effect in (
S4
) if
\alpha>0
.
Pretending
I_{\mathrm{vc}}(t)
was governed by a scalar first-order ordinary differential equation
\dot{I}_{\mathrm{vc}}=f_{IV}(I_{\mathrm{vc}},V_{\mathrm{h}})
, bias estimate (
S4
) would follow from implicit differentiation with respect to time and
I_{\mathrm{vc}}
, where
\alpha=-\partial_{1}f_{IV}(I_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}),V_{\mathrm{h}})>0
.

Justifying (
S4
) based on a more accurate model for the VC experiment such as a higher-dimensional conductance-based model requires that two conditions are met:

•

presence of a dominant eigenvalue with
spectral gap
in the linearization near the steady-state curve, and

•

observability
of fluctuations with the slowest rate of decay in the measured current.

Examples of such models are equations (
1
), (
2
) in the main text; see also equation (
8
) for the concrete Morris-Lecar model used for Fig.
2
in the main text and Fig.
S4
.
More precisely, let us collect the entire vector of conductance-based model variables into
y=(V,x_{j})_{j=1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{c}}}
, such that the conductance-based model (
1
), (
2
) for voltage clamp with a slow ramp of hold voltage
V_{\mathrm{h}}
from the main text is of the general form

\displaystyle\dot{y}

\displaystyle=f(y,V_{\mathrm{h}}),

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{\mathrm{h}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\Delta_{V}.

(S6)

For each fixed hold voltage
V_{\mathrm{h}}
(so, at
\varepsilon=0
) the voltage clamp stabilizes an equilibrium
y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})
, such that
0=f(y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}),V_{\mathrm{h}})
.

Spectral gap

The first condition for validity of (
S4
) is that among the eigenvalues of the matrix
J(V_{\mathrm{h}}):=\partial_{1}f(y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}),V_{\mathrm{h}})
(which all have negative part), there is one
dominant
eigenvalue
-\alpha(V_{\mathrm{h}})
of
J(V_{\mathrm{h}})
with largest real part. This dominance is expressed as a spectral gap condition for
J(V_{\mathrm{h}})
, which means that all other eigenvalues must have real part less than some bound
-\beta(V_{\mathrm{h}})<-\alpha(V_{\mathrm{h}})<0
with the condition

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}(V_{\mathrm{h}})

\displaystyle\ll\alpha^{-1}(V_{\mathrm{h}})\sim\mbox{\ order 1.}

(S7)

The current flowing through the membrane is the scalar output
I_{\mathrm{vc}}(y)=\sum_{j}I_{j}(x_{j},V)\in\mathbb{R}
, depending on the state variables of the conductance-based model, which we measure. Near the equilibrium
y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})
this output is to first order of the form

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{vc}}(y)\approx I_{\mathrm{vc}}(y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}))+\partial I_{\mathrm{vc}}(y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}))[y-y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})]\mbox{,\quad where $\partial I_{\mathrm{vc}}(y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}))=:c(V_{\mathrm{h}})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times(n_{\mathrm{c}}+1)}$ is a row vector.}

Observability

The second condition for validity of (
S4
) is that the right eigenvector
y_{\mathrm{ev}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})
for the dominant eigenvalue
-\alpha(V_{\mathrm{h}})
has a non-zero (more precisely, non-small) projection in variations of this measured output:

\displaystyle\partial I_{\mathrm{vc}}(y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}))y_{\mathrm{ev}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})=c(V_{\mathrm{h}})^{\mathsf{T}}y_{\mathrm{ev}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})\neq 0\mbox{.}

(S8)

If the dominant direction
y_{\mathrm{ev}}
is observable according to (
S8
), then we may normalize the eigenvector
y_{\mathrm{ev}}
such that
c(V_{\mathrm{h}})^{\mathsf{T}}y_{\mathrm{ev}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})=1
for all
V_{\mathrm{h}}
. We denote the left eigenvector of
J(V_{\mathrm{h}})
for the dominant eigenvalue
-\alpha(V_{\mathrm{h}})
by
y_{\mathrm{ad}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})
, normalized such that
y_{\mathrm{ad}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})^{\mathsf{T}}y_{\mathrm{ev}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})=1
. If
J(V_{\mathrm{h}})
satisfies the spectral gap condition (
S7
), its inverse is
J(V_{\mathrm{h}})^{-1}\approx-\alpha(V_{\mathrm{h}})y_{\mathrm{ev}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})y_{\mathrm{ev}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})^{\mathsf{T}}
(ignoring small terms of order
\beta^{-1}
), thus, it is close to the rank-
1
matrix
-\alpha y_{\mathrm{ev}}y_{\mathrm{ad}}^{\mathsf{T}}
. As
y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}})
is the steady-state curve of (
S6
) (so,
0=f(y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}),V_{\mathrm{h}})
), we have (abbreviating
\partial_{2}f_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}):=\partial_{2}f(y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}),V_{\mathrm{h}})
and dropping argument
V_{\mathrm{h}}
from
\alpha
,
c
,
y_{\mathrm{ev}}
,
y_{\mathrm{ad}}
,
J
, and
\partial_{2}f_{\mathrm{eq}}
)

\displaystyle y_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})

\displaystyle=-J^{-1}\partial_{2}f_{\mathrm{eq}}\approx-\alpha y_{\mathrm{ev}}y_{\mathrm{ad}}^{\mathsf{T}}\partial_{2}f_{\mathrm{eq}}\mbox{, \quad and, hence,}

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})

\displaystyle=\partial I_{\mathrm{vc}}(y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}))y_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})=c^{\mathsf{T}}y_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})\approx-\alpha y_{\mathrm{ad}}^{\mathsf{T}}\partial_{2}f_{\mathrm{eq}}\mbox{,\quad implying}

\displaystyle y_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})

\displaystyle\approx y_{\mathrm{ev}}I_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})

when neglecting terms of order
\beta^{-1}
. Expansion of (
S6
) near the steady-state curve
(y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}),V_{\mathrm{h}})
implies that solutions
y(t)
of (
S6

\displaystyle y(t)-y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}(t))\approx J^{-1}y_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})\dot{V}_{\mathrm{h}}\approx-\alpha y_{\mathrm{ev}}y_{\mathrm{ad}}^{\mathsf{T}}y_{\mathrm{ev}}I_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})\dot{V}_{\mathrm{h}}=-\alpha y_{\mathrm{ev}}I_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})\dot{V}_{\mathrm{h}},

ignoring terms of order
\varepsilon^{2}
and
\varepsilon\beta^{-1}
. Inserting this expansion into the expansion for the output

gives the desired first-order bias estimate,

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{vc}}(y(t))-I_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}(t))\approx\partial I_{\mathrm{vc}}(y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}))[y(t)-y_{\mathrm{eq}}(V_{\mathrm{h}}(t))]\approx-\alpha c^{\mathsf{T}}y_{\mathrm{ev}}I_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})\dot{V}_{\mathrm{h}}=-\alpha I_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\prime}(V_{\mathrm{h}})\dot{V}_{\mathrm{h}}.

(S9)

Finally, we observe that the measured current
