Zero Mach Number Limit of the Compressible Primitive Equations: Ill-prepared Initial Data
Xin Liu, Edriss S. Titi

Abstract
In the work, we consider the zero Mach number limit of compressible primitive equations in the domain ℝ 2 × 2 ​ 𝕋 \mathbb{R}^{2}\times 2\mathbb{T} or 𝕋 2 × 2 ​ 𝕋 \mathbb{T}^{2}\times 2\mathbb{T} . We identify the limit equations to be the primitive equations with the incompressible condition. The convergence behaviors are studied in both ℝ 2 × 2 ​ 𝕋 \mathbb{R}^{2}\times 2\mathbb{T} and 𝕋 2 × 2 ​ 𝕋 \mathbb{T}^{2}\times 2\mathbb{T} , respectively. This paper takes into account the high oscillating acoustic waves and is an extension of our previous work in [ 29 ] . Mathematics Subject Classification: 35B25, 35B40, 76N10. Keywords: Low Mach number limit, Compressible primitive equations, incompressible primitive equations.



1
Introduction

1.1
Zero Mach number limit

As an example of multi-scale analysis,
the zero Mach number limit problem for compressible flows has been an important classical problem in the study of hydrodynamic equations. Pioneered by Klainerman and Majda in
[
20
,
19
]
, it is shown that the solutions of inviscid compressible Euler equations converge to that of inviscid incompressible Euler equations for the isentropic flows in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. The initial data are well-prepared and almost incompressible. The result is further studied in bounded domains for nonisentropic flows by Schochet in
[
38
,
39
]
. In
[
43
]
, the author establishes the zero Mach number limit with general (ill-prepared) initial data in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As pointed out in
[
33
]
, in comparison to the case when the initial data are well-prepared, the time derivatives are no longer uniformly bounded with respect to the Mach number when the system is complemented with ill-prepared initial data. This leads to high frequency acoustic waves with large amplitude.

We have studied the zero Mach number limit of compressible primitive equations with well-prepared initial data in
[
29
]
. In this work, we are considering such a singular limit problem with ill-prepared initial data.

To present the general idea of singular limit problems with ill-prepared initial data, consider the following equation of the unknown function
G
,

\partial_{t}G+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}L(\partial_{x})G=\mathcal{N}(G),

(1.1)

where
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
is the singular parameter,
L(\partial_{x})
is an anti-symmetric differential operator with constant coefficients, and
\mathcal{N}(G)
is the nonlinearity of
G
. Then one can perform the
H^{s}
estimate for some large
s\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, on system (
1.1
). Since
L(\partial_{x})
is anti-symmetric,
\bigl\Arrowvert G\bigr\Arrowvert_{H^{s}}
is uniformly bounded with respect to
\varepsilon
, at least locally in time.
If one considers the projection of (
1.1
) in the kernel of operator
L(\partial_{x})
, which is called the non-singular part of the equation, it follows that

\partial_{t}P_{\ker(L(\partial_{x}))}G=P_{\ker(L(\partial_{x}))}\mathcal{N}(G)

is uniformly bounded in
H^{s^{\prime}}
space for some
s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\cup\{0\}
with respect to
\varepsilon
. On the other hand,
G-P_{\ker(L(\partial_{x}))}G
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(G-P_{\ker(L(\partial_{x}))}G)+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}L(\partial_{x})(G-P_{\ker(L(\partial_{x}))}G)

(1.2)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(G)-P_{\ker(L(\partial_{x}))}\mathcal{N}(G),

which has wave packet solutions with fast oscillations as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Consequently,
G-P_{\ker(L(\partial_{x}))}G
is oscillating at high frequency.
Moreover, as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, the
H^{s}
norm preserving property of the anti-symmetric operator
L(\partial_{x})
implies that the amplitude of the oscillations does not vanish in general.
Instead, the limit of the oscillatory part is driven by certain PDEs.
To resolve the singular limit problem of (
1.1
) as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, one has to study the interactions of the non-singular part and the oscillatory part in the nonlinearity
\mathcal{N}(G)
in order to identify the limit equations. Such a method of studying the singular limit problem of (
1.1
) is developed by Schochet in
[
37
]
for hyperbolic PDEs with applications to the incompressible limit problem of Euler equations, nonlinear wave equations and the theory of weakly nonlinear geometric optics. Later, this method is further developed for some parabolic equations by Gallagher in
[
16
]
. We remark here that, if equation (
1.1
) is complemented with well-prepared initial data, the amplitude of oscillations is small, and consequently, the interaction between the non-singular part and the oscillatory part is much weaker.

In the study of zero Mach number limit of hydrodynamic equations for isentropic flows, if one writes the equations in the form of (
1.1
), the corresponding kernel of
L(\partial_{x})
consists the solenoidal velocity field. The equations corresponding to the fast oscillation equations (
1.2
) are referred to as the acoustic wave equations. Using these terminologies, the theorem developped by Ukai in
[
43
]
is basically showing that the acoustic waves decay to zero as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Such a fact is the consequence of the Strichartz estimates for linear wave equations, as pointed out by Desjardins and Grenier in
[
10
]
(see, e.g.,
[
17
,
25
,
18
]
about the Strichartz estimate). In
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, Lions and Masmoudi study the resonance of the high frequency osciallations and show that in the sense of distribution, the solutions to the compressible Navier–Stokes equations converge to that to the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations as the Mach number goes to zero in
[
27
]
. Later in
[
31
]
, Masmoudi studies the incompressible, inviscid limit, with low Mach number and large Reynolds number, of compressible Navier–Stokes equations and identities the equations in both
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. We refer readers, for further developments, to
[
7
,
6
,
8
,
40
]
.

Additionally, we would like to mention that
when considering the non-isentropic flows, the corresponding operator
L(\partial_{x})
in (
1.1
) has coefficients depending on space and time variables. This causes non-trivial difficulties to study the resonances between the oscillations (see, e.g.,
[
33
,
34
,
3
,
2
,
1
]
). Moreover, when taking into account the stratification effect of gravity, the compressible Navier–Stokes equations with gravity may converge to the Oberbeck-Boussinesq equations or the anelastic equations, depending on the strength of the gravity effect. We refer readers, for more discussions of related topics, to
[
12
,
11
,
13
,
32
,
9
,
4
,
44
,
35
,
15
,
36
,
14
]
. Also, for more multi-scale analysis, we refer readers to

[
21
,
22
,
24
,
23
,
30
]
.

1.2
The compressible primitive equations

As mentioned before, we aim at studying the low Mach number limit of the compressible primitive equations. As discussed in our previous work
[
29
]
, this is part of the justification of the PE diagram (see Figure 1 in
[
29
]
). We refer readers, for more background of the compressible primitive equations, to
[
29
]
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
denote the Mach number, and
let
\rho_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R},v_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},w_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
represent the density, the horizontal and the vertical velocities, respectively. Then the compressible primitive equations can be written as, after rescaling the original CPE, similar to that of the compressible Navier–Stokes equations (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
):

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho_{\varepsilon}+div_{h}(\rho_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon})+\partial_{z}(\rho_{\varepsilon}w_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in}~\Omega_{h}\times(0,1),\\
\partial_{t}(\rho_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon})+div_{h}(\rho_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}\otimes v_{\varepsilon})+\partial_{z}(\rho_{\varepsilon}w_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon})&\\
~~~~~~~~+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{h}P(\rho_{\varepsilon})=div_{h}\mathbb{S}(v_{\varepsilon})+\partial_{zz}v_{\varepsilon}&\text{in}~\Omega_{h}\times(0,1),\\
\partial_{z}P(\rho_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in}~\Omega_{h}\times(0,1).\end{cases}

(CPE)

where
P(\rho_{\varepsilon})=\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}
,
\mathbb{S}(v_{\varepsilon})=\mu(\nabla_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+\nabla_{h}v_{\varepsilon}^{\top})+(\lambda-\mu)div_{h}v_{\varepsilon}\mathbb{I}_{2}
represent the pressure for isentropic flows and the viscous stress tensor for Newtonian flows, respectively. Here, we assume that
\mu,\lambda>0
and
\gamma>1
.
We consider
\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{T}^{2}
in this paper, where
\mathbb{T}^{2}
represents the periodic domain with period
1
in both directions in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. (
CPE
) is complemented with the stress-free and non-permeable boundary conditions:

\partial_{z}v_{\varepsilon}\big|_{z=0,1}=0,~w_{\varepsilon}\big|_{z=0,1}=0.

(BC-CPE)

Hereafter, we have and will use
\nabla_{h},div_{h}
and
\Delta_{h}
to represent the horizontal gradient, the horizontal divergence, and the horizontal Laplace operator, respectively; that is,

\displaystyle\nabla_{h}:=\biggl(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{x}\\
\partial_{y}\end{array}\biggr),~div_{h}:=\nabla_{h}\cdot,~\Delta_{h}:=div_{h}\nabla_{h}\cdot.

Notice, (
CPE
) with (
BC-CPE
) is invariant with respect to the following symmetry:

v_{\varepsilon}~\text{and}~w_{\varepsilon}~\text{are even and odd, respectively, in the $z$-variable}.

(SYM)

Owing to such symmetry, in order to study the limit system of (
CPE
) as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, it suffices to consider the following system:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho_{\varepsilon}+div_{h}(\rho_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon})+\partial_{z}(\rho_{\varepsilon}w_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in}~\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T},\\
\partial_{t}(\rho_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon})+div_{h}(\rho_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}\otimes v_{\varepsilon})+\partial_{z}(\rho_{\varepsilon}w_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon})&\\
~~~~~~~~+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{h}P(\rho_{\varepsilon})=div_{h}\mathbb{S}(v_{\varepsilon})+\partial_{zz}v_{\varepsilon}&\text{in}~\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T},\\
\partial_{z}P(\rho_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in}~\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T},\end{cases}

(1.3)

subject to the periodic boundary condition and symmetry (
SYM
).
Here
2\mathbb{T}
is the periodic domain with period
2
in
\mathbb{R}
.

We remark that the restrictions of solutions to (
1.3
) in
\Omega_{h}\times[0,1]
solve (
CPE
) with (
BC-CPE
), provided that the solutions exist and are regular enough.

We can rewrite
\eqref{CPE}_{2}
as, provided that
\rho_{\varepsilon}>0
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}+v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+w_{\varepsilon}\partial_{z}v_{\varepsilon}+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{h}\bigl(\dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma-1}\bigr)=\dfrac{\mu}{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\Delta_{h}v_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~+\dfrac{\lambda}{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{h}div_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+\dfrac{1}{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{zz}v_{\varepsilon}.

Also from the continuity equation
\eqref{CPE}_{1}
, one can derive

\partial_{t}\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma-1}+v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma-1}+(\gamma-1)\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma-1}div_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+(\gamma-1)\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma-1}\partial_{z}w_{\varepsilon}=0.

We consider
\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma-1}
with perturbations around the constant state given by
\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma-1}=\bar{\rho}^{\gamma-1}\in(0,\infty)
. Then we define the perturbation variable
\xi_{\varepsilon}
by

\xi_{\varepsilon}:=\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\biggl(\dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\dfrac{\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma-1}}{c^{2}}\biggr),

with
c^{2}:=\dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\bar{\rho}^{\gamma-1}
. Let
\alpha:=\dfrac{1}{\gamma-1}
,
c_{1}:=\bigl(\dfrac{\gamma}{(\gamma-1)c^{2}}\bigr)^{\alpha}
. With such notations,
\rho_{\varepsilon}=c_{1}^{-1}\exp{(\varepsilon\alpha\xi_{\varepsilon})}
. Without loss of generality, we take
\bar{\rho}\equiv 1
. Hence (
1.3
) can be written as

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\xi_{\varepsilon}+v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}+\dfrac{\gamma-1}{\varepsilon}(div_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+\partial_{z}w_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in}~\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T},\\
\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}+v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+w_{\varepsilon}\partial_{z}v_{\varepsilon}+\dfrac{c^{2}e^{\varepsilon\xi_{\varepsilon}}}{\varepsilon}\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}\\
~~~~=c_{1}e^{-\varepsilon\alpha\xi_{\varepsilon}}\bigl(\mu\Delta_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+\lambda\nabla_{h}div_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+\partial_{zz}v_{\varepsilon})&\text{in}~\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T},\\
\partial_{z}\xi_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{in}~\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T},\end{cases}

(1.4)

complemented with periodic initial data with symmetry (
SYM
).
In fact, (
1.4
) is already in the form of (
1.1
). In this work, we study the asymptotic limit of (
1.4
) as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
. In fact, we will demonstrate that the existence time of strong solutions to (
1.4
) has a uniform lower bound, independent of
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
for some small
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
. In addition, in the sense of distribution,
the limit equations of (
1.4
), as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, are the primitive equations (
2.9
) with the incompressible condition, below.
Also, the associated acoustic wave equations for the three dimensional system (
1.4
) are only two dimensional (see (
4.20
), below). Consequently, we are able to adopt the strategy of studying the acoustic wave equations for the compressible Navier–Stokes equations in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{T}^{2}
to investigate the oscillatory part of the equations. This is done in section
4
, below.

The rest of this work is organized as follows. In the next section, we will introduce some notations as well as some function spaces. Also, the main theorems in this work are stated in both
\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Next, in section
3
, we establish the uniform local well-posedness of strong solutions to system (
1.4
). This is done with uniform
a priori
estimates in section
3.1
, a local existence theorem in section
3.2
and a continuity argument in section
3.3
. In section
4
, we first identity the primitive equations, i.e., (
2.9
), below, as the limit of system (
1.4
) as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
in the sense of distribution. Then in section
4.1
, we argue that the acoustic waves decay to zero in the case when
\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
; in section
4.2
, we study the oscillation equations and identify the limit equations of oscillations in the case when
\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Consequently, we conclude the compactness in both cases as stated in the main theorems, below.

2
Preliminaries and main theorems

In this work, we denote by

\displaystyle\overline{f}(x,y):=\int_{0}^{1}f(x,y,z^{\prime})\,dz^{\prime},~\text{and}~\widetilde{f}(x,y,z):=f(x,y,z)-\overline{f}(x,y),

as the average and the fluctuation of any function
f=f(x,y,z)
over the
z
-variable.
We use
\partial_{h}
to denote the horizontal derivatives, i.e.,
\partial_{h}\in\{\partial_{x},\partial_{y}\}
.
\partial_{t}
and
\partial_{z}
denote the time derivative and the vertical derivative, respectively. For any function
f
,
\partial_{g}f
is sometimes denoted as
f_{g}
, for
g\in\{t,x,y,z,h\}
. Similar notations are also adopted for higher order derivatives.

As in
[
26
]
, we introduce the following function spaces. Denote by

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}^{\infty}:=\{u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T};\mathbb{R}^{2})|\int_{0}^{1}div_{h}u\,dz=0\},

\displaystyle C_{\tau}^{\infty}:=\{u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T};\mathbb{R}^{2})|u=\nabla_{h}\psi,~\text{for}~\psi\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{h};\mathbb{R}^{2})\}.

Then with respect to the
L^{2}
-inner product,
C_{\sigma}^{\infty}\perp C_{\tau}^{\infty}
and
C^{\infty}_{0}=C_{\sigma}^{\infty}\cup C_{\tau}^{\infty}
. We denote the closures in the
L^{2}
norm of
C_{\sigma}^{\infty},C_{\tau}^{\infty}
as
L_{\sigma}^{2}=L_{\sigma}^{2}(\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T};\mathbb{R}^{2}),L_{\tau}^{2}:=L_{\tau}^{2}(\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T};\mathbb{R}^{2})
, respectively.
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\tau}^{\prime},H^{s}=H_{\sigma}^{s}\cup H_{\tau}^{s}
are the spaces of test functions and Sobolev functions in
\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T}
, defined similarly for
s\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. We define the projection operators
\mathcal{P}_{\sigma},\mathcal{P}_{\tau}
in the following.
Let
u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T};\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Consider the elliptic problem

\begin{gathered}\Delta_{h}\psi_{u}=\int_{0}^{1}div_{h}u\,dz,\\
\begin{cases}\lim\limits_{|(x,y)|\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{u}=0&\text{in the case when}~\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
\int_{\Omega_{h}}\psi_{u}\,dxdy=0&\text{in the case when}~\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{T}^{2}.\end{cases}\end{gathered}

(2.1)

Let

\mathcal{P}_{\tau}u:=\nabla_{h}\psi_{u},~~\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}u:=u-\nabla_{h}\psi_{u}.

(2.2)

Then
\mathcal{P}_{\sigma},\mathcal{P}_{\tau}
are the projection operators from
C_{0}^{\infty}
to
C^{\infty}_{\sigma},C^{\infty}_{\tau}
, respectively. Also, by a density argument,
\mathcal{P}_{\sigma},\mathcal{P}_{\tau}
can act on functions in
L^{2},H^{s}
,
s\in(0,\infty)
.
In particular, the standard elliptic estimates yield, provided that
u\in H^{s}(\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T};\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
s\in(0,\infty)
,

\bigl|\psi_{u}\bigr|_{H^{s+1}}\lesssim\bigl|\overline{u}\bigr|_{H^{s}}\lesssim\bigl\Arrowvert u\bigr\Arrowvert_{H^{s}}.

Thus
\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}u\in H^{s}_{\sigma},\mathcal{P}_{\tau}u\in H^{s}_{\tau}
,
u=\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}u+\mathcal{P}_{\tau}u
and

\bigl\Arrowvert\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}u\bigr\Arrowvert_{H^{s}}\lesssim\bigl\Arrowvert u\bigr\Arrowvert_{H^{s}},~~\bigl\Arrowvert\mathcal{P}_{\tau}u\bigr\Arrowvert_{H^{s}}\lesssim\bigl\Arrowvert u\bigr\Arrowvert_{H^{s}}.

(2.3)

System (
1.4
) is complemented with initial data
(\xi_{0},v_{0})\in H^{2}(\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T};\mathbb{R})\times H^{2}(\Omega_{h}\times 2\mathbb{T};\mathbb{R}^{2})
with the compatibility conditions:

\partial_{z}\xi_{0}=0;~\text{$v_{0}$ is even in the $z$-variable.}

(2.4)

Also, in the case when
\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the far field condition

\lim\limits_{|(x,y)|\rightarrow\infty}|v_{\varepsilon}|=0,

(2.5)

is also imposed. We denote the constant
M\in(0,\infty)
satisfying

\dfrac{1}{2}\bigl\Arrowvert v_{0}\bigr\Arrowvert_{H^{2}}^{2}+\dfrac{c^{2}}{\gamma-1}\bigl\Arrowvert\xi_{0}\bigr\Arrowvert_{H^{2}}^{2}<M,

(2.6)

to be the bound of initial data
(\xi_{0},v_{0})
.
Now we describe our main theorems in this work. The first theorem is stating that the

\varepsilon

\mathcal{L}(t_{1}+t_{2})=\mathcal{L}(t_{1})\mathcal{L}(t_{2})
for
t_{1},t_{2}\in(0,\infty)
.
Then the solution
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}
of (
4.23
) can be represented as, using the Duhamel principle,

\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\mathcal{L}(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon})\mathcal{U}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}(\dfrac{t-s}{\varepsilon})\mathcal{G}(s)\,ds.

(4.28)

4.1
The case when
\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
: dispersion of the acoustic waves

In this section,
we shall establish the strong convergence of
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}
in the case when
\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
To be precise, we will show the following:

Proposition 5
.

Under the same assumptions as in Proposition
4
, in the case when
\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have

\xi_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0,~\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0~~\text{as}~\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}~~\text{in}~L^{2}(0,T;W^{\frac{1}{2},6}(\mathbb{R}^{2})).

(
2.12
)

In particular, we have, as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
,

\bigl\Arrowvert v_{\varepsilon}-v_{p}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{6}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}+\bigl\Arrowvert\xi_{\varepsilon}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}\rightarrow 0.

In order to show Proposition
5
, we will use the dispersion estimates of operator
\mathcal{L}
in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In particular, we will employ the Strichartz inequalities as in
[
10
]
, to derive the appropriate decay as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Indeed, let
\phi_{0}\in(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))^{3}
. The following inequality is from
[
10
, equation (3.4)]
:

\bigl|\mathcal{L}(t)\phi_{0}\bigr|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}\leq C_{p,q}\bigl|\phi_{0}\bigr|_{H^{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

(4.29)

provided that the right-hand side is finite, where
p,q>2
and
\eta\in(0,\infty)
satisfying

\dfrac{2}{q}=\dfrac{1}{2}-\dfrac{1}{p}~~~\text{and}~~~\eta q=3.

(4.30)

We will make use of (
4.29
) to derive the strong dispersion. To begin with, the following dispersion inequalities are analogies to inequalities (3.6) and (3.7) in
[
10
]
.

Lemma 1
.

For
\phi_{0}
and
\phi(s)\in(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))^{3}
,
p,q,\eta
satisfying (
4.30
) and
s\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathcal{L}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\phi_{0}\bigr|_{L^{q}(0,T;W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}\leq C\varepsilon^{1/q}\bigl|\phi_{0}\bigr|_{H^{\eta+s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

(4.31)

\displaystyle\bigl|\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}\bigl(\dfrac{t-s}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\phi(s)\,ds\bigr|_{L^{2}(0,T;W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~\leq C(1+T)\varepsilon^{1/q}\bigl|\phi\bigr|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{\eta+s}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))},

(4.32)

provided the right-hand sides are finite.

Proof.

Indeed (
4.31
) is a direct consequence of rescaling the temporal variable and replacing
\phi_{0}
with
(I-\Delta_{h})^{s/2}\phi_{0}
in (
4.29
). In order to show (
4.32
), notice that after applying the Minkowski and Hölder inequalities, one has

\bigl|\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}\bigl(\dfrac{t-s}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\phi(s)\,ds\bigr|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\leq Ct\int_{0}^{t}\bigl|\mathcal{L}\bigl(\dfrac{t-s}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\phi(s)\bigr|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\,ds.

Then by employing the Fubini theorem, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\bigl|\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}\bigl(\dfrac{t-s}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\phi(s)\,ds\bigr|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle~~~~\leq CT\int_{0}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}\bigl|\mathcal{L}\bigl(\dfrac{t-s}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\phi(s)\bigr|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\,dt\,ds

\displaystyle~~~~\leq CT\int_{0}^{T}(T-s)^{1-2/q}\biggl(\int_{s}^{T}\bigl|\mathcal{L}\bigl(\dfrac{t-s}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\phi(s)\bigr|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{q}\,dt\biggr)^{2/q}\,ds

\displaystyle~~~~\leq\varepsilon^{2/q}CT^{2-2/q}\int_{0}^{T}\bigl|\phi(s)\bigr|_{H^{\eta+s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\,ds=\varepsilon^{2/q}CT^{2-2/q}\bigl|\phi\bigr|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{\eta+s}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}^{q}.

This finishes the proof of (
4.32
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
5
.

Consider
\eta=\frac{1}{2},s=\frac{1}{2},q=6,p=6
in (
4.31
) and (
4.32
), which satisfy (
4.30
). Then from (
4.28
), after applying the triangle and Hölder inequalities,

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}\bigr|_{L^{2}(0,T;W^{\frac{1}{2},6}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}\lesssim T^{\frac{1}{3}}\bigl|\mathcal{L}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\mathcal{U}_{0}\bigr|_{L^{6}(0,T;W^{\frac{1}{2},6}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}

\displaystyle~~~~+\bigl|\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}\bigl(\dfrac{t-s}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\mathcal{G}(s)\,ds\bigr|_{L^{2}(0,T;W^{\frac{1}{2},6}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}\lesssim T^{\frac{1}{3}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{6}}\bigl|\mathcal{U}_{0}\bigr|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

\displaystyle~~~~+(1+T)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{6}}\bigl|\mathcal{G}\bigr|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}\lesssim(1+T)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{6}},

where the second and the third inequalities follow from (
4.31
), (
4.32
) and (
4.25
).
This yields (
2.12
) and finishes the proof of Proposition
5
.
∎

4.2
The case when
\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
: the fast oscillation

We will establish the convergence behavior of
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}
in the case when
\Omega_{h}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
in this subsection. Indeed, we shall investigate the fast oscillations of the acoustic waves as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
.
This is motivated by
[

]
(see also
[
16
]

The oscillation equations and the convergence of oscillations

To begin with,
\eqref{acoustic-wave}_{2}
can be written as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}+\dfrac{c^{2}}{\varepsilon}\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}-c_{1}(\mu\Delta_{h}\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}+\lambda\nabla_{h}div_{h}\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon})

(4.33)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{c^{2}(1-e^{\varepsilon\xi_{\varepsilon}})}{\varepsilon}\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}v_{\varepsilon})-\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(w_{\varepsilon}\partial_{z}v_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle+\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c_{1}(e^{-\varepsilon\alpha\xi_{\varepsilon}}-1)(\mu\Delta_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+\lambda\nabla_{h}div_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+\partial_{zz}v_{\varepsilon})).

Moreover, for any

\overline{u}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}u\,dz\in(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))^{2}
as a function on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times 2\mathbb{T}

\mathcal{P}_{\tau}u=\mathcal{P}_{\tau}\overline{u}

3.9
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(w_{\varepsilon}\partial_{z}v_{\varepsilon})=\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(-\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{z}w_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}\,dz)

(4.34)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(\int_{0}^{1}\bigl((\varepsilon\alpha\widetilde{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}+div_{h}\widetilde{v}_{\varepsilon})v_{\varepsilon}\bigr)\,dz)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(\int_{0}^{1}\bigl((\varepsilon\alpha\widetilde{\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}+div_{h}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}\bigr)\,dz)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\tau}((\varepsilon\alpha\widetilde{\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}+div_{h}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\tau}((e^{-\varepsilon\alpha\xi_{\varepsilon}}-1)\partial_{zz}v_{\varepsilon})=0,

where we have used the facts that
\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{P_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}}\,dz=0,\widetilde{v}_{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{P_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}}
and that
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon},\xi_{\varepsilon}
are independent of the
z
-variable.

On the other hand, notice that

\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}+\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}=div_{h}(\xi_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon})-\xi_{\varepsilon}div_{h}\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle~~~~+\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}.

\eqref{acoustic-wave}_{1}
can be written as

\partial_{t}\xi_{\varepsilon}+\dfrac{\gamma-1}{\varepsilon}div_{h}\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}=-div_{h}(\xi_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}\,dz)-\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}.

(4.35)

Additionally, integrating (
4.35
) in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
yields

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\xi_{\varepsilon}\,dxdy=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon})\,dxdy.

(4.36)

Consequently, after combining equations (
4.33
), (
4.34
), (
4.35
), (
4.36
), we have the following system of oscillations:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\xi_{\varepsilon}^{o}+\dfrac{\gamma-1}{\varepsilon}div_{h}\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}=-div_{h}(\xi_{\varepsilon}^{o}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}\,dz)\\
~~~~-\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}^{o}+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}^{o})\,dxdy&\text{in}~\mathbb{T}^{2},\\
\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}+\dfrac{c^{2}}{\varepsilon}\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}^{o}-c_{1}(\mu\Delta_{h}\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}+\lambda\nabla_{h}div_{h}\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon})\\
~~~~=-\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+(\varepsilon\alpha\widetilde{\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon})\\
~~~~+\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(div_{h}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon})+\dfrac{c^{2}(1-e^{\varepsilon\xi_{\varepsilon}})}{\varepsilon}\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}^{o}\\
~~~~+\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c_{1}(e^{-\varepsilon\alpha\xi_{\varepsilon}}-1)(\mu\Delta_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+\lambda\nabla_{h}div_{h}v_{\varepsilon}))&\text{in}~\mathbb{T}^{2},\end{cases}

(4.37)

where

\xi_{\varepsilon}^{o}:=\xi_{\varepsilon}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\xi_{\varepsilon}\,dxdy.

(4.38)

In the following, denote by

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}^{o}:=\biggl(\begin{array}[]{c}\xi_{\varepsilon}^{o}\\
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}\end{array}\biggr)=\biggl(\begin{array}[]{c}\xi_{\varepsilon}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\xi_{\varepsilon}\,dxdy\\
\nabla_{h}\psi_{\varepsilon}\end{array}\biggr),~~g_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\xi_{\varepsilon}\,dxdy,~~~~\text{and}\\
V_{\varepsilon}^{o}:=\mathcal{L}\bigl(-\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}^{o}=\biggl(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{L}_{1}\bigl(-\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}^{o}\\
\mathcal{L}_{2}\bigl(-\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}^{o}\end{array}\biggr)~~~~\bigl(\text{so}~\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}^{o}=\mathcal{L}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr),\end{gathered}

(4.39)

where
\mathcal{L}_{1}:(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))^{3}\mapsto\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),~\mathcal{L}_{2}:(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))^{3}\mapsto(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))^{2}
will be referred to as the first and the second components of
\mathcal{L}
, respectively.
Then
V_{\varepsilon}^{o}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}V_{\varepsilon}^{o}+\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,1}(\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon}^{o})+\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,2}(V_{\varepsilon}^{o},V_{\varepsilon}^{o})+\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,3}(g_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon}^{o})-\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(D)V_{\varepsilon}^{o}

(4.40)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\bigl(-\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\biggl(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
K+L_{1}+L_{2}\end{array}\biggr),

where the linear and bi-linear operators are defined as follows: for
V_{1},V_{2}\in(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))^{3},u\in(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times 2\mathbb{T}))^{2},g\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(D)V_{1}:=\mathcal{L}\bigl(-\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\biggl(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
c_{1}(\mu\Delta_{h}\mathcal{L}_{2}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{1}+\lambda\nabla_{h}div_{h}\mathcal{L}_{2}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{1})\end{array}\biggr),

(4.41)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,1}(\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}u,V_{1}):=\mathcal{L}\bigl(-\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\biggl(\begin{array}[]{c}div_{h}(\mathcal{L}_{1}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}u\,dz)\\
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}u\cdot\nabla_{h}\mathcal{L}_{2}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{1}+\mathcal{L}_{2}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{1}\cdot\nabla_{h}\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}u)\end{array}\biggr),

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,2}(V_{1},V_{2})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{L}\bigl(-\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\biggl(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{L}_{2}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{1}\cdot\nabla_{h}\mathcal{L}_{1}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{2}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathcal{L}_{2}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{1}\cdot\nabla_{h}\mathcal{L}_{1}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{2}\,dxdy\\

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,3}(g,V_{1}):=\mathcal{L}\bigl(-\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\biggl(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c^{2}g\nabla_{h}\mathcal{L}_{1}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{1})\end{array}\biggr).

Here,

\displaystyle K:=\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(-\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{h}\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}-div_{h}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}}P_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}),

(4.42)

\displaystyle L_{1}:=-\varepsilon\alpha\mathcal{P}_{\tau}((\widetilde{\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon})+\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(\dfrac{c^{2}(1+\varepsilon\xi_{\varepsilon}-e^{\varepsilon\xi_{\varepsilon}})}{\varepsilon}\nabla_{h}\xi_{\varepsilon}^{o}),

\displaystyle L_{2}:=\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c_{1}(e^{-\varepsilon\alpha\xi_{\varepsilon}}-1)(\mu\Delta_{h}v_{\varepsilon}+\lambda\nabla_{h}div_{h}v_{\varepsilon})).

Also
g_{\varepsilon}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\xi_{\varepsilon}\,dxdy
satisfies, from (
4.36

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}g_{\varepsilon}=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}((\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}^{o})_{2}\cdot\nabla_{h}(\mathcal{U}^{o}_{\varepsilon})_{1})\,dxdy

(4.43)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathcal{L}_{2}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\cdot\nabla_{h}\mathcal{L}_{1}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\,dxdy,

\displaystyle\text{with}~~g_{\varepsilon}(t=0)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\xi_{0}\,dxdy.

Now we will address the strong convergence of
V_{\varepsilon}^{o},g_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Notice that
L
defined in (
4.22
) is anti-symmetric with respect to
A:=\mathrm{diag}(c^{2},\gamma-1,\gamma-1)
(i.e.,
\int V^{\top}ALU\,d\vec{x}=-\int U^{\top}ALV\,d\vec{x}
), linear and commutative with
\partial_{h}\in\{\partial_{x},\partial_{y}\}
. Consequently the standard
H^{s}
estimate of the solutions to equation (
4.27
) implies that the operator
\mathcal{L}
preserves the
H^{s}
norm, i.e.,

\bigl|\mathcal{L}(t)V\bigr|_{H^{s}}\simeq\bigl|V\bigr|_{H^{s}}~\text{for}~t\in(0,\infty),s\in\mathbb{N},V\in(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))^{3}.

(4.44)

Therefore one has, as the consequence of (
4.25
) and (
4.39

\bigl|V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}+\bigl|\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}+\bigl|(\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}^{o})_{2}\bigr|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}<C.

(4.45)

Then it is easy to see from (
4.43

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\{|g_{\varepsilon}|+|\dfrac{d}{dt}g_{\varepsilon}|\}\leq(1+T)\bigl|\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}^{2}<C.

(4.46)

\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
,

g_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow g^{o}~\text{uniformly for some}~g^{o}\in C([0,T]),

(4.47)

and
|g^{o}|<C
. On the other hand, after applying the Hölder and Sobolev embedding inequalities, (
4.1
), (
4.41
), (
4.45
), and (
4.46
) imply the following estimates:

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(D)V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\bigl|V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

(4.48)

\displaystyle+\bigl|(\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}^{o})_{2}\bigr|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}<C,

\displaystyle\bigl|Q_{\varepsilon,1}(\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon}^{o})\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim\bigl|V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\times\bigl\Arrowvert\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}<C,

\displaystyle\bigl|Q_{\varepsilon,2}(V_{\varepsilon}^{o},V_{\varepsilon}^{o})\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim\bigl|V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}^{2}<C,

\displaystyle\bigl|Q_{\varepsilon,3}(g_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon}^{o})\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|g_{\varepsilon}|

\displaystyle\times\bigl|V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}<C,

and

\displaystyle\bigl|K\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim\bigl\Arrowvert\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}^{2}<C,

(4.49)

\displaystyle\bigl|L_{1}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim\varepsilon\bigl\Arrowvert\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}^{2}\bigl\Arrowvert\xi_{\varepsilon}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon e^{\varepsilon\bigl\Arrowvert\xi_{\varepsilon}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}}(\bigl\Arrowvert\xi_{\varepsilon}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}^{2}+1)<\varepsilon C,

\displaystyle\bigl|L_{2}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim\varepsilon e^{\varepsilon\bigl\Arrowvert\xi_{\varepsilon}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}}

\displaystyle\times(\bigl\Arrowvert\xi_{\varepsilon}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}+1)\bigl\Arrowvert v_{\varepsilon}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle<\varepsilon C.

Therefore, equation (
4.40
) implies

\bigl|\partial_{t}V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}<C.

Then the Aubin compactness lemma (see, i.e.,
[

\begin{gathered}V^{o}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\cap C(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))~~\text{with}\\

such that as

\begin{gathered}V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\rightharpoonup}\limits^{\ast}}V^{o}~~\text{weak-$\ast$ in}~L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})),\\

V_{\varepsilon}^{o}(t=0)=V^{o}(t=0)=\biggl(\begin{array}[]{c}\xi_{0}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\xi_{0}\,dxdy\\
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}v_{0}\end{array}\biggr).

(4.52)

, since

(4.53)

In conclusion, as

\displaystyle\bigl\Arrowvert v_{\varepsilon}-v_{p}-\mathcal{L}_{2}\bigl(\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\bigr)V^{o}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times 2\mathbb{T}))}

(4.54)

K+L_{1}+L_{2}\end{array}\biggr).

Then, from the regularity in (

We claim that the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle~~~~\times\bigl\Arrowvert\nabla_{h}(\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}-v_{p})\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}+\bigl|\nabla_{h}\mathcal{L}(\frac{t}{\varepsilon})V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle~~~~\times\bigl\Arrowvert\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}-v_{p}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim\bigl|V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle~~~~\times\bigl\Arrowvert\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}v_{\varepsilon}-v_{p}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\rightarrow 0,

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,1}(v_{p},V_{\varepsilon}^{o}-V^{o})\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim\bigl|V_{\varepsilon}^{o}-V^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle~~~~\times\bigl\Arrowvert v_{p}\bigr\Arrowvert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\rightarrow 0,

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,2}(V_{\varepsilon}^{o}-V^{o},V_{\varepsilon}^{o})\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim\bigl|\mathcal{L}(\frac{t}{\varepsilon})(V_{\varepsilon}^{o}-V^{o})\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle~~~~\times\bigl|\nabla_{h}\mathcal{L}(\frac{t}{\varepsilon})V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim\bigl|V_{\varepsilon}^{o}-V^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle~~~~\times\bigl|V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\rightarrow 0,

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,2}(V^{o},V_{\varepsilon}^{o}-V^{o})\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim\bigl|V^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle~~~~\times\bigl|V_{\varepsilon}^{o}-V^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\rightarrow 0,

\displaystyle~~~~\times\bigl|V_{\varepsilon}^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\rightarrow 0,

\displaystyle~~~~\times\bigl|V_{\varepsilon}^{o}-V^{o}\bigr|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\rightarrow 0,

4.47

4.50

\displaystyle 0=\bigl|V_{1}+\mathcal{L}(t)V_{2}\bigr|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}-\bigl|\mathcal{L}(-t)V_{1}+V_{2}\bigr|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}
