Efficient Nonlinear MPC by Leveraging LPV Embedding and Sequential Quadratic Programming footnoteinfo This work was funded by the German Research Foundation (DFG), project number 419290163.
Dimitrios S. Karachalios, Hossam S. Abbas

Abstract
In this paper, we present efficient solutions for the nonlinear program (NLP) associated with nonlinear model predictive control (NMPC) by leveraging the linear parameter-varying (LPV) embedding of nonlinear models and sequential quadratic programming (SQP). The corresponding quadratic program (QP) subproblem is systematically constructed and efficiently updated using the scheduling parameter from the LPV embedding, enabling fast convergence while adapting to the behavior of the controlled system. Furthermore, the approach provides insight into the problem, its connection to SQP, and a clearer understanding of the differences between solving NMPC as an NLP and using the LPV-MPC approach, compared to similar methods in the literature. The efficiency of the proposed approach is demonstrated against state-of-the-art methods, including NLP algorithms, in control benchmarks and practical applications.



keywords

Linear parametrically varying methodologies, Control of constrained systems, Nonlinear predictive control, Optimal control, Quadratic and nonlinear programming.

Introduction

Nowadays, model predictive control (MPC) is becoming one of the most essential modern control strategies in practice and research
(
Qin and Badgwell 2003
;
Rawlings and Mayne 2017
)
. One strength of MPC is its simple concept, which can accommodate variant classes of control problems with constraints. Its strategy is to compute the control trajectory after solving constrained optimization problems within a reasonable sampling time. Often, nonlinear (NL) MPC (NMPC) is more relevant in practice due to the inevitable emergence of nonlinear models in interesting applications.
However, implementing NMPC in practice by casting its optimization
as a nonlinear program (NLP) to control high-order nonlinear systems with long prediction horizons and to achieve complex tasks, such as trajectory planning
(
Alcalá et al. 2020b
)
, remains a challenge.

A simple and practical approach to mitigating the computational complexity of NMPC is to use the linear parameter-varying (LPV) framework
(
Tóth 2010
)
by introducing the so-called scheduling parameter
p
, which depends on the state of the system and/or the input, and its value is assumed to be measurable (available) at every sampling instant. If the values of the scheduling parameter over the MPC prediction horizon
N
are known, the MPC problem can be solved as a single simple quadratic program (QP), for which efficient solvers are available to achieve optimal real-time solutions.
However, due to the dependence of
p
on the
state and/or the input, unavailable before solving the optimization problem, different techniques have been employed to handle the prediction of the scheduling parameter with successful applications, see
Morato et al. 2020
.

To ensure low computational complexity, the underlying idea of LPVMPC is to solve its optimization problem as a single QP. To address the unknown values of
p
over
N
, the following methods have been introduced:
(1) They can be frozen at the current measured value. This leads to the so-called gain-scheduling predictive control method, e.g.,
Gidon et al. 2021
.
(2) They can be derived from the predictions of the MPC solution at the previous time step
(
Nezami et al. 2023
;
Alcalá et al. 2020a
;
Karachalios and Abbas 2024
)
.
(3) They can be approximated based on local Taylor expansions of
the scheduling function that defines
p
as a function of the state/input
(
Morato et al. 2023
)
.
(4) They can be considered uncertain, with an uncertainty bound based on the rate of variation of
p
. Typically, robust MPC addresses such uncertainty, e.g., tube-based MPC in
Hanema et al. 2016
;
Abbas 2024
.
The latter approach is usually the most computationally expansive due to the online construction of tubes
(
Karachalios et al. 2024
)
; however, it provides theoretical guarantees on stability and recursive feasibility.
An alternative approach was introduced in
Cisneros et al. 2016
, the qLMPC, based on solving the MPC optimization problem as a sequence of QPs based on method (2), updating the values of
p
over
N
iteratively until convergence.
The qLMPC performed better than the LPVMPC with a single QP at the expense of increasing the computational burden, which is still less than casting the problem as an NLP. The convergence properties have been studied in
Hespe and Werner 2021
. Since it solves a sequence of QPs at each MPC time interval, it resembles a sequential quadratic programming (SQP) method for solving NLPs
(
Nocedal and Wright 2006
)
. However, it does not directly provide insight into this connection to understand better the differences between solving NMPC as an NLP and using the LPVMPC approach, which can help evaluate and understand the suboptimality when considering the LPVMPC.

This paper considers the LPV framework for efficiently and cost-effectively solving NMPC, adopting a multi-QP approach similar to qLMPC. However, we introduce a more efficient and pragmatic alternative to qLMPC by directly leveraging LPVMPC with SQP
(
Nocedal and Wright 2006
)
to solve NMPC optimization problems. This can provide better insight into the differences between NLP-based NMPC and LPVMPC. Moreover, it can also incorporate other SQP tools, such as the globalization of SQP methods
(
Nocedal and Wright 2006
)
, leading to faster convergence with guarantees. We will consider both non-condensed and condensed forms of the optimization problem. Moreover, we demonstrate the improvement of the solution based on the proposed approach through practical applications in simulations, showing a reduction in computational complexity, significantly faster convergence, and solutions that closely match those of the original NLP compared to state-of-the-art algorithms.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: preliminaries and the problem setup in Sec.
2
, the proposed approach is presented in Sec.
3
, numerical examples in Sec.
4
, and conclusions in Sec.
5
.

Preliminaries and Problem Setup

2.1
LPV-based MPC and QP Formulations

An LPV discrete-time system is represented as

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A(p_{k})x_{k}+B(p_{k})u_{k},

(1a)

\displaystyle p_{k}

\displaystyle=\rho(x_{k},u_{k}),

(1b)

where
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state of the system,
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the input, and
p_{k}\in\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{p}}}
is a measurable, time-varying scheduling parameter that
belongs to a compact and convex set
\mathcal{P}
of its admissible values for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. The system matrices

A:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},~B:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
are assumed to be bounded due to the compactness of
\mathcal{P}
with affine dpendence on
p_{k}
, and
\rho\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{P})
. It is assumed in this work that the LPV representation (
1
) provides an exact embedding
of the dynamics of an NL system. For details about the embedding of NL dynamics in the form (
1
), see
Kwiatkowski et al. 2006
and
Tóth 2010
.

The LPVMPC is carried out by solving the following optimization problem online at each time instant
t_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where
t_{k}=k\cdot t_{s}
and
t_{s}
denotes the sample time,

\displaystyle\min_{u_{0},u_{1},\cdots,x_{0},x_{1},\cdots}

\displaystyle\quad x_{N}^{\top}Qx_{N}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}x_{k}^{\top}Qx_{k}+u_{k}^{\top}Ru_{k}

(2a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad x_{0}=x(t_{k})

(2b)

\displaystyle\quad x_{k+1}=A(p_{k})x_{k}+B(p_{k})u_{k}

(2c)

\displaystyle\quad x_{k}\in\mathcal{X},~k=1,~\ldots,N

(2d)

\displaystyle\quad u_{k}\in\mathcal{U},~k=0,~\ldots,N-1

(2e)

where
Q\succeq 0
and
R\succ 0
are the quadratic cost weights,
x(t_{k})
is the current state of the system and
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{U}
the state and input constraint sets are receptively represented by linear inequalities, see (
22
). Terminal conditions can be included in (
2
) to ensure recursive feasibility of the optimization problem and asymptotic stability of the closed-loop system with LPVMPC, typically computed offline using LMIs, see
Morato et al. 2020
for more details; however, they are not considered in this work. Since the LPV model (
2
c) is assumed to be an exact embedding of nonlinear dynamics, by replacing
p
with
\rho(x,u)
, the optimization problem (
2
) becomes equivalent to an NMPC.

Two formulations are presented below to introduce the proposed approach for solving (
2
).

2.1.1
Noncondensed Form

By introducing the following augmented vectors:

\bar{x}=\left[\begin{matrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{N}\end{matrix}\right],\quad\bar{u}=\left[\begin{matrix}u_{0}\\
u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
u_{N-1}\end{matrix}\right],\quad\bar{p}=\left[\begin{matrix}p_{0}\\
p_{1}\\
\vdots\\
p_{N-1}\end{matrix}\right],

(3)

we can reformulate (
2
) in a sparse, structured optimization problem where the underlying decision variables depend on both the input and state vectors. Moreover, define the augmented input-state decision vector as
z=[\bar{u}^{\top}~~\bar{x}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N(m+n)}
. The optimization problem (
2
) becomes

\displaystyle\min_{z}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}{M}z

(4a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad{C}(\bar{p})z=b_{x_{0}},

(4b)

\displaystyle\quad{G}^{\rm z}z\leq{h}^{\rm z},

(4c)

where
M
and
D
are defined, respectively, as

\displaystyle{M}

\displaystyle:=2\cdot\text{blkdiag}\left(\mathbb{I}_{N}\otimes R,\;\mathbb{I}_{N}\otimes Q\right),

\displaystyle b_{x_{0}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}-A^{\top}(p_{0})x_{0}&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

and
C(\bar{p})
,
{G}^{\rm z}
,
{h}^{\rm z}
are given in Appendix
A
. The optimization problem (
4
) is now a function of
\bar{p}
and since

M\succeq 0
, if
\bar{p}
is known, (
4
) becomes a standard QP, which can be solved at each time instant.

2.1.2
Condensed Form

By expressing
\bar{x}
in (
3
) in terms of
x_{0},\bar{u}
the perdition model over
N
is given as

\bar{x}=\bar{A}(\bar{p})x_{0}+\bar{B}(\bar{p})\bar{u},

(5)

where
\bar{A},~\bar{B}
are augmented matrices as
given in Appendix
A
.
Consequently, the cost function (
2
a) can be written solely in
\bar{u}
. By collecting all the input and state constraints in one inequality constraint of
\bar{u}
, the constrained optimization problem (
2
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\min_{\bar{u}}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}\bar{u}^{\top}H(\bar{p})\bar{u}+x_{0}^{\top}g(\bar{p})\bar{u}

(6a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad{G}^{\rm u}(\bar{p})\bar{u}\leq{h}^{\rm u}(\bar{p}),

(6b)

where

H(\bar{p})\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{Nm}\\
\bar{B}(\bar{p})\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\!\!M\!\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{Nm}\\
\bar{B}(\bar{p})\end{bmatrix},\;\;g(\bar{p})\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}0_{Nm}\\
\bar{A}(\bar{p})\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\!\!M\!\begin{bmatrix}0_{Nm}\\
\bar{B}(\bar{p})\end{bmatrix}

and
{G}^{\rm u}(\bar{p})
,
{h}^{\rm u}(\bar{p})
are given in Appendix
A
. With
H(\bar{p})\succeq 0
,
\forall\bar{p}
, if
\bar{p}
is known, (
6
) becomes a standard QP.

Generally, the noncondensed form is numerically superior if the underlying solver can exploit sparsity; otherwise, the condensed form is preferable. The formulations (
4
) and (
6
) are used in the next section for introducing the proposed approach.

2.2
Basic SQP for solving NLP

Next, the basic SQP method
(
Nocedal and Wright 2006
)
is reviewed. It is widely used for solving NLP problems in optimal control and NMPC. Under certain regularity conditions, SQP guarantees local convergence to a solution of the NLP problem. Next, we illustrate the idea of the SQP approach using the noncondensed form (
4
)
1
1

1

Similar analysis can be performed using the condensed form (
6
).
, which, since
p=\rho(x,u)
, it can be reformulated in terms of
z
as follows

\displaystyle\min_{z}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}{M}z

(7a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad{C}(\bar{p}(z))z=b_{x_{0}},

(7b)

\displaystyle\quad{G}^{\rm z}z\leq{h}^{\rm z}.

(7c)

Therefore, (
7
) is an NLP and can be solved using SQP.
The principle idea is to successively linearize the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions of (
7
) to construct a sequence of QP subproblems. In the following, the dependence of
\bar{p}(z)
is omitted for simplicity of notation. The Lagrangian of the NLP (
7
) is given as

\mathcal{L}(z,\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}{M}z+\lambda^{\top}({C}(z)z-{b}_{x_{0}})+\mu^{\top}({G}^{\rm z}z-{h}^{\rm z}),

(8)

where
\lambda
and
\mu
are the Lagrange multipliers for the equality and inequality constraints, respectively. The SQP method then iteratively solves (
7
), yielding the updates

z^{l+1}=z^{l}+d^{l},\quad\lambda^{l+1}=\delta^{l},\quad\mu^{l+1}=\eta^{l},

(9)

where the current iterate

(d^{l},\delta^{l},\eta^{l})
is the solution of the following QP subproblem

\displaystyle\min_{d^{l}}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}{d^{l}}^{\top}\nabla^{2}_{z}\mathcal{L}(z^{l},\lambda^{l},\mu^{l})d^{l}+\left(MZ^{l}\right)^{\top}d^{l}

(10a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\partial[{C}(z^{l})z^{l}]}{\partial z}d^{l}=b_{x_{0}}-{C}(z^{l})z^{l},

(10b)

\displaystyle\quad{G}^{\rm z}d^{l}\leq{h}^{\rm z}-{G}^{\rm z}z^{l},

(10c)

where
\nabla^{2}_{z}\mathcal{L}(z^{l},\lambda^{l},\mu^{l})
is the Hessian
of the Lagrangian (
8
) and
\frac{\partial[{C}(z^{l})z^{l}]}{\partial z}
represents the Jacobian of the equality constraint
(
7
b), evaluated at the iterate (
z^{l},\lambda^{l},mu^{l}
). Under certain regularity assumptions, including the continuity and differentiability of the optimization problem and the satisfaction of constraint qualifications, SQP is guaranteed to converge to the optimal local solution
(z^{\ast},\lambda^{\ast},\mu^{\ast})
, provided that the initialization is sufficiently close to the solution, see
Nocedal and Wright 2006
for more details. However, the computational complexity of computing the Hessian and Jacobian at every iteration is one of the main challenges of SQP. To mitigate this, inexact formulations of these terms are commonly considered to reduce this complexity as shown in
Bock et al. 2007
, see also
Bock et al. 2007
;
Rawlings and Mayne 2017
. However, this often leads to suboptimal solutions.

In this work, based on the LPVMPC formulation, we present a systematic approach that can be seen as solving SQP using inexact Hessians and Jacobians, which can be easily updated using the scheduling parameter, enabling inexpensive construction of the QP subproblems and leading to suboptimal solutions closer to the optimal ones.

3
Proposed Approach

In this section, we propose an approach based on the LPV formulation to solve the NLP associated with (
2
) using approximate SQP methods, where inexact Hessians and Jacobians depend on the scheduling parameter according to the LPV embedding used to represent the nonlinear model. We apply this approach to both the noncondensed and the condensed forms.

Consider the noncondensed form (
7
) and introduce the following inexact Hessian and Jacobian for (
10
)

\nabla^{2}_{z}\mathcal{L}(z^{l},\lambda^{l},\mu^{l})\approx{M},\quad\frac{\partial[{C}(\bar{p}(z^{l}))z^{l}]}{\partial z}\approx{C}(\bar{p}^{l}),

(11)

where
\bar{p}^{l}=\bar{p}(z^{l})
.
This leads to the following approximate QP subproblem

\displaystyle\min_{d^{l}}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}{d^{l}}^{\top}{M}d^{l}+({M}z^{l})^{\top}d^{l}

(12a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad{C}(\bar{p}^{l})d^{l}=b_{x_{0}}-{C}(\bar{p}^{l})z^{l},

(12b)

\displaystyle\quad{G}^{\rm z}d^{l}\leq{h}^{\rm z}-{G}^{\rm z}z^{l},

(12c)

which should be solved iteratively given
z^{l}
and
\bar{p}^{l}
, updating
z^{l+1}=d^{l}+z^{l}
and
\bar{p}^{l+1}
accordingly until
d^{l}
converges to zero. It is worth mentioning that if such an iterative QP converges, it will not exactly reach the solution of (
7
) but rather a suboptimal one. Conditions similar to those used for analyzing the convergence properties of NLP algorithms with inexact Hessians and Jacobians, as discussed in
Bock et al. 2007
, can be applied to show convergence proprieties of (
12
), see also
Hespe and Werner 2021
. However, this paper focuses on introducing the approach, while the convergence properties will be addressed in a future study.

Note that adding the constant term
{z^{l}}^{\top}{M}z^{l}
to the cost function (
12
a)
does not affect the solution; however, it allows the expression to be written as
{z^{l+1}}^{\top}{M}z^{l+1}
. Then, by substituting
d^{l}+z^{l}
with
z^{l+1}
in (
12
b,c), we recover the LPVMPC nonconduced form in (
4
), where the QP being directly scheduled with
\bar{p}
. This provides insight into the proposed inexact Hessian and Jacobian in (
11
), enabling a systematic and cost-effective construction and update of the QP subproblem without explicitly forming these inexact components.
It is not just a simple approximation; it also offers meaningful insight into the control problem, as it allows the inexact Hessian and Jacobian to adapt to the behavior of the controlled system through the choice of scheduling parameter.

Next, we consider the condensed form in (
6
) in its NLP formulation by directly rewriting it in terms of
\bar{u}
, since
\bar{p}=\bar{p}(\bar{u})
, as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\bar{u}}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}\bar{u}^{\top}H(\bar{u})\bar{u}+x_{0}^{\top}g(\bar{u})\bar{u},

(13a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad{G}^{\rm u}(\bar{u})\bar{u}\leq{h}^{\rm u}(\bar{u}).

(13b)

Let
a(\bar{u})=\frac{1}{2}\bar{u}^{\top}H(\bar{u})\bar{u}+x_{0}^{\top}g(\bar{u})\bar{u}
, then the Lagrangian of (
13
) is given by

\mathcal{L}(\bar{u},\mu)=a(\bar{u})+\mu^{\top}\left({G}^{\rm u}(\bar{u})\bar{u}-{h}^{\rm u}(\bar{u})\right).

(14)

The SQP method for solving the NLP (
13
) results in an iteration update by solving the following QP subproblem

\displaystyle\min_{d^{l}}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}{d^{l}}^{\top}\nabla_{\bar{u}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\bar{u}^{l},\mu^{l})d^{l}+\nabla_{\bar{u}}a(\bar{u}^{l})^{\top}d^{l}

(15a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\partial[{G}^{\rm u}(\bar{u}^{l})\bar{u}^{l}-{h}^{\rm u}(\bar{u}^{l})]}{\partial\bar{u}}d^{l}\leq{h}^{\rm u}(\bar{u}^{l})-{G}^{\rm u}(\bar{u}^{l})\bar{u}^{l}.

(15b)

Under a regularity assumption for the solution of (
13
) and with exact Hessian, gradient, and Jacobian, it is guaranteed that solving (
15
) iteratively will converge to the optimal local solution of (
13
), provided a reasonable initialization close to the solution. However, the exact Hessian of the Lagrangian, the gradient of the cost function, and the Jacobian of the inequality constraints are quite complex, highlighting the difficulty of solving the NLP (
13
) based on the QP subproblem in
15
. To avoid such complexity, we propose the following approximations.

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bar{u}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\bar{u}^{l},\mu^{l})

\displaystyle\approx H(\bar{p}^{l}),

(16a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bar{u}}f(\bar{u}^{l})

\displaystyle\approx H(\bar{p}^{l})\bar{u}^{l}+g^{\top}(\bar{p}^{l})x_{0},

(16b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial[{G}^{\rm u}(\bar{u}^{l})\bar{u}^{l}-{h}^{\rm u}(\bar{u}^{l})]}{\partial\bar{u}}

\displaystyle\approx{G}^{\rm u}(\bar{p}^{l}),

(16c)

where the dependence in the right-hand side is now written in terms of
\bar{p}
, since
\bar{p}=\bar{p}(\bar{u})
, and the explicit dependence on
\bar{u}
is dropped.
These approximations yield the following condensed QP subproblem

\displaystyle\min_{d^{l}}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}{d^{l}}^{\top}H(\bar{p}^{l})d^{l}+\left(H(\bar{u}^{l})\bar{p}^{l}+g^{\top}(\bar{p}^{l})x_{0}\right)^{\top}d^{l}

(17a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad{G}^{\rm u}(\bar{p}^{l})d^{l}\leq{h}^{\rm u}(\bar{p}^{l})-{G}^{\rm u}(\bar{p}^{l})\bar{u}^{l}.

(17b)

As demonstrated in the noncondensed form, it is straightforward to show how (
6
) can be recovered from (
17
), leading to the intuition behind the approximations considered in (
16
).
Considering (
17
) iteratively for solving (
13
), convergence to a suboptimal solution holds once
d^{l}\approx 0
. The convergence properties can also be analyzed using the approach of
Bock et al. 2007
.

It is important to note that iteratively solving the QP subproblem in (
12
)/(
17
), which, if it converges, yields a solution that differs from that of (
7
)/(
13
) due to the approximations employed. However, it turns out that, through several numerical applications, the suboptimal solutions based on (
12
)/(
17
) usually lead to solutions very close to the optimal ones, see Section
4
.

Remark 1

It is worth mentioning that the qLMPC approach of
Cisneros et al. 2016
is based on iteratively solving QPs, with updates of
z^{l+1}/\bar{u}^{l+1}
according to the condensed form. In each iteration,
\bar{p}^{l}
is used to compute the current QP subproblem. However, the convergence of the qLMPC is achieved once the difference between
\bar{p}^{l+1}
and
\bar{p}^{l}
is smaller than a given stopping criterion. The convergence properties of this scheme have been studied in
Hespe and Werner 2021
. The key difference between the proposed approach here and the qLMPC is that solving the QP subproblem in our approach is based on the increment
d^{l}
, as proposed in (
12
) and (
17
) leading to a numerically robust scheme and can yield better suboptimal solutions, as demonstrated in the numerical simulations presented in the next section. Furthermore, it provides better insight into the problem, its connection to SQP, and a meaningful understanding of the difference between solving the NLP and considering the LPVMPC approach with the approximate SQP.

NUMERICAL EXAMPLES

This section tests the proposed approach, referred to as LPVMPC-SQP, against established benchmarks. The simulations are performed on a Dell Latitude 5590 laptop with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-8650U CPU and 16 GB of RAM. They are implemented in MATLAB. The LPVMPC-SQP approach is implemented using the
quadprog
command based on the
active-set
algorithm to solve the QP subproblem; therefore, execution follows the condensed form (
17
).

4.1
Example 1 - The forced Van der Pol oscillator

We start with the forced Van der Pol oscillator, a well-studied and challenging benchmark
(
Nguyen-Van and Hori 2014
)
.
The continuous-time nonlinear ordinary differential equation of the system is given by

\ddot{y}(t)=(1-y^{2}(t))\dot{y}(t)-y(t)+u(t),

(18)

where

The resulting closed-loop state trajectories are shown in the phase plot in Fig.

Example 2 - The dynamic unicycle

The second example concerns a dynamic unicycle system borrowed from

Conclusion

In this work, we proposed an approach for obtaining an efficient approximate solution to NMPC by combining LPVMPC with SQP. The approach leads to an associated inexact Hessian and Jacobian for the optimization problem, which depends on the scheduling parameter in the LPV embedding of the nonlinear MPC model. This enables a cost-effective update of these components throughout the SQP iterations. Based on several simulations with different benchmarks and comparisons with state-of-the-art approaches, LPVMPC-SQP demonstrates numerical advantages over NLP methods, which require costly updates of the exact Jacobian and Hessian leading to suboptimal solutions close to the optimal ones.

The approach can be extended to different SQP tools, such as globalization techniques for SQP methods, leading to even faster convergence with guarantees. Finally, further analysis is required to establish sufficient conditions for proving convergence, providing a theoretical justification for the proposed method.
