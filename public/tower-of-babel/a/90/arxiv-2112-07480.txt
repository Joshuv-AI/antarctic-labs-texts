Weak-strong uniqueness and energy-variational solutions for a class of viscoelastoplastic fluid models
Thomas Eiter, Katharina Hopf, Robert Lasarzik

Abstract
We study a model for a fluid showing viscoelastic and viscoplastic behavior, which describes the flow in terms of the fluid velocity and an internal stress. This stress tensor is transported via the Zaremba–Jaumann rate, and it is subject to two dissipation processes: one induced by a nonsmooth convex potential and one by stress diffusion. We show short-time existence of strong solutions as well as their uniqueness in a class of Leray–Hopf type weak solutions satisfying the tensorial component in the sense of an evolutionary variational inequality. The global-in-time existence of such generalized solutions has been established in a previous work. We further study the limit when stress diffusion vanishes. In this case, the above notion of generalized solutions is no longer suitable, and we introduce the concept of energy-variational solutions, which is based on an inequality for the relative energy. We derive general properties of energy-variational solutions and show their existence by passing to the non-diffusive limit in the relative energy inequality satisfied by generalized solutions for non-zero stress diffusion.

Introduction

In a three-dimensional bounded domain
\Omega\subset\R^{3}
,
we consider the flow of an incompressible viscoelastoplastic fluid
governed by the equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{v}}-\di\left(\eta\mathbb{S}+2\mu(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\right)+\nabla p={\boldsymbol{f}},\hskip 10.00002pt\di{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Se+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\Se+\Se(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\Se+\partial\mathcal{P}(\Se)-\gamma\Delta\Se

\displaystyle\ni\eta(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(1b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}=0,\hskip 20.00003pt\gamma\,\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(1c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot,0)=\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbb{S}(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1d)

where

is a time interval
with

.
Equation (
1a
)
describes the evolution of the fluid flow
(with constant density
\rho=1
)
in terms of the Eulerian velocity
\boldsymbol{v}\colon\Omega\times(0,T)\to\R^{3}
and pressure
p\colon\Omega\times(0,T)\to\R
,
subject to an external forcing
\boldsymbol{f}\colon\Omega\times(0,T)\to\R^{3}
.
The fluid stress
\mathbb{T}=\eta\mathbb{S}+2\mu(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}-p\mathbb{I}

decomposes into the classical term
2\mu(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}-p\mathbb{I}
for Newtonian fluids
and an additional stress
\mathbb{S}\colon\Omega\times(0,T)\to\R^{3\times 3}
.
Here
(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}+\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{\top})
is the rate-of-strain tensor,
and
\mu>0,\,\eta\geq 0
denote fixed constants.
Since
p
shall describe the physical pressure, that is,
the (negative of the) spherical part of the fluid stress,
we assume
\mathbb{S}
to be a symmetric deviatoric tensor field, that is,

\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{S}^{\top}
and
\trace\mathbb{S}=0
.
By equation (
1b
), the extra stress
\mathbb{S}

is transported via the Zaremba–Jaumann rate

\overset{\triangledown}{\mathbb{S}}\coloneqq\partial_{t}\Se+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\Se+\Se(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\Se,

(2)

where
(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}-\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{\top})
.
Moreover,
\mathbb{S}
is subject to a diffusion process induced by the term
\gamma\Delta\mathbb{S}
with
\gamma\geq 0

and an additional nonlinear dissipation due to the subdifferential
\partial\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S})

of the nonsmooth convex potential
\mathcal{P}
.
The system is completed
by no-slip conditions for
\boldsymbol{v}
and
homogeneous Neumann conditions for
\mathbb{S}
(in the case
\gamma>0
) on the boundary
as well as initial conditions.

The Zaremba–Jaumann derivative (
2
) describes only one possible choice
for the objective stress rate,
and there exist other objective tensor derivatives
used for the description of stress evolution.
However, as we explain below, the model (
1
) is motivated from
geodynamics, where the Zaremba–Jaumann derivative is a common choice
(see
[
21
,
12
,
13
,
22
]
).
Moreover, it is crucial in the mathematical analysis of (
1
)
since it (formally) guarantees the identity

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}{\lvert\mathbb{S}\rvert}^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\mathbb{S}:\mathbb{S}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{\Omega}\overset{\triangledown}{\mathbb{S}}:\mathbb{S}\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

This property can be used to reveal information on the evolution of the total quadratic energy

\mathcal{E}(t):=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}{\lvert\boldsymbol{v}(x,t)\rvert}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}{\lvert\mathbb{S}(x,t)\rvert}^{2}\bigg)\,{\mathrm{d}}x,

which consists of the kinetic energy associated with
\boldsymbol{v}

and the stored elastic energy associated with
\mathbb{S}
.
More precisely, smooth solutions
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{S})
to (
1
)
formally satisfy the energy-dissipation balance

\mathcal{E}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\big(\mu{\lvert(\nabla\boldsymbol{v})_{\text{sym}}\rvert}^{2}+\partial\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}):\mathbb{S}+\gamma{\lvert\nabla\mathbb{S}\rvert}^{2}\big)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\tau=\mathcal{E}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\,{\mathrm{d}}\tau

(3)

for all
t\in[0,T)
.
This shows that the total energy
is dissipated by three processes:
the direct fluid viscosity with parameter
\mu>0
,
the nonsmooth stress-dissipation potential,
and the stress diffusion with parameter
\gamma\geq 0
.

The main non-standard feature of the system (
1
) is
the occurrence of the set-valued subdifferential
\partial\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S})
in (
1b
),
the meaning of which will be specified in the following.
Observe that the energy-dissipation balance (
3
)
suggests to examine (
1
) in an
L^{2}
framework and we
thus seek stress tensors
\Se=\Se(t)
taking values in the space

L^{2}_{\delta}(\Omega)=\{\mathbb{S}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{3\times 3}:\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{S}^{\top},\ \trace\mathbb{S}=0\}
.
The dissipation potential
\mathcal{P}
is now defined to be a convex and lower semicontinuous function

\mathcal{P}\colon L^{2}_{\delta}(\Omega)\to[0,\infty]
that satisfies
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{O})=0
, where
\mathbb{O}
denotes the zero tensor.
By definition, the convex subdifferential
\partial\mathcal{P}
of
\mathcal{P}
is then given by

\mathbb{S}\mapsto\partial\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S})\coloneqq\biggl\{\mathbb{G}\in L^{2}_{\delta}(\Omega):\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\mathbb{S}})\geq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S})+\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{G}:(\tilde{\mathbb{S}}-\mathbb{S})\,{\mathrm{d}}x\ \text{ for all }\tilde{\mathbb{S}}\in L^{2}_{\delta}(\Omega)\biggr\}.

Such nonsmooth dissipation potentials allow to include plastic effects in the model, and related
viscoelastoplastic fluid models
are used in geodynamics to describe rock deformation in the lithosphere;
see
[
21
,
12
]
,
where

\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S})=\int_{\Omega}\mathfrak{P}(\mathbb{S}(x))\,{\mathrm{d}}x,\hskip 20.00003pt\mathfrak{P}(\mathbb{S})=\begin{cases}\frac{a}{2}{\lvert\mathbb{S}\rvert}^{2}&\text{if }{\lvert\mathbb{S}\rvert}\leq\sigma_{\text{yield}},\\
\infty&\text{if }{\lvert\mathbb{S}\rvert}>\sigma_{\text{yield}}.\end{cases}

(4)

Here
a>0
is a constant,
and the yield stress
\sigma_{\text{yield}}>0
determines the transition to plastic behavior.
One readily verifies that
\mathcal{P}
defined in this way has the above properties.
Further examples for possible choices of
\mathcal{P}
can be found in
[
10
]
.

The beginning of the mathematical analysis of viscoelastic fluid models,
also using objective derivatives different from the Zaremba–Jaumann rate (
2
),
can be dated back to the middle 1980s;
see
[
14
,
25
,
24
,
8
,
23
]
for example.
Since all objective derivatives come along with strong nonlinearities,
the first result on global existence of weak solutions was only established several years later
by Lions and Masmoudi
[
20
]
,
who studied the system (
1
)
for
\gamma=0

and a quadratic dissipation potential such that
\partial\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S})=a\mathbb{S}
for some

.
In this case, (
1b
) becomes a transport equation,
and existence can be deduced from the propagation of compactness in

.
This tool is no longer available when
\mathcal{P}
is nonlinear and nonsmooth.
For such potentials, large-data global existence can be achieved for diffusive regularizations of the tensorial transport equation, as recently demonstrated in
[
6
,
2
,
10
]
.
The article
[
10
]
considers problem (
1
) with
\gamma>0
and proves global existence of generalized solutions compsed of a weak formulation for (
1a
) and a variational inequality for (
1b
) (cf. Definition
3.1
below).
In the present article, we continue this analysis in two directions.

In a first part, we investigate the case
\gamma>0
, complementing and refining the existence analysis of generalized solutions in
[
10
]
.
Here, our main results are the short-time existence of strong solutions (see Theorem
3.4
) as well their uniqueness among generalized solutions (see Theorem
3.5
).
The presence of the nonsmooth dissipation potential
\mathcal{P}
renders the construction of (local) strong solutions a non-trivial and interesting question.
Indeed, some care has to be taken to derive
a priori
estimates compatible with the nonsmoothness of
\mathcal{P}
, and our construction strongly relies on the fact that
\gamma>0
.
Concerning our uniqueness result, we note that the generalized solutions as considered here comprise the family of Leray–Hopf weak solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations (for
\eta=0
),
and hence the uniqueness of generalized solutions seems to be out of reach.
The main step in the proof of the weak-strong uniqueness principle mentioned above is
the derivation of an evolutionary inequality for the relative energy

\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)=\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\Se-\tS\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,

of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{v}}(t),\Se(t)|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(t),\tS(t))+\int_{0}^{t}\big(\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{K})}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se;\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)\big)\,\text{{e}}^{\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)\,\text{d}\tau}\,\text{d}s

(5)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{0},\Se_{0}|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(0),\tS(0))\text{{e}}^{\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)\,\text{d}s}\,

for

,
see (
39
) below,
which allows to compare generalized solutions
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{S})

with (more regular) test functions
({\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tilde{\mathbb{S}})
.
Here
\mathcal{F}
contains more or less the (nonlinear) differential operator
associated with problem (
1
)
applied to the test function
({\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tilde{\mathbb{S}})
,
and
\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{K})}
consists of terms describing the relative dissipation as well as
terms arising from the nonlinearities in (
1
).
It further depends on the non-negative function
\mathcal{K}
,
which we call the regularity weight
since it determines the class of admissible test functions
({\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tilde{\mathbb{S}})

such that (
5
) is meaningful.

In the second part we study the problem without stress diffusion, that is, when
\gamma=0
.
Our interest in this case stems, among other things, from the original models used in geodynamics
[
21
,
12
]
, where stress diffusion does not appear.
If
\gamma=0
, problem (
1
) reduces to the system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{v}}-\di\left(\eta\mathbb{S}+2\mu(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\right)+\nabla p={\boldsymbol{f}},\hskip 10.00002pt\di{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(6a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Se+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\Se+\Se(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\Se+\partial\mathcal{P}(\Se)

\displaystyle\ni\eta(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(6b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(6c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot,0)=\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbb{S}(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega.

(6d)

As mentioned above,
stress diffusion serves as a regularization making existence results accessible
by means of parabolic theory.
When
\gamma=0
, the energy estimate (
3
) no longer controls the gradient of
\Se
in
L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T))
, and it becomes unclear how to pass to the limit along approximate solutions in the term
\big(\Se(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}{-}(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\Se\big)
in (
6b
) associated with the Zaremba–Jaumann derivative.
In this article, we provide a framework allowing us to treat the case
\gamma=0
.
It relies on the rather weak notion of energy-variational solutions to (
6
)
(cf. Def.
4.1
below),
which is based on an inequality for the relative energy
\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)
,
adapting
the aforementioned relative energy inequality
(
5
)
for generalized solutions when
\gamma>0
.
Under a convexity assumption, we may pass to the limit
\gamma\to 0

in this inequality using weak lower semicontinuity arguments.
This allows us to construct an energy-variational solution to (
6
)
as the limit of a sequence of generalized solutions
(\boldsymbol{v}_{\gamma},\mathbb{S}_{\gamma})

to (
1
) for
\gamma>0
, see Theorem

.

The idea to base a solution concept on a relative energy estimate goes back to
Lions
[
19
, Def. 4.1]
,
who introduced
the notion of dissipative solutions
for the incompressible Euler equations.
A dissipative solution does not fulfill the differential equation in a distributional sense, but rather satisfies a relative energy inequality with respect to any sufficiently smooth test function.
After the seminal work by Lions, this concept has been adopted in other contexts as well,

e.g.
,
in the context of viscous electro-magneto-hydrodynamics
[
1
]
, liquid crystals
[
16
]
, and nematic electrolytes
[
3
]
.

Another generalized solution concept, which is often used in the context of fluid dynamics,
is the notion of so-called measure-valued solutions
[
9
]
.
Measure-valued solutions carry more information than dissipative solutions, but this is achieved by increasing the degrees of freedom: In every point in time and space the solution carries an infinite dimensional measure.
The expectation of every measure-valued solution fulfills the dissipative formulation
[
5
]
, which is identified as a desirable quantity in the case of liquid crystals
[
15
]
.
Moreover, the concept of dissipative solution is amenable from a numerical point of view.
In the case of anisotropic fluids, a structure-preserving finite element scheme was proven to converge to a dissipative solution, but the convergence to a measure-valued solution seems to be out of reach
[
3
, Rem. 3.7]
.
Especially the high-order regularizations used to prove convergence to a measure-valued solution
[
16
]
are not amenable from a numerical standpoint.
Moreover, in contrast to the set of weak solutions, the set of dissipative solutions is convex and weakly-
\ast
closed in case of convex energy and dissipation potential.
This can be used to define selection criteria choosing a certain dissipative solution in order to achieve uniqueness
[
17
]
,
see also Remark
4.4
below.

The article at hand extends
previous concepts
of dissipative solutions in two directions.
Firstly, we do not fix the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}
in the
relative energy estimate (
5
)
defining energy-variational solutions.
This allows to consider different classes of test functions
and lets us derive different results for different choices of the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}
.
For one class, we preserve the weak formulation of the Navier–Stokes-like part in the generalized formulation (see Prop.
4.3
), and for another choice, we can deduce the convexity of the solution set (see Rem.
4.4
).
As mentioned above, this convexity property
may be used
to select a unique physically relevant solution
[
18
,
17
]
.
Secondly,
we refrain from dropping the term
\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{K})}
in (
5
),
which for suitable
\mathcal{K}
is non-negative and usually estimated by
0
.
By choosing
\mathcal{K}
such that the relevant terms in
\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{K})}

are convex and lower semicontinuous,
they can be kept when passing to the limit
\gamma\to 0
,
thus making the solution concept more selective (see Remark
4.2
).

The structure of this article is as follows.
We first introduce the general notation
and prepare some auxiliary results in Section
2
.
In Section
3
we recall the existence theorem for generalized solutions to (
1
)
in the case
\gamma>0
and show the local-in-time existence of strong solutions.
Subsequently, we prove that strong solutions are unique in the class of generalized solutions.
For this purpose, we derive a suitable relative energy inequality.
In Section
4
we introduce the notion of energy-variational solutions to (
1
)
in the case
\gamma=0
,
which is based on a similar relative energy inequality,
and we show their existence by an approximation with generalized solutions for
\gamma>0
.
Subsequently, we derive general properties of energy-variational solutions.
In the appendix we explain how to infer the present notion of generalized solutions
from the slightly different notion introduced in
[
10
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

,
b\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and
c\in L^{r}(\Omega)
,

r\in(3,\infty)
.
Then for every
\delta>0
,
there exists a constant

such that

\int_{\Omega}|a||\nabla b||c|\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\delta\big(\|a\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla b\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big)+C_{\delta}\|c\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}^{s}\|a\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,

for
s\in(2,\infty)
defined by
2/s+3/r=1
.
If

, we even have

\int_{\Omega}|a||\nabla b||c|\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\delta\big(\|\nabla a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla b\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big)+C_{\delta}\|c\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}^{s}\|a\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Proof.

First consider the case

.
Hölder’s, Gagliardo–Nirenberg’s, and Young’s inequalities provide the estimate

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}{\lvert a\rvert}\,{\lvert\nabla b\rvert}\,{\lvert c\rvert}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq{}&\|a\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\|\nabla b\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|c\|_{L^{2p/(p-2)}(\Omega)}\\
\leq{}&c_{p}\|a\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{(1-\alpha)}\|\nabla a\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{\alpha}\|\nabla b\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|c\|_{L^{2p/(p-2)}(\Omega)}\\
\leq{}&\delta\left(\|\nabla a\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla b\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)+C_{\delta}\|c\|_{L^{2p/(p-2)}(\Omega)}^{2/(1-\alpha)}\|a\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,,\end{split}

where
p\in(2,\infty)
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
is chosen according to Gagliardo–Nirenberg’s inequality by

\alpha=3(p-2)/2p\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\hskip 10.00002pt3<2p/(p-2)\,.

Letting

concludes the proof for
a\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
If we only have

,
then we can merely apply the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality with

instead of

\|\nabla a\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,
which yields the asserted inequality in this case.

3

In this section, we investigate system (

\gamma>0

We begin by introducing the notions of strong and generalized solutions, and show that

The global-in-time existence of generalized solutions has been established in

The second main result of this section concerns a weak-strong stability estimate for the

An important implication of this estimate is

for

,

Using Agmon’s inequality as well as the Poincaré and Young inequalities

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\mu\|P\Delta\boldsymbol{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\mu^{-3}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{6}+C\mu^{-1}\eta^{2}\|\nabla\mathbb{S}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

4

In this section, we investigate problem (

where stress diffusion is not present.
In this case, the basic energy estimates no longer provide weak sequential compactness in

for the nonlinear term

Thus, we have to further extend the concept of generalized solutions introduced in Def.

Here, we use a concept in the spirit of
[
15

17

solutions introduced by P.-L. Lions in

In the literature, the term

of the regularity weight

We show existence of energy-variational solutions and

4.1

We introduce the notation

While the assumption

and observing that

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\phi^{\prime}\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{\gamma},\Se_{\gamma}|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)\,{\mathrm{d}}s+\int_{0}^{t}\phi\mathcal{W}_{0}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{\gamma},\Se_{\gamma}|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)\text{{e}}^{\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)\,\text{d}\tau}\,\text{d}s

for all

We now show that, when choosing

as in (

we may pass to the limit

in each of the terms on the left-hand side
of (

via the convergence properties from (

Since

, the first term in (

as a function of

. Hence, it is weakly

) and (

In order to pass to the limit in the second integral term in (

\begin{split}\mathcal{Q}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS):={}&\mu\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}-\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\mathcal{K}(\tS)\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)+\int_{\Omega}(\Se-\tS)\otimes({\boldsymbol{v}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}})\text{\,\put(0.0,-2.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,0.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,2.0){$\cdot$}}\,\,\,\,\nabla\tS\;\text{d}x\\

The convergence of the part of the integral involving the term

Concerning the remaining part involving

(for fixed

To show the non-negativity of

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}(\Se-\tS)\otimes({\boldsymbol{v}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}})\text{\,\put(0.0,-2.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,0.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,2.0){$\cdot$}}\,\,\,\,\nabla\tS\;\text{d}x\right|\leq\frac{\mu}{2}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}-\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\|\nabla\tS\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS).

Combined with the bound

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\big((\Se-\tS)(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}{-}\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}})_{\text{skw}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}{-}\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}})_{\text{skw}}(\Se-\tS)\big):\tS\;\text{d}x\right|

in the integral term

.

For the term involving the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
,
we first estimate the
\gamma

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\gamma\big(\nabla\tS\,,\nabla(\Se_{\gamma}-\tS)\big)\,{\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\gamma}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\tS\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\gamma\|\nabla\Se_{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\right)^{1/2}\!\!+\gamma\|\tS\|_{L^{2}(0,t;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}

for all
t\in(0,T)

Due to the bound (

and the regularity of

Since the remaining terms do not explicitly depend on

and
({\boldsymbol{v}}_{\gamma},\Se_{\gamma})
occurs at most linearly,

In total, we can pass to the limit inferior with

.
Therefore,

Since

4.3
Further properties

Here we collect general properties of energy-variational solutions.
Most importantly, we show that
energy-variational solutions are subject to the weak formulation of
the Navier–Stokes equations (
6a
)
if the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}
vanishes on
C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega)\times\{\mathbb{O}\}
,
where
\mathbb{O}\in L^{2}_{\delta}(\Omega)
denotes the zero tensor.
Thus, this is in particular the case for the solutions obtained in Theorem
4.2
.
Notice that this property is to be expected for problem (
6
) since the lack of weak sequential compactness in
L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T))
only occurs in the tensor component (
6b
) but not in (
6a
).

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose that the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}:Q\to[0,\infty]

satisfies

\mathcal{K}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\mathbb{O})=0
for all

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\in C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega)
.
Then every energy-variational solution of type
\mathcal{K}
is a weak solution of the Navier–Stokes component in the sense of (
16
).

Proof.

The hypothesis on
\mathcal{K}
implies the identity
\text{{e}}^{\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\mathbb{O})\,\text{d}s}\equiv 1
for all
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\in C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega\times[0,T))
. Hence, choosing
\tS\equiv\mathbb{O}
in (
48
) yields the inequality

\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{v}}(t),\Se(t)|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(t),\mathbb{O})+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mathcal{W}_{0}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\mathbb{O})+\mathcal{P}(\Se)+\left\langle\mathcal{A}_{0}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\mathbb{O}),\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{v}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\\
\Se\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle\right)\,\text{d}s\\
\leq\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{0},\Se_{0}|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(0),\mathbb{O})\,

for all
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\in C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega\times[0,T))
and for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
.
Applying Lemma
2.2
and observing that

\langle\mathcal{A}^{(1)}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\mathbb{O}),(\boldsymbol{v}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}})\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}|^{2}+(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\cdot\nabla)\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}+2\mu(\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}})_{\text{sym}}:(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}-\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}})\,{\mathrm{d}}x-\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{v}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rangle

and
\langle\mathcal{A}^{(2)}_{0}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\mathbb{O}),\Se\rangle=-\int_{\Omega}\eta(\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}})_{{\text{sym}}}:\Se\;\text{d}x
,
we find

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}{-}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\Se\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\,\text{d}t-\left(\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}{-}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\Se_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)

(53)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\phi\left(\mu\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}{-}\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\mathcal{P}(\Se)+\int_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}}{-}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}})\otimes({\boldsymbol{v}}{-}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}):\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}-\eta(\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}})_{{\text{sym}}}:\Se\;\text{d}x\!\right)\!\,{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\phi\left(\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}|^{2}+(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\cdot\nabla)\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}+\mu\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}:(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}{-}\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}})\,{\mathrm{d}}x-\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{v}}{-}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rangle\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}t\leq 0\,.

We now integrate by parts in time the second term in the last line of (
53
), regroup terms,
let
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\alpha\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
for given
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\in C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega\times[0,T))
and
\alpha>0
, and multiply the inequality by
1/\alpha
to infer

\int_{0}^{T}\phi^{\prime}\left({\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)\;\text{d}t+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\phi^{\prime}\left(\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\Se\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}t-\frac{1}{2}\left(\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\Se_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\right)\\
+\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{T}\phi\left(\mu\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\mathcal{P}(\Se)-\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\rangle\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}t\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\phi\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mu\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}:\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}}):\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}-\eta(\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}})_{\text{sym}}:\Se\;\text{d}x+\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\rangle\bigg)\,{\mathrm{d}}t+\left({\boldsymbol{v}}_{0},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(0)\right)\leq 0\,.

In the limit
\alpha\ra\infty
the two terms with the prefactor
\frac{1}{\alpha}
disappear. Thus, appealing to Lemma
2.2
we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mu\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}:\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}}):\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}-\eta(\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}})_{\text{sym}}:\Se\;\text{d}x+\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\rangle\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}t+\left({\boldsymbol{v}}_{0},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(0)\right)\leq 0\,,

where we used the fact that
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\cdot,T)=0
.
Since the left-hand side of the last inequality is a linear functional of
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
with the latter being allowed to vary in the linear space
C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega\times[0,T))
, we infer the asserted equation (
16
).
∎

Another quite natural property is that
energy-variational solutions are monotonic in the type in the following manner.

Proposition 4.4

(Monotonicity)
.

If
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{S})
is an energy-variational solution of type

,
and if
\mathcal{K}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tilde{\mathbb{S}})\leq\mathcal{L}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tilde{\mathbb{S}})

and a.a.

,
then
(\boldsymbol{v},\mathbb{S})
is an energy-variational solution of type
\mathcal{L}
.

Proof.

This can readily be seen by expressing (
48
) in a weak form with Lemma
2.2
,
using the test function

\phi(s)=\varphi(s)\text{{e}}^{-\int_{0}^{s}(\mathcal{L}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tilde{\mathbb{S}})-\mathcal{K}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tilde{\mathbb{S}}))\,{\mathrm{d}}\tau}

for
\varphi\in\tilde{W}((0,T))
,
and using Lemma
2.2
again
to return to a pointwise version of (
48
) with

replaced with

.
∎

Remark 4.4

(Convex solution set)
.

By Proposition
4.4
,
the energy-variational solution established in Theorem

is
also an energy-variational solution of type

4.4
Energy-variational solutions versus strong solutions

In this subsection, we prove two results
attempting to further justify the concept of energy-variational solutions.
First, in Proposition
4.6
we show that any hypothetical strong solution is unique in the class of energy-variational solutions.
Second, in Proposition
4.8
we prove that any energy-variational solution enjoying some additional regularity is a (unique) strong solution.
Observe that both results only hold for strong solutions that belong to
D_{\mathcal{K}}
.
In this sense, the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}
also determines the
minimal regularity of a strong solution for comparison with an energy-variational solution.

Definition 4.5

(Strong solution)
.

a strong solution of problem (

) with initial data

,
(\boldsymbol{v}(0),\mathbb{S}(0))=(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\mathbb{S}_{0})
and

•

equation (
6a
) is satisfied in the weak sense,

i.e.
, equation (
16
)
holds true
for all

\Phi\in C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega\times[0,T))
.

•

inclusion (
6b
) is satisfied pointwise a.e. in time,

i.e.
,
it holds

-\big(\partial_{t}\Se+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\Se+\left(\Se(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\Se\right)-\eta(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\big)(t)\in\partial\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}(t))\hskip 10.00002pt

for a.a.

.

We show next that

and a strong solution in the class

This statement and its proof are parallel to the one given in Theorem

In particular, we also derive a corresponding weak-strong stability estimate.

Proposition 4.6

(Weak-strong uniqueness)

Assume that

From the assumption that

, we observe that

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathcal{A}^{(1)}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS),{\boldsymbol{v}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

To infer that sufficiently regular energy-variational solutions

\mathcal{P}
is given in integral form

\mathcal{P}(\Se)=\int_{\Omega}\mathfrak{P}(\Se)\,\text{d}x
.
This leads to approximation properties
that are sufficient to
show that energy-variational solutions
with more regularity, that is, belonging to
D_{\mathcal{K}}\cap\mathfrak{Z}_{0}
,
are already strong solutions.
For simplicity, we confine ourselves to energy-variational solutions of type
\mathcal{K}_{s}

given in (
54
). Note that, by the monotonicity property in Proposition
4.4
, this includes the (smaller) set of solutions of type
\mathcal{K}(\tS)=C\big(\|\tS\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla\tS\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}\big)\leq\mathcal{K}_{s}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)
, whose existence follows from Theorem
4.2
.

Proposition 4.8

(Regular energy-variational solutions)
.

Let
({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se)\in\mathfrak{X}_{0}
be an energy-variational solution of type
\mathcal{K}
according to Definition
4.1
with
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{s}
given in (
54
),
which satisfies
({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se)\in D_{\mathcal{K}}\cap\mathfrak{Z}_{0}
.
Further suppose that the associated initial data
(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\Se_{0})
satisfy
\mathcal{P}(\Se_{0})<\infty
,
and that
\mathcal{P}
is in integral form, that is,

\mathcal{P}(\Se)=\int_{\Omega}\mathfrak{P}(\Se)\,\text{d}x

for a lower semicontinuous, convex function
\mathfrak{P}:\R^{3\times 3}_{\delta}\to[0,\infty]
with
\mathfrak{P}(\mathbb{O})=0
.
Then
({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se)
is a strong solution in the sense of Definition
4.5
taking the same initial data
(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\Se_{0})
.

Proof.

First, we show that the initial data are attained.
To this end, we choose a sequence of regular functions
\{(\boldsymbol{v}_{0,\varepsilon},\Se_{0,\varepsilon})\}\subset C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega)\times C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega};\R^{3\times 3}_{\delta})
converging to

(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\Se_{0})
in
L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega)\times L^{2}_{\delta}(\Omega)

in such a way that
\mathcal{P}(\Se_{0,\varepsilon})\to\mathcal{P}(\Se_{0})
. A sequence
\{\Se_{0,\varepsilon}\}
with these properties can be obtained by mollification of the function
\Se_{0}
(extended by zero on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega
).
Indeed, note that for a standard mollifying kernel
(\rho_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\subset C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with
\rho_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{\varepsilon}=1
, Jensen’s inequality ensures that the function
\mathbb{S}_{0,\varepsilon}
defined by
\mathbb{S}_{0,\varepsilon}(x)\coloneqq\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast\Se_{0}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)\Se_{0}(x-y)\,\text{d}y
satisfies
the pointwise inequality

\mathfrak{P}(\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast\Se_{0})\leq\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast\mathfrak{P}(\Se_{0})\hskip 20.00003pt\text{in }\Omega,

and hence

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}_{0,\varepsilon})\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast\mathfrak{P}(\Se_{0})\,\text{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\mathfrak{P}(\Se_{0})\,\text{d}x=\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}_{0}).

Since
\mathbb{S}_{0,\varepsilon}\to\mathbb{S}_{0}
in
L^{2}_{\delta}(\Omega)

and
\mathcal{P}
is lower semicontinuous,
this even implies that
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}_{0,\varepsilon})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}_{0})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Choosing
({\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)
constant in time and equal to
(\boldsymbol{v}_{0,\varepsilon},\Se_{0,\varepsilon})
yields test functions admissible in the relative energy inequality (
48
). It now suffices to pass to the limit
t\to 0
in this inequality along an admissible sequence of times, which gives (thanks to
\mathfrak{Z}_{0}\subset C([0,T);L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega)\times L^{2}_{\delta}(\Omega))
) the estimate

\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{v}(0),\mathbb{S}(0)|\boldsymbol{v}_{0,\varepsilon},\Se_{0,\varepsilon})\leq\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\mathbb{S}_{0}|\boldsymbol{v}_{0,\varepsilon},\Se_{0,\varepsilon}).

(57)

Taking the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, we infer that
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{v}(0),\mathbb{S}(0)|\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\Se_{0})=0
,
whence
\boldsymbol{v}(0)=\boldsymbol{v}_{0}
and
\mathbb{S}(0)=\mathbb{S}_{0}
.

In the next step we show the weak formulation (
16
).
Due to
\boldsymbol{v}(0)=\boldsymbol{v}_{0}
, this is equivalent to showing the identity

\int_{0}^{T}\left[\langle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}},\Phi\rangle+\int_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\Phi+(\eta\Se+\mu\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}):\nabla\Phi\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}t=\int_{0}^{T}\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},\Phi\rangle\,{\mathrm{d}}t

(58)

for all
\Phi\in C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega\times[0,T))
.
Since
({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se)\in D_{\mathcal{K}}\cap\mathfrak{Z}_{0}
, we can use

(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tS)=({\boldsymbol{v}}+\alpha{\boldsymbol{u}},\Se+\alpha\mathbb{T})
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
({\boldsymbol{u}},\mathbb{T})\in D_{\mathcal{K}}\cap\mathfrak{Z}_{0}

as a test function in (
48
).
Due to the convexity of
\mathcal{P}
, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
we may estimate

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}(\Se)-\mathcal{P}(\Se+\alpha\mathbb{T})={}&\mathcal{P}(\Se)-\mathcal{P}\left((1-\alpha)\Se+\alpha(\Se+\mathbb{T})\right)\\
\geq{}&\mathcal{P}(\Se)-(1-\alpha)\mathcal{P}(\Se)-\alpha\mathcal{P}(\Se+\mathbb{T})\\
={}&\alpha\left(\mathcal{P}(\Se)-\mathcal{P}(\Se+\mathbb{T})\right)\,.\end{split}

(59)

Inserting this into (
48
), multiplying the resulting relation by
1/\alpha
, and sorting the different terms according to the appearing exponent of
\alpha
, we end up with

\displaystyle\alpha R({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se,{\boldsymbol{u}},\mathbb{T},\alpha)+\int_{0}^{t}\left[\mathcal{P}(\Se)-\mathcal{P}(\Se+\mathbb{T})-\left\langle\mathcal{A}_{0}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se),\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{u}}\\
\mathbb{T}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle\right]\text{{e}}^{\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{v}}+\alpha{\boldsymbol{u}},\Se+\alpha\mathbb{T})\,\text{d}\tau}\,\text{d}s\leq 0\,.

(60)

The remainder term
R
contains all terms with prefactor
\alpha
and is given by

\displaystyle R({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se,{\boldsymbol{u}},\mathbb{T},\alpha):={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\|{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mathbb{T}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)+\int_{0}^{t}R_{2}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se,{\boldsymbol{u}},\mathbb{T},\alpha)\text{{e}}^{\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{v}}+\alpha{\boldsymbol{u}},\Se+\alpha\mathbb{T})\,\text{d}\tau}\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(\|{\boldsymbol{u}}(0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mathbb{T}(0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\text{{e}}^{\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{v}}+\alpha{\boldsymbol{u}},\Se+\alpha\mathbb{T})\,\text{d}s}\,,

where
R_{2}
is given by

\displaystyle R_{2}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se,{\boldsymbol{u}},\mathbb{T},\alpha):={}

\displaystyle\mu\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\int_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot({\boldsymbol{v}}+\alpha{\boldsymbol{u}})-\mathbb{T}\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}\text{\,\put(0.0,-2.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,0.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,2.0){$\cdot$}}\,\,\,\,\nabla(\Se+\alpha\mathbb{T})\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathbb{T}(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})_{\text{skw}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})_{\text{skw}}\mathbb{T}\right):(\Se+\alpha\mathbb{T})\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{u}}+({\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)({\boldsymbol{v}}+\alpha{\boldsymbol{u}})+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{u}}\right)\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}+\left(\eta\mathbb{T}+2\mu(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})_{\text{sym}}\right):\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left(\partial_{t}\mathbb{T}+({\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)(\Se+\alpha\mathbb{T})+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{T}+\mathbb{T}(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\mathbb{T}\right):\mathbb{T}\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left((\Se+\alpha\mathbb{T})(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})_{\text{skw}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})_{\text{skw}}(\Se+\alpha\mathbb{T})-\eta(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})_{\text{sym}}\right):\mathbb{T}\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{v}}+\alpha{\boldsymbol{u}},\Se+\alpha\mathbb{T})\left(\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mathbb{T}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\,.

Passing to the limit
\alpha\ra 0
in (
60
), we infer from the boundedness of all terms in

and the continuity property
\lim_{\alpha\ra 0}\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{v}}+\alpha{\boldsymbol{u}},\Se+\alpha\mathbb{T})=\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se)
, which holds for
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{s}
given in (
54
), that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left[\mathcal{P}(\Se)-\mathcal{P}(\Se+\mathbb{T})-\left\langle\mathcal{A}_{0}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se),\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{u}}\\
\mathbb{T}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle\right]\text{{e}}^{\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se)\,\text{d}\tau}\,\text{d}s\leq 0\,.

(61)

Choosing
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{O}
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}=\Phi\text{{e}}^{-\int_{\cdot}^{t}\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se)\,\text{d}\tau}
for
\Phi\in C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega\times[0,T))
,
we first infer the weak formulation (
58

{\boldsymbol{u}}=-\Phi\text{{e}}^{-\int_{\cdot}^{t}\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{v}},\Se)\,\text{d}\tau}

In summary, equation (

Reinserting this information into (

By choosing

(\boldsymbol{0},\tilde{\mathbb{T}})\in D_{\mathcal{K}}\cap\mathfrak{Z}_{0}

\varphi\geq 0

\mathcal{P}(\hat{\mathbb{T}})-\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}(t))\geq\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{G}:(\hat{\mathbb{T}}-\mathbb{S}(t))\,\,{\mathrm{d}}x

(63)

for a.a.
t\in(0,T)

It is not difficult to see that the null set where (

\hat{\mathbb{T}}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega};\R^{3\times 3}_{\delta})

Now fix
t\in(0,T)
such that (
63

If
\hat{\mathbb{T}}\in L^{2}_{\delta}(\Omega)
with
\mathcal{P}(\hat{\mathbb{T}})=\infty

63

If
\hat{\mathbb{T}}\in L^{2}_{\delta}(\Omega)

In conclusion, (

and identity (
