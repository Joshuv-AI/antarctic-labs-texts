Dual metrics on the boundary of strictly polyhedral hyperbolic 3-manifolds
Roman Prosanov

Abstract
Let M M be a compact oriented 3-manifold with non-empty boundary consisting of surfaces of genii > 1 >1 such that the interior of M M is hyperbolizable. We show that for each spherical cone-metric d d on ∂ M \partial M such that all cone-angles are greater than 2 ​ π 2\pi and the lengths of all closed geodesics that are contractible in M M are greater than 2 ​ π 2\pi there exists a unique strictly polyhedral hyperbolic metric on M M such that d d is the induced dual metric on ∂ M \partial M .



1
Introduction

1.1
Metric geometry of convex polyhedra

The class of problems dealing with determining a surface in space by some associated geometric data has a rich history. This paper constitutes another its chapter, studying a discrete analogue of the third fundamental form,
the dual metric
, on the boundary of polyhedral hyperbolic 3-manifolds. We start from a basic definition.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
S
be a closed surface. We say that
d
is a
Euclidean cone-metric
(resp.
spherical
or
hyperbolic
) on
S
if it is locally isometric to the Euclidean plane (resp. the standard sphere or the hyperbolic plane) except at finitely many points where it is locally isometric to a Euclidean cone (resp. spherical or hyperbolic) of total angle
\neq 2\pi
. We say that
d
is
convex
if all cone angles are
<2\pi
. We say that
d
is
concave
if all cone angles are
>2\pi
.

A theorem of Alexandrov
[
1
, Chapter VI]
states that a convex Euclidean cone-metric on the 2-sphere is realized as the induced intrinsic metric on the boundary of a unique up to isometry compact convex polyhedron in the Euclidean 3-space
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. The induced intrinsic metric here means the induced path metric and the realization might degenerate to the double cover of a convex polygon. This is a polyhedral version of a fundamental result in differential geometry: every Riemannian metric of positive Gaussian curvature on the 2-sphere is realized as the induced metric on the boundary of a unique up to isometry smooth convex body in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. This completely characterizes the induced metrics on the boundaries of smooth strictly convex bodies, i.e., those with a positive-definite shape operator at every boundary point. The existence part is known as the famous
Weyl Problem
resolved by Nirenberg
[
50
]
from one side, and by Alexandrov and Pogorelov
[
1
,
51
]
from another side. The uniqueness part is known as the global rigidity of closed smooth strictly convex surfaces in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. We refer to Cohn-Vossen
[
14
]
and to Herglotz
[
31
]
as to one of the main contributors.

Alexandrov noticed that his theorem can be transferred without changes to polyhedra in the standard 3-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and in the hyperbolic 3-space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. Convex bodies in these spaces have another interesting associated metric structure, which we call
the dual metric
, and which is somewhat degenerate in the Euclidean 3-space
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. In the standard 3-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{3}
there is a natural duality between points and oriented 2-planes. For a convex body
C\subset\mathbb{S}^{3}
this allows to define
the Gauss map
as a multivalued map on
\partial C
sending
p\in\partial C
to the set of points dual to supporting planes to
C
at
p
oriented outwards
C
. The image of
\partial C
under this map is a surface bounding another closed convex set
C^{*}\subset\mathbb{S}^{3}
, which we call
the dual
of
C
. We can also define
C^{*}
as the set of points dual to all 2-planes not intersecting
{\rm int}(C)
oriented outwards
C
.

This duality is polar on closed convex sets, i.e.,
(C^{*})^{*}=C
. It follows from the Alexandrov Theorem for
\mathbb{S}^{3}
that a convex polyhedron
C\subset\mathbb{S}^{3}
is also uniquely determined by the induced intrinsic metric on the boundary of
C^{*}
. This does not work the same in Euclidean 3-space: the natural metric structure on
the co-Euclidean space
, which is the space of oriented 2-planes in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
, is degenerate, and the induced intrinsic metric on the dual convex set is always isometric to the standard 2-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Parallel translations allow to consider
\mathbb{S}^{2}
as the target for the Gauss map on convex surfaces in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. We refer to
[
27
]
for a more detailed exposition.

The situation is more interesting in the hyperbolic 3-space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. The set of oriented 2-planes in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
is naturally identified with
the de Sitter 3-space

d\mathbb{S}^{3}
, which is a model Lorentzian 3-space of constant curvature 1. See Section
2.1
for more details on this duality. A non-trivial work was required to understand metric properties of dual convex bodies in this setting. For the case of compact convex polyhedra in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
this resulted in the following theorem of Rivin–Hodgson
[
58
]
:

Definition 1.2
.

A metric on a surface
S
is called
large
if the lengths of all closed contractible geodesics are greater than
2\pi
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For every concave large spherical cone-metric
d
on the 2-sphere
S
there is a unique up to isometry compact convex polyhedron
C\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
such that
(S,d)
is isometric to the boundary of its dual
C^{*}\subset d\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

One can see that the conditions on
d
in Theorem
1.3
are also necessary (the only non-trivial condition is largeness, and its necessity is proven in
[
58
]
). Theorem
1.3
can be seen as a generalization of the Andreev Theorem
[
3
]
on compact convex polyhedra in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with non-obtuse dihedral angles, another generalization of which was famously used by Thurston in his Hyperbolization Theorem for Haken 3-manifolds, see
[
69
,
48
]
. For a corrected proof of the Andreev Theorem and more references we refer to
[
59
]
. Theorem
1.3
was also used by Rivin in
[
56
]
to describe the dihedral angles of ideal polyhedra in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, which in turn implied a resolution of the Steiner Problem. The latter asked how to distinguish the combinatorial types of convex Euclidean 3-polyhedra that admit a realization with all vertices on the standard 2-sphere. A more general Steiner Problem, with the 2-sphere replaced by the other types of quadrics in
\mathbb{RP}^{3}
, was resolved in
[
17
]
. In
[
61
]
Schlenker proved a version of Theorem
1.3
for smooth strictly convex bodies in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. We note that if
C\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
is a smooth strictly convex body, then the pull-back of the dual metric by the Gauss map is exactly
the third fundamental form
of
\partial C
.

1.2
Hyperbolic 3-manifolds with convex boundary

In the seventies Thurston revolutionized the field of 3-dimensional topology by showing the importance of homogeneous geometric structures on 3-manifolds, posing his famous Geometrization Conjecture (whose proof was completed by Perelman) and demonstrating a special role of hyperbolic geometry in dimension 3. In this paper we will be interested in studying compact hyperbolic 3-manifolds with convex polyhedral boundary.

Let
M
be an oriented smooth compact 3-manifold with non-empty boundary and
N
be a 3-manifold homeomorphic to the interior of
M
.

Definition 1.4
.

A subset
C
of a complete hyperbolic manifold is called
totally convex
if it is closed and contains every geodesic segment between any two points of
C
.

Definition 1.5
.

A hyperbolic metric
\overline{g}
on
N
is called
convex cocompact
if it is complete and
(N,\overline{g})
contains a compact totally convex subset.

We assume that
N
admits a convex cocompact hyperbolic metric. Then
M
is irreducible and atoroidal. We also assume that
M
is distinct from the 3-ball and the solid torus. Then all its boundary components have genii at least two. Thurston’s Hyperbolization Theorem implies that the converse holds: the interior of a compact 3-manifold satifying these conditions admits a convex cocompact hyperbolic metric, see
[
40
,
48
]
. We will call such
M

admissible
. Convex cocompact hyperbolic metrics are generic as they constitute the interior of the moduli space of complete hyperbolic metrics on
N
.

Let
M
be endowed with a hyperbolic metric
g
such that the boundary is locally convex.
A developing map
{\rm dev}:\widetilde{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{3}
embeds isometrically the universal cover
(\widetilde{M},g)
to
\mathbb{H}^{3}
as a (non-compact) convex set equivariantly with respect to the holonomy map
\rho:\pi_{1}(M)\rightarrow G
, where
G
is the group of orientation-preserving isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. We look at the dual convex set
({\rm dev}(\widetilde{M}))^{*}\subset d\mathbb{S}^{3}
defined in Section
2.1
. The group
G
can be also identified with the identity component of the isometry group of
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
, and
({\rm dev}(\widetilde{M}))^{*}
is, of course, also invariant with respect to
\rho
. However, the action may not be free on
({\rm dev}(\widetilde{M}))^{*}
. But under mild restrictions (positive distance from
\partial M
to the convex core of
(M,g)
, which will be defined later) it is free and properly discontinuous on
\partial({\rm dev}(\widetilde{M}))^{*}
. By taking the quotient, we obtain
the dual metric
on
\partial M
. Again, in the case of smooth strictly convex boundary, it is exactly the third fundamental form of
\partial M
.

Gabai-Meyerhoff-Thurson
[
29
]
showed that any homeomorphism between closed hyperbolic 3-manifolds is isotopic to an isometry, refining in dimension 3 the well-known Mostow rigidity
[
49
]
. Due to the rigidity and the dual rigidity of convex bodies in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, it is interesting to ask if the intrinsic metric or the dual metric of the boundary determine
g
up to isotopy, and if all metrics satisfying natural necessary conditions are realized for some choice of
g
. For the case of smooth strictly convex boundary this was done by Schlenker in
[
65
]
:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
M
be admissible. For every smooth Riemannian metric
d
on
\partial M
of Gaussian curvature
>-1
there exists a unique up to isotopy hyperbolic metric
g
on
M
such that
\partial M
is strictly convex in
g
and the induced intrinsic metric on
\partial M
is
d
.

Definition 1.7
.

We say that a metric
d
on
\partial M
is
large for
M
if the lengths of all closed geodesics in
(\partial M,d)
that are contractible in
M
are greater than
2\pi
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
M
be admissible. For every smooth large for
M
Riemannian metric
d
on
\partial M
of Gaussian curvature
<1
there exists a unique up to isotopy hyperbolic metric
g
on
M
such that
\partial M
is strictly convex in
g
and the induced dual metric on
\partial M
is
d
.

It is natural to expect that polyhedral counterparts of Theorem
1.6
and
1.8
should hold. Curiously enough, the approach to infinitesimal rigidity (which is the key part of the argument) from
[
65
]
is too much specific to the smooth situation and does not seem to admit a discrete analogue. In the same time, standard methods of proving the infinitesimal rigidity of polyhedra in space-forms use fundamentally the fact that the boundary is the 2-sphere, and become not applicable in more non-trivial topological situations. Hence, we needed to work out a different technique to bridge this gap. For a polyhedral version of Theorem
1.6
an additional complication arises from the necessity to consider not just hyperbolic 3-manifolds with polyhedral boundary, but a more general class of so-called
bent
hyperbolic 3-manifolds. A good example is provided by
the convex core
of a convex cocompact hyperbolic 3-manifold, which is the inclusion-minimal totally convex subset, see more details in Section
2.3
. Its boundary is convex and intrinsically locally isometric to the hyperbolic plane, but it is bent along a geodesic lamination. This lamination may consist of uncountably many disjoint open geodesics winding around each other in a sophisticated manner, see
[
69
,
6
]
for explanations. It is not known currently, how to establish the infinitesimal rigidity for such manifolds. However, in our very recent work
[
54
]
we prove the existence part of the polyhedral version of Theorem
1.6
and obtain the uniqueness under mild restrictions on the boundary.

The mentioned effects, however, do not considerably impact the dual problem, as the dual metric degenerates in the mentioned cases (though then it can be replaced by another object, and it is a very interesting task to deal with the rigidity in this setting, see the end of this section). This paper is devoted to a resolution of the dual problem, i.e., to a polyhedral counterpart of Theorem
1.8
.

Definition 1.9
.

We say that
g
is a
strictly polyhedral
hyperbolic metric on
M
if the interior is locally hyperbolic, the boundary is locally isometric to convex polyhedral sets in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, and the distance from
\partial M
to the convex core is positive.

Again, for more explanations on convex cores we refer to Section
2.3
. Our result is

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
M
be admissible. For each concave large for
M
spherical cone-metric
d
on
\partial M
there exists a unique up to isotopy strictly polyhedral hyperbolic metric
g
on
M
such that the induced dual metric on
\partial M
is
d
.

This is a generalization of Theorem
1.3
to arbitrary compact hyperbolic 3-manifolds with boundary. We note that the condition of strict convexity in Theorems
1.6
and
1.8
implies that the boundary is at positive distance from the convex core. Thus, Theorem
1.10
includes a polyhedral version of Theorem
1.8
. When the boundary of
M
touches the convex core, the dual metric degenerates to a pseudo-metric, hence the condition of strict polyhedrality in Theorem
1.10
is necessary to have a well-definite dual metric on
\partial M
. In Section
2.4
we explain that the conditions on
d
are also necessary (again, this is non-trivial only for the condition of largeness for
M
).

The only previously known case of Theorem
1.10
was established by Schlenker in
[
64
]
for so-called
Fuchsian hyperbolic 3-manifolds of the first kind
with convex polyhedral boundary. They are homeomorphic to
S\times[-1,1]
, where
S
is a closed surface of genus
>1
, and contain an embedded totally geodesic surface isotopic to
S\times\{0\}
. See also a paper of Fillastre
[
23
]
. We note that our Theorem
1.10
answers positively to Question 2 in
[
23
]
. In the Fuchsian case of the first kind Fillastre also established in
[
22
]
a polyhedral counterpart to Theorem
1.6
. Similar results for the Minkowski and anti-de Sitter 3-spaces were obtained in
[
23
]
.

A particularly interesting case of Theorem
1.10
is given by so-called
quasi-Fuchsian hyperbolic 3-manifolds
with boundary. Those are also homeomorphic to
S\times[-1,1]
, but the condition on the embedded geodesic surface is dropped. There is a pair of domains in
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
, on which the holonomy map of a quasi-Fuchsian manifold acts freely and properly discontinuously.
The quotient of every of them is known in Lorentzian geometry as a
globally hyperbolic maximal Cauchy compact
(GHMC for short) (2+1)-spacetime of constant curvature 1. See, e.g., the fundamental paper of Mess
[
44
]
for more details and a paper of Scannell
[
60
]
. This case of Theorem
1.10
can be interpreted as a convex polyhedral realization and rigidity result in such spacetimes.

Our proof of Theorem
1.10
follows the standard continuity approach popularized by Alexandrov. We consider the space
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
of isotopy classes of strictly polyhedral metrics on
M
with marked faces, and the space
\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
of isotopy classes of concave large for
M
spherical cone-metrics on
\partial M
with marked vertices. We endow these spaces with appropriate topologies and consider the dual metric map
\mathcal{I}_{V}:\mathcal{P}(M,V)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
. We show that
\mathcal{I}_{V}
is a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism.

The proof is decomposed into three pieces: establishing differentiability properties of
\mathcal{I}_{V}
and its infinitesimal rigidity, showing its properness, and studying connectivity properties of the spaces
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
and
\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
. The last item was basically done before us: it is a generalization of the connectivity results from
[
58
]
, as was mentioned by Schlenker in
[
64
]
. Thus, we mostly focus on the infinitesimal rigidity and the properness. Note also that the connectivity results on the spaces above are obtained only in some weak sense, and a special topological argument is required to finish the proof.

We deduce the infinitesimal rigidity via the doubling construction from the works on the infinitesimal rigidity of hyperbolic cone-3-manifolds due to Weiss and Montcouquiol-Weiss
[
73
,
74
,
47
]
. Note that in the presence of vertices of valence
>3
the infinitesimal rigidity in our problem is not the same as the infinitesimal rigidity with respect to the dihedral angles, which follows from the aforementioned works rather directly, and we need to extract the former with some care. Hyperbolic cone-3-manifolds have interesting applications to various geometric problems, see, e.g.,
[
5
,
8
,
33
,
52
,
53
]
. The proof of properness is partially inspired by Schlenker’s works
[
62
,
63
]
, which deal with Alexandrov-type problems for polyhedra in the union of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

An interesting issue with the continuity method is that it gives no construction of a hyperbolic metric
g
inducing the dual metric
d
. This might be resolved by a variational approach. A variational proof of Alexandrov’s Theorem was given by Volkov, a student of Alexandrov, in his thesis, published only recently as
[
70
]
. A refinement of Volkov’s approach was given in
[
4
]
by Bobenko–Izmestiev. It was applied to similar problems for polyhedral hyperbolic 3-manifolds, e.g., in
[
24
,
53
]
. A particularly relevant for us reference is
[
25
]
where the problem of induced dual metrics on the so-called
hyperbolic 3-cusps
with convex polyhedral boundary was resolved by variational techniques. It would be very interesting to obtain a variational proof of Theorem
1.10
or a proof of a polyhedral analogue of Theorem
1.6
, but at the moment there are difficulties with attempts to implement such intentions.

Up to now we discussed in parallel questions for polyhedral and for smooth boundaries. It is very natural to ask if there is a common generalization unifying these two settings. This was famously initiated by Alexandrov in his work on general metrics on surfaces with curvature bounded from below
[
1
]
. He particularly proved a realization theorem, obtaining a complete intrinsic classification of the induced path metrics on the boundaries of convex bodies in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
,
\mathbb{H}^{3}
or
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. The rigidity question for general convex bodies was settled by Pogorelov
[
51
]
and is known as notoriously difficult. In recent years there was a stream of work showing general realization results on hyperbolic 3-manifolds, e.g.,
[
66
,
26
]
. The only known general rigidity result for manifolds topologically more complicated than the 3-ball (for Fuchsian manifolds of the first kind) was done by the author in
[
52
]
. The corresponding results for the dual metrics are still not known. An important particular case, again, is given by the convex cores of convex cocompact hyperbolic 3-manifolds. Their dual metric is somewhat degenerate, but can be replaced with another object called
the pleating lamination
measuring how their boundary is bent. Thurston conjectured that convex cores are uniquely determined by their pleating lamination as well as by the induced boundary metric. The realization part of the former question was shown in
[
6
]
and
[
37
]
by Bonahon–Otal and Lecuire, while for the latter it is, e.g., a particular corollary of the work
[
36
]
by Labourie, where another proof of the realization part of Theorem
1.6
was given. The rigidity part for pleating laminations was recently resolved by Dular–Schlenker in
[
18
]
, while for the induced metric it remains an open problem, with some progress obtained in
[
54
]
. This is reminiscent to the famous Ahlfors–Bers Theorem (see Theorem
2.12
), which could be stated as that convex cocompact hyperbolic metrics on 3-manifolds are determined up to isotopy by the conformal structure at infinity.

Preliminaries

2.1
Hyperbolic and de Sitter 3-spaces and the duality

For some expositions on the duality between hyperbolic and de Sitter spaces we refer to
[
58
,
62
,
27
]
.

By
\langle.,.\rangle_{M}
we denote the
Minkowski scalar product
on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, i.e.,

\langle x,y\rangle_{M}:=-x_{0}y_{0}+x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}+x_{3}y_{3}.

The hyperbolic 3-space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
can be identified then with the upper sheet of the two-sheeted hyperboloid

\mathbb{H}^{3}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:\langle x,~x\rangle_{M}=-1,x_{0}>0\}

endowed with the induced metric. The de Sitter 3-space
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
can be identified with the one-sheeted hyperboloid

d\mathbb{S}^{3}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:\langle x,x\rangle_{M}=1\}

endowed with the induced metric. It is a Lorentzian 3-manifold of constant curvature 1 homeomorphic to
S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. Let us say that a subset of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
or
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
is
properly convex
when it is proper, has non-empty interior and is the intersection of closed half-spaces (the latter is equivalent to be closed and convex in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, but the convexity is more subtle in
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
).

As the hyperbolic 3-space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
is compactified by adding the boundary at infinity
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
, the de Sitter 3-space is compactified similarly by adding the boundary at infinity
\partial_{\infty}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
consisting of two connected components,
\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
and
\partial_{\infty}^{-}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
, corresponding to the two parts of the light cone of the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. The component
\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
is naturally identified with
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
. We consider
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
time-oriented with the future-directions pointing towards
\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

There is a well-known duality between oriented planes in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and points in
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
, and a duality between space-like planes in
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
and points in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. Indeed, each plane in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
is the intersection of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with a linear hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
passing through the origin. Its unit normal in the positive direction belongs to
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Conversely, for any point in
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
the orthogonal hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
intersects
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and determines an oriented geodesic plane there. Similarly, one produces a correspondence between space-like planes in
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
and points in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. Note that we consider the planes oriented only in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. To avoid this asymmetry, one either needs to pass to the projective space (and consider the respective quotient of
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
), or add to the consideration the mirror copy of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, however, for our purposes we prefer not to do this.

If
C\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
is properly convex, then the dual points in
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
to all outward-oriented planes that do not intersect the interior of
C
form the dual properly convex set
C^{*}\subset d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. We say that
C^{*}
is
the Gauss dual
of
C
. By
the Gauss map
we mean the multivalued map
\partial C\rightrightarrows\partial C^{*}
sending a point
p\in\partial C
to the set of the outward normals to all supporting planes to
C
at
p
. It is easy to see that no supporting plane to
C^{*}
is time-like, and that
\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
belongs to the convex side of
C^{*}
. We will call a properly convex set in
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
satisfying these two conditions
future-convex
. Equivalently, a future-convex set is a properly convex set that contains the future cone of every point, or a properly convex set that is the intersection of closed future half-spaces bounded by space-like planes. Conversely, for any future-convex set
C\subset d\mathbb{S}^{3}
we similarly define its dual convex set
C^{*}\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
. This duality is polar, i.e.,
C^{**}=C
.

2.2
Cone-metrics

Let
S
be a closed oriented surface and
V\subset S
be a finite set of points.

Definition 2.1
.

A
triangulation

\mathcal{T}
of
(S,V)
is a collection of simple disjoint paths with endpoints in
V
that cut
S
into triangles. Here a triangle is a 2-disk with three marked points at the boundary (possibly coinciding as points of
V
). Two triangulations with the same vertex set
V
are
equivalent
if they are isotopic by an isotopy fixing
V
.

We denote by
E(\mathcal{T})
the set of edges of
\mathcal{T}
.

Let
d
be a cone-metric on
S
(Definition
1.1
) and
V\subset S
be a finite set containing the singular set
V(d)
of
d
. In this situation we say that
d
is a cone-metric on
(S,V)
.

Definition 2.2
.

A
geodesic triangulation
of
(S,V,d)
is a triangulation of
(S,V)
such that all edges are geodesics in
d
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a triangulation of
(S,V)
and
d
be a cone-metric on
(S,V)
. We say that
d
is
\mathcal{T}
-triangulable
if there is a geodesic triangulation of
(S,V,d)
equivalent to
\mathcal{T}
. If
d
is spherical, then we require additionally that each triangle is convex, i.e., isometric to a triangle in an open hemisphere.

We remark that not all triples
(S,V,d)
, when
d
is a spherical cone-metric, admit a geodesic triangulation even without the condition on the convexity of triangles. An example is the standard sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with
V
being three points contained in an open hemisphere. However, for concave large spherical cone-metrics there are no such obstructions.

Lemma 2.4
.

For each concave large spherical cone-metric
d
on
(S,V)
there exists a geodesic triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of
(S,V,d)
into convex triangles.

This was basically shown by Rivin in
[
57
]
although he does not formulate the convexity condition on the triangles, but it follows from his proof. For surfaces of genus
>1
, which is the case we need, this also follows directly from the Fuchsian version of Theorem
1.8
due to Schlenker
[
64
]
. It says that each concave large spherical cone-metric on
S
can be realized as the dual metric on the boundary of a Fuchsian manifold of the first kind. When we consider the respective dual embedding of the universal cover to
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
, we see that any invariant geodesic triangulation refining the face decomposition projects to a geodesic triangulation of the metric into convex triangles.

We claim that if a concave large spherical cone-metric
d
on
(S,V)
is
\mathcal{T}
-triangulable, then a geodesic triangulation into convex triangles equivalent to
\mathcal{T}
is unique. Indeed, if between two points on the standard 2-sphere there exists a geodesic segment of length
<2\pi
(i.e., the points are not diametrically opposite), then this segment is unique. Hence, if two geodesic triangulations of
(S,V,d)
into convex triangles are isotopic, then the lengths of all edges are equal. It follows that there exists a self-isometry of
(S,V,d)
isotopic to the identity by an isotopy fixing
V
. Consider the Fuchsian realization of
(S,V,d)
given by
[
64
]
. Since the Fuchsian realization is unique, the self-isometry of
d
extends to a self-isometry of the Fuchsian realization. Every Fuchsian manifold of the first kind contains a unique totally geodesic copy of
S
, which then also admits a self-isometry isotopic to the identity. It is, however, easy to see that no closed hyperbolic surface admits such an isometry.

For a map
f:(S_{1},d_{1})\rightarrow(S_{2},d_{2})
between two compact metric spaces we define its
distortion

{\rm dist}(f)
as

{\rm dist}(f):=\sup_{p,q\in S_{1},p\neq q}\left|\ln\frac{d_{2}(f(p),f(q))}{d_{1}(p,q)}\right|.

We say that a sequence of metrics
\{d_{i}\}
on a surface
S
converges to a metric
d

in the Lipschitz sense
if the identity maps
(S,d_{i})\rightarrow(S,d)
have distortions
\varepsilon_{i}\rightarrow 0
. We remark that some authors allow the action by homeomorphisms in the definitions of the Lipschitz convergence, e.g., in
[
58
, Section 6]
. In a more general setting the Lipschitz convergence may be defined between non-homeomorphic metric spaces, see
[
9
, Section 7.2]
.
We denote by
\mathfrak{D}(S,V)
the set of spherical cone-metrics on
(S,V)
that are
\mathcal{T}
-triangulable for at least one triangulation. We endow it with the topology of Lipschitz convergence.

We will need two equivalence relations on
\mathfrak{D}(S,V)
. Denote by
H_{0}(S,V)
the group of self-homeomorphisms of
S
fixing
V
and isotopic to identity, and denote by
H^{\sharp}_{0}(S,V)
its normal subgroup consisting of those homeomorphisms that are isotopic to the identity by an isotopy fixing
V
. The quotient
B_{0}(S,V):=H_{0}(S,V)/H^{\sharp}_{0}(S,V)
is the
pure braid group
of
(S,V)
. The group
H_{0}(S,V)
acts on
\mathfrak{D}(S,V)
and we denote the quotient by
\mathcal{D}(S,V)
, while we denote the quotient by
H^{\sharp}_{0}(S,V)
by
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,V)
. It follows that
\mathcal{D}(S,V)
is the quotient of
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,V)
by
B_{0}(S,V)
. When the context is clear, we may abuse the notation and write
d\in\mathcal{D}(S,V)
, when
d
is a metric representing a class belonging to
\mathcal{D}(S,V)
.
We will mostly deal with the space
\mathcal{D}(S,V)
, though in the last stages of the proof, for technical reasons we will have to employ the space
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,V)
.

For a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of
(S,V)
we denote by
\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathcal{T})\subset\mathfrak{D}(S,V)
the subset of
\mathcal{T}
-triangulable metrics. It is
H^{\sharp}_{0}(S,V)
-invariant, and we denote by
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,\mathcal{T})\subset\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,V)
its quotient.
The set
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,\mathcal{T})
can be considered as an open polyhedron in
\mathbb{R}^{E(\mathcal{T})}
defined by the strict triangle inequalities and the conditions on edge lengths to be in
(0,\pi)
. The charts
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,\mathcal{T})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{E(\mathcal{T})}
endow
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,V)
with the structure of a real-analytic manifold of dimension
3(n-k)
where
n:=|V|
and
k:=\chi(S)
. It is easy to see that this topology coincides with the quotient topology induced from
\mathfrak{D}(S,V)
. The group
B_{0}(S,V)
acts on
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,V)
freely and properly discontinuously, thereby
\mathcal{D}(S,V)
is also a real-analytic manifold of dimension
3(n-k)
.

Pick
d\in\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathcal{T})
. There is a canonical section through
d
of the forgetful map
\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathcal{T})\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,\mathcal{T})
. Indeed, for any two convex spherical triangles
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
with marked vertices there exists a canonical “affine” homeomorphism between them preserving the marking. To see this, develop
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
on the standard sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
embedded in Euclidean 3-space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Project
T_{1}
radially to the Euclidean triangle subtending it, send it by the unique linear map to the affine triangle subtending
T_{2}
and project it radially to
T_{2}
. This defines the desired map. For every class
d\in\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathcal{T})
such affine maps produce the canonical section of
\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathcal{T})\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,\mathcal{T})
through
d
. Acting by
H^{\sharp}_{0}(S,V)
on the metrics of this section, one can see that
\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathcal{T})\cong\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,\mathcal{T})\times H^{\sharp}_{0}(S,V)
. This produces the structure of a principal bundle on
\mathfrak{D}(S,V)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,V)
with the structure group
H^{\sharp}_{0}(S,V)
. Since the latter is known to be contractible
[
30
]
, this bundle is trivial.

We will need the following simple lemma:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
d\in\mathfrak{D}(S,V)
. There exists a neighborhood
U\ni d
in
\mathfrak{D}(S,V)
such that there are only finitely many classes of triangulations
\mathcal{T}
, for which there exists a
\mathcal{T}
-triangulable metric in
U
.

Proof.

We show that for any
v,w\in V
there are only finitely many isotopy classes on
S\backslash V
of paths between
v
and
w
containing a geodesic path of length
\leq\pi
in
d
. Indeed, suppose the converse.
The set of all geodesic paths from
v
to
w
of length
\leq\pi
is uniformly Lipschitz. Thus, by the Arzelá–Ascoli Theorem it is compact. Hence, if there are infinitely many such classes, we can take a geodesic representative of length
\leq\pi
from each, and extract a converging subsequence. However, every geodesic path of length
<\pi
between
v
and
w
is isolated, i.e., it has a neighborhood where there are no other geodesic paths on
S\backslash V
between its endpoints, and every geodesic path of length
\pi
is isolated from geodesic representatives of other classes on
S\backslash V
. Thus, no path can appear in the limit.

This argument implies that there are finitely many equivalence classes of triangulations that have a geodesic representative in
d
with all edges
\leq\pi
. Thus, there are finitely many triangulations
\mathcal{T}
such that
d
belongs to the closure of
\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathcal{T})
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Denote by
\mathfrak{D}_{c}(S,V)\subset\mathfrak{D}(S,V)
the subset of concave spherical cone-metrics on
(S,V)
with
V(d)=V
and by
\overline{\mathfrak{D}}_{c}(S,V)\subset\mathfrak{D}(S,V)
denote just the subset of concave spherical cone-metrics on
(S,V)
. The latter is the topological closure of the former in
\mathfrak{D}(S,V)
. The same notation applies to the respective subsets of
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(S,V)
and
\mathcal{D}(S,V)
, and also when
V
is replaced by
\mathcal{T}
.

Now let
M
be an admissible 3-manifold,
V\subset\partial M
be a finite set of points with at least one point at each boundary component. We denote by
\mathfrak{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
the subset of concave large for
M
spherical cone-metrics on
(\partial M,V)
with
V(d)=V
. We denote by
\overline{\mathfrak{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)\subset\mathfrak{D}(\partial M,V)
just the subset of concave large for
M
spherical cone-metrics on
(\partial M,V)
. We note that here our notation has a downside as
\overline{\mathfrak{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)
is not the topological closure of
\mathfrak{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
because the topological closure also contains metrics with closed contractible in
M
geodesics of length
2\pi
. The same notation applies to the respective subsets of
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(\partial M,V)
and
\mathcal{D}(\partial M,V)
, and also when
V
is replaced by
\mathcal{T}
. We will show that
\mathfrak{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
is an open subset of
\mathfrak{D}(\partial M,V)
. Clearly, the concavity condition is open, so we need to check the condition of largeness for
M
. For a metric
d
on
\partial M
denote by
{\rm ml}_{M}(d)
the infimum of lengths of all closed geodesics in
(\partial M,d)
that are contractible in
M
.

We denote by
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{l}
the metric circle of length
l
. A
closed global geodesic
in a metric space
(S,d)
is the image of an isometric map
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{l}\rightarrow(S,d)
(here it is crucial that it preserves distances in
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{l}
globally). A
closed
\varepsilon
-quasi-geodesic
is the image of
S^{1}_{l}
under a map
S^{1}_{l}\rightarrow(S,d)
with distortion at most
\varepsilon
. The following consequence of the Arzelá–Ascoli Theorem was shown in
[
58
, Theorem 6.1]
:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\{d_{i}\}
be a sequence of metrics on
S
converging in the Lipschitz sense to a metric
d
. Let
\{\gamma_{i}\subset S\}
be closed
\varepsilon_{i}
-quasi-geodesics in
d_{i}
with
\varepsilon_{i}\rightarrow 0
and the lengths
l_{i}
of
\gamma_{i}
converging to
l>0
. Then up to extracting a subsequence,
\gamma_{i}
converge to a closed global geodesic
\gamma\subset S
in
d
of length
l
.

From this we can deduce

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
\{d_{i}\}
be a sequence of metrics on
\partial M
converging in the Lipschitz sence to a metric
d
. Then

{\rm ml}_{M}(d)\leq\liminf_{i\rightarrow\infty}{\rm ml}_{M}(d_{i}).

Proof.

When a sequence of closed curves on
\partial M
converges, their free homotopy classes stabilize. Hence, the limit of a sequence of curves in
\partial M
that are contractible in
M
is also contractible in
M
. Next, for each closed geodesic in
(\partial M,d_{i})
of length
l
that is contractible in
M
there exists a closed global geodesic of length at most
l
that is contractible in
M
. Indeed, this is because there is a correspondence between closed geodesics in
(\partial M,d_{i})
contractible in
M
and closed geodesics in
(\partial\widetilde{M},d_{i})
, where
\widetilde{M}
is the universal cover of
M
. Thus, by Lemma
2.6
the infimum
{\rm ml}_{M}(d_{i})
is realized by a closed global geodesic
\gamma_{i}
contractible in
M
. Applying Lemma
2.6
again, we see that a subsequence of
\gamma_{i}
converges to a closed global geodesic in
(\partial M,d)
contractible in
M
of length
\liminf_{i\rightarrow\infty}{\rm ml}_{M}(d_{i})
.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

The function
{\rm ml}_{M}
is lower-semicontinuous in the Lipschitz topology of metrics on
\partial M
, i.e., for each
l\in\mathbb{R}
the set of metrics
d
on
\partial M
satisfying
{\rm ml}_{M}(d)>l
is open. In particular,
\mathfrak{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
is an open subset of
\mathfrak{D}(\partial M,V)
.

2.3
Convex cocompact hyperbolic manifolds

Let
g
be a hyperbolic metric on
M
such that
\partial M
is locally convex. Recall that we denote by
G
the group of orientation-preserving isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. It is naturally isomorphic to
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
.

A developing map embeds the universal cover
(\widetilde{M},g)
as a convex set in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
(see
[
10
, Section 1.4]
) and the respective holonomy map is a discrete and faithful representation
\rho:\pi_{1}(M)\rightarrow G
. The quotient
N
of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
by
\rho
is a complete hyperbolic 3-manifold, to which
M
naturally embeds inducing an isomorphism of the fundamental groups. Denote this embedding by
\iota:M\hookrightarrow N
. It is easy to see that
N
is homeomorphic to
{\rm int}(M)
by a map
\kappa
such that
\iota\circ\kappa
is homotopic to the identity.

For the rest of the paper, however, we will use a convention that
N
is a smooth 3-manifold without a hyperbolic structure, but homeomorphic to
{\rm int}(M)
. We will consider such a homeomorphism fixed for the whole paper, so, particularly, there is a fixed 1-1 correspondence between ends of
N
and components of
\partial M
. The construction above endows
N
with a convex cocompact hyperbolic metric defined up to isotopy, which we denote by
\overline{g}
, and with an isometric embedding
\iota:(M,g)\rightarrow(N,\overline{g})
.
We will denote the pair
(N,\overline{g})
rather by
N(\overline{g})
, so
N(\overline{g})
is a hyperbolic 3-manifold, while
N
is the underlying smooth 3-manifold. We denote by
\partial_{\infty}N(\overline{g})
its boundary at infinity, which is homeomorphic to
\partial M
and is equipped with a conformal structure. We denote by
M(g)
the
\iota
-image of
(M,g)
in
N(\overline{g})
. Generally we denote by
\overline{g}
the convex cocompact hyperbolic metric on
N
obtained from a metric
g
as above. However, sometimes we will abuse the notation, and will denote by
\overline{g}
a convex cocompact hyperbolic metric on
N
in the absence of a metric
g
.

Abusing the notation, we denote the extension of
\overline{g}
to the universal cover
\widetilde{N}
still by
\overline{g}
, and the hyperbolic 3-manifold
(\widetilde{N},\overline{g})
by
\widetilde{N}(\overline{g})
. We will frequently identify
\widetilde{N}(\overline{g})
with
\mathbb{H}^{3}
by an arbitrary developing map (recall that a developing map is defined up to a global isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
).
We denote by
\widetilde{M}(g)
the respective embedding of the universal cover of
M(g)
as a convex set.
Let
\rho_{\overline{g}}:\pi_{1}(M)\rightarrow G
be the corresponding holonomy representation. We denote by
\Lambda(\overline{g})=\Lambda(\rho_{\overline{g}})
its limit set and by
\widetilde{C}(\overline{g})=\widetilde{C}(\rho_{\overline{g}})
we denote the convex hull of
\Lambda(\overline{g})
. If
\Lambda(\overline{g})
is contained in a circle, we say that
\overline{g}
and
\rho_{\overline{g}}
are
Fuchsian
. They are
Fuchsian of the first kind
if
\Lambda(\overline{g})
is a circle, and
of the second kind
otherwise. The set
\widetilde{C}(\overline{g})
is
\rho_{\overline{g}}
-invariant and projects to the
convex core

C(\overline{g})\subset N(\overline{g})
. The latter is totally convex, and is homeomorphic to
M
, except in the Fuchsian case. We will refer to the connected components of
N(\overline{g})\backslash C(\overline{g})
and of
\widetilde{N}(\overline{g})\backslash\widetilde{C}(\overline{g})
as to the
geometric ends
of
N(\overline{g})
and
\widetilde{N}(\overline{g})
. Since the
\rho_{\overline{g}}
-orbit of any point of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
accumulates at
\Lambda(\overline{g})
,
C(\overline{g})
is contained in any totally convex subset of
N(\overline{g})
. It is also well-known that

Lemma 2.9
.

The induced path metric on
\partial C(\overline{g})
is hyperbolic.

See, e.g.,
[
19
]
or
[
67
]
. Thus, a connected component of
\partial C(\overline{g})
is an embedded hyperbolic surface, which, however, may not be totally geodesic as it may be bent along a geodesic lamination. Here we mean by
\partial C(\overline{g})
the boundary in
N(\overline{g})
, so in the Fuchsian case, when
C(\overline{g})
is 2-dimensional, we mean
\partial C(\overline{g})=C(\overline{g})
. In the Fuchsian case of the first kind
C(\overline{g})
is an embedded closed totally geodesic surface, and
M
is an interval bundle over this surface. In the Fuchsian case of second kind
C(\overline{g})
is a totally geodesic surface with geodesic relative boundary, and
M
is a handlebody, so it has compressible boundary.

Denote by
\mathcal{CH}(N)
the space of convex cocompact hyperbolic metrics on
N
, where we consider two metrics equivalent if they are related by a self-homeomorphism of
M
isotopic to the identity on
M
, where we use
N\cong{\rm int}(M)
. The rest of this section is devoted to the discussion of a topology on this space. While it seems to be folklore, we did not find in the literature a reference suitable to our needs. Thereby, we devote some time to extract it from the existing literature. We will frequently abuse the notation and write
\overline{g}\in\mathcal{CH}(N)
meaning that
\overline{g}
is a concrete convex cocompact hyperbolic metric on
N
. Note that one can also consider metrics up to isotopy on
N
, which a priori can lead to a stricter equivalence. However, this is not the case, see Remark
2.10
, but we will not use it.

It turns out to be easier to describe the topology on the space
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N)
of convex cocompact hyperbolic metrics on
N
up to homotopy, i.e., up to isometry homotopic to the identity. Hence, first we accomplish this, and then we discuss the difference between
\mathcal{CH}(N)
and
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N)
.

We denote by
\mathcal{R}(\pi_{1}(M),G)
the space of representations of
\pi_{1}(M)
in
G
. The group
G
acts on
\mathcal{R}(\pi_{1}(M),G)
by conjugation, and we denote the resulting quotient by
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{1}(M),G)
. Since
G\cong{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is an algebraic group over
\mathbb{C}
, the space
\mathcal{R}(\pi_{1}(M),G)
can be viewed as a complex algebraic variety, and we consider it with the induced topology. We endow the space
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{1}(M),G)
with the quotient topology. Note that
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{1}(M),G)
is not even a Hausdorff space, and should not be confused with
the character variety
, which is a more accurate quotient that can be constructed by means of algebraic geometry. However, we are interested only in the quotient of an invariant open smooth subset of
\mathcal{R}(\pi_{1}(M),G)
, corresponding to the holonomies of convex cocompact metrics, over which the action behaves well. We will be anyway calling the elements of
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{1}(M),G)

characters
.

Denote by
\mathcal{HM}_{h}(N)
the space of complete hyperbolic metrics on
N
up to homotopy, so
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N)\subset\mathcal{HM}_{h}(N)
.
The character of holonomy sends injectively
\mathcal{HM}_{h}(N)
to
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{1}(M),G)
. Indeed, if two hyperbolic metrics
\overline{g}_{1}
,
\overline{g}_{2}
have the same character, then we have an orientation-preserving isometry between
N(\overline{g}_{1})
and
N(\overline{g}_{2})
inducing the identity map on the fundamental groups. Since
M
is aspherical,
\overline{g}_{1}
and
\overline{g}_{2}
are homotopic. We endow
\mathcal{HM}_{h}(N)
with the topology pull-backed from
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{1}(M),G)
. Note that we can endow
\mathcal{HM}_{h}(N)
with a more natural topology, which comes from
C^{\infty}
-convergence on compact subsets for developing maps. Then the so-called Ehresmann-Thurston Theorem implies that these topologies are the same. (The non-trivial part is that the convergence of holonomies implies, up to homotopy, the convergence of the developing maps. It follows, e.g., from applying
[
10
, Theorem 1.7.1]
to compact totally convex subsets exhausting
N
.)

Let
\overline{g}\in\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N)
and
\rho_{\overline{g}}
be its holonomy. It follows from Marden’s Isomorphism Theorem
[
38
, Section 8]
that a convex cocompact representation
\rho\in\mathcal{R}(\pi_{1}(M),G)
is a holonomy representation of a convex cocompact metric on
N
if and only if it is related to
\rho_{\overline{g}}
by a quasi-conformal deformation. Thus, the set of holonomy representations of metrics from
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N)
can be identified with the set
\mathcal{QC}(\rho_{\overline{g}})
of quasi-conformal deformations of
\rho_{\overline{g}}
. In particular, it is connected. Marden’s Stability Theorem
[
38
, Section 10]
implies also that it is an open subset of the smooth part of
\mathcal{R}(\pi_{1}(M),G)
. The set
\mathcal{QC}(\rho_{\overline{g}})
stays invariant under the action of
G
by conjugation, and
G
acts freely and smoothly on it. Moreover,
[
38
, Theorem 10.8]
implies that this action is proper, so the quotient is a complex manifold. Other approaches to the properness can be seen in
[
34
, Proposition 1.1]
or in
[
73
, Lemma 6.24]
.
We identify
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N)
with this quotient. We refer to the expositions in
[
40
, Chapter 5]
and
[
11
, Section 7]
.

Now we discuss the difference between equivalences of metrics up to homotopy and up to isotopy. We denote by
\textrm{MCG}(M)
the
mapping class group
of
M
, i.e., the group of isotopy classes of orientation-preserving self-homeomorphisms of
M
. We denote by
\textrm{MCG}_{h}(M)\subset\textrm{MCG}(M)
the subgroup of isotopy classes of orientation-preserving self-homeomorphisms that are homotopic to the identity. There is a natural homomorphism
\textrm{MCG}(M)\rightarrow{\rm Out}(\pi_{1}(M))
, which sends a homeomorphism class to the induced outer automorphism of
\pi_{1}(M)
. Clearly,
\textrm{MCG}_{h}(M)
belongs to the kernel of this homomorphism.

The work of Waldhausen
[
71
]
establishes that when the boundary of
M
is incompressible, the group
\textrm{MCG}_{h}(M)
is trivial. However, this is no longer the case when the boundary is compressible. Let
\gamma\subset\partial M
be an oriented simple closed curve that is contractible in
M
, and
D\subset M
be an embedded 2-disk contracting
\gamma
, which exists thanks to the Dehn Lemma. Do a Dehn twist around
\gamma
. Let
D\times[-1,1]\subset M
be a small neighborhood of
D
such that
D\times\{0\}=D
and
\partial D\times[-1,1]
is an annulus around
\gamma
supporting the Dehn twist. The Dehn twist naturally extends to
D\times[-1,1]
. Extending it by identity outside of
D\times[-1,1]
, we obtain a self-homeomorphism of
M
called a
twist
of
M
. One can see that it is homotopic to the identity, but not isotopic. McCullough and Miller showed in
[
43
]
that the kernel of
\textrm{MCG}(M)\rightarrow{\rm Out}(\pi_{1}(M))
is generated by twists. Thereby,
\textrm{MCG}_{h}(M)
is equal to the kernel and is generated by twists. Let
S_{j}
be a connected component of
\partial M
. We denote by
\textrm{MCG}_{h,j}(M)
the subgroup of
\textrm{MCG}_{h}(M)
generated by twists along curves belonging to
S_{j}
. As compressing disks of disjoint simple closed curves in
\partial M
can be made disjoint,
\textrm{MCG}_{h}(M)
is the direct product of all
\textrm{MCG}_{h,j}(M)
.

Remark 2.10
.

One can consider a similarly-defined group
\textrm{MCG}_{h}(N)
. We have a morphism
\textrm{MCG}_{h}(M)\rightarrow\textrm{MCG}_{h}(N)
. One can show that this is an isomorphism. We will not rely on it, hence we do not go into the details of it.

We denote by
\mathcal{TS}(\partial M)
the Teichmüller space of
\partial M
. It is the direct product of the Teichmüller spaces of all connected components of
\partial M
. The group
\textrm{MCG}_{h}(M)
acts on
\mathcal{TS}(\partial M)
and the action splits on actions of
\textrm{MCG}_{h,j}(M)
on the respective components of
\mathcal{TS}(\partial M)
. As the action of the mapping class group of
\partial M
on
\mathcal{TS}(\partial M)
is propely discontinuous (see, e.g.,
[

), so is the action of
\textrm{MCG}_{h}(M)
. In
[

Marden shows that this action is free. We denote by
\mathcal{TS}_{h}(\partial M,M)
the quotient of
\mathcal{TS}(\partial M)
over this action.

In
[

Marden states a version of the Ahlfors–Bers Theorem (based also on the works of Kra and Maskit), which says that if
\overline{g}
is a convex cocompact hyperbolic metric on

on

defines a biholomorphic map from the quotient of

by conjugation of

[

]
and references therein. Additional expositions can be found in
[

. Due to the discussion above, we can state this result as

Theorem 2.11

The map
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N)\rightarrow\mathcal{TS}_{h}(\partial M,M)
sending a class represented by a metric

to the class of its conformal structure at infinity

is a biholomorphic map.

Finally, we return to

.

The map

We endow

2.4
Polyhedral hyperbolic manifolds

Definition 2.13
.

We say that a hyperbolic metric
g
on
M
is
polyhedral
if each point
p\in\partial M(g)
has a neighborhood isometric to a convex polyhedral set in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. It is
strictly polyhedral
if, in addition, the distance between the boundary and the convex core is positive.

For
p\in\partial M
the
spherical link
of
p
in
M(g)
is the part of the unit sphere in
T_{p}N(\overline{g})
that is cut out by the directions to
M(g)
. If the spherical link of
p
is a half-sphere, then
p
is called
regular
in
M(g)
. If it is a spherical lune, then
p
is called a
ridge point
in
M(g)
. Otherwise, it is called a
vertex
of
M(g)
, and its spherical link is a convex spherical polygon.
The closures of the connected components of the set of regular points in
M(g)
are called
faces
of
M(g)
and constitute
the face decomposition
of
M(g)
. We denote by
F(g)
the set of faces of
M(g)
. The
edges
of
M(g)
are closures of the connected components of the set of ridge points of
M(g)
.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
g
be a strictly polyhedral metric on
M
. Then each edge is a segment between vertices and all faces are isometric to compact hyperbolic polygons.

Proof.

If an edge can be extended infinitely at least in one direction or is a closed geodesic, then its lift to the universal cover ends in the limit set
\Lambda(\overline{g})
. Hence, the distance to
\widetilde{C}(\overline{g})
would tend to zero along this lift, which is a contradiction as
\partial M
is compact and the distance to the convex core of
g
is positive.

By the same reasoning, every face of
\widetilde{M}(g)
is compact. It is also simply connected. It follows that every face of
M(g)
is isometric to a compact hyperbolic polygon.

∎

Let
g
be a strictly polyhedral metric on
M
. Consider
\widetilde{M}(g)\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
and its Gauss dual
\widetilde{M}(g)^{*}\subset d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. As
g
is strictly polyhedral, no supporting plane to
\widetilde{M}(g)
passes through a point of
\Lambda(\overline{g})
. Indeed, this would imply the existence on
\partial\widetilde{M}(g)
of a curve, infinite in one direction, such that the distance to
\widetilde{C}(\overline{g})
would tend to zero along it. Hence, each boundary component of
\partial\widetilde{M}(g)^{*}
is space-like, so the induced metric is a spherical cone-metric. Another consequence of strict polyhedrality is that
\rho_{\overline{g}}
acts on
\partial\widetilde{M}(g)^{*}
freely. Indeed, a point in
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
is fixed by
\rho_{\overline{g}}(\gamma)
only if it is dual to a plane in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
containing the axis of the isometry
\rho_{\overline{g}}(\gamma)
. But all such axes are contained in
\widetilde{C}(\overline{g})
. Therefore, by taking the quotient of
\partial\widetilde{M}(g)^{*}
, we obtain a spherical cone-metric
d
on
\partial M
, determined up to isotopy. We say that
d
is the
dual metric
induced by
g
on
\partial M
.

There is another way to construct
d
. For each vertex
v
of
M(g)
we consider its spherical link, which is a convex spherical polygon, and its spherical polar dual is another convex spherical polygon. By gluing together these dual polygons with respect to the face decomposition of
M(g)
, we obtain
d
. It is easy to see from this description that
d
is concave.

The main result of the work
[
12
]
implies that if
C\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
is a convex polyhedral set, then any two points in
\partial C^{*}
at distance less than
\pi
can be connected by a unique minimizing intrinsic geodesic segment in
\partial C^{*}
. Moreover, a refined version
[
12
, Theorem 4.1.1]
also states that if
\gamma\subset\partial C^{*}
is a closed intrinsic geodesic of length
2\pi
, then it encloses a cusp point of
C^{*}
, i.e., a point of tangency with
\partial_{\infty}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. This particularly implies

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
g
be a strictly polyhedral metric on
M
. Then the dual metric
d
on
\partial M
is large for
M
.

2.5
Topology on the space of strictly polyhedral metrics

Let
V\subset\partial M
be a finite set,
g
be a strictly polyhedral metric on
\partial M
and
f:V\rightarrow F(g)
be a bijection such that the points from the
j
-th component
S_{j}
of
\partial M
mark faces of this component. We say that the pair
(g,f)
is
a strictly polyhedral metric on
M
with faces marked by
V
. We denote by
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
the space of strictly polyhedral metrics
g
on
M
with faces marked by
V
up to isometry preserving the marking and isotopic to the identity. We will frequently abuse the notation and write

meaning that some
f
is also chosen and with the class of
g
it is in
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
. Our current goal is to endow
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
with a natural topology. We will achieve this by embedding it into a larger space.

For
\overline{g}\in\mathcal{CH}(N)
consider an oriented plane in
\widetilde{N}(\overline{g})=\mathbb{H}^{3}
. It gives rise to an immersed oriented plane in
N(\overline{g})
. We say that an oriented plane is
positive
if it is at positive distance from
\widetilde{C}(\overline{g})
and is oriented outwards
\widetilde{C}(\overline{g})
. We say the same about the immersed image of the plane. We denote by
\mathcal{CH}(N,V)
the space of convex cocompact hyperbolic metrics on
N
equipped with a set of immersed positive planes marked by
V
, considered up to isometries sending marked planes to marked planes, preserving the marking and isotopic to the identity. When we say that the planes are marked by
V
, we mean that the planes in the geometric end of
N(\overline{g})
corresponding to
S_{j}
are marked by points from
S_{j}
. The elements of
\mathcal{CH}(N,V)
are represented by pairs
(\overline{g},f)
, where
\overline{g}
is a convex cocompact hyperbolic metric on
N
and
f
is a map from
V
onto a set of immersed positive planes. We will write
f_{v}
instead of
f(v)
. We are going now to endow the space
\mathcal{CH}(N,V)
with a natural topology.

First, we have to define similarly a space
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N,V)
with isotopy replaced by homotopy. As with
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N)
, it is easier to endow
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N,V)
with a topology. We denote by
(d\mathbb{S}^{3})^{V}
the space of maps
\widetilde{f}:V\rightarrow d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. We can interpret
(d\mathbb{S}^{3})^{V}
as the configuration space of oriented planes in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
marked by
V
. We consider the direct product

\mathcal{R}(\pi_{1}(M),G)\times(d\mathbb{S}^{3})^{V}

with a left action of
G
on it, by conjugation on the first factor and by isometries on the second factor. There is also a left action of
\pi_{1}(M)
given by
\gamma(\rho,\widetilde{f})=(\rho,\rho(\gamma)\widetilde{f})
, where
(\rho,\widetilde{f})\in\mathcal{R}(\pi_{1}(M),G)\times(d\mathbb{S}^{3})^{V}
and
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(M)
. These two actions commute, and the standard approach via developing maps allows us to inject
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N,V)
in the quotient. Since the action of
G
on the holonomies of convex cocompact metrics is free and proper (see Section
2.3
), and for every such holonomy
\rho
, its action on the space of positive planes is free and properly discontinuous, this endows
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N,V)
with the topology of a smooth manifold of dimension
3(n-k)
where
k
is the Euler characteristic of
\partial M
.

For
\overline{g}\in\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N,V)
the fiber of the forgetful map
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N,V)\rightarrow\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N)
can be naturally identified with the space of positive planes in
N(\overline{g})
marked by
V
. Recall that the action of
{\rm MCG}_{h}(M)
on
\mathcal{CH}(N)
is free, properly discontinuous and determines a covering map
\mathcal{CH}(N)\rightarrow\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N)
. Consider the commutative diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{CH}(N,V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N,V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{CH}(N)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N)}

Using this diagram, we pull-back the topology on
\mathcal{CH}_{h}(N,V)
to a topology on
\mathcal{CH}(N,V)
making it also a smooth manifold of dimension
3(n-k)
.
In Section
5.1
we will show that the vertical forgetful maps are fiber bundles.

For
g\in\mathcal{P}(M,V)
every face gives rise to an immersed plane, which we consider oriented outwards
M(g)
. As shown in Section
2.4
, the lift of any such plane does not contain points of
\Lambda(\overline{g})
at infinity, hence, these planes are at positive distance from
C(\overline{g})
. This allows us to consider
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
as a subset of
\mathcal{CH}(N,V)
. Clearly, it is open. We endow
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
with the induced topology.

Lemma 2.16
.

For every admissible
M
if
V
has sufficiently many points in every component of
\partial M
, then the space
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
is non-empty.

Proof.

We show even more, that for every
\overline{g}\in\mathcal{CH}(N)
one can choose sufficiently large
V
so that there exists
g\in\mathcal{P}(M,V)
such that
\overline{g}
is the underlying metric on
N
. Indeed, for a component
S_{j}
of
\partial M
let
\nu_{j}:\pi_{1}(S_{j})\rightarrow\pi_{1}(M)
be the push-forward homomorphism induced by the inclusion. Consider
\widetilde{N}(\overline{g})\cong\mathbb{H}^{3}
, there exists a component
\Omega_{j}
of
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}\backslash\Lambda(\overline{g})
invariant under
\rho_{\overline{g}}|_{{\rm im}(\nu_{j})}
. There exists a compact fundamental domain in
\Omega_{j}
for the latter action. As
\Omega_{j}
is open in
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
, there exists a finite cover of the chosen fundamental domain by disks in
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
contained in
\Omega_{j}
. Every such disk determines a plane in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
that is at positive distance from
\widetilde{C}(\overline{g})
. We do this for every component of
\partial M
and then consider the union of
\rho_{\overline{g}}
-orbits of all chosen planes. Take the intersection of all the half-spaces containing
\widetilde{C}(\overline{g})
bounded by these planes. It is evident that the
\rho_{\overline{g}}
-quotient of this set is homeomorphic to
M
and is a strictly polyhedral manifold.
∎

For every pair
(\overline{g},f)\in\mathcal{CH}(N,V)
we consider the intersection
K
of all the closed negative immersed half-spaces in
N(\overline{g})
bounded by the immersed marked planes. We denote by
\overline{\mathcal{P}}(M,V)
the subset of
\mathcal{CH}(N,V)
such that (1)
K
is homeomorphic to
M
; (2)
f
is injective; (3) each marked plane is supporting to
K
. We remark that here we use a non-intuitive notation as
\overline{\mathcal{P}}(M,V)
is not a topological closure of
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
: the latter also contains configurations with non-injective markings and with cusps. Our motivation for this notation is that an element of
\overline{P}(M,V)
determines a dual metric in
\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{c}(\partial M,V)
. We denote by
g
the metric on
M
induced by a homeomorphism between
M
and
K
, where a homeomorphism is chosen so that when it is composed with our fixed homeomorphism
N\cong{\rm int}(M)
, we get the identity on the fundamental groups. The metric
g
is determined up to isotopy. We will represent the elements of
\overline{\mathcal{P}}(M,V)
by pairs
(g,f)
. For every such pair there is a subset
V(g)\subseteq V
where
v\in V(g)
if and only if the intersection of the plane
f_{v}
with
\partial M(g)
has non-empty relative interior, i.e., corresponds to a face. A pair
(g,f)
also determines a class in
\mathcal{P}(M,V(g))
.

For
g\in\mathcal{P}(M,V)
the dual metric
d
is defined on
\partial M
up to isotopy, so it determines an element of
\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
. For a pair
(g,f)
representing an element of
\overline{\mathcal{P}}(M,V)
the dual points to the lifts of the planes

belong to the boundary of the dual set
\widetilde{M}(g)^{*}
. Hence, in this case we may consider the class of the dual metric in
\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)
. We denote the obtained map by

\mathcal{I}_{V}:\overline{\mathcal{P}}(M,V)\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V).

It is not hard to see that it is continuous. We now show that it is
C^{1}
over
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
.

First, if
g\in\mathcal{P}(M,V)
and
e
is an edge of
M(g)
, then
e
remains to be an edge in some neighborhood of
g
in
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
. From our definition of the topology on
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
, when we vary
g
smoothly, the planes containing faces of
\widetilde{M}(g)
vary smoothly. This implies

Lemma 2.17
.

The dihedral angle of
e
is a smooth function in a neighborhood of
g
in
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
.

Now we prove

Lemma 2.18
.

The map
\mathcal{I}_{V}
is
C^{1}
over
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
.

Proof.

Let
g\in\mathcal{P}(M,V)
and
d
be the respective dual metric. The boundary of
\widetilde{M}(g)^{*}
is decomposed into faces, which are compact convex spherical polygons. It projects to a decomposition of
(\partial M,V)
into faces. Let
\mathcal{T}
be any triangulation of
(\partial M,V)
refining this decomposition. The metric
d
is
\mathcal{T}
-triangulable. We need to show that the edge-lengths of
\mathcal{T}
are
C^{1}
-functions of
g
. Due to Lemma
2.17
, it remains only to check what happens when there are non-triangular faces in the face decomposition of
\widetilde{M}(g)^{*}
, or, equivalently, when there are non-simple vertices of
\widetilde{M}(g)
.

Let
Q\subset d\mathbb{S}^{3}
be a convex geodesic space-like polygon with more than three vertices representing a face of
\widetilde{M}(g)
,
V_{Q}
be the set of its vertices,
\widetilde{e}
be its interior edge with endpoints
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
representing an edge
e
of
\mathcal{T}
. We denote by
s
the de Sitter distance between
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
.
When we slightly vary the positions of
V_{Q}
and take the convex hull, we may obtain a 3-dimensional polytope, whose boundary is naturally divided into two parts, both of which can be considered as variations of
Q
. Let us restrict the attention to one of the parts, we call it
upper
. If the deformation is small enough, this polytope has only space-like faces, and the total angles at the vertices of the upper part in the intrinsic metric stay less than
\pi
. Let
X_{Q}\subset(d\mathbb{S}^{3})^{V_{Q}}
be a small enough neighborhood of the initial position of
V_{Q}
, for which this holds. Then we can identify a deformation of
\widetilde{e}
in the upper part, which is a broken line if
\widetilde{e}
is not an edge of the obtained polytope. Consider the length
\widetilde{l}
of
\widetilde{e}
as a function over
X_{Q}
. There exists a small neighborhood
Y
of
g
in
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
, over which the length
l
of
e
can be defined, and a smooth map
q:Y\rightarrow X_{Q}
such that
l=\widetilde{l}\circ q
. Thus, it is enough to show that
\widetilde{l}
is a
C^{1}
-function.

Now let
\mathcal{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{T}_{r}
be all possible triangulations of
Q
. Fix some
\mathcal{T}_{i}
and consider deformations of
Q
triangulated with
\mathcal{T}_{i}
under small variations of
V_{Q}
. Here the obtained polyhedral surface may become non-convex. We assume that
X_{Q}
is small enough that for all
\mathcal{T}_{i}
all faces of the deformed surface are space-like and all angles of the vertices in the intrinsic metric are less than
\pi
. Let
l_{i}
be the length of
\widetilde{e}
under such deformations. Clearly, it is a smooth function in
X_{Q}
as all the edge-lengths of
\mathcal{T}_{i}
are smooth functions. For each
v\in V_{Q}
we choose coordinates such that two of them, called
horizontal
, are the coordinates of the projection of
v
to the plane containing
Q
in the initial moment, and the third, called
vertical
, is the oriented length of a perpendicular from
v
to this plane. Note that since the plane is space-like, the perpendicular is time-like, but here we consider its length as a real number (with the sign depending on the position of
v
with respect to the plane).

We claim that all vertical partial derivatives of
l_{i}
at the initial moment are zero. Indeed, let
ABC
be a de Sitter triangle with
BC
time-like,
AB
and
AC
space-like and
AB
orthogonal to
BC
. Denote by
a
,
b
,
c
the respective side-lengths (again, we treat the length of
BC
as a real number). Then we have

\cos(b)=\cos(c)\cosh(a).

Thus, for fixed
c\neq 0
,
\partial b/\partial a=0
at
a=0
. Thus, the vertical partial derivatives of all edge-lengths of
\mathcal{T}_{i}
are zero. In turn, this shows that the vertical partial derivatives of
l_{i}
are zero too. Now we remark that all horizontal partial derivatives of
l_{i}
in the initial moment coincide with the horizontal partial derivatives of
s
, the de Sitter distance between the endpoints of
\widetilde{e}
.

We return to the convex hull of
V_{Q}
. The domain
X_{Q}
admits a piecewise-smooth decomposition into cells
E_{1},\ldots,E_{r}
corresponding to the face triangulations of the upper part of
{\rm conv}(V_{Q})
. The function
\widetilde{l}
coincides with
l_{i}
over
E_{i}
. The argument above shows that in the initial moment the differential of each
l_{i}
coincides with the differential of
s
. Thus,
\widetilde{l}
is differentiable in the initial moment. Moreover, it follows that at any point of
X_{Q}
belonging to several
E_{i}
, the differentials of the respective

coincide. This implies that
\widetilde{l}
is differentiable over
X_{Q}
. Since all partial derivatives of
l_{i}
are continuous over
E_{i}
and coincide with the partial derivatives of
E_{j}
at the common points of
E_{i}
and
E_{j}
, we see that
\widetilde{l}
is
C^{1}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

The goal of this section is to prove

Lemma 3.1

If

is such that

, then

.

Take two copies of
M
and identify the corresponding boundary points reversing the orientation. We obtain a closed 3-manifold
M^{D}
called
the double of

. A strictly polyhedral hyperbolic metric
g
on

produces a metric
g^{D}
on
M^{D}
, which is a hyperbolic cone-metric with the singular locus coming from the edges of
M(g)
and with all cone-angles smaller than

. We refer to
[
32
]
,
[

]
or
[

]
for introductions to hyperbolic cone-3-manifolds. To uniformize the notation, we will write
M^{D}(g^{D})
for the hyperbolic cone-3-manifold
(M^{D},g^{D})
. We denote by
\Sigma(g^{D})
the singular locus of

and denote by

We claim that if
g_{0}
,
g_{1}

\mathcal{P}(M,V)
, then

,

are isotopic as subsets of

.
Indeed, for every
g\in\mathcal{P}(M,V)
that has a vertex of degree
>3
and for every four marked planes in

intersecting in the same point, the subset of
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
, consisting of

such that these planes continue to intersect in the same point in

, is a smooth submanifold of codimension 1 in
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
around

.
(Furthermore, this submanifold is analytic for a natural analytic structure on
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
.) Locally in
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
there are finitely many such submanifolds. Therefore, for any two

belonging to the same component of
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
, we can connect them by a smooth path

transversal to all such submanifolds. Along the path some vertices of

can merge or split. In both cases one can see that these changes do not affect the isotopy type of

in

.

We need now some preliminaries on the deformation spaces of hyperbolic cone-3-manifolds similar to our treatment of convex cocompact hyperbolic metrics in Section

. Fix a connected component
\mathcal{P}_{0}
of
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
. Let

be a smooth open manifold diffeomorphic to

for

. Note that

is diffeomorphic to the interior of a compact manifold with boundary, obtained by deleting from

g_{1}
on

extends to a hyperbolic metric

The map

determines a map

Lemma 3.2

The map

Proof.

Let

, fix a natural embedding

, and let

be a point projecting to
\iota({\rm int}(M))

and on a basis in the tangent space of this point. Hence,

varies smoothly, where

is the respective holonomy representation of
\pi_{1}(L)

is a smooth map.

Now suppose that there is

such that

the closure of

in

. Thus,

induces zero variation at the first order on the holonomy representation

of

It follows that

is vertical with respect to the forgetful submersion

. Thus,

can be represented by a Killing vector field

on each immersed marked plane

in
N(\overline{g})
. Let
\widetilde{f}_{v}\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
be a lift of

to
\widetilde{N}(\overline{g})\cong\mathbb{H}^{3}
. The vector field

lifts to a vector field

, which is the restriction of a global Killing field of

to the plane

. As

,

induces zero first-order variation on all lengths and dihedral angles of

\widetilde{M}(g)
the restriction to

coincide. Indeed, in the dual picture in

we have a face dual to

and a tangent vector at every vertex such that these vectors preserve this face infinitesimally. This means that they are restrictions of a global Killing field of
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
to this face. In the non-dual picture this exactly means that the vector fields coincide on vertices, and thereby, on the edges too. Since all the dihedral angles are infinitesimally preserved, the obtained vector field on every component of

is the restriction of a global Killing field on

. On the other hand, it should be invariant with respect to the restriction of

to the subgroup preserving the given component. The only such option is that this Killing field is zero. Hence, all

\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(\Xi_{r})}=O(r^{\alpha+1}),~~~~~\|\nabla_{e_{i}}\xi\|_{L^{2}(\Xi_{r})}=O(r^{\alpha})~~~~~{\rm for}~i=1,2.

Now we have all ready to prove Lemma

Proof of Lemma

.

Since

-harmonic 1-form

\int_{L_{r}}(|d^{E}\omega|^{2}+|\delta^{E}\omega|^{2})=\int_{L_{r}}(|D\omega|^{2}+|D^{t}\omega|^{2}+\langle Q\omega,\omega\rangle)-\int_{K_{r}}(*T\omega\wedge\omega+T^{t}\omega\wedge*\omega),

We decompose

At every point

The second summand is naturally isomorphic to

(3.6)

see

For
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{u}

Substituting this into (

As we mentioned, both sub-bundles

\|\xi^{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Xi_{u,r})}=O(r^{\alpha+1}),~~~~~\|\nabla_{e_{i}}\xi^{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Xi_{u,r})}=O(r^{\alpha}),

A_{u,r}^{s}=A_{u,r}-A_{u,r}^{\omega}=2\int_{K_{u,r}}\langle(Ds^{1}+Ts^{2}+\omega^{1}_{u})(e_{1}),(Ds^{2}+Ts^{1})(e_{2})\rangle-

We expand this expression into further integrals of the scalar products of the individual summands and bound every integral separately by the Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality. We will compute the

Now it remains to bound

\omega_{u}=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{u}}\lambda_{\gamma}\omega_{\gamma}

for some

. Since the supports of

are disjoints, we get

A^{\omega}_{u,r}=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{u}}2\lambda_{\gamma}\int_{K_{u,r}}\omega^{1}_{\gamma}\wedge\omega^{2}_{\gamma}

(recall that we do not distinguish in notation the forms on

and their pullbacks on

). Define

For a given

the form
\omega_{\gamma}
on

is supported in

, which belongs to one of the pair of pants determined by

(since the collars are disjoint for different curves). Let us denote this pair of pants by

. Denote the respective surface
\Xi(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})

now as a

to

by
\omega_{\gamma}
. Define

with

4
Properness

The goal of this section is to prove

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\{g_{i}\}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
such that the sequence of the induced dual metrics
\{d_{i}\}
converges to a metric
d\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)
. Then, up to extracting a subsequence,
\{g_{i}\}
converges to
g\in\overline{\mathcal{P}}(M,V)
with
V(g)=V(d)
.

We will employ the
spherical model
of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Denote the
Euclidean scalar product
on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
by
\langle.,.\rangle_{E}
, i.e.,

\langle x,y\rangle_{E}:=x_{0}y_{0}+x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}+x_{3}y_{3}.

We denote by
\mathbb{S}^{3}\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
the standard unit sphere

\mathbb{S}^{3}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:\langle x,x\rangle_{E}=1\}

endowed with the metric induced from
\langle.,.\rangle_{E}
. The central projection from the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
maps injectively
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
to subsets of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
denoted by
\mathbb{H}_{s}^{3}
and
d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}
. Notably it sends geodesics to geodesics, and, thus, convex sets to convex sets. The boundary at infinity
\partial_{\infty}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
projects to two spherical caps denoted by
\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}
and
\partial_{\infty}^{-}d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}
.

Remark 4.2
.

If
C\subset\mathbb{H}_{s}^{3}
or
C\subset d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}
is (future-)convex, then the dual set
C^{*}
can be obtained from the usual polarity on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
composed with the reflection with respect to the hyperplane
x_{0}=0
. Indeed, this follows from the fact that the linear plane, orthogonal to a vector with respect to the Minkowski metric, differs from the linear plane, orthogonal to the same vector with respect to the Euclidean metric, by reflection with respect to the hyperplane
x_{0}=0
.

Let
S=S_{j}
be a connected component of
\partial M
and
W=V_{j}\subset V
be the subset of
V
marking the faces of
S
. (To simplify the notation, for the moment we will omit the subscript
j
.) We denote by
\nu=\nu_{j}:\pi_{1}(S)\rightarrow\pi_{1}(M)
the push-forward homomorphism induced by the inclusion of
S
to
M
, and denote by
D=D_{j}
the covering space of
S
corresponding to
\ker(\nu)
. The latter is homeomorphic to a boundary component of
\widetilde{M}
(possibly to infinitely many of them). It is either homeomorphic to the open 2-disk if
S
is incompressible, otherwise
D
is infinitely connected.

Denote by
d\in\mathcal{D}(S,W)
the restriction of a metric from
\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
. We continue to denote the lift of
d
to
D
by
d
. Note that all closed geodesics in
(D,d)
have lengths greater than
2\pi
. Let
\mathcal{T}
be a triangulation of
(S,W)
such that
d
is
\mathcal{T}
-triangulable and its lift to
D
is a simplicial complex. We continue to denote this lift by
\mathcal{T}
, but we denote the lift of
W
to
D
by
\widetilde{W}
.

Definition 4.3
.

An isometric embedding
\phi:(D,d)\rightarrow d\mathbb{S}^{3}
is called
\mathcal{T}
-regular
if

1) it is polyhedral with respect to
\mathcal{T}
,

2) it is future-convex, i.e., the image belongs to the boundary of a future-convex set.

We assume that
\phi
maps to the spherical model
d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}\subset\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Since
\phi
is an isometric embedding, all faces are necessarily space-like.
From future-convexity,
\phi(D)\subset{\rm conv}_{s}(\phi(D))
, where
{\rm conv}_{s}
is the closed convex hull in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, though a priori it is possible that
{\rm conv}_{s}(\phi(D))\cap d\mathbb{S}^{3}
is not future-convex.

But this is not the case for us, because the metric
d
is complete. Indeed, we claim that all limit points of
\phi(D)
that do not belong to
\phi(D)
are in
\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Suppose the contrary, that such a point
q
exists in
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Then
q\in\partial{\rm conv}_{s}(\phi(D))
. Pick
p\in\phi(D)
, there exists a rectifiable curve (with respect to the spherical metric)
\gamma:[0,a_{0}]\rightarrow\partial{\rm conv}_{s}(D)\cap d\mathbb{S}^{3}
connecting
p
and
q
. Let
[0,a]
be the maximal segment such that
\gamma|_{[0,a)}
belongs to
\phi(D)
. The curve
\gamma|_{[0,a)}
has finite spherical length and belongs to a space-like polyhedral surface, hence it also has a finite de Sitter length. Since
\phi(D)
is relatively open in
\partial{\rm conv}_{s}(\phi(D))
, the point
\gamma(a)
is not in
\phi(D)
. But
\phi^{-1}\circ\gamma|_{[0,a)}
is a curve of finite length in
(D,d)
, hence, due to completeness of
d
, it extends to
a
. Thereby,
\gamma(a)\in\phi(D)
, which is a contradiction. It follows that
{\rm conv}_{s}(\phi(D))\cap d\mathbb{S}^{3}_{s}
is a future-convex set and

\big(\partial{\rm conv}_{s}(\phi(D))\backslash\phi(D)\big)\subset\big(\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}\cup\mathbb{H}_{s}^{3}\big).

In the setting of Lemma
4.1
consider the sequence of
\widetilde{M}(g_{i})
embedded to
\mathbb{H}^{3}
equivariantly with respect to the holonomy maps
\rho_{i}
. There exists a connected component of
\partial\widetilde{M}(g_{i})
that is invariant with respect to
\rho_{i}|_{{\rm im}(\nu)}
. The dual surface to this component is an embedding
\phi_{i}:D\rightarrow d\mathbb{S}^{3}
. We first study what happens with this sequence of embeddings provided that the induced metrics converge (which are the dual metrics for the respective components of
\partial\widetilde{M}(g_{i})
). We now focus on the restrictions of
d_{i}
and
d
to
S
, so for the moment we perceive them as elements of
\mathcal{D}(S,W)
.

Due to Lemma
2.5
, there are only finitely many triangulations
\mathcal{T}
such that one of

is
\mathcal{T}
-triangulable. By passing to a subsequence, we may assume that there is a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
such that its lift to

is a face triangulation of all
\phi_{i}(D)
, particularly all

are
\mathcal{T}
-triangulable. Note that the lift of
\mathcal{T}
to
D
is a simplicial complex and all
\phi_{i}
are
\mathcal{T}
-regular.

Lemma 4.4
.

After possibly composing each
\phi_{i}
with an isometry
\eta_{i}
of

and passing to a subsequence,
\phi_{i}
converge to a
\mathcal{T}
-regular embedding
\phi:(D,d)\rightarrow d\mathbb{S}^{3}

Proof.

After passing to a subsequence, we can assume that for all
v\in\widetilde{W}
the sequence
\phi_{i}(v)
converges to a point in

\overline{d\mathbb{S}}_{s}^{3}:=d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}\cup\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}\cup\partial_{\infty}^{-}d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}.

We denote this point by
\phi(v)
, so
\phi
is a map from
\widetilde{W}
to
\overline{d\mathbb{S}}_{s}^{3}
.

Note that due to the future-convexity of
\phi_{i}
, the intersection
\phi(\widetilde{W})\cap\partial_{\infty}^{-}d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}
consists of at most one point. If this intersection is a point
p
, then we can compose each
\phi_{i}
with an isometry
\eta_{i}
of
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
that has
p
as its fixed repulsive point so that this intersection becomes empty. Abusing the notation, we continue to denote the corrected maps
\eta_{i}\circ\phi_{i}
just by
\phi_{i}
. By a similar procedure we can also assume that
\phi(\widetilde{W})\cap d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}
consists of at least one point, continuing to keep
\phi(\widetilde{W})\cap\partial_{\infty}^{-}d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}
empty. Let us call the points of
\widetilde{W}
with
\phi
-images in
\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}

degenerate
. One of the key steps in our proof is to establish that there are no degenerate points.

Observe that if
v,w\in\widetilde{W}
are degenerate and adjacent in
\mathcal{T}
, then
\phi(v)=\phi(w)\in\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}
, as otherwise for some large

the segment between
\phi_{i}(v)
and
\phi_{i}(w)
is not space-like.

We now extend
\phi
to a continuous
\mathcal{T}
-polyhedral map sending all
D
to
d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}\cup\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}
. For a triangle
T
without non-degenerate vertices, we just extend
\phi
to
T
isometrically. The same we do for every remaining edge with both non-degenerate vertices. For an edge or a triangle with all degenerate vertices, we just extend
\phi
by sending all the edge or all the triangle to the image of the vertices. For edges that have both degenerate and non-degenerate vertices, we must send them to light-like segments, and there is no canonical way to do this, so we do this arbitrarily. After this we arbitrarily extend the map to the triangles that have both degenerate and non-degenerate vertices, by sending their interiors to the relative interiors of the linear spans of the vertices.

This way
\phi
becomes a continuous
\mathcal{T}
-polyhedral map from
D
to
d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}\cup\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}
. We note that by continuity, we still have that the image of
\phi
belongs to the closure of the boundary of a future-convex set. We will refer to this as to the future-convexity of
\phi
.

For
z\in(D\backslash\widetilde{W})
belonging to the interior of a triangle of
\mathcal{T}
, we define the open star
{\rm ost}(z)
of
z
to be the interior of this triangle. If
z
belongs to the relative interior of an edge of
z
, then by
{\rm ost}(z)
we denote this relative interior together with the interiors of both triangles adjacent to this edge. When

, by
{\rm ost}(z)
we mean the relative interiors of all edges and triangles adjacent to
z
together with
z
itself. The open star
{\rm ost}(U)
of a set of points
U\subset D
is defined as the union of
{\rm ost}(z)
for all
z\in U
.

Define
X:=D\backslash{\rm ost}(\phi^{-1}(\partial_{\infty}^{+}d\mathbb{S}_{s}^{3}))
. By construction,
X
is non-empty. By continuity, the restriction of
\phi
to
X
is a path-isometry. We claim that the restriction of
\phi
to
X
is injective. Indeed, since it is a path-isometry, it is locally injective. Now suppose that for two points
x,y\in X
we have
\phi(x)=\phi(y)
. Due to local injectivity,
x\notin{\rm ost}(y)
and vice versa. Pick some small
r
such that for all sufficiently large
i
the
r
-neighborhood of
y
in
(D,d_{i})
is contained in
{\rm ost}(y)
. As
\phi(x)\in d\mathbb{S}^{3}_{s}
, we can pick its neighborhood

Proof of Lemma

Because

Proof.

.

Our goal now is to describe a map
\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{V}:\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{\sharp}(M,V)\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}^{\sharp}(\partial M,V)
lifting the map
\mathcal{I}_{V}
. Denote by
H_{0}(\partial M)
the space of self-homeomorphisms of
\partial M
isotopic to the identity, endowed with the compact-open topology. Recall that
H_{0}^{\sharp}(\partial M,V)
is its subgroup consisting of self-homeomorphisms isotopic to the identity by an isotopy fixing
V
. The inclusion
V\hookrightarrow\partial M
may be considered as an element of the configuration space
S_{\ast V}
. Fix its lift
\widehat{V}
to the universal cover
\widetilde{S_{\ast V}}
. Every
h\in H_{0}(\partial M)
determines a self-homeomorphism of
S_{\ast V}
, which, thanks to the homotopy lifting, determines a self-homeomorphism of
\widetilde{S_{\ast V}}
. By considering its evaluation at
\widehat{V}
, we define a map
H_{0}(S)\rightarrow\widetilde{S_{\ast V}}
, which factors through
H_{0}^{\sharp}(S,V)
to the evaluation map

{\rm ev}:H_{0}(\partial M)/H_{0}^{\sharp}(\partial M,V)\rightarrow\widetilde{S_{\ast V}}.

It was shown in
[
28
, Lemma 3.1]
that it is a homeomorphism. We fix such a map (which depends only on the choice of
\widehat{V}
).

Let
\overline{g}\in\mathcal{CH}(N)
and
\Sigma\subset N(\overline{g})
be a separating convex surface in the geometric end
E_{j}
corresponding to
S_{j}
(particularly,
\Sigma
is at a positive distance from
C(\overline{g})
). The set of the closest points to
C(\overline{g})
on the supporting planes to
\Sigma
form another surface
\Sigma^{\prime}\subset E_{j}
. By construction, no two points of
\Sigma^{\prime}
belong to the same gradient line of
\psi_{j}
. Thereby, trivialization (
5.1
) provides a homeomorphism
\chi:\Sigma^{\prime}\rightarrow S_{j}
. The dual metric of
\Sigma
may be considered as a metric on
\Sigma^{\prime}
. When some supporting planes to
\Sigma
are marked by a set
V_{j}
, the set
V_{j}
respectively mark points on
\Sigma^{\prime}
.

Pick now
g^{\sharp}\in\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{\sharp}(M,V)
, let
(g,f)\in\overline{\mathcal{P}}(M,V)
be its projection, and
\Sigma
be the
j
-th component of
\partial M(g)
. Construct the surface
\Sigma^{\prime}
and the homeomorphism
\chi:\Sigma^{\prime}\rightarrow S_{j}
as above. Consider the element of
\widetilde{S_{\ast V}}
associated to
g^{\sharp}
by trivialization (
5.3
). By applying the inverse of the chosen evaluation map
{\rm ev}
, we get a class in
H_{0}(S_{j})/H_{0}^{\sharp}(S_{j},V_{j})
. Let
h\in H_{0}(S_{j})
be a representative in this class. The map
h^{-1}\circ\chi
is again a homeomorphism from
\Sigma^{\prime}
to
S_{j}
. Let
f^{\prime}:V\rightarrow\Sigma^{\prime}
be the marking map, which sends
v\in V
to a point on
\Sigma^{\prime}
determined by
f
. By construction,
h^{-1}\circ\chi\circ f^{\prime}
sends every
v\in V
to itself. Thereby,
h^{-1}\circ\chi
associates the dual metric of
\Sigma
to an element of
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(S_{j},V_{j})
. By applying this procedure to each component of
\partial M(g)
, we obtain an element of
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
, which we denote by
\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{V}(g^{\sharp})
.

Remark 5.2
.

The group of deck transformations of
\widetilde{S_{\ast V}}\rightarrow S_{\ast V}
is naturally isomorphic to the pure braid group
B_{0}(\partial M,V)=H_{0}(\partial M,V)/H_{0}^{\sharp}(\partial M,V)
, see
[
28
, Remark 3.5]
. Thereby, it is also the group of deck transformations of the covering map
\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{\sharp}(M,V)\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{P}}(M,V)
. It is also the group of deck tranformations of the covering map
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)
. By construction, the map
\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{V}
is equivariant with respect to
B_{0}(\partial M,V)
.

Remark 5.3
.

Every geometric end
E_{j}
of a convex cocompact manifolds
N(\overline{g})
is associated to a de Sitter spacetime
E^{*}_{j}
, which appears as the quotient of the subset of
d\mathbb{S}^{3}
corresponding to the positive planes in a lift of
E_{j}
to the universal cover. The function
\psi_{j}
on
E_{j}
determines the so-called
cosmological time
on
E^{*}_{j}
. Instead of considering the surface
\Sigma^{\prime}
, we may consider the surface
\Sigma^{*}\subset E^{*}_{j}
dual to
\Sigma\subset E_{j}
. The induced metric on
\Sigma^{*}
is exactly the dual metric of
\Sigma
. We, however, chose not to pursue this point of view, as some things become subtler from this perspective.

5.2
Weak connectivity properties

In this section we establish several weak connectivity properties of the spaces
\mathcal{P}^{\sharp}(M,V)
,
\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
and
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\widehat{g}^{\sharp}_{0}
,
\widehat{g}^{\sharp}_{1}\in\mathcal{P}^{\sharp}(M,V)
. Then there exists
W\supseteq V
and a path
g^{\sharp}_{t}\subset\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{\sharp}(M,W)
for
t\in[0,1]
such that
g^{\sharp}_{0}
and
g^{\sharp}_{1}
are lifts of
\widehat{g}^{\sharp}_{0}
and
\widehat{g}^{\sharp}_{1}
respectively.

Proof.

The space
\mathcal{CH}^{\sharp}(N,V)
is connected, so we connect
\widehat{g}^{\sharp}_{0}
and
\widehat{g}^{\sharp}_{1}
by a path
\widehat{g}^{\sharp}_{t}
in
\mathcal{CH}^{\sharp}(N,V).
Denote by
(g_{t},f_{t})\in\mathcal{CH}(N,V)
and by
\overline{g}_{t}\in\mathcal{CH}(N)
the projections of
\widehat{g}^{\sharp}_{t}
to the respective spaces. Note that by definition of
\mathcal{CH}^{\sharp}(N,V)
, the marking maps
f_{t}
are injective. The intersection of the immersed negative half-spaces of the planes
f_{t}(v)
,
v\in V
, is a totally convex set
K_{t}\subset N(\overline{g}_{t})
. The boundary
\partial K_{t}
never touches
C(\overline{g}_{t})
by construction. For
t=0,1
the set
K_{t}
coincides with
M(g_{t})
. Moreover, near
t=0,1
the set
K_{t}
is homeomorphic to
M
and
\widehat{g}^{\sharp}_{t}\in\mathcal{P}^{\sharp}(M,V)
. We pick some
a,b\in(0,1)
,
a<b
, such that this holds for
t\in[0,a]
and
t\in[b,1]
. What can go wrong with
K_{t}
for
t\in[a,b]
? The issues are (1) a part of
K_{t}
escapes outside
N(\overline{g}_{t})
, so
K_{t}
becomes non-compact; (2) a plane from
f_{t}(V)
becomes not supporting to
K_{t}
.

Let us deal with the first issue. Let
E_{t}\subset N(\overline{g}_{t})
be the geometric end corresponding to the component
S_{j}
of
\partial M
and
\nu_{j}:\pi_{1}(S_{j})\rightarrow\pi_{1}(M)
be the push-forward map. Develop the path
(g_{t},f_{t})
to
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and let
\rho_{t}
be the holonomy map. Denote by
\Omega_{t}
the component of
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}\backslash\Lambda(\rho_{t})
fixed by
\rho_{t}|_{{\rm im}(\nu_{j})}
and by
\widetilde{E}_{t}
denote the respective geometric end of
\widetilde{N}(\overline{g}_{t})
. Note that the map
t\mapsto\Lambda(\rho_{t})
is continuous with respect to the Hausdorff topology on the closed subsets of
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
, see
[
2
, Theorem E]
.
We choose a continuous family of compact fundamental domains
X_{t}
,
t\in[a,b]
, for
\Omega_{t}
, i.e., such that the map
t\mapsto X_{t}
is continuous too. It is easy to deduce from the Hausdorff continuity that for every
t\in[a,b]
there exist an interval
I_{t}\subset[a,b]
and disjoint compact sets
Y_{t}
,
Z_{t}\subset\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
such that
I_{t}
is open in
[a,b]
, contains
t
and for every
t^{\prime}\in I_{t}
we have
X_{t^{\prime}}\subset Y_{t}
and
\Lambda(\rho_{t^{\prime}})\subset Z_{t}
. Pick a finite covering
I_{t_{1}},\ldots,I_{t_{r}}
of
[a,b]
by such intervals
I_{t}
. For every
i=1,\ldots,r
choose a covering of
Y_{t_{i}}
by finitely many open disks in
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
such that their closures are disjoint from
Z_{t_{i}}
. We now view all these disks as positive hyperbolic planes in
\widetilde{E}_{t^{\prime}}
,
t^{\prime}\in I_{t_{i}}
. Consider their projection to
E_{t^{\prime}}
. If necessary, we perturb them slightly so that no two planes have their closest points to
C(\overline{g}_{t^{\prime}})
on the same gradient line of the distance function to
C(\overline{g}_{t^{\prime}})
. For the moment, every such plane exists only over the interval
I_{t_{i}}
, but we extend it arbitrarily continuously over all
[0,1]
so that at
t=0,1
the new planes are supporting to
K_{t}
. We do this for every
S_{j}
, and we mark these planes, together with the old ones, by a set
W\supseteq V
, extending the initial marking. Denote by
\widehat{K}_{t}
the modified sets
K_{t}
. By construction, every
\widehat{K}_{t}
is compact and homeomorphic to
M
.

We now need to resolve the second issue. To this purpose, for every
t
we replace every plane
f_{t}(v)
that is not supporting to
\widehat{K}_{t}
with the unique supporting plane on the same gradient line of the distance function to
C(\overline{g}_{t})
. The obtained planes are moving continuously. This produces a path of metrics
g^{\sharp}_{t}\in\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{\sharp}(M,W)
connecting
g^{\sharp}_{0}
and
g^{\sharp}_{1}
that are lifts of metrics
\widehat{g}^{\sharp}_{0}
and
\widehat{g}^{\sharp}_{1}
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

For every
V_{0},V_{1}\subset\partial M
and
d_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V_{0})
,
d_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V_{1})
there exist
W\supseteq(V_{0}\cup V_{1})
and a path
\alpha:[0,1]\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(\partial M,W)
such that
\alpha(0)
and
\alpha(1)
are lifts of
d_{0}
and
d_{1}
respectively, and that for all
t\in(0,1)
we have
\alpha(t)\in\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,W)
.

Lemma 5.6
.

For every continuous map
\beta:S^{1}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
there exist
W\supseteq V
, a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of
(\partial M,W)
and a continuous map
\alpha:\overline{D}^{2}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
of the closed 2-disk
\overline{D}^{2}
with the boundary identified to
S^{1}
such that
\alpha|_{S^{1}}
is a lift of
\beta
, and that
\alpha(D^{2})\subset\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
, where
D^{2}
is the interior of
\overline{D}^{2}
.

We note that Lemma
5.6
does not hold for spaces
\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)
. The reason is that not every loop in
\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)
lifts to a loop in
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
, and hence to a loop in
\overline{\mathfrak{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)
, while every loop in
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
lifts to a loop in
\overline{\mathfrak{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)
. This is actually the only reason why we introduce the spaces
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
and
\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{\sharp}(M,V)
. Lemma
5.5
obviously holds for spaces
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
too.

Denote by
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{cl}(S,V)
the space of isotopy classes of concave large spherical cone-metrics
d
on
(S,V)
with
V(d)=V
. Lemma
5.5
was first proven in
[
58
, Section 9]
for the spaces
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{cl}(S,V)
when
S
is the 2-sphere. Then Lemmas
5.5
and
5.6
for
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{cl}(S,V)
and arbitrary closed
S
appeared in a manuscript
[
64
]
of Schlenker. Brief proof sketches were given there. Basically, Schlenker noticed that a proof of Lemma
5.5
can be given exactly in the same way as it was done for spheres in
[
58
]
. For Lemma
5.6
Schlenker remarks that a proof is an easy generalization of the proof of Lemma
5.5
. We claim that these results hold straightforwardly also for the spaces
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
. Indeed, we only need to modify the proofs to gain control of the smallest geodesic that is contractible not just in
\partial M
, but in
M
. For a metric
d
on
S
by
{\rm ml}(d)
we denote the infimum of lengths of closed geodesics in
(S,d)
that are contractible in
S
. The control on
{\rm ml}(d)
in
[
58
]
follows from two facts. The first is that if
\lambda>0
is a number and
d
is a Riemannian metric on
S
, then
{\rm ml}(\lambda d)=\lambda{\rm ml}(d)
, which clearly holds also for the function
{\rm ml}_{M}
. The second fact is that
{\rm ml}
is upper-semicontinuous in the Lipschitz topology. For
{\rm ml}_{M}
this was shown in Corollary
2.8
.

We now provide proof sketches of Lemmas
5.5
and
5.6
. All the details can be reconstructed with the help of
[
58
]
.

Proof.

We lift our initial classes of metrics (
d_{0}
and
d_{1}
for Lemma
5.5
or
\beta(S^{1})
for Lemma
5.6
) to
\mathfrak{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
(where
V=V_{0}
for
d_{0}
and
V=V_{1}
for
d_{1}
in Lemma
5.5
). For the loop
\beta
this is possible because
\mathfrak{D}(\partial M,V)\cong\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(\partial M,V)\times H^{\sharp}_{0}(\partial M,V)
, see Section
2.2
. We continuously smoothen these metrics in the class of Riemannian metrics with conical singularities and obtain in the end a pair or a loop of Riemannian metrics. As the set of Riemannian metrics on
S
is connected and simply connected, we can connect our pair of smooth metrics by a path or we can contract the loop. Now we multiply each metric of our path or our disk by a scalar (equal 1 near the boundary of the path or of the disk) to achieve that the sectional curvature is bounded from above by 1 and that the length of the shortest geodesic loop in
\partial M
that is contractible in
M
is greater than some constant greater than
2\pi
. This is easy to perform due to the compactness of the path or the disk. For the involved cone-metrics we also take care that the cone-angles stay greater than
2\pi
during our procedure.

We approximate the obtained metrics by spherical cone-metrics. To this purpose one constructs a very small triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of
\partial M
with a vertex set
W
, which can be geodesically realized in all involved metrics. Next, for each metric we replace each triangle by a spherical triangle with the same lengths. If the triangulation was sufficiently fine, then the distances in the obtained cone-metrics got distorted by at most
\varepsilon
comparing with the approximated metrics for an arbitrarily small globally defined
\varepsilon>0
. From comparison geometry one shows that the resulting cone-metrics are concave. Provided that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, it also follows from the upper semi-continuity of the function
{\rm ml}_{M}
that the length of the shortest geodesic in
\partial M
that is contractible in
M
remains greater than
2\pi
.
∎

It remains to make a few easy remarks on the topological boundary of
\mathcal{D}_{c}(\partial M,V)
in
\mathcal{D}(\partial M,V)
. Let
d^{\sharp}\in\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be a number. We denote by
(\lambda*_{\mathcal{T}}d^{\sharp})
a metric obtained by changing the length
l_{e}
of each edge
e\in E(\mathcal{T})
to
e^{\lambda}l_{e}
if the lengths remain staying
<\pi
under this operation (otherwise the operation is not defined). We will use some corollaries of the following easy lemma

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
T
be a spherical triangle with side lengths
a
,
b
and
c
less than
\pi
. Consider a spherical triangle
T^{\prime}
with the lengths
e^{\lambda}a
,
e^{\lambda}b
,
e^{\lambda}c
for
\lambda>0
such that the side lengths of the new triangle are
<\pi
. Then the angles of
T^{\prime}
are bigger than the respective angles of
T
.

The proof is identical to the proof of a similar lemma for hyperbolic triangles (
[
52
, Lemma 2.3.9]
) with all inequalities reversed.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
d^{\sharp}\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{c}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
and
\lambda>0
be sufficiently small. Then
(\lambda*_{\mathcal{T}}d^{\sharp})\in\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{c}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
.

This is straightforward since the only difference between
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{c}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
and
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{c}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
is that in the former the cone-angles of some points of
V
may be
2\pi
.

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
d\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)
. There exists an open neighborhood
U
of
d
in
\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)
such that
U\cap{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)
is connected.

Proof.

Since the forgetful map
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(\partial M,V)
is a covering map, it is enough to prove Lemma
5.9
for the space
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
.
If
d^{\sharp}\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
, then the claim is trivial. Suppose that
d^{\sharp}\notin{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
. Note that
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{c}(\partial M,V)
is locally determined by finitely many non-strict analytic inequalities in the manifold
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}(\partial M,V)
. Hence,
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{c}(\partial M,V)
is locally connected. Choose a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
and a connected open neighborhood

of

in
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{c}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
. Since

and

is upper-semicontinuous, we can assume that

. Pick any two metrics
d_{0},d_{1}\in U\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
and connect them by a path
d^{\sharp}_{t}
,
t\in[0,1]
, in
U
. Choose a function
\lambda:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\varepsilon]
for some small
\varepsilon>0
such that
\lambda(0)=\lambda(1)=0
and
\lambda(t)>0
for
t\in(0,1)
. Then, provided that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, the path
(\lambda(t)*_{\mathcal{T}}d^{\sharp}_{t})
connects

and
d^{\sharp}_{1}
in
U\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)
.
∎

5.3
End of the proof

Now we can collect together the proof of the main result.

Proof of Theorem
2.19
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
is non-empty. Consider the restriction of the map
\mathcal{I}_{V}
to
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
. From Lemma
2.18
, it is
C^{1}
. Due to Lemma
3.1
, the differential
d\mathcal{I}_{V}
is non-degenerate. By the Inverse Function Theorem,
d\mathcal{I}_{V}
is locally injective. Since the manifolds
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
and
\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
have the same dimensions, by Brouwer’s Invariance of the Domain Theorem, the restriction of
\mathcal{I}_{V}
is a local homeomorphism. Due to Lemma
4.1
, it is proper. Thus, it is a covering map. We highlight that we can not really show the connectedness of
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
and
\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
, so we take into account that it is a covering map between possibly disconnected spaces.

By Lemma
2.16
, there exists some
V_{0}
such that
\mathcal{P}(M,V_{0})
is non-empty. Let
d_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V_{0})
be in the image of
\mathcal{I}_{V_{0}}
. Set
V_{1}:=V
and pick an arbitrary
d_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V_{1})
. By Lemma
5.5
, there exists
W\supseteq(V_{0}\cup V_{1})
and a path
\alpha:[0,1]\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(\partial M,W)
such that
\alpha(0)
and
\alpha(1)
are lifts of
d_{0}
and
d_{1}
, and that the relative interior of the path belongs to
\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,W)
. We have
\alpha(0)
in the image of
\mathcal{I}_{W}
. We denote its preimage by
g_{0}
. We can modify the path so that for all small
t
the metrics
\alpha(t)
are in the image of
\mathcal{I}_{W}
. Indeed, consider any map
f_{W}:W\rightarrow F(g_{0})
, where
F(g_{0})
is the set of faces of
g_{0}
, extending the marking map
f_{0}:V_{0}\rightarrow F(g_{0})
of
g_{0}
. We can slightly perturb the respective faces by splitting them in parts to obtain a metric
g^{\prime}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(M,W)
. By Corollary
5.9
, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
\alpha(0)
such that
U\cap\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,W)
is connected. Hence, we can choose
g^{\prime}_{0}
so that
\mathcal{I}_{W}(g^{\prime}_{0})
can be connected with the metric
\alpha(t)
for some small
t
by a path in
\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,W)
. Taking the concatenation of this path with the rest of the initial path, we obtain a path satisfying our demand. Then the infinitesimal rigidity and the properness of
\mathcal{I}_{W}
imply that all
\alpha(t)
are in the image of
\mathcal{I}_{W}
. Hence, also
d_{1}
is in the image of
\mathcal{I}_{V_{1}}=\mathcal{I}_{V}
, which means that
\mathcal{P}(M,V)
is non-empty and
\mathcal{I}_{V}
is surjective.

It remains to show that each metric in
\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)
has only one preimage. Consider a commutative diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{P}^{\sharp}(M,V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{P}(M,V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{M}(\partial M,V)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{V}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau^{P}_{V}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau^{D}_{V}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{I}_{V}}

Here we denote by
\tau^{P}_{V}
and
\tau^{D}_{V}
the respective projection maps.
We already know that all the arrows except
\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{V}
are covering maps. It follows that also
\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{V}
is a covering map. We will show that it is a homeomorphism. The groups of the deck transformations of
\tau^{P}_{V}
and
\tau^{D}_{V}
are both naturally isomorphic to
B_{0}(\partial M,V)
and
\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{V}
is equivariant with respect to it, see Remark
5.2
. Thereby, this will imply that
\mathcal{I}_{V}
is also a homeomorphism.

Suppose that for
g^{\sharp}_{0},g^{\sharp}_{1}\in\mathcal{P}^{\sharp}(M,V)
we have
\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{V}(g^{\sharp}_{0})=\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{V}(g^{\sharp}_{1})
. Due to Lemma
5.4
, there exists
W^{\prime}\supseteq V
and a path
\eta:[0,1]\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{\sharp}(M,W^{\prime})
such that
\eta(0)
and
\eta(1)
are lifts of
g^{\sharp}_{0}
and
g^{\sharp}_{1}
respectively. Consider
S^{1}
as
[0,1]
with identified endpoints. Then
\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{W^{\prime}}\circ\eta
is a loop, which we denote by
\beta:S^{1}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,W^{\prime})
. Due to Lemma
5.6
, there exist
W\supseteq W^{\prime}
, a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of
(\partial M,W)
and a map
\alpha:\overline{D}^{2}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
such that
\alpha|_{S^{1}}
is a lift of
\beta
, and that
\alpha(D^{2})\subset\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
.

There exists a lift
\zeta:[0,1]\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{\sharp}(M,W)
of the map
\eta
such that
\alpha|_{S^{1}}=\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{W}\circ\zeta
. We claim that there exists a homotopy
\zeta_{s}
of
\zeta_{0}=\zeta
with
s\in[0,\varepsilon]
for some small
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
s>0
we have
\zeta_{s}\subset\mathcal{P}^{\sharp}(M,W)
. Indeed, denote by
\overline{g}_{t}\in\mathcal{CH}(N)
the projection of
\zeta(t)
. For
s>0
and every
t\in[0,1]
we move every immersed plane in
N(\overline{g}_{t})
marked by
v\in V
at distance
s
from its previous position along the segment connecting the closest point on this plane to the closest point on
C(\overline{g}_{t})
towards
C(\overline{g}_{t})
. One can see that for sufficiently small
s
the intersection of the immersed negative half-spaces of these planes defines a subset of
N(\overline{g}_{t})
homeomorphic to
M
, and that every marked plane intersects the boundary of this subset by a set with non-empty relative interior. This determines an element
\zeta_{s}(t)\in\mathcal{P}^{\sharp}(M,W)
.

Define
\theta_{s}(t):=\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{W}\circ\zeta_{s}(t)
. Since
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
is open, we may assume that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small so that
\theta_{s}(t)\in\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
for all
t\in[0,1]
and
s\in(0,\varepsilon]
. Consider the arcs

\theta^{\prime}_{s}:=\{\theta_{r}(0),r\in[0,s]\}\cup\{\theta_{r}(1),r\in[0,s]\}.

For each
s
the union
\theta_{s}\cup\theta^{\prime}_{s}
is a loop. We parametrize these loops as
\iota_{s}:S^{1}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
so that the arc
\theta^{\prime}_{s}
corresponds to the restriction of
\iota_{s}
to the interval
[1/2-s,1/2+s]
and
\iota_{s}(1/2)=\theta_{0}(0)=\theta_{0}(1)
.

Recall that for a number
\lambda
and a
\mathcal{T}
-triangulable spherical cone-metric
d
on
(\partial M,W)
we denote by
(\lambda*_{\mathcal{T}}d)
the metric obtained from
d
by multiplication of all the side-lengths of
\mathcal{T}
by
e^{\lambda}
. Consider a 1-parameter family of functions
\lambda_{s}:S^{1}\rightarrow[0,\varepsilon]
, where
s\in[0,\varepsilon]
, such that
\lambda_{0}
is identically zero, and for all
s>0
we have
\lambda_{s}(t)>0
for
t\in(1/2-s,1/2+s)
, but
\lambda_{s}
is zero outside
(1/2-s,1/2+s)
. Define loops
\delta_{s}:=(\lambda_{s}*_{\mathcal{T}}\iota_{s})
. Provided that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, for
s>0
from Corollary
5.8
we get that
\delta_{s}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
. We note that the loop
\delta_{0}
is a reparametrization of
\alpha|_{S^{1}}
.

Because
\delta_{0}
is contractible in
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
, so are the loops
\delta_{s}
. We note that this means that for
s>0
the loops
\delta_{s}
are contractible in
{\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
. Indeed, let
\alpha_{s}:\overline{D}^{2}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
be a homotopy contracting
\delta_{s}
in
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T})
. Choose a function
\lambda_{s}:\overline{D}^{2}\rightarrow[0,\varepsilon]
such that
\lambda_{s}|_{S^{1}}=0
and
\lambda_{s}|_{D^{2}}>0
. Consider now a homotopy
\alpha^{\prime}_{s}:=(\lambda_{s}*_{\mathcal{T}}\alpha_{s})
. By Corollary
5.8
, provided that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, we get

\alpha^{\prime}_{s}(D^{2})\subset{\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}}_{M}(\partial M,\mathcal{T}).

Because the restriction of
\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{W}
to
\mathcal{P}^{\sharp}(M,W)
is a covering map and the loops
\delta_{s}
for
s>0
are contractible in
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}_{M}(\partial M,W)
, they lift to loops in
\mathcal{P}^{\sharp}(M,W)
. This means that for
s>0
there are paths
\kappa_{s}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{P}^{\sharp}(M,W)
with
\kappa_{s}(0)=\zeta_{s}(0)
,
\kappa_{s}(1)=\zeta_{s}(1)
such that

\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{W}\circ\kappa_{s}(t)=\delta_{s}(1/2-s+2ts).

In particular, the arcs
\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{W}\circ\kappa_{s}
converge to
\delta_{0}(1/2)
as
s
tends to zero.

We now project the situation to the spaces
\overline{\mathcal{P}}(M,W)
and
\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{M}(M,W)
. Define
\xi_{s}:=\tau^{D}_{W}\circ\delta_{s}
and
\omega_{s}:=\tau^{P}_{W}\circ\kappa_{s}
(here we consider
\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{\sharp}(M,W)
and
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{M}(M,W)
as the domains of
\tau^{P}_{W}
and
\tau^{D}_{W}
). Consider a compact neighborhood
U
of
\xi_{0}(1/2)
such that all arcs obtained from the restrictions of
\xi_{s}
to
[1/2-s,1/2+s]
belong to
U
. By Lemma
4.1
,
\mathcal{I}_{W}
is proper, thus, the set
X:=(\mathcal{I}_{W})^{-1}(U)
is compact. (We note that at this point we do not know whether
\mathcal{I}^{\sharp}_{W}
is proper, this is the reason why we had to use
\mathcal{I}_{W}
.) Hence, there exists a Hausdorff limit
\omega\subset X
of the arcs
\omega_{s}
(here we perceive
\omega_{s}
and
\omega
not as maps, but as point-sets). By continuity,
\mathcal{I}_{W}(\omega)=\xi_{0}(1/2)
. Because
\mathcal{I}_{V}
is a local homeomorphism, the points
\tau^{P}_{W}\circ\zeta(0)
,
\tau^{P}_{W}\circ\zeta(1)
are isolated in
(\mathcal{I}_{W})^{-1}(\xi_{0}(1/2))
. Hence,
\omega
contains isolated points. This can not happen as
\omega
is the Hausdorff limit of the connected arcs
\omega_{s}
and
\overline{\mathcal{P}}(M,W)
is a Hausdorff space. This contradiction finishes the proof.
