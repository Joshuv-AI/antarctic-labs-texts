Macaulay’s theorem for vector-spread algebras
Marilena Crupi, Antonino Ficarra, Ernesto Lax

Abstract
. Let S = K ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x n ] S=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}] be the standard graded polynomial ring, with K K a field, and let 𝐭 = ( t 1 , … , t d − 1 ) ∈ ℤ ≥ 0 d − 1 {\bf t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d-1})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d-1} , d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , be a ( d − 1 ) (d-1) -tuple whose entries are non negative integers. To a t -spread ideal I I in S S , we associate a unique f 𝐭 f_{\bf t} -vector and we prove that if I I is t -spread strongly stable, then there exists a unique t -spread lex ideal which shares the same f 𝐭 f_{\bf t} -vector of I I via the combinatorics of the t -spread shadows of special sets of monomials of S S . 1.
Introduction

One of the main well–studied and important numerical invariant of a graded ideal in a standard graded polynomial ring is its Hilbert function which gives the sizes of the graded components of the ideal. There is an extensive literature on this topic (see, for instance,
[
10
]
and the references therein). Usually, Hilbert functions are described using the well–known Macaulay’s expansion with binomials. This fact often implies the use of combinatorial tools and furthermore the arguments consist of very clever computations with binomials. The crucial idea of Macaulay is that there exist special monomial ideals, the so called
lex ideals
, that attain all possible Hilbert functions. The pivotal property is that a lex ideal grows as slowly as possible. The “squarefree” analogue of Macaulay’s theorem is known as the Kruskal–Katona theorem. Indeed, if Macaulay’s theorem describes the possible Hilbert functions of the graded ideals in polynomial rings, the possible
f
-vectors of a simplicial complex are characterized in the theorem of Kruskal–Katona
[
10
,
13
,
14
]
. In fact, the Hilbert function of the Stanley–Reisner ring of a simplicial complex
\Delta
is determined by the
f
-vector of
\Delta
, and vice versa. Kruskal–Katona’s theorem is a fundamental result in topological combinatorics and discrete geometry which quickly have aroused much interest in face enumeration questions for various classes of simplicial complexes, polytopes, and manifolds. Furthermore, it may be also interpreted as a theorem on Hilbert functions of quotients of exterior algebras
[
3
]
.
The lex ideals as well as squarefree lex ideals play a key role in the study of the minimal free resolutions of monomial ideals. Indeed, if one considers the stable and squarefree stable ideals and the formulas for computing their graded Betti numbers
[
10
]
one can deduce the Bigatti-Hulett theorem
[
4
,
11
]
which says that lex ideals have the largest graded Betti numbers among all graded ideals with the same Hilbert function (see also
[
12
,
15
]
).

Let

be the standard graded polynomial ring, with
K
a field, and let

,

, be a
(d-1)
-tuple whose entries are non negative integers. Recently in
[
8
]
, the class of

-spread strongly stable ideals

t
-spread strongly stable ideals in
[
7
]
(see also,
[
1
,
6
]
and the references therein),

(

) of degree
\ell\leq d
of

is called a

{\bf t}
or simply a
{\bf t}
-spread monomial
if

, for

and a
{\bf t}
-spread monomial ideal

The aim of this article is to generalize Macaulay’s theorem for the class of

, we put our attention on the classification of the possible

2.
Preliminaries and notations

Let
S=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be the standard graded polynomial ring, with
K
a field, and let
{\bf t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d-1})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}
,
d\geq 2
, be a
(d-1)
-tuple whose entries are non negative integers. A monomial
u=x_{j_{1}}x_{j_{2}}\cdots x_{j_{\ell}}
(
1\leq j_{1}\leq j_{2}\leq\cdots\leq j_{\ell}\leq n
) of degree
\ell\leq d
of
S
is called a
vector-spread monomial of type
{\bf t}
or simply a
{\bf t}
-spread monomial
if
j_{i+1}-j_{i}\geq t_{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,\ell-1
.

If
I
is a graded ideal of
S
we denote by
I_{j}
the
j
-graded component of
I
and by
\operatorname{indeg}(I)
the initial degree of
I
,
i.e.
, the smallest
j
such that
I_{j}\neq 0
. Moreover, for a monomial ideal
I\subset S
, we denote by
G(I)
the unique minimal set of monomial generators. Furthermore, if
j\geq 0
is an integer, we set
G(I)_{j}=\{u\in G(I):\deg(u)=j\}
.

A
{\bf t}
-spread monomial ideal
is a monomial ideal of
S
generated by
{\bf t}
-spread monomials.
For instance,
I=(x_{1}x_{4}^{2}x_{5},x_{1}x_{4}^{2}x_{6},x_{1}x_{5}x_{7})
is a
(3,0,1)
-spread monomial ideal of the polynomial ring
S=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{7}]
, but it is not
(3,0,2)
-spread as
x_{1}x_{4}^{2}x_{5}\in G(I)
is not a
(3,0,2)
-spread monomial.

Note that any monomial (ideal) is
{\bf 0}
-spread, where
{\bf 0}=(0,0,\dots,0)
. If
t_{i}\geq 1
, for all
i
, a
{\bf t}
-spread monomial (ideal) is a
squarefree
monomial (ideal).

We denote by
M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
the set of all
{\bf t}
-spread monomials of degree
\ell
in
S
. If
\ell\leq d
, by
[
8
, Corollary 2.4]
,

\lvert M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}\rvert=\binom{n+(\ell-1)-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}t_{j}}{\ell}.

(2.1)

Definition 2.1
.

For a set
L
of monomials of
S
, one defines the vector-spread shadow or simply the
{\bf t}
-spread shadow of
L
to be the set

\displaystyle\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)\

\displaystyle=\ \big\{wx_{j}:w\in L,wx_{j}\ \textup{is}\ {\bf t}\textup{-spread},\,j=1,\dots,n\big\}.

Note that
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}})=M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}
for all
\ell\geq 0
and that
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)=\emptyset
whenever all monomials in
L
have degrees
\geq d
. Moreover, one can quickly observe that if
L
is a set of monomials of
S
, then the definition of
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf 0}(L)
coincides with the classical notion of shadow of
L

[
10
, Chapter 6]
.

Definition 2.2
.

If
I
is a monomial ideal of
S
, we denote by
[I_{j}]_{\bf t}
the set of all
{\bf t}
-spread monomials in
I_{j}
. Furthermore, we set

f_{{\bf t},\ell-1}(I)=|M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}|-|[I_{\ell}]_{\bf t}|,\qquad 0\leq\ell\leq d.

and define the vector

f_{\bf t}(I)=(f_{{\bf t},-1}(I),f_{{\bf t},0}(I),\ldots,f_{{\bf t},d-1}(I)).

Such a vector is called the
f_{\bf t}
-vector of
I
. Note that
f_{{\bf t},-1}(I)=1
.

Remark 2.3
.

For
{\bf t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d-1})
with
t_{i}\geq 1
, for all
i
, then
I
is the Stanley–Reisner ideal
I_{\Delta}
of a uniquely determined simplicial complex
\Delta
on vertex set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
f_{\bf 1}(I)
as
f
-vector, where
{\bf 1}=(1,\ldots,1)
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
L\subseteq M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
.
L
is called a
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable set if for all
u\in L
,
j<i
such that
x_{i}
divides
u
and
x_{j}(u/x_{i})
is
{\bf t}
-spread, then
x_{j}(u/x_{i})\in L
.
L
is called a
{\bf t}
-spread lex set, if for all
u\in L
,

such that
v\geq_{\operatorname{lex}}u
, then
v\in L
.

Here
\geq_{\operatorname{lex}}
stands for the lex order induced by
x_{1}>\dots>x_{n}

[
10
]
.

For our convenience, throughout the article, we assume the empty set to be both a
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable set and a lex set.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
I
be a
{\bf t}
-spread ideal.
I
is said to be a
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable ideal if
[I_{\ell}]_{\bf t}
is a
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable set, for all
\ell
.

I
is said to be a
{\bf t}
-spread lex ideal if
[I_{\ell}]_{\bf t}
is a
{\bf t}
-spread lex set, for all
\ell
.

One can observe that any
t
-spread lex set (ideal) is a
t
-spread strongly stable set (ideal). Moreover, for
{\bf t}={\bf 0}
(
{\bf t}={\bf 1}=(1,\ldots,1)
) one obtains the classical notions of (squarefree) strongly stable ideal and (squarefree) lex ideal
[
10
]
.

3.
Combinatorics on vector-spread shadows

In this section, if
{\bf t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d-1})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}
,
d\geq 2
, we deal with the combinatorics of the
t
-spread shadows of
t
-spread strongly stable sets and
t
-spread lex sets. As a consequence, given a
t
-spread strongly stable ideal
I
of the polynomial ring
S
, we prove the existence of a unique
t
-spread lex ideal of
S
with the same
f_{\bf t}
-vector of
I
.

For
i,j
integers, we set
[i,j]=\{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}:i\leq k\leq j\}
. Note that
[i,j]\neq\emptyset
if and only if
i\leq j
.

Let
u
be a monomial. We set
\max(u)=\max\{i:x_{i}\ \textup{divides}\ u\}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
L\subseteq M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
be a
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable set. Then

\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)=\big\{wx_{j}:w\in L,\ j\geq\max(w),\ wx_{j}\ \textup{is}\ {\bf t}\textup{-spread}\big\}.

Proof.

Let
u\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)
. Then,
u=wx_{j}
for some
w\in L
. If
\max(w)\leq j
there is nothing to prove. Suppose
j<\max(w)
. We can write
u=x_{\max(u)}w^{\prime}
, with
w^{\prime}=x_{j}(u/x_{\max(u)})
and
\max(w^{\prime})\leq\max(u)
. The proof is complete if we show that
w^{\prime}\in L
. Let
u=x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{\ell+1}}
with
j_{1}\leq\dots\leq j_{\ell+1}
. Then,
j=j_{p}
for some
p<\ell+1
and
w^{\prime}=x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{\ell}}=x_{j_{p}}(u/x_{\max(u)})
is a
t
-spread monomial because
w
is
t
-spread. Moreover,
w^{\prime}\in L
since
j_{p}<\max(u)=j_{\ell+1}
and
L
is a
t
-spread strongly stable set.

∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
L\subseteq M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
be a
{\bf t}
-spread set.

(a)

If
L
is a
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable set, then
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)\subseteq M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}
is a
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable set.

(b)

If
L
is a
{\bf t}
-spread lex set, then
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)\subseteq M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}
is a
{\bf t}
-spread lex set.

Proof.

Let
u=wx_{j}\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)
. For the proofs of both (a) and (b), by Lemma
3.1
, we can assume that
\max(w)\leq j
.

(a) Let
i<k
such that
x_{k}
divides
u
and
u^{\prime}=(u/x_{k})x_{i}
is
t
-spread. We prove that
u^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)
. If
k=j
, then
u^{\prime}=wx_{i}\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)
by definition. Suppose
k\neq j
. Since
j=\max(u)
, then
i<k<j
and consequently
x_{k}
divides
w
. Therefore,
u^{\prime}=x_{i}(w/x_{k})x_{j}\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)
because
x_{i}(w/x_{k})\in L
as
x_{i}(w/x_{k})
is a
t
-spread monomial,
i<k
and
L
is a
t
-spread strongly stable set.

(b) Let
v\in M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}
with
v>_{\operatorname{lex}}u
. We prove that
v\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)
. By definition of the lex order, it follows that
v/x_{\max(v)}\geq_{\operatorname{lex}}u/x_{\max(u)}
. The hypothesis on
L
guarantees that
v/x_{\max(v)}\in L
. Hence,
v=(v/x_{\max(v)})x_{\max(v)}\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)
.

∎

Let
L\subseteq M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
be a set of monomials, where
\ell\leq d
. For every
i\in[1,n]
we denote by
m_{i}(L)
the number of monomials
u\in L
such that
\max(u)=i
and then we set
m_{\leq j}(L)=\sum_{i=1}^{j}m_{i}(L)
. Note that
m_{i}(L)=0
if
i\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}t_{j}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
L\subseteq M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
be a
\bf t
-spread strongly stable set with
\ell<d
. Then

(a)

m_{i}(\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L))=m_{\leq i-t_{\ell}}(L)
for all
i
;

(b)

\left\lvert\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)\right\rvert=\sum_{k=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}t_{j}}^{n-t_{\ell}}m_{\leq k}(L)
.

Proof.

(a) If
i\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}t_{j}
the proof is trivial. Let
i\geq 1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}t_{j}
. Consider the map

\varphi:\left\{u\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)\,:\,\max(u)=i\right\}\longrightarrow\left\{w\in L\,:\,\max(w)\leq i-t_{\ell}\right\},

defined as follows. Let
u\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)
with
\max(u)=i
. By Lemma
3.1
,
u=wx_{i}
where
w\in L
is the unique monomial such that
\max(u)=i
. Thus, we set
\varphi(u)=w
. The map
\varphi
is well defined by the uniqueness of
w
. To prove (a), it is enough to show that
\varphi
is a bijection.
\varphi
is clearly injective. To prove that
\varphi
is surjective, let
w\in L
with
\max(w)\leq i-t_{\ell}
. Then,
u=wx_{i}
is
{\bf t}
-spread because
\max(w)\leq i-t_{\ell}
. Since
\max(u)=i
, then
u
belongs to the domain of
\varphi
and
\varphi(u)=w
, as desired.

(b) Since

\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)=\bigcup_{i=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}t_{j}}^{n}\big\{u\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L):\max(u)=i\big\},

where the union is disjoint, by (a),
|\{u\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L):\max(u)=i\}|=m_{i}(\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L))=m_{\leq i-t_{\ell}}(L)
, and so

\displaystyle\left\lvert\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}t_{j}}^{n}m_{i}(\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}t_{j}}^{n}m_{\leq i-t_{\ell}}(L)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}t_{j}}^{n-t_{\ell}}m_{\leq k}(L).

∎

The following result is a vector-spread analogue of a well known theorem due to Bayer, see
[
10
, Theorem 6.3.3]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
L\subset M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
be a
t
-spread lex set and let
N\subset M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
be a
t
-spread strongly stable set. Suppose
\left\lvert L\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert N\right\rvert
. Then,
m_{\leq i}(L)\leq m_{\leq i}(N)
.

Proof.

We first observe that
N=N_{0}\cup N_{1}x_{n}
, where
N_{0}
and
N_{1}
are the unique
t
-spread strongly stable sets such that

\displaystyle N_{0}\

\displaystyle=\ \{u\in N:\max(u)<n\},

\displaystyle N_{1}\

\displaystyle=\ \{u/x_{n}:u\in N,\max(u)=n\}.

Similarly, we can write
L=L_{0}\cup L_{1}x_{n}
, where
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
are
{\bf t}
-spread lex sets defined as above.

We proceed by induction on
n\geq 1
, with the base case being trivial. Let
n>1
. Firstly, observe that
m_{\leq n}(L)=\left\lvert L\right\rvert
and
\left\lvert N\right\rvert=m_{\leq n}(N)
. Hence, the assertion holds for
i=n
. Note that
m_{\leq n-1}(L)=|L_{0}|
and
m_{\leq n-1}(N)=|N_{0}|
. Thus, to say that
m_{\leq n-1}(L)\leq m_{\leq n-1}(N)
is equivalent to prove that

|L_{0}|\leq|N_{0}|.

(3.1)

Assume for a moment that inequality (
3.1
) holds. Then, applying our inductive hypothesis to the sets
L_{0},N_{0}\subset M_{n-1,\ell,{\bf t}}
we obtain

m_{\leq i}(L)=m_{\leq i}(L_{0})\leq m_{\leq i}(N_{0})=m_{\leq i}(N)\ \ \ \text{for}\ \ \ i=1,\dots,n-1,

as desired. Thus, it remains to prove the inequality (
3.1
).

Let
N_{0}^{*}\subset M_{n-1,\ell,{\bf t}}
be a
t
-spread lex set with
\left\lvert N_{0}^{*}\right\rvert=\left\lvert N_{0}\right\rvert
and
N_{1}^{*}\subset M_{n-t_{\ell-1},\ell-1,{\bf t}}
be a
t
-spread lex set with
\left\lvert N_{1}^{*}\right\rvert=\left\lvert N_{1}\right\rvert
. Let
N^{*}=N_{0}^{*}\cup N_{1}^{*}x_{n}
. We claim that
N^{*}
is a
t
-spread strongly stable set. Let
u\in N^{*}
. We shall prove that for every
j<i
such that
x_{i}
divides
u
and
x_{j}(u/x_{i})
is
{\bf t}
-spread, then
x_{j}(u/x_{i})\in N^{*}
. If
u\in N_{0}^{*}
there is nothing to prove since
N_{0}^{*}
is a
{\bf t}
-spread lex set. Suppose
u\in N_{1}^{*}x_{n}
, then we can write
u=wx_{n}
, where
w\in N_{1}^{*}
. If
i<n
, then
w^{\prime}=x_{j}(w/x_{i})
belongs to
N_{1}^{*}
and
x_{j}(u/x_{i})=w^{\prime}x_{n}\in N_{1}^{*}x_{n}
. If
i=n
, then
x_{j}(u/x_{i})=wx_{j}
. Now, if
x_{n}
divides
wx_{j}
, then again
x_{j}w\in N_{1}^{*}x_{n}
. Otherwise, if
x_{n}
does not divide
wx_{j}
, then
wx_{j}\in N^{*}
if and only if
wx_{j}\in N_{0}^{*}
. Thus, we must show that
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(N_{1}^{*})\subset N_{0}^{*}
. For this aim, it is sufficient to prove that
|\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(N_{1}^{*})|\leq|N_{0}^{*}|
, as both sets are
{\bf t}
-spread lex sets (Proposition
3.2
(b)). By Lemma
3.3
and the induction hypothesis we obtain

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(N_{1}^{*})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-2}t_{j}}^{n-t_{\ell-1}}m_{\leq i}(N_{1}^{*})\leq\sum_{i=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-2}t_{j}}^{n-t_{\ell-1}}m_{\leq i}(N_{1})

\displaystyle=|\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(N_{1})|\leq|N_{0}|=|N_{0}^{*}|.

Finally,
N^{*}
is a
t
-spread strongly stable set.

Since
|N|=|N^{*}|
, we may replace
N
by
N^{*}
and assume that
N_{0}
is a
t
-spread lex set. We suppose
n>1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}t_{j}
, otherwise
M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}=\{x_{1}x_{1+t_{1}}\cdots x_{1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}t_{j}}\}
and the assertion is trivial.

Let
m=x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{\ell}}
be a
t
-spread monomial and
\alpha:M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}\rightarrow M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
be the map defined as follows:

(a)

if
j_{\ell}\neq n
, then
\alpha(m)=m
;

(b)

if
j_{\ell}=n
and
m\neq\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}=x_{n-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}t_{j}}x_{n-\sum_{j=2}^{\ell-1}t_{j}}\cdots x_{n-t_{\ell-1}}x_{n}
, then there exists
r\in[2,\ell]
such that
j_{r}>j_{r-1}+t_{r-1}
. Hence, if
r
is the largest integer with this property, we define

\alpha(m)=x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{r-1}}x_{j_{r}-1}\cdots x_{j_{\ell-1}-1}x_{n-1};

(c)

if
j_{\ell}=n
and
m=\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}=x_{n-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}t_{j}}x_{n-\sum_{j=2}^{\ell-1}t_{j}}\cdots x_{n-t_{\ell-1}}x_{n}
, then

\alpha(m)=x_{n-1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}t_{j}}x_{n-1-\sum_{j=2}^{\ell-1}t_{j}}\cdots x_{n-1-t_{\ell-1}}x_{n-1}.

Such map
\alpha
is well defined and is easily seen to be a lexicographic order preserving map,
i.e.
, if
m_{1},m_{2}\in M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
and
m_{1}<_{\operatorname{lex}}m_{2}
, then
\alpha(m_{1})<_{\operatorname{lex}}\alpha(m_{2})
, too.

To prove (
3.1
), since both
L_{0}
and
N_{0}
are
t
-spread lex sets, it is enough to show that
\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}L_{0}\geq_{\operatorname{lex}}\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}N_{0}
.

Let
u=\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}L=x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{\ell}}
and
v=\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}N=x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{\ell}}
. We claim that
\alpha(u)=\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}L_{0}
and
\alpha(v)=\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}N_{0}
. Indeed,

(a)

if
v\in N_{0}
, then
\alpha(v)=v\in N_{0}

(b)

if
v\in N_{1}x_{n}
and
\alpha(v)=x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{r-1}}x_{j_{r}-1}\cdots x_{j_{\ell-1}-1}x_{n-1}
, where
r\in[2,\ell]
is the largest integer such that
j_{r}>j_{r-1}+t_{r-1}
, then

(i)

if
r=\ell
, then
\alpha(v)=x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{\ell-1}}x_{n-1}=(v/x_{n})x_{n-1}\in N_{0}
, because
N
is a
t
-spread strongly stable set.

(ii)

if
r<\ell
, since
N
is a
t
-spread strongly stable set, we have

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{j_{k}-1}(v/x_{j_{k}})\in N,

\displaystyle v_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{j_{k+1}-1}(v_{1}/x_{j_{k+1}})\in N,

\displaystyle\phantom{..}\vdots

\displaystyle v_{\ell-r}

\displaystyle=x_{j_{\ell-1}-1}(v_{\ell-k-1}/x_{j_{\ell-1}})\in N,

then
\alpha(v)=x_{n-1}(v_{\ell-k}/x_{n})\in N_{0}
.

(c)

if
v\in N_{1}x_{n}
and
\alpha(v)=x_{j_{1}-1}\cdots x_{j_{\ell-1}-1}x_{n-1}
, we have

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{j_{1}-1}(v/x_{j_{1}})\in N,

\displaystyle v_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{j_{2}-1}(v_{1}/x_{j_{2}})\in N,

\displaystyle\phantom{..}\vdots

\displaystyle v_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle=x_{j_{\ell}-1}(v_{\ell-2}/x_{j_{\ell}-1})\in N,

and
\alpha(v)=x_{n-1}(v_{\ell-1}/x_{n})\in N_{0}
, because
N
is a
t
-spread strongly stable set.

Hence, in all possible cases
\alpha(v)\in N_{0}
. Thus, we have
\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}N_{0}\leq_{\operatorname{lex}}\alpha(v)
, and
\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}N_{0}\geq_{\operatorname{lex}}v=\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}N
. Since
\max(\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}N_{0})<n
, we have

\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}N_{0}=\alpha(\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}N_{0})\geq_{\operatorname{lex}}\alpha(v)\geq_{\operatorname{lex}}\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}N_{0},

and so
\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}N_{0}=\alpha(v)
. Similarly one can prove that
\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}L_{0}=\alpha(u)
.

Finally, since
L
is a
t
-spread lex set and
|L|\leq|N|
, we have
u\geq_{\operatorname{lex}}v
. Consequently,
\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}L_{0}=\alpha(u)\geq_{\operatorname{lex}}\alpha(v)=\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}N_{0}
and the proof is completed.

∎

As a striking consequence of the previous result, we prove that to every
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable ideal

one can associate a unique
{\bf t}
-spread lex ideal which shares the same
f_{\bf t}
-vector of
I
. It is necessary to highlight that if
I
is an arbitrary
{\bf t}
-spread ideal of
S
, then such a
{\bf t}
-spread lex ideal does not always exist as pointed out in the next example.

Example 3.5
.

(
[
2
, Remark 2]
)
Let
I=(x_{2}x_{8},x_{2}x_{6},x_{2}x_{4})\subset K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{8}]
, which is a
{\bf t}
-spread ideal, with
{\bf t}=(2)
. Such

is not strongly stable, and we have the impossibility to construct a
{\bf t}
-spread lex ideal with the same

-vector as

.

Nevertheless, there can exist a
{\bf t}
-spread ideal
I
of

which is not
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable but for which there exists a
{\bf t}
-spread lex ideal with the same

-vector of

(see, for instance,
[

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
I\subset S
be a
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable ideal. Then, there exists a unique
{\bf t}
-spread lex ideal
I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}}\subset S
such that
f_{\bf t}(I)=f_{\bf t}(I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}})
.

Proof.

We construct a
{\bf t}
-spread lex ideal
J
verifying
f_{\bf t}(J)=f_{\bf t}(I)
as follows. For all
\ell\in[0,d]
, let
L_{\ell}
be the unique
t
-spread lex set of
M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
with
|L_{\ell}|=|[I_{\ell}]_{\bf t}|
. Whereas, for
\ell>d
we set
L_{\ell}=\emptyset
. For all
\ell\geq 0
, we denote by
J_{\ell}
the
K
-vector space spanned by the monomials in the set

L_{\ell}\cup\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf 0}(B_{\ell-1}),

where
B_{-1}=\emptyset
and for
\ell\geq 1
,
B_{\ell-1}
is the set of monomials in
J_{\ell-1}
.

Then, we set
J=\bigoplus_{\ell\geq 0}J_{\ell}
. We claim that
J
satisfies our statement. Firstly, we must show that
J
is a
{\bf t}
-spread lex ideal. For this purpose, it is enough to observe that
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf 0}(B_{\ell-1})\subseteq B_{\ell}
for all
\ell\geq 1
by construction

It remains to prove that
f_{\bf t}(J)=f_{\bf t}(I)
,
i.e.
,
|[J_{\ell}]_{\bf t}|=|[I_{\ell}]_{\bf t}|
for all
\ell\in[0,d]
. Let
\delta=\operatorname{indeg}(I)=\operatorname{indeg}(J)
. Then,
\delta\leq d
and
|[J_{\ell}]_{\bf t}|=|[I_{\ell}]_{\bf t}|=0
for all
\ell\in[0,\delta-1]
. Now, let
\ell\geq\delta
. Since
|L_{\ell}|=|[I_{\ell}]_{\bf t}|
, then

|[J_{\ell}]_{\bf t}|=|L_{\ell}\cup\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(B_{\ell-1})|=|[I_{\ell}]_{\bf t}|

if and only if
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(B_{\ell-1})\subseteq L_{\ell}
. We proceed by finite induction on
\ell\in[\delta,d]
. For the base case
\ell=\delta
, just note that
B_{\delta-1}=\emptyset
. Now, let
\ell>\delta
, then
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(B_{\ell-2})\subseteq L_{\ell-1}
by the inductive hypothesis. Hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(B_{\ell-1})\

\displaystyle=\ \operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L_{\ell-1}\cup\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf 0}(B_{\ell-2}))

\displaystyle=\ \operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L_{\ell-1}\cup\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(B_{\ell-2}))

\displaystyle=\ \operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L_{\ell-1}).

Indeed,

\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf 0}(B_{\ell-2}))=\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(B_{\ell-2})).

It is clear that the second set is included in the first one. For the other inclusion, let
u\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf 0}(B_{\ell-2}))
. Then, by Lemma
3.1

u=vx_{i}x_{j}
with
i\in[\max(v),j]
,
v\in B_{\ell-2}
and
\deg(v)=\ell-2
. Since
u
is
{\bf t}
-spread and
\max(u)=j
, then
vx_{i}
is
t
-spread as well. Hence,
vx_{i}\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(B_{\ell-2})
and so
u\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(B_{\ell-2}))
.

Thus, it remains to prove that
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L_{\ell-1})\subseteq L_{\ell}
. Both sets are
t
-spread lex sets. Therefore, the previous inclusion holds if and only if
|\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L_{\ell-1})|\leq|L_{\ell}|
. By Lemma
3.3
(b) and Theorem
3.4
applied to the sets
L_{\ell-1}
and
[I_{\ell-1}]_{\bf t}
satisfying
|L_{\ell-1}|=|[I_{\ell-1}]_{\bf t}|
, we have,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L_{\ell-1})\right\rvert\

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-2}t_{j}}^{n-t_{\ell-1}}m_{\leq i}(L_{\ell-1})\leq\sum_{i=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-2}t_{j}}^{n-t_{\ell-1}}m_{\leq i}([I_{\ell-1}]_{\bf t})

\displaystyle=\ |\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}([I_{\ell-1}]_{\bf t})|\leq|[I_{\ell}]_{\bf t}|=|L_{\ell}|.

The inductive proof is complete.

We denote
J
by
I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}}
. It is clear that
I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}}
is the unique ideal meeting the requirements of the statement.

∎

4.
The vector-spread Macaulay theorem

The purpose of this section is to give a classification of all possible
f_{\bf t}
-vectors of a
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable ideal. We follow the steps of the classical Macaulay theorem, see
[
10
, Theorem 6.3.8]
.

We quote the following result from
[
10
, Lemma 6.3.4]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\ell
be a positive integer. Then, each positive integer
a
has a unique expansion

a=\binom{a_{\ell}}{\ell}+\binom{a_{\ell-1}}{\ell-1}+\dots+\binom{a_{p}}{p},

with
a_{\ell}>a_{\ell-1}>\dots>a_{p}\geq p\geq 1
.

The previous expansion is called the
binomial expansion
(or
Macaulay expansion
) of
a
with respect to
\ell
. Hereafter, suppose we can write a positive integer
a
as

a=\binom{a_{\ell}}{\ell}+\dots+\binom{a_{p}}{p}+\dots+\binom{a_{1}}{1},

(4.1)

where
a_{\ell}>a_{\ell-1}>\dots>a_{p}\geq p
and
a_{j}<j
for
j=1,\dots,p-1
. Then, by Lemma
4.1
,
a=\sum_{j=p}^{\ell}\binom{a_{j}}{j}
is the (unique) binomial expansion of
a
with respect to
\ell
. However, for our convenience, we refer to (
4.1
) also as a binomial expansion of
a
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
n,\ell
be positive integers,
{\bf t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d-1})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}
,
d\geq 2
such that
n>\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}t_{j}
and
\ell<d
. For all
\ell\in[1,d-1]
, we define a
t
-spread operator as follows: for any positive integer
a\leq|M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}|
, let
a=\sum_{j=p}^{\ell}\binom{a_{j}}{j}
be the binomial expansion of
a
with respect to
\ell
. We define

a^{(\ell,{\bf t})}=\sum_{j=p+1}^{\ell+1}\binom{a_{j-1}+1-t_{\ell}}{j}.

Let
u\in M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
. We define the
initial
{\bf t}
-spread lexsegment set determined by
u
to be the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u)\

\displaystyle=\ \{v\in M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}:v\geq_{\operatorname{lex}}u\}.

Note that any
{\bf t}
-spread lex set
L\subset M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
is an initial
{\bf t}
-spread lexsegment set.

Definition
4.2
is justified by the next result.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
u\in M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
with
\ell<d
and
a=|M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u)|
. Then,

\big|M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}\setminus\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u))\big|\ =\ a^{(\ell,{\bf t})}.

In order to prove the theorem, we need some preliminary lemmata.

Given
\emptyset\neq A\subseteq[1,n]
, we set

M_{A,\ell,{\bf t}}=M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}\cap K[x_{a}:a\in A].

Moreover, if
{\bf t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d-1})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}
, we set
{\bf t}_{\geq k}=(t_{k},\dots,t_{d-1})
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
u=x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{\ell}}\in M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
. Then

M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u)=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k-1}}M_{[i_{k}+1,n],\ell-(k-1),\,{\bf t}_{\geq k}}.

(4.2)

This union is disjoint, and the binomial expansion of
\left\lvert M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u)\right\rvert
is

\left\lvert M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u)\right\rvert=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\binom{a_{j}}{j},

(4.3)

where
a_{j}=n-i_{\ell-(j-1)}+j-1-\sum_{h=\ell-(j-1)}^{\ell-1}t_{h}
, for all
j\in[1,\ell]
.

Proof.

Since
\geq_{\operatorname{lex}}
is a total order, we have
M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u)=\left\{v\in M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}:v<_{\operatorname{lex}}u\right\}
.Let
v=x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{\ell}}\in M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
, with
v<_{\operatorname{lex}}u
. Then,
i_{1}=j_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1}=j_{k-1}
and
i_{k}<j_{k}
, for some
k\in[1,\ell]
. Hence,
v=x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k-1}}w
, where
w\in M_{[i_{k}+1,n],\ell-(k-1),{\bf t}_{\geq k}}
and (
4.2
) follows.

To prove (
4.3
) one can apply (
2.1
), observing that the union in (
4.2
) is disjoint and that
\lvert x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k-1}}M_{[i_{k}+1,n],\ell-(k-1),{\bf t}_{\geq k}}\rvert=\lvert M_{n-i_{k},\ell-(k-1),{\bf t}_{\geq k}}\rvert
. In fact,

\displaystyle\left\lvert M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\Big\lvert\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k-1}}M_{[i_{k}+1,n],\ell-(k-1),\,{\bf t}_{\geq k}}\Big\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\left\lvert M_{n-i_{k},\ell-(k-1),{\bf t}_{\geq k}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\binom{n-i_{k}+(\ell-(k-1)-1)-\sum_{h=k}^{\ell-1}t_{h}}{\ell-(k-1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\binom{n-i_{\ell-(j-1)}+j-1-\sum_{h=\ell-(j-1)}^{\ell-1}t_{h}}{j},

where in the last equality we set
j=\ell-(k-1)
.

It remains to prove that (
4.3
) is the binomial expansion of
\left\lvert M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u)\right\rvert
. Let
p=\min\{j:a_{j}\geq j\}
. By Lemma
4.1
, it is enough to show the following facts:

(i)

a_{\ell}>a_{\ell-1}>\dots>a_{p}\geq p
, and

(ii)

a_{j}<j
, for
j=1,\dots,p-1
.

Statement (ii) follows from the definition of
p
. For the proof of (i), let
\ell>j\geq p
. Then, we have

a_{j+1}-a_{j}=i_{\ell-(j-1)}-i_{\ell-j}+1-t_{\ell-j}\geq t_{\ell-j}+1-t_{\ell-j}=1,

since
i_{\ell-(j-1)}-i_{\ell-j}\geq t_{\ell-j}
. Thus,

a_{j+1}\geq a_{j}+1,

and so
a_{\ell}>a_{\ell-1}>\dots>a_{p}\geq p
, as desired.

∎

Let
L\subseteq M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
be a
{\bf t}
-spread lex set, with
\ell<d
. By Proposition
3.2
(b),
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)\subseteq M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}
is again a
{\bf t}
-spread lex set. Let

u=\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}L=x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{\ell}}.

Then,
L=\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u)
. Hence, if we set
\widetilde{L}=\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)
and
\widetilde{u}=\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)
, then
\widetilde{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(\widetilde{u})
. Therefore, to determine the
{\bf t}
-spread shadow
\widetilde{L}
of
L
it is enough to determine the monomial
\widetilde{u}
. This is accomplished in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

With the notation and assumptions as above, we have

\widetilde{u}=\big(\prod_{m=1}^{\ell-r}x_{i_{m}}\big)\big(\prod_{j=1}^{r+1}x_{n-\sum_{p=\ell-(r-j)}^{\ell}t_{p}}\big),

(4.4)

where we set
i_{0}=t_{0}=0
and

r=\min\Big\{s\in[0,\ell]:n-i_{\ell}+\sum_{h=1}^{s}(i_{\ell-(h-1)}-i_{\ell-h}-t_{\ell-h})\geq t_{\ell}\Big\}.

(4.5)

Proof.

Let us prove that
\widetilde{u}
belongs to
\widetilde{L}
. For this aim, it is enough to show that
v=\widetilde{u}/x_{n}\in L
. Note that

n-i_{\ell}+\sum_{h=1}^{s}(i_{\ell-(h-1)}-i_{\ell-h}-t_{\ell-h})=n-i_{\ell-s}-\sum_{h=1}^{s}t_{\ell-h},

for all
s\in[0,\ell]
. Thus,
r=\min\big\{s\in[0,\ell]:n-i_{\ell-s}-\sum_{h=1}^{s}t_{\ell-h}\geq t_{\ell}\big\}
. Hence,

\big(n-\sum_{h=1}^{r}t_{\ell-h}\big)-i_{\ell-r}\geq t_{\ell}.

By definition of
>_{\operatorname{lex}}
we have
v\geq_{\operatorname{lex}}u
. Since
v
is
{\bf t}
-spread, it follows that
v\in L
.

To prove that
\widetilde{u}=\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}\widetilde{L}
, suppose by contradiction that there exists
w\in\widetilde{L}
such that
w<_{\operatorname{lex}}\widetilde{u}
. Write
w=x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{\ell+1}}
,
\widetilde{u}=x_{k_{1}}\cdots x_{k_{\ell+1}}
. Then,
j_{1}=k_{1},\dots,j_{q-1}=k_{q-1}
and
j_{q}>k_{q}
, for some
q\in[1,\ell+1]
.

If
q\geq\ell-r+1
, then
j_{q}>k_{q}=n-\sum_{p=q}^{\ell}t_{p}
. This is absurd, because all monomials
x_{s_{1}}\cdots x_{s_{\ell+1}}\in M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}
satisfy the inequalities
s_{q}\leq n-\sum_{h=q}^{\ell}t_{h}
,
q\in[1,\ell+1]
.

If
1\leq q\leq\ell-r
, then
j_{q}>k_{q}=i_{q}
. By Lemma
3.1
,
w/x_{j_{\ell+1}}=w^{\prime}\in L
. Hence,
\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}L=u>_{\operatorname{lex}}w^{\prime}
, a contradiction. Finally,
\widetilde{u}=\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}\widetilde{L}
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

As before, let
L=\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u)
,
\widetilde{L}=\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L)
and
\widetilde{u}=\min_{>_{\operatorname{lex}}}\widetilde{L}
. Write
\widetilde{u}=x_{k_{1}}x_{k_{2}}\cdots x_{k_{\ell+1}}
, where the indices
k_{j}
are determined in (
4.4
) and

r=\min\Big\{s\in[0,\ell]:n-i_{\ell}+\sum_{h=1}^{s}(i_{\ell-(h-1)}-i_{\ell-h}-t_{\ell-h})\geq t_{\ell}\Big\}.

Then, by Lemma
4.4
, we have the binomial expansions

\displaystyle|M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}\setminus L|\ =\ \sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\binom{a_{j}}{j},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ |M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}\setminus\widetilde{L}|\ =\ \sum_{j=1}^{\ell+1}\binom{\widetilde{a}_{j}}{j},

where

(a)

a_{j}=n-i_{\ell-(j-1)}+j-1-\sum_{h=\ell-(j-1)}^{\ell-1}t_{h}
, for all
j\in[1,\ell]
, and

(b)

\widetilde{a}_{j}=n-k_{\ell+1-(j-1)}+j-1-\sum_{h=\ell+1-(j-1)}^{\ell}t_{h}
, for all
j\in[1,\ell+1]
.

It remains to prove that
|M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}\setminus\widetilde{L}|=a^{(\ell,{\bf t})}
. Firstly, we establish how the coefficients
a_{j}
and
\widetilde{a}_{j}
are related. Note that, for
j\in[1,r+1]
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}_{j}

\displaystyle=n-k_{\ell+1-(j-1)}+j-1-\sum_{h=\ell+1-(j-1)}^{\ell}t_{h}

\displaystyle=n-\Big(n-\sum_{p=\ell+1-(j-1)}^{\ell}t_{p}\Big)+j-1-\sum_{h=\ell+1-(j-1)}^{\ell}t_{h}

\displaystyle=j-1.

Since
\binom{j-1}{j}=0
, we may write as well

|M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}\setminus\widetilde{L}|=\sum_{j=r+2}^{\ell+1}\binom{\widetilde{a}_{j}}{j}.

Instead, since
k_{\ell+1-(j-1)}=i_{\ell-(j-1)}
, for
j\in[r+2,\ell+1]
, we have

\widetilde{a}_{j}=a_{j-1}+1-t_{\ell}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\big|M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}\setminus\widetilde{L}\big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=r+2}^{\ell+1}\binom{a_{j-1}+1-t_{\ell}}{j}.

Let
p=\min\{j:a_{j}\geq j\}
. The theorem is proved if we show that

\big|M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}\setminus\widetilde{L}\big|=\big|M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}\setminus L\big|^{(\ell,{\bf t})}=\sum_{j=p+1}^{\ell+1}\binom{a_{j-1}+1-t_{\ell}}{j}.

If
p+1=r+2
this is clear. Suppose
p+1>r+2
. Then, it is enough to show that

\binom{a_{j-1}+1-t_{\ell}}{j}=0,\ \ \ \text{for all}\ j\in[r+2,p].

If
j\leq p
, then
a_{j-1}<j-1
. Hence,
a_{j-1}+1-t_{\ell}\leq a_{j-1}+1<j
and
\binom{a_{j-1}+1-t_{\ell}}{j}=0
, as desired. Now let
r+2>p+1
. We must prove that

\binom{a_{j-1}+1-t_{\ell}}{j}=0,

for all
j\in[p+1,r+1]
. Set
a_{\ell+1}=n-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}t_{j}
. Then

r=\min\{s\in[0,\ell]:a_{s+1}-s\geq t_{\ell}\}.

If
j\leq r+1
, then
j-2\leq r-1
. Hence,
a_{(j-2)+1}-(j-2)=a_{j-1}-(j-2)<t_{\ell}
. It follows that
a_{j-1}+1-t_{\ell}<a_{j-1}+2-t_{\ell}<j
and
\binom{a_{j-1}+1-t_{\ell}}{j}=0
, as desired.

∎

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
f=(f_{-1},f_{0},\dots,f_{d-1})
be a sequence of non-negative integers. The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

there exists a
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable ideal
I\subset S=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
such that

f_{\bf t}(I)=f;

(ii)

f_{-1}=1
and
f_{\ell+1}\leq f_{\ell}^{(\ell+1,{\bf t})}
, for all
\ell=-1,\dots,d-2
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). Assume that
f_{\bf t}(I)=f
. By Proposition
3.6
, we may replace
I
by
I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}}
without changing the
f_{\bf t}
-vector. Thus, we may assume as well that
I
is a
{\bf t}
-spread lex ideal. Then,
f_{-1}=f_{{\bf t},-1}(I)=1
and for all
\ell\in[-1,d-2]
we have
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}([I_{\ell+1}]_{\bf t})\subseteq[I_{\ell+2}]_{\bf t}
. Hence,

\displaystyle f_{\ell+1}=f_{{\bf t},\ell+1}(I)=|M_{n,\ell+2,{\bf t}}|-|[I_{\ell+2}]_{\bf t}|\

\displaystyle\leq\ |M_{n,\ell+2,{\bf t}}|-|\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}([I_{\ell+1}]_{\bf t})|

\displaystyle=\ |M_{n,\ell+2,{\bf t}}\setminus\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}([I_{\ell+1}]_{\bf t})|

\displaystyle=\ f_{{\bf t},\ell}(I)^{(\ell+1,{\bf t})}=f_{\ell}^{(\ell+1,{\bf t})},

where the last equality follows from Theorem
4.3
. Statement (ii) is proved.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i). First we prove that

f_{\ell+1}\ \leq\ |M_{n,\ell+2,{\bf t}}|,\ \ \ \text{for all}\ \ \ \ell=-2,\dots,d-2.

For
\ell=-2
,
f_{-1}=1=|M_{n,0,{\bf t}}|
because there is only one
{\bf t}
-spread monomial of degree 0, namely
u=1
. Now we proceed by induction. Let
\ell\geq-1
. By the hypothesis (ii), we have
f_{\ell+1}\leq f_{\ell}^{(\ell+1,{\bf t})}
and, by induction,
f_{\ell}\leq|M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}|
. Thus, there exists a unique monomial
u\in M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}
such that
|M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u)|=f_{\ell}
. By Theorem
4.3
, we have
f_{\ell}^{(\ell+1,{\bf t})}=|M_{n,\ell+2,{\bf t}}\setminus\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(\mathcal{L}_{\bf t}^{i}(u))|
. This shows that
f_{\ell}^{(\ell+1,{\bf t})}\leq|M_{n,\ell+2,{\bf t}}|
and consequently we have
f_{\ell+1}\leq|M_{n,\ell+2,{\bf t}}|
, as desired.

For all
\ell\in[0,d]
, let
L_{\ell}
be the unique
{\bf t}
-spread lex set of
M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}
such that
|L_{\ell}|=|M_{n,\ell,{\bf t}}|-f_{\ell-1}
. For
\ell>d
we set
L_{\ell}=\emptyset
. As in Proposition
3.6
, we construct the ideal
I=\bigoplus_{\ell\geq 0}I_{\ell}
where
I_{\ell}
is the
K
-vector space spanned by the set

L_{\ell}\cup\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf 0}(B_{\ell-1}),

where
B_{-1}=\emptyset
and for
\ell\geq 1
,
B_{\ell-1}
is the set of monomials generating
I_{\ell-1}
. As in Proposition
3.6
, one shows that
I
is a
t
-spread lex ideal. Hence, it remains to prove that
f_{\bf t}(I)=f
. As in the proof of Proposition
3.6
, this boils down to proving that
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L_{\ell+1})\subseteq L_{\ell+2}
, for all
\ell\in[-1,d-2]
. Since
f_{\ell+1}\leq f_{\ell}^{(\ell+1,{\bf t})}
we have

|M_{n,\ell+2,{\bf t}}\setminus L_{\ell+2}|\leq|M_{n,\ell+1,{\bf t}}\setminus L_{\ell+1}|^{(\ell+1,{\bf t})}=|M_{n,\ell+2,{\bf t}}\setminus\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L_{\ell+1})|,

where the last equality follows from Theorem
4.3
. Thus,
|\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L_{\ell+1})|\leq|L_{\ell+2}|
. Hence,
\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}(L_{\ell+1})\subseteq L_{\ell+2}
, because both are
{\bf t}
-spread lex sets. The proof is complete.

∎

5.
An application

In this final section, as an application we recover the vector-spread version of the well–known result proved by Bigatti
[
4
]
and Hulett
[
11
]
, independently (see, also,
[
12
,
15
]
). More precisely, we prove that in the class of all
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable ideals with a given
f_{\bf t}
-vector, the
{\bf t}
-spread lex ideals have the largest graded Betti numbers.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
I\subset S=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a
{\bf t}
-spread strongly stable ideal. Then,

\beta_{i,j}(I)\ \leq\ \beta_{i,j}(I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}}),\ \ \ \text{for all}\ i\ \text{and}\ j.

Proof.

By
[
8
, Corollary 5.2]
, we have

\beta_{i,i+j}(I)\ =\ \sum_{u\in G(I)_{j}}\binom{\max(u)-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i}.

(5.1)

We are going to write (
5.1
) in a more suitable way. We observe that
I
is a
t
-spread ideal and thus

G(I)_{j}\ =\ [I_{j}]_{\bf t}\setminus\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}([I_{j-1}]_{\bf t}).

Hence, we can write the Betti number in (
5.1
) as a difference
A-B
, where

\displaystyle A\ =

\displaystyle\ \sum_{u\in G([I_{j}]_{\bf t})}\binom{\max(u)-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})\binom{k-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(m_{\leq k}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})-m_{\leq k-1}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})\Big)\binom{k-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}m_{\leq k}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})\binom{k-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i}-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}m_{\leq k}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})\binom{k-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i}

and

\displaystyle B\ =

\displaystyle\ \sum_{u\in\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}([I_{j-1}]_{\bf t})}\binom{\max(u)-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=1+\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}^{n}\!\!\!\!m_{k}(\operatorname{Shad}_{\bf t}([I_{j-1}]_{\bf t}))\binom{k-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=1+\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}^{n}m_{\leq k-t_{j-1}}([I_{j-1}]_{\bf t})\binom{k-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i},

where the last equality follows from Lemma
3.3
(a).

Furthermore, we can write
A=A_{1}-A_{2}
with

\displaystyle A_{1}\ =

\displaystyle\ m_{\leq n}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})\binom{n-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i},

\displaystyle A_{2}\ =

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}m_{\leq k}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})\bigg[\binom{k-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i}-\binom{k-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=1+\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}^{n-1}m_{\leq k}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})\binom{k-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i-1}.

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta_{i,i+j}(J)

\displaystyle=\ m_{\leq n}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})\binom{n-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i}

(5.2)

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1+\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}^{n-1}m_{\leq k}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})\binom{k-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i-1}

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1+\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}^{n}m_{\leq k-t_{j-1}}([I_{j-1}]_{\bf t})\binom{k-1-\sum_{h=1}^{j-1}t_{h}}{i}.

Now, we compute the graded Betti numbers
\beta_{i,i+j}(I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}})
. Recall that
I
and
I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}}
share the same
f_{\bf t}
-vector. Therefore,
|[I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}}_{j}]_{\bf t}|=|[I_{j}]_{\bf t}|
, for all
j
. Applying Theorem
3.4
, we have
m_{\leq k}([I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}}_{j}]_{\bf t})\leq m_{\leq k}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})
for all
k\in[n]
. Moreover,

m_{\leq n}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})=|[I_{j}]_{\bf t}|=|[I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}}_{j}]_{\bf t}|=m_{\leq n}([I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}}_{j}]_{\bf t}).

Therefore, replacing in (
5.2
), for all
k
and
j
, every occurrence of
m_{\leq k}([I_{j}]_{\bf t})
with
m_{\leq k}([I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}}_{j}]_{\bf t})
, we get the Betti number
\beta_{i,i+j}(I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}})
. Finally,
\beta_{i,i+j}(I)\leq\beta_{i,i+j}(I^{{\bf t},\operatorname{lex}})
, for all
i,j\geq 0
.

∎
