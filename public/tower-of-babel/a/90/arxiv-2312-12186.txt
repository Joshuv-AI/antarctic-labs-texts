Abstract
Traditional social learning frameworks consider environments with a homogeneous state, where each agent receives observations conditioned on that true state of nature. In this work, we relax this assumption and study the distributed hypothesis testing problem in a heterogeneous environment, where each agent can receive observations conditioned on their own personalized state of nature (or truth). We particularly focus on community structured networks, where each community admits their own true hypothesis. This scenario is common in various contexts, such as when sensors are spatially distributed, or when individuals in a social network have differing views or opinions. We show that the adaptive social learning strategy is a preferred choice for nonstationary environments, and allows each cluster to discover its own truth.

II
Social Learning Model

Consider a collection of agents denoted by
\mathcal{N}
, interacting to form belief vectors that reflect their confidence in each possible hypothesis
\theta\in\Theta
based on their private streaming observations and on interactions with their immediate neighbors. These interactions are governed by a combination graph
A\in[0,1]^{|\mathcal{N}|\times|\mathcal{N}|}
, where nonzero elements indicate an edge between two nodes. For any two connected agents, the value
a_{\ell k}=[A]_{\ell,k}>0
determines the level of trust that agent
k\in\mathcal{N}
assigns to information arriving from agent
\ell\in\mathcal{N}
. The combination matrix is assumed to be left-stochastic, i.e., the weights on every column add up to one:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}a_{\ell k}=1,\;\forall k\in\mathcal{N}.

(1)

In this way, the total trust that each agent assigns to its neighbors is equal to
1
. Additionally, the combination graph is assumed to be strongly connected, which means that there exists at least one self-loop with a positive weight, i.e.,
a_{kk}>0
for some
k
and, moreover, there exists a path with positive weights between any two nodes. This condition implies that the matrix
A
is primitive, i.e., there exists an integer power
t>0
such that all entries of
[A^{t}]_{k,\ell}
are positive
[
41
]
. These assumptions are common in the literature and they have been shown to guarantee the global truth learning for homogeneous environments under traditional social learning
[
5
,
10
,
3
]
. It follows from the primitiveness of
A
and the Perron-Frobenius theorem
[
42
, Chapter 8]
,
[
41
]
that the power matrix
A^{s}
converges to
u\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}
as
s\rightarrow\infty
at an exponential rate, where
\mathds{1}
is the vector with all entries equal to
1
and
u
refers to the Perron eigenvector of
A
. This is the eigenvector that is associated with the eigenvalue at
1
and its entries are normalized to be positive and to add up to
1
:

\displaystyle Au=u,\qquad u_{\ell}>0,\qquad\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}u_{\ell}=1.

(2)

At each time instant
i
, agent
k
receives some random observation
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}
from the environment. In traditional social learning algorithms, it is assumed that there exists a single global true state of nature, denoted by
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
. In other words, the observations at every agent
k
are generated according to some likelihood model conditioned on this
\theta^{\star}
, denoted by
L_{k}(\zeta_{k,i}|\theta^{\star})
. The aim of the social learning algorithm then becomes to enable all agents to discover the value of
\theta^{\star}
from the streaming observations. In this work, however, we are going to allow the truth
\theta^{\star}
to be agent-dependent and denote it by
\theta^{\star}_{k}
. That is, each agent can now have its own generative model. The observations are then conditioned on the local model and we write
L_{k}(\zeta_{k,i}|\theta_{k}^{\star})
. Sometimes, for compactness, we will denote the likelihood functions by writing
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}\sim L_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star})
and drop the observation variables.
We assume the observations are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d) over time.

The traditional social learning strategy is described as follows. Each agent
k\in\mathcal{N}
assigns an initial belief
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,0}(\theta)\in[0,1]
for each state
\theta\in\Theta
such that the total confidence sums up to
1

\sum_{\theta}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,0}(\theta)=1

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,0}

k

\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}
and performs a local Bayesian update

,

3

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta)=\frac{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}|\theta)\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i-1}(\theta)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}|\theta^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i-1}(\theta^{\prime})},\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{N}.

(3)

The vector

is a probability mass function and we refer to it as the
intermediate
(or

) belief. The qualification “public” refers to the fact that this vector is shared among neighbors. For the adaptive social learning (ASL) strategy from
[

3

III
Truth Learning

III-A

Traditional Social Learning

First, we examine how the performance of the traditional social learning strategy (
3
) and (
5
) is affected when there are multiple models/hypotheses for the data received by the agents. Normally, when all agents receive observations generated by the same model
\theta^{\star}
, it is known that the network reaches consensus and all agents converge to that value of
\theta
that minimizes the following objective function
[
10
,
8
,
2
,
3
]
:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}u_{k}D_{\textrm{KL}}\big(L_{k}(\theta^{\star})||L_{k}(\theta)\big)

(9)

Here, the notation
D_{\textrm{KL}}
denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between two distributions:

\displaystyle D_{\textrm{KL}}\big(L_{k}(\theta^{\star})||L_{k}(\theta)\big)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}\sim L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}|\theta^{\star})}\log\frac{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}|\theta^{\star})}{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}|\theta)}

(10)

Actually, the minimum value of (
9
) is
0
for
\theta=\theta^{\star}
, and the KL divergence takes positive values for any other
\theta\neq\theta^{\star}
2
. This conclusion is valid when all agents in the network are assumed to receive observations that are generated by the same model
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
. If, however, the agents happen to receive observations generated by a distribution that does not necessarily agree with any of the assumed likelihoods, i.e., if
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}\sim f(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k})\neq L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}|\theta)
for any
\theta\in\Theta
, then it is known in this case that the agents will not satisfy (
7
) but rather they will converge to the optimal subset of hypotheses
\Theta^{\star}
that minimizes the following objective function
[
5
]
:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}u_{k}D_{\textrm{KL}}\big(f_{k}(\cdot)||L_{k}(\theta)\big).

(11)

In other words, each agent’s belief on any other hypothesis
\theta\notin\Theta^{\star}
will converge to zero
[
5
, Theorem 1]
:

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)=0,\textrm{ a.s. }\forall\theta\notin\Theta^{\star},\;\forall k\in\mathcal{N}.

(12)

In general, the hypotheses in the subset
\Theta^{\star}
need not necessarily be optimal for any of the individual agents. In particular, there can exist some agent
k
and some model
\theta^{\circ}\notin\Theta^{\star}
such that
D_{\textrm{KL}}\big(f_{k}(\cdot)||L_{k}(\theta^{\circ})\big)<D_{\textrm{KL}}\big(f_{k}(\cdot)||L_{k}(\theta^{\star})\big)
.

The setting in this work is different. We assume that each agent
k
has its own truth
\theta_{k}^{\star}
and we would like it to learn/discover that truth. According to (
11
), in this case the agents in the network will converge to the consensus subset resulting from:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}u_{k}D_{\textrm{KL}}\big(L(\theta_{k}^{\star})||L_{k}(\theta)\big)

(13)

Hence, under traditional social learning, even if the agents have different local truths, the network will reach consensus on some subset
\Theta^{\star}
.

Before stating the convergence result from
[
5
]
, we first define the
network divergence
between hypotheses
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
:

\displaystyle K(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\triangleq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}u_{k}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}\sim L_{k}(\cdot|\theta_{k}^{\star})}\log\frac{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}|\theta)}{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}|\theta^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}u_{k}\Big(D_{\textrm{KL}}\big(L_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star})||L_{k}(\theta^{\prime})\big)-D_{\textrm{KL}}\big(L_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star})||L_{k}(\theta)\big)\Big)

(14)

It is straightforward to verify that for any
\theta^{\star}
in the optimal subset
\Theta^{\star}
of (
13
) and for any other
\theta\notin\Theta^{\star}
, the network divergence is strictly positive:

\displaystyle K(\theta^{\star},\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}u_{k}D_{\textup{KL}}\big(L_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star})||L_{k}(\theta)\big)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-\min_{\theta^{\prime}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}u_{k}D_{\textup{KL}}\left(L_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star})||L_{k}(\theta^{\prime})\right)>0

(15)

Lemma 1

(
Convergence of traditional social learning
[
5
, Thm. 1]
).

Let
\Theta^{\star}\subset\Theta
denote the solution to (
13
). In steady-state, as
i\rightarrow\infty
, the belief of every agent
k
at any hypothesis
\theta\notin\Theta^{\star}
vanishes almost surely:

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)=0\textup{, a.s.}

(16)

at the following rate of convergence:

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)}{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta^{\star})}=-K(\theta^{\star},\theta)

(17)

for any
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta^{\star}
.

.

Due to the Bayesian update (
3
), the same conclusion holds for the public beliefs
\{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k,i}\}_{k\in\mathcal{N}}
.
\blacksquare

It follows that when there are clusters in the graph with different true hypotheses, the algorithm behaves conservatively and forces the entire network to agree on the optimal subset
\Theta^{\star}
. A more desirable result would be for the agents in each cluster to converge to their own truth. Thus, depending on the setting, the traditional strategy (

)–(

III-B

Adaptive Social Learning

The main advantage of the adaptive strategy (
4
)–(
5
) is its capacity to adapt to changes in the models over time. The hyperparameter
\delta\in(0,1)
plays an important role in this regard and defines the confidence attached to newly received samples: a smaller
\delta
makes the algorithm lean more towards the previous true state.

Lemma 2

(
Log-belief ratios under adaptive social learning).

For each agent
k
and for any pair of hypotheses
\theta\neq\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
, the log-belief ratio of private beliefs converges in distribution to the following random variable:

\displaystyle\log\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)}{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta^{\prime})}\xrightarrow[i\to\infty]{d}

\displaystyle\;\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\triangleq\delta\sum_{\ell}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(1-\delta)^{t}[A^{t+1}]_{\ell k}\boldsymbol{{\nu}}_{\ell,t}

(18)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{{\nu}}_{\ell,t}\triangleq\log\frac{L_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{\ell,t}|\theta)}{L_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{\ell,t}|\theta^{\prime})}

(19)

Its expected value is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})}=

\displaystyle\;\delta\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(1-\delta)^{t}[A^{t+1}]_{\ell k}\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{{\nu}}_{\ell,t}

(20)

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{{\nu}}_{\ell,t}=

\displaystyle\;D_{\textup{KL}}\big(L_{\ell}(\theta_{\ell}^{\star})||L_{\ell}(\theta^{\prime})\big)-D_{\textup{KL}}\big(L_{\ell}(\theta_{\ell}^{\star})||L_{\ell}(\theta)\big)

(21)

Assuming finiteness of second-order moments for the log-likelihoods, i.e.
\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\ell,t}^{2}<\infty
for any agent
\ell\in\mathcal{N}
, the variance of
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k}
becomes on the order of
\delta
:

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k}(\theta,\theta^{\prime}))=O(\delta).

(22)

Proof.

This statement can be obtained by using
[
8
, Theorem 1]
and
[
8
, Lemma 1]
.
∎

Remark 2
.

Following similar arguments, the result can be extended to the log-belief ratio of public beliefs with
A^{t}
replacing
A^{t+1}
in (
18
) and (
20
).

\blacksquare

The results of the above lemma imply that the expectation
\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})}
determines which hypothesis agent
k
will prioritize in the steady-state
on average
. As opposed to the case when
\delta\to 0
studied in
[
8
]
, there is no almost sure convergence guarantee toward the prioritized hypothesis, and the log beliefs will fluctuate around their mean
\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})}
with the variance on the order of
O(\delta)
.

These results also reveal that each agent
k
can arrive at its own locally optimal solution because the expression on the right-hand side of (
18
) depends on
k
. This is in contrast to traditional social learning where it is seen from (
16
) that the beliefs of all agents converge to the same zero value with a rate determined by the network divergence. In the ASL, the final inference for each agent depends on the local network, and thus on the observations and the true states of these agents: observe from (
20
) that each agent gives higher importance to its close neighbors. In particular, the weight
(1-\delta)
scales the immediate one-hop neighbors (namely, those agents
\ell
for which
a_{\ell k}
is non-zero), while the weight
(1-\delta)^{2}
scales the agents from the 2-hop neighborhood, and so on. This way, as the value of
\delta
increases, the influence of further connected nodes diminishes.

This observation suggests that under certain network conditions, such as community structured graphs, and for large enough
\delta
, each agent
k
should be able to arrive on average to their own truth
\theta_{k}^{\star}
. This is because over these graphs there is a higher probability for each agent to be connected to the nodes that share the same underlying truth. We will verify that this is indeed the case.

We highlight that when
\delta\to 0
, the algorithm behaves similarly to traditional social learning and the network converges to the same subset of hypotheses
\Theta^{\star}
that minimizes (
13
).
Particlularly, it can be shown
[
8
, Theorem 1]
that:

\displaystyle\log\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)}{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta^{\prime})}\xrightarrow[\delta\to 0,i\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}K(\theta,\theta^{\prime})

(23)

Thus, in the following we focus on studying the behavior of adaptive social learning when it is distinct from traditional social learning, which means that we will not let
\delta\rightarrow 0
.

IV
Stochastic Block Model

The Stochastic Block Model (SBM) defines each community as a collection of agents that have a large probability of connection with each other, while the probability of connection between communities is small
[
23
,
43
,
24
]
. It is a popular framework for modeling social networks and graphs with polar opinions
[
44
,
45
]
, which reflects the fact that there need not exist a single truth. For example, over social networks, groups of people may support different political parties, therefore they end up consuming media (i.e., observations) conditioned on different “true states”. In a similar manner, over a network of spatially distributed sensors, different sensors might experience different weather conditions (different temperature or precipitation).
This motivates us to study social learning under polar true hypotheses. Assuming that each cluster receives data conditioned on a different true state, we are interested in determining conditions that enable each of the communities to discover their own truth. From Lemma
1
we know that the traditional social learning strategies always converge to one single solution. Therefore, in this section, we focus on the adaptive social learning strategy due to its richer convergence behavior. We remark that the majority of works on community-structured graphs focus their analysis on the case of graphs with two communities
[
23
,
43
]
. While extending the experimental part to more general cases with multiple communities is feasible, theoretical bounds often become intractable. In this work, we will similarly focus on the common scenario with two communities.

We describe next the SBM model. We denote by
\boldsymbol{E}
the adjacency matrix of the network. Specifically, when the corresponding edge
(\ell,k)
is present in the graph, the entry
[\boldsymbol{E}]_{\ell,k}=1
, and agent
k
is able to receive signals from the neighboring agent
\ell
; otherwise, the corresponding entry
[\boldsymbol{E}]_{\ell,k}=0
. The entries of the adjacency matrix are assumed to be drawn from a Bernoulli distribution,
\boldsymbol{E}\sim Bernoulli(P)
, conditioned on the probability matrix
P\in[0,1]^{|\mathcal{N}|\times|\mathcal{N}|}
(i.e., each entry
\boldsymbol{E}_{\ell,k}\sim Bernoulli(P_{\ell,k})
is generated independently). We introduce the main idea by considering a model with two communities of sizes
n_{0}
and

Agents in the network will communicate with each other according to a combination protocol that is defined by some matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
, which amounts to a weighted version of the adjacency matrix. We assume the combination weights are set using the averaging rule
[
46
]
. More formally, we let
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}
where
\boldsymbol{D}=\textrm{diag}(\sum_{\ell}\boldsymbol{E}_{\ell,1},\dots,\sum_{\ell}\boldsymbol{E}_{\ell,|\mathcal{N}|})
. This way, each column is normalized and agents give equal confidence to their neighbors with each entry equal to:

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{A}]_{\ell,k}=\frac{\boldsymbol{E}_{\ell,k}}{\sum_{\ell}\boldsymbol{E}_{\ell,k}}

(27)

We show one example of such a combination matrix in Fig.
1(b)
, based on the adjacency network from Fig.
1(a)
.

We additionally assume that all agents within the same community receive data arising from the same underlying model (or hypothesis). We are then interested in devising conditions under which the communities can discover their unknown models from the observations. In our particular two-communities case, we assume the binary hypotheses set
\Theta=\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\}
. Thus, we let the first
n_{0}
agents
k\in\mathcal{C}_{0}\triangleq\{1,\dots,n_{0}\}
to follow hypothesis
\theta_{0}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}\sim L_{k}(\cdot|\theta_{0})
, and the remaining agents
k\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\triangleq\{n_{0}+1,\dots,|\mathcal{N}|\}
to follow hypothesis
\theta_{1}
. For simplicity, we assume that agents in each community (or cluster) have the same level of informativeness measured in terms of the KL divergence between the two models, as defined by the following statement.

Assumption 1

(
Homogeneous likelihoods).

Within each cluster
\mathcal{C}_{i}
, the agents have the same level of informativeness, i.e., for any
k\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
:

\displaystyle d_{0}\triangleq D_{\textup{KL}}\big(L_{k}\left(\theta_{0}\right)||L_{k}\left(\theta_{1}\right)\big)

(28)

and for any
k\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
:

\displaystyle d_{1}\triangleq D_{\textup{KL}}\big(L_{k}\left(\theta_{1}\right)||L_{k}\left(\theta_{0}\right)\big)

(29)

\blacksquare

While this assumption is not strictly necessary, it is introduced for the sake of clarity and for analytical tractability of the results. It holds, for example, when all likelihoods within each community are equal, i.e., agents receive samples from the same or similar sources.

IV-A

SBM Network Properties

In this subsection, we study the properties of the combination matrix (
27
) generated according to the SBM model. First, we show that for a large number of agents, we can approximate the
t
-th moment of the combination matrix by the
t-
th power of its average value.

Lemma 3

(
Expected combination matrix).

The expected combination matrix
\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{A}
is left-stochastic and takes the following block form:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{A}=\overline{A}+O\left(\min\{n_{0},n_{1}\}^{-4/3}\right)

(30)

where

\displaystyle\overline{A}\triangleq\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\begin{array}[]{c}\dfrac{p_{0}}{p_{0}n_{0}+q_{1}n_{1}}\mathds{1}_{n_{0}}\mathds{1}_{n_{0}}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\textrm{ }\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{c}\dfrac{q_{0}}{q_{0}n_{0}+p_{1}n_{1}}\mathds{1}_{n_{0}}\mathds{1}_{n_{1}}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\textrm{ }\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c}\textrm{ }\\
\dfrac{q_{1}}{p_{0}n_{0}+q_{1}n_{1}}\mathds{1}_{n_{1}}\mathds{1}_{n_{0}}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{c}\textrm{ }\\
\dfrac{p_{1}}{q_{0}n_{0}+p_{1}n_{1}}\mathds{1}_{n_{1}}\mathds{1}_{n_{1}}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{array}\end{array}\right]

and
\mathds{1}_{n}
is a column vector of ones of size
n
.
Moreover, for integer powers
t\leq|\mathcal{N}|
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{A}^{t}=\overline{A}^{t}+O\left(\min\{n_{0},n_{1}\}^{-4/3}\right)

(39)

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.
∎

In the special case when the communities are of the same size with equal probabilities of connection inside the communities and across them, we can determine an exact expression for the matrix powers of
\overline{A}
.

Lemma 4

(
Matrix powers).

When the communities are symmetric, i.e.
n\triangleq n_{0}=n_{1}
,
p\triangleq p_{0}=p_{1}
and
q\triangleq q_{0}=q_{1}
, the powers of
\overline{A}^{t}\approx\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{A}^{t}
for
t\leq 2n
, take the following form:

\displaystyle\overline{A}^{t}=\frac{1}{2n}\begin{bmatrix}1+\left(\frac{p-q}{p+q}\right)^{t}&1-\left(\frac{p-q}{p+q}\right)^{t}\\
1-\left(\frac{p-q}{p+q}\right)^{t}&1+\left(\frac{p-q}{p+q}\right)^{t}\end{bmatrix}\otimes\left(\mathds{1}_{n}\mathds{1}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)

(40)

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.

Proof.

See Appendix
B
.
∎

IV-B

Truth Learning

First, we focus on the symmetric communities case from Lemma
4
where we have a closed-form expression for
\overline{A}^{t}
, which can be used in expression (
20
). We remark first that when
\boldsymbol{A}
is random, as is the case in the SBM structure, the power matrix
A^{t+1}
in (
20
) should be replaced by the
(t+1)
-th moment of
\boldsymbol{A}
. This is because the result (
20
) does not take into account the additional layer of randomness for combination weights, which are sampled independently from the observations. By adjusting
\delta
, we can force each cluster to arrive at their own truth:

Theorem 1

(
Log-belief ratios for symmetric communities).

Under the same scenario of Lemma
4
, the expected log-belief ratio from (
20
) under adaptive social learning takes the following form in the steady state for any
k\in{\cal C}_{0}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k}(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})}=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{2}(d_{0}-d_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta(d_{0}+d_{1})(p-q)}{p+q-(1-\delta)(p-q)}

\displaystyle\;+O\left(n^{-1/3}\right)

(41)

while for any
k\in{\cal C}_{1}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k}(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})}=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{2}(d_{0}-d_{1})-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta(d_{0}+d_{1})(p-q)}{p+q-(1-\delta)(p-q)}

\displaystyle\;+O\left(n^{-1/3}\right)

(42)

where the expectations are taken with respect to the randomness in both the combination matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
and the observations
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
C
.
∎

This result allows us to conclude that for large enough steps-size
\delta
and large enough
n
, each cluster will converge in expectation to its own hypothesis if their corresponding
\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{k}(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})
assumes positive or negative signs depending on the cluster, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{0}\colon\frac{1}{2}(d_{0}-d_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta(d_{0}+d_{1})(p-q)}{p+q-(1-\delta)(p-q)}>0

(43a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}\colon\frac{1}{2}(d_{0}-d_{1})-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta(d_{0}+d_{1})(p-q)}{p+q-(1-\delta)(p-q)}<0

(43b)

Assuming
p>q
, these conditions can be satisfied if we select:

\displaystyle\delta>\max\left\{\frac{d_{1}-d_{0}}{d_{0}},\frac{d_{0}-d_{1}}{d_{1}}\right\}\times\frac{q}{p-q}

(44)

Remark 3
.

Conditions (
44
) is also valid for public beliefs. Indeed, in view of (
4
)–(
5
), we observe that the log-belief ratios for
k\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
satisfy:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\log\frac{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta_{0})}{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta_{1})}=\delta d_{0}+(1-\delta)\mathbb{E}\log\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i-1}(\theta_{0})}{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i-1}(\theta_{1})}

(45)

Thus, if
\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{k}
is positive, then the public belief is positive too, and therefore the same hypothesis is prioritized.
\blacksquare

Remark 4
.

From an information-theoretic perspective
[
23
, Theorem 5]
, under the same scenario of Lemma
4
, one cannot exactly recover the communities if the following condition holds:

\displaystyle\Bigg|\sqrt{\frac{(n/2)p}{\log(n/2)}}-\sqrt{\frac{(n/2)q}{\log(n/2)}}\Bigg|<\sqrt{2}

(46)

This implies that exact community detection becomes infeasible for highly sparse networks where
p,\;q=O(1/n)
. However, condition (
44
) for the choice of
\delta
does not introduce additional assumptions on
p
and
q
(except that
p<q
). If we let
p
and
q
to be on the order of
O(1/n)
, the dependence on
n
cancels out, allowing the social learning algorithm to perform well in such cases. We demonstrate this property in the experiments in Section
V
.

\blacksquare

By examining (
43a
)–(
43b
), we notice that both conditions share the same first term, while the second terms with
\delta
have the opposite signs. If we let
\delta\to 0
, then one of the inequalities will not hold. This observation suggests that for small
\delta
, the network will converge to one single hypothesis (or the subset of hypotheses) that solves (
13
) and will therefore behave similarly to traditional social learning. In contrast, a reasonably large
\delta
would allow the less dominant cluster (i.e., cluster with smaller informativeness level
d_{i}
) to drive itself to their own truth.

We should also remark that since
\delta\in(0,1)
, it is not always guaranteed that a
\delta
will exist that satisfies (
44
). For example, if the agents in one of the clusters have much lower level of informativeness (i.e., low
d_{i}
), then the right-hand side in (
44
) can exceed 1. In such a case, the designer may consider reducing the connectivity between clusters. Moreover, it is important to keep in mind that a large
\delta
enlarges the variance of the log-belief ratio (according to Lemma
2
). In this case, a more accurate estimate for the expected value of the belief can be obtained by relying on the average of a window of beliefs, say,
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{t=i-M+1}^{i}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)
for some window size
M
. We illustrate this trade-off in the experiment section.

Next we consider asymmetric communities, where
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
need not agree, as well as
q_{0}
and
q_{1}
. Likewise, the sizes
n_{0}
and
n_{1}
can be different.

Theorem 2

(
Log-belief ratios for asymmetric communities).

If the following relations hold:

\displaystyle p_{0}n_{0}d_{0}-q_{1}n_{1}d_{1}>0,\quad p_{1}n_{1}d_{1}-q_{0}n_{0}d_{0}>0

(47)

Then, there exist a
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
, such that for any
\delta>\delta_{0}
, on average, each cluster converges to its own hypothesis, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k}(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})}
is strictly positive or strictly negative depending on the cluster.

Proof.

See Appendix
D
.
∎

We can interpret expression (
47
) as a condition wherein the internal connections are more influential than the external connections for both clusters. This influence can be regulated by factors such as community sizes, connection probabilities, or the average informativeness (i.e.,
d_{i}
) of the agents.

Remark 5
.

Similarly to Remark
3
, the above conditions are also sufficient for public beliefs.
\blacksquare

.

According to (
97
)–(
98
), for a network with
d_{0}=0.035
,
d_{1}=0.04
,
n_{0}=10
,
n_{1}=8
,
p_{0}=p_{1}=0.8
, and
q_{0}=q_{1}=0.2
, a step-size
\delta>0.15
is required.

Example 2
.

For the symmetric case where
n_{0}=n_{1}
, and
p_{0}=p_{1}
as well as
q_{0}=q_{1}
, the bound (
44
) is more precise than the bounds (
97
)–(

). Consider Example

and let

. Then, (

, while (

.

V-A

Twitter data

To illustrate the results from the previous sections, we consider a Twitter dataset. We will demonstrate that the adaptive social learning strategy is a more suitable fit for the opinion dynamics occuring there. Using the Twitter API and the Tweepy library, we downloaded tweets related to the Brexit discussion from 01 January 2020 to 01 April 2020 among UK parliament members. We also collected follower-followee relations to build a network of
535
users, which is shown in Figure

.

Twitter (now
\mathbb{X}
) is an example of a social media platform that can be used to illustrate the social learning paradigm. There, users form a directed network of follower-followee relations.
It is reasonable to assume that each user gives a certain trust level (unknown to the observer) to their followees (i.e., to the users they follow). These trust levels form the combination matrix
A
. Over Twitter, users often engage in discussions on topics of mutual interest. Every tweet can reflect a positive or negative sentiment about the topic. Thus, we can treat each tweet as a reflection of a public belief (or opinion)
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta)
albeit expressed in verbal form.

First, we would like to illustrate that the Stochastic Block Model is a useful model that arises over social networks. Applying the Louvain community detection algorithm
[
47
]
to the collected graph (the algorithm requires knowledge of the adjacency matrix, whereas our algorithms mainly work with opinion dynamics without knowing the graph structure), we can observe three groups shown in Figure
2(a)
.

Similarly to the work
[
16
]
, we process the text of the collected tweets using sentiment analysis to extract public beliefs
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta)
from them. For this purpose, we use the Roberta-based language model
[
48
,
49
]
trained on 124M tweets for the sentiment analysis task.
We assume a binary hypothesis problem aimed at deciding whether Brexit is a good or bad idea. We analyze each parliament member tweet about Brexit during a given period of time and plot their average opinions in Figure
2(b)
.
We see that people belonging to the same cluster tend to share similar opinions
[
30
,
50
]
. The traditional social learning falls short in capturing such opinion dynamics with polarized beliefs, while the adaptive strategy allows such discovery, as Theorem
2
predicts.

\mathcal{N}=535

VI
Conclusions

This work examines the behavior of social learning algorithms under multiple true hypotheses. We show that traditional social learning techniques are robust to malicious agents and the entire network converges to one solution that “explains” the observations in the “best way possible”. In other words, if the majority of high-informative agents operate with observations following the true hypothesis, malicious or malfunctioning agents (the agents that receive observations from another state) will not be able to drive the network to a wrong conclusion. Adaptive social learning strategies behave similarly when the adaptation hyperparameter
\delta\to 0
, however, they are the preferred choice for graphs with community structure (such as SBM). We derived values for
\delta
for the case of two communities for each cluster to find their own truth, and illustrated the behavior of the methods through real and simulated data.

Lemma 5

(
Inverse moments of binomial random variables).

Consider any random variable
\boldsymbol{b}\sim B(n,p)
. For any integer

and constant

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{(c+\boldsymbol{b})^{t}}=\frac{1}{(c+np)^{t}}+O\left(n^{-(t+1/3)}\right).

Proof.

In
[
51

it is shown that the deviations of binomial random variables are bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{b}\leq an\right)\leq e^{-nH(a,p)}

(52)

where
a\in(0,p)
and
H
denotes the KL divergence between two Bernoulli distributions (or the relative entropy between them):

\displaystyle H(a,p)\triangleq a\log\frac{a}{p}+(1-a)\log\frac{1-a}{1-p}

(53)

Therefore, since
\boldsymbol{b}
takes nonnegative values, it holds that for any power

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{b}^{t}\leq(an)^{t}\right)\leq e^{-nH(a,p)}

Now, consider the negative moments of
\boldsymbol{b}
of the form

\overline{\omega}
. By the property of total probability and using (
52

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{(c+\boldsymbol{b})^{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{(c+\boldsymbol{b})^{t}}\;\middle|\;\omega\right)\mathbb{P}(\omega)+\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{(c+\boldsymbol{b})^{t}}\;\middle|\;\overline{\omega}\right)\mathbb{P}(\overline{\omega})

(55)

Assume that for some small
\varepsilon
, we choose
a=p-\varepsilon\in(0,p)
. Then, the KL divergence (

) becomes:

\displaystyle H(p-\varepsilon,p)

\displaystyle=(p-\varepsilon)\log\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{p}\right)+(1-p+\varepsilon)\log\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{1-p}\right).

(56)

Using Taylor’s expansion, for small
x
, namely,
\log(1+x)=x+O(x^{2})
, we proceed with:

\displaystyle H(p-\varepsilon,p)

\displaystyle=(p-\varepsilon)\left(-\frac{\varepsilon}{p}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+(1-p+\varepsilon)\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{1-p}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon+\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})=O(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{(c+\boldsymbol{b})^{t}}\leq\frac{1}{c^{t}}e^{-nO(\varepsilon^{2})}+\frac{1}{(c+(p-\varepsilon)n)^{t}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{(c+\boldsymbol{b})^{t}}\leq\frac{1}{c^{t}}e^{-nO(\varepsilon^{2})}+\frac{1}{(c+np)^{t}}+O(\varepsilon/n^{t})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{c^{t}}e^{-O(n^{1/3})}+\frac{1}{(c+np)^{t}}+O(n^{-(t+1/3)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(c+np)^{t}}+O(n^{-(t+1/3)})

\displaystyle u_{k}=\frac{q_{i}r_{i}}{q_{0}r_{0}n_{0}+q_{1}r_{1}n_{1}}\leq[\overline{A}^{t}]_{\ell,k}\leq\frac{p_{i}}{r_{i}}=a_{\ell,k}

(89)

On the other hand, if
\ell,k\in\mathcal{C}_{i},\mathcal{C}_{j}
belong to different clusters, then

\displaystyle a_{\ell,k}=\frac{q_{i}}{r_{j}}\leq[\overline{A}^{t}]_{\ell,k}\leq\frac{q_{i}r_{i}}{q_{0}r_{0}n_{0}+q_{1}r_{1}n_{1}}=u_{k}

(90)

Since (
89
) and (
90
) are independent of
t
, the following upper and lower bounds hold for both public and private belief log-ratios. Referring to (
20
), we can write for an agent
k
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\delta\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(1-\delta)^{t}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{A}^{t+1}]_{\ell,k}d_{0}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-\delta\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(1-\delta)^{t}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{A}^{t+1}]_{\ell,k}d_{1}

\displaystyle=\delta\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{A}]_{\ell,k}d_{0}-\delta\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{A}]_{\ell,k}d_{1}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+\delta\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}(1-\delta)^{t}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{A}^{t+1}]_{\ell,k}d_{0}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-\delta\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}(1-\delta)^{t}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{A}^{t+1}]_{\ell,k}d_{1}

(91)

Due to Lemma
3
, we proceed with:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\delta\frac{p_{0}n_{0}}{r_{0}}d_{0}-\delta\frac{q_{1}n_{1}}{r_{0}}d_{1}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+\delta\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}(1-\delta)^{t}[\overline{A}^{t+1}]_{\ell,k}d_{0}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-\delta\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}(1-\delta)^{t}[\overline{A}^{t+1}]_{\ell,k}d_{1}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+O(\min\{n_{0},n_{1}\}^{-4/3})

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+\delta(1-\delta)^{T}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}\sum_{t=T}^{\infty}(1-\delta)^{t}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{A}^{T+t}-\overline{A}^{T+t}]_{\ell k}d_{0}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-\delta(1-\delta)^{T}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\sum_{t=T}^{\infty}(1-\delta)^{t}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{A}^{T+t}-\overline{A}^{T+t}]_{\ell k}d_{1}

(92)

Since both powers of
\overline{A}
and
\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{A}
are left stochastic, their difference is bounded. Thus, due to the presence of
O(\min\{n_{0},n_{1}\}^{-1/3})
, for each
\delta\in(0,1)
, we can choose
T\leq\min\{n_{0},n_{1}\}
large enough such that the terms that contain
(1-\delta)^{T}
can be neglected. We omit these terms for simplicity of notation.
Now, using (
89
) and (
90
), we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{k}

\displaystyle\geq\delta\frac{p_{0}n_{0}}{r_{0}}d_{0}-\delta\frac{q_{1}n_{1}}{r_{0}}d_{1}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+\delta(1-\delta)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(1-\delta)^{t}\frac{q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}}{q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}+q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}}d_{0}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-\delta(1-\delta)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(1-\delta)^{t}\frac{q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}}{q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}+q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}}d_{1}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+O(\min\{n_{0},n_{1}\}^{-4/3})

\displaystyle=\delta\frac{p_{0}n_{0}d_{0}-q_{1}n_{1}d_{1}}{r_{0}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+(1-\delta)\frac{q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}d_{0}-q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}d_{1}}{q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}+q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+O(\min\{n_{0},n_{1}\}^{-4/3})

(93)

Similarly, for agent
k\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{k}\leq

\displaystyle\;-\delta\frac{p_{1}n_{1}d_{1}-q_{0}n_{0}d_{0}}{r_{1}}

\displaystyle\;+(1-\delta)\frac{q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}d_{0}-q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}d_{1}}{q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}+q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}}

\displaystyle\;+O(\min\{n_{0},n_{1}\}^{-4/3})

(94)

The condition for
k\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
to converge to hypothesis
\theta_{0}
and for
k\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
to converge to hypothesis
\theta_{1}
amounts to the term
\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{k}
taking positive or negative signs correspondingly. Thus, for
k\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
and for sufficiently large
\min\{n_{0},n_{1}\}\gg 1
, it follows from (
D
) that we need
\delta
to satisfy:

\displaystyle\delta\frac{p_{0}n_{0}d_{0}-q_{1}n_{1}d_{1}}{r_{0}}+(1-\delta)\frac{q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}d_{0}-q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}d_{1}}{q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}+q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}}\geq 0

(95)

which transforms into

\displaystyle\delta\left(\frac{p_{0}n_{0}d_{0}-q_{1}n_{1}d_{1}}{r_{0}}+\frac{q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}d_{1}-q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}d_{0}}{q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}+q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\geq\frac{q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}d_{1}-q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}d_{0}}{q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}+q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}}

(96)

Taking sufficiently small
q_{i}
, the difference
p_{0}n_{0}d_{0}-q_{1}n_{1}d_{1}
(
47
) is positive. If the RHS of (
D
) is negative, then the inequality holds for any
\delta\in(0,1)
. If the RHS is positive, then (
D
) is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\delta\geq\tfrac{r_{0}(q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}d_{1}-q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}d_{0})}{(p_{0}n_{0}d_{0}-q_{1}n_{1}d_{1})(q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}+q_{1}n_{1}r_{1})+r_{0}(q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}d_{1}-q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}d_{0})}

(97)

In other words, the sign of
q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}d_{1}-q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}d_{0}
determines which hypothesis is prevalent in the network. Thus, if
\theta_{0}
is prevalent, then
q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}d_{1}-q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}d_{0}
is negative, and we do not need additional assumptions on
\delta
to converge to
\theta_{0}
. Otherwise, we need (
97
) to be satisfied.
Similarly, if
q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}d_{1}-q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}d_{0}>0
, then for the second cluster to converge to
\theta_{1}
, we need
\delta
to satisfy:

\displaystyle\delta\geq\tfrac{r_{1}(q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}d_{0}-q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}d_{1})}{(p_{1}n_{1}d_{1}-q_{0}n_{0}d_{0})(q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}+q_{1}n_{1}r_{1})+r_{1}(q_{0}n_{0}r_{0}d_{0}-q_{1}n_{1}r_{1}d_{1})}

(98)
