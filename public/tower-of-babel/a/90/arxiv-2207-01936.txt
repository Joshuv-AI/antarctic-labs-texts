A Calabi–Yau threefold coming from two black holes
Dino Festi, Bert van Geemen

Abstract
. In this paper, we show that a set of six square roots of homogeneous polynomials in four variables, related to a binary system of black holes studied by Stefan Weinzierl, is not rationalizable. We prove it by showing that the variety X X associated to the product of four of the six square roots is not unirational. In particular, we show that the smooth model of X X is a Calabi–Yau threefold.



1.
Introduction

Experiments in high energy physics often require predictions based on the computation of Feynman integrals, see for example
[
16
]
.
Unfortunately, an exact evaluation of these integrals is often unfeasible and so practitioners resort to numerical integration.
In order to achieve results of high enough precision in a reasonable time, it is useful to “solve” the square root of polynomials appearing in the arguments of these integrals.
Motivated by this problem, Marco Besier and the first author introduced the concept of
rationalizability
of a square root, cf.
[
2
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a squarefree polynomial.
We say that
\sqrt{f}
is rationalizable if there is a morphism of fields
\phi\colon\mathbb{C}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\to\mathbb{C}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

such that
\phi(z)=z
for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
\phi(f)=h^{2}
, for some
h\in\mathbb{C}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.

In the same article, they show that the rationalizability of a square root is equivalent to the unirationality of a certain algebraic variety associated to it,
[
2
, Theorem 1]
.
This equivalence is then used to give some criteria to decide rationalizability of given square roots.
The notion of rationalizability can be extended to sets of square roots of polynomials, also called
alphabets
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\}
be a set of squarefree polynomials in
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.
We say that
\mathcal{A}
is rationalizable if there is a morphism of fields
\phi\colon\mathbb{C}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\to\mathbb{C}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

such that
\phi(z)=z
for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
and for
i=1,\ldots,r
we have
\phi(f_{i})=h_{i}^{2}
, for some
h_{i}\in\mathbb{C}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.

The notion of rationalizability is further generalised to algebraic field extensions in
[
6
]
by Andreas Hochenegger and the first author, allowing for more results to decide rationalizability of given (sets of) polynomials.

Discussing these new developments, Stefan Weinzierl asked us about the rationalizability of an alphabet of six square roots of homogeneous polynomials in four variables related to the production of gravitational waves from a binary
system of two black holes
[
10
]
.
After some straightforward changes of variables and reordering of the roots, we write Weinzierl’s alphabet as the set
\mathcal{A}
of the square roots of the following six homogeneous polynomials in
R:=\mathbb{C}[x,y,z,t]
:

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle:=4x-z,

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle:=4y-z,

\displaystyle f_{3}

\displaystyle:=4x^{2}y-z(y-t)^{2},

\displaystyle f_{4}

\displaystyle:=4xy^{2}-z(x-t)^{2},

\displaystyle f_{5}

\displaystyle:=(x-y)^{2}-2(x+y)t+t^{2},

\displaystyle f_{6}

\displaystyle:=(x-y)^{2}-2(x+y)(z+t)+(z+t)^{2}.

Unfortunately, this set seems too complicated for the methods presented in the previous papers, and requires an ad-hoc study that we present in this paper.
As by-product, we also developed a strategy to
guess
(but not prove/disprove) the rationalizability of square roots in certain cases.

The rationalizability of
\mathcal{A}
would imply the rationalizability of the square root of every product of the polynomials
f_{i}
.
Conversely, if one can prove that the square root of one of the products is not rationalizable, then
\mathcal{A}
is not rationalizable.
Weinzierl has studied several products of two and three polynomials (see
2.1
),
proving the rationalizability of all of them but leaving the rationalizability of the whole alphabet open.
In these notes we are going to show that the set is not rationalizable.

Theorem 1.3
.

The square root
\sqrt{f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}f_{4}}
is not rationalizable and hence neither is the set
\mathcal{A}
.

The proof of
Theorem 1.3
is presented in
§3
.
In
§4
we propose a method to guess the rationalizability of square roots in certain cases.
This method provided a decisive hint towards the non-rationalizability of
\sqrt{f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}f_{4}}
, see
§4.6
.

2.
The double octic

As stated above, Stefan Weinzierl studied the rationalizability of several products of two and three polynomials, obtaining the result below.

Proposition 2.1

(Weinzierl, private communication)
.

The square roots of the following products are rationalizable:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}f_{1}f_{5},&f_{2}f_{5},&f_{1}f_{2}f_{3},\\
f_{1}f_{2}f_{4},&f_{1}f_{2}f_{5},&f_{1}f_{4}f_{3},\\
f_{1}f_{3}f_{5},&f_{2}f_{3}f_{5},&f_{2}f_{4}f_{5}.\end{array}

The proof of the proposition above is made easier by the relations between the polynomials, stated below.

Lemma 2.2
.

The involution of
R
defined by switching
x
and
y
,
exchanges
f_{1}
with
f_{2}
and
f_{3}
with
f_{4}
;
it fixes
f_{5}
and
f_{6}
.

The automorphism of
R
defined by

sends
f_{5}
to
f_{6}
and fixes
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
.

Proof.

Immediate by direct computations.
∎

The next natural step in the study of
\mathcal{A}
is then to consider the product of four polynomials.

2.1.
A product of four polynomials

We define the polynomial
f
of degree
8
as the product

(1)

f:=f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}f_{4}.

Let
\mathbb{P}
denote the weighted projective space
\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,1,4)
with coordinates
x,y,z,t,w
of weights
1,1,1,1,4
, respectively.
We define the variety
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{P}
as

(2)

\mathcal{X}\colon\;w^{2}=f\;.

Then the rationalizabilty of
\sqrt{f}
is equivalent to the unirationality of
\mathcal{X}
(cf.
[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
).

Notice that
\mathcal{X}
is a threefold given as a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
branched along the octic surface
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
defined by

(3)

\mathcal{V}\colon\;f=0\;.

If
\mathcal{V}
were smooth, then
\mathcal{X}
would also be smooth.
Moreover, a smooth double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
branched above an octic surface (a
double octic threefold
or
octic double solid
) is a Calabi–Yau threefold (cf.
4.1
), hence it is not unirational.
This would already answer the question about the rationalizabilty of
\mathcal{A}
.
Unfortunately,
\mathcal{V}
is (very) singular,
making hard to see whether there is a desingularization of
\mathcal{X}
that is a Calabi–Yau or not.
This is why we look for a more convenient birationally equivalent model.

2.2.
A more convenient model

Consider the Cremona-like transformation of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
given by

\sigma\colon\;\mathbb{P}^{3}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}\,,\quad(x:y:z:y)\mapsto(xz:yz:4xy:tz).

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}
be the threefold defined by

(4)

X\colon\;w^{2}=xy(x-z)(y-z)(yz-(x-t)^{2})(xz-(y-t)^{2})\;.

Notice that
X
is again a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
ramified above an octic surface, namely the surface

B\colon\;xy(x-z)(y-z)(yz-(x-t)^{2})(xz-(y-t)^{2})=0\;\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}\,.

Lemma 2.3
.

The following statements hold.

(1)

\sigma
is a rational involution; in particular it is a birational map.

(2)

\sigma
induces a birational morphism between
X
and
\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

It easy to see that
\sigma\circ\sigma
is the identity, proving the first statement.

The map
\sigma
sends the surface
\mathcal{V}
to the surface
B^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
defined by

B^{\prime}\colon\;x^{3}y^{3}z^{4}(x-z)(y-z)(yz-(x-t)^{2})(xz-(y-t)^{2})=0.

Let
X^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,1,8)
be the double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
branched above
B^{\prime}
and let
w^{\prime}
be the coordinate of weight
8
.
As
B^{\prime}
is birationally equivalent to
\mathcal{V}
(via
\sigma
), we have that
X^{\prime}
is birationally equivalent to
\mathcal{X}
;
on the other hand, the change of variable
w^{\prime}=xyz^{2}w
shows that
X

and
X^{\prime}
are also birationally equivalent,
concluding the proof of the second statement.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

\mathcal{X}
is unirational if and only if
X
is.

Proof.

By
2.3
,
X
and
\mathcal{X}
are birationally equivalent.
As unirationality is preserved by birational morphisms,
the statement follows.
∎

3.
Resolution of the singularities

In this section we show that by suitably resolving the singularities of
X
we obtain a Calabi–Yau threefold, hence
X
is not unirational.
We follow the procedure to desingularize
X
described in
[
4
, §2]
and, later, in
[

, §4.1]
.
Some concrete applications can be found in
[

[

Definition 3.1
.

Let
U
be a smooth threefold and

S\subset U
a surface.
We call
S

an arrangement
if locally it looks like an union of planes,
that is,
S
is the union of smooth irreducible surfaces
S_{1},...,S_{r}
such that:

(1)

for any
i\neq j
, the surfaces
S_{i},S_{j}
either intersect transversally along a smooth curve
C_{i,j}
or are disjoint;

(2)

the curves
C_{i,j},\,C_{k,l}
either intersect transversally or coincide.

If
U=\mathbb{P}^{3}
and the surfaces
S_{1},\dots,S_{r}
are of degree

such that
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r}=8
,
then
S
is called an
octic
arrangement.

An irreducible curve
C\subset S
is said to be
q
-
fold
if it lies on exactly
q
of the surfaces
S_{1},\dots,S_{r}
;
a point
P\in S
is said to be
p
-
fold
if it lies on exactly
p
of the surfaces
S_{1},\dots,S_{r}
.

intersect transversally in a line,

•

B_{4}^{(2)}
and
B_{5}^{(2)}
intersect in two disjoint lines.

Proof.

Let
E
be the exceptional divisor of
U^{(2)}
introduced by the blow up
\sigma^{(2)}
.
Then, reasoning as in the proof of
3.4
,
one can show that
B^{(2)}={\sigma^{(2)}}^{*}B^{(1)}-2E
and
{\sigma^{(2)}}^{*}K^{(1)}=K^{(2)}-E
.
As above, these two equalities and (
9
)
yield

B^{(2)}=-2K^{(2)}.

Again, the second statement follows immediately form the definition of blow up, noticing that the node of
B_{5}^{(1)}
lies on
B_{2,5}^{(1)}
,
and this node is also the intersection point of the two components of
B_{4,5}^{(1)}
.
∎

3.3.
The third blow up

We proceed as above, blowing up the strict transform of one of the components of

. Recall that

has two irreducible components meeting in two points.
The strict transform

stays reducible, with the two components still meeting in two points.

In order to solve this obstruction, we blow up one of the two components.

Definition 3.7
.

We define

\sigma^{(3)}\colon\;U^{(3)}\to U^{(2)}

as the blow up of

along
(B^{1}_{5,6})^{(2)}
, the strict transform of
(B^{1}_{5,6})^{(1)}
in

.

and

the strict transforms in

of

Lemma 3.8
.

The divisor

is linearly equivalent to
-2K^{(3)}
and it inherits all the singularities of

Proof.

As for

.
∎

3.4.
The fourth and fifth blow ups

We proceed our plan of resolving the singularities of
B
in order to obtain an arrangement.
In order to do so, we have to make sure that the strict transform of

and
B_{6}
, respectively

and

As
B_{3}\cap B_{6}
, resp.
B_{4}\cap B_{5}
, meet in two incident lines, and so do their strict transforms in

, it is enough to blow up one component of each intersection.
Therefore,
we define the blow up

\sigma^{(4)}\colon\;U^{(4)}\to U^{(3)}

of
U^{(3)}
along
(B^{1}_{3,6})^{(3)}
.
Subsequently,
we define

\sigma^{(5)}\colon\;U^{(5)}\to U^{(4)}

as the blow up of
U^{(4)}
along
(B^{1}_{4,5})^{(4)}
.
For
j=4,5
, we define

B^{(j)}
and
B_{i}^{(j)}
as the strict transforms in
U^{(j)}
of
B^{(j-1)}

and
B_{i}^{(j)}
, respectively.
Moreover, for
j=4,5
we denote by
K^{(j)}
the canonical divisor of

.

Lemma 3.9
.

The divisor
B^{(5)}
is linearly equivalent to
-2K^{(5)}
and it is an arrangement in the sense of
3.1
;
it only has 2-fold curves and at most 4-fold points.

Proof.

The first statement is proven as in the previous cases.

To see that
B^{(5)}
is an arrangement,
note that
B^{(5)}
is isomorphic to
B=B^{(0)}
outside the curves that have been blown up:
B_{1,6},B_{2,5},B_{5,6}^{1},B_{3,6}^{1},B_{4,5}^{1}
.
As noted in
3.2
, these are exactly the loci not meeting the conditions for

to be an arrangement.
Indeed,

is the union of six smooth surfaces:

•

B^{(5)}_{1},\ldots,B^{(5)}_{4}
are strict transforms of planes;

•

B^{(5)}_{5}
and
B^{(5)}_{6}
are the strict transforms of quadrics with a node blown up (also) along a line containing the node, hence they are smooth;

•

for every

, the intersection

is a smooth curve: either a line or a conic.

As

only has 2-fold curves and at most 4-fold points,
so does

.
Notice that some of the 4-fold points have not been affected by the blow ups, e.g.,
(0:0:0:1)
, hence
B^{(5)}
does have 4-fold points.
∎

3.5.
Further blow ups

Following
[
4
]
and
[
12
, §4.1]
, we proceed by blowing up the 4-fold points and then the double curves of
B^{(5)}\subset U^{(5)}
,
eventually obtaining a smooth surface
\tilde{B}:=B^{(n)}\subset U^{(n)}=:U
.
Let
\tilde{X}
be the double cover of
U
ramified above
\tilde{B}
.

For the convenience of the reader, we also recall the definition of a Calabi–Yau threefold:
a smooth threefold
Y
is a Calabi–Yau threefold if and only if its canonical divisor
K_{Y}
is trivial and
H^{i}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y})=0
for
i=1,2
;
see also
4.1
.

Proposition 3.10
.

The threefold
\tilde{X}
is smooth and birationally equivalent to
X
.
Moreover, it is a Calabi–Yau threefold.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=\sigma^{(n)}\circ\ldots\circ\sigma^{(1)}
be the composition of all the blow ups

\sigma\colon\;U=U^{(n)}\to U^{(n-1)}\cdots U^{(1)}\to U^{(0)}=\mathbb{P}^{3}\;.

Then
\sigma
is a birational map between
U
and
\mathbb{P}^{3}
,
inducing a birational map between
\tilde{B}
and
B
.
Therefore
\tilde{X}
and
X
are birationally equivalent.

It remains to prove that
\tilde{X}
is a Calabi–Yau threefold, i.e.
it is smooth, has trivial canonical divisor, and
H^{i}(\tilde{X},\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{X}})=0
for
i=1,2
.

As
\tilde{X}
is the double cover of
U
, which is smooth, branched above the smooth (reducible) surface
\tilde{B}
, we have that
\tilde{X}
is also smooth.

After every blow up we have that
B^{(k)}=-2K^{(k)}
, where
K^{(k)}
is the canonical divisor of
U^{(k)}
.
Hence

\tilde{B}=B^{(n)}=-2K^{(n)}=-2K_{U},

yielding
K_{\tilde{X}}=0
.

Let
\pi\colon\tilde{X}\to U
denote the double cover projection.
As
\pi
is finite, it is affine and hence
H^{i}(\tilde{X},\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{X}})\cong H^{i}(U,\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{X}})
for each
i\geq 0
.
As
\tilde{X}
is the double cover of
U
branched above
\tilde{B}=-2K_{U}
,
it follows that

\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{X}}=\mathcal{O}_{U}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{U}(K_{U}),

and hence

H^{i}(X,\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{X}})\cong H^{i}(U,\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{X}})\oplus H^{i}(U,\mathcal{O}_{\tilde{X}}(K_{U})).

By Serre duality we have that

H^{i}(U,\mathcal{O}_{U}(K_{U}))\cong H^{3-i}(U,\mathcal{O}_{U}).

So we are only left to compute
H^{i}(U,\mathcal{O}_{U})
for
i=1,2
.

To see this, consider the sequence of blow ups

U:=U^{(n)}\to U^{(n-1)}\to\cdots U^{(1)}\to U^{(0)}:=\mathbb{P}^{3},

where
\sigma^{(\ell)}\colon U^{(\ell)}\to U^{(\ell-1)}
is the blow up of
U^{(\ell-1)}
along a curve or a point, say
Z^{(\ell-1)}
or
P^{(\ell-1)}
, respectively.

If
\sigma^{(\ell)}
is the blow up along the curve
Z^{(\ell-1)}
,
then
\codim_{U^{(\ell-1)}}Z^{(\ell-1)}=2
.
From
[
15
, Theorem 7.31]
, it follows that

H^{i}(U^{(\ell)},\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{i}(U^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z})\oplus H^{i-2}(Z^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z}).

Then, for
i=1
, we have

H^{1}(U^{(\ell)},\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{1}(U^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z})\,;

for
i=2
,

H^{2}(U^{(\ell)},\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{2}(U^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z})\oplus H^{0}(Z^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{2}(U^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z})\oplus\mathbb{Z},

as
Z^{\ell-1}
is a curve
and hence
H^{0}(U^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}
.
Moreover,
H^{0}(U^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z})
is of type
(0,0)
, and hence its contribution to
H^{2}(U^{\ell},\mathbb{Z})
is of type
(1,1)
.

The above reasoning applies also if
\sigma^{(\ell)}
is the blow up along the point
P^{(\ell)}
.
In this case
\codim_{U^{(\ell)}}P^{(\ell)}=3
.
Then, from
[
15
, Theorem 7.31]
, it follows that

H^{i}(U^{(\ell)},\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{i}(U^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z})\oplus H^{i-2}(P^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z})\oplus H^{i-4}(P^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z}).

From this it follows that

\displaystyle H^{1}(U^{(\ell)},\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\cong H^{1}(U^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z})\,,

\displaystyle H^{2}(U^{(\ell)},\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\cong H^{2}(U^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z})\oplus\mathbb{Z}\,,

exactly as in the previous case.
Also in this case, the contribution to
H^{2}(U^{(\ell)},\mathbb{Z})
comes from
H^{0}(P^{(\ell-1)},\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}
and it is of type
(1,1)
.

For
\ell=1
we then have

\displaystyle H^{1}(U^{(1)},\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\cong H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{3},\mathbb{Z})=0\,,

\displaystyle H^{2}(U^{(1)},\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\cong H^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{3},\mathbb{Z})\oplus H^{0}(Z^{(1)},\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,.

As
H^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{3},\mathbb{Z})
is of type
(1,1)
, so is
H^{2}(U^{(1)},\mathbb{Z})
.
Then, proceeding by induction, we obtain

\displaystyle H^{1}(U,\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{3},\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle H^{2}(U,\mathbb{Z})=H^{2}(U^{(n)},\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}\,,

with
H^{2}(U,\mathbb{Z})
purely of type
(1,1)
.
From
H^{1}(U,\mathbb{Z})=0
it follows that
0=h^{1}(U)=h^{1,0}+h^{0,1}
and hence

0=h^{0,1}=\dim H^{1}(U,\mathcal{O}_{U}),

concluding that
H^{1}(U,\mathcal{O}_{U})=0
;
from
H^{2}(U,\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
, of type
(1,1)
, it follows that

n+1=h^{2}(U)=h^{2,0}(U)+h^{1,1}(U)+h^{0,2}=h^{2,0}(U)+(n+1)+h^{0,2},

yielding
h^{2,0}=h^{0,2}=0
.
In particular,
h^{0,2}=0
means that
H^{2}(U,\mathcal{O}_{U})=0
,
concluding the proof.

∎

We are now ready to prove the main theorem.

Proof of
Theorem 1.3
.

From
[
2
, Proposition 18]
, the square root
\sqrt{f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}f_{4}}
is rationalizable if and only if
Y
is unirational.
As
Y
is birationally equivalent to
X
, then the condition above is equivalent to the unirationality of
X
.
From
3.10
we know that
X
is birationally equivalent to
\tilde{X}

and that
\tilde{X}
is a Calabi–Yau threefold, hence it is not unirational.
Therefore
\sqrt{f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}f_{4}}
is non-rationalizable.

The non-rationalizability of
\mathcal{A}
follows
immediately from the first statement and
[
2
, Proposition 47]
.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

Notice that in order to prove the theorem, it would have been enough to show that
\tilde{X}
has a trivial, and in fact even just effective, canonical divisor. Indeed, this would already imply that
h^{3,0}=1\gneq 0
and hence
\tilde{X}
cannot be unirational.

4.
Guessing unirationality via point counting

In this section we show how to use modularity and point counting to
guess
the unirationality of a given threefold
Y
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, cf.
4.21
.
The technique reported below is very quick to employ but subject to assumptions that are often hard to check,
making this method not viable to give a proof.
A consistency check can be made under the assumption that the smooth model
\tilde{Y}
of
Y
is a K3 surface or a Calabi–Yau threefold, cf.
4.25
and
4.31
, respectively.

4.1.
Introduction to modularity of Calabi–Yau varieties

In what follows,
Y
will denote a (not necessarily smooth) complex projective variety of dimension
d
that can be defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.
In this subsection we will mostly follow
[
12
, Chapter 1]
.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that
Y
is a
Calabi–Yau
variety if it is smooth,
H^{i}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y})=0
for every
0<i<d
, and the canonical divisor
K_{Y}\sim 0
is trivial.

From the second condition and by Serre duality we get that

h^{0,0}(Y)=h^{d,0}(Y)=h^{0,d}(Y)=h^{d,d}(Y)=1,

where
h^{p,q}(Y):=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{q}(Y,\Omega^{p}_{Y})
is the
p,q
-th Hodge number of
Y
.

Example 4.2
.

A Calabi–Yau variety of dimension
d=1
(equipped with a rational point) is an
elliptic curve
.
It has the following Hodge diamond.

\begin{array}[]{ccc}&1&\\
1&&1\\
&1&\end{array}

Example 4.3
.

A Calabi–Yau variety of dimension
d=2
is a
K3 surface
.
It has the following Hodge diamond.

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}&&1&&\\
&0&&0&\\
1&&20&&1\\
&0&&0&\\
&&1&&\end{array}

Example 4.4
.

A Calabi–Yau variety of dimension
d=3
is an
Calabi–Yau threefold
, from now on abbreviated into
CY 3-fold
.
It has the following Hodge diamond.

\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}&&&1&&&\\
&&0&&0&&\\
&0&&h^{1,1}(Y)&&0&\\
1&&h^{2,1}(Y)&&h^{1,2}(Y)&&1\\
&0&&h^{2,2}(Y)&&0&\\
&&0&&0&&\\
&&&1&&&\\
\end{array}

Remark 4.5
.

CY manifolds are Kähler, hence
h^{1,1}(Y)>0
.
For CY 3-folds, all
2
-cycles are algebraic, hence
H^{2}(Y,\mathbb{Z})\cong\Pic Y
, that is,
h^{1,1}(Y)=h^{2}(Y)=\rho(Y)
, the
Picard number
of
Y
.

Definition 4.6
.

We say that a CY 3-fold
Y
is
rigid
if
h^{2,1}(Y)=0
,
otherwise we call it
\emph{non-rigid}
.

As
Y
is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, it admits a model over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 4.7
.

Let
Y
be a CY variety.
We say that a prime
p
is
of good reduction
for
Y
if
the reduction
\bar{Y}_{p}
of
Y
over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
is again a CY variety.
So, in particular,
\bar{Y}_{p}
is smooth.

Let
p
be a prime of good reduction for
Y
.
Then the Frobenius map
\Frob_{p}\colon\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}\to\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p},\;x\mapsto x^{p}
, acts on
\bar{Y}_{p}
;
it induces an action on the cohomology groups, in particular on
H_{\acute{e}t}^{d}(\bar{Y}_{p},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})\cong H_{\acute{e}t}^{d}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
,
where
\ell\neq p
is a prime.
We can then consider the trace of the pullback of
\Frob_{p}
on
H_{\acute{e}t}^{d}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
,
denoted by

\trace(\Frob_{p}^{*}\,|\,H_{\acute{e}t}^{d}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})).

Definition 4.8
.

We recall that the
L
-function
of
Y
(or
H^{d}(Y))
is a series

L(Y,s):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}(Y)}{k^{s}},

and one has

•

a_{1}(Y)=1
,

•

for every prime
p
of good reduction,
a_{p}=\trace(\Frob_{p}^{*}\,|\,H_{\acute{e}t}^{d}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))
.

Theorem 4.9

(Lefschetz trace formula)
.

Let
Y
be a smooth variety over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
and, for
r\geq 1
, let
\#Y_{p^{r}}
denote the number of points of
Y
over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
.
Then

\#Y_{p^{r}}=\sum_{i=0}^{2d}(-1)^{i}\trace((\Frob_{p}^{*})^{r}\,|\,H_{\acute{e}t}^{d}(\bar{Y},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})).

Theorem 4.10

(Weil–Deligne)
.

Let
Y
be a smooth complex variety that can be defined over
\mathbb{Q}
and let
p
be a prime of good reduction for
Y
.
Then, for every
i=0,...,2d
the quantity
\trace(\Frob_{p}^{*}\,|\,H_{\acute{e}t}^{i}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))
is an integer and

|\trace(\Frob_{p}^{*}\,|\,H_{\acute{e}t}^{i}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))|\leq h^{i}(Y)p^{i/2}.

So, in particular,

|a_{p}(Y)|\leq h^{d}(Y)p^{d/2}.

Using the knowledge of the Hodge diamond it is possible to give formulas to compute
a_{p}(Y)
when
Y
is a CY variety that can be defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, see below.

Example 4.11
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve,
p
a prime of good reduction and
\ell\neq p
another prime,
then

•

a_{1}(E)=1
,

•

a_{p}(E)=\trace(\Frob^{*}_{p}\,|\,H_{\acute{e}t}^{1}(E,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))
,

•

\#E_{p}=\sum_{i=0}^{2}(-1)^{i}\trace(\Frob^{*}_{p}\,|\,H_{\acute{e}t}^{i}(E,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))=1-a_{p}(E)+p
,

from which it follows that
a_{p}(E)=p+1-\#E_{p}
.

Example 4.12
.

Let
Y
be a complex K3 surface that can be defined over
\mathbb{Q}
,
p
a prime of good reduction and
\ell\neq p
another prime,
then

•

a_{1}(Y)=1
,

•

a_{p}(Y)=\trace(\Frob^{*}_{p}\,|\,H_{\acute{e}t}^{2}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))
,

•

\#Y_{p}=\sum_{i=0}^{4}(-1)^{i}\trace(\Frob^{*}_{p}\,|\,H_{\acute{e}t}^{i}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))=1-0+a_{p}(Y)-0+p^{2}
,

from which it follows that
a_{p}(Y)=\#Y_{p}-1-p^{2}
.
Notice that the same computations hold for any smooth surface with
h^{1}=h^{3}=0
.

Theorem 4.13

(Modularity of singular K3 surfaces
[
11
]
)
.

Let
S
be a K3 surface defined over
\mathbb{Q}
having maximal Picard number over
\overline{Q}
,
and let
q=e^{2\pi iz}
with
\im(z)>0
.
Then there exists a newform

f(q)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}q^{k}\in S^{\textrm{new}}_{3}(\Gamma(N))

of weight
3
with respect to a congruence subgroup
\Gamma\leq\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
N=-m^{2}\cdot\det\Pic S
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
(cf.
[
13
, Prop. 7.1]
), such that

a_{p}(Y)=b_{p}

for all the primes
p
of good reduction for
Y
.

Example 4.14
.

Let
Y
be a CY 3-fold that can be defined over
\mathbb{Q}
,

p
a prime of good reduction and
\ell\neq p
another prime,
then

•

a_{1}(Y)=1
,

•

a_{p}(Y)=\trace(\Frob^{*}_{p}\,|\,H_{\acute{e}t}^{3}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))
,

•

\displaystyle\#Y_{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{6}(-1)^{i}\trace(\Frob^{*}_{p}\,|\,H_{\acute{e}t}^{i}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))=

\displaystyle=1-0+\trace(\Frob_{p}^{*}\,|\,H^{2}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))-a_{p}(Y)+\trace(\Frob_{p}^{*}\,|\,H^{4}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))-0+p^{3}.

By definition of CY 3-fold, we have that
H^{2}(Y,\mathbb{C})=H^{1,1}(Y)
and hence
\Pic Y=H^{2}(Y,\mathbb{Z})
.
Then

\trace(\Frob_{p}^{*}\,|\,H^{2}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))=k_{p}(Y)p,

where
k_{p}(Y)\in\mathbb{Z}
and
|k_{p}(Y)|\leq h^{2}(Y)=h^{1,1}(Y)
.
The equality
k_{p}(Y)=h^{1,1}(Y)
holds if and only if
H_{\acute{e}t}^{2}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})=\Pic(Y_{\overline{Q}})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
is generated by divisors defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.
By Poincaré duality it follows that

\trace(\Frob_{p}^{*}\,|\,H^{4}(Y,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}))=k_{p}(Y)p^{2},

yielding

a_{p}(Y)=1+(p+p^{2})k_{p}(Y)+p^{3}-\#Y_{p}.

Theorem 4.15

(Modularity of rigid CY 3-folds
[
8
]
)
.

Let
Y
be a rigid CY 3-fold, and
q=e^{2\pi iz}
, with
\im(z)>0
.
Then, for some integer
N
, there exists a new form

f(q)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}q^{k}\in S_{4}^{\textrm{new}}(\Gamma_{0}(N))

of weight
4
with respect to
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, such that

b_{p}=a_{p}(Y)

for all the primes
p
of good reduction for
Y
.
The level
N
is only divisible by the primes of bad reduction of
Y
;
the maximal exponent
e_{p}
of a prime dividing
N
is bounded:

(1)

e_{p}\leq 2
for
p>3
;

(2)

e_{3}\leq 5
;

(3)

e_{2}\leq 8
.

Remark 4.16
.

A similar result should hold for every smooth complex variety
Y
of dimension
2k-1
with
h^{2k-1}(Y,\mathbb{C})=2
and
h^{2k-1,0}(Y)=1
if we replace
S_{4}^{\textrm{new}}
with

, see
[

, §2]
.
So in particular it should hold for possibly singular threefolds

such that its desingularization
\tilde{Z}
has

and

.

4.2.
Non-unirationality via point counting

Let
Y
be a complex variety, possibly non-smooth,
and let
\tilde{Y}\to Y
be a smooth resolution of singularities.

Assumption 4.17
.

The resolution
\tilde{Y}\to Y
is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
and
\tilde{Y}
is projective.

Definition 4.18
.

By assumption,
Y
has a non-singular projective model
\tilde{Y}
that can be defined over
\mathbb{Q}
and hence over
\mathbb{Z}
.
This means that there exists a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-scheme
\mathcal{Y}\to\Spec\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that its generic fiber
\mathcal{Y}_{\eta}
is isomorphic to
\tilde{Y}
.

We say that a prime
p
is of good reduction for
Y
if

\mathcal{Y}_{p}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a smooth variety, for any
q=p^{m}
.

Notice that
\mathcal{Y}_{p}
can be viewed as the reduction
\tilde{Y}_{p}
of
\tilde{Y}
modulo
p
.

Assumption 4.19
.

For every prime of good reduction
p
,

\#\mathcal{Y}_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\equiv\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\mod p\,.

Lemma 4.20
.

[
1
, Theorem 1.1]

Let
\mathcal{Y}\to\Spec\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be a smooth
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-scheme of dimension
d
.
Let
\mathcal{Y}_{\eta}
denote the fiber above
\eta=(0)\in\Spec\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and
\mathcal{Y}_{p}
the closed fiber above
(p)
.
If
h^{k,0}(\mathcal{Y}_{\eta})=0
for every
k=1,...,d
,
then
\#\mathcal{Y}_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\equiv 1\mod p
.

Using
4.20
, one can make a first guess about the unirationality of
Y
via point counting under
4.19
,
as shown by the following result.

Corollary 4.21
.

Let
Y
be defined as before and
let
p
be a prime of good reduction for
Y
.
Under
4.19
,
if
\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\not\equiv 1\mod p
,
then
Y
is not unirational.

Proof.

Assume that
Y
is unirational, then it follows that
\tilde{Y}
and hence
\mathcal{Y}_{\eta}
are unirational too.
In particular,
h^{k,0}(\mathcal{Y}_{\eta})=0
for
k=1,2,3
,
Then, as
p
is a prime of good reduction for
Y
,
c.f.
4.18
,
we have that
\#\mathcal{Y}_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\equiv 1\mod p
by
4.20
.
Moreover, by
4.19
, it holds that

\#\mathcal{Y}_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\equiv\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\mod p
.
So, in particular,

1\equiv\#\mathcal{Y}_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\equiv\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\not\equiv 1\mod p\;,

a contradiction.
This proves that
Y
is not unirational.
∎

We can apply the above tools to the more specific cases of K3 surfaces and Calabi–Yau threefolds,
for which, in some cases, we can use the modularity theorems to perform a consistency check.
In what follows, we will need the following definition.

Definition 4.22
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{N}
a positive integer. Then we define the sets:

\displaystyle\Sigma(B)

\displaystyle=\{p\textrm{ prime }:\;p\leq B\textrm{ and }p\textrm{ is of good reduction for }Y\}\,,\;and

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}(B)

\displaystyle=\{p\in\Sigma(B)\;:\;\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\not\equiv 1\mod p\}.

4.3.
Point counting for K3 surfaces

In this subsection we assume that
\dim Y=2
and
Y
satisfies assumptions
4.17
and
4.19
.
We denote by
\tilde{Y}
a fixed minimal smooth model of
Y
.

Lemma 4.23
.

If
\tilde{Y}
is a K3 surface,
then
the following congruence holds:

a_{p}(\tilde{Y})\equiv\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})-1\mod p

for every prime of good reduction
p
.

Proof.

As
\tilde{Y}
is a K3 surface and
p
is a prime of good reduction, we know that

a_{p}(\tilde{Y})=\#\mathcal{Y}_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})-1-p^{2},

see
4.12
.
By
4.19
,
this yields

a_{p}(\tilde{Y})\equiv\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})-1\mod p,

concluding the proof.
∎

If we suspect that

Y
is not unirational (for example by
4.21
)
and, moreover,
that its desingularization
\tilde{Y}
is a singular K3 surface, i.e. a K3 surface with maximal Picard number over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
,
we can use
4.23
to do a consistency check via point counting, instead of explicitly computing the cohomology of
\tilde{Y}
.
To do so, we consider the set of primes of good reduction
\Sigma(B)
, cf.
4.22
, and we compute the number of points
\#Y(\mathbb{F}_{p})
for
p\in\Sigma(B)
.
In the examples below, this set does not need to be very large,
we took
B=100
.
So, upon computing the number of points
\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
for a finite set
\Sigma(B)
of primes of good reduction,
we can look for levels
N
, congruence subgroups
\Gamma\leq\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and modular forms

f(q)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}q^{k}\in S_{3}^{\textrm{new}}(\Gamma(N))

such that
b_{p}\equiv\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})-1\mod p
for any
p\in\Sigma(B)
.

Assumption 4.24
.

For some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, subgroup
\Gamma\leq\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, and a positive integer
B
such that
\#\Sigma_{0}(B)\geq 10
,
there exists a newform
f\in S_{3}^{\textrm{new}}(\Gamma(N))
such that

b_{p}\equiv\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})-1\mod p

for every
p\in\Sigma(B)
.

Conjecture 4.25
.

If
Y
satisfies
4.24
,
then
Y
is birationally equivalent to a smooth surface
\tilde{Y}
with
h^{2,0}(\tilde{Y})\geq 1
.
In particular,
Y
is not unirational.

Remark 4.26
.

It is important to take
all
the primes up to a certain bound
B
:
given a surface
Y
such that
\tilde{Y}
is a singular K3 surface,
it might still happen that all for the primes
p
satisfying a certain congruence one has
\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\equiv 1\mod p
,
giving the false impression that
Y
is unirational.
In fact this happens for the surface
S
in
§4.5
:
in this case we find that
\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\equiv 1\mod p
for all the primes
p\equiv 5,7\mod 8
.
The same happens for the Fermat quartic and all the primes
p\equiv 3\mod 4
.

Remark 4.27
.

If
\tilde{Y}
is a K3 surface with Picard number
20
over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
,
then the level
N
of the form
f
determines the discriminant of
\Pic\tilde{Y}
modulo squares, cf.
Theorem 4.13
.
In this case we expect that
b_{p}\equiv\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})-1\mod p
for all primes
p
of good reduction.

If
\tilde{Y}
is a K3 surface with Picard number
20
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
,
then we expect even more:

\#\mathcal{Y}_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})=1+20p+b_{p}+p^{2}
.

4.4.
Point counting for Calabi–Yau threefolds

In this subsection we assume that
\dim Y=3
and
Y
satisfies assumptions
4.17
and
4.19
.
We denote by
\tilde{Y}
a fixed smooth model of
Y
.

Remark 4.28
.

If
Y
has only ordinary double points, then

4.19
is satisfied.
Indeed, an ordinary double point gets resolved after one blow up, introducing a
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
as exceptional divisor.
Then the claim follows from the computations in
[
12
, §1.6.1]
.

Lemma 4.29
.

If
\tilde{Y}
is a Calabi–Yau threefold,
then
the following congruence holds:

a_{p}(\tilde{Y})\equiv 1-\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\mod p

for every prime of good reduction
p
.

Proof.

As
\tilde{Y}
is a CY 3-fold, we know that

a_{p}(\tilde{Y})=1+(p+p^{2})k_{p}(\tilde{Y})+p^{3}-\#\mathcal{Y}_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p}),

cf.
4.14
.
By
4.19
,
this yields

\displaystyle a_{p}(\tilde{Y})

\displaystyle\equiv 1+(k_{p}(\tilde{Y})-\alpha_{p})p+(k_{p}(\tilde{Y})-\beta_{p})p^{2}+p^{3}-\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\mod p

\displaystyle\equiv 1-\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\mod p\,.

∎

Analogously to the previous section,

4.29
tells us that if we suspect that
Y
is not unirational (for example by
4.21
) and that, moreover, its desingularization
\tilde{Y}
is a rigid CY 3-fold,
then we can do a consistency check via point counting instead of explicitly computing the cohomology of
\tilde{Y}
.
To do so, it is enough to consider the primes in the set
\Sigma(B)
, cf.
4.22
, for some bound
B
.
Also in the example below, we take
B=100
.

We can then look for levels
N
and modular forms

f(q)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}q^{k}\in S_{4}^{\textrm{new}}(\Gamma_{0}(N))

such that
b_{p}\equiv 1-\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\mod p
for any
p\in\Sigma(B)
.
Note that in this case
the congruence subgroup is fixed,
and

Theorem 4.15
gives us a hint about the possible values for

,
making the search more practical.

Assumption 4.30
.

For some
N\in\mathbb{N}
and a positive integer

such that
\#\Sigma_{0}(B)\geq 10
,
there exists a newform

such that

b_{p}\equiv 1-\#Y_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\mod p

for every

.

Conjecture 4.31
.

If
Y
satisfies
4.30
,
then
Y
is birationally equivalent to a smooth threefold
\tilde{Y}
with
h^{3,0}(\tilde{Y})\geq 1
.
In particular,

is not unirational.

4.5.
Application to two surfaces

Let
Q\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
be the quartic surface defined by

Q\colon\;-x_{3}^{2}(x_{4}^{2}-x_{1}x_{2})+(x_{1}+x_{2})(x_{1}x_{4}^{2}+x_{2}x_{4}^{2}-4x_{1}x_{2}x_{4}+x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{2}^{2})=0,

and
S\subset\mathbb{P}(3,1,1,1)
the double sextic defined by

S\colon\;y_{0}^{2}=(y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}-2y_{3}^{2})(y_{1}^{2}y_{2}^{2}-y_{3}^{4}),

both studied in
[
7
]
.
The two surfaces have several singular points, so we cannot immediately conclude that they are birationally equivalent to K3 surfaces.
After counting points of the reductions modulo all the odd primes smaller than
100
,
one sees that
\#Q_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p}),\#S_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\not\equiv 1\mod p
for many primes
p

Moreover
Q
and
S
both satisfy
4.24
with

\displaystyle f(q)

\displaystyle=q-6q^{5}+9q^{9}+10q^{13}-30q^{17}+11q^{25}+42q^{29}-70q^{37}+O(q^{41})\in S_{3}^{\textrm{new}}(\Gamma_{1}(16)),

\displaystyle g(q)

\displaystyle=q-2q^{2}-2q^{3}+4q^{4}+4q^{6}-8q^{8}-5q^{9}+14q^{11}-8q^{12}+O(q^{14})\in S_{3}^{\textrm{new}}(\Gamma_{1}(8))

respectively.
Therefore we expect
Q
and
S
not to be unirational.

The prediction for
S
is confirmed by
[
7
, Theorem]
,
where it is shown that its smooth model
\tilde{S}
a K3 surface whose Picard lattice has rank
20
and discriminant
-8
.
In the same paper, the authors suggest that
Q
and
S
are birationally equivalent, which contradicts the heuristic associating them to two distinct modular forms, moreover of levels differing by
2
, which is not a square.
Below we show that the suggestion is indeed wrong.

Proposition 4.32
.

The surfaces
Q
and
S
are not birationally equivalent.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{Q}
and
\tilde{S}
denote the smooth models of
Q
and
S
, respectively.
It is easy to check that
Q
has only ADE singularities, and hence
\tilde{Q}
is a K3 surface.
The same holds for
S
and
\tilde{S}
.
Recall that if two K3 surfaces are birationally equivalent then they are isomorphic.
This means that if
Q
and
S
are birationally equivalent,
\tilde{Q}
and
\tilde{S}
are isomorphic;
in particular, they have isometric Picard lattices.

From
[
7
, Theorem]
we know that
\Pic\tilde{S}
has rank
20
and discriminant
-8
.
By projecting from a node of
Q
, we obtain a model of
Q
as double sextic.
It is then easy to find some
-2
-curves on this model.
Using these curves and the exceptional divisors we generate a lattice
\Lambda\subseteq\Pic\tilde{Q}
of rank
20
, discriminant
-16
and index
[\Pic\tilde{Q}:\Lambda]\leq 2
.
It follows that
\Pic\tilde{Q}
has rank
20
and discriminant either
-16
or
-4
.
In any case,
\det\Pic\tilde{Q}\not\cong\det\Pic\tilde{S}
,
showing that
Q
and
S
are not birationally equivalent.
∎

Note that the level of

is a square multiple of

, as predicted by

The same holds for
