The eigenvalue problem for the complex hessian operator on m m -pseudoconvex manifolds
Jianchun Chu, Yaxiong Liu, Nicholas McCleerey

Abstract
. We establish C 1 , 1 C^{1,1} -regularity and uniqueness of the first eigenfunction of the complex Hessian operator on strongly m m -pseudoconvex manifolds, along with a variational formula for the first eigenvalue. From these results, we derive a number of applications, including a bifurcation-type theorem and geometric bounds for the eigenvalue.

32W20 (Primary) 32U05, 35J65, 35J70 (Secondary)

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main Results

A classical observation of Lions
[
41
]
is that the real Monge-Ampère operator admits a well-defined first eigenvalue on a strictly convex domain with smooth boundary; somewhat surprisingly, this eigenvalue shares many properties with the first eigenvalue of a general linear elliptic operator, despite the fact that the Monge-Ampère operator is fully non-linear. Since then, eigenvalues of non-linear operators have generated lots of interest and been studied by many authors (see Subsection
1.3
below).

In this work, we will be concerned with the eigenvalue problem for certain complex operators, namely the
m
-Hessian operators, building off of a pair of recent papers by Badiane-Zeriahi
[
3
,
4
]
. Let us briefly recall some of their results.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a smoothly bounded, sufficiently pseudoconvex domain,
\omega
the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
m
an integer
1\leqslant m\leqslant n
, and
0\leqslant f\in L^{p}(\Omega)
for some
p>n
. Then Badiane-Zeriahi consider the problem of finding a number
\lambda_{1}>0
and a function
u_{1}\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{2}(\Omega)
solving:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{1})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=(-\lambda_{1}u_{1})^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u_{1}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\\
\inf_{\Omega}u_{1}=-1.\end{cases}

The number
\lambda_{1}:=\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)
is the first (twisted) eigenvalue of the complex
m
-Hessian operator
\mathrm{H}^{m}(u):=(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}/\omega^{n}
, and
u_{1}
is the corresponding (normalized) eigenfunction. As is well-known, when
m=n
,
\mathrm{H}^{m}=\mathrm{MA}
, the complex Monge-Ampère operator, and when
m=1
,
\mathrm{H}^{m}=\tfrac{1}{n}\Delta_{\omega}
, the
\omega
-Laplacian operator.

When
m=n
and
f>0
is smooth up to the boundary, Badiane-Zeriahi
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
show the existence of a unique pair
(\lambda_{1},u_{1})
solving (
1.1
); they moreover show that
u_{1}
is smooth on the interior of
\Omega
and
C^{1,\alpha}
up to the boundary, for all
0<\alpha<1
. This corresponds to the
C^{1,1}
boundary regularity shown in Lions for the eigenfunction of the real Monge-Ampère operator (both follow from
a priori
Laplacian bounds). Badiane-Zeriahi also prove a Rayleigh quotient formula for
\lambda_{1}
, in terms of the Monge-Ampère energy, similar to results of Tso
[
53
]
and Wang
[
54
]
for the real Monge-Ampère and
m
-Hessian equations (although the techniques in
[
3
,
4
]
are quite different from those in
[
53
,
54
]
).

When
\frac{n-1}{2}<m\leqslant n
, Badiane-Zeriahi again show the Rayleigh quotient formula for
\lambda_{1}

[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
(which implies that
\lambda_{1}
is unique in this case), and also the existence of a Hölder continuous eigenfunction
u_{1}
. Their techniques are unable to establish higher regularity or uniqueness in this case (this can be partially explained by noting that their methods apply to much more general right-hand sides, where higher regularity should not hold – see
[
4
, Theorem 1.2]
).

Our goal in this paper is to expand upon
[
3
,
4
]
, and answer some special cases of questions they pose. Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
\Omega
is an strongly
m
-pseudoconvex manifold
(
see Section
2
for a definition
)
,
\omega
is a Kähler metric on
\Omega
, and
0<f\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
. Then there exists some
\lambda_{1}:=\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)>0
and
u_{1}\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
(\lambda_{1},u_{1})
is the unique solution to (
1.1
).

Theorem
1.1
answers a question posed in
[
3
, page 3, line 7]
when
m=n
, and answers important special cases of
[
4
, Question 1 and 2]
when the right-hand side function is smooth. Note that we do not require any restriction on the value of
m
. Even further, we show that uniqueness holds for weak solutions which,
a priori
, are only assumed to be in the Cegrell class
\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
; see Theorem
5.2
. Our proof of uniqueness crucially uses the
C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega})
regularity of
u_{1}
.

Moreover, our results apply to abstract strongly
m
-pseudoconvex manifolds
\Omega
(see Section
2
for the precise definition we use). In general, there may be no embedding of
\Omega
into
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(e.g. if
\Omega
contains a proper closed subvariety of (positive) dimension
\leqslant n-m
). As such, we also obtain a generalization of Badiane-Zeriahi’s Rayleigh quotient formula for
\lambda_{1}
:

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose we are in the setting of Theorem
1.1
. Then the first eigenvalue of
\mathrm{H}^{m}
satisfies:

\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)^{m}=\inf\left\{\frac{E_{m}(u)}{I_{m}(u)}~\middle|~u\in\mathcal{E}_{m}^{1}(\Omega),\ u\neq 0\right\},

where we define:

E_{m}(u)=\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{\Omega}(-u)(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m},

and

I_{m}(u)=\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{\Omega}(-u)^{m+1}f^{m}\omega^{n}.

From our main results, we deduce several applications. The first is a sufficient condition to solve certain Hessian equations whose right-hand side depends on the unknown function, without having to assume the existence of a subsolution:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\psi(z,s)
be a smooth (strictly) positive function on
\overline{\Omega}\times(-\infty,0]
such that
\partial_{s}\psi\geqslant-\gamma_{0}>-\lambda_{1}
, where
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}(\Omega,1)
is the first eigenvalue of
\mathrm{H}^{m}
associated to
\omega^{n}
. Then the equation:

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\sigma_{m}(u)=\psi^{m}(z,u)&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

admits a unique solution
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
.

When
\partial_{s}\psi\geqslant 0
, this is a well-known result of Caffarelli-Kohn-Nirenberg-Spruck
[
13
]
; Theorem
1.3
weakens this to an essentially optimal condition. Again, the importance of results such as Theorem
1.3
comes from the fact that they do not require the existence of a subsolution – while the subsolution theory has proven to be highly successful in many applications (particularly to complex geometry), it is often quite difficult to verify the existence of a subsolution directly. As such, it is beneficial to avoid this assumption when possible.

In order to apply Theorem
1.3
effectively however, one needs control over
\lambda_{1}
. To this end, we present some geometric estimates for
\lambda_{1}
, including a lower bound for
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
(Theorem
6.5
) and a monotonicity statement (Theorem
6.2
) that generalizes
[
3
, Theorem 5.6]
. Our results are inspired by work of Le
[
38
]
, for the real Monge-Ampère eigenvalue.

The comparison of the first eigenvalue of Laplacian, as established by Cheng
[
17
]
, is the classical result on Riemannian manifolds, which provides an upper bound of the first eigenvalue of Laplacian.
The key element in its proof is the Laplacian comparison of the distance function under certain curvature condition. Using the Laplacian comparison together with Rayleigh quotient formula (Theorem
1.2
), we can derive an upper bound on manifolds with non-negative Ricci curvature:

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that the Ricci curvature
\mathrm{Ric}(\omega)
is non-negative. Let
R>0
be the largest number such that there exists a geodesic ball
B_{R}(p)\subseteq\Omega
with
\mathrm{dist}_{\omega}^{2}(z,p)
smooth and plurisubharmonic on
B_{R}(p)
. Then:

\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)\leqslant c(n,m)(\inf_{\Omega}f)^{-\frac{2m+1}{m+1}}R^{-\frac{2n}{m+1}-2}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)^{\frac{2n}{m+1}}\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)^{-\frac{1}{m+1}}\|f\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)}^{\frac{m}{m+1}}.

In particular, when
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the number
R
is the in-radius.

1.2.
Remarks on the Proofs

We now briefly discuss some of the main points in our proofs. As already mentioned, our paper builds directly on the general outlines in Badiane-Zeriahi
[
3
,
4
]
, which in turn build upon work of Lions
[
41
]
and Wang
[
54
]
for the real Monge-Ampère operator. We use the idea of
[
22
]
to control the largest eigenvalue of the real Hessian for solutions, which is what allows us to show that
u_{1}
is
C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega})
; in fact, we prove more general
C^{2}
-estimates up to the boundary, under the assumption of a subsolution and supersolution (Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
). Our boundary Hessian estimates differ from the previous work of Collins-Picard
[
23
]
in that we require our constants to be independent of a lower bound for
\psi
, which we accomplish by utilizing the supersolution and
m
-pseudoconvexity of
\Omega
.

Needing to produce a supersolution creates additional difficulties when we attempt to apply our Hessian estimates to the eigenvalue problem; in
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
, a crucial step in overcoming these difficulties is to use an explicit
a priori
gradient bound for solutions to the complex Monge-Ampère equation, due to Błocki
[
12
]
. The corresponding estimate for the complex
m
-Hessian equation is a long standing open problem. We overcome this difficulty by using instead the stability estimate of Dinew-Kołodziej
[
24
]
.

Finally, we are able to improve upon the methods in
[
4
]
by employing both a robust integration by parts argument due to Le
[
38
]
, and a modification of the classical Hopf lemma in our proof of uniqueness (Theorem
5.2
). The integration by parts argument only needs Dinew-Lu’s mixed Hessian inequality
[
25
]
, which allows us to avoid having to show any regularity of the minimizer of the Rayleigh quotient. Our Hopf lemma argument uses the
C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega})
regularity of
u_{1}
to show some extra positivity for the linearization of
\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(u_{1})
near the boundary of
\Omega
, where the operator is not uniform elliptic.

1.3.
Previous Works

Since Lions
[
41
]
, many authors have studied eigenfunctions for the real Hessian equations; general existence and uniqueness of the eigenfunction for smooth domains was established by Wang
[
54
]
. For the real Monge-Ampère equation, recently Savin
[
48
]
has established
C^{2}
regularity of the eigenfunction up to the boundary. Subsequently, Le-Savin
[
39
]
were able to extend this to smoothness up to the boundary. The results in
[
48
]
are based strongly on convex geometric techniques, which cannot be applied to the complex setting. For general bounded convex domains, Le
[
38
]
has recently established existence and uniqueness of the eigenfunction and eigenvalue for the real Monge-Ampère operator, along with several interesting applications.

The variational characterization of the eigenvalue presented here is originally due to Tso
[
53
]
, who proved the equivalent characterization for the real Hessian equations. He also studied a family of eigenvalue problems by varying the power of
-u
on the right-hand side. Very recently, generalizations of this family have been studied by Tong-Yau
[
52
]
and forthcoming work of Collins-Tong-Yau, with applications to the existence of complete Calabi-Yau metrics on complements of divisor.

In the complex setting, comparably less work has been. Aside from the works of Badiane-Zeriahi
[
3
,
4
]
, which we’ve already discussed at length, there are a pair of papers by Koutev-Ramadanov
[
36
,
37
]
, which discuss some applications of the Monge-Ampère eigenfunction, similar in spirit to Theorem
1.3
. There is also some work done in the case when
\Omega
is the unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{n}

[
36
,
40
,
56
,
44
]
(unlike the real case, there is apparently no general theory which shows that the eigenfunction is radially symmetric in this setting).

Finally, we mention a number of works on Sobolov-type inequalities for
m
-subharmonic and
m
-convex functions, which have been relevant to some of the previous works listed above. The basic version we need, which is recalled in Section
2
below, is due to Błocki
[
9
]
; the real analogues were shown in
[
54
,
53
]
. More powerful Sobolev inequalities for
m
-sh function have recently been shown by Zhou
[
57
]
, Åhag-Czyż
[
1
]
, and Wang-Zhou
[
55
]
.

1.4.
Further Directions

Our work presents many interesting follow-up questions. First, it is a natural question to ask about higher order boundary regularity of the eigenfunction. This is likely very difficult; the proof of boundary regularity for the real Monge-Ampère eigenfunctions
[
48
,
39
]
is quite involved, and, as already mentioned, seems to have little to offer the complex setting.

Second, it would be interesting to sharpen several of the results presented. For instance, it seems possible that one might be able to weaken
m
-pseudoconvexity of the boundary. We also believe that it might be possible to improve our geometric bounds for the eigenvalue so that it depends only on the
L^{n}(\Omega)
-norm of
f
.

Another potentially more difficult direction of inquiry would be to investigate if the supersolution condition in Theorem
4.1
could be removed – this would follow from an improved normal-normal estimate for the full hessian on the boundary. The difficulty is that the estimate would need to be independent of a lower bound for
\psi
, which is why the method of Collins-Picard
[
23
]
cannot be applied verbatim.

Finally, it would be interesting to study the Tso families in the complex setting as well
[
53
]
; it is known in the real setting that, as the family parameter varies, the solutions converge to the solution of the limiting parameter. Additionally, the “sub-critical” case enjoys better variational properties than the “critical case” (which is the one discussed in this paper). Taken together, this suggests an alternate approach to tackling some of the questions raised at the end of
[
4
]
.

2.
Background Results on Strongly
m
-Pseudoconvex Manifolds

2.1.
Definitions

Suppose that
\overline{\Omega}
is a compact Kähler manifold with (non-empty) smooth boundary; we write
\Omega
for the interior of
\overline{\Omega}
. Fix a Kähler metric
\omega
on
\overline{\Omega}
(by which we mean that, in local holomorphic coordinates centered at
z\in\partial\Omega
,
\omega
can always be extended as a smooth Kähler metric on an open neighborhood of
z
). We write
m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega):=m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega,\omega)
for the space of
\omega
-
m
-subharmonic functions on
\Omega
; since we regard
\omega
as fixed, we will often omit it in the notation/terminology.

As in the case of a domain, we write
\mathcal{E}_{m}^{1}(\Omega,\omega)\subset m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)
(or just
\mathcal{E}_{m}^{1}(\Omega)
) for the finite energy class of
m
-sh functions, which will be the largest space we consider in this paper. Recall that functions in
\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
have zero “boundary values” in a weak sense, which allows for, e.g., integration by parts – see Subsection
2.5
and Appendix
A
for definitions and further details. For
u\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
, the complex
m
-Hessian operator is well-defined. We shall variously write:

\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)=(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}

for this operator, which in general is only a non-negative Radon measure on
\Omega
. When
\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\omega^{n}
, we also define:

\sigma_{m}(u):=\binom{n}{m}\frac{\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)}{\omega^{n}}.

This is the case of course when
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
. Then
\sigma_{m}(u)\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
, and
\sigma_{m}(u)
can moreover be computed by evaluating the
m^{\text{th}}
elementary symmetric polynomial on the eigenvalues of
\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u
, with respect to
\omega
.

Definition 2.1
.

We shall say that
\Omega
is a
strongly
m
-pseudoconvex manifold
if there exists a negative, strictly
m
-sh function
\rho\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\rho-\varepsilon\omega
is an
m
-positive form for some
\varepsilon>0
,
\{\rho=0\}=\partial\Omega
, and
d\rho\not=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

This definition is a generalization of the
[
45
, Definition 2.3]
. As usual, we understand
d\rho|_{\partial\Omega}
by looking at local smooth extensions of
\rho
in coordinate balls.

From a potential theoretic viewpoint, there seems to be only minor differences between strongly
m
-pseudoconvex manifolds and bounded strongly
m
-pseudoconvex domains (which we always mean to be open subsets of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
). There are however some important geometric differences, unlike in the more familiar plurisubharmonic case – for instance, strongly
m
-pseudoconvex manifolds can contain non-trivial closed complex submanifolds (see e.g.
[
21
]
).

The rest of this section is devoted to recalling some background results we will need. Before doing this, we state three definitions which we will use repeatedly throughout the paper.

Definition 2.2
.

Suppose that
\psi
is a smooth function on
\overline{\Omega}\times(-\infty,0]
, and write
z\in\Omega
. We consider the PDE:

(2.1)

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=\psi^{m}(z,u)\omega^{n}&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Then we say that:

(1)

A function
\underline{u}\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
is a
subsolution
to (
2.1
) if:

(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\underline{u})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\geqslant\psi^{m}(z,\underline{u})\omega^{n}\ \text{in $\Omega$}.

(2)

A function
\underline{u}\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
is a
strict subsolution
to (
2.1
) if:

(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\underline{u})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\geqslant(\psi(z,\underline{u})+\varepsilon_{0})^{m}\omega^{n}\ \text{in $\Omega$}.

for some constant
\varepsilon_{0}>0
.

(3)

A function
\underline{u}\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
is a
supersolution
to (
2.1
) if:

(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\overline{u})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\leqslant\psi^{m}(z,\overline{u})\omega^{n}\ \text{in $\Omega$}.

Since our
\psi
is general, one cannot conclude that an arbitrary subsolution
\underline{u}
lies below an arbitrary supersolution
\overline{u}
.

Note that being a supersolution (in the above sense) imposes some additional regularity on
\overline{u}
, as it forces
\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)
is to be absolutely continuous with respect to
\omega^{n}
.

2.2.
Dirichlet problem

We recall the following important result of Collins-Picard
[
23
]
on solvability of the Dirichlet problem for compact Hermitian manifolds when the right-hand side does not depend on the solution.

Theorem 2.3

(Theorem 1.1 of
[
23
]
)
.

Let
(\Omega,\omega)
be a compact Hermitian manifold with smooth boundary,
\chi\in\Gamma_{m}(\overline{\Omega},\omega)
,
f\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
a (strictly) positive function, and
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
. Suppose that there exists
\underline{u}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
\chi+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\underline{u}\in\Gamma_{m}(\overline{\Omega},\omega)
and

\begin{cases}(\chi+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\underline{u})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\geqslant f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
\underline{u}=\varphi&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Then there exists a unique
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
\chi+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u\in\Gamma_{m}(\overline{\Omega},\omega)
and

\begin{cases}(\chi+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=\varphi&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

See
[
23
]
for definitions. We have the following corollary:

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
(\Omega,\omega)
be a strongly
m
-pseudoconvex manifold,
f\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
a (strictly) positive function, and
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
. Then there exists a unique
u\in m\mathrm{SH}({\Omega},\omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that

(2.2)

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=\varphi&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Choose
\chi=\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\rho
, extend
\varphi
to
\overline{\Omega}
smoothly, and set
\underline{u}=\varphi+A\rho
. When
A
is sufficiently large,
\underline{u}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfies
\chi+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\underline{u}\in\Gamma_{m}(\overline{\Omega},\omega)
and

\begin{cases}(\chi+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\underline{u})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\geqslant f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
\underline{u}=\varphi&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

By Theorem
2.3
, there exists a unique
v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
\chi+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v\in\Gamma_{m}(\overline{\Omega},\omega)
and

\begin{cases}(\chi+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
v=\varphi&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Then
u=\rho+v\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega,\omega)
is the unique solution of (
2.2
).
∎

2.3.
First eigenvalue of the complex Laplacian

It will be crucial to consider the solution to the linearized eigenvalue problem in our proofs of the main theorems. We briefly recall the results we shall need:

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
(\overline{\Omega},\alpha)
be a compact Hermitian manifold with smooth boundary,
\Delta_{\alpha}=\alpha^{i\overline{j}}\partial_{i}\partial_{\overline{j}}
the associated complex Laplacian operator, and
f\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
a (strictly) positive function. Then:

(1)

There exists a unique pair
(\mu_{1},v_{1})
such that
\mu_{1}
is a positive constant,
v_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfies
\inf_{\Omega}v_{1}=-1
, and

\begin{cases}\Delta_{\alpha}v_{1}=-\mu_{1}v_{1}f&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
v_{1}=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$},\\
v_{1}<0&\text{in $\Omega$}.\end{cases}

(2)

The constant
\mu_{1}
can be characterized as follows:

\begin{split}\mu_{1}=\sup\{\mu~|~&\exists\,v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}),\text{ with }\Delta_{\alpha}v\geqslant-\mu vf\text{ in }\Omega,\\
&\text{$v=0$ on $\partial\Omega$, and $v<0$ in $\Omega$}\}.\end{split}

(3)

If
v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
is such that
\Delta_{\alpha}v\geqslant-\mu_{1}vf
in
\Omega
,
v=0
on
\partial\Omega
, and
v<0
in
\Omega
, then there exists a constant
c
such that
v=cv_{1}
.

Proof.

If
\Omega
is a domain in the Euclidean space, then the result is well-known, see e.g.
[
27
, Section 6.5.2, Theorem 3]
. The same argument also works for general manifolds with smooth boundary; we provide a short sketch.

Let
k=n+2
and consider the Banach space
X=H^{k}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
We define the linear, compact operator
A:X\rightarrow X
given by setting
A(w)=u
, where
u
is the unique solution to

\begin{cases}\Delta_{\alpha}u=-wf&\text{in }\Omega\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Define also the cone
\mathcal{C}:=\{u\in X~|~u\geqslant 0\text{ in }\Omega\}
.

Fix a non-zero
w\in\mathcal{C}
, and define
v:=A(w)
. By the strong maximum principle and the Hopf Lemma, there exists a constant
\theta>0
such that
\theta v\geqslant w
in
\Omega
.

Let
\varepsilon,\eta>0
and consider the equation:

(2.3)

\displaystyle u=\eta A(u+\varepsilon w),

where
u\in\mathcal{C}
is the unknown. Observe that the existence of a solution to (
2.3
) implies that
\eta\leqslant\theta
; indeed, by the comparison principle, we have:

u\,\geqslant\,\eta\varepsilon A(w)\,\geqslant\,\frac{\eta}{\theta}\varepsilon w.

We can iterate this:

u\,\geqslant\,\eta\varepsilon A(u)\,\geqslant\,\frac{\eta^{2}}{\theta}\varepsilon A(w)\,\geqslant\,\frac{\eta^{2}}{\theta^{2}}\varepsilon w.

Repeating this indefinitely then gives a contradiction unless
\eta\leqslant\theta
.

Consider now the subspace

\displaystyle S_{\varepsilon}:=\{u\in\mathcal{C}~|~\text{there exists }0\leqslant\eta\leqslant 2\theta\text{ such that }u=\eta A(u+\varepsilon w)\},

which is unbounded in
X
(if not, by Schaefer’s fixed point theorem
[
27
, Section 9.2.2, Theorem 4]
, there exists a solution to (
2.3
) with
\eta
replaced by
2\theta
, a contradiction).

Hence, we may find sequences
0\leqslant\eta_{\varepsilon}\leqslant 2\theta
and
v_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{C}
with
\|v_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}\geqslant 1
and
v_{\varepsilon}=\eta_{\varepsilon}A(v_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon w)
. Consider the renormalized sequence

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{v_{\varepsilon}}{\|v_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}}.

By the compactness of the operator
A
, there exists a subsequence
\varepsilon_{k}\rightarrow 0
such that

\eta_{\varepsilon_{k}}\rightarrow\eta
and
u_{\varepsilon_{k}}\rightarrow u
in
X
.
Then we have
\|u\|_{X}=1
.
Since
u_{\varepsilon}=\eta_{\varepsilon}A(u_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varepsilon w}{\|v_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}})
, by taking the limit, we have
u=\eta A(u)
with
\eta>0
.
By standard elliptic regularity theory, we know that
u
is smooth. This proves (1) with
\mu_{1}=\eta
and
v_{1}=u
.

Similarly, (3) follows from the proof of
[
27
, Section 6.5.2, Theorem 3]
.

We now show (2). Write
\mu_{0}:=\sup I
, where

\begin{split}I=\{\mu~|~&\exists\,v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})\text{ with }\Delta_{\alpha}v\geqslant-\mu vf\text{ in }\Omega,\\
&\text{$v=0$ on $\partial\Omega$ and $v<0$ in $\Omega$}\}.\end{split}

By (1), we have
\mu_{1}\in I
. Then
\mu_{1}\leqslant\mu_{0}
.
If
v<0
and
\Delta_{\alpha}v\geqslant-\mu vf
for some
\mu
, then we notice that

\mu\leqslant\inf_{\Omega}\left(-\frac{\Delta_{\alpha}v}{vf}\right).

Thus, we have

\mu_{0}=\sup_{v}\left(\inf_{\Omega}\left(-\frac{\Delta_{\alpha}v}{vf}\right)\right),

where
v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
with
v<0
in
\Omega
and
v|_{\partial\Omega}=0
.

If
\mu_{0}>\mu_{1}
, there exists a
v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
with
v<0
in
\Omega
and
v|_{\partial\Omega}=0
such that

\inf_{\Omega}\left(-\frac{\Delta_{\alpha}v}{vf}\right)>\mu_{1},

i.e.
\Delta_{\alpha}v>-\mu_{1}vf
. Then by (3), there exists a constant
c
such that
v=cv_{1}
.
Then we have

\mu_{1}<\inf_{\Omega}\left(-\frac{\Delta_{\alpha}v}{vf}\right)=\inf_{\Omega}\left(-\frac{c\Delta v_{1}}{cv_{1}f}\right)=\mu_{1}.

This is a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\alpha
be a Hermitian metric on
\overline{\Omega}
and
(\mu_{1},v_{1})
the unique eigenvalue of
\Delta_{\alpha}
, normalized to
\inf_{\Omega}v_{1}=-1
. Suppose that
c\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
is such that
c<\mu_{1}
. If
w\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfies

\begin{cases}\Delta_{\alpha}w\geqslant-cw&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
w=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$},\end{cases}

then
w\leqslant 0
.

Proof.

Define the constant
\theta
by

\theta=\inf\{\gamma~|~w\leqslant\gamma(-v_{1})\ \text{in $\Omega$}\}.

Then
w+\theta v_{1}\leqslant 0
in
\Omega
. It suffices to show
\theta\leqslant 0
. Supposing for the sake of a contradiction that
\theta>0
, we compute

(2.4)

\Delta_{\alpha}(w+\theta v_{1})\geqslant-cw-\theta\mu_{1}v_{1}>-c(w+\theta v_{1})\ \ \text{in $\Omega$}.

Let
C>0
be such that
-C\leqslant c
. Since
w+\theta v_{1}\leqslant 0
, (
2.4
) implies that:

(\Delta_{\alpha}-C)(w+\theta v_{1})\geqslant 0.

Using the strong maximum principle, either
w+\theta v_{1}\equiv 0
or
w+\theta v_{1}<0
in
\Omega
. By (
2.4
), the former case cannot happen. In the latter case, by the Hopf lemma, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

w+\theta v_{1}\leqslant\varepsilon v_{1}\ \ \text{in $\Omega$},

contradicting the definition of
\theta
.
∎

2.4.
Stability estimate

As mentioned in the introduction, we will make critical usage of the stability theorem of Dinew-Kołodziej
[
24
]
; it can be shown for strongly
m
-pseudoconvex manifolds in the same way as for domains, so we only sketch the proof. We need the following standard
L^{\infty}
bound for smooth solutions:

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
(\overline{\Omega},\omega)
be a compact strongly
m
-pseudoconvex Kähler manifold with smooth boundary, and let
p>\frac{n}{m}
. Suppose that
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega,\omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfies

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=f\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=\varphi&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

for some (strictly) positive function
f\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
. Then there exists a constant depending only on
p
,
m
,
n
and
(\overline{\Omega},\omega)
such that

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}+C\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{1/m}.

Theorem
2.7
can be proved exactly as for domains, by using Dinew-Kołodziej’s volume-capacity estimate
[
24
]
.

Theorem 2.8

(Theorem 2.8 of
[
24
]
)
.

For
i=1,2
, suppose that
u_{i}\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega,\omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfies

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{i})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=f_{i}^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u_{i}=\varphi_{i}&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

for some (strictly) positive function
f_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and
\varphi_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
. Then for any
p>n/m
, there exists a constant
C
depending only on
p
and
(\Omega,\omega)
such that

\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}+C\|f_{1}^{m}-f_{2}^{m}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{1/m}.

Proof.

By Corollary
2.4
, there exists
w_{\varepsilon}\in m\mathrm{SH}(\overline{\Omega},\omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that:

\begin{cases}\mathrm{H}^{m}(w_{\varepsilon})=\sqrt{(f_{1}^{m}-f_{2}^{m})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
w_{\varepsilon}=-\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Then the domination principle (Proposition
A.2
), shows that
u_{1}+w_{\varepsilon}\leqslant u_{2}
, since:

\begin{cases}\mathrm{H}^{m}(u_{1}+w_{\varepsilon})\geqslant\mathrm{H}^{m}(u_{1})+\mathrm{H}^{m}(w_{\varepsilon})\geqslant\mathrm{H}^{m}(u_{2})&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u_{1}+w_{\varepsilon}\leqslant u_{2}&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

We conclude that:

u_{1}-u_{2}\leqslant\left\lVert w_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}+C\left\lVert\sqrt{(f_{1}^{m}-f_{2}^{m})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{1/m},

by Theorem
2.7
. Letting
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and switching the roles of
u_{1},u_{2}
finishes.
∎

An immediate corollary of this and the continuity of the complex Hessian operator is the existence and uniqueness of a continuous solution
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
to the equation:

\begin{cases}\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)=f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u=\varphi&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

when
0\leqslant f\in L^{p}(\Omega)
with
p>n
and
\varphi\in C^{0}(\partial\Omega)
.

2.5.
Finite Energy Classes

We recall some facts about finite energy classes of
m
-subharmonic functions, see e.g.
[
6
,
15
,
42
]
. These results are standard for strongly
m
-pseudoconvex domains in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and the adaptations to the manifold case are straightforward; we have collected some further details in the Appendix.

If
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, then the Chern-Levine-Nirenburg inequalities imply that:

\mathrm{H}^{m}(u):=(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}

is a well-defined Radon measure on
\Omega
.

Definition 2.9
.

We define the class
\mathcal{E}^{0}_{m}(\Omega)
to be the class of
m
-subharmonic functions
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
which additionally satisfy
\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)<\infty
and

\lim_{x\rightarrow z}u(x)=0\text{ for all }z\in\partial\Omega.

One can show that if
u_{j}\in\mathcal{E}^{0}_{m}(\Omega)
decrease to
u\in\mathcal{E}^{0}_{m}(\Omega)
as
j\rightarrow\infty
, then the measures
\mathrm{H}^{m}(u_{j})
weakly converge to
\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)
, following
[
7
,
15
,
42
]
.

We then define the finite energy class
\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
as follows:

Definition 2.10
.

Suppose
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)
. Then we say that
u\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
, the finite energy class, if there exists a decreasing sequence
u_{j}\in\mathcal{E}^{0}_{m}(\Omega)
such that:

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}u_{j}=u\ \text{ and }\ \lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}(-u_{j})\mathrm{H}^{m}(u_{j})=:(m+1)E_{m}(u)<\infty.

If
u\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
and
u_{j}\in\mathcal{E}^{0}_{m}(\Omega)
is any sequence decreasing to
u
, then it is standard
[
42
]
to show that:

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}E_{m}(u_{j})=E_{m}(u).

Moreover, the equality
E_{m}(u)=\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{\Omega}(-u)\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)
holds.

We conclude with some energy estimates which play an important role in Section
5
. The first is due to Błocki
[
9
]
:

Proposition 2.11
.

Suppose that
w\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega),v\in\mathcal{E}^{0}_{m}(\Omega)
. Then:

(2.5)

\int_{\Omega}(-w)^{m+1}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\leqslant(m+1)!\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}^{m}E_{m}(w);

Proof.

The proof follows from an integration by parts; indeed, for any
1\leqslant p\leqslant m
, we have:

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}(-w)^{p+1}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}w)^{m-p}\wedge(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v)^{p}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\\
={}&\int_{\Omega}v\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}(-w)^{p+1}\wedge(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}w)^{m-p}\wedge(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v)^{p-1}\wedge\omega^{n-m}.\end{split}

Since
\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}(-w)^{p+1}\geqslant-(p+1)(-w)^{p}\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}w,
we can apply induction to conclude the argument. The validity of these operations can be shown in the same way as the domain case, see e.g.
[
42
]
.
∎

3.
A Priori Estimates

In this section, we prove
a priori
estimates for the complex
m
-Hessian equation, which will play an important role in the next section.

Let
\psi
be a smooth (strictly) positive function on
\overline{\Omega}\times(-\infty,0]
, and suppose that
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfies

(3.1)

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=\psi^{m}(z,v)\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$},\end{cases}

for some
v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
. Suppose that there exist
\underline{u}\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and
w\in\mathrm{SH}_{1}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that

(3.2)

\underline{u}\leqslant u\leqslant w<0\ \text{in \text{$\Omega$}},\quad\underline{u}=u=w=0\ \text{on \text{$\partial\Omega$}}.

The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

Under the above assumptions, there exists a constant
C
depending only on
\|\psi\|_{C^{2}}
,
\|\underline{u}\|_{C^{2}}
,
\|w\|_{C^{1}}
and
(\overline{\Omega},\omega)
such that:

(3.3)

\sup_{\Omega}|u|+\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\partial u|\leqslant C

and

(3.4)

\sup_{\overline{\Omega}}|\nabla^{2}u|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}v|+C\sup_{\Omega}|\partial u|^{2}+C\sup_{\Omega}|\partial v|^{2}+C.

For convenience, by replacing
\psi^{m}
with
\binom{n}{m}^{-1}\psi^{m}
, we can rewrite (
3.1
) as:

(3.5)

\begin{cases}\sigma_{m}(u)=\psi^{m}(z,v)&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

It it clear that (
3.3
) follows (more or less) immediately from (
3.2
). We thus must show (
3.4
), which we do in two parts. We first show the interior Hessian estimates in Proposition
3.2
; we then show the boundary Hessian estimates in Proposition
3.7
. For notational convenience, we shall say that a constant is uniform if it depends only on
\|\psi\|_{C^{2}}
,
\|\underline{u}\|_{C^{2}}
,
\|w\|_{C^{1}}
and
(\overline{\Omega},\omega)
; we will always write
C
for such a constant, whose exact value may change from line to line.

3.1.
Interior Hessian estimate

Proposition 3.2
.

Under the same assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, there exists a uniform constant
C
such that

(3.6)

\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}u|\leqslant C\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\nabla^{2}u|+\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}v|+C\sup_{\Omega}|\partial u|^{2}+C\sup_{\Omega}|\partial v|^{2}+C.

Proof.

We shall use the techniques of
[
22
,
20
,
19
]
. Let
g
be the Riemannian metric corresponding to
\omega
, and write
\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\lambda_{2n}(\nabla^{2}u)
for the eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}u
with respect to
g
(not to be confused with
\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)
, which is not used in this proof). Denote the Laplace-Beltrami operator of
g
by
\Delta_{\mathbb{R}}
and write

\Delta_{\omega}u=\frac{n\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u\wedge\omega^{n-1}}{\omega^{n}}.

Using Maclaurin’s inequality and (
3.5
),

\sum_{\alpha=1}^{2n}\lambda_{\alpha}(\nabla^{2}u)=\Delta_{\mathbb{R}}u=2\Delta_{\omega}u\geqslant 2n\left[\binom{n}{m}^{-1}\sigma_{m}(u)\right]^{\frac{1}{m}}=2n\binom{n}{m}^{-\frac{1}{m}}\psi>0.

This shows
\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)>0
and

(3.7)

|\nabla^{2}u|\leqslant C_{n}\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)

for some constant
C_{n}
depending only on
n
. Set

K=\sup_{\Omega}|\partial u|^{2}+\sup_{\Omega}|\partial v|^{2}+1,\ \ N=\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}u|+1,\ \ \theta=\nabla^{2}u+Ng.

We consider the quantity

Q=\log\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)+\xi(|\theta|^{2})+\eta(|\partial u|^{2})+A\rho,

where

\xi(s)=-\frac{1}{6}\log(5N^{2}-s),\ \ \eta(s)=-\frac{1}{6}\log(2K-s),

and
A
is a uniform constant to be determined later.

The first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)
is a continuous function in
\overline{\Omega}
. Assume that
x_{0}
is a maximum point of
Q
. By the definition of
Q
and the fact that
\rho\leqslant 0
, we see that

(3.8)

Q(x_{0})\leqslant\log\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)(x_{0})-\frac{1}{6}\log N^{2}-\frac{1}{6}\log K.

We assume without loss of generality that
\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}u|\geqslant 1
(i.e.
N\geqslant 2
), and let
y_{0}
be the maximum point of
|\nabla^{2}u|
. Then (
3.7
) shows

\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)(y_{0})\geqslant\frac{|\nabla^{2}u(y_{0})|}{C_{n}}=\frac{N-1}{C_{n}}\geqslant\frac{N}{2C_{n}}

and so

(3.9)

Q(y_{0})\geqslant\log\frac{N}{2C_{n}}-\frac{1}{6}\log(5N^{2})-\frac{1}{6}\log(2K)-A\|\rho\|_{C^{0}}.

Combining (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) with
Q(y_{0})\leqslant Q(x_{0})
, we see that

(3.10)

N\leqslant e^{C_{0}A}\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)(x_{0})

for some uniform constant
C_{0}
. Hence, to prove (
3.6
), it suffices to prove

(3.11)

\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)(x_{0})\leqslant\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\nabla^{2}u|+\frac{1}{2e^{C_{0}A}}\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}v|+C_{A}K

for some uniform constant
C_{A}
depending on
A
. In the following argument, we always assume that
x_{0}\notin\partial\Omega
(otherwise there is nothing to show) and that

(3.12)

\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)(x_{0})>\frac{1}{2e^{C_{0}A}}\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}v|+K.

Choose a holomorphic normal coordinate system
(U,\{z^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
for
g
centered at
x_{0}
. Writing
z^{i}=x^{2i-1}+\sqrt{-1}x^{2i}
, we have that
(U,\{x^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{2n})
is a real coordinate system near
x_{0}
. After rotating the coordinates, we assume that

u_{i\overline{j}}=\delta_{ij}u_{i\overline{i}},\ \ u_{1\overline{1}}\geqslant u_{2\overline{2}}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant u_{n\overline{n}},\ \ \text{at $x_{0}$}.

Let
\Lambda_{1}\geqslant\Lambda_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\Lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u
with respect to
\omega
. Then
\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(u)=\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(\Lambda)
. Let
F^{i\overline{j}}
and
F^{i\overline{j},k\overline{l}}
be the first and second derivatives of
\sigma_{m}^{1/m}
, i.e.

F^{i\overline{j}}=\frac{\partial\sigma_{m}^{1/m}}{\partial u_{i\overline{j}}},\ \ F^{i\overline{j},k\overline{l}}=\frac{\partial^{2}\sigma_{m}^{1/m}}{\partial u_{i\overline{j}}\partial u_{k\overline{l}}}.

Then at
x_{0}
, we have (see e.g.
[
26
,
49
]
)

F^{i\overline{j}}=\delta_{ij}F^{i\overline{i}}=\frac{\partial\sigma_{m}^{1/m}}{\partial\Lambda_{i}},\ \ F^{1\overline{1}}\leqslant F^{2\overline{2}}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant F^{n\overline{n}}

and (see e.g.
[
2
,
29
,
49
]
)

F^{i\overline{j},k\overline{l}}=\frac{\partial^{2}\sigma_{m}^{1/m}}{\partial\Lambda_{i}\partial\Lambda_{k}}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\frac{F^{i\overline{i}}-F^{j\overline{j}}}{\Lambda_{i}-\Lambda_{j}}(1-\delta_{ij})\delta_{il}\delta_{jk},

where the quotient is interpreted as a limit if
\Lambda_{i}=\Lambda_{j}
.

When
\lambda_{1}(x_{0})=\lambda_{2}(x_{0})
, the function
\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)
might not be smooth. To avoid such this, we apply a standard perturbation argument (see e.g.
[
51
,
50
,
22
]
). For
1\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 2n
, let
V_{\alpha}=V_{\alpha}^{\beta}\partial_{\beta}
be the
g
-unit eigenvector of
\lambda_{\alpha}
at
x_{0}
, and extend
V_{\alpha}
to a vector field in
U
by taking the components
V_{\alpha}^{\beta}
to be constant. Define

\Phi=\Phi_{\beta}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\otimes dx^{\beta}=g^{\alpha\gamma}\left[u_{\gamma\beta}-(\delta_{\gamma\beta}-V_{1}^{\gamma}V_{1}^{\beta})\right]\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\otimes dx^{\beta}.

Let
\lambda_{1}(\Phi)\geqslant\lambda_{2}(\Phi)\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\lambda_{2n}(\Phi)
be the eigenvalues of
\Phi
with respect to
g
. It is clear that
\lambda_{1}(\Phi)(x_{0})>\lambda_{2}(\Phi)(x_{0})
,
\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)(x_{0})=\lambda_{1}(\Phi)(x_{0})
and
\lambda_{1}(\nabla^{2}u)\geqslant\lambda_{1}(\Phi)
in
U
. Define the perturbed quantity in
U
:

\hat{Q}=\log\lambda_{1}(\Phi)+\xi(|\theta|^{2})+\eta(|\partial u|^{2})+A\rho.

Then
\hat{Q}
is smooth at
x_{0}
and
x_{0}
is still a maximum point of
\hat{Q}
. For convenience, we denote
\lambda_{\alpha}(\Phi)
by
\lambda_{\alpha}
in the following argument.

Lemma 3.3
.

At
x_{0}
, we have

(3.13)

\frac{u_{V_{1}V_{1}i}}{\lambda_{1}}=-\xi^{\prime}(|\theta|^{2})_{i}-\eta^{\prime}(|\partial u|^{2})_{i}-A\rho_{i}

and

(3.14)

\begin{split}0\geqslant{}&G_{1}+G_{2}+G_{3}-B+\xi^{\prime\prime}F^{i\overline{i}}|(|\theta|^{2})_{i}|^{2}+\eta^{\prime\prime}F^{i\overline{i}}|(|\partial u|^{2})_{i}|^{2}\\

Proof.

Using (

3.1.1.

We now need to deal with the term

Lemma 3.4
.

For (1), it is clear that

Using

For (2), by (

) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

Substituting this into (

), dropping the non-negative terms

(

Using (2) and

∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Choosing

, then at

Proof.

For

(1). Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality again,

\begin{split}B_{32}\leqslant{}&(1-\varepsilon)(1+\gamma)\sum_{i\in I}\frac{2F^{i\overline{i}}}{\lambda_{1}^{2}}\left(\sum_{q\notin I}\frac{(u_{q\overline{q}}-u_{i\overline{i}})|\nu_{q}|^{2}}{F^{i\overline{i}}-F^{q\overline{q}}}\right)\left(\sum_{q\notin I}\frac{(F^{i\overline{i}}-F^{q\overline{q}})|u_{i\overline{q}V_{1}}|^{2}}{u_{q\overline{q}}-u_{i\overline{i}}}\right).\end{split}

For

, using Lemma

\begin{split}&\sum_{q\notin I}\frac{(u_{q\overline{q}}-u_{i\overline{i}})|\nu_{q}|^{2}}{F^{i\overline{i}}-F^{q\overline{q}}}\leqslant\sum_{q\notin I}\frac{(u_{q\overline{q}}-u_{i\overline{i}})|\nu_{q}|^{2}}{(1-A^{-4})F^{i\overline{i}}}\\
={}&\frac{1}{(1-A^{-4})F^{i\overline{i}}}\left(\sum_{q}u_{q\overline{q}}|\nu_{q}|^{2}-\sum_{q\in I}u_{q\overline{q}}|\nu_{q}|^{2}-u_{i\overline{i}}\sum_{q\notin I}|\nu_{q}|^{2}\right)\\
\leqslant{}&\frac{1}{(1-A^{-4})F^{i\overline{i}}}\left(u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}+C_{A}K\right).\end{split}

Then

B_{32}\leqslant\frac{(1-\varepsilon)(1+\gamma)}{(1-A^{-4})\lambda_{1}}\left(u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}+C_{A}K\right)\cdot\frac{2}{\lambda_{1}}\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{q\notin I}\frac{(F^{i\overline{i}}-F^{q\overline{q}})|u_{i\overline{q}V_{1}}|^{2}}{u_{q\overline{q}}-u_{i\overline{i}}}.

Using
F^{i\overline{q},q\overline{i}}=\frac{F^{i\overline{i}}-F^{q\overline{q}}}{u_{i\overline{i}}-u_{q\overline{q}}}
, we have

\frac{2}{\lambda_{1}}\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{q\notin I}\frac{(F^{i\overline{i}}-F^{q\overline{q}})|u_{i\overline{q}V_{1}}|^{2}}{u_{q\overline{q}}-u_{i\overline{i}}}=-\frac{2}{\lambda_{1}}\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{q\notin I}F^{i\overline{q},q\overline{i}}|u_{i\overline{q}V_{1}}|^{2}\leqslant G_{2}

and so

(3.26)

\begin{split}B_{32}\leqslant{}&\frac{(1-\varepsilon)(1+\gamma)}{(1-A^{-4})\lambda_{1}}\left(u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}+C_{A}K\right)G_{2}\\
={}&\frac{1+\gamma}{(1+\varepsilon)\lambda_{1}}\left(u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}+C_{A}K\right)G_{2},\end{split}

where we used
\varepsilon=A^{-2}
in the second line. By Lemma
3.4
(3), increasing
C_{A}
if needed, we assume that
u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}+C_{A}K
is positive. To show (
3.24
), we split the argument into two cases.

Case 1.

\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}+\sum_{\alpha>1}\lambda_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha}^{2})>\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}(u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}+C_{A}K)>0
.

Using (
3.25

B_{31}+B_{32}\leqslant\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2\lambda_{1}}\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\left(\lambda_{1}-\sum_{\alpha>1}\lambda_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha}^{2}\right)G_{1}+\frac{1+\gamma}{2\lambda_{1}}\left(\lambda_{1}+\sum_{\alpha>1}\lambda_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha}^{2}\right)G_{2}.

Since
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
, after choosing

\gamma=\frac{\lambda_{1}-\sum_{\alpha>1}\lambda_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}+\sum_{\alpha>1}\lambda_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha}^{2}}>0,

we obtain (

).

Case 2.

\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}+\sum_{\alpha>1}\lambda_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha}^{2})\leqslant\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}(u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}+C_{A}K)
.

By the definitions of
W_{1}
and
\mu_{\alpha}

(3.27)

u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}=\frac{1}{2}(u_{V_{1}V_{1}}+u_{JV_{1}JV_{1}})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda_{1}+\sum_{\alpha>1}\lambda_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha}^{2}\right).

Combining this with the assumption of Case 2,

u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}\leqslant\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}(u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}+C_{A}K).

Then
u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}\leqslant\varepsilon^{-1}C_{A}K
and so

(3.28)

u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}+C_{A}K\leqslant\frac{C_{A}K}{\varepsilon}.

Using (
3.27
) and Lemma

(2),

\lambda_{1}+\sum_{\alpha>1}\lambda_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha}^{2}=2u_{W_{1}\overline{W_{1}}}\geqslant-C_{A}K,

which implies

(3.29)

\lambda_{1}-\sum_{\alpha>1}\lambda_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha}^{2}\leqslant 2\lambda_{1}+C_{A}K\leqslant 2(1+\varepsilon^{2})\lambda_{1},

provided that
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\varepsilon^{-1}C_{A}K
. Hence, combining (
3.25
), (
3.26
), (
3.28
), (
3.29
) and choosing

,

\begin{split}B_{31}+B_{32}\leqslant{}&(1-\varepsilon)(1+\varepsilon^{2})^{2}G_{1}+\frac{C_{A}K}{\varepsilon^{3}\lambda_{1}}G_{2}\leqslant G_{1}+\frac{C_{A}K}{\varepsilon^{3}\lambda_{1}}G_{2}.\end{split}

This implies (
3.24
) as long as

.
∎

Completion of the proof

Substituting Lemma

3.2.
Boundary Hessian estimate

Proposition 3.7
.

Under the same assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, there exists a uniform constant
C
such that

(3.30)

\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\nabla^{2}u|\leqslant C\sup_{\Omega}|\partial u|^{2}+C\sup_{\Omega}|\partial v|^{2}+C.

Proof.

We mostly follow Collins-Picard
[
23
]
, with the following differences. First, as already remarked in the introduction, we by-pass their complicated boundary normal-normal derivative estimate by using the “supersolution”
w
and
m
-pseudoconvexity of the boundary. Secondly, we require the constant in (
3.30
) to be independent of
\inf_{\Omega}\psi
, which requires some further modifications throughout the argument.

We divide the proof into three steps.

Step 1:
Setup and tangent-tangent derivatives.

We start with some setup. Fix a point
p\in\partial\Omega
, and suppose that
z=(z^{1},\ldots,z^{n})
is a coordinate system centered at
p
, valid on some small ball
B
. Let:

T^{1,0}_{p}\partial\Omega:=\{V\in T^{1,0}_{p}\Omega\ |\ V(\rho)=0\};

then, after a coordinate change, we can assume that
\omega_{i\overline{j}}(0)=\delta_{i\overline{j}}
,

T^{1,0}_{p}\partial\Omega=\mathrm{Span}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{n-1}}\right\},

and that
x^{n}>0
in
\Omega\cap B
, where we write
z^{i}=x^{i}+\sqrt{-1}y^{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. It follows that, after rescaling
\rho
by a positive constant if necessary, we have:

\rho(z)=-x_{n}+O(|z|^{2})

in
B
. Define
t=(t^{1},\ldots,t^{2n-1})
by
t^{\alpha}=y^{\alpha}
for
1\leqslant\alpha\leqslant n
and
t^{\alpha}=x^{\alpha-n}
for
n+1\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 2n-1
. From (
3.2
), we obtain

|\partial_{t^{\alpha}}\partial_{t^{\beta}}u(0)|=|-\partial_{x^{n}}u(0)\partial_{t^{\alpha}}\partial_{t^{\beta}}\rho(0)|\leqslant C,\ \ \text{for $1\leqslant\alpha,\beta\leqslant 2n-1$}.

Step 2:
Tangent-normal derivatives.

The main task is to deal with the tangent-normal derivatives, which we bound using a barrier argument. We follow
[
23
, Section 4]
to construct the barrier function. Define

K:=\sup_{\Omega}|\partial u|^{2}+\sup_{\Omega}|\partial v|^{2}+1,\quad\mathcal{G}:=\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\omega_{p\overline{q}}.

Let
\{e_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n}
be an orthonormal frame for
T^{1,0}\Omega
such that the
\{e_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n}
are tangential to the level sets of
\rho
and
e_{a}(0)=\partial_{a}
for
1\leqslant a\leqslant n-1
(see the start of
[
23
, Section 4.5]
for the construction). Write
e_{a}=e_{a}^{i}\partial_{i}
and set:

U=AK^{1/2}V+BK^{1/2}|z|^{2}-\frac{1}{K^{1/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial_{y^{i}}(u-\underline{u}))^{2}-\frac{1}{K^{1/2}}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\left\lvert\nabla_{a}(u-\underline{u})\right\rvert^{2},

where we define the function
V
by:

V:=u-\underline{u}+c_{0}d-N_{0}d^{2},

with
d
the
\omega
-distance function to
\partial\Omega
and
A,B,c_{0},N_{0}>0
uniform constants to be chosen later.

Fix
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,2n-1\}
and consider the tangential operators:

T_{\alpha}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\alpha}}-\frac{\rho_{t^{\alpha}}}{\rho_{x^{n}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{n}}.

It is clear that
T_{\alpha}(u-\underline{u})=0
on
\partial\Omega\cap B_{\delta}
. We claim that

(3.31)

\begin{cases}\ U(0)=0&\\[2.84526pt]
\ U\geqslant\left\lvert T_{\alpha}(u-\underline{u})\right\rvert&\mbox{on $\partial(\Omega\cap B_{\delta})$}\\[2.84526pt]
\ \sigma^{p\overline{q}}_{k}\partial_{p}\partial_{\overline{q}}U\leqslant-\left\lvert\sigma^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{\overline{q}}T_{\alpha}(u-\underline{u})\right\rvert&\mbox{in $\Omega\cap B_{\delta}$}.\end{cases}

Accepting the claim, we show how to conclude the argument. First, by the maximum principle, the claim implies that

U\pm T_{\alpha}(u-\underline{u})\geqslant 0\ \ \text{in $\Omega\cap B_{\delta}$}.

Since
U(0)=T_{\alpha}(u-\underline{u})(0)=0
, we have that

\partial_{x^{n}}(U\pm T_{\alpha}(u-\underline{u}))(0)\geqslant 0,

and hence

\left\lvert\partial_{x^{n}}\partial_{t^{\alpha}}u(0)\right\rvert\leqslant\left|AK^{1/2}\partial_{x^{n}}V(0)\right|+\left|\left(\partial_{x^{n}}\frac{\rho_{t^{\alpha}}}{\rho_{x^{n}}}\right)(0)\cdot\partial_{x^{n}}(u-\underline{u})(0)\right|+\left|\partial_{x^{n}}\partial_{t^{\alpha}}\underline{u}(0)\right|.

Combining this with
\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\partial u|\leqslant C
, we obtain the tangent-normal derivative estimate:

\left\lvert\partial_{x^{n}}\partial_{t^{\alpha}}u(0)\right\rvert\leqslant CK^{1/2}.

We are left to verify the properties of
U
listed in (
3.31
). The first property is immediate. For the second, we separate
\partial(\Omega\cap B_{\delta})
into the two pieces
\partial\Omega\cap B_{\delta}
and
\Omega\cap\partial B_{\delta}
. On the first piece, we have:

T_{\alpha}(u-\underline{u})=\nabla_{a}(u-\underline{u})=0\text{ and }(\partial_{y^{i}}(u-\underline{u}))^{2}\leqslant C|z|^{2},

giving:

U-\left\lvert T_{\alpha}(u-\underline{u})\right\rvert\geqslant AK^{1/2}V+BK^{1/2}\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2}-C|z|^{2}\geqslant AK^{1/2}V,

for
B\gg 1
. On the second piece, we have
|z|=\delta
(which we choose later), giving:

U-\left\lvert T_{\alpha}(u-\underline{u})\right\rvert\geqslant AK^{1/2}V+BK^{1/2}\delta^{2}-CK^{1/2}\geqslant AK^{1/2}V,

for
B\gg 1
. Then the second property in (
3.31
) follows from the above and
V\geqslant 0
(see Lemma
3.8
below).

Lemma 3.8

(Lemma 4.2 of
[
23
]
)
.

There exist uniform constants
\delta,c_{0},N_{0},\tau>0
such that

\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{\overline{q}}V\leqslant-\tau(\psi^{m-1}+\mathcal{G})\ \text{and}\ V\geqslant 0\ \text{in $\Omega\cap B_{\delta}$}.

Proof.

Recall
\sigma_{m}=\psi^{m}
and
\mathcal{G}=\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\omega_{p\overline{q}}
.
The first three equations in
[
23
, page 1654]
imply

\begin{split}\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{\overline{q}}(u-\underline{u})\leqslant{}&-\tau\mathcal{G}+\sigma_{m}\big(\log\sigma_{m}+C(\underline{u})\big)\\
\leqslant{}&-\tau\mathcal{G}+\psi^{m-1}\big(\psi\log\psi^{m}+C(\underline{u})\psi\big)\\
\leqslant{}&-\tau\mathcal{G}+C(\underline{u},\psi)\psi^{m-1},\end{split}

where we used the fact that
x\log x^{m}\leqslant C(m,D)
for
x\in(0,D]
. Then
[
23

becomes

\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{\overline{q}}V\leqslant-\frac{3\tau}{4}\mathcal{G}-\frac{N_{0}}{2}\sigma_{m}^{1\overline{1}}+C\psi^{m-1}.

By the first three equations in

-\frac{\tau}{2}\mathcal{G}-\frac{N_{0}}{2}\sigma_{m}^{1\overline{1}}\leqslant-m\sigma_{m}^{\frac{m-1}{m}}\left(\frac{N_{0}\tau^{m-1}}{2^{m}}\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}=-\frac{m\tau^{\frac{m-1}{m}}N_{0}^{\frac{1}{m}}}{2}\psi^{m-1}.

Choosing
N\gg 1
and renaming
\tau

\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{\overline{q}}V\leqslant-\tau(\psi^{m-1}+\mathcal{G})
. The part

[
23
, Lemma 4.2]
.
∎

We now show that we can choose
A\gg 1
such that the third property in (
3.31
) holds. We use
\mathcal{E}
to denote a term satisfying

|\mathcal{E}|\leqslant CK^{1/2}\mathcal{G}+C\sum_{i}\sigma_{m-1}(\Lambda|i)|\Lambda_{i}|+C\psi^{m-1}.

Lemma 3.9

((4.16), (4.26), (4.31) of
[
23
]
)
.

The following inequalities hold:

\bullet\ \left\lvert\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{\overline{q}}T_{\alpha}(u-\underline{u})\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{1}{K^{1/2}}\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{y^{n}}(u-\underline{u})\cdot\partial_{\overline{q}}\partial_{y^{n}}(u-\underline{u})+\mathcal{E},

\bullet-\frac{1}{K^{1/2}}\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{\overline{q}}(\partial_{y^{i}}(u-\underline{u}))^{2}\leqslant-\frac{2}{K^{1/2}}\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{y^{i}}(u-\underline{u})\cdot\partial_{\overline{q}}\partial_{y^{i}}(u-\underline{u})+\mathcal{E},

\begin{split}\bullet-\frac{1}{K^{1/2}}\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{\overline{q}}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}&\left\lvert\nabla_{a}(u-\underline{u})\right\rvert^{2}\leqslant-\frac{1}{2nK^{1/2}}\sum_{i\not=r}\sigma_{m-1}(\Lambda|i)\Lambda_{i}^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{K^{1/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{y^{i}}(u-\underline{u})\cdot\partial_{\overline{q}}\partial_{y^{i}}(u-\underline{u})+\mathcal{E},\end{split}

where

is some chosen index such that
1/n\leqslant|e^{r}_{n}|^{2}\leqslant 1

Proof.

It suffices to show that for
1\leqslant\beta\leqslant 2n
,

(3.32)

\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{\overline{q}}\partial_{\beta}u=\mathcal{E}.

Given this, the lemma follows from the arguments of
[
23
, (4.16), (4.26), (4.31)]
. By the definitions of
t^{\beta}
and
z^{i}
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\beta}}=\begin{cases}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\beta-n}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}^{\beta-n}}&\mbox{if $1\leqslant\beta\leqslant n$},\\[2.84526pt]
\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{-1}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\beta-n}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}^{\beta-n}}\right)&\mbox{if $n+1\leqslant\beta\leqslant 2n$}.\end{cases}

Since both cases are similar, we only deal with the first case. Applying
\nabla_{\beta}
to the equation
\sigma_{m}=\psi^{m}
,

\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\nabla_{\beta}\nabla_{p}\nabla_{\overline{q}}u=\nabla_{\beta}\psi^{m}.

Writing

and using
[
23

,

(3.33)

\begin{split}\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{\overline{q}}\partial_{\beta}u={}&\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\left(\nabla_{\beta}\nabla_{p}\nabla_{\overline{q}}u+\Gamma_{ip}^{r}u_{r\overline{q}}+\Gamma_{\overline{i}\overline{q}}^{\overline{r}}u_{p\overline{r}}\right)\\
={}&m\psi^{m-1}(\partial_{\beta}\psi+\partial_{v}\psi\cdot\partial_{\beta}v)+\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\Gamma_{ip}^{r}u_{r\overline{q}}+\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\Gamma_{\overline{i}\overline{q}}^{\overline{r}}u_{p\overline{r}}.\end{split}

Maclaurin’s inequality (see (

\mathcal{G}=\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\omega_{p\overline{q}}=(n-m+1)\sigma_{m-1}\geqslant m\sigma_{m}^{\frac{m-1}{m}}=m\psi^{m-1},

which implies

(3.34)

|m\psi^{m-1}(\partial_{\beta}\psi+\partial_{v}\psi\cdot\partial_{\beta}v)|\leqslant CK^{1/2}\mathcal{G}.

Using
[

,

(3.35)

\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\Gamma_{ip}^{r}u_{r\overline{q}}+\sigma_{m}^{p\overline{q}}\Gamma_{\overline{i}\overline{q}}^{\overline{r}}u_{p\overline{r}}=\mathcal{E}.

Substituting (

).
∎

Lemma 3.10

By Lemma

\begin{split}&\sigma^{p\overline{q}}\partial_{p}\partial_{\overline{q}}(U\pm T_{\alpha}(u-\underline{u}))\\[5.69054pt]

Step 3
: The normal-normal derivative.

We are left to show that the normal-normal derivative of

-u_{x_{n}}(0)\geqslant-w_{x_{n}}(0)\geqslant C^{-1}

u_{i\overline{j}}(0)=-u_{x_{n}}(0)\rho_{i\overline{j}}(0),\ \ \text{for $1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n-1$}.

where

4.
Existence theorem

In this section, we solve the following Dirichlet problem for the complex Hessian equation when the right-hand side is decreasing in
u
, by using an iteration argument:

(4.1)

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=\psi^{m}(z,u)\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\psi
be a smooth (strictly) positive function on
\overline{\Omega}\times(-\infty,0]
such that
\psi
is decreasing in the second variable. Suppose that there exist a subsolution
\underline{u}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and a supersolution
\overline{u}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
\underline{u}<\overline{u}<0
in
\Omega
. Then there exists
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that

(4.2)

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=\psi^{m}(z,u)\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$},\end{cases}

and:

(4.3)

\|u\|_{C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})}\leqslant C,

where
C
is a constant depending only on
(\overline{\Omega},\omega)
,
\|\psi\|_{C^{2}}
,
\|\underline{u}\|_{C^{2}}
, and the choice of a non-zero function
h\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
with
0\leqslant h\leqslant\psi(z,\overline{u})
.

The role of the function
h
is to smooth out the non-zero function
\psi(z,\overline{u})
, so the estimates are independent of the
C^{2}
-norm of
\overline{u}
.

Proof.

We define a sequence of functions
\{u_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
inductively. Set
u_{0}=\underline{u}
. By Corollary
2.4
, there exists
u_{j}\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
solving

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{j})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=\psi^{m}(z,u_{j-1})\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u_{j}=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Since
\psi
is decreasing in the second variable, induction and the domination principle (see
[
3
, Subsection 3.5: Step 1]
) show that
\underline{u}\leqslant u_{j-1}\leqslant u_{j}\leqslant\overline{u}<0
.

We now smooth out
\overline{u}
. Choose some non-zero function
h\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that:

0\leqslant h\leqslant\psi(z,\overline{u}).

Using
u_{j}\leqslant\overline{u}<0
, we have

(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{j})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\geqslant\psi^{m}(z,\overline{u})\omega^{n}\geqslant h^{m}\omega^{n}.

By Maclaurin’s inequality,

\Delta_{\omega}u_{j}\geqslant n\left(\frac{(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{j})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}}{\omega^{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}\geqslant nh\ \text{in $\Omega$}.

We then let
w\in\mathrm{SH}_{1}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
be the non-zero solution of the Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}\Delta_{\omega}w=nh&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
w=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$};\end{cases}

from the comparison principle, it follows that for any
j
,

\underline{u}\leqslant u_{j}\leqslant w<0\ \text{in \text{$\Omega$}},\quad\underline{u}=u_{j}=w=0\ \text{on \text{$\partial\Omega$}}.

We may now apply Theorem
3.1
. Define

L_{j}=\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}u_{j}|,\quad\text{ and }\quad K_{j}=\sup_{\Omega}|\partial u_{j}|^{2}+1.

Theorem
3.1
shows that:

\|u_{j}\|_{C^{0}}\leqslant C_{0},\quad L_{j}\leqslant\frac{L_{j-1}}{2}+C_{0}K_{j}+C_{0}K_{j-1},

so that

(4.4)

L_{j}\leqslant\frac{L_{0}}{2^{j}}+3C_{0}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\frac{K_{j-i}}{2^{i}}\leqslant L_{0}+3C_{0}\max_{0\leqslant\beta\leqslant j}K_{\beta}.

Fix some
j\geqslant 1
, and choose
1\leqslant\alpha_{j}\leqslant j
such that:

K_{\alpha_{j}}=\max_{0\leqslant\beta\leqslant j}K_{\beta}.

Then

\|u_{\alpha_{j}}\|_{C^{0}}\leqslant C_{0},\quad L_{\alpha_{j}}\leqslant L_{0}+3C_{0}K_{\alpha_{j}}.

Applying the blow-up argument of
[
23
, Section 6]
, we obtain

K_{\alpha_{j}}\leqslant C,

for some
C
independent of
j
. Then by (
4.4
), we have
L_{\alpha}\leqslant C
for all
1\leqslant\alpha\leqslant j
for
C
independent of
j
, which gives:

\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}u_{\alpha}|+\sup_{\Omega}|\partial u_{\alpha}|^{2}\leqslant C,

for all
\alpha\geqslant 1
. By standard elliptic theory, we conclude that
u=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow\infty}u_{\alpha}
is the required solution of (
4.2
).
∎

We can use a perturbation argument to deal with the case when
\psi
is zero on the boundary:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\psi
be a smooth non-negative function on
\overline{\Omega}\times(-\infty,0]
such that
\psi
is (strictly) positive on
\Omega\times(-\infty,0)
and decreasing in the second variable. Suppose that there exist a strict subsolution
\underline{u}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and a supersolution
\overline{u}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
\underline{u}<\overline{u}<0
in
\Omega
. Then there exists
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega})
such that

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=\psi^{m}(z,u)\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}
be the constant in the definition of strict subsolution (see Definition
2.2
(2)). For
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, by Theorem
4.1
, there exists
u_{\varepsilon}\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{\varepsilon})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=(\psi(z,u_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon)^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$},\end{cases}

and

(4.5)

\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})}\leqslant C,

where
C
is a constant depending only on
(\overline{\Omega},\omega)
,
\|\psi\|_{C^{2}}
,
\|\underline{u}\|_{C^{2}}
, and
h
, which we can choose independent of
\varepsilon
. Since
\psi
is (strictly) positive in
\Omega\times(-\infty,0)
, for any
\Omega^{\prime}\Subset\Omega
, we can find some
c_{\Omega^{\prime}}>0
, which is independent of
\varepsilon
, and such that

(\psi(z,u_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon)^{m}\geqslant\psi^{m}(z,u_{\varepsilon})\geqslant c_{\Omega^{\prime}}>0;

by standard elliptic theory, we obtain uniform higher order estimates for
u_{\varepsilon}
on
\Omega^{\prime}
. Combining these with (
4.5
) and an approximation argument, we conclude.
∎

5.
Existence and Uniqueness of the Eigenfunction

In this section we will prove out main results, Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. We start with:

Theorem 5.1

(Theorem
1.1
)
.

Suppose that
\Omega
is a strongly
m
-pseudoconvex manifold and
f\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
is (strictly) positive. Then there exists a unique
\lambda_{1}>0
and a unique
u_{1}\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega})
solving:

(5.1)

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{1})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=(-\lambda_{1}u_{1})^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u_{1}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\\
\inf_{\Omega}u_{1}=1.\end{cases}

Proof.

Following Lions
[
41
]
and Wang
[
54
]
, for
\lambda\geqslant 0
, we consider the Dirichlet problem:

(5.2)

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=(1-\lambda u)^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Define:

I=\{\lambda\geqslant 0~|~\text{there is $u_{\lambda}\in m\mathrm{SH}({\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})$ solving \eqref{continuity path}}\},

and

\lambda_{1}=\sup_{\lambda\in I}\lambda.

We split the argument into several steps.

Step 1.

\lambda_{1}
is well-defined with
\lambda_{1}\in(0,\infty)
.

By Corollary
2.4
, when
\lambda=0
, there exists
u_{0}\in m\mathrm{SH}({\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
solving

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{0})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u_{0}=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Then
I
is not empty and so
\lambda_{1}
is well-defined with
\lambda_{1}\in[0,\infty]
. It is clear that for
\lambda\in I
,

(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{0})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\leqslant(1-\lambda u_{0})^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}.

This means
u_{0}
is a supersolution of (
5.2
). To show
\lambda_{1}>0
, we observe that for
\lambda\in(0,2^{-1}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{-1})
,

(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}(2u_{0}))^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=2^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}\geqslant(1-\lambda(2u_{0}))^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n},

which implies that
2u_{0}
is a subsolution of (
5.2
). By Theorem
4.1
(with
\underline{u}=2u_{0}
and
\overline{u}=u_{0}
), we have
(0,2^{-1}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{-1})\subseteq I
, so
\lambda_{1}\geqslant 2^{-1}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{-1}>0
.

To show
\lambda_{1}<\infty
, for any
\lambda\in I
, Maclaurin’s inequality shows

\Delta_{\omega}u_{\lambda}\geqslant n\left(\frac{(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{\lambda})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}}{\omega^{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}=n(1-\lambda u_{\lambda})f\geqslant-(n\lambda\inf_{\Omega}f)u_{\lambda}.

By Theorem
2.5
, we obtain
n\lambda\inf_{\Omega}f\leqslant\mu_{1}(\Delta_{\omega})
and so

\lambda_{1}\leqslant\frac{\mu_{1}(\Delta_{\omega})}{n\inf_{\Omega}f}<\infty.

Step 2.

\|u_{\lambda}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\rightarrow\infty
as
\lambda\rightarrow\lambda_{1}
.

Indeed, if not, there exists a constant
M
and a sequence
\lambda_{j}\in(0,\lambda_{1})
, converging to
\lambda_{1}
such that

\|u_{\lambda_{j}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant M.

For any
\lambda\in I
, it is clear that

(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{\lambda})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\geqslant f^{m}\omega^{n}=(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{0})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}

and

(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{\lambda})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\leqslant(1+\lambda_{1}M)^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}=(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}(1+\lambda_{1}M)u_{0})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}.

Then the domination principle shows that

(1+\lambda_{1}M)u_{0}\leqslant u_{\lambda}\leqslant u_{0},

so by Theorem
3.1
(with
\underline{u}=(1+\lambda_{1}M)u_{0}
and
w=u_{0}
), we obtain

\|u_{\lambda}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant C,\quad\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}u_{\lambda}|\leqslant C\sup_{\Omega}|\partial u_{\lambda}|^{2}+C,

for
C
independent of
\lambda
. By the blow-up argument in
[
23
, Section 6]
, we obtain

\|u_{\lambda}\|_{C^{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant C.

By standard elliptic theory, we can choose a subsequence
u_{\lambda_{j}}
which smoothly converges to
u_{*}\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, so that
(u_{*},\lambda_{1})
is a solution of (
5.2
). Choosing

\delta\in\left(0,\|u_{*}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{-1}\right)\ \text{and}\ C_{\delta}=\frac{1}{1-\delta\|u_{*}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}},

we compute

\begin{split}&(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}(C_{\delta}u_{*}))^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=C_{\delta}^{m}(1-\lambda_{1}u_{*})^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}\\[2.84526pt]
={}&(C_{\delta}-\lambda_{1}(C_{\delta}u_{*}))^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}\geqslant(1-(\lambda_{1}+\delta)(C_{\delta}u_{*}))^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}.\end{split}

However, by Theorem
4.1
(with
\underline{u}=C_{\delta}u_{0}
and
\overline{u}=u_{0}
), there exists a solution
u_{\lambda}
of (
5.2
) for
\lambda_{1}\leqslant\lambda\leqslant\lambda_{1}+\delta
, a contradiction with the definition of
\lambda_{1}
.

Step 3.

\lambda_{1}
is the required first eigenvalue.

We consider the normalized family

v_{\lambda}:=\frac{u_{\lambda}}{\|u_{\lambda}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}}

for
\lambda<\lambda_{1}
. Then
v_{\lambda}
satisfies
\|v_{\lambda}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=1
and

(5.3)

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v_{\lambda})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=(\|u_{\lambda}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{-1}-\lambda v_{\lambda})^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
v_{\lambda}=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Using Step 2 and
\lambda<\lambda_{1}
, we have

(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v_{\lambda})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\leqslant(1+\lambda_{1})^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}=(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}(1+\lambda_{1})u_{0})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}.

Then the domination principle implies

(5.4)

(1+\lambda_{1})u_{0}\leqslant v_{\lambda}.

To apply Theorem
3.1
, we need to construct an appropriate supersolution,
w
. By compactness of
m
-subharmonic functions, there exists a subsequence
\{\lambda_{j}\}
, with
\lambda_{j}\rightarrow\lambda_{1}
as
j\rightarrow\infty
, such that the
v_{\lambda_{j}}
converge in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
to some
u_{1}\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)
. In
[
3
, Section 4: Step 5]
, Badiane-Zeriahi used the
a priori
gradient estimate of Błocki
[
12
]
for the complex Monge-Ampère equation to upgrade this to
C^{0}
-convergence; the corresponding gradient estimate for the complex
m
-Hessian equation is not currently known. Instead, we will use the stability estimate, Theorem
2.8
, to show this convergence. After that, we can follow
[
3
, Section 4: Step 5]
to construct the required
w
.

Specifically, we will show that
\{v_{\lambda_{j}}\}
is a Cauchy sequence in
C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
. Using
u_{0}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\Omega^{\prime}\Subset\Omega
such that

\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime})}\leqslant\varepsilon

and so

\|v_{\lambda_{k}}-v_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime})}\leqslant 2\|Cu_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime})}\leqslant 2\varepsilon,

by (
5.4
). On the other hand, we have
v_{\lambda_{j}}\to u_{1}
in
L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\Omega)
, so

\|v_{\lambda_{k}}-v_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leqslant\varepsilon.

Using
\|v_{\lambda_{k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=\|v_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=1
, we have

\|v_{\lambda_{k}}-v_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leqslant\|v_{\lambda_{k}}-v_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})}^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\cdot\|v_{\lambda_{k}}-v_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})}^{\frac{1}{p}}\leqslant 2^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Thus

\|v_{\lambda_{k}}-v_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\|v_{\lambda_{k}}-v_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime})}+\|v_{\lambda_{k}}-v_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leqslant 2\varepsilon+2^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Using Step 2, for sufficiently large
k
and
l
.

\|u_{\lambda_{k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{-1}+\|u_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{-1}\leqslant\varepsilon.

Now Theorem
2.8
shows that:

\begin{split}&\|v_{\lambda_{k}}-v_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\\
\leqslant{}&\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\cdot\left\|(\|u_{\lambda_{k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{-1}-\lambda_{k}v_{\lambda_{k}})^{m}-(\|u_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{-1}-\lambda_{l}v_{\lambda_{l}})^{m}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{m}}\\
\leqslant{}&C\left\|(\|u_{\lambda_{k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{-1}-\lambda_{k}v_{\lambda_{k}})-(\|u_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{-1}-\lambda_{l}v_{\lambda_{l}})\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{m}}\\
\leqslant{}&C\left(\|\lambda_{k}v_{\lambda_{k}}-\lambda_{l}v_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+C\varepsilon\right)^{\frac{1}{m}},\end{split}

where we used the elementary equality
a^{m}-b^{m}=(a-b)(a^{m-1}+\ldots+b^{m-1})
in the third line. So combining the previous two estimates gives:

\|v_{\lambda_{k}}-v_{\lambda_{l}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\left(2\varepsilon+2^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{p}}+C\varepsilon\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}.

Then
\{v_{\lambda_{j}}\}
is a Cauchy sequence in
C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
. We thus conclude that

(5.5)

v_{\lambda_{j}}\to u_{1}\ \text{in $C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})$}.

We can now follow the argument of
[
3
, Section 4: Step 5]
to construct a super-barrier. Since
\|u_{1}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})}=1
and
u_{1}=0
on
\Omega
, then there exists
z_{0}\in\Omega
such that
u_{1}(z_{0})=-1
. Let
B
be a ball such that

z_{0}\in B\Subset\Omega,\ \text{and}\ u_{1}\leqslant-\frac{2}{3}\ \text{in $B$}.

For
j\gg 1
, we have
\lambda_{j}\geqslant\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}
and
-v_{\lambda_{j}}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}
in
B
. Then

(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v_{\lambda_{j}})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\geqslant(-\lambda_{j}v_{\lambda_{j}})^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}\geqslant\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}f}{6}\right)^{m}\omega^{n}\ \text{in $B$}.

By Maclaurin’s inequality,

\Delta_{\omega}v_{\lambda_{j}}\geqslant n\left(\frac{(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v_{\lambda_{j}})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}}{\omega^{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}=\frac{n\lambda_{1}f}{6}\ \text{in $B$}.

Let
\theta_{B}
be a smooth function in
\overline{\Omega}
such that
0\leqslant\theta_{B}\leqslant 1
,
\mathrm{Supp}(\theta_{B})\subset B
and
\theta_{B}\equiv 1
near
z_{0}
. Then there exists
w\in\mathrm{SH}_{1}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
solving the Dirichlet problem:

\begin{cases}\Delta_{\omega}w=\frac{n\lambda_{1}}{6}f\theta_{B}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
w=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Using the comparison principle, we obtain

v_{\lambda_{j}}\leqslant w<0\ \text{in $\Omega$},\ \ w=0\ \text{on $\partial\Omega$}.

By combining this with (
5.4
) and arguing as in Step 2, we obtain

\|v_{\lambda_{j}}\|_{C^{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant C.

For any
\Omega^{\prime}\Subset\Omega
, using (
5.5
) and
u_{1}<0
in
\Omega
, there exists a constant
c_{\Omega^{\prime}}
such that

v_{\lambda_{j}}\leqslant-c_{\Omega^{\prime}}<0\ \text{in $\Omega^{\prime}$},

which shows that (
5.3
) is non-degenerate in
\Omega^{\prime}
. Then by standard elliptic theory, we obtain higher order estimates of
v_{\lambda_{j}}
in
\Omega^{\prime}
. Thus we can take a further subsequence of the
v_{\lambda_{j}}
which converges to
u_{1}
in
C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega})
. We conclude
(\lambda_{1},u_{1})
solves the eigenvalue problem (
5.1
).

Step 4.
Uniqueness of
(\lambda_{1},u_{1})
.

This immediately follows from Theorem
5.2
below.
∎

We now prove a strong uniqueness result. It shows that the eigenfunction constructed in Theorem
5.1
is unique in the finite energy class
\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
, significantly improving on the results in
[
4
]
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose that
w\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
and
\alpha>0
are such that
(w,\alpha)
is a weak solution to (
1.1
). Then
\alpha=\lambda_{1}
and
w=\theta u_{1}
for some
\theta\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

For notational convenience, we will replace
f^{m}
with
\binom{n}{m}^{-1}f^{m}
and write
u=u_{1}
,
\lambda=\lambda_{1}
, throughout the proof, so that:

\begin{cases}\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(u)=-\lambda uf&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$},\\
\inf_{\Omega}u=-1,\end{cases}

and
\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(w)=-\alpha wf
with
w\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
.

First, we show
\alpha=\lambda
; the argument is essentially due to Le
[
38
, Proposition 5.6]
. Since
u,w\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
, we can integrate by parts, so that:

\int_{\Omega}(-w)(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=\int_{\Omega}(-u)\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}w\wedge(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m-1}\wedge\omega^{n-m}.

By the mixed Hessian inequality of Dinew-Lu
[
25
, Theorem 3.10]
, we also have that:

(5.6)

\binom{n}{m}\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}w\wedge(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m-1}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\geqslant\sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{m}}(w)\,\sigma_{m}^{\frac{m-1}{m}}(u)\,\omega^{n}.

We now combine these to compute:

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}(-w)(-\lambda uf)^{m}\omega^{n}\\
={}&\binom{n}{m}\int_{\Omega}(-w)(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\\
={}&\binom{n}{m}\int_{\Omega}(-u)\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}w\wedge(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m-1}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\\
\geqslant{}&\int_{\Omega}(-u)\sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{m}}(w)\,\sigma_{m}^{\frac{m-1}{m}}(u)\,\omega^{n}\\
={}&\lambda^{-1}\alpha\int_{\Omega}(-w)(-\lambda uf)^{m}\omega^{n}.\end{split}

It follows that
\lambda\geqslant\alpha
; interchanging the roles of
(u,\lambda)
and
(w,\alpha)
, we obtain
\alpha=\lambda
.

We now show
w=u
. Denote the derivatives of
\sigma_{m}
and
\sigma_{m}^{1/m}
at
u
by
\sigma_{m}^{i\overline{j}}
and
F^{i\overline{j}}
, respectively. We define the linear elliptic operator
L=F^{i\overline{j}}\partial_{i}\partial_{\overline{j}}
, which is degenerate on
\partial\Omega
. We have

L=F^{i\overline{j}}\partial_{i}\partial_{\overline{j}}=\frac{1}{m}\sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{m}-1}\sigma_{m}^{i\overline{j}}\,\partial_{i}\partial_{\overline{j}}=\frac{1}{m}(-\lambda u)^{1-m}\sigma_{m}^{i\overline{j}}\,\partial_{i}\partial_{\overline{j}}

and

(5.7)

Lu=F^{i\overline{j}}u_{i\overline{j}}=-\lambda uf.

Note that, by (
5.6
) and
\alpha=\lambda
, we also have:

\binom{n}{m}\frac{\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}w\wedge(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{m-1}\wedge\omega^{n-m}}{\omega^{n}}\geqslant(-\lambda uf)^{m-1}(-\lambda wf),

which implies

Lw\geqslant-\lambda wf\geqslant 0.

Define
\theta:=\sup\{a\geqslant 0\ |\ w\leqslant au\ \text{in $\Omega$}\}
. Since
w\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
, then
w\leqslant 0
in
\Omega
and
\theta
is well-defined. Suppose for a contradiction that
w\not=\theta u
. By combining (
5.7
) with the last inequality, we have:

(5.8)

L(w-\theta u)\geqslant-\lambda(w-\theta u)f\geqslant 0.

Note that the operator
L
in not uniform elliptic near
\partial\Omega
and so the Hopf lemma can not be applied here directly. We will now show that, by a modification of the Hopf lemma, we can increase
\theta
slightly, which will give us a contradiction.

We first claim that there exists
c_{0}>0
such that

(5.9)

F^{i\overline{j}}u_{i}u_{\overline{j}}\geqslant c_{0}(-\lambda uf)|\partial u|^{2}\ \text{in $\Omega$}.

Assuming the claim for now, we show how the result follows.

By
\Delta_{\omega}u\geqslant 0
and the usual Hopf lemma,
|\partial u|=\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}>0
on
\partial\Omega
, where
\nu
is the outer normal vector to
\partial\Omega
. Then there exist
c_{1},\delta_{0}>0
such that

|\partial u|^{2}>c_{1}\ \ \text{in $\{-\delta_{0}<\rho<0\}$}.

Together with the claim (
5.9
), this implies

(5.10)

F^{i\overline{j}}u_{i}u_{\overline{j}}\geqslant c_{0}c_{1}(-\lambda uf)\ \ \text{in $\{-\delta_{0}<\rho<0\}$}.

Define
v=e^{-Au}-1-u
. Using (
5.7
) and (
5.10
), in
\{-\delta_{0}<\rho<0\}
, we compute

\begin{split}Lv={}&e^{-Au}\left(A^{2}F^{i\overline{j}}u_{i}u_{\overline{j}}-AF^{i\overline{j}}u_{i\overline{j}}\right)-F^{i\overline{j}}u_{i\overline{j}}\\
\geqslant{}&e^{-Au}(-\lambda uf)\left(c_{0}c_{1}A^{2}-A-e^{Au}\right).\end{split}

Choosing
A\gg 1
, we obtain

(5.11)

Lv\geqslant 0\ \ \text{in $\{-\delta_{0}<\rho<0\}$}.

By (
5.8
) and using the strong maximum principle for
L
on the set
{\{\rho<-\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{0}\}}
(where
L
is uniformly elliptic), we have that
\max_{\{\rho\leqslant-\delta_{0}\}}(w-\theta u)<0
. It follows that there exists some
\varepsilon_{0}\ll 1
such that

w-\theta u+\varepsilon_{0}v\leqslant 0\ \ \text{on $\{\rho=-\delta_{0}\}$}.

Combining this with (
5.8
) and (
5.11
),

\begin{cases}L(w-\theta u+\varepsilon_{0}v)\geqslant 0&\text{in $\{-\delta_{0}<\rho<0\}$},\\[2.84526pt]
w-\theta u+\varepsilon_{0}v\leqslant 0&\text{on $\{\rho=-\delta_{0}\}$},\\[2.84526pt]
\displaystyle{\limsup_{x\to z}}\,(w-\theta u+\varepsilon_{0}v)(x)\leqslant 0&\text{for all $z\in\partial\Omega=\{\rho=0\}$}.\\
\end{cases}

Using the maximum principle, we see that

w-\theta u\leqslant-\varepsilon_{0}v\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}u\ \ \text{in $\{-\delta_{0}<\rho<0\}$}.

Since
\max_{\{\rho\leqslant-\delta_{0}\}}(w-\theta u)<0
, then

(5.12)

w\leqslant(\theta+\varepsilon)u\text{ in }\Omega

for some
0<\varepsilon\ll\varepsilon_{0}
, contradicting the definition of
\theta
.

Now it suffices to prove claim (
5.9
). For any point
x_{0}\in\Omega
, we choose a holomorphic normal coordinate system
(U,\{z^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
for
g
centered at
x_{0}
such that

u_{i\overline{j}}=\delta_{ij}u_{i\overline{i}},\ \ u_{1\overline{1}}\geqslant u_{2\overline{2}}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant u_{n\overline{n}},\ \ \text{at $x_{0}$}.

Let
\Lambda_{1}\geqslant\Lambda_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\Lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u
with respect to
\omega
. It then follows that

\sigma_{m}^{n\overline{n}}\geqslant\sigma_{m}^{n-1\overline{n-1}}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\sigma_{m}^{1\overline{1}}.

By
[
18
, Lemma 3.1]
(see also
[
34
, Lemma 2.2]
), we have

\sigma_{m}^{1\overline{1}}\geqslant\frac{m}{n\Lambda_{1}}\sigma_{m}(u)=\frac{m}{n\Lambda_{1}}(-\lambda uf)^{m}.

In Step 3 of the proof of Theorem
5.1
, we know that
u\in C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega})
, which implies
\Lambda_{1}\leqslant C_{0}
for some constant
C_{0}
. Hence,

F^{i\overline{j}}u_{i}u_{\overline{j}}\geqslant\frac{1}{m}(-\lambda uf)^{1-m}\sigma_{m}^{1\overline{1}}|\partial u|^{2}\geqslant c_{0}(-\lambda uf)|\partial u|^{2},

as claimed.
∎

Finally, we prove the Rayleigh quotient formula for
\lambda_{1}
(Theorem
1.2
).

Theorem 5.3

(Theorem
1.2
)
.

Suppose that
(\Omega,\omega)
and
f
are as in Theorem
1.1
. Then the eigenvalue can be characterized as:

\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)^{m}=\min\left\{\frac{E_{m}(u)}{I_{m}(u)}~\middle|~u\in\mathcal{E}_{m}^{1}(\Omega),\ u\neq 0\right\}.

Proof.

We will follow broadly the proof of Badiane-Zeriahi in
[
4
]
. Define:

(5.13)

\begin{split}\alpha^{m}:={}&\inf\left\{\frac{E_{m}(u)}{I_{m}(u)}~|~u\in\mathcal{E}_{m}^{1}(\Omega),\ u\neq 0\right\}\\
={}&\inf\left\{E_{m}(u)~|~u\in\mathcal{E}_{m}^{1}(\Omega),I_{m}(u)=1,u\neq 0\right\}.\end{split}

We break the proof into four steps.

Step 1.

\alpha>0
.

Suppose
u\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
. By Corollary
2.4
, let
u_{0}\in m\mathrm{SH}({\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
be the solution to:

\begin{cases}(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u_{0})^{m}\wedge\omega^{n-m}=f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u_{0}=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$},\end{cases}

and set
A=(m+1)!\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{m}
. Then by Proposition
2.11
, we have:

I(u)=\int_{\Omega}(-u)^{m+1}\mathrm{H}^{m}(u_{0})\leqslant A(\sup_{\Omega}f)^{m}E_{m}(u);

since
u
is arbitrary, we conclude
\alpha\geqslant(A\sup_{\Omega}f^{m})^{-1}>0
.

Step 2.
The infimum in (
5.13
) is attained.

Consider some sequence
w_{j}\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
with
I(w_{j})=1
and
E_{m}(w_{j})\searrow\alpha^{m}
. Then by Proposition
A.3
, after possibly taking a subsequence, the
w_{j}
converge in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
to some
w\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
. Since
E_{m}
is lower semi-continuous, we conclude
\alpha^{m}=E_{m}(w)
, by Fatou’s lemma and the definition of
\alpha
.

Step 3.
The pair
(w,\alpha)
is a weak solution to (
1.1
).

By definition, the function
w
is a minimizer of the functional
\Phi:\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, defined by:

\Phi(u):=E_{m}(u)-\alpha^{m}I_{m}(u),\quad u\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega).

As discussed at the end of the Appendix, it follows that
w
is a weak solution to the eigenvalue equation (with
\alpha
instead of
\lambda_{1}
of course).

Step 4.

\alpha=\lambda_{1}
.

This follows immediately from Theorem
5.2
.
∎

6.
Applications

6.1.
Bifurcation

We now present several applications of our main theorems. The first is a standard bifurcation theorem, originally found in Lions
[
41
, Corollary 2]
, which is similar to ones for the first eigenvalue of linear elliptic operators. Compared to
[
4
, Theorem 1.2]
, we have no restriction on
m
, and are able to additionally prove uniqueness of the solution, although of course our assumptions on the right-hand side are more restrictive.

Theorem 6.1

(Theorem
1.3
)
.

Let
\psi(z,s)
be a smooth (strictly) positive function on
\overline{\Omega}\times(-\infty,0]
such that
\partial_{s}\psi\geqslant-\gamma_{0}>-\lambda_{1}
, where
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}(\Omega,1)
is the first eigenvalue of
\mathrm{H}^{m}
associated to
\omega^{n}
. Then the equation:

(6.1)

\begin{cases}\sigma_{m}(u)=\psi(z,u)^{m}&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

admits a unique solution
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Proof.

We first show existence of a solution. It is well-known principle that this should follow from the existence of a subsolution (see e.g.
[
13
,
32
]
), but we were unable to locate an exact reference for our setting. We thus include the proof here.

We first construct a subsolution. Fix
\gamma\in(\gamma_{0},\lambda_{1})
and let
u_{\gamma}
be the corresponding solution to (
5.2
) with
f=\binom{n}{m}^{-1}
. Then for any constant
C\geqslant\left\lVert\psi(z,0)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
, we have:

\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(Cu_{\gamma})=C(1-\gamma u_{\gamma})\geqslant\left\lVert\psi(z,0)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}-C\gamma_{0}u_{\gamma}\geqslant\psi(z,Cu_{\gamma});

see
[
3
, Proposition 4.2]
. We conclude that
\underline{u}:=Cu_{\gamma}
is a subsolution to (
6.1
).

We now construct our solution. Consider the continuity path:

(6.2)

\begin{cases}\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(u_{t})=t\psi(z,u_{t})+(1-t)\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(\underline{u})&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u_{t}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Define

I=\{t\in[0,1]~|~\text{\eqref{bifurcation continuity path} admits a smooth solution $u_{t}$}\}.

We will show that
I
is non-empty, open, and closed. It is clearly non-empty, since
u_{0}=\underline{u}
is a solution when
t=0
.

We show openness. Fix
t\in I
and denote the linearized operator of (
6.2
) by
L_{t}
. Write
F=(F^{i\overline{j}})
for the first derivative of
\sigma_{m}^{1/m}
. Then

L_{t}=F^{i\overline{j}}(u_{t})\partial_{i}\partial_{\overline{j}}-t\partial_{s}\psi(z,u_{t}).

Let
\mu_{1}(t)
be the first eigenvalue of the operator
F^{i\overline{j}}(u_{t})\partial_{i}\partial_{\overline{j}}
. We claim that:

t\psi_{s}(z,u_{t})>-t\gamma\geqslant-\mu_{1}(t).

By concavity of
\sigma_{m}^{1/m}
, we have:

F^{i\overline{j}}(u_{t})\partial_{i}\partial_{\overline{j}}(\underline{u}-u_{t})\geqslant\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(\underline{u})-\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(u_{t}).

Combining this with
F^{i\overline{j}}(u_{t})\partial_{i}\partial_{\overline{j}}u_{t}=\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(u_{t})
and the definition of
\underline{u}
, we have

F^{i\overline{j}}(u_{t})\underline{u}_{i\overline{j}}\geqslant\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(\underline{u})=C-\gamma\underline{u}\geqslant-\gamma\underline{u}.

Using Theorem
2.5
we obtain
\gamma\leqslant\mu_{1}(t)
.

Lemma
2.6
now shows that
L_{t}
is injective. By combining this with
[
30
, Theorem 6.15]
,

L_{t}
is also surjective, so that
I
is open.

To show
I
is closed, it will suffice to establish uniform estimates on
u_{t}
. Using (
6.2
), we have

\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(u_{t})-\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(\underline{u})=t\left(\psi(z,u_{t})-\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(\underline{u})\right)\leqslant t\big(\psi(z,u_{t})-\psi(z,\underline{u})\big).

It follows that

\alpha(t)^{i\overline{j}}\partial_{i}\partial_{\overline{j}}(u_{t}-\underline{u})\leqslant c(t)(u_{t}-\underline{u}),

where

\alpha(t)^{i\overline{j}}=\int_{0}^{1}F^{i\overline{j}}(ru_{t}+(1-r)\underline{u})dr,\ \ c(t)=t\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{s}\psi(z,ru_{t}+(1-r)\underline{u})dr.

Let
\tilde{\mu}_{1}(t)
be the first eigenvalue of
\alpha(t)^{i\overline{j}}\partial_{i}\partial_{\overline{j}}
. By the same argument as above, we know that

c(t)>-\tilde{\mu}_{1}(t).

By Lemma
2.6
, we obtain
u_{t}\geqslant\underline{u}
.

For the
C^{2}
-estimate, note that
\psi(z,s)\geqslant\tau>0
on
\overline{\Omega}\times[-\|\underline{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)},0]
for some small constant
\tau
. It follows that

\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(u_{t})=t\psi(z,u_{t})+(1-t)\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(\underline{u})\geqslant t\psi(z,u_{t})+(1-t)\psi(z,\underline{u})\geqslant\tau>0.

If we now choose
\tau_{0}>0
sufficiently small such that
w=\tau_{0}\rho
satisfies

\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(w)=\tau_{0}\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(\rho)\leqslant\tau\leqslant\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(u_{t}),

then we may applying Theorem 3.1 and the blow-up argument of
[
23
, Section 6]
,
to obtain a uniform
C^{2}
estimate. The higher order estimates follow from the standard Evans-Krylov theory and bootstrapping.

We now establish uniqueness. Suppose that
u
and
v
are both solutions. Then

\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(u)-\sigma_{m}^{1/m}(v)=\psi(z,u)-\psi(z,v)

and so

\left(\int_{0}^{1}F^{i\overline{j}}(ru+(1-r)v)dr\right)\partial_{i}\partial_{\overline{j}}(u-v)=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{s}\psi(z,ru+(1-r)v)dr\right)(u-v).

By applying Lemma
2.6
in the same way as before, we obtain
u=v
.
∎

6.2.
Monotonicity

We now discuss monotonicity property of the first eigenvalue.

Theorem 6.2
.

Suppose we are in the setting of Theorem
1.1
, and
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega
is another strongly
m
-pseudoconvex manifold. Then

\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)<\lambda_{1}(\Omega^{\prime},f).

Proof.

By Theorem
1.2
, there exists some
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega^{\prime})
such that:

\lambda_{1}^{m}(\Omega^{\prime},f)=\frac{E_{m,\Omega^{\prime}}(w^{\prime})}{I_{m,\Omega^{\prime}}(w^{\prime})}.

By Theorem
2.12
, we can find some
w\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
with
w\leqslant w^{\prime}
and
E_{m,\Omega}(w)\leqslant E_{m,\Omega^{\prime}}(w^{\prime})
. We also clearly have:

I_{m,\Omega^{\prime}}(w^{\prime})\leqslant I_{m,\Omega}(w);

we claim this inequality is actually strict. If not, then
w\equiv 0
on
\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}
(since
\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}
has positive measure), and
w=w^{\prime}
on
\Omega^{\prime}
. But then
w
would attain its maximum at an interior point, and so by the strong maximum principle,
w\equiv 0
, which is a contradiction.

We conclude then that:

\lambda_{1}^{m}(\Omega,f)\leqslant\frac{E_{m,\Omega}(w)}{I_{m,\Omega}(w)}<\lambda_{1}^{m}(\Omega^{\prime},f),

proving the theorem.
∎

6.3.
Geometric Bounds

Motivated in part by Theorem
6.1
, we conclude with some geometric bounds on
\lambda_{1}
; our results are inspired by
[
38
]
. The variational characterization in Theorem
1.2
gives an upper bound on manifolds with non-negative Ricci curvature:

Theorem 6.3

(Theorem
1.4
)
.

Suppose that the Ricci curvature
\mathrm{Ric}(\omega)
is non-negative. Let
R>0
be the largest number such that there exists a geodesic ball
B_{R}(p)\subseteq\Omega
with
r^{2}(z):=\mathrm{dist}_{\omega}^{2}(z,p)
smooth and plurisubharmonic on
B_{R}(p)
. Then:

\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)\leqslant c(n,m)(\inf_{\Omega}f)^{-\frac{2m+1}{m+1}}R^{-\frac{2n}{m+1}-2}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)^{\frac{2n}{m+1}}\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)^{-\frac{1}{m+1}}\|f\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)}^{\frac{m}{m+1}}.

Proof.

Let
v
be the smooth solution to:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\sigma_{m}(v)=(4n)^{m}f^{m}\omega^{n}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
v=0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Consider the non-zero function

\displaystyle w(z)=(\inf_{\Omega}f)(r^{2}-R^{2}),

which is a smooth plurisubharmonic function on the ball
B_{R}(p)
. By Maclaurin’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{m}(w)\leqslant(\Delta_{\omega}w)^{m}=(\inf_{\Omega}f)^{m}(\Delta_{\omega}r^{2})^{m}.

Since
\mathrm{Ric}(\omega)\geqslant 0
, by the Laplacian comparison theorem, one has

\sigma_{m}(w)\leqslant(\inf_{\Omega}f)^{m}(\Delta_{\omega}r^{2})^{m}\leqslant(\inf_{\Omega}f)^{m}(4n)^{m}\leqslant\sigma_{m}(v).

Since
v\leqslant 0
on
\partial B_{R}(p)
, by the comparison principle, we see that
v\leqslant w
on
B_{R}(p)
.

Now, by the variational characterization of
\lambda_{1}
, we have:

(6.3)

\lambda_{1}^{m}(\Omega,f)\leqslant\frac{\int_{\Omega}(-v)\mathrm{H}^{m}(v)}{\int_{\Omega}(-v)^{m+1}f^{m}\omega^{n}}.

By the Hölder inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(-v)\mathrm{H}^{m}(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(-v)f^{m}\omega^{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\int_{\Omega}(-v)^{m+1}f^{m}\omega^{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{m+1}}\left(\int_{\Omega}f^{m}\omega^{n}\right)^{\frac{m}{m+1}},

so by combining with (
6.3
), we have:

\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)\leqslant\left(\frac{\int_{\Omega}f^{m}\omega^{n}}{\int_{\Omega}(-v)^{m+1}f^{m}\omega^{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{m+1}}.

Let
0<c<1
. We estimate the denominator by:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(-v)^{m+1}f^{m}\omega^{n}

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{B_{R}}(-v)^{m+1}f^{m}\omega^{n}

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{B_{cR}}(-w)^{m+1}f^{m}\omega^{n}

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-c^{2})^{m+1}(\inf_{\Omega}f)^{m+1}R^{2(m+1)}\int_{B_{cR}(p)}f^{m}\omega^{n}

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-c^{2})^{m+1}(\inf_{\Omega}f)^{2m+1}R^{2(m+1)}\mathrm{vol}(B_{cR}(p)).

Set
R_{1}=\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)
, so that
B_{R_{1}}(p)=\Omega
. By the relative volume comparison, we have

\frac{\mathrm{vol}(B_{R_{1}}(p))}{\mathrm{vol}(B_{cR}(p))}\leqslant\frac{R_{1}^{2n}}{(cR)^{2n}},

which implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}(B_{cR}(p))\geqslant\frac{c^{2n}R^{2n}}{\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)^{2n}}\mathrm{vol}(\Omega).

In summary, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(-v)^{m+1}f^{m}\omega^{n}\geqslant c^{2n}(1-c^{2})^{m+1}(\inf_{\Omega}f)^{2m+1}R^{2(m+1)+2n}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)^{-2n}\mathrm{vol}(\Omega).

We can now choose
c
to maximize the constant, giving:

\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)\leqslant c(n,m)(\inf_{\Omega}f)^{-\frac{2m+1}{m+1}}R^{-\frac{2n}{m+1}-2}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)^{\frac{2n}{m+1}}\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)^{-\frac{1}{m+1}}\|f\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)}^{\frac{m}{m+1}}.

∎

When
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we can simplify the above by computing the volume of
B_{cR}
explicitly, giving the bound:

\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)\leqslant c(n,m)(\inf_{\Omega}f)^{-\frac{2m+1}{m+1}}R^{-\frac{2n}{m+1}-2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{m}(\Omega)}^{\frac{m}{m+1}}.

Further, if
f=1
, we can apply Theorem
6.2
to the ball
B_{R}
to give an upper bound for
\lambda_{1}(\Omega)
solely in terms of the in-radius:

\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\leqslant c(n,m)R^{-2}.

For domains in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we also have a lower bound for the first eigenvalue. For the rest of this subsection, we take
\omega
to be the Kähler form of the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and write
\left\lvert E\right\rvert
for the Lebesgue measure of any measurable
E\subseteq\Omega
.

Recall the following version of Aleksandrov’s maximum principle
[
30
, Lemma 9.2]
(see also
[
11
,
50
]
):

Lemma 6.4
.

For
v\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
with
v|_{\partial\Omega}=0
, we have

\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant\omega_{2n}^{-1/2n}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)\left(\int_{\Gamma^{+}}\det D^{2}v\right)^{1/2n},

where
\Gamma^{+}:=\{x\in\Omega~|~v(x)+Dv(x)\cdot(y-x)\leqslant v(y)\text{ for all }y\in\Omega\}
, and
\omega_{2n}
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Proposition 6.5
.

We have the following lower bound

\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\omega_{2n}^{1/2n}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)^{-1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2n}(\Omega)}^{-1}.

Proof.

Let
(\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f),u)
be the unique eigenvalue pair on
\Omega
(i.e. the unique solution to (
1.1
)), with
\inf_{\Omega}u=-1
.

Applying Lemma
6.4
to
-u
on
\Omega
gives:

(6.4)

\begin{split}1\leqslant{}&\omega_{2n}^{-1/2n}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)\left(\int_{\Gamma^{+}}\det D^{2}u\right)^{1/2n}\\
\leqslant{}&2\omega_{2n}^{-1/2n}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)\left(\int_{\Gamma^{+}}(\det u_{i\overline{j}})^{2}\right)^{1/2n},\end{split}

where the second inequality follows as in
[
11
]
. By Maclaurin’s inequality, we have:

\det u_{i\overline{j}}=\frac{(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{n}}{\omega^{n}}\leqslant\left(\frac{\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)}{\omega^{n}}\right)^{n/m}.

Using the eigenvalue equation and the
L^{\infty}
bound on
u
, (
6.4
) becomes:

1\leqslant 2\omega_{2n}^{-1/2n}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)\left(\int_{\Gamma^{+}}(\lambda_{1}f)^{2n}\right)^{1/2n}.

Replacing
\Gamma^{+}
by
\Omega
finishes.

∎

When
f=1
, we can also derive a lower bound solely in terms of the diameter, as in
[
3
, Remark 4.1]
; by Theorem
6.2
, we know that:

\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\geqslant\lambda_{1}(B(a,R)),

where
a\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
R=\frac{\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)}{2}
. Then, by using the continuity path (
5.2
) as in
[
3
]
, we have:

\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\geqslant 4\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)^{-2}.

When
\Omega
is itself a ball, the above inequality is sharper than Proposition
6.5
by a factor of 4; of course, when
\Omega
is very thin,
\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert
may be much smaller than the diameter, so that Proposition
6.5
provides a better estimate.

Finally, similar to the real case discussed in
[
38
]
, we expect that
\lambda_{1}(\Omega)
should ultimately scale like
\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert^{-1/n}
, although proving this currently seems to require new ideas. More generally, we (perhaps naively) expect
\lambda_{1}(\Omega,f)
to scale like
1
over the
L^{n}
-norm of
f
.

Appendix A
Some Pluripotential Theory

We briefly sketch some fundamental results for the pluirpotential theory on strongly
m
-pseudoconvex manifolds. Since we assume our manifolds to be Kähler, these results follow from standard techniques.

We say a smooth function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
is
\omega

m
-subharmonic (
m
-subharmonic or
m
-sh for short), if:

(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{k}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\geqslant 0\text{ for all }1\leqslant k\leqslant m.

Following Błocki
[
10
]
, an upper semi-continuous,
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
-function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
is defined to be
m
-sh if, for any smooth
m
-sh functions
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m-1}
, we have:

\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u\wedge\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v_{m-1}\wedge\omega^{n-m}\geqslant 0.

By Gårding’s inequality
[
28
]
, the two definitions agree for smooth
u
, see
[
10
]
. We write
m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)
for the set of all
m
-sh functions (omitting the dependence on
\omega
).

If
u
is
m
-sh and
\omega
is flat, then local convolutions can be used to produce a sequence of smooth
\omega
-sh functions,
u_{j}
, which decrease to
u
; this is an important technical tool for many results. For general
\omega
, these convolutions may fail to be
m
-sh, and so the proof of smooth approximation becomes more difficult. It can be shown to hold on an strongly
m
-pseudoconvex manifold by copying exactly the ideas of Pliś
[
47
]
and Lu-Nguyen
[
43
]
; the key technical tool needed is the “zero-temperature limit” of Berman
[
5
]
, which follows from slight modifications to the theorem of Collins-Picard
[
23
]
. See also
[
31
, Theorem 3.18]
and
[
35
, Proposition 2.9]
, where smooth approximation on certain Hermitian manifolds with boundary is shown.

We can now follow the classical ideas of Bedford-Taylor
[
7
,
8
]
to show that if
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, then the complex Hessian measure of
u
:

\mathrm{H}^{m}(u):=\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}(u\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}(\ldots u\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u\wedge\omega^{n-m})\ldots)

is a well-defined Radon measure. Moreover, if
u_{j}\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is a decreasing sequence with
u_{j}\searrow u
, then the measures
\mathrm{H}^{m}(u_{j})
converge weakly to
\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)
. These results follow from the Chern-Levine-Nirenberg inequalities, which are proved by integration by parts using the exhaustion function
\rho
. See also the recent paper of Kołodziej-Nguyen
[
35
, Section 3]
, where they prove similar results in the more general setting of Hermitian manifolds with boundary.

Another fundamental tool we use repeatedly is the comparison principle:

Proposition A.1
.

Suppose that
u,v\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, with:

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\rightarrow z\\
x\in\Omega\end{subarray}}v(x)\leqslant\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\rightarrow z\\
x\in\Omega\end{subarray}}u(x)\text{ for all }z\in\partial\Omega.

Then:

\chi_{\{u<v\}}\mathrm{H}^{m}(v)\leqslant\chi_{\{u<v\}}\mathrm{H}^{m}(u).

We omit the proof, and refer the reader to Cegrell
[
14
,
15
]
and Lu
[
42
]
.

An important and immediate consequence is the so-called domination principle:

Proposition A.2
.

Suppose that
u,v\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, with:

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\rightarrow z\\
x\in\Omega\end{subarray}}v(x)\leqslant\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\rightarrow z\\
x\in\Omega\end{subarray}}u(x)\text{ for all }z\in\partial\Omega,

and
\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)\leqslant\mathrm{H}^{m}(v)
. Then
v\leqslant u
in
\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Since
\rho\leqslant 0
, we may apply the comparison principle to
u
and
v+\varepsilon\rho
, giving:

\chi_{\{u<v+\varepsilon\}}\mathrm{H}^{m}(v+\varepsilon\rho)\leqslant\chi_{\{u<v+\varepsilon\}}\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)\leqslant\chi_{\{u<v+\varepsilon\}}\mathrm{H}^{m}(v).

By multilinearity of the complex Hessian operator and strict
m
-subharmonicity of
\rho
, we have
\mathrm{H}^{m}(v+\varepsilon\rho)\geqslant\mathrm{H}^{m}(v)+c\omega^{n}
, for some
c>0
depending on
\varepsilon
. It follows that
\{u<v+\varepsilon\rho\}
has
\omega^{n}
-measure zero for all
\varepsilon>0
, so that
u\geqslant v
a.e.; since
u,v
are
\omega
-subharmonic, standard theory implies the inequality holds everywhere, see e.g.
[
42
]
.
∎

We also need some results about the finite energy class
\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Define
\mathcal{E}^{0}_{m}(\Omega)
to be:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{0}_{m}(\Omega):=\{u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\ |\

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega),\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)<\infty,

\displaystyle\text{ and }\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\rightarrow z\\
x\in\Omega\end{subarray}}u(x)=0\text{ for all }z\in\partial\Omega\}.

Then, following Lu
[
42
]
(see also
[
6
,
15
]
) we define the energy of a function
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)
to be:

E_{m}(u):=\sup\left\{\int_{\Omega}(-v)\mathrm{H}^{m}(v)\ \Big|\ v\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{E}^{0}_{m}(\Omega),\ u\leqslant v\right\}.

If
u
satisfies the Dirichlet boundary condition
\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\rightarrow z\\
x\in\Omega\end{subarray}}u(x)=0
and
E_{m}(u)<\infty
, then we say
u
has finite energy; we write
\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
for the set of all such functions.

Although elements in
\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
may be unbounded, they still have a well-defined complex Hessian measure, i.e. for any
u\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique Radon measure, denoted
\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)
, such that, if
u_{j}\in\mathcal{E}^{0}_{m}(\Omega)
is a sequence decreasing to
u
, then the sequence
\mathrm{H}^{m}(u_{j})
weakly converges to
\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)
. For
m
-hyperconvex domains, this result was proven by Lu
[
42
]
; it is easy to see that the proof works for strongly
m
-pseudoconvex manifolds as well.

We also have some important properties of the energy functional. First, one may show that
E_{m}
is continuous and monotone increasing along any decreasing sequence
\{u_{j}\}\subset\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
– this can be shown using integration by parts and some classical inequalities, exactly following
[
42
]
. Along general
L^{1}
-convergent sequences in
\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
,
E_{m}
is only lower semi-continuous. From this, one deduces:

Proposition A.3
.

Fix
C>0
. Then the set:

\{u\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)\ |\ E_{m}(u)\leqslant C\}

is compact in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

By Błocki’s inequality, Proposition
2.11
, the above set is bounded in
L^{1}(\Omega)
, so any subset admits an
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
limit point
u\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)
. From lower semi-continuity,
E_{m}(u)\leqslant C
.
∎

Using these continuity results, an integration by parts arguments can be used to prove the cocycle formula, which says that if
u,v\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
, then:

E(v)-E(u)=\frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\int_{\Omega}(u-v)(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{k}\wedge(\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v)^{m-k}\wedge\omega^{n-m}.

The special case
u=0
gives the formula
E_{m}(v)=\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{\Omega}(-v)\mathrm{H}^{m}(v)
.

The main motivation for considering
\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
is that the complex Hessian measure can be interpreted as the derivative of the energy functional
E_{m}
, via the projection formula:

Proposition A.4
.

Suppose that
u\in\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
and
v\in C^{0}_{c}(\Omega)
. Then:

\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}E_{m}(P_{m}(u+tv))=\int_{\Omega}(-v)\mathrm{H}^{m}(u),

where we define:

P_{m}(h):=\sup\{w\in m\mathrm{SH}(\Omega)\ |\ w\leqslant h\}

for any upper semi-continuous function
h
on
\Omega
.

Proof.

We only sketch the basic points of the proof. It is sufficient to show that:

\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{E_{m}(P_{m}(u+tv))-E_{m}(u)}{t}=\int_{\Omega}(-v)\mathrm{H}^{m}(u).

By the cocycle formula, integration by parts, and Cauchy-Schwarz, we have:

\displaystyle E(P_{m}(u+tv))-E(u)

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{\Omega}(u-P_{m}(u+tv))\,\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)\geqslant t\int_{\Omega}(-v)\mathrm{H}^{m}(u).

Dividing by
t
and taking the limit gives one inequality.

The other direction follows from the orthogonality property
\int_{\Omega}(P(\psi)-\psi)\mathrm{H}^{m}(P(\psi))=0
, whose proof is local and hence standard. Using this property, we have:

\displaystyle E(P_{m}(u+tv))-E(u)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Omega}(u-P_{m}(u+tv))\,\mathrm{H}^{m}(P_{m}(u+tv))

\displaystyle=t\int_{\Omega}(-v)\mathrm{H}^{m}(P_{m}(u+tv)).

We conclude by the weak convergence
\mathrm{H}^{m}(P_{m}(u+tv))\rightarrow\mathrm{H}^{m}(u)
as
t\rightarrow 0
.
∎

Recall that we have defined:

I_{m}(u):=\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{\Omega}(-u)^{m+1}\omega^{n}.

It now follows from Proposition
A.4
that critical points of the functional
E_{m}\circ P_{m}-\lambda_{1}^{m}I_{m}
are weak solutions to the eigenvalue equation (
1.1
). Since
\mathrm{H}^{m}
is well-defined for finite energy functions, the critical points will also be solutions in the pluripotential theoretic sense.
