On the independence number of sparser random Cayley graphs
Marcelo Campos and Gabriel Dahia and João Pedro Marciano

Abstract
. The Cayley sum graph Γ A \Gamma_{A} of a set A ⊆ ℤ n A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n} is defined to have vertex set ℤ n \mathbb{Z}_{n} and an edge between two distinct vertices x , y ∈ ℤ n x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{n} if x + y ∈ A x+y\in A . Green and Morris proved that if the set A A is a p p -random subset of ℤ n \mathbb{Z}_{n} with p = 1 / 2 p=1/2 , then the independence number of Γ A \Gamma_{A} is asymptotically equal to α ⁡ ( G ⁡ ( n , 1 / 2 ) ) \alpha(G(n,1/2)) with high probability. Our main theorem is the first extension of their result to p = o ⁡ ( 1 ) p=o(1) : we show that, with high probability, α ⁡ ( Γ A ) = ( 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ​ α ​ ( G ⁡ ( n , p ) ) \alpha(\Gamma_{A})=(1+o(1))\alpha(G(n,p)) as long as p ≥ ( log n ) − 1 / 80 p\geq(\log n)^{-1/80} . One of the tools in our proof is a geometric-flavoured theorem that generalises Freĭman’s lemma, the classical lower bound on the size of high dimensional sumsets. We also give a short proof of this result up to a constant factor; this version yields a much simpler proof of our main theorem at the expense of a worse constant.

1.
Introduction

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a large prime. The Cayley sum graph
\Gamma_{A}
of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is defined to have
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
as its vertices and an edge between two distinct
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
if
x+y\in A
.

Alon [1, Conjecture 4.4]
conjectured that for each
t\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, there exists a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
such that
|A|=t
and
\Gamma_{A}
has independence number at most
\frac{n}{t}(\log n)^{O(1)}
, and moreover remarked that “the conjecture may well hold for a random choice of
A
.”

Green
more generally asked (see
[
17
, Question 2]
) which parameters of
\Gamma_{A}
match those of the random graph
G(n,p)
when
A
is a random subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
In that same work, he showed that if
A
is chosen uniformly at random over all subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, then with high probability the size of the largest independent set
1
1

1

{1/2}

\Gamma_{A}
is at most
\alpha(G(n,1/2))
, up to a constant factor.

This result is in fact a consequence of a theorem in additive combinatorics.
Recall that the sumset
X+Y
and the restricted sumset
X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X
are defined as

X+Y=\{x+y:x\in X,y\in Y\},\qquad X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X=\{x_{1}+x_{2}:x_{1},x_{2}\in X,x_{1}\neq x_{2}\},

and observe that
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is independent in
\Gamma_{A}
exactly when
X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\subseteq A^{\mathsf{c}}
.

Green
deduced his bound on the typical size of
\alpha(\Gamma_{A})
from estimates on the number of sets
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
such that
|X|=k
and
|X+X|\leq\sigma k
, where
\sigma
is some upper bound on the doubling of
X
.
When referring to the doubling of the set itself, we will use
\sigma[X]
, i.e.
\sigma[X]=|X+X|/|X|
.

Later,
Green and Morris [18]
showed that better estimates on the number of such sets are possible if one handles ranges of
\sigma[X]
differently.
When
\sigma[X]
is constant, they used an arithmetic regularity lemma to obtain a tighter bound.
Leveraging the isoperimetric inequality on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, they obtain a bound on the number of “quasi-random” elements in sets with doubling close to maximum, thus refining
Green
’s estimate for the number of such sets.
As a consequence, they determined the correct constant for the independence number of
\Gamma_{A}
when
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is uniform random, showing that
\alpha(\Gamma_{A})
asymptotically matches
\alpha(G(n,1/2))
with high probability
[
18
]
.

Our main
theorem
extends the above result of
Green and Morris
to the more challenging setting when
A
is a sparse random subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, and can be seen as progress towards
Alon

To state our result, it is useful to denote by
\Gamma_{p}
the (random) Cayley sum graph
\Gamma_{A}
when
A
is a
p

\mathbb{Z}_{n}
for
p=p(n)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n
be a prime number and let
p=p(n)
satisfy
p\geq(\log n)^{-1/80}
.
The random Cayley sum graph
\Gamma_{p}
of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
satisfies

(1)

\alpha(\Gamma_{p})=\big(2+o(1)\big)\log_{\frac{1}{1-p}}n

with high probability as
n\to\infty
.

If
p=o(1)
, then this bound is asymptotically equal to
(2+o(1))(\log n)/p
; it also matches
\alpha(G(n,p))
in the corresponding range of
p

[
4
,
21
]
.
We remark that we do not believe the lower bound on
p
in
Theorem

1.1
to be close to optimal, so we do not optimize the constant in the exponent of the
\log
.
Nonetheless, there is a natural barrier for our methods when proving upper bounds at around
p\approx(\log n)^{-1}
(see
Section

10
).

The lower bound in (
1
) follows from a second moment computation together with a simple combinatorial argument (see
Section

9
).
The proof of the upper bound, on the other hand, is much more challenging, even though it has the same broad outline pioneered by
Green [17]
.
In fact, we use his bounds on the number of sets with given doubling for the “middle” range
k^{\beta}<\sigma[X]=o(k)
, and a minor extension (that follows from the same proof) of the theorem of
Green and Morris [18]
for the “upper” range
\sigma[X]=\Theta(k)
.
Our contribution is therefore in the “lower” doubling range,
\sigma[X]\leq k^{\beta}
for a small constant
\beta>0
, where even the correct count of such sets would not be enough to prove our main
theorem
.

To overcome this obstacle, we show that each of those sets contains a much smaller subset
F
– we call it a “fingerprint” of
X
– so that, after determining that
F
possesses some special properties, it suffices to count the fingerprints to deduce
Theorem

1.1
.
This idea is inspired by the application of the asymmetric container method of
Morris, Samotij, and Saxton [23]
to the problem of counting sets with small sumset by the first author
[
9
]
.
Indeed, we could also use asymmetric containers as a tool to prove our main
theorem
, but we prefer this simpler approach as it results in better bounds and a more self-contained proof.

The special property we require of each fingerprint
F\subseteq X
is having a sufficiently large sumset.
Ideally, we would like
F+F
to be as large as
X+X
; this obviously imposes a lower bound of
|F|\geq|X+X|^{1/2}
.

Bollobás, Leader, and Tiba
recently studied the question of finding such sets for general Abelian groups: they obtain
F\subseteq X
satisfying
|F+F|\approx|X+X|
when
X
has bounded doubling
[
7
, Theorem 2]
.
However, their result does not serve our needs because it does not handle the case of
\sigma[X]
being
k^{\beta}
for fixed, small
\beta>0
.

We pursue a different approach to obtain our collection of fingerprints, one that is able to handle sets with doubling that is polynomial in their size.
This approach yields fingerprints such that
|F|\approx|X+X|^{1/2}\log|X|
, which is only a logarithmic factor away from the ideal bound, but
|F+F|
is in general far from
|X+X|
.
What we can show is that the size of the sumset is as large as in Freĭman’s lemma, which is just enough for our application.

It is therefore useful to recall Freĭman’s lemma, and to do so we need a definition.
We will write
\operatorname{rank}(X)
for the minimum dimension of a hyperplane that contains a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
; if
\operatorname{rank}(X)=d
, then we say
X
has full rank.
The statement of the lemma is then:

Lemma 1.2

(
Freĭman [15]
)
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite set of full rank.
Then,

|X+X|\geq(d+1)|X|-\binom{d+1}{2}.

Observe that
Lemma

1.2
is a statement about subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
rather than
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
To amend this, we can use the (by now) standard machinery of Freĭman isomorphisms (see, for example,
[
27
, Chapter 5.3]
) to reach similar conclusions for subsets of the integers modulo
n
, changing rank for Freĭman dimension when appropriate.
We therefore change the setting to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the rest of this informal discussion.

Like applications of the hypergraph container method, the way to show that
F+F
is almost as large as
(d+1)|X|
is by proving a suitable supersaturation result (see
Theorem

2.2
, below).
In fact, from this result we will be able to show that picking a fingerprint randomly inside
X
works.

Theorem

2.2
informally says the following: if a set
Y
approximates
X+X
in terms of its popular sums, then it is (almost) as large as the bound in Freĭman’s lemma.

This key step in the proof of
Theorem

2.2
is a result stating that we can find some small
T\subseteq X
such that
X+T
almost attains the bound in Freĭman’s lemma.
Proving this
theorem
is the most difficult part of our proof, and we consider it to be of independent interest.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
d,r\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\gamma>r^{-1/3}
and let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite set with
\operatorname{rank}(X)\geq r
.
There exists
T\subseteq X
such that
|T|\leq 4(r+1)/\gamma
and

|X+T|\geq(1-5\gamma)\frac{(r+1)|X|}{2}.

The proof of this theorem is technical; however, we will also show that if we were satisfied with
1/6
being the leading constant instead of
(1-5\gamma)/2
, then a much simpler approach would suffice.
This weaker version would also be enough to prove the upper bound in
Theorem

1.1
with 6 being the leading constant instead of 2.

Recently,
Jing and Mudgal [22]
proved the following
theorem
that is closely related to
Theorem

1.3
.
It obtains the correct leading constant, i.e. without the
(1-5\gamma)/2
or
1/6
loss in our bound, at the expense of a worse relationship between the number of translates and the dimension of the set:

Theorem 1.4

(
[
22
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

Given
d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
C=C(d)>0
such that, for every finite set of full rank
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exist
x_{1},\ldots,x_{C}\in X
satisfying

|X+\{x_{1},...,x_{C}\}|\geq(d+1)|X|-5(d+1)^{3}.

Theorem

1.4
is part of a recent line of work
[
6
,
13
]
that relies on a beautiful theorem of
Bollobás, Leader, and Tiba [6, Theorem 8]
to obtain sumset lower bounds via few-translates.
Unfortunately, the proof of this theorem uses
Shao
’s
[
26
]
almost-all Balog–Szemerédi–Gowers theorem, and, as a consequence, the dependency of the number of translates
C
on the dimension

is of tower-type
[
6
,
26
]
.

A related result, which avoids super-polynomial dependencies between its parameters, is the following elegant
theorem
proved by
Fox, Luo, Pham, and Zhou [13]
.
Its proof relies on a clever path counting argument akin to Gowers’ proof of the Balog–Szemerédi theorem.
Note that we state a specialized version of their much more general result.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

There exists
c>0
such that the following holds.
Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|X|=k
.
For every
s\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, there exists
T\subseteq X
such that
|T|\leq s
and

(2)

|X+T|\geq c\min\big\{\sigma[X]^{1/3},s\big\}|X|.

This result would work for our needs if we could ensure
\sigma[X]>c^{-1}d^{3}
, but we cannot.
Alas,
Fox, Luo, Pham, and Zhou
also exhibit a construction
[
13
, Proposition 2.4]
which shows that we cannot even replace
\sigma[X]^{1/3}
in (

) by
\sigma[X]^{1/1.29}
; this means that our requirement for the rank of the set in
Theorem

1.3
is essential.

In the next

, we give a detailed sketch of our proof strategy, and a proof of our fingerprint

Then, in
Section

, we give a simple proof of a weaker version of our “Freĭman’s lemma via few translates” theorem that nevertheless contains some of the main ideas required for the full proof of

.

Section

, assuming two additional technical lemmas, which we prove in

and

.
In

.
Finally, in

2.
Overview of the proof

Throughout,
n\in\mathbb{N}
will always be a sufficiently large prime number; we will also adopt the standard convention of omitting floors and ceilings whenever they are not essential.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be the bound that we want to show for the independence number and let
A_{p}
be a
p
-random subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
Denoting
\mathcal{X}:={\mathbb{Z}_{n}\choose k}
, we will show that

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\,X\in\mathcal{X}:X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\subseteq A_{p}^{\mathsf{c}}\right)\to 0

as
n\to\infty
, which is equivalent to proving that
\alpha(\Gamma_{p})<k

w.h.p
.

We will follow
Green
’s general approach of partitioning
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{1}\cup\mathcal{X}_{2}\cup\mathcal{X}_{3}
where each sub-collection is defined based on the doubling
\sigma[X]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{X\in\mathcal{X}:\sigma[X]\leq k^{\beta}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{X\in\mathcal{X}:k^{\beta}<\sigma[X]\leq\delta k/10\right\},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{3}

\displaystyle=\left\{X\in\mathcal{X}:\delta k/10<\sigma[X]\right\},

for
\beta=1/40
and some
\delta=o(1)
, and handle each one differently.
Explicitly, we use the union bound to deduce

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\,X\in\mathcal{X}:X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\subseteq A^{\mathsf{c}}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\,X\in\mathcal{X}_{i}:X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\subseteq A^{\mathsf{c}}\right).

It turns out that we can bound the terms related to
\mathcal{X}_{2}
and
\mathcal{X}_{3}
using the same techniques of
Green [17]
and
Green and Morris [18]
; we therefore defer working out the details regarding these terms to
Section

8
.
For the remaining term, we could try to follow the methods in
[
18
]
and take a union bound over the sets in
\mathcal{X}_{1}
:

(3)

\mathbb{P}(\exists\,X\in\mathcal{X}_{1}:X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\subseteq A_{p}^{\mathsf{c}})\leq\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{1}}\mathbb{P}(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\subseteq A_{p}^{\mathsf{c}}).

Observe that, for each
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, the probability in the summand is

\mathbb{P}(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\subseteq A_{p}^{\mathsf{c}})=(1-p)^{|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|}.

Since there are more than
{m/2\choose k}
sets
X
such that
|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|=m
, the right-hand side of (
3
) is at least

\sum_{m=2k-1}^{k^{1+\beta}}{m/2\choose k}(1-p)^{m}\geq{2k\choose k}\exp(-5kp)\to\infty,

as
n\to\infty
, whenever
p=o(1)
and
k\to\infty
.
The conclusion is that any approach that uses the union bound over all sets with small doubling cannot give the optimal upper bound on the independence number for
p
smaller than some explicit constant, like
1/5
.

As we mentioned in the introduction, one of the key new ideas in this paper is to show that there exists a family
\mathcal{F}
of fingerprints such that it suffices to consider only the events
\{F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F\subseteq A_{p}^{\mathsf{c}}\}_{F\in\mathcal{F}}
instead of the collection
\{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\subseteq A_{p}^{\mathsf{c}}\}_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{1}}
.
Trivially,
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{X}_{1}
is such a family, so, to make this strategy work and improve upon taking the union bound over all
X
, we must choose
\mathcal{F}
in a more clever way.

The first property of these fingerprints that we require is that
|\mathcal{F}|
is sufficiently small, where small vaguely means that a union bound over all
F\in\mathcal{F}
works.
One direct way to achieve that is picking each fingerprint
F
to be small, but there is a subtle trade-off between the size of
F
and the upper bound on the probability term
(1-p)^{|F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F|}
.
To circumvent this issue, we can use the fact that we have a bound (even if a polynomially large one) on the doubling of each
X\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
.
In this setting, Freĭman’s theorem
[
14
]
says that
X
is contained in a generalised arithmetic progression
P
such that both the size
s
and dimension
d
of
P
are small.
Recall that a
d
-dimensional generalised arithmetic progression is a set of the form

\bigg\{a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}a_{i}:w_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},\,0\leq w_{i}<\ell_{i}\bigg\}

for some differences
a_{0},\ldots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and side-lengths
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}\in\mathbb{N}
, and
P
is proper when every possible choice of
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{d}\}
corresponds to a distinct element of
P
.
Therefore, instead of choosing
F
directly from
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, we first choose
P
and then select
F
inside
P
.

Now we can state the first requirement for
F\in\mathcal{F}
in more detail.
Let
P
be the generalised arithmetic progression given by Freĭman’s theorem for
X
.
Then,
F
should be small enough compared to
|F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F|
for a union bound over choices of
F\subseteq P
to work:

(4)

\mathbb{P}\big(\exists\,F\subseteq P:F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F\subseteq A_{p}^{\mathsf{c}}\big)\leq{s\choose|F|}(1-p)^{|F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F|}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.
To get to this point, however, we must also choose this generalised arithmetic progression in a previous union bound.
Hence, our second requirement for each
F
is that
F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F
is sufficiently large to pay for the number of choices for the generalised arithmetic progression
P
.

As we can count generalised arithmetic progressions with dimension
d
and size
s
by choosing the
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}
and
l_{1},\ldots,l_{d}
, there are at most
(ns)^{d+1}
of them.
Temporarily ignoring the number of choices for the fingerprint inside the progression (which we already dealt with in (
4
)), this amounts to requiring that
F
satisfies

(ns)^{d+1}(1-p)^{|F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F|}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.
While these conditions may seem too strict – small
F
with large
F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F
for every
X
– this is exactly what we are able to do.

To state the actual
theorem
, we need to relate the dimension of the progression to some notion of dimension for
X
.
The notion that we use is the Freĭman dimension of
X
, but to state its definition, we must first define what are Freĭman homomorphisms and Freĭman isomorphisms.
A Freĭman homomorphism is a function
{\phi:X\to Y}
such that for every
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}\in X
,

a_{1}+b_{1}=a_{2}+b_{2}\quad\text{ implies }\quad\phi(a_{1})+\phi(b_{1})=\phi(a_{2})+\phi(b_{2}).

A Freĭman isomorphism is a bijection
\phi
such that both
\phi
and its inverse
\phi^{-1}
are Freĭman homomorphisms.
The Freĭman dimension of
X
,
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)
, is then defined to be the largest
d\in\mathbb{N}
for which there is a full rank subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that is Freĭman isomorphic to
X
.

It is furthermore useful to define the robustness of the Freĭman dimension of
X
: we say that
X
has
\varepsilon
-robust Freĭman dimension
d
if
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)\geq d
and there is no
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
such that
|X^{\prime}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)|X|
and
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X^{\prime})<d
.
In words, this means that the Freĭman dimension of
X
is at least
d
even if we remove an
\varepsilon
proportion of its elements.

With these definitions, we can state our fingerprint
theorem
:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
n
be a large enough prime and let
k,d\in\mathbb{N}
.
For every
0<\gamma<1/2
, there exists
C=C(\gamma)>0
such that the following holds for all
m\geq(d+1)k/2
and
C/k<\varepsilon<\gamma
.
For each
d
-dimensional generalised arithmetic progression
P\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, there exists a collection
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{k,m,\varepsilon}(P)
of subsets of
P
satisfying:

(1)

For every
F\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

(5)

|F|\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}\sqrt{m\log m}\quad\text{ and }\quad|F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F|\geq\frac{(1-\gamma)(d+1)k}{2}.

(2)

For all
X\in\binom{P}{k}
with
|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|\leq m
and
\varepsilon
-robust Freĭman dimension
d
, there exists
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F\subseteq X
.

We will deduce this
theorem
from the following supersaturation result.

Theorem 2.2
.

For every
0<\gamma<1
, there exists a constant
{c=c(\gamma)>0}
such that, for every sufficiently large set
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, every
d\in\mathbb{N}
and every
0<\varepsilon<\gamma
, the following holds.
If
X
has
\varepsilon
-robust Freĭman dimension
d
and
Y\subseteq X+X
satisfies

(6)

\big|\big\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}:x_{1}+x_{2}\not\in Y\big\}\big|\leq c\varepsilon|X|^{2},

then
|Y|\geq(1-\gamma)(d+1)|X|/2
.

In words, whenever
Y\approx X+X
in the sense of (
6
), then it also (almost) satisfies the lower bound given by Freĭman’s lemma (
Lemma

1.2
), up to a factor of 1/2.
Once we have this
theorem
, the proof of
Theorem

2.1
is substantially simpler than usual for a similarly strong container theorem.
In fact, it is also the only part of the proof that we could omit by using the container theorem for sumsets
[
9
, Theorem 4.2]
.
The self-contained proof is so simple that we include it here in the overview to motivate the usefulness of
Theorem

2.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.1
assuming
Theorem

2.2
.

Our aim is to show that, for every
X\in\binom{P}{k}
with
|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|\leq m
and
\varepsilon
-robust Freĭman dimension
d
, there exists a subset
F\subseteq X
such that (
5
) holds.
We will show that a
q
-random subset
F
of
X
satisfies the first inequality by a suitable choice of
q
, and the second one via an application of
Theorem

2.2
with
Y=F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F
.

In order to apply
Theorem

2.2
to
Y=F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F
, we need to show that
Y
satisfies (
6
).
To this end, we will first prove that a random choice of
F
makes it unlikely for
F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F
to miss any
y
such that

(7)

\rho_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}(y)\geq\frac{2\varepsilon k^{2}}{Cm},

where

\rho_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}(y)=\big|\big\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}:x_{1}\neq x_{2},\,x_{1}+x_{2}=y\big\}\big|

is the number of pairs of distinct elements of
X
that sum to a given
y\in X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X
.
Hereon, we will refer to the
y
satisfying (
7
) as the popular elements of
X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X
.
We will take a
q
-random subset where either

(8)

q=\frac{C\sqrt{m\log m}}{2\varepsilon k}

if the right-hand side is at most 1, and
q=1
otherwise (a trivial case which we will ignore).

Notice that we can upper bound the number of missing pairs by:

(9)

\big|\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}:x_{1}+x_{2}\not\in F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F\}\big|\leq|X|+\sum_{y\in(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\setminus(F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F)}\rho_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}(y)

where the term
|X|
comes from the pairs
(x,x)
for
x\in X
.
Once we have proved that
F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F
contains all popular
y\in X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X
, we will have an upper bound on
\rho_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}(y)
for every
y\in(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\setminus(F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F)
.
Inserting this into (
9
), we deduce that the number of missing pairs is at most

(10)

|X|+\sum_{y\in(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\setminus(F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F)}\rho_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}(y)<|X|+\frac{2\varepsilon k^{2}}{Cm}|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|\leq c\varepsilon|X|^{2},

if we take
C\geq 3/c
, where
c=c(\gamma)
is the constant in
Theorem

2.2
, since
|X|=k
,
|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|\leq m
and
\varepsilon k>C
.
The upper bound in (
10
) is what we need to apply
Theorem

2.2
; doing so gives

|F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F|\geq\frac{(1-\gamma)(\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)+1)k}{2},

from which we can use our assumption that
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)\geq d
to complete the proof.

It therefore only remains to prove our claim that with positive probability
F
contains all popular elements of
X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X
while also being sufficiently small.
Notice that our choice of
F
as a
q
-random subset of
X
implies, for each
y\in X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X
, that

(11)

\mathbb{P}(y\not\in F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F)=(1-q^{2})^{\rho_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}(y)/2}

because (i) each pair
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}
that satisfies
x_{1}+x_{2}=y
and
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
is counted twice in
\rho_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}(y)
and (ii) the probability that such a pair is chosen in
F
is
q^{2}
.

Take
B_{F}
to be the random variable counting the number of
y\in X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X
such that

\rho_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}(y)\geq\frac{2\varepsilon k^{2}}{Cm}\quad\text{ and }\quad y\not\in F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F.

By linearity of expectation and (
11
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[B_{F}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\\
\rho_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}(y)\geq 2\varepsilon k^{2}/Cm\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(y\not\in F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F)\leq m(1-q^{2})^{\varepsilon k^{2}/Cm}\leq m\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon q^{2}k^{2}}{Cm}\right),

where we used
|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|\leq m
to bound the number of terms in the sum.

Since
\varepsilon q^{2}k^{2}/(Cm)=(C/4\varepsilon)\log m
by our choice (
8
) of
q
, it follows by Markov’s inequality that

\mathbb{P}(B_{F}>0)\leq m^{-1},

where we also used that
\varepsilon<1
and
C\geq 8
.
Using Chernoff’s inequality to bound the probability that
|F|>2qk
, we deduce that

\mathbb{P}(|F|>2qk)+\mathbb{P}(B_{F}>0)<1,

which proves that there exists a fingerprint
F\subseteq X
satisfying (
5
), as required.
∎

Before moving on with the overview, let us briefly discuss the robustness property in
Theorem

2.2
.
This condition may at first seem unnatural, but the following simple construction shows that some form of robustness is necessary: take
X
to be the union of
d-1
random points with a progression
P
, and define
Y=P+P
.
We have simultaneously
w.h.p
. that
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)=d
,
|Y|\approx 2|X|
and the sum of almost all pairs of elements of
X
are in
Y
.

With
Theorem

2.1
in hand, we can now check that for the family
\mathcal{F}
of all sets satisfying (
5
), our requirements for the fingerprints
F
are satisfied.
Recall that what we need from the size of the sumset is

(ns)^{d+1}(1-p)^{|F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F|}\to 0,

where
s=|P|
and
d=\dim(P)
.
Modern formulations of Freĭman’s theorem tells us that we can take
s\leq\exp(\sigma^{1+o(1)})k
, which in our range of
\sigma
and
k
corresponds to
n^{o(1)}
.
However, we can only apply
Theorem

2.1
to sets
X\subseteq P
such that
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)\geq\dim(P)
(ignoring the robustness for the moment).
Standard formulations of Freĭman’s theorem only guarantee that
\dim(P)
is at most
\sigma[X]
, which would not be good enough for us.

Fortunately,
Chang [10]
proved a version of Freĭman’s theorem that guarantees that
\dim(P)
is at most
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)
, at the cost of a weaker bound on the size of
P
as compared to the more recent results of
Schoen [25]
and
Sanders [24]
.
The impact of the suboptimal size of the progression is a slight reduction in the range of
p
for which our proof works.

Theorem 2.3

(
Chang [10]
, see
[
19
, Proposition 1.3]
)
.

There exists
C^{\prime}>0
such that for all finite subsets
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
with
|X|\geq 2
and
\sigma[X]\leq\sigma
, there is a
d
-dimensional generalised arithmetic progression
P
such that
X\subseteq P
,

|P|\leq\exp\hskip-2.0pt\big(C^{\prime}\sigma^{2}(\log\sigma)^{3}\big)|X|

and
{d\leq\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)}
.

Now, we can use the lower bound on
|F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F|
given by
Theorem

2.1
to obtain

(1-p)^{|F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F|}\leq\exp\hskip-2.0pt\big(-(1-\gamma)(d+1)kp/2\big),

which, choosing
k=(2+o(1))\log_{1/(1-p)}n
(a little larger than
(2\log n)/p
), is at most

(12)

\exp\big(-(1+\gamma)(d+1)\log n\big).

Replacing this back in the previous equation, and recalling that
s=n^{o(1)}
, thus yields

(ns)^{d+1}(1-p)^{|F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F|}\leq n^{-\gamma}\to 0.

The attentive reader may have noticed that
Chang
’s
theorem
is stated for subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
instead of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
To use it for subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, we will use instead a version of Green–Ruzsa’s theorem (Freĭman’s theorem for Abelian groups) due to
Cwalina and Schoen [12]
.
It does not bound
\dim(P)\leq\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)
directly, though, but it yields a proper progression, which we can combine with a
theorem
by
Tao and Vu [28]
to obtain what we need (see
Corollary

8.4
).

Moreover, it is not true that every
X
has
\varepsilon
-robust Freĭman dimension equal to
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)
.
This is not a problem, however, since it is straightforward to prove (see
Proposition

8.3
) that every set
X
has a large enough subset
X^{\prime}
with
\varepsilon
-robust Freĭman dimension
d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
.

Finally, let us check that the size of
F
given to us by
Theorem

2.1
is compatible with the range of
p
in
Theorem

1.1
.
To do so, we need to show that, as
n\to\infty
,

{s\choose|F|}(1-p)^{|F\mathbin{\hat{+}}F|}\to 0.

As we already know from (
12
) that the second term is at most
n^{-d}
, we need

(13)

{s\choose|F|}\leq s^{|F|}\leq\exp\big(C\varepsilon^{-1}(m\log m)^{1/2}\log s\big)=n^{o(1)}.

The second inequality in (
13
) follows from (
5
) in
Theorem

2.1
, while the third is a consequence of our choice of
k
and the bound on
s
given by
Theorem

2.3
.
Indeed, we have

(14)

C\varepsilon^{-1}(m\log m)^{1/2}\log s\ll k^{3/4}=o(\log n),

because
m\leq k^{1+\beta}
for
X\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
, which implies that
\log s\leq k^{3\beta}
, and we can take
\varepsilon=k^{-2\beta}
and
C
to be a constant.
Our choice of
\beta=1/40
is therefore more than sufficient to prove (
14
).

At this point, one might ask why we decided to keep track of the constant
C
up to the final computation.
Note that it is crucial that the value of
C
does not increase too quickly with
d
growing, since otherwise (
14
) would not hold for large
d
.
Recall that in the proof of
Theorem

2.1
, we took
C\approx c^{-1}
.
The constant
c
is essentially given to us by our supersaturation result and its value is tied to how many translates we need in our approximate bound for Freĭman’s lemma (
Theorem

1.3
).

To see where the dependency of
c
on the number of translates comes from, consider the contrapositive of
Theorem

2.2
: if the set
Y
is small, then it misses many pairs
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}
.
By
Theorem

1.3
, we can find a small
T\subseteq X
such that

|X+T|-|Y|\geq\gamma(d+1)|X|.

The pigeonhole principle then ensures us that there is some
x\in T
satisfying

\big|(X+x)\setminus Y\big|\geq\frac{\gamma(d+1)|X|}{|T|}=c|X|

for
c=\gamma(d+1)/|T|
.
Now, if we add the
c|X|
pairs determined by
(X+x)\setminus Y
to our collection of missing pairs
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}
such that
x_{1}+x_{2}\not\in Y
, remove
x
from
X
and repeat this procedure
t
times, we would have at least
c|X|t
missing pairs in total.
Recalling that
X
has
\varepsilon
-robust Freĭman dimension
d
, we can take
t=\varepsilon|X|
while maintaining
\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)\geq d
, and hence obtain
c\varepsilon|X|^{2}
missing pairs, as required.

The above sketch proof of
Theorem

2.2
shows that to prove our supersaturation result with
c
being an absolute constant, we really need the size of
T
to be a linear function of
d
, as in
Theorem

1.3
.
In fact, for (
14
), a bound of the form
d^{O(1)}
would suffice.
Unfortunately, the
theorem
of
Jing and Mudgal
(
Theorem

1.4
) gives a super-polynomial dependency on
d
.
Nevertheless, we use their result to prove
Theorem

2.2
in the range
d=O(1)
.

The only missing part in this overview is a proof of
Theorem

1.3
itself.
Instead of sketching it, we complete the picture with the (short) proof of its weaker version in the next section.

3.
A simple proof of a weaker Freĭman’s lemma via few translates

This
section
is dedicated to proving a weaker form of
Theorem

1.3
.
Although it is not strong enough to prove the upper bound in our main
theorem
, it does imply a weaker version where the constant
2
in (
1
) is replaced by a
6
.
The deduction of this weaker result is the same as that of
Theorem

1.1
(see
Sections

7
and
8
) simply replacing
Theorem

1.3
by
Theorem

3.1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
d,r\in\mathbb{N}
and let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite set with
\operatorname{rank}(X)\geq r
.
There exists a set
T\subseteq X
such that
|T|\leq r/2+1
and

|X+T|\geq\frac{r|X|}{6}.

The idea of the proof is to add elements of
X
to
T
one by one, picking in each step a new translate that adds a substantial number of new elements to the sumset.
This suggests a greedy argument, picking
x\in X\setminus T
that increases the size of the sumset the most.
However, it is not clear how to show that we can make substantial progress for a sufficient number of steps.
Previous arguments, such as the one by
Fox, Luo, Pham, and Zhou [13, Theorem 1.1]
, show that if progress stops, then
X
must have small doubling; as we must handle polynomially large
\sigma[X]
, such strategies do not work in our case.
Instead, we adopt a variation of this idea that incorporates the geometry of the set, allowing us to reach conclusions that do not rely on the doubling of the set and depend only on its rank.

Roughly speaking, in the proof of
Proposition

3.2
below, we will show that we can add a new element to
T
so that it increases the size of
X+T
by a factor proportional to
|X\setminus\operatorname{span}(T)|
.
Notice that, as long as
|T|<\operatorname{rank}(X)
, we can take a non-trivial step.

Now, if we have enough steps that add
|X|/3
elements to the sumset, then after
r/2
steps we will have both the bound for the sumset and for
|T|
in
Theorem

3.1
.
Otherwise, as we will show that every step makes at least
|X\setminus\operatorname{span}(T)|/2
“progress”, it follows that, after the last “good” step, we must have
|X\cap\operatorname{span}(T)|\geq|X|/3
.
In this scenario, we define

X^{*}=X\cap\operatorname{span}(T)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad X^{\prime}=X\setminus\operatorname{span}(T),

and observe that

(15)

\operatorname{rank}(X^{\prime})\geq\operatorname{rank}(X)-\operatorname{rank}(T)\geq r-r/2=r/2,

since
|T|\leq r/2
.
At this point, we now discard our old
T
and greedily choose elements of a new set of translates
Z\subseteq X^{\prime}
each increasing the rank of
X^{*}\cup Z
by one.
Each new element that we add yields a disjoint, translated copy of
X^{*}
in the sumset.
We then obtain

|X+Z|\geq|X^{*}+Z|=\frac{r|X^{*}|}{2}\geq\frac{r|X|}{6},

because, by (
15
), we can greedily add
r/2
elements to
Z
, and
|X^{*}|\geq|X|/3
.

We proceed by formalizing the notion that either the greedy argument suffices, or a significant part of
X
lies in the span of the elements already in
T
.
The version we state below is more general than we need to prove
Theorem

3.1
because we will reuse it when proving
Theorem

1.3
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
d,r\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\gamma>0
and let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite set with
\operatorname{rank}(X)\geq r
.
If
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies
0\in T
,
\operatorname{rank}(T)<r
and
|X\cap\operatorname{span}(T)|\leq\gamma|X|
, then there exists an element
x_{*}\in X
such that

\big|X+\big(T\cup\{x_{*}\}\big)\big|\geq|X+T|+\frac{(1-\gamma)|X|}{2}.

The key idea here is to find a co-dimension 1 hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
which intersects
X
exactly in
X\cap\operatorname{span}(T)
.
We can find such a hyperplane because
X
is finite and
\operatorname{rank}(T)<\operatorname{rank}(X)
.
Note that the two open half-spaces defined by
\mathcal{H}
induce a partition
X^{\prime}=X_{+}\cup X_{-}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
|X_{+}|\geq|X_{-}|
, and our goal is now to add, for each point in
X_{+}
, a new element to the sumset.
To achieve this, we let
u
be a normal vector of
\mathcal{H}
, choose
y_{+}\in X_{+}
to maximise
\left\langle y_{+},u\right\rangle
, and observe that
y_{+}+X_{+}
is disjoint from
X+T
.

Proof.

First, choose a vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be the normal of the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
discussed above.
It should satisfy the following properties

(1)

u\neq 0
,

(2)

{\left\langle z,u\right\rangle=0}
, for all
z\in\operatorname{span}(T)
, and

(3)

\left\langle z,u\right\rangle\neq 0
, for all
z\in X\setminus\operatorname{span}(T)
.

There is a
u
satisfying
items

1
and
2
since
0\in T
and
\operatorname{rank}(T)<r
.
We can furthermore find a
u
for which
item

3
also holds because there are only finitely many elements in
X\setminus\operatorname{span}(T)
, as
X
itself is finite.

We partition
X=X_{+}\cup X_{*}\cup X_{-}
according to the position of its elements relative to the hyperplane defined by
\left\langle x,u\right\rangle=0
, i.e.

\displaystyle X_{+}=\{x\in X:\left\langle x,u\right\rangle>0\},

\displaystyle X_{*}=\{x\in X:\left\langle x,u\right\rangle=0\},

\displaystyle X_{-}=\{x\in X:\left\langle x,u\right\rangle<0\}.

Assume without loss of generality that
|X_{+}|\geq|X_{-}|
by swapping the sign of
u
if necessary, and take
y_{+}\in X_{+}
to be a maximiser of
f(y)=\left\langle y,u\right\rangle
.

We claim that
X_{+}+y_{+}
is disjoint from
X+T
.
In fact, if we let
a\in X_{+},b\in X
and
c\in T
, then:

\displaystyle\left\langle a+y_{+},u\right\rangle

\displaystyle>\left\langle y_{+},u\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\text{as }a\in X_{+},

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle b,u\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\text{by the maximality of }y_{+},

\displaystyle=\left\langle b+c,u\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\text{as we chose }u\text{ with }c\perp u.

Therefore, if
|X_{+}|\geq(1-\gamma)|X|/2
, we can pick
x_{*}=y_{+}
and prove the
proposition
:

\displaystyle|X+(T\cup\{x_{*}\})|

\displaystyle\geq|X+T|+|X_{+}+x_{*}|

\displaystyle\geq|X+T|+\frac{(1-\gamma)|X|}{2}.

Otherwise, since we took
|X_{+}|\geq|X_{-}|
and
X_{+}\cup X_{*}\cup X_{-}
partition
X
, we have

(16)

|X_{*}|=|X|-|X_{-}|-|X_{+}|>\gamma|X|.

By our choice of
u
and the definition of
X_{*}
, we have
X_{*}=X\cap\operatorname{span}(T)
, which, by (
16
), contradicts our assumption that
|X\cap\operatorname{span}(T)|\leq\gamma|X|
.
∎

The 1-dimensional perspective we took in this proof suggests that instead of adding a single maximiser
y_{+}
to
T
at each step, we should pick both the maximiser
y_{+}
and the minimiser
y_{-}
.
Unfortunately, if we add
\{y_{+},y_{-}\}
to
T
, then we could increase
\operatorname{rank}(T)
by two instead of one, causing the greedy argument to run for only half as many steps.

We need the following simple lemma to handle the case when
|X\cap\operatorname{span}(T)|
is large.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
d,r,s\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\gamma>0
and let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite set with
\operatorname{rank}(X)\geq r
.
If
X_{*}\subseteq X
with
\operatorname{rank}(X_{*})<s
, then there exists a set
Z\subseteq X\setminus X_{*}
such that

|X_{*}+Z|\geq(r-s)|X_{*}|

and
|Z|\leq r-s
.

Proof.

First, note that for any set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\operatorname{rank}(S)<s
, we have
\operatorname{rank}(\operatorname{span}(S))\leq s
.
That is, taking the
\operatorname{span}
of a set that does not contain
0
may increase its rank by 1.

We will construct
Z=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{r-s}\}\subseteq X\setminus X_{*}
one element at a time, also defining

Z_{i}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{i}\}\quad\text{ and }\quad W_{i}=\operatorname{span}(X_{*}\cup Z_{i})

for
i\in\{0,\ldots,r-s\}
.
Notice that if
i<r-s
, then
\operatorname{rank}(W_{i})\leq s+i<r
, by our assumption on
X^{*}
and the definition of
W_{i}
.
Therefore, there exists
z_{i+1}\in X\setminus W_{i}
, as
\operatorname{rank}(X)\geq r
.
Since
X^{*}\subseteq W_{i}
, this implies that
X^{*}+z_{i+1}
and
W_{i}
are disjoint, and hence that
X^{*}+z_{i+1}
is disjoint from
X^{*}+Z_{i}
.
This readily implies the
lemma
because

|X_{*}+Z|=\sum_{i=1}^{r-s}|X_{*}+z_{i}|=(r-s)|X_{*}|,

where in the first equality we repeatedly used that
X_{*}+z_{i+1}
and
X_{*}+Z_{i}
are disjoint.
∎

The proof of
Theorem

3.1
now follows easily from
Proposition

3.2
and
Lemma

3.3
:

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

We may assume that
0\in X
; notice that this translation does not change
\operatorname{rank}(X)
.
First, we construct sets
T_{i}=\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{i}\}\subseteq X
by choosing
x_{0}=0
and
x_{i+1}
as given by
Proposition

3.2
.
In details, let
t
be the first index for which

(17)

|X\cap\operatorname{span}(T_{t})|>\frac{|X|}{3}.

Note that while (
17
) does not hold, we have
\operatorname{rank}(T_{i})<\operatorname{rank}(X)
since
T_{i}\subseteq X
.
We may therefore apply
Proposition

3.2
to
T_{i}
with
\gamma=1/3
to define
x_{i}
, which then implies that

(18)

|X+T_{i}|\geq\frac{|T_{i}|\,|X|}{3}=\frac{(i+1)|X|}{3}

for all
i\leq t
.
Hence, if
t\geq r/2
, then, by (
18
), we have

|X+T_{t}|\geq\frac{(t+1)|X|}{3}\geq\frac{r|X|}{6},

and taking
T=T_{t}
concludes the proof.
We may therefore assume that
t<r/2
.

In this case, we want to apply
Lemma

3.3
with
X_{*}=X\cap\operatorname{span}(T_{t})
and
s=r/2
.
Note that, as
\operatorname{rank}(T_{t})\leq t
and
0\in T_{t}
, we have

\operatorname{rank}(X_{*})\leq\operatorname{rank}(\operatorname{span}(T_{t}))\leq t<r/2,

where in the last inequality we used our assumption that
t<r/2
.
This application yields a set
Z\subseteq X\setminus X_{*}
such that
|Z|\leq r-s=r/2
and

\displaystyle|X+Z|

\displaystyle\geq|X_{*}+Z|

\displaystyle\text{ because }X_{*}\subseteq X,

\displaystyle\geq\frac{r|X_{*}|}{2}

\displaystyle\text{ by \lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{stmt:disjoint-translates-with-X*}},

\displaystyle\geq\frac{r|X|}{6}

\displaystyle\text{ since }|X_{*}|\geq|X|/3\text{ by \eqref{eq:greedy-condition-for-weaker-freiman}}.

Taking
T=Z
concludes the proof.
∎

4.
Improving the bound for Freĭman’s lemma via few-translates

To obtain the sharp upper bound for
\alpha(\Gamma_{p})
, the bound
|X+T|\geq r|X|/6
is not enough; we need at least
|X+T|\geq(1-5\gamma)(r+1)|X|/2
for small
\gamma>0
.

5.
Offsetting the loss of the zero fibre: proof of

As we remarked in the previous section, we offset the loss of the zero fibre by adding two carefully selected points from each non-empty fibre to the final set of translates.
These points are the maximiser and minimiser of the linear function
x\mapsto\left\langle x,u^{*}\right\rangle
in each fibre.
Let
Z=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\}
.
For each
z_{i}\in Z
, we choose

to be, respectively, a maximiser and a minimiser of

in
\llbracket z_{i}\rrbracket

Recall that

is now defined as the projection of

onto

, where

and

come from the statement of the

Similarly to the proof of

\displaystyle\llbracket 0\rrbracket_{+}=\big\{x\in\llbracket 0\rrbracket:\left\langle x,u^{*}\right\rangle>0\big\}\quad\text{and}\quad\llbracket 0\rrbracket_{-}=\big\{x\in\llbracket 0\rrbracket:\left\langle x,u^{*}\right\rangle<0\big\},

which complement the “null part”
\llbracket 0\rrbracket_{*}=\{x\in\llbracket 0\rrbracket:\left\langle x,u^{*}\right\rangle=0\}

Each pair of minimiser and maximiser we put in the set of translates will add to the sumset a translated copy of the sets
\llbracket 0\rrbracket_{+}
and
\llbracket 0\rrbracket_{-}

As they form a partition of
\llbracket 0\rrbracket\setminus\llbracket 0\rrbracket_{*}

Showing this only requires the following simple geometric observations.
The first says that the translates
\llbracket 0\rrbracket+Y_{i}
and
\llbracket 0\rrbracket+Y_{j}

Observation 5.3

Proof of

.

Recall that

Lemma 6.1
.

Let

For every finite

\sum_{w\in U+U}\max_{u\in(w-U)\cap U}f(u)\geq\frac{\operatorname{rank}(U)+1}{2}\sum_{u\in U}f(u).

We will also need a simple observation to repeat one of the steps in the proof of Freĭman’s lemma and prove

.

Observation 6.2
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite set.
If
v\in U
is a vertex of
\operatorname{conv}(U)
and

, then there exists a hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
such that
v
and
U^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{H}
are on different sides of
\mathcal{H}
.
Moreover,
|\mathcal{H}\cap U^{\prime}|\geq\operatorname{rank}(U^{\prime})

Proof.

Write
\operatorname{conv}(U^{\prime})=\bigcap_{\mathcal{H}\in\mathbf{H}}\mathcal{H}^{-}
, where
\mathbf{H}
is the collection of hyperplanes supporting the facets of
\operatorname{conv}(U^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{H}^{-}
denotes a closed half-space defined by
\mathcal{H}
.
Since
v\not\in\operatorname{conv}(U^{\prime})
, there exists
\mathcal{H}\in\mathbf{H}
such that

, so

and
U^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{H}
are on different sides of
\mathcal{H}
.
The second part of the statement follows from
\mathcal{H}
intersecting a facet of
\operatorname{conv}(U^{\prime})
and the fact that every facet contains at least
\operatorname{rank}(U^{\prime})

∎

Now, we can prove

.

Proof of
Lemma

6.1
.

We prove the

by induction on
|U|
.
In the base case, we have an empty set, so the left-hand side of (

We can therefore assume that for every
U^{\prime}\subsetneq U
, we have (
54
).

For the induction step, we proceed similarly to the standard proof of Freĭman’s lemma.
The main difference is that instead of choosing an arbitrary element from
V

\operatorname{conv}(U)
, we must choose a vertex considering the weight function
f
.
To be precise, we choose a vertex
v\in V

(55)

f(v)\leq\frac{1}{s+1}\sum_{u\in U}f(u),

(s+1)\min_{v\in V}f(v)\leq|V|\min_{v\in V}f(v)\leq\sum_{v\in V}f(v)\leq\sum_{u\in U}f(u).

{\operatorname{rank}(U^{\prime})=s}

Consider first the case

\sum_{w\in U^{\prime}+U^{\prime}}\max_{u\in(w-U)\cap U}f(u)\geq\sum_{w\in U^{\prime}+U^{\prime}}\max_{u\in(w-U^{\prime})\cap U^{\prime}}f(u)\geq\frac{s+1}{2}\sum_{u^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}}f(u^{\prime})

.
For the remaining elements of

|P|\leq\exp(k^{1/5})=:s
for every
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
by (
80
), we can group terms in the right-hand side of (
77
) based on
d=\dim(P)
to deduce that, by (
87
), we have

(88)

\mathbb{P}(\exists\,X\in\mathcal{X}_{1}:X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\subseteq A_{p}^{\mathsf{c}})\leq\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}s(ns)^{d+1}\exp(k^{4/5})(1-p)^{(1-2\gamma)(d+1)k/2},

where we have bounded the number of
d
-dimensional generalised arithmetic progressions in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with size at most
s
by
s(ns)^{d+1}
.

It is therefore enough to prove that the right-hand side of (
88
) goes to
0
as
n\to\infty
.
Note that, as
k=(2+4\delta)\log_{\frac{1}{1-p}}n
, we have

(1-p)^{k/2}=n^{-(1+2\delta)},

which combined with a suitably small choice of
\gamma=\gamma(\delta)
, implies that

(89)

n^{d+1}(1-p)^{(1-2\gamma)(d+1)k/2}=n^{d+1-(1-2\gamma)(1+2\delta)(d+1)}\leq n^{-\delta(d+1)}.

The final observation is that it follows from
s=\exp(k^{1/5})
that there is
1>\nu>0
such that

(90)

s^{d+2}\exp(k^{4/5})\leq\exp\big((d+1)(\log n)^{1-\nu}\big)

since
k\leq 3(\log n)/p
for
\delta<1/4
and
n
is sufficiently large.
The value of
\nu
depends on the exponent of the
\log n
term in our choice of
p
, and
\nu=0.18
works for
p\geq(\log n)^{-1/80}
.

Combining (
89
) with (
90
), we show, for sufficiently large
n
, that (
88
) is at most

\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}s(ns)^{d+1}\exp(k^{4/5})(1-p)^{(1-2\gamma)(d+1)k/2}\leq\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}n^{-\delta d/2}

which goes to 0 as
n\to\infty
, as we wanted to show.
∎

8.2.
Bounding the probability over choices in
\mathcal{X}_{2}

Our goal is to show that the term corresponding to choices in
\mathcal{X}_{2}
tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
.
We emphasize that, in this
section
, no new ideas, or even modification of previous, existing results, are needed.
We just need to use the following result of
Green [17]
.

Proposition 8.5

(
[
17
]
, see
[
18
, Proposition 6.1]
)
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\geq 2k-1
, there exists
r\in\mathbb{N}
with

(91)

r\leq\min\{4m/k,k\}\quad\text{ and }\quad r\leq\frac{2m}{k}+\frac{1}{k}\binom{r}{2},

such that

(92)

\big|\mathcal{X}_{2}^{(m)}\big|\leq n^{r}k^{4k},

where
\mathcal{X}_{2}^{(m)}=\{X\in\mathcal{X}_{2}:|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|=m\}
.

With
Proposition

8.5
, we can take a union bound over each
X\in\mathcal{X}_{2}
to obtain

(93)

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\,X\in\mathcal{X}_{2}:X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\subseteq A_{p}^{\mathsf{c}}\right)\leq\sum_{m=k^{1+1/40}}^{\delta k^{2}/10}\big|\mathcal{X}_{2}^{(m)}\big|\,(1-p)^{m}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

and we prove the last step below.

Proof of (
93
).

First, we bound the
r
in
Proposition

8.5
using (
91
):

r\leq\frac{2m}{k}+\frac{1}{k}\left(\frac{4m}{k}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{m}{k}\left(2+2\delta\right)

since sets
X\in\mathcal{X}_{2}^{(m)}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{2}
satisfy
m/k=\sigma[X]\leq\delta k/10
.
Now, applying
Proposition

8.5
to each
\mathcal{X}_{2}^{(m)}
, we obtain that

(94)

\big|\mathcal{X}_{2}^{(m)}\big|\leq n^{r}k^{4k}\leq n^{(2+2\delta)m/k+5(\log n)^{1/50}\log\log n}

and the last inequality follows from
k\leq(\log n)^{51/50}
, which is implied by
p\geq(\log n)^{-1/80}
.

Notice that it follows from
k=(2+4\delta)\log_{\frac{1}{1-p}}n
that

(95)

(1-p)^{m}=n^{-(2+4\delta)m/k}.

Together with (
94
) and the fact that
m/k=\sigma[X]\geq k^{1/40}\geq(\log n)^{1/40}
, (
95
) yields

\big|\mathcal{X}_{2}^{(m)}\big|\,(1-p)^{m}\leq n^{-\delta m/k}

from which the result follows by summing over all
k^{1+1/40}\leq m\leq\delta k^{2}/10
.
∎

8.3.
Bounding the probability over choices in
\mathcal{X}_{3}

Similarly to the previous case, we start with a union bound

(96)

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\,X\in\mathcal{X}_{3}:X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\subseteq A_{p}^{\mathsf{c}}\right)\leq\sum_{m=\delta k^{2}/10}^{k^{2}}\big|\mathcal{X}_{3}^{(m)}\big|\,(1-p)^{m},

letting
\mathcal{X}_{3}^{(m)}
be the sub-collection of
\mathcal{X}_{3}
consisting of subsets
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with
|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|=m
.
We will need the following slight strengthening of Proposition 5.1 of
[
18
]
.

Proposition 8.6
.

Let
\delta>0
be sufficiently small and let
\eta>0
.
If
k\leq(\log n)^{2-\eta}
and
m\geq\delta k^{2}/10
, then

\big|\mathcal{X}_{3}^{(m)}\big|\leq n^{(2+\delta+o(1))m/k},

as
k\to\infty
, where
\mathcal{X}_{3}^{(m)}=\{X\in\mathcal{X}_{3}:|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|=m\}
.

The proof of
Proposition

8.6
is essentially the same as that in
[
18
]
; we only optimize constants and exponents.
Therefore, we defer its presentation to
Appendix

B
.

Proof that (
96
)
\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Observe that

k\leq\frac{3\log n}{p}\leq(\log n)^{41/40}\leq(\log n)^{2-\eta},

by our choice of
p\geq(\log n)^{-1/80}
.
Hence, we can apply
Proposition

8.6
to bound, for every
m\geq\delta k^{2}/10
, the size of
\mathcal{X}_{3}^{(m)}
by

\big|\mathcal{X}_{3}^{(m)}\big|\leq n^{(2+\delta+o(1))m/k}\leq n^{(2+2\delta)m/k}

where the last inequality holds for large
k
.
We can bound each term of (
96
) by:

\big|\mathcal{X}_{3}^{(m)}\big|\,(1-p)^{m}\leq n^{(2+2\delta)m/k}n^{-(2-4\delta)m/k}\leq n^{-\delta m/k}.

Summing over
m\geq\delta k^{2}/10
yields the desired result.
∎

9.
The lower bound

In this
section
, we prove the lower bound in
Theorem

1.1
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
n
be a prime number and let
p=p(n)
satisfy
1/2\geq p\geq n^{-o(1)}
.
The random Cayley sum graph
\Gamma_{p}
of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
satisfies

\alpha(\Gamma_{p})\geq\big(2+o(1)\big)\log_{\frac{1}{1-p}}n

with high probability as
n\to\infty
.

The proof of this result is significantly easier than the upper bound.
In fact, using only the pseudorandom properties of
\Gamma_{p}
is enough to obtain
\alpha(\Gamma_{p})\geq\big(1/2+o(1)\big)(\log n)/p
(see
[
1
]
and
[
2
, Corollary 2.2]
).
To improve the leading constant to
2
, we use both the randomness (as opposed to only the pseudorandomness) and the fact that we can restrict our attention to any sub-collection of potential independent sets in
\Gamma_{p}
.

More precisely, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
Z_{k}
to be the random variable counting all independent
k
-sets in
\Gamma_{p}
with maximal doubling, that is

(97)

Z_{k}=\left|\left\{X\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}:X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X\subset A_{p}^{c}\right\}\right|

where

(98)

\mathcal{Z}_{k}=\left\{X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}:|X|=k,\,|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|=\binom{k}{2}\right\}.

If
Z_{k}>0
, then
\alpha(\Gamma_{p})\geq k
regardless of the potential independent
k
-sets that
Z_{k}
overlooks.

In order to prove the lower bound, it is enough to show that
\operatorname{Var}(Z_{k})=o(\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]^{2})
since

\mathbb{P}(\alpha(\Gamma_{p})\geq k)\geq\mathbb{P}(Z_{k}>0)\geq 1-\frac{\operatorname{Var}(Z_{k})}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]^{2}},

by Chebyshev’s inequality.
The first step is to estimate
\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]
, which we do by showing that
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
is large and using linearity of expectation.

Lemma 9.2
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k=o(n^{1/4})
, we have

|\mathcal{Z}_{k}|=\big(1-o(1)\big)\binom{n}{k}.

Proof.

We will (equivalently) show that almost all
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with
|X|=k
satisfy
{|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|=\binom{k}{2}}
.
Observe that if
|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X|<\binom{k}{2}
, then there are distinct
x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}\in X
such that

x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{1}^{\prime}+x_{2}^{\prime}.

Motivated by that observation, define

\mathcal{Q}=\big\{\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}:x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{1}^{\prime}+x_{2}^{\prime},\,\text{ and }x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}\text{ are distinct}\big\},

and let
Y
be a uniformly random
k
-set in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
Taking a union bound over
\mathcal{Q}
yields

\mathbb{P}(Y\not\in\mathcal{Z}_{k})\leq\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{P}(Q\subseteq Y)\leq n^{3}\bigg(\frac{k}{n}\bigg)^{4}=\frac{k^{4}}{n},

where the second inequality is due to the choices of
x_{1},x_{2}
and
x_{1}^{\prime}
determining
x_{2}^{\prime}=x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{1}^{\prime}
for
\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{Q}
.
Since
k=o(n^{1/4})
, the
lemma
follows.
∎

The second
lemma
that we need to prove
Theorem

9.1
gives a bound on
\operatorname{Var}(Z_{k})
.

Lemma 9.3
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
p=p(n)\in(0,1)
, we have

\operatorname{Var}(Z_{k})\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]+\binom{n}{k}\sum_{s=1}^{k}k^{3s}\binom{n}{k-s}(1-p)^{2\binom{k}{2}-ks/2}.

The main step in the proof of
Lemma

9.3
is to show, for every
X\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}
, that

(99)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}\\
Y\sim X\end{subarray}}(1-p)^{|(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\cup(Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y)|}\leq\sum_{s=1}^{k}k^{3s}\binom{n}{k-s}(1-p)^{2\binom{k}{2}-ks/2}

where
Y\sim X
if

(100)

Y\neq X\qquad\text{ and }\qquad(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\cap(Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y)\neq\emptyset.

In order to do that, define

\mathcal{I}(X,Y)=\big\{Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y:(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\cap(Y^{\prime}\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y^{\prime})=\emptyset\big\},

and

(101)

\mathcal{I}^{*}(X,Y)=\big\{Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(X,Y):(y+Y^{\prime})\cap(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\neq\emptyset\,\text{ for all }\,y\in Y\setminus Y^{\prime}\big\}.

The definition of
\mathcal{I}^{*}(X,Y)
is motivated by the following
lemma
, which gives an upper bound on
\big|(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\cap(Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y)\big|
:

Lemma 9.4
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
X,Y\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}
.
If
Y\sim X
, then

(102)

\big|(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\cap(Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y)\big|\leq\frac{k(k-t)}{2},

where
t=\min\big\{|Y^{\prime}|:Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(X,Y)\big\}.

To prove
Lemma

9.4
, we will need the following simple observation about graphs.

Observation 9.5
.

Let
G
be a graph with
k
vertices.
If all maximal independent sets of
G
have at least
t
vertices, then
G
has at most
k(k-t)/2
edges.

Proof.

Take
v\in V(G)
to be a vertex of maximum degree, and
I_{v}\subseteq V(G)
to be a maximal independent set containing
v
.
Then,

t\leq|I_{v}|\leq k-d(v)=k-\Delta(G).

Thus,
\Delta(G)\leq k-t
, and the claimed bound follows.
∎

To deduce
Lemma

9.4
, we will apply
Observation

9.5
to
\Gamma_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}[Y]
, the Cayley sum graph over
X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X
restricted to the vertex set
Y
.

Proof of
Lemma

9.4
.

We claim that

(103)

\big|(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\cap(Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y)\big|=e(\Gamma_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}[Y]),

which not only implies that the collection
\mathcal{I}^{*}(X,Y)
is exactly the collection of maximal independent sets in
\Gamma_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}[Y]
, but also reduces the proof to applying
Observation

9.5
with
t
to
\Gamma_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}[Y]
.
Therefore, we first establish (
103
), and then the characterization of
\mathcal{I}^{*}(X,Y)
.

To show (
103
), recall that, by the definition of the Cayley sum graph,
y_{1}y_{2}\in\binom{\,Y}{2}
is an edge of
\Gamma_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}[Y]
if and only if
y_{1}+y_{2}\in X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X
.
We obtain equality by observing that each sum in
Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y
corresponds to exactly one pair
y_{1}y_{2}\in\binom{\,Y}{2}
, since
|Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y|=\binom{k}{2}
by
Y\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}
.

It follows from (
103
) that
\mathcal{I}(X,Y)
corresponds to independent sets in
\Gamma_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}[Y]
.
Moreover, for every
Y^{*}\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(X,Y)
, we know that there are no
Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(X,Y)
such that
Y^{*}\subsetneq Y^{\prime}
by the condition in (
101
).
Our definition of
t
then corresponds to all maximal independent sets in
\Gamma_{X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X}[Y]
having at least
t
vertices, so we can apply
Observation

9.5
as desired.
∎

With
Lemma

9.4
in hand, the proof of (
99
) now relies on an efficient count of
Y\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}
with
Y\sim X
, for each fixed
X\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}
.
Notice that
\mathcal{I}^{*}(X,Y)
is not empty, as every graph has at least one maximal independent set, so we can fix a set
Y^{*}\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(X,Y)
of minimum size.
Our counting strategy is to consider the choices for elements in
Y^{*}
and then the choices for
Y\setminus Y^{*}
; in fact, a trivial count of all sets
Y^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
suffices, so we only need to count the possible elements in
Y\setminus Y^{*}
efficiently.

In order to bound the choices for
Y\setminus Y^{*}
, notice that for every
y\in Y\setminus Y^{*}
, we have
(y+Y^{*})\cap(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\neq\emptyset
by (
101
).
It follows that there are
y^{*}\in Y^{*}
and distinct
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
such that
y+y^{*}=x_{1}+x_{2}
, or, equivalently,

Y\setminus Y^{*}\subseteq X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X-Y^{*}.

We can therefore choose the elements of
Y\setminus Y^{*}
from a set of size at most

|X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X-Y^{*}|\leq|X|^{2}|Y^{*}|\leq k^{3},

so there are at most
k^{3s}
choices for
Y\setminus Y^{*}
if
s=|Y\setminus Y^{*}|
.
Bounding the number of choices for
Y^{*}
by
\binom{n}{k-s}
yields the bound in (
99
), except for the
(1-p)^{2\binom{k}{2}-ks/2}
term, which we obtain by using
Lemma

9.4
.

We now have all the ingredients to prove
Lemma

9.3
.

Proof of
Lemma

9.3
.

Observe that, via standard calculations, we have that

(104)

\operatorname{Var}(Z_{k})\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]+\sum_{X\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}\\
Y\sim X\end{subarray}}(1-p)^{|(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\cup(Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y)|},

where, recall,
Y\sim X
was defined in (
100
).
We therefore need to show that

(105)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}\\
Y\sim X\end{subarray}}(1-p)^{|(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\cup(Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y)|}\leq\sum_{s=1}^{k}k^{3s}\binom{n}{k-s}(1-p)^{2\binom{k}{2}-ks/2}

for each
X\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}
.

To prove (
105
), fix
X\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}
and, for each
Y\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}
with
Y\sim X
, choose a set
Y^{*}=Y^{*}(X,Y)
of minimum size.
If we group the sets
Y
by the size of their corresponding
Y^{*}
, we can count them by first enumerating the choices for
Y^{*}
and then the choices for
Y\setminus Y^{*}
.
For fixed
s=|Y\setminus Y^{*}|=k-|Y^{*}|
, there are at most
\binom{n}{k-s}
choices for
Y^{*}
, and there are at most
k^{3s}
choices for
Y\setminus Y^{*}
since
Y\setminus Y^{*}\subseteq X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X-Y^{*}
.

We then bound the size of the union in (
105
) by applying
Lemma

9.4
to the pair
(X,Y)

\big|(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\cap(Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y)\big|\leq\frac{ks}{2}

which means that we can ignore the case
s=0
because it would contradict
Y\sim X
.
A trivial inclusion-exclusion now yields the bound we need for the size of the union:

(106)

\big|(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\cup(Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y)\big|=2\binom{k}{2}-\big|(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\cap(Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y)\big|\geq 2\binom{k}{2}-\frac{ks}{2}.

Replacing (
106
) and the above count of
Y
for fixed
s
into the left-hand side of (
105
) gives

\sum_{X\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}\\
Y\sim X\end{subarray}}(1-p)^{|(X\mathbin{\hat{+}}X)\cup(Y\mathbin{\hat{+}}Y)|}\leq\sum_{X\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}}\sum_{s=1}^{k}k^{3s}\binom{n}{k-s}(1-p)^{2\binom{k}{2}-ks/2}.

Trivially bounding the number of choices for
X\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}
by
\binom{n}{k}
and plugging the result into (
104
) completes the proof.
∎

With
Lemma

9.2
and
Lemma

9.3
, the proof of
Theorem

9.1
is just checking that the bounds match those of the statement.

Proof of
Theorem

9.1
.

Fix
p=p(n)
satisfying
1/2\geq p\geq n^{-\delta/8}
for some
\delta>0
, and let
k=(2-2\delta)\log_{\frac{1}{1-p}}n
.
It suffices to show that

(107)

\frac{\operatorname{Var}(Z_{k})}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]^{2}}\to 0\qquad\text{ as }\qquad n\to\infty.

First, we compute the expected value of
Z_{k}
using
Lemma

9.2
and linearity of expectation:

(108)

\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]=\big(1-o(1)\big)\binom{n}{k}(1-p)^{\binom{k}{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{k}n^{-(1-\delta)(k-1)}\to\infty

as
n\to\infty
, by our choice of
k
.
Now, if we assume that

(109)

\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]\geq\binom{n}{k}\sum_{s=1}^{k}k^{3s}\binom{n}{k-s}(1-p)^{2\binom{k}{2}-ks/2},

then, by
Lemma

9.3
, we have
\operatorname{Var}(Z_{k})\leq 2\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]
and

\frac{\operatorname{Var}(Z_{k})}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]}\to 0\qquad\text{ as }\qquad n\to\infty,

by (
108
).
We therefore assume that the converse of (
109
) holds.

Before we proceed, observe that applying the standard binomial inequality

\binom{n}{k-s}\leq\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{s}\binom{n}{k}

to the right-hand side of (
109
) yields

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}\sum_{s=1}^{k}k^{3s}\binom{n}{k-s}(1-p)^{2\binom{k}{2}-ks/2}

\displaystyle\leq\binom{n}{k}^{2}(1-p)^{2\binom{k}{2}}\sum_{s=1}^{k}k^{3s}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{s}(1-p)^{-ks/2}

(110)

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]^{2}\sum_{s=1}^{k}\left(\frac{k^{4}}{n^{\delta}}\right)^{s},

where in the last inequality we used (
108
) with
n
sufficiently large and also

(1-p)^{-ks/2}=n^{(1-\delta)s}

because
k=(2-2\delta)\log_{\frac{1}{1-p}}n
.

By
Lemma

9.3
, our assumption that the converse of (
109
) holds, and (
110
), we have

(111)

\operatorname{Var}(Z_{k})\leq 8\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}]^{2}\sum_{s=1}^{k}\left(\frac{k^{4}}{n^{\delta}}\right)^{s}.

Replacing (
111
) into (
107
), we conclude that the proof is complete if we show that

\sum_{s=1}^{k}\left(\frac{k^{4}}{n^{\delta}}\right)^{s}\to 0\qquad\text{ as }\qquad n\to\infty.

This is easily seen to be true when
k=o(n^{\delta/4})
, which holds for our choice of
p\geq n^{-\delta/8}
.
∎

10.
Concluding remarks

The most important open question left by our work is extending the upper bound in
Theorem

1.1
to
p
as small as possible.
However, already when
p\leq(\log n)^{-1}
, there is an obstacle that prevents any approach similar to ours from working.
To understand the barrier, consider the following approximate summary of our strategy.
We find a family
\mathcal{S}=\{F(X)+F(X):X\in{\mathbb{Z}_{n}\choose k}\}
of subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
of size
s
(for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
) with the following two properties:

(1)

For each set
X\in{\mathbb{Z}_{n}\choose k}
, there exists
S\in\mathcal{S}
with
S\subseteq X+X
.

(2)

The family is small, that is,
\left|\mathcal{S}\right|\leq(1-p)^{-s}
.

Observe that each
S\in\mathcal{S}
is of the form
F(X)+F(X)
and has size
s
, so we must trivially have
|F(X)|\geq\sqrt{s}
for all
X\in{\mathbb{Z}_{n}\choose k}
.
Naively counting every set
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with
|F|=\sqrt{s}
when bounding the size of
\mathcal{S}
, we obtain

(112)

|\mathcal{S}|\geq\binom{n}{\sqrt{s}}\approx\exp(\sqrt{s}\log n).

In our proof, we show that we can choose
F
inside a small generalised arithmetic progression
P
, thus replacing the
\log n
term in (
112
) with
\log|P|
.
However, even if we could find such a set
P
with
|P|=O(\sqrt{s})
, we could not improve the bound in (
112
) beyond
\exp(\sqrt{s})
.

Combining this lower bound on the size of
\mathcal{S}
with the upper bound that we require in property (2) gives

\exp(\sqrt{s})\leq|\mathcal{S}|\leq(1-p)^{-s},

which implies that
s\geq p^{-2}
.
Now, consider any
X\in{\mathbb{Z}_{n}\choose k}
with
\sigma[X]=O(1)
: the corresponding set
S\in\mathcal{S}
satisfies
S\subseteq X+X
and therefore

p^{-2}\leq s=|S|\leq|X+X|\leq O(k).

As
k
is the upper bound we are trying to prove for
\alpha(\Gamma_{p})
, it follows that the best we can hope for this approach is, for some constant
C>2
,

(113)

\alpha(\Gamma_{p})\leq C\max\{p^{-2},p^{-1}\log n\},

where the second term in the maximum is the lower bound that we proved in
Section

9
.

While (
113
) would still be far from what we believe is true for
p
much smaller than
(\log n)^{-1}
, proving it would still be very interesting; even if we optimized our approach, we could not get close to proving (
113
) for all
p\geq(\log n)^{-1}
.

Improving the upper bound is related to the following problem due to Shachar Lovett: is there a list of
2^{n^{O(1)}}
subsets of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with density at least
1/100
such that, whenever
X\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
has density at least
1/3
,
X+X
contains one of these sets?
This question is relevant because we can embed sets
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with
\sigma[X]=O(1)
as dense subsets of an Abelian group using Ruzsa’s model lemma.
Now, if the positive answer to this problem does not rely too much on the details of the setting (the structure of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and the particular densities of the subsets) and a similar list exists for any Abelian group, then we could take a union bound over its elements and hope to circumvent (
113
) in the bounded doubling setting.

We would also like to highlight that for very small
p
, close to
n^{-1}\log n
, upper and lower bounds for
\alpha(\Gamma_{p})
follow from a theorem of
Bourgain [8]
; this is the only other result that we are aware of in the regime
p=o(1)
.
Another way to obtain an upper bound in this range is as follows: observe that the first eigenvalue of
\Gamma_{p}
is highly concentrated around
np
, and that we can use elementary Fourier analysis to show that its second eigenvalue is, with high probability, at most
O(\sqrt{np\log n})
(see, for example,
[
1
, Sections 3.1 and 3.2]
).
Using Hoffman’s ratio bound, we then obtain

\alpha(\Gamma_{p})\leq\bigg(\frac{Cn\log n}{p}\bigg)^{1/2},

which matches
\alpha(G(n,p))
up to a logarithmic factor if
p\approx n^{-1}\log n
.

Another interesting open question is to determine the minimum number of translates necessary to obtain the correct leading constant of
d+1
in Freĭman’s lemma.
This is closely related to the question asked (and partially settled) by
Bollobás, Leader, and Tiba [6]
on whether three translates suffice to obtain the Cauchy–Davenport lower bound for two sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
Even though their question was recently resolved by
Fox, Luo, Pham, and Zhou [13]
in the affirmative, it is not clear what is the truth in higher dimensions, even in the simpler case of
A=B
.
We conjecture the following:

Conjecture 10.1
.

There exists
C>0
such that the following holds.
For all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and all finite sets
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of full rank,
X
contains a subset
T
such that
|T|\leq Cd
and

(114)

|X+T|\geq(d+1)|X|-d^{C}.

The best negative result we have for this problem is given by the following simple construction.
Let
d\geq 3
, and let
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{d}\}
be the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Consider

X=P+\{0,e_{1},\ldots,e_{d-1}\}\qquad\text{ where }\qquad P=\{0,e_{d},2e_{d},\ldots,ke_{d}\}

for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
It is not hard to show that the following holds for this example: for every
\gamma>0
, there is
c=c(\gamma)>0
such that if
T\subseteq X
with
|T|<(2-\gamma)d
, then

|X+T|\leq(1-c)(d+1)|X|.

Theorem

1.3
is actually not the best result that we know towards
Conjecture

10.1
.
While we were finishing the writing of this paper,
Gowers, Green, Manners, and Tao [16]
made a breakthrough and proved Marton’s conjecture, also known as the Polynomial Freĭman–Ruzsa (PFR) conjecture for finite fields.
In a previous work,
Green, Manners, and Tao [20, Corollary 1.12]
proved that a positive answer to Marton’s conjecture would imply what is known as the “weak” Polynomial Freĭman–Ruzsa conjecture for
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
: for every
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
\sigma[X]\leq\sigma
, there exists
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
such that

\operatorname{rank}(X^{\prime})\leq C\log\sigma\qquad\text{ and }\qquad|X^{\prime}|\geq\sigma^{-C}|X|

for some absolute constant
C>0
.

Combining this weak version of PFR in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with a variation of the argument we developed in
Section

6
, we can prove a version of
Theorem

1.3
that attains a bound of the form
|X+T|\geq(d+1-O(\log d))|X|
at the cost of
d^{C}
translates, for
C>0
an absolute constant.
We develop these ideas in a separate work, dedicated to answering a question of
Green and Morris [18]
about the number of subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with prescribed doubling, where we require that both the number of translates is a polynomial in
d
, and the leading constant is at least
1-o(1)
.
Nonetheless, we think that
Conjecture

10.1
should not depend on results as deep as variants of PFR.

The final future research direction we would like to highlight is the extension of our result to other groups.
During the preparation of this paper, it came to our knowledge that
Conlon, Fox, Pham, and Yepremyan [11]
proved an upper bound of
O(\log N\log\log N)
for the clique/independence number in the uniform random Cayley graph of any group
G
, where
N
is the order of
G
.
As there are some groups, like
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, where this is tight up to the constant factor, their result can be seen as a generalisation of
Green
’s theorem
[
17
]
about
\alpha(\Gamma_{1/2})
.

Moreover, over certain groups, like
\mathbb{F}_{5}^{n}
, they can show that there exist Cayley graphs which have both clique and independence number
(2+o(1))\log N
, even though a uniform random Cayley graph has clique number
\Theta(\log N\log\log N)
.
It would be interesting if our methods, combined with their techniques, can extend these results to sparser graphs.

Appendix A
Chang’s theorem for
\mathbb{Z}_{n}

This
appendix
is devoted to the proof of
Corollary

8.4
, the variant of
Chang
’s theorem that we used in the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.
See
8.4

We rely on two theorems to prove
Corollary

8.4
:
Cwalina and Schoen
’s strengthening of the Green–Ruzsa theorem (Freĭman’s theorem for general Abelian groups)
[
12
]
and the discrete John’s theorem of
Tao and Vu [28]
.
To state these two results, we need some auxiliary definitions.
As in
[
12
]
, define a coset progression to be a set of the form
P+H
, where
H
is a subgroup of
G
and
P
is a generalised arithmetic progression.
Moreover, the dimension of
P+H
is
\dim(P)
, we say that a coset progression is proper if (a)
P
is proper, and (b)
|P+H|=|P|\,|H|
.
We say that
P+H
is
2
-proper if
(P+P)+H
is proper.

Theorem A.1

(
Cwalina and Schoen [12, Theorem 4]
)
.

There exists
C^{\prime}>0
such that the following holds.
Let
G
be an Abelian group, and let
X\subseteq G
be a finite set with
\sigma[X]\leq\kappa
.
Either there exists a proper coset progression
P+H
such that

X\subseteq P+H,\quad\dim(P+H)\leq 2\kappa+1\quad\text{ and }\quad|P+H|\leq\exp(C^{\prime}\kappa^{4}(\log(\kappa+2))^{2})|X|,

or
X
is fully contained in at most
C^{\prime}\kappa^{3}(\log\kappa)^{2}
cosets, whose total cardinality is bounded by
\exp(C^{\prime}\kappa^{4}(\log(\kappa+2))^{2})|X|
, of some subgroup of
G
.

As our group of interest is
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, a proper coset progression
P+H
is either all of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, or simply a proper generalised arithmetic progression
P+0=P
.
We also need a
lemma
by the same authors to obtain, from a
d
-dimensional coset progression
P+H
, a
2
-proper coset progression that contains
P+H
, has dimension at most
d
, and whose size is not much larger than
|P+H|
.

Lemma A.2

(
Cwalina and Schoen [12, Lemma 6]
)
.

There exists
C^{\prime}>0
such that the following holds.
Suppose that
P+H
is a
d
-dimensional coset progression in an Abelian group
G
.
Then there exists a
2
-proper coset progression
P^{\prime}+H^{\prime}
such that

P+H\subseteq P^{\prime}+H^{\prime},\qquad\dim(P^{\prime}+H)\leq d,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad|P^{\prime}+H^{\prime}|\leq d^{C^{\prime}d^{2}}|P+H|.

These two results enable us to give an overview of the (somewhat standard) proof of
Corollary

8.4
.
First, we apply
Theorem

A.1
to
X
.
Using (
71
) and
Lemma

A.2
, we will show that this application yields a small
2
-proper generalised arithmetic progression
P\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
such that
X\subseteq P
.
However, the bound on
d=\dim(P)
is not strong enough for our purposes, so we use it instead to define a Freĭman isomorphism
\phi:P\to P_{d}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
in the natural way.

Restricting
P_{d}
to an
r=\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)
dimensional subspace, then, yields an
r
-dimensional
convex
progression, which is a set of the form
\mathcal{C}=K\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
In this definition,
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a convex set of rank
r\leq d
.
At this point, we use
Tao and Vu
’s discrete John’s theorem
[
28
]
(
Theorem

A.3
below) to obtain a small generalised arithmetic progression
P^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq P^{\prime}
and
\dim(P^{\prime})\leq r
.
We complete the proof by taking the progression
\phi^{-1}(P^{\prime})\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.

The above sketch is not quite right for two reasons.
The first is that
\phi^{-1}
may not be defined for every element in
P^{\prime}
, but the linearity of the mapping allows us to extend it to all of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
The second reason is that the statement of
Theorem

A.3
requires the convex progression to be (centrally) symmetric, i.e.
\mathcal{C}=-\mathcal{C}
.
We amend this by applying it instead to
\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}
, bounding its size by
|P-P|\leq 2^{d}|P|
, and its dimension by that of the same
d^{\prime}
-dimensional subspace that contains
\mathcal{C}
.

Theorem A.3

(
Tao and Vu [28, Theorem 1.6, Lemma 3.3]
)
.

There exists
C^{\prime}>0
such that the following holds.
Let
d,r\in\mathbb{N}
with
r\leq d
.
For any
r
-dimensional symmetric convex progression
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, there exists a generalised arithmetic progression
P\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that

\mathcal{C}\subseteq P,\qquad|P|\leq r^{C^{\prime}r^{2}}|\mathcal{C}|\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\dim(P)\leq r=\dim(\mathcal{C}).

The bound for the size of the generalised arithmetic progression in
Theorem

A.3
has since been improved
[
3
,
29
]
, but the one by
Tao and Vu [28]
suffices for our purposes.
Their statement crucially defines the convex progression on an arbitrary lattice
\Lambda
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, rather than
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
This allows us to avoid the full rank requirement
r=d
in their original result, and take
r\leq d
as in
Theorem

A.3
by first setting
\Lambda=\mathcal{H}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
\mathcal{H}
is an
r
-dimensional subspace containing
\mathcal{C}
.
With this lattice, we then take a suitable linear isomorphism
f
and apply their original result to
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=f(\mathcal{C})
with
\Lambda^{\prime}=f(\Lambda)
and
\mathbb{R}^{r}=f(\mathcal{H})
.

Using these results, we can now prove
Corollary

8.4
.

Proof of
Corollary

8.4
.

As all the subgroups of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
are trivial when
n
is a prime, we claim that we cannot obtain the second case when applying
Theorem

A.1
to
X
.
Observe that none of the cosets obtained in the second outcome can be
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
itself.
The reason for that is that a single one of those cosets would, by (
71
), exceed the total cardinality bound given in
Theorem

A.1
if
C>C^{\prime}
is sufficiently large:

|\mathbb{Z}_{n}|=n>\exp(C^{\prime}\kappa^{4}(\log(\kappa+2))^{2})|X|.

The claim follows by noting that the cosets also cannot all be of the form
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, since their total number is bounded by
C^{\prime}\kappa^{3}(\log\kappa)^{2}
, and, by (
71
), this is insufficient to cover
X
.

We have shown that we always get the first outcome of
Theorem

A.1
under our assumptions, so applying it to
X
yields a proper coset progression
P^{\prime\prime}+H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
such that

X\subseteq P^{\prime\prime}+H,\qquad\dim(P^{\prime\prime})\leq 2\kappa+1\qquad\text{ and }\qquad|P^{\prime\prime}+H|\leq\exp(C\kappa^{4}(\log\kappa)^{2})|X|,

where we used that
\kappa\geq 2
and that
C>C^{\prime}
is appropriately large.
Applying now
Lemma

A.2
to
P^{\prime\prime}+H
gives a
2
-proper coset progression
P^{\prime}+H^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
such that

(115)

X\subseteq P^{\prime}+H^{\prime},\quad d=\dim(P^{\prime})\leq 2\kappa+1\quad\text{ and }\quad|P^{\prime}+H^{\prime}|\leq\exp(C\kappa^{4}(\log\kappa)^{2})|X|,

again by
C>C^{\prime}
.
Moreover,
H^{\prime}=\{0\}
because
H^{\prime}=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is impossible by (
71
):

\exp(C\kappa^{4}(\log\kappa)^{2})|X|<n=|P^{\prime}+\mathbb{Z}_{n}|,

so we will write simply
P^{\prime}
for
P^{\prime}+H^{\prime}
.

Let
\phi:P^{\prime}\to P^{\prime}_{d}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be the function defined by

\phi(y)=(w^{(y)}_{1},\ldots,w^{(y)}_{d})\quad\text{ where }\quad y=a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}^{(y)}a_{i}

and

P^{\prime}=\bigg\{a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}a_{i}:w_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},\,0\leq w_{i}<\ell_{i}\bigg\}.

Since
P^{\prime}
is
2
-proper, we know that
\phi
is a Freĭman isomorphism; this and

imply that so is
\phi|_{X}:X\to\phi(X)
.
Let
X_{d}=\phi(X)
and assume without loss of generality (by translating
\phi
if necessary) that
0\in X_{d}
.
If we define
\mathcal{H}
to be the minimal subspace that contains
X_{d}
, then
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}\cap P^{\prime}_{d}
is a convex progression and
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}-\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is a symmetric convex progression satisfying
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
.
We can therefore apply
Theorem

A.3
to
\mathcal{C}
and obtain a generalised arithmetic progression
P_{d}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
which, we claim, satisfies

(116)

X_{d}\subseteq P_{d},\qquad\dim(P_{d})\leq\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad|P_{d}|\leq\exp(C\kappa^{4}(\log\kappa)^{2})|X|.

The containment
X_{d}\subseteq P_{d}
follows from
X_{d}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}\subseteq P_{d}
, where the last step is given by
Theorem

A.3
.
In order to bound the dimension of
P_{d}
, let
d_{\mathcal{C}}=\dim(\mathcal{C})
and observe that

(117)

\dim(P_{d})\leq d_{\mathcal{C}}\leq\dim(\mathcal{H})\leq\dim_{\mathrm{F}}(X)

by
Theorem

A.3
,
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
and the definition of
\mathcal{H}
as the minimal subspace containing
X_{d}
.
Finally, to obtain the claimed bound on the size of

, we first use that, by

A.3
,

(118)

|P_{d}|\leq d_{\mathcal{C}}^{C^{\prime}d_{\mathcal{C}}^{2}}|\mathcal{C}|\leq\exp(C\kappa^{2}\log\kappa)|\mathcal{C}|

where we used that
d_{\mathcal{C}}\leq d\leq 2\kappa+1

) and that

The second step is showing that the size of

|\mathcal{C}|=|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}-\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|\leq|P_{d}^{\prime}-P_{d}^{\prime}|\leq 2^{d}|P_{d}^{\prime}|\leq\exp(C\kappa^{4}(\log\kappa)^{2})|X|,

which is due first to

than previous occurrences and using (

Combining (

It remains to define a generalised arithmetic progression

Observe that

is linear (or affine), so we can extend it to obtain a

Counting sets with large doubling

Recall that

We restate the result which we aim to prove for the reader’s convenience:

Before proceeding, we recall a key definition from

Definition B.1

A set

The first result that we need to optimize is

.

Lemma B.2
.

For every sufficiently small
\delta>0
and
\eta>0
, the following holds.
Fix a large prime

and
M=(\log n)/(\log\log n)^{4}
.

, then any

.

We will begin by giving an overview of the proof from

, alongside some useful definitions of our own.
Whenever there is
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

\{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\}

\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\}
.

The proof starts by fixing the value of
M=(\log n)/(\log\log n)^{4}
and any

-dissociated set

.
We define
G^{\prime}
to be the graph with vertex set
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and edges between a pair of vertices if their difference can be written as
\pm x_{i}\pm x_{j}
for some

.
The graph
G^{\prime}
is useful because of the following

:

Observation B.3
.

For each
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, let
V_{a}\subseteq V(G^{\prime})
denote its connected component in

.
Every element

can be expressed as

with
\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\lambda_{j}|\leq 2\operatorname{diam}(V_{a})
.

Take
b\in V_{a}
, and consider any shortest path

to

.
Observe that

count respectively the number of times

appear as terms in

.
Then,

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(|\lambda_{j}^{+}|+|\lambda_{j}^{-}|\big)\leq 2|P|\leq 2\operatorname{diam}(V_{a})

where the last step follows from

being a shortest path in
V_{a}
.
∎

Having thus related the span of
D
with the diameter of connected components in

, we will decompose

using the following

, also from
[

]
, with

(

)

Let

be a graph with vertex set

There exists a partition

such that

|X_{*}|\leq 32(v(G)/\Delta)^{2}
.

(2)

e(X_{i},X_{j})=0

The diameter of

.

Applying

B.4
to

such that

(1)

if
X_{i}\neq X_{j}
, then
X_{i}+D
and
X_{j}+D
are disjoint, and

(2)

every

has a

such that

is contained in the

-span of

.

To see that property (i) holds, notice that if

and

G
connecting
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
, contradicting item (2) of
Lemma

B.4
.
Property (ii), on the other hand, follows directly from an application of
Observation

B.3
to
X_{i}
, the diameter of which is bounded by item (3) in
Lemma

B.4
.

To combine these two properties into a proof of
Lemma

B.2
, notice that the disjointness given by property (i) yields a lower bound for
X+X
in terms of each
X_{i}
:

|X+X|\geq\sum_{i}|X_{i}+D|.

We now want a lower bound for each
|X_{i}+D|
in terms of
d
.
Towards that goal, we will define, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
, a Freĭman isomorphism
\phi_{i}:X_{i}\to X_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Each will map (a translate of)
D
to a structured set, where structured only means that we have a lower bound for its sumset with any sufficiently small set.
These isomorphisms are naturally defined by the
2\Delta
-bounded decomposition of
X_{i}-y_{i}
given by property (ii).

Observation B.5
.

The function
\phi_{i}:X_{i}\to X_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
defined by
\phi_{i}(x)=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
, where
x-y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}x_{j}
is a
2\Delta
-bounded decomposition of
x-y_{i}
, is a Freĭman isomorphism.

Proof.

The fact that
\phi^{-1}:X_{i}^{\prime}\to X_{i}
is a Freĭman homomorphism follows from its linearity, so we focus on the other direction of the implication.
Take
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\in X_{i}
such that

(121)

a_{1}+a_{2}=a_{3}+a_{4},

and we want to show that
\phi_{i}(a_{1})+\phi_{i}(a_{2})=\phi_{i}(a_{3})+\phi_{i}(a_{4})
.
For
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
, write

(122)

a_{\ell}-y_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\lambda^{(\ell)}_{i},

a
2\Delta
-bounded combination of
D
.
We now replace (
122
) in (
121
) and rearrange to obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}(\lambda_{j}^{(1)}+\lambda_{j}^{(2)}-\lambda_{j}^{(3)}-\lambda_{j}^{(4)})=0.

Using the fact that each (
122
) is
2\Delta
-bounded yields, as
k\to\infty
,

\sum_{\ell=1}^{4}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\lambda_{j}^{(\ell)}|\leq 4(2\Delta)\leq 8k^{1/2}\log k\leq(\log n)^{1-\eta/3}\leq M,

where the inequalities follow by the definitions/assumptions which we now recall

\Delta=k^{1/2}\log k,\qquad k\leq(\log n)^{2-\eta}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad M=(\log n)/(\log\log n)^{4}.

However,
D
is
M
-dissociated, so we conclude that, for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

(\lambda_{j}^{(1)}+\lambda_{j}^{(2)})-(\lambda_{j}^{(3)}+\lambda_{j}^{(4)})=0,

which, by the definition of
\phi_{i}
, is just another way to write
\phi_{i}(a_{1})+\phi_{i}(a_{2})=\phi_{i}(a_{3})+\phi_{i}(a_{4})
.
∎

Observe that
\phi_{i}(D+y_{i})=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
, where
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Hence, to obtain a lower bound for the size of
X_{i}+D
in terms of
d
and complete the proof of
Lemma

B.2
, we will bound
|\phi_{i}(X_{i})+\phi_{i}(D+y_{i})|
using the isoperimetric inequality of
Wang and Wang [30]
, as stated in
[
18
]
(see also
[
5
]
).

Proposition B.6

(
[
30
]
, see
[
18
, “The Isoperimetric Inequality”]
)
.

For every
\gamma>0
and
C>0
, there exists
d_{0}=d_{0}(\gamma,C)
such that the following holds for every
d\geq d_{0}
.
If
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a set of size at most
Cd
, then

\big|S+\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}\big|\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\gamma\right)d|S|.

Proof of
Lemma

B.2
.

Fix
X\in\mathcal{X}_{3}^{(m)}
and let
D=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\}
be any
M
-dissociated subset of
X
.
Towards our aim of showing that
|D|=d\leq\big(2+\delta+o(1)\big)m/k
, recall the definition of the graph
G^{\prime}
: its vertices are
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and there are edges between vertices
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
only when some of
\pm(a-b)
can be written as either
x_{i}-x_{j}
or
x_{i}+x_{j}
for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

We apply
Lemma

B.4
to
G=G^{\prime}[X]
with
\Delta=k^{1/2}\log k
and obtain
X_{*}\cup X_{1}\cup\cdots\cup X_{t}
, a partition of
X
such that
e(X_{i},X_{j})=0
for all
i\neq j
,

(123)

\operatorname{diam}(G[X_{i}])\leq k^{1/2}\log k

and

(124)

|X_{*}|\leq 32\left(\frac{k}{k^{1/2}\log k}\right)^{2}=o(k).

In order to obtain a lower bound for
X+X
, we use property (i) of
G
to show that

(125)

|X+X|\geq\sum_{i=1}^{t}|X_{i}+D|.

Now, we define, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
, the function
\phi_{i}
; by property (ii) and
Observation

B.5
, we have that
\phi_{i}
is a Freĭman isomorphism from
X_{i}
to
X_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
By the definition of Freĭman isomorphisms, we obtain

(126)

|X_{i}+D|=|X_{i}+(D+y_{i})|=|X_{i}^{\prime}+\phi_{i}(D+y_{i})|.

Recall that the definition of
\phi_{i}
implies that
\phi_{i}(D+y_{i})=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
.
Hence, to apply
Proposition

B.6
to the right-hand side of (
126
), we need to bound
|X_{i}^{\prime}|
in terms of
d
and show that
d
is sufficiently large.
We will show both of these by observing that if
k>10d/\delta
, then we are already done, since

d\leq\frac{\delta k}{10}\leq\frac{m}{k}\leq\big(2+\delta+o(1)\big)\frac{m}{k},

which is what we wanted to show.
We can therefore assume that
d\geq\delta k/10
, which simultaneously implies that
d
is large when
k\to\infty
, and that
|X_{i}^{\prime}|\leq k\leq 10d/\delta
.
Applying
Proposition

B.6
to
X_{i}^{\prime}
with
\gamma=\delta^{2}
and
C=10/\delta
, we obtain

(127)

\big|X_{i}^{\prime}+\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}\big|\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\delta^{2}\right)d|X_{i}^{\prime}|

for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
.

Replacing (
127
) in (
125
), and using (
126
), we conclude that

|X+X|\geq d\left(\frac{1}{2}-\delta^{2}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{t}|X_{i}^{\prime}|.

Thus, using the fact that
|X_{i}^{\prime}|=|X_{i}|
, which follows from each
\phi_{i}
being a Freĭman isomorphism, and that
X=X_{*}\cup X_{1}\cup\cdots X_{t}
is a partition, we have

d\left(\frac{1}{2}-\delta^{2}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{t}|X_{i}^{\prime}|=d\left(\frac{1}{2}-\delta^{2}\right)\big(|X|-|X_{*}|\big)=d\left(\frac{1}{2}-\delta^{2}\right)(1-o(1))k

where we used (
124
) to bound
|X_{*}|
.
Finally, it follows from
|X+X|\leq m
and
\delta
being sufficiently small that
d\leq\big(2+\delta+o(1)\big)m/k
as
k\to\infty
.
∎

With
Lemma

B.2
, we need only one more piece, the following
lemma
.
It is a simple count of the number of choices for coefficients in a dissociated set, and we will use it to repeat the proof of Proposition 5.1 in
Green and Morris [18]
and obtain
Proposition

8.6
.

Lemma B.7

(
[
18
, Lemma 5.5]
)
.

For every
M,d\in\mathbb{N}
, the number of choices for
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\lambda_{i}|\leq M
is at most
(4d)^{M}
.

Proof of
Proposition

8.6
.

Fix
M=(\log n)/(\log\log n)^{4}
and take
X\in\mathcal{X}_{3}^{(m)}
.
We will count the choices for
X
by first choosing a maximal
M
-dissociated subset
D=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\}\subseteq X
and then choosing the remaining elements of
X\setminus D
using the properties of
D
.

First, we count the choices for
D
naively, and rely on
Lemma

B.2
to bound its size.
That is, we apply
Lemma

B.2
to
D
, obtain
{|D|\leq d\leq\big(2+\delta+o(1)\big)m/k}
, and thus deduce that the number of choices for
D
is at most:

(128)

\sum_{t=1}^{d}\binom{n}{t}\leq(n+1)^{d}\leq n^{(2+\delta+o(1))m/k}.

The second step is counting the choices for
X\setminus D
, and we do so by counting the possible ways to write each of its elements as an
M
-bounded combination of
D
.
Fix
x^{\prime}\in X\setminus D
, and note that the maximality of
D
implies that there is
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
such that

\lambda_{0}x^{\prime}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}x_{j}=0

and the elements of
\Lambda
satisfy

\sum_{j=0}^{d}|\lambda_{j}|\leq M,\qquad\lambda_{0}\neq 0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\lambda_{i}\neq 0\text{ for some }i>0.

We can therefore use
Lemma

B.7
to count the number of choices for
\Lambda
and observe that

(4d+4)^{M}\leq\exp(3M\log k)\leq\exp\left(\frac{\log n}{(\log\log n)^{2}}\right)\leq n^{1/\log k}

where the first inequality follows from the (trivial) observation that
d\leq k
, and the rest is a consequence of our choice of
M=(\log n)/(\log\log n)^{4}
and our assumption that
k\leq(\log n)^{2-\eta}
.

We can now choose each of the
k-d
elements of
X\setminus D
by the above procedure, and obtain that there are at most

(129)

n^{(k-d)/(\log k)}=n^{o(k)}

such elements.
Combining (
128
) and (
129
) with
m\geq\delta k^{2}/10
thus yields

\big|\mathcal{X}_{3}^{(m)}\big|\leq n^{(2+\delta+o(1))m/k+o(k)}=n^{(2+\delta+o(1))m/k},

as required.
∎
