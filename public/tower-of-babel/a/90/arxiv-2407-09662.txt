Analytical Expression for Continuum-continuum Transition Amplitude of Hydrogen-like Atoms with Angular-momentum Dependence
J B Ji, K Ueda, M Han, H J Wörner

Abstract
Attosecond chronoscopy typically utilises interfering two-photon transitions to access the phase information. Simulating these two-photon transitions is challenging due to the continuum-continuum transition term. The hydrogenic approximation within second-order perturbation theory has been widely used due to the existence of analytical expressions of the wave functions. So far, only (partially) asymptotic results have been derived, which fail to correctly describe the low-kinetic-energy behaviour, especially for high angular-momentum states. Here, we report an analytical expression that overcome these limitations. They are based on the Appell’s F 1 F_{1} function and use the confluent hypergeometric function of the second kind as the intermediate states. We show that the derived formula quantitatively agrees with the numerical simulations using the time-dependent Schrödinger equation for various angular-momentum states, which improves the accuracy compared to the other analytical approaches that were previously reported. Furthermore, we give an angular-momentum-dependent asymptotic form of the outgoing wavefunction and their continuum-continuum dipole transition amplitudes.

The transition amplitudes of the absorption and the emission pathways can be written as:

\mathcal{A}_{\mp}(\Delta t)=\mathcal{A}_{\mp,0}\exp(\pm\mathrm{i}\omega\Delta t)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\Delta t)={\left|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{abs}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega\Delta t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{emi}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega\Delta t}\right|}^{2}={\left|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{abs}}\right|}^{2}+{\left|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{emi}}\right|}^{2}+2\left|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{abs}}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{emi}}\right|\cos(2\omega\Delta t-\phi)

where
\phi=\arg\{\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{emi}}\}-\arg\{\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{abs}}\}

\frac{\phi}{2\omega}=\tau_{\mathrm{XUV}}+\tau_{\rm A}

\tau_{\mathrm{XUV}}=(\phi^{\mathrm{XUV}}_{+}-\phi^{\mathrm{XUV}}_{-})/(2\omega)

\tau_{\rm A}=(\arg\{\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(2ph)}}_{\mathrm{emi}}\}-\arg\{\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(2ph)}}_{\mathrm{abs}}\})/(2\omega)
is known as the two-photon ionisation time delay, since it corresponds to the phase difference of the two-photon transition amplitudes between the emission and the absorption pathways. More discussions and examples can be found in the book chapter
[
21
]

From the theoretical point of view, the
ab initio
computation such as numerically solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE) is limited to the modelled potential with one active electron, e.g. the hydrogen-like atoms, while modelling the potential for molecules can be very challenging.
A common approach for handling this problem is to separate the two-photon transition into two parts, i.e. the bound-continuum transition and the continuum-continuum (CC) transition, so that the two parts can be treated individually.
This essentially stems from the lowest-order perturbation theory.
The two-photon transition matrix element from an bound initial state
|n\rangle
to a final state in the continuum with momentum
\vec{k}
can be represented as
[
22
]
:

\mathcal{M}_{n}(\vec{k}^{\prime};\Omega)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\sumint_{\nu}\frac{{\langle\vec{k}^{\prime}|\hat{d}_{\omega}|\nu\rangle}{\langle\nu|\hat{d}_{\Omega}|n\rangle}}{\epsilon_{n}+\Omega-\epsilon_{\nu}+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon}

(5)

and the two-photon transition amplitude reads

\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(2ph)}}_{n\rightarrow l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(E^{\prime};\theta,\varphi)=\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\mathcal{E}_{\Omega}\mathcal{M}_{n}(\vec{k}^{\prime};\Omega)

(6)

where
\mathcal{E}_{\Omega}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
are the complex amplitudes (field strengths with phases included) of the XUV and dressing fields, respectively, and
\vec{k}^{\prime}
is the vector with modulus of
\sqrt{2E^{\prime}}
and pointing towards the emission angle
(\theta,\varphi)
, either in the lab frame or in the molecular frame for molecules. Within the framework of dipole approximation, the dipole terms for the XUV and dressing fields are
\hat{d}_{\Omega}
and
\hat{d}_{\omega}
, respectively. The
\sumint_{\nu}
sign refers to summing over all discrete states and integrating over all continuous states.
If one assumes that the two-photon process is dominated by the pathway that the electron absorbs one XUV photon from the initial state to an intermediate state in the continuum (assuming that the XUV photon energy is higher than the photoionisation energy) with the corresponding kinetic energy, after which a CC transition occurs due to the presence of the dressing IR field, where the electron either absorbs or emits one IR photon (multi-IR processes are ignored here, assuming the dressing field is relatively weak), as sketched in figure
1
, then the two-photon transition amplitude can be expressed as:

\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(2ph)}}_{n\rightarrow l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(E^{\prime})=\sum_{l,m}\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(1ph)}}_{n\rightarrow l,m}(E)\mathfrak{T}^{(\mathrm{CC})}_{l,m\rightarrow l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(E,E^{\prime})

(7)

where the terms
\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(1ph)}}(E)
and
\mathfrak{T}^{(\mathrm{CC})}(E,E^{\prime})
refer to the bound-continuum and continuum-continuum transitions, respectively. Here we have decomposed the emission-angle-dependent transition amplitude into the spherical harmonics, i.e.

\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(2ph)}}_{n}(E^{\prime};\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(2ph)}}_{n\rightarrow l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(E^{\prime})Y_{l^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}(\theta,\varphi)

(8)

where
Y_{l^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}(\theta,\varphi)
is the spherical harmonic (in the Condon–Shortley phase convention) with angular momentum and magnetic quantum numbers of
l^{\prime}
and
m^{\prime}
, respectively.
For the continuum-continuum part, the transition amplitude can be expressed as the product of the angular part and the radial part:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}^{(\mathrm{CC})}_{l,m\rightarrow l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(E,E^{\prime})=\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\int_{4\pi}{\left(\ Y_{l^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}\right)}^{*}Y_{1}^{\mu}Y_{l}^{m}\mathrm{d}\Omega\times(-\pi)\int_{0}^{\infty}{\left(R_{l^{\prime}}(E)\right)}^{*}rR_{l}(E)r^{2}\mathrm{d}r

(9)

where
Y_{1}^{\mu}
is the polarisation of the dressing photon represented in the spherical harmonics. The
(-\pi)
-factor originates from the contour integral around the pole in equation (
5
), which is explained in detail in
[
22
]
. The angular part can be calculated by the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients:

\int_{4\pi}{\left(\ Y_{l^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}\right)}^{*}Y_{1}^{\mu}Y_{l}^{m}\mathrm{d}\Omega=\sqrt{\frac{3(2l+1)}{4\pi(2l^{\prime}+1)}}\langle l,0,1,0|l^{\prime},0\rangle\langle l,m,1,\mu|l^{\prime},m^{\prime}\rangle

(10)

where
\langle j_{1},m_{1},j_{2},m_{2}|J,M\rangle
is the Clebsch–Gordan coefficient and is real under the Condon–Shortley phase convention.
The latter integral between the radial wavefunctions, with the
(-\pi)
-term included, is denoted as
\mathcal{T}_{l\rightarrow l^{\prime}}(k,k^{\prime})
in the following discussion, where
k=\sqrt{2E}
and
k^{\prime}=\sqrt{2E^{\prime}}
are the momenta of the intermediate and final states, respectively.

The radial wavefunctions in principle depend on the shape of the radial potential, which can be complicated for molecular cases. If we assume that the CC transition is dominated by the long-range effect and is not sensitive to the local potential, then we can use the transition amplitudes derived from the hydrogen-like atoms.
Therefore, instead of expression (
1
), the two-photon Wigner-like time delay is defined using the finite-difference method:

\tau^{\mathrm{(2ph)}}(E^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\omega}\left[\arg\{\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(2ph)}}_{\mathrm{emi}}(E^{\prime})\}-\arg\{\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(2ph)}}_{\mathrm{abs}}(E^{\prime})\}\right]

(11)

which can be represented either in the spherical-coordinate basis
(\theta,\varphi)
or in the spherical-harmonic
(l,m)
basis. If we further assume that the CC transition amplitude in equation (
7
) does not depend on
(l,m)
or
(l^{\prime},m^{\prime})
, then the two-photon time delay can be separated into two parts:

\displaystyle\tau^{\mathrm{(2ph)}}(E^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\omega}\big[\arg\{\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(1ph)}}(E_{\mathrm{emi}})\mathfrak{T}^{(\mathrm{CC})}(E_{\mathrm{emi}},E^{\prime})\}-\arg\{\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(1ph)}}(E_{\mathrm{abs}})\mathfrak{T}^{(\mathrm{CC})}(E_{\mathrm{abs}},E^{\prime})\}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\omega}\Big\{\left[\arg\{\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(1ph)}}(E_{\mathrm{emi}})\}-\arg\{\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(1ph)}}(E_{\mathrm{abs}})\}\right]

\displaystyle+\left[\arg\{\mathfrak{T}^{(\mathrm{CC})}(E_{\mathrm{emi}},E^{\prime})\}-\arg\{\mathfrak{T}^{(\mathrm{CC})}(E_{\mathrm{abs}},E^{\prime})\}\right]\Big\}

\displaystyle\approx\tau^{\mathrm{(1ph)}}(E^{\prime})+\tau_{\mathrm{CC}}(E^{\prime};\omega)

(12)

where we have approximated the derivative in expression (
1
) by its finite difference, and the CC time delay is defined as

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{CC}}(E^{\prime};\omega)=\frac{1}{2\omega}\left[\arg\{\mathfrak{T}^{(\mathrm{CC})}(E_{\mathrm{emi}},E^{\prime})\}-\arg\{\mathfrak{T}^{(\mathrm{CC})}(E_{\mathrm{abs}},E^{\prime})\}\right].

(13)

By separating the two-photon time delay into the one-photon part and the continuum-continuum part, the problem is thus simplified as how to calculate the term
\mathfrak{T}^{(\mathrm{CC})}(E,E^{\prime})
, or equivalently the integral
\mathcal{T}(k,k^{\prime})
for the hydrogen-like atoms. Dahlström
et al.
reported two models based on the Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin (WKB) approximation with asymptotic expansion, i.e. considering the phase modulation (P) and considering the phase and amplitude (modulus) modulation (P+A), both expressed by the analytical formulae involving the gamma function
[
23
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{(\mathrm{P})}_{\mathrm{asym}}(k,k^{\prime})=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{N_{k}N_{k^{\prime}}}{{|k-k^{\prime}|}^{2}}\exp\left[-\frac{\pi Z}{2}(\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k^{\prime}})\right]\frac{{(2k)}^{\mathrm{i}Z/k}}{{(2k^{\prime})}^{\mathrm{i}Z/k^{\prime}}}\frac{\Gamma[2+\mathrm{i}Z(1/k-1/k^{\prime})]}{{(k-k^{\prime})}^{\mathrm{i}Z(1/k-1/k^{\prime})}}

(14)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{(\mathrm{P+A})}_{\mathrm{asym}}(k,k^{\prime})=\mathcal{T}^{(\mathrm{P})}_{\mathrm{asym}}(k,k^{\prime})\left[1+\frac{\mathrm{i}Z}{2}\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}}+\frac{1}{{k^{\prime}}^{2}}\right)\frac{k-k^{\prime}}{1+\mathrm{i}Z(1/k+1/k^{\prime})}\right]

(15)

where
N_{k}=\sqrt{2/(\pi k)}
and
N_{k^{\prime}}=\sqrt{2/(\pi k^{\prime})}
are the normalisation factors for the intermediate state and the final state, respectively.
Note that the normalisation factor depends on normalising according to the cross section or normalising according to the flux. For the scattered wave the total flux is

\int_{4\pi}j_{\rm s}~r^{2}~\mathrm{d}\Omega=k\int_{4\pi}{|f_{\rm s}|}^{2}~\mathrm{d}\Omega=k\int_{4\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\Omega}~\mathrm{d}\Omega

(16)

where
j_{\rm s}
is the scattering flux density,
f_{\rm s}
is the scattering amplitude, and
\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}\Omega
is the differential cross section.
The well-known solutions to the Coulomb wave equation are conventionally normalised according to the cross section, while the WKB approximation yields the flux-based normalisation factor, which contains an additional factor
\propto k^{-1/2}
, which is clear from comparing their asymptotic oscillation moduli
[
24
]
.
Regarding the phase and the corresponding time delay of the CC transitions, the TDSE results lie typically between these two models, and they converge towards the same values for high kinetic energies
[
23
]
.
A number of works have explicitly
[

,
25

26
,
27
,
28
,
29

30

or implicitly (e.g. to assume the two-photon time-delay difference between two species with the same or similar electron kinetic energies is equal or almost equal to their one-photon time-delay difference)

used this assumption and have shown good agreement with the experimental results on molecules, indicating that this approximation is reasonable.

Despite the great success of this elegant model in the past decade, some features of experimental observations cannot be explained by these models. For example, the phases of these models do not depend on the angular momentum
l
of the intermediate or the final state, which causes that the angle-dependent time delay of the two-photon ionisation is simply that of the one-photon ionisation plus an isotropic CC time delay.
This can be proven as follows. Using equations (

) and (

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(2ph)}}_{n\rightarrow l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(E^{\prime})=\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\sum_{l,m}\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(1ph)}}_{n\rightarrow l,m}(E)\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{asym}}(\sqrt{2E},\sqrt{2E^{\prime}})\int_{4\pi}{\left(\ Y_{l^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}\right)}^{*}Y_{1}^{\mu}Y_{l}^{m}\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(17)

Writing this formula into the spherical coordinates using equation (
8

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(2ph)}}_{n}(E^{\prime};\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})=\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{asym}}(\sqrt{2E},\sqrt{2E^{\prime}})\int_{4\pi}\sum_{l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}Y_{l^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}(\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})\sum_{l,m}\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(1ph)}}_{n}(E;\theta,\varphi){\left(Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\varphi)\right)}^{*}

\displaystyle\times{\left(\ Y_{l^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}(\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\varphi})\right)}^{*}Y_{1}^{\mu}(\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\varphi})Y_{l}^{m}(\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\varphi})~\mathrm{d}\tilde{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{asym}}(\sqrt{2E},\sqrt{2E^{\prime}})\int_{4\pi}\delta(\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime};\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\varphi})\delta(\theta,\varphi;\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\varphi})

\displaystyle\quad\times Y_{1}^{\mu}(\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\varphi})\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(1ph)}}_{n}(E;\theta,\varphi)~\mathrm{d}\tilde{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{asym}}(\sqrt{2E},\sqrt{2E^{\prime}})Y_{1}^{\mu}(\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})\mathfrak{T}^{\mathrm{(1ph)}}_{n}(E;\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})

(18)

where the term
Y_{1}^{\mu}(\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})
depends on the polarisation of the dressing field and does not contribute to the energy-dependent phase variation, i.e. the time delay.
Hence, for helium one would expect an isotropic two-photon time delay, since its one-photon ionisation time delay has no angular dependence. This contradicts the experimental observation

and

The missing piece was partially filled by Boll
et al.
who replaced the final-state wavefunction from the asymptotic formula by the regular solution of the Coulomb wave equation, which is expressed by the Kummer’s function, or the confluent hypergeometric function of the first kind
{{}_{1}F_{1}}

Method

2.1
Regular and irregular solutions to the Coulomb wave equation

The radial Coulomb electronic wave equation for the hydrogen atom (central charge
Z=1
) with asymptotic kinetic energy
E>0
and angular momentum
l=0,1,2,\cdots
reads (see
[
42
]
, section (33.14) and
[
43
]
, whereby equation (2) needs to be multiplied with
(-1)
for describing an attractive potential):

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}w}{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}+\left(2E+\frac{2}{r}-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^{2}}\right)w=0

(19)

where
R(r)=w(r)/r
is the radial wavefunction.
Equation (
19
) can be adapted for arbitrary
Z>0
by transforming
\tilde{E}=Z^{2}{E}
and
\tilde{r}={r}/Z
, where
\tilde{E}
and
\tilde{r}
are the asymptotic kinetic energy and the radius, respectively. For simplicity, in the discussion below we let
Z=1
, while the treatment is general for any
Z>0
, and the corresponding result can be found in
B
.
The regular solution of the outgoing radial wavefunction that satisfies the Coulomb wave equation can be written as (note that we have absorbed the factor
k^{l+1}
into
C_{l}(k)
, compared to the definition in
[
43
]
):

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{reg}}_{k,l}(r)\equiv f_{l}(k,r)=|C_{l}(k)|~r^{l}~\exp\left({\mathrm{i}kr}\right)\Phi\left(l+1-\mathrm{i}/k,2l+2,-2\mathrm{i}kr\right)

(20)

where
k=\sqrt{2E}
is the momentum, and
\Phi
is the confluent hypergeometric function (Kummer’s function), also known as the
{{}_{1}F_{1}}
function. Note that
f_{l}(k,r)
is a real function under such definition. The normalisation factor for the regular solution of the Coulomb wave equation with the Coulomb phase included is expressed as:

C_{l}(k)={k}^{l+1}~{2}^{l}~\mathrm{e}^{{\pi}/{(2k)}}~\frac{\Gamma\left(l+1+\mathrm{i}/k\right)}{(2l+1)!}

(21)

such that
rf_{l}(k,r)
oscillates with amplitude of 1 at
r\rightarrow\infty
.
Since equation (
19
) is a second-order differential equation, it has two linearly-independent solutions, and the other one can be expressed using the confluent hypergeometric function of the second kind
U(a,b;z)
, also known as the Tricomi’s function:

\displaystyle u_{l}(k,r)=B_{l}(k)~|C_{l}(k)|~r^{l}~\exp\left({\mathrm{i}kr}\right)U\left(l+1-\mathrm{i}/k,2l+2,-2\mathrm{i}kr\right),

(22)

where we define the prefactor
B_{l}(k)
as:

B_{l}(k)=-2\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{-{\pi}/{k}}{(-1)}^{l}\frac{(2l+1)!}{\Gamma\left(l+1+\mathrm{i}/k\right)}

(23)

such that

f_{l}(k,r)=\Im\{u_{l}(k,r)\}.

(24)

Its real part is another real function that satisfies the Coulomb wave equation, also known as the irregular solution:

R^{\mathrm{irr}}_{k,l}(r)\equiv g_{l}(k,r)=\Re\{u_{l}(k,r)\}.

(25)

Note that
f_{l}(k,r)
and
g_{l}(k,r)
are linear combinations of
u_{l}(k,r)
and its complex conjugate
u_{l}^{*}(k,r)
, which are two linearly independent solutions.
Asymptotically,
u_{l}(k,r)
approaches the spherical wave with a position-dependent phase (the Coulomb phase)
[
43
]
:

\displaystyle u_{l}(k,r\rightarrow\infty)=g_{l}(k,r\rightarrow\infty)+\mathrm{i}f_{l}(k,r\rightarrow\infty)

\displaystyle\approx{(-\mathrm{i})}^{l}\frac{1}{r}\exp\left(\mathrm{i}kr+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{k}\ln(2kr)-\mathrm{i}\arg\{C_{l}(k)\}\right).

(26)

Near the origin, the regular solution
f_{l}(k,r)\sim r^{l}
approaches zero, while the irregular solution
g_{l}(k,r)\sim 1/r^{l+1}
diverges.

2.2
General expression of integral containing the product of the confluent hypergeometric functions of the first and the second kind

Using the definition of

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{l\rightarrow l^{\prime}}(k,k^{\prime})=-\pi N_{k}N_{k^{\prime}}\mathrm{i}^{l-l^{\prime}-1}B_{l}(k)C_{l}(k)C_{l^{\prime}}^{*}(k^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times U\left(l+1-\mathrm{i}/k,2l+2,-2\mathrm{i}kr\right)

\displaystyle\times\Phi\left({l^{\prime}}+1-\mathrm{i}/k^{\prime},2{l^{\prime}}+2,-2\mathrm{i}k^{\prime}r\right)\mathrm{d}r

Although the similar integral involving two Kummer’s functions has already been discussed by Gordon almost a century ago

Let us consider the integral with a more general form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\sigma}_{\rho}(a,b,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-Q\xi}\xi^{\rho+\sigma}U(a,b;\lambda\xi)\Phi(-n^{\prime},\rho+1;\lambda^{\prime}\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi.

\displaystyle U(a,b;z)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\sigma}_{\rho}(a,b,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\big[(b-a-1)\mathcal{J}^{\sigma-1}_{\rho}(a,b-1,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{J}^{\sigma-1}_{\rho}(a-1,b-1,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)\big].

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{1}_{\rho}(a,b,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\big[(b-a-1)\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\rho}(a,b-1,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\rho}(a-1,b-1,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)\big]

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{3}_{\rho}(a,b,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}}\big[(b-a-3)(b-a-2)(b-a-1)\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\rho}(a,b-3,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)

\displaystyle+3(b-a-2)(b-a-1)\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\rho}(a-1,b-3,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)

\displaystyle+3(b-a-1)\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\rho}(a-2,b-3,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\rho}(a-3,b-3,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)\big].

The base case

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\rho}(a,b,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)=\frac{\Gamma(\rho-b+2)}{\Gamma(\rho-b+a+2)}\frac{\rho!~{(Q-\lambda^{\prime})}^{n^{\prime}}}{Q^{\rho+1+n^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\times F_{1}\left(a;\rho+1+n^{\prime},-n^{\prime};\rho-b+a+2;1-\frac{\lambda}{Q},1-\frac{\lambda}{Q-\lambda^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\rho-b+2)}{\Gamma(\rho-b+a+2)}\frac{\rho!}{\lambda^{\rho+1}}

\displaystyle\times F_{1}\left(\rho-b+2;\rho+1+n^{\prime},-n^{\prime};\rho-b+a+2;1-\frac{Q}{\lambda},1-\frac{Q-\lambda^{\prime}}{\lambda}\right)

(44)

where in the last step we used the relation

\displaystyle F_{1}(\alpha;\beta_{1},\beta_{2};\gamma;z_{1},z_{2})={(1-z_{1})}^{-\beta_{1}}{(1-z_{2})}^{-\beta_{2}}F_{1}\left(\gamma-\alpha;\beta_{1},\beta_{2};\gamma;\frac{z_{1}}{z_{1}-1},\frac{z_{2}}{z_{2}-1}\right).

2.3
Asymptotic formula for the continuum-continuum transition amplitude with the inclusion of centrifugal potential

The purpose of this section is to demonstrate the mechanism with simpler mathematical expressions. Such approaches are usually available for describing the asymptotic behaviour at high kinetic energies, while we try to make the compromise that the formula applied at low kinetic energies should at least give a semi-quantitative description, such that the main features are still captured. Let us consider the phase contribution of the centrifugal potential under the WKB approximation. The phase factor of the outgoing wave can be written as:

\displaystyle S_{l}(k,r;Z)=\int_{0}^{r}\sqrt{\frac{2Z}{\mathfrak{r}}-\frac{2b}{\mathfrak{r}^{2}}+k^{2}}~\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}k+\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{-1}Z}{k}-\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{-2}(2k^{2}b+Z^{2})}{2k^{3}}+\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{r}^{-3})~\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{r}

\displaystyle=kr+\ln(2kr)Z/k+r^{-1}\frac{2k^{2}b+Z^{2}}{2k^{3}}+\mathcal{O}(r^{-2})

(48)

where
b=l(l+1)/2
comes from the centrifugal potential, and the expansion is made at
r\rightarrow\infty
. Thus, the outgoing radial wave function can be estimated as:

\displaystyle rR_{k,l}(r)\approx\exp(\mathrm{i}S_{l}(k,r;Z))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kr}{(2kr)}^{\mathrm{i}Z/k}\exp(\mathrm{i}qr^{-1}+\mathcal{O}(r^{-2}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kr}{(2kr)}^{\mathrm{i}Z/k}\left(1+\mathrm{i}qr^{-1}+\mathcal{O}(r^{-2})\right)

(49)

where
q=(2k^{2}b+Z^{2})/(2k^{3})
scales with
k^{-3}
for the
s
(
l=0
) wave, but with
k^{-1}
for waves with higher angular momenta. This explains why the phase contribution of the centrifugal potential should not be neglected, as
k^{-1}
is a rather slow convergence under the experimental context (typically
E={10}^{1}\sim{10}^{2}~\mathrm{eV}
,
k=1\sim 3~\mathrm{a.u.}
). The dipole transition between two outgoing waves can thus be approximated as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{(\mathrm{P})}_{l\rightarrow l^{\prime}}(k,k^{\prime};r_{0};Z)\approx-\frac{\pi}{2}N_{k}N_{k^{\prime}}\frac{{(2k)}^{\mathrm{i}Z/k}}{{(2k^{\prime})}^{\mathrm{i}Z/k^{\prime}}}\int_{r_{0}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(k^{\prime}-k)r}~r^{\mathrm{i}Z/k-\mathrm{i}Z/k^{\prime}}\left[r+\mathrm{i}(q-q^{\prime})+\mathcal{O}(r^{-1})\right]~\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\pi}{2}N_{k}N_{k^{\prime}}\frac{{(2k)}^{\mathrm{i}Z/k}}{{(2k^{\prime})}^{\mathrm{i}Z/k^{\prime}}}\left[\frac{\Gamma(s,\Lambda r_{0})}{\Lambda^{s}}+\mathrm{i}(q-q^{\prime})\frac{\Gamma(s-1,\Lambda r_{0})}{\Lambda^{s-1}}\right]

(50)

where
s=2+\mathrm{i}Z/k-\mathrm{i}Z/k^{\prime}
and
\Lambda=\mathrm{i}(k^{\prime}-k)
.
The factor of
1/2
comes from the fact that the final state represented by the Kummer’s function contains the
k^{\prime}
- and
(-k^{\prime})
-parts, while the
(-k^{\prime})
-part practically vanishes due to the large imaginary part in the gamma function
[
58
]
.
Here we have used the incomplete gamma function

\Gamma(s,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t}~\mathrm{d}t

(51)

to include the case where the integral starts from
r_{0}>0
instead of 0. This can be useful for the more complex scattering problem, where the (time-dependent) Schrödinger equation is numerically solved inside the radius
r_{0}
, while the outgoing electron wavefunction is extrapolated beyond
r_{0}
using the
l
-dependent asymptotic formula, assuming that the potential is converged except the Coulomb (
\sim\mathcal{O}(r^{-1})
) and centrifugal-like (
\sim\mathcal{O}(r^{-2})
) parts.
When
r_{0}=0

\Gamma(s,0)=\Gamma(s)
is the gamma function, and
\Gamma(s)/\Gamma(s-1)=s-1
.
The first term in equation (
50
) corresponds to equation (
14
) reported by Dahlström
et al.

[
22
,
23
,
59
]
, and the second term depends on the angular momenta of the initial and the final states.

Note that Dahlström
et al.
also reported another formula (equation (
15

[
23
]
:

rR_{k,l}(r)\approx\exp(\mathrm{i}S_{l}(k,r;Z)){k}^{1/2}{\left(2Z/r+k^{2}\right)}^{-1/4}

(52)

where the amplitude factor is

{\left(\frac{2Z}{k^{2}r}+1\right)}^{-1/4}=1-\frac{Z}{2k^{2}r}+\mathcal{O}(r^{-2}).

(53)

Following the similar treatment, equation (
50

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{(\mathrm{P+A})}_{l\rightarrow l^{\prime}}(k,k^{\prime};r_{0};Z)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\pi}{2}N_{k}N_{k^{\prime}}\frac{{(2k)}^{\mathrm{i}Z/k}}{{(2k^{\prime})}^{\mathrm{i}Z/k^{\prime}}}\left[\frac{\Gamma(s,\Lambda r_{0})}{\Lambda^{s}}+(\mathrm{i}q-\mathrm{i}q^{\prime}-Q-Q^{\prime})\frac{\Gamma(s-1,\Lambda r_{0})}{\Lambda^{s-1}}\right]

(54)

where
q=(2k^{2}b+Z^{2})/(2k^{3})=(l(l+1)k^{2}+Z^{2})/(2k^{3})
and
Q=Z/(2k^{2})
. One can see the parallelity between these terms and the parameter defined in equations (13) and (16a) of reference

The analytical formula (

Time delays near Cooper minima

76

are known to have either local maximum or minimum, and in a work recently published by the present authors
[

, we demonstrate that the sign of the time delay relies on the topological property of the transition amplitude on the complex plane. Here, the Cooper-like minimum leads to a local maximum of the CC time delay, provided that the channel is pure. When the channel is coupled to other channels, as analysed in

, the time-delay structure may be affected.
Numerical verification and applications to Rydberg atoms and molecules are in progress and will be published as a following work.

4
Conclusion

In conclusion, we have proposed an analytical formula for calculating the CC transition amplitudes for the hydrogen-like atoms which uses the confluent hypergeometric functions of the first and second kinds as the final and intermediate states, respectively.
We first derived the integral between the confluent hypergeometric functions of the first and second kind, which can be expressed using the Appell’s
F_{1}
function.
Then we showed that this formula excellently reproduces the phase and the Fano’s propensity rule in the CC transition, compared to the TDSE and other theoretical approaches.
In addition, we modified the WKB asymptotic approximation by expanding the terms to
r^{-1}
, to which the centrifugal potential contributes. The Fano’s propensity rule and the main feature of the phases in CC transitions can be well captured with this relatively simple expression that involves only the gamma function.
The CC transition time delays derived from this analytical method coincide with the formula describing the Coulomb-laser coupling time delay, indicating that our approach may be extended for calculating the angle-dependent time delay in the streaking experiments. Detailed investigation in the streaking context is in progress and will be published in a separate work.
Finally, we used our method to calculate the relative phases of linear-linear and circular-circular RABBIT schemes at different electron emission angles. Even at kinetic energy as low as 3.3 eV, our proposed analytical formula quantitatively agrees with both the TDSE calculation and the experimental values.
We also checked the case with the shifted expansion center for calculating the spherical harmonics, and the results indicates that our method is not very sensitive to the shape of the short-range potential field, which opens the possibility of adapting our method to molecules.
For states with high angular momenta, our analytical formula predicts that the absorption pathway of
l\rightarrow l-1
has a Cooper-like minimum, which affects its CC time delay. The detailed investigation will be presented in a following work.
We further note that the analytical form for the radial integral is not limited to the dipole transition, and the expression for multipole transitions is given in
C
. Liao
et al.
recently investigated the linearly polarised RABBIT beyond the dipole approximation based on a modified version of the formula reported by Dahlström
et al.

[
78
]
. Our formula may also provide a more accurate evaluation of the non-dipole terms in the low-kinetic-energy region.

Appendix A
Derivation for the radial integral

In order to obtain
\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\rho}(a,b,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)
, we use the integration form of the confluent hypergeometric function of the second kind:

U(a,b;z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-zt}t^{a-1}{(t+1)}^{b-a-1}\mathrm{d}t

(60)

and thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\rho}(a,b,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-Q\xi}\xi^{\rho}U(a,b;\lambda\xi)\Phi(-n^{\prime},\rho+1;\lambda^{\prime}\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(Q+\lambda t)\xi}t^{a-1}{(t+1)}^{b-a-1}\xi^{\rho}\Phi(-n^{\prime},\rho+1;\lambda^{\prime}\xi)\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\xi.

(61)

By using the following formula for the
{{}_{2}F_{1}}
function
[
54
]
, equation (07.23.07.0003.01):

\displaystyle{{}_{2}F_{1}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\xi}\xi^{\beta-1}\Phi(\alpha,\gamma;\xi z)\mathrm{d}\xi

(62)

and swapping the order of integration, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\rho}(a,b,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)=\frac{\rho!}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{a-1}{(t+1)}^{b-a-1}}{{(Q+\lambda t)}^{\rho+1}}{{}_{2}F_{1}}\left(-n^{\prime},\rho+1,\rho+1,\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{Q+\lambda t}\right)\mathrm{d}t.

(63)

Using the definition of the generalised hypergeometric functions
[
54
]
, equation (07.19.02.0002.01):

{{}_{2}F_{1}}(\alpha,\beta,\beta;z)=~_{1}F_{0}(\alpha;;z)={(1-z)}^{-\alpha}

(64)

we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\rho}(a,b,\lambda;n^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime};Q)=\frac{\rho!}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{a-1}{(t+1)}^{b-a-1}}{{(Q+\lambda t)}^{\rho+1}}{\left(1-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{Q+\lambda t}\right)}^{n^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho!~{(Q-\lambda^{\prime})}^{n^{\prime}}}{Q^{\rho+1+n^{\prime}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{a-1}{(t+1)}^{b-a-1}{\left(\frac{\lambda}{Q}t+1\right)}^{-(\rho+1+n^{\prime})}{\left(\frac{\lambda}{Q-\lambda^{\prime}}t+1\right)}^{n^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t.

(65)

Let us use the integral representation of the Appell’s
F_{1}
function
[
54
]
, equation (07.36.07.0001.01):

\displaystyle F_{1}(\alpha;\beta_{1},\beta_{2};\gamma;z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\gamma-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{h^{\alpha-1}(1-h)^{\gamma-\alpha-1}}{(1-hz_{1})^{\beta_{1}}(1-hz_{2})^{\beta_{2}}}\mathrm{d}h

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\gamma-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(t+1)^{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}-\gamma}t^{\alpha-1}}{{\left[(1-z_{1})t+1\right]}^{\beta_{1}}{\left[(1-z_{2})t+1\right]}^{\beta_{2}}}\mathrm{d}t

(66)

where we used
h=\frac{t}{t+1}
. Compared to equation (
65
), we have
\alpha=a
,
\beta_{1}=\rho+1+n^{\prime}
,
\beta_{2}=-n^{\prime}
,
\gamma=\rho-b+a+2
,
z_{1}=1-\frac{\lambda}{Q}
, and
z_{2}=1-\frac{\lambda}{Q-\lambda^{\prime}}
. Therefore, we finally get equation (
44
) in the main text.

Appendix D
Explanation of Cooper-like minima

The Cooper-like minimum in the absorption pathway of the
l\rightarrow l-1
channel of CC transition originates from the transition amplitude between the irregular part of the intermediate-state wavefunction and the final-state wavefunction. Let us consider the asymptotic behaviour of the function
u_{l}(k,r)
in expression (
26
). The irregular (
g_{l}(k,r)
) and the regular (
f_{l}(k,r)
) parts behave like the cosine and sine functions in the far field, respectively. Due to the factor
{(-\mathrm{i})}^{l}
, the wave of two neighboring
l
s will have phase shift of
\pi/2
; i.e. the sine and cosine are swapped.
