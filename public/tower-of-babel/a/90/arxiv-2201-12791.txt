Integral operators defined “up to a polynomial”
Serena Dipierro, Aleksandr Dzhugan, Enrico Valdinoci

Abstract
. We introduce a suitable notion of integral operators (comprising the fractional Laplacian as a particular case) acting on functions with minimal requirements at infinity. For these functions, the classical definition would lead to divergent expressions, thus we replace it with an appropriate framework obtained by a cut-off procedure. The notion obtained in this way quotients out the polynomials which produce the divergent pattern once the cut-off is removed. We also present results of stability under the appropriate notion of convergence and compatibility results between polynomials of different orders. Additionally, we address the solvability of the Dirichlet problem. The theory is developed in general in the pointwise sense. A viscosity counterpart is also presented under the additional assumption that the interaction kernel has a sign, in conformity with the maximum principle structure.

1.
Introduction

A classical line of investigation in mathematical analysis and mathematical physics consists in the study
of integro-differential operators. The motivations for this stream of research come both from
theoretical mathematics (such as harmonic analysis, singular integral theory, fractional calculus, etc.)
and concrete problems in applied sciences
(with questions related to water waves, crystal dislocations, finance, optimization, minimal surfaces, etc.):
see e.g. the introduction in
[
8
]
and the references therein for a number of explicit motivations
and examples.

A special focus of this stream of research deals with linear
integro-differential operators of the form

(1.1)

Au(x)={\mbox{P.V.}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u(x)-u(y))K(x,y)\,dy=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}(u(x)-u(y))K(x,y)\,dy.

As customary, the notation
B_{\varepsilon}(x)
denotes the open ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at the point
x

(when
x
is the origin, one simply uses the notation
B_{\varepsilon}
for short).
The notation “P.V.” above (which will be omitted in the rest of this paper for the sake of simplicity)
means “in the principal value sense” and takes into account possible integral cancellations.
The action of such operator is to “weight” the oscillations of the function
u

according to the kernel
K
. To make sense of the expression above, two types of assumptions
need to be accounted for:

✓

if the kernel
K
is singular when
x=y
,
the function
u
needs to be regular enough near the point
x

(to allow integral cancellations and take advantage of the principal value in (
1.1
)),

✓

the function
u
needs to be sufficiently well-behaved at infinity
(namely, its growth has to be balanced by the kernel
K

to obtain in (
1.1
) a convergent integral at infinity).

Roughly speaking, these two conditions correspond to the request that the integral in (
1.1
)
converges both in the vicinity of the given point
x
and at infinity.
With respect to this,
the regularity condition is necessary for the local convergence of the integral
and it is common to differential (rather than integral) operators:
in a sense, for differential problems
the regularity of
u
ensures
that incremental quotients converge to derivatives
and, somewhat similarly, for integral problems
the regularity of
u
allows the increment inside the integral
to compensate the possible singularity of the kernel.
Instead, the second assumption on the behavior of
u
at infinity is needed only to guarantee the tail convergence,
it is a merely nonlocal feature
and has no counterpart for the case
of differential operators.

Conditions “at infinity” are also technically more difficult to deal with.
First of all, they are more expensive to be computed, since they need to account for
virtually all the values of the given function (while regularity ones
deal with the values in an arbitrarily small region). Furthermore,
these conditions are typically lost after one analyzes the problem at a small scale
(since blow-up procedures alter the behavior of the solutions at infinity,
with the aim of detecting the local patterns).
Moreover, it is sometimes difficult to detect optimal assumptions
for nonlocal problems even in very basic and fundamental questions
(see e.g. the open problem after Theorem 3.2 in
[
15
]
),
hence any theory based only on “essential” assumptions is doomed to have promising
future developments.

It would be therefore very desirable to develop a theory of integral
operators that does not heavily rely on the conditions at infinity
(in spite of the striking fact that these conditions are needed
even in the definition of the operator itself!).
To this end, a theory of “fractional Laplacian operators up to polynomials”
has been developed in
[
12
,
13
]

to address the case of functions with polynomial growth
(see also
[
17
]
for related approaches;
see e.g.
[
23
,
11
,
18
,
2
,
10
]

and the references therein for the basics
on the fractional Laplace operators).
The gist of this method
is to consider the family of cut-offs

(1.2)

\chi_{R}(x):=\begin{cases}1,&{\mbox{ if }}x\in B_{R};\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

and apply the operator to the function
\chi_{R}u
. Of course, in general,
it is not possible to send
R\to+\infty
, since the operator is not well-defined on
u
,
nevertheless it is still possible to perform such an operation once an appropriate polynomial
is “taken out” from the equation. Given the “rigidity” of the space of polynomials
(which is finite dimensional and easily computable) the method is flexible
and solid, it produces interesting results and can be efficiently combined with blow-up procedures,
see
[
5
,
1
]
.

The goal of this note is twofold: on the one hand, we review and extend
the theory developed in
[
12
,
13
]
, on the other hand, we generalize
the previous setting in order to include much more general classes of kernels
(in particular, kernels which are not necessarily scaling invariant).
Besides its interest in pure mathematics, this generalization
has a concrete impact on the study of
interaction potentials of interatomic type arising in molecular mechanics and materials science, such as
the Morse potential
[
19
]

(1.3)

K(x,y)=e^{-2(|x-y|-1)}-e^{-(|x-y|-1)},

the Buckingham potential
[
4
]

(1.4)

K(x,y)=e^{-|x-y|}-\frac{1}{|x-y|^{6}},

as well as their desingularized forms obtained by setting
K_{\varepsilon}(x,y):=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon},\,K(x,y)\right\}
.
Other classical potentials arising in probability and modelization include also the Gauss kernel

(1.5)

K(x,y)=e^{-|x-y|^{2}},

the Abel kernel

(1.6)

K(x,y)=e^{-|x-y|},

the mollification kernel

(1.7)

K(x,y)=\begin{cases}e^{-\frac{1}{1-|x-y|^{2}}}&{\mbox{ if }}|x-y|<1,\\
0&{\mbox{ if }}|x-y|\geqslant 1\end{cases}

and the class of kernels comparable to that of the fractional
Laplacian

(1.8)

\frac{\lambda}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\leqslant K(x,y)\leqslant\frac{\Lambda}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}

for
s\in(0,1)
and
\Lambda\geqslant\lambda>0
.

The theory of integral operators that we develop
is broad enough to include the kernels above (and others as well) into a unified setting.
The operators will be suitably defined
“up to a polynomial”, in a sense that will be made precise in Definition
2.3
.
This framework relies on a suitable decomposition of the integral operator
with respect to cut-off functions that is showcased in Theorem
2.1
.
This setting is stable under the appropriate
notion of convergence, as it will be detailed in Proposition
2.11
,
and it presents nice compatibility results between polynomials of different orders,
as it will be pointed out in
Corollary
2.13
and Lemma
2.14
.
We also stress that the generalized notion of operators that we deal with
is “as good as the classical one” in terms of producing solutions
for the associated Dirichlet problem: indeed,
as it will be clarified in
Theorem
3.1
, the solvability
of the classical Dirichlet problem in the class of functions
with nice behavior at infinity is sufficient to ensure the solvability
of the generalized Dirichlet problem for the operator defined “up to a polynomial”.

To develop this theory, we mainly focused on the case of sufficiently smooth
(though not necessarily well-behaved at infinity) functions. This choice was dictated by
three main reasons. First of all, we aimed at developing the core of the theory by focusing
on its essential features, rather than complicating it by additional difficulties.
Moreover, we intended to split the complications arising
from the possible lack of smoothness of the solutions with those produced by their behavior at infinity,
consistently with the initial discussion
presented right after (
1.1
). Additionally, we stress that
the generality of kernels addressed by our theory
goes far beyond the ones of “elliptic” type, therefore a comprehensive
regularity theory does not hold in such an extensive framework.

However, one can also recast our theory in terms of viscosity solutions.
For this, since viscosity theory relates to maximum principles,
one needs the additional assumption that the kernel has a sign.
In particular, in this context
one can obtain a viscosity definition of operators “up to a polynomial”
(see Definition
4.2
) and discuss its stability properties under uniform convergence
(see Lemma
4.7
) and the consistency properties with respect of polynomials of different degree
(see Corollary
4.8

and Lemma
4.9
).
When the structure is compatible with both settings,
the pointwise framework and the viscosity one are essentially equivalent
(see Lemma
4.6
).
Furthermore, for kernels comparable with that of the fractional Laplacian
a complete solvability of the Dirichlet problem can be obtained
(see Theorem
4.13
).

In the forthcoming Section
2
we introduce the main
definitions for integral operators “up to a polynomial”
and present their fundamental properties. The corresponding Dirichlet problem
will be discussed in Section
3
.

While Sections
2
and
3

focus on the pointwise definition of this generalized notion of operators,
we devote Section
4
to the corresponding viscosity theory.

A natural question deals with the consistency of the operator setting for functions that
are sufficiently well-behaved to allow definitions related to two different indices: roughly
speaking, in the best possible scenario, if we know that
Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f
and
j\leqslant m
,
can we say that
Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j}}{{=}}f
? Posed like this, the answer to this question is negative,
since, after all, in light of Lemma
2.12
, the function
f
is uniquely defined only “up to a polynomial”.
Nevertheless, the answer becomes positive if we take into account this additional polynomial normalization,
as stated in the next result:

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
j,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with
j\leqslant m
,
\vartheta\in[0,2]

and
K\in\mathcal{K}_{j,\vartheta}\cap\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}
.
Let
f
be bounded and continuous in
B_{1}
and let
u\in\mathcal{C}_{\vartheta,K}

such that

(2.57)

Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f

in
B_{1}
.

Then, there exist a function
\bar{f}
and a polynomial
P
of degree at most
m-1
,
such that
\bar{f}=f+P
and
Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j}}{{=}}\bar{f}
in
B_{1}
.

Proof.

Let
v:=(1-\chi_{4})u
and
w:=\chi_{4}u
. We notice that
v
,

w\in\mathcal{C}_{\vartheta,K}
. Hence, since
K\in\mathcal{K}_{j,\vartheta}
, recalling
Remark
2.6
, (
2.10
),
(
2.25
) and (
2.26
), we can write that

Av\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j}}{{=}}f_{v}=\int_{B_{4}^{c}}u(y)\psi(x,y)

in
B_{1}
. That is, by Definition
2.3
,

(2.58)

A(\chi_{R}v)=\int_{B_{4}^{c}}u(y)\psi(x,y)\,dy+\tilde{\varphi}_{R}+Q_{R},

for some
\tilde{\varphi}_{R}
such that
\tilde{\varphi}_{R}\to 0
in
B_{1}
as
R\to+\infty
and a polynomial
Q_{R}
of degree at most
j-1
.

Furthermore, by (
2.57
), and recalling Definition
2.3
, we get that

(2.59)

A(\chi_{R}u)=f+\varphi_{R}+P_{R},

for some
\varphi_{R}
such that
\varphi_{R}\to 0
if
R\to+\infty

and a polynomial
P_{R}
with deg

. Therefore,
subtracting (
2.58
) from (

f+\varphi_{R}+P_{R}-\int_{B_{4}^{c}}u(y)\psi(x,y)\,dy-\tilde{\varphi}_{R}-Q_{R}=A(\chi_{R}(u-v))=A(\chi_{R}w).

We notice that, for every

and every

\displaystyle A(\chi_{R}w)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\chi_{R}w(x)-\chi_{R}w(y))\,K(x,y)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\int_{B_{R}}(w(x)-w(y))\,K(x,y)\,dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}}w(x)\,K(x,y)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\int_{B_{4}}(w(x)-w(y))\,K(x,y)\,dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{4}}w(x)\,K(x,y)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\chi_{4}w(x)-\chi_{4}w(y))\,K(x,y)\,dy=A(\chi_{4}w)(x).

[

3.
The Dirichlet problem

In this section we consider the existence problem
for equations involving general operators that are defined “up to a polynomial”.
The main result is the following:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\vartheta\in[0,2]
,
K\in\mathcal{K}_{0,\vartheta}\cap{\mathcal{K}}_{m,\vartheta}

and
u\in\mathcal{C}_{\vartheta,K}
. Assume that

u_{0}\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(B_{1}^{c})
satisfies (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) and
f:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded and
continuous in
B_{1}
.

Additionally, suppose that
for any
\tilde{f}:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
which is bounded and
continuous in
B_{1}
and any
\tilde{u}_{0}\in L^{1}(B_{1}^{c})

there exists a unique
solution
\tilde{u}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\vartheta}
to the Dirichlet problem

(3.1)

\begin{cases}A\tilde{u}=\tilde{f}&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
\tilde{u}=\tilde{u}_{0}&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

Then, there exists a
function
u\in\mathcal{C}_{\vartheta,K}
such that

(3.2)

\begin{cases}Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
u=u_{0}&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

Also, the solution to (
3.2
) is not unique, since
the space of solutions of (
3.2
) has dimention
N_{m}
, with

(3.3)

N_{m}:=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\binom{j+n-1}{n-1}.

Proof.

To begin with, we prove the existence of solutions for (
3.2
). To do this, we define

u_{1}:=\chi_{B_{4}^{c}}\,u_{0}\qquad{\mbox{ and }}\qquad\tilde{u}_{0}:=\chi_{B_{4}\setminus B_{1}}\,u_{0}.

Since
u_{1}
vanishes in
B_{4}
and

K\in\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}
, we can write
Au_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f_{u_{1}}
in
B_{1}
,
for some function
f_{u_{1}}
, due to Remark
2.6
.

We now consider
the solution of (
3.1
) with
\tilde{f}:=f-f_{u_{1}}
. Therefore, using
Remark
2.5
and Corollary
2.13

we obtain that

\begin{cases}A\tilde{u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}\tilde{f}&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
\tilde{u}=\tilde{u}_{0}&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

Then, we set
u:=u_{1}+\tilde{u}
and we get that
Au=Au_{1}+A\tilde{u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f_{u_{1}}+\tilde{f}=f
in
B_{1}
.
Moreover, we have that
u=u_{1}+\tilde{u}_{0}=u_{0}
in
B_{1}^{c}
, that is
u\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\vartheta,K}

is solution of (
3.2
). This
establishes the existence of solution for (
3.2
).

Now we prove that solutions of (
3.2
)
are not unique and determine the dimension
of the corresponding linear space. For this, we notice that for any polynomial
P
with
\deg P\leqslant m-1
there exists a unique solution
\tilde{u}_{P}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\vartheta}
of the problem

(3.4)

\begin{cases}A\tilde{u}_{P}=P&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
\tilde{u}_{P}=0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c},\end{cases}

due to the existence and uniqueness assumption
for (
3.1
). This is equivalent to say that
A\tilde{u}_{P}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{=}}P
in
B_{1}
, thanks to Corollary
2.13
. Using Remark
2.5
, we obtain that
A\tilde{u}_{P}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}P
in
B_{1}
. Thus, applying
Remark
2.7
, we obtain that
\tilde{u}_{P}
is a solution of

(3.5)

\begin{cases}A\tilde{u}_{P}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
\tilde{u}_{P}=0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

From this it follows that if
u
is a solution of (
3.2
), then
u+\tilde{u}_{P}
is also a solution of (
3.2
).

Viceversa, if
u
and
v
are two solutions of (
3.2
), then
w:=u-v
is a solution of

\begin{cases}Aw\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
w=0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

Here we can apply Lemma
2.14
with
j:=0
thus obtaining that
Aw\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{=}}P
in
B_{1}
, where
P
is a polynomial of
\deg P\leqslant m-1
. Using again Corollary
2.13
, one deduces that

(3.6)

\begin{cases}Aw=P&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
w=0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

Therefore, the uniqueness of the solution of (
3.6
),
confronted with (
3.4
), gives us that
w=\tilde{u}_{P}
, and thus
v=u+\tilde{u}_{P}
.

This reasoning gives that the space of solutions of (
3.2
) is isomorphic to the space of polynomials with degree
less than or equal to
m-1
, which has exactly
dimension
N_{m}
, given by (
3.3
) (see e.g.
[
14
]
).
∎

4.
A viscosity approach

Up to now, we focused our attention on the case of equations defined pointwise.
In principle, this requires functions that are “sufficiently regular” for the equation to be satisfied at every given point.
However, a less restrictive approach adopted in the classical theory of elliptic equations
is to consider weaker notions of solutions (and possibly recover the pointwise setting via
an appropriate regularity theory): in this spirit, a convenient setting, which is also useful in case
of fully nonlinear equations, is that of viscosity solutions,
which does not require a high degree of regularity of the solution itself
since the equation is computed pointwise only at smooth functions touching from either below or above
(see e.g.
[
6
]
for a thorough discussion on viscosity solutions).

In this section, we recast the setting of general operators defined “up to a polynomial”
into the viscosity solution framework. To this end, we proceed as follows.
For all
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

and
\vartheta\in[0,2]
, we define
\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}^{+}
as the space of kernels
K=K(x,y)

verifying (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), and
such that

(4.1)

K(x,y)\geqslant 0{\mbox{ for all $x\in B_{1}$ and $y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}}.

Given
K\in\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}^{+}
, we consider the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{K}

of all the functions
u\in L^{1}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C(B_{4})\cap L^{\infty}(B_{4})
for which

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant m-1}\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{3}}|u(y)|\,|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(x,y)|\,dy<+\infty\quad{\mbox{ for all\penalty\ $R>3$ and $x\in B_{1}$}}

(4.3)

and

\displaystyle\int_{B^{c}_{3}}|u(y)|\sup_{{|\alpha|=m}\atop{x\in B_{1}}}|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(x,y)|\,dy<+\infty.

Remark 4.1
.

Notice that if (
4.1
) holds true and
K\in\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
some
\vartheta\in[0,2]
, then
K\in\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}^{+}
.

In the viscosity framework we introduce the following definition.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\vartheta\in[0,2]
,

K\in\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}^{+}
,

u\in\mathcal{V}_{K}
and
f:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and continuous.
We say that

Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f\quad\mbox{in $B_{1}$ in the viscosity sense}

if there exist a family of polynomials
P_{R}
, with
\deg P_{R}\leqslant m-1
, and bounded
and continuous functions
f_{R}:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(4.4)

A(\chi_{R}u)=f_{R}+P_{R}

in
B_{1}
in the viscosity sense, with

(4.5)

\lim_{R\to+\infty}f_{R}(x)=f(x)\quad{\mbox{ uniformly in }}B_{1}.

Remark 4.3
.

We point out that the limit in (
4.5
)
is assumed to hold uniformly (this is a stronger assumption
than the one in (
2.28
) that was assumed
for the pointwise setting,
and it is taken here to make the setting compatible with the viscosity method,
see e.g. the proof of the forthcoming Corollary
4.8
). See also
[
1
]

for related observations.

Remark 4.4
.

We observe that,
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
K\in\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}^{+}\cap\mathcal{K}_{m+j,\vartheta}^{+}
,

\mbox{if }Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f,\mbox{ then }Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m+j}}{{=}}f

in
B_{1}
in the viscosity sense of Definition
4.2
.

Remark 4.5
.

From Definition
4.2
it follows that any polynomial of degree less than
or equal to
m-1
can be arbitrarily added to
f_{R}

and subtracted from
P_{R}
in (
4.4
),
hence, for any polynomial
P
with
\deg P\leqslant m-1
we have that

\mbox{if }Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f,\mbox{ then }Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f+P

in
B_{1}
in the viscosity sense of Definition
4.2
.

We now establish that when the structure is compatible with the both the settings in Definitions
2.3

and
4.2
, the pointwise and viscosity frameworks are equivalent:

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\vartheta\in[0,2]
,
K\in{\mathcal{K}}_{m,\vartheta}
,

u\in\mathcal{C}_{\vartheta,K}
and
f:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and continuous.
If (
4.1
) holds true and
u
is a solution of

Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f\mbox{ in the
sense of Definition\penalty\ \ref{def}},

then
K\in\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}^{+}
,
u\in\mathcal{V}_{K}
and
it is a solution of

Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f\mbox{ in the viscosity sense of Definition\penalty\ \ref{defv}}.

Conversely,
let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\vartheta\in[0,2]
,

K\in\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}^{+}
,

u\in\mathcal{V}_{K}
and
f:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and continuous.
If
K\in\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}

and
u\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\vartheta,K}
is a solution of

Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f\mbox{ in the viscosity sense of Definition\penalty\ \ref{defv}},

then
u
is a solution of

Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f\mbox{ in the sense of Definition\penalty\ \ref{def}}.

Proof.

Assume that
u
is a solution of
Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f\mbox{ in $B_{1}$ in the sense of Definition\penalty\ \ref{def}}
. It follows that there exist a family of polynomials
P_{R}
with
\deg P_{R}\leqslant m-1
and functions
f_{R}
such that

(4.6)

A(\chi_{R}u)=f_{R}+P_{R}

pointwise in
B_{1}
.
Since
u\in\mathcal{C}_{\vartheta,K}

we have that also
u\in\mathcal{V}_{K}
. Moreover, we have that
K\in\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}^{+}
, due to (
4.1
) and Remark
4.1
.

Now we observe that if
v\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\vartheta}
and vanishes outside
B_{2}
,
g
is a bounded and continuous function,
and
Av=g
pointwise in
B_{1}
, then also

(4.7)

Av=g
in
B_{1}
in the viscosity sense.

To check this,
let
\varphi
be a smooth function touching
v
from below at some point
x_{0}\in B_{1}
. Then, we have that
v(y)-\varphi(y)\geqslant 0=v(x_{0})-\varphi(x_{0})
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and therefore, by (
4.1
),

A\varphi(x_{0})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi(y))K(x_{0},y)\,dy\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(v(x_{0})-v(y))K(x_{0},y)\,dy=Av(x_{0})=g(x_{0}).

Similarly, if
\varphi
touches
v
from above, one obtains the opposite inequality, and these
observations complete the proof of (
4.7
).

As a consequence of (
4.6
)
and (
4.7
), we obtain that

A(\chi_{R}u)=f_{R}+P_{R}

in
B_{1}
in the viscosity sense.

Hence, to finish the first part of the proof, we show that

(4.8)

f_{R}\to f
uniformly in
B_{1}
.

For this, we observe that, in light of Remark
2.6
, we can write
Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f_{u}
in
B_{1}

in the sense of Definition
2.3
. That is, recalling Corollary
2.2
,
there exist functions
f_{u,R}
and a family of polynomials
P_{u,\tau_{R}}
, which have degree at most
m-1
, such that

(4.9)

A(\chi_{R}u)=f_{u,R}+P_{u,\chi_{R}}

pointwise in
B_{1}
.

Furthermore, using Lemma
2.8
, we have that, if
R^{\prime}>R>4
, there exists a polynomial
P_{R,R^{\prime}}

of degree at most
m-1
such that

(4.10)

\|f_{u,R^{\prime}}-f_{u,R}-P_{R,R^{\prime}}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\leqslant\int_{B_{R}^{c}}|u(y)|\sup_{{|\alpha|=m}\atop{x\in B_{1}}}|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(x,y)|\,dy.

We now claim that

(4.11)

P_{R,R^{\prime}}=0.

Indeed, by a careful inspection of the proof of Lemma
2.8
, one can notice that
the polynomial
P_{R,R^{\prime}}
is explicit and, denoting by
v:=(1-\chi_{4})u
, it is equal to

P_{v,\chi_{R^{\prime}}}-P_{u,\chi_{R^{\prime}}}-P_{v,\chi_{R}}+P_{u,\chi_{R}},

where we have used the notation of Theorem
2.1
.
In particular, recalling the notation in formulas (
2.19
) and (
2.21
), we have that
the coefficients of the polynomial
P_{R,R^{\prime}}
are given by

\displaystyle\int_{B^{c}_{3}}(\chi_{R^{\prime}}v)(y)\frac{\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(0,y)}{\alpha!}\,dy-\int_{B^{c}_{3}}(\chi_{R^{\prime}}u)(y)\frac{\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(0,y)}{\alpha!}\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{B^{c}_{3}}(\chi_{R}v)(y)\frac{\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(0,y)}{\alpha!}\,dy+\int_{B^{c}_{3}}(\chi_{R}u)(y)\frac{\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(0,y)}{\alpha!}\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}\setminus B_{4}}u(y)\frac{\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(0,y)}{\alpha!}\,dy-\int_{B_{R^{\prime}}\setminus B_{3}}u(y)\frac{\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(0,y)}{\alpha!}\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{4}}u(y)\frac{\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(0,y)}{\alpha!}\,dy+\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{3}}u(y)\frac{\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(0,y)}{\alpha!}\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{4}\setminus B_{3}}u(y)\frac{\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(0,y)}{\alpha!}\,dy+\int_{B_{4}\setminus B_{3}}u(y)\frac{\partial^{\alpha}_{x}K(0,y)}{\alpha!}\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

for every
|\alpha|\leqslant m-1
, which proves (
4.11
).

Hence, using the information of formula (
4.11
) into (
4.10
), we obtain
that
f_{u,R}
converges to
f_{u}
uniformly in
B_{1}
.

Now, as a consequence of (
4.6
) and (
4.9
),
we have that

(4.12)

\lim_{R\to+\infty}\big(P_{u,\chi_{R}}-P_{R}\big)=\lim_{R\to+\infty}\big(f_{R}-f_{u,R}\big)=f-f_{u}

in
B_{1}
. Therefore, in light of Lemma 2.1 in
[
12
]
, we have that
the convergence in (
4.12
) is uniform in
B_{1}
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle\|f_{R}-f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\leqslant\|f_{R}-f_{u,R}+f_{u}-f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+\|f_{u,R}-f_{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}.

These considerations prove (
4.8
) and therefore,
the first part of the proof is complete.

Now take
K\in\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}^{+}

and a solution
u\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\vartheta,K}
to
Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f\mbox{ in $B_{1}$ in the viscosity
sense of Definition\penalty\ \ref{defv}}
. We have that there exist a family of
polynomials
P_{R}
with
\deg P_{R}\leqslant m-1
and functions
f_{R}
such that

(4.13)

A(\chi_{R}u)=f_{R}+P_{R}
in
B_{1}
in the
viscosity sense.

Our objective is now to check that

(4.14)

the equation in (
4.13
) holds true in the pointwise
sense as well.

Indeed, once this is established, we can send
R\to+\infty

and conclude that
Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f
in the pointwise
sense of Definition
2.3
.
To prove (
4.14
), we use a convolution argument.
We pick
\rho\in(0,1)
and we define
v_{\varepsilon}
to be the convolution
of
\chi_{R}u
against a given mollifier
\eta_{\varepsilon}
.
We also denote by
g_{\varepsilon}
the convolution of
f_{R}+P_{R}

against
\eta_{\varepsilon}
and we remark that, if
\varepsilon
is small enough, then
Av_{\varepsilon}=g_{\varepsilon}
in
B_{\rho}
in the
viscosity sense, and actually also in the pointwise sense, since
v_{\varepsilon}

is smooth and can be used itself as a test function in the viscosity definition.
Hence, we can take any point
x_{0}\in B_{\rho}
and conclude that

(4.15)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})-v_{\varepsilon}(y))K(x_{0},y)\,dy=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}Av_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}g_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})=f_{R}(x_{0})+P_{R}(x_{0}).

We now claim that, for all
R>5
,

(4.16)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})-v_{\varepsilon}(y))K(x_{0},y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\chi_{R}(x_{0})u(x_{0})-\chi_{R}(y)u(y))K(x_{0},y)\,dy.

We stress that, once this is proved, then (
4.14
)
would follow directly from (
4.15
).
Hence, our goal now is to check (
4.16
).
We perform the argument when
\vartheta\in(1,2]

(the argument when
\vartheta\in[0,1]
being similar
and simpler, not requiring any additional symmetrization).
We exploit (
2.4
) to see that

(4.17)

\begin{split}&2\int_{B_{1}(x_{0})}(v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})-v_{\varepsilon}(y))K(x_{0},y)\,dy\\
&\qquad=\int_{B_{1}}(v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})-v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}+z))K(x_{0},x_{0}+z)\,dz+\int_{B_{1}}(v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})-v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}-z))K(x_{0},x_{0}-z)\,dz\\
&\qquad=\int_{B_{1}}\big(2v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})-v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}+z)-v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}-z)\big)K(x_{0},x_{0}+z)\,dz.\end{split}

Also, since
u\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\vartheta,K}
(and we are supposing
\vartheta\in(1,2]
),
for all
z\in B_{1}
,

\displaystyle|2v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})-v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}+z)-v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}-z)|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}+tz)-\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}-tz)\Big)\cdot z\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant|z|\int_{0}^{1}\Big|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}+tz)-\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}-tz)\Big|\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad=|z|\int_{0}^{1}\left|\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}\nabla(\chi_{R}u)(x_{0}+tz-\zeta)\eta_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)\,d\zeta-\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}\nabla(\chi_{R}u)(x_{0}-tz-\zeta)\eta_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\right|\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant|z|\int_{0}^{1}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla(\chi_{R}u)(x_{0}+tz-\zeta)-\nabla(\chi_{R}u)(x_{0}-tz-\zeta)\right|\eta_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant C|z|^{\vartheta}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}\eta_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad=C|z|^{\vartheta},

for some
C>0
.

From this and the Dominated Convergence Theorem,
recalling (
2.3
), we deduce from (
4.17
)
that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}2\int_{B_{1}(x_{0})}(v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})-v_{\varepsilon}(y))K(x_{0},y)\,dy=\int_{B_{1}}\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\big(2v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})-v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}+z)-v_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}-z)\big)K(x_{0},x_{0}+z)\,dz

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{B_{1}}\big(2(\chi_{R}u)(x_{0})-(\chi_{R}u)(x_{0}+z)-(\chi_{R}u)(x_{0}-z)\big)K(x_{0},x_{0}+z)\,dz

\displaystyle\qquad=2\int_{B_{1}(x_{0})}\big((\chi_{R}u)(x_{0})-(\chi_{R}u)(y)\big)K(x_{0},y)\,dy.

Using again the Dominated Convergence Theorem,
one deduces (
4.16
) from the previous equation, as desired.
∎

Next result shows
the stability of the equation under the uniform convergence in the viscosity sense. To this end, we will also
assume other mild conditions on the kernel.
First of all, we assume a continuity hypothesis in the first variable,
that is we suppose that

(4.18)

{\mbox{for all\penalty\ $y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and all\penalty\ $x_{0}\in B_{3}\setminus\{y\}$, }}\;\lim_{x\to x_{0}}K(x,y)=K(x_{0},y).

Additionally, we assume
a local integrability condition outside a possible singularity of the kernel
and a locally uniform version of condition (
2.3
), namely we suppose that

(4.19)

{\mbox{for all $x_{0}\in B_{3}$ and all\penalty\ $r>0$, }}\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}(x_{0})}\;\sup_{x\in B_{3}\setminus B_{r}(y)}|K(x,y)|\,dy<+\infty

and

(4.20)

\int_{B_{3}}\sup_{x\in B_{1}}|x-y|^{2}\,|K(x,y)|\,dy<+\infty.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\vartheta\in[0,2]
.
Let
K\in\mathcal{K}_{0,\vartheta}^{+}
satisfying (
4.18
), (
4.19
) and (
4.20
).
For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
u_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{K}
and
f_{k}
be bounded and continuous
in
B_{1}
. Assume that

(4.21)

Au_{k}=f_{k}

in
B_{1}
in the viscosity sense, that

f_{k}
converges uniformly in
B_{1}
to some function
f
as
k\to+\infty
,

that

(4.22)

u_{k}
converges uniformly in
B_{4}
to some function
u\in\mathcal{V}_{K}
as
k\to+\infty

and that

(4.23)

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3}}\big|u(y)-u_{k}(y)\big|\sup_{x\in B_{1}}|K(x,y)|\,dy=0.

Then,

Au=f

in
B_{1}
in the viscosity sense.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in B_{1}
and
\rho>0
such that
B_{\rho}(x_{0})\Subset B_{1}
.
Let
\varphi\in C^{2}(\overline{B_{\rho}(x_{0})})
with
\varphi=u
outside
B_{\rho}(x_{0})
.
Suppose that
v:=\varphi-u
has a local maximum at
x_{0}
.

We define, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\varepsilon_{k}:=\|u-u_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+\frac{1}{k}

and

\varphi_{k}(x):=\begin{cases}\varphi(x)-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}\,|x-x_{0}|^{2}&{\mbox{ in }}\overline{B_{\rho}(x_{0})},\\
u_{k}(x)&{\mbox{ in }}\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}.\end{cases}

We let
v_{k}:=\varphi_{k}-u_{k}
and
x_{k}\in\overline{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}
be such that

v_{k}(x_{k})=\max_{\overline{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}}v_{k}.

We observe that

\displaystyle|x_{k}-x_{0}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(x_{k})}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v(x_{k})-v_{k}(x_{k})+u(x_{k})-u_{k}(x_{k})}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{v(x_{0})-v_{k}(x_{0})+\varepsilon_{k}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi_{k}(x_{0})-u(x_{0})+u_{k}(x_{0})+\varepsilon_{k}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-u(x_{0})+u_{k}(x_{0})+\varepsilon_{k}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}.

Thus, since
\varepsilon_{k}
is infinitesimal due to (
4.22
),
we have that
x_{k}
converges to
x_{0}
as
k\to+\infty
and, in particular,
the function
v_{k}
has an interior maximum at
x_{k}
.
This and (
4.21
) give that

(4.24)

0\leqslant A\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-f_{k}(x_{k})\leqslant A\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-f(x_{0})+|f(x_{0})-f(x_{k})|+\|f-f_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}.

Now we claim that

(4.25)

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y)\,dy=\int_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}\big(\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi(y)\big)K(x_{0},y)\,dy.

To this end, we first observe that

(4.26)

\begin{split}\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\,=\,&\varphi(x_{k})-\varphi(y)+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}|y-x_{0}|^{2}-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}|x_{k}-x_{0}|^{2}\\
=\,&\varphi(x_{k})-\varphi(y)+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}(2x_{0}-x_{k}-y)\cdot(x_{k}-y).\end{split}

We define

(4.27)

F(y):=\sup_{x\in B_{1}}|x-y|^{2}\,|K(x,y)|

and we observe that
F\in L^{1}(B_{3})
, thanks to (
4.20
).
Accordingly, by the absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integrals,
for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that if the Lebesgue
measure of a set
Z\subset B_{3}
is less than
\delta
, then

(4.28)

\int_{Z}F(y)\,dy\leqslant\varepsilon.

We recall (
2.4
) and we see that, for
k
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\rho/2}(x_{k})}\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y)\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{\rho/2}}\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(x_{k}+z)\big)K(x_{k},x_{k}+z)\,dz+\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{\rho/2}}\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(x_{k}-z)\big)K(x_{k},x_{k}-z)\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{\rho/2}}\big(2\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(x_{k}+z)-\varphi_{k}(x_{k}-z)\big)K(x_{k},x_{k}+z)\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}\chi_{B_{\rho/2}(x_{k})}(y)\big(2\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)-\varphi_{k}(2x_{k}-y)\big)K(x_{k},y)\,dy.

Thus, for all
y\in B_{\rho}(x_{0})
we define

\zeta_{k}(y):=\frac{1}{2}\chi_{B_{\rho/2}(x_{k})}(y)\big(2\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)-\varphi_{k}(2x_{k}-y)\big)K(x_{k},y)

and we point out that

\displaystyle|\zeta_{k}(y)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big|\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\big)+\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(2x_{k}-y)\big)\Big|\,|K(x_{k},y)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left|\left(\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\varphi_{k}\big(tx_{k}+(1-t)y\big)\,dt-\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\varphi_{k}\big(tx_{k}+(1-t)(2x_{k}-y)\big)\,dt\right)\cdot(x_{k}-y)\right|\,|K(x_{k},y)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left((1-t)\int_{0}^{1}D^{2}\varphi_{k}\Big(\tau\big(tx_{k}+(1-t)y\big)+(1-\tau)\big(tx_{k}+(1-t)(2x_{k}-y)\big)\Big)\,d\tau\right)\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad(x_{k}-y)\cdot(x_{k}-y)\Bigg|\,|K(x_{k},y)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C\,|x_{k}-y|^{2}\,|K(x_{k},y)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C\,F(y),

where the notation in (
4.27
) was used.
In particular, since
F\in L^{1}(B_{3})
by (
4.20
),
we can exploit
the absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integrals (see (
4.28
)) and deduce that
for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that if the Lebesgue
measure of a set
Z\subset B_{\rho}(x_{0})
is less than
\delta
, then

\int_{Z}|\zeta_{k}(y)|\,dy\leqslant C\varepsilon.

Hence, recalling (
4.18
),
we utilize the Vitali Convergence Theorem
and obtain that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}\zeta_{k}(y)\,dy=\int_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}\lim_{k\to+\infty}\zeta_{k}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{\rho/2}(x_{0})}\big(2\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi(y)-\varphi(2x_{0}-y)\big)K(x_{0},y)

which proves that

(4.29)

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B_{\rho/2}(x_{0})}\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y)\,dy=\int_{B_{\rho/2}(x_{0})}\big(\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi(y)\big)K(x_{0},y)\,dy.

Now, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\rho/2}(x_{0})
we define

\eta_{k}(y):=\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y).

We observe that, if
k
is sufficiently large,
for every
y\in B_{3}\setminus B_{\rho/2}(x_{0})
,

|\eta_{k}(y)|\leqslant C\,\sup_{x\in B_{3}\setminus B_{\rho/4}(y)}|K(x,y)|,

which belongs to
L^{1}(B_{\rho}(x_{0})\setminus B_{\rho/2}(x_{0}))
due to (
4.19
).

Consequently, by (
4.18
) and the Dominated Convergence Theorem,

\begin{split}&\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})\setminus B_{\rho/2}(x_{0})}\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y)\,dy=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})\setminus B_{\rho/2}(x_{0})}\eta_{k}(y)\,dy\\
&\qquad=\int_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})\setminus B_{\rho/2}(x_{0})}\lim_{k\to+\infty}\eta_{k}(y)\,dy=\int_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})\setminus B_{\rho/2}(x_{0})}\big(\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi(y)\big)K(x_{0},y)\,dy.\end{split}

This and (
4.29
)
show the validity of (
4.25
).

Having completed the proof of (
4.25
), we now claim that

(4.30)

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\rho}(x_{0})}\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\rho}(x_{0})}\big(\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi(y)\big)K(x_{0},y)\,dy.

Indeed, for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}
we set

\mu_{k}(y):=\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y)=\big(\varphi(x_{k})-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}|x_{k}-x_{0}|^{2}-u_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y).

If
y\in B_{3}\setminus\overline{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}

and
k
is sufficiently large, then, by (
4.22
),

\displaystyle|\mu_{k}(y)|\leqslant C\,(1+|u(y)|)\,|K(x_{k},y)|\leqslant C\,(1+\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{3})})\,\sup_{x\in B_{3}\setminus B_{\rho/2}(y)}|K(x,y)|

and the latter function belongs to
L^{1}(B_{3}\setminus\overline{B_{\rho}(x_{0})})

owing to (
4.19
).

This, (
4.18
), (
4.22
) and the Dominated Convergence Theorem
lead to

(4.31)

\begin{split}&\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B_{3}\setminus{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}}\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y)\,dy\,\\
&\qquad=\,\int_{B_{3}\setminus{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}}\lim_{k\to+\infty}\big(\varphi(x_{k})-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}|x_{k}-x_{0}|^{2}-u_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y)\,dy\\
&\qquad=\,\int_{B_{3}\setminus{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}}\big(\varphi(x_{0})-u(y)\big)K(x_{0},y)\,dy.\end{split}

In light of (
4.3
) and (
4.23
) we also remark that, for large
k
,

(4.32)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3}}|u_{k}(y)|\sup_{{x\in B_{1}}}K(x,y)\,dy\\
&\qquad\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3}}\big|u(y)-u_{k}(y)\big|\sup_{x\in B_{1}}K(x,y)\,dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3}}|u(y)|\sup_{{x\in B_{1}}}K(x,y)\,dy\\
&\qquad\leqslant 1+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3}}|u(y)|\sup_{{x\in B_{1}}}K(x,y)\,dy\leqslant C,\end{split}

up to renaming
C
once again.
Furthermore, if
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3}
and
\delta_{k}:=\big|\varphi(x_{k})-\varphi(x_{0})\big|+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}
,
we have that
\delta_{k}
is infinitesimal as
k\to+\infty
and

\displaystyle\Big|\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y)-\big(\varphi(x_{0})-u(y)\big)K(x_{0},y)\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\Big(\big|\varphi(x_{k})-\varphi(x_{0})\big|+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}+|u_{k}(y)-u(y)|\Big)|K(x_{k},y)|+\big|\varphi(x_{0})-u(y)\big|\,\big|K(x_{0},y)-K(x_{k},y)\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\delta_{k}\sup_{x\in B_{1}}|K(x,y)|+|u_{k}(y)-u(y)|\sup_{x\in B_{1}}|K(x,y)|+C\,(1+|u(y)|)\,\big|K(x_{0},y)-K(x_{k},y)\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\delta_{k}\sup_{x\in B_{1}\setminus B_{1}(y)}|K(x,y)|+|u_{k}(y)-u(y)|\sup_{x\in B_{1}}|K(x,y)|+C\,(1+|u(y)|)\,\big|K(x_{0},y)-K(x_{k},y)\big|.

Gathering this information, (
4.19
) and (
4.23
), we find that

(4.33)

\begin{split}&\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3}}\Big|\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y)-\big(\varphi(x_{0})-u(y)\big)K(x_{0},y)\Big|\,dy\\
&\qquad\leqslant\lim_{k\to+\infty}C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3}}(1+|u(y)|)\,\big|K(x_{0},y)-K(x_{k},y)\big|\,dy.\end{split}

We also point out that, if
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3}
,

(1+|u(y)|)\,\big|K(x_{0},y)-K(x_{k},y)\big|\leqslant 2(1+|u(y)|)\,\sup_{x\in B_{1}\setminus B_{1}(y)}|K(x,y)|

and the latter function belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3})
, thanks to (
4.3
)
and (
4.19
). The Dominated Convergence Theorem and (
4.18
)
thereby give that

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3}}(1+|u(y)|)\,\big|K(x_{0},y)-K(x_{k},y)\big|\,dy=0.

This and (
4.33
) yield that

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3}}\Big|\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{k})-\varphi_{k}(y)\big)K(x_{k},y)-\big(\varphi(x_{0})-u(y)\big)K(x_{0},y)\Big|\,dy=0,

which, combined with (
4.31
), proves (
4.30
).

By combining (
4.25
) and (
4.30
), we deduce that
A\varphi_{k}(x_{k})\to A\varphi(x_{0})
as
k\to+\infty
.
As a result, by passing to the limit in (
4.24
), we conclude that
A\varphi(x_{0})\geqslant f(x_{0})
.

Similarly, one sees that if the function
\varphi-u
has a local minimum at
x_{0}
then
A\varphi(x_{0})\leqslant f(x_{0})
.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
\vartheta\in[0,2]
.
Let
K\in\mathcal{K}_{0,\vartheta}^{+}

satisfying (
4.18
), (
4.19
) and (
4.20
)
and
u\in\mathcal{V}_{K}
. Let
f
be bounded and continuous in
B_{1}
.

Then

Au=f{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}

in viscosity sense is equivalent to

Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{=}}f{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}

in the viscosity sense of Definition
4.2
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
Au=f
in
B_{1}

in viscosity sense. For every
R>5
, we define

f_{R}(x):=f(x)+\int_{B_{R}^{c}}u(y)K(x,y)\,dy.

Notice that

\sup_{x\in B_{1}}|f_{R}(x)-f(x)|\leqslant\int_{B_{R}^{c}}|u(y)|\sup_{x\in B_{1}}K(x,y)\,dy,

which is infinitesimal, thanks to (
4.3
) (used here with
m:=0
),
and thus
f_{R}
converges to
f
uniformly in
B_{1}
.

Our objective is to prove that
A(\chi_{R}u)=f_{R}
in
B_{1}
in the viscosity sense
(from which we obtain that
Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{=}}f
in
B_{1}

in the viscosity sense of Definition
4.2
).

To check this claim, we pick a point
x_{0}\in B_{1}
and touch
\chi_{R}u

from below by a test function
\varphi
at
x_{0}
, with
\varphi=\chi_{R}u

outside
B_{2}
.
We define
\psi:=\varphi+(1-\chi_{R})u
and we observe that
\psi

touches
u
by below at
x_{0}
and that
\psi=u
outside
B_{2}
.
As a result,
A\psi(x_{0})\geqslant f(x_{0})
and therefore

\displaystyle f_{R}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(x_{0})+\int_{B_{R}^{c}}u(y)K(x_{0},y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle A\psi(x_{0})+\int_{B_{R}^{c}}u(y)K(x_{0},y)\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\psi(x_{0})-\psi(y))K(x_{0},y)\,dy+\int_{B_{R}^{c}}u(y)K(x_{0},y)\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\varphi(x_{0})-\psi(y))K(x_{0},y)\,dy+\int_{B_{R}^{c}}u(y)K(x_{0},y)\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}(\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi(y))K(x_{0},y)\,dy+\int_{B_{R}^{c}}(\varphi(x_{0})-\psi(y))K(x_{0},y)\,dy+\int_{B_{R}^{c}}u(y)K(x_{0},y)\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A\varphi(x_{0})+\int_{B_{R}^{c}}(\varphi(y)-\psi(y))K(x_{0},y)\,dy+\int_{B_{R}^{c}}u(y)K(x_{0},y)\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A\varphi(x_{0}).

Similarly, if
\varphi
touches
\chi_{R}u

from above, then
A\varphi(x_{0})\leqslant f_{R}(x_{0})
.
These observations entail that
A(\chi_{R}u)=f_{R}
in
B_{1}
in the viscosity sense.

This proves one of the implications of Corollary
4.8
.
To prove the other, we assume now that
Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{=}}f
in
B_{1}

in the viscosity sense of Definition
4.2
.
Then, we find
f_{R}:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
A(\chi_{R}u)=f_{R}

in
B_{1}
in viscosity sense, with
f_{R}
converging
to
f
uniformly in
B_{1}
.
We remark that

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3}}\big|u(y)-(\chi_{R}u)(y)\big|\sup_{x\in B_{1}}|K(x,y)|\,dy=0,

thanks to (
4.3
) (used here with
m:=0
) and the Dominated Convergence Theorem.

We can therefore apply Lemma
4.7
and conclude that
Au=f

in
B_{1}
in the sense of viscosity, as desired.
∎

In the next result we state the viscosity counterpart
of Lemma
2.14
(its proof is omitted since

, just noticing that
the functions

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
j,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with
j\leqslant m
,
\vartheta\in[0,2]

and
K\in\mathcal{K}_{j,\vartheta}^{+}\cap\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}^{+}
.
Let

be bounded and continuous in

and let

such that

(4.34)

Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f

in
B_{1}
.

Then, there exist a function
\bar{f}
and a polynomial
P
of degree at most
m-1

such that
\bar{f}=f+P
and
Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j}}{{=}}\bar{f}
in
B_{1}
.

Next, as a possible application, we show a
specific case in which the existence of solution to a Dirichlet problem is guaranteed. For this, we consider a
family of kernels comparable to the fractional Laplace operator, as follows.
For any
s\in(0,1)
, given real numbers
\Lambda\geqslant\lambda>0
, we consider the family of
kernels
K
as defined in (
1.8
). We suppose that

(4.35)

there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that condition (
2.2
) is satisfied.

We also assume that condition (
2.4
) holds true
and that
K
is
translation invariant, i.e.

(4.36)

K(x+z,y+z)=K(x,y)
for any
x
,
y
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

With this, we have that
K
belongs to
\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}^{+}
, with
m
as in (
4.35
)
and for every
\vartheta\in(2s,2]
. Moreover, it also satisfies (
4.19
) and (
4.20
).

We introduce the fractional Sobolev space

\mathbb{H}^{s}(B_{1}):=\left\{u\in L^{2}(B_{1}):\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus(B_{1}^{c}\times B_{1}^{c})}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}K(x,y)\,dy<+\infty\right\}

and, given
g\in\mathbb{H}^{s}(B_{1})
, the class

J_{g}(B_{1}):=\Big\{u\in\mathbb{H}^{s}(B_{1}):\;u=g\;{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}\Big\}.

We use this class to seek solutions to the Dirichlet problem (see
[
20
]
).
More precisely, the following result can be proved by using the Direct Methods of the Calculus of Variations and
the strict convexity of the functional.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
K
be as in (
1.8
),
(
2.4
),
(
4.35
)
and (
4.36
).
Let
f\in L^{2}(B_{1})
and
g\in\mathbb{H}^{s}(B_{1})
.
Then, there exists a unique minimizer of the functional

(4.37)

\mathcal{E}(u):=\frac{1}{4}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}K(x,y)\,dx\,dy-\int_{B_{1}}f(x)u(x)\,dx

over
J_{g}(B_{1})
.

In addition,
u\in J_{g}(B_{1})
is a minimizer of (
4.37
) over
J_{g}(B_{1})
if and only if it is a weak solution of

(4.38)

\begin{cases}Au=f&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
u=g&\mbox{ in }B_{1}^{c},\end{cases}

that is, for every
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(B_{1})
,

\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(\phi(x)-\phi(y)\big)\,K(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\int_{B_{1}}f(x)\phi(x)\,dx.

The next result is a generalization of Theorem 2 in
[
22
]
, which shows the global continuity of
weak solutions of an equation which includes the operator of our interest.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
K
be as in (
1.8
)
(
2.4
),
(
4.35
)
and (
4.36
). Let
f\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
and
g\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

for some
\alpha\in(0,\min\{2s,1\})
. Assume that

|g(x)|\leqslant C|x|^{\alpha}\qquad{\mbox{ for all }}x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}.

Let also
u\in J_{g}(B_{1})
be a weak solution of

(4.39)

Au=f\quad{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}.

Then,
u\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

First of all, we exploit
Proposition
4.10
with
g:=0
to find a weak solution
v
of

\begin{cases}Av=f&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
v=0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

By Proposition 7.2 in
[
21
]

(see also
[
16
]
for related results), we have that
v\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Let now
w:=u-v
. We see that
w
is a weak solution of

\begin{cases}Aw=0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
w=u=g&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

We thus exploit Theorem 1.4 in
[
3
]
and find that
w\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
From these observations, we find that
u=v+w\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, as desired.
∎

With this, we can now prove that, in this setting, week solutions are also viscosity solutions.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
K
be as in (
1.8
)
(
2.4
), (
4.18
),
(
4.35
)
and (
4.36
). Let
f
be bounded and continuous in
B_{1}
and
g\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

for some
\alpha\in(0,\min\{2s,1\})
. Assume that

(4.40)

|g(x)|\leqslant C|x|^{\alpha}\qquad{\mbox{ for all }}x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}.

Let also
u\in J_{g}(B_{1})
be a weak solution of

(4.41)

\begin{cases}Au=f&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
u=g&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

Then,
u
is a viscosity solution of (
4.41
).

Proof.

By Proposition
4.11
, we know that
u\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Now, we take a point
x_{0}\in B_{1}

and a function
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{0}(B_{1},[0,1])
and we
consider an even mollifier
\rho_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon^{-n}\rho(x/\varepsilon)
, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
We set
u_{\varepsilon}:=u\ast\rho_{\varepsilon}
and
f_{\varepsilon}:=f\ast\rho_{\varepsilon}
(where we identified
f
with its null extension outside
B_{1}
).

We claim that

(4.42)

Au_{\varepsilon}=f_{\varepsilon}
in the weak sense in any ball
B_{r}(x_{0})
such that
B_{r}(x_{0})\Subset B_{1}
.

To prove this, we take a ball
B_{r}(x_{0})
such that
B_{r}(x_{0})\Subset B_{1}
and
a function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(B_{\rho}(x_{0}))
. We observe that

(4.43)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\;\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(u(x+z)-u(y+z))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)K(x,y)\,dx\,dy\right)\,dz\\
=\,&\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}\left(\quad\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus(B_{r}^{c}(x_{0})\times B^{c}_{r}(x_{0}))}(u(x+z)-u(y+z))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)K(x,y)\,dx\,dy\right)\,dz\\
\leqslant\,&\frac{\varepsilon^{-n}}{2}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}\left(\quad\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus(B_{1}^{c}\times B^{c}_{1})}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}K(x,y)\,dx\,dy+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus(B_{1}^{c}\times B^{c}_{1})}(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))^{2}K(x,y)\,dx\,dy\right)\,dz\\
<\,&+\infty,\end{split}

thanks to (
4.36
).

Therefore, Tonelli’s Theorem gives us that the function

(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto(u(x+z)-u(y+z))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)K(x,y)\quad{\mbox{lies in }}L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}).

One can interchange the order of integration in (
4.43
), thanks to Fubini’s Theorem, and exploit the definition of
u_{\varepsilon}
to obtain

(4.44)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}&\left(\;\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(u(x+z)-u(y+z))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)K(x,y)\,dx\,dy\right)\,dz\\
&=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\left(\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u(x+z)-u(y+z))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)K(x,y)\,dz\right)\,dx\,dy\\
&=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))K(x,y)\,dx\,dy.\end{split}

Then, we can use Fubini’s Theorem once again to get

(4.45)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}&f_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x+z)\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)\varphi(x)\,dx\right)\,dz\\
&=\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}\left(\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\tilde{x})\varphi(\tilde{x}-z)\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)d\tilde{x}\right)\,dz\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}\left(\;\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(u(\tilde{x})-u(\tilde{y}))(\varphi(\tilde{x}-z)-\varphi(\tilde{y}-z))\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)K(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})d\tilde{x}d\tilde{y}\right)\,dz\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\;\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(u(x+z)-u(y+z))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)K(x,y)\,dx\,dy\right)\,dz\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))K(x,y)\,dx\,dy,\end{split}

since the kernel
K

is translation invariant and
u
satisfies (
4.41
) in weak sense. This shows (
4.42
).

Now, given
B_{r}(x_{0})\Subset B_{1}
, we show that

(4.46)

{\mbox{the map
}}B_{r}(x_{0})\ni x\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(y))\,K(x,y)\,dy{\mbox{
is continuous.}}

For this, we let
x_{k}
be a sequence converging to a given point
x\in B_{r}(x_{0})
and we define

\zeta_{k}(z):=(2u_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})-u_{\varepsilon}(x_{k}+z)-u_{\varepsilon}(x_{k}-z))\,K(0,z).

Since
u_{\varepsilon}
is smooth and its growth at infinity is controlled via (
4.40
), we know that

|2u_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})-u_{\varepsilon}(x_{k}+z)-u_{\varepsilon}(x_{k}-z)|\leqslant C_{\varepsilon}\min\{|z|^{2},|z|^{\alpha}\},

for some
C_{\varepsilon}>0
. For this reason and (
1.8
),

|\zeta_{k}(z)|\leqslant\frac{C_{\varepsilon}\min\{|z|^{2},|z|^{\alpha}\}}{|z|^{n+2s}},

up to renaming
C_{\varepsilon}
and therefore we are in the position of applying the Dominated Convergence Theorem and
conclude that

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(2u_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})-u_{\varepsilon}(x_{k}+z)-u_{\varepsilon}(x_{k}-z))\,K(0,z)\,dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(2u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(x+z)-u_{\varepsilon}(x-z))\,K(0,z)\,dz.

In view of (
2.4
) and (
4.36
),
this proves (
4.46
).

We also observe that

(4.47)

Au_{\varepsilon}=f_{\varepsilon}
pointwise in any ball
B_{r}(x_{0})
such that
B_{r}(x_{0})\Subset B_{1}
.

Indeed, by (
2.4
),
(
4.42
) and (
4.46
),
if
x\in B_{r}(x_{0})
and
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(B_{r}(x_{0}))
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(y))\varphi(x)K(x,y)\,dx\,dy.

Since
\varphi
is arbitrary, we arrive at

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(y))K(x,y)\,dy,

from which we obtain (
4.47
).

We also have that

(4.48)

Au_{\varepsilon}=f_{\varepsilon}
in the viscosity sense in any ball
B_{r}(x_{0})
such that
B_{r}(x_{0})\Subset B_{1}
.

For this, we take a smooth function
\psi
touching, say from below, the function
u_{\varepsilon}
at some point
p\in B_{r}(x_{0})
. Since the kernel
K
is positive (thanks to (
1.8
)) and recalling (
4.47
), we have that

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(p)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u_{\varepsilon}(p)-u_{\varepsilon}(y))K(p,y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\psi(p)-u_{\varepsilon}(y))K(p,y)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\psi(p)-\psi(y))K(p,y)\,dy=A\psi(p).

This and a similar computation when
\psi
touches from above give (
4.48
).

We also remark that
u_{\varepsilon}
and
f_{\varepsilon}
converge uniformly to
u
and
f
, respectively,
in any ball
B_{r}(x_{0})\Subset B_{1}
, due to Theorem 9.8 in
[
24
]
.
In addition, by (
4.40
), we see that, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3r}(x_{0})
,

|u_{\varepsilon}(y)|\leqslant\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}|u(y-z)|\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)\,dz\leqslant C\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}|y-z|^{\alpha}\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)\,dz\leqslant C|y|^{\alpha},

up to renaming

. As a consequence of this and (
1.8
), we have that, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3r}(x_{0})
,

|u(y)-u_{\varepsilon}(y)|\,\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x_{0})}K(x,y)\leqslant C|y|^{\alpha}\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x_{0})}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\leqslant\frac{C}{|y|^{n+2s-\alpha}},

up to relabeling
C>0
. Since
\alpha<\min\{2s,1\}
, this function is in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3r}(x_{0}))
, and therefore
we exploit the Dominated Convergence Theorem to obtain that

\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{3r}(x_{0})}|u(y)-u_{\varepsilon}(y)|\,\sup_{x\in B_{1}}K(x,y)\,dy=0.

Consequently, condition (
4.23
) is satisfied, and
therefore we can apply Lemma
4.7
, thus obtaining that
Au=f

in the viscosity sense, as desired.
∎

With this preliminary work, we can now address the existence of solutions for
a Dirichlet problem in a generalized setting.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
K
be as in (
1.8
)
(
2.4
), (
4.18
),
(
4.35
)
and (
4.36
). Let
f
be bounded and continuous in
B_{1}
and
g\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

for some
\alpha\in(0,\min\{2s,1\})
. Assume that

|g(x)|\leqslant C|x|^{\alpha}\qquad{\mbox{ for all }}x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}.

Then, there exists a
function
u\in\mathcal{V}_{K}
such that

(4.49)

\begin{cases}Au\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
u=g&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

Also, the solution to (
4.49
) is not unique, since
the space of solutions of (
4.49
) has dimension
N_{m}
, with

N_{m}:=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\binom{j+n-1}{n-1}.

Proof.

Firstly, we prove the existence of solutions for (
4.49
). For this goal, we set

u_{1}:=\chi_{B_{4}^{c}}\,g\qquad{\mbox{and }}\qquad\tilde{g}:=\chi_{B_{4}\setminus B_{1}}\,g.

Since
u_{1}
is identically zero in
B_{4}
and

K\in\mathcal{K}_{m,\vartheta}^{+}
, we can write
Au_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f_{u_{1}}
in
B_{1}
in both
pointwise and viscosity sense,
for some function
f_{u_{1}}
, due to Remark
2.6
and Lemma
4.6
.

We now define
\tilde{f}:=f-f_{u_{1}}
and consider
the Dirichlet problem given by

(4.50)

\begin{cases}A\tilde{u}=\tilde{f}&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
\tilde{u}=\tilde{g}&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

By Proposition
4.10
, we find that (
4.50
) has a unique weak solution
\tilde{u}
.
Moreover, thanks to Proposition
4.12
, we get that
\tilde{u}
is a viscosity solution of (
4.50
).

Furthermore, by Remark
4.4
and Corollary
4.8

we obtain that

\begin{cases}A\tilde{u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}\tilde{f}&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
\tilde{u}=\tilde{g}&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

Now, we set
u:=u_{1}+\tilde{u}
and we get that
Au=Au_{1}+A\tilde{u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}f_{u_{1}}+\tilde{f}=f
in
B_{1}
.
Moreover, we have that
u=u_{1}+\tilde{g}=g
in
B_{1}^{c}
. These observations give that is
u
is solution of (
4.49
).
This proves the existence of solution for (
4.49
).

Now, we focus on the second part of the proof. Namely we establish that solutions of (
4.49
)
are not unique and we determine the dimension
of the corresponding linear space. For this, we notice that, exploiting Propositions
4.10

and
4.12
, one can find a unique solution
\tilde{u}_{P}
of the problem

(4.51)

\begin{cases}A\tilde{u}_{P}=P&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
\tilde{u}_{P}=0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

Furthermore,
A\tilde{u}_{P}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{=}}P
in
B_{1}
, due to Corollary
4.8
.
Using Remark
4.4
, we obtain that
A\tilde{u}_{P}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}P
in
B_{1}
.
Moreover, from Remark
4.5
, we obtain that
\tilde{u}_{P}
is a solution of

(4.52)

\begin{cases}A\tilde{u}_{P}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
\tilde{u}_{P}=0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

This yields that if
u
is a solution of (
4.49
), then
u+\tilde{u}_{P}
is also a solution of (
4.49
).

Viceversa, if
u
and
v
are two solutions of (
4.49
), then
w:=u-v
is a solution of

\begin{cases}Aw\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{=}}0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
w=0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

Here we can apply Lemma
4.9
with
j:=0
thus obtaining that
Aw\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{=}}P
in
B_{1}
, where
P
is a polynomial of
\deg P\leqslant m-1
. We use Corollary
4.8
one more time to find that

(4.53)

\begin{cases}Aw=P&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1},\\
w=0&{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}^{c}.\end{cases}

Therefore, the uniqueness of the solution of (
4.53
),
confronted with (
4.51
), gives us that
w=\tilde{u}_{P}
, and thus
v=u+\tilde{u}_{P}
.

This reasoning gives that the space of solutions of (
4.49
) is isomorphic to the space of polynomials with degree
less than or equal to
m-1
, which has dimension
N_{m}
, given by (
3.3
).
∎
