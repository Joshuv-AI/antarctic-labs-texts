Permutation and Multi-permutation Codes Correcting Multiple Deletions Thanks: This paper was presented in part at the 2024 IEEE International Symposium on Information Theory (ISIT) in 2024 [ 1 ] .
Shuche Wang 1, The Nguyen 2, Yeow Meng Chee 3, Van Khu Vu 3

Abstract
Permutation codes in the Ulam metric, which can correct multiple deletions, have been investigated extensively recently. In this work, we are interested in the maximum size of permutation codes in the Ulam metric and aim to design permutation codes that can correct multiple deletions with efficient decoding algorithms. We first present an improvement on the Gilbert–Varshamov bound of the maximum size of these permutation codes by analyzing the independence number of the auxiliary graph. The idea is widely used in various cases and our contribution in this section is enumerating the number of triangles in the auxiliary graph and showing that it is small enough. Next, we design permutation codes correcting multiple deletions with a decoding algorithm. In particular, the constructed permutation codes can correct t t deletions with at most ( 3 ​ t − 1 ) ​ log ⁡ n + o ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) (3t-1)\log n+o(\log n) bits of redundancy where n n is the length of the code. Our construction is based on a new mapping which yields a new connection between permutation codes in the Hamming metric and permutation codes in various metrics. Furthermore, we construct permutation codes that correct multiple bursts of deletions using this new mapping. Finally, we extend the new mapping for multi-permutations and construct the best-known multi-permutation codes in Ulam metric.

Notation and Preliminaries

Given an integer
n
, let
[n]
denote the set
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. A permutation is a bijection
\bm{\sigma}:[n]\mapsto[n]
and denoted
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\ldots,\sigma(n))
.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
be the set of all permutations on
[n]
, that is the symmetric group of order

. Denote
\bm{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dotsc,\pi_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
as a permutation with length
n
. Given a permutation
\bm{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\ldots,\pi_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, the inverse permutation is denoted as
\bm{\pi}^{-1}=(\pi^{-1}_{1},\pi^{-1}_{2},\ldots,\pi^{-1}_{n})
. Here,
\pi^{-1}_{i}
indicates the position of the element
i
in the permutation
\bm{\pi}
. For an integer
x\in[n]
,
\pi^{-1}(x)
indicates the position of
x
in permutation
\bm{\pi}
. Also,
\bm{\pi}_{[i,j]}
denotes the subsequence beginning at index
i
and ending at index
j
, inclusive. For functions, if the output is a sequence, we also write them with bold letters, such as
{\bm{p}}(\bm{\pi})
. The
i
th position in
{\bm{p}}(\bm{\pi})
is denoted
p(\bm{\pi})_{i}
.

Example 1
.

Suppose
\bm{\pi}=(1,3,4,2,5)
, we have
\bm{\pi}^{-1}=(1,4,2,3,5)
and
\pi^{-1}(2)=4
.

Basic Definitions

Definition 1
.

For distinct
i,j\in[n]
, a transposition
\tau(i,j)
leads to a new permutation obtained by swapping
\pi_{i}
and
\pi_{j}
in
\bm{\pi}

\bm{\pi}\tau(i,j)=(\pi_{1},\dotsc,\pi_{i-1},\pi_{j},\pi_{i+1},\dotsc,\pi_{j-1},\pi_{i},\pi_{j+1},\dotsc,\pi_{n}).

If
|i-j|=1
,
\tau(i,j)
is called the
adjacent transposition
.

Definition 2
.

For distinct
i,j\in[n]
, if
i<j
, a translocation
\phi(i,j)
leads to a new permutation obtained by moving
\pi_{i}
to the position of
j
and shifting symbols
\pi_{[i+1,j]}
by one in
\bm{\pi}
. There is

\bm{\pi}\phi(i,j)=(\pi_{1},\dotsc,\pi_{i-1},\pi_{i+1},\dotsc,\pi_{j},\pi_{i},\pi_{j+1},\dotsc,\pi_{n}).

If
i>j
, a translocation
\phi(i,j)
leads to a new permutation obtained by moving
\pi_{i}
to the position of
j
and shifting symbols
\pi_{[j,i-1]}
by one in
\bm{\pi}
. There is

\bm{\pi}\phi(i,j)=(\pi_{1},\dotsc,\pi_{j-1},\pi_{i},\pi_{j},\dotsc,,\pi_{i-1},\pi_{i+1},\dotsc,\pi_{n}).

In
[
6
]
, Chee and Vu introduced the
generalized transposition
as follows. Denote
[a,b]\prec[c,d]
as the interval
[a,b]
precedes the interval
[c,d]
.

Definition 3
.

For distinct two intervals
A=[i,j],B=[k,\ell]
, a generalized transposition
\tau_{g}(A,B)
leads to a new permutation obtained by swapping
\bm{\pi}_{[i,j]}
and
\bm{\pi}_{[k,\ell]}
in
\bm{\pi}
. If
A\prec B
, there is

\bm{\pi}\tau_{g}(A,B)=(\pi_{1},\dotsc,\pi_{i-1},\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1},\dotsc,\pi_{\ell},\pi_{j+1},\dotsc,\\
\pi_{k-1},\pi_{i},\dotsc,\pi_{j},\pi_{\ell+1},\dotsc,\pi_{n}).

If
k-j=1
, we denote
\tau_{a}(A,B)
as the generalized adjacent transposition.

We have
\phi(i,\ell)=\tau_{a}(A,B)
with
A=[i,j],B=[k,\ell]
if

. Hence, we say that a

can be considered as a special case of a

. We note that computing the exact generalized adjacent transposition distance between two permutations is an NP-hard problem.

Example 2
.

Let
\bm{\pi}=(1,6,4,3,2,5)
. We have the following:

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}\tau(2,5)=(1,2,4,3,6,5),

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}\phi(2,5)=(1,4,3,2,6,5),

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}\tau_{g}([2,3],[5,6])=(1,2,5,3,6,4),

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}\tau_{a}([2,3],[4,6])=(1,3,2,5,6,4).

Definition 4
.

The Hamming distance between two permutations
\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, denoted by
d_{H}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})
, is defined as the number of positions for which
\bm{\pi}
and
\bm{\sigma}
differ, i.e,

d_{H}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})=|\{i\in[n]:\pi_{i}\neq\sigma_{i}\}|.

Definition 5
.

The Levenshtein distance between two permutations
\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, denoted by
d_{L}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})
, is defined as the minimum number of insertions or deletions which are needed to change
\bm{\pi}
to
\bm{\sigma}
.

Definition 6
.

The Kendall-
\tau
distance between two permutations
\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, denoted by
d_{K}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})
, is defined as the minimum number of adjacent transpositions which are needed to change
\bm{\pi}
to
\bm{\sigma}
, i.e,

d_{K}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})=\min\{m:\bm{\pi}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\dotsm\tau_{m}=\bm{\sigma}\},

where
\tau_{1},\dotsc,\tau_{m}
are adjacent transpositions.

Definition 7
.

The generalized Kendall-
\tau
distance between two permutations
\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, denoted by
d_{\bar{K}}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})
, is defined as the minimum number of generalized adjacent transpositions which are needed to change
\bm{\pi}
to
\bm{\sigma}
, i.e,

d_{\bar{K}}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})=\min\{m:\bm{\pi}\tau_{a1}\tau_{a2}\dotsm\tau_{am}=\bm{\sigma}\},

where
\tau_{a1},\dotsc,\tau_{am}
are generalized adjacent transpositions.

Definition 8
.

The generalized Cayley distance between two permutations
\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, denoted by
d_{C}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})
, is defined as the minimum number of generalized transpositions which are needed to change
\bm{\pi}
to
\bm{\sigma}
, i.e,

d_{C}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})=\min\{m:\bm{\pi}\tau_{g1}\tau_{g2}\dotsm\tau_{gm}=\bm{\sigma}\},

where
\tau_{g1},\dotsc,\tau_{gm}
are generalized transpositions.

Definition 9
.

The Ulam distance between two permutations
\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, denoted by
d_{U}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})
, is defined as the minimum number of translocations which are needed to change
\bm{\pi}
to
\bm{\sigma}
, i.e,

d_{U}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})=\min\{m:\bm{\pi}\phi_{1}\phi_{2}\dotsm\phi_{m}=\bm{\sigma}\},

where
\phi_{1},\dotsc,\phi_{m}
are translocations.

Proposition 1
.

For two permutations
\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, let
\mathrm{LCS}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})
be the length of a longest common subsequence of
\bm{\pi}
and
\bm{\sigma}
. The Ulam distance
d_{U}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})
between
\bm{\pi}
and
\bm{\sigma}
equals
n-\mathrm{LCS}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})
.

Example 3
.

Let
\bm{\pi}=(4,3,1,2,5)
and
\bm{\sigma}=(4,3,5,1,2)
. Then
d_{H}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})=3
and
d_{U}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})=1
.

II-B

Permutation Code

Given a permutation
\bm{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, let
\mathcal{D}_{t}(\bm{\pi})
be the set of all vectors of length
n-t
received as a result of
t
deletions in
\bm{\pi}
and let
\mathcal{B}_{t}(\bm{\pi})\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{n}
denote the
confusable set
of
\bm{\pi}
, i.e., the set of permutations
\bm{\sigma}
other than
\bm{\pi}
for which
\mathcal{D}_{t}(\bm{\pi})\cap\mathcal{D}_{t}(\bm{\sigma})\neq\emptyset
. Constructing the permuation codes for correcting
t
deletions is to design codes
\mathcal{P}_{t}(n)
over
\mathcal{S}_{n}
where for any
\bm{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}_{t}(n)
, we can recover
\bm{\pi}
from
\bm{\pi}^{\prime}
, provided that
\bm{\pi}^{\prime}
is the result of
t
deletions occurring in
\bm{\pi}
. The code
\mathcal{P}_{t}(n)
is a
t
-deletion permutation code if and only if for distinct
\bm{\pi}_{1},\bm{\pi}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{t}(n)
such that
\mathcal{D}_{t}(\bm{\pi}_{1})\cap\mathcal{D}_{t}(\bm{\pi}_{2})=\emptyset
. The size of a permutation code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{n}
is denoted
|\mathcal{C}|
and its redundancy is defined as
\log(n!/|\mathcal{C}|)
. All logarithms in this paper are to the base 2.

In
[
36
]
, Levenshtein demonstrated that for sequences
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
with a length of
n
, the Levenshtein distance between
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
satisfies
d_{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=2(n-\mathrm{LCS}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
. This formula applies to permutations as well. Combining with the Proposition
1
, we have:

d_{L}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})=2d_{U}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}).

(1)

Therefore, a
t
-deletion permutation code
of length
n
is a subset
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
such that the Ulam distance of any pair of distinct elements in
\mathcal{C}
is at least
t+1
. We would like to determine the maximum size
M(n,t)
of a
t
-deletion permutation code.

Lemma 1
.

Let
n>t
be positive integers.
M(n,t)
is bounded as follows

\Omega_{t}\left(\frac{n!}{n^{2t}}\right)\leq M(n,t)\leq O_{t}\left(\frac{n!}{n^{t}}\right).

(2)

Proof:

The lower bound is from the Gilbert–Varshamov (GV) bound, where the size of the confusable set of
\bm{\pi}
is bounded by
|\mathcal{B}_{t}(\bm{\pi})|\leq{n\choose t}\cdot t!{n\choose t}
for any
\bm{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Hence, we have

M(n,t)\geq\frac{n!}{{n\choose t}\cdot t!{n\choose t}}.

For the upper bound, let
\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathcal{S}_{n})=\cup_{\bm{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\mathcal{D}(\bm{\pi})
. We have
|\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathcal{S}_{n})|=n!/t!
since
\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
is the set of all sequences consisting of
n-t
distinct symbols from
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Applying the sphere-packing method, we have

\displaystyle\frac{n!}{t!}\geq M(n,t)\cdot{n\choose t}.

This completes the proof.∎

Corollary 1
.

The lower bound of the minimal redundancy of the permutation codes for correcting
t
deletions is
t\log n
.

Lemma 2

(Section IV-C,
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{n}
be a permutation code for correcting

deletions with length

is

|\mathcal{C}_{n}|\geq\frac{(\frac{2n}{t}!)^{\frac{t}{2}}}{(n+1)^{\frac{3t}{2}-4}(\frac{2n}{t})^{\frac{t}{2}}}.

The redundancy of the permutation code
\mathcal{C}_{n}
for correcting

III
Improved GV Bound

In this section, we achieve a logarithmic improvement on the GV bound of
M(n,t)
. The improved GV bound is presented as the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

For
n\geq t\geq 1
be fixed positive integers, then

M(n,t)\geq\Omega_{t}\left(\frac{n!\log n}{n^{2t}}\right).

The proof idea of Theorem
1
relies on techniques from graph theory by constructing an auxiliary graph on the set of permutations such that each
t
-deletion permutation code is an independent set of this graph. Therefore, studying the size of codes is equivalent to analyzing the independence number of the auxiliary graph. It is well-known that if a graph has few triangles, we can improve the independence number by a log factor, compared with the classical bound by the greedy algorithm. Mention that this idea was initially used by Jiang and Vardy
[
28
]
to improve GV bound for binary codes with Hamming distance, followed by subsequent papers in other settings, see
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
for examples. However, counting triangles is more challenging in our graph than in the highly symmetric setting studied by Jiang and Vardy
[
28
]
, where the corresponding graph is simply a power of the Hamming cube. In our case, the graph
\mathcal{G}_{n,t}
, defined below, is not even regular, as some sequences of transportations can yield the same permutation from a given permutation. These challenges also arise in the recent work of Alon et al.
[
30
]
, where they study
q
-ary codes with the deletion metric. They restricted attention to “pseudorandom” words
in the graph that are related by “pseudorandom” sequences of insertion and deletion operations, for suitable
notions of pseudorandomness. However, their approach does not carry over to our setting with permutations, since each insertion step depends on the previous deletion. Otherwise, there would be
n
possible options for this operation, whereas in their case, the number of options is limited to
q
, which can be treated as a constant. To overcome these difficulties, we focus on the symbols used in each transportation instead of their positions, as the choice of symbols depends on the specific permutation.

Particularly, we demonstrate the proof of Theorem
1
through a two-step process: First, we simplify the issue by focusing on the enumeration of triangles within the
t
-deletion graph, denoted as
\mathcal{G}_{n,t}
. Following that, we approximate the count of these triangles.
To achieve it, we shall use the following standard lemma of Bollobás, which states that graphs with few triangles have large independence numbers. This lemma is a generalization of a result of Ajtai, Komlós, and Szemerédi
[
37
]
on triangle-free graphs.

Lemma 3

(Lemma 15
[
38
]
, p.296)
.

Let
G
be a graph with maximum degree
\Delta
(
\Delta\geq 1
) and suppose that
G
has
T
triangles. Then

\alpha(G)\geq\frac{|V(G)|}{10\Delta}\left(\log\Delta-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{T}{|V(G)|}\right)\right).

(3)

where
\alpha(G)
is the independent number of
G
and
|V(G)|
is the number of vertices of
G
.

We define the graph
\mathcal{G}_{n,t}
with vertex set
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, and two permutations are connected by an edge if they have a common subsequence of length at least
n-t
. Therefore, a
t
-deletion permutation code is an independent set of
\mathcal{G}_{n,t}
. Note that
|V(\mathcal{G}_{n,t})|=n!
and
\Delta=O_{t}(n^{2t})
, since from any given permutation
\bm{\sigma}
, a neighbor
\bm{\pi}
can be obtained by choosing
t
positions of
\bm{\sigma}
to delete in at most
\binom{n}{t}
ways and then
t
positions with letters to insert in at most
t!\binom{n}{t}
. Thus, in order to prove Theorem
1
, by using Lemma
3
, it suffices to show that the number of triangles in
\mathcal{G}_{n,t}
is
O_{t}(n!n^{4t-\varepsilon})
for some
\varepsilon>0
. We will prove this holds for
\varepsilon=t
. More precisely, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

The number of triples
(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\pi},\bm{\tau})\in(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{3}
such that
d_{U}(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\pi})\leq t,d_{U}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\tau})\leq t
, and
d_{U}(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau})\leq t
is at most
O_{t}(n!n^{3t})
.

For
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we will count the number of permutations
\bm{\pi}
and
\bm{\tau}
such that
(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\pi},\bm{\tau})
forms a triangle in
\mathcal{G}_{n,t}
. Note that
(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\pi},\bm{\tau})
is uniquely determined by
\bm{\sigma}
and sequences
S_{1}=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\dotsc,\phi_{h}),S_{2}=(\phi^{\prime}_{1},\phi^{\prime}_{2},\dotsc\phi^{\prime}_{\ell})
of translocations for which
\bm{\pi}=\bm{\sigma}\phi_{1}\phi_{2}\dotsm\phi_{h}
and
\bm{\tau}=\bm{\pi}\phi^{\prime}_{1}\phi^{\prime}_{2}\dotsm\phi^{\prime}_{\ell}
. For the sake of brevity, we denote
\bm{\pi}\circ S=\bm{\pi}\phi_{1}\phi_{2}\dotsm\phi_{m}
, where the translocation sequence is
S=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\dotsc,\phi_{m})
.

Since
d_{U}(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\pi})\leq t
and
d_{U}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\tau})\leq t
, we can choose
S_{1},S_{2}

IV
Relationship of distances between two permutations

In this section, we first build a tight inequality relationship between the Ulam distance and Hamming distance via a novel mapping function. Then we show that it is possible to construct permutation codes in various metrics using the known permutation code in the Hamming metric.
We note that permutation codes in the Hamming metric have been well-studied while there is a lack of knowledge in permutation codes in other metrics, such as the Ulam metric, generalized Kendall-
\tau
metric, and generalized Cayley metric. We also show that our newly constructed permutation codes have better size than previously known permutation codes.

Farnoud, Skacheck, and Milenkovic
[
8
]
derived inequalities to present the relationship between the Ulam distance and Hamming distance over two permutations
\bm{\pi}
and
\bm{\sigma}
. More precisely, they showed that

\frac{1}{n}d_{H}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})\leq d_{U}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})\leq d_{H}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}),\text{ for all }\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}.

(5)

In this paper, we propose a novel mapping function such that we can obtain a tighter inequality relationship between the Ulam distance and Hamming distance over two permutations.

Given a permutation
\bm{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we append
n+1
at the end of
\bm{\pi}
to obtain
\bar{\bm{\pi}}=(\bm{\pi},n+1)\in\mathcal{S}_{n+1}
. Let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}
be the subset of
\mathcal{S}_{n+1}
that consists of all permutations with the last element fixed as
n+1
, i.e.

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}=\left\{\bar{\bm{\pi}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n+1}\mid\bar{\bm{\pi}}_{n+1}=n+1\right\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}
is group isomorphic to
\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}|=n!
.

Definition 10
.

For
n\geq 1
, we define the function
{\bm{f}}:\mathcal{S}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{n+1}
, such that
{\bm{f}}(\bm{\pi})=(f(\bm{\pi})_{1},\dots,f(\bm{\pi})_{n+1})
where

f(\bm{\pi})_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\bar{\pi}_{\bar{\pi}^{-1}_{i}+1},&i=1,2,\dotsc,n\\
\bar{\pi}_{1},&i=n+1\end{array}\right.

(6)

for all
\bm{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
in which
\bar{\bm{\pi}}=(\bm{\pi},n+1)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}
. For convenience, we will write
{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\pi}})
instead of
{\bm{f}}(\bm{\pi})
and consider
{\bm{f}}
as a function from
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}
to
\mathcal{S}_{n+1}
.

Example 4
.

Suppose
\bm{\pi}=(1,3,4,2,5)\in\mathcal{S}_{5}
, we have
\bar{\bm{\pi}}=(1,3,4,2,5,6)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{6}
and
\bar{\bm{\pi}}^{-1}=(1,4,2,3,5,6)
. Hence,
{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\pi}})=(3,5,4,2,6,1)
.

One way we can think about
{\bm{f}}
is the following. Starting with
\bm{\pi}
, we get
\bm{\pi}^{-1}=(\pi^{-1}_{1},\dots,\pi^{-1}_{n})
, increase all positions of
\bm{\pi}^{-1}
by one, and append
1
at the end to obtain a permutation
\bm{\alpha}_{\pi}
in
\mathcal{S}_{n+1}
. Then
{\bm{f}}(\bm{\pi})
is obtained by taking the composition of
\bar{\bm{\pi}}
and
\bm{\alpha}
, which is denoted as
\bar{\bm{\pi}}\circ\bm{\alpha}_{\pi}
. The process can be formally described as follows

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}\to\bm{\pi}^{-1}=(\pi_{1}^{-1},\dots,\pi_{n}^{-1})\to\bm{\alpha}_{\pi}:=(1+\pi_{1}^{-1},\dots,1+\pi_{n}^{-1},1)\to{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\pi}})=\bar{\bm{\pi}}\circ\bm{\alpha}_{\pi}.

Another more descriptive way to view
{\bm{f}}
is that for a given permutation
\bar{\bm{\pi}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}
,
{\bm{f}}
maps elements
i
to the elements that follow it in the sequence form of
\bar{\bm{\pi}}
, here the following element of
\bar{\pi}_{n+1}
is
\bar{\pi}_{1}
. For example, suppose
\bar{\bm{\pi}}=(2,1,3,4,5)
then
{\bm{f}}(1)=3
as
1
is followed by
3
,
{\bm{f}}(3)=4
as
3
is followed by
4
in
\bar{\bm{\pi}}
, and
{\bm{f}}(5)=2
for similar reason.
From this viewpoint, it is easy to see that
{\bm{f}}
is injective. For the sake of completeness, we include a formal proof below.

Claim 1
.

The function
{\bm{f}}
is injective.

Proof:

Suppose there are two permutations
\bar{\bm{\pi}}
and
\bar{\bm{\sigma}}
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}
such that
{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\pi}})={\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\sigma}})
, i.e.

f(\bar{\bm{\pi}})_{i}=f(\bar{\bm{\sigma}})_{i}
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n+1.
By the definition of
{\bm{f}}
, we have
\bar{\pi}_{1}=f(\bar{\bm{\pi}})_{n+1}=f(\bar{\bm{\sigma}})_{n+1}=\bar{\sigma}_{1}.
Now, if
\bar{\pi}_{k}=\bar{\sigma}_{k}
for some
1\leq k<n+1
, we have

\bar{\pi}_{k+1}=\bar{\pi}_{\bar{\pi}^{-1}_{i}+1}=f(\bar{\bm{\pi}})_{i}=f(\bar{\bm{\sigma}})_{i}=\bar{\sigma}_{\bar{\sigma}^{-1}_{i}+1}=\bar{\sigma}_{k+1}

where
i=\bar{\pi}_{k}=\bar{\sigma}_{k}
. Therefore, it follows by induction that
\bar{\pi}_{k}=\bar{\sigma}_{k}
for all
k=1,2,\dots,n+1
. In other words,
\bar{\bm{\pi}}=\bar{\bm{\sigma}}
.
This proves Claim
1
.
∎

Let
f(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1})
be the image of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}
under the function
{\bm{f}}
, namely

f(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}):=\{{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\pi}})\,|\,\bar{\bm{\pi}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}\}.

By Claim
1
, we have
|f(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1})|=|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}|=|\mathcal{S}_{n}|=n!
and the inverse function
{\bm{f}}^{-1}(\cdot)\colon f(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1})\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1}
is well-defined. Moreover, for any given
\bm{\sigma}\in f(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{n+1})
, the permutation
{\bm{f}}^{-1}(\bm{\sigma})=(f^{-1}(\bm{\sigma})_{1},\dots,f^{-1}(\bm{\sigma})_{n+1})
can be recursively computed from
\bm{\sigma}
. In particular, we have
f^{-1}(\bm{\sigma})_{1}=\sigma_{n+1}
and

f^{-1}(\bm{\sigma})_{i}=\sigma_{f^{-1}(\bm{\sigma})_{i-1}}\text{ for }i=2,3,\dots,n+1.

Our main result of this section is the following theorem, showing a relationship between the Ulam distance over
\bm{\pi}
and
\bm{\sigma}
and the Hamming distance over two permutations
{\bm{f}}(\bm{\pi})
and
{\bm{f}}(\bm{\sigma})
for any
\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

Theorem 2
.

Given two permutations
\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we have
d_{U}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})\geq\frac{1}{3}d_{H}({\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\pi}}),{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\sigma}}))
.

Proof:

Firstly, we consider the case when
d_{U}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})=1
, which means there is a translocation
\phi(i,j)
such that
\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\pi}\phi(i,j)
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
1\leq i<j\leq n
since we can consider
\bm{\pi}=\bm{\sigma}\phi(j,i)
if otherwise. Suppose
\bm{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n})
, we have

\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\pi}\phi(i,j)=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{i-1},\pi_{i+1},\dots,\pi_{j},\pi_{i},\pi_{j+1},\dots,\pi_{n}).

(7)

We recall that for a given permutation
\bar{\bm{\pi}}
,
f(\bar{\bm{\pi}})_{i}
is the element that follow
i
in
\bar{\bm{\pi}}
. Hence

f(\bar{\bm{\pi}})_{\bar{\pi}_{n+1}}=\bar{\pi}_{1}\text{ and }f(\bar{\bm{\pi}})_{\bar{\pi}_{i}}=\bar{\pi}_{i+1}\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,n.

Similarly, it follows from (
10
) that
f(\bar{\bm{\sigma}})_{\bar{\pi}_{n+1}}=\bar{\pi}_{1},f(\bar{\bm{\sigma}})_{\bar{\pi}_{k}}=\bar{\pi}_{k+1}
if
k\notin\{i-1,i,j\}
, and

\displaystyle f(\bar{\bm{\sigma}})_{\bar{\pi}_{i-1}}=\pi_{i+1},f(\bar{\bm{\sigma}})_{\bar{\pi}_{i}}=\pi_{j+1},f(\bar{\bm{\sigma}})_{\bar{\pi}_{j}}=\pi_{i}.

This shows that
{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\pi}})
and
{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\sigma}})
only differ at three positions
\bar{\pi}_{i-1},\bar{\pi}_{i}
, and
\bar{\pi}_{j}
. Hence
d_{H}({\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\pi}}),{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\sigma}}))=3
.

Now, we suppose that
d_{U}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma})=k
. By definition, there are
k
translocations
\phi_{i}
such that
\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\pi}\phi_{1}\phi_{2}\dots\phi_{k}
. Let
\bm{\sigma}^{i}=\bm{\pi}\phi_{1}\dots\phi_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,k
. We have
\bm{\sigma}^{i}=\bm{\sigma}^{i-1}\phi_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,k
where
\bm{\sigma}^{0}=\bm{\pi}
and
\bm{\sigma}^{k}=\bm{\sigma}
. By above argument, we have
d_{H}({\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\sigma}}^{i}),{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\sigma}}^{i-1}))=3
for
i=1,\dots,k
. It is followed by triangle inequalities that

d_{H}({\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\pi}}),{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\sigma}}))\leq d_{H}({\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\sigma}}^{0}),{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\sigma}}^{1}))+d_{H}({\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\sigma}}^{1}),{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\sigma}}^{2}))+\dots+d_{H}({\bm{f}}((\bar{\bm{\sigma}}^{k-1}),{\bm{f}}(\bar{\bm{\sigma}}^{k}))=3k=3d_{U}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\sigma}).

This completes the proof of Theorem
2
.
∎

In this section, we aim to design permutation codes for correcting
t

While the best-known permutation codes correcting

[

are based on the auxiliary codes in the Ulam metric, our construction is achieved by incorporating the base code in the Hamming metric. Let
\mathcal{C}_{H}(n,d_{H})
be a permutation code with minimum Hamming distance

. It is known that

can correct

substitutions and
t_{2}
erasures if

[

In this section, we will extend the permutation codes for correcting

\mathcal{M}^{{\bm{r}}}_{n}=\{\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{r_{1}},\underbrace{2,\ldots,2}_{r_{2}},\ldots,\underbrace{k,\ldots,k}_{r_{k}}\}.

A multiset where each element appears exactly

-regular multiset
. For brevity, we denote the
r
-regular multiset as
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{n}
. An
r
-regular multipermutation
is defined as any permutation of an
r
-regular multiset. Throughout this paper, we focus on
r
-regular multipermutations.

Let
n=r\cdot k
, where
r\geq 1
and

. Consider the set
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{r}
of

-regular multipermutations of length

r
times.
We extend

by appending

at the end, resulting in:

\bar{{\bm{x}}}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{n},\underbrace{k+1,k+1,\dotsc,k+1}_{\text{$r$ times}})\in\mathcal{M}_{n+r}^{r}.

Let
\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{n+r}^{r}
be the subset of

that consists of all multipermutations with the last

, i.e.

\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{n+r}^{r}=\left\{\bar{{\bm{x}}}\in\mathcal{M}_{n+r}^{r}\mid\bar{{\bm{x}}}_{[n+1:n+r]}=k+1\right\}.

Clearly,
\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{n+r}^{r}
is group isomorphic to

and

VIII
Conclusion

Motivated by various applications of permutation codes, we studied the theoretical bound and construction of permutation codes in Levenshtein, Ulam, and Generalized Kendall-
\tau
metrics in this paper. We achieve a logarithmic improvement on the GV bound of the maximum size of the
t
-deletion correcting permutation codes. We then provide a construction of permutation codes correcting
t
deletions with an efficient decoding algorithm. We also show permutation codes for correcting multiple bursts of deletions and multipermutation codes for correcting
t
deletions. Our constructed codes are better than the previously known results. However, the constructed code is not a systematic code. Hence, in future work, we will aim to design a systematic code with an efficient encoding/decoding algorithm.
Besides that, there are many avenues for future research including constructing permutation codes for correcting
2
deletions with a lower redundancy.

Proof of Claim

Proof:

We first denote
|\mathcal{C}_{\text{FM}}(n,r,d_{u})|
and
|\mathcal{C}(n,r,d_{u})|
as
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
respectively for simplification.
We have:

\log E_{1}=r\left[\log\left(\dfrac{n}{r}-d_{u}+1\right)!-\log\dbinom{n/r}{d_{u}-1}\right].

Also,

\log\dbinom{n/r}{d_{u}-1}=\log\left(\dfrac{n}{r}!\right)-\log(d_{u}-1)!-\log\left(\dfrac{n}{r}-d_{u}+1\right)!.

Then,

\displaystyle\log E_{1}

\displaystyle=r\left[\log\left(\dfrac{n}{r}-d_{u}+1\right)!-\left(\log\left(\dfrac{n}{r}!\right)-\log(d_{u}-1)!-\log\left(\dfrac{n}{r}-d_{u}+1\right)!\right)\right]

\displaystyle=r\left[2\log\left(\dfrac{n}{r}-d_{u}+1\right)!-\log\left(\dfrac{n}{r}!\right)+\log(d_{u}-1)!\right].

We apply Stirling’s approximation and let
N=\dfrac{n}{r}
, so
N\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Then, we have:

\displaystyle\log\left(N!\right)

\displaystyle\approx N\log N-N,

\displaystyle\log\left(N-d_{u}+1\right)!

\displaystyle\approx\left(N-d_{u}+1\right)\log\left(N-d_{u}+1\right)-\left(N-d_{u}+1\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle\log E_{1}

\displaystyle\approx r\left[2\left(N-d_{u}+1\right)\log\left(N-d_{u}+1\right)-2\left(N-d_{u}+1\right)-N\log N+N+\log(d_{u}-1)!\right].

Since
d_{u}
is a constant and
N\to\infty
, we can approximate as
\log\left(N-d_{u}+1\right)\approx\log N
.
We have

\displaystyle\log E_{1}\approx r\left[N\log N-N-2(d_{u}-1)\log N\right]+\text{constant}.

We recall that
rN=n
and
\log N=\log n-\log r
, then

\log E_{1}\approx n\log n-n(1+\log r)-2r(d_{u}-1)\log n+\text{constant}.

(12)

On the other side, we have

\log E_{2}=\log n!-\frac{n}{r}\log(r!)-\log\dbinom{n}{(r+2)d_{u}}-(r+2)d_{u}\log\left(\dfrac{n}{r}\right).

Term
\log n!
:

\log n!\approx n\log n-n.

Term
\frac{n}{r}\log(r!)
:

\frac{n}{r}\log(r!)=nC_{1},\quad\text{where }C_{1}=\frac{\log(r!)}{r}.

Term
\log\dbinom{n}{(r+2)d_{u}}
:

\log\dbinom{n}{(r+2)d_{u}}=\log n!-\log\left((r+2)d_{u}\right)!-\log\left(n-(r+2)d_{u}\right)!.

Applying Stirling’s approximation:

\displaystyle\log n!

\displaystyle\approx n\log n-n,

\displaystyle\log\left((r+2)d_{u}\right)!

\displaystyle\approx(r+2)d_{u}\log\left((r+2)d_{u}\right)-(r+2)d_{u},\quad(\text{constant})

\displaystyle\log\left(n-(r+2)d_{u}\right)!

\displaystyle\approx\left(n-(r+2)d_{u}\right)\log\left(n-(r+2)d_{u}\right)-\left(n-(r+2)d_{u}\right).

(r+2)d_{u}

n\to\infty
, we have
n-(r+2)d_{u}\approx n
and
\log\left(n-(r+2)d_{u}\right)\approx\log n.

Then,

\displaystyle\log\left(n-(r+2)d_{u}\right)!

\displaystyle\approx\left(n-(r+2)d_{u}\right)\log n-\left(n-(r+2)d_{u}\right).

By combining terms:

\log\dbinom{n}{(r+2)d_{u}}\approx(r+2)d_{u}\log n+\text{constant}.

Term
(r+2)d_{u}\log\left(\dfrac{n}{r}\right)
:

(r+2)d_{u}\log\left(\dfrac{n}{r}\right)=(r+2)d_{u}(\log n-\log r)=(r+2)d_{u}\log n+\text{constant}.

\log E_{2}\approx n\log n-n(1+C_{1})-2(r+2)d_{u}\log n+\text{constant}.

\frac{E_{1}}{E_{2}}=e^{-n\delta}\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.
