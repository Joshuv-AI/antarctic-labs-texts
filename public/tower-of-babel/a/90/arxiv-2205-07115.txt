Nearly optimal resolution estimate for the two-dimensional super-resolution and a new algorithm for direction of arrival estimation with uniform rectangular array Thanks: This work was supported in part by the Swiss National Science Foundation grant number 200021–200307.
Ping Liu, Habib Ammari

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a new technique to obtain nearly optimal estimate of the computational resolution limit introduced in [ 44 , 43 , 42 ] for two-dimensional super-resolution problems. Our main contributions are fivefold: (i) Our work improves the resolution estimate for number detection and location recovery in two-dimensional super-resolution problems to nearly optimal; (ii) As a consequence, we derive a stability result for a sparsity-promoting algorithm in two-dimensional super-resolution problems (or Direction of Arrival problems (DOA)). The stability result exhibits the optimal performance of sparsity promoting in solving such problems; (iii) Our techniques pave the way for improving the estimate for resolution limits in higher-dimensional super-resolutions to nearly optimal; (iv) Inspired by these new techniques, we propose a new coordinate-combination-based model order detection algorithm for two-dimensional DOA estimation and theoretically demonstrate its optimal performance, and (v) we also propose a new coordinate-combination-based MUSIC algorithm for super-resolving sources in two-dimensional DOA estimation. It has excellent performance and enjoys many advantages compared to the conventional DOA algorithms. The coordinate-combination idea seems to be a promising way for multi-dimensional DOA estimation.

[
54
,
15
]
. From the mathematical perspective, the resolution limit could only be set when taking into account the noise
[
23
,
21
,
12
]
and surpassing these classical resolution limits is very promising for imaging modalities with high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). This understanding motivates new works on deriving more rigorous resolution limits
[
30
,
31
,
48
,
47
]
. At the beginning of this century, the dependence of two-point resolution on the noise level has been thoroughly investigated from the perspective of statistical inference
[
61
,
62
,
63
]
, but the resolution estimates for resolving multiple sources only achieve breakthroughs in recent years due to its nonlinearity.

To understand the resolution in resolving multiple sources, in the earlier works
[
44
,
43
,
42
]
we have defined “computational resolution limits” for number detection and location recovery in the one- and multi-dimensional super-resolution problems and characterized them by the signal-to-noise ratio, cutoff frequency, and number of sources. In
[

, we derived sharp estimates for the computational resolution limits in one dimensional super-resolution problems. We extended the estimations to multi-dimensional cases in
[
42
]
, but the new estimation is not that sharp due to the techniques of projection used there. Specifically, the upper bound for the resolution increases rapidly as the source number
n
and space dimensionality

increases. To address this issue, this paper aims to derive better and nearly optimal estimates for the computational resolution limits in two-dimensional super-resolution problems and provide a better way to tackle general multi-dimensional cases. The main contribution of our work are fivefold: (i) Our work improves the resolution estimate in
[
42
]
for number detection and location recovery in two-dimensional super-resolution problems to nearly optimal; (ii) As a consequence, we derive a stability result for a sparsity-promoting algorithm in two-dimensional super-resolution problems (or Direction of Arrival problems (DOA)). Although it is well-known that the total variation optimization

1.1
Existing works on the resolution limit problem

The first theory for quantifying the resolution limit was derived by Ernst Abbe
[
1
,
71
]
. Since then, there have been various proposals for the resolution limit
[
55
,
64
,
59
,
33
]
, among which the famous and widely used ones are the Rayleigh limit
[
55
]
and the full width at half maximum (FWHM)
[
20
]
. However, these classical resolution limits neglect the effect of noise and hence are not mathematically rigorous
[
23
,
21
,
12
]
. From a mathematical perspective, there is no resolution limit when one has perfect access of the exact intensity profile of the diffraction images. Therefore, the resolution limit can only be rigorously set when taking into account the measurement noise or aberration to preclude perfect access to the diffraction images. Based on this understanding, many works were devoted to characterize the dependence of the two-point resolution on the signal-to-noise ratio from the perspective of statistical inference
[
30
,
31
,
48
,
47
,
61
,
62
,
63
]
. These classical and semi-classical limits of two-point resolution have been well-studied and we refer the reader to
[
41
,
12
,
17
,
21
]
for more detailed introductions.

For the resolution limit of superresolving multiple point sources, the problem becomes much more difficult due to the high degree of nonlinearity. To our knowledge, the first breakthrough was achieved by Donoho in 1992
[
24
]
. He considered a grid setting where a discrete measure is supported on a lattice (spacing by
\Delta
) and regularized by a so-called "Rayleigh index"
b
. The problem is to reconstruct the amplitudes of the grid points from their noisy Fourier data in
[-\Omega,\Omega]
with
\Omega
being the band limit. He demonstrated that the minimax error for the amplitude reconstruction is bounded from below and above by
SRF^{2b-1}\sigma
and
SRF^{2b+1}\sigma
respectively with
\sigma
being the noise level and the super-resolution factor
SRF=1/({\Omega\Delta})
. His results emphasize the importance of sparsity and signal-to-noise in super-resolution. But the estimate has not been improved until recent years. In recent years, due to the enormous development of super-resolution modalities in biological imaging
[
29
,
70
,
32
,
10
,
57
]
and the popularity of researches of super-resolution algorithms in applied mathematics
[
11
,
7
,
25
,
53
,
69
,
68
,
51
,
50
,
22
,
40
,
38
]
, the inherent superresolving capacity of the imaging problem is drawing increasing interest and has been well-studied for the one-dimensional case. In
[
19
]
, the authors considered resolving
n
-sparse point sources supported on a grid and improved the results of Donoho. They showed that the minimax error in the amplitude recovery scales as
SRF^{2n-1}\sigma
in the presence of noise with intensity
\sigma
. The case of multi-clustered point sources was considered in
[
37
,
8
]
and similar minimax error estimations were derived. In
[
4
,
9
]
, the authors considered the minimax error for recovering off-the-grid point sources. Based on an analysis of the "prony-type system", they derived bounds for both amplitude and location reconstructions of the point sources. More precisely, they showed that for
\sigma\lessapprox(SRF)^{-2p+1}
, where
p
is the number of point sources in a cluster, the minimax error for the amplitude and the location recoveries scale respectively as
(SRF)^{2p-1}\sigma
and
(SRF)^{2p-2}{\sigma}/{\Omega}
, while for the isolated non-clustered source, the corresponding minimax error for the amplitude and the location recoveries scale respectively as
\sigma
and
{\sigma}/{\Omega}
. We also refer the reader to
[
49
,
12
]
for understanding the resolution limit from the perceptive of sample complexity and to
[
67
,
16
]
for the resolving limit of some algorithms.

On the other hand, in order to characterize the exact resolution rather than the minimax error in recovering multiple point sources, in the earlier works
[
44
,
42
,
43
]
we have defined "computational resolution limits" which characterize the minimum required distance between point sources so that their number and locations can be stably resolved under certain noise level. By developing a nonlinear approximation theory in a so-called Vandermonde space, we have derived sharp bounds for computational resolution limits in the one-dimensional super-resolution problem. In particular, we have showed in
[
43
]
that the computational resolution limits for the number and location recoveries should be respectively
\frac{C_{\mathrm{num}}}{\Omega}(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}})^{\frac{1}{2n-2}}
and
\frac{C_{\mathrm{supp}}}{\Omega}(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}})^{\frac{1}{2n-1}}
, where
C_{\mathrm{num}}
and
C_{\mathrm{supp}}
are constants and
m_{\min}
is the minimum strength of the point sources. We have extended these estimates to multi-dimensional cases in
[
42
]
but the results are not that optimal due to the projection techniques used there. In this paper, we improve the estimates for the two-dimensional super-resolution problem by a new technique. The improvements shall be discussed in detail in Section
2
. Also, our new technique paves the way for improving the results in higher-dimensional super-resolution problems.

1.2
Direction of Arrival estimation

Our work also inspires new ideas for the two-dimensional direction of arrival estimation. Direction of arrival (DOA) estimation refers to the process of retrieving the direction information of several electromagnetic waves/sources from the received data of a number of antenna elements in a specific array. It is an important problem in array signal processing and finds wide applications in radar, sonar, wireless
communications, etc; see, for instance,
[
6
]
.

In one-dimensional DOA estimation, if the antenna elements are uniformly spaced in a line, the well-known MUSIC, ESPRIT algorithms, and other subspace methods can resolve the direction of each incident signal/source with high resolution. But for the two-dimensional DOA estimation with regular rectangular array (URA) where both azimuth and elevation angles should be determined, these subspace methods cannot be simply extended to the two-dimensional case to directly determine the azimuth and elevation angle of each source. A major idea to solve the two-dimensional DOA problem is to decompose it into two independent one-dimensional DOA estimations in which the subspaces methods can be leveraged to efficiently restore the direction components of sources corresponding to
x
-axis and
y
-axis. We call the methods with this decoupling idea as one-dimensional-based algorithms throughout the paper for convenience of discussion. It is worth emphasizing that other ways for directly obtaining the azimuth and elevation angles of each source were also considered
[
78
,
76
,
39
]
, but the signal processing in a higher dimensional space damped their computational efficiency.

Although the one-dimensional-based algorithms are usually much more computationally efficient, they still suffer from some issues: (i) the loss of distance separation for
x
-axis or
y
-axis components; (ii) pair matching of the estimated elevation and azimuth angles. For the first issue, the
x
-axis (or
y
-axis) components of two sources may be closely spaced even though the two sources are far away in the two-dimensional space. This causes very unstable reconstruction of the one-dimensional components and the sources. Most of the researches usually ignored these issues and some papers proposed different ways to enhance the reconstruction but the proposed methods are complicated
[
73
,
72
]
. For example, in
[
73
]
, the authors utilized Taylor expansion, subspace projection, and a tree structure to enhance the reconstruction when the recovered one-dimensional components are unstable. The second issue is that the pair matching of the estimated elevation and azimuth angles is very time consuming when dealing with multiple components of sources. It usually requires a complex process or two-dimensional search
[
66
,
78
,
18
,
46
,
35
]
.

In this paper, we propose a new efficient one-dimensional-based algorithm for the two-dimensional DOA estimation which solves the above two issues in a simple way. First, our algorithm employs a new idea named coordinate-combination to avoid severe loss of distance separation between sources in certain region; see Section
5.4
for the detailed discussion. On the other hand, unlike conventional
one-dimensional-based algorithms, the pair matching problem of our algorithm is a simple balanced assignment problem
[
52
]
which can be solved efficiently by many algorithms such as the Hungarian algorithm.

1.3
Organization of the paper

The rest of the paper is organized in the following way. In Section 2, we present the main results on computational resolution limits for the number detection and the location recovery in the two-dimensional super-resolution problem. We also provide a stability result for a sparsity promoting algorithm. In Section 3, we prove the main results in Section 2. Inspired by the techniques in the proofs, in Section 4 and Section 5 we introduce respectively the coordinate-combination-based number detection
and source recovery algorithms in two-dimensional DOA estimations. We also conduct numerical experiments to demonstrate their super-resolution capability. In the appendix, we prove a technical lemma.

Main results

2.1
Model setting

We consider the following model of a linear combination of point sources in a two-dimensional space:

\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}},

where
\delta
denotes Dirac’s
\delta
-distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},1\leq j\leq n
, which are the supports of the measure, represent the locations of the point sources and
a_{j}\in\mathbb{C},1\leq j\leq n
, their amplitudes. We remark that, throughout the paper, we will use bold symbols for vectors and matrices, and ordinary ones for scalar values.
We call that the measure
\mu
is
n
-sparse if all
a_{j}
’s are nonzero.
We denote by

m_{\min}=\min_{j=1,\cdots,n}|a_{j}|,\quad D_{\min}=\min_{p\neq j}\Big|\Big|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{p}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}\Big|\Big|_{1}.

(2.1)

We assume that the available measurement is the noisy Fourier data of
\mu
in a bounded domain, that is,

\mathbf{Y}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})=\mathcal{F}\mu(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})+\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}+\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}),\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}\in[0,\Omega]^{2},

(2.2)

where
\mathcal{F}\mu
denotes the Fourier transform of
\mu
,

\Omega
is the cut-off frequency, and
\mathbf{W}
is the noise. We assume that

||\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})||_{\infty}<\sigma,

where
\sigma
is the noise level. We are interested in the resolution limit for a cluster of tightly spaced point sources.
To be more specific, we denote by

B_{\delta,\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}):=\Big\{\mathbf{y}\ \Big|\ \mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\ ||\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}||_{\infty}<\delta\Big\},

and assume that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\in B_{\frac{(n-1)\pi}{6\Omega},\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}),j=1,\cdots,n
, or equivalently
||\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}||_{\infty}<\frac{(n-1)\pi}{6\Omega}
.

The inverse problem we are interested in is to recover the discrete measure
\mu
from the above noisy measurement
\mathbf{Y}
.

2.2
Computational Resolution Limit for number detection in the two-dimensional super-resolution problem

In this section, we estimate the super-resolving capacity of the source number detection in two-dimensional super-resolution problems. To be specific, we will define and characterize a computational resolution limit for the corresponding number detection problems. Our main results are built upon delicate analysis of the
\sigma
-admissible measure defined below.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a measurement
\mathbf{Y}
, we say that
\hat{\mu}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{a}_{j}\delta_{\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{j}},\ \mathbf{\hat{y}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a
\sigma
-admissible discrete measure of
\mathbf{Y}
if

||\mathcal{F}\hat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})||_{\infty}<\sigma,\ \text{for all}\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}\in[0,\Omega]^{2}.

Note that the set of
\sigma
-admissible measures of
\mathbf{Y}
characterizes all possible solutions to the inverse problem with the given measurement
\mathbf{Y}
. If all
\sigma
-admissible measures have at least
n
supports, then detecting the correct source number is possible, for example by targeting at the sparsest admissible measures. However, if there exists one
\sigma
-admissible measure with less than
n
supports, detecting the source number
n
is impossible without additional prior information. This leads to the following new definition of resolution limit, named computational resolution limit.

Definition 2.2
.

The computational resolution limit to the number detection problem in two dimensions is defined as the smallest nonnegative number
\mathcal{D}_{2,num}
such that for all
n
-sparse measures
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{\mathbf{y}_{j}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\in B_{\frac{(n-1)\pi}{6\Omega},\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}})
and the associated measurement
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}
in (
2.2
), if

\min_{p\neq j}||\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{y}_{p}||_{1}\geq\mathcal{D}_{2,num},

then there does not exist any
\sigma
-admissible measure with less than
n
supports for
\mathbf{Y}
.

The above resolution limit is termed “computational resolution limit” to distinguish it from the classic Rayleigh limit. Compared to the Rayleigh limit, the definition of the computational resolution limit is more rigorous from the mathematical perspective. It is related to the noise, by which it is more applicable for modern imaging techniques. In
[
44
,
43
,
42
]
, the authors defined similar computational resolution limits and present rigorous estimations for them. Here by the following theorem, we derive a nearly optimal estimate to the
\mathcal{D}_{2,num}
, which substantially improves the estimate in
[
42
]
for the two-dimensional case.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let the measurement
\mathbf{Y}
in (
2.2
) be generated by a
n
-sparse measure
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{\mathbf{y}_{j}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\in B_{\frac{(n-1)\pi}{6\Omega},\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}})
. Let
n\geq 2
and assume that the following separation condition is satisfied

\min_{p\neq j,1\leq p,j\leq n}\Big|\Big|\mathbf{y}_{p}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\Big|\Big|_{1}\geq\frac{16.6\pi(n-1)}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-2}}.

(2.3)

Then there does not exist any
\sigma
-admissible measures of
\mathbf{Y}
with less than
n
supports.

Theorem
2.1
reveals that when
\min_{p\neq j,1\leq p,j\leq n}\Big|\Big|\mathbf{y}_{p}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\Big|\Big|_{1}\geq\frac{16.6\pi(n-1)}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-2}}
, recovering exactly the source number
n
is possible. Compared with the Rayleigh limit
\frac{c_{2}\pi}{\Omega}
, where
c_{2}
is a constant, Theorem
2.1
also indicates that resolving the source number in the sub-Rayleigh regime is theoretically possible if the SNR is sufficiently large.

Moreover, the estimate in Theorem
2.1
substantially improves the result in
[
42
]
, where the upper bound estimation for the two-dimensional computational resolution limit is

\frac{Cn(n-1)}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-2}}

with
C
being an explicit constant. By the techniques of this paper, we also pave the way for estimating the resolution limit for higher dimensions. It is indicated that we can demonstrate that the corresponding resolution limit in the
k
-dimensional super-resolution problem can be bounded above by

\frac{C_{num}(k)(n-1)}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-2}},

where
C_{num}(k)
is a constant determined by the space dimensionality. This substantially improves the result in
[
42
]
that the computational resolution limit is estimated to be bounded above by

\frac{4.4\pi e\ (\pi/2)^{k-1}(n(n-1)/\pi)^{\xi(k-1)}}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-2}},

where
\xi(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j},\ k\geq 1
. By these new estimates, we get rid of the exponential dependence of the index
n
on the dimensionality
k
.

On the other hand, it is already known from
[
42
]
that the computational resolution limit for the number detection in the
k
-dimensional super-resolution problem is bounded below by
\frac{C_{1}}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-2}}
for some constant
C_{1}
. Thus the
\mathcal{D}_{2,num}
is bounded by

\frac{C_{1}}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-2}}\leq\mathcal{D}_{2,num}\leq\frac{C_{2}n}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-2}}.

(2.4)

This estimate is nearly optimal.

The above estimates further indicate a phase transition phenomenon in the two-dimensional number detection problem. Specifically, by (
2.4
) we expect the presence of a line of slope
2n-2
in the parameter space
\log(SRF)-\log(SNR)
above which the source number can be correctly detected in each realization. This phenomenon is confirmed exactly by the number detection algorithm (
Algorithm
2
) later in Section
4.5
and illustrated in Figure
4.2
.

2.3
Computational Resolution Limit for location recovery in the two-dimensional super-resolution problem

We next present our results on the resolution limit for the location recovery problem in two-dimensions. We first introduce the following concept of
\delta
-neighborhood of discrete measures.

Define

B_{\delta,1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}):=\Big\{\mathbf{y}\ \Big|\ \mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\ ||\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}||_{1}<\delta\Big\}.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}
be a
n
-sparse discrete measure in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
\delta>0
be such that the
n
balls
B_{\delta,1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}),1\leq j\leq n
are pairwise disjoint. We say that

\hat{\mu}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{j}\delta_{\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{j}}
is within
\delta
-neighborhood of
\mu
if each
\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{j}
is contained in one and only one of the
n
balls
B_{\delta,1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}),1\leq j\leq n
.

According to the above definition, a measure
\hat{\mu}
in a
\delta
-neighborhood of
\mu
preserves the inner structure of the collection of point sources. For a stable location (or support of measure) recovery algorithm, the output should be a measure in some
\delta
-neighborhood of the underlying sources. Moreover,
\delta
should tend to zero as the noise level
\sigma
tends to zero. We now introduce the computational resolution limit for the support recovery problem. For ease of exposition, we only consider measures supported in
B_{\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{12\Omega},\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}})
, where
n
is the source number.

Definition 2.4
.

The computational resolution limit in the two-dimensional location recovery problem is defined as the smallest non-negative number
\mathcal{D}_{2,supp}
so that for
any
n
-sparse measure
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{\mathbf{y}_{j}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\in B_{\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{12\Omega},\infty}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}})
and the associated measurement
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}
in (
2.2
),
if

\min_{p\neq j,1\leq p,j\leq n}\Big|\Big|{\mathbf{y}_{p}-\mathbf{y}_{j}}\Big|\Big|_{1}\geq\mathcal{D}_{2,supp},

then there exists
\delta>0
such that any
\sigma
-admissible measure of
\mathbf{Y}
with
n
supports in
B_{\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{12\Omega}}(\mathbf{0})
is within
\delta
-neighbourhood of
\mu
.

We have the following estimate for the upper bound of
\mathcal{D}_{2,supp}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Let the measurement
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}
in (
2.2
) be generated by a
n
-sparse measure
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\in B_{\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{12\Omega},\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}})
in the two-dimensional space. Assume that

D_{\min}:=\min_{p\neq j}\Big|\Big|\mathbf{y}_{p}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\Big|\Big|_{1}\geq\frac{15.3\pi(n-\frac{1}{2})}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}}.

(2.5)

If
\hat{\mu}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{j}\delta_{\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{j}}
supported on
B_{\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{12\Omega},\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}})
is a
\sigma
-admissible measure of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}
, then
\hat{\mu}
is in a
\frac{D_{\min}}{2}
-neighborhood of
\mu
. Moreover, after reordering the
\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{j}
’s, we have

\Big|\Big|\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{j}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\Big|\Big|_{1}\leq\frac{C(n)}{\Omega}SRF^{2n-2}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}},\quad 1\leq j\leq n,

(2.6)

where
SRF:=\frac{\pi}{D_{\min}\Omega}
is the super-resolution factor and

C(n)=\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}2^{5n-1}(2n-1)^{2n-1}\pi}{3^{2n-0.5}}.

Theorem
2.1
demonstrates that when
\min_{p\neq j,1\leq p,j\leq n}\Big|\Big|\mathbf{y}_{p}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\Big|\Big|_{1}\geq\frac{15.3\pi(n-1)}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-2}}
, it is possible to recover stably the source locations. For sufficiently large SNR, the limit in Theorem
2.1
is less than the Rayleigh limit. This indicates that super-resolution is possible for two-dimensional imaging problems. Also, the estimate here is better than the one obtained in
[
42
]
, which is

\frac{Cn(n-1)}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}},

with an explicit constant
C
. By the techniques of this paper, we also pave the way for estimating the resolution limit of location recovery in higher dimensional super-resolution problems. In fact, the corresponding resolution limit in the
k
-dimensional super-resolution problem can be bounded above by

\frac{C_{supp}(k)(n-1)}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}},

where
C_{supp}(k)
is a constant depending on the space dimensionality. This substantially improves the estimate,

\frac{5.88\pi e4^{k-1}((n+2)(n-1)/2)^{\xi(k-1)}}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}},\quad\xi(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j},\ k\geq 1,

derived in
[
42
]
. Again, one can get rid of the exponential dependence of the index of
n
on the dimensionality
k
by using these new estimates.

It has been already shown in
[
42
]
that the computational resolution limit for the location recovery in the
k
-dimensional super-resolution problem is bounded below by
\frac{C_{3}}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}}
for some constant
C_{3}
. Thus the
\mathcal{D}_{2,supp}
is bounded by

\frac{C_{3}}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}}\leq\mathcal{D}_{2,supp}\leq\frac{C_{4}n}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}}.

(2.7)

This estimate is nearly optimal.

On the other hand, (
2.7
) indicates a phase transition in the location recovery problem. From (
2.7
) we expect that there exists a line of slope
2n-1
in the parameter space of
\log SRF-\log SNR
such that the location recovery is stable in every point above the line. This is confirmed by
Algorithm
4
in Section
5.4.2
and illustrated in Figure
5.2
.

2.4
Stability of a sparsity-promoting algorithm

Sparsity-promoting algorithms are popular methods in imaging processing and many other fields. By the results for resolution limit, we can derive a stability result for a
l_{0}
-minimization in the two-dimensional super-resolution problems. We consider the following
l_{0}
-minimization problem:

\min_{\rho\in\mathcal{O}}||\rho||_{0}\quad\text{subject to}\quad|\mathcal{F}\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}(\omega)|<\sigma,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}\in[0,\Omega]^{2},

(2.8)

where
||\rho||_{0}
is the number of Dirac masses representing the discrete measure
\rho
. As a corollary of Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
, we have the following stability result.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\sigma\leq m_{\min}
. Let the measurement
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}
in (
2.2
) be generated by a
n
-sparse measure
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\in B_{\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{12\Omega},\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}})
in the two-dimensional space. Assume that

D_{\min}:=\min_{p\neq j}\Big|\Big|\mathbf{y}_{p}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\Big|\Big|_{1}\geq\frac{15.3\pi(n-\frac{1}{2})}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}}.

(2.9)

Let
\mathcal{O}
in the minimization problem (
2.8
) be
B_{\frac{(n-1)\pi}{6\Omega},\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}})
, then the solution to (
2.8
) contains exactly
n
point sources. For any solution
\hat{\mu}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{j}\delta_{\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{j}}
, it is in a
\frac{D_{\min}}{2}
-neighborhood of
\mu
. Moreover, after reordering the
\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{j}
’s, we have

\Big|\Big|\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{j}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\Big|\Big|_{1}\leq\frac{C(n)}{\Omega}SRF^{2n-2}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}},\quad 1\leq j\leq n,

(2.10)

where
SRF:=\frac{\pi}{D_{\min}\Omega}
and

C(n)=\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}2^{5n-1}(2n-1)^{2n-1}\pi}{3^{2n-0.5}}.

Theorem
2.3
reveals that sparsity promoting over admissible solutions could resolve the source locations to the resolution limit level. It provides an insight that theoretically sparsity-promoting algorithms would have excellent performance on the two-dimensional super-resolution problems. Especially, under the separation condition (
2.9
), any tractable sparsity-promoting algorithms (such as total variation minimization algorithms
[
11
]
) rendering the sparsest solution
could stably reconstruct all the
source locations.

3
Proofs of the main results

The idea for proving the main results of the paper is to use some new techniques to reduce the two-dimensional problem to a one-dimensional case. The reduction techniques are mainly based on the three crucial observations in the following subsection. The estimation methods for the one-dimensional super-resolution problem are based on a nonlinear approximation theory in Vandermonde space, which we present in Section
6
.

3.1
Three crucial observations

We here introduce three crucial observations that reduce the two-dimensional super-resolution problem to its one-dimensional analog, by which we are able to derive the resolution limit theory of this paper. Our observations also pave the way for extending the resolution estimates to higher dimensions. Moreover, they inspire a new direction for the DOA algorithms; see Sections
4
and
5
.

Translation invariant:

By the translation invariant we mean that if a measure
\hat{\mu}=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\hat{a}_{j}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j}}
is a
\sigma
-admissible measure for the measurement
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}
, then
\hat{\mu}=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\hat{a}_{j}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}
is a
\sigma
-admissible measure for measurement
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})
for any vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. More precisely, we have

\Big|{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\hat{a}_{j}e^{i(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}-e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})}\Big|=\Big|{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\hat{a}_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})}\Big|<\sigma,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}\in[0,\Omega]^{2}.

(3.1)

In addition, if for certain
\delta\geq 0
,

\Big|{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\hat{a}_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}}\Big|<\delta,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}\in[0,\Omega]^{2},

(3.2)

then for any vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\Big|{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\hat{a}_{j}e^{i(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}e^{i(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}}\Big|<\delta,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}\in[0,\Omega]^{2}.

Combination of coordinates:

The second observation is that if we suppose that (
3.2
) is satisfied, we have a similar estimate for the summation of combinations of
e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,1}},e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,2}}
and
e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,1}},e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,2}}
for certain
\tau
. Specifically, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any integer
t\geq 0
and
\tau\leq\frac{\Omega}{t}
, the measurement constraint (
3.2
) implies

\Big|{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\hat{a}_{j}(e^{ir_{1}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,1}}+e^{ir_{2}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,2}})^{t}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(e^{ir_{1}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,1}}+e^{ir_{2}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,2}})^{t}}\Big|<2^{t}\delta,\quad r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

Let
\hat{d}_{j}=e^{ir_{1}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,1}}+e^{ir_{2}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,2}}
and
d_{j}=e^{ir_{1}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,1}}+e^{ir_{2}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,2}}
. We have

\displaystyle\Big|{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\hat{a}_{j}\hat{d}_{j}^{t}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}d_{j}^{t}}\Big|=\Big|{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\hat{a}_{j}(e^{ir_{1}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,1}}+e^{ir_{2}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,2}})^{t}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(e^{ir_{1}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,1}}+e^{ir_{2}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,2}})^{t}}\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|{\sum_{t_{1}+t_{2}=t,0\leq t_{1},t_{2}\leq t}{t\choose t_{1}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{q}\hat{a}_{j}e^{ir_{1}t_{1}}e^{ir_{2}t_{2}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,1}t_{1}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,2}t_{2}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}e^{ir_{1}t_{1}}e^{ir_{2}t_{2}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,1}t_{1}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,2}t_{2}}\Big)}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{t_{1}+t_{2}=t,0\leq t_{1},t_{2}\leq t}{t\choose t_{1}}\Big|{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\hat{a}_{j}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,1}t_{1}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,2}t_{2}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,1}t_{1}}e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,2}t_{2}}}\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t_{1}+t_{2}=t,0\leq t_{1},t_{2}\leq t}{t\choose t_{1}}\Big|{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\hat{a}_{j}e^{i(t_{1}\tau,t_{2}\tau)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}e^{i(t_{1}\tau,t_{2}\tau)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}}\Big|

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\sum_{t_{1}+t_{2}=t,0\leq t_{1},t_{2}\leq t}{t\choose t_{1}}\delta\quad\Big(\text{by $\tau\leq\frac{\Omega}{t}$ and (\ref{equ:measureconstraint2})}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{t}\delta.

∎

This is the key observation of the paper. It reduces the two-dimensional super-resolution problem to nearly a one-dimensional super-resolution one. Since it is about the difference between summation of combinations of
e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,1}},e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j,2}}
and
e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,1}},e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,2}}
, we refer to this observation as combination of coordinates and call the elements
e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,1}}+e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,2}}
coordinate-combined elements. This coordinate-combination technique will be used in deriving new algorithms for the DOA problem in Sections
4
and
5
.

Compared to the projection techniques in
[
42
,
12
]
which utilize the measurement constraint only in several one-dimensional spaces to derive stability results, our formulation utilizes more measurement constraints and consequently yields better estimates.

Preservation of the separation distance for the coordinate-combined elements:

The last observation is that, for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j}
’s in
[0,\frac{2\pi}{3}]^{2}
, the coordinate-combined elements
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j,2}}
still preserve the separation distance between the
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j}
’s. This is revealed by Lemma
3.2
. Note that the projection trick in
[
42
,
12
]
and many conventional two-dimensional DOA algorithms do not preserve the separation distance between the original source. This causes many issues in the reconstruction and resolution estimation. Lemma
3.2
is the main result of this paper by which we could overcome the above issues and hence find a new way to solve two-dimensional DOA problems.

Lemma 3.2
.

For two different vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j}\in\mathbb{[}0,\frac{2\pi}{3}]^{2},j=1,2
with
\frac{\pi}{3}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j,2}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j,1}\leq\frac{2}{3}\pi,j=1,2
, if
||\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2}||_{1}\geq\Delta
, then

\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}}-(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}})}\Big|\geq\frac{3}{2\pi}\Delta.

Proof.

Note that
0\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j,1}<\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j,2}\leq\frac{2\pi}{3},j=1,2
. We prove the lemma by considering the following two cases.

Case 1:

0\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}<\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}\leq\frac{2\pi}{3}
.

In this case,

\displaystyle\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}}-(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}})}\Big|\geq

\displaystyle\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}}}\Big|-\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}}}\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\Big(\cos(\frac{\phi_{2}}{2})-\cos(\frac{\phi_{1}}{2})\Big),

where
\phi_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j,2}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j,1},j=1,2
. By the assumption made in the lemma, we have
\Delta\leq\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}\leq\frac{\pi}{3}
. Note also that
\frac{\pi}{6}\leq\frac{\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}}{4}\leq\frac{\pi}{3}
. Thus

2\Big(\cos(\frac{\phi_{2}}{2})-\cos(\frac{\phi_{1}}{2})\Big)=4\sin(\frac{\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}}{4})\sin(\frac{\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}}{4})\geq 4\sin(\frac{\pi}{6})\sin(\frac{\Delta}{4})\geq\frac{3\Delta}{2\pi},

where the last inequality uses
\sin(\frac{\Delta}{4})\geq\frac{3}{\pi}\frac{\Delta}{4}
for
0<\frac{\Delta}{4}\leq\frac{\pi}{12}
.

Case 2:

0\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}\leq\frac{2\pi}{3}
.

The idea is to calculate the angle between
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}}
and
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}}
. By simple analysis of the angle relations between
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}},e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}},e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}},e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}}
, we obtain that the angle between
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}}
and
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}}
is
\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}}{2}
, which is larger than
\frac{\Delta}{2}
. Thus

\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}}-(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}})}\Big|\geq\max\Big(\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}}}\Big|,\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}}}\Big|\Big)\sin(\frac{\Delta}{2}).

Since
\frac{\pi}{3}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j,2}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j,1}\leq\frac{2}{3}\pi,j=1,2
, we have

\max\Big(\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}}}\Big|,\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}}}\Big|\Big)\geq 1.

Therefore,

\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1,2}}-(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2,2}})}\Big|\geq\sin(\frac{\Delta}{2})\geq\frac{3\Delta}{2\pi},

where the last inequality uses
\sin(\frac{\Delta}{2})\geq\frac{3}{\pi}\frac{\Delta}{2}
for
0<\frac{\Delta}{2}\leq\frac{\pi}{6}
.
∎

\displaystyle\Big|{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{j}\hat{g}_{j}^{t}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}g_{j}^{t}}\Big|<2^{t+1}\sigma,\quad t=0,1,\cdots,2n-1.

(3.13)

Let

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{j}\hat{d}_{j}^{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}d_{j}^{0},\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{j}\hat{d}_{j}^{1}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}d_{j}^{1},\quad\cdots,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{j}\hat{d}_{j}^{2n-1}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}d_{j}^{2n-1}\Big)^{\top},

and

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{j}\hat{g}_{j}^{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}g_{j}^{0},\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{j}\hat{g}_{j}^{1}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}g_{j}^{1},\quad\cdots,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{j}\hat{g}_{j}^{2n-1}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}g_{j}^{2n-1}\Big)^{\top}.

Equations (
3.12
) and (
3.13
) imply respectively

||\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}||_{2}<\frac{2^{2n+1}\sigma}{\sqrt{3}},\quad||\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}||_{2}<\frac{2^{2n+1}\sigma}{\sqrt{3}}.

Note also that by (
3.8
), (
3.9
), (
3.10
), and (
3.11
), we get

|\hat{d}_{j}|,|d_{j}|,|\hat{g}_{j}|,|g_{j}|\leq\sqrt{3},\quad j=1,\cdots,n.

Define
d_{\min}:=\min_{p\neq q}\Big|{d_{p}-d_{q}}\Big|
and
g_{\min}:=\min_{p\neq q}\Big|{g_{p}-g_{q}}\Big|
. Applying Theorem
6.2
, we thus have that

\Big|\Big|\eta_{n,n}(d_{1},\cdots,d_{n},\hat{d}_{1},\cdots,\hat{d}_{n})\Big|\Big|_{\infty}<\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}}{d_{\min}^{n-1}}\frac{2^{2n+1}}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}},

(3.14)

and

\Big|\Big|\eta_{n,n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n},\hat{g}_{1},\cdots,\hat{g}_{n})\Big|\Big|_{\infty}<\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}}{g_{\min}^{n-1}}\frac{2^{2n+1}}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}.

(3.15)

We now demonstrate that we can reorder
\hat{d}_{j},\hat{g}_{j}
to have
|\hat{d}_{j}-d_{j}|<\frac{d_{\min}}{2}
and
|\hat{g}_{j}-g_{j}|<\frac{g_{\min}}{2},j=1,\cdots,n
. First, since (
3.8
) and (
3.10
) hold, by Lemma
3.2
we have

\displaystyle d_{\min}\geq\frac{3}{2\pi}\min_{p\neq q}\tau\Big|\Big|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{p}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{q}\Big|\Big|_{1}\geq 11.475\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}}>2^{3/2}(1+\sqrt{3})\Big(\frac{2^{5/2}}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}},

(3.16)

and

g_{\min}\geq\frac{3}{2\pi}\min_{p\neq q}\tau\Big|\Big|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{p}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{q}\Big|\Big|_{1}\geq 11.475\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}}>2^{3/2}(1+\sqrt{3})\Big(\frac{2^{5/2}}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}},

where we also use separation condition (
2.5
) in the above derivation. Let

\epsilon_{d}=\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}}{d_{\min}^{n-1}}\frac{2^{2n+1}}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}},\quad\epsilon_{g}=\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}}{g_{\min}^{n-1}}\frac{2^{2n+1}}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}.

By (
3.16
), we have

d_{\min}^{2n-1}\geq\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}2^{3n+1}}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}},\ \text{or equivalently},d_{\min}^{n}\geq 2^{n}\epsilon_{d}.

A similar result holds for
g_{\min}
and
\epsilon_{g}
. Thus the conditions of Lemma
6.8
are satisfied. By Lemma
6.8
, we have that after reordering
\hat{d}_{j},\hat{g}_{j}
,

\Big|\hat{d}_{j}-d_{j}\Big|<\frac{d_{\min}}{2},\quad\Big|\hat{g}_{j}-g_{j}\Big|<\frac{g_{\min}}{2},

and

\displaystyle\Big|{\hat{d}_{j}-d_{j}}\Big|\leq\Big(\frac{2}{d_{\min}}\Big)^{n-1}\epsilon_{d}=\Big(\frac{1}{d_{\min}}\Big)^{2n-2}\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}2^{3n}}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}},

\displaystyle\Big|{\hat{g}_{j}-g_{j}}\Big|\leq\Big(\frac{2}{g_{\min}}\Big)^{n-1}\epsilon_{g}=\Big(\frac{1}{g_{\min}}\Big)^{2n-2}\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}2^{3n}}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}.

Observing

e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j,1}}-e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\hat{d}_{j}-d_{j}+\hat{g}_{j}-g_{j}\Big),\qquad e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j,2}}-e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\hat{d}_{j}-d_{j}-(\hat{g}_{j}-g_{j})\Big),

we conclude that

\Big|e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j,1}}-e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}}\Big|+\Big|e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j,2}}-e^{i\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}}\Big|\leq\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{d_{\min}}\Big)^{2n-2}+\Big(\frac{1}{g_{\min}}\Big)^{2n-2}\Big)\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}2^{3n}}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}.

On the other hand, by (
3.8
) and (
3.9
),

|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j,1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}|\leq\frac{\pi}{6}\quad\mbox{and}\quad|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j,2}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}|\leq\frac{\pi}{6}.

We further have

\displaystyle\tau\Big|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j,1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}\Big|+\tau\Big|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j,2}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}\Big|\leq\frac{\pi}{3}\Big(\Big|e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j,1}}-e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}}\Big|+\Big|e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j,2}}-e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}}\Big|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{d_{\min}}\Big)^{2n-2}+\Big(\frac{1}{g_{\min}}\Big)^{2n-2}\Big)\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}2^{3n}\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}.

Recalling that
\tau=\frac{\Omega}{2n-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j,1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}\Big|+\Big|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j,2}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}\Big|\leq\frac{2n-1}{\Omega}\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{d_{\min}}\Big)^{2n-2}+\Big(\frac{1}{g_{\min}}\Big)^{2n-2}\Big)\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}2^{3n}\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}.

Note that by (
3.16
), we obtain that

D_{\min}\leq\frac{2\pi(2n-1)}{3\Omega}d_{\min}\quad\mbox{and}\quad D_{\min}\leq\frac{2\pi(2n-1)}{3\Omega}g_{\min}.

Thus

\displaystyle\Big|\Big|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}\Big|\Big|_{1}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}2^{3n+1}\pi(2n-1)}{3\sqrt{3}\Omega}\Big(\frac{2(2n-1)}{3}\Big)^{2n-2}\Big(\frac{\pi}{\Omega D_{\min}}\Big)^{2n-2}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}2^{5n-1}(2n-1)^{2n-1}\pi}{3^{2n-0.5}\Omega}\Big(\frac{\pi}{\Omega D_{\min}}\Big)^{2n-2}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}.

Since
||\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}||_{1}=||\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}||_{1}
, we further get

\displaystyle\Big|\Big|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}\Big|\Big|_{1}\leq\frac{(1+\sqrt{3})^{2n-1}2^{5n-1}(2n-1)^{2n-1}\pi}{3^{2n-0.5}\Omega}\Big(\frac{\pi}{\Omega D_{\min}}\Big)^{2n-2}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}.

Since
D_{\min}\geq\frac{15.3\pi(n-0.5)}{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-1}}
, together with the above estimate, we can also show that

\Big|\Big|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}\Big|\Big|_{1}<\frac{D_{\min}}{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4
An algorithm for the model order detection in two-dimensional DOA estimation

In this section, based on the observations made in Section
3.1
, we propose a new algorithm, named coordinate-combination-based sweeping singular-value-thresholding number detection algorithm, for the model order detection in two-dimensional DOA estimations.

Figure 4.1
:
The geometry of a uniform rectangular array.

4.1
Problem formulation

The existing two-dimensional DOA algorithms usually try to estimate the azimuth and elevation angles
(\theta_{j},\phi_{j})
’s that are shown in Figure
4.1
. More precisely, we consider
n
narrowband signals/sources impinging on an
(\Omega+1)\times(\Omega+1)
uniform rectangular array (URA) with
(\Omega+1)^{2}
well calibrated and identically polarized antenna elements. The signal received by these antenna elements in a single snapshot can be expressed by

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}p_{j}e^{jkd_{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,1}}e^{jkd_{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,2}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}),\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}\in[0,1,\cdots,\Omega]^{2},

(4.1)

where
s_{j}
is the
j
-th incident signal,
p_{j}
is a complex constant denoting the signal/antenna polarization mismatch,
k
represents the wavenumber of the carrier frequency, and
d_{x}
and
d_{y}
denote the distance between adjacent antenna element along the
x
-axis and
y
-axis, respectively.
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,1}=\sin\phi_{j}\cos\theta_{j}
is the direction component of signal
s_{j}
propagating along the
x
-axis and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j,2}=\sin\phi_{j}\sin\theta_{j}
is the one propagating along the
y
-axis. The
\phi_{j}
and
\theta_{j}
denote respectively the elevation and azimuth angles of
s_{j}
.
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})
is the additive noise, which is usually assumed to be white Gaussian noise.

For convenience, we consider the following simplified form of (
4.1
):

\mathbf{Y}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}+\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}),\qquad\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}\in[0,1,\cdots,\Omega]^{2},

(4.2)

where
\mathbf{W}
is the noise with
||\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})||_{\infty}<\sigma
and
\sigma
being the noise level. We aim to recover stably the number of the signals and the
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}
’s, by which the elevation and azimuth angles are stably resolved. For a better exposition, we still consider a discrete measure
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}
and denote the
a_{j}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}
’s as sources. The measurement (
4.2
) can be viewed as the noisy Fourier data of the measure
\mu
at some discrete points.

In this section and the next one, we shall propose new algorithms for detecting the model order and recovering the supports of
\mu
from the measurement (
4.2
). Our number detection method is based on thresholding on a Hankel matrix assembled by data from modifications of (
4.2
) . The following subsection shall introduce the details of the Hankel matrix formulation. We refer to
[
2
,
3
,
74
,
60
,
56
,
75
,
36
,
13
,
28
,
27
,
43
,
42
]
for other model detecting algorithms.

4.2
Hankel matrix construction

The Hankel matrix is constructed by the following three steps.

Measurement modification by source translation

Due to the translation invariance, suppose the sources are supported in
[-\lambda,\lambda]^{2}
, we consider them displacing with a vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
and get that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
. Using a simple measurement modification technique, we obtain the measurement for the new source
\tilde{\mu}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}
. Specifically, we consider

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})=

\displaystyle e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}e^{i(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})

(4.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{W}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}),\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}\in[0,1,\cdots,\Omega]^{2},

with
|\mathbf{\tilde{W}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}})|<\sigma
.

Measurement modification by coordinate-combination

The second procedure consists in modifying the measurement based on coordinate-combination. For
s>0
, let
r=\frac{\Omega}{2s}
. From the measurement
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
, we construct a list of new data given by

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(t)=\sum_{t_{1}+t_{2}=t,0\leq t_{1},t_{2}\leq t}{t\choose t_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}_{rt_{1},rt_{2}}),\quad t=0,\cdots,2s,

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}_{rt_{1},rt_{2}}=(rt_{1},rt_{2})^{\top}
. Note that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}r}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}r})^{t}+\sum_{t_{1}+t_{2}=t,0\leq t_{1},t_{2}\leq t}{t\choose t_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{W}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}_{rt_{1},rt_{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}r}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}r})^{t}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{W}}}(t),

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{W}}}(t)=\sum_{t_{1}+t_{2}=t,0\leq t_{1},t_{2}\leq t}{t\choose t_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{W}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\omega}}_{rt_{1},rt_{2}})
.

Hankel matrix construction and singular value decomposition

Finally, from these
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(t)
’s, we assemble the following Hankel matrix

\mathbf{H}(s)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{D}(0)&\mathbf{D}(1)&\cdots&\mathbf{D}(s)\\
\mathbf{D}(1)&\mathbf{D}(2)&\cdots&\mathbf{D}(s+1)\\
\cdots&\cdots&\ddots&\cdots\\
\mathbf{D}(s)&\mathbf{D}(s+1)&\cdots&\mathbf{D}(2s)\end{pmatrix}.

(4.4)

We observe that
\mathbf{H}(s)
has the decomposition

\mathbf{H}(s)=BAB^{T}+\Delta,

(4.5)

where
A=\text{diag}(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
and
B=\big(\phi_{s}(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}r}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}r}),\cdots,\phi_{s}(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}r}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}r})\big)
with
\phi_{s}(\omega)
being defined as

\phi_{s}(\omega)=(1,\omega,\cdots,\omega^{s})^{\top},

(4.6)

and

\Delta=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\hat{W}}(0)&\mathbf{\hat{W}}(1)&\cdots&\mathbf{\hat{W}}(s)\\
\mathbf{\hat{W}}(1)&\mathbf{\hat{W}}(2)&\cdots&\mathbf{\hat{W}}(s+1)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{\hat{W}}(s)&\mathbf{\hat{W}}(s+1)&\cdots&\mathbf{\hat{W}}(2s)\end{pmatrix}.

(4.7)

We denote the singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{H}(s)
as

\mathbf{H}(s)=\hat{U}\hat{\Sigma}\hat{U}^{*},

where
\hat{\Sigma}=\text{diag}(\hat{\sigma}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\sigma}_{n},\hat{\sigma}_{n+1},\cdots,\hat{\sigma}_{s+1})
with the singular values
\hat{\sigma}_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq s+1
, ordered in a decreasing manner. The source number
n
is then detected by a thresholding on these singular values. In the next subsection we will provide the theoretical guarantee of the threshold.

4.3
Theoretical guarantee

Note that when there is no noise,
\mathbf{H}(s)=BAB^{\top}
. We have the following estimate for the singular values of
BAB^{\top}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
s\geq n
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\in[-\frac{s\pi}{6\Omega},\frac{s\pi}{6\Omega}]^{2},1\leq j\leq n,
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
in (
4.3
) be
(0,\frac{s\pi}{\Omega})^{\top}
. Let

\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{n},0,\cdots,0

be the singular values of
BAB^{T}
in (
4.5
) ordered in a decreasing manner. Then the following estimate holds

\sigma_{n}\geq\frac{m_{\min}\big(3\theta_{\min}(\Omega,s)\big)^{2n-2}}{n(2(1+\sqrt{3})\pi)^{2n-2}},

(4.8)

where
\theta_{\min}(\Omega,s)=\min_{p\neq j}\Big|\Big|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{p}\frac{\Omega}{2s}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\frac{\Omega}{2s}}\Big|\Big|_{1}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\sigma_{n}
is the minimum nonzero singular value of
BAB^{\top}
. Let
\ker(B^{\top})
be the kernel space of
B^{\top}
and
\ker^{\perp}(B^{\top})
be its orthogonal complement. Then we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}=\min_{||x||_{2}=1,x\in\ker^{\perp}(B^{\top})}||BAB^{\top}x||_{2}\geq\sigma_{\min}(BA)\sigma_{n}(B^{\top})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(B)\sigma_{\min}(A)\sigma_{\min}(B).

On the other hand, since by the condition of the lemma
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\in[-\frac{s\pi}{6\Omega},\frac{s\pi}{6\Omega}]\times[\frac{5s\pi}{6\Omega},\frac{7s\pi}{6\Omega}]
, we have
\frac{\Omega\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}{2s}\in[-\frac{\pi}{12},\frac{\pi}{12}]\times[\frac{5\pi}{12},\frac{7\pi}{12}]
. Thus, by Lemma
3.2
, for
r=\frac{\Omega}{2s}
,

\min_{p\neq q}\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{p,1}r}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{p,2}r}-(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{q,1}r}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{q,2}r})}\Big|\geq\frac{3}{2\pi}\theta_{\min}(\Omega,s).

Note also that
|e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{p,1}r}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{p,2}r}|\leq\sqrt{3}
. Thus applying Lemma
6.3
and Corollary
6.2
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(B)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{\big(\frac{3}{2\pi}\theta_{\min}(\Omega,s)\big)^{n-1}}{(1+\sqrt{3})^{n-1}}.

Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}\geq\sigma_{\min}(A)\Big(\frac{\big(\frac{3}{2\pi}\theta_{\min}(\Omega,s)\big)^{n-1}}{(1+\sqrt{3})^{n-1}}\Big)^{2}\geq\frac{m_{\min}\big(3\theta_{\min}(\Omega,s)\big)^{2n-2}}{n(2(1+\sqrt{3})\pi)^{2n-2}}.

∎

We now present the main result on the threshold for the singular values of the matrix
\mathbf{H}(s)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
n\geq 2,s\geq n
and
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}
with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\in[-\frac{s\pi}{6\Omega},\frac{s\pi}{6\Omega}]^{2},1\leq j\leq n
. Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
in (
4.3
) be equal to
(0,\frac{s\pi}{\Omega})^{\top}
. Then for the singular values of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}(s)
in (
4.4
), We have

\hat{\sigma}_{j}<\frac{4^{s+1}\sigma}{3},\quad j=n+1,\cdots,s+1.

(4.9)

Moreover, if the following separation condition is satisfied

\min_{p\neq j}\Big|\Big|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{p}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}\Big|\Big|_{1}\geq\frac{4(1+\sqrt{3})\pi s}{3\Omega}\Big(\frac{2n4^{s+1}}{3}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-2}},

(4.10)

then

\hat{\sigma}_{n}\geq\frac{4^{s+1}\sigma}{3}.

(4.11)

Proof.

We first estimate
||\Delta||_{2}
for
\Delta
in (
4.7
). By the definition of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{W}}}(t)
, we have
|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{W}}}(t)<2^{t}\sigma
. Thus
||\Delta||_{2}\leq||\Delta||_{F}<\frac{4^{s+1}\sigma}{3}
. By Weyl’s theorem, we have
|\hat{\sigma}_{j}-\sigma_{j}|\leq||\Delta||_{2},j=1,\cdots,n
. Together with
\sigma_{j}=0,n+1\leq j\leq s+1
, we get
|\hat{\sigma}_{j}|\leq||\Delta||_{2}<\frac{4^{s+1}\sigma}{3},n+1\leq j\leq s+1
. This proves (
4.9
).

Let
\theta_{\min}(\Omega,s)=\frac{\Omega}{2s}\min_{p\neq q}\Big|\Big|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{p}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{q}}\Big|\Big|_{1}
. The separation condition (
4.10
) implies that

\theta_{\min}(\Omega,s)\geq\frac{2(1+\sqrt{3})\pi}{3}\Big(\frac{2n4^{s+1}}{3}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2n-2}}.

By Lemma
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}\geq\frac{m_{\min}\big(3\theta_{\min}(\Omega,s)\big)^{2n-2}}{n(2(1+\sqrt{3})\pi)^{2n-2}}>2\frac{4^{s+1}\sigma}{3}.

(4.12)

Similarly, by Weyl’s theorem,
|\hat{\sigma}_{n}-\sigma_{n}|\leq||\Delta||_{2}
. Thus,
\hat{\sigma}_{n}\geq 2(s+1)\sigma-||\Delta||_{2}\geq\frac{4^{s+1}\sigma}{3}
. The conclusion (
4.11
) then follows.
∎

Algorithm 1

Coordinate-combination-based MUSIC algorithm

After applying the MUSIC algorithm to both
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{d}(s),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{g}(s)

n

’s which is close to

\hat{g}_{j}

g_{j}=e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}}-e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}}
. The next question is how to link the pair
\hat{d}_{j},\hat{g}_{j}
that correspond to the same source. This is an inevitable pair matching issues in most of the two-dimensional DOA algorithms
[
42
]
, where ad hoc schemes
[
79
,
34
,
14

77
]
were derived to associate the estimated azimuth and elevation angles. Here, in contrast with conventional DOA algorithms, we do not need to link the azimuth and elevation angles but to link
\hat{d}_{j}
and
\hat{g}_{j}

Observe that
|d_{j}+g_{j}|=|2e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}}|=2
and
|d_{j}-g_{j}|=|2e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}}|=2
. We can use this criterion to match the pair
\hat{d}_{j},\hat{g}_{j}
that they should satisfy

|\hat{d}_{j}+\hat{g}_{j}|\approx 2,\quad|\hat{d}_{j}-\hat{g}_{j}|\approx 2.

(5.3)

For example, we could consider the following minimization problem:

\min_{\pi\in\zeta(n)}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big|{|\hat{d}_{j}+\hat{g}_{\pi_{j}}|-2}\Big|+\Big|{|\hat{d}_{j}-\hat{g}_{\pi_{j}}|-2}\Big|,

(5.4)

where
\zeta(n)
is the set of all permutations of
\{1,\cdots,n\}
. This can be viewed as a balanced assignment problem
[
52
]
, which can be solved efficiently by many algorithms such as the Hungarian algorithm.

We remark that our pair matching algorithm is not the one usually required in other one-dimensional based DOA algorithms. Unlike our case, the other pair matching problem is not an assignment problem, wherefore the pair matching is usually time consuming or complex processing is conducted to reduce the computational cost.

5.4
Superiority of the algorithm

5.4.1
Overcome the issue of separation distance loss in conventional two-dimensional DOA algorithms

Despite the fact that different recovering methods are proposed for DOA estimation in two dimensions, the conventional way for tackling the problem has hardly exceeded the scope of recovering the two direction (x- and y-direction) components of sources individually. Thus, as illustrated in Figure
5.1
, severe loss of the source separation distance in one dimension is always an inevitable issue that causes unstable recovery of the direction components. Most of the researches ignored this issue and some papers
[
73
,
72
]
proposed ad hoc schemes to enhance the reconstruction but in a complex manner.

Our method is a new one-dimensional-based algorithm where the issue of severe source separation distance loss is avoided in a simple way. In our algorithm, the separation distance between direction components of sources are still preserved. This has been demonstrated by Lemma
3.2
for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j}\in\mathbb{[}0,\pi]^{2},j=1,2
with
\frac{\pi}{3}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j,2}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{j,1}\leq\frac{2}{3}\pi,j=1,2
. Furthermore, Theorem
5.1
shows that, for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=(0,\frac{\pi}{2})^{\top}
, the separation distance between
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
’s can be preserved after the coordinate-combination. By Theorem
5.1
, if the distance between the
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
’s is a certain constant
C
, then the distance between
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}}
is larger than
\frac{2C}{\pi^{2}}
times the original distance. For better results of preservation of the distance, as indicated by Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
, we could consider sources in a smaller region with a specified translation. In the numerical experiments presented in this paper, for ease of discussion and presentation, we will consider sources in
[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]^{2}
and the translation vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=(0,\frac{\pi}{2})^{\top}
. We leave the recovering strategies of the whole region
[0,2\pi]^{2}
and other enhancement for future works.

Theorem 5.1
.

For two different vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\in\mathbb{[}0,\frac{\pi}{2}]\times[\frac{\pi}{2},\pi],j=1,2
, if
||\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2}||_{1}\geq C
for a constant
C
, then

\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}}-(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}})}\Big|\geq\frac{2C}{\pi^{2}}C.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by considering the following two cases.

Case 1:

0\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}\leq\pi
.

In this case,

\displaystyle\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}}-(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}})}\Big|\geq

\displaystyle\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}}}\Big|-\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}}}\Big|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2\Big(\cos(\frac{\phi_{2}}{2})-\cos(\frac{\phi_{1}}{2})\Big),

where
\phi_{j}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,2}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j,1},j=1,2
. By the assumption of the theorem, we have
C\leq\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}\leq\pi
and
C\leq\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}\leq 2\pi
. Thus

2\Big(\cos(\frac{\phi_{2}}{2})-\cos(\frac{\phi_{1}}{2})\Big)=4\sin(\frac{\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}}{4})\sin(\frac{\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}}{4})\geq 4\sin(\frac{C}{4})\sin(\frac{C}{4})\geq\frac{2C^{2}}{\pi^{2}}.

where the last inequality uses
\sin(\frac{C}{4})\geq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\frac{C}{4}
for
0\leq\frac{C}{4}\leq\frac{\pi}{4}
.

Case 2:

0\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}\leq\pi
.

Again, the idea is to calculate the angle between
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}}
and
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}}
. By a simple analysis of the angle relations between
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}},e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}},e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}},e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}},
we obtain that the angle between
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}}
and
e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}}
is
\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}}{2}
which is larger than
\frac{C}{2}
. Thus

\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}}-(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}})}\Big|\geq\max\Big(\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}}}\Big|,\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}}}\Big|\Big)\sin(\frac{C}{2}).

(5.5)

We next claim that

\max\Big(\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}}}\Big|,\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}}}\Big|\Big)\geq 2\cos\Big(\frac{\pi-C/2}{2}\Big).

Otherwise,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}>\pi-\frac{C}{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}>\pi-\frac{C}{2}
, which is impossible when
||\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2}||_{1}\geq C
. Thus the claim is proved. Together with (
5.5
), we arrive at

\Big|{e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1,2}}-(e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,1}}+e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2,2}})}\Big|\geq 2\sin(\frac{C}{4})\sin(\frac{C}{2})\geq\frac{2C^{2}}{\pi^{2}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

5.4.2
Phase transition and performance of Algorithm
4

Most of the conventional two-dimensional DOA algorithms consider multiple snapshots of measurements from coherent or incoherent signals. Also, the noise is usually assumed to be white Gaussian noise such that the expectation of the covariance matrix of the measurement vector is a sum of two terms, where the first term is from the correlation of the signals and the second one is the noise correlation matrix. Based on this crucial observation, many algorithms were derived to tackle the problem. Differently to the above model, we consider recovering the source from a single measurement with deterministic noise. Thus we do not compare the performance of our algorithm with those algorithms with statistical model. We demonstrate the super-resolution capacity of our algorithm for the single snapshot case by showing the phase transition of the algorithm. We will derive a coordinate-combination-based MUSIC algorithm for multiple snapshots case in a forthcoming work.

We now describe the numerical experiments for demonstrating the phase transition phenomenon of our algorithm in terms of the SNR versus the super-resolution factor. We fix
\Omega=10
and consider three and four sources separated by the minimum separation distance
D_{\min}
, i.e.,
\min_{p\neq q}||\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{p}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{q}||_{1}\geq D_{\min}

6
A nonlinear approximation theory in Vandermonde space

In this section, we introduce the main technique, a nonlinear approximation theory in Vandermonde space
[
44
,
43
]
,
that is used to deal with one-dimensional super-resolution problems. In
[
44
]
, we have derived the theory for real numbers and in
[
43
]
for complex numbers on the unit circle. Here, we derive a different theory for arbitrary bounded complex numbers, which are related to the proofs of the main results of the paper.

For a given positive integer
s
and
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
, we denote by

\phi_{s}(\omega)=(1,\omega,\cdots,\omega^{s})^{\top}

(6.1)

and call
\phi_{s}
a Vandermonde vector. At the heart of the theory is the following nonlinear approximation problem in the Vandermonde space

\min_{\hat{a}_{j},\hat{d}_{j}\in\mathbb{R},|\hat{d}_{j}|\leq d,j=1,\cdots,k}\Big|\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{k}\hat{a}_{j}\phi_{s}(\hat{d}_{j})-v\Big|\Big|_{2},

(6.2)

where
v=\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}a_{j}\phi_{s}(d_{j})
is a given vector.
We shall derive a sharp lower-bound for this problem. In addition, we shall also investigate the stability of the approximation problem (
6.2
) for
v=\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}\phi_{s}(d_{j})
.

6.1
Notation and Preliminaries

We introduce some notation and preliminaries. We denote the Vandermonde matrix by

\displaystyle V_{s}(k)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\cdots&1\\
d_{1}&\cdots&d_{k}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
d_{1}^{s}&\cdots&d_{k}^{s}\end{pmatrix}=\Big(\phi_{s}(d_{1})\ \ \phi_{s}(d_{2})\ \ \cdots\ \ \phi_{s}(d_{k})\Big).

(6.3)

For a real matrix or a vector
A
, we denote by
A^{\top}
its transpose and by
A^{*}
its conjugate transpose.

We first present some basic properties of Vandermonde matrices.

Lemma 6.1
.

For
k
distinct complex numbers
d_{j}
’s, we have

||V_{k-1}(k)^{-1}||_{\infty}\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\Pi_{1\leq p\leq k,p\neq i}\frac{1+|d_{p}|}{|d_{i}-d_{p}|},

where
V_{k-1}(k)
is the Vandermonde matrix
V_{k-1}(k)
defined as in (
6.3
).

Proof.

See Theorem 1 in
[
26
]
.∎

As a consequence, we directly have the following corollary.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
d_{\min}=\min_{i\neq j}|d_{i}-d_{j}|
and assume that
\max_{i=1,\cdots,k}|d_{i}|\leq d
. Then

||V_{k-1}(k)^{-1}||_{\infty}\leq\frac{(1+d)^{k-1}}{(d_{\min})^{k-1}}.

Lemma 6.3
.

For distinct
d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
, define the Vandermonde matrices
V_{k-1}(k),V_{s}(k)
as in (
6.3
) with
s\geq k-1
. Then the following estimate on their singular values holds:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\frac{1}{||V_{k-1}(k)^{-1}||_{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{||V_{k-1}(k)^{-1}||_{2}}\leq\sigma_{\min}(V_{k-1}(k))\leq\sigma_{\min}(V_{s}(k)).

Proof.

The result holds by using properties of matrix norms.
∎

Denote by

S_{1k}^{j}:=\Big\{\{\tau_{1},\cdots,\tau_{j}\}:\text{$\tau_{p}\in\{1,\cdots,k\},p=1,\cdots,j$ and $\tau_{p}\neq\tau_{q}$, for $p\neq q$}\Big\}.

Note that there is no order in
\{\tau_{1},\cdots,\tau_{j}\}
, i.e.,
\{1,2\}
and
\{2,1\}
are the same sets. We then have the following decomposition of the Vandermonde matrix.

Proposition 6.4
.

The Vandermonde matrix
V_{k}(k)
defined as in (
6.3
) can be reduced to the following form by using elementary column-addition operations, i.e.,

\displaystyle V_{k}(k)G(1)\cdots G(k-1)DQ(1)\cdots Q(k-1)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\\
v_{(k+1)1}&v_{(k+1)2}&\cdots&v_{(k+1)k}\end{pmatrix},

(6.4)

where
G(1),\cdots,G(k-1),Q(1),\cdots,Q(k-1)
are elementary column-addition matrices,

D=\text{diag}(1,\frac{1}{(d_{2}-d_{1})},\cdots,\frac{1}{\Pi_{p=1}^{k-1}(d_{k}-d_{p})})

and

v_{(k+1)j}=(-1)^{k-j}\sum_{\{\tau_{1},\cdots,\tau_{k+1-j}\}\in S_{1k}^{k+1-j}}d_{\tau_{1}}\cdots d_{\tau_{k+1-j}}.

(6.5)

Proof.

See Appendix B in
[
44
]
.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

For an
s\times k
complex matrix
A
of rank
k
with
s>k
, let
V
be the space spanned by columns of
A
and
V^{\perp}
be the orthogonal complement of
V
. Denote by
P_{V^{\perp}}
the orthogonal projection to
V^{\perp}
, and set
D=(A,v)
. We have

\min_{a\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}||Aa-v||_{2}=||P_{V^{\perp}}(v)||_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{\det(D^{*}D)}{\det(A^{*}A)}}.

Proof.

See Lemma 1 in
[
43
]
.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

We have

\sqrt{\frac{\det(V_{k}(k)^{*}V_{k}(k))}{\det(V_{k-1}(k)^{*}V_{k-1}(k))}}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{k}|v_{j}|^{2}},

(6.6)

where
V_{s}(k)
is defined as in (
6.3
) and
v_{j}=\sum_{\{\tau_{1},\cdots,\tau_{j}\}\in S_{1k}^{j}}d_{\tau_{1}}\cdots d_{\tau_{j}}
. Especially, if
|d_{j}|<d,j=1,\cdots,k
, then

\sqrt{\frac{\det(V_{k}(k)^{*}V_{k}(k))}{\det(V_{k-1}(k)^{*}V_{k-1}(k))}}\leq(1+d)^{k}.

(6.7)

Proof.

Note that in Proposition
6.4
,
all the elementary column-addition matrices have unit determinant. As a result,

\det(V_{k}(k)^{*}V_{k}(k))=\frac{\det(F^{*}F)}{\det(D^{*}D)}
, where
F
is the matrix in the right-hand side of (
6.4
), and
D
is the diagonal matrix in Proposition
6.4
. A direct calculation shows that
\det(F^{*}F)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}|v_{j}|^{2}
, where we use (
6.5
). On the other hand,
V_{k-1}(k)
is a standard Vandermonde matrix and we have
\det(V_{k-1}(k)^{*}V_{k-1}(k))=\frac{1}{\det(D^{*}D)}
. Combining these results,
(
6.6
) follows. The last statement can be derived from (
6.6
) and the estimate that

\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{k}|v_{j}|^{2}}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k}|v_{j}|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k}\begin{pmatrix}k\\
j\end{pmatrix}d^{j}=(1+d)^{k}.

∎

For reader’s convenience, we finally present two auxiliary lemmas. For positive integers
p,q

\eta_{p,q}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{p},\hat{z}_{1},\cdots,\hat{z}_{q})=\begin{pmatrix}|z_{1}-\hat{z}_{1}|\cdots|z_{1}-\hat{z}_{q}|\\
|z_{2}-\hat{z}_{1}|\cdots|z_{2}-\hat{z}_{q}|\\
\vdots\\
|z_{p}-\hat{z}_{1}|\cdots|z_{p}-\hat{z}_{q}|\end{pmatrix}.

(6.8)

The following two properties of
\eta_{p,q}
hold.

Lemma 6.7
.

For complex numbers
d_{j},\hat{d}_{j}
’s, we have the following estimate

\Big|\Big|\eta_{k+1,k}(d_{1},\cdots,d_{k+1},\hat{d}_{1},\cdots,\hat{d}_{k})\Big|\Big|_{\infty}\geq(\frac{d_{\min}}{2})^{k},

where
d_{\min}=\min_{j\neq p}|d_{j}-d_{p}|
and
\eta_{k+1,k}(d_{1},\cdots,d_{k+1},\hat{d}_{1},\cdots,\hat{d}_{k})

).

Because we have
k+1

d_{j}
’s and only
k

\hat{d}_{j}
’s, there must exist one

so that

|d_{j_{0}}-\hat{d}_{j}|\geq\frac{d_{\min}}{2},\quad j=1,\cdots,k.

Then the estimate in the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
d_{j},\hat{d}_{j}\in\mathbb{C},j=1,\cdots,k
satisfy
|d_{j}|,|\hat{d}_{j}|\leq d
. Assume that

||\eta_{k,k}(d_{1},\cdots,d_{k},\hat{d}_{1},\cdots,\hat{d}_{k})||_{\infty}<\epsilon,

(6.9)

where
\eta_{k,k}(\cdots)
is defined as in (
6.8
), and that

(6.10)

Then after reordering

’s, we have

\Big|{\hat{d}_{j}-d_{j}}\Big|<\frac{d_{\min}}{2},\quad j=1,\cdots,k,

(6.11)

and moreover

\Big|{\hat{d}_{j}-d_{j}}\Big|\leq\Big(\frac{2}{d_{\min}}\Big)^{k-1}\epsilon,\quad j=1,\cdots,k.

(6.12)

Proof.

.

6.2
Lower-bound for the approximation problem (
6.2
)

In this section, we derive a lower-bound for the nonlinear approximation problem (
6.2
). We first consider the special case when
v
is a Vandermonde vector.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
\hat{d}_{1},\cdots,\hat{d}_{k}
be
k
distinct complex numbers with
|\hat{d}_{j}|\leq\hat{d},1\leq j\leq k
. Define
A:=\big(\phi_{k}(\hat{d}_{1}),\cdots,\phi_{k}(\hat{d}_{k})\big)
, where
\phi_{k}(\hat{d}_{j})
’s are defined as in (
6.1
). Let
V
be the
k
-dimensional space spanned by the column vectors of
A
, and let
V^{\perp}
be the one-dimensional orthogonal complement of
V
in
\mathbb{C}^{k+1}
. Let
P_{V^{\perp}}
be the orthogonal projection onto
V^{\perp}
in
\mathbb{C}^{k+1}
. Then we have

\min_{a\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}\Big|\Big|{Aa-\phi_{k}(x)}\Big|\Big|_{2}=\Big|\Big|{P_{V^{\perp}}(\phi_{k}(x))}\Big|\Big|_{2}=\Big|{v^{*}\phi_{k}(x)}\Big|\geq\frac{1}{(1+\hat{d})^{k}}\Big|{\Pi_{j=1}^{k}(x-\hat{d}_{j})}\Big|,

where
v
is a unit vector in
V^{\perp}
and
v^{*}
is its conjugate transpose.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.5
, it follows that

\min_{a\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}\Big|\Big|{Aa-\phi_{k}(x)}\Big|\Big|_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{\text{det}(D^{*}D)}{\text{det}(A^{*}A)}},

where
D=\big(\phi_{k}(\hat{d}_{1}),\cdots,\phi_{k}(\hat{d}_{k}),\phi_{k}(x)\big)
. Denote
\tilde{A}=\big(\phi_{k-1}(\hat{d}_{1}),\cdots,\phi_{k-1}(\hat{d}_{k})\big)
. By (
6.7
), we have

\sqrt{\frac{\text{det}(A^{*}A)}{\text{det}(\tilde{A}^{*}\tilde{A})}}\leq(1+\hat{d})^{k}.

Therefore,

\min_{a\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}\Big|\Big|{Aa-\phi_{k}(x)}\Big|\Big|_{2}\geq\frac{1}{(1+\hat{d})^{k}}\sqrt{\frac{\text{det}(D^{*}D)}{\text{det}(\tilde{A}^{*}\tilde{A})}}.

Note that
D
and
\tilde{A}
are square Vandermonde matrices. We can use the determinant formula to derive that

\min_{a\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}\Big|\Big|{Aa-\phi_{k}(x)}\Big|\Big|_{2}\geq\frac{1}{(1+\hat{d})^{k}}\frac{|\Pi_{1\leq t<p\leq k}(\hat{d}_{t}-\hat{d}_{p})\Pi_{q=1}^{k}(x-\hat{d}_{q})|}{|\Pi_{1\leq t<p\leq k}(\hat{d}_{t}-\hat{d}_{p})|}=\frac{1}{(1+\hat{d})^{k}}|\Pi_{j=1}^{k}(x-\hat{d}_{j})|.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

We now consider the approximation problem (
6.2
)
for the general case when
v
is a linear combination of Vandermonde vectors.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
k\geq 1
. Assume
(k+1)
different complex numbers
d_{j}\in\mathbb{C},j=1,\cdots,k+1
with
|d_{j}|\leq d
and
(k+1)

a_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
with
|a_{j}|\geq m_{\min}
. Let
d_{\min}:=\min_{j\neq p}|d_{j}-d_{p}|
. For
q\leq k
, let
\hat{a}(q)=(\hat{a}_{1},\hat{a}_{2},\cdots,\hat{a}_{q})^{\top},a=(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k+1})^{\top}
, and

\hat{A}(q)=\big(\phi_{2k}(\hat{d}_{1}),\ \cdots,\ \phi_{2k}(\hat{d}_{q})\big),\ A=\big(\phi_{2k}(d_{1}),\ \cdots,\ \phi_{2k}(d_{k+1})\big),

where
\phi_{2k}(z)
is defined as in (
6.1
). Then

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{a}_{p},\hat{d}_{p}\in\mathbb{C},|\hat{d}_{p}|\leq\hat{d},p=1,\cdots,q}||\hat{A}(q)\hat{a}(q)-Aa||_{2}\geq\frac{m_{\min}(d_{\min})^{2k}}{2^{k}(1+d)^{k}(1+\hat{d})^{k}}.

Proof.

Step 1
. Note that for
q<k
, we have

\min_{\hat{a}_{p},\hat{d}_{p}\in\mathbb{C},|\hat{d}_{p}|\leq d,p=1,\cdots,q}||\hat{A}(q)\hat{a}(q)-Aa||_{2}\geq\min_{\hat{a}_{p},\hat{d}_{p}\in\mathbb{C},|\hat{d}_{p}|\leq d,p=1,\cdots,k}||\hat{A}(k)\hat{a}(k)-Aa||_{2}.

Hence we need only to consider the case when
q=k
. It then suffices to show that for any given
\hat{d}_{j}\in\mathbb{C},|\hat{d}_{j}|\leq\hat{d},j=1,\cdots,k
, the following holds

\min_{\hat{a}_{p}\in\mathbb{C},p=1,\cdots,k}||\hat{A}(k)\hat{a}(k)-Aa||_{2}\geq\frac{m_{\min}(d_{\min})^{2k}}{2^{k}(1+d)^{k}(1+\hat{d})^{k}}.

(6.13)

So we fix
\hat{d}_{1},\cdots,\hat{d}_{k}
in our subsequent argument.

Step 2
. For
l=0,\cdots,k
, we define the following partial matrices

\hat{A}_{l}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\hat{d}_{1}^{l}&\cdots&\hat{d}_{k}^{l}\\
\hat{d}_{1}^{l+1}&\cdots&\hat{d}_{k}^{l+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\hat{d}_{1}^{l+k}&\cdots&\hat{d}_{k}^{l+k}\end{array}\right),\quad A_{l}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}(d_{1})^{l}&\cdots&(d_{k+1})^{l}\\
(d_{1})^{l+1}&\cdots&(d_{k+1})^{l+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
(d_{1})^{l+k}&\cdots&(d_{k+1})^{l+k}\end{array}\right).

It is clear that for all
l
,

\min_{\hat{a}(k)\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}||\hat{A}(k)\hat{a}(k)-Aa||_{2}\geq\min_{\hat{a}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}||\hat{A}_{l}\hat{a}-A_{l}a||_{2}.

(6.14)

Step 3
. For each
l
, observe that
\hat{A}_{l}=\hat{A}_{0}\text{diag}(\hat{d}_{1}^{l},\ \cdots,\ \hat{d}_{k}^{l}),A_{l}=A_{0}\text{diag}(d_{1}^{l},\cdots,d_{k+1}^{l})
, and thus

\min_{\hat{a}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}||\hat{A}_{l}\hat{a}-A_{l}a||_{2}\geq\min_{\hat{\alpha}_{l}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}||A_{0}\hat{\alpha}_{l}-A_{0}\alpha_{l}||_{2},

(6.15)

where
\alpha_{l}=\left(a_{1}(d_{1})^{l},\cdots,a_{k+1}(d_{k+1})^{l}\right)^{\top}
. Let
V
be the space spanned by the column vectors of
A_{0}
. Then the dimension of
V
is
k
, and the dimension of
V^{\perp}
, the orthogonal complement of
V
in
\mathbb{C}^{k+1}
, is one. Let

P_{V^{\perp}}
be the orthogonal projection onto
V^{\perp}
. Note that
||P_{V^{\perp}}u||_{2}=|v^{*}u|
for
u\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
, where
v
is a unit vector in
V^{\perp}
and
v^{*}
is its conjugate transpose. We have

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\alpha}_{l}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}||\hat{A}_{0}\hat{\alpha}_{l}-A_{0}\alpha_{l}||_{2}=||P_{V^{\perp}}(A_{0}\alpha_{l})||_{2}=|v^{*}A_{0}\alpha_{l}|=\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}a_{j}(d_{j})^{l}v^{*}\phi_{k}(d_{j})\Big|=|\beta_{l}|,

(6.16)

where

\beta_{l}=\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}a_{j}(d_{j})^{l}v^{*}\phi_{k}(d_{j}),\quad\text{for $l=0,1,\cdots,k.$}

Step 4
. Denote
\beta=(\beta_{0},\cdots,\beta_{k})^{\top}
. We have
B\hat{\eta}=\beta
, where

B=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{k+1}\\
a_{1}d_{1}&a_{2}d_{2}&\cdots&a_{k+1}d_{k+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{1}(d_{1})^{k}&a_{2}(d_{2})^{k}&\cdots&a_{k+1}(d_{k+1})^{k}\end{array}\right),\quad\hat{\eta}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v^{*}\phi_{k}(d_{1})\\
v^{*}\phi_{k}(d_{2})\\
\vdots\\
v^{*}\phi_{k}(d_{k+1})\end{array}\right).

Corollary
6.2
yields

\displaystyle||\hat{\eta}||_{\infty}=||B^{-1}\beta||_{\infty}\leq||B^{-1}||_{\infty}||\beta||_{\infty}\leq\frac{(1+d)^{k}}{m_{\min}(d_{\min})^{k}}||\beta||_{\infty}.

On the other hand, applying Theorem
6.1
to each term
|v^{*}\phi_{k}(d_{j})|
,
j=1,2,\cdots k+1
, we have

||\hat{\eta}||_{\infty}\geq\frac{1}{(1+\hat{d})^{k}}||\eta_{k+1,k}(d_{1},\cdots,d_{k+1},\hat{d}_{1},\cdots,\hat{d}_{k})||_{\infty},

where
\eta_{k+1,k}(\cdots)
is defined as in (
6.8
). Combining this inequality with Lemma
6.7
, we get

||\hat{\eta}||_{\infty}\geq\frac{(d_{\min})^{k}}{2^{k}(1+\hat{d})^{k}}.

Then it follows that

||\beta||_{\infty}\geq\frac{m_{\min}(d_{\min})^{2k}}{2^{k}(1+d)^{k}(1+\hat{d})^{k}}.

Therefore, recalling (
6.14
)–(
6.16
), we arrive at

\min_{\hat{a}(k)\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}||\hat{A}(k)\hat{a}(k)-Aa||_{2}\geq\max_{0\leq l\leq k}\min_{\hat{a}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}||\hat{A}_{l}\hat{a}-A_{l}a||_{2}=\max_{0\leq l\leq k}|\beta_{l}|=||\beta||_{\infty}\geq\frac{m_{\min}(d_{\min})^{2k}}{2^{k}(1+d)^{k}(1+\hat{d})^{k}}.

This proves (
6.13
) and hence the theorem.
∎

6.3
Stability of the approximation problem (
6.2
)

In the section we present a stability result for the approximation problem (
6.2
).

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
k\geq 1
. Assume
k
different complex numbers
d_{j}\in\mathbb{C},j=1,\cdots,k
with
|d_{j}|\leq d
and
k

a_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
with
|a_{j}|\geq m_{\min}
. Let
d_{\min}:=\min_{p\neq q}|d_{p}-d_{q}|
. Assume that
\hat{d}_{j}\in\mathbb{C},j=1,\cdots,k
with
|\hat{d}_{j}|\leq d
satisfy

||\hat{A}\hat{a}-Aa||_{2}<\sigma,

where
\hat{a}=(\hat{a}_{1},\cdots,\hat{a}_{k})^{\top}
,
a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{k})^{\top}
, and

\hat{A}=\big(\phi_{2k-1}(\hat{d}_{1}),\ \cdots,\ \phi_{2k-1}(\hat{d}_{k})\big),\ A=\big(\phi_{2k-1}(d_{1}),\ \cdots,\ \phi_{2k-1}(d_{k})\big).

Then

\Big|\Big|\eta_{k,k}(d_{1},\cdots,d_{k},\hat{d}_{1},\cdots,\hat{d}_{k})\Big|\Big|_{\infty}<\frac{(1+d)^{2k-1}}{d_{\min}^{k-1}}\frac{\sigma}{m_{\min}}.

Proof.

Since
||\hat{A}\hat{a}-Aa||_{2}<\sigma
, we have

\min_{\hat{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}||\hat{A}\hat{\alpha}-Aa||_{2}<\sigma,

and hence

\max_{0\leq l\leq k-1}\min_{\hat{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}||\hat{A}_{l}\hat{\alpha}-A_{l}a||_{2}\leq\min_{\hat{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}||\hat{A}\hat{\alpha}-Aa||_{2}<\sigma,

(6.17)

where

\hat{A}_{l}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\hat{d}_{1}^{l}&\cdots&\hat{d}_{k}^{l}\\
\hat{d}_{1}^{l+1}&\cdots&\hat{d}_{k}^{l+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\hat{d}_{1}^{l+k}&\cdots&\hat{d}_{k}^{l+k}\end{array}\right),\quad A_{l}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}d_{1}^{l}&\cdots&d_{k}^{l}\\
d_{1}^{l+1}&\cdots&d_{k}^{l+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
d_{1}^{l+k}&\cdots&d_{k}^{l+k}\end{array}\right).

For each
l
, from the decomposition

(6.18)

where

V^{\perp}

7
Conclusions and future works

In this paper, we have improved the estimates of resolution limits in two-dimensional super-resolution problems. We also theoretically demonstrate the optimal performance of a sparsity-promoting algorithm. Leveraging the new techniques in the proof, we have proposed a coordinate-combination-based model order detection algorithm and a coordinate-combination-based MUSIC algorithm for DOA estimation in two dimensions. The superiority of the introduced algorithms were demonstrated both theoretically or numerically.

Our work is also a start of many new topics. Firstly, one could extend the techniques to three- and
k
-dimensional spaces to improve the resolution estimates in higher dimensional super-resolution problems. Secondly, the idea of coordinate-combination could inspire new algorithms for two-dimensional DOA estimations in the case of multiple snapshots. These works will be presented in a near future.
