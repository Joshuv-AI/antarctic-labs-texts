A query-optimal algorithm for finding counterfactuals
Guy Blanc, Caleb Koch, Jane Lange, Li-Yang Tan

Abstract
We design an algorithm for finding counterfactuals with strong theoretical guarantees on its performance. For any monotone model f : X d → { 0 , 1 } f:X^{d}\to\{0,1\} and instance x ⋆ x^{\star} , our algorithm makes S ​ ( f ) O ⁡ ( Δ f ​ ( x ⋆ ) ) ⋅ log ⁡ d {{S(f)^{O(\Delta_{f}(x^{\star}))}\cdot\log d}} queries to f f and returns an optimal counterfactual for x ⋆ x^{\star} : a nearest instance x ′ x^{\prime} to x ⋆ x^{\star} for which f ⁡ ( x ′ ) ≠ f ⁡ ( x ⋆ ) f(x^{\prime})\neq f(x^{\star}) . Here S ⁡ ( f ) S(f) is the sensitivity of f f , a discrete analogue of the Lipschitz constant, and Δ f ​ ( x ⋆ ) \Delta_{f}(x^{\star}) is the distance from x ⋆ x^{\star} to its nearest counterfactuals. The previous best known query complexity was d O ⁡ ( Δ f ​ ( x ⋆ ) ) d^{\,O(\Delta_{f}(x^{\star}))} , achievable by brute-force local search. We further prove a lower bound of S ​ ( f ) Ω ⁡ ( Δ f ​ ( x ⋆ ) ) + Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ d ) S(f)^{\Omega(\Delta_{f}(x^{\star}))}+\Omega(\log d) on the query complexity of any algorithm, thereby showing that the guarantees of our algorithm are essentially optimal.



1
Introduction

Counterfactual reasoning is at the very heart of causal inference
[
Pea09a
,
Pea09b
,
MW15
]
. Counterfactuals are answers to “what would have happened if” questions: if a person has been denied a loan, would their application have been approved if their annual income were $
20
k higher? In explainable ML, there is a growing interest in the use of
counterfactual explanations

[
WMR17
]
to understand the predictions of black box models: given a model
f
and an instance
x^{\star}
, we would like to efficiently determine how a few features of
x^{\star}
can be changed to obtain a similar instance
x^{\prime}
for which
f(x^{\prime})\neq f(x^{\star})
.

It is natural to seek
sparse
counterfactuals, meaning that
x^{\prime}
differs from
x^{\star}
in as few features as possible. Ideally, we would like counterfactuals that are
optimally
sparse:

Definition 1

(Sparsity and optimality)
.

For a model
f:X^{d}\to\{0,1\}
and two instances
x^{\star},x^{\prime}
such that
f(x^{\star})\neq f(x^{\prime})
, the

of
x^{\prime}
as a counterfactual for
x^{\star}
is the number of features on which they differ: the quantity
|\Delta(x^{\star},x^{\prime})|
, where

\Delta(x^{\star},x^{\prime})\coloneqq\{i\in[d]\colon x^{\star}_{i}\neq x^{\prime}_{i}\}.

The
counterfactual complexity
of
x^{\star}
with respect to
f
is the quantity:

\Delta_{f}(x^{\star})\coloneqq\min_{x^{\prime}\in X^{d}}\{{{|\Delta(x^{\star},x^{\prime})|}}\colon f(x^{\star})\neq f(x^{\prime})\},

and we say that
x^{\prime}
is an
optimal
counterfactual for
x^{\star}
if
{{|\Delta(x^{\star},x^{\prime})|}}=\Delta_{f}(x^{\star})
.

Another desideratum that has received significant attention, motivated by the need for actionable recourse
[
USL19
]
, is that of
diversity
in counterfactual explanations
[
WMR17
,
Rus19
,
MST20
,
KBBV20
]
: a wide range of counterfactuals instead of just a single one.

Next, we consider the setting of general feature spaces. In this setting it is infeasible to return

Theorems

1
and
2
give the first algorithms with query complexity that evades the curse of dimensionality, and indeed, strongly so. We contrast our query complexity to that of ball search, a simple and natural algorithm for finding counterfactuals: first query

and all instances that differ from

by a single feature. (By the monotonicity of

f
on the instances that differ by two features, and so on, until a counterfactual is found. This algorithm has query complexity

single

1.2
Overview of our algorithm and techniques

Theorems

1
and
2
circumvent the lower bound of
Theorem

4
via a novel approach that is
not
based on local search. The crux of our approach is a new algorithm for understanding monotone black box models that we believe will see further utility beyond counterfactual explanations: using only queries to a monotone black box model
f
, this algorithm allows us to navigate a decision tree representation
T
of
f
without actually building
T
in full. Crucially, since we strive to handle arbitrarily complex models
f
, this tree
T
may be too large to build efficiently in its entirety.

With this algorithm, given any instance
x^{\star}
, we will build only the “necessary part” of
T
to find all optimal counterfactuals for
x^{\star}
. We show that just a tiny portion of
T
suffices for this purpose: it suffices to build only the root-to-leaf path
\rho
in
T
that
x^{\star}
follows and the paths that are “close to”
\rho
in
T
in a sense that we will make precise (see the beginning of
Section

3
for more details on this notion of “ path distance”). Writing
h
to denote the depth of
T
, we prove that we only have to construct
h^{O(\Delta_{f}(x^{\star}))}
many nodes in
T
, which is only an exponentially small part of
T
. Next, by bringing together classical
[
Nis89
]
and recently-develop results
[
BKLT22
]
about the structure of monotone functions, we are able to bound the depth of
T
and in terms of
f
’s sensitivity.

To summarize, our overall approach allows us to enjoy the benefits of having access to a highly interpretable representation of a black box model
f
—a decision tree representation—without paying the price associated with
T
being intractably large for most complex models of interest.

1.3
Related work

Our work is theoretical in nature: we give an algorithm with strong bounds on its query complexity and all the counterfactuals that it returns are guaranteed to be optimal. We view our main contribution as proving the curse of dimensionality can be strongly evaded for a broad and natural class of models—all monotone models.

There is a substantial body of empirical work on counterfactual explanations. The algorithms in these works either do not guarantee optimal counterfactuals or do not come with a formal analysis of their query complexity. Many of these works rely on classic optimization tools: the algorithms of
[
WMR17
,
MST20
]
are based on gradient descent and hence are restricted to differentiable models, whereas the algorithms of
[
Rus19
,
USL19
]
use mixed integer programming and are limited to linear models.
[
KBBV20
]
encode the problem of finding optimal counterfactuals as a satisfiability problem, which they then solve using SMT solvers.

The importance of diversity in counterfactual explanations has been highlighted in several works
[
WMR17
,
Rus19
,
MST20
,
KBBV20
]
. Significant motivation comes from the need for actionable recourse
[
USL19
]
: a large set of counterfactuals is more likely to contain one that is actionable (e.g. in the context of a loan denial, an applicant can plausibly work towards earning a higher income but cannot change their history of defaults).

Regarding our techniques, they are in the spirit of a recent line of theoretical work on
implicit
learning algorithms: algorithms that are able to access their hypotheses efficiently without constructing them in full
[
KV18
,
BH18
,
KVB20
,
BBG20
,
BLT21
]
. In particular, Blanc, Lange, and Tan
[
BLT21
]
show how, given queries to a model
f
, one can efficiently navigate an implicit decision tree hypothesis
T
that is
\varepsilon
-close to
f
under the uniform distribution. The presence of errors in the hypothesis, and the fact that these errors are measured with respect to the uniform distribution, are significant limitations of their result. Our work shows how these limitations can be overcome, in a strong sense, in the case of monotone models
f
: our implicit decision tree hypothesis
T
computes
f

exactly
, which is crucial for our application to finding counterfactuals. Another limitation of
[
BLT21
]
’s analysis is that it only applies to the setting of binary features; we do not need this assumption.

Finally, we mention that counterfactual explanations are part of a broader landscape of local explanations
[
SK10
,
BSH+10
,
SVZ14
,
RSG16
,
KL17
,
LL17
,
RSG18
]
, which seek to explain a model’s prediction for specific inputs. Global explanations, on the other hand, approximate the entire model with a simple and interpretable one
[
CS95
,
BS96
,
VAB07
,
ZH16
,
VLJ+17
,
BKB17
,
VS20
,
LKCL19
,
LAB20
]
.

1.4
Preliminaries

We rely on a few standard notions from the study of Boolean functions.

Definition 2

(Sensitivity)
.

For a function
f:X^{d}\to\{0,1\}
and an instance
x\in X^{d}
, the
sensitivity of
f
at
x
is the quantity

S_{f}(x)=|\{i\in[d]\colon\,\exists\,a\in X\ \text{s.t.}\ f(x)\neq f(x_{i\leftarrow a})\}|,

where
i\leftarrow a
denotes
x
with its
i
-th feature set to
a
. That is,
S_{f}(x)
is the number of features
i
of
x
for which there is a way of changing
x_{i}
that flips
f
’s value on
x
.

The
sensitivity of
f
is the quantity
\displaystyle S(f)=\max_{x\in X^{d}}\{S_{f}(x)\}.

The sensitivity of a function can be viewed as a discrete analogue of the Lipschitz constant, with low-sensitivity discrete functions being considered smooth. To see this analogy, we observe that for all
x\in X^{d}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\mathop{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\in X^{d}}}_{\Delta(x,\bm{y})=\delta d}\big[|f(x)-f(\bm{y})|\big]\leq\delta\cdot S(f).

See
[
GNS+16
]
for more on this perspective. Introduced by Cook, Dwork, and Reischuk
[
CDR86
]
, the sensitivity of discrete functions is the subject of intensive study in theoretical computer science; it is, for example, the key notion in the recent breakthrough
Sensitivity Theorem
of Huang
[
Hua19
]
(see also
[
Knu19
]
), resolving a longstanding conjecture of Nisan and Szegedy
[
NS94
]
.

Monotonicity.

For a model
f:X^{d}\to\{0,1\}
, we assume an ordering
\leq
on the elements of
X
which we lift coordinatewise to a partial ordering on
X^{d}
. That is, for
x,y\in X^{d}
,
x\leq y
if and only if
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all
i\in[d]
. We say
f
is
monotone
if it is monotone with respect to such an ordering. We’ll also assume that
X
has a maximum and minimum element.

2
Implicit decision trees for monotone models

Our algorithm for finding counterfactuals will rely on having access to a decision tree
T
which exactly represents the model
f:\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
. At a high level, for an instance
x^{\star}
, the algorithm follows a unique root-to-leaf path
\rho
in
T
searches all root-to-leaf paths in
T
that are “close to”
\rho
in a sense that make precise in the next section. In general, the size of
T
will be exponentially larger than the search space of our counterfactual finding algorithm, and hence it is advantageous to avoid computing the entire tree. Instead, we maintain an
implicit
copy of the tree
T
. This copy allows us to compute only the parts of the tree that we need to find optimal counterfactuals for
x^{\star}
.

Restrictions.

An implicit decision tree
T
, a notion introduced in
[
BLT21
]
, is an algorithm which given query access to
f
can navigate
T
without building it in full. A node in
T
is specified by a
restriction
which is a partial function
\rho:[d]\rightharpoonup\{0,1\}
indicating which (if any) features are fixed to specific values. These features and their values correspond to those queried along a path in the tree. We write
\text{Dom}(\rho)\subseteq[d]
to denote the domain of the restriction. The size of a restriction
|\rho|
is
|\text{Dom}(\rho)|
, the number of values on which it is defined. We write
f_{\rho}:\{0,1\}^{d-|\text{Dom}(\rho)|}\to\{0,1\}
to denote the restriction of
f
to
\rho
. That is,
f_{\rho}(x)
is
f
evaluated on the instance
x^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
which is formed by inserting features specified by
\rho
into the instance
x
. For example,
\rho=\{1\mapsto 1,4\mapsto 1\}
is the restriction where the
1
st and
4
th features are fixed to
1
. If
f:\{0,1\}^{4}\to\{0,1\}
, then
f_{\rho}:\{0,1\}^{2}\to\{0,1\}
and e.g.
f_{\rho}(00)=f(1001)
under this restriction.

Definition 3

(Implicit decision tree)
.

An implicit decision tree (IDT)
T
for
f:\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
is an algorithm which has query access to
f
and supports the following operations. For a restriction
\rho:[d]\rightharpoonup\{0,1\}
corresponding to features queried along a root-to-
\rho
path in
T
and auxiliary information
A\subseteq[d]
:

1.

IsLeaf
{}_{f}(\rho,A)
indicates whether
\rho
is a leaf in
T
.

2.

Query
{}_{f}(\rho,A)
, for a non-leaf
\rho
, returns
i\in[d]
corresponding to the index queried at
\rho
in
T
and
A^{\prime}\subseteq[d]
, updated auxiliary information.

If each operation uses at most
q

f
with high probability then the algorithm is a
q
-query implicit decision tree for
f
.

The role of auxiliary information.

Our algorithm for finding counterfactuals updates the auxiliary information after each query and we assume that the auxiliary information being passed to the algorithm originates from the previous query (the auxiliary information at the root can be arbitrary). Internally, the auxiliary information will be a subset of features and will allow us to precompute parts of the tree instead of node by node. This way, most calls to

(\rho,A)
will simply return the appropriate feature index from

Given an instance

4.1
Certificate complexity and query-efficient certificate finding

At a high level, a certificate of

that “witness”

for all

on the coordinates in

Definition 5

(Certificate complexity)

For a model

and an input

complexity of certifying

’s value on

\min_{W\subseteq[d]}\big\{|W|\colon\text{$f(y)=f(x^{\star})$ for all $y$ s.t.~$y_{W}=x^{\star}_{W}$}\big\}.

The
certificate complexity of
f
is the quantity
\displaystyle C(f)\coloneqq\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\{C(f,x)\}
.

Example.

Because the set
W=[d]
is always a certificate of
f
on
x^{\star}
, we typically want
small
certificates satisfying
|W|\leq C(f)
. For a specific example, consider the function

defined by the decision tree in

. Then

is a certificate of
f
on
x^{\star}=10000
since

outputs

on any input

and

. Indeed, for an arbitrary instance

the indices queried along the root-to-leaf path for

constitute a certificate of
f
on

.

In general, we say

is a certificate of

if there exists some

5.1
Optimality of

We prove the following (slightly more general and formal) version of

Theorem 7
.

Fix any constant
c<1
and success probability
\varepsilon>0
. For any algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, and
S,d,\Delta\in\mathds{N}
such that
S\leq d
and
\Delta\leq S^{c}
, there is a monotone model
f:\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
with
S(f)\leq S
and instance
x^{\star}
with
\Delta_{f}(x^{\star})=\Delta
such that
\mathcal{A}
must make

q=S^{\Omega(\Delta)}+\Omega(\log d)

queries to find a single optimal counterfactual for

with success probability
\varepsilon
.

We’ll allow the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
to use randomness. In order to give lower bounds against randomized algorithms, we’ll use the easy direction of Yao’s Lemma.

Lemma 5.1

(
[
Yao77
]
)
.

For any
q\in\mathds{N}
, let
\mathcal{R}_{q}
and
\mathcal{D}_{q}
be the set of all
q
-query randomized and deterministic algorithms respectively, and let
I
be the set all of possible pairs
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
and
x^{\star}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
(i.e. instances of the counterfactual problem).

For any distribution
\mu
supported on
I
,

\displaystyle\min_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{q}}

\displaystyle\max_{(f,x^{\star})\in I}[\mathrm{error}_{R}(f,x^{\star})]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\min_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{q}}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{({\bm{f}},\bm{x}^{\star})\sim\mu}[\mathrm{error}_{D}({\bm{f}},\bm{x}^{\star})]

where
\mathrm{error}_{R}(f,x^{\star})
is the probability that
R
does not return an optimal counterfactual for
x^{\star}
, and
\mathrm{error}_{D}(f,x^{\star})=\mathds{1}[\text{$D$ does not return an optimal counterfactual for $x^{\star}$}]
.

First, we show that
s^{\Omega(\Delta)}
queries are necessary.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
\Delta,d,S,q\in\mathds{N}
where
S\leq d
, and
\mathcal{A}
a
q
-query randomized algorithm, there exists some monotone function
f:\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
with
S(f)\leq S
and input
x^{\star}\in\{0,1\}
satisfying
\Delta_{f}(x^{\star})=\Delta
on which
\mathcal{A}
successfully returns an optimal counterfactual for
x^{\star}
with probability at most
\frac{q+1}{\binom{S}{\Delta}}
.

Proof.

We will apply Yao’s Lemma: To apply it, we need to define a distribution over input instances
({\bm{f}},\bm{x}^{\star})
. That distribution is simple. With probability
1
,
\bm{x}^{\star}=(0,\ldots,0)
. Then, we sample a uniformly random
\bm{z}
from the
\binom{S}{\Delta}
elements of
\{0,1\}^{S}
with Hamming weight equal to
\Delta
and set
{\bm{f}}:\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
as

{\bm{f}}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1~|~\text{$\geq\Delta+1$ of the first $s$ coordinates of $x$ are $1$}\\
1~|~\text{the first $s$ coordinates of $x$ equal $\bm{z}$}\\
0~|~\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

First, we note that it is always true that
S({\bm{f}})\leq S
. This is because
{\bm{f}}(x)
only depends on the first
S
-coordinates of
x
, so flipping any of the other
d-S
coordinates cannot change
{\bm{f}}
’s output. Second, we note that the input
\bm{x}^{\prime}
being
\bm{z}
concatenated with
d-S
many
0
s satisfies
{\bm{f}}(x^{\star})\neq{\bm{f}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
and
|x^{\star}-\bm{x}^{\prime}|=\Delta
. Therefore,
\Delta_{{\bm{f}}}(x^{\star})\leq\Delta
. Furthermore,
\bm{x}^{\prime}
is the
only
counterfactual for
x^{\star}
at distance
\leq\Delta
, and so the algorithm succeeds if and only if it outputs
\bm{x}^{\prime}
.

Consider an arbitrary
q
-query
deterministic
algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
. By Yao’s Lemma, it is sufficient to prove that the probability
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
outputs
\bm{x}^{\prime}
given the random instance
({\bm{f}},\bm{x}^{\star})
is at most
\frac{q+1}{\binom{S}{\Delta}}
. As
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is deterministic, the queries it makes and answer it outputs are a deterministic function of the answers to its previous queries. Let
g:\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
be defined as

g(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1~|~\text{$\geq\Delta+1$ of the first $S$ coordinates of $x$ are $1$}\\
0~|~\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We will consider
one
possible execution path of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, when
{\bm{f}}
and
g
give the same answer on each query
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
makes. Specifically, the execution path of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
when every query it makes is answered consistently with
g
. In that case,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
will make (the deterministic) queries
x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(q)}
. We compute

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}

\displaystyle[\text{Any query inconsistent with $g$}]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{q}\operatorname{{Pr}}[{\bm{f}}(x^{(j)})\neq g(x^{(j)})]

(Union bound)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{The first $S$ coordinates of $x^{(j)}$ equal $\bm{z}$}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q}{\binom{S}{\Delta}}.

Lastly, if
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
follows the single execution path corresponding to every query it makes being answered consistently with
g
, it will output some answer
x^{(g)}
. This answer is correct if and only if it equals
\bm{x}^{\prime}
, which occurs with probability at most
\frac{1}{\binom{S}{\Delta}}
for any choice of
x^{(g)}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\text{ returns an optimal counterfactual}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\text{ returns an optimal counterfactual},\text{All queries consistent with $g$}]

\displaystyle\quad+\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\text{ returns an optimal counterfactual},\text{Any query inconsistent with $g$}]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{{Pr}}[x^{(g)}=\bm{z}]+\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{Any query inconsistent with $g$}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\binom{S}{\Delta}}+\frac{q}{\binom{S}{\Delta}}=\frac{q+1}{\binom{S}{\Delta}}

The desired result follows from Yao’s Lemma.

∎

d\in\mathds{N}
, and
\varepsilon>0
, there is a monotone model
f:\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
with
S(f)=1
and an instance
x^{\star}
with
\Delta_{f}(x^{\star})
such that
\mathcal{A}
must make
q\geq\varepsilon d
queries in order to find a single optimal counterfactual for
x^{\star}
.

Proof.

As in the proof of
Lemma

5.3
, we apply Yao’s Lemma with the distribution with
\bm{x}^{\star}=(0,\ldots,0)
with probability
1
and

{\bm{f}}(x)=x_{\bm{i}}

where
\bm{i}\in[d]
is chosen uniformly at random. Once again,
S({\bm{f}})=1
as
f
is only sensitive on the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
and there is a counterfactual from
\bm{x}^{\star}
of distance
1
.

Since we are applying Yao’s Lemma, it is sufficient to reason about deterministic local algorithms.
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
be a
q
-query local and deterministic algorithm. We will consider one execution path of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, namely that in which all its queries return
0
. In that case, it will make the deterministic queries
x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(q)}
. As
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is local, the queries
x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(q)}
must all be adjacent, and since
x^{(1)}=(0,\ldots,0)
, the number of coordinates
j\in[d]
on which
x^{(t)}_{j}=1
for some
t\in[q]
is at most
q-1
. The probability that
{\bm{f}}(x^{(t)})=1
for some
t\in[q]
is the probability that
{\bm{f}}(x^{(t)})_{\bm{i}}=1
for some
t\in[q]
which is at most
\frac{q-1}{d}
.

Therefore
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
follows a single execution path with probability at least
1-\frac{q-1}{d}
. At the end of that execution path, it outputs a single “guess” counterfactual
y
. However,
y
is an optimal counterfactual for
\bm{x}^{\star}
only if
y_{\bm{i}}=1
and
y_{j}=0
for all
j\neq\bm{i}
, which can occur with probability at most
\frac{1}{d}
for any single
y
. Therefore, the probability
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
outputs a correct counterfactual is at most

\operatorname{{Pr}}[y\text{ is an optimal counterfactual for }\bm{x}^{\star}]+\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\text{ does not output }y]\leq\frac{1}{d}+\frac{q-1}{d}=\frac{q}{d}.

By applying Yao’s Lemma, we conclude that any randomized
q
-query local algorithm succeeds in finding an optimal counterfactual with probability at most
\frac{q}{d}
. Therefore, to succeed with probability
\varepsilon
, we must have
q\geq\varepsilon d.

∎

6
Functions over general feature spaces

For a monotone model
f:X^{d}\to\{0,1\}
and an instance
x^{\star}\in X^{d}
, we reduce the problem of computing
\mathcal{C}_{f}(x^{\star})
to the problem of computing
\mathcal{C}_{f_{x^{\star}}}(y)
where
f_{x^{\star}}:\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
is a Boolean model defined in terms of
f
and
x^{\star}
and
y\in\{0,1\}^{d}
is a specific Boolean input to
f_{x^{\star}}
.

The reduction.

We assume
f:X^{d}\to\{0,1\}
is monotone with respect to some total ordering on
X
. Write
\overline{X}
for the top element of
X
and
\underline{X}
for the bottom element of
X
. Assuming
f
is nonconstant, we then have
f(\overline{X}^{d})=1
and
f(\underline{X}^{d})=0
. For
x\in X^{d}
and
y\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, we define
x\uparrow y,x\downarrow y\in X^{d}
as instances satisfying

(x\uparrow y)_{i}=\begin{cases}\overline{X}&y_{i}=1\\
x_{i}&y_{i}=0\end{cases}\qquad(x\downarrow y)_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i}&y_{i}=1\\
\underline{X}&y_{i}=0\end{cases}

for all
i\in[d]
. That is,
x\uparrow y
is the instance formed by “snapping” a subset of features, specified by
y
, to their largest possible value
\overline{X}
. In
x\downarrow y
, features are snapped in the opposite direction. Note that relative to the ordering
\leq
on
X
, we have
x\downarrow y\leq x\leq x\uparrow y
for all
y\in\{0,1\}^{d}
. Given a monotone
f:X^{d}\to\{0,1\}
, we write
x\uparrow\mathrel{\mspace{-1mu}}\downarrow_{f}y
to denote snapping
x
in a direction specified by
f(x)
:

x\uparrow\mathrel{\mspace{-1mu}}\downarrow_{f}y=\begin{cases}x\downarrow y&f(x)=1\\
x\uparrow y&f(x)=0.\end{cases}

We will just write
x\uparrow\mathrel{\mspace{-1mu}}\downarrow y
when
f
is known from context.
We then define the Boolean function
f_{x}:\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
as

f_{x}(y)=f(x\uparrow\mathrel{\mspace{-1mu}}\downarrow y).

Note that
f_{x}
is monotone since
y\leq y^{\prime}
implies
x\uparrow\mathrel{\mspace{-1mu}}\downarrow y\leq x\uparrow\mathrel{\mspace{-1mu}}\downarrow y^{\prime}
.

Properties of
f_{x}
.

We establish two important properties of the reduction
f_{x}
. The first property states that the set

\mathcal{C}_{f}(x)=\{\Delta(x^{\prime},x):x^{\prime}\in X^{d}\text{ is an optimal counterfactual for }x\text{ with respect to }f\}

is equal to the set
\mathcal{C}_{f_{x}}(f(x)^{d})
where
f(x)^{d}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
is the bit
f(x)
concatenated with itself
d
times. The second property states that the sensitivity of
f_{x}
is at most the sensitivity of
f
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
f:X^{d}\to\{0,1\}
be monotone. Then

1.

\mathcal{C}_{f}(x)=\mathcal{C}_{f_{x}}(f(x)^{d})

2.

S(f_{x})\leq S(f)

for all
x\in X^{d}
.

Proof.

For (1), fix an input
x\in X^{d}
. Suppose without loss of generality that
f(x)=1
(the arguments for
f(x)=0
are symmetric) so that
f_{x}(1^{d})=f(x\downarrow 1^{d})=f(x)=1
. We will show that if
y\in\{0,1\}^{d}
is a counterfactual for
1^{d}
then there exists a counterfactual
x^{\prime}\in X^{d}
for
x
satisfying
\Delta(x,x^{\prime})=\Delta(y,1^{d})
and likewise if there is a counterfactual
x^{\prime}
for
x
then there is a counterfactual
y
for
1^{d}
satisfying the same
\Delta(x,x^{\prime})=\Delta(y,1^{d})
. It then follows that
\Delta_{f}(x)=\Delta_{f_{x}(1^{d})}
and also
\mathcal{C}_{f}(x)=\mathcal{C}_{f_{x}}(1^{d})
.
Let
y\in\{0,1\}^{d}
be a counterfactual for
1^{d}
. Then,

f_{x}(y)=f(x\downarrow y)=0

which shows that
x\downarrow y\in X^{d}
is a counterfactual for
x
. Moreover, by definition,

\Delta(x\downarrow y,x)=\Delta(y,1^{d}).

On the other hand, suppose
x^{\prime}\in X^{d}
is a counterfactual for
x
. Let
y\in\{0,1\}^{d}
be the instance defined by

y_{i}=\begin{cases}0&i\in\Delta(x,x^{\prime})\\
1&i\not\in\Delta(x,x^{\prime})\end{cases}

for all
i\in[d]
. Then
x\downarrow y\leq x^{\prime}
which implies

f_{x}(y)=f(x\downarrow y)\leq f(x^{\prime})=0

by monotonicity. Hence,
y
is a counterfactual for
1^{d}
and again we have
\Delta(x\downarrow y,x)=\Delta(y,1^{d})
.

For (2), let
y\in\{0,1\}^{d}
be an instance satisfying
S_{f_{x}}(y)=S(f_{x})
. Then
f_{x}(y)=f(x\uparrow\mathrel{\mspace{-1mu}}\downarrow y)
. In particular,

S(f_{x})=S_{f_{x}}(y)=S_{f}(x\uparrow\mathrel{\mspace{-1mu}}\downarrow y)\leq S(f)

as desired.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2

Let
f:X^{d}\to\{0,1\}
be monotone and
x^{\star}\in X^{d}
. Assume without loss of generality
f(x^{\star})=1
. Using
Theorem

5
, we compute and return the set
\mathcal{C}_{f_{x^{\star}}}(1^{d})
which queries
f
at most
S(f_{x^{\star}})^{O(\Delta_{f_{x^{\star}}}(1^{d}))}
times.
Lemma

states that
\mathcal{C}_{f_{x^{\star}}}(1^{d})=\mathcal{C}_{f}(x^{\star})
which ensures we have returned the desired set. Moreover, since these two sets are equal we have that

. We can then write the query bound as

\displaystyle S(f_{x^{\star}})^{O(\Delta_{f_{x^{\star}}}(1^{d}))}\log d

7
Conclusion

We have given a query-optimal algorithm for finding counterfactuals for monotone models. Our algorithm achieves a logarithmic dependence on the dimension
d
, which is exponentially smaller than the previous best known query complexity of
d^{O(\Delta_{f}(x^{\star}))}
, achievable by brute-force local search.

There are several concrete avenues for future work. The key enabling ingredient in our overall algorithm is our implicit decision tree algorithm for monotone models. This gives us a way to efficiently navigate an interpretable representation of a black box model
f
— a decision tree representation — even in the case of complex models for which this representation is intractably large. This is a potentially versatile technique for understanding black box models — can we use it to efficiently compute other types of explanations? The query complexity of our algorithm scales with the sensitivity of
f
, a discrete analogue of the Lipschitz constant. Can our lower bounds be evaded by running our algorithm, or variants of it, for
smoothed
versions
\tilde{f}
of
f
, obtained from
f
by adding a small amount of noise? Finally, while we have focused on sparsity as our notion of distance in this work, it would be interesting to extend our techniques to accommodate other distance functions.

Appendix A
Certificate-based restrictions

In this section, we prove
Lemma

4.2
. We say a certificate
S\subseteq[n]
of
f
on
x\in\{0,1\}^{d}
is a
0
-certificate if
f(x)=0
and likewise
S
is a
1
-certificate if
f(x)=1
. We write
C_{0}(f)=\max_{x\in f^{-1}(0)}\{C(f,x)\}
to denote the
0
-certificate complexity of
f
and
C_{1}(f)=\max_{x\in f^{-1}(1)}\{C(f,x)\}
for the
1
-certificate complexity of
f
. Our proof will reference the fact that the intersection of a
1
-certificate with a
0
-certificate is necessarily nonempty (since otherwise there would be some instance
x\in\{0,1\}^{d}
having both a
0
-certificate and a
1
-certificate).

Fact A.1
.

Let
S_{0}
be a
0
-certificate of
f:\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
and let
S_{1}
be a
1
-certificate of
f
. Then
S_{0}\cap S_{1}\neq\varnothing
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.2
.

Let
\rho
be a restriction of
f:\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
such that
\text{Dom}(\rho)=\textsc{Cert}(f)
. We’ll show that
C_{0}(f_{\rho})+C_{1}(f_{\rho})\leq C_{0}(f)+C_{1}(f)-1
. The result then follows by induction. Suppose without loss of generality that
\text{Dom}(\rho)
is a
1
-certificate of
f
. Then we claim
C_{0}(f_{\rho})\leq C_{0}(f)-1
. Consider any
x\in f_{\rho}^{-1}(0)
. Consider the string
x^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
formed by inserting
\rho
into the string
x
so that
f(x^{\prime})=f_{\rho}(x)
. Let
S_{0}
be a
0
-certificate of
f
on
x^{\prime}
with
|S_{0}|\leq C_{0}(f)
. Then
S_{0}\setminus\text{Dom}(\rho)
is a
0
-certificate of
f_{\rho}
on
x
. Moreover,
S_{0}\cap\text{Dom}(\rho)\neq\varnothing
by
A.1
and so
|S_{0}\setminus\text{Dom}(\rho)|\leq|S_{0}|-1\leq C_{0}(f)-1
. Since
x
is any arbitrary

, we have that

It follows each such restriction

by at least

. Thus, after at most
2C(f)\geq C_{0}(f)+C_{1}(f)

∎
