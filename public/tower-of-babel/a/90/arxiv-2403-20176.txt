Abstract
. The present paper is concerned with the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem for fractional (and non-fractional) nonlinear diffusion equations posed in bounded domains. Main results consist of well-posedness in an energy class with no sign restriction and convergence of such (possibly sign-changing) energy solutions to asymptotic profiles after a proper rescaling. They will be proved in a variational scheme only, without any use of semigroup theories nor classical quasilinear parabolic theories. Proofs are self-contained and performed in a totally unified fashion for both fractional and non-fractional cases as well as for both porous medium and fast diffusion cases.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with boundary
\partial\Omega
. The present paper is concerned with (possibly sign-changing) energy solutions to the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem for the (fractional) nonlinear diffusion equation of the form,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(|u|^{q-2}u)+(-\Delta)^{\theta}u

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{ in }\ \Omega\times(0,\infty),

(1.1)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{ on }\ (\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega)\times(0,\infty),

(1.2)

\displaystyle(|u|^{q-2}u)(\,\cdot\,,0)

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{ in }\ \Omega,

(1.3)

where
\partial_{t}:=\partial/\partial t
,
1<q<\infty
,
0<\theta\leq 1
and
\rho_{0}
is an initial datum. In case
0<\theta<1
,
(-\Delta)^{\theta}
denotes the so-called
fractional Laplacian
given by

(-\Delta)^{\theta}f(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[|\xi|^{2\theta}\mathcal{F}f\right](x)=C(d,\theta)\,\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}y

for, e.g.,
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(see §
2.2
below for details). In case
\theta=1
,
(-\Delta)^{\theta}
stands for the standard (minus) Laplacian (i.e.,
-\Delta
), and the Dirichlet condition (
1.2
) is replaced by the standard one,

u=0\quad\text{ on }\ \partial\Omega\times(0,\infty).

[formula]

Therefore the case
\theta=1
fully corresponds to the classical
porous medium
(
1<q<2
) and
fast diffusion equations
(
q>2
), for which an enormous number of contributions have already been made (see, e.g.,
[
68
,
16
]
). Moreover, due to the change of variables,

\rho(x,t)=(|u|^{q-2}u)(x,t)\ \mbox{ and }\ m=\frac{1}{q-1}\in(0,\infty),

the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) can (equivalently) be rewritten as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+(-\Delta)^{\theta}(|\rho|^{m-1}\rho)

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\ \Omega\times(0,\infty),

\displaystyle(|\rho|^{m-1}\rho)

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\ (\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega)\times(0,\infty),

\displaystyle\rho(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\ \Omega

in the sense of Definition
3.1
below. In case
\theta=1
, the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition
\eqref{bc}^{\prime}
is nothing but the following:

(|\rho|^{m-1}\rho)=0\quad\text{ on }\ \partial\Omega\times(0,\infty).

Most of studies on fractional nonlinear diffusion equations have been performed for the Cauchy problem, where the equations are posed in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see, e.g.,
[
37
,
38
,
69
,
21
,
35
,
36
,
15
]
and references therein). On the other hand, there have also been a limited number of contributions to the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem in bounded domains. Among those, the
fractional porous medium equation
(
0<\theta<1
,
1<q<2
) is studied in
[
20
,
22
,
23
,
62
]
, where well-posedness is proved in the class of (possibly sign-changing) “
H^{*}
-solutions”, which is an analogue of the
H^{-1}
solution for
\theta=1
to the fractional setting, by a direct application of the Brézis–Kōmura theory (see
[
26
,
27
]
) and asymptotic behaviors of nonnegative
H^{*}
solutions are also studied (see also
[
17
,
18
]
). Thanks to a Bénilan–Crandall type estimate, the convergence of nonnegative
H^{*}
solutions (after a rescaling) to a single asymptotic profile is proved. Moreover, convergence of (possibly) sign-changing weak solutions (after the rescaling) to sign-definite asymptotic profiles in the uniform norm are also proved under certain variational criteria for initial energy in
[
44
]
, whose strategy is based on the paper
[
25
]
concerned with the (non-fractional) porous medium equation (see also
[
6
, Remark 3.3]
for some instability results). The study on the
fractional fast diffusion equation
(
0<\theta<1
,
q>2
) is still ongoing. We refer the reader to a pioneer work
[
54
]
, where Hölder regularity and asymptotic behavior of nonnegative solutions are studied, as well as to a recent comprehensive study
[
19
]
, where existence and uniqueness for
nonnegative
“weak dual solutions” are proved by means of the Brézis–Kōmura theory (to construct
H^{*}
solutions first as an approximation) along with a Steklov averaging and a monotonicity argument (available only for nonnegative data), and where
L^{p}
-
L^{q}
smoothing estimates and (finite-time) extinction estimates (in various norms) are further discussed for nonnegative weak dual solutions. Moreover, some of these works (see, e.g.,
[
20
,
19
]
) also cover more general nonlocal elliptic operators (e.g., spectral fractional Laplacian). On the other hand, concerning
0<\theta<1
, the study of (possibly)
sign-changing
weak solutions has not yet been fully pursued, and furthermore,
full convergence
to asymptotic profiles has never been proved even for nonnegative weak solutions to the fractional fast diffusion equation (cf. see
[
43
]
for the classical one
\theta=1
).

In the present paper, we are concerned with existence, uniqueness and continuous dependence on initial data of (possibly
sign-changing
) weak solutions in an
energy class
to the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem for (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) with
1<q<\infty
and
0<\theta\leq 1
, i.e., involving both nonlocal and local, both porous medium and fast diffusion equations.
Concerning
\theta=1
(i.e., the local case), the well-posedness of the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem has been proved in several different schemes, the most standard one of which may rely on
classical theories for quasilinear parabolic equations
(see, e.g.,
[
45
,
56
,
57
]
). Suitable modifications of the equation (
1.1
) and initial data
\rho_{0}
provide approximate problems being neither degenerate nor singular, and being instead uniformly parabolic (see
[
68
]
). In this scheme, approximate solutions enjoy classical regularity, e.g., of class
C^{2+\alpha,1+\alpha/2}(\Omega\times(0,T))
, and hence, one can manipulate the approximate equations as well as their solutions rigorously without any additional arguments. On the other hand, such approximations may not preserve intrinsic structures of the original equation, and therefore, one may make an extra effort to fill the gap between structures of the original and approximate problems. Another scheme is based on a couple of
nonlinear semigroup theories
. One of them is the so-called
H^{-1}
framework, where the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) is reduced, without any approximation, into the Cauchy problem for a nonlinear evolution equation governed by a subdifferential operator in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
, the dual space of
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then well-posedness can be proved by means of the Brézis–Kōmura theory (see
[
26
,
27
]
). Another one is often called an
L^{1}
framework, which is based on the celebrated Crandall–Liggett theory (see
[
34
]
). In this scheme, well-posedness of the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem is proved in a certain class of solutions (i.e.,
H^{-1}
and
L^{1}
solutions) by a direct application of nonlinear semigroup theories. On the other hand, this scheme is less flexible to extract additional information from solutions beyond the frame of each theory. Actually, although the
H^{-1}
framework is consistent with a variational (or energy) structure of (
1.1
), the regularity of
H^{-1}
solutions may be too weak to perform some of energy estimates rigorously. The
L^{1}
framework can provide stronger solutions, but it is less relevant to the variational structure. The third scheme relies on a
metric gradient flow approach
, where the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem is reduced to the Cauchy problem for a gradient flow in the Monge–Kantrovich metric (see
[
63
]
). It enables us to extract an entropy structure from the equation as well as to prove well-posedness in a fairly weak sense by a direct application of a general theory (see
[
10
]
). In each scheme, one can prove well-posedness, for which the class of solutions may depend on the scheme. On the other hand, these solutions eventually coincide each other, since the uniqueness can be proved in a rather weak formulation.

In this paper, we are also interested in revealing asymptotic behaviors of solutions to the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem posed in bounded domains, for which energy structures of the equation play a crucial role. Therefore we shall work in a proper class of weak solutions (called
energy solutions
) which enjoy energy identities (or inequalities) and corresponding regularities required for the analysis of their asymptotic behaviors. For the local case
\theta=1
, this purpose can be achieved with the first scheme mentioned above based on the quasilinear parabolic theory. However, for the nonlocal case
0<\theta<1
, this scheme is no longer applicable due to the nonlocality of the equation; indeed, the classical quasilinear parabolic theory is established for local equations only, and hence, we already face a difficulty in the approximation level. On the other hand, it is enough to exploit a nonlinear semigroup theory just for proving well-posedness in a weak formulation (like
H^{*}
solutions) and it is also possible to cover an even more general class of (nonlocal and nonlinear) diffusion equations simultaneously; however, there may still remain a delicate issue on whether or not energy inequalities and sufficient regularities are assured for such weak solutions (see Remark
4.5
in §
3
). In the present paper, we shall hence select another scheme, which is based on the time-discretization and energy methods, to prove well-posedness of the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) posed in general bounded domains as well as to derive energy identities (and inequalities) for the local and nonlocal cases. Our methods of proofs are fully variational and do not rely on the classical quasilinear parabolic theories nor nonlinear semigroup theories. It is self-contained, and moreover, it can handle the local (
\theta=1
) and nonlocal cases (
0<\theta<1
) as well as the porous medium (
1<q<2
) and fast diffusion cases (
q>2
) in a totally unified fashion. Furthermore, in order to prove existence of energy solutions, we can preserve intrinsic variational structures of the equation, and therefore, energy identities and inequalities can be derived in detail without extra complexity. To the best of authors’ knowledge, there is no literature providing a complete description of such a variational proof for existence of (possibly sign-changing) energy solutions even for the local case
\theta=1
(cf. see
[
1
]
for an outline).

This paper is composed of six sections. The next section is devoted to preliminary facts on the fractional Sobolev space and the fractional Laplacian. Main results of the present paper will be stated in Section
3
. The main technical novelties of the present paper may reside in proofs for (i) well-posedness along with energy identities and inequalities (in Section
4
) and also for (ii) the monotonicity of the Rayleigh quotient (see (
3.13
) below) through the evolution of energy solutions (in Section
5
). We shall pay a careful attention to the defect of absolute continuity of the Rayleigh quotient and bridge such a gap by means of the theory for functions of bounded variation (see, e.g.,
[
9
]
). Then the results such as (i) and (ii) enable us to derive immediately (optimal) decay and extinction estimates for the energy solutions (see also Section
5
). Finally, Section
6
is dedicated to discussing convergence of (properly rescaled) energy solutions to asymptotic profiles. The novelty of this part may be found in an application of a Łojasiewicz–Simon inequality of Feireisl–Simon type for the restricted fractional Laplacian (see
[
8
]
) to prove the full convergence to a single asymptotic profile for the fractional fast diffusion equation.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we shall briefly recall some preliminary facts on the
fractional Sobolev space
as well as the
fractional Laplacian
for later use.

2.1.
Fractional Sobolev spaces

For each
\theta\in(0,1)
, the
fractional Sobolev space

H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined by

H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\left\{w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon[w]_{H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty\right\},

where
[\,\cdot\,]_{H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
is the
Gagliardo seminorm
defined by

[w]_{H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}:=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|w(x)-w(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}y\quad\text{ for }\ w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Then
H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a Hilbert space endowed with the inner product,

\displaystyle(v,w)_{H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle:=(v,w)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{(v(x)-v(y))(w(x)-w(y))}{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}y

for
v,w\in H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Hence we have

\|w\|_{H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+[w]_{H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\quad\text{ for }\ w\in H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

We set

\mathcal{X}_{1}(\Omega):=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

and define a subspace of
H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as

\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega):=\left\{w\in H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon w=0\ \text{ a.e.~on }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega\right\}\quad\text{ for }\ \theta\in(0,1),

for which we also refer the reader to
[
24
,
41
]
and references therein. In what follows, we shall use the same letter
f
to denote the restriction of
f\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
to
\Omega
when no confusion can arise. Moreover, we may also use the same notation for the zero extension to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
.

A Poincaré type inequality is also available for the class
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
,
0<\theta<1
(see, e.g.,
[
7
]
,
[
8
, Proposition A.1]
).

Proposition 2.1

(Poincaré type inequality)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\theta\in(0,1)
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on
d
,
\theta
and the diameter of
\Omega
such that

\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C[w]_{H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\quad\text{ for all }\ w\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega).

Thanks to Proposition
2.1
, for each
\theta\in(0,1)
, the Gagliardo seminorm
[\,\cdot\,]_{H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
turns out to be a norm of
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
which is equivalent to
\|\cdot\|_{H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
. Therefore we endow the space
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
with
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}:=[\,\cdot\,]_{H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
. Moreover, we set
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}_{1}(\Omega)}:=\||\nabla\cdot|\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. Therefore, for
\theta\in(0,1]
, the space
(\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega),\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)})
is a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product,

(v,w)_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}:=\begin{cases}\int_{\Omega}\nabla v(x)\cdot\nabla w(x)\,\text{\rm d}x&\text{ if }\ \theta=1,\\
\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{(v(x)-v(y))(w(x)-w(y))}{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}y&\text{ if }\ \theta\in(0,1)\end{cases}

for
v,w\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
. We also refer the reader to, e.g.,
[
58
]
,
[
61
]
,
[
39
]
for more details on fractional Sobolev spaces.

We close this subsection with stating fractional Sobolev embeddings for the space
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
and density results. To this end, we define

2_{\theta}^{*}:=\begin{cases}\frac{2d}{d-2\theta}&\text{ if }\ d>2\theta,\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2.1)

We first recall fractional Sobolev embeddings (see, e.g.,
[
39
, Theorems 6.7, 6.9 and 7.2]
).

Proposition 2.2

(Sobolev–Poincaré type inequality)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\theta\in(0,1]
. Suppose that

q\in\begin{cases}[1,2_{\theta}^{*}]&\text{ if }\ d>2\theta,\\
[1,\infty)&\text{ if }\ d=2\theta,\\
[1,\infty]&\text{ if }\ d<2\theta.\end{cases}

Then
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
is continuously embedded in
L^{q}(\Omega)
, i.e., the following Sobolev–Poincaré type inequality holds,

\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq C_{q}\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}\quad\text{ for all }\ w\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega),

(2.2)

where
C_{q}>0
denotes the best constant. Moreover,
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
is compactly embedded in
L^{q}(\Omega)
, provided that
1\leq q<2_{\theta}^{*}
.

In particular, we note that, for any
0<\theta\leq 1
,

\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)\equiv L^{2}(\Omega)^{*}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}

(2.3)

with compact and continuous canonical injections. Indeed, the compact embedding
L^{2}(\Omega)^{*}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}
follows from the compact embedding
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
along with Schauder’s theorem (see, e.g.,
[
28

).

In order to give a representation to the space
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
, we present a density result. See Appendix §
B

Proposition 2.3

(Density)
.

Let
\Omega
be a smooth bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
0<\theta<1
. Then the space
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
is dense in
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
.

Hence, if
\theta\in(0,1]\setminus\{1/2\}
and

is smooth, then
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
can be identified with

H^{\theta}_{0}(\Omega):=\overline{C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)}^{H^{\theta}(\Omega)}.

Here for each
\theta\in(0,1)
we denote

H^{\theta}(\Omega):=\left\{w\in L^{2}(\Omega)\colon[w]_{H^{\theta}(\Omega)}<\infty\right\}

equipped with a seminorm
[\,\cdot\,]_{H^{\theta}(\Omega)}
and a norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{\theta}(\Omega)}

[w]_{H^{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}:=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}\frac{|w(x)-w(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}y\quad\text{ for }\ w\in H^{\theta}(\Omega)

respectively (see, e.g.,
[
58
]
). Indeed, Theorem

(proved in
[

]
) shows that

when

, and Proposition

implies that
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{\theta}_{0}(\Omega)
when
\Omega
is smooth. Furthermore, as for
\theta=1/2
,
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
is equivalent to the space

, which is introduced in

]
and different from both

and

2.2.
Restricted fractional Laplacian

For each
\theta\in(0,1)
, the
fractional Laplacian

(-\Delta)^{\theta}
is defined for, e.g.,
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\theta}\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[|\xi|^{2\theta}\mathcal{F}f\right](x)

\displaystyle=C(d,\theta)\,\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)}{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=C(d,\theta)\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B(x;\varepsilon)}\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)}{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}y\quad\mbox{ for }\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
denote the Fourier transform operator and its inverse, respectively, and
\mathrm{P.V.}
stands for the
Cauchy principal value
, and moreover,
B(x;\varepsilon)
denotes the open ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
x
and
C(d,\theta)
is the constant given by

C(d,\theta)=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1-\cos\xi_{1}}{|\xi|^{d+2\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}\xi\right)^{-1}.

In what follows, we may neglect the constant
C(d,\theta)
for simplicity. Note that, even though
\varphi
has a compact support, the support of
(-\Delta)^{\theta}\varphi
may no longer be compact.

For every
\theta\in(0,1)
, the
restricted fractional Laplacian
, still denoted by
(-\Delta)^{\theta}
, is defined for each
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
in terms of the restriction of
(-\Delta)^{\theta}\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
to the domain
\Omega
.

\mathbb{R}^{d}

, when no confusion can arise

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\psi(x)(-\Delta)^{\theta}\varphi(x)\,\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{(\psi(x)-\psi(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=(\psi,\varphi)_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)},

which is symmetric and continuous in

with respect to

Definition 2.4

(A weak form of the restricted fractional Laplacian)
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For each
\theta\in(0,1)
, the
restricted fractional Laplacian with the Dirichlet condition
is defined in a weak sense as an operator, still denoted by
(-\Delta)^{\theta}
, from
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
to
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}
by the relation,

\langle(-\Delta)^{\theta}u,v\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}y

for
u,v\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
.

As for
\theta=1
, we denote by
(-\Delta)^{1}:=-\Delta
the (minus) classical Dirichlet Laplacian, whose weak form is given as

\langle-\Delta u,v\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{1}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\,\text{\rm d}x

for
u,v\in\mathcal{X}_{1}(\Omega)=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
(and then,
\mathcal{X}_{1}(\Omega)^{*}=H^{-1}(\Omega)
).

From the above, for every
\theta\in(0,1]
,
(-\Delta)^{\theta}
turns out to be the Riesz map from
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
to its dual space
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}
, and moreover, we observe that

(-\Delta)^{\theta}w=\text{\rm d}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\ \text{ for }\ w\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega),

(2.4)

where
d
means the Fréchet derivative in
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
.

3.
Main results

This section is devoted to stating main results of the present paper. To this end, we begin with defining
weak solutions
for the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
).

Definition 3.1

(Weak solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.3
))
.

Let
1<q<\infty
,
\theta\in(0,1]
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}
. For
0<T<\infty
, a pair of measurable functions
(u,\rho):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called a
weak solution
on
[0,T]
to the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) if the following (i)–(iii) hold true:

(i)

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,T)
, it holds that
u\in L^{2}(\varepsilon,T;\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega))\cap L^{q}(Q_{T})
and
\rho\in C([0,T];\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*})\cap W^{1,2}(\varepsilon,T;\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*})\cap L^{q^{\prime}}(Q_{T})
, where
Q_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T)
.

(ii)

It holds that
\rho=|u|^{q-2}u
a.e. in
Q_{T}
, and moreover,

\left\langle\partial_{t}\rho(t),w\right\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}+(u(t),w)_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}=0\quad\mbox{ for all }\ w\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)

(3.1)

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. Here
\partial_{t}\rho
stands for the derivative of the function
t\mapsto\rho(t)
.

(iii)

The initial condition
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}
holds, that is,
\rho(t)\to\rho_{0}
strongly in
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}
as
t\to 0_{+}
.

Furthermore, for
0<T\leq\infty
, a function
(u,\rho):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called a weak solution on
[0,T)
to the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) if it is a weak solution on
[0,S]
for any
0<S<T
. In particular, when
T=\infty
, it may simply be called a weak solution to the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
).

In what follows, the weak solution may be denoted by
u
only (instead of the pair
(u,\rho)
), when no confusion can arise; indeed,
\rho
can uniquely be determined by
u
from the relation
\rho=|u|^{q-2}u
of (ii). Moreover,
\rho
will then be denoted by
|u|^{q-2}u
simply.

Weak solutions complying with some energy inequalities and regularity are called
energy solutions
(see (ii) of Theorem
3.5
below for more details).

Remark 3.2

(Other classes of solutions)
.

As for
\theta=1
, the class of weak solutions in the sense of Definition
3.1
clearly contains
H^{-1}
solutions, that is,
\rho\in C([0,T];H^{-1}(\Omega))
complying with the following conditions:

•

\rho\in L^{m+1}(Q_{T})\cap W^{1,2}_{\rm loc}((0,T];H^{-1}(\Omega))
,

•

u=|\rho|^{m-1}\rho\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}((0,T];H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
,

•

the absolute continuity of the function
t\mapsto\|\rho(t)\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)}^{m}
on
(0,T]
,

•

the weak form,

\langle\partial_{t}\rho(t),w\rangle_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(t)\cdot\nabla w\,\text{\rm d}x\quad\mbox{ for }\ w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,

•

and the initial condition
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}

(see
[
27
]
,
[
68
]
). Conversely, as will be seen in §
4.3
, the weak solutions turn out to be
H^{-1}
solutions. Thus the class of weak solutions coincides with that of
H^{-1}
solutions. Such an observation can also be extended to the fractional case
0<\theta<1
; indeed, the
H^{*}
solution introduced in
[
20
]
is an analogue of the
H^{-1}
solution to the fractional case
0<\theta<1
and it is equivalent to the weak solution in the sense of Definition
3.1
. On the other hand, the notion of
weak dual solutions
(see
[
19
]
) seems like a “very weak” version of a
weighted

L^{1}
solution. Hence there may be no direct relation between the weak dual solution and the weak solution concerned in this paper; however, as proved in
[
19
]
, due to the weight, every
nonnegative
weak solution turns out to be a weak dual solution.

Remark 3.3

(As a gradient flow)
.

The weak form (
3.1
) can also be written as an
evolution equation
,

\partial_{t}\rho(t)+(-\Delta)^{\theta}u(t)=0\ \text{ in }\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}\quad\text{ for a.e. }t\in(0,T),

(3.2)

which along with the relation
\rho=|u|^{q-2}u
has been well studied so far. Moreover, Definition
3.1
is consistent with a (generalized) gradient flow structure of the nonlinear diffusion equation (
1.1
) (along with (
1.2
)). Indeed, thanks to (
2.4
), one can rewrite (
3.2
) as

\partial_{t}(|u|^{q-2}u)(t)=-\text{\rm d}\varphi(u(t))\ \text{ in }\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}\quad\text{ for a.e. }t\in(0,T),

where
\text{\rm d}\varphi:\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}
denotes the Fréchet derivative of the functional
\varphi:\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\varphi(w)=\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\ \text{ for }\ w\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega).

Such a generalized gradient flow structure enables us to derive energy identities and inequalities, which will play a crucial role in analyzing long-time behaviors of weak solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.3
).

Remark 3.4

(Energy identity)
.

The following facts follow immediately from Definition
3.1
for general weak solutions
(u,\rho)
on
[0,T]
to the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
): We first observe that the function

t\mapsto\|\rho(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}=\left\langle\rho(t),(-\Delta)^{-\theta}\rho(t)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}

is absolutely continuous on
(0,T]
. Testing (
3.2
) by
(-\Delta)^{-\theta}\rho(t)
directly, we then find that

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\left\|\rho(t)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}+\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}=0\quad\mbox{ for a.e. }t\in(0,T),

which implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho(t)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}+\int^{t}_{s}\|u(r)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}\,\text{\rm d}r

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}

(3.3)

for
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
(including
s=0
by virtue of the fact that
\rho\in C([0,T];\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*})
). We shall further derive another energy identity for general weak solutions in §
4.3
.

v=v(x,s)
, respectively, and hence, the novelty of Theorem
3.9
may reside in the fast diffusion case (i.e.,
2<q<2^{*}_{\theta}
and either
\phi\geq 0
or
q
is even).

All these results will be proved in subsequent sections.

4.
Proof of Theorem
3.5

In this section, we give a proof for Theorem
3.5
. In §
4.1
, we shall briefly prove the uniqueness part (i) in a standard way. The main ingredients of the proof will be exhibited in §
4.2
, §
4.3
and §
4.4
, where the additional regularity of the energy solution as well as the energy identity and inequalities will directly be proved within the weak formulation as in Definition
3.1
without relying on any classical parabolic theories (see, e.g.,
[
56
,
45
,
57
]
) even for
\theta=1
; actually, they are no longer useful for the fractional case
0<\theta<1
. The
L^{1}
contraction estimate and the Bénilan–Crandall estimate may be more or less standard (see, e.g.,
[
68
,
38
]
), but they will finally be proved in §
4.5
for the completeness.

4.1.
Uniqueness of energy solutions

In this subsection, we shall give a proof for (i) of Theorem
3.5
, i.e., the uniqueness of weak solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.3
).

Proof of
(i)
of Theorem
3.5
.

Let
\rho_{0,1},\rho_{0,2}\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}
be two initial data and let
(u_{1},\rho_{1}),(u_{2},\rho_{2})
be weak solutions on
[0,T]
to the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem for the initial data
\rho_{0,1},\rho_{0,2}
, respectively. Then it follows from Remark
3.3
that

\partial_{t}\left(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\right)(t)+(-\Delta)^{\theta}\left(u_{1}(t)-u_{2}(t)\right)=0\ \text{ in }\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*},

(4.1)

where
\rho_{j}:=|u_{j}|^{q-2}u_{j}
(
j=1,2
), for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. Let
(-\Delta)^{-\theta}:\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}\to\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
be the inverse of the restricted fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{\theta}:\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}
. Testing both sides of (
4.1
) by
(-\Delta)^{-\theta}\left(\rho_{1}(t)-\rho_{2}(t)\right)
, we see that

\displaystyle{\left\langle\partial_{t}(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})(t),(-\Delta)^{-\theta}\left(\rho_{1}(t)-\rho_{2}(t)\right)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle+\left\langle(-\Delta)^{\theta}(u_{1}(t)-u_{2}(t)),(-\Delta)^{-\theta}\left(\rho_{1}(t)-\rho_{2}(t)\right)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}=0

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. Here we easily observe that

(-\Delta)^{-\theta}w=\text{\rm d}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}\right)\ \text{ for }\ w\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}.

Since
\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\in W^{1,2}_{\rm loc}((0,T];\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*})
and
(-\Delta)^{-\theta}(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})\in W^{1,2}_{\rm loc}((0,T];\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega))
, thanks to the standard chain-rule formula, the function
t\mapsto\|\rho_{1}(t)-\rho_{2}(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}
turns out to be absolutely continuous on
(0,T]
and to fulfill the relation,

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|\rho_{1}(t)-\rho_{2}(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}=\left\langle\partial_{t}(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})(t),(-\Delta)^{-\theta}(\rho_{1}(t)-\rho_{2}(t))\right\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. On the other hand, since
(-\Delta)^{\theta}
is symmetric and
\rho_{j}=|u_{j}|^{q-2}u_{j}
for
j=1,2
, it follows that

\displaystyle{\left\langle(-\Delta)^{\theta}(u_{1}(t)-u_{2}(t)),(-\Delta)^{-\theta}\left(\rho_{1}(t)-\rho_{2}(t)\right)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(u_{1}(t)-u_{2}(t)\right)\left(\rho_{1}(t)-\rho_{2}(t)\right)\,\text{\rm d}x\geq 0

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. Therefore combining all these facts, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|\rho_{1}(t)-\rho_{2}(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}\leq 0\quad\text{ for a.e. }\ t\in(0,T),

which yields

\|\rho_{1}(t)-\rho_{2}(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}\leq\|\rho_{1}(s)-\rho_{2}(s)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}

for any
0<s<t\leq T
. Passing to the limit as
s\to 0_{+}
and using the fact that
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*})
along with
\rho_{j}(0_{+})=\rho_{0,j}
for
j=1,2
(see Definition
3.1
), we obtain

\|\rho_{1}(t)-\rho_{2}(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}\leq\|\rho_{0,1}-\rho_{0,2}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}

for all
t\in[0,T]
. This completes the proof for (i) of Theorem
3.5
.
∎

4.2.
Existence of energy solutions

In this subsection, we prove existence of energy solutions and energy inequalities under the assumption that

u_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\cap L^{q}(\Omega)\quad\text{ and }\quad\rho_{0}:=|u_{0}|^{q-2}u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega),

(4.2)

which will slightly be relaxed in §
4.4
for proving (ii) of Theorem
3.5
. More precisely, this subsection is devoted to proving the following lemma:

Lemma 4.1

(Existence of energy solutions under (
4.2
))
.

Under the assumption (
4.2
), the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) admits a unique weak solution
u=u(x,t)
complying with the regularity properties
:

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in C([0,\infty);L^{q}(\Omega))\cap L^{q}(\Omega\times(0,\infty)),

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in C_{\rm weak}([0,\infty);\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega))\cap C_{+}([0,\infty);\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle|u|^{(q-2)/2}u

\displaystyle\in W^{1,2}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,\infty;\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle|u|^{q-2}u

\displaystyle\in W^{1,\infty}(0,\infty;\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*})\cap C([0,\infty);L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega))\cap L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega\times(0,\infty)),

\displaystyle|u|^{q-2}u

\displaystyle\in C_{\rm weak}([0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C_{+}([0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(|u|^{q-2}u)

\displaystyle\in C_{\rm weak}([0,\infty);\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*})\cap C_{+}([0,\infty);\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}),

the initial condition
:

u(t)\to u_{0}\quad\text{ strongly in }\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\ \text{ as }\ t\to 0_{+},

and the following energy inequalities
:

\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+\int_{s}^{t}\|u(r)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\,\text{\rm d}r\leq\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}\|u(s)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q},

(4.3)

\displaystyle{\frac{4}{qq^{\prime}}\int_{s}^{t}\left\|\partial_{t}(|u|^{(q-2)/2}u)(r)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,\text{\rm d}r+\frac{1}{2}\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|u(s)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2},

(4.4)

\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2}\left\|(|u|^{q-2}u)(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{4}{qq^{\prime}}\int_{s}^{t}\left\|(|u|^{(q-2)/2}u)(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\,\text{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|(|u|^{q-2}u)(s)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

(4.5)

for any
0\leq s\leq t<\infty
. Furthermore, if
q>2
, then
\partial_{t}(|u|^{q-2}u)
lies on
L^{2}(0,\infty;L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega))
.

In addition, if
\rho_{0}\in L^{\alpha}(\Omega)
for some
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
, then

\displaystyle|u|^{q-2}u

\displaystyle\in C_{\rm weak}([0,\infty);L^{\alpha}(\Omega))\cap C_{+}([0,\infty);L^{\alpha}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle|u|^{(\beta-2)/2}u

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(0,\infty;\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)),

where
\beta:=(q-1)(\alpha-1)+1
, and

\displaystyle{\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\|(|u|^{q-2}u)(t)\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}^{\alpha}+\frac{4}{\beta\beta^{\prime}}\int^{t}_{s}\left\|(|u|^{(\beta-2)/2}u)(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\,\text{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\|(|u|^{q-2}u)(s)\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}^{\alpha}

(4.6)

for
0\leq s\leq t<\infty
.

Proof.

This proof is divided into four steps.

Step 1 (Discretization via minimization).
We shall introduce a time-discretization for the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) and prove existence of solutions to some discretized problems. Set

X:=\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\cap L^{q}(\Omega)

being endowed with norm

\|w\|_{X}:=\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}+\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\ \text{ for }\ w\in X.

Let
T>0
be arbitrarily fixed and let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be the number of steps. Set
\tau:=T/N>0
being the time-step. We now consider the following discretized equations:

\frac{|u_{n+1}|^{q-2}u_{n+1}-|u_{n}|^{q-2}u_{n}}{\tau}+(-\Delta)^{\theta}u_{n+1}=0\ \text{ in }X^{*},\quad u_{n+1}\in X

(4.7)

for
n=0,1,\dots,N-1
. For each fixed
n
, we shall prove existence of
u_{n+1}\in X
satisfying (
4.7
) and
|u_{n+1}|^{q-2}u_{n+1}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
for each (prescribed)
u_{n}\in X
satisfying
|u_{n}|^{q-2}u_{n}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
in a variational fashion. To this end, define a functional
I_{n}:X\to\mathbb{R}
by

I_{n}(w)=\frac{1}{q\tau}\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}-\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u_{n}|^{q-2}u_{n}}{\tau}w\,\text{\rm d}x

for
w\in X
. Then
I_{n}
is strictly convex and of class
C^{1}
in
X
, and moreover, we find that

\lim_{\|w\|_{X}\to\infty}I_{n}(w)=\infty.

Indeed, using Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u_{n}|^{q-2}u_{n}}{\tau}w\,\text{\rm d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\tau}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q-1}\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\frac{1}{q^{\prime}\epsilon^{q^{\prime}}}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+\frac{\epsilon^{q}}{q}\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}\right)

for
\epsilon>0
. Choosing
\epsilon>0
small enough, we can take a constant
C
(which may depend on
u_{n}
prescribed) such that

\frac{1}{q\tau}\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}-\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u_{n}|^{q-2}u_{n}}{\tau}w\,\text{\rm d}x\geq\frac{1}{2q\tau}\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}-C.

Thus we derive a coercive estimate,

I_{n}(w)\geq\frac{1}{2q\tau}\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}-C

for
w\in X
. Combining the coercivity with the convexity and lower semicontinuity of
I_{n}
on
X
, we infer that
I_{n}
admits a unique minimizer
u_{n+1}
over
X
(see, e.g.,
[
28
, Corollary 3.23]
). Furthermore, the minimizer
u_{n+1}\in X
solves the Euler-Lagrange equation,

\text{\rm d}I_{n}(u_{n+1})=0\ \text{ in }X^{*},

which is equivalent to the equation (
4.7
). Moreover, noting that
u_{n}\in X=\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\cap L^{q}(\Omega)
, we can observe from (
4.7
) that
(-\Delta)^{\theta}u_{n+1}
lies on both
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}
and
L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)
, and hence, it follows that

\frac{|u_{n+1}|^{q-2}u_{n+1}-|u_{n}|^{q-2}u_{n}}{\tau}+(-\Delta)^{\theta}u_{n+1}=0\ \text{ in }\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}\cap L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega).

We shall also prove in the next step that
|u_{n+1}|^{q-2}u_{n+1}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Step 2 (Discrete energy inequalities).
We next derive energy inequalities for the solutions
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
of (
4.7
). They will provide uniform (with respect to the time-step
\tau
) estimates for approximate solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.3
). Let
j\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
and test (
4.7
) with
n=j
by
u_{j+1}\in X
. Then we see that

\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}-|u_{j}|^{q-2}u_{j}}{\tau}u_{j+1}\,\text{\rm d}x+\|u_{j+1}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}=0.

By virtue of Young’s inequality (or convexity), we have

\displaystyle{\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}-|u_{j}|^{q-2}u_{j}}{\tau}u_{j+1}\,\text{\rm d}x}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\tau q^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega}|u_{j+1}|^{q}\,\text{\rm d}x-\frac{1}{\tau q^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega}|u_{j}|^{q}\,\text{\rm d}x,

which yields

\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}\|u_{j+1}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}-\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}\|u_{j}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+\tau\|u_{j+1}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 0.

Summing it up for
j=0,\dots,n
, we infer that

\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}\|u_{n+1}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+\sum_{j=0}^{n}\tau\|u_{j+1}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}

(4.8)

for
n=0,\dots,N-1
.

We next test (
4.7
) with
n=j
by
(u_{j+1}-u_{j})/\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
. It then follows that

\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}-|u_{j}|^{q-2}u_{j}}{\tau}\frac{u_{j+1}-u_{j}}{\tau}\,\text{\rm d}x+\left(u_{j+1},\frac{u_{j+1}-u_{j}}{\tau}\right)_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}=0.

Employing Young’s inequality (or convexity), we see that

\left(u_{j+1},\frac{u_{j+1}-u_{j}}{\tau}\right)_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{1}{2\tau}\|u_{j+1}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\tau}\|u_{j}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}.

On the other hand, from the following elementary inequality:

(|a|^{q-2}a-|b|^{q-2}b)(a-b)\geq\frac{4}{qq^{\prime}}\left||a|^{(q-2)/2}a-|b|^{(q-2)/2}b\right|^{2}

(4.9)

for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
(see, e.g.,
[
4
, Appendix]
), we derive that

\displaystyle{\frac{|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}-|u_{j}|^{q-2}u_{j}}{\tau}\frac{u_{j+1}-u_{j}}{\tau}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4}{qq^{\prime}}\left|\frac{|u_{j+1}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{j+1}-|u_{j}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{j}}{\tau}\right|^{2}.

Therefore we observe that

\displaystyle{\frac{4}{qq^{\prime}}\tau\left\|\frac{|u_{j+1}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{j+1}-|u_{j}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{j}}{\tau}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\|u_{j+1}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|u_{j}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 0.

Summing it up for
j=0,\dots,n
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\frac{4}{qq^{\prime}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\tau\left\|\frac{|u_{j+1}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{j+1}-|u_{j}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{j}}{\tau}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\|u_{n+1}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|u_{0}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}

(4.10)

for any
n=0,\dots,N-1
.

We (formally) test (
4.7
) by
|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}
to see that

\displaystyle{\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}-|u_{j}|^{q-2}u_{j}}{\tau}|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}\,\text{\rm d}x}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(u_{j+1},|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}\right)_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}\leq 0.

Using Young’s inequality (or convexity), we see that

\displaystyle{\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}-|u_{j}|^{q-2}u_{j}}{\tau}|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}\,\text{\rm d}x}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\tau}\int_{\Omega}|u_{j+1}|^{2(q-1)}\,\text{\rm d}x-\frac{1}{2\tau}\int_{\Omega}|u_{j}|^{2(q-1)}\,\text{\rm d}x.

It also follows from the elementary inequality (
4.9
) that

\displaystyle{(u_{j+1}(x)-u_{j+1}(y))\left((|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1})(x)-(|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1})(y)\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4}{qq^{\prime}}\left|(|u_{j+1}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{j+1})(x)-(|u_{j+1}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{j+1})(y)\right|^{2}

for a.e.
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus we finally obtain

\displaystyle\left(u_{j+1},|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}\right)_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{4}{qq^{\prime}}\left\||u_{j+1}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{j+1}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}

for both cases
\theta\in(0,1)
and
\theta=1
(see §
2.1
). Therefore if
|u_{j}|^{q-2}u_{j}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, one can verify that
|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
|u_{j+1}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{j+1}\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
and

\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2\tau}\left\||u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{4}{qq^{\prime}}\left\||u_{j+1}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{j+1}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\tau}\left\||u_{j}|^{q-2}u_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(4.11)

Hence summing it up (
4.11
) for
j=0,\dots,n
and using the assumption that
|u_{0}|^{q-2}u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
(see (
4.2
)), we conclude that

\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2}\left\||u_{n+1}|^{q-2}u_{n+1}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{4}{qq^{\prime}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\tau\left\||u_{j+1}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{j+1}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\||u_{0}|^{q-2}u_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

(4.12)

for any
n=0,\dots,N-1
.

These formal computations can be justified in a standard way; in case
0<\theta<1
, we may introduce a family
(\beta_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}
of monotone Lipschitz continuous functions satisfying
\beta_{\varepsilon}(0)=0
,
|\beta_{\varepsilon}(r)|\leq|r|^{q-1}
for
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\beta_{\varepsilon}(r)\to|r|^{q-2}r
as
\varepsilon\to 0_{+}
for each
r\in\mathbb{R}
, and then, test (
4.7
) by
\beta_{\varepsilon}(u_{j+1})\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
instead of
|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}
. We can then reach the same conclusion by passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0_{+}
and by employing the dominated convergence theorem. In case
\theta=1
, we may also require additional properties that
|\beta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(r)|\leq C|r|^{q-2}
for
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\beta^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(r)\to(q-1)|r|^{q-2}
as
\varepsilon\to 0_{+}
for each
r\in\mathbb{R}
.

For later use, we further derive an
L^{\alpha}
estimate for
|u_{n}|^{q-2}u_{n}
, provided that
\rho_{0}\in L^{\alpha}(\Omega)
for some
\alpha>1
. Setting
\rho_{j+1}:=|u_{j+1}|^{q-2}u_{j+1}
, we also (formally) test (
4.7
) by
|\rho_{j+1}|^{\alpha-2}\rho_{j+1}=|u_{j+1}|^{\beta-2}u_{j+1}
with
\beta=(q-1)(\alpha-1)+1>1
and sum it up for
j=0,\ldots,n
. We can similarly deduce from (
4.9
) that

\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\|\rho_{n+1}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}^{\alpha}+\frac{4}{\beta\beta^{\prime}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left\||u_{j+1}|^{(\beta-2)/2}u_{j+1}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\|\rho_{0}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}^{\alpha},

(4.13)

where
\beta^{\prime}=\beta/(\beta-1)
, for any
n=0,\ldots,N-1
, provided that
\rho_{0}\in L^{\alpha}(\Omega)
. These formal procedures can also be justified as above.

Step 3 (Interpolation and weak convergence).
We define a
piecewise constant interpolant

\bar{u}_{\tau}:[0,T]\to X
of
\{u_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
and
piecewise linear interpolants

\rho_{\tau}:[0,T]\to L^{2}(\Omega)
and
w_{\tau}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
of
\{|u_{n}|^{q-2}u_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
and
\{|u_{n}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
, respectively, by setting

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{\tau}(t)

\displaystyle:=u_{n+1},

\displaystyle\rho_{\tau}(t)

\displaystyle:=|u_{n}|^{q-2}u_{n}+\frac{|u_{n+1}|^{q-2}u_{n+1}-|u_{n}|^{q-2}u_{n}}{\tau}(t-n\tau),

\displaystyle w_{\tau}(t)

\displaystyle:=|u_{n}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{n}+\frac{|u_{n+1}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{n+1}-|u_{n}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{n}}{\tau}(t-n\tau)

for
t\in(n\tau,(n+1)\tau]
and
n=0,1,\ldots,N-1
and

\bar{u}_{\tau}(0):=u_{0},\quad\rho_{\tau}(0):=|u_{0}|^{q-2}u_{0},\quad w_{\tau}(0):=|u_{0}|^{(q-2)/2}u_{0}.

(4.14)

Equations (
4.7
) for
n=0,1,\ldots,N-1
are then reduced to the evolution equation,

\partial_{t}\rho_{\tau}(t)+(-\Delta)^{\theta}\bar{u}_{\tau}(t)=0\ \text{ in }\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*},\quad t\in(0,T)\setminus I_{0},

(4.15)

where
I_{0}:=\{n\tau\colon n=1,2,\ldots,N-1\}
. Moreover, the discrete energy inequalities (
4.8
), (
4.10
) and (

4.3.
Energy identity

In this subsection, we shall derive the energy
identity
(
3.6
) of Theorem
3.5
for general weak solutions in the sense of Definition
3.1
. Actually, we have obtained a similar inequality (
4.3
) in Lemma
4.1
; however, it may not be enough to verify the absolute continuity of the function
t\mapsto\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}
as well as the energy
identity
(
3.6
). Instead, we shall use a chain-rule formula for subdifferentials and directly prove the absolute continuity and energy identity for general weak solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.3
).

Let
E
and
E^{*}
be a reflexive Banach space and its dual space, respectively. Let
\psi:E\to(-\infty,\infty]
be a proper (i.e.,
\psi\not\equiv\infty
) lower-semicontinuous convex functional. Moreover, let
\psi^{*}:E^{*}\to(-\infty,\infty]
be the
convex conjugate
(or
Legendre transform
) of
\psi
defined by

\psi^{*}(\eta)=\sup_{w\in E}\left\{\langle\eta,w\rangle_{E}-\psi(w)\right\}\ \text{ for }\ \eta\in E^{*}.

Then it is well known that
\psi^{*}
is also proper lower-semicontinuous and convex in
E^{*}
(see, e.g.,
[
28
, §1.4]
). Let
\partial_{E}\psi:E\to 2^{E^{*}}
and
\partial_{E^{*}}\psi^{*}:E^{*}\to 2^{E}
denote subdifferential operator of
\psi
and
\psi^{*}
, respectively, that is,

\partial_{E}\psi(w)=\left\{\eta\in E^{*}\colon\psi(v)-\psi(w)\geq\langle\eta,v-w\rangle_{E}\ \text{ for }\ v\in E\right\}

for
w\in D(\psi):=\{v\in E\colon\psi(v)<\infty\}
with domain
D(\partial_{E}\psi):=\{v\in D(\psi)\colon\partial_{E}\psi(v)\neq\emptyset\}
(moreover,
\partial_{E^{*}}\psi^{*}
can be defined analogously with the identification
E^{**}\equiv E
). Then we recall that, for
(w,\eta)\in E\times E^{*}
,

\eta\in\partial_{E}\psi(w)\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad w\in\partial_{E^{*}}\psi^{*}(\eta)

(see, e.g.,
[
11
]
). We recall that

Lemma 4.2

(Chain-rule for subdifferentials of convex conjugates)
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and let
(a,b)
be a nonempty open interval in
\mathbb{R}
. Let
E
be a reflexive Banach space and let
\psi:E\to(-\infty,\infty]
be a proper lower semicontinuous convex functional over
E
. Let
u\in L^{p}(a,b;E)
and
\xi\in W^{1,p^{\prime}}(a,b;E^{*})
be such that

\xi(t)\in\partial_{E}\psi(u(t))\quad\text{ for a.e. }t\in(a,b).

(4.32)

Then the function
t\mapsto\psi^{*}(\xi(t))
is absolutely continuous on
[a,b]
, and moreover, it holds that

\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\psi^{*}(\xi(t))=\langle\partial_{t}\xi(t),u(t)\rangle_{E}\quad\text{ for a.e. }t\in(a,b).

Proof.

The lemma above is a variant of the well-known chain-rule formula for subdifferentials in convex analysis (cf. see
[
26
, Lemma 3.3]
). Indeed, one can prove it by combining the chain-rule formula
[
33
, Lemma 4.1]
(see the appendix of
[
3
]
for a proof) with the facts that
E^{**}
is identified with
E
by reflexivity and (
4.32
) is equivalent to
u(t)\in\partial_{E^{*}}\psi(\xi(t))
for a.e.
t\in(a,b)
.
∎

Let
T\in(0,\infty)
and let
u=u(x,t)
be a weak solution on
[0,T]
to (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) in the sense of Definition
3.1
. Testing (
3.1
) with
u(t)\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
, we find that

\left\langle\partial_{t}(|u|^{q-2}u)(t),u(t)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}+\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}=0

(4.33)

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. We apply Lemma
4.2
by setting

E=X:=\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\cap L^{q}(\Omega)\quad\text{ and }\quad\psi(w)=\frac{1}{q}\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}\ \text{ for }\ w\in X.

Then
\psi
is of class
C^{1}
in
X
and
\partial_{X}\psi(w)=\{|w|^{q-2}w\}
for
w\in X
(indeed, the functional
w\mapsto(1/q)\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}
defined for
w\in L^{q}(\Omega)
is already of class
C^{1}
and its Fréchet derivative at
w
coincides with
|w|^{q-2}w
in
(L^{q}(\Omega))^{*}\equiv L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)
, and hence, a.e. in
\Omega
as well). Hence from Definition
3.1
, we find that
\xi=|u|^{q-2}u\in W^{1,2}_{\rm loc}((0,T];\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*})\hookrightarrow W^{1,2}_{\rm loc}((0,T];X^{*})
and

u_{j,n+1}^{q-1}:=|u_{j,n+1}|^{q-2}u_{j,n+1}
and
u_{j,n}^{q-1}:=|u_{j,n}|^{q-2}u_{j,n}
for
j=1,2
. Then we observe that

\displaystyle{\left\langle\frac{(u_{1,n+1}^{q-1}-u_{2,n+1}^{q-1})-(u_{1,n}^{q-1}-u_{2,n}^{q-1})}{\tau},\eta_{m}(u_{1,n+1}-u_{2,n+1})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{(u_{1,n+1}^{q-1}-u_{2,n+1}^{q-1})-(u_{1,n}^{q-1}-u_{2,n}^{q-1})}{\tau}\eta_{m}(u_{1,n+1}-u_{2,n+1})\,\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\to\int_{\Omega}\frac{(u_{1,n+1}^{q-1}-u_{2,n+1}^{q-1})-(u_{1,n}^{q-1}-u_{2,n}^{q-1})}{\tau}\mathrm{sgn}(u_{1,n+1}-u_{2,n+1})\,\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{(u_{1,n+1}^{q-1}-u_{2,n+1}^{q-1})-(u_{1,n}^{q-1}-u_{2,n}^{q-1})}{\tau}\mathrm{sgn}(u_{1,n+1}^{q-1}-u_{2,n+1}^{q-1})\,\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\Omega}(u_{1,n+1}^{q-1}-u_{2,n+1}^{q-1})_{+}\,\text{\rm d}x-\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\Omega}(u_{1,n}^{q-1}-u_{2,n}^{q-1})_{+}\,\text{\rm d}x,

where
(s)_{+}:=\max\{0,s\}
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore it follows that

\int_{\Omega}(u_{1,n+1}^{q-1}-u_{2,n+1}^{q-1})_{+}\,\text{\rm d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}(|u_{0,1}|^{q-2}u_{0,1}-|u_{0,2}|^{q-2}u_{0,2})_{+}\,\text{\rm d}x.

(4.42)

We can similarly prove that

\int_{\Omega}(u_{1,n+1}^{q-1}-u_{2,n+1}^{q-1})_{-}\,\text{\rm d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}(|u_{0,1}|^{q-2}u_{0,1}-|u_{0,2}|^{q-2}u_{0,2})_{-}\,\text{\rm d}x,

where
(s)_{-}:=\max\{0,-s\}
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus we obtain

\int_{\Omega}|u_{1,n+1}^{q-1}-u_{2,n+1}^{q-1}|\,\text{\rm d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}\left||u_{0,1}|^{q-2}u_{0,1}-|u_{0,2}|^{q-2}u_{0,2}\right|\,\text{\rm d}x.

Let
\bar{u}_{j,\tau}
be the piecewise constant interpolants of
\{u_{j,n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
for
j=1,2
. Then

\displaystyle{\int_{\Omega}\left|(|\bar{u}_{1,\tau}|^{q-2}\bar{u}_{1,\tau})(t)-(|\bar{u}_{2,\tau}|^{q-2}\bar{u}_{2,\tau})(t)\right|\,\text{\rm d}x}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left||u_{0,1}|^{q-2}u_{0,1}-|u_{0,2}|^{q-2}u_{0,2}\right|\,\text{\rm d}x\quad\text{ for }\ t\in[0,T],

which along with the convergence of

(see §
4.2
) and the arbitrariness of

implies (
(iii)
). Moreover, repeating the same argument as in §

, we can prove (
(iii)
) for
u_{0,1}
,
u_{0,2}
satisfying the assumptions as in (ii) of Theorem

, that is, (
4.34
). Similarly, one can prove from (

) the comparison principle: if
u_{0,1}\leq u_{0,2}
a.e. in

, then

a.e. in
\Omega\times(0,\infty)
.

The Bénilan–Crandall estimate can be proved by a scaling argument as in
[
68
]
. For the completeness, we shall give a proof. We first note that, if
u=u(x,t)
solves (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), then so does the scaled functions,

u_{\lambda}=u_{\lambda}(x,t):=\lambda^{-1/(q-2)}u(x,\lambda t)\quad\text{ for }\ \lambda>0.

Fix
t>0
. Let
h\in\mathbb{R}
be small enough and take
\lambda>0
such that
t+h=\lambda t
. Then we observe that

\displaystyle{\left\|(|u|^{q-2}u)(t+h)-(|u|^{q-2}u)(t)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\lambda^{(q-1)/(q-2)}(|u_{\lambda}|^{q-2}u_{\lambda})(t)-(|u|^{q-2}u)(t)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\lambda^{(q-1)/(q-2)}-1\right|\left\|(|u_{\lambda}|^{q-2}u_{\lambda})(t)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|(|u_{\lambda}|^{q-2}u_{\lambda})(t)-(|u|^{q-2}u)(t)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{L1-contr}}}{{\leq}}\left|1-\lambda^{-(q-1)/(q-2)}\right|\left\||u_{0}|^{q-2}u_{0}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|(|u_{\lambda}|^{q-2}u_{\lambda})(0)-(|u|^{q-2}u)(0)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

Since

, we have

\displaystyle{\left\|(|u|^{q-2}u)(t+h)-(|u|^{q-2}u)(t)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left|1-\lambda^{-(q-1)/(q-2)}\right|\left\||u_{0}|^{q-2}u_{0}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=2\frac{q-1}{|q-2|}\frac{\||u_{0}|^{q-2}u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}}{t}|h|+O(|h|^{2})\ \mbox{ as }\ h\to 0,

(4.43)

which also yields

\displaystyle{\left\|\frac{(|u|^{q-2}u)(t+h)-(|u|^{q-2}u)(t)}{h}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{q-1}{|q-2|}\frac{\||u_{0}|^{q-2}u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}}{t}+O(|h|)\ \mbox{ as }\ h\to 0.

(4.44)

In case

for a.e.

[

). Hence we infer from (

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{t}(|u|^{q-2}u)(t)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq 2\frac{q-1}{|q-2|}\frac{\||u_{0}|^{q-2}u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}}{t}

for a.e.

where

is given by

for

, and recall the following:

(See

)
.

Let

In addition, if

\displaystyle=\frac{(|u_{\lambda}|^{q-2}u_{\lambda})(t)-(|u|^{q-2}u)(t)}{h}+\frac{q-1}{q-2}\frac{(|u_{\lambda}|^{q-2}u_{\lambda})(t)}{t}+O(h)

5.
Monotonicity of Rayleigh quotient and decay estimates

This section is devoted to proving Theorem
3.6
. Let
u=u(x,t)
be an energy solution and set
\mathsf{R}(t):=R(u(t))
for
t\geq 0
as far as
u(t)\neq 0
(see below for the definition of the Rayleigh quotient
R(\cdot)
). By a simple calculation, we shall derive from (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) that
\text{\rm d}\mathsf{R}/\text{\rm d}t\leq 0
almost everywhere, from which, however, the monotonicity of
\mathsf{R}
does not follow immediately, since the absolute continuity of
\mathsf{R}
has not been guaranteed. In order to fill the gap, we shall exploit the theory for functions of bounded variation (see, e.g.,
[
9
]
). Moreover, as a corollary, we shall also derive decay and extinction estimates for energy solutions to the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) (see Corollary
3.7
).

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Let
u_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\cap L^{q}(\Omega)
satisfy (
3.5
) and
u_{0}\not\equiv 0
. Let
u
be the unique (global-in-time) energy solution to the Cauchy–Dirichlet problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) with the initial datum
\rho_{0}=|u_{0}|^{q-2}u_{0}
. Define the
Rayleigh quotient
by

R(w):=\frac{\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{2}}\quad\text{ for }\ w\in(\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\cap L^{q}(\Omega))\setminus\{0\}.

Since
u_{0}\not\equiv 0
and the function
t\mapsto\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}
is nonincreasing and (absolutely) continuous on
[0,\infty)
, the map
t\mapsto{\mathsf{R}}(t):=R(u(t))
can be defined on the interval
[0,t_{*})
where
t_{*}
is given by

t_{*}:=\sup\left\{T>0\colon\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}>0\ \text{ for all }\ t\in[0,T]\right\}\in(0,\infty].

(5.1)

When
t_{*}<\infty
, the solution
u=u(x,t)
is said to
extinct in finite time
and
t_{*}
is called the
extinction time
of
u
.

Let
0<T<t_{*}
be fixed. The function
t\mapsto\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}
is absolutely continuous and uniformly away from
0
on
[0,T]
(indeed, it is nonincreasing and
u(T)\not\equiv 0
). Moreover, due to (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) with
s=0
, the derivative
(\text{\rm d}/\text{\rm d}t)\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}
is uniformly bounded a.e. in
(0,T)
, and hence, the function
g(t):=1/\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{2}
is Lipschitz continuous on
[0,T]
. Thanks to (ii) of Theorem
3.5
, the function
f(t):=\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}
is nonincreasing on
[0,T]
(and hence, of bounded variation in
(0,T)
) and right-continuous on
[0,T]
. Therefore
{\mathsf{R}}=fg
is also right-continuous on
[0,T]
and of bounded variation in
(0,T)
(see
[
9
, Proposition 3.2]
). Therefore
\mathsf{R}
is differentiable a.e. in
(0,T)
.

We now claim that
\text{\rm d}{\mathsf{R}}/\text{\rm d}t\leq 0
a.e. in
(0,T)
. Indeed, we have

\frac{\text{\rm d}{\mathsf{R}}}{\text{\rm d}t}(t)=\frac{\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{2}\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}-\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{4}}.

Recall from (
3.6
) and (
3.8
) that

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q},

\displaystyle\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{8}{qq^{\prime}}\left\|\partial_{t}(|u|^{(q-2)/2}u)(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. Moreover, by a simple computation, since
\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}>0
for
t\in[0,T]
, we see that

\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{2}=\frac{2}{q}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{2-q}\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. Combining all these facts, we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{\text{\rm d}{\mathsf{R}}}{\text{\rm d}t}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{-\frac{8}{qq^{\prime}}\|\partial_{t}(|u|^{(q-2)/2}u)(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{2}{qq^{\prime}}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{2-q}(\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q})^{2}}{\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\frac{8}{qq^{\prime}}\|\partial_{t}(|u|^{(q-2)/2}u)(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+\frac{2}{qq^{\prime}}(\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q})^{2}}{\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q+2}}

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. Then noting that

\displaystyle\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\left\|(|u|^{(q-2)/2}u)(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=2(\partial_{t}(|u|^{(q-2)/2}u)(t),(|u|^{(q-2)/2}u)(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\text{\rm d}{\mathsf{R}}}{\text{\rm d}t}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{8}{qq^{\prime}}\frac{\|\partial_{t}(|u|^{(q-2)/2}u)(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\|(|u|^{(q-2)/2}u)(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q+2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{8}{qq^{\prime}}\frac{(\partial_{t}(|u|^{(q-2)/2}u)(t),(|u|^{(q-2)/2}u)(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q+2}}\leq 0

(5.2)

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. Here the last inequality follows from Schwarz’s inequality.

We next prove the monotonicity of the Rayleigh quotient
t\mapsto\mathsf{R}(t)
on
[0,\infty)
, which is still unclear, since
\mathsf{R}
is of bounded variation only and may not be absolutely continuous. Since
f(t)=\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}
is of bounded variation in
(0,T)
and right-continuous on
[0,T]
, thanks to
[
9
, Theorem 3.28]
and Lebesgue’s decomposition theorem, it follows that, for
0<s<t<T
,

\displaystyle f(t)-f(s)=\mathrm{D}f((s,t])=\int^{t}_{s}\dfrac{\text{\rm d}f}{\text{\rm d}r}(r)\,\text{\rm d}r+(\mathrm{D}f)_{s}((s,t]),

where
\mathrm{D}f
and
(\mathrm{D}f)_{s}
denote the distributional derivative of
f
represented as a finite Radon measure in
(0,T)
and its singular part (with respect to the one-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{1}
), respectively. Since
f
is nonincreasing (see, e.g.,
[
9
, Corollary 3.29]
), we see that

f(t)-f(s)\leq\int^{t}_{s}\dfrac{\text{\rm d}f}{\text{\rm d}r}(r)\,\text{\rm d}r\quad\text{ for }\ 0<s<t<T,

which implies
(\mathrm{D}f)_{s}((s,t])\leq 0
for any
(s,t]\subset(0,T)
. Since
f
is of bounded variation and
g
is Lipschitz continuous in
(0,T)
, by virtue of
[
9
, Proposition 3.2]
and Lebesgue’s decomposition theorem, we observe that

\mathrm{D}{\mathsf{R}}=g\mathrm{D}f+f\frac{\text{\rm d}g}{\text{\rm d}t}\mathcal{L}^{1}=g\dfrac{\text{\rm d}f}{\text{\rm d}t}\mathcal{L}^{1}+g(\mathrm{D}f)_{s}+f\frac{\text{\rm d}g}{\text{\rm d}t}\mathcal{L}^{1},

(5.3)

where
\mathcal{L}^{1}
denotes the one-dimensional Lebesgue measure. Therefore as
{\mathsf{R}}
is of bounded variation in
(0,T)
and right-continuous on
[0,T]
, we infer from
[
9
, Theorem 3.28]
and (
5.3
) that

\displaystyle{\mathsf{R}}(t)-{\mathsf{R}}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}{\mathsf{R}}((s,t])

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{s}g(r)\dfrac{\text{\rm d}f}{\text{\rm d}r}(r)\,\text{\rm d}r+[g(\mathrm{D}f)_{s}]((s,t])+\int^{t}_{s}f(r)\dfrac{\text{\rm d}g}{\text{\rm d}r}(r)\,\text{\rm d}r

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{s}\dfrac{\text{\rm d}{\mathsf{R}}}{\text{\rm d}r}(r)\,\text{\rm d}r+\int_{(s,t]}g\,\text{\rm d}(\mathrm{D}f)_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{dR-neg}}}{{\leq}}0

for
0<s<t<T
. Indeed,
g
is (Lipschitz) continuous and positive on
[0,T]
and
(\mathrm{D}f)_{s}
is a nonpositive finite Radon measure in
(0,T)
. Thus the function
t\mapsto{\mathsf{R}}(t)
is nonincreasing in
(0,T)
, and hence, it is so on
[0,t_{*})
from the right-continuity of
{\mathsf{R}}
on
[0,T]
and the arbitrariness of
T<t_{*}
. This completes the proof of Theorem
3.6
.
∎

Thanks to Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
, we can prove Corollary

.

Proof of Corollary
3.7
.

We assume (
3.14
) (which also implies
|u_{0}|^{q-2}u_{0}\in L^{(2_{\theta}^{*})^{\prime}}(\Omega)
). Let
u=u(x,t)
be the energy solution of (

)–(

) with initial data

and let

be the extinction time (see (

)). Recalling the energy identity (

) and using the Sobolev–Poincaré inequality (

\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+C_{q}^{-2}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 0

(5.4)

for a.e.
t>0
. In case

, we obtain

for all

for all

On the other hand, (

\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q-2}\leq-\frac{q-2}{q-1}C_{q}^{-2}

(5.5)

for a.e.
t\in(0,t_{*})

Next, recalling the energy identity (

(5.6)

for

for a.e.

for all

. Therefore we see that

. Consequently, there exists a constant

6.3.
Łojasiewicz–Simon gradient inequalities of Feireisl–Simondon type for the restricted fractional Laplacian

This subsection is devoted to recalling a fractional variant of the Łojasiewicz–Simon gradient inequality of Feireisl–Simondon type, which is developed in
[
8
]
and will play a crucial role in order to prove Theorem
3.9
for the (fractional) fast diffusion case
q>2
.

The
Łojasiewicz inequality
was proved by S. Łojasiewicz
[
59
]
in 1965 (see also
[
60
]
) and utilized for proving the (full) convergence of gradient flows in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a single equilibrium as time goes to infinity. This innovative method was then extended by L. Simon
[
67
]
to infinite-dimensional settings and employed to prove the convergence of gradient flows in function spaces. It is often refereed as the
Łojasiewicz–Simon gradient inequality
(see, e.g.,
[
53
,
48
,
47
,
49
,
51
,
31
,
32
,
50
]
). An essential assumption for the Łojasiewicz(–Simon) inequality is the
analyticity
of the functional for which the inequality and gradient flows are considered; however, such an assumption may be too restrictive in view of applications to nonlinear PDEs. Indeed, even power nonlinearity may be ruled out. Feireisl and Simondon
[
43
]
filled such a defect for some concrete functionals, which are associated with the Dirichlet problem for a wide class of (local) semilinear elliptic equations, with the aid of the classical elliptic regularity theory. Furthermore, the result of
[
43
]
was also extended in
[
8
]
to the restricted fractional Laplacian with the Dirichlet condition (we also refer the reader to, e.g.,
[
14
,
52
,
42
,
70
]
for other recent developments).

Let us consider the Dirichlet problem,

\left\{\begin{aligned} (-\Delta)^{\theta}\varphi+g(\varphi)&=0&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u&=0&&\text{ on }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(6.10)

where
\Omega
is a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
0<\theta\leq 1
and
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function. For
0<a,b\leq\infty
, we introduce assumptions for
g
as follows:

(H0):

g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
g(0)=0
.

(H1):

(Analyticity)

g\in C^{\infty}(-a,b)
, and moreover, for every
\alpha\in(0,a)
and
\beta\in(0,b)
, there exist constants
C,M\geq 0
such that

|g^{(n)}(r)|\leq CM^{n}n!\quad\text{ for }\ r\in(-\alpha,\beta)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
large enough.

(H2):

(Analyticity with singularity at the origin)

g\in C^{\infty}(0,b)
, and moreover, for every
\beta\in(0,b)

C,M\geq 0
such that

|g^{(n)}(r)|\leq C\frac{M^{n}n!}{r^{n}}\quad\text{ for }\ r\in(0,\beta)

for

There exist

\|v(s)-\phi\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}<\delta

Proof.

Recall the energy inequality (

\frac{4}{qq^{\prime}}\left\|\partial_{s}(|v|^{(q-2)/2}v)(s)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}s}J(v(s))\leq 0

Moreover, thanks to Hölder’s inequality,

\left\|\partial_{s}(|v|^{q-2}v)(s)\right\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{2}{q^{\prime}}\left\||v|^{(q-2)/2}(s)\right\|_{L^{2q/(q-2)}}\left\|\partial_{s}(|v|^{(q-2)/2}v)(s)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

for a.e.
s>0

), we can take a constant

for a.e.

c\left\|\partial_{s}(|v|^{q-2}v)(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}^{2}+\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}s}J(v(s))\leq 0

for a.e.
s>0
. Noting that

\partial_{s}(|v|^{q-2}v)(s)=-J^{\prime}(v(s))\quad\text{ in }\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}

for a.e.
s>0

), we see that

c\|J^{\prime}(v(s))\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}\left\|\partial_{s}(|v|^{q-2}v)(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)^{*}}+\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}s}J(v(s))\leq 0

(6.13)

for a.e.
s>0

We now apply (a) of Theorem

. To this end, we set

, (H3) also follows immediately. We next check (H2) for the case of (i), i.e.,

a.e. in
\Omega

in
\Omega

[

). For each

\displaystyle|g^{(n)}(r)|

\displaystyle=\lambda_{q}\left|(q-1)(q-2)\dots(q-n)\right|r^{q-n-1}

Thanks to (a) of Theorem

, one can take

whenever

provided

Appendix A
Some lemma

The following lemma may be standard, but it is given just for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{R^{N}}
. Let
1<q_{1},q_{2}<\infty
and set
\alpha:=q_{1}/q_{2}
. Then the map
u\mapsto|u|^{\alpha-1}u
is continuous from
L^{q_{1}}(\Omega)
to
L^{q_{2}}(\Omega)
.

This lemma can be assured with the use of a general theory of Nemytskii operators; here we give a simpler proof, which is specific to power nonlinearities.

Proof.

Let
u_{n},u\in L^{q_{1}}(\Omega)
be such that
u_{n}\to u
strongly in
L^{q_{1}}(\Omega)
. Then we can extract a (not relabeled) subsequence such that
u_{n}
converges to
u
a.e. in
\Omega
. Also,
\||u_{n}|^{\alpha-1}u_{n}\|_{L^{q_{2}}(\Omega)}^{q_{2}}=\|u_{n}\|_{L^{q_{1}}(\Omega)}^{q_{1}}
is bounded. Furthermore, we may further extract a (not relabeled) subsequence of
(n)
such that
|u_{n}|^{\alpha-1}u_{n}\to v
weakly in
L^{q_{2}}(\Omega)
for some
v\in L^{q_{2}}(\Omega)
. It is then easy to show (e.g., with the aid of Mazur’s Lemma, see
[
28
]
) that the pointwise limit of
(|u_{n}|^{\alpha-1}u_{n})
and its weak limit in
L^{q_{2}}(\Omega)
coincide, so that
v=|u|^{\alpha-1}u
. Noting by assumption that

\||u_{n}|^{\alpha-1}u_{n}\|_{L^{q_{2}}(\Omega)}^{q_{2}}=\|u_{n}\|_{L^{q_{1}}(\Omega)}^{q_{1}}\to\|u\|_{L^{q_{1}}(\Omega)}^{q_{1}}=\||u|^{\alpha-1}u\|_{L^{q_{2}}(\Omega)}^{q_{2}},

we can deduce from the uniform convexity of
L^{q_{2}}(\Omega)
that
|u_{n}|^{\alpha-1}u_{n}\to|u|^{\alpha-1}u
strongly in
L^{q_{2}}(\Omega)
. Moreover, from the uniqueness of the limit, the whole sequence
(|u_{n}|^{\alpha-1}u_{n})
also converges to
|u|^{\alpha-1}u
strongly in
L^{q_{2}}(\Omega)
.
∎

Appendix B
Density results in fractional Sobolev spaces

Let
\Omega
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\theta\in(0,1)
. The fractional Sobolev space on
\Omega
is defined by

W^{\theta,p}(\Omega):=\left\{u\in L^{p}(\Omega)\colon[u]_{W^{\theta,p}(\Omega)}<\infty\right\},

where

[u]_{W^{\theta,p}(\Omega)}:=\left(\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{d+p\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}y\right)^{1/p},

and which is endowed with the norm,

\|u\|_{W^{\theta,p}(\Omega)}:=\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+[u]_{W^{\theta,p}(\Omega)}.

We also define the space,

\displaystyle H^{\theta,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon

\displaystyle\qquad\|u\|_{H^{\theta,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\|\mathcal{F}^{-1}[(1+|\xi|^{2})^{\theta/2}\mathcal{F}u]\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty\Big\},

where
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
are the Fourier transform operator and its inverse, respectively, endowed with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{\theta,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
. In particular, we write
H^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=H^{\theta,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. It is shown in
[
61
, Examples 1.8 and 2.12]
that

\displaystyle W^{\theta,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle=(L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))_{\theta,p},

(B.1)

\displaystyle H^{\theta,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle=[L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]_{\theta}

for
\theta\in(0,1)
and
p\in(1,\infty)
. Here
(X,Y)_{\theta,p}
and
[X,Y]_{\theta}
denote the
real
and
complex interpolation spaces
, respectively, between
X
and
Y
for two Banach spaces
X
,
Y
. For more details on the interpolation theory, we refer the reader to, e.g.,
[
61
]
. The following two theorems are well known:

Theorem B.1
.

Let
\theta\in(0,1)
and
p\in[1,\infty)
. The space
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of compactly supported smooth functions is dense in
W^{\theta,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Theorem B.2
.

Let
\theta\in(0,1)
and
p\in[1,\infty)
and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set of class
C^{0,1}
with bounded boundary
\partial\Omega
. Then there exists an extension operator from
W^{\theta,p}(\Omega)
to
W^{\theta,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Namely, there exists a bounded linear operator
T:W^{\theta,p}(\Omega)\to W^{\theta,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
(Tu)|_{\Omega}=u
for
u\in W^{\theta,p}(\Omega)
.

We refer the reader to
[
39
, Theorems 2.4 and 5.4]
for proofs of Theorems
B.1
and
B.2
. In particular, the following theorem holds:

Theorem B.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set of class

with bounded boundary
\partial\Omega
and let
\theta\in(0,1)
and
p\in[1,\infty)
. Then

is dense in
W^{\theta,p}(\Omega)
.

Combining (

) with Theorem

is a Lipschitz open subset of

.

We focus from now on to the case where

. As shown in

(or

Appendix E
Uniqueness of positive solutions to fractional sublinear elliptic equations

Lemma
6.4
can be proved through Brézis–Oswald’s approach for the restricted fractional Laplacian. Brézis–Oswald’s approach (see
[
29
]
) allows us to prove uniqueness of positive solutions to general sublinear (nonlocal) elliptic problems of the form,

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\theta}u

\displaystyle=f(x,u)\

\displaystyle\text{ in }\ \Omega,

(E.1)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\geq 0,\ u\not\equiv 0\

\displaystyle\text{ in }\ \Omega,

(E.2)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0\

\displaystyle\text{ in }\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega,

(E.3)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded
C^{1,1}
domain and
f=f(x,u):\Omega\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. While the original paper
[
29
]
is concerned with the local case
\theta=1
, we extend below the result to the nonlocal case
0<\theta<1
. We make the same assumption on
f
as the original paper
[
29
]
:

(F1):

For a.e.
x\in\Omega
, the function
u\mapsto f(x,u)
is continuous on
[0,\infty)
and the function
u\mapsto f(x,u)/u
is strictly decreasing in
(0,\infty)
,

(F2):

for each
u\in[0,\infty)
, the function
x\mapsto f(x,u)
belongs to
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,

(F3):

there exists a constant
C>0
such that

f(x,u)\leq C(|u|+1)

for all
u\geq 0
and a.e.
x\in\Omega
.

In particular, (
E.1
) with the special choice
f(x,u)=\lambda_{q}|u|^{q-2}u
corresponds to (
3.24
).

Now, we have

Theorem E.1
.

Let
\Omega
is a bounded
C^{1,1}
domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and assume
(F1)–(F3)
. Then the problem (
E.1
)–(
E.2
) admits at most one weak solution
u\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

We start with the following

Lemma E.2
.

Assume
(F1)–(F3)
and let
u,v\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
be two weak solutions to the problem (
E.1
)–(
E.2
). Then
u,v\in C^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
u>0
and
v>0
in
\Omega
, and
u^{2}/v,v^{2}/u\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

We first note by (F1) and (F3) that

-|f(x,\|u\|_{\infty})|\leq f(x,u(x))\leq C(\|u\|_{\infty}+1)\quad\text{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega.

Therefore (F1)–(F3) imply that the function
x\mapsto f(x,u(x))
belongs to
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. We deduce from the fractional elliptic regularity result
[
64
, Proposition 1.4]
that
u\in C^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We can similarly show that
v\in C^{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Moreover, we see from (F1) and (F2) that there exists a constant
M>0
such that

f(x,u(x))\geq-Mu(x)\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega.

Therefore it holds that

(-\Delta)^{\theta}u+Mu\geq 0\quad\text{in }\Omega,

and the same is true for
v
. Thanks to Hopf’s lemma (see
[
46
]
) for the restricted fractional Laplacian, we deduce that
u>0
and
v>0
in
\Omega
, and that

and

. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{u^{2}}{v}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|(\frac{u^{2}}{v})(x)-(\frac{u^{2}}{v})(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big|\frac{u(x)}{v(x)}\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)+\frac{u(x)u(y)}{v(x)v(y)}\left(v(y)-v(x)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{u(y)}{v(y)}\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)\Big|^{2}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\,\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}y.

Therefore we see that

\left\|\frac{u^{2}}{v}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|v\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)<+\infty.

Consequently, we obtain

, and moreover, we can similarly show that

. This completes the proof.
∎

We next give a proof for Theorem

\displaystyle=\left\langle(-\Delta)^{\theta}u,u\right\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}-\left\langle(-\Delta)^{\theta}u,\frac{v^{2}}{u}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{\theta}(\Omega)}

in
