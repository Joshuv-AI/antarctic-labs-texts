1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{D}
denote the open unit disc in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
and
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
denote the Hardy space on
\mathbb{D}
. The study of Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
gained significant attention after the influential paper by Brown and Halmos
[
8
]
which explored the algebraic properties of these operators. A key result from their work provides a characterization of Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D}).
This result was subsequently extended to
H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
in
[
33
]
which states that a bounded linear operator
T
on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
is a Toeplitz operator if and only if

T_{j}^{*}TT_{j}=T\text{ for every }j=1,\ldots,n,

where
T_{j}
denotes the
j
-th coordinate multiplication operator on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n}).
In this article, our primary objective is to establish a similar characterization for Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(D),

D
being a proper holomorphic image of the polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{n}.
Inspired by
[
8
]
, we also prove various multiplicative properties of Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(D)
and in this case
D
is allowed to be any proper holomorphic image of a bounded symmetric domain. To achieve this, we proceed as follows.

(i)

Firstly, we identify an appropriate notion of Hardy space
H^{2}(D)
from a naturally occurring family of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.

(ii)

Subsequently, we show that
H^{2}(D)
can be realized as a closed subspace of an
L^{2}
-space on the Šilov boundary of
D
, which leads to the study of Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(D)
.

We begin by recalling some known facts to lay the groundwork for our results. Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
{\rm Aut}(\Omega)
be the group of all biholomorphic automorphisms of
\Omega.

Definition 1.1
.

[
3
, p. 8]

A bounded domain
\Omega
is said to be symmetric if for every
a,b\in\Omega
there exists an involution
\tau\in{\rm Aut}(\Omega)
which interchanges
a
and
b
.

The open unit disc
\mathbb{D}
, the unit polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{n},
the Euclidean ball
\mathbb{B}_{n}
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
are some examples of bounded symmetric domains. The Hardy space
H^{2}(\Omega)
on a bounded symmetric domain
\Omega
is a well-studied function space
[
26
,
28
]
. It is isometrically isomorphic to a closed subspace of
L^{2}(\partial\Omega,d\Theta),
where
d\Theta
is the unique normalised
I_{\Omega}(0)
-invariant measure on the Šilov boundary
\partial\Omega
of
\Omega
and
I_{\Omega}(0)=\{\phi\in{\rm Aut}(\Omega):\phi(0)=0\}
is the isotropy subgroup of
0
in
{\rm Aut}(\Omega).

Definition 1.2
.

[
5
,
14
]

A proper holomorphic map
\boldsymbol{\pi}:\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
is factored by automorphisms if there exists a finite subgroup
G\subseteq{\rm Aut}(\Omega)
such that for every
z\in\Omega,

\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\pi}(z)=\cup_{\sigma\in G}\{\sigma(z)\}.

It is known that such a group
G
is either a complex reflection group or conjugate to a complex reflection group. A finite complex reflection group
G
is characterized by the fact that the ring of
G
-invariants polynomials in
n
variables is a polynomial ring generated by some homogeneous system of polynomials
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
associated to
G

[
39
, p. 282]
.
If a bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a
G
-space, then the
basic polynomial
mapping

\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}):\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega),

is a proper holomorphic mapping factored by
G
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
is a domain, see
[
38
,
42
]
. Let
\widetilde{\Omega}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be an open set and
\phi:\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
be a proper map factored by
G,
then
\widetilde{\Omega}
is biholomorphic to
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)

[
21
, Proposition 4.4]
. Henceforth, we work with
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
instead of the image of
\Omega
under a proper holomorphic map factored by
G.

An element
\sigma
of
G
acts on
\Omega
by
\sigma\cdot z=\sigma^{-1}(z)
and therefore on the Hardy space
H^{2}(\Omega)
by
(\sigma\cdot f)(z)=f(\sigma^{-1}\cdot z).
Under this action the Szegö kernel
S_{\Omega}
of
\Omega
is
G
-invariant, that is,

S_{\Omega}(\sigma\cdot z,\sigma\cdot w)=S_{\Omega}(z,w)\,\,\text{ for all }\sigma\in G\text{ and }z,w\in\Omega,

which makes the left regular representation
R:G\to\mathcal{U}(H^{2}(\Omega))
well-defined,
\mathcal{U}(X)
being the group of all unitary operators on the Hilbert space
X.
Consequently,
H^{2}(\Omega)
decomposes (canonical decomposition) into an orthogonal direct sum of isotypic components of the left regular representation of
G
indexed by
\widehat{G}
(the set of equivalence classes of irreducible representations of
G
). In Proposition
3.7
and
3.13
, we prove the following:

•

For each one-dimensional representation
\varrho\in\widehat{G},
we prove that the associated isotypic component is isometrically isomorphic to some analytic Hilbert module
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
over the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}].

In other words, we obtain a family

\displaystyle\{H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)):\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1}\}

( 1.1)

of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, where
\widehat{G}_{1}
is the equivalence classes of one-dimensional representations of
G.
An analogous phenomenon was observed for the Bergman space on
\Omega,
where the isotypic component related to the sign representation is isometrically isomorphic to the Bergman space of
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)

[
21
]
. On the basis of this observation, it is natural to define the Hardy space on
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
in the following manner.

The
sign representation
of a finite complex reflection group
G,

{\rm sgn}:G\to\mathbb{C}^{*},
is defined by
[
40
, p. 139, Remark (1)]

\displaystyle{\rm sgn}(\sigma)=(\det(\sigma))^{-1},\,\,\,\,\,\sigma\in G,

see also Equation (
2.13
).

Definition 1.3
.

The reproducing kernel Hilbert space associated to the sign representation of
G
in Equation (
1.1
) is said to be the
Hardy space
on
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega),
denoted by
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)).

For
\Omega=\mathbb{D}^{n},
Definition
1.3
coincides with the one in
[
35
]
when
G
is the permutation group on
n
symbols and the one in
[
20
]
when
G
is a finite complex reflection subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{D}^{n}).

Now we turn our attention to define Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)).
Noting that
G
is a subgroup of
I_{\Omega}(0)
and
d\Theta
is a
I_{\Omega}(0)
-invariant measure on
\partial\Omega,
we conclude that
d\Theta
is also
G
-invariant on
\partial\Omega
. It ensures that the (left) regular representation
R:G\to\mathcal{U}(L^{2}(\partial\Omega,d\Theta))
is well-defined, and
L^{2}(\partial\Omega,d\Theta)
admits an orthogonal decomposition indexed by
\widehat{G}
into the isotypic components associated to the regular representation. For every
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1},
the associated isotypic component of
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
is isometrically isomorphic to some
L^{2}
-space with respect to some measure
d\Theta_{\varrho}
supported on the Šilov boundary of
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega),
where the measure
d\Theta_{\varrho}
is uniquely determined by the representation
\varrho.
We denote it by
L^{2}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega),d\Theta_{\varrho}).
Now one of our key findings states the following:

•

Each
H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
can be realized as a closed subspace of
L^{2}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega),d\Theta_{\varrho}).

For
u\in L^{\infty}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)),
the orthogonal projection
P_{\varrho}:L^{2}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega),d\Theta_{\varrho})\to H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
induces the Toeplitz operator
T_{u}
on
H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
by

T_{u}f=P_{\varrho}(uf).

We are now ready to present one of our main results. We refer to this characterization as a
Brown-Halmos type characterization
in analogy with Theorem 6 of the celebrated paper
[
8
]
by Brown and Halmos.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
m,p
and
n
be natural numbers such that
p|m
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
be the basic polynomial map associated to the irreducible complex reflection group
G(m,p,n)
which acts on
\mathbb{D}^{n}.

Suppose that
T:H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))\to H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))
is a bounded linear operator. Then
T
is a Toeplitz operator if and only if

\displaystyle T^{*}_{n}TT_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T\,\,\text{ ~and~ }\,\,

\displaystyle T^{*}_{i}TT^{p}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle TT_{n-i}\text{~for~}i=1,\ldots,n-1,

where
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))
denotes the
n
-tuple of multiplication operators by the coordinate functions.

An explicit description of
\boldsymbol{\theta}:\mathbb{D}^{n}\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
is given in Equation (
4.4
). We highlight the generality of our framework by noting that every irreducible complex reflection group either belongs to the infinite family
G(m,p,n)
indexed by three parameters, where
m,n,p
are positive integers and
p
divides
m,
or, is one of 34 exceptional groups
[
39
]
. In particular, Theorem
1.4
recovers main results from
[
6
]
and
[
11
]
for
G(1,1,2)
and
G(1,1,n)
, respectively.

Our next goal is to establish certain multiplicative properties of Toeplitz operators. The first question to consider is: under what conditions the product of two Toeplitz operators is itself a Toeplitz operator? The following result demonstrates that, on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)),
this question is closely tied to the corresponding behavior on
H^{2}(\Omega).

Theorem 1.5

(Generalized zero-product property)
.

Let the finite complex reflection group
G
act on the bounded symmetric domain
\Omega
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
be a basic polynomial map associated to
G.
Suppose that the
G
-invariant functions
\widetilde{u},\widetilde{v}\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
are of the form
\widetilde{u}=u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\widetilde{v}=v\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}.
If
T_{u}T_{v}
is a Toeplitz operator on
H^{2}_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
for some
\mu\in\widehat{G}_{1},
then

(i)

T_{u}T_{v}
is a Toeplitz operator on
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
for every
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1}.

(ii)

Moreover,
T_{\widetilde{u}}T_{\widetilde{v}}
is a Toeplitz operator on
H^{2}(\Omega).

Conversely, if
T_{\widetilde{u}}T_{\widetilde{v}}
is Toeplitz operator on
H^{2}(\Omega),
then so is
T_{u}T_{v}
on
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
for every
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1}.

In other words, we show that
T_{u}T_{v}
is a Toeplitz operator on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
if and only if
T_{\widetilde{u}}T_{\widetilde{v}}
is Toeplitz operator on
H^{2}(\Omega).
We illustrate an immediate application of Theorem
1.5
. Recall that on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, the product
T_{u}T_{v}
is a Toeplitz operator if and only if either
u
is co-analytic or
v
is analytic
[
8
]
. An analogous, though more involved, result for
H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
can be found in
[
25
]
. Using Theorem
1.5
and
[
25
]
, we conclude such a characterization in Theorem
4.14
for
H^{2}(D),

D
being a proper holomorphic image of the bidisc
\mathbb{D}^{2}.
An analogous phenomenon arises in the context of identifying commuting tuples of Toeplitz operators which we state below.

Theorem 1.6
.

(Commuting property)
With the same considerations as in Theorem
1.5
, if
T_{u}T_{v}=T_{v}T_{u}
on
H^{2}_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
for some
\mu\in\widehat{G}_{1},
then

(i)

T_{u}T_{v}=T_{v}T_{u}
on
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
for every
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1}.

(ii)

Moreover,
T_{\widetilde{u}}T_{\widetilde{v}}=T_{\widetilde{v}}T_{\widetilde{u}}
on
H^{2}(\Omega).

Conversely, if
T_{\widetilde{u}}T_{\widetilde{v}}=T_{\widetilde{v}}T_{\widetilde{u}}
on
H^{2}(\Omega),
then
T_{u}T_{v}=T_{v}T_{u}
on
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
for every
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1}.

Recall that two Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
commute if and only if either both are analytic, or both are co-analytic, or one is a linear function of the other
[
8
]
. An analogous result for Toeplitz operators with bounded pluriharmonic symbols on
H^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
can be found in
[
44
]
. Combining
[
44
]
and Theorem
1.6
, we extend this conclusion to
H^{2}(D),

D
being a proper holomorphic image of the unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{n},
cf. Theorem
4.11
.

The novelty of our work lies in the application of representation theory and the invariant theory of the groups of deck automorphisms associated with proper holomorphic maps. This approach allows us to study Toeplitz operators on the Hardy space of
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
without requiring any reference to the geometry of such domains. This framework opens up new possibilities for further research in operator theory and its connections to complex analysis.

2.
Preliminaries

We start this section by recalling some basic properties of proper holomorphic mappings which are of our interest.

2.1.
Proper holomorphic maps and complex reflection groups

Let
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
be two domains in
\mathbb{C}^{n}.
A holomorphic map
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
is said to be
proper
if
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{-1}(K)
is a compact subset of
\Omega_{1}
for every compact
K\subset\Omega_{2}.
A proper holomorphic mapping

\boldsymbol{\pi}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
is surjective and there exists a positive integer
m
such that
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}:\Omega_{1}\setminus{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}))\to\Omega_{2}\setminus{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}})
is a (unbranched) covering map with

\displaystyle\text{cardinality of }{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{-1}(w)

\displaystyle=m,\;\,\,w\in\Omega_{2}\setminus{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}})\text{ and }

\displaystyle\text{cardinality of }{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{-1}(w)

\displaystyle<m,\;\,\,w\in{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}),

where
\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}:=\{z\in\Omega_{1}:J_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(z)=0\},

J_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
being the determinant of the complex jacobian matrix of
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}

[
37
, Chapter 15]
. We refer to
m
as
the multiplicity of
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
and
\Omega_{2}
as a
proper holomorphic image
of
\Omega_{1}.

Let
{\rm Aut}(\Omega_{1})
be the group of all biholomorphic automorphisms of a domain
\Omega_{1}.
An element
\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(\Omega_{1})
is called a
deck transformation
of the proper holomorphic mapping
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
if
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\circ\sigma={\boldsymbol{\pi}}
. The deck transformations of the proper holomorphic mapping
\boldsymbol{\pi}
form a subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(\Omega_{1})
and we denote it by
\operatorname{Deck}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}).
If a proper holomorphic map
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is factored by (automorphisms)
G,
then
\operatorname{Deck}({\boldsymbol{\pi}})=G.

In this article, our point of interest is the images of bounded symmetric domains under proper holomorphic mappings that are factored by automorphisms. E. Cartan completely classified the irreducible bounded symmetric domains (Cartan domains) in
[
9
]
(up to biholomorphisms). The list consists of four families of classical type domains and two exceptional domains of dimensions
16
and
27.
We collectively call them the
Cartan
domains. An excellent exposition on Cartan domains is due to Arazy
[
3
]
. Any bounded symmetric domain
D
is of the form
D_{1}^{k_{1}}\times\cdots\times D_{r}^{k_{r}}
for non-equivalent (non-biholomorphic) Cartan domains
D_{i}:i=1,\ldots,r.
The unit ball with respect to the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{C}^{n},
denoted by
\mathbb{B}_{n},
is an example of an irreducible bounded symmetric domain. Rudin proved that every proper holomorphic mapping from
\mathbb{B}_{n}
to some domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n},\,n>1,
is factored by some (automorphisms)
G

[
38
]
. For
n>1,
this result is extended for an irreducible bounded symmetric domain of classical type in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
by Meschiari
[
34
, p. 18, Main Theorem]
. Moreover, if
\Omega
is a bounded symmetric domain, not necessarily irreducible and the multiplicity of
\boldsymbol{\pi}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\pi}(\Omega)
is
2,
then
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is factored by (automorphisms)
G

[
23
]
. In each case,
G
is either a finite complex reflection group or a conjugate to a finite complex reflection group. This is, indeed, a general fact for a proper holomorphic mapping factored by automorphisms, see
[
5
, Theorem 2.1, Theorem 2.2]
,
[
14
, Lemma 2.2, Theorem 2.5]
,
[
38
, Theorem 1.6]
,
[
4
, p. 506]
. Motivated by it, henceforth, we consider proper holomorphic mappings
\boldsymbol{\pi}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\pi}(\Omega),
where
\Omega
is a bounded symmetric domain and
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is factored by (automorphisms) a finite complex reflection group
G.
Now we recall the definition of a complex reflection in
\mathbb{C}^{n}.

Definition 2.1
.

A complex reflection on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is a linear homomorphism
\sigma:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that
\sigma
is of finite order in
GL(n,\mathbb{C})
and the rank of

is 1, where
I_{n}
is the identity operator on
\mathbb{C}^{n}.

In particular, if
\sigma
is
of order
2
,
we call it a
reflection
. A group generated by complex reflections is called a
complex reflection group
. A complex reflection group
G
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
by

\displaystyle\sigma\cdot z=\sigma^{-1}z\text{ for }\sigma\in G\text{ and }z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

( 2.1)

Example 2.2
.

Let
G=\mathfrak{S}_{n},
the permutation group on
n
symbols, acting on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by permuting the coordinates, that is,
\sigma\cdot(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=(z_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\ldots,z_{\sigma^{-1}(n)})
for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. The group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is generated by transpositions
\{(i\,j)\}_{n\geq i>j\geq 1}
. Thus
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is a reflection group. One can realize
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
in the following manner that aligns with above definition in a more appropriate way: consider the faithful representation

\displaystyle\rho:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to GL(n,{\mathbb{C}}):(i\,j)\mapsto A_{(i\,j)},

where
A_{(i\,j)}
is the permutation matrix obtained by interchanging the
i
-th and the

-th columns of the identity matrix.

Example 2.3
.

Let
G=D_{2k}=\langle\delta,\sigma:\delta^{k}=\sigma^{2}={\rm Identity,\sigma\delta\sigma^{-1}=\delta^{-1}}\rangle
be the dihedral group of order
2k.
We define its action on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
via the faithful representation
\rho
defined by

\displaystyle\rho:G\to GL(2,\mathbb{C}):\delta\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}\zeta_{k}&0\\

where

denotes a primitive

where

is a rotation having the eigenvalues

and

is a reflection having the eigenvalues

2.1.1.
Basic invariant polynomials

Chevalley-Shephard-Todd theorem states that the ring of
G
-invariant polynomials in
n
variables is equal to
{\mathbb{C}}[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}]
, where
\theta_{i}
’s are algebraically independent
G
-invariant homogeneous polynomials. These
\theta_{i}
’s are called basic invariant polynomials associated to
G.
In
[
38
]
, the mapping
\boldsymbol{\theta}:=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}):\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is said to be a basic polynomial mapping associated to the group
G.
Let a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a
G
-space, then
\boldsymbol{\theta}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
is a proper holomorphic mapping with the deck automorphism group
G

[
38
,
42
]
. In this paper, we refer to

\boldsymbol{\theta}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)

as a
basic polynomial mapping
associated to the group
G.
Moreover, any proper holomorphic map
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
with the deck automorphism group
G
is
isomorphic
to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
(that is,
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{h}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}\circ\boldsymbol{\psi}
for a biholomorphism
\boldsymbol{h}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\to\Omega^{\prime}
and an automorphism
\boldsymbol{\psi}:\Omega\to\Omega
) and
\Omega^{\prime}
is biholomorphic to
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)

[
21
, Proposition 4.4]
. Thus, the description of any proper holomorphic map
\boldsymbol{f}
from
\Omega
which is factored by (automorphisms)
G
can be recovered from a basic polynomial map
\boldsymbol{\theta}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
associated with
G
(up to an isomorphism) and the proper image
\boldsymbol{f}(\Omega)
is biholomorphic to
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega).
So we lose no generality if we work with a basic polynomial mapping associated to the finite complex reflection group
G
, instead of any proper holomorphic mapping factored by
G
.

Lastly we note that the choice of a basic polynomial mapping associated to
G
is not unique. Since any other basic polynomial mapping
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(\Omega)
is isomorphic to
\boldsymbol{\theta}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega),
our study is independent of the choice of
\boldsymbol{\theta}.
Example
2.5
explains it further.

2.1.2.
Proper holomorphic images of bounded symmetric domains

We provide a few examples of the domains

on which our results are applicable.

Example 2.4
.

We recall the definition of Šilov boundary of a domain from

]
.

.

The Šilov boundary
\partial\Omega
of a bounded domain

is given by the

is said to be a peak point of
\Omega
if there exists a function

such that
|f(w)|>|f(z)|
for all
z\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Omega\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\setminus\{w\},
where
\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
denotes the algebra of all functions holomorphic on
\Omega
and continuous on
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Omega\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu.

For example, the Šilov boundary of the polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{n}
is the
n
-torus
\mathbb{T}^{n}.
The Šilov boundary of the unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{n}
coincides with its topological boundary. Since
\boldsymbol{\theta}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
is a proper holomorphic map which can be extended to a proper holomorphic map of the same multiplicity from
\Omega^{\prime}
to
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)^{\prime},
where the open sets
\Omega^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)^{\prime}
contain
\overline{\Omega}
and
\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)},
respectively. Then
[
29
, p. 100, Corollary 3.2]
states that
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{-1}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))=\partial\Omega.
Thus

\displaystyle\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\theta}(\partial\Omega).

( 2.2)

For instance, the Šilov boundary of the symmetrized polydisc
\boldsymbol{s}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
is given by
\boldsymbol{s}(\mathbb{T}^{n}).
The Šilov boundary of
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is
\Lambda_{n}(\partial L_{n})
(cf. Example
2.9
)
[
23
, Proposition 4.1]
.

2.2.
Hardy space on bounded symmetric domains

A notion of the Hardy space on a bounded symmetric domain
\Omega
is given in
[
26
, p. 521]
. We reproduce it here for the sake of completeness of our exposition. Recall that
I_{\Omega}(0)
denotes the isotropy subgroup of
0
in
\Aut(\Omega).
The group
I_{\Omega}(0)
acts transitively on the Šilov boundary
\partial\Omega
. There exists a unique normalised
I_{\Omega}(0)
-invariant measure on
\partial\Omega
, say
d\Theta
. The
L^{2}
-space
L^{2}(\partial\Omega):=L^{2}(\partial\Omega,d\Theta)
is the Hilbert space of complex measurable functions on
\partial\Omega
with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{\partial\Omega}f(t)\overline{g(t)}d\Theta(t),\,\,f,g\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega).

The action of the group
I_{\Omega}(0)
on
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
is given by
\sigma(f)(z)=f(\sigma^{-1}\cdot z)
for
\sigma\in I_{\Omega}(0)
and
f\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega).
Since the measure
d\Theta
is
I_{\Omega}(0)
-invariant, for any
\sigma\in I_{\Omega}(0),
it follows that

\displaystyle\langle\sigma(f),\sigma(g)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}f(\sigma^{-1}\cdot t)\overline{g(\sigma^{-1}\cdot t)}d\Theta(t)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f(t)\overline{g(t)}d\Theta(t)=\langle f,g\rangle.

( 2.3)

Let
\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
denote the algebra of holomorphic functions on
\Omega.
The Hardy space
H^{2}(\Omega)
is defined by

\displaystyle H^{2}(\Omega):=\{f\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega):\|f\|_{H^{2}}:=\sup_{0<r<1}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}|f(rt)|^{2}d\Theta(t)\right)^{1/2}<\infty\}

in
[
26
]
. For every function
f\in H^{2}(\Omega),
its radial limit
\widetilde{f}
exists almost everywhere (with respect to
\Theta
) on
\partial\Omega,\,\,\widetilde{f}\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
and
\left\|{f}\right\|_{H^{2}}=\|\widetilde{f}\|_{L^{2}}

[
43
, p. 126]
. We identify
f
and
\widetilde{f},
henceforth, no distinction will be made between these two realizations. Let
\widetilde{P}
be the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
onto
H^{2}(\Omega).
Thus, there is an embedding of
H^{2}(\Omega)
into
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
as a closed subspace
[
28
]
,
[
26
, p. 526, Theorem 6]
. Moreover,
H^{2}(\Omega)
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space and its reproducing kernel
S_{\Omega}
is referred as the Szegö kernel of
\Omega.
For every
w\in\Omega,
the holomorphic function
S_{\Omega}(\cdot,w)
is in
H^{2}(\Omega).
Following
[
43
, p. 126]
, we have

\displaystyle(\widetilde{P}f)(z)=\langle f,S_{\Omega}(\cdot,z)\rangle_{L^{2}}

( 2.4)

for every
f\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega),\ \Omega
being an irreducible bounded symmetric domain. A proof can be found in
[
43
, Section 2.9]
, see also
[
18
]
. Further, if
\Omega=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i},
where each
\Omega_{i}
is an irreducible bounded symmetric domain, then
H^{2}(\Omega)
can be naturally identified with
\otimes_{i=1}^{n}H^{2}(\Omega_{i}).
The Szegö kernel
S_{\Omega}
of
H^{2}(\Omega)
is taken to be the reproducing kernel
\prod_{i=1}^{n}S_{\Omega_{i}}
of

\otimes_{i=1}^{n}H^{2}(\Omega_{i}),
that is,

\displaystyle S_{\Omega}(z,w)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}S_{\Omega_{i}}(z_{i},w_{i}),

where
z_{i},w_{i}\in\Omega_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and Equation (
2.4
) holds.

2.3.
Toeplitz Operators

For
u\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega),
the Laurent operator
M_{u}:L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
and the Toeplitz operator
T_{u}:H^{2}(\Omega)\to H^{2}(\Omega)
are defined by

M_{u}f=uf\text{~and~}T_{u}=\widetilde{P}M_{u},

respectively,
\widetilde{P}
being the orthogonal projection from
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
onto
H^{2}(\Omega).
From Equation (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle(T_{u}f)(z)=\langle uf,S_{\Omega}(\cdot,z)\rangle_{L^{2}}.

( 2.5)

We prove a lemma, one of whose immediate consequence is the fact that the linear map
u\mapsto T_{u}
from
L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
into
\mathcal{B}(H^{2}(\Omega))
is isometric,
\mathcal{B}(H^{2}(\Omega))
being the algebra of all bounded operators on
H^{2}(\Omega).
We follow the ideas from the proof of
[
12
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Suppose that
\Omega
is an irreducible bounded symmetric domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and the Toeplitz operator
T_{u}
is invertible in
\mathcal{B}(H^{2}(\Omega)).
Then
u
is invertible in
L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega).

Proof.

Let
h(z,w):=\langle z,w\rangle
and
\psi
be a non-negative measurable function on
\mathbb{C}^{n}.
For
z\in\partial\Omega,
let
F(\xi)=\psi(h(z,\xi))
for
\xi\in\partial\Omega.
Since
d\Theta
is
I_{\Omega}(0)
-invariant,
\int_{\partial\Omega}F(\xi)d\Theta(\xi)
is independent of
z.
For
k\geq 1,
let

a_{k}=\int_{\partial\Omega}|1+h(z,\xi)|^{2k}d\Theta(\xi)

and note that
a_{k}
is independent of
z.

We observe that for a fixed
z\in\partial\Omega,
the function
h(z,w)
has the only peak point at
w=z
in the Šilov boundary of
\Omega.
That is,
h(z,z)=1
for
z\in\partial\Omega
and

|h(z,w)|<1\text{~~for every~~}w\in\partial\Omega,\,\ w\neq z.

So there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
z
in
\partial\Omega
such that

\frac{1}{a_{k}}\int_{\partial\Omega\setminus U}|1+h(z,\xi)|^{2k}d\Theta(\xi)\rightarrow 0\text{ as }k\rightarrow\infty.

It follows that

\frac{1}{a_{k}}\int_{\partial\Omega}g(\xi)|1+h(z,\xi)|^{2k}d\Theta(\xi)\rightarrow g(z)\text{~~as~~}k\rightarrow\infty

for a continuous function
g
on
\partial\Omega.
Since
T_{u}
is invertible, there exists an
\epsilon>0
such that

\left\|{T_{u}f}\right\|\geq\epsilon\left\|{f}\right\|\text{~~for every~~}f\in H^{2}(\Omega).

In particular, for
f_{k}(z)=(1+h(z,\xi))^{k}
this gives
\left\|{T_{u}f_{k}}\right\|^{2}\geq\epsilon^{2}a_{k}.
For any positive valued continuous function
g,
it follows that

\frac{1}{a_{k}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{\partial\Omega}|u(z)|^{2}g(\xi)|1+h(z,\xi)|^{2k}d\Theta(\xi)d\Theta(z)\geq\epsilon^{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}g(\xi)d\Theta(\xi).

An application of Fubini’s theorem yields

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u(z)|^{2}g(z)d\Theta(z)\geq\epsilon^{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}g(\xi)d\Theta(\xi).

Since this inequality holds for every positive continuous function
g,
we have
|u(z)|^{2}\geq\epsilon^{2}
almost everywhere on
\partial\Omega.
This completes the proof.
∎

As a consequence, we have the following corollary. A proof along the line of
[
15
]
is included for the sake of completeness.

Corollary 2.12
.

If
\Omega
is an irreducible bounded symmetric domain, then
u\mapsto T_{u}
is a
*
-linear isometry of
L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
into
\mathcal{B}(H^{2}(\Omega)).

Proof.

We only prove
\|T_{u}\|=\|u\|_{\infty},
as the proof of
*
-linearity is trivial.
Since
T_{u}-\lambda=T_{u-\lambda}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C},
it follows from Lemma
2.11
that
{\mathrm{Spec}}(M_{u})\subseteq{\mathrm{Spec}}(T_{u}),
here
{\mathrm{Spec}}(T)
denotes the spectrum of
T.
Thus,

\mathcal{R}(u)={\mathrm{Spec}}(M_{u})\subseteq{\mathrm{Spec}}(T_{u}),

where
\mathcal{R}(u)
is the essential range of
u.
Therefore,

\|u\|_{\infty}\geq\|T_{u}\|\geq\text{spectral radius of}~~T_{u}\geq\sup\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\mathcal{R}(u)\}=\|u\|_{\infty}.

This completes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle S_{\varrho}(z,w)=(\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}S_{\Omega})(z,w)=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma^{-1})S_{\Omega}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot z,w).

( 2.9)

2.4.1.
One-dimensional representations

Since the one-dimensional representations of
G
play an important role in our discussion, we elaborate on some relevant results for the same. We denote the set of equivalence classes of the one-dimensional representations of
G
by
\widehat{G}_{1}.

A hyperplane
H
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is called reflecting if there exists a complex reflection in
G
acting trivially on
H.
For a complex reflection
\sigma\in G,
let
H_{\sigma}:=\ker(I_{n}-\sigma).
By definition, the subspace
H_{\sigma}
has dimension
n-1.
Clearly,
\sigma
fixes the hyperplane
H_{\sigma}
pointwise. Hence each
H_{\sigma}
is a reflecting hyperplane. By definition,
H_{\sigma}
is the zero set of a non-zero homogeneous linear polynomial
L_{\sigma}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, determined up to a non-zero constant multiple, that is,

H_{\sigma}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}:L_{\sigma}(z)=0\}.

Moreover, the elements of
G
acting trivially on a reflecting hyperplane forms a cyclic subgroup of
G.

Let
H_{1},\ldots,H_{t}
be the distinct reflecting hyperplanes associated to the group
G
and the corresponding cyclic subgroups be
G_{1},\ldots,G_{t},
respectively. Suppose
G_{i}=\langle a_{i}\rangle
and the order of each
a_{i}
is
m_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,t.
For every one-dimensional representation
\varrho
of
G,
there exists a unique
t
-tuple of non-negative integers
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}),
where
c_{i}

\displaystyle\varrho(a_{i})=\big(\det(a_{i})\big)^{c_{i}},\,\,i=1,\ldots,t.

( 2.11)

The
t
-tuple
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{t})
solely depends on the representation
\varrho.

For
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1},
the character of
\varrho,

\chi_{\varrho}:G\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
coincides with the representation
\varrho.
The set of elements of
H^{2}(\Omega)
relative to the one-dimensional representation
\varrho
is given by

\displaystyle R^{G}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))=\{f\in H^{2}(\Omega):\sigma(f)=\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma)f~{\rm for~~all~}\sigma\in G\}.

( 2.12)

The elements of the subspace
R^{G}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
are said to be
\varrho
-invariant functions. We recall a lemma concerning the
\varrho
-invariant functions which is going to be useful in the sequel.

Lemma 2.15
.

[
21
]

Suppose that the linear polynomial
\ell_{i}
is a defining function of
H_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,t

\ell_{\varrho}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\ell_{i}^{c_{i}}

’s are unique non-negative integers as described in Equation (

for a holomorphic function
\widetilde{f}
on
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega).

The

\displaystyle{\rm sgn}(\sigma)=(\det(\sigma))^{-1},\,\,\,\,\,\sigma\in G.

( 2.13)

Moreover, we note from Equation (

{\rm sgn}(a_{i})=(\det(a_{i}))^{-1}=\big(\det(a_{i})\big)^{m_{i}-1},\,\,i=1,\ldots,t,

Corollary 2.16
.

[
41
, p. 616, Lemma]

Let
H_{1},\ldots,H_{t}

G

of

\displaystyle R^{G}_{\varrho}(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))=\{f\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega):\sigma(f)=\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma)f~{\rm a.e.~~for~~all~}\sigma\in G\}.

3.
The Hardy space

Let
\Omega
be a bounded symmetric domain and a
G
-space for a finite complex reflection group
G.
We define a notion of Hardy space on
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
motivated by
[
35
]
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}
being a basic polynomial mapping associated to the group
G.

For
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1},
let
c_{\varrho}
denote the norm of the polynomial
\ell_{\varrho}
(cf. Lemma
2.15
) in
H^{2}(\Omega).
By Lemma
2.15
and Remark
2.17
, each
g\in\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
can be written as
g=\frac{1}{c_{\varrho}}\ell_{\varrho}\cdot(\widehat{g}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})
for a unique holomorphic function
\widehat{g}
on
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega).

Let
\widehat{\Gamma}_{\varrho}:\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))\to\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
be defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{\Gamma}_{\varrho}g=\widehat{g}.

Let
H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)):=\widehat{\Gamma}_{\varrho}(\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))).

Since
\widehat{\Gamma}_{\varrho}
is linear and injective by construction,
H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
can be made into a Hilbert space by borrowing the inner product from
H^{2}(\Omega),
that is,

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{h},\widehat{g}\rangle_{H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))}=\langle\widehat{\Gamma}_{\varrho}h,\widehat{\Gamma}_{\varrho}g\rangle_{H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))}:=\langle h,g\rangle_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\,\,\text{for all}\,\,h,g\in\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)).

This makes the map
\widehat{\Gamma}_{\varrho}:\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))\to H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)),
a unitary. Thus, for a holomorphic function
f:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{C},

\displaystyle\left\|{f}\right\|_{\varrho}^{2}:=\langle f,f\rangle_{H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))}=\frac{1}{c^{2}_{\varrho}}\Big({\rm sup}_{0<r<1}\int_{\partial\Omega}|(f\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(rt)|^{2}|\ell_{\varrho}(rt)|^{2}d\Theta(t)\Big).

( 3.1)

Clearly,
\left\|{1}\right\|_{\varrho}=1.
If
\Omega=\mathbb{D}^{n},
then for our choices of
\ell_{\rm sgn}\equiv J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\ell_{\rm tr}\equiv 1,
we get
c_{\rm sgn}=\sqrt{|G|}
and
c_{\rm tr}=1.
Since for every non-zero constant
c,\,\,c\ell_{\varrho}
will satisfy Lemma
2.15
, so one can adjust
c_{\varrho}
accordingly and consider it always to be equal to
\sqrt{|G|}
(with an appropriate moderation in the choice of
\ell_{\varrho}
).

In summary, associated to each one-dimensional representation
\varrho
of
G,
the Hilbert space
H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)):=\{f:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{C}\text{ holomorphic and }\left\|{f}\right\|_{\varrho}<\infty\}.

The Hilbert space
H^{2}_{\rm sgn}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
associated to the sign representation of
G
is defined to be the Hardy space on
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
and is denoted by
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)).

Definition 3.1
.

The Hardy space on
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
is defined by

Lemma 3.5
.

For every

Lemma 3.6
.

For every fixed
w\in\Omega,
there is a holomorphic function
S_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\theta}(w))\in H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
such that
\Gamma_{\varrho}^{h}:H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))\to\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
satisfies

\Gamma_{\varrho}^{h}:S_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\theta}(w))\mapsto c_{\varrho}\frac{S_{\varrho}(\cdot,w)}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\ell_{\varrho}(w)\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu},

where
S_{\varrho}
is the reproducing kernel of
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
and
c_{\varrho}=\left\|{\ell_{\varrho}}\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}.
Moreover, the function
S_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\times\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{C}
is the reproducing kernel for
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)).

Proof.

By the Kolmogorov decomposition of the reproducing kernel
S_{\varrho}
, there exists a function
F:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)),{\mathbb{C}})\text{~~such that~~}

S_{\varrho}(z,w)=F(z)F(w)^{*}\text{~~for~~}z,w\in\Omega

[
2
, Theorem 2.62]
, where
\mathcal{B}(X,Y)
denotes the space of all bounded linear operators from
X
into
Y.
We note that
F(z)=\textnormal{ev}_{z}
satisfies the requirement, where
\textnormal{ev}_{z}:f\mapsto f(z)
is the evaluation functional at
z
. Thus,
F
is a holomorphic function from
\Omega
into
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)),{\mathbb{C}})
such that

F(z)h=h(z)\text{~~for~~}z\in\Omega\text{~~and~~}h\in\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)).

For a fixed
w\in\Omega,
the analytic version of the Chevalley-Shephard-Todd theorem in
[
7
, Theorem 3.2, Theorem 3.12]
yields the following representation of the kernel function

S_{\varrho}(z,w)=\ell_{\varrho}(z)\widehat{S}_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),w),

where
\widehat{S}_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),w)
is a unique
G
-invariant holomorphic function in
z
and is anti-holomorphic function in
w.

Let
G=\{\alpha_{i}:i=1,\ldots,d\}
and
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}
be a basis of the module
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
over the ring
\mathbb{C}[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}].
Without loss of generality, we assume that
p_{1}=\ell_{\varrho}
and invoking
[
7
, Lemma 3.11]
write the following expression of
\widehat{S}_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),w):

\widehat{S}_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),w)=\frac{\det\big(\Lambda^{(1)}_{1}F(z)\big)}{\det\Lambda(z)}F(w)^{*},

where
\Lambda(z)=\big(\!\!\big(\big(\alpha_{i}\big(p_{j}(z)\big)\big)\!\!\big)_{i,j=1}^{d}
and
\Lambda^{(1)}_{1}F(z)
is the matrix
\Lambda(z)
with its first column replaced by the column
\Big(\big(\!\!\big(\alpha_{i}(F(z))\big)\!\!\big)_{i=1}^{d}\Big)^{\rm tr}.
This implies that
F_{1}(z)=\frac{\det\big(\Lambda^{(1)}_{1}F(z)\big)}{\det\Lambda(z)}
is in
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)\!),{\mathbb{C}}).
Hence
F_{1}(z)^{*}\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)\!))
and so there exists
h\in\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
satisfying
F_{1}(z)^{*}1=h.
Thus,

\overline{\widehat{S}_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),w)}=F(w)F_{1}(z)^{*}1=h(w).

So, for a fixed

the function
w\mapsto\overline{\widehat{S}_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),w)}
is in
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)).
Now another application of
[
7
, Theorem 3.2, Theorem 3.12]
to
\overline{\widehat{S}_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),w)}
(as a function of
w
) yields:

\displaystyle S_{\varrho}(z,w)=\ell_{\varrho}(z)\widetilde{S}_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),\boldsymbol{\theta}(w))\overline{\ell_{\varrho}(w)},

( 3.3)

where
\widetilde{S}_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),\boldsymbol{\theta}(w))
is unique and holomorphic function in
z,
anti-holomorphic in
w.

Let
S_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),\boldsymbol{\theta}(w)):=c_{\varrho}^{2}\widetilde{S}_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),\boldsymbol{\theta}(w)).
For a fixed
w,
it follows from the definition that
\left\|{S_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\theta}(w))}\right\|_{\varrho}<\infty.
We complete the proof by showing the reproducing property of
S_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\times\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{C}.
If

then

\displaystyle\langle f,S_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\theta}(w))\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{\varrho}^{h}f,\Gamma_{\varrho}^{h}S_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\theta}(w))\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\frac{1}{c_{\varrho}}\ell_{\varrho}(f\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}),c_{\varrho}\ell_{\varrho}\widetilde{S}_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\cdot),\boldsymbol{\theta}(w))\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\ell_{\varrho}(f\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}),\frac{S_{\varrho}(\cdot,w)}{\overline{\ell_{\varrho}(w)}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\theta}(w)),

where the last equality follows from Equation (
3.3
) and the reproducing property of
S_{\varrho}(\cdot,w).

From Equation (

\displaystyle S_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),\boldsymbol{\theta}(w))=\frac{c_{\varrho}^{2}}{|G|}\frac{1}{\ell_{\varrho}(z)\overline{\ell_{\varrho}(w)}}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma^{-1})S_{\Omega}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot z,w),

where

of

is called the Szegö kernel of
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega).
Explicit formulae for the Szegö kernels for different choices of

]

Remark 3.9
.

We would like to point out that the definition of
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
is independent of the choice of the basic polynomial map
\boldsymbol{\theta}
associated to
G.

•

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(\Omega)
be another basic polynomial mapping associated to the group
G.
Since there is a biholomorphic map
\boldsymbol{h}:\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\to\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(\Omega),
that is,
\boldsymbol{h}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime},
it follows from the chain rule and Corollary
2.16
that
J_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z))=c
for all
z\in\Omega,
where
c
is some non-zero constant. The linear map
U:H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(\Omega))\to H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
defined by
U(f)=c\cdot(f\circ\boldsymbol{h})
is a unitary. In fact,

S_{{\rm sgn},\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),\boldsymbol{\theta}(w))=|c|^{2}S_{{\rm sgn},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(z),\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(w))\text{~~for~~}z,w\in\Omega.

Therefore,
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(\Omega))
and
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
are isometrically isomorphic to each other.

•

Let
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1}
be a representation that is not isomorphic to the sign representation. Following analogous arguments as above, one can show that the definition of
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
is independent of the choice of
\boldsymbol{\theta}.
By the analytic Chevalley-Shephard-Todd theorem
[
7
, Theorem 3.12]
, every element
f\in\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
can be expressed as

f=\ell_{\varrho}\cdot(g\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})=\ell_{\varrho}\cdot(g\circ\boldsymbol{h}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}).

We note from Proposition
3.7
that
g\circ\boldsymbol{h}\in H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
and
g\in H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(\Omega)).
Since the map
U_{\varrho}:H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(\Omega))\to H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
defined by
U_{\varrho}(g)=g\circ\boldsymbol{h}
is a unitary,
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(\Omega))
and
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
are isomorphically isometric. In other words,
U_{\varrho}=\Gamma_{2}^{*}\Gamma_{1},
where
\Gamma_{1}:H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(\Omega))\to\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
and
\Gamma_{2}:H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))\to\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
are the unitary operators in Lemma
3.5
. Moreover,

S_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(z),\boldsymbol{\theta}(w))=S_{\varrho,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(z),\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(w))\text{~~for~~}z,w\in\Omega.

To eliminate any ambiguity in the two points mentioned above, we note that since we always choose
\ell_{\rm sgn}=J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},
it follows that
\left\|{1}\right\|_{H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(\Omega))}=c\left\|{1}\right\|_{H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))}.
So we had to adjust the operator
U
with a constant to make it an isometry. However, for any other one-dimensional representation
\varrho
, we do not choose different
\ell_{\varrho}
’s for
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(\Omega))
and
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
, so in the description of
U_{\varrho}
no adjustment is needed.

3.4.
Analytic Hilbert Module

Suppose
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a bounded symmetric domain. It is a well-known fact that
H^{2}(\Omega)
is an analytic Hilbert module over
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}].
We observe that this rich structure can be transferred to
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
in a proper setting.

We first recall two definitions from
[
16
]
. A Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is said to be a
H
ilbert module over an algebra
\mathcal{A}
if the map

(f,h)\mapsto f\cdot h,\,\,\,\,f\in\mathcal{A},h\in\mathcal{H},

defines an algebra homomorphism
f\mapsto T_{f}
of
\mathcal{A}
into
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}),
where
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is the algebra of bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
and
T_{f}
is the bounded operator defined by
T_{f}h=f\cdot h.

Definition 3.12
.

A Hilbert module
\mathcal{H}
(consisting of complex-valued holomorphic functions on
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
) over the polynomial algebra
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
is said to be an analytic Hilbert module if

(1)

\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
and

(2)

\mathcal{H}
possesses a reproducing kernel on
\Omega.

The module action in an analytic Hilbert module is given by pointwise multiplication, that is, for every
p\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
the module action is

{\mathfrak{m}}_{p}(h)(z)=p(z){h}(z),\,h\in\mathcal{H}\text{ and }z\in\Omega.

Since
H^{2}(\Omega)
is an analytic Hilbert module over the polynomial algebra, each multiplication operator
M_{\theta_{i}}:H^{2}(\Omega)\to H^{2}(\Omega)
is bounded for
i=1,\ldots,n.
Moreover,
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
is a Hilbert module over the polynomial algebra
\mathbb{C}[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}]
for every
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1}.

For
i=1,\ldots,n
and
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1},
let
M_{i}:H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))\to H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
be the
i
-th coordinate multiplication operator. The unitary
\Gamma_{\varrho}^{h}
defined in Equation (
3.2
) intertwines
(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
on
H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
and
(M_{\theta_{1}},\ldots,M_{\theta_{n}})
on
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)).

Further,
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}])=\ell_{\varrho}\cdot\mathbb{C}[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}]

[
36
]
and is dense in
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)).
This implies that
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
is dense in
H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
and leads to the following result.

Proposition 3.13
.

For every
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1},
the reproducing kernel Hilbert space
H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
is an analytic Hilbert module on
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
over
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}].
Moreover,
H_{\varrho}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
over
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
is unitraily equivalent to the Hilbert module
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
over
\mathbb{C}[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}].

3.5.
Equivalence of spaces

For every
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1},
let
d\Theta_{\varrho}
be the measure supported on the Šilov boundary
\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
obtained from the following equality:

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)}fd\Theta_{\varrho}=\int_{\partial\Omega}(f\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})|\ell_{\varrho}|^{2}d\Theta,

( 3.5)

where
\ell_{\varrho}
is as defined in Lemma
2.15
. The
L^{2}
-space on
\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
with respect to the measure

is given by

\displaystyle L^{2}_{\varrho}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))=\{f:\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{C}~\mbox{measurable}~|\int_{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)}|f|^{2}d\Theta_{\varrho}<\infty\}.

In the next couple of lemmas we realize
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
as a closed subspace of
L^{2}_{\varrho}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)).

Lemma 3.14
.

For every one-dimensional representation
\varrho
of
G,
the space
L^{2}_{\varrho}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
is isometrically isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(L^{2}(\partial\Omega)).

Proof.

The linear map
\Gamma_{\varrho}^{\ell}:L^{2}_{\varrho}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))\to\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))
defined by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\varrho}^{\ell}f=\frac{1}{c_{\varrho}}\ell_{\varrho}\cdot(f\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})

( 3.6)

is an isometry. For
\phi\in\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(L^{2}(\partial\Omega)),
Remark
2.17
guarantees the existence of
\widehat{\phi}
satisfying
\phi=\frac{1}{c_{\varrho}}\ell_{\varrho}\cdot(\widehat{\phi}\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}).
Clearly,
\|\widehat{\phi}\|=\|\phi\|
and
\widehat{\phi}\in L^{2}_{\varrho}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)).
Hence
\Gamma_{\varrho}^{\ell}
is a unitary. ∎

Lemma 3.15
.

For every one-dimensional representation
\varrho
of
G,

H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
is isometrically embedded in
L^{2}_{\varrho}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)).

Proof.

There is an isometric isomorphism of
H^{2}(\Omega)
onto a closed subspace of
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)

[
28
]
,
[
26
, p. 526, Theorem 6]
. More precisely, from
[
26
, p. 526, Theorem 6]
, it is clear that the isometric isomorphism sends a function of

to its radial limit. Moreover, the discussion in Subsection 2.4 implies that if

then its radial limit function is in
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(L^{2}(\partial\Omega)).

If
\widehat{i}_{\varrho}:\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))\to\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))
denotes the isometric embedding, then it follows that the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))}

of

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L_{\varrho}^{\infty}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))}

where

It evidently follows, since for every

Let

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)):=\{u:\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{C}~~\text{measurable}:u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)^{G}\}.

( 3.7)

If

then
u\in L_{\varrho}^{\infty}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
for every

.

If
u\in L^{\infty}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)),

on

on

4.
Toeplitz operators

We start this section with the definition of Toeplitz operator on
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)).
Let
P_{\varrho}:L^{2}_{\varrho}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))\to H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
be the orthogonal projection.

Definition 4.1
.

For
u\in L^{\infty}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)),
the Toeplitz operator
T_{u}
is defined on
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
by

\displaystyle T_{u}=P_{\varrho}M_{u}.

( 4.1)

The next lemma allows us the privilege of going back and forth between the operator
T_{u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}}|_{\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))}
(cf. Equation (
2.10
)) and the Toeplitz operator
T_{u}
on
{H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))}.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
u\in L^{\infty}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)),
then the Toeplitz operator
T_{u}
on
{H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))}
is unitarily equivalent to the restriction of
T_{\widetilde{u}}
to
{\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))}
for every
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1},
where
\widetilde{u}=u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}.

Proof.

The operator
T_{\widetilde{u}}:\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))\to\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
is given by the formula (cf. Equation (
2.10
))

T_{\widetilde{u}}(f)=\langle\widetilde{u}f,S_{\varrho}(\cdot,z)\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)},

where
S_{\varrho}
denotes the reproducing kernel of the subspace
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
cf. Equation (
2.9
). For
f\in\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
and
z\in\Omega,
it follows that (cf. Equation (
3.6
) and (
3.2
))

0=\langle uf-P_{\varrho}(uf),\Gamma_{\varrho}^{\ell*}S_{\varrho}(\cdot,z)\rangle=\langle\Gamma_{\varrho}^{\ell}(uf-P_{\varrho}(uf)),S_{\varrho}(\cdot,z)\rangle=\langle\Gamma_{\varrho}^{\ell}(uf)-\Gamma_{\varrho}^{h}P_{\varrho}(uf),S_{\varrho}(\cdot,z)\rangle.

If
f\in H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
then
\Gamma_{\varrho}^{\ell}(uf)=(u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})\Gamma_{\varrho}^{h}(f).
Therefore,

\displaystyle(\Gamma_{\varrho}^{h}T_{u}f)(z)=(\Gamma_{\varrho}^{h}P_{\varrho}(uf))(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{\varrho}^{h}P_{\varrho}(uf),S_{\varrho}(\cdot,z)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{\varrho}^{\ell}(uf),S_{\varrho}(\cdot,z)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})\Gamma_{\varrho}^{h}(f),S_{\varrho}(\cdot,z)\rangle=(T_{\widetilde{u}}\Gamma_{\varrho}^{h}f)(z),

whence the proof follows.
∎

Let
\tr:G\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
denote the trivial representation of the group
G.
For any one-dimensional representation
\varrho\in\widehat{G}
and
f\in\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega)),
it follows that

\ell_{\varrho}f\in\ell_{\varrho}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega))\subseteq R^{G}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))=\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)).

The density of
G
-invariant polynomials in
\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega))
implies that
\ell_{\varrho}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega))
is dense in
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)).

The following lemma identifies a crucial invariant subspace for the operator
T_{\widetilde{u}}.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
\widetilde{u}\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
is a
G
-invariant function, then the restriction of the operator
T_{\widetilde{u}}
on
{\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))}
leaves the subspace
\ell_{\varrho}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega))
invariant for every
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1}.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega))
be such that
f=\ell_{\varrho}f_{\varrho}
for
f_{\varrho}\in\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega)).
An appeal to Remark
2.17
shows that
\widetilde{u}f_{\varrho}\in\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))
and the following holds:

\displaystyle(T_{\widetilde{u}}f)(z)=\langle\widetilde{u}f,S_{\Omega}(\cdot,z)\rangle=\langle\widetilde{u}f_{\varrho},M^{*}_{\ell_{\varrho}}S_{\Omega}(\cdot,z)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell_{\varrho}(z)\langle\widetilde{u}f_{\varrho},S_{\Omega}(\cdot,z)\rangle

( 4.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell_{\varrho}(z)\langle\widetilde{u}f_{\varrho},S_{\tr}(\cdot,z)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell_{\varrho}(z)\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{u}f_{\varrho})(z),

where
S_{\tr}
denotes the reproducing kernel of
\mathbb{P}_{\rm tr}(H^{2}(\Omega)).
Therefore, we conclude that

T_{\widetilde{u}}(\ell_{\varrho}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega))\subseteq\ell_{\varrho}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega))\text{~~for every~~}\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1}.

∎

Remark 4.4
.

The conclusion of the preceding lemma can be extended to any representation
\varrho\in\widehat{G}
with
{\rm deg}(\varrho)>1.

Since
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}])
is a free module over
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]^{G}
of rank
({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}

[
36
]
, there is a basis
\{\ell_{\varrho,i}:i=1,\ldots,({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}\}
of
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}])
as a free module over
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]^{G}.
By the density of
\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\cdot\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]^{G}
in
\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\rm tr}(H^{2}(\Omega))
and the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\rm tr}(H^{2}(\Omega))
is contained in
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)),
it follows that
\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\rm tr}(H^{2}(\Omega))
is dense in
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)).
For
f=\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}f_{\varrho,i},
such that
f_{\varrho,i}\in\mathbb{P}_{\rm tr}(H^{2}(\Omega)),
it follows that:

\displaystyle(T_{\widetilde{u}}f)(z)=\langle\widetilde{u}f,S_{\Omega}(\cdot,z)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\widetilde{u}f_{\varrho,i},S_{\Omega}(\cdot,z)\rangle

( 4.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\langle\widetilde{u}f_{\varrho,i},M^{*}_{\ell_{\varrho,i}}S_{\Omega}(\cdot,z)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}(z)\langle\widetilde{u}f_{\varrho,i},S_{\Omega}(\cdot,z)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}(z)\langle\widetilde{u}f_{\varrho,i},S_{\tr}(\cdot,z)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}(z)\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{u}f_{\varrho,i})(z).

Hence
\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\rm tr}(H^{2}(\Omega))
is left invariant by the operator
T_{\widetilde{u}}
for every
\varrho\in\widehat{G}.

For a
G
-invariant function
\widetilde{u}\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega),
if
T_{\widetilde{u}}=0
on
H^{2}(\Omega),
then
T_{\widetilde{u}}=0
on
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)).
So
T_{u}=0
on
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
for every
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1}
by Lemma
4.2
. It is interesting to note that the converse also holds.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
u\in L^{\infty}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
and
\widetilde{u}=u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}.
If
T_{u}=0
on
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
for some
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1},
then
T_{\widetilde{u}}=0
on
H^{2}(\Omega).

Proof.

Lemma
4.2
shows that
T_{\widetilde{u}}=0
on
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)).
It follows from Equation (
4.2
) that

0=T_{\widetilde{u}}(\ell_{\varrho}f)=\ell_{\varrho}\widetilde{P}_{\tr}({\widetilde{u}}f){~~for~~every~~}f\in\mathbb{P}_{\rm tr}(H^{2}(\Omega)).

Since
\ell_{\varrho}
vanishes on a measure zero subset of
\Omega,

\widetilde{P}_{\tr}({\widetilde{u}}f)=0
for every
f\in\mathbb{P}_{\rm tr}(H^{2}(\Omega)).
If
f=\sum_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}f_{\varrho,i}
for
f_{\varrho,i}\in\mathbb{P}_{\rm tr}(H^{2}(\Omega)).
Then

T_{\widetilde{u}}(f)=\sum_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{u}f_{\varrho,i})=0.

Since
\sum_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\rm tr}(H^{2}(\Omega))
is dense in
H^{2}(\Omega),
the proof is complete.
∎

As a consequence of Corollary
2.12
,
T_{\widetilde{u}}=0
on
H^{2}(\Omega)
implies that
\widetilde{u}=0
whenever
\Omega
is an irreducible bounded symmetric domain. An analogous result holds for
T_{\widetilde{u}}
on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
as well. This leads to the following interesting conclusion.

Corollary 4.6
.

There is a natural
*
-linear embedding of
L^{\infty}(\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
into
\mathcal{B}(H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)))
given by
u\mapsto T_{u},
whenever
\Omega
is an irreducible bounded symmetric domain or the unit polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{n}.

Proof.

It suffices to show the following: If for
u\in L^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\partial\Omega)),

T_{u}=0
on
H^{2}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\partial\Omega))
for some
\varrho\in\widehat{G}_{1},
then
u=0
almost everywhere.

The hypothesis along with Proposition
4.5
yields
T_{\widetilde{u}}=0
on
H^{2}(\Omega),
consequently,
\widetilde{u}=u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}=0
.
∎

4.1.
Multiplicative properties

Lemma 4.7
.

If
\varrho\in\widehat{G}
and
\widetilde{u},\widetilde{v},\widetilde{q}\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
are
G
-invariant, then the following statements hold:

1.

If
T_{\widetilde{u}}T_{\widetilde{v}}=T_{\widetilde{q}}
on
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)),
then
T_{\widetilde{u}}T_{\widetilde{v}}=T_{\widetilde{q}}
on
H^{2}(\Omega).

2.

If
T_{\widetilde{u}}T_{\widetilde{v}}=T_{\widetilde{v}}T_{\widetilde{u}}
on
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)),
then
T_{\widetilde{u}}T_{\widetilde{v}}=T_{\widetilde{v}}T_{\widetilde{u}}
on
H^{2}(\Omega).

Proof.

Consider an element
f=\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\widehat{f}_{\varrho,i}
in
\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}(H^{2}(\Omega)),
where
\widehat{f}_{\varrho,i}\in\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega)).
Then
T_{\widetilde{u}}T_{\widetilde{v}}f=T_{\widetilde{q}}f
along with Equation (
4.3
) implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{u}~\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{v}\widehat{f}_{\varrho,i}))=\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{q}\widehat{f}_{\varrho,i}).

In case,
\varrho
is one-dimensional,
\ell_{\varrho,1}=\ell_{\varrho}
and thus for some
\widehat{f}_{\varrho}\in\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega)),

\ell_{\varrho}\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{u}~\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{v}\widehat{f}_{\varrho})=\ell_{\varrho}\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{q}\widehat{f}_{\varrho}).
This is equivalent to showing that

\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{u}~\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{v}\widehat{f}))=\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{q}\widehat{f})\,\,\text{~for every~}\widehat{f}\in\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega)).

If
{\rm deg}\varrho>1,
we take
\widehat{f}_{\varrho,i}=0
for
i=2,\ldots,({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}
and then repeat the argument analogous as above to arrive at the same conclusion. Therefore, the equality
T_{\widetilde{u}}T_{\widetilde{v}}=T_{\widetilde{q}}
holds on the dense subset
\sum_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\rm tr}(H^{2}(\Omega))
of
H^{2}(\Omega)
which proves the first claim.

Using Equation (
4.3
) and argument analogous as above one concludes that

\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{u}~\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{v}\widehat{f}))=\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{v}~\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{u}\widehat{f}))\,\,\text{~for every~}\widehat{f}\in\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega)).

Let
f=\sum_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}f_{\varrho,i},
for
f_{\varrho,i}\in\mathbb{P}_{\tr}(H^{2}(\Omega)),
then

\displaystyle T_{\widetilde{u}}T_{\widetilde{v}}f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{u}~\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{v}f_{\varrho,i}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\varrho\in\widehat{G}}\sum_{i=1}^{({\rm deg}\varrho)^{2}}\ell_{\varrho,i}\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{v}~\widetilde{P}_{\tr}(\widetilde{u}f_{\varrho,i}))=T_{\widetilde{v}}T_{\widetilde{u}}f

on a dense subset of
H^{2}(\Omega).
This completes the proof.
∎

.

of Lemma

is that if

.

4.2.
On proper images of the unit ball and the polydisc

Theorem
1.5
enables us to apply characterization of Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\Omega)
(for example, commuting or semi-commuting pairs etc.) to specify conditions for characterizing Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega))
(with the same property). The following results are an interesting depiction of it.

We start by recalling that a function
\phi
is called pluriharmonic in
\Omega
if

\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial z_{i}\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muz\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{j}}=0~\text{for all}~i,j=1,\ldots,n.

Definition 4.10
.

[
22
, Definition 5.3]

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a
G
-invariant domain and
\boldsymbol{\theta}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
be a basic polynomial map associated to the finite complex reflection group
G.
A function
\phi
defined on
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
is said to be
G
-pluriharmonic on
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
if

\phi\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a pluriharmonic function on
\Omega.

Suppose that
\widetilde{\phi}
is a pluriharmonic function on
\Omega.
Then we write
\phi\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}=\sum_{\sigma\in G}\widetilde{\phi}\circ\sigma
and
\phi
is a
G
-pluriharmonic function on
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega).

4.2.1.
For the unit ball

Recall that two Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
commute if and only if either both are analytic, or both are co-analytic, or one is a linear function of the other
[
8
, p. 98, Theorem 9]
. An analogous result for Toeplitz operators with bounded pluriharmonic symbols on
H^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
can be found in
[
44
, Theorem 2.2]
. We combine
[
44
, Theorem 2.2]
and Theorem
1.6
to conclude the following:

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
u
and
v
be two bounded
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
-pluriharmonic functions on
\mathcal{E}_{n}(m)
. Then
T_{u}T_{v}=T_{v}T_{u}

on the Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathcal{E}_{n}(m))
if and only if
u
and
v
satisfy one of the following conditions:

1.

Both
u
and
v
are holomorphic on
\mathcal{E}_{n}(m)
.

2.

Both
\overline{u}
and
\overline{v}
are holomorphic on
\mathcal{E}_{n}(m)
.

3.

Either
u
or
v
is constant on
\mathcal{E}_{n}(m)
.

4.

There is a nonzero constant
b
such that
u-bv
is constant on
\mathcal{E}_{n}(m)
.

4.2.2.
For the polydisc

We refer to the proper images of the open unit polydisc by
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}).
It is understood that
G
is a finite complex reflection group acting on
\mathbb{D}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}:\mathbb{D}^{n}\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
is a basic polynomial map associated to the group
G.

The description for commuting pairs and semi-commuting pairs of Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
can be found on
[
13
, p. 3336, Theorem 1.4]
and
[
25
, p. 176, Theorem 2.1]
, respectively. We combine it with Theorem
1.5
to classify commuting pairs and semi-commuting pairs of Toeplitz operators on

Notation 4.12
.

For
f,g\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),
we define
D_{i}(f,g):=\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\overline{z_{i}}},

Also,

Theorem 4.13
.

Let

Then
T_{u}T_{v}=T_{v}T_{u}
on

if and only if the following conditions hold:

1.

For almost all
\xi\in\mathbb{T},

D_{1}(u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta},v\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(z,\xi)=D_{1}(v\circ\boldsymbol{\theta},u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(z,\xi)
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}.

2.

For almost all
\xi\in\mathbb{T},

D_{2}(u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta},v\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(\xi,z)=D_{2}(v\circ\boldsymbol{\theta},u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(\xi,z)
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}.

3.

For every
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{D}^{2},

D_{1,2}(u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta},v\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(z_{1},z_{2})=D_{1,2}(v\circ\boldsymbol{\theta},u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(z_{1},z_{2}).

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
u,v\in L^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{2})).
Then
T_{u}T_{v}=T_{uv}
on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{2}))
if and only if the following conditions hold:

1.

For almost all
\xi\in\mathbb{T},

D_{1}(u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta},v\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(z,\xi)=0
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}.

2.

For almost all
\xi\in\mathbb{T},

D_{2}(u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta},v\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(\xi,z)=0
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}.

3.

For every
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{D},

D_{1,2}(u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta},v\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(z_{1},z_{2})=0.

Equivalently, we have the following from Theorem
1.5
and
[
25
, p. 176, Theorem 2.1]
.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
u,v\in L^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{2})).
Then
T_{u}T_{v}=T_{uv}
on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{2}))
if and only if for each
i=1,2;
either
\overline{u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}}
or
v\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
is holomorphic in
z_{i}.

[
31
, p. 190, Main Theorem]
provides a characterization of commuting pairs of Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n}).
One can apply Theorem
1.6
in combination with
[
31
, p. 190, Main Theorem]
to describe all commuting pairs of Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n})).
We close our discussion on multiplicative properties of Toeplitz operators on specific domains here. There is a vast literature in this direction for various bounded symmetric domains and using those results, the similar observations are possible for Toeplitz operators on their proper images as well.

4.3.
Brown-Halmos type characterization

We now specialize
\Omega
to be the open unit polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{n}
and prove a Brown-Halmos type characterization of Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n})),\,\,\boldsymbol{\theta}
being a basic polynomial mapping associated to
G(m.p,n),
where
m,n,p
are positive integers,
n>1
and
p
divides
m.
Let
q=m/p.
Recall form Example
2.4
that

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{i}(z_{1}^{m},\ldots,z_{n}^{m})\text{~for~}i=1,\ldots,n-1\,\,

\displaystyle\text{~and~}\,\,\theta_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(z_{1}\cdots z_{n})^{q}.

( 4.4)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\overline{\theta_{i}}(z)\theta_{n}^{p}(z)=\theta_{n-i}(z)\,\,\text{~for~}z\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\text{~and~}i=1,\ldots,n-1.

( 4.5)

Henceforth, for the sake of simplicity, we write
P:L^{2}_{\rm sgn}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))\to H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))
for the orthogonal projection. For
u\in L^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n})),
the Toeplitz operator
T_{u}:H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))\to H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))
is given by

T_{u}=PM_{u}.

For
\widetilde{u}=u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta},
the subspace
\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))
is reducing for the Laurent operator
M_{\widetilde{u}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n}).
We recall that the unitary
\Gamma_{\rm sgn}^{\ell}:L^{2}_{\rm sgn}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))\to\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))
intertwines the Laurent operators
M_{u}
and
M_{\widetilde{u}},
that is,
\Gamma_{\rm sgn}^{\ell}M_{u}=M_{\widetilde{u}}\Gamma_{\rm sgn}^{\ell}.
In particular, for the
i
-th coordinate multiplication
M_{i}
on
L^{2}_{\rm sgn}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n})),

\Gamma_{\rm sgn}^{\ell}M_{i}=M_{\theta_{i}}\Gamma_{\rm sgn}^{\ell},

where
M_{\theta_{i}}
denotes the multiplication operator by the polynomial
\theta_{i}
on
\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})).

Similarly, the unitary operator
\Gamma_{\rm sgn}^{h}:H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))\to\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))
intertwines
T_{u}
and
T_{\widetilde{u}}.
In particular,
If
T_{i}:H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))\to H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))
is the
i
-th coordinate multiplication defined by

(T_{i}f)(z)=z_{i}f(z)\text{ for }z\in\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}),

then
\Gamma_{\rm sgn}^{h}T_{i}=T_{\theta_{i}}\Gamma_{\rm sgn}^{h}.

Remark 4.16
.

We mostly use the notation
\theta_{i}
for denoting both the
i
-th component of the proper holomorphic map
\boldsymbol{\theta}:\Omega\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\Omega)
and the associated polynomial in
n
variables. Although while writing
M_{\theta_{i}},
it should be understood as the multiplication operator by the polynomial
\theta_{i},
without any ambiguity.

To prove the following results, we largely follow the constructions in
[
8
]
which was suitably adapted for several variables in
[
12
]
and
[
6
]
. We start with a couple of lemmas.

Lemma 4.17
.

For
i=1,\ldots,n-1,

M_{i}^{*}M_{n}^{p}=M_{n-i}
on
L^{2}_{\rm sgn}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))
and
T_{i}^{*}T_{n}^{p}=T_{n-i}
on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n})).

Proof.

The first part follows from Equation (
4.5
) and the unitary equivalence of
M_{i}|_{L^{2}_{\rm sgn}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))}
and
M_{\theta_{i}}|_{\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))}
. For the second part, we observe for
f,g\in H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))
that

\langle T_{i}^{*}T_{n}^{p}f,g\rangle=\langle T_{n}^{p}f,T_{i}g\rangle={\langle M_{n}^{p}f,M_{i}g\rangle}_{L^{2}}={\langle M_{i}^{*}M_{n}^{p}f,g\rangle}_{L^{2}}={\langle M_{n-i}f,g\rangle}_{L^{2}}=\langle T_{n-i}f,g\rangle.

∎

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
T
be a bounded operator on
L^{2}_{\rm sgn}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))
which commutes with
M_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n.
Then there exists a function
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))
such that
T=M_{\phi}.

Proof.

We first note that
(M_{1},\dots,M_{n})
is an
n
-tuple of commuting normal operators on
L^{2}_{\rm sgn}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))
. Therefore, the Taylor joint spectrum of
(M_{1},\dots,M_{n})
is
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n}).
Invoking the spectral theorem for commuting normal operators, it follows that the von Neumann algebra generated by
(M_{1},\dots,M_{n})
is given by the algebra
L^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n})).
Since it is a maximal von Neumann algebra, the commutant algebra of
(M_{1},\dots,M_{n})
is
*
-isomorphic to
L^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n})).
Hence the result follows.
∎

Let
T
be a bounded operator on
\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))
which commutes with
M_{\theta_{i}}
for
i=1,\ldots,n.
An appeal to Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.18
allows us to conclude that there exists a
G
-invariant function
\widetilde{\phi}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
such that
T=M_{\widetilde{\phi}}.

Now we are ready to prove a Brown-Halmos type characterization of Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n})),

\boldsymbol{\theta}
being a basic polynomial associated to the group
G(m,p,n)
satisfying Equation (
4.5
).

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
T=T_{\phi}
with
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n})).
Then

\langle T^{*}_{i}T_{\phi}T^{p}_{n}f,g\rangle=\langle T_{\phi}T^{p}_{n}f,T_{i}g\rangle=\langle M_{\phi}M_{n}^{p}f,M_{i}g\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle M^{*}_{i}M_{n}^{p}M_{\phi}f,g\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Consequently, it follows from Lemma
4.17
that

\langle T^{*}_{i}T_{\phi}T^{p}_{n}f,g\rangle=\langle M_{n-i}M_{\phi}f,g\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle PM_{\phi}M_{n-i}f,g\rangle_{H^{2}}=\langle T_{\phi}T_{n-i}f,g\rangle.

Since

\langle T^{*}_{n}T_{\phi}T_{n}f,g\rangle=\langle T_{\phi}T_{n}f,T_{n}g\rangle=\langle M_{\phi}M_{n}f,M_{n}g\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle M_{\phi}f,g\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle T_{\phi}f,g\rangle,

the second condition follows.

For the converse, we work on
\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})).
Depending on the group
G=G(m,p,n),
there exists a subset
\mathcal{I}_{G}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that

\{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}(z):=\sqrt{|G|}\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}z^{\boldsymbol{m}}:\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{I}_{G}\}

forms an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})),
cf. Example
2.13
. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{G,{\rm hol}}:=\mathcal{I}_{G}\cap\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}.

( 4.6)

The set
\{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}:\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{I}_{G,{\rm hol}}\}
forms an orthogonal basis for
\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n})).

Recall that
q=m/p.
Since for every
r\geq 0,

\theta_{n}^{r}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=(z_{1}\cdots z_{n})^{qr}
is a
G
-invariant polynomial,

\theta_{n}^{r}(z)\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}(z)=\sqrt{|G|}\theta_{n}^{r}(z)\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}z^{\boldsymbol{m}}=\sqrt{|G|}\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}\theta_{n}^{r}(z)z^{\boldsymbol{m}}=\sqrt{|G|}\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}z^{\boldsymbol{m}+q\boldsymbol{r}}=\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}+q\boldsymbol{r}}(z),

where
q\boldsymbol{r}
denotes the
n
-tuple
(qr,\ldots,qr).

By hypothesis and Lemma
4.2
, there exists a bounded operator
\widetilde{T}
on
\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))
which is unitarily equivalent to
T
on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))
and satisfies the conditions

T_{\theta_{i}}^{*}\widetilde{T}T_{\theta_{n}}^{p}=\widetilde{T}T_{\theta_{n-i}}\text{~~~~and~~~~}T_{\theta_{n}}^{*}\widetilde{T}T_{\theta_{n}}=\widetilde{T}.

Next, we show that
\widetilde{T}=T_{\widetilde{\phi}}
for a
G
-invariant symbol
\widetilde{\phi}
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{n}).
Clearly, for every non-negative integer
r,

T_{\theta_{n}}^{r*}\widetilde{T}T_{\theta_{n}}^{r}=\widetilde{T}.
Hence

\langle\widetilde{T}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle=\langle\widetilde{T}T_{\theta_{n}}^{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},T_{\theta_{n}}^{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle=\langle\widetilde{T}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}+q\boldsymbol{r}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}+q\boldsymbol{r}}\rangle\text{~~~~for every~~~~}r\geq 0\text{~~~~and~~~~}\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{I}_{G,{\rm hol}}.

For every non-negative integer
r,
we define an operator
A_{r}=M^{*r}_{\theta_{n}}\widetilde{T}\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}M^{r}_{\theta_{n}}
on
\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})),

\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}:\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))\to\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))
being the associated orthogonal projection. Note that

M_{\theta_{n}}^{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}}=\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}+q\boldsymbol{r}}\in\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))\text{~~for~~}\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{I}_{G}{~~and~~}r>0.

Moreover, for every
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{I}_{G},
there exists a sufficiently large
r
(depending on
\boldsymbol{p}
) such that

M_{\theta_{n}}^{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}}=\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}+q\boldsymbol{r}}\in\mathbb{P}_{{\rm sgn}}(H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n})).

For sufficiently large
r,
it follows that

\langle A_{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle=\langle\widetilde{T}\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}M^{r}_{\theta_{n}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},M^{r}_{\theta_{n}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle=\langle\widetilde{T}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}+q\boldsymbol{r}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}+q\boldsymbol{r}}\rangle\text{~~for~~}\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{I}_{G}.

Therefore, if
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are finite linear combinations of
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}
’s for
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{I}_{G},
then
\{\langle A_{r}\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\rangle\}
is convergent. Also, for every
r\geq 0,
we have
\left\|{A_{r}}\right\|\leq\left\|{A_{0}}\right\|=\|\widetilde{T}\|
which implies that
\{A_{r}\}
converges in weak operator topology to a bounded operator, say
A_{\infty},
on
\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})).
To prove that
A_{\infty}
commutes with each
M_{\theta_{i}},
we first observe that
A_{\infty}
commutes with
M_{\theta_{n}}
and for
1\leq i\leq n-1,
it follows that

\displaystyle\langle M_{\theta_{i}}^{*}A_{\infty}^{*}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r}\langle M_{\theta_{i}}^{*}A_{r}^{*}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r}\langle M_{\theta_{i}}^{*}M_{\theta_{n}}^{*r}\widetilde{T}^{*}\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}M_{\theta_{n}}^{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r}\langle M_{\theta_{i}}^{*}\widetilde{T}^{*}\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}M_{\theta_{n}}^{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},M_{\theta_{n}}^{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r}\langle T_{\theta_{i}}^{*}\widetilde{T}^{*}\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}M_{\theta_{n}}^{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},M_{\theta_{n}}^{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r}\langle T_{\theta_{n}}^{*p}\widetilde{T}^{*}T_{\theta_{n-i}}\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}M_{\theta_{n}}^{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},M_{\theta_{n}}^{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r}\langle M_{\theta_{n}}^{*p}\widetilde{T}^{*}\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}M_{\theta_{n}}^{r}M_{\theta_{n-i}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},M_{\theta_{n}}^{r}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r}\langle M_{\theta_{n}}^{*(r+p)}\widetilde{T}^{*}\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}M_{\theta_{n}}^{(r+p)}M_{\theta_{n}}^{*p}M_{\theta_{n-i}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r}\langle M_{\theta_{n}}^{*(r+p)}\widetilde{T}^{*}\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}M_{\theta_{n}}^{(r+p)}M^{*}_{\theta_{i}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle A_{\infty}^{*}M_{\theta_{i}}^{*}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle.

Thus,
A_{\infty}
commutes with each
M_{\theta_{i}}.
Hence there exists a
G
-invariant function
\widetilde{\phi}
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
such that
A_{\infty}=M_{\widetilde{\phi}}.
Let
f,g\in\mathbb{P}_{\rm sgn}(H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n})),
then

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}M_{\widetilde{\phi}}f,g\rangle=\langle A_{\infty}f,g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r}\langle A_{r}f,g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r}\langle M_{\theta_{n}}^{*r}\widetilde{T}\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}M_{\theta_{n}}^{r}f,g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r}\langle\widetilde{T}T_{\theta_{n}}^{r}f,T_{\theta_{n}}^{r}g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{T}f,g\rangle.

Thus,
\widetilde{T}=\widetilde{P}_{\rm sgn}M_{\widetilde{\phi}}=T_{\widetilde{\phi}},
where
\widetilde{\phi}=\phi\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
for
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{n})).

This completes the proof.
∎

We conclude this paper by characterizing the compact Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n})).

Theorem 4.19
.

The only compact Toeplitz operator on
H^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n}))
is the zero operator.

Proof.

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{I}_{G,{\rm hol}}
from Equation (
4.6
). For some
\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{I}_{G,{\rm hol}},
it follows that

\displaystyle\langle T_{u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle=\langle T^{r*}_{n}T_{u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}}T^{r}_{n}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle=\langle T_{u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}+q\boldsymbol{r}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}+q\boldsymbol{r}}\rangle\text{ (for every }r\geq 0).

Since
T_{u}
is compact,
\left\|{T_{u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}}}\right\|
goes to
0
as
\boldsymbol{p}
goes to infinity. Hence from the above, we have

\displaystyle|\langle T_{u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle|=|\langle T_{u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}+q\boldsymbol{r}},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}+q\boldsymbol{r}}\rangle|\leq\left\|{T_{u\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{p}+q\boldsymbol{r}}}\right\|\to 0

as
r\to 0.
Since
\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{I}_{G,{\rm hol}}
are chosen arbitrarily,
u
is identically zero.
∎
