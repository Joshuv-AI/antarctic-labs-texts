Limit Distribution Theory for the Smooth 1-Wasserstein Distance with Applications
Ritwik Sadhu, Ziv Goldfeld, Kengo Kato

Abstract
. The smooth 1-Wasserstein distance (SWD) 𝖶 1 σ \mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma} was recently proposed as a means to mitigate the curse of dimensionality in empirical approximation, while preserving the Wasserstein structure. Indeed, SWD exhibits parametric convergence rates in any data dimension and inherits the metric and topological structure of the classic 1-Wasserstein distance. To facilitate principled learning and inference with the SWD, this work conducts a thorough statistical study thereof, encompassing a high-dimensional limit distribution theory for empirical 𝖶 1 σ \mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma} , bootstrap consistency, concentration inequalities, and Berry-Esseen type bounds. We provide sharp characterization of the dependence of convergence rates on the smoothing parameter σ \sigma and account for regimes when it decays with n n at a sufficiently slow rate. As applications of the limit distribution theory, we study two-sample testing and implicit generative modeling (when both the data distribution and the model are sampled) via 𝖶 1 σ \mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma} minimum expected distance estimation (MEDE). We establish asymptotic validity of homogeneity testing based on 𝖶 1 σ \mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma} , while for MEDE, we prove measurability, almost sure convergence, and limit distributions for optimal estimators and their corresponding 𝖶 1 σ \mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma} error. Our results suggest that the SWD is well suited for high-dimensional statistical learning and inference. Keywords. intrinsic dimension, limit theorems, multivariate homogeneity testing, optimal transport, smooth Wasserstein distance, strong approximation.

Introduction

Optimal transport (OT) is a versatile framework for quantifying discrepancy between probability distributions. It has a longstanding history in mathematics, dating back to the Monge in the 18th century
[
72
]
. Recently, OT has emerged as a tool of choice for a myriad of machine learning tasks, encompassing clustering
[
57
]
, computer vision
[
85
,
86
,
65
]
, generative modeling
[
7
,
54
]
, natural language processing
[
6
,
103
,
53
]
, and model ensembling
[
37
]
. This wide adoption was driven by recent computational advances
[
27
,
91
,
1
]
and the rich geometric structure with which OT-based discrepancy measures, like Wasserstein distances, endow the space of probability measures.
However, Wasserstein distances suffer from the so-called curse of dimensionality (CoD) when distributions are estimated from samples, with empirical convergence rates of
n^{-1/d}
, whenever the dimension is
d>3
. This slow rate renders performance guarantees in terms of
\mathsf{W}_{1}
all but vacuous when
d
is large. It is a also a major roadblock towards a more delicate statistical analysis concerning limit distributions, bootstrapping, and second order rates, all of which are central for valid statistical inference.

Recently, smoothing the underlying distribution via convolution with a Gaussian kernel was proposed as a means for mitigating the CoD while preserving the Wasserstein structure. Specifically, for a smoothing parameter
\sigma>0
and
p\geq 1
, consider
\mathsf{W}_{p}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q):=\mathsf{W}_{p}(P\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma},Q\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma})
, where
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d})
is the
d
-dimensional isotropic Gaussian measure of parameter
\sigma
,
P\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
is the convolution of
P
and
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
, and
\mathsf{W}_{p}
is the regular
p
-Wasserstein distance. In contrast to the CoD rates,
Goldfeld et al. 2020b
showed that several popular statistical divergences, including
\mathsf{W}_{1}
and
\mathsf{W}_{2}
, enjoy fast empirical convergence of
n^{-1/2}
once distributions are smoothed by
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
. The followup work
Goldfeld and Greenewald 2020
focused on
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
and showed that the smooth distance inherits the metric and topological structure of its classic counterpart. That work also established regularity properties of
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
in
\sigma
, and quantified the smoothing as
\big|\mathsf{W}_{1}(P,Q)-\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q)\big|=O(\sigma\sqrt{d})
. The structural properties and parametric empirical convergence rates were extended to general
p\geq 1
in
[
76
]
. Other follow-up works explored relations between
\mathsf{W}_{p}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
and maximum mean discrepancies
[
105
]
, analyzed its rate of decay as
\sigma\to\infty

[
22
]
, considered it in the context of sequential estimation of divergences
[
69
]
, and adopted it as a performance metric for nonparametric mixture model estimation
[
56
]
, demonstrating accelerated (polynomial) MLE convergence rates.
Motivated by the above, herein we conduct an in-depth statistical study of the Gaussian smoothed Wasserstein distance (SWD), exploring high dimensional limit distributions of the empirical distance under the null and the alternative, bootstrapping, Berry-Esseen type bounds, vanishing
\sigma
analysis, as well as several applications.

1.1.
Contributions

This work extends our earlier conference paper
[
50
]
. That paper derived the first limit distribution result for the empirical SWD that holds in arbitrary dimensions, and applied it to study minimum distance estimation (MDE) under
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
. Alongside the fast empirical convergence of
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
in expectation
[
51
,
48
]
, this limit distribution result further demonstrated the enhanced statistical properties of
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
. However, the analysis in
Goldfeld et al. 2020a
was limited to the one-sample setting under the null, not accounting for statistical questions concerning two-sample approximation, speed of convergence to the distributional limit, sharpness of expectation bounds in
\sigma

Our first main result herein derives the limit distribution of the smooth empirical distance
\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)
in any dimension
d
, and characterizes sharp dependence of the expected empirical convergence rate on
\sigma

(Theorem
1
). Here,

is the empirical measure based on
n
independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples,
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, from
P
. The analysis relies on the Kantorovich-Rubinstein (KR) duality
[
99

, which allows representing
\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)
as a supremum of an empirical process indexed by the class of 1-Lipschitz functions convolved with a Gaussian density. We prove that this function class is
P
-Donsker (i.e., satisfies the uniform central limit theorem (CLT)) under a polynomial moment condition on

. By the continuous mapping theorem, we conclude that
\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)
converges in distribution to the supremum of a tight Gaussian process. We then extend this result to the two-sample case
\sqrt{mn/(m+n)}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{m})
, providing distributional limits under both the null (
P=Q
) and the alternative (
P\neq Q
). To enable approximation of the distributional limits, we prove consistency of the nonparametric bootstrap (Corollary
2
). Our high-dimensional SWD limit distribution theory contrasts the classic
\mathsf{W}_{1}
case, for which similar results are known only when
d=1

[
32
]
. Indeed, while 1-Lipschitz functions are generally not Donsker for
d>1

1
1

1

The reader is referred to, e.g.,

Given the limit distribution theory, it is natural to ask how accurate the distributional approximations are. To that end we explore a Berry-Esseen type analysis of empirical SWD, as well as its empirical bootstrap analogue (Theorems
2
and
3
in Section
4

We consider three applications of the derived theory: (i) dependence of the classic empirical

Lastly, we consider MEDE as a formulation of implicit generative modeling when only samples are available from both the data distribution and the model (as is often the case in practice, e.g., generative adversarial networks (GANS)
[
78
,
7

). We establish measurability and strong consistency of the estimator

Except

In particular,

43
]
; see also

Boissard 2011
,

2.2.
The 1-Wasserstein Distance

The Kantorovich OT problem
[
59
]
between
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with cost
c(x,y)
seeks to minimize
\int c(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)
over
\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)
,
where
\Pi(P,Q)
is the set of couplings (or transport plans) between
P
and
Q
. The 1-Wasserstein distance (
\mathsf{W}_{1}
)
[
99
, Chapter 6]
is the minimum value achieved for the Euclidean distance cost
c(x,y)=\|x-y\|
:

\mathsf{W}_{1}(P,Q)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|\ \,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y).

The Wasserstein space
\big(\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\mathsf{W}_{1}\big)
is a metric space, as
\mathsf{W}_{1}
metrizes weak convergence together with convergence of first moments
[
99
]
. KR duality
[
60
]
gives rise to the following representation of the 1-Wasserstein distance

\mathsf{W}_{1}(P,Q)=\inf_{f\in\mathsf{Lip}_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\,\mathrm{d}(P-Q).

This form is invariant to shifting the function
f
by a constant. Consequently we may assume without loss of generality that
f(0)=0
, and optimize over the class
\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}
instead.
We subsequently leverage this dual form to derive the limit distribution theory for the smooth version of the distance.

2.3.
Smooth 1-Wasserstein Distances

The smooth 1-Wasserstein distance levels out local irregularities in the distributions via convolution with the Gaussian kernel. It is defined as

\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q):=\mathsf{W}_{1}(P\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma},Q\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}),

where
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
is the
d
-dimensional isotropic Gaussian measure with parameter
\sigma
.
Goldfeld and Greenewald 2020
showed that
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
inherits the metric and topological structure of
\mathsf{W}_{1}
. Stability w.r.t. the smoothing parameter was also established in that work, showing that

(1)

\big|\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q)-\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\tau}(P,Q)\big|\leq 2\sqrt{d|\sigma^{2}-\tau^{2}|}.

This enabled proving regularity properties of
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q)
in
\sigma
, such as continuity, monotonicity, and
\Gamma
-convergence.
In addition,
Goldfeld et al. 2020b
showed that
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
alleviates the CoD, converging as
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)\big]=O(n^{-1/2})
in all dimensions under a sub-Gaussian condition on
P
. In the next section, sharpen the dependence on the expectation bound on
\sigma
, characterize the limit distributions of (scaled) empirical
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
under the null and the alternative, prove that the limits can be accurately estimated via the bootstrap, and establish concentration inequalities. Later, we move to a vanishing
\sigma
analysis, Berry-Esseen type bounds, and applications.

3.
Limit Distribution Theory for Smooth Wasserstein Distance

In this section we characterize the limit distribution of
\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)
in all dimensions. We also derive consistency of the bootstrap as a means for computing the limit distribution, establish concentration inequalities for
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)
, and then explore the two-sample setting.

3.1.
One-Sample Limit Distribution

To state the result, some definitions are due. For
P\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, let
G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}:=\big(G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(f)\big)_{f\in\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}}
be a centered Gaussian process on
\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}
with covariance
\mathbb{E}\big[G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(f)\,G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(g)\big]=\mathsf{Cov}_{P}(f\ast\varphi_{\sigma},g\ast\varphi_{\sigma})
, where
f,g,\in\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}
. One may verify that
|f\ast\varphi_{\sigma}(x)|\leq\|x\|+\sigma\sqrt{d}
(cf. Section
7.1
), so that
P|f\ast\varphi_{\sigma}|^{2}<\infty
, for all
f\in\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}
(which ensures that the said covariance function is well-defined). With that, we are ready to state the theorem.

Theorem 1

(One-sample limit distribution)
.

Let
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}I_{j}
be a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into bounded convex sets with nonempty interior such that
K:=\sup_{j}\diam(I_{j})<\infty
and
M_{j}:=\sup_{I_{j}}\|x\|\geq 1
for all
j
. If
P\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies

(2)

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M_{j}P(I_{j})^{1/2}<\infty,

then there exists a version of
G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
that is tight in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0})
, and denoting the tight version by the same symbol
G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
, we have
\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\|G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\|_{\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}}=:L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
. In addition, for any
\alpha>d/2
, we have

(3)

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)\big]\lesssim_{\alpha,d,K}\frac{\sigma^{-\alpha+1}}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M_{j}P(I_{j})^{1/2}.

The proof, given below in Section
7.1
, uses KR duality to translate the Gaussian convolution in the measure space to the convolution of Lipschitz functions with a Gaussian density. It is then shown that the class of Gaussian-smoothed Lipschitz functions is
P
-Donsker by bounding the metric entropy of the function class restricted to each
I_{j}
. As the next corollary shows, the dependence on
\sigma
in (
3
) can be tightened to
\sigma^{-d/2+1}
up to a
\log n
term.

Corollary 1

(Expectation bound)
.

Under Condition (
2
), we have

(4)

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)\big]\lesssim_{d,K}\frac{\sigma^{-d/2+1}\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M_{j}P(I_{j})^{1/2}.

Corollary
1
is proven in Appendix
A.2
. Combining (
4
) with the stability result (
1
) recovers the (correct)
n^{-1/d}
bound on the classic empirical
\mathsf{W}_{1}
, up to logarithmic factors. Specifically, taking
\sigma=n^{-1/d}\big(\log n\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M_{j}P(I_{j})^{1/2}\big)^{2/d}
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}(P_{n},P)\big]&=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)-\mathsf{W}_{1}(P_{n},P)\big]\\
&\lesssim_{d,K}\inf_{\sigma\in(0,1)}\frac{\sigma^{-d/2+1}\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M_{j}P(I_{j})^{1/2}+\sigma\\
&\lesssim_{d,K}n^{-1/d}(\log n)^{2/d}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M_{j}P(I_{j})^{1/2}\Big)^{\frac{2}{d}}.\end{split}

A bound similar to (
3
) and (
4
) was derived in Proposition 1 of
Goldfeld et al. 2020b
with explicit dependence on
\sigma
and
d
. However, their dependence on
\sigma
is not sharp enough to imply the known polynomial rates for the empirical unsmoothed distance.

Remark 1

(Discussion of Condition (
2
))
.

Let
\{I_{j}\}
consist of cubes with side length
1
and integral lattice points as vertices. One may then obtain the bound

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M_{j}P(I_{j})^{1/2}\lesssim_{d}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{d}P\big(\|x\|_{\infty}>k\big)^{1/2}\lesssim\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{d}P\big(\|x\|_{\infty}>t\big)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}t,

which is finite (by Markov’s inequality) if there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
P|x_{j}|^{2(d+1)+\epsilon}<\infty
for all
j
. Proposition 1 in
Goldfeld et al. 2020b
shows that
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)\big]=O(n^{-1/2})
whenever
P
is sub-Gaussian. Theorem
1
thus relaxes this moment condition, in addition to deriving a limit distribution. For
d=1
, the above condition can be relaxed to
\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{P(|x|>t)}\,\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty
via a tighter argument with disjoint unit cubes, matching the condition from
[
16
, Section 3.1]
for empirical
\mathsf{W}_{1}
.

3.2.
Bootstrap Consistency for One-Sample Limit Distribution

We next show that the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the limiting variable in Theorem
1
can be estimated via the bootstrap
[
96
, Chapter 3.6]
. To that end we first need to guarantee that the distribution of
L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
has a Lebesgue density that is sufficiently regular (see Appendix
A.3
).

Lemma 1

(Distribution of
L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
)
.

Assume that Conditions (
2
) holds and that
P
is not a point mass. Then the distribution of
L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure and its density is positive and continuous on
(0,\infty)
except for at most countably many points.

To define the bootstrap estimate, let
X_{1}^{B},\dots,X_{n}^{B}
be i.i.d. from
P_{n}
conditioned on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, and set
P_{n}^{B}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}^{B}}

as the bootstrap empirical distribution.
Let
\mathbb{P}^{B}
be the probability measure induced by the bootstrap (i.e.,
the conditional probability given
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
). We have the following bootstrap consistency.

Corollary 2

(Bootstrap consistency)
.

If Condition (
2
) holds and
P
is not a point mass, then

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left|\mathbb{P}^{B}\big(\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n}^{B},P_{n})\leq t\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\leq t\big)\right|\to 0\quad\mbox{a.s.}

This corollary, together with continuity of the distribution function of
L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
implies that for
\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}:=\inf\big\{t\geq 0:\mathbb{P}^{B}\big(\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n}^{B},P_{n})\leq t\big)\geq 1-\alpha\big\}
(which can be computed numerically), we have
\mathbb{P}\big(\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)>\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}\big)=\alpha+o(1)
.

3.3.
Concentration Inequalities

Next, we consider quantitative concentration inequalities for
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)
, under different moment conditions on
P
. For
\alpha>0
, let
\|\xi\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}:=\inf\{C>0:\mathbb{E}[e^{(|\xi|/C)^{\alpha}}]\leq 2\}
be the Orlicz
\psi_{\alpha}
-norm for a real-valued random variable
\xi
(if
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then
\|\cdot\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}
is a quasi-norm). We focus on the unbounded support case since a concentration inequality for compactly supported distributions was derived in
Goldfeld et al. 2020a
.

Proposition 1

(Concentration inequalities)
.

Let
X\sim P
with
\mathsf{supp}(P)=\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and suppose one of the following conditions holds:
(i)
\big\|\|X\|\big\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}<\infty
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
;
(ii)
P\|x\|^{q}<\infty
for some
q\in[1,\infty)
.
Then, for all
\eta\in(0,1)
,
\delta>0
, there exist constants
C:=C_{\eta,\delta}
, such that for all
t>0
:

(5)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)>(1+\eta)\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)\big]+t\Big)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{C\big(P\|x\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}d\big)}\right)+a(n,t),

where

a(n,t)=\begin{cases}3\exp\left(-\left(\frac{nt}{C\left((\log(1+n))^{1/\alpha}\left\|\|X\|\right\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}+\sigma\sqrt{d}\right)}\right)^{\alpha}\right)&,\text{ under (i)}\\
C\big(n^{1/q}\mathbb{E}[\|X\|^{q}]+\sigma^{q}d^{q/2}\big)(nt)^{-q}&,\text{ under (ii)}\end{cases}.

The proof of the above result can be found in Appendix
A.4
.

Remark 2

(Comparison to
Goldfeld and Greenewald 2020
)
.

Corollary 1 of
Goldfeld and Greenewald 2020
establishes the following Gaussian concentration of
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)
when
P
has bounded support:

(6)

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)\geq\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)\big]+t\big)\leq e^{-\frac{2nt^{2}}{\diam(\mathcal{X})^{2}}},\quad\forall t>0.

Herein, we generalize this result to unbounded support. The term
a(n,t)
accounts for different concentration behavior of
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)
for large
t
. We expect that the logarithmic term in Case (i) can be removed via refinements akin to
Fournier and Guillin 2015b
.

3.4.
Two-Sample Limit Distributions and Bootstrap

Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
can be extended to the two-sample case, i.e., accounting for
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{m})
, where
P_{n}
and
Q_{m}
correspond to possibly different population measures
P
and
Q
. We first treat the null case (
P=Q
) and then consider the alternative (
P\neq Q
). Let
N:=n+m
and consider the statistic

W_{m,n}:=\sqrt{\frac{mn}{N}}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{m})=\sqrt{\frac{mn}{N}}\|P_{n}-Q_{m}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}},

where
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}:=\left\{f\ast\varphi_{\sigma}:f\in\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}\right\}
(cf. (
12
)), and define its bootstrap version as follows. Let
H_{N}
be the pooled empirical measure constructed from i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\sim P
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}\sim Q
. Letting
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N}
be a bootstrap sample from
H_{N}
, construct bootstrap measures
P_{n}^{B}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Z_{i}}
,
Q_{m}^{B}:=m^{-1}\sum_{i=n+1}^{N}\delta_{Z_{i}}
, and let

W_{m,n}^{B}=\sqrt{\frac{mn}{N}}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n}^{B},Q_{m}^{B}).

The following corollary shows that when
P=Q
,
W_{m,n}
converges in distribution to
L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
, and the bootstrap distribution of
W_{m,n}^{B}
is also consistent for the same limit (see Appendix
A.5
for the proof).

Corollary 3

(Two-sample limit distribution and bootstrap under the null)
.

Let

P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M_{j}P(I_{j})^{1/2},\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M_{j}Q(I_{j})^{1/2}<\infty
and assume that
P
is non-degenerate (
M_{j}
is as in Theorem
1
).
For
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, set
N=m+n
, and assume
n/N\to\lambda\in(0,1)
as
m,n\to\infty
. The following hold:

(1)

If
P=Q
, then
W_{m,n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
, and
W_{m,n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}\infty
, otherwise.

(2)

For
H:=\lambda P+(1-\lambda)Q
, we have
\mathsf{Law}\big(W_{m,n}^{B}|X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\to}}\mathsf{Law}\big(L_{H}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\big),

\mathbb{P}-
a.s. When
P=Q=H
we further have

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\,\Big|\mathbb{P}^{B}\big(W_{m,n}^{B}\leq t\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\leq t\big)\Big|\to 0\quad\text{a.s.}

Evidently, the
W_{m,n}
statistic diverges when
P\neq Q
. Thus, to treat the alternative case (i.e., when distributions are different), consider the centered statistic

\overline{W}_{m,n}:=\sqrt{\frac{mn}{m+n}}\big(\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{m})-\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q)\big).

Theorem 6.1 of
Cárcamo et al. 2020
uses directional differentiability of the supremum norm and the functional delta method to derive limit distributions for integral probability metrics (IPMs)
[
106
,
73
]
over Donsker classes. The
\overline{W}_{m,n}
statistic satisfies this condition, since, up to centering, it is an IPM over
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
, which is
P
- and
Q
-Donsker under appropriate moment conditions. This leads to the following result.

Corollary 4

(Two-sample limit distribution under the alternative)
.

Assume the conditions of Corollary
3
and set

\mathsf{d}_{\sigma}(f,g):=\sqrt{P(f\ast\varphi_{\sigma}-g\ast\varphi_{\sigma})^{2}}+\sqrt{Q(f\ast\varphi_{\sigma}-g\ast\varphi_{\sigma})^{2}}
. Then
(\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0},\mathsf{d}_{\sigma})
is a totally bounded pseudometric space, and we have

(7)

\overline{W}_{m,n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\|G_{P,Q,\lambda}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\|_{\bar{M}_{\sigma}}=:L^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}_{P,Q,\lambda},

where
G_{P,Q,\lambda}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}=\sqrt{1-\lambda}\,\widetilde{G}_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}-\sqrt{\lambda}\,\widetilde{G}_{Q}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
and

\bar{M}_{\sigma}:=\Big\{\bar{f}\in\overline{\mathsf{Lip}}_{1,0}:\,(P-Q)\,\bar{f}\ast\varphi_{\sigma}=\sup\nolimits_{f\in\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}}(P-Q)\,f\ast\varphi_{\sigma}\Big\}.

Here
\overline{\mathsf{Lip}}_{1,0}
is the
\mathsf{d}_{\sigma}
-completion of
\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}
, and
\widetilde{G}_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
,
\widetilde{G}_{Q}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
are the extensions of
G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
,
G_{Q}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
to the completion, respectively.

This corollary follows from an application of Theorem 6.1 in
Cárcamo et al. 2020
.

Remark 3

(Relation between Corollaries
3
and
4
)
.

Under
P=Q
,
\widetilde{G}_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
and
\widetilde{G}_{Q}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
are independent copies of the same Gaussian process,
\mathsf{d}_{\sigma}(f,g)=2\mathsf{d}_{P}(f,g):=2\|f\ast\varphi_{\sigma}-g\ast\varphi_{\sigma}\|_{L^{2}(P)}
, and
\bar{M}_{\sigma}=\overline{\mathsf{Lip}}_{1,0}
. In this case, the distribution of
G_{P,Q,\lambda}^{\sigma}
above coincides with the limit distribution found in Corollary
3
. When
P\neq Q
, Corollary
3
shows that the bootstrap distribution of
W^{B}_{m,n}
(a symmetrized bootstrap version of
W_{m,n}
) consistently estimates the null limit distribution. For testing
H_{0}:\,P=Q
against the alternative
H_{1}:\,P\neq Q
, the quantiles of
W^{B}_{m,n}
can be used to choose critical values for the test. In contrast, Corollary
4
may be used to find confidence intervals for
W_{1}^{\sigma}(P,Q)
using samples from the two distributions
P
,
Q
.

Remark 4

(Uniqueness of potentials)
.

If the set
\bar{M}_{\sigma}
of optimal potentials for
P,Q
is a singleton up to additive constants (i.e., the optimal potential is unique), then the limit distribution in (
7
) is a univariate Gaussian. However, no such uniqueness result is known for
\mathsf{W}_{1}
in general, even for smooth measures, since the underlying cost function is not strictly convex. Recent results on uniqueness of Kantorovich potentials are given in
Bernton et al. 2021
,
Staudt et al. 2022
. Theorem 2 of
Staudt et al. 2022
shows uniqueness of potentials under certain regularity conditions on the optimal coupling and the cost. However
c_{1}(x,y):=\|x-y\|
is non differentiable at
x=y
, which violates Condition (3) of their result when, for instance,
P
has a density that is non-zero everywhere and
\mathsf{supp}(Q)
is compact with non-empty interior. Theorem B.2 of
Bernton et al. 2021
also requires differentiability of the cost and superlinear growth of
c(x,y)=h(x-y)
, neither of which holds for
c_{1}
.

We next consider the bootstrap analog of the centered statistic
\overline{W}_{m,n}
. This is motivated by the fact that the distribution of the limit random variable
L^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}_{P,Q,\lambda}
under the alternative is non-pivotal in the sense that it depends on the population distributions
P
and
Q
, which are unknown in practice. However, the naive bootstrap is inconsistent in estimating the alternative limit distribution in general. To state the result, let
\widetilde{P}_{n}^{B},\widetilde{Q}_{m}^{B}
be bootstrap samples from
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
, respectively, and define the bootstrap estimate

\overline{W}_{m,n}^{B}=\sqrt{\frac{mn}{m+n}}\left(\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(\widetilde{P}_{n}^{B},\widetilde{Q}_{m}^{B})-\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{m})\right).

Proposition 2

(Bootstrap inconsistency under the alternative)
.

Under the conditions of Corollary
4
, we have the following unconditional limit of
\bar{W}_{m,n}^{B}
.

\mathsf{Law}\left(\overline{W}_{m,n}^{B}\right)\overset{w}{\to}\mathsf{Law}\left(\|G^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}_{P,Q,\lambda}+\widetilde{G}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}_{P,Q,\lambda}\|_{\bar{M}_{\sigma}}-\|G^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}_{P,Q,\lambda}\|_{\bar{M}_{\sigma}}\right),

where
G^{\sigma}_{P,Q,\lambda}
is given in (
7
) and
\widetilde{G}^{\sigma}_{P,Q,\lambda}
is an independent copy thereof.

The proof of Proposition
2
is given in Appendix
A.6
. The unconditional limit in Proposition
2
is incompatible with the conditional limit distribution in Corollary
4
unless the Gaussian process
G_{P,Q,\lambda}^{\sigma}
is a.s. constant on
\bar{M}_{\sigma}
. A sufficient condition that ensures this, and consequently, consistency of the naive bootstrap, is when the set of optimal potentials
\bar{M}_{\sigma}
is a singleton up to an additive constant. However, conditions to ensure this are not known in general (cf. Remark
4
).

Even if the naive bootstrap is inconsistent, the alternative limit distribution can be estimated via the rescaled bootstrap scheme specified in
[
40
]
. We describe below an alternative approach, based on subsampling
[
79
]
, to estimate the quantiles of the alternative limit distribution and obtain confidence intervals for
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q)
from samples.

To describe the subsampling scheme, first let
1\leq a_{n}\leq n
,
1\leq b_{m}\leq m
be sequences of positive integers, and set
r_{m,n}:=\sqrt{mn/(m+n)}
. Take

A_{n}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\binom{\{1,.\ldots,n\}}{a_{n}}\quad;\quad B_{m}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\binom{\{1,.\ldots,m\}}{b_{m}},

where
\binom{S}{k}
designates the set of all
k
-subsets of a set
S
. Define
P_{n}^{\mathsf{Sub}}:=a_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i\in A_{n}}X_{i}
and
Q_{m}^{\mathsf{Sub}}:=b_{m}^{-1}\sum_{i\in B_{m}}Y_{i}
as the empirical measures of the subsamples. Lastly, let
J_{m,n}(x)
be the CDF of
\mathsf{Law}\big(r_{a_{n},b_{m}}\big(\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n}^{\mathsf{Sub}},Q_{m}^{\mathsf{Sub}})-\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{m})\big)\,\big|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}\big)
, and
J_{\lambda}(x)
be the CDF of
\mathsf{Law}\big(L^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}_{P,Q,\lambda}\big)
. Then,
J_{m,n}
acts as the subsampling estimator for
J_{\lambda}
, and the following result shows that it is a consistent estimator (under both
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
).

Lemma 2

(Consistency of subsampling)
.

Assume the conditions of Corollary
4

and suppose that
a_{n}\vee b_{m}\to\infty
,
a_{n}/(a_{n}+b_{m})\to\lambda
,
a_{n}/n\wedge b_{m}/m\to 0
, and
r_{a_{n},b_{m}}/r_{m,n}\to 0
. Then
J_{m,n}(x)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}J_{\lambda}(x)
for every continuity point
x
of
J_{\lambda}
.

This lemma follows from a modification of
Politis and Romano 2008
, so as to account for the dependence of the limit distribution on
\lambda=\lim_{m\vee n\to\infty}n/(m+n)
. Nevertheless, the proof is all but identical, and is therefore skipped.

As a consequence of Lemma
2
, we obtain confidence intervals for the SWD.

Corollary 5

(Consistent confidence intervals under the alternative)
.

Under the

conditions of Lemma
2
, an asymptotic
(1-\alpha)
confidence set for
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q)
is given by

\left[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{m})+\frac{J_{m,n}^{-1}(\alpha/2)}{r_{m,n}},\ \mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{m})+\frac{J_{m,n}^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)}{r_{m,n}}\right].

4.
Berry-Esseen Type Bounds for Vanishing Smoothing Parameter

Given a limit distribution theory, Berry-Esseen type bounds quantify the accuracy of the distributional approximation. The classic Berry-Esseen theorem fulfills this role for the CLT, specifying the rate at which the sample mean CDF (of standardized i.i.d. samples) converges towards the standard normal distribution in the sup-norm
[
14
,
41
]
. Specifically, let
F_{n}(x):=\mathbb{P}\big(n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\leq x\big)
for i.i.d.
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
with mean zero and variance one, and take
\Phi
as the CDF of
\mathcal{N}_{1}
. The Berry-Esseen theorem yields

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big|F_{n}(x)-\Phi(x)\big|\leq\frac{C\mathbb{E}\big[|\xi_{1}|^{3}\big]}{\sqrt{n}},

where the constant
C
above was refined multiple times throughout the years (see
Shevtsova 2011
for a review and a recent proof). This result has seen many extensions and generalizations, e.g., to Banach spaces
[
100
]
and similar approximation bounds for empirical and bootstrap processes over bounded and/or VC-type function classes
[
70
,
23
,
25
]
.

In this section, we derive Berry-Esseen type bounds for finite-sample approximations of the limiting distribution from Theorem
1
, by either the (scaled) empirical SWD or its bootstrap analogue. Motivated by noise annealing techniques used in machine learning, our results allow the smoothing parameter
\sigma
to vanishes as
n\to\infty
at a sufficiently slow rate. Recalling that
\big|\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q)-\mathsf{W}_{1}(P,Q)\big|\leq 2\sigma\sqrt{d}
, the vanishing
\sigma
analysis enables viewing the SWD as a proxy of classic
\mathsf{W}_{1}
. The discrepancy between the limit distribution and its non-asymptotic approximation is measured by the Prokhorov distance, given by

\rho(P,Q):=\inf\big\{\epsilon>0\ :\ P(A)\leq Q(A^{\epsilon})+\epsilon\,,\ \forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big\}.

With some abuse of notation, we write
\rho(X,Y)
instead of
\rho(P,Q)
, for
X\sim P
and
Y\sim Q
.

We first consider approximating the limiting variable
L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
from Theorem
1
by the empirical SWD
\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)
, for small
\sigma
values. To simplify notation, we introduce the following quantities: for
d\geq 2
and any
\alpha>d/2
, define
a:=\alpha-1,\,b:=d/(2\alpha)
.

Theorem 2

(Berry-Esseen type bound)
.

Suppose
P\|x\|_{\infty}^{l}=M<\infty
for some
l>4\alpha-d+3
and let
n^{-p}\leq\sigma_{n}\leq 1
for some
p<\frac{1-b}{8ab}
. Then:

(1)

there exist versions
V_{n}
and
W_{n}
of
L^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma_{n}}_{P}
and
\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma_{n}}(P_{n},P)
, respectively, such that for any sequence
r_{n}\gg n^{-(1-b)/\left(2(3+b)\right)}\sigma_{n}^{-4ab/(3+b)}
, we have
V_{n}-W_{n}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(r_{n})
;

(2)

the Prokhorov distance is bounded as
\rho\big(\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma_{n}}(P_{n},P),\,L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma_{n}}\big)\lesssim_{\alpha,d,M,l}n^{-(1-b)/8}\sigma_{n}^{-ab}
.

The proof of Theorem
2
leverages the methodology of
Chernozhukov et al. 2014a
for approximating of the supremum of an empirical process over a VC-type class by that of a Gaussian process. Several adaptations of their technique are needed to account for the fact our function class is not VC-type.

Remark 5

(Fast Berry-Esseen rates)
.

If
\alpha=2d^{2}
, then
r_{n}\sim O(n^{-1/6+\epsilon_{d}})\sigma_{n}^{-4(d^{2}\mspace{-1mu}-\mspace{-1mu}1)}
,
where
\epsilon_{d}
is small for large
d
. Hence, if for instance,
\sigma_{n}
decays at an inverse logarithmic rate and
d
is sufficiently large, we have
r_{n}=o(n^{-1/6+\epsilon})
for some small
\epsilon>0
. Consequently, when the population distribution
P
has sufficiently high moments (say, it is subexponential), and
\sigma_{n}
is appropriately chosen, we can obtain dimension free Berry-Esseen rates, in the sense
n
and
d
are decoupled (although
d
can still enter through the constants). This is similar to the rate found in
Chernozhukov et al. 2014a
, for smaller (VC-type) function classes.

Remark 6

(Other distances)
.

With further restriction on
P
(e.g., anti-concentration assumptions akin to
Chernozhukov et al. 2014b
), the bound in Claim (ii) can be derived under a metric stronger than Prokhorov. However, such an analysis is quite technical and does not shed new light on the problem, and so we decided to omit it.

In Corollary
2
, it was shown that the empirical bootstrap is consistent in estimating the limiting distribution of SWD. The next result provides Berry-Esseen rates for the bootstrap estimate of
L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
, under slightly stronger moment conditions.

We establish the following analogue of Theorem
2
for the empirical bootstrap.

Theorem 3

(Berry-Esseen type bound for bootstrap)
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
2
,

(1)

there exist random variables
W_{n}
and
V_{n}
such that

\mathsf{Law}(W_{n}|X_{1:n})\mspace{-3mu}=\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{Law}\big(\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma_{n}}(P_{n}^{B},P_{n})\big|X_{1:n}\big)

and
\mathsf{Law}(V_{n}|X_{1:n})\mspace{-3mu}=\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{Law}\big(L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma_{n}}\big)
, and for any sequence
r_{n}\gg n^{-(1-b)/(2(3+b))}\sigma_{n}^{-a}
, we have

|V_{n}-W_{n}|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(r_{n})
.

(2)

the Prokhorov distance is bounded as

\rho\big(\mathsf{Law}\left(\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma_{n}}(P_{n}^{B},P_{n})\middle|\,X_{1:n}\right),\mathsf{Law}\left(L_{P}^{\sigma_{n}}\right)\big)=O_{\mathbb{P}}\big(n^{-\frac{1-b}{4(3+b)}}\sigma_{n}^{-a/2}\big).

The empirical bootstrap process on
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
is defined as:

(8)

\mathbb{G}_{n}^{*}(f)=\sqrt{n}(P_{n}^{B}-P_{n})f,\ f\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}.

Recall that
P_{n}^{B}
is the empirical measure formed from a bootstrap sample from
P_{n}
, as defined earlier. Then,
\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n}^{B},P_{n})=\|\mathbb{G}_{n}^{*}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}
. The proof of this theorem then follows along similar lines to those of Theorem
2
above. The main difference is that conditional versions of the technical lemmas are needed. An outline of the argument is provided in Section
7.3
, with the detailed proof deferred to Appendix
B.2
.

Remark 7

(Measurability and existence of versions)
.

The existence of versions
W_{n}
and
V_{n}
is guaranteed under the assumption that the underlying probability space is rich enough to contain a
\mathsf{U}[0,1]
random variable independent of the sample observations (cf.
Chernozhukov et al. 2016
, Page 3). This can be ensured by taking the product probability space
\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),P\big)
(in which the samples
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are coordinate projections) and extending it as
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),P\big)\right]\times\big([0,1],\mathcal{B}([0,1]),\mathrm{Leb}\big)
, where
\mathrm{Leb}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
.

5.
Dependence on Intrinsic Dimension

The rate of convergence of the empirical 1-Wasserstein distance
\mathsf{W}_{1}(P_{n},P)
adapts to the intrinsic dimension of the population distribution
[
38
,
18
,
77
,
101
,
64
,
90
]
. For the SWD, while the rate is parametric irrespective of dimension, we next show that the dependence on the intrinsic dimension is captured by the Gaussian smoothing parameter
\sigma
.

5.1.
Empirical Convergence and Intrinsic Dimension

The notion of intrinsic dimension used in the aforementioned papers and in this work employs covering numbers. The idea is to leverage the fact that the
\epsilon
-covering number of a set of intrinsic dimension
s
will scale as
c\epsilon^{-s}
, where
c
may depend on the size and the dimension of the ambient space. To state our intrinsic dimension definition, for any
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, denote its
(\epsilon,\tau)
-covering number by

N_{\epsilon}(P,\tau):=\inf\big\{N(\epsilon,S,\|\cdot\|)\ :\ S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ P(S)\geq 1-\tau\big\},

and let
d_{\epsilon}(P,\tau)=-\frac{\log N_{\epsilon}(P,\tau)}{\log\epsilon}
be the corresponding
(\epsilon,\tau)
-dimension.

Definition 1

(Upper smooth 1-Wasserstein dimension)
.

For a probability measure
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the upper smooth 1-Wasserstein dimension is defined as:

\dim^{*}_{\mathsf{SW}_{1}}(P):=\inf\left\{s\in(2,\infty)\ :\ \limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}d_{\epsilon}\Big(P,\epsilon^{s^{2}/(s-2)}\Big)\leq s\right\}.

The above definition is stricter than regular upper 1-Wasserstein dimension
[
38
,
101
]
in the sense that for a given
\epsilon
, it considers sets that exclude a smaller probability mass (i.e., larger sets) for determining dimensionality. A detailed comparison between the two as well as relations to Minkowski dimension are provided in the next subsection. The stricter notion of dimensionality enables treating empirical convergence of distributions with unbounded support, as stated next.

Theorem 4

(Empirical convergence and intrinsic dimension)
.

Let
P\mspace{-3mu}\in\mspace{-3mu}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
P\|x\|^{s}<\infty
and
\dim^{*}_{\mathsf{SW}_{1}}(P)<s
. Then, for all
n>1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)\right]\leq C\sigma^{-\frac{s}{2}+1}(\log n)^{\frac{3}{2}},

where
C
is a constant independent of
\sigma
and
n
.

Theorem
4
follows from a stronger version that we establish in Section
7.4
, where the assumption that
\dim^{*}_{\mathsf{SW}_{1}}(P)<s
is relaxed to a condition based on entropy. The key proof idea used here is to derive sharp estimates of the entropy of
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
restricted to small covering balls provided by the entropy bound. These bounds are then combined to provide a bound on the
L^{2}(P)
entropy of
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
, and the result follows by the application of a maximal inequality.

We conclude this subsection with a sufficient primitive condition on
P
for
\dim^{*}_{\mathsf{SW}_{1}}(P)\leq s
to hold. The next lemma states that this is the case for sub-exponential distributions supported on an affine
s
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\geq s
.

Lemma 3

(Sufficient primitive condition)
.

Let
2<s\leq d
. Suppose that
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is supported on an affine space
H_{s}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ :x-x_{0}\in V_{s}\}
for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
V_{s}
is an
s
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Also suppose that
Z=\|X-x_{0}\|
for
X\sim P
has Orlicz
\psi_{\beta}
-norm
\|Z\|_{\psi_{\beta}}<\infty
, for some
\beta>0
. Then,
\dim^{*}_{\mathsf{SW}_{1}}(P)\leq s
and we have

\mathbb{E}\big[\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)\big]\leq C\sigma^{-\frac{s}{2}+1}(\log n)^{\frac{3\beta+1}{2\beta}},

where
C
is independent of
n
and
\sigma
.

5.2.
Implications to Classic 1-Wasserstein Distance

Recall that combining Corollary
1
with the bound on the gap between
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
and
\mathsf{W}_{1}

[
48
, Lemma 1]
, recovers the known
n^{-1/d}
rate for
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}(P_{n},P)\big]
up to logarithmic factors. We can adopt a similar strategy in conjunction with Theorem
4
to get an analogous result in terms of intrinsic dimension.

Corollary 6

(Sharp
\mathsf{W}_{1}
rate under intrinsic dimension)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4
, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}(P_{n},P)\big]\leq Cn^{-1/s}
, where
C
is a constant independent of
n
.

A direct computation suggests the presence of a log factor, but we can eliminate it at the cost of a constant dependent on
\dim^{*}_{\mathsf{SW}_{1}}(P)
(see Appendix
C
for a proof). The dependence of the constants on
P
is solely through its moment and
\dim_{\mathsf{SW1}}^{*}(P)
. Our bound is tight in the sense that if
P
is supported on a regular set of dimension
s
(see Section
5.3
), then Corollary 2 of
Weed and Bach 2019
implies

\mathbb{E}\big[W_{1}(P_{n},P)\big]\gtrsim n^{-1/t}
, for any
t<s=\dim^{*}_{\mathsf{SW}_{1}}(P)
. A similar bound was derived in
Weed and Bach 2019
under a compact support assumption, but Corollary
6
establishes this rate for distributions with unbounded support. Other empirical convergence bounds for
\mathsf{W}_{1}
on unbounded spaces have been recently derived in
Singh and Póczos 2018
,
Lei 2020
, but the dependence of their dimension definition on
P
is implicit. Nevertheless, an application of
Singh and Póczos 2018
recovers the
n^{-1/s}
rate but under more stringent conditions, e.g., such as those in Lemma
3
.

Remark 8

(Berry-Essen bounds w.r.t. intrinsic dimension)
.

Similar modification

of Theorems
2
and
3
in the low intrinsic dimension regime are possible. The resulting rate bounds would be the same, with the only change being a faster feasible decay of the smoothing parameter. The overall rate will remain nearly
n^{-1/6}
, and for that reason, we omit the details of such refinements.

5.3.
Relation to Minkowski and Wasserstein Dimension

The study of empirical convergence rates based on box-counting or Minkowski dimension and variants thereof dates back to
Dudley 1969
, with more recent results available in
Boissard and Gouic 2014
,
Weed and Bach 2019
. These notions of dimensionality are restricted to compact or bounded sets. We next compare these notions of intrinsic dimensions to our Definition
1
, under the appropriate boundedness assumptions.

Definition 2

(Minkowski dimension)
.

The Minkowski dimension
\dim_{\mathsf{M}}(S)
of bounded sets

S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is

\dim_{\mathsf{M}}(S):=\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\log N(\epsilon,S,\|\cdot\|_{2})}{-\log\epsilon}.

For a compactly supported
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we also write
\dim_{\mathsf{M}}(P):=\dim_{\mathsf{M}}\big(\mathsf{supp}(P)\big).

Definition 3

(Upper Wasserstein dimension
[
101
]
)
.

The upp-

er 1-Wasserstein dimension for a compactly supported
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is

\dim_{\mathsf{W}_{1}}^{*}(P):=\inf\left\{s\in(2,\infty):\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}d_{\epsilon}\left(P,\epsilon^{s/(s-2)}\right)\leq s\right\}.

An extension of
Weed and Bach 2019
gives the following.

Lemma 4

(Dimension comparison)
.

If
\dim_{\mathsf{M}}(P)\geq 2
, then

\dim_{\mathsf{W}_{1}}^{*}(P)\leq\dim^{*}_{\mathsf{SW}_{1}}(P)\leq\dim_{\mathsf{M}}(P).

Thus, the upper smooth 1-Wasserstein dimension lies between the unsmoothed variant and the classic Minkowski dimension. However, for probability measures with sufficiently well behaved supports,
Weed and Bach 2019
shows that
\dim_{\mathsf{W}_{1}}^{*}(P)=\dim_{\mathsf{M}}(P)
, as long as
\dim_{\mathsf{M}}(P)\geq 2
. Such supporting sets are called regular sets, as defined in
Graf and Luschgy 2007
:

Definition 4

(Regular sets
[
52
]
)
.

A compact set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called regular of dimension
s
if there exist
c>0,r_{0}>0
such that
its Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{s}(S)
satisfies

\frac{1}{c}r^{d}\leq\mathcal{H}^{s}\big(S\cap B(x,r)\big)\leq cr^{d}
, for all
x\in S
and
r\in(0,r_{0})
.

Proposition 9 in
Weed and Bach 2019
provides a list of common regular sets of dimension
s
. This includes, in particular, compact smooth
s
-dimensional manifolds and non-empty compact convex sets supported on an affine
s
-dimensional subspaces. Since
\dim_{\mathsf{W}_{1}}^{*}(P)=\dim_{\mathsf{M}}(P)
for any
P
that is supported on a regular set as defined above, we have
\dim_{\mathsf{W}_{1}}^{*}(P)=\dim^{*}_{\mathsf{SW}_{1}}(P)=\dim_{\mathsf{M}}(P)
in such cases.

6.
Applications

6.1.
Two-Sample Homogeneity Testing

The nonparametric two-sample homoegeneity testing problem seeks to detect difference between two distributions on the same measurable (usually Euclidean) space given only samples from them. For
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\sim P
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}\sim Q
be independent samples from these distributions, respectively. Based on these samples we wish to test whether
P
and
Q
are the same (null hypothesis) or not (alternative), i.e.,

H_{0}:P=Q\ \ \text{versus}\ \ H_{1}:P\neq Q.

For univariate distributions, the standard choice for two-sample homogeneity testing is the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which rejects the null hypothesis for large values
\|F_{n}-G_{m}\|_{\infty}
, where
F_{n}
and
G_{m}
are the corresponding empirical CDFs. Generalizations to multivariate samples take some care to account for the dependence between coordinates, which typically removes the distribution-free property of the limiting statistic. Herein, we propose a multivariate test statistic based on SWD.

Consider a general sequence of 2-sample tests
\phi_{m,n}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n+m}\to\{0,1\}
defined as

\phi_{m,n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}):=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }D_{m,n}>c_{m,n},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
D_{m,n}:=D_{m,n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m})
is a sequence of statistics, and
c_{m,n}
are real numbers. Here, an output of 1 corresponds to rejecting the null. We say that
\phi_{n,m}
is
asymptotically of level

\alpha\in[0,1]
, if
\limsup_{m,n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(D_{m,n}>c_{m,n})\leq\alpha
, for all possible distributions of the statistic
D_{m,n}
under
H_{0}
. We say that
\phi_{n,m}
is
consistent at asymptotic level

\alpha
if it is asymptotically level
\alpha
and
\liminf_{m,n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(D_{m,n}>c_{m,n})=1
for all possible distributions of the statistic
D_{m,n}
under
H_{1}
.

Corollary
3
allows us to use the bootstrap distribution of
W_{m,n}^{B}
for setting critical values. This results in a consistent two sample test.

Proposition 3

(Two-sample testing consistency)
.

Under the setting of Corollary
3
, consider the problem of testing
H_{0}:P=Q
versus
H_{1}:P\neq Q
using the rule

T_{m,n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}):=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }W_{m,n}>c^{B}_{\alpha},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
c^{B}_{\alpha}
is the
(1-\alpha)
-th quantile of
W_{m,n}^{B}
. Then, this test is consistent at asymptotic level
\alpha
.

The result follows immediately from Corollary
3
and the discussion that follows it.

Remark 9

(Comparison to classic Wasserstein testing)
.

OT distances have been

previously considered for both goodness-of-fit
[
33
,
55
]
and homogeneity
[
82
]
testing. These works could only provide critical values for the tests only when
d=1
due to the lack of a multivariate limit distribution theory for
\mathsf{W}_{p}
. Here, we showed that adopting SWD as the figure of merit for testing overcomes this bottleneck.

6.2.
Minimum Expected Smooth Wasserstein Estimation

Generative modeling concerns learning a parametrized model
\{Q_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
that best approximates a data distribution
P
, based on samples from
P
. Proximity between
P
and
Q_{\theta}
is typically measured by a chosen statistical distance, which gives rise to the MDE problem
[
80
]
. Adopting SWD as the distance metric and using the empirical distribution
P_{n}
as an estimate for
P
in the MDE problem gives rise to the minimum SWD estimation (M-SWE) problem

(9)

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\theta}),

with an optimal estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{n}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\theta})
. Results on M-SWE appeared in
[
50
]
, and are reproduced here in Appendix
D.1
.

In practice, the MDE problem in (
9
) is oftentimes intractable due to complexity of the density function of
Q_{\theta}
. Indeed, this is the case for many implicit generative models, such as GANs
[
78
,
7
,
54
]
. Nevertheless, samples
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
from the model
Q_{\theta}
can be readily generated via simulation in such scenarios. This suggests replacing
Q_{\theta}
with its empirical proxy
Q_{\theta,m}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i}}
and adopting the minimum expected distance estimation (MEDE) framework
[
12
,
74
]

(10)

\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\theta,m})\,\big|\,X_{1:n}\big],

where we assume that the
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\sim P
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}\sim Q_{\theta}
samples are independent, and use the shorthand
X_{1:n}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
. We term this problem minimum expected SWD estimation (M-ESWE) and denote its solution by

(11)

\widehat{\theta}_{m,n}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\theta,m})\,\big|\,X_{1:n}\big].

The expectations in (
10
) and (
11
) are w.r.t. the samples
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
from the model. This accounts for the fact that
m
can typically be as large as we like, in which case one might hope that the conditional expectation is close to
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\theta})
from (
9
). We also use the shorthands M-ESWD for the minimized ESWD in (
11
), and M-ESWE for the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{m,n}
achieving the minimized ESWD.

In what follows,
P
and
\{Q_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
are all assumed to be in
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and the parameter space
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
is assumed to be compact with nonempty interior. We will show existence, measurability, and consistency of the M-ESWE, as well as find the limit distribution of
\widehat{\theta}_{m,n}
, when
m=m(n)
is chosen so that
m\gg n
. We start from existence and measurability.

Theorem 5

(M-ESWE existence and measurability)
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume the map
\theta\mapsto Q_{\theta}
is continuous w.r.t. the weak topology. Then, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a measurable function
\widehat{\theta}_{n}(\omega)
such that:

\widehat{\theta}_{n}(\omega)\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\theta,m})\,\big|\,X_{1:n}\big](\omega)\neq\emptyset.

The proof of Theorem
5
is given in Appendix
D.2
. It relies on extending the lower-semicontinuity property to the expected SWD (Lemma
20
), and is otherwise similar to the analogous result on M-SWE (Theorem
10
).

Next, we show that if the number of samples from the parametric model
m
is chosen so that
m=m_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, the empirical M-ESWD from (
10
) converges to the population M-SWD, and the associated M-ESWE is also consistent for
\theta^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\theta}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q_{\theta})
.

This result requires a stronger continuity assumption on the map
\theta\mapsto Q_{\theta}
, similar to Assumption 2.7 in
Bernton et al. 2019
, and Assumption D4 in
[
74
]
.

Theorem 6

(Consistency of M-ESWE)
.

Set
m=m_{n}\to\infty
, and assume that
(a) the map
\theta\mapsto Q_{\theta}
is continuous w.r.t. the weak topology; and (b) if
\theta_{n}\to\theta
, then
\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(Q_{\theta_{n}},Q_{\theta_{n},m_{n}})]\to 0

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. Then, the following hold.

(1)

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\theta,m_{n}})\,\big|\,X_{1:n}\big]\to\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q_{\theta})
as
n\to\infty
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.;

(2)

For any sequence
\{\widehat{\theta}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of measurable estimators such that

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\widehat{\theta}_{n},m_{n}})\,\big|\,X_{1:n}\big]\leq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\theta,m})\,\big|\,X_{1:n}\big]+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

the set of cluster points of
\{\widehat{\theta}_{n}(\omega)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is included in
\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q_{\theta})

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.;

(3)

in particular, if
\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q_{\theta})
is unique, i.e.,
\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q_{\theta})=\{\theta^{\ast}\}
, then
\widehat{\theta}_{n}\to\theta^{\ast}
a.s..

Remark 10

(Continuity of
\theta\mapsto Q_{\theta}
)
.

The extra assumption (b) of Theorem
6
is weaker than Assumption 2.7 of
Bernton et al. 2019
for
m_{n}=n
since
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P,Q)\leq\mathsf{W}_{1}(P,Q)
. A sufficient condition that ensures both (b) and Assumption 2.7 is
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\int|x|^{q}\,\,\mathrm{d}Q_{\theta}<\infty
for some
q>1
. This allows bounding
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}(Q_{\theta_{n}},Q_{\theta_{n},n})\big]
uniformly over
\theta
via Theorem 1 of
[
43
]
. Alternatively, assuming that the map
\theta\mapsto Q_{\theta}
is continuous in
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
also ensures (b).

The proof of Theorem
6
is given in Appendix
D.3
. Statement (i) can be established using epi-covergence of the map
\theta\mapsto\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\theta,m_{n}})\big]
, similar to
Nadjahi et al. 2019
. The last two statements follow directly from (i).

Turning to limit distribution results, results are presented for the ‘well-specified’ scenario, i.e., when
P=Q_{\theta^{\ast}}
for some
\theta^{\ast}
in the interior of
\Theta
. The limit distribution result for M-SWE (Theorem

in Appendix ) was proven in

via an adpatation of the norm differentiability based arguments of
Pollard 1980

12
with an approximation argument produces a limit distribution for the M-ESWE error in the regime

. To simplify the proof (given in Appendix
D.4
), a uniform moment bound over the parametric family
Q_{\theta}
is assumed to hold. This result is stated below.

Theorem 7

(M-ESWD limit distribution)
.

Let
P
satisfy the conditions of Theorem
1
. In addition, suppose that:

(1)

the map
\theta\mapsto Q_{\theta}
is continuous relative to the weak topology;

(2)

P\neq Q_{\theta}
for any
\theta\neq\theta^{\ast}
;

(3)

there exists a vector-valued functional
D^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\mspace{-2mu}\in\mspace{-2mu}(\ell^{\infty}(\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}))^{d_{0}}
such that
\big\|Q^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}_{\theta}\mspace{-2mu}-Q^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}_{\theta^{\ast}}\mspace{-2mu}-\mspace{-2mu}\langle\theta-\theta^{\ast}\mspace{-3mu},\mspace{-2mu}D^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\rangle\big\|_{\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}}\mspace{-2mu}

=o(\|\theta-\theta^{\ast}\|)
as
\theta\to\theta^{\ast}
, where
\langle t,D^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{0}}t_{i}D_{i}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
for
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
;

(4)

the derivative
D^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
is nonsingular in the sense that
\langle t,D^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\rangle\neq 0
, i.e.,
\langle t,D^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\rangle\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0})
is not the zero functional for all
0\neq t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
;

(5)

m=m_{n}\gg n
;

(6)

\sup_{\Theta}\int|x|^{q}\,\,\mathrm{d}Q_{\theta}<\infty
for some
q>2(d+1)
.

Then,

\sqrt{n}\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\theta,m_{n}})\,\big|\,X_{1:n}\big]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}}\big\|G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}-\big<t,D^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\big>\big\|_{\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}},

where
G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
is the Gaussian process from Theorem
1
.

Finally, we establish a limit distribution for the M-ESWE solution. The proof of Theorem
7
shows that under certain assumptions, solution to the M-ESWE problem are also approximate solutions to the M-SWE problem. Combining this fact with a result on the limit distribution of approximate solutions to the M-SWE problem (Corollary
7
), we have the following result (see Appendix
D.5
for a proof).

Theorem 8

(M-ESWE limit distribution)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
7
, let
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
be a sequence
of measurable estimators for
\theta^{*}
satisfying

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\widehat{\theta}_{n},m_{n}})\,\big|\,X_{1:n}\big]\leq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},Q_{\theta,m_{n}})\,\big|\,X_{1:n}\big]+o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2}).

Then, provided that
\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}}\big\|G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}-\big<t,D^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\big>\big\|_{\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}}
is unique a.s., we have
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta^{*})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}}\big\|G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}-\big<t,D^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\big>\big\|_{\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}}
.

,

,

7.1.
Proof of Theorem
1

Recall that
\varphi_{\sigma}
is the density function of
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d})
, i.e.,
\varphi_{\sigma}(x)=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-d/2}e^{-\|x\|^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Since
P_{n}\ast\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
has a density given by

x\mapsto n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{\sigma}(x-X_{i}),
we arrive at the expression

(12)

\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)=\sup_{f\in\mathsf{Lip}_{1}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\ast\varphi_{\sigma}(X_{i})-Pf\ast\varphi_{\sigma}\right].

Observing that the RHS of (
12
) does not change if we replace
f
with
f-f(x^{\ast})
for any fixed
x^{\ast}
,
the problem boils down to showing that the function class
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}:=\{f\ast\varphi_{\sigma}:f\in\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}\}
is
P
-Donsker.

To that end we next derive a bound on the Hölder norm of
f_{\sigma}:=f\ast\varphi_{\sigma}
, for
f\in\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}
. The Donsker property will follow by metric entropy bounds for Hölder balls. For a vector
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})
of
d
nonnegative integers, define the differential operator

D^{k}=\frac{\partial^{|k|}}{\partial x_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{d}^{k_{d}}},

with
|k|=\sum_{i=1}^{d}k_{i}
.
For a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
defined on a bounded set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Hölder norm of order
\alpha>0
is

\|f\|_{\alpha}:=\|f\|_{\alpha,\mathcal{X}}:=\max_{0\leq|k|\leq\underline{\alpha}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\big|D^{k}f(x)\big|+\sup_{\overset{|k|=\underline{\alpha}}{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}}\frac{\big|D^{k}f_{\sigma}(x)-D^{k}f_{\sigma}(y)\big|}{\|x-y\|^{\alpha-\underline{\alpha}}},

where
\underline{\alpha}
is the greatest integer strictly smaller than
\alpha
and the suprema are taken over the interior of
\mathcal{X}
. We have the following lemma (see Appendix
A.1
for the proof).

Lemma 5

(Hölder norm bound)
.

For any
\alpha\geq 1
,
0<\sigma\leq 1
,
f\in\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}
, and a bounded convex
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\|f_{\sigma}\|_{\alpha,\mathcal{X}}\lesssim_{\alpha,d}\sigma^{-\alpha+1}\big(1\vee\sup_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|\big)
.

The following metric entropy bound from
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
will be subsequently used.

Lemma 6

(Metric entropy bound for Hölder ball)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is bounded and convex with nonempty interior. For
\alpha\geq 1
and
M>0
, let
C^{\alpha}_{M}(\mathcal{X})
be the set of continuous functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\|f\|_{\alpha}\leq M
.
Then, for any
0<\epsilon\leq 1
, we have

\ \log N\big(\epsilon M,C_{M}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{X}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)\lesssim_{d,\alpha,\diam(\mathcal{X})}\epsilon^{-d/\alpha}
.

We are now in position to prove Theorem
1
, which applies Theorem 1.1 in
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
to the function class
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
to show that it is
P
-Donsker. We begin with noting that the function class
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
has envelope
F(x):=\|x\|+\sigma\sqrt{d}
, which is
P
-integrable as required under the assumed moment conditions. Indeed, for any
f\in\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}
, since
|f(y)|\leq|f(0)|+\|y\|=\|y\|
, we have

\begin{split}|f_{\sigma}(x)|&\leq\int\|y\|\varphi_{\sigma}(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\int\big(\|x\|+\|x-y\|\big)\varphi_{\sigma}(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\leq\|x\|+\int\|y\|\varphi_{\sigma}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\|x\|+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{2}\varphi_{\sigma}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)^{1/2}=\|x\|+\sigma\sqrt{d}.\end{split}

Next, for each
j
, consider the restriction of
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
to
I_{j}
, denoted as
{\mathcal{F}}_{j}=\{f\mathds{1}_{I_{j}}:f\in{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}\}
.
To invoke
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
, we have to verify that each
\mathcal{F}_{j}
is
P
-Donsker and to bound
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{G}_{n}\|_{{\mathcal{F}}_{j}}]
, where
\mathbb{G}_{n}:=\sqrt{n}(P_{n}-P)
.
In view of Lemma
5
,
{\mathcal{F}}_{j}
can be regarded as a subset of
C_{M^{\prime}_{j}}^{\alpha}(I_{j})
with
\alpha>d/2
and
M_{j}^{\prime}=\sigma^{-\alpha+1}C_{\alpha,d}\sup_{I_{j}}\|x\|
.
Lemma
6
then implies that the
L^{2}(Q)
-metric entropy of
{\mathcal{F}}_{j}
, for any probability measure
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, can be bounded as

\log N\left(\epsilon M_{j}^{\prime}Q(I_{j})^{1/2},{\mathcal{F}}_{j},L^{2}(Q)\right)\lesssim_{\alpha,d,K}\epsilon^{-d/\alpha},

where
K=\sup_{j}\diam(I_{j})<\infty
. For any choice of
\alpha>d/2
, the square root of the RHS is integrable (w.r.t.
\epsilon
) around
0
, so that
\mathcal{F}_{j}
is
P
-Donsker by Theorem 2.5.2 in
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
. By Theorem 2.14.1 in
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
we further obtain

(13)

\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{G}_{n}\|_{{\mathcal{F}}_{j}}]\lesssim_{\alpha,d,K}M_{j}^{\prime}P(I_{j})^{1/2}\lesssim_{\alpha,d}\sigma^{-\alpha+1}M_{j}P(I_{j})^{1/2},

with
M_{j}=1\vee\sup_{I_{j}}\|x\|
. By assumption, the RHS is summable over
j
.

By Theorem 1.1 in
var der Vaart 1996
we conclude that
{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}
is
P
-Donsker, which implies that there exists a tight version of
P
-Brownian bridge process
G_{P}
in
\ell^{\infty}({\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}})
such that
\big(\mathbb{G}_{n}(f)\big)_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}
converges weakly in
\ell^{\infty}({\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}})
to
G_{P}
.
Finally, the continuous mapping theorem yields that

\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)=\|\mathbb{G}_{n}\|_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\sigma}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\to}}\|G_{P}\|_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\sigma}}=\big\|G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}\big\|_{\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}},

where
G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(f):=G_{P}(f\ast\varphi_{\sigma})
. By construction, the Gaussian process
\big(G_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(f)\big)_{f\in\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0}}
is tight in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathsf{Lip}_{1,0})
. The moment bound for
\alpha>d/2
follows from summing up the moment bounds for
{\mathcal{F}}_{j}
.

7.2.
Proof of Theorem
2

The statistic
\sqrt{n}\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}(P_{n},P)
and its limit
L_{P}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
are, respectively, the suprema of the empirical process and its Gaussian process limit over the function class
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
. Given a finite
L^{2}(P)

\delta
-net of functions covering
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
, the difference in the distribution of these suprema can be bound by their difference w.r.t. the
\delta
-net plus approximation error terms. Formally, if
f_{1},\dots,f_{N_{\sigma,\delta}}
is a minimal
\delta
-net as above, where
N_{\sigma,\delta}=N\big(\delta,\mathcal{F}_{\sigma},L^{2}(P)\big)
, then, for any set
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(

\displaystyle\,\|\mathbb{G}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}\in A\Big)-\mathbb{P}\Big(\|G_{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}\in A^{3\eta}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|\|G_{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}-\max_{1\leq i\leq N_{\sigma,\delta}}G_{P}(f_{i})\big|>\eta\Big)}_{\text{(I)}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|\|\mathbb{G}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}-\max_{1\leq i\leq N_{\sigma,\delta}}\mathbb{G}_{n}(f_{i})\big|>\eta\Big)}_{\text{(II)}}

(14)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{1\leq i\leq N_{\sigma,\delta}}\mathbb{G}_{n}(f_{i})\in A^{\eta}\Big)-\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{1\leq i\leq N_{\sigma,\delta}}G_{P}(f_{i})\in A^{2\eta}\Big)}_{\text{(III)}}.

We wish to translate this inequality into a bound on the difference
V_{\sigma,n}-W_{\sigma,n}
, where
\big(V_{\sigma,n},W_{\sigma,n}\big)
are a coupling of
\|\mathbb{G}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}
and
\|G_{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}
. Strassen’s theorem allows us to ensure the existence of such couplings. (see
Chernozhukov et al. 2014a
for the version stated below).

Lemma 7

(Strassen’s theorem)
.

Let
X,Y
be real random variables. If
\mathbb{P}(X\in A)\leq\mathbb{P}(Y\in A^{\delta})+\epsilon
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
, then there exists a coupling
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
of
X
and
Y
such that
\mathbb{P}\big(|\widetilde{X}-\widetilde{Y}|>\delta\big)\leq\epsilon
.

In fact, we will derive an
O_{\mathbb{P}}
rate for the difference
|V_{n}-W_{n}|
, where
V_{n}:=V_{n,\sigma_{n},\delta_{n},\eta_{n}}
and
W_{n}:=W_{n,\sigma_{n},\delta_{n},\eta_{n}}
, for a smoothing parameter
\sigma=\sigma_{n}
that is allowed to decay at a slow enough rate. To that end, first observe that for each fixed
\sigma>0
, the class
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
is pre-Gaussian, which implies the existence of a tight version of the limit process
\big(G_{P}(f)\big),\ f\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
and ensures measurability of
\|G_{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}
. Since
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
is separable w.r.t. pointwise convergence,
\|\mathbb{G}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}
is also measurable.

Thus, if we establish a non-asymptotic (in
n
) bound on the RHS of (
7.2
) and characterizes the exact dependence on
\sigma
, we may set
\sigma=\sigma_{n}
and still obtain
(V_{n},W_{n})
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
, whose difference is bounded in probability. Combining the bounds for each
n
, we get an
O_{\mathbb{P}}
rate for
|V_{n}-W_{n}|
. The rate bound on the Prokhorov distance follows more directly from (
7.2
) without going through Lemma
7
.

We begin with a few technical lemmas that will be required throughout the proof; derivations of the lemmas are given in Appendix
B.1
. The first is a bound on
L^{2}
entropy classic of functions with unbounded support. This provides control on
N_{\sigma,\delta}
for a given discretization error level
\delta
.

Lemma 8

(Example 19.9 in
van der Vaart 1998
)
.

Let
\{I_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be an enumeration of unit cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with vertices on integer lattice points. If
\mathcal{F}
is a function class such that for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
the restriction
{\mathcal{F}}_{j}=\{f\mathds{1}_{I_{j}}:f\in{\mathcal{F}}\}
belongs to the Hölder class
C^{\alpha}_{M^{\prime}_{j}}(I_{j})
, for
\alpha>0
and
M_{j}>0
, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{j}\subset C^{\alpha}_{M^{\prime}_{j}}(I_{j})
, then

(15)

\log N_{[\,]}(\delta,\mathcal{F},L^{2}(P))\lesssim_{\alpha,d}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{d}{\alpha}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\big((M^{\prime}_{j})^{2}P(I_{j})\big)^{\frac{d}{d+2\alpha}}\right]^{\frac{d+2\alpha}{2\alpha}}.

The next lemma relates the probability law of the maximum of (centered) i.i.d. random vectors to that of the maximum of i.i.d. Gaussians with same covariance matrices. This will enable bounding term (III) in (
7.2
).

Lemma 9

(Theorem 3.1 in
Chernozhukov et al. 2016
simplified)
.

Suppose that
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. zero mean
\mathbb{R}^{p}
-valued random vectors
(p\geq 2)
with covariance matrix
\Sigma
and coordinate-wise finite absolute third moments, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{ij}|^{3}\big]<\infty
, for all
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq p
. Consider the statistic
Z:=\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}
. Let
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
and define
\widetilde{Z}:=\max_{1\leq j\leq p}Y_{j}
. Then, for every
\eta>0
and
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\mathbb{P}(Z\in A)\leq\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{Z}\in A^{C_{7}\eta})+\frac{C_{8}(\log p)^{2}}{\eta^{3}\sqrt{n}}\big(L_{n}+M_{n,X}(\eta)+M_{n,Y}(\eta)\big),

where
C_{7},C_{8}
are universal positive constants,
L_{n}=\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{ij}|^{3}\right]
, and

\displaystyle M_{n,X}(\eta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq p}|X_{ij}|^{3}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\max_{1\leq j\leq p}|{X}_{ij}|>\eta\sqrt{n}/\log p\right\}}\right],

\displaystyle M_{n,Y}(\eta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq p}|Y_{j}|^{3}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\max_{1\leq j\leq p}|{Y}_{j}|>\eta\sqrt{n}/\log p\right\}}\right].

We also use a Fuk-Nagev type inequality (Theorem 2,
Adamczkak 2010
) for empirical process concentration to bound the term (II) in (
7.2
).

Lemma 10

(Fuk-Nagev type inequality)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a function class
f:S\to\mathbb{R}
with a measurable envelope
F
, that is separable w.r.t. pointwise convergence, and let
P\in\mathcal{P}(S)
. Suppose that
Pf=0
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
, and that
F\in L^{p\vee 2}(P)
.
For
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
i.i.d. according to

define

and let
\sigma^{2}>0
be any positive constant such that

. Then

\mathbb{P}\big(Z\geq(1+\alpha)\mathbb{E}[Z]+x\big)\leq e^{-cx^{2}/(n\sigma^{2})}+\frac{c^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[M^{p}]}{x^{p}},\quad\forall\,\alpha,x>0,

where

and

are positive constants that depend only on

and
\alpha
.

Finally, we also use the following relation between moments of random variables and discrete sums approximating such moments.

Lemma 11

(Sufficient moment condition)
.

Let
\{\mspace{-1.5mu}I_{j}\mspace{-1.5mu}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be an enumeration of unit cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with vertices on integer lattice points, disjointified such that they form a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
P\|x\|_{\infty}^{l}=M<\infty
for any

, then we have
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\big(\sup_{I_{j}}\|x\|\big)^{p_{1}}P(I_{j})^{p_{2}}

\lesssim_{l,d,p_{1},p_{2}}M^{p_{2}}<\infty
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Proof of (i):
First, let
\sigma,\delta\in(0,1]
be arbitrary; these parameters will be specified later. Recall that we fix a smoothness parameter
\alpha>d/2
and let
a=\alpha-1,\,b=d/(2\alpha)
. Note that, if
f_{1},\dots,f_{N_{\sigma,\delta}}
is a minimal
L^{2}(P)

\delta
-net of
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
, then

(16)

\begin{split}\left|\|\mathbb{G}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}-\max_{1\leq i\leq N_{\sigma,\delta}}\mathbb{G}_{n}(f_{i})\right|&\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}:\\
\|f-g\|_{L^{2}(P)}<\delta\end{subarray}}\big|\mathbb{G}_{n}(f-g)\big|,\\
\left|\|G_{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}-\max_{1\leq i\leq N_{\sigma,\delta}}G_{P}(f_{i})\right|&\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}:\\
\|f-g\|_{L^{2}(P)}<\delta\end{subarray}}\big|G_{P}(f)-G_{P}(g)\big|.\end{split}

For the second inequality, we extend

to the linear span of
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
so that its sample paths are linear, and the RHS may be written as a supremum of

Step 1

\log N\big(\delta,\mathcal{F}_{\sigma},L^{2}(P)\big)\lesssim_{\alpha,d}K\sigma^{-d\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\delta^{-\frac{d}{\alpha}}\lesssim_{\alpha,d,M,l}\sigma^{-2ab}\delta^{-2b},

where

by Lemma

.

Step 2
(Empirical process discretization error).
Let

\mathbb{R}^{d}

and

{\mathcal{F}}_{j}=\{f\mathds{1}_{I_{j}}:f\in{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}\}
. Clearly,

is an envelope function for

. Then, using Lemma

with

and

(which are feasible choices by Lemma

), we have, for any probability measure

on a finite set,
\log N\big(\delta M^{\prime}_{j}Q(I_{j})^{1/2},{\mathcal{F}}_{j},L^{2}(Q)\big)\lesssim_{\alpha,d}\delta^{-2b}
. Observing that

(18)

N\left(2\delta M^{\prime}_{j}Q(I_{j})^{1/2},{\mathcal{F}}_{j}(\delta),L^{2}(Q)\right)\leq N\left(\delta M^{\prime}_{j}Q(I_{j})^{\frac{1}{2}},{\mathcal{F}}_{j},L^{2}(Q)\right)^{2},

we obtain

.

For the difference class
\mathcal{F}_{j}(\delta)
,
2F_{j}
is an envelope function. Next, we compute the entropy integral

8.
Concluding Remarks and Future Directions

This work conducted a statistical study of the SWD
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
, which convolves the considered distributions with an isotropic Gaussian kernel. Via KR duality, the 1-Wasserstein distance can be viewed as an IPM w.r.t. the class of 1-Lipschitz functions. The Gaussian convolution reduces the complexity of this class, allows to show that it is Donsker, and, in turn, enables a comprehensive statistical analysis of the empirical SWD. We established limit distribution, bootstrap consistency, concentration, and strong approximation results for the empirical smooth distance. Furthermore, we showed that its rate of convergence in expectation adapts to the intrinsic dimension through the smoothing parameter. This was used to derive sharp convergence rates for empirical
\mathsf{W}_{1}
(up to log factors) for unboundedly supported population distributions. We also studied applications to two-sample homogeneity testing and minimum SWD estimation on parametric families, proving consistency of tests for the former and deriving measurability, consistency, and limit distributions for the latter.

While this work focused on statistical aspects of empirical SWD, an important future avenue is to develop an efficient algorithm for computing it. While any computational method for classic
\mathsf{W}_{1}
is also applicable for the smooth framework (by sampling the kernel), we target algorithms that are tailored to exploit the Gaussian convolution structure. The simplest form of the computational question reduces to computing
\mathsf{W}_{1}
between Gaussian mixtures, which we plan to explore in the future. Another useful idea on the computational front is to replace the (analytically convenient) Gaussian kernel with a compactly supported smoothing kernel, so as to have control over the domain in which distributions are supported. Other possible future directions include evaluating asymptotic power and relative efficiency for two-sample homogeneity testing based on
\mathsf{W}_{1}^{\mspace{1mu}\sigma}
, and modifying the statistic
W_{m,n}
to test for independence of paired observations. While this paper extensively exploits the dual form of the smoothed transport problem, it would also be interesting to study the convergence rate of optimal empirical couplings, appropriately defined, to the set of optimal couplings of the population distributions. Properties of such smoothed couplings between measures, such as cross-correlations between the involved variables, are also of interest.
