A probabilistic analysis on general probabilistic scheduling problems
Daiki Suruga

Abstract
The scheduling problem is a key class of optimization problems and has various kinds of applications both in practical and theoretical scenarios. In the scheduling problem, probabilistic analysis is a basic tool for investigating performance of scheduling algorithms, and therefore has been carried out by plenty amount of prior works. However, probabilistic analysis has several potential problems. For example, current research interest in the scheduling problem is limited to i.i.d. scenarios, due to its simplicity for analysis. This paper provides a new framework for probabilistic analysis in the scheduling problem and aims to deal with such problems. As a consequence, we obtain several theorems including a theoretical limit of the scheduling problem which can be applied to general, non-i.i.d. probability distributions . Several information theoretic techniques, such as information-spectrum method , turned out to be useful to prove our results. Since the scheduling problem has relations to many other research fields, our framework hopefully yields other interesting applications in the future.

1.1
Background

Scheduling problems

The scheduling problem is a class of optimization problems in which we want to allocate a collection of jobs on machines appropriately in order to minimize (or maximize) a certain cost function.
In the most fundamental form, the subjective is to minimize the
makespan
, which is the total completion time of all machines which need to process all of the allocated jobs.
The scheduling problem has been introduced explicitly over 70 years ago
[
1
]
, and plays a central role in various kinds of research fields since then.

In this paper we specifically focus on the
uniform-machines scheduling problem
, (which includes the
identical-machines scheduling problem

which is one of the most well-investigated problems among many variants of scheduling problems.
In the uniform-machines scheduling problem, there are
m

on machine
i

i.e., the processing time is determined as

divided by the speed of the
i

v_{i}~(1\leq i\leq m)

The uniform-machines scheduling problem has so many applications in many kinds of practical scenarios.
This scheduling problem appears in real-world situations, such as production lines, university, hospitals, and computer systems (see
[
3

This is partially why the uniform machines scheduling problem has been paid much attention.
In addition to the importance in real-world situations, the uniform-machines scheduling problem also provides several interesting aspects in theoretical computer science.

Since NP-hardness and NP-completeness are the central concepts in complexity theory,

As the NP-hardness implies, computing the exact solution is usually really hard, and therefore

Due to practical importance as well as theoretical interest,

including operations research and computer science.

Probabilistic analysis

Today, there are many algorithms (often called as
heuristics
or
policies
) for the scheduling problem, such as
the list scheduling (LS) algorithm and the largest processing time (LPT).
In such cases, to select an appropriate scheduling algorithm for one’s purpose, the one need to evaluate each algorithm with an appropriately chosen measure of performance.
One such candidate of the measure is the worst-case performance evaluation: For an scheduling algorithm
A
, the worst-case performance of the algorithm
A
is given by
\mathrm{WORST}(A):=\max_{j^{n}\in J^{n}}A(j^{n})
where
A(j^{n})
denotes the makespan of the algorithm with input
j^{n}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n})
, which is a list of
n
jobs.
This measure of performance gives a theoretical guarantee that any scheduling instance of the algorithm
A
always has the makespan less than (or equal to) the quantity
\mathrm{WORST}(A)
.
This is one benefit of the worst-case analysis.
However the worst-case analysis has the crucial weak point: The makespan
A(j^{n})
of an input
j^{n}
is often significantly shorter than the value
\mathrm{WORST}(A)
.
In other words, the hardest list of jobs:
j^{n}_{\mathrm{HARD}}:=\mathrm{argmax}A(j^{n})
may be really unlikely to happen in practice.
Today, there are several other measures of performance applied to scheduling algorithm, to overcome the weak point of the worst-case analysis.

The average-case analysis, which is the other well-known method of the evaluation, can deal with the weak point of the worst-case analysis.
In the average-case analysis, a distribution
\Pr_{J^{n}}
on
J^{n}
is defined appropriately in order to reflect a practical situation,
and performance of an algorithm
A
is given as the expectation
\mathrm{AVE}(A,\Pr_{J^{n}}):=\mathbf{E}_{j^{n}\sim J^{n}}[A(j^{n})]
under the distribution
\Pr_{J^{n}}
.
As is easily seen, this analysis overcomes the weak point of the worst-case analysis, since jobs which occur with small probability affect the value
\mathrm{AVE}(A,\Pr_{J^{n}})
not so much, even if makespans of the jobs are really large.
Therefore, the average-case analysis is usually more appropriate for practical situations and has been paid attention in the scheduling problem.

As mentioned above, the average-case analysis has been paid attention over decades,
and therefore there is a certain number of works that analyze average-case behaviors of scheduling algorithms (for example,

For recent results on the LPT algorithm, see Ref.

For its practical importance as well as theoretical interests, the average-case analysis on the scheduling problem has been investigated in many prior works.

Considering the usefulness of the average-case analysis, it may seem that the average-case analysis is the best way to evaluate performance of scheduling algorithms, and has no weak point at all.
However, the average-case analysis has at least two following issues.
First, the average-case analysis usually need to assume i.i.d. conditions on distributions
\Pr_{J^{n}}
, which should reflect a practical situation appropriately.
Indeed, to the best of our knowledge, almost all the previous works

Jobs may form a Markov chain.
Second, in contrast to the worst-case analysis, the expectation

This means, the makespan

These are the two issues of the average-case analysis, and

1.2
Our contributions

As mentioned in Section
1.1
, one of the issues regarding the average-case analysis
is that current research focus only on the i.i.d. distributions, even though non-i.i.d. distributions will occur in many practical cases.
Therefore, in this paper, we focus on the asymptotic behavior of optimal scheduling algorithm under
non-i.i.d., general probability distributions
.

Our results can be divided into three parts.

First Result:

The asymptotic limit of the expectation under general probability distributions in Section
4
,

Second Result (main result):

The asymptotic limit of a new performance measure under general probability distributions in Section
5
,

Third Result:

Precise analysis on a new performance measure in case of the i.i.d. distributions in Section
6
.

In First Result, we characterize the asymptotic limit of the expectation of the optimal algorithm under general probability distributions.
The main result of this paper is Second Result. Second Result aims to resolve both of the two issues mentioned in Section
1.1
.
The two issues say that both the worst-case analysis and the average-case analysis may not appropriate for practical scenarios,
and hence another performance measure needs to be introduced.
The new performance measure is then suitably defined to resolve such issues. Even though the new performance measure had not been introduced in scheduling theory,
similar measures have played as natural measures of performance in several research fields such as in Information theory.
Second Result is devoted to the asymptotic characterization of the new performance measure in general probability distributions, which is successfully done by applying the well-established technique called
Information-spectrum method
.
Third Result provides more precise analysis, without relying on the asymptotic limit, on the new measure of performance in case of i.i.d. distributions.
Let us explain each of the results separately in detail.

1.2.1
First result

Our first result examines the asymptotic limit of the expectation of the optimal algorithm:

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{E}_{J^{n}}[\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},J^{n})],

where
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}},J^{n})
is the makespan of the optimal algorithm
\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}}
with input
J^{n}
.
Note that we need to use “
\limsup
” instead of “
\lim
” because of the generality of probability distributions.
In Section
4
, we characterize this quantity by another quantity which is expressed as the limit of the expectation of random variables.
(See Equation (
6
) for the explicit expression.)
Even though our characterization may seem difficult to compute, it becomes simpler in many important cases by applying techniques from probability theory.
For example,
a simplified proposition,
Proposition
1
, is obtained from the law of large numbers applied to i.i.d. scenarios,
and another simplified proposition, Proposition
2
, is obtained from the ergodic theorem applied to markov distributions.
Note that as a prior work, Ref.
[
15
]
shows the same result in case of the i.i.d. distributions.
Compared to Ref.
[
15
]
, our result is proved in an arguably simpler way because only combinatorial approach is used to show Proposition
1
,
whereas Ref.
[
15
]
uses several other non-trivial techniques such as linear programming and its Lagrange relaxation.

1.2.2
Second result

As mentioned, a new measure of performance is introduced in order to evaluate practical performance appropriately.
In short, our new measure discards a set
S_{n}
of jobs that are
unlikely to occur
,
and evaluates the worst-case performance over the set
S^{c}_{n}
of jobs which are likely to occur.
2
2

2

A^{c}
denotes the complement set of
A
.

Formally, the new measure is defined as follows.

Definition 1
.

Let
S_{n}\subseteq J^{n}
be a set of jobs unlikely to occur, i.e.,
\Pr(S_{n})
is close to zero.
For a scheduling algorithm
\phi_{n}
on
J^{n}
,
the new measure
\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})
is defined as

\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})=\max_{j^{n}\in S_{n}^{c}}\phi_{n}(j^{n})

where
\phi_{n}(j^{n})
is the makespan by the algorithm
\phi_{n}
with an input
j^{n}
.

This measure indeed captures the worst-case performance in a practical scenario,
while the average-case analysis fails to do so.
To see this, consider a scenario where a scheduling algorithm
\phi_{n}
processes an input
j^{n}\in J^{n}
only when the input belongs to the set
S^{c}_{n}
; otherwise the jobs
j^{n}
are discarded and not scheduled to machines.
In this scenario, the value
\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})
obviously corresponds to the worst-case makespan produced by the algorithm
\phi_{n}
.
In our main contributions, Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
, we successfully analyze fundamental behaviors of this new measure of performance.
In Theorem
1
, we evaluate the limit:
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})
for efficient algorithms

3

3

We often simply call a pair
(\phi_{n},S_{n})
as an
algorithm
.

(\phi_{n},S_{n})

whose discarding probability asymptotically vanishes, i.e.,
\Pr_{J^{n}}(S_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
More precisely, in Theorem
1
we evaluate the smallest possible value of
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})
when
\Pr_{J^{n}}(S_{n})
must satisfy
\Pr_{J^{n}}(S_{n})\to 0
.
To state the result formally, we introduce the notion of
rate
as follows:

Definition 2
.

The rate
R>0
is achievable if and only if there is a (sequence of) scheduling algorithm(s):
\{(\phi_{n},S_{n})\}_{n}

such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr_{J^{n}}(S_{n})=0
and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})\leq R
holds.

Our goal is then to evaluate the infimum of the achievable rate:

\inf\{R>0\mid\text{The rate $R$ is achievable.}\}
,
and we characterize this quantity as follows:

Theorem 1
.

For any
m
-uniform machines scheduling problem,

\inf\{R>0\mid\text{The rate $R$ is achievable.}\}=\bar{E}

holds
where

\bar{E}:=\inf\{\alpha\geq 0\mid\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\{j^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\sum_{i\leq n}T(j_{i})>\alpha\}=0\}
,

v_{\mathrm{sum}}
is the sum of speeds of all machines and
T(j_{i})
denotes the processing time of a job
j_{i}
at unit speed.

Let us mention several remarks regarding Theorem
1
.
First and foremost, Theorem
1
assumes almost nothing, and thus can be applied to any scheduling problem with any kind of distribution.
In particular, we can apply Theorem
1
to scheduling problems with non-i.i.d. distributions such as Markov processes, ergodic processes.
In the case of Markov processes, for example, the value
\bar{E}
turns out to be equal to
\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}_{J}[T(J)]
where
\mathbf{E}_{J}[T(J)]
denotes the expectation of the processing time
T(J)
with respect to its stationary distribution.
Second, considering that the discarding probability
\Pr_{J^{n}}(S_{n})
must need to vanish asymptotically,
one may expect that there is asymptotically no difference between the worst-case measure
\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{WORST}[\mathrm{OPT}_{n}]
and our evaluation
\bar{E}
.
However, this is not true. Even in a simple i.i.d. scenario, we see that
\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{WORST}[\mathrm{OPT}_{n}]\to\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{\mathrm{max}}
where
T_{\mathrm{max}}:=\max_{j}T(j)
, but
\bar{E}=\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}[T(J)]
.
This means that there is a certain amount of savings by admitting the negligible probability of discarding.
Lastly, for several important cases such as i.i.d. scenarios, the quantity
\bar{E}
and the average case evaluation
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{E}_{J}[\mathrm{OPT}_{n}(J)]/n
coincide.

1.2.3
Third result

Even though our first result successfully characterizes the asymptotic rate of any scheduling problem with any distribution of jobs,
the result only gives the optimal rate in the asymptotic setting.
Needless to say, there are many practical situations where one may want to estimate the optimal value of
\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})
for finite number of jobs, e.g.,
n=1000
.
To deal with this problem, in our third result, we employ more precise analysis on the value
\mathrm{COST}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n})

where
(\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}},S_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}})
is an optimal scheduling (with discarding probability
\varepsilon
) defined as
(\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}},S_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}})=\mathrm{argmin}_{\Pr(S_{n})\leq\varepsilon}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})
,
and characterize this value accurately for any finite
n
, up to an additive constant factor.
Our third result can be applied to any kind of i.i.d. scheduling problem.
The formal statement of our third result is as follows.

Theorem 2
.

For any i.i.d. scheduling problem,

\mathrm{COST}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n})=\frac{n}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}[T(J)]-\frac{\sqrt{V(T(J))n}}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\Phi^{-1}(\varepsilon)+\Theta(1).

holds where
\Phi(x)
is the standard Gaussian distribution and
V(T(J)):=\mathbf{E}_{J}[(T(J)-\mathbf{E}[T(J)])^{2}]
.

1.2.4
Our framework and Information theory

Even though our results may seem no obvious relation to Information theory, they actually have something in common in spirit.
This section briefly describes the underlying connection between our framework and fixed length noiseless coding in Information theory.

In fixed length noiseless coding scheme, a coding is defined as a pair
\phi_{n}=(\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{Enc}},\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{Dec}})

where an encoder
\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{Enc}}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\{1,\ldots,M_{n}\}
maps a list of
n
symbols
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}

to an integer
\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{Enc}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\{1,\ldots,M_{n}\}
, and a decoder
\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{Dec}}
does the same reversely.
In this scheme,
n
symbols in
\mathcal{X}^{n}
are randomly generated, and the error probability is introduced as the probability of the event
S_{n}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}^{n}\mid\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{Dec}}\circ\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{Enc}}(x)\neq x\}
.

The seminal paper
[
29
]
shows that the rate “
\lim\frac{1}{n}\log M_{n}
” of an optimal coding subject to
\Pr(S_{n})\to 0
satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log M_{n}=H(X)

(1)

when the symbols are i.i.d.
(
H(X)
is the entropy of the randomly generated symbol on
\mathcal{X}
.)
This result characterizes the
asymptotic
behavior of the optimal rate for
i.i.d. symbols
.
Since the original result is limited to i.i.d. scenarios, Ref.
[
30
]
established a novel method,
Information-Spectrum method
, to deal with non i.i.d. scenarios
and showed that the optimal rate is, instead of the entropy
H(X)
, characterized as

\bar{H}(X):=\inf\left\{\alpha\geq 0\mid\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\{x^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{\Pr_{\mathcal{X}^{n}}(x^{n})}>\alpha\}=0\right\}

(2)

for general probability distributions.
There is another way of generalizing the original result called
second-order asymptotics
(e.g.,
[
31
,
32
]
) which investigates non-asymptotic aspects of fixed length coding.
Without relying on the limit, Ref.
[
31
]
characterizes the optimal rate as

\log M_{n}=n\cdot H(X)+\sqrt{nV(X)}\Phi^{-1}(\varepsilon)-\frac{1}{2}\log n+\Theta(1)

for i.i.d. symbols. (
V(X)
is the variance of
\log\frac{1}{\Pr(X)}
and
\varepsilon:=\Pr(S_{n})
.)

There are several similarities observed between fixed length coding scheme and our framework for scheduling problem.
Job are randomly generated, the set
S_{n}
represents the event of error, and, the notion of rate
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})
is defined in a similar fashion
to fixed length coding.
Due to such similarities, this paper successfully adapts the information-spectrum method to our framework and obtains Theorem
1
which deals with non-i.i.d. scenarios.
This paper also adapts the second order asymptotic method and obtains Theorem
2
.
(We also obtain a result corresponding to the strong converse theorem, which is left to Appendix
A
.)
Even though several modifications are necessary to adapt these techniques to our framework, our results certainly have some connection to Information theory.

3
Lemmas

Here we prove several basic or fundamental lemmas required to show our main theorems.

Lemma 1
.

For an optimal scheduling
(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S_{n})
, i.e.,
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})=\min_{\phi_{n}^{\prime}}\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n}^{\prime},j^{n})
for any
j^{n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}
,

\frac{1}{m}\left(1-\frac{m}{n}\right)\frac{T_{\mathrm{min}}}{v_{\mathrm{max}}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S_{n})\leq\frac{1}{m}\left(1+\frac{m}{n}\right)\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}

holds where
T_{\mathrm{min}}:=\min_{j\in\mathcal{J}}T(j)
,
T_{\mathrm{max}}:=\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}}T(j)
,

v_{\mathrm{max}}:=\max_{i\leq m}v_{m}
and
v_{\mathrm{min}}:=\min_{i\leq m}v_{m}
.

Proof.

Suppose
j^{n}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n})
satisfies
T(j_{i})=T_{\mathrm{min}}
for any
i\leq n
.
This list of jobs
j^{n}
trivially yields the minimum makespan among all jobs in
\mathcal{J}^{n}
when a scheduling is optimal.
We now evaluate
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})
.
First, consider a scenario where each machine has the speed
v_{\mathrm{max}}
, faster than the actual one.
Then by an analogue of the pigeon hole principle, the optimal makespan becomes
(1_{\{c^{\prime}\neq 0\}}+n^{\prime})T_{\mathrm{min}}/v_{\mathrm{max}}

where
n^{\prime}
and
c
are two natural numbers satisfying
n=n^{\prime}m+c~(0\leq c<m)
.
Since the actual machines are slower, we have

\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})\geq(1_{\{c^{\prime}\neq 0\}}+n^{\prime})\frac{T_{\mathrm{min}}}{v_{\mathrm{max}}}

which leads to

\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})\geq(1_{\{c^{\prime}\neq 0\}}+n^{\prime})\frac{T_{\mathrm{min}}}{v_{\mathrm{max}}}\geq n^{\prime}\frac{T_{\mathrm{min}}}{v_{\mathrm{max}}}=\frac{n-c}{m}\frac{T_{\mathrm{min}}}{v_{\mathrm{max}}}\geq\frac{n-m}{m}\frac{T_{\mathrm{min}}}{v_{\mathrm{max}}}.

By dividing the both sides with
n
, we get

\frac{1}{m}\left(1-\frac{m}{n}\right)\frac{T_{\mathrm{min}}}{v_{\mathrm{max}}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S_{n}).

On the other hand, suppose
j^{n}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n})
satisfies
T(j_{i})=T_{\mathrm{max}}
for any
i\leq n
.
Then, in a similar manner, we have

\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})=(1_{\{c^{\prime}\neq 0\}}+n^{\prime})\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}\leq(1+n^{\prime})\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}=\frac{m+n-c}{m}\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}\leq\frac{n+m}{m}\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}.

This yields

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S_{n})\leq\frac{1}{m}\left(1+\frac{m}{n}\right)\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}

which completes proof.
∎

By defining a sequence
\{(\phi_{n},S_{n})\}
as
\phi_{n}=\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}}
and
S_{n}=\emptyset
, and by taking the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}
,
we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

The optimal rate
R(\mathcal{S}_{m})
belongs to the interval
[T_{\mathrm{min}}/(m\cdot v_{\mathrm{max}}),T_{\mathrm{max}}/(m\cdot v_{\mathrm{min}})]
.

Lemma 2
.

For any
n
, there is a scheduler
\phi_{n}
such that, for any list of jobs
j^{n}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n})
,

\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n},j^{n})\leq\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})+\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}

(3)

holds.

Proof.

Take the optimal scheduler
\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\textrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})=\min_{\phi_{n}^{\prime}}\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n}^{\prime},j^{n})
for any
j^{n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}
.
We prove Equation (
3
) by assuming the opposite inequality
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})>\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})+\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}
and giving a contradiction.

Suppose
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})>\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})+\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}
holds.
In this case, there needs to be one or more machines which finish all of the allocated jobs before the time
T_{n}(j^{n})/v_{\mathrm{sum}}
with the scheduling
\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n}
on the jobs
j^{n}
.
To see this, let us calculate the maximum amount of jobs that the machines are able to process within the time
T_{n}(j^{n})/v_{\mathrm{sum}}
.
The machine 1 is able to process at most
v_{1}\cdot T_{n}(j^{n})/v_{\mathrm{sum}}
amount of jobs within the time
T_{n}(j^{n})/v_{\mathrm{sum}}
, which is followed by the definition of the speed.
Applying this idea for all
i\leq m
, we see that, for any
i\leq m
,
each machine
i
is able to process at most
v_{i}\cdot T_{n}(j^{n})/v_{\mathrm{sum}}
amount of jobs within the time
T_{n}(j^{n})/v_{\mathrm{sum}}
.
This implies the amount of jobs processed by all of the machines with the time
T_{n}(j^{n})/v_{\mathrm{sum}}
is

v_{1}\frac{T_{n}(j^{n})}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}+v_{2}\frac{T_{n}(j^{n})}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}+\cdots+v_{m}\frac{T_{n}(j^{n})}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}=T_{n}(j^{n})

(4)

which equals to the entire amount of jobs the machines needed to process, if the jobs are fully assigned.
By the assumption of
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})
which is strictly larger than the time
T_{n}(j^{n})/v_{\mathrm{sum}}
,
we therefore see that there is one or more machines which finish all of the allocated jobs before the time
T_{n}(j^{n})/v_{\mathrm{sum}}
.
We label one such machines by
i_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
.

The assumption,
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})>\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})+\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}
,
also means that there exists one or more jobs whose processing starts later than
\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})
and finishes exactly at
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})

since
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})-\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})>T_{\mathrm{max}}/v_{\mathrm{min}}
holds and any job processed by any machine requires processing time less than or equal to
T_{\mathrm{max}}/v_{\mathrm{min}}
.
Then, the job can be re-assigned to the machine
i_{0}
, which finishes all of it’s assigned jobs earlier than
\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})
.
After re-assigning the job, we recursively re-assign other jobs whose processing finishes exactly at
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})
in the same manner until all the jobs have been re-assigned to such machines.
This new scheduling has the makespan strictly less than
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})
. This contradicts the fact that
\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n}
is the optimal scheduling and therefore obtain

\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})\leq\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})+\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3
.

For any
n
, any scheduler
\phi_{n}
and any list of jobs
j^{n}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n})
,

\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})\leq\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n},j^{n})

(5)

holds.

Proof.

Take the optimal scheduler
\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\textrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})=\min_{\phi_{n}^{\prime}}\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n}^{\prime},j^{n})
for any
j^{n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}
.
We then only need to show
\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})\leq\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})
.
Suppose the opposite direction
\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})>\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})
holds.
This means that every machine completes the assigned jobs earlier than the time
\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})
.
Therefore, the sum of the amount of jobs processed by all machines is less than
T_{n}(j^{n})
which follows from the same argument as in Equation
4
.
This leads to a contradiction since the sum of the amount of jobs for all machines must be equal to
T_{n}(j^{n})
, as desired.
∎

4
The average case analysis

From Lemma
2
and Lemma
3
, we have

\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})\leq\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n},j^{n})\leq\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})+\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}.

Divide each of them by
n
and take
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
yields the following equation (
6
).

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{E}_{J^{n}}[\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},J^{n})]=\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{E}_{J^{n}}[T_{n}(J^{n})]

(6)

for any scheduling problem
\mathcal{S}_{m}
.
Using the same argument, we obtain several propositions for important classes of probability distributions.

The i.i.d. scenario:

Suppose
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\}
is a sequence of i.i.d. distributions of
P_{\mathcal{J}}
.
Then, by the law of large numbers on the random variable
\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T(J)
, we have the following corollary.

Proposition 1
.

For any i.i.d. scheduling problem,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{E}_{J^{n}}[\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},J^{n})]=\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}_{J}[T(J)].

Markov distributions:

Suppose the distribution
P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}
is Markovian, i.e.,

P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}(j^{n})=P(j_{n}|j_{n-1})P(j_{n-1}|j_{n-2})\cdots P(j_{2}|j_{1})P(j_{1}).

Then, we see
\frac{1}{nv_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})\overset{a.s.}{\to}\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}_{J}[T(J)]
by the ergodic theorem.
Considering the fact that almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability, we then obtain the following corollary:

Proposition 2
.

For any Markov scheduling problem,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{E}_{J^{n}}[\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},J^{n})]=\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}_{J}[T(J)].

5
Characterization of optimal rate

This section is devoted to prove the following theorem.

Theorem
1
.

For any scheduling problem
\mathcal{S}_{m}
,
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=R(\mathcal{S}_{m})
holds.

We also give several important applications of Theorem
1
in Section
5.2
.

5.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Using Lemma
2
and Lemma
3
, we show Theorem
1
as follows.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We show
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})\geq R(\mathcal{S}_{m})
and
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})\leq R(\mathcal{S}_{m})
separately.

[Proof of
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})\geq R(\mathcal{S}_{m})
]: For any
\gamma>0
, we show that the rate
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})+\gamma
is achievable by constructing an appropriate sequence of scheduling
\{(\phi_{n},S_{n})\}
.

By the definition of
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\{j^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})>\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})+\gamma\}=0
.
Define
S_{n}:=\{j^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})>\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})+\gamma\}
and
\phi_{n}
as a scheduler in Lemma
2
,
we now see
\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\{j^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})>\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})+\gamma\}=0
.
Therefore, the rest is to show

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})\leq\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})+\gamma.

First, by Lemma
2
,

\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},j^{n})\leq\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})+\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}

(7)

holds for any
j^{n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}
.
We also observe that for any
j^{n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}\setminus S_{n}
,
\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})\leq n(\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})+\gamma)
holds by the definition of
S_{n}
.
Therefore, taking
\max_{j^{n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}\setminus S_{n}}
on both sides of Equation (
7
) yields

\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n}):=\max_{j^{n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}\setminus S_{n}}\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n},j^{n})\leq\max_{j^{n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}\setminus S_{n}}\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})+\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}\leq n(\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})+\gamma)+\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}.

Therefore, taking
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
yields

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})\leq\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})+\gamma

which completes proof.

[Proof of
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})\leq R(\mathcal{S}_{m})
]:
To prove this, assume
R(\mathcal{S}_{m})<\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
and derive a contradiction.
Let
\gamma>0
be a small real number and suppose
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-2\gamma
is achievable. That is, there is a sequence
\{(\phi_{n},S_{n})\}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}(S_{n})=0\text{~and~}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})\leq\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-2\gamma

hold. This implies that there is
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n\geq n_{0}
,

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})\leq\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\gamma

holds. Therefore, by the definition of
\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})
, we have

\forall n\geq n_{0},\quad\forall j^{n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}\setminus S_{n},\quad\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n},j^{n})\leq n(\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\gamma)

which implies that, for any
n\geq n_{0}
and any
j^{n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}
,

\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n},j^{n})>n(\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\gamma)\Longrightarrow j^{n}\in S_{n}.

Together with the monotonicity of the probability measure:
A\subseteq B\Rightarrow P(A)\leq P(B)
,
we have for any
n\geq n_{0}
,

P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}(S_{n})\geq P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}(\{j^{n}\mid\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n},j^{n})>n(\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\gamma)\}).

(8)

On the other hand, the definition of
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
ensures that there is
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that

\exists\{n_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{N}\text{~s.t.~}\forall i\in\mathbb{N},\quad P_{\mathcal{J}^{n_{i}}}(\{j^{n_{i}}\mid\frac{1}{{n_{i}}\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n_{i}}(j^{n_{i}})>\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\gamma\})\geq\varepsilon_{0}.

(9)

Combining Lemma
3
and the two inequalities (
8
) and (
9
), we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{J}^{n_{i}}}(S_{n_{i}})

\displaystyle\geq P_{\mathcal{J}^{n_{i}}}(\{j^{n_{i}}\mid\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n_{i}},j^{n_{i}})>{n_{i}}(\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\gamma)\})

(10)

\displaystyle\geq P_{\mathcal{J}^{n_{i}}}(\{j^{n_{i}}\mid\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}\cdot n_{i}}T_{n_{i}}(j^{n_{i}})>{n_{i}}(\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\gamma)\})

(11)

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon_{0}.

(12)

This leads to a contradiction because as
i\to\infty
,
P_{\mathcal{J}^{n_{i}}}(S_{n_{i}})\to 0
holds but
\varepsilon_{0}
is a positive constant.
This completes proof.
∎

5.2
Applications of Theorem
1

We now know that the optimal rate
R(\mathcal{S}_{m})
is completely characterized by the quantity
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
.
In this section, we investigate how the quantity
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
changes depending on scheduling problems
\mathcal{S}_{m}
.

The i.i.d. scenario:

Let us analyze the value of
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
when
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\}
is a sequence of i.i.d. distributions of
P_{\mathcal{J}}
.
Recall that
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
is defined as

\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m}):=\inf\{\alpha\geq 0\mid\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\{j^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})>\alpha\}=0\}

where
T_{n}(j^{n})=\sum_{i\leq n}T(j_{i})
.
Then, by the law of large numbers on the random variable
\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T(J)
, we have, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\left\{\left|\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{E}_{J}[T(J)]-\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})\right|>\varepsilon\right\}=0

(13)

This shows that
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}_{J}[T(J)]
holds, since Equation (
13
) implies
for any
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})+\varepsilon
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\{j^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})>\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})+\varepsilon\}=0

and
for any
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\varepsilon
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\{j^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})>\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\varepsilon\}=1
.

Therefore, we have

Corollary 2
.

When
\mathcal{P}
is i.i.d.,
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}_{J}[T(J)]
holds.

Mixture distributions:

Next, we analyze
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
when
P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}
is defined as a convex combination of i.i.d. distributions
\{P^{i}_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\}
.
For simplicity, we assume the distribution
P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}
is expressed by two i.i.d. distributions
P^{1}_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}
and
P^{2}_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}
:

P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}(j^{n})=\alpha_{1}P^{1}_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}(j^{n})+\alpha_{2}P^{2}_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}(j^{n})

for
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=1,\alpha_{1}>0,\alpha_{2}>0
.
In this case, we get

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\left(\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})>\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}\\
(1/n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}})T_{n}(j^{n})>\alpha\end{subarray}}P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}(j^{n})

(14)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}P^{1}_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\left(\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})>\alpha\right)+\alpha_{2}P^{2}_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\left(\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})>\alpha\right).

(15)

Therefore, applying the law of large numbers to each of
P^{1}_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}
and
P^{2}_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 3
.

For a scheduling problem
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=(\mathcal{J},T,\mathcal{V},\mathcal{P})
whose distribution is a mixture of two i.i.d. distributions
P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}^{1}
and
P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}^{2}
,

\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=\max\left\{\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}^{1}_{J}[T(J)],\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}^{2}_{J}[T(J)]\right\}

holds where
\mathbf{E}^{i}_{J}[T(J)]~(i=1,2)
is the expectation of
T(J)
when the distribution is
P^{i}_{\mathcal{J}}
.

In fact, this corollary can be generalized for a mixture of
countably infinitely many,

general
(i.e., non-i.i.d.) distributions.
Suppose
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\}
is defined as

P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}(j^{n})=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}^{i}(j^{n})

where
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq[0,1]
satisfies
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=1
and
P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}^{i}
is a general distribution on
\mathcal{J}^{n}
.
In this case, we can show the following in a similar manner as
[
30
, Theorem 1.4.2]
.

Corollary 4
.

For a scheduling problem
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=(\mathcal{J},T,\mathcal{V},\mathcal{P})
whose distribution is a mixture of distributions
\{P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}^{i}\}
,

\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=\sup_{i:\alpha_{i}>0}\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}^{i}_{m})

holds where
\mathcal{S}^{i}_{m}:=(\mathcal{J},T,\mathcal{V},\{P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}^{i}\})
.

Markov distributions:

As mentioned in Proposition
2
,
\frac{1}{nv_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})\overset{a.s.}{\to}\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}_{J}[T(J)]
holds by the ergodic theorem.
Considering the fact that almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability, we then obtain the following corollary:

Corollary 5
.

For a scheduling problem
\mathcal{S}_{m}
with a Markovian distribution,

\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}_{J}[T(J)]
holds.

6
Second order asymptotics

In Section
5
, we showed Theorem
1
which tells us
that the optimal scheduling
\{(\phi^{\textrm{opt}}_{n},S^{\textrm{opt}}_{n})\}
satisfies

\mathrm{COST}(\phi^{\textrm{opt}}_{n},S^{\textrm{opt}}_{n})=\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})\cdot n+o(n)

(16)

when
\Pr(S_{n}^{\textrm{opt}})
satisfy the condition
\Pr(S_{n}^{\textrm{opt}})\to 0
.
This is further extended to Theorem
3
which shows that for some scheduling problems such as the i.i.d. scenario,
the optimal rate does not change even if the condition is relaxed to
\Pr(S_{n}^{\textrm{opt}})\leq\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon<1
.
In Section
6
, we refine the equation
16
and show the explicit factors hidden
in
o(n)
in the case of the most fundamental scenario, the i.i.d. scheduling problem.

Theorem
2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{m}
be an i.i.d. scheduling problem.
Suppose
(\phi_{n}^{\textrm{opt}},S_{n}^{\textrm{opt}})
is an optimal scheduling among all schedulings
(\phi_{n},S_{n})
for
\mathcal{S}_{m}
satisfying
\Pr(S_{n})\leq\varepsilon
. Then,

\mathrm{COST}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n})=\frac{n}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}[T(J)]-\frac{\sqrt{V(\mathcal{S}_{m})n}}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\Phi^{-1}(\varepsilon)+\Theta(1).

holds where
\Phi(x)
is the standard Gaussian distribution and
V(\mathcal{S}_{m}):=\mathbf{E}_{J}[(T(J)-\mathbf{E}[T(J)])^{2}]
.

Proof.

First, we observe that the optimal set is expressed as
S^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n}=\{j^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})>R_{n}^{+}(\varepsilon)\}

where
R_{n}^{+}(\varepsilon):=\inf\{\alpha\geq 0\mid\Pr(\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(J^{n})>\alpha)\leq\varepsilon\}
.
(We can check that for any scheduling
(\phi_{n},S_{n})
with the condition
\Pr(S_{n})\leq\varepsilon
,
\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}},S^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n})\leq\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})
holds.)
Note that
\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{opt}}
is defined in Lemma
2
.
Therefore, the optimal scheduling is a pair
(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n})
.

We now evaluate
\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n})
. By Lemma
2
and Lemma
3
,

\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\max_{j^{n}\in S_{n}^{c}}T_{n}(j^{n})\leq\frac{1}{n}\max_{j^{n}\in S_{n}^{c}}\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n})\leq\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\max_{j^{n}\in S_{n}^{c}}T_{n}(j^{n})+\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{min}}}

(17)

holds. We also obtain
\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\max_{j^{n}\in S_{n}^{c}}T_{n}(j^{n})=R_{n}^{+}(\varepsilon)
by the definition of
R_{n}^{+}(\varepsilon)
. Therefore, the equation (
17
) is simplified to

R_{n}^{+}(\varepsilon)\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n})\leq R_{n}^{+}(\varepsilon)+\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{min}}}.

(18)

This means that it is sufficient to analyze the term
R^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon)
.

We now apply the Berry-Esseen theorem:

Theorem

(Berry-Esseen)
.

Let
X_{i}
be an i.i.d. random variable with zero mean and
T:=\mathbf{E}[|X_{i}|^{3}]<\infty
. Then

\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\Pr\left(\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i\leq n}X_{i}<a\right)-\Phi(a)\right|\leq\frac{T}{\sigma^{3}\sqrt{n}}

(19)

where
\sigma^{2}:=\mathbf{E}[X_{i}^{2}]
.

For any
R\in\mathbb{R}
, applying the Berry-Esseen theorem with

X_{i}:=\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}(T[J_{i}]-\mathbf{E}[J])\text{~and~}a=n\cdot\frac{v_{\mathrm{sum}}\cdot R-\mathbf{E}[T(J)]}{\sqrt{V(\mathcal{S}_{m})n}}

yields

\left|\Pr\left(\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(J^{n})<R\right)-\Phi\left(n\cdot\frac{v_{\mathrm{sum}}\cdot R-\mathbf{E}[T(J)]}{\sqrt{V(\mathcal{S}_{m})n}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{T}{\sqrt{nV(\mathcal{S}_{m})^{3}}}.

This implies

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(J^{n})\geq R\right)-\frac{T}{\sqrt{nV(\mathcal{S}_{m})^{3}}}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\Phi\left(n\cdot\frac{v_{\mathrm{sum}}\cdot R-\mathbf{E}[T(J)]}{\sqrt{V(\mathcal{S}_{m})n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(-n\cdot\frac{v_{\mathrm{sum}}\cdot R-\mathbf{E}[T(J)]}{\sqrt{V(\mathcal{S}_{m})n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(J^{n})\geq R\right)+\frac{T}{\sqrt{nV(\mathcal{S}_{m})^{3}}}

where
\Phi(x)=1-\Phi(-x)
is used.
We therefore obtain, by the simple relation:

\Pr\left(\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(J^{n})>R\right)\leq\Pr\left(\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(J^{n})\geq R\right),

\Pr\left(\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(J^{n})>R\right)-\frac{T}{\sqrt{nV(\mathcal{S}_{m})^{3}}}\leq\Phi\left(-n\cdot\frac{v_{\mathrm{sum}}\cdot R-\mathbf{E}[T(J)]}{\sqrt{V(\mathcal{S}_{m})n}}\right)

and, by taking the sequence
R_{n}\searrow R
and the continuity of
\Phi(\cdot)
and the probability measure,

\Phi\left(-n\cdot\frac{v_{\mathrm{sum}}\cdot R-\mathbf{E}[T(J)]}{\sqrt{V(\mathcal{S}_{m})n}}\right)\leq\Pr\left(\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(J^{n})>R\right)+\frac{T}{\sqrt{nV(\mathcal{S}_{m})^{3}}}

for any
R\in\mathbb{R}
.
By substituting
R=R_{n}^{+}(\varepsilon)
and taking the inverse
\Phi^{-1}
, these inequalities imply

\Phi^{-1}\left(\varepsilon-\frac{T}{\sqrt{nV(\mathcal{S}_{m})^{3}}}\right)\leq-n\cdot\frac{v_{\mathrm{sum}}\cdot R-\mathbf{E}[T(J)]}{\sqrt{V(\mathcal{S}_{m})n}}\leq\Phi^{-1}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{T}{\sqrt{nV(\mathcal{S}_{m})^{3}}}\right).

(20)

We now expand the term

\Phi^{-1}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{T}{\sqrt{nV(\mathcal{S}_{m})^{3}}}\right)

by the Taylor approximation as follows

\Phi^{-1}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{T}{\sqrt{nV(\mathcal{S}_{m})^{3}}}\right)=\Phi^{-1}(\varepsilon)+\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(21)

Combining the inequality (
20
) and the equation (
21
), we get

nv_{\mathrm{sum}}\cdot R_{n}^{+}(\varepsilon)=n\mathbf{E}[T(J)]-\sqrt{V(\mathcal{S}_{m})n}\Phi^{-1}(\varepsilon)+\Theta(1).

Together with the inequality (
18
), we finally obtain

\mathrm{COST}(\phi^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n},S^{\mathrm{opt}}_{n})=\frac{n}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\mathbf{E}[T(J)]-\frac{\sqrt{V(\mathcal{S}_{m})n}}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}\Phi^{-1}(\varepsilon)+\Theta(1).

This completes proof.
∎

Appendix A
Strong converse theorem

Theorem
1
tells us that the optimal rate of scheduling problem
\mathcal{S}_{m}
is characterized by the quantity
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})

when schedulings need to satisfy
P(S_{n})\to 0
.
Interestingly, in some scheduling problems, the probability
P(S_{n})
must approach to
1

when a scheduling
\{(\phi_{n},S_{n})\}
is designed to satisfy
\lim\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})\leq R(S_{m})-\varepsilon
for any small
\varepsilon
.
This is called the strong converse property in the information theory literature.
The definition of the strong converse property is as follows:

Definition 6
.

A scheduling problem
\mathcal{S}_{m}
satisfies the strong converse property if and only if
for any
\varepsilon>0
and any scheduling
\{\phi_{n},S_{n}\}
for
\mathcal{S}_{m}
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})\leq R(S_{m})-\varepsilon
implies
\lim P(S_{n})=1
.

In Theorem
3
below, we give a necessary and sufficient condition for the strong converse property.

Theorem 3
.

A scheduling problem
\mathcal{S}_{m}
satisfies the strong converse property if and only if
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
holds
where

\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m}):=\sup\left\{\beta\geq 0\mid\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\{j^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})<\beta\}=0\right\}.

Remark 1
.

By definition,
\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})\leq\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
holds. Using the law of large numbers and the ergodic theorem respectively,
we see that the i.i.d. scenario and the markov distribution in Section
5.2
satisfy the condition
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
.
On the other hand, in the case of mixture distributions, the condition does not hold in general.

A.1
Proof of Theorem
3

The proof of Theorem
3
is as follows.

proof of Theorem
3
.

The proof consists of the sufficiency part and the necessity part.

[Sufficiency]:

Assume
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
.
Suppose a scheduling
\{(\phi_{n},S_{n})\}
satisfies
\lim\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})\leq R(S_{m})-2\varepsilon=E(S_{m})-2\varepsilon
.
This implies that there is
n_{0}
such that for any
n>n_{0}
,
\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})<\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\varepsilon
holds.
By the definition of
\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})
, this further implies that for any
j^{n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}\setminus S_{n}
,

\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})\leq\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})<\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\varepsilon

holds. Therefore, we see

\displaystyle\Pr(S_{n}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(\{j^{n}|\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})<\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\varepsilon\})

\displaystyle=\Pr(\{j^{n}|\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})<\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\varepsilon\})

\displaystyle\to 0

where the first equality follows from the assumption
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
and the convergence comes from the definition of
\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
.
This shows
\Pr(S_{n})\to 1
and completes the proof.

[Necessity]:

Assume that for any
\varepsilon>0
and any scheduling
\{(\phi_{n},S_{n})\}
satisfying
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})\leq R(S_{m})-\varepsilon
,

\Pr(S_{n})\to 1
holds. Define a scheduler
\phi_{n}
as in Lemma
2
and define
S_{n}:=\{j^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})\geq\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\varepsilon\}
.
We first show the scheduler satisfies
\lim\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})\leq\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\varepsilon
.

By Lemma
2
, we see
\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n},j^{n})\leq\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})+\frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{min}}}
and therefore we have

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\max_{j^{n}\in S_{n}^{c}}\mathrm{SPAN}(\phi_{n},j^{n})\leq\max_{j^{n}\in S_{n}^{c}}\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})+\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{min}}}T_{\mathrm{max}}.

Therefore, we obtain

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})\leq\max_{j^{n}\in S_{n}^{c}}\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})+\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{min}}}T_{\mathrm{max}}\leq\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\varepsilon+\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{min}}}T_{\mathrm{max}}

by the definition of
S_{n}
. Then, taking
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
of both sides yields

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{COST}(\phi_{n},S_{n})\leq\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\varepsilon.

This in turn implies
\Pr(S_{n})\to 1
and equivalently,
\Pr(S^{c}_{n})\to 0
because of the assumption. Recall that
\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
is defined as

\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=\sup\left\{\beta\geq 0\mid\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\mathcal{J}^{n}}\{j^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})<\beta\}=0\right\}.

Therefore,
\Pr(S^{c}_{n})=\Pr(\{j^{n}\mid\frac{1}{n\cdot v_{\mathrm{sum}}}T_{n}(j^{n})<\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\varepsilon\})\to 0
means
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})-\varepsilon\leq\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
for any
\varepsilon>0
.
This means
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})\leq\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
and therefore we obtain
\bar{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})=\underline{E}(\mathcal{S}_{m})
which completes proof.
∎
