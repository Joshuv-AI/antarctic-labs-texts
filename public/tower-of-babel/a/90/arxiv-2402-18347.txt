Abstract
. In this article, we consider energy-critical complex Ginzburg-Landau equation in three and four dimensions. We give the dynamics when the energy of the initial data is equal to the energy of the stationary solution. Keywords : Complex Ginzburg-Landau equation, energy-critical, well-posedness, ground state, blow up Mathematics Subject Classification (2020) Primary: 35Q56; Secondary: 35Q35, 35B40



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider energy-critical complex Ginzburg-Landau (CGL) equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{t}-z\Delta u=z|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\quad\text{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d},\\
&u(t=0)=u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

where
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
\Re z>0
,
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=3,4
. This equation is the
L^{2}
gradient flow of the energy functional

\displaystyle E(u)=\int\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{d-2}{2d}|u|^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

It is well-known that the equation (
1.1
) has a stationary solution
W
in
\dot{H}^{1}

(d=3,4)
, defined by

\displaystyle W(x)=\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{|x|^{2}}{d(d-2)}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}},

(1.2)

such that
W
satisfies

-\Delta W=|W|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}W.

The general complex Ginzburg-Landau equation is of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\gamma u+(a+i\alpha)\Delta u-(b+i\beta)|u|^{p-1}u,

where
u
is a complex-valued function of
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
a,b,\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and
p>1
. This class of equations could be derived from Bénard convection and turbulence. See for instance
[
4
]
. The complex Ginzburg-Landau equations are closely related to the semi-linear heat equation and nonlinear Schrödinger equation.
In general, the inviscid limit of the complex Ginzburg-Landau equation is the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, and the rigorous limit theory can be seen in
[
3
,
12
,
13
]
.

In
[
2
]
, by following the argument in
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, we have proven the dichotomy of global well-posedness versus finite time blow-up of (
1.1
) when
d=3,4
, that is

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
E(u_{0})<E(W)
, where
W
is the stationary solution of (
1.1
) given by (
1.2
). Then we have

(1)

When
\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}<\|\nabla W\|_{L^{2}}
, the solution
u
of (
1.1
) is global and satisfies

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}=0
.

(2)

When
\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}>\|\nabla W\|_{L^{2}}
and
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the solution
u
of (
1.1
) blows up in forward in finite time.

The result in Theorem
1.1
shows the classification of the solutions to (
1.1
)
when the energy of the initial data is less than
E(W)
.

Based on the result in Theorem
1.1
, it is interesting to study the long time behavior of the solution to (
1.1
) when the energy of the initial data is equal to
E(W)
. In this paper, we address this problem. We now state our main result of classification.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
d=3,4,
let
u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Suppose that
E(u_{0})=E(W)
. Then we have

(A).

When
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}=\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
, then

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}W\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)

for some
\lambda_{0}>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(B).

When
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}<\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
,
(
1.1
) admits a global solution
u
and

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\to 0\text{ as $t\to\infty$.}

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\lesssim(1+t)^{-\gamma},\text{ as }t\to\infty,

(1.3)

where
\gamma=\min\left(1+\frac{r^{*}}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
with
r^{*}\in(-2,\infty)
is some fixed constant.

(C).

When
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}>\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
,
u
blows up in finite time.

In this theorem, our result shows the trichotomy of the solutions of (
1.1
)
when the energy of the initial data is equal to the energy of the stationary solution.
Precisely, when the energy of the initial data is equal to the energy of
W
for (
1.1
),
we have proven that if the kinetic energy of the initial data is less than the kinetic energy of
W
, the solution of (
1.1
) is global and dissipates to 0 as time tends to infinity; if the kinetic energy of the initial data is greater than the kinetic energy of
W

, the solution of (
1.1
) blows up in finite time; if the kinetic energy of the initial data is equal to the kinetic energy of
W
, the solution of (
1.1
) is
W
up to translation and scaling.
We prove Theorem
1.2
by following the argument in
[
2
]
together with uniqueness result of CGL. To get the decay rate (
1.3
), we rely on the argument in
[
8
]
, where the Fourier splitting method developed by M. E. Schonbek
[
10
,
11
]
in studying the decay of solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations is heavily relied on. In a forthcoming paper, we will study the dynamics of CGL around the stationary solution
W
by following the important work of Z. Lin and C. Zeng
[
9
]
.

Notice that when
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
(
1.1
) is reduced to energy-critical semi-linear heat equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &v_{t}-\Delta v=|v|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}v,\\
&v(0)=v_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).\end{aligned}\right.

(1.4)

All argument in the classification of the threshold solutions to (
1.1
) in Theorem
1.2
also trivially works for the threshold solutions
to (
1.4
).
Thus, we have the following classification result of the threshold solutions to (
1.4
).

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
v_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Suppose that
E(v_{0})=E(W)
. Then we have

(A).

When
\|v_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}=\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
, then
v(t)=W
up to translation and scaling.

(B).

When
\|v_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}<\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
,
(
1.4
) admits a global solution such that
\|v(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

(C).

When
\|v_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}>\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
,
v
blows up in finite time.

2.
Proof of the main result

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem
1.2
. As a preliminary before giving the proof, we present some useful results throughout the proof.

Lemma 2.1

(Sharp Sobolev inequality)
.

For any function
f\in\dot{H}^{1}_{x}
, we have the Sobolev inequality

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}}\leq C_{d}\|f\|_{\dot{H}_{x}^{1}}.

(2.1)

The equality holds if and only if

\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}W\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right),\ x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \lambda_{0}>0.

(2.2)

One important ingredient to prove Theorem
1.2
is the following uniqueness result of strong solutions.

Lemma 2.2

(Unconditional uniqueness)
.

Let
u
and
v
be two solutions to (
1.1
) with the same initial data
u_{0}
, and
v
is bounded in
L_{t}^{\infty}L_{x}^{\frac{4d}{d-2}}
, then we have
u=v.

Proof.

Let
w=u-v
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} w&=z\int_{0}^{t}e^{z(t-s)\Delta}\left(|v(s)+w(s)|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}(v+w)(s)-|v|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}v\right)\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&=z\int_{0}^{t}e^{z(t-s)\Delta}\left(O\left(|w(s)|^{\frac{d+2}{d-2}}\right)+O\left(|v|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}w(s)\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s.\end{aligned}

(2.3)

Then by the Strichartz estimates,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|w\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}_{x}}&\lesssim\left\||w|^{\frac{d+2}{d-2}}\right\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}_{x}}+\left\||v|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}|w|\right\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\\
&\lesssim\|w\|_{L^{\frac{2(d+2)}{d-2}}_{t}L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}_{x}}^{\frac{d+2}{d-2}}+\|v\|_{L^{\frac{8}{d-2}}_{t}L^{\frac{4d}{d-2}}_{x}}^{\frac{4}{d-2}}\|w\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}_{x}}\\
&\lesssim\|w\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}}^{\frac{4}{d-2}}\|w\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}_{x}}+\|v\|_{L^{\frac{8}{d-2}}_{t}L^{\frac{4d}{d-2}}_{x}}^{\frac{4}{d-2}}\|w\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}_{x}}.\end{aligned}

(2.4)

Because
\|v\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}L^{\frac{4d}{d-2}}_{x}}
is finite, then by choosing the time interval
I
so much small that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{\frac{8}{d-2}}_{t}L^{\frac{4d}{d-2}}_{x}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\varepsilon.

(2.5)

Since
w\in C^{0}_{t}\dot{H}^{1}_{x}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\subseteq C^{0}_{t}L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we might restrict
I
to be smaller so that

\displaystyle\|w\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\varepsilon.

(2.6)

Then the estimate (
2.4
) is reduced to

\displaystyle\|w\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}_{x}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{4}{d-2}}\|w\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}_{x}},

(2.7)

that implies
u(t,x)=v(t,x)
in
I\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then by a standard continuity argument,

u(t,x)=v(t,x)
in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
∎

2.1.
The case (A)

Now we present the proof of main result
(A)
in Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that

\displaystyle E(u_{0})=E(W)\text{ and }\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}=\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}.

(2.8)

Then for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}W\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)\ \text{for some}\ x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \text{and}\ \lambda_{0}>0.

(2.9)

Proof.

By (
2.8
), we have

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}}=\|W\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}}=C_{d}\|W\|_{\dot{H}_{x}^{1}}=C_{d}\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}_{x}^{1}},

(2.10)

where
C_{d}
is the best constant of the Sobolev inequality (
2.1
). Hence

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}}=C_{d}\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}_{x}^{1}}.

(2.11)

Then by Lemma
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle u_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}W\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right),\ x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \lambda_{0}>0.

(2.12)

We can see that
\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}W\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)
is a
\dot{H}^{1}
solution to (
1.1
), then by Lemma
2.2
, we have

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}W\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right),\text{ for any }t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

∎

2.2.
The case (B)

In this subsection, we consider dynamics of the solution to (
1.1
) when the kinetic energy of the initial data is less than the kinetic energy of
W
.

We now show the proof of part
(B)
in our main theorem.

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose that

\displaystyle E(u_{0})=E(W),\ \|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}<\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}.

(2.13)

Then the solution
u
with initial data
u_{0}
is global. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L_{t,x}^{\frac{2(d+2)}{d-2}}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty,\text{ and }\ \|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\to 0\ \text{as}\ t\to\infty.

(2.14)

Proof.

Let
u
be the maximal lifespan solution with lifespan
[0,T_{*})
. First, we claim the solution
u
satisfies

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}<\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}},\ \forall\,t\in(0,T_{*}).

(2.15)

Indeed, if not, there is
t_{1}\in(0,T_{*})
such that

\|u(t_{1})\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}=\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}

and

E(u(t_{1}))\leq E(u_{0})=E(W)
. We can see that when

\displaystyle E(u(t_{1}))<E(W),\ \|u(t_{1})\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}=\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}},

this case cannot happen by the variational argument.
Thus we have

\displaystyle E(u(t_{1}))=E(W),\ \|u(t_{1})\|_{\dot{H^{1}}}=\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}.

Then by Theorem
2.3
and Lemma
2.2
, we see

\displaystyle u(t,x)=W(x)\ \text{ up to scaling and translation.}

But this contradicts the fact that

\displaystyle E(u_{0})=E(W),\ \|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}<\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}.

Thus, we have (
2.15
).

We claim that there exists
t_{0}\in(0,T_{*})
, such that
E(u(t_{0}))<E(W)
and
E(u(t_{0}))\leq(1-\delta)E(W)
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Otherwise,
E(u(t))=E(W)
for any
t\in(0,T_{*})
. From the energy equality in
[
2
]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(u(t))=-\Re z\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{t}|^{2}=0.

(2.16)

We could see that
u_{t}\equiv 0
for
t\in[0,T_{*})
, so that
u\in\dot{H}^{1}
is stationary and satisfies
-\Delta u=|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u
.
By Lemma
2.2
,
u(t,x)=W(x)
, for
t\in[0,T_{*})
. But this contradicts the assumption
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}<\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
.

By
\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}<\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}},

\forall\,t\in[0,T_{*}),
we have there exists
t_{2}\in[0,T_{*})
such that

\displaystyle E(u(t_{2}))<E(W)\text{ and }\|u(t_{2})\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}<\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}.

Then by the result in
[
2
]
, we have
T_{*}=\infty
, and
u
satisfies (
2.14
).

∎

We now turn to show the decay rate (
1.3
). By (
2.14
), we see for
t
large enough,
\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
is small enough. Thus, without loss of generality, we may assume the
\dot{H}^{1}
-norm of the initial data
u_{0}
to (
1.1
) is small enough.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
r^{\ast}=r^{\ast}\left(|\nabla|u_{0}\right)>-2
, and
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
small enough. Then for the global solution
u
to (
1.1
) we have

\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{1}{2}\min\left(2+r^{\ast},1\right)}.

(2.17)

In Theorem
2.5
, we introduce the decay character
r^{*}
of
|\nabla|u_{0}
. This concept is introduced by C. Bjorland and M. E. Schonbek
[
1
]
.

Definition 2.6

(Decay character
r^{*}
of
|\nabla|u_{0}
)
.

Let
u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For
r\in\left(-\frac{d}{2},\infty\right)
, we define the decay indicator
P_{r}(|\nabla|u_{0})
corresponding to
|\nabla|u_{0}
as

P_{r}(|\nabla|u_{0})=\lim_{\rho\to 0}\rho^{-2r-d}\int_{B(0,\rho)}|\xi|^{2}\left|\left(\mathcal{F}{u_{0}}\right)(\xi)\right|^{2}\,d\xi,

provided this limit exists, where
\mathcal{F}
is the Fourier transform. The decay character of
|\nabla|u_{0}
, denoted by
r^{\ast}=r^{\ast}(|\nabla|u_{0})
is the unique
r\in\left(-\frac{d}{2},\infty\right)
such that
0<P_{r}(|\nabla|u_{0})<\infty
, provided that this number exists. We set
r^{\ast}=-\frac{d}{2}
, when
P_{r}(|\nabla|u_{0})=\infty
for all
r\in\left(-\frac{d}{2},\infty\right)
or
r^{\ast}=\infty
, if
P_{r}(|\nabla|u_{0})=0
for all
r\in\left(-\frac{d}{2},\infty\right)
.

In particular, in
[
1
]
, C. Bjorland and M. E. Schonbek proved
if
-\frac{d}{2}<r^{\ast}<\infty
, for
\alpha>0
,

\displaystyle\|e^{t\alpha\Delta}u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\sim(1+t)^{-\left(\frac{d}{2}+r^{\ast}\right)}.

(2.18)

We use this estimate to the complex case to show (
2.17
).
Before giving the proof of Theorem
2.5
, we first show the
\dot{H}^{1}
norm is a Lyapunov function.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be small enough. Then,
the norm in
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a Lyapunov function for equation (
1.1
).

Proof.

By integration by parts, we can obtain the following energy identity in
[
3
]
,

\displaystyle\|u(t_{2})\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}}-\|u(t_{1})\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\Re\int z\overline{\nabla u}\cdot\nabla\left(|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle-\Re z\int_{0}^{t}\int|\Delta u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau.

where
t_{2}>t_{1}
. Note that from
[
3
]
, we have
\Delta u\in L_{t,x}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\nabla u\in L_{t}^{2}\dot{H}_{x}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, thus
the right hand side in this equality is finite.

By Hölder and fractional Leibnitz rule, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\Re\int z\overline{\nabla u}\cdot\nabla\left(|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\lesssim\||\nabla|u\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}}^{2}\|u\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}}^{\frac{4}{d-2}}\lesssim\left\|\nabla^{2}u\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1}_{x}}^{\frac{4}{d-2}},

(2.19)

and therefore

\|u(t_{2})\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}}+2\Re z\left(1-C\,\sup_{t>0}\|u(t)\|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}_{\dot{H}^{1}}\right)\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\|\nabla u(\tau)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}}\,d\tau\leq\|u(t_{1})\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}}\,.

Choosing
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
small enough, by the well-posedness and Theorem
2.4
,
we have that
\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}\dot{H}_{x}^{1}}
is small for all
t>0
. Hence
C\sup\limits_{t>0}\|u(t)\|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}_{\dot{H}^{1}}\leq 1
.
Then for any
t_{1}<t_{2}
, we have

\|u(t_{2})\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\leq\|u(t_{1})\|_{\dot{H}^{1}},

which entails the result.

∎

We can now give the proof of Theorem
2.5
.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

From Proposition
2.7
, we know that the
\dot{H}^{1}
norm is a non-increasing function, hence is differentiable a.e. Then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle=2\left\langle\nabla u(t),\nabla\left(\Delta u(t)+|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u(t)\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\|\nabla u(t)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}}+2\left\langle\nabla u(t),\nabla\left(|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u(t)\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-2\left(1-C\|u(t)\|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}_{\dot{H}^{1}}\right)\|\nabla u(t)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}}\leq-{C}_{0}\|\nabla u(t)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}},

(2.20)

where we have used (
2.19
) and also the smallness of
\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}\dot{H}_{x}^{1}}
for all
t>0
.

We now use the Fourier splitting method. Let
B(t)
be a ball around the origin in frequency space with continuous, time-dependent radius
\rho(t)
such that

B(t)=\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|\xi|\leq\rho(t)=\left(\frac{g^{\prime}(t)}{{C}_{0}g(t)}\right)^{\frac{2(d+4)}{d(3d-4)}}\right\},

with
g
an increasing continuous function such that
g(0)=1
. Then by decomposing the frequency domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
B(t)
and
B(t)^{c}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(t)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq-{C}_{0}\|\nabla u(t)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}}=-{C}_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{2}\,\left||\xi|\mathcal{F}u(t,\xi)\right|^{2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=-{C}_{0}\int_{B(t)}|\xi|^{2}\,\left||\xi|\mathcal{F}{u}(t,\xi)\right|^{2}d\xi-{C}_{0}\int_{B(t)^{c}}|\xi|^{2}\,\left||\xi|\mathcal{F}{u}(t,\xi)\right|^{2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{g^{\prime}(t)}{g(t)}\int_{B(t)^{c}}\left||\xi|\mathcal{F}{u}(t,\xi)\right|^{2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{\prime}(t)}{g(t)}\int_{B(t)}\left||\xi|\mathcal{F}{u}(t,\xi)\right|^{2}\,d\xi-\frac{g^{\prime}(t)}{g(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left||\xi|\mathcal{F}{u}(t,\xi)\right|^{2}\,d\xi.

Now multiply both sides by
g(t)
and rewrite the above inequality in order to obtain the key inequality

\frac{d}{dt}\left(g(t)\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\right)\leq g^{\prime}(t)\int_{B(t)}\left||\xi|\mathcal{F}{u}(t,\xi)\right|^{2}\,d\xi,

(2.21)

which allows us to estimate the left-side of (
2.21
) by
\mathcal{F}{u}
over the ball
B(t)
.
We now need a pointwise estimate for
\left||\xi|\mathcal{F}{u}(t,\xi)\right|
in
B(t)
in order to go forward. We first have

\displaystyle\int_{B(t)}\left||\xi|\mathcal{F}{u}(t,\xi)\right|^{2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B(t)}\left|e^{-zt|\xi|^{2}}|\xi|\mathcal{F}{u_{0}}(t,\xi)\right|^{2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle+C\int_{B(t)}\left|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-z(t-s)|\xi|^{2}}|\xi|\mathcal{F}\left(|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\right)(s,\xi)\,ds\right|^{2}\,d\xi.

(2.22)

The first term corresponds to the linear part,
so by (
2.18
), this can be estimated as

\int_{B(t)}\left|e^{-zt|\xi|^{2}}|\xi|\mathcal{F}{u_{0}}(t,\xi)\right|^{2}\,d\xi\leq C\|e^{t\Re z\Delta}|\nabla|u_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\left(\frac{d}{2}+r^{\ast}\right)},

(2.23)

where
r^{\ast}=r^{\ast}\left(|\nabla|u_{0}\right)
.

Now, for the nonlinear term, by Hausdorff-Young inequality inside the time integral we have,

\displaystyle\left(\int_{B(t)}\left|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\Re z(t-s)|\xi|^{2}}|\xi|\mathcal{F}\left(|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\right)(s,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{B(t)}e^{-2\Re z(t-s)|\xi|^{2}}|\xi|^{2}\left|\mathcal{F}\left(|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\right)(s,\xi)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left(\int_{B(t)}\left(e^{-2\Re z(t-s)|\xi|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{d+4}{3d-4}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right)^{\frac{3d-4}{d+4}}\left\||\xi|\mathcal{F}\left(|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\right)\right\|_{L_{\xi}^{\frac{d+4}{4-d}}}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq|B(t)|^{\frac{3d-4}{2(d+4)}}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\nabla\left(|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\right)\right\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{d+4}{2d}}}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq|B(t)|^{\frac{3d-4}{2(d+4)}}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}\|u\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}}^{\frac{4}{d-2}}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq C\rho(t)^{\frac{d(3d-4)}{2(d+4)}}\int_{0}^{t}\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{\frac{d+2}{d-2}}\,\mathrm{d}s,

(2.24)

which leads to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(g(t)\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\right)\leq Cg^{\prime}(t)(1+t)^{-\left(2+r^{\ast}\right)}+Cg^{\prime}(t)r(t)^{\frac{d(3d-4)}{d+4}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{\frac{d+2}{d-2}}\,ds\right)^{2},

(2.25)

We now use a bootstrap argument to prove the estimate in Theorem
2.5
. More precisely, we first obtain a weaker estimate for
u
which we then plug in in the right hand side of (
2.25
) to find a stronger one. To get started this, we take
g(t)=\left(\ln(e+t)\right)^{3}
, so that

\displaystyle\rho(t)=\left(\frac{g^{\prime}(t)}{{C}_{0}g(t)}\right)^{\frac{2(d+4)}{d(3d-4)}}=\left(\frac{3}{{C}_{0}(e+t)\ln(e+t)}\right)^{\frac{2(d+4)}{d(3d-4)}}.

From Theorem
2.4
, we know a weaker estimate that
\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\leq C
for all
t>0
. Then from (
2.25
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\left(\ln(e+t)\right)^{3}\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\right)\leq C\frac{\left(\ln(e+t)\right)^{2}}{e+t}(1+t)^{-\left(2+r^{\ast}\right)}+C\frac{1}{(e+t)^{3}}t^{2}.

(2.26)

Now notice that the first term in the right-hand side of (
2.26
) is integrable. More precisely,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\left(\ln(e+s)\right)^{2}}{e+s}(1+s)^{-\left(2+r^{\ast}\right)}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{1}^{\ln(e+s)}u^{2}\,e^{-(2+r^{\ast})u}\,du

\displaystyle<\infty

Then (
2.26
) implies

\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\leq C\left(\ln(e+t)\right)^{-2},

(2.27)

which is a better estimate than the boundedness of
u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
.
We now use this estimate to do our bootstrap argument. As a trivial start point, take
g(t)=(1+t)^{\alpha}
, with
\alpha>0
to be determined later, so
\rho(t)=\left(\frac{\alpha}{{C}_{0}(1+t)}\right)^{\frac{2(d+4)}{d(3d-4)}}
. Then, from (
2.25
) and (
2.27
) we obtain, after integrating, that

\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\leq C(1+t)^{-\alpha}+C(1+t)^{-\left(2+r^{\ast}\right)}+C(1+t)^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\|u(s)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}}{\left(\ln(e+s)\right)^{4(6-d)}}\,ds,

which, after restricting
\alpha>\max\left(2+r^{\ast},1\right)
, leads to

\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\leq C(1+t)^{-\left(2+r^{\ast}\right)}+C(1+t)^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\|u(s)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}}{\left(\ln(e+s)\right)^{4(6-d)}}\,ds.

(2.28)

If
r^{\ast}>-1
, we directly use Gronwall’s inequality to obtain

\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\leq C(1+t)^{-\left(2+r^{\ast}\right)}+C(1+t)^{-1}\leq C(1+t)^{-1}.

Otherwise, for
r^{\ast}\leq-1
, we rewrite (
2.28
) as

\displaystyle(1+t)\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\leq C(1+t)^{1-\left(2+r^{\ast}\right)}+C\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(1+s)\|u(s)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}}{(1+s)\left(\ln(e+s)\right)^{4(6-d)}}\,ds.

Then we still use Gronwall’s inequality to get

\displaystyle(1+t)\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\leq C(1+t)^{1-\left(2+r^{\ast}\right)}.

Therefore We have proved Theorem
2.5
.

∎

2.3.
The case (C)

Theorem 2.8
.

Suppose that
E(u_{0})=E(W)
, and
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}>\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
.
Then the solution
u
blows up in finite time.

Proof.

By the continuity argument, we have

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}>\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}

and

\displaystyle E(u(t))\leq E(u_{0})=E(W),\forall\,t\geq 0.

We find there is
t_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle E(u(t_{1}))<E(W)\text{ and }\|u(t_{1})\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}>\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}.

Indeed, if not, we can do as we have done in the proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Thus, we can take
t_{1}
as the new initial time, then by the result in
[
2
]
, we get finite time blow-up.

∎
