Introduction

Recall that a group
\Gamma
is called
linear
,
or
linear over a ring
in case of ambiguity, if
there is an embedding
\Gamma\subset{\mathrm{GL}}(n,R)

for some integer
n
and some commutative ring

.
Moreover
\Gamma
is called

if it can be embedded into
{\mathrm{GL}}(n,K)
for some integer
n
and some field
K

Let
\mathrm{Aut}\,K^{2}
be the group of polynomial automorphisms
of the affine plane
K^{2}
. In this paper, we will investigate the linearity or nonlinearity properties of the subgroups
of
\mathrm{Aut}\,K^{2}
. In particular, we will consider the following subgroups

\mathrm{Aut}_{0}\,K^{2}=\{\phi\in\mathrm{Aut}\,K^{2}|\,\phi({\bf 0})={\bf 0})\}
,

\mathrm{SAut}\,K^{2}=\{\phi\in\mathrm{Aut}\,K^{2}|\,\mathrm{Jac}(\phi)=1\}
,

\mathrm{SAut}_{0}\,K^{2}=\mathrm{SAut}\,K^{2}\cap\mathrm{Aut}_{0}\,K^{2}
, and

\mathrm{Aut}_{1}\,K^{2}=\{\phi\in\mathrm{Aut}_{0}\,K^{2}|\,{\textnormal{d}}\phi|_{\bf 0}=\mathrm{id}\}
,

where
\mathrm{Jac}(\phi):=\det\,\mathrm{d}\phi
is the jacobian of

. The first result is

Theorem A
.

(A.1) If

\mathrm{SAut}_{0}\,K^{2}
is not linear, even over a ring.

(A.2) Moreover if
\operatorname{ch}\,K=0

contains finitely generated subgroups which are not linear,
even over a ring.

On the opposite there is

Therefore the previous question can be reformulate as

Theorem C provides a complicated condition

The van der Kulk Theorem

In this section, we review the basic facts about the amalgamated products of groups. Then the we recall the classical
van der Kulk Theorem.

1.1 Group functors

A group functor
is a functor
G:R\mapstochar\rightarrow G(R)
from the category of commutative rings
R
to the category of groups. In most cases, we will only define the group
G(K)
for a
field
K
and the reader should understand that the definition over a ring is similar. When
I
is an ideal of a ring
R
, we denote by

G(I)
the kernel of the map
G(R)\rightarrow G(R/I)
.
For the functorial approach to group theory, see
e.g.
[
7
]
and
[
19
]
.

Nevertheless, will use a nonconsistent notation, namely

\mathrm{Aut}\,K^{2}
,
for the group of polynomial automorphisms of
K^{2}
, as well for its subgroups defined in the introduction.
Similarly, we will use the notation
\mathrm{Aut}\,K^{3}
in section 10.

A group subfunctor
H\subset G
of
finite codimension
means that the functor
R\mapstochar\rightarrow G(R)/H(R)
is represented
by a scheme of finite dimension. The reader should understand this as an unformal definition,
and we will not care to be more precise. For example,
the subgroups
\mathrm{Aut}_{0}\,K^{2}
has codimension
2

in
\mathrm{Aut}\,K^{2}
.

1.2 Amalgamated products

Let
A
,
G_{1}
and

be groups. Their

is denoted by

, see
[
10
]
ch.4. Now let

A{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\rightarrow}\limits^{f_{1}}}}G_{1}
and

A{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\rightarrow}\limits^{f_{2}}}}G_{2}
be two injective group morphisms and let

be the invariant subgroup
generated by the elements
f_{1}(a)f_{2}(a^{-1})
when
a
runs
over
A
. By definition the group

is called

of

and
H
over
A
,
and it is denoted by

see e.g.
[
13
]

In the literature, the amalgamated products are also called

Recall that the natural maps

and

are

The group

and

, and

1.3 Reduced words

The usual definition
[
13
]
of reduced words
is based on the right
A
-cosets. In order to avoid a confusion
between the set difference notation

and the
A
-orbits notation

use a definition based on the left

-cosets.

Let
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
be two groups sharing a common subgroup

,
and let
\Gamma=G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}

Set
G_{1}^{*}=G_{1}\setminus A

G_{2}^{*}=G_{2}\setminus A

(respectively

Let

A

of

(x_{1},\dots x_{n},x_{0})
where

is in

Let

be the set of all reduced words.
The next lemma is well-known, see

[

]

Lemma 1
.

The map

(x_{1},\dots x_{n},x_{0})\in{\cal R}\mapstochar\rightarrow x_{1}\dots x_{n}x_{0}\in G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}

Set
\Gamma=G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}

there is some integer

, some

\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})\in\Sigma

1.4 Amalgamated product of subgroups

Let
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
be two groups sharing a common subgroup

\Gamma=G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}

Let
G_{1}^{\prime}\subset G_{1}
,
G_{2}^{\prime}\subset G_{2}

and

Lemma 2
.

(i) The natural map

G_{1}^{\prime}*_{A^{\prime}}G_{2}^{\prime}\rightarrow\Gamma
is injective.

(ii) Let
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
be a subgroup
and set
G_{1}^{\prime}=G_{1}\cap\Gamma^{\prime}
,

G_{2}^{\prime}=G_{2}\cap\Gamma^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime}=A\cap\Gamma^{\prime}
.
Assume that
\Gamma^{\prime}.A=\Gamma
. Then we have

\Gamma^{\prime}=G_{1}^{\prime}*_{A^{\prime}}G_{2}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

First step: proof of Assertion (i).

For
i=1,2
set
G_{i}^{*}=G_{i}\setminus A
,

G_{i}^{{}^{\prime}*}=G^{\prime}_{i}\setminus A^{\prime}
. Let

T^{*}_{i}\subset G_{i}^{*}
and
T^{{}^{\prime}*}_{i}\subset G_{i}^{{}^{\prime}*}

be a set of representatives of

G_{i}^{*}/A
and, respectively, of
G_{i}^{{}^{\prime}*}/A^{\prime}
.

Since the maps

G_{i}^{\prime}/A^{\prime}\rightarrow G_{i}/A
are injective, it can be assumed that
T^{{}^{\prime}*}_{i}\subset T^{*}_{i}
. Let
\cal R

and
\cal R^{\prime}
be the set of reduced words of

G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}
, and respectively of
G_{1}^{\prime}*_{A^{\prime}}G_{2}^{\prime}
.
By definition, we have
\cal R^{\prime}\subset\cal R
, thus

1

G_{1}^{\prime}*_{A^{\prime}}G_{2}^{\prime}\mapstochar\rightarrow G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}

is injective.

Second step: proof of Assertion (ii).

We will use the notations of the previous proof.
Since
\Gamma^{\prime}.A=\Gamma
, it follows that
the maps
G^{\prime}_{1}/A^{\prime}\rightarrow G/A
and
G_{2}^{\prime}/A^{\prime}\rightarrow G_{2}/A
are bijective. Therefore

is the set of
all reduced words
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{0})\in{\cal R}
such that

. It follows easily that

G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}/G_{1}^{\prime}*_{A^{\prime}}G_{2}^{\prime}\simeq A/A^{\prime}=\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}
,

and therefore we have

∎

2
Amalgamated Products and
Linearity.

In this section, it is shown that, under a mild assumption, an amalgamated product
G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}

which is linear over a ring is also linear over a field,
see Lemma
6
.

2.1 Linearity Properties

For a group
\Gamma
, the strongest form of linearity is the linearity over a field. On the opposite, there are also groups

\Gamma
which contain a FG subgroup which is
not linear, even over a ring: these groups are nonlinear in the strongest sense.

2.2 Minimal embeddings

Let
R
be a commutative ring, let
n\geq 1
and let
\Gamma
be a subgroup of
{\mathrm{GL}}(n,R)
. For an ideal
J\subset R
, the elements
g\in{\mathrm{GL}}(n,J)
can be written as
g=\mathrm{id}+A
, where
A
is a
n
-by-
n
matrix with entries in

J
.

The embedding
\Gamma\subset{\mathrm{GL}}(n,R)
is called
minimal
if
for any ideal
J\neq 0
we have
\Gamma\cap{\mathrm{GL}}(n,J)\neq\{1\}
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\Gamma\subset{\mathrm{GL}}(n,R)
. There exist an ideal
J
with

\Gamma\cap{\mathrm{GL}}(n,J)=\{1\}
such that the induced embedding

\Gamma\rightarrow{\mathrm{GL}}(n,R/J)
is minimal.

Proof.

Since
R
is not necessarily noetherian,
the proof requires Zorn Lemma.

Let
\cal S
be the set of all ideals
J

of
R
such that
\Gamma\cap{\mathrm{GL}}(n,J)=\{1\}
. With respect to the inclusion,
\cal S
is a poset. For any chain
\cal C\subset\cal S
, the ideal

\cup_{I\in{\cal C}}\,I
belongs to
\cal S
. Therefore Zorn Lemma implies that
\cal S
contains a maximal element
J
. It follows that the induced embedding
\Gamma\rightarrow{\mathrm{GL}}(n,R/J)
is minimal.
∎

2.3 Groups with trivial centralizers

By definition, a group
\Gamma

has trivial centralizers
if the centralizer of any nontrivial normal subgroup
K
of
\Gamma
is trivial.
Equivalently, if
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are commuting invariant subgroups of
\Gamma
, then one of them is trivial.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group with trivial centralizers.

If
\Gamma
is linear over a ring,
then
\Gamma
is also linear over a field.

Proof.

By hypothesis we have

\Gamma\subset{\mathrm{GL}}(n,R)
for some commutative ring
R
.
By lemma
3
, it can be assumed that the embedding

\Gamma\rightarrow{\mathrm{GL}}(n,R)
is minimal.

Let
I_{1},\,I_{2}
be ideals
of
R
with
I_{1}.I_{2}=0
. Let
A_{1}
(respectively
A_{2}
) be
an arbitrary
n
-by-
n
matrix with entries in
I_{1}

(respectively in
I_{2}
) and set
g_{1}=1+A_{1}
,

g_{2}=1+A_{2}
. Since we have
A_{1}.A_{2}=A_{2}.A_{1}=0
,
we have
g_{1}.g_{2}=g_{2}.g_{1}=1+A_{1}+A_{2}
, therefore

{\mathrm{GL}}(n,I_{1})
and
{\mathrm{GL}}(n,I_{2})
are commuting invariant subgroups
of
GL(n,R)
.

Since
K_{1}=\Gamma\cap GL(n,I_{1})
and
K_{2}=\Gamma\cap GL(n,I_{2})

are commuting invariant subgroups of
\Gamma
, one of them is trivial. By minimality hypothesis,
I_{1}
or
I_{2}
is trivial.
Thus
R
is prime.

It follows that
\Gamma\subset{\mathrm{GL}}(n,K)
, where

K
is the fraction field of
R
.
∎

2.4 The hypothesis
{\cal H}

Let
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
be two groups sharing a common subgroup
A
and
set
\Gamma=G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}
.

Let
\Sigma
be the set of all finite alternating sequences

{\bf\epsilon}=\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n}
of ones and twos.
For
i,\,j\in\{1,2\}
, let
\Sigma_{i,j}
be
be the subset of all
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})\in\Sigma

starting with
i
and ending with
j
and
let
\Gamma_{i,j}
be the set of all
\gamma\in\Gamma

of type
\epsilon
for some
\epsilon\in\Sigma_{i,j}
.
Therefore we have

\Gamma=A\sqcup\Gamma_{1,1}\sqcup\Gamma_{2,2}\sqcup\Gamma_{1,2}\sqcup\Gamma_{2,1}
.

By definition, the amalgamated product

G_{1}*_{A}\,G_{2}
is called
nontrivial
if

G_{1}\neq A
, or
G_{2}\neq A
. It is called

dihedral
if
G_{1}=G_{2}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, and
A=\{1\}
,
and
nondihedral
otherwise.

Let consider the following hypothesis

({\cal H})
For any
a\in A
with
a\neq 1
, there is

\gamma\in\Gamma
such that

a^{\gamma}\notin A

where, as usual,
a^{\gamma}:=\gamma a\gamma^{-1}
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\Gamma=G_{1}*_{A}\,G_{2}
be a
nontrivial and nondihedral
amalgamated product satisfying the hypothesis

{\cal H}
.

For any element
g\neq 1
of
\Gamma
,
there are

\gamma_{1},\,\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma
such that

g^{\gamma_{1}}\in\Gamma_{1,1}
and

g^{\gamma_{2}}\in\Gamma_{2,2}
.

In particular
\Gamma
has trivial centralizers.

Proof.

First it should be noted that
A
cannot be
simultaneously a subgroup of index 2 in
G_{1}
and in
G_{2}
.
Otherwise,
A
would be an invariant subgroup of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
,
the hypothesis
{\cal H}
would imply that
A=\{1\}

and
\Gamma
would be the dihedral group.
Hence it can be assumed that
G_{2}/A
contains
at least
3
elements.

Next it is clear that

G^{*}_{i}.\Gamma_{j,k}\subset\Gamma_{i,k}

whenever
i\neq j
. Similarly, we have

\Gamma_{i,j}.G^{*}_{k}\subset\Gamma_{i,k}

whenever
j\neq k
.

Now we prove the first claim, namely that the conjugacy class of any
g\neq 1
intersects both
\Gamma_{1,1}
and
\Gamma_{2,2}
.

By hypothesis,
G^{*}_{1}
is not empty. Let

\gamma_{1}\in G^{*}_{1}
. We have

\Gamma_{2,2}^{\gamma_{1}}\subset\Gamma_{1,1}
. Simlarly we have

\Gamma_{1,1}^{\gamma_{2}}\subset\Gamma_{2,2}
for some

\gamma_{2}\in G_{2}^{*}
. Therefore the claim is proved for
any
g\in\Gamma_{1,1}\cup\Gamma_{2,2}
. Moreover
it is now enough to prove that the conjugacy class of any

g\neq 1
intersects

\Gamma_{1,1}
or
\Gamma_{2,2}
.

Assume now
g\in\Gamma_{2,1}
. We have
g=u.v
for some

u\in G^{*}_{2}
and
v\in\Gamma_{1,1}
. Since

[G_{2}:A]\geq 3
, there is
\gamma\in G^{*}_{2}
such that

\gamma.u
belongs to
G^{*}_{2}
. It follows that

\gamma.g
belongs to
\Gamma_{2,1}
, and therefore

g^{\gamma}
belongs to
\Gamma_{2,2}
.

Next, for
g\in\Gamma_{1,2}
, it inverse

g^{-1}
belongs to
\Gamma_{1,2}
and
the claim follows from the previous point.

Last, for
g\in A
, there is
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
g^{\gamma}
is not in
A
, by hypothesis
{\cal H}
. Thus
g^{\gamma}
belongs to
\Gamma_{i,j}
for some

i,\,j
. So
g
is conjugate to some element in

\Gamma_{1,1}\cup\Gamma_{2,2}
by the previous considerations.

Next we prove that
\Gamma
has trivial centralizers.
Let
K_{1},\,K_{2}
be nontrivial invariant subgroups. By the previous point, there are elements
g_{1},\,g_{2}
with

g_{1}\in K_{1}\cap\Gamma_{1,1}
and

g_{2}\in K_{2}\cap\Gamma_{2,2}
.

Since we have
g_{1}g_{2}\in\Gamma_{1,2}
and
g_{2}g_{1}\in\Gamma_{2,1}
, it follows that
g_{1}g_{2}\neq g_{2}g_{1}
. Therefore

K_{1}
and
K_{2}
do not commute.

∎

As an obvious corollary of Lemmas
4
and

5
, we get

Lemma 6
.

Let
\Gamma=G_{1}*_{A}\,G_{2}
be a
nontrivial amalgamated product satisfying the hypothesis
{\cal H}
.

If
\Gamma
is linear over a ring, then it is linear over a field.

Proof.

The dihedral group is linear over a field.
Hence it can be assumed that the amalgamated product

\Gamma=G_{1}*_{A}\,G_{2}
is also nondihedral. Thus
the lemma is an obvious corollary of Lemmas
4
and
5
.

∎

A nonlinear FG subgroup of

.

First we define a certain FG group
\Gamma=G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}
and we will show that
\Gamma
is nonlinear, even over a ring. Then we see that
\Gamma
is
a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}_{0}\,\mathbb{Q}^{2}
and therefore
\mathrm{Aut}_{0}\,K^{2}
contains

3.1 Quasi-unipotent endomorphisms

Let
V
be a finite dimensional vector space over an algebraically closed field
K
. An element
u\in{\mathrm{GL}}(V)
is called

quasi-unipotent
if all its eigenvalues are roots of unity.
The
quasi-order
of a quasi-unipotent endomorphism

u
is the smallest positiver integer
n
such that
u^{n}
is unipotent.

If
u
is unipotent and
\operatorname{ch}\,K=0
, set

\log\,u=\log(1-(1-u)):=\sum_{m\geq 1}\,(1-u)^{m}/m
,

which is well-defined since
1-u
is nilpotent.

Lemma 7
.

Let
h,u\in{\mathrm{GL}}(V)
.
Assume that
u
has infinite order and

u^{h}=u^{2^{k}}
for some
k\geq 1
.
Then
u
is quasi-unipotent of odd quasi-order
n
.

Moreover
K
has characteristic zero and

e^{h}=2^{k}e
, where
e=\log\,u^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathrm{Spec}}\,\,u
be the spectrum of
u
.
By hypothesis the map

{\mathrm{Spec}}\,\,u\rightarrow{\mathrm{Spec}}\,\,u,\lambda\mapstochar\rightarrow\lambda^{2^{k}}
is bijective,
hence for any
\lambda\in{\mathrm{Spec}}\,\,u
, we have

\lambda^{2^{kn}}=\lambda
for some integer
n
. It follows that all
eigenvalues are odd roots of unity, what proves that
u
is
quasi-unipotent of odd quasi-order.

Over any field of finite characteristic, the unipotent
endomorphisms have finite order. Hence we have
\operatorname{ch}\,K=0
. The last assertion comes from the fact that

\log
induces a bijection from unipotent elements to
nilpotent elements.

∎

3.2 The Group
\Gamma=G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}

Set
G_{1}=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, and let
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
be a basis

.
Set
\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}=\{x\in\mathbb{Q}|2^{n}x\in\mathbb{Z}
for
n>>0\}
.
Indeed
Z_{(2)}
is the localization of the ring
\mathbb{Z}
at
2
, but,
in what follows, we will only consider its group structure. The element

in

will be denoted by
\tau

and the addition in
Z_{(2)}
will be denoted multiplicatively.

Let
\sigma
act on
\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}
by multiplication by

, so we can consider the semi-direct product

.
Let
\Gamma=G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}
, where

. It is easy to show
that
\Gamma
is generated by
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
and
\tau
, and
it is defined by the following two relations

\sigma\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma^{\prime}\sigma
, and

\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1}=\tau^{2}

Lemma 8
.

The group

\Gamma
has trivial centralizers.

Proof.

By Lemma
6
, it is enough to
prove that the amalgamated product

\Gamma=G_{1}*_{A}G_{2}
satisfies the hypothesis
{\cal H}
.

Indeed we claim that, for any

, its conjugate

\tau^{-1}a\tau

We have

for some

First assume that

We have

and therefore

Hence the amalgamated product

.
∎

3.3 Nonlinearity of
\Gamma

Lemma 9
.

The group
\Gamma
is not linear, even over a ring.

Proof.

Assume that
\Gamma
is linear over a ring.
By Lemma
8
the group
\Gamma

has trivial centralizers. Thus, by Lemma
4
,

\Gamma
is linear over a field.

Let
\rho:\Gamma\rightarrow{\mathrm{GL}}(V)
be an embedding, where
V
is a finite dimensional vector space over a field

.
Since
\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1}=\tau^{2}
it follows from
Lemma
7
that
\rho(\tau)
is quasi-unipotent and its

is odd.

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be the subgroup of
\Gamma
generated by

\sigma,\,\sigma^{\prime}
and
\tau^{n}
. Since the morphism

\psi:\Gamma\rightarrow\Gamma^{\prime}
defined by
\psi(\sigma)=\sigma
,

\psi(\sigma^{\prime})=\sigma^{\prime}
and
\psi(\tau)=\tau^{n}
is an isomorphism,
it can be can assumed that
\rho(\tau)
is unipotent.

By Lemma
7
,

K
has characteristic zero. Set

,

and

.

By Lemma
7
, we have

heh^{-1}=2e
.

It can be assumed that

is algebraically closed.
Let

be the decomposition of

into generalized eigenspaces relative to

\lambda\in K

Let

commutes with

\rho(G_{1})V_{(\lambda)}\subset V_{(\lambda)}
.

Moreover we have

It follows that

is a
\Gamma
-module.
Since

the image of

has a composition series by one-dimensional

However a solvable group contains a nontrivial invariant abelian subgroup.
This contradicts that

∎

4
Semi-algebraic characters.

Let
K
be an infinite field of characteristic
p
. It is shown in this section that the
degree of any semi-algebraic character is well-defined,
see Lemma
12
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer prime to
p
. Then for any
x\in K
, there is an integer
m
and a collection
x_{1},\,x_{2}\dots x_{m}
of elements of
K^{*}
such that

x=\sum_{1\leq k\leq m}\,x_{i}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
K^{\prime}
be the additive span of
the set
\{y^{n}|y\in K\}
. Since

-y^{n}=(p-1)y^{n}
,
K^{\prime}
is an additive subgroup of
K
. Clearly,

K^{\prime}
is a subring of
K
.
The formula
x^{-1}=(1/x)^{n}x^{n-1}
for any

x\in K\setminus 0
implies that
K^{\prime}
is a subfield.

We claim that
K^{\prime}
is infinite. Assume otherwise. Then

K^{\prime}=\mathbb{F}_{q}
for some power
q
of
p
. Since any element of
K
is
algebraic over
K^{\prime}
of degree
\leq n
, it follows that

K\subset\cup_{k\leq n}\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}
, a fact that contradicts that
K
is infinite.

It remains to prove that
K^{\prime}=K
.
For
x\in K
, let
P(t)\in K[t]
be the polynomial
P(t)=(x+t)^{n}
. Let
y_{0},\dots y_{n}
be
n+1

distinct elements in
K^{\prime}
. We have
P(y_{i})\in K^{\prime}
for any

0\leq i\leq n
. Using Lagrange’s interpolation polynomials, there exist a polynomial

Q(t)\in K^{\prime}[t]
of degree
\leq n
such that
P(y_{i})=Q(y_{i})
for any
0\leq i\leq n
. Since
P-Q
has at least
n+1
roots, we have

P=Q
and therefore
P(t)\in K^{\prime}[t]
. We have

P(t)=t^{n}+nxt^{n-1}+\dots
,

hence
nx
belongs to
K^{\prime}
, and therefore
x\in K^{\prime}
, what proves that
K^{\prime}=K
.

∎

Lemma 11
.

Let
K
be an infinite field of characteristic
p
,
let
\mu:K\rightarrow K
be a field automorphism and
let
n,\,m
be positive integers which are prime to
p
. Assume that

x^{n}=\mu(x)^{m}
, for all
x\in K^{*}
.

Then we have
m=n
.

Remark:
Indeed, the hypotheses of the lemma
imply also that
\mu=\mathrm{id}
, but this is not required in what follows.

Proof.

Step 1: proof for
K=\mathbb{F}_{p}(t)
.

There are
a,\,b,\,c
and
d\in\mathbb{F}_{p}

with
ad-bc\neq 0
such that

\mu(t)=\frac{at+b}{ct+d}
. The identity

t^{n}=(\frac{at+b}{ct+d})^{m}

clearly implies that
n=m
.

Step 2: proof for
K\subset\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.

Since
K
is infinite,
K
contains arbitrarily large finite fields. So we have

K\supset\mathbb{F}_{p^{N}}
for some positive integer
N
with
nm<p^{N}
. Since
K
contains a unique
field of cardinality
p^{N}
we have
\mu(\mathbb{F}_{p^{N}})=\mathbb{F}_{p^{N}}
.
There is an non negative integer
a<N
such that

\mu(x)=x^{p^{a}}
for any
x\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{N}}
. Therefore we have

x^{n}=x^{m{p^{a}}}
for all
x\in F_{p^{N}}^{*}
.

It follows that
n\equiv m{p^{a}}\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{N}-1
.
Let
n=\sum_{k\geq 0}\,n_{k}p^{k}
and

m=\sum_{k\geq 0}\,m_{k}p^{k}
be the
p
-adic expansions of
n
and
m
. By definition each digit
n_{k}
,
m_{k}
is an integer between
0
and
p-1
. Since
n.m<p^{N}
, we have

m_{k}=m_{k}=0
for
k\geq N
.

For each integer
k
, let
[k]
be its residue modulo
N
, so
we have
0\leq[k]<N
by definition. We have

n\equiv mp^{a}\equiv\sum_{0\leq k<N}m_{k}p^{[a+k]}\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{N}-1
.

Since both integers
n
and
\sum_{0\leq k<N}m_{k}p^{[a+k]}

belongs to
[1,p^{N}-1]
and are congruent modulo
p^{N}-1
, it
follows that

n=\sum_{0\leq k<N}m_{k}p^{[a+k]}
.

Assume
a>0
.
We have
n_{a}=m_{0}
and
n_{0}=m_{N-a}
.
Since
n
and
m
are prime to
p
, the digits
n_{0}
and

m_{0}
are not zero, therefore we have

n_{a}\neq 0
and
m_{N-a}\neq 0
. It follows that

n\geq p^{a}
and
m\geq p^{N-a}
,

which contradicts that
nm<p^{N}
.

Therefore, we have
a=0
, and the equality
n=m
is obvious.

Step 3: proof for any infinite field
K
.

In view of the previous step, it can be assumed that

K
contains a transcendental element
t
. Therefore,

K
contains the subfield
L:=\mathbb{F}_{p}(t)
.

By Lemma
10
,
L
and
\mu(L)
are the additive span of the set

\{x^{n}|x\in K^{*}\}=\{\mu(x)^{m}|x\in K^{*}\}
. Therefore,
we have
\mu(L)=L
. Since
\mu
induces a field automorphism of
L
,
the equality
n=m
follows from the first step.

∎

Let
L
be another field. A group morphism

\chi:K^{*}\rightarrow L^{*}
is called a
character
of
K^{*}
.
Let
X(K^{*})
be the set of all
L
-valued
characters of
K^{*}
.
Let
n
be an integer prime to
p
. A character

\chi\in X(K^{*})
is called
semi-algebraic
of
degree

n
if

\chi(x)=\mu(x)^{n}
, for any
x\in K^{*}
,

for some field embedding
\mu:K\rightarrow L
.

Let
{\cal X}_{n}(K^{*})
be the set of all semi-algebraic characters of
K^{*}
of degree
n
. Of course it can be assumed that
L
has characteristic
p
, otherwise the set
{\cal X}_{n}(K^{*})
is empty.

The next lemma shows that for an integer
n>0
prime to
p
, the degree
is uniquely determined by the character
\chi
. Indeed this statement is also true for the
negative integers
n
and moreover the field embedding
\mu
is also determined by
\chi
. For simplicity of the exposition, the lemma is stated and proved in its minimal form.

However, it should be noted that the condition that
n
is prime to
p
is essential.
Indeed, if
\mu:K\rightarrow L
is a field embedding into a perfect field
L
, then

\mu(x)^{n}=\mu^{\prime}(x)^{pn}
, where
\mu^{\prime}
is the field embedding
defined by
\mu^{\prime}(x)=\mu(x)^{1/p}
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
n\neq m
be positive integers which are prime to
p
. Then
{\cal X}_{n}(K^{*})\cap{\cal X}_{m}(K^{*})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Let
n
,
m
be positive integers prime to
p

and let
\chi\in{\cal X}_{n}(K^{*})\cap{\cal X}_{m}(K^{*})
.
By definition, there are fields embeddings

\nu,\nu^{\prime}:K\rightarrow L
such that

\chi(x)=\nu(x)^{n}=\nu^{\prime}(x)^{m}
for any
x\in K^{*}
.

By Lemma
10
,
\nu(K)
and
\nu^{\prime}(K)
are the linear span of
\mathrm{Im}\,\chi
. Therefore
we have
\nu(K)=\nu^{\prime}(K)
and

\mu:=\nu^{\prime-1}\circ\nu
is a well defined field automorphism of

K
. We have

\mu(x)^{n}=\nu^{\prime-1}\circ\nu(x^{n})=\nu^{\prime-1}\circ\nu^{\prime}(x^{m})=x^{m}
.

Therefore by Lemma
11
, we have
n=m
.

∎

Except stated otherwise, it will be assumed that

We follow the same idea than

5.2 Recognition of semi-algebraic characters

From now on,

Lemma 14
.

Let
\chi\in X(K^{*})
, let
\mu:K\rightarrow L

Assume that

Proof.

By Lemma

is the additive span

For

Lemma 15

Let

In what follows,

Since

, it follows that

\mathrm{End}(V)_{(\chi)}\neq 0
.

∎

5.3 Nonlinearity over a field of
\mathrm{SElem}_{0}(K^{2})

Set

\mathrm{SElem}_{0}(K)=\mathrm{Elem}(K)\cap\mathrm{SAut}_{0}\,K^{2}
.

For
n\geq 2
, we will identify

G_{n}(K)
with the subgroup of
\mathrm{SElem}_{0}(K)
of all
automorphism of the form

(x,y)\mapstochar\rightarrow(zx,z^{-1}y+ax^{n-1})

for some

and

.

Lemma 16
.

Let
K
be an infinite field of characteristic

. The group
\mathrm{SElem}_{0}(K)
is not linear over a field.

Proof.

Assume otherwise, and let

\rho:\mathrm{SElem}_{0}(K)\rightarrow{\mathrm{GL}}(V)
, where

is a finite dimensional vector space over an algebraically closed field

. By Lemma
13
, the field

L
has characteristic
p
.

Let
\Omega
be the set of all characters
\chi
of

K^{*}
, such that
\mathrm{End}(V)_{(\chi)}\neq 0
.
Let
n\geq 2
prime to
p
.
Since
\mathrm{SElem}_{0}(K)\rightarrow{\mathrm{GL}}(V)
contains

,
\Omega
contains some
character in
\chi_{n}\in{\cal X}_{n}(K^{*})
by Lemma
15
.
By Lemma
12
these characters

are all

is a finite set.

∎

Lemma 17
.

The group
\mathrm{SElem}_{0}(\mathbb{Q})
is not linear over a field.

Proof.

Assume otherwise, and let

\rho:\mathrm{SElem}_{0}(\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow{\mathrm{GL}}(V)
, where

is a finite dimensional vector space over a field

.
Let

be the automorphism
(x,y)\mapstochar\rightarrow(2x,1/2y)
.

For any

, let

be the automorphism

(x,y)\mapstochar\rightarrow(x,y+x^{n})

. This would imply that

∎

From now on,

Set

Lemma 18
.

The amalgamated product

∎

Theorem A.1
.

Let

The Tits Ping-Pong

6.1 The Ping-Pong lemma

Let
(E_{p})_{p\in P}
be a collection of groups indexed by
a set
P
. Let
\Gamma:=*_{p\in P}\,E_{p}
be the free product of
these groups. Let
\Sigma_{P}
be the set of all finite sequences

(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
such that
p_{i}\neq p_{i+1}
for any

i<n
. For each
p\in P
, set

E^{*}_{p}=E\setminus\{1\}
.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma
. There is a unique

{\bf p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in\Sigma_{P}
and a unique decomposition
of
\gamma

\gamma=\gamma_{1}\dots\gamma_{n}
,

where
\gamma_{i}\in E^{*}_{p_{i}}
. The sequence
{\bf p}
is
called the
type
of
\gamma
.

The free product
\Gamma:=*_{p\in P}\,E_{p}
is called
nontrivial
if

(i) for any
p\in P
,
E_{p}\neq\,\{1\}
,

(ii)
\mathrm{Card\,}\,P\geq 2
.

Also recall that the free product

*_{p\in P}\,E_{p}
is called
nondihedral
if it is not the free product

\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}*\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 19
.

Assume that a nontrivial
nondihedral
free product
\Gamma=*_{p\in P}\,E
acts on some set
\Omega
. Let
(\Omega_{p})_{p\in P}
be a collection of subsets
in
\Omega
. Assume

(i) The subsets
\Omega_{p}
are nonempty and disjoint, and

(ii) we have
E^{*}_{p}.\Omega_{q}\subset\Omega_{p}
whenever
p\neq q
.

Then the action of
\Gamma
on
\Omega
is faithful.

The hypothesis (ii) is called a Ping-Pong hypothesis.

Proof.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma
with
\gamma\neq 1
,
and let
{\bf p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
be its type.
We claim that there is
x\in\Omega
such that
\gamma.x\neq x

If
\mathrm{Card\,}\,P\geq 3
let
q\in P
with

q\neq p_{1}
and
q\neq p_{n}
. Let
x\in\Omega_{q}
.
By the Ping-Pong hypothesis,
\gamma.x
belongs to

\Omega_{p_{1}}
, therefore
\gamma.x\neq x
.

If
\mathrm{Card\,}\,P=2
it can be assumed that
P=\{1,2\}
. It follows from Lemma
5
that
\gamma
has a conjugate
\gamma^{\prime}
of
type
{\bf q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{m})
with
q_{1}=q_{m}=1
. Let

x^{\prime}\in\Omega_{2}
. By the Ping-Pong hypothesis,
\gamma^{\prime}.x^{\prime}
belongs to

\Omega_{1}
, therefore
\gamma^{\prime}.x\neq x^{\prime}
. It follows that there is some
x\in\Omega
with
\gamma.x\neq x
.

In both cases, any
\gamma\neq 1
acts nontrivially.
Hence the action of
\Gamma
on
\Omega
is faithful.
∎

6.2 Mixture of free products, amalgamated products and semi-direct product

This section is devoted to three technical lemmas,
for groups with a mix of free products, amalgamated products and
semi-direct products.

Let
G
be a group. A
G
-structure
on a group
E
is a
G
-action on
E
,
where
G
acts by group automorphisms. Equivalently, it means that the semi-direct product

G\ltimes E
is defined. For simplicity, a group
E
with a
G
-structure is called a

G
-group
. Two
G
-groups
E
,
E^{\prime}
are called

G
-isomorphic
if there is an isomorphism from
E
to
E^{\prime}
which commutes with the

G
-structure.

Let
P
be a set on which
G
acts, let
E
be a group and
for each
p\in P
let
E_{p}
be a copy of
E
. A
G
-structure on
*_{p\in P}\,E_{p}
is called

compatible with the
G
-action on
P
if
E_{p}^{g}=E_{g(p)}
for any
g\in G
and
p\in P
. Roughly speaking,
it means that
G
acts on
*_{p\in P}\,E_{p}
by permuting the free factors. When it is not ambiguous, we just say that the

G
-structure is
compatible
.

Now let
G
,
U
be two groups sharing a common group

A
. Assume moreover that
U=A\ltimes E
,
for some normal subgroup
E
of
U
. Set

\Gamma_{0}=G*_{A}U
. The natural map

U\rightarrow U/E\simeq A
induces a group morphism

\chi:\Gamma_{0}=G*_{A}U\rightarrow G=G*_{A}A
.

and let
\Gamma_{1}
be its kernel.

Set
P=G/A
. For
\gamma\in G
, the subgroup

E^{\gamma}
lies obviously in
\Gamma_{1}
and it depends only on

\gamma\,\mathrm{mod}\,A
.

Lemma 20
.

The group
\Gamma_{1}
is the free product
of all
E^{\gamma}
, where
\gamma
runs over
P
.

Lemma 21
.

For each
p\in P
, let
E_{p}
be
a copy of
E
. A compatible
G
-structure on
*_{p\in P}E_{p}

obviously provides a
A
-structure on
E
, and we have

G\ltimes(*_{p\in P}E_{p})\simeq G*_{A}(A\ltimes E)
.

Proof.

Set
\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}=*_{\gamma\in P}\,E^{\gamma}
.
Clearly
G
acts over
\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}
, so we can consider

\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}:=G\ltimes\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}
.

Using the universal properties of amalgamated, free and
semi-direct products, one defines morphisms

\phi:\Gamma_{0}\rightarrow\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
and
\psi:\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}\rightarrow\Gamma_{0}

which are inverse of each other. It follows that

\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
are isomorphic, and

\phi
induces an isomorphism from
\Gamma_{1}

to
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
, which proves Lemma
20
.

Let
1
be the distinguished point of

G/A
. We have
E_{1}^{a}=E_{1}
for any
a\in A
, hence the group

E=E_{1}
has an
A
-structure. The rest of the proof
of Lemma
21
follows from
universal properties, as before.

∎

For the last lemma, let
G
be a group acting on a set
P

and let
E
and
E^{\prime}
be two other groups. For each
p\in P
, let
G_{p}
be the stabilizer in
G
of
p
and let

E_{p}
(respectively
E^{\prime}_{p}
) be a copy of
E
(respectively of

E^{\prime}
).

Assume given some compatible
G
-structures on

*_{p\in P}\,E_{p}
and
*_{p\in P}\,E^{\prime}_{p}
. Obviously, it
provides some
G_{p}
-structure on
E_{p}
and
E^{\prime}_{p}
, for any
p\in P
.

Lemma 22
.

Assume that the groups
E_{p}
and
E^{\prime}_{p}
are
G_{p}
-isomorphic
for any
p\in P
. Then the groups
G\ltimes(*_{p\in P}\,E_{p})
and

G\ltimes(*_{p\in P}\,E^{\prime}_{p})
are isomorphic.

Proof.

First, assume that
G
acts transitively on

P
. Let
p
be a point of
P
,
and let
A:=G_{p}
be its stabilizer. It follows from Lemma
21
that

G\ltimes(*_{p\in P}\,E_{p})\simeq G*_{A}(A\ltimes E_{p}))
, and

G\ltimes(*_{p\in P}\,E^{\prime}_{p})\simeq G*_{A}(A\ltimes E^{\prime}_{p})
.

Hence the groups

*_{p\in P}\,E_{p}
and
*_{p\in P}\,E^{\prime}_{p}
are
G
-isomorphic.
From this, it follows that the groups

*_{p\in P}\,E_{p}
and
*_{p\in P}\,E^{\prime}_{p}
are

G
-isomorphic even if
G
does not act transitively on
P
. Therefore
G\ltimes(*_{p\in P}\,E_{p})
and

G\ltimes(*_{p\in P}\,E^{\prime}_{p})
are isomorphic.
∎

6.3 The subgroups of
{\mathrm{GL}}_{S}(2,K[t])
in
{\mathrm{GL}}(2,K[t])

For
G(t)\in{\mathrm{GL}}(2,K[t])
, let
G(0)
its evaluation at
0
. For any subgroup
S\subset{\mathrm{GL}}(2,K)

set

{\mathrm{GL}}_{S}(2,K[t]):=\{G(t)\in{\mathrm{GL}}(2,K[t])|G(0)\in S\}
.

For
S=\{1\}
, the group
{\mathrm{GL}}_{S}(2,K[t])
will be denoted by

{\mathrm{GL}}_{1}(2,K[t])
.

For any
\gamma\in\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}
, let
e_{\gamma}\in\mathrm{End}(K^{2})
such that

e_{\gamma}^{2}=0
and
\mathrm{Im}e_{\gamma}=\gamma
. Since

e_{\gamma}
is unique up to a constant multiple, the group

E_{\gamma}:=\{\mathrm{id}+tf(t)e_{\gamma}|\,f\in K[t]\}

is a well defined subgroup of
{\mathrm{GL}}_{1}(2,K[t])
.

Lemma 23
.

The group
{\mathrm{GL}}_{1}(2,K[t])
is generated by its subgroups
E_{\gamma}
, where
\gamma
runs over
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}
.

Proof.

For
A
,
B\in\mathrm{End}(K^{2})
, set

<A|\,B>=\det\,(A+B)-\det\,A-\det\,B
.

Any element
G(t)\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{1}(2,K[t])
can be written as a
polynomial

G(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\,A_{n}t^{n}

where
A_{n}
belong to
\mathrm{End}(K^{2})
and
A_{0}=\mathrm{id}
.

The proof runs by induction on the degree
N
of
G
.
It can be assumed that
N\geq 1
. For any
n\geq 0
, let

I_{n}
be the set of pairs of integers
(i,j)

with
0\leq i<j
and
i+j=n
. We have

\det\,G(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\,c_{n}t^{n}
, where the scalars
c_{n}
are given by

c_{n}=\det\,A_{n/2}+\sum_{(i,j)\in I_{n}}\,<A_{i}|A_{j}>
,

where it is understood that
\det\,A_{n/2}=0
if
n
is odd.

Since
\det\,G(t)
is an invertible polynomial, we have
c_{n}=0
for any
n>0
.
The identity
c_{2N}=0
implies that
\det\,A_{N}=0
hence
A_{N}
has rank one.
Let
\delta
be the image of
\,A_{N}
and
set
E=\{a\in\mathrm{End}(K^{2})|\,\mathrm{Im}\,a\subset\delta\}

There exist an integer
n
such that

A_{n}\notin E
,
but
A_{m}\in E
for any
m>n
.

We have

0=c_{N+n}=\det\,A_{N+n\over 2}+\sum_{(i,j)\in I_{N+n}}\,<A_{i}|A_{j}>

Since
E
consists of rank-one endomorphisms, we have

\det a=0
for any
a\in E
and
<a,b>=0
for any
a,\,b\in E
.
Therefore we have
\det\,A_{N+n\over 2}=0
and
<A_{i}|A_{j}>=0
, whenever

(i,j)
lies in
I_{N+n}
and
i\neq n
. Thus it follows that

<A_{n}|\,A_{N}>=0
.

Set
\delta^{\prime}=\mathrm{Ker}A_{N}
. An easy computation shows that the previous relation

<A_{N}|\,A_{n}>=0
implies
that
A_{n}(\delta^{\prime})\subset\delta
. Set
B=e_{\delta}\circ A_{n}
.
Clearly we have
\mathrm{Ker}\,B\supset\delta^{\prime}
and
\mathrm{Im}\,B\subset\delta
, therefore

B
is proportional to
A_{N}
. Since
A_{n}
is not in
E
, we have
B\neq 0
and therefore

ce_{\delta}\circ A_{n}=A_{N}
for some
c\in K
.
Set

H(t)=(1-ct^{N-n}e_{\delta}).G(t)
.

We have
e_{\delta}.a=0
for any
a\in E
, therefore we have

e_{\delta}.A_{m}=0
for any
m>n
. It follows that

H(t)
has degree
\leq N
. Moreover, its degree
N
component
is
A_{N}-ce_{\gamma}.A_{n}=0
. Therefore
H(t)
has degree

<N
, and the proof runs by induction.
∎

Lemma 26

The collection of isomorphisms

Let

8.1 Abelian groups

Let

be a torsion free abelian group, where the product is denoted multiplicatively. Let

be the fraction field of the group algebra

. Viewed as a

-module,

is called the

-module
, and
it is denoted
{\mathrm{St}}(A)
.
A direct sum of standard module

is indeed a

F(A)
-vector space, and the multiplicity of

{\mathrm{St}}(A)
dans
M
, denoted by

[M:{\mathrm{St}}(A)]
, is
\dim_{F(A)}\,M
.

For any

, let

{}_{n}\rho:A\rightarrow A
be the group morphism
a\mapstochar\rightarrow a^{n}
.
For a
F[A]
-module

, the

-module

is denoted

.

Lemma 28
.

The
F[A]
-module

is a
direct sum of standard modules, and

[F(A)^{(n)}:{\mathrm{St}}(A)]=[A:A^{n}]
.

Proof.

Since
F[A]
is an algebraic extension of

, it follows that

F(A)=F(A^{n})\otimes_{F[A^{n}]}\,F[A]
, and therefore

F(A)=\oplus_{a\in T}\,F(A^{n})a

where

is a set of representative of

, from which the Lemma follows.

8.2 The condition
{\cal G}

Let
A
be a subgroup of
K^{*}
.
By definition, its
rank
is the cardinal
\mathrm{rk}\,A:=\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}\,\mathbb{Q}\otimes A
and its
degree
, denoted as
\deg\,A
, is the transcendental degree of the subfield generated by
A
. It is clear that

\deg\,A\leq\mathrm{rk}\,A
. Moreover,
we have
\deg\,A^{\prime}=\mathrm{rk}\,A^{\prime}
for any finite rank
subgroup
A^{\prime}\subset A
iff the map

F[A]\rightarrow K
is one-to-one, and therefore

K
contains the field
F(A)
.

Let
S
be a subgroup
of
{\mathrm{SL}}(2,K)
. For any
\delta\in\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}
,
set
S_{\delta}:=\{g\in S|g.\delta=\delta\}
.
By definition, any

acts over
\delta
by multiplication by some scalar
\chi(g)
and
the map

\chi:g\in S_{\delta}\mapstochar\rightarrow\chi(g)\in K^{*}

is a group morphism. Set

A(\delta)=\chi(S_{\delta})
.

By definition, we say that
S

has the property

{\cal G}
in
{\mathrm{SL}}(2,K)
if for any
\delta\in\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}
,
the group

is torsion free,
and we have
\deg\,A=\mathrm{rk}\,A
for any
finite rank subgroup
A
in
A(\delta)
.
As usual,
S
is called
virtually
{\cal G}

in
{\mathrm{SL}}(2,K)
if some finite index subgroup

has the property
{\cal G}
.

8.3 The Theorem C

Let
S
be a subgroup of
{\mathrm{SL}}(2,K)
. Recal that

{\mathrm{GL}}_{S}(2,K[t])=\{G\in{\mathrm{GL}}(2,K[t])|G(1)\in S\}
.

note that we have indeed

{\mathrm{GL}}_{S}(2,K[t])\subset{\mathrm{SL}}(2,K[t])
.

Theorem C
.

Assume that the subgroup
S
has the property
{\cal G}
in
{\mathrm{SL}}(2,K)
. Then
\mathrm{Aut}_{S}\,K^{2}
is linear
over some field extension of
K
.

Moreover if
\deg\,K\leq\aleph_{0}
, then we have

\mathrm{Aut}_{S}\,K^{2}\simeq{\mathrm{GL}}_{S}(2,K[t])
.

In particular,
\mathrm{Aut}_{S}\,K^{2}
is linear over
K(t)
.

Proof.

There exists a field extension
L\supset K
,
which satisfies one of the following two hypotheses

({\cal I}_{1})

[L:K]\geq\deg K
if

\deg\,K>\aleph_{0}
, or

({\cal I}_{2})

L=K
if
\deg K\leq\aleph_{0}

It follows from Lemmas
24
and
25

that

\mathrm{Aut}_{1}\,K^{2}=*_{\delta\in\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}}\,F_{\delta}
.

Using that
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}_{L}
, we also have

{\mathrm{GL}}_{1}(2,L[t])\supset*_{\delta\in\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}}\,E_{\delta}
.

Step 1: existence of some

S_{\delta}
-equivariant embeddings

F_{\delta}\rightarrow E_{\delta}
.
Let
\delta\in\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}
. We claim that

({\cal I}_{1})
implies the existence of a

S_{\delta}
-equivariant embedding

\psi_{\delta}:F_{\delta}\rightarrow E_{\delta}
,

({\cal I}_{2})
implies the existence
a
S_{\delta}
-equivariant isomorphism

\psi_{\delta}:F_{\delta}\rightarrow E_{\delta}
.

Note that
the kernel of the morphism

\chi:S_{\delta}\rightarrow A(\delta)
acts trivially
on
F_{\delta}
and
E_{\delta}
, so both of them are

F[A(\delta)]
-modules, where
F
is the prime field of
K
.

Note that
e_{\delta}^{g}=\chi(g)^{2}e_{\delta}
for any

g\in S_{\delta}
. Hence

E_{\delta}\simeq\oplus_{n\geq 1}L.z^{n}e_{\delta}
is a countable sum of modules isomorphic to
L^{(2)}
. It follows from Lemma
28
that
E_{\delta}
is a direct sum of standard modules with multiplicity

[E_{\delta}:{\mathrm{St}}(A(\delta)]=\aleph_{0}[L:K][K:F(A\delta))][A(\delta):A^{2}(\delta)]
.

Let
\phi_{n}
be the automorphism

(x,y)\mapstochar\rightarrow(x,y+x^{n})
. Note that

\phi_{n}^{g}
is the automorphism

(x,y)\mapstochar\rightarrow(x,y+\chi(g)^{n+1}x^{n}
for any

g\in S_{\delta}
. Hence we have

F_{\delta}\simeq\oplus_{n\geq 3}\,K^{(n)}
.

By Lemma
28
,
F_{\delta}
is a direct sum of standard modules with multiplicity

[F_{\delta}:{\mathrm{St}}(A(\delta)]=\sum_{n\geq 3}\,[K:F(A\delta)][A(\delta):A^{n}(\delta)]
.

Note that
[A(\delta):A^{n}(\delta)]
is obviously

\leq\deg\,K
. It follows from hypothesis

({\cal I}_{1})
that

[F_{\delta}:{\mathrm{St}}(A(\delta)]\leq[E_{\delta}:{\mathrm{St}}(A(\delta)]

and from hypothesis
({\cal I}_{1})
that

[F_{\delta}:{\mathrm{St}}(A(\delta)]=[E_{\delta}:{\mathrm{St}}(A(\delta)]
.
Therefore both claims are proved.

Step 2: end of the proof.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}
be a set of
representative of
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}/S
.
By Lemma
22
, the collection of morphisms

(\psi_{\delta})_{\delta\in\Omega}
provides

(i) an embedding
\mathrm{Aut}_{S}\,K^{2}\subset{\mathrm{GL}}_{S}(2,L[t])
under hypothesis
({\cal I}_{1})
,

(ii) an isomorphism

\mathrm{Aut}_{S}\,K^{2}\simeq{\mathrm{GL}}_{S}(2,K[t])
under hypothesis

({\cal I}_{2})
,

from which the lemma follows.

∎

by all polynomials
f(t+u)
when
u
runs over
E
.
The group
E\ltimes M
, which is a subgroup of
G(A)
,
is obvioulsly nilpotent.

Lemma 32
.

Assume that the algebra
A
is prime.

(i) If
F=\mathbb{Q}
, the nilpotency index of
E\ltimes M
is
\deg\,f
.

(ii) Assume that
F=\mathbb{F}_{p}
, that
E
is an elementary
group of rank
r
and that
f(t)=ax^{p^{r}-1}
for some

a\in A\setminus 0
. Then the group
E\ltimes M
has nilpotency index

rp
.

Proof.

The first statement is obvious, and the proof
will be skipped. From now on, we will assume that
F=\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Step 1.
We claim that

\sum_{u\in E}\,u^{p^{r}-1}=c\prod_{u\in E\setminus 0}\,u
.

for some
c\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
independent of
A
and

E
.
It is enough to prove the claim for

A=\mathbb{F}_{p}[x_{1},\dots,x_{r}]
and
E=\oplus_{i}\mathbb{F}_{p}.x_{i}
.

Let
u\in E\setminus 0
and let
H\subset E
be an
hyperplane with
u\notin H
. For any integer
n\geq 0
, we have

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\,\lambda^{n}=0

except if
n
is a positive multiple of
p-1
. Therefore we have

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\,(\lambda u+v)^{p^{r}-1}=\sum_{n>0}\,c_{n}u^{n(p-1)}v^{p^{r}-1-n(p-1)}

for some
c_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, for any
v\in H
.
Since any
w\in E
can be uniquely written as

w=\lambda u+v
, for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
and

v\in H
, it follows that the polynomial

\sum_{w\in E}\,w^{p^{r}-1}
is divisible by
u^{p-1}
for
any
u\in E\setminus 0
. Therefore the polynomial

\sum_{w\in E}\,w^{p^{r}-1}
is divisible by

\prod_{u\in E\setminus 0}\,u
. Since both polynomials have
degree
p^{r}-1
, it follows that

\sum_{u\in E}\,u^{p^{r}-1}=c\prod_{u\in E\setminus 0}\,u
, for some
c\in F_{p}
.

Step 2.

Next we prove that
c=1
. Since
c
is a universal constant, it can be computed for
A=E=\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
.
Since

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r}}\,\lambda^{p^{r}-1}=-1
, and

\prod_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r}}\,\lambda=-1
,

it follows that
c=1
.

Step 3.
Set

g(t)=\sum_{u\in E}\,f(t+u)
. We have

g(0)=\sum_{u\in E}\,au^{p^{r}-1}
, therefore

g(0)=a\prod_{u\in E\setminus 0}\,u
. Since

g\neq 0
, it follows from Lemma
31
that
the
\mathbb{F}_{p}[E]
-module
M
is free of rank one.
Therefore
E\ltimes M
is isomorphic to
G(r)
. Thus
its nilpotency index is
pr
by
by Lemma
30
.

∎

A obvious corollary of the previous lemma is

Lemma 33

Assume that the algebra
A
is prime and let

be a nonzero ideal. Then

contains subgroups of arbitrarily large nilpotency index.

Proof.

The lemma follows directly from Lemma

32
. When

, on should add the remark that the hypotheses imply that

10.4 The amalgamated product

Let
\mathrm{Aff}(2,I)
and

be the

Lemma 35
.

The group

\mathrm{Aff}(2,I)*_{B(I)}\mathrm{Elem}(I)
is not

Proof.

By Lemma
34
, the group

\mathrm{Elem}(I)
is not linear over a field. Therefore
by Lemma
6
, it is enough to show that
the amalgamated product
\Gamma:=\mathrm{Aff}(2,I)*_{B(I)}\mathrm{Elem}(I)
satisfies
the hypothesis
{\cal H}
.

In order to do so, we first define two

\gamma
and
\phi
as follows.
Let

and
let

be an integer prime to

\operatorname{ch}\,K
. Let

\phi\in\mathrm{Elem}(I)

Let

By definition,
