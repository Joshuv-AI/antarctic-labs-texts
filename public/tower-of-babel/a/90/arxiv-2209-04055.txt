Cotton gravity and 84 galaxy rotation curves
Junpei Harada

Abstract
Recently, as a generalization of general relativity, a gravity theory has been proposed in which gravitational field equations are described by the Cotton tensor. That theory allows an additional contribution to the gravitational potential of a point mass that rises linearly with radius as Φ = − G M / r + γ r / 2 \displaystyle\Phi=-GM/r+\gamma r/2 , where G \displaystyle G is the Newton constant. The coefficients M \displaystyle M and γ \displaystyle\gamma are the constants of integration and should be determined individually for each physical system. When applied to galaxies, the coefficient γ \displaystyle\gamma , which has the dimension of acceleration, should be determined for each galaxy. This is the same as having to determine the mass M \displaystyle M for each galaxy. If γ \displaystyle\gamma is small enough, the linear potential term is negligible at short distances, but can become significant at large distances. In fact, it may contribute to the extragalactic systems. In this paper, we derive the effective field equation for Cotton gravity applicable to extragalactic systems. We then use the effective field equation to numerically compute the gravitational potential of a sample of 84 rotating galaxies. The 84 galaxies span a wide range, from stellar disk-dominated spirals to gas-dominated dwarf galaxies. We do not assume the radial density profile of the stellar disk, bulge, or gas; we use only the observed data. We find that the rotation curves of 84 galaxies can be explained by the observed distribution of baryons. This is due to the flexibility of Cotton gravity to allow the integration constant γ \displaystyle\gamma for each galaxy. In the context of Cotton gravity, “dark matter” is in some sense automatically included as a curvature of spacetime. Consequently, even galaxies that have been assumed to be dominated by dark matter do not need dark matter.



I
Introduction

The missing gravity problem in extragalactic systems appears when gravity is extremely weak.
Empirically, the typical magnitude of gravitational acceleration is
\displaystyle 1\ {\rm km^{2}\ s^{-2}\ pc^{-1}}\sim 10^{-11}\ {\rm m\ s^{-2}}
or less.
If gravity is much stronger than that scale, no acceleration discrepancy has been observed.
For understanding the missing gravity problem, it is necessary to test the laws of gravity at the extremely weak regimes.
To date, no gravity theory has been experimentally established applicable to such extremely weak regimes.

Recently, a new gravity theory has been proposed
Harada 2021
, in which gravitational field equations are described by the Cotton tensor (named after mathematician, Émile Cotton)
Cotton 1899
.
Here, the theory is called
Cotton gravity
.
Cotton gravity is a generalization of general relativity.
The field equations of Cotton gravity have solutions that are not solutions of Einstein equations. In particular, Cotton gravity allows an extra contribution to the gravitational potential of a point mass that rises linearly with radius,
\displaystyle\Phi=-GM/r+\gamma r/2
.
Thus, Cotton gravity may give the extra contributions at large distances, which cannot be explained by general relativity.

The purpose of this paper is to report that in Cotton gravity, the galaxy rotation curves can be explained by the observed distribution of baryons. We investigate 84 rotating galaxies with very different properties; galaxies extend a wide range from largest galaxies known to the smallest, low mass to high mass, low surface brightness to high surface brightness, and low gas fraction to high.

In this paper, we do not assume a radial density profile for the stellar disk, bulge, or gas—only the data is used.
We also do not assume any profiles for the dark matter halo.
As we will see, the observed baryonic mass and the gravitational field equation of Cotton gravity alone can explain galaxy rotation curves.

Before beginning this work, it should be mentioned about Milgromian dynamics (MOND)
Milgrom 1983
.
MOND is one of the most extensively studied modified gravity approaches to the missing gravity problem.
The very detailed recent review of MOND (and its applications) is given in
Banik and Zhao 2022
, and the detailed fits to the galaxy rotation curves without an extra free parameter per galaxy was given in
Li et al. 2018
. The comparison between MOND and dark matter approach was presented in
McGaugh 2020
.

It may be also mentioned about conformal gravity. Conformal gravity also allows the linear term in the potential of a point mass
Mannheim and Kazanas 1989
.
Applications of conformal gravity to galaxy rotation curves were reviewed in
Mannheim and O’Brien 2013
,
but some severe problems have been reported in
Horne 2016
;
Campigotto et al. 2019
;
Hobson and Lasenby 2022
.
Although a spherically symmetric exact solution of conformal gravity is approximately equivalent to that of Cotton gravity,
it should be noted that Cotton gravity is a different theory from conformal gravity
Harada 2021
.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
II
briefly summarizes Cotton gravity. In Sec.
III
, the effective field equation of Cotton gravity is derived. Section
IV
plots the rotation velocities of 84 galaxies. Section
V
is devoted to discussion and conclusions.
The appendix shows the details of the calculations.
The signature of metric is
\displaystyle(-,+,+,+)
throughout the paper.

II
Cotton gravity

In Cotton gravity, the field equations are given by
Harada 2021

C_{\nu\rho\sigma}=16\pi G\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu}{}_{\nu\rho\sigma},

(1)

where
\displaystyle C_{\nu\rho\sigma}
is the Cotton tensor,
\displaystyle G
is the Newton constant,
\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}
is a covariant derivative associated with the Levi-Civita connection, and
\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
is defined by Eq. (
3
).

Equation (
1
) is a generalization of Einstein equations of general relativity.
Indeed, Eq. (
1
) has all solutions of Einstein equations—with or without the nonzero cosmological constant—and other solutions that are not solutions of Einstein equations
Harada 2021
.
This means the following; if the Einstein equations (
\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}=8\pi GT_{\mu\nu}
) are satisfied, then Eq. (
1
) is also satisfied.
Furthermore, if the Einstein equations with the nonzero cosmological constant (
\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}=8\pi GT_{\mu\nu}
) are satisfied, then Eq. (
1
) is still satisfied (note that Eq. (
1
) does not include the cosmological constant
\displaystyle\Lambda
). Most importantly, even if Einstein equations are
not
satisfied, Eq. (
1
) can be satisfied.

Cotton gravity is more general than general relativity in that sense.
Since Eq. (
1
) has solutions that are not solutions of Einstein equations, it may describe physics that cannot be explained by general relativity.
This paper shows that galaxies may be typical systems, in which deviations from general relativity are significant.

In Eq. (
1
), the Cotton tensor
\displaystyle C_{\nu\rho\sigma}
is defined by
Cotton 1899

C_{\nu\rho\sigma}=\nabla_{\rho}R_{\nu\sigma}-\nabla_{\sigma}R_{\nu\rho}-\frac{1}{6}(g_{\nu\sigma}\nabla_{\rho}{\cal R}-g_{\nu\rho}\nabla_{\sigma}{\cal R}),

(2)

where
\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}
is the Ricci tensor and
\displaystyle{\cal R}
is the Ricci scalar. Here and hereafter, we denote the Ricci scalar with
\displaystyle{\cal R}
—following the convention in astronomy, we will denote the radial distance in a cylindrical coordinate system with
\displaystyle R
.
The tensor
\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
in Eq. (
1
) is defined by
Harada 2021

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(g_{\mu\rho}T_{\nu\sigma}-g_{\nu\rho}T_{\mu\sigma}-g_{\mu\sigma}T_{\nu\rho}+g_{\nu\sigma}T_{\mu\rho})

(3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{1}{6}(g_{\mu\rho}g_{\nu\sigma}-g_{\nu\rho}g_{\mu\sigma})T,

where
\displaystyle T:=g^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu}
, and
\displaystyle g^{\nu\sigma}T_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}=T_{\mu\rho}
holds.

Multiplying Eq. (
1
) by
\displaystyle g^{\nu\sigma}
, we find that the conservation law
\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=0
is a consequence of Eq. (
1
) as

g^{\nu\sigma}C_{\nu\rho\sigma}=16\pi G\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu}{}_{\rho}=0,

(4)

where the Bianchi identity
\displaystyle g^{\nu\sigma}C_{\nu\rho\sigma}=0
has been used.
With
\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=0
, the right-hand side of Eq. (
1
) is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu}{}_{\nu\rho\sigma}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{\rho}T_{\nu\sigma}-\nabla_{\sigma}T_{\nu\rho})-\frac{1}{6}(g_{\nu\sigma}\nabla_{\rho}-g_{\nu\rho}\nabla_{\sigma})T.

In the next section, we will derive the effective field equation of Eq. (
1
) applicable to galaxies.

III
Effective field equation

When gravity is weak, a metric can be written as

g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu},

(6)

where
\displaystyle\eta_{\mu\nu}
is the Minkowski metric, and
\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}
is small.
For galaxies studied in this work, the energy-momentum tensor
\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}
is approximately given by

T_{00}=\rho,\quad T_{ij}=0,\quad T_{0i}=0,\quad T=-\rho,

(7)

where
\displaystyle\rho
is a mass density. We assume that
\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}
and
\displaystyle\rho
are time independent (or assume that their time dependence sufficiently small so that time derivative can be ignored).

For
\displaystyle\nu=\sigma=0
and
\displaystyle\rho=1,2,3
in Eq. (
1
), we have

\nabla\left(R_{00}+\frac{1}{6}{\cal R}\right)=\frac{16\pi G}{3}\nabla\rho,

(8)

or equivalently

R_{00}+\frac{1}{6}{\cal R}=\frac{16\pi G}{3}\rho+{\rm const}.

(9)

The constant in Eq. (
9
) is the cosmological constant—recall that in Cotton gravity, the cosmological constant is a constant of integration
Harada 2021
. We assume that the contributions of the cosmological constant are negligible at galactic scales. In that case, Eq. (
9
) reduces to

R_{00}+\frac{1}{6}{\cal R}=\frac{16\pi G}{3}\rho.

(10)

Let us calculate
\displaystyle R_{00}
and
\displaystyle{\cal R}
in Eq. (
10
).
At far from a center, a metric can be approximately written by

ds^{2}=-(1+2\Phi)dt^{2}+(1-2\Phi)dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2},

(11)

where
\displaystyle d\Omega^{2}=d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\phi^{2}
, and
\displaystyle\Phi
is the potential. Transforming from spherical to Cartesian coordinates, we find that

\displaystyle\displaystyle h_{00}=-2\Phi,\quad h_{ij}=-2\Phi n_{i}n_{j},\quad h_{0i}=0,

(12)

where
\displaystyle{\bm{n}}:={\bm{r}}/|\bm{r}|
, and
\displaystyle h:=\eta^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}=2\Phi-2\Phi\bm{n}\cdot\bm{n}=0
.
At a linear order in
\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}
, the Ricci tensor is given by

R_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\left(-\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}h+\partial_{\mu}\partial^{\rho}h_{\nu\rho}+\partial_{\nu}\partial^{\rho}h_{\mu\rho}-\Box h_{\mu\nu}\right).

(13)

Plugging Eq. (
12
) into Eq. (
13
), we obtain

R_{00}=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}h_{00}=\nabla^{2}\Phi.

(14)

If Einstein equations are satisfied, then the field equation (
10
) just reduces to the usual Poisson equation as

\nabla^{2}\Phi=\frac{16\pi G}{3}\rho-\frac{8\pi G}{6}\rho=4\pi G\rho,

(15)

where the Ricci scalar
\displaystyle{\cal R}=8\pi G\rho
has been used.
In Cotton gravity, however, Einstein equations are
not
necessarily satisfied
Harada 2021
. In that case, the field equation (
10
) does
not
reduce to the Poisson equation as follows.

At a linear order in
\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}
, the Ricci scalar is given by

{\cal R}=\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}h_{\mu\nu}-\Box h.

(16)

Plugging Eq. (
12
) into Eq. (
16
), we find that

{\cal R}=-\frac{2}{|\bm{r}|^{2}}\left((\bm{r}\cdot\nabla)^{2}+4(\bm{r}\cdot\nabla)+2\right)\Phi,

(17)

and the field equation (
10
) reduces to

\left[\nabla^{2}-\frac{1}{3|\bm{r}|^{2}}\left((\bm{r}\cdot\nabla)^{2}+4(\bm{r}\cdot\nabla)+2\right)\right]\Phi=\frac{16\pi G}{3}\rho.

(18)

This is the effective field equation of Eq. (
1
).
Thus, operators other than
\displaystyle\nabla^{2}
are necessary to determine
\displaystyle\Phi
.

Equation (
18
) yields a crucial difference from the Poisson equation (
15
) at large distances.
To see it, in the spherically symmetric system for example, we have

R_{00}=\nabla^{2}\Phi=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}\Phi^{\prime}\right)=\Phi^{\prime\prime}+\frac{2\Phi^{\prime}}{r},

(19)

where the primes denote the derivative with respect to the radial coordinates
\displaystyle r
.
From Eq. (
17
), we also have

{\cal R}=-2\left(\Phi^{\prime\prime}+\frac{4\Phi^{\prime}}{r}+\frac{2\Phi}{r^{2}}\right).

(20)

Therefore, the left-hand side of Eq. (
18
) is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle R_{00}+\frac{1}{6}{\cal R}=\left[\nabla^{2}-\frac{1}{3|\bm{r}|^{2}}\left((\bm{r}\cdot\nabla)^{2}+4(\bm{r}\cdot\nabla)+2\right)\right]\Phi

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}\left(\Phi^{\prime\prime}+\frac{\Phi^{\prime}}{r}-\frac{\Phi}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{2}{3r^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left[r^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{\Phi}{r}\right)\right].

(21)

Then, Eq. (
18
) is given in a simple form as,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left[r^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{\Phi}{r}\right)\right]=8\pi Gr^{2}\rho(r).

(22)

For the mass density
\displaystyle\rho(r)=0\ (r\geq a)
and
\displaystyle\rho(r)\not=0\ (r\leq a)
,
the solution of Eq. (
22
) is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Phi(r\geq a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{GM}{r}+\frac{\gamma}{2}r,\quad M=\int_{0}^{a}4\pi\xi^{2}\rho(\xi)d\xi,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Phi(r\leq a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{G}{r}\int_{0}^{r}4\pi\xi^{2}\rho(\xi)d\xi-Gr\int_{r}^{a}4\pi\rho(\xi)d\xi+\frac{\gamma}{2}r,

where
\displaystyle\gamma
is an integration constant(
\displaystyle 1/2
is a convention).
While the mass
\displaystyle M
is determined by
\displaystyle\rho(r)
,
\displaystyle\gamma
is arbitrary unless the boundary condition will be determined.
The linear potential term
\displaystyle\gamma r/2
—which cannot be obtained from general relativity—is significant at large
\displaystyle r
.

In Sec.
IV
, it is shown that Eq. (
18
) can explain galaxy rotation curves from the distribution of baryons. In the context of Cotton gravity, the missing gravity problem is originated from the Ricci curvature (
17
), rather than dark matter.

IV.2
Gravitational potential

We solve the effective field equation (
18
) numerically to determine the gravitational potential
\displaystyle\Phi
—we also solve the Poisson equation (
15
) for comparison. The appendix presents the formulas and the details of calculations. The mass density is totally from the stellar disk, bulge, and gas. In particular, we do not assume a radial profile for the stellar disk, bulge, or gas—we use only the data.
For the vertical distributions from the galactic planes, we assume the finite thickness for the stellar disk and gas. We assume that bulge is spherical as an approximation. These details are shown in the appendix.

When we solve Eq. (
18
), following the solution (
), we adopt the boundary condition at far from the center,

\Phi=\gamma_{\rm galaxy}\sqrt{R^{2}+z^{2}}/2,

(24)

where
\displaystyle R
is the radius in a cylindrical coordinate system,
\displaystyle z
is the vertical coordinate, and
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
is a constant.
The values of
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
should be determined for each galaxy. This is the same as the mass
\displaystyle M
should be determined for each galaxy.
The constant
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
is just an integration constant rather than a fundamental parameter of the theory—the field equations (
1
) does not include
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
.
We determine the value of
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
as a fitting parameter for each galaxy.
We find that many galaxies have typically
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}\sim 1\ {\rm km^{2}\ s^{-2}\ pc^{-1}}
(column (5) in Table
1
). The flexibility to allow the individual values
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
for each galaxy is a significant advantage of Eq. (
1
).

IV.3
Rotation curves

In Cotton gravity, the observed rotation curves can be explained by the distributions of baryons (Fig.
1
).
The flat shape of rotation curves (NGC 5907, UGC 2885, UGC 2487, and NGC7331 in Fig.
1
) and the nonflat/rising shape (NGC 3109 and NGC 100 in Fig.
1
) can be obtained in the same context without assumptions.
In the context of Cotton gravity, the calculated shape of rotation curves are essentially determined by the distributions of baryons.
This is in agreement with one of the most important observations: “For any feature in the luminosity profile there is a corresponding feature in the rotation curve and vise verse”
Sancisi 2004
.

In Cotton gravity, both large galaxies (UGC 2885 and UGC 2487 in Fig.
1
) and small galaxies (NGC 3109 and NGC 100 in Fig.
1
) can be explained.
Figure
2
shows the rotation curves of 84 galaxies.
The 84 galaxies are very different in morphologies, luminosities, masses, sizes, and gas fractions.
Nevertheless, 84 galaxy rotation curves can be explained by the single field equation (
18
).

The baryonic Tully-Fisher relation is an empirical relation between the total baryonic mass
\displaystyle M_{\rm bar}
and the flat rotation speed
\displaystyle V_{\rm flat}

McGaugh et al. 2000
.
Figure

shows the baryonic Tully-Fisher relation for the present study.
From the data, we find that

M_{\rm bar}/M_{\odot}=10^{2.37}\left(\frac{V_{\rm flat}}{{\rm km/s}}\right)^{3.72},

(26)

or equivalently

M_{\rm bar}/(10^{9}M_{\odot})=6.5\left(\frac{V_{\rm flat}}{100\ {\rm km/s}}\right)^{3.72},

(27)

\displaystyle 1\times 10^{8}<M_{\rm bar}/M_{\odot}<6\times 10^{11}

Equation (

\displaystyle{\cal O}(1)

In Cotton gravity, this relation is obtained as a consequence of the field equations.

\displaystyle V_{\rm flat}
is just an effective concept—the rotation velocities should not be necessarily flat in Cotton gravity (Fig.

In general, the exponent is not exactly equals to 4 in the context of Cotton gravity.

IV.6

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}

The parameter
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
should be determined for each galaxy.
In Cotton gravity, it is theoretically unnatural to assume that
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
takes a single value for all galaxies.
The particular value of
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
per galaxy is allowed, and it is a significant advantage of Cotton gravity.

There is no correlation between the baryonic mass
\displaystyle M_{\rm bar}
and the value of
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
(Fig.

\displaystyle 0.2<\gamma_{\rm galaxy}/2<1.3\ {\rm km^{2}\ s^{-2}\ pc^{-1}}
(Fig.

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}/2=3.0\ {\rm km^{2}\ s^{-2}\ pc^{-1}}

Changing
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}

Figure

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}/2
by 50% from the best fit values.
We find that changing the values of
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}/2
does not affect the whole shape of the curves.

Cotton gravity is flexible in the sense that it allows an additional parameter
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
for each galaxy.
Therefore, one may think that the observed rotation curves can be easily explained by adjusting arbitrarily
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
.
Figure

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
is allowed for each galaxy.

V
Discussion and conclusions

We found that Cotton gravity can explain the rotation curves of 84 galaxies without the need for dark matter. In Cotton gravity, the flat rotation curves and the nonflat rising shape of rotation curves can be explained in the same context without special assumptions (Fig.
2
). All we need is the data on the distribution of stars and gas, and the gravitational field equation (
18
). This is satisfactory because the observed data (surface density) explains the observed data (rotation velocity).

Cotton gravity allows an extra linear term in the gravitational potential of a point mass.
The coefficient of the linear term
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
is a constant of integration. Therefore, it (and the mass
\displaystyle M
) should be determined separately for each galaxy. Various values of
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm galaxy}
are completely consistent with Eq. (
1
).
According to this flexibility of Cotton gravity, galaxy rotation curves can be explained.

From this work, we have observed the following:
the observed potential
\displaystyle\Phi
and the observed mass densities of 84 galaxies does not satisfy the Poisson equation, but (at least approximately) satisfy the effective field equation (
18
). This is empirical. That is, what we found is that the effective field equation (
18
) has the particular solution that is consistent with the observed gravitational potential
\displaystyle\Phi
and the baryonic masses.

Here it should be also mentioned the following. Cotton gravity was proposed with the pure theoretical motivation as a generalization of general relativity
Harada 2021
. It was not intended to solve a particular problem in astrophysics. In particular, the galaxy rotation curve was not the motivation for Cotton gravity. Nevertheless, Cotton gravity can explain the rotation curves—it is just a consequence of the field equations of Cotton gravity.

The results of this paper suggest the following:

1.

When gravity is extremely weak, the law of gravity may deviate from general relativity.

2.

Galaxies are typical systems in which such deviations from general relativity cannot be ignored.

3.

Galaxies have no dark matter halos.

These may be alternative to the dark matter paradigm.
For understanding gravity and matter in the universe, it is necessary to test the laws of gravity in the extremely weak regimes.
Galaxies are unique physical systems in which the effects of very weak gravity can be precisely observed. Investigating galaxies would help to test the law of gravity applicable to extremely weak regimes.

This work is the first quantitative test of Cotton gravity.
The first test suggests that Cotton gravity is a possible candidate as a generalization of general relativity. Further investigations would be expected.

A.1
Poisson equation

In the axially symmetric system—we assume that the rotating galaxies have the axial symmetry—, the Poisson equation (
15
) can be written in a cylindrical coordinate,

\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R^{2}}+\frac{1}{R}\frac{\partial}{\partial R}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}\right]\Phi(R,|z|)=4\pi G\rho(R,|z|),

(28)

where
\displaystyle R
is the radial distance and
\displaystyle z
is the height.

The mass density of baryons has two components,

\rho_{\rm bar}=\rho_{\rm disk}+\rho_{\rm gas}.

(29)

For galaxies with the central bulge,
\displaystyle\rho_{\rm bulge}
should be added (only 11 galaxies in this work).
We assume that the stellar disk and gas have a small but finite thickness—though, we find that the results are not sensitive to finite thickness. We assume that the bulge is spherical.

For the stellar disk, we adopt the following profile,

\rho_{\rm disk}(R,|z|)=\Upsilon_{\star}\Sigma_{\rm disk}(R)\frac{e^{-|z|/z_{\rm d}}}{2z_{\rm d}},

(30)

where
\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\star}
is the mass-to-light ratio (listed in column (6) of Table
1
),
\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\star}\Sigma_{\rm disk}(R)
is the surface density, and
\displaystyle z_{\rm d}
is the scale height.
We use
\displaystyle z_{\rm d}=0.196(R_{\rm d}/{\rm kpc})^{0.633}\ {\rm kpc}

Lelli et al. 2016
;
Bershady et al. 2010
, where
\displaystyle R_{\rm d}
is the scale length. Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle 4\pi G\rho_{\rm disk}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{137.9e^{-|z|/z_{\rm d}}}{(R_{\rm d}/{\rm kpc})^{0.633}}\frac{\Upsilon_{\star}}{M_{\odot}/L_{\odot}}\frac{\Sigma_{\rm disk}(R)}{L_{\odot}/{\rm pc^{2}}}\quad{\rm km^{2}\ s^{-2}\ kpc^{-2}}.

(31)

The values of the surface brightness
\displaystyle\Sigma_{\rm disk}(R)
and the scale length
\displaystyle R_{\rm d}
are available from SPARC
Lelli et al. 2016
.

For the gas, we adopt the following profile,

\rho_{\rm gas}(R,|z|)=1.33\Sigma_{\rm HI}(R)\frac{{\rm sech^{2}}\left(z/z_{\rm g}\right)}{2z_{\rm g}},
