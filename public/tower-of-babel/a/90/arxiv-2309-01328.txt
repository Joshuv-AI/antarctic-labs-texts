Restoration Guarantee of Image Inpainting via Low Rank Patch Matrix Completion
Jian-Feng Cai, Jae Kyu Choi, Jingyang Li, Guojian Yin

Abstract
In recent years, patch-based image restoration approaches have demonstrated superior performance compared to conventional variational methods. This paper delves into the mathematical foundations underlying patch-based image restoration methods, with a specific focus on establishing restoration guarantees for patch-based image inpainting, leveraging the assumption of self-similarity among patches. To accomplish this, we present a reformulation of the image inpainting problem as structured low-rank matrix completion, accomplished by grouping image patches with potential overlaps. By making certain incoherence assumptions, we establish a restoration guarantee, given that the number of samples exceeds the order of r ​ log 2 ​ ( N ) r\log^{2}(N) , where N × N N\times N denotes the size of the image and r > 0 r>0 represents the sum of ranks for each group of image patches. Through our rigorous mathematical analysis, we provide valuable insights into the theoretical foundations of patch-based image restoration methods, shedding light on their efficacy and offering guidelines for practical implementation.

Introduction

Image restoration is a widely studied inverse problem that aims to restore a high-quality image from a degraded version caused by various factors such as imaging, acquisition, and communication processes. Due to the ill-posed nature of linear inverse problems encountered in image restoration, it is essential to incorporate prior information about the target image to achieve a high-quality recovery. Consequently, before delving into image restoration tasks, it is natural and fundamental to address the question of identifying an appropriate model or descriptor for images.
Several well-established models have been widely used in image restoration, including variational approaches such as the Rudin-Osher-Fatemi model
[
47
]
, the inf-convolution model
[
19
]
, and the total generalized variation (TGV) model
[
4
,
5
]
. In addition to these variational approaches, applied harmonic analysis methods have also been introduced, such as curvelets
[
17
]
, Gabor frames
[
35
,
41
]
, shearlets
[
37
]
, complex tight framelets
[
31
]
, and wavelet frames
[
3
,
8
,
13
]
. The common underlying concept in these approaches is to seek a sparse approximation of images by employing a pre-designed linear transformation in conjunction with a sparsity-promoting regularization term. It is worth noting that the thresholding algorithms inherent in these sparse regularization techniques can be linked to nonlinear evolution partial differential equations (PDEs)
[
25
]
. As a result, these approaches aim to preserve the sharpness of image singularities while simultaneously regularizing smooth regions.

While the connections between variational approaches and applied harmonic analysis approaches have expanded the scope of mathematical modeling and numerical algorithms
[
11
,
23
,
26
]
, it is important to recognize that these approaches typically assume a target image to be a piecewise smooth function. Consequently, they excel in restoring images with prominent cartoon-like structures. However, natural images are incredibly diverse, with each image containing its unique textures, features, and, in addition, the cartoon-like components. These additional features cannot be adequately modeled or approximated as piecewise smooth functions.
In the literature, there are some variational approaches
[
43
,
51
]
and applied harmonic analysis approaches
[
13
,
48
]
that aim to incorporate texture modeling simultaneously. However, when we endeavor to model or regularize each layer of an image based on its distinct properties, these model-based approaches may fall short in capturing the true diversity of natural images.

Recently, a plethora of image restoration approaches have emerged, employing adaptive techniques on image patches either within the target image itself or across a database of images.
Existing denoising methods
[
6
,
24
,
27
,
30
]
exploited the self-similarity within patches from the image.
In particular, similar patches are assumed to lie on some low dimensional subspace
[
33
]
or low dimensional manifold
[
42
,
38
]
.
Another approach approximates patch distributions using parametric models, like the non-local Bayesian method assuming Gaussian patch distributions
[
39
]
or Gaussian mixture model
[
54
]
.
Interestingly, convolution neural networks (CNNs) can also be viewed as a patch-based method that mysteriously exploits the underlying patch structures. Despite of its complication in theoretical explanation, it has demonstrated remarkable success and widespread utility across various image processing tasks such as image classification
[
32
,
49
]
, segmentation
[
46
]
, and denoising
[
53
]
. However, a notable limitation of these deep learning-based approaches lies in their substantial data requirements.
To this end,
[
2
]
proposed a patch normalizing flow regularizer trained on patches from a limited number of clean images.
The success of CNNs, is attributed to the implicit regularization capabilities embedded within CNNs when dealing with image patches.
This is also the idea of deep image prior framework, as proposed by
[
50
]
, which asserts that the architecture of a generator network alone is sufficiently adept at capturing a substantial portion of low-level image statistics, even without training data.

These patch-based approaches have demonstrated significant improvements in restoration quality compared to conventional variational methods. The foundation of these approaches lies in the assumption of self-similar patches. Exploiting this self-similarity allows for the inference of information about degraded regions from remote regions, enabling the utilization of image structures more effectively during the restoration process. Despite the recent advancements in modeling and numerical algorithms, it is worth noting that there is a paucity of mathematical analysis concerning patch-based image restoration approaches in the current body of literature.

To fill this gap, we consider a simple model with the focus on image inpainting, which can be formulated as the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\text{find}~~{\boldsymbol{z}}~~\text{subject to}~~{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}+{\boldsymbol{b}},

(1.1)

where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
represents the set of indices of the image,
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Omega}
represents the set of indices where the observed image
{\boldsymbol{y}}
is available, and
{\boldsymbol{b}}
characterizes the noise. We assume that the indices in
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
are i.i.d. samples drawn from the uniform distribution over
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, denoting the cardinality of
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
as
m
. It is worth mentioning that we could also consider the scenario where
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
is drawn from all
m
-subsets of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
to achieve the same error bound without the possibility of collisions
[
28
]
. However, throughout this paper, we capitalize on the advantages of using i.i.d. samples drawn from
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. The task of inpainting
{\boldsymbol{z}}
on
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
from the observed image
{\boldsymbol{y}}
arises when certain pixels are randomly missing due to factors such as unreliable communication channels
[
21
]
, dust spots or cracks in film
[
34
]
, or corruption by salt-and-pepper noise
[
20
]
.

In many patch-based image restoration methods, image patches are grouped based on their similarity or linear correlation. Specifically, we define a linear operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}
, which maps an
N\times N
discrete image
{\boldsymbol{z}}
into an
n^{2}\times N_{p}
patch matrix
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
by concatenating its
n\times n
patches into column vectors. Here,
N_{p}
denotes the number of patches, which depends on the boundary conditions defining the image patches. Using two-dimensional multi-indices, we express this as:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\boldsymbol{z}})({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{l}})={\boldsymbol{z}}({\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{l}}),

(1.2)

where all indices start from
0
throughout this paper.
Assuming an underlying true image
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
, we consider grouping
n\times n
patches of
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
into
K

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{K}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, with possible overlaps based on their linear correlations. For each group
k=1,\cdots,K

{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}
can be well approximated by a low-rank matrix. Motivated by this, we propose the following nuclear norm minimization problem for patch-based image inpainting using the notation in (
1.1

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}~\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\boldsymbol{z}})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}\right\|_{\ast}~~~~\text{subject to}~~~{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}+{\boldsymbol{b}}.

(1.3)

In this paper, our goal is to demonstrate that the problem in
Eq.

enables perfect restoration (when
{\boldsymbol{b}}=\mathbf{0}
) or stable restoration (when
{\boldsymbol{b}}

r\log^{2}\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right|
, where
r=r_{1}+\cdots+r_{K}
, and

for
k=1,\cdots,K
.

1.1
Key observation and overview of related works

Our mathematical analysis is based on the observation that most existing patch based approaches can be viewed as various matrix factorizations of structured matrices with low dimensional properties. More precisely, developing theory of these patch-based approaches are equivalent to developing theory of low dimensional structured matrix restoration problems. The low dimensionality is often manifested as a low-rank property, which allows us to leverage extensive studies on low-rank matrix restoration in the literature
[
7
,
18
,
22
,
28
,
44
,
45
]
. In fact, our model has close relation with the following existing models.

\bullet

K-SVD
.
Consider the K-SVD method for image denoising
[
1
]
, which explicitly learns a dictionary from image patches, assuming sparse coefficients. Given a noisy image, the goal of K-SVD denoising is to restore a clear image
{\boldsymbol{z}}
that satisfies the factorization
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}\right)\approx{\boldsymbol{D}}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{T}
, where
{\boldsymbol{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}\times d}
is a dictionary with unit length, and
{\boldsymbol{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}\times d}
is a sparse coefficient matrix.

\bullet

Data-driven tight frame.

Another example is the data-driven tight frame
[
12
]
, which aims to construct adaptive discrete wavelet tight frame filter banks from a given clean or noisy image
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
. By relaxing the unitary extension principle and considering square orthogonal matrix constraints through filter concatenation, the data-driven tight frame aims for the factorization
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)\approx{\boldsymbol{D}}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{T}
, where
{\boldsymbol{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}\times n^{2}}
is an orthogonal matrix representing the tight frame filter banks, and
{\boldsymbol{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}\times n^{2}}
is a sparse matrix with each row corresponding to the sparse frame coefficients.

\bullet

Extension of superposition of complex sinusoids.

In various signal processing applications such as fluorescence microscopy, radar imaging, nuclear magnetic resonance spectrography, and others, the underlying signal can be expressed as a superposition of
r
complex sinusoids
[
14
,
15
]
. These signals satisfy a linear recurrence formula (LRF), which implies that each patch can be expressed as a causal linear combination of its precedent patches. Consequently, the resulting Hankel matrix satisfies
\text{rank}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)\right)\leq r
. Therefore, the singular value decomposition of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)
yields a data-driven tight frame factorization
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)={\boldsymbol{D}}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{T}
, where
{\boldsymbol{D}}
is an orthogonal matrix, and
{\boldsymbol{C}}
is a frame coefficient matrix with at most
r
nonzero row vectors
[
9
,
10
]
.

Indeed, many patch-based approaches employ grouping strategies to exploit the linear correlation among image patches. The works in
[
30
,
33
,
40
]
, for example, collect similar patches into a matrix and promote (weighted) nuclear norm minimization to restore a patch matrix, ensuring that each grouped submatrix is of low rank. Let
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{K}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Omega}
be
K
possibly overlapped groups, where
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}
can be well approximated by a matrix of rank
r_{k}\ll\min\left\{n^{2},|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}|\right\}
for
k=1,\cdots,K
. With this approximation, we have
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}\approx{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{k}^{T}
where
{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}\times r_{k}}
and
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}|\times r_{k}}
. This leads to the following factorization:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{1}}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{K}}\end{array}\right]\approx\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\boldsymbol{D}}_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&{\boldsymbol{D}}_{K}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{1}^{T}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&{\boldsymbol{C}}_{K}^{T}\end{array}\right]:={\boldsymbol{D}}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{T}.

This representation can be seen as a dictionary representation, with the sparsity pattern of
{\boldsymbol{C}}
determined by the grouping.

Additionally, some patch-based approaches assume that image patches can be embedded into a low-dimensional manifold
[
42
]
. In this case, neighboring patches, according to the induced metric on the manifold, can be well approximated by a tangent space of the manifold, which corresponds to a low-rank matrix. Therefore, similar to the previous case, the grouping argument can be applied to obtain the factorization
Section

1.1
.

2
Model and main results

In this paper, we consider the true image
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=[N]^{2}
represents the set of indices for the image pixels. However, only partial information of
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
is available for restoration. Let
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=\{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{k}}_{m}\}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Omega}
be the set of indices where the information of
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
is available. Typically, the image inpainting problem is formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\text{Find}~{\boldsymbol{z}}~\text{such that}~~{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\boldsymbol{z}})={\boldsymbol{y}},

(2.1)

where
{\boldsymbol{y}}
represents the partial measurements of
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
given by:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}),

(2.2)

and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\boldsymbol{z}}({\boldsymbol{k}}_{i}){\boldsymbol{e}}_{k_{i,1}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k_{i,2}}^{T}
. The goal is to recover the original image
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
from the available measurements
{\boldsymbol{y}}
.

Generally speaking, it is impossible to restore
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
from its partial entries
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)
as we can take arbitrary values on the missing region
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
without violating the equality constraint in
Eq.

2.1
. Throughout this paper, we will consider the following prior of
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
in the patch domain. Recall that
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)
is an
n^{2}\times N_{p}
two-fold Hankel matrix generated by concatenating all
n\times n
patches of
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
into column vectors. Let
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{K}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Omega}
be
K
possibly overlapped groups such that patches in each
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}
are linearly correlated. Assume for simplicity that
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{K}
are given and fixed with
|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}|=N_{p}
. Then each
n^{2}\times N_{p}
matrix
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}
is of low rank, say
\text{rank}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})\right)\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}=r_{k}<\min\left\{n^{2},N_{p}\right\}
. Hence, instead of directly restoring
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
directly, we can restore the
n^{2}\times N_{p}
two-fold Hankel matrix such that each submatrix
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}
is of low rank. This leads us to solve

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\text{rank}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}\right)\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}\right)~~\text{subject to}~~{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}\right)={\boldsymbol{y}}.

(2.3)

In practice, the partial measurement
{\boldsymbol{y}}
can contain noise, i.e.

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)+{\boldsymbol{b}}.

(2.4)

In this case, we solve

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\text{rank}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}\right)\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}\right)~~\text{subject to}~~\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}\right)-{\boldsymbol{y}}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{m}\delta,

(2.5)

where
\delta\geq m^{-1/2}\|{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{2}
characterizes the noise level.

The rank minimization problem is known to be NP-hard in general
[
18
]
. However, the nuclear norm minimization has been widely recognized as an effective approach for approximating low-rank matrices
[
45
]
. Therefore, we can consider solving the following alternative formulations to address the rank minimization problem.
For the noise-free case, where the partial measurement
{\boldsymbol{y}}
is equal to
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})
, we can solve the problem:

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\boldsymbol{z}})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}\right\|_{\ast}\quad\text{subject to}\quad{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\boldsymbol{z}})={\boldsymbol{y}}.

(2.6)

This formulation promotes low-rank submatrices within each group while satisfying the observed measurements constraint.

When noise is present in the partial measurement
{\boldsymbol{y}}
, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})+{\boldsymbol{b}}
, we can solve the following problem to account for the noise:

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\boldsymbol{z}})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}\right\|_{\ast}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\boldsymbol{z}})-{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{m}\delta,

(2.7)

where
\delta=\|{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{\infty}
. This formulation promotes low-rank submatrices while allowing for a bounded noise discrepancy between the observed measurements and the reconstructed image.
By solving either
Eq.

2.6
or
Eq.

2.7
, we aim to restore the two-fold Hankel matrix
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})
with low-rank submatrices within each group while respecting the observed measurements constraint.

The effectiveness of low rank priors in image inpainting has been demonstrated in several works, such as
[

,
33
,
40
]
, where significant improvements have been reported. These methods show that utilizing a low rank prior can effectively capture the underlying structure of an image and provide more reliable image representation. Furthermore, the theoretical aspects of low rank matrix completion have been extensively studied in the literature

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

Motivated by these theoretical studies, we provide a theoretical result that guarantees exact recovery of patch-based image inpainting when the set of observed indices
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{k}}_{m}\}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is randomly drawn from a uniform distribution over

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{U}}

\displaystyle=\textsf{bdiag}\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{U}}_{k}:k=1,\cdots,K\right\}\right),

(2.11)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\textsf{bdiag}\left(\left\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}:k=1,\cdots,K\right\}\right),

(2.12)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{V}}

\displaystyle=\textsf{bdiag}\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{V}}_{k}:k=1,\cdots,K\right\}\right).

Assumption 2.1

\displaystyle\max_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\max_{i\in\left[n^{2}\right]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{e}}_{k_{1}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k_{2}}^{T}\right)^{(i,:)}\right\|_{0}\leq 4,~~\text{and}~~\max_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\max_{j\in\left[N_{p}\right]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{e}}_{k_{1}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k_{2}}\right)^{(:,j)}\right\|_{0}\leq 4,

The

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{K}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Omega}

\displaystyle\max_{{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{l}}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{e}}_{k_{1}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k_{2}}^{T}\right)\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}\right\|_{0}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{e}}_{l_{1}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{l_{2}}^{T}\right)\big|_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}}\right\|_{0}}\leq M

for some absolute constant
0<M<+\infty
.

(d)

(Incoherence condition) There exists a constant
\nu>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{Kn^{2}}{r}\max_{i\in[Kn^{2}]}\|{\boldsymbol{U}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\nu~~\text{and}~~\frac{KN_{p}}{r}\max_{j\in[KN_{p}]}\|{\boldsymbol{V}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\nu

where
{\boldsymbol{U}}
and
{\boldsymbol{V}}
are left and right singular vectors of
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}
defined as
Eq.

2.11
and
Eq.

2.13
.

Remark 2.2
.

Before introducing our main results, we briefly discuss meanings of
2.1
, in particular the second to fourth assumptions.

\bullet

The constant
4
in Assumption
.(b)
is related to the boundary condition used to define the two-fold Hankel matrix
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})
. If we discard patches involving the boundary extension of
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
or impose the periodic boundary condition, the quantities in Assumption
.(b)
are bounded by
1
. On the other hand, if we impose the symmetric boundary condition, the quantities are bounded by
4
.

\bullet

Assumption
.(c)
states that the occurrence of each pixel in the matrix
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}
needs to be of the same order. This means that each pixel should appear a similar number of times in
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})
. If some pixels appear only a few times, we have very limited information about them, making it difficult to restore those pixels accurately.

\bullet

The incoherence assumption (Assumption
.(d)
) is a common assumption in completion tasks
[
52
]
. It ensures that the energy of the matrices
{\boldsymbol{U}}
and
{\boldsymbol{V}}
is evenly distributed along their rows. This implies that there is some information sampled by each operator, avoiding the pessimistic case when the energy is highly concentrated in few entries.

Under these assumptions, our main results are summarized as follows.

Theorem 2.3
.

Assume that
2.1
holds. Let
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\left[N\right]^{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{k}}_{m}\right\}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Omega}
be a data set whose entry is i.i.d. randomly drawn from the uniform distribution on
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and assume
m\leq N^{2}
. Assume that

\displaystyle c_{s}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{N^{2}},\frac{N_{p}}{N^{2}}\leq C_{s}

for some absolute constants
c_{s}>0
and
C_{s}>0
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on
c_{s}
such that if

\displaystyle m\geq CMK^{-1}\nu r\log^{2}(N),

1.

{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
is the unique solution to
Eq.

2.6
with probability at least
1-4|\boldsymbol{\Omega}|^{-4}
;

2.

\left\|{\boldsymbol{z}}-{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\widetilde{C}\left(MKN^{2}\max\{n^{2},N_{p}\}\right)^{1/2}\delta
with probability at least
1-4|\boldsymbol{\Omega}|^{-4}
for some absolute constant
\widetilde{C}>0
, where
{\boldsymbol{z}}
is a solution to
Eq.

2.7
with
\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}\right)-{\boldsymbol{y}}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{m}\delta
.

The proof is presented in Section
3
. Before concluding this section, we discuss the implications of Theorem
2.3
.

Firstly, if we assume that
M>0
in Assumption
.(c)
is an absolute constant, Theorem
2.3
asserts that under the incoherence condition, problem
Eq.

2.6
enables exact restoration with high probability as long as the number of samples
m
exceeds
O(r\log^{2}(N))
.
If
K=1
, then problems
Eq.

2.6
and
Eq.

2.7
reduce to Hankel matrix completion through nuclear norm minimization. In other words, our problem encompasses spectral compressed sensing as a special case
[
16
]
, indicating that the degrees of freedom are at least of order
O(r)
.
When assuming the number of groups
K=O(1)
, the parameters needed to represent
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})
is at least of the order
r
.
Thus, the number of samples matches the degrees of freedom up to logarithmic factors.

Furthermore, when the measurement
{\boldsymbol{y}}
is contaminated by an error of size
O(\delta)
on average, the distance between the original image and the solution to problem
Eq.

2.7
is bounded by the measurement error. In the lifted matrix, the entry-wise error is on average bounded by
O(\sqrt{\max\{n^{2},N_{p}\}}\delta)
, which also serves as the average entry-wise error bound for the restored image.

Lemma 3.5

\min_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}c_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\geq\frac{KN_{p}n^{2}}{MN^{2}}

\left\|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{z}}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}\leq CMKN^{2}\max\{n^{2},N_{p}\}\delta^{2}.

for some absolute constant
C>0
and we finish the proof of the theorem.

5
Conclusion and future works

In this paper, we provide theoretical guarantees for exact recovery of image models based on the patch based low rank prior, which has proven effective in various image restoration problems. Under mild incoherence conditions, we demonstrate that the image can be recovered accurately with only a few measurements. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to provide theoretical analysis of the patch-based method.

Moving forward, we would like to address several aspects in future research. Firstly, it remains unclear what is the approximation ability of the set
\mathbb{M}_{r}\cap\text{range}({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{G}}}})
approximate the true image.
Given a set of parameters much smaller than the image size (
N^{2}
), it is important to investigate the extent to which we can approximate a specific class of images.
This problem is closely related to variational autoencoders (VAE)
[
36
]
. However, VAE typically requires a large amount of training data for learning the generative model, whereas our focus is on a specific image.

Secondly, since our method restricts the lifted matrix to a specific subspace and ensures its low rank, it is worth considering subspace completion problems. Examples of such problems include matrix completion, where the subspace is the entire space, and the recovery of spectral sparse signals, which involves lifting to a Hankel matrix and considering the subspace of all Hankel matrices. A natural question arises: under what conditions on the sampling basis can we expect successful recovery of the object? While these examples have been extensively studied individually, their analysis heavily relies on the structure of the basis.

Thirdly, most existing patch-based methods treat patches as vectors, potentially leading to the loss of patch-specific information. It would be intriguing to explore low rank tensor completion problems instead. However, determining the appropriate rank model for our purpose remains a challenge. Due to the similarity between patches, we can expect the tensor to be well-approximated by a low rank tensor along the third direction. However, it is difficult to determine the rank along the other two directions. Therefore, it is essential to explore low-parameter models that best describe the underlying structure.

Lastly, the patch-based method can be viewed as a dictionary learning problem, aiming to find a dictionary
{\boldsymbol{D}}
and a sparse coefficient matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}
. In our case, the sparsity of
{\boldsymbol{C}}
is enforced by lifting all the matrices
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})\big|_{\Omega_{k}},k\in[K]
, to a block diagonal matrix. This implies that both
{\boldsymbol{C}}
and
{\boldsymbol{D}}
in this problem are block diagonal, with zero off-diagonal blocks, resulting in a sparse matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}
. However, there are other possible constraints that can be imposed on the dictionary and coefficients to establish different models. Exploring these alternative decompositions and constraints is an interesting avenue for future research.
