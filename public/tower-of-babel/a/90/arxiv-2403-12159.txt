Walks on tiled boards
László Németh

Abstract
Several articles deal with tilings with various shapes, and also a very frequent type of combinatorics is to examine the walks on graphs or on grids. We combine these two things and give the numbers of the shortest walks crossing the tiled ( 1 × n ) (1\times n) and ( 2 × n ) (2\times n) square grids by covering them with squares and dominoes. We describe these numbers not only recursively, but also as rational polynomial linear combinations of Fibonacci numbers. The final publication is available via journal Mathematica Slovaca . Key Words: Tiling, walk on square grids, self-avoiding walk, recurrence sequence, Fibonacci sequence. MSC code: Primary 05B45, 11B37; Secondary 05C38, 52C20, 11Y55.

Introduction

Hundreds, if not thousands, of articles can be found about tilings and walks on graphs, but to the best of our knowledge, there are not any paper that does both at the same time. In this article, we connect these two subfields of combinatorics as follows.

Let
t_{n}
be the number of the different tilings (
n
-tilings) with
(1\times 1)
-squares and
(1\times 2)
-dominoes (two squares with a common edge) of a
(2\times n)
-board or square grid. Hereafter, a square always means
(1\times 1)
-square and a domino means
(1\times 2)
-domino.
It is known, e.g., in
[
4

(1\times n)
-board is
t_{n}=F_{n+1}
, where
(F_{n})
denotes the Fibonacci sequence (defined by
F_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}
,
F_{0}=0
,

and A000045 in OEIS
[
18
]
). McQuistan and Lichtman
[
14
]
(generalizations in
[
7

(2\times n)

satisfies the third-order recurrence relation

(A030186 in OEIS
[

Some researchers generalized the tiling with colored shapes
[

[

]
examined the tilings with colored squares and dominoes on the hyperbolic
(2\times n)
-board, and they gave the fourth order linear homogeneous recurrence relation of
t_{n}
, where the coefficients satisfy recurrence sequences, as well.

A self-avoiding walk on a graph is a walk that never visits the same vertex more than once. In the literature, self-avoiding walks have been studied mostly on infinite graphs. A central problem in this area is to consider the number of distinct self-avoiding walks of a prescribed length on regular grids (see book of Madras and Slade
[
12
]
or articles by Benjamin
[
3

and Williams
[

However, self-avoiding walks have also been studied on finite graphs, such as square grids, i.e.,
[
5

]

[

or complete graphs by Slade
[

]
. Major et al.
[
13
]
examined the self-avoiding walks on square grids in a special way when they defined forbidden direction steps, and they gave the numbers of walks in case of some special grids.

In this article, we combine both of the above-mentioned combinatorial examinations. We consider square grids or boards and all the possible tilings with objects of them, and then we calculate the numbers of the shortest walks crossing the tiled grids.

Lemma 2.1

(Benjamin and Quinn
[

]
)
.

The number of

\binom{n-k}{k},\qquad(k=0,1,\ldots,\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor),

Thus, because

v_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n-k}{k}(n-k+1)
. Finally, using identity
\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n-k}{k}=F_{n+1}

[

, after the following calculations

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}\binom{n-k}{k}(n-k+1)=(n+1)\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}\binom{n-k}{k}-\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}k\binom{n-k}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n+1)F_{n+1}-\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}k\binom{n-k}{k}=(n+1)F_{n+1}-\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor(n-2)/2\rfloor}(k+1)\binom{n-(k+1)}{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n+1)F_{n+1}-\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor(n-2)/2\rfloor}(k+1)\frac{((n-2)-k+1)((n-2)-k)!}{(k+1)k!((n-2)-2k)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n+1)F_{n+1}-\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor(n-2)/2\rfloor}\binom{n-2-k}{k}(n-2-k+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n+1)F_{n+1}-v_{n-2},

our new relation is

v_{n}+v_{n-2}=(n+1)F_{n+1}.

(5)

Eliminating the Fibonacci numbers from (

Proof of Corollary
1
.

We find the recurrence relation of the sequence
v_{n}
in form

v_{n}=Av_{n-1}+Bv_{n-2}+Cv_{n-3}+Dv_{n-4},\quad n\geq 4,

(6)

where

, and

are constant real numbers. Now, we express

and

from recurrence relations (
1
) and its shifted version
(n-2)v_{n-2}=(n-1)v_{n-3}+nv_{n-4}
for
n\geq 4
, respectively, and substitute them into (

). We then have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{n+1}{n}v_{n-1}+\frac{n+2}{n}v_{n-2}&=&Av_{n-1}+Bv_{n-2}+Cv_{n-3}+D(\frac{n-2}{n}v_{n-2}-\frac{n-1}{n}v_{n-3}),\\

((1-A)n+1)v_{n-1}&=&((B+D-1)n-2(D+1))v_{n-2}+((C-D)n+D)v_{n-3},\\
((A-1)n-1)v_{n-1}&=&((1-B-D)n+2(D+1))v_{n-2}+((D-C)n-D)v_{n-3}.\end{array}

Since from (

(n-1)v_{n-1}=nv_{n-2}+(n+1)v_{n-3},

then from the equalities of the coefficients we obtain the system of linear sequences

\begin{array}[]{rcl}n-1&=&(A-1)n-1,\\
n&=&(1-B-D)n+2(D+1),\\
n+1&=&(D-C)n-D,\end{array}

where the solutions are

, and

.
Thus, for

For the proof of the second part of the corollary, first, we sum the equations (

Finally, the sum of twice of the first equation and the second one gives the statement. ∎
