Quantum thermodynamics of the Caldeira-Leggett model with non-equilibrium Gaussian reservoirs
Vasco Cavina, Massimiliano Esposito

Abstract
We introduce a non-equilibrium version of the Caldeira-Leggett model in which a quantum particle is strongly coupled to a set of engineered reservoirs. The reservoirs are composed by collections of squeezed and displaced thermal modes, in contrast to the standard case in which the modes are assumed to be at equilibrium. The model proves to be very versatile. Strongly displaced/squeezed reservoirs can be used to generate an effective time dependence in the system Hamiltonian and can be identified as sources of pure work. In the case of squeezing, the time dependence is stochastic and breaks the fluctuation-dissipation relation, this can be reconciled with the second law of thermodynamics by correctly accounting for the energy used to generate the initial non-equilibrium conditions. To go beyond the average description and compute the full heat statistics, we treat squeezing and displacement as generalized Hamiltonians on a modified Keldysh contour. As an application of this technique, we show the quantum-classical correspondence between the heat statistics in the non-equilibrium Caldeira-Leggett model and the statistics of a classical Langevin particle under the action of squeezed and displaced colored noises. Finally, we discuss thermodynamic symmetries of the heat generating function, proving a fluctuation theorem for the energy balance and showing that the conservation of energy at the trajectory level emerges in the classical limit.

2
The model

In the CL model we consider a system coupled with
N
thermal reservoirs labeled by
\nu=1,...N
and initialized in Gibbs states with Hamiltonians
\hat{H}_{\nu}
:

\hat{\rho}(0)=\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\bigotimes_{\nu=1}^{N}\frac{e^{-\beta_{\nu}\hat{H}_{\nu}}}{Z_{\nu}},

(1)

where
\beta_{\nu},Z_{\nu}
are the inverse temperature and the partition function of the reservoir
\nu
, respectively.
Each reservoir is a collection of quantum harmonic oscillators

\hat{H}_{\nu}=\sum_{k}\hat{H}_{\nu,k}=\sum_{k}\big[\frac{\hat{P}_{\nu,k}^{2}}{2m_{\nu,k}}+\frac{m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}\hat{X}_{\nu,k}^{2}}{2}\big],

(2)

where
m_{\nu,k},\omega_{\nu,k}
are the mass and frequency of the mode
k
of the reservoir
\nu
and the reservoir operators satisfy the canonical commutation relations
[\hat{X}_{\nu,k},\hat{P}_{\nu^{\prime},k^{\prime}}]=i\hbar\delta_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}
.
For ease of notation, we leave the limits of the sums over

k
in each reservoir unspecified. We emphasize, however, that we do not restrict ourselves to reservoirs with infinitely many modes, as often assumed for the Caldeira–Leggett model. Work reservoirs may for instance consist of a single mode, provided that such modes are strongly out of equilibrium, as we will discuss in Sec. III.
We assume a total Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{S}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{N}\hat{H}_{\nu}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{N}\chi_{\nu}(t)\hat{V}_{\nu},

(3)

where the coupling has to be linear in the reservoir variables
\hat{V}_{\nu}=\sum_{\nu,k}V_{\nu,k}(\hat{X})\otimes\hat{X}_{\nu,k}
where
\hat{X}
is the position operator in the system
S
and
\hat{H}_{S}
is its free Hamiltonian.
The scalar functions
\chi_{\nu}(t)
are the so-called
switching functions
and are used to couple/decouple the system to the reservoirs.

To go from the standard CL to the NECL, the reservoirs are initially moved out of equilibrium by applying a

and a

, respectively given by

\displaystyle\hat{S}(r_{\nu,k})=\exp\frac{1}{2}\big(r^{*}_{\nu,k}\hat{a}^{2}_{\nu,k}-r_{\nu,k}\hat{a}^{\dagger\,2}_{\nu,k}\big),

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\alpha_{\nu,k})=\exp\big(\alpha_{\nu,k}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\nu,k}-\alpha^{*}_{\nu,k}\hat{a}_{\nu,k}\big),

(5)

where
\hat{a}_{\nu,k}=\sqrt{\frac{m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}}{2\hbar}}(\hat{X}_{\nu,k}+i\frac{\hat{P}_{\nu,k}}{m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}})
and
\alpha_{\nu,k},r_{\nu,k}
are arbitrary complex numbers.
After applying both squeezing and displacement operators to all the environmental modes, the global initial state can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(0)=\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\bigotimes_{\nu,k}\hat{\rho}_{\nu,k}(0)

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\nu,k}(0)=\hat{D}^{{\dagger}}(\alpha_{\nu,k})\hat{S}^{{\dagger}}(r_{\nu,k})\frac{e^{-\beta_{\nu}\hat{H}_{\nu,k}}}{Z_{\nu,k}}\hat{S}(r_{\nu,k})\hat{D}(\alpha_{\nu,k})

where
\hat{\rho}_{\nu,k}(0)
represents the new initial state of the mode
k
in the reservoir
\nu
.
Although implicit in the notation, the displacement and squeezing operators with arguments
\alpha_{\nu,k},r_{\nu,k}
act on the Hilbert space of the reservoir mode identified by
\nu,k
.
Due to the arbitrariness of
\alpha_{\nu,k},\beta_{\nu}
and
r_{\nu,k}
, the equation above represents the most general initial state of a system coupled to independent Gaussian modes up to phase shifts
[
55
]
.

3
Average thermodynamics

We assume that the switching functions in Eq. (
3
) are set to zero outside the time interval
[0,\tau]
.
The total unitary evolution reads

\hat{U}(\tau,0)=\mathcal{T}\{e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\tau}\hat{H}(t)dt}\},

(10)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the time-ordering operator and
\hat{H}(t)
is given in Eq. (
3
).
As a result, the final state is

\hat{\rho}(\tau)=\hat{U}(\tau,0)\hat{\rho}(0)\hat{U}^{{\dagger}}(\tau,0)

(11)

where
\hat{\rho}(0)
is given by (
6
).
In this section, we will discuss the general properties of the energy and entropy flows between the system and its reservoirs during the unitary process (
10
).

3.1
First and second laws of thermodynamics

We build on the conventional formulation of the second law for open quantum systems

[
31
,
32
,
33
,
25
]
.
The main difference is that a quench, consisting in the squeeze and displacement operations in Eq. (
6
), is initially performed on the reservoirs.

As a preliminary, we define the average energy, entropy, and free energy in the system
X=S
(resp. reservoir
X=\nu
) as:

\displaystyle\langle E_{X}\rangle(\tau)=Tr[\hat{H}_{X}\hat{\rho}_{X}(\tau)],

\displaystyle S_{X}(\tau)=-Tr[\hat{\rho}_{X}(\tau)\ln\hat{\rho}_{X}(\tau)\big]],

(12)

\displaystyle F_{X}(\tau)=\langle E_{X}\rangle(\tau)-\beta^{-1}_{X}S_{X}(\tau),

where
\hat{\rho}_{X}
denotes the reduced density matrix. The changes in these quantities between time
0
(right after the quench) and time
\tau
are denoted respectively
\langle\Delta E_{X}\rangle
,
\Delta S_{X}
, and
\Delta F_{X}
. Here,
\hat{\rho}_{\nu}(0)
is the initial state of the reservoir
\nu
taken from Eq. (
6
).
The initial quench is unitary so it does not produce any change in the entropy of the reservoirs,
S_{\nu}(0)=S_{\nu}^{\rm{eq}}
, while their average energy changes by an amount

\langle W_{\nu}\rangle=Tr[\hat{H}_{\nu}\big[\hat{\rho}_{\nu}(0)-\frac{e^{-\beta_{\nu}\hat{H}_{\nu}}}{Z_{\nu}}\big]],

(13)

which can be thus seen as a source of work.
The variation in the average energy of reservoir
\nu
that occurs subsequently up to
\tau
is

\displaystyle\langle\Delta E_{\nu}\rangle=Tr[\hat{H}_{\nu}\big[\hat{\rho}_{\nu}(\tau)-\hat{\rho}_{\nu}(0)\big]]\;,

(14)

and is solely due to the interaction with the system.
Therefore, the overall variation in the average energy in the reservoir
\nu
is

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\langle Q_{\nu}\rangle=\langle W_{\nu}\rangle+\langle\Delta E_{\nu}\rangle=Tr[\hat{H}_{\nu}\big[\hat{\rho}_{\nu}(\tau)-\frac{e^{-\beta_{\nu}\hat{H}_{\nu}}}{Z_{\nu}}\big]].

(15)

We can derive a set of entropic inequalities for the reservoirs:

\displaystyle D_{\nu}

\displaystyle\equiv D\big(\hat{\rho}_{\nu}(\tau)||\frac{e^{-\beta_{\nu}\hat{H}_{\nu}}}{Z_{\nu}}\big)=\beta_{\nu}\langle Q_{\nu}\rangle-\Delta S_{\nu}

(16)

\displaystyle=\beta_{\nu}(F_{\nu}(\tau)-F_{\nu}^{\rm{eq}})=\beta_{\nu}(\langle W_{\nu}\rangle+\Delta F_{\nu})\geq 0,

where
F_{\nu}^{\rm{eq}}=-\beta_{\nu}^{-1}\ln Z_{\nu}
and
D
denotes the quantum relative entropy. From the last two equalities, we see that the overall change in free energy in a given reservoir consists of a gain initially supplied as work by the squeezing and displacement operations, followed by a change in free energy due to the subsequent interaction with the system.

We now give a closer look on what happens in the system.
The interaction between the system and each reservoir
\nu
is only turned on after the squeezing and displacement operations and the energy supplied as work for switching on and off such interaction reads

\displaystyle\langle W_{\nu}^{\chi}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\frac{d}{dt}[\chi_{\nu}(t)]\;Tr[\hat{V}_{\nu}\hat{\rho}(t)].

(17)

The sum of the contribution in Eq. (
17
) and the work supplied by the initial quench must be equal to the overall energy variation in the system and reservoirs. The following first law thus holds

\displaystyle\langle\Delta E_{S}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}\langle W_{\nu}^{\chi}\rangle+\sum_{\nu}\langle W_{\nu}\rangle-\sum_{\nu}\langle Q_{\nu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}\langle W_{\nu}^{\chi}\rangle-\sum_{\nu}\langle\Delta E_{\nu}\rangle.

(18)

Furthermore, a second law inequality can be derived by introducing the
entropy production
:

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle\equiv D\big(\hat{\rho}(\tau)||\hat{\rho}_{S}(\tau)\bigotimes_{\nu=1}^{N}\frac{e^{-\beta_{\nu}\hat{H}_{\nu}}}{Z_{\nu}}\big)

\displaystyle=\Delta S_{S}+\sum_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}\langle Q_{\nu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}[\sum_{\nu}\langle W_{\nu}^{\chi}\rangle+\sum_{\nu}\langle W_{\nu}\rangle-\Delta F_{S}^{1}]

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359pt-\sum_{\nu}(\beta_{1}-\beta_{\nu})\langle Q_{\nu}\rangle\geq 0,

(19)

where defining
\Delta F_{S}^{1}=\langle\Delta E_{S}\rangle-\beta_{1}^{-1}\Delta S_{S}
we singled out the contribution of the cold bath
\nu=1
and used (
18
) to obtain the last equality.
This reveals that besides the non-conservative forces resulting from thermal gradients, which vanish in isothermal situations,
\langle W_{\nu}^{\chi}\rangle
and
\Delta F_{\nu}
can now be used as resources to increase the free energy of the system.
We can further split
\Sigma
as
[
32
,
33
]

\Sigma=I+\sum_{\nu}D_{\nu}\;,

(20)

where
I
is the mutual information between the system and all the reservoirs established during the time evolution.
Using (
16
) and (
18
),
we find that
I
can be written as

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=D\big(\hat{\rho}(\tau)||\hat{\rho}_{S}(\tau)\bigotimes_{\nu=1}^{N}\hat{\rho}_{\nu}(\tau)\big)=\Delta S_{S}+\sum_{\nu}\Delta S_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}[\sum_{\nu}\langle W_{\nu}^{\chi}\rangle-\sum_{\nu}\Delta F_{\nu}-\Delta F_{S}^{1}]

\displaystyle\;\;\;-\sum_{\nu}(\beta_{1}-\beta_{\nu})\beta_{\nu}^{-1}\Delta S_{\nu}\geq 0.

(21)

Putting again non-isothermal effects aside, we observe that an increment of
I
goes at the expense of the free energy of the system (at parity of resources
\langle W_{\nu}^{\chi}\rangle,\,\Delta F_{\nu}
).
As we will see in Section
3.2
the quantities
I,\Sigma
can be used to discriminate deterministic from stochastic work reservoirs and understand how their performances are related.

3.2
Work reservoirs in the NECL

If we want a reservoir
\nu
to transfer a positive amount of energy to the system we need
\langle\Delta E_{\nu}\rangle\leq 0
.
In addition, to interpret this energy as work, it should not be associated with any outgoing entropy from
\nu
, i.e., we have to impose
\Delta S_{\nu}=0
.
Using these two restrictions in the bound (
16
) results in
|\langle\Delta E_{\nu}\rangle|\leq\langle W_{\nu}\rangle
, that is, the non-equilibrium nature of the reservoir is essential to pump a positive amount of energy in
S
.
Based on these premises, we define a
deterministic work reservoir
as one whose influence does not affect the entropy of the system. In other words, if this reservoir were the only one coupled to
S
we would have
\Delta S_{S}=0
and (from Eq. (
21
))
I=0
.

We naturally extend this concept by exploring a scenario where the mutual information
I=\Delta S_{S}
does not vanish.
This scenario, considering again the case in which the reservoir of interest
\nu
is the sole coupled to the system, is compatible with the requirement
\Delta S_{\nu}=0
made at the beginning of this section.
We call this case
stochastic work reservoir

since it represents a situation in which the reservoir does not change its entropy but its randomness leads to a stochastic transfer of energy to the system.
A comparison of the performances of deterministic and stochastic work reservoirs is offered by the second line of Eq. (
19
). The stochastic reservoirs allow to transfer more of the initial energetic resources
\langle W_{\nu}\rangle
,
\langle W^{\chi}_{\nu}\rangle
to the system, at the expense of increasing its entropy (such increment of entropy would decrease
\Delta F_{S}^{1}
and make room for a larger increment in
\langle\Delta E_{S}\rangle
).
In the following we will see that a strongly displaced reservoir behaves like a deterministic work reservoir, while a strongly squeezed one behaves like a stochastic work reservoir.

Deterministic work from displaced reservoirs -

For ease of calculation, let us assume that the system interacts with a single displaced mode. The results can be easily generalized to the case with many reservoir modes since the different modes are independent one from the other.
In the language of Eqs. (
7
), (
8
) and (
9
), we now ignore the coupling with the reservoirs 1,2,3 and consider the reservoir 4 with
V_{4,k}(\hat{X})=\delta_{1,k}V_{4,1}(\hat{X})
, that is, only the first mode of the reservoir
4
is put in contact with the system.
In this case the total Hamiltonian (
3
) is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{S}+\frac{1}{2}m_{4,1}\omega_{4,1}^{2}\hat{X}_{4,1}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2m_{4,1}}\hat{P}_{4,1}^{2}+\chi_{4}\hat{V}_{4,1}(\hat{X})\hat{X}_{4,1}

(22)

in which
\hat{X}
is the system position operator and
\hat{X}_{4,1},\hat{P}_{4,1}
are the single mode position and momentum operators, according to the notation introduced in Sec.
2
.
In Eq. (
22
) we also assumed
\chi_{4}(t)=\chi_{4}
for simplicity.
We look for a regime in which the fluctuations are negligible and the effect of the reservoir is only to induce a time-dependent deterministic modification of the system Hamiltonian.
This can be achieved by assuming that the typical length scale of environmental fluctuations is much smaller than the initial displacement of the reservoir position

L_{4,1}=-\sqrt{\frac{2\hbar}{m_{4,1}\omega_{4,1}}}\mathrm{Re}\alpha_{4,1}
.
The typical length scale of fluctuations is measured by the standard deviation of the observable
\hat{X}_{4,1}
in the initial state of reservoir
4
, so that we impose

|L_{4,1}|\gg\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\coth\big(\frac{\beta_{4}\hbar\omega_{4,1}}{2}\big)}{2m_{4,1}\omega_{4,1}}}.

(23)

We immediately see that the condition above can be realized by choosing
|L_{4,1}|\rightarrow\infty
and consequently renormalizing the coupling strength to ensure that the force acting on
S
remains finite, that is, working in the limit
\chi_{4}\rightarrow 0
, while keeping the product
\chi_{4}L_{4,1}
constant. Under these assumptions, we obtain the following equation of motion for the reduced density matrix of the system:

\dot{\hat{\rho}}_{S}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{S}+\chi_{4}L_{4,1}V_{4,1}(\hat{X})\cos\omega_{4,1}t,\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)].

(24)

Since the reduced dynamics of the system expressed by Eq. (
24
) is unitary, in this regime
\Delta S_{S}=0
.
In Appendix
B
we give more details on the derivation of Eqs. (
23
), (
24
) and we also prove that the increase in entropy of the reservoir is negligible, thus concluding that the entropic quantities introduced in Sec.
3.1
satisfy
\Delta S_{B}=I\approx 0
and coincide with the case of an ideal deterministic work reservoir. In the appendix the rate of energy flowing into the reservoir is also computed, and reads

\langle\dot{W}\rangle=\langle\dot{E}_{4,1}\rangle=Tr[\dot{\hat{H}}_{eff}(t)\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)],

(25)

where
\hat{H}_{eff}(t)
is the time dependent Hamiltonian in Eq. (
24
).
This coincides with the phenomenological equation typically used to describe the power in the context of driven heat engines
[
56
,
57
,
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
]
.

Squeezed reservoirs as stochastic work sources -

A similar approach can be used to determine the effect of the contact with a squeezed reservoir mode.
Starting again from Eqs. (
7
), (
8
) and (
9
), we assume that the only mode coupled with the system is the first mode of the reservoir with
\nu=3
, that is,
V_{3,k}(\hat{X})=\delta_{k,1}V_{3,1}(\hat{X})
.
In line with our previous discussion regarding the displaced case, we seek a regime where the fluctuations in the system’s dynamics caused by the initial squeezing are significantly greater than the thermal fluctuations, yet remain finite.
This scenario is realized again in the weak coupling limit, in which
\chi_{3}\rightarrow 0
and
r_{3,1}\rightarrow\infty
while keeping the product
\chi_{3}e^{r_{3,1}}
finite (see Appendix
C
).
Under these assumptions, the system evolves under an equation of the form

\dot{\hat{\rho}}^{(c)}_{S}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{S}+\xi\hat{\Gamma}(t),\hat{\rho}_{S}^{(c)}(t)],

(26)

where
\hat{\Gamma}(t)=\chi_{3}(t)V_{3,1}[\hat{X}(t)]\frac{e^{r_{3,1}}}{m_{3,1}\omega_{3,1}}\cos\omega_{3,1}t
and
\xi
is a Gaussian random variable such that
\langle\xi\rangle=0
and

\langle\xi^{2}\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2m_{3,1}\omega_{3,1}}\coth\big(\frac{\beta_{3}\hbar\omega_{3,1}}{2}\big)
.
Equation (
26
) is a particular case of a stochastic von Neumann equation
[
62
,
63
,
64
]
. Such equations hold under the fundamental assumptions that the reservoirs are Gaussian and linearly coupled to the system, and they generally give rise to rich non-Markovian features. In the specific case of Eq. (
26
), these features are absent due to the single-mode assumption and to the weak-coupling condition, which makes the back-action of the system on the mode negligible.
As is customary when the generator depends on a classical stochastic variable, the standard density matrix can be recovered by averaging over the possible realizations of the noise, such that
\langle\hat{\rho}_{S}^{(c)}(t)\rangle=\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)
, in analogy with stochastic Master Equations
[
65
,
66
,
67
]
. Stochastic Hamiltonians of the form (
26
) have also been used to generate squeezing and improve the thermalization speed of the system
[
68
,
69
]
.
In App.
C
we show that also in this case the entropy variation in the reservoir, multiplied by its temperature, is negligible compared to the energy one, so that this squeezed mode behaves like a stochastic work source.
From a physical point of view, this can be understood by noting that equation (
26
) could also be obtained by assuming that an external agent randomly picks a coupling constant
\xi
between a system and a driving Hamiltonian
\hat{\Gamma}(t)
, and then evolves the system.
The stochastic reservoir thus implements a form of effective stochastic driving on the system
[
70
]
.

3.3
Multimode work reservoirs

By combining the results of Section
3.2
we can obtain a broad class of effective system Hamiltonians.
We can also extend the results by considering many different modes in the reservoir 4. This allows us to engineer the phase of the cosine in Eq. (
24
)
so that we can perform the following replacement

\displaystyle L_{4,1}V_{4,1}(\hat{X})\cos\omega_{4,1}t\rightarrow

\displaystyle\rightarrow\sum_{k}A_{4,k}V_{4,k}(\hat{X})\cos{(\omega_{4,k}t+\phi_{k})},

where
A_{4,k}=\sqrt{2\hbar m_{4,k}^{-1}\omega_{4,k}^{-1}}|\alpha_{4,k}|
and
\phi_{k}=\arctan[Im(\alpha_{4,k})/Re(\alpha_{4,k})]
.
If we now assume that the functional dependence of
V_{4,k}
by
\hat{X}
is uniform over the modes, i.e.
V_{4,k}(\hat{X})=c_{4,k}V_{\nu}(\hat{X})
,
we can write the equation of motion for the density matrix of the system as

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}_{S}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{S}+f_{4}(t)V_{4}(\hat{X}),\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)],

(27)

where
f_{4}(t)=\chi_{4}\sum_{k}c_{4,k}A_{4,k}\cos[\omega_{4,k}t+\phi_{k}]
.
Eqs. (
27
) and (
25
) tell us that, in the regime considered, the reservoir is not only a work reservoir but it is also Markovian, i.e. the energy transferred to the system and its dynamics are described by equations that are local in time.
Similarly, we can depict the dynamics induced by equilibrium reservoirs using a time-local master equation, based on appropriate assumptions regarding weak coupling and timescale separation
[
71
,
72
,
8
,
73
,
74
,
75
,
76
]
.
Under such assumptions the dynamics of a particle coupled to the displaced reservoir with
\nu=4
and two equilibrium reservoirs with
\nu=1,2
can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}_{S}(t)=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}^{\prime}(t)+f_{4}(t)V_{4}(\hat{X}),\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)]

\displaystyle+\sum_{\nu=1,2}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(t)[\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)]

(28)

where
\hat{H}^{\prime}(t)
is the original system Hamiltonian plus a Lamb shift contribution induced by the reservoirs
[
8
]
and
\mathcal{D}_{\nu}
is the dissipator due to the action of the reservoir
\nu
on the system (see Fig.
2
). According to the approximation scheme used, the dissipators can depend on the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the driven Hamiltonian
[
75
,
77
,
78
]
.
Equations of the form (
28
) have been extensively used in quantum thermodynamics as a paradigmatic theoretical model to describe heat engines. Their suitability for this purpose has been thoroughly investigated, also starting from consistent microscopic descriptions
[
53
,
21
,
79
]
.
In the setting of Eq. (
28
), the work performed by, or extracted from, the system can be of two distinct types: thermoelectric work, arising from the presence of a bias (i.e., a chemical potential difference) between the two leads, and work generated by an explicit time dependence of the system Hamiltonian, which is more relevant from our perspective. Our approach provides a microscopic and self-consistent justification of the latter contribution [see Eq. (25)] and is closely connected to experimental implementations in which quantum systems—such as qubits—are directly driven by out-of-equilibrium photons, as in realizations of the Dicke or Tavis–Cummings models
[
80
]
.

The displaced case corresponds to a regime in which the cavity undergoes lasing. In this regime, coherent photons are generated and can be interpreted as a form of extractable work, closely related to recent proposals and experimental realizations of quantum batteries based on light–matter systems
[
81
,
82
]
. More generally, the possibility not only to drive a system but also to harvest the extracted work by storing energy in a harmonic “piston mode” has been thoroughly discussed in the literature on autonomous quantum engines
[
18
]
.
In the forthcoming sections, when studying the full energy statistics in the NECL, we will abstain from doing specific approximations, even though we confine our analysis to the case of Gaussian reservoirs.

4
Full energy statistics in the quantum regime

In this section we study the fluctuating thermodynamics of the NECL by computing the moment generating function (MGF) of the energy flows in the reservoirs.
The MGF can be expressed as a functional of the sole variables of the system if we manage to trace away the degrees of freedom of the reservoirs. A possible way to approach this problem is to write a path integral expression of the MGF using Keldysh techniques, as we will detail in the sections below.

4.1
Fluctuating thermodynamics

To go beyond the average description of the preceding section and study fluctuations, we apply the
two point energy measurement (TPEM)
technique
[
38
]
, in which the system and reservoirs Hamiltonians are measured at the initial and final times and the difference of the outcomes is used to quantify the variation of internal energy and heat.
The Hamiltonians of the system and reservoirs can be measured simultaneously since
[\hat{H}_{S},\hat{H}_{\nu}]=0
for all
\nu=1,...N
, this will produce a set of outcomes
\epsilon_{S}(\tau_{0}),\epsilon_{S}(\tau)
for the initial and final energies of the system and

\epsilon_{\nu}(\tau_{0}),\epsilon_{\nu}(\tau)
for the initial and final energies of the reservoirs. The results of the measurement can be gathered in a joint probability distribution
P[\epsilon_{S}(\tau)-\epsilon_{S}(\tau_{0}),\bm{\epsilon_{B}}(\tau)-\bm{\epsilon_{B}}(\tau_{0})]
, where
\bm{\epsilon_{B}}=(\epsilon_{1},...\epsilon_{N})
,
and used to build the MGF for the total energy balance

M(\lambda_{S},\bm{\lambda_{B}},\tau)=\sum_{\epsilon_{S},\bm{\epsilon_{B}}}P[\Delta_{S},\bm{\Delta_{B}}]e^{\lambda_{S}\Delta_{S}+\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{\Delta_{B}}},

(29)

where
\sum_{\epsilon_{S},\bm{\epsilon_{B}}}
is a shorthand notation for
\sum_{\epsilon_{S}(\tau_{0}),\epsilon_{S}(\tau)}\sum_{\bm{\epsilon_{B}}(\tau_{0}),\bm{\epsilon_{B}}(\tau)}
and
\Delta_{S}=\epsilon_{S}(\tau)-\epsilon_{S}(\tau_{0})
,
\bm{\Delta_{B}}=\bm{\epsilon_{B}}(\tau)-\bm{\epsilon_{B}}(\tau_{0})
(see also
[
53
]
).
If the initial Hamiltonian commutes with the initial state, the MGF assumes a rather simple form:

\displaystyle M(\lambda_{S},\bm{\lambda_{B}},\tau)

\displaystyle=Tr\big[\hat{U}_{\lambda_{S},\bm{\lambda_{B}}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\hat{\rho}(\tau_{0})

\displaystyle\times\hat{U}^{\dagger}_{\lambda_{S},\bm{\lambda_{B}}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\big]

(30)

in which
\hat{U}(\tau,\tau_{0})
is the evolution operator of the process and we introduced the tilted evolution operator as

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\lambda_{S},\bm{\lambda_{B}}}

\displaystyle=e^{(\lambda_{S}\hat{H}_{S}+\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{\hat{H}_{B}})/2}\hat{U}(\tau,\tau_{0})

\displaystyle\times e^{-(\lambda_{S}\hat{H}_{S}+\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{\hat{H}_{B}})/2}

(31)

where
\bm{\hat{H}_{B}}=(\hat{H}_{1},..,\hat{H}_{N})
is a vector containing the Hamiltonians of the reservoirs.
We now apply the TPEM to the setting described in Secs.
2
and
3.1
in which a system evolves after being coupled to non-equilibrium reservoirs.
First, we need to define at which times
\tau_{0},\tau
the measurements are performed.
The non-stationarity of the squeezed and displaced reservoirs is crucial if we want to observe any non-equilibrium reservoir-induced effect on the dynamics of the system.

Figure 3:
Schematization of the TPEM for the four reservoirs example introduced at the end of sec
2
.
The reservoirs, prepared at equilibrium, are measured at time
t=0^{-}
and collapse to eigenvectors of their respective Hamiltonians. The system energy is measured as well. After the collapse, at time
0
, some of the reservoirs, in this case the ones with
\nu=3,4
, are quenched out of equilibrium. At the same time, the coupling with the system is switched on. Subsequently, the time evolution of the system and reservoirs occurs up to time
\tau
, when the second energy measurement is performed.

Performing the projective measurement of
\hat{H}_{\nu}
after the squeeze and displacement operations would cause the reservoir
\nu
to collapse into an eigenstate of
\hat{H}_{\nu}
. As a result, the reservoir would become stationary and the coherent effects induced by initial displacement and squeezing would be lost.
To bypass the problem of the destruction of initial coherences, alternative approaches to evaluate the energy statistics that do not rely on TPEMs have been proposed
[
83
,
84
,
85
,
86
,
87
,
88
]
.
However, one can dodge the issue by assuming that the reservoirs are initially prepared at equilibrium and the
first measurement is performed
before
the squeezing and displacement operations.
In this way the TPEM scheme will fully capture the coherences produced by these initial operations.
From now on we assume that the first measurement happens at time
\tau_{0}=0^{-}
, when the modes of the reservoirs are still described by equilibrium states, while squeezing and displacement are performed immediately after, at time
0
.

This idea is schematized in Fig.
3
.
Under these assumptions the energy flows
\bm{\Delta_{B}}
represent the total energy variation in the reservoirs and
constitute a fluctuating version of the average heat flows
\langle Q_{\nu}\rangle
introduced in Section
3.1
.
We identify them as
fluctuating heat
, that is,
\bm{\Delta_{B}}=\bm{Q_{B}}
where
\bm{Q_{B}}=(Q_{1},...,Q_{N})
.
The energy variation of the system is the fluctuating version of the one discussed in Section
3.1
, that is,
\Delta_{S}=\Delta E_{S}
.

The MGF associated to the heat and system energy statistics in the TPEM above is equal to (
30
) with
\tau_{0}=0^{-}
and

\displaystyle\hat{U}(\tau,0^{-})

\displaystyle\equiv\hat{U}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{r}}(\tau,0^{-})

(32)

\displaystyle=\hat{U}(\tau,0)\prod_{\nu,k}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha_{\nu,k})\hat{S}^{\dagger}(r_{\nu,k}),

where we introduced
\bm{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{\nu,k}\}_{\nu,k}
and
\bm{r}=\{r_{\nu,k}\}_{\nu,k}
i.e. vectors containing all the displacement and squeezing parameters.
The equation above takes into account the fact that if we perform the first measurement at time
0^{-}
, the evolution between the initial and final measurement is a composition of the initial quench with the evolution between times
0
and
\tau
generated by (
3
).
Finally, we obtain

\displaystyle M(\lambda_{S},\bm{\lambda_{B}},\tau)=\frac{1}{Z}Tr[e^{\lambda_{S}\hat{H}_{S}+\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{\hat{H}_{B}}}\hat{U}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{r}}(\tau,0^{-})

\displaystyle\times e^{-\lambda_{S}\hat{H}_{S}-(\bm{\lambda_{B}}+\bm{\beta_{B}})\cdot\bm{\hat{H}_{B}}}\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\hat{U}^{\dagger}_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{r}}(\tau,0^{-})],

(33)

with
Z=\prod_{\nu}Z_{\nu}
.
Note that by computing the first derivatives, we can recover the average thermodynamic quantities introduced in Sec.
3.1
that is

\frac{\partial}{\partial{\lambda_{\nu}}}M=\langle Q_{\nu}\rangle
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial{\lambda_{S}}}M=\langle\Delta E_{S}\rangle
. The statistics of the fluctuating work can be obtained by setting
\lambda_{\nu}=\lambda_{S}=\lambda_{W}
for all values of
\nu
, that is, by computing
M(\lambda_{W},\lambda_{W}\bm{1},\tau)
where
\bm{1}=(1,....1)
(similarly to what happens for equilibrium reservoirs
[
53
]
).

4.2
Contour integrals and heat statistics

A convenient way to perform an exact elimination of the degrees of freedom of the reservoirs is to use a path integral formulation.
We start by computing the path integral representation of the amplitude associated to the process
(
10
)

\bra{x_{i}}\hat{U}(\tau,0)\ket{x_{f}}=\int_{x(0)=x_{i}}^{x(\tau)=x_{f}}\mathcal{D}x\mathcal{D}\bm{x_{B}}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}[\{x(t),\bm{x}_{B}(t)\}_{0}^{\tau}]}

(34)

in which
\bm{x_{B}}
is a vector of the coordinates of all the modes of the reservoirs and
\mathcal{D}x\mathcal{D}\bm{x_{B}}
is the measure of the path integral in the configuration space
[
89
]
. In the equation above we already integrated over the momentum variables to write the path integral in terms of the Lagrangian form of the action
[
90
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}\big\{\frac{m}{2}\dot{x}(t)^{2}-V[x(t)]

\displaystyle-\sum_{\nu,k}\chi_{\nu}(t)x_{\nu,k}(t)V_{\nu,k}[x(t)]

(35)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\nu,k}\big[\frac{m_{\nu,k}}{2}\dot{x}_{\nu,k}(t)^{2}-\frac{m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}}{2}x_{\nu,k}(t)^{2}\big]\big\}dt.

To grasp dissipative effects, we need to work in the density matrix formalism, and the amplitude (
34
) is not sufficient for such a task.
However, the path integral formalism can be extended to offer a comprehensive description of dissipative effects
[
1
,
2
,
91
,
92
]
.
Instrumental in this sense is the introduction of the
Keldysh contour

[
93
,
94
,
95
,
96
]
.
The idea behind the Keldysh contour is that,
in the same way a unitary evolution operator
\hat{U}(\tau,0)
can be written as a time-ordered product by introducing a time-ordering on the real axis (between
0
and
\tau
) the same can be done for two evolution operators acting jointly as in
\hat{U}(\tau,0)\hat{\rho}(0)\hat{U}^{{\dagger}}(\tau,0)
, provided that we define a new suitable ordering domain.
The solution is to duplicate the real axis in two branches, one for
\hat{U}(\tau,0)
and one for
\hat{U}^{{\dagger}}(\tau,0)
, respectively called the
forward
(
\gamma_{-}
) and
backward
(
\gamma_{+}
) branch, the Keldysh contour is the union of the two
[
97
,
98
]
.
After the time-ordered products are formulated in terms of the new contour, a path integral expression of the density matrix can be obtained with the usual textbook approach
[
6
]
.

We follow recent literature
[
11
,
12
,
41
]
to adapt the ideas mentioned above to the calculation of the MGF in a TPEM scheme. To make the procedure clear, we start by considering the MGF of the heat statistics (
33
) for a system coupled to a single equilibrium reservoir
\nu
:

\displaystyle M^{eq}_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\nu}}Tr[e^{\lambda_{\nu}\hat{H}_{\nu}}\hat{U}(\tau,0)\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)

\displaystyle\otimes e^{-(\beta_{\nu}+\lambda_{\nu})\hat{H}_{\nu}}\hat{U}^{{\dagger}}(\tau,0)],

(36)

in which we used the shortened notation
M_{\nu}\equiv M(0,0,...,\lambda_{\nu},...,0,\tau)
.
We can see Eq. (
36
) as a single ordered exponential of the form

\displaystyle M^{eq}_{\nu}=\frac{1}{Z_{\nu}}\mathop{\text{Tr}}\nolimits\left[\mathcal{T}_{\gamma^{\nu}}\big\{e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{\gamma^{\nu}}\hat{H}_{\gamma^{\nu}}(z)dz}\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\big\}\right],

(37)

where we introduced a new contour
\gamma^{\nu}
(represented in Fig.
4
) with its ordering operator
\mathcal{T}_{\gamma^{\nu}}
, and defined the contour Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\gamma^{\nu}}(z)=\begin{cases}\hat{H}(t)&\text{for }z=t\in\gamma_{+},\gamma_{-},\\
\hat{H}_{\nu}&\text{for }z\in\gamma^{\nu}_{\uparrow},\gamma^{\nu}_{\downarrow},\gamma^{\nu}_{M},\end{cases}

(38)

where
\gamma_{\uparrow}^{\nu},\gamma_{\downarrow}^{\nu},\gamma_{M}^{\nu}
are the branches of the new contour respectively associated to the initial and final measurements and to the initial state.
A step-by-step derivation of the equations above is contained in
[
41
]
.

Figure 4:
The contour of integration for the MGF in Eq. (
39
). The horizontal branches
\gamma_{-},\gamma_{+}
are already present in the standard Keldysh contour. In agreement with the ordering of the exponential operators in Eq. (
36
), in the new contour there are new vertical tracks
\gamma_{\uparrow}^{\nu}
,
\gamma_{\downarrow}^{\nu}
and
\gamma_{M}^{\nu}
respectively associated to the exponentials of
\lambda_{\nu}\hat{H}_{\nu}
,
-\lambda_{\nu}\hat{H}_{\nu}
and
-\beta_{\nu}\hat{H}_{\nu}
. The contour starts in
0
(at the beginning of the forward branch) and ends in
-i\hbar\beta_{\nu}
.

Using a Trotter decomposition of Eq. (
37
), the MGF for the energy flows in the reservoir
\nu
can be written as a path integral over the modified contour
\gamma^{\nu}
:

\displaystyle M_{\nu}=\frac{1}{Z_{\nu}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{D}x\mathcal{D}\bm{x_{B}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\big(\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}[\{x(z),\bm{x_{B}}(z)\}_{\gamma^{\nu}}]\big)\bra{x_{-}}\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\ket{x_{+}}

(39)

where
\mathcal{B}
is the boundary condition imposing that the system field is equal to
x_{-},x_{+}
at the beginning of the forward and backward branch respectively, while the reservoir fields satisfy periodic boundary conditions on
\gamma^{\nu}
.
The action reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\int_{\gamma_{\pm}}

\displaystyle\big\{\frac{m}{2}\dot{x}^{2}(z)-V[x(z)]\big\}dz+\sum_{k}\mathcal{S}_{\nu,k}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\nu,k}=\int_{\gamma^{\nu}}

\displaystyle\big\{\frac{m_{\nu,k}}{2}\dot{x}_{\nu,k}(z)^{2}-\frac{m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}}{2}x_{\nu,k}^{2}(z)

\displaystyle-\chi^{(\gamma)}_{\nu}(z)x_{\nu,k}(z)V_{\nu,k}[x(z)]\big\}dz,

(40)

and we extended the switching function
\chi_{\nu}
to a function with arguments on
\gamma^{\nu}

\displaystyle\chi^{(\gamma)}_{\nu}(z)=\begin{cases}\chi_{\nu}(t)&\text{for }z=t\in\gamma_{\pm},\\
0&\text{for }z\in\gamma^{\nu}_{M,\downarrow,\uparrow}.\end{cases}

(41)

Note that
\chi^{(\gamma)}_{\nu}(z)=0
on
\gamma^{\nu}_{\uparrow,\downarrow}
since when the measurements are performed the system and the reservoir are decoupled (see Section
4.1
).
In Eq. (
39
) we dropped the superscript
eq
because, as we will show in the next section, the equations (
39
) and (
40
) hold also out of equilibrium, provided that we suitably redefine the quantities
m_{\nu,k},\chi_{\nu}^{\gamma}
and
V_{\nu,k}
.

4.3
Out of equilibrium reservoirs in the contour

The formalism with the modified contour can be easily extended to the case in which the initial state of the reservoir is sent out of equilibrium by performing additional squeezing/displacement operations.
For ease of notation, we start by considering a single reservoir
\nu
composed of a single mode
k
(the generalization to many reservoirs and many modes is straightforward, since the different modes are independent).
Following the protocol described in Section
4.1
,
performing an initial displacement corresponds to using

\hat{U}_{\bm{\alpha},0}(\tau,0^{-})=\hat{U}(\tau,0)\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha_{\nu,k}),

(42)

inside equation (
36
).
We obtain

\displaystyle M_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\nu}}Tr[e^{\lambda_{\nu}\hat{H}_{\nu}}\hat{U}(\tau,0)\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)

\displaystyle\otimes e^{-(\beta_{\nu}+\lambda_{\nu})\hat{D}^{{\dagger}}(\alpha_{\nu,k})\hat{H}_{\nu}\hat{D}(\alpha_{\nu,k})}\hat{U}^{{\dagger}}(\tau,0)].

(43)

The effect of a displacement with real parameter reduces to a translation of the initial position of the harmonic mode
\hat{X}_{\nu,k}\rightarrow\hat{X}_{\nu,k}+L_{\nu,k}
where
L_{\nu,k}=-\sqrt{\frac{2\hbar}{m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}}}\alpha_{\nu,k}
.
We have

\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha_{\nu,k})\hat{H}_{\nu}\hat{D}(\alpha_{\nu,k})=\hat{H}_{\nu}-m_{\nu,k}\omega^{2}_{\nu,k}L_{\nu,k}\hat{X}_{\nu,k}+N_{\nu,k},

with
N_{\nu,k}=\frac{m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}L_{\nu}^{2}}{2}
.
Up to the constant
N_{\nu,k}
, this shift in the Hamiltonian corresponds to an equal shift in the action on the branches
\gamma^{\nu}_{\downarrow}
,
\gamma^{\nu}_{M}
and it can be absorbed in a redefinition of the switching function

\displaystyle\chi^{(\gamma)}_{\nu}(z)=\begin{cases}\chi_{\nu}(t)&\text{for }z=t\in\gamma_{\pm},\\
1&\text{for }z\in\gamma^{\nu}_{M,\downarrow,\uparrow},\end{cases}

(44)

and a modification of the potential

\displaystyle V^{(\gamma)}_{\nu,k}(z)=\begin{cases}V_{\nu,k}(t)&\text{for }z=t\in\gamma_{\pm},\\
-m_{\nu,k}\omega^{2}_{\nu,k}L_{\nu,k}&\text{for }z\in\gamma^{\nu}_{M},\gamma^{\nu}_{\downarrow},\\
0&\text{for }z\in\gamma^{\nu}_{\uparrow}.\end{cases}

(45)

A single squeezed reservoir mode can be treated in the same way, after substituting the operator
\hat{D}(\alpha_{\nu,k})
with the squeeze operators
\hat{S}(r_{\nu,k})
. Since for a real squeeze parameter
r_{\nu,k}
we have

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{{\dagger}}(r_{\nu,k})\hat{H}_{\nu,k}\hat{S}(r_{\nu,k})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2r_{\nu,k}}}{2m_{\nu,k}}\hat{P}^{2}_{\nu,k}

(46)

\displaystyle+\frac{e^{-2r_{\nu,k}}m_{\nu,k}\omega^{2}_{\nu,k}}{2}\hat{X}^{2}_{\nu,k},

the squeeze operator acts as a quench of the mass
m_{\nu,k}
operated at time equal to
0
. The masses appearing inside
\hat{U},\hat{U}^{{\dagger}}
remain untouched. This translates, in the language of the modified contour, into a dependence of the form

\displaystyle m_{\nu,k}(z)=\begin{cases}m_{\nu,k}&\text{for }z=t\in\gamma_{\pm},\gamma^{\nu}_{\uparrow},\\
e^{-2r_{\nu,k}}m_{\nu,k}&\text{for }z\in\gamma^{\nu}_{M},\gamma^{\nu}_{\downarrow}.\end{cases}

(47)

Note that, if squeezing and displacement are both present, the redefinition (
47
) affects also the mass appearing in Eq. (
45
) and in the constant
N_{\nu,k}
.
Equations (
44
), (
45
) and (
47
) allow us to encode the non-equilibrium properties of the initial state of the reservoir in suitable redefinitions of the physical quantities, without altering the quadratic structure of the action (
40
), hence, it will be possible to perform an exact elimination of the reservoirs degrees of freedom through standard Gaussian integration methods.

4.4
Effective system action and heat statistics

For equilibrium reservoirs the action is given by Eq. (
40
) which after an integration by parts reduces to the sum over
\nu,k
of the differential operators
x_{\nu,k}(z)(\partial_{z}^{2}+\omega_{\nu,k}^{2})x_{\nu,k}(z)
, which can be eliminated by a Gaussian integration of the reservoirs degrees of freedom in (
39
).
Using the Gaussian integral formula, the result is an exponential of the inverse of the differential operator
[
6
]
, i.e. the function
G_{\nu,k}
satisfying

(\partial_{z}^{2}+\omega_{\nu,k}^{2})G_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})=\omega_{\nu,k}\delta(z-z^{\prime}),

(48)

with
z,z^{\prime}\in\gamma^{\nu}
and periodic boundary conditions

G_{\nu,k}(0,z^{\prime})=G_{\nu,k}(-i\hbar\beta,z^{\prime})
,
G_{\nu,k}^{\prime}(0,z^{\prime})=G^{\prime}_{\nu,k}(-i\hbar\beta,z^{\prime})
.
This solution is the contour
Green’s function
(GF)
[
6
,
97
,
41
]

\displaystyle G_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2}\coth\big(\frac{\hbar\omega_{\nu,k}\beta_{\nu}}{2}\big)\cos\omega_{\nu,k}(z-z^{\prime})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\big[\Theta(z-z^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\big]\sin\omega_{\nu,k}(z-z^{\prime}),

(49)

where
\Theta(z-z^{\prime})
is the Heaviside function on the contour
\gamma^{\nu}
, valued
1
if
z^{\prime}
precedes
z
in
\gamma^{\nu}
in Fig.
4
and
0
otherwise.
In the case of a squeezed reservoir, the mass of the modes in the contour is not constant (see Eq. (
47
)) so that the equation of the GF has to be replaced with the one of an oscillator with a time-dependent mass:

[\partial_{z}\frac{m_{\nu,k}(z)}{m_{\nu,k}}\partial_{z}+\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}\frac{m_{\nu,k}(z)}{m_{\nu,k}}]\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})=\omega_{\nu,k}\delta(z-z^{\prime}),

(50)

where
m_{\nu,k}(z)
satisfies Eq. (
47
).
Summarizing, if there is no initial squeezing, the reduced action of the system after eliminating the degrees of freedom of all the reservoirs is (see also App.
D
)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}=\int_{\gamma_{\pm}}\big\{\frac{m}{2}\dot{x}^{2}(z)-V[x(z)]\big\}dz+\sum_{\nu,k}\mathcal{S}_{\nu,k}

(51)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\nu,k}=\iint_{\gamma_{\nu}}\frac{dzdz^{\prime}}{2m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}}G_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\chi^{(\gamma)}_{\nu}(z)\chi^{(\gamma)}_{\nu}(z^{\prime})V^{(\gamma)}_{\nu,k}[z]V^{(\gamma)}_{\nu,k}[z^{\prime}]

(52)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+i\hbar(\beta_{\nu}+\lambda_{\nu})N_{\nu,k},

where
\chi_{\nu}^{\gamma}(z)
is given by Eq. (
41
) for equilibrium reservoirs and by Eq. (
44
) for displaced ones and
N_{\nu,k}
is defined above Eq. (
44
).
In the presence of squeezing we replace the GF with the result of Eq. (
50
) obtaining

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\nu,k}=\iint_{\gamma_{\nu}}\frac{dzdz^{\prime}}{2m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}}\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\chi^{(\gamma)}_{\nu}(z)\chi^{(\gamma)}_{\nu}(z^{\prime})V^{(\gamma)}_{\nu,k}[z]V^{(\gamma)}_{\nu,k}[z^{\prime}]

(53)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+i\hbar(\beta_{\nu}+\lambda_{\nu})N^{\prime}_{\nu,k},

with
N^{\prime}_{\nu,k}=\frac{m_{\nu,k}e^{-2r_{\nu,k}}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}L_{\nu,k}}{2}
.
The dependence of (
52
) and (
53
) on the counting fields
\lambda_{\nu}
is not evident at first sight, but is "hidden" in the geometry of the contour
\gamma^{\nu}
.
This dependence becomes clear when selecting specific values of
z,z^{\prime}
, thus introducing the
components

[
99
,
97
]
of the Green’s function on the contour.
This can be done by adopting a function that selects the real or imaginary parts of
z
according to the branch of the contour,
\Psi^{j}(t+i\zeta)=t
for
j=-,+
and
\Psi^{j}(t+i\zeta)=\zeta
for
j=\uparrow,\downarrow,M
and defining

\displaystyle{\rm if}\;z\in\gamma^{\nu}_{i},\,z^{\prime}\in\gamma^{\nu}_{j}\quad\>

\displaystyle G_{\nu,k}^{i,j}(\Psi^{j}(z),\Psi^{j}(z^{\prime}))\equiv G_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime}).

(54)

Analogous definitions work for
\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}
and for any function with arguments on the contour e.g.
x_{\pm}(t)\equiv x(t+i\zeta)
for
t+i\zeta\in\gamma_{\pm}
.
With these definitions we immediately recover the lesser, greater, time-ordered, anti time-ordered and Matsubara components
[
97
,
6
]
of the Green’s function respectively by selecting
(i,j)=(-,+),(+,-),(-,-),(+,+),(M,M)
.
In the usual Keldysh contour, the greater and lesser components
are associated with emission and absorption of excitations, the same is true for the contour
\gamma^{\nu}
in which they bear an additional dependence by
\lambda_{\nu}
(see the Appendix
E
for a list of all the GF components).
To write Eq. (

) (or, analogously, Eq. (
52
)) in terms of the components (
54
) it is sufficient to split the integrals on the contour
\gamma^{\nu}
as a sum of integrals over
\gamma_{-},\gamma_{-},\gamma^{\nu}_{M},\gamma^{\nu}_{\downarrow}
.
After dividing the integral in components
and assuming that the coupling between the system and the modes of a given reservoir are functionally the same apart from a prefactor
v_{\nu,k}(t)=c_{\nu,k}v_{\nu}(t)
where
v_{\nu}(t)=\chi_{\nu}(t)V_{\nu}(t)

we have (see also App.
D
)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\nu,k}=\sum_{l,j=\pm}\iint_{0}^{\tau}\frac{c^{2}_{\nu,k}v^{l}_{\nu}(t)v_{\nu}^{j}(t^{\prime})}{2m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}}jl\mathcal{G}^{l,j}_{\nu,k}(t,t^{\prime})dtdt^{\prime}

\displaystyle+i\sum_{l=\pm}\int_{0}^{\tau}\,l\omega_{\nu,k}c_{\nu,k}v^{l}_{\nu}(t)e^{-2r_{\nu,k}}L_{\nu,k}

(55)

\displaystyle\times\big[\int_{0}^{-\hbar\beta}\mathcal{G}^{l,M}_{\nu,k}(t,\zeta)d\zeta+\int_{\hbar\lambda_{\nu}}^{0}\mathcal{G}^{l,\downarrow}_{\nu,k}(t,\zeta)d\zeta]dt+\mathcal{N}_{\nu,k},

where
\mathcal{N}_{\nu,k}
is equal to
i\hbar(\beta_{\nu}+\lambda_{\nu})N^{\prime}_{\nu,k}
plus the contribution of
\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{M,\downarrow}
,
\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{\downarrow,\downarrow}
,
\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{M,M}
and does not depend on the system fields (see Appendix
D
), and
v^{l}_{\nu}(t)
is a shorthand notation for
v_{\nu}[x_{l}(t)]
with
l=\pm
.
To obtain the statistics of the heat flows in the bath
\nu
we have to add the contributions of all the modes;
the sum over
k
is often replaced by an integral
over the
spectral density
of the reservoir
\nu
, defined as

\displaystyle J_{\nu}(\omega)\equiv\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{k}\frac{c_{\nu,k}^{2}}{m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}}\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{\nu,k}\right).

(56)

The spectral density at a specific frequency
\omega
measures how much the modes of a given reservoir are concentrated around
\omega
and how strong the coupling between these modes and the system is.
In an analogous way, we can introduce

K_{\nu}(\omega)\equiv\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{k}c_{\nu,k}L_{\nu,k}e^{-2r_{\nu,k}}\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{\nu,k}\right).

(57)

The function
K_{\nu}(\omega)
measures how the magnitude of the force induced by the displacement of the reservoir modes is distributed over their spectrum.
We can now sum over
k
in Eq. (
55
) and use the definitions (
56
), (
57
) to obtain
\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\nu}\equiv\sum_{k}\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\nu,k}
, that reads

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\nu}=\sum_{l,j=\pm}\int d\omega\iint_{0}^{\tau}\frac{J_{\nu}(\omega)}{\pi}v_{\nu}^{l}v_{\nu}^{j}jl\mathcal{G}^{l,j}_{\nu}(\omega)dtdt^{\prime}

\displaystyle+i\sum_{l=\pm}\int d\omega\>\frac{2K_{\nu}(\omega)\omega}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\tau}\,l\big[\int_{0}^{-\hbar\beta_{\nu}}\mathcal{G}^{l,M}_{\nu,k}(\omega)d\zeta

\displaystyle+\int_{\hbar\lambda_{\nu}}^{0}\mathcal{G}^{l,\downarrow}_{\nu}(\omega)d\zeta]v_{\nu}^{l}dt+\mathcal{N}_{\nu},

(58)

where
\mathcal{N}_{\nu}=\sum_{k}\mathcal{N}_{\nu,k}
and we did not write the time dependences for ease of notation (they are the same as in Eq. (
55
)).
The equation above is the effective system action appearing in the path integral expression for the calculation of the heat flows.
If
\lambda_{\nu}=0
and with no squeezing and displacement, the action reduces to the standard action of the CL model (see App.
J.1
) and the contour reduces to the standard Keldysh contour augmented with the Matsubara branch for initial states (see Fig.

A). The other panels of Fig.

contain a recap of the effects of initial displacement and squeezing of the bath
\nu
on the effective action of the system.

5
Fluctuating thermodynamics in the Zwanzig model and classical limit of the quantum action

In this chapter we obtain the MGF of the heat flows in the classical analog of the NECL and show the quantum/classical correspondence at the level of generating functions. We compute all the relevant quantities in the generic framework in which both squeezing and displacement are present, but show the full quantum/classical correspondence only in the displaced case.

5.1
Thermodynamics in the classical regime

We now turn to the classical counterpart of the quantum NECL.
Our intention is to later recover it as the classical limit
\hbar\rightarrow 0
of the NECL.

The classical analog of the quenches in Fig.
3
are the classical squeezing and displacement operations, i.e. a quench of the masses
m_{\nu,k}
and a change of the center of the average initial harmonic potential.
The analog of the Hamiltonian (
3
) is the Zwanzig
[
45
]
model

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{p(t)^{2}}{2m}+V[x(t)]+\sum_{\nu,k}\chi_{\nu}(t)x_{\nu,k}(t)V_{\nu,k}[x(t)]

\displaystyle+\sum_{\nu,k}\big[\frac{p_{\nu,k}(t)^{2}}{2m_{\nu,k}}+\frac{m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}x_{\nu,k}(t)^{2}}{2}\big],

(59)

where
x(t),p(t),x_{\nu,k}(t),p_{\nu,k}(t)
are position and momentum variables of the system and of all the modes of the reservoirs. From (
59
) we derive the force exerted by all reservoir modes on the system
F^{(S)}_{\nu,k}[x,\bm{x_{B}}]
and the force exerted by the system on each reservoir mode
F_{\nu,k}[x,\bm{x_{B}}]
.
The energy variation in the system is defined as the difference between the final and initial energies i.e. the integral of the associated power

\displaystyle\Delta E_{S}(\tau_{0},\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\frac{d}{dt}H_{S}(t)dt

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt=\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\big\{\frac{p(t)\dot{p}(t)}{m}+V^{\prime}[x(t)\big\}\dot{x}(t)\big]dt

(60)

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt=\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\big\{\frac{p(t)(-V^{\prime}+\sum_{\nu,k}F^{(S)}_{\nu,k})}{m}+V^{\prime}[x(t)\big\}\dot{x}(t)\big]dt.

The terms containing
V^{\prime}
cancel out, leaving the energy variation of the system and each reservoir respectively equal to

\displaystyle\Delta E_{S}(\tau_{0},\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu,k}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}F^{(S)}_{\nu,k}[\bar{x}(t),\bm{\bar{x}_{B}}(t)]\dot{x}(t)dt,

\displaystyle\Delta E_{\nu}(\tau_{0},\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}F_{\nu,k}[\bar{x}(t),\bm{\bar{x}_{B}}(t)]\dot{\bar{x}}_{\nu,k}(t)dt,

(61)

where the latter can be obtained by following analogous steps to those in Eq. (
60
), the trajectories
\bar{x}(t)
,
\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(t)
satisfy the Hamilton equations derived from (
59
) and

\displaystyle F^{(S)}_{\nu,k}[\bar{x}(t),\bm{\bar{x}_{B}}(t)]

\displaystyle=-\chi_{\nu}(t)V^{\prime}_{\nu,k}[\bar{x}(t)]\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(t),

(62)

\displaystyle F_{\nu,k}[\bar{x}(t)]

\displaystyle=-\chi_{\nu}(t)V_{\nu,k}[\bar{x}(t)].

(63)

Eq. (
61
) assumes that
\tau_{0}
is greater of equal to
0
, so that the energy variation in the reservoirs contains only the contribution due to the contact with the system.
As in Sec.
3
, we distinguish this energy variation from the total heat
Q_{\nu}
that can be written as the sum of
\Delta E_{\nu}(0,\tau)
and the stochastic energy initially pumped in the reservoirs by the initial quench.
The contributions of the squeezing and displacement are respectively given by

\displaystyle\Delta E^{0,sq}_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\big[\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-2r_{\nu,k}})m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2m_{\nu,k}}(1-e^{+2r_{\nu,k}})\bar{p}_{\nu,k}^{2}(0)\big],

(64)

\displaystyle\Delta E_{0,\nu}^{dp}

\displaystyle=e^{-2r_{\nu,k}}m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}

\displaystyle\times\big[\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0)L_{\nu,k}-\frac{1}{2}L_{\nu,k}^{2}\big],

(65)

that are expressed in terms of the values
\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0),\bar{p}_{\nu,k}(0)
of the position and momentum after the quench, as we show in App.
F
.
We introduce two generating functions, one for
\Delta E_{\nu}
and one for
Q_{\nu}=\Delta E_{\nu}+\Delta E_{\nu}^{0,sq}+\Delta E_{\nu}^{0,dp}
. In the first case, we have

M^{ex}_{cl}(\lambda_{S},\bm{\lambda_{B}},\tau)=\langle e^{\lambda_{S}\Delta E_{S}(0,\tau)+\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{\Delta E_{\nu}}(0,\tau)}\rangle,

(66)

where
\langle...\rangle
is the average over all the initial preparations of the system and the reservoirs, and
{\bf\Delta E_{B}}=(\{\Delta E_{\nu}\}_{\nu=1}^{N})
.
Instead, the classical analog of the moment generating function (
33
) is given by

M^{tot}_{cl}(\lambda_{S},\bm{\lambda_{B}},\tau)=\langle e^{\lambda_{S}\Delta E_{S}(0,\tau)+\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{Q}}\rangle.

(67)

Note that in the TPEM for the quantum case, there is no analog of the MGF (
66
), since every measurement performed after the quenches inevitably destroys the initial coherences and affects the subsequent dynamics.

5.2
Stochastic dynamics for system variables

Our goal in the next two sections is to express Eqs. (
66
) and (
67
) in a path integral form and compare the associated actions with Eq. (
58
).
We start by showing that both the dynamics generated by (
59
) and the thermodynamic quantities (
61
) can be expressed in terms of noise variables associated to the reservoirs.
The solution of the Hamilton equations generated by (
59
) is
\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(t)=y_{\nu,k}(t)+w_{\nu,k}(t)
where

\displaystyle y_{\nu,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\big[\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0)\cos\omega_{\nu,k}t+\frac{\bar{p}_{\nu,k}(0)}{m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}}\sin\omega_{\nu,k}t\big],

\displaystyle w_{\nu,k}(t)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\frac{\chi_{\nu}(s)c_{\nu,k}}{m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}}V_{\nu}[x(s)]\sin{\omega_{\nu,k}(t-s)}.

(68)

In the equation above, we assumed that the coupling between the system and each mode of a specified reservoir has the same form for all the modes up to a constant, i.e.
V_{\nu,k}(x)=c_{\nu,k}V_{\nu}(x)
.
The external force acting on the system can be divided into a fluctuating and a deterministic contribution, obtaining the following generalized Langevin equation:

m\ddot{x}=-V^{\prime}(x)-\sum_{\nu}\big[\chi_{\nu}V^{\prime}_{\nu}(x)\xi_{\nu}+\chi_{\nu}V^{\prime}_{\nu}(x)R_{\nu}\big]

(69)

where
\xi_{\nu}=\sum_{k}c_{\nu,k}y_{\nu,k}
,
R_{\nu}=\sum_{k}c_{\nu,k}w_{\nu,k}
. A step by step derivation of (
69
) is given in App.
G
.
For reservoirs that are initially at equilibrium, it is possible to show that
\langle\xi_{\nu}\rangle=0
and by defining
\langle\xi_{\nu}(t)\xi_{\nu^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle\equiv C^{(2)}_{\nu}(t-t^{\prime})
and
R_{\nu}(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{\nu}(s)C_{\nu}^{(1)}(t-s)V_{\nu}[x(s)]
we have

\displaystyle C^{(2)}_{\nu}(t-t^{\prime})=\int\frac{2J_{\nu}(\omega)\cos\omega(t-t^{\prime})}{\pi\beta_{\nu}\omega}d\omega,

(70)

\displaystyle C^{(1)}_{\nu}(t-t^{\prime})=\frac{2}{\pi}\int J_{\nu}(\omega)\sin\omega(t-t^{\prime})d\omega,

(71)

where we introduced the spectral density of the reservoir
\nu
,
J_{\nu}(\omega)
.
The variation in system energy and the energy flow in the reservoir
\nu
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Delta E_{S}(0,\tau)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\tau}\chi_{\nu}(t)V^{\prime}_{\nu}[x(t)]\dot{x}

\displaystyle\times(\xi_{\nu}(t)+R_{\nu}(t))dt,

(72)

\displaystyle\Delta E_{\nu}(0,\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{d}{dt}\{\chi_{\nu}(t)V_{\nu}[x(t)]\}

\displaystyle\times(\xi_{\nu}(t)+R_{\nu}(t))dt.

(73)

We highlight that there is a term proportional to
\dot{\chi}_{\nu}
in both equations (
73
) and (
69
), in the latter, this can be explicitly obtained by integrating by parts in
s
in the definition of
R_{\nu}(t)
.
This term is the so-called
Sekimoto force

[
100
,
101
]
. It disappears when the coupling is not driven.
If we allow for the possibility of an initial displacement
L_{\nu,k}
in the reservoir modes, we have
\langle\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0)\rangle_{d}=L_{\nu,k}
which in turn gives

\langle y_{\nu,k}(t)\rangle_{d}=L_{\nu,k}\cos\omega_{\nu,k}t
, where the subscript
d
is to make explicit that we are averaging on a displaced mode,
thus the random force in Eq.
(
69
) satisfies

\langle\xi_{\nu}(t)\rangle_{d}\equiv-F_{\nu,d}(t)=\frac{2}{\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}K_{\nu}(\omega)\cos\omega td\omega,

(74)

with
K_{\nu}(\omega)
given by Eq. (
57
) (with
r_{\nu,k}=0
).
In the case of initial quench of the reservoir masses, the structure of the correlation function (
70
) changes, becoming

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\nu}^{(2)}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\beta_{\nu}\omega}\big[\mathcal{J}_{\nu}(\omega)\cos\omega(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\Delta\mathcal{J}_{\nu}(\omega)\cos\omega t\cos\omega t^{\prime}\big]d\omega

(75)

where
\mathcal{J}_{\nu}
and
\Delta\mathcal{J}_{\nu}
are two squeezed versions of the spectral density, that respectively reduce to
J_{\nu}
and
0
in the absence of squeezing (see App.
G.1
).
It is not surprising that using squeezed reservoirs can lead to a violation of the second law since the correlation function (
75
) violates the fluctuation/dissipation theorem. We will see how this relates with the second law and the fluctuation theorem in Sec.
6
.

5.3
MSRJD path integral and thermodynamics

As demonstrated in the preceding section, the average on the initial conditions in Eq. (
66

72
) and (
73
) over the noise variables.
Using the MSRJD approach
[
46

47

For any stochastic functional
f[\{x(t),\bm{\xi_{B}}(t)\}^{\tau}_{t=0}]

Denoting with

\displaystyle\langle f[...]\rangle

Due to the specific form of the functional inside Eq. (

(78)

\mathcal{S}^{(cl)}
for ease of notation and
\Delta E_{S}
and
\Delta E_{\nu}
are given by Eqs. (

We conclude by performing the average in Eq. (

In a completely general setting, in which the average of the noise can be different from

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S}_{lin}+\mathcal{S}_{quad}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}\eta_{x}(t)\big[m\ddot{x}+V^{\prime}(x)+\sum_{\nu}\chi_{\nu}V_{\nu}^{\prime}(x)R^{\prime}_{\nu}\big]dt

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\tau}\sum_{\nu}\big\{\big[i\lambda_{\nu}\frac{d}{dt}(\chi_{\nu}V_{\nu})-i\lambda_{S}\chi_{\nu}V_{\nu}^{\prime}(x)\dot{x}\big]R^{\prime}_{\nu}\big\}dt

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

{}_{quad}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{\nu}\iint_{0}^{\tau}\Xi_{\nu}(t)\Xi_{\nu}(t^{\prime})\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{\nu,s}(t,t^{\prime})dtdt^{\prime},

(79)

in which, for ease of notation, we suppressed the time arguments (when there is no ambiguity) and introduced the auxiliary quantities

\Xi=\chi_{\nu}V_{\nu}^{\prime}[x][\eta_{x}+i\lambda_{S}\dot{x}]-i\lambda_{\nu}\frac{d}{dt}[\chi_{\nu}V_{\nu}(x)],

(80)

and the total average force induced by the contact with the reservoir
R^{\prime}_{\nu}=(R_{\nu}-F_{\nu,d})
.
We can do the same calculations in the case of the MGF (

\Delta E_{\nu}
with
Q_{\nu}
. To compute the MGF (

\Delta E^{0}_{\nu}=\Delta E^{0,sq}_{\nu}+\Delta E^{0,dp}_{\nu}

\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{S}^{(cl)}-i\lambda_{S}\Delta E_{S}(0,\tau)-i\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{Q}.

(81)

The explicit calculation (limited to the case without squeezing) is done in Appendix

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{S}_{lin}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}\eta_{x}(t)\big[\sum_{\nu}\frac{\lambda_{\nu}}{\beta_{\nu}}\chi_{\nu}V_{\nu}^{\prime}(x)F_{\nu,d}\big]dt

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\tau}\big\{\sum_{\nu}\frac{\lambda_{\nu}}{\beta_{\nu}}\big[i\lambda_{\nu}\frac{d}{dt}(\chi_{\nu}V_{\nu})

\displaystyle-i\lambda_{S}\chi_{\nu}V_{\nu}^{\prime}(x)\dot{x}\big]F_{\nu,d}\big\}dt-i\sum_{\nu}\mathcal{M}_{\nu},

with

5.4
Classical limit of the quantum action

The two path integrals in (
51
) and (
79
) are formally different, indeed

1.

The action (
79
) depends on 2 fields, the physical field
x(t)
and the auxiliary field
\eta_{x}(t)
, while the quantum action depends on a single field
x(z)
.

2.

The domain of integration of the classical action is
[0,\tau]
, while every component of the quantum action containing
\nu
lives on a modified contour, like the one in Fig.
1
.

For the standard dissipative action problem, based on the Keldysh contour, there is a way to transform the quantum action that solves both the problems mentioned above, that consists in introducing the components and performing the
Keldysh rotation

[
6
,
102
,
103
]
.
First, after introducing the components as in (
54
), the double integral on the contour in Eq. (
51
) reduces to a set of integrals on
\gamma^{\nu}_{\uparrow,\downarrow,M}
and
\gamma_{\pm}
(see Eq. (
58
)). The integrals on
\gamma_{\pm}
can be transformed into integrals between
0
and
\tau
since
\int_{\gamma_{+}}dt=-\int_{\gamma_{-}}dt=-\int_{0}^{\tau}dt
.
In this way, we go from a description based on a single field in a “doubled" contour (
\gamma_{+}\oplus\gamma_{-}
) to a two-field description on the real segment
[0,\tau]
.
The Keldysh rotation is now a redefinition of the fields that ensure the convergence between the quantum and classical dynamical actions for
\bm{\lambda_{B}}=0
,
\hbar\rightarrow 0
.
This transformation is

x_{-}(t)=x_{cl}(t)+\frac{x_{q}(t)}{2},\quad x_{+}(t)=x_{cl}(t)-\frac{x_{q}(t)}{2}.

(83)

The "quantum field"
x_{q}
measures the asymmetry between the forward and backward quantum trajectories,
as such, is a purely quantum term and it is customary to assume that it is small in the classical limit, with a scaling linear in
\hbar

[
103
,
102
,
6
]
.
The same rotation can be applied to other quantities defined on the contour, for instance the interaction potential

v_{\nu}^{-}(t)=v_{\nu}^{cl}(t)+\frac{v_{\nu}^{q}(t)}{2},\quad v_{\nu}^{+}(t)=v_{\nu}^{cl}(t)-\frac{v_{\nu}^{q}(t)}{2}.

(84)

The linear transformations (
83
) and (
84
) make it convenient to introduce the Keldysh components of the contour Green’s function, defined as (see also
[
6
]
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{cl,cl}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{-,-}+\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{+,+}-\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{+,-}-\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{-,+},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{cl,q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big[\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{-,-}-\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{+,+}-\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{+,-}+\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{-,+}\big],

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{q,cl}

7
Conclusions

We studied the thermodynamics of the NECL, a non-equilibrium version of the CL model in which a particle is coupled to squeezed and displaced reservoirs.
This framework can be used to give a self-consistent description of work exchanges in driven thermal engines, by representing the external driving as a collection of displaced sources.
Our approach provides a microscopic framework to quantify both the energetic cost of such driving protocols and the associated fluctuations, going beyond phenomenological descriptions.
We proved that, to conciliate the non-equilibrium nature of the reservoirs with the fluctuation theorem, the energy that is used to squeeze and displace the reservoirs has to be explicitly taken into account: the sole energy flows between the system and the reservoirs do not lead to a statistics satisfying the FT, and this is strictly connected with the violation of the fluctuation-dissipation relation for squeezed reservoirs.
To compute the full energy statistics, we devised a new way to represent the energetic corrections due to squeezing and displacement by using generalized Hamiltonians on modified Keldysh contours.
This approach allows us to bridge the gap between the two-point measurement framework and the common techniques
used in stochastic thermodynamics for classical systems, where
the energy flows are defined at the trajectory level.
Due to its generality, the framework presented here can be used as a paradigm to study
the fluctuating thermodynamics of several out of equilibrium setups in which the reservoirs are Gaussian.
From a practical point of view, this encompassess a variety of relevant physical platforms, including quantum optics (e.g., lasing cavities) and superconducting architectures driven by resonators
[
122
]
.
Future applications could also include the study of fluctuating thermodynamics in quantum computing devices, in which gates are often realized by coupling the qubits to auxiliary non-equilibrium degrees of freedom
[
123
,
124
]
.
By not relying on specific approximations, the present treatment extends beyond the conventional approaches based on weak coupling and secular regimes, and could be used as a starting point to assess the role of non-Markovian effects in non-equilibrium quantum thermodynamics.

We want the displacement to change the center of the Gaussian packet to

Taking this second part as a perturbation, we can further decompose the unitary evolution as

Otherwise, note that

(115)

Note that the first correction nullifies, since

\displaystyle Tr[\hat{X}_{4,1}\frac{\hat{P}_{4,1}}{m_{4,1}\omega_{4,1}}\hat{\rho}_{4,1}(0^{-})]=\frac{i\hbar}{2m_{4,1}\omega_{4,1}}.

(116)

\tilde{\tilde{O}}_{4,1}(s)=\tilde{U}^{(0)\,\dagger}_{D}(s,0)e^{\frac{is\hat{H}_{S}}{\hbar}}V_{4,1}(\hat{X})e^{-\frac{is\hat{H}_{S}}{\hbar}}\tilde{U}^{(0)}_{D}(s,0)

Comparing the standard deviation of the fluctuating coupling constant associated to Eq. (

We conclude that if the displacement is sufficiently large if compared to the range of the position fluctuations (induced by both thermal and quantum effects) of the Gaussian mode, we can neglect their noisy contribution to the dynamics of the system and we are left with an evolution that is effectively unitary.
This implies that

3.1
for pure driving sources. To complete the discussion, we compute the energy and entropy variations of the reservoir mode.
For this sake, we derive the reduced density matrix of the reservoir mode, which reads

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{E}(t)

\displaystyle=Tr_{S}[\hat{U}^{(0)}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha_{4,1})\tilde{U}_{D}\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{4,1}(0^{-})\tilde{U}^{\dagger}_{D}\hat{D}(\alpha_{4,1})\hat{U}^{(0)\,\dagger}]

At first order in the expansion of Eq. (

\displaystyle Tr_{S}[...]

\displaystyle=\hat{\rho}_{4,1}(0^{-})-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}Tr_{S}\big\{[\chi_{4}\tilde{U}^{(0)\,\dagger}_{D}(s,0)e^{\frac{is\hat{H}_{S}}{\hbar}}V_{4,1}(\hat{X})e^{-\frac{is\hat{H}_{S}}{\hbar}}\tilde{U}^{(0)}_{D}(s,0)\times

\displaystyle\times\big(\hat{X}_{4,1}\cos\omega_{4,1}s-\frac{\hat{P}_{4,1}}{m_{4,1}\omega_{4,1}}\sin\omega_{4,1}s\big),\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{4,1}(0^{-})]\big\}

(121)

Our goal is to expand the Von Neumann entropy in the perturbation above; for this, we can use the equation

S(\hat{A}+\epsilon\hat{B})=S(\hat{A})-\epsilon Tr[\hat{B}\log\hat{A}]-\epsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}Tr\big\{s\big(\hat{A}+s\big)^{-1}\big[\hat{B}\big(\hat{A}+s\big)^{-1}\big]^{2}\big\}ds+O(\epsilon^{3}),

(122)

The variation of energy is, instead, order zero in the perturbation; indeed, we have

\displaystyle E_{4,1}(t)-E_{4,1}(0)

\displaystyle=Tr_{E}[\hat{H}_{4,1}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha_{4,1})Tr_{S}[\tilde{\tilde{U}}_{D}(t,0)\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{4,1}(0^{-})\tilde{\tilde{U}}^{\dagger}_{D}(t,0)]\hat{D}(\alpha_{4,1})]

\displaystyle-Tr_{E}[\hat{H}_{4,1}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha_{4,1})\hat{\rho}_{4,1}(0^{-})\hat{D}(\alpha_{4,1})].

(124)

L_{4,1}\hat{X}_{4,1}
and replacing it into the Eq. (

\chi_{4}L_{4,1}

In this discussion, we did not consider the cost of switching on the coupling between the system and the reservoir. This coupling contribution can be safely disregarded since we are assuming
\chi_{4}

We conclude the section with a proof of Eq. (
25

In the weak coupling limit we can expand Eq. (
123

\chi_{4}

E_{4,1}(t)-E_{4,1}(0)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}Tr\big[\hat{D}(\alpha_{4,1})\hat{H}_{4,1}\hat{D}^{{\dagger}}(\alpha_{4,1})[\tilde{\tilde{H}}_{D}(s),\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{4,1}(0)]\big]ds.

(126)

We now replace Eq. (

Appendix C
Work source limit for a system in contact with a squeezed reservoir mode

In the case of a squeezed reservoir mode, we can reason in an analogous way to what we did for the displaced case, but we have to replace the displacement with a squeeze. The interaction Hamiltonian (see also Eq. (
111
)) in this case reads

\tilde{H}_{SQ}(t)=\hat{S}(r_{3,1})\tilde{H}(t)\hat{S}^{{\dagger}}(r_{3,1})=\chi_{3}(t)e^{\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{S}}V_{3,1}(\hat{X})e^{-\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{S}}[\hat{X}_{3,1}e^{+r_{3,1}}\cos\omega_{3,1}t-\frac{\hat{P}_{3,1}e^{-r_{3,1}}}{m_{3,1}\omega_{3,1}}\sin\omega_{3,1}t],

(130)

in which we assumed
r_{3,1}
to be real.
If the squeeze is sufficiently large, we can neglect the term proportional to
\hat{P}_{3,1}
and write

\tilde{U}_{SQ}(t,0)=\hat{S}(r_{3,1})\tilde{U}(t)\hat{S}^{{\dagger}}(r_{3,1})=\mathcal{T}\{e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}V_{3,1}[\tilde{X}(s)]\hat{X}_{3,1}e^{r_{3,1}}\cos\omega_{3,1}s\,ds}\},

(131)

where
V_{3,1}[\tilde{X}(s)]=e^{\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{S}}V_{3,1}(\hat{X})e^{-\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{S}}
.
The evolution of the system density matrix in the interaction picture is now given by

\displaystyle Tr_{B}[\tilde{U}_{SQ}(t,0)\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{3,1}(0^{-})\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{\dagger}(t,0)]

\displaystyle\hskip-113.81102pt=Tr_{B}\big[\big(\mathds{I}-\hat{X}_{3,1}\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\Gamma}(s)-\frac{\hat{X}_{3,1}^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}}\iint_{0}^{t}\mathcal{T}\{\tilde{\Gamma}(s)\tilde{\Gamma}(s^{\prime})\}dsds^{\prime}+...\big)\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{3,1}(0^{-})

\displaystyle\hskip-113.81102pt\times\big(\mathds{I}+\hat{X}_{3,1}\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\Gamma}(s)-\frac{\hat{X}_{3,1}^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}}\iint_{0}^{t}\bar{\mathcal{T}}\{\tilde{\Gamma}(s)\tilde{\Gamma}(s^{\prime})\}dsds^{\prime}+...\big)\big],

(132)

where we introduced
\tilde{\Gamma}(s)=\chi_{3}(s)V_{3,1}[\tilde{X}(s)]e^{r_{3,1}}\cos\omega_{3,1}s
and
\bar{\mathcal{T}}
is the anti time-ordering operator.
The expansion above can be written more conveniently by using the Keldysh contour

\displaystyle Tr_{B}[\tilde{U}_{SQ}(t,0)\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{3,1}(0^{-})\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{\dagger}(t,0)]

\displaystyle\hskip-113.81102pt=Tr_{B}[\mathcal{T}_{K}\big\{\big(\mathds{I}-\hat{X}_{3,1}\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{\gamma_{K}}\tilde{\Gamma}(s)ds-\frac{\hat{X}_{3,1}^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}}\iint_{\gamma_{K}}\tilde{\Gamma}(s)\tilde{\Gamma}(s^{\prime})\,ds\,ds^{\prime}+...\big)\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{3,1}(0^{-})\big\}]

\displaystyle\hskip-113.81102pt=Tr_{B}[\mathcal{T}_{K}\big\{\big(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{\gamma_{K}}\,ds\tilde{\Gamma}(s)\hat{X}_{3,1})^{k}}{k!}\big)\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{3,1}(0^{-})\big\}],

(133)

where
\mathcal{T}_{K}
orders the operators according to their position on the Keldysh contour
\gamma_{K}
.
The averages of the powers of
\hat{X}_{3,1}
over the initial state of the mode can be easily computed, and we have

Tr[\hat{X}_{3,1}^{2k}\hat{\rho}_{3,1}(0^{-})]=(2k-1)!!\langle\hat{X}_{3,1}^{2}\rangle^{k},

(134)

where
!!
denotes the double factorial and
\langle\hat{X}_{3,1}^{2}\rangle
the expectation value in Eq. (
116
).
The expectation value for odd values of
k
is
0
.
Using
(2k-1)!!=\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!}
we obtain

\displaystyle Tr_{B}[\tilde{U}_{SQ}\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{3,1}(0^{-})\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{K}\big\{\big[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\big(-\int_{\gamma}\tilde{\Gamma}(s)\,ds\big)^{2k}\langle\hat{X}_{3,1}^{2}\rangle^{k}}{k!2^{k}\hbar^{2k}}\big]\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{K}\big\{\exp\big[\frac{\big(-\int_{\gamma}\tilde{\Gamma}(s)ds\big)^{2}\langle\hat{X}_{3,1}^{2}\rangle}{2\hbar^{2}}\big]\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\big\}.

(135)

We note that this time ordered exponential is the same we would obtain by considering the solution of the stochastic Von Neumann equation

\dot{\hat{\rho}}^{(c)}_{S}(t)=-i\frac{\xi}{\hbar}[\tilde{\Gamma}(s),\hat{\rho}_{S}^{(c)}(t)],

(136)

where the standard density matrix can be obtained by averaging over the noise
\langle\hat{\rho}_{S}^{(c)}(t)\rangle=\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)
and the noise is Gaussian with

\langle\xi^{2}\rangle=\langle\hat{X}^{2}_{3,1}\rangle
.

To compute the energy and entropy variations, we can use a similar approach to the displaced case.
We calculate the evolution operator generated by
\tilde{H}_{SQ}(t)
in (
130
), by decomposing it in a contribution relative to the dominant term, proportional to
\hat{X}_{3,1}
, and the rest

\tilde{U}_{SQ}(t,0)=\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)}(t,0)\mathcal{T}\big\{\exp\big[\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{3}(s)\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)\,\dagger}(s,0)\frac{\hat{P}_{3,1}}{m_{3,1}\omega_{3,1}}V_{3,1}[\tilde{X}(s)]e^{-r_{3,1}}\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)}(s,0)\sin\omega_{3,1}sds\big]\big\},

(137)

where
\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)}(t,0)
is the time-ordered exponential generated by
\tilde{\Gamma}(t)\hat{X}_{3,1}
.
Using that
[\hat{P}_{3,1},f(\hat{X}_{3,1})]=-i\hbar f^{\prime}(\hat{X}_{3,1})
for every function
f
, we derive

\hat{P}_{3,1}\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)}(t,0)=\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)}(t,0)\hat{P}_{3,1}-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{X}}\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)}(t,0).

(138)

The time-ordered exponential in (
137
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\big\{\exp\big[\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{3}(s)\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)\,\dagger}(s,0)\frac{\hat{P}_{3,1}}{m_{3,1}\omega_{3,1}}V_{3,1}[\tilde{X}(s)]e^{-r_{3,1}}\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)}(s,0)\sin\omega_{3,1}sds\big]\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}\big\{\exp\big[\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{3}(s)\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)\,\dagger}(s,0)V_{3,1}[\tilde{X}(s)]e^{-r_{3,1}}\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)}(s,0)\frac{\hat{P}_{3,1}}{m_{3,1}\omega_{3,1}}\sin\omega_{3,1}sds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{m_{3,1}\omega_{3,1}}\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{3}(s)\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)\,\dagger}(s,0)V_{3,1}[\tilde{X}(s)]e^{-r_{3,1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{X}}\tilde{U}_{SQ}^{(0)}(s,0)\sin\omega_{3,1}sds\big]\big\}.

(139)

The time-ordered exponential above is sum of two contributions of order
\chi_{3}e^{-r_{3,1}}
, that is second order in the expansion parameter
\chi_{3}\approx e^{-r_{3,1}}
.
We conclude that the contact with the system induces a variation of the state of the reservoir that is second order in the expansion parameter and thus contributes to the Von Neumann entropy only at second order (see Eq. (
122
)).
Conversely, the correction to the average final energy of the reservoir is order zero in the small parameter, since it is the expectation value of the Hamiltonian

\hat{S}(r_{3,1})\hat{H}_{3,1}\hat{S}^{\dagger}(r_{3,1})=\frac{e^{-2r_{3,1}}\omega_{3,1}^{2}m_{3,1}}{2}\hat{X}_{3,1}^{2}+\frac{e^{2r_{3,1}}}{2m_{3,1}}\hat{P}_{3,1}^{2},

(140)

that diverges as
e^{2r_{3,1}}
.

Appendix F
Corrections to Eq. (
79

The energy
\Delta E_{\nu}
in Eq. (
61

The energy pumped into the reservoir mode
\nu,k
by initially displacing a particle in position
x_{\nu,k}(0^{-})

\Delta E_{0,\nu}^{dp}=\frac{1}{2}m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}(\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0^{-})+L_{\nu,k})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}\bar{x}_{\nu,k}^{2}(0^{-})=\frac{1}{2}m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}L_{\nu,k}^{2}+m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0^{-})L_{\nu,k}.

(152)

Differently from the quantum case, we are free to express the energy variation also in terms of the position of the reservoir mode
after
the displacement.
For every position
\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0)
resulting from the displacement, the original position was given by

\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0)-L_{\nu,k}
, so that, in terms of this new variable, the energy variation reads

\Delta E_{0,\nu}^{dp}=\frac{1}{2}m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}\bar{x}_{\nu,k}^{2}(0)-\frac{1}{2}m_{\nu,k}\omega^{2}_{\nu,k}(\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0)-L_{\nu,k})^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}L_{\nu,k}^{2}+m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0)L_{\nu,k}.

65
) in the main text.

(154)

\omega_{\nu,k}e^{r_{\nu,k}}
and let the mode evolve for a quarter of a period, then evolve with the normal frequency for three quarters of a period).
The energy exchanged during the process is

\Delta E_{0,\nu}^{sq}=\frac{1}{2}m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}\bar{x}^{2}_{\nu,k}(0)+\frac{1}{2m_{\nu,k}}\bar{p}^{2}_{\nu,k}(0)-\frac{1}{2}m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}e^{-2r_{\nu,k}}\bar{x}^{2}_{\nu,k}(0)-\frac{1}{2m_{\nu,k}}e^{2r_{\nu,k}}\bar{p}^{2}_{\nu,k}(0).

If squeezing and displacement are both present,
the position of the particle before the quench is

65

64

In the main text we found the action for the MGF (

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\exp\big[-\frac{\beta_{\nu}m_{\nu,k}\omega^{2}_{\nu,k}}{2}\big(\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0)-L_{\nu,k}\big)^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{\nu}}{2}m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}L_{\nu,k}^{2}+\lambda_{\nu}m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}\bar{x}_{\nu,k}(0)L_{\nu,k}\big)\big]

Appendix H
Path integral and energy statistics for classical stochastic equations

Let us consider a system coupled to many thermal reservoirs evolving under the action of Eq. (
69

The corresponding Hamilton equations write

\displaystyle m\dot{\bar{x}}(t)=\bar{p}(t),\quad\dot{\bar{p}}(t)=\mathcal{F}[\bar{x}(t),\bm{\xi_{B}}(t)],

(175)

where
\mathcal{F}
are the total forces acting on the system (that is, the right hand side of Eq. (
69

\bar{\cdot}
to denote a variable that satisfies the equations of motion.
After introducing a time slicing in
L
intervals such that
t_{n}=\frac{n}{L}\tau=n\Delta
with
n=0,...L-1
, we can discretize the Hamilton equations as

\displaystyle\bar{x}(t_{n+1})=\bar{x}(t_{n})+\frac{\bar{p}(t_{n})}{m}\Delta,\quad\quad\bar{p}(t_{n+1})=\bar{p}(t_{n})+\mathcal{F}[\bar{x}(t_{n}),\bm{\xi_{B}}(t_{n})]\Delta.

(176)

To simplify the notation, we can introduce a phase space vector
\bm{q}=(x,p)
and reduce the set of equations (
176
) to a compact
\bm{\bar{q}}(t_{n+1})=\bm{\bar{q}}(t_{n})+\Delta\mathcal{L}[\bm{\bar{q}}(t_{n})]

where
\mathcal{L}
is a linear operator acting on
\bm{q}
defined using Eq. (
176
).
The next step is to write the equations of motion as a constraint in an integral over a set of free fields

f[\bm{\bar{q}}(t_{n+1})]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\bm{q}\,f[\bm{q}]\delta\{\bm{q}-\bm{\bar{q}}(t_{n})-\Delta\mathcal{L}[\bm{\bar{q}}(t_{n})]\},

(177)

where
f
denotes a generic function of the trajectory.
Using the integral representation of the Dirac delta function, the equation above can also be expressed in terms of an auxiliary field
\bm{\eta}
(up to a normalization factor that is irrelevant for our purposes) as

f[\bm{\bar{q}}(t_{n+1})]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\bm{\eta}\,d\bm{q}\,f[\bm{q}]e^{i\bm{\eta}\cdot\{[\bm{q}-\bm{\bar{q}}(t_{n})]-\Delta\mathcal{L}[\bm{\bar{q}}(t_{n})]\}}.

(178)

If we have a functional of the whole trajectory,
f[\{\bm{\bar{q}}(t_{n})\}_{n=0}^{L-1}]

the above procedure can be iterated.
By definition, we have

f[\{\bm{\bar{q}}(t_{n})\}_{n=0}^{L-1}]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{n=0}^{L-1}d\bm{q}_{n}\delta[\bm{\bar{q}}(t_{n})-\bm{q}_{n}]f[\{\bm{q}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{L-1}].

(179)

The first Dirac delta (
n=0
) gives the initial condition, while the others (
1\leq n\leq L-1

Appendix K
Approximate calculation of the squeezed Green’s function

In this appendix we study the solution of Eq. (
50
) using a perturbative approach.
As a first step, it is convenient to note that a quench of the masses is equivalent (i.e. produces the same MGF) of a quench of
\beta_{\nu},\lambda_{\nu}
alongside a quench in the frequencies.
This is clear from the formula of the squeezed Hamiltonian (
106
): the squeezed thermal state can be obtained by replacing
m_{\nu,k}\rightarrow e^{-2r_{\nu,k}}m_{\nu,k}
but also by replacing
\beta_{\nu}\rightarrow\beta_{\nu}e^{2r_{\nu,k}}
and
\omega_{\nu,k}\rightarrow\omega_{\nu,k}e^{-2r_{\nu,k}}
for every mode of the reservoir.
Using this property, we can represent the quench in the masses as a combination of a quench in the temperatures and the frequencies, and turn our attention to the calculation of the Green’s function for an on oscillator with a varying frequency on the contour

\displaystyle\omega_{\nu,k}(z)=\begin{cases}\omega_{\nu,k}&\text{for }z=t\in\gamma_{\pm},\gamma^{\nu}_{\uparrow},\\
e^{-2r_{\nu,k}}\omega_{\nu,k}&\text{for }z\in\gamma_{M},\gamma^{\nu}_{\downarrow}.\end{cases}

(228)

In this case, the Green’s function will satisfy

\big[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}+\omega_{\nu,k}(z)^{2}\big]\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})=\delta(z-z^{\prime})\omega_{\nu,k}

(229)

and the domain of integration is given by a "quenched" version of the contour
\gamma^{\nu\star}
in which
\beta_{\nu}\rightarrow\beta_{\nu}e^{2r_{\nu,k}}
and
\lambda_{\nu}\rightarrow\lambda_{\nu}e^{2r_{\nu,k}}
.
If we introduce the quenched value of the frequency
\Omega_{\nu,k}\equiv\omega_{\nu,k}e^{-2r_{\nu,k}}
and the function

\displaystyle f(z)=\begin{cases}0&\text{for }z\in\gamma_{\pm},\\
1&\text{for }z\in\gamma^{\nu\star}_{M},\gamma^{\nu\star}_{\downarrow},\gamma^{\nu\star}_{\uparrow},\end{cases}

(230)

the Eq. (
229
) becomes

\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}+\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}\Big)\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})+f(z)(\Omega_{\nu,k}^{2}-\omega_{\nu,k}^{2})\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})=\omega_{\nu,k}\delta(z-z^{\prime}).

(231)

Eq. (
53
) can also be written as

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\nu,k}=\iint_{\gamma^{\nu\star}}\frac{dzdz^{\prime}}{2m_{\nu,k}\omega_{\nu,k}}\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})\chi^{(\gamma)}_{\nu}(z)\chi^{(\gamma)}_{\nu}(z^{\prime})V^{(\gamma)}_{\nu,k}[x(z)]V^{(\gamma)}_{\nu,k}[x(z^{\prime})]+i\hbar(\beta_{\nu}+\lambda_{\nu})N^{\prime}_{\nu}

(232)

where
N^{\prime}_{\nu}=\sum_{k}\frac{m_{\nu,k}e^{-2r_{\nu,k}}\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}L_{\nu,k}}{2}
and we replaced
\gamma^{\nu}
with
\gamma^{\nu\star}
.
Assuming a perturbative expansion in
\varepsilon_{\nu,k}=\Omega_{\nu,k}^{2}-\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}
that is

\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})+\varepsilon_{\nu,k}\mathcal{G}^{(1)}_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})+O(\varepsilon_{\nu,k}^{2})
we have

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}+\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}\Big)\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{(0)}(z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\omega_{\nu,k}\delta(z-z^{\prime}),

(233)

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}+\omega_{\nu,k}^{2}\Big)\mathcal{G}^{(1)}_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-f(z)\mathcal{G}^{(0)}_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime}).

(234)

It is clear from the system of equations above that
\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{(0)}(z,z^{\prime})
follows the same equations of the Green’s function (
49
), thus we have

\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{(0)}(z,z^{\prime})=G_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime}),

(235)

where
G_{\nu,k}(z,z^{\prime})
is given in Eq. (
49
).
The first correction instead can be solved with standard Green’s function techniques. Since the Green’s function of the differential equation (
234
) is just the Eq. (
49
) divided by
\omega_{\nu,k}
, the correction takes the form of

\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{(1)}(z,z^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{\omega_{\nu,k}}\int_{\gamma_{\nu}^{*}}G_{\nu,k}(z,\bar{z})f(\bar{z})\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{(0)}(\bar{z},z^{\prime})d\bar{z}=-\frac{1}{\omega_{\nu,k}}\int_{\gamma_{\nu}^{*}}G_{\nu,k}(z,\bar{z})f(\bar{z})G_{\nu,k}(\bar{z},z^{\prime})d\bar{z}.

(236)

We can start by looking only at the contribution of the squeezing to the dynamical part of the action by setting
\lambda_{\nu}=0
.
In this case, the only nonzero contribution of the integral in Equation (
236
) is on
\gamma^{M\star}
.
We are interested in the correlation function, that can be obtained by looking at the quantum component of
\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}
.
Following equations (
85
) the quantum part of
\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{(1)}(z,z^{\prime})
is obtained by combining its components as

\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{(1),q,q}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4}[\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{(1),+,+}(t,t^{\prime})+\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{(1),-,-}(t,t^{\prime})+\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{(1),+,-}(t,t^{\prime})+\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{(1),-,+}(t,t^{\prime})].

(237)

Let us compute the first contribution to the equation above

\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{(1),+,+}(t,t^{\prime})=-\frac{i}{\omega_{\nu,k}}\int_{0}^{-\hbar\beta_{\nu}e^{2r_{\nu,k}}}G_{\nu,k}^{+,M}(t,\zeta)G_{\nu,k}^{M,+}(\zeta,t^{\prime})d\zeta.

(238)

Directly from (
150
) we can check that, for what concerns the term in the integral above, the four contributions (
237
) are the same, so that we obtain, at first order in the small parameter

\mathcal{G}_{\nu,k}^{q,q}(t,t^{\prime})=G_{\nu,k}^{q,q}(t,t^{\prime})-\frac{i\epsilon_{\nu,k}}{\omega_{\nu,k}}\int_{0}^{-\hbar\beta_{\nu}e^{2r_{\nu,k}}}G_{\nu,k}^{+,M}(t,\zeta)G_{\nu,k}^{M,+}(\zeta,t^{\prime})d\zeta.

(239)

For
\lambda_{\nu,k}=0
and
\hbar\rightarrow 0
, keeping the first order in
\hbar
we have, from Eqs. (

) and (
219

G_{\nu,k}^{q,q}(t,t^{\prime})\approx\frac{i}{\hbar e^{2r_{\nu,k}}\beta_{\nu}\omega_{\nu,k}}\cos\omega_{\nu,k}(t-t^{\prime})

After dividing by
\omega_{\nu,k}m_{\nu,k}
as prescribed by Eq. (

To prove that the two equations are matching, we use that in Eq. (

(241)

while in the Eq. (

The two quantities are equal at the first order in

.

Appendix L
Fluctuation theorem for squeezed and displaced reservoirs

Let us consider the generating function of internal energy and heat for a system coupled with many squeezed and displaced harmonic modes.
Equations (
30
) and (
31
) applied to the protocol described in Section
4.1
allow us to write the following compact relation for the MGF

\displaystyle M(

\displaystyle\lambda_{S},\bm{\lambda_{B}},\tau,\bm{\alpha},\bm{r})=Tr[e^{\lambda_{S}\hat{H}_{S}+\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{\hat{H}_{B}}}\hat{U}(\tau,0)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha_{\nu})\hat{S}^{\dagger}(r_{\nu})e^{-\lambda_{S}\hat{H}_{S}-\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{\hat{H}_{B}}}\hat{\rho}(0^{-})\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}\hat{S}(r_{\nu})\hat{D}(\alpha_{\nu})\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\tau,0)],

(243)

where we made the dependence from the squeezing and displacement parameters explicit and used the shorthand notation
\hat{D}(\alpha_{\nu})=\prod_{k}\hat{D}(\alpha_{\nu,k})
,

\hat{S}(r_{\nu})=\prod_{k}\hat{S}(r_{\nu,k})
.
As in Sec.
4.1
, we have chosen
\tau_{0}=0^{-}
to denote the initial time in which squeezing and displacement have not yet been performed.
In the time reversal protocol, any time-dependent external driving is inverted in time.
In our case, this corresponds to doing the following substitution

\hat{U}(\tau,0)\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha_{\nu})\hat{S}^{\dagger}(r_{\nu})\rightarrow\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}\hat{S}^{\dagger}(r_{\nu})\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha_{\nu})\hat{U}_{rev}(\tau,0)

(244)

where
\hat{U}_{rev}(\tau,0)
represents the unitary evolution in which any time dependence in the Hamiltonian has been reversed
\hat{H}_{rev}(s)=\hat{H}(\tau-s)
. The idea behind the transformation above is that the initial squeezing and displacement are part of the "evolution" of the system and reservoir, and as such, since the time evolution is inverted, their contribution now comes
after
the propagator
\hat{U}_{rev}(\tau,0)
.
The time reversal operation is the combination of the replacement above with an initial time reversal applied to the quantum state, thus we finally obtain

\displaystyle M^{R}(

\displaystyle\lambda_{S},\bm{\lambda_{B}},\tau,\bm{\alpha},\bm{r})=Tr[e^{\lambda_{S}\hat{H}_{S}+\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{\hat{H}_{B}}}\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}\hat{S}^{\dagger}(r_{\nu})\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha_{\nu})

\displaystyle\times\hat{U}_{rev}(\tau,0)e^{-\lambda_{S}\hat{H}_{S}-\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{\hat{H}_{B}}}\hat{\Theta}\hat{\rho}(0^{-})\hat{\Theta}\hat{U}^{\dagger}_{rev}(\tau,0)\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}\hat{D}(\alpha_{\nu})\hat{S}(r_{\nu})],

(245)

where
\hat{\Theta}
is the time reversal operator such that
\hat{\Theta}i=-i\hat{\Theta}
and
\hat{\Theta}\hat{\Theta}=\mathds{1}
.
To proceed we have to recall the properties of the displacement and squeezing under time reversal

\hat{\Theta}\hat{D}(\alpha_{\nu})\hat{\Theta}=\hat{D}(\alpha^{*}_{\nu}),\quad\quad\hat{\Theta}\hat{S}(r_{\nu})\hat{\Theta}=\hat{S}(r_{\nu}^{*}),

(246)

that together with
\hat{\Theta}\hat{U}_{rev}(\tau,0)\hat{\Theta}=\hat{U}^{{\dagger}}(\tau,0)
allow us to write

\displaystyle M^{R}(

\displaystyle\lambda_{S},\bm{\lambda_{B}},\tau,\bm{\alpha},\bm{r})=Tr[e^{\lambda_{S}\hat{H}_{S}+\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{\hat{H}_{B}}}\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}\hat{S}^{\dagger}(r^{*}_{\nu})\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha^{*}_{\nu})

\displaystyle\times\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\tau,0)e^{-\lambda_{S}\hat{H}_{S}-\bm{\lambda_{B}}\cdot\bm{\hat{H}_{B}}}\hat{\rho}(0^{-})\hat{U}(\tau,0)\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}\hat{D}(\alpha^{*}_{\nu})\hat{S}(r^{*}_{\nu})],

(247)

under the assumption that
\hat{H}_{S}
is time reversal invariant.
To conclude the derivation of Eq. (
93
) we do the replacement
\lambda_{\nu}\rightarrow-\lambda_{\nu}-\beta_{\nu}
,
\lambda_{S}\rightarrow-\lambda_{S}-\beta_{S}
and invert the signs of the real parts of
\alpha_{\nu}
and
r_{\nu}

\displaystyle M^{R}(-\lambda_{S}-\beta_{S},-\bm{\lambda_{B}}-\bm{\beta_{B}},

\displaystyle\tau,-\bm{\alpha}^{*},-\bm{r}^{*})=\frac{1}{Z_{S}(0)}Tr[e^{-(\lambda_{S}+\beta_{S})\hat{H}_{S}-(\bm{\lambda_{B}}+\bm{\beta_{B}})\cdot\bm{\hat{H}_{B}}}\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}\hat{S}(r_{\nu})\hat{D}(\alpha_{\nu})\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\tau,0)

\displaystyle\times e^{+(\lambda_{S}+\beta_{S})\hat{H}_{S}+(\bm{\lambda_{B}}+\bm{\beta_{B}})\cdot\bm{\hat{H}_{B}}}\hat{\rho}(0^{-})\hat{U}(\tau,0)\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha_{\nu})\hat{S}^{\dagger}(r_{\nu})].

(248)

Using that the system and the reservoir are initially prepared in a Gibbs state and rearranging the terms, we obtain that the r.h.s of the equation above is equal to
1/Z_{S}(\tau)
times the r.h.s. of (
243
), thus proving the FT in Eq. (
93
).
