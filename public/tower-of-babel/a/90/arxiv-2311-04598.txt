Robust Portfolio Optimization under Ambiguous Chance Constraints
Pulak Swain*, Akshay Kumar Ojha

Abstract
In this paper, we discuss the ambiguous chance constrained based portfolio optimization problems, in which the perturbations associated with the input parameters are stochastic in nature, but their distributions are not known precisely. We consider two different families of perturbation distributions– one is when only upper & \& lower bounds of mean values are known to us, and the second one is along with the mean bounds, we also have the knowledge of the standard deviations of the perturbations. We derive the safe convex approximations of such chance constrained portfolio problems by using some suitable generating functions such as a piecewise linear function, an exponential function, and a piecewise quadratic function. These safe approximations are the robust counterparts to our ambiguous chance constrained problem and they are computationally tractable due to the convex nature of these approximations.

Introduction

The problem of optimal asset allocation is an important aspect of investment studies and it has been truly identified by Harry Markowitz in 1952
Markowitz J 1952
. Markowitz’s studies show the importance of diversification of investment through the optimization models
Markowitz 1959
. In such problems, the prime objective of the investor is to find the right balance between two conflicting components- risk and return. To visualize this opposing behavior of the above two components, a trade-off curve can be drawn which is known as the efficient frontier. Markowitz’s mean-variance portfolio model is defined by using the expected returns and covariance of returns of the individual assets as the input parameters. For a mathematical overview, let’s assume that the investor wants to invest his total wealth among
n
assets where the return of any arbitrary
i^{th}
asset over a time period
t
is given by
r_{it}\ (t=1,2,\dots,T)
. Let
\mu_{i}
and
\sigma_{ij}
are respectively the expected return of
i^{th}
asset and the covariance of return between
i
-
j
pair of assets, which are defined as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mu_{i}=\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{it},\quad\sigma_{ij}=\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(r_{it}-\mu_{i})(r_{jt}-\mu_{j})\end{aligned}

Let
x_{i}
be the weight allocated for
i^{th}
asset in the portfolio. Then the expected portfolio return (
\mu_{p}
) and the variance of portfolio return (
\sigma_{p}^{2}
) can be calculated as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mu_{p}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}x_{i},\quad\sigma_{p}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\end{aligned}

Markowitz portfolio model minimizes the variance of portfolio return by constraining the expected return to become more than or equal to a target return (
\tau
). Moreover, the model assumes the weights to be non-negative and the sum of the weights to be
1
. Mathematically the Markowitz portfolio model is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\min_{x_{i}}&&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\\
&\textrm{s.t.:}&&\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}x_{j}\geq 1,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}{x_{j}}=1,\quad{x_{j}}\geq 0,\ j=1,2,\dots,n.\end{aligned}

(1)

One of the biggest challenges in portfolio optimization is to get the exact input parameters such as expected asset returns and the covariance of asset returns. The main reason for this is the several economic factors involved in the market which can affect these input parameters. This leads to the presence of some uncertainty factor in these parameters, which if ignored can potentially affect the optimal asset allocation. To address this issue, several approaches such as sensitivity analysis, dynamic programming, fuzzy programming, etc. are used in the field of optimization. However, in the last two decades, robust optimization approach has been widely used by researchers due to its ability to produce ”completely immunized against uncertainty” solutions. This approach was first studied by El Ghaoui and Lebret
Ghaoui 1997
, in which they worked on the uncertain least square problems. Then El Ghaoui et al.
Ghaoui 1998
used the robust optimization approach for uncertain semidefinite programs and provided sufficient conditions for the robust solutions. Later on Ben-Tal and Nemirovski
Ben-Tal 1999
studied the robust optimization for linear programming problems for ellipsoidal uncertainty and showed the tractability of the robust counterpart. Another work of Ben-Tal and Nemirovski
Ben-Tal 2000
was based on some uncertain linear programming problems from NETLIB collection and their results showed that the robust solutions nearly lose nothing in optimality. In the literature, there are some more studies on the geometrical shape based uncertainties such as box, ellipsoidal, polyhedral, etc. The robust optimization approach was first used in the uncertain portfolio problems when Goldfarb and Iyenger
Goldfarb 2003
introduced some uncertain structures for the market parameters and reformulated the robust portfolio problems into second order cone programming. Tütüncü and Koenig
Tutuncu 2004
formulated the robust counterparts when the expected return vector and the covariance matrix of asset returns are defined by lower and upper bounds and they also showed the robust efficient frontiers. Fliege and Werner
Fliege 2014
studied the application of robust multiobjective optimization on uncertain mean-variance portfolio problems. Sehgal and Mehra
Sehgal 2019
proposed robust portfolio optimization models for reward–risk ratios
by using Omega, semi-mean absolute deviation ratio, and weighted stable tail adjusted return ratio and also showed the efficiency of these models.

On the other hand, the chance constrained based optimization problems
Biswal 1998
is another type of uncertain optimization problem in which the uncertain parameters are stochastic in nature. Moreover, chance constraints basically indicate the probability of uncertain constraint(s) to be satisfied, where the perturbations act as random variables. Calafiore and El Ghaoui
Calafiore 2006
studied distributional robust chance constrained linear problems with radially distributed perturbations. They showed that the linear chance constraints can be converted explicitly into convex second-order cone constraints so that they can be efficiently solvable. Erdoğan and Iyengar
Erdogan 2006
studied the ambiguous chance constrained problem which is approximated by a robust sampled problem. Nemirovski and Shapiro
Nemirovski 2007
studied the convex approximations for the ambiguous chance constraints. In particular, they derived the Bernstein approximation and their approximation was convex and efficiently solvable. Yanıkoğlu and den Hertog
Yanıkoğlu 2013
used the available historical data of uncertain parameters to get the safe approximations of ambiguous chance constraints and their approach has the advantage that it can also easily handle joint chance constraints. Bertsimas et al.
Bertsimas 2018
derived safe approximations to nonlinear robust individual chance constraints by using statistical hypothesis tests. Zhang et al.
Zhang 2018
solved distributionally robust chance-constrained binary programs by equivalently reformulating them as
0-1
second order cone programs.

The main contribution of the paper is as follows: Sometimes the uncertainty present in the portfolio optimization problems is stochastic in nature and we don’t know the exact distribution of the uncertain parameters. Such problems are in general difficult to solve, as in those problems we not only need to handle the chance constraints, but also we need to take care of the ambiguous nature of probability distributions present in the chance constraints. So the challenge is to convert such problems into deterministic as well as computationally tractable optimization problems. In this paper we use some generating functions to get the safe convex approximations to such problems. We consider two different families of perturbation distributions– (i) when we know the bounds of mean perturbation values and (ii) when we know the mean and standard deviations of perturbation values. The safe convex approximations which we get for the family-i are quadratic programming problems and the approximations we get for the family-ii are quadratically constrained quadratic programming problems, which are computationally tractable. Moreover, we implemented the results into a real life stock market problem to find out the robust optimal allocation. Also, the risk-return efficient frontiers are drawn for all the safe approximation cases.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section
2
presents some definitions and basic concepts regarding robust optimization and the chance constrained based portfolio optimization problems. Section
3
constitutes the discussion on generating function based safe convex approximation and then the safe approximations for several families of perturbation distributions are derived in
4
. Further, a numerical example consisting of an Indian stock market problem is given in Section
5
. Finally, some concluding remarks are provided in Section
6
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Uncertainty in Optimization Problems and the Robust Optimization Approach

When the constant coefficients in an optimization problem are not fixed values; but they perturb around some nominal values, such problems are called uncertain optimization problems. Mathematically this can be represented as
Bertsimas 2011
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\min_{\bm{x}}&&f(\bm{x},\bm{u})\\
&\textrm{s.t.:}&&c(\bm{x},\bm{u})\geq 0,\quad\forall\bm{u}=\bm{u}^{(0)}+\displaystyle{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\zeta_{j}\bm{u}^{(j)}\in\mathscr{U}\end{aligned}

(2)

where
\bm{x}
is the vector of decision variables and
\bm{u}
is the vector of uncertain variables,
\bm{u}^{(0)}
is the vector of nominal values of uncertain parameters and
\bm{u}^{(j)}
(for
j=1,2,\dots,n
) are the basic shifts.

Now we will go through some definitions related to robust optimization.

Definition 1
(Robust Feasible Solution)

Ben-Tal 2009

A vector
\bm{x}
is said to be the robust feasible solution of the uncertain problem (
2
) if it satisfies the uncertain constraints for all realizations of the uncertain set
\mathscr{U}
, that is, if
\bm{x}
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} c(\bm{x},\bm{u})\leq 0,\quad\forall\bm{u}\in\mathscr{U}\end{aligned}

Definition 2
(Robust Value)

Ben-Tal 2009

For a given candidate solution
\bm{x}
, the robust value
\widehat{f}(\bm{x})
of the objective in problem (
2
) is the largest value of
f(\bm{x},\bm{u})
over all realizations of the data from the uncertain set, that is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \widehat{f}(\bm{x})=\sup_{\bm{u}\in\mathscr{U}}f(\bm{x},\bm{u})\end{aligned}

It is to be noted that if the problem (
2
) were a maximization problem, then the robust value would have been
\widehat{f}(\bm{x})=\inf_{\bm{u}\in\mathscr{U}}f(\bm{x},\bm{u})
.

Definition 3
(Robust Counterpart)

Ben-Tal 2009

The robust counterpart (RC) of the uncertain problem (
2
) is the optimization problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\min_{\bm{x}}&&\left\{\sup_{\bm{u}\in\mathscr{U}}f(\bm{x},\bm{u})\right\}\\
&\text{s.t.:}&&c(\bm{x},\bm{u})\leq\bm{0},\quad\forall\bm{u}\in\mathscr{U}\end{aligned}

of minimizing the robust value of the objective over all the robust feasible solutions to the uncertain problem.

Definition 4
(Robust Optimal Solution)

Ben-Tal 2009

The solution of the RC problem is said to be the robust optimal solution of the uncertain problem (
2
).

2.2
Ambiguous Chance Constraint

Consider the uncertain constraint given in the problem (
2
),

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} c(\bm{x},\bm{u})\geq 0,\quad\bm{u}=\bm{u}^{(0)}+\displaystyle{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\zeta_{j}\bm{u}^{(j)}\end{aligned}

(3)

In the ideal scenario, we would like to find a solution
\bm{x}
which satisfies the constraint (
3
) for all realizations of the uncertain set. But when the data perturbation is stochastic in nature, it is not always possible to find such a solution. So we look for a candidate solution that satisfies (
3
) for ”nearly all” realizations of
\bm{\zeta}
. Thus we want our constraint to be satisfied with a predefined reliability level. Then the probability chance constraint to (
3
) is introduced which is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {Prob}_{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}\left\{\bm{\zeta}:c(\bm{x},\bm{u})\geq 0,\quad\bm{u}=\bm{u}^{(0)}+\displaystyle{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\zeta_{j}\bm{u}^{(j)}\right\}\geq\beta\end{aligned}

(4)

Eq. (
4
) indicates that the constraint (
3
) would be satisfied with a probability of at least
\beta
, where
\beta
may be taken as close to 1.

However, sometimes the probability distribution of
\bm{\zeta}
is not known precisely; all we know is that the distribution belongs to a given family
\mathcal{P}
of probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In such cases, we need to convert the problem (
4
) into an ambiguously chance
constrained version which is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {Prob}_{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}\left\{\bm{\zeta}:c(\bm{x},\bm{u})\geq 0,\quad\bm{u}=\bm{u}^{(0)}+\displaystyle{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\zeta_{j}\bm{u}^{(j)}\right\}\geq\beta,\quad\forall P\in\mathcal{P}\end{aligned}

One of the biggest drawbacks of these ambiguous chance constrained problems is the tractability issues of the robust counterparts. The problem becomes NP-hard even for the cases when P is simple.

2.3
Uncertain Portfolio Problem with Chance Constraint

Consider the portfolio optimization problem (
1
) in vector form given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\min_{\bm{x}}&&\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{x}\\
&\textrm{s.t.:}&&\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{x}\geq\tau,\quad\bm{e}^{\top}\bm{x}=1,\quad\bm{x}\geq\bm{0}\end{aligned}

(5)

where
\bm{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1}\\
\mu_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{n}\end{bmatrix}
,
\bm{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{11}&\sigma_{12}&\dots&\sigma_{1n}\\
\sigma_{21}&\sigma_{22}&\dots&\sigma_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\sigma_{n1}&\sigma_{n2}&\dots&\sigma_{nn}\\
\end{bmatrix}
,

\bm{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{bmatrix}
,

\bm{e}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{bmatrix}
,

\bm{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{bmatrix}
.

The uncertainty in portfolio optimization problems in general occurs due to the input parameters – expected returns and covariance of returns of the assets. However, the studies show that the uncertainty in the covariance of asset returns does not affect the optimal solution so much
Pulak 2021
. So in this study, we assume that the uncertainty occurs only in the expected returns of assets.

Then the uncertain portfolio model can be written as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\min_{\bm{x}}&&\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{x}\\
&\textrm{s.t.:}&&\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{x}\geq\tau,\quad\bm{\mu}\in\mathscr{U}_{\bm{\mu}},\\
&&&\bm{e}^{\top}\bm{x}=1,\quad\bm{x}\geq\bm{0}\end{aligned}

(6)

where
\mathscr{U}_{\bm{\mu}}
is the uncertainty set associated with
\bm{\mu}
given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathscr{U}_{\mu}=\left\{\bm{\mu}:\bm{\mu}\equiv\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})=\bm{\mu}^{(0)}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}\bm{\mu}^{(j)}\right\}\end{aligned}

Now the model (
6
) can be written with ambiguous chance constraint as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\min_{\bm{x}}&&\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{x}\\
&\textrm{s.t.:}&&\forall P\in\mathcal{P}:{Prob}_{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}\left\{[\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})]^{\top}\bm{x}\geq\tau\right\}\geq\beta,\\
&&&\bm{e}^{\top}\bm{x}=1,\quad\bm{x}\geq\bm{0}\end{aligned}

(7)

The chance constraint in problem (
7
) can also be written as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &{Prob}_{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}\left\{[\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})]^{\top}\bm{x}<\tau\right\}\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

(8)

3
Generating-Function-Based Safe Convex Approximation for Ambiguous Chance Constrained Portfolio Optimization Model

The major drawbacks of chance constrained problems are:

•

It is difficult to evaluate with high accuracy the probability on the left side of a chance constraint.

•

In most of the cases the feasible set of the chance constraint is non-convex, due to which the optimization problem becomes very difficult to solve.

One way to overcome these issues is to replace the chance constraint in the model (
7
) with its computationally tractable safe approximation and then solve that approximation to get the robust solution.

Definition 5
(Safe Convex Approximation)

Ben-Tal 2009

Let
\{\bm{\mu}^{(j)}\}_{j=0}^{n}
,
Prob
,
\beta
be the data of chance constraint (
8
) and let
S
be a system of convex constraints on
\bm{x}
and additional variables
\bm{v}
. Then
S
is said to be a safe convex approximation of chance constraint (
8
) if the
\bm{x}
component of every feasible solution
(\bm{x};\bm{v})
of
S
is feasible for the chance constraint.

Moreover, a safe convex approximation
S
of (
8
) is said to be computationally tractable, if the convex constraints forming
S
are efficiently computable.

Nemirovski and Shapiro
Nemirovski 2007
suggested a generating function based approach to obtain the computationally tractable safe convex
approximation, where the generating function
\gamma(\cdot)
must satisfy the
following properties:

(i)
\gamma(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-negative non-decreasing function.

(ii)
\gamma(\cdot)
is convex.

(iii)
\gamma(0)\geq 1
and
\gamma(t)\to 0
as
t\to-\infty
.

Let’s denote the left hand side of the chance constraint (
8
) by
p(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})
and the constraint is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &p(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})\equiv{Prob}_{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}\left\{[\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})]^{\top}\bm{x}<\tau\right\}\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

(9)

The constraint can also be written as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &E\left[\chi(\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x})\right]\leq 1-\beta,\end{aligned}

(10)

where
\chi(t)
is the characteristic function given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\chi(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{ for }t\leq 0\\
1&\mbox{ for }t>0\end{array}\right.\end{aligned}

and
E[.]
is the expectation of the function defined as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &E\left[\chi(\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x})\right]=\int\chi(\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x})\ dP(\bm{\zeta}).\end{aligned}

Since
\chi(\cdot)
is not a convex function, it follows that we can not convert (
10
) into a convex constraint of
\bm{\mu}
by using the function
\chi(\cdot)
.

To find out a convex approximation for the chance constraint (
9
), let
\gamma(t)
be a convex function on the axis which is everywhere
\geq

\chi(t)
and it satisfies the properties (i)-(iii) mentioned before.

Then we have,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &p(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})=E\left[\chi(\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x})\right]\leq E\left[\gamma(\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x})\right]=\psi(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})\text{ (say)}\end{aligned}

(11)

Now let
\psi^{+}(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})
is some upper bound of
\psi(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})
, that is,
\psi(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})\leq\psi^{+}(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})~\forall\bm{\mu}
. Then the constraint

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\psi^{+}(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

(12)

can be a safe convex approximation of the chance constraint (
8
).

When the function
\psi^{+}(\cdot)
is efficiently computable, the safe approximation (
12
) is tractable.

This observation can be converted into the following result:

Theorem 3.1

For the ambiguous chance constraint (
8
) of an uncertain portfolio optimization problem (
7
), a generating function
\gamma(\cdot)
can be used to write the safe convex approximation given by (
12
), where
\psi^{+}(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})\geq E\left[\gamma(\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x})\right]
. Moreover, this approximation is tractable, provided that
\psi^{+}(\cdot)
is efficiently computable.

It is to be noted that, to reduce the conservatism of the above approximation, we can also do scaling as follows:

From the definition of
\chi(t)
, it can be observed that,
\chi(t)=\chi(\alpha^{-1}t)
,
\forall\alpha>0
, which implies
\gamma(\alpha^{-1}t)\geq\chi(\alpha^{-1}t)=\chi(t)
,
\forall t
.

So from eq. (
8
) we get,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &p(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})\geq\psi^{+}(\alpha^{-1}\bm{\mu},\bm{x})\end{aligned}

(13)

and the constraint

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\psi^{+}(\alpha^{-1}\bm{\mu},\bm{x})\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

(14)

is also a safe convex approximation for all
\alpha>0
.

This implies
\alpha\psi^{+}(\alpha^{-1}\bm{\mu},\bm{x})-\alpha(1-\beta)\leq 0
is a safe approximation for any
\alpha>0
.

So the infimum of the left hand side of the function can also be used to write the safe convex approximation, which is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \inf_{\alpha>0}\left[\alpha\psi^{+}(\alpha^{-1}\bm{\mu},\bm{x})-\alpha(1-\beta)\right]\leq 0\end{aligned}

(15)

When the function
\psi^{+}(\cdot)
is efficiently computable, the left hand side of (
15
) is also efficiently computable. Then the approximation (
15
) is tractable.

In the next section, we obtain the robust counterparts of the problem (
7
) for several families
\mathcal{P}
of perturbation distributions.

4
Safe Convex Approximations for Several Families of Perturbation Distributions

We consider two different families of perturbation distributions– (i) the family whose upper
\&
lower mean bounds are only known to us, and (ii) the family whose upper
\&
lower mean bounds, and the standard deviations are known to us.

To derive the safe approximations we use some generating functions
\gamma(t)
which are greater or equal to
\chi(t)
everywhere and satisfy the properties given earlier.

4.1
Family of Perturbation Distributions with Known Upper
\&
Lower Mean Bounds

In this case we consider the family of
\bm{\zeta}=[\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\dots,\zeta_{n}]^{\top}
with the upper
\&
lower bounds of each
\zeta_{j}
,
(j=1,2,\dots,n)
are known to be
m_{j}^{U}

\&

m_{j}^{L}
respectively. For deriving the safe convex approximation we use a piecewise linear function and the exponential function as the generating functions.

4.1.1
Piecewise Linear Generating Function Based Approximation

The function

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\gamma(t)=[1+t]_{+}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{ for }t\leq-1\\
1+t&\mbox{ for }t>-1\end{array}\right.\end{aligned}

(16)

is everywhere greater than or equal to
\chi(t)
. Further, it
is a non-negative and non-decreasing convex function satisfying
\gamma(0)\geq 1
and
\lim_{t\to-\infty}\gamma(t)=0
and hence can be considered as a generating function.

Figure 1:
Piecewise Linear Generating Function

Now since
E\left[\chi(\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x})\right]\leq E\left[\gamma(\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x})\right]
by eq. (
11
), it follows that we can replace the chance constraint (
10
) by the constraint given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\qquad E_{\bm{\zeta\sim P}}\left[\gamma(\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x)\right]\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

(17)

By using the definition of
\gamma(.)
given by (
16
), the constraint (
17
) can be written as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\qquad E_{\bm{\zeta\sim P}}\left[\max\left\{0,1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x\right\}\right]\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

(18)

The constraint (
18
) can be replaced with two equivalent constraints,

\displaystyle E_{\bm{\zeta\sim P}}\left[0\right]\leq 1-\beta

(19a)

\displaystyle E_{\bm{\zeta\sim P}}\left[1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x\right]\leq 1-\beta

(19b)

Here the constraint (
19a
) is trivial, as by the assumption of chance constraint problems the value of confidence level
\beta
lies between
0
and
1
, and hence we have
1-\beta\geq 0
. Therefore now we only need to proceed with the constraint (
19b
).

The left hand side of (
19b
) can be simplified in component form as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{\bm{\zeta\sim P}}\left[1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x\right]&=E_{{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}}\left[1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}}\right]\\
&=1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}}-E_{{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}{(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}}\right]\\
&=1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})E_{{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}}\left[\zeta_{j}\right]}\end{aligned}

(20)

It is to be noted that
\zeta_{j}
’s are the random variables in eq. (
20
), so the terms without
\zeta_{j}
are treated as constants.

Now our objective is to find out an upper bound of the right hand side of eq. (
20
), which would be our
\psi^{+}(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})
given in Theorem
3.1
. Since the upper
\&
lower mean bounds each perturbation
\zeta_{j}
in the family are known to be
m_{j}^{U}

\&

m_{j}^{L}
respectively, that is,
m_{j}^{L}\leq E_{{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}}\left[\zeta_{j}\right]\leq m_{j}^{U}
, it follows from eq. (
20
) that,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{\bm{\zeta\sim P}}\left[1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x\right]\leq 1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}}\end{aligned}

(21)

Thus by taking
\psi^{+}(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})=1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}}
, the condition in Theorem
3.1
is satisfied and the constraint

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}}\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

can be replaced with the chance constraint.

Therefore a safe approximation of the robust counterpart to the portfolio model (
7
) is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\min_{x_{i}}&&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\\
&\textrm{s.t.:}&&1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}}\leq 1-\beta,\\
&&&\sum_{j=1}^{n}{x_{j}}=1,\quad{x_{j}}\geq 0,\ j=1,2,\dots,n.\end{aligned}

(22)

The robust counterpart model (
22
) is a quadratic programming problem, so it is computationally tractable.

The above result can be represented as the following theorem:

Theorem 4.1

Consider the ambiguous chance constrained portfolio optimization model (
7
), in which the probability distribution of the perturbations
\zeta_{j}
,
(j=1,2,\dots,n)
are not known precisely, and the only information regarding each
\zeta_{j}
is that it belongs to a family of perturbations having known upper
\&
lower mean bounds
m_{j}^{U}

\&

m_{j}^{L}
respectively. Then a safe tractable convex approximation to this model can be a quadratic programming problem given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\min_{x_{i}}&&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\\
&\textrm{s.t.:}&&1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}}\leq 1-\beta,\\
&&&\sum_{j=1}^{n}{x_{j}}=1,\quad{x_{j}}\geq 0,\ j=1,2,\dots,n.\end{aligned}

4.1.2
Bernstein Approximation

One of the first studies of the Bernstein approximation scheme was done by Nemirovski and Shapiro
Nemirovski 2007
. The scheme has two assumptions:

(i)
\mathcal{P}
can be written as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\left\{P_{1}\times P_{2}\times\dots\times P_{n}:P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{j},\ j=1,2,\dots,n\right\}

where for
j=1,2,\dots,n
, the distributions
{P}_{j}
are independent and belong to the families
\mathcal{P}_{j}
of distributions.

(ii) The moment generating functions
M_{j}(t)=E[e^{t\zeta_{j}}]=\int e^{tz}dP_{j}(z)

(j=1,2,\dots,n)
are finite valued for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and are efficiently computable.

The generating function for this approximation is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \gamma(t)=e^{t}\end{aligned}

(23)

which is everywhere greater than or equal to
\chi(t)
. Further, it can be easily verified that
e^{t}
satisfies the properties of generating function.

Exponential Generating Function

By using the definition of
\gamma(.)
given by (
23
), the constraint (
17
) can be written as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &E_{{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}}\left[e^{\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x}}\right]\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

(24)

Taking the logarithm in both sides of the constraint (
24
) we get,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &E_{{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}}\left[\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x}\right]\leq\log(1-\beta)\end{aligned}

(25)

Now the left hand side of (
25
) can be simplified in component form as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}\left[{\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x}}\right]&=E_{\bm{\zeta_{j}}\sim P_{j}}\left[{\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}}\right]\\
&=\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})E_{\bm{\zeta_{j}}\sim P_{j}}\left[\zeta_{j}\right]\end{aligned}

(26)

Now our objective is to find out an upper bound of the right hand side of eq. (
26
), which would be our
\psi^{+}(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})
given in Theorem
3.1
. Since we have,
m_{j}^{L}\leq E_{{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}}\left[\zeta_{j}\right]\leq m_{j}^{U}
, it follows from eq. (
26
) that,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}\left[{\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x}}\right]&\leq\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}\end{aligned}

(27)

Then we get
\psi^{+}(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})=\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}
and thus the safe approximation of the chance constraint becomes,

\displaystyle\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}\leq\log(1-\beta)

Therefore a safe approximation of the robust counterpart to the portfolio model (
7
) is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\min_{x_{i}}&&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\\
&\textrm{s.t.:}&&\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}}\leq\log(1-\beta),\\
&&&\sum_{j=1}^{n}{x_{j}}=1,\quad{x_{j}}\geq 0,\ j=1,2,\dots,n.\end{aligned}

(28)

The robust counterpart model (
28
) is a quadratic programming problem, so it is computationally tractable.

The above result can be represented as the following theorem:

Theorem 4.2

Consider the ambiguous chance constrained portfolio optimization model (
7
), in which the probability distribution of the perturbations
\zeta_{j}
,
(j=1,2,\dots,n)
are not known precisely, and the only information regarding each
\zeta_{j}
is that it belongs to a family of perturbations having known upper
\&
lower mean bounds
m_{j}^{U}

\&

m_{j}^{L}
respectively. Then a safe tractable convex approximation to this model can be a quadratic programming problem given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\min_{x_{i}}&&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\\
&\textrm{s.t.:}&&\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}}\leq\log(1-\beta),\\
&&&\sum_{j=1}^{n}{x_{j}}=1,\quad{x_{j}}\geq 0,\ j=1,2,\dots,n.\end{aligned}

4.2
Family of Perturbation Distributions with Known Upper
\&
Lower Mean Bounds and Standard Deviations

In this case we consider the family of
\bm{\zeta}=[\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\dots,\zeta_{n}]^{\top}
with the upper
\&
lower bounds of each
\zeta_{j}
,
(j=1,2,\dots,n)
are known to be
m_{j}^{U}

\&

m_{j}^{L}
respectively and the standard deviation is known to be
s_{j}
. For deriving the safe convex approximation we use a piecewise quadratic generating function.

4.2.1
Piecewise Quadratic Generating Function Based Approximation

The function

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \gamma(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{ for }t\leq-1\\
(1+t)^{2}&\mbox{ for }t>-1\end{array}\right.\end{aligned}

(29)

is everywhere greater than or equal to
\chi(t)
. Further, it
is a non-negative and non-decreasing convex function satisfying
\gamma(0)\geq 1
and
\lim_{t\to-\infty}\gamma(t)=0
and hence can be considered as a generating function.

Figure 3:
Piecewise Quadratic Generating Function

We can also rewrite
\gamma(t)
given in (
29
) as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\gamma(t)=(1+t)\cdot[1+t]_{+}=(1+t)\cdot\max\left\{0,1+t\right\}\end{aligned}

(30)

Similar to the previous two approximations, let’s begin with the constraint

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &E_{\bm{\zeta\sim P}}\left[\gamma(\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x)\right]\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

(31)

By the use of (
30
), the constraint (
31
) becomes,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &E_{\bm{\zeta\sim P}}\left[(1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x)\cdot\max\left\{0,(1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x)\right\}\right]\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

(32)

Now here arises two cases for
\max\left\{0,1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x\right\}
.

Case I:
When
\max\left\{0,1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x\right\}=0

Then the constraint (
32
) becomes,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{\bm{\zeta\sim P}}\left[0\right]\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

Case II:
When
\max\left\{0,1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x\right\}=1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x

Then the constraint (
32
) becomes,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{\bm{\zeta\sim P}}\left[1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x\right]^{2}\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

So the constraint (
32
) can be replaced by two equivalent constraints,

\displaystyle E_{\bm{\zeta\sim P}}\left[0\right]\leq 1-\beta

(33a)

\displaystyle E_{\bm{\zeta\sim P}}\left[1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}x\right]^{2}\leq 1-\beta

(33b)

Here the constraint (
33a
) is trivial, as by the assumption of chance constraint problems we have
1-\beta\geq 0
. Therefore now we only need to proceed with the constraint (
33b
).

Now the left hand side of (
33b
) can be simplified in component form as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}\left[1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x}\right]^{2}&=E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}\left[1+\tau-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}\right)\right]^{2}\\
&=E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}\left[(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j})-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}\right]^{2}\\
&=E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}\left[\left(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}\right)^{2}\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.-2\left(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}\right)\right]\end{aligned}

(34)

Since in our problem the perturbations
\zeta_{j}
’s are the random variables, the terms without
\zeta_{j}
can be treated as constants. So the eq. (
34
) can be written as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &E_{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}\left[1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x}\right]^{2}=&&(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j})^{2}+E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}\right)\right]^{2}\\
&&&-2\left(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}\right)\cdot E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}\right]\end{aligned}

(35)

Now our objective is to find out an upper bound of the right hand side of eq. (
35
), which would be our
\psi^{+}(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})
given in Theorem
3.1
. For this, we use the following lemma.

Lemma 1

Let
\zeta_{j},\ j=1,2,\dots,n
be independently distributed random variables, each with its upper
\&
lower mean bounds are respectively
m_{j}^{U}

\&

m_{j}^{L}
and its standard deviation is
s_{j}
. Then we have,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(i)&&\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}\leq E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}\right]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{U},\\
&&&\text{ and, }\\
&(ii)&&\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})^{2}s_{j}^{2}+\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}\right]^{2}\leq E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}\right]^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})^{2}s_{j}^{2}+\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{U}\right]^{2}\end{aligned}

Proof

We first denote the random variable
\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}
by
Y
. So our objective is to find out the bounds of
E\left[Y\right]
and
E\left[Y^{2}\right]
.

Now the mean of
Y
is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E\left[Y\right]&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\cdot E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}\left[\zeta_{j}\right]\end{aligned}

(36)

Using the fact that
m_{j}^{L}\leq E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}\left[\zeta_{j}\right]\leq m_{j}^{U}
in (
36
), we get,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}\leq E\left[Y\right]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{U},\end{aligned}

(37)

which proves (i).

Now the variance of
Y
is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} Var\left[Y\right]&=Var\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}\right]\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})^{2}\cdot Var\left[\zeta_{j}\right]+2\cdot\displaystyle{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}}(\mu_{i}^{(i)}x_{i})(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\cdot Cov(\zeta_{i},\zeta_{j})\end{aligned}

(38)

Since
\zeta_{j}
’s are independently distributed random variables, we have
Cov(\zeta_{i},\zeta_{j})=0
, which reduces (
38
) to,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} Var\left[Y\right]&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})^{2}s_{j}^{2}\end{aligned}

(39)

Again from the variance formula we have,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E\left[Y^{2}\right]=Var\left[Y\right]+\left(E\left[Y\right]\right)^{2}\end{aligned}

(40)

Using (
37
) and (
39
) in (
40
), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})^{2}s_{j}^{2}+\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}\right]^{2}\leq E\left[Y\right]^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})^{2}s_{j}^{2}+\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{U}\right]^{2},\end{aligned}

(41)

and this proves (ii).
∎

Now applying the results (i) and (ii) of Lemma
1
in eq. (
35
) we get,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}\left[1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x}\right]^{2}\leq&\left(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}\right)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})^{2}s_{j}^{2}+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{U}\right)^{2}\\
&\qquad-2(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j})\cdot\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}\right)\end{aligned}

(42)

Then we get
\psi^{+}(\bm{\mu},\bm{x})=\left(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}\right)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})^{2}s_{j}^{2}+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{U}\right)^{2}-2(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j})\cdot\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}\right)
and thus the safe approximation of the chance constraint becomes,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}\right)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})^{2}s_{j}^{2}+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{U}\right)^{2}-2(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j})\cdot\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq 1-\beta\end{aligned}

Therefore a safe approximation of the robust counterpart to the portfolio model (
7
) is given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\min_{x_{i}}&&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\\
&\textrm{s.t.:}&&\left(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j}\right)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})^{2}s_{j}^{2}+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{U}\right)^{2}\\
&&&\qquad\qquad\qquad-2(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j})\cdot\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})m_{j}^{L}\right)\leq 1-\beta,\\
&&&\sum_{j=1}^{n}{x_{j}}=1,\quad{x_{j}}\geq 0,\ j=1,2,\dots,n.\end{aligned}

(43)

The robust counterpart model (
43
) is a quadratically constrained quadratic programming (QCQP) problem, so it is computationally tractable.

The above result can be represented as the following theorem:

4.3
Extension to Higher Orders

In Section
4.1
, the considered perturbation family has the information regarding first-moment statistics of the distribution, that is, the mean. On the other hand, the perturbation family in Section
4.2
has information regarding the first two moments of the distribution, that is, the mean and the variance (or the standard deviation). A piecewise linear function (
16
) is used to derive the safe convex approximation for the former family, whereas a piecewise quadratic function (
29
) is used for the latter family.

Inductively, if we have the information regarding the first
n
moments of the perturbation distribution, then we can use the piecewise generating function of degree
n
given by,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \gamma(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{ for }t\leq-1\\
(1+t)^{n}&\mbox{ for }t>-1\end{array}\right.\end{aligned}

(44)

to derive the safe convex approximation.

This is because, while deriving the left hand side of the chance constraint componentwise by using the above generating function, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &E_{\bm{\zeta}\sim P}\left[1+\tau-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\zeta})^{\top}\bm{x}\right]^{n}=&&E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}\left[(1+\tau-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}^{(0)}x_{j})-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mu_{j}^{(j)}x_{j})\zeta_{j}\right]^{n}\end{aligned}

(45)

After applying the binomial expansion to the right hand side of eq. (
45
), we require the information regarding the values of
E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}[\zeta_{j}]
,
E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}[\zeta_{j}^{2}]
,
\dots
,
E_{\zeta_{j}\sim P_{j}}[\zeta_{j}^{n}]
to derive the safe convex approximation of the uncertain model. Therefore the generating function (
44
) can be applied for the family of perturbations, whose first
n
moments’ information is known.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we derive safe convex approximations for the ambiguous chance constraint based uncertain portfolio optimization problems. Our study comprises two families of perturbation distributions– (i) family with known upper
\&
lower mean bounds, and (ii) family with known upper
\&
lower mean bounds and standard deviations. We use a piecewise linear function and an exponential function as the generator to derive the safe approximation for the former case, whereas a piecewise quadratic function is used for the latter case. The approximations which we get from family-i are quadratic programming problems and the approximation for family-ii is a quadratically constrained quadratic programming problem. Thus these approximations are computationally tractable and by solving them we get the robust solutions to our original ambiguous chance constrained problems. Moreover, we solve a numerical problem by the use of these approximations and we plot the optimal allocations and efficient frontiers for each case. From the two approximations of family-ii, it is observed that the optimal portfolio risks for the Bernstein approximation are higher than that of the piecewise linear generator based approximations.

Furthermore, our study can be used for any optimization problem with the linear chance constraint(s). In this study, we use piecewise linear and quadratic functions as the generators for deriving the safe approximations. For the derivation of piecewise linear generator based approximations, we require the information regarding the mean bounds of the perturbations, whereas for piecewise quadratic generator based approximations we need the standard deviation (or variance) information of the perturbations along with their mean bounds. Similarly, if we have the information regarding the first
n
moment statistics of the perturbations, then we can extend our study to use piecewise generating functions of any degree
n
, where
n
is a positive integer.
