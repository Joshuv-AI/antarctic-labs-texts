Group field theory on quantum groups
Florian Girelli, Matteo Laudonio

Abstract
We introduce the framework of Hopf algebra field theory (HAFT) which generalizes the notion of group field theory to the quantum group (Hopf algebra) case. We focus in particular on the 3d case and show how the HAFT we considered is topological. The highlight of the construction is the notion of plane-wave which leads, in the specific example of SU q ​ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}_{q}(2) with q q real, to a discretization of the Euclidian B ​ F BF action with a negative cosmological constant. This work can be viewed as the generalization of the seminal work by Baratin and Oriti [ 1 ] : we have a (non-commutative) formulation of simplicial 3d gravity in the presence of a non-zero cosmological constant.

The reason is that group field theories have a configuration and a momentum formulation which correspond to formulating the model in the triangulation picture or in its dual complex. The group field theory approach on top of conveniently generating the different triangulations (or dual complex) as Feynman diagrams, emphasizes the fact that a pair of dual Hopf algebras are actually at play, one decorating the triangulation, the other one decorating the dual complex. First we define the notion of

3
Hopf algebra field theory

In this section we describe what we will call a Hopf algebra field theory (HAFT). This is a field theory based on Hopf algebras which can be seen as a generalization of ordinary group field theories

We call three dimensional HAFT the field theory which Feynman diagrams can be seen as dual of a three dimensional triangulation. This will be the focus of our discussion. It can be generalized in a direct manner to any dimensions.

HAFT is meant to be a model about discrete geometries.

4.2.1

Let us focus on the Euclidian case with a negative or null cosmological constant

The fundamental fields are the frame field

The frame field variables are difficult to quantize since they are valued in the boosts. It was shown in

Let us emphasize that this action depends on two constants, Newton’s constant

When looking at the action (

In terms of the underlying symmetry structure, the actions (

\displaystyle\mathfrak{su}(2)\bowtie\mathfrak{an}(2)\,\,\,\rightarrow\,\,\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{su}(2)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad\lambda=0\\

In terms of the action, we can write (

We have therefore the two types of limiting actions

where in the last case, we omitted the boundary term and we recall that in this case,

\mathbb{R}^{3}

5
Conclusion

We proposed a model based on Hopf algebras that provides a (3d) topological state sum. The formulation of the model is a generalization of the notion group field theories, where the fundamental degrees of freedom are encoded in a gauge invariant field and the Feynman diagram amplitudes are expressed as a sum over the amplitudes associated to three dimensional geometries.

The field theory is discussed both in the configuration and momentum spaces, and a map (generalized Fourier transform) between the two is provided. The object used as the kernel of such transformation is a generalized notion of plane-wave given by the canonical element of
D^{*}(A,H,\sigma)
.
It is the first time, to the best of our knowledge, that such canonical element takes a central place in the definition of of a topological model and is used to provide a discretization of the
BF
action.

The main scope of this work was to construct a field theory generating curved discrete geometries as Feynman diagrams, in such a way that the amplitudes would provide a discretization of the
BF
action with non-vanishing cosmological constant. In this sense, HAFT provides a non-commutative formulation of simplicial 3d gravity. The non-commutativity appears due to the homogeneous curvature induced by the cosmological constant (and also due to the Planck length
[
1
]
). We emphasize that the discretization is not done at the level of the action but instead at the level of the amplitude
e^{i{\cal S}^{\lambda}_{BF}}
.

Let us mention several directions that we would find interesting to explore.

Relation with integrable systems.

The canonical element/plane-wave associated to the dual Hopf algebras also appeared in the context of integrable systems under the shape of the transfer/transport matrix, or T-matrix
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
. In this context, we consider a lattice which sites are associated with a T-matrix
\sigma
. It can be written as

\sigma=e^{x\cdot k}\,,

(141)

where
L=x\cdot k
originates from a Lax pair and the
x^{i}
are the dynamical variables, which can be seen as generating a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
, while the
k_{i}
are the generators of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{k}
. We can multiply the T-matrices sitting at different sites, with dynamical variables
x
and
x^{\prime}
to obtain a new one
x^{\prime\prime}=x\oplus x^{\prime}
. This is precisely the structure we would expect from a plane-wave. The dynamical variables are equipped with a Poisson bracket which induces a Poisson structure for the T-matrix. We expect then that the Poisson bracket (associated with the dynamical variables) should be compatible with the product of the T-matrices. Since there is an obvious symmetry in (
141
), we can make the same type of construction for the
k
sector.
The main features of the T-matrix are captured by the expected plane-wave properties

\displaystyle\sigma_{x,k_{1}}\sigma_{x,k_{2}}=e^{ix\cdot k_{1}}e^{ix\cdot k_{2}}=e^{ix\cdot(k_{1}\oplus k_{2})}\equiv\sigma_{x,(k_{1}\oplus k_{2})}\,,

(142)

\displaystyle\sigma_{x_{1},k}\sigma_{x_{2},k}=e^{ix_{1}\cdot k}e^{ix_{2}\cdot k}=e^{i(x_{1}\oplus x_{2})\cdot k}\equiv\sigma_{(x_{1}\oplus x_{2})\cdot k}\,,

such that the product is a Poisson map.
Note that, at the quantum level, by performing some projection in terms of representations, we can recover from the T-matrix the notion of
R
-matrix
[
15
]
which also play a fundamental role in the study of integrable systems.
It would be interesting to explore whether the fact that this common structure appears both for integrable systems and 3d gravity could clarify some interesting questions, such as holography for example.

Generalization to quasi Hopf algebras.

Our construction relied on (co-associative) Hopf algebras. The original TV model is defined for quasi Hopf algebras, such as for the deformation at
q
root of unity of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
. It would be interesting to explore how our proposed framework extends to this quasi case. As a starting point we could explore how twisting the Drinfeld double and weakening the co-associativity would modify our construction.

Introducing matter.

Krasnov has argued that to introduce matter, one should not only consider a field on some copies of
H=F(\mathrm{SU}(2))
as we did, but instead on the full Drinfeld double
H=F(\mathrm{SU}(2))\rtimes{\cal U}(\mathfrak{su}(2))

[
16
]
.
It would be interesting to see how this is implemented in our general framework so that we could deal with particles coupled to gravity in the case where the cosmological constant is not zero. Along a similar line, it is well understood now that the Kitaev model is equivalent to a
BF
theory with particle excitations, in particular the fundamental symmetry is given by the Drinfeld double. In
[
22
]
, some abstract Fourier transform was discussed, for the specific case of a quadrangulation which is therefore self-dual. It would be interesting to check how our proposal of Fourier transform could be applied in this set up.

Four dimensional model: quantum 2-groups.

Despite the HAFT can be easily generalized to the
d
dimensional case, we discussed the three dimensional version of the model for two reasons.
First, in 3
d
it is easier to visualize the underlying geometric objects. Second, if we were to consider the 4d case, instead of using Hopf algebras we could/should use categorified Hopf algebras according to the categorical ladder
[
33
]
.
In particular, in 4d we expect to have triangulation faces, as dual to the links of the dual complex to be decorated. Without any edge decoration, it is actually not possible to decorate faces by non-abelian group elements (eg at the classical level) due to the Eckmann-Hilton argument
[
34
]
. This therefore prevents the description of a discrete curved geometry unless we also decorate the edges. A 2-group or crossed module or their deformation appears therefore as the natural structure to describe discrete curved geometries in 4d. Even though there already exists a proposal for the notion quantum 2-group (see
[
35
]
) the relevant structures and duality properties (such as a Fourier transform for 2-groups) to extend our construction are not clear yet. Note that some recent work has extended the notion of GFT when using 2-groups instead of groups
[
36
]
. It is therefore an interesting question to determine what is the notion of plane-wave when dealing with 2-groups.

Inverse of the Fourier transform: Prop.
2

We prove Prop.

Here we first use both the definitions of Fourier transform and inverse

(\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{F}^{-1})[\hat{\Phi}]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\left(\int_{H}^{L}\otimes\,\textrm{id}\right)\,\big(\sigma\cdot(\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\hat{\Phi}]\otimes 1)\big)=\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\int_{H}^{L}\otimes\int_{A}^{L}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,\big(\sigma_{13}\cdot\sigma_{12}^{-1}\cdot(1\otimes\hat{\Phi}\otimes 1)\big)\,.

(143)

Then, using the definition of inverse skew co-pairing (
36

(\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{F}^{-1})[\hat{\Phi}]=\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\int_{H}^{L}\otimes\int_{A}^{L}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(\textrm{id}\otimes S\otimes\textrm{id})\,\big((1\otimes S^{-1}\hat{\Phi}\otimes 1)\cdot(\sigma_{13}\cdot\sigma_{12})\big)\,.

(144)

Since the co-integral of
H
of the product of co-pairing
\sigma_{13}\cdot\sigma_{12}
can be written as the co-product of the delta function of
A

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\left(\int_{H}^{L}\otimes\textrm{id}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(\sigma_{13}\cdot\sigma_{12})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\left(\int_{H}^{L}\otimes\Delta\right)\,\sigma=\Delta\hat{\delta}_{A}\,,

(145)

one can use the first of the identities (
55

(\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{F}^{-1})[\hat{\Phi}]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\left(\int_{A}^{L}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(S\otimes\textrm{id})\,\big((S^{-1}\hat{\Phi}\otimes 1)\cdot\Delta\hat{\delta}_{A}\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\left(\int_{A}^{L}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(S\otimes\textrm{id})\,\big((1\otimes\hat{\Phi})\cdot\Delta\hat{\delta}_{A}\big)\,.

(146)

Last, since the antipode is an anti-homomorphism map, is passes through the co-product, which becomes
\Delta_{21}
. This allows us to use the left invariance property of the co-integral and compute it using the normalization property of the delta function (
56

(\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{F}^{-1})[\hat{\Phi}]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\left(\int_{A}^{L}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,\big(\Delta_{-21}(S\hat{\delta}_{A})\cdot(1\otimes\hat{\Phi})\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\left(\int_{A}^{L}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,\big((S\hat{\delta}_{A}\otimes 1)\cdot(1\otimes\hat{\Phi})\big)=\hat{\Phi}\,.

(147)

Similarly, we can also prove the identity
(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{F})=\textrm{id}
.

\displaystyle(\hat{\Phi}\otimes 1)\cdot\Delta\hat{\delta}_{A}

Proposition 14

(Integral property of the skew co-pairing element)
.

Let
H
and
A
be skew co-paired bi-algebras with invertible skew co-pairing. It satisfies the identities below.

\begin{aligned} &\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,\sigma=\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(\sigma^{\pm 1}_{23}\cdot\sigma_{13})\,,\\
&\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,\sigma^{-1}=\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(\sigma^{-1}_{13}\cdot\sigma^{\pm 1}_{23})\,,\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} &\left(\textrm{id}\otimes\int_{A}\right)\,\sigma=\left(\textrm{id}\otimes\textrm{id}\otimes\int_{A}\right)\,(\sigma_{13}\cdot\sigma^{\pm 1}_{12})\,,\\
&\left(\textrm{id}\otimes\int_{A}\right)\,\sigma^{-1}=\left(\textrm{id}\otimes\textrm{id}\otimes\int_{A}\right)\,(\sigma^{\pm 1}_{12}\cdot\sigma^{-1}_{13})\,.\end{aligned}

(154)

Proof.

Consider the first of the identities (
154
). We prove it simply using the property (
35
) and the left invariance of the co-integral on
H
.

\displaystyle\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,\sigma

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(\sigma_{23}^{-1}\cdot\sigma_{23}\cdot\sigma_{13})

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(\sigma_{23}^{-1}\cdot(\Delta_{21}\otimes\textrm{id})\,\sigma)=\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(\sigma_{23}^{-1}\cdot\sigma_{13})\,.

(155)

Alternatively, using the same properties, we have

\displaystyle\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,\sigma

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(\sigma_{23}\cdot\sigma^{-1}_{23}\cdot\sigma_{13})=\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(\sigma_{23}\cdot(\textrm{id}\otimes S^{-1}\otimes\textrm{id})\,(\sigma_{23}\cdot\sigma_{13}))

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(\sigma_{23}\cdot(\textrm{id}\otimes S^{-1}\otimes\textrm{id})\circ(\Delta_{21}\otimes\textrm{id})\,\sigma)=\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}\otimes\textrm{id}\right)\,(\sigma_{23}\cdot\sigma_{13})\,.

(156)

The other three identities in (

A.2
Geometric computations

Let us provide here some useful results which will be used later in this section. The computations below are strictly related to the geometric interpretation of Hopf algebra field theory, as a path integral formulation of a model of discrete curved geometries.

We remind that the closure constraint (
63
) represents the closure of the boundary of a triangle.
Let us use the tensor notation
\hat{{\cal C}}=\hat{{\cal C}}_{1\,2\,3}\in A^{3}
.
We list below some propositions involving the geometric operations on the closure constraint that we will need in Hopf algebra field theory, such as the cyclic permutation of the edges, inversion or gluing of two triangles.

Proposition 15

(Cyclic permutation and inversion properties of a the closure constraint)
.

The closure constraint (
63
) is invariant under cyclic permutation of its elements and under inversion.

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal C}}_{1\,2\,3}=\hat{{\cal C}}_{-3\,-2\,-1}\,,

(158)

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal C}}_{1\,2\,3}=\hat{{\cal C}}_{2\,3\,1}\,.

Moreover, the closure constraint is a projector,

\hat{{\cal C}}\cdot\hat{{\cal C}}=\hat{{\cal C}}\,.

(159)

Proof.

Let us prove first the invariance under the inversion.
We carry out the computation just using the definition of closure constraint (
63
) and that of opposite delta function (
54
), plus the left invariance of the co-integral on
H
.

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal C}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{H}}\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}^{\otimes 3}\right)\,\big((\textrm{id}\otimes\Delta^{3})\,\sigma^{-1}\big)=\frac{1}{V_{H}}\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}^{\otimes 3}\right)\,\big(\sigma^{-1}_{12}\sigma^{-1}_{13}\sigma^{-1}_{14}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{H}^{2}}\left(\int_{H^{2}}\otimes\textrm{id}^{\otimes 3}\right)\,\big((\sigma^{-1}_{13}\sigma^{-1}_{14}\sigma^{-1}_{15})\cdot(\sigma^{-1}_{23}\sigma^{-1}_{24}\sigma^{-1}_{25})\cdot(\sigma_{25}\sigma_{24}\sigma_{23})\big)\qquad\text{\tiny{use \eqref{SkewEl_CoProduct-id} and the left invariance}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{H}^{2}}\left(\int_{H^{2}}\otimes\textrm{id}^{\otimes 3}\right)\,\big((\sigma^{-1}_{13}\sigma^{-1}_{14}\sigma^{-1}_{15})\cdot(\sigma_{25}\sigma_{24}\sigma_{23})\big)\qquad\text{\tiny{use \eqref{SkewEl_CoProduct-id} with the antipode and the left invariance}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{H}^{2}}\left(\int_{H^{2}}\otimes\textrm{id}^{\otimes 3}\right)\,\big(\sigma_{25}\sigma_{24}\sigma_{23}\big)=\frac{1}{V_{H}}\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}^{\otimes 3}\right)\,\big(\sigma_{14}\sigma_{13}\sigma_{12}\big)=S^{3}\circ\tau^{3}\,\hat{{\cal C}}=\hat{{\cal C}}_{-3\,-2\,-1}\,.

(160)

We can prove in a similar way the invariance under cyclic permutations by multiplying the closure constraint by the properly chosen identity.

The fact that the product of two closure constraints reduces to a single one, eq. (
159
), is expected since the closure constraint is the Fourier transform of a projector. We carry out the proof writing the closure constraints in terms of skew co-pairing elements and then using the left invariance of the co-integral on
H
.

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal C}}\cdot\hat{{\cal C}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{V_{H}^{2}}\big(\Delta^{3}\hat{\delta}_{A}^{-1}\cdot\Delta^{3}\hat{\delta}_{A}^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{H}^{2}}\left(\int_{H^{2}}\otimes\textrm{id}^{\otimes 3}\right)\,\big((\sigma^{-1}_{13}\sigma^{-1}_{14}\sigma^{-1}_{15})\cdot(\sigma^{-1}_{23}\sigma^{-1}_{24}\sigma^{-1}_{25})\big)\qquad\text{\tiny{use \eqref{SkewEl_CoProduct-id_inv} and the left invariance}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{H}^{2}}\left(\int_{H^{2}}\otimes\textrm{id}^{\otimes 3}\right)\,\big(\sigma^{-1}_{13}\sigma^{-1}_{14}\sigma^{-1}_{15}\big)=\frac{1}{V_{H}}\left(\int_{H}\otimes\textrm{id}^{\otimes 3}\right)\,\big(\sigma^{-1}_{12}\sigma^{-1}_{14}\sigma^{-1}_{15}\big)=\hat{{\cal C}}\,.

(161)

∎

Triangulation picture.

The definition of Feynman amplitude of a given triangulation in the

Dual complex picture.

The amplitude of the Feynman diagrams of Hopf algebra field theory in the dual complex is more subtle. We consider the combination of an arbitrary number of tetrahedron amplitudes (
71

74
) such that they share a single edge, as in Prop.

The resulting amplitude encodes the local curvature around the edge, and the overall Hopf algebra field theory Feynman amplitude of a dual complex is given by the sum over all the edges of the triangulation, of such local curvatures.

The vertex amplitude is given in terms of the co-product
\Delta_{{\cal V}}

24

The gluing that we are interested in, involves only one loop of solid blue and red links of Fig.

in the kernel (

\Delta

Use the property (

{\cal Z}=\prod_{\{{\cal L}_{N}\}}\,\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu^{2N+1}}\,V_{H}^{N}}\,\left(\int_{H^{2N}}\otimes\int_{A}\right)\,\big(\Delta^{2N}_{1\,-2\,3\,-4\,\dots\,(2N-1)\,-2N}\otimes\textrm{id}\big)\,\sigma\,.

\displaystyle\approx\Phi^{u}+\frac{2i\lambda}{q^{2}-1}\sum_{a=0}^{u-1}\,(-i\lambda)^{u-1}(-1)^{a}\,\psi=\Phi^{u}-2\frac{(-i\lambda)^{u}}{q^{2}-1}\frac{1-(-1)^{u}}{2}\psi\,.

(230)

Above we used the symbol
:\,:
to address at the normal order of the basis.
Plugging the results in the
[X_{+}\,\overset{\star}{,}\,X_{-}]
commutator, we get

\displaystyle[X_{+}\,\overset{\star}{,}\,X_{-}]

\displaystyle=\delta_{v,1}\delta_{w,1}\delta_{u,0}\,\sigma\big(X_{+}\wedge X_{-}\,,\,(\varphi_{+}\otimes\varphi_{-}+\varphi_{-}\otimes\varphi_{+})\big)\,X_{-}^{v}H^{u}X_{+}^{w}

\displaystyle\quad-\delta_{v,0}\delta_{w,0}\sum_{u=0}^{\infty}\frac{i^{u}}{u!}\frac{(-i\lambda)^{u}\ell^{2}}{q^{2}-1}\frac{1-(-1)^{u}}{2}\,\sigma\big(X_{+}\wedge X_{-}\,,\,\varphi_{-}\wedge\varphi_{+}\big)\,X_{-}^{v}H^{u}X_{+}^{w}

\displaystyle=\ell^{2}q^{-1}\frac{\sinh(\lambda H)}{\sinh(\ell\lambda)}\,.

(231)

∎

Appendix D
Further examples

In order to make contact with more familiar versions of group field theories, we consider here some explicit realizations of Hopf algebra field theory.
In the first example we pick the Hopf algebras associated to a finite group; this choice reduces the Hopf algebra field theory to ordinary group field theory based on finite groups.
In the second example we show how to derive ordinary three dimensional group field theories
[
19
,
13
]
based on the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
Lie group. This example can be alternatively derived as the limiting case of the group field theory based on the
q
-deformation of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
presented in Sec.

D.1
Finite Groups

Let
G
a finite group of cardinality
|G|
,
F(G)
the algebra of functions on
G
and
\mathbb{C}[G]
the group algebra constructed as the vector space generated by the span of the group elements
g\in G
. Since the generators of the algebra
\mathbb{C}[G]
are group elements

, any element of the group algebra can be written as the linear combination
\mathbb{C}[G]\ni\hat{f}=\sum_{u}\hat{f}(g)g
, where the coefficients
\hat{f}(g)
are regarded as functions on
G
. The
\mathbb{C}[G]
bi-algebra structure can either be given in terms of the group element

\displaystyle\text{Co-product:}

\displaystyle\Delta g=g\otimes g\,,

(232)

\displaystyle\text{Co-unit:}

\displaystyle\varepsilon g=e\,,

or alternatively in terms of the coefficients
\hat{f}(u)
:

\displaystyle\text{Product:}

\displaystyle(\hat{f}_{1}\cdot\hat{f}_{2})(g)=\sum_{v}\hat{f}_{1}(v)\hat{f}_{2}(v^{-1}g)\,,

(233)

\displaystyle\text{Co-product:}

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{f}(g,v)=\delta_{g}(v)\hat{f}(g)\,,

\displaystyle\text{Co-unit:}

\displaystyle\varepsilon\hat{f}=\sum_{g}\hat{f}(g)\,.

A basis for the algebra of functions
F(G)
is given instead by the Kronecker delta on the group
\delta_{g}
, and the bi-algebra structure, given in terms of functions
f
on the group, is

\displaystyle\text{Product:}

\displaystyle(f_{1}\cdot f_{2})(g)=f_{1}(g)\,f_{2}(g)\,,

(234)

\displaystyle\text{Co-product:}

\displaystyle\Delta f(u,v)=f(u\cdot v)\,,

\displaystyle\text{Co-unit:}

\displaystyle\varepsilon f=f(e)\,.

The co-integrals on the algebras
H
and
A
are the maps

\int_{F(G)}\equiv\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g}g:F(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{K}\,\,,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{C}[G]}\equiv\delta_{e}:\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow\mathbb{K}\,.

(235)

We used the symbol

as the identity in the group
G

\mathbb{C}[G]

D.2

We consider the Lie group
\mathrm{SU}(2)
and the associated Hopf algebras
F(SU(2))
and

The Hopf algebra

\displaystyle\text{Product:}

\displaystyle[x_{0},x_{\pm}]_{\star}=\pm 2\ell x_{\pm}\,,

\displaystyle\quad[x_{+},x_{-}]_{\star}=\ell x_{0}\,,

\displaystyle\text{Co-product:}

\displaystyle\Delta x_{a}=x_{a}\otimes 1+1\otimes x_{a}\,,

\displaystyle\text{Co-unit:}

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x_{a})=0\,,

\displaystyle\text{Antipode:}

\displaystyle S(x_{a})=-x_{a}\,,

with

Here the symbol

249

The Hopf algebra

\displaystyle\text{Product:}

\displaystyle[p_{a},p_{b}]=0\,,

(250)

\displaystyle\text{Co-product:}

\displaystyle\Delta p_{3}=p_{3}\otimes p_{0}+p_{0}\otimes p_{3}-i\ell p_{+}\wedge p_{-}\,,

\displaystyle\Delta p_{\pm}=p_{\pm}\otimes p_{0}+p_{0}\otimes p_{\pm}\pm i\ell p_{\pm}\wedge p_{3}\,,

\displaystyle\Delta p_{0}=p_{0}\otimes p_{0}+-\frac{1}{2}\ell^{2}(2p_{3}\otimes p_{3}+p_{+}\otimes p_{-}+p_{-}\otimes p_{+})\,,

\displaystyle\text{Co-unit:}

\displaystyle\varepsilon(p_{a})=0\,,

\displaystyle\text{Antipode:}

\displaystyle S(p_{a})=-p_{a}\,,

with
p_{0}=\sqrt{1-\ell^{2}|p|^{2}}
and
p_{a}=\{p_{3},p_{\pm}\}
.
Note that by choosing
p_{0}>0
the coordinates
p_{a}
only cover half of the

with
p_{0}>0

p_{0}<0

28
]
.

and

\mathrm{SU}(2)
and by the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Boulatov group field theory is a specific example of Hopf algebra field theory with the choices

and
