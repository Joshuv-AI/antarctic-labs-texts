Large and moderate deviations in Poisson navigations
Partha Pratim Ghosh, Benedikt Jahnel, Sanjoy Kumar Jhawar

Abstract
. We derive large- and moderate-deviation results in random networks given as planar directed navigations on homogeneous Poisson point processes. In this non-Markovian routing scheme, starting from the origin, at each consecutive step a Poisson point is joined by an edge to its nearest Poisson point to the right within a cone. We establish precise exponential rates of decay for the probability that the vertical displacement of the random path is unexpectedly large. The proofs rest on controlling the dependencies of the individual steps and the randomness in the horizontal displacement as well as renewal-process arguments.



Key words and phrases.
traffic network; directed-navigation; Poisson point process; renewal process; moderate-deviation principle; large-deviation principle.

AMS 2020 Subject Classifications.
Primary: 60D05,  60G70. Secondary: 60G55, 05C80.

1.
Introduction

We consider decentralized traffic-flow networks or drainage networks, where nodes transmit data individually to dedicated neighboring nodes according to local rules, or respectively liquid flows towards a certain direction. For this, directed navigations on homogeneous Poisson point processes in the Euclidean plane can be used as an underlying network model
[
5
,
3
]
. The traffic generated at each node is forwarded iteratively to the nearest node for example to the right in horizontal direction. While analyzing such models, it has been observed in
[
9
]
, that the spatial traffic-flow density at a given location in the domain, i.e., the spatial average of accumulated traffic at a microsopic area around that point, follows a law of large numbers in the high-density limit. Here, the asymptotic traffic density can be captured as an integral in terms of the spatial intensity of the point process along with their rate of traffic generation. The bulk contribution in the limit in this asymptotics comes from the traffic generated within a thin horizontal strip at that given point due to sub-ballisticity of the trajectory of the navigation. This sub-ballistic behavior is nothing but a version of the straightness condition studied in
[
11
]
.

Going beyond sub-ballisticity and the law of large numbers, questions about the deviation behavior of paths in the traffic network arise. In a stationary setting, is it rare to have a path originating from the origin to deviate much away from the horizontal axis? For this, we denoted by
{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
the vertical position of the path measured as a function of horizontal position
t
. If the answer to the question is no, then essentially these paths are not so unlikely to miss the microscopic region around a given location and the traffic accumulated along the path may not contribute to the aggregated traffic at the location of interest. This in turn would lead to deviations in the throughput at the target location and it is an indication of unexpected network performance. On the other hand, if the answer is yes, that means that the paths do not deviate too much and contribute to the bulk of the throughput. But how rare is the deviation event? In other words, at what scale does the probability of such an event live on and what are the rates? To answer this sort of questions, in this article, we investigate the large- and moderate-deviation behavior of the deviation of these paths away from the horizontal axis in a Poisson navigation model where the consecutive
successors
are defined as the closest neighbor within a cone to the right of the current point in the Euclidean plane.

Large- and moderate-deviation principles constitute a corner stone in the analysis of random geometric models and in general in stochastic processes in the last four to five decades, well-documented for example in
[
16
,
10
,
7
]
.
These principles essentially capture rare events by quantifying their asymptotic unlikeliness. The small probability of the events is expressed in terms of an exponential rate and a rate function. In the context of telecommunication networks, large-deviation principles can be used to point out how badly the network is performing and what the most prominent reasons for this behavior are. The large-deviation principle has been studied for many different telecommunication network models, mainly identifying poor service indicator, see for example
[
12
, Chapter 6]
and references therein.
On the other hand, moderate deviations are nothing but large deviations with a slower scaling. The moderate-deviation principle for a sequence of iid centered random variables has been studied for example in
[
8
]
as a much cleaner and shorter version of
[
1
]
and for sequences of random variables from Banach space in the classic work by
[
13
]
. In case of moderate deviations, the rate function turns out to be of Gaussian type irrespective of the model under consideration, whereas in large deviations the rate function depends on characteristics of the random variables.

The model we study in this article is the underlying setup for the traffic-flow network mentioned above.
It is known as a
navigation
on Poisson point processes in Euclidean space and specifically the directed and radial navigation where introduced in
[
5
]
.
However, we introduce an additional parameter
0<\theta<\pi/2
that controls the angle of the cone in which the navigation searches for the next neighbor. By definition, if the navigation starts at multiple points, this type of navigation gives rise to a tree structure. In application to telecommunication networks, the root of the spanning trees can be seen as a network head where all the information is gathered along the edges of the tree and processed. Hence, it is worthwhile to know the local and global structural properties of the tree. In the work
[
3
]
, the quantities of interest were the local tree functionals around a vertex, for example, the degree, properties of the path in the tree from a vertex to the root, namely the total length or properties of the tree structure and its shape. In a somewhat more geometric and analytical work, the convergence of the tree to a Brownian web under the appropriate scaling limit has been studied in
[
6
]
and in
[
15
]
for the Poisson setting and in a recent work
[
14
]
for a discrete setting on perturbed lattice. In this manuscript, we in particular improve the polynomial-decay properties in
[
3
, Lemma 4.11]
to stretched-exponential decay of moderate-deviation type as well as go beyond the associated central limit theorem
[
3
, Theorem 4.7]
. Let us also mention the work
[
4
]
where navigation characteristics are investigated in a high-density regime for not necessarily homogeneous Poisson point processes.

When
\pi/4<\theta<\pi/2
, the model of navigation possesses a challenging dependence structure along its path, since each step of the navigation carries an extra piece of information from the previous steps via non-trivial regions that have been observed as void spaces for the underlying Poisson point process. We call this piece of information the
history set
and identify the steps where the history set is empty. This is only possible since
\theta<\pi/2
and gives rise to a renewal structure that is essential for our analysis. However, the tail properties of the step variables of the renewal process are challenging to analyse, which makes up for the bulk of the technical part of the manuscript.
For this, we control the exponential decay of the inter-stopping time gaps via bounds on the dynamics of the width of the history set, using Markov-chain comparison ideas from
[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
. However, this control fails to be sufficiently detailed in order to establish the large deviations in the whole parameter regime.
Indeed, the model becomes substantially simpler for
\theta\in(0,\pi/4]
as the steps become iid and we can then provide the full large- and moderate-deviation analysis with rather explicit rate functions.

Organization of the article: In the following Section
2
, we describe the model in detail, state the main results about large and moderate-deviation principles and the scaling property of the rate functions with respect to the intensity of the Poisson point process. In Section
3
, we first uncover the hidden independence structure in the model and state the key supporting results that enables us to prove the main result for the moderate deviations as well as the large-deviation result in the restricted parameter setting. Section
4
contains the proofs of all the supporting results that are stated in Section
3
and the large-deviation principle for
0<\theta\leq\pi/4
. The large-deviation principle in the dependent case, i.e.,
\pi/4<\theta<\pi/2
, is presented in Appendix
5
and proved under a key assumption for the tail behavior of the renewal-step variables.

3.
Strategy of proof

The proof of Theorem
2
is a consequence of Lemma
1

\theta\leq\pi/4

4

Let us now focus on the moderate deviations.
In general, the setting of Theorem
1
is more challenging since for
\theta>\pi/4
we do not see independence in every step. Indeed, in the case where
\theta=\pi/2
we almost surely never see independence of a step from the previous steps. This is the reason, why we only consider the case where
\theta<\pi/2

Conceiving the in-between steps as one segment, we re-enter a regime of independent segments for which we derive the moderate-deviation result. However, the distribution of the segments is hard to trace and has exponential tails. This is the main reason why we restrict ourselves to moderate deviations and only establish the large deviations under some assumptions on the segment distribution in Appendix
5
.

To make this precise, we iteratively define a sequence of

. We set
H_{0}=\emptyset

H_{1}:={\mathcal{C}}_{\theta}(U_{1})\cap B(o,|U_{1}|),

Based on our history sets, we define

{\tau}^{\theta}_{1}:=\inf\{n>0\colon H_{n}=\emptyset\},

\tau^{\theta}_{k}=k

{\tau}^{\theta}_{1}

Exponential tails of the stopping times

, let us collect concentration properties of the horizontal displacement.

Lemma 6
.

We have that
\kappa<\infty
and for all
\varepsilon>0
there exist constants

such that for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(K^{\prime}_{t}/t\notin B_{\varepsilon}(\kappa)\right)\leq c_{1}{\operatorname{e}}^{-c_{2}t}.

(13)

Proof of Lemma

.

First, note that

\kappa^{-1}=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{\prime}]\geq\mathbb{E}[X_{1}{\mathbbm{1}}\{\tau^{\theta}_{1}=1\}]=\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}rr^{2}{\operatorname{e}}^{-\theta r^{2}}\int{\rm d}\varphi\cos\varphi{\mathbbm{1}}\{\theta-\pi/2\leq\varphi\leq\pi/2-\theta\}>0,

since independence after one step is achieved precisely if the angle is in the interval
[\theta-\pi/2,\pi/2-\theta]
.
For the second part of the statement, note that we can bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(K^{\prime}_{t}/t\notin B_{\varepsilon}(\kappa))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(K^{\prime}_{t}\leq t(\kappa-\varepsilon))+\mathbb{P}(K^{\prime}_{t}\geq t(\kappa+\varepsilon))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil t(\kappa-\varepsilon)\rceil}X^{\prime}_{i}\geq t\Big)+\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t(\kappa+\varepsilon)\rfloor}X^{\prime}_{i}<t\Big),

where we used that
K^{\prime}_{t}
, as defined in (
2
), can also be represented as

K^{\prime}_{t}=\sup\Big\{n>0:\sum_{i=1}^{n}X^{\prime}_{i}<t\Big\}.

Now, by the exponential Markov inequality, for any
s>0
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil t(\kappa-\varepsilon)\rceil}X^{\prime}_{i}\geq t\Big)\leq-s\Big(1-(\kappa-\varepsilon)s^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}\Big[{\operatorname{e}}^{sX_{1}^{\prime}}\Big]\Big),

where,
s^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}\big[{\operatorname{e}}^{sX_{1}^{\prime}}\big]
converges to
\kappa^{-1}
for

tending to zero. In particular, there exists
c>0
, such that
\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil t(\kappa-\varepsilon)\rceil}X^{\prime}_{i}\geq t\Big)<-c
. The same argument holds also for the other term, as for all

,

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t(\kappa+\varepsilon)\rfloor}-X^{\prime}_{i}>-t\Big)\leq s\Big(1+(\kappa+\varepsilon)s^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}\Big[{\operatorname{e}}^{-sX_{1}^{\prime}}\Big]\Big),

where
s^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}\big[{\operatorname{e}}^{-sX_{1}^{\prime}}\big]
converges to
-\kappa^{-1}
, as desired.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3
.

Let us fix
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
and note that

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\big(|{\mathcal{Y}}^{\kappa}_{t}-{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}_{t}|\geq\delta t^{1/2+\varepsilon}\big)&\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big\{\big|\sum_{i=\lfloor t\kappa\rfloor+1}^{K^{\prime}_{t}}Y_{i}^{\prime}\big|\geq\delta t^{1/2+\varepsilon}\Big\}\cap\{\kappa\leq K^{\prime}_{t}/t\leq\kappa+\varepsilon^{\prime}\}\Big)\\

(14)

where, by Lemma

\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-2\varepsilon}\log\mathbb{P}(K^{\prime}_{t}/t\not\in B_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\kappa))=-\infty
and hence the third summand plays no role, by logarithmic equivalence.
For the first summand in (

Y_{i}^{\prime}
are iid, we have the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{m=\lfloor t\kappa\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor t(\kappa+\varepsilon^{\prime})\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big\{\big|\sum_{i=\lfloor t\kappa\rfloor+1}^{K^{\prime}_{t}}Y_{i}^{\prime}\big|\geq\delta t^{1/2+\varepsilon}\Big\}\cap\{K^{\prime}_{t}=m\}\Big)\leq\sum_{m=1}^{r_{1}(t,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}^{\prime}\big|\geq\delta t^{1/2+\varepsilon}\Big),

(15)

where the number of summands in the above sum is
r_{1}(t,\varepsilon^{\prime}):=\lfloor t(\kappa+\varepsilon^{\prime})\rfloor-\lfloor t\kappa\rfloor
. Note that the corresponding upper bound is valid also for the second summand in (
14
) with
r_{1}(r,\varepsilon^{\prime})
replaced by
r_{2}(t,\varepsilon^{\prime}):=\lfloor t\kappa\rfloor-\lfloor t(\kappa-\varepsilon^{\prime})\rfloor

Further, since
\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-2\varepsilon}\log r(t,\varepsilon^{\prime})=0
, where
r(t,\varepsilon^{\prime})=r_{1}(t,\varepsilon^{\prime})\vee r_{2}(t,\varepsilon^{\prime})

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-2\varepsilon}\log\mathbb{P}\big(|{\mathcal{Y}}^{\kappa}_{t}-{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}_{t}|\geq\delta t^{1/2+\varepsilon}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-2\varepsilon}\sup_{0\leq\alpha\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}}\log\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t\alpha\rfloor}Y_{i}^{\prime}\big|\geq\delta t^{1/2+\varepsilon}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-2\varepsilon}\log\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t\varepsilon^{\prime}\rfloor}Y_{i}^{\prime}\big|\geq\delta t^{1/2+\varepsilon}\Big),

where we used that, by symmetry and independence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t\alpha\rfloor}Y_{i}^{\prime}\geq\delta t^{1/2+\varepsilon}\Big)

.

, we have that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-2\varepsilon}\log\mathbb{P}\big(|{\mathcal{Y}}^{\kappa}_{t}-{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}_{t}|\geq\delta t^{1/2+\varepsilon}\big)

\displaystyle\leq-I^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\delta),

which tends to

as

∎

Proof of Theorem

Let us now present the proof for our large-deviation statement.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

The statement follows from an application of the multivariate Cramér theorem for empirical means of sequences of iid random variables that possess exponential moments, see
[
7
, Corollary 6.1.6]
. In order to compute the exponential moments, we use polar coordinates, i.e., consider
U_{1}=(R,\Phi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times[-\pi,\pi]
. As used already earlier, the radius follows a Rayleigh distribution, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}(R>r)=\exp(-\lambda\theta r^{2})

and we note that, due to the isotopy of the model,
\Phi
is uniformly distributed in
[-\theta,\theta]
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\langle\gamma,U_{1}\rangle)]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}r\frac{1}{2\theta}\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}{\rm d}\varphi\exp(\gamma_{1}r\cos\varphi+\gamma_{2}r\sin\varphi-\lambda\theta r^{2})\lambda 2\theta r

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}rr\exp(-\lambda\theta r^{2})\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}{\rm d}\varphi\exp(\gamma_{1}r\cos\varphi+\gamma_{2}r\sin\varphi),

as desired.
In particular,

\displaystyle J_{\lambda,\theta}(\gamma)\leq 2\pi\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}rr\exp(-\lambda\theta r^{2})\exp(r(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}))<\infty.

The continuity follows from the fact, that the Legendre transform of strictly convex and continuously differentiable functions is differentiable and
J_{\lambda,\theta}
is strictly convex and differentiable.

Further note that, writing
u=(r_{u},\varphi_{u})
in polar coordinates,

\displaystyle{\operatorname{e}}^{-{\mathcal{I}}_{\lambda,\theta}(u)}=\inf_{q\geq 0,\,\alpha\in[-\pi,\pi]}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}r\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}{\rm d}\varphi\lambda r\exp\left(-\lambda\theta r^{2}+q\left[r\cos(\varphi-\alpha)-r_{u}\cos(\varphi_{u}-\alpha)\right]\right),

and, if
\varphi_{u}\notin(-\theta,\theta)
, then we can always choose
\alpha_{0}\in[-\pi,\pi]
such that for all
\varphi\in[-\theta,\theta]
, we have

r\cos(\varphi-\alpha_{0})-r_{u}\cos(\varphi_{u}-\alpha_{0})\leq 0
.
One choice of
\alpha_{0}
for example is given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\pi}/{2}+\theta&\text{ if }\varphi_{u}\in[\theta,\pi],\\
-{\pi}/{2}-\theta&\text{ if }\varphi_{u}\in[-\pi,-\theta].\end{array}\right.

Then, since

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}r\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}{\rm d}\varphi\lambda r\exp\left(-\lambda\theta r^{2}\right)<\infty,

by the dominated-convergence theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle e^{-{\mathcal{I}}_{\lambda,\theta}(u)}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{q\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}r\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}{\rm d}\varphi\lambda r\exp\left(-\lambda\theta r^{2}+q\left[r\cos(\varphi-\alpha_{0})-r_{u}\cos(\varphi_{u}-\alpha_{0})\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}r\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}{\rm d}\varphi\lim_{q\rightarrow\infty}\lambda r\exp\left(-\lambda\theta r^{2}+q\left[r\cos(\varphi-\alpha_{0})-r_{u}\cos(\varphi_{u}-\alpha_{0})\right]\right)=0.

Therefore, we get that
{\mathcal{I}}_{\lambda,\theta}(u)=\infty
whenever
\varphi_{u}\notin(-\theta,\theta)
. A similar calculation also gives that
{\mathcal{I}}_{\lambda,\theta}(u)=\infty
for
r_{u}=0
. This implies that
{\mathcal{I}}_{\lambda,\theta}(u)=\infty
for
u\notin{\mathcal{C}}^{o}_{\theta}
.

Now, we show that
{\mathcal{I}}_{\lambda,\theta}(u)<\infty
for
u\in{\mathcal{C}}^{o}_{\theta}
. In that case,
r_{u}>0
and
\varphi_{u}\in(-\theta,\theta)
. For this, we pick
0<\eta<\pi/16
small enough such that
[\varphi_{u}-4\eta,\varphi_{u}+4\eta]\in(-\theta,\theta)
and define two sets
A_{\alpha}
,
B_{\alpha}
as

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(r_{u}/(\sin\eta),\infty)&\text{ if }|\alpha-\varphi_{u}|\leq\pi/2+\eta,\\[0.0pt]
(0,r_{u}\sin\eta)&\text{ if }|\alpha-\varphi_{u}|>\pi/2+\eta,\end{array}\right.

and

\displaystyle B_{\alpha}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}[\varphi_{u}+2\eta,\varphi_{u}+3\eta]&\text{ if }\alpha\in[\varphi_{u},\varphi_{u}+\pi],\\[0.0pt]
[\varphi_{u}-3\eta,\varphi_{u}-2\eta]&\text{ if }\alpha\in[\varphi_{u}-\pi,\varphi_{u}).\end{array}\right.

Here, we consider the range of
\alpha
as
[\varphi_{u}-\pi,\varphi_{u}+\pi]
instead of
[-\pi,\pi]
for simplicity.
Now, for any
\alpha\in[\varphi_{u}-\pi,\varphi_{u}+\pi]
,
r\in A_{\alpha}
and
\varphi\in B_{\alpha}
, we have
r\cos(\varphi-\alpha)-r_{u}\cos(\varphi_{u}-\alpha)\geq 0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle e^{-{\mathcal{I}}_{\lambda,\theta}(u)}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\alpha\in[\varphi_{u}-\pi,\varphi_{u}+\pi]}\int_{A_{\alpha}}{\rm d}r\int_{B_{\alpha}}{\rm d}\varphi\lambda r\exp\left(-\lambda\theta r^{2}\right)>0

\displaystyle=\eta\lambda\min\Big\{\int_{0}^{r_{u}\sin\eta}{\rm d}rr\exp\left(-\lambda\theta r^{2}\right),\int_{r_{u}/(\sin\eta)}^{\infty}{\rm d}rr\exp\left(-\lambda\theta r^{2}\right)\Big\}>0,

which implies that for all
u\in{\mathcal{C}}^{o}_{\theta}
we have
{\mathcal{I}}_{\lambda,\theta}(u)<\infty
. This finishes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\langle\gamma,\overline{U}_{1}\rangle)]

\displaystyle=2\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}rr\exp(-\lambda\theta r^{2})\int_{0}^{\theta}{\rm d}\varphi\exp(\gamma_{1}r\cos\varphi+\gamma_{2}r\sin\varphi)<\infty,

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The key observation is that the sequence of progress variables
\{U_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is iid for
\theta<\pi/4
. Indeed, note that for all
i\geq 1
,
\big(C_{\theta}(V_{i})\cap B(V_{i},|U_{i+1}|)\big)\cap C_{\theta}(V_{i+1})=\emptyset
. Hence, in every step, the navigation discovers a previous undiscovered region in space and the corresponding Poisson point clouds are stochastically independent.

Step 1:
(Large deviations for iid steps.) Even though the progress steps are iid, the statement is not a direct application of, e.g., Cramér’s theorem, since
\{{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
only tracks the vertical displacement, however there is also a random horizontal displacement. Let us write
U_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i})
where
X_{i}
is the first and
Y_{i}
the second Cartesian coordinate of
U_{i}
and recall that
K_{t}:=\sup\left\{n>0\colon\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}<t\right\}

denotes the number of steps the navigation takes before reaching
t
along the
x
-axis.

Step 2:
(Lower bound for upper tail.)
Let us start with the lower bound for the upper tail. It suffices to consider
a>0
. Then, for all
1>b>0,c>0
and
\alpha>\beta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{Y}}_{t}>at)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{Y}}_{t}>at,\lfloor\beta t\rfloor\leq K_{t}<\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta t\rfloor}Y_{i}>at+\sum_{j=\lfloor\beta t\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor}|Y_{j}|,bt<\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta t\rfloor}X_{i}<t,\sum_{j=\lfloor\beta t\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor}X_{j}>(1-b)t\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{j=\lfloor\beta t\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor}|Y_{j}|<ct,\sum_{j=\lfloor\beta t\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor}X_{j}>(1-b)t\Big)\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta t\rfloor}Y_{i}>(a+c)t,bt<\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta t\rfloor}X_{i}<t\Big)

by independence. Consequently, for
\delta=\alpha-\beta
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{Y}}_{t}>at)

\displaystyle\geq-\delta\inf\{\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\theta,\lambda}(x,y)\colon x>(1-b)/\delta,y<c/\delta\}-\beta\inf\{\mathcal{I}_{\theta,\lambda}(x,y)\colon b/\beta<x<1/\beta,y>(a+c)/\beta\}.

Sending
b
to one and fixing
\delta=\delta(c)
such that
c/\delta>\mathbb{E}[|Y_{1}|]
, the first summand vanishes and we have that

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{Y}}_{t}>at)\geq-\beta\mathcal{I}_{\theta,\lambda}(1/\beta,(a+c)/\beta).

Sending
c
to zero, we arrive at

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{Y}}_{t}>at)\geq-\inf\{\beta\mathcal{I}_{\theta,\lambda}(1/\beta,a/\beta)\colon\beta>0\}.

This expression reflects that the process has to find the optimal compromise between making the right number of steps for the displacement along the
y
axis to be not too unlikely as well as hitting the time
t
along the
x
axis.

Step 3:
(Upper bound for upper tail.)
For the upper bound, we can proceed similarly. For all
\alpha>0
we can bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{Y}}_{t}\geq at)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}}Y_{i}\geq at-|Y_{K_{t}+1}|,K_{t}\leq\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor\Big)+\mathbb{P}(K_{t}>\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}\geq at-|Y_{m+1}|,K_{t}=m\Big)+\mathbb{P}(K_{t}>\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}\geq t(a-\varepsilon),t(1-\varepsilon)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}<t\Big)+\mathbb{P}(B_{\varepsilon}^{c}(t))+\mathbb{P}(K_{t}>\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor),

where
B_{\varepsilon}(t)=\{X_{m+1}\leq\varepsilon t,|Y_{m+1}|\leq\varepsilon t\}
with
\varepsilon>0
. Note that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}(B^{c}_{\varepsilon}(t))=-\infty,

since the upper tails of
|U_{i}|
are of order
O(-t^{2})
on the exponential level.
Further,

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\uparrow\infty}\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}(K_{t}>\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor)=\limsup_{\alpha\uparrow\infty}\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor}X_{i}<t\Big)=-\infty,

and hence the error terms play no role on the exponential scale with rate
t
. Now, let
(t_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a subsequence such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}

\displaystyle\log\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}\geq t(a-\varepsilon),t(1-\varepsilon)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}<t\Big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}t_{n}^{-1}\log\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\alpha t_{n}\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}\geq t_{n}(a-\varepsilon),t_{n}(1-\varepsilon)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}<t_{n}\Big)

and define

\beta_{n,\varepsilon}:={{\rm argmax}}\big\{\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta t_{n}\rfloor}Y_{i}\geq t_{n}(a-\varepsilon),t_{n}(1-\varepsilon)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta t_{n}\rfloor}X_{i}<t_{n}\Big)\colon 0\leq\beta\leq\alpha\Big\},

where we simply take
\beta_{n,\varepsilon}
to be the smallest solution in case of ambiguity. Then, for a suitable further sub-sequence
(t_{n_{k}})_{k\geq 0}
, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}t_{n}^{-1}\log\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\alpha t_{n}\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}\geq t_{n}(a-\varepsilon),t_{n}(1-\varepsilon)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}<t_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}t_{n}^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta_{n,\varepsilon}t_{n}\rfloor}Y_{i}\geq t_{n}(a-\varepsilon),t_{n}(1-\varepsilon)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta_{n,\varepsilon}t_{n}\rfloor}X_{i}<t_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}t_{n_{k}}^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta_{{n_{k}},\varepsilon}t_{n_{k}}\rfloor}Y_{i}\geq t_{n_{k}}(a-\varepsilon),t_{n_{k}}(1-\varepsilon)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta_{{n_{k}},\varepsilon}t_{n_{k}}\rfloor}X_{i}<t_{n_{k}}\Big)

and we note that
(\beta_{{n_{k}},\varepsilon})_{k\geq 0}
must contain a convergent sub-sequence
(\beta_{{n_{k_{l}}},\varepsilon})_{l\geq 0}
with limit
0\leq\beta^{*}_{\varepsilon}\leq\alpha
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\lim_{l\uparrow\infty}t_{n_{k_{l}}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta_{{n_{k_{l}}},\varepsilon}t_{n_{k_{l}}}\rfloor}Y_{i}\geq t_{n_{k_{l}}}(a-\varepsilon),t_{n_{k_{l}}}(1-\varepsilon)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta_{{n_{k_{l}}},\varepsilon}t_{n_{k_{l}}}\rfloor}X_{i}<t_{n_{k_{l}}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq-\inf\{\beta\mathcal{I}_{\theta,\lambda}\big(1/\beta,a/\beta\big)\colon\beta>0\},

since
\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor=0
and we could use continuity.

Step 4:
(General sets.)
Note that, by symmetry,
\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{Y}}_{t}\geq at)=\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{Y}}_{t}\leq-at)
and hence, for
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
open, we have that for all
z=(x,y)\in O
, there exists
\varepsilon_{z}>0
such that
\bar{B}_{\varepsilon_{z}}(z)\subset O
. In particular, for all
z\in O
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(t^{-1}{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}\in O)\geq\mathbb{P}(z-\varepsilon_{z}<t^{-1}{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}<z+\varepsilon_{z})=\mathbb{P}(t^{-1}{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}>-z-\varepsilon_{z})-\mathbb{P}(t^{-1}{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}\geq-z+\varepsilon_{z}),

where the rate to zero is dominated by the first summand. Taking an infimum over
z\in O
gives the desired result.
The upper bound can be proved similarly.

Step 5:
(Scaling.)
If we denote by
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\lambda,t}
the vertical displacement at time
t
in the navigation based on a Poisson point process with intensity
\lambda>0
, then, by scaling both the coordinates by
\sqrt{\lambda}
, we get that
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\lambda,t}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1,\sqrt{\lambda}t}/\sqrt{\lambda}
are equal in distribution. Therefore, for any
x>0
,

\displaystyle I_{\lambda,\theta}(x)

\displaystyle=-\lim_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\big(t^{-1}{\mathcal{Y}}_{\lambda,t}>x\big)

\displaystyle=-\lim_{t\uparrow\infty}\sqrt{\lambda}(\sqrt{\lambda}t)^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\big((\sqrt{\lambda}t)^{-1}{\mathcal{Y}}_{1,\sqrt{\lambda}t}>x\big)=\sqrt{\lambda}I_{1,\theta}(x).

By a similar calculation, we also get that for any
x<0
,
I_{\lambda,\theta}(x)=\sqrt{\lambda}I_{1,\theta}(x)
. This proves the theorem.
∎

5.1.
Large deviations for the dependent case

First note that for all
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\Big(|{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}}Y_{i}|\geq\delta t\Big)=-\infty,

since

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(|{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}}Y_{i}|\geq\delta t\Big)\leq 2\mathbb{P}(\tilde{Y}_{1}\geq\delta t)\leq C{\operatorname{e}}^{-c(\delta t)^{2}},

and hence, for the large deviations, we can focus on
\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}}Y_{i}
. For this, note that we can split

\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}}Y_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{K^{\prime}_{t}}Y^{\prime}_{i}+\sum_{j=\tau^{\theta}_{K^{\prime}_{t}}+1}^{K_{t}}Y_{j}=:\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}^{\prime}+\hat{Y}_{t}=:{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}_{t}.

The first summand is a sum of iid segments, but the second summand is a sum of dependent random variables.
The challenge comes from the fact that all the involved random variable
Y^{\prime}_{i},\hat{Y}_{t}
have exponential tails and therefore contribute on the large-deviation scale
t
. Let us start with the large deviations for the empirical average.

Lemma 8
.

For any
0<\lambda
and
\pi/4<\theta<\pi/2
,
\{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}U^{\prime}_{i}\}_{n\geq 1}
satisfies the large-deviation principle with rate
n
and rate function

\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\lambda,\theta}(u):=\sup\{\langle\gamma,u\rangle-J^{\prime}_{\lambda,\theta}(\gamma)\colon\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\},

where, for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

J^{\prime}_{\lambda,\theta}(\gamma):=\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\langle\gamma,U^{\prime}_{1}\rangle)]
.

We present the proof of this lemma and the following statements in the next section.
One would like to establish now a large-deviation principle for
{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}^{\prime}
similar to the one provided in Theorem
2
. However, the situation is more complicated. The key reason for this is that the upper tails of
|U_{1}|
are of order
O(-t^{2})
(this is crucially used in Step 3 in the proof of Theorem
2
), however, the upper tails of
|U^{\prime}_{1}|
are only of order
O(-t)
, as can be seen from the following statement.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\pi/4<\theta<\pi/2
, then, for all
n\geq 0
,
\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\theta}_{1}>n)\geq((4\theta-\pi)/(4\theta))^{n}
.

We present the proof further below. As a consequence, for example for
X^{\prime}_{1}
, we have for all
n
and
s>0
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X^{\prime}_{1}>t)\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{{\tau}^{\theta}_{1}}X_{i}>t,{\tau}^{\theta}_{1}>n\Big)\geq\mathbb{P}({\tau}^{\theta}_{1}>n)-\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{X}_{i}\leq t\Big)\geq{\operatorname{e}}^{an}-{\operatorname{e}}^{st+n\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(-sX_{1})]},

where
a:=\log((4\theta-\pi)/(4\theta))
and the
\hat{X}_{i}
are iid copies of
X_{1}
. Hence we have at least exponential decay if, for
n
coupled to
t
as
n=bt
with
b>0
, we have
s/b+\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(-sX_{1})]<a
. But this is the case by first picking
s
large and then picking
b
large. We can proceed similarly for
Y^{\prime}_{1}
.

Hence, it is reasonable to assume that we have the following tail behavior within a segment.

Assumption 1
.

Let
0<\lambda
and
\pi/4<\theta<\pi/2
. Then, for all
a,b>0
, we have that

\lim_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t\rfloor}X_{i}\geq at,\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t\rfloor}Y_{i}\geq bt,{\tau}^{\theta}_{1}>t\Big)=-H_{\lambda,\theta}(a,b)\in(0,\infty).

Note that it is clear that the rate function must obey our usual scaling relation
H_{\lambda,\theta}=\sqrt{\lambda}H_{1,\theta}
.
With this in mind, we can prove the large-deviation principle for
{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}_{t}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Then, for any
0<\lambda
and
\pi/4<\theta<\pi/2
,
\big\{t^{-1}{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}\big\}_{t\geq 0}
obeys the large-deviation principle
with rate
t
and rate function

I^{\prime}_{\lambda,\theta}(x):=\inf_{b\in\mathbb{R},\ c\in(0,1)}\big\{\inf\{\beta\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\lambda,\theta}(c/\beta,b/\beta)\colon\beta>0\}+\inf\{dH_{\lambda,\theta}((1-c)/d,(a-b)/d)\colon d>0\}\big\}.

Moreover,
I^{\prime}_{\lambda,\theta}(x)
satisfies the scaling relation
I^{\prime}_{\lambda,\theta}(x)=\sqrt{\lambda}I^{\prime}_{1,\theta}(x)
.

Let us try to explain the rate function in words. In the dependent case, the rate function is given by an optimization between the unlikely vertical displacement (up to level
b
, represented by the term involving
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\lambda,\theta}
), which is cheaper to achieve if the navigation performs fewer steps (up to level
c<1
) and the, then necessary, cost produced by having an unlikely large last horizontal step (of length of order
(1-c)t
that covers the remaining vertical displacement, represented by the term involving
H_{\lambda,\theta}
).

Proof of Theorem
3
.

We proceed again via several steps. First, by the initial observation in this section, it suffices to consider
{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}_{t}
.

Step 1:
(Upper bound for upper tail.)
For all
\alpha>0
we can bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}_{t}\geq at)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{K^{\prime}_{t}}Y^{\prime}_{i}+\hat{Y}_{t}\geq at,K^{\prime}_{t}\leq\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor\Big)+\mathbb{P}(K^{\prime}_{t}>\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0<\beta\leq\alpha}\alpha t\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta t\rfloor}Y^{\prime}_{i}+\hat{Y}_{t}\geq at,K^{\prime}_{t}=\lfloor\beta t\rfloor\Big)+\mathbb{P}(K^{\prime}_{t}>\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor),

where the second summand plays no role on the exponential scale with rate
t
since

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\uparrow\infty}\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}(K^{\prime}_{t}>\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor)=\limsup_{\alpha\uparrow\infty}\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\alpha t\rfloor}X^{\prime}_{i}<t\Big)=-\infty.

Similarly, for all
0<\beta\leq\alpha
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{\gamma\uparrow\infty}\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta t\rfloor}Y^{\prime}_{i}>\gamma t)=-\infty

and

\displaystyle\limsup_{\delta\uparrow\infty}\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}({\tau}^{\theta}_{1}>\delta t)=-\infty

and hence, it suffices to further bound as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta t\rfloor}Y^{\prime}_{i}+\hat{Y}_{t}\geq at,K^{\prime}_{t}=\lfloor\beta t\rfloor\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{|b|<\gamma,\ c<1}\gamma t^{2}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta t\rfloor}Y^{\prime}_{i}{\ \hat{=}\ }bt,\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta t\rfloor}X^{\prime}_{i}{\ \hat{=}\ }ct\Big)\times

\displaystyle\qquad\sup_{d\leq\delta}\delta t\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor dt\rfloor}Y_{i}\geq(a-b)t,\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor dt\rfloor}X_{i}\geq(1-c)t,{\tau}^{\theta}_{1}>dt\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{P}(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\beta t\rfloor}Y^{\prime}_{i}>\gamma t)+\mathbb{P}({\tau}^{\theta}_{1}>\delta t),

where we write
x{\ \hat{=}\ }y
iff
\lfloor x\rfloor=\lfloor y\rfloor
and used independence. Now, we can combine Assumption
1
and Lemma
8
to get

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}_{t}\geq at)

\displaystyle\quad\leq-\inf_{b\in\mathbb{R},\ c\in(0,1)}\big\{\inf\{\beta\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\lambda,\theta}(c/\beta,b/\beta)\colon\beta>0\}+\inf\{dH_{\lambda,\theta}((1-c)/d,(a-b)/d)\colon d>0\}\big\}

as desired.

Step 2:
(Lower bound for upper tail.)
Next, we consider the lower bound for the upper tail with
a>0
. Then, for
b\in\mathbb{R},0<c<1
and
\beta,d>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}_{t}>at)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{K^{\prime}_{t}}Y^{\prime}_{i}>bt,K^{\prime}_{t}=\lfloor\beta t\rfloor,\hat{Y}_{t}>(a-b)t\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\beta t\rfloor}Y^{\prime}_{i}>bt,\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\beta t\rfloor}X^{\prime}_{i}{\ \hat{=}\ }ct\Big)\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor dt\rfloor}Y_{i}>(a-b)t,\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor dt\rfloor}X_{i}>(1-c)t,{\tau}^{\theta}_{1}>\lfloor dt\rfloor\Big),

where we used independence.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\uparrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}_{t}>at)\geq-\beta\mathcal{I^{\prime}_{\lambda,\theta}}(c/\beta,b/\beta)-dH_{\lambda,\theta}((1-c)/d,(a-b)/d).

Optimizing first with respect to
\beta
and
d
in the individual summands and then with respect to
b,c
in the joint expression, we arrive at the desired lower bound which matches the upper bound.

Step 3:
(General sets.) Using symmetry and the previous steps, we can follow the exact same arguments as in the independent case, Step 4 in the proof of Theorem
2
, to arrive at the large-deviation principle.

Step 4:
(Scaling.)
A scaling argument similar to that in the proof of Theorem
2
implies that
I^{\prime}_{\lambda,\theta}(x)=\sqrt{\lambda}I^{\prime}_{1,\theta}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
8
.

The proof follows again from an application of the multivariate Cramér theorem for empirical means of sequences of iid random variables, see
[
7
, Corollary 6.1.6]
. We need to show existence of
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
, such that
J^{\prime}_{\lambda,\theta}(\gamma)<\infty
, but this is an immediate consequence of Lemma
2
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
9
.

Observe that, if for all
1\leq m\leq n
,
\Phi_{m}\in(\pi/2-\theta,\theta]
, then, for all
1\leq m\leq n
,
H_{m}\neq\emptyset
, which then ensures
{\tau}^{\theta}_{1}>n
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\big({\tau}^{\theta}_{1}>n\big)\geq\mathbb{P}\big(\Phi_{m}\in(\pi/2-\theta,\theta]\text{ for all }1\leq m\leq n\big).

Note that, conditioned on
V_{m-1}
,
R_{m}
and
H_{m-1}
,
\Phi_{m}
is uniformly distributed on the set

\{\varphi\in[-\theta,\theta]\colon V_{m-1}+(R_{m},\varphi)\notin H_{m-1}\}.

Hence, with
\ell
denoting the Lebesgue measure,

\mathbb{P}(\Phi_{m}\in(\pi/2-\theta,\theta]|V_{m-1},R_{m},H_{m-1})=\frac{\ell(\{\varphi\in(\pi/2-\theta,\theta]\colon V_{m-1}+(R_{m},\varphi)\notin H_{m-1}\})}{\ell(\{\varphi\in[-\theta,\theta]\colon V_{m-1}+(R_{m},\varphi)\notin H_{m-1}\})}.

Also, note that, if for all
1\leq i<m
,
\Phi_{i}>0
, then
{\mathcal{C}}_{\theta}^{u}(V_{m-1})\cap H_{m-1}=\emptyset
. Therefore, in that case,

\displaystyle\frac{\ell(\{\varphi\in(\pi/2-\theta,\theta]\colon V_{m-1}+(R_{m},\varphi)\notin H_{m-1}\})}{\ell(\{\varphi\in[-\theta,\theta]\colon V_{m-1}+(R_{m},\varphi)\notin H_{m-1}\})}\geq\frac{\ell((\pi/2-\theta,\theta])}{\ell([-\theta,\theta])}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\theta-\pi/2}{2\theta}=\frac{4\theta-\pi}{4\theta}.

As a result, we get that

\mathbb{P}(\Phi_{m}\in(\pi/2-\theta,\theta]|\Phi_{i}\in(\pi/2-\theta,\theta]\text{ for all }1\leq i<m)\geq(4\theta-\pi)/(4\theta)

and thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau>n)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(\Phi_{m}\in(\pi/2-\theta,\theta]\text{ for all }1\leq m\leq n)

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\Phi_{m}\in(\pi/2-\theta,\theta]|\Phi_{i}\in({\pi}/{2}-\theta,\theta]\text{ for all }1\leq i<m)

\displaystyle\geq((4\theta-\pi)/(4\theta))^{n},

which completes the proof.
∎
