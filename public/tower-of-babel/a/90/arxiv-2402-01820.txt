Signature volatility models: pricing and hedging with Fourier
Eduardo Abi Jaber, Louis-Amand Gérard

Abstract
We consider a stochastic volatility model where the dynamics of the volatility are given by a possibly infinite linear combination of the elements of the time extended signature of a Brownian motion. First, we show that the model is remarkably universal, as it includes, but is not limited to, the celebrated Stein-Stein, Bergomi, and Heston models, together with some path-dependent variants. Second, we derive the joint characteristic functional of the log-price and integrated variance provided that some infinite-dimensional extended tensor algebra valued Riccati equation admits a solution. This allows us to price and (quadratically) hedge certain European and path-dependent options using Fourier inversion techniques. We highlight the efficiency and accuracy of these Fourier techniques in a comprehensive numerical study.

Introduction

An important challenge of stochastic volatility modeling is the construction of realistic models that remain tractable for option pricing, risk hedging, trading and calibration purposes. Notable realistic features encompass various aspects, such as inter-temporal and path dependencies, which are inherent phenomena in financial markets. These phenomena have been established empirically at different timescales through extensive research, either in the form of long/short range dependence since
Mandelbrot and Van Ness 1968
and as documented in
[

,

,

,

. They can also be understood more strategically using the transitory nature of the decisions of market participants and their impact on prices
[
16
,
17
]
.

Incorporating path dependencies and memory effects into the modeling framework gives rise to non-Markovian models, which generally pose computational challenges. Recent developments have identified interesting mathematical classes of models that address this issue. One such class comprises stochastic Volterra models, which provide a flexible framework for capturing certain inter-temporal dependencies while maintaining computational tractability under specific affine and quadratic structures
[
1
,
3
,
25
,
26
,
33
]
. Another avenue worth exploring lies in the application of signature-based methods. The signature of a path, initially introduced by Chen
[
20
]
in 1957, consists of the (infinite) sequence of iterated integrals of a path. It plays a crucial role in the theory of rough paths
[
39
,
51
]
and is recently gaining considerable momentum in the fields of Machine Learning
[
21
,
36
,
56
]
and Mathematical Finance
[

,

,

,
18
,

,

,

,
32
,

]
, namely due to its universal linearization property: any functional of the path can be approximated by a linear combination of the elements of the signature of the path, provided some regularity. In our present work, we explore the modeling and numerical aspects of these signature-based approaches for stochastic volatility modeling. We uncover their potential in addressing the computational challenges associated with path-dependent models and we highlight their versatility and universality.

We consider a class of stochastic volatility models for a stock price
S
in the form

\mathrm{d}S_{t}=S_{t}\Sigma_{t}\left(\rho\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{\perp}\right),

where the stochastic volatility process
\Sigma
is of a general path-dependent form

f(t,W_{0\leq s\leq t}),\quad t\leq T,

for a certain class of measurable functions
f
and
(W,W^{\perp})
a two-dimensional Brownian motion. The correlation
\rho\in[-1,1]
accounts for the leverage effect. More precisely, we assume that the volatility process
\Sigma_{t}
is a (possibly infinite) linear combination of the elements of the signature process
\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}
of the time extended Brownian motion
\widehat{W}_{t}:=(t,W_{t})
defined by the infinite sequence of iterated

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(1,\begin{pmatrix}t\\
W_{t}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\frac{t^{2}}{2!}&\int_{0}^{t}s\mathrm{d}W_{s}\\
\int_{0}^{t}W_{s}\mathrm{d}s&\frac{W_{t}^{2}}{2!}\end{pmatrix},\cdots\right).

(1.1)

We call such models signature volatility models. They have been introduced by
Arribas et al. 2020
, with

for pricing and calibration purposes. They allow to naturally embed inter-temporal and path dependencies. The construction is very elementary, yet, from the mathematical perspective, such class of models enjoys a beautiful and powerful universality feature. So far, the universal approximation property of signatures have been invoked to argue that “the framework is universal in the sense that classical models can be approximated arbitrarily well” by signature volatility models, see for instance
[
28

. In contrast to the existing literature on signature volatility models, we allow
\Sigma_{t}
to be an infinite linear combination of the elements of the signature process
\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}
. This introduces intricate theoretical challenges, particularly regarding convergence issues, but provides a more profound comprehension of the elegant universal structure inherent to these models. Our approach draws inspiration from
Cuchiero et al. 2023c
where infinite linear combinations of signature elements are considered in the context of signature stochastic differential equations.

Universality and flexibility of signature volatility models.

In a first step, by considering infinite linear combinations of signature elements, we go beyond the ‘approximated universality’ and we prove that the class of signature volatility models is universal in the sense of exact representations. We show that many classical and popular Markovian models, and even more advanced not necessarily Markovian models, belong to the class of signature volatility models, using novel exact representations formulas derived in the sequel and in the accompanying paper
[
5
]
. This includes:

(i)

Affine Markovian models:
The models of
Stein and Stein 1991
,
Schöbel and Zhu 1999
and
Heston 1993
which became popular because of the explicit knowledge of the characteristic function of the log-price, allowing for fast and accurate pricing and hedging using Fourier inversion techniques.

(ii)

Non-affine Markovian models:
The models of
Bergomi 2005
and
Hull and White 1987
, which are more flexible than their affine counterparts but less tractable. In addition, the recently introduced Quintic Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model of
Abi Jaber et al. June 2023
, which is able to jointly capture SPX and VIX smiles, also belongs to the class of signature volatility models.

(iii)

Non-Markovian models:
A large class of models based on stochastic delayed equations and Volterra processes, including the class of polynomial Gaussian volatility models
[
4
]
, Volterra Stein-Stein model
[
1
]
, the Volterra
[
2
]
and rough
[
12
]
Bergomi models.

The above list is far from being exhaustive and we believe that more known and important volatility models can be exactly re-written as a signature stochastic volatility model. In addition, when such exact representation cannot be found for a specific model, building an approximated signature volatility model is possible thanks to the universal approximation property of the signature. The representations are derived in Section
3
and illustrated on numerical examples.

Tractability of signature volatility models.

In a second step, we develop a generic framework for pricing and quadratic hedging certain vanilla and path-dependent options on the log-price and the integrated variance using Fourier inversion technology. More specifically, we obtain in Theorem
4.1
that for any signature volatility model, the joint characteristic functional of the log-price and the integrated variance is known up to the solution of an infinite dimensional Riccati equation. This result opens the door to fast and accurate Fourier pricing and hedging going beyond the standard affine classes
(i)
including the whole list of Markovian and non-Markovian models in
(ii)
-
(iii)
above, for which pricing and hedging is an intricate task.
Our representation formula for the characteristic function can be directly related to the ones that have appeared in
Cuchiero et al. 2023c
, and share similarities with the formulas in
Friz et al. 2022
and
Lyons et al. 2024
.

Using a comprehensive numerical study we highlight the efficiency and the accuracy of the Fourier techniques for pricing and hedging in signature volatility models in Sections
5

6
. We stress that the numerics are not straightforward, since they involve several approximations and truncations. We use ideas in the spirit of ‘control variate’ with Black-Scholes prices and deltas to stabilize the Fourier inversions and reduce the number of evaluations of the characteristic functional. We also point out that the proposed implementation is both generic and scalable, as it can be applied, or fine-tuned if needed, to any signature volatility model. It only requires as inputs the coefficients (i.e. the parameters) of the signature volatility model, which essentially define the model itself whether it is Markovian/non-Markovian/non-semimartingale, and it generates as outputs option prices and hedging strategies by Fourier methods.

2.1
Tensor algebra

In this section, we setup the framework for dealing with signatures of semimartingales. One can also refer to the first sections in
[

]
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and denote by
\otimes
the tensor product over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, e.g.
(x\otimes y\otimes z)_{ijk}=x_{i}y_{j}z_{k}
, for
i,j,k=1,\dots,d
, for
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
n\geq 1
, we denote by
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}
the space of tensors of order
n
and by
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes 0}=\mathbb{R}
. In the sequel, we will consider mathematical objects, path signatures, that live on the extended tensor algebra space
T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is the space of (infinite) sequences of tensors defined by

T((\mathbb{R}^{d})):=\left\{\bm{\ell}=(\bm{\ell}^{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}:\bm{\ell}^{n}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}\right\}.

Similarly, for
M\geq 0
, we define the truncated tensor algebra
T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as the space of sequences of tensors of order at most
M
defined by

T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\left\{\bm{\ell}\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d})):\bm{\ell}^{n}=0,\text{ for all }n>M\right\},

and the tensor algebra

as the space of all finite sequences of tensors defined by

We clearly have
T(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
For
\bm{\ell}=(\bm{\ell}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\bm{p}=(\bm{p}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and

\displaystyle\bm{\ell}+\bm{p}:

\displaystyle=(\bm{\ell}^{n}+\bm{p}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\quad\bm{\ell}\otimes\bm{p}:=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\bm{\ell}^{k}\otimes\bm{p}^{n-k}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\quad\lambda\bm{\ell}:=(\lambda\bm{\ell}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.

These operations induce analogous operations on
T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
T(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

and

Moreover, we denote by

2.3
Signatures

We define the (path) signature of a semimartingale process as the sequence of iterated stochastic integrals in the sense of Stratonovich. Throughout the paper, the Itô integral is denoted by
\int_{0}^{\cdot}Y_{t}\mathrm{d}X_{t}
and the Stratonovich integral by
\int_{0}^{\cdot}Y_{t}\circ\mathrm{d}X_{t}.
If both
X
and
Y
are semimartingales then, we have the relation
\int_{0}^{\cdot}Y_{t}\circ\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\int_{0}^{\cdot}Y_{t}\mathrm{d}X_{t}+\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]_{\cdot}
.

Definition 2.3

(Signature)
.

Fix
T>0
. Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a continuous semimartingale in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on some filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
. The signature of
X
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}:\Omega\times[0,T]

\displaystyle\to T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))

\displaystyle(\omega,t)

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbb{X}_{t}(\omega):=(1,\mathbb{X}_{t}^{1}(\omega),\dots,\mathbb{X}_{t}^{n}(\omega),\dots),

where

\mathbb{X}_{t}^{n}:=\int_{0<u_{1}<\cdots<u_{n}<t}\circ\mathrm{d}X_{u_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\circ\mathrm{d}X_{u_{n}}

takes value in
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}
,
n\geq 0
. Similarly, the truncated signature of order
M\in\mathbb{N}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{\leq M}:[0,T]

\displaystyle\to T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(2.10)

\displaystyle(\omega,t)

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbb{X}_{t}^{\leq M}(\omega):=(1,\mathbb{X}_{t}^{1}(\omega),\dots,\mathbb{X}_{t}^{M}(\omega),0,\dots,0,\dots).

(2.11)

The signature plays a similar role to polynomials on path-space. Indeed, in dimension
d=1
, the signature of
X
is the sequence of monomials
\left(\frac{1}{n!}(X_{t}-X_{0})^{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. In particular, any finite combination of elements of the signature
\left\langle\bm{\ell},\mathbb{X}_{t}\right\rangle
, defined in (

) for
\bm{\ell}\in T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is a polynomial of degree

.

Remark 2.3.1
.

Explicitly we can write the term
\mathbb{X}_{t}^{n}
as
(\mathbb{X}_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}}})_{({{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}}})\in V_{n}}
. So the definition can be written in iterated form as

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}}}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{X}_{s}^{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{i_{1}\cdots i_{n-1}}}}\circ\mathrm{d}X_{s}^{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{i_{n}}}}}.

(2.12)

\blacksquare

In what will follow, we are exclusively interested in the case
d=2
and
X_{t}=\widehat{W}_{t}:=(t,W_{t})
where
W
is a 1-dimensional Brownian motion.
Its first few signature orders are given by

\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}^{0}=1,\quad\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}t\\
W_{t}\end{pmatrix},\quad\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{t^{2}}{2!}&\int_{0}^{t}s\mathrm{d}W_{s}\\
\int_{0}^{t}W_{s}\mathrm{d}s&\frac{W_{t}^{2}}{2!}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.13)

2.4
Infinite linear combinations of signature elements

In this section, we recall some results on infinite linear combinations
\left\langle\bm{\ell},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle
for certain admissible
\bm{\ell}\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
for which the infinite series will make sense. Two crucial ingredients for our paper are the shuffle product (Proposition
2.4
) and an Itô’s formula (Lemma
2.5
). We follow the presentation in
[
5
, Section 2]
, and we refer to
[
29
]
for more general results.

We first introduce the space
\mathcal{A}
of admissible elements
\bm{\ell}
below, using the associated semi-norm:

||\bm{\ell}||_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}\in V_{n}}\bm{\ell}^{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}\right|,\quad t\geq 0,

recall the definition of
V_{n}
in (
2.1
) and the decomposition (
2.2
). Whenever,
||\bm{\ell}||_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}<\infty
a.s., the infinite linear combination

\left\langle\bm{\ell},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}\in V_{n}}\bm{\ell}^{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}}\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}^{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}}

is well-defined. This leads to the following definition for the admissible set
\mathcal{A}
:

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{\bm{\ell}\in T((\mathbb{R}^{2})):||\bm{\ell}||_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}<\infty\text{ for all }t\in[0,T],\text{ a.s.}\right\}.

We stress that the null set in
\mathcal{A}
does not depend on
t
. Note that
T(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subset\mathcal{A}
and that
\left\langle\bm{\ell},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle
is an extension of (
2.5
), as the two bracket operations
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
coincide whenever
\bm{\ell}\in T(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

The admissible set
\mathcal{A}
has another very interesting property, as it allows us to linearize polynomials on infinite linear combination of the signature, see Proposition
2.4
. This is what most of the literature refers to when putting forth the linearization power of the signature.

Proposition 2.4

(Shuffle property)
.

If
\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
, then
\bm{\ell}_{1}\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\ell}_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
and

\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{1},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{2},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle=\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{1}\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\ell}_{2},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle.

Proof.

This follows from a particular instance of shuffle compatible partitions, see
[
29
, Lemma 4.4]
. Alternatively, we refer to
[
5
, Proposition 3.1]
.
∎

For elements
\bm{\ell}\in\mathcal{A}
, the process
(\langle\bm{\ell},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\rangle)_{t\leq T}
is well-defined. An important question is to know whether it is a semimartingale and compute its Itô’s decomposition. The answer is positive, thanks to an Itô’s formula in Lemma
2.5
below, for elements in the set

\mathcal{I}:=\left\{\bm{\ell}\in\mathcal{A}:\text{ for all }t\in[0,T],\>||\bm{\ell}||_{t}^{\mathcal{I}}<\infty\text{ and }\int_{0}^{T}||\bm{\ell}||_{t}^{\mathcal{I}}\mathrm{d}t<\infty\text{ a.s.}\right\},

(2.14)

where

||\bm{\ell}||_{t}^{\mathcal{I}}:=||\bm{\ell}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}||_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}+||\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\ell}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}}||_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}+\left(||\bm{\ell}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}}||_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}\right)^{2}.

More generally, we state the result for time dependent linear combinations
(\langle\bm{\ell}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\rangle)_{t\leq T}
with
\bm{\ell}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{A}
in the set

\mathcal{I}^{\prime}:=\left\{\bm{\ell}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{I}:\begin{matrix}\text{ for all }t\in[0,T],\>\bm{\ell}_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}\in C^{1}([0,T])\text{ for all }{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}\in V,\\
\text{ and }||\dot{\bm{\ell}}_{t}||_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}<\infty\text{ and }\int_{0}^{T}||\dot{\bm{\ell}}_{t}||_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}t<\infty\text{ a.s.}\hfill\end{matrix}\right\},

(2.15)

where
\dot{\bm{\ell}_{t}}:=\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}\in V}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bm{\ell}_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Lemma 2.5

(Itô’s decomposition)
.

Let
\bm{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\left\langle\bm{\ell},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle=\left\langle\bm{\ell}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\ell}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\left\langle\bm{\ell}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(2.16)

Let
\bm{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle=\left\langle\dot{\bm{\ell}}_{t}+\bm{\ell}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\ell}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(2.17)

3
The signature volatility model

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{Q})
be a probability space supporting a two-dimensional Brownian motion
(W,W^{\perp})
. We denote by
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
the filtration generated by
(W,W^{\perp})
. We set

\displaystyle B=\rho W+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}W^{\perp},

(3.1)

for some
\rho\in[-1,1]
. We define the time-augmented process
\widehat{W}_{t}=(t,W_{t})
and we consider that the dynamics of the risky asset
S
, under the risk neutral probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
, are given by a stochastic volatility model where the volatility process
\Sigma
is a (possibly infinite) linear combination of the signature of
\widehat{W}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{t}}{S_{t}}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle,

(3.3)

where
\bm{\sigma}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{A}
corresponds to the parameters of the volatility process and is such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma_{t}^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

(3.4)

We recall the definition of the set
\mathcal{A}
in (
2.4
).
The condition (
3.4
) ensures that the stochastic integral

\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Sigma_{s}\mathrm{d}B_{s}

is well defined as an Itô integral, so that there exists a unique solution to (
3.2
) given by

S_{t}=S_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma_{s}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma_{s}\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right),\quad t\geq 0.

Condition (
3.4
) can be made more explicit by observing that the instantaneous variance
\Sigma^{2}_{t}
is also linear in the signature, i.e.

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}^{2}=\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle,

(3.5)

thanks to the shuffle product in Proposition
2.4
. We note that the time-independent case
\bm{\sigma}_{t}=\bm{\sigma}
for all
t\in[0,T]
and some
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}
, leads to
\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma_{t}^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t=\langle\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}},\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{T}]\rangle
where the quantity
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{T}]
can be computed explicitly using Fawcett’s formula
[
34
]
extended to time-augmented Brownian motions in
[
52
, Proposition 4.10]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}]=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\left({{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}+\frac{1}{2}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}\right)^{\otimes n}.

(3.6)

In practice, we will usually be interested in truncated elements
\bm{\sigma}:[0,T]\to T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
, which automatically satisfy the condition (
3.4
) since in this case
\bm{\sigma}
has only a finite number of non-zero terms and all the integrated moments of the Brownian motion are finite.

Notice that for general
\bm{\sigma}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{A}
the process
\Sigma
is not necessarily Markovian nor a semimartingale. It is a semimartingale if in addition
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
thanks to Itô’s formula in Lemma
2.5
. For truncated elements
\bm{\sigma}:[0,T]\to T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
the process
\Sigma
is a semimartingale. So far, truncated elements have been considered in the related literature
[
10
,
27
]
.

In the next subsection, we highlight the flexibility introduced by infinite linear combinations of signature elements in terms of exact representations and provide numerical implementations of their truncated form, i.e.

\Sigma_{t}^{\leq M}:=\left\langle\bm{\sigma}^{\leq M},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle,\quad M\geq 0,

where
\bm{\sigma}^{\leq M}:[0,T]\to T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is the truncated form of
\bm{\sigma}
at order
M
, i.e. its
M
first levels coincide with
\bm{\sigma}
and everything else is set to
0
. Note that
\left\langle\bm{\sigma}^{\leq M},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle=\left\langle\bm{\sigma},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}^{\leq M}\right\rangle
where
\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}^{\leq M}
is defined in (
2.10
).

3.3

24
]
(CIR) process
V
, driven by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}V_{t}=\kappa(\theta-V_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\eta\sqrt{V_{t}}\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad V_{0}=v>0,

(3.17)

seems to admit a conjectured linear representation

\displaystyle V_{t}=\left\langle\bm{\ell}^{\textnormal{CIR}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle=\left(\left\langle\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{CIR}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\right)^{2},

(3.18)

where
\bm{\ell}^{\textnormal{CIR}}:=\left(\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{CIR}}\right)^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2}
with
\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{CIR}}
satisfying the non-linear algebraic equation

\left(\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{CIR}}\right)^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2}=v{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+\left(\left(\kappa\theta-\frac{\eta^{2}}{4}\right){\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}-\kappa\left(\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{CIR}}\right)^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2}\right){{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}+\eta\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{CIR}}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}.

Remark 3.2.2
.

The theoretical convergence, i.e. proving that
\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{CIR}}\in\mathcal{A}
, seems intricate to obtain and is still an open problem. From the numerical perspective, the truncated version of (
3.18
) is well-defined and Figure
5
suggests a certain type of convergence under the
Feller 1951
condition
2\kappa\theta>\eta
, with
V
in (
3.18
) being the strong solution. Moreover, Figure

(b) below also provides a numerical convergence of pricing of our truncated representation (
3.18
) in the context of the Heston model, where the Feller condition is not satisfied. In this case, we expect the representation in (
3.18
) to yield a weak solution.

\blacksquare

3.1.4
Models based on path-dependent processes

As shown in
[
5
]
, several path-dependent stochastic Volterra processes:

Z_{t}=Z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)(a_{0}+a_{1}Z_{s})\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)(b_{0}+b_{1}Z_{s})\mathrm{d}W_{s},

with
Z_{0},a_{0},a_{1},b_{0},b_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, for certain locally square-integrable kernels
K
also admit linear representations in the form
Z_{t}=\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle
. This includes non-semimartingale processes such as the Riemann-Liouville fractional Brownian motion

W_{t}^{H}=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{H-1/2}\mathrm{d}W_{s},\quad H\in(0,1).

For instance, the (time-dependent) representation of
W^{H}
reads

W_{t}^{H}=\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{t}^{\textnormal{RL}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle,\quad\bm{\ell}_{t}^{\textnormal{RL}}=t^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{-n}\left(H-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{\bar{n}}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}^{\otimes n}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}},

where
(\cdot)^{\bar{n}}
is the falling factorial. Please refer to
[
5
, Theorem 4.5]
for more details and illustrations on such representations. Again any volatility process that is an analytic function of such processes falls into the framework of signature volatility models (
3.2
)-(
3.3
) thanks to the shuffle property, recall (
3.10
). This includes for instance the class of Volterra polynomial models
[
4
]
, in particular Volterra
[
2
]
and rough Bergomi models
[
12
]
.

As as final example, the delayed equation (DE) process
U
, given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}U_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(a_{1}+b_{1}U_{t}+c_{1}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda_{1}(t-s)}U_{s}\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathrm{d}t+\left(a_{2}+c_{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda_{2}(t-s)}U_{s}\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad U_{0}=u\in\mathbb{R},

(3.19)

for some

, can be represented as a linear combination of the signature of the time-extended Brownian motion with

\displaystyle U_{t}=\left\langle\bm{\ell}^{\textnormal{DE}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle,\quad\bm{\ell}^{\textnormal{DE}}=(u{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+a_{1}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}+a_{2}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}})\left({\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}-b_{1}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}-{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}\left(c_{1}e^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\lambda_{1}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}+c_{2}e^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\lambda_{2}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}\right)\right)^{-1}.

(3.20)

The reader can refer to

for more details and illustrations on the linear delayed equation process.

in the framework of option pricing when

3.2
Leverage effect

When
\Sigma
is a semimartingale, the
leverage coefficient
in the model (
3.2
)-(
3.3
) is defined as the instantaneous correlation between the log-price
S
and its instantaneous volatility
|\Sigma|
, with

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}[\log S,|\Sigma|]_{t}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{d}[\log S]_{t}}\sqrt{\mathrm{d}[|\Sigma|]_{t}}},

In practice, the leverage coefficient is negative on equity markets and one usually would like to control its sign and intensity via the correlation parameter
\rho
between
B
and
W
in (
3.1
). Since
\Sigma
is not necessarily positive at all time, the leverage coefficient can flip sign in the model (
3.2
)-(
3.3
), which is not realistic. The next Lemma provides a necessary and sufficient condition on the coefficients
\bm{\sigma}
to control the sign of the leverage coefficient and its driving intensity
\rho
at all time.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, then the covariation between the log-price and the instantaneous volatility is proportional to
\rho
, and of the same sign if
\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\geq 0
, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}[\log S,|\Sigma|]_{t}=\rho\,\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle|\Sigma_{t}|.

(3.21)

Proof.

The dynamics of
\log S
are in the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\log S_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}\Sigma_{t}^{2}\mathrm{d}t+\Sigma_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

(3.22)

The instantaneous volatility of the model is
|\Sigma|
. By an application of Itô-Tanaka’s formula on
|\Sigma|
and of Lemma
2.5
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}|\Sigma|_{t}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{sign}\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}+\mathrm{d}L^{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{sign}\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\textnormal{sign}\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\sigma}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}}+\dot{\bm{\sigma}}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}L^{0}(t),

where
L^{0}
is the local time of
\Sigma
at 0. (
3.21
) then trivially follows.
∎

Moreover controlling the sign of
\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle
can be made explicit in the Stein-Stein, Quintic and Bergomi models as shown in the next example.

so that
\bm{\sigma}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}}=\alpha_{1}{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+\alpha_{3}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}+\alpha_{5}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2222}}}

3.3
Approximated representations and comparison with exact representations

More generally, if exact linear representations are not available for certain processes, approximate representations can be obtained thanks to the universal approximation property
[
36
]
of path-signatures. Informally any continuous functional
F
can be approximated by a linear combination of signature elements

F(t,(W_{s})_{0\leq s\leq t})\approx\left\langle\bm{f},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle,

for
\bm{f}\in T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and a given truncation order
M
, see
[
48
, Theorem 1]
for more details.

In practice, one would perform a linear regression on trajectories of a given process
X
against a finite linear combination of the signature elements. Such approach was used in
[
28
, Section 4.1.2]
to regress trajectories of the price process in the Heston model and the volatility process in a SABR-type model. In the same spirit,
Lyons et al. 2020
regressed certain option payoffs on the signature of price trajectories to get an approximation.

We are going to illustrate this regression approach on trajectories of an inverse CIR process and fractional Brownian motions and show some of its limitations. We will also compare it with a couple of exact representations from the previous Section.

Let us now explicit the algorithm used to get the regress linear representation:

X_{t}=F(t,(W_{s})_{s\leq t}).

:

the truncation order of

,

•

and

Online
:

4
The joint characteristic functional

The following theorem provides the joint conditional characteristic functional of the log-price
\log S
and the integrated variance
\bar{V}:=\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Sigma^{2}_{s}\mathrm{d}s
in the model (
3.2
)–(
3.3
) in terms of a solution to an infinite-dimensional system of time-dependent
T((\mathbb{R}^{2}))
-valued Riccati differential equations.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f,g:[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
be measurable and bounded functions and
\bm{\sigma}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{A}
. Assume that there exists
\bm{\psi}\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, solution to the following infinite-dimensional system of time-dependent Riccati equations

\displaystyle-\dot{\bm{\psi}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}})^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2}+\rho f(t)(\bm{\sigma}_{t}\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}})+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}}+\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}+\left(\frac{f(t)^{2}-f(t)}{2}+g(t)\right)\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2},

(4.1)

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}_{T}

\displaystyle=0.

(4.2)

Define the processes

\displaystyle U_{t}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}f(s)\mathrm{d}\log S_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\mathrm{d}\bar{V}_{s},

(4.3)

\displaystyle M_{t}

\displaystyle=e^{U_{t}}.

(4.4)

Then
M
is a local martingale. If in addition it is a true martingale, then the following expression holds for the joint characteristic functional of
(\log S,\bar{V})
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\exp\left(\int_{t}^{T}f(s)\mathrm{d}\log S_{s}+\int_{t}^{T}g(s)\mathrm{d}\bar{V}_{s}\right)\right|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\exp\left(\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\right),\quad t\leq T.

(4.5)

Proof.

To show that
M
is a local martingale we show that its part in
\mathrm{d}t
vanishes using Itô’s formula. The dynamics of
M
read

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}M_{t}=M_{t}\left(\mathrm{d}U_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\left[U\right]_{t}\right).

(4.6)

We start by deriving the dynamics of
U
in (
4.3
). By using Lemma
2.5
, since
\bm{\psi}\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
by assumption, we have that
\left(\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\right)_{t\geq 0}
is a semimartingale with dynamics

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\dot{\bm{\psi}}_{t}+\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

Combining the previous identity with
d\bar{V}_{t}=\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\rangle\mathrm{d}t
, recall (
3.5
), and the dynamics of
\log S
in (
3.22
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}U_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle+f(t)\mathrm{d}\log S_{t}+g(t)\mathrm{d}\bar{V}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\dot{\bm{\psi}}_{t}+\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\left(g(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}f(t)\right)\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t+f(t)\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}B_{t}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\dot{\bm{\psi}}_{t}+\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}}+\left(g(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}f(t)\right)\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}W_{t}+f(t)\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

Using the shuffle product of Proposition
2.4
and the fact that
B
and
W
are correlated, recall (
3.1
), we get that the quadratic variation of
U
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\left[U\right]_{t}

\displaystyle=\left\langle(\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}})^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2}+2\rho f(t)(\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}}\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\sigma}_{t})+f(t)^{2}\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t.

This yields that the drift of
dM_{t}/M_{t}
in (
4.6
) is given by

\displaystyle\left\langle\dot{\bm{\psi}}_{t}+\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}}+\left(g(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}f(t)\right)\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left[(\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}})^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2}+2\rho f(t)(\bm{\psi}_{t}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}}\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\sigma}_{t})+f(t)^{2}\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2}\right],\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle,

which is equal to
0
from the Riccati equations (
4.1
). This shows that
M
is a local martingale. By assumption
M
is even a true martingale. After observing that the terminal value of
M
, is given by

M_{T}=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}f(s)\mathrm{d}\log S_{s}+\int_{0}^{T}g(s)\mathrm{d}\bar{V}_{s}\right),

recall that
\bm{\psi}_{T}=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}f(s)\mathrm{d}\log S_{s}+\int_{0}^{T}g(s)\mathrm{d}\bar{V}_{s}\right)\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[M_{T}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=M_{t}=\exp\left(U_{t}\right),

which yields (
4.5
).
∎

Remark 4.1.1
.

If
\bm{\psi}
is such that
\,\mathfrak{Re}\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\leq 0
for all
t\in[0,T]
a.s.,
M
would be a martingale. We expect this relation to hold whenever
\,\mathfrak{Re}f(t)\in[0,1]
and
\,\mathfrak{Re}g(t)\leq 0
.

\blacksquare

Theorem
4.1
is a verification result to obtain the exponentially affine representation of the joint characteristic functional (
4.5
). It disentangles the algebraic affine structure in infinite dimension. It can be related to
[
29
, Theorem 4.24]
if one considers the signature of the three dimensional process
((t,W_{t},B_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
there. It relies on the two crucial assumptions that a well-behaved
\mathcal{I}
-valued solution
\bm{\psi}
exists to the Riccati equation (
4.1
), together with the true martingality of
M
. These assumptions seem very intricate to prove, even in one dimensional settings, partial results in these directions for
T((\mathbb{R}))
-valued Riccati equations can be found in
[
29
, Section 6]
and
[
6
]
. We note that no semimartingality assumption for
\Sigma_{t}=\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t},\rangle
is required in Theorem
4.1
.

In the following two sections, we will validate the representation (
4.5
) numerically and we will highlight the application of Theorem
4.1
to the pricing and the hedging of several contingent claims by Fourier inversion techniques. The general functions
f
and
g
allow for enough flexibility to cover a broad set of contingent claims. For instance:

1.

Certain vanilla options that depend on the values
(S_{T},\bar{V}_{T})
, like European call and put options and volatility swaps, are recovered using constant

and

.

2.

Geometric Asian options on the average

can be recovered by setting

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}f(s)\mathrm{d}\log S_{s}

(4.7)

where

has its first

5.1
European options

Let us consider a European call option on
S
with maturity
T>0
and strike
K>0
. Its price at time
t\leq T
is given by
C_{t}(T,K)=\mathbb{E}[(S_{T}-K)^{+}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]
. From
Lewis 2001
, one can price this option using the Fourier inversion formula

C_{t}(S_{t};T,K)=S_{t}-\frac{K}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\mathfrak{Re}\left[e^{i(u-\frac{i}{2})k_{t}}\phi_{t}\left(u-\tfrac{i}{2}\right)\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\left(u^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\right)},

(5.1)

where
\phi
is the conditional characteristic function
\phi_{t}(u)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{iu\log\frac{S_{T}}{S_{t}}}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]
and
k_{t}=\log\frac{S_{t}}{K}
.

Moreover, we add a ‘control variate’ to quicken the convergence, as in
Andersen and Andreasen 2000
.
Given
\sigma_{\textnormal{BS}}<\infty
, one has

C_{t}(S_{t};T,K)=C_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(S_{t};T,K)-\frac{K}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\mathfrak{Re}\left[e^{i(u-\frac{i}{2})k_{t}}\left(\phi_{t}\left(u-\tfrac{i}{2}\right)-\phi_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}\left(u-\tfrac{i}{2}\right)\right)\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\left(u^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\right)},

(5.2)

where

\displaystyle C_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(S_{t};K,T)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(d_{1})S_{t}-\mathcal{N}(d_{2})K,

(5.3)

with

\displaystyle d_{1}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\textnormal{BS}}\sqrt{T-t}}\left(\log\frac{S_{t}}{K}+\frac{\sigma_{\textnormal{BS}}^{2}}{2}(T-t)\right),\quad d_{2}=d_{1}-\sigma_{\textnormal{BS}}\sqrt{T-t}

(5.4)

and

\displaystyle\phi_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(u)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\frac{\sigma_{\textnormal{BS}}^{2}}{2}\left(u^{2}+iu\right)(T-t)\right].

(5.5)

This Black-Scholes control variate can also be applied to other products and, as will be seen in Section
6
, to Fourier hedging. The quantity
\sigma_{\textnormal{BS}}
can be determined to ensure approximate moment matching for instance, and one can use the characteristic function
\phi_{t}
to approximate (by finite differences) the second order cumulant of the distribution of the log price
\log S
.

Numerically, for the discretization of the Fourier integral, our numerical experiments show that Gauss-Laguerre quadrature outperform other quadrature rules in our class of models, which is in line with the empirical findings that appeared in
[
59
]
. In particular, the higher the maturity is, the lower the degree of the quadrature needs to be for the same set of parameters. In addition, the use of a control variate makes computations much quicker yielding fewer calls of the characteristic function to make the quadrature converge, see Figure
10
. Moreover, we can see that the added value of the control variate, in terms of speed of convergence, grows with the time horizon. See
[
59
, Section 6]
for more details on numerical refinements.

For a given signature volatility model (
3.2
)-(
3.3

4.1

5.2
q
-Volatility swaps

The payoff of a
q
-volatility swap is
R_{T}^{q}-K^{q}
where
R_{T}^{q}=\left(\frac{1}{T}\bar{V}_{T}\right)^{q}
is the realized
q
-volatility and
K^{q}
the
q
-volatility strike. The aim in pricing
q
-volatility swaps is to find the fair strike price, i.e.
K^{q}=\mathbb{E}[R_{T}^{q}]
. This is made possible by Laplace inversion, see
Schürger 2002
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{q}]=\frac{q}{\Gamma(1-q)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-uX}\right]}{u^{q+1}}\mathrm{d}u,

(5.7)

for
q\in(0,1)
and
X\geq 0
. It is straightforward to see that setting
f=0
and
g(t)=-\frac{u}{T}
in (
4.3
) gives
\tilde{M}_{0}(u)=\mathbb{E}[e^{-\frac{u}{T}\bar{V}_{T}}]
, which allows us to compute analytically
q
-volatility swaps in the framework of the signature volatility models with

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{T}\bar{V}_{T}\right)^{q}\right]=\frac{q}{\Gamma(1-q)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\tilde{M}_{0}(u)}{u^{q+1}}\mathrm{d}u.

Specifically, the fair strike of the volatility swap, i.e.
q=\frac{1}{2}
, is thus of the form

\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\bar{V}_{T}}\right]=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\tilde{M}_{0}(u)}{u^{3/2}}\mathrm{d}u.

Moreover, the fair strike of the variance swap, i.e.
q=1
, can be written in closed form thanks to Fawcett’s formula (
3.6
) for time-independent representations, i.e.

K_{T}^{1}=\frac{1}{T}\left\langle\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}},\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{T}]\right\rangle.

6
Quadratic hedging by Fourier methods

The signature volatility model (
3.2
)-(
3.3
) generates an incomplete market in general (unless the correlation
\rho
between the two Brownian motions
B
and
W
is
\pm 1
). Therefore contingent claims on the stock
S
cannot be perfectly hedged. We will consider quadratic hedging methods instead, we refer to
Schweizer 1999
for a detailed overview of quadratic hedging approaches. We will show that in our setup, quadratic hedging remains highly tractable in signature volatility models using Fourier techniques on the conditional characteristic function (
4.5
). We do this in two steps:

1.

We first solve in Section
6.1
the quadratic hedging problem for a generic contingent claim
\xi
, in a general stochastic volatility model driven by the two dimensional Brownian motion
(W,W^{\perp})
. The hedging strategy depends on the quantities that appear in the martingale representation theorem of
\mathbb{E}[\xi|\mathcal{F}_{t}]
. We provide a concise proof in this setting.

2.

We then show in Section
6.2
that in the setting of a signature volatility model and for contingent claims that admit Fourier representation, such as European and Asian call and put options, the hedging strategy can be recovered from the conditional characteristic function (
4.5
).

6.1
A generic solution

Let
\xi
be an
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable non-negative random variable such that
\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}]<\infty
that we are looking to hedge using a self-financing portfolio. We recall that
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the filtration generated by
(W,W^{\perp})
. A self-financing hedging portfolio
X
consists of an initial wealth
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and a progressively measurable strategy
(\vartheta_{u})_{u\leq T}
of the amount of shares invested in asset
S
given in (
3.2
) at time
u\leq T
. It has the following dynamics

\displaystyle X_{t}^{\alpha}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\vartheta_{u}\mathrm{d}S_{u}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\alpha_{u}\mathrm{d}B_{u},

(6.1)

with
\alpha_{u}:=\vartheta_{u}S_{u}\Sigma_{u}
.
The set of admissible hedging strategies
\alpha
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\left\{\alpha\text{ progressively measurable such that }\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha_{s}^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}s<\infty\right\}.

(6.2)

We stress that in this section we do not impose specific dynamics for the stochastic volatility
\Sigma
, i.e. (
3.3
),
\Sigma
is only assumed to be adapted to the Brownian motion
(W,W^{\perp})
.

A quadratic hedging strategy aims at minimizing the following objective function

\displaystyle J(X_{0},\alpha)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X_{T}^{\alpha}-\xi\right)^{2}\right]

(6.3)

over
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{H}
.

Following
Föllmer and Sondermann 1985
it is easy to derive a solution of the quadratic hedging problem using the martingale representation theorem.
Note that
(\mathbb{E}[\xi|\mathcal{F}_{t}])_{t\leq T}
is a square integrable martingale with terminal value
\xi
at
T
. An application of the martingale representation theorem
[
47
, Theorem 4.15]
yields the existence of two progressively measurable and square integrable processes
Z
and
Z^{\perp}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\xi|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\xi-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{s}\mathrm{d}W_{s}-\int_{t}^{T}Z^{\perp}_{s}\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{\perp}.

(6.4)

The value of the quadratic hedging problem is consequently given by

\displaystyle\inf_{X_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\alpha\in\mathcal{H}}J(X_{0},\alpha)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(Z_{t}^{2}+(Z_{t}^{\perp})^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\left(\rho Z_{t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{t}^{\perp}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right],

(6.5)

where the optimum is attained for
(X_{0}^{*},\alpha^{*})
given by

6.2
Fourier implementation in the signature volatility model

We now illustrate how the optimal hedging strategy
X_{0}^{*}
and
\alpha^{*}
given in (
6.6
) can be recovered numerically from the knowledge of the conditional characteristic functional (
4.5
) in the specific case of a signature volatility model, i.e. when
\Sigma
is of the form (
3.3
) and for contingent claims that admit a Fourier representation. In this section, we only consider European options, Asian options have been included in Appendix
C.2
for the interested reader. For more general payoffs, a similar approach can be adapted, following the representation formulas in
Di Tella et al. 2019
.

In order to implement the quadratic hedging, the idea is to re-express the Fourier inversion formula (
5.2
) in terms of the process
M_{t}(u)
in (
4.4
) with
f(t)=iu
and
g=0
and apply Itô. Since in this case

M_{t}(u)=\phi_{t}(u)e^{iu\log S_{t}},

the representation (
5.2
) directly leads to

C_{t}(S_{t};T,K)=C_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(S_{t};T,K)-\frac{K}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\mathfrak{Re}\left[e^{-i(u-\frac{i}{2})\log K}\left(M_{t}\left(u-\tfrac{i}{2}\right)-M_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}\left(u-\tfrac{i}{2}\right)\right)\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\left(u^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\right)},

(6.7)

where

M_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(u)=\phi_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(u)e^{iu\log S_{t}},

and
\phi_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}
as defined in (
5.5
).

Set
w(u):=\frac{K}{\pi}\frac{e^{-i(u-\frac{i}{2})\log K}}{u^{2}+\frac{1}{4}}
and
\tilde{u}:=(u-\frac{i}{2})
and assume that
\sup_{u:\,\mathfrak{Im}(u)=-1/2}\mathbb{E}[|M_{t}(u)|^{2}]<\infty
,
t\leq T
, then an application of Fubini’s theorem combined with Itô’s Lemma, see for instance
[
30
, Proposition 4.1]
, yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}C_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}C_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\mathfrak{Re}\left[w(u)\left(\mathrm{d}M_{t}(\tilde{u})-\mathrm{d}M_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(\tilde{u})\right)\right]\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}\mathrm{d}S_{t}+\left(\Theta_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(S_{t}\Sigma_{t})^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\mathfrak{Re}\Big[w(u)\Big(M_{t}(\tilde{u})\left(\mathrm{d}U_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}[U]_{t}\right)-M_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(\tilde{u})\left(\mathrm{d}\log M_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(\tilde{u})+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}[\log M^{\textnormal{BS}}(\tilde{u})]_{t}\right)\Big)\Big]\mathrm{d}u,

where
\Delta_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial S_{t}}C_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}
,
\Theta_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}C_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}
and
\Gamma_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial S_{t}^{2}}C_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}
. Furthermore, using equalities between Black-Scholes
Greeks
, one can show that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}C_{t}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}S_{t}\Sigma_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\frac{\Theta_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}}{\sigma_{\textnormal{BS}}^{2}}\left(\sigma_{\textnormal{BS}}^{2}-\Sigma_{t}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\mathfrak{Re}\Big[w(u)\Big(M_{t}(\tilde{u})\left(\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t}(\tilde{u})|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}W_{t}+i\tilde{u}\Sigma_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-M_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(\tilde{u})\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{u}^{2}+i\tilde{u}\right)\left(\sigma_{\textnormal{BS}}^{2}-\Sigma_{t}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t+i\tilde{u}\Sigma_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right]\Big)\Big]\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}S_{t}\Sigma_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\mathfrak{Re}\Big[w(u)\Big(M_{t}(\tilde{u})\left(\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t}(\tilde{u})|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}W_{t}+i\tilde{u}\Sigma_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right)-M_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(\tilde{u})i\tilde{u}\Sigma_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\Big)\Big]\mathrm{d}u.

The final equality comes from the fact that
\Theta_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}
can also be written as a Fourier integral. This gives option price dynamics of the following form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}C_{t}

\displaystyle=Z_{t}\mathrm{d}W_{t}+Z_{t}^{\perp}\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{\perp},

(6.8)

where
Z
and
Z^{\perp}
are defined as follows

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{t}S_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}\rho+\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\mathfrak{Re}\left[\zeta_{t}(\tilde{u})w(u)\right]\mathrm{d}u,\qquad Z_{t}^{\perp}=\Sigma_{t}S_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\mathfrak{Re}\left[\zeta_{t}^{\perp}(\tilde{u})w(u)\right]\mathrm{d}u,

with

\displaystyle\zeta_{t}(u):

\displaystyle=iu\Sigma_{t}\left(M_{t}(u)-M_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(u)\right)\rho+M_{t}(u)\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t}(u)|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\zeta_{t}^{\perp}(u):

\displaystyle=iu\Sigma_{t}\left(M_{t}(u)-M_{t}^{\textnormal{BS}}(u)\right)\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}.

This allows us to solve the quadratic hedging problem in (
6.6
) numerically for European call options in the framework of signature volatility models. Moreover, applying the put-call parity allows us to easily extend it to European put options.

Proposition A.1
.

Whenever

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{mGBM}}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{mGBM}}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\left\langle\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{mGBM}}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left(\kappa\theta-\tfrac{\alpha\eta}{2}\right){\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}-\left(\kappa+\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{mGBM}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\alpha\eta{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+\alpha^{2}\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{mGBM}}\right),\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\left\langle\eta{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+\alpha\bm{\sigma}^{\textnormal{mGBM}},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}W_{t}
