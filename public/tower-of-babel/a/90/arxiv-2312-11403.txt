Learning Temporal Properties is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard
Benjamin Bordais, Daniel Neider, Rajarshi Roy

Abstract




1
Introduction

Temporal logics are probably the most popular specification languages employed to specify the temporal behavior of computing systems.
Originally introduced in the context of program verification, temporal logics such as Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)
[
18
]
and Computation Tree Logic (CTL)
[
10
]
now find applications in a wide variety of topics, including reinforcement learning
[
13
,
25
,
15
,
5
]
, motion planning
[
14
,
11
]
, formal verification
[
18
,
10
,
21
,
9
,
3
]
, reactive synthesis
[
20
,
26
]
, process mining
[
7
,
8
]
, and so on.
The primary reason for the popularity of temporal logics is that its specifications are not only mathematically rigorous but also human-understandable due to the resemblance to natural language.

Most applications that employ temporal logics, even today, rely on manually writing specifications based on human intuition.
This manual process, however, is inefficient and tedious and often leads to several errors in formulating specifications
[
1
,
24
,
4
]
.
To mitigate these issues, recent research has focused on automatically learning temporal logic formulae based on observations of the underlying system.

Perhaps, the most basic setting for learning temporal logic is that of inferring a concise formula from a given set of positive (or desirable) and negative (or undesirable) executions of a system.
The focus on learning a
concise
formula is inspired by the principle of Occam’s razor, which advocates that formulae that are small in size are less complex and thus easy to understand.
For this basic setting, several works
[
17
,
6
,
22
]
have devised efficient algorithms with impressive empirical performance.

However, the theoretical analysis of the problem of learning temporal logic in terms of computational complexity is significantly lacking.
The only notable work that deals with the complexity of learning temporal logic is the work by Fijalkow and Lagarde
[
12
]
.
They show that learning certain fragments of
LTL
(i.e. subclasses of
LTL
with a restricted set of temporal operators) from finite executions is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

In this work, we conduct a detailed study of the computational complexity of learning temporal properties in its full generality.
We assume that we are given finite sets
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{N}
of positive and negative examples of system behavior, respectively, and a desired size
B
given in unary for the prospective formula as input.
We now study the following decision problem: given
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{N}
and
B
, does there exist a formula
\varphi
of size less than or equal to
B
that all the positive examples satisfy and that all negative examples violate. Note that, contrary to what is done in
[
12
]
by Fijalkow, the bound
B
that we consider is given in unary, not in binary. Then, we consider the above problem (i) for
LTL
-formulae, in which case, we consider the examples to be formalized as
infinite words
, and (ii) for
CTL
-formulae, in which case, we consider the examples to be system models formalized as
Kripke structures
. We show that both learning problems, for
LTL
and
CTL
are
\mathsf{NP}
-hard. Furthermore, it is rather straightforward to show that
LTL
and
CTL
learning is in
\mathsf{NP}
. Indeed, one can simply guess a solution, i.e., a prospective formula, of size less than or equal to
B
— which is possible since
B
is written in unary — and verify that the solution guessed is correct in polynomial time. Note that this is possible for both
LTL
and
CTL
formulae
[
16
]
.

Our proof of
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness for
LTL
learning is done via a reduction from the satisfiability problem (
\mathsf{SAT}
). The reduction itself is easy to define, the main difficulty lies in proving that from an
LTL
-formula separating the words in
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{N}
, one can extract a satisfying valuation (for the
\mathsf{SAT}
formula that we consider). The proof of this fact follows two consecutive stages: first, we show that it is sufficient to only consider the
LTL
-formulae satisfying a specific property, namely being
concise
, see Definition
17
. Second, we show that these concise formulae imply — in a specific sense — an
LTL
-formula that is
disjunctive
, see Definition
20
, that is itself an
LTL
-formula separating the words in
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{N}
. From such a disjunctive formula, it is then straightforward to extract a satisfying valuation. The proof of
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness for
CTL
learning is straightforward via a reduction from the
LTL
learning case.

2
LTL
and
CTL
learning

2.1
LTL
: syntax and semantics

Before introducing
LTL
-formulae
[
19
]
, let us first introduce the objects on which these formulae will be interpreted: infinite words.

Infinite and ultimately periodic words.

Given a set of propositions
\mathsf{Prop}
, an infinite word is an element of the set
(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})^{\omega}
. For all infinite words
w\in(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})^{\omega}
, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
w[i]\in 2^{\mathsf{Prop}}
denote the element in
2^{\mathsf{Prop}}
at position
i
of the word
w
and
w[i:]\in(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})^{\omega}
denote the infinite word starting at position
i
.

Furthermore, we will be particularly interested in ultimately periodic words, and in size-1 ultimately periodic words. They are formally defined below.

Definition 1
.

Consider a set of propositions
\mathsf{Prop}
. An ultimately periodic word
w\in(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})^{\omega}
is such that
w=u\cdot v^{\omega}
for some finite words
u\in(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})^{*},v\in(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})^{+}
. In that case, we set the size
|w|
of
w
to be equal to
|w|:=|u|+|v|
. Then, for all sets
S
of ultimately periodic words, we set
|S|:=\sum_{w\in S}|w|
.

An ultimately periodic word
w
is of size-1 if
|w|=1
. That is,
w=\alpha^{\omega}
for some
\alpha\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
.

Syntax.

Given a set of propositional variables
\mathsf{Prop}
,
LTL
-formulae are defined inductively as follows:

\displaystyle\varphi\Coloneqq p

\displaystyle\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi\wedge\varphi\mid\varphi\vee\varphi\mid\varphi\Rightarrow\varphi\mid\varphi\Leftrightarrow\varphi

\displaystyle\mid\lX\varphi\mid\lF\varphi\mid\lG\varphi\mid\varphi\lU\varphi\mid\varphi\lR\varphi\mid\varphi\lW\varphi\mid\varphi\lM\varphi

where
p\in\mathsf{Prop}
. These formulae use the following temporal operators:

•

\lX
: neXt;

•

\lF
: Future;

•

\lG
: Globally;

•

\lU
: Until;

•

\lR
: Release;

•

\lW
: Weak until;

•

\lM
: Mighty release.

We let
\mathsf{Op}_{Un}:=\mathsf{Op}_{Un}^{\mathsf{lg}}\cup\mathsf{Op}_{Un}^{\mathsf{temp}}
where
\mathsf{Op}_{Un}^{\mathsf{lg}}:=\{\neg\}
and
\mathsf{Op}_{Un}^{\mathsf{temp}}:=\{\lX,\lF,\lG\}
denote the set of unary operators. We also let
\mathsf{Op}_{Bin}:=\mathsf{Op}_{Bin}^{\mathsf{lg}}\cup\mathsf{Op}_{Bin}^{\mathsf{temp}}
where
\mathsf{Op}_{Bin}^{\mathsf{lg}}:=\{\wedge,\vee,\Rightarrow,\Leftrightarrow\}
and
\mathsf{Op}_{Bin}^{\mathsf{temp}}:=\{\lU,\lR,\lW,\lM\}
denote the set of binary operators. Note that these operators will also be used in
CTL
formulae. We also let
LTL
denote the set of all
LTL
-formulae. We then define the size
\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi)
of an
LTL
-formula
\varphi\in\textrm{LTL}
to be its number of sub-formulae. That set of sub-formulae
\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)
is defined inductively as follows:

•

\mathsf{SubF}(p):=\{p\}
for all
p\in\mathsf{Prop}
;

•

\mathsf{SubF}(\bullet\varphi):=\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)\cup\{\bullet\varphi\}
for all unary operators
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Un}
;

•

\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{1}\bullet\varphi_{2}):=\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{1})\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{2})\cup\{\varphi_{1}\bullet\varphi_{2}\}
for all binary operators
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Bin}
.

Semantics.

The semantics of
LTL
-formulae is defined inductively as follows, for all
w\in(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})^{\omega}
:

\displaystyle w\models p

\displaystyle\text{ iif }p\in w[0],

\displaystyle w\models\neg\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ iif }w\not\models\varphi;

\displaystyle w\models\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }w\models\varphi_{1}\text{ and }w\models\varphi_{2};

\displaystyle w\models\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }w\models\varphi_{1}\text{ or }w\models\varphi_{2};

\displaystyle w\models\varphi_{1}\Rightarrow\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }w\models\neg\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2};

\displaystyle w\models\varphi_{1}\Leftrightarrow\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }w\models(\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2})\vee(\neg\varphi_{1}\wedge\neg\varphi_{2});

\displaystyle w\models\lX\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ iif }w[1:]\models\varphi;

\displaystyle w\models\lF\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ iif }\exists i\in\mathbb{N},\;w[i:]\models\varphi;

\displaystyle w\models\lG\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ iif }\forall i\in\mathbb{N},\;w[i:]\models\varphi;

\displaystyle w\models\varphi_{1}\lU\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }\exists i\in\mathbb{N},\;w[i:]\models\varphi_{2}\text{ and }\forall j\leq i-1,\;w[j:]\models\varphi_{1};

\displaystyle w\models\varphi_{1}\lR\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }w\models\neg(\neg\varphi_{1}\lU\neg\varphi_{2})

\displaystyle w\models\varphi_{1}\lW\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }w\models(\varphi_{1}\lU\varphi_{2})\vee\lG\varphi_{1};

\displaystyle w\models\varphi_{1}\lM\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }w\models(\varphi_{1}\lR\varphi_{2})\wedge\lF\varphi_{1}

When
w\models\varphi
, we say that
\varphi
accepts
w
, otherwise it rejects it. In the following, we will consider ultimately periodic words, i.e. words of the shape
u\cdot v^{\omega}
.

2.2
CTL
: syntax and semantics

Before introducing
CTL
-formulae, let us first introduce Kripke structures, i.e. the objects on which
CTL
-formulae will be interpreted. The way the following definitions are presented is essentially borrowed from
[
23
]
.

Kripke structure.

A
Kripke structure

M=(S,I,R,L)
over a finite set
\mathsf{Prop}
of propositions consists of a set of states
S
, a set of initial states
I\subseteq S
, a transition relation
R\subseteq S\times S
such that for all states
s\in S
there exists a state
s^{\prime}\in S
satisfying
(s,s^{\prime})\in R
, and a labeling function
L\colon S\rightarrow 2^{\mathsf{Prop}}
. We define the size of
M
as
\lvert S\rvert
. For all states
s\in S
, the set
\mathrm{post}(s)=\{s^{\prime}\in S\mid(s,s^{\prime})\in R\}
contains all successors of
s\in S
.

A (infinite)
path
in the Kripke structure
M
is an infinite sequence of states
\pi=s_{0}s_{1}\dots
such that
s_{i+1}\in\mathrm{post}(s_{i})
for each
i\geq 0
. For a state
s
, we let
\mathrm{paths}(s)
denote the set of all (infinite) paths starting from
s
.

Syntax.

To define the syntax of
CTL
-formulae, we introduce two types of formulae: state formulae and path formulae. Intuitively, state formulae express properties of states, whereas path formulae express temporal properties of paths.
For ease of notation, we denote state formulae and path formulae with the Greek letter
\varphi
and the Greek letter
\psi
, respectively.
CTL
-state formulae over the set of propositions
\mathsf{Prop}
are given by the grammar:

\displaystyle\varphi\Coloneqq p\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi\wedge\varphi\mid\varphi\vee\varphi\mid\varphi\Rightarrow\varphi\mid\varphi\Leftrightarrow\varphi\mid\lE\psi\mid\lA\psi

where
p\in\mathsf{Prop}
, and
\psi
is a path formula. Next,
CTL
-path formulae are given by the grammar

\displaystyle\psi\Coloneqq\lX\varphi\mid\lF\varphi\mid\lG\varphi\mid\varphi\lU\varphi\mid\varphi\lR\varphi\mid\varphi\lW\varphi\mid\varphi\lM\varphi

When we refer to
CTL
-formulae, we usually refer to
CTL
-path formulae. We denote by
CTL
the set of all

CTL
-formulae. The set of sub-formulae
\mathsf{SubF}(\Phi)
of a
CTL
-formula
\varphi
is then defined inductively as follows:

•

\mathsf{SubF}(p):=\{p\}
for all
p\in\mathsf{Prop}
;

•

\mathsf{SubF}(\neg\Phi):=\{\neg\Phi\}\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\Phi)
;

•

\mathsf{SubF}(\diamond(\bullet\Phi)):=\{\diamond(\bullet\Phi)\}\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\Phi)
for all
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Un}^{\mathsf{temp}}
and
\diamond\in\{\exists,\forall\}
;

•

\mathsf{SubF}(\Phi_{1}\bullet\Phi_{2}):=\{\Phi_{1}\bullet\Phi_{2}\}\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\Phi_{1})\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\Phi_{2})
for all
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Bin}^{\mathsf{lg}}
;

•

\mathsf{SubF}(\diamond(\Phi_{1}\bullet\Phi_{2})):=\{\diamond(\Phi_{1}\bullet\Phi_{2})\}\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\Phi_{1})\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\Phi_{2})
for all
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Bin}^{\mathsf{temp}}
and
\diamond\in\{\exists,\forall\}
.

The size
|\varphi|
of a
CTL
-formula is defined as its number of sub-formulae:
|\varphi|:=|\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)|
.

Semantics.

As mentioned above, we interpret
CTL-formulae over Kripke structures using the standard definitions
[
2
]
. Given a state
s
and state formula
\Phi
, we define when
\varphi
holds in state
s
, denoted using
s\models\varphi
, inductively as follows:

\displaystyle s\models p

\displaystyle\text{ iif }p\in L(s),

\displaystyle s\models\neg\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ iif }s\not\models\varphi,

\displaystyle s\models\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }s\models\varphi_{1}\text{ and }s\models\varphi_{2},

\displaystyle s\models\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }s\models\varphi_{1}\text{ or }s\models\varphi_{2},

\displaystyle s\models\varphi_{1}\Rightarrow\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }s\models\neg\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2},

\displaystyle s\models\varphi_{1}\Leftrightarrow\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }s\models(\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2})\lor(\neg\varphi_{1}\wedge\neg\varphi_{2}),

\displaystyle s\models\lE\psi

\displaystyle\text{ iif }\pi\models\psi\text{ for some }\pi\in\mathrm{paths}(s)

\displaystyle s\models\lA\psi

\displaystyle\text{ iif }\pi\models\psi\text{ for all }\pi\in\mathrm{paths}(s)

Furthermore, given a path
\pi
and a path formula
\psi
, we define when
\psi
holds for the path
\pi
, also denoted using
\pi\models\varphi
, inductively as follows:

\displaystyle\pi\models\lX\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ iif }\pi[1:]\models\varphi;

\displaystyle\pi\models\lF\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ iif }\exists i\in\mathbb{N},\;\pi[i:]\models\varphi;

\displaystyle\pi\models\lG\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ iif }\forall i\in\mathbb{N},\;\pi[i:]\models\varphi;

\displaystyle\pi\models\varphi_{1}\lU\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }\exists i\in\mathbb{N},\;\pi[i:]\models\varphi_{2}\text{ and }\forall j\leq i-1,\;\pi[j:]\models\varphi_{1};

\displaystyle\pi\models\varphi_{1}\lR\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }\pi\models\neg(\neg\varphi_{1}\lU\neg\varphi_{2})

\displaystyle\pi\models\varphi_{1}\lW\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }\pi\models(\varphi_{1}\lU\varphi_{2})\vee\lG\varphi_{1};

\displaystyle\pi\models\varphi_{1}\lM\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ iif }\pi\models(\varphi_{1}\lR\varphi_{2})\wedge\lF\varphi_{1}

We now say that a
CTL
-formula
\varphi
holds on a Kripke structure
M
, denoted by
M\models\varphi
, if
s\models\varphi
for all initial states
s\in I
of
M
.

2.3
Decision problems

We define the
LTL
and
CTL
learning problems below, where an example for
LTL
is an ultimately periodic word, whereas an example for
CTL
is a Kripke structure.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathsf{L}\in\{\textrm{LTL},\textrm{CTL}\}
. We denote by
\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
the following decision problem:

•

Input:
(\mathsf{Prop},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N},B)
where
\mathsf{Prop}
is a set of propositions,
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N}
are two finite sets of examples for
LTL
, and
B\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

Output: yes iff there exists an
LTL
-formula
\varphi\in\textrm{LTL}
such that
|\varphi|\leq B
that is separating
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{N}
, i.e. such that:

–

for all
X\in\mathcal{P}
, we have
X\models\varphi
;

–

for all
X\in\mathcal{N}
, we have
X\not\models\varphi
.

The size of the input is equal to
|\mathsf{Prop}|+|\mathcal{P}|+|\mathcal{N}|+B
(i.e.
B
is written in unary).

As mentioned in the introduction, since the model checking problems for
LTL
and
CTL
can be solved in polynomial time, it follows that both problems
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
and
\textrm{CTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
are in
\mathsf{NP}
, as stated below.

Proposition 3
.

The decision problem
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
(resp.
\textrm{CTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
) is in
\mathsf{NP}
.

3
LTL
learning is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard

The goal of this section is to show the theorem below:

Theorem 4
.

The decision problem
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

Theorem
4
above will be proved via a reduction from
3-\mathsf{SAT}
. Hence, for the remaining of this section, we are going to consider a
\mathsf{SAT}
-formula:

\Phi:=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}(l_{1}^{i}\vee l^{i}_{2}\vee l^{i}_{3})

on a set of variable
X:=\{x_{k}\mid 1\leq k\leq m\}
where, for all
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq 3
, we have
l_{j}^{i}
a literal equal to either
l_{j}^{i}=x
or
l_{j}^{i}=\lnot x
for some
x\in X
. Then,
\Phi
is a positive instance of the problem
3-\mathsf{SAT}
if there is a valuation
v:X\rightarrow\{\top,\bot\}
satisfying the formula
\Phi
, denoted
v\models\Phi
.

Let us formally define below the reduction that we will consider.

Definition 5
.

We let:

•

\mathsf{Prop}:=\{x_{k},\bar{x_{k}}\mid 1\leq k\leq m\}
denote the set of propositions that we will consider;

•

for all
1\leq i\leq n
:
C_{i}:=\{\tilde{l_{1}^{i}},\tilde{l_{2}^{i}},\tilde{l_{3}^{i}}\}\in(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})
with, for all
x\in X
and
1\leq j\leq 3
:

\displaystyle\tilde{l_{j}^{i}}:=\begin{cases}x&\text{ if }l_{j}^{i}=x\\
\bar{x}&\text{ if }l_{j}^{i}=\neg x\\
\end{cases}

Then, we define the input
\mathsf{In}_{\Phi,X}=(\mathsf{Prop},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N},B)
of the
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
decision problem where:

•

\mathcal{P}:=\{c_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}\uplus\{\rho_{k}\mid 1\leq k\leq m\}
where for all
1\leq i\leq n
, we have
c_{i}:=C_{i}^{\omega}\in(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})^{\omega}
and for all
1\leq k\leq m
,
\rho_{k}:=(\{x_{k},\bar{x_{k}}\})^{\omega}\in(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})^{\omega}
;

•

\mathcal{N}:=\{\eta\}
with
\eta:=\emptyset^{\omega}\in(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})^{\omega}
;

•

B=2m-1
.

Let us describe on an example below what this reduction amounts to on a specific
\mathsf{SAT}
formula
\Phi
. Note that we will come back twice to this example in the following.

Example 6
.

Assume that
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}
and:

\Phi:=x_{1}\wedge(x_{2}\lor\lnot x_{3}\lor\lnot x_{1})\wedge(\lnot x_{2}\lor x_{3})

Then, we have
\mathsf{Prop}=\{x_{1},\bar{x_{1}},x_{2},\bar{x_{2}},x_{3},\bar{x_{3}}\}
,
\rho_{1}=(\{x_{1},\bar{x_{1}}\})^{\omega}
,
\rho_{2}=(\{x_{2},\bar{x_{2}}\})^{\omega}
and
\rho_{3}=(\{x_{3},\bar{x_{3}}\})^{\omega}
. Furthermore,
C_{1}=\{x_{1}\}
,
C_{2}=\{\bar{x_{1}},x_{2},\bar{x_{3}}\}
and
C_{3}=\{\bar{x_{2}},x_{3}\}
. Finally,
B=5
.

Clearly,
\mathsf{In}_{\Phi,X}
can be computed in polynomial time, given
(\Phi,X)
. The goal now is to show that
\Phi
is satisfiable if and only if
\mathsf{In}_{\Phi,X}
is a positive instance of the
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
decision problem.

3.1
The easy direction

We start with the easy direction, i.e. we show that from a satisfying valuation
v
, we can define an
LTL
-formula of size (at most)
B
that accepts all words in
\mathcal{P}
and rejects all words in
\mathcal{N}
. This is formally stated and proved below.

Lemma 7
.

If
\Phi
is satisfiable then
\mathsf{In}_{\Phi,X}
is a positive instance of the
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
decision problem.

Proof.

Consider a valuation
v:X\rightarrow\{\top,\bot\}
that satisfies the formula
\Phi
. For all
1\leq k\leq m
, we let
x_{k}^{v}\in\mathsf{Prop}
be such that:

\displaystyle x_{k}^{v}:=\begin{cases}x_{k}&\text{ if }v(x_{k})=\top\\
\bar{x_{k}}&\text{ if }v(x_{k})=\bot\end{cases}

Then, we consider the
LTL
-formula:

\varphi:=x_{1}^{v}\lor x_{2}^{v}\lor\ldots\lor x_{m}^{v}

(1)

Clearly, we have
\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi)=2m-1=B
. Furthermore, consider any
1\leq k\leq m
. We have
\rho_{k}\models x_{k}^{v}
, hence
\rho_{k}\models\varphi
. Furthermore,
\eta\not\models\varphi
. Consider now some
1\leq i\leq n
. Since
v
satisfies
\Phi
, there is some
1\leq j\leq 3
and
1\leq k\leq m
such that
l_{j}^{i}=x_{k}
and
v(x_{k})=\top
or
l_{j}^{i}=\neg x_{k}
and
v(x_{k})=\bot
. In the first case, we have
x_{k}^{v}=x_{k}=\tilde{l_{j}^{i}}\in C_{i}
. In the second case, we have
x_{k}^{v}=\bar{x_{k}}=\tilde{l_{j}^{i}}\in C_{i}
. Thus, in any case, we have
c_{i}=C_{i}^{\omega}\models\varphi
.

Therefore,
\varphi
accepts all words in
\mathcal{P}
and rejects all words in
\mathcal{N}
.
∎

Note that there is not only one such
LTL
-formula, we give an example below of another that would do the job.

Example 8
.

Continuing Example
6
, one can realize that the valuation
v:X\rightarrow\{\top,\bot\}
such that
v(x_{1})=v(x_{2})=v(x_{3})=\top
satisfies the formula
\Phi
.

Hence, the
LTL
-formula
\varphi
corresponding to this valuation that is defined in the above proof is:

\varphi:=x_{1}\lor x_{2}\lor x_{3}

However, other
LTL
-formulae
\varphi^{\prime}
could have worked as well, for instance:

\varphi^{\prime}:=(x_{1}\Leftrightarrow x_{2})\Rightarrow x_{3}

3.2
The hard direction

There now remains the hard direction, i.e. showing that from an
LTL
-formula of size (at most)
B
that accepts all words in
\mathcal{P}
and rejects all words in
\mathcal{N}
, it is possible to define a satisfying valuation
v
, as stated in the lemma below.

Lemma 9
.

If
\mathsf{In}_{\Phi,X}
is a positive instance of the
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
decision problem, then
\Phi
is satisfiable.

The difficulty to the prove this lemma lies in the fact that from an arbitrary
LTL
-formula, albeit of size at most
B
, it is not straightforward, a priori, to extract a valuation from it. Let us first handle the simple case where there is
a separating
LTL
-formula whose shape is similar to the
LTL
-formula that we have exhibited in the proof of Lemma
7
.

Lemma 10
.

Assume that there is an
LTL
-formula
\varphi
separating
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{N}
of the following shape:

\varphi=z_{1}\lor z_{2}\lor\cdots\lor z_{m}

where, for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we have
z_{k}\in\{x_{k},\bar{x_{k}}\}
. Then, there is a valuation satisfying the formula
\Phi
.

Proof.

We define the valuation
v_{\varphi}:X\rightarrow\{\top,\bot\}
as follows, for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
:

\displaystyle v_{\varphi}(x_{k}):=\begin{cases}\top&\text{ if }z_{k}=x_{k}\\
\bot&\text{ if }z_{k}=\bar{x_{k}}\\
\end{cases}

Let
i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. We have
c_{i}\models\varphi
. Hence, there is
1\leq j\leq 3
and
1\leq k\leq m
such that
\tilde{l^{i}_{j}}=z_{k}
. That is, we have either
l^{i}_{j}=x_{k}=z_{k}
, it which case
v_{\varphi}(x_{k})=\top
and
v_{\varphi}\models l^{i}_{1}\lor l^{i}_{2}\lor l^{i}_{3}
, or
l^{i}_{j}=\lnot x_{k}
and
\bar{x_{k}}=z_{k}
, it which case
v_{\varphi}(x_{k})=\bot
and
v_{\varphi}\models l^{i}_{1}\lor l^{i}_{2}\lor l^{i}_{3}
. Thus, in any case, we have
v_{\varphi}\models l^{i}_{1}\lor l^{i}_{2}\lor l^{i}_{3}
. Since this holds for all
1\leq i\leq n
, it follows that
v_{\varphi}\models\Phi
. ∎

Our goal is to show that whenever
\mathsf{In}_{\Phi,X}
is a positive instance of the
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
decision problem, then the premise of Lemma
10
holds. The method that we take to achieve this takes two stages:

•

First, we reduce the set of
LTL
-formulae that we need to consider with the use of syntactical requirements. Informally, this consists in proving that if there is an arbitrary
LTL
-formula of size at most
B
that accepts all words in
\mathcal{P}
and rejects all words in
\mathcal{N}
, then there is also one in that set of
LTL
-formula.

•

Second, once restricted to that set of
LTL
-formulae, we establish, by induction, that these formulae imply (in a specific sense) formulae of the shape described in Lemma
10
.

3.2.1
Removing all temporal operators

The first step that we take is to show that we can disregard any temporal operators, i.e. we show that we can restrict ourselves to temporal-free
LTL
-formulae. This notion is defined below.

Definition 11
.

An
LTL
-formula
\varphi
is
temporal-free
is none of the operators
\lX,\lF,\lG,\lU,\lW,\lR,\lM
occur in
\varphi
.

To prove this result, we define a function
\mathsf{tr}:\textrm{LTL}\rightarrow\textrm{LTL}
on
LTL
-formulae that removes all temporal operators, that does not increase the size of formulae while doing so and that preserves an equivalence on size-1 ultimately periodic words. This is formally stated below.

Lemma 12
.

There exists a function
\mathsf{tr}:\textrm{LTL}\rightarrow\textrm{LTL}
such that, for all
LTL
-formulae
\varphi\in\textrm{LTL}
:

1.

\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi))\subseteq\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)]
, hence
\mathsf{Sz}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi))\leq\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi)
;

2.

\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)
is temporal-free;

3.

\varphi\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)
, i.e. for all
\alpha\in 2^{\mathsf{Prop}}
, we have
\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\models\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)
.

To make the proof of this lemma not too cumbersome, let first state and prove another that guides us on how to translate temporal operators into non-temporal operators, while preserving an equivalence on size-1 ultimately periodic words.

Lemma 13
.

Consider two arbitrary
LTL
-formulae
\varphi,\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\textrm{LTL}
. We have:

1.

for all
\bullet\in\{\lX,\lF,\lG\}
, we have
\varphi\Leftrightarrow_{1}\bullet\varphi
;

2.

for all
\bullet\in\{\lU,\lR\}
, we have
\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow_{1}\varphi_{1}\lU\varphi_{2}
;

3.

we have
\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow_{1}\varphi_{1}\lW\varphi_{2}
;

4.

we have
\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow_{1}\varphi_{1}\lM\varphi_{2}
;

Proof.

Consider any
\alpha\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
. Note that for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\alpha^{\omega}[i:]=\alpha^{\omega}
. Therefore:

1.

•

\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}[1:]\models\varphi\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\models\lX\varphi
;

•

\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi\Leftrightarrow\exists i\in\mathbb{N},\;\alpha^{\omega}[i:]\models\varphi\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\models\lF\varphi
;

•

\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi\Leftrightarrow\forall i\in\mathbb{N},\;\alpha^{\omega}[i:]\models\varphi\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\models\lG\varphi;

2.

If
\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi_{2}
, then
\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi_{1}\lU\varphi_{2}
. Furthermore, if
\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi_{1}\lU\varphi_{2}
, then there is some
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha^{\omega}[i:]\models\varphi_{2}
. Hence,
\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi_{2}
. In fact,
\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi_{1}\lU\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi_{2}
.

In addition, we have:

\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi_{1}\lR\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\not\models\neg\varphi_{1}\lU\neg\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\not\models\neg\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi_{2}

3.

\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi_{1}\lW\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\models(\varphi_{1}\lU\varphi_{2})\lor\lG\varphi_{1}\Leftrightarrow\varphi_{2}\lor\varphi_{1}
;

4.

\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi_{1}\lM\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\models(\varphi_{1}\lR\varphi_{2})\wedge\lF\varphi_{1}\Leftrightarrow\varphi_{2}\wedge\varphi_{2}
.

∎

The proof of Lemma
12
is now straightforward, although somewhat long since there are several cases to tackle.

Proof.

We define
\mathsf{tr}:\textrm{LTL}\rightarrow\textrm{LTL}
by induction on
LTL
-formulae. Consider a formula
\varphi\in\textrm{LTL}
:

•

Assume that
\varphi=x
for any
x\in\mathsf{Prop}
. We set
\mathsf{tr}(\varphi):=\varphi
which satisfies conditions 1.-3.; .

•

for all
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Un}
, assume that
\varphi=\bullet\varphi^{\prime}
and that
\mathsf{tr}
is defined on all sub-formulae of
\varphi^{\prime}
and satisfies conditions 1.-3. on
\varphi^{\prime}
.

–

if
\bullet=\lnot
, we set
\mathsf{tr}(\varphi):=\lnot\mathsf{tr}(\varphi^{\prime})
, which ensures that
\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi))=\{\lnot\mathsf{tr}(\varphi^{\prime})\}\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi^{\prime}))\subseteq\mathsf{tr}[\{\lnot\varphi^{\prime}\}]\cup\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi^{\prime})]=\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)]
. Furthermore, for all
\alpha\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
, we have
\alpha^{\omega}\models\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\not\models\mathsf{tr}(\varphi^{\prime})\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\not\models\varphi^{\prime}\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi
. Thus,
\varphi\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)
;

–

if
\bullet\in\{\lX,\lF,\lG\}
, we set
\mathsf{tr}(\varphi):=\mathsf{tr}(\varphi^{\prime})
, which ensures that
\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi))=\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi^{\prime}))\subseteq\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi^{\prime})]\subseteq\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)]
. Furthermore,
\varphi\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)
by Lemma
13
and since
\varphi^{\prime}\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi^{\prime})
.

•

for any
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Bin}
, assume that
\varphi=\varphi_{1}\bullet\varphi_{2}
and that
\mathsf{tr}
is defined on all sub-formulae of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
and satisfies conditions 1.-3. on
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
.

–

if
\bullet\in\{\lor,\wedge,\Rightarrow,\Leftrightarrow\}
, then we set
\mathsf{tr}(\varphi):=\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{1})\bullet\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{2})
, which ensures that
\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi))=\{\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)\}\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{1}))\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{2}))\subseteq\mathsf{tr}[\{\varphi\}]\cup\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{1})]\cup\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{2})]\subseteq\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)]
. Furthermore, since
\varphi_{1}\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{1})
and
\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{2})
, we have
\varphi\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)
;

–

if
\bullet\in\{\lU,\lR\}
, then we set
\mathsf{tr}(\varphi):=\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{2})
, which ensures that
\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi))=\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{2}))\subseteq\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{2})]\subseteq\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)]
. Furthermore,
\varphi\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)
by Lemma
13
and since
\varphi_{1}\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{1})
and
\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{2})
;

–

if
\bullet\in\{\lW\}
, then we set
\mathsf{tr}(\varphi):=\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{1})\lor\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{2})
, which ensures that
\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi))=\{\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)\}\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{1}))\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{2}))\subseteq\mathsf{tr}[\{\varphi\}]\cup\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{1})]\cup\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{2})]\subseteq\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)]
, and
\varphi\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)
by Lemma
13
and since
\varphi_{1}\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{1})
and
\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{2})
;

–

if
\bullet\in\{\lM\}
, then we set
\mathsf{tr}(\varphi):=\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{1})\wedge\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{2})
, which ensures that
\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi))=\{\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)\}\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{1}))\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{2}))\subseteq\mathsf{tr}[\{\varphi\}]\cup\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{1})]\cup\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{2})]\subseteq\mathsf{tr}[\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)]
and
\varphi\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)
by Lemma
13
and since
\varphi_{1}\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{1})
and
\varphi_{2}\Leftrightarrow_{1}\mathsf{tr}(\varphi_{2})
;

This concludes the proof since, by construction, for all
LTL
-formulae
\varphi
, no temporal operator occur in the formula
\mathsf{tr}(\varphi)
.
∎

3.2.2
LTL
-formulae with at least
k
different propositions

Second, we show that for a formula
\varphi
to accept all words in
\mathcal{P}
and reject all words in
\mathcal{N}
, then it must be that that, for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, at least one variable among
\{x_{k},\bar{x_{k}}\}
must occur in
\varphi
. Let first us define below a few notations that will be useful in the following.

Definition 14
.

For all
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we let
X_{k}:=\{x_{k},\bar{x_{k}}\}\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
. Furthermore, for all
\mathsf{I}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we let
X_{\mathsf{I}}\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
denote the set of propositions
X_{\mathsf{I}}:=\cup_{k\in\mathsf{I}}X_{k}
.

Consider an
LTL
-formula
\varphi
. For all subsets of propositions
Y\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
, we say that
Y
occurs in
\varphi
if a proposition in
Y
is a sub-formula of
\varphi
, i.e.
Y\cap\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)\neq\emptyset
.

Furthermore, for all
S\subseteq(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})^{\omega}
, we say that the formula
\varphi
accepts (resp. rejects)
S
if
\varphi
accepts all words in
S
(resp. rejects all words in
S
). We say that
\varphi
distinguishes two sets of words if it accepts one set and rejects the other.

We can now state the lemma of interest for us.

Lemma 15
.

Consider a temporal-free
LTL
-formula
\varphi
. Consider three subsets of propositions
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},Y\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
, and assume that, for all
x\in\mathsf{Prop}\setminus Y
, we have
x\in\alpha_{1}
if and only if
x\in\alpha_{2}
. In that case, if the formula
\varphi
distinguishes the words
\alpha_{1}^{\omega}
and
\alpha_{2}^{\omega}
, then the set
Y
occurs in
\varphi
.

Proof.

Let us prove this property
\mathcal{P}(\varphi)
on
LTL
-formulae
\varphi
by induction. Consider such an
LTL
-formula
\varphi
:

•

Assume that
\varphi=x
for some
x\in\mathsf{Prop}
. Then, if
\varphi
distinguishes
\alpha_{1}^{\omega}
and
\alpha_{2}^{\omega}
, it must be that
x\in\mathsf{Prop}
, by assumption.

•

Assume that
\varphi=\neg\varphi^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{P}(\varphi^{\prime})
holds. Then, if
\varphi
distinguishes
\alpha_{1}^{\omega}
and
\alpha_{2}^{\omega}
, so does
\varphi^{\prime}
. Hence,
\mathcal{P}(\varphi)
follows.

•

Finally, assume that
\varphi=\varphi_{1}\bullet\varphi_{2}
for some
\bullet\in\{\lor,\wedge,\Rightarrow,\Leftrightarrow\}
and that
\mathcal{P}(\varphi_{1})
and
\mathcal{P}(\varphi_{2})
hold. Then, if neither
\varphi_{1}
nor
\varphi_{2}
distinguish
\alpha_{1}^{\omega}
and
\alpha_{2}^{\omega}
, neither does
\varphi
. Hence, assuming that
\varphi
distinguish
\alpha_{1}^{\omega}
and
\alpha_{2}^{\omega}
, then
\mathsf{Prop}\setminus Y
occurs in
\varphi_{1}
or
\varphi_{2}
, and it therefore also occurs in
\varphi
. Hence,
\mathcal{P}(\varphi)
holds.

∎

When applied to the reduction that we have defined in Definition
5
, we obtain the corollary below.

Corollary 16
.

Assume that a temporal-free
LTL
-formula distinguishes
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{N}
. Then, for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, a variable in
X_{k}
must occur in
\varphi
.

Proof.

Let
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. We have
\rho_{k}=X_{k}^{\omega}
and
\eta=\emptyset^{\omega}
. Therefore, by Lemma
15
, if an
LTL
-formula
\varphi
distinguishes
\rho_{k}
and
\eta
, it must be that
X_{k}
occurs in
\varphi
. Since this holds for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
and for all
k,k^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we have
X_{k}\cap X_{k^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset\Rightarrow k=k^{\prime}
, the corollary follows.
∎

3.2.3
Concise formulae

With the lemmas we have proven so far, we know that we can restrict ourselves to
LTL
-formulae:

•

that are temporal-free;

•

where, for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, a variable in
X_{k}
occurs.

In addition, recall that the formulae that we consider are of size at most
B=2m-1
. Our next goal is to establish that, for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, when at least
k
different propositions occur in a formula of size at most
2k-1
, then that formula must be
concise
. Informally, an
LTL
-formula is concise if it never uses any unary operators and never uses several times a sub-formula. We define formally (by induction) this notion below.

Definition 17
.

For all
LTL
-formulae, we let
\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)=\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)\cap\mathsf{Prop}
denote the of propositions occurring in
\varphi
. Then, we define by induction the
concise

LTL
-formulae:

•

for all
x\in\mathsf{Prop}
, the formula
\varphi:=x
is
concise
;

•

given any two concise formulae
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
such that
\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi_{1})\cap\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
, then the formula
\varphi:=\varphi_{1}\bullet\varphi_{2}
is concise for any
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Bin}
.

We denote by
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{cs}}
the set of concise
LTL
-formulae.

For all
Y\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
, we say that an
LTL
-formula
\varphi

represents concisely

Y
if
Y\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)
and
\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi)\leq 2|Y|-1
.

Our goal is now to show the lemma below.

Lemma 18
.

For all
Y\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
, if an
LTL
-formula
\varphi
represents concisely
Y
then it is concise and
Y=\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)
.

In order to prove this lemma, we are first going to prove the lemma below, relating in a given
LTL
-formula, the number of sub-formulae and the number of different propositions that occur.

Lemma 19
.

For all
Y\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
, we let
\mathsf{Occ}(Y),\mathsf{Occ}^{+}(Y),\mathsf{NbSubF}(Y),\mathsf{NbSubF}^{+}(Y)
denote the properties on
LTL
-formulae such that an
LTL
-formula
\varphi
satisfies:

•

\mathsf{Occ}(Y)
if all variables in
Y
occur in
\varphi
, i.e.
Y\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)
;

•

\mathsf{Occ}^{+}(Y)
if all variables in
Y
occur in
\varphi
, i.e.
Y\subsetneq\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)
;

•

\mathsf{NbSubF}(Y)
if there are at least
2|Y|-1
different sub-formulae of
\varphi
where
Y
occurs;

•

\mathsf{NbSubF}^{+}(Y)
if there are at least
2|Y|
different sub-formulae of
\varphi
where
Y
occurs.

Then, consider any
LTL
-formula
\varphi
on
\mathsf{Prop}
. If
\varphi
satisfies
\mathsf{Occ}(Y)
(resp.
\mathsf{Occ}^{+}(Y)
), then it also satisfies
\mathsf{NbSubF}(Y)
(resp.
\mathsf{NbSubF}^{+}(Y)
).

Proof.

Let us show this lemma by induction on
LTL
-formulae
\varphi
:

•

Assume that
\varphi=x
. Then,
\varphi
satisfies
\mathsf{Occ}(Y)
only for
Y=\emptyset
and
Y=\{x\}
, and it also satisfies
\mathsf{NbSubF}(\emptyset)
and
\mathsf{NbSubF}(\{x\})
. Furthermore,
\varphi
satisfies
\mathsf{Occ}^{+}(Y)
only for
Y=\emptyset
, and it also satisfies
\mathsf{NbSubF}^{+}(\emptyset)
;

•

for all
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Un}
, assume that
\varphi=\bullet\varphi^{\prime}
. Consider any
Y\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
. If
\varphi
satisfies
\mathsf{Occ}(Y)
(resp.
\mathsf{Occ}^{+}(Y)
), then so does
\varphi^{\prime}
. Hence,
\varphi^{\prime}
satisfies
\mathsf{NbSubF}(Y)
(resp.
\mathsf{NbSubF}^{+}(Y)
), and therefore so does
\varphi
;

•

for all
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Bin}
, assume that
\varphi=\varphi_{1}\bullet\varphi_{2}
. Consider any
Y\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
and assume that
\varphi
satisfies
\mathsf{Occ}(Y)
. Let
Y_{1}:=\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi_{1})\cap Y\subseteq Y
denote the set of different propositions in
Y
occurring in
\varphi_{1}
(they may also occur in
\varphi_{2}
). Let also
Y_{2}^{\prime}:=(\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi_{2})\setminus\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi_{1}))\cap Y\subseteq Y
denote the set of different propositions in
Y
occurring in
\varphi_{2}
and not in
\varphi_{1}
. We have
Y=Y_{1}\cup Y_{2}^{\prime}
. Furthermore:

–

by our induction hypothesis on
\varphi_{1}
, there are at least
k_{1}:=2|Y_{1}|-1
sub-formulae in
\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{1})\subseteq\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)
where
Y_{1}
occurs;

–

by our induction hypothesis on
\varphi_{2}
, there are also at least
k_{2}^{\prime}:=2|Y_{2}^{\prime}|-1
sub-formulae in
\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{2})\subseteq\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)
where
Y^{\prime}_{2}
occurs. By definition of
Y^{\prime}_{2}
, it follows that all these sub-formulae are not sub-formulae of
\varphi_{1}
;

–

Finally, the sub-formula
\varphi
itself is a sub-formula of
\varphi
that is neither a sub-formula of
\varphi_{1}
nor of
\varphi_{2}
and where
Y
occurs.

Therefore, there are at least
k_{1}+k_{2}^{\prime}+1=2|Y_{1}|-1+2|Y^{\prime}_{2}|-1+1\geq 2|Y|-1
different sub-formulae of
\varphi
where
Y
occurs. That is,
\varphi
satisfies
\mathsf{NbSubF}(Y)
. Furthermore, if
\varphi
satisfies also
\mathsf{Occ}^{+}(Y)
, then
\mathsf{Prop}\setminus Y
occurs in
\varphi_{1}
or
\varphi_{2}
. Therefore, the first item holds with
k_{1}=2|Y_{1}|
or the second item holds with
k_{2}^{\prime}=2|Y_{2}^{\prime}|
. In both cases, we obtain that they are at least
k_{1}+k_{2}^{\prime}+1\geq 2|Y|
different sub-formulae of
\varphi
where
Y
occurs. That is,
\varphi
satisfies
\mathsf{NbSubF}^{+}(Y)
.

∎

We can now proceed to the proof of Lemma
18
.

Proof.

We show by induction on
LTL
-formulae
\varphi
the following property

: for all

, if

•

if

for all

•

for all

. Consider any

and assume that

. Let

. Let

and
Y_{1,2}:=\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi_{1})\cap\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi_{2})
. Let
k_{1}:=|Y_{1}|
,
k_{2}:=|Y_{2}|
and
k_{1,2}:=|Y_{1,2}|
. We have
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{1,2}\geq k
and
|\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)|=1+|\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{1})\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{2})|
, hence
|\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{1})\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{2})|\leq 2k-2
.

Now, let us assume towards a contradiction that
Y_{1,2}\neq\emptyset
. In that case, by Lemma
19
, since
\varphi_{1}
satisfies
\mathsf{Occ}^{+}(Y_{1})
, it follows that there are at least
2k_{1}
sub-formulae of
\varphi_{1}
where
X_{1}
occurs. Recall that
X_{1}
does not occur in
\varphi_{2}
. It follows that:

|\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{2})|\leq 2k-2-2k_{1}\leq 2(k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{1,2})-2-2k_{1}=2(k_{2}+k_{1,2})-2

However, by Lemma
19
, since
\varphi_{2}
satisfies
\mathsf{Occ}(Y_{1,2}\cup Y_{2})
, it follows that
\varphi_{2}
has at least
2(k_{2}+k_{1,2})-1
sub-formulae, which is a contradiction. In fact,
Y_{1,2}=\emptyset
.

By Lemma
19
, there are at least
2k_{1}-1
(resp.
2k_{2}-1
) sub-formulae in
\varphi_{1}
(resp.
\varphi_{2}
) where
Y_{1}
(resp.
Y_{2}
) occurs. Therefore:

2k-1\geq|\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)|=1+|\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{1})\cup\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{2})|\geq 1+2k_{1}-1+2k_{2}-1=2(k_{1}+k_{2})-1

We obtain that
k=k_{1}+k_{2}
,
|\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi)|=2k-1
,
|\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{1})|=2k_{1}-1
and
|\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{2})|=2k_{2}-1
. Therefore:

–

\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi_{1})=|\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{1})|=2k_{1}-1
, hence
\varphi_{1}
represents concisely
Y_{1}
. Hence, by our induction hypothesis,
\varphi_{1}
is concise;

–

similarly,
\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi_{2})=|\mathsf{SubF}(\varphi_{2})|=2k_{2}-1
, hence
\varphi_{2}
represents concisely
Y_{2}
. Hence, by our induction hypothesis,
\varphi_{2}
is concise;

–

Y_{1,2}=\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi_{1})\cap\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi_{2})=\emptyset
.

Hence,
\varphi
is concise.

We can conclude that
\mathcal{P}(\varphi)
holds for all
LTL
-formulae
\varphi
.

Consider now any
LTL
-formula
\varphi
and any
Y\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
. If
\varphi
represents concisely
Y
, then by
\mathcal{P}(\varphi)
, we have that
\varphi
is concise. Furthermore, by Lemma
19
, it must be that
\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)\setminus Y=\emptyset
since
\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi)\leq 2|Y|-1
. Therefore,
Y=\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)
and
\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi)=2|Y|-1
.
∎

3.2.4
Disjunctive
LTL
-formulae

The lemmas that we have proven so far allow us to restrict the kind of
LTL
-formulae that we need to consider. We know that it is sufficient to consider the
LTL
-formulae satisfying the following:

•

they are are temporal-free;

•

for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, a variable in
X_{k}
occurs;

•

they are concise.

However, these restrictions are not enough to prove that the premise of Lemma
10
holds, since the
LTL
-formulae that we need to consider may use any of the four binary logical connectors.

Let us give below the definition of the
LTL
-formulae that are interested in.

Definition 20
.

Consider some
\mathsf{I}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}
. An
LTL
-formula
\varphi
is
disjunctive on
\mathsf{I}
if:

•

\varphi
is temporal free;

•

\varphi
is concise;

•

the only binary logical operator occurring in
\varphi
is the
\lor
operator;

•

|\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)|=|\mathsf{I}|
and for all
k\in\mathsf{I}
, we have
|\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)\cap X_{k}|=1
.

A simple proof by induction shows that if an
LTL
-formula
\varphi
is disjunctive on
\{1,\ldots,m\}
, then it is of the shape:

\varphi=z_{1}\lor z_{2}\lor\cdots\lor z_{m}

where, for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we have
z_{k}\in X_{k}=\{x_{k},\bar{x_{k}}\}
.

Then, we define what it means for us that an
LTL
-formula implies a disjunction.

Definition 21
.

Consider any
\mathsf{I}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}
and an
LTL
-formula
\varphi
. We say that
\varphi

implies a disjunction on
\mathsf{I}
if there exists an
LTL
-formula
\varphi^{\prime}
that is a disjunction on
\mathsf{I}
such that, for all
\alpha\subseteq X_{\mathsf{I}}
, if
\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi
, then
\alpha\models\varphi^{\prime}
.

Furthermore, we say that
\varphi
is
\mathsf{I}
-concise
if it is concise,
|\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)|=|\mathsf{I}|
and for all
k\in\mathsf{I}
, we have
|\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)\cap X_{k}|=1
.

Our goal is now to establish the lemma below.

Lemma 22
.

Consider a temporal-free
LTL
-formula
\varphi
that is
\{1,\ldots,m\}
-concise and assume that it accepts
\mathcal{P}
and rejects
\mathcal{N}
. Then,
\varphi
implies a disjunction on
\{1,\ldots,m\}
.

It may seem that the proof of this lemma is a straightforward application of well-known results of propositional logic about formulae in conjunctive normal form (CNF). Indeed, since we are considering a temporal-free
LTL
-formula, it can be seen as a formula in propositional logic. In this logic, the fact that a formula implies a disjunction is well known: given the expression of this formula in CNF, any clause is a disjunction implied by the formula. However, there are crucial differences here: first, in the disjunctions that we consider, there cannot be any negation. Nonetheless, one can say that
\lnot x_{1}
is equivalent to
\bar{x_{1}}
and
\lnot\bar{x_{1}}
is equivalent to
x_{1}
, since the proposition
\bar{x}
is meant as a stand in for
\lnot x
. But the above equivalences do not hold with the
LTL
semantics. The reason for that is that
LTL
-formulae are evaluated on subsets of propositions that may not consider at all a variable in
X
(i.e. neither
x_{k}
not
\bar{x_{k}}
appears in the subset for some
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
). Hence, it is not straightforward, a priori, to come up with a disjunction that is implied by
LTL
-formulae of interest. Let us illustrate this discussion on an example.

Example 23
.

Let us come back to the
\mathsf{SAT}
-formula from Example
6
. Consider the
LTL
-formula
\varphi
below:

\varphi:=(x_{1}\Leftrightarrow x_{2})\Leftrightarrow x_{3}

One can check that this formula accepts
\mathcal{P}
and rejects
\mathcal{N}
, as defined in Example
6
. Furthermore, in propositional logic, this formula implies the disjunction defined below:

C=\lnot x_{1}\lor\lnot x_{2}\lor x_{3}

Let us first explain why we do not allow negations in the disjunctions that we consider. To do so, let us assume that the
\mathsf{SAT}
-formula
\Phi^{\prime}
that we consider is defined by
\Phi^{\prime}=\Phi\wedge\Phi_{3}
where
\Phi_{3}
is either equal to
x_{3}
or
\lnot x_{3}
. Then, the formula
C
would accept
\mathcal{P}
and reject
\mathcal{N}
regardless of what
\Phi_{3}
is equal to, which is not what we want. In a way,
C
accepts
\Phi_{3}
not because it has properly chosen either
x_{3}
or
\bar{x_{3}}
but because it has negated another variable (
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
).

This is why we do not want negations in the disjunctions that we consider. Hence, let us write
C
as a disjunction without negation. We obtain:

\tilde{C}=\bar{x_{1}}\lor\bar{x_{2}}\lor x_{3}

However, it is now not true that
\varphi
implies
\tilde{C}
. This is witnessed e.g. by the infinite word
(\{x_{1}\})^{\omega}
that is accepted by
\varphi
and rejected by
\tilde{C}
.

By the way, one can check that the formula
\varphi
implies on
\{1,2,3\}
the disjunction
x_{1}\lor x_{2}\lor x_{3}
.

Nonetheless the proof of Lemma
22
is not complicated, though it is quite long as it involves a proof by induction, with several cases to handle.

. Hence,
\alpha_{\mathsf{I}_{1}}\models\varphi^{d}_{1}
and
\alpha_{\mathsf{I}_{1}}\models\varphi^{d}
. Therefore
\alpha\models\varphi^{d}
since
\varphi^{d}
is a disjunction.

Otherwise (i.e. if

), then
\alpha^{\omega}\models\varphi_{2}
. In that case, by Lemma
15
, it follows that
(\alpha_{\mathsf{I}_{2}})^{\omega}\models\varphi_{2}

\alpha_{\mathsf{I}_{2}}\subseteq X_{\mathsf{I}_{2}}

\alpha_{\mathsf{I}_{2}}\models\varphi^{d}_{2}

\alpha\models\varphi^{d}
since
\varphi^{d}
is a disjunction.

Hence,
\varphi
implies a disjunction on
\mathsf{I}
.

(b)

Assume that
\varphi_{1}
rejects
\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{I}_{1}}
. Then,
\varphi_{2}
accept
\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{I}_{1}}

\varphi_{2}

\mathcal{N}

\varphi_{1}

Therefore

\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{I}_{1}}

\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{I}_{2}}

\mathcal{N}

\neg\varphi_{1}
(resp.
\neg\varphi_{2}

\varphi^{d}_{\neg 1}

\mathsf{I}_{1}

\varphi^{d}_{\neg 1}

\mathsf{I}_{2}

\mathsf{I}

Consider any
\alpha\subseteq X_{\mathsf{I}}

Otherwise (i.e. if

3.3
Discussion

We conclude this section by a discussion on what our proof shows, and what it does not. First, our proof shows that the decision problem is
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard. However, it also shows on the way that variants of this problem are
\mathsf{NP}
-hard. More precisely:

•

Since our reduction only uses size-1 infinite words, our proof shows that the restriction
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}^{=1}
of the decision problem
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
to size-1 infinite words is still
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

•

Further, since in the proof of Lemma
7
, the formula that we exhibit has size exactly
B
, it follows that the variant of
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
where we look for an
LTL
-formula of size exactly
B
, instead of being at most
B
, is still
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

•

Any variant of
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
where only the set unary operators are changed is still
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

•

Any variant of
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
with less binary operators, while keeping either the
\lor
binary logical operator or the
\lW
binary temporal operator (recall Lemma
13
) is still
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

However, they are still many unanswered questions:

•

We have only considered classical binary operators, hence we do not handle arbitrary binary operators (seen as functions
\{\top,\bot\}^{2}\rightarrow\{\top,\bot\}
). We believe that this could be done, however, this would add several cases to the proof of Lemma
22
.

•

Assuming that we have achieved the previous item, we believe that we could then handle arbitrary binary temporal operators (seen as functions
(\{\top,\bot\}^{\omega})^{2}\rightarrow\{\top,\bot\}
), because we consider size-1 infinite words. Hence, we could extend Lemma
13
to handle such arbitrary binary temporal operators.

•

Overall, we believe that our work can be slightly adapted and/or extended to show that any fragment of
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
that still contains
\lor
or
\lW
(the latter being a “temporal version “ of the former) as binary operators is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard, regardless of what other operators are added or removed. However, we do not know what happens in a variant of
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
where the operator
\lor
(and any of its temporal version) cannot be used anymore. We believe that we can still prove that it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard assuming that the
\wedge
operator can be used (or any of its temporal version, such as
\lM
, recall Lemma
13
). The reduction for the
\wedge
case could be seen as the dual of the reduction for the
\lor
case that we have presented in this paper. However, we do not know how to handle other operators. For instance, is the decision problem
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
still
\mathsf{NP}
-hard if the only binary operator that can be used is
\Leftrightarrow
? (Note that this question is asked from a purely theoretical point of view, whether or not such a restriction would make sense in practice is also important to consider.)

4
CTL
learning is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard

The goal of this section is to show the theorem below.

Theorem 24
.

The decision problem
\textrm{CTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

We proceed via a reduction from the decision
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}^{=1}
that is the variant of
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
where all ultimately periodic words are of size 1. To properly define this reduction, let us first define how to translate a size-1 ultimately periodic word into a single-state Kripke structure.

Definition 25
.

Consider a set of propositions
\mathsf{Prop}
and a size-1 ultimately periodic word
w=\alpha^{\omega}\in(2^{\mathsf{Prop}})^{\omega}
for some
\alpha\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
. We define the Kripke structure
M_{w}=(S,I,R,L)
where:

•

S:=\{q\}
;

•

I:=S=\{q\}
;

•

R:=\{(q,q)\}
;

•

L(q):=\alpha\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
.

Before we define our reduction, we want to express what the above definition satisfies. To do so, we define below how to translate
CTL
-formulae into
LTL
-formulae.

Definition 26
.

Consider a set of propositions
\mathsf{Prop}
. We let
\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}:\textrm{CTL}\rightarrow\textrm{LTL}
be such that it removes all
\exists
or
\forall
quantifiers from a
CTL
-formula, while keeping all other operators. More formally:

•

\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(p)=p
for all
p\in\mathsf{Prop}
;

•

\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\neg\varphi)=\neg\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\varphi)
;

•

\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\diamond(\bullet\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\varphi))=\bullet\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\varphi))
for all
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Un}^{\mathsf{temp}}
and
\diamond\in\{\exists,\forall\}
;

•

\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\varphi_{1}\bullet\varphi_{2})=\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\varphi_{1})\bullet\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\varphi_{2})
for all
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Un}^{\mathsf{lg}}
;

•

\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\diamond(\varphi_{1}\bullet\varphi_{2}))=\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\varphi_{1})\bullet\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\varphi_{2})
for all
\bullet\in\mathsf{Op}_{Un}^{\mathsf{temp}}
and
\diamond\in\{\exists,\forall\}
.

A straightforward proof by induction shows that the above definition satisfies the lemma below:

Lemma 27
.

Consider a set of propositions
\mathsf{Prop}
and any
CTL
-formula
\varphi
. We have:

•

\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi)=\mathsf{Sz}(\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\varphi))
;

•

for all
\alpha\subseteq\mathsf{Prop}
, we have
\alpha^{\omega}\models\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\varphi)
if and only if
M_{\alpha^{\omega}}\models\varphi
.

Furthermore, the function
\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{LTL}-\textrm{CTL}}:\textrm{LTL}\rightarrow\textrm{CTL}
that adds an
\exists
quantifier in an
LTL
-formula before every temporal operator, and therefore that defines a
CTL
-formula that way, ensures the following:

•

for all
LTL
-formulae
\varphi
,
\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi)=\mathsf{Sz}(\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{LTL}-\textrm{CTL}}(\varphi))
;

•

for all
LTL
-formulae
\varphi
, we have
\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{CTL}-\textrm{LTL}}(\mathsf{f}_{\textrm{LTL}-\textrm{CTL}}(\varphi))=\varphi
.

We can now define the reduction that we consider.

Definition 28
.

Consider an instance
\mathsf{In}_{\textrm{LTL}}=(\mathsf{Prop},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N},B)
of the
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}^{=1}
decision problem. We define the input
\mathsf{In}_{\textrm{CTL}}=(\mathsf{Prop},\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{N}^{\prime},B)
with:

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}:=\{M_{w}\mid w\in\mathcal{P}\}

(2)

and
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
is defined similarly from
\mathcal{N}
.

Clearly this reduction can be computed in polynomial time. Let us now show that it satisfies the desired property.

Lemma 29
.

The input
\mathsf{In}_{\textrm{LTL}}
is a positive instance of the
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}^{=1}
decision problem if and only if
\mathsf{In}_{\textrm{CTL}}
is a positive instance of the
\textrm{CTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
decision problem.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathsf{In}_{\textrm{LTL}}
is a positive instance of the
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}^{=1}
decision problem. Let
\varphi_{\textrm{LTL}}
be an
LTL
-formula separating
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{N}
of size at most
B
. Consider the
CTL
-formula
\varphi_{\textrm{CTL}}:=\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{LTL}-\mathsf{CTL}}(\varphi_{\textrm{LTL}})
. By Lemma
27
, we have
\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi_{\textrm{CTL}})=\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi_{\textrm{LTL}})\leq B
and
\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{CTL}-\mathsf{LTL}}(\varphi_{\textrm{CTL}})=\varphi_{\textrm{LTL}}
.
Consider now any
M_{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
with
w\in\mathcal{P}
. Again by Lemma
27
, we have
M_{w}\models\varphi_{\textrm{CTL}}
if and only if
w\models\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{CTL}-\mathsf{LTL}}(\varphi_{\textrm{CTL}})=\varphi_{\textrm{LTL}}
, since
w
is a size-1 ultimately periodic word. Since
w\in\mathcal{P}
, it follows that
w\models\varphi_{\textrm{LTL}}
and
M_{w}\models\varphi_{\textrm{CTL}}
. This is similar for any
M_{w}\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
with
w\in\mathcal{N}
. Hence,
\mathsf{In}_{\textrm{CTL}}
is a positive instance of the
\textrm{CTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
decision problem.

Assume now that
\mathsf{In}_{\textrm{CTL}}
is a positive instance of the
\textrm{CTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
decision problem. Let
\varphi_{\textrm{CTL}}
be a
CTL
-formula separating
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
of size at most
B
. Consider the
LTL
-formula
\varphi_{\textrm{LTL}}:=\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{CTL}-\mathsf{LTL}}(\varphi_{\textrm{CTL}})
. By Lemma
27
, we have
\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi_{\textrm{LTL}})=\mathsf{Sz}(\varphi_{\textrm{CTL}})\leq B
. Consider now any
w\in\mathcal{P}
. Again, by Lemma
27
, we have
M_{w}\models\varphi_{\textrm{CTL}}
if and only if
w\models\varphi_{\textrm{LTL}}
, since
w
is a size-1 ultimately periodic word. Since
M_{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, it follows that
M_{w}\models\varphi_{\textrm{CTL}}
and
w\models\varphi_{\textrm{LTL}}
. This is similar for any
w\in\mathcal{N}
. Hence,
\mathsf{In}_{\textrm{LTL}}
is a positive instance of the
\textrm{LTL}_{\mathsf{Learn}}
decision problem.
∎

The proof of Theorem
24
is now direct.

Proof.

It is straightforward consequence of Lemma
29
and the fact that the reduction from Definition
28
can be computed in polynomial time.
∎

5
Conclusion

We have proved that both
LTL
learning and
CTL
learning are
\mathsf{NP}
-hard. As argued in the introduction and stated in Proposition
3
, we obtain that both
LTL
learning and
CTL
learning are
\mathsf{NP}
-complete. Hence, it would seem that
LTL
learning and
CTL
learning are equivalently hard. However, we believe that, in some sense,
CTL
learning is harder than
LTL
learning. As a future work, we plan to investigate syntactical restrictions on formulae such that the resulting
LTL
fragment becomes easy (i.e. it could now be decided in polynomial time) whereas the resulting
CTL
fragment stays
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.
