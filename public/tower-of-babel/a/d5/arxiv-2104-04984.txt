Adiabatic approximation for the motion of Ginzburg-Landau vortex filaments
Jingxuan Zhang

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the concentration property of solutions to the dispersive Ginzburg-Landau (or Gross-Pitaevskii) equation in three dimensions. On a spatial domain, it has long been conjectured that such a solution concentrates near some curve evolving according to the binormal curvature flow, and conversely, that a curve moving this way can be realized in a suitable sense by some solution to the dispersive Ginzburg-Landau equation. Some partial results are known with rather strong symmetry assumptions. Our main theorems here provide affirmative answer to both conjectures under certain small curvature assumption. The results are valid for small but fixed material parameter in the equation, in contrast to the general practice to take this parameter to its zero limit. The advantage is that we can retain precise description of the vortex filament structure. The results hold on a long but finite time interval, depending on the curvature assumption.



1
Introduction

Consider the dispersive Ginzburg-Landau (or Gross-Pitaevskii) equation on a spatial domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

i\tfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\Delta\psi+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}(\left\lvert\psi\right\rvert^{2}-1)\psi.

(1)

This equation has its origin from condensed matter physics.
The complex scalar field
\psi:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
describes the Bose-Einstein
condensate in superfluidity, or, ignoring the effect of magnetic
field, the electronic condensate in superconductivity.
Here
\epsilon>0
is a material parameter measuring the characteristic length scale of
\left\lvert\psi\right\rvert
.

Realistically, the coherent length

\epsilon
is very small compared to the size of the domain
\Omega
.
Thus in what follows we take

\Omega:=\left\{(x,z):x\in\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},z\in I:=[0,1]\right\},

(2)

where
\omega
is a bounded
domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, whose size is large compared to the scale
\epsilon
.
The choice
I=[0,1]
can be replaced by any interval
[a,b]
with
b-a\gg\epsilon
,
which amounts to a change of coordinate along
z
-direction.
The reason for taking a bounded domain is to
circumvent certain undesirable decay properties
that are not essential to our argument
(see Sec.
2.1
for details).
In addition, for technical reason we also assume
\omega
is star-shaped
around the origin, and the boundary
\partial\omega
is smooth.

On this cylindrical domain
\Omega
, we impose the boundary conditions

\left\lvert\psi\right\rvert\to 1
as
x
approaches
\partial\omega
horizontally, and
\psi(x,0)=\psi(x,1)

for every
x\in\omega
.
Note that through the transform
\psi=e^{i\epsilon^{-2}t}u
, equation (
1
)
becomes the cubic defocusing subcritical nonlinear Schrödinger equation.
Global well-posedness for such equations has been known since the eighties
[
13
]
.
Using standard blow-up arguments, one can show that
for every initial configuration
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying
the boundary conditions above, there exists
a unique global solution
u_{t}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
to (
1
)
generated by
u_{0}
.
Therefore in this paper we will mainly work with
Sobolev spaces
H^{k},\,k\geq 1
.

1.1
The geometric structure of (
1
)

The dispersive Ginzburg-Landau equation (
1
) can be viewed as a Hamiltonian system as follows.
Let
X\subset L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
be a suitable configuration space for (
1
).
If we identify the space
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})=L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\times L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})

through
u\mapsto(\Re u,\Im u)
, then we can regard
X
as
a real vector space,
equipped with
the inner product
\langle(\Re u,\Im u),\,(\Re v,\Im v)\rangle=\int(\Re u\Re v+\Im u\Im v).

Under this identification, the operator

J:\psi\mapsto-i\psi
can be represented by the symplectic matrix

J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

Thus we say
J
is a symplectic operator, in the sense that
it induces a symplectic form
\tau=\langle J\cdot,\,\cdot\rangle
on
X
, satisfying

\tau({w},{w^{\prime}})=-\tau({w^{\prime}},{w})
for every
w,w^{\prime}\in X
.

Using the operator
J
, we can write (
1
) as the Hamiltonian system

\tfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=JE^{\prime}(\psi),\quad E(\psi)=E_{\Omega}^{\epsilon}(\psi):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert\nabla\psi\right\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{4\epsilon^{2}}\left(\left\lvert\psi\right\rvert^{2}-1\right)^{2}.

(4)

Here
E
is the Ginzburg-Landau energy, measuring the difference
of Helmholtz free energy between a transition phase
\psi
and the normal phase.

E^{\prime}(\psi)
denotes the
L^{2}
-gradient of the energy functional w.r.t. the real inner product given above.
This gradient
E^{\prime}(\psi)
is given explicitly as the r.h.s. of (
1
).

It is well-known by now that as the material parameter
\epsilon\to 0
in
(
1
), the energy
E_{\Omega}^{\epsilon}
of a
finite-energy configuration
\psi

concentrates near some lower dimensional
manifold
\gamma
.
This
concentration phenomenon can be made precise
in measure theoretic terms. For instance, see
[
17
,
2
,
3
]
.
This leads one to expect, at least at the heuristic level,
that as
\epsilon\to 0
, (
4
) reduces, in an appropriate manner, to
a Hamiltonian system in the space of curves,

\tfrac{\partial\gamma}{\partial t}=\mathcal{J}L^{\prime}(\gamma),

(5)

where
\gamma=\gamma_{t}
is a
C^{1}
path of curves in
\Omega
,
and
\mathcal{J}
is a suitable (symplectic) operator.

1.2
Outline of the main results

Suppose
\gamma
in (
5
) is parametrized by arclength,
L(\gamma)=\int\left\lvert\partial_{s}\gamma\right\rvert^{2}
,
L^{\prime}(\gamma)=-\partial_{ss}\gamma
, and
\mathcal{J}:=-\partial_{s}\gamma\times
.
Then (
5
) becomes the binormal curvature flow (
41
).
The purpose of this paper is to formulate rigorously the connection
between the dispersive Ginzburg-Landau equation (
1
) and the binormal curvature flow.

To this end, we first make precise the notion of
concentration set in Lemma
2
for a class of low energy configurations.
Then our first main result, Theorem
2
, states that if
u_{t}
is a solution
to (
1
) generated by
u_{0}
, and
u_{0}
has concentration set
\gamma_{0}
,
then the flow
u_{t}
has concentration set given in the leading order by
\gamma_{t}
, the flow generated by
\gamma_{0}
under the binormal
curvature flow (
41
).
As a corollary, we derive the second main result, Theorem
3
, which states the converse: If
\gamma_{t}
is the flow generated by
\gamma_{0}
,
then we can find some solution
u_{t}
to (
1
) such that the concentration
set associated to
u_{t}
is given in the leading order by
\gamma_{t}
.
Both main results hold on some long but possibly finite time interval
depending on the initial configuration.

The precise statements of the main results of this paper are as follows:

.

where

Moreover, for

, the flow of moduli

evolves according

where the operator

is defined in (

).

2.

For any
\beta>0
, there exist

).
Let

),

and

with

, we have

(8)

(9)

where for

,

Remark 1
.

In the formulation of Theorem
1
, we do not adopt the convention
of taking
\epsilon\to 0
. Instead, for small but fixed
\epsilon
, the main assumption is that the concentration sets

\gamma_{t},\,t\geq 0
have uniformly small curvature. (See the definitions
of the various spaces in Theorem
1
in Sec.
2.2
.)
This allows us to retain precise description of the vortex structure of the
evolving configurations.

We obtain the results in Theorem
1
by developing an adiabatic
approximation scheme for (
1
). This allows us to decompose a flow
u_{t}
solving (
1
) to a slowly
evolving main part
v_{t}

and a uniformly small remainder
w_{t}:=u_{t}-v_{t}
. The flow
v_{t}
consists of some low energy configurations
whose motion is explicitly governed by their concentration sets
\gamma_{t}
.
The small curvature assumption on
\gamma_{t}
is used here to ensure
v_{t}
has low energy,
which is essential for the validity of adiabatic approximation. (If the curvature is large at a point on the concentration curve, then the speed of the vortex filament at this point is large, and consequently the adiabatic approximation breaks down.)

The main technical ingredients include 1. to show that the concentration sets of
v_{t}
indeed evolve according to the binormal curvature flow (
41
) to the leading order,
assuming the the remainder
w_{t}
is small

and 2. to get apriori estimate on the remainder
w_{t}
. The second part is analogous
to proving dynamical stability of solitons, except for that the configurations
v_{t}
need not to
be stationary.

1.3
Historical remark

Let us briefly comment on the existing literature on related problems.
If the evolution (
1
) is instead the
energy dissipative
dynamics (so that (

) becomes a cubic nonlinear heat equation), rigorous result
that formulates the kind of connection in which we are interested is known
[

]
. This is one example of a vast literature in the

scheme.
See an excellent review
[
16
]
of works along this line.
Note our method is completely different from the measure theoretic schemes for the

limit.
One can also ask
similar questions about the hyperbolic and gauged analogues of (

). The method we develop in the present paper is applicable to the latter cases,
which we will treat elsewhere.

The problem we study here is motivated by a conjecture of R.L. Jerrard
[
16
, Conj. 7.1]
. The method we use here is inspired by
a series of papers by I.M. Sigal with several co-authors
[
20
,
19
,
14
,
12
,
10
,
5
]
.
Other results along this line which we have referred to include
[
26
,
27
,
23
,
6
,
28
,
15
,
11
,
9
,
7
,
8
]
.
Most of these papers consider planar problems (possibly after some symmetry reduction),
or
higher dimensional problems with
concentration sets given by finite collections of points. In this regard,
the geometry of our problem is more involved.

1.4
Arrangement

The structure of this paper is as follows: In Section 2, we construct the
manifold
M
consisting of what we call approximate vortex filaments.
These are low energy configurations with explicit concentration sets.
We find a path of appropriate approximate filaments
v_{t}

as the “adiabatic part” of a given
solution
u_{t}
to (
1
), provided
u_{t}
stays uniformly close to
M
.
In Section 3, we show that the path
v_{t}
as above
is governed by the binormal curvature flow (
41
).
We prove then two main theorems by finding an apriori estimate for
w_{t}:=u_{t}-v_{t}
,
valid on a long but finite interval.
The proofs rely on the adiabaticity of the approximate filaments, as well as the energy conservation for (
1
). In Section 4, we discuss the properties of certain linearized operators involved in the preceding analysis.
In Appendix, we recall some basic
concepts of Fréchet derivatives that we use repeatedly, and collect various
technical estimates for operators used earlier.

Notations

Throughout the paper, when no confusion arises, we shall drop the time dependence
t
in subscripts.
A domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open connected subset.
The symbols
L^{p}(\Omega),\,H^{s}(\Omega),\,0<p\leq\infty,\,s\in\mathbb{R}
denote respectively the Lebesgue space of order
p
, and
the Sobolev space of order
s
, consisting of functions from a domain

\Omega
into
\mathbb{C}
.

2
Approximate vortex filaments

In this section, we construct the manifold of approximate (vortex) filaments
and discuss their key properties.

2.1
The planar 1-vortex

On the entire plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, it is well-known that (
1
) has smooth, stationary, radially symmetric solutions

\psi^{(n)}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
, where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
labels the winding number

\deg\psi|_{\left\lvert x\right\rvert=R}=n

for large
R\gg 0
.
The characteristic feature of
\psi^{(n)}
is that they
concentrate near the origin
r=0
.
Among these vortices, the simple ones with
\left\lvert n\right\rvert=1
are stable, and
the higher order ones with
\left\lvert n\right\rvert>1
are unstable
[
21
]
.

For this reason, in what follows we will only be concerned with
the
1
-vortex.

Write
\psi^{(1)}=\varphi(r)e^{i\theta}
in polar coordinate. Then
\varphi\in C^{\infty}
,
\varphi^{\prime}>0

for
r>0
, and the following
asymptotics hold
[
22
, Sec. 3.1]
:

\varphi\sim 1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2r^{2}}\quad(r\to\infty),\quad\varphi\sim\frac{r}{\epsilon}-\frac{r^{3}}{8\epsilon^{3}}\quad(r\to 0).

(10)

The planar stationary equation

-\Delta\psi+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\left\lvert\psi\right\rvert^{2}-1\right)\psi=0,\quad\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}

is called the the (ungauged) Ginzburg-Landau equation. It has rotation, translation,
and global gauge symmetries. Among these the, latter two classes
are broken by
\psi^{(1)}
.
Consequently, if
L^{(1)}_{x}:H^{2}(\omega)\to L^{2}(\omega)

is the linearized operator at
\psi^{(1)}
,
then the vectors

\tfrac{\partial\psi^{(1)}}{\partial x_{j}},\,i\psi^{(1)},\quad j=1,2

are in the kernel of
L^{(1)}_{x}
. We call these vectors
the symmetry zero modes.

Note that these modes
are not in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

On a bounded star-shaped domain
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
around the origin, the following estimates are well-known

[
4
, Chap. III, X]
:

\displaystyle E_{\omega}(\psi^{(1)})\leq\pi\left\lvert\log\epsilon\right\rvert+C(\omega),

(11)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\psi^{(1)}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\omega)}\leq 1,

(12)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\psi^{(1)}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\omega)}\leq\frac{C(\omega)}{\epsilon},

(13)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\psi^{(1)}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}\leq C(\omega)\left\lvert\log\epsilon\right\rvert^{1/2},

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\omega}\left(\left\lvert\psi^{(1)}\right\rvert^{2}-1\right)^{2}\leq C(\omega).

(15)

The finite energy property (
11
) fails if
\omega
is unbounded.
Consequently, if
\omega
is unbounded, then in general (say
\omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}
), even the simple
planar vortex
\psi^{(1)}
has infinite
energy. Moreover, the translation zero modes are not
L^{2}
.

One can study the problem with
\omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
either using some kind of renormalized energy
[
21
]
, or
posing the problem in a weighted Sobolev space
[
22
]
.
Though it will incur significantly more involved estimates,
we believe the arguments below can extend to the non-compact case using one of these methods.

2.2
Construction of approximate vortex filaments

To construct the manifold of approximate vortex filaments,
we first define some configuration spaces.
Put

\displaystyle X^{s}:=\left\{\psi\in H^{s}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}):\psi|_{\partial\omega\times I}=0,\;\psi(x,0)=\psi(x,1)\text{ for every }x\in\omega\right\},

(16)

\displaystyle Y^{k}:=\mathbb{R}\times C^{k}(I,\mathbb{R}^{2})\quad(s\in\mathbb{R},\,k\in\mathbb{N}).

(17)

We write elements in
Y^{k}
as
\sigma=(\lambda,\gamma)
.

X^{s},\,Y^{k}
are real Hilbert spaces with the inner products given
respectively by

\displaystyle\langle\psi,\,\psi^{\prime}\rangle_{X}=\int_{\Omega}\Re(\overline{\psi}\psi^{\prime})\quad(\psi,\psi^{\prime}\in X^{s}),

(18)

\displaystyle\langle\sigma,\,\sigma^{\prime}\rangle_{Y}=\int_{I}\gamma\cdot\gamma^{\prime}+\mu\mu^{\prime}\quad(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in Y^{k}).

(19)

The norm on
Y^{k}
is
\left\lVert(\lambda,\gamma)\right\rVert_{Y^{k}}:=\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{C^{k}}+\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert
.
The Ginzburg-Landau energy
E
defined in (
4
) is smooth
on
1+X^{k}
with
k\geq 1
, which we call
the energy space
.

Write

C^{k}_{\text{per}}:=\left\{\gamma\in C^{k}(I,\omega):\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)\right\}\quad(k\in\mathbb{N}).

Here we require periodic boundary condition for
\gamma
, so as to match
the periodic boundary condition along the

z
-axis for (
1
).
Indeed, we impose such boundary conditions
so that the arguments below can be naturally extended to an infinitely long cylindrical
domain with
I=\mathbb{R}
. In general, one can take
I=[0,a]
for any
a\gg\epsilon
,
and impose other appropriate boundary conditions.
Notice that if one varies the vertical boundary condition for (
1
),
then the definition of
C^{k}_{\text{per}}
must be changed accordingly.

Define

\displaystyle\Sigma:=\mathbb{R}\times\left\{\gamma\in C^{2}_{\text{per}}:z\mapsto(\gamma(z),z)\text{ is an embedding of $I$ into $\Omega$}\right\},

(20)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\delta}:=\left\{\sigma\in\Sigma:\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert_{Y^{2}}<\delta\right\}.

(21)

We view
\Sigma
as a manifold.
Each fibre
T_{\sigma}\Sigma
can be trivialized as a subspace of
Y^{0}
.
We view

\Sigma_{\delta}
as an open submanifold of
\Sigma
.

Definition 1

(Manifold of approximate vortex filaments)
.

For
\gamma\in C^{k}_{\text{per}}
and a function
\psi:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
,
let
\psi_{\gamma}(x,z):=\psi^{(1)}(x-\gamma(z))
, where

\psi^{(1)}
is the simple planar vortex in Sec.
2.1
.

Define a map

\begin{array}[]{ccrcl}{f}&\colon&{\Sigma_{\delta}}&\longrightarrow&{X^{0}+\psi_{0}}\\
\mbox{}&\mbox{}&{\sigma=(\lambda,\gamma)}&\longmapsto&{e^{i\lambda}\psi_{\gamma}(x,z)}\end{array},

(22)

where
\psi_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
is the lift of the planar vortex
\psi^{(1)}

to
X^{0}
.
The map
f
is
C^{1}
, as we show in Appendix.
f
parametrizes a submanifold

M:=f(\Sigma_{\delta})\subset X^{0}+\psi_{0}.

The tangent space to
M
at
f(\sigma)
is
T_{f(\sigma)}M=df(\sigma)(T_{\sigma}\Sigma_{\delta})
, where
df(\sigma)
denotes the Fréchet derivative of
f
at
\sigma
, given explicitly
in Appendix.
We call the elements in
M
the approximate vortex filaments.

Remark 2
.

The construction of
M
is motivated by the broken symmetries by
\psi^{(1)}
.
We use the term

approximate (vortex) filaments

because the configurations in
M
concentrate near some curves around

\left\{0\right\}\times I

by construction.
We can trivialize
T_{f(\sigma)}M
as a subspace of
X^{0}
.
The spaces
M,\,\Sigma
are Riemannian manifolds
w.r.t. the inner products given in (
18
)-(
19
).

2.3
Properties of approximate filaments

In what follows, we always assume the material parameter
\epsilon\ll 1
in (
1
).

A key observation is that since
u:=\left\lvert\psi^{(1)}\right\rvert
(resp.
v:=\left\lvert\nabla\psi^{(1)}\right\rvert
) is strictly increasing (resp. decreasing)
sufficiently away from
r=0
, using the asymptotics in (
10
), we have
control over the oscillation of
u
and
v
as

\left\lvert u(r)-u(s)\right\rvert\leq C\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{R^{2}},\quad\left\lvert v(r)-v(s)\right\rvert\leq C\frac{\epsilon}{R}\quad(r>s\geq R\gg 0),

(23)

where
C
is independent of
\epsilon
.

Let
\alpha>0
be given such that the planar domain
\omega
contains the ball of radius
1+\epsilon^{\alpha}
. Then it is not hard to see that for
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\epsilon^{\alpha}}
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert f(\sigma)\right\rVert_{X^{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\psi_{\gamma}\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\int_{\omega}\left\lvert\psi_{\gamma}\right\rvert^{2}(x,z)\,dxdz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{I}\left(\int_{\omega}\left\lvert\psi^{(1)}\right\rvert^{2}(x)\,dx+\int_{\omega}\left(\left\lvert\psi_{\gamma}\right\rvert^{2}(x,z)-\left\lvert\psi^{(1)}\right\rvert^{2}(x)\right)\,dx\right)\,dz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{I}\left(\int_{\omega}\left\lvert\psi^{(1)}\right\rvert^{2}(x)\,dx+\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert_{Y^{0}}\diam(\omega)\sup_{r>s\geq 1}\left\lvert u(r)-u(s)\right\rvert^{2}\right)\,dz

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\psi^{(1)}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+C(\omega)\epsilon^{4+\alpha}.

Therefore we have

\left\lVert\psi_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{X^{0}}=\left\lVert\psi^{(1)}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}+O(\epsilon^{2+\alpha/2}).

(24)

Similarly,
one can show using (
23
) that

\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{X^{0}}=\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\psi^{(1)}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}+O(\epsilon^{1+\alpha/2}).

(25)

In what follows, for a
C^{1}
-curve
\gamma:I\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write

\gamma_{z}=\partial_{z}\gamma
, etc.. (Not to be confused with the
subscript
t
in time-parametrized families.)

Lemma 1

(approximate critical point)
.

Let
\alpha>0
.
If
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\epsilon^{\alpha}}
, then
\left\lVert E^{\prime}(f(\sigma))\right\rVert_{X^{0}}\leq C\epsilon^{\alpha}\left\lvert\log\epsilon\right\rvert^{1/2}
where
C
is independent
of
\sigma
.

Proof.

Using the fact that
\psi^{(1)}
is stationary solution to (
1
), one can
compute

E^{\prime}(f(\sigma))=e^{i\lambda}\left(\nabla_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\cdot\gamma_{zz}-\nabla^{2}_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\gamma_{z}\cdot\gamma_{z}\right).

For
\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{C^{2}}\ll 1
, the leading order term in this
expression is

\nabla_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\cdot\gamma_{zz}

2.4
The adiabatic decomposition

In this section we derive a key result that is essential for the
development in Section 3.
The point is that
on a tubular neighbourhood
with definite volume
around the manifold
M
of approximate filaments, one can define a nonlinear
projection into
\Sigma
. This way we can make precise
the notion of concentration set for low energy configurations that are
uniformly close to the approximate filaments.

Recall the symplectic form
\tau
is given in (
27
).

Lemma 2

(concentration set)
.

Let
M:=f(\Sigma_{\epsilon^{\alpha}})
be the manifold of
approximate vortex filaments.
Then there is
\delta>0
such that for every
u\in X^{1}+\psi_{0}
with
\dist_{X^{2}}(u,M)<\delta
,
there exists a unique
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\epsilon^{\alpha}}
such that

\tau(u-f(\sigma),\phi)=0\quad(\phi\in T_{f(\sigma)}M).

(28)

Remark 4
.

We call
\sigma=(\lambda,\gamma)
associated to
u
the moduli of the
latter. This terminology is taken from e.g.
[
26
,
27
]
with similar context.

We call the curve
\gamma
the concentration
set of
u
. The real number
\lambda
is the global gauge parameter and is not physically meaningful.
One can view (
28
) as an orthogonality condition w.r.t. the symplectic form (
27
),
and the association
u\mapsto\sigma
is optimal in this sense.

Proof.

1. First, for
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\epsilon^{\alpha}}
, define the linear projection
Q_{\sigma}
by

\begin{array}[]{ccrcl}{Q_{\sigma}}&\colon&{X^{0}}&\longrightarrow&{X^{0}}\\
\mbox{}&\mbox{}&{\phi}&\longmapsto&{g_{\sigma}\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}g_{\sigma}^{*}J^{-1}\phi}\end{array}.

(29)

This map
Q_{\sigma}
is the skewed (i.e.
Q_{\sigma}^{*}=J^{*}Q_{\sigma}J
) projection onto
the tangent space
T_{f(\sigma)}M
.
In the definition of
Q_{\sigma}
, each factor is uniformly bounded, as we show in Appendix (
87
)-(
88
) and (
92
).
So we get the uniform estimate
\left\lVert Q_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{X^{0}\to X^{0}}\leq C.

One can check
Q_{\sigma}\phi=\phi
for every
\phi\in T_{f(\sigma)}M
by
writing
\phi=g_{\sigma}\xi
for some
\xi\in Y^{0}
, since

Q_{\sigma}g_{\sigma}\xi=g_{\sigma}\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}(g_{\sigma}^{*}J^{-1}g_{\sigma})\phi=g_{\sigma}(\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\mathcal{J}_{\sigma})\xi=g_{\sigma}\xi=\phi
.

Next, we find the concentration set
\sigma
using Implicit Function Theorem.
Consider the map

\begin{array}[]{ccrcl}{F}&\colon&{X^{2}\times\Sigma_{\epsilon^{\alpha}}}&\longrightarrow&{Y^{0}\times\mathbb{R}}\\
\mbox{}&\mbox{}&{(\phi,\sigma)}&\longmapsto&{g_{\sigma}^{*}J^{-1}(\phi-f(\sigma))}\end{array}.

Condition (
28
) is satisfied if
Q_{\sigma}(u-f(\sigma))=0
.
To see this, one uses the property
Q^{*}=-JQJ
,
which implies

\tau(Q\phi,\phi^{\prime})=\tau(\phi,Q\phi^{\prime})
.
Thus, if
F(\phi,\sigma)=0
, then (
28
)
is satisfied.

By construction, we have the following expression
for the partial Fréchet derivative

\partial_{\sigma}F|_{(f(\sigma),\sigma)}=-\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}.

It is invertible since
\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}
is invertible, see Appendix.
The equation
F(\phi,\sigma)=0
has the trivial solution

(f(\sigma),\sigma)
. It follows that for any fixed
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\epsilon}
, there is
\delta=\delta(\sigma,\epsilon)>0

and a map
S_{\sigma}:B_{\delta}(f(\sigma))\to\Sigma_{\epsilon}
such that

F(\phi,S(\phi))=0
for
\phi\in B_{\delta}(f(\sigma))
.

2. It remains to show that in fact
\delta
can be made independent of
\sigma
.
This is important because we must retain a definite volume for the
projeciton neighbourhood, so that later on a flow can fluctuate within
this neighbourhood.

Write

A_{\phi}:=\partial_{\sigma}F|_{(\phi+f(\sigma),\sigma)},\quad V_{\phi}:=A_{\phi}-A_{0}.

Then

A_{0}=-\mathcal{J}_{\sigma},\quad V_{\phi}=(d_{\sigma}g_{\sigma}^{*})\left(\cdot\right)|_{J^{-1}\phi}.

The size of
\delta
is determined by the condition
that for every
\phi\in B_{\delta}(f(\sigma))
,

\displaystyle A_{\phi}\text{ is invertible},

(30)

\displaystyle\left\lVert A_{\phi}^{-1}\right\rVert_{Y^{0}\to Y^{0}}\leq\frac{1}{4\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\right\rVert_{Y^{0}\to Y^{0}}},

(31)

\displaystyle\left\lVert F(\phi+f(\sigma),\sigma)\right\rVert_{Y^{0}}\leq\frac{\delta_{0}}{4\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\right\rVert_{Y^{0}\to Y^{0}}},

(32)

where
\delta_{0}
is determined
that for every
\xi\in B_{\delta_{0}}(\sigma)
and
\phi\in B_{\delta}(f(\sigma))
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert R(\phi,\xi)\right\rVert_{Y^{0}}\leq\frac{\delta_{0}}{4\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\right\rVert_{Y^{0}\to Y^{0}}},

(33)

\displaystyle R(\phi,\xi):=F(\phi+f(\sigma),\sigma+\xi)-F(\phi+f(\sigma),\sigma)-\partial_{\sigma}F(\phi+f(\sigma),\sigma)\xi,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\sigma+\xi}-\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{Y^{0}\to Y^{0}}\leq\frac{1}{4\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\right\rVert_{Y^{0}\to Y^{0}}}.

(34)

See for instance
[
1
, Sec. 2]
. Note that the r.h.s. of (
31
)-(
34
) are independent of
\sigma

by the uniform estimate for
\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}
.

Conditions (
33
)-(
34
) are satisfied for some

\delta_{0}=C(\Omega)\epsilon
. To get (
33
), one uses (
92
) and the fact that
\left\lVert R(\phi,\xi)\right\rVert_{Y^{0}}=o(\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{Y^{0}})
, since it
is the super-linear remainder of the expansion of
F
in
\sigma
.
To get (
34
), one uses the continuity of the map

\sigma\mapsto\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}\in L(Y^{0},Y^{0}).

The claim now is that (
30
)-(
32
) are satisfied for
\delta=O(\epsilon^{3})
.
Indeed, plugging
\delta_{0}=C\epsilon
to (
32
) and using the uniform estimate for

g_{\sigma}^{*}
and
\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}
, one sees that (
32
) is satisfied so long
as
\delta=O(\epsilon\left\lvert\log\epsilon\right\rvert^{3/2}).

By elementary perturbation theory, since

A_{0}
is invertible, and the partial Fréchet derivative
A_{\phi}

is continuous in
\phi
as a map from
X^{2}\to L^{2}(Y^{0},Y^{0})
, it follows that condition (
30
) is satisfied provided

\left\lVert V_{\phi}\right\rVert_{Y^{0}\to Y^{0}}<\left\lVert A_{0}^{-1}\right\rVert_{Y^{0}\to Y^{0}}^{-1}\leq C\left\lvert\log\epsilon\right\rvert^{2}.

By the uniform estimate on
d_{\sigma}g_{\sigma}^{*}
, we can arrange this with
\delta=O(\epsilon^{3})
.

Lastly, referring to the Neumann series for the inverse

A_{\phi}^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{0}^{-1}\left(-V_{\phi}A_{0}^{-1}\right)^{n},

one can see that
A_{\phi}^{-1}
is also continuous in
\phi
, and
\left\lVert A_{\phi}^{-1}\right\rVert_{Y^{0}\to Y^{0}}=O(\left\lvert\log\epsilon\right\rvert^{-2})

so long as
\left\lVert V_{\phi}\right\rVert_{Y^{0}\to Y^{0}}=o(\left\lvert\log\epsilon\right\rvert^{2})
.
The latter holds with
\delta=O(\epsilon^{3})
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.1
The connection of (

The following lemma translates the r.h.s. of (

Afterwards, we show this defines an evolution on

In this subsection, the operator

Lemma 3
.

Let
\alpha>2
. If
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\epsilon^{\alpha}}
, then

\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}d_{\sigma}E(f(\sigma))=\left(o(\epsilon^{\alpha}),J\gamma_{zz}+o_{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{C^{0}}}(\epsilon^{\alpha})\right).

(35)

Proof.

Recall
\sigma=(\lambda,\gamma)
consists of a
U(1)
-gauge parameter
\lambda

and a concentration
curve
\gamma
.
The
\lambda
-component is therefore not
physically relevant, and we are interested in the
\gamma

For
\sigma\in Y^{k}
, we write
\sigma=:([\sigma]_{\lambda},[\sigma]_{\gamma})
.

The
partial-Fréchet derivative
of the energy
E(f(\sigma))
w.r.t.
\gamma
is given by

\partial_{\gamma}E(f(\sigma))=\int_{x}\langle\nabla_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\cdot\gamma_{zz}+\nabla^{2}_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\gamma_{z}\cdot\gamma_{z},\,\nabla_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\rangle,

(36)

where we write

\gamma_{z}=\partial_{z}\gamma
, etc..
Using this and the expression for
\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}
given in (
89
), we find
that the first term in the r.h.s. of (
36
)

[\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\int_{x}\langle\nabla_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\cdot\gamma_{zz},\,\nabla_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\rangle]_{\gamma}=J\gamma_{zz}+o(\epsilon^{\alpha}).

It suffices then to show the second term

\left\lVert[\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\int_{x}\langle\nabla^{2}_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\gamma_{z}\cdot\gamma_{z},\,\nabla_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\rangle]_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{C^{0}}=o(\epsilon^{\alpha}),

(37)

i.e. this term can be absorbed into the remainder.

First, we use (
14
) and (
25
) to get

\displaystyle\left\lVert\int_{x}\langle\nabla^{2}_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\gamma_{z}\cdot\gamma_{z},\,\nabla_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\rangle\right\rVert_{C^{0}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\Omega)\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{X^{0}}\left\lVert\gamma_{z}\right\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

(38)

\displaystyle\leq C(\Omega)\epsilon^{2\alpha}\left\lvert\log\epsilon\right\rvert^{1/2}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Next, note the function
\psi_{\gamma}
satisfies the linear second order equation

\Delta\psi+\nabla_{x}^{2}\psi\gamma_{z}\cdot\gamma_{z}+\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot\gamma_{z}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}(1-\left\lvert\psi_{\gamma}\right\rvert^{2})\psi=0\quad\text{on }\Omega.

This equation is elliptic for
\left\lVert\gamma_{z}\right\rVert_{C^{0}}\ll 1
, so the Schauder
estimate implies for all
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}\ll 1
and
0<\mu<1
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\psi_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{C^{2,\mu}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\Omega,\epsilon_{0})\left(\left\lVert\psi_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}(1-\left\lvert\psi_{\gamma}\right\rvert^{2})\right\rVert_{C^{0,\mu}(\Omega)}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle=C(\Omega,\epsilon_{0})\epsilon^{-2(1+\mu)}.

Here we have used the estimates on the uniform norm
\left\lVert\psi_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1
and Hölder seminorm

\left[\psi_{\gamma}\right]_{\mu,\Omega}\leq C(\Omega)\epsilon^{-\mu}.

These follow from (
13
) and (
24
).

Plugging (
39
) to (
38
), we see that

\left\lVert\int_{x}\langle\nabla^{2}_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\gamma_{z}\cdot\gamma_{z},\,\nabla_{x}\psi_{\gamma}\rangle\right\rVert_{C^{0}}\leq C(\Omega,\epsilon_{0})\left\lvert\log\epsilon\right\rvert^{1/2}\epsilon^{2(\alpha-1-\mu)}.

(40)

In Appendix we show

\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\right\rVert_{Y^{k}\to Y^{k}}\leq C(\omega)\left\lvert\log\epsilon\right\rvert^{-2}
.
This, the assumption
\alpha>2
, and the above estimate together show that if we choose
\mu<\alpha/2-1

then (
37
) holds. The proof is complete.
∎

We now discuss the geometric meaning of the r.h.s. of (
35
).
A family of curves
\vec{\gamma}_{t}\in C^{2}(I,\Omega)
satisfies the
binormal curvature flow

if parametrized by arclength, the curves satisfy

\partial_{t}\vec{\gamma}=\vec{\gamma}_{s}\times\vec{\gamma}_{ss}\quad\left(\left\lvert\vec{\gamma}_{s}\right\rvert\equiv 1\right).

(41)

Here we write derivatives w.r.t. to the arclength parameter as
\vec{\gamma}_{s}\equiv\partial_{s}\vec{\gamma}
, etc..

Now reparametrize
\vec{\gamma}_{t}
as
\vec{\gamma}_{t}(z)=(\gamma_{t}(z),z)
with
\gamma\in C^{2}(I,\omega)
. Then
(
41
) becomes

\partial_{t}\vec{\gamma}=\vec{\gamma}_{z}z_{s}\times(\vec{\gamma}_{zz}z_{s}^{2}+\vec{\gamma}_{z}z_{ss}).

(42)

The arclength parameter
s
is given in terms of the new parameter
z
by

s(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\left\lvert\vec{\gamma_{z}}\right\rvert^{2}=z+\int_{0}^{z}\left\lvert\gamma_{z}\right\rvert^{2}.

Differentiating this expression, for
\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{C^{2}}=O(\delta)
, we get

z_{s}=\frac{1}{\left\lvert\gamma_{z}\right\rvert^{2}+1}=1+O(\delta^{2}),\quad z_{ss}=-\frac{\gamma_{z}\cdot\gamma_{zz}}{\left(1+\left\lvert\gamma_{z}\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{3}}=O(\delta^{2}).

Geometrically,
z
and
s
are close because the curve parametrized by

\vec{\gamma}
with
\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{C^{2}}\ll 1
is approximately
a vertical straight line, in which case

.

Thus (
42
) in the leading order reads

\partial_{t}\vec{\gamma}=\vec{\gamma}_{z}\times\vec{\gamma}_{zz}+O(\delta^{2})=(J\gamma_{zz},\gamma_{z}^{1}\gamma_{zz}^{2}-\gamma_{z}^{2}\gamma_{zz}^{2})+O(\delta^{2}).

(43)

In the r.h.s. of (
43
),
\gamma_{z}^{1}\gamma_{zz}^{2}-\gamma_{z}^{2}\sigma_{zz}^{2}
is also
O(\delta^{2})
.
In conclusion, combing with Lemma

\text{$\partial_{t}\sigma=(\partial_{t}\lambda,\partial_{t}\gamma)=\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}d_{\sigma}E(f(\sigma))+o(\delta)\implies\vec{\gamma}_{t}$ solves ({\ref{BNF}}) up to $o(\delta)$}.

Appendix A
Properties of the Fréchet derivatives

In this section we consider the Fréchet derivatives of the immersion
f
defined in (
22
).

A.1
Basic variational calculus

First, we recall some basic elements of variational derivatives that we used
repeatedly.
For details, see for instance
[
25
, Appendix C]

Fréchet derivative
. Let
X,\,Y
be two Banach spaces,
U
be an open set in
X
.
For a map
g:U\subset X\to Y
and a vector
u\in U
,
the Fréchet derivative
dg(u)
is a linear map from
X\to Y
such that

g(u+v)-g(u)-dg(u)v=o(\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{X})
for every
v\in X
.
If
dg(u)
exists at
u
, then it is unique. If
dg(u)
exists for every
u\in U
,
and the map
u\mapsto dg(u)
is continuous from
U
to the space of linear operators
L(X,Y)
, then we
we say
g
is
C^{1}
on
U
.
In this case,
dg(u)
is uniquely given by

v\mapsto\tfrac{dg(u+tv)}{dt}|_{t=0}\quad(v\in X).

Iteratively, we can define higher order derivatives this way.

Gradient and Hessian
. If
X
is a Hilbert space over scalar field
Y
, then by Riesz representation,
we can identify
dg(u)
as an element in
X
, denoted
g^{\prime}(u)
. The vector

g^{\prime}(u)
is called the
X
-gradient of
g
.
Similarly, we denote
g^{\prime\prime}(u)
the second-order Fréchet derivative

d^{2}g(u)
. If
g
is
C^{2}
, then
g^{\prime\prime}
can be identified as a symmetric
linear operator
uniquely determined by the relation

\langle g^{\prime\prime}(u)v,\,w\rangle=\tfrac{\partial{{}^{2}}g(u+tv+sw)}{\partial{t}\partial{s}}|_{s=t=0}\quad(v,w\in X).

Expansion
. Let
X
be a Hilbert space over scalar field
Y
. Suppose
g
is
C^{2}
on
U\subset X
. Define a scalar function
\phi(t):=g(u+tv)
for vectors

v,w
such that
v+tw\in U
for every
0\leq t\leq 1
.
Then the elementary Taylor expansion at
\phi(1)
gives

g(v+w)=g(v)+\langle g^{\prime}(v),\,w\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{\prime\prime}(v)w,\,w\rangle+o(\left\lVert w\right\rVert^{2}).

Here we have used the definition of
g^{\prime}
and
g^{\prime\prime}
from the last paragraph.

Composition
. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary.
Fix
r>d/2,\,f\in C^{r+1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
define a map
g:u\mapsto f\circ u
. Then
g:H^{r}(\Omega)\to H^{r}(\Omega)
,
and is
C^{1}
. The Fréchet derivative is given by
v\mapsto\nabla f\cdot v
.
