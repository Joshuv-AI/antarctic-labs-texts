Search for Dark Photon Dark Matter in the Mass Range 𝟒𝟏 ​ – ​ 𝟕𝟒 ​ 𝝁 ​ 𝐞𝐕 \mathbf{41\text{--}74}\,\bm{\mu}\mathbf{eV} using Millimeter-Wave Receiver and Radioshielding Box
S. Adachi, R. Fujinaka, S. Honda, Y. Muto, H. Nakata, Y. Sueno, T. Sumida, J. Suzuki, O. Tajima, H. Takeuchi, DOSUE-RR Collaboration

Abstract
Dark photons have been considered potential candidates for dark matter. The dark photon dark matter (DPDM) has a mass and interacts with electromagnetic fields via kinetic mixing with a coupling constant of χ \chi . Thus, DPDMs are converted into ordinary photons at metal surfaces. Using a millimeter-wave receiver set in a radioshielding box, we performed experiments to detect the conversion photons from the DPDM in the frequency range 10–18 GHz , which corresponds to a mass range 41–74 ​ μ ​ eV \text{41--74}\,\mu\text{eV} . We found no conversion photon signal in this range and set the upper limits to χ < ( 0.5 ​ – ​ 3.9 ) × 10 − 10 \chi<(0.5\text{--}3.9)\times 10^{-10} at a 95% confidence level.



I
Introduction

The study on dark matter is a prominent topic in the fields of particle physics and cosmology.
Dark matter is localized in most galactic halos.
However, its interactions with ordinary particles, standard-model particles, remain unexplained except via gravity.
Dark photons are considered potential candidates for dark matter.
The mass (
m_{\rm DP}
) is nonzero and the dark photon interacts with the electromagnetic fields via kinetic mixing with a coupling constant of
\chi

Arias et al. 2012
.
Dark photon dark matter (DPDM) in the
\mu\text{eV}
or meV mass ranges have been predicted to exist in the context of high-scale inflation models
and a part of string theories
Graham et al. 2016
;
Arias et al. 2012
.

DPDMs convert to standard-model photons at the boundary of the mediums via the kinetic mixing
Caputo et al. 2021
.
Metal surfaces are used for such conversions
Horns et al. 2013
;
Tomita et al. 2020
;
Kotaka et al. 2023
;
Ramanathan et al. 2023
;
Brun et al. 2019
.
Because the speed of DPDM (
v_{\rm DP}
) is considerably low compared to the speed of light (
v_{\rm DP}/c\approx 10^{-3}
),
the direction of the conversion photons is almost perpendicular to the surface of the plate, within
0.1^{\circ}

Jaeckel and Knirck 2016
.
The frequency of the conversion photons approximately
\nu_{0}
roughly corresponds to the mass of the DPDM because of energy conservation, that is,
h\nu_{0}\simeq m_{\rm DP}c^{2}
, where
h
is the Planck constant.
Owing to the low speed of the DPDM,
the frequency width of the conversion photons (
\Delta\nu_{\text{DP}}
) is considerably narrow compared with the peak frequency,

\Delta\nu_{\text{DP}}/\nu_{0}\approx 10^{-6}

Sikivie 1983
.
Thus, the conversion photons should be observed as a peak in the frequency spectrum.

The signal power of the conversion photons
P_{\text{DP}}
is denoted by
Horns et al. 2013
,

\displaystyle P_{\text{DP}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(6.4\times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{aW})\times\left(\frac{\chi}{10^{-10}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{A_{\text{eff}}}{10\,\text{cm}^{2}}\right)

(1)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\frac{\rho}{0.39\,\text{GeV}/\text{cm}^{3}}\right)\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{2/3}}\right)^{2},

where
A_{\text{eff}}
is the effective antenna aperture,

\rho=0.39\pm 0.03\,\text{GeV/cm}^{3}
is the energy density of the dark matter in the galactic halo
Catena and Ullio 2010
,
and
\alpha
is a factor determined by the angular distribution of the DPDM field relative to the sensitive polarization axis.
We selected
\alpha=\sqrt{1/3}
for the case of random distribution using a single-polarization detector
Horns et al. 2013
.

The DOSUE-RR (Dark-photon dark-matter observing system for Un-Explored Radio-Range)
aims to detect the conversion photons of DPDM using millimeter-wave receivers.
We achieved stringent constraints,
\chi<(\text{0.3--2.0})\times 10^{-10}
in the mass range
74\text{--}110\,\mu\text{eV}
by using a cryogenic millimeter-wave receiver
Kotaka et al. 2023
.
Searches using millimeter-wave receivers have the advantage of broad coverage of the mass range.
In this study,
we switched the explored mass range
\text{41--74}\,\mu\text{eV}
, corresponding to the frequency range
10–18

GHz
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
In Sec. II, we describe the experimental setup.
The calibration procedures are described in Sec. III.
Section IV details the measurement procedure and analysis of the DPDM search.
Section V describes the systematic errors.
In Sec. VI, we present the constraints on the coupling constant
\chi
.
Finally, Sec. VII presents the conclusions of this study.

II
Experimental Setup

Figure
1
shows the setup to search for DPDM.
A radioshielding box was essential for this measurement because manmade millimeter-wave signals and
their harmonics can enter the antenna, for example, Wi-Fi and telecommunication signals.
This box comprises 1 mm-thick aluminum plates and sheets of the radio-wave absorber, Eccosorb CV-3, with a reflectance of
-40\,\text{dB}
above 8 GHz
Laird Technologies, Inc. 2021
.
An aluminum plate with a thickness of 4 mm and an area of
690\,\text{mm}\times 690\,\text{mm}
was placed at the top of the box.
The conversion photons from the plate surface entered the horn antenna at the bottom of the box.
The horn antenna was Pasternack PE9856/SF-60, with an aperture size of
123.8\,\text{mm}\times 91.9\,\text{mm}
.
Using a tiltmeter, we confirmed that the antenna direction was perpendicular to the plate with
0.1^{\circ}
precision.

The signals collected by the antenna were transmitted to a coaxial cable through a waveguide-to-coaxial adapter.
A low-noise amplifier (Low-Noise Factory LNF-LNR6_20A) and another amplifier (Mini-Circuits ZVA-183G-S+) were used to amplify the signals.
An isolator (Pasternack PE8304) and two attenuators were inserted to suppress standing waves over the signal path.
The amplified signals were measured using a signal analyzer (ANRITSU MS2840A).
The signal analyzer performed a Fast Fourier Transform to measure the frequency spectra.
The resolution bandwidth was set to 300 Hz (
\ll\Delta\nu_{\text{DP}}\approx 10\,\text{kHz}
).
The signal analyzer can simultaneously obtain spectral data for a limited frequency range of 2.5 MHz using this resolution bandwidth.
There are 32,769 data points in each dataset for the 2.5 MHz range, that is, the frequency interval was 76.3 Hz.

III.1
Effective antenna aperture and beam pattern

The effective antenna aperture
A_{\text{eff}}

is a visible area of the antenna and is smaller than its physical aperture area.
This can be estimated from the beam pattern
B
using the antenna theorem
Kraus 1966

\displaystyle A_{\text{eff}}\Omega_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2},

\displaystyle\Omega_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int B(\theta,\phi)d\Omega,

where
\lambda
is the wavelength,
\theta
is the polar angle, and
\phi
is the azimuthal angle.

B
is defined as the relative antenna gain in each direction, whose peak value is normalized to 1.
Using a finite element method simulation software, ANSYS-HFSS (2022R2),
we simulated the beam patterns from 10.0 GHz to 18.0 GHz with 0.5 GHz intervals.
Thereafter, we calculated the
A_{\text{eff}}
at each frequency, as shown in Figure
2
.

III.2
Receiver gain

The power measured by the signal analyzer
P_{\text{out}}
is
an amplified total power of the receiver input power
P_{\text{in}}
and receiver noise,
which is represented by the receiver temperature
T_{\text{rx}}
:

P_{\text{out}}=G\left(P_{\text{in}}+k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{rx}}\Delta\nu\right),

(2)

where
G
is the receiver gain,
k_{\text{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant, and
\Delta\nu
is the frequency bandwidth.
To obtain
G
and
T_{\text{rx}}
, we performed a calibration using hot (room temperature) and cold (77K) blackbody radiation sources.
The millimeter-wave absorber Eccosrb CV-3 was used as the blackbody radiation source.
The temperature under room conditions (
T^{\text{hot}}\sim 290\,\text{K}
) was measured using a radiation thermometer for each calibration.
Cold radiation (
T^{\text{cold}}=77\,\text{K}
) was emitted by an absorber immersed in a liquid-nitrogen bath made of a foamed polystyrene box,
transparent to radio waves
Goldsmith 1998
;
Choi et al. 2013
.
Each source was set in front of the antenna aperture to fully cover the antenna.
Each
P_{\text{in}}
was calculated as
P_{\text{in}}^{\text{hot}}=k_{\text{B}}T^{\text{hot}}\Delta\nu
or
P_{\text{in}}^{\text{cold}}=k_{\text{B}}T^{\text{cold}}\Delta\nu
.
On this basis, we obtained
G
and
T_{\text{rx}}
according to Eq. (
2
).
In this calibration, we set the resolution bandwidth to be the same as that used in the DPDM search.
Figure
7
shows the
G
and
T_{\text{rx}}
as a function of frequency.
The typical gain was
60\,\text{dB}
and
T_{\text{rx}}
was
\approx 200\,\text{K}
.
The system temperature is the sum of
T_{\text{rx}}
and input radiation (
T_{\text{in}}
)
during the DPDM search; that is,
T_{\text{sys}}=T_{\text{in}}+T_{\text{rx}}
.

T_{\text{in}}
is dominated by the thermal loading at room temperature.

IV
Measurement and analysis

The DPDM search was performed from March 6 to 17, 2023.
We collected data for frequencies ranging from 10.0 GHz to 18.0 GHz, corresponding to a DPDM mass range of 41
\mu\text{eV}
to 74
\mu\text{eV}
.
The time to accumulate data was set to 2 s for each data chunk.
The center frequency was shifted by 2.0 MHz after obtaining 12 chunks of data for each frequency region.
We collected 48,000 data chunks in 4,000 frequency regions.
Two consecutive frequency ranges were overlapped and used to estimate the statistical error in the analysis.
Further, we performed gain calibration before and after each 100 MHz data acquisition.
The typical time interval between the calibrations was 40 min.
The maximum difference in
G
was
1.5\%
.

The analysis method is the same as that used in our previous study
Kotaka et al. 2023
.
The 12 data chunks at each frequency were averaged and rebinned to
\Delta\nu=2\,\text{kHz}
.
The averaged spectra were converted to
P_{\text{in}}
by Eq. (
2
).
To extract the signal power of the conversion photons
P_{\text{DP}}
,
we fitted a function to the
P_{\text{in}}
spectra at each frequency for the signal peak
\nu_{0}\equiv m_{\text{DP}}c^{2}/h
.
The fitting function at
\nu_{0}
comprises a signal model,
f_{\text{sig}}

and the baseline background noise
f_{\text{bg}}
, which is a one-dimensional polynomial
f_{\text{bg}}(\nu;a,b)=a(\nu-\nu_{0})+b
,

f(\nu;P_{\text{DP}},a,b)=P_{\text{DP}}\times f_{\text{sig}}(\nu;\nu_{0})+f_{\text{bg}}(\nu;\nu_{0},a,b)~.

(3)

Here,
a
and
b
are the parameters used to model the baseline noise, and

f_{\text{sig}}
is the difference in the cumulative functions introduced to account for the effect of the finite bin width

f_{\text{sig}}(\nu;\nu_{0})=F(\nu+\Delta\nu/2;\nu_{0})-F(\nu-\Delta\nu/2;\nu_{0}),

(4)

where,

\displaystyle F(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{v}{\text{d}v^{\prime}}\int^{4\pi}{\text{d}\Omega~g(\mathbf{v^{\prime}};v_{\text{c}},\mathbf{v_{\text{E}}})~v^{\prime 2}}~,

(5)

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{v};v_{\text{c}},\mathbf{v_{\text{E}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt{\pi}v_{\text{c}}\right)^{3}}\exp{-\frac{|\vb{v}+\vb{v_{\text{E}}}|^2}{v_\text{c}^2}}.

(6)

Here,
F
is a function of the DPDM speed;

v\equiv\absolutevalue{\vb{v}}=c\sqrt{1-(\nu_{0}/\nu)^{2}}
;

g(\mathbf{v})
denotes the velocity distribution,;

v_{\text{c}}
denotes the circular rotational speed of the galaxy,
and
\mathbf{v_{\text{E}}}
is the velocity of the Earth within the frame of the Galaxy.
We assume a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution for
g(\mathbf{v})

Drukier et al. 1986
.
Furthermore, we assumed
\absolutevalue{\vb{v}_\text{E}}=v_{\text{c}}=220\,\text{km}/\text{s}
in the fittings, as in many dark matter searches
Mayet et al. 2016
;
Akerib et al. 2017
;
Aprile et al. 2018
;
Fu et al. 2017
;
Agnes et al. 2018
.
The speed of the Earth
v_{\text{E}}
varied from
220\,\text{km}/\text{s}
to
242\,\text{km}/\text{s}
during the search measurement
owing to the revolution and rotation of the Earth
Mayet et al. 2016
.
This caused a small change in the peak frequency.
This effect is considered as a systematic error in Sec.
V
.

We varied the peak frequency
\nu_{0}
from 10.0 to 18.0 GHz at small intervals of
\Delta\nu=2\,\text{kHz}

and performed a fit by floating
P_{\text{DP}}
,
a
, and
b
in the frequency range from
\nu_{0}-50\,\text{kHz}
to
\nu_{0}+200\,\text{kHz}
.
The error on each data point in the fit was estimated from the standard deviations of the data for the 0.25 MHz regions below and above the fit range.

Figure
8
shows the fit results for the data.
Over the entire frequency range,
P_{\text{DP}}
was less than a few
aW
.
We calculated the statistical significance at each frequency in the same manner as in our previous study
Kotaka et al. 2023
.
The minimum local
p
value was
1.1\times 10^{-6}\,\%
at 13.913524 GHz.
The spectrum at this frequency is shown in the top panel of Fig.
9
.
Furthermore, we calculated the global
p
value
(i.e., the probability of exceeding the minimum local
p
value at any frequency)
to be
2.2\,\%
, which is not a significant excess.
The calculation methods for the local and global
p
values are described in Appendix
A

VII
Conclusions

We searched for DPDM using a room-temperature millimeter-wave receiver and radioshielding box. The explored frequency range
10–18

GHz
corresponds to the mass range
\text{41--74}\,\mu\text{eV}
. We found no significant signal and set the upper limits of
\chi<(0.5\text{--}3.9)\times 10^{-10}
at the 95% confidence level. The achieved constraint was tighter than that obtained from cosmological observations.

Appendix A
Statistical Calculations

A.1
Local
p
value

To understand the statistical significance of the fit results for
P_{\text{DP}}
, we employed the null-sample method.
This method was used in our previous study
Kotaka et al. 2023
.
The null samples are the spectra generated by subtracting six data chunks from the other six data chunks
in the measured 12 data chunks, which model the noise-only spectra.
There were 462 combinations (i.e.,
\binom{12}{6}\times 1/2
) generating null samples at each frequency range.
For each null sample at each frequency, we obtained
P_{\text{DP}}
, error
\sigma
, and their ratio
x\equiv P_{\text{DP}}/\sigma
.
Figure
12
shows the distribution of the
x
obtained from the fit to the data and null samples.
The null-sample distribution
\mathcal{P}(x)
is consistent with the data.
We calculated a local
p
value under the zero-signal hypothesis for the fitted result with
x=x_{0}
as follows:

p=\frac{\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}{\mathcal{P}(x)}dx}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathcal{P}(x)}dx}~.

(7)

The fraction of the shaded area in Fig.
12
corresponds to this value.

Figure 12:

Distributions of
x\equiv P_{\mathrm{DP}}/\sigma
for data and null samples(
\mathcal{P}(x)
).
Here, the plot for the null samples was normalized to be the same area as the data.
The local
p
value for the fit result with
x=x_{0}
was obtained from the fraction of the shaded area.

A.2
Global
p
value

There are many fitting results in the frequency range 10–18 GHz (
4\times 10^{6}
).
The global
p
value, which considers the look-elsewhere effect, is important for determining whether a signal signature exists.
Using the minimum
p
local
p^{\text{min}}
, the global
p
value for
N
fittings was calculated using the following formula
Tomita et al. 2020
;
Foster et al. 2018
:

p_{\text{global}}(p^{\text{min}};N)=1-(1-p^{\text{min}})^{\sum_{i=0}^{N}\mu_{i}},

(8)

where
\mu_{i}
(
0<\mu_{i}\leq 1
) is a scale factor for
i
-th fitting that accounts for the independence between the fittings in the neighbor bins.
Because the peak width of the DPDM signal depends on the frequency,
\mu_{i}
depends on the frequency.
To estimate
\mu
for this experiment, we separated the null samples into 2 MHz frequency intervals, each of which contained 1000 fit results,
and checked the distribution of the minimum local
p
values (
p^{\text{min}}
) in each of the 2 MHz intervals.
The
p^{\text{min}}
distribution for the frequency range of
10.0–10.1

GHz
is shown in the top panel of Fig.
13
.
A global
p
value with
N=1000
is calculated from the
p^{\text{min}}
distribution
