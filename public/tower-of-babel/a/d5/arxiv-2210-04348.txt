Lemma 3.2
.

Let
K_{1},\dots,K_{n}:[-1,1]\to\underline{\mathbb{R}}
be extended continuous functions and
J:[0,1]\to\underline{\mathbb{R}}
be an arbitrary upper bounded function. Consider the corresponding generalized sum of translates function
F
from (
5
). Let
[a,b]\subseteq[0,1]
be a closed interval. Then the function
m_{[a,b]}(\mathbf{y})=\sup_{t\in[a,b]}F(\mathbf{y},\cdot)
is (extended) continuous on
\overline{S}
.

Proof.

For technical simplicity, let us consider
\Phi(\mathbf{x},t):=\exp(F(\mathbf{x},t))
and

\mu(\mathbf{x}):=\exp(m_{[a,b]}(\mathbf{x}))=\exp(\sup_{[a,b]}F(\mathbf{x},\cdot))=\sup_{[a,b]}\exp(F(\mathbf{x},\cdot))=\sup_{[a,b]}\Phi(\mathbf{x},\cdot).

Also, put
f(\mathbf{x},t):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}(t-x_{j})
and
\varphi(\mathbf{x},t):=\exp(f(\mathbf{x},t))
. As
K_{j}
are all extended continuous, so is
f(\mathbf{x},t)
, whence
\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)
is standard (finite valued) continuous and is therefore uniformly continuous, too. Thus for any
\varepsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that in particular
|\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)-\varphi(\mathbf{y},t)|<\varepsilon
whenever
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|<\delta
.

Let now
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S}
be fixed with
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|<\delta
, and
t,s\in[a,b]
be points such that
\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)>\mu(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon
and
\Phi(\mathbf{y},s)>\mu(\mathbf{y})-\varepsilon
. We then have

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\geq\Phi(\mathbf{y},t)=\exp(J(t))\varphi(\mathbf{y},t)\geq\exp(J(t))(\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)-\exp(J(t))\varepsilon\geq\mu(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon(1+\exp(J(t)),

and similarly
\mu(\mathbf{x})\geq\mu(\mathbf{y})-\varepsilon(1+\exp(J(s))
. As
J
is upper bounded, we also have
0\leq\exp(J(\cdot))\leq C
with a finite constant

, whence this means
|\mu(\mathbf{x})-\mu(\mathbf{y})|\leq(C+1)\varepsilon
whenever
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|<\delta
. That is,
\mu
is continuous on
\overline{S}
, and therefore also
m_{[a,b]}=\log\mu
is extended continuous.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
K
be a kernel function, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n}>0
, let
J
be an
n
-field function, and let
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
. Moreover, in case
K
is non-singular, assume also
K(-1),K(1)>-\infty
.

(a)

If
J
is upper semicontinuous, then
m_{j}:\overline{S}\to\underline{\mathbb{R}}
is upper semicontinuous.

(b)

If
J
is lower semicontinuous, then
m_{j}:\overline{S}\to\underline{\mathbb{R}}
is lower semicontinuous.

(c)

If
J
is continuous in the extended sense, then
m_{j}:\overline{S}\to\underline{\mathbb{R}}
is continuous in the extended sense.

Proof.

We may assume that
K
is non-singular, otherwise the continuity of
m_{j}
is contained in Lemma
3.1
without any further conditions on
J
. So, according to the finiteness assumption,
f:\overline{S}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a real-valued continuous function, hence uniformly continuous and for any
\varepsilon>0
we have (
14
).

(a) Let
\mathbf{x}\in\overline{S}
and let
L>m_{j}(\mathbf{x})
be arbitrary. Take
\varepsilon>0
with
L-\varepsilon>m_{j}(\mathbf{x})
and the corresponding
\delta_{0}
from uniform continuity. If
J(x_{j})=-\infty
, let
a:=L-\varepsilon-\sup f
, and if
J(x_{j})>-\infty
, let
a:=J(x_{j})+\varepsilon
. Similarly, if
J(x_{j+1})=-\infty
, let
b:=L-\varepsilon-\sup f
, and if
J(x_{j+1})>-\infty
, let
b:=J(x_{j+1})+\varepsilon
.
By the upper semicontinuity of
J
there is
\delta<\delta_{0}
such that for every
t\in(x_{j}-\delta,x_{j}+\delta)\cap[0,1]
we have
J(t)<a
, and for every
t\in(x_{j+1}-\delta,x_{j+1}+\delta)\cap[0,1]
we have
J(t)<b
.

Let now
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S}
with
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|<\delta
. First, take
t\in[y_{j},y_{j+1}]\cap[x_{j},x_{j+1}]
. Then

F(\mathbf{y},t)=f(\mathbf{y},t)+J(t)\leq f(\mathbf{x},t)+J(t)+\varepsilon\leq m_{j}(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon.

Second, take
t\in[y_{j},y_{j+1}]\setminus[x_{j},x_{j+1}]
. Then
\min\{|t-x_{j}|,|t-x_{j+1}|\}<\delta
, so either
|t-x_{j}|<\delta
or
|t-x_{j+1}|<\delta
. We get in the first case
F(\mathbf{y},t)=f(\mathbf{y},t)+J(t)\leq f(\mathbf{x},x_{j})+\varepsilon+a
, and in the second that

F(\mathbf{y},t)=f(\mathbf{y},t)+J(t)\leq f(\mathbf{x},x_{j+1})+\varepsilon+b
.
Altogether we obtain

\displaystyle m_{j}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sup_{[y_{j},y_{j+1}]}F(\mathbf{y},\cdot)\leq\max\{m_{j}(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon,f(\mathbf{x},x_{j})+\varepsilon+a,f(\mathbf{x},x_{j+1})+\varepsilon+b\}\leq L

for every
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S}
with
\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|<\delta
. This proves that
\limsup_{\mathbf{y}\to\mathbf{x}}m_{j}(\mathbf{y})\leq m_{j}(\mathbf{x})
.

(b) We are to prove lower semicontinuity, i.e.
\liminf_{\mathbf{y}\to\mathbf{x}}m_{j}(\mathbf{y})\geq m_{j}(\mathbf{x})
, whence if
m_{j}(\mathbf{x})=-\infty
, then there remains nothing to prove. So we can assume without loss of generality that
m_{j}(\mathbf{x})>-\infty
is finite.

We distinguish two cases, the first being when
I_{j}(\mathbf{x})
is degenerate, i.e.
x_{j}=x_{j+1}
, in which case we necessarily have
J(x_{j})>-\infty
. Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary, and
\delta_{0}
be the bound furnished by the uniform continuity of
f
, as written above. Also, let
\delta\leq\delta_{0}
be so small, that
J(t)>J(x_{j})-\varepsilon
all over
(x_{j}-\delta,x_{j}+\delta)
. Such a
\delta>0
exists because
J
is lower semicontinuous. Now if
\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|<\delta
, then
y_{j}\in(x_{j}-\delta,x_{j}+\delta)
, and we are led to

\displaystyle m_{j}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\geq F(\mathbf{y},y_{j})=f(\mathbf{y},y_{j})+J(y_{j})

\displaystyle\geq f(\mathbf{x},x_{j})-\varepsilon+J(x_{j})-\varepsilon=F(\mathbf{x},x_{j})-2\varepsilon=m_{j}(\mathbf{x})-2\varepsilon,

proving lower semicontinuity of
m_{j}
in this case.

Let now
I_{j}(\mathbf{x})
be non-degenerate, i.e.
x_{j}<x_{j+1}
. Take
z\in I_{j}(\mathbf{x})
be a point with
F(\mathbf{x},z)>m_{j}(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon
. By lower semicontinuity of
J
there is
\delta_{1}>0
such that for points
t\in(z-\delta_{1},z+\delta_{1})\cap[0,1]
we have
J(t)>J(z)-\varepsilon
.
We thus find some
w\in(z-\delta_{1},z+\delta_{1})\cap(x_{j},x_{j+1})
with
J(w)>J(z)-\varepsilon
. Take
0<\delta\leq\min\{\delta_{0},\delta_{1},w-x_{j},x_{j+1}-w\}
and some
\mathbf{y}
with
\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|<\delta
, then
w\in I_{j}(\mathbf{y})
and hence

m_{j}(\mathbf{y})\geq F(\mathbf{y},w)=f(\mathbf{y},w)+J(w)\geq f(\mathbf{x},z)-\varepsilon+J(z)-\varepsilon=F(\mathbf{x},z)-2\varepsilon\geq m_{j}(\mathbf{x})-3\varepsilon
. This proves lower semicontinuity also for this case.

(c) follows obviously from (a) and (b).
∎

m_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\geq m_{j}(\mathbf{x})-3k\varepsilon
and
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\in Y\cap S
, which finishes the proof.
∎

By analyzing the previous proof one can also see the following.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\nu_{j}>0
(
j=1,\ldots,n
) be given positive reals, let
K
be an either singular or finite valued kernel function and suppose that the finiteness domain

of the
n

Then

Proof.

We only need to prove that any
\mathbf{x}\in Y\cap\partial S
can be arbitrarily well approximated from
Y\cap S
. So fix
\mathbf{x}\in Y\cap\partial S
.
Then there are
x_{\ell}=\cdots=x_{r}

and no node with index greater than

and

The set
[0,1]\setminus X=X^{c}

, the node

and the node

∎

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\nu_{j}>0
(
j=1,\ldots,n
) be given positive reals, let
K
be an either singular or finite valued kernel function and suppose that the finiteness domain
X^{c}
of the
n
-field function
J
has no isolated points. Then the set

(17)

W:=\{\mathbf{w}:\mathbf{w}\in Y\cap S,\>\rint[w_{j},w_{j+1}]\cap X^{c}\neq\emptyset\text{ for }j=0,1,\dots,n\}

is dense in
Y
, where
\rint
denotes the relative interior within
[0,1]
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
3.11
,

Y\cap S
is dense in
Y
. We need to prove that for all
\mathbf{x}\in Y\cap S
either
\mathbf{x}\in W
, or for any
\varepsilon>0
there are points of
W
closer to
\mathbf{x}
than
\varepsilon
.

So let
\varepsilon>0
and
\mathbf{x}\in Y\cap S
be fixed arbitrarily. Denote by
k\leq n+1
the number of indices
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
with
\rint[x_{j},x_{j+1}]\cap X^{c}=\emptyset
(call such indices “exceptional”). We prove the assertion by induction on
k
. If
k=0
then
\mathbf{x}\in W
, and then there remains nothing to prove. So assume
k>0
and consider the statement holding for all smaller values of the number of exceptional indices.

As
k>0
, there is an index
j
with
\rint[x_{j},x_{j+1}]\cap X^{c}=\emptyset
.
This implies either
J(x_{j})>-\infty
or
J(x_{j+1})>-\infty
(or both), because otherwise we would have
F|_{I_{j}(\mathbf{x})}\equiv-\infty
, hence
m_{j}(\mathbf{x})=-\infty
, contradicting to
\mathbf{x}\in Y
. Suppose that, e.g.,
J(x_{j})>-\infty
holds (the other case being similar). Note that in this case we must have
j>0
and
x_{j}>0
, because for
j=0
and
\mathbf{x}\in S
, we have
x_{0}=0\in\rint I_{j}(\mathbf{x})
, and
\rint I_{j}(\mathbf{x})\cap X^{c}=\emptyset
prohibits
x_{0}\in X^{c}
, i.e., we must have
J(x_{0})=-\infty
.

Let
\eta:=\min(\varepsilon,x_{j+1}-x_{j},x_{j}-x_{j-1})
.
Since
\mathbf{x}\in Y\cap S
, we have
\eta>0
.
According to the assumption,
x_{j}
is a limit point of
X^{c}
. As
F
is identically
-\infty
on
(x_{j},x_{j}+\eta)\subset\rint I_{j}(\mathbf{x})
, we therefore
have some points
z\in(x_{j}-\eta,x_{j})\subset(x_{j-1},x_{j})
satisfying
J(z)>-\infty
.

Now we define
\mathbf{y}
to be the node system obtained from
\mathbf{x}
by changing
x_{j}
to some point
y_{j}\in(z,x_{j})
(which is possible by construction), and retaining all the other nodes putting
y_{i}:=x_{i}\penalty\ (i\neq j)
. Then
\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|=|y_{j}-x_{j}|<x_{j}-z<\eta\leq\varepsilon
. Also,
y_{j-1}:=x_{j-1}\leq x_{j}-\eta<z<y_{j}<x_{j}<y_{j+1}:=x_{j+1}
, so together with
\mathbf{x}
also
\mathbf{y}\in S
. It is easy to see that we also have
\mathbf{y}\in Y
, because for the only two intervals changed
m_{j-1}(\mathbf{y})\geq F(\mathbf{y},z)=f(\mathbf{y},z)+J(z)>-\infty
and
m_{j}(\mathbf{y})\geq F(\mathbf{y},x_{j})=f(\mathbf{y},x_{j})+J(x_{j})>-\infty
, the latter holding because
x_{j}\in(0,1)\setminus\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
, whence
f(\mathbf{y},x_{j})>-\infty
, while
J(x_{j})>-\infty
by construction.

Note also that
z\in\rint I_{j-1}(\mathbf{y})
and
x_{j}\in\rint I_{j}(\mathbf{y})
, while
\rint I_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\rint I_{i}(\mathbf{y})
for all the other indices
i
, so the number of exceptional indices for
\mathbf{y}
is less than that for
\mathbf{x}
.

By the inductive hypothesis we thus have some
\mathbf{w}\in W
such that for the node system
\mathbf{y}\in Y\cap S
the distance is
\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}\|<\varepsilon
. The assertion is proved.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\nu_{j}>0
(
j=1,\ldots,n
) be given positive reals, let
K
be an either singular or finite valued kernel function and suppose that the
n
-field function
J
satisfies (
16
) (so its finiteness domain
X^{c}
has no isolated points).

Then for every
\mathbf{x}\in Y\cap S
there is a sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
W
, where
W
is defined in (
17
), such that

\mathbf{x}_{k}\to\mathbf{x}
and

\liminf_{k\to\infty}m_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\geq m_{j}(\mathbf{x})\quad\text{for each $j\in\{0,1,\dots,n$}\}.

Proof.

We can assume that
K
is non-singular, since otherwise the functions
m_{j}
are continuous according to Lemma
3.1
, and then any sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
W
converging to
\mathbf{x}
will do (and there exists some such sequence by Lemma
3.12
).

We shall prove that for every
\varepsilon,\delta>0

there is
\mathbf{y}\in W
with
\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|<\delta
and

m_{j}(\mathbf{y})\geq m_{j}(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon\quad\text{for every $j\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}$,}

which will clearly imply the statement of the lemma. Let
\delta_{0}\in(0,\delta)
be chosen to
\varepsilon
from the uniform continuity of the pure sum of translates function
f
(see (
14
)).

Let
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, and suppose
\rint I_{j}(\mathbf{x})\cap X^{c}=\emptyset
. Since
\mathbf{x}\in Y
, this implies either
J(x_{j})>-\infty
or
J(x_{j+1})>-\infty
(or both). Suppose that, e.g.,
J(x_{j})>-\infty
holds (the other case being similar). Note that in this case we must have
j>0
and
x_{j}>0
, because
\mathbf{x}\in S
,
J(x_{j})>-\infty
and
\rint I_{j}(\mathbf{x})\cap X^{c}=\emptyset
.
Since
\limsup_{t\to x_{j}}J(t)\geq J(x_{j})
,—by the weak limsup condition (

\limsup_{t\uparrow x_{j}}J(t)\geq J(x_{j})>-\infty
, hence also
\limsup_{t\to x_{j}}F(\mathbf{x},t)\geq F(\mathbf{x},x_{j})
. Moreover, there exists
z_{j-1}\in[x_{j-1},x_{j})\cap(x_{j}-\delta_{0},x_{j})
with
F(\mathbf{x},z_{j-1})\geq m_{j-1}(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon
.
Let
x^{\prime}_{j}\in(z_{j-1},x_{j})\subset(x_{j}-\delta_{0},x_{j})
, and
replace in
\mathbf{x}
the node
x_{j}
by
x^{\prime}_{j}
, the new node system being denoted by

. We obviously have

hence in particular
\rint I_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\cap X^{c}\neq\emptyset

Let

. If

.

For

, we have

(18)

\displaystyle m_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=m_{j-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},z_{j-1})+J(z_{j-1})\geq f(\mathbf{x},z_{j-1})-\varepsilon+J(z_{j-1})

\displaystyle=F(\mathbf{x},z_{j-1})-\varepsilon\geq m_{j-1}(\mathbf{x})-2\varepsilon.

Repeating this argument for all indices

, for which

, we arrive at some

∎

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\nu_{j}>0
(
j=1,\ldots,n
) be given positive reals, let
K
be an either singular or finite valued kernel function and suppose
J
is an upper semicontinuous
n
-field function and, moreover, satisfies
(
16
) (thus its finiteness domain
X^{c}
has no isolated points).

Then
J
also satisfies the so-called “limsup condition”

(19)

\limsup_{s\to t}J(s)=J(t)\quad\text{for every $t\in[0,1]$,}

and we have that for every
\mathbf{x}\in Y
there is a sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
W
such that
\mathbf{x}_{k}\to\mathbf{x}
and

\lim_{k\to\infty}m_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{k})=m_{j}(\mathbf{x})\quad\text{for each $j\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}$}.

Proof.

Upper semicontinuity means
\limsup_{s\to t}J(s)\leq J(t)
, while condition (
16
) is the reverse inequality, hence they together give (
19
).

Let
\mathbf{x}\in Y
. According to Lemma
3.13
there is a sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})
in
Y\cap S
such that
\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}\|<\frac{1}{k}
and
m_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\geq m_{j}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and for all
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
. According to Lemma
3.10
there are sequences
(\mathbf{x}_{k,\ell})
in
W
such that
\|\mathbf{x}_{k,\ell}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|<\frac{1}{\ell}
and
m_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{k,\ell})\geq m_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\frac{1}{\ell}
for all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and for all
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
. Consider the diagonal sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k,k})
for which we have
\|\mathbf{x}_{k,k}-\mathbf{x}\|\leq\|\mathbf{x}_{k,k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|+\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}\|<\frac{2}{k}
and

m_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{k,k})\geq m_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\frac{1}{k}\geq m_{j}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{2}{k}

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and for all
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.
It follows that
\liminf_{k\to\infty}m_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{k,k})\geq m_{j}(\mathbf{x})
, while
\limsup_{k\to\infty}m_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{k,k})\leq m_{j}(\mathbf{x})
holds according to Proposition
3.6
(a). Therefore,
\lim_{k\to\infty}m_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{k,k})=m_{j}(\mathbf{x})
.
∎

4.
A result about the failure of strict majorization

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\nu_{j}>0
(
j=1,\ldots,n
) be given positive reals, let
K
be a monotone (
M
) kernel function, and let
J
be an
n
-field function satisfying the “limsup condition” (
19
) (in particular,
J
is upper semicontinuous).

Then
strict majorization

m_{i}(\mathbf{x})>m_{i}(\mathbf{y})
for every
i=0,1,\ldots,n
cannot hold between any two node systems
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in Y
.

Proof.

We can assume that
K
is real valued, i.e., non-singular, otherwise the statement is contained in Theorem
1.2
. (Recall that once we have (
M
), non-singularity entails finiteness of
K
.)

We argue by contradiction and suppose that for some
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in Y
one has

(20)

m_{j}(\mathbf{x})>m_{j}(\mathbf{y})\quad\text{for each $j=0,\dots,n$.}

According to Corollary
3.14
we can assume that
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in W\subseteq S
.

Since
K
is real valued the pure some of translates function
f
is continuous, hence uniformly continuous, on the compact set
\overline{S}\times[0,1]
. Therefore, for each
\varepsilon>0
, in particular for
\varepsilon:=\frac{1}{2}\min\{m_{j}(\mathbf{x})-m_{j}(\mathbf{y}):j=0,1,\dots,n\}
,
there is
\delta>0
such that (
14
) holds.

Since
m_{j}
is a supremum, there exist points
t_{j}\in I_{j}(\mathbf{x})
with
F(\mathbf{x},t_{j})>m_{j}(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon
(
j=0,1,\ldots,n
). This implies that
J(t_{j})>-\infty
, because
m_{j}(\mathbf{x})>-\infty
. Let
\mathcal{T}:=\{t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}
.

Note that it can happen that
\#\mathcal{T}<n+1
, in case two points
t_{i}
coincide; that is the case when
t_{i}=x_{i+1}=t_{i+1}
. However, only this type of coincidence can happen, as
t_{i}\in I_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, and
\mathbf{x}\in S
excludes intersection of more than two of the base intervals
I_{i}(\mathbf{x})
.

Let us separate two subsets of
\mathcal{T}
: the “interior part”
\mathcal{I}:=\{t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}\penalty\ :\penalty\ t_{i}\in\rint I_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}
and the “boundary part”
\mathcal{B}:=\{t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}\penalty\ :\penalty\ t_{i}\in(I_{i}(\mathbf{x})\setminus\rint I_{i}(\mathbf{x}))\}=\mathcal{T}\cap\{x_{1},\ldots,,x_{n}\}
. Let
\alpha_{0}:=\min\{|t-x_{k}|:t\in\mathcal{I},k=1,\ldots,n\}
if
\mathcal{I}\neq\emptyset
, otherwise set
\alpha_{0}:=1
. Since for
t\in\mathcal{I}
we have
t=t_{i}\in\rint I_{i}(\mathbf{x})
for some
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
, this quantity is positive.

Take
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{0})\cap(0,\delta)
arbitrarily. In view of the assumption (
19
), for each
t\in\mathcal{B}
there is a
t^{*}\in(t-\alpha,t+\alpha)\cap[0,1]
, distinct from
t
, such that
J(t^{*})>J(t)-\varepsilon
, too (and in fact there are infinitely many such points). Note that
t\in\mathcal{B}
meant that we had (with one or two indices
i
)
t=t_{i}=x_{j}
for some uniquely determined node
x_{j}
with
1\leq j\leq n
(all
x_{j}
are different for
\mathbf{x}\in S
). For this index
j
, corresponding to the selected
t
, let us write
w_{j-1}:=\min(t,t^{*})
and
z_{j}:=\max(t,t^{*})
.

Now we define a new node system
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
with the following simple rule. If for some
j
the node
x_{j}
is matching with some of the points
t\in\mathcal{T}
(i.e.
t=t_{i}=x_{j}\in\mathcal{B}
), then we replace
x_{j}
by
x_{j}^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{2}(w_{j-1}+z_{j})
. If
x_{j}\not\in\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{T}\cap\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
, then we just leave
x_{j}^{\prime}:=x_{j}
.
Note that if at all
x_{j}^{\prime}\neq x_{j}
, then we have
|x_{j}^{\prime}-x_{j}|=|x_{j}-\frac{w_{j-1}+z_{j}}{2}|=|x_{j}-\frac{t+t^{*}}{2}|=\frac{|t^{*}-x_{j}|}{2}<\alpha/2
. This means in particular that if
\alpha<\min\{x_{j+1}-x_{j}:j=0,\ldots,n\}
, too (which minimum is positive because
\mathbf{x}\in S
), then we have
0<x_{1}^{\prime}<\cdots<x_{n}^{\prime}<1
, hence the ordering of the nodes remained intact, and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in S
, too.

There are two important properties of the new node system
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
. The first one has already been observed:
\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|<\alpha/2\leq\delta
. Therefore we also have
|f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},s^{\prime})-f(\mathbf{x},s)|<\varepsilon
for all
s,s^{\prime}\in[0,1]
with
|s^{\prime}-s|<\delta
. It follows that in particular
|F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},w_{j-1})-F(\mathbf{x},x_{j})|,|F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},z_{j})-F(\mathbf{x},x_{j})|<\varepsilon
and
F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},w_{j-1}),F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},z_{j})>F(\mathbf{x},x_{j})-\varepsilon
. Second, we claim that for each
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
there is
s_{i}\in\rint I_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
satisfying

(21)

F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},s_{i})>m_{i}(\mathbf{x})-2\varepsilon.

To see this, consider first the case when
t_{i}\in\mathcal{I}
. Then
t_{i}\in\rint I_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, and by construction its distance from
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
is at least
\alpha_{0}>\alpha
, hence even for
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
we have
s_{i}:=t_{i}\in\rint I_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
. By choice of the
t_{i}
and uniform continuity of
f
, however, we must have
F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},s_{i})=F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t_{i})>F(\mathbf{x},t_{i})-\varepsilon>m_{i}(\mathbf{x})-2\varepsilon
, as needed. Second, consider the case when
t_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
, so
t_{i}=x_{j}
for some
j
. By construction
w_{j-1}<x_{j}^{\prime}<z_{j}
. So if
i=j-1
then
x^{\prime}_{j-1}\leq x_{j-1}+\alpha/2<x_{j}-\alpha/2<w_{j-1}<x_{j}^{\prime}
and
w_{j-1}\in\rint I_{j-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
. So if
i=j-1
we can choose
s_{i}=w_{j-1}\in\rint I_{j-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
and have
F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},s_{i})=F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},w_{j-1})>F(\mathbf{x},x_{j})-\varepsilon>m_{i}(\mathbf{x})-2\varepsilon
, for in case
i=j-1
and
x_{j}=t_{j-1}
we had
F(\mathbf{x},x_{j})=F(\mathbf{x},t_{j-1})>m_{j-1}(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon
. Similarly, if
i=j
, then we choose
s_{j}=z_{j}
, noting that
x_{j}^{\prime}<z_{j}<x_{j}+\alpha/2<x_{j+1}-\alpha/2<x_{j+1}^{\prime}
and
z_{j}\in\rint I_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
. Then we have with
s_{j}:=z_{j}
that
F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},s_{i})=F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},z_{j})>F(\mathbf{x},x_{j})-\varepsilon>m_{i}(\mathbf{x})-2\varepsilon
, for in case
i=j
and
x_{j}=t_{j}
we had
F(\mathbf{x},x_{j})=F(\mathbf{x},t_{j})>m_{j}(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon
.
This establishes the asserted, second property of
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
.

In all, from (
21
) we get for each
i=0,1,\ldots,n
that
m_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\geq F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},s_{i})>m_{i}(\mathbf{x})-2\varepsilon
. It follows that, in particular,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in W
, too.

Finally, set
\eta_{0}:=\min\{|x^{\prime}_{j}-s_{i}|:i,j=1,\dots,n\}>0
, and consider the singular kernel functions
K^{(\eta)}(t):=K(t)+\log_{-}(|t|/\eta)
where
\log_{-}=\min(\log,0)
. Denote by
F^{(\eta)}
and
m_{j}^{(\eta)}
the sum of translates function and the interval maxima functions for the kernel
K^{(\eta)}
and the same
n
-field function
J
.
Since
K^{(\eta)}\leq K
, we immediately conclude
m_{j}^{(\eta)}\leq m_{j}
for each
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
. Note that for
\eta<\eta_{0}
we have

F^{(\eta)}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},s_{j})=F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},s_{j})
, because all the
\log_{-}
terms vanish. Therefore, for

is indeed a node system, i.e. the ordering of the nodes remain in the natural order, in view of the choice of the
\alpha_{i}
, and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in A_{i,j,\mathbf{x}}
for every
i
with
x_{j}\not\in I_{i}(\mathbf{x})
.

Now, let
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
be arbitrary.
If
x_{j}\not\in I_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, then
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in A_{i,j,\mathbf{x}}
and
\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|<\alpha\leq\alpha_{i}
, so by the above

m_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\geq m_{i}(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon.

Thus for such
i
, we have
m_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})>m(\overline{S})
(i.e.,
i\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
) if
m_{i}(\mathbf{x})>m(\overline{S})
(i.e., if
i\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x})
).

On the other hand, if
x_{j}\in I_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, then either
x_{j}=x_{i}
or
x_{j}=x_{i+1}
, hence also
x_{j}^{\prime}=x_{i}^{\prime}
or
x_{j}^{\prime}=x_{i+1}^{\prime}
, and then
x_{j}^{\prime}\in I_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
, too. Therefore,

\displaystyle m_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},x_{j}^{\prime})=f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},x_{j}^{\prime})+J(x_{j}^{\prime})\geq f(\mathbf{x},x_{j})-\varepsilon+J^{*}(x_{j})-\varepsilon

\displaystyle=F^{*}(\mathbf{x},x_{j})-2\varepsilon=m^{*}_{j}(\mathbf{x})-2\varepsilon>m(\overline{S}).

In particular, choosing
i=j
we see
m_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})>m(\overline{S})
—i.e.,
j\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
. Further, from the argumentations in the cases
x_{j}\not\in I_{i}(\mathbf{x})
and
x_{j}\in I_{i}(\mathbf{x})
altogether also
\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
follows.
The proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Consider the upper semicontinuous regularization
J^{*}
of
J
and denote the corresponding sum of translate function and the corresponding quantities by
F^{*}
,
M^{*}
and
m^{*}
, respectively. Then
m^{*}(\overline{S})=M^{*}(\overline{S})
by Proposition
5.4
and
M^{*}(\overline{S})=M(\overline{S})
by Lemma
6.1
. Altogether, we already have

(23)

m(\overline{S})\leq m^{*}(\overline{S})=M^{*}(\overline{S})=M(\overline{S}).

Furthermore, the equality
M(\overline{S})=m(\overline{S})
follows from (
23
) and Lemma
6.4
. Further, if
\mathbf{e}
is any equioscillation point (there may not be any in general!), then
M(\overline{S})\leq\overline{m}(\mathbf{e})=\underline{m}(\mathbf{e})\leq m(\overline{S})
so one has equality everywhere. Also, there exists a point
\mathbf{w}\in\overline{S}
with
\overline{m}(\mathbf{w})=M(\overline{S})
in view of the continuity of
\overline{m}
on
\overline{S}
, furnished by Lemma
3.3
. Moreover, if
J
is upper semicontinuous, then according to Proposition
5.3
there exists an equioscillation point.

Regarding majorization, assume for a contradiction that strict majorization holds for some node systems
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in Y
, that is,
m_{j}(\mathbf{y})>m_{j}(\mathbf{x})>-\infty
for all
j=0,1,\ldots,n
. Take
\varepsilon>0
with

\varepsilon<\min\{m_{j}(\mathbf{y})-m_{j}(\mathbf{x}):\ j=0,1,\ldots,n\}.

Further, for all
j=0,1,\ldots,n
, there exists
u_{j}\in I_{j}(\mathbf{y})
such that
F(\mathbf{y},u_{j})>m_{j}(\mathbf{y})-\varepsilon
and
v_{j}\in I_{j}(\mathbf{x})
such that
F(\mathbf{x},v_{j})>-\infty
. Let us consider the sets
U:=\{u_{j}\penalty\ :\penalty\ j=0,1,\ldots,n\}\subset[0,1]
and
V:=\{v_{j}\penalty\ :\penalty\ j=0,1,\ldots,n\}\subset[0,1]
. If
\#(U\cup V)=n+1
, then we are fine, but there can occur some coincidences among the
u_{j}
and
v_{k}
, which can cause
\#(U\cup V)
being smaller than
n+1
. To avoid arising complications, we can take any subset
W\subset X^{c}
with the weighted cardinality (with weights
1/2
at endpoints
0,1
) exceeding
n
. This is the exact condition for
J
being an
n
-field function, thus such a set
W
exists (even if it may not be disjoint from
U\cup V
). In any case, consider now the union
A:=U\cup V\cup W
.

Now we introduce the new upper bounded function

\widetilde{J}(t):=\begin{cases}J(t),&\text{ if }t\in A,\\
-\infty,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Observe that
\widetilde{J}
is upper semicontinuous, and it is an
n
-field function, too, as it clearly satisfies the finiteness condition for that category. Keeping the kernel
K
and the coefficients
\nu_{i},\penalty\ i=1,\ldots,n
, but taking
\widetilde{J}
in place of
J
we get the respective sum of translates function
\widetilde{F}
and interval maxima
\widetilde{m}_{j}
, respectively. It is clear that
\widetilde{J}\leq J
, hence
\widetilde{F}\leq F
and
\widetilde{m}_{j}\leq m_{j}
, too. By the choice of
v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
we have
\widetilde{m}_{j}(\mathbf{x})>-\infty
for every
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}
belongs to the regularity set
\widetilde{Y}
for the modified situation.

Finally,

m_{j}(\mathbf{y})-\varepsilon<F(\mathbf{y},u_{j})=\widetilde{F}(\mathbf{y},u_{j})\leq\widetilde{m}_{j}(\mathbf{y}),

therefore

\widetilde{m}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\leq m_{j}(\mathbf{x})<m_{j}(\mathbf{y})-\varepsilon\leq\widetilde{m}_{j}(\mathbf{y}),

which shows that we have strict majorization with the upper semicontinuous
n
-field function
\widetilde{J}
, contradicting Proposition
5.2
.
Theorem
1.3
is proved.
∎

7.
Concluding remarks and open problems

With the present study we have arrived at a considerably complete description
of Fenton type minimax theorems for the interval.
Therefore, it is in order to comment on the developments thus far
and the many directions where further work may be interesting.

Our above Theorem
1.3
formulates a Fenton type result under essentially minimal assumptions,
once one accepts the concavity assumption on
K
.
Neither singularity, nor the cusp condition (

∞
′

±

‣
1
) of Fenton is assumed here,
and neither strict concavity (or monotonicity), nor any smoothness assumptions or
conditions involving derivatives are used.
Furthermore, the field function can be almost arbitrary, satisfying only the necessary requirement
so that
F(\mathbf{x},\cdot)
becomes non-constant
-\infty
for every
\mathbf{x}
.
Beside convexity of
K
,
the only extra assumption is its monotonicity, which, on the other hand,
was used many times throughout the argumentations.
It was already shown in
[
9
]
that the monotonicity condition cannot be just dropped:
The assertions badly fail to hold without it.
However, the role of monotonicity is still a bit mysterious,
because in
[
10
]
we did not require it for the periodic (i.e., torus) case,
while in
[
7
]
concerning the case of singular kernels on the interval,
we could prove a homeomorphism theorem under a weaker assumption

K^{\prime}(t)-K^{\prime}(t-1)\geq c>0\quad\text{for a.e.{} $t\in[0,1]$},

called “periodized
c
-monotonicity”.
At the the same time in the periodic case (which would correspond to
c=0
)
also some singularity-type assumptions were needed for the field
J
.

For not necessarily singular kernels the argumentation had to follow
a different route than the one for singular kernels. Indeed, a crucial difference
is that in case of singular kernels a surprisingly general homeomorphism theorem was found.
This result, Theorem 2.1 of
[
7
]
, however, must necessarily fail in case the kernels are non-singular, as is discussed in Example 8.2 of the mentioned latter paper.

The yields of the homeomorphism theorem, so useful in case of singular kernels, are thus irrelevant
for the minimax results here in the case of non-singular kernels.
However, it would be interesting to clarify the following matters
(this has been done for singular
K
in
[
7
]
).
Do we still have that, under some suitable assumptions, the sum of translate function,
and even the functions
m_{j}
and the maximum vector
\mathbf{m}
are locally
Lipschitz functions (on an appropriate domain)?
Note that in full generality the
m_{j}
are not even continuous, as is seen from the above mentioned Example 8.2 of
[
7
]
, so any affirmative answer could be formulated on some appropriate domain and under some suitable assumptions at best. Further, do we have local Lipschitz continuity for the functions

\overline{m}
and
\underline{m}
, too? If yes, is it still true that the a.e. existing
derivative provides a non-singular Clarke derivative (generalized Jacobian)
for the difference function
\Phi:=(m_{1}-m_{0},\ldots,m_{n+1}-m_{n})
,
as happens for singular kernels, see Section 6 in
[
7
]
?
If yes, then
\Phi
is a locally invertible locally Lipschitz mapping.
Is it a global homeomorphism between
Y
and its image
\Phi(Y)\subsetneqq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
?
At first glance, there is quite a difficulty here. Indeed, in the singular case
we could apply the well-known topology result usually attributed to Hadamard and, according to our search, recorded first by Eilenberg
[
4
]
in the thirties (and recurred several times since then, see the references in
[
7
]
), which says that if a mapping is a local homeomorphism and
is unbounded as we approach the boundary, then it is a global homeomorphism, too. However, in the case
K
is non-singular, even if local invertibility may be proven, the properness of the difference mapping
\Phi
is not at all clear.

As said, for non-singular kernels there is no homeomorphism theorem,
a key ingredient to prove the failure of majorization
m_{j}(\mathbf{x})\leq m_{j}(\mathbf{y})

for every
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in Y
, called
intertwining
;
this is Theorem 4.1 of
[
9
]
.
However, on a completely different route—by elementary but involved arguments—in
[
8
]

we could arrive at such intertwining results even for non-singular
(but strictly concave and monotone) kernels, unfortunately only if
n\in\{1,2,3\}
.
In
[
8
]
we thus also formulated the conjecture that
the same phenomenon of intertwining occurs for larger
n
, too—a somewhat mysterious challenge which we could not progress with any further.
A possible approach is to seek the “next best thing” and replace the global homeomorphism by global injectivity. We have to admit that, although we have
local
injectivity, global injectivity of
\Phi
, appears to be totally out of grasp.
Nevertheless, to us it seems plausible that in cases when global injectivity of
\Phi
remains true—and maybe other favorable circumstances, such as continuity of the functions
m_{j}
, hold—then also the intertwining result extends.

The weaker property, the exclusion of
strict
majorization,
is completely settled in the present paper, as a part of the main result Theorem
1.3
.
This also indicates the plausibility of the conjecture put forward in
[
8
]
.
Given that we have the statement for small
n
, an inductive argument would seem natural,
but as the number of cases in the combinatorics of the possible inequalities
between the nodes
x_{i},y_{i}
grow super-exponentially, the case when
n>3
appeared intractable.
Of course, there can be also some other approach, proving the statement for sufficiently nice,
say continuous fields, and then one could try to argue for general fields as we did in the proof of
Theorem
1.3
at the end of Section
6
.

In this paper we looked for the possibly least restrictive conditions
under which the
original
result of Fenton may be extended.
We thus have dealt with intervals, normalized to
[0,1]
and
[-1,1]
.
However, other setups are also important. Analogous questions for the
torus
\mathbb{T}:={\mathbb{R}}/\mathbb{Z}
(periodic/unit circle) were already brought up
by Ambrus, Ball, Erdélyi
[
1
]
in connection with
the so-called strong polarization constant problem—we have accounted for
the development in this direction shortly in the Introduction and in more detail in
[
9
]
.
Even if the condition of monotonicity does not play a role in the torus setup, the corresponding
Fenton theory still stays much less developed in this case. In our work
[
10
]
we considered the situation when also
J
(called
K_{0}
there) is concave, but later developments in the interval case, relaxing considerably the assumptions on
J
, were not followed on
\mathbb{T}
. Therefore, the direction of relaxing conditions on
J
in the periodic case remains the subject of future investigations.

An interesting observation, however trivial, is that an essential difference
between the interval and torus setup is that for the interval certain parts of
the defining domain of the kernel functions
K
are translated in, and other parts
are translated out from the space (i.e.,
[0,1]
) where we consider the maxima and the minimax questions.
In the torus setup all the translates live on the whole
\mathbb{T}
,
where also the maxima etc. are considered.
That difference seems to classify two cases of reasonable
setups—our feeling is that this may be connected with the necessity
or dispensability of extra monotonicity conditions.
Note that interpreting the torus setup by taking
K
periodic
\mod 1
,
it is clear that periodicity and the monotonicity condition (
M
)
can both hold only in the trivial, i.e.
K\equiv\text{constant}
cases.

As for other meaningful setups, the cases of the real line and the positive halfline must be mentioned.
These are important because of their numerous applications; for example, much of weighted
approximation theory and weighted potential theory were developed right for them.
(In fact, Fenton’s original application, in the theory of entire functions,
would also fit more naturally to the situation of the infinite halfline.)
In both cases, the kernel
K
is defined on
{\mathbb{R}}
being concave on
(-\infty,0)
and
(0,\infty)
.
The differences are in the definition of
J
, and in the possible locations of nodes in
{\mathbb{R}}

or in
[0,\infty)
, respectively. The translating in – translating out aspect makes the halfline similar to the interval case, and
{\mathbb{R}}
similar to the torus case.

Also, for infinite intervals some kind of admissibility condition is required for
J
,
since otherwise the suprema on the unbounded intervals may be infinite. Such admissibility conditions are also present in weighted logarithmic potential theory on the real line resp. halfline. At this point we need to have a closer look at our normalization conditions. In logarithmic potential theory very often the total weight of translates—the total measure with which we consider potentials—is normalized to 1. If so, also the weight function
w
, or its logarithm
J:=\log w
can be considered with power (weight) 1. If on the other hand we consider degree
n
polynomials without normalizing them, then the total measure of translates becomes
n
and then also
w^{n}
becomes the natural counterpart. This motivates our normalization with considering the constant
\nu:=\sum_{i}\nu_{i}
, and assume, as a natural admissibility condition, that we have

(24)

\lim_{|t|\to\infty}\nu K(t)+J(t)=-\infty.

In the case of the halfline
[0,\infty)
this should be modified by writing “
t\to+\infty
” only.

This admissibility condition should be compared to the ones
generally used in logarithmic potential theory of weighted approximation,
for example to (iii) in Definition I.1.1 for an admissible weight
w:\Sigma\to[0,\infty)
,

\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{C}}
in the book
[
21
]
(the relation to our framework is
\log w=J
).

The situation there with an arbitrary, closed
\Sigma
, where the weight is defined, and corresponding questions of minimax etc. are posed, is seemingly more general than ours restricting to intervals.
However, for absolute value maximization etc. one can easily reconfigure
the system of conditions by extending
w
as identically
0
(
J\equiv-\infty
) outside
\Sigma

(a modification not spoiling the upper semicontinuity condition postulated in (ii)
in the mentioned Definition I.1.1 in view of the assumption that
\Sigma
is closed).

So, the logarithmic potential theory setup considers
\Sigma^{c}
as a set
where
w
is not defined, and invokes still another set
\Sigma_{0}\subset\Sigma
,
where it may not vanish, and which set must have positive inner capacity.
The distinction is, however, inessential: the setup corresponds to allowing
J=\log w

being
-\infty
on
\Sigma^{c}\cup(\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{0})
, i.e. altogether on
\Sigma_{0}^{c}
.
So our setup, when we allow
X:=J^{-1}(\{-\infty\})
to be any set which leaves
more than
n
points in its complement, is already much more general
(as we do not assume positive capacity) for sets
\Sigma_{0}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
. The same way, we are more general
in our basic setup
when skipping the condition of upper semicontinuity of
J
or
w
, assumed in (ii) of the said definition
(and throughout logarithmic potential theory). Needless to say,
\log|\cdot|
is just one particular example of concave (and also singular and strictly monotone) kernel functions.

Altogether, our setup is more general than that of weighted approximation theory
and logarithmic potential theory, so we can expect general results which
have direct consequences in various special settings of the latter, like e.g. Chebyshev
constants of perfect sets, Bojanov type approximation theorems etc.
On the other hand it is clear that Fenton theory, at least in its current degree of development,
cannot deal with the most important questions of asymptotic nature
when some limits are taken with respect to the number of translates etc. These asymptotic questions form the most important goals of logarithmic potential theory, and it is natural that for handling them some additional conditions need to be required. We have seen that Fenton’s approach can give surprisingly precise results in a greater generality—for the minimax, equioscillation and majorization questions studied for given
n
and coefficients
\nu_{i}
. However, we have not addressed asymptotic questions of potential theory with this approach. There is such a chapter of general potential theory, see, e.g.,
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
23
,
24
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
11
]
, where even without concavity much of potential theory is developed,
but it remains to future work to connect these and our Fenton setup.

Let us point out just one natural question, which might be of the same
crucial importance in the Fenton theory of infinite intervals
as it plays in logarithmic potential theory.
Namely, in the latter the existence of a so-called
Mhaskar-Rakhmanov-Saff number
a_{n}:=a_{n}(w)
is proved, with the property that once we consider a polynomial
p
of degree
not exceeding
n
with the given admissible weight
w
(or
w^{n}
),
then
\|p\|_{w}:=\|pw\|_{\infty}
(or
\|pw^{n}\|_{\infty}
) equals the supremum norm on
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
.
(This is generally expressed by saying that a weighted polynomial “lives” on the
Mhaskar-Rakhmanov-Saff interval
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
.) Taking logarithms of absolute values and
specifying
x_{i}
as the zeros of
p
, with taking
\nu_{i}=1

this translates to
\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}(\log|p(t)|+\log w(t))=\sup_{t\in[-a_{n},a_{n}]}(\log|p(t)|+\log w(t))
.
Our question is if in general, for arbitrary concave kernels and possibly also very general fields
J
satisfying (
24
) this remains in effect?

Last, but not least, let us mention a question, which goes in a different direction.
Already with the general homeomorphism theorem for singular kernels,
we could deal with
different
kernels
K_{j}
, not just
different multiples

\nu_{j}K
of the same base kernel function
K
.
It is hard to formulate a real life analogy, like potential theory, for this case,
but maybe in physical systems where different forces and laws, such as strong forces and gravity etc. superpose,
some heuristics can be found.
At any rate, the question is challenging: How much of the Fenton theory goes through,
and under which (possibly minimally restrictive) conditions?
Note that at several instances we have already formulated general results,
sometimes even dropping concavity, but these partial results did not yet compile into a unified theory.
In particular, minimax and maximin results as well as equioscillation property
of extremal points, can possibly hold true, at least for suitably tailored fields.
In our current work we were aiming at allowing the possibly most general field functions;
but it remains an open problem to clarify whether the basic findings of Fenton’s theory can be established also for different kernels under some suitable assumptions.
