Finding Bipartite Components in Hypergraphs A preliminary version of this work appeared at NeurIPS 2021. This work is supported by a Langmuir PhD Scholarship, and an EPSRC Early Career Fellowship (EP/T00729X/1).
Peter Macgregor, He Sun

Abstract
Hypergraphs are important objects to model ternary or higher-order relations of objects, and have a number of applications in analysing many complex datasets occurring in practice. In this work we study a new heat diffusion process in hypergraphs, and employ this process to design a polynomial-time algorithm that approximately finds bipartite components in a hypergraph. We theoretically prove the performance of our proposed algorithm, and compare it against the previous state-of-the-art through extensive experimental analysis on both synthetic and real-world datasets. We find that our new algorithm consistently and significantly outperforms the previous state-of-the-art across a wide range of hypergraphs.

Introduction

Spectral methods study the efficient matrix representation of graphs and datasets, and apply the algebraic properties of these matrices to design efficient algorithms. Over the last three decades, spectral methods have become one of the most powerful techniques in machine learning, and have had comprehensive applications in a wide range of domains, including clustering
[
NJW01
,
vL07
]
, image and video segmentation
[
SM00
]
, and network analysis
[
SR03
]

non-linear
Laplacian operators for more complex objects (i.e., hypergraphs) and employing these non-linear operators to design hypergraph algorithms with better performance.

Our contribution

Preliminaries

Graphs.

Throughout the paper,
‘graph’ always refers to a non-hyper graph.
We always use
{G}=({V}_{G},{E}_{G},{w})
to express a graph, in which every edge
e\in{E}_{G}
consists of two vertices in
{V}_{G}
and we let
n=\left\lvert{V}_{G}\right\rvert
.
The degree of any vertex
u\in{V}_{G}
is defined by
{d}_{G}(u)\triangleq\sum_{v\in{V}_{G}}{w}(u,v)
,
and for any
{S}\subseteq{V}_{G}
the volume of
{S}
is defined by
\mathrm{vol}_{G}({S})\triangleq\sum_{u\in{S}}{d}_{G}(u)
. Following
[
Tre12
]
, the
bipartiteness ratio
of any disjoint sets
{L},{R}\subset{V}_{G}
is defined by

{\beta}_{G}({L},{R})\triangleq\frac{2{w}({L},{L})+2{w}({R},{R})+{w}({L}\cup{R},\overline{{L}\cup{R}})}{\mathrm{vol}_{G}({L}\cup{R})}

where
{w}({A},{B})=\sum_{(u,v)\in{A}\times{B}}{w}(u,v)
,
and we further define

{\beta}_{G}\triangleq\min_{{S}\subset{V}}{\beta}_{G}({S},{V}\setminus{S})
.
Notice that a low
{\beta}_{G}
-value means that there is a dense cut between
{L}
and
{R}
, and there is a sparse cut between
{L}\cup{R}
and
{V}\setminus({L}\cup{R})
. In particular,
{\beta}_{G}=0
implies that
({L},{R})
forms a bipartite component of
{G}
.
We use
{\mathbf{D}}_{G}
to denote the
n\times n
diagonal matrix whose entries are
({\mathbf{D}}_{G})_{uu}={d}_{G}(u)
, for all
u\in{V}_{G}
.
Moreover, we use
{\mathbf{A}}_{G}
to denote the
n\times n
adjacency matrix whose entries are
({\mathbf{A}}_{G})_{uv}={w}(u,v)
, for all
u,v\in{V}_{G}
.
The Laplacian matrix is defined by
{\mathbf{L}}_{G}\triangleq{\mathbf{D}}_{G}-{\mathbf{A}}_{G}
.
In addition, we define the signless Laplacian operator
{\mathbf{J}}_{G}\triangleq{\mathbf{D}}_{G}+{\mathbf{A}}_{G}
, and its normalised counterpart
{\mathbf{Z}}_{G}\triangleq{\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{G}{\mathbf{J}}_{G}{\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{G}
.
For any real and symmetric matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
, the eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{A}}
are denoted by
\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{A}})\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n}({\mathbf{A}})
, and the eigenvector associated with
\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{A}})
is denoted by
{\bm{f}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}})
for
1\leq i\leq n
.

Hypergraphs.

Let
{H}=({V}_{H},{E}_{H},{w})
be a hypergraph with
n=\left\lvert{V}_{H}\right\rvert
vertices and weight function
{w}:{E}_{H}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{+}
. For any vertex
v\in{V}_{H}
, the degree of
v
is defined by
{d}_{H}(v)\triangleq\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\cdot\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{v\in e}\right.}v\in e\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{v\in e}\right]}
, where
\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{X}\right.}X\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{X}\right]}=1
if event
X
holds and
\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{X}\right.}X\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{X}\right]}=0
otherwise.
The
rank
of edge
e\in{E}_{H}
is written as
\mathrm{rank}(e)
and is defined to be the total number of vertices in
e
. For any
{A},{B}\subset{V}_{H}
, the cut value between
{A}
and
{B}
is defined by

{w}({A},{B})\triangleq\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\cdot\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{e\cap{A}\neq\emptyset\land e\cap{B}\neq\emptyset}\right.}e\cap{A}\neq\emptyset\land e\cap{B}\neq\emptyset\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{e\cap{A}\neq\emptyset\land e\cap{B}\neq\emptyset}\right]}.

Sometimes, we are required to analyse the weights of edges that intersect some vertex sets and not others. To this end, we define for any
{A},{B},{C}\subseteq{V}_{H}
that

{w}({A},{B}~|~{C})\triangleq\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\cdot\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{e\cap{A}\neq\emptyset\land e\cap{B}\neq\emptyset\land e\cap C=\emptyset}\right.}e\cap{A}\neq\emptyset\land e\cap{B}\neq\emptyset\land e\cap C=\emptyset\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{e\cap{A}\neq\emptyset\land e\cap{B}\neq\emptyset\land e\cap C=\emptyset}\right]},

and we sometimes write
{w}({A}~|~{C})\triangleq{w}({A},{A}~|~{C})
for simplicity.
Generalising the notion of the bipartiteness ratio of a graph, the
bipartiteness ratio
of sets
{L},{R}
in a hypergraph
{H}
is defined by

{\beta}_{H}({L},{R})\triangleq\frac{2{w}({L}|\overline{{L}})+2{w}({R}|\overline{{R}})+{w}({L},\overline{{L}\cup{R}}|{R})+w({R},\overline{{L}\cup{R}}|{L})}{\mathrm{vol}({L}\cup{R})},

and we define

{\beta}_{H}\triangleq\min_{{S}\subset{V}_{H}}{\beta}_{H}({S},{V}_{H}\setminus{S}).

For any hypergraph
H

, we define the discrepancy of an edge

with respect to

as

{\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)\triangleq\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v).

Moreover, the weighted discrepancy of

is

c_{{\bm{f}}}(e)\triangleq{w}(e)\left\lvert{\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)\right\rvert,

and for any set
{S}\subseteq{E}_{H}
we have

c_{{\bm{f}}}({S})\triangleq\sum_{e\in{S}}c_{{\bm{f}}}(e).

The discrepancy of
{H}
with respect to
{\bm{f}}
is defined by

D({\bm{f}})\triangleq\frac{\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\cdot{\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)^{2}}{\sum_{v\in{V}_{H}}{d}_{H}(v)\cdot{\bm{f}}(v)^{2}}.

For any

{\bm{f}},{\bm{g}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the weighted inner product between
{\bm{f}}
and
{\bm{g}}
is defined by

\langle{\bm{f}},{\bm{g}}\rangle_{w}\triangleq{\bm{f}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{H}{\bm{g}},

where

is the diagonal matrix consisting of the degrees of all the vertices of
{H}
. The weighted norm of
{\bm{f}}
is defined by
\left\|{\bm{f}}\right\|_{w}^{2}\triangleq\langle{\bm{f}},{\bm{f}}\rangle_{w}
.

For any non-linear operator
{\mathbf{J}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we say that
(\lambda,{\bm{f}})
is an eigen-pair if and only if
{\mathbf{J}}{\bm{f}}=\lambda{\bm{f}}
and note that in general, a non-linear operator can have any number of eigenvalues and eigenvectors.
Additionally, we define the weighted Rayleigh quotient of the operator
{\mathbf{J}}
and vector
{\bm{f}}
to be

R_{\mathbf{J}}({\bm{f}})\triangleq\frac{{\bm{f}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{J}}{\bm{f}}}{\left\|{\bm{f}}\right\|_{w}^{2}}.

It is important to remember that throughout the paper we always use the letter
{H}
to represent a hypergraph, and
{G}
to represent a graph.

Baseline Algorithms

Before we come to the definition of the new algorithm, let us first consider the baseline algorithms for finding bipartite components in hypergraphs.
One natural idea is to

;

4
Diffusion process and the algorithm

In this section, we introduce a new diffusion process in hypergraphs and use it to design a polynomial-time algorithm for finding bipartite components in hypergraphs.
We first study graphs to give some intuition, and then generalise to hypergraphs and describe our algorithm.
Finally, we sketch some of the detailed analysis which proves that the diffusion process is well defined.

4.1
The diffusion process in graphs

To discuss the intuition behind our designed diffusion process, let us look at the case of graphs. Let
{G}=({V},{E},{w})
be a graph, and we have for any
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that

\frac{{\bm{x}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Z}}_{G}{\bm{x}}}{{\bm{x}}^{\intercal}{\bm{x}}}=\frac{{\bm{x}}^{\intercal}({\mathbf{I}}+{\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{G}{\mathbf{A}}_{G}{\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{G}){\bm{x}}}{{\bm{x}}^{\intercal}{\bm{x}}}.

By setting
{\bm{x}}={\mathbf{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{G}{\bm{y}}
, we have that

\frac{{\bm{x}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Z}}_{G}{\bm{x}}}{{\bm{x}}^{\intercal}{\bm{x}}}=\frac{{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{G}{\mathbf{Z}}_{G}{\mathbf{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{G}{\bm{y}}}{{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{G}{\bm{y}}}=\frac{{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}({\mathbf{D}}_{G}+{\mathbf{A}}_{G}){\bm{y}}}{{\bm{y}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{G}{\bm{y}}}=\frac{\sum_{\{u,v\}\in{E}_{G}}{w}(u,v)\cdot({\bm{y}}(u)+{\bm{y}}(v))^{2}}{\sum_{u\in{V}_{G}}{d}_{G}(u)\cdot{\bm{y}}(u)^{2}}.

(4.1)

It is easy to see that
\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{Z}}_{G})=0
if
{G}
is bipartite, and it is known that
\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{Z}}_{G})
and its corresponding eigenvector
{\bm{f}}_{1}({\mathbf{Z}}_{G})
are closely related to two densely connected components of
{G}

[
Tre12
]
.
Moreover, similar to the heat equation for graph Laplacians
{\mathbf{L}}_{G}
, suppose
{\mathbf{D}}_{G}{\bm{f}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is some measure on the vertices of
{G}
, then a diffusion process defined by the differential equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{f}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-{\mathbf{D}}_{G}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{G}{\bm{f}}_{t}\

(4.2)

will converge to the eigenvector of
{\mathbf{D}}_{G}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{G}
with minimum eigenvalue and
can be employed to find two densely connected components of the underlying graph.

4.2
The hypergraph diffusion and our algorithm

Now we study whether one can construct a new hypergraph operator
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
which generalises the diffusion in graphs to hypergraphs.
First of all, we focus on a fixed time
t
with measure vector
{\mathbf{D}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and ask whether we can follow (
4.2
) and define the rate of change

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{f}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}

so that the diffusion can proceed for an infinitesimal time step.
Our intuition is that the rate of change due to some edge
e\in{E}_{H}
should involve only the vertices in
e
with the maximum or minimum value in the normalised measure
{\bm{f}}_{t}
.
To formalise this, for any edge
e\in{E}_{H}
, we define

{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)\triangleq\{v\in e:{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)=\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}_{t}(u)\}\mbox{\ \ \ and\ \ \ }{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)\triangleq\{v\in e:{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)=\min_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}_{t}(u)\}.

That is, for any edge
e
and normalised measure
{\bm{f}}_{t}
,
{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)\subseteq e
consists of the vertices
v
adjacent to
e
whose
{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)
values are maximum and
{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)\subseteq e
consists of the vertices
v
adjacent to
e
whose
{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)
values are minimum.
See Figure
3
for an example.
Then, applying the
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
operator to a vector
{\bm{f}}_{t}
should be equivalent to applying the operator
{\mathbf{J}}_{G}
for some graph
{G}
which we construct by splitting the weight of each hyperedge
e\in{E}_{H}
between the edges in
{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)\times{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
.
Similar to the case for graphs and (
4.1
), for any

4.2.1
Computing the minimum eigenvector of

The second statement of Theorem
4.1
answers an open question posed by Yoshida
[
Yos19
]
by showing that our constructed hypergraph operator satisfies a Cheeger-type inequality for hypergraph bipartiteness.
However, the following theorem shows that there is no polynomial-time algorithm to compute the minimum eigenvector of
any such operator
unless
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
.
This means that the problem we consider in this work is fundamentally more difficult than the equivalent problem for graphs, as well as the problem of finding a sparse cut in a hypergraph.

Theorem 4.2
.

Given any operator that satisfies a Cheeger-type inequality for hypergraph bipartiteness, there’s no polynomial-time algorithm that computes a multiplicative-factor approximation of the minimum eigenvalue or eigenvector unless
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
.

Proof.

Our proof is based on considering the following
Max Set Splitting
problem: For any given hypergraph, the problem looks for a partition
{L},{R}
with
{L}\cup{R}={V}_{H}
and
{L}\cap{R}=\emptyset
, such that it holds for any
e\in{E}_{H}
that
e\cap{L}\neq\emptyset
and
e\cap{R}\neq\emptyset
. This problem is known to be
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
[
GJ79
]
.
This is also referred to as
Hypergraph 2-Colorability
and we can consider the problem of coloring every vertex in the hypergraph either red or blue such that every edge contains at least one vertex of each color.

We will assume that there is some operator
{\mathbf{J}}
which satisfies the Cheeger-type inequality

\frac{\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{J}})}{2}\leq{\beta}_{H}\leq\sqrt{2\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{J}})}

(4.3)

and that there is an algorithm which can compute the minimum eigen-pair of the operator in polynomial time.
We will show that this would allow us to solve the
Max Set Splitting
problem in polynomial time.

Given a 2-colorable hypergraph
{H}
with coloring
({L},{R})
, we will use the eigenvector of the operator
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
to find a valid coloring.
By definition, we have that
{\beta}({L},{R})=0
and
\gamma_{1}=0
by (
4.3
).
Furthermore, by the second statement of Theorem
4.1
, we can compute disjoint sets
{L}^{\prime},{R}^{\prime}
such that
{\beta}({L}^{\prime},{R}^{\prime})=0
. Note that in general
{L}^{\prime}
and
{R}^{\prime}
will be different from
{L}
and
{R}
.

Now, let
{E}^{\prime}=\{e\in{E}_{H}:e\cap({L}^{\prime}\cup{R}^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\}
.
Then, by the definition of bipartiteness, for all
e\in{E}^{\prime}
we have
e\cap{L}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
and
e\cap{R}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset

Therefore, if we color every vertex in

blue and every vertex in

It remains to color the vertices in

The hypergraph

This algorithm will run in polynomial time since there are at most

4.3
Dealing with the diffusion continuity condition

It remains for us to discuss the diffusion continuity condition, which guarantees that
{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
and
{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)

will not change in infinitesimal time and the diffusion process
will eventually converge to some stable distribution.
Formally, let
{\bm{f}}_{t}
be the normalised measure on the vertices of
{H}
,
and let

{\bm{r}}\triangleq\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{f}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}

be the derivative of
{\bm{f}}_{t}
, which describes the rate of change for every vertex at the current time
t
.
We write
{\bm{r}}(v)
for any
v\in{V}_{H}
as

{\bm{r}}(v)=\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{\bm{r}}_{e}(v)
,
where
{\bm{r}}_{e}(v)
is the contribution of edge
e
towards the rate of change of
{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)
.
Now we discuss three rules that we expect the diffusion process to satisfy, and later prove that these three rules uniquely define the rate of change
{\bm{r}}
.

First of all, as we mentioned in Section
4.2
,
we expect that only the vertices in
{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)\cup{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
will participate in the diffusion process,
i.e.,
{\bm{r}}_{e}(u)=0
unless
u\in{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)\cup{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
. Moreover, any vertex
u
participating in the diffusion process must satisfy the following:

•

Rule (0a): if
\left\lvert{\bm{r}}_{e}(u)\right\rvert>0
and
u\in{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
, then
{\bm{r}}(u)=\max_{v\in{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)}\{{\bm{r}}(v)\}
.

•

Rule (0b): if
\left\lvert{\bm{r}}_{e}(u)\right\rvert>0
and
u\in{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
, then
{\bm{r}}(u)=\min_{v\in{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)}\{{\bm{r}}(v)\}
.

To explain Rule (0), notice that for an infinitesimal time,
{\bm{f}}_{t}(u)
will be increased according to
\left(\mathrm{d}{\bm{f}}_{t}/\mathrm{d}t\right)(u)={\bm{r}}(u)
. Hence, by Rule (0) we know that, if
u\in{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
(resp.
u\in{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
) participates in the diffusion process in edge
e
, then in an infinitesimal time
{\bm{f}}(u)
will
remain the maximum (resp. minimum) among the vertices in
e
.
Such a rule is necessary to ensure that the vertices involved in the diffusion in edge
e
do not change in infinitesimal time, and the diffusion process is able to continue.

Our next rule states that the total rate of change of the measure due to edge
e
is equal to
-{w}(e)\cdot{\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
:

•

Rule (1):
\sum_{v\in{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)}{d}(v){\bm{r}}_{e}(v)=\sum_{v\in{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)}{d}(v){\bm{r}}_{e}(v)=-{w}(e)\cdot{\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
for all
e\in{E}_{H}
.

This rule is a generalisation from the operator
{\mathbf{J}}_{G}
in graphs.
In particular, since
{\mathbf{D}}_{G}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{G}{\bm{f}}_{t}(u)=\sum_{\{u,v\}\in{E}_{G}}{w}_{G}(u,v)({\bm{f}}_{t}(u)+{\bm{f}}_{t}(v))/{d}_{G}(u)
, the rate of change of
{\bm{f}}_{t}(u)
due to the edge
\{u,v\}\in{E}_{G}
is
-{w}_{G}(u,v)({\bm{f}}_{t}(u)+{\bm{f}}_{t}(v))/{d}_{G}(u)
.
Rule (1) states that in the hypergraph case the rate of change of the vertices in
{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
and
{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
together behave like the rate of change of
u
and
v
in the graph case.

One might have expected that Rules (0) and (1) together would define a unique process. Unfortunately, this isn’t the case.
For example, let us define an unweighted hypergraph
{H}=({V}_{H},{E}_{H})
, where
{V}_{H}=\{u,v,w\}
and
{E}_{H}=\{\{u,v,w\}\}
. By setting the measure to be
{\bm{f}}_{t}=(1,1,-2)^{\intercal}
and
e=\{u,v,w\}
, we have
{\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)=-1
, and
{\bm{r}}(u)+{\bm{r}}(v)={\bm{r}}(w)=1
by Rule (1). In such a scenario, either
\{u,w\}
or
\{v,w\}
can participate in the diffusion and satisfy Rule (0), which makes the process not uniquely defined.
To overcome this, we introduce the following stronger rule to replace Rule (0):

•

Rule (2a): Assume that
\left\lvert{\bm{r}}_{e}(u)\right\rvert>0
and
u\in{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
.

–

If
{\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)>0
, then
{\bm{r}}(u)=\max_{v\in{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e))}\{{\bm{r}}(v)\}
;

–

If
{\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)<0
, then
{\bm{r}}(u)={\bm{r}}(v)
for all
v\in{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
.

•

Rule (2b): Assume that
\left\lvert{\bm{r}}_{e}(u)\right\rvert>0
and
u\in{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
:

–

If
{\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)<0
, then
{\bm{r}}(u)=\min_{v\in{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e))}\{{\bm{r}}(v)\}
;

–

If
{\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)>0
, then
{\bm{r}}(u)={\bm{r}}(v)
for all
v\in{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
.

Notice that the first conditions of Rules (2a) and (2b) correspond to Rules (0a) and (0b) respectively; the second conditions are introduced for purely technical reasons: they state that, if the discrepancy of
e
is negative (resp. positive), then all the vertices
u\in{S}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
(resp.
u\in{I}_{{\bm{f}}}(e)
) will have the same value of
{\bm{r}}(u)
.
Lemma
4.1
shows that there is a unique
{\bm{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that satisfies Rules (1) and (2), and
{\bm{r}}
can be computed in polynomial time.
Therefore, our two rules uniquely define a diffusion process, and we can use the computed
{\bm{r}}
to simulate the continuous diffusion process with a discretised version.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that

Proof.

Let

The proof is by case distinction. We first look at the case in which all the vertices in

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{x}}}(e)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{{\bm{x}}^{\star}(e)-\alpha}{1-\alpha\mathrm{vol}({P})}&\mbox{if }{\bm{x}}^{\star}(e)\geq 0\\

and

which implies that

{\bm{x}}^{\star}=\left(1-\alpha\mathrm{vol}({P})\right)\widehat{{\bm{x}}}+\alpha\cdot{\bm{1}}_{P}=\left(1-\alpha\mathrm{vol}({P})\right)\widehat{{\bm{x}}}+\alpha\cdot\mathrm{vol}({P})\cdot{\bm{x}}_{P},

and

\mathrm{cut}({X})=\mathrm{cut}(\{s\})-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{{T}^{\prime}}^{+}}c(e)+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{{T}^{\prime}}^{-}}c(e)+\sum_{v\in{T}^{\prime}}{d}(v)\delta({T}).

Since
{T}
maximises the objective function
\delta
, we have

\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{{T}^{\prime}}^{+}}c(e)-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{{T}^{\prime}}^{-}}c(e)=\mathrm{vol}({T}^{\prime})\delta({T}^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{vol}({T}^{\prime})\delta({T})=\sum_{v\in{T}^{\prime}}{d}(v)\delta({T})

and can conclude that
\mathrm{cut}({X})\geq\mathrm{cut}(\{s\})
which completes the proof.
∎

We can now combine the results in Lemmas
4.5
and
4.6
to prove Lemma
4.1
.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
4.6
together imply that there is a unique vector
{\bm{r}}
and corresponding
\{{\bm{r}}_{e}(v)\}_{e\in{E}_{H},v\in{V}_{H}}
which satisfies Rules (1) and (2).
Lemma
4.5
further shows that Algorithm
2
computes this vector
{\bm{r}}
, and the proof of Lemma
4.6
gives a polynomial-time method for computing the
\{{\bm{r}}_{e}(v)\}
values by solving a sequence of max-flow problems.
∎

In this section we will
analyse the
FindBipartiteComponents
algorithm and
prove Theorem
4.1
.
This section is split into two subsections which correspond to the two statements in Theorem
4.1
.
First, we show that the diffusion process converges to an eigenvector of
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
.
We then show that this allows us to find sets
{L},{R}\subset{V}_{H}
with low hypergraph bipartiteness.

5.1
Convergence of the diffusion process

We show in this section that the diffusion process determined by the operator
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
converges in polynomial time to an eigenvector of
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there is some
t=O\!\left(1/\epsilon^{3}\right)
such that for any starting vector
{\bm{f}}_{0}
, there is an interval
[c,c+2\epsilon]
such that

\frac{1}{\left\|{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}}\sum_{{\bm{u}}_{i}:\lambda_{i}\in[c,c+2\epsilon]}\langle{\bm{f}}_{t},{\bm{u}}_{i}\rangle_{w}\geq 1-\epsilon

where
({\bm{u}}_{i},\lambda_{i})
are the eigen-pairs of
{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{t}={\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}({\mathbf{D}}_{{G}_{t}}+{\mathbf{A}}_{{G}_{t}})
and
{G}_{t}
is the graph constructed to represent the diffusion operator
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
at time
t
.

By taking
\epsilon
to be some small constant, this shows that the vector
{\bm{f}}_{t}
converges to an eigenvector of the hypergraph operator in polynomial time.

Proof.

We will prove this by showing that the Rayleigh quotient
R_{{\mathbf{J}}_{H}}({\bm{f}}_{t})
is always decreasing at a rate of at least
\epsilon^{3}
whenever the conclusion of Theorem
5.1
does not hold. Since
R_{{\mathbf{J}}_{H}}({\bm{f}}_{t})
can only decrease by a constant amount, the theorem will follow.

First, we derive an expression for the rate of change of the Rayleigh quotient
R_{{\mathbf{J}}_{H}}({\bm{f}}_{t})
.
Let
{\bm{x}}_{t}\triangleq{\mathbf{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}
, and let
{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}={\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{H}({\mathbf{D}}_{{G}_{t}}+{\mathbf{A}}_{{G}_{t}}){\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{H}
. Then, we have

R_{{\mathbf{J}}_{H}}({\bm{f}}_{t})=\frac{{\bm{f}}_{t}^{\intercal}({\mathbf{D}}_{{G}_{t}}+{\mathbf{A}}_{{G}_{t}}){\bm{f}}_{t}}{{\bm{f}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}}=\frac{{\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}{\bm{x}}_{t}}{{\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\bm{x}}_{t}}.

For every eigenvector
{\bm{u}}_{j}
of
{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
, the vector
{\bm{v}}_{j}={\mathbf{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{H}{\bm{u}}_{j}
is an eigenvector of
{\mathbf{Z}}
with the same eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
.
Since
{\mathbf{Z}}
is symmetric, the vectors
{\bm{v}}_{1}
to
{\bm{v}}_{n}
are orthogonal.
Additionally, notice that

\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{v}}_{j}\rangle={\bm{f}}^{\intercal}_{t}{\mathbf{D}}_{H}{\bm{u}}_{j}=\langle{\bm{f}}_{t},{\bm{u}}_{j}\rangle_{w}

and we define
\alpha_{j}=\langle{\bm{f}}_{t},{\bm{u}}_{j}\rangle_{w}
so we can write
{\bm{x}}_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}{\bm{v}}_{j}
and
{\bm{f}}_{t}={\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{H}{\bm{x}}_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}{\bm{u}}_{j}
.
Now, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{\bm{x}}_{t},{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle+\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=-{\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}^{2}{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}^{2}{\bm{x}}_{t}

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}.

Additionally, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\bm{x}}_{t}=-{\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}{\bm{x}}_{t}=-2{\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}{\bm{x}}_{t}.

Recalling that
{\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\bm{x}}_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}
, this gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}R({\bm{f}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{({\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\bm{x}}_{t})^{2}}\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}{\bm{x}}_{t})\right)({\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\bm{x}}_{t})-\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\bm{x}}_{t})\right)({\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}{\bm{x}}_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\bm{x}}_{t}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}{\bm{x}}_{t})\right)+2R({\bm{f}}_{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=2\left[R({\bm{f}}_{t})^{2}-\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}\right]

(5.1)

We will now show that at any time
t
, if the conclusion of the theorem does not hold, then

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}R({\bm{f}}_{t})\leq-\epsilon^{3}.

(5.2)

Assuming that this holds, and using the fact that for all
t
it is the case that
0\leq R({\bm{f}}_{t})\leq 2
, when
t=2/\epsilon^{3}
, either
R({\bm{f}}_{t})=0
or there was some
t^{\prime}<t
when
(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t^{\prime})R({\bm{f}}_{t^{\prime}})>-\epsilon^{3}
and the conclusion of the theorem holds.

Now, to show (
5.2
), consider the partial derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}2\left[R({\bm{f}}_{t})^{2}-\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\left[\frac{2\alpha_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}R({\bm{f}}_{t})-\frac{2\alpha_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\lambda_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{4\alpha_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}(R({\bm{f}}_{t})-\lambda_{i}),

(5.3)

where we use the fact that
R({\bm{f}}_{t})=(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}\lambda_{j})/(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2})
.
Notice that
the derivative in (
5.3
) is greater than
0

if
\lambda_{i}<R({\bm{f}}_{t})

and
the derivative is less than
0

if
\lambda_{i}>R({\bm{f}}_{t})
.
This means that in order to establish an upper-bound for
(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t)R({\bm{f}}_{t})
, we can assume that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
are as close to the value of
R({\bm{f}}_{t})
as possible.

Now, we assume that at time
t
, the conclusion of the theorem does not hold. Then, one of the following cases must hold:

1.

(\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}>R({\bm{f}}_{t})+\epsilon}\alpha_{j}^{2})/(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2})>\epsilon/2
;

2.

(\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}<R({\bm{f}}_{t})-\epsilon}\alpha_{j}^{2})/(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2})>\epsilon/2
.

Suppose the first case holds.
By the conclusions we draw from (
5.3
), we can assume that there is an eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}=R({\bm{f}}_{t})+\epsilon
such that
\alpha_{i}^{2}/(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2})=\epsilon/2
and that
(\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}<R({\bm{f}}_{t})}\alpha_{j}^{2})/(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2})=1-\epsilon/2
.
Then, since
R({\bm{f}}_{t})=(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}\lambda_{j})/(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}<R({\bm{f}}_{t})}\alpha_{j}^{2}\lambda_{j}=R({\bm{f}}_{t})-\frac{\epsilon}{2}(R({\bm{f}}_{t})+\epsilon),

which is equivalent to

\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}<R({\bm{f}}_{t})}\alpha_{j}^{2}(\lambda_{j}-R({\bm{f}}_{t}))=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\cdot R({\bm{f}}_{t})-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\cdot(R({\bm{f}}_{t})+\epsilon)=-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}.

(5.4)

Now, notice that for any
\lambda_{j}<R({\bm{f}}_{t})
we have

\displaystyle(\lambda_{j}^{2}-R({\bm{f}}_{t})^{2})

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{j}+R({\bm{f}}_{t}))(\lambda_{j}-R({\bm{f}}_{t}))

\displaystyle\geq 2R({\bm{f}}_{t})(\lambda_{j}-R({\bm{f}}_{t})),

since
\lambda_{j}-R({\bm{f}}_{t})<0
.
As such, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}R({\bm{f}}_{t})

\displaystyle=2\left[R({\bm{f}}_{t})^{2}-\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}(\lambda_{j}^{2}-R({\bm{f}}_{t})^{2})

\displaystyle=-\epsilon\left((R({\bm{f}}_{t})+\epsilon)^{2}-R({\bm{f}}_{t})^{2}\right)-\frac{2}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}<R({\bm{f}}_{t})}\alpha_{j}^{2}(\lambda_{j}^{2}-R({\bm{f}}_{t})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq-\epsilon\left(2\epsilon R({\bm{f}}_{t})+\epsilon^{2}\right)-\frac{2}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}<R({\bm{f}}_{t})}2\alpha_{j}^{2}R({\bm{f}}_{t})(\lambda_{j}-R({\bm{f}}_{t}))

\displaystyle=-2\epsilon^{2}R({\bm{f}}_{t})-\epsilon^{3}+2\epsilon^{2}R({\bm{f}}_{t})

\displaystyle=-\epsilon^{3}

where the fifth line follows by (
5.4
).
We now consider the second case.
We can assume that there is an eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}=R({\bm{f}}_{t})-\epsilon
such that
\alpha_{i}^{2}/(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2})=\epsilon/2
and that
(\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}>R({\bm{f}}_{t})}\alpha_{j}^{2})/(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2})=1-\epsilon/2
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}>R({\bm{f}}_{t})}\alpha_{j}^{2}\lambda_{j}=R({\bm{f}}_{t})-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\left(R({\bm{f}}_{t})-\epsilon\right),

which is equivalent to

\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}>R({\bm{f}}_{t})}\alpha_{j}^{2}(\lambda_{j}-R({\bm{f}}_{t}))=\frac{\epsilon}{2}R({\bm{f}}_{t})-\frac{\epsilon}{2}(R({\bm{f}}_{t})-\epsilon)=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}.

(5.5)

Now, notice that for any
\lambda_{j}>R({\bm{f}}_{t})
we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}^{2}-R({\bm{f}}_{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{j}+R({\bm{f}}_{t}))(\lambda_{j}-R({\bm{f}}_{t}))

\displaystyle\geq 2R({\bm{f}}_{t})\cdot(\lambda_{j}-R({\bm{f}}_{t})).

As such, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}R({\bm{f}}_{t})

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}(\lambda_{j}^{2}-R({\bm{f}}_{t})^{2})

\displaystyle=-\epsilon\left((R({\bm{f}}_{t})-\epsilon)^{2}-R({\bm{f}}_{t})^{2}\right)-\frac{2}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}>R({\bm{f}}_{t})}\alpha_{j}^{2}(\lambda_{j}^{2}-R({\bm{f}}_{t})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq-\epsilon\left(\epsilon^{2}-2\epsilon R({\bm{f}}_{t})\right)-\frac{2}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}<R({\bm{f}}_{t})}2\alpha_{j}^{2}R({\bm{f}}_{t})(\lambda_{j}-R({\bm{f}}_{t}))

\displaystyle=2\epsilon^{2}g(t)-\epsilon^{3}-2\epsilon^{2}R({\bm{f}}_{t})

\displaystyle=-\epsilon^{3}

where the fourth line follows by (
5.5
).
These two cases establish (
5.2
) and complete the proof of the theorem.
∎

The eigenvector is at most the minimum eigenvector of the clique graph.

We now show that the eigenvector to which the algorithm converges is at most the minimum eigenvector of
{\mathbf{J}}_{G}
where
{G}
is the clique reduction of
{H}
.
We start by showing the following technical lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any hypergraph
{H}=({V}_{H},{E}_{H},{w})
, vector
{\bm{f}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and edge
e\in{E}_{H}
, it holds that

\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{u,v\in e}\frac{1}{\mathrm{rank}(e)-1}({\bm{f}}(u)+{\bm{f}}(v))^{2}.

The equality holds iff there is exactly one vertex
v\in e
with
{\bm{f}}(v)\neq 0
or
\mathrm{rank}(e)=2
.

Proof.

We will consider some ordering of the vertices in
e
,

u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{\mathrm{rank}(e)},

such that
u_{1}=\argmax_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)
and
u_{2}=\argmin_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)
and the remaining vertices are ordered arbitrarily.
Then, for any
2\leq k\leq\mathrm{rank}(e)
, we define

C_{k}=\sum_{u,v\in\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}}\frac{1}{k-1}({\bm{f}}(u)+{\bm{f}}(v))^{2}

and we will show by induction on
k
that

C_{k}\geq\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)^{2}

(5.6)

for all
2\leq k\leq\mathrm{rank}(e)
with equality iff
k=2
or there is exactly one vertex
u_{i}\in e
with
{\bm{f}}(v)\neq 0
.
The lemma follows by setting
k=\mathrm{rank}(e)
.

The base case when
k=2
follows trivially by the definitions and the choice of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
.

For the inductive step, we assume that (
5.6
) holds for some
k
and will show that it holds for
k+1
.
We have that

C_{k+1}=\sum_{u,v\in\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k+1}\}}\frac{1}{k}\cdot({\bm{f}}(u)+{\bm{f}}(v))^{2},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle C_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}({\bm{f}}(u_{i})+{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1}))^{2}+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{u,v\in\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}}({\bm{f}}(u)+{\bm{f}}(v))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}({\bm{f}}(u_{i})+{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1}))^{2}+\frac{k-1}{k}C_{k}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}({\bm{f}}(u_{1})+{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1}))^{2}

where the final inequality holds by the induction hypothesis.
Therefore, it is sufficient to show that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left({\bm{f}}(u_{i})+{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\right)^{2}\geq\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)^{2}.

We will instead show the stronger fact that

\left(\max_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)+{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\right)^{2}+\left(\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)+{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\right)^{2}\geq\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)^{2}.

(5.7)

The proof is by case distinction.
The first case is when
\mathrm{sign}(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(v))=\mathrm{sign}(\min_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u))
.
Assume w.l.o.g. that the sign is positive.
Then, since
{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\geq\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)
, we have

\left(\max_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)+{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\right)^{2}\geq\left(\max_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)+\min_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)^{2}

and (
5.7
) holds.
Moreover, the inequality is strict if
\left\lvert\min_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right\rvert>0
or
\left\lvert{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\right\rvert>0
.

For the second case, we assume that
\mathrm{sign}(\min_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u))\neq\mathrm{sign}(\max_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v))
.
Expanding (
5.7
), we would like to show

\displaystyle\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right)^{2}+\left(\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)^{2}+2{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right)+2{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\left(\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)+2{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right)^{2}+\left(\min_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right)^{2}-2\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right)\left\lvert\min_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right\rvert,

which is equivalent to

2{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})^{2}+2{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right)+2{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\left(\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)\geq-2\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right)\left\lvert\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right\rvert.

Notice that exactly one of the terms on the left hand side is negative.
Recalling that
\min_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\leq{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\leq\max_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)
, it is clear that

•

if
{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})<0
, then
-2\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right)\left\lvert\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right\rvert\leq 2{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\left(\max_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)\leq 0
and the inequality holds.

•

if
{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\geq 0
, then
-2\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right)\left\lvert\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right\rvert\leq 2{\bm{f}}(u_{k+1})\left(\min_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right)\leq 0
and the inequality holds.

Moreover, in both cases the inequality is strict if
-2\left(\max_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)\left\lvert\min_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right\rvert<0
or
\left\lvert{\bm{f}}(u_{k_{1}})\right\rvert>0
.
∎

Now, we can show that we always find an eigenvector which is at most the minimum eigenvector of the clique reduction.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any hypergraph
{H}=({V}_{H},{E}_{H},{w})
with clique reduction
{G}
, if
{\bm{f}}
is the eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{D}}_{G}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{G})
, then

\frac{{\bm{f}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}}{{\bm{f}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{H}{\bm{f}}}\leq\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{D}}_{G}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{G})

and the inequality is strict if
\min_{e\in{E}_{H}}\mathrm{rank}(e)>2
.

Proof.

Since
\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{D}}_{G}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{G})=({\bm{f}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{J}}_{G}{\bm{f}})/({\bm{f}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{G}{\bm{f}})
and
({\bm{f}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{G}{\bm{f}})=({\bm{f}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{H}{\bm{f}})
by the construction of the clique graph, it suffices to show that

{\bm{f}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}\leq{\bm{f}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{J}}_{G}{\bm{f}},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\left(\max_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)+\min_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(u,v)\in{E}_{G}}{w}_{G}(u,v)({\bm{f}}(u)+{\bm{f}}(v))^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\sum_{u,v\in e}\frac{1}{\mathrm{rank}(e)-1}({\bm{f}}(u)+{\bm{f}}(v))^{2}

which holds by Lemma
5.1
.

Furthermore, if
\min_{e\in{E}_{H}}\mathrm{rank}(e)>2
, then by Lemma
5.1
the inequality is strict unless every edge
e\in{E}_{H}
contains at most one
v\in e
with
{\bm{f}}(v)\neq 0
.
Suppose the inequality is not strict, then it must be that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{D}}_{G}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{G})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{(u,v)\in{E}_{G}}{w}_{G}(u,v)({\bm{f}}(v)+{\bm{f}}(u))^{2}}{\sum_{v\in{V}_{G}}{d}_{G}(v){\bm{f}}(v)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{v\in{V}_{G}}{d}_{G}(v){\bm{f}}(v)^{2}}{\sum_{v\in{V}_{G}}{d}_{G}(v){\bm{f}}(v)^{2}}

\displaystyle=1,

since for every edge
(u,v)\in{E}_{G}
, at most one of
{\bm{f}}(u)
or
{\bm{f}}(v)
is not equal to
0
.
This cannot be the case, since it is a well known fact that the maximum eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}({\mathbf{D}}_{G}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{G})=2
and so
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{D}}_{G}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{G})\geq(n-1)+2=n+1
which contradicts the fact that the trace
\mathrm{tr}({\mathbf{D}}_{G}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{G})
is equal to
n
. This proves the final statement of the lemma.
∎

5.2
Cheeger-type inequality for hypergraph bipartiteness

Before proving Lemma
4.1
, we will prove some intermediate facts about the new hypergraph operator
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
which will allow us to show that the operator
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
has a well-defined minimum eigenvector.
Given a hypergraph
{H}=({V}_{H},{E}_{H},{w})
, for any edge
e\in{E}_{H}
and weighted measure vector
{\bm{f}}_{t}
, let
r_{e}^{{S}}\triangleq\max_{v\in{S}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)}\{{\bm{r}}(v)\}
and
r_{e}^{{I}}\triangleq\min_{v\in{I}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)}\{{\bm{r}}(v)\}
, and recall that
c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)={w}(e)\left\lvert{\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\right\rvert
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Given a hypergraph
{H}=({V}_{H},{E}_{H},{w})
and normalised measure vector
{\bm{f}}_{t}
, let

{\bm{r}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{f}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}.

Then, it holds that

\left\|{\bm{r}}\right\|_{w}^{2}=-\sum_{e\in{E}}{w}(e){\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\left(r_{e}^{{S}}+r_{e}^{{I}}\right).

Proof.

Let
{P}\subset{V}
be one of the densest vertex sets defined with respect to the solution of the linear program (
4.6
). By the description of Algorithm
2
, we have
{\bm{r}}(u)=\delta({P})
for every
u\in{P}
, and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in{P}}{d}(u){\bm{r}}(u)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{vol}({P})\cdot\delta({P})^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}\left(\mathcal{S}_{{U},{P}}^{+}\right)+c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}\left(\mathcal{I}_{{U},{P}}^{+}\right)-c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}\left(\mathcal{S}_{{U},{P}}^{-}\right)-c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}\left(\mathcal{I}_{{U},{P}}^{-}\right)\right)\cdot\delta({P})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{S}_{{U},{P}}^{+}}c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{I}_{{U},{P}}^{+}}c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{S}_{{U},{P}}^{-}}c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{I}_{{U},{P}}^{-}}c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\right)\cdot\delta({P})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{S}_{{U},{P}}^{+}}c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\cdot r_{e}^{{S}}+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{I}_{{U},{P}}^{+}}c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\cdot r_{e}^{{I}}-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{S}_{{U},{P}}^{-}}c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\cdot r_{e}^{{S}}-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{I}_{{U},{P}}^{-}}c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\cdot r_{e}^{{I}}.

Since each vertex is included in exactly one set
{P}
and each edge will appear either in one each of
\mathcal{S}_{{U},{P}}^{+}
and
\mathcal{I}_{{U},{P}}^{+}
or in one each of
\mathcal{S}_{{U},{P}}^{-}
and
\mathcal{I}_{{U},{P}}^{-}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|{\bm{r}}\|^{2}_{w}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in{V}}{d}(v){\bm{r}}(v)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{P}}\sum_{v\in{P}}{d}(v){\bm{r}}(v)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{P}}\left(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{S}_{{U},{P}}^{+}}c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\cdot r_{e}^{S}+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{I}_{{U},{P}}^{+}}c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\cdot r_{e}^{{I}}-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{S}_{{U},{P}}^{-}}c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\cdot r_{e}^{S}-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{I}_{{U},{P}}^{-}}c_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\cdot r_{e}^{{I}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{e\in{E}}{w}(e){\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\left(r_{e}^{S}+r_{e}^{{I}}\right),

which proves the lemma.
∎

Next, we define
\gamma_{1}=\min_{\bm{f}}D({\bm{f}})
and show that any vector
{\bm{f}}
that satisfies
\gamma_{1}=D({\bm{f}})
is an eigenvector of
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
with eigenvalue
\gamma_{1}
.
We start by showing that the Raleigh quotient of the new operator is equivalent to the discrepancy ratio of the hypergraph.

Lemma 5.4
.

For any hypergraph
{H}
and vector
{\bm{f}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it holds that
D({\bm{f}}_{t})=R_{{\mathbf{J}}_{H}}({\bm{f}}_{t})
.

Proof.

Since
{\bm{f}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}=\sum_{v\in{V}}{d}(v){\bm{f}}_{t}(v)^{2}
, it is sufficient to show that

{\bm{f}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}=\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)\right)^{2}.

Recall that for some graph
{G}_{t}
,
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}={\mathbf{D}}_{{G}_{t}}+{\mathbf{A}}_{{G}_{t}}
.
Then, we have that

\displaystyle{\bm{f}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}

\displaystyle={\bm{f}}_{t}^{\intercal}({\mathbf{D}}_{{G}_{t}}+{\mathbf{A}}_{{G}_{t}}){\bm{f}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(u,v)\in{E}_{G}}{w}_{G}(u,v)({\bm{f}}_{t}(u)+{\bm{f}}_{t}(v))^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}\left(\sum_{(u,v)\in{S}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\times{I}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)}{w}_{{G}_{t}}(u,v)({\bm{f}}_{t}(u)+{\bm{f}}_{t}(v))^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)\right)^{2},

which follows since the graph
{G}_{t}
is constructed by splitting the weight of each hyperedge
e\in{E}_{H}
between the edges
{S}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\times{I}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

For a hypergraph
{H}
, operator
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
, and vector
{\bm{f}}_{t}
, the following statements hold:

1.

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{2}=-2{\bm{f}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}
;

2.

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}({\bm{f}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t})=-2\left\|{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{2}
;

3.

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}R({\bm{f}}_{t})\leq 0
with equality if and only if
{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\in\mathrm{span}({\bm{f}}_{t})
.

Proof.

By definition, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\left\|{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sum_{v\in{V}}{d}(v){\bm{f}}_{t}(v)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in{V}}{d}(v)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in{V}}{d}(v)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)^{2}}{\mathrm{d}{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)}\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{v\in{V}}{d}(v){\bm{f}}_{t}(v)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=2\langle{\bm{f}}_{t},\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{f}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\rangle_{w}=-2\langle{\bm{f}}_{t},{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\rangle_{w},

which proves the first statement.

For the second statement, by Lemma
5.4
we have

{\bm{f}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}=\sum_{e\in{E}}{w}(e)\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)\right)^{2},

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{\bm{f}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sum_{e\in{E}}{w}(e)\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{e\in{E}}{w}(e){\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}_{t}(v)\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{e\in{E}}{w}(e){\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\cdot\left(r_{e}^{{S}}+r_{e}^{{I}}\right),

(5.8)

where the last equality holds by the way that all the vertices receive their
{\bm{r}}
-values by the algorithm and the definitions of
r_{e}^{{S}}
and
r_{e}^{{I}}
. On the other side,
by definition
{\bm{r}}=-{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}
and so by Lemma
5.3
,

\left\|{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{2}=\left\|{\bm{r}}\right\|_{w}^{2}=-\sum_{e\in{E}}{w}(e){\Delta}_{{\bm{f}}_{t}}(e)\left(r_{e}^{{S}}+r_{e}^{{I}}\right).

(5.9)

By combining (
5.8
) with (
5.9
), we have the second statement.

For the third statement, notice that we can write
{\bm{f}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}
as
\langle{\bm{f}}_{t},{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\rangle_{w}
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\langle{\bm{f}}_{t},{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\rangle_{w}}{\left\|{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left\|{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}~\langle{\bm{f}}_{t},{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\rangle_{w}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\langle{\bm{f}}_{t},{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\rangle_{w}\cdot\frac{1}{\left\|{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{4}}\frac{\mathrm{d}~\left\|{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|^{2}_{w}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\left\|{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{4}}\cdot\left(2\cdot\left\|{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{2}\cdot\left\|{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{2}+\left\langle{\bm{f}}_{t},{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\rangle_{w}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}~\left\|{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|^{2}_{w}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\left\|{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{4}}\cdot\left(\left\|{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{2}\cdot\left\|{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\|_{w}^{2}-\left\langle{\bm{f}}_{t},{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\right\rangle_{w}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 0,

where the last inequality holds by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{w}
with the equality if and only if
{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{t}\in\mathrm{span}({\bm{f}}_{t})
.
∎

This allows us to establish the following key fact.

Lemma 5.6
.

For any hypergraph
{H}
,
\gamma_{1}=\min_{{\bm{f}}}D({\bm{f}})
is an eigenvalue of
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
and any minimiser
{\bm{f}}
is its corresponding eigenvector.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.4
, it holds that
R({\bm{f}})=D({\bm{f}})
for any
{\bm{f}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. When
{\bm{f}}
is a minimiser of
D({\bm{f}})
, it must hold that

\frac{\mathrm{d}R({\bm{f}})}{\mathrm{d}t}=0,

which implies by Lemma
5.5
that
{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}\in\mathrm{span}({\bm{f}})
and proves that
{\bm{f}}
is an eigenvector.
∎

We are now able to prove a Cheeger-type inequality for our operator and the hypergraph bipartiteness.
We first show that the minimum eigenvalue of
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
is at most twice the hypergraph bipartiteness.

Lemma 5.7
.

Given a hypergraph
{H}=({V}_{H},{E}_{H})
with sets
{L},{R}\subset{V}_{H}
such that
{\beta}({L},{R})={\beta}
, it is the case that

\gamma_{1}\leq 2{\beta}

where
\gamma_{1}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
.

Proof.

Let
{\bm{\chi}}_{{L},{R}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n}
be the indicator vector of the cut
{L},{R}
such that

{\bm{\chi}}_{{L},{R}}(u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}1&\mbox{if }u\in{L}\\
-1&\mbox{if }u\in{R}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

Then, by Lemma
5.4
, the Rayleigh quotient is given by

\displaystyle R_{{\mathbf{J}}_{H}}\left({\bm{\chi}}_{{L},{R}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{e\in{E}}{w}(e)\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{\chi}}_{{L},{R}}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{\chi}}_{{L},{R}}(v)\right)^{2}}{\sum_{v\in{V}}{d}(v){\bm{\chi}}_{{L},{R}}(v)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4{w}({L}|\overline{{L}})+4{w}({R}|\overline{{R}})+{w}({L},\overline{{L}\cup{R}}|{R})+{w}({R},\overline{{L}\cup{R}}|{L})}{\mathrm{vol}({L}\cup{R})}

\displaystyle\leq 2{\beta}({L},{R}),

which completes the proof since
\gamma_{1}=\min_{{\bm{f}}}R_{{\mathbf{J}}_{H}}({\bm{f}})
by Lemma
5.6
.
∎

The proof of the other direction of the Cheeger-type inequality is more involved, and forms the basis of the second claim in Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 5.2
.

For any hypergraph
{H}=({V},{E},{w})
, let
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
be the operator defined with respect to
{H}
, and
\gamma_{1}=\min_{{\bm{f}}}D({\bm{f}})=\min_{{\bm{f}}}R({\bm{f}})
be the minimum eigenvalue of
{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
. Then, there are disjoint sets
{L},{R}\subset{V}
such that

{\beta}({L},{R})\leq\sqrt{2\gamma_{1}}.

Proof.

Let
{\bm{f}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the vector such that
D({\bm{f}})=\gamma_{1}
.
For any threshold
t\in[0,\max_{u}{\bm{f}}(u)^{2}]
, define
{\bm{x}}_{t}
such that

{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}1&\mbox{if }{\bm{f}}(u)\geq\sqrt{t}\\
-1&\mbox{if }{\bm{f}}(u)\leq-\sqrt{t}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

We will show that choosing
t\in\left[0,\max_{u}{\bm{f}}(u)^{2}\right]
uniformly at random gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert\right]\leq\sqrt{2\gamma_{1}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\in{V}_{H}}{d}(v)\left\lvert{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert\right].

(5.10)

Notice that every such
{\bm{x}}_{t}
defines disjoints vertex sets
{L}
and
{R}
that satisfy

{\beta}({L},{R})=\frac{\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert}{\sum_{v\in{V}_{H}}{d}(v)\left\lvert{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert}.

Hence, (
5.10
) would imply that there is some
{\bm{x}}_{t}
such that the disjoint
{L},{R}
defined by
{\bm{x}}_{t}
would satisfy

\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert\leq\sqrt{2\gamma_{1}}\cdot\sum_{v\in{V}_{H}}{d}(v)\left\lvert{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert,

which implies that

{\beta}({L},{R})\leq\sqrt{2\gamma_{1}}.

Hence, it suffices to prove (
5.10
).
We assume without loss of generality that
\max_{u}\left\{{\bm{f}}(u)^{2}\right\}=1
, so
t
is chosen uniformly from
[0,1]
.
First of all, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\in{V}_{H}}{d}(v)\left\lvert{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert\right]=\sum_{v\in{V}_{H}}{d}(v)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert\right]=\sum_{v\in{V}_{H}}{d}(v){\bm{f}}(v)^{2}.

To analyse the left-hand side of (
5.10
), we will focus on a particular edge
e\in{E}_{H}
. Let
a_{e}=\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)
and
b_{e}=\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)
.
We will drop the subscript
e
when it is clear from context.
We will show that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert\right]\leq\left\lvert a_{e}+b_{e}\right\rvert(\left\lvert a_{e}\right\rvert+\left\lvert b_{e}\right\rvert).

(5.11)

1.

Suppose
\mathrm{sign}(a_{e})=\mathrm{sign}(b_{e})
. Our analysis is by case distinction:

•

\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert=2
with probability
\min(a_{e}^{2},b_{e}^{2})
;

•

\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert=1
with probability
\left\lvert a_{e}^{2}-b_{e}^{2}\right\rvert
;

•

\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert=0
with probability
1-\max(a_{e}^{2},b_{e}^{2})
.

Assume without loss of generality that
a_{e}^{2}=\min\left(a_{e}^{2},b_{e}^{2}\right)
. Then, it holds hat

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}(v)\right\rvert\right]=2a_{e}^{2}+\left\lvert a_{e}^{2}-b_{e}^{2}\right\rvert=a_{e}^{2}+b_{e}^{2}\leq\left\lvert a_{e}+b_{e}\right\rvert(\left\lvert a_{e}\right\rvert+\left\lvert b_{e}\right\rvert).

2.

Suppose
\mathrm{sign}(a_{e})\neq\mathrm{sign}(b_{e})
. Our analysis is by case distinction:

•

\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert=2
with probability
0
;

•

\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert=1
with probability
\left\lvert a_{e}^{2}-b_{e}^{2}\right\rvert
;

•

\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert=0
with probability
\min(a_{e}^{2},b_{e}^{2})
.

Assume without loss of generality that
a_{e}^{2}=\min\left(a_{e}^{2},b_{e}^{2}\right)
. Then, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert\right]=\left\lvert a_{e}^{2}-b_{e}^{2}\right\rvert=(\left\lvert a_{e}\right\rvert-\left\lvert b_{e}\right\rvert)(\left\lvert a_{e}\right\rvert+\left\lvert b_{e}\right\rvert)=\left\lvert a_{e}+b_{e}\right\rvert(\left\lvert a_{e}\right\rvert+\left\lvert b_{e}\right\rvert),

where the final equality follows because
a_{e}
and
b_{e}
have different signs.

These two cases establish (
5.11
).
Now, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}_{t}(v)\right\rvert\right]\leq\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\left\lvert a_{e}+b_{e}\right\rvert(\left\lvert a_{e}\right\rvert+\left\lvert b_{e}\right\rvert)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\left\lvert a_{e}+b_{e}\right\rvert^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)(\left\lvert a_{e}\right\rvert+\left\lvert b_{e}\right\rvert)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)(\left\lvert a_{e}\right\rvert+\left\lvert b_{e}\right\rvert)^{2}}.

By our assumption that
{\bm{f}}
is the eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue
\gamma_{1}
, it holds that

\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\left(\max_{u\in e}{\bm{f}}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{f}}(v)\right)^{2}\leq\gamma_{1}\sum_{v\in{V}_{H}}{d}(v){\bm{f}}(v)^{2}.

On the other side, we have that

\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)(\left\lvert a_{e}\right\rvert+\left\lvert b_{e}\right\rvert)^{2}\leq 2\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)(\left\lvert a_{e}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert b_{e}\right\rvert^{2})\leq 2\sum_{v\in{V}_{H}}{d}(v){\bm{f}}(v)^{2}.

This gives us that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{e\in{E}_{H}}{w}(e)\left\lvert\max_{u\in e}{\bm{x}}(u)+\min_{v\in e}{\bm{x}}(v)\right\rvert\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\gamma_{1}}\sum_{v\in{V}_{H}}{d}(v){\bm{f}}(v)^{2}=\sqrt{2\gamma_{1}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\in{V}_{H}}{d}(v)\left\lvert{\bm{x}}(v)\right\rvert\right],

which proves the statement.
∎

Now, we are able to combine these results to prove Theorem
4.1
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

The first statement of the theorem follows by setting the starting vector
{\bm{f}}_{0}
of the diffusion to be the minimum eigenvector of the clique graph
{G}
. By Lemma
5.2
, we have that
R_{{\mathbf{J}}_{H}}({\bm{f}}_{0})\leq\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{D}}_{G}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{G})
and the inequality is strict if
\min_{e\in{E}_{H}}\mathrm{rank}(e)>2
.
Then, Theorem
5.1
shows that the diffusion process converges to an eigenvector and that the Rayleigh quotient only decreases during convergence, and so the inequality holds.
The algorithm runs in polynomial time since, by Theorem
5.1
, the diffusion process converges in polynomial time, and each step of Algorithm
1
can be computed in polynomial time using a standard algorithm for solving the linear programs.

The second statement is a restatement of Theorem
5.2
. The sweep set algorithm runs in polynomial time since there are
n
different sweep sets, and computing the hypergraph bipartiteness for each one also takes only polynomial time.
∎

{\mathbf{L}}_{{G}_{1}}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{2}{3}&-\frac{1}{3}&-\frac{1}{3}\\
-\frac{1}{3}&\frac{2}{3}&-\frac{1}{3}\\
-\frac{1}{3}&-\frac{1}{3}&\frac{2}{3}\end{bmatrix}

and we have

{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{L}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{1}={\mathbf{L}}_{{G}_{1}}{\bm{f}}_{1}=[0,0,0]^{\intercal}

which shows that
{\bm{f}}_{1}
is the trivial eigenvector of
{\mathbf{D}}_{{H}}^{-1}{\mathbf{L}}_{H}
with eigenvalue
0
.

Now, consider
{\bm{f}}_{2}=[1,-2,1]^{\intercal}
. In this case,
{G}_{2}
shown in Figure
5
is the graph constructed such that
{\mathbf{L}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{2}
is equivalent to
{\mathbf{L}}_{{G}_{2}}{\bm{f}}_{2}
.
Then, we have

{\mathbf{L}}_{{G}_{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&-\frac{1}{2}&0\\
-\frac{1}{2}&1&-\frac{1}{2}\\
0&-\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}

and

{\mathbf{D}}_{{H}}^{-1}{\mathbf{L}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{2}={\mathbf{L}}_{{G}_{2}}{\bm{f}}_{2}=\left[\frac{3}{2},-3,\frac{3}{2}\right]^{\intercal}

and so
{\bm{f}}_{2}
is an eigenvector of
{\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{L}}_{H}
with eigenvalue
3/2
.

Finally, we consider
{\bm{f}}_{3}=[1,-1,0]
and notice that the graph
{G}_{3}
is the constructed graph.
Then,

{\mathbf{L}}_{{G}_{3}}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&-\frac{1}{2}&0\\
-\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}

and

{\mathbf{D}}_{{H}}^{-1}{\mathbf{L}}_{H}{\bm{f}}_{3}={\mathbf{L}}_{{G}_{3}}{\bm{f}}_{3}=\left[2,-2,0\right]^{\intercal}

which shows that
{\bm{f}}_{3}
is an eigenvector of
{\mathbf{L}}_{H}
with eigenvalue
2
.

Through an exhaustive search of the other possible constructed graphs on
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
, we find that these are the only eigenvalues.
By the symmetries of
{\bm{f}}_{2}
and
{\bm{f}}_{3}
this means that the operator
{\mathbf{L}}_{H}
has a total of
7
different eigenvectors and
3
distinct eigenvalues.
It is useful to point out that, since the
{\mathbf{L}}_{H}
operator is non-linear, a linear combination of eigenvectors with the same eigenvalue is
not
, in general, an eigenvector.
Additionally, notice that the constructions of
{\bm{f}}_{1}
and
{\bm{f}}_{2}
generalise to hypergraphs with more than
3
vertices which shows that the number of eigenvectors of
{\mathbf{L}}_{H}
can grow faster than the number of vertices.
This example answers an open question in
[
CLTZ18
]
which showed that there are always two eigenvalues and asked whether there can be any more, although further investigation of the spectrum of this operator would be very interesting.

To study the spectrum of our new operator
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
, we construct a hypergraph
{H}
in the following way:

•

There are
n
vertices split into two clusters
{L}
and
{R}
of size
n/2
. There is a clique on each cluster.

•

The cliques are joined by
n/3
edges of rank
3
, such that every vertex is a member of exactly one such edge.

Now, we can construct a vector
{\bm{f}}
as follows.
For each edge
e
of rank
3
in the hypergraph, let
u\in e
be the vertex alone in one of the cliques, and
v,w\in e
be the two vertices in the other clique.
Then, we set
{\bm{f}}(u)=1
and one of
{\bm{f}}(v)
or
{\bm{f}}(w)
to be
-1
and the other to be
0
.
Notice that there are
2^{n/3}
such vectors.
Each one corresponds to a different graph
{G}
, as illustrated in Figure
6
, which is the graph constructed such that
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
is equivalent to
{\mathbf{J}}_{G}
when applied to the vector
{\bm{f}}
.

Notice that, by the construction of the graph
{H}
, half of the edges of rank
3
must have one vertex in
{L}
and two vertices in
{R}
and half of the rank-
3
edges must have two vertices in
{L}
and one vertex in
{R}
.
This means that, within each cluster
{L}
or
{R}
, one third of the vertices have
{\bm{f}}
-value
1
, one third have
{\bm{f}}
-value
-1
and one third have
{\bm{f}}
-value
0
.

Now, we have that

\displaystyle\left({\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}\right)(u)

\displaystyle=\left({\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{G}{\bm{f}}\right)(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{u\sim_{G}v}({\bm{f}}(u)+{\bm{f}}(v))

where
u\sim_{G}v
means that
u
and
v
are adjacent in the graph
{G}
.
Suppose that
{\bm{f}}(u)=0
, meaning that it does not have an adjacent edge in
{G}
from its adjacent rank-
3
edge in
{H}
.
In this case,

\displaystyle\left({\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}\right)(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{u\sim_{G}v}{\bm{f}}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\left[\frac{n}{3\cdot 2}\cdot 1+\frac{n}{3\cdot 2}\cdot(-1)\right]

\displaystyle=0.

Now, suppose that
{\bm{f}}(u)=1
, and so it has an adjacent edge in
{G}
from its adjacent rank-
3
edge in
{H}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\left({\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}\right)(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{u\sim_{G}v}(1+{\bm{f}}(v))

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\left[\frac{n}{2}+\frac{n}{3\cdot 2}\cdot(-1)+\left(\frac{n}{3\cdot 2}-1\right)\cdot 1-1\right]

\displaystyle=1-\frac{4}{n}.

Similarly, if
{\bm{f}}(u)=-1
, we find that

\left({\mathbf{D}}_{H}^{-1}{\mathbf{J}}_{H}{\bm{f}}\right)(u)=\frac{4}{n}-1,

and so we can conclude that
{\bm{f}}
is an eigenvector of the operator
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
with eigenvalue
(n-4)/n
.
Since there are
2^{n/3}
possible such vectors
{\bm{f}}
, there are an exponential number of eigenvectors with this eigenvalue.
Once again, we highlight that due to the non-linearity of
{\mathbf{J}}_{H}
, a linear combination of these eigenvectors is in general not an eigenvector of the operator.

6
Experiments

In this section, we evaluate the performance of our new algorithm on synthetic and real-world datasets.
All algorithms are implemented in Python 3.6, using the
scipy
library for sparse matrix representations and linear programs. The experiments are performed using an Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-8500 CPU @ 3.00GHz processor, with 16 GB RAM. Our code can be downloaded from

https://github.com/pmacg/hypergraph-bipartite-components
.

Since ours is the first proposed algorithm for approximating hypergraph bipartiteness, we will compare it to a simple and natural baseline algorithm, which we call
CliqueCut
(
CC
).
In this algorithm, we construct the clique reduction of the hypergraph and
use the two-sided sweep-set algorithm described in
[
Tre12
]
to find a set with low bipartiteness in the clique reduction.

6

[

7
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we introduce a new hypergraph Laplacian-type operator and
apply this operator to design an algorithm that finds almost bipartite components in hypergraphs.
Our experimental results demonstrate the potentially wide applications of spectral hypergraph theory, and so we believe that designing faster spectral hypergraph algorithms is an important future research direction in algorithms and machine learning.
This will allow spectral hypergraph techniques to be applied more effectively to analyse the complex datasets which occur with increasing frequency in the real world.
