On a diffuse interface model for incompressible viscoelastic two-phase flows
Yadong Liu ∗ † and Dennis Trautwein †

Abstract
. This paper concerns a diffuse interface model for the flow of two incompressible viscoelastic fluids in a bounded domain. More specifically, the fluids are assumed to be macroscopically immiscible, but with a small transition region, where the two components are partially mixed. Considering the elasticity of both components, one ends up with a coupled Oldroyd-B/Cahn–Hilliard type system, which describes the behavior of two-phase viscoelastic fluids. We prove the existence of weak solutions to the system in two dimensions for general (unmatched) mass densities, variable viscosities, different shear moduli, and a class of physically relevant and singular free energy densities that guarantee that the order parameter stays in the physically reasonable interval. The proof relies on a combination of a regularization of the original system and a new hybrid implicit time discretization for the regularized system together with the analysis of an Oldroyd-B type equation.

1.
Introduction

In this article, we study a so-called
diffuse interface model
(also called
phase field model
) for two incompressible, viscoelastic fluids of different mass densities, viscosities and shear moduli. In the model, a partial mixing of the macroscopically immiscible fluids is considered and elastic effects are taken into account.

This model is quite new and was developed recently in Mokbel–Abels–Aland
[
51
]
, where they proposed a novel phase-field model for a fluid-structure interaction problem to handle very large deformations as well as topology changes like the contact of a solid to a wall. Under certain assumptions on the system parameters, the model is capable of describing a thermodynamically consistent, frame indifferent,
incompressible two-phase flow with viscoelasticity of Oldroyd-B type.

1.1.
State of the Art

Over the last decades mathematical analysis of fluid dynamics has been developed with an abundant amount of literature. In particular, the problem of two-phase fluids with a free interface fascinated the profound attention of mathematicians. However, the free interface is not easy to track in actual applications and mathematicians still do not have a thorough comprehension of the problems concerning singularities of interfaces and topological changes during the evolution of interfaces. Thus, an effective approximation of the interface has been introduced with the Ginzburg–Landau free energy since, e.g.,
[
32
,
34
]
, where the authors proposed a so-called “model H” by the phase-field method in terms of the phase variable that indicates the specific phase of the whole system. Later on, this model was investigated and employed explosively in many areas, especially in numerical simulations, since the model has the advantage that topological changes can happen. However, model H was endowed with a basic assumption that the densities of both components are the same, which is not always physically reasonable. To overcome this disadvantage, Abels–Garcke–Grün
[
6
]
derived the “AGG model”, which considers unmatched densities and is thermodynamically consistent and frame indifferent. For the analysis of weak solutions and strong solutions to the AGG model, we refer to
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
26
]
. An alternative and thermodynamically consistent model was derived before by Lowengrub–Truskinovski
[
46
]
by using the mass-averaged (barycentric) velocity. From the mathematical viewpoint, the latter has the disadvantage that the mass-averaged velocity is not divergence free, which is the case in the AGG model based on a volume-averaged velocity. In view of the physical advantages of the AGG model, there came many variants subsequently concerning different aspects. Readers are referred, for example, to
[
22
]
for the nonlocal Cahn–Hilliard–Navier–Stokes equations with unmatched densities, to
[
23
,
27
]
for diffuse interface models including moving contact lines, to
[
55
]
for polymeric fluids, to
[
38
]
for magnetohydrodynamic two-phase flow with different densities, and to
[
51
]
for a diffuse interface model simulating a fluid-structure interaction problem with viscoelasticity of Oldroyd-B type, which is exactly of our interest.

Now, let us recall some facts about viscoelasticity, in particular of Oldroyd-B type. Depending on the deformation gradient
\mathbf{F}
that is defined in Eulerian coordinates or the corresponding left Cauchy–Green tensor
\mathbb{B}\coloneqq\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{\top}
, viscoelasticity can be recognized as two regimes.
Note that this definition of
\mathbb{B}
here differs slightly from the one used in the current context. It corresponds to the definition of the model (
1.1
) with an infinite relaxation time
\lambda(\phi)
.
In the context of
\mathbf{F}
, the incompressible viscoelastic fluid model reads as

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}+\nabla p=\Div(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{\top}),\\
&\partial_{t}\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{F}=\nabla\mathbf{u}\mathbf{F},\\
&\Div\mathbf{u}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

whose derivation is referred to, e.g., Giga–Kirshtein–Liu
[
25
]
by the energy variational approach. The analysis of (
1.4
) has been quite popular in the last decade, and the global well-posedness and long time behavior have been established in, e.g.,
[
37
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
and their citations. However, due to the lack of compactness for the deformation gradient, problems concerning the existence of weak solutions are still open even in two dimensions. We refer to two recent progressive papers by Hu–Lin
[
36
]
for the existence of 2D small weak solutions in
L^{p}
, where the so-called
effective viscous flux
method was employed. Based on this viscoelastic approach, some very interesting models that describe the motion of two-phase flows have been introduced in very recent years. Readers are referred to Agosti–Colli–Garcke–Rocca
[
8
]
for a phase-field model coupled with viscoelasticity with large deformations, and Kim–Tawri–Temam
[
39
]
for a diffuse interface model describing the interaction between blood flow and a thrombus with Hookean elasticity during the stage of atherosclerotic lesion in human artery.

When it comes to the tensor
\mathbb{B}
, with relaxation and
\bm{\tau}\coloneqq\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I}
, we obtain the original Oldroyd-B model as

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}+\nabla p=\Div\bm{\tau},\\
&\partial_{t}\bm{\tau}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{\tau}-\nabla\mathbf{u}\bm{\tau}-\bm{\tau}\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{u}+a\bm{\tau}=\frac{b}{2}(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{u}),\\
&\Div\mathbf{u}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where
a,b\geq 0
and
1/a
denotes the relaxation time and
b/a
is the viscosity of the polymers. For a derivation, we refer to
[
49
,
52
,
54
]
. It can also be recovered from the micro-macroscopic FENE (Finite Extensible Nonlinear Elastic) dumbbell model, see, e.g.,
[
25
]
. Concerning the analysis of (
1.5
), the first existence result of weak solutions goes back to Lions–Masmoudi
[
44
]
, where they considered a co-rotational version of the stress tensor equation to get better estimates and compactness. Due to lack of compactness, the existence of weak solutions to (
1.5
) has been an open problem for a long time. We mention a breakthrough on a related model, for which Masmoudi
[
48
]
proved the existence of weak solutions to the FENE dumbbell model with many weak convergence techniques, based on the control of the propagation of strong convergence of some well-chosen quantity by studying a transport
equation for its defect measure. Instead of solving (
1.5
) directly, in recent years mathematicians tried to include some regularization terms for the Oldroyd-B equation, for example the diffusive stress
\Delta\bm{\tau}
, in order to obtain higher regularity and compactness, which is however still limited to two dimensions. Barrett–Boyaval
[
10
]
proved the existence of weak solutions to a regularized Oldroyd-B model with stress diffusion in 2D by employing an approximating finite element scheme, together with an entropy regularization, while the global regularity of 2D diffusive Oldroyd-B equations was obtained by Constantin–Kliegl
[
20
]
. Note that in
[
10
]
, the authors reformulated an equivalent system in terms of the tensor
\mathbb{B}
instead of
\bm{\tau}
, and made use of a physically relevant elastic energy
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})
and its estimate, which was derived before by Hu–Lelièvre
[
35
]
. One of the advantages of the formulation with respect to
\mathbb{B}
is that one can expect the positive definiteness of it, while in the other formulation this is not clear at all.
Later, a compressible counterpart of the (diffusive) Oldroyd-B model was proposed and analyzed in
[
11
]
by means of a combination of multilayer approximations from
[
10
]
and the compressible Navier–Stokes equation
[
21
]
, based on the Galerkin scheme. For other related viscoelastic models, one refers to
[
17
,
47
]
for Peterlin type models, and to
[
12
]
for a mixing of the Oldroyd-B model and the Giesekus model. A recent existence result for the three dimensional Giesekus model can be found in
[
18
]
.

Along with the development of diffuse interface models, two-phase viscoelasticity came up in recent years. Grün–Metzger
[
31
]
derived a micro-macro model for two-phase flow of dilute polymeric solutions, where a Fokker–Planck type equation describes orientation and elongation of polymers. Sieber
[
55
]
then extended the model in
[
31
]
to the case with different mass densities for polymeric fluids. Moreover, a viscoelastic phase separation model of Peterlin type was derived in
[
14
]
and investigated in
[
15
,
16
]
. By virtue of the Oldroyd-B model, recently Garcke–Kovács–Trautwein
[
24
]
established a viscoelastic Cahn–Hilliard model to describe tumor growth and obtained the existence of weak solutions in the case of matched mass densities with the help of finite element approximations.

In contrast to the growing literature on two-phase flows and viscoelastic fluids, the analytical results about two-phase viscoelasticity are quite limited, especially the Oldroyd-B type models with unmatched densities and variable shear moduli.
To the best of our knowledge, the only related results of diffuse interface model including viscoelasticity of Oldroyd-B type are
[
24
,
55
]
we mentioned above, where they showed the existence of weak solutions for 2D two-phase Oldroyd-B type fluids with polynomial potential, while none of them concerned the case of unmatched densities and phase-depending shear moduli, which in fact, is rather physical and interesting. The aim of the present paper is to provide a deep understanding on the theory of incompressible two-phase flow with viscoelasticity of Oldroyd-B type with more physical assumptions, in company with the AGG model
[
3
,
6
]
. More precisely, we are going to prove the existence of weak solutions to the incompressible Oldroyd-B/Cahn–Hilliard model in the presence of variable densities, shear moduli, viscosities, and a singular potential.

1.2.
Main results

In this section, we state the main result of this manuscript. Namely, the global existence of weak solutions to (

\mathbb{B}_{0}
positive definite a.e. in
\Omega
,
\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, and
\left|\phi_{0}\right|\leq 1
a.e. in
\Omega
and
\fint_{\Omega}\phi_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x\in(-1,1)
. Then for any
T\in(0,\infty)
, there exists a weak solution
(\mathbf{u},\mathbb{B},\phi,q)
of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.1
. Moreover, the tensor
\mathbb{B}
fulfills the following estimate

\left\|\mathbb{B}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\mathbb{B}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\nabla\mathbb{B}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C(\mathcal{E}_{0},\left\|\mathbb{B}_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2})

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
.

Remark 1.3

(Modified pressure)
.

The unknown function
\pi
from (
1.1
) is a modified pressure and it is related to the ‘standard’ pressure
p
(cf. Appendix
A
) via the relation

\displaystyle\pi=p+\frac{\tilde{\sigma}\epsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\phi\right|^{2}+\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{\epsilon}W(\phi)+\frac{\mu(\phi)}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I}).

This formulation is more beneficial for the analysis of weak solutions.
In fact, from the derivation of (
1.1
), cf. Appendix
A
, we know that the original momentum equation is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho(\phi)\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle+\Div(\rho(\phi)\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})+\Div(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{J})+\nabla p

\displaystyle-\Div\big(\mathbb{S}(\nabla\mathbf{u},\mathbb{B},\phi)\big)=-\epsilon\tilde{\sigma}\Div\big(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi\big),

where the function
p:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
is the pressure,
\epsilon\tilde{\sigma}\Div\big(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi\big)
is referred to as the capillary force.
Then by defining a new scalar pressure

\pi\coloneqq p+\frac{\tilde{\sigma}\epsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\phi\right|^{2}+\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{\epsilon}W(\phi)+\frac{\mu(\phi)}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I}),

we obtain

\nabla\pi=\nabla p+q\nabla\phi+\tilde{\sigma}\epsilon\Div\big(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi\big)+\frac{\mu(\phi)}{2}\nabla\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I}).

In
[
11
]
, a regularization term
\alpha\nabla\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}
was added in the momentum equation but here we get it for free, since the shear modulus
\mu(\phi)
is unmatched among the two phases.

Remark 1.4

(Stress diffusion)
.

For mathematical reasons, we introduced a diffusive regularization
\frac{\kappa}{\mu(\phi)}\Delta\mathbb{B}
in (
1.1c
) such that the evolution law of the tensor
\mathbb{B}
is of parabolic nature. The system is closed with an additional no-flux boundary condition
{\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mathbb{B}={\mathbb{O}}}
, see also in
[
8
,
10
,
12
,
20
,
24
]
.

In comparison to, e.g.,
[
10
]
, the special structure of the factor
\frac{\kappa}{\mu(\phi)}
in (
1.1c
) is of the main importance in the presence of a phase-depending shear modulus
\mu(\phi)
to keep the energy dissipation structure in (
1.3
).
In general, the additional diffusive term
\frac{\kappa}{\mu(\phi)}\Delta\mathbb{B}
can also be motivated by means of a nonlocal energy storage or nonlocal entropy production mechanisms, see Málek–Průša–Skřivan–Süli
[
50
]
.

Remark 1.5
.

In this manuscript, we consider an isotropic free energy and a regular mobility. These are simplifications as our main focus lies on the unmatched densities, viscosities and shear moduli. We comment that more general cases can be obtained by suitable modifications in our current framework. See also Remark
5.1
for further discussion.

Remark 1.6

(Extension to 3D)
.

The existence of weak solutions is only valid for the two-dimensional case, primarily due to the lack of compactness of the tensor
\mathbb{B}
in three dimensions.
Recently, for a simpler model of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations coupled with the diffusive Oldroyd-B equation, authors of
[
12
]
proved the 3D result by means of a modified Helmholtz free energy, which includes an additional quadratic term, e.g.,
\int_{\Omega}\big(\frac{1}{2}\mu\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})+\frac{1}{4}\mu\left|\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I}\right|^{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x
. Incorporating this quadratic term improves the compactness properties, as energy estimates directly provide an
L^{2}
estimate for
\mathbb{B}
in two and three dimensions.
We also refer to the recent work
[
33
]
that presents an existence result for a related diffuse interface model in three dimensions that includes quadratic contributions to the elastic energy.
This suggests that future work could extend the arguments presented here combined with the strategies from
[
12
]
to establish the existence of weak solutions in a three-dimensional setting.

1.3.
Strategy of the Proof (Technical Discussions)

Note that the system (
1.1
) has a similar structure as the AGG model
[
6
]
, which was solved with the help of an implicit time discretization scheme in
[
3
]
.
In order to combine this with the existence of weak solutions to an Oldroyd-B fluid system proved in
[
10
]
, we introduce a regularization to (
1.1
) (see
\mathscr{R}_{\eta}
below, also in (
4.1
)), such that we can solve the regularized system in a suitable way and have good uniform
a priori
estimates (see Section
4.1
), inspired by
[
1
,
13
,
43
]
. More specifically, the advantage of the regularization is twofold. Due to the presence of a phase-dependent shear modulus, the regularity of the terms with the coefficient
\mu(\phi)
will cause problems if one tries the standard testing procedure. For example, multiplying the second term in (
1.1c
) with
\frac{\mu(\phi)}{2}(\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{B}^{-1})
and integrating by parts over
\Omega
leads to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{B}:\frac{\mu(\phi)}{2}(\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{B}^{-1})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\mu(\phi)}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mu^{\prime}(\phi)}{2}\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\phi\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})\,\mathrm{d}x

where
\mathbf{u}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L_{\sigma}^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. The first term on the right-hand side vanishes for such
\mathbf{u}
(as it is solenoidal), while it is not clear if the second integral is well-defined or not under the current regularity setting. With the regularized terms
\mu(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\phi)
and
\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{u}
in the regularized system, it is no longer an issue since
\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\phi
and
\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{u}
are sufficiently smooth for any
\eta>0
.
Moreover, under such regularization, we separate the proof into two parts, focusing on the challenges of the individual subsystems rather than addressing the combined difficulties all at once. Note that this kind of regularization at the same time promotes the solvability of the regularized Oldroyd-B equation, see (
3.1
) below.

Concerning the solvability of the regularized Oldroyd-B equation in three dimensions, we make use of the standard Galerkin approximation to account for the parabolic nature. In addition, inspired by
[
10
]
, an entropy regularization for
\ln\mathbb{B}
is introduced, which is of importance since the tensor
\mathbb{B}
is not necessarily positive definite almost everywhere in
Q_{T}
and hence the physical energy
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})
is not necessarily well-defined in presence of the logarithmic term. Thanks to the regularization
\mathscr{R}_{\eta}
, we obtain uniform estimates and then pass to the limit for both the entropy regularization and the Galerkin approximation. Finally, we conclude the positive definiteness of the limit function (i.e. the tensor
\mathbb{B}
) with a contradiction argument.

To solve the regularized system in 3D, one natural idea would be to employ a time discretization scheme for the full system as in
[
3
]
. However, it is not reasonable to repeat all the regularization and approximation techniques for practicality and readability. Thus, in this manuscript, we propose a novel scheme, we called “
hybrid time discretization
” (see Section
4.2
), to obtain a suitable approximation of the full system, combined with the solvability of the Oldroyd-B system. Specifically, for the AGG part
(\mathbf{u},\phi)
we keep the implicit time discretization as in
[
3
]
, namely the piecewise constant discrete solution with time-averaged terms regarding
\mathbb{B}
, while the Oldroyd-B part is solved in each time interval continuously in time with the time-averaged solution from the previous time step as the initial datum. Due to the well-posedness of the Oldroyd-B part, one is able to construct a continuous mapping of
\mathbb{B}
in terms of
(\mathbf{u},\phi)
in the discrete AGG system, which is solvable in a similar fashion as in
[
3
]
. The first essential ingredient of the hybrid time discretization scheme is a uniform estimate, for which we exploit the energy estimate of
\mathbb{B}
on a discrete time interval and cancel out all the mixing terms. This is where we use the time-averaged approximation of terms containing
\mathbb{B}
, see (
4.16
) and (
4.17
). The second crucial element is the compactness of these time-averaged terms, as the limit passage in these terms requires additional arguments. For this, we provide compactness results for weakly-
*
convergent and weakly convergent sequences, respectively, with the help of a convolution (in time) with Dirac sequences and the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, see Appendix
B
. Note that one can not apply the Aubin–Lions lemma for the approximate tensor, say
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}^{N}
, since it is endowed with jumps across the time intervals and one can not expect the existence of
\partial_{t}\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
over the whole time interval. This is overcome by a compactness argument with time translations, cf. Section
4.5
.

The final step is the passage to the limit in the regularized system as the regularization parameter
\eta\to 0
. This can be realized in two dimensions with a compactness argument from the concise uniform
a priori
estimate (
4.7
) and a stronger estimate (
5.1
) for
\mathbb{B}
, which are both independent of
\eta>0
. The stronger estimate is necessary since the energy estimate (
4.7
) only provides merely
L^{1}
information of
\mathbb{B}
. Note that here the stronger estimate for
\mathbb{B}
relies on the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality which requires the restriction to two dimensions. Then, in light of the compactness of
\mathbf{u}
, which is derived with a Helmholtz projection and the convergence of the kinetic energy, one obtains the existence of a weak solution to the original full system (
1.1
) by passing to the limit as
\eta\to 0
.

In Section

2.1.1.
Vector and tensor analysis

Let
\partial_{j}=\partial_{x_{j}}
be the spatial derivative in
x_{j}
direction,
j=1,\dots,d
.
For a vector
\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{j=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the divergence and gradient are given by
\Div\mathbf{u}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}
,
\nabla\mathbf{u}=(\partial_{j}\mathbf{u}^{i})_{i,j=1}^{d}
. Additionally, the transpose of the gradient will be denoted by
\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{u}\coloneqq(\nabla\mathbf{u})^{\top}
. Given vectors
\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{j=1}^{d},\mathbf{v}=(\mathbf{v}_{j})_{j=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i},\quad(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{v})_{ij}=\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{j}.

Given matrices
\mathbb{A}=(\mathbb{A}_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d},\mathbb{B}=(\mathbb{B}_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we know
(\mathbb{A}\mathbb{B})_{ik}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{A}_{ij}\mathbb{B}_{jk}
. Then we denote by

\mathbb{A}:\mathbb{B}=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}^{\top}\mathbb{A})=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\mathbb{B}_{jk}\mathbb{A}_{jk}

the Frobenius inner product and
\left|\mathbb{A}\right|\coloneqq\sqrt{\mathbb{A}:\mathbb{A}}
the induced modulus.
For a matrix
\mathbb{A}=(\mathbb{A}_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we introduce the divergence as
(\Div\mathbb{A})_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{j}\mathbb{A}_{ij}
, and the gradient as
\nabla\mathbb{A}=(\partial_{j}\mathbb{A})_{j=1}^{d}
. Then, we define

\displaystyle\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}\mathbf{u}\cdot\partial_{i}\mathbf{v}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{i}\mathbf{u}_{j}\partial_{i}\mathbf{v}_{j},\ \nabla\mathbb{A}:\nabla\mathbb{B}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}\mathbb{A}:\partial_{i}\mathbb{B}=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\partial_{i}\mathbb{A}_{jk}\partial_{i}\mathbb{B}_{jk},

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbb{A})_{ijk}=\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbb{A}_{jk},\ (\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbb{A}):\nabla\mathbb{B}=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbb{A}_{jk}\partial_{i}\mathbb{B}_{jk},

Moreover, let
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d}
be the set of symmetric matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. For any
\mathbb{B}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d}
, there exists a diagonal decomposition

(2.1)

\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{O}^{\top}\mathbb{D}\mathbb{O},

with
\mathbb{D}=\mathrm{diag}\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{d}\}
, where
\lambda_{k}>0
,
k=1,\dots,d
, are the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{B}
, and
\mathbb{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is an orthogonal matrix.
Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a scalar function.
We define

(2.2)

g(\mathbb{B})=\mathbb{O}^{\top}g(\mathbb{D})\mathbb{O},

where
g(\mathbb{D})\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}\{g(\lambda_{1}),g(\lambda_{2}),\dots,g(\lambda_{d})\}
.
Although the diagonal decomposition (
2.1
) is not unique, the function
g(\mathbb{B})
is uniquely defined by (
2.2
).
For any positive definite matrix
\mathbb{B}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d}
, we define its real logarithm
\ln\mathbb{B}
as a symmetric matrix such that
e^{\ln\mathbb{B}}=\mathbb{B}
, where
e^{\mathbb{M}}
denotes the standard matrix exponential of a matrix
{\mathbb{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. In fact, in view of (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we have

\ln\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{O}^{\top}\mathrm{diag}\{\ln\lambda_{1},\ln\lambda_{2},\dots,\ln\lambda_{d}\}\mathbb{O},

which also implies
\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}=\ln\det\mathbb{B}
.

We recall a set of properties for matrix-valued functions.

Lemma 2.1

(Properties of matrix-valued functions)
.

Let
\mathbb{B}:U\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d}
be positive definite a.e. in
U
, with some open
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\leq d
,
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
, be differentiable with respect to a first-order differential operator
\partial\cdot
. Then

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})\geq 0,}

(2.4)

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}+\mathbb{B}^{-1}-2\mathbb{I})\geq 0,}

(2.5)

\displaystyle\partial\mathbb{B}:\mathbb{B}^{-1}=\mathrm{Tr}\big(\mathbb{B}^{-1}\partial\mathbb{B}\big)=\partial\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B},

(2.6)

\displaystyle\partial\mathbb{B}:(\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{B}^{-1})=\partial\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I}).

Moreover, let
\mathbb{B},\mathbb{C}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d}
,
\mathbf{v}\in C^{1}(U;\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
then it holds

(2.7)

\big(\nabla\mathbf{v}\mathbb{B}+\mathbb{B}\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{v}\big):\mathbb{C}=2\big(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{B}\big):\nabla\mathbf{v}.

Proof.

The proof of (
2.3
) follows directly from (
2.1
), (
2.2
) and the scalar inequality
s-\ln(s)-1\geq 0
for
s>0
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\lambda_{i}-\ln(\lambda_{i})-1)\geq 0,

where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}>0
are the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{B}
.
Similarly, the desired results (
2.4
)–(
2.6
) are based on (
2.1
), (
2.2
) and the scalar results
s+s^{-1}-2\geq 0
,
(\partial s)s^{-1}=\partial\ln(s)
and
(\partial s)(1-s^{-1})=\partial(s-\ln(s)-1)
for all positive scalar functions
s
, where
\partial
is a first order differential operator. The identity (
2.7
) follows directly from the standard properties of the Frobenius inner product and the symmetry of
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{C}
.

\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)
,
\left\|\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)}\leq\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)}
and
\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\rightarrow\mathbf{v}
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)
as
\eta\rightarrow 0
.

(4)

If
\mathbf{v}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)
,
1\leq p<\infty
, then
\left\|\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)}\leq C(\eta)\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)}
with
C(\eta)>0
depending on
\eta
.

(5)

If
\mathbf{v}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)
, then
\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)
, and
\left\|\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)}\leq\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)}
.

Proof.

The first statement is a direct consequence of the radial symmetry of
\psi
, while the fourth one can be derived by taking the supremum norm of
\psi
. The remaining statements are referred to
[
21
, Theorem 11.3]
.
∎

3.
Matrix-Valued Equation with Stress Diffusion

In this section, we are going to solve the tensor-valued equation for
\mathbb{B}
in dimension
d\in\{2,3\}
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}+\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{B}\nabla^{\top}\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}-\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\mathbb{B}+(\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{\mu_{\eta}(\xi)}\Delta\mathbb{B},

\displaystyle\text{in }Q_{T}

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mathbb{B}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{O}},

\displaystyle\text{on }S_{T}

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}(0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{B}_{0},

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

with
\mathbb{B}_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
positive definite a.e. in
\Omega
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, and given data
(\mathbf{v},\xi)
satisfying

(3.2)

\mathbf{v}\in C([0,T];W_{0,\sigma}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})),\quad\xi\in C([0,T];W^{2,2}(\Omega))\cap W^{1,2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Here,
\mathscr{R}_{\eta}
is the mollifier defined in (
2.9
) and
\mu_{\eta}(\phi)\coloneqq\mu(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\phi)
. As usual, we give the precise definition of
finite energy
weak solutions of the system (
3.1
).

Definition 3.1
.

Let
d\in\{2,3\}
,
T>0
,
\mathbb{B}_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d})
positive definite a.e. in
\Omega
,
\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, and
(\mathbf{v},\xi)
satisfy (
3.2
). We call
\mathbb{B}
a finite energy weak solution of (
3.1
) with data
(\mathbb{B}_{0},\mathbf{v},\xi)
, provided that

(1)

the tensor
\mathbb{B}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}\text{ is symmetric positive definite a.e.~in }Q_{T};

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}\in C_{w}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d}))\cap L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d}));

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}\in L^{2}(0,T;[W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d})]^{\prime});

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Omega));

(2)

for all
t\in(0,T)
and all
\mathbb{C}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q_{T}};\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d})
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\Big(\mathbb{B}:\partial_{t}\mathbb{C}-{(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{B}:\mathbb{C}}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau

(3.3)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\big(\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\mathbb{B}+\mathbb{B}\nabla^{\top}\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\big):\mathbb{C}-\kappa\nabla\mathbb{B}:\nabla\frac{\mathbb{C}}{\mu_{\eta}(\xi)}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\mathbb{B}:\mathbb{C}-\mathrm{Tr}\mathbb{C})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{B}(\cdot,t):\mathbb{C}(\cdot,t)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{B}_{0}:\mathbb{C}(\cdot,0)\,\mathrm{d}x;

(3)

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
we have the following estimate

(3.4)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B})(t)\right\|_{L^{1}}+\left\|\mathbb{B}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\left\|\mathbb{B}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\kappa\left\|\nabla\mathbb{B}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\left\|\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}+\mathbb{B}^{-1}-2\mathbb{I})(\tau)\right\|_{L^{1}}+\kappa\left\|\nabla\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq C,

where the constant
C>0
depends on
\left\|\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}_{0}\right\|_{L^{1}}
,
\left\|\mathbb{B}_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}}
,
\kappa
, bounds of
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
,
\mathbf{v},\xi
,
\eta
, and
T
.

Then main result of this section reads as follows:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
T>0
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, be a smooth bounded domain. Assume that
(\mathbf{v},\xi)
satisfies (
3.2
),
\mathbb{B}_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d})
positive definite a.e. in
\Omega
and
\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
. Then, there exists a finite energy weak solution of (
3.1
) in the sense of Definition
3.1
. Moreover, the solution is unique and depends continuously on
(\mathbf{v},\xi,\mathbb{B}_{0})
.

Remark 3.3
.

This theorem is valid for
(\mathbf{v},\xi)
satisfying (
3.2
), which is a quite general and rather strong assumption. Later, we will only consider the case with piecewise-in-time constant functions satisfying certain spatial regularity, which, for sure, fulfill (
3.2
) on each subinterval.

We now dedicate the remainder of this section to the step-by-step proof of Theorem
3.2
. We begin by introducing a two-layer approximation in Section
3.1
and establish uniform bounds for the approximate solution in Section
3.2
. Utilizing standard ODE theory, we justify the maximal existence time for solutions to the approximate system in Section
3.3
.
With these bounds in place, we apply compactness arguments to justify the passage to the limit in the Galerkin approximation in Section
3.4
. Subsequently, we derive uniform energy estimates at the regularized level in Section
3.5
and perform the corresponding limit passage in Section
3.6
. The positive definiteness of
\mathbb{B}
is established in Section
3.7
. Finally, we conclude the proof of Theorem
3.2
by providing the necessary statements regarding the limit system in Section
3.8
.

We start with the approximations.

3.1.
Two-layer Approximation

By the classical theory of eigenvalue problems for symmetric linear elliptic operators, one can carry out the finite space approximation. Let
\{\mathbb{A}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the eigenfunctions of the matrix-valued Laplace operator with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, namely,

-\Delta\mathbb{A}_{l}=\lambda_{l}\mathbb{A}_{l}\quad\text{in }\Omega,\quad\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mathbb{A}_{l}={\mathbb{O}}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

with eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{l}\rightarrow\infty
,
l\rightarrow\infty
. Moreover, it holds

\mathbb{A}_{l}\in W^{2,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}).

Then
\{\mathbb{A}_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
and an orthogonal basis in
W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
(see also
[
11
]
for the compressible Oldroyd-B case). Define
\mathbb{H}_{l}=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbb{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{A}_{l}\}\subset L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
with
l\in\mathbb{N}
and denote by
\mathcal{P}_{l}:W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})\rightarrow\mathbb{H}_{l}
the orthogonal projection on
\mathbb{H}_{l}
with respect to the inner product in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d})
such that

\left(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}\right)=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{A}:\mathbb{B}\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\forall\,\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

Now we make the Galerkin ansatz to approximate the solution
\mathbb{B}
of (
3.1
) as

\mathbb{B}_{l}=\sum_{k=1}^{l}c_{k}^{l}(t)\mathbb{A}_{k}(x),\quad\mathbb{B}_{l}(0)=\mathcal{P}_{l}\mathbb{B}_{0},

where
\{c_{k}^{l}\}
are scalar functions of time. Then we arrive at the following approximate system

(3.5)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}_{l},\mathbb{W}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l},\mathbb{W}\right)-\left(\mathbb{B}_{l}\nabla^{\top}\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{W}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\mathbb{B}_{l},\mathbb{W}\right)+\left(\mathbb{B}_{l}-\mathbb{I},\mathbb{W}\right)=-\left(\kappa\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l},\nabla\frac{\mathbb{W}}{\mu_{\eta}(\xi)}\right),

for all
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{H}_{l}
, with the initial data
\mathbb{B}_{l}(0)=\mathcal{P}_{l}\mathbb{B}_{0}
.

Classically, one can employ the usual ODE theory to verify the solvability of (
3.5
). However, in our paper, this is not the case. In fact, we need to ensure that
\mathbb{B}
is positive definite such that the energy
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})
makes sense (especially the logarithmic term).
Motivated by
[
10
]
, we employ a regularization for the logarithmic function
G(s)=\ln(s)
to construct a family of symmetric positive definite approximations for
\mathbb{B}
, namely, one defines a
C^{1}
function for
\delta>0

G_{\delta}(s)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{s}{\delta}+\ln\delta-1,\quad&s<\delta,\\
&\ln s,\quad&s\geq\delta,\end{aligned}\right.

and a cut-off function
\beta_{\delta}(s)=[G_{\delta}^{\prime}(s)]^{-1}=\max\{s,\delta\}
, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. This kind of regularization was also applied in, e.g.,
[
11
]
for a compressible Oldroyd-B system, or
[
24
]
for a Cahn–Hilliard tumor growth model including viscoelasticity.

Let us recall the following result from
[
10
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, and for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, it holds

(3.6a)

\displaystyle\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{A})G_{\delta}^{\prime}(\mathbb{A})=G_{\delta}^{\prime}(\mathbb{A})\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{A})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I},

(3.6b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\big(\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{A})+\beta_{\delta}^{-1}(\mathbb{A})-2\mathbb{I}\big)

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(3.6c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\big(\mathbb{A}-G_{\delta}(\mathbb{A})-\mathbb{I}\big)

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(3.6d)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{A}-\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{A})):(\mathbb{I}-G_{\delta}^{\prime}(\mathbb{A}))

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(3.6e)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{A}-\mathbb{C}):G_{\delta}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Tr}\big(G_{\delta}(\mathbb{A})-G_{\delta}(\mathbb{C})\big).

In addition, if
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, it holds

(3.6f)

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{A}-G_{\delta}(\mathbb{A}))}

\displaystyle{\geq\max\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\left|\mathbb{A}\right|,\,\frac{1}{2\delta}\left|[\mathbb{A}]_{-}\right|\Big\},}

(3.6g)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}:(\mathbb{I}-G_{\delta}^{\prime}(\mathbb{A}))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left|\mathbb{A}\right|-d,

where
[\cdot]_{-}
denotes the negative part function defined by
[s]_{-}\coloneqq\min\{s,0\},\forall s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Now employing the regularization from above with respect to
\delta>0
, we introduce the regularized Oldroyd-B equation with symmetric positive definite approximations.

(3.7)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta},\mathbb{W}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}),\mathbb{W}\right)-\left(\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta})\nabla^{\top}\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{W}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}),\mathbb{W}\right)+\left(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}-\mathbb{I},\mathbb{W}\right)=-\left(\kappa\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta},\nabla\frac{\mathbb{W}}{\mu_{\eta}(\xi)}\right)

for all
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{H}_{l}
, subjected to the initial values
\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}(0)=\delta\mathbb{I}+\mathcal{P}_{l}\mathscr{R}_{\delta}\mathbb{B}_{0}
, where we used a diagonal shift by
\delta\mathbb{I}
, since
\mathcal{P}_{l}\mathscr{R}_{\delta}\mathbb{B}_{0}
is not necessarily positive definite. Then we have
\delta\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{B}_{0}\to\mathbb{B}_{0}
in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d})
, as
\delta\to 0
, and
\delta\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{B}_{0}
is positive definite a.e. in
\Omega
.
Note that in the same way as in
[
11
,
21
]
, (
3.7
) is a system of ordinary differential equations for
\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}
with respect to the time coefficients,
for which the Picard–Lindelöf theorem is applicable.
In particular, the Lipschitz continuity regarding
\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}
is easily verified since
\beta_{\delta}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous and the other terms are linear in
\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}
.
We also note the reformulation

\displaystyle\left(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}),\mathbb{W}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{W},\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta})\right)-\left(\Div\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v},\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}):\mathbb{W}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n},\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}):\mathbb{W}\right)_{\partial\Omega},

which is based on integration by parts, where
\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\partial\Omega}
denotes the
L^{2}
inner product on
\partial\Omega
.
The assumption (
3.2
) ensures the continuity in time of the data.
This guarantees the existence of a unique solution on a local time interval
[0,T_{l,\delta}]
, where
T_{l,\delta}\in(0,T)
.

3.2.
Uniform bounds

Recalling the scalar inequality
\left|\beta_{\delta}(s)\right|\leq 1+\left|s\right|
,
\forall\,s\in\mathbb{R},\delta\in(0,1)
, it follows from Young’s inequality that

\left|\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta})\right|\leq\sqrt{2d}+\sqrt{2}\left|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right|
for all
\delta\in(0,1)
. Therefore,
setting
\mathbb{W}=\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}
in (
3.7
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\big)\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}):\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}x+\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{\underline{\mu}}\left\|\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(3.8)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{Tr}\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}x+\kappa\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mu_{\eta}^{\prime}(\xi)}{\mu_{\eta}^{2}(\xi)}(\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\xi\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}:\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}):\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathrm{Tr}\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\kappa\overline{\mu}^{\prime}}{\underline{\mu}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left|(\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\xi\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}:\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x+2\int_{\Omega}(\sqrt{2d}+\sqrt{2}\left|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right|)\left|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right|\left|\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x.

In light of Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, one obtains

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|(\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\xi\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}:\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\left\|\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\left\|\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\xi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega}(\sqrt{2d}+\sqrt{2}\left|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right|)\left|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right|\left|\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\big(1+\left\|\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\big)\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\left\|\nabla\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

for some small
\varepsilon>0
that will be specified later.
Note that in
[
11
, Appendix C]
it is shown that
\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{G})\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
with
\left\|\nabla\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{G})\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left\|\nabla\mathbb{G}\right\|_{L^{2}}
for
\mathbb{G}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
.
Hence, we infer from Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|\big(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\big)\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}):\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\left\|\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\left\|\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
that will be specified later.
In addition, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Young’s inequality, we derive

\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathrm{Tr}\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{d}\left|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{d}{2}\left|\Omega\right|+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Then integrating (
3.8
) over
(0,t)
,
t\in(0,T_{l,\delta})
together with choosing
\varepsilon\leq\frac{\kappa}{2\underline{\mu}}
yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\frac{\kappa}{2\underline{\mu}}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}(0)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{d}{2}\left|\Omega\right|t+C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\nabla\mathbf{v}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{0}^{t}(1+\left\|\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\xi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\left\|\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\left\|\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{\infty}})\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

which, by using a Gronwall argument (cf.
[
30
, Lemma 3.1]
), gives rise to

(3.9)

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq\tau\leq t}

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}(0)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left|\Omega\right|t+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\nabla\mathbf{v}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\left(1+C\zeta(t)e^{C\zeta(t)}\right)}\leq C,

for all
t\in[0,T_{l,\delta}]
, where
C>0
is independent of
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta>0
, but depends on
\kappa
,
\underline{\mu}
,
T_{l,\delta}
,
\|\mathbb{B}_{0}\|_{L^{2}}
,
\mathbf{v},\xi
,
\eta
, and
\zeta(t)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}(1+\left\|\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\xi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\left\|\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\left\|\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{\infty}})\,\mathrm{d}\tau
which is finite due to (
3.2
) and Lemma
2.5
.

Moreover, by letting
\mathbb{W}=-\Delta\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}
and
\mathbb{W}=\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}
, respectively, in (
3.7
), it further follows from integration by parts and from Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{\underline{\mu}}\left\|\Delta\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left|\int_{\Omega}\Delta\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}):\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|+\left|\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\big)\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}):\Delta\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa}{2\underline{\mu}}\left\|\Delta\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\left\|\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}(1+\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2})+C\left\|\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

and

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2\mu_{\eta}(\xi)}\left\|\nabla\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta})\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)+\left\|\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left|\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}):\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|+\left|\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\big)\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}):\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}_{l}^{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

3.7.
Positive Definiteness

To make sure that the weak formulation (

Since

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathbb{B}
be the limit function of the sequence of positive definite solutions
\mathbb{B}^{\delta}
. Then
\mathbb{B}
is positive definite a.e. in
Q_{T}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathbb{B}
is not positive definite a.e. in
Q_{T}
. Then, there exists a subset
D\subset Q_{T}
with nonzero measure and a vector-valued function
\mathbf{w}\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T};\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\left|\mathbf{w}\right|=1
a.e. in
D
and
\mathbf{w}=0
a.e. in
Q_{T}\setminus D
such that

(3.19)

[\mathbb{B}]_{+}\mathbf{w}=0,\text{ a.e.~in }Q_{T}.

On noting the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, Hölder’s inequality, (
3.16
) and the fact that
|\mathbf{w}|=1
a.e. in
D
, we calculate

\displaystyle\left|D\right|

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathbf{w}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{w}^{\top}\beta_{\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{B}^{\delta})\beta_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{B}^{\delta})\mathbf{w}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{w}^{\top}\beta_{\delta}^{-1}(\mathbb{B}^{\delta})\mathbf{w}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{w}^{\top}\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}^{\delta})\mathbf{w}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\beta_{\delta}^{-1}(\mathbb{B}^{\delta})|\,|\mathbf{w}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{w}^{\top}\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}^{\delta})\mathbf{w}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{w}^{\top}\beta_{\delta}(\mathbb{B}^{\delta})\mathbf{w}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where

does not depend on
\delta
due to the uniform estimate (
3.16
). Using (
3.18
) and (
3.19
), we obtain in the limit
\delta\to 0
, that
\left|D\right|=0
, which is a contradiction to the assumption of a nonzero measure of

\mathbb{B}
is positive definite a.e. in
Q_{T}
.
∎

On account of Lemma

\kappa

g(t)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}(\|\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{v}_{1}(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\xi_{1}(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\mathbb{B}_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\mathbb{B}_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}}^{2})\,\mathrm{d}\tau
is finite for
t\in(0,T)
thanks to the existence of weak solutions and mollifiers.

This completes the proof of Theorem
3.2
. ∎

Remark 3.6

Remark 4.3
.

The theorem is finally proved in Section
4.5
.
Thanks to the regularization
\mathscr{R}_{\eta}
we introduced, the existence theorem can be established both in two and three dimensions.
This cannot be reached when passing to the limit as
\eta\rightarrow 0
in Section
5
, where we need a stronger estimate of
\mathbb{B}
(uniform in
\eta
) in Section
5.1
, which is restricted to two dimensions.

In the rest of this section, we only proceed with the case
d=3
, while the other case
d=2
is even simpler with better Sobolev embeddings. For this, we refer to the final proof of Theorem
1.2
in Section
5.2
.

To prove Theorem
4.2
, we employ a hybrid time discretization scheme, drawing on the solvability of the AGG model as presented in
[
3
]
and the regularized Oldroyd-B equation from Section
3
.
We begin with the time discretization technique from
[
3
]
, which involves using piecewise continuous data for
\mathbb{B}
with time integral averaging (see Section
4.2
).
Then, applying the Leray–Schauder principle, we establish the solvability of the time-discrete solutions in Section
4.3
. For these time-discrete solutions, we construct approximate solutions to (
4.1
) in Section
4.4
. Finally, we derive the corresponding uniform estimates in Section
4.5
using a discrete energy inequality. We then pass to the limit, thus completing the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

In the presence of the singular, non-convex potential
W(\phi)
, we first rewrite the chemical potential.
The idea is to use the subdifferential of a convex potential, which was also employed in
[
7
]
for the Cahn–Hilliard equation and in
[
3
]
for the Cahn–Hilliard–Navier–Stokes equation. First we define an extended potential
\widetilde{W}
through

\widetilde{W}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad\widetilde{W}(s)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &W(s)&&\ \text{if }s\in[-1,1],\\
&+\infty&&\ \text{else},\end{aligned}\right.

where
W
is the potential fulfilling Assumption
(H3)
. Note that
\widetilde{W}
is not necessarily convex by this assumption. Hence, we define
\widetilde{W}_{0}(r)\coloneqq\widetilde{W}(r)+\frac{\omega}{2}r^{2}
, which satisfies
\widetilde{W}_{0}\in C([-1,1])\cap C^{2}((-1,1))
and is convex due to the Assumption
(H3)
. In particular, we have
\widetilde{W}^{\prime}(r)=\widetilde{W}_{0}^{\prime}(r)-\omega r
. Now (
4.1e
) is equivalent to

q+\omega\phi=\widetilde{W}_{0}^{\prime}(\phi)-\Delta\phi+\eta\partial_{t}\phi+\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\Big[\frac{\mu_{\eta}^{\prime}(\phi)}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})\Big],\quad\text{a.e.~in }Q_{T}.

Following
[
7
,
3
]
, we define a modified energy
\widetilde{E}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
by

\widetilde{E}(\phi)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\phi\right|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{W}_{0}(\phi)&&\ \text{for }\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{E}),\\
&+\infty&&\ \text{else},\end{aligned}\right.

with the domain of definition

\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{E})=\left\{\phi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega):-1\leq\phi\leq 1\text{ a.e.}\right\}.

Then, as in
[
3
]
, the domain of the definition of the subgradient
\partial\widetilde{E}
is

\mathcal{D}(\partial\widetilde{E})=\left\{\phi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega):\widetilde{W}_{0}^{\prime}(\phi)\in L^{2}(\Omega),\ \widetilde{W}_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\phi)\left|\nabla\phi\right|^{2}\in L^{1}(\Omega),\ \partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0\right\}.

With these definitions, one has

\partial\widetilde{E}(\phi)=-\Delta\phi+\widetilde{W}_{0}^{\prime}(\phi)\quad\text{for }\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\partial\widetilde{E}).

Note that
\partial\widetilde{E}
is maximal monotone by the convexity of
\widetilde{W}_{0}
and the lower semicontinuity.
Moreover, it holds that

(4.13)

\left\|\phi\right\|_{W^{2,2}}^{2}+\|\widetilde{W}_{0}^{\prime}(\phi)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{W}_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\phi(x))\left|\nabla\phi(x)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\Big(\|\partial\widetilde{E}(\phi)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+1\Big).

Inspired by the well-posedness result of the Oldroyd-B equation (

) and the implicit time discretization argument in

]
to solve the AGG model with unmatched densities and singular potential, we propose a

Remark 4.4
.

In the above system, we distinguish between time-discrete functions and functions that are continuous in time. That is the reason why we write a tilde on the top of the tensor
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
.
In order to make sure the time-continuous problem (
4.14a
) and the time-discrete subsystem (
4.14b
)–(
4.14d
) are compatible with each other, we make use of time averages for the terms containing
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
in the time-discrete problem. In fact, it is necessary to take advantage of all the information of
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
in a fixed time interval so that the terms regarding
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
are well-defined concerning the regularity.

Another motivation is to get the uniform energy estimate for the whole hybrid discrete problem with respect to
h
, for which we approximate
\mu_{\eta}^{\prime}(\phi)
due to the variable shear modulus by the difference quotient
\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi)-\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})}{\mathscr{R}_{\eta}(\phi-\phi_{k})}
, and proceed with the energy estimate (
4.17
) for
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
in the interval
I_{k+1}
. With all these ingredients, we establish a uniform estimate by canceling the mixed terms corresponding to
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
.

Remark 4.5
.

The approximation of
\mu_{\eta}^{\prime}(\phi)
with the difference quotient
\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi)-\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})}{\mathscr{R}_{\eta}(\phi-\phi_{k})}
is well-defined for

with bounded derivative in view of Assumption

for some

between

a.e. in

, it holds

\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi)-\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})}{\mathscr{R}_{\eta}(\phi-\phi_{k})}\to\mu_{\eta}^{\prime}(\phi).

Lemma 4.7

First of all, testing (

By means of Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, and the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality, one has the following estimates:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}q(\phi-\overline{\phi})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(q-\overline{q})\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\left\|q-\overline{q}\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left\|\nabla q\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C(\left\|\nabla\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}+1),

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi)-\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})}{2\mathscr{R}_{\eta}(\phi-\phi_{k})}\Big(\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\ln\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\mathbb{I})(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)\mathscr{R}_{\eta}(\phi-\overline{\phi})\,\mathrm{d}x

\left\langle L_{k}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{v}\right\rangle_{[W_{0,\sigma}^{1,2}]^{\prime}\times W_{0,\sigma}^{1,2}}=\int_{\Omega}\nu(\phi_{k})(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{u}):\nabla\mathbf{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{for }\mathbf{v}\in W_{0,\sigma}^{1,2}(\Omega),

and the second and third lines are understood in the pointwise sense.
Moreover, for
\mathbf{w}=(\mathbf{u},\phi,q)\in X
we introduce
\mathcal{F}_{k}:X\rightarrow Y
as

\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{w})=\left(\begin{gathered}F_{k}(\mathbf{w})\\
-\frac{\phi-\phi_{k}}{h}-\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\phi_{k}+\int_{\Omega}q\,\mathrm{d}x\\
-\eta\frac{\phi-\phi_{k}}{h}+\phi+q+\omega\frac{\phi+\phi_{k}}{2}-G(\mathbf{w},\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})\end{gathered}\right),

where

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{w},\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})=

\displaystyle\,\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi)-\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})}{\mathscr{R}_{\eta}(\phi-\phi_{k})}\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\ln\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\mathbb{I})(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\Big],

\displaystyle F_{k}(\mathbf{w})=

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho\mathbf{u}-\rho_{k}\mathbf{u}_{k}}{h}-\Div(\rho_{k}\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})+q\nabla\phi_{k}

\displaystyle-\Big(\Div\mathbf{J}-\frac{\rho-\rho_{k}}{h}-\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\rho_{k}\Big)\frac{\mathbf{u}}{2}-(\mathbf{J}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle-\Div\big(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\big[\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{k+1}-\mathbb{I})\big]\big)+\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\Big[\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})}{2}\nabla\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\ln\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\mathbb{I})\Big]\,\mathrm{d}t,

and
\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{k+1}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
are constructed in (
4.19
). Consequently, we see that
(\mathbf{u},\phi,q,\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})
is a weak solution of the system (
4.14
) if and only if
\mathbf{w}=(\mathbf{u},\phi,q)\in X
satisfies

(4.20)

\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{w})-\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{w})=0.

Note that by (
4.20
), if one can show the invertibility of
\mathcal{L}_{k}
and continuity of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, we obtain a fixed-point formulation
\mathbf{w}=\mathcal{L}_{k}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{w})
.

Similarly to
[
3
]
, one can check that
\mathcal{L}_{k}:X\rightarrow Y
is invertible with the inverse
\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{k}:Y\rightarrow X
. Note that
X
is not a Banach space since
\mathcal{D}(\partial\widetilde{E})
consists of inequality constraints. To get a continuous and even compact operator, we introduce for
0<s<\frac{1}{4}
the following Banach spaces

\widetilde{X}\coloneqq W_{0,\sigma}^{1,2}(\Omega)\times W^{2-s,2}(\Omega)\times W_{n}^{2,2}(\Omega),\quad\widetilde{Y}\coloneqq L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})\times W^{1,\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega)\times W^{1,2}(\Omega).

Then we obtain the continuity of
\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{k}:Y\rightarrow\widetilde{X}
from standard theory and with the above note concerning the continuity of the term
\partial\widetilde{E}(\phi)+\phi
within the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{k}
. In view of the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem in three dimensions, one knows that
\widetilde{Y}\subset\subset Y
compactly, which implies that the restriction
\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{k}:\widetilde{Y}\rightarrow\widetilde{X}
is a compact operator. Moreover, we infer that
\mathcal{T}_{k+1}:\widetilde{X}\rightarrow Z
is well-defined due to Theorem
3.2
and the restriction
\mathcal{F}_{k}:\widetilde{X}\rightarrow\widetilde{Y}
is continuous and maps bounded sets into bounded sets, which can be verified by the same argument as in
[
3
]
. Here we leave out the details except for the terms regarding
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
.
Thanks to the regularization operator
\mathscr{R}_{\eta}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\Div\big(\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\big[\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{k+1}-\mathbb{I})\big]\big)\right\|_{L^{\frac{3}{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq C\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}(\|\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2})\,\mathrm{d}t+\left\|\phi_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|\nabla\phi_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\leq C_{k}(1+\frac{1}{h}),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\left\|\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\Big[\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})}{2}\nabla\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\ln\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\mathbb{I})\Big]\right\|_{L^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq C\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\left\|\nabla\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\ln\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+C\leq C_{k}(1+\frac{1}{h}),

\displaystyle\left\|G(\mathbf{w},\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})\right\|_{W^{1,2}}\leq C\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\left\|\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\ln\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\mathbb{I})\right\|_{W^{1,2}}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C_{k}(1+\frac{1}{h}),

where
C_{k}=C_{k}(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{W^{1,2}})>0
. Hence,
\mathcal{F}_{k}
maps bounded sets into bounded sets.

Next we record the continuity of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, that is,
\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{w}^{\ell})\to\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{w})
in
\widetilde{Y}
if
\mathbf{w}^{\ell}\to\mathbf{w}
in
\widetilde{X}
as
\ell\to\infty
. Note that most of the terms of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
are similar to
[
3
]
, and the main differences are the terms corresponding to
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
. In order to prove the continuity, we take an arbitrary sequence
\{\mathbf{w}^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\widetilde{X}
such that
\mathbf{w}^{\ell}\to\mathbf{w}
in
\widetilde{X}
, as
\ell\to\infty
, which implies
\mathbf{u}^{\ell}\to\mathbf{u}
in
W_{0,\sigma}^{1,2}
, and then we investigate the continuity of
\mathcal{T}_{k+1}
in terms of
\mathbf{u}^{\ell}\in W_{0,\sigma}^{1,2}
for fixed
\phi_{k}
and
\mathbb{B}_{k}
. It follows from Theorem
3.2
and (
3.21
) that
\mathbb{B}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{B}
in
L^{2}(\Omega\times I_{k+1})
and
\nabla\mathbb{B}^{\ell}\to\nabla\mathbb{B}
in
L^{2}(\Omega\times I_{k+1})
. By applying a similar argument as in Section
3.7
, one is able to show the positive definiteness of
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}^{\ell}\to\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}
a.e. in
\Omega\times I_{k+1}
(up to a non-relabeled subsequence). Then, with the uniform boundedness of
\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}^{\ell}
in
L^{2}(\Omega\times I_{k+1})
, one concludes that
\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}^{\ell}\to\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}
strongly in
L^{1}(\Omega\times I_{k+1})
in view of Vitali’s convergence theorem. Hence,
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}^{\ell}-\ln\mathbb{B}^{\ell}-\mathbb{I})\to\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})
in
L^{1}(\Omega\times I_{k+1})
, which finally implies the continuity of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
.

Application of a fixed-point theorem.

The final step is to employ the Leray–Schauder principle on
\widetilde{Y}
, for which we denote
\mathcal{K}_{k}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{k}\circ\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{k}:\widetilde{Y}\rightarrow\widetilde{Y}
, rewrite (
4.20
) as

\mathbf{f}-\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mathbf{f})=0\quad\text{for }\mathbf{f}=\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{w}),

and find a fixed point of
\mathcal{K}_{k}
. Note that
\mathcal{K}_{k}
is a compact operator because
\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{k}
is compact and
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is continuous. Now we are in a position to apply the Leray–Schauder principle (see, e.g.,
[
57
, Lemma 3.1.1, Chapter II]
) to find a fixed point. Namely, we will show that

(4.21)

\exists R>0\text{ such that }\left\|\mathbf{f}\right\|_{\widetilde{Y}}\leq R,\text{ for }\mathbf{f}\in\widetilde{Y}\text{ fulfilling }\mathbf{f}=\lambda\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mathbf{f})\text{ for some }0\leq\lambda\leq 1.

Here,
\lambda
is a constant used for the proof and is unrelated to the relaxation time of the model (
1.1
). By the definition
\mathbf{w}=\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{k}(\mathbf{f})
, one may see that
\mathbf{f}=\lambda\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mathbf{f})
in (
4.21
) is equivalent to

(4.22)

\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{w})-\lambda\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{w})=0\quad\text{for some }0\leq\lambda\leq 1.

The rest of this subsection is devoted to the estimate of
\mathbf{w}
in
\widetilde{X}
satisfying (
4.22
), which thereby gives the estimate of
\mathbf{f}
in
\widetilde{Y}
by the fact that
\mathcal{F}_{k}:\widetilde{X}\rightarrow\widetilde{Y}
is bounded. The subsequent argument is rather lengthy but is very similar to
[
3
]
. Thus, we point out the main differences associated with
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
in the following. For the reader’s convenience, we give the equivalent weak formulation corresponding to (
4.22
) as follows.

(4.23a)

\displaystyle\lambda\left\langle\frac{\rho\mathbf{u}-\rho_{k}\mathbf{u}_{k}}{h},\mathbf{v}\right\rangle+\lambda\left\langle\Div(\rho_{k}\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{v}\right\rangle+\lambda\left\langle\Div(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{J}),\mathbf{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\left\langle\nu(\phi_{k})(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{u}),\nabla\mathbf{v}\right\rangle-\lambda\left\langle\Div\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\big[\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{k+1}-\mathbb{I})\big],\mathbf{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda\left\langle q\nabla\phi_{k},\mathbf{v}\right\rangle+\lambda\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\left\langle\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\Big[\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})}{2}\nabla\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\ln\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\mathbb{I})\Big],\mathbf{v}\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t,

for all
\mathbf{v}\in W_{0,\sigma}^{1,2}(\Omega)
,
and for a.e.

,

(4.23b)

\displaystyle\lambda\frac{\phi-\phi_{k}}{h}+\lambda\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\phi_{k}=\Div(m(\phi_{k})\nabla q),

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lambda q+\lambda\omega\frac{\phi+\phi_{k}}{2}+\lambda\phi&=\partial\widetilde{E}(\phi)+\phi+\lambda\eta\frac{\phi-\phi_{k}}{h}+\lambda G(\mathbf{w},\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}).\end{aligned}

By the analogous test procedure as for the energy estimate (also in
[
3
]
), that is, testing (
4.23a
) with
\mathbf{u}

4.23b
) with

and (

, we derive similar estimates. The main difference here compared to
[
3
]
is that we have extra terms associated with
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
, which in light of (
4.17
) can be canceled, as

\displaystyle\quad\lambda\int_{I_{k+1}}\int_{\Omega}\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\mathbb{I}):\nabla\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t-\lambda\int_{I_{k+1}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})}{2}\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\ln\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\mathbb{I})\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\int_{I_{k+1}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mathscr{R}_{\eta}(\phi-\phi_{k})}{2h}\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi)-\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})}{\mathscr{R}_{\eta}(\phi-\phi_{k})}\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\ln\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\mathbb{I})(t)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi)}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\ln\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}-\mathbb{I})(t)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t-\lambda\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi_{k})}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{k}-\ln\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{k}-\mathbb{I})\,\mathrm{d}x

Then we reach the estimate

\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{\widetilde{X}}+\left\|\partial\widetilde{E}(\phi)\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{k},

for fixed

, and hence

\left\|\mathbf{f}\right\|_{\widetilde{Y}}=\left\|\lambda\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{w})\right\|_{\widetilde{Y}}\leq C_{k}(\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{\widetilde{X}}+1)\leq C_{k},

which finishes the proof of Proposition
4.9
by the Leray–Schauder principle.

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}_{\eta}[\mu_{\eta}(\phi^{\eta})(\mathbb{B}^{\eta}-\mathbb{I})]

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{2}(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{2\times 2})),\ \forall\,2<p<\infty,

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}_{\eta}\Big[\frac{\mu_{\eta}(\phi^{\eta})}{2}\nabla\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}^{\eta}-\ln\mathbb{B}^{\eta}-\mathbb{I})\Big]

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})),

which means the all terms above are bounded in
L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{\frac{4}{3}}(\Omega))
(without specifying the dimensions). Then in (
4.8
), the test function can be restricted to

\mathbf{w},\nabla\mathbf{w}\in[L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{\frac{4}{3}}(\Omega))]^{\prime}=L^{4}(0,T;L^{4}(\Omega)).

Note that
\mathbf{w}
lies in the solenoidal space. Hence, with the help of the Leray projection
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big(\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\rho(\phi^{\eta})\mathbf{u}^{\eta})\big)

is bounded in

\displaystyle[L^{4}(0,T;W_{0,\sigma}^{1,4}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2}))]^{\prime}=L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;W_{\sigma}^{-1,4}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\rho(\phi^{\eta})\mathbf{u}^{\eta})

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})),

which together with the Aubin–Lions lemma implies the strong convergence

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\rho(\phi^{\eta})\mathbf{u}^{\eta})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\rho(\phi)\mathbf{u})},\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L_{\sigma}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})),

for some function
\overline{\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\rho(\phi)\mathbf{u})}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L_{\sigma}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2}))
. Analogously to Section
4.5
, we identify
\overline{\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\rho(\phi)\mathbf{u})}=\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\rho(\phi)\mathbf{u})
. Indeed, as
\rho(\phi^{\eta})\mathbf{u}^{\eta}\rightarrow\rho(\phi)\mathbf{u}\text{ weakly}\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2}))
, and by virtue of the weak continuity of the Leray projection
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}:L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2}))\rightarrow L^{2}(0,T;L_{\sigma}^{2}(\Omega))
, we obtain

\overline{\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\rho(\phi)\mathbf{u})}=\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\rho(\phi)\mathbf{u}).

Once again, we prove the strong convergence of
\mathbf{u}^{\eta}
to
\mathbf{u}
through the convergence of the kinetic energy. Namely,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\rho(\phi^{\eta})\left|\mathbf{u}^{\eta}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\rho(\phi^{\eta})\mathbf{u}^{\eta})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\eta}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\rightarrow\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\rho(\phi)\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\rho(\phi)\left|\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,

from which we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\rho(\phi^{\eta})}\mathbf{u}^{\eta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\sqrt{\rho(\phi)}\mathbf{u},\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})).

Because
\rho(r)
is affine regarding
r
, one gets

\displaystyle\rho(\phi^{\eta})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\rho(\phi),\quad

a.e.

\displaystyle\text{in }Q_{T}\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\rho(\phi^{\eta})\right|\geq C>0.

Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\eta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho(\phi^{\eta})}}\sqrt{\rho(\phi^{\eta})}\mathbf{u}^{\eta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho(\phi)}}\sqrt{\rho(\phi)}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u},\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})),

and hence

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\eta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbf{u},\quad

a.e.

\displaystyle\text{in }Q_{T}.

With all the compactness above, one can pass to the limit in (
4.8
) to (
1.6
) as
\eta\rightarrow 0
, combining with

\displaystyle m(\phi^{\eta})

\displaystyle\rightarrow m(\phi),\quad

a.e.

\displaystyle\text{in }Q_{T},

\displaystyle\nu(\phi^{\eta})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\nu(\phi),\quad

a.e.

\displaystyle\text{in }Q_{T},

\displaystyle\mu(\phi^{\eta})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mu(\phi),\quad

a.e.

\displaystyle\text{in }Q_{T},

which can be deduced by means of the continuity of
m(\cdot),\nu(\cdot),\mu(\cdot)
and the almost everywhere convergence of
\phi^{\eta}
. Similarly, we use integration by parts and the compactness of
\phi^{\eta}
to handle the term
\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}q^{\eta}\nabla\phi^{\eta}\cdot\mathbf{w}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t
.

In the final step of the proof, we derive the energy dissipation inequality (
1.9
). To this end, multiplying the differential inequality (
4.5
) by a function
\varsigma\in W^{1,1}(0,T)
with
\varsigma\geq 0
,
\varsigma(T)=0
, and integrating the resulting inequality over
(0,T)
and by parts with respect to the time variable, one obtains

(5.5)

\mathcal{E}_{\eta}(0)\varsigma(0)+\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{E}_{\eta}(\tau)\varsigma(\tau)^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\geq\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{D}_{\eta}(\tau)\varsigma(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

where
\mathcal{E}_{\eta}(t)
is given in (
4.6
) and, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\eta}(t)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\nu(\phi^{\eta})}{2}

\displaystyle\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\eta}+\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{u}^{\eta}\right|^{2}+m(\phi^{\eta})\left|\nabla q^{\eta}\right|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mu(\phi^{\eta})}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}^{\eta}+(\mathbb{B}^{\eta})^{-1}-2\mathbb{I})\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\kappa}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}^{\eta}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Thanks to the compactness of
\mathbf{u}^{\eta}
,
\phi^{\eta}
, we deduce that up to a subsequence (not relabeled),

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}^{\eta}(t)&\rightarrow\mathbf{u}(t),\quad&&\text{in }L_{\sigma}^{2}(\Omega),\\
\phi^{\eta}(t)&\rightarrow\phi(t),\quad&&\text{in }C(\overline{\Omega}),\\
\mathbb{B}^{\eta}(t)&\rightarrow\mathbb{B}(t),\quad&&\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{2\times 2}),\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}^{\eta}(t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}(t)\,\mathrm{d}x,

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, where the last statement holds true by Vitali’s convergence theorem in view of the pointwise convergence of
\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}^{\eta}
and the uniform boundedness in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
. Therefore, it comes up with

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{E}(t),\quad

\displaystyle\text{a.e.~in }(0,T),

where
\mathcal{E}(t)
is defined in (
1.2
). In view of the lower semicontinuity of norms
and the almost everywhere convergence of
\phi^{\eta}
to
\phi
, one has

\liminf_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{D}_{\eta}(\tau)\varsigma(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\geq\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{D}(\tau)\varsigma(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

for any
\varsigma\in W^{1,1}(0,T)
with
\varsigma\geq 0
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(t)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\nu(\phi)}{2}

\displaystyle\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}+m(\phi)\left|\nabla q\right|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mu(\phi)}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}+\mathbb{B}^{-1}-2\mathbb{I})\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\kappa}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathbb{B}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Note that here we employed the positivity of
m(\cdot)
,
\nu(\cdot)
and
\mu(\cdot)
, and

\liminf_{\eta\to 0}\int_{0}^{T}\eta\left\|\partial_{t}\phi^{\eta}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\varsigma(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\geq 0.

Passing to the limit in (
5.5
) as
\eta\rightarrow 0
yields

\mathcal{E}(0)\varsigma(0)+\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{E}(\tau)\varsigma^{\prime}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\geq\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{D}(\tau)\varsigma(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

for any
\varsigma\in W^{1,1}(0,T)
with
\varsigma\geq 0
and
\varsigma(T)=0
. By virtue of Lemma
2.2
, we get the desired energy dissipation inequality (
1.9
). The additional stronger estimate of
\mathbb{B}
can be obtained directly from (
5.1
) together with (
1.9
).

This finishes the proof.
∎

Appendix A
Derivation of the Model: Local Dissipation Laws

In this section, we provide the main arguments for a thermodynamically consistent derivation of the model (
1.1
). The general idea is to start from physical balance laws in a closed, isothermal system.
After that, we state phenomenological assumptions such that the system fulfills the second law of thermodynamics. At that point, there are some general frameworks that can be used for the constitutive assumptions, such as the
Local Dissipation Inequality and
the Lagrange Multiplier Approach
developed by Liu
[
45
]
, the
Onsager’s variational principle

[
53
]
or the
General Equation for Non-Equilibrium Reversible-Irreversible Coupling
(GENERIC) framework of Gmerla–Öttinger
[
28
,
29
]
. We also refer to
[
25
,
49
]
. In our case, we will follow
[
6
]
and use the
Local Dissipation Inequality and
the Lagrange Multiplier Approach
.

Following
[
6
]
one can derive the following balance law of mass in local form

(A.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\Div(\phi\mathbf{u})+\Div\mathbf{J}_{\phi}=0,

where
\phi
is the difference of volume fractions,
\mathbf{J}_{\phi}
is a diffusive flux and
\mathbf{u}
is the volume-averaged velocity satisfying the incompressibility condition

(A.2)

\displaystyle\Div\mathbf{u}=0.

The balance law of linear momentum reads

(A.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{u})+\Div(\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})=\Div\tilde{\mathbf{T}},

where
\rho=\hat{\rho}(\phi)=\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\rho}_{2}(1+\phi)+\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\rho}_{1}(1-\phi)
is the variable mass density of the mixture and
\tilde{\mathbf{T}}
is the full stress tensor of the system, which is symmetric due to the balance law of angular momentum. Here,
\overline{\rho}_{1},\overline{\rho}_{2}
denote the constant mass densities of the pure phases of the mixture. For simplicity, we assume that no external forces are present.
We consider the deformation gradient
\mathbb{F}
of the mixture, given by

(A.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{F}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{F}=\nabla\mathbf{u}\mathbb{F}.

For the derivation for the Oldroyd-B model with relaxation, we assume a virtual multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient into one part capturing the irreversible, dissipative processes and another part for the total elastic response of the material, i.e.,

(A.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{e}\mathbb{F}_{d},

also see
[
49
]
. Then, the left Cauchy–Green tensor associated with the elastic part of the total mechanical response
\mathbb{B}\coloneqq\mathbb{F}_{e}\mathbb{F}_{e}^{\top}
is the sought quantity for our model.
Introducing the tensorial quantity
\mathbb{L}_{d}\coloneqq(\partial_{t}\mathbb{F}_{d}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{F}_{d})\mathbb{F}_{d}^{-1}
, one can obtain with a simple calculation, using (
A.4
) and (
A.5
),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{F}_{e}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{F}_{e}=\nabla\mathbf{u}\mathbb{F}_{e}-\mathbb{F}_{e}\mathbb{L}_{d},

which gives for
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{F}_{e}\mathbb{F}^{\top}_{e}

(A.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{B}=\nabla\mathbf{u}\mathbb{B}+\mathbb{B}\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{u}-\mathbb{F}_{e}(\mathbb{L}_{d}+\mathbb{L}_{d}^{\top})\mathbb{F}_{e}^{\top}.

Later, the dependence on
\mathbb{D}_{d}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{L}_{d}+\mathbb{L}_{d}^{\top})
will be removed with a constitutive relation.

Assuming a general energy density of the form

\displaystyle e=\hat{e}(\phi,\nabla\phi,\mathbb{B})+\frac{1}{2}\rho(\phi)\left|\mathbf{u}\right|^{2},

composed of a (general) free energy density and the kinetic energy density of the system, the second law of thermodynamics for a closed physical system in the isothermal case gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{V(t)}e(\phi,\nabla\phi,\mathbf{u},\mathbb{B})\,\mathrm{d}x\leq-\int_{\partial V(t)}\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}+\int_{\partial V(t)}(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}\mathbf{n})\cdot\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1},

where
V(t)\subset\Omega
is an arbitrary test volume transported by the fluid velocity,
\mathbf{n}
is the outer unit normal to
\partial V(t)
and
\mathbf{J}_{e}
is a dissipative energy flux yet to be determined. Roughly speaking, the change of the total energy in a test volume
V(t)
cannot exceed the
change of energy due to diffusion and the working due to macroscopic stresses. With the help of the divergence theorem and Reynold’s transport theorem, and as the test volume
V(t)\subset\Omega
is arbitrary, one can obtain a local inequality for the dissipation by

\displaystyle-\mathcal{D}\coloneqq(\partial_{t}^{\bullet}e+e\Div\mathbf{u}+\Div\mathbf{J}_{e})-\Div(\tilde{\mathbf{T}})\cdot\mathbf{u}-\tilde{\mathbf{T}}:\nabla\mathbf{u}-\lambda_{\phi}(\partial_{t}^{\bullet}\phi+\phi\Div\mathbf{u}+\Div\mathbf{J}_{\phi})\leq 0,

where
\partial_{t}^{\bullet}\coloneqq\partial_{t}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)
denotes the material derivative and
\lambda_{\phi}
is a Lagrange multiplier for the balance law of mass (
A.1
) for the order parameter
\phi
.
Note that in general the unknowns
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{J}_{e},\tilde{\mathbf{T}},\phi,\mathbf{J}_{\phi},\lambda_{\phi}
(and their derivatives appearing in the local dissipation inequality) can attain arbitrary values for a given point in space and time.
It can be checked with a straightforward computation, using (
A.2
), (
A.3
), (
A.6
) and many reformulations (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Section 2.2]
for the AGG part and, e.g.,
[
49
, Section 4.4]
for the viscoelastic part) that this local dissipation inequality holds true if the following constitutive assumptions are applied:

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{e}=\lambda_{\phi}\mathbf{J}_{\phi}-\partial_{t}^{\bullet}\phi\frac{\partial\hat{e}}{\partial\nabla e}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{J}\left|\mathbf{u}\right|^{2},\quad\mathbf{J}=\rho^{\prime}(\phi)\mathbf{J}_{\phi},\quad\mathbf{J}_{\phi}=-m(\phi)\nabla\lambda_{\phi},

\displaystyle\lambda_{\phi}=\frac{\partial\hat{e}}{\partial\phi}-\Div\frac{\partial\hat{e}}{\partial\nabla\phi},\quad\mathbb{D}_{d}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}(\phi)}\mathbb{F}_{e}^{-1}\frac{\partial\hat{e}}{\partial\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{F}_{e},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{T}}=\mathbb{S}-p\mathbb{I}-(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{J})-\frac{\partial\hat{e}}{\partial\nabla\phi}\otimes\nabla\phi,\quad\mathbb{S}=\nu(\phi)(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{u})+2\frac{\partial\hat{e}}{\partial\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B},

where
m(\phi),\tilde{\lambda}(\phi),\nu(\phi)
are positive functions corresponding to a mobility, a relaxation and a viscosity, respectively.
Here, also a relative mass flux
\mathbf{J}
and the viscoelastic stress tensor
\mathbb{S}
were introduced, also see
[
6
,
51
]
.

The constitutive system of equations (with a general energy density) reads

(A.7a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}(\rho(\phi)\mathbf{u})+\Div(\rho(\phi)\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})&+\Div(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{J})+\nabla p\\
-\Div\big(\mathbb{S}(\nabla\mathbf{u},\mathbb{B},\phi)\big)&=-\Div\Big(\frac{\partial\hat{e}}{\partial\nabla\phi}\otimes\nabla\phi\Big),\end{split}

(A.7b)

\displaystyle\Div\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0,

(A.7c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{B}+\frac{2}{\tilde{\lambda}(\phi)}\frac{\partial\hat{e}}{\partial\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{B}\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}\mathbb{B},

(A.7d)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\phi

\displaystyle=\Div(m(\phi)\nabla q),

(A.7e)

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\hat{e}}{\partial\phi}-\Div\frac{\partial\hat{e}}{\partial\nabla\phi}\,.

Here, the local dissipation is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\frac{\nu(\phi)}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla^{\top}\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}+m(\phi)\left|\nabla q\right|^{2}+\frac{2}{\tilde{\lambda}(\phi)}\left|\mathbb{F}_{e}^{\top}\frac{\partial\hat{e}}{\partial\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{F}_{e}\right|^{2}\geq 0.

We note that the system (
1.1
) can be recovered from (
A.7
) with the specific choice of the free energy density

\displaystyle\hat{e}(\phi,\nabla\phi,\mathbb{B})=\frac{\tilde{\sigma}\epsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\phi\right|^{2}+\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{\epsilon}W(\phi)+\frac{\mu(\phi)}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{B}-\ln\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I}),

and a rescaling of the relaxation function
\tilde{\lambda}(\phi)=\lambda(\phi)\mu(\phi)/\alpha(\phi)
.

Appendix B
Compactness of Time-Averaged Terms

In this section, we give three technical lemmata, which are of much significance for the limit passage concerning time-averaged terms, in particular, the compactness of these terms. First, we give a uniform boundedness and compactness property.

Lemma B.1

(Compactness in
L^{p}
)
.

Let
h=T/N
with
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in L^{p}(0,T;X)
be a bounded function for
p\in(1,\infty)
, where
X
is a Banach space.
Define
\bar{f}^{N}(t)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
for
t\in I_{k+1}
as a piecewise-in-time constant function in
(0,T)
. Then
\{\bar{f}^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{p}(0,T;X)
. Moreover, it holds
\bar{f}^{N}\to f
in
L^{p}(0,T;X)
, as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

By virtue of (
4.24
) and Jensen’s inequality for the time integral (as
p\in(1,\infty)
), one knows that

\displaystyle\left\|\bar{f}^{N}\right\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X)}^{p}=\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\bar{f}^{N}(s)\right\|_{X}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=h\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left\|\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right\|_{X}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq h\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Big(\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\left\|f(s)\right\|_{X}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)^{p}

\displaystyle\leq h\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\left\|f(s)\right\|_{X}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{T}\left\|f(s)\right\|_{X}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq C,

where
C>0
is independent of
h>0
(and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, respectively). The second part of the lemma follows directly from the uniform boundedness and the Banach–Steinhaus theorem, as the convergence result holds true for any
f\in C^{\infty}([0,T];X)\subset L^{p}(0,T;X)
densely, i.e., for any
f\in C^{\infty}([0,T];X)
, it holds

\displaystyle\left\|\bar{f}^{N}-f\right\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X)}^{p}=\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\bar{f}^{N}(s)-f(s)\right\|_{X}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\int_{I_{k+1}}\left\|\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s-f(t)\right\|_{X}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\int_{I_{k+1}}\left\|\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\big(f(s)-f(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}s\right\|_{X}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq Ch\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\left\|\partial_{t}f(t)\right\|_{X}^{p}\to 0,

as
h\to 0
.
∎

Remark B.2
.

The first part of the lemma also holds true for
f
substituted by a uniformly bounded sequence
\{f^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
in
L^{p}(0,T;X)
, that is, the sequence
\{\bar{f}^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
with definition
\bar{f}^{N}(t)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}f^{N}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
for
t\in I_{k+1}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{p}(0,T;X)
.

Now concerning the weakly convergent sequences, we introduce the following lemma for the time-averaged functions.

Lemma B.3

(Weak compactness)
.

Let
h=T/N
with
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\{f^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{2}(Q_{T})
be a sequence satisfying
f^{N}\to f
weakly in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
, as
N\to\infty
(resp.
h\to 0
), with
f\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
. Defining a piecewise-in-time constant function
\bar{f}^{N}(t)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}f^{N}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
for
t\in I_{k+1}
, we have for any function
\varphi\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
independent of
h
,

\int_{Q_{T}}\bar{f}^{N}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\to\int_{Q_{T}}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,\text{ as }N\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Define
\bar{\varphi}^{N}(t)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\varphi(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
for
t\in I_{k+1}
. In light of Lemma
B.1
,
\bar{\varphi}^{N}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
and it holds

\bar{\varphi}^{N}\rightarrow\varphi,\text{ strongly in }L^{2}(Q_{T}).

With the help of (
4.24
) and Fubini’s theorem, we then have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\bar{f}^{N}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\int_{I_{k+1}}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\Big(\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}f^{N}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)\widetilde{\varphi}_{k+1}(t)\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}f^{N}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)\Big(\int_{I_{k+1}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{k+1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\int_{I_{k+1}}f^{N}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{k+1}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{k+1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{T}f^{N}(s)\bar{\varphi}^{N}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,

as
N\to\infty
, where the last convergence holds true for weakly convergent
f^{N}
and strongly convergent
\bar{\varphi}^{N}
.
∎

We now write the time-averaged terms as a convolution with a Dirac sequence. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
h=T/N
. We define
\zeta^{N}(t)=\frac{1}{h}\chi_{(-\frac{h}{2},\frac{h}{2})}(t)
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\chi_{I}(\cdot)
denotes the characteristic function on a given interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
. Let
X
be a Banach space and
p\in[1,\infty)
. Then, for
f\in L^{p}(0,T;X)
and
t\in(\tfrac{h}{2},T-\tfrac{h}{2})
, we rewrite the time average over the interval
(t-\tfrac{h}{2},t+\tfrac{h}{2})
as a convolution with
\zeta^{N}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle(\zeta^{N}*f)(t)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{N}(t-s)f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{h}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{(t-\tfrac{h}{2},t+\tfrac{h}{2})}(s)f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{h}\int_{t-\tfrac{h}{2}}^{t+\tfrac{h}{2}}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Here, the function
f
is naturally extended with a zero outside of
(0,T)
. We note some properties from, e.g.,
[
9
, Theorems 4.13 and 4.15]
, that we will use:

•

\{(\zeta^{N}*f)\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{p}(0,T;X)
with
(\zeta^{N}*f)\to f
in
L^{p}(0,T;X)
, as
N\to\infty
.

•

For
f,g\in L^{1}(0,T;X)
, it holds
\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\zeta^{N}*f)(t)g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)(\zeta^{N}*g)(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
.

Note that in fact the first one can be derived by the Lebesgue differentiation theorem for
p=1
and additionally with Jensen’s inequality for
1<p<\infty
.
The second one is a direct consequence, since
\zeta^{N}
is an even function by construction.

Regarding the weak-
*
convergence, we have the following lemma.

Lemma B.4

(Weak-
*
compactness)
.

Let
h=T/N
with
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, let
f^{N}\in L^{\infty}(-h,T)
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
, with
f^{N}\to f
weakly-
*
in
L^{\infty}(0,T)
, as
N\to\infty
(resp.
h\to 0
).
Then, for all
\varsigma\in L^{1}(0,T)
, it holds as
N\to\infty
(resp.
h\to 0
),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\frac{1}{h}\int_{t-h}^{t}f^{N}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)\varsigma(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\to\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\varsigma(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

To prove the lemma, we rewrite the inner integral with the help of the Dirac sequence defined above with a shift of
\tfrac{h}{2}
. Then one applies the associativity of the convolution with the Dirac sequence, the second property above, to move the convolution to the test function
\varsigma\in L^{1}(0,T)
. Finally, combining with the weak-
*
convergence of
f^{N}
to
f
in
L^{\infty}(0,T)
and the first property above, we finish the proof.
Namely,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\frac{1}{h}\int_{t-h}^{t}f^{N}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)\varsigma(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\zeta^{N}*f^{N})(t)\varsigma(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{N}(t)(\zeta^{N}*\varsigma)(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\varsigma(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\varsigma(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

as
N\to\infty
(resp.
h\to 0
).
∎

Remark B.5
.

In principle, it is possible to generalize Lemmata
B.3
and
B.4
for a broader class of Lebesgue spaces. However, we only make use of these special cases in this work.

We also note a compactness criterion with time translations, which can be found in, e.g.,
[
56
, Section 8, Theorem 5]
.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty]
and let
X,Y,Z
be Banach spaces with
X\subset\subset Y
compactly and
Y\subset Z
continuously. Moreover, let
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{p}(0,T;X)
be a bounded set and
\left\|f(\cdot+h)-f\right\|_{L^{p}(0,T-h;Z)}\to 0
uniformly for
f\in\mathcal{F}
, as
h\to 0
. Then,
\mathcal{F}
is relatively compact in
L^{p}(0,T;Y)
if
p\in[1,\infty)
, and in
C([0,T];Y)
if
p=\infty
, respectively.
