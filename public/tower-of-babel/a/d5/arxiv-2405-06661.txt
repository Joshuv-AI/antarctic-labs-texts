On the image of the total power operation for Burnside rings
Nathan Cornelius, Lewis Dominguez, David Mehrle, Lakshay Modi, Millie Rose, Nathaniel Stapleton

Abstract
. We prove that the image of the total power operation for Burnside rings A ⁡ ( G ) → A ⁡ ( G ≀ Σ n ) A(G)\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n}) lies inside a relatively small, combinatorial subring Å ⁡ ( G , n ) ⊆ A ⁡ ( G ≀ Σ n ) \AA(G,n)\subseteq A(G\wr\Sigma_{n}) . As n n varies, the subrings Å ⁡ ( G , n ) \AA(G,n) assemble into a commutative graded ring Å ⁡ ( G ) \AA(G) with a universal property: Å ⁡ ( G ) \AA(G) carries the universal family of power operations out of A ⁡ ( G ) A(G) . We construct character maps for Å ⁡ ( G , n ) \AA(G,n) and give a formula for the character of the total power operation. Using Å ⁡ ( G ) \AA(G) , we extend the Frobenius–Wielandt homomorphism of [ DSY92 ] to wreath products compatibly with the total power operation. Finally, we prove a generalization of Burnside’s orbit counting lemma that describes the transfer map A ⁡ ( G ≀ Σ n ) → A ⁡ ( Σ n ) A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})\to A(\Sigma_{n}) on the subring Å ⁡ ( G , n ) \AA(G,n) .



1.
Introduction

This paper explores consequences of the isomorphism of
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets

(G/H)^{\times n}\cong(G\wr\Sigma_{n})/(H\wr\Sigma_{n}),

which gives great control over the total power operation for the Burnside ring of
G

\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon A(G)\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n}).

The total power operation sends the isomorphism class of a finite
G
-set
X
to the isomorphism class of the
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-set
X^{\times n}
. The smallest additive summand of
A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
generated by elements of the canonical basis and containing the image of the total power operation is a relatively small, combinatorial subring
\AA(G,n)\subseteq A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
.
With an understanding of this combinatorial structure, we prove that
\AA(G)=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\AA(G,n)
inherits a commutative graded ring structure from the transfer product on
\bigoplus_{n}A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
.
Additionally, we show that the construction
\AA(G)
is functorial in the underlying abelian group of
A(G)
.
With this in hand, we

A.)

Show that
\AA(G)
carries the universal family of power operations out of
A(G)
.

B.)

Describe a character map for
\AA(G,n)
.

C.)

Give a formula for the character of
\mathbb{P}_{n}
.

D.)

Explicitly describe how any map of abelian groups
A(H)\to A(G)
induces a map
\AA(H)\to\AA(G)
and give a formula for the character of this map.

This last item gives us the ability to extend the Frobenius–Wielandt map of
[
DSY92
, Theorem 1]
to the Burnside ring of the wreath product and also to give an elegant formula, in the spirit of Burnside’s orbit counting lemma, for the transfer map
A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})\to A(\Sigma_{n})
along the quotient
G\wr\Sigma_{n}\to\Sigma_{n}
after restriction to
\AA(G,n)\subseteq A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
.

To elaborate, let
G
be a finite group and let
A(G)
be the Burnside ring of
G
.
This is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank equal to the number of isomorphism classes of transitive
G
-sets.
The Burnside ring admits a “character map” landing in
\Marks(G)=\Fun(\Conj(G),\mathbb{Z})
, where
\Conj(G)
denotes the set of conjugacy classes of subgroups of
G
.
The character map is an isomorphism after rationalization.
The total power operation is a multiplicative map

\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon A(G)\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})

induced by taking a
G
-set
X
to the
n
-fold Cartesian product
X^{\times n}
equipped with the usual
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-action.

Let
\AA(G,n)\subseteq A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
be as defined above. It turns out that this subset of
A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
can be described in many more ways.
We provide three further descriptions here.

A finite
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-set
Y
is called submissive if
Y
admits an equivariant embedding into
X^{\times n}
for some
G
-set
X
.
The coproduct and product of submissive sets are submissive. In particular, we prove the following description of
\AA(G,n)
:

Proposition
(
Proposition 3.3
).

The ring
\AA(G,n)
is the Grothendieck ring of isomorphism classes of submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets.

The ring
\AA(G,n)
can also be described using the integer partitions of
n
decorated by the set
\Conj(G)
.
Such a partition of
n
is a function

\lambda\colon\Conj(G)\times\mathbb{N}_{>0}\to\mathbb{N}

such that
\sum_{[H],m}\lambda_{[H],m}\cdot m=n
.
The quantity
\lambda_{[H],m}
counts the number of occurrences in the partition
\lambda
of the natural number
m
decorated by the conjugacy class
[H]\in\Conj(G)
.
Let
\Parts(G,n)
be the set of integer partitions of
n
decorated by
\Conj(G)
and
\Parks(G,n)
be the ring of integer valued functions on
\Parts(G,n)
.

There is a ring map
\beta^{*}\colon\Parks(G,n)\to\Marks(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
induced by a canonical map
\beta\colon\Conj(G\wr\Sigma_{n})\to\Parts(G,n)
.

Proposition
(
Corollary 13.6
).

There is a pullback square of commutative rings

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\AA(G,n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Parks(G,n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Marks(G\wr\Sigma_{n}),}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta^{*}}

where

is the character map.

This provides
\AA(G,n)
with a character map
\AA(G,n)\to\Parks(G,n)
that is an isomorphism after rationalization.

For the third description, let
A
be an abelian group and let
R=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}R_{n}
be a commutative
\mathbb{N}
-graded ring.
An
R
-valued family of power operations on
A
is a family of functions
(P_{n}\colon A\to R_{n})_{n\geq 0}
with the property that
P_{0}
is the constant function
1
,
P_{n}(0)=0
for
n>0
, and

\displaystyle P_{n}(x+y)=\sum_{i+j=n}P_{i}(x)P_{j}(y).

Let
\Pops(A,R)
be the set of
R
-valued families of power operations on
A
.
This assignment is functorial in both
A
and
R
.
As a functor of
R
, it is corepresentable by a commutative graded ring
\Pow(A)
.
Using these functors, we produce the following isomorphism:

Proposition
(
Corollary 6.7
).

There is a canonical isomorphism
\Pow(A(G))\cong\bigoplus_{n}\AA(G,n)
of commutative graded rings.

To explain this isomorphism further, recall that the direct sum of abelian group
A(G\wr\Sigma)=\bigoplus_{n}A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
acquires a commutative graded ring structure induced by the “transfer multiplication”

X\star Y=(G\wr\Sigma_{n})\times_{G\wr\Sigma_{i}\times G\wr\Sigma_{j}}(X\times Y)

for
i+j=n
,
X
a
G\wr\Sigma_{i}
-set, and
Y
a
G\wr\Sigma_{j}
-set.
The family of power operations
(\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon A(G)\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n}))_{n\geq 0}
gives rise to a map of commutative graded rings
\Pow(A(G))\to A(G\wr\Sigma)
.
This map is injective with image
\AA(G)=\bigoplus_{n}\AA(G,n)
.

Similarly, one can consider
RU(G\wr\Sigma)=\bigoplus_{n}RU(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
. In this case, work of Zelevensky
[
Zel81
]
can be used to show that the family of power operations
(\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon RU(G)\to RU(G\wr\Sigma_{n}))
gives rise to an isomorphism of commutative graded rings
\Pow(RU(G))\xrightarrow{\cong}RU(G\wr\Sigma)
.

A map of abelian groups
F\colon A(H)\to A(G)
induces a map

\mathring{F}\colon\AA(H,n)\cong\Pow(A(H))_{n}\to\Pow(A(G))_{n}\cong\AA(G,n).

Rationalization induces a linear map
\mathbb{Q}\otimes F\colon\Marks(H,\mathbb{Q})\to\Marks(G,\mathbb{Q})
using the character isomorphism
\mathbb{Q}\otimes A(G)\cong\Marks(G,\mathbb{Q})
, where
\Marks(G,\mathbb{Q})
denotes
\mathbb{Q}\otimes\Marks(G)
.
Similarly, rationalization induces a linear map
\mathbb{Q}\otimes\mathring{F}\colon\Parks(H,n,\mathbb{Q})\to\Parks(G,n,\mathbb{Q})
, where
\Parks(G,n,\mathbb{Q})
denotes
\mathbb{Q}\otimes\Parks(G,n)
.

Theorem
(
Theorem 14.2
).

Let
F\colon A(H)\to A(G)
be a map of abelian groups, let
M
be the matrix representing
\mathbb{Q}\otimes F\colon\Marks(H,\mathbb{Q})\to\Marks(G,\mathbb{Q})
in the bases consisting of conjugacy classes of subgroups, and let
\mathring{M}
be the matrix representing
\mathbb{Q}\otimes\mathring{F}\colon\Parks(H,n,\mathbb{Q})\to\Parks(G,n,\mathbb{Q})
in the bases consisting of decorated partitions of
n
.
We give an explicit formula for the coefficients of
\mathring{M}
in terms of the coefficients of
M
.

Note that this theorem applies to any additive map – not just to restriction and transfer maps.
Particularly interesting examples to consider are the transfer map along
G\to e
and the Frobenius–Wielandt map.

Let
m=|G|
. The Frobenius–Wielandt map is a ring homomorphism
A(C_{m})\to A(G)
, where
C_{m}
is the cyclic group of order
m
. It is induced by the map
\Marks(C_{m})\to\Marks(G)
coming from the function
\Conj(G)\to\Conj(C_{m})
sending a conjugacy class of subgroups
[H]
to the unique subgroup of
C_{m}
of order
|H|
.
Notably, the Frobenius–Wielandt map is not, in general, induced by a group homomorphism.

Theorem 14.2
together with the map
\beta
mentioned above, allows us to extend the Frobenius–Wielandt map to wreath products:

Proposition
(
Corollary 18.2
).

Let
m=|G|
and
n>0
. We construct a Frobenius–Wielandt map

w_{n}\colon A(C_{m}\wr\Sigma_{n})\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})

compatible with the usual Frobenius–Wielandt map
A(C_{m})\to A(G)
through the total power operation.

With this in hand, we explore the relationship between power operations and work of Romero
[
Rom20
]
that gives a group-theoretic condition under which the Frobenius–Wielandt map commutes with norms.

2.1.
Burnside rings

Let
G
be a finite group.
Let
\Fin_{G}
denote the category of finite
G
-sets and let
A^{+}(G)=\pi_{0}{\Fin_{G}}^{\cong}
denote the set of isomorphism classes of finite
G
-sets.
We denote the isomorphism class of a finite
G
-set
X
by
[X]\in A^{+}(G)
.
The coproduct (disjoint union) and product of finite
G
-sets make
A^{+}(G)
into a commutative semiring.

The Burnside ring
A(G)
is the Grothendieck ring of
A^{+}(G)
.
As a
\mathbb{Z}
-module,
A(G)
has a canonical basis consisting of isomorphism classes of transitive finite
G
-sets.
In this basis, the multiplication is determined by the double coset formula

G/H\times G/K\cong\coprod_{HgK\in H\backslash G/\penalty K}G/(H^{g}\cap K),

where
H^{g}
denotes the conjugation
g^{-1}Hg
.

2.2.
Restriction and transfer

Given a group homomorphism
\varphi\colon H\to G
, there are functors

\Res_{\varphi}\colon\Fin_{G}\to\Fin_{H}\text{ and }\Tr_{\varphi}\colon\Fin_{H}\to\Fin_{G}

respectively called restriction and transfer. Restriction is induced by restricting the
G
-action on a
G
-set to
H
along
\varphi
, and transfer is induced by sending an
H
-set
X
to the
G
-set
G\times_{H}X
. In the literature, restriction is often called inflation when
\varphi
is surjective and transfer is often called induction when
\varphi
is injective and deflation when
\varphi
is surjective.

These functors induce maps

\Res_{\varphi}\colon A(G)\to A(H)\text{ and }\Tr_{\varphi}\colon A(H)\to A(G).

Restriction is a ring map and transfer is an
A(G)
-module map for the action of
A(G)
on
A(H)
through the restriction map.

2.3.
External products

Given a finite
G
-set
X
and a finite
H
-set
Y
, the Cartesian product
X\times Y
is a finite
G\times H
-set.
To avoid ambiguity, we denote this operation by
X\boxtimes Y
, which we call the external product.
The external product induces a functor

\boxtimes\colon\Fin_{G}\times\Fin_{H}\to\Fin_{G\times H}

and a homomorphism

\boxtimes\colon A(G)\otimes A(H)\to A(G\times H).

2.4.
Wreath products

We write
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
for the wreath product, which is the semidirect product
G^{\times n}\rtimes\Sigma_{n}
, where
\Sigma_{n}
acts on
G^{\times n}
on the left via permutations.
Explicitly, for
\bar{g}=(\bar{g}_{1},\ldots,\bar{g}_{n})\in G^{\times n}
and
\tau\in\Sigma_{n}
, we write
\tau\bar{g}
for the element in
G^{\times n}
with
(\tau\bar{g})_{i}=\bar{g}_{\tau^{-1}(i)}
.
Then the multiplication in
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
is given by
(\bar{g},\sigma)(\bar{h},\tau)=(\bar{g}\,\sigma\bar{h},\sigma\tau)
.
If
G
acts on the left on a space
X
, then
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
acts on
X^{\times n}
on the left. Explicitly, we have

((\bar{g},\sigma)x)_{i}=\bar{g}_{i}x_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}.

2.5.
Finite sets and orders

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
[n]
denote a set of size
n
.
We identify the symmetric group
\Sigma_{n}
with the group
\Sigma_{[n]}=\Aut([n])
of bijections of the set
[n]
.
When
i+j=n
, any identification
[i]\amalg[j]\cong[n]
induces an embedding
\Sigma_{i}\times\Sigma_{j}\hookrightarrow\Sigma_{n}
.
Each of these embeddings is conjugate, and we will only use these maps to induce operations on Burnside rings or similar conjugacy invariant objects, so the choice of embedding is immaterial.
More generally, for
X
some set and functions
m,f\colon X\to\mathbb{N}
where
f
has finite support, we will consider embeddings of the form

\prod_{x\in X}\Sigma_{m(x)}^{\times f(x)}\hookrightarrow\Sigma_{n}\text{ where }n=\sum_{x\in X}f(x)m(x).

Such embeddings arise from choosing any identification
\coprod_{x\in X}[f(x)]\times[m(x)]\cong[n]
.
Again, all such embeddings are conjugate, so the particular choice is immaterial.
These identifications also induce embeddings of the form

G\wr\Sigma_{i}\times G\wr\Sigma_{j}\hookrightarrow G\wr\Sigma_{n}\text{ and }\prod_{x\in X}(G\wr\Sigma_{m(x)})^{\times f(x)}\hookrightarrow G\wr\Sigma_{n},

each of which is conjugate to any other.

Similarly, for a set
X
, we will identify
X^{\times n}
with
X^{[n]}=\prod_{[n]}X
.
Any identification
[i]\amalg[j]\cong[n]
induces a bijection
X^{\times i}\times X^{\times j}\cong X^{\times n}
.
Unfortunately, the distinction between these bijections often matters:
sometimes it is necessary that the
X^{\times i}
factor appear ‘first.’
Accordingly, we implicitly choose total orders on the sets
[n]
, and we put the lexicographic ordering on products and disjoint unions.
In this situation, there is exactly one order preserving isomorphism
[i]\amalg[j]\cong[n]
, and we use this one to induce ‘the’ bijection
X^{\times i}\times X^{\times j}\cong X^{\times n}
.
Similarly, when an arbitrary total order is selected on
X
, there is exactly one order preserving isomorphism
\coprod_{x\in X}[f(x)]\times[m(x)]\cong[n]
.

We use these choices to induce ‘the’ embeddings

\Sigma_{i}\times\Sigma_{j}\subseteq\Sigma_{n}\text{, }\prod_{x\in X}\Sigma_{m(x)}^{\times f(x)}\subseteq\Sigma_{n}\text{, }G\wr\Sigma_{i}\times G\wr\Sigma_{j}\subseteq\Sigma_{n}\text{, and }\prod_{x\in X}(G\wr\Sigma_{m(x)})^{\times f(x)}\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n},

when such a particular choice is desired.

2.6.
Transfer products

Let
n=i+j
.
Given a finite
G\wr\Sigma_{i}
-set
X
and a finite
G\wr\Sigma_{j}
-set
Y
, we will be interested in the induced
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-set

X\star Y=\Tr_{\varphi}(X\boxtimes Y),

where
\varphi
is the embedding
G\wr\Sigma_{i}\times G\wr\Sigma_{j}\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
.
To avoid clutter, we suppress the groups from the notation and write
\Tr_{i,j}^{n}
for the transfer along
\varphi
.
This determines a functor

\star\colon\Fin_{G\wr\Sigma_{i}}\times\Fin_{G\wr\Sigma_{j}}\to\Fin_{G\wr\Sigma_{n}}

and a homomorphism

\star\colon A(G\wr\Sigma_{i})\otimes A(G\wr\Sigma_{j})\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n}),

which we call the transfer product.

2.7.
The marks homomorphism

Let
\Conj(G)
denote the set of conjugacy classes of subgroups of
G
.
The conjugacy class of a subgroup
H\subseteq G
is denoted by
[H]
. There is a canonical bijection between the set
\Conj(G)
and the set of isomorphism classes of transitive
G
-sets sending the conjugacy class
[H]
to the isomorphism class
[G/H]
.

For a commutative ring
R
, let
\Marks(G,R)
be the ring of
R
-valued functions on the set
\Conj(G)
.
If
R
is omitted, it is assumed to be
\mathbb{Z}
. This is often referred to as the “ghost ring” in the literature.
The ring
\Marks(G,R)
has an
R
-linear basis given by the indicator functions

\mathbbm{1}_{[H]}\colon[K]\mapsto\begin{cases}1,&[H]=[K]\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for
[H]\in\Conj(G)
.

The marks homomorphism, which we also call the character map, is the ring homomorphism

\chi=\chi_{G}\colon A(G)\to\Marks(G)

that sends a
G
-set
X
to the function on
\Conj(G)
given by cardinalities of fixed point sets

\chi([X])\colon[H]\mapsto|X^{H}|.

The marks homomorphism is injective and an isomorphism after rationalization. In other words,

\mathbb{Q}\otimes\chi\colon\mathbb{Q}\otimes A(G)\to\mathbb{Q}\otimes\Marks(G)\cong\Marks(G,\mathbb{Q})

is an isomorphism of commutative rings.

2.9.
Formula for restriction on marks

To give a formula for the restriction along
\varphi\colon H\to G
on marks, consider the induced function

\varphi\colon\Conj(H)\to\Conj(G)\colon[K]\mapsto[\varphi(K)].

Precomposition with this function gives the restriction:

\Res_{\varphi}(f)([K])=f(\varphi[K])=f([\varphi(K)])

for
f\in\Marks(G,\mathbb{Q})
and
[K]\in\Conj(H)
.
In fact, this formula defines restrictions

\Res_{\varphi}\colon\Marks(G,R)\to\Marks(H,R)

for any coefficient ring
R
.

2.10.
Formula for transfer on marks

The formula for the transfer map on marks is more complicated.
Along a subgroup inclusion
H\subseteq G
, the formula is given by

\Tr_{H\subseteq G}(f)([K])=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}gH\in G/H\\
K^{g}\subseteq H\end{subarray}}f([K^{g}])

for
f\in\Marks(H)
and
[K]\in\Conj(G)
.
This defines transfers

\Tr_{H\subseteq G}\colon\Marks(H,R)\to\Marks(G,R)

for any coefficient ring
R
.
At the other extreme, when
\varphi\colon G\twoheadrightarrow e
is a surjection onto the trivial group, Burnside’s orbit counting lemma gives the beautiful formula

\Tr_{G\twoheadrightarrow e}(f)([e])=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}f([\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle g}}\right\rangle]),

for
f\in\Marks(G,\mathbb{Q})
, where
[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle g}}\right\rangle]
denotes the conjugacy class of the cyclic subgroup generated by
g\in G
.
Applied to
\chi([X])
for a finite
G
-set
X
, the formula demonstrates that the number of orbits for the
G
-action on
X
is equal to the average of the number of fixed points of
X
by the elements of
G
.
More general transfer formulas can be found in
[
Bou10
, Chapter 5]
.

2.11.
Formula for external product on marks

To give a formula for the external product on marks, note that for a finite
G
-set
X
, a finite
H
-set
Y
, and a subgroup
K\subseteq G\times H
,

(X\times Y)^{K}\cong X^{\pi_{1}K}\times Y^{\pi_{2}K},

where the

From this, we conclude that

for

,
g\in\Marks(H,\mathbb{Q})
, and

.
In fact, this formula defines external products

\boxtimes\colon\Marks(G,R)\otimes\Marks(H,R)\to\Marks(G\times H,R)

for any coefficient ring
R
.

2.12.
Formula for the transfer product on marks

Let
n=i+j
.
The formula for the transfer product on marks is given by

(f\star g)([H])=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma(\Sigma_{i}\times\Sigma_{j})\in\Sigma_{n}/(\Sigma_{i}\times\Sigma_{j})\\
H^{\sigma}\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{i}\times G\wr\Sigma_{j}\end{subarray}}f(\pi_{[i]}[H^{\sigma}])g(\pi_{[j]}[H^{\sigma}])

for
f\in\Marks(G\wr\Sigma_{i},\mathbb{Q})
,
g\in\Marks(G\wr\Sigma_{j},\mathbb{Q})
, and
[H]\in\Conj(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
where
\pi_{[i]}\colon\Sigma_{[i]}\times\Sigma_{[j]}\to\Sigma_{[i]}
denotes the projection and similarly for
\pi_{[j]}
.

In fact, since this formula is integral, it defines transfer products

\star\colon\Marks(G\wr\Sigma_{i},R)\otimes\Marks(G\wr\Sigma_{j},R)\to\Marks(G\wr\Sigma_{n},R)

for any coefficient ring
R
.

To see the correctness of the formula, notice that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma(\Sigma_{i}\times\Sigma_{j})\in\Sigma_{n}/(\Sigma_{i}\times\Sigma_{j})\\
H^{\sigma}\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{i}\times G\wr\Sigma_{j}\end{subarray}}f(\pi_{[i]}[H^{\sigma}])g(\pi_{[j]}[H^{\sigma}])=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\bar{g},\sigma)(G\wr\Sigma_{i}\times G\wr\Sigma_{j})\in(G\wr\Sigma_{n})/(G\wr\Sigma_{i}\times G\wr\Sigma_{j})\\
H^{(\bar{g},\sigma)}\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{i}\times G\wr\Sigma_{j}\end{subarray}}f(\pi_{[i]}[H^{(\bar{g},\sigma)}])g(\pi_{[j]}[H^{(\bar{g},\sigma)}])

using the bijection
\Sigma_{n}/(\Sigma_{i}\times\Sigma_{j})\cong(G\wr\Sigma_{n})/(G\wr\Sigma_{i}\times G\wr\Sigma_{j})
and that the latter expression is exactly the composition of the formulas for the external product and for
\Tr_{i,j}^{n}
.

2.13.

Define

2.15.
Power operations

For
n\geq 0
, the total power operation

\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon A^{+}(G)\to A^{+}(G\wr\Sigma_{n})

is defined by
\mathbb{P}_{n}([X])=[X^{\times n}]
for
X
a finite
G
-set.
These maps are multiplicative and satisfy an additivity relation in terms of the transfer product

\mathbb{P}_{n}(X+Y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=n\\
i,j\geq 0\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{i}(X)\star\mathbb{P}_{j}(Y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=n\\
i,j\geq 0\end{subarray}}\Tr_{i,j}^{n}(\mathbb{P}_{i}(X)\boxtimes\mathbb{P}_{j}(Y)).

The operation
\mathbb{P}_{0}
is the constant function
1
, and
\mathbb{P}_{n}(0)=0
for
n>0
.
The total power operation extends to a map

\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon A(G)\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})

by enforcing the above additivity relation.
Specifically,
\mathbb{P}_{n}(X-Y)
may be computed inductively by using that

\mathbb{P}_{n}(X)=\mathbb{P}_{n}((X-Y)+Y)=\mathbb{P}_{n}(X-Y)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=n\\
i\geq 0,\,j>0\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{i}(X-Y)\star\mathbb{P}_{j}(Y).

A closed form is given in
Corollary 12.2
.

This ring map is unusual since it is not induced by restriction along a group homomorphism.

sending a conjugacy class

of order

This induces a ring map

In

3.
The summand
\AA(G,n)\subseteq A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\AA(G,n)\subseteq A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
be the summand spanned by the smallest subset of the canonical basis whose span contains the image of the total power operation
\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon A(G)\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
.

In this paper, we give many equivalent descriptions of

.
We begin with a description of

as a Grothendieck ring.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that a finite
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-set

is submissive if it admits a
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-equivariant injection into a
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-set of the form
Y^{\times n}
for some
G
-set
Y
.

The disjoint union and product of submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets are submissive:
If
X_{1}\hookrightarrow{Y_{1}}^{\times n}
and
X_{2}\hookrightarrow{Y_{2}}^{\times n}
, then
X_{1}\amalg X_{2}\hookrightarrow(Y_{1}\amalg Y_{2})^{\times n}
and
X_{1}\times X_{2}\hookrightarrow(Y_{1}\times Y_{2})^{\times n}
, and it is clear that the empty union
\emptyset
and empty product
\ast
are submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets.
Thus, we may consider the Grothendieck ring of (isomorphism classes of) submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets.

Any transitive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-subset of a submissive set is also submissive, so the category of submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets is generated under finite coproducts by the transitive submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets in the same way that the category of finite
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets is generated under finite coproducts by the transitive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets.
Thus the Grothendieck ring of submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets is freely generated as an abelian group by the isomorphism classes of transitive submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets.
In this sense, we may consider the Grothendieck ring of submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets as a subring of
A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The Grothendieck ring of submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets is the summand
\AA(G,n)\subseteq A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
.

Proof.

First we show that the Grothendieck ring of submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets is contained in
\AA(G,n)
.
This follows from the fact that if
X\hookrightarrow Y^{\times n}
is a submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-set, each of the transitive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-subsets of
X
are also subsets of
Y^{\times n}
.
Since
\mathbb{P}_{n}([Y])=[Y^{\times n}]
is in the image of the total power operation, each of these transitives is in
\AA(G,n)
, and thus
[X]
is in
\AA(G,n)
.

For the other inclusion, note that if
[(G\wr\Sigma_{n})/K]
appears in the basis decomposition of
\mathbb{P}_{n}([Y])
, then
(G\wr\Sigma_{n})/K
injects into

and so
(G\wr\Sigma_{n})/K
is a submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-set.
Thus, we see that the Grothendieck ring of submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets contains the basis members required to express the image of the power operation
A^{+}(G)\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
.
Further, this is enough to express the image of the larger power operation

because each of the transitives appearing in the basis decomposition of

appear in the basis decomposition of

for large enough

.
Although the proof of this fact is too technical to appear here, we prove it as

using the language of partitions and compositions to manipulate the relation describing power operations applied to sums.
Alternatively, one may use the graded ring structure of

together with

to complete the proof.

4.
Decorated partitions

Partitions, i.e. formal sums of positive integers, are important in the study of symmetric groups.
In this paper, we will make use of
X
-decorated partitions, where
X
is a set. These are sometimes called colored partitions.
An
X
-decorated partition is a formal sum of pairs in
X\times\mathbb{N}_{+}
.
Such a pair is called a part.
A part is interpreted as its integer component, called the size of the part, ‘decorated’ by the
X
component.
The classical theory of integer partitions is recovered when
X=*
is a singleton.

We write the collection of
X
-decorated partitions as
P(X)=\mathbb{N}\{X\times\mathbb{N}_{+}\}
.
We identify this collection with finite support functions

\lambda\colon X\times\mathbb{N}_{+}\to\mathbb{N}\colon x,m\mapsto\lambda_{x,m}.

The finite support function
\lambda
is identified with the formal sum

\lambda=\sum_{x\in X,\,m\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}\lambda_{x,m}\cdot(x,m)

containing
\lambda_{x,m}
-many parts of size
m
and decoration
x
.
When
\lambda\in P(*)
is an integer partition, we write
\lambda_{m}
instead of
\lambda_{*,m}
.
In this situation, it is important to note that
\lambda_{m}
denotes the number parts of size
m
in the partition and not the
m
th formal summand (in some chosen ordering).
Decorated partitions will be generally denoted by the lowercase Greek letters
\kappa,\lambda,\mu
or less frequently
\gamma,\delta,\varepsilon
.

We generalize the structure (and notation) of integer partitions to decorated partitions.
Formal summation gives
P(X)
the structure of a free commutative monoid

\kappa+\lambda\colon x,m\mapsto\kappa_{x,m}+\lambda_{x,m}.

The identity of this monoid is the empty partition 0.
The number of parts in a partition
\lambda
is called its length and is denoted

|\lambda|=\sum_{x,m}\lambda_{x,m}.

The sum of the sizes of the parts in a partition
\lambda
is called its size and is denoted

\|\lambda\|=\sum_{x,m}\lambda_{x,m}\cdot m.

A partition of size
n
may also be called a partition of
n
.
If
X
is finite, there are finitely many
X
-decorated partitions of size
n
.
Length and size are additive so that
|\kappa+\lambda|=|\kappa|+|\lambda|
and
\|\kappa+\lambda\|=\|\kappa\|+\|\lambda\|
.
The size map
P(X)\to\mathbb{N}
gives
P(X)
the structure of an augmented monoid.

We also define the factorial and multifactorial which are respectively given by

\lambda!=\prod_{x,m}\lambda_{x,m}!\text{ and }(\!(\lambda)\!)!=\prod_{x,m}(m!)^{\lambda_{x,m}}.

The multifactorial is exponential so that
(\!(\kappa+\lambda)\!)!=(\!(\kappa)\!)!\cdot(\!(\lambda)\!)!
, but there is no such relationship for the factorial.
Given a function
f\colon X\to Y
, there is a pushforward map
f_{*}\colon P(X)\to P(Y)
which corresponds to a fiber sum of finite support functions

f_{*}\lambda\colon y,m\mapsto\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(y)}\lambda_{x,m}.

The pushforward
f_{*}
preserves addition, length, size, and the multifactorial, and it preserves the factorial when
f
is injective.

Of particular importance in this paper are partitions decorated by
\Conj(G)
. Recall that
\Conj(G)
denotes the conjugacy classes of subgroups of
G
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\Parts(G)=P(\Conj(G))
denote the set of
\Conj(G)
-decorated partitions.
The set of
\Conj(G)
-decorated partitions of size
n
is denoted by
\Parts(G,n)
.

In this notation
\Parts(G)=\coprod_{n}\Parts(G,n)
.
We identify
\Parts(e)
with the set of integer partitions

.

(5.9)

\mathbb{P}_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}}}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}+\ldots+i_{m}=n\\
i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\mathop{\vphantom{\sum}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{524296}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}}\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}_{i_{k}}(X_{k}).

It follows that
[(G\wr\Sigma_{n})/\alpha(\lambda)]
appears as a summand in

\mathbb{P}_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{[H],m}\lambda_{[H],m}\cdot[G/H]}}\right)

and hence is the isomorphism class of a submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-set.
By
Proposition 3.3
, this demonstrates that the image of
\mathbb{Z}[\Parts(G)]\hookrightarrow A(G\wr\Sigma)
is contained in
\AA(G)
.

For the reverse inclusion, note that if each of the
X_{k}
’s in (
5.9
) is taken to be an isomorphism class of a transitive
G
-set, then each of the summands in the formula is of the form
[(G\wr\Sigma_{n})/\alpha(\lambda)]
for some
\lambda
.
Thus,
\mathbb{Z}[\Parts(G)]\hookrightarrow A(G\wr\Sigma)
contains all the basis elements necessary for it to contain the image of
\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon A^{+}(G)\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
and thus the image of
\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon A(G)\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
by
Corollary 12.3
.
This demonstrates that the image of
\mathbb{Z}[\Parts(G)]\hookrightarrow A(G\wr\Sigma)
contains
\AA(G)
, which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.10
.

The commutative graded ring
\AA(G)
is canonically isomorphic to the graded monoid ring
\mathbb{Z}[\Parts(G)]
.

Corollary 5.11
.

The subgroup
\AA(G,n)\subseteq A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
is the subgroup generated by the isomorphism classes of transitive

-sets of the form

for

Corollary 5.12
.

The commutative graded ring

\mathbb{N}
-graded polynomial ring with generators

in degree

.

Proof.

This is immediate from the fact that a monoid ring on a free commutative monoid is a polynomial ring and from the correspondence between
([H],n)\in\Parts(G,n)
and
[(G/H)^{\times n}]\in\AA(G,n)
.
∎

Corollary 5.13
.

The ring
\AA(G)
is the smallest subring of

containing

for all

.

Proof.

Use
Corollary 5.12
together with the fact that containing the image of

∎

Corollary 5.14
.

The summand
\AA(G,n)

6.
A universal family of power operations

In this section, we provide a description of
\AA(G)=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\AA(G,n)
as an object carrying a universal family of power operations out of
A(G)
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
(A,+)
be an abelian group and
R=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}R_{n}
be a commutative graded ring.
An
R
-valued family of power operations on
A
will refer to a collection of functions
P_{n}\colon A\to R_{n}
for
n\geq 0
such that
P_{0}(x)=1
for all
x\in A
,
P_{n}(0)=0
for
n>0
, and

\displaystyle P_{n}(x+y)=\sum_{i+j=n}P_{i}(x)P_{j}(y),

for all
x,y\in A
.
We denote the collection of
R
-valued families of power operations on
A
as
\Pops(A,R)
.

This construction assembles into a functor

\Pops\colon\Ab^{\textrm{op}}\times\CGrRing\to\Set,

where
\CGrRing
denotes the category of commutative graded rings (maps preserve the grading).

Proposition 6.2
.

The functor
\Pops
is corepresentable in the first variable.
That is, there is a functor

\Pow\colon\Ab\to\CGrRing

with a natural bijection

\Pops(A,R)\cong\CGrRing(\Pow(A),R).

Proof.

This is best seen in terms of generators and relations.
Let
\Pow(A)
be the commutative graded ring generated in degree
n
by elements of the form
P_{n}(a)
for each
a\in A
subject to the relations
P_{0}(a)=1
,
P_{n}(0)=0
for
n>0
, and

\displaystyle P_{n}(x+y)=\sum_{i+j=n}P_{i}(x)P_{j}(y).

By construction, we have a natural isomorphism
\Pops(A,R)\cong\CGrRing(\Pow(A),R)
.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

If
f\colon A\to B
is a surjective homomorphism of abelian groups, the induced map
\Pow(f)\colon\Pow(A)\to\Pow(B)
is surjective.

Proof.

The map
\Pow(f)
surjects onto the generators of
\Pow(B)
in the above description.
∎

Let
R
be a commutative graded ring and let
\widehat{R}=\prod_{n\geq 0}R_{n}
denote the completion of
R
at the system of ideals
R_{\geq n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We refer to
\widehat{R}
as the formal completion of
R
.
The formal completion
\widehat{R}
inherits a ring structure from
R
.
The addition is component wise, and the multiplication is given by

(rs)_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=n\\
i,j\geq 0\end{subarray}}r_{i}s_{j}.

This is motivated by identifying
r\in\widehat{R}
with the infinite sum
\sum_{n\geq 0}r_{n}
.
The units of the formal completion are exactly those
r\in\widehat{R}
such that
r_{0}
is a unit in
R_{0}
.
The corresponding inversion formula for elements of this form is identical to the inversion formula for formal power series.
A unit is called strict when
r_{0}=1
.
Let
\widehat{R}^{s}
denote the group of strict units i.e. the group

\widehat{R}^{s}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{r\in\widehat{R}\mid r_{0}=1}}\right\}.

Proposition 6.4
.

The functor
\Pops(A,R)
is representable in the second variable by the group of strict units in the formal completion of
R
.
That is, there is a natural bijection

\Pops(A,R)\cong\Ab(A,\widehat{R}^{s}).

Proof.

For some
R
-valued family of power operations on
A
and each
a\in A
, the sequence
(P_{n}(a))_{n\geq 0}\in\widehat{R}
is a strict unit.
The axioms of the power operations exactly correspond to this being a homomorphism
A\to\widehat{R}^{s}
.
Thus,
\Pops(A,R)\cong\Ab(A,\widehat{R}^{s})
.
∎

Corollary 6.5
.

Any graded subring of
A(G\wr\Sigma)
containing the image of
\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon A^{+}(G)\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
for all
n
automatically contains the image of image of
\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon A(G)\to A(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
for all
n
.

Proof.

There is a monoid homomorphism

A^{+}(G)\to\widehat{A(G\wr\Sigma)}^{s}\colon[X]\mapsto(\mathbb{P}_{n}([X])_{n\geq 0}

and any monoid homomorphism from
A^{+}(G)
to a group extends to a group homomorphism from
A(G)
.
∎

Recall from
Section 4
that
P(X)
denotes the set of partitions decorated by
X
, which becomes a free commutative monoid under formal addition of decorated partitions.

Corollary 6.6
.

Let
X
be a set and let
\mathbb{Z}\{X\}
be the free abelian group on
X
.
Then there are canonical isomorphisms of commutative graded rings

\Pow(\mathbb{Z}\{X\})\cong\mathbb{Z}[(x,n)\mid x\in X,\,n>0,\,\deg(x,n)=n]\cong\mathbb{Z}[P(X)].

Proof.

The second isomorphism, between the polynomial ring and the monoid ring, follows from the definition of
P(X)
as a free commutative monoid.
For the first isomorphism, we see that
\Pow(\mathbb{Z}\{X\})
and the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[(x,n)]
have the same universal property since they both represent functions from
X
into the group of strict units.
Explicitly, a map
f\colon\mathbb{Z}[(x,n)]\to R
is uniquely determined by elements
f(x,n)\in R_{n}
for
x\in X
and
n>0
, which is the data of a function
X\to\widehat{R}^{s}
.
∎

Corollary 6.7
.

There is a canonical isomorphism of commutative graded rings

\Pow(A(G))\cong\AA(G).

Proof.

Proposition 5.7
shows that
\AA(G)
is canonically isomorphic to the graded monoid ring
\mathbb{Z}[\Parts(G)]
. The corollary then follows immediately from
Corollary 6.6
with
X=\Conj(G)
.
∎

Definition 6.8
.

Given an abelian group homomorphism
F\colon A(H)\to A(G)
, let

\mathring{F}\colon\AA(H)\to\AA(G)

denote the map induced by
Corollary 6.7
and
\Pow(F)
.

7.
Burnside rings and representation rings

Let
RU(G)
be the complex representation ring of
G
and let
RU(G\wr\Sigma)=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}RU(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
. This object was thoroughly studied by Zelevinsky in
[
Zel81
]
.

There is a commutative graded ring structure on
RU(G\wr\Sigma)
by making use of a transfer multiplication defined similarly to
\star
.
The
RU(G\wr\Sigma)
-valued family of power operations
(P_{n}\colon RU(G)\to RU(G\wr\Sigma_{n}))_{n\geq 0}
given by
P_{n}([V])=[V^{\otimes n}]
induces a map of commutative graded rings
\Pow(RU(G))\to RU(G\wr\Sigma)
.
We will show that this map is an isomorphism.
There are at least two ways to do this.
One is to prove results similar to
Section 5
, replacing the set of isomorphism classes of transitive
G
-sets with the set of isomorphism classes of irreducible
G
-representations.
Another is to make use of the “transfer ideal.”
For completeness, we will take the second approach because it also provides another way to prove
Corollary 6.7
.
This result follows easily from the work of Zelevinsky, but we include a complete proof with no claim to originality.

Let
(R,\star)
be a commutative
\mathbb{N}
-graded ring. Let
I_{n}\subseteq R_{n}
denote the image of the sum of the multiplication maps from lower degrees

\star\colon\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=n\\
i,j>0\end{subarray}}R_{i}\otimes R_{j}\to R_{n}.

Equivalently, let
I=(R_{>0})^{\star 2}
denote the square of the irrelevant ideal
R_{>0}=\bigoplus_{n>0}R_{n}\subseteq R
and let
I_{n}
be the
n
-th graded piece of
I
.

Proposition 7.1
.

The canonical map of commutative graded rings

\Pow(RU(G))\to RU(G\wr\Sigma)

is an isomorphism.

Proof.

We prove this by induction on the degree.

We have a map of exact sequences

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\displaystyle\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=n\\
i,j>0\end{subarray}}\Pow(RU(G))_{i}\otimes\Pow(RU(G))_{j}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Pow(RU(G))_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Pow(RU(G))_{n}/I_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\displaystyle\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=n\\
i,j>0\end{subarray}}RU(G\wr\Sigma_{i})\otimes RU(G\wr\Sigma_{j})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces RU(G\wr\Sigma_{n})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces RU(G\wr\Sigma_{n})/I_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}

By induction on
n
, the left vertical map is an isomorphism.
For the right vertical map,
Corollary 6.6
implies that
\Pow(RU(G))_{n}/I_{n}\cong RU(G)
and
[
Zel81
, Lemma 1.7 and Section 7.2]
implies that
RU(G\wr\Sigma_{n})/I_{n}\cong RU(G)
.
By construction, the right map sends
P_{n}(V)
to
[V^{\otimes n}]
, which is an isomorphism.
This implies that the middle map is surjective, but since both abelian groups are free of the same rank, the middle map is an isomorphism.
∎

At this stage, we can give a generalization of the following well-known result
[
Ful97
, Section 7.3]
. Consider the composite of ring maps

\AA(e,n)\to A(\Sigma_{n})\to RU(\Sigma_{n}),

where
RU(\Sigma_{n})
is the complex representation ring of
\Sigma_{n}
and
A(\Sigma_{n})\to RU(\Sigma_{n})
is the linearization map taking a finite
\Sigma_{n}
-set to the associated permutation representation.
This map turns out to be an isomorphism.
That is, the linearization map
A(\Sigma_{n})\to RU(\Sigma_{n})
splits as a map of commutative rings.

The ring
\AA(e,n)
has been studied by others.
In particular, in
[
OTY16
]
, Oda, Takegahara, and Yoshida refer to it as the partial Burnside ring relative to the Young subgroups of the symmetric group.
In this sense, the subgroups
H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
such that
(G\wr\Sigma_{n})/H
is submissive are a generalization of the notion of a Young subgroup of
\Sigma_{n}
to
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
.

Corollary 7.2
.

Assume the linearization map
A(G)\to RU(G)
is surjective (resp. an isomorphism), then the induced map
\AA(G,n)\to RU(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
is surjective (resp. an isomorphism). In particular, the composites

\AA(e,n)\to A(\Sigma_{n})\to RU(\Sigma_{n})

and

\AA(\Sigma_{2},n)\to A(\Sigma_{2}\wr\Sigma_{n})\to RU(\Sigma_{2}\wr\Sigma_{n})

are isomorphisms.

Proof.

This follows immediately from
Corollary 6.3
together with the fact that the linearization maps
A(e)\to RU(e)
and
A(\Sigma_{2})\to RU(\Sigma_{2})
are isomorphisms.
∎

8.
\Parks(G)

We seek to produce a kind of character map for
\AA(G)
analogous to the character map
A(G)\to\Marks(G)
.
In this section we construct and study the target ring.

Definition 8.1
.

For a finite group
G
and a commutative ring
R
, let
\Parks(G,R)
denote the abelian group of finite support functions
\Parts(G)\to R
.
We give
\Parks(G,R)
an
\mathbb{N}
-grading by declaring the homogeneous elements of degree
n
to be those functions supported on partitions of size
n
.
We identify the graded pieces with the abelian groups
\Parks(G,n,R)=\Fun(\Parts(G,n),R)
so that
\Parks(G,R)\cong\bigoplus_{n}\Parks(G,n,R)
.
When
R
is omitted, it is assumed to be
\mathbb{Z}
.

The graded ring
\Parks(G,R)
has an
R
-linear basis given by the indicator functions

\mathbbm{1}_{\lambda}\colon\kappa\mapsto\begin{cases}1,&\lambda=\kappa\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

of homogeneous degree
\|\lambda\|
for
\lambda\in\Parts(G)
.

Proposition 8.2
.

Define a graded multiplication
\star\colon\Parks(G,R)\otimes\Parks(G,R)\to\Parks(G,R)
given by

(f\star g)(\mu)=\sum_{\kappa+\lambda=\mu}\frac{\mu!}{\kappa!\lambda!}f(\kappa)g(\lambda)

for
\mu\in\Parts(G)
.
This gives
\Parks(G,R)
the structure of a commutative graded ring.

Proof.

The commutativity and bilinearity of
\star
are clear.
That
\star
is graded follows from the fact that the size of partitions is additive.
The
\star
-unit is the indicator function of the empty partition
0\in\Parts(G,0)
.
To see that
\star
is associative, note that

\displaystyle(f\star(g\star h))(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma+\kappa=\lambda}\frac{\lambda!}{\gamma!\kappa!}f(\gamma)(g\star h)(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma+\kappa=\lambda}\frac{\lambda!}{\gamma!\kappa!}f(\gamma)\sum_{\delta+\varepsilon=\kappa}\frac{\kappa!}{\delta!\varepsilon!}g(\delta)h(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma+\delta+\varepsilon=\lambda}\frac{\lambda!}{\gamma!\delta!\varepsilon!}f(\gamma)g(\delta)h(\varepsilon)

and that
((f\star g)\star h)
expands to this same expression.
∎

Note that the graded pieces
\Parks(G,n,R)
are commutative rings, but this structure is not induced by the
\star
-product.

Next we produce a
\Parks(G,R)
-valued family of power operations

(\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon\Marks(G)\to\Parks(G,n))_{n\geq 0}.

Proposition 8.3
.

The maps
\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon\Marks(G)\to\Parks(G,n)
given by

\mathbb{P}_{n}(f)(\lambda)=\prod_{[H],m}f([H])^{\lambda_{[H],m}}

are a family of power operations.

Proof.

It is clear that
\mathbb{P}_{0}=1
and that
\mathbb{P}_{n}(0)=0
for
n>0
.
Thus, it suffices to check that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}(f+g)(\mu)

\displaystyle=\prod_{[H],m}((f+g)([H]))^{\mu_{[H],m}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{[H],m}(f([H])+g([H]))^{\mu_{[H],m}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{[H],m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{\kappa_{[H],m}+\lambda_{[H],m}=\mu_{[H],m}}\frac{\mu_{[H],m}!}{\kappa_{[H],m}!\lambda_{[H],m}!}f([H])^{\kappa_{[H],m}}g([H])^{\lambda_{[H],m}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\kappa+\lambda=\mu}\frac{\mu!}{\kappa!\lambda!}\mathbb{P}_{\|\kappa\|}(f)(\kappa)\mathbb{P}_{\|\lambda\|}(g)(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i+j=n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa+\lambda=\mu\\
\|\kappa\|=i,\,\|\lambda\|=j\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu!}{\kappa!\lambda!}\mathbb{P}_{i}(f)(\kappa)\mathbb{P}_{j}(g)(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i+j=n}\mathbb{P}_{i}(f)\star\mathbb{P}_{j}(g).\qed

Corollary 8.4
.

The maps
\mathbb{P}_{n}\colon A(G)\to\Parks(G,n)
given by

\mathbb{P}_{n}(X)(\lambda)=\prod_{[H],m}\chi(X)([H])^{\lambda_{[H],m}}

are a family of power operations.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that this formula is the formula of
Proposition 8.3
precomposed with the marks homomorphism.
∎

We can use this family of power operations along with
Proposition 6.2
to produce a map
\AA(G)\to\Parks(G)
.

Definition 8.5
.

Let

\chi=\chi_{G}\colon\AA(G)\to\Parks(G)

be the map obtained by composing the isomorphism
\AA(G)\cong\Pow(A(G))
of
Corollary 6.7
and the map
\Pow(A(G))\to\Parks(G)
induced by
Corollary 8.4
and
Proposition 6.2
.
We call this the character map or parks homomorphism in analogy to the terminology for the map
\chi\colon A(G)\to\Marks(G)
.

Alternatively,
\chi\colon\AA(G)\to\Parks(G)
is the composite of the isomorphism
\AA(G)\cong\Pow(A(G))
,
\Pow(\chi)\colon\Pow(A(G))\to\Pow(\Marks(G))
, and the map
\Pow(\Marks(G))\to\Parks(G)
induced by
Proposition 8.3
and
Proposition 6.2
.

On the polynomial generators of
Corollary 5.12
, the parks homomorphism is given by

\chi\colon\mathbb{P}_{n}([G/H])\in\AA(G,n)\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda\mapsto\prod_{[K],m}\chi([G/H])([K])^{\lambda_{[K],m}}}}\right)\in\Parks(G,n)

for a subgroup
H\subseteq G
and
n\geq 0
.

Although the parks homomorphism and the marks homomorphism
\AA(G)\to A(G\wr\Sigma)\to\Marks(G\wr\Sigma)
are written with the same symbol, it is usually easy to distinguish them in context; the marks homomorphism is evaluated at conjugacy classes of subgroups while the parks homomorphism is evaluated at decorated partitions.

The parks homomorphism
\chi\colon\AA(G)\to\Parks(G)
will be studied more thoroughly in
Section 13
.
In particular, we will show it is closely related to the marks homomorphism
\chi\colon A(G\wr\Sigma)\to\Marks(G\wr\Sigma)
.

9.
The retract
\beta

Recall the augmented monoid homomorphism
\alpha\colon\Parts(G)\to\Conj(G\wr\Sigma)
as described in
Section 5
.
In this section, we produce an augmented monoid retract

\beta\ \colon\Conj(G\wr\Sigma)\to\Parts(G)

of
\alpha
.

Consider a subgroup
H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
.
Choose a set
[n]
of size
n
and identify
\Sigma_{n}
with the group
\Sigma_{[n]}=\Aut([n])
of bijections of
[n]
as in
Subsection 2.5
.
For
i,j\in[n]
, define

H_{ij}=\{\bar{g}_{j}\mid(\bar{g},\sigma)\in H,\,\sigma\colon i\mapsto j\}\subseteq G.

Notice that these sets are subsets of
G
that satisfy compositional relations similar to that of a groupoid:

e\in H_{ii}\text{, }H_{jk}H_{ij}\subseteq H_{ik}\text{, and }H_{ij}^{-1}=H_{ji}.

To see the middle property, choose
(\bar{g},\sigma),(\bar{h},\tau)\in H
such that
\sigma\colon i\mapsto j
and
\tau\colon j\mapsto k
.
Then, the multiplication

(\bar{h},\tau)(\bar{g},\sigma)=(\bar{h}\tau\bar{g},\tau\sigma)

gives us

(\bar{h}\tau\bar{g})_{k}=\bar{h}_{k}\bar{g}_{\tau^{-1}(k)}=\bar{h}_{k}\bar{g}_{j}.

so that
H_{jk}H_{ij}\subseteq H_{ik}
.
The proofs of the other two properties are similar.
It follows that
H_{ii}
is a subgroup of
G
.

Notice that
H
has a left action on
[n]
via the quotient
G\wr\Sigma_{n}\twoheadrightarrow\Sigma_{n}
.
Accordingly, we write
H\backslash[n]
(read
H
under
[n]
) for the collection of orbits of
[n]
under the left action of
H
.
Elements of
H\backslash[n]
are of the form
Hi
for
i\in[n]
.
The set
H_{ij}
is nonempty if and only if
Hi=Hj
.

Definition 9.1
.

For a subgroup
H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
, choose
S\subseteq[n]
containing a unique representative of each orbit in
H\backslash[n]
(equivalently choose a section of the quotient map
[n]\to H\backslash[n]
).
Then, define

\beta([H])=\sum_{i\in S}([H_{ii}],|Hi|).

Once we show that this sum is independent of the choice of
S
, we may write

\beta([H])=\sum_{Hi\in H\backslash[n]}([H_{ii}],|Hi|)\in\Parts(G)

without reference to the choice
S
.

Proposition 9.2
.

The formula above is a well-defined homomorphism

\beta\colon\Conj(G\wr\Sigma)\to\Parts(G)

of augmented monoids, and
\beta
is a retract of
\alpha
.

Proof.

We begin by showing this definition is independent of the choice of
S
.
To see this, note that if
i
and
j
are in the same
H
-orbit, then we may choose
h\in H_{ij}
so that
hH_{ii}h^{-1}=H_{jj}
.

The fact that this definition is independent of the chosen representative
H
of the conjugacy class
[H]
follows from the behavior of the sets
H_{ij}
under conjugation by elements of
H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
.
First, note the following formula for conjugation in the wreath product:

(\bar{h},\tau)^{(\bar{g},\sigma)}=(\bar{g},\sigma)^{-1}(\bar{h},\tau)(\bar{g},\sigma)=(\sigma^{-1}(\bar{g}\,^{-1}\bar{h}\tau\bar{g}),\sigma^{-1}\tau\sigma).

If we write

\bar{h}^{(\bar{g},\sigma)}\text{ for }\sigma^{-1}(\bar{g}\,^{-1}\bar{h}\tau\bar{g})

then the conjugation formula is much simpler:

(\bar{h},\tau)^{(\bar{g},\sigma)}=(\bar{h}^{(\bar{g},\sigma)},\tau^{\sigma}).

This is justified by identifying
G^{\times n}
with the normal subgroup
G^{\times n}\trianglelefteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
and considering the conjugation action of
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
on this normal subgroup.
Additionally, note that

\bar{h}^{(\bar{g},\sigma)}{}_{i}={\bar{g}_{\sigma(i)}}^{-1}\bar{h}_{\sigma(i)}\bar{g}_{\tau^{-1}(\sigma(i))}

for
i\in[n]
.

With this in mind, consider
(\bar{g},\sigma)\in G\wr\Sigma_{n}
and compute that

\displaystyle{H^{(\bar{g},\sigma)}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\{\bar{h}_{j}\mid(\bar{h},\tau)\in H^{(\bar{g},\sigma)},\tau\colon i\mapsto j\}

\displaystyle=\{\bar{h}_{j}\mid(\bar{h},\tau)^{(\bar{g},\sigma)^{-1}}\in H,\tau\colon i\mapsto j\}

\displaystyle=\{\bar{h}^{(\bar{g},\sigma)}{}_{j}\mid(\bar{h},\tau)\in H,\tau^{\sigma}\colon i\mapsto j\}

\displaystyle=\{{\bar{g}_{\sigma(j)}}^{-1}\bar{h}_{\sigma(j)}\bar{g}_{\tau^{-1}(\sigma(j))}\mid(\bar{h},\tau)\in H,\tau\colon\sigma(i)\mapsto\sigma(j)\}

\displaystyle={\bar{g}_{\sigma(j)}}^{-1}\{\bar{h}_{\sigma(j)}\mid(\bar{h},\tau)\in H,\tau\colon\sigma(i)\mapsto\sigma(j)\}\bar{g}_{\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle={\bar{g}_{\sigma(j)}}^{-1}H_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}\bar{g}_{\sigma(i)}.

Now, the fact that the definition of
\beta
is independent of the chosen representative
H
of
[H]
follows from the fact that

{H^{(\bar{g},\sigma)}}_{ii}=\bar{g}_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}H_{\sigma(i)\sigma(i)}\bar{g}_{\sigma(i)}

and that if
S
is a set of orbit representatives for
H\backslash[n]
, then
\sigma^{-1}(S)
is a set of orbit representatives for
H^{(\bar{g},\sigma)}\backslash[n]
.
Thus, we have shown that
\beta
is well-defined.

The fact that
\beta
preserves the augmentation i.e. that
\|\beta([H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}])\|=n
follows from the fact that

\sum_{Hi\in H\backslash[n]}|Hi|=n.

To see that
\beta
is a monoid homomorphism, consider
[H]\in\Conj(G\wr\Sigma_{i})
and
[K]\in\Conj(G\wr\Sigma_{j})
where
n=i+j
.
Recall that
[H]\star[K]=[H\times K]\in\Conj(G\wr\Sigma_{n})
.
We may choose
[n]=[i]\amalg[j]
so that
(H\times K)\backslash[n]\cong(H\backslash[i])\amalg(K\backslash[j])
.
Additionally, we may recognize that

(H\times K)_{k\ell}=\begin{cases}H_{k\ell},&k,\ell\in[i]\\
K_{k\ell},&k,\ell\in[j]\\
\emptyset,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for
k,\ell\in[n]
.
Then,

\displaystyle\beta([H]\star[K])

\displaystyle=\sum_{(H\times K)k\in(H\times K)\backslash[n]}([(H\times K)_{kk}],|(H\times K)k|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{Hk\in H\backslash[i]}([H_{kk}],|Hk|)+\sum_{K\ell\in K\backslash[j]}([K_{\ell\ell}],|K\ell|)

\displaystyle=\beta([H])+\beta([K]).

To see that
\beta
is a retract of
\alpha
, consider some
\lambda\in\Parts(G,n)
.
As described in
Section 5
,
\alpha(\lambda)
is the conjugacy class

\alpha(\lambda)=\mathop{\vphantom{\sum}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{524296}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}}\displaylimits_{[H],m}[H\wr\Sigma_{m}]^{\star\lambda_{[H],m}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{[H],m}(H\wr\Sigma_{m})^{\times\lambda_{[H],m}}}}\right].

For the sake of this proof, let
[n]
be the set

\coprod_{[H],m}[\lambda_{[H],m}]\times[m]=\{([H],m,i,j)\mid[H]\in\Conj(G),m\in\mathbb{N}_{+},i\in[\lambda_{[H],m}],j\in[m]\}.

This determines the embedding
\prod_{[H],m}(G\wr\Sigma_{m})^{\times\lambda_{[H],m}}\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
as in
Subsection 2.5
.
Let
P
be the product subgroup

P=\prod_{[H],m}(H\wr\Sigma_{m})^{\times\lambda_{[H],m}}\subseteq\prod_{[H],m}(G\wr\Sigma_{m})^{\times\lambda_{[H],m}}\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}

so that
\alpha(\lambda)=[P]
.
Then, the
P
-orbits of
[n]
are the sets
\{([H],m,i)\}\times[m]\subseteq[n]
for
[H]\in\Conj(G)
,
m\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, and
i\in[\lambda_{[H],m}]

For
k=([H],m,i,j)
a representative of such an orbit, the associated subgroup
P_{kk}
is
H
.
Thus, it follows that

\displaystyle\beta(\alpha(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\beta([P])=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P([H],m,i,j)\in P\backslash[n]\end{subarray}}([P_{kk}],|Pk|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[H],m,i\\
i\in[\lambda_{[H],m}]\end{subarray}}([H],m)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[H],m\end{subarray}}\lambda_{[H],m}\cdot([H],m)=\lambda,

where
k
in the first sum denotes
([H],m,i,j)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 9.3
.

Given
H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
, there is a unique representative
H^{\prime}\in\alpha\beta([H])
such that
H\subseteq H^{\prime}
.

Note that we only rely on the existence of this representative in the remainder of the document.

Proof.

First we show existence.
Let
H^{\prime}
denote the subgroup

H^{\prime}=\{(\bar{g},\sigma)\in G\wr\Sigma_{n}\mid\bar{g}_{\sigma(i)}\in H_{i\sigma(i)}\ \forall i\in[n]\}\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}.

The fact that this is a subgroup follows from the groupoid-like properties satisfied by the
H_{ij}
’s, and the fact that
H\subseteq H^{\prime}
follows directly from the definition of the
H_{ij}
’s.
It also follows that
H^{\prime}_{ij}=H_{ij}
for any
i,j\in[n]
and that the orbit
H^{\prime}i
equals the orbit
Hi
for
i\in[n]
.
Accordingly
H^{\prime}\backslash[n]\cong H\backslash[n]
.

Choose a section
s\colon H\backslash[n]\to[n]
of the quotient
q\colon[n]\to H\backslash[n]
and choose any
\bar{g}\in G^{\times n}\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
so that
\bar{g}_{i}\in H_{sq(i)i}
.
Then, observe that

H^{\bar{g}}{}_{ij}=\bar{g}_{j}^{-1}H_{ij}\bar{g}_{i}=H_{sq(i)sq(j)}=\begin{cases}H_{sq(i)sq(i)},&\text{if }Hi=Hj\\
\emptyset,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let
S=sq([n])
be the collection of representatives of the orbits
H\backslash[n]
determined by the section
s
.
It follows that

H^{\prime\bar{g}}=\prod_{i\in S}H_{ii}\wr\Sigma_{Hi}\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n},

where
\Sigma_{Hi}
denotes the symmetric group on the orbit
Hi\subseteq[n]
and using the embedding
\prod_{i\in S}G\wr\Sigma_{Hi}\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
arising from the bijection
\coprod_{i\in S}Hi\cong[n]
.
This establishes that
[H^{\prime}]=\alpha\beta([H])=\alpha\beta([H^{\prime}])
.

To see that
H^{\prime}
is unique, recognize that any representative
H^{\prime\prime}\in\alpha\beta([H])
necessarily has the form

H^{\prime\prime}=\{(\bar{g},\sigma)\in G\wr\Sigma_{n}\mid\bar{g}_{\sigma(i)}\in H^{\prime\prime}_{i\sigma(i)}\ \forall i\in[n]\}

because
H^{\prime}
has this form and
H^{\prime\prime}
is conjugate to
H^{\prime}
.
Thus, in order for
H^{\prime\prime}
to contain
H
, it must be that
H_{ij}\subseteq H^{\prime\prime}_{ij}
for all
i,j\in[n]
and thus that
H_{ij}=H^{\prime\prime}_{ij}
for all
i,j
, so that
H^{\prime}=H^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

For completeness, we describe a few more ways to think about the map
\beta
. Using the notation established above, for
i\in S
and

, we have

\Stab_{H}(i)\subseteq\Stab_{G\wr\Sigma_{n}}(i)\cong G\wr\Sigma_{\{i\}}\times G\wr\Sigma_{[n]-\{i\}}\cong G\times G\wr\Sigma_{n-1},

where

denotes the set difference.
Then

is another description of
H_{ii}
, where
\pi_{\{i\}}
denotes projection onto

.

The data of a

-decorated partition of size

is equivalent to an equivalence class of pairs

, where

is a finite right

-set,

for

, and

The associated

-decorated partition of size

is given by

(X,f)\rightsquigarrow\sum_{xG\in X/G}([\Stab_{G}(x)],|f^{-1}(xG)|).

Equivalently,

\lambda_{[H],m}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{xG\in X/G\colon|f^{-1}(xG)|=m\text{ and }[\Stab_{G}(x)]=[H]}}\right\}}}\right|.

To construct the

pair corresponding to

for

, recall that

where

Then,

is a

-biset, and the

pair corresponding to

is

\beta([H])\mathrel{\mathchoice{\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\rightsquigarrow$}}{\reflectbox{$\textstyle\rightsquigarrow$}}{\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\rightsquigarrow$}}{\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightsquigarrow$}}}\bigg(H\backslash([n]\times G),\ [n]\cong([n]\times G)/G\twoheadrightarrow H\backslash([n]\times G)/G\bigg).

Finally, note that the augmented monoid homomorphism

induces a map of graded monoid rings
\mathbb{Z}[\Conj(G\wr\Sigma)]\to\mathbb{Z}[\Parks(G)]
.
Under the identifications
\mathbb{Z}[\Conj(G\wr\Sigma)]\cong A(G\wr\Sigma)
and

.

Definition 9.4
.

Let

10.
Fixed points of submissive sets

In this section, we study the fixed point sets of
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets of the form
X^{\times n}
for a finite
G
-set
X
, and more generally of submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-sets, for any subgroup
H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
.
These fixed point sets are closed related to the map
\beta
of the previous section, and we continue to use the notation of that section.

In particular, any subgroup
H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
has a left action on
[n]
via the quotient
G\wr\Sigma_{n}\twoheadrightarrow\Sigma_{n}
.
Accordingly, we write
H\backslash[n]
(read
H
under
[n]
) for the collection of orbits of
[n]
under the left action of
H
.
Elements of
H\backslash[n]
are of the form
Hi
for
i\in[n]
.
Further, we make use of the previously defined sets
H_{ij}
for
i,j\in[n]
.

As usual,
[n]
is the disjoint union of its
H
-orbits.
Explicitly,

[n]\cong\coprod_{Hi\in H\backslash[n]}Hi.

We write
X^{\times Hi}=\prod_{j\in Hi}X\cong X^{\times|Hi|}
and
\Sigma_{Hi}
for the group of bijections of the set
Hi\subseteq[n]
.
With this notation, there is a canonical isomorphism

\prod_{Hi\in H\backslash[n]}X^{\times Hi}\cong X^{\times[n]}=X^{\times n}\text{ and embedding }\prod_{Hi\in H\backslash[n]}\Sigma_{Hi}\subseteq\Sigma_{[n]}=\Sigma_{n}.

Then, it follows that

H\subseteq\prod_{Hi\in H\backslash[n]}G\wr\Sigma_{Hi}\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}.

Further, let

\pi_{Hj}\colon\prod_{Hi\in H\backslash[n]}G\wr\Sigma_{Hi}\to G\wr\Sigma_{Hj}

denote projection onto the
Hj
factor for
Hj\in H\backslash[n]
.

There are three main propositions that we use to characterize the fixed point sets of submissive sets.
The other interesting facts are corollaries.

Proposition 10.1
.

Let
X
be a finite
G
-set and
H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
.
Then, under the notation above, there is a bijection

(X^{\times n})^{H}\cong\prod_{Hi\in H\backslash[n]}(X^{\times Hi})^{\pi_{Hi}H}.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the fact that if
K
is a subgroup of a product group
G\times H
and
X
and
Y
are
G
and
H
-sets respectively, then

(X\times Y)^{K}\cong X^{\pi_{1}K}\times Y^{\pi_{2}K}

where
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
denote the projections onto
G
and
H
respectively.
∎

Proposition 10.2
.

Let
X\hookrightarrow Y^{\times n}
be a submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-set and
H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
.
Then,

X^{H}=X^{H^{\prime}},

where
H^{\prime}
is the unique representative of the conjugacy class
\alpha\beta([H])
such that
H\subseteq H^{\prime}
as in
Proposition 9.3
.

Proof.

It suffice to prove the claim for a submissive
G\wr\Sigma_{n}
-set
X\subseteq Y^{\times n}
.
The idea is that, because the elements of
X
are
n
-tuples, fixed points can be checked ‘component-wise.’
That is,

\displaystyle X^{H}

\displaystyle=\{x\in X\mid(\bar{g},\sigma)x=x\ \forall(\bar{g},\sigma)\in H\}

\displaystyle=\{x\in X\mid\bar{g}_{\sigma(i)}x_{i}=x_{\sigma(i)}\ \forall(\bar{g},\sigma)\in H\ \forall i\in[n]\}

\displaystyle=\{x\in X\mid gx_{i}=x_{j}\ \forall i,j\in[n]\ \forall g\in H_{ij}\}.

where the sets
H_{ij}
are defined as in the previous section.
From the proof of
Proposition 9.3
,
H^{\prime}_{ij}=H_{ij}
for all
i,j
.
The result follows.
∎

Corollary 10.3
.

If
X
is submissive and
H^{\prime}
is any representative of
\alpha\beta([H])
, then
X^{H}\cong X^{H^{\prime}}
.

We say that a subgroup
H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
is a transitive if the induced action of
H
on
[n]
is transitive.
By construction, the subgroups
\pi_{Hi}H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{Hi}
of
Proposition 10.1
are transitive.

Proposition 10.4
.

Let
X
be a finite
G
-set,
H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
be a transitive subgroup, and
i\in[n]
.
Then, the projection
\pi_{i}\colon X^{\times n}\to X
induces a bijection

(X^{\times n})^{H}\cong X^{H_{ii}}.

Proof.

As in the proof of the previous proposition,

(X^{\times n})^{H}=\{x\in X^{\times n}\mid gx_{j}=x_{k}\ \forall j,k\in[n]\ \forall g\in H_{jk}\}.

With this in mind, it is clear that the projection
\pi_{i}\colon(X^{\times n})^{H}\to X
lands in the desired fixed points
X^{H_{ii}}
.
To see that this is a bijection, choose
g_{j}\in H_{ij}
for each
j\in[n]
, which is possible since
H
is transitive.
Define the inverse of the projection by
x\in X^{H_{ii}}\mapsto y(x)\in X^{\times n}
where
y(x)_{j}
is defined to be
g_{j}x
.
This lands in the
H
-fixed points as desired because for
h\in H_{jk}
,

hy(x)_{j}=hg_{j}x=g_{k}g_{k}^{-1}hg_{j}x=g_{k}x=y(x)_{k},

since
g_{k}^{-1}hg_{j}\in H_{ii}
and
H_{ii}
fixes
x
.
∎

Corollary 10.5
.

Let
X
be a finite
G
-set and
H\subseteq G\wr\Sigma_{n}
.
There is a bijection

(X^{\times n})^{H}\cong\prod_{Hi\in H\backslash[n]}X^{H_{ii}}.

Proof.

This follows from
Proposition 10.1
and
Proposition 10.4
together with the fact that
(\pi_{Hi}H)_{ii}=H_{ii}
.
∎

Corollary 10.6
.

Let
X
be a finite
G
-set and
\lambda\in\Parts(G,n)
. Choose a representative
H\in\alpha(\lambda)
.
Then,

|(X^{\times n})^{H}|=\prod_{[K],m}|X^{K}|^{\lambda_{[K],m}}.

Proof.

This follows from
Corollary 10.5
and
Definition 5.4
which defines
\alpha
.
∎

11.
Decorated compositions

Much like it is often convenient to choose representatives of conjugacy classes of subgroups, it is often useful to choose finer objects representing partitions.
This is the notion a composition, which refers to an indexed or ordered formal sum of integers.

Accordingly, a formal sum in
X\times\mathbb{N}_{+}
indexed by a finite set
I
is called an
I
-indexed
X
-decorated composition, and the collection of such compositions is denoted
C(I,X)=(X\times\mathbb{N}_{+})^{I}
.
For
c\in C(I,X)
and
i\in I
, the
i
-th pair in
c
is denoted
c_{i}
and is called the
i
-th part of
c
.
We denote the components of these parts as
c_{i}=(c_{i}^{\flat},c_{i}^{\sharp})
so that
c_{i}^{\flat}
denotes the decoration and
c_{i}^{\sharp}
denotes the integer component.
If
c\in C(I,*)
is an integer composition, we may write
c_{i}
for
c_{i}^{\sharp}
since the decoration contributes no information.

There is a function

u\colon C(I,X)\to P(X)\colon c\mapsto uc=\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}

that un-indexes a composition by taking its formal sum.
Equivalently,

uc\colon x,m\mapsto|\{i\in I\mid c_{i}=(x,m)\}|.

We denote the preimage under
u
of a partition
\lambda
by
C(I,\lambda)
and we call the elements of this set
I
-indexed compositions of
\lambda
.
The set
C(I,\lambda)
is nonempty if and only if
|I|=|\lambda|
.
Compositions are generally denoted by lowercase Latin letters toward the beginning of the alphabet, but sometimes notation will be chosen to match corresponding partitions e.g. we may choose
\ell
for an element of
C(I,\lambda)
.
Certain expressions involving partitions are related to certain expressions related to compositions.
In particular, if
\lambda\in P(X)
,
\ell\in C(I,\lambda)
, and
f\colon X\times\mathbb{N}_{+}\to M
is a function into a commutative monoid, then

(11.1)

\begin{gathered}\sum_{x,m}\lambda_{x,m}\cdot f(x,m)=\sum_{i}f(\ell_{i})\text{ or }\prod_{x,m}f(x,m)^{\lambda_{x,m}}=\prod_{i}f(\ell_{i})\\
\text{ or }\mathop{\vphantom{\sum}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{524296}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}}\displaylimits_{x,m}f(x,m)^{\star\lambda_{x,m}}=\mathop{\vphantom{\sum}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{524296}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}}\displaylimits_{i}f(\ell_{i})\end{gathered}

depending on which symbol is used for the operation of
M
.

We extend the operations of partitions to compositions such that operations applied to compositions correspond to operations applied to the underlying partitions.
Addition of partitions corresponds to a concatenation map
+\colon C(I,X)\times C(J,X)\to C(I\amalg J,X)
defined by

(c+d)_{i}=\begin{cases}c_{i},&i\in I\\
d_{i},&i\in J,\end{cases}

for
c\in C(I,X)
and
d\in C(J,X)
.
For
J\subseteq I
, there is a restriction map
C(I,X)\to C(J,X)\colon c\mapsto c|_{J}
which remembers only the
i
-th parts of
c
for
i\in J
.
This interacts with concatenation so that
c=c|_{I}+c|_{J}
for
c\in C(I\amalg J,X)
.
Consequently the concatenation map is a bijection.
Concatenation of compositions corresponds to addition of partitions in the sense that
u(c+d)=uc+ud
.
Accordingly, for
\lambda,\mu\in P(X)
, the concatenation map restricts to a map
C(I,\lambda)\times C(J,\mu)\to C(I\amalg J,\lambda+\mu)
but this is not usually bijection, even when the domain and codomain are nonempty.

We define the length and size of a composition
c\in C(I,X)
by

|c|=|I|=|uc|\text{ and }\|c\|=\sum_{i}c_{i}^{\sharp}=\|uc\|

and the factorial and multifactorial by

c!=(uc)!\text{ and }(\!(c)\!)!=\prod_{i}c_{i}^{\sharp}!=(\!(uc)\!)!.

Given a function
f\colon X\to Y
, there is a pushforward map
f_{*}\colon C(I,X)\to C(I,Y)
which applies
f
to the decorations i.e.
f_{*}c_{i}=(f(c_{i}^{\flat}),c_{i}^{\sharp})
.
This has the property that
f_{*}uc=uf_{*}c
.
The pushforward along the map
X\to*
is the un-decoration map
C(I,X)\to C(I,*)\colon c\mapsto c^{\sharp}
.
Similarly, we denote the un-decoration map
P(X)\to P(*)\colon\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{\sharp}
so that

\lambda^{\sharp}\colon m\mapsto\sum_{x}\lambda_{x,m}.

When we do not care about the particular set which indexes compositions, we write
C(k,X)
for
C([k],X)
where
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Similarly, we write
C(\lambda)
for
C(|\lambda|,\lambda)
and call the elements of this set compositions of
\lambda
.
Sometimes, it is useful to refer to the collection of

.
The map

is a homomorphism of augmented monoids when

is augmented by the size map (as opposed to the augmentation

given by the disjoint union decomposition).
For

and

where

is the set of size

, so we usually opt to elide the domains of such sums (or products or
\star

We may identify
X\times\mathbb{N}_{+}
with

so that for

,

to the end of

If

It is often useful to choose a composition for a specific partition.
Given a partition

The particular values

are not defined, but regardless certain expressions in terms of them are still defined.
For example, if

\displaystyle\sum_{i}f(c(\lambda)_{i})=\sum_{x,m}\lambda_{x,m}\cdot f(x,m)\text{ or }\prod_{i}f(c(\lambda)_{i})=\prod_{x,m}f(x,m)^{\lambda_{x,m}}

\displaystyle\text{ or }\mathop{\vphantom{\sum}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{524296}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}}\displaylimits_{i}f(c(\lambda)_{i})=\mathop{\vphantom{\sum}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{524296}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\bigstar$}}}}}\displaylimits_{x,m}f(x,m)^{\star\lambda_{x,m}}

is well-defined depending on which symbol is used for the operation of

.

Notice that

has an action of
\Sigma_{I}=\Aut(I)
.
For

Then,
c!=|\Sigma_{c}|

The collection

is the

-orbit of

Accordingly, we find that

\frac{|\lambda|!}{\lambda!}=[\Sigma_{|\lambda|}\colon\Sigma_{c(\lambda)}]=|C(\lambda)|.

The canonical embedding

gives a relationship between the stabilizers:

As mentioned, the pushforward along a function

12.
Examples of partition and composition notation

We dedicate this section to example applications of partitions and compositions.
The results in this section are either reformulations of existing results into new language or are elementary results that can be efficiently stated in terms of partitions and compositions.
We invite the reader to use this section to internalize the notation of the previous section, as it will be heavily used in the remainder of the paper.

The multinomial theorem

The multinomial theorem

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}}}\right)^{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}+\cdots+i_{m}=n\\
i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{n!}{\textstyle\prod_{k}i_{k}!}\prod_{k}x_{k}^{i_{k}}

can be expressed in terms of integer compositions.
An equivalent formulation is

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}}}\right)^{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|c\|=n\\
J\subseteq I,\,c\in C(J,*)\end{subarray}}\frac{n!}{(\!(c)\!)!}\prod_{i\in J}x_{i}^{c_{i}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|c\|=n\\
J\subseteq I,\,c\in C(J,*)\end{subarray}}\frac{n!}{(\!(c)\!)!}x^{c},

where
x^{c}
denotes the quantity
\prod_{i\in J}x_{i}^{c_{i}}
.
Another equivalent formulation involves integer partitions:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}}}\right)^{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\lambda\|=n\\
\lambda\in P(*)\end{subarray}}\frac{n!}{(\!(\lambda)\!)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in C(J,\lambda)\\
J\subseteq I,\,|J|=|\lambda|\end{subarray}}x^{\ell}.

The quantities
n!/(\!(\lambda)\!)!=\|\lambda\|!/(\!(\lambda)\!)!
are called the multinomial coefficients and are integral.
The necessity of summing over subsets
J\subseteq I
is an artifact of our definition of integer compositions having positive parts.
While other choices may result in more elegant formulas, we choose this formulation to avoid introducing too many variants of compositions and partitions.

The multinomial theorem for power operations

The notion of
R
-valued power operations on
A
for
R
a commutative graded ring and
A
an abelian group as defined in
Section 6
has three axioms:
P_{0}(x)=1
,
P_{n}(0)=0
for
n>0
and

P_{n}(x+y)=\sum_{i+j=n}P_{i}(x)P_{j}(y).

From this, one can conclude a version of the multinomial theorem without the coefficients:

P_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}}}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|c\|=n\\
J\subseteq I,\,c\in C(J,*)\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\in J}P_{c_{i}}(x_{i})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|c\|=n\\
J\subseteq I,\,c\in C(J,*)\end{subarray}}\hskip-10.00002ptP_{c}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\lambda\|=n,\,\ell\in C(J,\lambda)\\
\lambda\in P(*),\,J\subseteq I,\,|J|=|\lambda|\end{subarray}}\hskip-20.00003ptP_{\ell}(x),

where
P_{c}(x)
denotes
\prod_{i\in J}P_{c_{i}}(x_{i})
.
This is essentially a rewriting of (
5.9
) in the language of partitions and compositions.

The proof of this formula is the same as the inductive proof of the classical multinomial theorem.
This single formula implies each of the other axioms:
P_{0}(x)=1
follows from
n=0
and
|I|=1
,
P_{n}(0)=0
for
n>0
follows from
|I|=0
, and
P_{n}(x+y)=\sum_{i+j=n}P_{i}(x)P_{j}(y)
follows from
|I|=2
.
Thus, taking this as the only axiom provides an equivalent definition for power operations.

Power operations of additive inverses

The language of partitions allows an elegant formula for power operations applied to additive inverses.
Let
P
be an
R
-valued family of power operations on
A
.
For an integer partition
\lambda\in P(*)
and

P_{\lambda}(x)=\prod_{k}P_{k}(x)^{\lambda_{k}}\in R_{\|\lambda\|}
.
Similarly, for an integer composition

P_{c}(x)=\prod_{i}P_{c_{i}}(x)=P_{uc}(x)

Note that this agrees with the notation

of the previous subsection when

is a constant family.

Proposition 12.1
.

P_{n}(-x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\lambda\|=n\\
\lambda\in P(*)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\lambda|}\frac{|\lambda|!}{\lambda!}P_{\lambda}(x).

Proof.

This can be proved by induction.
First, note that

P_{n}(0)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\lambda\|=n\\
|\lambda|=0\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\lambda|}\frac{|\lambda|!}{\lambda!}P_{\lambda}(x)

because the value of the summand is

Suppose for the sake of induction that the formula of the proposition holds for

Then,

\displaystyle P_{n}(0)

\displaystyle=P_{n}(x-x)=\sum_{i+j=n}P_{i}(x)P_{j}(-x)=P_{n}(-x)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=n\\
i>0\end{subarray}}P_{i}(x)P_{j}(-x)

\displaystyle=P_{n}(-x)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=n\\
i>0\end{subarray}}P_{i}(x)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\lambda\|=j\\
\lambda\in P(*)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\lambda|}\frac{|\lambda|!}{\lambda!}P_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=P_{n}(-x)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=n\\
i>0\end{subarray}}P_{i}(x)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\ell\|=j\\
\ell\in C(*)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\ell|}P_{\ell}(x)

\displaystyle=P_{n}(-x)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=n\\

\displaystyle=P_{n}(-x)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|c\|=n\\

\displaystyle=P_{n}(-x)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\lambda\|=n\\
|\lambda|>0\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\lambda|}\frac{|\lambda|!}{\lambda!}P_{\lambda}(x).

The equality between the second and third lines follows by reparameterizing from partitions to compositions.
The equality between the third and fourth lines follows by rewriting in terms of the composition

The equality between the fifth and sixth lines follows by reparameterizing back to partitions.
We find that

\displaystyle P_{n}(-x)

\displaystyle=P_{n}(0)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\lambda\|=n\\
|\lambda|>0\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\lambda|}\frac{|\lambda|!}{\lambda!}P_{\lambda}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\lambda\|=n\\

|\lambda|>0\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\lambda|}\frac{|\lambda|!}{\lambda!}P_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\|\lambda\|=n}(-1)^{|\lambda|}\frac{|\lambda|!}{\lambda!}P_{\lambda}(x).\qed

14.
Formulas for induced operations on parks

Let
G
and
H
be finite groups. We consider a fixed abelian group homomorphism
F\colon A(H)\to A(G)
. Let
\mathring{F}\colon\AA(H)\to\AA(G)
be the map of
Definition 6.8
.
Rationalizing, we obtain maps

F\colon\Marks(H,\mathbb{Q})\to\Marks(G,\mathbb{Q})\text{ and }\mathring{F}\colon\Parks(H,\mathbb{Q})\to\Parks(G,\mathbb{Q}).

The map on marks can be given a formula using the canonical bases.
In this section, we provide a formula for the map
\mathring{F}
on parks in terms of the canonical bases.

Let
M(F)
denote the matrix representation of
F
on marks in the bases of indicator functions.
That is,

F(f)([K])=\sum_{[L]\in\Conj(H)}M(F)^{[K]}_{[L]}f([L]),

where
f\in\Marks(H,\mathbb{Q})
and
[K]\in\Conj(G)
or equivalently

F(\mathbbm{1}_{[L]})=\sum_{[K]}M(F)^{[K]}_{[L]}\mathbbm{1}_{[K]},

where
\mathbbm{1}_{[L]}
denotes the indicator function of a conjugacy class
[L]
.

Proposition 14.1
.

Define the quantity
\mathring{M}(F)^{\kappa}_{\lambda}
for
\kappa\in\Parts(G)
and
\lambda\in\Parts(H)
by the formula

\mathring{M}(F)^{\kappa}_{\lambda}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\sharp}=c(\kappa)^{\sharp}\\
\ell\in C(\lambda)\end{subarray}}\prod_{i}M(F)^{c(\kappa)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}.

This is a well-defined formula.

Proof.

There are two potential well-definition issues.
First, the product
\prod_{i}M(F)^{c(\kappa)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}
with the domain of summation omitted is only defined when
\ell
and
c(\kappa)
have the same length (and thus the same set indexing their parts), but this is the case when
\ell^{\sharp}=c(\kappa)^{\sharp}
.
Then, one must verify that this quantity is independent of the choice of composition
c(\kappa)
, but this follows from the fact that any two such compositions differ by a permutation in
\Sigma_{|\kappa|}
which amounts to reordering the factors in
\prod_{i}M(F)^{c(\kappa)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}
for an
\ell
differing by the same permutation.
∎

Theorem 14.2
.

The map
\mathring{F}\colon\Parks(H,\mathbb{Q})\to\Parks(G,\mathbb{Q})
is given by

\mathring{F}(f)(\kappa)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Parts(H)}\mathring{M}(F)^{\kappa}_{\lambda}f(\lambda)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda^{\sharp}=\kappa^{\sharp}\\
\lambda\in\Parts(H)\end{subarray}}\mathring{M}(F)^{\kappa}_{\lambda}f(\lambda).

Equivalently,

\mathring{F}(f)(\kappa)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\sharp}=c(\kappa)^{\sharp}\\
\ell\in C(\Conj(G))\end{subarray}}f(u\ell)\prod_{i}M(F)^{c(\kappa)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}.

Proof.

By
Proposition 6.2
, to verify the correctness of this formula, it suffices to check that it is compatible with the power operations and the transfer product.
First, we check compatibility with the power operations.
For
f\in\Marks(H,\mathbb{Q})
and
\kappa\in\Parts(G,n)
, we compute that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}(F(f))(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}[K]\in\Conj(G)\\
m\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\end{subarray}}F(f)([K])^{\kappa_{[K],m}}=\prod_{i}F(f)(c(\kappa)_{i}^{\flat})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}\sum_{\ell_{i}\in\Conj(H)}M(F)^{c(\kappa)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}}f(\ell_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\sharp}=c(\kappa)^{\sharp}\\
\ell\in C(\Conj(H))\end{subarray}}\prod_{i}M(F)^{c(\kappa)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}f(\ell_{i}^{\flat})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\sharp}=c(\kappa)^{\sharp}\\
\ell\in C(\Conj(H))\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{n}(f)(u\ell)\prod_{i}M(F)^{c(\kappa)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Parts(H)}\mathbb{P}_{n}(f)(\lambda)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\sharp}=c(\kappa)^{\sharp}\\
\ell\in C(\lambda)\end{subarray}}\prod_{i}M(F)^{c(\kappa)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Parts(H)}\mathring{M}(F)^{\kappa}_{\lambda}\mathbb{P}_{n}(f)(\lambda)=\mathring{F}(\mathbb{P}_{n}(f))(\kappa).

The nontrivial equality on the first line translates between partitions and composition as in (
11.1
).
The equality between the second and third lines follows from the fact that a choice
\ell_{i}\in\Conj(H)
for each
i
which indexes
c(\kappa)
is the same data as choosing a decoration from
\Conj(H)
for each of the parts of
c(\kappa)^{\sharp}
.
The equality between the fourth and fifth lines follows from the fact that summing over compositions is the same as first summing over partitions and then the compositions of those partitions.
The other equalities are applying definitions.

Next, we check compatibility with the transfer product.
For
f,g\in\Parks(H,\mathbb{Q})
and
\mu\in\Parts(G)
, we compute that

\displaystyle(\mathring{F}(f)\star\mathring{F}(g))(\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}J=[|\mu|]\end{subarray}}\mathring{F}(f)(uc(\mu)|_{I})\mathring{F}(g)(uc(\mu)|_{J})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}J=[|\mu|]\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k^{\sharp}=c(\mu)^{\sharp}|_{I}\\
\ell^{\sharp}=c(\mu)^{\sharp}|_{J}\\
k\in C(I,\Conj(H))\\
\ell\in C(J,\Conj(H))\end{subarray}}f(uk)g(u\ell)\prod_{i\in I}M(F)^{c(\mu)_{i}^{\flat}}_{k^{\flat}}\prod_{j\in J}M(F)^{c(\mu)_{j}^{\flat}}_{\lambda_{j}^{\flat}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}J=[|\mu|]\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\sharp}=c(\mu)^{\sharp}\\
\ell\in C(\Conj(H))\end{subarray}}f(u\ell|_{I})g(u\ell|_{J})\prod_{i}M(F)^{c(\mu)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\sharp}=c(\mu)^{\sharp}\\
\ell\in C(\Conj(H))\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}J=[|u\ell|]\end{subarray}}f(u\ell|_{I})g(u\ell|_{J})\prod_{i}M(F)^{c(\mu)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\sharp}=c(\mu)^{\sharp}\\
\ell\in C(\Conj(H))\end{subarray}}(f\star g)(u\ell)\prod_{i}M(F)^{c(\mu)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}=\mathring{F}(f\star g)(\mu).

The equality of the first line comes from
Proposition 12.5
.
The equality between the second and third line follows from the bijection
C(I,\Conj(H))\times C(J,\Conj(H))\cong C([|\mu|],\Conj(H))
.
The other equalities follow from manipulating definitions and summation order.
∎

Corollary 14.3
.

Let
\lambda\in\Parts(H)
and let
\mathbbm{1}_{\lambda}\in\Parks(H,\mathbb{Q})
denote the indicator function of

.
Then,

\mathring{F}(\mathbbm{1}_{\lambda})=\sum_{\kappa\in\Parks(G)}\mathring{M}(F)^{\kappa}_{\lambda}\mathbbm{1}_{\kappa}

so that

If the matrix components

, then the formula of
Theorem 14.2
provides a commutative graded ring map

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\AA(H)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\AA(G)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Parks(H,R)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Parks(G,R)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathring{F}}

15.
A general formula for transfers on parks

In this section, we analyze the specific case of
Theorem 14.2
when the map
F\colon A(H)\to A(G)
is a transfer along a (not necessarily injective) group homomorphism.
We only rely on one fact about transfers along group homomorphisms: that certain values
M(F)^{[K]}_{[L]}
are zero.
Thus, we start by proving a generalization of the result for transfers along group homomorphisms, and then conclude the result about group homomorphisms as a corollary. We continue to use the notation of the previous section.

Proposition 15.1
.

Let
F\colon A(H)\to A(G)
be a map of abelian groups.
Suppose that for fixed
[L]
, there is at most one
[K]
such that
M(F)^{[K]}_{[L]}
is nonzero.
Equivalently, suppose there is a function
\psi\colon\Conj(H)\to\Conj(G)
with the property that
M(F)^{[K]}_{[L]}
is nonzero only if
\psi[L]=[K]
.
Write
M(F)_{[L]}
for
M(F)^{\psi[L]}_{[L]}
so that

F(f)([K])=\sum_{[L]\in\psi^{-1}[K]}M(F)_{[L]}f([L])

for
f\in\Marks(H,\mathbb{Q})
and
[K]\in\Conj(G)
.
Define coefficients

\mathring{M}(F)_{\lambda}=\frac{(\psi_{*}\lambda)!}{\lambda!}\prod_{[L],m}(M(F)_{[L]})^{\lambda_{[L],m}}

for
\lambda\in\Parts(H)
.
Then,
\mathring{F}\colon\Parks(H,\mathbb{Q})\to\Parks(G,\mathbb{Q})
has the following formula:

\mathring{F}(f)(\kappa)=\sum_{\lambda\in\psi_{*}^{-1}\kappa}\mathring{M}(F)_{\lambda}f(\lambda)

for
f\in\Parks(H,\mathbb{Q})
and
\kappa\in\Parts(G)
, where
\psi_{*}^{-1}
denotes the preimage under
\psi_{*}\colon\Parts(H)\to\Parts(G)
.
Equivalently,

\mathring{F}(f)(\kappa)=\sum_{\lambda\in\psi_{*}^{-1}\kappa}\frac{\kappa!}{\lambda!}f(\lambda)\prod_{[L],m}(M(F)_{[L]})^{\lambda_{[L],m}}.

Note that the quantities
\kappa!/\lambda!=\psi_{*}\lambda!/\lambda!
are integral.

Proof.

To prove this claim, it suffices to study the coefficients

\mathring{M}(F)^{\kappa}_{\lambda}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\sharp}=c(\kappa)^{\sharp}\\
\ell\in C(\lambda)\end{subarray}}\prod_{i}M(F)^{c(\kappa)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}

used in
Theorem 14.2
.
First, note that if
\psi_{*}\ell\neq c(\kappa)
, the product
\prod_{i}M(F)^{c(\kappa)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}
will be zero since at least one of the values
M(F)^{c(\kappa)_{i}^{\flat}}_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}
will be zero.
Further, in the case that
\psi_{*}\ell=c(\kappa)
, this product is simply
\prod_{i}M(F)_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}
.
Thus,

\mathring{M}(F)^{\kappa}_{\lambda}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi_{*}\ell=c(\kappa)\\
\ell\in C(\lambda)\end{subarray}}\prod_{i}M(F)_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\{\ell\in C(\lambda)\colon\psi_{*}\ell=c(\kappa)\}}}\right|\prod_{[L],m}(M(F)_{[L]})^{\lambda_{[L],m}}.

Thus, it suffices to enumerate those
\ell\in C(\lambda)
such that
\psi_{*}\ell=c(\kappa)
.
There exists such an
\ell
if and only if
\psi_{*}\lambda=\kappa
.
In such a case, choose
c(\lambda)
such that
\psi_{*}c(\lambda)=c(\kappa)
.
Any other choice of
\ell
differs from
c(\lambda)
by permutation in
\Sigma_{|\lambda|}
which fixes
c(\kappa)
i.e. a permutation in
\Sigma_{c(\kappa)}
.
Two such permutations give the same choice of
\ell
whenever
\sigma\in\Sigma_{c(\lambda)}
.
Thus,

(15.2)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\{\ell\in C(\lambda)\colon\psi_{*}\ell=c(\kappa)\}}}\right|=[\Sigma_{c(\kappa)}\colon\Sigma_{c(\lambda)}]=\frac{\kappa!}{\lambda!}.

The result now follows.
∎

Corollary 15.3
.

On indicator functions,

\mathring{F}(\mathbbm{1}_{\lambda})=\mathring{M}(F)_{\lambda}\mathbbm{1}_{\psi_{*}\lambda}=\frac{(\psi_{*}\lambda)!}{\lambda!}\mathbbm{1}_{\psi_{*}\lambda}\prod_{[L],m}(M(F)_{[L]})^{\lambda_{[L],m}}

for
\lambda\in\Parts(H)
.

Let
\varphi\colon H\to G
be a homomorphism of finite groups. The map
\Tr_{\varphi}\colon A(H)\to A(G)
is of the form required for
Proposition 15.1
.
The map
\psi
of the proposition is the induced map
\varphi\colon\Conj(H)\to\Conj(G)
.
That is, the transfer map on marks is determined by values
M(\Tr_{\varphi})_{[L]}\in\mathbb{Q}
for
[L]\in\Conj(H)
such that

\Tr_{\varphi}(f)([K])=\sum_{[L]\in\varphi^{-1}([K])}M(\Tr_{\varphi})_{[L]}f([L]),

where
\varphi^{-1}
denotes the preimage under
\varphi\colon\Conj(H)\to\Conj(G)
.
For arbitrary
\varphi
, it is difficult to give an elegant formula for the coefficients
M(\Tr_{\varphi})_{[L]}
, or even for the transfer map on marks.
A formula for the coefficients
M(\Tr_{\varphi})_{[L]}
in terms of Möebius inversion can be extracted from the results of Bouc in
[
Bou10
, Chapter 5]
.

Corollary 15.4
.

Let
\varphi\colon H\to G
be a homomorphism of finite groups.
Then, the transfer map on parks
\Tr_{\varphi}\colon\Parks(H,\mathbb{Q})\to\Parks(G,\mathbb{Q})

has the following formula:

\Tr_{\varphi}(f)(\kappa)=\sum_{\lambda\in\varphi_{*}^{-1}\kappa}\mathring{M}(\Tr_{\varphi})_{\lambda}f(\lambda)

for
f\in\Parks(H,\mathbb{Q})
and
\kappa\in\Parts(G)
.
On indicator functions,

\Tr_{\varphi}(\mathbbm{1}_{\lambda})=\mathring{M}(\Tr_{\varphi})_{\lambda}\mathbbm{1}_{\varphi_{*}\lambda}

for
\lambda\in\Parts(H)
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from
Proposition 15.1
using
F=\Tr_{\varphi}
and
\psi=\varphi\colon\Conj(H)\to\Conj(G)
.
∎

16.
Special cases of the transfer formula

Although it is difficult to give an elegant formula for the transfer map on marks for an arbitrary homomorphism
\varphi\colon H\to G
, some homomorphisms have a convenient transfer formula.
In this section, we seek to provide alternative formulas for
Corollary 15.4
in such cases.
In particular, we consider subgroup inclusions and the map
H\to e
.

We begin by discussing subgroup inclusions.
Recall the classical formula for the transfer on marks along a subgroup inclusion

(16.1)

\Tr_{H\subseteq G}(f)([K])=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}gH\in G/H\\
K^{g}\subseteq H\end{subarray}}f([K^{g}]).

In particular, this involves choosing a representative for the conjugacy class and the conjugation action on subgroups.
We seek to reproduce these ideas on compositions.

Note that if
G
acts on
X
(say, on the right), then
G^{I}
acts on
C(I,X)
by acting on decorations.
Let
q\colon X\to X/G
denote the orbit map.
Then,
q_{*}\colon C(I,X)\to C(I,X/G)
induces to an isomorphism
C(I,X)/G^{I}\to C(I,X/G)
.
Of particular utility is the conjugation action of
G
on
\Sub(G)
, where
\Sub(G)
denotes the set of subgroups of
G
. Recall the map
u
of
Section 11
that associates to a decorated composition its underlying decorated partition.

Proposition 16.2
.

Let
\varphi
be a subgroup inclusion
H\subseteq G
.
Let
f\in\Parks(H)
and
\kappa\in\Parts(G)
.
Choose a composition
k\in q_{*}^{-1}C(\kappa)\subseteq C(\Sub(G))
and let
c(\kappa)=q_{*}k
.
The composition
k
amounts to a choice of ordering of the parts of
\kappa
, along with a choice of representative for each of the conjugacy classes that decorate the parts.

Then,
\Tr_{H\subseteq G}\colon\Parks(H,\mathbb{Q})\to\Parks(G,\mathbb{Q})
is given by

\Tr_{H\subseteq G}(f)(\kappa)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}gH^{\times|\kappa|}\in G^{\times|\kappa|}/H^{\times|\kappa|}\\
k^{g}\in C(|\kappa|,\Sub(H))\end{subarray}}f(uq_{*}k^{g}),

where
C(|\kappa|,\Sub(H))\subseteq C(|\kappa|,\Sub(G))
.

Proof.

The coefficients
M(\Tr_{H\subseteq G})_{[L]}
used in
Proposition 15.1
are given by

(16.3)

M(\Tr_{H\subseteq G})_{[L]}=[N_{G}(L)\colon N_{H}(L)].

We will prove
Proposition 16.2
using (
16.3
) and
Corollary 15.4
.
Notice that the formula of
Proposition 16.2
on
\Parks(H,1,\mathbb{Q})\cong\Marks(H,\mathbb{Q})
is equivalent to the classical formula (
16.1
).
Thus, our proof of
Proposition 16.2
will additionally verify the correctness of (
16.3
).

Let
=_{G}
denote the equivalence relation of two subgroups being
G
-conjugate and
=_{H}
denote the equivalence relation of two subgroups being
H
-conjugate.
Choose subgroups
L\subseteq H
and
K\subseteq G
and suppose
K=_{G}L
.
Choose
a\in G
witnessing that
K^{a}=L
.
Then,

\{g\in G/H\colon K^{g}=_{H}L\}=a\{gH\colon L^{g}=_{H}L\}=aN_{G}(L)H/H\cong N_{G}(L)/N_{H}(L)

so that

(16.4)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\{gH\colon K^{g}\in[L]\}}}\right|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\{gH\colon K^{g}=_{H}L\}}}\right|=\frac{|N_{G}(L)|}{|N_{H}(L)|}=M(\Tr_{H\subseteq G})_{[L]}.

Notice that
[L]\in\varphi_{*}^{-1}([K])
if and only if
L=_{G}K
.
Thus, (
16.4
) holds when
\varphi_{*}[L]=[K]
.

Now, we compute that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}gH^{\times|\kappa|}\in G^{\times|\kappa|}/H^{\times|\kappa|}\\
k^{g}\in C(\Sub(H))\end{subarray}}f(uq_{*}k^{g})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}gH^{\times|\kappa|}\in G^{\times|\kappa|}/H^{\times|\kappa|}\\
\ell\in C(\Conj(H))\\
k^{g}\in C(\Sub(H))\\
q_{*}k^{g}=\ell\end{subarray}}f(u\ell)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in C(\Conj(H))\end{subarray}}f(u\ell)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{gH^{\times|\kappa|}\in G^{\times|\kappa|}/H^{\times|\kappa|}\colon\begin{subarray}{c}k^{g}\in C(\Sub(H))\\
q_{*}k^{g}=\ell\end{subarray}}}\right\}}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi_{*}\ell=q_{*}k\\
\ell\in C(\Conj(H))\end{subarray}}f(u\ell)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{gH^{\times|\kappa|}\in G^{\times|\kappa|}/H^{\times|\kappa|}\colon\begin{subarray}{c}(k_{i}^{\flat})^{g_{i}}\subseteq H\\
(k_{i}^{\flat})^{g_{i}}\in\ell^{i}_{\flat}\end{subarray}\ \forall i}}\right\}}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi_{*}\ell=q_{*}k\\
\ell\in C(\Conj(H))\end{subarray}}f(u\ell)\prod_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{gH\in G/H\colon(k_{i}^{\flat})^{g}\in\ell^{i}_{\flat}}}\right\}}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi_{*}\ell=c(\kappa)\\
\ell\in C(\Conj(H))\end{subarray}}f(u\ell)\prod_{i}M(\Tr_{H\subseteq G})_{\ell_{i}^{\flat}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\varphi_{*}^{-1}\kappa}f(\lambda)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi_{*}\ell=c(\kappa)\\
\ell\in C(\lambda)\end{subarray}}\prod_{[L],m}(M(\Tr_{H\subseteq G})_{[L]})^{\lambda_{[L],m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\varphi_{*}^{-1}\kappa}\frac{\kappa!}{\lambda!}f(\lambda)\prod_{[L],m}(M(\Tr_{H\subseteq G})_{[L]})^{\lambda_{[L],m}}=\Tr_{H\subseteq G}(f)(\kappa).

The first equality follows by reparameterizing the sum according to the value of
q_{*}\kappa^{g}
.
The second equality follows by recognizing the the summand is independent of most of the sum’s quantifiers.
The third equality follows by restricting the sum to only those compositions whose corresponding counting problem gives a nonzero result and by replacing statements about compositions with index-wise statements.
The fourth equality follows by replacing the index-wise counting problem with a product of counting problems.
The fifth equality follows from (
16.4
).
The sixth equality follows from (
11.1
) and reparameterizing the sum according to partitions.
The seventh equality follows from (
15.2
).
∎

Next, we consider transfers to the trivial group.
Recall the Burnside orbit counting lemma which gives a formula for the transfer
A(G)\to A(e)
on marks:

and

denotes the pushforward

\mathring{F}(\mathbbm{1}_{\lambda})=\sum_{\kappa\in\psi_{*}^{-1}\lambda}\mathbbm{1}_{\kappa}

for
\lambda\in\Parts(H)

Proof.

This is a simple consequence of

.
First, notice that the matrix components

are given by

M(F)^{[K]}_{[L]}=\begin{cases}1,&\psi[K]=[L]\\

\displaystyle\mathring{M}(F)^{\kappa}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\sharp}=c(\kappa)^{\sharp}\\

Let
