Benedikt Diederichs, Mihail N. Kolountzakis, Effie Papageorgiou

Abstract
. We are looking at families of functions or measures on the torus which are specified by a finite number of parameters N N . The task, for a given family, is to look at a small number of Fourier coefficients of the object, at a set of locations that is predetermined and may depend only on N N , and determine the object. We look at (a) the indicator functions of at most N N intervals of the torus and (b) at sums of at most N N complex point masses on the multidimensional torus. In the first case we reprove a theorem of Courtney which says that the Fourier coefficients at the locations 0 , 1 , … , N 0,1,\ldots,N are sufficient to determine the function (the intervals). In the second case we produce a set of locations of size O ⁡ ( N ​ log d − 1 ​ N ) O(N\log^{d-1}N) which suffices to determine the measure.

1.
Introduction

Assume that the function
f
belongs to a given
k
-parameter explicit family of functions.
Can we recover
f
by looking at
k
(or, at least, not many more than
k
) of its Fourier coefficients?
This situation is often called “a signal with a finite rate of innovation” in the engineering literature
[
VMB02
]
.
We are particularly interested in families where the dependence on the parameters is non-linear.

The recovery of a function by not too many of its Fourier coefficients may be viewed as a problem in the general field of sparse representation (see, e.g.,
[
CRT06
]
). The specific rules that apply to this paper are the following:

•

We consider functions or measures on the torus
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
or
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The classes of functions we examine have a finite number of degrees of freedom whose number is constrained by the parameter
N
. For instance we might consider sums of point masses on
{\mathbb{T}}
with the number of points being at most
N
.

•

We seek an a priori known finite set
\Omega=\Omega_{N}
of Fourier coefficients (a subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}
or
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
) which are assumed to be known for our class of functions. The set
\Omega
is allowed to depend on
N
and on nothing else.

•

The aim is to show that the mapping
f\to\widehat{f}\restriction_{\Omega}
is one to one. Though our proofs can often be turned into algorithms for the recovery of
f
we do not concern ourselves with matters of numerical stability or efficiency.

We deal with two problems in this paper.

(1)

Intervals in
{\mathbb{T}}
.

Our function
f
is the
indicator
function of the union of at most
N
open intervals on
{\mathbb{T}}
. Courtney
[
Cou10
]
has shown that such a function is determined by its Fourier coefficients at the locations
0,1,\ldots,N
. We give a new proof of this fact which is completely elementary (Courtney’s proof uses Blaschke products and conformal mapping). We further discuss the problem of whether functions of this class are determined by different sets of Fourier coefficients.

We need to emphasize here that, apart from Courtney
[
Cou10
]
this problem has not been considered elsewhere. There are papers
[
VMB02
]
where one recovers a function on
{\mathbb{T}}
which is piecewise constant (or even piecewise a polynomial) with the correct number of samples (roughly equal to the number of degrees of freedom) but all these methods fail to take into account the fact that the function only takes two values (0 or 1) and will accordingly use a number of samples that is larger than the minimum by at least
{\rm const.}N
samples. The “extreme” nonlinearity of this problem (not only in allowing variable nodes in the decomposition of
{\mathbb{T}}
, but also in the values of the function) does not seem to make it amenable to the usual methods such as Prony’s method, if one wants to use the minimal number of samples (or close to the minimal).

This we do in §
2
.

(2)

Point masses in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.

We examine the class of measures which are sums of at most
N
complex point masses on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. Using the corresponding question in dimension 1 (solvable with the so-called Prony’s method) we show an explicit set of locations
\Omega
such that the Fourier coefficients on
\Omega
determine the measure. This set
\Omega
is of size
O(N\log^{d-1}N)
. We believe it is the first such set given of size
o(N^{2})
, though several other methods have been described for this problem under additional assumptions on the locations of the point masses
[
PW13
,
MV04
,
KPRvdO16
,
DI15
,
PT13
,
CL18
,
DI17
,
Sau17
,
Sau18
]
. We emphasize that the set
\Omega
depends only on
N
and is not determined on the fly by looking at the Fourier coefficients of the measure.

This we show in §
3
where we also give a set
\Omega
of size
O(k^{d-1}N)
when we assume that the set of point masses has at most
k
points with the same
x
-coordinate.

In §
3
we also describe a general connection of this problem with the problem of interpolation.

2.
At most
N
intervals on
{\mathbb{T}}

2.1.
Determination from the Fourier coefficients at
0,1,\ldots,N

We consider sets
E\subset{\mathbb{T}}
of the form

E=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}(a_{j},b_{j})

where
k\leq N
and the open intervals
(a_{j},b_{j})
are disjoint.
We show that
f={\bf 1}_{E}
is determined by the complex data

\widehat{f}(0),\widehat{f}(1),\ldots,\widehat{f}(N).

The family has
\leq 2N
real degrees of freedom and the data has
2N+1
, since
\widehat{f}(0)
is always real.

Several similar problems with functions supported on intervals are treated in
[
PW13
]
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that the sets
E,E^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathbb{T}}
are both unions of at most
N

open arcs and that
\widehat{\chi_{E}}(\nu)=\widehat{\chi_{E^{\prime}}}(\nu)
for

\nu=0,1,\ldots,N
.
Then
E=E^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
let
\sigma_{k}(x)
denote the
k
-th elementary
symmetric function of
the variables
x_{i}
and let
s_{k}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{k}
denote the
k
-th
power sum of the
x_{i}
.

We use the Newton-Girard formulas

(1)

k\cdot\sigma_{k}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{i-1}\sigma_{k-i}(x)s_{i}(x),\ \ (k\geq 1).

Note that
\sigma_{0}(x)=1
.
What is important about these formulas is that if we know
s_{1},\ldots,s_{\nu}

then we know also the numbers
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\nu}
, for all
\nu\geq 1
.
The precise dependence is irrelevant for our purposes.

Suppose
N
is given and that the sets
E=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}I_{j}
and
E^{\prime}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}I_{j}^{\prime}
(with
n,n^{\prime}\leq N
and the
I_{j},I_{j}^{\prime}
being arcs) have the same Fourier coefficients of order up to
N
:

\widehat{\chi_{E}}(\nu)=\widehat{\chi_{E^{\prime}}}(\nu),\ \ \nu=0,1,2,\ldots,N.

If
I_{j}=(a_{j},b_{j})
and
I_{j}^{\prime}=(a_{j}^{\prime},b_{j}^{\prime})
then, differentiating the functions

\chi_{E},\chi_{E^{\prime}}
, we obtain
that the measures

\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{a_{j}}-\delta_{b_{j}},\ \ \mu^{\prime}=\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\delta_{a_{j}^{\prime}}-\delta_{b_{j}^{\prime}},

have the same Fourier coefficients of order up to
N
. Writing
z_{j}=e^{-2\pi ia_{j}},w_{j}=e^{-2\pi ib_{j}},z_{j}^{\prime}=e^{-2\pi ia_{j}^{\prime}},w_{j}^{\prime}=e^{-2\pi ib_{j}^{\prime}}
we obtain the relations

\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}^{\nu}-w_{j}^{\nu}=\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}z_{j}^{\prime\nu}-w_{j}^{\prime\nu},\ \ \ \nu=1,2,\ldots,N.

From this we get
s_{\nu}(z,w^{\prime})=s_{\nu}(z^{\prime},w)
, for
\nu=1,2,\ldots,N
, where

s_{\nu}(z,w^{\prime}):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}^{\nu}+\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}w_{j}^{\prime\nu},\mbox{\ and\ }s_{\nu}(z^{\prime},w)=\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}z_{j}^{\prime\nu}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}^{\nu}.

By the Newton-Girard formulas the numbers
s_{1},\ldots,s_{N}
determine the numbers
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{N}
,
hence we have

(2)

\sigma_{\nu}(z,w^{\prime})=\sigma_{\nu}(z^{\prime},w),\ \ \ \nu=1,2,\ldots,N.

Write
M=n+n^{\prime}\leq 2N
and observe that

\sigma_{M}(z,w^{\prime})=\sigma_{M}(z^{\prime},w),

i.e.,

\prod z_{j}w_{j}^{-1}=\prod z_{j}^{\prime}w_{j}^{\prime-1}
. This comes from
the fact that the total length of
E
and
E^{\prime}
is the same, as testified by

\widehat{\chi_{E}}(0)=\widehat{\chi_{E^{\prime}}}(0)
.

We now use the fact that
{\left|{z_{j}}\right|}={\left|{w_{j}}\right|}={\left|{z_{j}^{\prime}}\right|}={\left|{w_{j}^{\prime}}\right|}=1
:

(3)

\overline{\sigma_{k}(z,w^{\prime})}=\sigma_{k}\left(\frac{1}{z},\frac{1}{w^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{\sigma_{M-k}(z,w^{\prime})}{\sigma_{M}(z,w^{\prime})},\ \ \ k=0,1,\ldots,M,

and similarly for the elementary symmetric functions of the vector
(z^{\prime},w)
.
For
k=1,2,\ldots,M-N-1\leq N
we obtain from (
3
) the missing values
of
\sigma_{\nu}
for
\nu=N+1,\ldots,M-1
.

We have proved that

\sigma_{\nu}(z,w^{\prime})=\sigma_{\nu}(z^{\prime},w),\ \ \ \nu=0,1,2,\ldots,M,

hence the multisets
{\left\{{z_{j},w_{j}^{\prime}}\right\}}
and
{\left\{{z_{j}^{\prime},w_{j}}\right\}}
are equal,
since the elementary symmetric functions determine the polynomials
p
and

q
with roots at
{\left\{{z,w^{\prime}}\right\}}
and
{\left\{{z^{\prime},w}\right\}}
respectively and they are equal.
But
{\left\{{z_{j}}\right\}}\cap{\left\{{w_{j}}\right\}}={\left\{{z_{j}^{\prime}}\right\}}\cap{\left\{{w_{j}^{\prime}}\right\}}=\emptyset
so
the only possibility is that
{\left\{{z_{j}}\right\}}={\left\{{z_{j}^{\prime}}\right\}}
and
{\left\{{w_{j}}\right\}}={\left\{{w_{j}^{\prime}}\right\}}
, as we had to show.

∎

Remark 1
.

Of course it is also possible to solve this problem using Prony’s method, which we will introduce in Section
3.1
. However, Prony’s method will require more samples, as it cannot exploit the fact that the coefficients of
\hat{\chi_{E}}
alternate between plus and minus one.

2.2.
Determination from other sets of Fourier coefficients

The problem is sensitive to the choice of which Fourier coefficients to use in order to determine the set, even in the case of one interval
E=(a,b)
. In this case, we have

(4)

2\pi i\nu\widehat{\chi_{E}}(\nu)=e^{-2\pi i\nu a}-e^{-2\pi i\nu b}:=z^{\nu}-w^{\nu},\;\nu\neq 0.

(Here, again,
z=e^{-2\pi ia}
,
w=e^{-2\pi ib}
.)

A single Fourier coefficient is not enough to determine the interval uniquely: for
\nu=0
this is obvious as
\widehat{\chi_{E}}(0)=b-a
. For
\nu=1
, consider
E^{\prime}=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
such that
z^{\prime}=-w
,
w^{\prime}=-z
(but this is the only other option). Last, for
\nu\geq 2
, we may take
E=(0,\frac{1}{\nu})
and
E^{\prime}=(a^{\prime},a^{\prime}+\frac{1}{\nu})
, for some
a^{\prime}\neq 0
.

For two coefficients, apart from the case when
\widehat{\chi_{E}}(0),\widehat{\chi_{E}}(1)
are known, thus defining
E
uniquely, it is also easy to see that knowing
\widehat{\chi_{E}}(1),\widehat{\chi_{E}}(2)
also determines
E
. No other combination of two Fourier coefficients

\widehat{\chi_{E}}(m),\widehat{\chi_{E}}(n)

determines the set (we skip the details).

For
N\geq 2
, let us point out that even equality of all Fourier coefficients of two sets

E=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}(a_{j},b_{j}),\ \ \ E^{\prime}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}(a_{j}^{\prime},b_{j}^{\prime})

at
1,2,\ldots 2N-1
, is not enough to conclude
E=E^{\prime}
, while, by Theorem
1
, the Fourier coefficients from 0 to
N
suffice.

To prove the claim, let us begin with a simple observation. Let
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2N})
. Then,

(5)

s_{1}(x)=s_{2}(x)=\ldots s_{2N-1}(x)=0\iff\sigma_{1}(x)=\sigma_{2}(x)=\ldots\sigma_{2N-1}(x)=0.

(We keep the notation for power sums and elementary symmetric functions that was used in the proof of Theorem
1
.)
The fact that vanishing power sums give vanishing elementary symmetric functions follows directly from the Newton-Girard formulas (
1
). For the converse, observe that always
s_{1}(x)=\sigma_{1}(x)
and then apply (
1
) consecutively for
k=2,\dots,2N-1
(or observe that the power sums are themselves symmetric functions, hence they can be expressed via the elementary symmetric functions).

Fix some
\theta\in(0,\pi/N)
and consider two regular
2N
-gons
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
on the unit circle, with vertices arranged counterclockwise, defined by

V_{\mathcal{P}}={\left\{{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N},w_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,w_{N}^{\prime}}\right\}}

and

V_{\mathcal{Q}}={\left\{{w_{1},\ldots,w_{N},z_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{N}^{\prime},z_{1}^{\prime}}\right\}}

(see Fig.
1
, where
V_{\mathcal{P}}
is blue and
V_{\mathcal{Q}}
is red) where

z_{1}=1,\ w_{1}=e^{i\theta}.

\displaystyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}(z_{j},w_{j})

(shown for

It follows that the numbers in
V_{\mathcal{P}}
are the roots of the polynomial
z^{2N}-1
and that the numbers in
V_{\mathcal{Q}}
are the roots of the polynomial
z^{2N}-e^{i2N\theta}
. Since the elementary symmetric functions of the roots of a polynomial are the coefficients of the polynomial, it follows that the numbers
\sigma_{\nu}(z,w^{\prime})
,
\sigma_{\nu}(z^{\prime},w)
vanish for
\nu=1,\dots,2N-1
. By (
5
), we also have
s_{\nu}(z,w^{\prime})=s_{\nu}(z^{\prime},w)=0
for
\nu=1,\dots,2N-1
. Then, we have
\widehat{\chi_{E}}(\nu)=\widehat{\chi_{E^{\prime}}}(\nu)
for all
\nu=1,\dots,2N-1
, however the sets
E
,
E^{\prime}
do not coincide, as implied by the arrangement of
V_{\mathcal{P}}
,
V_{\mathcal{Q}}
.

It is interesting to see that many more examples are possible with the points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{N},w_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,w_{N}^{\prime}
located at the vertices of a regular
2N
-gon and the points
z_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{N}^{\prime},w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}
located at the vertices of a rotated regular
2N
-gon, but not necessarily in the order shown in Fig.
1
. As explained above these locations guarantee that the two sets
\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}(z_{j},w_{j})
and
\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}(z_{j}^{\prime},w_{j}^{\prime})
(with an obvious and excusable abuse of notation) have the same Fourier coefficients of order
1,2,\ldots,2N-1
and they are of course not equal.

One needs to find the arrangements of the points
z_{j},w_{j},z_{j}^{\prime},w_{j}^{\prime}
on the vertices of these two polygons so that the following rules are satisfied:

(1)

The points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{N},w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}
appear on the circle in the counterclockwise order. Same for the points
z_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{N}^{\prime},w_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,w_{N}^{\prime}
. This rule ensures that the arcs
(z_{i},w_{i})
are non-overlapping and the same for the arcs
(z_{i}^{\prime},w_{i}^{\prime})
.

(2)

The
z_{j}
and
w_{j}^{\prime}
are blue (polygon
{\mathcal{P}}
) and the
z_{j}^{\prime}
and
w_{j}
are red (polygon
{\mathcal{Q}}
).

We can enumerate these arrangements by viewing this problem as a variant of the so-called
Terquem’s problem
(see, e.g.,
[
Sta11
, Problem 30 on p. 120, and solution on p. 170]
). Terquem’s problem asks in how many ways we can choose a sequence
a_{1}<a_{2}<\ldots<a_{k}
from the set
{\left\{{1,2,\ldots,n}\right\}}
whose elements alternate between odd and even. By viewing blue as odd and red as even on our polygons we see that we can restate our problem as follows:

In how many ways can we select an alternating sequence
a_{1}<a_{2}<\ldots<a_{2N}
from the set
{\left\{{1,2,\ldots,4N}\right\}}
such that its complement is also alternating.

The numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{2N}
correspond to the choices for the labels
z_{1},w_{1},\ldots,z_{N},w_{N}
and the complementary set corresponds to the labels
z_{1}^{\prime},w_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{N}^{\prime},w_{N}^{\prime}
.

Following
[
Sta11
, p. 170]
we can encode the sequence
a_{i}
via the sequence
b_{i}
defined by

b_{i}=a_{i}-i+1.

This sequence is increasing

b_{1}\leq b_{2}\leq\ldots\leq b_{2N}

and gives back the sequence
a_{i}
as
a_{i}=b_{i}+i-1
(which is strictly increasing).
It also satisfies the bounds

1\leq b_{i}\leq 2N+1.

The alternating property of the sequence
a_{i}
translates exactly to the
b_{i}
being all odd. If we did not care about the complement of the sequence
a_{i}
being also alternating then, as explained in
[
Sta11
]
, all we would have to do is select
with replacement
the
2N
numbers
b_{i}
among the odd numbers of the set
{\left\{{1,2,\ldots,2N+1}\right\}}
, that is from
N+1
numbers. To ensure that the complement is also an alternating sequence it is necessary and sufficient to ensure that the intervals (consecutive values) defined by the sequence
a_{i}
are all of
even
length. But an interval of the
a_{i}
translates into an interval of constancy for the corresponding
b_{i}
. Summarizing, the
b_{i}
must be odd and be selected an even number of times each. To achieve this we select with replacement
N
numbers from the odd numbers of the set
{\left\{{1,2,\ldots,2N+1}\right\}}
and then double the number of times each selection appears. This enumerates the
b_{i}
and therefore also the
a_{i}
. We omit the details.

Point masses on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}

3.1.
Point masses on
{\mathbb{T}}

The one-dimensional problem has a very long history, going back to Gaspard de Prony’s work
[
dP95
]
from 1795. Since then, many solutions have been proposed, like Pisarenko’s method
[
Pis73
]
, MUSIC
[
Sch86
]
or ESPRIT
[
RK89
]
. Still, there is ongoing research on further improvements, see
[
DPP21
]
for a recent approach.

We show here another approach from the Electrical Engineering literature (see e.g.
[
VMB02
]
) with so-called annihilation filters. We will make use of Theorem
2
repeatedly when solving the same problem on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
\mu
is a measure on
{\mathbb{T}}
which is a sum of at most
N
complex point masses.
Then
\mu
is determined by the data

(6)

\widehat{\mu}(j),\ \ \ j=-N+1,-N+2,\ldots,N.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{j}\delta_{\theta_{j}}
, with
c_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
, the

\theta_{j}
all different and
K\leq N
.
It follows that

(7)

\widehat{\mu}(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{j}\rho_{j}^{-n},\ \ \ (\rho_{j}=e^{2\pi i\theta_{j}},\ n\in{\mathbb{Z}}).

Define the polynomial

a(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{K}(z-\rho_{j})=\widehat{a}(0)+\widehat{a}(1)z+\ldots+\widehat{a}(K-1)z^{K-1}+z^{K},

and note that
\widehat{a}(n)
also denotes its Fourier coefficients when viewed as a function on
{\mathbb{T}}
.
Since
a\mu=0
it follows that

(8)

\widehat{a}*\widehat{\mu}(n)=0,\ \ \ (n\in{\mathbb{Z}}).

We now show that the polynomial
a(z)
is determined up to constant multiples by the conditions

(9)

\displaystyle\deg{a}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K

(10)

\displaystyle\widehat{a}*\widehat{\mu}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\ \ \ (n\in[1,K]).

It is enough to show that (
9
) and (
10
) together imply
that
a(z)
vanishes on the
\rho_{j}
(this is the same as (
8
)),
whose number is
K
, the same as the degree of
a
.

Substituting (
7
) in (
10
) we get for
l=1,2,\ldots,K

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K}\widehat{a}(k)\widehat{\mu}(l-k),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K}\widehat{a}(k)\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{j}\rho_{j}^{k-l},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{j}\rho_{j}^{-l}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\widehat{a}(k)\rho_{j}^{k},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{j}\rho_{j}^{-l}a(\rho_{j}).

Observe that the
K\times K
Vandermonde matrix
\rho_{j}^{-\nu}
,

j,\nu=1,\ldots,K
, is nonsingular and,
therefore, all
c_{j}a(\rho_{j})
are 0,
j=1,2,\ldots,K
. Since all
c_{j}
are nonzero this implies that

a(\rho_{j})=0,\ \ \ j=1,2,\ldots,K.

Suppose
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
are two measures with the same Fourier data (
6
):

\mu_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{1}}c_{1,j}\delta_{\theta_{1,j}},\ \ \mu_{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{2}}c_{2,j}\delta_{\theta_{2,j}},\ K_{1}\leq K_{2}\leq N,

and

(11)

\widehat{\mu_{1}}(n)=\widehat{\mu_{2}}(n),\ \ (n=-N+1,\ldots,N-1,N).

We are assuming that all
\theta_{1,j}
are distinct and so are all
\theta_{2,j}
, and
that
c_{1,j},c_{2,j}\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
.
Write also
\rho_{1,j}=e^{2\pi i\theta_{1,j}}
and

\rho_{2,j}=e^{2\pi i\theta_{2,j}}
.

Write

a_{1}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{K_{1}}(z-\rho_{1,j}),\ \ a_{2}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{K_{2}}(z-\rho_{2,j}).

We have
\widehat{a_{1}}*\widehat{\mu_{1}}(n)=\widehat{a_{2}}*\widehat{\mu_{2}}(n)=0
for all
n
but we also
have
\widehat{a_{1}}*\widehat{\mu_{2}}(n)=0
for
n=1,\ldots,N
, because of (
11
).
Applying the fact that
a(z)
is determined by (
9
) and (
10
) with
K=K_{2}
we obtain
that
a_{1}(z)=a_{2}(z)
hence
K_{1}=K_{2}
and
{\left\{{\rho_{1,j}}\right\}}={\left\{{\rho_{2,j}}\right\}}
.

It remains to show that the linear map

(c_{1},\ldots,c_{K})\to(\widehat{\mu}(1),\ldots,\widehat{\mu}(K))

is injective. This map is given by (
7
) and it is easily seen to be nonsingular as its
determinant is a multiple of the Vandermonde determinant.

Remark 2
.

The number of samples is sharp, as we recover the
2N
parameters using
2N
samples. However, note that we use complex samples, to recover
N
complex parameters and
N
parameters in
{\mathbb{T}}
. One can easily check that the proof extends to the case of point measures on
{\mathbb{T}}+i{\mathbb{R}}
, where (
7
) becomes

\widehat{\mu}(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{j}\rho_{j}^{-n},\ \ \ (\rho_{j}=e^{2\pi i(\theta_{j}+i\xi_{j})},\ n\in{\mathbb{Z}}).

In case of real coefficients and point measures on
{\mathbb{T}}
one can utilize
\overline{\hat{\mu}(-k)}=\hat{\mu}(k)
to use only
\hat{\mu}(k),~k=0,\ldots,N
. This observation is an important part of the unitary ESPRIT algorithm
[
HN95
]
, which uses only real-valued computation to solve the problem.

3.2.
Connections to interpolation

Suppose
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}\delta_{u_{j}}
, where
u_{j}=(u_{j1},\ldots,u_{jd})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
are distinct points, all
c_{j}
are non-zero and
d>1
.
Can we recover
\mu
from a number of Fourier coefficients that is close to the number of degrees of
freedom (in this case
(d+1)N
or
(d+2)N
depending on whether
c_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}
or
c_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}
)?

Here the existing results do not seem to be final. In the special case where all
u_{j1}
are different (or equivalently all
u_{jk}
for a fixed
k=1,\ldots,d
) the problem is solved using the one-dimensional theory with
O(dN)
Fourier coefficients, namely by using the Fourier coefficients at the locations
(m,\epsilon_{2},\dots,\epsilon_{d})
with
m=0,1,\ldots,N
and
\epsilon_{j}\in{\left\{{0,1}\right\}}
, assuming all
u_{j1}
are different (we generalize this in Theorem
8
).
In the general case and without imposing any restrictions on the locations
u_{j}
, it has only been known until this work how to recover
\mu
using
O(N^{2})
Fourier coefficients
[
MV04
, Section III.C]
in the two dimensional case and using
O(N^{2}\log^{2d-2}N)
coefficients in the general case
[
Sau18
]
.

If one allows for the collection of Fourier coefficients used to depend on the data then one can recover the parameters with
O(N)
Fourier coefficients, see
[
PW13
]
for the case
d=2
and
[
CL18
]
for arbitrary
d
. It was conjectured in
[
PW13
]
that recovery of the parameters in this problem with
d=2
is always possible with
O(N)
Fourier coefficients which do not depend on the data and are on four predetermined lines. This conjecture was disproved in
[
DI15
]
but the possibility remains that some more general set of
O(N)
Fourier coefficients suffices. (In Theorem
9
we show that
O(N\log N)
Fourier coefficients suffice.) For the general
d
dimensional case it was shown in
[
GS17
]
that taking a total of
O(N^{2})
samples on scattered line allows for a reconstruction.

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
k=1,2,3,\ldots
. We call
\Omega

k
-interpolating
if
whenever
u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,
\ell\leq k
, are distinct and
d_{1},\ldots,d_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{C}}
we can find coefficients

c_{\omega}
,
\omega\in\Omega
, such that

d_{j}=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}c_{\omega}e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot u_{j}},\ \ j=1,2,\ldots,\ell.

We call
\Omega

k
-sufficient

if we can recover any measure
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}c_{j}\delta_{u_{j}}
,
\ell\leq k
,
(with unknown
c_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
, unknown and distinct
u_{j}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
) from its Fourier coefficients at
\Omega

\widehat{\mu}(\omega)=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}c_{j}e^{-2\pi i\omega\cdot u_{j}},\ \ \omega\in\Omega.

The connection between the concepts of
k
-interpolation and
k
-sufficiency is the following.

Theorem 3
.

(12)

\Omega\mbox{ is $(2N)$-interpolating }\Longrightarrow\Omega\mbox{ is $N$-sufficient }\Longrightarrow\Omega\mbox{ is $N$-interpolating.}

Proof.

To prove Theorem
3
let us first make the
following remark, which says that if we can solve the problem of sufficiency
with the locations fixed then
we can also solve the problem with unknown (but fewer) locations.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
is such that the mapping

(13)

\mu\to(\widehat{\mu}(\omega),\ \omega\in\Omega)

is injective on the set

(14)

{\left\{{\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}\delta_{u_{j}}:\ c_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}}\right\}},

for any choice of

and distinct points
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.
Then the mapping (
13
) is injective also on the set

(15)

{\left\{{\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}c_{j}\delta_{u_{j}}:\ \ell\leq N,c_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}},u_{j}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\mbox{ $c_{j}\neq 0$ and the $u_{j}$ are distinct}}\right\}}.

Proof.

Suppose
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{1}}c_{j}\delta_{u_{j}}
and
\nu=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{2}}d_{j}\delta_{v_{j}}
,

\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\leq N
,
are two different measures with the same image under (
13
).
Then
\mu-\nu
is a non-zero measure supported on
\leq 2N
points and is mapped to
0

under (
13
).
This conflicts with the injectivity of (
13
) on the set (
14
).

∎

For the mapping (
13
) on the set (
14
) to be injective it is necessary and sufficient that the matrix

(16)

\left(e^{2\pi iu_{j}\cdot\omega}\right)_{j=1,\ldots,k,\ \omega\in\Omega}

has rank
k
.
Write
e_{\omega}(x)=e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot x}
.

Lemma 5
.

The mapping (
13
) on the set (
14
)
is injective if and only if the functions
e_{\omega}
form an interpolating
set for the set
{\left\{{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k}}\right\}}
,
i.e. for any values

there is a
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear combination of the

which
takes the value

at
u_{j}
.

Proof.

The
e_{\omega}
are interpolating at the
u_{j}
if and only if the rank of the
matrix in (

) is

. By the preceding remark this is equivalent to the mapping (
13
) on the set (

) being injective.

∎

Let us complete the proof of Theorem
3
. If
\Omega
is
(2N)
-interpolating
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{2N}
are distinct points in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}

it follows from Lemma
5
that the mapping
\mu\to\widehat{\mu}|_{\Omega}
is injective
on the set
(
14
)
(with

). From Lemma
4
it follows that
it is also injective on the set (

), hence
\Omega
is
N
-sufficient.

If
\Omega
is
N
-sufficient and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,

, are distinct points then the matrix
(
16
) has rank

. Therefore the mapping (

) is injective (with the

fixed)
and from Lemma

we get that the functions

are interpolating,
which is what it means for

-interpolating.

∎

Now it becomes clear that the situation in dimension 2 is significantly harder than in dimension 1.
The reason is that interpolation is harder. Indeed, in dimension 1 one can easily find a set
of
N
functions the linear combinations of which can interpolate any data on any
N
points. One such example
of functions are the monomials
1,x,x^{2},\ldots,x^{N-1}
and another example
are the functions
1,e^{2\pi ix},e^{2\pi i2x},\ldots,e^{2\pi i(N-1)x}
(when all
u_{j}\in{\mathbb{T}}
).

Such a set of functions is called a
Chebyshev or Haar system
and it is
well known and easy to prove that
continuous
Chebyshev systems
do not exist except in dimension 1
[
Mai56
]
. Indeed, suppose that
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is an open set and
the continuous functions
f_{j}:S\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
j=1,2,\ldots,N
,
are such that for any set of
N
distinct points
u_{j}\in S

we can find a linear combination of the
f_{j}
which interpolates any given
real
data at the
u_{j}
.
This means that for any choice of the distinct points
u_{j}
the determinant of the matrix
f_{i}(u_{j})
,
i,j=1,2,\ldots,N
,
is non-zero. Choose then the
u_{j}
to belong to an open disk in
S
and carry out a continuous movement
of the points
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
so that they do not collide between themselves and with any of the other points
and such that, at the end of the motion, the two points have exchanged their positions. The determinant
of the matrix has changed sign and, since it has varied continuously during the motion, it follows that the
determinant has vanished at some point during the exchange, a contradiction.
It is proved in
[
Mai56
]
that the existence of a (continuous) Chebyshev system on a set

S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is only possible when
S
is homeomorphic to a closed subset of a circle.

This argument is strictly for the real case of course but it has been extended
[
SY61
]

to the case of complex functions:
there is a complex Chebyshev system for domains in
{\mathbb{C}}
but not for domains in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
or in higher dimension.
More specifically, it is proved in
[
SY61
,
HU73
]

that a
complex
continuous Chebyshev
system exists on a locally connected set
S
if and only if
S
is homeomorphic to a closed subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
This result allows us to prove that the situation in
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
is strictly worse than in
{\mathbb{T}}
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
d>1
. Suppose
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
is
N
-sufficient. Then
{\left|{\Omega}\right|}>N
.

Proof.

If such an
\Omega
had size
N
then, according to Theorem
3
, the corresponding
set of characters
e_{\omega}(x)=e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot x}
,
\omega\in\Omega
, would be a continuous Chebyshev system on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,
According to
[
SY61
,
HU73
]
this would make
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
embeddable into the plane,
which it is not.

∎

Again using the connection to interpolation let us now give a new proof, different from the one given in
[
MV04
, Section III.C]
for the case
d=2
and
[
KPRvdO16
,
Sau17
]
for general
d
, of the following fact.

Theorem 7
.

There is
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
of size
O(N^{d})
which is
N
-sufficient.

Proof.

It is enough to produce a set
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
of size
O(N^{d})
such that the set of corresponding
exponentials
e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot x}
is
2N
-interpolating, i.e. its linear combinations can interpolate
any values at any
2N
distinct points in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.
We use the fact that for any set of
2N
distinct points in
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
and any complex data
on them there is a complex
polynomial in
d
variables of degree at most
2N-1
(the degree of each monomial is the sum of the exponents of the variables) which interpolates the data.
To see this observe that for any set of
2N
distinct points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2N}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
we can find a vector
u\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
such that the complex numbers
t_{i}=v\cdot x_{i}
are all different. Let now
p
be a one-variable polynomial of degree
\leq 2N-1
which interpolates the given data on the points
t_{i}
. Then
q(x)=p(v\cdot x)
is a two-variable polynomial that interpolates the given data on the points
x_{i}
. The degree of
q
is no larger than
2N-1
.

Take
\Omega={\left\{{m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}:\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}\leq 2N-1}\right\}}
.
Suppose

u_{1},\ldots,u_{2N}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}

are distinct and
d_{1},\ldots,d_{2N}\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
Let
p(z)=\sum_{m}p_{m}z^{m}
have degree at most
2N-1
and interpolate the data
d_{k}
at the points

(e^{2\pi iu_{j1}},\ldots,e^{2\pi iu_{jd}})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
,
j=1,2,\ldots,2N
(note that these are distinct points as
the
u_{d}
are in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
not in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
).
We have

d_{j}=p(e^{2\pi iu_{j1}},\ldots,e^{2\pi iu_{jd}})=\sum_{m\in\Omega}p_{m}e^{2\pi iu_{j}\cdot m},

which means that the functions
e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot x}
,
\omega\in\Omega
, are interpolating
the arbitrary data
d_{j}
at the
2N
arbitrary points
u_{j}
, as we had to prove.

∎

3.3.
Small sufficient sets for
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}

Next we provide a case where the sufficient number of coefficients for
N
points of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
is
O(k^{d-1}N)
, for some
1\leq k\leq N
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}\delta_{u_{j}}
, where
u_{j}=(x_{j},y_{j})\in{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d-1}
are distinct points and all
c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
. Assume that the number of points
u_{j}
that share the same
x
coordinate is at most
k
, for some
1\leq k\leq N
. Then
\mu
can be recovered by
O(k^{d-1}N)
Fourier coefficients.

Proof.

Write
U={\left\{{u_{j}:\;j=1,\ldots,N}\right\}}
and
X={\left\{{x_{j}:\;j=1,\ldots,N}\right\}}
for the set of
distinct

x
that appear as first coordinates for the points in
U
. Notice that
X
may have fewer than
N
points.

Recall that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}(m,n)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}e^{-2\pi i(mx_{j}+n\cdot y_{j})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in X}\left(\sum_{y:\;(x,y)\in U}c_{(x,y)}e^{-2\pi in\cdot y}\right)e^{-2\pi imx},\;m\in\mathbb{Z},\,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d-1}.

For fixed
n
the numbers
\widehat{\mu}(m,n)
are the Fourier coefficients of a collection of point masses at the points of
X
(some of these point masses may be 0).

Consider the data

\widehat{\mu}(-N,\ell),\;\ldots,\;\widehat{\mu}(0,\ell),\;\widehat{\mu}(1,\ell),\;\ldots,\;\widehat{\mu}(N,\ell),

where
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d-1}
is fixed. By Theorem
2
, we can recover the sums

(17)

S(x,\ell)=\sum\limits_{y:\;(x,y)\in U}c_{(x,y)}e^{-2\pi i\ell\cdot y},\quad\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d-1}.

and the corresponding
x\in X
. Notice that we only “see” the
x
for which
S(x,\ell)\neq 0
.

For fixed
x\in X
, define the measure on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d-1}

\lambda_{x}=\sum\limits_{y:\;(x,y)\in U}c_{(x,y)}\delta_{y},

which is supported on at most
k
locations on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d-1}
. By Theorem
7
, there is
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d-1}
of size at most
O(k^{d-1})
that is
k
-sufficient. Thus, knowing the Fourier coefficients of
\lambda_{x}
at
\ell\in\Omega
is sufficient to recover the measure. Knowing these Fourier coefficients means precisely knowing the sums in (
17
), so we recover the points
y
sitting over each
x\in X
and the corresponding coefficients. The proof of the Theorem is complete.

∎

Finally we come to the main result of this section. One can view Theorem
9
as a more sophisticated version of Theorem
8
, where the gain comes from being able to distinguish which
x\in{\mathbb{T}}
have many points projected onto them. This set of
x
cannot be large.

This theorem was first proved in
[
Sau18
]
, using techniques from computational algebra. We give an elementary proof.

Theorem 9
.

There is
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
of size
{\left|{\Omega}\right|}\leq C_{d}N\log^{d-1}(N)
which is
N
-interpolating.
One such set is the positive octant of the hyperbolic cross

\Gamma_{N}^{d}=\left\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}~:~\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(n_{j}+1\right)\leq N\right\}.

Proof.

Let
U={\left\{{u_{j}:\;j=1,\ldots,N}\right\}}
. As noted in (
16
), it suffices to show that the vectors

v_{\Gamma}(u_{j})=(e^{2\pi iu_{j}\cdot w})_{\omega\in\Gamma_{N}^{d}},\quad j=1,\ldots,N

are linearly independent. We use induction in
d
. For the base case
d=1
, observe that the matrix
(e^{2\pi iu_{j}\omega})_{j=1,\ldots,N,\;\omega=0,\ldots,N-1}
is Vandermonde. For
d\geq 2
, assume on the contrary that there are
c_{u}\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}},\;u\in U
(we can exclude nodes with zero coefficients) satisfying

\sum_{u\in U}c_{u}v_{\Gamma}(u)=0.

Let
X={\left\{{x_{j}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d-1}:\;j=1,\ldots,N}\right\}}
be the set of distinct
x
that appear as the first
d-1
entries of points in
U
(again,
X
may have fewer than
N
points). As in the proof of Theorem
8
, we note that the condition of linear dependence rewrites as

(18)

\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{u_{j}}e^{-2\pi i(m\cdot x_{j}+ky_{j})}=\sum_{x\in X}\left(\sum_{y:\;(x,y)\in U}c_{(x,y)}e^{-2\pi iky}\right)e^{-2\pi im\cdot x}=0,\quad m\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d-1},k\in{\mathbb{Z}},\;(m,k)\in\Gamma_{N}^{d}.

Observe that we have

(19)

(m,0)\in\Gamma_{N}^{d}\;\text{ for }m\in\Gamma_{N}^{d-1}\quad\text{ and }\quad(m,k-1)\in\Gamma_{N}^{d}\;\text{ for }m\in\Gamma_{\lfloor N/k\rfloor}^{d-1},\;k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Then, as (
18
) holds for
(m,0)\in\Gamma_{N}^{d}
for all

, we can use the induction hypothesis to conclude that

(20)

\sum_{y:\;(x,y)\in U}c_{(x,y)}e^{-2\pi iyk}=0,\quad\text{for }k=0,\;\text{for all }x\in X.

We partition
X
according to how many points of
U
project to each point:

X=X_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup X_{r}\ \ \ (r\leq N),

where
X_{t}={\left\{{x\in X:\ {\left|{{\left\{{y:(x,y)\in U}\right\}}}\right|}=t}\right\}}
. By (
20
), we see that

c_{(x,y)}=0\quad\text{for all }x\in X_{1},\;(x,y)\in U,

which contradicts
c_{u}\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
and thus
X_{1}=\emptyset
. Now we use the crucial observation that

(21)

|U|=\sum_{j=1}^{r}j|X_{j}|

which implies with
X_{1}=\emptyset
that
|X|\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
.

Taking data at (
19
) for
k=0,1
, by the induction hypothesis we see that (
20
) holds true for
k=0,1
. If
X_{2}
is not empty, then, for any

, the summands in (

) are exactly

, for each

. Thus, we have a homogeneous

system, with a Vandermonde matrix of coefficients, so

c_{(x,y)}=0\quad\text{for all }x\in X_{2},\;(x,y)\in U,

a contradiction. That allows us to deduce
X_{2}=\emptyset
, giving us
|X|\leq\lfloor N/3\rfloor
. Repeating the argument
r
times results in the contradiction

.

We use Theorem

to see that
