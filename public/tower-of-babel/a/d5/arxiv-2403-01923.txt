Abstract
. We address three questions posed by Bibak [ 1 ] , and generalize some results of Bibak, Lehmer and K G Ramanathan on solutions of linear congruences ∑ i = 1 k a i ​ x i ≡ b ⁡ ( mod ​ n ) \sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}x_{i}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n) . In particular, we obtain explicit expressions for the number of solutions where x i x_{i} ’s are squares modulo n n . In addition, we obtain expressions for the number of solutions with order restrictions x 1 ≥ ⋯ ≥ x k x_{1}\geq\cdots\geq x_{k} or, with strict order restrictions x 1 > ⋯ > x k x_{1}>\cdots>x_{k} in some special cases. In these results, the expressions for the number of solutions involve Ramanujan sums and are obtained using their properties.

Let

n

u_{1},\dots,u_{k}

\ell n^{k-1}

(

In a different direction, order-restricted solutions

applications. In 1962, Riordan
[
27

for all

,

and

. More recently, Bibak
[
1
]
extended Riordan’s

for all

1.1.
Distinct Solutions and Schönemann’s Theorem

Another interesting problem related to the linear
congruences that has been considered in the literature is counting
distinct solutions. This problem was first addressed in a special
case by Schönemann
[
28
]
almost two centuries ago. Let

D_{n}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
denote the number of solutions of
the linear congruence
a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n),
with all
x_{i}
distinct. Schönemann
[
28
]
proved
the following result.

Theorem (Schönemann - 1839).
Let

p
be a prime,
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
be arbitrary integers, and

\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)
and
\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\not\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)
for all
\phi\neq I\subsetneq\{1,\dots,k\}
. Then, the number
D_{p}(k,0)
is independent of the
coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
and is equal to

D_{p}(0;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!(p-1)+(p-1)\cdots(p-k+1).

Recently, in 2013, Grynkiewicz et al.

[
14
]
obtained necessary and sufficient condition to
determine
D_{n}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})>0
. In 2019, Bibak et al.
(
[
4
]
) generalized Schönemann’s theorem using a graph
theoretic method. They proved the following result:

Theorem 4
.

(Theorem 2.3,
[
4
]
)
Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{k},b
be arbitrary integers and
n
be a positive integer, and
(\sum_{i\in I}a_{i},n)=1
for all
\phi\neq I\subsetneq\{1,\dots,k\}
. Then, we have

D_{n}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})

=\begin{cases}(-1)^{k}(k-1)!+(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1),&\text{if }\big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i},n\big)\nmid b,\\
(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\big(\big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i},n\big)-1\big)+(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1),&\text{if }\big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i},n\big)\mid b.\end{cases}

Furthermore, they posed a question about finding an
explicit formula for
D_{n}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
without
imposing any restrictions on the gcd of
a_{i}
’s and
n
(see,
problem 1,
[
4
]
). When
a_{1}=\dots=a_{k}=a
, the problem
of counting solutions of strict order-restricted linear congruence
is equivalent to counting the distinct solutions of the linear
congruence up to permutations. As a consequence of Theorem

2
, we have the following corollary:

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. Then, for any given integer
a
with
f=(a,n)
and
f\mid b
, we have

(6)

D_{n}(a,b)=D_{n}(b;a,\dots,a)=\frac{k!f(-1)^{k}}{n}\sum_{d\mid(\frac{n}{f},k)}(-1)^{\frac{k}{d}}\binom{\frac{n}{d}}{\frac{k}{d}}C_{d}(b),

where
C_{d}(b)
denote the Ramanujan sum.

Since all the coefficients are
a
, the gcd conditions mentioned in the Theorem
4
are the same as to the conditions
(a\cdot i,n)=1
, for all
1\leq i\leq k-1
. Thus, assuming these conditions on
n
implies that
f=(a,n)=1
and either
(k,n)=1
or
k
is the smallest prime divisor of
n
. Suppose
k
is the smallest prime divisor of
n
, then by (
6
), we have

\displaystyle D_{n}(a,b)=

\displaystyle\frac{k!(-1)^{k}}{n}\bigg((-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}C_{1}(b)-\binom{\frac{n}{k}}{1}C_{k}(b)\bigg),

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(-1)^{k}(k-1)!+(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1),&\text{if }k\nmid b,\\
(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!(k-1)+(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1),&\text{if }k\mid b.\end{cases}

Suppose
(k,n)=1
, then from (
6
) it follows that

D_{n}(b)=(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1).

Therefore, the obtained formula (
6
) provides a proof
of Theorem
4
in the special case when all
coefficients
a_{1}=\dots=a_{k}=a
. Moreover, this formula also
addresses the problem posed in
[
4
]
for the specific case
where
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
are all equal to
a
.

2.
Square solutions - some subtleties

We say that
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is a square solution
of (
1
), if
x_{i}
’s are squares modulo
n
, for
1\leq i\leq k
. Given integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{k},b
and a
positive integer
n
, it is an interesting problem to determine the
necessary and sufficient conditions that guarantee the existence of
a square solution to the congruence represented by (
1
). Thus, we are looking at the set

\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{k}:\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}x_{i}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n),x_{i}=y_{i}^{2},y_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}\}.

We point
out that square solutions cannot simply be counted by enumerating
the set

\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{k}:\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}y_{i}^{2}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)\}.

For instance, if we consider the congruence

x_{1}+x_{2}\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 27)
, there are four square solutions

\{(1,0),(0,1),(9,19),(19,9)\}

whereas, in the latter set

\{(y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{9}^{2}:y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 27)\},

6
elements correspond to
(1,0)
and
12
elements correspond to

(9,19)
. Thus, the complexity of this problem can vary
significantly, depending on the specific values of
a_{i}
,
b
, and

n
. However, a necessary condition for the latter set to be
non-empty also gives a necessary condition for a square solution to
exist. One can easily see that when
n=p^{\ell}
, if
(b,p)=1
and
there exists a subset
S
of
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}
such that

s=\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\ell}}^{*}\ \text{and }\bigg(\frac{s}{p}\bigg)=\bigg(\frac{b}{p}\bigg)

then (
1
) has a square solution
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
, namely

x_{i}=\begin{cases}s^{-1}b,&\text{if }a_{i}\in S,\\
0,&\text{otherwise }.\end{cases}

But then, due to Lemma
6
, the above observation holds for any odd positive
n
. A related problem explored in the literature is the counting of representations of an integer
b
as a sum of squares modulo
n
, where
b
is any given integer. More generally, for given integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{k},b
and positive integers
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
and
n
counting the number of solution of the congruence

(7)

a_{1}x_{1}^{t_{1}}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}^{t_{k}}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)

has also been studied in the literature; for example (see,
[
9
,
13
,
16
,
22
,
30
]
and §8.6 of
[
17
]
). When
n
is an odd prime,
t
is any positive integer and
t_{1}=\dots=t_{k}=t
, the solutions of (
7
) were first studied by Lebesgue in 1837 (see, Chapter X of
[
12
]
). In 1932, Hull
[
16
]
proved a formula for counting the solutions to (
7
), when
t
is any positive integer such that
t_{1}=\dots=t_{k}=t
and
a_{1}=\dots=a_{k}=1
. Recently, Tóth
[
30
]
investigated the solutions of (
7
) under various conditions on the
a_{i}
’s, depending on the modulus
n
when
t_{1}=\dots=t_{k}=2
. More recently, Li and Ouyang
[
22
]
have provided an algorithm for computing the number of solutions of (
7
) under the additional restriction that
x_{i}
is a unit for every
i\in J\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}
.

Along with the other results, Hull (in Theorem 23,
[
16
]
) discussed a sufficient condition on
k
in order to (
7
) has a solution, when
t\geq 2
is a fixed integer,
t_{1}=\dots=t_{k}=t
and
a_{1}=\dots=a_{k}=1
. Specifically, for the case when
t=2
and
n=p^{\ell}
, where
p
is an odd prime, the provided sufficient condition is as follows:

(i)

If
(b,p)=1
, then there is a solution whenever
k\geq 2
.

(ii)

If
p\mid b
, then there is a solution whenever
k\geq 3
.

Notice that this sufficient condition also guarantees the existence of a square solution to the congruence represented by (
1
). This is because
x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{k}^{2}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)
is equivalent to
y_{1}+\dots+y_{n}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)
, where
y_{i}\equiv x_{i}^{2}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)
. As a result, (
1
) has a square solution whenever
k\geq 3
and
a_{1}=\dots=a_{k}=1
.

Furthermore, in the case of
k=1
, the congruence (
1
) has a square solution if and only if
b
is a square. Also, for
k=2
, there exist linear congruences with
a_{1}=a_{2}=1
and
(b,n)>1
that do not have square solutions. For example, consider the congruence
x_{1}+x_{2}\equiv 3\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 9)
, which lacks square solutions as the squares modulo
9
are
{0,1,4,7}
.

Unlike the case of existence, the question of counting the number of square solutions for (
1
) is not equivalent to the question of counting the number of representations as a sum of squares. This happens because the number of solutions to all solvable congruences of the form
x^{2}\equiv a\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)
may not be the same for every integer
a
, although it is the same for every coprime
a
. As a result, determining the number of representations that correspond to the same square solution is rather difficult.

For example, consider the square solutions of the congruence
x_{1}+x_{2}\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 27)
, which are given by
{(1,0),(0,1),(19,9),(9,19)}
. In this case, the number of representations corresponding to
(1,0)
is
6
, while the number of representations corresponding to
(19,9)
is
12
. This discrepancy arises because the congruence
x^{2}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 27)
has
3
solutions, and
x^{2}\equiv 9\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 27)
has
6
solutions.

Hence, our aim is to count the number of
square solutions for (
1
). Let
S_{n}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
denote the number of square solutions of
(
1
).

Theorem
1
provides a formula for
S_{n}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
.
The proof of the theorem and its corollary stated in the
introduction are given in section
4
.

3.1.
Discrete Fourier transform

An arithmetic function

if for every
b\in\mathbb{Z}
,
f(b+n)=f(b)
. From the definition (
8
), it is clear that
C_{n}(b)
is a periodic function of
b
with period
n
. For an
n
-periodic arithmetic function
f
, its discrete (ﬁnite) Fourier transform (DFT) is deﬁned to be the function

\hat{f}(b)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(j)e\bigg(\frac{-bj}{n}\bigg),\ \ \text{for}\ \ b\in\mathbb{Z}.

A Fourier representation of
f
is given by

f(b)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{f}(j)e\bigg(\frac{bj}{n}\bigg),\ \ \text{for}\ \ b\in\mathbb{Z},

which is the inverse discrete Fourier transform.

3.2.
Gauss sums and Ramanujan sums

Let
n
be a positive integer. A Dirichlet character
\chi
modulo
n
is an arithmetic function
\chi:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with period
n
which is an extension of a group homomorphism from the multiplicative group
(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{*}
to the set of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
via

\chi(m)=\begin{cases}\chi(m\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)),&\text{if }(m,n)=1,\\
0,&\text{if }(m,n)\geq 1.\end{cases}

Indeed, the extension of the trivial homomorphism
\chi_{0}
is known as the principal character modulo
n
. This particular Dirichlet character, denoted as
\chi_{0}
, is defined as follows:

\chi_{0}(m)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }(m,n)=1,\\
0,&\text{if }(m,n)\geq 1.\end{cases}

The conductor of a Dirichlet character
\chi
modulo
n
is the smallest divisor of
n
for which
\chi
is periodic. We say that a Dirichlet character
\chi
modulo
n
is primitive if the conductor of
\chi
is
n
. Otherwise, we say that
\chi
is imprimitive.

For any Dirichlet character
\chi(m)
to the modulus
n
, the Gauss sum

\tau(\chi)
is defined by

\tau(\chi)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\chi(m)e\bigg(\frac{m}{n}\bigg),

where
e(x)
denote
e^{2\pi ix}
. Further, the more general Gauss sum is the discrete Fourier transform of a Dirichlet character
\chi
modulo
n
, namely

\tau_{b}(\chi)=\hat{\chi}(-b)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\chi(m)e\bigg(\frac{bm}{n}\bigg),\ \ \text{for}\ \ b\in\mathbb{Z}.

For a Dirichlet character
\chi
modulo
n
induced by a primitive character
\chi^{\star}
modulo
n^{\star}
, the following lemma reduces the computation of the general Gauss sum
\tau_{b}(\chi)
to the Gauss sum
\tau(\chi^{\star})
.

Lemma 1
.

(Theorem 9.7 and Theorem 9.12,
[
23
]
)
Let
\chi
modulo
n
be a non-principal character induced by the primitive character
\chi^{\star}
modulo
n^{\star}
. Put
r=\frac{n}{(m,n^{\star})}
. If
n^{\star}\nmid r
then
\tau_{m}(\chi)=0
, while
n^{\star}\mid r
then

\tau_{m}(\chi)=\bar{\chi}^{\star}\bigg(\frac{m}{(n,m)}\bigg)\mu(r/n^{\star})\chi^{\star}(r/n^{\star})\frac{\varphi(n)}{\varphi(r)}\tau(\chi^{\star}).

In particular,

\tau_{m}(\chi)=\bar{\chi}(m)\mu(n/n^{\star})\chi^{\star}(n/n^{\star})\tau(\chi^{\star}),\ \text{if}\ (m,n)=1.

Furthermore, we have

|\tau_{m}(\chi^{\star})|=\sqrt{n^{\star}}.

Though, we know that
|\tau(\chi)|=\sqrt{n}
holds for any primitive character
\chi
modulo
n
the determination of the argument of the
|\tau(\chi)|
is a difficult problem. In the case of real primitive characters
\tau(\chi)
were evaluated completely by Gauss.

Lemma 2
.

(Gauss)
Let
n\geq 1
be an odd squarefree integer and let
\chi
be a real primitive character modulo
n
. Then, we have

\tau(\chi)=\varepsilon_{n}\sqrt{n},

where
\varepsilon_{n}
is defined as in (
4
).

When
\chi_{0}
is the principal character modulo
n
, the general Gauss sum
\tau_{b}(\chi_{0})
is called the Ramanujan sum, (i.e.)

(8)

\tau_{b}(\chi_{0})=C_{n}(b)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(j,n)=1\end{subarray}}^{n}e\bigg(\frac{jb}{n}\bigg).

Now, we list some properties of the Ramanujan sums:

(i)

C_{n}(b)
is integer-valued.

(ii)

For fixed
b\in\mathbb{Z}
the function
b\rightarrow C_{n}(b)
is multiplicative, (i. e.) if
(n_{1},n_{2})=1
, then
C_{n_{1}n_{2}}(b)=C_{n_{1}}(b)C_{n_{2}}(b)
.

(iii)

The function
b\rightarrow C_{n}(b)
is multiplicative for a fixed
n
if and only if
\mu(n)=1
, where
\mu
denote the Möbius function.

(iv)

C_{n}(b)
is an even function of
b
, that is,
C_{n}(b)=C_{n}((b,n))
, for every
b,n
.

(v)

For integers
b
and
n\geq 1
, we have

C_{n}(b)=\frac{\varphi(n)}{\varphi{\bigg(\frac{n}{(b,n)}\bigg)}}\mu\bigg(\frac{n}{(b,n)}\bigg).

3.3.
Generating functions of partition with certain conditions

For a positive integer
n
, a partition of
n
is a non-increasing sequence of positive integers
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{k}
whose sum is
n
. Each
p_{i}
is called a part of the partition. Let the function
p(n)
denote the number of partitions of the integer
n
. It is well-known that the generating function of the sequence
\{p(n)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}p(n)q^{n}=

\displaystyle(1+q+q^{2}+q^{3}+\dots)(1+q+q^{2}+q^{3}+\dots)(1+q^{3}+q^{6}+\dots)\dots

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-q}\cdot\frac{1}{1-q^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-q^{3}}\cdots=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-q^{i}}\ \ \text{for }\ |q|<1.

From a combinatorial perspective, the monomial chosen from the
i
-th parenthesis
1+q^{i}+q^{2i}+q^{3i}+\dots
in (
9
) represents the number of times the part
i
appears in the partition. In particular, if we choose the monomial
q^{n_{i}i}
from the
i
-th parenthesis, then the value
i
will appear
n_{i}
times in the partition. Each selection of monomials makes one contribution to the coefficient of
q^{n}
in the expression. More precisely, each contribution must be of the form
q^{1n_{1}}\cdot q^{2n_{2}}\cdot q^{3n_{3}}\cdots=q^{n_{1}+2n_{2}+3n_{3}+\cdots}
. Thus, the coefficient of
q^{n}
is the number of ways of writing
n=n_{1}+2n_{2}+3n_{3}+\cdots
where
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3},\ldots
are non-negative integers representing the total count of parts of size
1,2,3,\ldots
in the partition, respectively.

Suppose
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\dots\}
is a set of positive integers. In general, the generating function for the number of partitions of
n
into members of set
A
is

\displaystyle f_{A}(q)=\prod_{a_{j}\in A}\frac{1}{1-q^{a_{j}}}.

Moreover, the following lemma gives the generating function of the number of partitions of
n
into
k
parts each taken from the given set
A
.

Lemma 3
.

[
15
]

Let
A
be a set of positive integers. Let
k
be a positive integer and
b
be a non-negative integer. The number of partitions of
b
into
k
parts each taken
from the set
A
, is the coefficient of
q^{b}z^{k}
in

\prod_{a_{j}\in A}\frac{1}{1-zq^{a_{j}}}.

Furthermore, if we multiply the coefficient of
q^{b}z^{k}
in the expression:

\prod_{a_{j}\in A}(1-zq^{a_{j}})

by
(-1)^{k}
, then we obtain the number of distinct partitions of
b
into exactly
k
parts, with each taken from the set
A
.

4.
Square Solutions - proof of Theorem 1

It is convenient to use the characteristic function for
squares in our proofs. Using this and Hensel’s lemma, we prove
multiplicativity for square solutions.

Recall that an element
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is a square in

\mathbb{Z}_{n}
(or square modulo
n
) if and only if
x^{2}\equiv a\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)
has a solution. The units (elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}

that are relatively prime to
n
) that are squares are called
quadratic residues modulo
n
. We define a function
\square_{n}:\mathbb{Z}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{n}
by

(10)

\square_{n}(b)=\begin{cases}1,&\ \ \text{if }\ b\text{ is a square modulo}\ n,\\
0,&\ \ \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The following statement is a version of Hensel’s lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
and
f(a)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p^{m})
and

f^{\prime}(a)\not\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)
. Then there is a unique
t\in\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
such that
f(a+tp^{m})\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p^{m+1})
.

We use the following observations in the next lemma.

Let
s(n)
and
q(n)
denote the number of squares in

\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and the number of quadratic residues in

\mathbb{Z}_{n}
respectively. Equivalently,

s(n)=\sum_{b=1}^{n}\square_{n}(b)\ \ \text{and }\ \ q(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b=1\\
(b,n)=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\square_{n}(b).

It is well known that
q(n)
is a multiplicative function. Stangl

[
29
]
, showed that
s(n)
is a multiplicative function.
Furthermore, for any odd prime
p
, he derived the following
recursion formula for
s(p^{r})
:

(11)

s(p^{r})=q(p^{r})+s(p^{r-2}),\ \ \ \text{for }r\geq 3

and
s(p)=q(p)+1=\frac{p+1}{2}
,

s(p^{2})=q(p^{2})+1=\frac{p^{2}-p+2}{2}
. This recursion formula
follows from the observation that an element
b
is a square in

\mathbb{Z}_{p^{r-2}}
if and only if
bp^{2}
is a square in

\mathbb{Z}_{p^{r}}
. As a consequence of this recursion formula, we
have the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

For an odd prime
p
and a positive integer
\ell
, we have

(12)

\sum_{x=1}^{p^{\ell}}\square_{p^{\ell}}(x)e\bigg(\frac{xm}{p^{\ell}}\bigg)=1+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\end{subarray}}^{\ell-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=1\\
(x,p^{\ell-j})=1\end{subarray}}^{p^{\ell-j}}\bigg(1+\bigg(\frac{x}{p}\bigg)\bigg)e\bigg(\frac{xm}{p^{\ell-j}}\bigg),

where
\left(\frac{\cdot}{p}\right)
is the Legendre symbol mod
p
.

Proof.

For any
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\ell}}
with
(x,p^{\ell})=1
, we can
express
x
as

x=x_{\ell-1}p^{\ell-1}+\dots+x_{1}p+x_{0},

where
0\leq x_{i}\leq p-1
for
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
and
x_{0}\neq 0
. If
x_{0}
is a residue modulo
p
, then the congruence
y^{2}\equiv x\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)
has a solution. Using Lemma
4
with
the polynomial
f(x)=y^{2}-x
, we can lift this solution modulo

p^{\ell}
. Alternatively, we can define

\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\left(\frac{x}{p}\right)\right)
as a
characteristic function for quadratic residues modulo
p^{\ell}
.

We now prove (
12
) by induction
on
\ell
. If
\ell=1
, then (
12
) follows from the
definition of the Legendre symbol. If
\ell=2
, then we have

s(p^{2})=q(p^{2})+1
. Therefore, (
12
) follows from
the above observation.

Now, we assume that the claim is true if
\ell\leq m-1
. By using (
11
) and the hypothesis, we write

\displaystyle\sum_{x=1}^{p^{m}}\square_{p^{m}}(x)e\bigg(\frac{xm}{p^{m}}\bigg)=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\end{subarray}}^{m-3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=1\\
(x,p^{m-2-j})=1\end{subarray}}^{p^{m-2-j}}\bigg(1+\bigg(\frac{x}{p}\bigg)\bigg)e\bigg(\frac{xm}{p^{m-2-j}}\bigg)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=1\\
(x,p^{m})=1\end{subarray}}^{p^{m}}\square_{p^{m}}(x)e\bigg(\frac{xm}{p^{m}}\bigg).

By using the above observation, we write

\displaystyle\sum_{x=1}^{p^{m}}\square_{p^{m}}(x)e\bigg(\frac{xm}{p^{m}}\bigg)=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\end{subarray}}^{m-3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=1\\
(x,p^{m-2-j})=1\end{subarray}}^{p^{m-2-j}}\bigg(1+\bigg(\frac{x}{p}\bigg)\bigg)e\bigg(\frac{xm}{p^{m-2-j}}\bigg)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=1\\
(x,p^{m})=1\end{subarray}}^{p^{m}}\bigg(1+\bigg(\frac{x}{p}\bigg)\bigg)e\bigg(\frac{xm}{p^{m}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\end{subarray}}^{m-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=1\\
(x,p^{m-j})=1\end{subarray}}^{p^{m-j}}\bigg(1+\bigg(\frac{x}{p}\bigg)\bigg)e\bigg(\frac{xm}{p^{m-j}}\bigg).

This completes the proof of Lemma
5
.
∎

Recall that
S_{n}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
denotes the number of square solutions of (
1
).
Now, we show that the function
n\rightarrow S_{n}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
is multiplicative, for any given integers

.

Lemma 6
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{k},b
be integers. Then, for any
n
and
n^{\prime}

relatively prime, we have

S_{nn^{\prime}}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=S_{n}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\cdot S_{n^{\prime}}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k}).

Proof.

It is easy to see that every square solution of the linear
congruence
a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ nn^{\prime})

corresponds a square solution of
a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)
and a square solution of
a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n^{\prime})
. Thus, we have

S_{nn^{\prime}}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\leq S_{n}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\cdot S_{n^{\prime}}(b;a_{1},\dots,a_{k}).

Conversely, let
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
and
(x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{k})
be square solutions of
a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)
and
a_{1}x^{\prime}_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x^{\prime}_{k}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n^{\prime})

respectively. Since
(n,n^{\prime})=1
, it follows from the Chinese
remainder theorem that there is a unique
\gamma_{i}
modulo
nn^{\prime}

for each
1\leq i\leq k
such that

\gamma_{i}\equiv x_{i}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n),\ \gamma_{i}\equiv x^{\prime}_{i}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n^{\prime}),\ \text{for}\ 1\leq i\leq k.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle a_{1}\gamma_{1}+\dots+a_{k}\gamma_{k}-b

\displaystyle\equiv a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}-b\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n),

\displaystyle a_{1}\gamma_{1}+\dots+a_{k}\gamma_{k}-b

\displaystyle\equiv a_{1}x^{\prime}_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x^{\prime}_{k}-b\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n^{\prime})

and hence
(n,n^{\prime})=1
implies that

a_{1}\gamma_{1}+\dots+a_{k}\gamma_{k}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ nn^{\prime})
. Also,
for each
1\leq i\leq k
, the congruences

y_{i}^{2}\equiv\gamma_{i}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)\equiv x_{i}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n),\ \ y_{i}^{2}\equiv\gamma_{i}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n^{\prime})\equiv x^{\prime}_{i}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n^{\prime}),

has solutions implies that
n\mid(y_{i}^{2}-\gamma_{i})
and
n^{\prime}\mid(y_{i}^{2}-\gamma_{i})
, for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Since
n

and
n^{\prime}
relatively prime, we obtain
y_{i}^{2}\equiv\gamma_{i}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ nn^{\prime})
. Therefore,
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k})
is a square
solution of
a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ nn^{\prime})
. This
completes the proof of Lemma
6
.

5.
Order restricted congruence - proof of Theorem 3

In this section, we discuss the proof of Theorem
3
. We shall use the following lemma.

Lemma 8
.

(Lemma IV.6,
[
7
]
)
Let
n
be a positive integer and
a,m
be non-negative integers. Then, we have

\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bigg(1-ze\bigg(\frac{jam}{n}\bigg)\bigg)=(1-z^{\frac{n}{d}})^{d},\ \text{where}\ d=(am,n).

Proof of Theorem
3
.
Let
k=k_{1}+\dots+k_{t}
be a partition of
k
given as in (
5
).
By using Lemma
3
, we see that the number of
partitions of
b
into
k
parts such that exactly
k_{1}
parts
taken from the set
A_{1}
, exactly
k_{2}
parts taken from the set

A_{2}
, and so on, up to exactly
k_{2}
parts taken from the set

A_{2}
, is the coefficient of

in

\prod_{j_{1}\in A_{1}}\frac{1}{1-z_{1}q^{j_{1}}}\times\cdots\times\prod_{j_{t}\in A_{t}}\frac{1}{1-z_{t}q^{j_{t}}}.

Taking
A_{i}=\{a_{i},2a_{i},\dots,na_{i}\}
, for
i=1,\dots,t

and
q=e^{2\pi im/n}
, where

is a non-negative integer, we

\sum_{b=1}^{n}M_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{t},a_{1},\dots,a_{t},b)e\bigg(\frac{bm}{n}\bigg)

to be the coefficient of
z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{t}^{k_{t}}

\prod_{i=1}^{t}\prod_{j_{i}=1}^{n}\bigg(1-z_{i}e\bigg(\frac{j_{i}m}{n}\bigg)\bigg)^{-1}.

By using Lemma
8
, we obtain

\sum_{b=1}^{n}M_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{t},a_{1},\dots,a_{t},b)e\bigg(\frac{bm}{n}\bigg)=(-1)^{\frac{k_{1}d_{1}+\dots+k_{t}d_{t}}{n}}\binom{-d_{1}}{\frac{k_{1}d_{1}}{n}}\cdots\binom{-d_{t}}{\frac{k_{t}d_{t}}{n}},

where

, for

. Now, by taking the
inverse Fourier transform, we write
M_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{t},a_{1},\dots,a_{t},b)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}(-1)^{\frac{k_{1}d_{1}+\dots+k_{t}d_{t}}{n}}\binom{-d_{1}}{\frac{k_{1}d_{1}}{n}}\cdots\binom{-d_{t}}{\frac{k_{t}d_{t}}{n}}e\bigg(\frac{-bm}{n}\bigg)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\frac{d_{1}}{d_{1}+\frac{k_{1}d_{1}}{n}}\cdots\frac{d_{t}}{d_{t}+\frac{k_{t}d_{t}}{n}}\binom{d_{1}+\frac{k_{1}d_{1}}{n}}{\frac{k_{1}d_{1}}{n}}\cdots\binom{d_{t}+\frac{k_{t}d_{t}}{n}}{\frac{k_{t}d_{t}}{n}}e\bigg(\frac{-bm}{n}\bigg)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d_{1}\mid n}\dots\sum_{d_{t}\mid n}\frac{d_{1}}{d_{1}+\frac{k_{1}d_{1}}{n}}\cdots\frac{d_{t}}{d_{t}+\frac{k_{t}d_{t}}{n}}\binom{d_{1}+\frac{k_{1}d_{1}}{n}}{\frac{k_{1}d_{1}}{n}}\cdots\binom{d_{t}+\frac{k_{t}d_{t}}{n}}{\frac{k_{t}d_{t}}{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\

Proof of Theorem

By taking

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{k}f}{n}\sum_{d\mid(\frac{n}{f},k)}(-1)^{\frac{k}{d}}\binom{\frac{n}{d}}{\frac{k}{d}}C_{d}(b).
