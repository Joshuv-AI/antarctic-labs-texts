Overdrawing Urns using Categories of Signed Probabilities
Bart Jacobs and Dario Stein

Abstract
A basic experiment in probability theory is drawing without replacement from an urn filled with multiple balls of different colours. Clearly, it is physically impossible to overdraw, that is, to draw more balls from the urn than it contains. This paper demonstrates that overdrawing does make sense mathematically, once we allow signed distributions with negative probabilities. A new (conservative) extension of the familiar hypergeometric (‘draw-and-delete’) distribution is introduced that allows draws of arbitrary sizes, including overdraws. The underlying theory makes use of the dual basis functions of the Bernstein polynomials, which play a prominent role in computer graphics. Negative probabilities are treated systematically in the framework of categorical probability and the central role of datastructures such as multisets and monads is emphasised.



1
Introduction

For drawing (multiple) coloured balls from a statistical urn, we
distinguish three well-known modes:

1.

hypergeometric
or
draw-and-delete
, which is
drawing a ball from the urn without replacement, so that the urn
shrinks;

2.

multinomial
or
draw-and-replace
: drawing with
replacement, so that the urn remains the same;

3.

Pólya
or
draw-and-duplicate
, which is drawing a
ball from the urn and replacing it together with an additional ball
of the same colour, so that the urn grows.

Multinomial and Pólya draws may be of arbitrary size, but
hypergeometric draws are limited in size by the number of balls in the
urn. In this paper we lift this limitation and allow hypergeometric
draws of arbitrary size, including ‘overdraws’, containing more balls
than in the urn. Physically this is strange, but, as will show,
mathematically it makes sense once we allow negative probabilities.

Negative probabilities have emerged in quantum physics
(
e.g.
in double slit experiments) and have been discussed in
the work of famous physicists like Wigner, Dirac, and Feynman (see

e.g.

[
8
]
and the references mentioned
there). There are also ‘classical’ (non-quantum) examples, such as the
one of Piponi (discussed in
[
2
]
) or of
Székely
[
23
]
with two half coins, involving infinitely
many both positive and negative probabilities, whose (convolution) sum
is an ordinary (fair) coin. Also, negative probabilities have come up
in finance, see
e.g.

[
21
]
. Despite the lack of
the clear operational meaning that their nonnegative counterparts
have, negative probabilities appear as convenient tools in a variety
of contexts in mathematics and physics,
see
[
3
,
24
,
2
,
8
]
.

We briefly explain the nature of our extension, already using some
notation that will be explained below. The Pólya distribution can be
expressed as a mixture of multinomial draws; that is, we can break up
such a draw into two stages: first sample a random distribution

\omega
from the Dirichlet distribution, and then make independent
(multinomial) draws from
\omega
. The self-reinforcing behaviour of
Pólya’s urn is entirely captured by the latent Dirichlet
distribution. In the Kleisli category of the Giry-monad, with
(Kleisli) morphisms called channels, this corresponds to a
factorisation of the Pólya channel
\mathsl{pol}[K]
through the
multinomial channel
\mathsl{mn}[K]
, with draws of size
K
.

e.g.

).

The hypergeometric distribution does not admit such a De Finetti
factorisation since an urn containing

balls is exhausted after

draws. However, there is a way out, if we extend our notion of
probability to allow negative (signed) probabilities. Such models

De Finetti factorization of the hypergeometric channel

, for draws of size

, of the form:

It uses a signed ‘Dual Dirichlet’ distribution

which we develop in analogy to the Dirichlet distribution occurring in
Pólya’s urn. Existence of the factorisation can be deduced from
earlier
work
[
17
,
6
,
19
]

connecting finite versions of De Finetti’s theorem to signed
probability. In this case, the factorisation (
2
) is not
unique. We claim that a canonical choice is given by the Dual
Bernstein polynomials, which have been studied widely in computer
graphics
[
20
,
7
,
25
,
18
]
,
but their appearance in a probabilistic context is
novel. Evaluating (

) for overdraws
K\geq N
defines a
signed extension of the hypergeometric distributions which includes
overdraws, while agreeing with the usual distribution for ordinary
draws
K\leq N
.

Our contributions are:

1.

a principled approach to signed probability (discrete and continuous) using multisets and monads;

2.

conceptualizing dual Bernstein polynomials as signed probability densities;

3.

defining signed hypergeometric distributions that
conservatively extend hypergeometric draws while preserving good
properties;

4.

explicating the dual Dirichlet distribution and its conjugate
prior relationships via string diagrams.

2
Multisets

A multiset, also known as bag, is like a subset except that elements
may occur multiple times. We shall use ket notation
n_{1}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{1}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\cdots+n_{k}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{k}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
to describe a multiset with
k
elements,
where element
x_{i}
, say from a set
X
, occurs
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
many
times. Equivalently, such a multiset may be described as a function

\varphi\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
with finite support
\mathsl{supp}(\varphi)\coloneqq\{x\in X\;|\;\varphi(x)\neq 0\}
. The number

\varphi(x)\in\mathbb{N}
is the multiplicity of
x\in X
; it says how many
times
x
occurs in the multiset
\varphi
.

We shall write
\mathcal{M}(X)
for the set of multisets with elements from a
set
X
, and
\mathcal{M}_{{\kern-0.2pt}\mathsl{fs}}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{M}(X)
for the subset of
multisets
\varphi
with full support, that is, with
\mathsl{supp}(\varphi)=X
. The latter only makes sense when
X
is a finite set. As canonical
finite sets we write
\bm{n}\coloneqq\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, for

n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The size
\|\varphi\|
of a multiset
\varphi
is the total number of
elements, including multiplicities. Thus,
\|\varphi\|\coloneqq\sum_{x}\varphi(x)
, or, in ket notation,
\|\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\|=\sum_{i}n_{i}
. We write
\mathcal{M}[K](X)\coloneqq\big\{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(X)\;\big|\;\|\varphi\|=K\big\}
for the set of multisets
of size
K\in\mathbb{N}
. When the set
X
has
n\geq 1
elements, the
number of multisets of size
K
in
\mathcal{M}[K](X)
is

\big(\!\binom{n}{K}\!\big)=\binom{n+K-1}{K}=\frac{(n+K-1)!}{K!\cdot(n-1)!}
. For instance, for a set
X=\{a,b,c\}
with three elements there are
\big(\!\binom{3}{3}\!\big)=\frac{5!}{3!\cdot 2!}=10
multisets of size
K=3
, namely:

3|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
,
\,3|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
,
\,3|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
,
\,2|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
,

\,2|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
,
\,1|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
,
\,2|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
,

\,1|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
,
\,1|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
,

\,1|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. Only the last one has full support.
The factorial
n!
and binomial coefficients
\binom{n}{m}
and

\left({\kern-1.99997pt}\binom{n}{m}{\kern-1.99997pt}\right)
are extended from numbers to multisets (as
in
[
14
]
).

Definition 1

Let
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
be two multisets. We define

1.

\varphi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}\coloneqq\prod_{x}\varphi(x)!
;

2.

({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\coloneqq\frac{\|\varphi\|!}{\varphi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}
;

3.

\varphi\leq\psi
iff
\varphi(x)\leq\psi(x)
for each
x\in X
, and
\varphi\leq_{K}\psi
iff
\varphi\leq\psi
and

\|\varphi\|=K
;

4.

(\psi-\varphi)(x)=\psi(x)-\varphi(x)
, when
\varphi\leq\psi
;

5.

\binom{\psi}{\varphi}\coloneqq\frac{\psi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}{\varphi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}\cdot(\psi-\varphi){\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}=\prod_{x}\binom{\psi(x)}{\varphi(x)}
, when
\varphi\leq\psi
;

6.

\big(\binom{\psi}{\varphi}\big)\coloneqq\frac{(\psi+\varphi-\mathbf{1}{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}{\varphi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}\cdot(\psi-\mathbf{1}){\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}=\prod_{x}\big(\binom{\psi(x)}{\varphi(x)}\big)
when
\psi
has full support,
where
\mathbf{1}=\sum_{x}1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
is the multiset of singletons.

3
Discrete distributions

A discrete probability distribution
\sum_{i}r_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
looks
like a multiset, except that the multiplicities
r_{i}
are now in the
unit interval
[0,1]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and add up to one:
\sum_{i}r_{i}=1
. We write
\mathcal{D}(X)
for the set of such distributions with

x_{i}\in X
. Alternatively, like for multisets, elements

\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
may be described as functions
\omega\colon X\rightarrow[0,1]
with finite support and with
\sum_{x}\omega(x)=1
. When the set
X
is finite, we write
\mathcal{D}_{{\kern-0.4pt}\mathsl{fs}}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{D}(X)
for the discrete distributions with full support:

\mathsl{supp}(\omega)=X
. An example is the uniform distribution

\sum_{x\in X}\frac{1}{n}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, where
n\geq 1
is the number of
elements of a non-empty set
X
. Concretely, a fair coin is described
by the distribution
\frac{1}{2}|{\kern 1.00006pt}H{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}|{\kern 1.00006pt}T{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
for
X=\{H,T\}
.

Each non-empty multiset
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
can be turned into
a distribution via normalisation. We call this frequentist learning,
since it involves learning a distribution by counting, and write
it as:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathsl{flrn}(\varphi)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\frac{\varphi}{\|\varphi\|}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\frac{\varphi(x)}{\|\varphi\|}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\;\in\;\mathcal{D}(X).\end{array}

(3)

This frequentist learning is natural in
X
, but it is
not a map of monads, from (non-empty) multisets to distributions.

The set
\mathcal{D}(\bm{n})
of distributions on
\bm{n}=\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
can be identified with the simplex
\Delta^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
where:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Delta^{n}&\coloneqq&\big\{(r_{0},\ldots,r_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\;\big|\;\sum_{i}r_{i}=1\big\}.\end{array}

(4)

This is commonly called the
n-1
simplex.

There are three famous ‘draw’ distributions, called multinomial,
hypergeometric and Pólya. We briefly describe them in the style
of
[
15
]
and refer there for more information. These
distributions are all parameterised by a draw size
K
and form
distributions on the set
\mathcal{M}[K](X)
of multisets (as draws). One may
think of
X
as a set of colors.

Definition 2

We fix a set
X
and a number
K\in\mathbb{N}
.

1.

For a distribution
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
, used as
abstract urn, the multinomial distribution

\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\in\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K](X)\big)
is defined as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\prod_{x\in X}\omega(x)^{\varphi(x)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

2.

For an ‘urn’ multiset
\upsilon\in\mathcal{M}(X)
, with size
L\coloneqq\|\upsilon\|\geq K
there is a hypergeometric
distribution
\mathsl{hg}[K](\upsilon)\in\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K](X)\big)
with:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{hg}[K](\upsilon)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\leq_{K}\upsilon}\frac{\binom{\upsilon}{\varphi}}{\binom{L}{K}}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

The size restriction
K\leq L
excludes overdraws.

3.

Similarly, for a multiset
\upsilon\in\mathcal{M}[L](X)
with full
support, there is the Pólya distribution

\mathsl{pol}[K](\upsilon)\in\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K](X)\big)
with:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{pol}[K](\upsilon)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\frac{\big(\binom{\upsilon}{\varphi}\big)}{\big(\binom{L}{K}\big)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

Intuitively, in the multinomial case drawn balls are returned to the
urn, so that the urn does not change and can be described abstractly
as a discrete distribution. In the hypergeometric case a drawn ball is
removed from the urn, and in the Pólya case the drawn ball is
returned together with a new ball of the same colour. Thus in the
hypergeometric case the urns shrinks, whereas in the Pólya case the
urn grows.

Given two distributions
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
and
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(Y)
we can
form a parallel (tensor) product
\omega\otimes\rho\in\mathcal{D}(X\times Y)
, via
(\omega\otimes\rho)(x,y)=\omega(x)\cdot\rho(y)
.

X\mapsto\mathcal{D}(X)

4
Continuous distributions

In the previous section, we have seen finite discrete probability
distributions over an arbitrary set. There are also continuous
distributions, defined on measurable spaces. Here we need such
distributions only on one particular kind of spaces, namely on
simplices
\Delta^{n}
, see (
4
). The only
distributions that we need are given by (polynomial) functions
f\colon\Delta^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\int_{\Delta^{n}}f=1
.
Such an
f
is called a (probability) density function. It gives rise
to probability measure
\Phi
that sends a measurable subset
M\subseteq\Delta^{n}
to the probability
\int_{M}f\in[0,1]
. Such measures
are elements of the set
\mathcal{G}\big(\Delta^{n}\big)
, where
\mathcal{G}
is
the Giry monad, see
e.g.

[
22
,
12
]
for
further information. At first we require that such density functions
are nonnegative, so
f\geq 0
, but later we drop this requirement,
for so-called signed distributions (see the next section).

Definition 4

Let

1.

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathsl{dir}(\upsilon)(\bm{r})&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\frac{(\|\upsilon\|-1)!}{(\upsilon-\mathbf{1}_{n}){\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}\cdot\prod_{i\in\bm{n}}\,r_{i}^{\upsilon(i)-1}&\qquad\mbox{where}&\mathbf{1}_{n}&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<n}1|{\kern 1.00006pt}i{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\,\in\,\mathcal{M}_{{\kern-0.2pt}\mathsl{fs}}(\bm{n}).\end{array}

5
Signed distributions

We now introduce signed distribution, both in the discrete case and in
the continuous case. As before, we only need continuous distributions
on simplices.

Definition 6

1.

A signed discrete probability distribution on a set
X
is a
function
\sigma\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with finite support

\mathsl{supp}(\sigma)\coloneqq\{x\in X\;|\;\sigma(x)\neq 0\}
and with

\sum_{x\in X}\sigma(x)=1
. We may equivalently write such a
signed discrete distribution in ket notation as a finite formal sum

\sum_{i}r_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
where
r_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
\sum_{i}r_{i}=1
. We shall write
\mathcal{S}(X)
for the set of signed discrete probability
distributions on
X
.

2.

A signed continuous probability distribution, on a simplex

\Delta^{n}
, is given by a signed density function
f\colon\Delta^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\int_{\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}}f(\bm{r}){\kern 1.99997pt}\mathrm{d}{\kern 0.29999pt}\bm{r}=1
.

An example of a signed discrete distribution is
\frac{1}{2}|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle-\frac{1}{4}|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{3}{4}|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. We do not offer an
operational explanation for what such negative probabilities mean but
treat signed distributions as mathematical objects of their own. It is
not hard to see that signed discrete distributions
\mathcal{S}
form a monad
on the category of sets and functions. It is affine, in the sense that

\mathcal{S}(\bm{1})\cong\bm{1}
, but it differs from
\mathcal{D}
for
instance because it is not
strongly
affine, as defined
in
[
11
]
.

6
Dual bases

The probability mass function of the multinomial distribution is of a
particularly tractable form, namely a polynomial function
\Delta^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, on the simplex
\Delta^{n}
.

Definition 7

For
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})
, we define the
multinomial

\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi}
as

\begin{array}[]{rccclcrcl}\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi}(\bm{x})&\coloneqq&({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\bm{x}^{\varphi}&=&\displaystyle({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\prod_{i\in\bm{n}}\,x_{i}^{\varphi(i)}&\qquad\mbox{with `monomial'}&\bm{x}^{\varphi}&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\prod_{i\in\bm{n}}\,x_{i}^{\varphi(i)}.\end{array}

For every probability vector
\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}
, one has

\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi}(\bm{r})=\mathsl{mn}[K](\bm{r})(\varphi)
, via the identification

\Delta^{n}\cong\mathcal{D}(\bm{n})
.

Definition 8

For numbers
n,K
we write
\mathsl{P}_{K}(\Delta^{n})
for the real
vector space of polynomial functions
\Delta^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of degree
K
. The multinomials

\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi}
for

\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})
form a basis of this
space, and so we have as dimension
\dim\big(\mathsl{P}_{K}(\Delta^{n})\big)=\left({\kern-1.99997pt}\binom{n}{K}{\kern-1.99997pt}\right)
. This vector space
\mathsl{P}_{K}(\Delta^{n})

is a Hilbert space via an inner product defined on

f,g\colon\Delta^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle{\kern 1.0pt}f,g{\kern 1.0pt}\rangle&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\int_{\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}}f(\bm{r})\cdot g(\bm{r}){\kern 1.99997pt}\mathrm{d}{\kern 0.29999pt}\bm{r}.\end{array}

(8)

The dual of a basis
(b_{i})
of a space
V
is generally
understood as a basis of the dual space
V^{*}
. In a Hilbert
space such a dual basis can be described as the elements

(d_{i})
of the space itself which are uniquely determined by
the relationship
\langle{\kern 1.0pt}b_{i},d_{j}{\kern 1.0pt}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
, so
that
\langle{\kern 1.0pt}b_{i},d_{i}{\kern 1.0pt}\rangle=1
and
\langle{\kern 1.0pt}b_{i},d_{j}{\kern 1.0pt}\rangle=0
for
i\neq j
.

Definition 9

The
dual multinomials

(\mathfrak{d}_{\varphi})
are defined
as the dual basis of
\mathsl{P}_{K}(\Delta^{n})
to the multinomials

(\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi})
, and are as such uniquely
characterised by the property
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})
,

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\langle{\kern 1.0pt}\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi},\mathfrak{d}_{\psi}{\kern 1.0pt}\rangle&=&\delta_{\varphi,\psi}&=&\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }\varphi=\psi\\
0&\mbox{if }\varphi\neq\psi,\end{cases}\end{array}

(9)

What do we know about this dual basis? Of course we can
express the dual basis vectors
\mathfrak{d}_{\psi}
in
terms of the original basis, say via scalars
c_{\chi,\psi}

satisfying, for each
\psi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})
,

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathfrak{d}_{\psi}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}c_{\chi,\psi}\cdot\bm{x}^{\chi}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}\frac{c_{\chi,\psi}}{({\kern 1.0pt}\chi{\kern 1.0pt})}\cdot\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}.\end{array}

(10)

By exploiting the equations (
9
)
and using the linearity of the inner product in each of its
arguments (
i.e.
bilinearity), we obtain the equation

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\delta_{\varphi,\psi}&=&\langle{\kern 1.0pt}\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi},\mathfrak{d}_{\psi}{\kern 1.0pt}\rangle&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}c_{\chi,\psi}\cdot\frac{\langle{\kern 1.0pt}\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi},\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}{\kern 1.0pt}\rangle}{({\kern 1.0pt}\chi{\kern 1.0pt})}.\end{array}

(11)

We note that:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle{\kern 1.0pt}\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi},\mathfrak{m}_{\chi}{\kern 1.0pt}\rangle\hskip 2.0pt\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{InnerProdEqn}}}{{=}}}\hskip 2.0pt({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot({\kern 1.0pt}\chi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\displaystyle\int_{\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}}\!\!\left(\prod_{i\in\bm{n}}r_{i}^{\varphi(i)}\right)\!\cdot\!\left(\prod_{i\in\bm{n}}r_{i}^{\chi(i)}\right)\!\!{\kern 1.99997pt}\mathrm{d}{\kern 0.29999pt}\bm{r}&=&({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot({\kern 1.0pt}\chi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\displaystyle\int_{\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}}\!\prod_{i\in\bm{n}}r_{i}^{(\varphi+\chi)(i)}{\kern 1.99997pt}\mathrm{d}{\kern 0.29999pt}\bm{r}\\
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DirichletNormalisationEqn}}}{{=}}}&({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot({\kern 1.0pt}\chi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\displaystyle\frac{(\varphi+\chi){\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}{(2K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}n{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!}\end{array}

There are three square matrices at hand, of size

\left({\kern-1.99997pt}\binom{n}{K}{\kern-1.99997pt}\right)\times\left({\kern-1.99997pt}\binom{n}{K}{\kern-1.99997pt}\right)
, with multisets as
indices, namely:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrclcrcl}C&=&\Big(c_{\varphi,\psi}\Big)_{\!\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}&&\mathsl{FS}&=&\Big((\varphi+\psi){\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}\!\Big)_{\!\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}&&\mathsl{D}&=&\Big(({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\delta_{\varphi,\psi}\Big)_{\!\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}.\end{array}

This
C
is the matrix of scalars that we are
looking for,
\mathsl{FS}
contains the factorials-of-sums of
multisets, and
\mathsl{D}
is a diagonal matrix with multiset
coefficients. Equation (
11
) can now be
written as:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}(2K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}n{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!\cdot\delta_{\varphi,\psi}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\mathsl{FS}_{\varphi,\chi}\cdot C_{\chi,\psi}&=&\Big(\mathsl{D}\cdot\mathsl{FS}\cdot C\Big)_{\varphi,\psi}.\end{array}

We then get
(2K+n-1)!\cdot\mathsl{FS}^{-1}\cdot\mathsl{D}^{-1}=C
, so that the coefficients that we seek are
obtained as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}c_{\varphi,\psi}&=&\displaystyle\frac{(2K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}n{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!}{({\kern 1.0pt}\psi{\kern 1.0pt})}\cdot\Big(\mathsl{FS}^{-1}\Big)_{\!\varphi,\psi}.\end{array}

(12)

These matrix inverses
\mathsl{FS}^{-1}
exist since

\mathsl{FS}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix. We give an extended example calculation in the appendix (Example
A.26
).

The following is a crucial property of dual bases, as
introduced in Definition
9
.

Proposition 10

Each dual basis function
\mathfrak{d}_{\psi}\in\mathsl{P}_{K}(\Delta^{n})
, associated with a multiset

\psi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})
, is a continuous signed
probability density on
\Delta^{n}
, that is:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\int_{\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}}\!\mathfrak{d}_{\psi}(\bm{r}){\kern 1.99997pt}\mathrm{d}{\kern 0.29999pt}\bm{r}&=&1.\end{array}

Proof 6.11
.

We use that multinomial distributions
\mathsl{mn}[K]
form a
probability distribution; this means that the multinomials

\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi}
form a partition of unity,
i.e. for all
\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi}(\bm{r})&=&1.\end{array}

Hence we obtain

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\displaystyle\int_{\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}}\!\mathfrak{d}_{\psi}(\bm{r}){\kern 1.99997pt}\mathrm{d}{\kern 0.29999pt}\bm{r}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\int_{\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}}\!1\cdot\mathfrak{d}_{\psi}(\bm{r}){\kern 1.99997pt}\mathrm{d}{\kern 0.29999pt}\bm{r}&=&\displaystyle\int_{\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}}\!\left(\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi}(\bm{r})\right)\cdot\mathfrak{d}_{\psi}(\bm{r})\hbox to0.0pt{${\kern 1.99997pt}\mathrm{d}{\kern 0.29999pt}\bm{r}$\hss}\\[13.99995pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}\int_{\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}}\!\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi}(\bm{r})\cdot\mathfrak{d}_{\psi}(\bm{r}){\kern 1.99997pt}\mathrm{d}{\kern 0.29999pt}\bm{r}\\[13.99995pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{InnerProdEqn}}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}\langle{\kern 1.0pt}\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi},\mathfrak{d}_{\psi}{\kern 1.0pt}\rangle\hskip 2.0pt\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{MHPDualInprodEqn}}}{{=}}}\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}\delta_{\varphi,\psi}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt1.\end{array}

[formula]

In the beginning of this proof, we use that the pointwise sum of
the multinomial basis functions
\mathfrak{m}_{\varphi}
, for
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})
, is equal to the constant-one function
\mathbf{1}\colon\Delta^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The sum of the dual basis functions

\mathfrak{d}_{\varphi}
is also constant.

Proposition 6.12
.

Fix numbers
n,K
and consider the dual basis function

\mathfrak{d}_{\varphi}\in\mathsl{P}_{K}(\Delta^{n})
, for
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})
. Then their pointwise sum is a constant function:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}\!\mathfrak{d}_{\varphi}&\,=&\displaystyle\frac{(K+n-1)!}{K!}.\end{array}

Proof 6.13
.

Since the vectors
\mathfrak{d}_{\varphi}
form a
basis we can express the constant-one function
\mathbf{1}\colon\Delta^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with respect to this
basis, say as:
\mathbf{1}=\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}a_{\varphi}\cdot\mathfrak{d}_{\varphi}
, for certain coefficients

a_{\varphi}
. For a fixed multiset

\psi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})
we compute the constant

a_{\psi}
as follows.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}a_{\psi}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}a_{\varphi}\cdot\delta_{\psi,\varphi}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}a_{\varphi}\cdot\langle{\kern 1.0pt}\mathfrak{m}_{\psi},\mathfrak{d}_{\varphi}{\kern 1.0pt}\rangle\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\langle{\kern 1.0pt}\mathfrak{m}_{\psi},\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}a_{\varphi}\cdot\mathfrak{d}_{\varphi}{\kern 1.0pt}\rangle\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\langle{\kern 1.0pt}\mathfrak{m}_{\psi},\mathbf{1}{\kern 1.0pt}\rangle\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\int_{\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}}\!\mathfrak{m}_{\psi}(\bm{r}){\kern 1.99997pt}\mathrm{d}{\kern 0.29999pt}\bm{r}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle({\kern 1.0pt}\psi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\int_{\bm{r}\in\Delta^{n}}\!\bm{r}^{\psi}(\bm{r}){\kern 1.99997pt}\mathrm{d}{\kern 0.29999pt}\bm{r}\hskip 2.0pt\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DirichletNormalisationEqn}}}{{=}}}\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\frac{K!}{\psi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}\cdot\frac{\psi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}{(K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}n{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\frac{K!}{(K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}n{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!}.\end{array}

Thus, all these constants
a_{\psi}
are the same. As a
result:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathbf{1}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}\frac{K!}{(K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}n{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!}\cdot\mathfrak{d}_{\varphi}&=&\displaystyle\frac{K!}{(K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}n{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!}\cdot\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\bm{n})}\mathfrak{d}_{\varphi}.\end{array}

By moving the fraction to the other side we are done.

Definition 7.14

Theorem 7.15

9
Conclusions and further work

This paper covers a fascinating topic, namely negative
probabilities. It does not offer operational meaning, but it
does provide a solid mathematical basis for the emergence of
negative probabilities in classical, non-quantum probability
theory. The techniques of categorical probability provide the
toolbox for describing the relevant properties.

There is plenty of further work. High on our list is an explicit
formula for the dual basis vectors in the general multivariate case
(like in the bivariate case). Also, we would like to develop a deeper
understanding of the conjugate situation described in the string
diagrams in Theorem
8.24
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathfrak{b}_{i}(r)\hskip 2.0pt\coloneqq\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\binom{K}{i}\cdot r^{i}\cdot(1{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}r)^{K-i}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq K-i}\binom{K}{i}\cdot\binom{K{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}i}{j}\cdot r^{i}\cdot 1^{j}\cdot(-r)^{K-i-j}\\
