Limit point and limit circle trichotomy for Sturm-Liouville problems with complex potentials
Florian Leben, Edison Leguizamón, Carsten Trunk, Monika Winklmeier

Abstract
. The limit point and limit circle classification of real Sturm-Liouville problems by H. Weyl more than 100 years ago was extended by A.R. Sims around 60 years ago to the case when the coefficients are complex. Here, the main result is a collection of various criteria which allow us to decide to which class of Sims’ scheme a given Sturm-Liouville problem with complex coefficients belongs. This is subsequently applied to a second order differential equation defined on a ray in ℂ \mathbb{C} which is motivated by the recent intensive research connected with 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{P}\mathcal{T} -symmetric Hamiltonians.

34E05; 34M10

1.
Introduction

The search for criteria that guarantee that a Sturm-Liouville equation with real coefficients is in the limit point or the limit circle case has a long tradition since the seminal paper of H. Weyl
[
19
]
in 1910
where he classifies Sturm-Liouville problems on an interval
(a,b)
with a regular end point
a
into two classes:
Either all solutions of the eigenvalue problem are square-integrable close to
b
(limit circle)
or there exists at least one solution without this property (limit point).
This behaviour is independent of the chosen eigenvalue parameter.

Sturm-Liouville
problems with
complex-valued
coefficients were investigated
in the (also seminal) paper by A.R. Sims in 1957
[
18
]

with further refinements in

[
6
]
and
[
16
,
17
]
. The classification
proposed by A.R. Sims and later Brown et al. in
[
6
]

contains three different cases, where
one takes into account also the behaviour of the solution and its derivative in a weighted
L^{2}
-space (for details we refer to Section
2
).

Our interest in the classification proposed by A.R. Sims
arises from a second order differential equation defined on a
ray in
\mathbb{C}
. This is motivated
by recent intensive research connected with
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
-symmetric
Hamiltonians, cf.
[
5
]
.
In the seminal paper by C.M. Bender
and S. Boettcher
[
5
]
a new view of quantum mechanics was proposed
which adopts all its axioms except the one that restricts the Hamiltonian to be Hermitian, relaxing it to the assumption that the Hamiltonian is
{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{T}}
-symmetric. Here,
\mathcal{P}
is parity and
\mathcal{T}
is time reversal.
Since then,
{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{T}}
-symmetric Hamiltonians have been intensively analysed by many authors.
In
[
13
]

{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{T}}
-symmetry was embedded into a more general mathematical framework: pseudo-Hermiticity or, equivalently,
self-adjoint operators in Krein spaces,
[
1
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.
For a general introduction to
{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{T}}
-symmetric quantum mechanics we refer
to
[
14
]
and
[
3
]
.

Let
\Gamma
be a ray in the complex plane with angle
\phi\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
,

\Gamma:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:z=xe^{i\phi},x\in[a,\infty)\}

for some
a\geq 0
.
Our main interest is to obtain a Weyl criterion for the differential equation

(1.1)

-y(z)^{\prime\prime}+\textbf{q}(z)y(z)=\lambda y(z),\qquad z\in\Gamma,

where

\textbf{q}:\Gamma\to\mathbb{C}
is locally integrable.

A prominent class of potentials consists of the
{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{T}}
-symmetric potentials

\textbf{q}(z):=-(iz)^{N+2}

where
N
is a positive integer
[
5
,
4
]
.
Other Hamilitonians can be found for instance in
[
7
,
14
]
.

Via a parametrization (
1.1
) can be mapped back to the real line leading to a Sturm-Liouville problem on an interval of the form

(1.2)

-\big(py^{\prime}\big)^{\prime}+qy=w\lambda y.

We give an asymptotic approximation for its solutions in Section
3

via an approach based on
[
8
]
.
A careful analysis of these asymptotic approximations leads
to new criteria for limit point/limit circle cases in the sense of
A.R. Sims for the equation in (
1.2
)
(see Section
4
)
and then,
by parameterisation, also for (
1.1
),
see Section
5
.

Notations.

For
-\infty<a<b\leq\infty
we denote by
\ACloc(a,b)
the set of locally absolutely continuous functions on each compact subinterval of
(a,b)
.
For a locally integrable function
w:(a,b)\to\mathbb{C}
we set

L_{w}^{2}(a,b):=\{f:(a,b)\to\mathbb{C}:f\text{ measurable},\ \int_{a}^{b}|f(x)|^{2}|w(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty\}
.
If
w=1
, then we write
L^{2}(a,b)
.
Recall that the normed space of uniformly locally integrable functions
L^{1}_{\mathrm{u}}(a,b)
is defined as

\displaystyle L^{1}_{\mathrm{u}}(a,b)=\left\{f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b):\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{[n,n+1]\cap(a,b)}|f(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty\right\}.

2.
Weyl’s alternative for complex Sturm-Liouville problems

Consider the Sturm-Liouville problem

(2.1)

-\big(p(x)y^{\prime}(x)\big)^{\prime}+q(x)y(x)=w(x)\lambda y(x),\quad x\in[a,b)

where
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
b\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\},

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}

and
w,\,1/p,\,q:[a,b)\to\mathbb{C}
are locally integrable in
[a,b)
and satisfy

w(x)>0
,
p(x)\neq 0
for a.a.
x\in[a,b)
.
Here we always assume that the end point
a
is regular and
b
is singular (which is indicated by writing
x\in[a,b)

or
L^{1}_{\textnormal{loc}}[a,b)
).
A
solution
for (
2.1
) is a function
y
such that

y,\,py^{\prime}\in\ACloc(a,b)
and
y
satisfies (
2.1
) for a.a.
x\in[a,b)
.

Given
A\subset\mathbb{C}
, we define
\cco(A)
as the closed convex hull of
A
.
We impose

(2.2)

Q:=\cco\left\{\frac{q(x)}{w(x)}+rp(x)\ :\ 0<r<\infty,\ x\in[a,b)\right\}\neq\mathbb{C}.

Let
\lambda\notin Q
.
Then there exists a unique point
K\in Q
which minimizes the distance between
\lambda
and
Q
.
Moreover, there exists an angle
\theta
such that

(2.3)

\re[e^{i\theta}\left(z-K\right)]\geq 0\quad\mbox{for all }z\in Q.

In fact, let
Q-K:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:z=w-K,w\in Q\}
.
Since the set
\interior(Q-K)=\interior(Q)-K
is open and convex and does not intersect the trivial subspace
\{0\}
, the geometric form of the Hahn-Banach Theorem
(see e.g.
[
15
, Theorem 7.7.4]
)
shows that there is a linear functional

f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\re[f(z)]>0
for all
z\in\interior(Q)-K
.
Without restriction we may assume that
f
is normalized.
Hence there exists a real number
\theta
such that

f(z)=e^{i\theta}z
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
\re[e^{i\theta}v]>0
for all
v\in\interior(Q)-K
.

For
K\in\mathbb{C}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
we define the open half-plane

\ \Lambda_{K,\theta}:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\re[e^{i\theta}\left(z-K\right)]<0\right\},

and the set

S:=\left\{(\theta,K):\eqref{condition}\mbox{ is satisfied}\right\}.

In
[
6
]
it is proved that the Sturm-Liouville problem (
2.1
) falls in exactly one of the cases of the next definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Given
(\theta,K)\in S
and
\lambda\in\Lambda_{K,\theta},
we have the following cases:

(1)

There is, up to a multiplicative constant, only one solution
y
of the equation (
2.1
) such that

(2.4)

\int_{a}^{b}\re(e^{i\theta}p)|y^{\prime}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{a}^{b}\re[e^{i\theta}(q-Kw)]|y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{a}^{b}w|y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty

and this is the only solution belonging to

L_{w}^{2}(a,b)
.
In this case we say that (
2.1
) is in the
limit point I case
.

(2)

All solutions of (
2.1
) are in
L^{2}_{w}(a,b)
but, up to a multiplicative constant, there is only one solution that satisfies (
2.4
).
In this case we say that (
2.1
) is in the
limit point II case
.

(3)

All solutions of (
2.1
) are in
L^{2}_{w}(a,b)
and all solutions satisfy (
2.4
).
In this case we say that (
2.1
) is in the
limit circle case
.

Remark 2.2
.

In the situation of Definition
2.1
(3) we have

\displaystyle\re[e^{i\theta}(q-Kw)]=\re\left[we^{i\theta}\left(\frac{q}{w}-K\right)\right]=w\re\left[e^{i\theta}\left(\frac{q}{w}-K\right)\right]\geq 0.

Hence the three summands on the left hand side of (
2.4
) are nonnegative and therefore, if a solution of (
2.1
) satisfies (
2.4
),
then it is automatically in
L^{2}_{w}(a,b)
.

Remark 2.3
.

In
[
6
, Remark 2.2]
the method of variation of parameters is used to deduce that the classification is independent of
\lambda
, that is

•

If all solutions are in
L^{2}_{w}(a,b)
for some
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
, then the same is true for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

•

If all solutions satisfy (
2.4
) for some
\lambda_{0}\in\Lambda_{\theta,K}
, then the same is true for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

Note that
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
in the case of real coefficients.
Hence the first two terms in (
2.4
) are zero and therefore the limit point case II is not possible for real Sturm-Liouville equations.

3.
Asymptotic approximation of second order differential equations

In this section we derive an asymptotic approximation of solutions of (
3.1
).
Since (
1.1
) can be transformed into such an equation, this will allow us to establish limit point/limit circle criteria in Section
5
.
Our approximations of the solutions are primarily based on
[
8
, Theorem 1.3.1]
.
Consider the differential equation

(3.1)

(py^{\prime})^{\prime}(x)=s(x)y(x),\quad x\in[a,b)

where
s,p:[a,b)\to\mathbb{C}
are
functions such that

\frac{1}{p},s\in L^{1}_{\textnormal{loc}}[a,b)
.
In what follows we define the
n
th root of a complex number
z=re^{i\arg(z)}
with
-\pi<\arg(z)\leq\pi
as
z^{1/n}=r^{1/n}e^{i\arg(z)/n}
. Our first theorem is a variation of Theorem 2.5.1 in
[
8
]
. It leads to slightly different assertions which form the basis for the subsequent section.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
p(x)\neq 0
,
s(x)\neq 0
,
\arg\frac{s(x)}{p(x)}\neq\pi
,
\arg p(x)s(x)\neq\pi
for all
x\in[a,b)
and
let
u:=(ps)^{-1/4}
.
Assume that
u,pu^{\prime}\in\ACloc(a,b)

and

u(pu^{\prime})^{\prime}\in L^{1}(a,b)
. Then there exist
F_{j},\widehat{F}_{j}:[a,b)\to\mathbb{C}
and a fundamental system
\{y,\widehat{y}\}
for (
3.1
) with

(3.2)

\displaystyle y(x)

\displaystyle=(p(x)s(x))^{-1/4}e^{-\int_{a}^{x}\sqrt{s(t)/p(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t}(F_{1}(x)+1),

\displaystyle\quad x\in[a,b),

\displaystyle((ps)^{1/4}y)^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=(s(x)/p(x))^{1/2}e^{-\int_{a}^{x}\sqrt{s(t)/p(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t}(F_{2}(x)-1),

\displaystyle\quad x\in[a,b),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\widehat{y}(x)

\displaystyle=(p(x)s(x))^{-1/4}e^{\int_{a}^{x}\sqrt{s(t)/p(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t}(\widehat{F}_{1}(x)+1),

\displaystyle\quad x\in[a,b),

\displaystyle((ps)^{1/4}\widehat{y})^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=(s(x)/p(x))^{1/2}e^{\int_{a}^{x}\sqrt{s(t)/p(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t}(\widehat{F}_{2}(x)-1),

\displaystyle\quad x\in[a,b),

and, for
j=1,2,

\|F_{j}\|_{\infty},\ \|\widehat{F}_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq 2e^{2M}-2,\quad\text{and}\quad F_{j}(x),\ \widehat{F}_{j}(x)\to 0,\quad\mbox{when }x\to b,

where
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
is the supremum norm and
M:=\|u(pu^{\prime})^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}}
.

Proof.

Note that
y
is a solution for
(
3.1
)
if and only if
Y
is a solution for
Y^{\prime}(x)=A(x)Y(x)
where

(3.4)

Y(x):=\begin{pmatrix}y(x)\\
p(x)y^{\prime}(x)\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad A(x):=\begin{pmatrix}0&1/p(x)\\
s(x)&0\end{pmatrix}\quad x\in[a,b).

We call
Y
a solution for
Y^{\prime}=AY
if
Y
is locally absolutely continuous in
[a,b)
and satisfies the differential equation (
3.4
) for a.a.
x\in[a,b)
.

For
z(x):=u(x)^{-1}y(x)
and

(3.5)

Z(x):=\begin{pmatrix}z(x)\\
p(x)u(x)^{2}z^{\prime}(x)\end{pmatrix}

we obtain the relations

(3.6)

Y(x)=\begin{pmatrix}u(x)&0\\
p(x)u^{\prime}(x)&u^{-1}(x)\end{pmatrix}Z(x)

and

Z(x)=\begin{pmatrix}u^{-1}(x)&0\\
-p(x)u^{\prime}(x)&u(x)\end{pmatrix}Y(x).

The transformation (
3.6
) takes (
3.4
) into

(3.7)

Z^{\prime}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&p(x)^{-1}u(x)^{-2}\\
s(x)u(x)^{2}-u(x)\left(p(x)u^{\prime}(x)\right)^{\prime}&0\end{pmatrix}Z(x),\qquad x\in[a,b).

By the transformation

(3.8)

W(x):=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}[r]1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}Z(x)

with inverse transformation

Z(x)=\begin{pmatrix}[r]1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}W(x)

we obtain the following system, using that

p^{-1}u^{-2}=su^{2}=\sqrt{s/p}\in L^{1}_{\textnormal{loc}}[a,b)
,

(3.9)

W^{\prime}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\frac{s(x)}{p(x)}\right)^{1/2}&0\\
0&-\left(\frac{s(x)}{p(x)}\right)^{1/2}\end{pmatrix}W(x)-\frac{1}{2}(u(x)(pu^{\prime})^{\prime}(x))\begin{pmatrix}[r]1&1\\
-1&-1\end{pmatrix}W(x).

If we set

S(x):=\frac{-u(x)(pu^{\prime})^{\prime}(x)}{2}\begin{pmatrix}[r]1&1\\
-1&-1\end{pmatrix},

then (
3.9
) can be written as

(3.10)

W^{\prime}(x)=\left(\frac{s(x)}{p(x)}\right)^{1/2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}W(x)+S(x)W(x).

Note that by hypothesis
\int_{a}^{b}\|S(t)\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{a}^{b}\|u(pu^{\prime})^{\prime}(t)\|\,\mathrm{d}t=M<\infty
and

M(x):=\int_{x}^{b}\|S(t)\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq M<\infty,\qquad x\in[a,b)

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}
denotes the operator norm of a
2\times 2
matrix.
Now we will construct two linearly independent solutions of (
3.9
) using a fixed point argument.
For our first solution, we set

V(x):=W(x)e^{\int_{a}^{x}\sqrt{s(t)/p(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t}
which solves the differential equation

(3.11)

V^{\prime}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}2\left(\frac{s(x)}{p(x)}\right)^{1/2}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}V(x)+S(x)V(x),\qquad x\in[a,b).

A fundamental system for the homogeneous differential equation

(3.12)

V_{0}^{\prime}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}2\left(\frac{s(x)}{p(x)}\right)^{1/2}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}V_{0}(x)

is given by the matrix

\Phi(x)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{2\int_{a}^{x}\sqrt{s(t)/p(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\re\left[p(x)s(x)\right]^{1/2}\geq 0
in
[a,b)
, we obtain

(3.13)

\|\Phi(x)\Phi(\widehat{x})^{-1}\|_{2}=\left\|\begin{pmatrix}e^{-2\int_{x}^{\widehat{x}}\left(\frac{s(t)}{p(t)}\right)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}t}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}=1,\qquad a\leq x<\widehat{x}.

Let
C([a,b),\mathbb{C}^{2})
denote the Banach space of continuous bounded functions
f:[a,b)\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
equipped with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.
We define the continuous operator
F:C([a,b),\mathbb{C}^{2})\to C([a,b),\mathbb{C}^{2})
by

(3.14)

(Ff)(x):=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}-\Phi(x)\int_{x}^{b}\Phi(t)^{-1}S(t)f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Next we construct a sequence of functions
\{h_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
by

h_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},\qquad h_{k+1}(x)=(Fh_{k})(x),\quad k\geq 1.

We proceed by induction to prove that for all
x\geq a

(3.15)

\|h_{k+1}(x)-h_{k}(x)\|\leq\frac{1}{k!}\left(\int_{x}^{b}\|S(t)\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{k}=\frac{(M(x))^{k}}{k!}\leq\frac{M^{k}}{k!}.

To this end, note that for all
x\geq a

\displaystyle\|h_{2}(x)-h_{1}(x)\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\,-\Phi(x)\int_{x}^{b}\Phi(t)^{-1}S(t)h_{1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\right\|\leq\int_{x}^{b}\|S(t)\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t=M(x)\leq M.

By the induction hypothesis we obtain

\displaystyle|h_{k+1}(x)-h_{k}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{x}^{b}\|S(t)\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}|h_{k}(t)-h_{k-1}(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{x}^{b}\|S(t)\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\frac{\left(\int_{t}^{b}\|S(u)\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\left|-\int_{x}^{b}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\left(\int_{t}^{b}\|S(u)\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{k}}{k!}\,\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\left(\int_{x}^{b}\|S(u)\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{k}=\frac{(M(x))^{k}}{k!}\leq\frac{M^{k}}{k!}.

For all
n\geq m\geq 1
we have that

\|h_{n}(x)-h_{m}(x)\|=\left\|\sum_{k=m}^{n-1}h_{k+1}(x)-h_{k}(x)\right\|\leq\sum_{k=m}^{n-1}\frac{(M(x))^{k}}{k!}\leq e^{M(x)}

and consequently

\|h_{n}-h_{m}\|_{\infty}\leq e^{M}
,
so
\{h_{n}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges uniformly to a bounded and continuous function
h\in C([a,b),\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
Moreover,
h
is a fixed point of
F
because
F
is continuous.
Let us show that
h
is a solution of (
3.11
).

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(F(h))(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}2\sqrt{s(x)/p(x)}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}h_{1}-\Phi^{\prime}(x)\int_{x}^{b}\Phi(t)^{-1}S(t)h(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+S(x)h(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}2\sqrt{s(x)/p(x)}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\left(h_{1}-\Phi(x)\int_{x}^{b}\Phi(t)^{-1}S(t)h(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)+S(x)h(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}2\sqrt{s(x)/p(x)}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}F(h)(x)+S(x)h(x)=\begin{pmatrix}2\sqrt{s(x)/p(x)}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}h(x)+S(x)h(x).

From (
3.15
) we obtain

(3.16)

\|h-h_{1}\|_{\infty}=:\left\|\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}\\
G_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{M^{k}}{k!}=e^{M}-1.

Finally, by hypothesis, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
C\in\mathbb{R}
such that

M(C)<\ln(1+\epsilon)

and therefore, by (
3.15
),

|h(x)-h_{1}(x)|\leq e^{M(x)}-1<\epsilon

for all
x\geq C.

It follows that

G_{i}(x)\to 0,\quad\mbox{when}\quad x\to b,\quad\mbox{for }i=1,2,

hence by (
3.16
)

h(x)=\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}(x)\\
G_{2}(x)+1\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{for }x\to b.

Since
h
is a solution for
(
3.11
)
, a solution
Z_{0}
for (
3.7
) is given by

(3.17)

Z_{0}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}z_{0}(x)\\
p(x)u^{2}z_{0}^{\prime}(x)\end{pmatrix}=e^{-\int_{a}^{x}\sqrt{s(t)/p(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t}\begin{pmatrix}[r]1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}(x)\\
G_{2}(x)+1\end{pmatrix}.

Recall that
z=uy
.
Setting
F_{1}:=G_{1}+G_{2}
and
F_{2}:=G_{1}-G_{2}
we obtain

y(x)=(p(x)s(x))^{-1/4}e^{-\int_{a}^{x}\sqrt{s(t)/p(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t}(1+F_{1}(x))

and

((ps)^{1/4}y)^{\prime}(x)=(s/p)^{1/2}(x)e^{-\int_{a}^{x}\sqrt{s(t)/p(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t}(F_{2}(x)-1)

where we used that
(ps)^{1/4}y=z_{0}
and
z_{0}^{\prime}
as given in (
3.17
).

In order to obtain a second solution of (
3.9
), we set

\widehat{V}(x):=W(x)e^{-\int_{a}^{x}\sqrt{s(t)/p(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t}
.
It satisfies

(3.18)

\widehat{V}^{\prime}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-2\sqrt{s(x)/p(x)}\end{pmatrix}\widehat{V}(x)+\frac{1}{2}(-u(x)(pu^{\prime})^{\prime}(x))\begin{pmatrix}[r]1&1\\
-1&-1\end{pmatrix}\widehat{V}(x).

As before, we note that the constant

\widehat{h}_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}

is a solution of the homogeneous equation

(3.19)

\widehat{V}^{\prime}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-2\sqrt{s(x)/p(x)}\end{pmatrix}\widehat{V}(x)

and that

\widehat{\Phi}(x):=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&e^{-2\int_{a}^{x}\sqrt{s(t)/p(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t}\end{pmatrix}

is a fundamental system for (
3.19
).
Again, for
a\leq\widehat{x}<x

\|\widehat{\Phi}(x)\widehat{\Phi}^{-1}(\widehat{x})\|_{2}=\left\|\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&e^{-2\int_{\widehat{x}}^{x}\sqrt{s(t)/p(t))}\,\mathrm{d}t}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}=1

and arguing as before, the operator
\widehat{F}
defined by

(\widehat{F}f)(x):=\widehat{h}_{1}+\widehat{\Phi}(x)\int_{a}^{x}\widehat{\Phi}^{-1}(t)S(t)f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

yields a solution

In order to prove that

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume

(b)

The function

(c)

(d)

(e)

|\arg s|\leq\pi-\epsilon_{0}
for some

\epsilon_{0}>0
.

Proof.

(a)

By assumption,
\arg(s(t))\neq\pi
, hence

\re[s(t)^{1/2}]>0
for all
t\in[a,\infty)
.
Moreover
\widehat{F}_{1}
is a bounded function with
\lim_{x\to\infty}\widehat{F}_{1}(x)=0
.
Therefore, for any
c\in(0,1)
we can take
a_{0}>a
such that

for

and hence

(4.1)

|\widehat{y}(x)|=|s(x)|^{-1/4}|1+\widehat{F}_{1}(x)|e^{\int_{a}^{x}\re[s(t)^{1/2}]\,\mathrm{d}t}\geq c|s(x)|^{-1/4},\qquad x>a_{0}.

Therefore the right hand side in (

) is not square integrable on

which shows that
\widehat{y}\notin L^{2}[a,\infty)
.

(b)

If
s
is bounded, then clearly
|s|^{-1/2}
is not square integrable on
[a,\infty)
and the claim follows from
(a)
.

(c)

By assumption,

\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{\re[s(x)^{1/2}]}{|s(x)|^{1/2}}:=c>0\quad\mbox{and}\quad 0<\int_{a}^{\infty}\re[s(t)^{1/2}]\,\mathrm{d}t=:R<\infty.

Hence the Lebesgue measure of the set

A:=\{t\in[a,\infty):\re[s(t)^{1/2}]\leq 1\}
is infinite.
Note that the function
x\mapsto\int_{a}^{x}\re\sqrt{s(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t

is non-decreasing and for large enough

, we have that

\int_{a}^{x}\re[s(t)]^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\frac{R}{2}.

We conclude

|\widehat{y}(x)|^{2}=|1+F_{1}(x)|^{2}\frac{e^{2\int_{a}^{x}\re[s(t)]^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}t}}{|s(x)|^{1/2}}\geq\frac{ce^{R}}{2}\frac{1}{\re[s(x)^{1/2}]}\geq\frac{ce^{R}}{2}\chi_{A}(x)

where
\chi_{A}
is the characteristic function of

.
Since
\frac{ce^{R}}{2}>0
it follows that
\widehat{y}\notin L^{2}[a,\infty)
.

(d)

We define

\psi(x):=\frac{e^{2\int_{a}^{x}\re[s(t)^{1/2}]\,\mathrm{d}t}}{(\re s(x)^{1/2})^{N}},\qquad x\in[a,\infty).

Then

\psi(x)^{-1}\frac{e^{2\int_{a}^{x}\re[s(t)^{1/2}]\,\mathrm{d}t}}{|s(x)|^{1/2}}=\frac{(\re s(x)^{1/2})^{N}}{|s(x)|^{1/2}}

and by Lemma
4.2

(b)
it suffices to show that

\psi\notin L^{1}[a,\infty)
. If we set

g(x):=\int_{a}^{x}\re[s(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}]\,\mathrm{d}t,

then

satisfies the differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2g(x)}}{(g^{\prime}(x))^{N}}

\displaystyle=\psi(x).

For

G(x):=\int_{a}^{x}(\psi(t))^{-1/N}\,\mathrm{d}t

we obtain

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{2}{N}g(x)}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2}{N}G(x).

By assumption,
g(x)\to\infty
for
x\to\infty
, so

G(x)\to N/2
and
\int_{a}^{\infty}\psi^{-1/N}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty
.
Similarly as above, the Lebesgue measure of the set

is infinite.
Hence

which implies that
\int_{a}^{\infty}\psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
diverges.

(e)

The assumption on

implies that

|\arg(s(x))^{1/2}|<(\pi-\epsilon_{0})/2
.
Setting

,
we have that
\re s(x)^{1/2}=|s(x)|^{1/2}\cos\arg(s(x))^{1/2}
and

\frac{\re s(x)^{1/2}}{|s(x)|^{1/2}}\geq c,\qquad x\in[a,\infty).

If
\int_{a}^{\infty}\re[s(t)^{1/2}]\,\mathrm{d}t=\infty
, then the claim follows from

with

If on the other hand

the claim follows from

.
∎

. This is motivated
