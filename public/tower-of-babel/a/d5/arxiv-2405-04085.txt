Solution of the mean-field Hubbard model of graphene rectangulenes
Amador García-Fuente 1,2, Jaime Ferrer 1,2

Abstract
We present a complete analytical solution of the mean-field Hubbard model of undoped and doped graphene rectangulenes. These are non-chiral ribbons of arbitrary length and width, whose dimensions range from simple short acene molecules all the way up to the bulk limit. We rewrite the Hubbard model in the basis of bulk and edge non-interacting eigen-states, and provide explicit expressions for the Coulomb matrix elements. We present a general mean-field decoupling of the Hamiltonian, and discuss in detail the paramagnetic, ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic mean-field solutions. We calculate the eigen-energies, occupations, spin densities and addition energies of rectangulenes with lengths and widths ranging from a nanometer to several hundreds of them. We rewrite the exact mean-field tight-binding Hamiltonian back in the site-occupation basis, that can be used to model electronic, thermo-electric, transport and optical properties of experimental-size graphene flakes.



I
Introduction

The experimental demonstration of the ability to isolate single graphene sheets
Novoselov et al. 2004
promoted the
vision that atomic-scale two-dimensional nanoelectronics and nano-optics could be a viable future
technology
Westervelt 2008
. Elementary units of the graphene lego would then range from
simple graphenoid molecules such as acenes
Yang et al. 2016
;
Eisenhut et al. 2020
, graphene nanoribbons (GNR) and all the
way up to graphene flakes.

Part of the interest in graphene nanostructures stems from the old prediction by Dresselhaus and coworkers
that GNRs having zigzag terminations could host edge states
Nakada et al. 1996
. GNRs’ peculiar electronic
and magnetic structure were the subject of intense theoretical work for the first years after the discovery of graphene

Brey and Fertig 2006
;
Wakabayashi et al. 2010
;
Castro Neto et al. 2009
;
Akhmerov 2011
;
Son et al. 2006
;
Yang et al. 2007
;
Fernández-Rossier 2008
;
Jung and MacDonald 2009
;
Jung et al. 2009
.

Parallel efforts to fabricate GNRs by unzipping carbon nanotubes were only partially successful because the graphene
edges were quite defective
Kosynkin et al. 2009
. However, GNRs having atomically-precise edges were finally synthesized
by bottom-up techniques
Cai et al. 2010
. This breakthrough opened the door to a plethora of subsequent developments in
GNR fabrication and characterization
Kimouche et al. 2015
;
Wang et al. 2016
;
Talirz et al. 2017
;
Lawrence et al. 2022
;
Besteiro-Sáez et al. 2024
.

The topological nature of edge states
Delplace et al. 2011
and the connection between GNRs and the Schrieffer-Heeger-Su
(SSH) model
Su et al. 1979
;
Asbóth et al. 2016
has also been uncovered and analyzed both theoretically
Cao et al. 2017
and
experimentally
Gröning et al. 2018
;
Rizzo et al. 2018
. GNR edge states are predicted to be magnetic
Fujita et al. 1996
;
Lee et al. 2005
;
Son et al. 2006
,
so that magnetism at the edge has been paid attention throughout these years
Slota et al. 2018
;
Lawrence et al. 2020
.
GNRs have been explored in optics for their potential utility as plasmon waveguides
Christensen et al. 2012
;
Fei et al. 2015
.
Recently, single armchair GNRs of precise width have been deposited onto ultra-clean graphene gaps, therefore
creating all-carbon single electron transistors displaying quantum dot behavior
Niu et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023a
;
Zhang et al. 2023b
.

GNRs are theoretically well-described by both Density Functional Theory (DFT)
Son et al. 2006
;
Yang et al. 2007

and the mean-field (MF) Hubbard model
Wakabayashi et al. 1998
;
Fernández-Rossier 2008
. These two approaches estimate successfully
many of the electronic, magnetic and optical properties of GNRs. However, both approaches are numerically costly for
large-size GNRs, which limits the ability to simulate them to narrow widths and short lengths, which are usually much
smaller than the size of the experimental samples.

We view in this article finite-length GNRs rather as graphene rectangles, so that we have named them

rectangulenes
. We have recently been able to solve analytically the tight-binding model of non-chiral
rectangulenes of arbitrary length and width by mapping the model to a wave-guide of finite-length SSH
chains
García-Fuente et al. 2023
. This solution has allowed us to unveil explicitly the bulk-boundary correspondence

Hasan and Kane 2010
in graphene. We have also provided a detailed mapping between DFT-simulated rectangulenes
and a simple two-site Hubbard model of the edge states of narrow ribbons.

We expand here our previous development to a full analytical solution of the MF Hubbard model of graphene
rectangulenes. This new development allows us to simulate undoped and doped rectangulenes of any given size,
ranging from graphenoid molecules all the way to lengths and widths of several hundreds of nanometers or even
micrometers, that we regard as the bulk limit.

Our solution is relevant because it addresses current issues in different fields of physics, chemistry and materials
science. It is also important because it allows us to address theoretically the typical graphene sizes happening in
experimental samples. The solution therefore opens the way to accurate and realistic theoretical descriptions of
an array of electronic, magnetic, transport, thermo-electric and optical phenomena of real-life graphene devices.
The solution can also be extended in the future to more sophisticated descriptions of strong correlations like
the GW approximation
Honet et al. 2023
or Dynamical Mean Field theory
Katanin 2021
.

We perform a basis change from site creation and destruction operators to the basis of bulk and edge
eigen-states. One of the central results of this article concerns the Coulomb integrals, because they can
all be determined analytically. The resulting Hamiltonian is therefore fully known with explicit expressions in
terms of
U/t
. Former DFT and MF calculations were numerically very costly for sizes of about 50 nm, while
simulations for sizes larger than 100-200 nm were beyond the power of today’s computers. However, we
demonstrate that our analytical MF solution delivers all the physics that had to be
previously computed numerically. We illustrate this by performing the popular MF paramagnetic,
ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic solutions (PM, FM and AFM, respectively). The figures shown
in this article have been plotted with the aid of a simple matlab script running in a laptop. Site occupation
calculations lasted the longest, with rectangulenes of size 200 nm
\times
400 nm taking four to five minutes.

Section

III
Hubbard model of a graphene rectangulene

III.1
Transformation to the eigen-state basis

The mean-field solution to the Hubbard model of a rectangulene is initiated by expanding site-creation and
annihilation operators in the basis of rectangulene eigen-states

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}\hat{a}_{{\bf R}i\sigma}\\
\hat{b}_{{\bf R}i\sigma}\end{matrix}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\alpha\tau}\left(\begin{matrix}\braket{{\bf R},{\cal A}_{i}\,|\,\phi_{m\alpha\tau}}\\
\braket{{\bf R},{\cal B}_{i}\,|\,\phi_{m\alpha\tau}}\end{matrix}\right)\,\hat{\phi}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}+

(14)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{m\tau}\left(\begin{matrix}\braket{{\bf R},{\cal A}_{i}\,|\,\psi_{m\tau}}\\
\braket{{\bf R},{\cal B}_{i}\,|\,\psi_{m\tau}}\end{matrix}\right)\,\hat{\psi}_{m\tau\sigma}

We split the site number operators into bulk and edge pieces,

\displaystyle\hat{n}_{{\bf R}i\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{n}^{B}_{{\bf R}i\sigma}+\hat{n}^{E}_{{\bf R}i\sigma}

(15)

\displaystyle\hat{n}^{B}_{{\bf R}i\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{m\alpha}\,\frac{4\,f_{m,i}^{2}}{{\cal M}_{x}\,M_{y}\,\Lambda^{\phi}_{m\alpha}}\,\left(\begin{matrix}(\phi_{m\alpha,i}^{\cal A})^{2}\\
(\phi_{m\alpha,i}^{\cal B})^{2}\end{matrix}\right)\,\hat{n}^{B}_{m\alpha\sigma}

\displaystyle\hat{n}^{E}_{{\bf R}i\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\,\frac{4\,f_{m,i}^{2}}{{\cal M}_{x}\,M_{y}\,\Lambda^{\psi}_{m}}\,\,\left(\begin{matrix}(\psi_{m,i}^{\cal A})^{2}\,(\hat{n}^{E}_{m\sigma}-\hat{P}_{m\sigma})\\
(\psi_{m,i}^{\cal B})^{2}(\hat{n}^{E}_{m\sigma}+\hat{P}_{m\sigma})\end{matrix}\right)

where we have dropped cross bulk-edge, bulk
(m,\,\alpha)\leftrightarrow(m^{\prime},\,\alpha^{\prime})
, bulk

(m,\,\alpha,\,\tau)\leftrightarrow(m,\,\alpha,\,\tau^{\prime})
and
edge
m\leftrightarrow m^{\prime}
terms, but have kept interband edge states
(m,\,\tau)\leftrightarrow(m,\,\tau^{\prime})
because they are
degenerate. Furthermore, we have introduced the following band-summed and band-mixing operators

\displaystyle\hat{n}^{B}_{m\alpha\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=\pm}\hat{n}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}=\sum_{\tau=\pm}\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}\,\hat{\phi}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}

(16)

\displaystyle\hat{n}^{E}_{m\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=\pm}\hat{n}_{m\tau\sigma}=\sum_{\tau=\pm}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{m\tau\sigma}\,\hat{\psi}_{m\tau\sigma}

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{m\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=\pm}\hat{P}_{m\tau\sigma}=\sum_{\tau=\pm}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{m\tau\sigma}\,\hat{\psi}_{m\bar{\tau}\sigma}

where
\bar{\tau}=-\tau
.

The second-quantized version of the rectangulene non-interacting tight-binding Hamiltonian is simply

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}\,\tau\,\epsilon^{B}_{m\alpha}\,\hat{n}^{B}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}+\sum_{m\tau\sigma}\,\tau\,\epsilon^{E}_{m}\,\hat{n}^{E}_{m\tau\sigma}

(17)

The full rectangulene’s Hubbard Hamiltonian is:

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{0}+\hat{V}^{ee}=\hat{H}^{0}+U\,\sum_{{\bf R}i}\,\hat{n}_{{\bf R}i\uparrow}\hat{n}_{{\bf R}i\downarrow}

(18)

III.2
Coulomb integrals

Using Eq. (
15
), we decompose the interacting term in the Hamiltonian into bulk, edge and crossed
contributions as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{V}^{ee}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{V}^{\mathrm{B}}+\hat{V}^{\mathrm{E}}+\hat{V}^{\mathrm{BE}}

(19)

\displaystyle\hat{V}^{\mathrm{B}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{mm^{\prime}\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,U^{B}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\,\hat{n}^{B}_{m\alpha\uparrow}\hat{n}^{B}_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}\downarrow}

\displaystyle\hat{V}^{\mathrm{E}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{mm^{\prime}}\,U^{E}_{m,m^{\prime}}\,\left(\hat{n}^{E}_{m\uparrow}\hat{n}^{E}_{m^{\prime}\downarrow}+\hat{P}_{m\uparrow}\hat{P}_{m^{\prime}\downarrow}\right)

\displaystyle\hat{V}^{\mathrm{BE}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{mm^{\prime}\alpha\sigma}\,U^{BE}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}}\,\hat{n}^{B}_{m\alpha\sigma}\hat{n}^{E}_{m^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}

with
\bar{\sigma}=-\sigma

The Coulomb matrix elements are

\displaystyle U^{B}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\cal C}{\Lambda^{\phi}_{m\alpha}\,\Lambda^{\phi}_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}}\,\sum_{i=1,2}\,U_{m,m^{\prime},i}^{y}\,U_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime},i}^{B,x}

(20)

\displaystyle U^{E}_{m,m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\cal C}{\Lambda^{\psi}_{m}\,\Lambda^{\psi}_{m^{\prime}}}\,\sum_{i=1,2}\,U_{m,m^{\prime},i}^{y}\,U^{E,x}_{m,m^{\prime},i}

\displaystyle U^{BE}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\cal C}{\Lambda^{\phi}_{m\alpha}\,\Lambda^{\psi}_{m^{\prime}}}\,\sum_{i=1,2}\,U_{m,m^{\prime},i}^{y}\,U^{BE,x}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime},i}

where

\displaystyle{\cal C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{32\,U}{{\cal M}_{x}^{2}\,M_{y}^{2}}

(21)

\displaystyle U_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime},i}^{B,x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{R_{x}}\,\left(\phi_{m\alpha,i}^{\cal A}(R_{x})\,\,\phi_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime},i}^{\cal A}(R_{x})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle U^{E,x}_{m,m^{\prime},i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{R_{x}}\,\left(\psi_{m,i}^{\cal A}(R_{x})\,\,\psi_{m^{\prime},i}^{\cal A}(R_{x})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle U^{BE,x}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime},i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{R_{x}}\,\left(\phi_{m\alpha,i}^{\cal A}(R_{x})\,\,\psi_{m^{\prime},i}^{\cal A}(R_{x})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle U_{m,m^{\prime},i}^{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{R_{y}}\,\left(f_{m,i}(R_{y})\,\,f_{m^{\prime},i}(R_{y})\right)^{2}

The above sums can be evaluated analytically. So after some straightforward but lengthy algebra we find that

\displaystyle U^{B}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal D}\,\,\frac{\delta^{+}_{mm^{\prime}}\,C^{B,+}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}+\delta^{-}_{mm^{\prime}}\,C^{B,-}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}/{\cal M}_{x}}{\Lambda^{\phi}_{m\alpha}\,\Lambda^{\phi}_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle U^{E}_{m,m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal D}\,\,\frac{\delta^{+}_{mm^{\prime}}\,C^{E,+}_{m,m^{\prime}}+\delta^{-}_{mm^{\prime}}\,C_{m,m^{\prime}}^{E,-}/{\cal M}_{x}}{G^{1}_{m}\,G^{1}_{m^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle U^{BE}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,{\cal D}\,\,\,\frac{\delta^{+}_{mm^{\prime}}\,C^{BE,+}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}}+\delta^{-}_{mm^{\prime}}\,C^{BE,-}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}}/{\cal M}_{x}}{\Lambda_{m\alpha}\,G^{1}_{m^{\prime}}}

We have introduced the following short-hand notation to simplify the expressions above

\displaystyle{\cal D}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{U}{2\,{\cal M}_{x}\,M_{y}}

(22)

\displaystyle\delta_{mm^{\prime}}^{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}+\delta_{m,M_{y}}\delta_{m^{\prime},M_{y}}\right)

\displaystyle\delta_{mm^{\prime}}^{-}

IV
Mean-field solutions of the Hubbard model of a graphene rectangulene

IV.1
Bulk states

We apply first a mean-field approximation to the bulk piece of the Hamiltonian, where

n^{B}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}=\braket{\hat{n}^{B}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}}
. We find

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{B}_{MF}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}\,\xi^{B}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}\,\hat{n}^{B}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}

(31)

\displaystyle\xi^{B}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau\,\epsilon^{B}_{m\alpha}+\sum_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\,U^{B}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\,n^{B}_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}+\sum_{m^{\prime}}U^{BE}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}}\,n^{E}_{m^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}

where the dispersion relation is spin-split because of the bulk-edge cross-terms. The bulk contribution to the
total energy is

\displaystyle E_{T}^{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}\,\xi^{B}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}\,n_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}-E_{\mathrm{dc}}^{B}

(32)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{dc}}^{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{mm^{\prime}\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,U^{B}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\,n^{B}_{m\alpha\uparrow}\,n^{B}_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}\downarrow}

We shall assume for now that the rectangulene is in the
edge-only doping
regime, where doping is low enough that missing/extra
electrons only affect edge states. As
a consequence, bulk states will always be half-filled,
n_{m\alpha-\sigma}=1
and
n_{m\alpha+\sigma}=0
.
Hence the number of electrons residing in bulk states is
{\cal N}^{B}=2(\,M_{x}\,N-N^{\mathrm{edge}})
, where the number of
edge states
N^{\mathrm{edge}}
is given in Eq. (
5
). The bulk dispersion relation simplifies to

\displaystyle\xi^{B}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau\,\epsilon^{B}_{m\alpha}+{\cal U}^{B}_{m\alpha}+\sum_{m^{\prime}}\,U^{BE}_{m\alpha,m^{\prime}}\,n^{E}_{m^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}

(33)

And the contribution of bulk states to the rectangulene total energy is

\displaystyle E^{B}_{T}=\sum_{m\alpha}\left(\xi^{B}_{m\alpha-\uparrow}+\xi^{B}_{m\alpha-\downarrow}-{\cal U}^{B}_{m\alpha}\right)

(34)

We also have that the bulk contribution to the magnetization is identically zero,
m^{B}=0
.

The apparently anodyne result in Eq. (
33
) when paired with the sum rule in Eq.
(
III.2
) amounts to a huge simplification that enables us to carry out calculations for huge rectangulenes,
because the number of Coulomb matrix elements to be calculated and stored is reduced from
M_{x}^{2}\,N^{2}
to
M_{x}\,NN^{edge}
.

IV.2
Edge states

We perform now a mean-field approximation to the edge piece of the Hamiltonian so that

n^{E}_{m\tau\sigma}=\braket{\hat{n}^{E}_{m\tau\sigma}}
and
P_{m\tau\sigma}=\braket{\hat{P}_{m\tau\sigma}}
.
We also have the band-summed relationships

n^{E}_{m\sigma}=n^{E}_{m-\sigma}+n^{E}_{m+\sigma}
and

P_{m\sigma}=P_{m-\sigma}+P_{m+\sigma}
. The edge mean-field Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{E}_{MF}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\tau\sigma}\,\xi^{E}_{m\tau\sigma}\,\hat{n}^{E}_{m\tau\sigma}+\sum_{m\sigma}\,U^{E}_{m,m^{\prime}}\,P_{m^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}\,\hat{P}_{m\sigma}

(35)

\displaystyle\xi^{E}_{m\tau\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau\,\epsilon^{E}_{m}+{\cal U}^{BE}_{m}+\sum_{m^{\prime}}\,U^{E}_{m,m^{\prime}}\,n^{E}_{m^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}

Diagonalization of the mean-field edge Hamiltonian provides us with the edge eigen-energies
\xi^{E}_{m\tau\sigma}
and
edge eigen-states. The edge states’ occupations are determined by the equation of state

\displaystyle n^{E}_{m\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{F}(\xi^{E}_{m+\sigma})+n_{F}(\xi^{E}_{m-\sigma})

(36)

where
n_{F}
is the Fermi function. The order parameter
P_{m\sigma}
is determined by solving the self-consistency
equations adequate to each mean-field solution, as discussed below.

We introduce the edge filling
\delta^{E}
by taking half-filling as a reference:

\displaystyle\delta^{E}={\cal N}^{E}-2\,N^{\mathrm{edge}}=\sum_{m\sigma}n^{E}_{m\sigma}-2\,N^{\mathrm{edge}}

(37)

The edge contribution to the rectangulene total energy is determined by the equation

\displaystyle E^{E}_{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\tau\sigma}\xi^{E}_{m\tau\sigma}\,n^{E}_{m\tau\sigma}-E_{\mathrm{dc}}^{E}

(38)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{dc}}^{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{mm^{\prime}}\,U^{E}_{m,m^{\prime}}\,\left(n^{E}_{m\uparrow}\,n^{E}_{m^{\prime}\downarrow}+P_{m\uparrow}\,P_{m^{\prime}\downarrow}\right)+\sum_{m}\,{\cal U}^{BE}_{m}\,n^{E}_{m}

IV.3
Paramagnetic solution

The PM mean-field solution is found by setting
n^{E}_{m\tau\uparrow}=n^{E}_{m\tau\downarrow}=n^{E}_{m\tau}/2
and

P_{m\tau\sigma}=0
. Then the edge and bulk dispersion relations do not depend on the spin degree of freedom

\displaystyle\xi^{E}_{m\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau\,\epsilon^{E}_{m}+{\cal U}^{BE}_{m}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m^{\prime}}U^{E}_{mm^{\prime}}\,n^{E}_{m^{\prime}}

(39)

\displaystyle\xi^{B}_{m\alpha\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau\,\epsilon^{B}_{m\alpha}+{\cal U}^{B}_{m\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m^{\prime}}U^{BE}_{m\alpha m^{\prime}}n^{E}_{m^{\prime}}

Finally, the edge double-counting contribution to the total energy is

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{dc}}^{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\,\sum_{mm^{\prime}}\,U^{E}_{m,m^{\prime}}\,n^{E}_{m}\,n^{E}_{m^{\prime}}+\sum_{m}\,{\cal U}^{BE}_{m}\,n^{E}_{m}

(40)

IV.4
Ferromagnetic solution

The FM mean-field solution is found by setting
n^{E}_{m\tau\uparrow}\neq n^{E}_{m\tau\downarrow}
and

P_{m\tau\sigma}=0
. Then the edge and bulk dispersion relations are

\displaystyle\xi^{E}_{m\tau\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau\,\epsilon^{E}_{m}+{\cal U}^{BE}_{m}+\sum_{m^{\prime}}U^{E}_{mm^{\prime}}\,n^{E}_{m^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}

(41)

\displaystyle\xi^{B}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau\,\epsilon^{B}_{m\alpha}+{\cal U}^{B}_{m\alpha}+\sum_{m^{\prime}}U^{BE}_{m\alpha m^{\prime}}n^{E}_{m^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}

and the edge double-counting term is

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{dc}}^{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{mm^{\prime}}\,U^{E}_{m,m^{\prime}}\,n^{E}_{m\uparrow}\,n^{E}_{m^{\prime}\downarrow}+\sum_{m}\,{\cal U}^{BE}_{m}\,n^{E}_{m}

\displaystyle\sum_{m\alpha\sigma}\,\xi^{B}_{m\alpha-\sigma}+\sum_{m\tau\sigma}\,\xi^{E}_{m\tau\sigma}\,n^{E}_{m\tau\sigma}-\frac{U}{2}(1+\delta^{E})

The AFM solution is selected by letting the order parameter

be different from zero.
In this case the edge Hamiltonian looks

IV.6
Beyond the
edge-only doping
regime

The
edge-only doping
regime is too restrictive an approximation in several instances. Examples are
gate- or voltage-biased finite-length 7-AGNR
Zhang et al. 2023b
or bulk-size rectangulenes where the Dirac-point
gap is negligible. Fortunately, the approximation can be released to include low-lying bulk states within the
self-consitency procedure. This can be achieved by choosing a small energy cutoff
E_{c}
so that occupations of
bulk states with energies
|\xi^{B}_{m\alpha\tau\sigma}-\mu|
smaller than
E_{c}
are determined self-consistently, while
those above this energy cutoff are frozen at 0 or 1.

IV.7
Dispersion relations

The analysis above opens the door to determine easily the electronic structure on rectangulenes of any size in
the edge-only doping regime. We will take below a hopping integral

A first simple example of a small nano-structure is shown in Figure

IV.9
Energy differences among phases

Jung and MacDonald analyzed phase stabilities of narrow infinite-length zigzag GNRs as a function of
doping
Jung et al. 2009
. We discuss here energy differences of undoped rectangulenes as a function of width and
length (see Fig.
7
). Overall, we find that the magnetic energy, measured as the energy difference
E_{T}^{PM}-E_{T}^{AFM}
is
roughly independent of
M_{x}
, especially for
M_{x}
larger than about 20. This is expected because for large
enough
M_{x}
, the tails of the edge wavefunctions decay enough that tails at opposite edges do not overlap.
It is in contrast roughly proportional to
M_{y}
, e.g.: to the number of edge states. We also find that the
energy difference among the FM and AFM phases
E_{T}^{FM}-E_{T}^{AFM}
decays quickly with
M_{x}
so that the
edge states at opposite edges become independent for
M_{x}
larger than about 40-60.
For the
M_{y}
dependence, we find that
E_{T}^{FM}-E_{T}^{AFM}
presents oscillations related to the
change in the number of edge states of the system.

IV.11
There and back again: real-space tight-binding Hamiltonian

The real-space mean-field Hubbard Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{MF}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf R}i\sigma}\sum_{a=A,B}\,\epsilon_{{\bf R}i\sigma}^{a}\hat{n}^{a}_{{\bf R}i\sigma}-

(60)

\displaystyle-t\,\sum_{<{\bf R}i\sigma,{\bf R^{\prime}}i^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}>}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{{\bf R}i\sigma}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{{\bf R^{\prime}}i^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}+c.c.\right)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{{\bf R}i\sigma}^{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\epsilon_{0}+U\,n^{a}_{Ri\bar{\sigma}}\right)

incorporates already electron correlations and edge physics. This Hamiltonian can be used to address more
complex phenomena by adding to it additional pieces. Site/hopping disorder can be addressed by replacing

\epsilon_{0}
/
t
by a random distribution of on-site energies/hopping integrals. Similarly, a Peierls phase
can be attached to the hopping integrals to investigate Hall physics. Coupling to a gauge vector can
be included to analyse the optical response of the rectangulene to light.

V
Conclusions

We have presented in this article a full analytical solution of the mean-field Hubbard model of non-chiral
graphene rectangulenes of arbitrary length and width. A central aspect of the article has been the
determination of the bulk, edge and cross Coulomb integrals of the rectangulene, that are written here
for the first time. This solution is not only an algebraic curiosity, but rather is a powerful and flexible
platform that enables us to address a wide range of experimental issues in STM, transport, magnetic, Hall and
optical phenomena of real-life graphene rectangulenes. It can also be used to address strong
electron correlations, by including GW on top of it, or any other perturbative approach.
