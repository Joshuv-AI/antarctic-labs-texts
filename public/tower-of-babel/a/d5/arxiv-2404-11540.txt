Bremsstrahlung Radiation Power in Fusion Plasmas Revisited: Towards Accurate Analytical Fitting
Huasheng Xie

Abstract
In fusion plasmas, where electron temperatures T e T_{e} range from keV to hundreds of keV, Bremsstrahlung radiation constitutes a significant energy loss mechanism. While various thermal average fitting formulas exist in the literature, their accuracy is limited, particularly for T e ≤ 20 T_{e}\leq 20 keV with error > 10 % >10\% . Additionally, non-relativistic fitting formulas become invalid for T e ≥ 50 T_{e}\geq 50 keV. The accurate calculation of Bremsstrahlung radiation is important for fusion gain study, especially of advanced fuels fusion. In this work, we develop new but still simple fitting formulas that are valid for electron temperatures ranging from small ( ≲ 0.1 \lesssim 0.1 keV) to extreme relativistic range, with errors of less than 1% even for high atomic number ions (e.g., Z = 30 Z=30 ), which could be sufficient for fusion plasma applications. Both electron-ion (e-i) and electron-electron (e-e) Bremsstrahlung radiations are considered. Both polynomial fitting with truncated ( t = k B ​ T e / m e ​ c 2 ≤ 10 t=k_{B}T_{e}/m_{e}c^{2}\leq 10 ) and one-fit-all formulas are provided. Additionally, we offer fitting formulas for e-i and e-e specifically for electron energies, which could prove useful in non-Maxwellian Bremsstrahlung radiation studies.

Bremsstrahlung radiation

The incoming particle loses kinetic energy, which is converted into radiation, i.e., photons. In general, we refer to bremsstrahlung as the radiation emitted by an electron as it decelerates in the field of other charged particles. In fusion plasmas, since the atoms are usually fully ionized, the major Bremsstrahlung radiation loss is electron-ion (e-i) radiation, i.e., free-free radiation. 2
Revisiting the calculation models

Due to the complicated relativistic and quantum effects, the accurate calculation of Bremsstrahlung radiation is challenging. Many models have been developed. We review some of the most commonly used models in this section. We are mainly interested in the total power loss, and thus the energy spectrum of the radiation can be integrated out. We use SI units here.

Firstly, we summarize some useful notations. The Thomson cross section is given by

\sigma_{t}=\frac{8\pi}{3}r_{e}^{2}=6.652\times 10^{-29}\,\rm{m}^{2},

(1)

where the classical electron radius is

r_{e}=\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}m_{e}c^{2}}=\alpha\frac{hc}{2\pi m_{e}c^{2}}=2.8179\times 10^{-15}\,\rm{m},

(2)

and the fine structure constant is

\alpha=\frac{e^{2}}{2\epsilon_{0}hc}\approx\frac{1}{137.0}.

(3)

Here,
c=2.99792458\times 10^{8}\,\rm{m/s}
is the speed of light,
h=6.626\times 10^{-34}\,\rm{J}\cdot\rm{s}
is Planck’s constant,
e=1.602\times 10^{-19}\,\rm{C}
is the electron charge,
m_{e}=9.1094\times 10^{-31}\,\rm{kg}
is the electron mass,
\epsilon_{0}=8.8542\times 10^{-12}\,\rm{F/m}
is the vacuum dielectric constant, and
k_{B}=1.3806\times 10^{-23}\,\rm{J/K}
is the Boltzmann constant.

The Rydberg energy is given by

R_{y}=\frac{m_{e}e^{4}}{8\epsilon_{0}^{2}h^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}m_{e}c^{2}=2.17987\times 10^{-18}\,\rm{J}=13.6\,\rm{eV}.

(4)

After integrating over the angles of the outgoing electron and photon, unit time, and unit volume, the radiation photon number between the energy/frequency range
[\nu,\nu+d\nu]
is given by
[

\displaystyle\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{1+\delta_{12}}\int d^{3}\mathbf{p}_{1}d^{3}\mathbf{p}_{2}f_{1}(\mathbf{p}_{1})f_{2}(\mathbf{p}_{2})\cdot

(5)

\displaystyle\left[(\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{v}_{2})^{2}-\frac{\mathbf{v}_{1}\times\mathbf{v}_{2}}{c^{2}}\right]^{1/2}\frac{d\sigma}{d\nu},

where
n_{1,2}

and

2.1
Non-relativistic e-i

The non-relativistic e-i case has been well solved
[
31
,
33
,
34
]
. The classical model is due to Kramers, and more accurate ones include the Born approximation and Elwert’s correction for low energy. The most accurate one is due to Sommerfeld. The Kramers cross-section is given by
[
40
]

\frac{d\sigma_{k}}{d\nu}=\frac{A}{\nu\sqrt{3}\pi}\eta_{0}^{2}.

(6)

The Born approximation is expressed as

\frac{d\sigma_{b}}{d\nu}=\frac{A}{\nu\pi^{2}}\eta_{0}^{2}\ln\left(\frac{\eta_{f}+\eta_{0}}{\eta_{f}-\eta_{0}}\right).

(7)

The Elwert correction to the Born approximation is given by

\frac{d\sigma_{e}}{d\nu}=\frac{A}{\nu\pi^{2}}\eta_{0}\eta_{f}\frac{1-e^{-2\pi\eta_{0}}}{1-e^{-2\pi\eta_{f}}}\ln\left(\frac{\eta_{f}+\eta_{0}}{\eta_{f}-\eta_{0}}\right).

(8)

The most accurate Sommerfeld non-relativistic expression is
[
32
]

\frac{d\sigma_{s}}{d\nu}=\frac{A}{\nu}\frac{\eta_{0}^{2}}{(e^{2\pi\eta_{0}}-1)(1-e^{-2\pi\eta_{f}})}x\frac{d}{dx}|F(x)|^{2},

(9)

where

\displaystyle\eta_{0,f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{Z^{2}R_{y}}{E_{0,f}}\right)^{1/2},~~x=\frac{4\eta_{0}\eta_{f}}{(\eta_{0}-\eta_{f})^{2}},

(10)

\displaystyle v_{0,f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2E_{0,f}}{m_{e}}},~~E_{f}=E_{0}-h\nu,

(11)

and
F(x)
is the hypergeometric function defined as

F(x)=\,_{2}F_{1}(i\eta_{0},i\eta_{f};1;-x),

(12)

and

A=\frac{2\pi\sigma_{t}}{\alpha}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{he^{2}}{\epsilon_{0}m_{e}^{2}c^{3}}=5.728\times 10^{-26}\,\mathrm{m^{2}}.

(13)

Note that in some references, the unit
\epsilon_{0}
is taken as
\frac{1}{4\pi}
. The radiation photon energy
h\nu\in[0,E_{0}]

E_{0}

To avoid differentiation, another equivalent equation is given by

\displaystyle x\frac{d}{dx}|F(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{0}\eta_{f}\frac{|F^{2}(-i\eta_{0}+1,-i\eta_{f},1;-x)-F^{2}(-i\eta_{f}+1,-i\eta_{0},1;-x)|}{\eta_{f}-\eta_{0}}.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(E_{0})dE_{0}=1,

\displaystyle P_{\nu}=n_{e}n_{i}\int_{h\nu}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{2E_{0}}{m_{e}}\Big)^{1/2}h\nu f(E_{0})\frac{d\sigma}{d\nu}dE_{0}.

For thermal Maxwellian distributions

\displaystyle P_{\nu,k}=n_{e}n_{i}\frac{32\pi Z^{2}e^{6}}{3(4\pi\epsilon_{0})^{3}m_{e}c^{3}}\Big(\frac{2\pi}{3m_{e}k_{B}T_{e}}\Big)^{1/2}e^{-\frac{h\nu}{k_{B}T_{e}}},

(20)

\displaystyle P_{\nu,b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{e}n_{i}\frac{32\pi Z^{2}e^{6}}{3(4\pi\epsilon_{0})^{3}hm_{e}c^{3}}\Big(\frac{2\pi}{m_{e}k_{B}T_{e}}\Big)^{1/2}\cdot

\displaystyle P=n_{e}n_{i}\frac{64Z^{2}e^{6}}{3(4\pi\epsilon_{0})^{3}m_{e}c^{3}}\Big(\frac{2\pi k_{B}T_{e}}{m_{e}}\Big)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ax}\ln(\sqrt{x+1}+\sqrt{x})dx=\frac{1}{2a}e^{a/2}K_{0}\Big(\frac{a}{2}\Big),

(23)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ax}K_{0}(ax)dx=\frac{1}{a}.

2.1.2
For arbitrary distributions

For arbitrary distributions, the total power can be obtained by first integrating over frequency and then integrating over energy, i.e.,

\displaystyle P=n_{e}n_{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{E_{0}}\Big(\frac{2E_{0}}{m_{e}}\Big)^{1/2}h\nu f(E_{0})\frac{d\sigma}{d\nu}d\nu dE_{0}.

(25)

That is

\displaystyle Q_{E}=\int_{0}^{E_{0}}h\nu\frac{d\sigma}{d\nu}d\nu,

(26)

\displaystyle P=n_{e}n_{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{2E_{0}}{m_{e}}\Big)^{1/2}Q_{E}f(E_{0})dE_{0}.

(27)

Define

\displaystyle w=\frac{\nu\sqrt{3}\pi}{AR_{y}Z^{2}}\frac{d\sigma}{d\nu},

(28)

which gives

\displaystyle w_{k}=\frac{1}{E_{0}},

(29)

\displaystyle w_{b}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\frac{1}{E_{0}}\ln\Big(\frac{\sqrt{E_{0}}+\sqrt{E_{f}}}{\sqrt{E_{0}}-\sqrt{E_{f}}}\Big).

(30)

And define the frequency integral weight

\displaystyle W_{E}(E_{0})=\int_{0}^{E_{0}}wdh\nu,

(31)

which yields,

\displaystyle W_{E,k}(E_{0})=1,

(32)

\displaystyle W_{E,b}(E_{0})=\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\pi},

(33)

where we have used
\int_{0}^{a}[2\ln(\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{a-x})-\ln x]dx=2a
. The Elwert
W_{E,e}(E_{0})
and Sommerfeld
W_{E,s}(E_{0})

For arbitrary distributions, the total power is

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{e}n_{i}\frac{AR_{y}Z^{2}}{\sqrt{3}\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{2E_{0}}{m_{e}}\Big)^{1/2}W_{E}(E_{0})f(E_{0})dE_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{e}n_{i}\frac{16\pi^{2}Z^{2}e^{6}}{3\sqrt{3}(4\pi\epsilon_{0})^{3}hm_{e}c^{3}}\langle v_{0}\rangle_{W},

\displaystyle\langle v_{0}\rangle_{W}\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{2E_{0}}{m_{e}}\Big)^{1/2}W_{E}(E_{0})f(E_{0})dE_{0},

2.2
Relativistic e-i

The usual relativistic e-i model used is from Bethe–Heitler (BH) with Born approximation, which is valid for high energy but not correct for low electron energy and low photon energies because the Coulomb distortion of the wave functions becomes too large, and the Born approximation breaks down. Using the notation of Chluba et al
[
25
]
, the classical Kramers model is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\sigma_{k}(\omega,p_{0})}{d\omega}=\frac{2\alpha Z^{2}}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sigma_{t}}{p_{0}^{2}\omega}=\frac{16\pi\alpha Z^{2}}{3\sqrt{3}}\frac{r_{e}^{2}}{p_{0}^{2}\omega}.

(39)

The first-order relativistic Born approximation model (BH)
[
42
]
with Elwert correction is

\displaystyle\frac{d\sigma_{BH}}{d\omega}=\frac{d\sigma_{k}(\omega,p_{0})}{d\omega}\frac{\eta_{f}}{\eta_{0}}\frac{(1-e^{-2\pi\eta_{0}})}{(1-e^{-2\pi\eta_{f}})}g_{BH}(\omega,p_{0}),

(40)

\displaystyle g_{BH}(\omega,p_{0})=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\Big[\frac{p_{0}p_{f}}{4}-\frac{3}{8}\gamma_{0}\gamma_{f}\Big(\frac{p_{0}}{p_{f}}+\frac{p_{f}}{p_{0}}\Big)+\gamma_{0}\gamma_{f}L+

\displaystyle\frac{3}{8}\omega L\Big[\Big(1+\frac{\gamma_{0}\gamma_{f}}{p_{0}^{2}}\Big)\frac{\lambda_{0}}{p_{0}}-\Big(1+\frac{\gamma_{0}\gamma_{f}}{p_{f}^{2}}\Big)\frac{\lambda_{f}}{p_{f}}+\omega\Big(1+\frac{\gamma_{0}\gamma_{f}}{p_{0}^{2}p_{f}^{2}}+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}\gamma_{f}^{2}}{p_{0}^{2}p_{f}^{2}}\Big)\Big]+\frac{3}{8}\Big(\frac{\gamma_{f}p_{f}}{p_{0}^{2}}\lambda_{0}+\frac{\gamma_{0}p_{0}}{p_{f}^{2}}\lambda_{f}-2\lambda_{0}\lambda_{f}\Big)\Big],

(41)

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}=\ln(\gamma_{i}+p_{i}),~~L=\ln\Big(\frac{\gamma_{0}\gamma_{f}+p_{0}p_{f}-1}{\omega}\Big),

\displaystyle\omega=\frac{\hbar\nu}{m_{e}c^{2}},~~\eta_{i}=\frac{aZ\gamma_{i}}{p_{i}},~~\gamma=(1+p^{2})^{1/2},

\displaystyle E=\sqrt{p^{2}c^{2}+(m_{e}c^{2})^{2}}=\gamma m_{e}c^{2},~~p_{f}=\sqrt{p_{0}^{2}+\omega(\omega-2\gamma_{0})}.

Here,
\hbar=h/(2\pi)
,
p_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}
have been normalized with
m_{e}c
and
m_{e}c^{2}
. Note that the electron velocity is given by
v=pc^{2}/E
, and the kinetic energy by
E_{k}=m_{e}c^{2}(\gamma-1)
.

Again, we define

\displaystyle w=\frac{\omega\sqrt{3}\pi}{ARZ^{2}}\frac{d\sigma}{d\omega},~~W_{E}(E_{k0})=\int_{0}^{E_{k0}}wdh\nu,

(42)

which gives

\displaystyle w_{BH}=\frac{1}{m_{e}c^{2}}\frac{2}{p_{0}^{2}}\frac{\eta_{f}}{\eta_{0}}\frac{(1-e^{-2\pi\eta_{0}})}{(1-e^{-2\pi\eta_{f}})}g_{BH}(\omega,p_{0}).

(43)

For arbitrary distribution, the total power is

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{e}n_{i}\frac{AR_{y}Z^{2}}{\sqrt{3}\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{0}^{2}W_{E}|E_{rel}|f(p_{0})dp_{0}

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{e}n_{i}\frac{AR_{y}Z^{2}}{\sqrt{3}\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}W_{E}\frac{cp_{0}^{3}}{\gamma_{0}}f(p_{0})dp_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{e}n_{i}\frac{16\pi^{2}Z^{2}e^{6}}{3\sqrt{3}(4\pi\epsilon_{0})^{3}hm_{e}c^{3}}\langle p_{0}c\rangle_{W},

where

\displaystyle|E_{rel}|=\frac{cp_{0}}{\gamma_{0}},~~\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{0}^{2}f(p_{0})dp_{0}=1,

(45)

and

\displaystyle\langle p_{0}c\rangle_{W}=\int_{0}^{\infty}W_{E}\frac{cp_{0}^{3}}{\gamma_{0}}f(p_{0})dp_{0},

(46)

is the weight averaged velocity.

The relativistic Maxwellian distribution is given by

\displaystyle f(p)=\frac{e^{-\gamma(p)/t}}{\theta K_{2}(1/t)},~~\gamma=(1+p^{2})^{1/2},~~t=k_{B}T/mc^{2},

(47)

where
K_{2}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind. Reduced to the non-relativistic case, we have

\displaystyle f(p)\simeq\frac{\sqrt{2}e^{-p^{2}/2t}}{\pi^{1/2}t^{3/2}},

(48)

where
K_{\alpha}(z\to\infty)\to\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}e^{-z}
. Hence the average velocity is

\displaystyle\langle p_{0}c\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{cp_{0}^{3}}{\gamma_{0}}f(p_{0})dp_{0}=c\frac{2t(t+1)e^{-1/t}}{K_{2}(1/t)}

(49)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle c\Big(\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}}\sqrt{t}-\frac{7}{\sqrt{8\pi}}\sqrt{t^{3}}+\cdots\Big).

Define the Gaunt factor as

\displaystyle g_{ei}=\frac{\langle p_{0}\rangle_{W}}{\langle p_{0}\rangle_{M}},~~\langle p_{0}\rangle_{M}=\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}}\sqrt{t},

(50)

where
\langle p_{0}\rangle_{M}
is the non-relativistic lowest order average velocity normalized by
c
. The relativistic Gaunt factor comes from both the relativistic effect and the distribution function, i.e., the relativistic distribution has changed the relation between temperature and the average velocity. The calculation of the relativistic BH model could be even simpler than the non-relativistic Sommerfeld model, since no complicated special function such as the hypergeometric function is involved. Chluba et al
[
25
]
use a more complicated five-dimensional integral EH
[
27
]
model which can reduce to BH and Sommerfeld, showing that the direct combination of these two models as in Hoof
[
24
]
can still be a good approximation for most regions. Hence, we use the data in Hoof
[
24
]
as our starting point. Our calculation agrees well with Hoof
[
24
]
, as shown in Fig.
2
. The comparison of different models is also shown.

For the extreme relativistic (ER) case (
t\gg 1
), the above result reduces to
[
43
]

\displaystyle g_{ei}^{ER}(t)=\frac{9\sqrt{6}}{8\sqrt{\pi}}t^{1/2}\Big[\ln(2t)+\frac{3}{2}-C_{E}\Big],

(51)

where
C_{E}=0.5772
is the Euler constant.

2.3
Non-relativistic e-e

The e-e radiation can also be calculated via Gaunt factor:

\displaystyle g_{ee}(t,u)=tJ(t,u).

(52)

For Maxwellian plasma
[

,

\displaystyle J(t,u)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{10\sqrt{2}\pi}u^{2}e^{u}I(t,u),~~t=\frac{k_{B}T_{e}}{m_{e}c^{2}},~~u=\frac{h\nu}{k_{B}T_{e}},

(53)

\displaystyle I(t,u)=\int_{0}^{1}dyF_{ee}A(u,y),

(54)

\displaystyle A(u,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-u/y}}{y^{3}}\Big\{\Big[17-\frac{3y^{2}}{(2-y)^{2}}\Big]\sqrt{1-y}+\ln\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{y}-1}\Big)

(55)

\displaystyle\cdot\Big[\frac{12(2-y)^{4}-7(2-y)^{2}y^{2}-3y^{4}}{(2-y)^{3}}\Big]\Big\},

\displaystyle F_{ee}(u,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-y}}\frac{\exp\Big(\pi\alpha\sqrt{\frac{y}{tu}}\Big)-1}{\exp\Big(\pi\alpha\sqrt{\frac{y}{tu(1-y)}}\Big)-1},

(56)

where the integral term

in

comes from Elwert’s Coulomb correction for low energy
[

[

,

After integrating over frequency:

\displaystyle g_{ee}(t)=t\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u}J(t,u)du=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{10\sqrt{2}\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}I(t,u)du.

(57)

If we omit the Elwert correction, i.e., set
F_{ee}=1
, the Gaunt factor can be integrated out analytically to
[

,

,
22

\displaystyle g_{ee}(t)=g_{0}^{NR}=\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\frac{5(44-3\pi^{2})}{24\sqrt{2}}t\simeq\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}t,

(58)

where

is used.

3
Analytical Fitting for thermal averaged electron temperature

After the knowledge learned from Section
2
, we can fit firstly the accurate non-relativistic part and extreme-relativistic part, and then fit the middle range, which can keep all regions accurate. The total radiation power for Maxwellian quasi-neutral plasma is

\displaystyle P=\frac{32\pi e^{6}n_{e}^{2}}{3(4\pi\epsilon_{0})^{3}hm_{e}c^{3}}\Big(\frac{2\pi k_{B}T_{e}}{3m_{e}}\Big)^{1/2}[Zg_{ei}(t,Z)+g_{ee}(t)].

(61)

In this section, we provide the fittings with truncated polynomial to
t\leq 10
to error less than 1%. Some more fittings will be listed in
A
.

3.1
Fitting for e-i

We firstly fit the non-relativistic term
f_{nr}
, which depends on
t/Z^{2}
. Then we fit the remaining relativistic term
f_{r}
for
Z=1
. And finally, we add the small term
f_{z}
for
Z\neq 1
. For
t\leq 10
, we have (
g_{ei}^{fit\#1}
)

\displaystyle g_{ei}(t,Z)=c_{0}(1+f_{nr}-f_{z})+f_{r},

(62)

\displaystyle f_{nr}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{nr1}\Big\{1-\exp\Big[-\Big(c_{nr2}\frac{Z^{2}}{t}\Big)^{c_{nr4}}\Big]\Big\}-

(63)

\displaystyle(c_{nr1}+c_{nr6})\exp\Big[-\Big(\frac{t}{c_{nr3}Z^{2}}\Big)^{c_{nr5}}\Big],

\displaystyle f_{r}=c_{0}\sum_{j=1}^{10}c_{rj}t^{j},

(64)

\displaystyle f_{z}=\frac{\Big(\frac{Z}{10}\Big)c_{z1}\Big(100t\sqrt{\frac{10}{Z}}\Big)^{c_{z2}}}{\exp\Big[c_{z3}\Big(100t\sqrt{\frac{10}{Z}}\Big)^{c_{z4}}\Big]-1}.

(65)

where

\displaystyle c_{nr}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[0.4302,~24.2255e-5,~0.7546e-5,~0.5282,~

\displaystyle 0.3301,~0.0911],

\displaystyle c_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[0.55467,~2.6346,~-2.277595,~1.1480,~

\displaystyle-0.36465,~0.07451,~-0.00975,~0.0007885,

\displaystyle-3.5841e-5,~6.99834e-7],

\displaystyle c_{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[5.760e4,~3.440,~16.80,~0.1333].

For
t>10
, we use
g_{ei}(t)=g_{ei}^{ER}(t)
in Eq.(
51
) for the fitting.

For low accuracy with error less than 5%, using only
f_{nr}
and sixth-order polynomial

can be used for

, as given in
A.1
. Additionally, a more complicated form to fit all ranges to high accuracy is provided in

The results are shown in Fig.

3.2
Fitting for e-e

It is difficult to use a polynomial alone (less than tenth order) to fit all the effects of Elwert correction at low temperature, non-relativistic factor, and relativistic effect to high accuracy. Hence, we use the combination of three factors to do the fitting for
t\leq 10
. We use

\displaystyle g_{ee}(t)=F_{ee}F_{NR}(1+0.53t+9.48t^{2}-0.67t^{3}+0.027t^{4}),

(66)

with

\displaystyle F_{ee}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\Big\{\tanh\Big[0.602(\log_{10}t+5.06)\Big]+1\Big\},

(67)

and

\displaystyle F_{NR}(t)=t\frac{6\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2}\pi}\frac{1}{2}\Big\{\tanh\Big[-2.153\log_{10}\Big(\frac{t}{0.43}\Big)\Big]+1\Big\}.

(68)

The above Elwert correction factor
F_{ee}
is rather accurate to an error less than 0.2%. The
F_{NR}
with
\tanh
function is to separate the non-relativistic term since it is proportional to
t
. The remaining polynomial term is to fit the relativistic effect.

For low accuracy with an error less than 2%, using only
F_{ee}
and a fifth-order polynomial can be used for
t\leq 2
, as given in
A.1
. Additionally, a more complicated form for fitting all ranges to high accuracy is also provided in
A.2
.

The results are shown in Fig.
6
, with an overall error less than 1% compared with Nozawa09. The Elwert correction is only important at low energy range, say,
T_{e}\leq 10
keV, where the e-e is far smaller than e-i. Thus, high accuracy is not a must and one can also set
F_{ee}=1
for simplification. For
t>10
, one can use
g_{ee}(t)=g_{ee}^{ER}(t)
in Eq.(
60
) for the fitting.

4
Analytical Fitting for electron energy

To study the arbitrary distribution of electrons, the fitting of the Bremsstralung cross-section for energy could be useful to reduce the calculations.

4.1
Fitting for e-i

From Eq.(
42
), we need only fitting
W_{E}(E,Z)
. We use a similar method as in Hoof15
[
24
]
to merge the non-relativistic result and BH relativistic result and then give the fitting. The merged result is set as

\displaystyle W_{E}=(W_{E,S})^{f_{w}}(W_{E,BH})^{1-f_{w}},

(69)

with weight

\displaystyle f_{w}=\frac{1}{2}\Big\{1+\tanh\Big[-2.5\log_{10}\Big(\frac{E}{E_{m}}\Big)\Big]\Big\},

(70)

where
E_{m}
is the energy where the distance between
W_{E,S}
and
W_{E,BH}
is minimum.

Taking
e=E_{k}/m_{e}c^{2}=\epsilon-1
, we have the following fitting

\displaystyle W_{E,ei}=c_{0}\cdot f_{nr}\cdot f_{r}\cdot(1-f_{z}),

(71)

with

\displaystyle f_{nr}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+c_{nr1}\Big\{1-\exp\Big[-\Big(c_{nr2}\frac{Z^{2}}{e}\Big)^{c_{nr4}}\Big]\Big\}-

(72)

\displaystyle\Big(c_{nr1}+1-\frac{1}{c_{0}}\Big)\exp\Big[-\Big(\frac{e}{c_{nr3}Z^{2}}\Big)^{c_{nr5}}\Big],

\displaystyle f_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+1.069\frac{p^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}-2.1939\frac{p^{3}}{\epsilon^{3}}+

(73)

\displaystyle\Big[2.315\frac{p^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}-1.5652\frac{p^{4}}{\epsilon^{4}}\Big]\epsilon\Big[\ln(p+\epsilon)-\frac{1}{3}\Big],

\displaystyle f_{z}=\frac{0.433Z\Big(10e/\sqrt{Z}\Big)^{-1.106}}{\exp\Big[2.8975\Big(e/\sqrt{{Z}}\Big)^{-0.256}\Big]-1}.

(74)

where

\displaystyle c_{nr}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[0.5165,~0.3246e-3,~0.2391e-4,~0.5907,~0.3169].

Fig.
7
shows the result, with a total error less than 1% for
Z=1-35
[see Fig.
7
b]. If
f_{z}
is omitted, the max error for
Z=35
is still less than 10% [see Fig.
7
b].

It is possible to find a better treatment of
f_{z}
. The correction of the

effects other than

and

and within 2.5% for

\displaystyle W_{E,ei}=c_{0}\cdot f_{nr}+f_{r},

(75)

with

modified to

\displaystyle f_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1.215\frac{p^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}-2.3484\frac{p^{3}}{\epsilon^{3}}+

\displaystyle\Big[2.344\frac{p^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}-1.517\frac{p^{4}}{\epsilon^{4}}\Big]\epsilon\Big[\ln(p+\epsilon)-\frac{1}{3}\Big].

The above form could be sufficient for most usage in fusion plasma.

The above fitting can be reduced to the extreme relativistic (ER) case

\displaystyle W_{E}^{ER}(\epsilon)=6\epsilon\Big[\ln(2\epsilon)-\frac{1}{3}\Big].

(77)

4.2
Fitting for e-e

Define the normalized quantity in the center-of-mass system (C.M.S.) as

\displaystyle Q_{cm}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\alpha r_{e}^{2}}\int_{0}^{k_{\rm{max}}}k_{\rm{cm}}\frac{d\sigma}{dk_{\rm{cm}}}dk_{\rm{cm}},

(78)

we can obtain the total e-e Bremsstrahlung power
[

\displaystyle W_{ee}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n_{e}^{2}m_{e}c^{2}\alpha r_{e}^{2}}{2}\int\int\frac{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}}{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}[({\bm{p}}_{1}{\bm{p}}_{2})-1]}\cdot

\displaystyle f({\bm{p}}_{1})f({\bm{p}}_{2})Q_{cm}d^{3}p_{1}d^{3}p_{2},

where the momenta and energies
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},{\bm{p}}_{1},{\bm{p}}_{2}
are in any system
S
. We have

\displaystyle p=|{\bm{p}}_{1}-{\bm{p}}_{2}|,~~\epsilon=\sqrt{p^{2}+1}.

For Maxwellian electrons, the integral can reduce to
[

48

\displaystyle W_{ee}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4n_{e}^{2}c\alpha r_{e}^{2}}{t[K_{2}(1/t)]^{2}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\epsilon^{2}p^{2}K_{2}(2\epsilon/t)Q_{cm}(\epsilon)d\epsilon.

(80)

The
Q_{cm}(\epsilon)
can be obtained using the
d\sigma/dk
in
B
[for small energy, we use Eq.(

is the cross section which plays a central role in calculating the Bremsstrahlung power for arbitrary electron distributions, similar to the calculation of fusion reactivity
[
10

. Haug
[
29
,
45
]

The extreme relativistic (ER) case (

) is
[

]

\displaystyle Q_{cm}^{ER}(\epsilon)=16\epsilon\Big[\ln(2\epsilon)-\frac{1}{6}\Big],

(81)

and the nonrelativistic (

\displaystyle Q_{cm}^{NR}(\epsilon)=8p^{2}=8(\epsilon^{2}-1).

(82)

\displaystyle Q_{cm}(\epsilon)\simeq 8\frac{p^{2}}{\epsilon}\Big[1-\frac{4}{3}\frac{p}{\epsilon}+\frac{2}{3}\Big(2+\frac{p^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\Big)\ln(\epsilon+p)\Big],

The below new but still simple fitting is found to fit the exact

\displaystyle Q_{cm}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle 8\frac{p^{2}}{\epsilon}F_{ee}\Big\{1.007+2.11\frac{p}{\epsilon}-3.45\frac{p^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}+

(85)

For the with Elwert case at

5
Summary and Conclusion

This work provides more accurate analytical formulas for Bremsstrahlung radiation power calculation in fusion plasmas, with errors less than 1% for a wide energy range and high
Z
(e.g.,
Z=30
). For Maxwellian plasmas, the results are demonstrated using the Gaunt factor. For arbitrary distributions of electrons, accurate fitting formulas for the cross section are provided. The present fitting addresses some limitations of previous fittings. For example, the most recognized fitting by Svensson
[
14
]
is discontinuous at
t=1
and also inaccurate for
T_{e}\leq 20
keV. Most fittings are inaccurate for high
Z
and could be incorrect for
t\to 0
.

Here, we are only interested in the total power. To study the accurate spectrum, one can use the models in section
2
, or refer to Hoof
[
24
,
25
]
for
e
-
i
, and Nozawa
[
26
]
for
e
-
e
. Another widely used Bremsstrahlung study in fusion plasmas is for fast electron studies such as runaway electrons or wave heating electrons colliding with the background ions and electrons
[
49
]
.
Incorporating the Bremsstrahlung effect into simulation models is crucial for studying the intricate physics of high-energy density plasmas, particularly in particle simulation codes
[
50
]
.

One should also notice that, due to
g_{ei}
depending on
Z
, the commonly used
Z_{\rm eff}=\sum_{i}n_{i}Z_{i}^{2}/n_{e}
for
g=Z_{\rm eff}g_{ei}+g_{ee}
may not be rigorous. For accurate calculations of the total power for multi-ion plasmas, the term
[Zg_{ei}(t,Z)+g_{ee}(t)]
in Eq.(
61
) should be replaced by
[\sum_{i}Z_{i}^{2}\frac{n_{i}}{n_{e}}g_{ei}(t,Z_{i})+g_{ee}(t)]
.

It should be noted that here we only use the available theoretical models, which are believed to be accurate enough for practical usage. Even more accurate calculations (cf.
[
51
]
) could be extremely complicated due to the sum of Feynman-Dyson graphs
[
27
]
. Some experimental comparisons are also available (cf.
[
52
]
