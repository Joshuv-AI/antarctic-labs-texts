1.
Introduction and statement of results

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in\N
, the Sudler product at stage
N
is defined as

P_{N}(\alpha):=\prod_{r=1}^{N}2\left\lvert\sin\pi r\alpha\right\rvert.

Sudler products appear in many different areas of mathematics that include, among others, KAM theory
[
25
]
, restricted partition functions
[
38
]
, Padé approximants
[
31
]
, almost Mathieu operators
[
7
,
26
]
, and invariants of hyperbolic knots

[
1
,
2
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.

Writing
\|P_{N}\|_{\infty}=\sup\limits_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}P_{N}(\alpha)
, Erdős and Szekeres
[
16
]
claimed that the limit

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|P_{N}\|_{\infty}^{1/N}
exists and equals a value between
1
and
2
, without formally proving it. This was done by Sudler
[
38
]
and Wright
[
40
]
who showed that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|P_{N}\|_{\infty}^{1/N}=C\approx 1.22
.
Inspired by this, the order of growth of Sudler products was extensively examined from a metric point of view. For more results in this area, we refer
the reader to
[
6
,
8
,
9
,
13
,
15
,
17
,
27
,
28
]
.

The object of interest in this article is the pointwise behaviour of the Sudler product, which has also gained a lot of interest in recent years. Here,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is fixed and the behaviour of

P_{N}(\alpha)
when
N\to\infty
is examined. Observe that by
1
-periodicity and the fact that for
\alpha=\frac{p}{q}\in\Q
we have
P_{N}(\alpha)=0
for
N\geqslant q
, the asymptotic analysis can be restricted to irrational numbers
\alpha\in[0,1)
.
A central object in this setting is given by the question of characterizing the set

S:=\left\{\alpha\in[0,1):\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)>0\right\}.

First studied by Erdős and Szekeres
[
16
]
,
it was proven that
S
forms a set of Lebesgue measure
0
. This was improved by Lubinksy
[
30
]
who was the first to develop a connection between the asymptotic behaviour of the Sudler product and the Diophantine properties of
\alpha
. Thereby, he showed a certain rate of convergence of
\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)
to
0
for almost every
\alpha
; for further results for almost all irrationals see e.g.
[
14
,
21
]
. Furthermore, Lubinsky proved that
S
is contained in the set of badly approximable numbers.
By a careful analysis of Lubinsky’s proof, it was recovered in
[
18
]
that there is actually a finite cutoff value
K\approx e^{800}
such that (when writing
\alpha=[0;a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]
in its continued fraction expansion),

S\subseteq\tilde{E}_{K}:=\{\alpha\in[0,1):\limsup_{i\to\infty}a_{i}(\alpha)\leqslant K\}.

Lubinsky conjectured that
S
is actually empty, which was disproven by Verschueren
[
39
]
and independently Grepstad, Kaltenböck and Neumüller
[
19
]
, as they
proved that the Golden Ratio
\phi=[0;1,1,1,\ldots]\in S
.

This result was generalized by Aistleitner, Technau, and Zafeiropoulos
[
4
]
who showed that for
\beta(b):=[0;b,b,b,\ldots]
, we have

\beta(b)\in S\Leftrightarrow b\leqslant 5.

At the end of the article, they raised the question of characterizing those irrationals whose Sudler product grows at most linearly. This is known to be equivalent to characterizing
S
since it can be deduced from the work of Aistleitner and Borda
[
1
]
that for any irrational
\alpha
,

(1)

\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{P_{N}(\alpha)}{N}=\infty.

In recent articles, significant progress was made in providing sufficient conditions under which an irrational does
not
lie in
S
. Grepstad, Neumüller and Zafeiropoulus
[
20
]
showed that
\alpha\notin S
if
\alpha
is a quadratic irrational with
\limsup_{n\to\infty}a_{n}\geqslant 23
, conjecturing that already

\limsup_{n\to\infty}a_{n}\geqslant 6
suffices. This turned out to be false in a recent paper of the second author
[
23
]
: generalizing the method of proof to arbitrary badly approximable numbers, the value
23
was decreased to the optimal bound, showing that

(2)

S\subseteq\tilde{E}_{6},\quad S\not\subseteq\tilde{E}_{5},

where

\tilde{E}_{k}:=\{\alpha\in[0,1):\limsup_{i\to\infty}a_{i}(\alpha)\leqslant k\}.

The current article aims to enlarge the set of known irrationals
\alpha
where

\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)>0
holds. Note that up to now, there is no single non-quadratic irrational known to lie in
S
. This is due to the fact that all proofs given so far heavily use the existence of periodicity in the continued fraction expansion of quadratic irrationals. However, based on numerical evidence, it was conjectured in
[
23
]
that

\tilde{E}_{3}\subseteq S
. As a main result of this paper, we confirm this conjecture:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\alpha
be an irrational number with its continued fraction expansion

\alpha=[0;a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]
satisfying
\limsup_{i\to\infty}a_{i}\leqslant 3
.
Then we have

\liminf\limits_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)>0,\quad\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{P_{N}(\alpha)}{N}<\infty.

As an immediate corollary of Theorem
1
, we obtain non-trivial (upper and lower) Hausdorff dimension estimates for the set
S
:

Theorem 2
.

Let
S:=\left\{\alpha\in[0,1):\liminf\limits_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)>0,\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{P_{N}(\alpha)}{N}<\infty\right\}.

Then we have

0.7056\leqslant\dim_{H}(S)\leqslant 0.8677.

Proof of Theorem
2
, assuming Theorem
1
.

By Theorem
1
and (
2
), we have

\tilde{E}_{3}\subseteq S\subseteq\tilde{E}_{6}
.
By standard arguments (taking a countable union of sets of the same Hausdorff dimension), we obtain

\dim_{H}(\tilde{E}_{k})=\dim_{H}(E_{k})

where

E_{k}:=\{\alpha\in[0,1):\max_{i\in\N}a_{i}(\alpha)\leqslant k\}.

Using estimates on
\dim_{H}(E_{k})
(see e.g.
[
24
, Table 1]
and
[
34
, Theorem 4.22]
), we obtain

0.7056\leqslant\dim_{H}(E_{3})\leqslant\dim_{H}(S)\leqslant\dim_{H}(\tilde{E}_{6})\leqslant 0.8677.

∎

Here and in preceding results on the characterization of
S
, it seems that the question of
\alpha\in S
does not depend on its first finitely many partial quotients, or in other words,
that
S
is invariant under the Gauss map
T(x):=\left\{\frac{1}{x}\right\}
.
However, to the best knowledge of the authors, this was never proven explicitly, thus we provide a proof of this fact.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
arbitrary. Then

\alpha\in S\Leftrightarrow T^{n}(\alpha)\in S.

Remarks on the Theorems and directions for further research

•

Since all the elements in
S
known so far were quadratic irrationals,
S
was not even known to be uncountable.
Actually, by considering the (in view of Theorem
3
) natural equivalence relation given by
\alpha\sim\beta\iff T^{m}(\alpha)=T^{n}(\beta),m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
there were only seven examples (see
[
4
,
23
]
) known to lie in
S
.
Hence, Theorem
2
is a significant improvement to the question raised in
[
4
]
by not only showing uncountability but giving nontrivial estimates on the Hausdorff dimension.

•

In terms of the value of
K(\alpha):=\limsup_{i\to\infty}a_{i}
, Theorem
1
provides a sharp threshold, thus completing the best possible description of
\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)
in terms of
K(\alpha)
: If
K(\alpha)\leqslant 3
, we have
\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)>0
, if
K(\alpha)\geqslant 7
, we have
\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)=0
. If

K(\alpha)\in\{4,5,6\}
, the value of
K(\alpha)
does not determine whether
\alpha\in S
: for each value
4,5,6
, there exist irrationals
\alpha,\beta
with
K(\alpha)=K(\beta)
with
\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)>0
and
\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\beta)=0
(for examples, see
[
4
]
and
[
23
]
) .

•

For integers
u\geqslant 2
, the numbers
\alpha_{u}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u^{2^{k}-1}}

are known to be transcendental, with their partial quotients bounded by
u
.
These numbers are known as examples of badly approximable numbers that are not quadratic irrationals,
but their corresponding continued fraction expansions can still be described by a relatively simple pattern, see
[
37
]
.
The second-named author was asked by J. Shallit in private communication whether
something can be said about the question for which
u
,
\alpha_{u}\in S
. By Theorem
1
we know that
\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in S
and by (
2
),
\alpha_{u}\notin S
for
u\geqslant 7
. Again, the question of whether
\alpha_{u}\in S
for
u\in\{4,5,6\}
remains open, but the question might be easier to approach than proving statements for arbitrary non-quadratic badly approximable numbers since the continued fraction expansion follows a particular pattern.

•

Most probably, both the upper and lower bounds for the Hausdorff dimension can be improved by considering different criteria when
K(\alpha)\in\{4,5,6\}
. While some patterns like
(1,6,1)
appearing infinitely often in the continued fraction expansion probably lead to
\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)=0
, the approach considered in this paper has its limitations when it comes to determining the
precise
Hausdorff dimension, let alone complete characterization for the set
S
. To determine this, we expect a genuinely new approach to be needed.

•

In this paper we prove a
pointwise
lower bound for
P_{N}(\alpha)
when
\alpha\in\tilde{E}_{3}
. Having particularly large numbers in the first finitely many partial quotients seems to decrease the value of
\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)
more and more, hence most probably,

\inf_{\alpha\in\tilde{E}_{3}}\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)=0.

However,
we actually show a
uniform
lower bound for
E_{3}
, i.e.

(3)

\inf_{\alpha\in E_{3}}\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)=c>0.

It would be interesting to determine the value of
c
or to characterize a minimizing
subsequence along which the
\liminf
is obtained for fixed
\alpha
. In
[
22
]
, the actual value of
\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}([0;1,1,1,\ldots])
was determined,
with a minimizing subsequence being the Fibonacci numbers, that is, in the corresponding Ostrowski expansion (see Section
2
), we only have one non-zero Ostrowski digit. The method of proof reveals that for any
\alpha\in E_{3}
, every minimizing subsequence can only have boundedly many non-zero Ostrowski digits, but the particular structure of a minimizing subsequence remains unclear in the more complicated setting of arbitrary badly approximable numbers.

•

From a technical perspective,
there are two obstacles to circumvent in order to prove Theorem
1
. Firstly, showing
\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)=0
is much easier than proving
\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\alpha)>0
since it suffices to consider a suitable subsequence, instead of proving a uniform positive lower bound for
all
indices. For this, our method follows a refined version of the ideas established in
[
4
]
for quadratic irrationals.
Secondly, analyzing arbitrary badly approximable numbers instead of quadratic irrationals is a tougher challenge since there is no periodicity to exploit. For this, we borrow ideas from
[
23
]
. The proof of Theorem
1
combines these two methods, together with new ideas and an extensive case distinction.

In the subsequent proofs, we will make use of several elementary properties.
Recall that a function

We recall the following well-known facts:

•

If

•

Products of log-concave functions are log-concave.

Any real number

The convergents of a continued fraction are the rational numbers

p_{0}=a_{0},\;p_{1}=a_{1}a_{0}+1,\;q_{0}=1,\;q_{1}=a_{1}

p_{k+1}(\alpha)=a_{k+1}(\alpha)p_{k}(\alpha)+p_{k-1}(\alpha),\ \ q_{k+1}(\alpha)=a_{k+1}(\alpha)q_{k}(\alpha)+q_{k-1}(\alpha).

Throughout this article, we will omit the dependency of

Perturbed Sudler products and convergence of the Sudler product along subsequences

The proof of Theorem
1
relies on a decomposition approach that was implicitly used in
[
19
]
to prove
\liminf\limits_{N\to\infty}P_{N}(\phi)>0
, and more explicitly in even more recent articles such as
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. The Sudler product
P_{N}(\alpha)
is
decomposed into a finite product with factors of the form

P_{q_{n}}(\alpha,\varepsilon):=\prod_{r=1}^{q_{n}}2\left\lvert\sin\Big(\pi\Big(r\alpha+(-1)^{n}\frac{\varepsilon}{q_{n}}\Big)\Big)\right\rvert,\quad\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}

We state the fact in the formulation of
[
23
, Proposition 4]
:

Proposition 4
.

Let
\alpha
be a fixed irrational and let
N=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}q_{i}(\alpha)
be the Ostrowski expansion of an arbitrary integer
q_{n}\leqslant N<q_{n+1}
.
For
0\leqslant i\leqslant n
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

\varepsilon_{i,t}(N)=\varepsilon_{i,t}(\alpha,N):=q_{i}\left(t\delta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-i}(-1)^{j}b_{i+j}\delta_{i+j}\right).

Then we have

P_{N}(\alpha)=\prod_{i=0}^{n}\prod_{t=0}^{b_{i}-1}P_{q_{i}}\bigl(\alpha,\varepsilon_{i,t}(N)\bigr).

Since the set
\{n\alpha\}_{n=1}^{q_{k}}
is (for badly approximable
\alpha
) very regularly distributed in the unit interval, the Sudler product at stage
q_{k}
and its perturbed variant are much easier to control than the Sudler product at an arbitrary stage
N
. The first result in this direction was found by Mestel and Verschueren
[
32
]
who proved that along the Fibonacci sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\N}
(which are the convergents’ denominators of the Golden Ratio), the limit
\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{F_{n}}(\phi)
exists and equals
C\approx 2.407
. This convergence phenomenon was extended in
[
4
]
, showing that for
\beta(b)=[0;\overline{b}]
, we have

(13)

\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{q_{n}(\beta)}(\beta,\varepsilon)=G_{\beta}(\varepsilon),

where
G_{\beta}
is a continuous function that can be described in a closed expression. Furthermore, on intervals that are bounded away from the zeros of
G_{\beta}
, the function is even
C^{\infty}
and strictly log-concave. The convergence in (
13
) is locally uniform and even a convergence rate was established, see
[
1
,
22
]
.

The statement (
13
) was generalized in
[
20
]
to arbitrary quadratic irrationals: Given a period length
p
, the convergence of
P_{q_{np+m}}(\alpha,\varepsilon)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}G_{m,\alpha}(\varepsilon),\ m=0,\ldots,p-1
was proven, with
G_{m,\alpha}
having the same properties as
G_{\beta}

Construction of
f_{\bm{c},\infty}
.

Next, we construct the function
f_{\bm{c},\infty}
satisfying

(38)

\prod_{n=n_{0}+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}h_{n,k}(\alpha,\varepsilon)\geqslant f_{\bm{c},\infty}(\varepsilon)-o(1),\quad\varepsilon\in(\varepsilon_{\bm{c}}^{\min},\varepsilon_{\bm{c}}^{\max}).

The function is defined by

f_{\bm{c},\infty}(\varepsilon)=1-\Biggl(2\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max}\biggl(W_{\bm{c}}(n_{0},T,m)+\frac{9}{2}\frac{1+\log{T}}{T}\biggr)+\frac{1}{n_{0}}\biggl(\frac{(\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max})^{2}}{4}+(\varepsilon+0.5\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max})^{2}+E^{2}_{\bm{c}}(n_{0},\varepsilon)\biggr)\Biggr).

The value
W_{\bm{c}}(n_{0},T,m)
and the function
E^{2}_{\bm{c}}(n_{0},\varepsilon)
will be defined in this subsection.

Note that for fixed
k,\alpha,\varepsilon
, the sequence
h_{n,k}(\alpha,\varepsilon)
tends to
1
as
n
grows. That is why it is convenient to analyze the logarithm of the left-hand side of (
38
) which replaces the product with a sum. We use the following lemma
[
22
, Lemma 9]
, which follows from Taylor approximation of the logarithm.

Proposition 12
.

Let
x_{n}
be a finite sequence of real numbers that satisfy
|x_{n}|<\frac{1}{2}
and
|x_{n}|<\frac{c}{n}
for some
c>0
. Then

\prod\limits_{n=N}^{M}(1-x_{n})\geqslant 1-\Biggl(\Bigl|\sum\limits_{n=N}^{M}x_{n}\Bigr|+\frac{c^{2}}{N-1}\Biggr).

Lemma 13
.

Let
\alpha\in E_{3}
and
T\geqslant n_{0}+1\geqslant 11
. Defining

S_{\ell}(\alpha):=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\ell}1/2-\{n\alpha\},\ell\in\N,
we have

(39)

\prod_{n=n_{0}+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}h_{n,k}(\alpha,\varepsilon)\geqslant 1-\Biggl(2\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max}\left(\left|\sum_{\ell=n_{0}+1}^{T}\frac{S_{\ell}(\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k})}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right|+\frac{9}{2}\frac{1+\log{T}}{T}\right)+\frac{1}{n_{0}}\biggl(\frac{(\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max})^{2}}{4}+(\varepsilon+0.5\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max})^{2}+E^{2}_{\bm{c}}(n_{0},\varepsilon)\biggr)\Biggr)-o(1)

where

E_{\bm{c}}(n_{0},\varepsilon):=\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max}+\frac{\varepsilon\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max}+\varepsilon^{2}}{n_{0}+1}.

Proof.

One can see that for
n\geqslant n_{0}+1
, we have

\lvert 1-h_{n,k}(\varepsilon)\rvert\leqslant\frac{1}{n_{0}+1}\left(\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max}+\frac{\varepsilon\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max}+\varepsilon^{2}}{n_{0}+1}\right)+o(1)=\frac{E_{\bm{c}}(n_{0},\varepsilon)}{n_{0}+1}+o(1),\quad k\to\infty.

Hence, an application of Proposition
12
gives

\displaystyle\prod_{n=n_{0}+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}h_{n,k}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\Biggl(\Bigg\lvert\sum\limits_{n=n_{0}+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}\Biggl(\bigg(1-\lambda_{k}\frac{\{n\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k}\}-\frac{1}{2}}{n}\bigg)^{2}-\frac{\left(\varepsilon+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\right)^{2}}{n^{2}}-1\Biggr)\Bigg\rvert+\frac{E^{2}_{\bm{c}}(n_{0},\varepsilon)}{n_{0}}\Biggr)-o(1)

(40)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\Biggl(2\lambda_{k}\Bigg\lvert\sum\limits_{n=n_{0}+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}\frac{\{n\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k}\}-\frac{1}{2}}{n}\Bigg\rvert+\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{4}\sum\limits_{n=n_{0}+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}\frac{1}{n^{2}}+(\varepsilon+\lambda_{k}/2)^{2}\sum\limits_{n=n_{0}+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}\frac{1}{n^{2}}+\frac{E^{2}_{\bm{c}}(n_{0},\varepsilon)}{n_{0}}\Biggr)-o(1).

If any term of (
40
) is unbounded, then (
39
) holds by the trivial estimate
\prod_{n=n_{0}+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}h_{n,k}(\alpha,\varepsilon)\geqslant 0
. If all terms in (
40
) are bounded we use
\lambda_{k}\leqslant\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max}+o(1)
to obtain

\prod_{n=n_{0}+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}h_{n,k}(\alpha,\varepsilon)\geqslant 1-\Biggl(2\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max}\Bigg\lvert\sum\limits_{n=n_{0}+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}\frac{\frac{1}{2}-\{n\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k}\}}{n}\Bigg\rvert+\frac{1}{n_{0}}\biggl(\frac{(\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max})^{2}}{4}+(\varepsilon+0.5\lambda_{\bm{c}}^{\max})^{2}+E^{2}_{\bm{c}}(n_{0},\varepsilon)\biggr)\Biggr)-o(1).

Therefore, we are left to show that

\left|\sum_{n=n_{0}+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}\frac{\frac{1}{2}-\left\{n\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k}\right\}}{n}\right|\leqslant\left|\sum_{\ell=n_{0}+1}^{T}\frac{S_{\ell}(\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k})}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right|+\frac{9}{2}\frac{1+\log{T}}{T}+o(1),\quad k\to\infty.

Applying summation by parts,
we obtain

\begin{split}\left|\sum_{n=n_{0}+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}\frac{\frac{1}{2}-\left\{n\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k}\right\}}{n}\right|&\leqslant\left|\sum_{\ell=n_{0}+1}^{T}\frac{S_{\ell}(\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k})}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right|+\frac{|S_{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}|}{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}+\sum_{\ell=T+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}\frac{\lvert S_{\ell}(\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k})\rvert}{\ell^{2}}.\end{split}

We write
\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k}=[0;b_{1}^{(k)},b_{2}^{(k)},\ldots]
with convergents

\frac{p_{i}^{(k)}}{q_{i}^{(k)}}
. Since

, we have

.
Thus Ostrowski’s estimate (
12
) gives for any

that

\lvert S_{\ell}(\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k})\rvert\leqslant\frac{9}{2}\log\ell,

hence we obtain

(41)

\sum_{\ell=T+1}^{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}\frac{\lvert S_{\ell}(\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k})\rvert}{\ell^{2}}\leqslant\frac{9}{2}\sum_{\ell=T+1}^{\infty}\frac{\log\ell}{\ell^{2}}\leqslant\frac{9}{2}\frac{1+\log{T}}{T}.

Since
\alpha
is badly approximable, another application of (

\frac{|S_{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}|}{\lfloor q_{k}/2\rfloor}=O_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\log(q_{k})}{q_{k}}\right)=o(1),\quad k\to\infty.

We use an approach similar to the one established in

One can easily see that if

(43)

\left|\sum_{\ell=n_{0}+1}^{T}\frac{S_{\ell}(\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle\alpha\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle\alpha\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{k})}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right|\leqslant\max\left(\left|\sum_{\ell=n_{0}+1}^{T}\frac{w_{\min}(\ell,x,y)}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right|,\left|\sum_{\ell=n_{0}+1}^{T}\frac{w_{\max}(\ell,x,y)}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right|\right)=:w_{est}(n_{0},T,x,y).
