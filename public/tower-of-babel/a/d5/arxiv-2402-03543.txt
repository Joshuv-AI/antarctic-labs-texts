Polynomial Lawvere Logic
Bacci Giorgio, Radu Mardare, Prakash Panangaden, Gordon Plotkin

Abstract
We study Polynomial Lawvere logic ( ℙ ​ 𝕃 \mathbb{PL} ), a logic defined over the Lawvere quantale of extended positive reals with sum as tensor, to which we add multiplication, thereby obtaining a semiring structure. ℙ ​ 𝕃 \mathbb{PL} is designed for complex quantitative reasoning, allowing judgements that express inequalities between polynomials on the extended positive reals. We introduce a deduction system and demonstrate its expressiveness by deriving a classical result from probability theory relating the Kantorovich and the total variation distances. Although the deductive system is not complete in general, we achieve completeness for finitely axiomatizable theories. The proof of completeness relies on the Krivine-Stengle Positivstellensatz (a variant of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz). Additionally, we provide new complexity results, both for ℙ ​ 𝕃 \mathbb{PL} and its affine fragment 𝔸 ​ 𝕃 \mathbb{AL} , regarding two decision problems: satisfiability of a set of judgements and semantical consequence from a set of judgements. The former is NP-complete in 𝔸 ​ 𝕃 \mathbb{AL} and in PSPACE for ℙ ​ 𝕃 \mathbb{PL} ; the latter is co-NP complete in 𝔸 ​ 𝕃 \mathbb{AL} and in PSPACE for ℙ ​ 𝕃 \mathbb{PL} .

Preliminaries and Notation

A
quantale
is a complete lattice with a binary, associative operation
\oplus
(
tensor
), such that for every element
a
, both
a\oplus-
and
-\oplus a
have right adjoints (equivalently,
\oplus
preserves all joins).
A quantale is
commutative
whenever its tensor is; and
unital
if there
is an element
u
(
unit
) s.t.
u\oplus a=a=a\oplus u
, for all
a
; when the unit is the top element, the quantale is
integral
.
For commutative quantales we denote the right adjoint to
a\oplus-
by
a\multimap-
. Hence,
a\oplus b\leq c\Longleftrightarrow b\leq a\multimap c
.

Commutative integral quantales are
(i) the
Boolean quantale
with logical conjunction as tensor;
(ii) the
Łukasiewicz quantale
on
[0,1]
with truncated addition as tensor; and (iii) the
Lawvere quantale
over the complete lattice of the extended positive reals
[0,\infty]
taken with reverse order
and with sum as tensor. The first two have been used
for interpreting Boolean and Łukasiewicz logics
[
38
]
; the third one to interpret Lawvere logics
[
10
]
.

In this paper, we work with sum
+
and multiplication
\cdot
on
[0,\infty]
as defined in Table

. They form a semiring, i.e., two commutative monoids with
0
and
1
as their identity elements, with multiplication distributing over the sum, and

we would obtain a multiplication that preserves all joins w.r.t. the inverse order, but we would not obtain a semiring. If we worked with the usual order on the reals we would obtain a semiring in the category of complete lattices, but we would no longer be working with Lawvere’s quantale. So, while no choice is perfect, ours maintains a connection with Lawvere’s quantale and we do have a semiring. Having a semiring is particularly useful as it enables us to encode examples from measure theory (Section

3
Polynomial Lawvere Logic

In this section, we introduce
Polynomial Lawvere logic
(
\mathbb{PL}
), a propositional logic interpreted over the semiring. It extends Affine Lawvere logic (
\mathbb{AL}
)
[
10
]
by allowing the multiplication of formulas, thereby enabling the encoding of polynomial functions over the positive extended reals. As a result,
\mathbb{PL}
offers a more expressive logic, capable of handling more complex quantitative reasoning.

Syntax.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be a set of propositional variables. The formulas of
\mathbb{PL}
are freely generated by the following grammar.

\displaystyle\phi,\psi::=\bot\mid x\mid r\mid\phi\oplus\psi\mid\phi\multimap\psi\mid\phi\psi

(for
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
r\in[0,\infty)
)

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
\phi^{n}
inductively as

\phi^{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=1
and
\phi^{(n+1)}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\phi\phi^{n}
.
Moreover, from the quantale connectives, one can derive the usual logical connectives

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\top\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\bot\multimap\bot\,,\\
&\lnot\phi\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\phi\multimap\bot\,,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\phi\land\psi\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\phi\oplus(\phi\multimap\psi)\,,\\
&\phi\lor\psi\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=((\psi\multimap\phi)\multimap\phi)\land((\phi\multimap\psi)\multimap\psi)\,,\\
&\phi\multimapboth\psi\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\phi\multimap\psi)\land(\psi\multimap\phi)\,.\end{aligned}

Let
\phi[\psi/x]
be the result of substituting the variable
x\in\mathcal{X}
for the formula
\psi
in
\phi
. This notation extends canonically to lists and sets of formulas.

We assume all binary operators are left-associative and follow an
operator precedence rule
: multiplication has the highest precedence, followed by
\lnot
, then
\oplus
, next
\land
and
\lor
, and finally
\multimap
and
\multimapboth
with the lowest precedence.
Thus, the formula
\theta\phi\oplus\psi\land\lnot\theta\psi\multimap\theta
is interpreted as
(((\theta\phi)\oplus\psi)\land(\lnot(\theta\psi)))\multimap\theta

Semantics.
The models are maps
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty]
interpreting the
propositional variables in our semiring, extended to all formulas as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{M}(\bot)&\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\infty\,,\\
\mathcal{M}(r)&\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=r\,,\\
\mathcal{M}(\phi\psi)&\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{M}(\phi)\mathcal{M}(\psi)\,,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{M}(\phi\oplus\psi)&\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{M}(\phi)+\mathcal{M}(\psi)\,,\\
\mathcal{M}(\phi\multimap\psi)&\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{M}(\psi)\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}\mathcal{M}(\phi)\,,\end{aligned}

Consequently, the derived connectives are interpreted as follows.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{M}(\top)&=0\,,\\
\mathcal{M}(\lnot\phi)&=\infty-\mathcal{M}(\phi)\,,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{M}(\phi\land\psi)&=\max\{\mathcal{M}(\psi),\mathcal{M}(\phi)\}\,,\\
\mathcal{M}(\phi\lor\psi)&=\min\{\mathcal{M}(\psi),\mathcal{M}(\phi)\}\,,\\
\mathcal{M}(\phi\multimapboth\psi)&=|\mathcal{M}(\phi)-\mathcal{M}(\psi)|\,.\end{aligned}

Affine Lawvere Logic
(
\mathbb{AL}
), introduced in
[
10
]
, follows the grammar below and shares the same semantics as
\mathbb{PL}
, appropriately restricted.

\displaystyle\phi,\psi::=\bot\mid x\mid r\mid\phi\oplus\psi\mid\phi\multimap\psi\mid r\psi

(for
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
r\in[0,\infty)
)

Boolean formulas.
Observe that while, in general, a model evaluates a formula to any value in
[0,\infty]
, formulas such as
\lnot\phi
or
\phi\bot
can either be evaluated to
0
(“true”) or to
\infty
(“false”). For instance,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{M}(\lnot\phi)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\mathcal{M}(\phi)$ is infinite}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{M}(\lnot\lnot\phi)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\mathcal{M}(\phi)$ is finite}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}\end{aligned}

We call such formulas
Boolean
, and they define more useful derived operators.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\phi=\psi\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\phi\multimapboth\psi)\bot\,,\\
&\phi\neq\psi\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\lnot(\phi\multimapboth\psi)\bot\,,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\phi\geq\psi\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\phi\multimap\psi)\bot\,,\\
&\phi>\psi\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\lnot(\psi\multimap\phi)\bot\,,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &|\phi|\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\bot>\phi\,.\end{aligned}

These have the expected “Boolean” meaning:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{M}(\phi=\psi)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\mathcal{M}(\phi)=\mathcal{M}(\psi)$}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}\\
\mathcal{M}(\phi\neq\psi)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\mathcal{M}(\phi)\neq\mathcal{M}(\psi)$}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{M}(\phi>\psi)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\mathcal{M}(\phi)>\mathcal{M}(\psi)$}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}\\
\mathcal{M}(\phi\geq\psi)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\mathcal{M}(\phi)\geq\mathcal{M}(\psi)$}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}\end{aligned}

Using them, we can express useful facts about our models,
e.g.
,
|\phi|
says that “
\phi
is finite
” and
\phi>0
that “
\phi
is strictly positive
”.

Hereafter, we use
\phi\leq\psi
and
\phi<\psi
as synonyms for
\psi\geq\phi
and
\psi>\phi
respectively. Also, concatenation of these formulas are used to express their conjunction: for example,
\phi\leq\psi<\theta
means
(\phi\leq\psi)\land(\psi<\rho)
.

Judgements.
A
judgement
in
\mathbb{PL}
is a syntactic construct of the form

\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\vdash\psi\,,

(Judgement)

where
\phi_{i}
,
\psi
are logical formulas. The antecedent
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}
of a judgement is a finite ordered list, possibly, with repetitions.
As customary, for
\Gamma
and
\Delta
lists of formulas, their comma-separated juxtaposition
\Gamma,\Delta
denotes concatenation; and
\vdash\phi
is the notation for a judgement with empty list of antecedents.

A judgement
\Gamma\vdash\psi

is satisfied by
a model
\mathcal{M}
, denoted
\Gamma\models_{\mathcal{M}}\psi
, whenever

\textstyle\sum_{\phi\in\Gamma}\mathcal{M}(\phi)\geq\mathcal{M}(\psi)\,.

(Semantics of judgements)

A judgement is
satisfiable
if it is satisfied by a model;
unsatisfiable
if it is not satisfiable; and a
tautology
if it is satisfied by all models.

In particular,
\vdash\phi\multimap\phi
,
\vdash\top
, and

\vdash\lnot\lnot\phi\multimapboth(\bot>\phi)
are examples of tautologies, while
\vdash\phi\multimapboth(\lnot\lnot\phi)

is not.

Note the distinction between
\phi\multimap\psi
and the Boolean formula
\phi\geq\psi
: while for all models
\mathcal{M}
, we have
\models_{\mathcal{M}}\phi\multimap\psi
iff
\models_{\mathcal{M}}\phi\geq\psi
, it may not hold that
\mathcal{M}(\phi\multimap\psi)=\mathcal{M}(\phi\geq\psi)
, as
\mathcal{M}(\phi\multimap\psi)
could be a non-zero finite number.

Definition 1
(Semantic Consequence)

A judgement
\gamma
is a
semantic consequence
of a set
S
of judgements, in symbols
S\models\gamma
,
if every model that satisfies all the judgements in
S
also satisfies
\gamma
.

S
be a set of judgements.
We say that a judgement
\gamma
is
provable from

(or
deducible from
)
S
, if there exists a
sequence
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}
of judgements
ending in
\gamma
whose members are either
members of
S
, or each follows from some preceding members of the sequence by using the inference rules of the deduction system.
A sequence
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}
as above is called
proof
.

In what follows we will (safely) abuse notation: if
\gamma
is a judgement,
S
a set of judgements and
\mathcal{M}
a model, we write

\frac{\;S\;}{\gamma}
, if
\gamma
is provable from
S
;

\mathcal{M}\models S
if
\mathcal{M}
is a model of all judgements in
S
. If
S=\{\gamma\}
, we write
\mathcal{M}\models\gamma
.

Theorem 4.1
(Soundness)

If a judgement
\gamma
is provable from
S
in
\mathbb{PL}
, then
\gamma
is a semantic consequence of
S
. In symbols:

\displaystyle\frac{\;S\;}{\gamma}

implies

\displaystyle S\models\gamma\,.

Any judgement
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\vdash\psi
is provably equivalent to
\phi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\phi_{n}\vdash\psi
. Moreover, any judgement of type
\phi\vdash\psi
is provably equivalent to
\vdash\phi\multimap\psi
. Hence, without loss of generality, we may assume that arbitrary judgements in
\mathbb{PL}
are of the form
\vdash\theta
, for some formula
\theta
.

In
[
10
]
it is shown that
\mathbb{AL}
does not enjoy a deduction theorem, not even in the weak form that holds for fuzzy logics, such as Łukasiewicz, Gödel, or product logics. This is because we have proven that in
\mathbb{AL}
it is not possible to “internalize” provability in the language of the logic. However, in
\mathbb{PL}
, the expressivity provided by multiplication allows us to do it by “Booleanizing” the judgements.

Theorem 4.2
(Deduction Theorem)

For any formulas
\phi,\psi
in
\mathbb{PL}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\;\vdash\phi\;}{\vdash\psi}

iff

\displaystyle\frac{\;\vdash 0\geq\phi\;}{\vdash 0\geq\psi}

iff

\displaystyle\vdash(0\geq\phi)\multimap(0\geq\psi)\,.

Total Variation.
The total variation distance between

\displaystyle\vdash K_{\mu,\nu}\multimapboth\bigoplus_{i,j}W_{ij}d_{ij}\,.

(1)

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{i,j}W_{ij}d_{ij}\oplus\bigoplus_{i}F_{i}\mu_{i}\vdash\bigoplus_{j}F_{j}\nu_{j}\,,

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{i,j}W_{ij}d_{ij}\oplus\bigoplus_{j}F_{j}\nu_{j}\vdash\bigoplus_{i}F_{i}\mu_{i}

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{i,j}W_{ij}d_{ij}\oplus\bigoplus_{i}F_{i}\mu_{i}

\displaystyle\vdash\bigoplus_{i,j}W_{ij}d_{ij}\oplus\bigoplus_{i}F_{i}(\bigoplus_{j}W_{ij}))

Quantitative Equational Logic (QEL).

Already in
[

]
we have shown how one can embed QEL in

Since

Canonical & Polynomial Forms

We define canonical forms for
\mathbb{PL}
formulas and their judgements and provide
a method for reducing judgements to their canonical form. The canonicalisation of judgements is a crucial ingredient for our proof of completeness (Section
7
) and also an essential tool for establishing the complexity results in
Section
8
.

Definition 3
(Canonical Forms)

A formula is in
canonical form
(CF) if it is either
\bot
or in
proper canonical
form
(PCF),
i.e.
, a formula generated by

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{::}}=x\mid r\mid\theta\oplus\theta\mid\theta\multimap\theta\mid\theta\theta\,

\displaystyle\text{for $x\in\mathcal{X}$ and $r\in[0,\infty)$}\,.

A formula in PCF is in
polymomial form
when it has no occurrences of
\multimap
.

Formulas in PCF are closed under conjunction, disjunction and double implication, but not negation. Observe that
\bot
does not occur in formulas in PCF.

Canonical forms are extended to judgements in the obvious way:
\phi\vdash\psi
is
in a certain form (
e.g.
, CF, PCF, or polynomial) if both
\phi
and
\psi
are in that form.

Provable Equalities between Polynomial Formulas.

By identifying formulas up to commutativity-associativity of
\oplus
and multiplication (and alpha-conversion), we have that polynomial formulas on
n
-variables are in 1-1 correspondence with polynomials with positive coefficients in
n
-variables. For a polynomial formula
\theta
with variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, the corresponding polynomial
\llbracket\theta\rrbracket\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as follows, for
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \llbracket x_{i}\rrbracket(v)&=v_{i}\,,\\
\llbracket r\rrbracket(v)&=r\,,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \llbracket\theta\otimes\vartheta\rrbracket(v)&=\llbracket\theta\rrbracket(v)+\llbracket\vartheta\rrbracket(v)\,,\\
\llbracket\theta\vartheta\rrbracket(v)&=\llbracket\theta\rrbracket(v)\llbracket\vartheta\rrbracket(v)\,.\end{aligned}

It is not difficult to prove, inductively, that

\mathcal{M}(\theta)=\llbracket\theta\rrbracket(\mathcal{M}(x_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{M}(x_{n}))
, for all positive real-valued models
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty)
.

Lemma 1

Let
\theta,\vartheta
be in polynomial form.
\llbracket\theta\rrbracket=\llbracket\vartheta\rrbracket
iff
{\vdash\theta\multimapboth\vartheta}
is provable.

The following corollary allows us to reason about provable equalities between non-polynomial formulas in terms of equalities over
polynomial formulas.

Corollary 1

Let
\theta
,
\vartheta
be polynomial formulas such that
\llbracket\theta\rrbracket=\llbracket\vartheta\rrbracket
,

\phi
,
\psi
two formulas and
S
a finite set of judgements in
\mathbb{PL}
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{S}{\phi\multimapboth\psi\vdash\theta\multimapboth\vartheta}\,\;\text{ and }\;\,\frac{S}{\theta\multimapboth\vartheta\vdash\phi\multimapboth\psi}

implies

\displaystyle\frac{S}{\vdash\phi\multimapboth\psi}\,.

Canonicalisation.

For a formula
\phi
in
\mathbb{PL}
, we define its canonical form
\phi^{\sf cf}
, by induction on the formula as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} x^{\sf cf}=x&&\bot^{\sf cf}=\bot&&r^{\sf cf}=r\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\phi\oplus\psi)^{\sf cf}&=\begin{cases}\bot&\text{if $\phi^{\sf cf}=\bot$ or $\psi^{\sf cf}=\bot$}\\
\phi^{\sf cf}\oplus\psi^{\sf cf}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\\
(\phi\multimap\psi)^{\sf cf}&=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\phi^{\sf cf}=\bot$}\\
\bot&\text{if $\phi^{\sf cf}\neq\bot$ and $\psi^{\sf cf}=\bot$}\\
\phi^{\sf cf}\multimap\psi^{\sf cf}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\\
(\phi\psi)^{\sf cf}&=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\phi^{\sf cf}=0$ or $\psi^{\sf cf}=0$}\\
\bot&\text{if $\phi^{\sf cf},\psi^{\sf cf}\neq 0$ and ($\phi^{\sf cf}=\bot$ or $\psi^{\sf cf}=\bot$)}\\
\phi^{\sf cf}\psi^{\sf cf}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\end{aligned}

Formulas in
\mathbb{PL}
are not necessarily semantically equivalent to their canonical form, but they get the same value in all

(0,\infty)
-valued models.

Proposition 1

For any formula
\phi
,

\phi^{\sf cf}
is in canonical form, and for all models
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
,

\mathcal{M}(\phi)=\mathcal{M}(\phi^{\sf cf})
.

Observe that the restriction to
\infty
-free models is necessary, as if

\mathcal{M}(x)=\infty
,
\mathcal{M}(x\multimap\bot)=0
while
\mathcal{M}((x\multimap\bot)^{\sf cf})=\mathcal{M}(\bot)=\infty
. Similarly,

(Canonicalisation)

The

of a judgement

Proposition 2

Both

Remark 1

The inferences below are both unsound, thus not provable in the deductive system for

\displaystyle\phi^{\sf cf}\vdash\psi^{\sf cf}\phi\vdash\psi

\displaystyle\phi\vdash\psi\phi^{\sf cf}\vdash\psi^{\sf cf}

A counterexample for the soundness of (A) is when

and

A counterexample for the soundness of (B) is when

and

\mathcal{M}((x\multimap\bot)^{\sf cf})=0\geq\mathcal{M}(x^{\sf cf})

Intuitively, the canonicalisation of a judgement is better interpreted as an “over approximation”, where the propositional variables are assumed to be always finite real-valued and strictly positive. Next,
we introduce a definition that will help us express

Fix the sets of judgements
\mathfrak{F}=\{\vdash|x|\mid x\in\mathcal{X}\}

and
\mathfrak{P}=\{\vdash x>0\mid x\in\mathcal{X}\}

Definition 5
(Restricted Provability)

For a judgment
\gamma
and a set of judgments

,
(0,\infty]

Intuitively, adding
\mathfrak{F}
to the hypothesis asserts the finiteness of the variables, while

ensures they are strictly positive.

Proposition 3

All judgements in
\mathbb{PL}
are
(0,\infty)
-provably equivalent to their canonical forms, i.e., for all formulas

,
\psi
in

\displaystyle\frac{\phi\vdash\psi\quad\mathfrak{F}\cup\mathfrak{P}}{\phi^{\sf cf}\vdash\psi^{\sf cf}}

\displaystyle\frac{\phi^{\sf cf}\vdash\psi^{\sf cf}\quad\mathfrak{F}\cup\mathfrak{P}}{\phi\vdash\psi}\,.

Completeness and Incompleteness Results for
\mathbb{PL}

We prove firstly that, in general,
\mathbb{PL}
is incomplete.

Theorem 7.1
(Incompleteness)

The logic
\mathbb{PL}
is incomplete, meaning that there exist a set of judgements
S
and a judgement
\gamma
such that
\gamma
is a semantically consequence of
S
, but
\gamma
is not provable from
S
in
\mathbb{PL}
.

Proof

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and
S=\{(n+1)x\vdash ny\mid~n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Note that
x\vdash y
is a semantical consequence of
S
. All the proofs in
\mathbb{PL}
are finite, hence if there exists a proof for
x\vdash y
from
S
, there must exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that the only judgements used
in the proof are
V=\{(n+1)x\vdash y\mid 0\leq n\leq k\}
. If this is the case, any model of
V
is a model of
x\vdash y
(from soundness). But this is false: consider
\mathcal{M}
such that

\mathcal{M}(x)=\frac{k}{k+1}
and
\mathcal{M}(y)=1
; then, for all
n\leq k
,
\mathcal{M}((n+1)x)\leq\mathcal{M}(y)
, but

\mathcal{M}(x)<\mathcal{M}(y)
. Hence,
x\vdash y
is not provable from
S
.
∎

However,
\mathbb{PL}
is complete if we only consider finitely axiomatized theories.

Theorem 7.2
(Finite Completeness)

Let
S
be a finite set of judgements in
\mathbb{PL}
.
If a judgement
\gamma
is a semantic consequence of
S
, then
\gamma
is provable from
S

\displaystyle S\models\gamma

implies

\displaystyle\frac{\;S\;}{\gamma}\,.

The proof plan is to reduce the statement above to the
following restricted completeness theorem which applies only to judgements in polynomial form.

Theorem 7.3
(Polynomial Completeness)

Let
\gamma
be a judgement and
S
a finite set of judgements, both in polynomial form. Then,

\displaystyle S\cup\mathfrak{F}\models\gamma

implies

\displaystyle\frac{S\quad\mathfrak{F}}{\gamma}\,.

Note that,

represents a restricted form of semantical consequence where the models are assumed to be
[0,\infty)
-valued, which hereafter we denote by
S\models_{[0,\infty)}\gamma
.
We extend this notation to any
R\subseteq[0,\infty]
, where
S\models_{R}\gamma
denotes that every model
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to R
that satisfies
S
satisfies also
\gamma
, for which we say that

\gamma
is a
R

of
S
.

Before delving into the proof of Theorem

The proposed reduction is characterised by a set of nondeterministic moves of the form

(S,\gamma)\longrightarrow(S_{i},\gamma_{i})\qquad\text{(for $i=1,\dots,k$)}

\gamma

Reliable:

If
\gamma

is a semantical consequence of

. In symbols:

\displaystyle S\models\gamma

\displaystyle S_{i}\models\gamma_{i}\quad\text{(for $i=1,\dots,k$)}\,;

Nice:

If for all

The reduction technique sketched above is similar to “

We divide the reduction into four sets of moves, which are applied in the following order: (1) initialisation, (2) choice of domain, (3) reduction to canonical form, (4) reduction to proper canonical form; (5) reduction to polynomial form.
The order of application is important for the correctness of the reduction.

Step 1 (Initialisation).

The initialisation step has a single (deterministic) move

(S,\phi\vdash\psi)\longrightarrow(S\cup\{p\vdash\phi,q\vdash\psi\},p\vdash q)

Step 2 (Choice of domain).

The set of non-deterministic moves for this second step are given by

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\longrightarrow S\cup\{\vdash|p|,q^{2}p\vdash 1\}

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\longrightarrow S[\bot/p]

The reduction to canonical form consists of a single (determistic) move

Step 4 (Reduction to PCF).

After the previous step of canonicalisation, the only judgements that are not in proper canonical form are either trivially valid (

\displaystyle S

In this section, we present complexity bounds for standard decidability problems, specifically, satisfiability for a finite set of judgements and semantic consequence. We provide these results for both

Below, we describe a non-deterministic procedure for transforming a set of judgments in

9
Conclusions

This paper develops and studies Polynomial Lawvere logic (
\mathbb{PL}
), a logic based on the Lawvere quantale extended with multiplication, whose monoidal structure interacts with that on the tensor as a semiring. This logic is well-suited for encoding quantitative reasoning principles naturally, as demonstrated in Section
5
.

We propose a deduction system for
\mathbb{PL}
and show that, while the logic is generally incomplete, finitely axiomatized theories are complete. The core of the completeness proof draws on results from algebraic geometry, specifically the Krivine-Stengle Positivstellensatz.
The use of such results in the completeness proof provides compelling evidence of the deep connection between arithmetic and logical reasoning.

Additionally, we present new complexity results for both
\mathbb{PL}
and its affine fragment (
\mathbb{AL}
).
We demonstrate that the satisfiability of a finite set of judgements is NP-complete in
\mathbb{AL}
and in PSPACE for
\mathbb{PL}
; and that deciding the semantical consequence from a finite set of judgements is co-NP complete in
\mathbb{AL}
and in PSPACE for
\mathbb{PL}
.

Finally, building on the Weierstrass approximation theorem, which states that continuous real-valued functions on compact subsets can be approximated arbitrarily well by polynomials, one might consider developing an approximation theory grounded in
\mathbb{PL}
. This direction
is left for future work.
