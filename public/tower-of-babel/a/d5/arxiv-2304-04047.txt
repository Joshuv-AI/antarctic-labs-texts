Abstract
. We justify the Weyl asymptotic formula for the eigenvalues of the Poincaré-Steklov spectral problem for a domain bounded by a Lipschitz surface.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Poincaré-Steklov problem.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
be a connected bounded open set. We suppose that the boundary
\Sigma=\partial\Omega
is connected. The classical
Poincaré-Steklov (P-S) eigenvalue problem
consists in the study of the spectral properties for the Laplacian in
\Omega
with the spectral parameter entering in the boundary condition,

-\Delta u(x)=0,\,x\in\Omega;\partial_{\nu}u(x)-\lambda^{-1}u(x)=0,\,x\in\Sigma,

(1.1)

where
\partial_{\nu}
is the derivative in the direction of the exterior normal
\nu(x)
at
x\in\Sigma.
Further on, we assume that the boundary
\Sigma
is Lipschitz and we equip
\Sigma
with the surface measure induced by the Lebesgue measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1},
which coincides with the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.

This problem, often attributed to and named after V.A. Steklov,
[
40
]
, was, in fact, first considered by H. Poincaré in 1896, see
[
32
]
, in relation to the analysis of tidal waves. Another early application, dealing with liquid waves in an open container was studied by D. Hilbert in
[
22
]
.

The P-S spectral problem and its numerous generalizations found a lot of applications in physics and technology (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
and the quite recent review
[
17
]
, for a, far from complete, bibliography on this topic.) Studies dealing with this problem continue up to now. The Steklov eigenvalue
problem appears in quite a few physical fields, such as
fluid mechanics, electromagnetism, elasticity, etc. It has applications for the study of various kinds of wave phenomena (see, e.g.
[
42
]
), as well as in the seismology and tomography. Mathematically, this problem keeps being in the center of interest in spectral geometry and approximations.
MathSciNet shows more than 600 publications where various facets of Steklov type spectral problems are dealt with.

One of traditional topics in this field is the study of the behavior of eigenvalues of the P-S problem.
In the earliest paper by L. Sandgren on the asymptotics of these eigenvalues (see
[
39
]
), inspired by Lars G
\overset{{}_{{}_{\circ}}}{\mathrm{a}}
rding and
\AA
ke Plejel, with co-operation of Lars Hörmander, the author considered a moderately smooth domain
\Omega
in a Riemannian manifold. The boundary of the domain was supposed be of the class
C^{2}
, but the coefficients of the main operator were set to belong to
C^{1}.
Under these conditions, the asymptotic formula of H. Weyl type was obtained. Further on, the conditions imposed on the domain and coefficients were being gradually relaxed. Finally, in 2006, M.S. Agranovich
[
2
]
established the Weyl type formula for the case of a Lipschitz boundary, being, however, ’almost smooth’ in the sense that this boundary should be at least
C^{1}
outside a closed set of zero surface measure; the symmetric second order elliptic operator
{\mathcal{L}}
replacing the Laplacian in (
1.1
) is supposed to be of divergence form with continuous coefficients. Even earlier, probably, since
[
6
]
, M.S. Agranovich started popularizing the problem of extending the eigenvalue asymptotics results to domains with Lipschitz boundary, without additional assumptions. This question was morally supported by a flow of impressive results on various properties of elliptic boundary problems in Lipschitz domains by A.P.Calderón, B.Dahlberg, C.Kenig, S.Hoffman, M.Taylor, M.Mitrea, E.Fabes, G.Verchota, M.S.Agranovich himself, and many others. It turned out that the setting of Lipschitz boundary problems and the typical results are usually more or less the same as for a more smooth boundary, while the methods used in analysis need to be essentially new – and often quite complicated. This latter circumstance is, partially, caused by the fact that certain important results in potential theory break down when passing from
C^{1}
to Lipschitz surfaces.

,

,

]
, and references therein, where various aspects of this problem were investigated. Important results were obtained even for boundaries considerably less regular than the Lipschitz ones, see, e.g.,
[

. Quite extensive became studies in the spectral geometry relating the geometric properties of the boundary to spectral properties. Especially rich were the results in the two-dimensional case. Here, it was known since long ago, see
[

, that in the infinitely smooth case, the Steklov eigenvalues are, faster than any negative power of their sequential number, close to those for the disk with the same perimeter. Quite a lot of further results in the two-dimensional case were obtained since then. A description and huge bibliography can be found in
[

]
,
[

]
, and
[

]
. We just mention here that in a series of papers, starting with

, for the case of a piecewise smooth Lipschitz boundary, the authors succeeded in describing how the corners influence the deviation of the eigenvalues from their behavior in the smooth case. Meanwhile, in the Lipschitz case, many important properties of the P-S (or the Neumann-to-Dirichlet, N-to-D,
N\!\!\!D
operator) were established, see, e.g.,

Finally, after all these years, the essential progress in the eigenvalue asymptotics for a Lipschitz boundary was made in
[

, where, in the two-dimensional case, the Weyl eigenvalue asymptotics for the problem (

) was proved. Moreover, in
[

, the boundary of the domain may be even somewhat more rough than Lipschitz. The method in
[

is based upon some deep results in the theory of conformal mappings, therefore, seemingly, it cannot be extended to higher dimensions. Thus, the eigenvalue asymptotics problem for Lipschitz boundary remained unresolved. In the recent fundamental review
[
17

and in

The author of the present paper, together with Grigory Tashchiyan, spent quite a lot of time attacking this problem, being initially inspired by M.S. Agranovich. Our hope was based upon our result in
[
37
]
, where, for integral operators of the type of single layer potential on a Lipschitz surface, a Weyl type eigenvalue asymptotic formula was proved. Since the P-S operator can be expressed in a simple way via the single layer and double layer potentials (see, e.g.
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
, and in a very general setting,
[
43
]
), it would be sufficient, for example, to know that the double layer potential is a compact operator. Unfortunately, for a Lipschitz surface, one should not expect this property of the double layer potential to hold; in fact, the opposite is known. Our efforts to circumvent this obstacle led to some partial results (see a mentioning in
[
42
]
); we, however, did not consider these partial results deserving being published (a brief description of our efforts here, is given in Appendix, in hope that someone might be interested and more lucky – if successful, such proof will, probably, be more esthetic than the present one).

A different approach is used in
this
paper, not based directly upon the results in
[
37
]
but rather on the
idea
in
[
37
]
, a quite natural one, of approximating the Lipschitz surface by smooth surfaces and tracing how the corresponding compact Neumann-to-Dirichlet operators converge. In the study of solvability of elliptic boundary problems in Lipschitz domains, such approximation was, probably, first used by G.Verchota in
[
45
]
. In fact, a somewhat different realization of this approximation idea is implemented here. By a change of variables, the P-S problem in a Lipschitz domain for an elliptic operator in divergence form with coefficients continuous at the boundary, is relocated to the problem for another elliptic operator, this time in a smooth domain. Unfortunately, under such transformation, the coefficients of the operator
cease to be continuous
even at the boundary, but stay only bounded. Functions in
L_{\infty}
cannot be approximated by smooth functions in
L_{\infty}
norm. We, however, construct an approximation of the coefficients in a certain
L_{p}
-related norm,
p<\infty,
and after a series of further transformations, we succeed in establishing a sufficiently strong convergence of operators describing the P-S spectrum, which enables us to perform the passage to the limit in eigenvalue asymptotic formulas.

The reasoning in the paper is, to a large extent, based upon the ideas the author absorbed many years ago while being a student of M.Sh. Birman and M.Z. Solomyak; the paper is dedicated to their memory. The author thanks Prof. Tatiana Suslina for useful discussions and Prof. Jean Lagacé who acquainted him with an early version of the paper
[
26

, which encouraged our efforts. Permanent discussions with Prof. Grigory Tashchiyan for at least 15 years contributed a lot to a better understanding of the problem and stimulated the author, who is deeply grateful to Grigory for his involvement.

1.2.
Setting

We are going to study the distribution of eigenvalues and singular numbers of various kinds of compact operators. By
n_{\pm}(\lambda,{\mathbf{K}})
we denote the distribution function of positive (negative) eigenvalues of the
self-adjoint
operator
{\mathbf{K}}
; for an arbitrary compact operator
{\mathbf{K}}
,
n(\lambda,{\mathbf{K}})
denotes the counting function of singular numbers of
{\mathbf{K}}.
In the case when the operator is described by a quadratic form
\mathfrak{B}[u]
in the Hilbert space with norm defined by means of the quadratic form
\mathfrak{A}[u]
, we replace
{\mathbf{K}}
by the ratio of these quadratic forms in the notation
n(\lambda,.)
,
n_{\pm}(\lambda,.)
. For
\theta>0
, we denote by
{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}_{\pm}(\theta,{\mathbf{K}})
the quantity
\limsup_{\lambda\to 0}\lambda^{\theta}n_{\pm}(\lambda,{\mathbf{K}}).
Without the subscript
\pm
, these notations concern the distribution of singular numbers of these operators. Without the superscript
\sup
, these symbols denote the limits
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\lambda^{\theta}n_{\pm}(\lambda,{\mathbf{K}}),
provided these limits exist. Finally, if some operator or the ratio of quadratic form is defined in the displayed formula with tag (X.Y), the above notation is used with this tag replacing the notation of the operator, e.g.,
{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}(\theta,(X.Y))
, etc.

We consider a bounded domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
with connected Lipschitz boundary
\Sigma
. (The connectedness condition can be easily removed, at the cost of certain notational complications.) This means that
\Sigma=\partial\Omega
can be covered by a finite collection of co-ordinate neighborhoods
{\mathcal{U}}_{\iota}
such that the portion of
\Sigma
in
{\mathcal{U}}_{\iota}
can be, in a properly rotated Euclidean co-ordinate system,
(x^{\prime},x_{d+1})\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\iota}
, represented by the equation
x_{d+1}=\psi^{(\iota)}(x^{\prime}),\,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{V}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with Lipschitz function
\psi^{(\iota)}
(sometimes such surfaces are called strongly Lipschitz in the literature, see, e.g.,
[
23
]
.) It is convenient to use a global co-ordinate system in a neighborhood of the boundary, see Sect.3.

In
[
39
]
,
[
2
]
, and some other papers, a more general setting is considered, namely the spectral problem (
1.1
), with the Laplacian in the equation replaced by some formally self-adjoint second order elliptic differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}
in divergence form, with the normal derivative
\partial_{\nu}
replaced by the derivative along the conormal associated with
{\mathcal{L}}
and with a weight function present on the right-hand side of the eigenvalue equation. For us, it is also convenient to consider such more general, the
weighted

N\!\!\!D
, Neumann-to-Dirichlet, problem. Namely, for a uniformly positive definite (elliptic) real matrix-function
\mathfrak{a}(x)=(a_{j,k}(x),\,j,k=1,\dots,d+1)
,
\mathfrak{a}\in L_{\infty}(\Omega),
we denote by
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}_{\mathfrak{a}}
the formal differential operator

{\mathcal{L}}\equiv{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathfrak{a}}=-\sum_{j,k}\partial_{j}a_{jk}(x)\partial_{k}.

(1.2)

The weighted
N\!\!\!D
problem for
{\mathcal{L}}
is the eigenvalue problem

{\mathcal{L}}u(x)=0,\,x\in\Omega;\partial_{\mathfrak{a}}u(x)=\lambda^{-1}\rho(x)u(x),\,x\in\Sigma;\,\partial_{\mathfrak{a}}=\sum_{j,k}a_{j,k}\nu_{k}\partial_{x_{j}},

(1.3)

with a real function
\rho(x).
The conormal differential operator
\partial_{\mathfrak{a}}
has symbol
\imath\langle\mathfrak{a}(x)\xi,\nu_{x}\rangle.

A direct setting of the problem (
1.3
) requires an explicit description of the space of functions
u
where the equation is considered. Under very weak conditions imposed here on
\Omega
and
\mathfrak{a},
this task is very hard. Instead of this, we follow
[
39
]
,
[
2
]
in considering the
N\!\!\!D
problem in the variational form, where all spaces under consideration admit explicit description, see Section 2. It is noted in
[
2
]
that such variational approach follows the way of reasoning of H.Poincaré, V.A.Steklov and other researchers of that time, for whom it is the variational setting of the eigenvalue problem, based upon considering energy balance, was the primary point of the eigenvalue analysis, while the differential Euler-Lagrange equation appeared as a secondary object.

1.3.
Asymptotic formulas. The main result

The asymptotic eigenvalue formula for the problem (
1.2
) can be written in the following way (see, e.g.,
[
2
]
). In a fixed orthogonal co-ordinate system, we associate with the matrix
\mathfrak{a}(x)
its sesquilinear form
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{x}
:
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{x}(\xi)=\sum_{j,k}a_{jk}(x)\xi_{j}\xi_{k};
let
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{x}(\xi,\eta)
be the corresponding bilinear form.
For
x\in\Sigma,

\xi^{\prime}\in\mathrm{T}^{*}_{x}\Sigma
and the normal vector
\nu
at
x,
we denote by
\beta(x;\xi^{\prime})
the positive square root of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{x}(\nu)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{x}(\xi^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{x}(\xi^{\prime},\nu)^{2}
,

\beta(x,\xi^{\prime})=(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{x}(\nu)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{x}(\xi^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{x}(\xi^{\prime},\nu)^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}};

(1.4)

this is a function positively homogeneous in
\xi^{\prime}
of order
1
.
Then the density
\alpha_{\pm}(x)
is defined as

\alpha_{\pm}(x)={\rm vol}\,\{\xi^{\prime}\in\mathrm{T}^{*}_{x}\Sigma:\beta(x;\xi^{\prime})<\rho_{\pm}(x)\},

(1.5)

and the asymptotic formulas to be proved are

{\mathbf{n}}_{\pm}(d,\ref{LSteklov})=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\Sigma}\alpha_{\pm}(x)d\mu_{\Sigma}(x),

(1.6)

where
\mu_{\Sigma}
is the surface measure on
\Sigma
generated by the embedding of
\Sigma
into
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}.

For a
continuous
matrix-function
\mathfrak{a},
such definition of the eigenvalue problem (
1.3
) and the understanding of the formula (
1.6
) do not cause confusion, even if the matrix
\mathfrak{a}
is continuous only at the boundary
\Sigma.
In our case, the matrix
\mathfrak{a}(x)
may be discontinuous. However, under the conditions imposed below, the limit values of
a_{jk}
at the boundary are well defined almost everywhere in
\Sigma,
and therefore these formulas (
1.6
) make sense.

In
[
2
]
, the asymptotic formula (
1.6
) was justified under the following conditions, see
Theorem
on P.242.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
be a bounded domain; suppose that
\Sigma=\partial\Omega
is a surface of class
C^{1}
and
\rho\in L_{\infty}(\Sigma).
Suppose finally that the coefficients
a_{jk}
are continuous in
\overline{\Omega}
. Then the asymptotics (
1.6
) holds. Moreover, the same conclusion is correct if
\Sigma
is Lipschitz but belongs to
C^{1}
in a neighborhood of the support of
\rho,
outside a closed set of zero surface measure. The function
\rho
may belong to
L_{d}(\Sigma)
for
d>1
and any
L_{q}(\Sigma)
with
q>1,
for
d=1.

The
elementary
formulation of the main result of our present paper is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
be a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\Sigma.
Suppose that the operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathfrak{a}}
is uniformly elliptic in
\Omega
, the coefficients
a_{jk}
are continuous in
\overline{\Omega}
. Suppose that
\rho\in L_{d}(\Sigma)
for
d>1
and
\rho
belongs to the Orlicz class
L\log L(\Sigma)
for
d=1.
Then the asymptotic formula (
1.6
) is valid.

The continuity condition in Theorem
1.2
can be considerably relaxed. We say that a function
a(x)\in L_{\infty}(\Omega)
is
continuous at the boundary
if there exists a continuous function
a_{b}\in C(\Sigma),

\Sigma=\partial\Omega
such that for any
x^{\prime}\in\Sigma
,

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\|a(.)-a_{b}(x^{\prime})\|_{L_{\infty}(B(x^{\prime},\delta)\cap\Omega)}=0,

(1.7)

where
B(x^{\prime},\delta)
is the ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
with radius
\delta
, centered at
x^{\prime}.

Since the functions in
L_{\infty}(\Omega)
are defined up to a set of measure
0
, we may identify in the above definition the continuous function
a_{b}
with the restriction of
a
to
\Sigma
.

Having this definition, the formulation of the more general result that we are going to prove here, is as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
be a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\Sigma.
Suppose that the operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathfrak{a}}
is uniformly elliptic in
\Omega
, the coefficients
a_{jk}
are bounded and measurable and they are continuous at the boundary in the sense of (
1.7
). Then the asymptotic formula (
1.6
) is valid.

Remark
.

The conditions imposed on the coefficients of the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
can be further relaxed, allowing them to be unbounded in a certain sense and admitting certain degeneration of ellipticity. We do not pursue this line here, in order to keep the elementary level of the presentation.

We describe here briefly the structure of the proof. After certain transformations, the weighted
N\!\!\!D
problem in the domain with Lipschitz boundary is relocated to a similar problem for a uniformly elliptic operator
{\mathcal{L}}
but with bounded coefficients, possibly, discontinuous at the boundary, in a domain with smooth boundary. This operator is approximated by operators
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}
with smooth coefficients which converge to the coefficients of
{\mathcal{L}}
in a certain
L_{p}
- based norm, with some
p<\infty
. Then it is proved that the compact operators describing the P-S spectrum for
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}
converge so strongly that it becomes possible to pass to the limit in the formulas for coefficients
{\mathbf{n}}(d,.)
in the Steklov eigenvalue asymptotics for
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}.

2.
The variational representation

The setting of the eigenvalue problem under very weak regularity conditions imposed on the coefficients and the domain, is variational. As usual, for more regular data, the variational setting is equivalent to the classical, ’strong’ one.

2.1.
The weighted
N\!\!\!D
operator

Since the earliest paper on the topic by Lenart Sandgren
[
39
]
in 1955, the most commonly used method of the study of Steklov eigenvalues is the variational one. Its main advantage is its robustness with respect to various perturbations of the problem. We too will need a version of the variational setting of the Steklov eigenvalue problem (see, however, Appendix where we discuss a different approach).

First of all, since we are going to use the instruments of the perturbation theory for compact operators, we will need to pass from the unbounded Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators
D\!\!\!N
(which are often considered) to the N-to-D ones,
N\!\!\!D,
since the latter operators are compact (this corresponds to the placement of the spectral parameter in (1.3)). A minor inconvenience here is that the Neumann problem for the Laplacian (as well as for other second order elliptic operators without zero order terms) has an eigenfunction with zero eigenvalue, so the Neumann operator is not invertible. There are several standard ways to deal with this circumstance. Say, often, the Neumann operator is restricted to the codimension 1 subspace of functions orthogonal to constants. In our considerations, it is more convenient to avoid hitting the zero eigenvalue by means of adding a nontrivial nonnegative function
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}(x)
to the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
, namely to consider the operator
{\mathcal{L}}+{\mathbf{v}}_{0}(x)
. Such perturbation is relatively compact with respect to self-adjoint realizations of
{\mathcal{L}}
, and, by the usual perturbation reasoning, see, e.g., Lemma 1.3 in
[
13
]
, it does not influence the asymptotics of the spectrum. One might have set
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}\equiv 1,
but this does not simplify the matter. For economy of symbols, we use the notation
{\mathcal{L}}
for the operator with
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}
already absorbed, further on. At a proper moment, we will use the freedom in choosing
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}.

We denote by
{\mathbf{T}}\equiv{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathfrak{a}}
the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
with
Neumann
boundary conditions on
\Sigma
. Namely, it corresponds to the quadratic form

{\mathbf{a}}_{0}[u]=\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}[u](x)+{\mathbf{v}}_{0}(x)|u(x)|^{2})dx,\,\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}[u](x):=\sum_{j,k}a_{j,k}(x)\partial_{j}u(x)\partial_{k}\bar{u}(x),

(2.1)

with domain
u\in H^{1}(\Omega);
this form is equivalent to the square of the standard norm
\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
in the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\Omega).
Being considered in
L_{2}(\Omega)
, this quadratic form defines the positive self-adjoint operator
{\mathbf{T}}
satisfying

{\mathbf{a}}_{0}[u]=({\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u,{\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u),\,u\in H^{1}(\Omega)={\mathcal{D}}({\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}).

(This fact, which we will use systematically, that the domain of a closed positive quadratic form coincides with the domain of the square root of the operator defined by this quadratic form, is a standard property in the abstract spectral theory, see, e.g.,
[
27
]
, Theorem 2.23 in Sect. VI. For quadratic forms which are only sectorial (the case we do not need), this equality constitutes the famous
Kato’s square root problem
which has been the topic of deep extensive studies since 1980-s.)

Further on, we will sometimes use the notion of the spectrum of the ratio of quadratic forms in the Hilbert space. Actually, the spectrum of the ratio
\frac{\mathfrak{B}[f]}{\mathfrak{A}[f]}
with
f\in\mathfrak{X}
is a shorthand for the spectrum of the operator defined by the quadratic form
\mathfrak{B}[f]
in the Hilbert space
\mathfrak{X}
equipped with the norm defined by the quadratic form
\mathfrak{A}[f]
.

For a real function
\rho_{0}(x),x\in\Sigma
we consider the quadratic form

\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{0}[f]=\int_{\Sigma}\rho_{0}(x)|f(x)|^{2}d\mu_{\Sigma}(x).

(2.2)

Let
\rho_{0}
belong to
L_{d}(\Sigma).

By Kondrashov’s trace theorem, for
d>1,
the trace mapping
u\mapsto u_{|_{\Sigma}},
defined initially on continuous functions
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, extends by continuity to the bounded mapping
\gamma:u\mapsto u_{|_{\Sigma}},

\gamma:H^{1}(\Omega)\to L_{q}(\Sigma),

q=\frac{2d}{d-1}.
Therefore, by the Hölder inequality, if
\rho_{0}\in L_{d}(\Sigma)
, with
d^{-1}+2q^{-1}=1
, the quadratic form (
2.2
) satisfies

\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{0}[\gamma u]\leq C\|\rho_{0}\|_{L_{d}(\Sigma)}{\mathbf{a}}_{0}[u],\,u\in H^{1}(\Omega).

(2.3)

For
d=1,
a somewhat more complicated reasoning (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
) gives

\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{0}[\gamma u]\leq C\|\rho_{0}\|_{L\log L(\Sigma)}{\mathbf{a}}_{0}[u],\,u\in H^{1}(\Omega),

(2.4)

where
{L\log L(\Sigma)}
is the Orlicz space consisting of functions
u
on
\Sigma
for which
|u|(1+|\log|u||)\in L_{1}(\Sigma),
with the Luxemburg norm.

The following variational representation is known since
[
39
]
, see e.g.,
[
2
]
,
[
43
]
, and sources cited there: the Steklov spectrum is described by the ratio of quadratic forms
\frac{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{0}[\gamma u]}{{\mathbf{a}}_{0}[u]}
considered on the space of
L_{2}-
solutions of the equation
{\mathcal{L}}u=0.
Since
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{0}[\gamma u]=0
for
u\in\overset{\circ}{H}{}^{1}(\Omega),
one can, as this is usually done, drop the condition
{\mathcal{L}}u=0
in the variational setting and consider the spectrum of the ratio

\frac{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{0}[\gamma u]}{{\mathbf{a}}_{0}[u]},\,u\in H^{1}(\Omega).

(2.5)

2.2.
A freedom in the choice of the weight function
\rho_{0}
.

Estimates (
2.3
), (
2.4
) mean that the quadratic form (
2.2
) is bounded in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, the equality in (
2.2
) can be therefore extended by continuity to all traces of functions in
H^{1}(\Omega)
and therefore the form
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{0}
defines a bounded operator
{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{S}}[{\rho_{0}}]
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, with norm estimated by
\|\rho_{0}\|_{L_{d}(\Sigma)},
resp.,
\|\rho_{0}\|_{L\log L(\Sigma)}.
If the function
\rho_{0}
is real-valued, this operator is self-adjoint.
In fact, this operator is compact and its eigenvalues satisfy a power order estimate.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1},

d>1,
be a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\Sigma
and let
\rho_{0}\in L_{d}(\Sigma)
. Then for the singular numbers of
{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{S}}{[\rho_{0}]}
the estimate holds

n(\lambda,{\mathbf{S}}{[\rho_{0}]})\leq C(\Omega,\mathfrak{a})\lambda^{-d}\|\rho_{0}\|_{L_{d}(\Sigma)}^{d}.

(2.6)

If the function
\rho_{0}
is real-valued, estimates of the form (
2.6
) hold, separately, for positive and negative eigenvalues,

n_{\pm}(\lambda,{\mathbf{S}}{[\rho_{0}]})\leq C(\Omega,\mathfrak{a})\lambda^{-d}\|(\rho_{0})_{\pm}\|_{L_{d}(\Sigma)}^{d}.

(2.7)

For
d=1
the above results hold, with
L_{d}(\Sigma)
replaced by
L\log L(\Sigma).

Proof.

For
d>1,
we use the estimate for the eigenvalues of Birman-Schwinger type operators with singular measures, obtained recently in
[
38

The eigenvalue estimates in Theorem

Lemma 2.2

Let

Among most recent examples of applying this way of reasoning, one can cite

Suppose that for the weights

The same statement is valid for the dimension

In particular, this means that we can restrict ourselves to considering as

We follow now

\rho_{0}

2.3.
The asymptotic coefficient

The next preparatory step consists in obtaining a convenient expression for the coefficient in the asymptotic formula for the eigenvalues of the P-S problem so that it is possible to pass to the limit in this expression as the coefficients of the operator converge in a proper sense.

The asymptotic coefficient, known to be correct in the smooth case and aimed for in the Lipschitz case,

For our further needs, it will be more convenient to use a somewhat different representation of the coefficient in (

Namely, for a given

we can represent
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{x}(\xi)

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{x}(\xi)=\xi^{*}\mathfrak{a}(x)\xi,\,\,\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{x}(\xi,\eta)=\xi^{*}\mathfrak{a}(x)\eta=\eta^{*}\mathfrak{a}(x)\xi,

\displaystyle\beta(x,\xi^{\prime})^{2}=\nu^{*}\mathfrak{a}(x)\nu{\xi^{\prime}}^{*}\mathfrak{a}(x)\xi^{\prime}-{\xi^{\prime}}^{*}\mathfrak{a}(x)\nu\nu^{*}\mathfrak{a}(x)\xi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\nu^{*}\mathfrak{a}(x)\nu\langle\mathfrak{a}(x)\xi^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}\rangle-\langle[(\nu^{*}\mathfrak{a}(x))^{*}(\nu^{*}\mathfrak{a}(x))]\xi^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}\rangle:=\langle\Theta(x)\xi^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}\rangle

(2.13)

therefore,

\alpha_{\pm}(x)={\rm vol}\,\{\xi^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:0<\langle\Theta^{\prime}_{x})\xi^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}}<(\rho_{0})_{\pm}(x)\}=\omega_{d}(\rho_{0})_{\pm}(x)^{d}\det(\Theta^{\prime}_{x})^{-1/2},

It follows from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality that

2.4.
D-to-N and N-to-D operators as pseudodifferential ones

It is well known that for the Laplacian, the
D\!\!\!N
operator equals, up to lower order terms,
(-\Delta_{\Sigma})^{\frac{1}{2}}
, for a smooth boundary; an interesting discussion can be found in
[
19
]
. For general elliptic operators, in
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
, M.S.Agranovich presented an explanation of the coefficient
\beta(x,\xi^{\prime})
for the case of a smooth (or ’almost smooth’) surface
\Sigma.
We discuss now the latter formula. Suppose first that the coefficients matrix
\mathfrak{a}(x)
does not depend on
x\in\Omega.
Consider the fundamental solution
R(x-y)
for the operator
{\mathcal{L}},
so that
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}R(x-y)=\delta(x-y).
With this fundamental solution the classical potential operators are associated, namely, the single layer potential operator
S
,

f\mapsto\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt_{f}(x)=\int_{\Sigma}R(x-y)f(y)d\mu(y),\,x\in\Sigma,

and the ’direct value’ of the conormal derivative of the single layer potential (often called the Neumann-Poincaré operator)

\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt^{\prime}:\,f\mapsto\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt^{\prime}_{f}(x)=\int_{\Sigma}\partial_{\mathfrak{a}(x)}R(x-y)f(y)d\mu(y),\,x\in\Sigma,

the latter integral understood in the principal value sense.
The adjoint to
\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt^{\prime}_{f}
is the integral operator
\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt,
called the direct value of the double layer potential,

\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt:\,f\mapsto\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt_{f}(x)=\int_{\Sigma}\partial_{\mathfrak{a}(y)}R(x-y)f(y)d\mu(y),\,x\in\Sigma.

Then the
N\!\!\!D
operator is, up to lower order terms, the composition

N\!\!\!D=(\frac{1}{2}+\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt^{\prime})^{-1}\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt=\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt(\frac{1}{2}+\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt)^{-1}.

(2.19)

For a smooth boundary
\Sigma
, the operators
S
and
D
are order
-1
pseudodifferential operators on
\Sigma.
Since
S
is a restriction of the order
-2
pseudodifferential operator to the boundary, its symbol is calculated according to the usual rules of pseudodifferential calculus,

\mathfrak{s}(x,\xi^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[\mathfrak{a}(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{d+1})]^{-1}d\xi_{d+1},\,x\in\Sigma,

(2.20)

in co-ordinates where
x_{d+1}
is directed along the normal vector
\nu_{x}
to the tangent plane at the point
x\in\Sigma
and the corresponding co-ordinates
\xi^{\prime},\xi_{d+1},
with
\xi^{\prime}
in the cotangent plane and
\xi_{d+1}
directed along
\nu_{x}
,
see, e.g.,
[
4
]
, (4.28). Calculations by
(
2.20
)
give the expression for
\beta(x,\xi^{\prime})=\mathfrak{s}(x,\xi^{\prime})^{-1}
in (
1.4
) as the principal symbol of the operator
D\!\!\!N=N\!\!\!D^{-1}.

Similar considerations cover the case of variable smooth coefficients, where the fundamental solution takes the form
R(x,y),

{\mathcal{L}}_{x}R(x,y)=\delta(x-y)
and in (
2.20
) one should change
\mathfrak{a}(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{d+1})^{-1}
to
\mathfrak{a}(x^{\prime},0,\xi^{\prime},\xi_{d+1})^{-1},
see
[

For a non-smooth, Lipschitz surface, the function

The principal symbol of the double layer operator

3.
Some geometry considerations

In this Section we introduce a global co-ordinate system in an interior collar neighborhood of the boundary
\Sigma=\partial\Omega.
After the passage to this co-ordinate system and a special co-ordinates change, the Poincaré-Steklov problem in a Lipschitz domain, for the equation with coefficients,
continuous at the boundary
, reduces to an equivalent problem in a domain with smooth boundary, but for an elliptic operator with
discontinuous
bounded measurable coefficients, fortunately, still with a certain very weak continuity property.

3.1.
A smooth surface approximating the boundary

By the Rademacher theorem, a Lipschitz function has derivatives almost everywhere and these derivatives are bounded. Therefore, the Lipschitz surface
\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
has a tangent plane almost everywhere. This fact enables one to describe explicitly the surface measure on
\Sigma
generated by the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}.
Namely, on the local Lipschitz graph
x_{d+1}=\psi(x^{\prime}),\,x^{\prime}\in\sc\mbox{V}\hskip 1.0pt\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d},
the surface measure is given by

\mu_{\Sigma}(E)=\int_{\pi E}\sqrt{1+|\nabla\psi(x^{\prime})|^{2}}dx^{\prime},

(3.1)

where
\pi E
is the projection of the set
E\subset\Sigma
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.
It is important to note that for a Lipschitz function
\psi,
the integrand in (
3.1
) is a bounded function, an algebraic expression of the partial derivatives of
\psi.

A detailed study of geometric properties of Lipschitz domains is presented in
[
23
]
. In particular, for a domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
with Lipschitz boundary, the distributional gradient
{\rm grad}\,(\chi_{\Omega})
of the characteristic function of
\Omega
is a vector measure which is absolutely continuous with respect to the measure
\mu_{\Sigma}
in (
3.1
),

{\rm grad}\,(\chi_{\Omega})=-{\nu}(x)\mu_{\Sigma}

(3.2)

with the vector-valued density
{\nu}
,
|{\nu}(x)|=1
coinciding with the normal at
x
almost everywhere on
\Sigma.

(Such vector measure can be well defined for a class of domains even somewhat less regular as well, see
[
23
]
.)

Starting from
[
39
]
, and then in
[
2
]
,
[
41
]
,
[
3
]
, etc., a localization was used, reducing the spectral analysis for operators related with the P-S problem in
\Omega
to the ones in Lipschitz cylinders. In our study, it is more convenient to define the surface
\Sigma
and perform related constructions globally.

To do this, we will need the classical result on the existence of a smooth vector field transversal to the Lipschitz surface. Namely, there exists a
C^{\infty}
-vector field
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(x),

x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
such that at the points
x\in\Sigma=\partial\Omega,
those points where the tangent plane
exists,
|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(x)|=1
and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(x)
forms an acute angle with the normal
\nu(x)
to
\Sigma
at
x,
not exceeding some constant which is
strictly smaller than
\pi/2.
The construction of such field can be found, e.g., in
[
23
]
, Proposition 2.3 (in fact a similar construction was used as long ago as in 1983 by A.P. Calderón, see
[
16
]
).

For further reasoning, we need one more geometric construction. We suspect that it might have been well known since long ago, but were not able to locate a reference in the literature. Therefore, we describe it here.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
be a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\Sigma.
Let
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
be the transversal smooth vector field, as above. In a neighborhood of
\Sigma
denote by
\ell_{x}
the integral curve of this vector field passing through
x
. Then, for a certain interior collar neighborhood
U
of
\Sigma
there exists a smooth surface
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\subset\sc\mbox{U}\hskip 1.0pt
, which has exactly one intersection point with every integral curve
\ell
of the field
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
in
\sc\mbox{U}\hskip 1.0pt,
and this intersection is transversal.

Proof.

In some two-sided collar neighborhood
\widetilde{\sc\mbox{U}\hskip 1.0pt}
of
\Sigma,
the integral curves
\ell_{x^{\prime}},

x^{\prime}\in\Sigma,
of the vector field
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
form a one-dimensional foliation. We define the function
\mathfrak{f}(x),

x\in\widetilde{\sc\mbox{U}\hskip 1.0pt},
as the distance of the point
x
to the intersection point
x^{\prime}\in\Sigma
along the integral curve
\ell_{x^{\prime}}
passing through
x
(with proper sign, positive inside
\Sigma.
) Then
x=(x^{\prime},t)
,
t=\mathfrak{f}(x)
form a new co-ordinate system in
\widetilde{\sc\mbox{U}\hskip 1.0pt}.
In these co-ordinates the function
\mathfrak{f}
is continuous in
\widetilde{\sc\mbox{U}\hskip 1.0pt}
, differentiable almost everywhere and has derivatives of any order in
t
variable, in other words, along
\ell_{x^{\prime}}
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{f}
is strictly decaying in
t
variable, i.e., along the curves
\ell_{x^{\prime}}
.

We take a smooth non-negative cut-off function
\chi_{\epsilon}(x),\,\operatorname{diam\,}\hbox{{\rm supp}}\,\chi_{\epsilon}<\epsilon,

\int\chi_{\epsilon}(x)dx=1,
with sufficiently small
\epsilon
, and consider the convolution, the smooth function
\mathfrak{f}_{\epsilon}=\mathfrak{f}*\chi_{\epsilon}.
Sufficiently close to
\Sigma
, this function is smooth, growing in
t
variable, and it has the same sign as
\mathfrak{f}
outside the
\epsilon
-neighborhood of
\Sigma
. We consider the level surface
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
for
\mathfrak{f}_{\epsilon},

\mathfrak{f}_{\epsilon}(x)=\delta
for some
\delta>0.
By construction, being the level surface of a smooth function without stationary points, this surface is smooth, has only one intersection point with each of trajectories
\ell_{x^{\prime}}
and is uniformly nontangent with these trajectories. Due to the monotonicity of
\mathfrak{f},
the level
\delta
can be chosen in such way that the surface
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
lies strictly inside
\Omega;
we may also suppose that
{\rm dist}\,(\Sigma,\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})>{\mathbf{r}}_{0}>0.

∎

3.2.
Change of variables

We introduce now new co-ordinates in

to the surface
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}

to be positive outside

. According to the properties of the surface

this is a smooth co-ordinates change from the initial Euclidean co-ordinates.

In these co-ordinates, the initial surface
\Sigma
is described

by the equation
x_{d+1}=\boldsymbol{\psi}(x^{\prime})
,
x^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},
with some Lipschitz function
\boldsymbol{\psi}(x^{\prime})>{\mathbf{r}}_{0}>0
for all
x^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
(recall that the surface
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
lies strictly inside
\overline{\Omega}
).
We denote by
\sc\mbox{C}\hskip 1.0pt_{0}
the truncated cylinder with base
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
and top

\sc\mbox{C}\hskip 1.0pt_{0}=\{(x^{\prime},x_{d+1}):x^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},x_{d+1}\in[0,\boldsymbol{\psi}(x^{\prime})]\}\subset{\Omega},

which is diffeomorphic to a collar neighborhood of
\Sigma
in
\Omega,
and denote by
\Omega_{0}
the complement of this cylinder in
\Omega
,

\Omega_{0}=\Omega\setminus\sc\mbox{C}\hskip 1.0pt_{0}

In this setting the domain

can be considered as a smooth manifold with Lipschitz boundary
\Sigma
,

\Omega=\Omega_{0}\cup\sc\mbox{C}\hskip 1.0pt_{0}

having a truncated cylindrical exit
\sc\mbox{C}\hskip 1.0pt_{0}=\sc\mbox{C}\hskip 1.0pt_{0}[\boldsymbol{\psi}]
with base section
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
and boundary
\Sigma
.

Our next aim now is to perform a change of variables
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
of
\Omega
to the standard domain
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\Omega_{0}\cup\sc\mbox{C}\hskip 1.0pt
where

is the

,
\sc\mbox{C}\hskip 1.0pt=[0,1]\times\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}.
This change of variables is the identity mapping,
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(x)=x
on
\Omega_{0}
and an adjoining part of the cylinder
\sc\mbox{C}\hskip 1.0pt_{0}[\boldsymbol{\psi}]
,
x_{d+1}<t_{0}
, while near the Lipschitz boundary, it is glued together, by means of proper cut-offs, with the
stretching
along the
x_{d+1}
variable, i.e., along the integral curves of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
namely, with the mapping

\boldsymbol{\Phi}(x)\equiv\boldsymbol{\Phi}(x^{\prime},x_{d+1})=(y^{\prime},y_{d+1})\equiv\left(x^{\prime},\frac{x_{d+1}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}(x^{\prime})}\right),\,x^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},

(3.3)

so, at the top,
\Sigma
, we have
y_{d+1}=1
.
Thus, in new co-ordinates, the Lipschitz boundary
\Sigma
of the domain
\Omega
is transformed to the smooth boundary of the domain
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
:
y^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},y_{d+1}=1.

We denote by
x=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(y)
the mapping inverse to
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
(it, obviously, exists and it is the inverse stretching along the
y_{d+1}
variable near
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\times\{1\}
). The mapping
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is is a bilipschitz mapping of
\Omega
to
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. Its restriction to
\Sigma
is a bilipschitz mapping to
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
. Such changes of variables leave invariant the Sobolev spaces
H^{1}
and for the weak derivatives the usual chain rule holds, see, e.g.,
[
48
]
, Theorem 2.2.2.

The observation, crucial for our further reasoning is the following.

Lemma 3.2
.

(1)

The components of the mappings
\boldsymbol{\Phi},

\boldsymbol{\Psi}
are continuous, their first order derivatives in
x^{\prime}
, resp.,
y^{\prime}
variables and all their derivatives in
{x_{d+1}},
resp.,
y_{d+1}
variables are bounded functions, smooth in

resp.,
y_{d+1}
variable; they are continuous as functions of these variables with values in

, resp. in

(2)

The Jacobian matrices of the mappings
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
and

are bounded; their entries are continuous in
y_{d+1},
resp.,
x_{d+1},
variables as functions with values in
L_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
, resp. in
L_{\infty}(\Sigma)
.

All these properties follow automatically from the definition of the mapping
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
and the chain rule for derivatives, taking into account that the first order derivatives of a Lipschitz function are bounded.

4.
Transformations and approximation of the P-S operator.

In the course of the proof of our main result we will often transform operators under study while preserving their spectra, so that the initial and the approximating operator can be comfortably compared. Our considerations take place in the domain
\Omega
with cylindrical exit, as presented in the previous section. Having the quadratic forms
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{0}
,
{\mathbf{a}}_{0}
, we find out what happens with them under the transformation
\boldsymbol{\Psi}.

4.1.
Transformation of quadratic forms

We study here how the quadratic forms defining the spectrum of the Poincare-Steklov problem transform under the change of variables, as described in Sect.
3
; we set here
v(y)=u(x),

y=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(x)
,
x=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(y)
,
x\in\Omega,

y\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}.

First, we consider the quadratic form

\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{0}[u]=\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}[v]\equiv\int_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}\rho_{0}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(y))|v(y^{\prime},1)|^{2}|\det J(y^{\prime},1)|d\mu_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}(y^{\prime}),

where
J(y^{\prime},1)
is the Jacobian matrix of the mapping
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
and
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
is now the natural measure on
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},
a smooth surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
.
This quadratic form can be written as

\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}[v]=\int_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}{\rho}(y^{\prime})|v(y^{\prime},1)|^{2}d\mu_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}(y^{\prime}),

with density
{\rho}(y^{\prime})=\rho_{0}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(y^{\prime},1))|\det J(y^{\prime},1)|
.

The quadratic form
{\mathbf{a}}_{0}[u],
see (
2.1
), is transformed to

{\mathbf{a}}_{0}[u]\equiv{{\mathbf{a}}}[v]=\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d+1}\check{a}_{j,k}(y)\partial_{j}v(y)\overline{\partial_{k}{v(y)}}dy+\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\check{{\mathbf{v}}}(y)|v(y)|^{2}dy,

(4.1)

v\in H^{1}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}).

The transformed coefficients
\check{a}_{j,k}(y),\check{{\mathbf{v}}}(y)
, in the quadratic forms (
4.1
), can be calculated, using (
3.3
), but we do not need here their particular explicit expression at this point. What is important is the kind of dependence of these coefficients
on the variables
y_{k}
.

Recall that the coefficients
a_{j,k}(x)
are bounded and are continuous at
\Sigma
in the sense of (
1.7
). This property persists after the continuous change of variables
x=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(y)
, so the functions
a_{j,k}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(y))
are continuous at the new, smooth, boundary
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
in the sense of (
1.7
). Next, to obtain
\check{a}_{j,k}(y)
, we need to multiply
a_{j,k}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(y))
by some algebraic combinations of derivatives of the mapping
\boldsymbol{\Psi},
namely, the derivatives of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
, appearing in the chain rule when passing from
x
-derivatives to
y
-derivatives and also by the Jacobian of the mapping
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
arising in the passage from
dx
in the integral to
dy
. The multiplication by these derivatives which are bounded but, generally, not continuous, destroys the continuity of the boundary values of
a_{j,k}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(y))
for
y\in\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
. However, these derivatives are still continuous in
y_{d+1}
variable as functions with values in
L_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
. We introduce the following definition.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
q(y)=q(y^{\prime},y_{d+1})
be a bounded measurable function in the variables
y^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},

y_{d+1}\in(1-\delta^{\prime},1].
Let
{{\mathbf{q}}}(y^{\prime})\in L_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
be fixed. Denote by
{{\mathbf{q}}^{\diamond}}(y)\equiv{{\mathbf{q}}^{\diamond}}(y^{\prime},y_{d+1})
the continuation of
{{\mathbf{q}}}(y^{\prime})
as a function not depending on
y_{d+1},

{{\mathbf{q}}^{\diamond}}(y^{\prime},y_{d+1}):={{\mathbf{q}}}(y^{\prime}),

y^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}.
If for any
y^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\|q-{\mathbf{q}}^{\diamond}\|_{L_{\infty}(B((y^{\prime},1),\delta)}=0,

(4.2)

we say that
q(y)
has
L_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
limit
{{\mathbf{q}}}(y^{\prime})
at
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
in the variable
y_{d+1}\to 1
. If this is the case, we redefine the initial function
q(y)
on
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},
by setting
q(y^{\prime},1)={\mathbf{q}}(y^{\prime})
, thus changing
q
on a set of
d+1
-dimensional measure
0
. After this, we say that
q
is
L_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
-continuous at
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}.

One can easily see that the product of a function
L_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
-continuous at
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
and a function continuous at
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
is again a function
L_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
-continuous at
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}.

Therefore, by Lemma
3.2
, the coefficients
\check{a}_{j,k}
of the quadratic form
{\mathbf{a}}[v]
in (
4.1
) are
L_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
-continuous at
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}.
This ’very week’ continuity property proves to be sufficient for our approximation construction.

We recall that the ratio of quadratic forms
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{0}/{\mathbf{a}}_{0}
defines an operator whose spectrum coincides with the one of the P-S operator. The domain of the quadratic forms is
H^{1}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})
. Therefore, our transformations can be summarized in the following way.

Proposition 4.2
.

The spectrum of the variational Poincaré-Steklov problem (
1.3
) coincides with the spectrum of the ratio

\frac{{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}[v]}{{{\mathbf{a}}}[v]},\,v\in H^{1}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}),

(4.3)

in a domain
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
with smooth boundary
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
, and where the quadratic form
{\mathbf{a}}[v]
is elliptic and has bounded coefficients, which are
L_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
- continuous in
y_{d+1}
variable at the boundary
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\partial\boldsymbol{\Omega}.

From now on, we may forget about the initial Lipschitz problem and study, from scratch, the eigenvalue distribution for the ratio (
4.3
). To simplify notations, we drop further on the ’check’ over the symbols and, again, denote the main domain by
\Omega
and its, now smooth, boundary by
\Sigma
(
=\Sigma\times\{1\}
in our
y
co-ordinates). Recall that we have paid for the smoothness of the boundary by weakening the continuity property of the coefficient matrix at the boundary, from continuity to
L_{\infty}(\Sigma)
continuity. In the notations of the quadratic forms
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{0},

{\mathbf{a}}_{0}
we drop the subscript ’0’.

4.2.
An operator representation of the eigenvalue problem.

We recall that we consider the case of a non-negative function
\rho.

The quadratic form
{\mathbf{a}}[v]
with domain
H^{1}(\Omega)
defines the self-adjoint positive ’Neumann’ operator
{\mathbf{T}}={\mathbf{T}}_{\mathfrak{a}}
in
L_{2}(\Omega).
This elliptic operator acts, formally, as

{\mathbf{T}}v\equiv{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathfrak{a}}v=-\sum_{j,k}\partial_{j}a_{j,k}\partial_{k}v+{\mathbf{v}}v,

in the sense of distributions, but its domain is rather hard to describe due to the lack of continuity of the coefficients. Fortunately, neither the domain nor the exact action are needed in our reasoning.
Instead, since
{\mathbf{T}}
is a positive self-adjoint elliptic operator with bounded coefficients, the domain of the square root
{\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
can be described explicitly: it coincides with
H^{1}(\Omega)
and
{\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
is an isomorphism from
H^{1}(\Omega)
to
L_{2}(\Omega)
.

It was already stated that we are free in the choice of the function
{\mathbf{v}}
. Now we fix this choice, supposing that
{\mathbf{v}}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
(after the above co-ordinates change).

Since
{\mathbf{a}}[v]=({\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}v,{\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}v),
we can set
f={\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}v\in L_{2}(\Omega)
and therefore the ratio (
4.3
)
transforms to

\frac{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}[{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}f]}{\|f\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}=\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|(\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}f)(y^{\prime})|^{2}\rho(y^{\prime})d\mu_{\Sigma}(y^{\prime})}{\|f\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}},\,f\in L_{2}(\Omega).

(4.4)

Here and further on, we denote by
\gamma:f\mapsto\gamma f
the trace operator acting as the restriction to
\Sigma
of a function
f
defined on the domain
\Omega
with cylindric exit
C
having the boundary lid
\Sigma
identified with
\Sigma\times\{1\}
. We recall that the boundary
\Sigma
is smooth. Therefore, this restriction is known to act as a bounded operator from
H^{s}(\Omega)
to
H^{s-1/2}(\Sigma)
for all
positive

s

except the half-integer ones
(
s\in{\mathbb{N}}+\frac{1}{2}).
In particular, this implies that the operator
\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is bounded as acting from
L_{2}(\Omega)
to
L_{2}(\Sigma)
(and even to
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Sigma)
). Following Sect.2, we restrict ourselves to
\rho\geq 0
and
\rho\in L_{\infty},
therefore, the operator
\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}:L_{2}(\Omega)\to L_{2}(\Sigma)
is bounded as well.

As a result, the numerator in (
4.4
) can be written as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\rho}}[{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}f]}=(\rho(y^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}f,\rho(y^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}f)_{L_{2}(\Sigma)}=

\displaystyle\left(\left[\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{*}[\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}]f,f\right)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}.

In this way, we have reduced our spectral problem to the study of the spectrum of the self-adjoint operator

{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathfrak{a}}=\left[\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{*}\left[\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right],

acting in
L_{2}(\Omega)
.
Since the nonzero discrete spectrum of the product does not change under the cyclic permutation of the factors,
the nonzero eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathfrak{a}}
are the same as the ones of
the self-adjoint operator

{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathfrak{a}}\equiv\left[\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]\left[\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{*},

(4.5)

acting in
L_{2}(\Sigma).

It is more convenient to have a somewhat different representation of the operator
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathfrak{a}}.

Lemma 4.3
.

The following equality holds

{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathfrak{a}}=\left[\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]\left[\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{*}=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma(\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-1})^{*}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma(\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-1})^{*}.

(4.6)

Proof.

We start with the equality

[\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}]{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}:L_{2}(\Omega)\to L_{2}(\Sigma),

(4.7)

where we have the product of two bounded operators on the left. Next we take adjoint of both parts in (
4.7
),

{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}[\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}]^{*}=(\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-1})^{*}:L_{2}(\Sigma)\to L_{2}(\Omega).

(4.8)

This is a bounded operator acting from
L_{2}(\Sigma)
to
H^{1}(\Omega)
, again a product of two bounded operators. Therefore, the restriction operator
\gamma
is well defined on the range of the operator in (
4.8
) and we can apply
\gamma
on the left, and then multiply by the bounded function
\rho^{\frac{1}{2}},

which gives us (
4.6
).
∎

4.3.
Smooth approximation

Our study of P-S eigenvalues will be based upon a special smooth approximation of the coefficient matrix
\mathfrak{a}.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}\in L_{\infty}(\Omega)
be a Hermitian matrix-function, continuous at
\Sigma
as a function of
y_{d+1}
variable with values in
L_{\infty}(\Sigma)
in the sense of Definition
4.2
. Then
for any
\epsilon>0
and
p<\infty,
there exist a
C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
-smooth matrix-function
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}(y),

y\in\bar{\Omega},
such that the following conditions are satisfied

(1)

\|\mathfrak{a}(.,1)-\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}(.,1)\|_{L_{p}(\Sigma)}<\epsilon
;

(2)

the function
\mathfrak{a}(y)-\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}(y)
is continuous at
\Sigma
as a function of
y_{d+1}
variable with values in
L_{\infty}(\Sigma);

(3)

\|\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq c\|\mathfrak{a}\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}
;

(4)

if the matrix
\mathfrak{a}
is uniformly positive in
\Omega,
then, additionally,
\|\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq c\|\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}.

with the constant
c
not depending on
\mathfrak{a}.

The condition (3) means that the
L_{\infty}
norm for the approximating matrix can be arranged to be not depending on
\epsilon
, being controlled, by the
L_{\infty}
norm of the initial matrix
\mathfrak{a};
by condition (4), in the elliptic case, the same can be arranged for the ellipticity constant of the approximating matrix. Note that the approximation quality in Lemma
4.4
is required only at the boundary
\Sigma
.

Proof.

We discuss the ellipticity case,
\mathfrak{a}(y)\geq C>0,
first. Consider the constant matrix
\mathfrak{a}_{1}(y)=C_{1}\mathbf{I}_{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
,
where
\|\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}^{-1}\leq C_{1}\leq\|\mathfrak{a}\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}.
Next, to construct
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}(y)
near the boundary, we take
\delta>0
such that
for any ball

the inequality

\|\mathfrak{a}(y)-\mathfrak{a}^{\diamond}(y)\|_{L_{\infty}(B(y^{\prime},\delta))}<\epsilon
holds (where
{\mathfrak{a}}^{\diamond}(y)
is the continuation of
\mathfrak{a}(y^{\prime},1)
from
\Sigma
to the
\delta
-neighborhood of
\Sigma,

{\mathfrak{a}}^{\diamond}(y^{\prime},y_{d+1})=\mathfrak{a}(y^{\prime},1).
)
Next we approximate the matrix
\mathfrak{a}(.,1)
by a smooth matrix
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{0}\in L_{p}(\Sigma)\cap L_{\infty}(\Sigma)

such that
\|\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{0}(.)-\mathfrak{a}(.,1)\|_{L_{p}(\Sigma)}<\epsilon,
thus the condition 1) in Lemma
4.4
is satisfied. Also we require that

\|\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{0}\|_{L_{\infty}(\Sigma)}\leq C\|\mathfrak{a}\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)},\,\|\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{0}^{-1}\|_{L_{\infty}(\Sigma)}\leq C^{\prime}\|\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)};

(4.9)

all these conditions can be satisfied, for example, by setting
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{0}(.)={\mathbf{V}}(\tau)\mathfrak{a}(.,1)
, where
{\mathbf{V}}(\tau)
is the heat semigroup on
\Sigma,
since
{\mathbf{V}}(\tau)\to\boldsymbol{1}
strongly in
L_{p}(\Sigma),

p<\infty
as
\tau\to+0
and
{\mathbf{V}}(\tau)
is bounded in
L_{\infty},

\tau\geq 0.

We consider now the matrix
{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{0}}^{\diamond}(y),
the continuation of
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{0}(y^{\prime}),
namely
{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{0}}^{\diamond}(y^{\prime},y_{d+1})=\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{0}(y^{\prime}).
This smooth matrix, not depending on
y_{d+1}
is, of course, continuous in the
y_{d+1}
variable as a function with values in

. Therefore, the difference,
\mathfrak{a}(y)-{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{0}}^{\diamond}(y)
is also continuous at
\Sigma
as a function of

with values in
L_{\infty}(\Sigma)
. This grants the condition 2) in Lemma. Conditions 3) and 4) are satisfied due to (

). Finally, we glue together the matrix

defined in a neighborhood of
\Sigma
and the matrix
\mathfrak{a}_{1}(y)
defined outside such neighborhood by means of proper cut-off functions, and this matrix satisfies all conditions of Lemma.

In the case when the matrix
\mathfrak{a}
is not supposed to be uniformly positive definite, the simplified reasoning succeeds: namely we just skip all estimates concerning
\mathfrak{a}^{-1}
, therefore, we may simply set
\mathfrak{a}_{1}(y)=0
.
∎

We will use Lemma
4.4
twice; first time when approximating the elliptic coefficients matrix

where the control of the ellipticity constant is important, and the second time for the approximation of the matrix

when proving the required operator convergence of

to
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathfrak{a}}
.

4.4.
The approximating operator

Consider, for a given
\epsilon>0
, the approximating coefficient matrix
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}
constructed in Section 4.3 for the elliptic coefficient matrix
\mathfrak{a}
and the corresponding elliptic differential operator
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}={\mathcal{L}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}},

with the same zero order term

{\mathbf{v}}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}).

With the coefficient matrix
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}
we associate the Neumann operator
\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}={\mathbf{T}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}},
by means of the quadratic form
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}}[v],

v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.

Since the boundary of
\Omega
is smooth, as well as the coefficients matrix
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}
, some more can be said about the properties of the operator (
4.5
) for this matrix.
The corresponding compact operator, which we denote by
{{\mathbf{H}}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}},
has, by Lemma
4.3
, the form

{{\mathbf{H}}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}}=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma(\gamma{\mathbf{T}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}}^{-1})^{*}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.10)

First of all, for the eigenvalues of this operator, which coincide with eigenvalues of the corresponding
N\!\!\!D
operator, the asymptotic formula holds, of the form (
1.6
), (
1.4
), (
1.5
), with the matrix
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}
replacing
\mathfrak{a},
due to the existing results, see Theorem
1.1
.
Next, since the coefficients of
{\mathbf{T}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}}
are smooth, the domain and action of the operator
{\mathbf{T}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}}
can be described explicitly. Namely, by the standard elliptic regularity results, the domain
{\mathcal{D}}(\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}})
of
\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}
is the subspace in the Sobolev space
H^{2}(\Omega)
, consisting of functions
v
satisfying the classical Neumann boundary conditions,
\sum_{k}\tilde{a}_{j,k}\nu_{k}\partial_{j}v|_{\partial\Omega}=0,
where
\nu_{k}
are the components of the unit normal vector to
\Sigma=\partial\Omega.

Lemma 4.5
.

For a smooth matrix
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}},
for any
f\in L_{2}(\Sigma)
, the function
v=(\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1})^{*}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}f
satisfies in
\Omega

the elliptic equation

\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}v\equiv\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}(\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1})^{*}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}f=0\,\,\mathrm{in}\,\,\Omega.

(4.11)

Proof.

Let
g\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
be a smooth function with compact support in
\Omega.
Consider the sesquilinear form
{\mathbf{I}}[f,g]=(\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}(\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1})^{*}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}f,g)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}.
The function
g
belongs to the domain of the self-adjoint operator
\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}},
therefore,

{\mathbf{I}}[f,g]=((\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1})^{*}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}f,\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}g)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=(\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}f,\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}g})_{L_{2}(\Sigma)}=(f,\rho\gamma g)_{L_{2}(\Sigma)}.

(4.12)

Since
g=0
on
\Sigma,
the last expression in (
4.12
) is zero. Therefore, the function
\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}(\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1})^{*}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}f
is orthogonal to
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega),
and this implies that
\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}(\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1})^{*}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}f
is zero in
\Omega.

∎

4.5.
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathfrak{a}}
as a pseudodifferential operator

Here we establish an important property of the approximating operator
{\mathbf{H}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}},
needed further for evaluating the rate of approximation of spectra.

Proposition 4.6
.

For a smooth approximating matrix
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}
and a smooth weight
\rho
, the operator
{\mathbf{H}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}}
is an order
-1
pseudodifferential operator on the boundary
\Sigma.

Proof.

When finding, in the smooth case, an expression for the operator
{\mathbf{H}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}},
we study its component
(\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1})^{*}
first.
Recall that
\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}
is the inverse of the realization of the operator
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}
with Neumann boundary conditions,
\gamma_{1}u=0
, where
\gamma_{1}
is the conormal derivative. Continue the coefficients
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}
outside
\Omega
in a smooth way. We denote by
{\mathbf{R}}
the fundamental solution for
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}
; so that
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}{\mathbf{R}}-1,

{\mathbf{R}}{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}-1
are infinitely smoothing operators;
{\mathbf{R}}
is the order
-2
pseudodifferential operator with symbol
{\mathbf{r}}(y,\eta)
having the principal term
{\mathbf{r}}_{-2}(y,\eta)=\boldsymbol{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}}_{y}(\eta)^{-1},
where
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}}_{y}(\eta)=\sum_{j,k}\tilde{a}_{j,k}(y)\eta_{j}\eta_{k}
is the principal symbol of the operator
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}
. Let
d>1
first. Then the operator
{\mathbf{R}}
acts on functions on
\Omega
as an integral operator with kernel
R(y,z-y)
having leading singularity of order
1-d
at the diagonal
y=z
. Namely, the principal term,
R_{1-d}(y,y-z),
the leading singularity in
R(y,z-y)
is the Fourier transform of the principal symbol
{\mathbf{r}}_{-2}(y,\eta)
,

R_{1-d}(y,y-z)={\mathcal{F}}_{\eta\to y-z}{\mathbf{r}}_{-2}(y,\eta).

For the dimension
d+1=2
, the kernel
R(y,z-y)
has logarithmic singularity at the diagonal.

Let
f
be a function in
L_{2}(\Omega)
. In order to find a representation for
u=\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}f,
we set
u_{0}=\int_{\Omega}R(y,y-z)f(z)dz
. This function satisfies the equation
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}u_{0}=f
in
\Omega
but not the Neumann boundary condition
\gamma_{1}u=0
, where
\gamma_{1}
is the conormal derivative at the boundary, corresponding to the elliptic operator
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}
. We construct the correction
u_{1},
such that
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}u_{1}=0,

\gamma_{1}u_{1}=-\gamma_{1}u_{0}
. To do this, we consider the
Green function

{\mathcal{G}}_{1}(y,z)
for the Neumann problem, in other words, the integral kernel of the solution operator
\boldsymbol{G}_{1}
of the Neumann problem for
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}
. This means that for a smooth function
h
on
\Sigma
,

{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\boldsymbol{G}_{1}h:={\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}_{y}\int_{\Sigma}{\mathcal{G}}_{1}(y,z)h(z)dz=0,\,y\in\Omega,

(4.13)

and

\lim_{y\to y_{0}\in\Sigma}\gamma_{1,y}\int_{\Sigma}{\mathcal{G}}_{1}(y,z)h(z)dz=h(y_{0}).

(4.14)

The operator
\boldsymbol{G}_{1}:C^{\infty}(\Sigma)\to C^{\infty}(\Omega)
is a Poisson operator in the Boutet-de-Monvel algebra.
By (
4.14
), it satisfies
\gamma_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{1}:C^{\infty}(\Sigma)\to C^{\infty}(\Sigma)=\boldsymbol{1},
i.e., it gives the identity operator.
Therefore the solution of the boundary problem
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}u=f
in
\Omega,

\gamma_{1}u=0
on
\Sigma
can be expressed as

\displaystyle u(y)=u_{0}(y)+u_{1}(y)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}R(y,y-z)f(z)dz-\int_{\Sigma}{\mathcal{G}}_{1}(y,z)\gamma_{1}\left(\int_{\Omega}R(y,y-z)f(z)dz\right)

\displaystyle=({\mathbf{R}}f)(y)-(\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\gamma_{1}{\mathbf{R}}f)(y).

As a result, the operator
\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}
can be represented as

\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}={\mathbf{R}}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\gamma_{1}{\mathbf{R}}=(1-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\gamma_{1}){\mathbf{R}};

such representation, in a somewhat different setting, can be found, e.g., in
[
21
]
, Theorem 9.20. There, after the standard straightening of the boundary, the operator
{\mathbf{R}}
is treated as a truncated pseudodifferential operator,

{\mathbf{R}}=\chi_{\Omega}\mathfrak{R}{\mathbf{e}}_{\Omega},

(4.15)

where
{\mathbf{e}}_{\Omega}
is the operator of extension by zero of a function in
\Omega
to the whole
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
,
\mathfrak{R}
is a pseudodifferential operator in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
with symbol
{\mathbf{r}}(y,\eta)
and
\chi_{\Omega}
is the restriction of functions in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
to
\Omega.

We also need an expression for the operator
\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}_{0}^{-1}
, the resolvent of the
Dirichlet
problem for the equation
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}u=f
in
\Omega.
In a similar way,

\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}_{0}}{}^{-1}={\mathbf{R}}-\boldsymbol{G}_{0}\gamma{\mathbf{R}}=(1-\boldsymbol{G}_{0}\gamma){\mathbf{R}},

(4.16)

where
\boldsymbol{G}_{0}
is the Poisson operator (the Green function) solving the nonhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary problem for
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}
in
\Omega
:

{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}u=0,\,\gamma u=h;
this means that
\gamma\boldsymbol{G}_{0}=\boldsymbol{1}.

We pass now to
\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}.
Since
\gamma{\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}_{0}{}^{-1}}=0,

we can write

\displaystyle\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}=\gamma(\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}-\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}_{0}{}^{-1})=\gamma(\boldsymbol{G}_{0}\gamma{\mathbf{R}}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\gamma_{1}{\mathbf{R}})

(4.17)

\displaystyle=\gamma\boldsymbol{G}_{0}\gamma{\mathbf{R}}-\gamma\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\gamma_{1}{\mathbf{R}}=(\gamma\boldsymbol{G}_{0}\gamma-\gamma\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\gamma_{1}){\mathbf{R}}.

We recall now that
\gamma\boldsymbol{G}_{0}=\boldsymbol{1}.
Further on, the function
u_{1}=\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\gamma_{1}{\mathbf{R}}f
satisfies the equation
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}u_{1}=0
in
\Omega
, therefore
\gamma u_{1}
and
\gamma_{1}u_{1}
are connected by the Neumann-to Dirichlet operator
N\!\!\!D
,
\gamma u_{1}=N\!\!\!D\gamma_{1}u_{1}
. We set all this into (
4.17
) and obtain

\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1}=(\gamma-N\!\!\!D\gamma_{1}){\mathbf{R}}.

(4.18)

Consequently,

(\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1})^{*}=(\gamma{\mathbf{R}})^{*}-(N\!\!\!D\gamma_{1}{\mathbf{R}})^{*}.

Finally, we have

\gamma(\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1})^{*}=\gamma(\gamma{\mathbf{R}})^{*}-\gamma(N\!\!\!D\gamma_{1}{\mathbf{R}})^{*}.

(4.19)

Both terms on the right in (
4.19
) belong to the Boutet-de-Monvel algebra, see e.g.,
[
21
]
, where the composition rules are described in detail. In this setting,
{\mathbf{R}}
is a truncated pseudodifferential operator of order
-2
,
\gamma
is a trace operator of zero order, further,
(\gamma{\mathbf{R}})^{*}
is a Poisson operator, and as a result,
\gamma(\gamma{\mathbf{R}})^{*}
is a pseudodifferential operator of order
-1.
Similarly,
\gamma_{1}
is a trace operator of order
1
, the Neumann-to Dirichlet operator
N\!\!\!D
is an order
-1
pseudodifferential operator on the boundary, therefore, the second term in (
4.19
) is a pseudodifferential operator of order
-1
as well. Its principal symbol can be expressed in a standard way, algebraically, via the principal symbols of
{\mathbf{R}}
and
N\!\!\!D
, as we will see in Sect.7. Finally, the multiplication by the smooth function
\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}
produces, again, a pseudodifferential operator.
∎

5.
Operator perturbations

Our approach to establishing the asymptotic formula for Steklov eigenvalues, similarly to
[
37
]
, is based upon the operator approximation.
This approximation idea was successfully used by M.S. Agranovich in
[
2
]
on the base of the variational setting of the problem. In fact, for more smooth boundaries, the ones of class
C^{1,1}
, a similar perturbational approach was even used in
[
39
]
, the earliest paper on the Steklov eigenvalue asymptotics.

Namely, if the coefficients matrix
\mathfrak{a}(y)
is continuous, it can be approximated in
C(\Omega)
, both from above and from below, by smooth elliptic matrices
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{\pm}(y)
, and the required closeness of spectra of operators
{\mathbf{T}}
and
\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}
follows in
[
39
]
and in
[
2
]
from rather simple monotonicity estimates. The weakening of conditions imposed on the boundary in
this
paper, actually, the passage from the
continuous
derivatives of the function
\psi
defining the boundary to the function having only
bounded
derivatives, seemingly, a minor one, is, in fact, rather essential, the resulting coefficients
a_{j,k}
are not continuous any more and they cannot be approximated in the
C
metric by smooth ones. Instead, we use an approximation in a weaker,
L_{p}
sense, as in Lemma
4.4
, which turns out to be sufficient.

5.1.
Approximation of the operator

In this section, we find the expression for the difference of operators describing the eigenvalues of the N-to-D operators. Let
\mathfrak{a},\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}
be the matrices of coefficients of the operators
{\mathcal{L}},\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}
, described in Sect.4, so that
\mathfrak{a},\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}{}^{-1}
belong to
L_{\infty}(\Omega)
,
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}},\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}|_{\Sigma}-\mathfrak{a}|_{\Sigma}
is small in the sense of Lemma
4.4
.

Consider the operators
{\mathbf{T}},\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}
, the Neumann operators for
{\mathcal{L}},{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Under the above conditions, the following factorization is valid:

{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}-\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}={\mathbf{X}}^{*}{\mathbf{Y}},

(5.1)

where
{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}}
are bounded operators acting from
L_{2}(\Omega)
to
L_{2}(\Omega)\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{d+1}
.

{\mathbf{X}}=\nabla{\mathbf{T}}^{-1},{\mathbf{Y}}=\mathfrak{b}(y)\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1},

(5.2)

with
\mathfrak{b}=(\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}-\mathfrak{a}).

Proof.

Essentially, our Lemma
5.1
is an analogy of Lemma 8.1 in
[
13
]
. For the sake of completeness, we present the detailed proof of (
5.1
) in our setting.

The equality (
5.1
) is equivalent to

({\mathbf{T}}^{-1}f,g)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}-(\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}f,g)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}\langle(\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}-\mathfrak{a})\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}f,\nabla{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}g\rangle dy,\,f,g\in L_{2}(\Omega),

(5.3)

where the angle brackets denote the scalar product in
{\mathbb{C}}^{d+1}.

In our conditions, the domains of the operators
{\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}},\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{\frac{1}{2}}
coincide, both are equal to the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\Omega).
It follows, in particular, that
{\mathcal{D}}(\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}})\subset H^{2}(\Omega)\subset{\mathcal{D}}({\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}).
Let
u,v
be arbitrary functions in
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}})={\mathcal{D}}(\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{\frac{1}{2}})=H^{1}(\Omega).
Consider the equality

\displaystyle(\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{\frac{1}{2}}u,\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{\frac{1}{2}}v)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}-({\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u,{\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}v)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=\tilde{{\mathbf{a}}}[u,v]-{\mathbf{a}}[u,v]=

(5.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle dy-\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathfrak{a}\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle(\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}-\mathfrak{a})\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle dy.

We set here
u=\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}f,

v={\mathbf{T}}^{-1}g,
where
f,g
are arbitrary elements in
L_{2}(\Omega)
. These functions
u,v
belong to
H^{1}(\Omega)
, therefore (
5.4
) is satisfied. Such substitution leads to (
5.3
):

(\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u,\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}v)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=(\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1}f,\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1}g)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=(f,{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1}g)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=({{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1}f,g)_{L_{2}(\Omega)},

and, similarly, for
({\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u,{\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}v),

\displaystyle({{\mathbf{T}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u,\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}v)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=({\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1}f,{\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1}g)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=

\displaystyle({\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1}f,{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}g)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=(\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1}f,g)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}.

∎

Using (
5.2
), we arrive at the representation of the difference of the operators
{\mathbf{H}}_{a}
and
{\mathbf{H}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}}
, see (
4.5
), (
4.10
):

{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathfrak{a}}-{\mathbf{H}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}}\equiv\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma(\gamma({\mathbf{T}}^{-1}-\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}))^{*}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma[\gamma{\mathbf{X}}^{*}{\mathbf{Y}}]^{*}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(5.5)

We need a somewhat different representation for the operator in (
5.5
).

Lemma 5.2
.

The following equality is valid

{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathfrak{a}}-{\mathbf{H}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}}=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma{\mathbf{Y}}^{*})(\gamma{\mathbf{X}}^{*})^{*}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(5.6)

Proof.

The operator
\gamma{\mathbf{X}}^{*}{\mathbf{Y}}=(\gamma{\mathbf{X}}^{*})({\mathbf{Y}})
is a product of two bounded operators, therefore,
(\gamma{\mathbf{X}}^{*}{\mathbf{Y}})^{*}={\mathbf{Y}}^{*}(\gamma{\mathbf{X}}^{*})^{*}.
We apply the operator
\gamma
from the left, multiply by the bounded function
\rho^{\frac{1}{2}},
and obtain (
5.6
).
∎

We will use expression (
5.6
) to evaluate the singular numbers of the difference
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathfrak{a}}-{\mathbf{H}}_{\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}}.
Since the function
\rho
is bounded, the multiplication by
\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}
preserves spectral estimates, therefore, it suffices to drop this weight in further estimates.

5.2.
Spectral estimates for the operator
\gamma{\mathbf{X}}^{*}

We represent the operator
{\mathbf{X}}=\nabla{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}
in the following way as a product of two bounded operators,

{\mathbf{X}}=(\nabla{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}){\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}},{\mathbf{X}}^{*}={\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\nabla{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}.

This gives

\gamma{\mathbf{X}}^{*}=(\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})(\nabla{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}.

The operator
(\nabla{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})
is bounded in
L_{2}(\Omega)
since
{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}:L_{2}(\Omega)\to H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\nabla
is bounded as acting from
H^{1}(\Omega)
to
L_{2}(\Omega).
The norm of
\nabla{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is controlled by the ellipticity constant of
{\mathcal{L}}
and does not depend on the approximation
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}
. Therefore,
s
-numbers of the operator
\gamma{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
are majorated by
s
-numbers of
\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}(2d,\gamma{\mathbf{X}}^{*})\leq C{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}(2d,\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

Next we have

n(\lambda,\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})=n(\lambda^{2},(\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}(\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})).

Therefore,

{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}(2d,\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})={\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}(d,(\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}(\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}))

(5.7)

The expression on the right in (
5.7
) characterizes the counting function for the eigenvalues of the ratio

\frac{\|\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}f\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\Sigma)}}{\|f\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\Omega)}},\,f\in L_{2}(\Omega).

(5.8)

We set
f={\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u
,
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
in (
5.8
) and obtain the ratio

\frac{\|\gamma u\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\Sigma)}}{\|{\mathbf{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}}=\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|u(y^{\prime},1)|^{2}d\mu_{\Sigma}(y^{\prime})}{{\mathbf{a}}[u]}\leq\frac{C^{-1}\int_{\Sigma}|u(y^{\prime},1)|^{2}d\mu_{\Sigma}(y^{\prime})}{\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}},\,u\in H^{1}(\Omega),

(5.9)

since
{\mathbf{a}}[u]\geq C\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
, with constant determined by ellipticity constant of
{\mathcal{L}},
the ratio (
5.9
) is majorated by the spectral problem considered in Theorem
2.1
. From this theorem, taking into account (
5.7
), we obtain

{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}(2d,\gamma{\mathbf{T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})\leq C,

(5.10)

with constant
C
depending only on the
L_{\infty}
norm of the matrix
\mathfrak{a}^{-1},
therefore, returning to the original problem, only on the ellipticity constant of the initial operator and the Lipshitz norm of the function
\psi.

6.
Spectral estimates for the operator
\gamma{\mathbf{Y}}^{*}
. Rough estimates

In the study of the second term in the factorization (
5.5
), (
5.6
), namely,
\gamma{\mathbf{Y}}[\mathfrak{b}]^{*}
, where we set
{\mathbf{Y}}[\mathfrak{b}]=\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1},
we will need to obtain an asymptotic singular numbers estimate for
{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}(2d,\gamma{\mathbf{Y}}^{*})=\limsup_{\lambda\to 0}{\lambda^{2d}n(\lambda,\gamma{\mathbf{Y}}^{*})}.
This will be done in two steps. First, in this section, for an
arbitrary
matrix function
\mathfrak{b}
,
L_{\infty}(\Sigma)
-continuous at
\Sigma\times\{1\},
we find an estimate of
{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}(2d,\gamma{\mathbf{Y}}^{*})
in terms of certain integral norm of
\mathfrak{b}(.,1).
The constant in this estimate will, unfortunately, depend, in an uncontrollable manner, on the approximating matrix
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}
. However, this estimate enables us to restrict ourselves to smooth matrices
\mathfrak{b}
, using Lemma
2.2
. Then, in the next section, for a,
now smooth,
matrix
\mathfrak{b}
, we can use the pseudodifferential calculus to establish the
asymptotic estimate
for singular numbers of
\gamma{\mathbf{Y}}^{*},
containing the integral norm of
\mathfrak{b}
but depending now only on the ellipticity constant and some algebraic norm of the approximating matrix
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}
and its inverse, or, what is the same, on these bounds for the initial matrix
\mathfrak{a}
. Finally, we collect our estimates and establish the asymptotic singular numbers bound for
\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{a}-\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}.

The important point here that we have already got rid of the non-smoothness of the coefficients of the operator, the latter stayed behind in the operator
{\mathbf{X}},
therefore our reasoning uses essentially the smoothness of the coefficients
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}(y).

As a preparation, we note that we can suppose that
\mathfrak{b}=0
outside an (arbitrarily small) neighborhood of the boundary
\Sigma
of the domain
\Omega.
This observation has been used many times in papers on our topic, including
[
39
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
41
]
. A simple explanation is that if
{\rm dist}\,(\hbox{{\rm supp}}\,\mathfrak{b},\partial\Omega)>0
then the integral kernel of
\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1}
is a smooth function on the set
\hbox{{\rm supp}}\,\mathfrak{b}\times\Sigma
(which is separated from the diagonal) and the eigenvalues of the corresponding operator decay faster than any power,

{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}(\theta,\gamma(\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1})^{*})=0,\,\mbox{for}\,\mbox{any}\,\theta>0,\,\hbox{{\rm supp}}\,{\mathfrak{b}}\cap\Sigma=\varnothing.

(6.1)

Therefore, we may suppose that
\mathfrak{b}
is supported in a conveniently small neighborhood of
\Sigma
where it possesses the properties discussed in Section
3
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}(y),y\in\Omega,
be a smooth elliptic matrix function and
\mathfrak{b}(y)\in L_{\infty}(\Omega).
Suppose that
\mathfrak{b}
is
L_{\infty}(\Sigma)
- continuous at
\Sigma\times\{1\}
and
\mathfrak{b}
is zero outside some neighborhood of
\Sigma\times\{1\}
.
Then

{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}(2d,\gamma{\mathbf{Y}}[\mathfrak{b}]^{*})\leq C(\tilde{\mathfrak{a}})\|\mathfrak{b}(.,1)\|_{2d+2}^{\frac{d}{2}}\|\mathfrak{b}(.,1)\|_{\infty}^{\frac{d}{2}}.

(6.2)

Proof.

We consider the operator
{\mathbf{Z}}[\mathfrak{b}]=\gamma{\mathbf{Y}}^{*}
, where
{\mathbf{Y}}={\mathbf{Y}}[\mathfrak{b}]=\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1}
.
Since we are studying the singular numbers of
{\mathbf{Z}}[\mathfrak{b}]:L_{2}(\Omega)\to L_{2}(\Sigma)
, we can consider the adjoint operator
{\mathbf{Z}}[\mathfrak{b}]^{*}:L_{2}(\Sigma)\to L_{2}(\Omega)
instead. This operator admits a convenient representation, namely,

{\mathbf{Z}}[\mathfrak{b}]^{*}=(\gamma{\mathbf{Y}}^{*})^{*}=\left[\gamma(\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1})^{*}\right]^{*}=(\mathfrak{b}\nabla)(\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1})^{*}.

(6.3)

In fact, starting with the identity

\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1}=(\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

we obtain

(\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1})^{*}=\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*};

further, we have

\gamma(\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1})^{*}=\left(\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left(\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{*}.

And now we take adjoints,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathfrak{b})^{*}\equiv\left[\gamma(\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1})^{*}\right]^{*}=

\displaystyle\left(\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{**}\left(\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{*}=\mathfrak{b}\nabla\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}=\mathfrak{b}\nabla(\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1})^{*},

since
\mathfrak{b}
is a bounded function.
We consider now the function
w(y)=(\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}{}^{-1})^{*}g\in L_{2}(\Omega)
for
g\in L_{2}(\Sigma).
By Lemma
4.5
, this function satisfies the second order elliptic equation
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}w=0
in
\Omega.
Its restriction to
\Sigma
is
\gamma w=\gamma(\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1})^{*}g
, and, since by Proposition
4.6
the operator
\gamma(\gamma\tilde{{\mathbf{T}}}^{-1})^{*}
is an order
-1
pseudodifferential operator, the inequality holds

\|\gamma w\|_{H^{1}(\Sigma)}\leq C\|g\|_{L_{2}(\Sigma)}.

(6.4)

By the elliptic regularity property, for the solution
w
of the second order elliptic equation
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}w=0
in
\Omega
with the Dirichlet boundary condition
\gamma w
, the Sobolev spaces estimate holds:

\|w\|_{H^{s+\frac{1}{2}}(\Omega)}\asymp\|\gamma w\|_{H^{s}(\Sigma)},

(6.5)

now for
all

s\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1}.

Appendix A
Potential theory approach

We consider here the Poincaré-Steklov problem for the Laplacian, first, in dimension
d+1\geq 3.
Consider the single and double layer potential operators on the Lipschitz boundary
\Sigma,

\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt:L_{2}(\Sigma)\to L_{2}(\Sigma),\,\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt:f(x)\mapsto\int_{\Sigma}R(x-y)f(y)d\mu_{\Sigma}(y),

(A.1)

and

\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt:L_{2}(\Sigma)\to L_{2}(\Sigma),\,\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt:f(x)\mapsto\int_{\Sigma}\partial_{\nu(y)}R(x-y)d\mu_{\Sigma}(y),

(A.2)

where
R
is the fundamental solution for the Laplacian in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}.

The N-to-D operator
N\!\!\!D
is expressed via these operators as

N\!\!\!D=\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt(\frac{1}{2}+\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt)^{-1},

(A.3)

see, e.g.,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
21
]
.
To be more exact, the
N\!\!\!D
operator is considered here on functions, orthogonal in
L_{2}(\Sigma)
to constants. For a smooth surface
\Sigma,
both potential operators are order
-1
pseudodifferential operators on
\Sigma.
Being considered on functions orthogonal to constants, the operator
(\frac{1}{2}+\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt)
is invertible and

(\frac{1}{2}+\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt)^{-1}=2-2\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt(\frac{1}{2}+\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt)^{-1}.

(A.4)

This means that

N\!\!\!D=2\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt-2\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt(\frac{1}{2}+\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt)^{-1},

(A.5)

Therefore the difference between the N-to-D operator
N\!\!\!D
and twice the single layer potential
2\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt
is an order
-2
pseudodifferential operator, and, by standard perturbational arguments, the eigenvalue asymptotics of
N\!\!\!D
is the same in the leading term as the asymptotics for the single layer potential
2\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt
; finding the latter is a simple exercise for a smooth surface.

If
\Sigma
is not infinitely smooth, for example, belongs to
C^{1+\alpha}
,
\alpha>0
(such surfaces are often called Lyapunov ones), the above scheme still works. First of all, discarding the pseudodifferential approach, one should return to considering the operators
S
and
D
as integral operators with kernel having singularity at the diagonal. Here, on the one hand, the eigenvalue asymptotics of the operator
S
is known since the results of M.Sh. Birman and M.Z. Solomyak on general weakly polar integral operators, see
[
12
]
, where such eigenvalue asymptotics was found under rather weak regularity conditions. At the same time, the operator
D
is now a singular integral operator, but for a surface of class
C^{1+\alpha}
, it is still compact. Thus, the operator
N\!\!\!D
becomes, by (
A.5
), a relatively compactly perturbed
single layer operator, and, again, by standard methods (say, using the Keldysh perturbation theorem) we arrive at the Weyl asymptotics for the
N\!\!\!D
operator.

So, it looks like this is not that long way remains to Lipschitz surfaces. However some serious obstacles appear. First of all, for a Lipschitz surface, the double layer integral operator
D
is not obviously bounded. In fact, it required a hard work to prove that it is bounded in
L_{2}(\Sigma)
and, moreover, that the operator
\frac{1}{2}+\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt
is invertible. Further on, it turned out that
D
is not necessarily compact; even in the two-dimensional case, in the presence of a corner point of a piece-wise smooth boundary, it has a fragment of essential spectrum near zero. As for the single layer potential
\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt,
the results of
[
12
]
are, by themselves, not sufficient to establish eigenvalue asymptotics. However, in
[
6
]
, M.S.Agranovich and B.A.Amosov succeeded in proving order sharp two-sided eigenvalue estimates for potential type operators
on Lipschitz surfaces, which, in their turn, produce two-sided eigenvalue estimates for
N\!\!\!D.
Moreover, a localization technic was developed in
[
6
]
. It turned out that if the non-smooth singularities of the surface
\Sigma
are localized in the sense that the surface is smooth outside a closed set of zero measure, then the contribution of these singularities of
\Sigma
both to the behavior of eigenvalues of
S
and to the non-compactness of
D
is negligible, and the Weyl asymptotic formula for the P-S problem was proved in this setting.

In that period, after M.S.Agranovich started popularizing the problem on Steklov eigenvalue asymptotics for Lipschitz surfaces without additional restrictions, Prof. Grigory Tashchiyan and the author of this paper managed in 2006 to dispose of one of complications. Namely, in
[
37
]
, we used the approximation of Lipschitz surfaces by smooth ones, in the sense of a certain integral norm, to prove that the corresponding potential type integral operators, properly relocated, so that they act on one and the same surface, can be compared, their difference becomes small in the spectral sense (see Lemma
6.3
), and the passage to limit in formulas for spectral asymptotics becomes possible. Although this result did not solve the Lipschitz Steklov eigenvalue asymptotics problem, it solved a closely related one. Namely, in the
interface
spectral problem, where the spectral parameter is placed, for solutions of the (Laplace, in the simplest case) equation at the
jump
of the normal derivative across the Lipschitz surface
S\subset\Omega:

\displaystyle\Omega=\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}\cup S,\,S=\overline{\Omega_{1}}\cap\overline{\Omega_{2}},\,\Delta u=0,\,\mbox{in}\,\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2},

(A.6)

\displaystyle\lambda\left[\frac{\partial u}{\partial_{\nu(x)}}\right]_{x}=u(x),\,x\in S,

the solution involves only the single layer potential operator
S
and the Weyl asymptotics for the Lipschitz surface is justified. Rather optimistically, we hoped that the Steklov problem will succumb as well.

One of the approaches we tried to explore was the following. Although we know that the double layer operator
D
is not expected to be compact, probably, the
composition

D

S
can be shown to be
weaker
than
S
itself. The notion ’weaker’ should imply that the singular numbers of
D

S
decay faster than the ones of
S
,

s_{n}(\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt)=o(s_{n}(\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt))=o(n^{-\frac{1}{d}}).

(A.7)

In the eigenvalue studies, there are a lot of occasions when the multiplication by a non-compact operator nevertheless improves the rate of decay of the eigenvalues of the product.

Hypothesis
. Consider the composition
D

S
of single and double layer potentials. Recall that
S
is the integral operator with kernel
C|x-y|^{1-d}
over a Lipschitz surface of dimension
d>1.
Let
{\mathcal{T}}(x,y)
be the integral kernel of the composition
S

D
,

{\mathcal{T}}(x,y)=\int_{\Sigma}|x-z|^{1-d}\partial_{\nu(z)}|z-y|^{1-d}dz.

(A.8)

Suppose, hopefully, that

{\mathcal{T}}(x,y)|x-y|^{d-1}=o(1)\,\mbox{as }\,y\to x,\,\mbox{uniformly }\,\mbox{in}\,x\in\Sigma.

(A.9)

Proposition A.1
.

Suppose that
d\geq 3
and (
A.9
) is satisfied. Then
\lambda_{j}(\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt)=o(\lambda_{j}(\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt)).

Proof.

For a fixed
\epsilon
, we find a neighborhood
\mathfrak{U}_{\epsilon}\subset\Sigma\times\Sigma
of the diagonal

such that
|{\mathcal{T}}(x,y)|<\epsilon|x-y|^{1-d}
for
(x,y)\in\mathfrak{U}_{\epsilon}

which equals zero outside

and equals

The operator
\sc\mbox{T}\hskip 1.0pt=\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt\sc\mbox{D}\hskip 1.0pt

\sc\mbox{T}\hskip 1.0pt_{\epsilon},

\sc\mbox{T}\hskip 1.0pt_{\epsilon}^{\prime}

The second operator has a bounded kernel, therefore, in particular, belongs to the Hilbert-Schmidt class, and its singular values decay at least as
\lambda_{j}(\sc\mbox{T}\hskip 1.0pt_{\epsilon}^{\prime})=O(j^{-\frac{1}{2}}),
i.e. faster than the eigenvalues of
\sc\mbox{S}\hskip 1.0pt.
As for the first operator,
\sc\mbox{T}\hskip 1.0pt_{\epsilon},
we can apply the result by G.Kostometov,
[
28
]

[
6
]

{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}(\theta,\sc\mbox{T}\hskip 1.0pt)\leq C\|\phi\|_{L_{\infty}}^{\theta},\theta=\frac{d}{d-{\mathbf{k}}.}

(A.10)

In our case, in local co-ordinates, for

and

A.10

we arrive at the required estimate.
∎

This estimate leads to the justification of the Weyl asymptotics, provided (
A.9
).

Using somewhat finer estimates, one covers the case of

(excluded in the estimates in
[

]
,
[

]
, since here the condition

is not met). The hypothesis itself, meanwhile, stays unresolved, although for some particular nonregular surfaces we were able to justify it.

Appendix B
Relaxing the smoothness conditions.

Another possible approach consists in finding the conditions on the surface
\Sigma

D

By a localization of this property, as it was done, e.g., in (see, e.g.,
[
6
]

[

]

]

.

Let the
\Omega
