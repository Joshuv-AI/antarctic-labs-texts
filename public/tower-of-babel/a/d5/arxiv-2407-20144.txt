Deciphering the Instability of the Black Hole Ringdown Quasinormal Spectrum
A. Ianniccari, A. J. Iovino, A. Kehagias, P. Pani, G. Perna, D. Perrone, A. Riotto

Abstract
The spectrum of the quasinormal modes of the gravitational waves emitted during the ringdown phase following the merger of two black holes is of primary importance in gravitational astronomy. However, the spectrum is extremely sensitive to small disturbances of the system, thus potentially jeopardizing the predictions of the gravitational wave observables. We offer an analytical and intuitive explanation of such an instability and its properties based on the transfer matrix approach of quantum mechanics. We also give a simple interpretation of the fact that the prompt ringdown response in the time domain and the black hole greybody factor receive parametrically small corrections, thus being robust observables.

The transfer matrix formalism –
We consider the equation of motion of the tensor modes in the Schwarzschild spacetime to which a small potential bump is added away from the location of the photon ring
[
20
]

\left[\partial_{r_{*}}^{2}+\omega^{2}-V^{\epsilon}_{\ell}(r)\right]h_{\ell}(r)=0,

(1)

where

r_{*}=r+r_{s}\ln\left|\frac{r-r_{s}}{r_{s}}\right|

(2)

is the standard tortoise coordinate,
\omega
is the frequency,
\ell
is the spherical-harmonic multipole, and
r_{s}
is the Schwarzschild radius (for a spherically symmetric background, the azimuthal number is degenerate and can be set to zero without loss of generality).

The potential reads (say, for the odd parity helicity)
[
29
]

\displaystyle V^{\epsilon}_{\ell}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{0}(r)+\epsilon V_{1}(r),

\displaystyle V_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{r_{s}}{r}\right)\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}-\frac{3r_{s}}{r^{3}}\right],

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{r_{s}^{2}}{\rm sech}^{2}\left(\frac{r_{*}-c}{r_{s}}\right).

(3)

The Pöschl-Teller bump
V_{1}
is located at a distance
c
much larger than the location
r_{g}\approx 3r_{s}/2
of the peak of the main potential (where the photon ring approximately resides). It has an amplitude
{\cal O}(\epsilon)
and a width
{\cal O}(r_{s})\ll c
.

The problem of finding the effect of the small bump onto the frequency of the QNMs can be rephrased in the language of the transfer matrix representation.

Schematic representation of the potential with the ingoing and outgoing states and the corresponding reflection and transmission coefficients.

We describe the ingoing and outgoing solutions in a vector space with basis

e^{-i\omega r_{*}}\to\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},\quad e^{i\omega r_{*}}\to\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

In the case of a single unspecified potential, the transfer matrix which maps the left basis to the right basis with respect to a potential barrier can be generically written as (see the Supplementary Material (SM) for more details)

M=\begin{pmatrix}t-rr^{\prime}/t^{\prime}&r^{\prime}/t^{\prime}\\
-r/t^{\prime}&1/t^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

Here
r(r^{\prime})
and
t(t^{\prime})
are the reflection coefficients of a wave coming from left (right) and the transmission coefficient of a wave propagating from left to right (from right to left), respectively, see Fig.
1
and the SM.

In the language of scattering theory, the QNM frequencies are the resonances of the system and the equality

\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}=M\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}r^{\prime}/t^{\prime}\\
1/t^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}

(6)

imposes the condition
1/t^{\prime}=0
, which identifies the QNMs as the poles of the transmission coefficient.

We now suppose that between the two maxima the potentials are negligible. Since both
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
are well localized, this assumption is certainly justified in the most relevant cases with
|c|\gg r_{s}
, corresponding to a model for small environmental effects away from the horizon (
c>0
)
[
20
]
or near-horizon structure (
c<0
) as those predicted by dark-matter overdensities or by certain quantum-gravity models
[
19
,
51
,
52
]
.

Crucially for our argument, the transfer matrix is linear. Thus, to obtain the global transfer matrix of the system of two potentials under study, it is sufficient to multiply two transfer matrices: one for potential
V_{0}
and one for the small bump
V_{1}
, the latter after applying the translation operator
[
53
]

U(c)=e^{i\omega\sigma_{3}c},\,\,\,\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,,

(7)

to account for the displacement of the bump relative to the main peak. Henceforth we assume the bump is on the right of the main peak (the opposite case is equivalent). The total transfer matrix reads

M_{\text{\tiny tot}}=U^{-1}(c)\cdot M_{V_{1}}\cdot U(c)\cdot M_{V_{0}},

(8)

i.e. it brings a state from left to right passing through the first potential and through the second (shifted) potential.

The effect on the QNMs –

Since the potentials are positive,
there can be no bound states trapped in the middle
because states have always positive energy. Any wave located in the
middle will ultimately tunnel out. On the other hand, we may expect that
the wave stays trapped for some time before it eventually
escapes. In this situation, we refer to a resonance or an unstable or metastable state. Since the QNMs are exactly such states, to study the effect on the QNMs of the tiny bumpy potential we need to identify the poles of the total transmission
coefficient. This imposes the condition

1/t^{\prime}_{\text{\tiny tot}}=\frac{1}{t_{0}^{\prime}t_{1}^{\prime}}\left(1-e^{2i\omega c}r_{0}^{\prime}r_{1}\right)=0\,.

(9)

The roots of Eq. (
9
) are no longer only the poles of the individual transmission coefficients, but also those satisfying
the relation

e^{-2i\omega c}=r_{0}^{\prime}(\omega)r_{1}(\omega).

(10)

This is already a signal that the unperturbed frequencies of the QNMs may migrate to other values. Notice that Eq. (
10
) is exact for any value of
\epsilon
and
c>r_{s}
.

The physical interpretation of formula (
9
) is more clear if we expand
t_{\text{\tiny tot}}
as

\displaystyle t^{\prime}_{\text{\tiny tot}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{\prime}_{0}\left(1+r^{\prime}_{0}e^{2i\omega c}r_{1}\right.

(11)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.r^{\prime}_{0}e^{2i\omega c}r_{1}r^{\prime}_{0}e^{2i\omega c}r_{1}+\cdots\right)t^{\prime}_{1}.

The transmission amplitude
t_{\text{\tiny tot}}
is given by the sum of the contributions
of all possible paths through the two potentials
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
: on the right of the bumpy potential the wave can appear either directly transmitted across the two potentials from the left or after having been reflected between the two potentials several times.

Notice also that the
result is symmetric (upon changing the order of the matrices in Eq. (
8
)) for a bumpy potential located between the horizon and the photon ring, that is for
c<0
.

Discontinuous overtaking of the fundamental mode –

For large enough values of the distance between the two peaks,
the fundamental mode is overtaken by other longer-living modes with
n\geq 1

[
29
]
. There is a simple and intuitive explanation for such behaviour, again from Eq. (
10
).
The first overtaking takes place when the imaginary part of the frequency of the
(n,\ell)=(1,2)
mode becomes equal to the imaginary part of the frequency of the fundamental mode
(n,\ell)=(0,2)
. Since overtones migrate faster than the fundamental mode, in this regime
\overline{\omega}_{02}\simeq\omega_{02}
, and from Eq. (
10
) the overtaking happens roughly when

e^{-2{\rm Im}\,\omega_{02}c}\simeq e^{-2{\rm Im}\,\overline{\omega}_{12}c}\simeq\left|r_{0}^{\prime}(\overline{\omega}_{12})r_{1}(\overline{\omega}_{12})\right|\sim 0.6\,\epsilon,

(19)

which gives
c\sim 38
, in good agreement with the value found in Ref.
[
29
]
. One can proceed similarly for the higher modes and find the subsequent overtakings.

The destabilized region –
For even larger distances, the wave decays and loses most of its amplitude before reaching the second peak. It is efficiently reflected by the bump, slowly forming a quasi-bound state between the peaks. In such a case the frequency of the QNM cannot be anything else than of the order
1/c
, the only relevant scale in the problem. The reader can find a formal proof of such statement for the case of a two-delta model in the SM.

For the potentials of Eq. (
3
) we can make the following estimate. Starting from Eq. (
10
) we know that the reflection coefficient
r_{1}(\omega)
has poles with frequencies proportional to
\epsilon
. In the regime of large values of
c
, but keeping

\epsilon\,c\ll 1,

(20)

we can approximate the reflection coefficient as

r_{1}\simeq-i\frac{\epsilon}{\omega}.

(21)

As for the Schwarzschild potential, we can estimate the reflection coefficient
r_{0}^{\prime}
by evaluating it at
\omega\simeq 0
,

r_{0}^{\prime}(\omega\simeq 0)\simeq-1.

(22)

With such approximations Eq. (
10
) becomes

e^{-2i\omega c}\simeq ic\,\frac{\epsilon}{\omega c}.

(23)

Ignoring orders of
\mathcal{O}(\log(\omega c))
, we get the expected scaling

\omega\simeq\frac{i}{2c}\ln\left(i\epsilon c\right)+n\frac{\pi}{c},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}

(24)

for
c\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt$\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}}40
when perturbativity is lost. In particular, by considering the periodicity of the solution for the real part we obtain

{\rm Re}\,\omega_{n+1}-{\rm Re}\,\omega_{n}=\frac{\pi}{c},

(25)

which reproduces the result of Refs.
[

Robust observables against the tiny perturbations –

After having explained the physics of the QNM change due to the addition of small perturbations, we turn our attention to discuss two observables which have been identified in the literature to be numerically robust against the presence of a small bump, again offering a simple analytical explanation of such a behaviour.

Stability of the prompt ringdown in time-domain –

The expression (
11
) explains why the changes in the prompt ringdown signal are parametrically small and why the fundamental QNM extracted from the observable time-domain signal is stable against small perturbations
[
54
,
30
]
.

The point is that
for times
\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt$\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$<$}}c
, one can observe only the wave propagating from the photon ring, which did not have time to be reflected by the tiny bump and therefore the frequency of the fundamental QNM is close to the unperturbed one. Only after a time
\gg c
, the wave seen by an asymptotic observer have time to be bounced back and forth between the two potentials, thus changing the frequency of the fundamental mode.
To see the causality argument more formally, one can use the retarded Green function method. We recall that
time-evolution of the wave is

\displaystyle h_{\ell}(r_{*},\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}y\,G(r_{*},\tau|y,\tau^{\prime})\partial_{\tau^{\prime}}h_{\ell}(y,\tau^{\prime})

(26)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}y\,\partial_{\tau^{\prime}}G(r_{*},\tau|y,\tau^{\prime})h_{\ell}(y,\tau^{\prime}),

where
\tau
indicates time and
G(r_{*},\tau|y,\tau^{\prime})
is the retarded Green’s function

\displaystyle G(r_{*},\tau|y,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a-i\infty}^{a+i\infty}\frac{{\rm d}s}{2\pi i}\frac{e^{s(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}}{W_{0+1}}

(27)

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle\left[h_{\ell}^{-}(s,r_{*})h_{\ell}^{+}(s,y)\theta(r_{*}-y)\right.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.h_{\ell}^{+}(s,r_{*})h_{\ell}^{-}(s,y)\theta(y-r_{*})\right],

where the contour runs parallel to the
({\rm Im}\,s)
axis, but with a positive real part (
a>0
) so that it lies to the right of all singularities,
h_{\ell}^{\pm}

are the two solutions of the problem with the appropriate boundary conditions and the frequencies (in Laplace space) are poles of the inverse of the Wronskian
W_{0+1}
. For sufficiently short times
(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\ll c
, that is for large values of
({\rm Re}\,s)
, and using Ref.
[
55
]
, we derive that

\frac{1}{W_{0+1}}\simeq\frac{t_{0}^{\prime}t_{1}^{\prime}}{2s}\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}e^{-2ksc},

(28)

so that only the first term
k=0
survives. Causality forbids back and forth bounces, and one observes the QNMs associated with the poles of the individual transmission coefficients. The fundamental QNM will then be very similar to that of the unperturbed system.

The greybody factor –
The greybody factor is the absorption cross section of the BH, as it measures how much of the incident field is effectively transmitted through the potential barrier and “absorbed” by the BH.
For real frequencies we can define the greybody factor as the absolute value squared of the transmission coefficient
[
56
]

\gamma_{\text{\tiny tot}}(\omega)=t^{\prime}_{\text{\tiny tot}}(\omega)t^{\prime*}_{\text{\tiny tot}}(\omega)\,,

(29)

reducing to
\gamma_{0}(\omega)=t^{\prime}_{0}(\omega)t^{\prime*}_{0}(\omega)
in the unperturbed case. Using the transmission coefficients of the full potential, we get

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{\tiny tot}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t^{\prime}_{0}(\omega)t^{\prime}_{1}(\omega)t^{\prime*}_{0}(\omega)t^{\prime*}_{1}(\omega)}{\left(1-e^{2i\omega c}r^{\prime}_{0}r_{1}\right)\left(1-e^{-2i\omega c}r^{\prime*}_{0}r^{*}_{1}\right)}\,.

(30)

In the limit in which the destabilization via migration of the fundamental mode takes place, the pole of the transmission coefficient of the unperturbed potential cancels at leading order in
\epsilon
(see Eq. (
13
)), and

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{\tiny tot}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm Res}\,t^{\prime}_{0}(\omega_{02})t^{\prime}_{1}(\omega)t^{\prime*}_{0}(\omega)t^{\prime*}_{1}(\omega)}{(\omega-\overline{\omega}_{02})\left(1-e^{-2i\omega c}r^{\prime*}_{0}r^{*}_{1}\right)},

(31)

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm Res}\,t^{\prime}_{0}(\omega_{02})}{\omega-\omega_{02}}t^{\prime*}_{0}(\omega).

(32)

The integral of the change induced by the bump,
\Delta\gamma(\omega)=\gamma_{\text{\tiny tot}}(\omega)-\gamma_{0}(\omega)
, over all possible frequencies
can be computed by deforming it to a contour integral in the
complex plane,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}\omega\Delta\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm Res}\,t^{\prime}_{0}(\omega_{02})t^{\prime}_{1}(\overline{\omega}_{02})t^{\prime*}_{0}(\overline{\omega}_{02})t^{\prime*}_{1}(\overline{\omega}_{02})}{\left(1-e^{-2i\overline{\omega}_{02}c}r^{\prime*}_{0}(\overline{\omega}_{02})r^{*}_{1}(\overline{\omega}_{02})\right)}

(33)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle{\rm Res}\,t^{\prime}_{0}(\omega_{02})t^{\prime*}_{0}(\omega_{02})\,.

In the
\epsilon\ll 1
limit,
t^{\prime*}_{0}(\overline{\omega}_{02})\simeq t^{\prime*}_{0}(\omega_{02})
,
t^{\prime*}_{1}(\overline{\omega}_{02})\sim 1
, and
r^{*}_{1}(\overline{\omega}_{02})\sim\epsilon
. Therefore, the previous expression reduces to

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}\omega\Delta\gamma={\rm Res}\,t^{\prime}_{0}(\omega_{02})t^{\prime*}_{0}(\omega_{02})\left[t^{\prime}_{1}(\overline{\omega}_{02})-1\right].

(34)

The key point is now that (see the SM)

t^{\prime}_{1}(\overline{\omega}_{02})\simeq 1+{\cal O}(\epsilon)e^{-\pi{\rm Re}\,\overline{\omega}_{02}}.

(35)

Therefore, the integral over the frequencies, which is a finite quantity, must be
{\cal O}(\epsilon)
and does not feel the exponential migration of the frequency of the fundamental QNM. This confirms the findings of Refs.
[
50
,
57
]
, where the greybody factor has been proposed as an observable which does not feel the QNM instability.

Conclusions –
It is well-known that small perturbations to the effective potential governing the gravitational-wave generation and propagation around a BH can lead to large variations in the QNM spectrum. In this paper we have clarified the physics of such perturbations using the simple language of the transfer matrix of quantum mechanics, reproducing the known results in the literature and providing simple and intuitive explanations for the various regimes. We have also explained the physical behavior of the time-domain signals for tiny perturbations in the potential and why the fundamental mode in the prompt ringdown signal is not altered by the presence of small bumps in the potential. Similarly, we have offered a simple motivation of why the greybody factor receives parametrically small corrections, thus being a robust observable against perturbations in the effective potential.

There are other directions over which expand our work. For instance, it would be interesting to understand the case of spinning BHs as well and to identify other observables insensitive to the migration of the QNM spectrum. Furthermore, it is well-known that the QNMs
can be thought of as null particles trapped at the unstable circular orbit and slowly leaking out

[
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
]
, with
the real part of the QNM
frequency being set by the angular velocity at the
unstable null geodesic, while the imaginary part being related to
the instability timescale of the orbit
[
62
,
63
]
and to the properties of the BH collapse
[
64
]
. An interesting question is if such a correspondence persists in the presence of small perturbations of the effective potential. For instance (the reader is referred again to the SM), the quasi-bound state frequency
\omega_{02}\sim 1/c
in the destabilized region can be reproduced by such arguments. We intend to investigate these issues in the near future.

(S5)

\begin{pmatrix}r&t^{\prime}\\
t&r^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}r\\
t\end{pmatrix},

(S6)

which gives the correct reflection and transmission coefficients for the solutions at the left and the right of the potential

e^{i\omega r_{*}}+r\,e^{-i\omega r_{*}}\longleftrightarrow t\,e^{i\omega r_{*}},

(S7)

where the arrow represents the connection of the asymptotic states through the potential.

Having defined the scattering matrix in this way it is immediate to connect the reflection and transmission coefficients with the matrix elements of the transfer matrix

M\begin{pmatrix}A_{L}\\
B_{L}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{R}\\
B_{R}\end{pmatrix}.

(S8)

This leads to the definition of the transfer matrix as

M=\begin{pmatrix}t-rr^{\prime}/t^{\prime}&r^{\prime}/t^{\prime}\\
-r/t^{\prime}&1/t^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},

(S9)

as in Eq. (
5
).

Furthermore we can use the property
SS^{\dagger}=1
and
S^{\dagger}S=1
to write

|r|^{2}+|t^{\prime}|^{2}=1,\quad|r^{\prime}|^{2}+|t|^{2}=1,\quad rt^{*}=-t^{\prime}r^{\prime*}

(S10)

|r|^{2}+|t|^{2}=1,\quad|r^{\prime}|^{2}+|t^{\prime}|^{2}=1,\quad r^{*}t^{\prime}=-t^{*}r^{\prime}.

(S11)

If the problem is symmetric under time reversal there is also the additional relation
t=t^{\prime}
,
which implies

r=-r^{\prime*}\frac{t}{t^{*}}.

(S12)

II
Two-delta example

In this section we provide a toy model to understand the various regimes in which we evaluated the effect given by the presence of the bump. In this simplified model we consider the two-delta potential

V=2\beta\delta(r_{*})+2\epsilon\beta\delta(r_{*}-c),

(S26)

where
\beta
is real and can be thought of order
\beta\sim r_{s}^{-1}
. The main advantage of this model is that the transmission and reflection coefficients are known,

t_{0}(\omega)=\frac{1}{1+i\beta/\omega},\quad r_{0}(\omega)=\frac{-i\beta/\omega}{1+i\beta/\omega},

(S27)

and the same for
t_{1}
and
r_{1}
just by replacing
\beta
by
\epsilon\beta
,

t_{1}(\omega)=\frac{1}{1+i\epsilon\beta/\omega},\quad r_{1}(\omega)=\frac{-i\epsilon\beta/\omega}{1+i\epsilon\beta/\omega}.

(S28)

From the transmission coefficients we can immediately see that the two typical wavelengths related to their poles are respectively of order

\omega_{0}=-i\beta,\quad\omega_{b}=-i\epsilon\beta.

(S29)

To find the frequency of the two-delta system we need to solve Eq. (
10
), remembering that
r^{\prime}=-r^{*}t/t^{*}
because both the potentials are symmetric under time reversal. Therefore Eq. (
10
) becomes

e^{-2i\omega c}=-\frac{r_{0}^{*}t_{0}}{t_{0}^{*}}r_{1}=-\frac{\epsilon\beta^{2}/|\omega|^{2}}{(1+i\beta/\omega)(1+i\epsilon\beta/\omega)},

(S30)

which can be solved numerically. However we perform two limits to understand the behaviour of the solution found and gain insight on the original problem.

The first limit is for a spectrum close to the unperturbed one. In the limit
\epsilon\to 0
we have that the solution at leading order in
\epsilon
can be written as

\omega=\omega_{0}+\epsilon\omega_{1},

(S31)

and substituting in the equation above while keeping only the first order in
\epsilon
we get

e^{-2i\omega_{0}c}\simeq-\frac{\beta^{2}/|\omega_{0}|^{2}}{\omega_{1}/\omega_{0}(1+i\epsilon\beta/\omega_{0})}\quad{\rm or}\quad\omega_{1}\simeq i\beta e^{2\beta c}.

(S32)

The corresponding frequency is

\omega=\omega_{0}+\epsilon\omega_{1}=-i\beta\left(1-\epsilon\,e^{2\beta c}\right),

(S33)

which grows exponentially with
c
as expected.
This result is valid for all the values of
c
, until the point the perturbation reaches the same size of the original frequency, i.e. until
c=c_{*}
such that

\epsilon\,e^{2\beta c_{*}}\sim 1.

(S34)

However, even when the value
c_{*}
is reached, we still have

\epsilon\beta c_{*}\sim\epsilon\log(1/\epsilon)\ll 1,

(S35)

for typical values
\epsilon\sim(10^{-5}\div 10^{-3})
and
\beta c\sim(10\div 10^{2})
used in this problem. Therefore we can identify a second region for
c
where
c\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt$\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}}c_{*}
where

\epsilon\,e^{2\beta c_{*}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt$\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}}1,\quad\epsilon\beta c_{*}\ll 1.

(S36)

In this region our intuition is that the wave decays and looses most of its amplitude before reaching the second peak, therefore getting reflected and slowly forming a quasi-bound state between the peaks. This insight leads us to expect frequencies of the order
\omega\sim 1/c
.

From Eq. (
S30
) it is possible to notice that neither pole is picked given our estimate for the frequency. Consistently with our estimate, in the denominator we can approximate

\frac{1}{1+i\beta/\omega}\sim\frac{1}{i\beta/\omega},\quad{\rm and}\quad\frac{1}{1+i\epsilon\beta/\omega}\sim 1

(S37)

and we need to solve the simpler equation

e^{-2i\omega c}=i\frac{\epsilon\beta\omega}{|\omega|^{2}}.

(S38)

Expanding the equation and writing
\omega=\omega_{R}+i\omega_{I}
, the only solution is
\omega_{R}=0
, giving

e^{2\omega_{I}c}=-\frac{\epsilon\beta}{\omega_{I}}

(S39)

which has two solutions, one with
\omega_{I}\sim 0
and the other which can be approximated as

\omega_{I}\sim-\frac{\log(\epsilon\beta c)}{2c},

(S40)

giving a frequency written as

\omega\sim-\frac{i}{2c}\log(\epsilon\beta c).

(S41)

There are therefore two regimes, depending on the position of the bump. At first, the frequency is close to the original pole and it migrates, still being close to the original value. This changes when the perturbation become
\mathcal{O}(1)
, giving rise to an inverse distance regime, similar to a confined metastable particle in a potential well.

III
The effective metric

For completeness, in this section we reconstruct the metric associated with a bumpy potential. For simplicity we will consider the case of a scalar perturbation, although the argument can be generalized.
Consider the equation of motion of a massless scalar field in the Schwarzschild spacetime to which a small potential bump is added away from the location of the photon ring

\left[\partial_{r_{*}}^{2}+\omega^{2}-V^{\epsilon}_{\ell}(r)\right]\phi_{\ell}(r)=0,

(S42)

where the
potential reads

\displaystyle V^{\epsilon}_{\ell}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{0}(r)+\epsilon V_{1}(r),

\displaystyle V_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{r_{s}}{r}\right)\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}+\frac{r_{s}}{r^{3}}\right],

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{r_{s}}\delta(r-c).

(S43)

The Dirac delta in the potential is located at a distance
c\gg
than the location
r_{g}
of the peak of the main potential (where the photon ring resides), has an amplitude
{\cal O}(\epsilon)
and mimics a more realistic bump with a width
{\cal O}(r_{s})\ll c
.

Now, we wish to answer the following question: what is the effective metric which gives Eq. (
S42
)? For large distances of the bump,
r_{*}\simeq r
, and writing the effective metric as

{\rm d}s^{2}=-A(r){\rm d}t^{2}+\frac{1}{A(r)}{\rm d}r^{2}+r^{2}{\rm d}\Omega^{2},

(S44)

the equation of motion of the scalar field becomes

\left[\partial_{r_{*}}^{2}+\omega^{2}-A\frac{\partial_{r}A}{r}-A\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}\right]\phi_{\ell}=0.

(S45)

Setting

A(r)=A_{0}(r)+\epsilon A_{1}(r),

(S46)

we find

\displaystyle A_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{r_{s}}{r},

\displaystyle\frac{A_{0}}{r}\partial_{r}A_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{1}-\frac{A_{1}}{A_{0}}V_{0}.

(S47)

We can integrate the last equation to find

\displaystyle A_{1}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Kr}{(r-r_{s})^{\ell(\ell+1)+1}}

(S48)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\frac{c\,r(c-r_{s})^{\ell(\ell+1)}}{r_{s}(r-r_{s})^{\ell(\ell+1)+1}}\theta(r-c),

where
K
is an integration constant, which we set to zero so that the boundary condition is
A_{1}(r_{s}\ll r\ll c)=0
.

For
r
and
c\gg r_{s}
, the additional piece in the metric reduces to

A_{1}(r)\simeq\frac{2c}{r_{s}}\left(\frac{c}{r}\right)^{\ell(\ell+1)}\theta(r-c).

(S49)

The effective metric looks as follows: at distances
r_{g}\ll r\ll c
it is the usual Schwarzschild metric; at
r=c
it has a bump
{\cal O}(2\epsilon c/r_{s})
and then it decays as
r^{-\ell(\ell+1)}
.

Within the spacetime with metric (
S44
), null geodesics are determined by the trajectories which move along the equatorial plane such that

-A\dot{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{A}\dot{r}^{2}+r^{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}=0,

(S50)

where the dots indicate differentation with respect to the affine parameter and
\phi
is the azimuthal angle. Because of the spherical symmetry, one has
\dot{\phi}^{2}=L^{2}/r^{4}
, where
L
is the angular momentum. Similarly, stationarity gives
\dot{t}^{2}=E^{2}/A^{2}
, where
E
is the conserved energy. The equation of motion can be written as

\dot{r}^{2}=-V(r)=-A\left(-\frac{E^{2}}{A}+\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}}\right).

(S51)

A circular orbit at a given radius
r_{c}
exists if

V(r_{c})=V^{\prime}(r_{c})=0,

(S52)

where the prime indicates differentation with respect to radial coordinate. These conditions impose, respectively

\displaystyle\frac{E^{2}}{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{A_{c}}{r^{2}_{c}},

(S53)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{c}\frac{A^{\prime}_{c}}{2A_{c}},

(S54)

where the subscript
c
means that the quantity in question is evaluated at the radius
r_{c}
of the circular null geodesic.

In the problem at hand, there are three locations of
r_{c}
: the maxima of
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
(unstable orbits) and the minimum located close to
c
where
A_{0}\simeq\epsilon A_{1}
(stable orbit). Since the angular velocity at these values reads

\omega_{c}=\frac{A_{c}}{r_{c}},

(S55)

for the stable orbit we find
\omega_{c}(r_{c}\simeq c)\simeq 1/c
, reproducing the scaling for the quasi-bound state.
