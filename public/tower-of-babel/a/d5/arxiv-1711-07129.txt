Categorical characterization of quadrics
Duo Li

Abstract
. We give a characterization of smooth quadrics in terms of the existence of full exceptional collections of certain type, which generalizes a result of C.Vial for projective spaces.



1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a complex variety and
D^{b}(X)
be its bounded derived category.
An object
E\in\mathcal{D}^{b}(X)
is called exceptional if we have

\text{Hom}(E,E[l])=\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\mathbb{C}}\quad\text{if}\quad l=0\\
&0\quad\text{if}\quad l\neq 0\end{aligned}\right.

An exceptional collection is a sequence
\{E_{1}\cdots,E_{n}\}
of exceptional objects which satisfy that
\text{Hom}(E_{j},E_{i}[l])
vanishes for all
j>i
and all
l\in{\mathbb{Z}}.
An exceptional collection
\{E_{1}\cdots,E_{n}\}
is full if
\mathcal{D}
is generated by
\{E_{i}\}.

In general, full exceptional collections which consist of coherent sheaves are rare. The existence of such collections would impose strong restrictions on the geometry of the variety. We now list some varieties with such collections.

•

projective spaces: for any
a\in{\mathbb{Z}},

\{\mathcal{O}(a),\mathcal{O}(a+1),\cdots,\mathcal{O}(a+n)\}
is a full exceptional collection of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
(see
[
1
]
).

•

smooth quadric
Q^{n}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}.
Kapranov
[
5
]
shows that

–

if
n
is odd, for any
a\in{\mathbb{Z}},

\{S,\mathcal{O}(a),\mathcal{O}(a+1),\cdots,\mathcal{O}(a+n-1)\}
is a full exceptional collection of
Q^{n},

–

if
n
is even, for any
a\in{\mathbb{Z}},

\{S^{-},S^{+},\mathcal{O}(a),\mathcal{O}(a+1),\cdots,\mathcal{O}(a+n-1)\}
is a full exceptional collection of
Q^{n}

where
S,

S^{-},

S^{+}
are certain spinor bundles.

A
minifold
is a smooth projective variety
X
whose
D^{b}(X)
admits a full exceptional collection
\mathcal{C}
of minimal possible length
\dim X+1.

Galkin, Katzarkov, Mellit and Shinder classify
minifolds
up to dimension
4
(see
[
3
]
).
If
\mathcal{C}
purely consists of line bundles, C. Vial proves that
X
is necessarily a projective space (see
[
10
, Theorem 1.2]
). In
[
7
]
, S. Kobayashi and T. Ochiai show that if there exists an ample line bundle
H
on a smooth projective variety
X
with
\dim(X)c_{1}(H)\leq c_{1}(X),
then
X
is isomorphic to a projective space or a smooth quadric. The purpose of this article is to generalize C.Vial’s theorem and prove a categorical analog of S. Kobayashi and T. Ochiai’s classification. In view of Kapranov’s result, it is reasonable to consider full exceptional collections of length
\dim X+1
or
\dim X+2.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
n

(n\geq 3)
. Suppose that there is a full exceptional collection
\mathcal{C}
of
D^{b}(X)
which consists of coherent sheaves and is of length
n+1.
If
\mathcal{C}
contains a sub-collection
{
\mathcal{L}_{1},

\mathcal{L}_{2},\cdots,

\mathcal{L}_{n}
}
where
\mathcal{L}_{i}

(1\leq i\leq n)
are line bundles, then
X
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
or
Q^{n}.
Moreover, if
n
is an even number,
X
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
n
(
n\geq 3
). Suppose that there is a full exceptional collection
\mathcal{C}
of
D^{b}(X)
which consists of coherent sheaves and is of length
n+2.
If
\mathcal{C}
contains a sub-collection
{
\mathcal{L}_{1},

\mathcal{L}_{2},\cdots,

\mathcal{L}_{n}
}
where
\mathcal{L}_{i}

(1\leq i\leq n)
are line bundles, then
X
is an even-dimensional variety and is isomorphic to
Q^{n}.

To prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
, we use the method from C. Vial’s article
[
10
]
and some technical input (see Lemma
2.1
).

Convention:
In this article, a variety is an integral scheme of finite type defined over
{\mathbb{C}}
.

2.
A technical lemma

Firstly, we note an easy fact about series of numbers.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}\}
be
n
distinct integers with
n\geq 5.
Assume that the cardinality of the set
A=\{a_{j}-a_{i}|1\leq i<j\leq n\}
is
n.
Then there exists an integer
d
such that
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
is one of the following series:

(1)

(a_{1},\cdots,\widehat{a_{1}+kd},\cdots,a_{1}+nd)
where
1\leq k\leq n-1
is an integer.

(2)

(a_{1},a_{1}+2d,\cdots,a_{1}+(n-1)d,a_{1}+(n+1)d)

(3)

\sigma(a_{1},\cdots,a_{1}+(n-1)d)
where
\sigma
is a composition of disjoint permutations
(i_{1},i_{1}+1)\cdots(i_{l},i_{l}+1)
for some
1\leq l\leq n.

Proof.

We firstly assume
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{n}
and claim that
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
is of type
(1)
and
(2).
It is easy to verify our claim when
n
is
5.
For the case
n>5,
the cardinality of
B:=\{a_{j}-a_{i}|1\leq i<j\leq n-1\}
is
n-1
or
n-2.
If
\sharp B
is
n-2,
then
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n-1})
is an arithmetic progression which can be denoted by
(a_{1},a_{1}+d,\cdots,a_{1}+(n-2)d)
for some
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}.
It is easy to see
a_{n}
is
a_{1}+nd.
If
\sharp B
is
n-1,
by induction, we have
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n-1})=(a_{1},\cdots,\widehat{a_{1}+kd},\cdots,a_{1}+(n-1)d)
(
1\leq k\leq n-2
) or
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n-1})=(a_{1},a_{1}+2d,\cdots,a_{1}+(n-2)d,a_{1}+nd).
In the first case, if
k
is
1,
then
a_{n}
equals to
a_{1}+nd
or
a_{1}+(n+1)d.
If
k
is bigger than
2,
then
a_{n}
equals to
a_{1}+nd.
In the second case, we can assume
a_{n}=a_{1}+md
for some integer
m>n.
If
m
is
n+1
or
n+2,
then
(n-1)d
belongs to
A.
If we have
m>n+2,
then
(m-2)d
belongs to
A.
Both situations lead to the fact
\sharp A\geq n+1,
so we can exclude the case
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n-1})=(a_{1},a_{1}+2d,\cdots,a_{1}+(n-2)d,a_{1}+nd)
and prove our claim.

For an arbitrary series
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n}),
there is a permutation
\alpha
satisfying
a_{\alpha(1)}<a_{\alpha(2)}<\cdots<a_{\alpha(n)}.
Note that the cardinality of the set
C:=\{a_{\alpha(j)}-a_{\alpha(i)}|1\leq i<j\leq n\}
is less than
\sharp\{a_{j}-a_{i}|1\leq i<j\leq n\}.
It follows that
\sharp C
is
n-1
or
n.

If
\sharp C
is
n-1,
then there exists an integer
d
such that
(a_{\alpha(1)},\cdots,a_{\alpha(n)})
is arithmetic and
C
is
\{d,2d,\cdots,(n-1)d\}.
Note that for any integer
k\neq 1,
if
kd
belongs to
A,
then
\sgn(k)\cdot d
belongs to
A.
It follows that
\forall k>1,

kd\in A
implies
-kd\notin A.
Now we can assume
2d\in B,
then
(a_{\alpha(1)},a_{\alpha(1)}+2d,a_{\alpha(1)}+4d,\cdots),
as well as (
a_{\alpha(1)}+d,a_{\alpha(1)}+3d,a_{\alpha(1)}+5d,\cdots
), is a subseries of
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}).
If
a_{\alpha(1)}+d
appears on the right of
a_{\alpha(1)}
in
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}),
then we have
A=\{-d,d,2d,\cdots,(n-1)d\}.
If
a_{\alpha(1)}+d
appears on the left of
a_{\alpha(1)}
in
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}),
then we have
-d\in A
and
a_{\alpha(1)}+3d
appears on the right of
a_{\alpha(1)}.
It follows that
A
is
\{-d,d,2d,\cdots,(n-1)d\}.
So if
\sharp C
is
n-1,
the series
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})
is of type
(3).

Now suppose that
\sharp C
is
n,
then there exists an integer
d
such that
(a_{\alpha(1)},\cdots,a_{\alpha(n)})
is of type
(1)
or
(2).
For any integer
l>0,
the numbers
ld
and
-ld
can not belong to
A
at the same time. So we can assume
d\in A
and
-d\notin A.
By a similar argument, one can verify
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})=(a_{\alpha(1)},\cdots,a_{\alpha(n)}).

∎

Remark 2.2
.

For the case
n=4,

(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4})
is one of the following series:

(1)

(a,a+d,a+2d,a+4d)
or
(a,a+2d,a+3d,a+4d)
where
d
is an integer.

(2)

\sigma(a,a+d,a+2d,a+3d)
where
d
is a positive integer and
\sigma\neq id
is a permutation.

(3)

\sigma(a,a+d,b,b+d)
where
d
is a positive integer with
b>a+d
and
\sigma
is a permutation.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety. Recall that, for any two objects
E
and
F
in
D^{b}(X),
the Euler pairing
\chi
is the integer
\chi(E,F):=\sum_{l}(-1)^{l}\dim_{{\mathbb{C}}}\text{Hom}(E,F[l]).

Definition 3.1
.

An object
E
is said to be numerically exceptional if
\chi(E,E)=1.
A collection
\{E_{1},\cdots,E_{r}\}
of numerical exceptional objects is a numerical exceptional collection if
\chi(E_{j},E_{i})=0
for all
j>i.

For Fano varieties of Picard number
1,
we have the following result.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let Let
X
be a smooth projective Fano variety of dimension
n

(n\geq 3)
whose
\Pic(X)
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}.
Assume that there is a numerical exceptional collection
\{\mathcal{L}_{1},\cdots\mathcal{L}_{n}\}
where
\mathcal{L}_{i}

(1\leq i\leq n)
are line bundles. Then
X
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
or a smooth quadric
Q^{n}.

Proof.

We denote
c_{1}(X)
by
\lambda H
for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}.
The Euler character
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{X}(aH))
is a polynomial
P
with rational coefficients of degree
n
in the variable
a.
We write
\mathcal{L}_{i}
as
\mathcal{L}_{i}=O_{X}(a_{i}H)
for some integer
a_{i}
and we have
a_{i}\neq a_{j}
for any
i\neq j,
as
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{X})
is
1.
The equalities
\chi(\mathcal{L}_{j},\mathcal{L}_{i})=\chi(\mathcal{L}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{j}^{-1})=P(a_{i}-a_{j})=0

(i<j)
imply that
a_{i}-a_{j}

(i<j)
are roots of
P(a)=0.
So the cardinality
\mu
of the set {
a_{j}-a_{i}|1\leq i<j\leq n
} is
n-1
or
n.

If
\mu
is
n-1,
the progression
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
is an arithmetic progression with
a_{2}-a_{1}=d\in{\mathbb{Z}}.
The polynomial
P
vanishes at
-ld
for
1\leq l\leq n-1.
Let the remaining root of
P(a)=0
be
-N.
By Riemann-Roch, we have

P(a)=\chi(\mathcal{O}_{X}(aH))=\frac{\deg(H^{n})}{n!}a^{n}+\frac{\deg(H^{n-1}\cdot c_{1}(X))}{2(n-1)!}a^{n-1}+\cdots+\chi(\mathcal{O}_{X}).

Note the following equalities:

\frac{\deg(H^{n})}{n!}(n-1)!d^{n-1}N=1\qquad(1)

\frac{\deg(H^{n})}{n!}(N+\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}ld)=\frac{\deg(H^{n-1}\cdot c_{1}(X))}{2(n-1)!}\qquad(2).

One can deduce
d=1,

\deg H^{n}=1,

\lambda=n+1
or
d=1,

\deg H^{n}=2,

\lambda=n.
Then by
[
7
]
, the Fano variety
X
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
or
Q^{n}.

If
\mu
is
n,
we aim to show that
X
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}.
We firstly assume
n\geq 5,
then the progression
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
is classified in Lemma
2.1
. If
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
is of type
(1)
as in Lemma
2.1
, then there exists an integer
d
such that the polynomial
P(a)
vanishes exactly at
-ld

(1\leq l\leq n).
By a similar argument, we have
\deg H^{n}=1,

d=1
and
\lambda=n+1.
If
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
is of type
(2)
as in Lemma
2.1
, the roots of
P(a)=0
is
\{-d,-2d,\cdots,-(n-1)d,-(n+1)d\}
for some integer
d.
Then we have
\frac{\deg(H^{n})}{n!}(n-1)!d^{n}(n+1)=1,
which is absurd as
d
is an integer. If
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
is of type
(3)
as in Lemma
2.1
, the roots of
P(a)=0
is
\{\pm d,-2d,\cdots,-(n-1)d\}
for some integer
d.
Then it is easy to deduce
\deg(H^{n})d^{n}=-n
and
\lambda=\frac{(n+1)(n-2)}{n}d\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+},
which is impossible.
Now suppose
n=3.
The progression of integers
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
is not arithmetic. By a simple calculation, we have
\lambda=\frac{4}{3}(a_{3}-a_{1})\geq 4\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}.

Now suppose
n=4.
If
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4})
is of type
(1)
as in Remark
2.2
, by a similar argument, we can deduce that
\lambda
is
5.
If
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4})
is of type
(2)
as in Remark
2.2
, then there exists an integer
d
such that the roots of
P(a)=0
are
\{d,-d,-2d,-3d\},

so we have
\frac{\deg(H^{4})}{4!}\prod_{i<j}(a_{j}-a_{i})<0,
which is impossible. If
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4})
is of type
(3)
as in Remark
2.2
, by the method of exhaustion, the only possibility satisfying
\frac{\deg(H^{4})}{4!}\prod_{i<j}(a_{j}-a_{i})=1
and
\lambda>0
is
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4})=(a_{1},a_{1}+1,a_{1}+3,a_{1}+4),
hence
\lambda
is
5.

By all the above argument, if
\mu
is
n,
then
\lambda
is
n+1,
hence
X
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}.

∎

We now prove a lemma about the Picard group of a variety. For similar results, we refer to
[
2
, Theorem 3.4]
and
[
10
, Lemma 2.6]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
n
. Suppose that there is a full exceptional collection
\mathcal{C}
of
D^{b}(X).
If the length of
\mathcal{C}
is
n+1,
then
\Pic(X)
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}.
If the length of
\mathcal{C}
is
n+2
(
n\geq 3
), then
n
is an even number and the
\Pic(X)
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}.

Proof.

For any smooth projective variety
X,

the existence of a full exceptional collection of
D^{b}(X)
whose length is
m
implies that the Grothendieck group
K_{0}(X)
of
D^{b}(X)
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}.
Moreover, we have the identity

m=\rk K_{0}(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\rk\CH^{i}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(X)

where every
\rk\CH^{i}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(X)
is non-zero. If
m
equals to
n+1,
then
\Pic(X)
is of rank
1.
If
m
equals to
n+2,
by
[
6
]
or
[
9
, Proposition 3.10]
, the cycle class maps
cl_{i}:\CH^{i}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(X)\rightarrow H^{2i}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})

(0\leq i\leq n)
are all isomorphisms. So for any
0\leq i\leq n,
there exists a perfect pairing
\CH^{i}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(X)\times\CH^{n-i}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(X)\rightarrow{\mathbb{Q}}.
We have
\rk\CH^{i}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(X)=\rk\CH^{n-i}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(X)=1
for
i\neq n-i.
It follows that
n
is an even number and
X
is of Picard number
1.
By
[
4
, Lemma 2.2]
or
[
10
, Lemma 2.6]
, the Chow group
\CH^{1}(X)
is torsion free, so
\Pic(X)
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}.

∎

The proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By
[

, Proposition 3.2]
, the variety

is Fano. Then Theorem

To prove Theorem
1.2
, we need the concept of the anticanonical pseudoheight which is introduced by A. Kuznetsov.
For an exceptional collection
\mathcal{C}=\{E_{0},\cdots,E_{r}\},
the anticanonical pseudoheight is defined as

ph_{ac}(\mathcal{C}):=\mathop{\min}\limits_{1\leq a_{o}<\cdots<a_{p}\leq n}(e(E_{a_{0}},E_{a_{1}})+\cdots+e(E_{a_{p-1}},E_{a_{p}})+e(E_{a_{p}},E_{a_{0}}\otimes K_{X}^{-1})-p)

where
\forall F,

F^{\prime}\in D^{b}(X),
the relative height
e(F,F^{\prime})
is defined by
\min\{k|Ext^{k}(F,F^{\prime})\neq 0\}.
A. Kuznetsov shows in
[
8
, Corollary 6.2]
that if
ph_{ac}(\mathcal{C})>-\dim X,
then
\mathcal{C}
is not full.

1.2
.

, we have
