A Pedagogical Review of the Vacuum Retarded Dipole Model of Pulsar Spin Down DOI: %
J. C. Satherley, C. Gordon

Abstract
Pulsars are rapidly spinning highly magnetised neutron stars. Their spin period is observed to decrease with time. An early analytical model for this process was the vacuum retarded dipole (VRD) by [ 3 ] . This model assumes an idealised star and it finds that the rotational energy is radiated away by the electromagnetic fields. This model has been superseded by more realistic numerical simulations that account for the non-vacuum like surroundings of the neutron star. However, the VRD still provides a reasonable approximation and is a useful limiting case that can provide some qualitative understanding. We provide detailed derivations of the spin down and related electromagnetic field equations of the VRD solution. We also correct typographical errors in the general field equations and boundary conditions used by [ 3 ] .

INTRODUCTION

Pulsars are highly magnetised rapidly rotating neutron stars that emit electromagnetic radiation almost across the entire spectrum, from radio to
\gamma
-rays
(
Travelle 2011
)
. Because of the extreme magnetic and electric fields, the environment around a pulsar proves to be interesting and is believed to be the main cause of a pulsar’s energy emission. This energy emission results in a decreasing spin-rate for the neutron star. An analytical approximation for this process is given by the Vacuum Retarded Dipole (VRD) model which was originally derived by

Deutsch 1955
(hereafter referred to as
3
).

The derivations of the results in
3
are very concise, with many details missing.
The purpose of this review is to rederive the main results of
3
and in doing so provide details on how this derivation takes place mathematically and physically.
We also uncover three typographical errors in
3
,
the
most important of which is in our Eqn. (
31
), the polar component of the electric field. It is worth noting the first papers we found that mentioned the error in the general field equations are
Melatos 1997
and
Michel & Li 1999
. However, they do not show the derivations for the general field equations and how the typographical error is found. A reasonably detailed derivation of the VRD solution, without the typos, is shown in
the textbook by
Michel 1991
. However, the method used by Michel is different from
3
. While
3
uses methods outlined by
Stratton 1941
, Michel just considers multipole expansions at an inclination and does not derive the normalisations. Also the following working carried out by us goes into greater detail for the derivations.

In this review we use methods outlined in
Stratton 1941
(hereafter referred to as
16
). In particular, we refer frequently to Chapters VII and VIII, which consider spherical waves and radiation respectively.
We use
Griffiths 2017
(hereafter referred to as
5
) as a reference for standard electrodynamics theory.

In Sec.
2
, we begin by outlying the definition of an idealised star, along with fundamental equations that apply due to this assumption, which include Maxwell’s equations. We also note down
the
Alfvén theorem and a brief description of a vector field. In Sec.
3
, we define the
magnetic field symmetry axis
and its associated coordinate system. The magnetic field is assumed to take the form of a dipole about this axis. The transformation between the two coordinate systems is shown and used to derive the electromagnetic field boundary conditions in the rotation symmetry axis coordinates. In Sec.
4
, we show the derivations for the time-dependent and static components of the electromagnetic fields using series expansions and matching coefficients to the boundary conditions. In Sec.
5
, we simplify the form of the electromagnetic fields for a large radius, then the power and spin down equations are derived.

2
The Internal Fields of An Idealised Star

For the VRD solution, we consider the electric and magnetic fields of an
idealised star
. An idealised star is a star that is a sharply bounded sphere, is isotropic, is perfectly conducting, and has permittivity and permeability of a vacuum everywhere inside.
The boundary is not continuous but rather an immediate change between the star’s medium and the surrounding vacuum. Which, in the case of a real star is not true. However, it greatly simplifies the situation.

3.1
Matrix Transformation between Vector Field Basis Vectors

A vector transformation between the two coordinate systems mentioned is required whilst working out the boundary conditions for the fields in both coordinate systems. It is convenient to figure out the transformation now, then approach the boundary conditions. Define the transformation matrix
R:(\,\mathbf{\hat{r}},\,\bm{\hat{\theta}},\,\bm{\hat{\varphi}})\to(\,\mathbf{\hat{r}},\,\bm{\hat{\psi}},\,\bm{\hat{\nu}})
which rotates between the two unit vector systems at a point in space.
Figs.
1(a)
and
1(b)
show the two sets of basis vectors at the same point. It is clear that
\,\mathbf{\hat{r}}
points radially out, normal to the sphere’s surface, in both figures. Hence, at the same point in space

is the same in both coordinate systems. However, the angular components undergo a rotation as
\,\bm{\hat{\theta}},\,\bm{\hat{\varphi}},\,\bm{\hat{\psi}}

\,\bm{\hat{\nu}}

\,\mathbf{\hat{r}}

R=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cos\xi&-\sin\xi\\
0&\sin\xi&\cos\xi\end{bmatrix}.

(8)

This matrix transformation takes a basis of vectors at some point in space from
(\,\mathbf{\hat{r}},\,\bm{\hat{\theta}},\,\bm{\hat{\varphi}})
and rotates them anticlockwise by an angle
\xi
with
\,\mathbf{\hat{r}}
as axis to the unit vectors
(\,\mathbf{\hat{r}},\,\bm{\hat{\psi}},\,\bm{\hat{\nu}})
. To
reverse
this transformation the angle of rotation is replaced by

The angle
\xi
can be found in terms of already defined coordinate parameters and values. This is done by considering triangles on the surface of a sphere. The spherical triangle for this situation can be found in Fig.

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin\chi\sin\lambda}{\sin\psi},

(9)

\displaystyle\cos\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos\chi-\cos\psi\cos\theta}{\sin\psi\sin\theta}.

An interesting relationship that is obtained from the standard spherical triangle relation (

\cos\psi=\cos\chi\cos\theta+\sin\chi\sin\theta\cos\lambda,

(10)

which will be required momentarily.

4
Derivation of General Field Equations

The general field equations for the VRD solution external to the star are provided in the Appendix of
3
. However, with minimal explanation given in
3
about the derivation of the field equations, we wish to show how they are found.
We use results found in Chapters VII and VIII of
16
for this section.
Chapter VII proves beneficial in supplying general solutions to the vector wave equation which, must be satisfied by the time evolving electromagnetic fields around the neutron star. We apply these solutions to the neutron star situation by firstly considering
the
propagation factor
of the waves outside the star. In
16
, on page 392, the propagation factor (complex wavenumber) is given as,

k^{2}=\epsilon\mu\omega^{2}+i\sigma\mu\omega,

(15)

and as we are now considering the fields external of the star, in a vacuum,
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}
,
\mu=\mu_{0}
,
\sigma=0
and
c=1/\sqrt{\mu_{0}\epsilon_{0}}
. Hence,

k=\omega/c.

(16)

Then for convenience define,

\begin{gathered}\rho=kr=(\omega/c)r,\\
\alpha=ka=(\omega/c)a,\end{gathered}

(17)

where
\omega
is the rotational velocity of the star,
c
the speed of light, and
a
is the radius of the star. Here
\rho
and
\alpha
are unitless numbers.
A trivial relationship that will be required is

\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}=\frac{\omega}{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}.

Next consider the EM fields surrounding the star in two parts, a static time-independent component and a time-dependent component, where the complete field will be the linear combination of the two components (
5
).
The time-independent component will be axially symmetric about the axis
\bm{\omega}
and hence have no
\varphi
dependence (
m=0
,
m
will be defined clearer shortly). Whilst the time-dependent component will be non-axially symmetric and hence have a dependence on
\varphi
(
m\neq 0
).
This can be expressed as

\begin{gathered}\bm{H}=\bm{H}_{\text{static}}(r,\theta)+\bm{H}_{\text{time-dependent}}(r,\theta,\lambda),\\
\bm{E}=\bm{E}_{\text{static}}(r,\theta)+\bm{E}_{\text{time-dependent}}(r,\theta,\lambda).\end{gathered}

We first consider the time-dependent component in the following section on the wave solution. Then use a multipole expansion to provide the static component.

4.1
Wave Solution

Due to the rotating nature of the magnetic field symmetry axis around the star’s axis of rotation, the vector fields
\bm{E}
and
\bm{H}
satisfy the same vector differential wave equation from

\nabla^{2}\bm{C}-\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{C}}{\partial t^{2}}-\mu\sigma\frac{\partial\bm{C}}{\partial t}=0,

where
\bm{C}
represents either vector
\bm{E}

\bm{H}

We can express

\displaystyle 3ih_{2}(\rho)\cos\theta e^{i\varphi}\,\bm{\hat{\theta}}-3h_{2}(\rho)\cos{2\theta}e^{i\varphi}\,\bm{\hat{\varphi}},

\displaystyle 2\frac{h_{1}(\rho)}{\rho}\sin\theta e^{i\varphi}\,\mathbf{\hat{r}}+\frac{1}{\rho}(\rho h_{1}^{\prime}(\rho)+h_{1}(\rho))\cos\theta e^{i\varphi}\,\bm{\hat{\theta}}

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{\rho}(\rho h_{1}^{\prime}(\rho)+h_{1}(\rho))e^{i\varphi}\,\bm{\hat{\varphi}},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1,2}=

\displaystyle 9\frac{h_{2}(\rho)}{\rho}\sin{2\theta}e^{i\varphi}\,\mathbf{\hat{r}}+\frac{3}{\rho}(\rho h_{2}^{\prime}(\rho)+h_{2}(\rho))\cos{2\theta}e^{i\varphi}\,\bm{\hat{\theta}}

\displaystyle+\frac{3i}{\rho}(\rho h_{2}^{\prime}(\rho)+h_{2}(\rho))\cos\theta e^{i\varphi}\,\bm{\hat{\varphi}},

where we have neglected the operator that extracts the real part of the result. This will be added during the calculation for the coefficients in (
19
).

4.2
Multipole Expansion

A static field symmetric about the rotation axis can be expressed using a multipole expansion. Upon inspection of the static terms in the boundary conditions (
14
) and the multipole expansions given in
5
it can be seen, with some working, that only a magnetic dipole and electric quadrupole are present in this situation based on the conditions that multipole terms are linearly independent of each other and that the boundary conditions will uniquely determine the fields (electromagnetism uniqueness theorem, see
5
).

4.2.1
Magnetic Dipole

We can begin by taking a magnetic dipole and check whether it satisfies the
H
field boundary condition from (
14
). From equation (5.88) of

5
, a magnetic dipole is given as,

\bm{H}_{\text{dip}}(\bm{r})=\frac{m}{4\pi r^{3}}\left(2\cos\theta\,\mathbf{\hat{r}}+\sin\theta\,\bm{\hat{\theta}}\right),

(24)

where
m
is the magnetic dipole moment of the field. Now compare the radial component of (
24
) to the static term of the radial
H
field boundary condition from (
14
).

\frac{m}{4\pi a^{3}}2\cos\theta=R_{1}(a)\cos\chi\cos\theta.

We see that the function depending on
\theta
in the boundary condition is satisfied fully by the dipole term. Hence, a magnetic dipole is the only static term present due to the boundary conditions. This implies the ratio
m/(4\pi)
will satisfy,

\frac{m}{4\pi}=\frac{1}{2}a^{3}R_{1}(a)\cos\chi.

Therefore, the static component of the magnetic field will be

\bm{H}_{\text{static}}(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{2}\frac{a^{3}}{r^{3}}R_{1}(a)\cos\chi\left(2\cos\theta\,\mathbf{\hat{r}}+\sin\theta\,\bm{\hat{\theta}}\right).

(25)

4.2.2
Electric Quadrupole

We begin by assuming the solution for the static electric field to be a quadrupole, after which we show that it is the only component required to satisfy the boundary conditions due to the dependency on
\theta
. From
5
, equation (3.65), the quadrupole potential term is

V_{\text{quad}}(r)=\frac{B_{2}}{r^{3}}\frac{1}{2}\left(3\cos^{2}\theta-1\right).

where
B_{2}
is the weighting coefficient from the series expansion in
5
.

From equation (2.23) of
5
, the electric field is the negative gradient of the potential. Hence, the polar component of the
E
field is

E_{\text{quad},\,\theta}=-\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}=\frac{B_{2}}{r^{4}}3\cos\theta\sin\theta.

The functions depending on the variable
\theta
match those of the boundary condition (
14
). Therefore, the boundary condition only requires the static component of the electric field to be a quadrupole. Comparing the above result to (
14
) we can uniquely determine
B_{2}
for the situation,

\begin{gathered}\frac{B_{2}}{a^{4}}3\cos\theta\sin\theta=-\omega\mu_{0}aR_{1}(a)\cos\chi\cos\theta\sin\theta,\\
\implies B_{2}=-\frac{1}{3}\omega\mu_{0}a^{5}R_{1}(a)\cos\chi.\end{gathered}

and on applying the gradient function to the potential in spherical coordinates (see
E
),

\displaystyle E_{\text{quad},\,r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}=-\frac{1}{2}\omega\mu_{0}a\frac{a^{4}}{r^{4}}R_{1}(a)\cos\chi\left(3\cos^{2}\theta-1\right),

\displaystyle E_{\text{quad},\,\theta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}=-\omega\mu_{0}aR_{1}(a)\frac{a^{4}}{r^{4}}\cos\chi\cos\theta\sin\theta,

\displaystyle E_{\text{quad},\,\varphi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\varphi}=0.

The trigonometric identities
3\cos^{2}\theta-1=\frac{1}{2}\left(3\cos{2\theta}+1\right)
and
\cos\theta\sin\theta=\frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta,

can be used to write the quadrupole terms in a similar form as
3
,

\displaystyle E_{\text{quad},\,r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\omega\mu_{0}a\frac{a^{4}}{r^{4}}R_{1}(a)\cos\chi\left(3\cos 2\theta+1\right),

(26)

\displaystyle E_{\text{quad},\,\theta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\omega\mu_{0}aR_{1}(a)\frac{a^{4}}{r^{4}}\cos\chi\sin 2\theta,

\displaystyle E_{\text{quad},\,\varphi}

\displaystyle=0.

\bm{E}=\bm{E}_{\text{quad}}-(a_{1}\bm{\mathrm{m}}_{1,1}+a_{2}\bm{\mathrm{m}}_{1,2}+b_{1}\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1,1}+b_{2}\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1,2})e^{-i\omega t},\\

\displaystyle H_{r}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{ic\mu_{0}}a_{1}\frac{h_{1}(\alpha)}{\alpha}\cos\lambda=R_{1}(a)\sin\chi\cos\lambda,

As there is no

\displaystyle-b_{1}\frac{1}{\rho}(\rho h_{1}^{\prime}(\rho)+h_{1}(\rho))\cos\theta e^{i\varphi}e^{-i\omega t}

\displaystyle-b_{2}\frac{3}{\rho}(\rho h_{2}^{\prime}(\rho)+h_{2}(\rho))\cos{2\theta}e^{i\varphi}e^{-i\omega t},

Once again, matching functions of

\displaystyle-b_{2}\frac{3}{\alpha}(\alpha h_{2}^{\prime}(\alpha)+h_{2}(\alpha))\cos{2\theta}\cos\lambda

\begin{aligned} E_{\theta}=&\frac{1}{2}\omega\mu_{0}aR_{1}(a)\left[-\frac{a^{4}}{r^{4}}\cos\chi\sin{2\theta}\right.\\

5
The Radiation Fields of the Star

Far from a star, the electromagnetic fields take on their radiation form, allowing us to find the fields which ‘leak’ out of the magnetosphere and propagate away from the electromagnetic source. To find the radiation form of the electromagnetic fields we consider the limit in which
r\gg c/\omega

(
Griffiths 2017
)
.
Hence, from (
17
) if
r\gg c/\omega
then
\rho\gg 1
. Then the limit of the spherical Bessel functions when
\rho
is large (
46
) can be substituted into (
40
).

However, we find that
\alpha=(\omega/c)a\ll 1
, which means
h_{1}(\alpha)
can be approximated using (
45
).
Using
c\simeq$3\text{\times}{10}^{8}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
and the typical star values given by
3
,

\begin{gathered}a\simeq${10}^{9}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$,\quad\omega\simeq${10}^{-5}\text{\,}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$,\end{gathered}

we get
\alpha\simeq 3\times 10^{-5}\ll 1
. While a typical neutron star has

\begin{gathered}a\simeq${10}^{4}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$,\quad\omega\simeq 2\pi\,$\text{\,}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$,\end{gathered}

which gives
\alpha\simeq 2\times 10^{-4}\ll 1
.
We can also express this as a scaled equation,

\alpha=2\times 10^{-4}\left(\frac{a}{${10}^{4}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$}\right)\left(\frac{\omega}{2\pi\,$\text{\,}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$}\right)

The fastest recorded neutron star has
a\simeq$1.6\text{\times}{10}^{4}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
and
\omega\simeq$4.5\text{\times}{10}^{3}\text{\,}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$

(
Hessels et al. 2006
)
. We see from these values that
\alpha\simeq 0.2<1
. Though this limit is not as strong as slower neutron stars, it shows an appreciable upper limit that is still less than one. Suggesting that taking the approximation
\alpha\ll 1
in the following derivations is valid for slower stars but can still apply to faster stars, but with some error.

5.1
Radiation Form Derivation

To derive the radiation form we take the limit in which
r\gg c/\omega
. In taking this limit, we only consider terms that will be of the largest magnitude for each electromagnetic field component, neglecting terms containing high powers of
r
in their denominator. Recall
\Re
denotes the real part of an expression from (
22
) and
\lambda=\varphi-\omega t
from (
6
).

Working firstly on the
H
field’s radial component, after neglecting smaller magnitude terms
and substituting the limit forms of
h_{1}(\rho)
and
h_{1}(\alpha)
in,
H_{r}
becomes

\displaystyle H_{r}

\displaystyle\simeq\Re\left({R_{1}(a)\frac{e^{i\rho}}{\rho^{2}}}\frac{\alpha^{3}}{i}\sin\chi\sin\theta e^{i\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\Re\left(R_{1}(a)\frac{\omega}{c}\frac{a^{3}}{r^{2}}\frac{1}{i}\sin\chi\sin\theta e^{i(\lambda+\rho)}\right)

\displaystyle=\Re\left(R_{1}(a)\frac{\omega}{c}\frac{a^{3}}{r^{2}}\frac{1}{i}\sin\chi\sin\theta\left[\cos(\lambda+\rho)+i\sin(\lambda+\rho)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{c}a^{3}R_{1}(a)\frac{1}{r^{2}}\sin\chi\sin\theta\sin\left[\omega\left(\frac{r}{c}-t\right)+\varphi\right],

where
\lambda+\rho=\varphi-\omega t+\omega r/c=\omega(r/c-t)+\varphi
.
Repeating the same limit and substituting the approximations in for the polar component yields,

\begin{aligned} H_{\theta}&\simeq\Re\left(\frac{1}{2}R_{1}(a)\left[\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\alpha\frac{9i}{\alpha^{4}}-\frac{3i}{\alpha^{3}}}\right)\frac{ie^{i\rho}}{\rho}+\frac{\alpha^{3}}{-i}\left(\frac{-ie^{i\rho}}{\rho}-\frac{e^{i\rho}}{\rho^{2}}\right)\right]\sin\chi\cos\theta e^{i\lambda}\right)\\
&=\Re\left(\frac{1}{2}R_{1}(a)\left[\frac{\alpha^{5}}{6\rho}+\frac{\alpha^{3}}{\rho}+\frac{\alpha^{3}}{i\rho^{2}}\right]\sin\chi\cos\theta e^{i(\lambda+\rho)}\right).\end{aligned}

The term with the largest magnitude in the line above is the
\alpha^{3}/\rho
term as
\alpha\ll 1
and
\rho\gg 1
. So,

\displaystyle H_{\theta}

\displaystyle\simeq\Re\left(\frac{1}{2}R_{1}(a)\frac{\alpha^{3}}{\rho}\sin\chi\cos\theta e^{i(\lambda+\rho)}\right)

\displaystyle=\Re\left(\frac{1}{2}R_{1}(a)\frac{\alpha^{3}}{\rho}\sin\chi\cos\theta\left[\cos(\lambda+\rho)+i\sin(\lambda+\rho)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}a^{3}R_{1}(a)\frac{1}{r}\sin\chi\cos\theta\cos\left[\omega\left(\frac{r}{c}-t\right)+\varphi\right].

The azimuthal component has similar intermediate steps as above. Hence skipping intermediate working gives,

\begin{aligned} H_{\varphi}&\simeq\Re\left(\frac{1}{2}R_{1}(a)\left[\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\frac{9i}{\alpha^{4}}-\frac{3i}{\alpha^{3}}}\right)\frac{ie^{i\rho}}{\rho}\cos{2\theta}-\frac{\alpha^{3}}{i}\left(\frac{-ie^{i\rho}}{\rho}+\frac{-e^{i\rho}}{\rho^{2}}\right)\right]i\sin\chi e^{i\lambda}\right)\\
&=\Re\left(\frac{1}{2}R_{1}(a)\left[\left(\frac{\alpha^{6}}{9-3\alpha}\right)\frac{1}{\rho}\cos{2\theta}+\frac{\alpha^{3}}{\rho}+\frac{\alpha^{3}}{i\rho^{2}}\right]i\sin\chi e^{i(\lambda+\rho)}\right).\end{aligned}

The largest term in the line above is the
\alpha^{3}/\rho
term. Removing all smaller magnitude terms yields,

\displaystyle H_{\varphi}

\displaystyle\simeq\Re\left(\frac{1}{2}R_{1}(a)\frac{\alpha^{3}}{\rho}i\sin\chi e^{i(\lambda+\rho)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}a^{3}R_{1}(a)\frac{1}{r}\sin\chi\sin\left[\omega\left(\frac{r}{c}-t\right)+\varphi\right].

Now consider the
E
field. Using similar intermediate steps and the same principle as before where the largest order term is the only term considered
and the limit forms of
h_{1}(\rho)
and
h_{1}(\alpha)
are used
. The E field’s radial component will be,

\displaystyle E_{r}

\displaystyle\simeq\Re\left(\frac{1}{2}\omega\mu_{0}aR_{1}(a)3\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha\frac{9i}{\alpha^{4}}-\frac{3i}{\alpha^{3}}}\right)\frac{ie^{i\rho}}{\rho^{2}}\sin\chi\sin{2\theta}e^{i\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\Re\left(\frac{3}{2}\omega\mu_{0}aR_{1}(a)\frac{\alpha^{4}}{6}\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\sin\chi\sin{2\theta}e^{i(\lambda+\rho)}\right)

however, due to the
\alpha^{4}/\rho^{2}
term, the radial component is equivalently equal to zero when compared to the other
E
field components, this implies that
E_{r}\approx 0
. The polar component is,

\begin{aligned} E_{\theta}&\simeq\Re\left(\frac{1}{2}\omega\mu_{0}aR_{1}(a)\left[\frac{\alpha^{4}}{6i}\left(\frac{-e^{i\rho}+\frac{ie^{i\rho}}{\rho}}{\rho}\right)\cos{2\theta}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{i\rho}e^{i\rho}\right]\sin\chi e^{i\lambda}\right)\\
&=\Re\left(\frac{1}{2}\omega\mu_{0}aR_{1}(a)\left[\frac{\alpha^{4}}{6i}\left(\frac{-1}{\rho}+\frac{i}{\rho^{2}}\right)\cos{2\theta}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{i\rho}\right]\sin\chi e^{i(\lambda+\rho)}\right)\\
&\simeq-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\omega^{2}\mu_{0}}{c}a^{3}R_{1}(a)\frac{1}{r}\sin\chi\sin\left[\omega\left(\frac{r}{c}-t\right)+\varphi\right].\end{aligned}

By using some of the previous intermediate steps from above, the azimuthal component is,

\begin{aligned} E_{\varphi}&\simeq\Re\left(\frac{1}{2}\omega\mu_{0}aR_{1}(a)\left[\frac{\alpha^{4}}{6i}\left(\frac{-1}{\rho}+\frac{i}{\rho^{2}}\right)-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{i\rho}\right]i\sin\chi\cos\theta e^{i(\lambda+\rho)}\right)\\
&\simeq-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\omega^{2}\mu_{0}}{c}a^{3}R_{1}(a)\frac{1}{r}\sin\chi\cos\theta\cos\left[\omega\left(\frac{r}{c}-t\right)+\varphi\right].\end{aligned}

5.2
Power Equation - D55 Equation (15)

The power

\bm{S}=\bm{E}\times\bm{H}

5

P(r,t)=\oint{\bm{S}\cdot d\bm{a}}.

(32)

This is the power passing through the surface, so a negative must be introduced to give the rate at which energy is radiated away from the system,

-\frac{dW}{dt}=\oint{\bm{S}\cdot d\bm{a}}.

(33)

For the
E
and
H
fields
5
advocates taking the fields when
r
is very large, when they are in
radiation form
. Therefore, by taking the field equations (
41
) and (
42
) (which apply when
r\gg c/\omega
) leads to the Poynting vector,

\bm{S}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
E_{\theta}\\
E_{\varphi}\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}H_{r}\\
H_{\theta}\\
H_{\varphi}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}E_{\theta}H_{\varphi}-E_{\varphi}H_{\theta}\\
E_{\varphi}H_{r}\\
E_{\theta}H_{r}\end{pmatrix}

and by also taking the surface to be a sphere of radius
r
, this implies that
S_{r}\parallel\bm{a}
,
S_{\theta}\perp\bm{a}
and
S_{\varphi}\perp\bm{a}
, as
\bm{a}
is the normal of the surface (see Fig.
1(a)
).
So (
33
) becomes,

-\frac{dW}{dt}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}(E_{\theta}H_{\varphi}-E_{\varphi}H_{\theta})r^{2}\sin(\theta)\,d\theta\,d\varphi.

Upon applying expressions for the
H
and
E
fields from (
41
) and (
42
) we get,

\begin{aligned} -\frac{dW}{dt}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}&\frac{1}{4}\frac{\omega^{4}\mu_{0}}{c^{3}}a^{6}R_{1}^{2}(a)\sin^{2}(\chi)\left\{\sin^{2}\left[\omega\left(\frac{r}{c}-t\right)+\varphi\right]\right.\\
&\left.+\cos^{2}(\theta)\cos^{2}\left[\omega\left(\frac{r}{c}-t\right)+\varphi\right]\right\}\sin(\theta)\,d\theta\,d\varphi.\end{aligned}

(34)

Now we evaluate the integrals over the functions that depend on
\theta
and
\varphi
. The double integral can firstly be expressed as,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}&\sin(\theta)\sin^{2}\left[\omega\left(\frac{r}{c}-t\right)+\varphi\right]\\
&+\sin(\theta)\cos^{2}(\theta)\cos^{2}\left[\omega\left(\frac{r}{c}-t\right)+\varphi\right]\,d\theta\,d\varphi,\end{aligned}

(35)

and using the integration identities,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin(\theta)\,d\theta=2,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin(\theta)\cos^{2}(\theta)\,d\theta=\frac{2}{3},

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{2}\left[\omega\left(\frac{r}{c}-t\right)+\varphi\right]\,d\varphi\\
=\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos^{2}\left[\omega\left(\frac{r}{c}-t\right)+\varphi\right]\,d\varphi=\pi,\end{gathered}

which leads to
the integral
(

) yielding
8\pi/3
. On combining this result with (

) we get

-\frac{dW}{dt}=\frac{2\pi\omega^{4}\mu_{0}}{3c^{3}}a^{6}R_{1}^{2}(a)\sin^{2}(\chi).

(38)

This is the same result obtained by
3
in his equation (15). The RHS is always positive. Hence,
dW/dt
must always be negative. That is, energy is always being radiated away from the star. The energy that is radiated comes from the rotational kinetic energy causing the star’s spin down.

5.2.1
Spin Down Rate - D55 Equation (16)

To derive the spin down rate of a star in the VRD solution, we require two standard relationships (see for example
Moebs et al. 2016
, Eqns. (10.31) and (11.8)),

\bm{\tau}=\frac{d\bm{L}}{dt},\qquad\frac{dW}{dt}=P=\bm{\tau}\cdot\bm{\omega},\quad\implies\quad\frac{dW}{dt}=\frac{d\bm{L}}{dt}\cdot\bm{\omega},

where
\bm{L}
is the angular momentum and
\bm{\tau}
is the torque.
As the angular velocity and the angular momentum are in the same direction, we can rearrange the equation above to become,

\frac{d\bm{L}}{dt}=\frac{\bm{\omega}}{\left\lVert\bm{\omega}\right\rVert^{2}}\frac{dW}{dt},

which is the result obtained by
3
.

However, another typo is present in
3
. It is not obviously shown that his
\omega
is actually a vector and that the
\omega
in the bottom of the fraction is the norm of
\omega
. Explaining why he has
\omega/\omega^{2}
rather than just
1/\omega
.
Lastly,
3
displays it with a negative so it can be compared easier with (
38
),

-\frac{d\bm{L}}{dt}=\frac{\bm{\omega}}{\left\lVert\bm{\omega}\right\rVert^{2}}\left(-\frac{dW}{dt}\right).

(39)

It can be seen from this equation that the angular momentum also decreases with time. The star will be brought to rest by its own radiation as
3
states.

6
Conclusion

By considering an idealised star and assuming a dipolar form for the internal magnetic field, the general field equations of the electromagnetic fields external of the star were found analytically. These general field equations were then able to be reduced to their radiation limit (
r\gg c/\omega
). From the radiation form, the relationship for the power radiated by the rotating magnetic field was found.

It was found that the power equation matches the equation found in
3
and the derivation for which is shown in this report for scrutiny. However, a typo appears in
3
’s general field equations. In particular, the
E_{\theta}
(
31
) component appears to evaluate the wrong fraction at
\alpha
. This error in
3
causes trouble when reducing the general field equations to their radiation form. When using the
E_{\theta}
component found in this report the reduction to radiation form matches
3
.

A plot of the magnetic field using the general field equations was also produced, showing the summation of the magnetic dipole and the wave solution. This generalises a similar plot given in
3
which was zoomed in around the
r\ll c/\omega
region.

A 1969 paper by Goldreich and Julian argues that a pulsar cannot be surrounded by a vacuum but rather a magnetosphere containing plasma
(
Goldreich & Julian 1969
)
.
A related approach
can be taken when estimating the power equation. The Force-Free (FF) magnetosphere, which is the solution to Maxwell’s Equations for a rotating star with a dipole field. It ignores the plasma pressure and requires that the electric field parallel to the magnetic field is zero everywhere
(
Contopoulos et al. 1999
)
. This solution requires approximations just like the VRD solution. The most realistic way is to use numerical simulations. The results from
Kalapotharakos et al. 2018
find their power equations ranging from the near FF solution to near the VRD solution given in equation (
38
). The VRD solution proves to be an interesting limiting case of numerical simulations.

The VRD solution discussed in this review has been extended in a number of ways. For example,
general relativistic corrections to the Deustch solution can be added
(
Rezzolla et al. 2001
;
Pétri 2013
)
. Also, considering an electromagnetic field that consists of more multipole terms than just a dipole adds extra correction terms that are potentially required to match reality
(
Pétri 2015
;
Bonazzola et al. 2020
)
.
