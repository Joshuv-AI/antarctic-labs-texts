Explicit parametrization of more than one vector-like quark of Nelson-Barr type
G. H. S. Alves, A. L. Cherchiglia, C. C. Nishi

Abstract
Nelson-Barr models solve the strong CP problem based on spontaneous CP violation and generically requires vector-like quarks (VLQs) mixing with standard quarks to transmit the CP violation. We devise an explicit parametrization for the case of two VLQs of either down-type or up-type and quantitatively study several aspects including the hierarchy of the VLQ Yukawas and their irreducible contribution to θ ¯ \bar{\theta} . In particular, with the use of the parametrization, we show that a big portion of the parameter space for two up-type VLQs at the TeV scale is still allowed by the constraint on θ ¯ \bar{\theta} , although this case had been previously shown to be very restricted based on estimates.

III.4
Invariants estimating
\bar{\theta}

The CP violation of a model can be quantified through invariants that are defined by a combination of the CP violation sources of that model and are independent of the reparametrization of their fields. In the SM, the unique experimentally measured CP violation is described by the Jarlskog invariant
Jarlskog
.

In Nelson-Barr models,
\bar{\theta}
is arranged to vanish at tree level
nelson
;
barr
but loop corrections may arise already at the 1-loop level
BBP
.
These corrections, however, may be arbitrarily suppressed by suppressing one or both of the following couplings: the Yukawa coupling between SM quarks and VLQs or the scalar portal couplings between the CP violating scalar(s) and the SM Higgs.

However, we have shown in
nb-vlq
that the Yukawa couplings between the SM quarks and the VLQs cannot be arbitrarily small as full decoupling of the VLQs would not provide the SM with the necessary CP violation.
Therefore, as shown in Ref.
vecchi.1
, there are irreducible and non-decoupling contributions, first appearing at 3-loops, that depends solely on these Yukawa couplings.
These contributions were estimated through the construction of CP odd flavor invariants.
For a single down-type and a single up-type VLQ, the leading order invariants with fewer SM Yukawas insertions were shown to be
vecchi.1

\displaystyle I^{d}_{1,0}

\displaystyle\equiv\left({Y^{B}}^{\dagger}\left[Y^{d}{Y^{d}}^{\dagger},Y^{u}{Y^{u}}^{\dagger}\right]Y^{B}\right),

(40)

\displaystyle I^{u}_{1,0}

\displaystyle\equiv\left({Y^{T}}^{\dagger}\left[Y^{d}{Y^{d}}^{\dagger},Y^{u}{Y^{u}}^{\dagger}\right]Y^{T}\right)\,.

These invariants provide an estimate for
\bar{\theta}
within an order of magnitude since order one pre-factors are expected in a calculation within a full model.

for the 3-loop contribution to

\tilde{y}_{i}
is confined to this sphere,
\mathcal{O}
can be found from (
64
) as a rotation that connects it to
b_{i}
, except for rotations that leave
b_{i}
invariant.
We parametrize this freedom by an angle
\gamma
, which only affects (
59
).

One difficulty is that not all the sphere in (
69
) is physical. We need to ensure
ww^{\dagger}={\mathbbm{1}}_{3}-\Omega
is positive semidefinite.
Let us first analyze the case where all
x_{i}\neq 0
. This covers the case
n_{B}\geq 3
and most of
n_{B}=2
.
We prove in appendix
B
that
\tilde{y}_{i}
should be confined to the elipsoid

\frac{\tilde{y}_{1}^{2}(1-x_{2})(1-x_{3})}{x_{2}x_{3}}+\frac{\tilde{y}_{2}^{2}(1-x_{1})(1-x_{3})}{x_{1}x_{3}}+\frac{\tilde{y}_{3}^{2}(1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})}{x_{1}x_{2}}\leq 1\,.

(70)

The inequality is for
n_{B}\geq 3
and the equality is for
n_{B}=2
.
To have an intersection, the ellipsoid (
70
) cannot be internal to the sphere (
69
) and then at least one of its semiaxes needs to be larger than
\mu
, so we need for the largest semiaxis,

\frac{x_{2}x_{3}}{(1-x_{2})(1-x_{3})}\geq\mu^{2}\,.

(71)

Note that the function
f(x)=x/(1-x)
is a monotonically increasing function in the interval
[0,1)
with
f(1/2)=1
.
For
x_{2}=x_{3}
, the equality is achieved for
x_{2}=\mu/(1+\mu)
which is less than
1/2
.
If we introduce the shorthand notation

f_{i}=f(x_{i})\,,

(72)

we can rewrite (
71
) as

f_{2}f_{3}\geq\mu^{2}\,.

(73)

On the other extreme, if the smallest semiaxis obeys

f_{1}f_{2}\geq\mu^{2}\,,

(74)

then the whole sphere is allowed.

If
x_{1}=0
(
\tilde{y}_{2}=\tilde{y}_{3}=0
), then (
69
) implies that
\tilde{y}_{1}^{2}=\mu^{2}
.
As
\Omega
becomes block diagonal, the condition (
70
) is replaced exactly by (
73
) where the inequality is valid for
n_{B}=2
and the equality is for
n_{B}=1
.
The case of inequality restricts the region for
(x_{2},x_{3})
in the unit square
(0,1)\times(0,1)
, restricted to
x_{2}<x_{3}
.

Let us now specialize to
n_{B}=2
.
The subcase
x_{1}=0
was treated above so we assume
x_{1}>0
.
If
x_{1}>0
, we can isolate
f_{3}
in (
70
) for the equality as

f_{3}=\frac{1}{f_{1}f_{2}-\tilde{y}_{3}^{2}}\left(f_{1}\tilde{y}_{1}^{2}+f_{2}\tilde{y}_{2}^{2}\right)\,,

(75)

which is a solution once
f_{3}>0
is ensured.
Once
\tilde{y}_{i}
is restricted to the sphere (
69
),
condition (
71
) is indeed a necessity. Therefore we need to know the values of
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
and all
\tilde{y}_{i}
to determine
f_{3}
. In the case of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, we just need to know the values of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
.
As
\tilde{y}_{i}
are restricted to the sphere (
69
), we parametrize

\begin{split}\tilde{y}_{1}&=\mu\sin(\phi_{2})\cos(\phi_{1}),\\
\tilde{y}_{2}&=\mu\sin(\phi_{2})\sin(\phi_{1}),\\
\tilde{y}_{3}&=\mu\cos(\phi_{2}).\end{split}

(76)

Thus,
x_{3}
is determined from
f_{3}
which in turn is a function of
\Lambda
and the parameters

\{x_{1},x_{2},\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\}
.

Once
x_{3}
is determined,
\Omega_{1}
is obtained from

\Omega_{1}=\diag(1-x_{1},1-x_{2},1-x_{3})\,.

(77)

Finally,
\mathscr{Y}^{d}
is determined from the inversion formula (
59
) which depends on the SM parameters in
\Lambda
and additionally on

\{x_{1},x_{2},\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\gamma,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}.

(78)

For the case
x_{1}=0
, the additional parameters of the inversion formula becomes

\{x_{2},x_{3},\gamma,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}\,.

(79)

Let us check the number of parameters so far for

n_{B}=2
.
In this case, there are 21 parameters in total among which 10 must account for the SM flavor parameters.
Three of the latter are just the three up-type quark masses of the SM.
Another 7 SM parameters reside in
\Lambda
. The remaining 11 parameters should be free, 7 of which are listed in (
78
).
There are still 4 more parameters that are not needed for
\mathscr{Y}^{d}
.
We treat them in the following.

IV.2
Additional parameters in
w
and
\mathscr{M}^{B}

In Sec.
IV.1
, to account for the SM flavor parameters in

to describe
\mathscr{Y}^{d}
– the inversion formula –, we have chosen the basis (

) for

.
However, additional parameters are still needed to describe

itself and this quantity enters in

in (

We first analyze

where

and

and

The matrix

and the singular values in

in (

are the additional parameters in

Specializing now to

\end{pmatrix}\,.

In the basis (

So

We can now analyze

). Note that the latter is non-diagonal and complex in general.
For

V
Summary

Generalizing the parametrization found in Ref.

With the explicit parametrization in hand, we studied several aspects of the model.
In special, we analyzed the flavor invariants presented in Ref.

On the other hand, confirming Ref.

But even if the

Therefore, it is crucial that an explicit parametrization be used for a detailed and quantitative analysis, as some quantities may deviate from the estimates by few orders of magnitude.

Appendix A
Partial diagonalization

The changing of basis from (
1
) to (
2
) is easily described by comparing the complete mass matrix of the down-type quarks following from each case after EWSB:

\text{NB:}\quad\mathscr{M}^{d+B}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{v}{\sqrt{2}}\mathscr{Y}^{d}&0\cr\mathscr{M}^{Bd}&\mathscr{M}_{B}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\text{generic:}\quad M^{d+B}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{v}{\sqrt{2}}Y^{d}&\frac{v}{\sqrt{2}}Y^{B}\cr 0&M_{B}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(104)

Only a unitary transformation from the right is necessary to connect them:

\mathscr{M}^{d+B}W_{R}=M^{d+B}\,.

(105)

We can find an explicit form for
W_{R}
in (
105
) by assuming

W_{R}=\begin{pmatrix}\big({\mathbbm{1}}_{3}-ww^{\dagger}\big)^{1/2}&w\cr-w^{\dagger}&\big({\mathbbm{1}}_{n}-w^{\dagger}w\big)^{1/2}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(106)

It will be easier, however, to use a slightly different parametrization

W_{R}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbbm{1}}_{3}&\tilde{w}\cr-\tilde{w}^{\dagger}&{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}({\mathbbm{1}}_{3}+\tilde{w}\tilde{w}^{\dagger})^{-1/2}&0\cr 0&({\mathbbm{1}}_{n}+\tilde{w}^{\dagger}\tilde{w})^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(107)

valid for
\tilde{w}\neq 0
, where both matrices are not unitary but the product is.
The crucial point is that only the first matrix matters to guarantee the zero block in
M^{d+B}
so that
the solution is easily

\tilde{w}^{\dagger}={\mathscr{M}^{B}}^{-1}\mathscr{M}^{Bd}\,,

(108)

where
\mathscr{M}^{B}
is guaranteed to be nonsingular.
We can write everything in terms of
w
if needed.
For example,

w=\tilde{w}({\mathbbm{1}}_{n}+\tilde{w}^{\dagger}\tilde{w})^{-1/2}=({\mathbbm{1}}_{3}+\tilde{w}\tilde{w}^{\dagger})^{-1/2}\tilde{w}\,.

(109)

If
W_{R}\sim 2\times 2
were real, we would have
w=\sin\theta
while
\tilde{w}=\tan\theta
for some angle
\theta
.

Then we get explicitly

\displaystyle Y^{d}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{Y}^{d}({\mathbbm{1}}_{3}+\tilde{w}\tilde{w}^{\dagger})^{-1/2}=\mathscr{Y}^{d}({\mathbbm{1}}_{3}-ww^{\dagger})^{+1/2}\,,

(110a)

\displaystyle Y^{B}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{Y}^{d}\tilde{w}({\mathbbm{1}}_{n}+\tilde{w}^{\dagger}\tilde{w})^{-1/2}=\mathscr{Y}^{d}w\,,

(110b)

\displaystyle M^{B}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{M}^{B}({\mathbbm{1}}_{n}+\tilde{w}^{\dagger}\tilde{w})^{+1/2}=\mathscr{M}^{B}({\mathbbm{1}}_{n}-w^{\dagger}w)^{-1/2}\,.

(110c)

These are the relations in (
5
).

Appendix B
Parameter region for positive (semi)definite matrix

Let
A\sim 3\times 3
be a complex positive definite or positive semidefinite matrix.
So this discussion applies to
Y^{d}{Y^{d}}^{\dagger}
, to
ww^{\dagger}
or to
\Omega={\mathbbm{1}}_{3}-ww^{\dagger}
.

We first decompose
A
into real and imaginary parts:

A=A_{1}+iA_{2}\,.

(111)

Allowing real orthogonal basis change, we choose a specific basis where
A_{1}
is diagonal:

A_{1}=\diag(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\,,\quad(A_{2})_{ij}=\epsilon_{ijk}b_{k}\,.

(112)

Note that
A_{1}
is positive (semi)definite if
A
is positive (semi)definite.
Then
a_{i}\geq 0
and we can order
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq a_{3}
.
We assume
b_{i}b_{i}\neq 0
to exclude real
A
.

If we calculate the characteristic equation,

\det(A-\lambda I)=-\big[\lambda^{3}-\gamma_{1}(A)\lambda^{2}-\gamma_{2}(A)\lambda-\gamma_{3}(A)\big]\,,

(113)

we obtain in the basis (
112
),

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(A)

\displaystyle=\Tr[A]=a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}\,,

(114)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(A)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\large$\tfrac{1}{2}$}\Tr[A^{2}-\gamma_{1}(A)A]=b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}+b_{3}^{2}-a_{1}a_{2}-a_{2}a_{3}-a_{3}a_{1}\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{3}(A)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\large$\tfrac{1}{3}$}\Tr[A^{3}-\gamma_{1}(A)A^{2}-\gamma_{2}(A)A]=\det(A)=a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}-(a_{1}b_{1}^{2}+a_{2}b_{2}^{2}+a_{3}b_{3}^{2})\,.

Because
\Tr[A]=\Tr[A_{1}]
but the determinant
\det(A)\leq\det(A_{1})
, the spectrum of
A_{1}
is squashed compared to the spectrum of
A
.

Positive definiteness of
A
is equivalent to the conditions

\gamma_{1}(A)>0\,,\quad\gamma_{2}(A)<0\,,\quad\gamma_{3}(A)>0\,.\quad

(115)

If one eigenvalue of
A
is zero, then
\gamma_{3}(A)=0
, and if two are zero, then
\gamma_{2}(A)=0
as well.

For
A=Y^{d}{Y^{d}}^{\dagger}
positive definite, we can obtain an interesting formula for
\mu
in

\Lambda_{1}^{-1/2}\Lambda_{2}\Lambda_{1}^{-1/2}\sim\mu\begin{pmatrix}0&&\cr&0&1\cr&-1&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(116)

where the righthandside is the canonical form of a real antisymmetric matrix.
In the basis (
112
), the matrix in (
116
) can be written as

(\Lambda_{1}^{-1/2}\Lambda_{2}\Lambda_{1}^{-1/2})_{ij}=\epsilon_{ijk}\tilde{b}_{k}\,,

(117)

where

\tilde{b}_{i}\equiv\frac{\sqrt{a_{i}}b_{i}}{\sqrt{a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}}}\,.

(118)

It is clear that
\mu=\sqrt{\tilde{b}_{i}\tilde{b}_{i}}
.
Rewriting the last relation in (
114
) as

\det(A)=\det(A_{1})(1-\tilde{b}_{i}\tilde{b}_{i})\,,

(119)

we obtain the formula

\mu=\sqrt{1-\frac{\det A}{\det A_{1}}}\,.

(120)

For
A=ww^{\dagger}
in (
63
), we substitute
a_{i}\to x_{i}
and
b_{i}\to-y_{i}
. If
A
is rank 1, by orthogonal transformations, we can go to a basis where
A
is block diagonal. Then
x_{1}=0
and
y_{2}=y_{3}=0
. We are left with the down-right subblock nonzero. The zero determinant condition on this subblock leads to
y_{1}^{2}=x_{2}x_{3}
.
If
A
is rank 2, the determinant
\det(A)
in (
114
) is zero and

x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}=x_{1}y_{1}^{2}+x_{2}y_{2}^{2}+x_{3}y_{3}^{2}\,.

(121)

We can still have
x_{1}=0
as a special possibility, in which case,
y_{2}=y_{3}=0
.
But
\gamma_{2}(A)<0
leads to
x_{1}^{2}<y_{2}y_{3}
.
If
A
is rank 3,
\det(A)>0
leads to

x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}>x_{1}y_{1}^{2}+x_{2}y_{2}^{2}+x_{3}y_{3}^{2}\,.

(122)

Equations (
121
) and (
122
) written in terms of
\tilde{y}_{i}
in (
66
) leads to the ellipsoid condition (
70
).
