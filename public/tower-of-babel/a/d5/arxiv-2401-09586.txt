Linearization in magnetoelasticity
Stefano Almi, Martin Kružík, Anastasia Molchanova

Abstract
. Starting from a model of nonlinear magnetoelasticity where magnetization is defined in the Eulerian configuration while elastic deformation is in the Lagrangian one, we rigorously derive a linearized model that coincides with the standard one that already appeared in the literature, see, e.g., [ 19 ] and where the zero-stress strain is quadratic in the magnetization. The relation of the nonlinear and linear model is stated in terms of the Γ \Gamma -convergence and convergence of minimizers.



1.
Introduction

Magnetoelasticity is a characteristic observed in certain solids wherein there is a significant interdependence between their mechanical and magnetic properties. This coupling manifests as reversible mechanical deformations that can be induced by applying an external magnetic field. This phenomenon holds considerable practical value, especially in the design of sensors, actuators, and various innovative functional-material devices,
see e.g.
[
28
]
.

The origin of magnetoelasticity can be traced to the intricate interplay between the crystallographic structure of the material, where different crystals exhibit distinct easy axes of magnetization, and magnetic domains
[
31
]
. In the absence of external magnetic fields, these magnetic domains align to minimize long-range dipolar effects, leading to a generally minimal or even negligible magnetization of the medium. Upon the application of an external magnetic field, the magnetic domains undergo a reorientation towards it. This reorientation leads to the emergence of a macroscopic deformation since magnetizations are linked to specific stress-free reference strains. As the magnetic field’s intensity increases, an increasing number of magnetic domains align themselves so that their principal axes of anisotropy align with the magnetic field in each region, eventually reaching saturation. For a deeper exploration of the foundational aspects of magnetoelasticity, refer to
[
14
,
23
,
31
]
.

Magnetoelasticity is a dynamic area of mathematical research which reflects modeling in linear and nonlinear regimes
[
19
,
40
]
, analysis in the static and quasistatic setting
[
5
,
12
,
10
]
while also being
coupled with thermal and other phenomena, e.g.
[
37
]
.
Moreover, dimension-reduction problems covering lower-dimensional structures in various scaling regimes were recently as well analyzed; for instance, see
[
10
]
or
[
18
]
.

The aim of our contribution is to establish a relationship between nonlinear and linear models of magnetoelasticity in the static case. To our best knowledge, it is the first situation where this program is undertaken in the combined Eulerian and Lagrangian description.

Let
\Omega
be a (connected) bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

(d\geq 2)
with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
, representing a reference configuration occupied by an elastic body. We fix
\Gamma
open subset of
\partial\Omega
such that
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\Gamma)>0
, where
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
denotes the
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. We consider the magnetoelastic energy of the following form
[
14
,
32
,
12
,
39
]
:

(1.1)

\mathcal{G}(y,m):=\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla y,m\circ y){\,\rm d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{y(\Omega)}|\nabla m|^{2}{\,\rm d}z+\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla v_{m}|^{2}{\,\rm d}z

for a deformation
y\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

with
p>d
,

\det\nabla y>0
a.e.,
y
is injective a.e. in
\Omega
, and
y=y_{0}
on
\Gamma
where
y_{0}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a given boundary datum,
and a magnetization
m\in W^{1,2}(y(\Omega)\setminus y(\partial\Omega);\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
In (
1.1
), the first term represents the elastic energy, the second term is the exchange energy, and
the last term stands for magnetostatic energy;
\mu_{0}
is the permittivity of void and
v_{m}
is the magnetostatic potential generated by
m
.
In particular,
v_{m}
is a solution to the Maxwell equation

\nabla\cdot(-\mu_{0}\nabla v_{m}+\chi_{y(\Omega)}m)=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\chi_{y(\Omega)}
the characteristic function of
y(\Omega)
. Furthermore, we can assume that the specimen temperature is fixed and therefore the Heisenberg normalized constraint

(1.2)

\displaystyle|m\circ y|\,\det\nabla y=1\text{ a.e.~in }\Omega

holds for all deformations

[
14
,
32
]
.

Let us point out some peculiarities caused by the mixed Eulerian–Lagrangian formulation.
First, to correctly define the composition
m\circ y
, we must identify
y
with its continuous representative, due to Sobolev embeddings theorems
[
34
, Section 1.4.5]
.
Second, as it is conventional in magnetoelasticity
[
39
,
12
,
13
]
, we define
m\in W^{1,2}(y(\Omega)\setminus y(\partial\Omega);\mathbb{R}^{d})
, since
y(\Omega)\setminus y(\partial\Omega)
is an open set
and it differs form
y(\Omega)
just on a set of measure zero, i.e.
|y(\Omega)|=|y(\Omega)\setminus y(\partial\Omega)|
,
see for example
[
25
]
or
[
12
, Lemma 2.1]
.

As in
[
24
,
41
]
, we suppose the deformation gradient
F
can be multiplicatively decomposed into magnetic and elastic parts
F=F_{m}F_{el}

with the magnetic part
F_{m}
being volume-preserving, i.e.,
\det F_{m}=1
.
In our setting, we assume

F_{m}:=\exp(-E(M))
, i.e.,
F^{-1}_{m}=\exp(E(M))
, where
E(M)
is defined as

E(M):=-M\otimes M+\frac{1}{d}I.

Here,
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a placeholder for the Lagrangian magnetization whose length
|M|
satisfies
M\cdot M=|M|^{2}=1
, and
I
is the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Note that
\operatorname{tr}E(M)=0
, so that, indeed,
\det F_{m}^{-1}=1
. In view of the Heisenberg constraint (
1.2
),

(1.3)

\displaystyle M(x)=m(y(x))\det\nabla y(x)

satisfies
|M(x)|=1
for
x\in\Omega
.
Consequently,
using the change-of-variables formula for Sobolev mappings
[
29
]
(considering that

y
is a.e. injective and satisfies the Lusin
(N)
-condition)
for
\omega\subset\Omega

it holds

\int_{\omega}M(x)\,{\,\rm d}x=\int_{y(\omega)}m(z)\,{\,\rm d}z.

Therefore,
M
and
m
transform as volume densities in the reference (Lagrangian) and the deformed (Eulerian) configurations, respectively.

We define
e\colon\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
as

e(F,m)=-(\det F)^{2}m\otimes m+\frac{1}{d}I.

Whenever
m
and
M
are two magnetic fields related by (
1.3
), then
e(\nabla y,m\circ y)=E(M)
in
\Omega
.

In the case
F=I
, we simplify the notation by setting
e(m):=e(I,m)=-m\otimes m+\frac{1}{d}I
.
Next, we assume that the elastic energy density is of form

W(F,m)=\Phi\left(\exp(e(F,m))F\right)=\Phi(F_{el}).

We recall that the matrix exponential
\exp(A)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A^{k}/k!
is defined for every
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, setting also

A^{0}=I
. Here,
\Phi
is a nonnegative function that is minimized precisely at the set of proper rotations. This form of the energy density is inspired by energy expressions in the following papers on nematic elastomers
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
20
]
.

In the
small-strain regime
, we set

y_{\varepsilon}=id+\varepsilon u
and assume that the influence of the magnetization on the elastic deformation of the specimen vanishes along
\varepsilon\to 0
in the sense that
F_{m,\varepsilon}=\exp(-\varepsilon E(M))
and
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}F_{m,\varepsilon}=I
.
Then, we consider the rescaled energy

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}W(Id+\varepsilon\nabla u(x),m(x+\varepsilon u(x))){\,\rm d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}|\nabla m(z)|^{2}{\,\rm d}z+\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla v_{m}(z)|^{2}{\,\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\Phi(\exp(\varepsilon E(M))(I+\varepsilon\nabla u(x)))\,{\,\rm d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}|\nabla m(z)|^{2}{\,\rm d}z+\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla v_{m}(z)|^{2}{\,\rm d}z.

We formally get the expression for the elastic strain

F_{el,\varepsilon}=F^{-1}_{m,\varepsilon}\nabla y_{\varepsilon}=\operatorname{exp}(\varepsilon E(M))\cdot(I+\varepsilon\nabla u)\approx I+\varepsilon(\nabla u+e(m)).

Moreover, a formal Taylor expansion shows that the limit energy is, as in
[
19
]
, of the form:

(1.5)

\mathcal{G}_{0}(u,m):=\frac{1}{{2}}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{C}(\bm{\epsilon}(u)+e(m)):(\bm{\epsilon}(u)+e(m)){\,\rm d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla m|^{2}{\,\rm d}x+\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla v_{m}|^{2}{\,\rm d}z,

where

e(u):=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla u^{T}+\nabla u)
is the symmetric part of the displacement gradient and

\mathbb{C}:=D^{2}\Phi(I)
is the elasticity tensor.
Note that (
1.5
) suggests a decomposition of the total strain
\bm{\epsilon}(u)
into the magnetic part
-e(m)=m\otimes m-I/d
and the elastic part
\bm{\epsilon}(u)+e(m)
.
This decomposition has already been used, e.g., in
[
19
,
33
,
35
]
.
The aim of this contribution is to derive (
1.5
) rigorously by means of
\Gamma
-convergence.

We use the following assumptions for
p>d
:

(a)

\Phi
is frame-indifferent;

(b)

\Phi
belongs to
C^{2}
in some neighbourhood of
SO(d)
;

(c)

\Phi(F)=0
if
F\in SO(d)
;

(d)

\Phi(F)\geq g_{p}(\operatorname{dist}(F,SO(d)))
for some
p
, where

(1.6)

g_{p}(t):=\begin{cases}\frac{t^{2}}{2},&\text{if }0\leq t\leq 1,\\
\frac{t^{p}}{p}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p},&\text{if }t>1.\end{cases}

(e)

there exist
a>1
and
C>0
such that

\Phi(F)\geq C\bigg(\frac{1}{(\det F)^{a}}-1\bigg)\qquad\text{for every $F\in{\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}}$.}

Let us notice that
g_{p}
satisfies the following properties:

g_{p}
is convex,

(1.7)

\displaystyle g_{p}(t)\leq\frac{1}{p}\min\{t^{p},t^{2}\}\,,\qquad g_{p}(s+t)\leq C(g_{p}(s)+t^{2})

for some constant
C>0
depending on
p
, and for any
C_{1}>0
there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
, depending only on
p
and on
C_{1}
, such that

(1.8)

g_{p}(C_{1}t)\leq C_{2}g_{p}(t)\,.

Frame-indifference
(a)
implies that
W(F,m)=W(F,-m)=W(QF,Qm)
for all
F\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
all
Q\in\textrm{SO}(d)
, and all
m\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Moreover, for every
F\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

(1.9)

D^{2}\Phi(I)[F,F]=D^{2}\Phi(I)[F_{sym},F_{sym}].

Together with
(d)
, it implies that

D^{2}\Phi(I)[F,F]=0
if
F=-F^{\top}

and

(1.10)

D^{2}\Phi(I)[F_{sym},F_{sym}]\geq|F_{sym}|^{2}.

Above
F_{sym}
stands for the symmetric part of
F
.
While
(b)
together with
\Phi(I)=D\Phi(I)=0
leads to

(1.11)

\Phi(I+F)\geq\frac{1}{2}D^{2}\Phi(I)[F,F]-\eta(|F|)|F|^{2},

where
\eta
is an increasing nonnegative function such that
\eta(t)\leq C_{R}t
for
|t|\leq R
and
R>0
.

In what follows, the work of a mechanical load is modeled by a continuous linear functional
\mathscr{L}\colon L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
and the magnetic loading is described by a functional
\mathscr{M}\colon L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}

\mathscr{L}(y):=\int_{\Omega}f\cdot y{\,\rm d}x,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathscr{M}(y,m):=\int_{y(\Omega)}h\cdot m{\,\rm d}z

where
f\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a body force and

h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
represents an external magnetic field, while
\mathscr{M}
is called the Zeeman energy.

If
y\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
represents the deformation of the elastic body, the stable equilibria of the elastic body are obtained by minimizing the functional

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla y,m\circ y){\,\rm d}x-\mathscr{L}(y)-\mathscr{M}(y,m),

under the prescribed boundary conditions.
The area of interest of this paper is
the case where the load has the form
\varepsilon\mathscr{L}
, and
to study the behavior of the solution as
\varepsilon
tends to zero. We write

y_{\varepsilon}=id+\varepsilon u

and
assume the Dirichlet boundary condition of the form

y_{\varepsilon}=id+\varepsilon w\quad\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\text{-a.e. on }\Gamma,

with a given function
w\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Denoting a set of functions satisfying boundary conditions as

\displaystyle W^{1,p}_{w}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}):u=w\quad\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\text{-a.e. on }\Gamma\right\}\!,

\displaystyle W^{2,\infty}_{w}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{u\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}):u=w\quad\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\text{-a.e. on }\Gamma\right\}\!,

the corresponding minimum problem for
(u,m)
becomes

\displaystyle\min\biggl\{\int_{\Omega}W(I+\varepsilon\nabla u(x),m(x+\varepsilon u(x))){\,\rm d}x-\varepsilon\mathscr{L}(\varepsilon u)

\displaystyle-\mathscr{M}(id+\varepsilon u,m):

\displaystyle(u,m)\in W^{1,p}_{w}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times W^{1,2}(y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)\setminus y_{\varepsilon}(\partial\Omega);\mathbb{R}^{d})\biggr\}\!.

The class of admissible deformations is given by

(1.12)

\mathcal{Y}:=\left\{y\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}):\det\nabla y>0\text{ a.e. in }\Omega,\>y\text{ is a.e.~injective in }\Omega\right\}\!.

For
w\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\varepsilon>0
, define the class of admissible states as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{w}_{\varepsilon}:=\bigl\{(u,m)

\displaystyle\in W^{1,2}_{w}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}):y_{\varepsilon}:=id+\varepsilon u\in\mathcal{Y},

\displaystyle m\in W^{1,2}(y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)\setminus y_{\varepsilon}(\partial\Omega);\mathbb{R}^{d}),\>|m\circ y_{\varepsilon}|\,\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}=1\text{ a.e.~in }\Omega\bigr\},

and

\mathcal{A}^{w}_{0}:=W^{1,2}_{w}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{d-1}).

The main result of this work is the following
\Gamma
-convergence statement.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
W\colon\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
satisfies conditions
(a)
–
(e)
for some
p>d
, and let
w\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For every
\varepsilon_{j}\to 0
we have that

\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon_{j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{G}_{0},\quad\text{ as }j\to\infty

in the weak topology of
W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Remark 1.2
.

In view of the compactness properties stated in Proposition
3.1
and in order to state the
\Gamma
-convergence in
W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, In Theorem
1.1
we identify
m_{\varepsilon}
with
\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}m_{\varepsilon}
for
(u_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})\in\mathcal{A}^{w}_{\varepsilon}
and
y_{\varepsilon}=id+\varepsilon u_{\varepsilon}
. Similarly, in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
we identify the magnetization
m
with
\chi_{\Omega}m
.

Theorem
1.1
is completed by the following theorem, stating the convergence of minima and minimizers. Here, we consider loading terms as well. In particular, for every
\varepsilon>0
and every
(u,m)\in\mathcal{A}^{w}_{\varepsilon}
we set

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(u,m):=\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)-\mathscr{L}(u)-\mathscr{M}(id+\varepsilon u,m).

Theorem 1.3
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
, for every
\varepsilon>0
define
s_{\varepsilon}:=\inf\{\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(u,m):(u,m)\in\mathcal{A}^{w}_{\varepsilon}\}
and let

(u_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})\in\mathcal{A}^{w}_{\varepsilon}
be a sequence such that

(1.13)

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})=s_{\varepsilon}+o(1)\quad\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

Then,
u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u_{0}
weakly in
W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}m_{\varepsilon}\to\chi_{\Omega}m_{0}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}\nabla m_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\chi_{\Omega}\nabla m_{0}
weakly in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
, where
(u_{0},m_{0})\in\mathcal{A}_{0}^{w}
is the solution to the minimum problem

s_{0}:=\min\bigl\{\mathcal{F}_{0}(u,m):(u,m)\in\mathcal{A}^{w}_{0}\bigr\},

where
\mathcal{F}_{0}(u,m):=\mathcal{G}_{0}(u,m)-\mathscr{L}(u)-\mathscr{M}(id,m)
and
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is defined by (
1.5
).
Moreover,
s_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow s_{0}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
, let
(u_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})\in\mathcal{A}^{w}_{\varepsilon}
satisfy (
1.13
). Then, up to a subsequence,

(u_{\varepsilon},\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}m_{\varepsilon})\to(u_{0},\chi_{\Omega}m_{0})
strongly in

W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The proof of Theorems

–

is postponed to Section

Remark 1.5

(Discussion for the case
p\leq d
)
.

Let us notice that the
p
-integrability of deformations’ gradients plays an essential role only for the compactness.
The result of this paper can be extended for the case

using the means of quasiconformal analysis, see details in, e.g.,
[

First, an admissible deformation

is continuous and differentiable a.e., and a continuous representative satisfies the Lusin

Thus, the composition

is well defined, and the change-of-variables formula is valid for the chosen representative.
Second, instead of

one needs to use the topological image,

]
,
to define

Finally, there is no uniform convergence

Therefore, the proof of compactness in Proposition

needs more refined estimates.

There are several options in the literature, which offer ways to deal with the case

.
For instance, one may use the approach prohibiting cavitations as in Müller–Spector
[

]
, Barchiesi–Henao–Mora-Coral
[

]
, and Bresciani
[
11
]
.
Although a stronger coercivity condition is needed to guarantee weak convergence of determinants.
Another option, is to consider deformations with finite surface energy as in
[
5
]
, (INV)-condition or
weak limits of homeomorphisms
[
9
,
21
]
.
Admissible deformations in
the case

may be considered within the limits of homeomorphisms
[

,

]

2.
Auxiliary results

The homogeneous Sobolev space

L^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

is defined
as the space of
\varphi\in L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\nabla\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
We equip the space
L^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with a seminorm
\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})}
.
It can be as well supplied with a norm

\|\varphi\|_{L^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}
,
where
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is arbitrary bounded open nonempty set.
For more details, the interested reader is referred to
[
34
, Chapter 1]
.
The magnetic potential
v_{m}\in L^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
generated by magnetization
m
exists, unique up to a constant, and continuously depends on
m
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
there exists
v_{f}\in L^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which is unique up to additive constants, such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(-\nabla v_{f}+f)\cdot\nabla\varphi{\,\rm d}z=0\qquad\text{for all }\varphi\in L^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and

\|\nabla v_{f}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}}.

Moreover, if
f_{j}\to f
strongly in
L^{2}
, then

\nabla v_{f_{j}}\to\nabla v_{f}
strongly in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

This proposition is classical, see for example
[
5
, Proposition 8.8 & Theorem 8.9]
or
[
39
, Proof of Theorem 4.1]
.
∎

Let us now recall sufficient conditions for a deformation to be injective if the gradient of the displacement is small enough in the operator norm, i.e.,
|B|_{O}=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|Bv|}{|v|}
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
15
, Theorem 5.5-1]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set.

(1)

Let
y=id+u\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be differentiable in
x\in\Omega
.
Then

|\nabla u(x)|_{O}<1\Rightarrow\det\nabla y(x)>0.

(2)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary. Then there exists a constant
c(\Omega)
such that any

y=id+u\in C^{1}(\bar{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})
with

\sup_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}|\nabla u(x)|_{O}<c(\Omega)

is injective.

To establish compactness, we need the following geometric rigidity, proven in
[
26
, Theorem 3.1]
for
p=2
and in
[
16
, Section 2.4]
for
p\in(1,+\infty)
.

Theorem 2.3
.

There exists a constant
C=C(\Omega,p)>0
such that for every
v\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
there exists a constant rotation
R\in SO(d)
satisfying

(2.1)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v-R|^{p}{\,\rm d}x\leq C\int_{\Omega}g_{p}({\operatorname{dist}}(\nabla v,SO(d))){\,\rm d}x,

where
g_{p}
is defined by (
1.6
). In particular, we may choose
R
such that

(2.2)

\left|R-\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\,{\,\rm d}x\right|={\operatorname{dist}}\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\,{\,\rm d}x,SO(d)\right)\!.

Proof.

It is enough to resort to the classical rigidity estimates for

and notice that

\frac{1}{2p}(t^{p}+t^{2})\leq\frac{1}{p}\max\{t^{p},t^{2}\}\leq g_{p}(t)\leq\frac{1}{2}\max\{t^{p},t^{2}\}\leq\frac{1}{2}(t^{p}+t^{2}).

The particular choice of
R
as in (
2.2
) can be ensured arguing, e.g., as in
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
.
∎

3.
Compactness

This section is devoted to the proof of the following compactness result for sequences with bounded energy
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}
.

Proposition 3.1

(Compactness)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
, let
(u_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})\in\mathcal{A}^{w}_{\varepsilon}
be such that

(3.1)

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\,\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})<+\infty.

Then, there exists
(u_{0},m_{0})\in W^{1,2}_{w}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
such that, up to a subsequence,

\displaystyle y_{\varepsilon}:=id+\varepsilon u_{\varepsilon}\to id

\displaystyle\text{ strongly in }W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ weakly in }W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle\chi_{{y}_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}{m}_{\varepsilon}\to\chi_{\Omega}m_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ strongly in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle\chi_{{y}_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}\nabla{m}_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\chi_{\Omega}\nabla m_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}),

\displaystyle m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}\to m_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ strongly in }L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle m_{\varepsilon}\circ{y}_{\varepsilon}\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}\to m_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ strongly in }L^{r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\text{ for every }1\leq r<\infty.

Before proving Proposition
3.1
, we show the following lemmas, which provide boundedness of the displacement variable in terms of the energy
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, there exist positive constants
C_{1}=C_{1}(\Omega,d,p)>0
and
C_{2}=C_{2}(\Omega,d,p)>0
such that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and every
(u,m)\in\mathcal{A}^{w}_{\varepsilon}
it holds

(3.2)

\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)\geq C_{1}\int_{\Omega}g_{p}(\operatorname{dist}(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},SO(d))\,{\,\rm d}x-C_{2}\varepsilon^{2},

for
y_{\varepsilon}=id+\varepsilon u
.

Proof.

Let
(u,m)\in\mathcal{A}^{w}_{\varepsilon}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. By assumption
(d)
we can estimate

(3.3)

\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)\geq C\int_{\Omega}g_{p}(\operatorname{dist}\big(\exp(\varepsilon e(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},m\circ y_{\varepsilon})\nabla y_{\varepsilon};SO(d)\big)\,{\,\rm d}x.

By definition of
e(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},m\circ y_{\varepsilon})
, by the properties of the exponential map, and by the fact that
\det{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}}|m\circ y_{\varepsilon}|=1
in
\Omega
, we have that for
x\in\Omega
and
R\in SO(d)

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}

\displaystyle(\nabla y_{\varepsilon};\exp(-\varepsilon e(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},m\circ y_{\varepsilon}))SO(d))\leq|\nabla y_{\varepsilon}-\exp(-\varepsilon e(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},m\circ y_{\varepsilon}))R|

\displaystyle\leq|\exp(-\varepsilon e(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},m\circ y_{\varepsilon}))|\,|\exp(\varepsilon e(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},m\circ y_{\varepsilon}))\nabla y_{\varepsilon}-R|\leq C|\exp(\varepsilon e(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},m\circ y_{\varepsilon}))\nabla y_{\varepsilon}-R|.

This implies that for a.e.
x\in\Omega

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(\nabla y_{\varepsilon};\exp(-\varepsilon e(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},m\circ y_{\varepsilon}))SO(d))\leq C\,\operatorname{dist}(\exp(\varepsilon e(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},m\circ y_{\varepsilon})\nabla y_{\varepsilon};SO(d)))\,.

Again by the properties of the exponential, we further estimate

(3.4)

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(\nabla y_{\varepsilon};SO(d))\leq C\,\operatorname{dist}(\nabla y_{\varepsilon};\exp(-\varepsilon e(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},m\circ y_{\varepsilon})SO(d))+C\varepsilon\qquad\text{a.e.~in~$\Omega$.}

Combining (
3.3
)–(
3.4
) and the properties of
g_{p}
(
1.7
)–(
1.8
), we get (
3.2
).
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, there exists a positive constant
C=C(\Omega,d,p)>0
such that for every
\varepsilon>0
, every
(u,m)\in\mathcal{A}^{w}_{\varepsilon}
, and every
\varepsilon>0
the following estimates hold:

(3.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}{\,\rm d}x\leq C\Bigg(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)+\int_{\Gamma}|w|^{2}{\,\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}+1\bigg),

(3.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\varepsilon\nabla u|^{p}{\,\rm d}x\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\bigg(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)+\int_{\Gamma}|w|^{2}{\,\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}+1\bigg).

Proof.

Along the proof, we denote by
C
a generic positive constant depending only on
\Omega
,
d
, and
p
, but not on
\varepsilon
and on
(u,m)\in\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{w}
.

Let us prove (
3.5
). For
(u,m)\in\mathcal{A}^{w}_{\varepsilon}
we set
y_{\varepsilon}:=id+\varepsilon u
. Applying the Geometric Rigidity Theorem
2.3
and Lemma
3.2
, we deduce that there exists
R_{\varepsilon}\in SO(d)
such that

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla y_{\varepsilon}-R_{\varepsilon}|^{2}{\,\rm d}x\leq C\int_{\Omega}g_{p}(\operatorname{dist}(\nabla y_{\varepsilon};SO(d))){\,\rm d}x\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\big(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)+1).

By triangle inequality, we continue with

(3.7)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla y_{\varepsilon}-I|^{2}{\,\rm d}x\leq C\big(\varepsilon^{2}\big(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)+1)+|R_{\varepsilon}-I|^{2}\big).

Arguing as in
[
17
, Proposition 3.4]
, we get that

(3.8)

|R_{\varepsilon}-I|^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\bigg(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)+\int_{\Gamma}|w|^{2}{\,\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}+1\bigg),

which, together with (
3.7
), implies (
3.5
).

As for (
3.6
), we notice that, by Theorem
2.3
and Lemma
3.2
, for the same rotation
R_{\varepsilon}
it holds

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla y_{\varepsilon}-R_{\varepsilon}|^{p}{\,\rm d}x\leq C\int_{\Omega}g_{p}(\operatorname{dist}(\nabla y_{\varepsilon};SO(d))){\,\rm d}x+C\varepsilon^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\big(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)+1\big).

Hence, recalling (
3.8
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\varepsilon\nabla u|^{p}{\,\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq 2^{p-1}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla y_{\varepsilon}-R_{\varepsilon}|^{p}{\,\rm d}x+|R_{\varepsilon}-I|^{p}\bigg)\leq C\bigg(\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)+\varepsilon^{2}+|R_{\varepsilon}-I|^{p}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)+\varepsilon^{2}+|R_{\varepsilon}-I|^{2}\bigg)\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\bigg(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u,m)+\int_{\Gamma}|w|^{2}{\,\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}+1\bigg),

where we have used the fact that
p>d\geq 2
and that
R_{\varepsilon}\in SO(d)
, so that
|R_{\varepsilon}-I|\leq 2\sqrt{d}
. This concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

We are now ready to prove the compactness result of Proposition
3.1
. We closely follow the arguments of
[
10
, Proposition 4.3]
(see also
[
13
, Proposition 3.7]
).

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let us notice that the Poincaré inequality and Lemma
3.3
guarantee, up to a subsequence, weak convergence
u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u_{0}
in
W^{1,2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Moreover, (
3.6
)
implies that

(3.9)

\|\nabla y_{\varepsilon}-I\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{p}}

for some positive constant
C
independent of
\varepsilon
.
And thus, strong convergence
y_{\varepsilon}\to id
in
W^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

For simplicity of notation, let us set
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}:=\exp(\varepsilon e(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}))\nabla y_{\varepsilon}
. Then, by the assumptions on
\Phi
we have that

(3.10)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{(\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon})^{a}}{\,\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{(\det\Lambda_{\varepsilon})^{a}}{\,\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}){\,\rm d}x+C\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega)\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(y_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})+C\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega).

Thus,
1/\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}
is equi-integrable in
\Omega
.

For
\delta>0
let us set

\Omega_{\delta}:=\{x\in\Omega:\,\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)>\delta\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}:=\chi_{\Omega_{\delta}}m_{\varepsilon}.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\Omega_{\delta}
is a Lipschitz set. Since
y_{\varepsilon}\to id
in
W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
p>d
, we have that
y_{\varepsilon}\to id
in
C^{0}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Hence,
for
\varepsilon>0
small enough, we may assume that
\Omega_{\delta}\subseteq y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)
.
Without going into details about the degree theory, see
[
25
]
, let us provide rather a standard argument to prove this fact.
For
y_{\varepsilon}
, let us define the topological degree
\deg(x,y_{\varepsilon},\Omega)
as a topological degree of its continuous representative.
Notice that
h_{t}(x):=ty_{\varepsilon}(x)+(1-t)x
is a homotopy between

y_{\varepsilon}
and
id
, and
by the uniform convergence
y_{\varepsilon}\to id
, for any
x\in\Omega_{\delta}
it holds that
x\not\in y_{\varepsilon}(\partial\Omega)
for any small enough
\varepsilon
.
Thus,
\deg(x,y_{\varepsilon},\Omega)=\deg(x,id,\Omega)>0
, in other words,
x\in y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)
.

Next, by change of variables, using the equality
|m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}|\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}=1
a.e. in
\Omega
, we get that

(3.11)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|^{2}{\,\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}|m_{\varepsilon}|^{2}{\,\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}|m_{\varepsilon}|^{2}{\,\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}|m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}{\,\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}}{\,\rm d}x.

Moreover, by definition of
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
it holds

(3.12)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|^{2}{\,\rm d}x\leq\int_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}|\nabla m_{\varepsilon}|^{2}{\,\rm d}x\leq\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(y_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon}).

Therefore,
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
is bounded in
W^{1,2}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{d})
uniformly for all
\delta>0
.
By a diagonal argument, we find
m_{0}\in W^{1,2}_{loc}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\rightharpoonup m_{0}
weakly in
W^{1,2}_{loc}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
While the Sobolev Embedding Theorems provide that

(3.13)

m_{\varepsilon}(x)=\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(x)\to m_{0}(x)\qquad\text{for a.e.~}x\in\Omega_{\delta}.

Passing to the liminf as
\varepsilon\to 0
and then to the limit as
\delta\to 0
in (
3.11
) and (
3.12
), we further deduce that
m_{0}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Note further that the second line in (
3.11
) ensures that

\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}m_{\varepsilon}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and thus converges weakly to some
\mu\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Using pointwise convergence (
3.13
), we conclude

\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}m_{\varepsilon}(x)\to\chi_{\Omega}m_{0}(x)
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Therefore
\mu=\chi_{\Omega}m_{0}
a.e., and, up to a subsequence,

(3.14)

\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}m_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\chi_{\Omega}m_{0}\quad\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Now it is not difficult to see that
|\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}m_{\varepsilon}|^{2}
converges in measure and, moreover, is equi-integrable by (
3.10
) and (
3.11
).
Thus,
applying the Lebesgue–Vitali Convergence Theorem (see
[
8
, Th. 4.5.4]
or
[
38
, Ch. 6, Ex. 10]
) to
|\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}m_{\varepsilon}|^{2}
, we obtain convergence of norms

\|\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}m_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\to\|\chi_{\Omega}m_{0}\|_{L^{2}}.

This, together with weak convergence (
3.14
) guarantees strong convergence

\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}m_{\varepsilon}\to\chi_{\Omega}m_{0}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Since the weak convergence in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
of
m_{\varepsilon}\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}
to
m_{0}
has been already shown, we deduce the strong
L^{2}
-convergence.

By (
3.1
) we know that
\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}\nabla m_{\varepsilon}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
. For every test
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
we consider

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}

\displaystyle(\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}\nabla m_{\varepsilon}-\chi_{\Omega}\nabla m_{0}):\varphi{\,\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}(\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}\nabla m_{\varepsilon}-\chi_{\Omega}\nabla m_{0}):\varphi{\,\rm d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega_{\delta}}(\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}\nabla m_{\varepsilon}-\chi_{\Omega}\nabla m_{0}):\varphi{\,\rm d}x.

Then, the first integral tends to
0
since
\Omega_{\delta}\subseteq y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)\cap\Omega
,
m_{\varepsilon}=\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
in
\Omega_{\delta}
, and
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\rightharpoonup m_{0}
weakly in
W^{1,2}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{d})
. As for the second integral, we have that

\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega_{\delta}}(\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}\nabla m_{\varepsilon}-\chi_{\Omega}\nabla m_{0}):\varphi{\,\rm d}x\bigg|\leq C\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(C_{\delta})},

where
C_{\delta}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\,\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)<\delta\}
. Since
\mathcal{L}^{d}(C_{\delta})\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
, we infer that
\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}\nabla m_{\varepsilon}
converges to
\chi_{\Omega}\nabla m_{0}
weakly in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
.

We now show that
m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}
converges to
m_{0}
in
L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon\ll 1
we write

(3.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}|m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}-m_{0}|{\,\rm d}x\leq\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}|m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}-\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|{\,\rm d}x+\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}|\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}-m_{0}|{\,\rm d}x.

For the second integral on the right-hand side of (
3.15
) we simply have that
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\to m_{0}
in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{d})
. As for the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.15
), we notice that
m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}
and
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
are equi-integrable in
\Omega_{\delta}
.
Thus, for
\eta>0
there exists
\rho>0
such that for every
A\subseteq\Omega_{\delta}
measurable

\mathcal{L}^{d}(A)<\rho\ \Rightarrow\ \limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{A}|m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}-\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|{\,\rm d}x<\eta.

Let us consider
\overline{\delta}\in(0,\delta)
such that
\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega_{\overline{\delta}}\setminus\Omega_{\delta})<\rho
and set
A_{\delta,\varepsilon}:=y^{-1}_{\varepsilon}(\Omega_{\delta})
. For
\varepsilon>0
small enough, we have that
A_{\delta,\varepsilon}\subseteq\Omega_{\overline{\delta}}
. We rewrite

(3.16)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}|m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}-\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|{\,\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega_{\delta}\cap A_{\delta,\varepsilon}}|m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}-\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|{\,\rm d}x+\int_{\Omega_{\delta}\setminus A_{\delta,\varepsilon}}|m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}-\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|{\,\rm d}x.

We notice that, by change of variable,

\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega_{\delta})=\mathcal{L}^{d}(y_{\varepsilon}(A_{\delta,\varepsilon}))=\mathcal{L}^{d}(A_{\delta,\varepsilon})+\int_{A_{\delta,\varepsilon}}(\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}-1){\,\rm d}x.

Since
\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}\to 1
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
,
A_{\delta,\varepsilon}\subseteq\Omega_{\overline{\delta}}
, and
\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega_{\overline{\delta}}\setminus\Omega_{\delta})<\rho
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega_{\delta}\setminus A_{\delta,\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Big(\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega_{\overline{\delta}})-\mathcal{L}^{d}(A_{\delta,\varepsilon})\Big)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Bigg(\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega_{\overline{\delta}}\setminus\Omega_{\delta})-\int_{A_{\delta,\varepsilon}}(\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}-1){\,\rm d}x\Bigg)<\rho.

In turn, this implies that

(3.17)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega_{\delta}\setminus A_{\delta,\varepsilon}}|m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}-\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|{\,\rm d}x<\eta.

To estimate the integral on
\Omega_{\delta}\cap A_{\delta,\varepsilon}
, we fix
\lambda\in(-1/p,0)
, extend
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
from
\Omega_{\delta}
to a map
M_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|M_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\|_{W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C(\Omega_{\delta})\|\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\|^{2}_{W^{1,2}(\Omega_{\delta})}
, and use the Lusin approximation of Sobolev functions: for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a set
G_{\delta,\varepsilon}
such that
M_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
is
\varepsilon^{\lambda}
-Lipschitz on
G_{\delta,\varepsilon}
and

(3.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus G_{\delta,\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon^{-2\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla M_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|^{2}{\,\rm d}x\leq C(\Omega_{\delta})\varepsilon^{-2\lambda}\|\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\|^{2}_{W^{1,2}(\Omega_{\delta})}.

Setting
X_{\delta,\varepsilon}:=y_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(G_{\delta,\varepsilon})
and noticing that
m_{\varepsilon}=M_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
on
\Omega_{\delta}\cap G_{\delta,\varepsilon}
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\delta}\cap A_{\delta,\varepsilon}\cap X_{\delta,\varepsilon}\cap G_{\delta,\varepsilon}}|m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}-\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|{\,\rm d}x

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\Omega_{\delta}\cap A_{\delta,\varepsilon}\cap X_{\delta,\varepsilon}\cap G_{\delta,\varepsilon}}|M_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\circ y_{\varepsilon}-M_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|{\,\rm d}x\leq\varepsilon^{\lambda}\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega)\|y_{\varepsilon}-id\|_{C^{0}}\leq\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{p}+\lambda}\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega).

Combining (
3.18
), the convergence rate (
3.9
), and a change of variable, we infer that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega_{\delta}\setminus G_{\delta,\varepsilon})=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega_{\delta}\setminus X_{\delta,\varepsilon})=0.

Thus,

(3.19)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega_{\delta}\cap A_{\delta,\varepsilon}\setminus(X_{\delta,\varepsilon}\cap G_{\delta,\varepsilon}})|m_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\circ y_{\varepsilon}-\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|{\,\rm d}x<\eta.

Combining (
3.15
), (
3.16
), (
3.17
), and (
3.19
) yields

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}|m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}-m_{0}|{\,\rm d}x<3\eta.

Since
\eta>0
is arbitrary, this implies that
m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}\to m_{0}
in
L^{1}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every
\delta>0
. By the equi-integrability of
m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}
, this entails the convergence in
L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Furthermore,
since strong
L^{1}
-convergence implies convergence a.e., we have

m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}\to m_{0}
a.e. and
|m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}|\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}=|m_{0}|=1
a.e. in
\Omega
.
Thus,

m\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

and
m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}\to m_{0}
in
L^{r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any
r\in[1,+\infty)
.
∎

4.
Proof of
\Gamma
-convergence

We are now able to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We divide the proof in lower and upper bound for the
\Gamma
-convergence.

\Gamma
-liminf inequality.
We start with the lower bound. Let
(u_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})\in\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{w}
be such that

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\,\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})<+\infty.

By Proposition
3.1
, there exist

u\in W^{1,2}_{w}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
m\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

such that, up to a subsequence,

u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u
weakly in
W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}\,\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}\to m
in
L^{r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any
1\leq r<\infty
,

m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}\to m
in
L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}\nabla m_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\chi_{\Omega}\nabla m
weakly in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
,
and
\chi_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}m_{\varepsilon}\to\chi_{\Omega}m
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Then, by Proposition
2.1
we have that

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla m|^{2}{\,\rm d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla v_{m}|^{2}{\,\rm d}x\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{y_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}|\nabla m_{\varepsilon}|^{2}{\,\rm d}z+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla v_{m_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}{\,\rm d}z.

For a fixed
0<\alpha<1
define the sets

E_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in\Omega:|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x)|<\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\}.

Then, by the Chebyshev inequality

\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon})=\mathcal{L}^{d}\big(\left\{x\in\Omega:|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x)|\geq\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\right\}\big)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{-\alpha}}\int_{\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x)|{\rm d}x\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}|\Omega|^{1/2}\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon^{\alpha}.

Thus,
\chi_{E_{\varepsilon}}\to 1
in measure
and
\chi_{E_{\varepsilon}}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\nabla u
in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
.
Indeed,
for
1\leq r<2
we may estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}-\nabla u|^{r}{\,\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}-\nabla u\|^{\frac{1}{r}}_{L^{2}}\cdot\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{2-r}{2r}}\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{(2-r)\alpha}{2r}}\to 0.

Therefore,

\chi_{E_{\varepsilon}}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}=\nabla u_{\varepsilon}-\chi_{\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon}}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\nabla u\text{ weakly in }L^{r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
.
Since the weak limit is unique, we get that
\chi_{E_{\varepsilon}}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\nabla u
weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
.

Moreover, since
m_{\varepsilon}\circ{y}_{\varepsilon}\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}\to m
strongly in
L^{r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every
1\leq r<\infty

then

(\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon})^{2}m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}\otimes m_{\varepsilon}\circ y_{\varepsilon}\to m\otimes m
strongly in

We now construct a recovery sequence.
Let

and

Define

\displaystyle y_{\varepsilon}(x)=x+\varepsilon u_{0}(x),

(4.5)

\displaystyle m_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon u_{0}(x))\det\nabla y_{\varepsilon}(x)=m_{0}(x).

We want to prove that

\displaystyle y_{\varepsilon}\to id\text{ in }W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(4.7)

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}=u_{0}\to u_{0}\text{ in }W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(4.8)

(4.9)

First, we notice that
