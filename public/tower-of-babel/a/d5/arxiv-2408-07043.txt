On local energy decay for solutions to the b-family of peakon equations
C. Hong

Abstract
. This work is concerned with the long time behavior of solutions to the b b -family of peakon equations. We prove local energy decay of global solutions under suitable hypotheses. Assuming the global bound of the H 1 ​ ( ℝ ) H^{1}(\mathbb{R}) norm, we show local energy decay along sequences of time in an expanding region around the origin. If we assume nonnegativity for an initial momentum, m 0 ​ ( x ) = ( u − ∂ x 2 u ) ​ ( 0 , x ) m_{0}(x)=(u-\partial_{x}^{2}u)(0,x) , we show strict decay on a similar region. Moreover, we show decay in an exterior region given the same nonnegativity condition.

1.
Introduction

In this work, we consider the Camassa-Holm (CH) equation posed on the real line

\partial_{t}u-\partial_{xxt}^{3}u+3u\partial_{x}u-u\partial_{x}^{3}u-2\partial_{x}u\partial_{x}^{2}u=0,\,\,(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(1.1)

The CH equation was first noticed by Fuchssteiner and Fokas
[
11
]
when they studied hereditary symmetries. Later it was derived by Camassa and Holm
[
4
]
as an alternative integrable model for shallow water waves in a channel. This model was also deduced by Dai
[
9
]
in the context of elastic rods. Among the notable properties of this system are the existence of a bi-Hamiltonian structure and the existence of peaked traveling wave solutions called peakons.

As discussed in
[
4
]
, the CH equation is completely integrable and posseses a Lax pair, which gives infinitely many conservation laws. The first two laws are given by

I(u)=\int u\,dx,\,\,\,E(u)=\int\left(u^{2}+(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\right)\,dx.

(1.2)

Li and Oliver
[
14
]
and Rodriguez
[
19
]
showed that the initial value problem (IVP) associated to the CH equationis (strongly) locally well-posed in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
s>3/2
. The second conservation law hints at the existence of global solutions in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
in the weak sense. Constantin and Molinet
[
7
]
managed to prove one can get such solutions that are continuous in time with respect to the
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm under a nonnegativity condition on the quantiy
m=u-\partial_{x}^{2}u
. McKean
[
17
]
then showed that solution blow-up occurs if and only if
m
has a positive part to the left of some negative part of itself. In addition, one can establish a type of local well posedness for solutions in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, which is a class that includes our peakon solutions. This was proven by Linares, Ponce, and Sideris in
[
15
]
.

Constantin
[
6
]
showed that classical solutions will only blow-up as wave-breaking, which means that the slope of a solution may diverge at a point. Moreover, Bressan and Constantin
[
3
]
managed to construct global conservative solutions in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
for any initial condition. Such solutions can experience wave breakdown, but can be extended globally in time after they blow up.

Using that
(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}g=f
implies
Gf=\frac{1}{2}e^{-|x|}*f=g
, one gets the conservative nonlocal form of the CH equation.

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+G\left(u^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\right)\right)=0,\,\,(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}.

(1.3)

This weaker formulation justifies the validity of peakon solutions, which are peaked traveling wave equations to our equation. These peakons take the general form

u(t,x)=cQ(x-ct),\,\,Q(x)=ce^{-|x|},\,\,c\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.4)

Notice that
Q\notin H^{3/2}(\mathbb{R})
.

As it was mentioned before, solutions to the CH equation may experience blow-up in finite time via wave breaking and still can be extended to
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
global solutions. Such behavior does not occur for similar models. For example, some solutions to the mass critical gKdV equation can blow up in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
by having mass escape to infinity in finite time. This is fully described by Martel and Merle
[
16
]
. Other examples, such as the KdV equation, possess global solutions in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

The CH equation demonstrates elastic collisions between peakons, much like soliton behavior for the KdV equation due to their integrability. The stability of single peakons was established by Constantin and Strauss
[
8
]
. As hypothesized during its derivation, asymptotic stability of trains of peakons solutions have been established by Molinet in
[
18
]
, which aligns with asymptotic stability of solitons for the KdV equation. The speed of these peakons are determined by their amplitude, which is bounded by their
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
by the Sobolev embedding. This implies that solutions should decay in the
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm in the shrinking region
|x|\gtrsim\beta t
for some
\beta>0
.

A train of peakons should decay around the origin since they will carry their mass away from the origin at constant speed. This combined with asymptotic stability results implies decay on some sort of expanding interval centered at the origin. Alejo, Cortez, Kwak, and Muñoz
[
1
]
proved this inside the interval approximately of the size
|x|\lesssim t^{1/2}
. The relevant theorem is

Theorem 1.1
.

[
1
]
Let
u
be a nontrivial global solution to (
1.3
), such that
u\in C([0,\infty]:H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}:L^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
. Then we have

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\|u(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Lambda_{1/2}(t))}=0

(1.5)

where

\Lambda_{c}(t)\coloneqq\left(-\frac{|t|^{c}}{\log|t|},\frac{|t|^{c}}{\log|t|}\right).

(1.6)

Remark 1.1
.

Note that (
1.2
) and the global existence of solutions with nonnegative initial data
[
6
]
imply that the hypotheses,
u\in C([0,\infty]:H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}:L^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
, are satisfied for
m_{0}(x)\geq 0
, and
m_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Remark 1.2
.

The above theorem was also proved for the
L^{2}
-norm for the Degasperis-Procesi (DP) equation, which was first discovered by Degasperis and Gaeta
[
10
]
. This has the form

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+\frac{3}{2}Gu^{2}\right)=0.

(1.7)

This equation also has the same peakon solutions, and is a bi-Hamiltonian system. In addition, (
1.7
) allows for shock peakon solutions, which are like peakons except they are discontinuous at a point. This behavior is possible since weak solutions to (
1.7
) may simply be
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
instead of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. These solutions take the form

u(x,t)=\frac{1}{t+k}\text{sgn}(x)e^{-|x|},\,\,k>0.

(1.8)

In
[
1
]
, one finds a similar result by accepting a growth in time of the
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm of solutions, as opposed to requiring a global bound. In particular, one allows polynomial growth. The result is

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u\in C([0,\infty):H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
be a solution to (
1.3
) or (
1.7
) such that

\int|u(t,x)|\,dx\lesssim\langle t\rangle^{a}.

(1.9)

For
0\leq a<1
, and
c\coloneqq 1-a
, we have

(1)

For solutions to CH (
1.3
)

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\|u(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Lambda_{c}(t))}=0.

(1.10)

(2)

For solutions to DP (
1.7
)

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Lambda_{c}(t))}=0.

(1.11)

If
a<1/2
, this result holds for a larger expanding interval, but it only gets a
\liminf
result. The proof extends to the
b
-family of equations, which are discussed below.

The CH equation and the DP equation belong to the
b
-family of equations

\partial_{t}u-\partial_{xxt}^{3}u+(b+1)u\partial_{x}u-b\partial_{x}u\partial_{x}^{2}u-u\partial_{x}^{3}u=0,

(1.12)

or equivalently

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+G\left(\frac{b}{2}u^{2}+\frac{3-b}{2}(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\right)\right)=0,\,b\in(0,3),

(1.13)

The CH equation is represented when
b=2
while the DP equation is represented when
b=3
. Note that all of these equations admit peakon solutions, but only the DP equation admits breakdown solutions. A caveat in this relation is that only the CH equation and the DP equation are integrable. A question we seek to answer in this paper is whether results similar to those in
[
1
]
apply to (
1.13
) solutions in general since the integrability of these equations are not directly applied in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. These hypotheses are not unfounded since it was shown in
[
5
]
that peakon solutions are stable for
b>1
. The same paper found numerical evidence of the formation of traveling wave-like behavior for all
0<b<3
, although the system behaved more like the viscous Burger’s equation when
b<1
. This also begs the question whether we will have decay in the region
|x|\gtrsim\beta t
. Since (
1.13
) is not integrable for
b\neq 2,3
, one does not have access to inverse scattering techniques to investigate this behavior. In addition, we are also interested in a similar result that does not require any assumptions on the longtime behavior of the
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm of the solution. This is a natural question since there exists global solutions to the CH equation that are bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, but have varying behavior in the
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm. This of course extends to the b-family.

Since the
b
-family equations are not integrable in general, we have to take advantage of different conserved quantities than what were used in the theorems mentioned above. Consider the quantity
m(t,x)=(1-\partial_{x}^{2})u(t,x)
. We can rewrite (
1.13
) in terms of
m
. As stated in
[
12
]
, This gives us

\partial_{t}m+u\partial_{x}m+bm\partial_{x}u=0.

(1.14)

It can be shown that the sign of
m(t,x)
is determined by the sign of
m(0,x)
. In particular, if
m(0,x)
is nonnegative,
m(t,x)
remains nonnegative. Using the Green’s function of
G
, we learn that
m(0,x)=m_{0}(x)\geq 0
implies
u(t,x)\geq 0
since
\frac{1}{2}e^{-|x|}
is nonnegative.

Like the
b=2
CH case,
m
is an important quantity for the analysis of these equations. For example, it was shown that
u\in C([0,\infty):H^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
is a global solution to (
1.3
) if
m_{0}
is signed. This is similar to the behavior of (
1.1
). Breakdown of solutions even occur in the same way, with the slope of our solutions diverging. The proof of these results can be found in
[
12
]
. Moreover,
\int m\,dx
is a conserved quantity for solutions of (
1.13
). This means that for all
u
that solves (
1.13
), we have

\frac{d}{dt}H(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}m\,dx=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(u-\partial_{x}^{2}u)(x,t)\,dx=0.

(1.15)

Note that (
1.3
) and (
1.7
) have other integrals of motion that are very useful. In particular, solutions to the CH equation (
1.3
) and the DP equation (
1.7
) have constants of motion that are equivalent to the
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm. Since the
b
-family lacks access to these conservation laws, it is difficult to obtain the exact same results for
b\neq 2,3
. This is in spite of the fact that
[
5
]
found very similar behavior numerically when
b>1
. The techniques used in this paper seem unable to take advantage of this fact, so it is possible that further results can be found by taking advantage of whatever properties that are tied to
b>1
.

Next, we will discuss our results.

If one weakens their assumptions on regularity of their solutions but without any hypotheses on the asymptotic behavior of the
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm of the solution, one can still get a weak decay result for solutions. In particular, one can get
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\|u(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Lambda_{2/3}(t))}=0
on expanding inervals.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
u\in C([0,\infty):H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))\cap L^{\infty}([0,\infty):H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
be a global solution to (
1.13
). Then we have

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\|u(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Lambda_{2/3}(t))}.

(1.16)

If we instead restrict the
L^{1}
norm of
m_{0}(x)
, we get a similar result to Theorem
1.1
. This result is

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
u\in C([0,\infty):H^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
be a global solution to (
1.13
) such that
m_{0}\geq 0
, and
m_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|m(t)\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda_{1/2}(t))}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda_{1/2}(t))}=L

(1.17)

for some
L\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 1.3
.

There is a similar decay result for the Zakharov-Kuznetsov equation that was proven by Mendez, Muñ oz, Poblete, and Pozo
[
2
]
. Our argument was inspired by their approach.

Remark 1.4
.

Note that we require
u
to be in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for all time. This is because we have to make use of
m=u-u_{xx}
to gain control over the
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm for

. For the CH equation, this is not necessary because
\|u\|_{H^{1}}
is a conserved quantity for the CH equation. Moreover, we do not know what the limit

will be.

Finally, we can show that signed solutions to (
1.13
) decay to zero on
(\sigma t,\infty)
and
(-\infty,-\sigma t)
. In essence, this implies the traveling waves we expect from these PDEs to have a velocity capped by some quantity related to the initial data.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
u\in C([0,\infty):H^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
be a global solution to (
1.13
) such that
m_{0}\geq 0
, and
m_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})

\sigma>0
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|u\|_{W^{1,p}(\overline{\lambda}(t))}\to 0,

where

\overline{\lambda}(t)=(\sigma t,\infty).

One can show a similar result and proof for
\tilde{\lambda}(t)=(-\infty,-\sigma t)
when
m_{0}\leq 0

We once again require

[

.

2.
Preliminary Estimates

We need to bound the
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm on
u
in time for solutions to (
1.13
). However, with the exception of
b=2
, the
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm is not a conserved quantity. We can take advantage of
m=u-u_{xx}
and
G
to get a bound on the
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm instead. To do this, note that

u=Gm=\frac{1}{2}e^{-|x|}*m,\,\,\,\partial_{x}u=\partial_{x}Gm=-\frac{\text{sgn}(x)}{2}e^{-|x|}*m.

One speical case of Young’s convolution inequality tells us that for
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
f\in L^{1}
, and
g\in L^{p}
.

\|f*g\|_{p}\leq\|f\|_{1}\|g\|_{p}.

We have
\|\frac{1}{2}e^{-|x|}\|_{p}=\|-\frac{\text{sgn}(x)}{2}e^{-|x|}\|_{p}\leq 1
. This then implies

\|u\|_{p}\leq\|m\|_{1},\,\,\,\|\partial_{x}u\|_{p}\leq\|m\|_{1},\,1\leq p\leq\infty.

(2.1)

Recall that
\int m\,dx
is a conserved quantity, and
m
remains signed if
m(0,x)
is signed. This gives a condition to bound the
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm of
m
, which can then be used to bound the
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
norm of
u
for all time. Moreover, a decay on
\|m\|_{1}
is equal to a decay in
\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
.

2.1.
Estimates on
\lambda(t)
and
G

We will be taking advantage of a couple of functions. Let
b\in[0,1)
. We will define the following weight function
\phi(x)
.

\phi(x)=\begin{cases}e^{x}-1&x\leq 0\\
1-e^{-x}&x>0\end{cases},\,\phi^{\prime}(x)=e^{-|x|},\,\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\text{sgn}(x)e^{-|x|}.

(2.2)

We will also use the following functions
\lambda_{c}(t),\mu_{c}(t)
.
\lambda_{c}(t)
is related to the growing interval where we measure decay, while
\mu_{c}(t)
is necessary to prove Theorem
1.3
.

\lambda_{c}(t)=\frac{t^{c}}{\log t},\quad\mu_{c}(t)=t^{1-c}\log^{2}t,\quad\mu_{c}(t)\lambda_{c}(t)=t\log t.

(2.3)

In the case
c=0
, we adopt the convention
\lambda_{c}(t)=1
. When
c>0
, we have

\lambda_{c}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{t^{c-1}}{\log t}\left(c-\frac{1}{\log t}\right),\quad\frac{\lambda_{c}^{\prime}(t)}{\lambda_{c}(t)}=\frac{1}{t}\left(c-\frac{1}{\log t}\right),\quad\frac{\mu_{c}^{\prime}(t)}{\mu_{c}(t)}=\frac{1-c}{t}+\frac{2\log t}{t}.

(2.4)

We will also take advantage of the fact that
(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}f=Gf=\frac{1}{2}e^{-|x|}*f
. Since
\frac{1}{2}e^{-|x|}>0
, we get

f\geq g\implies Gf\geq Gg,\quad f\geq 0\implies Gf\geq 0.

(2.5)

We will also need the following Lemma

Lemma 2.1
.

\displaystyle G\phi^{\prime}(x/\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\lambda^{2}-1}e^{-|x|/\lambda}-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{2}-1}e^{-|x|}\approx\phi^{\prime}(x/\lambda)

(2.6)

when
\lambda\gg 1
.

Proof.

Let’s consider when
x\leq 0

\displaystyle 2G\phi^{\prime}(x/\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-|x-y|}e^{-|y|/\lambda}\,dy

(2.7)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{y-x}e^{y/\lambda}\,dy+\int_{x}^{0}e^{x-y}e^{y/\lambda}\,dy+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{x-y}e^{-y/\lambda}\,dy

\displaystyle=e^{-x}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{y+y/\lambda}\,dy+e^{x}\int_{x}^{0}e^{-y+y/\lambda}\,dy+e^{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y-y/\lambda}\,dy

\displaystyle=e^{-x}\left.\frac{1}{1+1/\lambda}e^{y+y/\lambda}\right|_{-\infty}^{x}+e^{x}\left.\frac{1}{1/\lambda-1}e^{-y+y/\lambda}\right|_{x}^{0}

We get a similar computation for

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int\psi\left(\frac{x}{\lambda_{c}(t)}\right)u\,dx-\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}(t)}\int\psi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{x}{\lambda_{c}(t)}\right)u\,dx

(4.18)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int\psi\left(\frac{x}{\lambda(t)}\right)u\,dx.

By a similar argument as before, this implies that

e\mathcal{E}(t)\geq\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda_{1/2}(t))}\geq\mathcal{E}(t)

(4.19)

,

which then implies that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda_{1/2}(t))}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\|m(t)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda_{1/2}(t))}=L.

(4.20)

It is difficult to get any further results from here without further assumptions. The proof in
[
1
]
makes use of the fact that there exists a subsequential limit for a similar functional. From there, integrability would imply that limit, 0, would be the only possible limit. However, we do not know that 0 is the limit. In fact, we can’t even determine the limit for the
H^{1}
norm on
\Lambda_{1/2}(t)
because the
L^{1}
norm of
m
only gives an upper bound for the
H^{1}
norm of
u
.

∎

5.1.
Proof of Functional Estimate

We will follow the method of proof used in
[
13
]
. Consider the following functional

\mathcal{I}(t)=\int\phi\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)m(t,x)\,dx

(5.1)

where
\sigma,\,L>0
. By our assumptions, this functional is bounded in time, and if we take
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{2}(\tanh x+1)
, our functional is nonnegative as well given
m
is nonnegative. To prove the result, we want to show

Lemma 5.1
.

There exists some
\sigma>0
such that.

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{I}(t)\lesssim-\frac{\sigma}{L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)(u^{2}+(\partial_{x}u)^{2})\,dx

(5.2)

Proof.

Following a similar method to the proof of Lemma
4.2
, we first take the derivative of
\mathcal{I}(t)
, and get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{I}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sigma}{L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)m\,dx+\int\phi\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)m_{t}\,dx

(5.3)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sigma}{L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)m\,dx-\int\phi\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)(u\partial_{x}m+b\partial_{x}um)\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sigma}{L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)m\,dx-\int\phi\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)\left(u\partial_{x}u-u\partial_{x}^{3}u\right)\,dx

\displaystyle-b\int\phi\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)\left(u\partial_{x}u-\partial_{x}u\partial_{x}^{2}u\right)\,dx.

Then we integrate by parts to get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{I}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sigma}{L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)m\,dx-\frac{\sigma}{2L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)u^{2}\,dx

(5.4)

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma}{L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)u\partial_{x}^{2}u\,dx-\int\phi\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)\partial_{x}u\partial_{x}^{2}u\,dx

\displaystyle-b\frac{\sigma}{2L}\int\phi\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)\left(u^{2}-(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\right)\,dx.

Further integration by parts and collecting like terms gives us

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{I}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sigma}{L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)m\,dx-\frac{\sigma}{2L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)u^{2}\,dx

(5.5)

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\sigma}{L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\,dx+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{L^{2}}\int\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)u\partial_{x}u\,dx

\displaystyle-b\frac{\sigma}{2L}\int\phi\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)\left(u^{2}-(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\lesssim-\frac{\sigma}{L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)m\,dx-\frac{C-b}{2}\frac{\sigma}{L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)(u^{2}+(\partial_{x}u)^{2})\,dx

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma^{3}}{L^{3}}\int\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)u^{2}\,dx,

Note that
3-b>0
for
b\in(0,3)
. (
2.1
) implies we can also get the bound

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{I}(t)\lesssim-\left(\frac{\sigma}{L}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma^{3}}{L^{3}}\right)\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)m\,dx-\frac{C\sigma}{L}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-\sigma t}{L}\right)(u^{2}+(\partial_{x}u)^{2})\,dx

(5.6)

We can simply let
L
be sufficiently larger than
\sigma
to get the result. This guarantees that the first term in the above inequality is negative. Then we can simply ignore the first negative term to get the result.

∎
