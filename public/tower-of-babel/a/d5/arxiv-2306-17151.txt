Local Risk Bounds for Statistical Aggregation
Jaouad Mourtada, Tomas Vaškevičius, Nikita Zhivotovskiy

Abstract
In the problem of aggregation, the aim is to combine a given class of base predictors to achieve predictions nearly as accurate as the best one. In this flexible framework, no assumption is made on the structure of the class or the nature of the target. Aggregation has been studied in both sequential and statistical contexts. Despite some important differences between the two problems, the classical results in both cases feature the same “global” complexity measure. In this paper, we revisit and tighten classical results in the theory of aggregation in the statistical setting by replacing the global complexity with a smaller, “local” one. Some of our proofs build on the PAC-Bayes localization technique introduced by Catoni. Among other results, we prove localized versions of the classical bound for the exponential weights estimator due to Leung and Barron [Trans. Inf. Theory., 52(8):3396–3410, 2006] and deviation-optimal bounds for the Q Q -aggregation estimator. These bounds improve over the results of Dai, Rigollet and Zhang [Ann. Statist. 40(3):1878-1905, 2012] for fixed design regression and the results of Lecué and Rigollet [Ann. Statist. 42(1):211-224, 2014] for random design regression.

Sequential and statistical aggregation.

For concreteness and to emphasize the complexity aspect of the problem, we will concentrate on regression with the squared loss.
Henceforth, let
\mathcal{X}
be an input space and
\{f_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
be a class of functions
\mathcal{X}\to[-1,1]
, called base predictors or experts.
The goal is to approximate the performance of the best function in this class.
An important special case occurs when
\Theta
is a finite but otherwise arbitrary set.
In the statistical context, such a setting is called
model aggregation

[
56
,
63
]
, also known as
expert aggregation
in the sequential setting.

In the sequential setting, we are given a sequence of observations
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\in\mathcal{X}\times[-1,1]
revealed one at a time. The aim is to construct a sequence of predictors
\widehat{f}_{0},\dots,\widehat{f}_{n-1}:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,1]
such that
\widehat{f}_{i}
depends only on the first
i
observations and for any data sequence, the
regret

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\widehat{f}_{i-1}(x_{i})-y_{i}\big)^{2}-\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f_{\theta}(x_{i})-y_{i}\big)^{2}

(1)

is upper bounded by an appropriate function of
M
and
n
.

In contrast, in the statistical learning framework, the input-output pairs
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
are assumed to be
n
i.i.d. random variables with the same distribution
P
on
\mathcal{X}\times[-1,1]
as the generic pair
(X,Y)
.
The quality of a regression function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
is then measured by its
risk

R(f)={\mathbf{E}}[(f(X)-Y)^{2}]
.
The distribution
P
is unknown, but the sample
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
is available.
The aim is to produce a predictor
\widehat{f}_{n}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
depending on the random sample that achieves a small

excess risk

R(\widehat{f}_{n})-\min_{\theta\in\Theta}R(f_{\theta}),

either in expectation or with high probability.

The fact that the sequential problem deals with arbitrary (deterministic) data suggests that guarantees in this setting can be transported to the statistical setting.
This is indeed partly the case, as shown by a standard conversion procedure (see
[
12
,
46
,
72
,
16
,
18
]
): for any sequence of predictors
\widehat{f}_{0},\dots,\widehat{f}_{n}
(with
\widehat{f}_{i}
based on the observations
(X_{j},Y_{j})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant i}
) and any reference function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
, we have

\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}{\mathbf{E}}[R(\widehat{f}_{i-1})]-R(f)={\mathbf{E}}\bigg[\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(\widehat{f}_{i-1}(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}-\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(f(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}\bigg],

(2)

which holds because
\widehat{f}_{i-1}
is independent of
(X_{i},Y_{i})
.
Hence, the empirical regret with respect to
f_{0}
controls the average excess risk of the predictors
\widehat{f}_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n+1
.
This conversion is, however, only partial for two reasons.
First, the obtained guarantee is only on the
expected
risk rather than its deviations; as shown by Audibert
[
7
]
, there may be a real difference between the expected and high-probability behavior of the risk.
Second, the excess risk bound obtained this way features the complexity measure of the harder, sequential version of the problem. This leads to suboptimal bounds, as discussed below.

Limitation of selection rules and the need for aggregation.

An important aspect of aggregation theory is that it allows us to treat rather generic problems without imposing strong structural assumptions. For instance, the class of base predictors
may have unfavorable geometry due to being non-convex, and likewise,
the target to be predicted is not assumed to lie in some favorable position relative to the class of predictors.
An interesting consequence of this lack of structure
is that
selection/proper learning
rules, namely, rules that select an element of the class of base predictors, cannot achieve optimal prediction accuracy.
In particular, this eliminates the most natural procedure – that of empirical risk minimization – as a candidate for optimal aggregation.

The necessity of improper learning is a fundamental facet of the theory of aggregation, shared by both the sequential and statistical versions of the problem. More precisely, no proper/selection rule (deterministic or randomized) can improve upon the suboptimal worst-case regret or excess risk rate of order
\sqrt{\log M/n}
.
This lower bound holds due to the possible non-convexity of the class and the arbitrary position of the target
Y
with respect to it.
Roughly speaking, non-convexity can induce an erratic behavior of selection rules and prevents one from exploiting the curvature of the squared loss.
In contrast, when the base class of predictors is convex, curvature of the loss function can be exploited to obtain a faster rate scaling as
\log M/n
for empirical risk minimization.
For relevant lower bounds in the statistical setting, we refer
to
[
16
, §3.5]
,
[
3
, §20.2]
and
[
37
,
7
]
.
A corresponding lower bound in the sequential setup, which can be deduced from the statistical lower bound by (
2
), can be found in
[
19
, p. 72]
.

Aggregation by exponential weights.

Perhaps the most important aggregation procedure, both in the sequential and statistical settings, is the method of
exponential weights
.
This estimator is useful and nontrivial even in the case of model aggregation, but it is most naturally defined and analyzed in a more general context described below.

Let
\{f_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
denote the class of base predictors, where the parameter set
\Theta
is now general and not necessarily finite.
As before, we make no special assumptions on the structure of the class, which may be non-convex.
A natural idea to artificially introduce some structure to the class is to “convexify” it.
Specifically, let
\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
denote the set of probability measures on
\Theta
.
This set possesses a convex structure and
\Theta
naturally embeds into it by associating each
\theta\in\Theta
to the Dirac mass
\delta_{\theta}
. In addition, each function
h:\Theta\to\mathbf{R}
defines a linear function on
\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
by integration:
\rho\mapsto\langle\rho,h\rangle=\int_{\Theta}h(\theta)\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta)
.
Now, let
\pi
be a probability measure on
\Theta
, called “prior” in analogy with Bayesian statistics; this distribution encodes the “inductive bias” of the procedure, and should generally put mass on “simpler” regions of the class.
The prior
\pi
endows
\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
with a convex regularizer given by the
relative entropy
or
Kullback-Leibler divergence

\rho\mapsto\mathrm{KL}({\rho},{\pi})=\int_{\Theta}\log(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi})\mathrm{d}\rho
, with the convention that
\mathrm{KL}({\rho},{\pi})=+\infty
when
\rho
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi
.
The
exponential weights
rule is obtained by optimizing the linearized objective on
\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
with an entropic regularization. Specifically,
the exponential weights posterior
\widehat{\rho}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
with prior
\pi
and inverse temperature (learning rate)
\beta>0
is given by

\widehat{\rho}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\big\{\langle\rho,R_{n}\rangle+\beta^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{KL}({\rho},{\pi})\big\}

(3)

where
R_{n}(\theta)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f_{\theta}(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}
denotes the empirical risk of the predictor
f_{\theta}
.
The solution to this optimization problem can be expressed explicitly using the Donsker-Varadhan variational formula:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{\rho}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}(\theta)=\frac{\exp\{-\beta R_{n}(\theta)\}}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\{-\beta R_{n}(\vartheta)\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\vartheta)}.

(4)

Finally, each distribution
\rho
gives rise to the “averaged” or “mixture” predictor
f_{\rho}(x)=\int_{\Theta}f_{\theta}(x)\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta)
; in particular, the exponential weights predictor is
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}=f_{\widehat{\rho}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}}
.

In the model aggregation context with
|\Theta|=M
, the exponential weights algorithm with uniform prior on
\Theta
and inverse temperature
\beta_{i}=i/8
for
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{i}
has a regret (
1
) bounded by
8\log(M)/n

[
19
, pp. 45-46]
.
By identity (
2
) and convexity of the risk, this implies that the following averaged estimator, proposed in
[
12
,
16
,
72
]
and known as
progressive mixture
rule:

\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{pm}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{i}

(5)

achieves the excess risk bound

{\mathbf{E}}[R(\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{pm}}_{n})]-\min_{\theta\in\Theta}R(f_{\theta})\leqslant 8\log(M)/(n+1)
, which is known to be
the optimal rate of aggregation
[
63
]
.
Remarkably, while working in the space of measures (the convex hull of the class)
improves the geometry of the class by ensuring convexity, the above bound features the complexity of the original class rather than that of its convex hull.
Indeed, the best achievable excess risk over the convex hull of
M
functions is substantially larger than the
\log M/n
rate for model aggregation
[
63
]
.

In fact, the progressive mixture was the only known rate-optimal model aggregation procedure until the works of
[
7
,
41
]
, which introduced procedures with optimal high-probability bounds (that the progressive mixture does not achieve).

Global and local entropic complexities.

While the exponential weights/progressive mixture estimators achieve the optimal rates of aggregation for finite classes, entropic penalization is well-defined in a broader context. The class of “entropy-regularized” procedures leads to a natural “entropic” complexity measure, which is different from the more traditional measures of complexity such as covering numbers, Rademacher complexity, or VC dimension
[
65
,
39
,
31
,
70
]
.

Entropic complexities depend on the prior
\pi
. For any
\pi
, it follows from the regret bound for exponential weights in the sequential setting together with the identity (
2
), that if
\beta_{i}=i/c
with
c\geqslant 8
then

\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\mathbf{E}}[R(\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{i})]\leqslant\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\bigg\{\langle\gamma,R\rangle+c\cdot\frac{\mathrm{KL}({\gamma},{\pi})}{n+1}\bigg\}.

(6)

The quantity on the right-hand side plays an important role in the study of exponential weights and entropy-regularized aggregation.
We call this quantity the
global entropic complexity

[
69
,
73
,
8
]
. It is defined as

\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{glob}}(\beta,\pi)=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\Big\{\langle\gamma,R\rangle+\beta^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{KL}({\gamma},{\pi})\Big\}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\bigg(\int_{\Theta}e^{-\beta R(\theta)}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)\bigg).

(7)

It is worth noting that the complexity
\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{glob}}(\beta,\pi)
only depends on
\beta
and on the distribution of
R(\theta)
under
\theta\sim\pi
,
and not explicitly on the “size” or dimension of the class.
In addition, the more mass the prior puts on parameters
\theta
with small risk, the smaller the complexity; this quantity, therefore, measures the quality of the prior
\pi
for the actual risk
R
.

The global entropic complexity appears in classical results in aggregation, both in the sequential and statistical settings (and within the statistical framework, both for learning and denoising).
For instance, in the statistical learning setting, the bound (
6
) together with convexity of the risk
R
implies that the progressive mixture (
5
) satisfies the following bound:
{\mathbf{E}}[R(\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{pm}}_{n})]\leqslant\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{glob}}((n+1)/c,\pi)
.

At the same time, sequential prediction seems genuinely harder than statistical estimation, for the following reason.
In sequential prediction, one must issue predictions at each time step
i=1,\dots,n
, having seen only the first
i-1
observations, and the error is averaged over sample sizes.
Clearly, less information is available at early rounds, which should result in a lower precision.
In contrast, in statistical estimation, one has at hand a full sample of
n
observations.
That the global complexity
\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{glob}}(\beta,\pi)
bounds the hardness of sequential prediction suggests that it provides an overly pessimistic account of the hardness of the easier, statistical problem.
This suboptimality manifests itself in different ways, such as additional logarithmic factors,
inability to handle unbounded parameter domains, or it can provide misleading indication on the qualitative effect of some problem parameters.

The refinement of the global entropic complexity that we consider is the following
local entropic complexity
: letting
\pi_{h}=e^{h}\pi/\langle\pi,e^{h}\rangle\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
for a function
h:\Theta\to\mathbf{R}
,

\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(\beta,\pi)=\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}R(\theta)e^{-\beta R(\theta)}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta R(\theta)}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}=\langle\pi_{-\beta R},R\rangle.

(8)

The local complexity
is the average risk under the exponential weights posterior with respect to the
true
(rather than empirical) risk. In analogy with thermodynamics, the global complexity corresponds to the “free energy”, while the local complexity corresponds to the “average energy”.
The link between both complexities is given by the relations

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\{\beta\cdot\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{glob}}(\beta,\pi)\}=\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(\beta,\pi)\quad\text{{i.e.,}{} }\quad\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{glob}}(\beta,\pi)=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(\eta,\pi)\mathrm{d}\eta.

(9)

In addition, by a standard property of log-Laplace transforms,
-\beta\cdot\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{glob}}(\beta,\pi)
is convex in
\beta
, so that
\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(\beta,\pi)
is decreasing in
\beta
.
Combining this with the second identity in (
9
) shows that
\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(\beta,\pi)\leqslant\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{glob}}(\beta,\pi)
.
Another essentially equivalent quantity is

\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\Big\{\langle\gamma,R\rangle+\beta^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{KL}({\gamma},{\pi_{-\beta R}})\Big\}=\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{glob}}(\beta,\pi_{-\beta R})=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\beta}^{2\beta}\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(\eta,\pi)\mathrm{d}\eta,

(as since
\pi_{-\beta R})_{-\eta R}=\pi_{-(\beta+\eta)R}
.
In particular, by monotonicity of
\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(\eta,\pi)
we have

\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(2\beta,\pi)\leqslant\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{glob}}(\beta,\pi_{-\beta R})\leqslant\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(\beta,\pi).

(10)

Combining inequality (
6
) with the relation (
9
) leads to the bound

\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\mathbf{E}}[R(\widehat{f}_{i})]\leqslant\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{0}^{n+1}\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(c\,\eta,\pi)\,\mathrm{d}\eta\leqslant\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(c\,i,\pi).

(11)

In words, the averaged (over sample sizes
i=0,\dots,n
) risk of the exponential weights estimator
\widehat{f}_{i}
(based on the first
i
observations and with inverse temperature
\beta_{i}=c\,i
) is bounded by the averaged local complexity.
Given this, it is natural to aim for the following strengthening of (
11
): control the risk of exponential weights (or another aggregation procedure) with
n
samples by the local complexity
\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(c\,n,\pi)
.
However, this inequality does not follow from (
11
); instead, the classical approach is to deduce (using convexity of
R
) the global bound
{\mathbf{E}}[R(\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{pm}}_{n})]\leqslant\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{glob}}(c(n+1),\pi)
for the averaged estimator
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{pm}}_{n}
.
One of the main contributions of this work is to show that the risk of suitable entropy-regularized aggregation methods can indeed be controlled by the local complexity
\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(c\,n,\pi)
, at the same level of generality as the global bounds.

Summary of main results

The rest of the paper is split into two parts. First, in Section
2
, we state our localized PAC-Bayesian bounds in three different statistical learning settings. Then, in Section
3
, we instantiate our abstract results to concrete problems.
Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

In the fixed design regression setting, we prove two localized bounds. Theorem
1
provides a localized version of the classical
risk bound for the exponential weights estimator due to Leung and Barron
[
44
]
.
Theorem
2
proves an analogous bound for the
Q
-aggregation estimator, this time optimal in deviation.

•

Our fixed design local risk bound for the
Q
-aggregation estimator is extended to the random design setting in Theorem
3
.
A special case of this bound resolves a question raised by Lecué and Mendelson
[
42
, Question 1.2]
on model aggregation guarantees that adapt to the hardness of the problem instead of always paying for the worst-case minimax rate.

•

Section
3.2

deals with Gaussian process priors, making explicit some distinctions between local and global complexities and connecting the
Q
-aggregation and ridge regression estimators.

•

Section
3.3
is devoted to the analysis of improper ridge-type estimators within the context of random design regression. Although we utilize a leave-one-out type of analysis in this section, we are able to recover a complexity measure similar to those that appear in Section
3.2
.

•

Theorem
5
in Section
3.4
provides a deviation-optimal localized bound for the
Q
-aggregation estimator in the transductive learning setup with almost exchangeable priors. This result improves over the results from
[
58
]
for Vapnik-Chervonenkis classes by accounting for their local structure in the resulting bounds.

The proofs and further technical details are deferred to the appendix.

1.2
Notation

This section summarizes the notation used throughout the paper. We let
\Theta
and
\mathcal{X}
be measurable spaces, and
f:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
be a measurable function.
For any
\theta\in\Theta
, let
f_{\theta}=f(\theta,\cdot):\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
the space of all probability distributions on
\Theta
.
Henceforth, we leave measurability assumptions implicit to lighten the presentation, and adopt the convention that all introduced functions between measurable spaces are measurable. In order to distinguish between expectations over
\Theta
(which come from the fact that we work on the probability simplex
\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
) and randomness that comes from random samples
(X,Y)
in
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbf{R}
, we denote expectations over
\Theta
using integral and measure-theoretic notation, while we use probabilistic notation for random variables such as random samples.
Specifically, for
\mu
a measure on
\Theta
and
g:\Theta\to\mathbf{R}
, we denote by

\langle\mu,g\rangle=\int_{\Theta}g(\theta)\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta)

the integral of
f
with respect to the measure
\mu
, assuming it is well-defined.
For any distribution
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
, and any function
f:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
, the notation
\langle\rho,f\rangle
averages over
\Theta
and results in a function
f_{\rho}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
defined for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
by

f_{\rho}(x)=\langle\rho,f(\cdot,x)\rangle=\int_{\Theta}f_{\theta}(x)\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta).
We introduce the shorthand notation
R(\rho)=R(f_{\rho})
and
\langle\rho,R_{n}\rangle=\int_{\Theta}R(f_{\theta})\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta).

The empirical norm
\|\cdot\|_{n}^{2}
is defined by

\|f\|_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})^{2}
. For any function
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
, and any probability measure
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
, and any function
f:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
we also introduce the following shorthand notation

\langle\rho,\|f-g\|_{n}^{2}\rangle=\int_{\Theta}\|f(\theta,\cdot)-g(\cdot)\|_{n}^{2}\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta)
.
For any functions
f,g
, we write
f\lesssim g
if there is an absolute constant
c>0
such that
f\leqslant cg
.

2
Main results

We now state the main results of this paper: localized PAC-Bayesian
bounds for various statistical aggregation problems. In
Section
2.1
, we treat the fixed design (or
denoising) setting, where the covariates are assumed to be deterministic.
The case of random design regression (statistical learning) is then considered in
Section
2.2
.
We also mention that a third setting, namely
transductive learning, will be considered in Section
3.4
.

While each of the learning formulations listed above admits a different
population risk functional
R
, its empirical counterpart
R_{n}
remains the
same across the three problems.
Given a sample
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of
n
observations, the empirical risk
R_{n}(f)
of a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
is defined by

R_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}.

(12)

Our results apply to two entropy-regularized aggregation procedures.
The first one is the
exponential weights
estimator. Given a prior
distribution
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
and an inverse temperature parameter

\beta>0
, it is defined by

\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}=\langle\widehat{\rho}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n},f\rangle,\text{ where }\widehat{\rho}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}=\widehat{\rho}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}(\pi,\beta)=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\langle\rho,R_{n}\rangle+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\rho,\pi)\right\}.

(13)

The estimator
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}
is analyzed in Section
2.1
in the
classical Gaussian fixed design setting. The purpose of this analysis is to
demonstrate some challenges associated with PAC-Bayesian
localization in the simplest possible setting and also, to provide an
improvement over the classical non-localized bound of Leung and Barron
[
44
]
.

Despite optimal in-expectation performance in the denoising setting,
the exponential weights estimator
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}
is known to suffer from
sub-optimal tail behavior
[
21
]
.
In the fixed design setting, a known remedy is to replace the linear term

\langle\rho,R_{n}\rangle
in (
13
)
by the convex combination

\alpha\langle\rho,R_{n}\rangle+(1-\alpha)R_{n}(\rho)
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
The intuition for this manipulation is as follows. The choice
\alpha=1

yields the exponential weights estimator, which as mentioned above is deviation
sub-optimal. While minimizing the objective with the term
\langle\rho,R_{n}\rangle
introduces inductive bias towards “sparse” probability measures,
yielding good complexity regularization for the model selection aggregation
problem, this regularizing effect is completely lost at the other extreme

\alpha=0
.
On the other hand, any
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a good choice: the resulting
objective still benefits from the inductive bias of the linear term
\alpha\langle\rho,R_{n}\rangle
, while the term
(1-\alpha)R_{n}(\alpha)
allows us
to benefit from the curvature of the quadratic loss that is lost with the
choice
\alpha=1
; this curvature of the quadratic loss is crucial to
regularize variance terms that render the estimator
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}
deviation suboptimal
despite its in-expectation optimality for the denoising problem.
While any
\alpha\in(0,1)
would work, in order to simplify our results
we define the
Q
-aggregation
estimator with the particular choice
\alpha=1/2
:

\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}=\langle\widehat{\rho}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n},f\rangle,\text{ where }\widehat{\rho}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}=\widehat{\rho}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}(\pi,\beta)=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\langle\rho,R_{n}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}R_{n}(\rho)+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\rho,\pi)\right\}.

(14)

This estimator was first proposed by Audibert
[
5
]
in the context of convex aggregation (where one aims to compare to convex combinations of functions in the class).
It was first recognized as an optimal model aggregation procedure by Rigollet
[
59
]
,
and variants thereof were further analyzed in

[
21
,
43
,
13
]
.
In particular, Dai, Rigollet and Zhang
[
21
]
obtain a global PAC-Bayes bound in
the fixed design setup.

2.1
Fixed design

In this section, let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in\mathcal{X}

be arbitrary
deterministic
covariate vectors and consider the model:

Y_{i}=f^{\star}(X_{i})+Z_{i},

(15)

where
Y=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
is the vector of response
variables,
Z=(Z_{i},\dots,Z_{n})
is a random vector of zero-mean noise,
and
f^{\star}
is the regression function.
Observe that the only randomness in the model (
15
) comes from
the noise vector
Z
.
Define the population risk functional
R
by

R(f)=\mathbf{E}_{Y}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}\right]=\|f-f^{\star}\|_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Var}(Z_{i}),

(16)

while its empirical counterpart
R_{n}
is defined in
(
12
).
With a slight abuse of notation, in what follows we identify
f^{\star}
with
the vector
(f^{\star}(X_{i}))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

This section contains two results. The following result, proved in
Section
A.3
, provides the first localized
in-expectation bound for the exponential weights estimator under the assumption
that the noise vector
Z
is standard normal. In particular, this yields an
improvement over the classical
global
PAC-Bayesian bound obtained in the same
setup by Leung and Barron
[
44
]
.
The assumption that
Z
is Gaussian, in particular, allows us to leverage Stein’s Unbiased Risk Estimator (SURE) in the analysis of
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}
. Such an approach in the context of aggregation via
exponential weights was first considered by George
[
29
]
, and
later systematically applied by Leung and Barron
[
44
]
, Dalalyan and Tsybakov
[
22
]
and Dalalyan and Salmon
[
24
]
, among others.
For a treatment of more general noise distributions, we refer to
[
23
]
and references therein.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the fixed design setting described above
and assume that
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. centered
Gaussian random variables with variance
\sigma^{2}
.
Let
\{f_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
be a family of vectors in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
a fixed probability distribution.
Then, the exponential weights estimator
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}=\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}(\pi,\beta)
defined in (
13
), with prior
\pi
and inverse temperature
\beta=n/(8\sigma^{2})
, satisfies

{\mathbf{E}}\Big[\|\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n}-f^{\star}\|_{n}^{2}\Big]\leqslant\int_{\Theta}\lVert{f_{\theta}-f^{\star}}\rVert_{n}^{2}\,\pi_{-nR/(16\sigma^{2})}(\mathrm{d}\theta).

(17)

Equivalently and with the notation (
8
) from the introduction, the bound (
17
) writes

{\mathbf{E}}\big[R(\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{ew}}_{n})\big]\leqslant\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(n/(16\sigma^{2}),\pi).

As already mentioned at the beginning of Section
2
, the
exponential weights estimator is deviation suboptimal. On the other hand,
it was shown in
[
21
]
showed that the
Q
-aggregation estimator satisfies
a
global
exponential-tail bound in the fixed design setting with
sub-Gaussian noise; we say that a centered real random variable
Z
is

\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian if for any
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}
we have

{\mathbf{E}}\exp(\lambda Z)\leqslant\exp(\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2)
.
The following result shows that in fact, the
Q
-aggregation
estimator satisfied a deviation optimal
local
bound. See
Appendix
A.4
for the proof.

Theorem 2
.

Consider the fixed design setting
and assume that the noise random variables
Z_{i}
are i.i.d., zero-mean and

\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian.
Let
\{f_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
be a family of vectors in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
a fixed probability distribution.
Let
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}=\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}(\pi,\beta)
denote the
Q
-aggregation estimator defined in (
14
), with prior
\pi
and inverse temperature
\beta=n/(12\sigma^{2})
.
Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta

it holds that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}-f^{\star}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Theta}\lVert{f_{\theta}-f^{\star}}\rVert_{n}^{2}\,\pi_{-nR/(36\sigma^{2})}(\mathrm{d}\theta)+\frac{18\sigma^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{n}.

(18)

2.2
Random design

We now to to the
random design
setting, where we observe
n
input-output pairs
(X_{i},Y_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,n
, each drawn independently from some distribution
P
.
Unlike in the fixed design setting considered in Section
2.1
, we work under boundedness assumptions on both the response variable
Y
and the functions in the class.
Specifically, we assume in this section that, for some constant
b>0
, one has
|Y|\leqslant b
almost surely, and for every
\theta\in\Theta
,

\lVert{f_{\theta}}\rVert_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|f_{\theta}(x)|\leqslant b.

(19)

When working with arbitrary classes of functions, such boundedness conditions on the response variable
Y
and the reference functions are natural and have been considered by many authors; see, e.g.,
[
63
,
7
,
43
]
.
Finally, the population risk functional
R
is defined by

R(f)={\mathbf{E}}_{(X,Y)\sim P}\left[(f(X)-Y)^{2}\right],

while its empirical version
R_{n}
is defined in (
12
).
The following result in proved in Appendix
A.5
.

Theorem 3
.

Consider the random design setup described above.
Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
be any fixed probability distribution
and let
\beta=c_{1}n/b^{2}
be the inverse temperature parameter.
Let
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}=\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}(\pi,\beta)
denote the
Q
-aggregation estimator defined in
(
14
).
Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta

it holds that

R(\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n})\leqslant\int_{\Theta}R(f_{\theta})\,\pi_{-c_{1}nR/(3b^{2})}(\mathrm{d}\theta)+\frac{c_{2}b^{2}\log(3/\delta)}{n},

(20)

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are universal constants.

Finally, it is important to note that the estimator
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}
, within the framework of model aggregation involving random design, has not been previously analyzed. The question of analyzing the excess risk associated with this particular estimator was posed in
[
51
]
.

3
Applications

In this section, we instantiate the local bounds in several different contexts.

3.1
Model aggregation

As discussed in the introduction, a problem where
\Theta=\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M}\}
is a finite set is called
model aggregation
. While the results we are about to discuss are applicable in all three learning setups considered in Section
2
, for the sake of concreteness, let us consider the random design setup described in Section
2.2
. In particular, the response variable
Y
is almost surely contained in
[-b,b]
and the reference class of functions satisfies the boundedness condition (
19
).

Let
\widehat{f}=\widehat{f}(S_{n})
denote the output of any statistical estimator, where
S_{n}=(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is the observed data sample. In the model aggregation problem, we wish to obtain bounds on

R(\widehat{f})-\min_{\theta\in\Theta}R(f_{\theta}).

The following minimax lower bound is well-known
[
63
]
:

\inf_{\widehat{f}}\sup_{P,|\Theta|=M}{\mathbf{E}}R(\widehat{f})-\min_{\theta\in\Theta}R(f_{\theta})\geqslant c\frac{b^{2}\log M}{n},

(21)

where
c>0
is a universal constant, the expectation is taken with respect
to
S_{n}
, the infimum is taken over all statistical estimators

\widehat{f}
, and the supremum is taken over all data-generating
distributions and reference classes of functions of size
M
that respect the
above-described constraints.

Lecué and Mendelson
[
42
]
asked whether it is possible to prove an upper bound for the model aggregation problem that exhibits some form of “instance adaptivity”, that is, adaptivity to the hardness of the specific problem at hand.
This adaptivity should be quantitatively reflected in a risk bound that does not depend on the full size of the reference set
\Theta
, but only on the number of functions that are close to the best function in the class.
The proposition stated below provides such a guarantee.
We remark that partial results were obtained in
[
42
]
under the so-called Bernstein assumption.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the random design model aggregation setup described above and let
\theta^{\star}\in\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{j=1,\dots,M}R(\theta_{j})
.
Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
be the uniform distribution over the finite set
\Theta

and set the inverse temperature parameter
\beta=n/(576b^{2})
.
Let
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}=\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}(\pi,\beta)
denote
Q
-aggregation estimator defined in
(
14
).
Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta

it holds that

R(\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n})-R(f_{\theta^{\star}})\leqslant c_{1}\frac{b^{2}}{n}\bigg[\log\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{M}\exp\left(-c_{2}\frac{n}{b^{2}}\big(R(\theta_{j})-R(\theta^{\star})\big)\right)\bigg)+\log(1/\delta)\bigg],

(22)

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are universal constants.

Proof.

The result follows from Theorem
3
by upper bounding the local complexity using the inequality (
10
) and by plugging in
\gamma=\delta_{\theta^{\star}}
, a Dirac mass at
\theta^{\star}
, into the resulting bound.
∎

3.2
Gaussian priors

In this section, we consider the case where the prior
\pi
on functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
is a centered Gaussian process.
In order to simplify notation, we assume that
\pi
is supported on a finite-dimensional space of functions; this is not critical to our analysis, which would apply to the infinite-dimensional case with minor changes in terminology.
Furthermore, up to a change the of variable
x
, we may assume that
\mathcal{X}=\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{F}
is the class of linear functions
f_{\theta}(x)=\langle\theta,x\rangle
for
\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
; up to a linear transformation, we may assume that
\pi=\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma^{-1}I_{d})
for some
\gamma>0
.

We first introduce some notation for both fixed and random design.
The sample covariance matrix is
\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}X_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
, while for
\lambda>0
the
ridge estimator

\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda}
is:

\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big\{R_{n}(f_{\theta})+\lambda\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\big\}=(\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}X_{i}.

We now determine the
Q
-aggregation estimator under Gaussian prior for the linear class.

Proposition 2
.

Under the prior
\pi=\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma^{-1}I_{d})

and with inverse temperature
\beta/2
, the
Q
-aggregation posterior is
\mathcal{N}(\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda},\beta^{-1}(\frac{1}{2}\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}+\lambda I_{d})^{-1})
while the exponential weights posterior is
\mathcal{N}(\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda},\beta^{-1}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}+\lambda I_{d})^{-1})
, where
\lambda=\gamma/\beta
.
The associated prediction functions both coincide with that of the ridge regression estimator
f_{\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
.

Exponential weights is well-known in this context, and can be computed directly thanks to its explicit expression.
In contrast, the solution to the
Q
-aggregation optimization problem does not have a closed form in general.
In this case, the solution is obtained by first showing that it is Gaussian (using Proposition
5
), and then solving the optimization problem among Gaussian posteriors.

Next, we express and compare the global and local complexities.

Proposition 3
.

Assume that
\mathcal{X}=\mathbf{R}^{d}
and let

\mathcal{F}=\{x\mapsto\langle\theta,x\rangle:\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}\}

be the class of linear functions. Set
\theta_{\lambda}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\{R(\theta)+\lambda\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\}
, and
\Sigma={\mathbf{E}}[XX^{\mathsf{T}}]
(assumed finite) for random design, and
\Sigma=\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}X_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
for fixed design. Set
\pi=\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma^{-1}I_{d})
. Then, for any
\beta>0
, letting
\lambda=\gamma/\beta
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{glob}}(\beta/2,\pi)

\displaystyle=R(\theta_{\lambda})+\lambda\lVert{\theta_{\lambda}}\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{\beta}\log\det\big(I_{d}+\lambda^{-1}\Sigma\big)

(23)

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{loc}}(\beta/2,\pi)

\displaystyle=R(\theta_{\lambda})+\frac{1}{\beta}\cdot\tr[(\Sigma+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma].

(24)

In addition, assuming for the second inequality below that
0<\lambda\leqslant\|{\Sigma}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
,

\tr\big[(\Sigma+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma\big]\leqslant\log\det\big(I_{d}+\lambda^{-1}\Sigma\big)\leqslant 2\log\bigg(1+\frac{\|{\Sigma}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{\lambda}\bigg)\cdot\tr\big[(\Sigma+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma\big].

(25)

We note that the global entropic complexity already appears in the seminal work of
[
69
]
in online linear regression, where it bounds the regret of the so-called Vovk-Azoury-Warmuth forecaster.
Here, the local complexity improves the global complexity in two ways.
First, it replaces the log determinant by a trace, allowing in particular to take
\lambda\to 0
; this allows to remove a
\log n
factor as
n\to\infty
, and also to obtain bounds for arbitrary large norms of the comparison parameter
\theta
(by taking
\lambda
small enough that
\lambda\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\ll d/n
).
Second, it removes an approximation term of
\lambda\lVert{\theta_{\lambda}}\rVert^{2}
, which allows to obtain better rates in some regimes (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
). We also note that in the context of robust estimation, versions of local entropic complexities
have been computed in
[
10
,
9
]
.

Theorems
1
and
2
in the fixed design setting apply to the present context, and provide bounds for the ridge regression estimator featuring the local complexity of Proposition
3
.
However, the random-design result does not apply to the Gaussian setting, since it requires boundedness of the base functions.
In Theorem
4
, we indicate a different approach giving bounds for random design.

3.3
Random-design bounds for ridge-type improper estimators

In this section, we consider an alternative approach to obtaining excess risk bounds featuring related complexities as the one from the previous section, in the random design setting.

At this point, it is worth discussing some difficulties that are specific to the random design case.
Unlike for fixed design, we now aim to predict for a new, unseen point
X
.
This difference has the technical implication that, when analyzing an estimator like ridge regression, which is based on empirical risk minimization and corresponds to
Q
-aggregation as per Proposition
2
, we must relate two distinct covariance matrices: the sample covariance matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}
(which measures the closeness of predictions of different parameters on the sample) and the population covariance
\Sigma
(which controls closeness on a new random sample
X
).
To obtain guarantees of this nature, it is not hard to show that some assumptions on the distribution of
X
are required beyond just the existence of
\Sigma
, such as controlled moments of order
p>2
.

Here, a key point is that precise excess risk bounds can nonetheless be obtained without additional assumptions on the distribution of
X
, provided that different estimators than ridge regression are considered; we refer to
[
31
,
27
,
63
,
55
]
for more information on this topic, in the case of linear regression (that is, with guarantees uniform over the linear class).

An important aspect of the problem at hand is that, in order to obtain nontrivial guarantees without additional assumptions on the distribution of
X
, one must consider aggregation procedures that return a function outside the class, that is, a nonlinear function
[
60
,
55
]
.
While this is similar to the need for aggregation rules for finite classes discussed in the introduction, it calls for some explanation.
Indeed, for finite classes, the need for aggregation rules arises due to nonconvexity of the class; in contrast, the class of linear functions is convex.
In this case, the need for aggregation methods
stems from the unbounded nature of linear functions, leading to large errors when
X
distribution can take large values with small probabilities
[
55
]
. Hence, the guarantees of Section
2.2
do not apply as Theorem
2
needs bounded functions in the class. To ensure boundedness, we can truncate the original functions
[
31
,
55
]
, projecting their predictions to a bounded interval. However, this operation makes the resultant truncated linear functions class nonconvex, even if the original class was convex.

A natural approach to obtain guarantees is then to perform
Q
-aggregation on the class of
truncated
linear functions, under a Gaussian prior on parameters.
One could then apply Theorem
3
, since its boundedness condition does apply.
However, this approach has two main limitations.
First, the resulting estimator
involves a non-convex minimization problem, whose computational complexity could depend exponentially on the dimension.
Second, it is unclear that the resulting procedure would satisfy a risk bound featuring the local complexity from Proposition
3
.
Indeed, if one applies Theorem
2
, the resulting bound would depend on the local entropic complexity (under Gaussian prior) of
truncated
linear functions.
Assuming that
|Y|\leqslant b
, truncating linear functions at
b
only reduces their risk, and thus also the associated global entropic complexity.
Hence, a bound based on the global complexity of Proposition
3
holds for this method.
In contrast, replacing functions by functions with smaller risk does not necessarily decrease the
local
complexity, and as such the local entropic complexity of truncated linear functions could be larger than that of linear functions.

We now describe an alternative approach, which partly circumvents these limitations.
This approach is based on leave-one-out and exchangeability considerations, and has been used to design aggregation procedures for linear regression in
[
27
]
, and for density estimation and logistic regression in
[
54
]
.
This approach allows to obtain bounds of “local” nature, without the additional logarithmic factors associated with “global” bounds.
In addition, it generally leads to estimators that are simple efficiently computable.
On the other hand, it only leads to guarantees in expectation rather than in deviation
[
55
]
.
In addition, the resulting bound is slightly larger than the local entropic complexity of Proposition
3
.

We now describe the considered procedures and their guarantees.
Recall that the
ridge
estimator is given by

\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big\{R_{n}(\theta)+\lambda\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\big\}=(\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}X_{i}.

(26)

For any
\lambda>0
and
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, define the
ridge leverage score
of
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
by

h_{\lambda}(x)=\bigg\langle\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}X_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}+\lambda nI_{d}+xx^{\mathsf{T}}\bigg)^{-1}x,x\bigg\rangle,

(27)

and define (letting
\lambda^{\prime}=(1+1/n)\lambda
)

\widehat{f}_{\lambda}(x)=(1-h_{\lambda^{\prime}}(x))^{2}\cdot\langle\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda^{\prime}},x\rangle.

(28)

This estimator is a ridge-regularized analogue of the Forster-Warmuth procedure introduced in
[
27
]
, which corresponds to the case
\lambda=0
.
We therefore call it
ridge FW
.
It can also be seen a linear version of the ridge-regularized logistic regression procedure from
[
55
]
.
Also, for
b>0

define the truncation function
\psi_{b}:\mathbf{R}\to\mathbf{R}
by
\psi_{b}(x)=\max(-b,\min(b,x))
for
x\in\mathbf{R}
.
Then,
let

\widehat{g}_{\lambda,b}(x)=\psi_{b}\big(\langle\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda^{\prime}},x\rangle\big).

(29)

This procedure is a ridge-regularized version of the truncated least squares estimator considered in
[
31
,
55
]
.

These estimators satisfy the following excess risk bounds:

Theorem 4
.

For any distribution
P
on
\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{R}
such that
|Y|\leqslant b

almost surely and
\Sigma={\mathbf{E}}XX^{\mathsf{T}}
exists,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\big[R(\widehat{f}_{\lambda})\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\inf_{\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big\{R(\theta)+\lambda\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\big\}+2m^{2}\,\frac{\tr[(\Sigma+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma]}{n+1}\,;

(30)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\big[R(\widehat{g}_{\lambda,b})\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\inf_{\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big\{R(\theta)+\lambda\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\big\}+8m^{2}\,\frac{\tr[(\Sigma+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma]}{n+1}.

(31)

In addition, let
\widehat{b}=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|Y_{i}|
and
\widehat{g}_{\lambda}(x)=\widehat{g}_{\lambda,\widehat{b}}(x)
.
Then, we have

{\mathbf{E}}\big[R(\widehat{g}_{\lambda})\big]\leqslant\inf_{\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big\{R(\theta)+\lambda\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\big\}+8b^{2}\,\frac{\tr[(\Sigma+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma]}{n+1}+\frac{b^{2}}{n+1}.

(32)

The three estimators
\widehat{f}_{\lambda},\widehat{g}_{\lambda,b}
and
\widehat{g}_{\lambda}
achieve the same risk bound up to constant factors.
The main differences are:

•

\widehat{f}_{\lambda}

is more expensive computationally than truncated ridge at prediction time.
Indeed, truncated ridge only requires to store the vector
\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, which takes
O(d)
memory (in the case of infinite- or high-dimensional reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, using the so-called kernel trick one can replace
d
by
n
in all this discussion); computing predictions involves a scalar product, which takes
O(d)
time.
In contrast,
\widehat{f}_{\lambda}
requires storing both
\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda^{\prime}}
and the precision matrix
S_{\lambda}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}X_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}+\lambda(n+1)I_{d})^{-1}
, which takes
O(d^{2})
memory.
Computation of the leverage
h_{\lambda^{\prime}}(x)=\langle S_{\lambda}x,x\rangle/(1+\langle S_{\lambda}x,x\rangle)
(by Sherman-Morrison’s identity) involves computing
\langle S_{\lambda}x,x\rangle

O(d^{2})
time.

•

On the other hand, from a statistical perspective,
\widehat{g}_{\lambda,b}
requires prior knowledge of the parameter

, while
\widehat{f}_{\lambda}
is adaptive to
b
.
This shortcoming of
\widehat{g}_{\lambda,b}
is addressed by the adaptive truncated estimator
\widehat{g}_{\lambda}
.

In fact, one could replace
\tr[(\Sigma+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma]
by the (smaller) quantity
{\mathbf{E}}\tr[(\widehat{\Sigma}_{n+1}+\lambda)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}_{n+1}]
, which is always defined without any assumption on the distribution of
X
.
We stated the bound in terms of the population covariance since it is more explicit.
In any case, it is worth noting that this bound is true under only a second moment assumption on

.

We also mention that a remarkable procedure for linear regression in the sequential setting was proposed by Vovk
[

and Azoury and Warmuth
[

]

Combining its regret guarantee with the conversion described in the introduction, the averaged version

of this procedure satisfies the following risk bound:

{\mathbf{E}}R(\widehat{g}_{\lambda})\leqslant\inf_{\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big\{R(\theta)+\lambda\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\big\}+\frac{b^{2}\cdot\log\det(\lambda^{-1}\Sigma+I_{d})}{n+1}.

Compared to this bound, the guarantees from Theorem

3.4
Aggregation of classifiers and almost exchangeable priors

In the classic paper
[
36
]
, the following question is presented: Given a finite set
\mathcal{F}
comprised of
\{0,1\}
-valued functions and assuming that
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, how can one demonstrate the optimal sequential regret bound (
1
) for a particular loss function with curvature as a function of
n
and the cardinality of
\mathcal{F}
? An important particular case of their main result is the squared loss function, which is also the subject of our focus. Another interesting analysis of classes of binary-valued functions in the context of statistical aggregation appears in
[
58
]
.
To present their result, we first need some standard definitions.
Let
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
be a class of
\{0,1\}
-valued functions with the domain
\mathcal{X}
. The Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension of
\mathcal{F}
is the largest integer
d
such that

\max\limits_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\{(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{d})):f\in\mathcal{F}\}\right|=2^{d}.

Classes of
\{0,1\}
-valued functions with a finite VC dimension will be referred to as VC classes. Recall that for the squared loss we have
R(f)={\mathbf{E}}[(f(X)-Y)^{2}]
. The result in
[
58
, Theorem 3 and Remark 4]
can be stated as follows. For any VC class
\mathcal{F}
whose VC dimension if equal to
d
there is an aggregation procedure
\widehat{f}_{\textrm{RST}}
such that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

R(\widehat{f}_{\textrm{RST}})-\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}R(f)\leqslant c\left(\frac{d}{n}\log\left(\frac{en}{d}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right),

(33)

where
c>0
is some absolute constant provided that
n\geqslant d
. The primary difference from binary classification is the use of squared loss instead of the standard binary loss. This aligns with the work
[
36
]
. As a result, one can get the fast for the improper estimator
\widehat{f}_{\textrm{RST}}
without making any low noise/margin assumptions. Rakhlin, Sridharan and Tsybakov
[
58
]
also present an example of VC class showing that this bound cannot be improved (including the
\log\left(en/d\right)
term) up to multiplicative constant factors.
That is, at least in some cases, the inequality (
33
) can be reversed (but with a different absolute constant).

Recent efforts in the field of binary classification have focused on improving the
\log\left(en/d\right)
factor in the bounds. The authors of
[
34
]
and
[
75
]
leveraged additional complexity measures of VC classes to capture the refined local structure of the classes. A key complexity measure discussed in these papers is the
star number
, which is defined as the largest integer
s
such that there exists a subset
S=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{s}\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and functions
f_{0},f_{1},\dots,f_{s}\in\mathcal{F}

such that for all
i=1,\ldots,s
,
f_{0}
and
f_{i}
disagree on a unique point in
S
. That is, for all
i=1,\ldots,s
, we have
\{x_{i}\}=\{x\in S:f_{0}(x)\neq f_{i}(x)\}
. In binary classification, it is shown in
[
34
,
75
]
that the term
\log\left(en/d\right)
can usually be replaced in the risk bounds with
\log\left(\min\{en/d,s\}\right)
if the star number of
\mathcal{F}
is finite. A natural question arises as to whether the star number, which characterizes the local structure of VC classes, can be applied in the context of bound (
33
). It appears that the methods of this paper allow us to do so. In order to provide such a setup, we extend our analysis to handle the so-called
transductive algorithms
.
This approach was proposed by Vapnik
[
65
]
and has been demonstrated to have useful connections with PAC-Bayesian arguments in subsequent works
[
17
,
6
]
. In the simplest form of the transductive setup, the learner works with an i.i.d sample of size
2n
. Among these points, the first
n
points have their labels
Y_{i}
, while for the remaining
n
points we only observe the objects
X_{i}
, but not their labels. We are now aiming to prove the high probability upper bound on the empirical excess risk computed with respect to the second part of the sample:

R^{\prime}_{n}(\widehat{f})-\min\limits_{f\in\mathcal{F}}R^{\prime}_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\bigg(\sum_{i=n+1}^{2n}(\widehat{f}(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}-\min\limits_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=n+1}^{2n}(f(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}\bigg),

where the estimator
\widehat{f}
depends on
n
labeled instances and all
2n
unlabeled points. The idea is to replace the population risk
R(f)
by its empirical counterpart
R^{\prime}_{n}(f)
computed with respect to the second half of the sample.
Furthermore, for the inverse temperature parameter
\beta>0
, we redefine the (data-dependent) localized prior as follows:

\pi_{\beta(R_{n}+R^{\prime}_{n})}(\mathrm{d}\theta)=\frac{\exp(-\beta(R_{n}(f_{\theta})+R^{\prime}_{n}(f_{\theta})))}{\int_{\Theta}\exp(-\beta(R_{n}(f_{\vartheta})+R^{\prime}_{n}(f_{\vartheta})))\pi(\mathrm{d}\vartheta)}.

(34)

The next theorem is a direct extension of Theorem
3
to the transductive setup.

Theorem 5

(A local bound with almost exchangeable priors)
.

Consider the transductive classification setup discussed above. Assume that the boundedness assumption (
19
) holds. Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
be any probability distributions that may depend on the sample of size
2n
.
Let
\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}
be the output of the
Q
-aggregation estimator
defined in (
14
) with inverse temperature

\beta=c_{1}n/b^{2}
. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)

1-\delta

R_{n}^{\prime}(\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n})\leqslant\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\langle\gamma,R^{\prime}_{n}\rangle+\frac{3b^{2}}{2c_{1}n}\mathrm{KL}(\gamma,\pi_{-\frac{c_{1}n}{6b^{2}}(R_{n}+R^{\prime}_{n})})\right\}+c\frac{b^{2}\log(3/\delta)}{n}.

(35)

where
c,c_{1}>0
are universal constants.

The fact that our localized prior distribution now depends on the entire sample of size
2n
makes the setup of Theorem
5
closely related to the idea of
almost exchangeable priors
†
†

The prior distribution
\pi
depends on the entire sample of size
2n
, while two part of the sample are exchangeable due to independence, hence the terminology.
introduced in
[
17
,
6
]
and to
conditional mutual information
appearing in the recent works
[
61
,
30
,
32
]
.
Our focus is on the aggregation problem with squared loss without the realizability assumption, whereas prior work primarily addressed the standard binary classification setup.

in the context of binary classification.
Consider a VC class
\mathcal{F}
. Given an i.i.d. sample
S_{2n}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{2n}\}

\mathcal{F}|_{S_{2n}}=\{(f(X_{1}),\ldots,f(X_{2n})):f\in\mathcal{F}\}
sample of unlabeled instances and choose the prior distribution
\pi_{S_{2n}}
to be the uniform measure on
\mathcal{F}_{S_{2n}}
.
When working with our
Q
-Aggregation estimator, we identify
\mathcal{F}|_{S_{2n}}
with the set
\Theta
. Observe that
\mathcal{F}|_{S_{2n}}
is a finite class, so that our uniform distribution is correctly defined.

Proposition 4
.

Consider the transductive setup of Theorem

and let
\mathcal{F}

\{0,1\}

d

\widehat{f}^{\mathsf{q}}_{n}

In this section, we collect some basic facts related to the

quadratic loss and the curvature of the empirical the

We will repeatedly use the convex-conjugate duality
of the moment generating function and the
\mathrm{KL}
divergence written in the following form: for any function

such that the quantities below are well-defined,

\sup\limits_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\langle\rho,h\rangle-\mathrm{KL}(\rho,\pi)\right\}=\log\int_{\Theta}\exp\left(h(\theta)\right)\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta).

(36)

In order to obtain localized PAC-Bayes bounds, at some point in our proofs we
will need to replace the difference of localized
\mathrm{KL}
divergences

\mathrm{KL}(\rho,\pi_{-f})-\mathrm{KL}(\gamma,\pi_{-f})
by the difference
\mathrm{KL}(\rho,\pi)-\mathrm{KL}(\gamma,\pi)
. The following lemma shows that the price of this
interchange is equal to
\langle\rho-\gamma,f\rangle
, for any function

f
used to localize the prior
\pi
.

Lemma 1
.

For any probability distributions
\rho,\gamma,\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)

such that
\rho\ll\pi
and
\gamma\ll\pi
, and for any function

f:\Theta\to\mathbf{R}
we have

\mathrm{KL}(\rho,\pi_{-f})-\mathrm{KL}(\gamma,\pi_{-f})=\mathrm{KL}(\rho,\pi)-\mathrm{KL}(\gamma,\pi)+\langle\rho-\gamma,f\rangle.

(37)

Proof.

A direct computation yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\gamma,\pi_{-f})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}\log\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{-f}}(\theta)\bigg)\gamma(\mathrm{d}\theta)

(38)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma}{\mathrm{d}\pi}(\theta)\cdot\frac{\langle\pi,\exp(-f)\rangle}{\exp(-f(\theta))}\right)\gamma(\mathrm{d}\theta)

(39)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{KL}(\gamma,\pi)+\langle\gamma,f\rangle+\log\langle\pi,\exp(-f)\rangle.

(40)

Similarly, it holds that

\mathrm{KL}(\rho,\pi_{-f})=\mathrm{KL}(\rho,\pi)+\langle\rho,f\rangle+\log\langle\pi,\exp(-f)\rangle.

(41)

Subtracting (
41
) from (
40
)
concludes the proof of this lemma.
∎

Next, we recall some basic properties of the squared loss. Define

V_{n}(\rho)=\langle\rho,R_{n}\rangle-R_{n}(\rho)=\langle\rho,\|f-f_{\rho}\|_{n}^{2}\rangle=\int_{\Theta}\|f_{\theta}-f_{\rho}\|_{n}^{2}\,\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta),

(42)

is defined in (

we have

V_{n}(\rho)+\|f_{\rho}-f_{\gamma}\|_{n}^{2}=\langle\rho,\|f-f_{\gamma}\|_{n}^{2}\rangle.

(43)

The two identities stated above are repeatedly applied in the rest of the proofs without explicitly referring to this section.

The following lemma highlights the main property of the
Q
-aggregation estimator that distinguishes its analysis from the exponential weights.
This lemma bears similarity with the analysis using offset Rademacher complexities
[

-\|f_{\widehat{\rho}}-f_{\gamma}\|_{n}^{2}

Lemma 2
.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)

Then, for any

R_{n}(\widehat{\rho})-R_{n}(\gamma)-\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\gamma,\pi)+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\widehat{\rho},\pi)\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}\langle\widehat{\rho},\|f-f_{\gamma}\|_{n}^{2}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}V_{n}(\gamma).

(44)

Proof.

Let
Q_{n}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}R_{n}(\rho)+\frac{1}{2}\langle\rho,R_{n}\rangle+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\rho,\pi)
. Thus,
\widehat{\rho}
is the unique minimizer of

Observe that

\displaystyle R_{n}(\widehat{\rho})-R_{n}(\gamma)=\left(Q_{n}(\widehat{\rho})-Q_{n}(\gamma)\right)-\frac{1}{2}V_{n}(\widehat{\rho})+\frac{1}{2}V_{n}(\gamma)-\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\widehat{\rho},\pi)+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\gamma,\pi).

(45)

Strong convexity of the squared loss combined with the

\widehat{\rho}
for the objective

yields:

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\widehat{\rho})-Q_{n}(\gamma)\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}\|f_{\widehat{\rho}}-f_{\gamma}\|_{n}^{2}.

(46)

Plugging the above into (

yields

\displaystyle R_{n}(\widehat{\rho})-R_{n}(\gamma)

(47)

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}\left(\|f_{\widehat{\rho}}-f_{\gamma}\|_{n}^{2}+V_{n}(\widehat{\rho})\right)+\frac{1}{2}V_{n}(\gamma)-\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\widehat{\rho},\pi)+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\gamma,\pi).

(48)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\langle\widehat{\rho},\|f-f_{\gamma}\|_{n}^{2}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}V_{n}(\gamma)-\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\widehat{\rho},\pi)+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\gamma,\pi),

(49)

where the final identity follows from the variance identity

There exist (data-dependent) constants

Proof of Proposition
5

By definition of the
Q
-aggregation estimator, we have
\widehat{\rho}_{n}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\rho}\{\beta Q_{n}(\rho)+\mathrm{KL}({\rho},{\pi})\}

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\rho)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\langle\rho,R_{n}\rangle+\alpha R_{n}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Theta}(f_{\theta}(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta)+\frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(\int_{\Theta}f_{\theta}(X_{i})\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta)-Y_{i}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=\Phi\bigg\{\bigg(\int_{\Theta}f_{\theta}(X_{i})^{2}\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta)\bigg)_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n},\bigg(\int_{\Theta}f_{\theta}(X_{i})\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta)\bigg)_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\bigg\}\,,

\widehat{\rho}_{n}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\rho\in\Delta}\{\beta Q_{n}(\rho)+\mathrm{KL}({\rho},{\pi})\}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\rho\in\Delta}\mathrm{KL}({\rho},{\pi})

Proof of Theorem

Before presenting the proof, we make some remarks. It appears that the existing analysis of aggregation procedures
[
41
,
52
,
53
]
goes through the complexity of the class formed by pairwise differences between functions in the reference class. That is, the existing upper bounds are written in term of the localized complexity of the class
\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{F}=\{f-g:f,g\in\mathcal{F}\}
. While for convex classes
\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{F}
has essentially the same local complexity as
\mathcal{F}
itself, the gap can be significant for non-convex classes as shown by the following example.

Example 1

(The local complexity of the class
\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{F}
can be larger than that of
\mathcal{F}
)
.

Consider the class of thresholds in
\mathbb{Z}
. That is, define
\mathcal{F}=\{x\mapsto\bm{1}(x\geqslant t):t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. It is immediate to verify that the star number
s
of
\mathcal{F}
is equal to
2
. We further observe that the class
\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{F}
contains the class of singletons
\mathcal{G}=\{x\mapsto\bm{1}(x=t):t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. However, the star number of
\mathcal{G}
is equal to infinity. Informally, while each function in
\mathcal{F}
has at most two “neighbors” when restricted to a finite sample, the class
\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{F}
is more expressive and each function has a rich neighborhood.

Thus, if one wants to incorporate the star number in the bound such as (
33
), we need to avoid the existing techniques that focus on the class
\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{F}
. Our techniques are particularly suitable for this purpose.

5
. Fix

. Observe that our data dependent projection class

. Moreover, its VC dimension is bounded by

and its star number is bounded by
s
. Observe that

\gamma

The claim follows. ∎

Proof of Proposition

By the representer theorem (Proposition
5
), there exist
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n},c_{0}\in\mathbf{R}

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}(\theta)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}\theta}(\theta)\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}(\theta)=(2\pi\gamma^{-1})^{-d/2}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\gamma\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(a_{i}\langle\theta,X_{i}\rangle^{2}+b_{i}\langle\theta,X_{i}\rangle\big)+c_{0}\bigg\}

so that
\widehat{\rho}_{n}

\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
We write
\widehat{\rho}_{n}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{n},\Gamma_{n})
for
\mu_{n}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
\Gamma_{n}\in\mathbf{R}^{d\times d}
a positive symmetric matrix.

Now, for a Gaussian distribution
\rho=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Gamma)
, we have
f_{\rho}=f_{\mu}
(by linearity of
f_{\theta}
in
\theta
and since
\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\theta\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta)=\mu

R_{n}(\rho)=R_{n}(\mu).

In addition, since
R_{n}
is quadratic, we have

\langle\rho,R_{n}\rangle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big\{R_{n}(\mu)+\langle\nabla R_{n}(\mu),\theta-\mu\rangle+\langle\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}(\theta-\mu),\theta-\mu\rangle\big\}\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta)=R_{n}(\mu)+\tr(\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}\Gamma).

Finally, the Kullback-Leibler divergence writes:

\mathrm{KL}({\rho},{\pi})=\frac{1}{2}\big\{-\log\det(\gamma\Gamma)+\tr(\gamma\Gamma)-d+\gamma\lVert{\mu}\rVert^{2}\big\}.

Since
\widehat{\rho}_{n}
minimizes the
Q
-aggregation functional (with inverse temperature
\beta/2
) over Gaussian measures, by the previous computations and the definition (
14
) we have

\displaystyle(\mu_{n},\Gamma_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\mu,\Gamma}\Big\{(1-\alpha)\big[R_{n}(\mu)+\tr(\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}\Gamma)\big]+\alpha R_{n}(\mu)+

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2\cdot\beta/2}\big\{-\log\det(\gamma\Gamma)+\tr(\gamma\Gamma)-d+\gamma\lVert{\mu}\rVert^{2}\big\}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\mu,\Gamma}\Big\{R_{n}(\mu)+(1-\alpha)\tr(\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}\Gamma)+\beta^{-1}\big\{-\log\det(\gamma\Gamma)+\tr(\gamma\Gamma)+\gamma\lVert{\mu}\rVert^{2}\big\}\bigg\}.

Since the terms in
\mu
and
\Gamma
are decoupled, we have, recalling that
\lambda=\gamma/\beta

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\mu\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big\{R_{n}(\mu)+\lambda\lVert{\mu}\rVert^{2}\big\}=\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda}

(158)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\Gamma}\beta^{-1}\big\{-\log\det(\gamma\Gamma)+\tr\big[\big((1-\alpha)\beta\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}+\gamma I_{d}\big)\Gamma\big]\big\}

\displaystyle=\big((1-\alpha)\beta\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}+\gamma I_{d}\big)^{-1}

(159)

In particular, since

A.8.3
Proof of Theorem
4

Let
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})
be
n+1
i.i.d. samples from
P
.
For any regression procedure
f_{n}:(\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{R})^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbf{R}
and
1\leqslant i\leqslant n+1
, denote
\widehat{f}_{n}^{(i)}(x)=f_{n}\big((X_{j},Y_{j})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n+1,j\neq i},x\big)
; in particular, let
\widehat{f}_{n}=\widehat{f}_{n}^{(n+1)}
.
By exchangeability of the distribution of the sample
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[R(\widehat{f}_{n})]

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}}[(\widehat{f}_{n}^{(n+1)}(X_{n+1})-Y_{n+1})^{2}]={\mathbf{E}}\bigg[\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(\widehat{f}_{n}^{(i)}(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}\bigg].

In addition, define
R_{n+1}(f)=(n+1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(f(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}
for
f:\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbf{R}
, and
R_{n+1}(\theta)=R_{n+1}(f_{\theta})
for
\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
Letting
\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda,n+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}_{\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\{R_{n+1}(\theta)+\lambda\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big\{R(\theta)+\lambda\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\big\}

\displaystyle=R(\theta_{\lambda})+\lambda\lVert{\theta_{\lambda}}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}}\big[R_{n+1}(\theta_{\lambda})+\lambda\lVert{\theta_{\lambda}}\rVert^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\geqslant{\mathbf{E}}\big[R_{n+1}(\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda,n+1})+\lambda\lVert{\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda,n+1}}\rVert^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\geqslant{\mathbf{E}}\big[R_{n+1}(\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda,n+1})\big].

Combining the previous two inequalities gives

{\mathbf{E}}[R(\widehat{f}_{n})]-\inf_{\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big\{R(\theta)+\lambda\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\big\}\leqslant{\mathbf{E}}\bigg[\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\big\{(\widehat{f}_{n}^{(i)}(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}-(\langle\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda,n+1},X_{i}\rangle-Y_{i})^{2}\big\}\bigg].

(160)

Now, for
i=1,\dots,n+1
, denoting

h_{i}=\left\langle\left(\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{n+1}X_{j}X_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}+\lambda(n+1)I_{d}\right)^{-1}X_{i},X_{i}\right\rangle

and
\theta_{i}=\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{(i)}
, we have by Lemma
6
below (with
A=\sum_{j\neq i}X_{j}X_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}+\lambda(n+1)I_{d}
,
\theta=\theta_{i}
and
(x,y)=(X_{i},Y_{i})
) that

(\langle\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda,n+1},X_{i}\rangle-Y_{i})^{2}=(1-h_{i})^{2}(\langle\theta_{i},X_{i}\rangle-Y_{i})^{2}.

(161)

We now separately analyze the estimators
\widehat{f}_{\lambda},\widehat{g}_{\lambda,b}
and
\widehat{g}_{\lambda}
.

We start with
\widehat{f}_{\lambda}
which satisfies
\widehat{f}_{\lambda}^{(i)}(X_{i})=(1-h_{i})^{2}\langle\theta_{i},X_{i}\rangle
.
One therefore has, by (
161
) and using that
0\leqslant h_{i}\leqslant 1
,

\displaystyle(\widehat{f}_{n}^{(i)}(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}-(\langle\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda,n+1},X_{i}\rangle-Y_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=\big[(1-h_{i})^{2}\langle\theta_{i},X_{i}\rangle-Y_{i}\big]^{2}-(1-h_{i})^{2}\big[\langle\theta_{i},X_{i}\rangle-Y_{i}\big]^{2}

\displaystyle=-(1-h_{i})^{2}\big\{1-(1-h_{i})^{2}\big\}\langle\theta_{i},X_{i}\rangle^{2}+\big\{1-(1-h_{i})^{2}\big\}Y_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\big\{2h_{i}-h_{i}^{2}\big\}Y_{i}^{2}\leqslant 2b^{2}h_{i}.

Plugging this inequality into (
160
) gives:

{\mathbf{E}}[R(\widehat{f}_{\lambda})]-\inf_{\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big\{R(\theta)+\lambda\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\big\}\leqslant\frac{2b^{2}}{n+1}\,{\mathbf{E}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}h_{i}\bigg].

In addition, denoting
\widehat{\Sigma}_{n+1}=(n+1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}X_{i}X_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}h_{i}\bigg]

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\bigg\langle\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}X_{j}X_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}+\lambda(n+1)I_{d}\bigg)^{-1}X_{i},X_{i}\bigg\rangle\bigg]

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}}\,\tr\bigg\{\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}X_{j}X_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}+\lambda(n+1)I_{d}\bigg)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}X_{i}X_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}}\,\tr\big\{\big(\widehat{\Sigma}_{n+1}+\lambda I_{d}\big)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}_{n+1}\big\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\tr\big[(\Sigma+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma\big],

(162)

where the last step is by
convexity of
A\mapsto\tr(A^{-1})
over positive semi-definite matrices
[
15
, Lemma 2.7]
, together with
{\mathbf{E}}[\widehat{\Sigma}_{n+1}]=\Sigma
.
This implies the bound (
30
).

We now consider the truncated ridge estimator
\widehat{g}_{\lambda}
, such that
\widehat{g}_{\lambda}^{(i)}(X_{i})=\psi_{b}(\langle\theta_{i},X_{i}\rangle)=U_{i}\in[-b,b]

Note that since

\displaystyle(\widehat{g}_{\lambda,b}^{(i)}(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}-(\langle\widehat{\theta}_{\lambda,n+1},X_{i}\rangle-Y_{i})^{2}

as

Plugging this into the bound (
160

Under

Lemma 6
.

Let
A
be a symmetric positive
d\times d
matrix,
b\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
\theta=A^{-1}b
.
Let
(x,y)\in\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{R}
, and define
b^{\prime}=b+yx
,
A^{\prime}=A+xx^{\mathsf{T}}
and
\theta^{\prime}=A^{\prime-1}b^{\prime}
.
Define
h=\langle A^{\prime-1}x,x\rangle
.
Then, we have

\langle\theta^{\prime},x\rangle-y=(1-h)\big(\langle\theta,x\rangle-y\big).

(163)

Proof.

It follows from the Sherman-Morrison identity that

A^{\prime-1}x=\Big(A^{-1}-\frac{A^{-1}xx^{\mathsf{T}}A^{-1}}{1+\langle A^{-1}x,x\rangle}\Big)x=\frac{A^{-1}x}{1+\langle A^{-1}x,x\rangle},

(164)

so that, in particular,
h=\langle A^{\prime-1}x,x\rangle=\langle A^{-1}x,x\rangle/(1+\langle A^{-1}x,x\rangle)
(or alternatively
\langle A^{-1}x,x\rangle=h/(1-h)
), and thus (
164
) writes as
A^{\prime-1}x=(1-h)A^{-1}x
.
This implies that

\displaystyle\langle\theta^{\prime},x\rangle-y

\displaystyle=\langle A^{\prime-1}b^{\prime},x\rangle-y

\displaystyle=\langle A^{\prime-1}x,b+yx\rangle-y

\displaystyle=(1-h)\langle A^{-1}x,b+yx\rangle-y

\displaystyle=(1-h)\langle A^{-1}b,x\rangle+(1-h)\langle A^{-1}x,x\rangle y-y

\displaystyle=(1-h)(\langle\theta,x\rangle-y).

The claim follows.
∎
