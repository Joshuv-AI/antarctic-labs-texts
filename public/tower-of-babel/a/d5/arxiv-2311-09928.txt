Super-resolved Lasso
Clarice Poon, Gabriel Peyré

Abstract
Super-resolution of pointwise sources is of utmost importance in various areas of imaging sciences. Specific instances of this problem arise in single molecule fluorescence, spike sorting in neuroscience, astrophysical imaging, radar imaging, and nuclear resonance imaging. In all these applications, the Lasso method (also known as Basis Pursuit or ℓ 1 \ell^{1} -regularization) is the de facto baseline method for recovering sparse vectors from low-resolution measurements. This approach requires discretization of the domain, which leads to quantization artifacts and consequently, an overestimation of the number of sources. While grid-less methods, such as Prony-type methods or non-convex optimization over the source position, can mitigate this, the Lasso remains a strong baseline due to its versatility and simplicity. In this work, we introduce a simple extension of the Lasso, termed “super-resolved Lasso” (SR-Lasso). Inspired by the Continuous Basis Pursuit (C-BP) method, our approach introduces an extra parameter to account for the shift of the sources between grid locations. Our method is more comprehensive than C-BP, accommodating both arbitrary real-valued or complex-valued sources. Furthermore, it can be solved similarly to the Lasso as it boils down to solving a group-Lasso problem. A notable advantage of SR-Lasso is its theoretical properties, akin to grid-less methods. Given a separation condition on the sources and a restriction on the shift magnitude outside the grid, SR-Lasso precisely estimates the correct number of sources.



1
Introduction

Sparse-spike super-resolution refers to the problem of recovering point-wise sources from low-resolution linear measurements, which are essentially linear superpositions of patterns translated to the source locations. These measurements can for instance take the form of Fourier coefficients, convolutions, or Laplace transforms.
Typical examples of such an ill-posed inverse problems arose in single molecule fluorescence imaging
[
53
,
33
]
. For this type of imaging processes, the final high-resolution image (e.g., of a cell) is produced by combining multiple snapshots. Each snapshot consists of a sparse set of fluorescence molecules, which are treated as point-wise sources.
Other applications include astrophysics
[
51
]
(for estimating star locations), radar array imaging
[
38
]
, seismic imaging
[
37
]
(used to recover sharp localized transitions in the ground), Nuclear Magnetic Resonance spectroscopy
[
45
]
(for determining molecular structures), and intracellular neuron recording
[
40
]
(to sort spikes in time series).
To address these inverse problems, a common approach involves gridding the space and applying a sparse regularization principle, which promotes the appearance of spikes in the solution. The regularizer of choice is often the
\ell^{1}
norm, resulting in a convex optimization problem. Recently, there has been an inclination to eliminate the grid to achieve greater accuracy. However, this introduces computational challenges, given that the optimization problem becomes infinite-dimensional. This paper proposes a middle ground, where spikes between grid points are parameterized in a convex manner, aiming to capture the advantages of both approaches.

1.1
Previous Works

Lasso methods.

\ell^{1}
regularization, often referred to as the Lasso problem in statistics
[
59
]
or basis pursuit in signal processing
[
10
]
, has its roots in the seismic inversion community
[
11
]
. It serves as a simple but powerful convex regularizer inducing sparsity and can be readily applied to the super-resolution problem once the spatial domain is discretized.
Among the numerous extensions of the basic Lasso, the group Lasso
[
62
]
is of primary importance for this work. It enables the recovery of multiple channels in signals or images (e.g., in multi-spectral imaging) while enforcing the sparsity patterns of the different channels to coincide. Our SR-Lasso method necessitates the resolution of a group Lasso problem.
Although the main focus of this paper is not the development of new sparse optimization algorithms (as our method can employ any off-the-shelf state-of-the-art group Lasso solver), it is worth noting that there have been numerous improvements in state-of-the-art solvers. One straightforward yet effective method is the iterative soft thresholding algorithm
[
12
]
, rooted in non-smooth first-order optimization techniques. A comprehensive review can be found in
[
1
]
.
Greedy-type algorithms, which progressively increase the number of sources, are beneficial for highly sparse solutions. Simpler algorithms include homotopy-type methods or LARS, which track the regularization path
[
29
]
. More efficient solvers encompass safe screening rules and pruning techniques
[
46
,
44
]
.
Another category of solvers includes iterative least squares
[
13
]
. These can be enhanced using an Hadamard re-parameterization
[
34
]
. Such re-parameterization can further benefit from the VarPro marginalization technique
[
50
]
, which is the solver we employ in this paper.

Off the grid and BLASSO.

Recently, there has been a surge of interest in avoiding the explicit discretization of space, allowing for the optimization of source positions. This approach can be mathematically characterized as an infinite-dimensional convex problem over the space of measures
[
14
,
6
]
, commonly termed the “Beurling Lasso” (BLASSO). The thought after sparse solution should then be a sum of Dirac masses.
While this “off-the-grid” method is attractive in terms of precision, it presents computational challenges. Optimizing the source locations, which can be likened to the computation of an adaptive grid, is inherently non-convex, making it difficult.
A prominent category of optimization techniques for this problem draws from generalizations of the Frank-Wolfe optimization method applied over the space of measures
[
6
]
. These techniques often alternate between a convex optimization step on a (desirably small) grid and a non-convex optimization for new grid points
[
5
,
17
,
26
]
. In practical applications, they appear to be computationally superior to an a priori discretization of space. However, they are more complex to implement and lack robust theoretical backing due to the integration of non-convex optimization steps, despite enjoying favorable properties close to the minimizers
[
60
]
.

SOS, Prony and deep-learning methods.

Another class of techniques leverages sum-of-squares (SOS) relaxation to transform this infinite-dimensional optimization problem into a semi-definite program
[
14
,
8
]
. A significant limitation of these methods is that they are restricted to Fourier-type (translation invariant) operators and, more generally, to semi-algebraic (e.g., polynomial) operators
[
15
]
. Moreover, the resulting SDP problem’s dimensionality grows at least quadratically with the number of measured frequencies.
These SOS spectral methods bear a close resemblance to Prony-type methods. While they don’t minimize the
\ell^{1}
norm of the solution, they can also identify localized sources from Fourier frequencies. Notable methods in this category include MUSIC
[
55
,
48
,
42
]
, ESPRIT
[
52
]
, and matrix pencil
[
35
]
. Originally developed for signal processing, these methods can be extended to higher dimensions
[
39
]
. Let us also notice that their super-resolution properties as sources nearly collide can be precisely analyzed
[
3
,
41
]
.
A final category of methods, which also bypasses explicit spatial gridding, relies on deep networks to directly estimate source positions
[
4
]
. One challenge is the need for extensive pre-training using pairs of simulated ground truth and observations. Additionally, it is imperative to possess detailed knowledge of the signal generation process and the structure of the input sources. Such methods are gaining traction, especially in single-molecule microscopy, where in-depth understanding of the image formation process is accessible
[
56
,
9
,
36
,
47
]
.

Super-resolution theory.

A fundamental theoretical question is to determine the capability of these sparse estimation techniques to either exactly (in noise-free conditions) or approximately (in noisy conditions) recover source locations
[
19
]
. This necessitates constraints on the relative positioning of the sources. Indeed, closely positioned sources might be irretrievable, or the signal-to-noise ratio could explodes as the sources collapse.
Generic recovery conditions (not necessarily related to super-resolution problems) have been thoroughly examined for the Lasso technique
[
30
,
63
,
31
]
. These conditions have been expanded for the group Lasso
[
2
,
43
]
and other general sparsity-promoting regularizers
[
61
]
.
We should highlight that in Section
3
we propose a more detailed analysis of the group Lasso setting, which necessitates a milder injectivity condition.

Focusing on the specific instance of super-resolution with Fourier measurements (analogous to translation-invariant linear operators),
[
8
]
demonstrated that exact recovery is possible if sources are separated by a distance greater than a “Rayleigh limit”, which is proportional to the kernel’s width. This improves over previous works of
[
20
]
. Notably, this outcome is applicable “off-the-grid”, meaning it is relevant to the BLASSO problem.
This finding was further extended to accommodate noisy data, allowing for approximate recovery results
[
7
]
, as well as the exact support recovery (exact estimation of the number of spikes)
[
21
]
. it is also possible to incorporate more general operators (not strictly translation-invariant)
[
49
]
, and to under-sample measurements to implement compressed sensing
[
58
,
49
]
.

Discretization and continuous Basis Pursuit.

Although off-the-grid theoretical results are compelling, the prevalent approaches still involve discretizing the computational domain. This serves as an approximation to the original infinite-dimensional problem
[
57
]
. This approximation introduces discretization artifacts, which can be reduced using adaptive refinement strategies
[
27
]
. Often, these artifacts result in a single source being spread across multiple grid points
[
22
]
, hindering exact support recovery.
To somewhat mitigate these quantization errors, the Continuous Basis-Pursuit (C-BP), as detailed in
[
23
,
24
]
, introduces additional parameters to accommodate for source shifts outside the grid point locations.
A theoretical examination of this technique
[
22
]
reveals a more accurate source estimation. However, C-BP is not immune to quantization errors and does not guarantee recovery of the exact number of sources, even if they are distinctly separated. Our SR-Lasso approach, which draws significant inspiration from the C-BP procedure, exhibits superior support recovery properties.

1.2
Contributions

Our first major contribution is methodological: Section
2
introduces the Super-resolved Lasso (SR-Lasso), a new method capable of locating sources outside the grid using a Taylor expansion. Central to our approach is the strategy of coupling the source amplitude with the off-the-grid’s shift by imposing a shared support, which is achieved using a group Lasso regularization. This methods differs from the C-BP approach, which relies on a hard constraint. This enable the use of off-the-shelf group Lasso solvers, offers enhanced theoretical guarantees, and can cope with signed measure, unlike the C-BP which limits itself to positive measures.
Section
3
present a thorough sparsistency (i.e., exact support recovery) analysis of the group Lasso, which is of independent interest. The main result is Theorem
1
, which refines existing analyses like those in
[
2
,
43
]
by demanding a less stringent restricted injectivity condition (
IC
).
Section
4
instantiates in Theorem
2
these results in the case where this group Lasso problem is a discretization of some continuous (“off-the grid”) operator. The section exposes the relationship between kernel properties and the separation distance between spikes, which leads to sufficient conditions for exact support recovery.
Section
5
proposes a detailed analysis of the SR-Lasso method, focussing on translation-invariant kernels for clarity. By drawing from prior sections, Theorem
3
shows that – provided the sources are sufficiently apart and the off-the-grid shifts remain small enough – SR-Lasso can reliably determine the sources’ support. This is the first result of this kind, i.e. the first finite dimensional Lasso-type algorithm which can achieve a consistent support estimation.
Section
6
exemplify the usefulness of the SR-Lasso by contrasting it with Lasso and C-BP on synthetic examples inspired by applications in imaging sciences. Specifically, it underscores using several evaluation metrics the SR-Lasso’s capacity to consistently estimate the sought-after sources’ support.

2.1
BLASSO and Lasso

We consider linear inverse problems over the space of measures. The linear operator
\Phi
to invert maps a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
over a space of dimension
d
to measurements
\Phi\mu\in\mathcal{H}
where
\mathcal{H}
is some Hilbert space with norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}
. Under mild continuity condition, such an operator can be written in integral form as

\Phi\mu\coloneqq\int\varphi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

(1)

where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{H}
is a continuous function taking values in
\mathcal{H}
.
We assume that
\varphi
is normalized such that
\left\|\varphi(x)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1
for all
x
.
Typical examples includes Fourier measurement where
\varphi(x)=(e^{\mathrm{i}\langle x,\,\omega_{k}\rangle})_{k}
for some frequency set
(\omega_{k})_{k}
, (sampled) convolution, where
\varphi(x)=(k(x-x_{k}))_{k}
for some sample location
x_{k}
, and Laplace transform when
\varphi(x)=(e^{\langle x,\,\omega_{k}\rangle})_{k}
.

Given some measurements
y\in\mathcal{H}
, the BLASSO problem
[
14
]
aims at minimizing a weighted sum of a fidelity criterion using the Euclidean norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}
and a sparsity promoting penalty

\underset{\mu}{\min}\;\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|\Phi\mu-y\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+|\mu|(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(2)

The parameter
\lambda>0
should be tuned to the level of noise contaminating the measurements
y
.
Here
|\mu|(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the total mass of the absolute value of the measure, which is often called “total variation norm” in probability. If
\mu=\sum_{i}a_{j}\delta_{z_{i}}
is a discrete measure (weighted sum of Dirac masses), then

|\mu|(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\sum_{j}|a_{j}|=\left\|a\right\|_{1}

boils down to the
\ell^{1}
norm of the vector
a
.

Problem (
2
) is infinite dimensional, and we refer to Section
1.1
for a review of possible computational approaches. The simplest way to approximate it is to discretize the problem on a grid
X=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}
and impose that the measure is supported on
X
, so that
\mu=\sum_{i}a_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
. In this case, optimizing only on
a
replaces the infinite dimensional convex problem (
2
) by a standard finite dimensional Lasso problem

\underset{a\in\mathbb{R}_{n}}{\min}\;\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|\Phi_{X}a-y\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\left\|a\right\|_{1}

(3)

where the finite dimensional linear operator reads

\Phi_{X}a\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\varphi(x_{j})\in\mathcal{H}.

(4)

To improve the quality of the discretization of (

We consider a discrete measure

We denote

Choice of the parameter
\tau
.

The parameter
\tau
controls how far Dirac masses in the solution can “move” inside the grid.
The standard Lasso problem is obtained by setting
\tau=0

Our theoretical results will cover the case of

The normalization of
\Psi

In the special case where

In the case of non-translation invariant operators, the normalisation is not a constant and is important both in theory and practice to ensure a correct weighting between position and derivatives.
Note that in the multivariate setting, the normalisation in

2.3
Multivariate setting

For simplicity, we analyse the univariate case in this paper. However, the ideas also extend to the multivariate setting where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{H}
.
Define the metric
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\coloneqq\nabla\varphi(x)\nabla\varphi(x)^{*}
, the multi-dimensional SR-Lasso is then

\min_{a,b}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\Phi_{X}a+\Psi_{X}b-y\right\|^{2}+\lambda\sum_{j}\sqrt{a_{j}^{2}+\left\|b_{j}\right\|^{2}}

(10)

where
a=(a_{j})_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
b=(b_{j})_{j=1}^{n}\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{n}
,

\Phi_{X}a=\sum_{j}a_{j}\varphi(x_{j})\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi_{X}b=\sum_{j}\langle\tau\odot b_{j},\,\mathfrak{g}_{x}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\varphi(x_{j})\rangle.

(11)

for some
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
.
The use of the metric
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
is for normalisation purposes and the
\tau

Note that for
K(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\varphi(x),\,\varphi(x^{\prime})\rangle
,

K(x,x)=1\implies-\partial_{1}^{2}K(x,x)=\partial_{1}\partial_{2}K(x,x)=\nabla\varphi(x)\nabla\varphi(x)^{*}=\mathfrak{g}_{x}.

Note that in the univariate setting, one recovers the normalization (

3.1
Group Lasso

We consider a general group Lasso problem such as (

), with possibly arbitrary group structures.
Let
\Gamma:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathcal{H}
be a linear operator defined in the following way: Let
z=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
where

\Gamma z\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{i}z_{i}.

Similarly to (

\left\|z\right\|_{1,2}\coloneqq\sum_{i}\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}

\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lambda\left\|z\right\|_{1,2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\Gamma z-y\right\|^{2}.

Define

3.2
Refined Irrepresentability Condition (IC)

Given some (unknown)
z^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the goal is to assess the capability of (
12
) to recover
z^{*}
approximately given noisy measurements

y=y^{*}+w\in\mathcal{H}\quad\text{where}\quad y^{*}\coloneqq\Gamma z^{*}.

In particular, when
w
is small enough, one wishes to recover the correct group support
\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\left\{i\;;\;\left\|z^{*}_{i}\right\|\neq 0\right\}
of
z^{*}
.
It is well known in the literature on Lasso
[
30
]
and group Lasso problems
[
2
,
43
]
that this “sparsistency” property is governed by a so-called “Irrepresentability Condition” (IC) which somehow imposes some incoherence of
\Gamma
when operating on vectors supported on
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{I}^{c}

\left\|\Gamma_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}^{*}p_{0}\right\|_{\infty,2}<1\quad\text{and}\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{I},\;\Gamma_{i}^{*}p_{0}=\sign(z^{*})_{i}\quad\text{where}\quad p_{0}\coloneqq\Gamma_{\mathcal{I}}(\Gamma_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}\Gamma_{\mathcal{I}})^{\dagger}\sign(z^{*}),

(IC)

where we denoted
\left\|(z_{i})_{i}\right\|_{\infty,2}\coloneqq\max_{i}\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}
.
In addition to this IC condition, support stability analysis of the Lasso necessitate that the operator
\Gamma_{\mathcal{I}}
is injective. Existing analysis of the group Lasso assumes the same hypothesis, but as we show below, as soon as groups have size larger than one (i.e. if one is not simply in the Lasso setup), then this is not sharp.
We make instead the following weaker nullspace (N) assumptions:

\ker(\Gamma_{\mathcal{I}})\cap\ker(Q_{z^{*}}^{\perp})=\{0\}.

(N)

This weaker condition was also studied in
[
25
]
as a necessary condition for establishing uniqueness of minimizers in the noiseless setting, we complement their results in Theorem
1
by establishing sparsistency under low noise.

3.3

We now show that, in the small noise regime, under (N) and (IC), the solution of the group lasso share the same support as

Proposition 1
.

Assume that conditions (N) and (IC) holds.
For all

Comparison with Lasso and C-BP

6.1
Relationship to continuous basis pursuit

Continuous basis pursuit (C-BP)
[
23
,
24
]
is an off-the-grid approach to recovering

\min_{(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-\Phi_{X}a-\Phi_{X}^{\prime}b\right\|^{2}+\lambda\left\|a\right\|_{1}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\left\lvert b\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{h}{2}a,

where
h
is the grid size.
While our SR-Lasso problem is directly formulated as a group-Lasso problem,
this C-BP can be re-formulated as a Lasso problem with positivity constraint

\min_{(r,l)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{n}}\lambda\left\|r\right\|_{1}+\lambda\left\|l\right\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-\mathcal{A}_{h}\begin{pmatrix}r\\
l\end{pmatrix}\right\|^{2}

(33)

after the change of variable
a_{i}=r_{i}+l_{i}
and
b_{i}=\frac{h}{2}r_{i}-\frac{h}{2}l_{i}
, and

\mathcal{A}_{h}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A+\frac{h}{2}B&A-\frac{h}{2}B\end{pmatrix}.

Following
[
22
]
, it is possible to study the support stability of this problem by computing the standard dual certificate of the Lasso problem, which is defined as
\eta_{V}\coloneqq\Phi^{*}\Gamma_{x}^{\dagger,*}\binom{1_{n}}{0}
.
In
[
22
]
, it was shown that a sufficient condition (but this is in fact necessary by standard Lasso arguments) for support stable recovery of the measure
\sum_{i\in J}\alpha_{i}\delta_{x_{i}+s_{i}}
where
s_{i}
is some sufficiently small shift and
\left\lvert J\right\rvert=n
, is that

\max_{i\in J^{c}}\eta_{V}(x_{i})\pm\frac{h}{2}\eta_{V}^{\prime}(x_{i})<1.

(IC
h
)

By construction,
\eta_{V}(x_{i})=1
and
\eta_{V}^{\prime}(x_{i})=0
for
i\in J
, so that condition (
ICh
) is in general false when
h
is sufficiently small, since by Taylor expansion, for

\displaystyle\eta_{V}(x_{i}+h)-\frac{h}{2}\eta_{V}^{\prime}(x_{i}+h)=1+\frac{h^{3}}{12}\eta_{V}^{\prime\prime\prime}(x_{i})+\mathcal{O}(h^{4}),

and

\displaystyle\eta_{V}(x_{i}-h)+\frac{h}{2}\eta_{V}^{\prime}(x_{i}+h)=1-\frac{h^{3}}{12}\eta_{V}^{\prime\prime\prime}(x_{i})+\mathcal{O}(h^{4}).

If

Hence, there are two main advantages to SR-Lasso, compared to C-BP

1.

Generality:
SR-Lasso can handle

2.

Sparsistency:
One of the highlights of Theorem

On the other hand, in the case of continuous basis pursuit, even if the true signal lies on the grid, the problem is unstable when the grid becomes too fine since the condition (

6.2
Numerical Comparison

The previous section has demonstrated that SR-Lasso possesses better theoretical sparsity properties compared to C-BP (and of course, to the basic Lasso as well). We now illustrate, using synthetic numerical simulations, that this also translates into practical gains in terms of support estimation.

Evaluation metric.

The problem of source position estimation is infinite-dimensional (inverting the operator
\Phi
in (
1
)), rendering usual finite-dimensional performance criteria (such as the
\ell^{2}
or
\ell^{\infty}
norms on discretized recovered coefficients) less meaningful.
Each field of application typically develops its own measures of performance, often intertwining errors in source amplitude and position while penalizing both false positives and negatives in a somewhat ad-hoc manner. For an in-depth discussion in the context of single molecule localization for fluorescence imaging, see
[
54
]
.
A more mathematically rigorous approach to assess recovery quality is to view the problem as one of measure estimation, recovering
\mu_{0}
from a noisy observation
y\approx\Phi\mu_{0}
. In our case, to concentrate on the intrinsic capabilities of the methods for estimating the source support despite discretization errors, we do not add measurement noise to the observations, so that
y=\Phi\mu_{0}
. In light of this measure estimation problem, it makes sense to compute the estimation error using some norm or distance between measures.
For simplicity, we employ here the Maximum Mean Discrepancies (MMD) norms
[
32
]
, which are Hilbert kernelized norms over the space of measures. An alternative – and arguably more complex – approach is to use the Optimal Transport distance, as advocated in
[
16
,
18
]
.

For some positive kernel
k(x,y)
, its associated MMD norm of a complex-valued measure
\xi
is defined as

\|\xi\|_{k}^{2}:=\int k(x,y)\text{d}\xi(x)\bar{\xi}(y).

Provided that
k
is universal, this defines a norm, which metrizes the weak
∗
convergence on the space of measure, thus offering a way to asses the estimation error on both amplitude and position of the sources.
We consider here the Laplace kernel
k(x,y)=\exp(-\left\|x-y\right\|)
, which in practice has better behavior that the more usual Gaussian kernel (because it has a slower spacial decay), and define our error metric as

D(\mu,\nu)\triangleq\|\mu-\nu\|_{k}^{2}.

For all measures
\mu,\nu
, we have
D(\mu,\nu)\geqslant 0
and
D(\mu,\nu)=0
if and onlly if
\mu=\nu
.
An interesting connexion with the initial estimation problem is that, assuming
y=\Phi(\mu_{0})
, then the standard Euclidean loss minimized in Lasso-type problems such as (
2
) can be re-written as a MMD norm since

\left\|\Phi\mu-y\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\|\mu-\mu_{0}\|_{k_{0}}^{2}\quad\text{where}\quad k_{0}(x,x^{\prime})\triangleq\varphi(x)^{\top}\varphi(y).

Since the Laplace kernel
k
that we use for error assessment typically has a much slower Fourier decay than the measurement kernel
k_{0}
(which, in our experiments, is either an ideal low-pass filter or a Gaussian kernel), the discrepancy
D
evaluates the super-resolution capability of the method, i.e., its ability to recover “higher order moments”.

Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed a new source estimation method that relies on solving a finite-dimensional convex optimization problem. This approach allows bypassing the use of infinite-dimensional solvers by depending on a standard group lasso problem. In contrast to existing finite-dimensional alternatives with similar computational costs (such as Lasso or C-BP), it demonstrates superior sparsistency properties, which, in practice, translates into sharper source localization.

Proof of Lemma
6
.

Denote
\Upsilon_{\mathcal{I}}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{\top}&D\end{pmatrix}
,
where

A=\left(\Phi_{X}^{*}\Phi_{X}\right)_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}},\quad B\coloneqq\left(\tau\Phi_{X}^{*}\Psi_{X}\right)_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}},\quad D\coloneqq\tau^{2}\left(\Psi_{X}^{*}\Psi_{X}\right)_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}}.

then

v=(D^{-1}+D^{-1}B^{\top}S^{-1}BD^{-1})s_{b}-D^{-1}B^{\top}S^{-1}s_{a}\quad\text{and}\quad u=S^{-1}s_{a}-S^{-1}BD^{-1}s_{b}.

where
S\coloneqq\left(A-BD^{-1}B^{\top}\right)

We will make use of the following operator bounds:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{Id}-A\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\{i\}}\left\lvert\kappa(x_{i}-x_{j})\right\rvert\leqslant\delta_{\min},

\left\|S^{-1}\right\|\leqslant\frac{1-\delta_{\min}}{1-2\delta_{\min}}
