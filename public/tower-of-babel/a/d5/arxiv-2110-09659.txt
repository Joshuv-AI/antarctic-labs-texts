Abstract
. A pseudoisotopy of M M is a diffeomorphism of M × I M\times I which is the identity on M × 0 M\times 0 . We give an explicit construction of pseudoisotopies of 4-manifolds which realize certain elements of the “second obstruction to pseudoisotopy”. The next paper will give precise statements of our results for 5-manifolds which had been announced long ago.

Introduction

Recall that, for any smooth compact manifold
M
, a
pseudoisotopy
of
M
is a diffeomorphism of
M\times I
which is the identity on
M\times 0\cup\partial M\times I
. The restriction of this diffeomorphism to
M\times 1
gives a diffeomorphism of
M
which is the identity on
\partial M
. This diffeomorphism is said to be
pseudoisotopic
to the identity diffeomorphism of
M
rel
\partial M
. Let
{\mathcal{C}}(M)
be the group of pseudoisotopies of
M
. Thus

{\mathcal{C}}(M)=Dif\!f(M\times I\,rel\,M\times 0\cup\partial M\times I).

We give
{\mathcal{C}}(M)
a suitable topology so that, up to canonical homotopy, a path in
{\mathcal{C}}(M)
is given by a diffeomorphism of
M\times I\times I
which preserves the last coordinate. (Section 1.) Since
{\mathcal{C}}(M)
is a topological group,
\pi_{0}{\mathcal{C}}(M)
will be a group.

For
M
simply connected with
\dim M\geq 5
, Jean Cerf
[
1
]
showed that
{\mathcal{C}}(M)
is connected. For
\dim M\geq 6
, Hatcher and Wagoner
[
2
]
showed that there is an exact sequence

(0.1)

Wh_{1}^{+}(\pi_{1}M;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\oplus\pi_{2}M)\to\pi_{0}{\mathcal{C}}(M)\to Wh_{2}(\pi)\to 0.

When
\dim M\leq 5
, we apply a “suspension functor”
\sigma:{\mathcal{C}}(M)\to{\mathcal{C}}(M\times I)
several times to get to the
stable pseudoisotopy space

{\mathcal{P}}(M)=\colim{\mathcal{C}}(M\times I^{N})

[
6
]
which should be used instead of
{\mathcal{C}}(M)
in the above exact sequence.

In
[
5
]
,
[
4
]
and
[
3
]
the sequence (
0.1
) was extended to an exact sequence

(0.2)

K_{3}{\mathbb{Z}}[\pi_{1}M]\xrightarrow{\chi}Wh_{1}^{+}(\pi_{1}M;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\oplus\pi_{2}M)\xrightarrow{\theta}\pi_{0}{\mathcal{P}}(M)\to Wh_{2}(\pi)\to 0.

The question arises: Which elements of
\pi_{0}{\mathcal{P}}(M)
are realized when
\dim M<6
, i.e., which elements of
\pi_{0}{\mathcal{P}}(M)
lie in the image of the stabilization map
\pi_{0}{\mathcal{C}}(M)\to\pi_{0}{\mathcal{P}}(M)
? This question was answered in dimension 5 by the author in a preliminary version of
[
7
]
and partially answered in the topological category in dimension 4 by Kwasik in
[
8
]
using the techniques of
[
2
]
and one idea from
[
7
]
. In both
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
we need an assumption which trivializes the image of
\chi
in (

).

This series of papers discusses this question for
\dim M=4,5
. In the current paper we show that, for certain
4
-dimensional manifolds
M
, some elements of the second obstruction group

Wh_{1}^{+}(\pi_{1}M;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\oplus\pi_{2}M)=Wh_{1}^{+}(\pi_{1}M;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\oplus Wh_{1}^{+}(\pi_{1}M;\pi_{2}M)

can be realized by pseudoisotopies of

. Given a group

1.
Definition of second obstruction to pseudoisotopy

We will review the basic theory, due to Cerf
[
1
]
, relating diffeomorphisms of
M\times I
and Morse theory. Then we focus on the second obstruction, due to Hatcher and Wagoner
[
2
]
. We assume in this section that
M
is a compact connected smooth manifold of dimension
n\geq 4
.

1.1.
Basic setup

Let
{\mathcal{F}}(M)
be the space of all smooth functions
f:M\times I\to I
so that
f
is equal to projection to
I
in some neighborhood of

\partial(M\times I)=\partial M\times I\cup M\times 0\cup M\times 1.

Then
{\mathcal{F}}(M)
is clearly contractible:
{\mathcal{F}}(M)\simeq\ast
. Let
{\mathcal{E}}(M)
be the space of all
f\in{\mathcal{F}}(M)
with no singularities. Then, Cerf observed that

{\mathcal{E}}(M)\simeq{\mathcal{C}}(M)

where the homotopy equivalence
{\mathcal{C}}(M)\to{\mathcal{E}}(M)
is given by sending
g:M\times I\to M\times I
to
\pi_{I}\circ g:M\times I\to I
where
\pi_{I}:M\times I\to I
is the projection map. Given
f\in{\mathcal{E}}(M)
, the corresponding element of
{\mathcal{C}}(M)
is constructed uniquely up to contractible choice by integrating the gradient of
f
. As a consequence

\pi_{0}{\mathcal{C}}(M)\cong\pi_{1}({\mathcal{F}}(M),{\mathcal{E}}(M)).

For clarity we repeat: This bijection sends the isotopy class of
g\in{\mathcal{C}}(M)
to the homotopy class of the 1-parameter family
f_{t}
if and only if
f_{1}=\pi_{I}\circ g
(and
f_{0}=\pi_{I}
).

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
(f_{t}^{a}),(f_{t}^{b})
be elements of
\pi_{1}({\mathcal{F}}(M),{\mathcal{E}}(M))
corresponding to
g^{a},g^{b}\in\pi_{0}{\mathcal{C}}(M)
. Thus
f_{1}^{a}=\pi_{I}\circ g^{a}
,
f_{1}^{b}=\pi_{I}\circ g^{b}
. Then the product in
\pi_{0}{\mathcal{C}}(M)
, given by composition:
g^{a}\circ g^{b}
is
f_{t}^{ab}
given by putting
f_{t}^{b}
to the left of
f_{t}^{a}
:

f_{t}^{ab}=\begin{cases}f_{2t}^{b}&\text{if }t\leq\frac{1}{2}\\
f_{2t-1}^{a}\circ g^{b}&\text{if }t>\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

Proof.

First note that the family of function
f_{t}^{ab}
lies in
\pi_{1}({\mathcal{F}}(M),{\mathcal{E}}(M))
since, at
t=0
,
f_{0}^{b}=\pi_{I}
and, at
t=\frac{1}{2}
,
f_{1}^{b}=\pi_{I}\circ g^{b}=f_{0}^{a}\circ g^{b}
. It is also easy to see that
f^{ab}
corresponds to
g^{a}g^{b}
since
f_{1}^{ab}=f_{1}^{a}\circ g^{b}=\pi_{I}\circ g^{a}\circ g^{b}
.
∎

Corollary 1.2
.

The inverse of
(f_{t})\in\pi_{1}({\mathcal{F}}(M),{\mathcal{E}}(M))
is the 1-parameter family

f_{t}^{\prime}=f_{1-t}\circ g^{-1}:M\times I\to I

where
g\in{\mathcal{C}}(M)
is a diffeomorphism of
M\times I
whose composition with projection
\pi_{I}:M\times I\to I
is
f_{1}\in{\mathcal{E}}(M)
(so that
f_{0}^{\prime}\circ g=f_{1}=\pi_{I}\circ g
, making
f_{0}^{\prime}=\pi_{I}
.)

Proof.

Let
f^{a}_{t}=f_{t}^{\prime}
and
f^{b}_{t}=f_{t}
. By Proposition
1.1
, the product
f^{ab}=f^{a}f^{b}
at
t=1
is

f_{1}^{ab}=f_{1}^{a}\circ g^{b}=f^{b}_{0}\circ(g^{b})^{-1}\circ g^{b}=f_{0}^{b}=\pi_{I}.

This corresponds to the identity in
\pi_{0}{\mathcal{C}}(M)
. So,
f^{a}=(f_{t}^{\prime})
is the inverse of
f^{b}=(f_{t})
.
∎

Here is an alternate version of composition in
\pi_{0}{\mathcal{C}}(M)
in terms of functions.

Proposition 1.3
.

The group structure (composition) on
\pi_{0}{\mathcal{C}}(M)
corresponds to stacking of functions
f_{a},f_{b}:M\times I\to I
in
{\mathcal{E}}(M)
given by “putting
f_{b}
on top of
f_{a}
”:

(f_{a}\bullet f_{b})(x,t)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}f_{a}(x,2t)&\text{if }t\leq\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}f_{b}(x,2t-1)&\text{if }t>\frac{1}{2}\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
g_{a},g_{b}\in{\mathcal{C}}(M)
be given by
g_{a}(x,t)=(h_{a}(x,t),f_{a}(x,t))
and
g_{b}=(h_{b},f_{b})
. Then
g_{a},g_{b}
are isotopic to
g_{a}^{\prime},g_{b}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}(M)
given by “pushing
g_{a}
down and
g_{b}
up”:

g_{a}^{\prime}(x,t)=\begin{cases}(h_{a}(x,2t),\frac{1}{2}f_{a}(x,2t))&\text{if }t\leq\frac{1}{2}\\
(r(x),t)&\text{if }t>\frac{1}{2}\end{cases}

where
r\in Dif\!f(M)
is the restriction of
g_{a}
to
M\times 1
, i.e.,
g_{a}(x,1)=(r(x),1)
and

g_{b}^{\prime}(x,t)=\begin{cases}(x,t)&\text{if }t\leq\frac{1}{2}\\
(h_{b}(x,2t-1),\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}f_{b}(x,2t-1))&\text{if }t>\frac{1}{2}\end{cases}

Then
g_{a}\circ g_{b}
is isotopic to
g_{a}^{\prime}\circ g_{b}^{\prime}
given by

(g_{a}^{\prime}\circ g_{b}^{\prime})(x,t)=\begin{cases}(h_{a}(x,2t),\frac{1}{2}f_{a}(x,2t))&\text{if }t\leq\frac{1}{2}\\
(rh_{b}(x,2t-1),\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}f_{b}(x,2t-1))&\text{if }t>\frac{1}{2}\end{cases}

Taking the second coordinate gives
f_{a}\bullet f_{b}
.
∎

The group structure on
\pi_{1}({\mathcal{F}}(M),{\mathcal{E}}(M))
is also given by stacking functions.

The graphic

1.3.
Second obstruction

There are two steps in the process of simplification of the singular set
\Sigma(f)
of
f
. Hatcher and Wagoner
[
2
]
showed that, for
\dim M\geq 6
, there is a surjective homomorphism

p:\pi_{0}{\mathcal{C}}(M)\to Wh_{2}(\pi_{1}M)

where, for any group
\pi

is a quotient of
K_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}[\pi])

They called this invariant the “first obstruction to pseudo-isotopy”.

Hatcher and Wagoner also showed that the kernel of the map

is generated by “lens-shaped models” (Definition
1.4

k,k+1
where
k=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
is the greatest integer
\leq\frac{n}{2}
where
n=\dim M

To define these invariants, we will add extra structure, called “markings”, to these families of functions, define invariants on the marked lens-shaped models, then determine how these invariants change when we change the markings.

1.4.
Computation of second obstruction

To compute
\pi_{0}{\mathcal{D}}_{0}(M)
, we need the following. For any group
\pi
and left
\pi
-module
A
, we consider
A[\pi]:=A\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}[\pi]
, the direct sum of copies of
A
, one for each element of
\pi
. This is a
\pi
-module with the diagonal action of
\pi
where
\pi
acts on
{\mathbb{Z}}[\pi]
by conjugation:

(1.1)

\sigma\left(\sum a_{i}\otimes g_{i}\right)=\sum\sigma a_{i}\otimes\sigma g_{i}\sigma^{-1}.

Each conjugacy class in
\pi
gives a submodule of
A[\pi]
, namely the sum of all
A\otimes\tau
indexed by the conjugates
\tau
of some fixed
\sigma\in\pi
. Let

be the submodule indexed by the identity

. We consider the quotient module
A[\pi]/A[e]
.

Definition 1.7
.

We define

to be the additive group of coinvariants of the action of
\pi
on

Wh_{1}^{+}(\pi;A):=(A[\pi]/A[e])_{\pi}.

When the action of

on

is trivial, this is the direct sum of copies of

, one for every nonidentity conjugacy class in
\pi
. More generally

Wh_{1}^{+}(\pi;A)\cong\bigoplus A_{C(\sigma_{\alpha})}

This is the direct sum over all nonidentity conjugacy classes in
\pi
of the coinvariants of the action of
C(\sigma_{\alpha})
on
A
where
C(\sigma_{\alpha})
is the centralizer of a representative
\sigma_{\alpha}
of the conjugacy class.

The main example is
\pi=\pi_{1}M
and

. For example, if
\pi
is finite with trivial action on

then

Wh_{1}^{+}(\pi_{1}M;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\oplus\pi_{2}M)=\bigoplus_{c-1}\ ({\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\oplus\pi_{2}M)

where

is the number of conjugacy classes of elements of

.

Then the theorem of Hatcher and Wagoner can be restated as follows.

Theorem 1.8
.

[
2
]

For

we have an isomorphism of groups:

(1.2)

\lambda_{0}:\pi_{0}{\mathcal{D}}_{0}(M)\cong({\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\oplus\pi_{2}M)\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}[\pi_{1}M]/{\mathbb{Z}}[e]=({\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\oplus\pi_{2}M)[\pi_{1}M]/({\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\oplus\pi_{2}M)[e].

Furthermore, this isomorphism is equivariant with respect to the action of
\pi_{1}M\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
given by changing the markings on
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}(M)
and with the diagonal action of

\pi_{0}{\mathcal{D}}_{0}(M)_{\pi_{1}M}=Wh_{1}^{+}(\pi_{1}M;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\oplus\pi_{2}M)

Wh_{1}^{+}(\pi_{1}M;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\oplus\pi_{2}M)\xrightarrow{\overline{\theta}_{0}}\pi_{0}{\mathcal{C}}(M)\to Wh_{2}(\pi_{1}M)\to 0

where

The idea is that, by Morse theory, a 1-parameter family of functions gives a family of chain complexes. In this case the chain complexes are acyclic. Hatcher and Wagoner show that this family can be deformed until all critical points are in two indices (the “Two-index Theorem”). This gives a family of invertible matrices. There is a K-theoretic obstruction to simplifying this family into the constant family of an
m\times m
identity matrix. The resulting graphic is a disjoint union of

\pi_{0}{\mathcal{D}}_{0}(M)

1.6.
Involution

We also have a very useful involution
\varepsilon

\varepsilon f(x,t)=1-f(x,1-t).

This is projection to the second coordinate of the function

\varepsilon g(x,t)=(r^{-1}h(x,1-t),1-f(x,1-t))

. Since
\varepsilon
switches the stacking direction for functions (Proposition

\varepsilon(f\bullet f^{\prime})=\varepsilon(f^{\prime})\bullet\varepsilon(f).

The involution
\varepsilon
turns the graphic of a function upside-down. Also, it changes the index

of a Morse critical point

to index

(and the critical point moves to

to
