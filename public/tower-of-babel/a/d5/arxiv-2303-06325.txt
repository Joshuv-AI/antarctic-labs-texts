Dynamics of the infinite discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equation
Aleksis Vuoksenmaa

Abstract
The discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equation on ℤ d {\mathbb{Z}}^{d} , d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 is an example of a dispersive nonlinear wave system. Being a Hamiltonian system that conserves also the ℓ 2 ​ ( ℤ d ) \ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d}) -norm, the well-posedness of the corresponding Cauchy problem follows for square-summable initial data. In this paper, we prove that the well-posedness continues to hold for much less regular initial data, namely anything that has at most a certain power law growth far away from the origin. The growth condition is loose enough to guarantee that, at least in dimension d = 1 d=1 , initial data sampled from any reasonable equilibrium distribution of the defocusing DNLS satisfies it almost surely.



Keywords:
Discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equation, Nonlinear Schrödinger equation, Invariant measures

Introduction

The discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equation (DNLS) is a nonlinear differential equation that describes the evolution of a complex valued field

1

By field, we mean a function
\psi\colon X\to Y
from a space of sites
X
to a space of values (here

). This can be also conceptualized as a sequence indexed by

, i.e.
\psi\in Y^{X}
.

\psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
in a
d

t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, each lattice site

is associated with a value
\psi_{t}(x)
, and the time evolution of
t\mapsto\psi_{t}(x)
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm i}\derivative{t}\psi_{t}(x)=\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\alpha(x-y)\psi_{t}(y)+\lambda\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|^{2}\psi_{t}(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

(1.1)

It is instructive to compare this with the more well-known continuous (cubic) nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS), a partial differential equation where the time evolution is given by

\displaystyle{\rm i}\partial_{t}\psi(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\Delta\psi(t,x)+\lambda\left|\psi(t,x)\right|^{2}\psi(t,x),

(1.2)

with
\psi_{t}\colon{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
or
\psi_{t}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
. This equation has become an important example of dispersive equation, and it arises in a variety of different contexts, including as a description of the evolution of a condensate
[
8
,
10
]
.

One way to see the discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equation is as a finite grid approximation of the continuum nonlinear Schrödinger equation (
1.2
). As opposed to the continuous model, the discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equation (
1.1
) is an infinite system of differential equations, where the evolving variables are coupled to each others by a function
\alpha\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, which is called the hopping potential. The hopping potential plays the role of the Laplace operator in the discrete setting. In particular, if we set

\displaystyle\alpha(y)=\left({\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\left|y\right|_{\infty}=0\}}-\frac{1}{2d}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\left|y\right|_{\infty}=1\}}\right)

(1.3)

which is the standard discrete Laplacian. In this article, we allow for more general hopping potentials. We assume the hopping potential
\alpha
to have a finite support and to be symmetric.

While the hopping potential couples the time-evolution of the field at a given site to the value of the field at nearby sites, (
1.1
) also contains an onsite nonlinearity, whose strength is determined by the parameter
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
. If
\lambda<0
, the equations (
1.1
) with
\alpha
given by (
1.3
) and its continuum counterpart (
1.2
) are known as the
focusing
DNLS and NLS, respectively. If
\lambda>0
, these are known as the
defocusing
DNLS and NLS. As was already noticed in the pioneering work of Lebowitz, Rose, and Speer
[
13
]
, the behaviour of the equilibrium states of the NLS (both on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
) depends drastically on the sign of
\lambda
– see
[
3
,
2
,
1
]
for some results along these lines. On the discrete side,
[
6
]
studied the equilibrium states of the focusing DNLS.

For the deterministic results in this article, the sign and magnitude of the nonlinearity factor
\lambda\neq 0
will not play any role. Both the sign and the magnitude will, however, have a big effect on other aspects of the solutions. Since equilibrium measures are more readily defined for the defocusing DNLS, in the probabilistic statements in Section
7
we will assume
\lambda
to be strictly positive.

Equation (
1.1
) also acts as a possible starting point for the derivation of Boltzmann-like equations for wave systems. If the deterministic evolution prescribed by (
1.1
) is coupled with random initial data, then the time-evolved field
\psi_{t}
becomes a random field. It is conjectured that the correlations of this random field satisfy a Boltzmann-like equation in the limit
\lambda\to 0
,
\tau\sim\lambda^{-2}t
, where
\tau
is the mesoscopic time scale, obtained by speeding up the microscopic dynamics by a factor of
\lambda^{-2}
. Partial progress towards this result was achieved by Lukkarinen and Spohn in
[
14
]
, where the initial data is assumed to follow and equilibrium distribution. In that article, rather than studying directly the correlation structure of (
1.1
), the problem is first turned into a finite version:

\displaystyle{\rm i}\derivative{t}\psi_{t}(x)=\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}\alpha_{L}(x-y)\psi_{t}(y)+\lambda\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|^{2}\psi_{t}(x),\quad x\in\Lambda_{L},

(1.4)

where
\Lambda_{L}
is a finite periodic box. After this, the behaviour of the
L
-dependent time-correlation structure can be studied for large
L
or in the limit
L\to\infty
without appealing to the full dynamics given by (
1.1
). In any case, (
1.1
) and its finite version (
1.4
) acts as fruitful models for studying the derivation of wave kinetic equations, and understanding the well-posedness of (
1.1
) and its connection to the approximation (
1.4
) will be of use in trying to connect the approach of
[
14
]
to the more direct approach, where the initial values are sampled from an infinite dimensional distribution.

The well-posedness for (
1.1
) is known for
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
initial data, see
[
9
]
, which covers also fractional versions of the discrete Laplacian. In dimension
d=1
and with the standard Laplacean, the existence and uniqueness of solutions with

-norm, were proven in

for (

\psi_{0}\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
(and hence that of
\psi_{0}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
) zero. An illustrative example is to consider a Gaussian process indexed by
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with a translation invariant correlation structure, or any reasonable equilibrium measure of the system.

For this reason, the initial data for which the existence and uniqueness of the dynamics given by (
1.1
) holds needs additional work. If the initial data grows fast enough as
\left|x\right|\to\infty
, then at least uniqueness might be lost. In between these too quickly growing classes of initial data and the
\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
initial data, there are hopefully classes of initial data where the problem has a unique solution, and the solution of (
1.1
) is well-approximated by the finite box solutions (
1.4
) as

. Showing this is the main objective of the paper.

Techniques for studying the dynamics of such Hamiltonian systems on a lattice go back to the work of Lanford et al.
[
12
]
, where results concerning the dynamics of a family of anharmonic systems are proved. These tools may be adapted to our work with some modifications – in particular the proofs of existence and uniqueness of the dynamics, as well as showing that sets of good initial data have full probability with respect to certain measures – relies heavily on their proof strategy. On the other hand, in
[
5
,
4
]
the propagation of perturbations in anharmonic systems is studied, and it is here that we find techniques that can be adapted in order to show that the infinite box dynamics (
1.1
) is well approximated by (
1.4
).

As we already noted, we are interested in initial data that is typically not in
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
. The energy of the initial data will also typically be infinite. This has the upshot that we cannot use the standard Hamitonian system techniques to show that solutions exist and are uniquely defined. Instead, we have to formulate a local notion of the conservation laws, which can be shown to hold. The novel insight in the present paper is to use cancellations arising from symmetries of the free part of the Hamiltonian to control the time evolution of
local

\ell^{2}
-densities. This provides a way of showing that suitable weighted supremum norms propagate in the time-evolution.

Finally, it should be noted that there is an interesting – although still not completely understood – connection between between the DNLS and the NLS. Starting from (
1.1
) (DNLS), (
1.2
) (NLS) is formally attainable by taking a continuum limit, where the lattice spacing goes to zero and the graph laplacian is blown up to yield the standard laplacian in the limit. Proving this remains a formidable problem. This was done for the one dimensional DNLS as well as for long range hopping potentials in
[
11
]
, and results along the same line with stronger notion of convergence were obtained in
[
9
]
. Such results are very interesting, but in this paper nothing to that direction is proven, as we hold the lattice spacing constant (corresponding to UV cutoff).

2
Setup and main result

It is evident from (
1.1
) that the full DNLS on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
is a system of infinitely many differential equations, where each variable indexed by
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
is coupled to nearby variables through the hopping potential
\alpha
. In addition, each variable has a nonlinear onsite term, which in this case is cubic. In order to study (
1.1
) and the corresponding Cauchy problem rigorously, we begin by making the system finite by considering a box with a finite side-length. In other words, we move to study the system (
1.4
). Here
\Lambda_{L}
is a finite box in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, and the addition of points is taken modulo the side length of the box. We will describe this more precisely below, but in essence it turns the full system (
1.1
) into a finite system (
1.4
) with periodic boundary conditions. The problem of showing that the dynamics (
1.1
) exist is then reduced proving that these finite dynamics have a suitable limit as the side length
L
goes to infinity.

To this end, we define the periodic lattice of side-length
2L+1
as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{L}\coloneqq\{-L,\dots,0,\dots,L\}^{d}.

(2.1)

Restricting to lattices of odd side-length simplifies the notation, but does not change the remaining argument. The moniker
periodic
comes from the fact that the addition of two points on the lattice is defined periodically:

\displaystyle x+_{\Lambda_{L}}y=x+y\mod 2L+1,\quad x,y\in\Lambda_{L}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

(2.2)

Moving to the finite periodic problem allows us to define a finite dual lattice, and to diagonalize the linear part of the dynamics using the Fourier transform. However, this technique will not be used here.

We therefore turn our attention to the following initial value problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\rm i}\derivative{t}\psi_{t}(x)&=\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}\alpha_{L}(x-y)\psi_{t}(y)+\lambda\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|^{2}\psi_{t}(x),\quad x\in\Lambda_{L}\\
\psi_{t}(x)|_{t=0}&=\psi_{0}(x),\quad x\in\Lambda_{L}.\end{cases}

(2.3)

This is a finite dimensional system of partial differential equations, and stands in contrast with the infinite dimensional initial value problem, corresponding to the full dynamics:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\rm i}\derivative{t}\psi_{t}(x)&=\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\alpha(x-y)\psi_{t}(y)+\lambda\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|^{2}\psi_{t}(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\\
\psi_{t}(x)|_{t=0}&=\psi_{0}(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.\end{cases}

(2.4)

Definition 2.1

We say that a time-dependent sequence
\psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to({\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt})
is a solution to the initial value problem (
2.4
), in case for each
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, the mapping
t\mapsto\psi_{t}(x)
is continuously differentiable and satisfies (
2.4
).

Here the hopping potential
\alpha_{L}\colon\Lambda_{L}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is defined in terms of the hopping potential
\alpha\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by precomposing
\alpha
with the embedding
\Lambda_{L}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. We will typically drop the subscript from the notation. At this point, we make the following assumption on the hopping potential.

Assumption 2.2

The hopping potential
\alpha
is of finite range, i.e. there exists
\ell\geq 0
such that
\alpha(x)=0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with
\left|x\right|_{\infty}\geq\ell
.

One class of such hopping potentials are the nearest neighborhood potentials, where the interaction range is bounded by
1
. This class covers the standard discrete Laplace operator, but also many others.

The above definitions give full meaning to the finite DNLS, as described by (
1.4
). It is easy to see that (
1.4
) can be realized as a Hamiltonian system, with the energy function given by

\displaystyle H_{L}(\psi)=\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{L}}\alpha(x-y)\psi(x)\psi(y)^{*}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\left|\psi(x)\right|^{4}.

(2.5)

Here
\psi(x)^{*}
is the complex conjugate of
\psi(x)
. The canonical pair of variables is given by
\real\psi(x)
and
\imaginary\psi(x)
.

A direct calculation shows that both the
\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{L})
-norm
\|\cdot\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{L})}^{2}
and the energy
H_{L}
are conserved by the time-evolution:
\derivative{t}\|\psi_{t}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{L})}=0
and
\derivative{t}H_{L}(\psi_{t})=0.
With this property it is evident that (
2.3
) admits a unique, continuosly differentiable solution to any initial value
\psi_{0}
.

Each finite sequence
\psi^{L}\colon\Lambda_{L}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
defined on the periodic lattice can also be realized as an infinite infinite sequence
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
by lifting the
L
-periodicity into an

-dependent translation invariance condition. Given a periodic sequence
\psi^{L}\colon\Lambda_{L}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
, let define a new sequence
\psi\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
by letting

\psi(x+(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})L)\coloneqq\psi^{L}(x),\quad x\in\Lambda_{L}

for all
(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. For a fixed

, this mapping from
\Lambda_{L}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
sequences to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}

\psi\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
can be truncated into a finite sequence
\psi^{L}\colon\Lambda_{L}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
for any

. Finally, we can recover

pointwise as a limit of these truncations as

. Therefore, we have the following operations

\displaystyle\texttt{Trunc}_{L}

\displaystyle\colon({\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt})\to(\Lambda_{L}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt})

(2.6)

\displaystyle\texttt{Embed}_{L}

\displaystyle\colon(\Lambda_{L}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt})\to({\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt})

(2.7)

and these operations are realize the periodic boundary conditions in the sense that the function

(\texttt{Embed}_{L}\circ\texttt{Trunc}_{L})(\psi)\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}

is always
L
-periodic, and

(\texttt{Trunc}_{L}\circ\texttt{Embed}_{L})(\psi^{L})=\psi^{L}

for any finite sequence
\psi^{L}\colon\Lambda_{L}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
. Given
\psi\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
, we typically use the notation
\psi^{L}\coloneqq\texttt{Trunc}_{L}(\psi)
, and it should be clear from the context what we mean by this.

Looking at the finite DNLS (
1.4
), it is clear that with
\alpha
of finite range, we can write the evolution of each solution
\psi_{t}(x)
in terms of a local polynomial of the field, i.e.

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\psi_{t}(x)=-{\rm i}G_{x}(\psi_{t}),\quad x\in\Lambda_{L},

(2.8)

where
G_{x}
sees the value of the field
\psi_{t}
only at sites that are within the range of
\alpha
from

, taking into account the periodic structure of
\Lambda_{L}
.

From (
1.4
), it follows also that the complex conjugate evolves as

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\psi_{t}(x)^{*}

\displaystyle={\rm i}\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}\alpha(x-y)\psi_{t}(y)^{*}+{\rm i}\lambda\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|^{2}\psi_{t}(x)^{*}

(2.9)

\displaystyle={\rm i}G_{x}(\psi_{t})^{*},\quad x\in\Lambda_{L}.

(2.10)

assuming that
\psi_{t}
solves pointwise (
1.4
), we would then obtain

\displaystyle\derivative[2]{t}\psi_{t}(x)=

\displaystyle-\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}\sum_{z\in\Lambda_{L}}\alpha(x-y)\alpha(y-z)\psi_{t}(z)

\displaystyle-\lambda\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}\alpha(x-y)\left|\psi_{t}(y)\right|^{2}\psi_{t}(y)-2\lambda\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}\alpha(x-y)\psi_{t}(y)\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle-\lambda^{2}\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|^{4}\psi_{t}(x)+\lambda\psi_{t}(x)^{2}\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}\alpha(x-y)\psi_{t}(y)^{*}

So in short, we can write

\displaystyle\derivative[2]{t}\psi_{t}(x)=P^{x}(\psi_{t}),

(2.11)

where
P^{x}
is a local polynomial of order
\leq 5
and it depends only on
\psi_{t}(z)
that satisfy

\left|x-z\right|_{\infty}\leq 2\ell.

If
\psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
is a solution of (

) in the sense of Definition

, then it solves the following Duhamel iterations of first and secod order:

\displaystyle\psi_{t}(x)=\psi_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}(-{\rm i}G^{x}(\psi_{s})){\rm d}s,\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\quad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},

(2.12)

and

\displaystyle\psi_{t}(x)=\psi_{0}(x)-{\rm i}tG^{x}(\psi_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)P^{x}(\psi_{s}){\rm d}s,\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\quad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

(2.13)

Formulation (

) is equivalent with solving (
1.1
) in the sense that if
\psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}

and solves (

), then

is also differentiable for every

and

satisfies (
1.1
) with the initial condition

. This reduces the study of the infinite system of differential equations into solving the fixed point problem (
2.12
). We will use this in proving the existence of solutions to (

). To prove that we can find a solution to (

), we start by looking at the finite,
L
-periodic problem. From these solutions, we then obtain a subsequence of side lenghts

and a limit point
\psi
, such that
\psi_{t}(x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\psi^{L_{k}}_{t}(x)
and

are global solutions to

with side length

. After this, we simply note that the limit point

The second order Duhamel expansion (

1.1
) in the sense of Definition (
2.1
) must satisfy (

In order to prove that solutions to (

) exist and that they are unique, we will need to study the problem in suitable sequence spaces. To this end, we use the following definition and notation.

Definition 2.3

(Weighted sequence spaces)

Let
\Phi\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to(0,+\infty)
be a weight function. For a given sequence
\psi\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
, we say that
\psi\in X_{\Phi}
, in case it satisfies
\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\left|\psi(x)\right|\Phi(x)<+\infty
.

These spaces are all Banach spaces. Two classes of such weighted sequence spaces are of special interest: exponentially bounded sequences, and sequences bounded by a power law.

Definition 2.4

(Exponentially bounded sequences)

For
q>0
, we say that the space of
q
-exponentially bounded sequences is given by those
\psi\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
for which

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}{\rm e}^{-q\left|x\right|_{\infty}}\left|\psi(x)\right|<+\infty.

(2.14)

Such space is denoted by
U_{q}=U_{q}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
, and its norm by
\|\cdot\|_{U_{q}}
.

In the next definition, we use the standard notation
\langle x\rangle=(1+\left|x\right|^{2})^{1/2}
.

Definition 2.5

For
p>0
, the space of
p
-power law bounded sequences is given by

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\langle x\rangle^{-p}\left|\psi(x)\right|<+\infty.

(2.15)

Such space is denoted by
X_{p}
and its corresponding norm by
\|\cdot\|_{p}
.

Here we note that
X_{p}\subset X_{p^{\prime}}
for

,
U_{q}\subset U_{q^{\prime}}
for

, and
X_{p}\subset U_{q}
for all

.

be arbitrarily small. For

, such that

is the unique global solution to (

). This can be approximated in terms of the sequence of solutions of

3
Control of the local particle number density

To obtain useful bounds for the evolution of some weighted supremum norms of the field, we move from the conserved quantities
\|\cdot\|_{2}
and
H
(which may be infinite in the infinite volume limit) to quantities that are not completely conserved, but are always finite and whose growth rate is bounded from above. To this end, define the local particle number, centered around
x\in\Lambda_{L}
and with weight
\varepsilon
, as

N_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})\coloneqq\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}}\left|\psi_{t}(y)\right|^{2}=\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|y\right|_{\infty}}\left|\psi_{t}(x+y)\right|^{2}

and the local particle number density as

Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})\coloneqq\frac{1}{S_{\varepsilon}}N_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t}),\quad S_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x\right|_{\infty}}.

A direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}}F_{y,N}(\psi_{t})

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S_{\varepsilon}}M_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

(3.2)

where

\displaystyle F_{y,N}(\psi_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{L}}\alpha(y-x^{\prime}){\rm i}(\psi_{t}(y)\psi_{t}(x^{\prime})^{*}-\psi_{t}(y)^{*}\psi_{t}(x^{\prime}))

(3.3)

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}}F_{y,N}(\psi_{t})

(3.4)

If we can control the density of
M_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)
, then we have control over the evolution of the local particle number density. It should be noted that
Q_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
and
M_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
are both real quantities, and
Q_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
is positive.

Due to the antisymmetric role that the variables
y
and
x^{\prime}
take in the formula of
M_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
, we can also write it as

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y,x^{\prime}\in\Lambda}\left({\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}}-{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|_{\infty}}\right)\alpha(y-x^{\prime}){\rm i}(\psi_{t}(y)\psi_{t}(x^{\prime})^{*}-\psi_{t}(y)^{*}\psi_{t}(x^{\prime})).

(3.5)

The benefit of the above formula is that it makes clear that there are large cancellations in the local particle number densities. Taking
\varepsilon=0
, we see that
M_{0}(x,t)=0
for all
x\in\Lambda
and
t\geq 0
. For positive
\varepsilon
we migth have
M_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\neq 0
. However, since
x^{\prime}\mapsto\alpha(y-x^{\prime})
is concentrated near
y\in\Lambda
, the exponential terms
{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}}
and
{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|_{\infty}}
are comparable in size. Therefore, large cancellations should make
M_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
small enough. This is the content of the next lemma.

Lemma 3.1

For any
x\in\Lambda
,
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2\ell})
and
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\left|Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})\right|\leq{\rm e}^{\tilde{\varepsilon}t}\left|Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{0})\right|,

(3.6)

where
\tilde{\varepsilon}
depends on the localization scale
\varepsilon
, the dimension
d
of the system, and the hopping potential
\alpha
.

Proof.

We partition
\Lambda^{2}_{L}=\Lambda_{L}\times\Lambda_{L}
into two sets:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{L}_{1}(x)\coloneqq\{(y,x^{\prime})\in\Lambda^{2}_{L}\colon\left|y-x\right|_{\infty}\geq\left|x^{\prime}-x\right|_{\infty}\}

\displaystyle\Gamma^{L}_{2}(x)\coloneqq\{(y,x^{\prime})\in\Lambda^{2}_{L}\colon\left|y-x\right|_{\infty}<\left|x^{\prime}-x\right|_{\infty}\}.

The contribution to
\left|M_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\right|
coming from the sum over
\Gamma_{1}^{L}(x)
is bounded from above by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{(y,x^{\prime})\in\Gamma^{L}_{1}(x)}\left({\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}}-{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|_{\infty}}\right)\alpha(y-x^{\prime}){\rm i}(\psi_{t}(y)\psi_{t}(x^{\prime})^{*}-\psi_{t}(y)^{*}\psi_{t}(x^{\prime}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq c\left(\sum_{(y,x^{\prime})\in\Gamma^{L}_{1}(x)}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|_{\infty}}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left(\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}-\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|_{\infty}\right)}\right)\left|\alpha(y-x^{\prime})\right|\left|\psi_{t}(y)\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\left(\sum_{(y,x^{\prime})\in\Gamma^{L}_{1}(x)}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|_{\infty}}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left(\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}-\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|_{\infty}\right)}\right)\left|\alpha(y-x^{\prime})\right|\left|\psi_{t}(x^{\prime})\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

If
x^{\prime}
and
y
are from a distance
\left|x^{\prime}-y\right|_{\infty}>\ell
from each others, we have
\alpha(x^{\prime}-y)=0
, so such pairs will not contribute to the sum. The right hand side of this can thus be bounded from above by

\displaystyle c\varepsilon^{1/2}\ell^{1/2}\left(\sum_{(y,x^{\prime})\in\Gamma^{L}_{1}(x)}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|_{\infty}}\left|\alpha(y-x^{\prime})\right|\left|\psi_{t}(y)\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\varepsilon^{1/2}\ell^{1/2}\left(\sum_{(y,x^{\prime})\in\Gamma^{L}_{1}(x)}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|_{\infty}}\left|\alpha(y-x^{\prime})\right|\left|\psi_{t}(x^{\prime})\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq c\varepsilon\ell\left(\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{1}^{L}(x)}{\rm e}^{\varepsilon\ell}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}}\left|B^{d}(0,\ell)\right|\|\alpha\|_{\infty}\left|\psi_{t}(y)\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\left(\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{L}_{1}(x)}{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|_{\infty}}\left|B^{d}(0,\ell)\right|\|\alpha\|_{\infty}\left|\psi_{t}(x^{\prime})\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{d,\alpha,\ell}\varepsilon N_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})^{1/2}N_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})^{1/2}=\varepsilon C_{d,\alpha,\ell}N_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t}),

where we can pick the explicit bound
C_{d,\alpha,\ell}=c\ell{\rm e}^{\varepsilon\ell/2}\|\alpha\|_{\infty}(2\ell+1)^{d}

In combination with a symmetric calculation in the case that
\left|y-x\right|_{\infty}<\left|x^{\prime}-x\right|_{\infty}
, the above calculation shows that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{(y,x^{\prime})\in\Gamma_{2}(x)}\left({\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}}-{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|_{\infty}}\right)\alpha(y-x^{\prime}){\rm i}(\psi_{t}(y)\psi_{t}(x^{\prime})^{*}-\psi_{t}(y)^{*}\psi_{t}(x))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon C_{d,\alpha,\ell}N_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t}).

We divide both sides by
S_{\varepsilon}
and use the positivity of
Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})
to get the inequality

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{t}Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})\right|\leq\varepsilon C_{d,\alpha,\ell}Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t}).

This shows that

\displaystyle\left|Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\varepsilon C_{d,\alpha,\ell}\left|Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{s})\right|{\rm d}s+\left|Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{0})\right|,

(3.7)

so by Grönwall and the positivity of
Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})
, it follows that

\displaystyle Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})\leq{\rm e}^{\tilde{\varepsilon}t}Q_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{0}),

(3.8)

where
\tilde{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\cdot C_{d,\alpha,\ell}
.
∎

Remark 3.2

One way to think of this inequality is that it shows the the local particle number of the system cannot grow too fast. In fact, it can grow at most exponentially in time. This rate is determined by by localization scale
\varepsilon
, dimension of the system, and properties of the hopping potential.

Remark 3.3

If we take
L\to+\infty
, then with suitably regular initial data both sides of (
3.6
) are defined, so the inequality extends to the infinite lattice.

From
3.1
, it follows immediately that the non-normalized local particle number
N_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})
satisfies

\displaystyle N_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})\leq{\rm e}^{\tilde{\varepsilon}t}N_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{0}).

(3.9)

Here the left hand side controls
\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|^{2}
and the right hand side depends on the initial data only. Since
\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|^{2}\leq N_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{t})
, we also obtain

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|^{2}\leq{\rm e}^{\tilde{\varepsilon}t}N_{\varepsilon,x}(\psi_{0}).

(3.10)

This implies the following bound for
\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|
:

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|\leq{\rm e}^{\tilde{\varepsilon}t}\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}}\left|\psi_{0}(y)\right|.

(3.11)

Therefore, if
\Phi\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is a weight function for which
\psi_{0}
is in the corresponding weighted space, we have

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|\Phi(x)\leq{\rm e}^{\tilde{\varepsilon}t}\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}}\frac{\Phi(x)}{\Phi(y)}\|\psi_{0}\|_{\Phi},\quad x\in\Lambda_{L}.

(3.12)

This means that the weighted supremum norm at time
t
is controlled in terms of the same norm of the initial data, and this bound becomes worse exponentially in time. Moreover, the prefactor of the bound is determined by

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|x-y\right|_{\infty}}\frac{\Phi(x)}{\Phi(y)},

(3.13)

where
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2\ell}
, with
\ell\geq 1
being the coupling range of the hopping potential.

If we consider a sequence of finite initial value problems (
2.3
), with the initial data given by the
L
-truncation of a given initial data
\psi_{0}
, then uniform boundedness in
L
of (
3.13
) for a given
decreasing weight

\Phi
gives a bound for all times for the full lattice evolution, as the right hand side of the following inequality doesn’t depend on
L
:

\displaystyle\left|\psi^{L}_{t}(x)\right|\Phi(x)\leq{\rm e}^{\tilde{\varepsilon}t}\left(\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|x-y\right|}\frac{\Phi(x)}{\Phi(y)}\right)\|\psi_{0}\|_{\Phi},\quad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

(3.14)

4
Existence of solutions

Lemma 4.1

Let
\psi_{0}\in U_{q}
for
q\in(0,1/(4\ell))
, where
\ell
is the range of the hopping potential. Then there exists a solution to the Cauchy problem (
2.4
) in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

Proof.

We have fixed the initial value
\psi_{0}\in U_{q}
, so we can consider all the finite problems (
2.3
) with the truncated initial value
\psi_{0}^{L}
. Note that if we embed
\psi^{L}_{0}
into
{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
, we have
\|\psi_{0}^{L}\|_{U_{q}}\leq\|\psi_{0}\|_{U_{q}}
. Fix
T>0
to be a target time. The sequence of initial value problems parametrized by
L\in{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&{\rm i}\derivative{t}\psi_{t}^{L}(x)=\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}\alpha_{L}(x-y)\psi^{L}_{t}(y)+\lambda\left|\psi_{t}^{L}(x)\right|^{2}\psi_{t}^{L}(x),\quad x\in\Lambda_{L},t\in[0,T]\\
&\psi_{0}^{L}(x)=\psi_{0}(x),\quad x\in\Lambda_{L}\end{cases}

(4.1)

can be formulated in the integral form as.

\displaystyle\psi^{L}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\psi^{L}_{0}(x)-{\rm i}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}sG^{x}(\psi^{L}_{s}),\quad x\in\Lambda_{L},t\in[0,T].

(4.2)

From this formulation, we find a continuously differentiable solution

\psi^{L}\colon[0,T]\to(\Lambda_{L}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}).

This solution is continuous from
[0,T]
to
{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}^{\Lambda_{L}}
with the supremum norm, so for each
x\in\Lambda_{L}
, the function
f^{L}_{x}\colon[0,T]\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
,
f^{L}_{x}(t)=\psi^{L}_{t}(x)
is continuous (and continuously differentiable). Furthermore,
\left|f^{L}_{x}(t)\right|=\left|\psi^{L}_{t}(x)\right|\leq C_{T,x}
, where the constant is independent of
L
. This follows from (
3.14
). We also have equicontinuity in time with
L
being the varying parameter. By Arzela-Ascoli, we find a limit of a subsequence. Doing the diagonal trick on the collection
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, we find a common subsequence so that the limits
f_{x}\coloneqq\lim_{j\to\infty}f^{L_{j}}_{x}
exist in
C([0,T],{\mathbb{R}})
for every
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, and this limiting configuration satisfies for all every
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
:

\displaystyle f_{x}(t)

\displaystyle=f_{x}(0)-{\rm i}\int_{0}^{t}(\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\alpha(x-y)f_{y}(s)+\lambda\left|f_{x}(s)\right|^{2}f_{x}(s)){\rm d}s,\quad t\in[0,T].

(4.3)

After yet another application of the diagonal trick, in this instance for the target times
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we obtain a solution (denoted this time by
(\psi_{t}(x))_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
for each fixed time
t
) of (
2.12
). This also solves the Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\rm i}\derivative{t}\psi_{t}(x)&=\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\alpha(x-y)\psi_{t}(y)+\lambda\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|^{2}\psi_{t}(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\\
\psi_{t}(x)|_{t=0}&=\psi_{0}(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\end{cases}

(4.4)

in the sense of Definition
2.1
.
∎

Furthermore,
\psi_{t}
inherits the weighted sup-norm estimates:

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}{\rm e}^{-r\left|x\right|}\left|\psi_{t}(x)\right|\leq{\rm e}^{ct}C_{d,r}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}{\rm e}^{-r\left|x\right|}\left|\psi_{0}(x)\right|,\quad r\in(0,c/2).

(4.5)

Remark 4.2

Thus far, we have proven the existence of some limiting dynamics. Provided that we have uniqueness of the dynamics, it follows that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Phi(x)\left|\psi_{t}(x)-\psi_{s}(x)\right|\leq\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\int_{s}^{t}{\rm d}r\Phi(x)\left|G_{x}(\psi_{r})\right|

(4.6)

Here
G_{x}
is a local polynomial of order
3
. Therefore, if the norm satisfies uniformly a perturbation upper bound
\Phi(x+y)\leq c\Phi(x)
for
\left|y\right|_{\infty}\leq\tilde{c}
, and if
G(\psi_{r})
is also bounded in the norm with the weight
\Phi(x)^{1/3}
, then the integrand becomes bounded by
\|\psi_{r}\|_{\Phi^{1/3}}^{3}\leq C_{T}\|\psi_{0}\|_{\Phi^{1/3}}^{3}
, and the smallness coming from the size of the integration domain then guarantees that
\psi\in C([0,T],X_{\Phi})
as well.

5
Uniqueness of solutions with power law bounds

Lemma
4.1
shows that the dynamics exists for all initial data that is in some space of exponentially bounded sequences
U_{q}
, with
q<1/(4\ell)
,
\ell\geq 1
being the range of the hopping potential. The solution was constructed using compactness arguments, and it might not be unique. To prove the uniqueness of solutions, we have to require that the initial data satisfies additional regularity conditions, namely that the initial data is bounded by a power law with the exponent
\frac{1}{2}
. In other words, we require that the initial data belongs to
X_{1/2}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\langle x\rangle^{-1/2}\left|\psi(x)\right|<+\infty.

(5.1)

The space
X_{1/2}
is a Banach space, and we have
X_{1/2}\subset U_{q}
for every
q>0
. In particular,
X_{1/2}\subsetneq\bigcap_{q>0}U_{q}
as sets. This tells us that if we assume our initial data to belong to
X_{p}
, there exists a solution to the Cauchy problem, as shown in
4.1
.

Let
\psi_{0}\in X_{1/2}
be an initial value. For each fixed
T>0
, we can find a ball of radius
M<+\infty
such that
\|\psi_{t}\|_{1/2}\leq M,
for
t\in[0,T]
, when
\psi_{t}
is given by any solution to the Cauchy problem
2.4
. This is an a priori boundedness condition that allows us to prove the uniqueness of such solutions.

Lemma 5.1

Let
\psi_{0}\in X_{1/2}
be an initial lattice function
\psi_{0}\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}
, and fix
T>0
. If two functions
\psi^{1},\psi^{2}
solve the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.1
, then
\psi^{1}_{t}=\psi^{2}_{t}
for all
t\in[0,T]

Proof.

We know that both
\psi^{1}
and
\psi^{2}
satisfy the second order expansion

\displaystyle\psi_{t}(x)=\psi_{0}(x)-{\rm i}tG_{x}(\psi_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s(t-s)P_{x}(\psi_{s}),\quad t\in[0,T]

(5.2)

with the initial data
\psi^{1}_{0}=\psi^{2}_{0}=\psi_{0}
.

If we consider the distance between
\psi^{1}_{t}(x)
and
\psi^{2}_{t}(x)
, the first two terms always cancel, leaving only the contribution from the integral term:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s(t-s)P_{x}(\psi^{1}_{s})-\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s(t-s)P_{x}(\psi^{2}_{s})\right|.

(5.3)

This term compares
\psi^{1}_{s}
and
\psi^{2}_{s}
not only at site
x
, but also at sites that are inside an
x
-centric closed
\left|\cdot\right|_{\infty}
-ball with radius
2\ell
. Now, let’s try to find a closed system of estimates for the following quantities that measure how much discrepancy there is between
\psi_{t}^{1}
and
\psi_{t}^{2}
inside a centered ball with growing radii:

\delta_{n}(t)\coloneqq\sup\{\left|\psi^{1}_{t}(x)-\psi^{2}_{t}(x)\right|\colon\left|x\right|_{\infty}\leq n(2\ell)\}.

Indeed, we now see that

\displaystyle\left|\psi^{1}_{t}(x)-\psi^{2}_{t}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s_{1}(t-s_{1})\left|\left(P^{x}(\psi^{1}_{s_{1}})-P^{x}(\psi^{2}_{s_{1}})\right)\right|

(5.4)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s_{1}(t-s_{1})\delta_{n+1}(s_{1})cn^{2},

(5.5)

where
c
depends on some combinatorial constants,
\ell
,
M
, and the
X_{1/2}
-norm of the initial data. The term
n^{2}
comes from the fact that we have a weight
\langle x\rangle^{-1/2}
in the supremum norm.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s_{1}(t-s_{1})\delta_{n+1}(s_{1})cn^{2}.

(5.6)

We also note that we have the a priori bound
\delta_{n}(s)\leq 2MS_{n(2\ell)}
, where
S_{k}
is defined as the supremum of the inverse weight in the centered
k
-ball:

\displaystyle S_{k}=\sup_{\left|x\right|_{\infty}\leq k}\Phi(x)^{-1}=\sup_{\left|x\right|_{\infty}\leq k}\langle x\rangle^{1/2}\leq c_{d}\langle k\rangle^{1/2}.

(5.7)

This will be useful after we have iterated the estimate enough times. Indeed, a
k
-fold iteration of (
5.6
) gives

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s_{1}(t-s_{1})\int_{0}^{s_{1}}{\rm d}s_{2}(s_{1}-s_{2})\dots\int_{0}^{s_{k-1}}{\rm d}s_{k}(s_{k-1}-s_{k})\delta_{n+k}(s_{k})c^{k}(n\cdots(n+k))^{2}

(5.8)

After unravelling each
(t-s_{i})
into an integral using
\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}h(s){\rm d}r{\rm d}s=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)h(s){\rm d}s
, we obtain the following bound

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c^{k}t^{2k}}{{(2k)!}}2MS_{2(n+k)\ell}\left(n(n+1)\cdots(n+k)\right)^{2}.

(5.9)

Now, if we pick

\displaystyle t\leq T_{n}\coloneqq 1/2c^{-1/2}\left(\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left(2MS_{2(n+k)\ell}\left(n\cdots(n+k)\right)^{2}(2k)!^{-1}\right)^{1/2k}\right)^{-1},

(5.10)

then the above estimate gives
\delta_{n}(t)\leq 1/2
.
Furthermore, if
t\leq\overline{T}\coloneqq 1/2\min(\inf_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}T_{n},T)
, then
\delta_{n}(t)\leq 1/2
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. After this, the estimate (
5.8
) allows us to bootstrap this, whereby
\delta_{n}(s)=0
for all
n
. The key property here is that for each
n
, and each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
k_{0}\geq 1
such that
((n+k)!)^{2}\leq((1+\varepsilon)k)!^{2}
for all
k\geq k_{0}
and showing that for suitably small
\varepsilon
(here
\varepsilon=1/10
is small enough), the right hand side of the following inequality vanishes in the limit
k\to+\infty
sufficiently fast:

\displaystyle\frac{(n(n+1)\cdots(n+k))^{2}}{(2k)!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{k+1}(\varepsilon k+\ell)\right)^{2}}{\Gamma(2k+1)}=\frac{\Gamma((\varepsilon+1)k+1)^{2}}{\Gamma(\varepsilon k)^{2}\Gamma(2k+1)}.

(5.11)

Since
\delta_{n}(s)=0
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
when
s\leq\overline{T}
, it follows that
\psi^{1}_{s}=\psi^{2}_{s}
for
s\leq\overline{T}
.

What is left to show is that
\overline{T}>0
. For this to be the case, the expression

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{k\to\infty}\left(\frac{2MS_{2(n+k)\ell}(n(n+1)\cdots(n+k))^{2}}{(2k)!}\right)^{1/2k}

(5.12)

has to be uniformly bounded in
n
. This is the case precisely because we can find a small enough
\varepsilon>0
such that the expression (
5.12
) is bounded from above by

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left(\frac{2MS_{2(n+k)\ell}\Gamma((\varepsilon+1)k+1)^{2}}{\Gamma(\varepsilon k)^{2}\Gamma(2k+1)}\right)^{1/2k}

(5.13)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left(2MS_{2(n+k)\ell}\right)^{1/2k}\cdot\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left(\frac{\Gamma((\varepsilon+1)k+1)^{2}}{\Gamma(\varepsilon k)^{2}\Gamma(2k+1)}\right)^{1/2k}

(5.14)

The rightmost term is bounded from above, so all we have left to do is to show that there exists
C<+\infty
such that

\limsup\limits_{k\to\infty}S_{2(n+k)\ell}^{1/2k}\leq C<+\infty.

But this is clear, since

S_{2(n+k)\ell}^{1/2k}\leq((2(n+k)\ell)^{1/2})^{1/2k},

and for sufficiently large
k
we can estimate
n\leq k
. This gives

\limsup_{k\to\infty}S_{2(n+k)\ell}^{1/2k}\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}((4k\ell)^{1/2})^{1/2k}\leq C.

We have therefore shown that
\overline{T}>0
. Since
\overline{T}
only depends on
M
, the radius of the
\|\cdot\|_{1/2}
-ball where the solution stays for
t\in[0,T]
, we can use standard pasting arguments to show uniqueness on
[0,T]
.
∎

6
Approximation of the limit

We have shown that the full dynamics (
1.1
) exists and is unique for regular enough initial data. Existence was obtained through a compactness argument, and in proving uniqueness we used the second order expansion of the solution to control the difference between two different solutions. What is still left to show is that the full dynamics can in a suitable sense be approximated in terms of the finite periodizations of the dynamics.

Here we have to impose more restrictions on the space of solutions. More precisely, we need to consider a supremum norm with a more restrictive weight. To this end, we use the weight

\displaystyle\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\langle x\rangle^{1/2-c}},\quad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},

(6.1)

where
c>0
can be arbitrarily small. In other words, we work in the space
X_{1/2-c}
, so that
\|\psi\|_{1/2-c}<+\infty
means that

\displaystyle\left|\psi(x)\right|\leq C\langle x\rangle^{1/2-c}.

(6.2)

It seems that we really need to go below
1/2
in order to get the approximation property to work. In other words, there has to be a slightly tighter regularity restriction on the initial data than for the uniqueness.

As in the proof of uniqueness, given any initial data and a target time
T>0
we may always choose
M
so that
\|\psi_{t}\|_{1/2-c}\leq M
for all
t\leq T
.

Let
\psi_{0}\in X_{1/2-c}
. We can restrict this initial data to
\Lambda_{L}
and then extend it back periodically to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
in order to obtain a valid periodic initial data
\psi^{L}_{0}
. For
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
t\in[0,T]
, we would like to define the limiting object of
\psi^{L}_{t}
as

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(x)\coloneqq\lim_{L\to\infty}\psi_{t}^{L}(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

(6.3)

We don’t yet know if this definition makes sense, since we only have convergence along a subsequence, as shown in the construction of the solution to the infinite system. We have to prove an additional Cauchy condition on the sequence
(\psi_{t}^{L}(x))_{L\geq 1}
to be able to do this. This is the content of Lemma
6.3
below. After this, we are able to prove the following lemma, which is the main result this section.

Lemma 6.1

Let
\psi_{0}\in X_{1/2-c}
, then the finite problem
2.3
approximates
2.4
well in the sense that for each
T>0
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists

such that for all

, we have:

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\Lambda_{k}}\left|\psi_{s}(x)-\psi^{L}_{s}(x)\right|<\varepsilon.

(6.4)

Here
\psi_{s}(x)
is the limiting field constructed using the subsequence, and
\psi^{L}
are the unique solutions to the finite Cauchy problem

.

Remark 6.2

It follows from this result that we not only have a convergence through a subsequence of the truncated problem, but also that the stronger result

\displaystyle\psi_{t}(x)=\lim_{L\to+\infty}\psi^{L}_{t}(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

(6.5)

holds. Thus, the limiting object in (
6.3
) exists and is
\psi_{t}
.

Following the strategy of
[
5
]
we define, for fixed
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
k\leq L
, and
t\in[0,T]
, the following quantities

\Delta_{x}^{L}(t)\coloneqq\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left|\psi_{s}^{L+1}(x)-\psi^{L}_{s}(x)\right|,\quad x\in\Lambda_{L}

\overline{\Delta}_{k}^{L}(t)\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\Lambda_{k}}\Delta_{x}^{L}(t),

and

d^{L}(t)\coloneqq\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\sup_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\left|\psi_{s}^{L}(x)-\psi_{0}^{L}(x)\right|.

The first quantity measures the supremum-in-time distance of the values of the
L+1
-periodized field and the
L
-periodized field at site

\left|x\right|_{\infty}\leq L
, but as soon as the dynamics are turned on, we might start to see growth in
\delta_{x}^{L}(t)
. The second one looks at the supremum of these differences inside some smaller box of side length

Lemma 6.3

For any initial data
\psi_{0}
and target time
T>0
, we have that for each
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, and for each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
L_{k}\geq 1
and a constant

such that

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}_{k}^{L}(t)<A^{-L}\quad t\leq T,

(6.6)

for all
L\geq L_{k}

Proof.

For the sake of simplicity, we assume that the range of the hopping potential is
\ell=1
.
Assume that
\left|x\right|\leq k
. The first Picard iteration gives

\psi_{t}^{L}(x)=\psi_{0}^{L}(x)-{\rm i}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}sG^{x}(\psi_{s}^{L}),

where
G^{x}(\psi_{s}^{L})
depends only on
\psi^{L}_{s}(y)
with
\left|x-y\right|\leq 1
, and has a gradient bound controlled by
(1+\left|x\right|)^{1-2c}\leq(k+1)^{1-2c}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}^{L}(t)\leq C\int_{0}^{t}(k+1)^{1-2c}\overline{\Delta}_{k+1}^{L}(s){\rm d}s,

(6.7)

whereby

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}_{k}^{L}(t)\leq C\int_{0}^{t}(1+k)^{1-2c}\overline{\Delta}_{k+1}^{L}(s){\rm d}s

(6.8)

We can iterate this

L-k

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}_{k}^{L}(t)\leq C^{L-k}(1+k)^{1-2c}\dots(1+L)^{1-2c}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s_{1}\dots\int_{0}^{s_{L-k-1}}\overline{\Delta}_{L}^{L}(s_{L-k}){\rm d}s_{L-k}.

(6.9)

At the final step, we use the fact that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\left(\left|\psi_{s}^{L+1}(x)\right|+\left|\psi_{s}^{L}(x)\right|\right)\leq 2C_{T}\|\psi_{0}\|_{1/2-c}\langle L\rangle^{1/2-c}.

(6.10)

This shows that

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}_{k}^{L}(t)\leq C_{T,\|\psi_{0}\|_{1/2-c}}^{L-k}\frac{t^{L-k}}{(L-k)!}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{L-k}(k+i)\right)^{1-2c}L^{1/2-c}.

(6.11)

We can therefore pick a cutoff point
L_{k}
depending on
\varepsilon,k,\|\psi_{0}\|_{1/4}
, and

\overline{\Delta}_{k}^{L}(T)<A^{-L}

for all
L\geq L(k,\|\psi_{0}\|,T)
, where
A\geq 2
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the approximation result.

7
Random initial data

Theorem
2.6
shows that for initial data that is in
X_{1/2-c}
, the (
2.4
) has a unique solution that can be approximated in terms of solutions to (
2.3
). In this Section, we investigate whether the certain states – probability distributions on the configuration space – assign full probability to
X_{1/2-c}
.

We will only consider translation invariant probability measures on
{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
, since all equilibrium states satisfy this property and since the Gaussians used in studying homogeneous kinetic theory of waves are translation invariant. Translation invariance makes the use of the following lemma from
[
12
]
to use the uniform bounds for certain moments to establish almost sure growth conditions for the samples.

Proposition 7.1

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
. For a fixed
a>0
, if there exists
\xi>a\cdot d
satisfying

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.1507pt}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}}\left|\psi(x)\right|^{\xi}\mu({\rm d}\psi)<+\infty,

(7.1)

then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\limsup_{n\to\infty}E_{n})=0,

(7.2)

where

\displaystyle E_{n}\coloneqq\{\psi\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}\colon\left|\psi(x_{n})\right|\langle x_{n}\rangle^{-1/a}>1\}.

(7.3)

Here
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an enumeration of all
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

Remark 7.2

This is stated and proved in
[
12
, pp. 460–461]
.

In order to apply this proposition to our setting and get the full existence, uniqueness and approximability, we can take any
a>2
. Thus, we need need to show that with
\xi>2d
, something like (
7.1
) holds. Since
d\geq 1
, we will always need to go beyond
\xi=2
. On the other hand, if we want to obtain only existence, then any
a>0
will do, and it suffices to prove that (
7.1
) holds for, say
\xi=2
, which is typically easy.

Theorem 7.3

If
\mu
is a measure on
{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
from one of the following classes, then with probability
1
with respect to
\mu
a sequence is in
X_{1/2-c}
.

(1)

The measure
\mu
is a centered, translation invariant Gaussian process on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

(2)

d=1
and the measure
\mu
is a microcanonical measure for the Hamiltonian system (
1.1
) with particle density
\rho_{N}
and energy density
\rho_{E}
.

(3)

d=1
and the measure
\mu
is a grand canonical Gibbs measure for the Hamiltonian system (
1.1
).

Before proving the theorem, let us briefly mention what the microcanonical and Gibbs measures of the system would look like. These are both defined as limits of certain finite dimensional probability measures. In what follows, we use the notation
V_{d}=(2L+1)^{d}
to stand for the total volume of a
d
-dimensional box of side-length
2L+1
. The notation
H_{L}(\psi)
will stand for the Hamiltonian (
2.5
) and
N_{L}(\psi)
will stand for the particle number, i.e. the square of
\|\cdot\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{L})}
.

Definition 7.4

(Microcanonical measure)

Let
\rho_{E}
and
\rho_{N}
be the energy and particle density, respectively. Then, provided that the Diracs make sense, the measures
\mu_{L,\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}
on
{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.21529pt}^{\Lambda_{L}}
, are defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{L,\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}({\rm d}\psi)\coloneqq Z_{L,\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}^{-1}\delta(H_{L}(\psi)-\rho_{E}V_{d})\delta(N_{L}(\psi)-\rho_{N}V_{d})\prod_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}{\rm d}(\real\psi(x)){\rm d}(\imaginary\psi(x))

(7.4)

are the microcanonical measures of the finite system. Here
Z_{L,\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}
is a normalization constant that makes (
7.4
) a probability measure.

Provided that the limit
\lim_{L\to\infty}\mu_{L,\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}
exists in some sense, we call this a microcanonical measure of the infinite system (
1.1
).

Definition 7.5

(Grand Canonical Gibbs measure)

Let
\beta>0
be the inverse temperature and
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}
the chemical potential. For each
L
, we define the Gibbs measures of the finite DNLS as

\displaystyle\nu_{L,\beta,\mu}({\rm d}\psi)=Z^{-1}_{L,\beta,\mu}{\rm e}^{-\beta(H_{L}(\psi)-\mu N_{L}(\psi))}\prod_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}{\rm d}(\real\psi(x)){\rm d}(\imaginary\psi(x)).

(7.5)

Here
Z_{L,\beta,\mu}
is a normalization constant that makes (
7.5
) a probability measure.

Provided that the limit
\lim_{L\to\infty}\nu_{L,\beta,\mu}
exists in some sense, we call this a Grand canonical Gibbs measure of the infinite system (
1.1
).

The question of the existence or nonexistence of the Gibbs measures for infinite systems is a delicate matter and beyond the scope of this article. This should depend on the dimension of the problem, as well as the size of the nonlinearity in
H_{L}
and the negativity of the chemical potential
\mu
. For this reason, we will only state conditional results that any such grand canonical Gibbs measure must satisfy. This can be achieved by getting uniform estimates for the finite versions of the measures.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
7.3
.

Proof.

To prove the Gaussian case, we note that if we have a centered, translation invariant Gaussian process on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, then for any
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and any
\xi>2d
, the moment

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.1507pt}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}}\left|\psi(x)\right|^{\xi}\mu({\rm d}\psi)\leq C

(7.6)

is bounded. By translation invariance, it follows that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.1507pt}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}}\left|\psi(x)\right|^{\xi}\mu({\rm d}\psi)\leq C.

(7.7)

Thus, by the result of Lanford et al., it follows that
X_{1/2-c}
has full probability.

For item (2), we note that
N_{L}(\psi)
and
H_{L}(\psi)
are translation invariant for each
L
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.1507pt}^{\Lambda_{L}}}\left|\psi(x)\right|^{2}\mu_{L,\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}({\rm d}\psi)=\frac{1}{V_{d}}\rho_{N}V_{d}=\rho_{N}.

(7.8)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.1507pt}^{\Lambda_{L}}}H_{L}(\psi)\mu({\rm d}\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\int_{{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.1507pt}^{\Lambda_{L}}}\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{L}}\alpha(x-y)\psi(x)\psi(y)^{*}\mu_{L,\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}({\rm d}\psi)

(7.9)

\displaystyle+\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\int_{{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.1507pt}^{\Lambda_{L}}}\left|\psi(x)\right|^{4}\mu_{L,\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}({\rm d}\psi)

(7.10)

\displaystyle=\rho_{E}V_{d}

(7.11)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{L}^{2}}\alpha(x-y)\psi(x)\psi(y)^{*}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle\psi,S_{n}\psi\rangle.

(7.12)

Here each
S_{n}
is a self-adjoint operator on
\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{L})
that has uniform operator norm with respect to
L
. It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\int_{{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.1507pt}^{\Lambda_{L}}}\left|\psi(x)\right|^{4}\mu_{L,\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}({\rm d}\psi)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{E}V_{d}-C\int_{{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.1507pt}^{\Lambda_{L}}}\|\psi\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\mu_{L,\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}({\rm d}\psi)

(7.13)

\displaystyle=\rho_{E}V_{d}-C\rho_{N}V_{d}.

(7.14)

By translation invariance of
\mu_{L,\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}
, we have.

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{C}\hskip 0.1507pt}^{\Lambda_{L}}}\left|\psi(x)\right|^{4}\mu_{L,\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}({\rm d}\psi)\leq C^{\prime},

(7.15)

where
C^{\prime}
is uniform in
L
. Thus, if
\mu_{\rho_{E},\rho_{N}}
exists, any moment
\xi<4
must be bounded, which through the Proposition
7.1
of Lanford et al. implies that
X_{1/2-c}
has full measure when
d=1
.

For item (3), note (see
[
7
]
) that any accumulation point of the periodic Gibbs measures must satisfy the boundedness of (
7.1
) for every
\xi<4
. Thus, if
d=1
, the result is strong enough to conclude by the above argument that
X_{1/2-c}
has full probability with respect to such Gibbs measure.
∎
