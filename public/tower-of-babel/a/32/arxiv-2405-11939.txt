Abstract
A singularly perturbed reaction-diffusion problem posed on the unit square in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} is solved numerically by a local discontinuous Galerkin (LDG) finite element method. Typical solutions of this class of 2D problems exhibit boundary layers along the sides of the domain; these layers generally cause difficulties for numerical methods. Our LDG method handles the boundary layers by using a Shishkin mesh and also introducing the new concept of a “layer-upwind flux”—a discrete flux whose values are chosen on the fine mesh (which lies inside the boundary layers) in the direction where the layer weakens. On the coarse mesh, one can use a standard central flux. No penalty terms are needed with these fluxes, unlike many other variants of the LDG method. Our choice of discrete flux makes it feasible to derive an optimal-order error analysis in a balanced norm; this norm is stronger than the usual energy norm and is a more appropriate measure for errors in computed solutions for singularly perturbed reaction-diffusion problems. It will be proved that the LDG method is usually convergent of order O ⁡ ( ( N − 1 ​ ln ⁡ N ) k + 1 ) O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1}) in the balanced norm, where N N is the number of mesh intervals in each coordinate direction and tensor-product piecewise polynomials of degree k k in each coordinate variable are used in the LDG method. This result is the first of its kind for the LDG method applied to this class of problem and is optimal for convergence on a Shishkin mesh. Its sharpness is confirmed by numerical experiments.

Introduction

Consider the singularly perturbed reaction-diffusion problem

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\Delta u(x,y)+b(x,y)u(x,y)

\displaystyle=f(x,y)\ \text{ in }\Omega:=(0,1)\times(0,1),

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0\hskip 28.45274pt\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

1.1
Balanced norms

Unlike singularly perturbed convection-diffusion problems, the natural energy norm
associated with (
1.1
) is too weak to capture the contribution of
the layer component of the true solution, as was pointed out in
[
23
]
,
which advocated the replacement of the energy norm by a stronger so-called
balanced norm
.
In this new norm, the
H^{1}(\Omega)
component of the energy norm is rescaled in such a way
that the layer and smooth components of the true solution
u

of (
1.1
) have the same order of magnitude with respect to
\varepsilon
.

Balanced-norm error analyses have attracted much attention since the appearance of
[
23
]
.
These analyses can be divided into two classes:

(i)

Use a standard numerical method but carry out the error analysis in
a balanced norm, e.g.,
C^{0}
-interior penalty method
[
30
]
,

hp
-finite element method (FEM) on spectral boundary layer meshes
[
27
]
,
streamline-diffusion FEM
[
16
]
and a finite volume method
[
28
]
.
Note however that it is usually troublesome
to coax a balanced-norm error bound from a method that fits more naturally with a
standard energy norm.

(ii)

Construct a new numerical method that leads naturally to a balanced norm,
e.g., mixed FEM
[
23
]
, dual FEM
[
4
]
,
discontinuous Petrov-Galerkin method
[
20
]
,
and an exponentially weighted FEM
[
3
,
26
]
.

1.2
The LDG method

The local discontinuous Galerkin (LDG) method
[
13
]

is a popular FEM that uses discontinuous piecewise polynomial spaces for the trial and test solutions.
Its basic idea is to rewrite the second-order differential equation as an equivalent first-order system
to which one then applies the original DG method
[
29
]
.
In the LDG method one must specify a numerical flux on the boundary of each mesh element;
if this is done appropriately, then the auxiliary variables that approximate the derivatives of the solution
can be eliminated locally, which explains the name LDG.
The LDG method is superior to many other numerical methods
because of its strong stability, high-order accuracy, flexibility for
hp
-adaptivity, and high parallelizability.
It is well suited to problems whose solutions have boundary layers.
Furthermore, it can produce high-order accuracy for both the solution itself and its gradient;
the latter quantity is important in fluid dynamics applications
where values of pressure gradients are desired.

The LDG method has been used to solve various
singularly perturbed problems with boundary layers

[
7
,
8
,
9
,
22
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
and
several energy-norm convergence results have been derived;
in these papers the numerical flux is usually either a penalty flux
at all element interfaces or an alternating flux at the interior element
interfaces combined with a penalty flux at the domain boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Nevertheless, only a few
balanced-norm error bounds are known for LDG solutions of
singularly perturbed reaction-diffusion problems.
We now describe these results.

In
[
6
]
a local
L^{2}
projection and an inverse inequality were used
to derive a balanced-norm error estimate for a two-parameter singularly perturbed
two-point boundary value problem
which is of reaction-diffusion type when the convection coefficient parameter is zero;
a suboptimal convergence rate of order
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k})
was obtained
for the LDG method with a global penalty flux on element interfaces,
where
N
is the number of mesh elements and

k
is the degree of the piecewise polynomials in the FEM space.
Subsequently, in
[
11
]
an LDG method with interior alternating fluxes
and penalty fluxes at the boundary
\partial\Omega
was considered.
Different projectors for the smooth and layer components of the solution
were employed, improving the convergence rate on the elements adjacent to the transition points;
consequently the final convergence order was improved by one-half.
Under the restrictive assumption that the regular component of the solution
belongs to the FEM space,
the convergence rate was proved to be the optimal rate
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1})
.
But despite this progress, a gap still remained between the theoretical and numerical results.

1.3
Choice of numerical flux in the LDG method

In
[
10
]
we recently applied the LDG method
on a Shishkin mesh to solve a one-dimensional reaction-diffusion problem,
using a central flux at interior element interfaces and a penalty flux at the boundary.
By using the nodal superconvergence of the average of the local
L^{2}
projections at the element interfaces
when adjacent elements are the same size,
an optimal-order error bound was derived in a balanced norm:
we proved
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1})
for even
k
and
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k})
for odd
k
.
These convergence rates are sharp and agree with the numerical results
for this method.

Despite this positive convergence result for a 1D problem, the LDG method with central numerical flux
at every interior element interface has some drawbacks.
First, it has a lower rate of convergence when
k
is odd.
Second, it seems to be very difficult to extend the 1D error analysis
of
[
10
]
to the 2D case,
because the obvious 2D choice of projector is
the tensor product of the 1D projectors from
[
10
]
,
but in 2D these interact with each other in the vicinity of the mesh transition interfaces
in a complicated way that seriously obstructs the error analysis.
Third, the special nodal superconvergence property of the
L^{2}
projection on locally uniform meshes,
which was used in
[
10
]
to analyse the method on a Shishkin mesh,
cannot be extended to other layer-adapted meshes such as Bakhvalov-type meshes
[
24
,
31
]
that
are not locally uniform inside the boundary layers.

To address these issues, in the present paper we introduce and analyse a new design principle
for the numerical flux in the LDG method when solving singularly perturbed problems with layers.
We call it a
layer-upwind
flux since the choice of numerical flux on each element
of the fine Shishkin mesh depends on where the layer is weakest in that element.
On the coarse part of the Shishkin mesh we use the old idea of a central flux.
With these choices of numerical fluxes, the LDG method needs
no penalty terms at the domain boundary,
unlike
[
5
,
7
,
9
,
11
,
37
,
40
]
.

The new layer-upwind flux is natural and is symmetric across
\Omega

when the layers in the true solution of (
1.1
) are symmetric,
unlike the fluxes used in
[
10
]
.
Furthermore, with this choice of flux we can derive
a balanced-norm error estimate
that achieves a nearly optimal convergence rate
for both even and odd values of
k
.
To be precise, we show
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1})
convergence in the balanced norm
for even values of
k
under the reasonable assumption that
\varepsilon^{1/2}\leq N^{-1}
; see Remark
5.3
.
For odd values of
k
, we get the same result when
\varepsilon^{1/4}\leq N^{-1}(\ln N)^{k+1}
,
and if this condition is violated (but one still has
\varepsilon^{1/2}\leq N^{-1}
),
then the balanced norm convergence rate becomes
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1}+N^{-(k+1/2)})
,
but in practice this is unlikely to happen and so
one usually sees
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1})
convergence; again see Remark
5.3
.
To our knowledge, this is the best balanced-norm error estimate result that has been proved
for any LDG method applied to a 2D reaction-diffusion singularly perturbed problem.

1.4
Complexity of the error analysis

The bilinear form
B(\cdot;\cdot)
of our LDG method satisfies

B(\bm{\mathbbm{z}};\bm{\mathbbm{z}})=\interleave\bm{\mathbbm{z}}\interleave_{E}^{2}
for all functions
\bm{\mathbbm{z}}

in the finite element trial space
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{3}
,
where
\interleave\cdot\interleave_{E}
is an associated energy norm; see Section
2.3
.
Thus, the method falls into category (i) of Section
1.1
—its error analysis
fits naturally in an energy norm framework but is challenging when
the stronger balanced norm
\interleave\cdot\interleave_{B}
is used
(see (
2.13
)),
as the only direct relationship between
B(\cdot;\cdot)

and
\interleave\cdot\interleave_{B}

introduces a factor
\varepsilon^{-1/4}
into the error estimates, which is unacceptable
since we want error bounds that prove the accuracy of the LDG solution
for all values of
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

To deal with this fundamental difficulty,
our strategy is to prove a
supercloseness
result
for the LDG solution
\bm{\mathbbm{W}}\in\mathcal{V}_{N}^{3}
:
setting
\bm{w}:=(u,\varepsilon u_{x},\varepsilon u_{y})
,
we construct a projection
\mathcal{P}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{V}_{N}^{3}
of
\bm{w}

such that (see (
5.13
)) for some constant
C
one has

\interleave\mathcal{P}\bm{w}-\bm{\mathbbm{W}}\interleave_{E}\leq C\varepsilon^{1/4}\zeta(N)\ \text{ with }\zeta(N)\to 0\text{ as }N\to\infty,

(1.2)

where
N
is the number of mesh intervals in each coordinate direction.
This implies immediately that
\interleave\mathcal{P}\bm{w}-\bm{\mathbbm{W}}\interleave_{B}\leq C\zeta(N)

and the analysis is then reduced to establishing approximation theory estimates
for
\interleave\bm{w}-\mathcal{P}\bm{w}\interleave_{B}
.

One should note that the estimate (
1.2
) is extraordinarily delicate
because of the factor
\varepsilon^{1/4}
(recall that
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
is arbitrary)
and the sharpness of the bound
\zeta(N)
, which agrees exactly with our numerical results.
Thus, the construction of the complicated projector
\mathcal{P}
requires great care.
Furthermore, to bound
\interleave\bm{w}-\mathcal{P}\bm{w}\interleave_{B}
is far from routine—one must prove
a variety of precise estimates for
\mathcal{P}
.

The projector
\mathcal{P}
is a combination of
several composite Gauss-Radau and
L^{2}
projectors
that are tailored to the form of the numerical fluxes and have good approximation properties.
They are local projectors and unlike
[
10
]
they do not
need to be coupled near the transition interfaces in the mesh.
The analyses of our earlier LDG papers

[
7
,
8
,
9
,
11
]

also used Gauss-Radau and
L^{2}
projectors, but a comparison with our work
here—note the elaborate constructions in equation (
2.9
)
and in Sections
3.1
and
4
—shows that
the present paper devises a remarkable new combination of them
and presents some innovative approximation and superapproximation properties
that are able to yield a 2D optimal-order balanced-norm error analysis
in practical situations.
Where possible we reuse old results, but almost all our analysis here is new.

It is notable that our new error analysis on the fine Shishkin mesh
makes no use of the nodal superconvergence property of the
L^{2}
projection on locally uniform meshes
that was mentioned in Section
1.3
, thereby opening
the possibility of extending this work to
other layer-adapted meshes such as Shishkin-type and Bakhvalov-type meshes.
In general, our new layer-upwinded numerical flux appears to be a
very promising tool in the application of the LDG method to other problems whose solutions have boundary layers.

2.1
Problem and solution properties

Typical solutions

When the data of (

For convenience we write

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

Under suitable smoothness and compatibility conditions on the data,
the solution

)

and can be decomposed as

where

is the regular/smooth component,

is a layer associated with the edge

and each

is a layer associated with the corner

(see Figure

The derivatives of these components satisfy the following bounds:

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{i}\partial_{y}^{j}\bar{u}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq C

\displaystyle\text{for }0\leq i+j\leq m+2,

(2.1a)

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{i}\partial_{y}^{j}u_{1}^{\rm{b}}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{-i/2}e^{-\beta x/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\text{for }0\leq i+j\leq m+1,

(2.1b)

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{i}\partial_{y}^{j}u_{1}^{\rm{c}}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{-(i+j)/2}e^{-\beta(x+y)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\text{for }0\leq i+j\leq m+1

for all

For

,

2.2
Shishkin mesh

We shall construct our LDG method on a standard piecewise-uniform Shishkin mesh
that is refined near
\partial\Omega
. It is a tensor product of one-dimensional Shishkin meshes
as described in, e.g.,
[
24
,
31
,
34
]
.

Let
N\geq 4
be a positive integer that is divisible by
4
.
Our mesh uses
N+1
points in each coordinate direction.
Define the
mesh transition parameter

\tau
by

\tau=\min\left\{\frac{\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\beta}\ln N,\frac{1}{4}\right\},

(2.2)

where
\sigma>0
is a user-chosen parameter whose value will be discussed later.

The finite element space will be defined in Section
2.3
;
it uses piecewise polynomials of degree
k\geq 0
.
Define

\hat{k}=\begin{cases}k+1&\text{for $k$ even},\\
k&\text{for $k$ odd}.\end{cases}

(2.3)

In (
2.2
) we choose
\sigma\geq\hat{k}+1\geq k+1
to facilitate our error analysis.
We make the mild assumption that

\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\left(\frac{\beta}{4\sigma\ln N}\right)^{2},\ \text{ i.e., }\ \frac{\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\beta}\ln N\leq\frac{1}{4},

(2.4)

as otherwise the mesh is sufficiently fine to resolve the boundary layers in the solution
and the error in the numerical solution can be analysed in the classical way
without any special treatment of the singularly
perturbed nature of the problem. Consequently (
2.2
) becomes

\displaystyle\tau=\frac{\sigma\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\beta}\ln N\ \text{ with }\sigma\geq\hat{k}+1.

(2.5)

Define the mesh points
(x_{i},y_{j})
for
i,j=0,1,\dots,N
by

x_{i}=y_{i}=\begin{cases}4\tau iN^{-1}&\textrm{for}\;i=0,1,\dots,N/4,\\
\tau+2(1-2\tau)(iN^{-1}-1/4)&\textrm{for}\;i=N/4+1,\dots,3N/4,\\
1-4\tau(1-iN^{-1})&\textrm{for}\;i=3N/4+1,\dots,N.\end{cases}

(2.6)

By drawing axiparallel lines through these mesh points
we construct the layer-adapted mesh: set

\Omega_{N}:=\{K_{ij}\}_{i,j=1,2,\dots,N}
, where

K_{ij}:=I_{i}\times J_{j}:=(x_{i-1},x_{i})\times(y_{j-1},y_{j})
.

The right-hand picture of Figure

,
but is refined in the layer regions

\displaystyle\Omega_{11}:=(0,\tau)\times(0,\tau),

\displaystyle\Omega_{31}:=(1-\tau,1)\times(0,\tau),

\displaystyle\Omega_{12}:=(0,\tau)\times(\tau,1-\tau),

\displaystyle\Omega_{32}:=(1-\tau,1)\times(\tau,1-\tau),

\displaystyle\Omega_{13}:=(0,\tau)\times(1-\tau,1),

\displaystyle\Omega_{33}:=(1-\tau,1)\times(1-\tau,1),

\displaystyle\Omega_{21}:=(\tau,1-\tau)\times(0,\tau),

\displaystyle\Omega_{23}:=(\tau,1-\tau)\times(1-\tau,1).

\Omega_{i}^{x}:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{3}\Omega_{ij}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Omega_{j}^{y}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}\Omega_{ij}\ \text{ for }i,j=1,2,3.

2.3
The LDG method with layer-upwind flux

Let
k\geq 0
be a fixed integer, chosen by the user.
On each bounded interval
I=(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}
,
let
\mathcal{P}^{k}(I)
be the space of polynomials of degree at most
k
defined on
I
.
For each
K=I_{i}\times J_{j}
, set
\mathcal{Q}^{k}(K)=\mathcal{P}^{k}(I_{i})\otimes\mathcal{P}^{k}(J_{j})
.
Then define the piecewise continuous finite element space

\mathcal{V}_{N}=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\colon v|_{K}\in\mathcal{Q}^{k}(K)\,\forall K\in\Omega_{N}\}.

Functions in
\mathcal{V}_{N}
may be discontinuous across element interfaces.
For each
v\in\mathcal{V}_{N}
and
y\in J_{j}
, for
j=1,2,\dots,N

we use
v^{\pm}_{i,y}(y)=\lim_{x\to x_{i}^{\pm}}v(x,y)

and
v^{\pm}_{x,j}(x)=\lim_{y\to y_{j}^{\pm}}v(x,y)

to express the traces on element edges.
Furthermore, for
i=1,2,\dots,N-1
define the jump
[\![v]\!]_{i,y}(y)

and the average
\{\!\{v\}\!\}_{i,y}(y)
by

\displaystyle[\![v]\!]_{i,y}(y)

\displaystyle:=v_{i,y}^{+}(y)-v_{i,y}^{-}(y)

and

\displaystyle\ \{\!\{v\}\!\}_{i,y}(y):=\frac{1}{2}\big(v_{i,y}^{+}(y)+v_{i,y}^{-}(y)\big);

\displaystyle\text{with}\ [\![v]\!]_{0,y}(y)

\displaystyle:=v_{0,y}^{+}(y)=:\{\!\{v\}\!\}_{0,y}(y)

and

\displaystyle\ \{\!\{v\}\!\}_{N,y}(y):=v_{N,y}^{-}(y)=:-[\![v]\!]_{N,y}(y).

In a similar fashion, for each
x\in I_{i}
and
i=1,2,\dots,N
,
define the traces
v_{x,j}^{\pm}(x)
,
the jumps
[\![v]\!]_{x,j}(x)
and the averages
\{\!\{v\}\!\}_{x,j}(x)

on the horizontal edges for
j=0,1,\dots,N
.
To simplify the notation, we omit the dependent variables of these functions and simply write them as
[\![v]\!]_{i,y}
,

\{\!\{v\}\!\}_{i,y}
,
[\![v]\!]_{x,j}
and
\{\!\{v\}\!\}_{x,j}
in what follows.

To define the LDG method, rewrite (
1.1a
) as the equivalent first-order system

\displaystyle-p_{x}-q_{y}+bu=f,\quad\varepsilon^{-1}p=u_{x}\;\text{ and }\;\varepsilon^{-1}q=u_{y}\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

(2.7)

which are subject to the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition (
1.1b
).
By applying the DG method of
[
29
]

to (
2.7
) we get the LDG method,
which we now state precisely.
Find
\bm{\mathbbm{W}}=(\mathbbm{U},\mathbbm{P},\mathbbm{Q})\in\mathcal{V}_{N}^{3}:=\mathcal{V}_{N}\times\mathcal{V}_{N}\times\mathcal{V}_{N}
such that in each element
K_{ij}\in\Omega_{N}
,
the following variational equations hold true
for each
\bm{\mathbbm{z}}=(\mathbbm{v},\mathbbm{s},\mathbbm{r})\in\mathcal{V}_{N}^{3}
:

\displaystyle\left\langle b\mathbbm{U},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}+\left\langle\mathbbm{P},\mathbbm{v}_{x}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}-\left\langle\widehat{\mathbbm{P}}_{i,y},\mathbbm{v}^{-}_{i,y}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}+\left\langle\widehat{\mathbbm{P}}_{i-1,y},\mathbbm{v}^{+}_{i-1,y}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}

\displaystyle+\left\langle\mathbbm{Q},\mathbbm{v}_{y}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}-\left\langle\widehat{\mathbbm{Q}}_{x,j},\mathbbm{v}^{-}_{x,j}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}+\left\langle\widehat{\mathbbm{Q}}_{x,j-1},\mathbbm{v}^{+}_{x,j-1}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}=\left\langle f,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}},

(2.8a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-1}\left\langle\mathbbm{P},\mathbbm{s}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}+\left\langle\mathbbm{U},\mathbbm{s}_{x}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}-\left\langle\widehat{\mathbbm{U}}_{i,y},\mathbbm{s}^{-}_{i,y}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}+\left\langle\widehat{\mathbbm{U}}_{i-1,y},\mathbbm{s}^{+}_{i-1,y}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}=0,

(2.8b)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-1}\left\langle\mathbbm{Q},\mathbbm{r}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}+\left\langle\mathbbm{U},\mathbbm{r}_{y}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}-\left\langle\widehat{\mathbbm{U}}_{x,j},\mathbbm{r}^{-}_{x,j}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}+\left\langle\widehat{\mathbbm{U}}_{x,j-1},\mathbbm{r}^{+}_{x,j-1}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}=0,

(2.8c)

where for each
y\in J_{j}~(j=1,2,\dots,N)
and
x\in I_{i}~(i=1,2,\dots,N)

the numerical fluxes in (
2.8
) are defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbbm{U}}_{i,y}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&i=0,\\
\mathbbm{U}^{-}_{i,y},&i=1,\dots,N/4,\\
\{\!\{\mathbbm{U}\}\!\}_{i,y},&i=N/4+1,\dots,3N/4-1,\\
\mathbbm{U}^{+}_{i,y},&i=3N/4,\dots,N-1,\\
0,&i=N,\end{cases}

(2.9a)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbbm{P}}_{i,y}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathbbm{P}^{+}_{i,y},&i=0,1,\dots,N/4,\\
\{\!\{\mathbbm{P}\}\!\}_{i,y},&i=N/4+1,\dots,3N/4-1,\\
\mathbbm{P}^{-}_{i,y},&i=3N/4,\dots,N-1,N,\\
\end{cases}

(2.9b)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbbm{U}}_{x,j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&j=0,\\
\mathbbm{U}^{-}_{x,j},&j=1,\dots,N/4,\\
\{\!\{\mathbbm{U}\}\!\}_{x,j},&j=N/4+1,\dots,3N/4-1,\\
\mathbbm{U}^{+}_{x,j},&j=3N/4,\dots,N-1,\\
0,&j=N,\end{cases}

(2.9c)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbbm{Q}}_{x,j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathbbm{Q}^{+}_{x,j},&j=0,1,\dots,N/4,\\
\{\!\{\mathbbm{Q}\}\!\}_{x,j},&j=N/4+1,\dots,3N/4-1,\\
\mathbbm{Q}^{-}_{x,j},&j=3N/4,\dots,N-1,N.\\
\end{cases}

(2.9d)

Remark 2.2
.

In these definitions, note the unusual choice of
\widehat{\mathbbm{P}}_{i,y}

(and similarly
\widehat{\mathbbm{Q}}_{x,j}
) for the cases

i=1,\dots,N/4
and
i=3N/4,\dots,N-1
: here we specify the value of
\widehat{\mathbbm{P}}_{i,y}

using values of
\widehat{\mathbbm{P}}
on adjacent elements
that lie further from the boundary
\partial\Omega
, i.e., that lie in the direction where the layer weakens.
This choice is reminiscent of the technique of “upwinding” approximations of 1st-order derivatives when one
computes solutions of convection-dominated problems, and
\widehat{\mathbbm{P}}
is an approximation
of the 1st-order derivative
\varepsilon u_{x}
, so we call the numerical flux in (
2.9
)
a
layer-upwind
flux. On the coarse mesh (
i=N/4+1,\dots,3N/4-1
) we use a standard central flux
with the aim of deriving a sharper convergence result on
the coarse mesh domain
\Omega_{22}
; cf.
[
10
]
.

Our layer-upwind flux works well from both the practical and theoretical points of view:
it often yields better accuracy than other choices such as a globally central flux or an alternating flux,
and it enables us to prove an optimal-order error bound in a balanced norm.

Note that our LDG method has a certain degree of symmetry; furthermore, our method
does not use any penalty terms when defining the flux
for singularly perturbed problems, unlike many other LDG papers
[
7
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
40
]
.

To write the LDG method in a compact form, sum (
2.8
) over all
K_{ij}\in\Omega_{N}
: Find
\bm{\mathbbm{W}}=(\mathbbm{U},\mathbbm{P},\mathbbm{Q})\in\mathcal{V}_{N}^{3}
such that

B(\bm{\mathbbm{W}};\bm{\mathbbm{z}})=\left\langle f,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle
for all
\bm{\mathbbm{z}}=(\mathbbm{v},\mathbbm{s},\mathbbm{r})\in\mathcal{V}_{N}^{3}
,
where

\displaystyle B(\bm{\mathbbm{W}};\bm{\mathbbm{z}}):=\left\langle b\mathbbm{U},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle+\varepsilon^{-1}\left\langle\mathbbm{P},\mathbbm{s}\right\rangle+\varepsilon^{-1}\left\langle\mathbbm{Q},\mathbbm{r}\right\rangle

(2.10)

\displaystyle+\left\langle\mathbbm{U},\mathbbm{s}_{x}\right\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\left\langle\widehat{\mathbbm{U}}_{i,y},[\![\mathbbm{s}]\!]_{i,y}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}+\left\langle\mathbbm{U},\mathbbm{r}_{y}\right\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\left\langle\widehat{\mathbbm{U}}_{x,j},[\![\mathbbm{r}]\!]_{x,j}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}

\displaystyle+\left\langle\mathbbm{P},\mathbbm{v}_{x}\right\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\left\langle\widehat{\mathbbm{P}}_{i,y},[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{i,y}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}+\left\langle\mathbbm{P}_{0,y}^{+},\mathbbm{v}_{0,y}^{+}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}-\left\langle\mathbbm{P}_{N,y}^{-},\mathbbm{v}_{N,y}^{-}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}\right]

\displaystyle+\left\langle\mathbbm{Q},\mathbbm{v}_{y}\right\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\left\langle\widehat{\mathbbm{Q}}_{x,j},[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{x,j}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}+\left\langle\mathbbm{Q}_{x,0}^{+},\mathbbm{v}_{x,0}^{+}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}-\left\langle\mathbbm{Q}_{x,N}^{-},\mathbbm{v}_{x,N}^{-}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}\right]

and the “hat” terms are defined in (
2.9
).

Using the notation of (
2.7
), we write the solution of (
1.1
)
as
\bm{w}:=(u,p,q)
, analogously to
\bm{\mathbbm{W}}=(\mathbbm{U},\mathbbm{P},\mathbbm{Q})
.
One can then define
B(\bm{w};\bm{\mathbbm{z}})
for all
\bm{\mathbbm{z}}\in\mathcal{V}_{N}^{3}
analogously to (
2.10
)
and the consistency of the LDG scheme follows:
for the solution
\bm{w}\in C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})\times C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})\times C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
,

B(\bm{w};\bm{\mathbbm{z}})=\left\langle f,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle\quad\text{ for all }\bm{\mathbbm{z}}=(\mathbbm{v},\mathbbm{s},\mathbbm{r})\in\mathcal{V}_{N}^{3}.

Equivalently, we have the Galerkin orthogonality property

\displaystyle B(\bm{w}-\bm{\mathbbm{W}};\bm{\mathbbm{z}})=0\quad\text{ for all }\bm{\mathbbm{z}}=(\mathbbm{v},\mathbbm{s},\mathbbm{r})\in\mathcal{V}_{N}^{3}.

(2.11)

Define an energy norm
\interleave\cdot\interleave_{E}
on
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{3}

by
\interleave\bm{\mathbbm{z}}\interleave_{E}^{2}=B(\bm{\mathbbm{z}};\bm{\mathbbm{z}})
for each
\bm{\mathbbm{z}}=(\mathbbm{v},\mathbbm{s},\mathbbm{r})\in\mathcal{V}_{N}^{3}
.
Using integration by parts and the simple identities

[\![\mathbbm{v}\mathbbm{r}]\!]=\mathbbm{v}^{-}[\![\mathbbm{r}]\!]+\mathbbm{r}^{+}[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]=\mathbbm{v}^{+}[\![\mathbbm{r}]\!]+\mathbbm{r}^{-}[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]=\{\!\{\mathbbm{v}\}\!\}[\![\mathbbm{r}]\!]+\{\!\{\mathbbm{r}\}\!\}[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]

at each interior element interface, one obtains

\interleave\bm{\mathbbm{z}}\interleave_{E}^{2}=\varepsilon^{-1}\left(\left\|\mathbbm{s}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\mathbbm{r}\right\|^{2}\right)+\left\|b^{1/2}\mathbbm{v}\right\|^{2}.

(2.12)

One can apply this definition also to
\bm{w}=(u,p,q)
:

\interleave\bm{w}\interleave_{E}^{2}=\varepsilon^{-1}\left(\left\|p\right\|^{2}+\left\|q\right\|^{2}\right)+\left\|b^{1/2}u\right\|^{2}.

The norm
\interleave\cdot\interleave_{E}
seems natural for our LDG method, but
(recall the decomposition
u=\bar{u}+u_{\varepsilon}
of Lemma
2.1
)
the contribution to
\interleave\bm{w}\interleave_{E}
of its layer component

{\bm{w}}_{\varepsilon}:=(u_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon\partial_{x}u_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon})

is essentially lost when
\varepsilon
is small
because a short calculation shows that

\interleave{\bm{w}}_{\varepsilon}\interleave_{E}=O(\varepsilon^{1/4})
, which vanishes as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
To address this shortcoming of the energy norm,
we change the scaling
\varepsilon^{-1}
of the auxiliary variable terms to
\varepsilon^{-3/2}

to define the
balanced norm

\interleave\cdot\interleave_{B}
by
(cf.
[
11
,
20
,
23
,
30
]
)

\displaystyle\interleave\bm{\mathbbm{z}}\interleave_{B}^{2}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-3/2}\left(\left\|\mathbbm{s}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\mathbbm{r}\right\|^{2}\right)+\left\|b^{1/2}\mathbbm{v}\right\|^{2}.

(2.13a)

and

\displaystyle\interleave\bm{w}\interleave_{B}^{2}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-3/2}\left(\left\|p\right\|^{2}+\left\|q\right\|^{2}\right)+\left\|b^{1/2}u\right\|^{2}.

(2.13b)

This norm treats both components of the true solution equally: a calculation shows that

\interleave{\bm{w}}_{\varepsilon}\interleave_{B}=O(1)
and also
\interleave\bar{\bm{w}}\interleave_{B}=O(1)

for the regular component

\bar{\bm{w}}:=(\bar{u},\varepsilon\partial_{x}\bar{u},\varepsilon\partial_{y}\bar{u})

of
\bm{w}
.

3
Projectors: stability and approximation properties

We now begin our error analysis of the LDG method (
2.8
)
using the balanced norm (
2.13
).
In Section
3
we define various projectors that will be used in our error analysis,
then investigate their basic approximation properties.
(Superapproximation properties of the projectors will be described in Section
4
.)
It takes some time to establish these results, but they are needed for the error analysis of
Section
5
.

3.1
Construction of projectors

We begin by introducing some 1D projectors that are used later
to define various 2D projectors.
Let
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
Define the
L^{2}
-projector
\pi:L^{2}(I_{i})\to\mathcal{P}^{k}(I_{i})
for each
z\in L^{2}(I_{i})
by

\left\langle\pi z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}=\left\langle z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}
for all
\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(I_{i})
.
Then define two Gauss-Radau projectors
\pi^{\pm}:C(\bar{I}_{i})\to\mathcal{P}^{k}(I_{i})
by

\displaystyle\left\langle\pi^{+}z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}\ \forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(I_{i})\ \text{ and }\ (\pi^{+}z)^{+}_{i-1}=z^{+}_{i-1};

\displaystyle\left\langle\pi^{-}z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}\ \forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(I_{i})\ \text{ and }\ (\pi^{-}z)^{-}_{i}=z^{-}_{i}.

Let
K_{ij}=I_{i}\times J_{j}
be a mesh rectangle.
The two-dimensional
L^{2}
-projector

\Pi:L^{2}(K_{ij})\to\mathcal{Q}^{k}(K_{ij})
is defined for each
z\in L^{2}(K_{ij})
by

\displaystyle\left\langle\Pi z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}=\left\langle z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}\quad\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{Q}^{k}(K_{ij}).

(3.1a)

Define the following tensor-product projectors mapping from

C(\bar{K}_{ij})
to
\mathcal{Q}^{k}(K_{ij})
:

\displaystyle\Pi_{x}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\pi^{+}_{x}\otimes\pi_{y},

\displaystyle\Pi_{x}^{-}:=\pi^{-}_{x}\otimes\pi_{y},

\displaystyle\Pi_{y}^{+}:=\pi_{x}\otimes\pi^{+}_{y},

\displaystyle\Pi_{y}^{-}:=\pi_{x}\otimes\pi^{-}_{y};

(3.1b)

\displaystyle\Pi_{xy}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\pi^{+}_{x}\otimes\pi^{+}_{y},

\displaystyle\Pi_{xy}^{\pm}:=\pi^{+}_{x}\otimes\pi^{-}_{y},

\displaystyle\Pi_{xy}^{\mp}:=\pi^{-}_{x}\otimes\pi^{+}_{y},

\displaystyle\Pi_{xy}^{-}:=\pi^{-}_{x}\otimes\pi^{-}_{y},

(3.1c)

where a subscript
x
or
y
of the 1D projectors
\pi,\pi^{-}
and
\pi^{+}

refers to the spatial coordinate in which this projector is applied.
These 2D projectors can be described as local Gauss-Radau projectors;
several of them were discussed in

[
7
,
8
,
9
,
11
,
40
]
.
To analyse them systematically, we regard them as
“vertex-edge-element approximation” operators
and divide them into three categories that are
indicated by the symbols “o”, “x” and “
\bullet
” in Figure
2
, as we now explain.

(1) The symbol “o” indicates “element approximation”,
which corresponds to
the
L^{2}
projector
\Pi
satisfying the element orthogonality condition (
3.1a
).

(2) The symbol “x” indicates “edge-element approximation”,
which corresponds to the projectors

\Pi_{x}^{+},\Pi_{x}^{-},\Pi_{y}^{+}
and
\Pi_{y}^{-}

satisfying respectively

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left\langle\Pi_{x}^{+}z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}=\left\langle z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}&\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(I_{i})\otimes\mathcal{P}^{k}(J_{j}),\\
\left\langle(\Pi_{x}^{+}z)_{i-1,y}^{+},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}=\left\langle z^{+}_{i-1,y},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}&\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(J_{j});\end{cases}

(3.2a)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left\langle\Pi_{x}^{-}z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}=\left\langle z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}&\qquad\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(I_{i})\otimes\mathcal{P}^{k}(J_{j}),\\
\left\langle(\Pi_{x}^{-}z)_{i,y}^{-},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}=\left\langle z^{-}_{i,y},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}&\qquad\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(J_{j});\end{cases}

(3.2b)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left\langle\Pi_{y}^{+}z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}=\left\langle z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}&\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(I_{i})\otimes\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(J_{j}),\\
\left\langle(\Pi_{y}^{+}z)_{x,j-1}^{+},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}=\left\langle z_{x,j-1}^{+},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}&\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(I_{i});\end{cases}

(3.2c)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left\langle\Pi_{y}^{-}z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}=\left\langle z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}&\qquad\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(I_{i})\otimes\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(J_{j}),\\
\left\langle(\Pi_{y}^{-}z)_{x,j}^{-},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}=\left\langle z_{x,j}^{-},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}&\qquad\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(I_{i}).\end{cases}

(3.2d)

(3) The symbol “
\bullet
” indicates “vertex-edge-element approximation”, which corresponds to the projectors

\Pi_{xy}^{+},\Pi_{xy}^{\pm},\Pi_{xy}^{\mp}
and
\Pi_{xy}^{-}

satisfying respectively

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left\langle\Pi_{xy}^{+}z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}=\left\langle z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}&\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{Q}^{k-1}(K_{ij});\\
\left\langle(\Pi_{xy}^{+}z)_{i-1,y}^{+},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}=\left\langle z_{i-1,y}^{+},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}&\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(J_{j});\\
\left\langle(\Pi_{xy}^{+}z)_{x,j-1}^{+},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}=\left\langle z_{x,j-1}^{+},\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{I_{i}}&\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(I_{i});\\

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left\langle\Pi_{xy}^{\pm}z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}=\left\langle z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}&\quad\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{Q}^{k-1}(K_{ij});\\

3.2
Stability and approximation properties of projectors

Let
\mathcal{R}\in\{\Pi,\Pi_{x}^{-},\Pi_{x}^{+},\Pi_{y}^{-},\Pi_{y}^{+},\Pi_{xy}^{+},\Pi_{xy}^{\pm},\Pi_{xy}^{\mp},\Pi_{xy}^{-}\}

Similarly to
[
8
, Lemma 5]
,
one has the following stability properties in the
L^{\infty}
and
L^{2}
norms
(here
C>0
is some generic constant as described at the end of Section

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{R}z\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{ij})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|z\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{ij})},

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi z\right\|_{K_{ij}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|z\right\|_{K_{ij}},

(3.4b)

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi^{-}_{x}z\right\|_{K_{ij}}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big[\left\|z\right\|_{K_{ij}}+h^{1/2}_{i}\left\|z^{-}_{i,y}\right\|_{J_{j}}\Big],

(3.4c)

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi^{-}_{y}z\right\|_{K_{ij}}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big[\left\|z\right\|_{K_{ij}}+h^{1/2}_{j}\left\|z^{-}_{x,j}\right\|_{I_{i}}\Big],

(3.4d)

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi^{-}_{xy}z\right\|_{K_{ij}}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big[\left\|z\right\|_{K_{ij}}+h^{1/2}_{j}\left\|z^{-}_{x,j}\right\|_{I_{i}}+h^{1/2}_{i}\left\|z^{-}_{i,y}\right\|_{J_{j}}+(h_{i}h_{j})^{1/2}|z^{-}_{ij}|\Big],

(3.4e)

where
z^{-}_{ij}:=z(x_{i}^{-},y_{j}^{-})
.
Analogous bounds are valid for the remaining projectors

\Pi_{x}^{+}
,
\Pi_{y}^{+}
,
\Pi_{xy}^{\pm}
,
\Pi_{xy}^{\mp}
and
\Pi_{xy}^{+}
. Furthermore,
one has the following approximation properties
(see, e.g.,
[
8
, Lemma 3]
and
[
40
, Lemma 4.3]

for the case

, and
[

, Lemma 3.8]
and
[

for the case

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
K_{ij},K_{i+1,j}\in\Omega_{N}
with
h_{i}=h_{i+1}
.
Let
z\in C^{k+2}(\bar{K}_{ij}\cup\bar{K}_{i+1,j})
.
Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{N}
one has

\displaystyle\left|\int_{J_{j}}\{\!\{z-\Pi z\}\!\}_{i,y}[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{i,y}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left[h_{i}^{\hat{k}}\left\|\partial_{x}^{\hat{k}+1}z\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.+h_{i}^{k+1}\left\|\partial_{x}^{k+2}z\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}\right]\left\|\mathbbm{v}\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}.

Analogously, if
z\in C^{k+2}(\bar{K}_{ij}\cup\bar{K}_{i,j+1})
with
h_{j}=h_{j+1}
, then

\displaystyle\left|\int_{I_{i}}\{\!\{z-\Pi z\}\!\}_{x,j}[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{x,j}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left[h_{j}^{\hat{k}}\left\|\partial_{y}^{\hat{k}+1}z\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i,j+1}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.+h_{j}^{k+1}\left\|\partial_{y}^{k+2}z\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i,j+1}}\right]\left\|\mathbbm{v}\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i,j+1}}.

Proof.

We prove only the first inequality as the second is derived similarly.
For
m=0,1,\dots
, let
\ell_{m}(x)
be the rescaled Legendre polynomial of degree
m

defined via Rodrigues’ formula for the standard Legendre polynomial
\hat{\ell}_{m}(s)

on the interval
\hat{I}:=[-1,1]
, namely,

\displaystyle\ell_{m}(x)=\hat{\ell}_{m}\left(\frac{2x-x_{i}-x_{i-1}}{h_{i}}\right)=\hat{\ell}_{m}(s)=\frac{1}{2^{m}m!}\partial_{s}^{m}(s^{2}-1)^{m}.

(4.1)

Then one has

\left\|\hat{\ell}_{m}\right\|_{\hat{I}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{2m+1}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\|\ell_{m}\right\|_{I_{i}}=\sqrt{\frac{h_{i}}{2m+1}}.

(4.2)

Since
z\in C^{k+2}(\bar{K}_{ij})
, by
[
35
, Theorem 3.5]

an expansion in rescaled Legendre polynomials for each
y\in J_{j}

yields
z(x,y)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{y;m}^{ij}(y)\ell_{m}(x)
,
where for
m=0,1,\dots
one defines

\alpha_{y;m}^{ij}(y):=\frac{1}{\left\|\ell_{m}\right\|^{2}_{I_{i}}}\int_{I_{i}}z(x,y)\ell_{m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
.
But for each
x\in I_{i}
one also has the similar expansion

z(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{x;n}^{ij}(x)\ell_{n}(y)
,
where for each
n
one sets

\alpha_{x;n}^{ij}(x):=\frac{1}{\left\|\ell_{n}\right\|^{2}_{J_{j}}}\int_{J_{j}}z(x,y)\ell_{n}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
.
In these expansions,

|\alpha_{y;m}^{ij}(y)|=O(m^{-(k+5/2)})
and
|\alpha_{x;n}^{ij}(x)|=O(n^{-(k+5/2)})
for
m,n\geq k+3

follow from
z\in C^{k+2}(\bar{K}_{ij})
and
[
35
, Theorem 3.1]
.
Hence for each
m
one gets

\displaystyle\alpha_{y;m}^{ij}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left\|\ell_{m}\right\|^{2}_{I_{i}}}\int_{I_{i}}z(x,y)\ell_{m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left\|\ell_{m}\right\|^{2}_{I_{i}}}\int_{I_{i}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{x;n}^{ij}(x)\ell_{n}(y)\ell_{m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left\|\ell_{m}\right\|^{2}_{I_{i}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{I_{i}}\alpha_{x;n}^{ij}(x)\ell_{m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\ell_{n}(y),

as the uniform convergence of the series allows us to interchange the integration and the sum.
Recalling the definition of
\alpha_{x;n}^{ij}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{y;m}^{ij}(y)=\frac{1}{\left\|\ell_{m}\right\|^{2}_{I_{i}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\left\|\ell_{n}\right\|^{2}_{J_{j}}}\left(\int_{K_{ij}}z(x,y)\ell_{m}(x)\ell_{n}(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\ell_{n}(y).

Substituting this identity into
z(x,y)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{y;m}^{ij}(y)\ell_{m}(x)
, we obtain finally

z(x,y)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{ij}_{mn}\ell_{m}(x)\ell_{n}(y)\ \text{ for }(x,y)\in K_{ij},

(4.3)

where the coefficients are

\alpha^{ij}_{mn}:=\frac{1}{\left\|\ell_{m}\right\|^{2}_{I_{i}}\left\|\ell_{n}\right\|^{2}_{J_{j}}}\int_{K_{ij}}z(x,y)\ell_{m}(x)\ell_{n}(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\ \text{ for }m,n=0,1,\dots

(4.4)

Now the definition of
\Pi
implies that

\Pi z(x,y)|_{K_{ij}}=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\sum_{n=0}^{k}\alpha^{ij}_{mn}\ell_{m}(x)\ell_{n}(y).

Hence

\displaystyle(z-\Pi z)(x,y)|_{K_{ij}}=\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\alpha^{ij}_{mn}\ell_{m}(x)\ell_{n}(y)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{ij}_{mn}\ell_{m}(x)\ell_{n}(y).

Since
\hat{\ell}_{m}(\pm 1)=(\pm 1)^{m}
, the average

\displaystyle\{\!\{z-\Pi z\}\!\}_{i,y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[(z-\Pi z)^{-}_{i,y}+(z-\Pi z)^{+}_{i,y}\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\big[\alpha^{ij}_{mn}+\alpha^{i+1,j}_{mn}(-1)^{m}\big]\ell_{n}(y)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big[\alpha^{ij}_{k+1,n}+\alpha^{i+1,j}_{k+1,n}(-1)^{k+1}\big]\ell_{n}(y)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=k+2}^{\infty}\big[\alpha^{ij}_{mn}+\alpha^{i+1,j}_{mn}(-1)^{m}\big]\ell_{n}(y)

\displaystyle:=A_{1}(y)+A_{2}(y)+A_{3}(y).

(4.5)

The pairwise orthogonality property
\int_{J_{j}}\ell_{n}(y)\ell_{r}(y)\,dy=0
for
n\neq r
implies that

\int_{J_{j}}A_{1}(y)[\![v]\!]_{i,y}\,\mathrm{d}y=0\ \text{ for each }\ [\![v]\!]_{i,y}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(J_{j}).

(4.6)

We turn next to
\int_{J_{j}}A_{2}(y)[\![v]\!]_{i,y}\,\,\mathrm{d}y

Let

be the reference element and set

By (
4.4

one has

\displaystyle\left|\alpha^{ij}_{k+1,n}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[(z-\Pi_{y}^{-}z)^{-}_{i,y}+(z-\Pi_{y}^{-}z)^{+}_{i,y}\Big]

\displaystyle\big[\alpha^{ij}_{mn}+\alpha^{i+1,j}_{mn}(-1)^{m}\big]\ell_{n}(y)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}\big[\alpha^{ij}_{mn}+\alpha^{i+1,j}_{mn}(-1)^{m}\big]\ell_{n}(y)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\left[\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{ij}_{mn}+\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{i+1,j}_{mn}(-1)^{m}\right]\ell_{k}(y)

\displaystyle:=B_{1}(y)+B_{2}(y)+B_{3}(y).

The pairwise orthogonality of the rescaled Legendre polynomials forces

\int_{J_{j}}B_{1}(y)[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{i,y}\,\mathrm{d}y=0\ \text{ for any }[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{i,y}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(J_{j}).

(4.18)

Similarly to (
4.13
), since
h_{i}=h_{i+1}
we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{J_{j}}B_{2}(y)[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{i,y}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\int_{J_{j}}\sum_{n=0}^{k}\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}\big[\alpha^{ij}_{mn}+\alpha^{i+1,j}_{mn}(-1)^{m}\big]\ell_{n}(y)[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{i,y}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\leq Ch_{i}^{k}\left\|\partial_{x}^{k+1}z\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}\left\|\mathbbm{v}\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}.

(4.19)

Furthermore, imitating the derivation of (
4.13
), (
4.2
) and
recalling the inequalities (
4.17
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{J_{j}}B_{3}(y)[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{i,y}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\int_{J_{j}}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\left[\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{ij}_{mn}+\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{i+1,j}_{mn}(-1)^{m}\right]\ell_{k}(y)[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{i,y}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{J_{j}}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\left[\left|\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{ij}_{m,n}\right|+\left|\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{i+1,j}_{m,n}(-1)^{m}\right|\right]|\ell_{k}(y)||[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{i,y}|\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{J_{j}}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\left[\left|\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{ij}_{m,n}\right|^{2}+\left|\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{i+1,j}_{m,n}(-1)^{m}\right|^{2}\right]\ell^{2}_{k}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{J_{j}}[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]^{2}_{i,y}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\max_{0\leq m\leq k}\left[\left|\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{ij}_{mn}\right|+\left|\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{i+1,j}_{mn}(-1)^{m}\right|\right]\left\|\ell_{k}\right\|_{J_{j}}h_{i}^{-1/2}\left\|\mathbbm{v}\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{2k+1}\sqrt{\frac{h_{j}}{h_{i}}}h_{j}^{k}\left\|\partial_{y}^{k+1}z\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}\sqrt{\frac{h_{j}}{2k+1}}h_{i}^{-1/2}\left\|\mathbbm{v}\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch_{i}^{-1}h_{j}^{k+1}\left\|\partial_{y}^{k+1}z\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}\left\|\mathbbm{v}\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}.

(4.20)

Combining (
4.18
), (
4.19
) and (
4.20
) gives

\displaystyle\left|\int_{J_{j}}\{\!\{z-\Pi_{y}^{-}z\}\!\}_{i,y}[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{i,y}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left|\int_{J_{j}}B_{i}(y)[\![\mathbbm{v}]\!]_{i,y}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\leq C\left[h_{i}^{k}\left\|\partial_{x}^{k+1}z\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}+h_{i}^{-1}h_{j}^{k+1}\left\|\partial_{y}^{k+1}z\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}\right]\left\|\mathbbm{v}\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}.

Remark 5.3
.

When analysing numerical methods for solving (
1.1
), many authors
(e.g.,
[
3
,
21
,
25
]
) assume that
\varepsilon^{1/2}\leq N^{-1}

since this is usually true in practice.
With this assumption, the balanced-norm bound of Theorem
5.1
simplifies to

\displaystyle\interleave\bm{w}-\bm{\mathbbm{W}}\interleave_{B}\leq C\begin{cases}(N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1}&\text{for $k$ even};\\
(N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1}&\text{for $k$ odd and }\\
&\text{$\varepsilon^{1/4}\leq N^{-1}(\ln N)^{k+1}$};\\
(N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1}+N^{-(k+1/2)}&\text{for $k$ odd and}\\
&\text{$\varepsilon^{1/4}\geq N^{-1}(\ln N)^{k+1}$}.\end{cases}

That is, our LDG method achieves
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1})
convergence in the balanced norm
except when
k
is odd and
\varepsilon^{1/4}\geq N^{-1}(\ln N)^{k+1}
.
But this inequality, combined with our earlier mild assumption (
2.4
),
says that
N^{-1}(\ln N)^{k+1}\leq\varepsilon^{1/4}\leq\beta^{1/2}(4\sigma\ln N)^{-1/2}
.
In Table
1
, which lists some typical values of these quantities,
only for
k=1
are there entries (in bold font) with

N^{-1}(\ln N)^{k+1}\leq\beta^{1/2}(4\sigma\ln N)^{-1/2}
.
Similarly, the errors and observed rates of convergence in Table
3

depart from
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1})
convergence only in two entries (in bold font);
in these entries
\varepsilon=10^{-4}
and
N=256,512
so one has
N^{-1}<\varepsilon^{1/2}
,
which is generally regarded as not lying in the singularly perturbed regime when solving
(
1.1
) numerically. In other words, in practice
the exception mentioned above is unlikely to occur and
one usually expects to see a
balanced-norm
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1})
convergence rate
when using our LDG method to solve singularly perturbed reaction-diffusion problems.

Remark 5.4
.

With the commonly-used assumption
\varepsilon^{1/2}\leq N^{-1}
,
the energy-norm bound of Theorem
5.1
reduces to

\displaystyle\interleave\bm{w}-\bm{\mathbbm{W}}\interleave_{E}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big[\varepsilon^{1/4}(N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1}+N^{-(k+1)}\Big].

If
k
and
\varepsilon
are so small that
\varepsilon^{1/4}(\ln N)^{k+1}\ll 1
,
we would expect a convergence rate of order
O(N^{-(k+1)})

for the energy-norm error; otherwise, the error is likely to converge
at a rate of
O(\varepsilon^{1/4}(N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1})
.
These observations concur with our numerical results in Section
6
.

Remark 5.5
.

In
T_{6}
no integral terms arise from the domain boundary, which allows us to dispense with
penalty terms in the definition (
2.9
) of the numerical flux, unlike
traditional LDG methods

[
5
,
7
,
11
,
40
]
.

Remark 5.6
.

Our LDG method does not employ any penalty terms.
If one uses a numerical flux with penalty in the LDG equations (
2.8
), e.g.,

\widehat{\mathbbm{P}}^{\star}_{i,y}=\widehat{\mathbbm{P}}_{i,y}+\lambda_{i}[\![\mathbbm{U}]\!]_{i,y}

and
\widehat{\mathbbm{Q}}^{\star}_{x,j}=\widehat{\mathbbm{Q}}_{x,j}+\mu_{j}[\![\mathbbm{U}]\!]_{x,j}

for
i,j=0,1,\dots,N
,
where
\widehat{\mathbbm{P}}_{i,y}
and
\widehat{\mathbbm{Q}}_{x,j}

are defined in (
2.9
) and the penalty parameters
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{j}=\varepsilon^{1/2}
for
i,j=0,1,\dots,N
,
then modify our error analysis accordingly, one obtains an optimal-order
error estimate in the corresponding energy and balanced norms.
But the convergence rate is heavily influenced by the error from the boundary penalty terms;
consequently, the theoretical result for the
H^{1}
-error
is only a suboptimal convergence rate
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1/2})
,
which is a half-order inferior to the numerical results.

Remark 5.7
.

In our analysis we considered the standard Shishkin mesh,
whose coarse and fine components are each piecewise uniform.
If instead a randomly perturbed quasi-uniform mesh is used in
\Omega_{22}
,
then a slightly worse bound can be derived in Lemmas 4.1 and 4.3,
and finally the term
\varepsilon^{1/4}N^{-\hat{k}}
that appears in
the balanced-norm error bound will be changed to
\varepsilon^{1/4}N^{-k}
.
Because of the positive power of the small parameter,
the final convergence rate is only slightly affected.
Furthermore, our analysis
is applicable to other layer-adapted meshes such as
Bakhvalov-type meshes which are not locally uniform inside the layer regions,
but the convergence result will be significantly different.
The extension of our methodology to other types of layer-adapted meshes deserves further investigation
and we shall explore this in future work, as we state in Section
7
.

Remark 5.8
.

[non-constant
b
]
If
b\geq 2\beta^{2}>0
is non-constant
and
b\in W^{1,\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
,
then Theorem
5.1
is still valid: one alters
the error analysis by using a modification
\mathcal{I}^{\star}

of the projector
\mathcal{I}
in
\Omega_{22}
:

\mathcal{I}^{\star}u=\begin{cases}\mathcal{I}u&\text{in }\Omega\backslash\Omega_{22},\\
\Pi_{b}u&\text{in }\Omega_{22},\end{cases}

where for each
z\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
the weighted
L^{2}
projection
\Pi_{b}z\in\mathcal{V}_{N}
is defined by

\displaystyle\left\langle b\Pi_{b}z,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}=\left\langle bz,\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}\quad\forall\mathbbm{v}\in\mathcal{Q}^{k}(K_{ij})\quad\forall K_{ij}\in\Omega_{N}.

(5.18)

Then the bounds on the terms in (
5.1
) remain unchanged except for
T_{4}
and
T_{5}
.
In the subdivision of
T_{4}
, we only have a different expression
for the former two terms included in
T_{45}
, denoted by

T_{45}^{1}:=\left\langle u-\Pi_{b}u,(\xi_{p})_{x}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{22}}\text{ and }T_{45}^{2}:=\sum_{j=N/4+1}^{3N/4}\sum_{i=N/4+1}^{3N/4-1}\left\langle\{\!\{u-\Pi_{b}u\}\!\}_{i,y},[\![\xi_{p}]\!]_{i,y}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}.

We now show how to bound
T_{45}^{1}
and
T_{45}^{2}
satisfactorily.

For
T_{45}^{1}
,
on each
K_{ij}
set
b_{\mathrm{avg}}|_{K_{ij}}=(h_{i}h_{j})^{-1}\int_{K_{ij}}b(x,y)\,dx\,dy
,
so
b_{\mathrm{avg}}
is a piecewise constant approximation of
b
.
The definition (
5.18
), a Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and an inverse inequality
give us

\displaystyle\left|T_{45}^{1}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle u-\Pi_{b}u,(\xi_{p})_{x}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{22}}\right|=\left|\sum_{K_{ij}\in\Omega_{22}}\left\langle b_{\mathrm{avg}}^{-1}(b_{\mathrm{avg}}-b)(u-\Pi_{b}u),(\xi_{p})_{x}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{K_{ij}\in\Omega_{22}}\left\|b_{\mathrm{avg}}-b\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{ij})}\left\|u-\Pi_{b}u\right\|_{K_{ij}}H^{-1}\left\|\xi_{p}\right\|_{K_{ij}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|b\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\left\|u-\Pi_{b}u\right\|_{\Omega_{22}}\left\|\xi_{p}\right\|_{\Omega_{22}}\leq CN^{-(k+1)}\left\|\xi_{p}\right\|_{\Omega_{22}},

(5.19)

where we used an approximation property for the projector
\Pi_{b}

that is an analogue of (
3.6a
).

For
T_{45}^{2}
, we shall use the supercloseness result

\left\|\Pi_{b}z-\Pi z\right\|_{K_{ij}}\leq CN^{-(k+2)}\left\|z\right\|_{H^{k+1}(K_{ij})}\quad\forall K_{ij}\in\Omega_{22}.

(5.20)

To verify (
5.20
), use the definitions (
5.18
)
and (
3.1a
) to obtain

\left\langle b(\Pi_{b}z-\Pi z),\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}=\left\langle b(z-\Pi z),\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}=\left\langle(b-b_{\mathrm{avg}})(z-\Pi z),\mathbbm{v}\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}.

Take
\mathbbm{v}=\Pi_{b}z-\Pi z
and then a Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{b}z-\Pi z\right\|_{K_{ij}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(2\beta^{2})^{-1}\left\langle b(\Pi_{b}z-\Pi z),\Pi_{b}z-\Pi z\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}

\displaystyle=(2\beta^{2})^{-1}\left\langle(b-b_{\mathrm{avg}})(z-\Pi z),\Pi_{b}z-\Pi z\right\rangle_{K_{ij}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|b_{\mathrm{avg}}-b\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{ij})}\left\|z-\Pi z\right\|_{K_{ij}}\left\|\Pi_{b}z-\Pi z\right\|_{K_{ij}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|b\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}N^{-(k+2)}\left\|z\right\|_{H^{k+1}(K_{ij})}\left\|\Pi_{b}z-\Pi z\right\|_{K_{ij}}

for
K_{ij}\in\Omega_{22}
,
which leads to (
5.20
).
Now an inverse inequality gives

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{b}z-\Pi z\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{ij})}\leq CN\left\|\Pi_{b}z-\Pi z\right\|_{K_{ij}}\leq CN^{-(k+1)}\left\|z\right\|_{H^{k+1}(K_{ij})},\quad K_{ij}\in\Omega_{22}.

(5.21)

For
K_{ij},K_{i+1,j}\in\Omega_{22}
and the smooth component
\bar{u}
,
by a Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, an inverse inequality, (
5.21
)
and (
2.1a
) one has

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=N/4+1}^{3N/4}\sum_{i=N/4+1}^{3N/4-1}\left\langle\{\!\{\Pi_{b}\bar{u}-\Pi\bar{u}\}\!\}_{i,y},[\![\xi_{p}]\!]_{i,y}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{j=N/4+1}^{3N/4}\sum_{i=N/4+1}^{3N/4-1}N^{-1/2}\left\|\Pi_{b}\bar{u}-\Pi\bar{u}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j})}N^{1/2}\left\|\xi_{p}\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}

\displaystyle\leq CN^{-(k+1)}\sum_{j=N/4+1}^{3N/4}\sum_{i=N/4+1}^{3N/4-1}\left\|\bar{u}\right\|_{H^{k+1}(K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j})}\left\|\xi_{p}\right\|_{K_{ij}\cup K_{i+1,j}}\leq CN^{-k+1}\left\|\xi_{p}\right\|_{\Omega_{22}}.

Combining this inequality with (
4.15
),
and observing that (
4.16
) will hold when
\Pi
is replaced by
\Pi_{b}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|T_{45}^{2}\right|=\left|\sum_{j=N/4+1}^{3N/4}\sum_{i=N/4+1}^{3N/4-1}\left\langle\{\!\{u-\Pi_{b}u\}\!\}_{i,y},[\![\xi_{p}]\!]_{i,y}\right\rangle_{J_{j}}\right|\leq CN^{-\hat{k}}\left\|\xi_{p}\right\|_{\Omega_{22}}.

(5.22)

Adding (
5.19
), (
5.22
)
and the existing bounds for the remaining terms of
T_{45}

gives

T_{45}\leq CN^{-\hat{k}}\left\|\xi_{p}\right\|_{\Omega_{22}}

and thus the same bound for
T_{4}
.
We can bound
T_{5}
in a similar way.
Thus, we again obtain the error bound of Theorem
5.1
.

6
Numerical experiments

In this section, we present numerical results for the LDG method
on the Shishkin mesh (
2.6
) for two singularly perturbed
reaction-diffusion problems—one with a known solution and one with an unknown solution.
All the calculations were carried out in MATLAB R2017a.
The resulting discrete system of the FEM is solved directly using the Matlab backslash operator
after diagonal preconditioning of the linear system. All integrals were evaluated using the
5\times 5
Gauss-Legendre quadrature rule.

Convergence rates in the energy
and balanced norms are computed by the formula

r_{S}:=\frac{\ln(\bm{e}_{N}/\bm{e}_{2N})}{\ln(2\ln N/\ln(2N))},

where
\bm{e}_{N}
is the observed error when
N
elements are used.
This quantity
r_{S}
measures the convergence rate in the form
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{r_{S}})
,
which is natural for convergence on Shishkin meshes.

Example 6.1
.

Consider the linear constant-coefficient problem

-\varepsilon\Delta u+2u=f\text{ in }\Omega=(0,1)\times(0,1),\quad u=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

with
f
chosen such that the exact solution is

u(x,y)=\left[\frac{e^{-x/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}-e^{-(1-x)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}{1-e^{-1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}-\cos(\pi x)\right]\left[\frac{e^{-y/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}-e^{-(1-y)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}{1-e^{-1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}-\cos(\pi y)\right].

For any nonnegative integer
m
, this exact solution has precisely the layer behaviour that one assumes in Lemma
2.1
.

Fix
k=2
and
N=64
. Two numerical solutions
\mathbbm{U}
and their pointwise errors
|u-\mathbbm{U}|

are displayed in Figure
3
for
\varepsilon=10^{-4}
and
\varepsilon=10^{-8}
.
One can see that the LDG method produces an accurate solution with
sharp boundary layers and no oscillations near these layers,
demonstrating the ability of the LDG method to capture boundary layers.

In Tables
2
and
3

we present the values of

\interleave\bm{w}-\bm{\mathbbm{W}}\interleave_{E}
and
\interleave\bm{w}-\bm{\mathbbm{W}}\interleave_{B}

and their convergence rates.
The convergence rate of the energy-norm error is

O(\varepsilon^{1/4}(N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1})
in most cases, except for the data in boldface font, where
\varepsilon^{1/4}(\ln N)^{k+1}<1
and the convergence rate is
O(N^{-(k+1)})
.
This agrees with the predictions of
Theorem
5.1
and Remark
5.4
.
On the other hand, the balanced-norm errors attain the optimal order

O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1})
in all cases, which agrees with

expected orders of convergence
(EOC in our tables)
that are predicted by Theorem
5.1
.

7
Concluding remarks

In this paper we considered a singularly perturbed reaction-diffusion problem
posed on the unit square and derived an optimal-order balanced-norm error estimate for the
LDG method on Shishkin meshes by introducing layer-upwind numerical fluxes,
which are a new and very effective way of choosing numerical fluxes.
The terminology “layer-upwind” means that on the fine Shishkin mesh,
the value of the flux on each element is chosen at
the point where the layer is weakest,
somewhat analogously to the use of “upwinding” in discretisations of
singularly perturbed convection-diffusion problems.
Meanwhile, on the coarse Shishkin mesh one uses a standard central flux.
Our layer-upwind flux doesn’t require any penalty parameters at the domain boundary
and leads to optimal-order
O((N^{-1}\ln N)^{k+1})
error bounds
in a balanced norm associated with this problem.
This is the best theoretical result that has been proved for the LDG method
applied to singularly perturbed reaction-diffusion problems,
and numerical experiments show that our theoretical bounds are sharp.
In future work we aim to use similar ideas in applying the LDG method
to solve other types of singularly perturbed problems on layer-adapted meshes.
