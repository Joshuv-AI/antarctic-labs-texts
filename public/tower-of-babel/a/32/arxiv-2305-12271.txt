Electric current from Schwinger’s time-ordered propagator
Cristián Villavicencio

Abstract
The electric current density of fermions in the presence of an external electric field is determined through the utilization of a time-ordered Schwinger propagator. The study encompasses the necessary conditions for establishing a well-defined time-ordered propagator within the Schwinger formalism, specifically concentrating on constant and uniform electromagnetic fields. Within this theoretical framework, the introduction of the Schwinger phase induces a chemical potential, resulting in non-zero values for the electric current and charge number density. Consequently, the dependence of electric conductivity on the strength of the electric field and the charge number density is investigated.



Schwinger’s proper time method has been a crucial tool for studying the effect of external electromagnetic fields in quantum field theory
Schwinger 1951
.
In particular, the Green’s function constructed by this method is often used in quantum field theory for the description of particles propagating under the influence of an external magnetic field, with the versatility of being expressed as an integral in proper time, in power series of Landau levels or expanded in magnetic field powers
Chyi et al. 2000
;
Kharzeev et al. 2013
.

The effect of the electric field in the Schwinger formalism has been intensively studied due to the prediction of pair production in vacuum
Sauter 1931
;
Schwinger 1951
.
But recently, there has been a growing interest in studying the effects of the electric field in the context of relativistic heavy-ion collisions, in particular for peripheral collisions or collisions with different ions such as Au-Cu
Bzdak and Skokov 2012
;
Deng and Huang 2012
.
this has motivated the study of quark and hadronic matter under external electric and magnetic fields by investigating the modifications of different parameters in systems in thermal equilibrium

Tavares and Avancini 2018
;
Loewe and Zamora 2022
;
Endrődi and Markó 2022
;
Loewe et al. 2022a
;
Tavares et al. 2023
with particular interest in chiral restoration and deconfinement
Cao and Huang 2016
;
Tavares et al. 2020
;
Ahmad 2021
;
Loewe et al. 2022b
, as well as anomalous transport phenomena
Pu et al. 2015
;
Jiang et al. 2015
;
Huang 2016
and also analogous systems in (2+1) dimensions
Murguia et al. 2010
;
Ayala et al. 2010
;
Dudal et al. 2022
.

The problem to be studied here concerns how to regularize the Schwinger Green’s Green’s function.
Integral expressions in the Schwinger propagator are commonly regulated by an infinitesimal constant that coincides with the time-ordered regulator in vacuum.
However, this is not the general case, and there are some details that are not usually considered in the treatment of this method, as was clearly detected in
Tsamis and Woodard 2001
, concluding that the Schwinger propagator is a Green’s function, not a propagator itself, so some care must be taken when considering these Green’s functions.

The correct integration of momentum in the complex plane is full of subtleties, particularly when considering medium effects such as temperature and chemical potential. This has been addressed, also including the effects of a constant and uniform external magnetic field

Shuryak 1980
;
Chodos et al. 1990
;
Mizher et al. 2015
;
Mizher et al. 2018
.

The following discussion outlines the prerequisites for constructing Schwinger’s time-ordered propagator in the presence of constant electric and magnetic background fields.
Next, the general regularization of the propagator in the Schwinger formalism is introduced.
The specific case of external uniform electric field is obtained, ending with the derivation and analysis of the electric current density and charge density.

I
Time ordered propagator

The usual prescription for regularizing a time-ordered propagator in momentum space is to shift the momentum
p^{2}\to p^{2}+i\epsilon
in the Green’s function.
This can be considered as a general statement if the Green’s function is analytic near the poles of
p^{2}
, assuming poles on the real axis.
If the Lorentz symmetry is broken, the prescription must be generalized as
p_{0}\to p_{0}+i\epsilon p_{0}
, which must be taken into account, for example, if the chemical potential term is present
Shuryak 1980
.
This is also general if the Green’s function is analytic near the poles of
p_{0}
, assuming real poles.
In the case of the Schwinger propagator with a uniform external magnetic field and chemical potential, this regularization can be applied with some modifications in the integration in proper time
Chodos et al. 1990
.

The case of an external electric field is not as straightforward as that of a pure magnetic field, since its presence is could introduce a non-local time component in Green’s function through the Schwinger phase.
Therefore, we need to avoid this situation in order to have a well-defined time-ordered propagator.

Let’s begin by considering the Green’s function for fermions in configuration space

G(x,x^{\prime})=\langle x^{\prime}|\frac{i}{\not{\Pi}-m}|x\rangle,

(1)

where the conjugate momenta operator is defined as
\Pi=\hat{p}+qA(\hat{x})
, where
q
the charge (
q=-|e|
in the case of the electron),
\hat{x}
is the position operator,
\hat{p}
is the momentum operator and
A
is the electromagnetic vector potential.

As it was remarked in
Tsamis and Woodard 2001
, the expression in Eq. (
1
) is just a Green function.
However, we know that in the absence of external electromagnetic fields, or in the presence of an external uniform magnetic field, this Green’s function becomes local in time coordinates, so that a well-defined Fourier transform can be performed, such that the prescription
p_{0}\to p_{0}+i\epsilon p_{0}
makes sense.

If electric field is present, the locality argument is not in general valid, unless we consider the condition
\partial_{0}A(x)=0
.
In that case,
\hat{p}_{0}
commutes with all other operators, and therefore Green’s function becomes local in time coordinates.

G(x,x^{\prime})\to G(x_{0}-x_{0}^{\prime};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}).

(2)

The time fourier transform can then be performed on the Green’s function, or equivalently, a set of complete eigenstates of
\hat{p}_{0}
can be introduced, leading to

\tilde{G}(p_{0};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\int dt\,e^{ip_{0}t}\,G(t;\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}).

(3)

We know that once Schwinger’s procedure is applied, the Green’s function can be written in terms of a local function multiplied by the Schwinger’s phase, which is non-local:

G(x,x^{\prime})=e^{i\Phi(x,x^{\prime})}S(x-x^{\prime}),

(4)

where the Schwinger pase can be written as

\Phi(x,x^{\prime})=q\int_{x^{\prime}}^{x}d\xi\cdot A(\xi),\qquad\xi=xt+x^{\prime}(1-t).

(5)

Let’s see in detail what happens with the Schwinger phase.
Since we are dealing with constant electromagnetic fields, considering the imposed condition that
\partial_{0}A(x)=0
, the components of the vector potential will be

A_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})=E^{i}x_{i},\qquad A_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{ijk}B^{j}x^{k},

(6)

where the symmetric gauge for
A_{i}
was considered.
The Schwinger phase is then

\Phi(x,x^{\prime})=q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}\,(x_{0}-x^{\prime}_{0})-[q\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}]\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
is the average position.
Now we can do the Fourier transform with respect to
x-x^{\prime}
obtaining as a result a Green’s function in momentum space, but also as a function of the average position of the correlation.
The time-ordered, or Feynman propagator, is then

G_{F}(p;\boldsymbol{\bar{x}})=\tilde{S}(P_{0}+i\epsilon p_{0},\boldsymbol{P}),

(8)

where
\tilde{S}
is the Fourier transform of
S
in Eq. (
4
), with conjugate moments

P_{0}=p_{0}-q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\bar{x}},\qquad\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{p}+q\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}.

(9)

We can see how the electric field immediately introduces a chemical potential term corresponding to the Coulomb potential for a constant electric field.
This is nothing new, since the chemical potential is commonly introduced as a zero component of the gauge electromagnetic field.
However, this chemical potential is position-dependent, so we have to see how to interpret it.

II
Schwinger’s Green function regularization

The Schwinger Green function is commonly regularized by introducing an infinitesimal term to ensure that the integrals in proper time remain well-behaved and do not diverge.
The regulator will be incorporated by expressing the Green function in Eq. (
1
) as

G(x,x^{\prime})=\langle x^{\prime}|\frac{\not{\Pi}+m}{iH}|x\rangle,

(10)

where the Hamiltonian is defined as

H=-\not{\Pi}^{2}+m^{2}=-\Pi^{2}+\frac{q}{2}F_{\mu\nu}\sigma^{\mu\nu}+m^{2}.

(11)

Subsequently, Eq (
10
) can be formulated in integral form

G(x,x^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\langle x^{\prime}|[\not{\Pi}+m]e^{-isH}|x\rangle.

(12)

To integrate, avoiding divergences, a regulator is introduced by shifting
H\to H-i\eta
.
If
\eta
is positive, the convergence of the integration in
s
is guaranteed.
Alternatively, if
\eta
is negative, Eq. (
10
) can be formulated as

G(x,x^{\prime})=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}ds\langle x^{\prime}|[\not{\Pi}+m]e^{-isH}|x\rangle,

(13)

which is the same procedure as described in
Chodos et al. 1990
.
For the general case with an unknown regulator
\eta
, the Green’s function can be written as

G(x,x^{\prime})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds\,r_{s}(\eta)\,G_{s}(x,x^{\prime})

(14)

with
r_{s}
the general regulation function and
G_{s}
the integrand of Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
) which can be written as

r_{s}(\eta)=e^{-s\eta}[\theta(\eta)\theta(s)-\theta(-\eta)\theta(-s)]

(15)

\displaystyle G_{s}(x,x^{\prime})=\langle x^{\prime}(0)|[\not{\Pi}(0)-m]|x(s)\rangle.

(16)

The Green’s function can also be expressed in momentum space if we separate the correlation coordinates as
x=\bar{x}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta x
and
x^{\prime}=\bar{x}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta x
, and then we apply the Fourier transform with respect to
\Delta x
:

G(p;\bar{x})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds\,r_{s}(\eta)\,G_{s}(p;\bar{x}).

(17)

The regulator
\eta
is not restricted to being a constant, but can be a function of coordinates and/or momentum, so in principle it can be expressed differently in configuration space than in momentum space.
In fact, in the absence of external electromagnetic field, but with finite chemical potential, the regulator in momentum space is
\eta=\epsilon p_{0}(p_{0}+\mu)
.

III
Electric field

Let’s now calculate the Schwinger’s Green’s function in the presence of an external uniform electric field along the third direction
\boldsymbol{E}=E\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{3}
.
In this case, calculating the Fourier transform of the integrand of the Green’s function of Eq. (
16
), the following expression is obtained:

\begin{split}&G_{s}(p;z)=\exp\left\{is\left[\tau_{s}P_{\parallel}^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2}-m^{2}\right]\right\}\\
&\quad\quad\times\Big\{\not{P}_{\parallel}\left[1+\tanh^{2}(qEs)-2\tanh(qEs)\gamma_{0}\gamma_{3}\right]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+(\not{p}_{\perp}+m)\left[1-\tanh(qEs)\gamma_{0}\gamma_{3}\right]\Big\}\end{split}

(18)

with

\tau_{s}\equiv\tanh(qEs)/qEs

(19)

and where
z=\frac{1}{2}(x^{3}+x^{\prime 3})
,
P_{\parallel}=(p^{0}-qEz,0,0,p^{3})
and
p_{\perp}=(0,p^{1},p^{2},0)
.
The Minkowsky metric convension used is
g_{\mu\nu}=g^{\parallel}_{\mu\nu}+g^{\perp}_{\mu\nu}
.
This result is directly derived from the general Schwinger Green’s function
Schwinger 1951
;
Schwartz 2014
.

The Feynman propagator is then obtained by shifting
p_{0}\to p_{0}+i\epsilon p_{0}
.
In this case, the following is obtained:

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\epsilon\tau_{s}\,2p_{0}(p_{0}-qEz).

(20)

Since
0\leq\tau_{s}\leq 1
, this function can be absorved in the infinitesimal parameter
\epsilon
.
This regulator is the same regulator with a chemical potential.
Finally, the Feynman propagator in momentum space is

G_{F}(p;z)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds\,r_{s}\big(\epsilon p_{0}(p_{0}-qEz)\big)\,G_{s}(p;z),

(21)

IV
Electric current and charge number density

The expectation values of bilinear fermion operators are defined as

\langle\bar{\psi}(x)\,\Gamma\,\psi(x)\rangle=-\textrm{tr}[\Gamma G(x,x)],

(22)

where in this case
G
refers to a time-ordered propagator that includes interactions.
The leading order expectation values for the electric current density operator and the charge number density operator are then

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle=q\,\langle\bar{\psi}\gamma^{3}\psi\rangle=-q\,\textrm{tr}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,\gamma^{3}G_{F}(p;z),

(23)

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=\langle\psi^{\dagger}\psi\rangle=-\textrm{tr}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,\gamma^{0}G_{F}(p;z),

(24)

respectively.
Both quantities depend on the coordinate
z
.
The Coulomb potential now plays the role of a chemical potential
\mu=-qEz
, and the
z
coordinate can be understood as the distance from the source of the constant magnetic field.
In the absence of a reference system, this interpretation is ambiguous, so the
z
coordinate should be treated merely as a parameter that changes the value of the chemical potential.
Then, unambiguously, we can calculate the electric current as a function of the number density and the electric field
j(n,E)
parameterized by
z
, or equivalently, by the chemical potential.

Using Eqs. (
18
) and (
21
), tracing over spin, integrating the spatial components of momentum and redefining the integral variable
p_{0}\to p_{0}+qEz=p_{0}-\mu
, the current density and charge number density are then

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{5/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds\,f_{s}\,\frac{q^{2}E\sqrt{\tau_{s}}}{\sqrt{is}}

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{5/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds\,f_{s}\,\frac{\left[1+\tau_{s}^{2}(qEs)^{2}\right]}{2s\sqrt{\tau_{s}}\sqrt{is}}

with

f_{s}\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp_{0}\,r_{s}\big(\epsilon p_{0}(p_{0}-\mu)\big)ip_{0}\,e^{is\left[\tau_{s}\,p_{0}^{2}-m^{2}\right]},

(25)

and where
\tau_{s}
was defined in Eq. (
19
).
Integrating in
p_{0}
according to the different sign possibilities of the chemical potential and the proper time
s
, the electric current density and the charge number density becomes

\displaystyle j={}

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{sign}(\mu)}{2\pi^{5/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,g_{s}\,\frac{q^{2}E}{s^{3/2}\sqrt{\tau_{s}}},

(26)

\displaystyle n={}

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{sign}(\mu)}{2\pi^{5/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,g_{s}\,\frac{1+\tau_{s}^{2}(qEs)^{2}}{2s^{5/2}\,\tau_{s}^{3/2}},

(27)

with

g_{s}\equiv\sin\left(sm^{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)-\sin\left(s(m^{2}-\tau_{s}\mu^{2})-\frac{\pi}{4}\right),

(28)

where the phase factor
\pi/4
comes from the
\sqrt{i}
terms that appears in Ecs. (
26
) and (
27
).

V
Results

To obtain the electric current density as a function of the charge number density and the external electric field, we have to obtain the values of
n
for different values of the chemical potential, or equivalently, for the coulomb potential
-qEz
.
The integrand of the number density
n
in Eq. (
27
) is strongly divergent when
s\to 0
, making numerical integration difficult.
However, this can be handled by adding and subtracting its value in the limit
E\to 0
, but keeping the chemical potential finite

n(\mu,E)=[n(\mu,E)-n(\mu,0)]+n_{0}(\mu),

(29)

where
n_{0}
is the well known value of the number density in vacuum at finite chemical potential

n_{0}(\mu)=\frac{\epsilon(\mu)}{3\pi^{2}}\theta(|\mu|-m)(\mu^{2}-m^{2})^{3/2}.

(30)

It is more illustrative to show the electric conductivity
\sigma=j/E
.
The conductivity will be represented in one case as a function of the number density for a fixed value of the field
E
, and in the other case as a function of the external electric field for a fixed value of
n
.

Electric conductivity normalized by
q^{2}
as a function of the external electric field number in units of the fermion mass for different values of the charge density.

The case of electric current density as a function of external electric field for different values of charge number density is shown in Fig.
1
.
For all number density values, the electrical conductivity starts from the same point at
E=0
, and then begins to oscillate as
E
increases.
Let’s call this initial value as
\sigma_{0}
whose expression is

\sigma_{0}=\frac{q^{2}m}{\pi^{2}}.

(31)

Apparently, the only difference between the different values of the charge number density is a sort of phase in the oscillation around
\sigma_{0}
.
Although they appear very similar, the oscillation amplitudes vary for the different cases, and their values are almost twice as large above
\sigma_{0}
than below
\sigma_{0}
.

Electric conductivity normalized by
q^{2}
as a function of the number charge density in units of the fermion mass for different values of the external electric field.

The electrical conductivity as a function of
n
is plotted in Fig.
2
for different values of the external electric field.
It is interesting to note the case where
E=0
in which the conductivity becomes independent of
n
(gray line).
When the electric field is turned on, the value of the conductivity grows rapidly from zero to
\sigma_{0}
and then oscillates around this value.
As the external electric field increases, the wider the oscillations.

Observing the behavior of the conductivity for
E\neq 0
, it can be seen that all the curves start from
\sigma=0
when
n=0
.
Therefore, it can be deduced that the case with
n=0
and
E=0
the conductivity must be zero.
In other words, the conductivity jumps from 0 to
\sigma_{0}
as soon as a charge distribution appears in the system.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, the electric current density is obtained by means of a time-ordered Schwinger propagator for fermions immersed in a constant and uniform external electric field.
The time-ordered propagator can be well defined if we consider a time-independent electromagnetic vector potential.

In this scheme, the Schwinger phase generates a non-local chemical potential corresponding to the Coulomb potential, which depends on the mean position of the fermion correlator.
The conclusion is that the position dependence should be interpreted as a parameter that adjusts the value of the number density of charges.
This interpretation makes sense if one considers that the Coulomb potential for a constant electric field is ambiguous, and only the potential difference has physical meaning since it depends on the reference frame.
These types of interpretations are necessary because we are considering an electric field that permeates the entire space, so that no source of the field can be correctly identified or located.

The electrical conductivity obtained shows oscillations for electric field values beyond the leading order approximation.
This oscillation occurs around the conductivity in the
E\to 0
limit, which is independent of the number density, but vanishes in the absence of net charges.

Since
n
corresponds to the number of fermions minus the number of antifermions in the system, this behavior should be independent of the pair creation predicted by Schwinger’s theory for a strong electric field.

This procedure can be applied in more complicated systems without further complications.
The expectation value for operators with external electric and magnetic field at finite temperature will be reported elsewhere.
