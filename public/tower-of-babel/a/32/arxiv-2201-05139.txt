Kernel methods for long term dose response curves
Rahul Singh, Hannah Zhou

Abstract
A core challenge in causal inference is how to extrapolate long term effects, of possibly continuous actions, from short term experimental data. It arises in artificial intelligence: the long term consequences of continuous actions may be of interest, yet only short term rewards may be collected in exploration. For this estimand, called the long term dose response curve, we propose a simple nonparametric estimator based on kernel ridge regression. By embedding the distribution of the short term experimental data with kernels, we derive interpretable weights for extrapolating long term effects. Our method allows actions, short term rewards, and long term rewards to be continuous in general spaces. It also allows for nonlinearity and heterogeneity in the link between short term effects and long term effects. We prove uniform consistency, with nonasymptotic error bounds reflecting the effective dimension of the data. As an application, we estimate the long term dose response curve of Project STAR, a social program which randomly assigned students to various class sizes. We extend our results to long term counterfactual distributions, proving weak convergence.



Keywords:
continuous treatment, reproducing kernel Hilbert space, surrogate variable

1
Introduction and related work

Experimental data have randomized actions, yet they are typically limited in duration due to the expense of conducting an experiment.
From an artificial intelligence perspective, the long term consequence of an action may be of interest, yet only short term rewards may be collected in exploration.
From a program evaluation perspective, the long term effect of an action is what often matters, yet only short term outcomes may be collected in experimental surveys.

The goal of long term causal inference is to properly combine short term experimental data, which contain randomized actions, with long term observational data, which lack randomized actions
[
Prentice, 1989
,
Freedman et al., 1992
,
Begg and Leung, 2000
,
Frangakis and Rubin, 2002
]
.
Our research question is how to extrapolate long term effects of continuous actions, allowing nonlinearity and heterogeneity in the link between the short term and the long term.

We study nonparametric causal functions of a continuous action
D
, which may refer to intervention intensities, medical dosages, or program lengths. For example, Project STAR randomly assigned kindergarten students to various class sizes
D
, ranging from 12 to 28 students. The short term test scores
S
of these students were collected, however their long term earnings
Y
were not. Separately, researchers may have access to test scores
S
and earnings
Y
for students in another school district.
Several social scientists
[
Krueger and Whitmore, 2001
,
Chetty et al., 2011
]
have asked: how much would the average student benefit, in the long term, from enrolling in a certain kindergarten class size? This quantity is an example of a long term dose response
\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}\}
, where the class size is the dose, and the expected long term benefit is the response.

The difficulty in estimating long term dose response curves is the complex nonlinearity and heterogeneity in the link between short term response curve
\mathbb{E}\{S^{(d)}\}
and long term response curve
\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}\}
. For example, we would like to allow for the link between counterfactual test scores and counterfactual earnings to be nonlinear, and for students with different baseline characteristics to have different links. The identification of long term dose response curves is well known
[
Rosenman et al., 2018
,
Athey et al., 2020b
,
Athey et al., 2020a
,
Rosenman et al., 2020
,
Kallus and Mao, 2020
]
, however it appears that no nonlinear, nonparametric estimators have been previously proposed for the response curve, highlighting the difficulty of extrapolating the long term effects of continuous actions.

Our primary contribution is to propose and analyze what appears to be the first nonlinear, nonparametric estimator for the long term dose response curve.
We adapt kernel ridge regression, a classic statistical algorithm
[
Wahba, 1990
]
, to this learning problem.
By embedding the distribution of the short term experimental data using kernels, we derive interpretable weights for extrapolating long term effects from short term effects.
The final estimator has a simple closed form solution, while preserving nonlinearity and heterogeneity in the link between the short term and long term.
As an extension, we study long term counterfactual distributions in Appendix
A
.

We use the statistical framework of the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
[
Steinwart and Christmann, 2008
,
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan, 2011
]
to overcome the technical issues that arise when estimating long term dose response curves.
The identifying formula for the long term dose response curve is generally unbounded when actions are continuous
[
van der Vaart, 1991
,
Newey, 1994
]
, but we prove that it is bounded in the RKHS.
Therefore, our proposed kernel embedding of short term experimental data exists. To adjust for how experiments may be limited in not only duration but also scope, we further define and characterize a “fused” kernel embedding.

Using this embedding, we prove the estimator is uniformly consistent across actions, with finite sample rates that combine well known minimax rates for kernel ridge regression.
The rates do not directly depend on the ambient dimension of the data, but rather the effective dimension and smoothness of the data with respect to an approximating basis
[
Caponnetto and De Vito, 2007
,
Fischer and Steinwart, 2020
]
. The approximating basis is the spectrum of a kernel chosen by the researcher. See Section
3
for interpretation and comparison to Sobolev rates.

We illustrate the practicality of our approach by estimating the long term dose response of Project STAR, modelling class size as a continuous action. By allowing for continuous actions and heterogeneous links, our long term dose response estimate suggests that the effects of class size are nonlinear. Using short term experimental data and long term observational data, our method measures similar long term effects as an oracle method that has access to long term experimental data.

Several works provide strong semiparametric guarantees for the long term effects of binary actions
[
Kallus and Mao, 2020
,
Chen and Ritzwoller, 2021
,
Meza and Singh, 2021
]
.
By contrast, we prove what appear to be the first nonparametric guarantees for the nonlinear, long term dose response curves of continuous actions.
Whereas binary actions imply boundedness of the identifying formula, continuous actions imply unboundedness in general; see Section
2
. Continuous actions introduce technical issues that we overcome with RKHS techniques.

We complement a literature that incorporates RKHS techniques into the estimation of other causal functions.
For experimental data with joint observations of the randomized actions and final rewards, previous works analyze heterogeneous effects
[
Nie and Wager, 2021
]
, dose response curves
[
Singh et al., 2024
]
, and time-varying dose response curves
[
Singh et al., 2021
]
. Another strand of this literature incorporates instrumental variables to deal with unobserved confounding
[
Carrasco et al., 2007
,
Singh et al., 2019
]
. We study a different problem that combines short term experimental data with long term observational data.
Previous methods using kernels for continuous actions do not handle the complex linkage between short term and long term effects across data sources, and therefore cannot analyze long term causal inference. Our contribution is a method to do so.

Prior to this work,
[
Battocchi et al., 2021
]
allow for continuous actions but impose restrictive linearity and homogeneity conditions, in both of the responses and in their link. By contrast, we study a nonparametric dose response estimator with nonlinearity and heterogeneity in the responses and their link.
Subsequently to this paper’s circulation
[
Singh, 2022
]
,
[
Zeng et al., 2024
]
provide complementary results for the long term dose response curve.
For a given action value, the authors prove pointwise asymptotic consistency of a pseudo-outcome estimator, and pointwise asymptotic normality centered at the biased probability limit of a local linear estimator.
By contrast, we prove uniform consistency across action values, with nonasymptotic rates, for a kernel ridge regression estimator.
Uniform inference for kernel ridge regression over general domains is an open question in statistics, so uniform inference for our estimators is an important question for future work. Finally, we note that this paper subsumes a related working paper
[
Singh, 2021
]
.

Section

Section

Section

2
Problem: Long term causal inference

2.1
Goal: Long term dose response curve

A long term dose response curve is a causal function that summarizes the expected, counterfactual, long term reward
Y^{(d)}
, given a hypothetical intervention on a continuous action that sets
D=d
.
For example, Figure
1
shows that Project STAR randomly assigned students to many different class sizes, which we model as a continuous action.
The crux of long term causal inference is that we do not have joint observations of the randomized action and long term reward. Nonetheless, we wish to estimate their causal relationship from short term experimental data
(G=\textsc{exp})
and long term observational data
(G=\textsc{obs})
. We study several variations of long term dose response curves. Unless noted, expectations are with respect to a superpopulation distribution
\mathbb{P}
with density
\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}
.

Figure 1
:
Real data are reasonably modeled as continuous actions. Much empirical work focuses on “small” (light blue) versus “large” (dark blue) class sizes, yet the supports are rich and overlapping.

Definition 1

(Long term dose response curves)
.

1.

Dose response:
\theta_{0}(d)=\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}\}
is the counterfactual mean long term reward, of action
D=d
, for the average context.

2.

Distribution shifted dose response:
\theta_{0}^{\textsc{ds}}(d,\tilde{\mathbb{P}})=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}\{Y^{(d)}\}
is the counterfactual mean long term reward, given action
D=d
, for an alternative context with distribution
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
.

3.

Experimental dose response:
\theta_{0}^{\textsc{exp}}(d)=\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}|G=\textsc{exp}\}
is the counterfactual mean long term reward, given action
D=d
, for the average experimental context.

4.

Observational dose response:
\theta_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}(d)=\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}|G=\textsc{obs}\}
is the counterfactual mean long term reward, given action
D=d
, for the average observational context.

The dose response curve
\theta_{0}(d)
is for a representative context, drawn from the superpopulation of contexts which generate the short term experimental data
(G=\textsc{exp})
and the long term observational data
(G=\textsc{obs})
. It may depart from the experimental dose response
\theta_{0}^{\textsc{exp}}(d)
and the observational dose response
\theta_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}(d)
due to a selection process:
G
, viewed as a random variable, may not be drawn completely at random. By distinguishing among these functions, we emphasize that the experimental data may be limited in not only duration but also scope; the long term effect of an action in a representative context may be of interest, yet only short term rewards for select contexts may be collected in exploration.

The distribution shifted dose response curve
\theta_{0}^{\textsc{ds}}(d,\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
concerns transfer learning: though our experimental and observational data are drawn from a superpopulation distribution of contexts
\mathbb{P}
, what would be the dose response for an alternative distribution of contexts
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}

[
Pearl and Bareinboim, 2014
]
? We will consider a rich form of distribution shift formalized below.

Definition
1
generalizes from long term dose response curves to long term counterfactual distributions by replacing the
\mathbb{E}
symbols with
\mathbb{P}
symbols. Long term counterfactual distributions capture aspects of the long term reward distribution beyond its mean, e.g. its variance and skewness. Appendix
A
extends our results accordingly. This work appears to provide the first nonlinear, nonparametric estimators for long term dose response curves and counterfactual distributions.

2.2
Previous work: Long term identification

Several previous works identify long term dose response curves, under a variety of identifying assumptions. For clarity of exposition, we focus on the surrogate model for analysis, then demonstrate how our results apply to related models in Remark
2
.
We implement kernel estimators for multiple long term causal inference models in Section
6
.

The surrogate model posits the following data generating process. A single observation begins with a context
X
drawn from the distribution
\mathbb{P}
. Conditional on the context, the selection
G\in\{\textsc{exp},\textsc{obs}\}
is as good as random:
\mathbb{P}(G=\textsc{exp}|X)
is a function of the context
X
, but the actual realization of
G
is a biased coin toss.

For experimental data, i.e. if
G=\textsc{exp}
, then the action
D
is also random conditional upon
X
: the density
\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(D|G=\textsc{exp},X)
is a function of the context
X
, but the actual realization of
D
is a random draw from the density. Finally, we observe the short term reward
S
drawn from the density
\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(S|D=d,G=\textsc{exp},X)
. From experimental data alone, the counterfactual distribution of context-specific short term effects
\mathbb{P}\{S^{(d)}|G=\textsc{exp},X\}
is identified as
\mathbb{P}\{S|D=d,G=\textsc{exp},X\}
, by standard arguments, under a further overlap condition for actions across experimental contexts.

To identify long term effects, long term data are necessary. However, these long term data are observational: if
G=\textsc{obs}
, then the action
D
is not randomized. In the simplest version of the model, the action is not observed at all when
G=\textsc{obs}
. What we do observe is the short term reward
S
and long term reward
Y
, so
\mathbb{P}(Y|S,G=\textsc{obs},X)
is identified.

To combine short and long term data,
[
Prentice, 1989
,
Athey et al., 2020b
]
, and others, articulate nonparametric surrogacy and comparability assumptions. Surrogacy imposes that the action only affects the long term reward via the short term reward in the experiment:
\mathbb{P}(Y|S,D,G=\textsc{exp},X)=\mathbb{P}(Y|S,G=\textsc{exp},X)
. Comparability imposes that the long term reward follows a common conditional distribution across the experimental and observational data:
\mathbb{P}(Y|S,G=\textsc{obs},X)=\mathbb{P}(Y|S,G=\textsc{exp},X)
. With overlap, these conditions identify the counterfactual distribution of context-specific long term effects
\mathbb{P}\{Y^{(d)}|X\}
as
\int\mathbb{P}(Y|s,G=\textsc{obs},X)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(s|d,G=\textsc{exp},X)
.

From this identifying formula, we determine what kinds of distribution shift can be tolerated. It helps if the alternative distribution of contexts
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
preserves
\mathbb{P}(Y|S,G=\textsc{obs},X)
and
\mathbb{P}(S|D,G=\textsc{exp},X)
. For distribution shift, we impose
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(Y,S,G=\textsc{obs},X)=\mathbb{P}(Y|S,G=\textsc{obs},X)\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(S,G=\textsc{obs},X)
and
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(S,D,G=\textsc{exp},X)=\mathbb{P}(S|D,G=\textsc{exp},X)\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(D,G=\textsc{exp},X)
, again with overlap. Importantly, we tolerate shifts in the observational distribution of short term rewards i.e.
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(S|G=\textsc{obs},X)\neq\mathbb{P}(S|G=\textsc{obs},X)
, and shifts in the experimental distribution of randomized actions i.e.
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(D|G=\textsc{exp},X)\neq\mathbb{P}(D|G=\textsc{exp},X)
. This extension, to long term transfer learning, appears to be a modest innovation.

Lemma 1

(Surrogate formula; e.g. Theorem 1 of
[
Athey et al., 2020b
]
)
.

Suppose standard surrogacy conditions hold, as well as distribution shift conditions for the surrogate model, formalized in Appendix
B
. Define the regression function
\gamma_{0}(S,G,X)=\mathbb{E}(Y|S,G,X)
. Then

1.

\theta_{0}(d)=\int\int\gamma_{0}(s,G=\textsc{obs},x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(s|d,G=\textsc{exp},x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)
;

2.

\theta^{\textsc{ds}}_{0}(d)=\int\int\gamma_{0}(s,G=\textsc{obs},x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(s|d,G=\textsc{exp},x)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(x)
;

3.

\theta^{\textsc{exp}}_{0}(d)=\int\int\gamma_{0}(s,G=\textsc{obs},x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(s|d,G=\textsc{exp},x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x|G=\textsc{exp})
;

4.

\theta^{\textsc{obs}}_{0}(d)=\int\int\gamma_{0}(s,G=\textsc{obs},x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(s|d,G=\textsc{exp},x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x|G=\textsc{obs})
.

Lemma
1
expresses each causal function as a double integral of the regression function
\gamma_{0}
learned from observational data, integrated according to a conditional distribution learned from experimental data, and then further integrated according to a possibly conditional distribution of contexts. Since
x
appears in
\gamma_{0}(s,G=\textsc{obs},x)
,
\mathbb{P}(s|d,G=\textsc{exp},x)
, and
\mathbb{P}(x)
, the components in this integral are coupled, so the long term dose response seems challenging to estimate.

Remark 1

(The surrogate formula is generally unbounded when actions are continuous)
.

Let
\mathbb{L}^{2}
be the space of square integrable functions. Define the functional
F:\mathbb{L}^{2}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
as
F:\gamma\mapsto\int\int\gamma(s,G=\textsc{obs},x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(s|d,G=\textsc{exp},x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)
. When the action is continuous,
F
is generally unbounded, i.e. there does not exists some constant
C<\infty
such that
F(\gamma)\leq C\|\gamma\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}
for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{L}^{2}

[
Newey, 1994
,
van der Vaart, 1991
]
. The technical challenge introduced by continuous actions is well known is causal inference
[
van der Laan et al., 2018
]
.

Remark 2

(Alternative long term models)
.

Under alternative identifying assumptions, the integrals are similar. For example, the alternative assumptions of missingness-at-random
[
Kallus and Mao, 2020
]
or external validity and latent unconfoundedness
[
Athey et al., 2020a
]
, lead to
\theta_{0}(d)=\int\int\gamma_{0}(s,d,G=\textsc{obs},x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(s|d,G=\textsc{exp},x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)
where now
\gamma_{0}(S,D,G,X)=\mathbb{E}(Y|S,D,G,X)
. Our estimation results apply to all of these variations by slightly modifying
\gamma_{0}
.

3
Statistical framework: RKHS

3.1
Kernel techniques for algorithm derivation

We use techniques from the RKHS framework to overcome the practical challenge of estimating the double integral in Lemma
1
, and the technical challenge of estimating an unbounded formula in Remark
1
. These algorithmic techniques lead to a simple estimator with a closed form solution.

A scalar-valued RKHS
H
is a Hilbert space of functions
f:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is some Polish space. Functions in this space are characterized by the feature map, which takes a point
a\in\mathcal{A}
and maps it to features
\phi(a)
in the RKHS. The closure of
\text{span}\{\phi(a)\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
is
H
; the features are the dictionary of basis functions for functions in
H
. In particular, to evaluate
f\in H
at
a
, we take the RKHS inner product
f(a)=\langle f,\phi(a)\rangle_{H}
. The kernel
k:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the inner product of features:
k(a,a^{\prime})=\langle\phi(a),\phi(a^{\prime})\rangle_{H}
. It is continuous, symmetric, and positive definite.

The RKHS is widely used for nonparametric regression of some output
Y\in\mathbb{R}
on some input
a\in\mathcal{A}
. A kernel ridge regression estimator of the regression function
f_{0}(A)=\mathbb{E}(Y|A)
is

\hat{f}=\argmin_{f\in H}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{Y_{i}-\langle f,\phi(A_{i})\rangle_{H}\}^{2}+\lambda\|f\|_{H}^{2},

(1)

where
\lambda>0
is a ridge penalty hyperparameter. The minimizer has a well known closed form solution
[
Kimeldorf and Wahba, 1971
]
, as a weighting
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the output vector
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\hat{f}(a)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\alpha_{i}=\frac{1}{n}Y^{\top}\alpha,\quad\alpha=n(K_{AA}+n\lambda I)^{-1}K_{Aa}.

(2)

Here,
K_{AA}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the kernel matrix with
(i,j)
th entry
k(A_{i},A_{j})
, and
K_{Aa}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the kernel vector with
i
th entry
k(A_{i},a)
.
For tuning
\lambda
, previous work has shown that generalized cross validation and leave-one-out cross validation have closed form solutions, the former is asymptotically optimal, and they are similar in practice
[
Craven and Wahba, 1978
,
Li, 1986
]
.
We will derive interpretable weights for long term rewards, calculated from the short term experiment, which deliver long term dose response curves.

To take the expectation of a function
f\in H
, a useful technique is the kernel mean embedding:

\mathbb{E}\{f(A)|B=b\}=\int\langle f,\phi(A)\rangle_{H}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(a|b)=\left\langle f,\int\phi(a)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(a|b)\right\rangle=\langle f,\mu_{a}(b)\rangle_{H},

where
\mu_{a}(b)=\int\phi(a)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(a|b)
embeds the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}(a|b)
using the features
\phi(a)

[
Smola et al., 2007
]
. Exchanging the expectation and inner product uses weak regularity conditions (Assumption
1
below). In summary, the expectation of a function in the RKHS equals the product of the function and the mean embedding.
We will embed the short term experimental data distribution appropriately, for the extrapolation of long term effects.

The mean embedding
\mu_{a}(b)=\mathbb{E}\{\phi(A)|B=b\}
is a generalized regression function, so we will estimate it in a manner that generalizes (
1
) and
2
. In particular, we will estimate its equivalent conditional expectation operator
E_{0}:f(\cdot)\mapsto\mathbb{E}\{f(A)|B=(\cdot)\}
, which we assume maps from an RKHS
H_{\mathcal{A}}
with features
\phi(a)
to another RKHS
H_{\mathcal{B}}
with features
\phi(b)
, for simplicity.
1
1

1

Formally,
H_{\mathcal{A}}
has the kernel
k_{\mathcal{A}}(a,a^{\prime})
and features
\phi_{\mathcal{A}}(a)
, while
H_{\mathcal{B}}
has the possibly different kernel
k_{\mathcal{B}}(b,b^{\prime})
and features
\phi_{\mathcal{B}}(b)
. We suppress the subscripts to lighten notation.
This ensures that the expectation of a function of
A
, conditional upon
B
, is a smooth function of
B
. It leads to the simple equivalence
\mu_{a}(b)=E_{0}^{*}\phi(b)
, where
E_{0}^{*}
is the adjoint of
E_{0}
. We estimate
\hat{E}
as a generalized kernel ridge regression in the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators
\mathcal{L}_{2}(H_{\mathcal{A}},H_{\mathcal{B}})

[
Micchelli and Pontil, 2005
]
, and hence
\hat{\mu}_{a}(b)=\hat{E}^{*}\phi(b)
.
This generalized regression is how we will learn the appropriate representation of the short term experiment for long term causal inference.

For functions defined over multiple variables, we use product kernels. Consider the function
f:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined over
A\in\mathcal{A}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}
. We define an RKHS
H
for this function by combining RKHSs
H_{\mathcal{A}}
and
H_{\mathcal{B}}
containing functions
f_{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
f_{\mathcal{B}}:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, respectively. Its kernel is the product of the individual kernels:
k(a,b;a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=k_{\mathcal{A}}(a,a^{\prime})k_{\mathcal{A}}(b,b^{\prime})
. If
k_{\mathcal{A}}
and
k_{\mathcal{B}}
are Gaussian kernels, then so is their product, making it a natural choice. Because we use product kernels, the estimators will have the symbol
\odot
for the elementwise product of kernel matrices. Formally,
H=H_{\mathcal{A}}\otimes H_{\mathcal{B}}
is a tensor product RKHS with features
\phi(a,b)=\phi(a)\otimes\phi(b)
, where the symbols
\otimes
means outer product:
(x\otimes y)z=x\langle y,z\rangle
. For any
f\in H
,
f(a,b)=\langle f,\phi(a)\otimes\phi(b)\rangle_{H}
and
\|\phi(a,b)\|_{H}=\|\phi(a)\|_{H_{\mathcal{A}}}\|\phi(b)\|_{H_{\mathcal{B}}}
.
[
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan, 2011
, Section 4]
give background.

3.2
Main assumptions: Low effective dimension and smoothness

Our main assumption is that the RKHS used in estimation has a low effective dimension: while the features may be infinite dimensional, relatively few feature dimensions explain most of the variation in the data. We also assume that the estimands are smooth with respect to these features. In what follows, we formalize these familiar assumptions from RKHS analysis
[
Caponnetto and De Vito, 2007
,
Fischer and Steinwart, 2020
]
in terms of the spectrum of the kernel.

To begin, we express the features as a possibly infinite expansion of the kernel. Let
\mathbb{L}^{2}
denote the space of square integrable functions from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathbb{R}
with respect to measure
\mathbb{P}
. For a kernel
k
, define the convolution operator
L:\mathbb{L}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{L}^{2}
as
L:f\mapsto\int k(a,\cdot)f(a)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(a)
, which may be expressed in terms of its spectrum:
Lf=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\eta_{j}\langle f,\varphi_{j}\rangle_{H}\varphi_{j}
. Here,
(\eta_{j})
are weakly decreasing eigenvalues, and
(\varphi_{j})
are corresponding eigenfunctions, of the data passed through the kernel. For simplicity, suppose
(\varphi_{j})
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
. With Gaussian data and the Gaussian kernel,
(\varphi_{j})
are weighted Hermite polynomials
[
Rasmussen and Williams, 2006
, Section 4.3]
. In general,

\mathbb{L}^{2}=\left(f=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}\varphi_{j}:\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}^{2}<\infty\right),\quad H=\left(f=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}\varphi_{j}:\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\eta_{j}^{-1}f_{j}^{2}<\infty\right).

The RKHS
H
is the subset of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
for which higher order terms in the series
(\varphi_{j})
have a smaller contribution, as penalized by the eigenvalues
(\eta_{j})
. By Mercer’s theorem, the feature map is the infinite dictionary of basis functions given by the spectrum of the kernel:
\phi(a)=\{\eta_{j}^{1/2}\varphi_{j}(a)\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
.

Our main approximation assumption is that the features have a low effective dimension:

\eta_{j}\leq Cj^{-b},\quad C<\infty,\quad b\geq 1.

(3)

The eigenvalues decay at least polynomially. Any bounded kernel satisfies (
3
)
[
Fischer and Steinwart, 2020
, Lemma 10]
. A higher value of
b
corresponds to a lower effective dimension, better control of the variance of our estimator, and hence a faster rate. The limit
b\rightarrow\infty
gives an RKHS with finite dimension
[
Caponnetto and De Vito, 2007
]
. The empirical eigenvalues are simple to compute, so it is simple to validate this assumption with a diagnostic plot. Figure
2
verifies polynomial decay of the empirical eigenvalues in the real world application of Section

4
Method: Long term dose response via kernels

4.1
Short term kernel embedding

We construct an RKHS for long term dose response curve estimation, in which we embed the distribution of the short term experimental data. We define RKHSs for the short term reward
S\in\mathcal{S}
, action
D\in\mathcal{D}
, and context
X\in\mathcal{X}
.
For example, for the short term reward, we define the RKHS
H_{\mathcal{S}}
with feature map
\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(s)
and kernel
k_{\mathcal{S}}(s,s^{\prime})=\langle\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(s),\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(s^{\prime})\rangle_{H_{\mathcal{S}}}
.

Remark 3

(Subscripts)
.

To lighten notation, we suppress subscripts when arguments are provided, e.g. we write
\phi(s)=\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(s)
and
\phi(x)=\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(x)
, though the feature maps may differ.

From these RKHSs for short term rewards and contexts, we construct an RKHS
H
for the long term reward mechanism
\gamma_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}(S,X)=\gamma_{0}(S,G=\textsc{obs},X)=\mathbb{E}(Y|S,G=\textsc{obs},X)
. We assume that it belongs to the RKHS with the product kernel
k(s,x;s^{\prime},x^{\prime})=k_{\mathcal{S}}(s,s^{\prime})k_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})
. Equivalently,
\gamma_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}\in H=H_{\mathcal{S}}\otimes H_{\mathcal{X}}
, where
\otimes
means tensor product.
3
3

3

For the alternative long term causal inference models in Remark
2
,
H=H_{\mathcal{S}}\otimes H_{\mathcal{D}}\otimes H_{\mathcal{X}}

The features for
\gamma_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}
are the outer product of features for the individual RKHSs:
\gamma_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}(s,x)=\langle\gamma_{0}^{\textsc{obs}},\phi(s,x)\rangle_{H}=\langle\gamma_{0}^{\textsc{obs}},\phi(s)\otimes\phi(x)\rangle_{H}
.

Example 1

(Quadratic kernels)
.

As a toy example, consider
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}
and quadratic kernels, e.g.
k_{\mathcal{S}}(s,s^{\prime})=(ss^{\prime}+1)^{2}
. Each kernel has three features, e.g.
\phi(s)=(s^{2},\sqrt{2}s,1)
. Hence the product kernel has
3\times 3
induced features;
\phi(s,x)
contains each interaction among
(s^{2},\sqrt{2}s,1)
and
(x^{2},\sqrt{2}x,1)
.

Example 2

(Gaussian kernels)
.

With Gaussian data and Gaussian kernels, the features of each kernel are weighted Hermite polynomials, of which there are countably many. In this richer example,
\phi(s,x)
interacts the Hermite polynomials of
s
and
x
. This basis allows for nonlinearity and heterogeneity in the link between short and long term effects. It satisfies our regularity conditions.

Assumption 1

(RKHS regularity)
.

1.

The features
\phi(S)
,
\phi(D)
, and
\phi(X)
are measurable.

2.

The kernels
k_{\mathcal{S}}
,
k_{\mathcal{D}}
, and
k_{\mathcal{X}}
are bounded:
\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\|\phi(s)\|_{H_{\mathcal{S}}}\leq\kappa_{s}

\sup_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\|\phi(d)\|_{H_{\mathcal{D}}}\leq\kappa_{d}
, and
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|\phi(x)\|_{H_{\mathcal{X}}}\leq\kappa_{x}

3.

The kernels
k_{\mathcal{S}}
and
k_{\mathcal{X}}
are characteristic: for all Borel probability measures
\mathbb{Q}(s)
and
\mathbb{Q}(x)

\mathbb{Q}(s)\mapsto\int\phi(s)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}(s)
and
\mathbb{Q}(x)\mapsto\int\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}(x)
are injective.

Measurability, boundedness, and the characteristic property are weak regularity conditions satisfied by commonly used kernels, e.g. the Matérn and Gaussian kernels. The characteristic property ensures that kernel mean embeddings of distributions are unique
[
Sriperumbudur et al., 2010
]

4.3
Towards trustworthiness: Long term reward weights

A mature literature on kernel methods for binary actions, with joint observations of randomized actions and final outcomes, emphasizes the interpretation of outcome weights; see e.g.
[
Bruns-Smith et al., 2023
]
for a recent review and many references, going back to the classical work of
[
Speckman, 1979
]
. We show that our estimators share some of these virtues despite the additional challenges of our setting: continuous actions and long term extrapolation.

Corollary 1

(Interpretable weights)
.

Our estimators may be viewed as long term reward weights:
\hat{\theta}(d)=\frac{1}{n_{\textsc{obs}}}\sum_{i\in\textsc{obs}}Y_{i}\hat{\alpha}_{i}(d)
for a weight vector
\hat{\alpha}(d)\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\textsc{obs}}}
that is a byproduct of Algorithm
1
.

These weights allow us to evaluate how trustworthy our estimator is in certain respects, e.g. its robustness and transparency.
For example, we may compare different choices of the kernels
k_{\mathcal{S}}
and
k_{\mathcal{X}}
in the nonlinear model
\gamma_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}
that links the short term and long term. For each choice, we can examine the magnitudes of the weights to assess which regions of the short term reward and context space receive greater weight. A larger
|\hat{\alpha}_{i}(d)|
means that observation
i\in\textsc{obs}
has more influence on the final estimate. For each choice, we can also examine the signs of the weights to assess how much extrapolation is taking place. A negative weight, i.e.
\hat{\alpha}_{i}(d)<0
, means that our counterfactual prediction of the long term reward does not simply interpolate among
Y_{i}
for
i\in\textsc{obs}
.

Such weights would not exist in general, for long term dose response curve estimators outside of the RKHS. As shown in Remark
1
, the surrogate formula with continous actions is unbounded over
\mathbb{L}^{2}
in general. As such, it does not have a Riesz representer over
\mathbb{L}^{2}
. The weights, if they exist, are empirical analogues of the Riesz representer.

Our algorithm keenly relies on the fact that such weights do exist for long term dose response curve estimators in the RKHS. As shown in Remark
5
, the surrogate formula with continuous actions is bounded over
H
. As such, it does have a Riesz representer over
H
, given by the fused embedding
\mu_{s,x}(d)
in Theorem
1
. The weights
\hat{\alpha}(d)
are calculated from its empirical analogue.

5
Theory: Uniform consistency with finite sample rates

For identification in Section
2
, we placed the long term causal inference assumptions. To derive a closed form estimator in Section

, we assumed RKHS regularity (Assumption

). To prove uniform consistency across actions, we now place three final assumptions: original space regularity (Assumption

).

Assumption 2

(Original space regularity)
.

1.

The long term reward
Y\in\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
is bounded.

2.

The spaces
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{D}
, and
\mathcal{X}
are Polish, i.e. separable and completely metrizable.

For simplicity, the long term reward is a bounded scalar. Our results in Appendix

relax this condition, allowing
\mathcal{Y}
to be separable Hilbert space. We allow short term rewards, actions, and contexts to be discrete or continuous. This generality means our results can be applied to nonstandard data such as texts, images, and graphs

Next, we place our main approximation assumptions for the long term regression and short term conditional expectation operator. We assume that they have low effective dimensions and are smooth in the sense of (
3
) and (
4
), which are standard assumptions in the RKHS framework. Extending the notation of Section
3.2
, we specify the measure and space of the eigendecomposition.

5

5

\eta^{\textsc{obs}}_{j}(H)

Assumption 3

(Effective dimension and smoothness for long term regression)

Assumption 4

(Effective dimension and smoothness for short term conditional expectation operator)

The eigenvalues decay, and the operator satisfies a source condition:

Remark 8

(Compensating for small experiments and complex links)

We prove uniform rates for an RKHS estimator of the long term dose response curve, under effective dimension and smoothness conditions on the regression and embedding. 7
Discussion

Long term causal inference is a core challenge in both the development of artificial intelligence and in the evaluation of social programs; exploratory and experimental data are often short term, due to collection expenses, privacy concerns, or ethical considerations. We propose a kernel method that learns a representation of the short term experimental data distribution, in order to extrapolate long term dose response curves. This representation provides interpretable weights. We consider the additional challenge of distribution shift, i.e. of transfer learning.

As a contribution to long term causal inference, we propose what appears to be the first nonlinear, nonparametric estimator for long term dose response curves, by adapting techniques and assumptions from the RKHS statistical framework. We derive a closed form for the estimator using RKHS techniques, and prove that it uniformly consistent across actions under RKHS assumptions. The RKHS framework improves our scientific understanding of real data from Project STAR.

Future work may extend our results to neural network embeddings, or to reinforcement learning problems. Future work may also improve scalability via Nyström approximations.

Appendix A
Extension: Long term counterfactual distributions

A.1
Goal: Distribution of long term reward

While the mean of counterfactual long term rewards may be of primary interest, additional moments of counterfactual long term rewards may be of secondary interest. For example, the variance and skewness of the long term reward distribution contain important information. In artificial intelligence applications, it is common for the long term reward to be sparse, i.e. to frequently take the value of zero. In this common scenario, the counterfactual distribution of long term rewards is more informative than the counterfactual mean.

For these reasons, we extend our methods and theory: from counterfactual means of long term rewards, to counterfactual distributions of long term rewards. We propose what appear to be the first nonparametric estimators for long term counterfactual distributions. In our analysis, we relax the condition in Assumption
2
that
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
, to the more general setting where

is a Polish space. As such, the long term reward, short term reward, action, and context may be discrete or continuous, and even texts, images, or graphs. The estimands mirror Definition

, replacing

with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}|X\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}|G=\textsc{exp},X\}=\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}|D=d,G=\textsc{exp},X\}=\mathbb{E}(Y|D=d,G=\textsc{exp},X)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}(Y|S,D=d,G=\textsc{exp},X)|D=d,G=\textsc{exp},X\}

by unconfounded selection, experimental action, causal consistency, and the law of iterated expectations.
Focusing on the inner expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y|S,D=d,G=\textsc{exp},X)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(Y|S,G=\textsc{exp},X)=\mathbb{E}(Y|S,G=\textsc{obs},X)

by surrogacy and comparability. In summary,

\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}|X\}=\int\mathbb{E}(Y|s,G=\textsc{obs},X)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(s|D=d,G=\textsc{exp},X).

By the law of iterated expectations,

\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}\}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}|X\}],\quad\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}\{Y^{(d)}\}=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}[\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}\{Y^{(d)}|X\}],\quad\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}|G\}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}|G,X\}|G].

This completes the argument for
\theta_{0}
. For
\theta_{0}^{\textsc{ds}}
, experimental transferability and observational transferability within Assumption
B.2
imply that
\mathbb{P}(S|D,G=\textsc{exp},X)
and
\mathbb{P}(Y|S,G=\textsc{obs},X)
are invariant across
\mathbb{P}
and
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
. Finally, for
\theta_{0}^{\textsc{exp}}
and
\theta_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}
, unconfounded selection implies
\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}|G,X\}=\mathbb{E}\{Y^{(d)}|X\}
.
∎

Appendix D
Hyperparameter tuning

D.1
Kernels

Consider the real valued kernel
k_{\mathcal{A}}(a,a^{\prime})
over
\mathcal{A}
. Throughout, we use the popular Gaussian kernel
k_{\mathcal{A}}(a,a^{\prime})=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\|a-a^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}}{\iota^{2}}\right)
, which satisfies Assumption
1
. Here,
\iota
is a hyperparameter called the lengthscale, which we tune using the well known median heuristic: we set
\iota
equal to the median interpoint distance of
(A_{i})
, where the interpoint distance between
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
is
\|A_{i}-A_{j}\|_{\mathcal{A}}
.

When
\mathcal{A}
is multidimensional, we use the product of kernels for each dimension. For example, if
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we take
k(a,a^{\prime})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(a_{j}-a_{j}^{\prime})^{2}}{\iota_{j}^{2}}\right\}
. We set each lengthscale according to the median interpoint distance for that dimension.

D.2
Long term regression regularization

Consider the kernel ridge regression of
Y\in\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
on
\phi(A)\in H_{\mathcal{A}}
with regularization
\lambda
:

\hat{f}=\argmin_{f\in H_{\mathcal{A}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{Y_{i}-\langle f,\phi(A_{i})\rangle_{H_{\mathcal{A}}}\}^{2}+\lambda\|f\|_{H_{\mathcal{A}}}^{2}.

This is a simplified version of the long term regression estimator
\hat{\gamma}^{\textsc{obs}}
. We recap known tuning procedures with closed form solutions: leave-one-out cross validation (LOOCV), and generalized cross validation (GCV). Both are similar in practice. The latter is asymptotically optimal for regression in both
\mathbb{L}^{2}
and
H_{\mathcal{A}}
norms, so it is compatible with our theoretical analysis. Both procedures are well known; see e.g.
[
Singh et al., 2024
]
for their derivation from first principles.

Algorithm D.1

(LOOCV for kernel ridge regression)
.

Construct the
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
matrices
C_{\lambda}=I-K_{AA}(K_{AA}+n\lambda I)^{-1}
and
\tilde{C}_{\lambda}=\text{diag}(C_{\lambda})
, where
\tilde{C}_{\lambda}
has the same diagonal entries as
C_{\lambda}
and off diagonal entries of zero. Set
\lambda^{*}=\argmin_{\lambda\in\Lambda}n^{-1}\|\tilde{C}_{\lambda}^{-1}C_{\lambda}Y\|_{2}^{2}
.

Algorithm D.2

(GCV for kernel ridge regression)
.

Set
\lambda^{*}=\argmin_{\lambda\in\Lambda}n^{-1}\|\{\text{trace}(C_{\lambda})\}^{-1}C_{\lambda}Y\|_{2}^{2}
.

D.3
Short term conditional expectation operator regularization

Consider the generalized kernel ridge regression of
\phi(A)\in H_{\mathcal{A}}
on
\phi(B)\in H_{\mathcal{B}}
with regularization
\lambda
:

\hat{E}=\argmin_{E\in\mathcal{L}_{2}(H_{\mathcal{A}},H_{\mathcal{B}})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\phi(A_{i})-E^{*}\phi(B_{i})\}^{2}+\lambda\|E\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(H_{\mathcal{A}},\otimes H_{\mathcal{B}})}^{2}.

This is a simplified version of the short term conditional expectation operator estimator
\hat{E}^{\textsc{exp}}
. We recap leave-one-out cross validation (LOOCV) and generalized cross validation (GCV). Their derivations from first principles and their properties are similar to Algorithms
D.1
and
D.2
above.

Algorithm D.3

(LOOCV for conditional expectation operator)
.

Construct the
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
matrices:

1.

R=K_{BB}(K_{BB}+n\lambda I)^{-1}
;

2.

S
with
S_{ij}=1(i=j)\left(\frac{1}{1-R_{ii}}\right)^{2}
where
R_{ii}
is the
i
th diagonal element of
R
;

3.

T=S(K_{AA}-2K_{AA}R^{\top}+RK_{AA}R^{\top})
.

Set
\lambda^{*}=\argmin_{\lambda\in\Lambda}n^{-1}\text{trace}(T)
.

Algorithm D.4

(GCV for conditional expectation operator)
.

Construct the
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
matrices:

1.

R=K_{BB}(K_{BB}+n\lambda I)^{-1}
;

2.

S=\{\text{trace}(I-R)\}^{-2}I
;

3.

T=S(K_{AA}-2K_{AA}R^{\top}+RK_{AA}R^{\top})
.

Set
\lambda^{*}=\argmin_{\lambda\in\Lambda}n^{-1}\text{trace}(T)
.

r_{\mu}(\delta,n)
is always smaller than
r(\delta,n^{\textsc{obs}},b^{\textsc{obs}},c^{\textsc{obs}})+r_{E}(\delta,n^{\textsc{exp}},b^{\textsc{exp}},c^{\textsc{exp}})
. The arguments for the other long term dose response curves are similar.
∎

Proof of Theorem
A.2
.

Fix
d\in\mathcal{D}
. By Theorem
A.1
,

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}(d)-\nu_{0}(d)

\displaystyle=(\hat{E}^{\textsc{obs}})^{*}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\hat{\mu}_{s}^{\textsc{exp}}(d,X_{i})\otimes\phi(X_{i})\}\right]-(E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}})^{*}\left[\int\{\mu_{s}^{\textsc{exp}}(d,x)\otimes\phi(x)\}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=(\hat{E}^{\textsc{obs}})^{*}\{\Delta(d)\}+(\hat{E}^{\textsc{obs}}-E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}})^{*}\left[\int\{\mu_{s}^{\textsc{exp}}(d,x)\otimes\phi(x)\}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=(\hat{E}^{\textsc{obs}}-E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}})^{*}\{\Delta(d)\}+(E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}})^{*}\{\Delta(d)\}+(\hat{E}^{\textsc{obs}}-E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}})^{*}\left[\int\{\mu_{s}^{\textsc{exp}}(d,x)\otimes\phi(x)\}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)\right].

Therefore, by the triangle inequality, the definition of the operator norm, and the fact that the Hilbert-Schmidt norm upper bounds the operator norm,
\|\hat{\nu}(d)-\nu_{0}(d)\|_{H_{\mathcal{Y}}}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\left\|(\hat{E}^{\textsc{obs}}-E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}})^{*}\{\Delta(d)\}\right\|_{H_{\mathcal{Y}}}+\left\|(E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}})^{*}\{\Delta(d)\}\right\|_{H_{\mathcal{Y}}}+\left\|(\hat{E}^{\textsc{obs}}-E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}})^{*}\left[\int\{\mu_{s}^{\textsc{exp}}(d,x)\otimes\phi(x)\}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)\right]\right\|_{H_{\mathcal{Y}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{E}^{\textsc{obs}}-E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}\|_{\text{op}}\left\|\Delta(d)\right\|_{H}+\|E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}\|_{\text{op}}\left\|\Delta(d)\right\|_{H}+\|\hat{E}^{\textsc{obs}}-E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}\|_{\text{op}}\left\|\int\{\mu_{s}^{\textsc{exp}}(d,x)\otimes\phi(x)\}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)\right\|_{H}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{E}^{\textsc{obs}}-E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}\left\|\Delta(d)\right\|_{H}+\|E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}\left\|\Delta(d)\right\|_{H}+\|\hat{E}^{\textsc{obs}}-E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}\left\|\int\{\mu_{s}^{\textsc{exp}}(d,x)\otimes\phi(x)\}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)\right\|_{H},

where in the final line we abbreviate
\mathcal{L}_{2}=\mathcal{L}_{2}(H_{\mathcal{Y}},H_{\mathcal{S}}\otimes H_{\mathcal{X}})
.

By Lemma
E.3
, for
n^{\textsc{obs}}
sufficiently large, with probability
1-\delta
,
\|\hat{E}^{\textsc{obs}}-E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}\lesssim r(\delta,n^{\textsc{obs}},b^{\textsc{obs}},c^{\textsc{obs}}).
By Proposition
E.1
, for
n^{\textsc{exp}}
sufficiently large, with probability
1-2\delta
,
\sup_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\|\Delta(d)\|_{H}\lesssim r_{E}(\delta,n^{\textsc{exp}},b^{\textsc{exp}},c^{\textsc{exp}})+r_{\mu}(\delta,n)
. By Assumption
A.3
,
\|E_{0}^{\textsc{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}
is bounded by a constant. By Assumption
1
,
\left\|\int\{\mu_{s}^{\textsc{exp}}(d,x)\otimes\phi(x)\}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)\right\|_{H}\leq\kappa_{s}\kappa_{x}
.

In summary, for
n^{\textsc{obs}}
and
n^{\textsc{exp}}
sufficiently large, with probability
1-3\delta
,

\sup_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\|\hat{\nu}(d)-\nu_{0}(d)\|_{H_{\mathcal{Y}}}\lesssim r_{E}(\delta,n^{\textsc{obs}},b^{\textsc{obs}},c^{\textsc{obs}})+r_{E}(\delta,n^{\textsc{exp}},b^{\textsc{exp}},c^{\textsc{exp}})+r_{\mu}(\delta,n).

Substituting in these rates from Lemmas
E.2
and
E.3
yields the desired result for
\nu_{0}(d)
. In particular,
r_{\mu}(\delta,n)
is always smaller than
r_{E}(\delta,n^{\textsc{obs}},b^{\textsc{obs}},c^{\textsc{obs}})+r_{E}(\delta,n^{\textsc{exp}},b^{\textsc{exp}},c^{\textsc{exp}})
. The arguments for the other long term counterfactual embeddings are similar.
∎

Proof of Corollary
A.1
.

By Theorem
A.2
, for any
d\in\mathcal{D}
,

\|\hat{\nu}(d)-\nu_{0}(d)\|_{H_{\mathcal{Y}}}=O_{p}\left\{(n^{\textsc{obs}})^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{c^{\textsc{obs}}-1}{c^{\textsc{obs}}+1/b^{\textsc{obs}}}}+(n^{\textsc{exp}})^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{c^{\textsc{exp}}-1}{c^{\textsc{exp}}+1/b^{\textsc{exp}}}}\right\}.

Denote the samples constructed by Algorithm
A.2
by
(Y_{j}^{\prime})
where
j=1,...,m
. Then by
[
Bach et al., 2012
, Section 4.2]
,

\left\|\hat{\nu}(d)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi(Y_{j}^{\prime})\right\|_{H_{\mathcal{Y}}}=O(m^{-1/2}).

Therefore by the triangle inequality,

\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi(Y_{j}^{\prime})-\nu_{0}(d)\right\|_{H_{\mathcal{Y}}}=O_{p}\left\{(n^{\textsc{obs}})^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{c^{\textsc{obs}}-1}{c^{\textsc{obs}}+1/b^{\textsc{obs}}}}+(n^{\textsc{exp}})^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{c^{\textsc{exp}}-1}{c^{\textsc{exp}}+1/b^{\textsc{exp}}}}+m^{-1/2}\right\}.

The desired result follows from
[
Sriperumbudur, 2016
]
, as quoted by
[
Simon-Gabriel et al., 2020
, Theorem 1.1]
. The arguments for other long term counterfactual distributions are identical.
∎

E.3
Connection to semiparametric inference

An earlier draft
[
Singh, 2022
]
further demonstrated how Theorem
2
satisfies the rate conditions for inference on the effect of a binary action. In particular, it proved nonasymptotic Gaussian approximation by verifying the conditions of
[
Meza and Singh, 2021
]
, when the action is binary. This draft focuses on the continuous action setting, which has received less attention in long term causal inference.

Appendix F
Application details

F.1
Sample and variable definitions

We use the same sample as
[
Athey et al., 2020a
]
, where
n^{\textsc{exp}}=2,052
from Project STAR and
n^{\textsc{obs}}=10,240
from NYC public schools. In particular, the NYC students are from cohorts with year of birth from
1985
to
1995
. For the benchmark that uses covariates, we keep only complete observations, yielding
n^{\textsc{obs}}=10,222
.

The test score definitions follow
[
Krueger and Whitmore, 2001
,
Chetty et al., 2011
,
Athey et al., 2020a
]
. They combine both math and reading scores on the grade-appropriate Stanford Achievement Test, which is multiple choice.

The covariates are binary: race indicates whether a student is white, gender indicates whether a student is female, and free and reduced price lunch status indicates whether a student is eligible for discounted meals at school.

F.2
Implementation details

We use Gaussian kernels for class size and test scores. Following Appendix
D
, we set lengthscales according to the median interpoint distance heuristic. When using covariates, we use a binary kernel for each covariate. We combine multiple kernels by taking the product.

We tune the regularization hyperparameters using leave-one-out cross validation as described in Appendix
D
. We set
\lambda^{\textsc{obs}}
according to Algorithm
D.1
and
\lambda^{\textsc{exp}}
according to Algorithm
D.3
.

We follow similar tuning procedures for the oracle and benchmarks, which are versions of previous work
[
Singh et al., 2024
]
. In particular, we set the regularization according to Algorithm
D.1
, as suggested by those authors.
