Computationally Bounded Robust Compilation and Universally Composable Security Thanks: ∗ Work done in part while author was at the University of Maryland.
Robert Künnemann, Marco Patrignani, Ethan Cecchetti

Abstract
Universal Composability ( 𝖴𝖢 \mathsf{UC} ) is the gold standard for cryptographic security, but mechanizing proofs of 𝖴𝖢 \mathsf{UC} is notoriously difficult. A recently-discovered connection between 𝖴𝖢 \mathsf{UC} and Robust Compilation ( 𝑅𝐶 \mathit{RC} )—a novel theory of secure compilation—provides a means to verify 𝖴𝖢 \mathsf{UC} proofs using tools that mechanize equality results. Unfortunately, the existing methods apply only to perfect 𝖴𝖢 \mathsf{UC} security, and real-world protocols relying on cryptography are only computationally secure. This paper addresses this gap by lifting the connection between 𝖴𝖢 \mathsf{UC} and 𝑅𝐶 \mathit{RC} to the computational setting, extending techniques from the 𝑅𝐶 \mathit{RC} setting to apply to computational 𝖴𝖢 \mathsf{UC} security. Moreover, it further generalizes the 𝖴𝖢 \mathsf{UC} – 𝑅𝐶 \mathit{RC} connection beyond computational security to arbitrary equalities, providing a framework to subsume the existing perfect case, and to instantiate future theories with more complex notions of security. This connection allows the use of tools for proofs of computational indistinguishability to properly mechanize proofs of computational 𝖴𝖢 \mathsf{UC} security. We demonstrate this power by using CryptoVerif to mechanize a proof that parts of the Wireguard protocol are computationally 𝖴𝖢 \mathsf{UC} secure. Finally, all proofs of the framework itself are verified in Isabelle/HOL.



I
Introduction

In cryptography, universal composability (
\mathsf{UC}
)
[
16
]
is a framework for the specification and analysis of cryptographic protocols with a key guarantee about compositionality
[
53
,
15
,
36
]
:
If a protocol is
\mathsf{UC}
-secure, it behaves like some high-level, secure-by-construction ideal functionality no matter what the protocol interacts with.
If that protocol is then used as a building block inside a larger protocol,
it is safe to replace the smaller protocol with its ideal functionality when reasoning about the security of the larger protocol.
In other words,
\mathsf{UC}
protocols are secure even when composed with larger protocols.

Proving that a protocol attains
\mathsf{UC}
security is notoriously complex and error-prone, so it is important to provide ways to mechanize these proofs.

Patrignani et al. 2022
recently identified a simple and scalable way to define proofs of
\mathsf{UC}
by relying on a surprising connection between
\mathsf{UC}
and Robust Compilation (
\mathit{RC}
).

\mathit{RC}
is a hierarchy of criteria for secure compilation introduced by
Abate et al. 2019
,
Abate et al. 2021a
.
The criteria describe the security of a compiler by which (hyper)properties
[
21
]
it preserves.

Patrignani et al. 2022
identify that
\mathsf{UC}
security is deeply connected to Robust Hyperproperty-Preserving Compilation (
Section

II-B
), the
\mathit{RC}
requirement that a compiler preserve arbitrary hyperproperties.
Unfortunately, the connection they identify considers only
perfect

\mathsf{UC}
security, where the protocol and ideal functionality exhibit
identical
behaviors.
Real cryptographic protocols are almost never perfectly secure, they rely on cryptographic primitives whose security depends on computational hardness assumptions.
As a result, real “
\mathsf{UC}
-secure” protocols rely on a
computational
definition of
\mathsf{UC}
security, which allows the protocol to behave differently from the ideal functionality, but only in ways that are indistinguishable to a computationally bounded adversary.
Since the results of
Patrignani et al. 2022
apply only to the perfect case, they provide little help in verifying the far more prevalent proofs of computational
\mathsf{UC}
security.

For example, consider the following single-bit commitment protocol due to
Canetti and Fischlin 2001
.
To commit to bit
b
, generate a
4n
-bit pseudo-random value
\mathit{pr}
, and output
\mathit{pr}
if
b=0
and
\mathit{pr}\oplus\sigma
if
b=1
where
\sigma
is a public
4n
-bit truly random value.
This protocol does not perfectly
\mathsf{UC}
emulate a functionality that simply indicates a bit has been committed by does nothing else before opening.
An
unbounded
attacker can simply check if the commitment is one of the (exponentially many) possible pseudo-random values and correctly guess the value of
b
with overwhelming probability.
The protocol is, however,
computationally

\mathsf{UC}
secure given a pseudo-random generator with the right structure
[
17
]
,
a proof that relies on a
polynomial
adversary’s inability to distinguish
\mathit{pr}
from a truly random value.

This work addresses the limitation of
Patrignani et al. 2022
by lifting their results to the computational case,
allowing us to consider the security of protocols like
Canetti and Fischlin 2001
’s commitment.
To accomplish this goal, we replace equality of behaviors with
computational indistinguishability
in both the
\mathsf{UC}
and
\mathit{RC}
theories.
Making this switch in both the
\mathsf{UC}
and
\mathit{RC}
contexts produces a notion of robust compilation that corresponds precisely to the already-established definition of computational
\mathsf{UC}
security.

On the
\mathit{RC}
side, the change requires two fundamental modifications.
First,
Patrignani et al. 2022
describe programs as producing probability distributions over possible traces and consider a protocol (or compiler) secure if the ideal functionality (source program) and protocol (compiled program) produce identical distributions.
Computational indistinguishability, however, does not relate individual distributions.
It relates
families
of distributions
\{X_{n}\}
and
\{Y_{n}\}
indexed by a security parameter
n
,
and requires an adversary’s ability to distinguish between
X_{n}
and
Y_{n}
to shrink quickly as
n
grows.
We thus expand the definition of program behavior to include explicit security parameters.
Second,
\mathit{RC}
definitions in general define robustness for all programs against all adversaries, while computational
\mathsf{UC}
-security concerns only
polynomial-time
programs and attackers.
We therefore extend the
\mathit{RC}
framework to consider specific classes of protocols and attackers.

With these two modifications to the
\mathit{RC}
theory of
Abate et al. 2019
,
we can define a new class of hyperproperties,
\mathit{CH}
, where each hyperproperty is the set of behaviors that are computationally indistinguishable from some ideal functionality.
We also define a notion of Computationally-Robust Hyperproperty-Preserving Compilation (
Section

III-B
), a new notion of
\mathit{RC}
that preserves
\mathit{CH}
against polynomial-time attackers.
Finally, we prove that computational
\mathsf{UC}
security is equivalent to
Section

III-B
.

Technically, proving
Section

III-B
amounts to proving that some source program (an ideal functionality), linked with an existentially bound attacker (a simulator) is computationally indistinguishable from a target program (a protocol).
Fortunately, existing tools such as
CryptoVerif

[
13
]
provide ways to mechanize such proofs.
Thus, we showcase the ability to provide scalable proofs of computational
\mathsf{UC}
via proofs of
Section

III-B
by using
CryptoVerif
to mechanize a proof of computational indistinguishability for the
Wireguard
protocol
[
37
]
.

Notably, the results of lifting the connection between
\mathsf{UC}
and
\mathit{RC}
to the computational case contain nothing specific about computational indistinguishability and polynomial time.
As a result, we are able to substantially generalize the theory
by using any equivalence
\equiv
defining indistinguishable behaviors,
and an arbitrary predicate
\mathit{Q}
on programs and contexts to define the class of programs and contexts.
Doing so produces a notion of
\mathsf{UC}
security up-to
\equiv
, an
\mathit{RC}
notion of
\mathit{Q}
-robust preservation of
\equiv
-hyperproperties, and a proof that the two are equivalent.
This result immediately subsumes the original connection of
Patrignani et al. 2022
, using
=
as
\equiv
and the trivial predicates allowing all programs and contexts,
our lifted result, using computational indistinguishability as the equivalence and polynomial time as the predicate,
and suggests more definitions and connections to explore.

To summarize, the main contributions of this paper are:

•

III

\mathit{RC}
framework, and uses these structures to extend the result of

•

Section

IV

•

Section

VI
uses these results to mechanize the proof of
\mathsf{UC}
for the WireGuard protocol using the
CryptoVerif
tool, which provides computational security guarantees.

The rest of this paper provides background notions related to the main results (

interesting details of the proofs of our main theorems (

V
),
the presentation of related work (

VII
), and conclusions (

).

,
while theorems in

II-A

Universally Composable Security

Universally Composable (
\mathsf{UC}
) security
[
16
]

defines a security notion that combines functional correctness and
privacy and is both transitive as well as closed under protocol
composition. This is achieved by refinement: a “secure” protocol is
one that is
“at least as secure” as a protocol where all parties forward their
communication to a single entity that
(a) computes the correct output (which they forward to the environment)
and
(b) leaks only the minimal amount of information via the network
(which is conservatively modelled by sending this information to the attacker).
There is a distinction between the
environment, which models the trusted input and output from
either higher-level protocols or the user of the system,
and
the attacker, which models the hostile network.
Together,
the environment
\mathcal{Z}
,
protocol
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}

and
attacker
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}

constitute a system
that can be executed.
The execution ends when the environment decides whether it is interacting with the real protocol or an ideal simulation
and outputs a final bit to indicate its guess.
Let
\textsc{Exec}(\mathcal{Z},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}})
be a random variable describing the outcome of this probabilistic process.

The strongest notion of “at least as secure as” says that any attack on a protocol can be simulated using the functionality.
That is, only knowing the “acceptable” leakage built into the functionality is enough to convincingly reproduce any real attacker’s behavior.
For an encryption functionality, for instance, this leakage is the message length.

If this simulation can always produce
identical
behavior to the real attacker,
the protocol is said to
perfectly
emulate the ideal functionality.

Definition 1

(Perfect
\mathsf{UC}
Emulation)
.

A protocol
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}

perfectly
\mathsf{UC}
-emulates
a functionality
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}F}
, denoted
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}\vdashucperf\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}F}

if, for all (unbounded) adversaries
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}
, there is a simulator
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}S}
, such that for all environments
\mathcal{Z}
,

\textsc{Exec}(\mathcal{Z},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}})=\textsc{Exec}(\mathcal{Z},\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}S},\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}F})

For realistic cryptographic protocols, however, perfect emulation is impossible.
Their security almost invariably relies on computational hardness assumptions, so an unbounded attacker or environment
can easily glean information beyond the ideal functionality’s “acceptable” leakage.
As a result, the random variables will not be identically distributed.

To still recover a meaningful notion of security, we include a security parameter
n

and compare the behaviors asymptotically in
n
.
That is, instead of considering
\textsc{Exec}(\mathcal{Z},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}})
to be a single random variable,
we consider it to be a family of random variables,
\{\textsc{Exec}_{n}(\mathcal{Z},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}})\}
, one for each value of
n
.
Two such families are
indistinguishable
if the difference between them shrinks very rapidly (usually exponentially) as
n
grows.
This notion is defined formally as follows.

Definition 2

(Indistinguishability
[
16
]
)
.

Two ensembles of binary probability distributions
X=\{X_{n}\}
and
Y=\{Y_{n}\}

are
indistinguishable
, denoted
X\approx Y
, if,
for all
c\in\mathbb{N}
, there is some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\forall n>N.\,|\Pr[X_{n}=1]-\Pr[Y_{n}=1]|<n^{-c}

Indistinguishability is not enough by itself.
The computational hardness assumptions of the cryptographic primitives force us to limit the computational power of the parties within the system.
In particular, the
\mathsf{UC}
framework demands that all protocols, attackers, and environments execute in polynomial time.
The result is the following formal definition of computational
\mathsf{UC}
security.

Definition 3

(Computational
\mathsf{UC}
Emulation
[
16
]
)
.

A poly-time protocol
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}

computationally
\mathsf{UC}
-emulates
a functionality
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}F}
, denoted
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}\vdashuccomp\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}F}

if, for all PPT adversaries
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}
, there is a PPT simulator
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}S}
, such that for all PPT environments
\mathcal{Z}
,

\textsc{Exec}(\mathcal{Z},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}})\approx\textsc{Exec}(\mathcal{Z},\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}S},\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}F})

II-B

Robust Compilation

The Robust Compilation (
\mathit{RC}
) framework
[
2
,
3
]
formalizes security of compilers as their ability to preserve arbitrary classes of (hyper)properties
[
21
]

robustly
, i.e., in the presence of active adversaries linked with compiled code.

Compilers (denoted by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\cdot}\right\rrbracket}
) are functions that translate components, or
partial programs
(
\mathit{P}
), from a source language (
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}S}
) to a target language (
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}T}
).
In both languages, a partial program
\mathit{P}
can link with a
program context

A{}
to form a
whole program
, denoted
A{}\mathbin{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}\mathit{P}
.
Whole programs come equipped with an operational semantics called a
robust trace semantics
(
\mathrel{\rightsquigarrow}
),
which captures all security-relevant behavior of
A{}\mathbin{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}\mathit{P}
in a trace of events (
\overline{t}
)
where each event indicates whether it comes from the context or the program.

In their original work,
Abate et al. 2019
define what criteria compilers must meet to prove that they preserve:

•

trace properties, including all trace properties, safety properties, and dense properties, a variation of liveness,

•

hyperproperties, including all hyperproperties, subset-closed hyperproperties, and 2-hypersafety, and

•

relational hyperproperties.

A compiler that robustly preserves all hyperproperties satisfies
\mathit{RHC}
, defined formally as follows.

Definition 4

(Robust Hyperproperty-Preserving Compiler)
.

\displaystyle\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\cdot}\right\rrbracket}:\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{cr:rhc}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}\forall\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\ldotp\exists\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\ldotp\forall\overline{t}\ldotp\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}{}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket}\mathrel{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\mathrel{\rightsquigarrow}}}}\overline{t}\text{ iff }\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}{}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}\mathrel{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\mathrel{\rightsquigarrow}}}\overline{t}

For a compiler to preserve arbitrary hyperproperties, the traces exhibited by any compiled component while interacting with an arbitrary target context must be the same to the traces exhibited by a the source component linked with an existentially quantified source context.

\mathsf{UC}
–

Connection

\mathit{RC}

\mathsf{UC}

\mathit{RC}

\mathsf{UC}

III
Computational Robust Hyperproperty Preservation

To see the connection between perfect
\mathsf{UC}
and
Section

II-B
,
we look at the relationship between the definition of
\mathsf{UC}
security and the behavior of a program
W
, denoted
\mathit{Behav(W)}
.

Patrignani et al. 2022
define
\mathit{Behav(W)}=\{\overline{t}\mid W\mathrel{\rightsquigarrow}\overline{t}\}
as the set of traces
W
can produce,
and a trace
\overline{t}
as a pair
(\overline{\tau},\rho)
, where
\overline{\tau}
is a (potentially infinite) sequence of actions
and
\rho
is the probability that
W
will produce
\overline{\tau}
.
They also include all actions of the environment in
\overline{\tau}
(including outputting the final bit),
so we can view
\mathit{Behav(W)}
as containing different probability distributions for different environments.
If we let
\mathit{Behav(W)}|_{\mathcal{Z}}
denote the probability distribution over final bits
for just the subset of behaviors consistent with environment
\mathcal{Z}
,
the structure of
Section

II-B
begins to look very much like the structure of
\mathsf{UC}
security.

Indeed, we can rephrase
4
to require equality of behaviors
and then universally quantify over environments.
The result is the following definition of
Section

II-B
.

\forall\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\ldotp\exists\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\ldotp\forall\mathcal{Z}\ldotp\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}Behav(\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket})}|_{\mathcal{Z}}=\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}Behav(\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P})}|_{\mathcal{Z}}

And recall from
1
that
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}
perfectly
\mathsf{UC}
-emulates
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}F}
if

\forall\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\ldotp\exists\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}S}\ldotp\forall\mathcal{Z}\ldotp\textsc{Exec}(\mathcal{Z},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}})=\textsc{Exec}(\mathcal{Z},\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}S},\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}F})

\mathsf{UC}

First, the equality on distributions is replaced with computational indistinguishability.

III-A

Computational Indistinguishability

Defining indistinguishability of program behaviors requires modifying the structures introduced by
Patrignani et al. 2022
.
Recall from
Section

II-A
that indistinguishability, denoted
X\approx Y
,
is defined over
families
of distributions,
X=\{X_{n}\}
and
Y=\{Y_{n}\}
,
and requires the ability of an adversary to separate
X_{n}
from
Y_{n}
to shrink rapidly as
n
grows.
To modify the above definition of
Section

II-B
to match the structure of computational
\mathsf{UC}
security,
we would like to say
\mathit{Behav(W_{\rm 1})}|_{\mathcal{Z}}\approx\mathit{Behav(W_{\rm 2})}|_{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Unfortunately, with
\mathit{Behav(W)}
structured as a set of pairs
(\overline{\tau},\rho)
, as described above,
we can interpret
\mathit{Behav(W)}|_{\mathcal{Z}}
as a single probability distribution, but not the requisite family of them.
Luckily, because the semantics of the program are left abstract, we can straightforwardly introduce a security parameter
n
.
We thus define a program execution by a triple
(\overline{\tau},\rho,n)
instead of a pair.
Here
\overline{\tau}
and
\rho
are the same as before, and
n
is a security parameter.
With this change, we can view
\mathit{Behav(W)}
as a
family of behaviors
, indexed by
n
,
which we can similarly restrict by an environment to get a family of distributions.
That is,

\displaystyle\mathit{Behav_{n}(W)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}\{(\overline{\tau},\rho)\mid W\mathrel{\rightsquigarrow}(\overline{\tau},\rho,n)\}

\displaystyle\mathit{Behav(W)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}\{\mathit{Behav_{n}(W)}\}

\displaystyle\mathit{Behav(W)}|_{\mathcal{Z}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}\{\mathit{Behav_{n}(W)}|_{\mathcal{Z}}\}

2
now applies with these families of distributions.

Quantifying over all possible environments, however, results in a definition of statistical indistinguishability, not computational indistinguishability.
Achieving the computational goal requires restricting to only polynomial-time environments.
Because we assume the program interfaces of the different languages match, the space of environments is the same,
we use the same
PPT
set to represent all probabilistic poly-time environments.

These two modifications are sufficient to define computationally indistinguishable program behaviors.

Definition 5

(Computational Indistinguishability of Programs)
.

Whole programs
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
have
computationally indistinguishable behavior
if

\forall\mathcal{Z}\in\textrm{PPT}\ldotp\mathit{Behav(W_{\rm 1})}|_{\mathcal{Z}}\approx\mathit{Behav(W_{\rm 2})}|_{\mathcal{Z}}

We denote this equivalence by
\approxeq
for both whole programs and sets of traces.
That is, the above equivalence defines both
W_{1}\approxeq W_{2}
and
\mathit{Behav(W_{\rm 1})}\approxeq\mathit{Behav(W_{\rm 2})}
.

III-B

Computational Robust Compilation

Recall that computational
\mathsf{UC}
security allows not only this looser computational notion of indistinguishability,
but also restricts all protocols, attackers, and simulators to be polynomial time.
The language of robust compilation, however, quantifies over
all
programs and contexts, not just poly-time ones.

Without bounding the computational power of programs and contexts, we cannot hope to represent computational
\mathsf{UC}
security.
To see why, consider the single-bit commitment protocol due to
Canetti and Fischlin 2001
discussed in
Section

I
.
In the
\mathit{RC}
context, if we consider a compiler that takes
Canetti and Fischlin 2001
’s commitment ideal functionality
and produces their commitment protocol, we would hope to call that compiler robust.
But because the language of
\mathit{RC}
always considers
all
contexts (attackers) and a computationally unbounded attacker can break the protocol, we cannot.
To address this concern, we expand the framework with a notion of
computationally-robust
preservation of hyperproperties
that considers only polynomial-time programs and contexts.

As in
\mathsf{UC}
, execution complexity depends on the context (adversary), the program (protocol), and how they interact.
We therefore define a poly-time predicate over whole programs.
As with the program semantics, we leave this predicate abstract to avoid the need to restrict
to a specific computational model with specific execution times.
Because the source and target languages may have different semantics and computational models,
we include both a source-language predicate
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}poly}
and a target-language predicate
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}poly}
.

These predicates are sufficient to define a more permissive notion of robust compilation:
the
computationally-robust hyperproperty-preserving compiler
(
\mathit{CRHC}
).

Definition 6

(Computationally-Robust Hyperproperty-Preservating Compiler)
.

\displaystyle\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\cdot}\right\rrbracket}:\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{cr:comprhc}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}{}

\displaystyle\forall\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}\ldotp\forall\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\ldotp\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}poly(\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket})}\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\quad\exists\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\ldotp\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}poly(\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P})}\land\left(\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket}\approxeq\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}\right)

This computational notion considers only executions of poly-time programs in both the source and target language.
It demands that, for any target context
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}
where
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket}
is poly-time,
there must be a source context
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}
such that
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}
is also poly-time
and the behaviors of the whole programs are computationally indistinguishable.

Section

III-B
and Optimizations

Section

III-B
demands that, if there is
any
target context
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}
such that
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket}

is poly-time, then there must be some source context
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}
such that
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}
is also poly-time.
This requirement has an odd ramification:
a
Section

III-B
compiler may not optimize a super-polynomial program into a poly-time one, say, by introducing memoization.
This may appear to be a limitation, but from a security standpoint it is not.
Computational
\mathsf{UC}
security considers only the behavior of poly-time programs and poly-time ideal functionalities.
It says nothing about the security of a system with super-polynomial protocols, adversaries, environments, or ideal functionalities.
Correspondingly, the security defined by
Section

III-B
does not aim to be meaningful for super-polynomial programs.
Instead, it demands that the compiler produce super-polynomial outputs on any super-polynomial inputs,
allowing us to restrict all relevant analysis to the polynomial case.

III-C

Identifying Preserved Hyperproperties

In the original work defining
Section

II-B
,
Abate et al. 2019
identify that
Section

II-B
is equivalent to a compiler robustly preserving all hyperproperties.
Recall that a hyperproperty is a set of sets of traces.
This means that, for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\cdot}\right\rrbracket}
to be
Section

II-B
,
then for any hyperproperty
\mathit{H}
and partial program
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}
,
if the behavior of
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}
is in
\mathit{H}
for every possible source context,
then the behavior of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket}
is in
\mathit{H}
for every possible target context.

More formally, let
\mathcal{T}=\{\overline{t}\}
be the set of all traces and
\mathbb{T}=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{T})
be the set of sets of traces.
Then

\displaystyle\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\cdot}\right\rrbracket}:{}

\displaystyle\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{cr:rhc}\iff

\displaystyle\forall\mathit{H}\subseteq\mathbb{T}\ldotp\forall\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}\ldotp\begin{array}[t]{@{}l@{}}(\forall\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\ldotp\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}Behav(\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P})}\in\mathit{H})\Longrightarrow\\
(\forall\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\ldotp\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}Behav(\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket})}\in\mathit{H})\end{array}

This connection with
Section

II-B
raises a question in our context:
what hyperproperties does computational robustness preserve?
Or, more generally speaking: which class of (hyper)properties can
\mathsf{UC}
security actually express?
One might guess that
Section

III-B
is equivalent to computationally-preserving all hyperproperties.
That is, preserving all hyperproperties when considering only poly-time programs.
However, restricting to poly-time environments, which attempt to distinguish program behaviors, makes this guess incorrect.
Instead,
Section

III-B
corresponds to preserving a narrower class of hyperproperties,
\mathit{CH}
,
which represent computationally-indistinguishable programs.

To define
\mathit{CH}
, we first define
\mathit{CH}(F)
, a single hyperproperty representing
the family of behaviors that are computationally indistinguishable from what
F

can produce when given an appropriate context.
Formally,

\displaystyle\mathit{CH}(F)\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}\{T\in\mathbb{T}

\displaystyle\mid\exists S\ldotp\mathit{poly}(S\mathbin{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}F)

\displaystyle\land\left(\forall\mathcal{Z}\in\textrm{PPT}\ldotp T|_{\mathcal{Z}}\approx\mathit{Behav(S\mathbin{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}F)}|_{\mathcal{Z}}\right)\}

Intuitively,
\mathit{Behav(W)}\in\mathit{CH}(F)
means that there is some simulator
S

such that the behavior of
S\mathbin{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}F
cannot be distinguished from the behavior of
W
.
If we instead consider a partial program
\mathit{P}
and quantify over poly-time contexts,

\forall A\ldotp\mathit{poly}(A\mathbin{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}\mathit{P})\Longrightarrow\mathit{Behav(A\mathbin{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}\mathit{P})}\in\mathit{CH}(F)

means that
\mathit{P}
computationally emulates
F
.

The class
\mathit{CH}
that a
Section

III-B
compiler preserves (for poly-time programs)
is precisely the set of all of these hyperproperties.
That is, we define

\mathit{CH}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}\{\mathit{H}\mid\exists F\ldotp\mathit{H}=\mathit{CH}(F)\}

and obtain the following result.

Theorem 1

(
Section

III-B
is Computational Preservation of
\mathit{CH}
)
.

\displaystyle\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\cdot}\right\rrbracket}:{}

\displaystyle\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{cr:comprhc}\iff\forall\mathit{H}\in\mathit{CH}\ldotp

\displaystyle\forall\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}\ldotp

\displaystyle\left(\forall\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\ldotp\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}poly(\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P})}\Longrightarrow\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}Behav(\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P})}\in\mathit{H}\right)\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\forall\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\ldotp\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}poly(\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket})}\Longrightarrow\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}Behav(\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket})}\in\mathit{H}\right)

This theorem, proven in Isabelle/HOL (
\mathtt{compRHCisRHPX}
),
is a special case of
Theorem

3
(see
Section

IV
).

Theorem

1
shows that
Section

III-B
is equivalent to preserving secure emulation.
That is, a compiler
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\cdot}\right\rrbracket}
satisfies
Section

III-B
if and only if,
whenever a source program
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}
computationally emulates some functionality
F
in the source language,
then the compiled
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket}
also computationally emulates
F
in the target language.

III-D

Connecting Computational
\mathsf{UC}
and
Section

III-B

This equivalence between
Section

III-B
and secure emulation of functionalities suggests
a similarly deep connection to computational
\mathsf{UC}
security.
Indeed, the modifications to get from
Section

II-B
to
Section

III-B
were designed explicitly to mirror the differences between perfect
\mathsf{UC}
and computational
\mathsf{UC}
.
To prove this correspondence formally, we rely on four axioms laid out by
Patrignani et al. 2022

to move between the Interactive Turing Machine (ITM) semantics of the
\mathsf{UC}
framework and the abstract semantics of the
\mathit{RC}
framework.
We modify Axioms
1
,
2
, and
4
only to add the security parameter
n
.

The first uses the function
z(\overline{m})
to define a
canonical environment

for a trace prefix
\overline{m}
that produces exactly this prefix and then halts the execution with final bit 1.
It says that a trace prefix is possible if and only if a corresponding execution is possible for the ITM.

Axiom 1

(
\mathsf{UC}
and
\mathit{RC}
Semantics
[
43
]
)
.

If
\overline{m}=(\overline{\mu},\rho,n)
and
\overline{\mu}
is a finite sequence of actions, then

A\mathbin{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}{\mathit{P}}\mathrel{\rightsquigarrow}\overline{m}\iff\Pr[\textsc{ExecT}_{n}(z(\overline{m}),A,\mathit{P})=\overline{\mu}]=\rho>0.

The second axiom requires that the canonical environment for a trace correctly represent the behavior
of all environments that produce the same trace.

Axiom 2

(Canonical Environment Correctness
[
43
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{Z}
is non-probabilistic,
\overline{m}=(\overline{\mu},\rho,n)
,
and for some
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A^{\prime}}
and
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}^{\prime}}
,
\Pr[\textsc{ExecT}_{n}(\mathcal{Z},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A^{\prime}},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}^{\prime}})=\overline{\mu}]>0
,
then for all
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}
and
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}
,

\Pr[\textsc{ExecT}_{n}(\mathcal{Z}{},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}})=\overline{\mu}]=\Pr[\textsc{ExecT}_{n}(z(\overline{m}),\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}})=\overline{\mu}]

This axiom assumed that the environment
\mathcal{Z}
is non-probabilistic, meaning the probability of
\overline{m}

depends only on the randomness of
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}
and
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}
.
This assumption simplifies reasoning and is not a limitation, because we also assume that
anything a probabilistic environment can do, a non-probabilistic one can do as well.

Axiom 3

(Non-Probabilistic Environment Completeness)
.

For any
\mathcal{Z}\in\textrm{PPT}
, if
\textsc{Exec}\left(\mathcal{Z},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}\right)\not\approx\textsc{Exec}\left(\mathcal{Z},\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}S},\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}F}\right)
,
then there exists some non-probabilistic poly-time
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
such that

\textsc{Exec}\left(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}\right)\not\approx\textsc{Exec}\left(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime},\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}S},\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}F}\right)
.

Patrignani et al. 2022
’s [
43
] version of Axiom
3
uses (in)equality
instead of (in)distinguishability and does not bound
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
, but is otherwise the same.
Intuitively, this axiom is valid because, for any distinguishing
\mathcal{Z}
,
one can select from the random choices made by
\mathcal{Z}
(but not the attacker or protocol)
the ones that maximize
\mathcal{Z}
’s ability to distinguish the real and ideal worlds.
Fixing those choices produces a deterministic environment that distinguishes at least as well as
\mathcal{Z}
.

Finally, we assume that trace prefixes specify whether or not the environment has decided on a final bit
b
, and if so, what that value is.
The
\mathsf{UC}
framework assumes the environment terminates execution with that final bit,
but we follow
Patrignani et al. 2022
and abstract this process over an arbitrary encoding with an extraction function
\beta:\overline{\mu}\to\{0,1,\bot\}

with the following property.

Axiom 4

(Finite Traces Contain the Final Bit
[
43
]
)
.

\displaystyle\Pr[

\displaystyle\textsc{Exec}_{n}(\mathcal{Z},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}})=b]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta(\overline{\mu})=b}\Pr[\textsc{ExecT}_{n}(\mathcal{Z},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A},\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}})=\overline{\mu}]

Using these axioms, we are able to prove the desired correspondence between
\mathsf{UC}
security and
Section

III-B
.

Theorem 2

(Computational
\mathsf{UC}
and
Section

III-B
Coincide)
.

\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\cdot}\right\rrbracket}:\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{cr:comprhc}\iff\forall\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}\ldotp{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket}\vdashuccomp\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}

This theorem is verified in Isabelle/HOL (
\mathtt{compRHCeqUC}
).

As we will see in

,

creates a powerful new means of mechanizing a proof of

satisfies

III-B
or, equivalently, preserves

for polynomial-time programs.
That is, it suffices to prove computational indistinguishability between
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}F}
and
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\bm{\pi}}

IV
Generalizing Equivalences and Predicates

In
Sections

III-A
and
III-B
we modified
Section

II-B
to obtain the computational analogue
Section

III-B
.
Very little about that process, however, was specific to polynomial time or computational indistinguishability.
Indeed, we can generalize nearly all of those modifications and achieve a far more general result
that subsumes our results from
Section

III
, as well as the perfect security results of
Patrignani et al. 2022
,
and points to other notions of security as well.

In
Section

III-A
, we loosened the requirement of
equality
of behaviors to
computational indistinguishability
.
This change required modifying the definition of traces to interpret a set of traces as a family of probability distributions.
To capture both the original equality view and this looser view, we can generalize to an arbitrary equivalence relation
\equiv
over sets of traces,
which we will apply to program behaviors.

In
Section

III-B
, we expanded the robust compilation framework with polynomial-time predicates.
We required that behaviors only be preserved for poly-time programs,
though we restricted the existentially quantified source contexts to be poly-time as well.
There is nothing special about poly-time in that process.
Indeed, we could instead have used abstract predicates
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}Q}
and
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}Q}
over source and target programs.

Making this change leads to a more general notion of
predicate-robust hyperproperty preservation
(
\mathit{PRHC}
)
parameterized on an equivalence and two predicates.

Definition 7

(Predicate-Robust Hyperproperty-Preservating Compiler)
.

\displaystyle\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\cdot}\right\rrbracket}

\displaystyle:\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{cr:predrhc}(\equiv,\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}Q},\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}Q})\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}\forall\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}\ldotp\forall\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\ldotp\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}Q(\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket})}\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\exists\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\ldotp\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}Q(\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P})}\land\left(\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}Behav(\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket})}\equiv\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}Behav(\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P})}\right)

That is, source contexts linked with source programs can produce
all the behavior of compiled programs linked with target contexts (up to
\equiv
)
when only considering programs satisfying
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}Q}
and
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}Q}
, the respective predicates for the source and target language.

This notion generalizes both
Section

II-B
and
Section

III-B
.
If we instantiate
\equiv
with set equality (
=
) and both
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}Q}
and
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}Q}
with the trivial predicate
\mathsf{True}
that holds for all programs,

\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{cr:predrhc}(=,\mathsf{True},\mathsf{True})
requires source contexts to produce exactly the behavior of target contexts
when considering all programs—precisely
Section

II-B
.

Proposition 1

(
Section

IV
expresses
Section

II-B
)
.

\begin{array}[]{c}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\cdot}\right\rrbracket}:\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{cr:predrhc}(=,\mathsf{True},\mathsf{True})\\
\Updownarrow\\
\forall\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}\ldotp\forall\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\ldotp\exists\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\ldotp\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}Behav(\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P}}\right\rrbracket})}=\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}Behav(\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}A}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P})}\\
\end{array}

which is the definition of
\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\llbracket{\cdot}\right\rrbracket}:\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{cr:rhc}
.

Similarly, we can instantiate
\equiv
with computational indistinguishability (
\approxeq
),
interpreting traces as families of distributions and restricting to poly-time environments,

\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}Q}
with
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}poly}
, and
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}Q}
with
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}poly}

VI-A

The
CryptoVerif
Tool

CryptoVerif
is a mechanized prover for properties of security protocols in the computational model.
A
CryptoVerif
proof is a polynomial-length sequence of cryptographic games—where an active attacker attempts to break some security property and is allowed to run multiple sessions in parallel.
Each game is represented in a language with a probabilistic semantics where all computations run in polynomial time,
and the goal is to show that each game is indistinguishable from the next.

CryptoVerif
proofs are thus proofs of computational indistinguishability between two games,
one of which is generally trivially secure (e.g., distinguishing between two encryptions of 0).

We use the game language of
CryptoVerif
as an instance for both the source and target languages of the compilers described in
Section

III
.
Those languages require semantics that are probabilistic and where processes run in polynomial time, assumptions which
CryptoVerif
satisfies.
Our strategy is to encode both the ideal functionality and the protocol as games, and prove that they are computationally indistinguishable.
This, in turn, shows that the compiler that translates the functionality into the protocol (and does nothing else) satisfies
Section

III-B
,
and thus the protocol
\mathsf{UC}
-realises the functionality.

CryptoVerif
provides two input languages, channels and oracles.
We use the oracle language, as it is closer to
CryptoVerif
’s internal representation and thus grants more flexibility in the encoding.
In this model, the parties to a protocol are represented by oracles, which are simple probabilistic programs with shared state.
The adversary controls the network as well as the scheduling of messages by invoking these oracles.
An adversary invoking an oracle models sending a message to a party and then receiving a response.
This structure supports the attacker injecting messages—by invoking an oracle with an input it generated—dropping messages—by declining to send the output of one oracle as the input to another—or simply observing all network traffic.

CryptoVerif
models parties that answer many requests in a uniform way,
such as servers, by replicating an oracle:
given some finite bound
n
, the attacker can use
n
different copies of the oracle,
each with their own state.

VI-B

The
Wireguard
Protocol

Wireguard
is a widely-used protocol for establishing a VPN tunnel between two remote hosts in order to securely encapsulate all Internet Protocol (IP) traffic between them.
It consists of two parts: a key exchange and a record subprotocol.
To establish a tunnel, the key exchange subprotocol requires
the IP address and long-term public key for the remote host.
It then uses an instantiation of the Noise framework for key exchange
with fast, modern cryptographic primitives, like
Curve25519 and BLAKE2, to establishes two ephemeral keys (one per
direction) that parties use to
encrypt and authenticated messages in the subsequent record subprotocol.
The record subprotocol can then use these symmetric keys to construct a secure channel.

We verify a model of
Wireguard
introduced by
Lipp et al. 2019
, which is already encoded in
CryptoVerif
.
1
1

1

They provide several models. We build on a model in which long-term keys can be dynamically corrupted
(
WG.25519.AB-BA.S_i_compr.S_r_compr.replay_prot.cv
).

For the sake of simplicity, we focus on the 2-party version of
Wireguard
, with a sender and receiver who are both honest throughout the run.

We also only model the record protocol, where payloads are transmitted after parties have agreed on a common, ephemeral symmetric session key.
The compositional nature of
\mathsf{UC}
security means that one could obtain a proof of the full
Wireguard
protocol
simply by combining our proof with a corresponding proof for the key exchange protocol, which we leave for future work.
We make this choice due to a conceptual challenge in proving the security of key exchange.
The key confirmation message in
Wireguard
—as well as other secure channel protocols like TLS—leaks a very small amount of information about the key, complicating composition arguments.

Fischlin et al. 2016
discuss this problem and
provide a (non-composable) solution,
while the model of
Lipp et al. 2019
omits this message altogether.

Finally, we introduce a modification to the original model.
As an argument to the protocol,
Lipp et al. 2019
include a secret bit that the two parties share.
Before sending a payload, a party waits for two messages from the adversary and uses the secret bit to determine which one to transmit.
The attacker must then determine the value of this secret bit.
This modeling choice deviates from any actual
Wireguard
implementation, which should not include this secret bit or a second payload.
Instead, a more faithful model would take this direction from the environment, not the adversary.
To represent this more realistic model, we build a simple model of communication between the
Wireguard
parties (sender and receiver),
add a dummy adversary, and use the
CryptoVerif
attacker to model the environment.
We then add functionality from
Lipp et al. 2019
’s model in a piece-wise fashion until we have recovered the full record protocol.

VI-D

Computational Indistinguishability Proof

We prove our two models (real and ideal) are computationally indistinguishable through a series
of eight
CryptoVerif
games that the tool does not find automatically.
Two of those games are cryptographic reductions, while the others are perfect equivalences.
We guide
CryptoVerif
to these games with a proof script consisting of seven steps.

The scrip starts from the real world
\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}A_{d}}\mathbin{\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}\boldsymbol{\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}}\mathbf{\color[rgb]{1,0.23,0.13}P_{WG}}
.
The first step applies the IND-CTXT assumption of the encryption scheme, which guarantees ciphertext integrity.
This check matches a structural check in the ideal functionality and ensures that every received ciphertext was sent by the sender.
The second step applies the IND-CPA assumption of the encryption scheme, which guarantees ciphertext confidentiality.
This step replaces message encryptions with encryptions of zero.
The other five steps consist of removing dead code, inlining variables,
and minimally modifying code structure to produce exactly the ideal world
\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}S_{WG}}\mathbin{\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\raisebox{1.42082pt}{$\scriptstyle\bowtie$}}}\mathit{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}P_{WG}}
.

VI-E

Discussion

The lack of private communication such as private channels led us to use cross-oracle state sharing to transfer information between oracles and to inline parties where they are called.
This is a manual process that is highly prone to encoding errors, and we believe adding private channels to
CryptoVerif
would mitigate this issue.
Indeed, this feature seems available internally, but we confirmed through the manual and communication with the maintainers that is is not exposed to the user.
In the long term, the addition of a module system that allows for compositional proofs might be even better, as it could syntactically ensure that composition is correctly encoded, for example according to
Patrignani et al. 2022
.
In addition, such a system could easily validate that states are properly encapsulated, e.g., that simulator oracles cannot directly access the state of the functionality.

The most closely-related work is that of

, which highlights the
\mathsf{UC}
–

connection but does not broach the topic of computational indistinguishability.
The authors use the connection to mechanize the proof that 1-bit commitment protocols

\mathsf{UC}

VII-A

Universally Composable Cryptographic Models

Universal composability
[
16
]
extended
simulated-based notions of correctness and privacy for multi-party
computation
[
29
]

to the interactive case, additionally ensuring that security
guarantees are preserved when a protocol is used as a subroutine within
a larger protocol.
Many competing frameworks
[
7
,
30
,
15
,
35
,
53
]
followed to improve on perceived
inadequacies in the model or to correct subtle errors in the proofs.
Currently, the original
\mathsf{UC}
framework is in its sixth version. A common
point of contention is how to define polynomial runtime in a reactive
system.

Hofheinz et al. 2013
explain the
problem in great detail and propose
a solution that is (conceptually) reflected in all these frameworks

[
16
,
30
,
15
,
35
,
53
]
,
at least in their latest versions.
Our abstract poly-time predicate over attacker-protocol combinations
(see
Section

III
) can capture the poly-time notions in each of
them.

These frameworks define
\mathsf{UC}
bottom-up, fixing a plethora of
technical details like the machine model (often interactive Turing
Machines), message formats, message and process schedulers,
virtualisation mechanisms, addressing mechanisms, etc.
None of these details are at all similar to how networks and programs operate in the real world,
yet many of the differences between the frameworks boil down to technical
minutia at very low-level of abstraction.
This is reflected in the proofs, which are as hard to formalize as
they are to write down.

By contrast, our results operate at a much higher level of abstraction.

Patrignani et al. 2022
provide a high-level composition proof for this model,
which we verified in Isabelle/HOL is not impacted by any of our modifications (
\mathtt{composition}
).
Indeed, that result relies on a small set of assumptions
[
43
, Axioms 5 to 7]

that most likely hold for any of the frameworks discussed above.

Canetti et al. 2019
confirmed these issues, when
they
mechanized parts of UC in
EasyCrypt
[
10
]
, lamenting
that
“despite the relative simplicity [of their case studies], [proving UC] took an immense amount of work.”
It is thus unsurprising that other attempts to mechanize the
concept
[
4
,
11
,
36
]

avoid this technical baggage and
build their frameworks on formal languages that provide a much higher degree of
abstraction. Still, all these frameworks perceive
\mathsf{UC}
as a property
between programs written in one and the same language.
With
Theorem

2
, one can use
\mathit{RC}
as a tool
to transfer such properties across language boundaries.

VII-B

Alternative Tools for
CryptoVerif

We verified our case study in
CryptoVerif
, but there are a variety of other tools for cryptographic verification.

EasyCrypt
[
23
]
is a stand-alone theorem prover with focus on cryptographic
primitives and small protocols.
It reasons at at a code level using probabilistic relational Hoare logic and external SMT solvers.
There are also various embeddings of probabilistic languages in general-purpose theorem provers like
CryptHOL
[
11
]
and FCF
[
44
]
.
They all require most of the proof to be written out, although some
automated proof tactics can help with simple steps.

Squirrel
[
8
]
is a protocol-specific prover
for computational indistinguishability based on a computationally-sound
attacker model that can be symbolically reasoned about. In contrast to

CryptoVerif
and EasyCrypt, which focus on program transformations to deduce
equivalences, Squirrel’s reasoning focusses on (symbolic) traces.
Currently Squirrel proofs are as detailed as proofs in EasyCrypt and similar tools,
though the project aims to reduce the complexity.

Only EasyCrypt has previously been used for
\mathsf{UC}
-style proofs
[
18
]
.
Leveraging our results, we were able to produce a proof in
CryptoVerif
,
which we chose because it promised a higher degree of automation.
Our case study confirms this promise, though manual guidance was still required.

VII-C

Language-Based Tools for Cryptographic Security

Several language-based tools exist with the goal of easing the process of developing and verifying secure cryptographic protocols.
Languages like Wysteria
[
45
]
, Wys
∗

[
46
]
and Symphony
[
50
]
are designed to simplify
the design and implementation of secure distributed systems using cryptography.
Viaduct
[
6
]
and Jif/Split
[
56
,
58
]

use security policies specified by information flow labels to automatically partition programs,
synthesizing uses of particular cryptographic primitives when necessary.
These systems generally do not contain formal proofs of security,
and we hope our results will make it easier to provide them with
\mathsf{UC}
-style guarantees.

Security type systems can also help prove security.
An information-flow type system can ensure proper combination of specific cryptographic primitives
[
25
,
26
]
.
Owl
[
27
]
uses a security type system with built-in primitives
to enforce computational guarantees.
While these builtins do not fit our goal of
abstracting (any) primitive via functionalities,
they provide a meaningful level of composition
and are worth investigating. In general, type-systems are fast
and compositional.

VII-D

Robust Compilation

Abate et al. 2019
introduced a large hierarchy of
\mathit{RC}
criteria
that have been used to reason about the security of compilers that preserve memory safety
[
41
,
1
]
,
absence of speculation leaks
[
42
]
, and cryptographic constant time
[
33
,
34
]
.
They require source and target languages to have the same trace model, though they show how to lift that limitation in subsequent work
[
3
]
.
While we also require our languages to share a trace model, we make that choice for simplicity.
The same approach should generalize our result as well.
Interestingly, lifting the same limitation in
\mathsf{UC}
would let us formalize the existence of two distinct, but related, environments, one in the real and one in the ideal world.
We are unaware of a notion of
\mathsf{UC}
with distinct environments; after all, they are all just (interactive) Turing Machines.

VIII
Conclusion

In this work, we have generalized the connection between
\mathsf{UC}
and
\mathit{RC}
to the computational setting,
and then generalized the connection further to arbitrary indistinguishability relations and predicates over programs.
We showcased the benefits of this expanded connection by using
CryptoVerif
, a tool for proving computational indistinguishability,
to mechanize a proof of
\mathsf{UC}
security for the
Wireguard
protocol.

These results let us conclude that
CryptoVerif
can be used to
modularize cryptographic proofs, which are currently very monolithic.
We believe our demonstrated connections will extend similar benefits to other tools,
though we leave this investigation for future work.

Additionally, there exists a compiler from
CryptoVerif
into OCaml
[
14
]

that the authors proved, already in 2013, robustly preserves hyperproperties.
Though the
\mathit{RC}
framework was only developed years later, the result is equivalent.
Since translating from the ideal functionality into the protocol is a compiler,
we can create a toolchain that translates
CryptoVerif
ideal functionalities all the way to executable OCaml protocols.
This toolchain would combine properties of sequential composition of compilers
[
34
]
and our results
to ensure end-to-end guarantees of
\mathsf{UC}
security for real-world executable protocols.
However, as we hint at the end of
Section

VI
, a module system in
CryptoVerif
would streamline the creation of this toolchain, which we also leave for future work.
