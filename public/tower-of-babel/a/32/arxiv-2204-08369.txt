Benign Overfitting in Time Series Linear Model with Over-Parameterization
Shogo Nakakita † and Masaaki Imaizumi †‡

Abstract
. The success of large-scale models in recent years has increased the importance of statistical models with numerous parameters. Several studies have analyzed over-parameterized linear models with high-dimensional data, which may not be sparse; however, existing results rely on the assumption of sample independence. In this study, we analyze a linear regression model with dependent time-series data in an over-parameterized setting. We consider an estimator using interpolation and develop a theory for the excess risk of the estimator. Then, we derive non-asymptotic risk bounds for the estimator for cases with dependent data. This analysis reveals that the coherence of the temporal covariance plays a key role; the risk bound is influenced by the product of temporal covariance matrices at different time steps. Moreover, we show the convergence rate of the risk bound and demonstrate that it is also influenced by the coherence of the temporal covariance. Finally, we provide several examples of specific dependent processes applicable to our setting.

1.
Introduction

In this study, we analyze the high-dimensional time-series linear regression problem with dependent covariates.
Suppose there are
n
covariates,
x_{1},...,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with dimension
p\in\mathbb{N}
, and
n
responses,
y_{1},...,y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, observed from the following linear model with an unknown true parameter
\beta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
:

\displaystyle y_{t}=\langle\beta^{*},x_{t}\rangle+\varepsilon_{t},~t=1,...,n,

(1)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Here, the covariates
\{x_{t}:t=1,...,n\}
and the independent noise
\{\varepsilon_{t}:t=1,...,n\}
are stationary, centered Gaussian processes that are independent of each other.
It should be noted that we allow for dependence within
\{x_{t}:t=1,...,n\}
and
\{\varepsilon_{t}:t=1,...,n\}
.
We focus on the over-parameterized case, where the dimension
p
is significantly larger than
n
(and possibly infinite).
In this study, we derive sufficient conditions under which the risk of an estimator for the model converges to zero as
n\to\infty
, indicating benign overfitting, under several settings of dependence within
\{x_{t}:t=1,...,n\}
.

Statistical methods for high-dimensional and large-scale data analysis have garnered significant attention over the past decades. A prominent approach leverages sparsity through
\ell_{1}
norm regularization and its variants
(
Candes and Tao 2007
;
Van de Geer 2008
;
Bühlmann and Van De Geer 2011
;
Hastie et al. 2019
)
, which is effective when the target signal contains many zero elements.
Another line of research has explored the asymptotic risk behavior of estimators in high-dimensional settings
(
Dobriban and Wager 2018
;
Belkin et al. 2019
;
Hastie et al. 2022
;
Bartlett et al. 2020
;
Tsigler and Bartlett 2023
;
Nakakita et al. 2024
)
and has revealed the limiting risk behavior when the number of data points
n
and parameters
p
tend to infinity while their ratio
p/n
converges to a positive constant.
Interpolators, which are estimators that perfectly fit the observed data in the
p\gg n
regime, have been shown to have risk or variance that converges to zero
(
Liang and Rakhlin 2020
;
Hastie et al. 2022
;
Ba et al. 2019
;
Tsuda and Imaizumi 2024
)
.
Notably, these studies did not exploit the sparsity of data or signals; however, they remain compatible with recent large-scale data analysis.

In the context of high-dimensional time-series data, several challenges remain unresolved.
The first challenge is that no consistent estimator has been established unless sparsity is imposed.
Although several consistent estimators with sparsity exist
(
Wang et al. 2007
;
Basu and Michailidis 2015
;
Han et al. 2015
;
Wong et al. 2020
)
, demonstrating the consistency of estimators without sparsity is a non-trivial task, as discussed in
Daskalakis et al. 2019
;
Kandiros et al. 2021
.
This difficulty arises because many probabilistic tools used in high-dimensional statistics rely on the assumption of variable independence.
The second challenge is identifying a statistic that influences the estimation risk in this setting.
In the case of independent data,
Bartlett et al. 2020
and
Tsigler and Bartlett 2023
showed that the effective rank explains the risk.
For low-dimensional or sparse high-dimensional time-series data, it has been shown that temporal covariance matrices characterize the risk of estimators
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
;
Wong et al. 2020
)
.
However, in high-dimensional time-series settings without sparsity, these statistics are not valid measures.

In this study, we investigate the risk of an interpolation estimator in stochastic linear regression (
1
) with high-dimensional dependent processes to tackle these challenges.
Specifically, we introduce an assumption on the coherence of temporal covariance matrices and derive non-asymptotic bounds for the prediction risk of the interpolation estimator, motivated by recent advances in neural networks that achieve perfect data fitting.
We further demonstrate the convergence rate of the derived bound as
n\to\infty
under tail assumptions on the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix of the data.
These results hold when the data dimension is sufficiently large, under a condition on the covariance matrix.
This setup includes the following cases: a stationary Gaussian process
\{x_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
, which can be (i) a separable vector autoregressive moving-average (ARMA) process; (ii) a non-separable vector ARMA process; or (iii) a time-varying regression model under temporal dependence.

Our results address the challenges of high-dimensional time-series analysis, as summarized below:
(i) We develop a consistency theory for the estimator in a high-dimensional time-series regression model (
1
) without assuming sparsity of the target parameter.
Specifically, we clarify that the interpolation estimator is consistent under a spectral condition on the covariance matrix in the time direction, in addition to the spectral condition on the covariance matrix in the spatial direction.
(ii) We identify that
coherent temporal covariance
, defined as the product of temporal covariance matrices with a reference matrix, characterizes the risk in high-dimensional time-series estimation.
Specifically, we denote the covariance matrix of the
k
-th component of the covariate as
\Xi_{k,n}
and an arbitrary reference matrix as
\Xi_{0,n}
, and we show that ensuring the eigenvalues of
\Xi_{k,n}^{-1}\Xi_{0,n}
remain finite and bounded away from zero is crucial for risk control.
Unlike the spatial-directional condition identified by
Bartlett et al. 2020
, the temporal-directional condition we derive is expressed in terms of the eigenvalues of a matrix product.
Furthermore, this property has not been addressed in studies on high-dimensional time-series models with sparsity, e.g.,
Basu and Michailidis 2015
, and it represents a unique characteristic of our over-parameterized setup.

On the technical side, our core results rely on two key contributions.
First, we observe that the interpolation estimator in linear regression can implicitly decorrelate observations.
This result alone provides insight into benign overfitting when the covariate process has a separable structure.
Second, we analyze the spectral structure of temporally dependent random matrices.
This represents a sophisticated extension of the analyses by
Bartlett et al. 2020
and
Tsigler and Bartlett 2023
to time series models.
Specifically, we control the deviation of the spectral structure from the i.i.d. case and show that it asymptotically coincides up to constant factors.

1.1.
Related studies

In this paper, we discuss the following two types of related studies.

High-dimensional time-series
:
Time-series analysis of high-dimensional data has been conducted for stochastic regression and vector auto-regression (VAR) estimation problems using various sparsity-induced regularizations.
These studies applied regularization under the assumption of sparsity.

Wang et al. 2007
proposed a flexible parameter tuning method for the lasso that can be applied to VAR.

Alquier and Doukhan 2011
studied a general scheme of
\ell_{1}
-norm regularization for dependent noise with a wide class of loss functions.

Song and Bickel 2011
focused on large-scale VAR and its sparse estimation.

Basu and Michailidis 2015
developed a spectrum-based characterization of dependent data and employed a restricted eigenvalue condition for
\ell_{1}
-norm regularization of dependent data.

Kock and Callot 2015
studied the lasso for time-series data and established an oracle inequality and model selection consistency.

Han et al. 2015
employed the Danzig selector for high-dimensional VAR and analyzed its efficiency based on the spectral norm of a transition matrix.

Wu and Wu 2016
developed a widely applicable risk analysis method for time-series lasso under various tail probability distributions.

Guo et al. 2016
focused on applying the lasso to VAR with banded transition matrices.

Davis et al. 2016
proposed a two-step estimation algorithm for VAR with sparse coefficients.

Medeiros and Mendes 2016
studied time-series lasso with non-Gaussian and heteroscedastic covariates.

Masini et al. 2022
derived an oracle inequality for sparse VAR with fat probability tails and various dependent settings.

Wong et al. 2020
studied time-series lasso with general tail probability and dependence by relaxing a restriction in
Basu and Michailidis 2015
.
These studies imposed sparsity on the data; thus, they are applicable to non-sparse high-dimensional data in our setting.

Bunea et al. 2022
studied a factor model with over-parameterization within the framework of benign overfitting.

Large-scale models with dependent data
:
In contrast to studies focusing on sparsity, few studies have analyzed dependent data in the context of large-scale models. Learning theory has advanced predictive risk analysis of dependent data through uniform convergence and complexity measures. Unlike sparsity-based approaches, these methods can be applied relatively easily to large-scale models.

Yu 1994
demonstrated uniform convergence of empirical processes under mixing conditions and derived its convergence rate.
Mohri and Rostamizadeh 2008
introduced Rademacher complexity to analyze predictive risks in stationary mixing processes.
Berti et al. 2009
established uniform convergence of empirical processes under exchangeability.
Mohri and Rostamizadeh 2010
proved data-dependent Rademacher complexity bounds for various types of mixing processes.
Agarwal and Duchi 2012
derived upper bounds on generalization errors for general loss functions and mixing processes.
Kuznetsov and Mohri 2015
established an upper bound on predictive errors for stochastic processes without assuming stationarity or mixing.
Dagan et al. 2019
derived a generalization error bound for dependent data that satisfied the Dobrushin condition.
Daskalakis et al. 2019
and
Kandiros et al. 2021
applied the Ising model, analyzed several linear models with dependent data, and derived novel estimation risk bounds.
These studies yielded general results, but they did not consider scenarios where the number of parameters tends to infinity, such as in over-parameterization.

2.
Setting and assumption

2.1.
Gaussian process with spatio-temporal covariance

We first define a
p
-dimensional covariate process
\{x_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
and the corresponding design matrix
X=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We construct the covariate process by multiplying temporal and spatial covariance matrices with a random matrix whose entries are i.i.d. This approach generalizes separable models in spatial and spatio-temporal statistics
(
Cressie 1993
;
Kyriakidis and Journel 1999
;
Chen et al. 2021
, for details, see)
and encompasses studies on random matrices with temporal dependence
(
Couillet et al. 2015
;
Tian 2022
;
Tian and Yao 2022
)
.

Consider positive definite matrices
\Xi_{i,n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i=1,\ldots,p
and
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{p}
, that represent the temporal and the spatial covariance, respectively.
We also introduce

Z=[z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}]
,
z_{i}\sim N(\mathbf{0},I_{n})
as a matrix with standard Gaussian i.i.d. entries.
Let
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}>0
be the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
,
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{p}\}
be a diagonal matrix, and
U\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{p}
be an orthogonal matrix such that
\Sigma=U\Lambda U^{\top}
.
Then, we set the design matrix
X=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{p}
for the covariates as

\displaystyle X=\left[\Xi_{1,n}^{1/2}z_{1},\ldots,\Xi_{p,n}^{1/2}z_{p}\right]\Lambda^{1/2}U^{\top}.

(2)

If there exists
\Xi_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\Xi_{i,n}=\Xi_{n}
for all
i=1,...,n
, the definition (
2
) is simplified to

\displaystyle X=\Xi_{n}^{1/2}Z\Lambda^{1/2}U^{\top},

(3)

which we call a separable model.
The covariate process
\{x_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
has the spectral decomposition,
that is,
\{x_{t}^{\top}u_{i}\}_{t}
and
\{x_{t}^{\top}u_{j}\}_{t}
with the column vectors
\{u_{i}\}
of
U
and
i\neq j
are independent of each other.
We set this independence to yield the concentration inequalities for some random matrices, which play important roles in our analysis (see Appendix
E
).
This property appears in some physical models with stochastic partial differentiable equations; see
Lototsky 2009
;
Lototsky and Rozovsky 2017
.

The process has simple (possibly lagged) autocovariance matrices as follows: for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in\{1,2,...,n\}
, the autocovariance matrix is written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[x_{t_{1}}x_{t_{2}}^{\top}\right]=U\mathrm{diag}\left\{\Xi_{1,n}^{(t_{1},t_{2})}\lambda_{1},\ldots,\Xi_{p,n}^{(t_{1},t_{2})}\lambda_{p}\right\}U^{\top}.

(4)

If all
\Xi_{i,n}
are Toeplitz matrices, i.e. their
(t_{1},t_{2})
-th elements dependent only on
|t_{1}-t_{2}|
, the covariance (
4
) is further simplified.
Note that the autocovariance matrix of
x_{t}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}]
, is not necessarily equal to the spatial covariance matrix
\Sigma
.
This fact does not matter in our study.

We present an auto-regressive moving-average (ARMA) process as simple examples of processes with these structures.
Let
\{w_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of independent
p
-dimensional Gaussian vectors whose mean is zero and covariance matrix is
Q
with
\mathrm{rank}{(Q)}>n
.

Example 1

(vector ARMA process)
.

We present a vector ARMA process with the structure (
2
):

\displaystyle x_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{1}}\rho_{j}x_{t-j}+w_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{2}}\varphi_{j}w_{t-j},

(5)

where
\rho_{j},\varphi_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{p}
are coefficient matrices with spectral decompositions with an orthogonal basis
\left\{e_{k}\right\}
such that
\rho_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell_{1}}\rho_{j,k}e_{k}e_{k}^{\top}
, and
\varphi_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell_{2}}\varphi_{j,k}e_{k}e_{k}^{\top}
.
We also assume that
Q=\sum_{k=1}^{p}q_{k}e_{k}e_{k}^{\top}
and represent
e_{k}^{\top}w_{t}=\sqrt{q_{k}}z_{t,k}
with i.i.d.
z_{t,k}\sim N(0,1)
.
Here, we define
\varpi_{1,k}\left(z\right)=1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{1}}\rho_{j,k}z^{j}
and
\varpi_{2,k}\left(z\right)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{2}}\varphi_{j,k}z^{j}
as characteristic polynomials of the AR and MA parts, respectively, and assume that
\varpi_{1,k}\left(z\right)\neq 0
for all
z
with
|z|\leq 1
and they do not have roots on the unit circle.
There exist causal MA representations
e_{k}^{\top}x_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j,k}e_{k}^{\top}w_{t-j}
with
\{\phi_{j,k}\in\mathbb{R}\}
such that
\varpi_{2,k}(z)/\varpi_{1,k}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j,k}z^{j}

(
Brockwell and Davis 1991
, Theorem 3.1.1)
.
It holds that
\Xi_{k,n}^{(t,t+h)}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j,k}\phi_{j+|h|,k}/\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j,k}^{2}
and
\Sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{p}q_{k}(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j,k}^{2})e_{k}e_{k}^{\top}
.

Remark 1
.

Note that the representation of
\Xi_{k,n}
and
\Sigma
are not unique.
We see that
\Xi_{k,n}^{\rm new}:=c\Xi_{k,n}
and
\Sigma^{\rm new}:=c^{-1}\Sigma
with a scaling factor
c>0
give the same covariance structure for
x_{t}
as
\Xi_{k,n}
and
\Sigma
.
One of the reasons to introduce the MA representation with
\{\phi_{j,k}\}
is to let these representations be unique.

2.2.
Stochastic regression problem and interpolation estimator

We consider the stochastic regression problem with the covariate process
\{x_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
defined above.
Suppose that we have
n
observations,
(x_{1},y_{1}),...,(x_{n},y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}
with dimension
p
, which follow the stochastic linear regression model (
1
) for
t=1,...,n
with an unknown true parameter,
\beta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

\{\varepsilon_{t}:t=1,...,n\}
is a Gaussian process such that
\mathcal{E}:=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})^{\top}\sim N\left(\mathbf{0},\Upsilon_{n}\right)
, where
\Upsilon_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a positive definite matrix denoting the autocovariance of
\varepsilon_{t}
.
Note that
x_{t}
and
\varepsilon_{t}
are independent of each other.
We define
Y=(y_{1},...,y_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a design vector.

We consider an interpolation estimator with the minimum norm:

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\|\beta\|^{2}\mbox{~~s.t.~~}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}(y_{t}-\langle\beta,x_{t}\rangle)^{2}=0,

(6)

with norm
\|\cdot\|
for
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
A solution satisfying this constraint in (
6
) is guaranteed to exist if
\{x_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
spans an
n
-dimensional linear space, i.e., if
\mathbb{R}^{p}
has a larger dimensionality than
n
(for example,
\mathbb{R}^{p}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
p\geq n
), then multiple solutions satisfy the linear equation
Y=\langle X,\beta\rangle
.
The interpolation estimator (
6
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}=(X^{\top}X)^{\dagger}X^{\top}Y=X^{\top}(XX^{\top})^{-1}Y,

(7)

where
†
denotes the pseudo-inverse of matrices.

We study an excess prediction risk of the interpolation estimator
\widehat{\beta}
as

\displaystyle R(\widehat{\beta}):=\mathbb{E}^{*}[(y^{*}-\langle\widehat{\beta},x^{*}\rangle)^{2}-(y^{*}-\langle{\beta}^{*},x^{*}\rangle)^{2}],

(8)

where
(x^{\ast},\varepsilon^{\ast})
is an independent random element such that
x^{\ast}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\Sigma)
,
\varepsilon^{\ast}\sim N(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
with arbitrary
\sigma_{\varepsilon}>0
, and
y^{\ast}=(x^{\ast})^{\top}\beta^{\ast}+\varepsilon^{\ast}
and expectation
\mathbb{E}^{*}[\cdot]
is taken with respect to
(x^{*},y^{*})
.
The setting of
(x^{*},y^{*})
can be justified as an approximation of
(x_{T},y_{T})
with large
T
when
x_{t}
is mixing, or
x_{t}
and
x_{t+h}
with large
h
are asymptotically independent.

2.3.
Effective ranks and numbers

In preparation for our analysis, we define several notions related to matrices.
The first is the
effective rank
of matrices, which measures the complexity of a matrix using its eigenvalues.

Definition 1

(effective rank)
.

For a matrix
T\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define two types of effective ranks of
T
:

\displaystyle r_{k}(T)=\frac{\sum_{i>k}\mu_{i}(T)}{\mu_{k+1}(T)},\mbox{~and~}R_{k}(T)=\frac{(\Sigma_{i>k}\mu_{i}(T))^{2}}{(\sum_{i>k}\mu_{i}(T)^{2})}.

Furthermore, we define an effective number of bases with constants
b>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle k^{*}(b)=\min\{k\geq 0:r_{k}(\Sigma)\geq bn\}.

If the set is empty, we set
k^{*}(b)=\infty
.

This notion has been used in studies related to the matrix concentration inequality
(
Koltchinskii and Lounici 2017
)
, and its application to high-dimensional linear regression
(
Bartlett et al. 2020
)
. This measure is based on the degree of decay of the eigenvalues, rather than the number of nonzero eigenvalues.

3.
Non-asymptotic risk analysis

We present a non-asymptotic analysis of the excess risk of the interpolation estimator. Subsequently, we provide a sufficient condition on the covariance matrices of the covariates for the excess risk to converge to zero.

3.1.
Assumption: coherent temporal covariance

We provide an assumption for our analysis of the excess risk. Importantly, we require an assumption regarding the coherence of the temporal covariance
\Xi_{0,n}
, as defined in (
2
), as stated below.

Assumption 1

(coherent temporal covariance)
.

There exist a positive number
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and a family of
n\times n
positive definite matrices
\Xi_{0,n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
(reference matrices) such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i=1,\ldots,p
, we have

\displaystyle\epsilon\leq\inf_{i,n}\mu_{n}(\Xi_{0,n}^{-1}\Xi_{i,n})\leq\sup_{i,n}\mu_{1}(\Xi_{0,n}^{-1}\Xi_{i,n})\leq 1/\epsilon.

Under this assumption, we have
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}(\|\Xi_{0,n}^{-1}\Xi_{i,n}-I\|\vee\|\Xi_{i,n}^{-1}\Xi_{0,n}-I\|)\leq(1-\epsilon)/\epsilon
.
In summary, this assumption aims to preserve the spectral structure of
x_{t}
even under temporal dependence.
A similar assumption has been observed in high-dimensional time-series analysis. For example,
Bi et al. 2022
suppose a time-uniform spectral gap condition on lagged autocovariance matrices to conserve the spectral structure.

Let us introduce some notation used within this section:
\Sigma_{0:k}^{\dagger}
has the representation
\Sigma_{0:k}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{-1}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}
.
In addition, we use the notation

A_{k}=\sum_{j>k}\lambda_{j}\widetilde{z}_{j}\widetilde{z}_{j}
, where
\widetilde{z}_{j}=\Xi_{0,n}^{-1/2}\Xi_{j,n}^{1/2}z_{j}
for all
k=0,\ldots,p
under Assumption
1
.
In Section
6
, we show that several stochastic processes have covariance matrices that satisfy this assumption.

3.2.
Upper bound on the excess risk

We first provide the upper bound on the risk.
Here, we utilize the effective rank of
\Sigma
and weighted norm of
\beta^{*}
, which is projected onto a subspace spanned by
\Sigma_{k^{*}:\infty}
and
\Sigma_{0:k}^{\dagger}
.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the interpolation estimator
\widehat{\beta}
in (
6
) for the stochastic regression problem (
1
).
Suppose that Assumption
1
and
\lambda_{n+1}>0
holds.
Then, there exist constants
b=b(\epsilon)\geq 1
and
c=c(\epsilon)\geq 1
dependent only on
\epsilon
such that for all
\delta\in(0,1)
with
\delta<1-c\exp(-n/c)
: (i) if
k^{*}\geq n/c
with
k^{\ast}:=k^{\ast}(b)
holds, then
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}R(\widehat{\beta})\geq 1/b^{2}c^{2}\left\|\Upsilon_{n}^{-1}\Xi_{0,n}\right\|
holds with probability at least
1-c\exp(-n/c)
; (ii) otherwise, it holds that

\displaystyle R(\widehat{\beta})

\displaystyle\leq c\left(\left\|\beta^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{k^{*}:\infty}}^{2}+\left\|\beta^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{0:k^{*}}^{\dagger}}^{2}\left(\frac{\sum_{i>k^{*}}\lambda_{i}}{n}\right)^{2}\right)+c\|\Xi_{0,n}^{-1}\Upsilon_{n}\|\left(\frac{k^{*}}{n}+\frac{n}{R_{k^{*}}\left(\Sigma\right)}\right)\log(\delta^{-1})

with probability at least
1-\delta-c\exp(-n/c)
.

The upper bound is composed of two elements: the first term bounds the bias part of the risk, and the second term represents the variance.
We will describe a way to derive this bound in Section
5
.

An important implication of this theorem is that the effect of temporal dependence structures on benign overfitting is negligible under the coherence of temporal covariance matrices.
Let us compare our results with the bound for i.i.d. sub-Gaussian settings: a combination of
Bartlett et al. 2020
and
Tsigler and Bartlett 2023

gives an upper bound as

\displaystyle R(\widehat{\beta})

\displaystyle\leq c\left(\left\|\beta^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{k^{*}:\infty}}^{2}+\left\|\beta^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{0:k^{*}}^{\dagger}}^{2}\left(\frac{\sum_{i>k^{*}}\lambda_{i}}{n}\right)^{2}\right)+c\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(\frac{k^{*}}{n}+\frac{n}{R_{k^{*}}\left(\Sigma\right)}\right)\log(\delta^{-1}),

(9)

with probability at least
1-\delta-c\exp(-n/c)
, where
\sigma_{\varepsilon}>0
is the upper bound on the sub-Gaussian norms of
\varepsilon_{i}
.
Our bound given by Theorem
1
depends on
\Sigma
in the same manner as (
9
) under the coherence of temporal covariances such as Assumption
1
and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|\Xi_{0,n}^{-1}\Upsilon_{n}\|<\infty
.
It is worth mentioning that setting
\Xi_{0,n}=I_{n}
and
\Upsilon_{n}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I_{n}
coincides the two bounds.

We provide additional insights by comparing Theorem
1
with other high-dimensional time-series studies:
(i) Theorem
1
depends on the closeness of the covariances of
x_{t}
and
\varepsilon_{t}
, i.e.,
\|\Xi_{0,n}^{-1}\Upsilon_{n}\|
, rather than on the dependence between
x_{t}
and
\varepsilon_{t}
.
This is an unusual property in time-series analysis; previous studies on sparsity-based high-dimensional analysis
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
;
Wu and Wu 2016
;
Wong et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
rely on the long-term dependence structures of
x_{t}
and/or
\varepsilon_{t}
, but the coherence of their structures is not necessarily beneficial.
(ii) The upper bound given by Theorem
1
depends on the
\ell^{2}
-norm of
\beta^{*}
, in contrast to
Basu and Michailidis 2015
, which depends on the
\ell^{1}
-norm (or the number of nonzero coordinates) of
\beta^{*}
.
(iii) The variance term increases inversely with the smallest eigenvalue of
\Xi_{0,n}
when we assume
\Upsilon_{n}=I_{n}
, which indicates that the covariate process should not degenerate in the time direction.
This point can be regarded as a time-series analog of the restricted eigenvalue condition in high-dimensional analysis
(
Bickel et al. 2009
;
Van de Geer 2008
)
.

Remark 2

(implicit temporal decorrelation)
.

Theorem
1
demonstrates an implicit temporal decorrelation effect induced by the interpolation estimator (
6
).
If there exists
\sigma_{\varepsilon}>0
such that
\Xi_{0,n}^{-1}\Upsilon_{n}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I_{n}
, then the bound in Theorem
1
is identical to that in the i.i.d. case (
9
).
This observation suggests that when
\Xi_{0,n}^{-1}\Upsilon_{n}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I_{n}
, the interpolation estimator
implicitly decorrelates
temporal dependence, even if the true values of
\Xi_{0,n}
or
\Upsilon_{n}
are unknown.
Theorem
1
can thus be interpreted as an approximation of this implicit decorrelation.

3.3.
Lower bound on the excess risk

We establish a lower bound for the risk.
To this end, we consider a randomized version of
\beta^{*}
using a prior distribution.
We adopt this setting only in this section to derive the lower bound.
We also assume that
U=I_{p}
without loss of generality.

Assumption 2

(prior of
\beta^{*}
)
.

Let
\bar{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be an arbitrary nonrandom vector and
\beta^{\ast}
is an
\mathbb{R}^{p}
-valued random vector such that we have

\displaystyle\left(U^{\top}\beta^{\ast}\right)^{(i)}=R_{i}\bar{\beta}^{(i)},\ i=1,\ldots,p,

where
\{R_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,p}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with
P(R_{i}=1)=P(R_{i}=-1)=1/2
and independent of other random variables.

This setting is employed in
Tsigler and Bartlett 2023
for the i.i.d. data scenario. The response variable
\{y_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
is assumed to be generated by the regression model (
1
), conditioned on
\beta^{*}
. Within this framework, we derive the following lower bound, which considers the expectation of the risk with respect to
\beta^{*}
as part of the results.

Theorem 2
.

The following statements hold:

(i)

Under the same assumptions as Theorem
1
, there exist constants
b=b(\epsilon)\geq 1
and
c=c(\epsilon)\geq 1
dependent only on
\epsilon
such that if
k^{*}\leq n/c
with
k^{\ast}:=k^{\ast}(b)
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[R(\widehat{\beta})]\geq\frac{1}{c\left\|\Upsilon_{n}^{-1}\Xi_{0,n}\right\|}\left(\frac{k^{*}}{n}+\frac{n}{R_{k^{*}}\left(\Sigma\right)}\right),

with probability at least
1-c\exp(-n/c)
.

(ii)

Under Assumption
2
and the assumptions in Theorem
1
, there exist constants
b=b(\epsilon)\geq 1
and
c=c(\epsilon)\geq 1
dependent only on
\epsilon
such that for any
k\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
with
r_{k}(\Sigma)\geq bn
, with probability at least
1-10e^{-n/c}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E},\beta^{\ast}}[R(\widehat{\beta})]\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\lambda_{i}\left(\bar{\beta}^{(i)}\right)^{2}}{\left(1+\frac{c\lambda_{i}n}{\lambda_{k+1}r_{k}(\Sigma)}\right)^{2}}.

These lower bounds demonstrate the tightness of each term in the upper bound in Theorem
1
.
Specifically, the first lower bound (i) corresponds to the variance term in Theorem
1
, while the second bound corresponds to the bias term, up to some constants.

Regarding the comparison with the i.i.d. setting, the lower bounds match those in the i.i.d. setting (Theorem 10 of
Tsigler and Bartlett 2023
) up to a constant factor.
More precisely, the result provides several insights: (i) the dependence among data does not affect the bias term of the risk, as shown in Theorem
1
, and (ii) the dependence affects the variance term in the bounds.

4.
Benign covariance: convergence rate analysis

We present sufficient conditions under which the upper bound converges to zero as
n\to\infty
.
Recall that both
\Sigma
and its eigenvalues may depend on the sample size
n
, such that
\Sigma=\Sigma_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

4.1.
Preparation

Further, we introduce a characterization on the (spatial) covariance,
\Sigma
, developed by
Bartlett et al. 2020
.

Definition 2

(benign covariance)
.

The covariance matrix
\Sigma
is
benign
, if for all
\beta_{n}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}
with
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|\beta_{n}^{\ast}\|<\infty
, the following sequences
\{\tau_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
\{\eta_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
are convergent to
0
:

\displaystyle\tau_{n}:=\left\|\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{k^{*}:\infty}}^{2}+\left\|\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{0:k^{*}}^{\dagger}}^{2}\left(\frac{\sum_{i>k^{*}}\lambda_{i}}{n}\right)^{2},\mbox{~~and~~}\eta_{n}:=\max\left\{\frac{k^{*}}{n},\frac{n}{R_{k^{*}}(\Sigma)}\right\}.

The study provides specific examples of benign covariance.
Although the analysis of covariance does not have a significant direct relationship with dependent data, we included it for completeness.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\lambda_{i,n}=\mu_{i}(\Sigma_{n})
for all
i=1,\ldots,p_{n}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(i)

If we have
\lambda_{i,n}=i^{-\gamma}\mathbf{1}\{i\leq p_{n}\}
for
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
n\prec p_{n}\prec n^{1/(1-\gamma)}
, then
\Sigma_{n}
is benign.
Moreover, we have
\tau_{n}=\mathcal{O}(p_{n}^{1-\gamma}n^{-1})
and

\eta_{n}=\begin{cases}\Theta_{\gamma}\left((n/p_{n})^{1/\gamma-1}+n/p_{n}\right)&\text{if }\gamma\in(0,0.5),\\
\Theta_{\gamma}\left((n/p_{n})^{1/\gamma-1}+\frac{\log(p_{n}/n)}{p_{n}/n}\right)&\text{if }\gamma=0.5,\\
\Theta_{\gamma}\left((n/p_{n})^{1/\gamma-1}\right)&\text{if }\gamma\in(0.5,1).\end{cases}

(ii)

If we have
\lambda_{i,n}=i^{-1}\mathbf{1}\{i\leq p_{n}\}
and
n\exp(\omega(\sqrt{n}))\prec p_{n}\prec n\exp(o(n))
, then
\Sigma_{n}
is benign.
Moreover, we have
\tau_{n}=\mathcal{O}(\log(p_{n}/n)/n)
and
\eta_{n}=\Theta_{\gamma}(1/\log(p_{n}/n)+n/(\log(p_{n}/n))^{2})
.

(iii)

If we have
\lambda_{i,n}=(\gamma_{i}+\epsilon_{n})\mathbf{1}\{i\leq p_{n}\}
with
\gamma_{i}=\Theta(\exp(-i/\tau))
,
p_{n}=\omega(n)
,
\epsilon_{n}p_{n}=n\exp(-o(n))
, and
\epsilon_{n}p_{n}=o(n)
, then
\Sigma_{n}
with
\|\Sigma_{n}\|=1
is benign.
Moreover, we have
\tau_{n}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{n}p_{n}/n)
and
\eta_{n}=\mathcal{O}(\log(n/\epsilon_{n}p_{n}))/n+\max\{1/n,n/p_{n}\})
.

(iv)

If we have

\lambda_{i,n}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i\leq s_{n},\\
\epsilon_{n}&\text{if }s_{n}<i\leq p_{n},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

with
\epsilon_{n}>0
,
s_{n}=o(n)
,
p_{n}=\omega(n)
, and
\epsilon_{n}p_{n}=o(n)
, then
\Sigma_{n}
is benign.
Moreover, we have
\tau_{n}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{n}+(\epsilon_{n}p_{n}/n)^{2})
and
\eta_{n}=\mathcal{O}(s_{n}/n+n/p_{n})
.

(v)

If we have

\lambda_{i,n}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i=1,\\
\epsilon_{n}\frac{1+\theta^{2}-2\theta\cos(k\pi/(p_{n}+1))}{1+\theta^{2}-2\theta\cos(\pi/(p_{n}+1))}&\text{if }1<k\leq p_{n},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

with
\theta<1
and
\epsilon_{n}>0
,
p_{n}=\omega(n)
and
\epsilon_{n}p_{n}=o(n)
, then
\Sigma_{n}
is benign.
Moreover, we have
\tau_{n}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{n}+(\epsilon_{n}p_{n}/n)^{2})
and
\eta_{n}=\mathcal{O}(1/n+n/p_{n})
.

In case (i),
\Sigma
is independent of
n
; otherwise, it depends on
n
.
Cases (i) and (ii) yield infinite nonzero eigenvalues, while cases (iii) and (iv) yield
p_{n}
nonzero eigenvalues, which represent finite dimensionality increasing in
n
.
Note that all the sequences
\{\zeta_{n}\}_{n},\{\tau_{n}\}_{n}
and
\{\eta_{n}\}_{n}
are simply calculated from the proof of Theorem 31 in
Bartlett et al. 2020
.

4.2.
Convergence rate

We derive a general form of convergence rates of the risk revealed in Section
3
.
In preparation, we define a notation for the temporal correlation

\displaystyle\nu_{n}:=\|\Xi_{0,n}^{-1}\Upsilon_{n}\|.

(10)

In the study by
Basu and Michailidis 2015
, a similar condition was required, namely, that the lower bound on the spectral density of the sums of lagged autocovariance matrices should be positive. By contrast, we employed a non-asymptotic approach to study the eigenvalues of matrices under finite time widths.

We derive a convergence rate of risk by using the notations for the benign covariance.

Proposition 4

(convergence rate)
.

Consider the setting and assumption for Theorem
1
.
Suppose that
\Sigma
is a benign covariance, as stated in Definition
2
.
Also suppose that
\|\beta^{*}\|=O(1)
as
n\to\infty
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle R(\widehat{\beta})=O_{P}\left(\tau_{n}+\nu_{n}\eta_{n}\right),\mbox{~as~}n\to\infty.

This rate consists of two terms: the first corresponds to the bias part, and the second part corresponds to the variance.
The bias part is determined by the volume of
k^{*}
and the decay speed of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
, while the variance part is determined by
\eta_{n}
and the strength of temporal correlation
\nu_{n}
.
Given that the rate in the i.i.d. case by
Tsigler and Bartlett 2023
is given as

\displaystyle R(\widehat{\beta})=O_{P}\left(\tau_{n}+\eta_{n}\right),

we can see that
\nu_{n}^{-1}
reveals the effect of temporal dependence.

5.
Proof outline of upper bounds

Theorems
1
and
2
are primarily based on the upper and lower bounds for the bias term established by
Tsigler and Bartlett 2023
.
The proof relies on the bias–variance decomposition of the excess risk
R(\widehat{\beta})
into a bias term
T_{B}
and a variance term
T_{V}
, after which we bound each term separately.
Notably, this decomposition plays a crucial role in the proof of Theorem
1
.

Lemma 5
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, we obtain the following with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle R(\widehat{\beta})\leq 2\beta^{*\top}T_{B}\beta^{*}+2C_{\delta}\mathrm{tr}(T_{V}),

where
C_{\delta}=2\log(1/\delta)+1
. Also,
T_{B}
and
T_{V}
are defined as

\displaystyle T_{B}:=(I-X^{\top}(XX^{\top})^{-1}X)\Sigma(I-X^{\top}(XX^{\top})^{-1}X),

\displaystyle T_{V}:=\Upsilon_{n}^{1/2}\left(XX^{\top}\right)^{-1}X\Sigma X^{\top}\left(XX^{\top}\right)^{-1}\Upsilon_{n}^{1/2}.

Furthermore, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}R(\widehat{\beta})\geq\beta^{*\top}T_{B}\beta^{*}+\mathrm{tr}(T_{V})
.

We briefly describe the proof outline via this lemma, as well as the implicit decorrelation by the interpolator.
Specifically, we consider the simplest structure of
x_{t}
given by (
3
).
If
\Xi_{n}
is non-degenerate, we see that

\widehat{\beta}=X^{\top}(XX^{\top})^{-1}Y=X^{\top}\Xi_{n}^{-1/2}(\Xi_{n}^{-1/2}XX^{\top}\Xi_{n}^{-1/2})^{-1}\Xi_{n}^{-1/2}Y.

Therefore, we can regard the interpolator
\widehat{\beta}
in (
6
) is equivalent to

\widehat{\beta}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\|\beta\|^{2}\text{ s.t. }\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{t}^{\text{deco}}-\langle\beta,x_{t}^{\text{deco}}\rangle)^{2}=0,

where

Y^{\text{deco}}=\left[\begin{matrix}y_{1}^{\text{deco}}\\
\vdots\\
y_{n}^{\text{deco}}\end{matrix}\right]=\Xi_{n}^{-1/2}\left[\begin{matrix}y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
y_{n}\end{matrix}\right],\ X^{\text{deco}}=\left[\begin{matrix}(x_{1}^{\text{deco}})^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
(x_{n}^{\text{deco}})^{\top}\end{matrix}\right]=\Xi_{n}^{-1/2}\left[\begin{matrix}x_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}^{\top}\end{matrix}\right]=Z\Lambda^{1/2}U^{\top}.

Hence, we can analyze
(X^{\text{deco}},Y^{\text{deco}})
in the same way as our observations instead of considering the observable
(X,Y)
itself.
It is noteworthy that
X^{\text{deco}}
is equivalent to the i.i.d. case considered in
Bartlett et al. 2020
and
Tsigler and Bartlett 2023
, and that the matrix
T_{B}
depends only on the covariates and is independent of the noise.
As a result, we obtain an upper bound for
(\beta^{\ast})^{\top}T_{B}\beta^{\ast}
that is identical to that of
Tsigler and Bartlett 2023
.

We also discuss the role of Assumption
1
in the temporal covariance matrices.
When the covariate takes the form (
2
), a more delicate analysis of random matrices with non-homogeneous temporal dependence across coordinates is required.
Assumption
1
allows us to address such heterogeneity in a concise manner.
We can again transform the problem in (
6
) as follows:

\widehat{\beta}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\|\beta\|^{2}\text{ s.t. }\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{t}^{\text{deco}}-\langle\beta,x_{t}^{\text{deco}}\rangle)^{2}=0,

where
Y^{\text{deco}}=\Xi_{0,n}^{-1/2}Y
and
X^{\text{deco}}=[\Xi_{0,n}^{-1/2}\Xi_{1,n}^{1/2}z_{1},\ldots,\Xi_{0,n}^{-1/2}\Xi_{p,n}^{1/2}z_{p}]\Lambda^{1/2}U^{\top}
.
Under Assumption
1
, even though each
\Xi_{0,n}^{-1/2}\Xi_{1,n}^{1/2}z_{i}
is not necessarily isotropic, its deviation from the isotropic case can be uniformly bounded in
n
.
Hence, we can extend the results for the i.i.d. case to our dependent case via a more elaborate analysis.

Furthermore, we obtain information specific to time-series data when decomposing the variance term.
Regarding
\mathrm{tr}(T_{V})
, the temporal covariance matrix of the noise term after decorrelation is given by
\Xi_{n}^{-1/2}\Upsilon_{n}\Xi_{n}^{-1/2}
.
Therefore, using
\mathrm{tr}(AB)\leq\|A\|\mathrm{tr}(B)
for positive semi-definite matrices
A
and
B
, we obtain an upper bound on
\mathrm{tr}(T_{V})
that is identical to that of
Tsigler and Bartlett 2023
, up to the scaling factor
\|\Xi_{n}^{-1}\Upsilon_{n}\|
.
This factor plays the same role as
\nu_{n}
in Section
4
.

6.
Example

As an example, we analyze multiple stochastic processes and risks of the stochastic regression problem with the processes.

6.1.
Separable vector ARMA process

We consider a separable vector ARMA process,
x_{t}
, is defined as

\displaystyle x_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{1}}\left(a_{i}I_{p}\right)x_{t-i}+w_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{2}}\left(b_{i}I_{p}\right)w_{t-i},

(11)

where
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
is a number of lag, and
a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is a coefficient.
Let us assume that the characteristic polynomial of the auto-regressive (AR) part,
\varpi_{1}\left(z\right):=1-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{1}}a_{i}z^{i}
, for
z\in\mathbb{C}
does not have roots for any
z
with
|z|\leq 1
, nor common roots with the characteristic polynomial for the moving-average (MA) part,
\varpi_{2}\left(z\right):=1+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{2}}b_{i}z^{i}
in
\mathbb{C}
.
There exists a causal MA representation
x_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j}w_{t-j}
with a sequence
\{\phi_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\}
, which is square-summable and satisfies
\left(1+\sum_{i}b_{i}z^{i}\right)/\left(1-\sum_{i}a_{i}z^{i}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j}z^{j}

(e.g.,
Brockwell and Davis 1991
, Theorem 3.1.1)
.
We can see that
x_{t}
is a separable process such that
\Xi_{n}^{\left(t,t+h\right)}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j}\phi_{j+\left|h\right|}
for all
t=1,\ldots,n
and
h
with
t+h\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
and
\Sigma=Q
.
If those assumptions are not satisfied, stationarity may not hold; see Section 3.1 of
Brockwell and Davis 1991
.

With the conditions on
\varpi_{1}(z)
and
\varpi_{2}(z)
, Proposition 4.5.3 of
Brockwell and Davis 1991
yield the following inequality for the neighboring condition:

\displaystyle\min_{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1}\left|\frac{\varpi_{2}(z)}{\varpi_{1}(z)}\right|\leq\inf_{n}\mu_{n}\left(\Xi_{n}\right)\leq\sup_{n}\mu_{1}\left(\Xi_{n}\right)\leq\max_{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1}\left|\frac{\varpi_{2}(z)}{\varpi_{1}(z)}\right|.

This property gives the following convergence of excess risk without proof.

Corollary 6
.

Consider the setting and assumptions for Theorem
1
and that the covariate follows the process (
11
).
Suppose that
\Sigma=Q
is a benign covariance with sequences
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\eta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in Definition
2
, and
\sup_{n}\mu_{1}(\Upsilon_{n})<\infty
holds.
Then, we have
\nu_{n}\leq\sup_{n}\mu_{1}(\Upsilon_{n})/\min_{z:|z|=1}(|\varpi_{2}(z)|/|\varpi_{1}(z)|)<\infty
and thus we get

\displaystyle R(\widehat{\beta})=O_{P}\Bigl(\tau_{n}+\eta_{n}\Bigr).

This result shows that the case of ARMA has the same convergence rate as that of independent data with the same condition on
\Sigma
.

6.2.
Non-separable vector ARMA process

We present an example of processes in (
5
) in Example
1
.
For the characteristic polynomials
\varpi_{1,k}\left(z\right)
and
\varpi_{2,k}\left(z\right)
defined in Example
1
, we additionally assume there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\left|\varpi_{1,k}\left(z\right)\right|
and
\left|\varpi_{2,k}\left(z\right)\right|
are in
[\epsilon,\epsilon^{-1}]
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\left|z\right|=1
.
Recall that we have
\Sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\lambda_{k}e_{k}e_{k}^{\top}
with
\lambda_{k}:=q_{k}(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j,k}^{2})
where
\{\phi_{j,k}\in\mathbb{R}\}
satisfies
\Xi_{k,n}^{(t,t+h)}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j,k}\phi_{j+|h|,k}/\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j,k}^{2}
.

The following proposition verifies the equivalence of
\Sigma
and
Q
and the coherence condition by Assumption
1
.

Proposition 7
.

The following properties hold true for (
5
):

(i)

1\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j,k}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{-4}
.

(ii)

\epsilon^{8}\leq\inf_{k,n}\mu_{n}\left(\Xi_{k,n}\right)\leq\sup_{k,n}\mu_{1}\left(\Xi_{k,n}\right)<\epsilon^{-4}
.

We see that
\Sigma
and
Q
are equivalent by Proposition
7
-(i), and
Assumption
1
can be satisfied by Proposition
7
-(ii).
Therefore, Theorem
1
immediately gives the following result without proof:

Corollary 8
.

Consider the setting in Theorem
1
with covariate
\{x_{t}\}_{t}
that follows the process (
5
).
Suppose that
Q
is a benign covariance with sequences
\{\zeta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\eta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, as stated in Definition
2
.
Then,
\Sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{p}q_{k}(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi_{j,k}^{2})e_{k}e_{k}^{\top}
is also a benign covariance and we have

\displaystyle R(\widehat{\beta})=O_{P}\left(\tau_{n}+\nu_{n}\eta_{n}\right).

(12)

This upper bound does not depend on dependence-based terms, such as
\bar{\Sigma}_{n}
; therefore, it can be observed that the upper bound is equivalent to that in the independent data case in
Bartlett et al. 2020
.
Additionally, the derived convergence rate is identical to that in the independent case.
In other words, the same rate is achieved for the excess risk as in the independent case because the process has short memory.

6.3.
Time-varying regression model under temporal dependence

In time-series data analysis, it is often natural to assume that model structures, such as the autocovariance functions of
x_{t}
and/or
\varepsilon_{t}

(
Kuznetsov and Mohri 2015
;

Conclusion and discussion

In this study, we investigate the excess risk of an estimator for an over-parameterized linear model with dependent time-series data. We construct an estimator using an interpolator that perfectly fits the data and measure the excess prediction risk. In addition to the concept of effective ranks of autocovariance matrices used for independent data, we develop several notions to handle temporal covariance and subsequently derive upper and lower bounds on the excess risk. This result holds in the high-dimensional regime, where
p\gg n
, regardless of sparsity. Our analysis shows that the coherence of temporal covariance matrices affects the risk bound and the convergence rates we derive. The analysis is based on the implicit decorrelation achieved by the interpolation estimator and the control of the coherence property.

A limitation of this study is that the dependent data are assumed to be Gaussian. While assuming Gaussianity is common in dependent data analysis, several methods exist to relax this assumption. However, in our study, Gaussianity plays a crucial role in reducing data correlation to handle the bias term. In the over-parameterized setting, exploring methods to avoid the Gaussianity assumption is an important direction for future work.

An important avenue for future research is the application to more complex models, such as latent variable models. For example,
Bunea et al. 2022
considered a factor model in an independent data setting and showed that latent noise significantly impacts the conditions for benign overfitting. Understanding how this effect changes under dependent data is an interesting direction for further study.

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\epsilon^{-2}\max\left(\left(t+n\log 9\right)\lambda_{1},\sqrt{\left(t+n\log 9\right)\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}}\right).

By Lemma S.8 of

, there exists a universal constant

such that with probability at least

\displaystyle\left\|A-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\tilde{\Xi}_{i,n}\right\|\leq c_{3}\epsilon^{-2}\left(\left(t+n\log 9\right)\lambda_{1}+\sqrt{\left(t+n\log 9\right)\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}}\right).

Note that

\displaystyle\left\|A-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\Xi_{i,n}\right\|=\max_{v\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}\left|v^{\top}\left(A-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\Xi_{i,n}\right)v\right|\geq\max_{v\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}v^{\top}Av-\epsilon^{-1}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i},

and

\displaystyle\left\|A-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\Xi_{i,n}\right\|=\max_{v\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}\left|v^{\top}\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\Xi_{i,n}-A\right)v\right|\geq\epsilon\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}-\min_{v\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}v^{\top}Av.

The remaining discussion is parallel to
Bartlett et al. 2020
.
∎

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left((u^{\top}\otimes I_{n})^{\top}(u^{\top}\otimes I_{n})\right)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\left[\begin{matrix}u^{(1)}u^{(1)}I_{n}&\cdots&u^{(1)}u^{(k)}I_{n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
u^{(k)}u^{(1)}I_{n}&\cdots&u^{(k)}u^{(k)}I_{n}\end{matrix}\right]\right)=n.

Therefore, for all
t>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\epsilon>c\epsilon^{-1}(t+k\log 9)
, with probability
1-2e^{-t}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(\Sigma_{0:k}^{-1/2}X_{0:k}^{\top}\Xi_{0,n}^{-1}X_{0:k}\Sigma_{0:k}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\inf_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{k-1}}\left\|\Xi_{0,n}^{-1/2}X_{0:k}\Sigma_{0:k}^{-1/2}u\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\left(\sqrt{n\epsilon}-\sqrt{c\epsilon^{-1}(t+k\log 9)}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\Sigma_{0:k}^{-1/2}X_{0:k}^{\top}\Xi_{0,n}^{-1}X_{0:k}\Sigma_{0:k}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{k-1}}\left\|\Xi_{0,n}^{-1/2}X_{0:k}\Sigma_{0:k}^{-1/2}u\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(\sqrt{n\epsilon^{-1}}+\sqrt{c\epsilon^{-1}(t+k\log 9)}\right)^{2}.

(Step 2) We control
\tilde{Z}_{k:\infty}
.
For some absolute constant
c>0
, for all
u\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{p-k}
, we have

P\left(\left|u^{\top}\tilde{Z}_{k:\infty}v\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}\epsilon}{c\|v\|^{2}}\right)

since it holds that

u^{\top}\tilde{Z}_{k:\infty}v=\sum_{i=1}^{p-k}v^{(i)}u^{\top}\Xi_{0,n}^{-1/2}\Xi_{k+i,n}^{1/2}z_{k+1}\sim N\left(\mathbf{0},\sum_{i=1}^{p-k}(v^{(i)})^{2}u^{\top}\Xi_{0,n}^{-1/2}\Xi_{i+k,n}\Xi_{0,n}^{-1/2}u\right).

Therefore, we obtain that for some absolute constant
c>0
(different than
c
above)

P\left(\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left|u^{\top}\tilde{Z}_{k:\infty}v\right|^{2}\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t\epsilon}{c\|v\|^{2}}+n\log 9\right),

and as a result, we obtain

P\left(\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left|u^{\top}\tilde{Z}_{k:\infty}v\right|^{2}\geq c\epsilon^{-1}\|v\|^{2}(t+n\log 9)\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-t\right).

(Step 3) The results above show that for some absolute constant
c>0
, for any
t>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
t+k\log 9<nc^{-1}\epsilon^{2}
, with probability
1-4e^{-t}
, we have

\displaystyle(\beta^{\ast})^{\top}T_{B}\beta^{\ast}/c

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\beta_{k:\infty}^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{k:\infty}}^{2}

(22)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mu_{1}\left(A_{k}^{-1}\right)^{2}}{\mu_{n}\left(A_{k}^{-1}\right)^{2}}\frac{\left(\sqrt{n\epsilon^{-1}}+\sqrt{c\epsilon^{-1}(t+k\log 9)}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{n\epsilon}-\sqrt{c\epsilon^{-1}(t+k\log 9)}\right)^{4}}c\epsilon^{-1}(t+n\log 9)\left\|\beta_{k:\infty}^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{k:\infty}}^{2}

(23)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\left\|\beta_{0:k}^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{0:k}^{-1}}}{\mu_{n}\left(A_{k}^{-1}\right)^{2}\left(\sqrt{n\epsilon}-\sqrt{c\epsilon^{-1}(t+k\log 9)}\right)^{2}}

(24)

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda_{k+1}\mu_{1}\left(A^{-1}\right)c\epsilon^{-1}(t+n\log 9)\left\|\beta_{k:\infty}^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{k:\infty}}^{2}

(25)

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda_{k+1}\frac{\mu_{1}\left(A_{k}^{-1}\right)}{\mu_{n}\left(A_{k}^{-1}\right)^{2}}\frac{\left(\sqrt{n\epsilon^{-1}}+\sqrt{c\epsilon^{-1}(t+k\log 9)}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{n\epsilon}-\sqrt{c\epsilon^{-1}(t+k\log 9)}\right)^{4}}\left\|\beta_{0:k}^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{0:k}^{\dagger}}^{2}.

(26)

By setting

sufficiently large, we obtain the conclusion.

Corollary 23
.

Under Assumption 1, there are constants

and

such that for any

such that

and

, with probability at least

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\beta_{k:\infty}^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{k:\infty}}^{2}+\left\|\beta_{0:k}^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{0:k}^{-1}}^{2}\left(\frac{\lambda_{k+1}r_{k}(\Sigma)}{n}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

We consider the first term on the right-hand side.

,
\mu_{1}(A_{k}^{-1})^{2}/\mu_{n}(A_{k}^{-1})^{2}\leq c(\epsilon)

\mu_{1}(A^{-1})\leq\mu_{1}(A_{k}^{-1})=\mu_{n}(A_{k})^{-1}\leq c(\epsilon)/(\lambda_{k}r_{k+1}(\Sigma))\leq c(\epsilon)/(b(\epsilon)n),

where the first inequality holds with probability 1 by Lemma S.11 of
Bartlett et al. 2020
.

For the second term, Lemma 15 again gives that

Lemma 24

Proof of Theorem 3.

All the results on
\eta_{n}
are immediate from Theorem S.14 of
Bartlett et al. 2020
.
Therefore, we examine only the rate of convergence of
\tau_{n}
.

To give bounds on
\tau_{n}
for (i)–(iii), the following fact is useful: since
k^{\ast}=k^{\ast}(b)=\min\{k\geq 0;\sum_{i>k}\lambda_{i}\geq bn\lambda_{k+1}\}
and
\sum_{i>k^{\ast}}\lambda_{i}\leq\sum_{i\geq k^{\ast}}\lambda_{i}\leq bn\lambda_{k^{\ast}}
as long as
k^{\ast}\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\tau_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=k^{\ast}+1}^{p_{n}}\lambda_{i}\left(u_{i}^{\top}\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k^{\ast}}\lambda_{i}^{-1}\left(u_{i}^{\top}\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\sum_{i=k^{\ast}+1}^{p_{n}}\lambda_{i}}{n}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{k^{\ast}+1}\sum_{i=k^{\ast}+1}^{p_{n}}\left(u_{i}^{\top}\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k^{\ast}}\lambda_{i}^{-1}\left(u_{i}^{\top}\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right)^{2}\left(b\lambda_{k^{\ast}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{k^{\ast}}\sum_{i=k^{\ast}+1}^{p_{n}}\left(u_{i}^{\top}\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right)^{2}+b^{2}\lambda_{k^{\ast}}\sum_{i=1}^{k^{\ast}}\frac{\lambda_{k^{\ast}}}{\lambda_{i}}\left(u_{i}^{\top}\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq b^{2}\lambda_{k^{\ast}}\left\|\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right\|^{2},

where
u_{i}
is the column vectors of
U
.
The exact orders of
k^{\ast}
for (i)–(iii) are given by
Bartlett et al. 2020
and we obtain the conclusions via them.

As for
\tau_{n}
in (iv) and (v), we just evaluate them directly.
For (iv), since
k^{\ast}=s_{n}
holds, we have

\displaystyle\tau_{n}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{n}\sum_{i=s_{n}+1}^{p_{n}}\left(u_{i}^{\top}\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{s_{n}}\left(u_{i}^{\top}\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\sum_{i=s_{n}+1}^{p_{n}}\epsilon_{n}}{n}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\epsilon_{n}+\left(\frac{\epsilon_{n}p_{n}}{n}\right)^{2}\right)\left\|\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right\|^{2}.

For (v), since
k^{\ast}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\tau_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{p_{n}}\lambda_{i,n}\left(u_{i}^{\top}\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right)^{2}+\left(u_{1}^{\top}\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\sum_{i=2}^{p_{n}}\lambda_{i,n}}{n}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\epsilon_{n}\frac{\left(1+\theta\right)^{2}}{\left(1-\theta\right)^{2}}+\left(\frac{\epsilon_{n}p_{n}}{n}\right)^{2}\frac{\left(1+\theta\right)^{4}}{\left(1-\theta\right)^{4}}\right)\left\|\beta_{n}^{\ast}\right\|^{2}.

Proof of Proposition 4.

In this proof, we study the terms in the bias part of the bound in Theorem 1.
By Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\beta^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{k^{*}:\infty}}^{2}+\left\|\beta^{\ast}\right\|_{\Sigma_{0:k^{*}}^{\dagger}}^{2}\left(\frac{\sum_{i>k^{*}}\lambda_{i}}{n}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\beta^{*}\right\|^{2}\left(\lambda_{k^{*}+1}+\lambda_{k^{*}}^{-1}\left(\frac{\sum_{i>k^{*}}\lambda_{i}}{n}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|\beta^{*}\right\|^{2}\left(\lambda_{k^{*}+1}+\lambda_{k^{*}}^{-1}\left(\frac{\sum_{i\geq k^{*}}\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k^{*}}}{n}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|\beta^{*}\right\|^{2}\left(\lambda_{k^{*}+1}+\lambda_{k^{*}}^{-1}\lambda_{k^{*}}^{2}\left(\frac{\sum_{i\geq k^{*}}\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k^{*}}}{\lambda_{k^{*}}n}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|\beta^{*}\right\|^{2}\left(\lambda_{k^{*}+1}+\lambda_{k^{*}}\left(\frac{r_{k^{*}-1}(\Sigma)-1}{n}\right)^{2}\right),

which follows the definition of
r_{k}(\Sigma)
in Definition 1.
With the loss of generality, we set
r_{k^{*}-1}(\Sigma)\geq 1
.
With the specified constant
b\geq 1
with
k^{\ast}=k^{\ast}(b)\geq 1
, the relation
r_{k^{*}-1}(\Sigma)\leq bn
by Definition 1 yields

\displaystyle\left\|\beta^{*}\right\|^{2}\left(\lambda_{k^{\ast}+1}+\lambda_{k^{\ast}}\left(\frac{r_{k^{\ast}-1}(\Sigma)-1}{n}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\beta^{*}\right\|^{2}\left(\lambda_{k^{\ast}+1}+\lambda_{k^{\ast}}\left(\frac{bn-1}{n}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\beta^{*}\right\|^{2}\left(\lambda_{k^{\ast}+1}+\lambda_{k^{\ast}}b^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c\|\beta^{*}\|^{2}\lambda_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq c\|\beta^{*}\|^{2}\frac{\sum_{i\geq k^{*}}\lambda_{i}}{bn}

\displaystyle\leq c\|\beta^{*}\|^{2}\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)}{bn},

where

The last inequality follows
