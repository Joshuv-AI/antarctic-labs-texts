On the integral Hodge conjecture for real abelian threefolds
Olivier de Gaay Fortman

Abstract
We prove the real integral Hodge conjecture for several classes of real abelian threefolds. For instance, we prove the property for real abelian threefolds with connected real locus, and for real abelian threefolds A A which are the product A = B × E A=B\times E of an abelian surface B B and an elliptic curve E E with connected real locus E ⁡ ( ℝ ) E(\mathbb{R}) . Moreover, we show that every real abelian threefold satisfies the real integral Hodge conjecture modulo torsion, and reduce the principally polarized case to the Jacobian case.

Introduction

The Hodge structure on the singular cohomology ring of a smooth projective variety
X
over
\mathbb{C}
is a powerful tool to study the algebraic cycles on
X
. Where the Hodge conjecture predicts that the space of rational Hodge classes in any degree
2k
is spanned by algebraic cycles, the
integral Hodge conjecture
asks for something stronger, namely that this is already true with
\mathbb{Z}
-coefficients. As such, the integral Hodge conjecture is a property rather than a conjecture: it may hold or fail depending on
k
and the geometry of
X

[
AH62
,
BCC92
,
Tot97
,
Voi06
,
CTV12
,
Sch19
,
BO20
,
Tot21
,
BGF23
]
.

Recently, the analogue of the integral Hodge conjecture for real algebraic varieties has been formulated
[
BW20
,
BW20a
]
. Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over
\mathbb{R}
, and let
G=\textnormal{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
. Building on work of Krasnov
[
Kra91
,
Kra94
]
and Van Hamel
[
Ham97
]
, Benoist and Wittenberg define a subgroup
\textnormal{Hdg}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))_{0}
of the
G
-equivariant cohomology group
{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))
in the sense of Borel, and study the cycle class map

\displaystyle\textnormal{CH}_{i}(X)\to{\mathrm{\textnormal{Hdg}}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))_{0},\quad\quad i+k=\dim(X).

(1)

The
real integral Hodge conjecture for
i
-cycles
refers to the property that (
1
) is surjective. As in the complex situation, this property holds for every
X
if
i\in\{\dim(X),\dim(X)-1,0\}

[
Kra91
,
MH98
,
Ham97
,
BW20
]
, but may fail for other values of
i\in\left\{0,1,\dotsc,\dim(X)\right\}
.

Complex uniruled threefolds, as well as threefolds
X
over
\mathbb{C}
with
K_{X}=0
, satisfy the integral Hodge conjecture by work of Grabowski, Voisin and Totaro
[
Gra04
,
Voi06
,
Tot21
]
. In
[
BW20
, Question 2.16]
, Benoist and Wittenberg ask whether the same is true over
\mathbb{R}
. In fact, in
[
BW20a
]
they provide positive answers for various classes of uniruled threefolds. For real Calabi-Yau varieties, however, nothing seems to be known.

In particular, one may ask:

Question 1.1
.

Do real abelian threefolds satisfy the real integral Hodge conjecture?

The goal of this paper is to provide evidence towards a positive answer to Question
1.1
.

Let us explain our results. If
A
is a real abelian variety, then for each
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
the following sequence is exact (see Lemma
2.2
):

\displaystyle 0\to\textnormal{Hdg}^{2k}_{G}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))_{0}[2]\to\textnormal{Hdg}^{2k}_{G}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))_{0}\to\textnormal{Hdg}^{2k}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))^{G}\to 0.

Definition 1.2
.

A real abelian variety
A
satisfies the
real integral Hodge conjecture for
i
-cycles modulo torsion
if the following map is surjective:

\displaystyle\textnormal{CH}_{i}(A)\to\textnormal{Hdg}^{2k}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))^{G},\quad\quad i+k=\dim(A).

(2)

We say that
A
satisfies the
real integral Hodge conjecture modulo torsion
if
A
satisfies the real integral Hodge conjecture for
i
-cycles modulo torsion for
every
i\in\left\{0,1,\dotsc,\dim(A)\right\}
.

Our first main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

Every abelian threefold over
\mathbb{R}
satisfies the real integral Hodge conjecture modulo torsion.

By using the Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence (see Section
2.2
), one can calculate the torsion rank of the equivariant cohomology of a real abelian threefold. We obtain:

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
A
be an abelian threefold over
\mathbb{R}
such that
A(\mathbb{R})
is connected. Then
A
satisfies the real integral Hodge conjecture.

Our proof of Theorem
1.3
is inspired by Grabowski’s proof of the integral Hodge conjecture for complex abelian threefolds
[
Gra04
]
. It consists of two steps:

1.

Reduce the real integral Hodge conjecture for one-cycles modulo torsion for abelian varieties of dimension
g
to the algebraicity of the minimal class

\gamma_{\theta}=\theta^{g-1}/(g-1)!\in\textnormal{Hdg}^{2g-2}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(g-1))^{G}

for every principally polarized abelian variety
(A,\theta)
of dimension
g
over
\mathbb{R}
.

2.

Reduce the algebraicity of
\gamma_{\theta}
on a principally polarized real abelian threefold
A
to the case where
A=J(C)
is the Jacobian of a real algebraic curve
C
of genus three with non-empty real locus, where this is clear.

An essential ingredient for the first reduction step is the fact that any polarized abelian variety over
\mathbb{R}
is isogenous to a principally polarized one. Although the analogue of this statement over any algebraically closed field is classical
[
Mum08
]
, it fails over general fields
[
How01
]
. We will prove this fact in Theorem
4.1
.

As for step
2
, our strategy is to use Hecke orbits.
For
g\geq 1
, let
{\mathcal{A}}_{g}
be the moduli stack of principally polarized abelian varieties of dimension
g
, and consider the real-analytic topology on its real locus
{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
(c.f.
[
GF22a
, Definition 7.5 & Theorem 8.1]
or
[
GF22
, Definition 2.7 & Theorem 2.15]
). For integers
a
and
b
, define the
(a,b)
-Hecke orbit
of a moduli point
[(A,\lambda)]\in{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
as the set of
[(B,\mu)]\in{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
admitting an isogeny
A\to B
preserving the polarizations up to a product of powers of
a
and
b
(see Definition
3.6
). Hecke orbits are well-known to be dense in
{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{C})
(see
[
GF22
, Lemma 7.14]
); we obtain the following real analogue.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
p
and
q
be distinct odd prime numbers. The
(p,q)
-Hecke orbit of any point
x\in{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
is analytically dense in the connected component of
{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
containing
x
.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
p
and
q
be as above. Every principally polarized abelian threefold over
\mathbb{R}
is isogenous, via an isogeny that preserves the polarizations up to a product of powers of
p
and
q
, to the Jacobian of a non-hyperelliptic curve with non-empty real locus.

Reduction step
2
follows because if an odd multiple of
\theta^{2}/2
is algebraic for a principally polarized real abelian threefold
(A,\theta)
, then
\theta^{2}/2
is algebraic. In the course of proving Theorem
1.3
, we also show that the main theorem of
[
BGF23
]
has the following analogue over
\mathbb{R}
: a principally polarized real abelian variety
(A,\theta)
satisfies the real integral Hodge conjecture for one-cycles modulo torsion if and only if
\gamma_{\theta}
is algebraic (see Theorem
5.2
).

Corollary
1.6
turns out to be useful for the general principally polarized case as well:

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{3}(\mathbb{R})^{+}
be a connected component of the moduli space of principally polarized real abelian threefolds. Assume the real integral Hodge conjecture holds for every Jacobian
J(C)
such that
[J(C)]\in{\mathcal{A}}_{3}(\mathbb{R})^{+}
and the real locus
C(\mathbb{R})
of
C
is non-empty. Then the real integral Hodge conjecture holds for every real abelian variety in
{\mathcal{A}}_{3}(\mathbb{R})^{+}
.

In view of Theorems
1.3
and
1.7
, Question
1.1
restricted to the principally polarized case can be rephrased as follows. Let
C
be a real algebraic curve of genus three with non-empty real locus. Is the torsion subgroup of
\textnormal{Hdg}^{4}_{G}(J(C)(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(2))_{0}
algebraic?

Our next result reduces this question further. The theorem concerns torsion cohomology classes of degree four on real abelian varieties
A
of any dimension
g
. Let
F^{\bullet}
be the filtration on
\textnormal{Hdg}^{2k}_{G}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))_{0}\subset{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))
induced by the Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence (see Section
2.2
).

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
A
be an abelian variety over
\mathbb{R}
. The group
F^{3}\textnormal{Hdg}^{4}_{G}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(2))_{0}
is zero and the group
F^{2}\textnormal{Hdg}^{4}_{G}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(2))_{0}
is algebraic.

The proof of Theorem
1.8
relies on Proposition 3.11 in
[
BGF23
]
and an analysis of the Abel-Jacobi map for zero-cycles.

For an abelian variety
A
over
\mathbb{R}
, the Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence degenerates
[
Kra83
]
. Define
{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(G,{\mathrm{H}}^{3}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(2)))_{0}
as the image of the canonical homomorphism

\displaystyle F^{1}\textnormal{Hdg}^{4}_{G}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(2))_{0}\to{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(G,{\mathrm{H}}^{3}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(2))).

(3)

In fact, for abelian threefolds
A
over
\mathbb{R}
, the homomorphism (
3
) is surjective unless
\left|\pi_{0}(A(\mathbb{R}))\right|=8
(Corollary
9.2
). Combining Theorems
1.3
and
1.8
with the compatibility of the cycle class map (
1
) and the real Abel-Jacobi map (Theorem
7.2
), we obtain:

Corollary 1.9
.

Let
A
be an abelian threefold over
\mathbb{R}
. Then
A
satisfies the real integral Hodge conjecture
if and only if the Abel-Jacobi map

\textnormal{CH}_{1}(A)_{\textnormal{hom}}\to{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(G,{\mathrm{H}}^{3}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(2)))_{0}

is surjective, where
\textnormal{CH}_{1}(A)_{\textnormal{hom}}
denotes the kernel of
\textnormal{CH}_{1}(A)\to{\mathrm{H}}^{4}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(2))
.

See Section
7
for the definition of the real Abel-Jacobi map.
One can then use Corollary
1.9
and the Künneth formula to prove the following unconditional result.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
A=B\times E
be the product of a real abelian surface
B
and a real elliptic curve
E
with
E(\mathbb{R})
connected. Then
A
satisfies the real integral Hodge conjecture.

It follows that there are no topological obstructions to the real integral Hodge conjecture for those real abelian threefolds
A
with
\left|\pi_{0}(A(\mathbb{R}))\right|\neq 8
. In the case
A=E^{3}
for a real elliptic curve
E
with disconnected
E(\mathbb{R})
, we reduce the real integral Hodge conjecture to the algebraicity of one specific class
\omega\in{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(G,{\mathrm{H}}^{3}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(2)))_{0}
, see Proposition
9.4
.

Finally, we establish a connection between the real integral Hodge conjecture and the Griffiths group of an abelian threefold
A
over
\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathscr{C}^{2}(A)\subset{\mathrm{Griff}}^{2}(A_{\mathbb{C}})^{G}\otimes\mathbb{Z}/2
be the subgroup generated by pull-backs of Ceresa cycles
[\Xi]\in{\mathrm{Griff}}^{2}(J(C)_{\mathbb{C}})^{G}\otimes\mathbb{Z}/2
along isogenies
\phi\colon A\to J(C)
to Jacobians of curves of genus three over
\mathbb{R}
(c.f. Section
10
).

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
A
be a real abelian threefold such that
A(\mathbb{R})
is not connected. Suppose that the complex abelian threefold
A_{\mathbb{C}}
is very general. If
A
satisfies the real integral Hodge conjecture, then
{\mathscr{C}}^{2}(A)\subsetneq{\mathrm{Griff}}^{2}(A_{\mathbb{C}})^{G}\otimes\mathbb{Z}/2
.

The real integral Hodge conjecture

2.1

Generalities

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over
\mathbb{R}
. The group

G=\textnormal{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})=\{\textnormal{id},\sigma\}

acts on
X(\mathbb{C})
via the canonical anti-holomorphic involution
\sigma\colon X(\mathbb{C})\to X(\mathbb{C})
. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we denote by
\mathbb{Z}(k)
the
G
-module that has
\mathbb{Z}
as underlying
\mathbb{Z}
-module, on which
G
acts by
\sigma(1)=(-1)^{k}
. Thus,
\mathbb{Z}(k)=\mathbb{Z}(q)
for every
q\in\mathbb{Z}
with
k\equiv q\bmod 2
. We also denote by
\mathbb{Z}(k)
the constant
G
-sheaf on
X(\mathbb{C})
attached to the
G
-module
\mathbb{Z}(k)
. For
k,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, the
G
-action on the group
{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(q))
is understood to be the one induced by the involution
{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(\sigma)\circ F_{\infty}
, where
F_{\infty}=\sigma^{\ast}
is the pull-back of the anti-holomorphic involution
\sigma
on
X(\mathbb{C})
, and
{\mathrm{H}}^{k}(\sigma)
is the involution on cohomology induced by
\sigma\colon\mathbb{Z}(q)\to\mathbb{Z}(q)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Attached to
X
is also the so-called degree
2k

equivariant cohomology group
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}(k)
, see
[
Gro57
]
. It is denoted by
{\mathrm{H}}_{G}^{2k}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))
, and relates to singular cohomology via a canonical homomorphism

\displaystyle\varphi\colon{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))\to{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))^{G}.

(4)

A real subvariety
Z\subset X
of codimension
k
induces a class
[Z]\in{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))
whose image
\varphi([Z])
in
{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))^{G}
is the class
[Z_{\mathbb{C}}]
attached to the subvariety
Z_{\mathbb{C}}\subset X_{\mathbb{C}}
.

It turns out that such algebraic cycle classes satisfy an additional condition, discovered by Kahn and Krasnov
[
Kah87
,
Kra94
]
, and defined purely in terms of the structure of
X(\mathbb{C})
as topological
G
-space. For
i\in\{0,\dotsc,2k\}
, define

\displaystyle\phi_{i}\colon{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))\to{\mathrm{H}}^{i}(X(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{Z}/2)

as the composition

\displaystyle{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\bmod 2}{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}/2)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{restriction}}{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{Z}/2)={\mathrm{H}}^{2k}(X(\mathbb{R})\times BG,\mathbb{Z}/2)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\sim]{\text{K\"{u}nneth}}{\mathrm{H}}^{0}(X(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{Z}/2)\oplus\cdots\oplus{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}(X(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{Z}/2)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{projection}}{\mathrm{H}}^{i}(X(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{Z}/2).

For
\alpha\in{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))
, define
\alpha_{i}=\phi_{i}(\alpha)\in{\mathrm{H}}^{i}(X(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{Z}/2)
, and let
{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))_{0}
be the group of classes
\alpha\in{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))
such that

\displaystyle(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{k},\dotsc,\alpha_{2k})=\left(0,\dotsc,0,\alpha_{k},Sq^{1}(\alpha_{k}),Sq^{2}(\alpha_{k}),\dotsc,Sq^{k}(\alpha_{k})\right).

(5)

Here, the
Sq^{i}
are the Steenrod operations

Sq^{i}:{\mathrm{H}}^{p}(X(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{Z}/2)\to{\mathrm{H}}^{p+i}(X(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{Z}/2).

Definition 2.1

(Benoist–Wittenberg)
.

The subgroup

\displaystyle\textnormal{Hdg}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))_{0}\subset{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))

is the group of classes
\alpha\in{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))
that satisfy the following conditions:

1.

The class
\alpha
lies in the subgroup
{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))_{0}
of classes satisfying (
5
).

2.

The image of
\alpha
in
{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))
under (
4
) is a Hodge class.

By
[
BW20
, §1.6.4]
, Definition
2.1
is compatible with cup products, pull-backs and proper push-forwards. Moreover, by
[
Kra94
]
(c.f.
[
BW20
, Theorem 1.18]
), we have that
[Z]\in\textnormal{Hdg}^{2k}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))_{0}
for every algebraic subvariety
Z\subset X
. This defines the map (
1
).

2.2

Hochschild-Serre

For a smooth projective variety
X
over
\mathbb{R}
, the
Hochschild-Serre
spectral sequence

\displaystyle E^{p,q}_{2}={\mathrm{H}}^{p}(G,{\mathrm{H}}^{q}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k)))\implies{\mathrm{H}}^{p+q}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))

(6)

is obtained by viewing
{\mathrm{H}}^{i}_{G}(X(\mathbb{C}),-)
as the right-derived functor of the composition of taking global sections and
G
-invariants on the category of
G
-sheaves on
X(\mathbb{C})
.

Let
A
be an abelian variety over
\mathbb{R}
. Then (
6
) degenerates for
k=0
by
[
Kra83
, §5.7]
. It follows that (
6
) degenerates for every

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))_{\textnormal{tors}}&={\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))[2]\\
&=\textnormal{Ker}\left({\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))\to{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))\right)\\
&=F^{1}{\mathrm{H}}^{2k}_{G}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k)).\end{split}

(7)

Moreover, these
\mathbb{Z}/2
-modules are (non-canonically) isomorphic to the
\mathbb{Z}/2
-module

\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}p+q=k\\
p>0\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{H}}^{p}(G,{\mathrm{H}}^{q}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))).

Let

3.3

Density of Hecke orbits over the real numbers

Before we prove Theorem
1.5
, let us properly introduce the notion of Hecke orbits over the real numbers.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
(A,\lambda)
be a principally polarized abelian variety of dimension
g
over
\mathbb{R}
, and let
x=[(A,\lambda)]\in\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|
the corresponding moduli point. For a tuple of integers
(a,b)
, the
(a,b)
-Hecke orbit of
x
is the subset
{\mathcal{G}}_{a,b}(x)\subset\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|
of isomorphism classes
[(B,\nu)]\in\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|
of principally polarized abelian varieties
(B,\nu)
of dimension
g
over
\mathbb{R}
, for which there exist
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and an isogeny

\phi\colon A\to B\quad\textnormal{ such that } \quad\phi^{\ast}(\nu)=a^{n}b^{m}\cdot\lambda.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
p
and
q
be distinct odd prime numbers.

Step 1:

If
x=[(A,\lambda)]\in\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|
and
\tau\in{\mathscr{T}}(g)
is the type of
(A,\lambda)
, then
{\mathcal{G}}_{p,q}(x)\subset\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|^{\tau}
. Indeed, for any
y=[(B,\mu)]\in{\mathcal{G}}_{p,q}(x)
, there exists an isogeny
\phi\colon A\to B
such that
\phi^{\ast}(\mu)=n\cdot\lambda
for some odd positive integer
n
. Such a map
\phi
induces an isomorphism

{\mathrm{H}}_{1}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}/2)\cong{\mathrm{H}}_{1}(B(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}/2)

of symplectic spaces with involution. Since
x\in\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|^{\tau}
, this implies that
y\in\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|^{\tau}
as well, see
[
GH81
, Section 9]
.

Step 2:

Define
S=\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{q}\right]
. The ring homomorphism
S\to S/2S
induces a group homomorphism

\textnormal{GL}_{g}(S)\to\textnormal{GL}_{g}(S/2S).
For
\tau\in{\mathscr{T}}(g)
, we define

\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)=\{T\in\textnormal{GL}_{g}(S)\colon T^{t}\cdot M(\tau)\cdot T\equiv M(\tau)\bmod 2\}.

Fix one such
\tau\in{\mathscr{T}}(g)
. Observe that the action of
\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(\mathbb{Z})
on
{\mathrm{H}}_{g}
extends to a transitive action of
\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
on
{\mathrm{H}}_{g}
. We claim:

Let
x=[(A_{x},\lambda_{x})]\in\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|^{\tau}
, and lift
x
to a point
y\in{\mathrm{H}}_{g}
. Consider the orbit
\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)\cdot y\subset{\mathrm{H}}_{g}
as well as its image
\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(\mathbb{Z})\setminus\left(\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)\cdot y\right)
in
\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|^{\tau}=\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(\mathbb{Z})\setminus{\mathrm{H}}_{g}
. Then

\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(\mathbb{Z})\setminus\left(\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)\cdot y\right)={\mathcal{G}}_{p,q}(x).

Indeed, if
{\mathbb{H}}_{g}
is the genus
g
Siegel space of symmetric, complex
g\times g
matrices
Z=X+{\mathrm{i}}Y
whose imaginary part
Y
is positive definite, then the inclusion

\rho_{\tau}\colon{\mathrm{H}}_{g}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{H}}_{g},\quad N\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\cdot M(\tau)+{\mathrm{i}}N

is equivariant for the embedding

f_{\tau}\colon\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow\textnormal{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{R}),\quad T\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}T^{t}&\frac{1}{2}\left(M(\tau)\cdot T^{-1}-T^{t}\cdot M(\tau)\right)\\
0&T^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover, the action of the group
\textnormal{Sp}_{2g}(S)
on
{\mathbb{H}}_{g}
has the following geometric meaning: if we consider
{\mathbb{H}}_{g}
as a moduli space of
g
-dimensional, principally polarized complex abelian varieties with symplectic basis, then two points
y=[A_{y}]
and
z=[A_{z}]\in{\mathbb{H}}_{g}
are in the same
\textnormal{Sp}_{2g}(S)
-orbit if and only if there exists an isogeny
\phi\colon A_{y}\to A_{z}
that preserves the polarizations up to a product of powers of
p
and
q
. Since the intersection

f_{\tau}\left(\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right)\cap\textnormal{Sp}_{2g}(S)=f_{\tau}\left(\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)\right)

equals the subgroup of
\textnormal{Sp}_{2g}(S)
that preserves the locus

\rho_{\tau}({\mathrm{H}}_{g})=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\cdot M(\tau)+{\mathrm{i}}N\right\}\subset{\mathbb{H}}_{g}

of real abelian varieties of type
\tau
, this concludes Step 2.

Step 3:

For any
\tau\in{\mathscr{T}}(g)
, the subgroup
\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)\subset\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
is dense in the analytic topology.

Define

\textnormal{SL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)=\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)\cap\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)=\{T\in\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)\colon T^{t}\cdot M(\tau)\cdot T\equiv M(\tau)\bmod 2\}.

We claim that

\displaystyle\textnormal{SL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})=\overline{\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)}=\overline{\textnormal{SL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)}\subset\overline{\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)}\subset\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R}).

(11)

Indeed, this follows from the following two statements:

(a)

The closure of
\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)
in
\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
is
\textnormal{SL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
.

(b)

The subgroup
\textnormal{SL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)\subset\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)
has finite index.

To prove (
a
), observe that the subgroup
\textnormal{SL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\subset\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
is closed, which implies that the closure of
\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)
in
\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
equals the closure of
\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)
in
\textnormal{SL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
. Thus, (
a
) follows from the density of
\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)
in
\textnormal{SL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
, which is true by strong approximation; see
[
GF22

for the precise argument. As for (
b
), the group
\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)
acts on
{\mathrm{M}}_{g}(S/2S)
via
N\mapsto T^{t}\cdot N\cdot T\bmod 2
for
T\in\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)
, so there is an injection

\textnormal{SL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)\setminus\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)\hookrightarrow{\mathrm{M}}_{g}(S/2S)={\mathrm{M}}_{g}(\mathbb{Z}/2),\quad T\mapsto T^{t}\cdot M(\tau)\cdot T\mod 2.

Statement (
b
) implies that the index of
\overline{\textnormal{SL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)}\subset\overline{\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)}
is finite. e have
\overline{\textnormal{SL}_{g}(S)}=\textnormal{SL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
by (

), thus
\overline{\textnormal{SL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)}\subset\textnormal{SL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
is a closed subgroup of finite index, hence open. Therefore
\overline{\textnormal{SL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)}=\textnormal{SL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
by connectivity of
\textnormal{SL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
, proving Claim (
11

Write
G=\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)
. If
H\subset\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
is any Lie subgroup such that
\textnormal{SL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\subset H
, then
H=\det^{-1}(\det(H))
.
Consequently, using (

\displaystyle\overline{G}={\det}^{-1}(\det(\overline{G}))\subset\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R}).

(12)

The equality (

{\det}^{-1}(\det(\overline{G}))=\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})
. This follows from the equality
\det(\overline{G})=\mathbb{R}^{\ast}
, which we now prove. The morphism
\det\colon\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{\ast}
is open because its differential at the identity matrix

is the trace homomorphism
{\mathrm{M}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
. Writing

\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})={\det}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{\ast})={\det}^{-1}(\det(\overline{G})\sqcup\det(\overline{G})^{c})=\overline{G}\sqcup{\det}^{-1}\left(\det(\overline{G})^{c}\right),

it follows that
\det(\overline{G})
is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{\ast}

\overline{\det(G)}\subset\overline{\det(\overline{G})}=\det(\overline{G})
. Thus, to show that
\det(\overline{G})=\mathbb{R}^{\ast}

\det(G)
is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{\ast}
. The homomorphism
\det\colon\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)\to S^{\ast}
is surjective because it admits the section

S^{\ast}\to\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S),\quad x\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&I_{g-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,

\det(G)=\det(\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S))=S^{\ast}=\left\{\pm p^{n}q^{m}\colon n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},

and it remains to prove that the latter is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{\ast}
. This holds, since
S^{\ast}_{>0}=\left\{p^{n}q^{m}\colon n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
because
\log(S^{\ast}_{>0})=\mathbb{Z}\log(p)+\mathbb{Z}\log(q)
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
; the latter follows from the fact that
\log(S^{\ast}_{>0})
is not a cyclic subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
.

Step 4:

Finish the proof.
Let
x=[(A,\lambda)]\in\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|
, and let
\tau\in{\mathscr{T}}(g)
be the type of the principally polarized real abelian variety
(A,\lambda)
. Lift
x
to a point
y\in{\mathrm{H}}_{g}
. By Step 3, we know that the orbit
\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)\cdot y
is dense in
\textnormal{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\cdot y={\mathrm{H}}_{g}
. Consequently, the image of
\textnormal{GL}_{g}^{\tau}(S)\cdot y
under the projection
{\mathrm{H}}_{g}\to\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|^{\tau}
is dense in
\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|^{\tau}
. By Step 2, this image is precisely
{\mathcal{G}}_{p,q}(x)
. Thus
{\mathcal{G}}_{p,q}(x)
is dense in
\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{g}(\mathbb{R})\right|^{\tau}
as desired.

∎

4

Principal polarizations in real isogeny classes

The goal of this section is to prove the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(A,\lambda_{A})
be a polarized abelian variety over
\mathbb{R}
. Then there exists a principally polarized abelian variety
(B,\lambda_{B})
over
\mathbb{R}
and an isogeny

\phi\colon A\to B\quad\textnormal{ \emph{such that} }\quad\phi^{\ast}(\lambda_{B})=\lambda_{A}.

Proof.

Let
K\subset A(\mathbb{C})
be the kernel of the analytified polarization
\lambda_{A}\colon A(\mathbb{C})\to{\widehat{A}}(\mathbb{C})
. Then
K
is a finite group of order
d^{2}
, where
d^{2}
is the degree of
\lambda_{A}
, such that the real structure
\sigma\colon A(\mathbb{C})\to A(\mathbb{C})
restricts to an involution

\sigma\colon K\to K.

We may assume that
K\neq(0)
. Let
p
be any prime number that divides the order of
K
. We claim that there exists a subgroup
K_{1}\subset K
of order
p
such that
\sigma(K_{1})=K_{1}
. To see this, let
H[p]\subset K
be the
p
-torsion subgroup of
K
. Then
H[p]
is preserved by
\sigma
, so that
H[p]
is an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-vector space of finite rank equipped with a linear involution
\sigma
. Therefore,
H[p]
contains a one-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-subspace
K_{1}
preserved by
\sigma
, which proves our claim.

The group
K_{1}\subset A(\mathbb{C})
descends to a finite subgroup scheme
K_{1}\subset A
over
\mathbb{R}
; define
A_{1}
to be the abelian variety
A/K_{1}
over
\mathbb{R}
. Let
\Lambda={\mathrm{H}}_{1}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z})
and
M={\mathrm{H}}_{1}(A_{1}(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z})
; the projection
A\to A_{1}
induces an exact sequence

0\to\Lambda\to M\to K_{1}\to 0.

Let
E\colon\Lambda\times\Lambda\to\mathbb{Z}
be the alternating form attached to the polarization
\lambda_{A}
of
A
. Since
M/\Lambda=K_{1}\subset K=\Lambda^{\vee}/\Lambda
, we have inclusions

\Lambda\subset M\subset\Lambda^{\vee},\quad\textnormal{ where }\quad\Lambda^{\vee}=\left\{x\in\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{Q}\mid E(x,\Lambda)\subset\Lambda\right\}.

The
\mathbb{Z}
-valued alternating form
E
on the lattice
\Lambda
gives rise to a bilinear form

\overline{E}\colon\Lambda^{\vee}/\Lambda\times\Lambda^{\vee}/\Lambda\to\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}

which vanishes on
M/\Lambda
because
M/\Lambda\cong\mathbb{Z}/p
and
\overline{E}
is alternating. Thus
E\colon\Lambda^{\vee}\times\Lambda^{\vee}\to\mathbb{Q}
restricts to an integer-valued form
E_{1}
on
M
. The latter induces a polarization
\lambda_{A_{1}} \colon A_{1}\to{\widehat{A}}_{1}
that makes the following diagram commute:

\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\lambda_{A}}
\scriptstyle{\pi}
\textstyle{A_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\lambda_{A_{1}}}
\textstyle{{\widehat{A}}}
\textstyle{{\widehat{A}}_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces.}
\scriptstyle{{\widehat{\pi}}}

Here
\pi
is the quotient map
A\to A_{1}
and
{\widehat{\pi}}
its dual. Since the degree of an isogeny is multiplicative in compositions, and
\deg(\pi)=p
, we have

p^{2}\cdot\deg(\lambda_{A_{1}})=\deg(\pi)^{2}\cdot\deg(\lambda_{A_{1}})=\deg\left(\lambda_{A}\right)=d^{2}.

If
d=p
, we are finished – otherwise, we repeat the above procedure until the real abelian variety
A_{n}=A_{n-1}/K_{n-1}
becomes principally polarized. ∎

Integral Hodge classes modulo torsion

5.1

The Fourier transform

Let
A
be a real abelian variety of dimension

, and consider the Poincaré bundle
{\mathcal{P}}_{A}
on
A\times{\widehat{A}}
. Let

be integers. The Chern character

\textnormal{ch}({\mathcal{P}}_{A_{\mathbb{C}}})=\exp(c_{1}({\mathcal{P}}_{A_{\mathbb{C}}}))\in{\mathrm{H}}^{2\bullet}(A(\mathbb{C})\times{\widehat{A}}(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(\bullet))

defines the
Fourier transform

\displaystyle{\mathscr{F}}_{A}\colon\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}{\mathrm{H}}^{i}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(a_{i}))\to\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}{\mathrm{H}}^{i}({\widehat{A}}(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(a_{2g-i}-g+i)).

(13)

It is defined as
{\mathscr{F}}_{A}(x)=\pi_{2,\ast}(\textnormal{ch}({\mathcal{P}}_{A})\cdot\pi_{1}^{\ast}(x))
, where
\pi_{i}
is the projection of
A\times{\widehat{A}}
onto the

[
Bea83
]
, the map (

) is an isomorphism, inducing isomorphisms

\displaystyle{\mathscr{F}}_{A}\colon{\mathrm{H}}^{i}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(a_{i}))\xrightarrow{\sim}{\mathrm{H}}^{2g-i}({\widehat{A}}(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(a_{i}+g-i)).

(14)

Since
\textnormal{ch}({\mathcal{P}}_{A_{\mathbb{C}}})
is fixed by

, these maps are isomorphisms of
G
-modules.

5.2

Divisors

Let
A
be an abelian variety over
\mathbb{R}
. Both homomorphisms in the following composition are surjective:

\displaystyle\textnormal{CH}^{1}(A)\to\textnormal{Hdg}_{G}^{2}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(1))\to\textnormal{Hdg}^{2}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(1))^{G}.

(15)

Indeed, the first map is surjective by the real integral Hodge conjecture for divisors (see

), and the second by the degeneration of the Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence (see Section

).

5.3

The real integral Hodge conjecture for one-cycles modulo torsion

The goal of this section is to provide an application of Theorems
1.5
and
4.1
combined: the real integral Hodge conjecture for one-cycles modulo torsion follows, in some cases, from the real integral Hodge conjecture for divisors modulo torsion (c.f. Section
5.2
). For this, we use Fourier transforms on Betti cohomology of real abelian varieties (c.f. Section
5.1
) and their lifts to integral Chow groups, see
[
BGF23
,
MP10
]
. The theory in
[
BGF23
, Section 3]
was developed for abelian varieties over a general field

– to apply it, we take

.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
A
be a real abelian variety, and
k
a non-negative integer. An element
\alpha\in\textnormal{Hdg}^{2k}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))^{G}
is called

if it is in the image of

\textnormal{CH}^{k}(A)\to\textnormal{Hdg}^{2k}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(k))^{G}.

By the main theorem of
[
BGF23
]
, the integral Hodge conjecture for one-cycles on a fixed principally polarized abelian variety
(A,\theta)
over
\mathbb{C}
is equivalent to the algebraicity of the minimal class
\gamma_{\theta}=\theta^{g-1}/(g-1)!
on

(see
loc.cit.
, Theorem 1.1). Moreover, Grabowski reduced the integral Hodge conjecture for one-cycles for every complex abelian variety of dimension

to the algebraicity of
\gamma_{\theta}
for every principally polarized abelian variety of dimension
g
, see
[
Gra04
]
. We have the following real analogue of these results:

Theorem 5.2
.

Fix a positive integer
g
. Let
(A,\theta)
be a principally polarized abelian variety of dimension

over
\mathbb{R}
. The following are equivalent:

1.

The real abelian variety
A
satisfies the real integral Hodge conjecture for one-cycles modulo torsion.

2.

The minimal class

3.

The Chern character

\textnormal{ch}({\mathcal{P}}_{A_{\mathbb{C}}})=\exp(c_{1}({\mathcal{P}}_{A_{\mathbb{C}}}))\in\textnormal{Hdg}^{2\bullet}(A(\mathbb{C})\times{\widehat{A}}(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(\bullet))^{G}\quad\text{ is algebraic.}

Moreover, if the real integral Hodge conjecture for one-cycles modulo torsion holds for every principally polarized abelian variety of dimension
g
over
\mathbb{R}
, then it holds for every abelian variety of dimension
g
over
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The direction

(A,\theta)
be a principally polarized abelian variety of dimension

\mathbb{R}
, and suppose that

holds, and let

\textstyle{\textnormal{CH}^{1}(A)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\textnormal{CH}^{\bullet}(A)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\textnormal{Hdg}^{2\bullet}(A(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z}(\bullet))^{G}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

.

Next, suppose that

; we may thus assume that

Corollary 5.3
.

Let

be smooth projective geometrically integral curves over
\mathbb{R}
such that
C_{i}(\mathbb{R})\neq\emptyset
for each

. The real abelian variety

satisfies the real integral Hodge conjecture for one-cycles modulo torsion.

Proof.

The minimal class on a product of principally polarized abelian varieties over
\mathbb{R}
is the sum of the pull-backs of the minimal classes on the factors, so by Theorem
5.2
, it suffices to treat the case

. For a real algebraic curve

where the class on the right hand side of the equality is the minimal cohomology class
\gamma_{\theta}
of

. Thus
\gamma_{\theta}
is algebraic, so we are done by Theorem
5.2
.
∎
