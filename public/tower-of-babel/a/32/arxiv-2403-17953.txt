Solutions to a Pillai–type equation involving Tribonacci numbers and S − S- units
Herbert Batte, Florian Luca

Abstract
Let { T n } n ≥ 0 \{T_{n}\}_{n\geq 0} be the sequence of Tribonacci numbers. In this paper, we study the exponential Diophantine equation T n − 2 x ​ 3 y = c T_{n}-2^{x}3^{y}=c , for n , x , y ∈ ℤ ≥ 0 n,x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} . In particular, we show that there is no integer c c with at least six representations of the form T n − 2 x ​ 3 y T_{n}-2^{x}3^{y} .

1.1
Background

We consider the sequence of Tribonacci numbers
\{T_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
defined by
T_{0}=0

T_{1}=T_{2}=1

T_{n+3}=T_{n+2}+T_{n+1}+T_{n},

The first few terms of this sequence are given by

The Diophantine equation

(1.1)

[

(x,y)

Further explorations of the Pillai problem have focused on the cases where

is fixed as
2
,
3

p

. These studies, see for example
[
2
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
13
]
, and
[
14
]
, have largely confirmed the conclusion of the original Pillai problem: all integers are uniquely represented in the above way, except for finitely many outliers.

In a number field
{\mathbb{K}}

Preliminaries

The Tribonacci sequence

\displaystyle\left|T_{n}-C_{\alpha}\alpha^{n-1}\right|<\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{for all }n\geq 0.

2.2
Linear forms in logarithms

We use several times Baker–type lower bounds for nonzero linear forms in four or more logarithms of algebraic numbers. There are many such bounds mentioned in the literature like that of Baker and Wüstholz from
[
1
]
or Matveev from
[
19
]
. Before we can formulate such inequalities we need the notion of height of an algebraic number recalled below.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\lambda
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal primitive polynomial over the integers

a_{0}x^{d}+a_{1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}=a_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\lambda^{(i)}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive. Then, the logarithmic height of
\lambda
is given by

h(\lambda):=\dfrac{1}{d}\Big(\log a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\max\{|\lambda^{(i)}|,1\}\Big).

In particular, if
\lambda
is a rational number represented as
\lambda:=p/q
with coprime integers
p
and
q\geq 1
, then
h(\lambda)=\log\max\{|p|,q\}
.
The following properties of the logarithmic height function
h(\cdot)
will be used in the rest of the paper without further reference:

\displaystyle h(\lambda_{1}\pm\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle\leq h(\lambda_{1})+h(\lambda_{2})+\log 2;

\displaystyle h(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq h(\lambda_{1})+h(\lambda_{2});

\displaystyle h(\lambda^{s})

\displaystyle=|s|h(\lambda)\quad{\text{\rm valid for}}\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}.

A linear form in logarithms is an expression

\Lambda:=b_{1}\log\lambda_{1}+\cdots+b_{t}\log\lambda_{t},

(2.5)

where for us
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{t}
are positive real algebraic numbers and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
are nonzero integers. We assume,
\Lambda\neq 0
. We need lower bounds
for
|\Lambda|
. We write
{\mathbb{L}}:={\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{t})
and
D
for the degree of
{\mathbb{L}}
.
We start with the general form due to Matveev
[
19
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Matveev,
[
19
]
)
.

Put
\Gamma:=\lambda_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{t}^{b_{t}}-1=e^{\Lambda}-1
. Assume
\Gamma\neq 0
. Then

\log|\Gamma|>-1.4\cdot 30^{t+3}\cdot t^{4.5}\cdot D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\cdots A_{t},

where
B\geq\max\{|b_{1}|,\ldots,|b_{t}|\}
and
A_{i}\geq\max\{Dh(\lambda_{i}),|\log\lambda_{i}|,0.16\}
for
i=1,\ldots,t
.

We also employ a
p
–adic variation of Laurent’s result as established by Bugeaud and Laurent in
[
9
]
, Corollary 1. Prior to outlining their result, we require a few additional notations.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
p
be a prime number. The

\nu_{p}(x)
, is defined by

\nu_{p}(x):=\begin{cases}\max\{k\in\mathbb{N}:p^{k}\mid x\},&\text{if }x\neq 0;\\
\infty,&\text{if }x=0.\end{cases}

Additionally, if
x=a/b
is a rational number and
a,~b
are integers, then we put

\nu_{p}(x):=\nu_{p}(a)-\nu_{p}(b).

The formula for
\nu_{p}(x)
when
x
is rational given by Definition
2.2
does not depend on the representation of
x
as a ratio of integers
a/b
. It follows easily from Definition
2.2
that if
x
is rational, then

\nu_{p}(x)=\text{ord}_{p}(x),

where
\text{ord}_{p}(x)
is the exponent of
p
in the factorization of
x
. For example,
\nu_{2}(9/8)=-3
. Now for the algebraic number
\lambda
in Definition
2.1
, we define

\nu_{p}(\lambda):=\frac{\nu_{p}(a_{d}/a_{0})}{d},

that is, it is the
p
–adic valuation of the rational number
a_{d}/a_{0}
divided by the degree

of
\lambda
. For example, when
x
is a rational number, we write it as
x=a_{d}/a_{0}
with coprime integers
a_{d}
and
a_{0}\geq 1
, then
its minimal polynomial is
f(X)=a_{0}X-a_{d}
has degree

\nu_{p}(x)=\nu_{p}(a_{d}/a_{0}),

consistent with Definition
2.2
.
The
p
–adic valuation gives rise to the conventional absolute value. When the rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}
are completed with the standard absolute value, the outcome is the set of real numbers,
\mathbb{R}
. Conversely, employing the
p
-adic absolute value for the completion of

yields the

-adic numbers, represented as

.

Similarly to the preceding context, let
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
be algebraic numbers over
\mathbb{Q}

h^{\prime}(\lambda_{i})\geq\max\left\{h(\lambda_{i}),\dfrac{\log p}{D}\right\},~~\text{for }~i=1,2.

Theorem 2.2

(Bugeaud and Laurent,
[

]
)

Let

2.3
Reduced Bases for Lattices and LLL–reduction methods

Let
k
be a positive integer. A subset
\mathcal{L}
of the
k
–dimensional real vector space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is called a lattice if there exists a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}b_{i}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}b_{i}\mid r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

In this situation we say that
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
form a basis for
\mathcal{L}
, or that they span
\mathcal{L}
. We
call
k
the rank of
\mathcal{L}
. The determinant
\text{det}(\mathcal{L})
, of
\mathcal{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathcal{L})=|\det(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})|,

with the
b_{i}
being written as column vectors. This is a positive real number that does not depend on the choice of the basis (see
[
10
]
Sect 1.2).

Given linearly independent vectors
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we refer back to the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization technique. This method allows us to inductively define vectors
b^{*}_{i}
(with
1\leq i\leq k
) and real coefficients
\mu_{i,j}
(for
1\leq j\leq i\leq k
). Specifically,

\displaystyle b^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mu_{i,j}b^{*}_{j},~~~\mu_{i,j}=\dfrac{\langle b_{i},b^{*}_{j}\rangle}{\langle b^{*}_{j},b^{*}_{j}\rangle},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the ordinary inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Notice that
b^{*}_{i}
is the orthogonal projection of
b_{i}
on the orthogonal complement of the span of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{i-1}
, and that
\mathbb{R}b_{i}
is orthogonal to the span of
b^{*}_{1},\ldots,b^{*}_{i-1}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. It follows that
b^{*}_{1},b^{*}_{2},\ldots,b^{*}_{k}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Definition 2.3
.

The basis

for the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called reduced if

\displaystyle\|\mu_{i,j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq j<i\leq n,~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\|b^{*}_{i}+\mu_{i,i-1}b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\|b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1<i\leq n,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the ordinary Euclidean length. The constant
\frac{3}{4}
above is arbitrarily chosen, and may be replaced by any fixed real number
\mathcal{P}
with
\frac{1}{4}<\mathcal{P}<1
,
(see
[
17
]
Sect 1).

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a

and denote by

.
We define

where
||\cdot||
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}

Lemma 2.6
.

The equation

\displaystyle\dfrac{1-\gamma^{v}}{1-\gamma^{u}}=\dfrac{1-\alpha^{v}}{1-\alpha^{u}}.

(2.7)

has no integer solutions
u,v
, with
u>v\geq 1
.

Proof.

Let
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}
, with
u>v\geq 1
. Then we can write (
2.7
) as

\displaystyle\alpha^{u}-\alpha^{v}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{u}-\gamma^{v}+\gamma^{v}\alpha^{u}-\gamma^{u}\alpha^{v};

\displaystyle\alpha^{u}-\alpha^{v}=\left|\alpha^{u}-\alpha^{v}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\gamma^{u}-\gamma^{v}+\gamma^{v}\alpha^{u}-\gamma^{u}\alpha^{v}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\gamma|^{u}+|\gamma|^{v}+\alpha^{u-0.5v}+\alpha^{v-0.5u},

where we have used the fact that
|\gamma|=\alpha^{-1/2}
. Assume for a moment that
v\geq 5
which implies that
u\geq 6
since
u>v
. Then the above inequality implies

\displaystyle\alpha^{u-2}+\alpha^{u-3}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{u}-\alpha^{u-1}\leq\alpha^{u}-\alpha^{v}\leq 1+\alpha^{u-0.5v}+\alpha^{v-0.5u}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\alpha^{u-2.5}+\alpha^{u-1-0.5\cdot 6}=1+\alpha^{u-2.5}+\alpha^{u-4}

\displaystyle<1+\alpha^{u-2.5}+\alpha^{u-3}.

Therefore,
\alpha^{u-2}<1+\alpha^{u-2.5}
. From this, we see that
\alpha^{u-2}\left(1-\alpha^{-0.5}\right)<1
. This implies that

\displaystyle\alpha^{u-2}

\displaystyle<\dfrac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}-1}<\dfrac{\sqrt{1.84}}{\sqrt{1.83}-1}<3.85,

so that
u<4.24
. This contradicts the assumption that
u\geq 6
. Therefore (
2.7
) has no solutions with
v\geq 5
. This means that (
2.7
) may have solutions only for
1\leq v\leq 4
. We go back and rewrite (
2.7
) as

\displaystyle\alpha^{u}-\alpha^{v}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{u}-\gamma^{v}+\gamma^{v}\alpha^{u}-\gamma^{u}\alpha^{v};

\displaystyle\alpha^{u}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{u}-\gamma^{v}+\gamma^{v}\alpha^{u}-\gamma^{u}\alpha^{v}+\alpha^{v}

\displaystyle\leq|\gamma|^{u}+|\gamma|^{v}+\alpha^{u-0.5v}+\alpha^{v-0.5u}+\alpha^{v}

\displaystyle<2+\alpha^{u-0.5}+\alpha^{3.5}+\alpha^{4},

so that
\alpha^{u}\left(1-\alpha^{-0.5}\right)<22
, which gives
u\leq 7
. With help of SageMath, we check for
v\in\{1,2,3,4\}
and
u\in\{2,3,4,5,6,7\}
that satisfy (
2.7
) and find no solutions.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Assume that the algebraic numbers

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}:=\dfrac{\alpha}{\beta}\qquad{\text{\rm and}}\qquad\lambda_{2}:=\dfrac{C_{\alpha}}{C_{\beta}}\left(\dfrac{\left(1-\alpha^{n_{1}-n}\right)\left(1-\gamma^{n_{2}-n}\right)-\left(1-\alpha^{n_{2}-n}\right)\left(1-\gamma^{n_{1}-n}\right)}{\left(1-\beta^{n_{1}-n}\right)\left(1-\gamma^{n_{2}-n}\right)-\left(1-\beta^{n_{2}-n}\right)\left(1-\gamma^{n_{1}-n}\right)}\right),

are multiplicatively dependent for
n>n_{1}>n_{2}
. Then
\lambda_{2}=\pm z^{t}
for some integer
t
with
|t|<80n+244
and
z
is defined in Proposition
2.1
.

Proof.

Suppose
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are multiplicatively dependent. This means that there exists integers
A
and
B
, not both zero, such that
\lambda_{1}^{A}=\lambda_{2}^{B}
. Since
\lambda_{1}=z^{3}
, we have

\lambda_{2}^{B}=z^{3A}.

Let
d:=\gcd(3A,B)
and write
3A=A_{1}d,~B=B_{1}d
. Then
\lambda_{2}^{B_{1}}=\zeta_{d}z^{A_{1}}
where
\zeta_{d}
is a root of unity of order dividing
d
. Since
{\mathbb{K}}
does not contain roots of unity other than
\pm 1
,
it follows that
\lambda_{2}^{B_{1}}=\pm z^{A_{1}}
. We show that
B_{1}=1
. Indeed, since
\gcd(A_{1},B_{1})=1
, we get that
A_{1}p+B_{1}q=1
for some integers
p,~q
. Thus,

z=z^{A_{1}p+B_{1}q}=(\pm 1)^{p}(\lambda_{2}^{p}z^{q})^{B_{1}}.

The above calculation shows that
\pm z
is a power of exponent
B_{1}
of some other number in
{\mathbb{K}}
, so by (ii) of Proposition
2.1
, we get
B_{1}=1
. Thus,
\lambda_{2}=\pm z^{t}
with

t=A_{1}
, and we need to bound
t
. Note that
|t|=|\log|\lambda_{2}||/|\log z|
. We estimate

\displaystyle|\log|\lambda_{2}||

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\log\left|\dfrac{C_{\alpha}}{C_{\beta}}\left(\dfrac{\left(1-\alpha^{n_{1}-n}\right)\left(1-\gamma^{n_{2}-n}\right)-\left(1-\alpha^{n_{2}-n}\right)\left(1-\gamma^{n_{1}-n}\right)}{\left(1-\beta^{n_{1}-n}\right)\left(1-\gamma^{n_{2}-n}\right)-\left(1-\beta^{n_{2}-n}\right)\left(1-\gamma^{n_{1}-n}\right)}\right)\right|\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\log|C_{\alpha}||+|\log|C_{\beta}||+\left|\log\left|\left(1-\alpha^{n_{1}-n}\right)\left(1-\gamma^{n_{2}-n}\right)-\left(1-\alpha^{n_{2}-n}\right)\left(1-\gamma^{n_{1}-n}\right)\right|\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\log\left|\left(1-\beta^{n_{1}-n}\right)\left(1-\gamma^{n_{2}-n}\right)-\left(1-\beta^{n_{2}-n}\right)\left(1-\gamma^{n_{1}-n}\right)\right|\right|.

The first two terms above are bounded as
|\log|C_{\alpha}||<0.5
and
|\log|C_{\beta}||<1.67
. The next two terms are absolute values of logarithms of absolute values of differences of complex numbers.
These can be very small or can be very large. In the “large” part it is easy. Since
\alpha>1
and
n>n_{1}>n_{2}
,
|\beta|=|\gamma|=\alpha^{-1/2}
, we have by the absolute value inequality and the fact that
|1-\alpha^{n_{i}-n}|=1-\alpha^{n_{i}-n}<1
for
i=1,2
, that

\displaystyle|(1-\alpha^{n_{1}-n})(1-\gamma^{n_{2}-n})-(1-\alpha^{n_{2}-n})(1-\gamma^{n_{1}-n})|<|1-\gamma^{n_{2}-n}|+|1-\gamma^{n_{1}-n}|

(2.8)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\alpha^{(n-n_{1})/2}+1+\alpha^{(n-n_{2})/2}<2\alpha^{(n-n_{2})/2+2}<\alpha^{n+4},

where we used
\alpha^{3}>\alpha+1
and
2<\alpha^{2}
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle|(1-\beta^{n_{1}-n})(1-\gamma^{n_{2}-n})-(1-\beta^{n_{2}-n})(1-\gamma^{n_{1}-n})|

(2.9)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle|1-\beta^{n_{1}-n}||1-\gamma^{n_{2}-n}|+|(1-\beta^{n_{2}-n}||1-\gamma^{n_{1}-n}|<2(\alpha^{(n-n_{1})/2}+1)(\alpha^{(n-n_{2})/2}+1)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 2(\alpha^{(n-n_{2})/2+2})^{2}=2\alpha^{n-n_{2}+4}<\alpha^{n+5}.

The challenge is computing lower bounds. Let

u:=1-\beta^{n_{1}-n},\quad v:=1-\gamma^{n_{2}-n}.

Then we need to compute a lower bound for

|(uv)^{(\sigma)}-(uv)^{(\tau)}|

over all the distinct conjugates of
uv
. For example, taking
\sigma={\text{\rm id}}
and
\tau
to be the map that swaps
\beta
with
\gamma
, we get a lower bound on the absolute value the expression appearing in
the denominator of
\lambda_{2}
in the statement of Lemma
2.7
(ignoring
C_{\beta}
), while taking
\sigma
to be the map that swaps
\alpha
with
\beta
and
\tau
the three cycle
\beta\mapsto\gamma\mapsto\alpha
we get a lower bound on the numerator appearing in the expression of
\lambda_{2}
in the statement of Lemma
2.7
(ignoring
C_{\alpha}
). For this, we use a well–known root separation result of Mahler
[
18
]
. In our particular case, it says that if

R(X)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(X-z_{i})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X]

is a monic polynomial with integer coefficients and distinct roots then writing

M(R):=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\max\{1,|z_{i}|\}

for its Mahler measure, we have

|z_{i}-z_{j}|>\frac{1}{d^{d/2}M(P)^{d-1}}.

For us,

R(X)=\prod_{\sigma\in S_{3}}(X-(uv)^{(\sigma)})

has degree
d=6
. Writing down the 6 conjugates
(uv)^{(\sigma)}
, we get a total of
12
factors namely two occurrences of each of
1-\delta^{n_{s}-n}
for each

and
\delta\in\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}
.
Ignoring the contributions of
1-\alpha^{n_{s}-n}
(as they are in
(0,1)
) and using
|1-\delta^{n_{s}-n}|\leq 1+\alpha^{(n-n_{s})/2}<\alpha^{(n-n_{s}+4)/2}
as before twice for each of
s\in\{1,2\}
and
\delta\in\{\beta,\gamma\}
, we get that

M(P)<(\alpha^{(n-n_{1}+4)/2})^{4}(\alpha^{(n-n_{2}+4)/2})^{4}=\alpha^{4n-2n_{2}-2n_{1}+16}\leq\alpha^{4n+10}.

Thus,

|(uv)^{(\sigma)}-(uv)^{(\tau)}|>\frac{1}{6^{3}(\alpha^{4n+10})^{5}},

showing that if the left–hand side above is
<1
, then

|\log|((uv)^{(\sigma)}-(uv)^{(\tau)})||<\log(6^{3}\alpha^{20n+50})<(20n+50)\log\alpha+3\log 6.

Comparing the above with (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), we conclude that the last bound above holds in all cases. Putting everything together, we get

|\log|\lambda_{2}||<0.5+1.67+2((20n+50)\log\alpha+3\log 6)<(40n+100)\log\alpha+13.

We thus get

|t|\leq\frac{|\log|\lambda_{2}||}{\log|z|}\leq\frac{(40n+100)\log\alpha+13}{0.5\log\alpha}<80n+244.

∎

Lemma 2.8
.

For any positive integer
t
, we have

(i)

\nu_{2}(z^{t}-1)<1+\nu_{2}(n),\quad\text{and}

(ii)

\nu_{3}(z^{t}-1)\leq 1+\nu_{3}(n).

Proof.

For a prime ideal
\pi
of
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{K}}
and an element
x\in{\mathbb{K}}
, we write
\nu_{\pi}(x)
for the exponent at which
\pi
appears in the factorization of the principal
fractional ideal
x{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{K}}
. We shall use the following well-known fact. Let
p\in{\mathbb{Z}}
be the prime such that
\pi
sits above
p
(so,
\pi\mid p
) and let
e_{\pi}:=\nu_{\pi}(p)
is the ramification index of
\pi
. If
\zeta\in{\mathbb{K}}
is such that

\nu_{\pi}(\zeta-1)\geq\frac{e_{\pi}}{p-1},\qquad{\text{\rm then}}\qquad\nu_{\pi}(\zeta^{t}-1)=\nu_{\pi}(\zeta-1)+\nu_{\pi}(n)

(2.10)

(see Lemma 1 in
[
22
]
or Lemma 4.4 in
[
5
]
).
We start with
p=2
. Then
2{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{K}}=\pi^{3}
, where
\pi
is a prime ideal of
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{K}}
. In fact,
\pi
is principal and generated by
1+z+z^{2}
, as it can be seen by rewriting the equation

Q(z)=0\qquad{\text{\rm as}}\qquad(z^{2}+z+1)^{3}=2z(z+1)^{2}(z^{2}+1),

and using that
z+1,~z^{2}+1
are units in
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{K}}
. Now
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{K}}/\pi
is a field with
4
elements. Since
\nu_{2}(z-1)=\nu_{2}(z^{2}-1)=0
, we get that the order of
z
modulo
\pi
is
3
. Thus,
\nu_{\pi}(z^{t}-1)=0
unless
3\mid t
and
\nu_{\pi}(z^{3}-1)=1
. For
t=3k
, we get that

\nu_{\pi}(z^{3k}-1)=\nu_{\pi}(z^{3}-1)((z^{3})^{k-1}+\cdots+1)=\nu_{\pi}(z^{3}-1)=1,

provided
k
is odd. This proves (i) when
n
is odd. Since
z^{3}+1=z^{3}-1+2\equiv{z^{3}-1}\pmod{\pi^{3}}
, we get that
\nu_{\pi}(z^{3}+1)=1
. Thus,
\nu_{\pi}(z^{6}-1)=2
. Now
z^{6}+1=z^{6}-1+2\equiv z^{6}-1\pmod{\pi^{3}}
, so also

\nu_{\pi}(z^{6}+1)=2
. Thus,

\nu_{\pi}(z^{12}-1)=\nu_{\pi}((z^{6}-1)(z^{6}+1))=\nu_{\pi}(z^{3}-1)+\nu_{\pi}(z^{3}+1)=4.

The above calculations show that
\nu_{\pi}(z^{t}-1)<4
unless
12\mid t
. In particular, unless
12\mid t
, we get
\nu_{2}(z^{t}-1)=\nu_{\pi}(z^{t}-1)/3\leq 1
, again confirming (i) in this case. However, if
12\mid t
, then setting
k:=t/12
, then since

\nu_{\pi}(z^{12}-1)=4>\frac{e_{\pi}}{p-1}(=3),

we get by formula (
2.10
) that

\nu_{\pi}(z^{t}-1)=\nu_{\pi}((z^{12})^{k}-1)=\nu_{\pi}(z^{12}-1)+\nu_{\pi}(k)=4+(\nu_{\pi}(n)-6)<\nu_{\pi}(n)=3\nu_{2}(n),

and since
\nu_{2}(z^{t}-1)=\nu_{\pi}(z^{t}-1)/3
, we get the conclusion (i).
Conclusion (ii) is even easier. Indeed,
\pi=3{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{K}}
is prime. Further, the order of
z
in
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{K}}/\pi
is exactly
13
and
\nu_{3}(z^{13}-1)=1>1/12
. Thus, formula (
2.10
) applies and gives that
\nu_{3}(z^{t}-1)=0
unless
13\mid t
and if
13\mid t
, then
\nu_{3}(z^{t}-1)=1+\nu_{3}(n)
.
∎
