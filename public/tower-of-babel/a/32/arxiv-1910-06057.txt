Abstract
. We present syntactic characterisations for the union closed fragments of existential second-order logic and of logics with team semantics. Since union closure is a semantical and undecidable property, the normal form we introduce enables the handling and provides a better understanding of this fragment. We also introduce inclusion-exclusion games that turn out to be precisely the corresponding model-checking games. These games are not only interesting in their own right, but they also are a key factor towards building a bridge between the semantic and syntactic fragments. On the level of logics with team semantics we additionally present restrictions of inclusion-exclusion logic to capture the union closed fragment. Moreover, we define a team based atom that when adding it to first-order logic also precisely captures the union closed fragment of existential second-order logic which answers an open question by Galliani and Hella.

1.
Introduction

One branch of model theory engages with the characterisation of semantical fragments, which typically are undecidable, as syntactical fragments of the logics under consideration.
Prominent examples are van Benthem’s Theorem characterising the bisimulation invariant fragment of first-order logic as the modal-logic
[
vB76
]
or preservation theorems like the Łoś-Tarski Theorem, which states that formulae preserved in substructures are equivalent to universal formulae
[
Hod97b
]
.
In this paper we consider formulae
\varphi(X)
of existential second-order logic,
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
, in a free relational variable
X
and investigate the property of being closed under unions,
meaning that whenever a family of relations
X_{i}
all satisfy
\varphi
, then their union
\bigcup_{i}X_{i}
should also do so.
Certainly closure under unions is an undecidable property.
We provide a syntactical characterisation of all formulae of existential second-order logic obeying this property via a normal form called
myopic
-
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
, a notion based on ideas of Galliani and Hella
[
GH13
]
.
By Fagin’s Theorem,
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
is the logical equivalent of the complexity class
NP
which highlights the importance to understand its fragments.
Towards this end we employ game theoretic concepts and introduce a novel game type, called
inclusion-exclusion games
, suited for formulae
\varphi(X)
with a free relational variable.
In these games a strategy no longer is simply winning for one player — and hence proving whether a sentence is satisfied — but it is moreover adequate for a certain relation
Y
over
\mathfrak{A}
showing that the formula is satisfied by
\mathfrak{A}
and
Y
, in symbols
\mathfrak{A}\vDash\varphi(Y)
.
We construct myopic-
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
formulae that can define the winning regions of specifically those inclusion-exclusion games that are (semantically) closed under unions.
Conceptually such games are eligible for any
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
-formula, but since our interest lies in those formulae that are closed under unions, we introduce a restricted version of such games, called
union games
, that precisely correspond to the model-checking games of union closed
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
-formulae.
Consequently, the notion of union closure is captured on the level of formulae by the myopic fragment of
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
and on the game theoretic level by union games.

Existential second-order logic has a tight connection to modern logics of dependence and independence that are based on the concept of teams, introduced by Hodges
[
Hod97a
]
, and later refined by Väänänen in 2007
[
Vää07
]
.
In contrast to classical logics, formulae of such a logic are evaluated against a set of assignments, called a
team
.
One main characteristic of these logics is that dependencies between variables, such as “
x
depends solely on
y
”, are expressed as atomic properties of teams.
Widely used dependency atoms include dependence (
\operatorname{=}(x,y)
), inclusion (
x\subseteq y
), exclusion (
x\mathbin{|}y
) and independence (
x\bot y
).
It is known that both independence logic
\operatorname{FO}(\bot)
and inclusion-exclusion logic
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq,{}\mathbin{|}{})
have the same expressive power as full existential second-order logic
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}

[
Gal12
]
.
The team in such logics corresponds to the free relational variable in existential second-order formulae, enabling us to ask the same questions about fragments with certain closure properties in both frameworks.
One example of a well understood closure property is
downwards closure
stating that if a formula is satisfied by a team then it is also satisfied by all subteams (i.e. subsets of that team).
It is well known that exclusion logic
\operatorname{FO}({}\mathbin{|}{})
is equivalent to dependence logic
\operatorname{FO}(\operatorname{dep})

[
Gal12
]
,
which corresponds to the downwards closed fragment of
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}

[
KV09
]
.
The issue of union closure is different.
Galliani and Hella have shown that inclusion logic
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq)
corresponds to greatest fixed-point-logic
\text{GFP}^{+}
and, hence, by using the Immerman-Vardi Theorem, it captures all
Ptime
computable queries on ordered structures
[
GH13
]
.
They also proved that every union closed dependency notion that itself is first-order definable (where the formula has access to a predicate for the team) is already definable in inclusion logic.
However, there are union closed properties that are not definable in inclusion logic (think of a union closed
NP
property).
For a concrete example we refer to the atom
\mathcal{R}
from
[
GH13
]
.
Thus Galliani and Hella asked the question whether there is a union closed atomic dependency notion
\beta
, such that the logic
\operatorname{FO}(\beta)
captures precisely the union closed fragment of
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq,{}\mathbin{|}{})
.
In the present work we answer this question positively with the aid of inclusion-exclusion games.
Furthermore, we present a syntactical restriction of all
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq,{}\mathbin{|}{})
formulae that also precisely describe the union closed fragment.
This syntactical fragment corresponds to myopic-
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
and is in harmony with the game theoretical view, which is described by union games.

This paper is based on
[
HW19
,
HW20
]
and also contains some improvements from
[
Hoe19
]
like the more direct translations into the myopic fragments (Theorem
6
and Theorem
15
), while Theorem
9
and Theorem
21
are based on the original proofs and are enhanced by the observation that they produce only a limited number of in-/exclusion atoms or literals with second-order symbols.
Furthermore, we also present restricted variants of the inclusion-exclusion games that are the model-checking games of
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq)
or
\operatorname{FO}({}\mathbin{|}{})
.

Sections

,

5

In section three the central notion of this paper, inclusion-exclusion
games, is introduced, which is used in section four to characterise
the union closed fragment within existential second-order logic.
Section five provides a restriction of the games specifically suited for this fragment.
Based on these notions, Section

Finally, in Section

Team semantics for a first-order formula

Among the most frequently considered atoms are the following.

Dependence:

\mathfrak{A}\vDash_{X}\operatorname{=}({\bar{x}},y)\mathbin{:\!\!\iff}s({\bar{x}})=s^{\prime}({\bar{x}})\text{ implies }s(y)=s^{\prime}(y)\text{ for all }s,s^{\prime}\in X

\mathfrak{A}\vDash_{X}{\bar{x}}\bot{\bar{y}}\mathbin{:\!\!\iff}\text{for all }s,s^{\prime}\in X\text{ exists }s^{\prime\prime}\in X\text{ s.t.~}s({\bar{x}})=s^{\prime\prime}({\bar{x}})

Union Closure

A formula

Analogously, a formula

3.
Inclusion-Exclusion Games

Classical model-checking games are designed to express satisfiability of sentences, i.e. formulae without free variables.
The instantiation of a first-order formula with free variables by some values is dealt with by constructing expanded structures having constants that are assigned the respective values.
Since our focus lies on second-order formulae in a free relational variable we are in need for a game that is able to not only express that a formula is satisfied, but moreover that it is satisfied by a certain relation.
In the games we are about to describe a set of designated positions is present — called the
target set
— which corresponds to the full relation
A^{k}
(where the free relational variable has arity
k
).
A winning strategy is said to be
adequate
for a subset
X
of the target positions, if the target vertices visited by it are
X
.
On the level of logics this matches the relation satisfying the corresponding formula, i.e. there is a winning strategy adequate for
X
if and only if the formula is satisfied by
X
.

{defi}

An
inclusion-exclusion game

\mathcal{G}=(V,V_{0},V_{1},E,I,T,{E_{\mathsf{ex}}})
is
played by two players 0 and 1 where

\bullet

V_{\sigma}
is the set of vertices of player
\sigma
,

\bullet

V=V_{0}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}V_{1}
,

\bullet

E\subseteq V\times V
is a set of possible moves,

\bullet

I\subseteq V
is the (possibly empty) set of initial positions,

\bullet

T\subseteq V
is the set of target vertices and

\bullet

{E_{\mathsf{ex}}}\subseteq V\times V
is the exclusion condition, which defines the winning condition for player
0
.
2
2

2

{E_{\mathsf{ex}}}
can always be replaced by the symmetric closure of
{E_{\mathsf{ex}}}
without altering its semantics.

The edges going into
T
, that is
{E_{\mathsf{in}}}\coloneqq E\cap(V\times T)
, are called
inclusion edges
, while
{E_{\mathsf{ex}}}
is the set of
exclusion edges
(sometimes also called conflicting pairs).

Inclusion-exclusion games are
second-order
games, so instead of single plays we are more interested in sets of plays that are admitted by some winning strategy for player
0
.
Model-checking games for logics with team semantics made their first appearance in
[
Grä13
]
and, in fact, inclusion-exclusion games can be considered to be a variant of the second-order games introduced in the same paper.

For a subset
X\subseteq T
the aim of player 0 is to provide a winning strategy (which can be viewed as a set of plays respecting the exclusion condition and containing all possible strategies of player 1) such that the vertices of
T
that are visited by this strategy correspond precisely to

{defi}

A
winning strategy
(for player 0)
\mathcal{S}
is a possibly empty subgraph
\mathcal{S}=(W,F)
of

ensuring the following four consistency conditions.

(1)

For every
v\in W\cap V_{0}
holds
\operatorname{N}_{\mathcal{S}}(v)\neq\varnothing
.

(2)

For every
v\in W\cap V_{1}
holds
\operatorname{N}_{\mathcal{S}}(v)=\operatorname{N}_{G}(v)
.

(3)

I\subseteq W
.

(4)

(W\times W)\cap{E_{\mathsf{ex}}}=\varnothing
.

Intuitively, the conditions (
1
) and (
2
)
state that the strategy must provide at least one move from each node of player
0
used by the strategy but does not make assumptions about the moves that player
1
may make whenever the strategy
plays a node belonging to player
1
.
In particular, the strategy must not play any terminal vertices that are in
V_{0}
.
Furthermore, (
3
) enforces that at least the initial vertices are contained while
(
4
) disallows playing with conflicting pairs
(v,w)\in{E_{\mathsf{ex}}}
, i.e.
v
and

must not
coexist in any winning strategy for player
0
.
If

(\varnothing,\varnothing)
is the trivial winning strategy.
Another interesting observation is that
(W,F)
is a winning strategy for player
0
, if and only if the subgraph of the game induced by
W

(W,(W\times W)\cap E)
, is a winning strategy.
In other words, me may freely add edges that are present in the game graph between nodes of a winning strategy, i.e. a winning strategy is determined by its nodes.

Since we do
not
have a notion for a winning strategy for player
1

Of course, the winning condition of an inclusion-exclusion game

is first-order definable.
The formula
\varphi_{\operatorname{win}}(W)
has the property that
\mathcal{G}\vDash\varphi_{\operatorname{win}}(W)
if and only if
(W,(W\times W)\cap E)
is a winning strategy for player 0 in
\mathcal{G}
, where

\displaystyle\varphi_{\operatorname{win}}(W)\;\coloneqq\;

\displaystyle\forall v(Wv\rightarrow[(V_{0}v\land\exists w(Evw\land Ww))\,\lor

\displaystyle\phantom{\forall v[Wv\rightarrow(]}(V_{1}v\land\forall w(Evw\rightarrow Ww))])\,\land

\displaystyle\forall v(Iv\rightarrow Wv)\land\forall v\forall w((Wv\land Ww)\rightarrow\neg{E_{\mathsf{ex}}}vw).

describes the winning conditions imposed on the subgraph induced by
W
.

We are mainly interested in the subset of target vertices that are visited by a winning strategy
\mathcal{S}=(W,F)
.
More formally,
\mathcal{S}
induces
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{S})\coloneqq W\cap T

\mathcal{S}
.
This definition is reminiscent to the notion

Now we can associate with every inclusion-exclusion game

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{G})\coloneqq\{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{S}):\mathcal{S}\text{ is a winning strategy for player 0 in }\mathcal{G}\}

Intuitively, as already pointed out, games of this kind will also be the model-checking games for

Given a structure
\mathfrak{A}
and such a formula, we are interested in the possible relations

We will construct the game such that

It will be more convenient for our purposes that the target vertices of an inclusion-exclusion game are not required to be terminal positions.
However it would be no restriction as it is easy to transform any given game into one that agrees on the (possible) targets, in which all target vertices are terminal.

Let us start the analysis of inclusion-exclusion games from a complexity theoretical point of view.
The satisfiability problem of propositional logic can be reduced to deciding whether player 0 has a winning strategy in an inclusion-exclusion game.

Theorem 2
.

The problem of deciding whether
X\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{G})
for a finite inclusion-exclusion game

is
NP-complete
in the size of
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof 3.1
.

Determining whether
X\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{G})
holds is clearly in

For the

Let
\varphi
be formula in conjunctive normal form, i.e.
\varphi=\bigwedge_{j\leq m}C_{j}
where
C_{j}=\bigvee_{i}L_{i}
is a disjunction of literals (variables or negated variables).
The game
\mathcal{G}_{\varphi}
is constructed as follows.
For every variable

we add two vertices (of player 1)
x
and
\neg x
connected by an exclusion edge.
Moreover, for every clause

we add a vertex, belonging to player 0, which has an outgoing edge into each literal

occurring in it. There are no initial vertices, i.e.
I\coloneqq\varnothing

and the target set

is the set of all clauses

of
\varphi
.
Now,
S\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{G})
if and only if

is satisfiable.
In particular,

\varphi
is satisfiable.

The complexity is, of course, ascribed to the exclusion edges.

{obs}

The problem of deciding whether
X\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{G})
for a finite inclusion-exclusion game
\mathcal{G}
without exclusion edges, i.e.

and in fact complete for this class.

Proof 3.2
.

We describe the reduction from, and to, the well known

.
The input is a game graph

and a vertex

and the task is to determine whether player 0 has a winning strategy starting from
v
.
Here again a player loses if he cannot make a move.

It is easy to see that inclusion-exclusion games are an extension of this problem.
For the converse direction add a fresh vertex

of player 1 to the game that has outgoing edges to all

and

Further, turn all

into player 0 vertices and remove all their outgoing edges.
Now player 0 wins the

.

3.1.
Model-Checking Games for Existential Second-Order Logic

In this section we define model-checking games for formulae

Characterising the Union Closed Formulae within Existential Second-Order Logic

In this section we investigate formulae
\varphi(X)
of existential second-order logic that are closed under unions with respect to their free relational variable

Union closure, being a semantical property of formulae, is undecidable.
However, we present a

{defi}

A formula
\varphi(X)\in\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
is called

if it has the shape
\varphi(X)=\forall{\bar{x}}(X{\bar{x}}\rightarrow\exists{\bar{R}}\varphi^{\prime}(X,{\bar{R}}))

\varphi^{\prime}\in\operatorname{FO}
and

occurs only positively

3

3

Variants of myopic formulae have already been considered for first-order logic

and for greatest fixed-point logics
[
Grä16

,
but to our knowledge myopic

Let
\mathcal{U}
denote the set of all union closed

-formulae.
To establish the claim that myopic formulae are a normal form of

we need to show that all myopic formulae are indeed closed under unions and, more importantly, that every union closed formula can be translated into an equivalent myopic formula.
This translation is in particular constructive.

In order to strengthen the intuition on myopic formulae we start with a basic property.

Proposition 4
.

Every myopic formula is union closed.

Proof 4.1
.

Let
\varphi=\forall{\bar{x}}(X{\bar{x}}\rightarrow\exists{\bar{R}}\varphi^{\prime}(X,{\bar{R}}))
and
(\mathfrak{A},X_{i})\vDash\varphi
for all

We claim that

Let

By assumption

A fortiori (

occurs only positively in

), we obtain

Since

was chosen arbitrarily, this property holds for all

Of course, the reverse direction is the difficult and interesting part.
The main theorem of this section combines both parts.

Theorem 5
.

\varphi(X)\in\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}

is equivalent to some myopic

We provide two proofs of the remaining direction, a direct one which is short and easy to understand, while it does not utilise the methods developed in Section

\mathfrak{A}\nvDash_{X{\upharpoonright_{{\bar{x}}={\bar{a}}}}}{\bar{v}}\mathbin{|}{\bar{w}}
holds
for some
{\bar{a}}\in X({\bar{x}})
if and only if there are some

and

, i.e.

Conversely, if

for some

The third item follows from the simple fact that
{\bar{x}}
is never quantified and that those atoms are not in the scope of a disjunction, hence the values of

are preserved.

The
{\bar{x}}
-guarded version of a formula

do not occur is the formula

which results from

by replacing every inclusion/exclusion atom by its

-guarded variant, i.e.

would become

would be turned into
{\bar{x}}{\bar{u}}\mathbin{|}{\bar{x}}{\bar{v}}
.
By property (

) of Proposition

, the connection between a formula and its

-guarded version is the following.

Lemma 13
.

Let

be the
{\bar{x}}
-guarded version of

for every
{\bar{a}}\in X({\bar{x}})
.

Like union games an
{\bar{x}}
-myopic formula is evaluated componentwise, which leads to the union closure of this fragment.

Theorem 14
.

Let

-myopic and

Proof 6.3
.

{\bar{y}}

{\bar{x}}

(3)

\mathfrak{A}\vDash_{X_{\bar{a}}}\varphi({\bar{x}})
for every

\Longleftarrow

.

Let

be an
{\bar{x}}
-myopic formula of the form

\mathfrak{A}\vDash_{X}\varphi
if and only if there exists

such that

for every

and
\mathfrak{A}\vDash_{X[{\bar{s}}\mapsto F]{\upharpoonright_{{\bar{x}}={\bar{a}}}}}\psi^{\prime}
for all
{\bar{a}}\in X({\bar{x}})

is the unguarded version of

Proof 6.4
.

By induction on
\psi
and applying Proposition

.

We present two different proofs for the next theorem, which bring a myopic
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
-formula into this normal form.
The following proof is based on methods of Galliani, Kontinen and Väänänen
[
Gal12
,
KV09

while the other one resembles the proof of Theorem

.

Theorem 17
.

Let
\varphi(X)
be a myopic
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
-formula.
There is an equivalent
{\bar{x}}
-myopic formula of
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq,{}\mathbin{|}{})
where
|{\bar{x}}|=\operatorname{ar}(X)
.

Proof 6.5
.

First of all let us introduce a normal form of myopic
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
-formulae.
Since in myopic formulae the variable
X
may occur only positively in the subformula
\varphi^{\prime}
, we can transform every
\forall{\bar{x}}(X{\bar{x}}\rightarrow\exists{\bar{R}}\varphi^{\prime}({\bar{R}},X,{\bar{x}}))
into the equivalent formula
\forall{\bar{x}}(X{\bar{x}}\rightarrow\exists S(S\subseteq X\land\exists{\bar{R}}\varphi^{\prime}({\bar{R}},S,{\bar{x}})))
, where
S\subseteq X
is a shorthand for
\forall{\bar{y}}(S{\bar{y}}\rightarrow X{\bar{y}})
.
We now apply the Skolem-normal form of
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
-formulae to
\exists{\bar{R}}\varphi^{\prime}({\bar{R}},S,{\bar{x}})
, which yields the formula
\sigma(S,{\bar{x}})\coloneqq\exists{\bar{f}}\forall{\bar{y}}((f_{1}({\bar{w}})=f_{2}({\bar{w}})\leftrightarrow S{\bar{w}})\land\psi({\bar{f}},{\bar{x}},{\bar{y}}))
, where
\psi
is a quantifier-free first-order formula and
{\bar{w}}
is a subtuple of
{\bar{y}}
and, moreover, every
f_{i}
occurs in
\sigma
only with a unique tuple
{\bar{w}}_{i}
(consisting of pairwise different variables) as argument, that is
f_{i}({\bar{w}}_{i})
(see
[
KV09
]
where an analogous construction is made).
The original formula can thus be transformed into
\forall{\bar{x}}(X{\bar{x}}\rightarrow\exists S(S\subseteq X\land\sigma(S,{\bar{x}})))
.
Similarly to
[
Gal12
]
we embed
\sigma(S,{\bar{x}})
into inclusion-exclusion logic as
\vartheta({\bar{s}},{\bar{x}})\coloneqq\forall{\bar{y}}\exists{\bar{z}}\big(\bigwedge_{i}\operatorname{=}({\bar{x}}{\bar{w}}_{i},z_{i})\land(({\bar{x}}{\bar{w}}\subseteq{\bar{x}}{\bar{s}}\land z_{1}=z_{2})\lor({\bar{x}}{\bar{w}}\mathbin{|}{\bar{x}}{\bar{s}}\land z_{1}\neq z_{2}))\land\psi^{\prime}({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},{\bar{z}})\big)
.
Here
\psi^{\prime}
is obtained from
\psi
by simply replacing every occurrence of
f_{i}({\bar{w}}_{i})=f_{j}({\bar{w}}_{j})
by
z_{i}=z_{j}
.
The only difference in our case is that every dependency atom is
{\bar{x}}
-guarded due to the fact that the subformula at hand is inside the scope of the universally quantified variables
{\bar{x}}
in
\forall{\bar{x}}(X{\bar{x}}\rightarrow\dots)
.
Notice that dependence atoms of the form
\operatorname{=}({\bar{x}}{\bar{w}}_{i},z_{i})
can also be regarded as
{\bar{x}}
-myopic.
Formally, we can embed such an atom into exclusion logic via the formula
\forall v({\bar{x}}{\bar{w}}_{i}v\mathbin{|}{\bar{x}}{\bar{w}}_{i}z_{i}\lor z_{i}=v)
, which has the intended shape
[
Gal12
]
.
The whole formula
\varphi(X)
thus translates into
\mu({\bar{x}})\coloneqq\exists{\bar{s}}({\bar{s}}\subseteq{\bar{x}}\land\vartheta({\bar{s}},{\bar{x}}))
.
Let
\vartheta^{\prime}({\bar{s}},{\bar{x}})
be the unguarded version of
\vartheta({\bar{s}},{\bar{x}})
.
Analogously to the argumentation of Galliani
[
Gal12
]
by additionally making use of Proposition
12
, we see that
(\mathfrak{A},Y{\upharpoonright_{{\bar{x}}={\bar{a}}}}({\bar{s}}))\vDash_{{\bar{x}}\mapsto{\bar{a}}}\sigma(S,{\bar{x}})
if and only if
\mathfrak{A}\vDash_{Y{\upharpoonright_{{\bar{x}}={\bar{a}}}}}\vartheta^{\prime}({\bar{x}})
for
{\bar{a}}\in Y({\bar{x}})
, where
Y
is a team with domain
\{{\bar{s}},{\bar{x}}\}
(here the variable
S
takes the role of the team).
Using Lemma
16
we have
\mathfrak{A}\vDash_{X}\mu({\bar{x}})
if and only if there is a function
F\colon X\to\mathcal{P}^{+}(A^{\operatorname{ar}({\bar{s}})})
such that
F(s)\subseteq X({\bar{x}})
for every
s\in X
and
\mathfrak{A}\vDash_{X[{\bar{s}}\mapsto F]{\upharpoonright_{{\bar{x}}={\bar{a}}}}}\vartheta^{\prime}({\bar{s}},{\bar{x}})
for all
{\bar{a}}\in X({\bar{x}})
, which again holds if and only if there exists such an
F
and
(\mathfrak{A},F(s))\vDash_{t}\sigma(S,{\bar{x}})
for all
t\in X
, but this just means
(\mathfrak{A},X({\bar{x}}))\vDash\forall{\bar{x}}(X{\bar{x}}\rightarrow\exists S(S\subseteq X\land\sigma(S,{\bar{x}})))
.

Corollary 18

(Normal form of myopic-
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq,{}\mathbin{|}{})
)
.

Let
\varphi({\bar{x}})
be a union closed formula of
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq,{}\mathbin{|}{})
.
There is a logically equivalent
{\bar{x}}
-myopic formula
\psi({\bar{x}})=\exists{\bar{s}}({\bar{s}}\subseteq{\bar{x}}\land\vartheta)
where in
\vartheta
only
{\bar{x}}
-guarded dependency atoms occur.

6.1.
Alternative Proof

The proof of Theorem
17
uses a translation into a special Skolem-normal form
that in the end produces a lot of guarded in-/exclusion atoms.
We can prevent this by using a different proof technique that similar to the proof of Theorem
17

exploits that winning strategies of union games are definable in the myopic fragment of
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq,{}\mathbin{|}{})
-formulae
and that the model-checking games of myopic formulae are first-order interpretable.
The rest of this section is organised as follows.
First, we define target sets of a union game by a myopic
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq,{}\mathbin{|}{})
-formula, then
we adapt the interpretation lemma for logics with team semantics and finally present the alternative proof of Theorem
17
.

{exa}

Let us demonstrate that (vertex sets of) winning strategies of
general
inclusion-exclusion games can be defined in
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq,{}\mathbin{|}{})
:

\displaystyle\psi_{\operatorname{win}}(y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\psi_{\operatorname{init}}(y)\land\psi_{\operatorname{move}}(y)\land\psi_{{E_{\mathsf{ex}}}}(y)\text{ where}

\displaystyle\psi_{\operatorname{init}}(y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\forall z(Iz\rightarrow z\subseteq y)

\displaystyle\psi_{\operatorname{move}}(y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\exists z([(V_{0}y\land\exists z^{\prime}(Eyz^{\prime}\land z^{\prime}\subseteq z))\lor(V_{1}y\land\forall z^{\prime}(Eyz^{\prime}\rightarrow z^{\prime}\subseteq z))]\land z\subseteq y)

\displaystyle\psi_{{E_{\mathsf{ex}}}}(y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\forall z(({E_{\mathsf{ex}}}yz\lor{E_{\mathsf{ex}}}zy)\rightarrow y\mathbin{|}z)

It is not difficult to verify that these formulae are just expressing the conditions for winning strategies (cf. Definition
3
).
More formally, we have the following claim:

{clm}

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an inclusion-exclusion game
and
Y
be a non-empty team over
\mathcal{G}
with
y\in\operatorname{dom}(Y)
.
Then
\mathcal{G}\vDash_{Y}\psi_{\operatorname{win}}(y)
if and only if
Y(y)

An Atom capturing the Union Closed Fragment

The present work was motivated by a question of Galliani and Hella in 2013

Galliani and Hella asked whether there is a union closed atomic dependency notion

Moreover, they have established a theorem stating that every union closed atomic property that is definable in first-order logic (where the formula has access to the team via a predicate) is expressible in inclusion logic.
Thus, whatever atom characterises all union closed properties of

must axiomatise an inherently second-order property.

Intuitively speaking, as we have seen in Section

Therefore, a canonical solution to this question is to propose an atomic formula that defines the winning regions in a union game.
Towards this we must describe how a game can be encoded into a team.
This is not as straightforward as one might think, because there is a technical pitfall we need to avoid.
The union of two teams describing union games, each won by player 0, might encode a game won by player 1, but by union closure it must satisfy the atomic formula.

We encode union games in teams by using variable tuples for the respective components, where we also encode the complementary relations in order to ensure that the union of two different games cannot form a different game.

9.
Concluding Remarks

The central notion in this paper are “inclusion-exclusion games” which, as we have proven are model-checking games for existential second-order logic
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
, or, equivalently, inclusion-exclusion logic
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq,{}\mathbin{|}{})
.
A syntactical restriction of these games lead to union games, the model-checking games for the class of all formuale of
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
that are closed under unions, class defined by its semantics.
In Section
8
other restrictions gave access to games for exclusion and inclusion logic, respectively.
We want to point out that even for other fragments, such as closure under intersection, negation, super sets and so forth, it is not difficult to find variations of inclusion-exclusion games that capture those.
We refrain from making the constructions explicit as it is a simple exercise and we do not see how these special cases are of any benefit.
However, the mere fact that inclusion-exclusion games are easily adapted to fit any such setting makes us belief that they are a natural notion.

Let us remark on the “naturalness” of the atom
\cup\mathsf{-game}
.
Certainly inclusion, exclusion and the notions alike can be regarded as natural atomic dependency formulae, whereas the just introduced atom has to be classified differently.
Nevertheless, it is a canonical candidate since it solves a complete problem of the desired class.
Of course, a more natural — and more usable — atom might be found, but it will not be as simplistic as e.g. inclusion for Galliani and Hella have shown that every first-order definable union closed property is already expressible in inclusion logic.
Hence, whatever atom one proposes, it must make use of some inherently second-order concepts.
For concretely expressing properties, the introduced myopic fragments of
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
and
\operatorname{FO}(\subseteq,{}\mathbin{|}{})
are more practical.

The various syntactical characterisations of the union closed fragments presented in this work now enables their further investigation.
This could result in a complexity theoretical analysis or a more detailed classification of
\operatorname{\Sigma^{1}_{1}}
.
