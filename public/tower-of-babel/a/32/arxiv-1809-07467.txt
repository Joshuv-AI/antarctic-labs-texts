Morita Equivalent Blocks of Symmetric Groups This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on the Representation Theory of the Symmetric Groups and Related Topics. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/symmetric-groups-2018.html
Benjamin SAMBALE

Abstract
A well-known result of Scopes states that there are only finitely many Morita equivalence classes of p p -blocks of symmetric groups with a given weight (or defect). In this note we investigate a lower bound on the number of those Morita equivalence classes.

Introduction

Every
p
-block
B
of a symmetric group
S_{n}
is uniquely determined by its
weight

w\geq 0
and its
core

\mu
(a partition of
n-pw
, see next Section
3
for details). Scopes
[
12
]
showed that there are only finitely many Morita equivalence classes of
p
-blocks of symmetric groups over the field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with a given weight (this confirms a special case of Donovan’s conjecture). Puig
[
9
]
extended her result to blocks defined over
\mathbb{Z}
. More precisely, the number of these so-called
Scopes classes
is
\frac{1}{p}\binom{wp}{p-1}
provided
w>0
. If
p>2
and
w>1
, then there exist pairs of distinct Scopes classes whose cores are conjugate to one another. Since the corresponding blocks of such pairs are Morita equivalent (even isomorphic), the number of Morita equivalence classes
M(p,w)
of
p
-blocks with weight
w
satisfies

\displaystyle M(p,w)\leq\frac{1}{2p}\binom{wp}{p-1}+\frac{1}{2}\binom{\lfloor wp/2\rfloor}{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}

(1.1)

for
w>0
(see
[
10
, Corollary 3.10]
). In this paper we are interested in the sharpness of this bound. Obviously, there is only one class of blocks with weight (defect)
0
or
1
respectively. The blocks of weight
2
have been investigated by many authors. For instance, Richards
[
10
]
obtained a formula for the decomposition matrix of those blocks. Since the decomposition matrix is a Morita invariant over
\mathbb{Z}
(up to permutations of rows and columns), it is possible to count Morita equivalence classes at least when
p
is small. We have used the computer algebra system GAP
[
4
]
to confirm the following conjecture for
p\leq 11
.

Conjecture 1.1
.

\displaystyle M(p,2)=\frac{1}{2p}\binom{2p}{p-1}+\frac{1}{2}\binom{p}{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}.

For blocks of weight
w=3
and
p\geq 5
, Fayers
[
3
]
has shown that all decomposition numbers are
0
or
1
. Using the Jantzen–Schaper formula
[
11
]
it is therefore possible to compute the decomposition matrices recursively. In this way we obtain
M(5,3)=147
and
M(7,3)=3936
using GAP. Note that (
1.1
) is sharp in these cases as well.

Much less is known about the blocks of weight
w>3
. Nevertheless, for small primes the following complementary result seems to hold.

Conjecture 1.2
.

\displaystyle M(p,w)=\begin{cases}2&\text{if }p=2,\ w=3,\\
w&\text{if }p=2,\ w>3,\\
\left\lfloor\dfrac{3w^{2}+2w}{4}\right\rfloor&\text{if }p=3,\ w>0.\end{cases}

Using an algorithm which is explained in the next sections, we have checked Conjecture
1.2
for
p=2
,
w\in\{0,\ldots,28\}\cup\{32\}
and
p=3
,
w\leq 15
. The case
(p,w)=(2,3)
is in fact an exception. Here, the Scopes classes are represented by the cores
()
,
(1)
and
(2,1)
. One can show with Magma
[
1
]
that the cores
(1)
and
(2,1)
correspond to Morita equivalent blocks at least over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

Unfortunately, our method does not extend directly to
p>3
. Nevertheless, we obtain the following intervals with GAP:
496\leq M(5,4)\leq 507
and
1278\leq M(5,5)\leq 1298
.

Finally, we remark that our observations should also apply to Hecke algebras.

2
A Morita invariant

Since the decomposition matrix of a block is usually not available (like in the situation of Conjecture
1.2
), it is important to know other Morita invariants. The following approach applies to any finite group
G
and any prime
p
. We denote the set of
p
-regular elements of
G
by
G^{0}
and the set of irreducible Brauer characters by
\operatorname{IBr}(G)
. For
\chi,\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\cup\operatorname{IBr}(G)
we are interested in the following
p
-scalar product

\displaystyle[\chi,\psi]^{0}:=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G^{0}}\chi(g)\psi\big(g^{-1}\big)\in\mathbb{C}.

The following lemma states that these numbers are preserved under Morita equivalence.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
be Morita equivalent blocks of
(
possibly different
)
finite groups with respect to a complete discrete valuation ring. Let

\displaystyle M_{i}:=\big([\chi,\psi]^{0}\big)_{\chi,\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(B_{i})}

for
i=1,2
. Then there exists a permutation matrix
T
such that

\displaystyle TM_{1}=M_{2}T.

Proof.

Let
Q_{i}=(d_{\chi\psi})
be the decomposition matrix of
B_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Then
C_{i}:=Q_{i}^{\text{t}}Q_{i}
is the Cartan matrix of
B_{i}
where
Q_{i}^{\text{t}}
denotes the transpose of
Q_{i}
. By
[
5
, Theorem 2.13]
we have
C_{i}^{-1}=\big([\varphi,\theta]^{0}\big)_{\varphi,\theta\in\operatorname{IBr}(B_{i})}
. It follows that

\displaystyle M_{i}=\bigg(\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G_{i}^{0}}\sum_{\varphi,\theta\in\operatorname{IBr}(B_{i})}d_{\chi\varphi}d_{\psi\theta}\varphi(g)\theta\big(g^{-1}\big)\bigg)_{\chi,\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(B_{i})}

\displaystyle\hphantom{M_i}{}=\bigg(\sum_{\varphi,\theta\in\operatorname{IBr}(B_{i})}d_{\chi\varphi}d_{\psi\theta}[\varphi,\theta]^{0}\bigg)_{\chi,\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(B_{i})}=Q_{i}C_{i}^{-1}Q_{i}^{\text{t}}.

(2.1)

Since
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are Morita equivalent, there exist permutation matrices
S
,
T
such that
TQ_{1}S=Q_{2}
. Since
T
is orthogonal, we conclude that

\displaystyle M_{2}T=Q_{2}\big(Q_{2}^{\text{t}}Q_{2}\big)^{-1}Q_{2}^{\text{t}}T=TQ_{1}S\big(S^{\text{t}}Q_{1}^{\text{t}}T^{\text{t}}TQ_{1}S\big)^{-1}S^{\text{t}}Q_{1}^{\text{t}}T^{\text{t}}T=TQ_{1}C_{1}^{-1}Q_{1}^{\text{t}}=TM_{1}.\!\!\!

∎

The matrix
M:=M_{1}
in Lemma
2.1
encodes several important numerical invariants of
B:=B_{1}
. Obviously, the size of
M
is
k(B)\times k(B)
where
k(B):=|\operatorname{Irr}(B)|
. By (
2.1
), the rank of
M
equals
l(B):=|\operatorname{IBr}(B)|
. Recall that the
height

h(\chi)\geq 0
of
\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(B)
is defined by
\chi(1)_{p}=p^{a-d+h(\chi)}
where
|G|_{p}=p^{a}
and
d
is the defect of
B
. If
h(\chi)=0
, then the
p
-adic valuation of
[\chi,\psi]^{0}
equals
h(\psi)-d
for every
\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(B)
(see
[
5
, Theorem 3.24]
). Moreover, if
h(\chi)>0
, then the
p
-adic valuation of
[\chi,\chi]^{0}
is larger than
-d
(see
[
5
, Lemma 3.22(a)]
).
In this way,
M
encodes all character heights. Finally, one can show that
M
determines the elementary divisors of the Cartan matrix of
B
. However, the next theorem implies that all these invariants do not suffice to distinguish blocks of symmetric groups.

Theorem 2.2

(Chuang–Rouquier
[
2
, Theorem 7.2]
)
.

Blocks
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
of symmetric groups with the same weight are splendidly derived equivalent. In particular there exists a
signed
permutation matrix
T
such that
TM_{1}=M_{2}T
in the situation of Lemma
2.1
.

We will see in the following that for blocks of symmetric groups the matrix
M
can be computed from the character table of a local subgroup which only depends on the weight of the block (see Theorem
4.1
). At the same time we determine the signs of
T
in Theorem
2.2
without using the characters explicitly. To do so, we need to introduce a lot of notation.

3
Notation

We fix the following notation (details can be found in
[
7
]
). A
partition
of
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is a non-increasing sequence of positive integers
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{l})
such that
|\lambda|:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\lambda_{i}=n
. The number
l(\lambda):=l
is called the
length
of
\lambda
. We allow the empty partition
()
of
0
. The set of partitions of
n
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(n)
.

It is well-known that the conjugacy classes of the symmetric group
S_{n}
of degree
n
consist of the elements with a common cycle structure. Hence, we can choose a set of representatives
\{s_{\lambda}\colon\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)\}
of those conjugacy classes. Similarly, the irreducible characters of
S_{n}
are parametrized by
\mathcal{P}(n)
and we will write

\displaystyle\operatorname{Irr}(S_{n})=\{\chi_{\lambda}\colon\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)\}.

Let
p
be a prime. Successively removing all hooks of length
p
from (the Young diagram of)
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
yields the
p
-core

\lambda_{(p)}
of
\lambda
. The number of removed hooks is called the
weight

w
of
\lambda
. Observe that
n=|\lambda_{(p)}|+pw
. Moreover, the
p
-sign
of
\lambda
is defined by
\delta_{p}(\lambda)=(-1)^{\sum l_{i}}
where the
l_{i}
are the leg lengths of the removed hooks. By Nakayama’s conjecture, characters
\chi_{\lambda},\chi_{\mu}\in\operatorname{Irr}(S_{n})
lie in the same
p
-block if and only if
\lambda_{(p)}=\mu_{(p)}
. Therefore, we may speak of the core and the weight of a block
B
of
S_{n}
.

Next we define the
p
-quotient of
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
. Let
l=l(\lambda)
and choose
s\geq 0
such that
l+s\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. Define

\displaystyle\beta:=(\lambda_{1}-1+l+s,\,\lambda_{2}-2+l+s,\ldots,\lambda_{l}+s,\,s-1,\,s-2,\ldots,0)

(this is a
\beta
-set
for
\lambda
). For
i=0,\ldots,p-1
let
\beta_{i}:=\{x\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\colon px+i\in\beta\}
. Writing the elements of
\beta_{i}
in decreasing order
\beta_{i}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}
gives a partition
P(\beta_{i}):=(b_{1}-k+1,b_{2}-k+2,\ldots,b_{k})
where we omit parts which are zero. Finally, the
p
-tuple of partitions

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}:=\bigl(P(\beta_{0}),\ldots,P(\beta_{p-1})\bigr)

is called the
p
-quotient
of
\lambda
. It turns out that
\sum\limits_{i=0}^{p-1}|P(\beta_{i})|
equals the weight of
\lambda
. In general, the set of
p
-tuples
\bm{\lambda}=(\bm{\lambda}_{0},\ldots,\bm{\lambda}_{p-1})
of partitions such that
\sum|\bm{\lambda}_{i}|=w
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}^{p}(w)
. The elements of
\mathcal{P}^{p}(w)
will always be denoted by bold Greek letters.

Now we relate the characters of the block
B
above to the characters of the wreath product
G_{w}:=C_{p}\wr S_{w}
where
C_{p}
is a (fixed) group of order
p
. Let
\operatorname{Irr}(C_{p})=\{\psi_{0}=1,\ldots,\psi_{p-1}\}
and
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}^{p}(w)
. For
i=0,\ldots,p-1
, the linear character
\psi^{\otimes|\bm{\lambda}_{i}|}:=\psi_{i}\otimes\cdots\otimes\psi_{i}\in\operatorname{Irr}(C_{p}^{|\bm{\lambda}_{i}|})

has a unique extension to
C_{p}\wr S_{|\bm{\lambda}_{i}|}
(still denoted by
\psi^{\otimes|\bm{\lambda}_{i}|}
) which acts trivially on
S_{|\bm{\lambda}_{i}|}
. Then we can define
\psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{i}}:=\psi^{\otimes|\bm{\lambda}_{i}|}\chi_{\bm{\lambda}_{i}}\in\operatorname{Irr}(C_{p}\wr S_{|\bm{\lambda}_{i}|})
where
\chi_{\bm{\lambda}_{i}}
is the inflation from
S_{|\bm{\lambda}_{i}|}
. Finally let

\displaystyle\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}:=(\psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{p-1}})^{G_{w}}.

The degree of this character is

\displaystyle\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}(1)=\binom{w}{|\bm{\lambda}_{0}|,\ldots,|\bm{\lambda}_{p-1}|}\chi_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}(1)\cdots\chi_{\bm{\lambda}_{p-1}}(1).

(3.1)

By the hook formula and
[
6
, Lemma 2.1]
, the map
\operatorname{Irr}(B)\to\operatorname{Irr}(G_{w})
,
\chi_{\lambda}\mapsto\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}
is a height preserving bijection, i.e.,
p^{w}(w!)_{p}\chi_{\lambda}(1)_{p}=(n!)_{p}\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}(1)_{p}
for every
\chi_{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Irr}(B)
.

In order to obtain information on the decomposition matrix of
B
we need to label the conjugacy classes of
G_{w}
.
Here we identify
C_{p}
with
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
to simplify notation. For
(x_{1}\cdots x_{w},\sigma)\in G_{w}
(with
x_{1},\ldots,x_{w}\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
,
\sigma\in S_{w}
) we define
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}^{p}(w)
as follows: For every cycle
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{s})
in
\sigma
let
s\in\bm{\lambda}_{x_{a_{1}}+\dots+x_{a_{s}}}
. It turns out that two elements of
G_{w}
are conjugate if and only if they yield the same
\bm{\lambda}
. Let

\displaystyle\big\{g_{\bm{\lambda}}\colon\bm{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}^{p}(w)\big\}

be a set of representatives for the conjugacy classes of
G_{w}
. The class sizes can be computed with the formula

\displaystyle|{\operatorname{C}}_{G_{w}}(g_{\bm{\lambda}})|=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}p^{l(\bm{\lambda}_{i})}\big|{\operatorname{C}}_{S_{|\bm{\lambda}_{i}|}}(s_{\bm{\lambda}_{i}})\big|.

4
Osima’s result

Theorem 4.1

(Osima
[
8
, Theorem 8]
)
.

With the notation above let
Q
be the decomposition matrix of
B
and let
D:=\operatorname{diag}(\delta_{p}(\lambda)\colon\chi_{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Irr}(B))
. Moreover, let

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\bigl\{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}^{p}(w)\colon\bm{\lambda}_{0}=()\bigr\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad X:=\bigl(\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}(g_{\bm{\mu}})\bigr)_{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}^{p}(w),\,\bm{\mu}\in\Gamma}.

Then there exists
S\in\operatorname{GL}(l(B),\mathbb{C})

(
depending on
B)
such that

\displaystyle Q=DXS,

(4.1)

where the row of
\chi_{\lambda}
corresponds to the row of
\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}
.

We remark that (
4.1
) does not depend on the labeling of
\operatorname{IBr}(B)
, since any permutation of Brauer characters can be realized by
S
. As in the proof of Lemma
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle M:=\bigl([\chi_{\lambda},\chi_{\mu}]^{0}\bigr)_{\chi_{\lambda},\chi_{\mu}\in\operatorname{Irr}(B)}=DX\big(X^{\text{t}}X\big)^{-1}X^{\text{t}}D=D\overline{X}\big(X^{\text{t}}\overline{X}\big)^{-1}X^{\text{t}}D,

where
\overline{X}
denotes the complex conjugate of
X
. The second orthogonality relation for
G_{w}
implies that

\displaystyle X^{\text{t}}\overline{X}=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(|{\operatorname{C}}_{G_{w}}(g_{\bm{\mu}})|\colon\bm{\mu}\in\Gamma\bigr).

(4.2)

In this way,
M
can be computed from the character table of
G_{w}
and some elementary combinatorics for
D
. Doing so, we are able to distinguish the Scopes classes for
p=3
and
w\leq 15
. For
p=5
and
w\in\{4,5\}
, some of the Scopes classes yield the same
M
(see last section). Hence, we obtain only a lower bound on
M(5,w)
in these cases. For
p=2
we refine our method in the next section in order to deal with larger weights.

5
The case
\bm{p=2}
in Conjecture
1.2

For
p=2
we notice first that
X
is integral and (
4.2
) simplifies to

\displaystyle X^{\text{t}}X=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(2^{l(\mu)}|{\operatorname{C}}_{S_{w}}(s_{\mu})|\colon\mu\in\mathcal{P}(w)\bigr).

We now define a “small” submatrix
M^{0}
of
M
which suffices to distinguish the
2
-blocks with weight
w\in\{1,\ldots,28\}\cup\{32\}
. We do not know if this approach works in general for
p=2
.
Let
\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(B):=\{\chi_{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Irr}(B)\colon h(\chi_{\lambda})=0\}
. Since
M
encodes character heights, it is clear that the permutation matrix
T
in Lemma
2.1
permutes
\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(B)
(the characters of height
0
correspond to the diagonal entries of
M
with the lowest
p
-adic valuation, see remark after Lemma
2.1
). We may therefore replace
M
by
\bigl([\chi_{\lambda},\chi_{\mu}]^{0}\bigr)_{\chi_{\lambda},\chi_{\mu}\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(B)}
. Let
w=\sum\limits_{i\geq 0}a_{i}2^{i}
be the
2
-adic expansion of the weight of
B
. By
[
7
, Corollary 11.8]
we have

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(B)|=2^{\sum\limits_{i\geq 1}ia_{i-1}}.

The cores of
2
-blocks are represented by the “staircase” partitions:
(k,k-1,\ldots,1)
for
k\geq 0
. The Scopes classes are represented by those cores with
0\leq k\leq w-1
. In particular, there are just
w
Scopes classes (unless
w=0
) and these cores are self-conjugate. Hence, for
\chi_{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Irr}(B)
we have
\chi_{\lambda^{\prime}}=\operatorname{sgn}\cdot\chi_{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Irr}(B)
where
\operatorname{sgn}
is the sign character and
\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(n)
is conjugate to
\lambda
. Since all
2
-regular elements lie in the alternating group, it follows that
[\chi_{\lambda^{\prime}},\chi_{\mu}]^{0}=[\chi_{\lambda},\chi_{\mu}]^{0}
. One can show further that
\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}=((\bm{\lambda}_{1})^{\prime},(\bm{\lambda}_{0})^{\prime})
. Since
\chi_{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(B)
implies that
\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}(1)
is odd, we conclude that
|\bm{\lambda}_{0}|\neq|\bm{\lambda}_{1}|
by (
3.1
) (unless
w=0
). In particular,
\chi_{\lambda}\neq\chi_{\lambda^{\prime}}
and it is enough to consider only half of the height
0
characters. Let

\displaystyle\Delta:=\bigl\{\chi_{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(B)\colon|\bm{\lambda}_{0}|>|\bm{\lambda}_{1}|\bigr\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad M^{0}:=\bigl([\chi_{\lambda},\chi_{\mu}]^{0}\bigr)_{\chi_{\lambda},\chi_{\mu}\in\Delta}.

(5.1)

Now for Morita equivalent
2
-blocks
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
with matrices
M_{1}^{0}
and
M_{2}^{0}
as above there exists a permutation matrix
T^{0}
such that
T^{0}M_{1}^{0}=M_{2}^{0}T^{0}
(since
M
is invariant under the transpositions
(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})
). If
w\leq 28
and
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
belong to different Scopes classes, we can show by computer that
T^{0}
cannot exist. The computation for
w=29
could not be completed due to memory restrictions.

Next, we further restrict ourselves to the case where
w
is a
2
-power. Then
\chi_{\lambda}\in\Delta
satisfies
\bm{\lambda}_{1}=()
and
\bm{\lambda}_{0}
is a hook partition by (
3.1
) and the hook formula. Hence,

\displaystyle\Delta=\bigl\{\chi_{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Irr}(B)\colon\bm{\lambda}_{0}=\big(r,1^{w-r}\big),\ r=1,\ldots,w\bigr\}

and
|\Delta|=w
. The matrix
X
in Theorem
4.1
becomes

\displaystyle\bigl(\psi_{\bm{\lambda}}(g_{\bm{\mu}})\bigr)_{\chi_{\lambda}\in\Delta,\,\bm{\mu}\in\Gamma}=\bigl(\chi_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}(s_{\bm{\mu}_{1}})\bigr),

i.e.,
X
consists of rows of the character table of
S_{w}
.

Now we determine the signs
\delta_{p}(\lambda)
for
\chi_{\lambda}\in\Delta
. Let
\bm{\lambda}_{0}=\big(r,1^{w-r}\big)
, and let
\lambda_{(2)}=(k,k-1
,
\ldots,1)
be the core of
B
. If
k
is odd, then we add the partition

\displaystyle\big(2r,2^{\min\{k,w-r\}},1^{2\max\{0,w-r-k\}}\big)

to
\lambda_{(2)}
(componentwise) to obtain
\lambda
. In particular,
\delta_{p}(\lambda)=(-1)^{\max\{0,w-r-k\}}
. If, on the other hand,
k
is even, then we add

\displaystyle\big(2(w-r+1),2^{\min\{k,r-1\}},1^{2\max\{0,r-1-k\}}\big)

to
\lambda_{(2)}
and conjugate afterwards to get
\lambda
. In this case we obtain
\delta_{p}(\lambda)=(-1)^{w-r+1+\min\{k,r-1\}}
. These observations made it possible to check Conjecture
1.2
for
w=32
. The next case
w=64
is again out of reach.

In the final paragraph of this section we investigate further properties of
M
which might be of interest for a potential theoretical proof.
We consider the diagonal of
M
which is also the diagonal of

\displaystyle X\operatorname{diag}\big(2^{-l(\mu)}|{\operatorname{C}}_{S_{w}}(s_{\mu})|^{-1}\big)X^{\text{t}}.

It is easy to see that
[\chi_{\lambda},\chi_{\lambda}]^{0}=[\chi_{\mu},\chi_{\mu}]^{0}
if
\bm{\lambda}_{0}=(\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\prime}
. This means that the diagonal entries of
M
come in pairs. On the other hand, our calculations indicate that
[\chi_{\lambda},\chi_{\lambda}]^{0}>[\chi_{\mu},\chi_{\mu}]^{0}
whenever
\bm{\lambda}_{0}>\bm{\mu}_{0}>(w/2,1^{w/2})
where
>
denotes the lexicographical order. If this turns out to be true, then the permutation matrix
T
in Lemma
2.1
must be a product of disjoint transpositions. In this case it suffices to find characters
\chi_{\lambda},\chi_{\mu}\in\operatorname{Irr}(B_{1})
and
\chi_{\sigma},\chi_{\tau}\in\operatorname{Irr}(B_{2})
such that
\bm{\lambda}_{0}=\bm{\sigma}_{0}=(\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\prime}=(\bm{\tau}_{0})^{\prime}
and
\delta_{p}(\lambda)\delta_{p}(\mu)\neq\delta_{p}(\sigma)\delta_{p}(\tau)
. We do not know if this can be done in general.

6
Implementation

To check Conjecture
1.1
for small primes, we have implemented the following steps in GAP by making use of the
hecke
package:

•

determine the cores of all Scopes classes for given
p
and
w
up to conjugation of partition,

•

use the Jantzen–Schaper formula to compute the decomposition matrices of Scopes classes (this is somewhat faster than Richard’s formula),

•

compute the multisets of row sums and column sums for a given decomposition matrix,

•

partition the set of decompositions matrices according to their row sums and column sums (decomposition matrices in different parts of this partition cannot correspond to Morita equivalent blocks),

•

use the GAP command
TransformingPermutations
to distinguish decomposition matrices with the same row sums and column sums.

We have verified our code with the known decomposition matrices of
S_{n}
with
n\leq 18
. The applicability of the procedure above is only limited by the time it takes to run through all Scopes classes, but it can run in parallel. The computation of
M(11,2)=29{,}624
took about one day on an Intel Xeon E5520 processor. We could not check Conjecture
1.1
for
p=13
, since there are already
372{,}308
classes to consider (hence
\approx 10^{11}
comparisons) and the decomposition matrix has size
104\times 90
in each case. Similarly, computing
M(11,3)
is hopeless, since this number is expected to be
4{,}209{,}504
.

On the other hand, our GAP programs for Conjecture
1.2
are mainly limited by the available physical memory. Our procedure here is given as follows:

•

compute the columns of the character table of
G_{w}
corresponding to the elements
g_{\bm{\mu}}
with
\bm{\mu}\in\Gamma
using a code provided by Thomas Breuer (this uses much less memory than to compute the full character table via

•

determine the cores of the Scopes classes as above,

•

compute the
p

\delta_{p}(\lambda)
for

with respect to the Scopes class just using the definition,

•

compute
M^{0}
for each Scopes class,

•

compare the matrices
M^{0}
using

,

•

for
p=2

\chi\in\Delta
.

All computations for Conjecture
1.2
take about five days on an Intel Xeon E5520 processor with 128GB memory.

Finally, for the exceptional case

1.2

7
Concluding remarks

In the following paragraph we indicate an alternative approach to the conjectures stated in the introduction. Let
T
be the signed permutation matrix from Theorem
2.2
with
TM_{1}=M_{2}T
. Suppose that
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are Morita equivalent. Then we may assume that
M:=M_{1}=M_{2}
by Lemma
2.1
. Since
M
is real and symmetric,
M
and
T
are simultaneously diagonalizable. Since
M^{2}=M
(see (
2.1
)), all eigenvalues of
M
are
0
and
1
. However, the situation gets more interesting if we restrict to characters of a fixed height. As in Section
5
, let
p=2
and
w
be a
2
-power. Then the matrix
M^{0}
in (
5.1
) seems to have very interesting eigenvalues. For instance, the eigenvalues of
2^{12}M^{0}
for
w=8
are

\displaystyle 2^{11},

\displaystyle 2^{10},

\displaystyle 2^{9},

\displaystyle 2^{7}\cdot 3,

\displaystyle 2^{5}\cdot 3^{2},

\displaystyle 2^{4}\cdot 3\cdot 5,

\displaystyle 2^{3}\cdot 5^{2},

\displaystyle 5^{2}\cdot 7.

If we can show that the eigenvalues of
M^{0}
are pairwise distinct, then
M
and
T
can be diagonalized by the same real basis transformation. In this case
T
has real eigenvalues and we conclude that
T
is a product of disjoint transpositions (modulo signs).

For
p=3
in Conjecture
1.2
it is not always enough to consider only height
0
characters. Even worse for
p\geq 5
, Lemma
2.1
cannot tell all Scopes classes apart. For example the
5
