An Elliptic Hypergeometric Function Approach to Branching Rules This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Elliptic Integrable Systems, Special Functions and Quantum Field Theory. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/elliptic-integrable-systems.html
Chul-hee LEE †, Eric M. RAINS ‡ and S. Ole WARNAAR §

Abstract
We prove Macdonald-type deformations of a number of well-known classical branching rules by employing identities for elliptic hypergeometric integrals and series. We also propose some conjectural branching rules and allied conjectures exhibiting a novel type of vanishing behaviour involving partitions with empty 2-cores.

Preliminaries

2.1
Partitions

A partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots)
is a sequence of weakly decreasing
nonnegative integers such that
|\lambda|:=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\cdots
is finite.
If
|\lambda|=n
we say that
\lambda
is a partition of
n
, written as

\lambda\vdash n
.
The number of strictly positive
\lambda_{i}
(the parts of
\lambda
) is
called the length of the partition
\lambda
and denoted by
l(\lambda)
.
We also use
l^{\odd}(\lambda)
to denote the number of odd parts of
\lambda
.
If
l^{\odd}(\lambda)=0
we say that
\lambda
is an even partition.
The set of all partitions of length at most
n
is denoted by
P_{+}(n)
,
and typically we write
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
for partitions in
P_{+}(n)
.
The multiplicity of parts of size
i
in the partition
\lambda
is denoted by

m_{i}=m_{i}(\lambda)
.
We sometimes use the multiplicities to write a partition
\lambda
as

\big(1^{m_{1}}2^{m_{2}}\dots\big)
.
When only a single multiplicity arises, i.e., a partition is of the
form
(m^{n})
, we refer to it as a rectangle.
When
m_{i}(\lambda)\leqslant 1
for all
i
we say that
\lambda
is a distinct partition.
Given a partition
\lambda\in P_{+}(n)
such that
\lambda_{1}\leqslant m
, we write

(m^{n})-\lambda
for the complement of
\lambda
with respect to the rectangle

(m^{n})
.
That is,
(m^{n})-\lambda:=(m-\lambda_{n},\dots,m-\lambda_{1})\in P_{+}(n)
.
The partition
\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\lambda^{\prime}_{2},\dots)
such that

\lambda^{\prime}_{i}=\sum_{j\geqslant i}m_{j}(\lambda)
is called the conjugate of
\lambda
.
Perhaps more simply, if we identify a partition
\lambda
with its Young diagram
(in which the parts are represented by
l(\lambda)
left-aligned rows of
boxes or squares, with
i
th row containing
\lambda_{i}
squares)
then the parts
\lambda^{\prime}
correspond to the columns of
\lambda
.
Other special notation for partitions that we will employ is

2\lambda:=(2\lambda_{1},2\lambda_{2},\dots)
and
\lambda^{2}:=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2},\dots)
,
so that
2\lambda
(resp.
\lambda^{2}
) corresponds to the partition in which
the length of each row (resp. column) of
\lambda
has been doubled.
The partition
\mu
is contained in
\lambda
, denoted as

\mu\subset\lambda
, if
\mu_{i}\leqslant\lambda_{i}
for all
i
, i.e., if the
diagram of
\mu
fits in the diagram of
\lambda
.
We write
\mu\prec\lambda
if
\mu\subset\lambda
such that the interlacing
condition
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\mu_{1}\geqslant\lambda_{2}\geqslant\mu_{2}\geqslant\cdots
holds.
(Alternatively,
\mu\prec\lambda
if the skew shape
\lambda/\mu
is a horizontal
strip, see
[
26
]
.)
A partition
\lambda
has empty
2
-core, written as
\core(\lambda)=0
,
if its diagram can be tiled by dominoes.
For example, the partition
(5,4,4,1)
has empty
2
-core since it admits
the tiling

as well as four other such tilings.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\lambda\in P_{+}(n)
and
m
an even integer such that
m\geqslant n
.
Then
\core(\lambda)=0
if and only if the ordered set

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}:=\{\lambda_{1}+m-1,\lambda_{2}+m-2,\dots,\lambda_{n}+m-n,m-n-1,\dots,0\}

contains
m
even and
m
odd integers.

Proof.

The claim is (almost) trivially true by induction on the size of
\lambda
.
Either we cannot remove a single domino from the border of
\lambda
, in which
case
\lambda
does not have a trivial
2
-core (it is in fact a
2
-core itself)
and is of the form
(n,n-1,\dots,1)
for some positive
n
, or it is possible
to remove a domino from the border of
\lambda
to form a partition of size

|\lambda|-2
.
The removal of a domino of shape​​

simply decreases one of the elements of
\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}
by
2
,
whereas the removal of a domino of shape​​

decreases two consecutive elements of
\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}
by
1
.
The claim thus follows.
∎

Given a square
s=(i,j)\in\lambda
the
arm-length, arm-colength, leg-length and leg-colength of

s
are defined as

\displaystyle a(s)=a_{\lambda}(s):=\lambda_{i}-j,\qquad

\displaystyle a^{\prime}(s)=a^{\prime}_{\lambda}(s):=j-1,

\displaystyle l(s)=l_{\lambda}(s):=\lambda^{\prime}_{j}-i,\qquad

\displaystyle l^{\prime}(s)=l^{\prime}_{\lambda}(s):=i-1.

Extending this to type-
\mathrm{C}
, we also set

\displaystyle\hat{a}(s)=\hat{a}_{\lambda}(s):=\lambda_{i}+j-1,\qquad\hat{l}(s)=\hat{l}_{\lambda}(s):=\lambda^{\prime}_{j}+i-1.

The rationale for denoting the set of partitions of length at
most
n
as
P_{+}(n)
is that we identify such partitions with
the dominant (integral) weights of
\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
.
Frequently we also require the superset

\displaystyle P(n):=\big\{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\colon\lambda_{1}\geqslant\lambda_{2}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\lambda_{n}\big\}

of all (integral) weights.
By mild abuse of notation, we sometimes write for
\mu\in P(n+1)
with

\mu_{n+1}=0
that
\mu\in P_{+}(n)
, i.e., we consider
P_{+}(n)
not just
as a subset of
P(n)
but also of
P(n+1)
.

For
\lambda
a partition, define the statistic

\displaystyle n(\lambda):=\sum_{s=(i,j)\in\lambda}(i-1)=\sum_{i\geqslant 1}(i-1)\lambda_{i}=\sum_{s=(i,j)\in\lambda}(\lambda^{\prime}_{j}-1)/2=\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\binom{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}}{2}.

This extends to skew shapes
\lambda/\mu
in the obvious manner:

n(\lambda/\mu):=\sum_{i}(i-1)(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i})=n(\lambda)-n(\mu)
.
By further abuse of notation (since conjugation no longer makes sense)
we will also use
n(\lambda)
and
n(\lambda^{\prime})
for
\lambda\in P(n)
,
defined as

\displaystyle n(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(i-1)\lambda_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad n(\lambda^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\binom{\lambda_{i}}{2}.

Two describe some of our conjectures we also require three types of
even and odd analogues of
n(\lambda)
for
\lambda
a partition:

\displaystyle n^{\textrm{e/o}}(\lambda):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s=(i,j)\in\lambda\\
a(s)+l(s)\textrm{ even/odd}\end{subarray}}(i-1)

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\hat{n}^{\textrm{e/o}}(\lambda):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s=(i,j)\in\lambda\\
a(s)+l(s)\textrm{ even/odd}\end{subarray}}(\lambda^{\prime}_{j}-1)/2

(2.1b)

and

\displaystyle\bar{n}^{\textrm{e/o}}(\lambda):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s=(i,j)\in\lambda\\
i+j\textrm{ even/odd}\end{subarray}}(i-1).

(2.1c)

Although

\displaystyle n(\lambda)=n^{\textrm{e}}(\lambda)+n^{\textrm{o}}(\lambda)=\hat{n}^{\textrm{e}}(\lambda)+\hat{n}^{\textrm{o}}(\lambda)=\bar{n}^{\textrm{e}}(\lambda)+\bar{n}^{\textrm{o}}(\lambda),

it is generally not true that
n^{\textrm{e/o}}(\lambda)
,

\hat{n}^{\textrm{e/o}}(\lambda)
and

\bar{n}^{\textrm{e/o}}(\lambda)
(for fixed parity) coincide.
In fact,
\hat{n}^{\textrm{e/o}}(\lambda)
can take
half-integer values.

We will in fact only use the above six functions for partitions
that have empty
2
-core.
In that case, we have the following simple relation.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
\lambda
a partition such that
\core(\lambda)=0
,

\displaystyle\bar{n}^{\textup{e/o}}(\lambda)=2\hat{n}^{\textup{o/e}}(\lambda)-n^{\textup{e/o}}(\lambda).

Proof.

We again prove the claim by induction on the size of the partition
\lambda
.
For
\lambda=0
the claim is trivially true.
Now let
\lambda
be a partition of size at least two.
Because
\lambda
can be tiled by dominoes, it is always possible to
remove a domino from its border to form a partition
\mu
of size

|\lambda|-2
.

First assume it is possible to remove a domino of shape​​

such that the row-coordinate of the two boxes of the domino is
i
.
Then

\displaystyle n^{\textup{e/o}}(\lambda)-n^{\textup{e/o}}(\mu)=\bar{n}^{\textup{e/o}}(\lambda)-\bar{n}^{\textup{e/o}}(\mu)=2\hat{n}^{\textup{e/o}}(\lambda)-2\hat{n}^{\textup{e/o}}(\mu)=i-1.

The above gives

\displaystyle n^{p_{1}}(\lambda)+\bar{n}^{p_{2}}(\lambda)-2\hat{n}^{p_{3}}(\lambda)=n^{p_{1}}(\mu)+\bar{n}^{p_{2}}(\mu)-2\hat{n}^{p_{3}}(\mu)

(2.2)

irrespective of the choice of the parities
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
and
p_{3}
.
(This is consistent with the trivial fact that for
\lambda
an
even partition,
n^{p_{1}}(\lambda)=\bar{n}^{p_{2}}(\lambda)=\hat{n}^{p_{3}}(\lambda)
.)

Next assume that
\lambda^{\prime}
is a distinct partition so that it is impossible
to remove a domino of shape​​

.
We now only need to consider the first column of
\lambda
from which
a domino of shape​​

can be removed.
If this is the
j
th column, then
\lambda^{\prime}
is a distinct partition
such that
\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-\lambda^{\prime}_{i+1}=1
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant j-1
and

\lambda^{\prime}_{j}-\lambda^{\prime}_{j+1}\geqslant 2
. (Of course, not each such a partition
necessarily has an empty
2
-core.)
From a case-by-case analysis it follows that

\displaystyle n^{\textup{e}}(\lambda)-n^{\textup{e}}(\mu)=\lambda^{\prime}_{1}-1,\qquad n^{\textup{o}}(\lambda)-n^{\textup{o}}(\mu)=\lambda^{\prime}_{1}-2j,

\displaystyle\bar{n}^{\textup{e}}(\lambda)-\bar{n}^{\textup{e}}(\mu)=\begin{cases}\lambda^{\prime}_{1}-j&\text{if $\lambda^{\prime}_{1}$ is odd},\\
\lambda^{\prime}_{1}-j-1&\text{if $\lambda^{\prime}_{1}$ is even},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\bar{n}^{\textup{o}}(\lambda)-\bar{n}^{\textup{o}}(\mu)=\begin{cases}\lambda^{\prime}_{1}-j&\text{if $\lambda^{\prime}_{1}$ is even},\\
\lambda^{\prime}_{1}-j-1&\text{if $\lambda^{\prime}_{1}$ is odd},\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle 2\hat{n}^{\textup{e}}(\lambda)-2\hat{n}^{\textup{e}}(\mu)=\begin{cases}2\lambda^{\prime}_{1}-3j&\text{if $\lambda^{\prime}_{1}$ is even},\\
2\lambda^{\prime}_{1}-3j-1&\text{if $\lambda^{\prime}_{1}$ is odd},\end{cases}

\displaystyle 2\hat{n}^{\textup{o}}(\lambda)-2\hat{n}^{\textup{o}}(\mu)=\begin{cases}2\lambda^{\prime}_{1}-j-1&\text{if $\lambda^{\prime}_{1}$ is odd},\\
2\lambda^{\prime}_{1}-j-2&\text{if $\lambda^{\prime}_{1}$ is even}.\end{cases}

Hence (
2.2
) again holds, but now with
p_{1}=p_{2}\neq p_{3}
.
∎

2.2
Generalised shifted factorials

In this paper we require several types of shifted factorials.
For complex
q
such that
|q|<1
the ordinary
q
-shifted
factorial
(z;q)_{\infty}
is defined as

\displaystyle(z;q)_{\infty}:=\prod_{k\geqslant 1}\big(1-zq^{k-1}\big).

(2.3)

This may be used to define
(z;q)_{N}
for arbitrary
integer
N
as

\displaystyle(z;q)_{N}:=\frac{(z;q)_{\infty}}{(zq^{N};q)_{\infty}}.

(2.4)

In particular, if
N
is nonnegative,
(z;q)_{N}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\big(1-zq^{k-1}\big)

and if
N
is a negative integer
1/(q;q)_{N}=0
.
To generalise both definitions to the elliptic case, we need the
elliptic gamma function
[
46
]

\displaystyle\Gamma_{p,q}(z):=\prod_{i,j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1-p^{i+1}q^{j+1}/z}{1-zp^{i}q^{j}},

where
z\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
and
p,q\in\mathbb{C}

such that
|p|,|q|<1
.
This function is symmetric in
p
and
q
, satisfies the reflection formula

\Gamma_{p,q}(z)\Gamma_{p,q}(pq/z)=1
and functional equation

\displaystyle\Gamma_{p,q}(qz)=\theta(z;p)\Gamma_{p,q}(z),

(2.5)

where
\theta(z;p)
is the modified theta function

\displaystyle\theta(z;p):=(z;p)_{\infty}(p/z;p)_{\infty}.

Since
\lim\limits_{p\to 0}1/\Gamma_{p,q}(z)=(z;q)_{\infty}
, the reciprocal
of the elliptic gamma function can be viewed as an elliptic analogue of (
2.3
).
The elliptic analogue of (
2.4
) is then

\displaystyle(z;q,p)_{N}:=\frac{\Gamma_{p,q}\big(zq^{N}\big)}{\Gamma_{p,q}(z)},

(2.6)

which for nonnegative
N
can also be expressed as

\displaystyle(z;q,p)_{N}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\theta\big(zq^{k-1};p\big).

Clearly,
(z;q,0)_{N}=(z;q)_{N}
.

Three important generalisations of
(z;q,p)_{N}
to the case of
partitions are given by
[
36
,
52
]

\displaystyle C^{0}_{\lambda}(z;q,t;p):=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}\theta\big(zq^{j-1}t^{1-i};p\big),

(2.7a)

\displaystyle C^{-}_{\lambda}(z;q,t;p):=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}\theta\big(zq^{\lambda_{i}-j}t^{\lambda^{\prime}_{j}-i};p\big),

(2.7b)

\displaystyle C^{+}_{\lambda}(z;q,t;p):=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}\theta\big(zq^{\lambda_{i}+j-1}t^{2-\lambda^{\prime}_{j}-i};p\big),

(2.7c)

where it is noted that

\displaystyle C^{0}_{(N)}(z;q,t;p)=C^{-}_{(N)}(z;q,t;p)=(z;q,p)_{N}

and

\displaystyle C^{+}_{(N)}(z;q,t;p)=\frac{(z;q,p)_{2N}}{(z;q,p)_{N}}.

From the simple functional equations for the theta function

\displaystyle\theta(pz;p)=\theta(1/z;p)=-z^{-1}\theta(z;p),

(2.8)

it follows that the elliptic
C
-symbols satisfy the
quasi-periodicities

\displaystyle C^{0}_{\lambda}(pz;q,t;p)=(-z)^{-|\lambda|}q^{-n(\lambda^{\prime})}t^{n(\lambda)}C^{0}_{\lambda}(z;q,t;p),

(2.9a)

\displaystyle C^{-}_{\lambda}(pz;q,t;p)=(-z)^{-|\lambda|}q^{-n(\lambda^{\prime})}t^{-n(\lambda)}C^{-}_{\lambda}(z;q,t;p),

(2.9b)

\displaystyle C^{+}_{\lambda}(pz;q,t;p)=(-zq)^{-|\lambda|}q^{-3n(\lambda^{\prime})}t^{3n(\lambda)}C^{+}_{\lambda}(z;q,t;p),

(2.9c)

as well as a long list of other simple identities, such as

\displaystyle C^{0}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(z;t,q;p)=C^{0}_{\lambda}(p/z;q,t;p),

(2.10a)

\displaystyle C^{-}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(z;t,q;p)=C^{-}_{\lambda}(z;q,t;p),

(2.10b)

\displaystyle C^{+}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(z;t,q;p)=C^{+}_{\lambda}(p/zqt;q,t;p),

(2.10c)

\displaystyle C^{0,\pm}_{\lambda}(1/z;1/q,1/t;p)=C^{0,\pm}_{\lambda}(pz;q,t;p),

(2.11)

\displaystyle C^{0,\pm}_{2\lambda}(z;q,t;p)=C^{0,\pm}_{\lambda}\big(z,zq;q^{2},t;p\big),

(2.12a)

\displaystyle C^{0}_{\lambda^{2}}(z;q,t;p)=C^{0}_{\lambda}\big(z,z/t;q,t^{2};p\big),

(2.12b)

\displaystyle C^{-}_{\lambda^{2}}(z;q,t;p)=C^{-}_{\lambda}\big(z,zt;q,t^{2};p\big),

(2.12c)

\displaystyle C^{+}_{\lambda^{2}}(z;q,t;p)=C^{+}_{\lambda}\big(z/t,z/t^{2};q,t^{2};p\big),

(2.12d)

From (

it follows that the rational functions defined in

\displaystyle c_{\lambda^{2}}(w,z;q,t)=c_{\lambda^{2}}(1/w,1/z;1/q,1/t)=\left(\frac{q}{t}\right)^{|\lambda|}\frac{C^{-}_{\lambda^{2}}(t;q,t)}{C_{\lambda^{2}}^{-}(q;q,t)}d_{2\lambda^{\prime}}(1/w,1/z;t,q),

In Sections

2.3
Elliptic hypergeometric series

Our proof of Theorem
1.2
relies on
(the
p\to 0
limit of) two higher-dimensional quadratic
summation formulas for elliptic hypergeometric series.
In the one-dimensional case the simplest form an elliptic
hypergeometric series can take is
[
13
,
50
,
52
]

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Delta_{(k)}(a|t,b_{1},\dots,b_{r};q,t;p)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(apq;q,p)_{2k}}{(a;q,p)_{2k}}\frac{(a,b_{1},\dots,b_{r};q,p)_{k}}{(pq,c_{1},\dots,c_{r};q,p)_{k}},

(2.17)

where, for reasons of convergence, it is assumed that one of the
b_{i}
is of the form
q^{-N}
with
N
a nonnegative integer
so that the series terminates.
If the upper and lower parameters satisfy

ab_{1}\cdots b_{r}(pq)^{3}=c_{1}\cdots c_{r}
the series (
2.17
)
is said to be balanced, and if
b_{i}c_{i}=apq
for all
i
it is said
to be very-well poised.
If both these conditions are satisfied then (
2.17
) is an
elliptic function (in multiplicative form) in each of the variables

a,b_{1},\dots,b_{r}
, see, e.g.,
[
50
]
.

The most important identity for one-dimensional elliptic hypergeometric
series corresponds to (
2.17
) for
r=5
, and is given by Frenkel
and Turaev’s elliptic analogue of Jackson’s sum
[
12
]
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(apq;q,p)_{2k}}{(a;q,p)_{2k}}\frac{(a,b,c,d,e,q^{-N};q,p)_{k}}{(pq,apq/b,apq/c,apq/d,apq/e,apq^{N+1};q,p)_{k}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{(apq,apq/bc,apq/bd,apq/cd;q,p)_{N}}{(apq/b,apq/c,apq/d,apq/bcd;q,p)_{N}},

(2.18)

where
bcdeq^{-N}=a^{2}pq
.
Four other balanced, very-well poised instances of (
2.17
)
for
r=7
that admit closed-form evaluations are given by

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(apq;q,p)_{2k}}{(a;q,p)_{2k}}\frac{\big(a,bq,abq^{N},q^{-N};q,p\big)_{k}}{\big(pq,ap/b,pq^{1-N}/b,apq^{N+1};q,p\big)_{k}}\frac{\big(ap/b;q,p^{2}\big)_{2k}}{\big(abpq;q,p^{2}\big)_{2k}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{\theta\big(abp;p^{2}\big)}{\theta\big(abpq^{2N};p^{2}\big)}\frac{(apq;q,p)_{N}}{(b;q,p)_{N}}\frac{\big(pq,b^{2}q;q,p^{2}\big)_{N}}{\big(abp,ap^{2}/b;q,p^{2}\big)_{N}}

(2.19a)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(apq;q,p)_{2k}}{(a;q,p)_{2k}}\frac{\big(a,bp,abq^{N},q^{-N};q,p\big)_{k}}{\big(pq,aq/b,pq^{1-N}/b,apq^{N+1};q,p\big)_{k}}\frac{\big(aq/b;q^{2},p\big)_{k}}{\big(abpq;q^{2},p\big)_{k}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\chi(N\text{ even})\frac{\big(q,b^{2};q^{2},p\big)_{N/2}}{\big(abpq,apq^{2}/b;q^{2},p\big)_{N/2}}\frac{(apq;q,p)_{N}}{(b;q,p)_{N}},

(2.19b)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(apq;q,p)_{2k}}{(a;q,p)_{2k}}\frac{\big(b,pq/b,q^{N+1},q^{-N};q,p\big)_{k}}{\big(apq/b,ab,apq^{-N},apq^{N+1};q,p\big)_{k}}\frac{(a^{2};q^{2},p)_{k}}{(pq^{2};q^{2},p)_{k}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{(apq,b/a;q,p)_{N}}{(q/a,abp;q,p)_{N}}\frac{\big(abpq^{-N};q^{2},p\big)_{N}}{\big(bq^{-N}/a;q^{2},p\big)_{N}}

(2.19c)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\big(a^{2}p^{2}q^{2};q^{2},p^{2}\big)_{2k}}{\big(a^{2};q^{2},p^{2}\big)_{2k}}\frac{\big(a^{2},b^{2}p^{2}q^{2},a^{2}b^{2}q^{2N},q^{-2N};q^{2},p^{2}\big)_{k}}{\big(p^{2}q^{2},a^{2}/b^{2},p^{2}q^{2-2N}/b^{2},a^{2}p^{2}q^{2N+2};q^{2},p^{2}\big)_{k}}\frac{(a/b;q,p)_{2k}}{(abpq;q,p)_{2k}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{(ab;q,p)_{2N}}{(abpq;q,p)_{2N}}\frac{\big({-}pq,b^{2}pq;q,p\big)_{N}}{(ab,-a/b;q,p)_{N}}\frac{\big(a^{2}p^{2}q^{2};q^{2},p^{2}\big)_{N}}{\big(b^{2};q^{2},p^{2}\big)_{N}}.

(2.19d)

The last three identities are equations
(1.10), (1.15) and (1.4) of
[
54
]
respectively.
The identity (
2.19a
) does not appear to have been stated before.
It follows by inverting, using
[
53
, Lemma 3.1]
, the elliptic Jackson
sum (
2.18
) with

\displaystyle(b,c,d,e,p)\mapsto\big(b^{2}q,aq^{N}/b,apq^{N}/b,pq^{-N},p^{2}\big).

Because of the occurrence of base
q
and
q^{2}
(and nomes
p
and
p^{2}
),
the above identities are commonly referred to as quadratic summation
formulas.
In Section
7
we obtain higher-dimensional analogues of
(
2.19a
), (
2.19b
) and (

).
Two of these play a key role in our proof of Theorem

.
We remark that higher-dimensional analogues of a different type of quadratic

3
Schur functions and classical branching rules

In this section we briefly review the definitions of the Schur functions
of classical type as well as their occurrence in some of the branching rules
stated in Section
1
.
For a more in-depth treatment we refer the reader to

[
19
,
21
,
25
,
26
,
31
,
42
]
.

Let
\Lambda_{n}:=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]^{S_{n}}

denote the ring of symmetric functions in
n
variables, and

\Lambda
the ring of symmetric functions in countably many
variables, see
[
26
,
51
]
.
The monomial symmetric functions
\{m_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in P_{+}(n)}

and
\{m_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
, where

\displaystyle m_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{w\in S_{n}/S_{n}^{\lambda}}w\big(x^{\lambda}\big),\qquad\lambda\in P_{+}(n),

form
\mathbb{Z}
-bases of
\Lambda_{n}
and
\Lambda
respectively.
The elementary, complete and power-sum symmetric functions
e_{r}
,
h_{r}

and
p_{r}
are defined in terms of the monomial symmetric functions
as

\displaystyle e_{r}:=m_{(1^{r})}=\sum_{1<i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{r}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{r}},

\displaystyle h_{r}:=\sum_{\lambda\vdash r}m_{\lambda}=\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1}\leqslant i_{2}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant i_{r}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{r}},

\displaystyle p_{r}:=m_{(r)}=\sum_{i\geqslant 1}x_{i}^{r}.

These functions form algebraic bases of either
\Lambda
(in the case of the

e_{r}
and
h_{r}
) or of

\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}:=\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
(in the case
of the power sums).

A number of classical branching rules for universal characters
discussed below are related by the involution
\omega
on
\Lambda

defined by
\omega(h_{r})=e_{r}
or
\omega(p_{r})=(-1)^{r-1}p_{r}
for all
r\geqslant 1
.

The ordinary (or
\mathrm{GL}(n)
) Schur function indexed by the partition

\lambda
is defined as

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=\frac{\det_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\big(x_{i}^{\lambda_{j}+n-j}\big)}{\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}(x_{i}-x_{j})}

(3.1)

if
l(\lambda)\leqslant n
and
0
otherwise.
To simultaneously extend this to
\Lambda
as well as skew shapes,
we use the Jacobi–Trudi identity or its dual

[
26
, pp. 70–71]
:

\displaystyle s_{\lambda/\mu}:=\det_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}(h_{\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j}-i+j})=\det_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant m}(e_{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-\mu^{\prime}_{j}-i+j}),

(3.2)

where
n
and
m
are arbitrary integers such that

n\geqslant l(\lambda)
and
m\geqslant\lambda_{1}
.
Obviously,
\omega(s_{\lambda/\mu})=s_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}
.
The Littlewood–Richardson coefficients
c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}

may now be defined by

\displaystyle s_{\lambda/\mu}=\sum_{\nu}c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}s_{\nu}.

From (
3.2
) it follows that

s_{\lambda/\mu}=s_{((m^{n})-\mu)/((m^{n})-\lambda)}

for
\lambda\subset(m^{n})
so that the Littlewood–Richardson
coefficients satisfy the complementation symmetry

\displaystyle c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}=c_{(m^{n})-\lambda,\nu}^{(m^{n})-\mu}\qquad\text{for $\lambda\subset(m^{n})$}.

(3.3)

For
\lambda
a partition, the universal orthogonal and
symplectic characters indexed by
\lambda
are given by

[
19
, Definition 2.1.1]

\displaystyle\ortho_{\lambda}:=\det_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}(h_{\lambda_{i}-i+j}-h_{\lambda_{i}-i-j})

(3.4)

\displaystyle\symp_{\lambda}:=\det_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant m}(e_{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-i+j}-e_{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-i-j}),

(3.5)

with
n
and
m
as above.
Hence
\omega(\ortho_{\lambda})=\symp_{\lambda^{\prime}}
.
In particular,

\displaystyle g_{\lambda}\big(x_{1}^{\pm},\dots,x_{n}^{\pm},0,\dots\big)=\begin{cases}g_{2n,\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})&\text{if $\lambda\in P_{+}(n)$},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(3.6)

where
g=\symp
(resp.
g=\ortho
) corresponds to an
orthogonal or symplectic character indexed by
\lambda
.
We add to the above the universal special orthogonal character
indexed by
\lambda
as

\displaystyle\so_{\lambda}:=\det_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}(h_{\lambda_{i}-i+j}+h_{\lambda_{i}-i-j+1})=\det_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant m}(e_{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-i+j}+e_{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-i-j+1}),

(3.7)

so that
\omega(\so_{\lambda})=\so_{\lambda^{\prime}}
.
The character
\so_{\lambda}
is the unique symmetric function such that

\displaystyle\so_{\lambda}\big(x_{1}^{\pm},\dots,x_{n}^{\pm},0,\dots\big)=\begin{cases}\so_{2n+1,\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})&\text{if $\lambda\in P_{+}(n)$},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(3.8)

where
\so_{2n+1,\lambda}
is the odd-orthogonal Schur function
indexed by
\lambda
.
The character
\so_{\lambda}
may readily be related to the
universal symplectic and orthogonal characters as

\displaystyle\so_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu^{\prime}\prec\lambda^{\prime}}\symp_{\mu}=\sum_{\mu\prec\lambda}\ortho_{\mu}

(3.9a)

and

\displaystyle\symp_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu\prec\lambda}(-1)^{|\lambda/\mu|}\so_{\mu},\qquad\ortho_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu^{\prime}\prec\lambda^{\prime}}(-1)^{|\lambda/\mu|}\so_{\mu}.

(3.9b)

For the actual symplectic, orthogonal and odd-orthogonal Schur
functions we have
[
25
]

\displaystyle\symp_{2n,\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\det_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\big(x_{i}^{\lambda_{j}+2n-j+1}-x_{i}^{-\lambda_{j}+j-1}\big)}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}^{2}-1)\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}(x_{i}-x_{j})(x_{i}x_{j}-1)},

\displaystyle\ortho_{2n,\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=f_{l(\lambda),n}\frac{\det_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\big(x_{i}^{\lambda_{j}+2n-j-1}+x_{i}^{-\lambda_{j}+j-1}\big)}{\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}(x_{i}-x_{j})(x_{i}x_{j}-1)},

where
f_{n,n}=1
and
f_{i,n}=1/2
if
i<n
, and

\displaystyle\so_{2n+1,\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\det_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}\big(x_{i}^{\lambda_{j}+2n-j}-x_{i}^{-\lambda_{j}+j-1}\big)}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-1)\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}(x_{i}-x_{j})(x_{i}x_{j}-1)}.

(3.10)

Littlewood
[
25
]
and Koike and Terada
[
19
]
proved some very general branching
formulas for the classical groups.
For example, in the universal case
[
19
, Theorem 2.3.1]
,

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu}\bigg(\sum_{\nu\text{ even}}c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}\bigg)\ortho_{\mu}=\sum_{\mu}\bigg(\sum_{\nu^{\prime}\text{ even}}c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}\bigg)\symp_{\mu}.

(3.11)

By (
3.3
) it is not hard to show that
(
1.4
) and (
1.10
) follow from
(
3.11
).
Indeed,

\displaystyle s_{(m^{r-1},m-p)}=\sum_{\mu}\sum_{\nu\text{ even}}c_{\mu\nu}^{(m^{r-1},m-p)}\ortho_{\mu}=\sum_{\mu\subset(m^{r})}\sum_{\nu\text{ even}}c_{(1^{p}),\nu}^{\mu}\ortho_{(m^{r})-\mu}.

By the
e
-Pieri rule
[
26
, p. 73]
,

\displaystyle c_{(1^{p}),\nu}^{\mu}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\nu^{\prime}\prec\mu^{\prime}$ and $|\mu/\nu|=p$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Hence

\displaystyle s_{(m^{r-1},m-p)}=\sum_{\mu\subset(m^{r})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu^{\prime}\prec\mu^{\prime}\\
\nu\text{ even}\\
|\nu|=|\mu|-p\end{subarray}}\ortho_{(m^{r})-\mu}.

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu^{\prime}\prec\mu^{\prime}\\
\nu\text{ even}\\
|\nu|=|\mu|-p\end{subarray}}1=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $l^{\odd}(\mu)=p$},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

the branching rule (
1.4
) follows.

We also remark that the three universal branching rules
(
1.4
), (
1.10
) and (
1.11
)
are not independent.
Obviously, (
1.10
) follows from
(
1.4
) by application of
\omega
and vice versa.
Also, the rectangular (i.e.,
p=0
) cases of each of the
branching rules are related via (
3.9
).
For example,
from (
1.11
) and (
3.9a
), and the fact
that
(m^{r})-\lambda\prec(m^{r})-\mu
(for
\lambda,\mu\subset(m^{r})
)
implies
\mu\succ\lambda
, it follows that

\displaystyle s_{(m^{r})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\subset(m^{r})}(-1)^{|\lambda|}\so_{(m^{r})-\lambda}=\sum_{\lambda\subset(m^{r})}(-1)^{|\lambda|}\sum_{\mu\succ\lambda}\ortho_{(m^{r})-\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\subset(m^{r})}\sum_{\lambda\prec\mu}(-1)^{|\lambda|}\ortho_{(m^{r})-\mu}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\subset(m^{r})\\[1.0pt]
\mu\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\ortho_{(m^{r})-\mu},

where in the last step we have used

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\prec\mu}(-1)^{|\lambda|}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\mu$ is even},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Conversely, from (
1.10
) and (
3.9b
),

\displaystyle s_{(m^{r})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\subset(m^{r})\\[1.0pt]
\lambda\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\ortho_{(m^{r})-\lambda}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\subset(m^{r})\\[1.0pt]
\lambda\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mu^{\prime}\succ\lambda^{\prime}}(-1)^{|\mu/\lambda|}\so_{(m^{r})-\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\subset(m^{r})}(-1)^{|\mu|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda^{\prime}\prec\mu^{\prime}\\[1.0pt]
\lambda\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\so_{(m^{r})-\mu}=\sum_{\mu\subset(m^{r})}(-1)^{|\mu|}\so_{(m^{r})-\mu},

since there is a unique even partition
\lambda
such that

\lambda^{\prime}\prec\mu^{\prime}
.

In the
q,t
-case, we neither have analogues of
(

) nor of (

),
making the proof of Theorem

much harder
than in the classical case.
As we shall see, however, (

) and (

)
are related by the

.

In this section we survey some necessary background material

4.1
Macdonald polynomials

Let
0<|q|,|t|<1
.
For
f
an
S_{n}
-symmetric function (not necessarily an
S_{n}
-symmetric
Laurent polynomial) we define

\displaystyle\big\langle f\big\rangle_{q,t}^{(n)}:=\frac{1}{S_{n}(q,t)}\frac{1}{n!(2\pi\hskip 0.5pt\mathrm{i})^{n}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f(z)\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}\frac{(z_{i}/z_{j},z_{j}/z_{i};q)_{\infty}}{(tz_{i}/z_{j},tz_{j}/z_{i};q)_{\infty}}\frac{\dup z_{1}}{z_{1}}\cdots\frac{\dup z_{n}}{z_{n}},

(4.1)

where
\mathbb{T}
is the positively-oriented unit circle
and
[
26
]

\displaystyle S_{n}(q,t):=\frac{1}{n!(2\pi\hskip 0.5pt\mathrm{i})^{n}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}\frac{(z_{i}/z_{j},z_{j}/z_{i};q)_{\infty}}{(tz_{i}/z_{j},tz_{j}/z_{i};q)_{\infty}}\frac{\dup z_{1}}{z_{1}}\cdots\frac{\dup z_{n}}{z_{n}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\big(t,qt^{i-1};q\big)_{\infty}}{\big(q,t^{i};q\big)_{\infty}}.

(4.2)

Recall the dominance (partial) order on partitions: for

\lambda,\mu\vdash m
,
\lambda\geqslant\mu
if

\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{i}\geqslant\mu_{1}+\dots+\mu_{i}
for all
i\geqslant 1
,
and
\lambda>\mu
if
\lambda\geqslant\mu
and
\lambda\neq\mu
.
Also let
x^{-1}:=\big(x_{1}^{-1},\dots,x_{n}^{-1}\big)
.
Then Macdonald polynomials
P_{\lambda}=P_{\lambda}(q,t)=P_{\lambda}(x;q,t)
for

\lambda\in P_{+}(n)
are the unique homogeneous symmetric functions
in

\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}(q,t)}:=\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}(q,t)=\mathbb{Q}(q,t)[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]^{S_{n}}
of the form

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}=m_{\lambda}+\sum_{\mu<\lambda}c_{\lambda\mu}(q,t)m_{\mu}

such that (for
0<|q|,|t|<1
)

\displaystyle\big\langle P_{\lambda}(x;q,t)P_{\mu}\big(x^{-1};q,t\big)\big\rangle_{q,t}^{(n)}=0

(4.3)

if
\lambda\neq\mu
, see
[
26
, pp. 368–376]
.
For
\lambda=\mu
,

\displaystyle\big\langle P_{\lambda}(x;q,t)P_{\lambda}\big(x^{-1};q,t\big)\big\rangle_{q,t}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}\frac{\big(t^{j-i+1},qt^{j-i-1};q\big)_{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}}{\big(t^{j-i},qt^{j-i};q\big)_{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{0}_{\lambda}\big(t^{n};q,t\big)C^{-}_{\lambda}(q;q,t)}{C^{0}_{\lambda}\big(qt^{n-1};q,t\big)C^{-}_{\lambda}(t;q,t)}.

(4.4)

The Macdonald polynomials satisfy the symmetry

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}(x;q,t)=P_{\lambda}(x;1/q,1/t)

(4.5)

and (dual) Cauchy identity

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\subset(m^{n})}P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};q,t)P_{\lambda^{\prime}}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m};t,q)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1+x_{i}y_{j}).

(4.6)

Since

\displaystyle P_{(\lambda_{1}+1,\dots,\lambda_{n}+1)}(x;q,t)=(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})P_{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})}(x;q,t),

they can be extended from

Let

\displaystyle m^{W}_{\lambda}=m^{W}_{\lambda}(x):=\sum_{w\in W/W^{\lambda}}w\big(x^{\lambda}\big).

Any non-constant

-symmetric polynomial is necessarily

It will thus be convenient to extend the dominance order from partitions

5.1

Let

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}=\kappa_{n}(p,q):=\frac{(p;p)_{\infty}^{n}(q;q)_{\infty}^{n}}{2^{n}n!(2\pi\hskip 0.5pt\mathrm{i})^{n}}.

[

,

,

]

\displaystyle\int\limits_{C}\Delta_{\mathrm{D}}(z;t_{0},\dots,t_{2n+3};p,q)\frac{\dup z_{1}}{z_{1}}\cdots\frac{\dup z_{n}}{z_{n}}=\prod_{0\leqslant r<s\leqslant 2n+3}\Gamma_{p,q}(t_{r}t_{s})

\displaystyle\hphantom{:}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(\Gamma_{p,q}\big(t^{i}\big)\prod_{0\leqslant r<s\leqslant 5}\Gamma_{p,q}\big(t^{i-1}t_{r}t_{s}\big)\bigg),

5.2

Let

Recall that the Macdonald polynomials

If

\lambda\in P(n)

By (

) it then follows that

\displaystyle\big[P_{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})}(q,t)\big]g=\big[P_{(-\lambda_{n},\dots,-\lambda_{1})}(q,t)\big]g,

(6.1)

so that it suffices to consider
\big[P_{\lambda}(q,t)\big]g

for
\lambda\in P(n)
such that
\lambda_{1}\geqslant 0

We are concerned with computing the transition coefficients

\displaystyle\mathpzc{c}_{\lambda\mu}^{(n)}(q,t,s):=\big[P_{\lambda}(q,t)\big]\bar{P}^{\ast}_{\mu}(q,t,s)\in\mathbb{Q}(q,t)\big[s,s^{-1}\big]

for

Apart from

it follows from (

\displaystyle\mathpzc{c}_{\lambda\mu}^{(n)}(q,t,s)=\mathpzc{c}_{\lambda\mu}^{(n)}(1/q,1/t,1/s)=(-1)^{|\lambda|-|\mu|}\mathpzc{c}_{\lambda\mu}^{(n)}(q,t,-s).

.
To this end we define

\displaystyle\mathpzc{C}_{\lambda\mu}^{(n)}(q,t,s):=(-st^{n-1})^{|\mu|-|\lambda|}q^{n(\mu^{\prime})-n(\lambda^{\prime})}t^{2n(\lambda)-2n(\mu)}\mathpzc{c}_{\lambda\mu}^{(n)}(q,t,s)\in\mathbb{Q}(q,t)\big[s^{2}\big],

With respect to this duality both vanishing integrals and
bounded Littlewood identities are self-dual.

Finally, define

\displaystyle\kappa^{(1)}_{\lambda}(q,t):=\lim_{z\to\infty}\kappa^{(1)}_{\lambda}(z;q,t)=q^{2\hat{n}^{\text{o}}(\lambda^{\prime})-2\hat{n}^{\text{e}}(\lambda^{\prime})}t^{n^{\text{e}}(\lambda)-n^{\text{o}}(\lambda)}\frac{C^{-,\text{e}}_{\lambda}(t;q,t)}{C^{-,\text{o}}_{\lambda}(q;q,t)},

\displaystyle\kappa^{(2)}_{\lambda}(q,t):=\lim_{z\to\infty}\kappa^{(2)}_{\lambda}(z;q,t)=q^{n^{\text{e}}(\lambda^{\prime})-n^{\text{o}}(\lambda^{\prime})}t^{2\hat{n}^{\text{o}}(\lambda)-2\hat{n}^{\text{e}}(\lambda)}\frac{C^{-,\text{e}}_{\lambda}(t;q,t)}{C^{-,\text{o}}_{\lambda}(q;q,t)}.

In the large-
m,n
limit, Conjecture
9.4
then simplifies to the following pair of unbounded Littlewood identities.

Conjecture 9.5

(Littlewood identities)
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}\kappa^{(1)}_{\lambda}(q,t)P_{\lambda}(x;q,t)=\prod_{i\geqslant 1}\frac{\big(tx_{i}^{2};q^{2}\big)_{\infty}}{\big(x_{i}^{2};q^{2}\big)_{\infty}}\prod_{i<j}\frac{(tx_{i}x_{j};q)_{\infty}}{(x_{i}x_{j};q)_{\infty}}

and

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}\kappa^{(2)}_{\lambda}(q,t)P_{\lambda}(x;q,t)=\prod_{i\geqslant 1}\frac{\big(qtx_{i}^{2};q^{2}\big)_{\infty}}{\big(qx_{i}^{2};q^{2}\big)_{\infty}}\prod_{i<j}\frac{(tx_{i}x_{j};q)_{\infty}}{(x_{i}x_{j};q)_{\infty}}.

The above identities are no longer self-dual and, as follows from
Lemma
2.3
and (
4.11
) as well as
Lemma
9.6
below, they form a dual pair with respect
to
\omega_{q,t}
.

Lemma 9.6
.

Let
\omega_{q,t}
be the automorphism of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}(q,t)}

given by (
4.10
).
Then

\displaystyle\omega_{q,t}\bigg(\prod_{i\geqslant 1}\frac{\big(tx_{i}^{2};q^{2}\big)_{\infty}}{\big(x_{i}^{2};q^{2}\big)_{\infty}}\prod_{i<j}\frac{(tx_{i}x_{j};q)_{\infty}}{(x_{i}x_{j};q)_{\infty}}\bigg)=\prod_{i\geqslant 1}\frac{\big(qtx_{i}^{2};t^{2}\big)_{\infty}}{\big(tx_{i}^{2};t^{2}\big)_{\infty}}\prod_{i<j}\frac{(qx_{i}x_{j};t)_{\infty}}{(x_{i}x_{j};t)_{\infty}}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(a;q,t):=\prod_{i\geqslant 1}\frac{(atx_{i}^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(ax_{i}^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}\prod_{i<j}\frac{(tx_{i}x_{j};q)_{\infty}}{(x_{i}x_{j};q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathcal{L}(a;q,t)}\;=\sum_{r\geqslant 0}\bigg(\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\big(\log\big(1-q^{2r}atx_{i}^{2}\big)-\log\big(1-aq^{2r}x_{i}^{2}\big)\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathcal{L}(a;q,t) :=}\hphantom{\sum_{r\geqslant 0}}{}+\sum_{i<j}\big(\log\big(1-q^{r}tx_{i}x_{j}\big)-\log\big(1-q^{r}x_{i}x_{j}\big)\big)\bigg).

Using
\log(1-x)=-\sum_{n\geqslant 1}x^{n}/n
, the sum over
r
can be carried out by the geometric series.
Since
\sum_{i<j}(x_{i}x_{j})^{n}=(p_{n}^{2}-p_{2n})/2
, we thus find

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(a;q,t)=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{1-t^{n}}{n}\left(\frac{a^{n}p_{2n}}{1-q^{2n}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{p_{n}^{2}-p_{2n}}{1-q^{n}}\right).

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(q;q,t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{1-t^{n}}{n}\left(\frac{p_{n}^{2}}{1-q^{n}}-\frac{p_{2n}}{1+q^{n}}\right).

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(1;q,t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{1-t^{n}}{n}\left(\frac{p_{n}^{2}}{1-q^{n}}+\frac{p_{2n}}{1+q^{n}}\right).

Applying
\omega_{q,t}
to this last expression yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(1;q,t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{1-q^{n}}{n}\left(\frac{p_{n}^{2}}{1-t^{n}}-\frac{p_{2n}}{1+t^{n}}\right)=\mathcal{L}(t;t,q),

completing the proof.
∎
