Deriving the Redfield Equation for Electronically Open Molecules
Bendik Støa Sannes, Jacob Pedersen †, Ida-Marie Høyvik

Abstract
We introduce a formalism to describe fractional charging of a molecule due to interactions with its environment. The interactions which induce fractional charging are contained in the Hamiltonian of the full system (molecule and environment). Such interactions can be singled out by expressing the Hamiltonian in a local spin orbital basis, and they are the main focus of this work. A reduced density operator for the molecule is derived starting from the Liouville-von Neumann equation for the full system by employing an explicitly constructed projection superoperator. By treating the molecule as an electronically open quantum system, we obtain a Redfield equation where the environment is included approximately. Phenomenological broadening of energy levels is included to mimic finite lifetimes of electronic states. The populations of the reduced density operator determine the mixture of different redox states and, hence, the fractional charging of the molecule. To illustrate the formalism, we use benzene physisorbed on a graphene sheet as a toy model. The work presented in this paper constitutes an initial step toward understanding molecules as electronically open quantum systems.

Introduction

State-of-the-art electronic structure models may describe a molecule interacting with its environment. Numerous approaches are developed to include such environmental effects, either quantum mechanically
Höfener and Visscher (2012)
;
Sæther et al. (2017)
;
Myhre et al. (2014)
;
Jacob and Neugebauer (2014)
;
Lee et al. (2019)
;
Manby et al. (2012)
or classically.
Senn and Thiel (2009)
;
Cappelli (2016)
;
Tzeliou et al. (2022)
A common feature of these approaches is that they only include interactions that conserve the number of electrons in the molecule and environment separately. Hence, the molecule is treated as an electronically closed system.
However, if a full quantum-mechanical description of both the molecule and the environment is carried out, electrons are not strictly enforced to belong to either subsystem. Consequently, a full quantum-mechanical description allows for electron transfer between subsystems. The electron transfer may range from integer transfer (redox reaction) to partial transfer (fractional charging). Fractional charging is the manifestation of electrons being shared to some extent between the subsystems, and while integer electron transfer is widely studied
Marcus (1964)
;
Marcus and Sutin (1985)
;
Newton (1991)
;
Fletcher (2010)
;
Blumberger (2015)
, fractional charging is largely ignored.

We will describe the fractional charging of a molecule due to the interaction with its environment. Through an analysis of the Hamiltonian of the full system in a basis of spin orbitals localized to either of the subsystems, the terms inducing fractional charging are identified. From these terms, an effective interaction operator is constructed. Our analysis of the Hamiltonian directly shows which one- and two-electron integrals are contained in the effective interaction strength.
We note that the effective operator is of the same form as in electron transport models
Dzhioev and Kosov (2012)
;
Galperin and Nitzan (2012)
;
Elenewski et al. (2017)
;
Philbin et al. (2021)
;
Naaman et al. (2022)
.
To describe the effect of the interaction on the molecule, we will use the density operator formalism and the theory of open quantum systems.
Breuer and Petruccione (2002)
;
May and Kühn (2011)
;
Nitzan (2013)
;
Rivas and Huelga (2011)
;
Stefanucci and van Leeuwen (2013)
The theory of open quantum systems aims to describe the time evolution of a system in the presence of an environment, where only the environment’s effect on the target system is included approximately. Previously, the theory has been used to model numerous phenomena, such as vibrational relaxation for spectroscopy
Yan and Mukamel (1988)
;
Khalil et al. (2004)
;
Galperin et al. (2008)
and energy transfer in electron transfer processes.
Jean et al. (1992)
;
Leegwater et al. (1997)
;
Jeske et al. (2015)
;
Storm et al. (2019)
;
Pedersen et al. (2022)

The advantage of using density operators is that they can describe irreversible processes not attainable when using the electronic wave function. Examples are dissipation and decoherence
Weiss (2012)
;
Blum (2012)
;
The (2013)
, and the extent of these effects depends on the interaction between the molecule and the environment.
Breuer and Petruccione (2002)
Furthermore, the density operator allows for direct access to the state populations.
The time development of the reduced density operator describing only the molecule explicitly is derived from the Liouville-von Neumann equation for the full system. This is achieved by using the projection approach of Nakajima and Zwanzig
Nakajima (1958)
;
Zwanzig (2004)
, resulting in two coupled equations. In the absence of the interaction operator, the two equations decouple and correspond to the Liouville-von Neumann equations for the separate subsystems. Hence, the interaction couples the electronic degrees of freedom in the molecule to the electronic degrees of freedom in the environment.

The coupled equations resulting from the Nakajima-Zwanzig approach are as challenging to solve as the Liouville-von Neumann equation for the full system. To simplify, the Born and Markov approximations are typically introduced.
Breuer and Petruccione (2002)
;
Nitzan (2013)
;
May and Kühn (2011)
These approximations assume that the environment is large and not significantly altered by the interaction, and that the environment’s relaxation time is short. Additionally, assuming that the molecule-environment interaction is weak leads to the Redfield equation. Applying the secular approximation on top of this yields a master equation of Lindblad form, ensuring complete positivity of the dynamics
Lindblad (1976)
;
Gorini et al. (1976)
;
Manzano (2020)
;
Hartmann and Strunz (2020)
;
Tupkary et al. (2022)
. The Redfield and Lindblad equations have been extensively used in quantum optics
Mukamel (1995)
;
Campaioli et al. (2024)
, and for electron transport and charge separation in molecular system.
Jean et al. (1992)
;
Novoderezhkin et al. (2004)
;
Kilin et al. (2000)
;
Novoderezhkin et al. (2007)
;
Dzhioev and Kosov (2011)
;
May and Kühn (2011)
;
Dzhioev and Kosov (2012)
;
Elenewski et al. (2017)
;
Pedersen et al. (2022)

We derive the Redfield equation using the exact eigenstates of the molecular electronic Hamiltonian for the non-interacting molecule and environment. Within a given one-electron basis, the exact eigenstates are the full configuration interaction (FCI) states.
Shavitt (1977)
;
Helgaker et al. (2014)
It is important to note that due to the form of the interaction operator, we need to consider states with different number of electrons. Since FCI calculations are only applicable to very small molecular systems, we use Slater determinants as the many-electron basis for the reduced density operator in the derived form of the Redfield equation. The determinants do not provide a quantitative description of the molecule, but serve the purpose of illustrating the presented formalism. From propagating the equation to a steady state, the populations of the determinants are obtained.
The average number of electrons in the molecule may be fractional due to mixing of determinants with different number of electrons. In this formalism, fractional charging can be calculated as the deviation from the number of electrons of the isolated molecule.

In standard electronic structure theory, fractional orbital occupations have been extensively used to recover static correlation for isolated molecules.
Bogolyubov (1959)
;
Slater et al. (1969)
;
Abdulnur et al. (1972)
;
Zerner (1989)
;
Perdew et al. (1982)
;
Ullrich and Kohn (2001)
;
Lee et al. (2022)
;
Mussard and Toulouse (2017)
. On the other hand, the particle-breaking Hartree-Fock (PBHF) model
Matveeva et al. (2023)
;
Matveeva et al. (2024)
is developed to describe the molecule as an electronically open quantum system. The PBHF wave function is obtained by using the time-independent Schrödinger equation and the variational principle. The interaction operator is parametrized prior to the optimization, and PBHF therefore represents a convenient approach to capture fractional charging effects. However, irreversible processes due to the interactions cannot be represented by the wave function.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the subsystem analysis of the Hamiltonian in the local spin-orbital basis and use FCI states of the non-interacting subsystems to construct the density operator for the full system. Next, we show the derivation of the Redfield equation for the populations of the reduced density operator. This involves the use of an explicitly constructed projection superoperator. In Section
3
, we present an illustrative example by using a benzene molecule physisorbed on graphene as a toy model. We further discuss the developments necessary to obtain an appropriate electronic description of the molecule. Finally, Section
4
provides a summary and concluding remarks.

2
Theory

We start from the full configuration interaction (FCI) solution to the electronic problem of
a closed system, as this provides eigenstates of the Hamiltonian within the given one-electron basis. Next, we partition the closed system into two interacting subsystems, the molecule and its environment. The goal is to obtain a reduced description of the molecule while retaining the components that induce fractional charging. We construct the density operator for the full system (molecule and environment) starting from the exact solutions of the isolated subsystems. We then present the steps involved in the derivation of the Redfield equation, which gives the time evolution of the reduced density operator. Finally, we briefly discuss how to represent properties of the environment and the effect of energy level broadening.

2.1
Electronic structure theory for closed systems

The time-independent electronic Schrödinger equation is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}\Psi=E\Psi

(1)

where
\hat{H}
is the molecular electronic Hamiltonian,
\Psi
is the electronic wave function and
E
is the energy.
The Hamiltonian in second quantization, excluding nuclear repulsion, is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{PQ}h_{PQ}a^{\dagger}_{P}a_{Q}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{PQRS}g_{PQRS}a^{\dagger}_{P}a^{\dagger}_{R}a_{S}a_{Q}

(2)

where
a^{\dagger}_{P}
and
a_{P}
are the creation and annihilation operators for spin-orbital
\phi_{P}
, respectively. The set of creation and annihilation operators for the spin orbitals obey the anticommutation rules
[\,a^{\dagger}_{P},a^{\dagger}_{Q}\,]_{+}=[\,a_{P},a_{Q}\,]_{+}=0
, and
[\,a^{\dagger}_{P},a_{Q}\,]_{+}=\delta_{PQ}
. Further, we have

\displaystyle h_{PQ}

\displaystyle=\int\phi_{P}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}-\sum_{I}\frac{Z_{I}}{|\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{R}_{I}|}\Bigr)\phi_{Q}(\mathbf{x}_{1})d\mathbf{x}_{1}

(3)

\displaystyle g_{PQRS}

\displaystyle=\int\int\phi_{P}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\phi_{Q}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2}|}\phi_{R}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\phi_{S}(\mathbf{x}_{2})d\mathbf{x}_{1}d\mathbf{x}_{2}

(4)

The integration variable
\mathbf{x}_{i}
includes spatial and spin coordinates of electron
i
,

Z_{I}
is the charge of nucleus
I
, and
\mathbf{r}_{i}
and
\mathbf{R}_{I}
are electron and nuclear coordinates, respectively. The Hamiltonian in eq
2
represents a closed molecular system, and it commutes with the electron number operator
\hat{N}=\sum_{P}a^{\dagger}_{P}a_{P}
. In other words, the number of electrons is conserved for the closed system.

The exact solution within a chosen atomic orbital basis is given by the FCI wave function,
Shavitt (1977)
;
Helgaker et al. (2014)

\displaystyle|\Psi_{k}\rangle=\sum_{I}C_{Ik}|I\rangle

(5)

The sum over
I
includes all determinants for distributing
N
electrons among
M
spin orbitals, with corresponding expansion coefficients
C_{Ik}
. The FCI states are orthogonal and may be normalized,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle\Psi_{k}|\Psi_{l}\rangle=\delta_{kl}\end{split}

(6)

The variational minimization of the energy with respect to the expansion coefficients amounts to diagonalizing
\mathbf{H}
, i.e., solving the FCI eigenproblem

\displaystyle\mathbf{HC}_{k}=E_{k}\mathbf{C}_{k}

(7)

where
E_{k}
is the energy of the
k
-th state,
|\Psi_{k}\rangle
, and the elements
H_{IJ}=\langle I|\hat{H}|J\rangle
are evaluated using the Slater-Condon rules.

2.2
Dividing the closed system into two interacting subsystems

We now consider an overall closed system, which we partition into a region of interest (the molecular system,
S
) and everything else (the environment,
E
). Henceforth, we will use the term environment for everything that is not treated explicitly. We further assume that we can transform the set of spin orbitals of the full system into a set where each spin orbital can be assigned to either the system or the environment. As long as there is no covalent bond between the subsystems, we know such localized spin orbitals exist.
Høyvik and Jørgensen (2016)

We note that this does not mean that the spin orbitals are all spatially localized, as they may be delocalized across either the molecule or the environment.

From now on, we will denote spin orbitals belonging to the molecule’s orbital space with unbarred indices
\{\phi_{p}\}
, and orbitals belonging to the environment with barred indices
\{\phi_{\bar{p}}\}
, such that the set of all spin orbitals
\{\phi_{P}\}=\{\phi_{p}\}\cup\{\phi_{\bar{p}}\}
. We also use this distinction of indices for the second quantized operators. We may now separate the Hamiltonian in eq
2
into three parts,

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\text{S}}+\hat{H}_{\text{E}}+\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}

(8)

with

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{pq}h_{pq}a^{\dagger}_{p}a_{q}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{pqrs}g_{pqrs}a^{\dagger}_{p}a^{\dagger}_{r}a_{s}a_{q}

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{E}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}h_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{p}}a_{\bar{q}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bar{p}\bar{q}\bar{r}\bar{s}}g_{\bar{p}\bar{q}\bar{r}\bar{s}}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{p}}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{r}}a_{\bar{s}}a_{\bar{q}}

(10)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}-\hat{H}_{\text{S}}-\hat{H}_{\text{E}}

(11)

\hat{H}_{\text{S}}
and
\hat{H}_{\text{E}}
describe the two subsystems separately, and they commute.
\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}
contains all interactions between the molecule and the environment. We may also divide the electron number operator into a number operator for the molecule and for the environment,
\hat{N}=\hat{N}_{\text{S}}+\hat{N}_{\text{E}}
. The subsystem number operators are

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{\text{S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}a^{\dagger}_{p}a_{p}

(12)

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{\text{E}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bar{p}}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{p}}a_{\bar{p}}

(13)

The molecule and environment number operators commute with their corresponding Hamiltonians, i.e.,
[\,\hat{H}_{\text{S}},\hat{N}_{\text{S}}\,]=[\,\hat{H}_{\text{E}},\hat{N}_{\text{E}}\,]=0
. In contrast, although the interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}
commutes with
\hat{N}
, only parts of it commute with
\hat{N}_{\text{S}}
and
\hat{N}_{\text{E}}
separately. We will therefore separate
\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}
into two components,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}=\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pc}}+\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pb}}

(14)

where
\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pc}}
and
\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pb}}
contain the particle-conserving and particle-breaking terms, respectively. By particle-breaking, we mean non-particle-conserving.
Matveeva et al. (2023)
;
Matveeva et al. (2024)

These properties follow from the commutation relations

\displaystyle[\,\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pc}},\hat{N}_{\text{S}}\,]

\displaystyle=0,\qquad[\,\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pc}},\hat{N}_{\text{E}}\,]=0

(15)

\displaystyle[\,\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pb}},\hat{N}_{\text{S}}\,]

\displaystyle\neq 0,\qquad[\,\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pb}},\hat{N}_{\text{E}}\,]\neq 0

(16)

Interactions contained in
\hat{H}^{\text{pc}}_{\text{S}\text{E}}
may change the energies of the subsystems, but the respective particle numbers in each subsystem are unchanged. Numerous approaches
Höfener and Visscher (2012)
;
Sæther et al. (2017)
;
Myhre et al. (2014)
;
Jacob and Neugebauer (2014)
;
Lee et al. (2019)
;
Manby et al. (2012)
;
Senn and Thiel (2009)
;
Cappelli (2016)
;
Tzeliou et al. (2022)
include these types of interactions and will therefore not be the focus of this work. However, interactions contained in
\hat{H}^{\text{pb}}_{\text{S}\text{E}}
results in electron number fluctuations which may induce fractional charging. Here, we define fractional charging by

\displaystyle\delta N_{\text{S}}=N_{\text{S},0}-\langle\hat{N}_{\text{S}}\rangle

(17)

where
N_{\text{S},0}
is the number of electrons in the isolated system, and
\langle\hat{N}_{\text{S}}\rangle
is the average number of electrons in the molecule upon interaction with the environment.

2.2.1
The particle-breaking interaction operator

We will now reduce
\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pb}}
to an approximate effective interaction operator
\hat{V}
. All terms of
\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pb}}
are

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{H}^{\text{pb}}_{\text{S}\text{E}}&=\sum_{p\bar{q}}\bigl(h_{p\bar{q}}a^{\dagger}_{p}a_{\bar{q}}+h_{p\bar{q}}^{*}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{q}}a_{p}\bigr)+\sum_{p\bar{q}rs}\bigl(g_{p\bar{q}rs}a^{\dagger}_{p}a^{\dagger}_{r}a_{s}a_{\bar{q}}+g_{p\bar{q}rs}^{*}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{q}}a^{\dagger}_{s}a_{r}a_{p}\bigr)\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\bar{q}r\bar{s}}\bigl(g_{p\bar{q}r\bar{s}}a^{\dagger}_{p}a^{\dagger}_{r}a_{\bar{s}}a_{\bar{q}}+g_{p\bar{q}r\bar{s}}^{*}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{q}}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{s}}a_{r}a_{p}\bigr)+\sum_{p\bar{q}\bar{r}\bar{s}}\bigl(g_{p\bar{q}\bar{r}\bar{s}}a^{\dagger}_{p}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{r}}a_{\bar{s}}a_{\bar{q}}+g_{p\bar{q}\bar{r}\bar{s}}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{q}}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{s}}a_{\bar{r}}a_{p}\bigr)\end{split}

(18)

\hat{H}^{\text{pb}}_{\text{S}\text{E}}
will be reduced based on size of the charge distributions,
\phi^{*}_{P}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\phi_{Q}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
, which enter the two-electron integrals (see eq
4
). Integrals such as
g_{p\bar{q}r\bar{s}}
will generally be much smaller than integrals such as
g_{p\bar{q}\bar{r}\bar{s}}
and
g_{p\bar{q}rs}
. This is due to the fact that
g_{p\bar{q}r\bar{s}}
contains an integral over two charge distributions,
\phi^{*}_{p}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\phi_{\bar{q}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
and
\phi^{*}_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\phi_{\bar{s}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})
, which both are necessarily small since
p
and
r
are localized to the system, whereas
\bar{q}
and
\bar{s}
are localized to the environment. In contrast,
g_{p\bar{q}rs}
contains an integral over the charge distributions,
\phi^{*}_{p}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\phi_{\bar{q}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
and
\phi^{*}_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\phi_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{2})
, of which
\phi^{*}_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\phi_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{2})
will be large, in particular for
r=s
. Retaining only the dominant terms, and wrapping it into an effective one-electron operator, we obtain a form of
\hat{V}
which connects Fock space states which differ in the number of electrons by one.

\displaystyle\hat{V}=\sum_{p\bar{q}}(V_{p\bar{q}}a^{\dagger}_{p}a_{\bar{q}}+V_{p\bar{q}}^{*}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{q}}a_{p})

(19)

The effective integral
V_{p\bar{q}}
contains one-electron components as well as two-electron components of a charge distribution
\phi^{*}_{p}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\phi_{\bar{q}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
interacting with electrons in both the molecule and the environment.

\hat{V}
has the bilinear form often found in descriptions of bipartite open quantum systems
Dzhioev and Kosov (2012)
;
Elenewski et al. (2017)
;
Philbin et al. (2021)
;
Kilin et al. (2000)
;
Novoderezhkin et al. (2007)
;
Dzhioev and Kosov (2011)
;
Pedersen et al. (2022)
, but in this derivation, we have kept the physical content of it in terms of standard one- and two-electron integrals. However, note that although we know the content of
V_{p\bar{q}}
, it can not be calculated without doing a full quantum mechanical calculation of the combined system and environment. It will therefore be parametrized based on knowledge of the magnitudes of the type of interactions it contains.

2.3
Density operator formalism for FCI states

We allow the two subsystems to interact by turning on the interaction
\hat{V}
at some time
t=0
. We will now construct the density operator for the full system from the eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{\text{S}}
and
\hat{H}_{\text{E}}
.
Using the effective interaction operator given in eq
19
, the Hamiltonian for the interacting system
\text{S}+\text{E}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{\text{S}}+\hat{H}_{\text{E}}+\hat{V}(t)\equiv\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{V}(t)

(20)

Turning on
\hat{V}(t)
at time
t=0
is enforced by using the Heaviside step function,
\hat{V}(t)=\theta(t)\hat{V}
. We regard
\hat{H}_{0}
as the zeroth-order Hamiltonian and
\hat{V}(t)
as a small perturbation. I.e., we assume that the interaction is weak, which is valid for the considered non-covalent interactions. We may construct common eigenstates for
\hat{H}_{0}
from the eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{\text{S}}
and
\hat{H}_{\text{E}}
since
\hat{H}_{0}
is additively separable.
The eigenstates for
\hat{H}_{\text{S}}
and
\hat{H}_{\text{E}}
are given by the FCI solutions

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\text{S}}\mathbf{C}^{\text{S}}_{a}

\displaystyle=E_{a}^{\text{S}}\mathbf{C}^{\text{S}}_{a}

(21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\text{E}}\mathbf{C}^{\text{E}}_{\bar{a}}

\displaystyle=E_{\bar{a}}^{\text{E}}\mathbf{C}^{\text{E}}_{\bar{a}}

(22)

where the elements of the Hamiltonians are
\langle I|\hat{H}_{\text{S}}|J\rangle
and
\langle\bar{I}|\hat{H}_{\text{E}}|\bar{J}\rangle
. The determinants are defined by
|I\rangle=\hat{A}_{I}^{\dagger}|\text{vac}\rangle
and
|\bar{I}\rangle=\hat{A}_{\bar{I}}^{\dagger}|\text{vac}\rangle
, where
\hat{A}_{I}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{A}_{\bar{I}}^{\dagger}
are strings of creation operators for the system and environment, respectively.
We thus have states for the system,
|a\rangle
, and the environment,
|\bar{a}\rangle
,

\displaystyle|a\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}C_{Ia}^{\text{S}}|I\rangle

(23)

\displaystyle|\bar{a}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bar{I}}C_{\bar{I}\bar{a}}^{\text{E}}|\bar{I}\rangle

(24)

which satisfy
\langle a|b\rangle=\delta_{ab}
and
\langle\bar{a}|\bar{b}\rangle=\delta_{\bar{a}\bar{b}}
. Using these solutions, we construct a combined basis for the system and environment

\displaystyle|a\bar{a}\rangle=\sum_{I\bar{I}}C_{Ia}^{\text{S}}C_{\bar{I}\bar{a}}^{\text{E}}\hat{A}_{I}^{\dagger}\hat{A}_{\bar{I}}^{\dagger}|\text{vac}\rangle

(25)

The orthonormality
\langle a\bar{a}|b\bar{b}\rangle=\delta_{ab}\delta_{\bar{a}\bar{b}}
follows from the properties of the FCI states (eqs
23
-
24
).
We note that the states
|a\bar{a}\rangle
are not eigenstates of the full Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, but they are eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}|a\bar{a}\rangle=\bigl(E_{a}^{\text{S}}+E_{\bar{a}}^{\text{E}}\bigr)|a\bar{a}\rangle

(26)

We build the density operator for the combined system from these zeroth-order solutions. In this basis, the density operator can be written as

\displaystyle\rho(t)=\sum_{a\bar{a}b\bar{b}}\rho_{a\bar{a},b\bar{b}}(t)|a\bar{a}\rangle\langle b\bar{b}|

(27)

This form of the density operator will be used to construct a reduced density operator for the system, implicitly taking the environment into account.

2.4
The Liouville-von Neumann equation for the density operator

The time evolution of the density operator is governed by the Liouville-von Neumann equation, which in the interaction picture is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\rho}(t)=-i[\,\tilde{V}(t),\tilde{\rho}(t)\,]

(28)

An operator
\hat{\Omega}
in the interaction picture is given by the transformation
\tilde{\Omega}(t)=e^{i\hat{H}_{0}t}\hat{\Omega}e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}t}
. The goal is to obtain an equation for the time evolution of the molecular system
S
. To do this, we use the projection operator technique of Nakajima and Zwanzig
Nakajima (1958)
;
Zwanzig (2004)
, where we define two orthogonal projection superoperators
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
.

\mathcal{P}
is explicitly constructed such that the
\mathcal{P}
-projected density operator describes the dynamics of the molecule under the influence of the environment. This is achieved by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}\tilde{\rho}(t)&\equiv\sum_{a\bar{a}b}|a\bar{a}\rangle\langle a\bar{a}|\tilde{\rho}(t)|b\bar{a}\rangle\langle b\bar{a}|\\
&=\sum_{a\bar{a}b}\rho_{a\bar{a},b\bar{a}}(t)e^{i\omega_{ab}t}|a\bar{a}\rangle\langle b\bar{a}|\end{split}

(29)

The exponential
e^{i\omega_{ab}t}
results from the interaction picture, where
\omega_{ab}
is the energy difference of the unperturbed molecule eigenstates,
\omega_{ab}=E_{a}^{\text{S}}-E_{b}^{\text{S}}
. From eq
29
, we see that the
\mathcal{P}
superoperator effectively averages out the environmental degrees of freedom.
\mathcal{Q}
is defined as the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{P}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}\tilde{\rho}(t)

2.6
A mean-field approximation to the Redfield equation

The above derivation assumes that we have the FCI states for the non-interacting subsystems, and the introduced approximations so far have been those of the Redfield equation. We will now start by making approximations for the environment by assuming mean-field states and a continuous spectrum. The correlation functions in eqs

\displaystyle C_{pq}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int d\omega V_{p}(\omega)V_{q}^{*}(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}[1-\bar{n}(\omega)]

\displaystyle=\int d\omega V_{p}(\omega)V_{q}^{*}(\omega)e^{i\omega\tau}\bar{n}(\omega)

\displaystyle\begin{split}k_{b\leftarrow a}=\sum_{pq}&\langle a|a^{\dagger}_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a_{q}|a\rangle J_{pq}(\omega_{ab})[1-\bar{n}(\omega_{ab})]\\

We now approximate the electronic states of the molecule by using single determinants expressed in the same spin orbital basis. The transfer rates in eq

\displaystyle\begin{split}k_{b\leftarrow a}=\sum_{p}\Bigl(&\langle a|a^{\dagger}_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a_{p}|a\rangle J_{p}(\varepsilon_{p})[1-\bar{n}(\varepsilon_{p})]\\

In principle, one may include all determinants. Similarly, low-energy spin orbitals will remain occupied. Spin-orbitals with energies close to the chemical potential therefore constitute an active space for constructing relevant determinants for the reduced density matrix.

2.6.1
The spectral density function

The spectral density function
J_{p}(\omega)
contains information about the environment’s density of states and the interaction strength of the system orbitals with the environment.
In this work, we approximate the interaction strengths to be equal for all spin orbitals. In this case, the spectral density function becomes proportional to the environment’s electronic density of states. The density of states can either be modeled or measured experimentally.
Berglund and Spicer (1964)
;
Reinert and Hüfner (2005)
We will estimate the density of states for the considered molecular environment by calculating its relative number of orbitals per some fixed energy spacing. In the wide band approximation,
J_{p}(\omega)
is set to a constant. This is a reasonable choice for metallic environments such as gold or silver with a near-constant density of states around the Fermi energy.
Verzijl et al. (2013)
;
Smith et al. (1974)

2.6.2
Energy level broadening

Excited states have finite lifetimes, and therefore broadened energy levels, due to interactions not captured by the electronic Hamiltonian.
Rotter (2009)
;
Nordlander and Tully (1990)
In electronic structure theory, this has been accounted for by introducing phenomenological damping.
Orr and Ward (1971)
;
Norman et al. (2005)
This relaxes the resonance requirement for transitions between molecule states.
We incorporate this effect by replacing Dirac delta functions in the time-correlation functions with normalized Gaussians with standard deviations
\gamma
(see Supporting Information). Eq

\displaystyle\begin{split}k_{b\leftarrow a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma^{2}}}\sum_{p}\Bigl(&\langle a|a^{\dagger}_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a_{p}|a\rangle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}J_{p}(\omega)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(\omega_{ab}-\omega)^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\Bigr)[1-\bar{n}(\omega)]d\omega\\
+&\langle a|a_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a^{\dagger}_{p}|a\rangle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}J_{p}(\omega)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(\omega_{ba}-\omega)^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\Bigr)\bar{n}(\omega)d\omega\Bigr)\end{split}

We have now arrived at the final expression for the population transfer rates.

Results and discussion

In this section, we illustrate how the Redfield equation can be used to describe the fractional charging of a molecular system upon interaction with an environment. We note that we include only the novel particle-breaking interactions described in Section
2.2
. However, particle-conserving interactions must also be considered for realistic chemical applications. As a model system, we consider a benzene molecule physisorbed on a finite graphene surface. The benzene molecule is the target for which we create a reduced density matrix, while the graphene sheet is the environment. 3.3
Future prospects of using the Redfield equation for electronically open molecular systems

The presented results are based on several key approximations, which we will now address. These include the assumptions of the interaction operator, the system states, and the inherent assumptions of the Redfield equation. The approximations will be discussed in the context of describing a molecular system interacting weakly with an environment, and with the electronic structure of the molecule as the main objective. First, particle-conserving interactions (see Section
2.2
) must also be included to appropriately account for the interaction with the environment. The particle-breaking interactions allow for describing fractional charging, but as discussed previously, we expect them to be only a small correction to the total interaction. The effect of particle-conserving interactions can, for example, be included by well-established quantum mechanical embedding methods or by a classical atomistic approach
Höfener and Visscher (2012)
;
Sæther et al. (2017)
;
Myhre et al. (2014)
;
Jacob and Neugebauer (2014)
;
Lee et al. (2019)
;
Manby et al. (2012)
;
Senn and Thiel (2009)
;
Cappelli (2016)
;
Tzeliou et al. (2022)
. Second, we have used single determinants with a common set of spin orbitals as the basis for the system’s reduced density matrix. Knowing that single-determinantal descriptions lack electron correlation, using correlated wave function models in the Redfield equation must be explored. A correlated model (e.g., the configuration interaction or coupled cluster wave functions) would change the Redfield equation’s dynamics and result in a different steady-state solution. Third, the optimal spin orbitals change when we get a mixture of multiple charge states. However, relaxing the spin orbitals while propagating the Redfield equation will greatly increase the complexity and computational cost of the method. In addition, recomputing spin orbitals for the fractionally charged molecule would require a wave function method that handles non-integral electron numbers and fractional occupations. However, we expect the effect of fractional charging to be small such that the spin orbitals of the neutral molecule dominate.

The approximations for the Redfield equation may also be explored to assess its validity for describing fractional charging. The Born approximation is appropriate for non-covalent interactions between the system and a large environment that is unchanged by the interaction. Consequently, the Redfield equation is less suitable for describing environments consisting of finite-sized molecules. For smaller environments, memory effects become more significant. Further, if the system and the environment are similar in size, the Markov approximation breaks down due to the inability to separate the time scales.
Breuer et al. (2016)
;
De Vega and Alonso (2017)
;
Zhang (2019)
Examining the validity of the Born and Markov approximations would require comparisons to more sophisticated methods, such as the hierarchical equations of motion.
Tanimura and Kubo (1989)
;
Zheng et al. (2012)

Another simplification is the assumption of equal interaction strengths for all benzene orbitals. In reality, interactions vary with distance and energy, and molecular orbitals are not uniquely defined. However, relative interaction strengths must be modeled in some way since explicit treatment of the environment is undesired. In principle, calculation of electronic coupling elements can be achieved by methods such as constrained DFT
Wu and Van Voorhis (2006)
;
Kaduk et al. (2012)
or generalized Mulliken-Hush.
Mulliken (1952)
;
Hush (1961)
;
Hush (1968)
;
Cave and Newton (1996)

4
Summary and conclusions

In this work, we introduce a reduced density operator formalism to include the effects of fractional charging of a molecule due to a non-covalent interaction with its environment. In order to develop the formalism, we start by analyzing the full configuration interaction solution in a spin orbital basis which is localized to either the molecule or the environment. In this basis, the terms of the molecular electronic Hamiltonian for the full system may be categorized into a molecule Hamiltonian, an environment Hamiltonian, and an interaction Hamiltonian. The interaction Hamiltonian may further be divided into particle-conserving and particle non-conserving (particle-breaking) terms by considering the commutators with the particle number operator for the molecule. The particle-breaking interactions and the fractional charging they induce are the focus of this work, and the paper thus addresses a type of interaction that has largely been neglected by the quantum chemistry community.

We include the particle-breaking interactions as a perturbation to the non-interacting molecule and environment. By introducing an explicit form of the projection superoperator we may use the Nakajima-Zwanzig formalism from the theory of open quantum systems. This allows us to derive an equation for the reduced density operator of the molecule starting from the Liouville-von Neumann equation for the full density operator. We employ the Born and Markov approximations to obtain a Redfield equation for the populations of the reduced density operator, where the effect of the environment is included approximately. The effect of the environment is modeled through the spectral density function. In this work, it is taken to be either a constant (the wide band approximation) or the computed electronic density of states for the environment. The fractional charging results from non-zero populations for molecular states with different integer charges and signifies that electrons are, to a certain extent, shared with the environment. Further, a phenomenological broadening of energy levels is introduced to mimic the finite lifetimes of electronic states. We present numerical illustrations for a mean-field approach of the formalism, using benzene physisorbed on graphene as a toy model.

The work presented in this paper represents an important step toward understanding molecules as electronically open quantum systems. To achieve quantitative accuracy, it will in the future be necessary to include particle-conserving interactions and use correlated electronic states as the many-electron basis.

(55)

We decompose the full interaction operator into its particle-conserving and particle-breaking components. The particle-conserving terms as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pc}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{pq\bar{r}\bar{s}}(g_{pq\bar{r}\bar{s}}e_{pq\bar{r}\bar{s}}+g_{qp\bar{s}\bar{r}}e_{qp\bar{s}\bar{r}})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\bar{q}\bar{r}s}(g_{p\bar{q}\bar{r}s}e_{p\bar{q}\bar{r}s}+g_{\bar{q}ps\bar{r}}e_{\bar{q}ps\bar{r}})\end{split}

(56)

and the particle-breaking terms as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pb}}=&\sum_{p\bar{q}}(h_{p\bar{q}}E_{p\bar{q}}+h_{\bar{q}p}E_{\bar{q}p})+\sum_{p\bar{q}rs}(g_{p\bar{q}rs}e_{p\bar{q}rs}+g_{\bar{q}psr}e_{\bar{q}psr})\\

(57)

We will only focus on the particle-breaking interactions. First, we disregard the terms including

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pb}}=\sum_{p\bar{q}}(h_{p\bar{q}}E_{p\bar{q}}+h_{\bar{q}p}E_{\bar{q}p})+\sum_{p\bar{q}rs}(g_{p\bar{q}rs}e_{p\bar{q}rs}+g_{\bar{q}psr}e_{\bar{q}psr})+\sum_{p\bar{q}\bar{r}\bar{s}}(g_{p\bar{q}\bar{r}\bar{s}}e_{p\bar{q}\bar{r}\bar{s}}+g_{\bar{q}p\bar{s}\bar{r}}e_{\bar{q}p\bar{s}\bar{r}})\end{split}

(58)

Using the anticommutation relations of the creation and annihilation operators and renaming the dummy summation indices, we can rewrite the expression as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E}}^{\text{pb}}=&\sum_{p\bar{q}}\Bigl(h_{p\bar{q}}+\sum_{\bar{r}\bar{s}}g_{p\bar{q}\bar{r}\bar{s}}E_{\bar{r}\bar{s}}+\sum_{rs}g_{p\bar{q}rs}E_{rs}-\sum_{r}g_{prr\bar{q}}\Bigr)E_{p\bar{q}}\\
+&\sum_{p\bar{q}}E_{\bar{q}p}\Bigl(h_{\bar{q}p}+\sum_{\bar{r}\bar{s}}g_{\bar{q}p\bar{s}\bar{r}}E_{\bar{s}\bar{r}}+\sum_{rs}g_{\bar{q}psr}E_{sr}-\sum_{r}g_{rp\bar{q}r}\Bigr)\end{split}

(59)

We construct an effective interaction operator

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{H}_{\text{S}\text{E},\text{eff}}^{\text{pb}}&=\sum_{p\bar{q}}\Bigl(h_{p\bar{q}}+\sum_{\bar{i}}g_{p\bar{q}\bar{i}\bar{i}}+\sum_{i}g_{p\bar{q}ii}-\sum_{r}g_{prr\bar{q}}\Bigr)a^{\dagger}_{p}a_{\bar{q}}\\
&+\sum_{p\bar{q}}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{q}}a_{p}\Bigl(h_{\bar{q}p}+\sum_{\bar{i}}g_{\bar{q}p\bar{i}\bar{i}}+\sum_{i}g_{\bar{q}pii}-\sum_{r}g_{rp\bar{q}r}\Bigr)\end{split}

(60)

Finally, we define

\displaystyle V_{p\bar{q}}

\displaystyle=h_{p\bar{q}}+\sum_{\bar{i}}g_{p\bar{q}\bar{i}\bar{i}}+\sum_{i}g_{p\bar{q}ii}-\sum_{r}g_{prr\bar{q}}

(61)

to arrive at the bilinear form of the interaction operator

\displaystyle\hat{V}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\bar{q}}(V_{\bar{p}q}a^{\dagger}_{\bar{p}}a_{q}+V_{p\bar{q}}^{*}a^{\dagger}_{q}a_{\bar{p}})

(62)

Appendix B
Details on the derivation of the Redfield equation

The differential equation for the time evolution of the projected density operator to second order in the interaction
\hat{V}
is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{P}\tilde{\rho}(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{P}[\,\tilde{V}(t),[\,\tilde{V}(t^{\prime}),\mathcal{P}\tilde{\rho}(t^{\prime})\,]\,]dt^{\prime}

(63)

We get an equation for the elements of the reduced density operator

by inserting our definitions of the projection superoperator

and the density operator

in the interaction picture. We project with the states

from the left and

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{\bar{a}}\langle a\bar{a}|\mathcal{P}\bigl(e^{iH_{0}t}\rho(t)e^{-iH_{0}t}\bigr)|b\bar{a}\rangle\\
=&e^{i\omega_{ab}t}Z_{\text{E}}\bigl(i\omega_{ab}\sigma_{ab}(t)+\frac{d}{dt}\sigma_{ab}(t)\bigl)\end{split}

Collecting terms, we get

The terms arising from the double commutator are

We start by looking at the first of these four terms. Inserting definitions for

We now apply two approximations:

1.

The Markov approximation

2.

The correlation functions go to

(85)

with

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\sigma_{ab}(t)=-i\omega_{ab}\sigma_{ab}(t)+\sum_{cd}\Bigl(&\Gamma_{dbac}^{+}(\omega_{da})+\Gamma_{dbac}^{-}(\omega_{bc})\\
-\delta_{bd}\sum_{e}&\Gamma_{aeec}^{+}(\omega_{de})-\delta_{ac}\sum_{e}\Gamma_{deeb}^{-}(\omega_{ec})\Bigr)(-1)^{\Delta N_{cd}}\sigma_{cd}(t)\end{split}

\mathbf{R}
as

\displaystyle R_{abcd}=\Bigl(\Gamma_{dbac}^{+}(\omega_{da})+\Gamma_{dbac}^{-}(\omega_{bc})-\delta_{bd}\sum_{e}\Gamma_{aeec}^{+}(\omega_{de})-\delta_{ac}\sum_{e}\Gamma_{deeb}^{-}(\omega_{ec})\Bigr)(-1)^{\Delta N_{cd}}

(87)

the Redfield equation can finally be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\sigma_{ab}(t)=-i\omega_{ab}\sigma_{ab}(t)+\sum_{cd}R_{abcd}\sigma_{cd}(t)

(88)

Appendix C
Simplifying the correlation function

We have the bath time-correlation functions

\displaystyle C_{pq}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}\sum_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}V_{p\bar{p}}V_{q\bar{q}}^{*}\frac{\rho_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}^{\text{E}}}{\rho^{\text{E}}}e^{i\omega_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}^{\text{E}}\tau}\langle\bar{p}|a_{\bar{p}}|\bar{q}\rangle\langle\bar{q}|a^{\dagger}_{\bar{q}}|\bar{p}\rangle

(89)

\displaystyle D_{pq}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}\sum_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}V_{p\bar{p}}V_{q\bar{q}}^{*}\frac{\rho_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}^{\text{E}}}{\rho^{\text{E}}}e^{i\omega_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}^{\text{E}}\tau}\langle\bar{p}|a^{\dagger}_{\bar{q}}|\bar{q}\rangle\langle\bar{q}|a_{\bar{p}}|\bar{p}\rangle

(90)

The environment is assumed to be large. We approximate it to have a continuous spectrum, and that its states are single determinants. We then rewrite the correlation functions as

\displaystyle C_{pq}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}\int d\omega_{1}\int d\omega_{2}V_{p}(\omega_{1})V_{q}(\omega_{2})^{*}\frac{\rho_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}^{\text{E}}}{\rho^{\text{E}}}e^{i\omega_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}^{\text{E}}\tau}\langle\bar{p}|a_{\omega_{1}}|\bar{q}\rangle\langle\bar{q}|a^{\dagger}_{\omega_{2}}|\bar{p}\rangle

(91)

\displaystyle D_{pq}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}\int d\omega_{1}\int d\omega_{2}V_{p}(\omega_{1})V_{q}(\omega_{2})^{*}\frac{\rho_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}^{\text{E}}}{\rho^{\text{E}}}e^{i\omega_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}^{\text{E}}\tau}\langle\bar{p}|a^{\dagger}_{\omega_{2}}|\bar{q}\rangle\langle\bar{q}|a_{\omega_{1}}|\bar{p}\rangle

(92)

We simplify

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{\bar{q}}e^{i\omega_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}^{\text{E}}\tau}\langle\bar{p}|a_{\omega_{1}}|\bar{q}\rangle\langle\bar{q}|a^{\dagger}_{\omega_{2}}|\bar{p}\rangle&=\sum_{\bar{q}}\langle\bar{p}|e^{iH_{\text{E}}\tau}a_{\omega_{1}}e^{-iH_{\text{E}}\tau}|\bar{q}\rangle\langle\bar{q}|a^{\dagger}_{\omega_{2}}|\bar{p}\rangle\\
&=\langle\bar{p}|e^{iH_{\text{E}}\tau}a_{\omega_{1}}e^{-iH_{\text{E}}\tau}a^{\dagger}_{\omega_{2}}|\bar{p}\rangle\\
&=\langle\bar{p}|e^{iE_{\bar{p}}^{\text{E}}\tau}a_{\omega_{1}}e^{-i(E_{\bar{p}}^{\text{E}}+\omega_{2})\tau}|\bar{p}+\omega_{2}\rangle[1-k_{\bar{p}}(\omega_{2})]\\
&=\langle\bar{p}|a_{\omega_{1}}|\bar{p}+\omega_{2}\rangle e^{-i\omega_{2}\tau}[1-k_{\bar{p}}(\omega_{2})]\\
&=\delta(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})e^{-i\omega_{2}\tau}[1-k_{\bar{p}}(\omega_{2})]\end{split}

(93)

where
k_{\bar{p}}(\omega)
is the occupation number at energy
\omega
for state
|\psi_{\bar{p}}^{\text{E}}\rangle
. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{\bar{q}}e^{i\omega_{\bar{p}\bar{q}}^{\text{E}}\tau}\langle\bar{p}|a^{\dagger}_{\omega_{2}}|\bar{q}\rangle\langle\bar{q}|a_{\omega_{1}}|\bar{p}\rangle&=\langle\bar{p}|e^{iH_{\text{E}}\tau}a^{\dagger}_{\omega_{2}}e^{-iH_{\text{E}}\tau}a_{\omega_{1}}|\bar{p}\rangle\\
&=\langle\bar{p}|a^{\dagger}_{\omega_{2}}a_{\omega_{1}}|\bar{p}-\omega_{1}\rangle e^{i\omega_{1}\tau}k_{\bar{p}}(\omega_{1})\\
&=\delta(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})e^{i\omega_{1}\tau}k_{\bar{p}}(\omega_{1})\end{split}

(94)

Since
\rho^{\text{E}}
is defined as
\sum_{\bar{p}}\rho_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}^{\text{E}}
we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{\bar{p}}\frac{\rho_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}^{\text{E}}}{\rho^{\text{E}}}k_{\bar{p}}(\omega)=\sum_{\bar{p}}\frac{\rho_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}k_{\bar{p}}(\omega)}{\sum_{\bar{q}}\rho_{\bar{q}\bar{q}}^{\text{E}}}\equiv\bar{n}(\omega)

(95)

\displaystyle\sum_{\bar{p}}\frac{\rho_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}^{\text{E}}}{\rho^{\text{E}}}[1-k_{\bar{p}}(\omega)]=\frac{\sum_{\bar{p}}\rho_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}^{\text{E}}}{\sum_{\bar{q}}\rho_{\bar{q}\bar{q}}^{\text{E}}}-\sum_{\bar{p}}\frac{\rho_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}k_{\bar{p}}(\omega)}{\sum_{\bar{q}}\rho_{\bar{q}\bar{q}}^{\text{E}}}=1-\bar{n}(\omega)

(96)

where
\bar{n}(\omega)
is the expected number of electrons in the environment at energy
\omega
, a number between
0
and
1
. Correspondingly,
[1-\bar{n}(\omega)]
is the expected number of holes at energy
\omega
. The function
\bar{n}(\omega)
could for instance be described by the Fermi-Dirac function given a temperature
T
and a chemical potential
p
,

\displaystyle\bar{n}(\omega;p,T)=\Bigl[\exp\Bigl(\frac{\omega-p}{k_{B}T}\Bigr)+1\Bigr]^{-1}

(97)

where
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant.
The time-correlation functions are then

\displaystyle C_{pq}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int d\omega V_{p}(\omega)V_{q}(\omega)^{*}e^{-i\omega\tau}[1-\bar{n}(\omega)]

(98)

\displaystyle D_{pq}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int d\omega V_{p}(\omega)V_{q}(\omega)^{*}e^{i\omega\tau}\bar{n}(\omega)

(99)

C.1
The secular approximation

Assuming fast-oscillating and decaying off-diagonal elements of the reduced density operator, the Redfield equation for the populations reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\sigma_{aa}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}k_{a\leftarrow b}\sigma_{bb}(t)-\sum_{b}k_{b\leftarrow a}\sigma_{aa}(t)

(100)

with the population transfer rates
k_{b\leftarrow a}=\Gamma_{abba}^{+}(\omega_{ab})+\Gamma_{abba}^{-}(\omega_{ba})
. We evaluate the rates to be

\displaystyle\begin{split}k_{b\leftarrow a}=\sum_{pq}\bigl(&\langle a|a^{\dagger}_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a_{q}|a\rangle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\omega_{ab}\tau}C_{pq}(\tau)d\tau\\
+&\langle a|a_{q}|b\rangle\langle b|a^{\dagger}_{p}|a\rangle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\omega_{ab}\tau}D_{pq}(\tau)d\tau\\
+&\langle a|a^{\dagger}_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a_{q}|a\rangle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\omega_{ba}\tau}C_{pq}(-\tau)d\tau\\
+&\langle a|a_{q}|b\rangle\langle b|a^{\dagger}_{p}|a\rangle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\omega_{ba}\tau}D_{pq}(-\tau)d\tau\bigr)\\
=\sum_{pq}\bigl(&\langle a|a^{\dagger}_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a_{q}|a\rangle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega_{ab}\tau}C_{pq}(\tau)d\tau\\
+&\langle a|a_{q}|b\rangle\langle b|a^{\dagger}_{p}|a\rangle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega_{ab}\tau}D_{pq}(\tau)d\tau\bigr)\end{split}

(101)

Inserting the simplified time-correlation functions of eqs (
98
) - (
99
), we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}k_{b\leftarrow a}=\sum_{pq}\Bigl(&\langle a|a^{\dagger}_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a_{q}|a\rangle\int d\omega V_{p}(\omega)V_{q}(\omega)^{*}[1-\bar{n}(\omega)]\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tau e^{i(\omega_{ab}-\omega)\tau}\\
+&\langle a|a_{q}|b\rangle\langle b|a^{\dagger}_{p}|a\rangle\int d\omega V_{p}(\omega)V_{q}(\omega)^{*}\bar{n}(\omega)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tau e^{i(\omega_{ab}+\omega)\tau}\Bigr)\\
=2\pi\sum_{pq}\Bigl(&\langle a|a^{\dagger}_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a_{q}|a\rangle\int d\omega V_{p}(\omega)V_{q}(\omega)^{*}[1-\bar{n}(\omega)]\delta(\omega_{ab}-\omega)\\
+&\langle a|a_{q}|b\rangle\langle b|a^{\dagger}_{p}|a\rangle\int d\omega V_{p}(\omega)V_{q}(\omega)^{*}\bar{n}(\omega)\delta(\omega_{ab}+\omega)\Bigr)\\
=2\pi\sum_{pq}\Bigl(&\langle a|a^{\dagger}_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a_{q}|a\rangle V_{p}(\omega_{ab})V_{q}(\omega_{ab})^{*}[1-\bar{n}(\omega_{ab})]\\
+&\langle a|a_{q}|b\rangle\langle b|a^{\dagger}_{p}|a\rangle V_{p}(\omega_{ba})V_{q}(\omega_{ba})^{*}\bar{n}(\omega_{ba})\Bigr)\\
=\sum_{pq}\Bigl(&\langle a|a^{\dagger}_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a_{q}|a\rangle J_{pq}(\omega_{ab})[1-\bar{n}(\omega_{ab})]\\
+&\langle a|a_{q}|b\rangle\langle b|a^{\dagger}_{p}|a\rangle J_{pq}(\omega_{ba})\bar{n}(\omega_{ba})\Bigr)\end{split}

(102)

where we have introduced the spectral density function
J_{pq}(\omega)=2\pi V_{p}(\omega)V_{q}(\omega)^{*}
which carries information about the density of states of the environment and the interaction strengths.

C.2
Energy level broadening

With broadened energy levels due to finite lifetimes of excited states, the Dirac delta functions containing excitation energies in eq (
102
) are replaced by normalized Gaussian functions with standard deviation
\gamma

\displaystyle\delta(\omega_{ab}\pm\omega)\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma^{2}}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(\omega_{ab}\pm\omega)^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\Bigr)

(103)

such that the population transfer rates for single-determinantal system states become

\displaystyle\begin{split}k_{b\leftarrow a}=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma^{2}}}\sum_{p}\Bigl(\\
&\langle a|a^{\dagger}_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a_{p}|a\rangle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega J_{p}(\omega)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(\omega_{ab}\pm\omega)^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\Bigr)[1-\bar{n}(\omega)]\\
+&\langle a|a_{p}|b\rangle\langle b|a^{\dagger}_{p}|a\rangle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega J_{p}(\omega)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(\omega_{ba}\pm\omega)^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\Bigr)\bar{n}(\omega)\Bigr)\end{split}

(104)
