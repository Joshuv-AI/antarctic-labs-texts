A conjecture by Bienvenu and Geroldinger on power monoids
Salvatore Tringali, Weihao Yan

Abstract
. Let S S be a numerical monoid, i.e., a submonoid of the additive monoid ( ℕ , + ) (\mathbb{N},+) of non-negative integers such that ℕ ∖ S \mathbb{N}\smallsetminus S is finite. Endowed with the operation of set addition, the family of all finite subsets of S S containing 0 0 is itself a monoid, which we denote by 𝒫 fin , 0 ​ ( S ) \mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},0}(S) . We show that, if S 1 S_{1} and S 2 S_{2} are numerical monoids and 𝒫 fin , 0 ​ ( S 1 ) \mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},0}(S_{1}) is isomorphic to 𝒫 fin , 0 ​ ( S 2 ) \mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},0}(S_{2}) , then S 1 = S 2 S_{1}=S_{2} . (In fact, we establish a more general result, in which S 1 S_{1} and S 2 S_{2} are allowed to be subsets of the non-negative rational numbers that contain zero and are closed under addition.) This proves a conjecture of Bienvenu and Geroldinger.

1.
Introduction

Let
S
be a semigroup. (We refer to Howie’s monograph
[
12
]
for generalities on semigroups and monoids; and unless a statement to the contrary is made, we assume throughout that all semigroups are written multiplicatively). Equipped with the (binary) operation of setwise multiplication defined by

(X,Y)\mapsto\{xy\colon x\in X,\kern 1.00006pty\in Y\},

the non-empty subsets of
S
do also form a semigroup, herein denoted by
\mathcal{P}(S)
and called the
large power semigroup
of
S
.
Additionally, the family of all non-empty
finite
subsets of
S
is a subsemigroup of
\mathcal{P}(S)
, herein denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(S)
and referred to as the
finitary power semigroup
of
S
.

The systematic investigation of these structures was initiated by Tamura in the late 1960s and continued by semigroup theorists and computer scientists through the 1980s–1990s. By that time, researchers were especially interested in properties of
S
that
do or do not ascend to
\mathcal{P}(S)
, as well as in the study of varieties (namely, classes of finite semigroups that are closed under taking homomorphic images, subsemigroups, and finite direct products) generated by
\mathcal{P}(S)
as
S
ranges over a specified family of finite semigroups (see the surveys by Pin
[
17
,
18
]
and Almeida
[
1
]
for additional background). A key question, known as the
isomorphism problem for power semigroups
and arisen from work of Tamura and Shafer
[
21
]
, was to prove or disprove that, if
\mathcal{P}(S)
is semigroup-isomorphic to the large power semigroup of a semigroup
T
, then
S
is semigroup-isomorphic to
T
. Answered (in the negative) by Mogiljanskaja
[
15
]
, the question is still open for
finite
semigroups (cf. the first paragraph on p. 5 of
[
10
]
).

Now suppose that
M
is a monoid with identity
1_{M}
. Both
\mathcal{P}(M)
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(M)
are then monoids too, their identity being the singleton
\{1_{M}\}
. Accordingly, we call
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(M)
the
finitary power monoid
(for short, FPM) of
M
. Moreover, the family of all finite subsets of
M
containing
1_{M}
is a submonoid of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(M)
, herein denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(M)
and named the
reduced FPM
of
M
. Introduced by Fan and Tringali in
[
5
]
(see
[
14
]
for the analogous notion where finite subsets are replaced with infinite ones) and further investigated in
[
2
]
,
[
22
, Sect. 4.2]
, and
[
3
]
, reduced FPMs are the most basic objects in a complex hierarchy of “highly non-cancellative” monoids, generically dubbed as
power monoids
.

Power monoids provide a natural algebraic framework for a number of problems of great importance in additive combinatorics and related fields, one notable example being Sárközy’s conjecture
[
20
, Conjecture 1.6]
on the “additive irreducibility” of the set of quadratic residues modulo
p
for all but finitely many primes
p\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
(the positive integers).
Furthermore, the arithmetic of power monoids — with emphasis on questions concerned with the possibility or impossibility of factoring certain sets as a product of other sets that, in a sense, cannot be “broken down into smaller pieces” — is a rich topic in itself and has been pivotal in the ongoing development of a “unifying theory of factorization”
[
4
,
22
]
that extends beyond the (somewhat narrow) boundaries of the classical theory
[
7
]
.

The present article adds to this line of research by resolving a conjecture put forth by Bienvenu and Geroldinger
[
3
]
that shares a similar spirit with Tamura and Shafer’s isomorphism problem for power semigroups. Our starting point is the following:

Remark 1.1
.

Assume
f\colon H\to K
is a monoid homomorphism and let
F
be the function

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)\colon X\mapsto f(X),

where
f(X):=\{f(x)\colon x\in X\}\subseteq K
is the (direct) image of
X
under
f
. Note that
F
is a well-defined function, because
f
sends the identity
1_{H}
of
H
to the identity
1_{K}
of
K
and hence
f(X)
is a finite subset of
K
containing
1_{K}
for each
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. In addition, we have
F(\{1_{H}\})=\{1_{K}\}
, namely,
F
maps the identity of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to the identity of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
; and it is immediate that, for every
X,Y\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
,

F(XY)=\{f(xy)\colon x\in X,\kern 1.00006pty\in Y\}=\{f(x)f(y)\colon x\in X,\kern 1.00006pty\in Y\}=F(X)F(Y).

All in all, this yields that
F
is a monoid homomorphism
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
.

Now suppose that
f
is bijective. If
X,Y\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
and
X\neq Y
, then there is at least one element
z\in H
such that either
z\in X\smallsetminus Y
or
z\in Y\smallsetminus X
. In either case, we gather from the injectivity of
f
that
F(X)\neq F(Y)
, which ultimately shows that
F
itself is injective. Moreover, if
Y\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
, then
X:=\allowbreak f^{-1}(Y)
is a finite subset of
H
with
1_{H}\in X
and
F(X)=Y
; that is,
F
is surjective. So,
F
is a monoid isomorphism from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
.

The examples below prove that, in general, the conclusions drawn in Remark
1.1
cannot be reversed: The reduced FPMs of two monoids
H
and
K
can be monoid-isomorphic even if
H
and
K
are not.

Examples 1.2
.

(1)

Let
H
be a
2
-element monoid.
Up to isomorphism,
H
is then either the additive group of integers modulo
2
, or the idempotent submonoid
E=\{0,1\}
of the multiplicative monoid of the ring of integers.
In both cases,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is an idempotent
2
-element monoid (whose elements are the sets
\{1_{H}\}
and
H
), and hence it is monoid-isomorphic to
E
.

(2)

Let
M
be a
breakable monoid
, meaning that
xy\in\{x,y\}
for all
x,y\in M
(see, e.g., Sect. 27 in
[
19
]
). It is then easily found that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(M)
is the monoid obtained by endowing the family of all finite subsets of
M
containing the identity
1_{M}
with the operation
(X,Y)\mapsto X\cup Y
. Indeed, we have

X\cup Y=X1_{M}\cup 1_{M}Y\subseteq XY=\{xy\colon x\in X,\kern 1.00006pty\in Y\}\subseteq X\cup Y,\qquad\text{for all }X,Y\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(M).

Now let
H
be a
unitization of the left zero semigroup
on a set
V
, i.e., define an (associative) multiplication on
V
by taking
xy:=x
for all
x,y\in V
, add a new element
e
to
V
, and extend the previous operation from
V
to
V\cup\{e\}
by taking
xe=ex:=x
for every
x\in V\cup\{e\}
. Next, let
H^{\rm op}
be the
opposite monoid
of
H
, so that, in
H^{\rm op}
, we have
xy=y
for all
x,y\in V
. Of course,
H
and
H^{\rm op}
are both breakable monoids (with identity
e
), which implies from the above that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)=\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H^{\rm op})
. Yet,
H
and
H^{\rm op}
are non-isomorphic monoids unless
|V|\leq 1
.

In fact, assume
|V|\geq 2
and suppose for a contradiction that there is a monoid isomorphism
f\colon H\to\allowbreak H^{\rm op}
. There then exist
u,v\in V
with
u\neq v
such that
uv=v
in
H^{\rm op}
. Since
f(e)=e
and
f
is a bijection, we can thus find
x,y\in V
with
f(x)=u
and
f(y)=v
. This is however impossible, since
f
being a monoid homomorphism
H\to H^{\rm op}
and considering that
xy=x
in
H
yield
v=uv=f(x)f(y)=f(xy)=f(x)=u
.

(3)

We get from items
(1)
and
(2)
that, for any cardinal number
\kappa\geq 2
(no matter if finite or not), there exist non-isomorphic monoids
H
and
K
with
|H|=|K|=\kappa
such that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
are monoid-isomorphic. It is open whether anything similar is possible when
H
and
K
are
both
cancellative.

We are thus led to the question: Are there any
interesting
classes
\mathscr{H}
of monoids with the property that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is (monoid-)isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
, for some
H,K\in\mathscr{H}
, if and only if
H
is isomorphic to
K
? It has been conjectured by Bienvenu and Geroldinger that the answer is positive when
\mathscr{H}
is the class of numerical monoids, where we recall that a
numerical monoid
is a submonoid
S
of the additive monoid
(\mathbb{N},+)
of the non-negative integers such that
\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus S
is a finite set.

More precisely, Conjecture 4.7 in
[
3
]
states that the reduced FPM of a numerical monoid
S_{1}
is isomorphic to the reduced FPM of a numerical monoid
S_{2}
if and only if
S_{1}=S_{2}
. (It is folklore that two numerical monoids are isomorphic if and only if they are equal, see
[
11
, Theorem 3]
.) Our main result (Theorem
2.5
) proves a stronger version of the conjecture, in which numerical monoids are replaced by (
rational
)
Puiseux monoids
, that is, subsets of the non-negative rational numbers that contain zero and are closed under addition
[
8
,
6
]
.
The corresponding question for the FPMs of numerical monoids was already settled by Bienvenu and Geroldinger themselves in
[
3
, Theorem 3.2(3)]
.

2.
The proof

For all
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\varnothing\neq A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, we take
\llbracket a,b\rrbracket:=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}\colon a\leq x\leq b\}
to be the
discrete interval
from
a
to
b
and
\gcd A
to be the
greatest common divisor
of
A
, i.e., the largest non-negative divisor of each
a\in A
(here the term “largest” refers to the divisibility preorder on the multiplicative monoid of the integers and not to the usual ordering of
\mathbb{Z}
). Observe that
\gcd\{0\}=0
.

We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S)
the reduced FPM of a Puiseux monoid
S
. In contrast to what is done in the introduction with the reduced FPM of an arbitrary monoid
H
, we write
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S)
additively. In particular, given an integer
k\geq 0
and a positive integer
n
, we let
kX
be the
k
-fold sum
and
X/n:=\{x/n\colon x\in X\}
be the
1/n
-dilate
of a set
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0})
, where
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
is the non-negative cone of the (ordered) field of rational numbers. Further terminology and notations, if not explained, are standard or should be clear from the context. Most notably, all morphisms mentioned in this section are
monoid
homomorphisms.

A key ingredient in our proof of the Bienvenu–Geroldinger conjecture is a classical result of Nathanson
[
16
]
, often referred to as the Fundamental Theorem of Additive Combinatorics:

Theorem 2.1

(Nathanson’s theorem)
.

Let
A
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{N}
with
0\in A
and
\gcd A=1
. There then exist
b,c\in\mathbb{N}
,
B\subseteq\allowbreak\llbracket 0,b-2\rrbracket
, and
C\subseteq\llbracket 0,c-2\rrbracket
such that
kA=B\cup\allowbreak\llbracket b,\allowbreak ka-c\kern 1.00006pt\rrbracket\cup\allowbreak(ka-C)
for every integer
k\geq a^{2}n
, where
a:=\allowbreak\max A
and
n:=|A|-1
.

The bound
a^{2}n
in Theorem
2.1
has been recently improved to
a-n+1
by Granville and Walker
[
9
, Theorem 1]
, and their result was further refined by Lev in
[
13
, Theorem 3]
(though nothing of this will be needed in the proofs below).
Also note that, according to our definitions, the condition
\gcd A=1
in Theorem
2.1
implies that the set
A
has at least two elements — and it holds that
|A|=2
if and only if
A=\{0,1\}
, in which case
kA=\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
(and hence the theorem is trivial).

Lemma 2.2
.

If
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0})
, then
(k+1)A=kA+\{0,\max A\}
for all large
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Since
A
is a non-empty finite subset of
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
containing
0
, there exist
d\in\allowbreak\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
A^{\prime}\in\allowbreak\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
such that
A=A^{\prime}/d
. It follows that
\max A=\max A^{\prime}/d
and
kA=kA^{\prime}/d
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, which makes it possible to simplify the remainder of the proof by taking
d=1
and hence
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
.

Accordingly, set
a:=\max A
and
q:=\gcd A
. We may suppose
\{0\}\subsetneq A
, or else
kA=\{0\}
for every
k\in\allowbreak\mathbb{N}
and the conclusion is obvious. Then,
q
is a positive integer and there is no loss of generality in assuming (as we do) that
q=1
, since letting
\hat{A}:=A/q\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and
\hat{a}:=\max\hat{A}
yields that (i)
\gcd\hat{A}=\allowbreak 1
and (ii)
(k+1)A=kA+\{0,a\}
, for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, if and only if
(k+1)\hat{A}=k\hat{A}+\{0,\hat{a}\}
.

Thus, we gather from Nathanson’s theorem (Theorem
2.1
) that there exist non-negative integers
b
,
c
, and
k_{0}
and sets
B\subseteq\llbracket 0,b-2\rrbracket
and
C\subseteq\llbracket 0,c-2\rrbracket
such that

kA=B\cup\llbracket b,ka-c\kern 1.00006pt\rrbracket\cup(ka-C),\qquad\text{for each }k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq k_{0}}.

(1)

Now fix an integer
h\geq\max\{k_{0},1+(b+c)/a\}
. It is obvious that
hA+\{0,a\}\subseteq hA+A=(h+1)A
,
and we claim that also the converse inclusion is true. In fact, pick
x\in(h+1)A
.

Since
h\geq k_{0}
, Eq. (
1
) holds for both
k=h
and
k=h+1
. So, either
x\in B\cup\llbracket b,ha-c-1\rrbracket
, and it is then evident that
x\in hA
; or
x-a\in\llbracket(h-1)a-c,ha-c\kern 1.00006pt\rrbracket\cup(ha-C)\subseteq hA
and then
x\in hA+a
, where we are especially using that
h\geq 1+(b+c)/a
and hence
(h-1)a-c\geq b
. In both cases, the conclusion is that
x\in hA+\{0,a\}
, which finishes the proof as
x
is an arbitrary element in
(h+1)A
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

An isomorphism
\phi\colon\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S_{1})\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S_{2})
, where
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are Puiseux monoids, sends a
2
-element set to a
2
-element set.

Proof.

Fix a non-zero
a\in S_{1}
. We need to show that
B:=\allowbreak\phi(\{0,\allowbreak a\})=\allowbreak\{0,b\}
for some (non-zero)
b\in S_{2}
.
Indeed, set
b:=\max B\in S_{2}
and note that
b
is non-zero, because
B\neq\phi(\{0\})=\{0\}
(by the injectivity of
\phi
).
By Lemma
2.2
, there then exists an integer
k\geq 0
such that

(k+1)B=kB+\{0,b\}
.

Put
A:=\phi^{-1}(\{0,b\})
, where
\phi^{-1}
is the (functional) inverse of
\phi
. Since
\phi^{-1}
is an isomorphism from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S_{2})
to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S_{1})
with
\phi^{-1}(B)=\{0,a\}
, we get from the above that

(k+1)\{0,a\}=(k+1)\phi^{-1}(B)=k\phi^{-1}(B)+\phi^{-1}(\{0,b\})=k\{0,a\}+A.

It follows that
\{0\}\subsetneq A\subseteq(k+1)\{0,a\}
and
\max A=(k+1)a-ka=a
. So, noticing that
a
is the least non-zero element of
(k+1)\{0,a\}
, we find
A=\{0,\allowbreak a\}
and hence
B=\allowbreak\phi(\{0,a\})=\phi(A)=\{0,b\}
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S_{1})\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S_{2})
be an isomorphism, where
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are Puiseux monoids, and pick
a_{1},a_{2}\in S_{1}
. The following hold:

(i)

There exist
b_{1},b_{2}\in S_{2}
such that
\phi(\{0,a_{1}\})=\{0,b_{1}\}
and
\phi(\{0,a_{2}\})=\{0,b_{2}\}
.

(ii)

\phi(\{0,a_{1}+a_{2}\})=\{0,b_{1}+b_{2}\}
.

Proof.

Define

A:=\{0,a_{1}\}+\{0,a_{2}\}
,
B:=\allowbreak\phi(A)
, and
a_{0}:=a_{1}+a_{2}=\max A\in S_{1}
.
We have from Lemma
2.3
that, for each
i\in\llbracket 0,2\rrbracket
, there is an element
b_{i}\in S_{2}
such that

\phi(\{0,\allowbreak a_{i}\})=\{0,b_{i}\}
.
So, it only remains to show that
b_{0}=b_{1}+b_{2}
.
To this end, we know from Lemma
2.2
that
(k+\allowbreak 1)A=\allowbreak kA+\{0,\allowbreak a_{0}\}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\phi(X+Y)=\phi(X)+\phi(Y)
for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S_{1})
, it is thus found that

B=\phi(A)=\phi(\{0,a_{1}\})+\phi(\{0,a_{2}\})=\{0,b_{1}\}+\{0,b_{2}\}

and

(k+1)B=(k+1)\phi(A)=\phi((k+1)A)=k\phi(A)+\phi(\{0,a_{0}\})=kB+\{0,b_{0}\}.

Consequently, we obtain that
b_{0}=(k+1)\max B-k\max B=\max B=b_{1}+b_{2}
, as wished.
∎

Theorem 2.5
.

The reduced finitary power monoids
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S_{1})
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S_{2})
of two Puiseux monoids
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are isomorphic if and only if
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are.

Proof.

In view of Remark
1.1
, we may focus our attention on the “only if” part of the statement.

Let
\phi
be an isomorphism
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S_{1})\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(S_{2})
. By
Lemma
2.3
,
\phi
maps a
2
-element set
\{0,a\}\subseteq\allowbreak S_{1}
to a
2
-element set
\{0,b\}\subseteq S_{2}
.
Conversely, any
2
-element set
\{0,b\}\subseteq S_{2}
is the image under
\phi
of a
2
-element set
\{0,a\}\subseteq S_{1}
, because the inverse
\phi^{-1}
of
\phi
is itself an isomorphism, with the result that, for each non-zero
b\in S_{2}
, there is a non-zero
a\in S_{1}
with
\phi^{-1}(\{0,b\})=\{0,a\}
.

It follows that the function
\Phi\colon S_{1}\to S_{2}\colon a\mapsto\max\phi(\{0,a\})
is bijective; and on the other hand, we get from Lemma
2.4
that
\Phi
is a homomorphism (from
S_{1}
to
S_{2}
). So, we are done.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

The reduced finitary power monoids of two numerical monoids
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are isomorphic if and only if
S_{1}=S_{2}
.

Proof.

Straightforward from Theorem
2.5
and the fact that two numerical monoids are isomorphic if and only if they are equal (see the comments after Examples
1.2
).
∎
