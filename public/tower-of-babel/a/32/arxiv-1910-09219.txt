A Transformation Perspective on Marginal and Conditional Models

\Abstract
Clustered observations are ubiquitous in controlled and observational
studies and arise naturally in multi-centre trials or longitudinal surveys.
We present a novel model for the analysis of clustered observations where
the marginal distributions are described by a linear transformation model
and the correlations by a joint multivariate normal distribution. The
joint model provides an analytic formula for the marginal distribution.
Owing to the richness of
transformation models, the techniques are applicable to any type of response
variable, including bounded, skewed, binary, ordinal, or survival responses.
We demonstrate how the common normal assumption for reaction times can be relaxed
in the sleep deprivation benchmark dataset and report marginal odds ratios
for the notoriously difficult toe nail data. We furthermore
discuss the analysis of two clinical trials aiming at the estimation of
marginal treatment effects. In the first trial, pain was repeatedly assessed
on a bounded visual analog scale and marginal proportional-odds models are
presented. The second trial reported disease-free
survival in rectal cancer patients, where the marginal hazard ratio from Weibull
and Cox models is of special interest. An empirical evaluation compares the
performance of the novel approach to general estimation equations for binary
responses and to conditional mixed-effects models for continuous responses.
An implementation is available in the

\pkg
tram add-on package to the
\proglang
R system and
was benchmarked against established models in the literature.

\Keywords
conditional mixed-effects models, marginal models, marginal predictive
distributions, survival analysis, categorical data analysis

1
Introduction

In the context of the analysis of dependent data or clustered observations,
many statistical approaches for fitting conditional and marginal models have been
studied.
Generalised mixed-models
(
Stroup 2012
, GLMMs,)
condition on
unobservable random effects and allow interpretation of covariate effects among
subjects sharing the same value of such a random effect. Conditional models
typically assume a specific random effects distribution and thus induce a
joint distribution from which marginal distributions can be derived either
analytically or by numerically integrating over random effects.
Models
formulating marginal covariate associations without requiring a model for
the joint or conditional distribution can be estimated by solving
generalised estimating equations
(
Zeger et al. 1986
;
Zeger et al. 1988
, GEEs,)
.
Later, marginalised multilevel models
(
Heagerty 1999
;
Heagerty and Zeger 2000
)

were introduced providing a likelihood-based approach to estimate marginal
coefficients in the framework of a conditional model.

Gory et al. 2021
(
Gory et al. 2021
) contribute a model definition allowing
parameters estimated in a conditional model to be interpreted in a marginal fashion,
and
McGee and Stringer 2022
(
McGee and Stringer 2022
) discuss marginal additive models for
potentially non-linear population-averaged associations, starting from
a generalised additive mixed-model framework.

Marginal predictive distributions with interpretable parameters are easy to
derive from normal linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) and binary probit GLMMs
as well as from some frailty models using a
copula representation
(
Goethals et al. 2008
)
. Regression coefficients
in other generalised linear mixed-effects or frailty models for non-normal
responses (binary logistic, Poisson, or Cox normal frailty models, for
example) only have a conditional interpretation, that is, given
unobservable normal random effects. It is possible to obtain the marginal
covariate effect by integrating out the normal random effects, however, the simple
interpretability of the fixed-effects regression coefficients is then lost.
In contrast, marginal models allowing a marginal interpretation of effects
cannot be defined in an unambiguous way without the specification of a joint
distribution and we refer to
Lee and Nelder 2004
(
Lee and Nelder 2004
) and

Muff et al. 2016
(
Muff et al. 2016
)
for a broader discussion of these issues.

Herein, we address the problem of formulating and estimating linear
transformation models for the joint distribution of cluster-correlated observations
arising, for example, in multi-centre trials or when a subject is repeatedly
examined over time. In contrast to many methods in the mixed-effects and
frailty literature primarily aiming at explanation, that is, inference for
regression coefficients conditional on random effects in the presence of correlated
observations, the focus of this paper is on inference for marginal
distributions which can be derived analytically from this novel joint
model.

Transformation models for correlated observations have been studied mostly
in the survival analysis context. Parameter estimation is typically
performed by non-parametric maximum likelihood estimation

(
Cai et al. 2000
;
Zeng et al. 2017
)
where the transformation
function is only allowed to jump at distinct observed event times and is
conceptually understood as a infinite-dimensional nuisance parameter.

Lin et al. 2017
(
Lin et al. 2017
) even go a step further and
propose a maximum rank
correlation estimator for estimating the regression coefficients
along with the mean of random effects while refraining from specifying
neither the transformation function nor a distribution for the random effects. While this model
is extremely general, interpretation of the parameters is unclear and
predictive distributions cannot be derived. Transformation models estimated
in a fully parametric way
(
McLain and Ghosh 2013
;
Hothorn et al. 2014
;
Hothorn et al. 2018
;
Klein et al. 2022
)

are practically as flexible as semiparametrically estimated models yet
technically much easier to handle. Therefore, various flavours of conditional
mixed-effects transformation models have been suggested

(
Manuguerra and Heller 2010
;
Garcia et al. 2019
;
Tang et al. 2018
;
Sun and Ding 2021
;
Tamási et al. 2022
)
.

The results presented herein allow the formulation and estimation of models
for the joint multivariate distribution of clustered non-normally
distributed responses. The marginal distributions obtained from the joint
distribution of observations in the same cluster feature directly interpretable
marginal effects. Analytic formulae of the log-likelihood and the corresponding
score function for absolute continuous and potentially non-normal responses
observed without censoring are available.
For censored and discrete observations, evaluation
of the likelihood requires evaluation of multivariate normal probabilities,
however, in low dimensions such that the approximation error can be made
arbitrarily small in reasonable time. Applications from different
domains presented in Section
3
highlight that the
methodology helps to unify models and inference procedures for clustered
observations across traditionally compartmentalised sub-disciplines of
statistics. An empirical evaluation of marginal effects and
distributions estimated by this novel approach is presented in
Section
4
.

2.1
Joint Transformation Models

To address these issues, we present a novel
transformation model for the joint distribution that provides simple
analytic expressions for marginal predictive distributions of the form
(
1
). In this setup,
i=1,\dots,N
independent observational units, each consisting of

N_{i}
correlated observations of the response
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}=(Y_{i1},\dots,Y_{iN_{i}})^{\top}\in\Xi^{N_{i}}
, are available for estimating the joint
distribution. While refraining to specify a certain
parametric joint multivariate distribution for
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
, we assume that
probabilities on the scale of a suitable
transformation
of

\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
can be evaluated using a multivariate normal distribution whose
structured covariance matrix captures the correlations between the
transformed elements of
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
. The aim of this paper is to simultaneously
estimate the transformation, regression coefficients, and the structured
covariance from data using models which emphasise predictive distributions
and parameter interpretability.

The non-decreasing transformation function
h:\Xi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
applied element-wise to the response vector
h_{N_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})=(h(Y_{i1}),\dots,h(Y_{iN_{i}}))^{\top}
ensuring that the same
transformation is applied to all
N_{i}
observations. Together with
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
, one
observes a corresponding matrix
\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}=(\text{\boldmath$x$}_{i1}\mid\dots\mid\text{\boldmath$x$}_{iN_{i}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}\times Q}
of full rank
containing treatment assignment or covariates whose corresponding
regression coefficients
\beta
are of interest. In addition, the design of the
experiment is described by a matrix
\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}=(\text{\boldmath$u$}_{i1}\mid\dots\mid\text{\boldmath$u$}_{iN_{i}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}\times R}
. We exclusively
study setups with simple cluster assignment encoded in this matrix (
\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}=(1)_{N_{i},1}
) or longitudinal data (
\text{\boldmath$u$}_{ij}=(1,t_{ij})
indicating that
Y_{ij}
for the
i
th subject was observed for at time

t_{ij}
).
We propose to study
models for the joint distribution function of
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
given
\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}

and
\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}
of the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}\leq\text{\boldmath$y$}\mid\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i},\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i})=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})}\left(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\Phi^{-1}_{N_{i}}(F_{N_{i}}\{\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}[h_{N_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$y$})-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}]\})\right).

(2)

Here,
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})}\left(\cdot\right)

is the distribution function of an
N_{i}
-dimensional
normal random vector with mean vector zero and structured covariance matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}):=\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}^{\top}+\text{\boldmath$I$}_{N_{i}}

as defined by the random effects design matrix and an unstructured Cholesky factor

\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
depending on
unknown variance parameters
\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}\in\mathbb{R}^{\nicefrac{{R(R+1)}}{{2}}}
;
\text{\boldmath$I$}_{N_{i}}
denotes the
N_{i}\times N_{i}
identity matrix.
We isolate the square roots of the diagonal elements of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})
in the
matrix
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})=\text{diag}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}))^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot\text{\boldmath$I$}_{N_{i}}=\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}^{\top}+\text{\boldmath$I$}_{N_{i}})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot\text{\boldmath$I$}_{N_{i}}
.
A positive-semidefinite covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})

is given under the constraint
\text{diag}(\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}))\geq\mathbf{0}_{R}
.
For the simple model with
\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}=(1,\dots,1)^{\top}
,
we have
\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})=\gamma_{1}
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})=(\gamma_{1}^{2})_{N_{i},N_{i}}+\text{\boldmath$I$}_{N_{i}}
, and
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}=(\gamma_{1}^{2}+1)^{-1}\cdot\text{\boldmath$I$}_{N_{i}}
is a scaling factor to the transformation function
h

and regression coefficients
\beta
which is instrumental for
the derivation of marginal distributions. In the longitudinal setup,

\mathbf{\Lambda}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\gamma_{1}&0\\
\gamma_{2}&\gamma_{3}\end{array}\right)

and the covariance
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})_{j,\jmath}
depends on the
observation times
t_{ij}
and
t_{i\jmath}
. The
key component is the shifted transformation
h_{N_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$y$})-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}
modelling the impact of the regression coefficients on the transformed
scale.

The transformation function
h
, the regression coefficients
\beta
,
and the variance parameters
\gamma
are unknowns to be estimated from
data. In (
2
),
\Phi_{N_{i}}^{-1}(\text{\boldmath$p$})=(\Phi^{-1}(p_{1}),\dots,\Phi^{-1}(p_{N_{i}}))^{\top}
applies the quantile function
\Phi^{-1}
of the
standard normal element-wise to some vector of probabilities
\text{\boldmath$p$}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{N_{i}})^{\top}\in(0,1)^{N_{i}}
. Furthermore,
F:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an a priori defined cumulative distribution function of some
absolute continuous distribution with log-concave density
f
;
F_{N_{i}}

and
f_{N_{i}}
are the element-wise applications of
F
and
f
,
respectively.

For absolute continuous responses
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}
, model
(
2
) implies that the latent variable

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}:=\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\Phi^{-1}_{N_{i}}(F_{N_{i}}\{\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}[h_{N_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}]\})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}

(3)

defined as an element-wise transformation of the observations
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}

follows a multivariate normal distribution
\text{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}\sim\ND_{N_{i}}\left(\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\right)
.
The model is distribution-free in the sense that for a baseline
configuration (with
\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}=\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}}
), such a transformation
into multivariate normality exists for all marginal distributions

(
Klein et al. 2022
)
. The model does, however, impose a certain
correlation structure through the choice of
\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}
. An example for the joint
distributions induced by increasing correlations among bivariate repeated
measurements with skewed marginal
distributions is given Figure
1
.

The key aspect of an implementation of model (
2
) is the
parameterisation of the transformation function as
h_{N_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$y$})=\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$y$})\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}
where
\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$y$})=(\text{\boldmath$a$}(y_{1})\mid\dots\mid\text{\boldmath$a$}(y_{N_{i}}))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}\times P}
is the matrix of
evaluated basis functions
\text{\boldmath$a$}:\Xi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{P}
.
Choices of basis functions
a
are problem-specific and
several options are discussed in Section
3
and, in more
detail, in
Hothorn et al. 2018
(
Hothorn et al. 2018
) and

Hothorn 2020
(
Hothorn 2020
).

2.2
Connection to Normal Linear Mixed-effects Models

We first consider the special case
F=\Phi
, where the
transformation of
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
simplifies to

\text{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}=h_{N_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}=\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}
.
Model (
2
) contains the LMM as a
special case. In its standard notation, the LMM reads

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}=\alpha+\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\tilde{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}+\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$R$}_{i}+\sigma\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{i}

(LMM)

with random effects
\text{\boldmath$R$}_{i}\sim\ND_{R}(\mathbf{0}_{R},\text{\boldmath$G$}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}))
, residuals
\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{i}\sim\ND_{N_{i}}(\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}},\text{\boldmath$I$}_{N_{i}})
under the assumption
\text{\boldmath$R$}_{i}\perp\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{i}
, intercept

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and residual standard deviation
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
The matrices
\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}
and
\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}
are typically referred to as “fixed
effects” and “random effects” design matrices in the literature.
This model can be reformulated as a model for the joint multivariate distribution

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}=\frac{\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}-\alpha-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\tilde{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}}{\sigma}=\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}\sigma^{-1}\text{\boldmath$R$}_{i}+\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{i}\sim\ND_{N_{i}}\left(\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}},\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}^{\top}+\text{\boldmath$I$}_{N_{i}}\right)

(4)

based on the relative covariance factorisation
\sigma^{-2}\text{\boldmath$G$}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})=\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
. This is model (
2
) with
F=\Phi
, linear
transformation
h_{N_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})=(\sigma^{-1}(Y_{i1}-\alpha),\dots,\sigma^{-1}(Y_{iN_{i}}-\alpha))^{\top}=\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}
with linear basis
functions
\text{\boldmath$a$}(y)=(y,-1)^{\top}
and parameters

\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}=(\sigma^{-1},\alpha\sigma^{-1})^{\top}
, and finally fixed effects

\text{\boldmath$\beta$}=\sigma^{-1}\tilde{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}
.

Using this notation, the conditional distribution
function of some element
y\in\Xi
of
Y
, conditional on
x
,
u
, and
unobservable random effects
\text{\boldmath$R$}=\text{\boldmath$r$}
, is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y\mid\text{\boldmath$x$},\text{\boldmath$u$},\text{\boldmath$r$})=\Phi\left(\text{\boldmath$a$}(y)^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}-\sigma^{-1}\text{\boldmath$u$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$r$}\right).

The marginal distribution of some element
y\in\Xi
of
Y
, which is
still conditional on
x
and
u
but integrates over the random effects

R
, can be obtained from the joint multivariate normal
(
4
) as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y\mid\text{\boldmath$x$},\text{\boldmath$u$})=\Phi\left(\frac{\text{\boldmath$a$}(y)^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}{\sqrt{\text{\boldmath$u$}^{\top}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$u$}+1}}\right).

The shrunken marginal fixed effects
\text{\boldmath$\beta$}/\sqrt{\text{\boldmath$u$}^{\top}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$u$}+1}
were also described by

Wu and Wang 2019
(
Wu and Wang 2019
) in a Bayesian implementation of this model.
Understanding the LMM as special case of a
transformation model allows to relax the normality assumption for
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
by
introducing non-linear transformation functions
h(y)=\text{\boldmath$a$}(y)^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}
defined by a non-linear basis
a

(
Hothorn et al. 2018
)
. Section
3.1

contains a comparison of the two models.
Probit GLMMs for binary responses
Y\in\Xi=\{0,1\}

can also be understood as a special case of a
transformation model with intercept
h(0)=\alpha
and
h(1)=\infty
.
Several implementations of such GLMMs are compared empirically to an
implementation motivated from a transformation model perspective in
Section
3.2
.

2.3
Distinction from Generalised Mixed-effects and Frailty Models

Two important extensions of the LMM include
GLMMs and frailty models. For binary
responses , the logistic GLMM
has the conditional, given normal random effects
r
, interpretation

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y=0\mid\text{\boldmath$x$},\text{\boldmath$u$},\text{\boldmath$r$})=\text{logit}^{-1}\left(\alpha+\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}+\text{\boldmath$u$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$r$}\right).

In survival analysis with
Y\in\Xi=\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a Weibull normal frailty model
leads to the conditional interpretation

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y\mid\text{\boldmath$x$},\text{\boldmath$u$},\text{\boldmath$r$})=\text{cloglog}^{-1}\left(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\log(y)+\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}+\text{\boldmath$u$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$r$}\right).

A normal frailty Cox model

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y\mid\text{\boldmath$x$},\text{\boldmath$u$},\text{\boldmath$r$})=\text{cloglog}^{-1}\left(h(y)+\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}+\text{\boldmath$u$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$r$}\right).

replaces the log-linear transformation function of the Weibull model
with a smooth log-cumulative hazard function
h(y)
. All three models are
special cases of mixed-effects transformation models (
tramME
).

Assuming normal random effects
u
, neither model can be understood in terms of model (
2
) and two
main difficulties are associated with these types of models assuming
additivity of the fixed and random effects on the log-odds ratio or
log-hazard ratio scales. First, unlike in the LMM (
LMM
), there is no analytic expression for the marginal
distribution and thus a marginal interpretation of the fixed effects

\beta
is difficult. Second, evaluation of the likelihood typically relies on a
Laplace approximation of the integral with respect to the random effects’
distribution and problems with this approximation have been reported,
for example by
Ogden 2015
(
Ogden 2015
). The novel multivariate transformation
model for clustered observations based on (
2
) addresses both of
these issues as shall be explained in the next subsections.

2.4
Transformation Models with Marginal Interpretation

Simple analytic expressions for the marginal distribution are available
(also for
F\neq\Phi
), independent of the choice of the basis function

a
, noting that the variance of the
j
th element of
\text{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}
(Equation
3
)
is
\text{\boldmath$u$}_{ij}^{\top}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$u$}_{ij}+1
.

The Gaussian copula distribution of Equation
2
directly implies the
marginal distribution function in form of a marginal transformation model
(mtram):

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y\mid\text{\boldmath$x$},\text{\boldmath$u$})

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(\frac{\sqrt{\text{\boldmath$u$}^{\top}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$u$}+1}\Phi^{-1}\left(F\left(\frac{\text{\boldmath$a$}(y)^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}{\sqrt{\text{\boldmath$u$}^{\top}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$u$}+1}}\right)\right)}{\sqrt{\text{\boldmath$u$}^{\top}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$u$}+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=F\left(\frac{\text{\boldmath$a$}(y)^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}{\sqrt{\text{\boldmath$u$}^{\top}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$u$}+1}}\right).

(mtram)

In this model, the fixed effects
\beta
divided by
\sqrt{\text{\boldmath$u$}^{\top}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$u$}+1}
are directly
interpretable given
\text{\boldmath$U$}=\text{\boldmath$u$}
, for example as log-odds ratios (
F=\text{logit}^{-1}
) or
log-hazard ratios (
F=\text{cloglog}^{-1}
). Because
\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}
is positive semidefinite, there might be a reduction
in effect size when comparing the fixed effects
\beta
from
formula (
2
) to the marginal effects
\text{\boldmath$\beta$}/\sqrt{\text{\boldmath$u$}^{\top}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$u$}+1}
from model (
mtram
).
For repeated measurements with

\text{\boldmath$u$}=1
we get a constant reduction by
1/\sqrt{\gamma_{1}^{2}+1}
. In
longitudinal models , the marginal effect at time
t
is
\text{\boldmath$\beta$}/\sqrt{\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}t+(\gamma_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{3}^{2})t^{2}+1}
because
\text{\boldmath$u$}=(1,t)^{\top}
. For positively correlated random intercepts and random slopes
(
i.e.

\gamma_{2}>0
), the marginal effect always decreases over time.

2.5
The Likelihood Function

For parameters
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$}
, and
\gamma
, the
log-likelihood contribution
\ell_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})
of the

i
th subject or cluster is based on the transformation

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})=\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\Phi^{-1}_{N_{i}}(F_{N_{i}}\{\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}[\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$y$})\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}]\})

(5)

of some
\text{\boldmath$y$}\in\Xi^{N_{i}}
.

For discrete or interval-censored
observations
(\underaccent{\bar}{\yvec}_{i},\bar{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}
the
log-likelihood contribution is

\displaystyle\ell_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{P}\left(\underaccent{\bar}{\yvec}_{i}\leq\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}<\bar{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}\right)=\log\mathbb{P}\left(\text{\boldmath$z$}(\underaccent{\bar}{\yvec}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\leq\text{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}<\text{\boldmath$z$}(\bar{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\right)

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left\{\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})}\left[\text{\boldmath$z$}(\underaccent{\bar}{\yvec}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}),\text{\boldmath$z$}(\bar{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\right]\right\}

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})}(\underaccent{\bar}{\zvec},\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}})=\int_{\underaccent{\bar}{\zvec}}^{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}_{\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})}(\text{\boldmath$z$})\,d\text{\boldmath$z$}

is the integral over the
N_{i}
-dimensional multivariate normal density

\text{\boldmath$\phi$}_{N_{i}}
with mean zero and covariance
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}
. The structure of

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})
can be exploited to dramatically reduce the
dimensionality of the integration problem. Applying the procedure by

Marsaglia 1963
(
Marsaglia 1963
), one can reduce this
N_{i}
-dimensional integral to an

R
-dimensional integral over the unit cube (see Appendix
A
).

For continuous observations
\text{\boldmath$y$}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}
, it is common practice

(
Lindsey 1999
, Section 5,)
to
approximate this log-likelihood by a log-density evaluated at the observations

\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}
:

\displaystyle\ell_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log\left|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\right|+

(7)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}-\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-2})\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})+

\displaystyle\quad\log_{N_{i}}(f_{N_{i}}\{\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}[\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i})\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}]\})^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N_{i}}+

\displaystyle\quad\log_{N_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\prime}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i})\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$})^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N_{i}}

where the Cholesky factorisation
\text{\boldmath$L$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\text{\boldmath$L$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})
is utilised. It should be noted that the exact
log-likelihood function (
6
) does not require the precision matrix

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}
to be computed.
In the above approximation,

\log_{N_{i}}
is the element-wise natural logarithm and
f_{N_{i}}
the
element-wise density of
F
.
\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\prime}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i})
denotes the matrix of
evaluated derivatives
\text{\boldmath$a$}^{\prime}
of the basis function
a
. The
log-likelihood of Equation
7
is derived in Appendix
B
.

Using either log-likelihood, we obtain simultaneous maximum-likelihood
estimates for all model parameters from

\displaystyle(\hat{\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}_{N},\hat{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{N},\hat{\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{N})=\argmax_{(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\in\mathbb{R}^{P+Q+M}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}).

Some models require additional constraints on
\vartheta
to be implemented

(
Hothorn et al. 2018
)
. Analytic score functions for
all model parameters
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$}
, and
\gamma
are available (see
Appendix
B
). Score functions for the discrete or censored
likelihood (
6
) and the observed Fisher information matrices for
both likelihoods are obtained numerically. The full parameterisation of
h

allows application of standard results for likelihood asymptotics

(
van der Vaart 1998
)
to independent observations
(
Hothorn et al. 2018
)
.
Because the model (
2
) is a special case of the multivariate
transformation model of
Klein et al. 2022
(
Klein et al. 2022
) where the transformation
function
h
and the fixed effects
\beta
are constrained to be the same
for all “coordinates” of the random vector
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
(that is, observations in the
same cluster). Therefore, model (
2
) benefits from the same asymptotic results
reported by
Klein et al. 2022
(
Klein et al. 2022
).

comparisons where model (
mtram

3.1
Non-normal Mixed-effects Models

The average reaction times to a specific task over several days of sleep
deprivation are given for
i=1,\dots,N=18
subjects

(
Belenky et al. 2003
)
. The data are often used to illustrate
LMMs with correlated random intercepts and slopes of the form
(
LMM
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{Reaction time}\leq y\mid\text{day},i)=\Phi\left(\frac{y-\alpha-\beta\text{day}-\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i}\text{day}}{\sigma}\right),(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})\sim\ND_{2}(\mathbf{0},\text{\boldmath$G$}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})).

(8)

This conditional normal model can be estimated by maximising the
corresponding normal log-likelihood and distinct implementations of classical
normal linear mixed
models
(
Bates et al. 2015
,
LMM
, package
\pkg
lme4,)
,
conditional mixed-effects transformation models
(
Tamási and Hothorn 2021
,
tramME
, package

\pkg
tramME,)
, and marginal transformation
models
(
Hothorn et al. 2022
,
mtram
, package
\pkg
tram,)
provide identical results
(in-sample log-likelihood
-875.97
).

Because the reaction times can hardly be expected to follow a symmetric
distribution, we consider the non-normal conditional and marginal
transformation model

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{Reaction}\leq y\mid\text{day},i)=\Phi\left(h(y)-\beta\text{day}-\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i}\text{day}\right),(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})\sim\ND_{2}(\mathbf{0},\text{\boldmath$G$}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}))

(9)

where a monotonically increasing transformation function
h(y)
is
allowed to deviate from linearity.
Such probit-type mixed-effects models have been studied before,
e.g.
by merging a
Box-Cox power transformation
h
with a grid-search over REML estimates

(
Gurka et al. 2006
)
, a conditional likelihood

(
Hutmacher et al. 2011
)
, or a grid-search maximising the profile
likelihood
(
Maruo et al. 2017
)
. Recently,
Tang et al. 2018
(
Tang et al. 2018
) and
Wu and Wang 2019
(
Wu and Wang 2019
)
proposed a monotone spline parameterisation of
h
in a Bayesian context.

We parameterise
h(y)=\text{\boldmath$a$}(y)^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}
in terms of a
monotonically increasing polynomial in Bernstein form of order six

(
Hothorn et al. 2018
)
. The conditional transformation model

(
Tamási et al. 2022
)
can be estimated by maximising a Laplace
approximation to the log-likelihood

(
Tamási and Hothorn 2021
)
simultaneously with respect to
all parameters
\vartheta
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
. Direct optimisation
of the log-likelihood (
7
) for the
marginal transformation model (
mtram
) leads to identical results (log-likelihood

-859.55
), because the conditional and marginal models are identical for

F=\Phi
and the Laplace approximation is
very accurate in this case. For

F\neq\Phi
, conditional and marginal transformation models differ, and numerical integration with respect to the normal random
effects is required when marginal distributions shall be obtained from a
conditional model. In contrast, the marginal transformation model (
mtram
)
provides a closed-form expression for marginal distributions for all choices
of
F
. With
F=\text{logit}^{-1}
, the log-likelihood of the marginal
model increases slightly (
-860.6377
).

The daily marginal distribution
functions of normal and non-normal models are compared to the daily marginal empirical
cumulative distributions in Figure
2
. Especially for
short reaction times early in the experiment, the non-normal transformation
models seem to fit the data better than the normal linear model. Between the
probit transformation model and the logistic marginal transformation model, only minor
discrepancies can be observed.

3.4
Marginally Interpretable Survival Models

The CAO/ARO/AIO-04 randomised clinical trial

(
Rödel et al. 2015
)
compared Oxaliplatin added to
fluorouracil-based preoperative chemoradiotherapy and postoperative
chemotherapy for rectal cancer patients to the same therapy using fluorouracil only.
Patients were randomised in the two treatment arms by block
randomisation taking the study centre, the lymph node involvement (negative
vs. positive), and tumour grading (T1-3 vs. T4) into account. The primary
endpoint was disease-free survival, defined as the time between
randomisation and non-radical surgery of the primary tumour (R2 resection),
locoregional recurrence after R0/1 resection, metastatic disease or
progression, or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. The
observed responses are a mix of exact dates (time to death or incomplete
removal of the primary tumour), right-censoring (end of follow-up or
drop-out), and interval-censoring (local or distant metastases).
The conditional hazard ratio
0.79

(0.64,0.98)
was reported as obtained from
a Cox mixed-effects model with normal
random intercepts and without stratification fitted to right-censored
survival times
(
Rödel et al. 2015
)
. This means that a rectal
cancer patient treated with
the novel combination therapy benefits from a
21\%
risk reduction
compared to a patient
from the same block
treated with fluorouracil
only.

We were interested in estimating a marginally interpretable treatment
effect
(acknowledging the fact that patients enrolled into the trial were

not
a random sample from all rectal cancer patients) based on a
marginally interpretable stratified (with respect to lymph node involvement
and tumour grading) Weibull model for clustered observations (blocks) in the
presence of interval-censored survival times. This model can be formulated
by (
mtram
) choosing
F=\text{cloglog}^{-1}
,
\text{\boldmath$a$}(y)=(1,\log(y))^{\top}
,
\text{\boldmath$u$}=1
being the block indicator, and variance
parameter
\gamma_{1}
(corresponding to the correlation structure of a random
intercept only model) as well as a treatment parameter
\beta

(comparing the novum to fluorouracil only). Stratification was implemented
by strata-specific parameters
\vartheta
for each of the four strata. It
should be noted that this model is not equivalent to a classical Weibull
normal frailty model.

A confidence interval for the marginal hazard ratio
\exp(\beta/\sqrt{\gamma_{1}^{2}+1})
was computed by simulating from the joint normal
distribution of
(\hat{\beta},\hat{\gamma}_{1})
. With a relatively small

\hat{\gamma}_{1}=0.15
(with standard error
0.13
), this resulted in a marginal
hazard ratio of
0.80
(
95\%
confidence interval
[0.65;0.98]
),
meaning that rectal cancer patients treated with
the combination therapy benefit from a
20\%
risk reduction
on average
.

By relaxing the Weibull assumption (log-linear transformation
h
) to a Cox proportional hazards model
(nonlinear transformation
h
), we obtain a
hazard ratio of
0.78
(
95\%
confidence interval
[0.64;0.96]
)
and a marginal probabilistic index of

0.56
(
95\%
confidence interval
[0.51;0.61]
), meaning that over all study centres, a randomly selected patient
receiving Oxaliplatin has a
56\%
probability of staying disease-free
longer than a randomly
selected patient receiving the standard treatment only, given that they both
have the same lymph node involvement and tumour grading.

4
Empirical Evaluation

Practitioners interested in inference for marginal effects will likely apply
some form of GEE estimation when analysing a binary response, or might
integrate over random effects in a conditional mixed-effects model for more
complex response distributions. In this section, we assess the quality of
likelihood-based marginal transformation inference (model
mtram
)

5
Discussion

There is a difference between a marginal and a marginally interpretable
model. A marginal model, for example defined by generalised estimation
equations
(
Zeger et al. 1988
)
, does not specify the joint distribution.
A marginally interpretable model is a model for the joint or conditional
(given random effects) distribution from which one can infer the marginal
distribution
(
Lee and Nelder 2004
)
. The models proposed here follow the
latter approach with the important distinctive feature that very simple
expressions for the marginal distribution function are available. Thus,
there is no need to apply numerical integration to the joint or conditional
model formulation. In our view, model (
mtram
) is especially
attractive because it allows the interpretation of scaled regression
coefficients as marginal effects acting on the marginal predictive
distribution in terms of a log-odds ratio or a log-hazard ratio, for example.
The Gaussian copula approach for obtaining marginally interpretable models has
gained some interest in the last years

(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Masarotto and Varin 2012
)
, however, the simple framework
of transformation models allows estimation for a wide range of responses without
encountering computational burdens or challenges that other methods typically do.

Naturally, the questions arises which model is to be preferred: a marginal, a
conditional, or a marginally interpretable one? In this case, the “right” model
is not the model which most closely reflects the data generating process, which
is usually unknown, but rather the model that allows the user to answer the
research question at hand by interpreting the estimated parameters, as

McGee and Stringer 2022
(
McGee and Stringer 2022
) point out. An advantage of transformation models is
that besides allowing for interpretation of the fixed-effects on a marginal level,
they also yield valid models for the whole marginal
distribution (
1
) of the response given the covariates.
An advantage of marginalised multilevel models
(
Heagerty and Zeger 2000
)

over marginal transformation models is that the former models are
parameterised in terms of marginal effects of interest, whereas effect
shrinkage is part of the latter models. The distribution-free nature,
general applicability to all types of responses, and the relative computational
simplicity are, in our opinion, attractive features of transformation models compared to
marginalised multilevel models.

The models and estimation procedures introduced here are limited by some
practical and some conceptual constraints. Response-varying regression
coefficients
\text{\boldmath$\beta$}(y)
define distribution regression models

(
Foresi and Peracchi 1995
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2013
)
where corresponding
mixed-effects models have been presented recently

(
Garcia et al. 2019
)
. This would be relatively straightforward
to implement in the framework presented here, in fact, stratification in
Weibull models was parameterised in a similar way.
A mix of continuous and
censored observations within one cluster would require to compute the
likelihood by partial integration over an
N_{i}
-dimensional normal, this is
currently not implemented. On a more conceptual level, it seems impossible
to implement multilevel models for discrete or censored responses, because
the likelihood (
7
) is only defined for contributions by
independent clusters.

Appendix A
Likelihood Function: Censored and Discrete Case

The
i
th contribution to the likelihood (
6
)is given by the

N_{i}
-dimensional normal integral

\displaystyle\exp(\ell_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}))=\int_{\text{\boldmath$z$}(\underaccent{\bar}{\yvec}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})}^{\text{\boldmath$z$}(\bar{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}_{N_{i}}\left(\text{\boldmath$z$},\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}},\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}^{\top}+\text{\boldmath$I$}_{N_{i}}\right)\,d\text{\boldmath$z$}.

With
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})=\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}^{\top}+\text{\boldmath$I$}_{N_{i}})\cdot\text{\boldmath$I$}_{N_{i}}

we obtain the corresponding correlation matrix as

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$C$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\text{\boldmath$V$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\text{\boldmath$V$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}+\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}\times N_{i}}

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$V$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}\times R}

and the integration limits become (again with
\text{\boldmath$z$}()
defined in (
5
))

\displaystyle\underaccent{\bar}{\zvec}=\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\text{\boldmath$z$}(\underaccent{\bar}{\yvec}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\text{ and }\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}=\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\text{\boldmath$z$}(\bar{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}).

According to
Marsaglia 1963
(
Marsaglia 1963
), the above normal probability can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{\underaccent{\bar}{\zvec}}^{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}_{N_{i}}\left(\text{\boldmath$z$},\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}},\text{\boldmath$C$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\right)\,d\text{\boldmath$z$}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{R}}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}_{R}(\text{\boldmath$w$},\mathbf{0}_{R},\text{\boldmath$I$}_{R})\int_{\underaccent{\bar}{\zvec}-\text{\boldmath$V$}\text{\boldmath$w$}}^{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}-\text{\boldmath$V$}\text{\boldmath$w$}}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}_{N_{i}}\left(\text{\boldmath$y$},\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}},\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}\right)\,d\text{\boldmath$w$}d\text{\boldmath$y$}

and can, following
Genz and Bretz 2009
(
Genz and Bretz 2009
) here, further be simplified to

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{R}}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}_{R}(\text{\boldmath$w$},\mathbf{0}_{R},\text{\boldmath$I$}_{R})\prod_{\imath=1}^{N_{i}}\left[\Phi\left(\frac{\bar{z}_{\imath}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}v_{\imath r}w_{r}}{\sqrt{d_{i}}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{z}_{\imath}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}v_{\imath r}w_{r}}{\sqrt{d_{i}}}\right)\right]d\text{\boldmath$w$}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\boldmath$w$}=\Phi^{-1}_{R}(\text{\boldmath$q$})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{R}}\prod_{\imath=1}^{N_{i}}\left[\Phi\left(\frac{\bar{z}_{\imath}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}v_{\imath r}\Phi^{-1}(q_{r})}{\sqrt{d_{\imath}}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{z}_{\imath}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}v_{\imath r}\Phi^{-1}(q_{r})}{\sqrt{d_{\imath}}}\right)\right]d\text{\boldmath$q$}.

The elements
d_{\imath}
are the diagonal elements of
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}

and thus standardisation of
z
and
\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})

cancel out in this case such that we get

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{R}}\prod_{\imath=1}^{N_{i}}\left[\Phi\left(\tilde{\bar{z}}_{\imath}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}\tilde{v}_{\imath r}\Phi^{-1}(q_{r})\right)-\Phi\left(\tilde{\underaccent{\bar}{z}}_{\imath}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}\tilde{v}_{\imath r}\Phi^{-1}(q_{r})\right)\right]d\text{\boldmath$q$}

with
\tilde{\bar{z}}_{\imath}
and
\tilde{\underaccent{\bar}{z}}_{\imath}
being the
elements of
\text{\boldmath$z$}(\bar{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})
and

\text{\boldmath$z$}(\underaccent{\bar}{\yvec}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})
, respectively, and

\tilde{v}_{\imath r}
are the elements of
\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})
.

The latter expression is an
R
-dimensional integral over the
unit cube (random intercept models have
R=1
and correlated
random intercept/random slope models correspond to
R=3
) of
products of univariate normal probabilities. It should be noted that,
unlike using an Laplace or other approximation of the likelihood, the above
term is the exact likelihood contribution. It can be approximated up to any
desired accuracy using numerical integration procedures. An analytic
expression for the score function seems quite challenging and one thus has
to rely on numerical approaches such as sparse grids
(
Heiss and Winschel 2008
)
.

Appendix B
Likelihood and Score Function: Continuous Case

The joint probability of
\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}\leq\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i},\text{\boldmath$U$}_{i})=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{0}_{N_{i}},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})}\left(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\Phi^{-1}_{N_{i}}\left(F_{N_{i}}\{\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}[\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i})\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}]\}\right)\right).

To simplify the notation, we define
\text{\boldmath$z$}()
as in (
5
):

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})=\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\Phi^{-1}_{N_{i}}(F_{N_{i}}\{\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}[\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i})\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}]\}).

We can derive the corresponding joint density for an arbitrary
F
:

\displaystyle f_{\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((2\pi)^{N_{i}}\left|\text{\boldmath$L$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\text{\boldmath$L$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\right|\right)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\times

\displaystyle\quad\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$L$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\times

\displaystyle\quad\prod_{\imath=1}^{N_{i}}\frac{\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})_{\imath\imath}f\{(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1})_{\imath\imath}[\text{\boldmath$a$}(y_{i\imath})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}]\}}{\phi(\Phi^{-1}\left(F\{\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}[\text{\boldmath$a$}(y_{i\imath})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}]\}\right))}\,(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1})_{\imath\imath}\,\text{\boldmath$a$}^{\prime}(y_{i\imath})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\text{\boldmath$L$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\text{\boldmath$L$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\right|^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\times

\displaystyle\quad\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$L$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\times

\displaystyle\quad\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\times

\displaystyle\quad\prod_{\imath=1}^{N_{i}}f\{(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1})_{\imath\imath}[\text{\boldmath$a$}(y_{i\imath})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}]\}\,\text{\boldmath$a$}^{\prime}(y_{i\imath})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\text{\boldmath$L$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\text{\boldmath$L$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\right|^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\times

\displaystyle\quad\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1}\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\right)\times

\displaystyle\quad\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-2}\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})\right)\times

\displaystyle\quad\prod_{\imath=1}^{N_{i}}f\{(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})^{-1})_{\imath\imath}[\text{\boldmath$a$}(y_{i\imath})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}-\text{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}]\}\,\text{\boldmath$a$}^{\prime}(y_{i\imath})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}.

The resulting log-likelihood contribution (
7
) for the
i
th observation is given by:

\displaystyle\ell_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\log(f_{\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{i}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}))

\displaystyle=
