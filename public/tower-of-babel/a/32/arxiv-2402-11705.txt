Learning Memory Kernels in Generalized Langevin Equations
Quanjun Lang, Jianfeng Lu

Abstract
We introduce a novel approach for learning memory kernels in Generalized Langevin Equations. This approach initially utilizes a regularized Prony method to estimate correlation functions from trajectory data, followed by regression over a Sobolev norm-based loss function with RKHS regularization. Our method guarantees improved performance within an exponentially weighted L 2 L^{2} space, with the kernel estimation error controlled by the error in estimated correlation functions. We demonstrate the superiority of our estimator compared to other regression estimators that rely on L 2 L^{2} loss functions and also an estimator derived from the inverse Laplace transform, using numerical examples that highlight its consistent advantage across various weight parameter selections. Additionally, we provide examples that include the application of force and drift terms in the equation.

Contents

1
Introduction

The Generalized Langevin Equation (GLE) is a widely used model for coarse-grained particles, which was first proposed by Mori
[
Mor65
]
and Zwanzig
[
Zwa61
]
. In their formalism, many irrelevant degrees of freedom in the dynamics are projected, left with a memory term and a strongly correlated random noise. Such a coarse-graining simplification makes direct computation feasible. More examples of derivations can be found in
[
CS06
,
Li10
]
. The GLE is particularly useful for studying complex systems such as biomolecules
[
GKC09
]
, climate
[
FH77
]
, chemistry
[
XCE24
]
, physics
[
Lü+19
]
, and quantum dynamics simulation
[
Dam+09
]
, where
the interactions depend not only on the current state but also on the sequence of past states.

Because of the wide interest in modeling, many efforts have been devoted to learning the memory kernel. Still, it can be difficult to obtain, even assuming the full observation of the system. For example, the memory kernel is approximated by a rational function, thus reducing the model complexity

[
LBL16
,
Gro+20
]
; by introducing auxiliary variables based on Prony’s estimation of the autocorrelation function
[
Boc+21
]
; by data-driven model reduction methods
[
LL21
,
LLC16
]
; and by an iterative algorithm which ensures accurate reproduction of the correlation functions
[
JHS17
]
.

In estimating the memory kernel, the above methods empirically balance the computation cost and accuracy and justify the performance using numerical examples. To give a theoretical guarantee of the estimator, we address the ill-posed nature of this inverse problem, as the memory kernel satisfies the Volterra equation of the first kind. Solving these equations using various regularization methods has been studied in
[
Lam00
,
Lam95
,
LE97
,
Lam97
]
. Learning memory kernel by constructing loss function based on the Volterra of the first kind has been considered in
[
Rus+22
,
Ker+23
]
.

Our major contribution is an algorithm with a performance guarantee, depending on the estimation accuracy of the correlation functions, through constructing a Sobolev loss function. The correlation functions are estimated by a regularized Prony method, which empirically preserves the accuracy of the derivatives under the smoothness assumption. The performance is examined in an
L^{2}(\rho)
space, where
\rho
is an exponential decaying measure representing our region of interest in our estimation. Using the Prony method to estimate correlation functions allows for the derivation of the memory kernel via a direct inverse Laplace transform. However, this estimation approach does not offer a guaranteed level of performance and is primarily used as a benchmark for comparison. We also compare the performance of the proposed estimator with other regression estimators based on
L^{2}
loss functions, showing that the proposed estimator has better performance across different scales of
\rho
. In the end, we give examples with the presence of force term
F
and a drift term in the equation, demonstrating the necessity of using ensemble trajectory data when the solution is not stationary.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we give preliminaries of the problem statement and introduce the notations. We propose the main algorithm in Section
3
. The identifiability and convergence of our estimator are proved in Section
4
, and the performance is demonstrated by numerical examples in Section
5
. We conclude in Section
6
.

2
Preliminary

2.1
Generalized Langevin equations (GLE)

In this paper, we restrict the discussion to one dimension and leave the generalization to high dimensions for future work. We consider the following GLE.

mv^{\prime}(t)=F(v(t))-\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(t-s)v(s)ds+R(t),

(2.1)

where
v:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the velocity of a macroparticle,
m
represents its mass which is assumed to be 1 in the rest of our discussion, and
F
represents
an external force term
. The memory kernel
\gamma:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is assumed to be continuous and integrable, and
R(t)
is a stationary Gaussian process with zero mean, satisfying the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, so that

\langle{R(t)R(s)}\rangle=\frac{1}{\beta}\gamma(t-s),

(2.2)

where
\beta
is the inverse temperature. We aim to estimate the memory kernel
\gamma
from the discrete observations of trajectory data
v
.

Multiply
v(0)
to the (
2.1
) and take expectations, we obtain

g(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(t-s)h(s)ds=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(s)h(t-s)ds,

(2.3)

where

\displaystyle h(t):=\langle{v(t)v(0)}\rangle,\ \ \ \varphi(t):=\langle{F(v(t))v(0)}\rangle,

(2.4)

so that

\displaystyle g(t):=\langle{v^{\prime}(t)v(0)}\rangle-\langle{F(v(t))v(0)}\rangle=-h^{\prime}(t)+\varphi(t).

(2.5)

and
\langle{R(t)v(0)}\rangle
is assumed to be 0 as in
[
CLL14
]
. In the case that the corresponding solution
v
is a stationary ergodic Gaussian process with zero means,
h(t)
defines the autocorrelation function of
v
, namely
h(t-s)=\langle{v(t)v(s)}\rangle
, and we call
g
the force correlation function.
Throughout the following discussion, we will assume that
g
,
\varphi
, and
h
are smooth.
For the special case
F=0
, we have
g(t)=-h^{\prime}(t)
. When
F(v)=-\mu v
, this becomes
g(t)=-h^{\prime}(t)+\mu h(t)
, recovering the standard GLE with linear friction
\mu
and no external potential.

Although the commonly used GLE formulation applies the force term to the position variable, here we intentionally consider a simplified first-order model where the force acts on the velocity. This setting allows us to focus on the identifiability of the memory kernel while also illustrating cases where the dynamics are non-stationary, such as when the force is nonlinear in
v
. Nevertheless, we point out that velocity-dependent potentials
[
Puc04
]
appear in the Lorentz force
[
BMO17
]
and Nuclear physics
[
RFL62
]
.

Remark 2.1
.

If
v
is not stationary, we cannot approximate
h
by the autocorrelation. Such a case might appear with the presence of a nonlinear force
F
and an extra drift term. Therefore, ensemble trajectory data is necessary to construct the correlation functions
h
and
g
. The details will be illustrated in Section
5
.

2.2
Volterra equations

Note that the equation (
2.3
) is a convolutional Volterra equation of the first kind, which is known to be ill-posed, meaning a small error of
h
and
g
will cause an arbitrarily large error in the estimated
\gamma
. In the following discussion, we use the notation

(\gamma*h)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(t-s)h(s)ds=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(s)h(t-s)ds.

(2.6)

Taking the derivative with
t
, the first kind Volterra equation (
2.3
) becomes a second kind, namely

g^{\prime}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(t-s)h^{\prime}(s)ds+h(0)\gamma(t)=(\gamma*h^{\prime})(t)+h(0)\gamma(t).

(2.7)

For the case that
h(0)\neq 0
, this equation is well-posed, therefore shaping our thoughts of constructing the loss function.
The ill-posedness of the equation (
2.3
) is because of the zero spectrum in the convolution operator, resulting in an unbounded inverse. However, when
h(0)\neq 0
, the spectrum of the operator in equation (
2.7
) has a lower bound
h(0)
, making its inverse bounded. Many efforts have been devoted to providing regularization techniques for solving the Volterra equation of the first kind
[
Lam00
,
Lam95
,
LE97
,
Lam97
]
.

2.3
Laplace transform

The Laplace transform of
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
is defined as

\mathcal{L}[f]=\widehat{f}(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)e^{-zt}dt.

(2.8)

The integral is a proper Lebesgue integral for complex domain parameters
z=\omega+i\tau
such that
\omega\geq 0
. A stronger assumption is that
f
has exponential decay,

\left|f(t)\right|\leq Ce^{-\sigma t}

(2.9)

for some
C,\sigma>0
. In later discussions, we assume that the memory kernel
\gamma
and the correlation function
h
,
\varphi
, and
g
have exponential decay.

Lemma 2.2

(Plancherel Theorem for Laplace transform)
.

Suppose
f
has exponential decay. Then the following equality holds for
\omega\geq 0
.

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left|\widehat{f}(w+i\tau)\right|^{2}d\tau=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2wt}\left|f(t)\right|^{2}dt.

(2.10)

Proof.

From the definition of Laplace transform,

\widehat{f}(\omega+i\tau)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-it\tau}e^{-\omega t}f(t)dt.

(2.11)

Notice that
\widehat{f}(\omega+i\cdot)
is the Fourier transform of
e^{-wt}f(t)
by extending
f=0
for
t\leq 0
. Using the assumption that
f
has exponential decay, the extended function
e^{-wt}f(t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for
\omega\geq 0
, hence
\widehat{f}(\omega+i\cdot)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then the Plancherel theorem of Fourier transform provides an isometry of
f\in L^{2}(\rho)
to its Laplace transform in
L^{2}(R)
with a scaling constant
\frac{1}{2\pi}
, where the measure
\rho
has density
e^{-2\omega t}
.
∎

The Lemma
2.2
will be applied to derive the coercivity constant for the loss function defined later in Section
3
. Moreover, the Laplace transform can convert the convolution into multiplication in the frequency domain.

Lemma 2.3

(Laplace transform of convolution)
.

Suppose
f_{1},f_{2}
both have exponential decay, then

\mathcal{L}\left[\int_{0}^{t}f_{1}(t-s)f_{2}(s)ds\right]=\widehat{f_{1}}(z)\cdot\widehat{f}_{2}(z).

(2.12)

The inverse Laplace transform is given by the Bromwich integral,

f(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left[{\widehat{f}}(t)\right]=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{\omega-iT}^{\omega+iT}e^{zt}\widehat{f}(z)dz

(2.13)

where
\omega
is a real number such that
\omega
is on the right of all the poles in
\widehat{f}
. In particular, if
f
has exponential decay, we could take
\omega\geq 0
. See
[
Kuh13
]
for a review of numerical methods for inverse Laplace transform. Among those algorithms, the method of accelerated Fourier series method is widely used
[
dHKS82
]
.

Laplace transform is particularly useful when solving the Volterra equations thanks to Lemma
2.3
. Taking the Laplace transform on both sides of the Volterra equation (
2.3
), we have

\widehat{g}(z)=\widehat{\gamma}(z)\widehat{h}(z),\ \text{and }\gamma=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left[\dfrac{\widehat{g}}{\widehat{h}}\right]

(2.14)

However, the numerical inverse Laplace transform is severely unstable. See, for example,
[
ES08
]
for a detailed discussion on the ill-posedness of inverse Laplace transform. We avoid such ill-posedness by explicitly computing the inverse Laplace transform of exponential functions, and also by avoid the inverses Laplace transform by minimizing a well-posed loss function.

3
Proposed Method

We present the main method in this section. The data is assumed to be noisy and discrete observations of the stationary solution
v
of the GLE. We first estimate the correlation functions
h
,
\varphi
, and
g
using regularized Prony methods. Then we apply regression based on a quadratic loss derived from a weighted Sobolev norm. We also derive an estimator for comparison purposes, using the explicit inverse Laplace transform based on the Prony estimation of the correlation functions.

3.1
Observation data

Suppose we have the following discrete and noisy observation data

\mathbf{v}_{l}=v(\mathbf{t}_{l})+\bm{\xi}_{l}

(3.1)

where
\mathbf{t}_{l}=l\Delta t
,
l=1,\dots,L
and
\bm{\xi}_{l}
are i.i.d. Gaussian noise with mean 0 and standard deviation
\sigma_{obs}
, independent of the initial condition
v(0)
.
This setup corresponds to the first-order GLE model, where velocity is assumed to be directly observed. While in many experimental settings, position is more commonly measured, velocity measurements are also feasible. For instance, Doppler-based techniques, such as Laser Doppler Velocimetry (LDV)
[
Tab+13
]
and Doppler radar
[
ASS73
]
, provide direct access to velocity data and fall within our observation model. If only positional observations are available, we could estimate the velocity using finite difference or interpolation and subsequently use it to compute the correlation functions.

Under the assumption that the solution
v
is ergodic, the autocorrelation functions
h
defined as in (
2.4
) equals to the temporal average of
v
,

h(\tau)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}v(t)v(t+\tau)dt.

(3.2)

The above integral can be approximated by a discrete sum,

\mathbf{h}_{n}=\frac{1}{L-n}\sum_{l=1}^{L-n}\mathbf{v}_{l}\mathbf{v}_{l+n},

(3.3)

and taking into account (
3.1
),

\mathbf{h}_{n}=\frac{1}{L-n}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L-n}v(\mathbf{t}_{l})v(\mathbf{t}_{l+n})+\sum_{l=n}^{L-n}v(\mathbf{t}_{l})\mathbb{\xi}_{l+n}+\sum_{l=1}^{L-n}\mathbb{\xi}_{l}v(\mathbf{t}_{l+n})+\sum_{l=1}^{L-n}\mathbb{\xi}_{l}\mathbb{\xi}_{l+n}\right).

(3.4)

Due to the independence of
\bm{\eta}_{n}
, the second and third term converges to
0
as
M
approaches infinity because of the central limit theorem, and the last term converges to 0 except for the case
n=0
. As a result, the
\mathbf{h}_{0}
is an estimation of
h(0)+\sigma_{obs}
. Moreover, when the memory kernel is integrable and continuous, the autocorrelation function
h
is differentiable with

h^{\prime}(0)=0
, see for example
[
BB13
]
.
In our subsequent discussion, we impose this condition and eliminate the estimation
\mathbf{h}_{0}
, focusing solely on the data
\{\mathbf{h}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
.

3.2
Regularized Prony method

The well-known Prony method assumes the target function
h
is a summation of exponential functions
[
HDS90
]
. It transfers the nonlinear parameter estimation into two linear regressions and a root-finding problem. For completeness, we briefly discuss the Prony method and introduce our regularization methods according to our assumptions.

Given discrete estimations
\{\mathbf{h}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, we aim to find
(\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathbf{r}_{k})_{k=1}^{p^{\prime}}
, so that

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\min_{\{\mathbb{w}_{k}\}}\left\|\mathbf{h}_{n}-\sum_{k=1}^{p{\prime}}\mathbb{w}_{k}\mathbf{r}_{k}^{n}\right\|^{2},}

(3.5)

where
\mathbf{w}_{k}
are the amplitudes,
\mathbf{r}_{k}
are the exponential of complex frequencies. Choosing the number of frequencies
{p^{\prime}}
and appropriate observation length
N
is another problem of interest. The choice of
N
reflects the precision of estimated
\mathbf{h}_{n}
, as larger values of
n
correspond to smaller sample sizes
L-n
, leading to increased estimation errors. The balance between increasing the number of estimation grids and maintaining estimation accuracy is achieved through careful consideration of the parameter
N
. We refer to
[
CM92
]
for a singular value decomposition approach in selecting the optimal parameter
N
and
p^{\prime}
, and
[
Boc+21
]
for numerical comparisons. The classical case (
N=2{p^{\prime}}
) obtains an exact fit between the sampled data and the exponentials if the regression matrices defined later are not singular. However, in practical cases, we often require
N>2{p^{\prime}}
and solve linear systems using least squares.

Firstly we solve the following equation for coefficients
\mathbf{a}=(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{p^{\prime}})^{\top}
.

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{h}_{p^{\prime}}&\mathbf{h}_{{p^{\prime}}-1}&\cdots&\mathbf{h}_{1}\\
\mathbf{h}_{{p^{\prime}}+1}&\mathbf{h}_{{p^{\prime}}}&\cdots&\mathbf{h}_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{h}_{N-1}&\mathbf{h}_{N-2}&\cdots&\mathbf{h}_{N-{p^{\prime}}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{a}_{p^{\prime}}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{h}_{{p^{\prime}}+1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{h}_{N}\end{bmatrix}

(3.6)

Given the coefficients
\mathbb{a}
, we construct the characteristic polynomial,

\phi(z)=z^{p^{\prime}}+\mathbf{a}_{1}z^{{p^{\prime}}-1}+\cdots\mathbf{a}_{{p^{\prime}}-1}z+\mathbf{a}_{p^{\prime}}.

(3.7)

The second step is finding the roots of
\phi(z)
, denoted by
\{\mathbf{r}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{r}_{p^{\prime}}\}
.
The process of root finding is converted into a generalized eigenvalue problem known as the matrix pencil method
[
HS90
]
to enhance stability. Yet, this root-finding is still very sensitive to small changes in coefficients, which can lead to disproportionately large errors in the estimation of roots. See, for example, the Wilkinson’s polynomial
[
Wil59
]
. However, we emphasize that instead of precisely determining the parameters
(\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathbf{r}_{k})
, our primary objective is to achieve a nonparametric estimation of the function
h
minimizing the
L^{2}(\rho)
error, with a measure
\rho
to be defined later. Such a nonparametric goal justifies our following regularization steps. To incorporate with the assumption of exponential decay in
h
, we adjust the root
\mathbf{r}_{k}
when its absolute value exceeds the threshold
\sigma
of the exponential decaying assumption, namely

\widetilde{\mathbf{r}_{k}}=\frac{\mathbf{r}_{k}}{e^{\sigma}\left|\mathbf{r}_{k}\right|},\text{ if }\left|\mathbf{r}_{k}\right|\geq e^{-\sigma},\widetilde{\mathbf{r}_{k}}=\mathbf{r}_{k}\text{ otherwise }.

(3.8)

We then define the exponential modes

\bm{\lambda}_{k}=\frac{\text{Log}(\widetilde{\mathbf{r}_{k}})}{\Delta t}.

(3.9)

Remark 3.1
.

The above formula applies when
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{k}\notin\mathbb{R}^{-}
. If
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{-}
, it lies on the branch cut of the complex logarithm, and
\text{Log}(\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{k})
becomes multivalued. To solve this, we augment the parameters
\bm{\lambda}_{k}
to include the two values on both sides of the cutline
[
Boc+21
]
,
\log\left|\mathbf{r}_{k}\right|+\pi i
and
\log\left|\mathbf{r}_{k}\right|-\pi i
.

After rearranging, we have
\{{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{p}
, where
p\geq p^{\prime}
because the augmentation. In the final step of our process, we focus on determining the coefficients
\mathbf{w}
. The original Prony solves the linear system denoted by
\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{w}=\mathbb{h}
(Note that we dropped the estimation of
\mathbf{h}_{0}
),

\begin{bmatrix}e^{{\bm{\lambda}_{1}}\Delta t}&\dots&e^{{\bm{\lambda}_{p}}\Delta t}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
e^{{\bm{\lambda}_{1}}N\Delta t}&\cdots&e^{{\bm{\lambda}_{p}}N\Delta t}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{w}_{p}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{h}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{h}_{N}\end{bmatrix}

(3.10)

Given that the number of data points
N
exceeds
p
, we employ a least squares solution with constraints based on
h^{\prime}(0)=0
,

\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{k}{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}=0,

(3.11)

and RKHS regularization
[
LLA22
]
, which provides inherent smoothness constraints on
h
determined by the matrix
\mathbb{Z}
itself. The method we used to determine
\mathbf{w}
is concluded as

\mathbf{w}=\argmin_{\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{k}{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}=0}\left\|\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{h}\right\|^{2}+\lambda\left\|(\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{Z}^{\top})^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{w}\right\|^{2}

(3.12)

where
(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z})^{{\dagger}}
represents the pseudo inverse of
\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}
, and the last term in the above equation represents the RKHS norm derived from
\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}
and the optimal parameter
\lambda
is selected by L-curve method. See
[
LLA22
]
for more details.

Overall, our regularized Prony method distinguishes from the classic approach by omitting the biased estimation of
\mathbf{h}_{0}
, segregating multi-valued Prony modes, constraining decay speed and
h^{\prime}(0)
, and adding regularization to the coefficients.
Now the target function
h
is approximated by

\widetilde{h}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{k}e^{\bm{\lambda}_{k}t}.

(3.13)

The method is concluded in the Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Regularized Prony method

Input:
Trajectory
\{\mathbf{v}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
observed on discrete time grid
\{\mathbf{t}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
.

Output:
Estimated auto correlation function
\widetilde{h}.

1:

Estimate
\mathbf{h}
on discrete time grids using (
3.3
) and drop
\mathbf{h}_{0}
.

2:

Choose
N
and
p^{\prime}
and estimate the polynomial coefficients
\mathbf{a}
using (
3.6
).

3:

Find the roots
\mathbf{r}
of the characteristic polynomial
\phi
and regularize using (
3.8
).

4:

Augment the logarithm
\{\bm{\lambda}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{p}
of
\mathbf{r}
using Remark
3.1
.

5:

Determine the weight
\mathbf{w}
using least square with RKHS regularization (
3.12
), so that
\widetilde{h}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{k}e^{\bm{\lambda}_{k}t}
.

3.3
The force term
F

The process for estimating
g
follows a similar pattern, but with a distinction based on
F
. We divide the scenario into two cases: when
F
equals 0 and when it does not.

3.3.1
The case
F=0

From (
2.5
), we have
g(t)=-h^{\prime}(t)
. Take Laplace transform, we have
\widehat{g}(z)=h(0)-z\widehat{h}(z)
. Therefore the following relationship holds for the Laplace transforms of
\gamma
and
h
.

\widehat{\gamma}(z)=\frac{\widehat{g}(z)}{\widehat{h}(z)}=\frac{h(0)-z\widehat{h}(z)}{\widehat{h}(z)},\ \ \widehat{h}(z)=\frac{h(0)}{z+\widehat{\gamma}(z)}

(3.14)

With the Prony approximation
\widetilde{h}
derived from above, we firstly have a straightforward estimation of
g
,

\widetilde{g}(t)=-\widetilde{h}^{\prime}(t)=-\sum_{k=1}^{p}{\mathbf{w}_{k}\bm{\lambda}_{k}}e^{\bm{\lambda}_{k}t}

(3.15)

With
\widetilde{g}
ready for later use, here we briefly introduce a direct estimation of
\gamma
based on explicit inverse Laplace transform. Note that the Laplace transform of
\widetilde{h}
and
\widetilde{g}
are given by

\mathcal{L}[\widetilde{h}]=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{k}}{z-\bm{\lambda}_{k}},\ \mathcal{L}[\widetilde{g}]=-\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{k}\bm{\lambda}_{k}}{z-\bm{\lambda}_{k}},

(3.16)

Using (
3.14
), we can derive an estimator
\theta_{L}
of the memory kernel, whose Laplace transform is given by

\widehat{\theta_{L}}(z)=\frac{\mathcal{L}[-\widetilde{h^{\prime}}]}{\mathcal{L}[\widetilde{h}]}=\frac{-\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{k}\bm{\lambda}_{k}\prod_{j\neq k}{(z-\bm{\lambda}_{j})}}{\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{k}\prod_{j\neq k}{(z-\bm{\lambda}_{j})}}

(3.17)

Because of the constraints (
3.11
) derived from
\widetilde{h}^{\prime}(0)=0
, the coefficients of the
(p-1)^{th}
order term in the numerator is 0, therefore we can use fraction decomposition and explicit inverse Laplace transform,

\widehat{\theta_{L}}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{k}}{z-\bm{\eta}_{k}},\ \ \theta_{L}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\mathbf{u}_{k}e^{-\bm{\eta}_{k}t}

(3.18)

Remark 3.2
.

Suppose the true memory kernel
\gamma
is given by
\gamma(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{p}u_{k}e^{\eta_{k}t}
, then we have from (
3.14
),

\widehat{h}(z)=\frac{h(0)}{z+\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{u_{k}}{z-\eta_{k}}}=\frac{h(0)\prod_{k=1}^{p}(z-\eta_{k})}{z\prod_{k=1}^{p}(z-\eta_{k})+\sum_{k=1}^{p}u_{k}\prod_{j\neq k}(z-\eta_{j})}.

(3.19)

Notice that the numerator has degree
k
and the denominator has degree
k+1
. Then using fractional decomposition, we have

\widehat{h}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{p+1}\frac{w_{k}}{s-\lambda_{k}}.

(3.20)

with the parameters derived from the above equality. Therefore we obtain a relationship between a Prony-like memory kernel and its autocorrelation function
h
. This relationship will be applied in the numerical examples in Section
5
.

3.3.2
The case
F
is not 0

Note that in this case,
g=-h^{\prime}+\varphi
. Firstly we use the
\widetilde{h}^{\prime}
from (
3.15
).
Since
\varphi
is given by the ergodic assumption on
v
,

\varphi(\tau)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}v(t)F(v(t+\tau))dt,

(3.21)

we use the Riemann sum to approximate the integral,

\bm{\varphi}_{n}=\frac{1}{L-n}\sum_{l=1}^{L-n}\mathbf{v}_{l}F(\mathbf{v}_{l+n}),

(3.22)

and then use the regularized Prony method as concluded in the Algorithm
1
. The result is denoted by

\widetilde{\varphi}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\prime}e^{\bm{\lambda}_{k}^{\prime}t}

(3.23)

In conclusion, we have

\widetilde{g}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{p}{\mathbf{w}_{k}\bm{\lambda}_{k}}e^{{\bm{\lambda}_{k}}t}+\sum_{k=1}^{q}{\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\prime}}e^{{\bm{\lambda}_{k}^{\prime}}t}

(3.24)

Again we provide a brief discussion about estimating the memory kernel using explicit inverse Laplace transform. By equation (
3.14
),

\widehat{{\theta_{L}}}(z)=\frac{\mathcal{L}[-\widetilde{h^{\prime}}]+\mathcal{L}[\widetilde{\varphi}]}{\mathcal{L}[\widetilde{h}]}=\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{k}}{z-\bm{\eta}_{k}}+\frac{\left[\sum_{k=1}^{q}\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\prime}\prod_{j\neq k}{(z-\bm{\lambda}_{j}^{\prime})}\right]\prod_{i=1}^{p}{(z-\bm{\lambda}_{j})}}{\left[\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{k}\prod_{j\neq k}{(z-\bm{\lambda}_{j})}\right]\prod_{i=1}^{q}{(z-\bm{\lambda}_{j}^{\prime})}}

(3.25)

where the first term on the left-hand side is derived from equation (
3.18
).
Recall that
\varphi(0)=\left<F(v(0)),v(0)\right>
, we cannot have
\varphi(0)=0
as the case for
h^{\prime}(0)
. Hence in the second term, both the numerator and the denominator have degree
p+q-1
, and its fractional decomposition has a constant term
C=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{q}\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\prime}}{\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{k}}
, leading to
\delta_{0}(t)
, a Dirac function at 0 in the estimation
\theta_{L}
, which is approximated by a mollified Gaussian density function in practice. The final result after partial fraction decomposition and explicit inverse Laplace transform is

\widehat{\theta_{L}}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{q^{\prime}}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{k}}{z-\bm{\eta}_{k}}+C,\ \ \theta_{L}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{q^{\prime}}\mathbf{u}_{k}e^{\bm{\eta}_{k}t}+C\delta_{0}(t)

(3.26)

3.4
Sobolev norm loss function

Although we have the Prony-like estimator
\theta_{L}
, it is hard to give an analysis of its performance. Instead, with the
given estimation of
\widetilde{h}
and
\widetilde{g}
, we use least square to learn the memory kernel with the following loss function,

\mathcal{E}(\theta)=\left\|\widetilde{g}-(\theta*\widetilde{h})\right\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\rho)}

(3.27)

where
\theta
is a candidate kernel function,
\rho
denotes the measure on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
with density
e^{-2\omega t}
and
\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\rho)}
represents the squared Sobolev norm with scale parameter
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
which satisfies
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}>0
and
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=1
, so that

\left\|f\right\|_{H^{1}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\rho)}^{2}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\alpha_{1}\left|f(t)\right|^{2}+\alpha_{2}\left|f^{\prime}(t)\right|^{2}\right)d\rho(t).

(3.28)

The Sobolev norm provides coercivity with respect to the
L^{2}(\rho)
norm. The details will be introduced in Section
4
. The measure
\rho
can be considered as a weight of estimation. A large value of
\omega
indicates our focus on the value of the memory kernel near the origin and vice versa.
Using the basis
\left\{\psi_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
,
the least square problem becomes estimating the coefficients
\mathbf{c}=(\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{K})^{\top}
, so chat

\mathbf{c}=\argmin_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c}-2\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{c}.

(3.29)

where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, and

\mathbf{A}_{ij}=\langle{\psi_{i}*\widetilde{h},\psi_{j}*\widetilde{h}}\rangle_{H^{1}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\rho)},\text{ }\mathbf{b}_{i}=\langle{\psi_{i}*\widetilde{h},\widetilde{g}}\rangle_{H^{1}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\rho)}

(3.30)

We again use RKHS regularization
[
LLA22
]
as in Section
3.2
, so that

\mathbf{c}=\argmin_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c}-2\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{c}+\lambda\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{c}

(3.31)

Finally, the estimator of the memory kernel is given by
\theta=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{c}_{k}\psi_{k}
.
Our algorithm is concluded in Algorithm
2
.

4
Identifiability

In this section, we prove the coercivity of the loss defined in (
3.27
), and control the estimation error in
\gamma
by the error in the estimation of
h
and
g
. The technique we used here is the Plancherel Theorem of Laplace transform as in Lemma
2.10
.

4.1
Sobolev loss functions

Suppose
g
and
h
are the true correlation functions, and the true kernel
\gamma
is a solution for the equation (
2.3
) and (
2.7
), from which we substitute
g
and
g^{\prime}
into the loss function,

\mathcal{E}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\alpha_{1}\left|(\gamma-\theta)*h\right|^{2}+\alpha_{2}\left|\left((\gamma-\theta)*h\right)^{\prime}\right|^{2}\right)\rho(t)dt.

(4.1)

We will make two assumptions before we give an analysis of this loss function. Firstly, a decaying and smoothness assumption on the kernel
\gamma
and the observation data
h
and
g
.

Assumption 1
.

The true kernel
\gamma
, the candidate kernel
\theta
, the correlation functions
h
and
g
, and their noisy estimation
h_{\varepsilon}
and
g_{\varepsilon}
are smooth. These functions and their derivatives has exponential decay with parameter
\sigma>0
.

Let
\widehat{f}
be the Laplace transform of a function
f
. For
\omega>0
, denote that

M^{f}_{\omega}=\sup_{z=\omega+i\tau,\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\left(\alpha_{1}\left|\widehat{f}(z)\right|^{2}+\alpha_{2}\left|z\widehat{f}(z)\right|^{2}\right),\quad m^{f}_{\omega}=\inf_{z=\omega+i\tau,\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\left(\alpha_{1}\left|\widehat{f}(z)\right|^{2}+\alpha_{2}\left|z\widehat{f}(z)\right|^{2}\right)

(4.2)

We make the next assumption on
\gamma
,
h
and
h_{\varepsilon}
.

Assumption 2
.

There exists
\omega>0
such that
m_{\omega}^{\gamma}
,
m_{\omega}^{h}
,
m_{\omega}^{h_{\varepsilon}}>0
and
M_{\omega}^{\gamma}
,
M_{\omega}^{h}
,
M_{\omega}^{h_{\varepsilon}}<\infty
.

In the above notations,
\omega
,
\gamma
,
h
and
h_{\varepsilon}
indicates the correspondence as in (
4.2
).

We first prove the coercivity condition of the loss function.

Theorem 4.1
.

Under Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
,
we have

m_{\omega}^{h}\left\|\theta-\gamma\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leq\mathcal{E}(\theta)\leq M_{\omega}^{h}\left\|\theta-\gamma\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}

(4.3)

where
\rho(t)=e^{-2\omega t}dt
and supported on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Hence
\gamma
is identifiable by
\mathcal{E}
in
L^{2}(\rho)
, i.e.
\mathcal{E}(\theta)=0
implies
\theta-\gamma=0
in
L^{2}(\rho)
.

Proof.

Let
f(t)=\theta(t)-\gamma(t)
and
\Lambda(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(s)h(t-s)ds
. Then we have
f
,
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}

have exponential decay with parameter
\sigma
. By the Lemma
2.10
, using the notation
z=\omega+i\tau
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\alpha_{1}\left|\Lambda(t)\right|^{2}+\alpha_{2}\left|\Lambda^{\prime}(t)\right|^{2}\right)e^{-2\omega t}dt=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\alpha_{1}\left|\widehat{\Lambda}(z)\right|^{2}+\alpha_{2}\left|\widehat{\Lambda^{\prime}}(z)\right|^{2}d\tau

Since
\widehat{\Lambda}(z)=\widehat{h}(z)\widehat{f}(z)
and
\widehat{\Lambda^{\prime}}(z)=z\widehat{\Lambda}(z)-\Lambda(0)=z\widehat{h}(z)\widehat{f}(z)
,

\mathcal{E}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\alpha_{1}\left|\widehat{h}(z)\right|^{2}+\alpha_{2}\left|z\widehat{h}(z)\right|^{2}\right)\left|\widehat{f}(z)\right|^{2}d\tau

(4.4)

The results follow from the bounds of
\alpha_{1}\left|\widehat{h}(z)\right|^{2}+\alpha_{2}\left|z\widehat{h}(z)\right|^{2}
in Assumption
2
and using Lemma
2.10
again. The identifiability is a natural result from (
4.3
).
∎

In practice, we only have the noisy estimations of
g
and
h
, denoted as
g_{\varepsilon}
and
h_{\varepsilon}
. We use
\mathcal{E}_{g_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}}
to denote the loss function when observing
g_{\varepsilon}
and
h_{\varepsilon}
, and similarly for
\mathcal{E}_{g_{\varepsilon},h}
. The minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{g_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{g_{\varepsilon},h}
are denoted by
\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}}
and
\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h}
respectively. Such solutions exist because of Assumption
1
.

Remark 4.1
.

With the bound for
h_{\varepsilon}
as in Assumption
2
, we have a similar coercivity condition for
\mathcal{E}_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}
, namely

m_{\omega}^{h_{\varepsilon}}\left\|\theta-\gamma_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leq\mathcal{E}_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}(\theta)\leq M_{\omega}^{h_{\varepsilon}}\left\|\theta-\gamma_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}

(4.5)

Given the coercivity condition, we can prove the convergence of the estimation error in
\gamma
with respect to the estimation error of
g
and
h
. This is equivalent to saying the estimation of
\gamma
is well-posed.

Theorem 4.2
.

With the notations defined above,

1.

When we have the true
h
, the
L^{2}(\rho)
error in the estimator is bounded by the error in
g
.

\left\|\gamma-\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m_{\omega}^{h}}\left\|g-g_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\rho)}

(4.6)

2.

With estimated
h_{\varepsilon}
and
g_{\varepsilon}
, we have

\left\|\gamma-\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{m_{\omega}^{h_{\varepsilon}}}\left(M^{\gamma}_{\omega}\left\|h-h_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}+\left\|g-g_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\rho)}\right)

(4.7)

Proof.

1. In the first case, since
\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h}
satisfies

g_{\varepsilon}(t)=(\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h}*h)(t)

(4.8)

we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|g(t)-(\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h}*h)(t)\right|^{2}\rho(t)dt+\alpha_{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|g^{\prime}(t)-(\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h}*h)^{\prime}(t)\right|^{2}\rho(t)dt

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|g(t)-g_{\varepsilon}(t)\right|^{2}\rho(t)dt+\alpha_{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|g^{\prime}(t)-g_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)\right|^{2}\rho(t)dt=\left\|g-g_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\rho)}

And by the coercivity condition (
4.3
),

\left\|\gamma-\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}\leq\frac{1}{m_{\omega}^{h}}\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h})=\frac{1}{m_{\omega}^{h}}\left\|g-g_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{1}(\rho)}^{2}

(4.9)

2. In the second case, since

\left\|\gamma-\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leq 2\left\|\gamma-\gamma_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}+2\left\|\gamma_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}-\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}

(4.10)

For the second term, we recall the bounded condition for
h_{\varepsilon}
in Assumption
2
and the coercivity condition (
4.5
) in Remark
4.1
. A similar step as in the previous case implies the following estimate.

\left\|\gamma_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}-\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m^{h_{\varepsilon}}_{\omega}}\mathcal{E}_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}(\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}})=\frac{1}{m^{h_{\varepsilon}}_{\omega}}\left\|g-g_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\rho)}^{2}

(4.11)

For the first term, by the coercivity condition (
4.3
),

\left\|\gamma-\gamma_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m^{h_{\varepsilon}}_{\omega}}\mathcal{E}_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}(\gamma)

(4.12)

For the second term, notice that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|g(t)-(\gamma*h_{\varepsilon})(t)\right|^{2}\rho(t)dt+\alpha_{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|g^{\prime}(t)-(\gamma*h_{\varepsilon})^{\prime}(t)\right|^{2}\rho(t)dt

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|(h-h_{\varepsilon})*\gamma\right|^{2}\rho(t)dt+\alpha_{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|((h-h_{\varepsilon})*\gamma)^{\prime}\right|^{2}\rho(t)dt

Because of the bounds for
\gamma
in Assumption
2
, taking
h-h_{\varepsilon}
as
f
and
\gamma
as
h
in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we can have a similar upper bound as in (
4.3
), namely

\mathcal{E}_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}(\gamma)\leq M^{\gamma}_{\omega}\left\|h-h_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}

(4.13)

And then using the coercivity condition (
4.5
) in Remark
4.1
,

\left\|\gamma-\gamma_{g,h_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leq\frac{M^{\gamma}_{\omega}}{m_{\omega}^{h_{\varepsilon}}}\left\|h-h_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}

(4.14)

Finally,

\left\|\gamma-\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{m_{\omega}^{h_{\varepsilon}}}\left(M^{\gamma}_{\omega}\left\|h-h_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}+\left\|g-g_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\rho)}\right).

(4.15)

∎

Notice that the estimation error
\left\|\gamma-\gamma_{g_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}
scales linearly with the estimation error in
\left\|g-g_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{1}(\rho)}^{2}
and
\left\|h-h_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}
, but the constant is inversely proportional to
m_{\omega}^{h_{\varepsilon}}
. To achieve a better convergence, we have to make sure
m_{\omega}^{h_{\varepsilon}}
is away from 0. Such a condition can be derived from Prony estimations, i.e. using
\widetilde{h}
as the estimated
h_{\varepsilon}
. To emphasize this advantage, we compare the current loss function with two other choices of loss functions.

4.2
Ill-posed loss functions

It is natural to consider the loss function directly based on the Volterra equation (
2.3
),

\mathcal{E}_{1}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|g(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\theta(t-s)h(s)ds\right|^{2}\rho(t)dt=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|(\gamma-\theta)*h\right|^{2}\rho(t)dt

(4.16)

Since the second kind Volterra equation is well-posed, we also construct the following loss function based on (
2.7
),

\mathcal{E}_{2}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|g^{\prime}(t)-\left(\int_{0}^{t}\theta(t-s)h^{\prime}(s)ds-h(0)\theta(t)\right)\right|^{2}\rho(t)dt=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|((\gamma-\theta)*h)^{\prime}\right|^{2}\rho(t)dt

(4.17)

The corresponding estimators using loss functions
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
are denoted as
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
. As we have demonstrated in the proof of the Theorem
4.2
, the crucial estimate for the loss function to be well-posed is when
h
is replaced by the estimated form
h_{\varepsilon}
, the following coercivity condition holds,

\mathcal{E}_{1}(\theta)\geq m_{1}\left\|\theta-\gamma\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)},\ \mathcal{E}_{2}(\theta)\geq m_{2}\left\|\theta-\gamma\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}

(4.18)

for some constants
m_{1},m_{2}>0
. However, we show that this cannot be achieved for
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, and for
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, the constant
m_{2}
scales badly with
\omega
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
are defined as above, and
h
is approximated by a Prony series of the form
h_{\varepsilon}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{k}e^{\bm{\lambda}_{k}t}
with
\bm{\lambda}_{k}<-\sigma
,
k=1,\dots,p
. Then such
m_{1}>0
does not exist and
m_{2}=O(\omega^{2})
as
\omega\to 0
.

Proof.

By the Plancherel Theorem
2.2
and similar steps in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left|\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)\right|^{2}\left|\widehat{f}(z)\right|^{2}d\tau,\ \ \ \ \mathcal{E}_{2}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left|z\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)\right|^{2}\left|\widehat{f}(z)\right|^{2}d\tau

(4.19)

where
z=\omega+i\tau
and
f=\theta-\gamma
. Based on the given form of
h_{\varepsilon}
, we have
\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{k}}{z-\bm{\lambda}_{k}}
. It is clear that

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\left|\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(\omega+i\tau)\right|=0.

(4.20)

hence, a positive lower bound
m_{1}
does not exist. And in the second case,
z\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{z\mathbf{w}_{k}}{z-\bm{\lambda}_{k}}
, which is not
0
when
\tau\to\infty
. It is possible that there exists
m_{2}>0
. However,

\lim_{z\to 0}\left|z\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)\right|=0,

(4.21)

hence
m_{2}=\inf_{z=\omega+i\tau,\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\left|z\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)\right|^{2}\sim O(\omega^{2})
when
\omega\to 0
.
∎

The fact that
m_{1}
does not exist prevents us from using the loss function
\mathcal{E}_{1}
. And even though
m_{2}
exists when
\omega>0
, it scales badly when
\omega
is small. Since the density for
\rho
is
e^{-2\omega t}
, our confidence about the estimated kernel
\theta
is constrained near the origin when
\omega
is large. Therefore
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is not able to provide a reasonable performance guarantee for the value of the estimated kernel away from the origin.

From the observations of
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, the proposed loss function
\mathcal{E}
is constructed as their linear combination,

\mathcal{E}(\theta)=\alpha_{1}\mathcal{E}_{1}(\theta)+\alpha_{2}\mathcal{E}_{2}(\theta)

(4.22)

And for
\mathcal{E}
, the minimum in Assumption
1
is achievable, since the function
\left|z\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)\right|^{2}+\left|\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)\right|^{2}
does not have zeros at the origin, nor infinity. We will show this fact using numerical examples in the next section.

Remark 4.3
.

The above construction of
\mathcal{E}
gives us some insight into selecting the optimal parameter
\bm{\alpha}
. Because of equation (
4.20
) and (
4.21
), and notice that

\alpha_{1}^{\prime}:=\lim_{z\to\infty}z\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{k}=h_{\varepsilon}(0),\ \ \alpha_{2}^{\prime}:=\lim_{z\to 0}\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{k}}{-\bm{\lambda}_{k}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{\varepsilon}(t)dt

Therefore in practice, we let
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}^{\prime 2}+\alpha_{2}^{\prime 2}}(\alpha_{1}^{\prime},\alpha_{2}^{\prime})
. Such an arrangement makes

\lim_{z\to\infty}\alpha_{1}\left|\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)\right|^{2}+\alpha_{2}\left|z\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)\right|^{2}=\lim_{z\to 0}\alpha_{1}\left|\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)\right|^{2}+\alpha_{2}\left|z\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)\right|^{2}=\frac{\alpha_{1}^{\prime}\alpha_{2}^{\prime}}{\alpha_{1}^{\prime 2}+\alpha_{2}^{\prime 2}},

so that optimally balances the value of the above function at 0 and
\infty
. However, there is no guarantee that the minimum of
\alpha_{1}\left|\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)\right|^{2}+\alpha_{2}\left|z\widehat{h_{\varepsilon}}(z)\right|^{2}
is achieved at 0 and
\infty
, but a strictly positive minimum is possible to exist.

5
Numerical Results

We justify our analysis and examine the performance of our algorithm in the following examples. First, we give a typical learning result with the force
F
equal to 0, demonstrating the algorithm and comparing the prediction error on the correlation functions. We also compare the performance with the proposed loss function
\mathcal{E}
and the ill-posed ones
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
. Next, we give the convergence of estimation error, both theoretical and empirical, with respect to the observation noise on the trajectory and the changing decaying speed of the measure
\rho
. Finally, we present the case of
F
not being 0 and an example of chaotic dynamics with an extra drift term. In the last two cases, the solution
v
is not ergodic. Hence, the ensemble trajectory data becomes necessary.

5.1
Typical learning result when
F=0

We take the true memory kernel to be
\gamma(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{5}\mathbf{u}_{k}e^{\bm{\eta}_{k}t}
, with

\mathbf{u}=[0.3488,0.3488,0.3615,0.5300,0.3045]
and
\bm{\eta}=[-0.1631-0.3211i,-0.1631+0.3211i,-0.8262+0.0000i,-0.9178+0.0000i,-0.3352+0.0000i]
sampled from random and ensured exponential decay. The correlated noise is generated using spectral representation
[
SD91
]
, with accelerated convergence
[
HS97
]
. The spectral grid we used is
N_{w}=10000
,
\Delta w=\pi/100
, the length of the time grid to be
L=2^{16}
with the inverse temperature
\beta=1
. Hence the resulting correlated noise
R(t)
is evaluated on a grid with
\Delta t=\frac{\pi}{N_{w}\Delta w}=0.01
and an artificial period
T_{0}=\frac{2\pi}{\Delta w}=200
, denoted as
\mathbf{R}_{l}=R(\mathbf{t}_{l})
, where
\mathbf{t}_{l}=l{\Delta t},l=0,1,\dots,L
. The trajectory is generated using the Euler scheme, with
\mathbf{v}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and

\mathbf{v}_{l+1}=\mathbf{v}_{l}+\Delta t\left[F(\mathbf{v}_{l})-\sum_{j=0}^{l}\gamma({\mathbf{t}}_{l-j})\mathbf{v}_{j}+\mathbf{R}_{l}\right]

(5.1)

We call

Examples with force term and drift

5.3.1
Double well potential

We consider a system with a force derived from a double-well potential and a memory kernel exhibiting power-law decay,

F(v)=-v(v^{2}-4),\quad\gamma(t)=\frac{1-3t^{2}}{(1+t^{2})^{3}}.

(5.3)

Since the true correlation functions
h
and
g
are not analytically available, we cannot provide a theoretical guarantee based on their estimation errors. Instead, we compare results across different data sizes. The external force and power-law memory kernel hinder mixing, leading to poor correlation estimates from temporal integrals. A natural remedy is to use ensemble independent trajectories for more accurate estimation.
Suppose our observed data is given by
\{\mathbf{v}_{l}^{m}\}_{l=1,m=1}^{L,M}

\mathbf{h}_{n}=\frac{1}{(L-n)M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{l=1}^{L-n}\mathbf{v}_{l}^{m}\mathbf{v}_{l+n}^{m},\ \ \ \bm{\varphi}_{n}=\frac{1}{(L-n)M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{l=1}^{L-n}\mathbf{v}_{l}^{m}F(\mathbf{v}_{l+n}^{m}),

(5.4)

then apply the regularized Prony method Algorithm

We further illustrate the previous idea by adding a drift term

6
Conclusion and future work

We propose a Sobolev norm loss function for the estimation of the memory kernel in the generalized Langevin equation. Based on this loss function, we provide a least square estimation algorithm with a performance guarantee. The accuracy of derivatives of the correlation functions used in the loss function is achieved through a regularized Prony method, assuming the exponential decay of the correlation functions. The comparison between commonly used loss functions is shown by numerical examples. The estimation of the correlation functions can be generalized to utilize multiple trajectories, with the presence of force term and drift term.

Our two-stage procedure can also be interpreted as a hierarchical regularization framework of bias-variance trade-off. The empirical correlation functions
h(t)
and
g(t)
are projected onto a low-dimensional exponential basis using the Prony method and further smoothed through RKHS regularization, which significantly reduces noise but introduces bias due to model truncation and possible loss of long-time memory effects. The second stage applies additional regularization by solving a regression problem with a mixed Sobolev loss. The tuning parameters
\omega
,
\alpha_{1}
, and
\alpha_{2}
act as smoothing controls, as larger
\omega
emphasizes low-frequency behavior, leading to lower variance but greater bias.

Future work can be conducted in the following aspects.

•

Extend the current framework to higher dimensions, for which case the memory kernel becomes a matrix. A similar analysis should be performed for each matrix element.

•

Express the error of correlation functions in terms of the parameters of Prony methods, trajectory length, and observation noise.
Also, express the estimation error in terms of the parameter
\omega
,
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
, which explicitly shows the bias-variance trade-off.
