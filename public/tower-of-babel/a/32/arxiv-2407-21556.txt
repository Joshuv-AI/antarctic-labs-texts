Operator-based semantics for choice programs: is choosing losing? (full version)
Jesse Heyninck

Abstract
Choice constructs are an important part of the language of logic programming, yet the study of their semantics has been a challenging task. So far, only two-valued semantics have been studied, and the different proposals for such semantics have not been compared in a principled way. In this paper, an operator-based framework allow for the definition and comparison of different semantics in a principled way is proposed.



1
Introduction

Logic programming is one of the most popular declarative formalisms, as it offers an expressive, rule-based modelling language and efficient solvers for knowledge representation. An important part of this expressiveness comes from
choice constructs

[
Simons et al., 2002
]
, that allow to state e.g. set constraints in the body or head of rules, and are, among others, part of the ASP-Core-2 standard
[
Calimeri et al., 2012
]
. For example, the rule
1\{p,q,r\}2\leftarrow s.
expresses that if
s
is true, between 1 and 2 atoms among
p
,
q
and
r
are true. Choice constructs are non-deterministic, in the sense that there is more than one way to satisfy them. For example,
1\{p,q,r\}2
can be satisfied by
\{p\}
,
\{p,q\}
,
\{r\}
,
\ldots
. Formulating semantics for such non-deterministic rules has proven a challenging task
[
Liu et al., 2010
;
Marek and Remmel, 2004
;
Faber et al., 2004
;
Son and Pontelli, 2007
]
: several semantics have been proposed but no unifying framework for defining and comparing these semantics exists. Furthermore, attention has been restricted to two-valued semantics, in contradistinction to many other dialects for logic programming for which three- or four-valued semantics have been proposed. Moreover, many proposed semantics only allow for choice constructs in the body, but not in the head. Finally, relations with the non-deterministic construct disjunction remain unclear.

In this paper, a unifying framework for the definition and study of semantics for logic programs with choice constructs in the head and body is provided. This framework is based on immediate consequence operators, which are also useful for the design of explanatory tools and provide foundations for solvers
[
Kaminski and
Schaub, 2023
;
Eiter and
Geibinger, 2023
]
.
The contributions of this paper are the following: (1) we show how the famework of non-deterministic approximation fixpoint theory (AFT)
[
Heyninck et al., 2024
]
can be used to define a wide variety of supported and stable semantics for choice logic programs, (2) we introduce the constructive stable fixpoints, allowing to (3) generalize many existing semantics for choice programs, (4) compare these semantics by introducing postulates, (5) provide a principled comparison with disjunctive logic programs.

Outline of the Paper
:
In Section
2
, the background on choice programs and non-deterministic approximation fixpoint theory is given. In Section
3
, approximation operators for choice programs are defined. In Section
4
, we study the resulting supported semantics. In Section
5
, we define stable semantics and show representation results, while giving a postulate-based study in Section
6
. In Section
7
, we relate choice constructs and disjunctions. Related work and a conclusion follows in Sections
8
and
9
.

Relation with Previous Work
:
This paper extends this previous work
Heyninck [2023]
by also considering choice construct in the body, which gives rise to different possible approximators, and comparing these operators using the notion of groundedness.

2
Background and Preliminaries

We recall choice programs and non-deterministic AFT.

2.1
Choice Rules and Programs

A
choice atom
(relative to a set of atoms
\mathscr{A}
) is an expression
C=(\mathsf{dom},\mathsf{sat})
where
\mathsf{dom}\subseteq\mathscr{A}
and
\mathsf{sat}\subseteq\wp(\mathsf{dom})
. Intuitively,
\mathsf{dom}
denotes the
domain
of
C
, i.e. the atoms relevant for the evaluation of
C
, whereas
\mathsf{sat}
is the set of
satisfiers
of
C
. We also denote, for
C=(\mathsf{dom},\mathsf{sat})
,
\mathsf{dom}
by
\mathsf{dom}(C)
and
\mathsf{sat}
by
\mathsf{sat}(C)
.
For a concrete example, consider
1\{p,q,r\}2
which intuitively states that between 1 and 2 of the atoms
p
,
q
and
r
have to be true, corresponds to the choice atom
(\{p,q,r\},\{\{p\},\{q\},\{r\},\{p,q\},\{p,r\},\{q,r\}\})
(notice that
\{p,q,r\}
is the domain and not a satisfier). For such choice atoms, we assume the domain and satisfiers are clear and can be left implicit (and similarly for constructs such as
\{p,q\}=1
or
\{p,q\}\neq 1
).

A
choice rule
has the form
C\leftarrow C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
where
C,C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
are choice atoms.

C
is called the
head
(denoted
\mathsf{hd}(r)
) and
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
the
body
. If
C_{i}
is a literal (i.e.
C_{i}=(\{\alpha\},\{\{\alpha\}\})
, abbreviated by
\alpha
, or
C_{i}=(\{\alpha\},\{\emptyset\})
, abbreviated by
\lnot\alpha
) for every
i=1,\ldots,n
, we call it a
normal choice rule
. If
C
is an atom, we call
r
an
aggregate rule
.
A
choice program
is a set of choice rules, and is called normal[aggregate] if all of the rules are so. A choice atom
C
is
monotone
if
\mathsf{dom}(C)\cap x\in\mathsf{sat}(C)
implies
\mathsf{dom}(C)\cap x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{sat}(C)
for any
x\subseteq x^{\prime}\subseteq\mathscr{A}
, and it is
convex
if for any s.t.
x\subseteq y\subseteq\mathscr{A}
,
\mathsf{dom}(C)\cap x\in\mathsf{sat}(C)
and
\mathsf{dom}(C)\cap y\in\mathsf{sat}(C)
,
\mathsf{dom}(C)\cap z\in\mathsf{sat}(C)
for any
x\subseteq z\subseteq y
.

Following
Liu et al. [2010]
, a set
x\subseteq\mathscr{A}

satisfies
a choice atom
C
if
\mathsf{dom}(C)\cap x\in{\sf sat}(C)
. An interpretation
x
satisfies a rule
r
if
x
satisfies the head of
r
or does not satisfy some choice atom in the body of
r
.
x
is a
model
of
\mathscr{P}
if it satisfies every rule in
\mathscr{P}
. A rule
r\in\mathscr{P}
is
x
-applicable
if
x
satisfies the body of
r
, and the set of
x
-applicable rules in
\mathscr{P}
is denoted by
\mathscr{P}(x)
.

x\subseteq\mathscr{A}
is a
supported model
of
\mathscr{P}
if it is a model and
x\subseteq\bigcup_{r\in\mathscr{P}(x)}\mathsf{dom}(\mathsf{hd}(r))
.
For some
x\subseteq\mathscr{A}
, let

\mathit{HD}_{\mathscr{P}}(x)=\{\mathsf{hd}(r)\mid r\in\mathscr{P}(x)\}
and
\mathit{IC}_{\mathscr{P}}(x)=

\{z\subseteq\bigcup_{C\in\mathit{HD}_{\mathscr{P}}(x)}\mathsf{dom}(C)\mid\forall C\in\mathit{HD}_{\mathscr{P}}(x):z(C)={\sf T}\}

Example 1
.

Consider the program
\mathscr{P}=\{1\{p,q\}2\leftarrow\{p,q\}\neq 1\}
.
The choice atoms behave as follows:

\emptyset

\{p\}

\{q\}

\{p,q\}

1\{p,q\}2

{\sf F}

{\sf T}

{\sf T}

{\sf T}

\{p,q\}\neq 1

{\sf T}

{\sf F}

{\sf F}

{\sf T}

This means that
\mathit{IC}_{\mathscr{P}}(\emptyset)=\mathit{IC}_{\mathscr{P}}(\{p,q\})=\{\{p\},\{q\},\{p,q\}\}
, whereas
\mathit{IC}_{\mathscr{P}}(\{p\})=\mathit{IC}_{\mathscr{P}}(\{q\})=\{\emptyset\}
. The models of
\mathscr{P}
are
\{p\}
,
\{q\}
and
\{p,q\}
, and only
\{p,q\}
is supported.

2.2
Approximation Fixpoint Theory

We first recall some basic algebraic notions. A
lattice
is a partially ordered set (poset)
\langle\mathscr{L},\leq\rangle
s.t. for every
x,y\in\mathscr{L}
, a least upper bound
x\sqcup y
and a greatest lower bound
x\sqcap y
exist. A lattice is
complete
if every
X\subseteq\mathscr{L}
has a least upper bound
\bigsqcup X
and a greatest lower bound
\bigsqcap X
.

\bigsqcup\mathscr{L}
is denoted by
\top
and
\bigsqcap\mathscr{L}
is denoted by
\bot
.
A function
f:X\rightarrow Y
from a poset
\langle X,\leq_{1}\rangle
to a poset
\langle Y,\leq_{2}\rangle
is
monotonic
if
x_{1}\leq_{1}x_{2}
implies
f(x_{1})\leq_{2}f(x_{2})
, and
x\in X
is a
fixpoint
of
f
if
x=f(x)

We define the ordinal powers of a function
f:X\rightarrow X
as
f^{0}(x)=x
,
f^{\alpha+1}(x)=f(f^{\alpha}(x))
for a successor ordinal
\alpha
, and
f^{\alpha}(x)=\bigsqcup_{\beta<\alpha}\!f^{\beta}(x)
for a limit ordinal
\alpha
. The following notation will be used:
[x,y]=\{z\mid x\leq z\leq y\}
. We say a pair
(x,y)
is consistent if
x\leq y
and total if
x=y
.

We now recall basic notions from non-deterministic approximation fixpoint theory (AFT) by
Heyninck et al. [2024]
, which generalizes approximation fixpoint theory as introduced by
Denecker et al. [2000]
to non-deterministic operators. We refer to the original paper
[
Heyninck et al., 2024
]
for more details.

The basic idea behind non-deterministic approximation operators is that we approximate a non-deterministic operator
O
by generating, for a given lower bound
x
and upper bound
y
that approximates
z
, a set of lower bounds
\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}
and a set of upper bounds
\{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots\}
that under- respectively over-approximate
O(z)=\{z_{1},z_{2},\ldots\}
.
Formally, a
non-deterministic operator on
\mathscr{L}
is a function
O:\mathscr{L}\rightarrow\wp(\mathscr{L})\setminus\{\emptyset\}
.
For example, the operator
\mathit{IC}_{\mathscr{P}}
is a non-deterministic operator on the lattice
{\langle\wp(\mathscr{A}),\subseteq\rangle}
.
As the ranges of non-deterministic operators are
sets
of lattice elements, one needs a way to compare them, such as the
Smyth order
and the
Hoare order
.
Let
L={\langle\mathscr{L},\leq\rangle}
be a lattice, and let
X,Y\in\wp(\mathscr{L})
. Then:
X\preceq^{S}_{L}Y
if for every
y\in Y
there is an
x\in X
such that
x\leq y
; and
X\preceq^{H}_{L}Y
if for every
x\in X
there is a
y\in Y
such that
x\leq y
. Given some
X_{1},X_{2},Y_{1},Y_{2}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
X_{1}\times Y_{1}\preceq^{A}_{i}X_{2}\times Y_{2}
iff
X_{1}\preceq^{S}_{L}X_{2}
and
Y_{2}\preceq^{H}_{L}Y_{1}
; and
X_{1}\times Y_{1}\preceq^{S}_{t}X_{2}\times Y_{2}
iff
X_{1}\preceq^{S}_{L}X_{2}
and
Y_{2}\preceq^{S}_{L}Y_{1}
.

\langle\mathscr{A},\subseteq\rangle
are recalled in Table
1
.

Let
L={\langle\mathscr{L},\leq\rangle}
be a lattice.
Given an operator
\mathscr{O}:\mathscr{L}^{2}\rightarrow\mathscr{L}^{2}
, we denote by
\mathscr{O}_{l}
the projection operator defined by
\mathscr{O}_{l}(x,y)=\mathscr{O}(x,y)_{1}
, and similarly for
\mathscr{O}_{u}(x,y)=\mathscr{O}(x,y)_{2}
.
An operator
\mathscr{O}:\mathscr{L}^{2}\rightarrow\wp(\mathscr{L}){\setminus\emptyset}\times\wp(\mathscr{L}){\setminus\emptyset}

is called a
non-deterministic approximating operator
(ndao, for short), if it is
\preceq^{A}_{i}
-monotonic (i.e.
(x_{1},y_{1})\leq_{i}(x_{2},y_{2})
implies
\mathscr{O}(x_{1},y_{1})\preceq^{A}_{i}\mathscr{O}(x_{2},y_{2})
),
and is
exact
(i.e., for every
x\in\mathscr{L}
,
\mathscr{O}(x,x)=(\mathscr{O}_{l}(x,x),\mathscr{O}_{l}(x,x))
).
(x,y)
is a fixpoint of
\mathscr{O}
if
x\in\mathscr{O}_{l}(x,y)
and
y\in\mathscr{O}_{u}(x,y)
.
A non-deterministic operator
O:\mathscr{L}\rightarrow\wp(\mathscr{L})
is
downward closed
if for every sequence
X=\{x_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon<\alpha}
of elements in
\mathscr{L}
such that:
(1) for every
\epsilon<\alpha
,
O(x_{\epsilon})\preceq^{S}_{L}\{x_{\epsilon}\}
, and
(2) for every
\epsilon<\epsilon^{\prime}<\alpha
,
x_{\epsilon^{\prime}}<x_{\epsilon}
,
it holds that
O(\bigsqcap X)\preceq^{S}_{L}\bigsqcap X
.
We also introduce the following generalisation of an approximation operator, as it will allow us to capture some further existing semantics.

Definition 1
.

An operator
\mathscr{O}:\mathscr{L}^{2}\rightarrow\wp(\mathscr{L}){\setminus\emptyset}\times\wp(\mathscr{L}){\setminus\emptyset}
is a semi-ndao iff:
(1) it is exact,
(2)
\mathscr{O}_{l}(\cdot,y)
is
\preceq^{S}_{L}
-monotonic for any
y\in\mathscr{L}
, and
(3)
\mathscr{O}_{u}(x,\cdot)
is
\preceq^{H}_{L}
-anti-monotonic for any
x\in\mathscr{L}
.

Any ndao is a semi-ndao (cf. Lemma 1 by
Heyninck et al. [2024]
). A (semi-)ndao
\mathscr{O}

approximates
an operator
O
if
\mathscr{O}_{l}(x,x)=O(x,x)
for every
x\in\mathscr{L}
.

We recall now stable operators (given an ndao
\mathscr{O}
).
The
complete lower stable operator
is defined by (for any
y\in\mathscr{L}
)
C(\mathscr{O}_{l})(y)\ =\{x\in\mathscr{L}\mid x\in\mathscr{O}_{l}(x,y)\mbox{ and }\lnot\exists x^{\prime}<x:x^{\prime}\in\mathscr{O}_{l}(x^{\prime},y)\}
.
The
complete upper stable operator
is defined by (for any
x\in\mathscr{L}
)
C(\mathscr{O}_{u})(x)\ =\ \{y\in\mathscr{L}\mid y\in\mathscr{O}_{u}(x,y)\mbox{ and }\lnot\exists y^{\prime}<y:y^{\prime}\in\mathscr{O}_{u}(x,y^{\prime})\}
.
The
stable operator
is given by:
S(\mathscr{O})(x,y)=(C(\mathscr{O}_{l})(y),C(\mathscr{O}_{u})(x))
.
(x,y)
is a
stable fixpoint
of
\mathscr{O}
if
(x,y)\in S(\mathscr{O})(x,y)
.

Other semantics (e.g. well-founded state, Kripke-Kleene fixpoints and state and semi-equilibrium semantics) have been defined (
[
Heyninck et al., 2024
;
Heyninck and
Bogaerts, 2023
]
) and can be immediately obtained once an ndao is formulated. Due to space limitations, they are not discussed or studied here.

3
Approximation Operators for Choice Programs

The central task is to define non-deterministic approximations of the immediate consequence operator
\mathit{IC}_{\mathscr{P}}
. As is usual, we conceive of pairs of sets of atoms
(x,y)
as
four-valued interpretations
, where atoms in
x
are
true
whereas those in
y
are
not false
. Thus, assuming
x\subseteq y
,
(x,y)
represents an approximation of some set
z\in[x,y]
.
The basic idea behind all the operators defined below is the same: given an input interpretation
(x,y)
, we determine a set of rules that are to be taken into account when constructing the new lower (respectively upper bound), and then take as new lower (respectively upper bounds) the interpretations that make true the heads of all these rules. As is well-known already in the case for aggregate programs, there are various ways to give formal substance to this idea.
We will consider four operators inspired by previous work on aggregate or choice programs, namely
\mathscr{IC}^{\sf GZ}_{\mathscr{P}}

[
Gelfond and Zhang, 2014
]
,
\mathscr{IC}^{\sf MR}_{\mathscr{P}}

[
Marek and Remmel, 2004
]
,
\mathscr{IC}^{\sf LPST}_{\mathscr{P}}

[
Liu et al., 2010
]
, and
\mathscr{IC}^{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{P}}

[
Heyninck and
Bogaerts, 2023
]
.
The study of further operators is left for future work.
Intuitively, the
{\sf GZ}
-operator takes into account only rules of satisfied bodies whose domain remains unchanged in
x
and
y
. The lower bound of the
{\sf LPST}
-operator takes into account heads of rules whose body is true in every interpretation between
x
and
y
, whereas the upper bound collects the results of applying
\mathit{IC}_{\mathscr{P}}
to every “non-false” interpretation (i.e. every member of
[x,y]
).
The lower bound of the
{\sf MR}
-operator looks at all rules whose body is satisfied by the upper bound
y
and by some subset of the lower bound
x
, whereas its upper bound is identical to the
{\sf LPST}
-operator upper bound. The
\mathscr{U}
-operator, finally, has as a lower and upper bound the
{\sf LPST}
-operator upper bound.
Given a choice program
\mathscr{P}
and pair of sets of atoms
(x,y)
, let:

\displaystyle\mathscr{HD}^{{\sf GZ},l}_{\mathscr{P}}(x,y)=\{C\mid\exists C\leftarrow C_{1},\ldots,C_{i}\in\mathscr{P},\forall i=1\ldots n:

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188ptx\cap\mathsf{dom}(C_{i})=y\cap\mathsf{dom}(C_{i})\in\mathsf{sat}(C_{i})\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{HD}^{{\sf LPST},l}_{\mathscr{P}}(x,y)=\{C\mid\exists C\leftarrow C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}\in\mathscr{P},\forall i=1\ldots n:

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\forall z\in[x,y]:z(C_{i})={\sf T}\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{HD}^{{\sf MR},l}_{\mathscr{P}}(x,y)=\{C\mid\exists C\leftarrow C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}\in\mathscr{P},\exists z\subseteq x:

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\forall i=1\ldots n:y(C_{i})={\sf T}\mbox{ and }z(C_{i})={\sf T}\},

We then define (for
{\sf x}\in\{{\sf LPST},{\sf MR},{\sf GZ}\}
) the lower bound operators by
\mathscr{IC}^{{\sf x},l}(x,y)=

\displaystyle\{z\subseteq\hskip-18.49411pt\bigcup_{C\in\mathscr{HD}^{{\sf x},l}_{\mathscr{P}}(x,y)}\hskip-17.07182pt\mathsf{dom}(C)\mid\forall C\in\mathscr{HD}^{{\sf x},l}_{\mathscr{P}}(x,y):z\cap{\mathsf{dom}(C)\in\mathsf{sat}(C)}\}

Furthermore, we define (for
\dagger\in\{{\sf MR},{\sf LPST},\mathscr{U}\}
):

\displaystyle\mathscr{IC}^{\mathscr{U},l}_{\mathscr{P}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{IC}^{\dagger,u}_{\mathscr{P}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{x\subseteq z\subseteq y}\mathit{IC}_{\mathscr{P}}(z)

whereas
\mathscr{IC}^{{\sf GZ},u}_{\mathscr{P}}(x,y)=\mathscr{IC}^{{\sf GZ},l}_{\mathscr{P}}(x,y)
.
Finally, we define (for
{\sf x}\in\{{\sf LPST},{\sf MR},{\sf GZ},\mathscr{U}\}
):

\mathscr{IC}^{{\sf x}}(x,y)=(\mathscr{IC}^{{\sf x},l}(x,y),\mathscr{IC}^{{\sf x},u}(x,y)).

Groundedness

We introduce several postulates to facilitate a comparison between semantics for choice programs (thus solving an open question in the literature

groundedness

Intuitively, the idea behind groundedness is that models should be derivable
from the ground up

We now carry forward our study of self-support on a more general level by introducing three postulates of decreasing strength.
The first notion (inspired by

Definition 6

A set

Disjunctions are Choice Constructs

Our study allows us to give a principled account of the relation between stable semantics for disjunctive logic programs (DLPs) and choice programs. Indeed, in this section, we show that for DLPs

This means that all semantics obtained on the basis of these operators coincide, thus explaining the difference between semantics for stable models of disjunctive logic programs and choice logic programs in terms of the choice of stable operator (minimality-based versus constructive). In the rest of this section, we show this claim in more detail, first providing the necessary background on DLPs and then showing DLP-operators are a special case of the operators studied in this paper.

Disjunctions as Choice Constructs

We now show how the operator
\mathscr{IC}^{d}_{\mathscr{P}}
can be seen as a special case of the operators for choice constructs. In more detail,
for a DLP
\mathscr{P}
, we define
{\tt D2C}(\mathscr{P})=\{1\Delta\leftarrow\phi\mid\bigvee\Delta\leftarrow\phi\in\mathscr{P}\}
. E.g.
{\tt D2C}(\{p\lor q\leftarrow\})=\{1\{p,q\}\leftarrow\}
. In other words, we replace every disjunction by the choice atom that requires at least one element of
\Delta
is true.
We will show here that the operator defined for disjunctive logic programs
[
Heyninck et al., 2024
]
then coincides with the operator
\mathscr{IC}^{\sf x}_{{\tt D2C}(\mathscr{P})}
(for
{\sf x}={\sf MR},{\sf LPST}
).
This implies that all AFT-based semantics coincide for DLPs and their conversion into choice rules. We can now point in a very exact way to the difference between DLPs and choice programs: it lies not in how the constructs of disjunction and choice atoms are treated (i.e. when they should be made true or false), but rather in how the stable semantics is defined: for disjunctions, typically (e.g. in the most popular solvers
[
Gebser et al., 2016
;
Eiter et al., 2012
]
), a minimality-based stable operator is used, whereas for choice constructs, the c-stable operator is more apt. Thus, disjunctive and choice programs use the same (approximation) operators, but differ in how the corresponding stable operator is constructed.

We now show that the operator
\mathscr{IC}^{d}_{\mathscr{P}}
is a special case of the
{\sf MR}
- and
{\sf LPST}
-operators from this paper, when applied to the choice program
{\tt D2C}(\mathscr{P})
.

Proposition 13
.

For any disjunctive normal logic program
\mathscr{P}
,
\mathscr{IC}_{\mathscr{P}}=\mathscr{IC}^{\sf MR}_{{\tt D2C}(\mathscr{P})}=\mathscr{IC}^{\sf LPST}_{{\tt D2C}(\mathscr{P})}
.

From this, we immediately obtain that all the major semantics for disjunctive logic programming are special cases of the semantics introduced in this paper. Namely, weakly supported models of
\mathscr{P}
coincide with fixpoints of
\mathscr{IC}^{\sf MR}_{{\tt D2C}(\mathscr{P})}
whereas minimality-based stable fixpoints of
\mathscr{P}
coincide with stable models of
\mathscr{P}
. Since, the c-stable semantics does not enforce minimality, it will in general
not
correspond to the stable models semantics:

Example 17
.

Let
\mathscr{P}=\{p\lor q\leftarrow\}
. Then
(\{p,q\},\{p,q\})
is a c-stable fixpoint of
\mathscr{IC}^{\sf MR}_{{\tt D2C}(\mathscr{P})}
but it is not a stable model of
\mathscr{P}
according to
Gelfond and
Lifschitz [1991]
.

This also gives an answer to the question of how to combine disjunctions and choice constructs in logic programs: a choice as to which stable semantics are used has to be made: either preserve the minimality of answer sets as in DLPs and loses some reasonable models, or one gives up the minimality requirement by using the constructive stable semantics. In this context, it is perhaps interesting to note that the constructive stable semantics still coincides with the standard stable semantics for normal logic programs. In that case, all stable models are minimal. On the other hand, we can now also use minimality-based stable semantics on more complicated choice construct than simple disjunctions.

Related Work

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of AFT to the semantics of choice programs.
We have shown how major semantics for choice programs
[
Marek and Remmel, 2004
;
Liu et al., 2010
]
can be characterized in our framework. Other well-known semantics for (disjunctive) aggregate programs are those by
Faber et al. [2004]
;
Alviano and
Faber [2019]
;
Ferraris [2011]
, which are the ones used in the solvers DLV respectively clingo
[
Ferraris, 2011
;
Alviano et al., 2017
]
.

Another semantics, the FLP-semantics
[
Faber et al., 2004
]
, were originally not defined for programs with choice constructs in the head, but were generalized to allow choice constructs in the head by
Eiter and
Geibinger [2023]
. Nevertheless, these semantics only allow for two-valued stable models, and were shown to differ from the semantics by
[
Gelfond and Zhang, 2014
]
,
[
Liu et al., 2010
]
and
[
Marek and Remmel, 2004
]
already for aggregate programs
[
Alviano et al., 2023
]
, which means, in view of Proposition
10
, that the stable semantics induced by the
{\sf LPST}
-,
{\sf MR}
- and
{\sf GZ}
-operators also differ from the FLP-semantics. This comparison also holds for the semantics by
Ferraris [2011]
as the latter coincide with the FLP-semantics for aggregates with positive atoms
[
Alviano et al., 2023
]
.

Another line of work that is relevant for this paper is the application of AFT to logic programs with aggregates.

Pelov et al. [2007]
introduce several approximation operators for aggregate programs, whereas
Pelov et al. [2007]
introduces operator-based semantics for disjunctive aggregate programs. These semantics were generalized by
Heyninck and
Bogaerts [2023]
in the framework of non-deterministic AFT. Generalizing the operator by
Pelov et al. [2007]
to choice programs is left for future work. An overview of semantics that are (non-)representable in the deterministic AFT-framework is given by
Vanbesien et al. [2022]
. This work severed as an important foundation of our paper, as the operators
\mathscr{IC}^{\sf GZ}_{\cal P}
and
\mathscr{IC}^{\sf MR}_{\cal P}
are generalizations of the operator-based characterisations by
Vanbesien et al. [2022]
of the corresponding semantics.

Conclusion

The main contributions of this paper are the definition of several ndaos for choice programs, the definition of the constructive stable operator, the characterisation of several existing semantics for various dialects of logic and choice programs and the introduction and study of postulates for choice programs. We provide a principled view of choice programs versus disjunctive programs.

This paper is subject to one restriction: we assume
\mathscr{IC}^{c}_{\mathscr{P}}(x,y)\neq\emptyset
for every interpretation
(x,y)
. In future work, we will generalize our results beyond this assumption. We also plan to study the complexity of the resulting semantics, device implementations and study other AFT-based semantics, such as the Kripke-Kleene and well-founded states and semi-equilibrium semantics
[
Heyninck and
Bogaerts, 2023
;
Heyninck et al., 2024
]
and study other operators, e.g. inspired by
Pelov et al. [2007]
.
