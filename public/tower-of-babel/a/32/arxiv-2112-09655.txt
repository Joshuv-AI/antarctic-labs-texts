Distillation of RL Policies with Formal Guarantees via Variational Abstraction of Markov Decision Processes Technical Report
Florent Delgrange, Ann Nowé, Guillermo A. Pérez

Abstract
We consider the challenge of policy simplification and verification in the context of policies learned through reinforcement learning (RL) in continuous environments. In well-behaved settings, RL algorithms have convergence guarantees in the limit. While these guarantees are valuable, they are insufficient for safety-critical applications. Furthermore, they are lost when applying advanced techniques such as deep-RL. To recover guarantees when applying advanced RL algorithms to more complex environments with (i) reachability, (ii) safety-constrained reachability, or (iii) discounted-reward objectives, we build upon the DeepMDP framework introduced by Gelada et al. to derive new bisimulation bounds between the unknown environment and a learned discrete latent model of it. Our bisimulation bounds enable the application of formal methods for Markov decision processes. Finally, we show how one can use a policy obtained via state-of-the-art RL to efficiently train a variational autoencoder that yields a discrete latent model with provably approximately correct bisimulation guarantees. Additionally, we obtain a distilled version of the policy for the latent model.



1
Introduction

While
reinforcement learning
(RL) has been applied to a wide range of challenging domains, from game playing
(
Mnih et al. 2015
)
to real-world applications such as effective canal control
(
Ren et al. 2021
)
, more widespread deployment in the real world is hampered by the lack of guarantees provided with the learned policies.
Although there are RL algorithms which have limit-convergence guarantees
in the discrete setting
(
Tsitsiklis 1994
)
— and even in some continuous settings with function approximation, e.g.,
Nowe 1994
— these are lost when applying more advanced
techniques which make use of general nonlinear function approximators
(
Tsitsiklis and Roy 1997
)
to deal with
continuous
Markov decision processes
(MDPs) such as
deep
-RL (e.g.,

Mnih et al. 2015
). In this paper, we apply such advanced RL algorithms to unknown continuous MDPs with (i)
reachability, (ii) safety-constrained reachability, or (iii) discounted-reward
objectives. To recover the formal guarantees, we use the obtained
policy to train a
variational autoencoder
(VAE) which gives us a

discrete latent model
that approximates the unknown environment.
We build upon the
DeepMDP
framework

(
Gelada et al. 2019
)
to provide guarantees on the quality of the abstraction induced by this model.
DeepMDPs are provided with such guarantees when their
loss functions
are minimized. These can be defined on the entire state space (
global
) or on states visited under a given policy (
local
).
The guarantees concern a
state embedding function
, linking the latent and original MDPs and are defined as bounds on the difference of their
value function
and
bisimulation distance
.
The latter was only developed for global losses.
While these are interesting in theory, they are often infeasible to measure in practice.
In contrast, we introduce such bounds in the local setting and further consider an
action embedding function
to handle continuous actions.
Importantly, we focus on general MDPs and do not restrict our attention to deterministic ones as was done by
Gelada et al. 2019
to enable the approximation and minimization of their losses via neural networks.
We also give PAC approximation schemes to compute both the losses and said bounds.

Our VAE is trained by maximizing a lower bound on the likelihood of traces generated by executing the RL policy in the environment.
We derive a loss function, incorporating variational versions of the local losses, that enables learning (i) discrete state and action spaces, (ii) an MDP defined over these spaces, (iii) state and action embedding functions, linking the original and discrete MDPs, and (iv) a
distilled
version of the RL policy set over the discrete spaces which can be executed in both models via the embedding functions.
An important challenge for our approach is the
posterior collapse problem
which often occurs when optimizing a variational model (e.g.,
Alemi et al. 2018
).
We present a novel approach based on
prioritized experience replay

(
Schaul et al. 2016
)
to resolve this when learning a discrete latent model.

All of the above result in an efficient way of training a VAE to obtain a discrete latent model that is provably approximately bisimilar to the unknown MDP, further yielding a distilled version of the RL policy.
These enable the application of formal
methods and tools that have been developed for discrete
MDPs: for instance,
Prism

(
Kwiatkowska, Norman, and Parker 2011
)
,
Modest

(
Hartmanns and Hermanns 2014
)
, and
Storm

(
Hensel et al. 2021
)
.

Other related work.

Frameworks providing formal guarantees
during the RL process
include the work of
Junges et al. 2016
,
Shielded-RL

(
Alshiekh et al. 2018
;
Jansen et al. 2020
)
, and
AlwaysSafe

(
Simão, Jansen, and Spaan 2021
)
.
These all require an abstract model of the safety aspect of the environment. Our approach is complementary in that we assume no prior knowledge and
learn an abstraction
.
Notably, our goal is not the same: they aim at verifying whether the exploration is safe while our goal is to verify policies learned via
any
RL technique.
Other approaches share ours
in the particular case of verifying deep-RL policies, but rely on a known (abstraction of the) environment model.

Bacci and Parker 2020
require the neural network (NN) specifying the policy, the environment to be deterministic, and a formal description of the probability of action failures.
Carr, Jansen, and Topcu 2020
require the environment to be discrete and focus on policies represented as recurrent NNs by discretizing their hidden states via
quantized
autoencoders, in the same spirit as our policy distillation.
Finally,
Alamdari et al. 2020
focus on tree-based policies distilled from deep-RL, without considering abstraction quality guarantees.

VAEs have been used in the context of (model-based) RL to learn latent representations of the unknown environment and train
simpler policies
from the features extracted
(e.g.,
Corneil, Gerstner, and Brea 2018
;
Freeman, Ha, and Metz 2019
;
Lee et al. 2020
;
Burden, Siahroudi, and Kudenko 2021
).
In particular,
Corneil, Gerstner, and Brea 2018
focused on learning discrete latent MDPs from continuous-state environments with discrete actions (without guarantees nor distilled policies) to plan via
prioritized sweeping
.

We write

Their discrepancy can be measured via

•

•

of

and
Q

•

Total Variation
(TV):

d_{{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(P,Q}}\right)=\sup_{A\in\Sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X}}}\right)}|P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)-Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)|.

is equipped with

Markov decision processes.
A
Markov decision process

\mathcal{M}=\left\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathbf{P},\mathcal{R},\ell,\mathbf{AP},s_{\mathit{I}}\right\rangle
where

;
\mathcal{R}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}

;

over a set of atomic propositions

; and

, the

.
The set of
enabled actions
of

is
\mathit{Act}(s)\subseteq\mathcal{A}
.
We assume

\mathit{Act}(s)\neq\emptyset

.
If

,

is a fully stochastic process called a

Let

, we write
\llbracket\mathsf{T}\rrbracket=\set{s\mid\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)\cap\mathsf{T}\neq\emptyset}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
and
\llbracket\neg\mathsf{T}\rrbracket=\mathcal{S}\setminus\llbracket\mathsf{T}\rrbracket
.
We assume
\mathbf{AP}
and labels being respectively

and binary encoded.
We write
\mathcal{M}_{s}
for the MDP obtained when we replace the initial state of
\mathcal{M}
by
s\in\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
when we replace the reward function by
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
,
and
\mathcal{M}^{\penalty\hskip 0.53265pt\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.80856pt}{\resizebox{1254916}{276523}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.86447pt}{$\displaystyle\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowright$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.80856pt}{\resizebox{1254916}{276523}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.86447pt}{$\textstyle\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowright$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.53673pt}{\resizebox{1096556}{276523}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.40169pt}{$\scriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowright$}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.53671pt}{\resizebox{1096557}{276523}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.28691pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowright$}$}}}}}B}
when we make absorbing states from
B\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, i.e., by changing
\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right)
to
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right)
such that
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B\mid s,a}}\right)=1
for all
s\in B
,
a\in\mathit{Act}(s)
.
We refer to MDPs with continuous states or actions spaces as
continuous MDPs
.
In that case, we assume
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
are complete separable metric spaces equipped with a Borel
\sigma
-algebra
and
\ell^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{T}}}\right)\in\Sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{S}}}\right)
for any

.

Trajectories.
A

\tau{}
of
\mathcal{M}
is a sequence of states and actions
\tau=\left\langle s_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T},a_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1}\right\rangle

,

A
(memoryless) policy

and

along with a unique probability measure

)
.
We drop the superscript when the context is clear.
For

, we denote by

, and by
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}

We write

for

Stationary distributions.
Let

Latent Space Models

Given the original (continuous, possibly unknown) environment modeled as an MDP, a
latent space model
is another (simpler, smaller, and explicit) MDP with state-action space linked to the original one via
embedding functions
.
The latter can be learned to optimize an
equivalence criterion
between the two models.
Formally, fix MDPs
\mathcal{M}=\left\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathbf{P},\mathcal{R},\ell,\mathbf{AP},s_{\mathit{I}}\right\rangle
and
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}=\left\langle\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}},\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}},\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}},\,\overline{\!{\ell}},\mathbf{AP},\,\overline{\!{s}}_{I}\right\rangle
such that
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
is equipped with metric

.
Let
\phi\colon\mathcal{S}\to\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
and
\psi\colon\mathcal{S}\times\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}\to\mathcal{A}
be respectively state and action embedding functions.
We refer to
\left\langle\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}},\phi,\psi\right\rangle
as a latent space model of
\mathcal{M}
and
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
as its
latent MDP
.
We write
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\textnormal{ml}}=\Pi_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}}^{\textnormal{ml}}
, and
{\,\overline{\!{Q}}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}

.

: states passed to

Let
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\textnormal{ml}}

\displaystyle W_{d_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}}}\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{1}}}\right),\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{2}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq K_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}d_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}_{1},\,\overline{\!{s}}_{2}}}\right).

Local losses.
Let
\xi\in\mathcal{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}}\right)
,

local losses
are defined as:

\displaystyle L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,\,\overline{\!{a}}\sim\xi}\left|\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)-\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)\right|,

\displaystyle L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,\,\overline{\!{a}}\sim\xi}W_{d_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}}}\left(\phi\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right),\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)\right)

where (i)
\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)
, (ii)

\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)
, and (iii)

\phi\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)
are
shorthand for (i)
\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)}}\right)
,
(ii)
\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)}}\right)
, and (iii) the distribution over
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
of sampling

s^{\prime}\sim\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)
and
then embedding
\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)
.

Assuming
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
is discrete, all
W_{d_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}}}
terms can be replaced by
d_{{TV}}

since Wasserstein coincides with TV when using the discrete metric.
In that case, (optimal) constants
K_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
and

K_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
can be computed in polynomial time in
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
for any
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\textnormal{ml}}
.

Henceforth, we make the following assumptions.

Assumption 3.1
.

MDP
\mathcal{M}
is ergodic.

Assumption 3.2
.

Rewards of
\mathcal{M}
are scaled in the interval
\mathopen{[}-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\mathclose{]}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{R}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathopen{[}-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\mathclose{]}
.

Assumption 3.3
.

The embedding function preserves the labels, i.e.,
\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)=\,\overline{\!{s}}\implies\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)=\,\overline{\!{\ell}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}}}\right)
for
s\in\mathcal{S}
,
\,\overline{\!{s}}\in\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
.

Seemingly restrictive at first glance, Assumption
3.1
is compliant with RL environments and a wide range of continuous learning tasks (every episodic RL process is ergodic, see
Huang 2020
).
Discarding it restricts the upcoming guarantees to BSCCs.
Assumption
3.2
basically requires rewards to be bounded and re-scalable in this interval.
This
is a reasonable assumption in practice and re-scaling is straightforward if bounds are known (otherwise, dynamical re-scaling is still feasible but complicates the implementation).
In Sect.
4.2
, we show that Assumption
3.3
can be made trivial.
Note that our approach requires Assumption
3.2
and
3.3
.

Proofs of the claims made in the following two subsections are provided in supplementary material (Appendix
B
).

3.1
Bisimulation and Value Difference Bounds

We aim now at formally checking
whether
the latent space model offers a good abstraction of the original MDP
\mathcal{M}
.
To do so, we present bounds that link the two MDPs.
We extend the bounds from
Gelada et al. 2019
to discrete spaces while additionally taking into account state
labels and discounted reachability events.
Moreover, we present new
bisimulation
bounds in the local setting.

Bisimulation.

A
(probabilistic) bisimulation

is a behavioral equivalence between states.
Formally, a bisimulation on
\mathcal{M}
is an equivalence relation
B_{\Phi}
such
that for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
and
\Phi\subseteq\set{\mathcal{R},\ell}
,
s_{1}B_{\Phi}s_{2}
iff

\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T\mid s_{1},a}}\right)=\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T\mid s_{2},a}}\right)
,

\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)=\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)
if
\ell\in\Phi
, and
\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1},a}}\right)=\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2},a}}\right)
if
\mathcal{R}\in\Phi
,
for each action
a\in\mathcal{A}
and (Borel measurable) equivalence class
T\in\mathcal{S}/B_{\Phi}
.
Properties of bisimulation include trace, trajectory, and value equivalence
(
Larsen and Skou 1989
;
Givan, Dean, and Greig 2003
)
.
The relation can be extended to compare two MDPs (in our case
\mathcal{M}
and
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
) by considering the disjoint union of their state space.
We denote the largest bisimulation relation by
\sim_{\Phi}
.

Pseudometrics.

Desharnais et al. 2004
introduced
bisimulation pseudometrics
for continuous Markov processes, generalizing the notion of bisimilariy by assigning a
bisimilarity distance
between states.
A
pseudometric

\tilde{d}
satisfies symmetry
and the triangle inequality.

Probabilistic bisimilarity can be characterized by a logical family of functional expressions
derived from a logic
\mathcal{L}
.
More specifically, given a policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}^{\textnormal{ml}}
, we consider a family
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi}}\right)
of real-valued functions
f
, parameterized by the discount factor
\gamma
and defining the semantics of
\mathcal{L}
in
\mathcal{M}_{\pi}
.
Such a logic allows formalizing discounted properties, including reachability (e.g.,
Chatterjee et al. 2008
).
The related pseudometric
\tilde{d}_{\pi}

is defined as:
\tilde{d}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1},s_{2}}}\right)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi}}\right)}\left|f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)-f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)\right|
, for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
.
We distinguish between pseudometrics
\tilde{d}^{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi}
, characterized by functional expressions including rewards (e.g.,
Ferns, Precup, and Knight 2014
),
and
\tilde{d}^{\ell}_{\pi}
, whose functional expressions are based on state labels (e.g.,
Chen, van Breugel, and Worrell 2012
).
Let
\tilde{P}
be the space of pseudometrics on
\mathcal{S}
and
\varphi\in\set{\mathcal{R},\ell}
.
Define
\Delta\colon\tilde{P}\to\tilde{P}
so that
\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d^{\varphi}_{\pi}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1},s_{2}}}\right)
is
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma}}\right)\left|\mathcal{R}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)-\mathcal{R}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)\right|\cdot\bm{1}_{\set{\mathcal{R}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi}}\right)+M
where:

M=\max\begin{cases}\gamma W_{d_{\pi}^{\varphi}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s_{1}}}\right),\mathbf{P}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s_{2}}}\right)\right),\\
\bm{1}_{\neq}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right),\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)}}\right)\cdot\bm{1}_{\set{\ell}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi}}\right),\end{cases}

then
\tilde{d}^{\varphi}_{\pi}
is its unique fixed point whose kernel is
\sim_{\{\varphi\}}
, i.e.,
\tilde{d}^{\varphi}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}(s_{1},s_{2})=0
iff

s_{1}\!\!\sim_{\{\varphi\}}\!\!s_{2}

(
van Breugel and Worrell 2001
;
Ferns, Precup, and Knight 2014
)
,
and

|V_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)-V_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)|\leq\nicefrac{{\tilde{d}^{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1},s_{2}}}\right)}}{{1-\gamma}}

(
Ferns, Panangaden, and Precup 2005
)
.

Bisimulation bounds.
While computing this distance is
intractable
in practice, especially
when continuous spaces are involved, we emphasize that local losses can be used to evaluate the original and latent model bisimilarity.
Fix
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\textnormal{ml}}
and assume
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
is discrete.
Given the induced stationary distribution
\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
in
\mathcal{M}
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\tilde{d}^{\mathcal{R}}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}+\gamma L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\frac{K_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}{1-\gamma K_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}},

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\tilde{d}^{\ell}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}{1-\gamma},

(1)

where
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
is
\langle K_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu},K_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\rangle
-Lipschitz.
The result provides us a general way to assess the quality of the
abstraction: the bisimulation distance between states and their embedding is
guaranteed to be small in average whenever local losses are small.
Analogously, local losses can further be used to check whether two states with the same representation are indeed bisimilarly close: for
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
with
\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)
,

\displaystyle\tilde{d}^{\mathcal{R}}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1},s_{2}}}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\left[L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}+\frac{\gamma L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}K_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}{1-\gamma K_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)+\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)}}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{d}^{\ell}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1},s_{2}}}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}{1-\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)+\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)}}\right).

(2)

Remark 1
.

Our goal is to quantify the gap between the behaviors induced by the latent policy executed in the original environment compared to those induced when executed in the latent MDP.
This allows for example to lift the guarantees obtained by model-checking
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
to
\mathcal{M}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
.
For this reason, notice that the expectations of Eq.
1
are set over states produced by
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

in the original environment
:
this way, states that are likely to be seen under
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
in
\mathcal{M}
are ensured to have this behavioral gap bounded by a factor of
L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}
and/or
L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}
.

Value difference bounds.
Considering discounted returns or a specific event, the quality of the latent abstraction can be in particular formalized by means of
value difference bounds
.
These bounds can be intuitively derived by taking the value function as a
real-valued function from
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}^{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi}}\right)
.
Let
\mathsf{C},\mathsf{T}\subseteq\mathbf{AP}
,
\varphi\in\set{\mathsf{C}\,\mathcal{U}\,\mathsf{T},\lozenge\mathsf{T}}
and
K_{\,\overline{\!{V}}}=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nicefrac{{|\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\star}|}}{{1-\gamma}}$\nicefrac{{|\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\star}|}}{{1-\gamma}}$$\nicefrac{{|\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\star}|}}{{1-\gamma}}$,\nicefrac{{K_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}{{1-\gamma K_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}$\nicefrac{{K_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}{{1-\gamma K_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}$$\nicefrac{{K_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}{{1-\gamma K_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}$}}\right)
,
then,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,\,\overline{\!{a}}\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\left|Q_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)-\,\overline{\!{Q}}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}+\gamma K_{\,\overline{\!{V}}}L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}{1-\gamma},

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,\,\overline{\!{a}}\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\left|Q_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\varphi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)-\,\overline{\!{Q}}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\varphi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}{1-\gamma}.

(3)

Moreover, for any states
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
with
\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)
,

\displaystyle\left|V_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right){-}V_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}{+}\gamma K_{\,\overline{\!{V}}}L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}}{1{-}\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right){+}\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)}}\right),

\displaystyle\left|V_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\varphi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right){-}V_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\varphi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{\gamma L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}{1{-}\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right){+}\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)}}\right).

(4)

Intuitively, when local losses are sufficiently small, then (i) the value difference of states and their embedding that are likely to be seen under a latent policy is also small, and (ii) states with the same embedding have close values.

3.2
Checking the Quality of the Abstraction

While bounding the difference between values offered by
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\textnormal{ml}}
in
\mathcal{M}
and
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
is theoretically possible using
L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}
and
L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}
, we need to accurately approximate these losses from samples to further offer practical guarantees (recall that
\mathcal{M}
is unknown).
Although the agent is able to produce execution traces by
interacting with
\mathcal{M}
,
estimating the expectation over the Wasserstein is intractable.
Intuitively, even if approximating Wasserstein from samples is possible (e.g.,
Genevay et al. 2019
),
this would require access to a generative model for
\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right)
(e.g.,
Kearns, Mansour, and Ng 2002
) from which we would have to draw a sufficient number of samples for each
s,a
drawn from
\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
to then be able to estimate the expectation.

Gelada et al. 2019
overcome this issue by assuming a deterministic MDP, which allows optimizing an approximation of
L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}
through gradient descent.
To deal with general MDPs, we study
an upper bound on
L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}
that can be efficiently approximated from samples:

\displaystyle L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,\,\overline{\!{a}},s^{\prime}\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}W_{d_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}}}\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s^{\prime}}}\right),\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)\right)=\dot{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}},

where
\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}(s,\,\overline{\!{a}},s^{\prime})=\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}(s,\,\overline{\!{a}})\cdot\mathbf{P}(s^{\prime}\mid s,\,\overline{\!{a}})

and
\phi(\,\overline{\!{s}}\mid s)=\bm{1}_{=}(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{s}})
.
We now provide
probably approximately correct
(PAC) guarantees for estimating local losses for discrete latent models, derived from
the

Hoeffding 1963
’s inequalities.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
is discrete and the agent interacts with
\mathcal{M}
by executing
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, thus producing
\left\langle s_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T},\,\overline{\!{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1},r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1}\right\rangle\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
.
Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathopen{]}0,1\mathclose{[}
and denote by

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left|r_{t}-\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t}}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}_{t}}}\right)\right|,

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left[1-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t+1}}}\right)\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t}}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}_{t}}}\right)\right].

Then after
T\geq\left\lceil\nicefrac{{-\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\delta}{4}}}\right)}}{{2\varepsilon^{2}}}\right\rceil
steps,

\left|L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}-\hat{L}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\right|\leq\varepsilon
and

\left|\dot{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}-\hat{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\right|\leq\varepsilon

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

This yields the following Theorem, finally allowing to check the abstraction quality as a bounded value difference.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\textnormal{ml}}
and assume
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
is discrete and
\langle K_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu},K_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\rangle
-Lipschitz.
Let
\mathsf{C},\mathsf{T}\subseteq\mathbf{AP}
,
\varphi\in\set{\mathsf{C}\,\mathcal{U}\,\mathsf{T},\lozenge\mathsf{T}}
, and

.
Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathopen{]}0,1\mathclose{[}
and
\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
be the stationary distribution of
\mathcal{M}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
.
Then, after

interaction steps through
\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,\,\overline{\!{a}}\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\left|Q_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)-\,\overline{\!{Q}}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\hat{L}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}+\gamma K_{\,\overline{\!{V}}}\hat{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}}{1-\gamma}+\varepsilon,

with probability at least

.

4
Variational Markov Decision Processes

We now provide a framework based on
variational autoencoders

(
Kingma and Welling 2014
)
that allows us to learn a discrete latent space model of
\mathcal{M}
through the interaction of the agent executing a pre-learned RL policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}^{\textnormal{ml}}
with the environment.
Concretely, we seek a discrete latent space model
\left\langle\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}_{\theta},\phi_{\iota},\psi_{\iota,\theta}\right\rangle
such that
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}_{\theta}=\left\langle\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}},\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}},\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta},\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}_{\theta},\,\overline{\!{\ell}}_{\theta},\mathbf{AP},\,\overline{\!{s}}_{I}\right\rangle
.
We propose to learn the parameters
\left\langle\iota,\theta\right\rangle
of an
encoder

Q_{\iota}
and a
behavioral model

P_{\theta}
from which we can retrieve
(i) the embedding functions

\phi_{\iota}
and
\psi_{\iota,\theta}
,
(ii) the latent MDP components
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
,
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}_{\theta}
, and
\,\overline{\!{\ell}}_{\theta}
,
and (iii) a latent policy
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\textnormal{ml}}
, via

\min_{\theta}D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\pi},P_{\theta}}}\right),

where
D
is a discrepancy measure.
Intuitively, the end goal is to learn
(i) a discrete representation of
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}

(ii) to mimic the behaviors of the original MDP over the induced latent spaces, thus yielding a latent MDP with a bisimulation distance close to
\mathcal{M}
, and
(iii) to
distill

\pi
into
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
.
In the following, we distinguish the case where we only learn
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
, with
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}=\mathcal{A}
(in that case,
\mathcal{A}
is assumed to be discrete), and the one where we additionally need to discretize the set of actions and learn
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}
.

4.1
Evidence Lower Bound

In this work, we focus on the case where
D_{{\mathrm{KL}}}
is used as discrepancy measure: the goal is optimizing
\min_{\theta}D_{{\mathrm{KL}}}(\mathcal{M}_{\pi}\parallel P_{\theta})
or equivalently maximizing the marginal

log-likelihood of traces
of
\mathcal{M}
, i.e.,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{\tau}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\pi}}\left[\log P_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\tau}}}\right)\right]
, where

P_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\tau}}}\right)=\int_{\mathit{Traj}_{\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\theta}}}}P_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\tau}\mid z_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}}}\right)\,d\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}}}\right),

(5)

\hat{\tau}=\langle s_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T},a_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1},r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1},l_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}\rangle
,

z\in\mathcal{Z}
with
\mathcal{Z}=\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
if
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}=\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{Z}=\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}\times\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}
otherwise,

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}(\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T})=\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}(\,\overline{\!{s}}_{t+1}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{t})
, and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}(\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T},\,\overline{\!{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1})=\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}(\,\overline{\!{a}}_{t}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{t})\cdot\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}(\,\overline{\!{s}}_{t+1}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{t},\,\overline{\!{a}}_{t})
.
The dependency of
\hat{\tau}
on
\mathcal{Z}
in Eq.
5

is made explicit by the law of total probability.

Optimizing
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{\tau}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\pi}}\left[\log P_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\tau}}}\right)\right]
through Eq.
5
is typically intractable
(
Kingma and Welling 2014
)
.
To overcome this, we use an encoder
Q_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}\mid\hat{\tau}}}\right)
to set up
a
lower bound
on the log-likelihood of produced traces,
often referred to as
evidence lower bound
(ELBO,
Hoffman et al. 2013
):

\displaystyle\log P_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\tau}}}\right)-D_{{\mathrm{KL}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Q_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\hat{\tau}}}\right)\parallel{P_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\hat{\tau}}}\right)}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}\sim Q_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\hat{\tau}}}\right)}\left[\log P_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\tau}\mid z_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}}}\right)\right]-D_{{\mathrm{KL}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Q_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\hat{\tau}}}\right)\parallel\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}}}\right).

The purpose of optimizing the ELBO is twofold.
First, this allows us learning
\phi_{\iota}
via (i)
Q_{\iota}(\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}\mid\hat{\tau})=\prod_{t=0}^{T}\phi_{\iota}(\,\overline{\!{s}}_{t}\mid s_{t})
or (ii)
Q_{\iota}(\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T},\,\overline{\!{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1}\mid\hat{\tau})=Q_{\iota}(\,\overline{\!{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T},\hat{\tau})\cdot Q_{\iota}(\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}\mid\hat{\tau})
, where
Q_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T},\hat{\tau}}}\right)=\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}Q^{\mathcal{A}}_{\iota}(\,\overline{\!{a}}_{t}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{t},a_{t})
,
Q^{\mathcal{A}}_{\iota}

being an
action encoder.
We assume here that
encoding states and actions to latent spaces is independent of rewards.
We additionally make them independent of
l_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}
by assuming that

\ell
is known.
This allows
\phi_{\iota}
to encode states and their labels directly into the latent space (cf. Sect.
4.2
).

Second, we assume the existence of latent reward and label models, i.e.,
P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{R}}
and
P_{\theta}^{\ell}
, allowing to recover respectively
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}_{\theta}
and
\,\overline{\!{\ell}}_{\theta}
, as well as a
generative model

P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{G}}
, enabling the reconstruction of states and actions.
This allows decomposing the behavioral model
P_{\theta}
into:

\quad\quad\begin{aligned} &P_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T},a_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1},r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1},l_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}\mid z_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}}}\right)\\
=&P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{G}}(s_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T})\cdot P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{G}}(a_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1}\mid z_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T})\\
&\cdot P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{R}}(r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T},\,\overline{\!{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1})\cdot P_{\theta}^{\ell}(l_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}),\end{aligned}\\
\begin{aligned} \text{where }P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{G}}(s_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T})&=\textstyle{\prod_{t=0}^{T}}\,P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{G}}(s_{t}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{t}),\\
P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{G}}(a_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1}\mid z_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1})&=\begin{cases}\textstyle{\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}}\,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{t})\text{ if }\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}=\mathcal{A}\\
\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\,\psi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{t},\,\overline{\!{a}}_{t})\text{ else,}\end{cases}\\
P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{R}}(r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T},\,\overline{\!{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1})&=\textstyle{\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}}\,P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{R}}(r_{t}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{t},\,\overline{\!{a}}_{t}),\text{and}\\
P_{\theta}^{\ell}(l_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T})&=\textstyle{\prod_{t=0}^{T}}\,P_{\theta}^{\ell}(l_{t}\mid{\,\overline{\!{s}}}_{t}).\end{aligned}

Model
\psi_{\theta}

allows learning the action embedding function via
\psi_{\iota,\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s,\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\,\overline{\!{s}}\sim\phi_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}\psi_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid\,\overline{\!{s}},\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A},\,\overline{\!{a}}\in\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}
.
We also argue that a
perfect reconstruction
of labels is possible, i.e.,
P_{\theta}^{\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(l_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}}}\right)=1
, due to the labels being encoded into the latent space.
From now on, we thus omit the label term.

Deterministic embedding functions
\phi_{\iota}
,
\psi_{\iota,\theta}

and
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}_{\theta}

can finally be obtained by
taking the mode of their distribution.

Back to the local setting.
Taking Assumption
3.1
into account, drawing multiple finite traces
\hat{\tau}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\pi}
can be seen as a continuous interaction with
\mathcal{M}
along an infinite trace
(
Huang 2020
)
.
This observation allows us to formulate the ELBO in the local setting

\max_{\iota,\theta}\;\mathrm{ELBO}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}_{\theta},\phi_{\iota},\psi_{\iota,\theta}}}\right)=-\min_{\iota,\theta}\left\{\mathbf{D}_{\iota,\theta}+\mathbf{R}_{\iota,\theta}\right\},

\mathbf{D}

The optimization of
\mathrm{ELBO}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}_{\theta},\phi_{\iota},\psi_{\iota,\theta}}}\right)
allows for an indirect optimization of the local losses through their variational versions:
(i)
L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}
via the log-likelihood of rewards produced, and
(ii)
\dot{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}
where we change the Wasserstein term to the KL divergence.
Note that this last change means we do not necessarily obtain the theoretical guarantees on the quality of the abstraction via its optimization.
Nevertheless, our experiments indicate KL divergence is a good proxy of the Wasserstein term in practice.
In particular, in the discrete setting, Wasserstein matches TV and one can relate the proxy with the original metric using the Pinkster’s inequality.

4.2
VAE Distributions

Discrete distributions.
We aim at learning
discrete
latent spaces
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
and
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}
, the distributions
\phi_{\iota}
,
Q_{\iota}^{\mathcal{A}}
,
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
, and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
are thus supposed to be discrete.
Two main challenges arise:
(i) gradient descent is not applicable to learn
\iota
and
\theta
due to the discontinuity of
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
and
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}
, and (ii) sampling from these distributions must be a
derivable operation
.
We overcome these by using
continuous relaxation of Bernoulli distributions
to learn a binary representation of the latent states, and the
Gumbel softmax trick
for the latent action space
(
Jang, Gu, and Poole 2017
;
Maddison, Mnih, and Teh 2017
)
.

Labels.
To enable
\log P_{\theta}^{\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(l_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T}}}\right)=0
, we linearly encode
\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t}}}\right)=l_{t}
into each
\,\overline{\!{s}}_{t}
via
\phi_{\iota}
.
Recall that labels are binary encoded, so we allocate them
|\mathbf{AP}|
bits in
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
.
Then,
\phi_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}\mid s}}\right)>0
implies
\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)=\,\overline{\!{\ell}}_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}}}\right)
, for all
s\in\mathcal{S},\,\overline{\!{s}}\in\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
,
satisfying Assumption
3.3
if
\phi_{\iota}
is deterministic.

Decoders.
For
P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{G}}
,
\psi_{\theta}
, and
P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{R}}
, we learn the parameters of multivariate normal distributions.
This further allows linking all
\,\overline{\!{s}}\in\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
to the parameters of
P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{G}}({\cdotp}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}})

4.3
Posterior Collapse

A common issue encountered while optimizing variational models via the ELBO is

posterior collapse
.
Intuitively, this results in a degenerate local optimum where the model learns to ignore the latent space.
With a discrete encoder, this translates into a deterministic mapping to a single latent state, regardless of the input.
From an information-theoretic point of view, optimizing the ELBO gives way to a trade-off between the minimization of

\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{D}
, where the feasible region is a convex set
(
Alemi et al. 2018
)
.
Posterior collapse occurs when
\mathbf{R}\approx 0

(
auto-decoding limit
).
On the other hand, one can achieve
\mathbf{D}\approx 0

(
auto-encoding limit
)
at the price of a higher rate.

Regularization terms.
Various solutions have been proposed in the literature to prevent posterior collapse.
They include
entropy regularization
(via
\alpha\in\mathopen{[}0,\infty\mathclose{[}
, e.g.,

Burke, Hristov, and Ramamoorthy 2019
;
Dong et al. 2020
) and

KL-scaling
(via
\beta\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
, e.g.,
Alemi et al. 2018
),
consisting in changing
\mathrm{ELBO}
to

\left\langle\alpha,\beta\right\rangle\text{-}\mathrm{ELBO}=-(\mathbf{D}+\beta\cdot\mathbf{R})+\alpha\cdot\displaystyle H(Q_{\iota})
, where

\displaystyle H(Q_{\iota})
denotes the entropy of an encoding distribution.
We choose to measure the entropy of the
marginal
encoder, given by
Q_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}}}\right)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s\sim\xi_{\pi}}\phi_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}\mid s}}\right)
.
Intuitively, this encourages the encoder to learn to make plenty use of the latent space.
The parameter
\beta
allows to interpolate between auto-encoding and auto-decoding behavior, which is not possible with the standard ELBO objective.

A drawback of these methods is that we no longer optimize a lower bound on the log-likelihood of the input while optimizing

\left\langle\alpha,\beta\right\rangle\text{-}\mathrm{ELBO}
.
In practice, setting up annealing schemes for
\alpha
and
\beta
allows to eventually recover
\mathrm{ELBO}
and avoid posterior collapse (
\alpha=0
and
\beta=1
matches
\mathrm{ELBO}
).

To enable meaningful use of latent space to represent input state-actions pairs,

to store transitions and sample them when optimizing
\mathrm{ELBO}

and let
p_{\left\langle s,a,r,s^{\prime}\right\rangle}

•

Bucket-based priority:
we partition the buffer in

buckets
.
Let

and

\nicefrac{{N}}{{b\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}}}\right)}}

and

of one.
This allows
\phi_{\iota}
to process states being infrequently visited under
\pi
and learn to fairly distribute
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
.

•

Loss-based priority:
we set
p_{\left\langle s,a,r,s^{\prime}\right\rangle}
to its individual transition loss,
which enables to learn improving the representation of states and actions that yield poor ELBO.

Experiments

The goal of our experiments
2
2

2

The code for conducting the experiments is available at
https://github.com/florentdelgrange/vae_mdp
is to evaluate the quality of the latent space model learned and the policy distilled via our VAE-MDP framework. This evaluation consists of:
an analysis of the training of the latent space model
and the benefits of our method to avoid posterior collapse,
assessing the quality of the abstraction learned via PAC local losses bounds, and
testing the performance of the distilled policy.
This allows to assess if the latent model learned yields a sound compression of the state-action space that retains the necessary information to optimize the return.
We evaluate our method on classic OpenAI environments
(
Brockman et al. 2016
)
with (i) continuous states and discrete actions (CartPole, MountainCar, and Acrobot), and (ii) where both, states and actions, are continuous (Pendulum and LunarLander).
We distill RL policies
\pi
learned via DQN
(
Mnih et al. 2015
)
for case (i), and SAC
(
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
for case (ii).
The exact setting and labeling functions used to reproduce our results
are provided in supplementary material (Appendix

).

Latent spaces.
Since latent spaces are trained to enable formal verification, we choose
\log_{2}|\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}|
and
|\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}|
ranging from
9

2

16

for a performance comparison of modern tools for a range of instances with large model size. Depending on the property to verify, the latent space may have to be finer since
(i) we need to reserve
|\mathbf{AP}|
bits in the representation of
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
for labels, and
(ii) this allows the agent to take more precise decisions over a finer partition of the latent space. We then select the model with the best trade-off between abstraction quality (measured via
\hat{L}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}}
and
\hat{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}}

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
in
\mathcal{M}
).

ELBO optimization.
In order to allow
\mathrm{ELBO}
to be trained efficiently, posterior collapse has to be tackled from the very first stages of training. In fact, we found that the KL-scaling and entropy regularization annealing schemes (cf. Sect.
4.3
) were necessary to avoid the latent space to collapse into a single state-action pair after only a few training steps.
We found the most efficient to start with an autoencoder behavior (
\beta_{0}=0
) and a large entropy regularizer (e.g.,
\alpha_{0}=10
) during the
10^{4}
first steps, and then anneal them via
\alpha_{t}=\alpha_{0}\cdot(1-\tau)^{t}
,
\beta_{t}=1-(1-\tau)^{t}
(e.g.,
\tau=10^{-5}
) to fully recover the original
\mathrm{ELBO}
in a second training phase.
We also found that prioritized experience replays further prevent posterior collapse during training (cf. Fig.
1
).
Fig.
2(a)
shows that training
\mathrm{ELBO}
this way results in a stable learning procedure that successfully minimizes
\mathbf{D}
while preventing an auto-decoding behavior by keeping
\mathbf{R}
away from
0
.

Local losses.
We compute the PAC local losses bounds presented in Sect.
3
along training steps (Fig.
2(b)
).
When the policy is eventually distilled (Fig.
2(c)
) and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
achieves performance similar to those of

\mathrm{ELBO}
successfully allows minimizing the local losses.
In every environment where we tested our method, a low reward loss is maintained while the transition loss reaches values in the interval
\mathopen{[}\nicefrac{{1}}{{5}},\nicefrac{{3}}{{5}}\mathclose{]}
for most of the instances.
These values are thus guaranteed to upper bound the expected bisimulation distance between
\mathcal{M}
and
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
as well as their value difference. We do not expect our approach to reach zero reward and transition loss though, since we pass from continuous to discrete spaces: the abstraction induced by our approach is always coarser than the original spaces, which translates in general to precision loss.
This precision loss is often encoded through the discrete probability transitions. For instance, a state
s
which deterministically transitions to a close state
s^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{M}
such that
\phi_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)=\,\overline{\!{s}}=\phi_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right)

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}}}\right)>0
.
Observe that the PAC computation of
\hat{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}}
is sensitive to the entropy of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}

is on the contrary not sensitive to this.
In practice, its value will thus often be lower than its approximated upper bound
\hat{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}}
.

The guarantees derived in Sect.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we presented VAE-MDPs, a framework for learning discrete latent models of unknown, continuous-spaces environments with bisimulation guarantees.
We detailed how such latent models can be learned by executing an RL policy in the environment and showed that the procedure yields a distilled version of the latter as a side effect.
To provide the guarantees, we introduced new local losses bounds aimed at discrete latent spaces with their PAC-efficient approximation algorithm derived from the execution of the distilled policy.
All this enables the verification of RL policies for unknown continuous MDPs.

Experimental results demonstrate the feasibility of our approach through the PAC bounds and the performance of the distilled policy achieved for various environments.
Our tool can also be used to highlight the lack of robustness of input policies when the distillation fails.

Complementary to safe-RL approaches addressed via formal methods, we emphasize the ability of our tool to be coupled with such algorithms when no model of the environment is known a priori.
The applicability of our method enables its use in future work for real-world case studies and
more complex settings, such as multi-agent systems.

Details on Discrepancy Measures

Let

be probability distributions over
\mathcal{X}
with density functions

and

Wasserstein.
The Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality allows formulating the Wasserstein distance between

and

W_{d}\left(P,Q\right)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{d}}\left|{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim P}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim Q}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right)}\right|,

is the set of 1-Lipschitz functions, i.e.,

.

Total Variation.
One can reformulate the total variation distance between

and
Q
as

\displaystyle d_{{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(P,Q}}\right)={}

\displaystyle\sup_{A\in\Sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X}}}\right)}|P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)-Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)|

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}|p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)|\,dx.

When

B.6

3.4
).

B.8
Proof of Lemma
3.4

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{M}
is ergodic, the agent acts in the BSCC induced by
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and each tuple
\left\langle s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1}\right\rangle
can be considered to be drawn independently from the stationary distribution
\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
of the BSCC in the limit.
The result follows then from the Hoeffding’s bound
(
Hoeffding 1963
)
:

•

consider the sequence of independent random variables
X^{\mathcal{R}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1}
such that

X^{\mathcal{R}}_{t}=|r_{t}-\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{R}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t}}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}_{t}}}\right)|\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]},

then

\hat{L}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X^{\mathcal{R}}_{t}
and

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\left|L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\star}-\hat{L}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\right|\geq\varepsilon}}\right)\leq\delta_{\mathcal{R}}
, with
\delta_{\mathcal{R}}=2e^{-2T\varepsilon^{2}}
and
L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\star}=L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}
if
\mathcal{R}
is defined over state-action pairs and
L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\star}=\dot{L}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}
if it is defined over transitions.

•

consider the sequence of random variables
X^{\mathbf{P}}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:T-1}
such that

X^{\mathbf{P}}_{t}=1-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t+1}}}\right)\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t}}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}_{t}}}\right)\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]},

The latent MDP
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
being discrete, we have

\displaystyle\dot{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,\,\overline{\!{a}},s^{\prime}\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}d_{{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s^{\prime}}}\right),\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),a}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,\,\overline{\!{a}},s^{\prime}\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\mid s^{\prime}}}\right)-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,\,\overline{\!{a}},s^{\prime}\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right)\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)}}\right)+\sum_{\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\set{\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right)}}\left|0-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)\right|}}\right)\right]

(because
\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\mid s^{\prime}}}\right)=1
if
\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right)=\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}
and
0
otherwise.)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,\,\overline{\!{a}},s^{\prime}\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right)\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)}}\right)+\sum_{\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\set{\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right)}}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,\,\overline{\!{a}},s^{\prime}\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right)\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,\,\overline{\!{a}},s^{\prime}\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\left[1-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}}}\right)\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)\right]

then
\hat{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X^{\mathbf{P}}_{t}
and
\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\left|\dot{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}-\hat{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\right|\geq\varepsilon}}\right)\leq\delta_{\mathbf{P}}
, with
\delta_{\mathbf{P}}=2e^{-2T\varepsilon^{2}}
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\left|L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\star}-\hat{L}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\right|\geq\varepsilon\vee\left|\dot{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}-\hat{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\right|\geq\varepsilon}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{\mathcal{R}}+\delta_{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle=2e^{-2T\varepsilon^{2}}+2e^{-2T\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle=4e^{-2T\varepsilon^{2}}.

We thus consider

, i.e.,
\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\delta}{4}}}\right)\geq-2T\varepsilon^{2}
and

, which yields

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\left|L_{\mathcal{R}}^{\star}-\hat{L}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\right|\leq\varepsilon\wedge\left|\dot{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}-\hat{L}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}\right|\leq\varepsilon}}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

∎

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

and

, then by applying Lemma

and Lemma

, we have

Lemma C.1
.

Let
\xi_{\pi}
be the stationary distribution of
\mathcal{M}_{\pi}
and

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{\tau}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\pi}[T]}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1}}}\right)\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s,a,r,s^{\prime}\sim\xi_{\pi}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a,r,s^{\prime}}}\right)

,

Proof.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a_{0}\sim\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s_{\mathit{I}}}}\right)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s_{1}\sim\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s_{\mathit{I}},a_{0}}}\right)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a_{1}\sim\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s_{1}}}\right)}\cdots\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s_{T}\sim\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s_{T-1},a_{T-1}}}\right)}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t},a_{t},\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1}}}\right),s_{t+1}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{\tau}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\pi}[t+1]}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1}}}\right).

Second, let

and

, also observe that

\displaystyle\xi_{\pi}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s\mid s_{\mathit{I}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathit{Traces}_{\mathcal{M}_{\pi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}\bm{1}_{=}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t},s}}\right)\,d\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Cyl\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:t},a_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:t-1}}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a,r,s^{\prime}}}\right)\cdot\int_{\mathit{Traces}_{\mathcal{M}_{\pi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}\bm{1}_{=}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{t},s}}\right)\,d\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Cyl\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:t},a_{\scriptscriptstyle 0:t-1}}}\right)}}\right)

Latent Distributions

Discrete latent state space.
W.l.o.g., we assume latent states to have a binary representation, we further see each bit as a Bernoulli random variable.
Let
{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in\mathopen{]}0,1\mathclose{]}
,
\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\lambda}\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
has a
relaxed Bernoulli distribution

\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\lambda}\sim\mathrm{RelaxedBernoulli}({\alpha},\lambda)
with logit
{\alpha}
and temperature parameter
\lambda
iff
(a) if
l
is a logistic sample with location parameter
{\alpha}\lambda^{-1}
and scale parameter
\lambda^{-1}
, i.e.,
l\sim\mathrm{Logistic}({\alpha}\lambda^{-1},\lambda^{-1})
, then
\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\lambda}=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(l}}\right)
,
\sigma
being the
sigmoid
function,
(b)
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\mathrm{RelaxedBernoulli}({\alpha},\lambda)=\mathrm{Bernoulli}({\alpha})
, meaning
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\textstyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\lambda}=1)=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\alpha}}}\right)
, and
(c) let
p_{{\alpha},\lambda}
be the density of
\mathrm{RelaxedBernoulli}({\alpha},\lambda)
, then
p_{{\alpha},\lambda}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\lambda}}}\right)
is log-convex in
\,\overline{\!{s}}_{\lambda}
.
Since the logistic distribution belongs to the location-scale family, it is fully reparameterizable, i.e.,
l\sim\mathrm{Logistic}({\alpha}\lambda^{-1},\lambda^{-1})\equiv x\sim\mathrm{Logistic}(0,1)\text{ and }l=\frac{{\alpha}+x}{\lambda}
.
In practice, we train
\phi_{\iota}
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
to infer and generate
\log_{2}|\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}|
logits, and we anneal
\lambda
to
0
during training while using Bernoulli distributions for evaluation.
As suggested by
Maddison, Mnih, and Teh 2017
, we use two different temperature parameters and we fix their initial values to
\lambda_{\phi_{\iota}}=\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
for the encoder distribution and
\lambda_{\mathbf{P}_{\theta}}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
for the latent transition function.

Discrete latent action space.
We learn discrete latent distributions for
Q_{\iota}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
by learning the logits of continuous relaxation of discrete distributions, through the
Gumbel softmax trick
.
Let
n=|\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}|
, and
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}=\set{\,\overline{\!{a}}_{1},\dots,\,\overline{\!{a}}_{n}}
.
Drawing
\,\overline{\!{a}}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Categorical}(\bm{{\alpha}})
from a discrete (categorical) distribution in
\mathcal{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}}}\right)
with logits parameter
\bm{{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is equivalent to applying an
\argmax
operator to the sum of
Gumble samples
and these logits, i.e.,
i=\argmax_{k}\left[\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}-\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\log\bm{\epsilon}_{k}}}\right)\right]
, where
\bm{\epsilon}\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}^{n}
is a uniform noise.
The sampling operator being fully reparameterizable here,
\argmax
is however not derivable, preventing the operation to be optimized through gradient descent.
The proposed solution is to replace the
\argmax
operator by a softmax function with temperature parameter
\lambda\in\mathopen{]}0,\left(n-1\right)^{-1}\mathclose{]}
.

Concretely,
\bm{x}\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}^{n}
has a
relaxed discrete distribution

\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{RelaxedCategorical}(\bm{{\alpha}},\lambda)
with logits
\bm{{\alpha}}
, temperature parameter
\lambda
iff
(a) let
\bm{\epsilon}\in[0,1]^{n}
be a uniform noise and
\bm{G}_{k}={-\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\log\bm{\epsilon}_{k}}}\right)}
, then
\bm{x}_{k}={\frac{\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}+\bm{G}_{k}}}\right)\lambda^{-1}}}\right)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bm{{\alpha}}_{i}+\bm{G}_{i}}}\right)\lambda^{-1}}}\right)}}}
for each
k\in[n]
,
(b)
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\mathrm{RelaxedCategorical}(\bm{{\alpha}},\lambda)=\mathrm{Categorical}(\bm{{\alpha}})
, meaning
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\textstyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\bm{x}_{k}=1)=\frac{\exp{\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp{\bm{{\alpha}}_{i}}}
,
and (c) let
p_{\bm{{\alpha}},\lambda}
be the density of
\mathrm{RelaxedCategorical}(\bm{{\alpha}},\lambda)
, then
p_{\bm{{\alpha}},\lambda}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\bm{x}}}}\right)
is log-convex in
{\bm{x}}
.
In practice, we train
Q_{\iota}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
to infer and generate logits parameter
\bm{{\alpha}}
, and we anneal
\lambda
from
(n-1)^{-1}
to
0
during training while using discrete distributions for evaluation.
Again, as prescribed by
Maddison, Mnih, and Teh 2017
, we use two different temperature parameters, one for
Q_{\iota}^{\mathcal{A}}
and another one for
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
.

C.2
Prioritized Experience Replay

Modern RL techniques allow to learn the parameters
\theta
of a policy, model, or value function, by processing encountered experiences
\eta

via a loss function
L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta,\theta}}\right)
.
If these
are processed sequentially and discarded after all parameter updates, the latter are strongly correlated and rare events are quickly forgotten.

Prioritized experience replay buffers

(
Schaul et al. 2016
)
are finite data structures
\mathcal{D}
that allow to overcome both issues by
(i) storing experiences
\eta
with some assigned priority
p_{\eta}
, and
(ii) producing
\eta\sim\mathcal{D}
according to
P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)=p_{\eta}^{\varsigma}/\sum_{\eta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}}p_{\eta^{\prime}}^{\varsigma}
,
\varsigma\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
, which allows minimizing
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[w_{\eta}\cdot L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta,\theta}}\right)\right]
for
importance sampling weight

w_{\eta}>0
.
The replay buffer
\mathcal{D}
is
uniform
if
\varsigma=0
.
Otherwise, when
\varsigma>0
, a bias is introduced because priorities change the real distribution of experiences and consequently the estimates of the expectation of
L
.
This bias is alleviated via
w_{\eta}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|\mathcal{D}|\cdot P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)}}\right)^{-\omega}
for each
\eta\in\mathcal{D}
,
\omega\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
, where
\omega=1
fully corrects it.

We use prioritized replay buffers to store transitions and sample them when optimizing our loss function, i.e.,
\mathrm{ELBO}
.
This allows to alleviate the posterior collapse problem, as shown in Figure
3
.
We introduced two priority functions, yielding a bucket-based and a loss-based prioritized replay buffer.
In the following, we elaborate how precisely we assign priorities via the loss-based priority function.

Loss-based priorities.
Draw
\left\langle s,a,r,s^{\prime}\right\rangle\sim\xi_{\pi}
, we aim at assigning
p_{\left\langle s,a,r,s^{\prime}\right\rangle}
to its
individual transition loss
, i.e.,

L_{\mathrm{ELBO}}
:
for
\,\overline{\!{s}},\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\sim\phi_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,s^{\prime}}}\right)
,
\,\overline{\!{a}}\sim Q_{\iota}^{\mathcal{A}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}},a}}\right)
if
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}\neq\mathcal{A}
and
\,\overline{\!{a}}=a
else,

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{ELBO}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\left\langle s,a,r,s^{\prime}\right\rangle,\left\langle\,\overline{\!{s}},\,\overline{\!{a}},\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\right\rangle}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\log P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}}}\right)-\log P_{\theta}^{\mathcal{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r\mid\,\overline{\!{s}},\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)-\log\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{a}}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}}}\right)+\log\phi_{\iota}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\mid s^{\prime}}}\right)

\displaystyle-\bm{1}_{=}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}},\mathcal{A}}}\right)\cdot\log\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}}}}\right)-\bm{1}_{\neq}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}},\mathcal{A}}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log\psi_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid\,\overline{\!{s}},\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)+\log\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}},\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)-\log Q_{\iota}^{\mathcal{A}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{a}}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}},a}}\right)}}\right).

Notice that we cannot assign directly
p_{\left\langle s,a,r,s^{\prime}\right\rangle}
to
L_{\mathrm{ELBO}}
since priorities require to be strictly positive to compute the replay buffer distribution.
A solution is to pass
L_{\mathrm{ELBO}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\left\langle s,a,r,s^{\prime}\right\rangle,\left\langle\,\overline{\!{s}},\,\overline{\!{a}},\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\right\rangle}}\right)
to the logistic function, i.e.,

p_{\left\langle s,a,r,s^{\prime}\right\rangle}=x^{\star}\cdot\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(L_{\mathrm{ELBO}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\left\langle s,a,r,s^{\prime}\right\rangle,\left\langle\,\overline{\!{s}},\,\overline{\!{a}},\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\right\rangle}}\right)-x_{0}}}\right)}}\right)

where
x^{\star}>0
is the scale,
k>0
is the growth rate, and
x_{0}
is the location of the logistic.
In practice, we set
x^{\star}
to the desired maximum priority and we maintain upper (
L^{\max}
) and lower bounds (
L^{\min}
) on the loss during learning.
Along training steps, we tune these values according to the current loss and we set
x_{0}=\frac{L^{\max}-L^{\min}}{2}
and
k=\frac{x^{\star}}{L^{\max}-L^{\min}}
.

Appendix D
Details on RL Policy Distillation

Concretely, the goal of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
is to mimic the behavior of
\pi
through the latent spaces.
However, executing
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
in
\mathcal{M}
via
\phi_{\iota}
and
\psi_{\iota,\theta}
as described in Sect.
3
to evaluate our latent space model (e.g., Lem.
3.4
) may
result in performance loss compared to that offered under
\pi
.
This is due to the abstraction learned via the ELBO: we do not expect a zero-distortion by encoding the state-action space through the VAE (and
\mathcal{M}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}}
to behave exactly like
\mathcal{M}_{\pi}
), but to minimize it.
Due to this remaining distortion, decisions taken according to
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
may result in unreachable states under
\pi
, causing
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
to behave poorly in that particular states since the learning process only allows to learn from transitions produced via
\pi
.
We propose two approaches that can be used alone or together to alleviate this problem.

Globally-robust RL policy.
In general, policies
\pi
learned through deep-RL provide
local
performance, meaning they provide good performance in states
s\in\mathcal{S}
that are likely to be reached from
s_{\mathit{I}}
by executing
\pi
.
It is, however, often suitable to distil and verify
globally-robust
RL policies, i.e., policies trained to provide good
global
performance, in a wider range of states, in particular those that are likely to be reached through the distillation.
Training globally robust RL policies can be achieved by allowing the agent to learn (or pursuing its training) in a modified version of
\mathcal{M}
, where
s_{\mathit{I}}
is picked at random from
\mathcal{S}
.
Training
\pi
this way will usually take longer since this may require more exploration.
The degree of randomness can be decided according to the exploration/exploitation trade-off and the additional training time allowed.
Moreover, we argue that this additional time is acceptable in our context since enabling and applying model checking are time-consuming tasks by nature.

\varepsilon
-mimic.
Assuming
\pi
is sufficiently robust for the distillation, we allow the encoder to process states reachable under both
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
and
\pi
by learning from a mixture
\hat{\pi}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}
of the two, defined as
\hat{\pi}^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)=\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)\cdot(1-\varepsilon)+\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)\cdot\varepsilon
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
,
a\in\mathcal{A}
, given
\varepsilon\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
.
Given the robustness assumption, states entered according to a decision
a\sim\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)
(produced with probability
\varepsilon
) should be efficiently processed
by
\pi
.
In practice, we start with a high value of
\varepsilon
(e.g.,
\varepsilon=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
) to encourage exploration of states possibly reachable under
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
and we anneal it to
0
during training.

E.3
Discrete Latent Dynamics

As pointed out by
Corneil, Gerstner, and Brea 2018
, the latent transition function learned via the continuous relaxation takes a simple form when discretized (i.e., when
\lambda_{\mathbf{P}_{\theta}}\to 0
):
the latent variables produced this way via
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}
are independent Bernoullis, meaning each bit of
\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\sim\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\,\overline{\!{s}},\,\overline{\!{a}}}}\right)
are independent, for any
\,\overline{\!{s}}\in\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}},a\in\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{A}}}
.
To remedy this, we adopt the approach of
Corneil, Gerstner, and Brea 2018
and we reconstruct the latent dynamics via
frequency estimation
(e.g.,
Bazille et al. 2020
) to check the abstraction quality in a PAC manner (cf. Fig.
2
): for each environment, we evaluated the latent space model quality based on frequency-estimated latent dynamics from transitions collected by (repeatedly) simulating the environment for
2\cdot 10^{5}
steps.
