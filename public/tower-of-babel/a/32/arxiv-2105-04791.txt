Poly-Cauchy numbers - the combinatorics behind
Beáta Bényi, José L. Ramírez

Abstract
. We introduce poly-Cauchy permutations that are enumerated by the poly-Cauchy numbers. We provide combinatorial proofs for several identities involving poly-Cauchy numbers and some of their generalizations. The aim of this work is to demonstrate the power and beauty of the elementary combinatorial approach.

1.
Introduction

Poly-Cauchy numbers were defined by Komatsu
[
11
]
motivated by the interesting properties of poly-Bernoulli numbers. While since the introduction many combinatorial interpretations and investigations appeared in the literature about poly-Bernoulli numbers, there is no such direction about the poly-Cauchy numbers yet.

Let
n
and
k
be integers with
n\geq 0
and
k\geq 1
.
The
poly-Cauchy numbers of the first kind
are defined by

\displaystyle c_{n}^{(k)}=n!\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\cdots\int_{0}^{1}}_{k}\binom{t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{k}}{n}\,dt_{1}dt_{2}\cdots dt_{k},

Moreover, the exponential generating function of
c_{n}^{(k)}
is given by

\displaystyle\text{\rm Lif}_{k}(\ln(1+t))=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}^{(k)}\frac{t^{n}}{n!},

where

\text{\rm Lif}_{k}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!(n+1)^{k}}

is the
k
-th
polylogarithm factorial function
. Similarly, the
poly-Cauchy numbers of the second kind
are defined by

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n}^{(k)}=n!\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\cdots\int_{0}^{1}}_{k}\binom{-t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{k}}{n}\,dt_{1}\cdots dt_{k},

and the exponential generating function of
\hat{c}_{n}^{(k)}
is given by

\displaystyle\text{\rm Lif}_{k}(-\ln(1+t))=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{c}_{n}^{(k)}\frac{t^{n}}{n!},\quad(k\in{\mathbb{Z}}).

Notice that the above generating functions have a meaning as a formal power series even if
k
is non-positive. Therefore, the poly Cauchy numbers of both kinds can be defined also for negative
k
. Poly-Cauchy numbers of the first kind have the explicit formula

\displaystyle c_{n}^{(k)}=(-1)^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{(m+1)^{k}},

where
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}
denotes the unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind that counts the number of permutations of
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
into
m
non-empty cycles, and can algebraically defined for instance by the following identity:

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}x^{m}=x(x+1)\cdots(x+n-1).

Poly-Cauchy numbers of the second kind have the explicit formula

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n}^{(k)}=(-1)^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\frac{1}{(m+1)^{k}}.

For more properties about these numbers see for example
[
8
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

In the literature, along the theory of poly-Bernoulli numbers and polynomials, the (analytical) theory of poly-Cauchy numbers were also developed, including several identities and generalizations (polynomials,
q

2.
Poly-Cauchy permutations

In this section we introduce a new family of permutations enumerated by the sequence
\hat{c}_{n,k}
. First we recall some well-known facts about permutations. Permutations can be defined and seen from different point of views, as a map, as an arrangement, as a product of cycles, etc. It is useful to switch between these point of views, however it is always important to be conscious of the particular approach that is used in an argument. In our case cycles will play the crucial role.

Any permutation is the product of cycles, and cycles can be recorded uniquely: we write the largest element of each cycle first and we arrange the cycles in increasing order of their first elements. This representation is called
canonical cycle notation
.

On the other hand, considering a permutation as a map,
\pi:[n]\rightarrow[n]
,
\pi_{i}=\pi(i)
each permutation can be written in one-line notation,

\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}
. There is a well-known bijection between permutations of
[n]
written in cycle notation and written in one-line-notation, which we recall now. We say that
\pi_{i}
is a
left-to-right maximum
if, for all
k<i
, we have
\pi_{k}<\pi_{i}
. For example, the permutation
3261457
has three left-to-right maxima. These are the entries

3,6
, and
7
. Let
\pi
be a permutation of
n
elements written in canonical cycle notation, and let
\pi^{\prime}
be the permutation written in the one-line notation that is obtained from
\pi
by omitting all parentheses. Notice that the canonical cycle notation can be obtained from the left-to-right maxima of the permutation.

Definition 1
.

Let
n
and
k
be non negative integers. A
(n,k)
-poly-Cauchy permutation
is a permutation of
[n+k]
with the following properties:

(A1)

each maximal contiguous substring whose support belongs to
\{n+1,n+2,\ldots,n+k\}
is increasing,

(A2)

the left-to-right maxima of the maximal contiguous substrings whose support belongs to
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
are increasing from left to right, when “reading” the whole permutation.

Example 2
.

A
(9,6)
-poly-Cauchy permutation is

\displaystyle\pi=11-13-6-1-2-15-7-4-8-10-12-14-9-5-3.

We introduce now some terminology and notation. Let
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
denote the set of all
(n,k)
-poly-Cauchy permutations. For simplicity of notation we use the notation of red elements for
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n}\}
and the notation of blue elements for
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}\}
instead of the set
\{n+1,n+2,\ldots,n+k\}
(which is essentially the same). Given an
(n,k)
-poly-Cauchy permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
we will denote the arrangement of the elements
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n}\}
, seeing itself as a permutation of
[n]
as
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi^{n}}
. Similarly, the permutation/or ordered partition on the elements
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}\}
as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\pi^{k}}
.

Theorem 5
.

The
(n,k)
-poly-Cauchy permutations are enumerated by the sequence
\hat{c}_{n,k}
.

Proof.

The
(n,k)
-poly-Cauchy permutations can be constructed by the following procedure:

(1)

First, permute the elements
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n}\}
, and write it into canonical cycle notation. This step will ensure the condition (A2).

(2)

Secondly, insert the elements of
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}\}
in between the cycles (before or after). Notice that we have
m+1
spaces and some of them may be left empty. The elements in between the cycles are written in increasing order. This step will ensure the condition (A1).

This way we obtain an alternating sequence of cycles of
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n}\}
and blocks of
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}\}
.
It is clear that the number of
(n,k)
-poly-Cauchy permutations constructed by this procedure is given by

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}(m+1)^{k}.

In Figure
2
we give an example of this procedure for a
(9,6)
-poly-Cauchy permutation.

In the following theorems we will give combinatorial arguments of some identities involving poly-Cauchy numbers, poly-Bernoulli numbers, Stirling numbers of both kinds, and Eulerian numbers.

Theorem 9
.

For non-negative
n
and
k
it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{n}(-1)^{m}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\hat{c}_{m,k}=(-1)^{n}(n+1)^{k}.

Proof.

The right hand side is (besides the
(-1)
factor) a poly-Cauchy permutation where the elements
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n}\}
are in increasing order (with some blocks of the elements
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}\}
in between, before or after them),
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi^{n}}
is the identity permutation. We call such a permutation
id-poly-Cauchy permutation
.

We define an involution,
\phi
, to show that the left hand side counts id-poly-Cauchy permutations of size
(n,k)
. The involution
\phi
is defined on the set of poly-Cauchy permutations with an extra structure on the red elements. Partition the elements
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n}\}
into
m
non-empty blocks (write the elements of a block into decreasing order, and consider the greatest element of a block as its “representative”). Then permute these blocks into a permutation with
j
cycles,
j\leq m\leq n
. Insert between the cycles of blocks the blue elements,
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}\}
. in order to obtain a
(n,k)
-poly-Cauchy permutation with an extra structure on the substrings of red elements (the blocks). Note
j
is the number of left-to-right maxima among the elements
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n}\}
.

For example, one set partition of
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}9}\}
into
m=5
blocks is
B_{1}=\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}4},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}\},B_{2}=\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}5},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}3}\},B_{3}=\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}6}\},B_{4}=\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}8},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}7}\},B_{5}=\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}9}\}
. Now, we permute these five blocks to obtain a permutation in
j=3
cycles. For example, the permutation
(32)(4)(51)
corresponds to the
(B_{3},B_{2})(B_{4})(B_{5},B_{1})
. Finally, we insert the blue blocks, for example we can insert the blocks
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}3}\},\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}4},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}5}\},\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}6}\},
and we obtain the
(9,6)
-poly-Cauchy permutation
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}13}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}653}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}87}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}245}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}9421}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}6}
.

If the number of cycles is
n
, that is
j=n
, that can happen only one way, every block contains only one element and each cycle one block, hence, after inserting the blocks of
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}\}
, we obtain an id-poly-Cauchy permutations.

Otherwise, i.e., if the number of cycle is less than
n
, then there is at least one cycle that contains at least two elements. Let
a
be the greatest element which is not alone in a cycle.

If
a
is in a block alone, merge it with the block that follows it in the cycle. If
a
is not alone separate it into a block to be alone.

This way we defined an involution,
\phi
, between the set of poly-Cauchy permutations with extra structure with even number of left-to-right maxima in
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi^{n}}
and odd number of left-to-right maxima in
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi^{n}}
.

∎

Many formulas given in the literature are consequences of Theorem
9
and modified forms of the involution
\phi
can be used to provide combinatorial proofs for them. For instance, Komatsu presents a formula in
[
14
]
(Theorem 3) that can be seen as a generalization of Theorem
9
. Let
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{r}
denote the
r
-Stirling numbers of the second kind that count for instance the number of partitions of
[n]
into

non-empty blocks such that
\{1,2,\ldots,r\}
are in distinct blocks. These numbers were introduced by Broder
[
7
]
.

Theorem 10

(
[
14
]
, Theorem 3)
.

For non-negative
n
,
k
and
r
, with
r\leq n
, it holds

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=r}^{n}(-1)^{j}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{j}_{r}\hat{c}_{j,k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r}{\ell}(n-r+\ell+1)^{k}.

Proof.

The idea of the proof is the same as in the proof of Theorem
9
. Partition the elements
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n}\}
into
j
non-empty blocks such that the elements
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}r}\}
are in distinct blocks. As usual, we call these elements
special elements
and the remaining elements,
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}r+1},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n}\}
, as
non-special elements
. Arrange the blocks into
i
cycles (
i\leq j\leq n
). Merge the so obtained permutation with blocks of
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}\}
in order to obtain a
(n,k)
-poly-Cauchy permutation with an extra structure on the substrings of red elements (the blocks). As before, if the number of cycles is
n
, every block contains only one element, and each cycle one block, hence after merging with the blocks of
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}\}
we obtain an id-poly-Cauchy permutations.

If
j\not=n
, there is at least one cycle that contains at least two elements. Note that the involution
\phi
, we defined in the proof of Theorem
9
can not be used in that form, since we can not merge blocks containing the special elements. We will modify slightly the definition of
a
, the base element of our involution,
\phi^{\prime}
. Let
a
be the greatest
non-special
element which is not alone in a cycle.

If such an
a
exists, we can apply the involution
\phi
based on this element, i.e., if
a
is in a block alone, merge it with the block that follows it in the cycle. If
a
is not alone separate it into a block to be alone.

However, such an
a
does not have to exist. If such an
a
does not exist, it means, all the non-special elements are in a distinct cycle, (and so distinct blocks), while the special elements are in distinct blocks by definition but not necessarily in distinct cycles.
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi^{n}}
is a permutation of the form:
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi^{n}}=(c_{1})(c_{2})\cdots(c_{\ell})(r+1)(r+2)\cdots(n)
, where
\ell
is the number of cycles into the special elements are ordered. Note that these cycles contain only special elements (otherwise
a
with the required property would exist). The blue elements are inserted in the poly-Cauchy permutation in between these cycles of
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi^{n}}
(before or after).

The involution
\phi^{\prime}
shows that the left hand side of the identity (
1
) counts such poly-Cauchy permutations.

On the other hand, such poly-Cauchy permutations can be constructed by the following procedure. First arrange the
r
special elements into
\ell
cycles, and take the non-special elements as fix points. Record this permutation in canonical cycle notation and insert the blue elements
[k]
in between, before or after the cycles decoding this insertion by a word
w=w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{k}
, where
w_{i}
is the number of cycles to the left of the element
i
. The number of ways of doing this is clearly given by the formula of the right hand side of the Identity (
1
).

\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}\}

Theorem 11

(
[
16
]
, Theorem 3.2)
.

For non-negative
n
and
k

\displaystyle B_{n}^{(-k)}=(-1)^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}m!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{\ell}\hat{c}_{\ell}^{(-k)}.

Proof.

Let us rewrite the identity as follow:

\displaystyle B_{n}^{(-k)}=(-1)^{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}m!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(-1)^{\ell}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{\ell}\hat{c}_{\ell,k}.

By Theorem

the inner sum equals to

\displaystyle B_{n}^{(-k)}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}m!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}(-1)^{n+m}(m+1)^{k}.

Combinatorially, by the proof of the Theorem
9
after applying the involution,
\phi

\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(-1)^{\ell}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{\ell}\hat{c}_{\ell,k}
counts
(m,k)
-id-poly-Cauchy permutations. Now let
A_{m}
be the set of Callan permutations with at most

different red blocks (blocks of elements
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n}\}

(n,k)
-Callan permutation from

as follows. Given an id-poly-Cauchy permutation, permute first the

red elements in
m!
ways. Then partition
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n}\}
into

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}B_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}B_{2}},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}B_{m}}

in the poly-Cauchy permutation by a block

∎

The other direction can be seen similarly combinatorially.

Theorem 12

(
[
16
]
, Theorem 3.1)
.

For non-negative
n
and

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n}^{(-k)}=(-1)^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\frac{1}{m!}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{m}{\ell}B_{\ell}^{(-k)}.

Take now a
(\ell,k)
-Callan permutation and a permutation of

into

Next we provide a combinatorial proof for the closed formula of poly-Cauchy numbers.

Theorem 14
.

For non-negative
n
and
k
it holds

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}j!\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{j+1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k+1}{j+1}.

Proof.

We construct the
(n,k)
-poly-Cauchy permutations as follows. Let
\overline{K}
be the set
[k]
extended by a special element
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}*}

\overline{K}
into
j+1
non-empty blocks. Second, order the set
[n]
into
\ell

. Then we choose
j

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}*}
) directly before each cycle, so we have
\ell(\ell-1)\cdots(\ell-j+1)=\binom{\ell}{j}j!
options to insert and organize these blocks. Finally, the extra block, that contains
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}*}
is inserted after the last red cycle, at the end of the arrangement, and we delete the element
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}*}

our poly-Cauchy permutation ends with a red element, if it contains also other elements, it ends with a blue element. Therefore we have:

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k+1}{j+1}\sum_{\ell=j}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{\ell}\binom{\ell}{j}j!=\sum_{j=0}^{k}j!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k+1}{j+1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{j+1}.

Notice that in the last equality we use the identity
\sum_{\ell=j}^{n}\binom{\ell}{j}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{\ell}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{j+1}

∎

In the following two theorems we give recurrence relations to calculate the sequence

Theorem 15
.

For

and

we have

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k}=(n-1)\hat{c}_{n-1,k}+\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\hat{c}_{n-1,k-i}.

Proof.

The greatest red element,

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi^{n}}

An
(n,k)
-poly-Cauchy permutation where

i

can be empty) in a block say
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}

(n-1,k-i)
-poly-Cauchy permutation and extend it by the sequence of
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}n}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}
at the end. This can be done in
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\hat{c}_{n-1,k-i}
ways.

In order to show that

-poly-Cauchy permutations where

we establish a bijection.

Given an
(n-1,k)
-poly-Cauchy permutation

-poly-Cauchy permutation

and
\pi^{k}
. Consider

we be shifted to the end, and other cycles may shifted to the left by one. Since each step can be reversed, this procedure is a bijection, which implies our statement.

Theorem 17
.

For
n\geq 1
and

we have

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(n-1)_{i}\binom{k}{j}\hat{c}_{n-1-i,k-j}.

Proof.

Let
j

The
Eulerian numbers

\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
counts the number of permutations
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}
with
k-1
descents, that is
k-1=|\{i\in[n-1]:\pi_{i}>\pi_{i+1}\}|
. Note that a permutation

, and

Theorem 18
.

For non-negative
n
and

it holds

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{k-i}{m-i}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{i}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{m+1}.

Proof.

We turn now our point of view a little over and consider the construction of a poly-Cauchy permutation as inserting red cycles into a permutation of blue elements,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\pi^{k}}
. The properties of poly-Cauchy permutations require that the ascending runs of

are separated by a red cycle. But it is not forbidden to insert cycles after an ascent, cutting ascending runs this way into more pieces. Hence, the

-poly-Cauchy permutations can be constructed by the following process. Take a permutation of

with

3.
Poly-Cauchy polynomials of the second kind

This section introduces a combinatorial interpretation for the poly-Cauchy polynomials.
The poly-Cauchy polynomials of the second kind, denoted by
\hat{c}_{n}^{k}(z)
, where introduced by Kamano and Komatsu
[
10
]
by using the following analytical formula

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n}^{(k)}(z):=n!\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\cdots\int_{0}^{1}}_{k}\binom{-x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{k}-z}{n}\,dx_{1}\,dx_{2}\cdots dx_{k}.

The above definition is equivalent to the explicit formula

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n}^{k}(z)=(-1)^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{m}{i}\frac{(-z)^{i}}{(m-i+1)^{k}}.

We consider poly-Cauchy polynomials of the second kind without negative
k
-indices and ignore the negative sign, i.e.,

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k}(z):=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{m}{i}(-1)^{i}(m-i+1)^{k}z^{i}

We define
partial poly-Cauchy permutations
as a pair
P=(\pi,\sigma)
, where the reduction of
\pi
on the red elements is a partial permutation of
[n]
(not all elements of
[n]
are included) and
\sigma
is a permutation of the remaining elements of
[n]
.
Further, we define the weight of a partial poly-Cauchy permutation,
w(P)=w(\pi,\sigma)
as the number of cycles in the permutation
\sigma
.

Example 19
.

The weight of the partial poly-Cauchy permutation
({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}24}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}61}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}6}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}78}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}135}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}93},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}(24)(5)})
is
2
.

Let
\mathcal{PP}_{n,k}
denote the set of partial poly-Cauchy permutations.

Theorem 20
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathcal{PP}_{n,k}}(-z)^{w(P)}=\hat{c}_{n,k}(z).

Proof.

The sum is clearly equal to the formula (
4
) according to our previous considerations.
∎

Recall the well-known relation between the falling factorial
(x)_{n}=x(x-1)\cdots(x-n+1)
and the (signed) Stirling number of the first kind,
s(n,k)
:

\displaystyle(x)_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}s(n,k)x^{k}.

From the combinatorial point of view this identity expresses that if we weight a permutation
\pi
with the number of its cycles, i.e.,
w(\pi)=\#\,\mbox{of cycles}
, we have

(-1)^{n}(x)_{n}=\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}(-x)^{w(\pi)},

where
S_{n}
denotes as usual the set of permutations of
[n]
.
The following theorem is based on this classical result.

Theorem 21

(
[
17
]
, Theorem 7)
.

For non-negative
n
and
k
it holds

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n}^{(-k)}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\binom{n}{m}\hat{c}_{n-m}^{(-k)}(z)_{m}.

Proof.

Let
m
be the number of elements that are contained in
\sigma
in the partial poly-Cauchy permutation
P=(\pi,\sigma)
. Choose these
m
elements in
\binom{n}{m}
ways and construct
\pi
from the remaining elements in
\hat{c}_{n-m,k}
ways. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathcal{PP}_{n,k}}(-z)^{w(P)}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\binom{n}{m}\hat{c}_{n-m,k}(-1)^{m}(z)_{m}.

Since
\hat{c}_{n-m,k}=(-1)^{n-m}\hat{c}_{n-m}^{(-k)}
, the theorem follows.
∎

The theorems proven in the previous section can be generalized easily to partial poly-Cauchy permutations, and hence to poly-Cauchy polynomials. As an example we present the combinatorial proof for the generalization of Theorem
9
.

Theorem 22

(
[
10
]
, Theorem 4)
.

For non-negative
n
and
k
it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{n}(-1)^{m}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\hat{c}_{m,k}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}(-1)^{i}z^{i}(n-i+1)^{k}.

Proof.

Applying the involution
\phi
of the proof of Theorem
9
on the set of partial poly-Cauchy permutations,
\mathcal{PP}
, we recognize that only such partial poly-Cauchy permutations do not cancel in that the red elements are in increasing order. This means now that
\sigma
is also fixed as an identity permutation on the elements that are contained there. So the weight of
\sigma
is simply
(-z)^{i}
, if
i
is the size of
\sigma
.
∎

As we see the proof is straightforward, so we omit the proof of other generalizations. Instead, we show combinatorially two identities that are typical for polynomials. Moreover, in these proofs we introduce another combinatorial interpretation of the poly-Cauchy polynomials (which arises also very naturally).

Looking at the formula (
5
) we can give the combinatorial interpretation for
z
positive integers as follow. Consider now a poly-Cauchy permutation with
z
ordered boxes. Choose some elements of
[n]
and put them into one of the boxes and form from the remaining elements a poly-Cauchy permutation. We call such a construction an
extended poly-Cauchy permutations
. Having this picture in mind, the following two identities are immediate.

Theorem 23

(
[
17
]
)
.

We have

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k}(x+1)=\hat{c}_{n,k}(x)+n\hat{c}_{n-1,k}(x).

Proof.

The left hand side counts extended poly-Cauchy permutations with
x+1
boxes. There are two cases, the last box contains an element, or not. If the last box is empty, we can delete it and the number of such extended permutations is just
\hat{c}_{n,k}(x)
. If the last box contains any of the
n
elements (that can be chosen in
n
ways) the remaining
n-1
elements create with the
k
elements an extended poly-Cauchy permutation with
x
boxes.
∎

Theorem 24

(
[
17
]
, Eq. (33))
.

(7)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k}(x+y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\hat{c}_{j,k}(x)(y)_{n-j}

Proof.

The left hand side counts extended poly-Cauchy permutations with
x+y
boxes. We obtain such an object by choosing
j
elements out of
[n]
from that we construct an extended poly-Cauchy permutation with
x
boxes, and we put the remaining
n-j
elements into the remaining
y
boxes. This construction corresponds to the right hand side.
∎

4.
Other generalizations

In this section we recall some of the generalizations of poly-Bernoulli numbers and possible extensions of our combinatorial model in these directions.

4.1.
Shifted poly-Cauchy numbers

Let
\alpha
be a positive real number. The shifted poly-Cauchy numbers of the second kind have the explicit formula (
[
19
]
)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,\alpha}^{k}:=(-1)^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\frac{1}{(m+\alpha)^{k}}.

Let us define the objects
augmented poly-Cauchy permutations
. Such a permutation is a poly-Cauchy permutation ending with a red element augmented with
\alpha
boxes at the end. Into the boxes we can place some blue elements, boxes may be empty. An example with
\alpha=3
is given below. The boxes are indicated with
\alpha-1

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}49}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}612}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}6}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}748}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}13}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}953}||{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}27}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}58}.

Theorem 25
.

The
(n,k)
augmented poly-Cauchy permutations are enumerated by the shifted poly-Cauchy numbers,
\hat{c}_{n,k,\alpha}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}(m+\alpha)^{k}
.

Proof.

The difference to the base case is that the possible places where blue elements can be inserted are augmented by
\alpha-1
bars. In a poly-Cauchy permutation blue elements can be inserted between (before or after) red cycles. Hence, there are now
m+\alpha
possibilities, when we consider a red permutation with
m
cycles.
∎

A closed formula of the shifted poly-Cauchy numbers, the generalization of Theorem
14
, can be shown combinatorially as follow.

Theorem 26

(
[
19
]
, Theorem 10)
.

For
n
,
k
, and
\alpha
non-negative integers it holds

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k,\alpha}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{i}j!\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{j+1}\binom{k}{i}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{i}{j}\alpha^{k-i}.

Proof.

Let
k-i
be the number of blue elements in the boxes. We choose these elements in
\binom{k}{i}
ways and put them into the boxes in
\alpha^{k-i}
ways. To the left of the boxes there is a poly-Cauchy permutation on the red elements
[n]
, and blue elements
[k]
, such that the last element is red. The argument of Theorem
14
has to be a slightly modified to take the type of the last element into account. Namely, we do not need the extra block, so we do not extend the blue element set now with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}*}
. We take a partition of the
i
blue elements into

blocks in
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{i}{j}
ways and insert each block directly
before
the chosen cycles.
∎

elements with
k
cycles with the restriction that each cycle contains at least
m
items. So, the sequence
\hat{c}_{n,k,\geq m}:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{i}_{\geq m}(i+1)^{k}
counts the number of poly-Cauchy permutations of
[n+k]
, such that the size of each cycle in the cycle decomposition of the permutation whose support belongs to
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
is at least
m
.

Example 28
.

For
n=3
and
k=1
,
m=2
we have the permutations

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}312}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}321}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}312},\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}321}.

So,
\hat{c}_{3,1,\geq 2}=4
.

4.3.
q
-poly-Cauchy numbers

The
q
-poly-Cauchy numbers of the second kind have the explicit formula
[
12
]

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,q}^{(k)}:=(-1)^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\frac{q^{n-m}}{(m+1)^{k}}.

It is clear that the sequence
\hat{c}_{n,k}(q):=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}q^{n-m}(m+1)^{k}
counts the number of poly-Cauchy permutations of
[n+k]
such that the non left-to-right maxima of
\pi^{n}
are colored by one of
q
colors.

Example 29
.

For
n=2
and
k=2
we have the permutations

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}12}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}12}\to 1,\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}12}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}21}\to q,\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}12}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}\to 1,\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}21}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}\to q,\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}12}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}\to 1,\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}21}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}\to q,\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2}\to 1

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2}\to 1,\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}12}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}12}\to 1,\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}21}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}12}\to q,\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}12}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2}\to 1,\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}\to 1,\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}\to 1

So,
\hat{c}_{2,2}(q)=4q+9.

4.4.
q
-poly-Cauchy permutations with a parameter

There is another generalization,
q
-poly-Cauchy numbers with a parameter
[
13
]
, given by the explicit formula

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,\rho,q}^{(k)}=(-1)^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\frac{\rho^{n-m}}{[m+1]_{q}^{k}},

whith

[n]_{q}=1+q+\cdots+q^{n-1}
.
The expression with negative
k
and ignoring the sign, defined as
\hat{c}_{n,k,\rho,q}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\rho^{n-m}[m+1]_{q}^{k}

counts poly-Cauchy permutations with some weight on both the red elements and blue elements. As in the case of
q
-poly-Cauchy permutations we can color the non left-to-right maxima of
\pi^{n}
by one of
\rho
colors. This notion is extended by a weight on each blue elements,
b
. Namely,
w(b)=q^{\#\mbox{red cycles to the left of $b$}}
. The weight of the poly-Cauchy permutation is the product of weights of all the elements.
Formally, let
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
denote the set of poly-Cauchy permutations of
[n+k]
, and
\mu(\pi)
the number of non left-to-right maxima in
\pi^{n}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k,\rho,q}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}}\rho^{\mu(\pi)}\prod_{i=1}^{k}q^{w(n+i)}.

Example 30
.

For
n=2
and
k=2
the weight of the permutations are

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}12}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}12}\to 1,\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}12}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}21}\to\rho,\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}12}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}\to q^{2},\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}21}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}\to q\rho,\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}12}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}\to q^{2},\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}21}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}\to q\rho,\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2}\to q

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2}\to q,\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}12}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}12}\to q^{4},\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}21}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}12}\to q^{2}\rho,\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}12}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2}\to q^{2},\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}\to q^{3},\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}\to q^{3}.

So,
\hat{c}_{n,k,\rho,q}=\rho(1+2q+q^{2})+(1+2q+3q^{2}+2q^{3}+q^{4})
.

5.
Conclusion

In this paper we presented a combinatorial interpretation of poly-Cauchy numbers of the second kind. We showed how this model can be extended to some of the generalizations that were introduced in the literature on these numbers. However, this paper on this topic is not even aimed to be complete. One of our goal was to show the power of combinatorial thinking, proofs with pictures.

In this section we list some open questions that would be interesting to understand combinatorially.

1.

We studied in this work only the poly-Cauchy numbers (and their generalizations) of the second kind. The question arises naturally, is it possible to give a similar interpretation of the poly-Cauchy of the first kind?
The relation between the two kinds of poly-Cauchy numbers is given for instance by the following formula:

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\frac{c_{n}^{k}}{n!}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\binom{n-1}{m-1}\frac{\hat{c}_{m}^{k}}{m!}.

2.

We did not present a combinatorial proof for all of the formulas that are known, though we think most of the identities could be attacked using our combinatorial interpretation. The interested reader can try to find combinatorial proof for them. For example, recently, Komatsu
[
14
]
(Theorem 8) showed an annihilation formula

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{n-\ell+1}_{n-k}\hat{c}_{n-\ell}^{(-k)}=0.

3.

There are also several interesting identities involving generalizations of the poly-Cauchy numbers that have a combinatorial “flavour”. As an example, we recall the expression that was given by Komatsu and Szalay
[
19
]
in their study about the shifted poly-Cauchy numbers.

Theorem 31
.

[
19
]

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,\alpha}^{(-k)}=(-1)^{\alpha-1}\sum_{\mu=0}^{\alpha-1}Q_{\mu}(n,\alpha)\hat{c}_{n+\mu}^{(-k)},

where

\displaystyle Q_{\mu}(n,\alpha)=\sum_{i=0}^{\alpha-\mu-1}\binom{\alpha-1}{i}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\alpha-i-1}{\mu}n^{i},\quad(0\leq\mu\leq\alpha-1).

4.

There are even more generalizations in the literature, as multi-poly-Cauchy numbers and polynomials (cf.
[
18
]
).

5.

It would be of course very interesting to find other combinatorial interpretations related to these numbers.
