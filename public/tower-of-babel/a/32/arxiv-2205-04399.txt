Nonparametric estimation of the incubation time distribution
Piet Groeneboom abel=u1, url]http://dutiosc.twi.tudelft.nl/~pietg/

Abstract
Nonparametric maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) in inverse problems often have non-normal limit distributions, like Chernoff’s distribution. However, if one considers smooth functionals of the model, with corresponding functionals of the MLE, one gets normal limit distributions and faster rates of convergence. We demonstrate this for a model for the incubation time of a disease. The usual approach in the latter models is to use parametric distributions, like Weibull and gamma distributions, which leads to inconsistent estimators. Smoothed bootstrap methods are discussed for constructing confidence intervals. The classical bootstrap, based on the nonparametric MLE itself, has been proved to be inconsistent in this situation.

Introduction

We consider the following model, used for estimating the distribution of the incubation time of a disease. There is an infection time
U
, uniformly distributed on an interval
[0,E]
, where
E
(“exposure time”) has an absolutely continuous distribution function
F_{E}
on an interval
[0,M_{2}]
, and where
U
is uniform on
[0,E]
, conditionally on
E
. Moreover there is an incubation time
V
with an absolutely continuous distribution
F_{0}
on an interval
[0,M_{1}]
and a time for getting symptomatic
S
, where
S=U+V
. We assume that
U
and
V
are independent, conditionally on
E
. Our observations consist of the pairs of exposure times and times of getting symptomatic

\displaystyle(E_{i},S_{i}),\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

The model is for example considered in
[
17
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
1
]
and
[
6
]
.

We define the (convolution) density
q_{F}
of
(E_{i},S_{i})
by

\displaystyle q_{F}(e,s)

\displaystyle=e^{-1}\{F(s)-F(s-e)\}

\displaystyle=e^{-1}\int_{u=(s-e)_{+}}^{s}\,dF(u),\qquad e>0,\,s\in[0,M],

(1.1)

w.r.t.
\mu
, which is the product of the measure
dF_{E}
of the exposure time
E
and Lebesgue measure on
[0,M]
, where
M=M_{1}+M_{2}
is the upper bound for the time
S
of getting symptomatic.
We define the underlying measure
Q_{0}
for
(E_{i},S_{i})
by

\displaystyle dQ_{0}(e,s)=q_{F}(e,s)\,ds\,\,dF_{E}(e),\qquad s\in[0,M],\qquad e\in(0,M_{2}].

(1.2)

For estimating the distribution function
F_{0}
of the incubation time, usually parametric distributions are used, like the Weibull, log-normal or gamma distribution. However, in
[
6
]
the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator is used. The maximum likelihood estimator
\hat{F}_{n}
maximizes the function

\displaystyle\ell(F)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{F(S_{i})-F(S_{i}-E_{i})\right\}

(1.3)

over
all
distribution functions
F
on
\mathbb{R}
which satisfy
F(x)=0
,
x\leq 0
, see
[
6
]
.

Although the model is rather different, the algorithmic problem of computing the MLE has similarities with the problem of computing the MLE in the so-called interval censoring, case 2, model. In the interval censoring, case 2, model the log likelihood is of the form

\displaystyle\ell(F)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\Delta_{i1}\log F(U_{i})+\Delta_{i2}\log\{F(V_{i})-F(U_{i})\}+\Delta_{i3}\log\{1-F(V_{i})\}\right\},

(1.4)

where
U_{i}<V_{i}
are observation times and we only have information on whether our hidden variable of interest, with distribution function
F
, is to the left of
U_{i}
(
\Delta_{i1}=1
), between
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
(
\Delta_{i2}=1
), or to the right of
V_{i}
(
\Delta_{i3}=1
), see
[
9
]
and
[
11
]
.

For the incubation time model we have a formally similar way of writing the log likelihood, as can be seen in the following way. First of all, we can introduce, as in
[
6
]
, the indicator
\Delta_{i1}
, defined by

\displaystyle\Delta_{i1}=\{S_{i}\leq E_{i}\}.

(1.5)

If
\Delta_{i1}=1
, then
S_{i}\leq E_{i}
, leading to a term
\log F(S_{i})
in the log likelihood. We can also introduce a second and third indicator
\Delta_{i2}
, and
\Delta_{i3}
, which depend on whether
S_{i}\leq\max_{j}(S_{j}-E_{j})
or
S_{i}>\max_{j}(S_{j}-E_{j})
. Note that if
S_{i}>\max_{j}(S_{j}-E_{j})
, the distribution function
F
maximizing (
1.3
) will take the value
1
at
S_{i}
, since there is no term
\log\{F(S_{j})-F(S_{j}-E_{j})\}
with
S_{j}-E_{j}\geq S_{i}
. So there is no impediment to giving
F(S_{i})
its maximal value, which is
1
.

Hence, defining this time

\displaystyle\Delta_{i2}=\{E_{i}<S_{i}\leq\max_{j:S_{j}>E_{j}}(S_{j}-E_{j})\},

(1.6)

and

\displaystyle\Delta_{i3}=\{E_{i}<S_{i},\,S_{i}>\max_{j:S_{j}>E_{j}}(S_{j}-E_{j})\},

(1.7)

we can write the log likelihood in the incubation time model in the form

\displaystyle\ell(F)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\Delta_{i1}\log F(S_{i})+\Delta_{i2}\log\{F(S_{i})-F(S_{i}-E_{i})\}+\Delta_{i3}\log\{1-F(S_{i}-E_{i})\}\right].

(1.8)

with the
\Delta_{ij}
’s defined by (
1.5
) to (
1.7
), using a preliminary reduction of the maximization problem that was also used in
[
11
]
in the interval censoring, case 2, problem. Note, however, that the indicators
\Delta_{i2}
and
\Delta_{i3}
have a very different meaning in the interval censoring model.

Remark 1.1
.

Note that if
S_{i}>M_{1}
, where
M_{1}
is the (unknown) upper bound for the length of the incubation time, we must have:

\displaystyle S_{i}>\max_{j:S_{j}>E_{j}}(S_{j}-E_{j})

since
\max_{j:S_{j}>E_{j}}(S_{j}-E_{j})
is a lower bound for
M_{1}
. So a distribution function
F
, maximizing the log likelihood, will assign the value
1
to
F(S_{i})
if
S_{i}>M_{1}
.

The limit distribution of the nonparametric MLE of the incubation time distribution was unknown, but because of the (at least algorithmic) similarity of its computation to the computation of the nonparametric MLE in the interval censoring problem, one would expect that similar techniques could be used in its derivation. The algorithmic similarity was indeed used in
[
6
]
, but the limit distribution remained an open problem.

On the other hand, the limit distribution of the MLE for the interval censoring problem was derived in
[
4
]
, in the so-called strictly separated case, where the length of the observation intervals has a strictly positive lower bound. Unfortunately, the proof is very complicated, and the result seems to be little known. Nevertheless, in the present absence of other methods, this is also the way we proceed in this paper, where we prove the limit result using similar methods, in the hope that this will revive the interest in these matters.

We give the result on the convergence of the rescaled MLE to Chernoff’s limit distribution in Section
4
,
under a condition that seems somewhat similar to the strict separation hypothesis in the interval censoring problem. There is the general expectation that Chernoff’s limit distribution will often occur as universal limit distribution in this context of inverse problems, but the difficulty of proving it lies in the fact that we have to derive it from the properties of solutions of (Fredholm) integral equations and that we do not have explicit representations to go on.

As a preparation to this result, we first characterize the MLE as the derivative of the least convex minorant off a self-induced cusum diagram in section
2
. The cusum diagram is an often used tool in the theory of isotonic regression (because the distribution function is monotone, our estimation problem is an isotonic regression problem), but the peculiar feature of the cusum diagrams used here is that they contain the solution
\hat{F}_{n}
(the MLE) itself in their definition. The necessary and sufficient condition in the characterization of the MLE in this way are given in section
2
. We illustrate the (iterative) algorithm for computing the MLE on a data set on COVID-19, also analyzed in
[
6
]
. Algorithms of this type (the iterative convex minorant algorithms) were proved to converge in
[
15
]
.

The characterization of section
2
is used to prove consistency of the MLE in section
3
. The convergence to Chernoff’s distribution is then given in section
4
, where also some numerical results on its variance are given.

Then, in section
5
we define the Smoothed Maximum Likelihood Estimator (SMLE)
\tilde{F}_{nh}(t)
at points
t
away from the boundary by

\tilde{F}_{nh}(t)=\int I\!\!K_{h}(t-x)\,d\hat{F}_{n}(x),\qquad I\!\!K_{h}(x)=I\!\!K(x/h),

(1.9)

where
I\!\!K
is an integrated kernel, defined by

I\!\!K(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}K(y)\,dy,

(1.10)

and
K
is a symmetric kernel of the usual kind, used in density estimation. Near the boundary we use the Schuster-type boundary correction, also used in Section 11.3 of
[
9
]
in the definition of the SMLE. We assume that
K
has support
[-1,1]
.

It is shown that the SMLE has a normal limit distribution and has a faster convergence than the nonparamtric MLE itself (rate
n^{2/5}
instead of
n^{1/3}
).
In spite of the fact that its variance is implicitly defined as the solution of an integral equation, we can compute bootstrap confidence intervals for the distribution function via the SMLE, where we do not have to assume that the (asymptotic) variance is known. In this section we also give a method for determining the asymptotically optimal bandwidth automatically (see subsection
5.1
).

It has several advantages to base the confidence intervals on the SMLE instead of the MLE. It follows from recently developed theory (see, e.g.,
[
18
]
) that direct bootstrap confidence intervals, based on the MLE, will be inconsistent. In principle one can base bootstrap confidence intervals on the MLE, using the SMLE intermediately to center the bootstrap intervals, as is done in
[
18
]
for the interval censoring model. But these intervals converge at a lower speed again and have the unpleasant property of having jumps at the locations of the jumps of the MLE, which is a kind of artefact of the method. Since one needs the SMLE anyway for making the bootstrap confidence intervals consistent, it seems preferable to also base the confidence intervals on the SMLE itself (as is done in the present paper), and not on the MLE. Moreover, the MLE has the property to jump to the value
1
too early, something that is avoided if one uses the SMLE.

Finally, in sections
6
to
7
we show that the SMLE is competitive with the parametric models, even if the parametric assumptions are satisfied, and avoids the inconsistency that is inherent in the use of the latter methods. Moreover, one is discharged from the duty of introducing several of these parametric methods, since there is no sound reason to choose one of them.

Our computations are reproducible by using the
R
scripts in
[
5
]
.

2
Characterization of the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) for the incubation time distribution

First, just as in
[
6
]
, we reduce the problem to the problem of maximizing on the cone
\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}:\bm{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}),\,0\leq y_{1}\leq\dots\leq y_{m}\}
, where the
y_{j}
represent the values
F(S_{i})
and
F(S_{i}-E_{i})
and where
m
is suitably chosen (see
[
6
]
).

Since, again just as in
[
6
]
, we want to reduce the problem to the problem of maximizing
inside
the cone, enabling us to differentiate w.r.t. the variables
y_{i}
, we define
F(S_{i})=1
if
S_{i}>\max_{j:S_{j}>E_{j}}(S_{j}-E_{j})
, and
F(S_{i}-E_{i})=0
if
S_{i}-E_{i}<\min_{j}S_{n}
. Note that other choices of
F
at these points would make the log likelihood smaller. For convenience of notation, we define

\displaystyle m_{n}=\max_{j:S_{j}>E_{j}}(S_{j}-E_{j}).

(2.1)

Then, for a distribution function
F
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, satisfying these conditions, we define the process

\displaystyle W_{n,F}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\{s\leq t\wedge e\}}{F(s)}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(e,s)-\int\frac{\{0<s-e\leq t,\,t<s\leq m_{n}\}}{F(s)-F(s-e)}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(e,s)

\displaystyle\quad+\int\frac{\{s\leq t\wedge m_{n},\,s>e\}}{F(s)-F(s-e)}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(e,s)-\int\frac{\{s-e\leq t,\,s>e\vee m_{n}\}}{1-F(s-e)}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(e,s).

(2.2)

where we define
0/0=0
, and where
{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}
is the empirical distribution of
(E_{1},S_{1}),\dots,(E_{n},S_{n})
. Note that the MLE is only determined at the points
S_{i}
and
(S_{i}-E_{i})1_{\{S_{i}>E_{i}\}}
and is zero at
0
.

We restrict the distribution functions, occurring in the problem of maximizing the likelihood to the following set.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
{\cal F}_{n}
be the set of discrete distribution functions
F
, which only have mass at the points
S_{i}
or
(S_{i}-E_{i})1_{\{S_{i}>E_{i}\}}

\displaystyle F(S_{i})=1\quad\text{\rm if }\quad S_{i}>\max_{j:S_{j}>E_{j}}(S_{j}-E_{j}),

(2.3)

and

\displaystyle F(S_{i}-E_{i})=0\quad\text{\rm if }\quad S_{i}-E_{i}<\min_{j}S_{j}.

(2.4)

Now, let
T_{1}<\dots<T_{m}
be the points
S_{i}
or
S_{i}-E_{i}
such that
S_{i}
not of type (
2.3
) and
S_{i}-E_{i}
is not of type (
2.4
). Then

, if the log likelihood for

\displaystyle{\cal Y}=\{\bm{y}\in(0,1)^{m}:\bm{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})=(F(T_{1}),\dots,F(T_{m}),\,F\in{\cal F}_{n}\}.

The log likelihood for

\displaystyle\ell_{n}(F)

\displaystyle=\int\log\{F(s)-F(s-e)\}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(s,e)

\displaystyle=\int_{s\leq e}\log F(s)\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(e,s)+\int_{e<s\leq m_{n}}\log\{F(s)-F(s-e)\}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(e,s)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\quad\qquad\quad\qquad\quad\qquad+\int_{s>e\vee m_{n}}\log\{1-F(s-e)\}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(e,s).

(2.5)

The corresponding function of

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(\bm{y})=\phi_{n}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})=\phi_{n}(F(T_{1}),\dots,F(T_{m}))=\ell_{n}(F).

We have the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1

Let
W_{n,F}
be defined by (

be defined by (
2.6
). Moreover, let

\displaystyle\bm{y}=(F(T_{1},\dots,F(T_{m})),

The following lemma characterizes the MLE.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let the class of distribution functions
{\cal F}_{n}
be defined by Definition
2.1
. Then
\hat{F}_{n}\in{\cal F}_{n}
maximizes (
1.3
) over
F\in{\cal F}_{n}
if and only if

(i)

\displaystyle\int_{u\in[t,\infty)}\,dW_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}(u)\leq 0,\qquad t\geq 0,

(2.8)

(ii)

\displaystyle\int\hat{F}_{n}(t)\,dW_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}(t)=0.

(2.9)

where
W_{n,F_{n}}
is defined by (
2
). Moreover,
\hat{F}_{n}\in{\cal F}_{n}
is uniquely determined by (
2.8
) and (
2.9
).

Suppose (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) are satisfied. Letting
\bm{y}=(F(T_{1},\dots,F(T_{m}))
and
\bm{x}=(\hat{F}_{n}(T_{1}),\dots,\hat{F}_{n}(T_{m}))
, we get from the concavity of the logarithmic function:

\displaystyle\ell_{n}(F)-\ell_{n}(\hat{F}_{n})\leq\langle\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\{F(t)-\hat{F}_{n}(t)\}\,dW_{n\hat{F}_{n}}(t)=\int F(t)\,dW_{n\hat{F}_{n}}(t),

using (ii) in the last step. But

\displaystyle\int F(t)\,dW_{n\hat{F}_{n}}(t)=\int_{0\leq u\leq t}\,dF(u)\,dW_{n\hat{F}_{n}}(t)=\int\left\{\int_{t\in[u,\infty)}\,dW_{n\hat{F}_{n}}(t)\right\}\,dF(t)\leq 0,

using (i) in the last inequality.

Conversely, if
\hat{F}_{n}
maximizes the likelihood and

\displaystyle\bm{y}=(\hat{F}_{n}(T_{1}),\dots,\hat{F}_{n}(T_{m})),

we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon^{-1}\left\{\phi_{n}(y_{1},\dots,y_{i}+\varepsilon,\dots,y_{m}+\varepsilon)-\phi_{n}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})\right\}=\int_{u\in[T_{i},\infty)}\,dW_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}(u)\leq 0,

for
i=1,\dots,m
, and hence

\displaystyle\int_{u\in[t,\infty)}\,dW_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}(u)\leq 0,\qquad t\geq 0,

and similarly

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon^{-1}\left\{\phi_{n}(y_{1}+\varepsilon y_{1},\dots,y_{m}+\varepsilon y_{m})-\phi_{n}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})\right\}=\int\hat{F}_{n}(t)\,dW_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}(t)=0.

The uniqueness can be proved aong the same lines as in the proof of Proposition 1.3 in
[
11
]
. We omit the details.

3
Consistency of the MLE

We have the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let the incubation time distribution function
F_{0}
have a strictly positive continuous density
f_{0}
on
(0,M_{1})
, for some
M_{1}>0
. Furthermore, let
F_{E}
be a distribution function on
[0,M_{2}]
,
M_{2}>M_{1}/2
, which is
0
on
[0,\varepsilon]
for some
\varepsilon\in[0,(M_{1}\wedge M_{2})/2)
and has a continuous strictly positive derivative
f_{E}
on
[\varepsilon,M_{2}]
. Let
\hat{F}_{n}\in{\cal F}_{n}
be the nonparametric MLE, where
{\cal F}_{n}
is the set of distribution functions defined in Definition
2.1
. Then
\hat{F}_{n}
converges almost surely to
F_{0}
on
[0,M_{1}]
in the supremum metric.

There are a lot of different ways to prove consistency, but we feel a preference for the elegant method in
[
14
]
, which is used in the proof below.

We use the same method as in section 4.2 of the second part of the book
[
11
]
. Let
\psi(F)
be defined by

\displaystyle\psi(F)=\int\log\{F(s)-F(s-e)\}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(e,s),

for distribution functions
F
on
\mathbb{R}
, which satisfy
F(x)=0
,
x\leq 0
. Then we get:

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon^{-1}\left\{\psi\left((1-\varepsilon)\hat{F}_{n}+\varepsilon F_{0}\right)-\psi\left(\hat{F}_{n}\right)\right\}\leq 0,

since
\hat{F}_{n}
is the MLE. The limit exists because of the concavity of
\psi
. Evaluating this limit, we get:

\displaystyle\int\frac{F_{0}(s)-F_{0}(s-e)}{\hat{F}_{n}(s)-\hat{F}_{n}(s-e)}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(e,s)\leq 1.

Proceeding as in section 4.2 of the second part of the book
[
11
]
, we get from this,
if
F
is a limit point for
\hat{F}_{n}
(using Helly’s compactness theorem):

\displaystyle\int e^{-1}\frac{\{F_{0}(s)-F_{0}(s-e)\}^{2}}{F(s)-F(s-e)}\,ds\,dF_{E}(e)\leq 1.

(3.1)

We want to show that the minimum over
F
on the left is equal to
1
, and that this can only be attained if
F=F_{0}
. Note that by Remark
1.1
,
\hat{F}_{n}(s)=1
, if
s\geq M_{1}
, and hence also
F(s)=1
, if
s\geq M_{1}
. This implies

\displaystyle\int e^{-1}\{F(s)-F(s-e)\}\,ds\,dF_{E}(e)=1.

(3.2)

We therefore have:

\displaystyle\int e^{-1}\left\{\frac{\{F_{0}(s)-F_{0}(s-e)\}^{2}}{F(s)-F(s-e)}+\{F(s)-F(s-e)\right\}\,ds\,dF_{E}(e)

\displaystyle=\int_{s\leq e}e^{-1}\left\{\frac{F_{0}(s)^{2}}{F(s)}+F(s)\right\}\,ds\,dF_{E}(e)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{e<s\leq M_{1}}e^{-1}\left\{\frac{\{F_{0}(s)-F_{0}(s-e)\}^{2}}{F(s)-F(s-e)}+\{F(s)-F(s-e)\right\}\,ds\,dF_{E}(e)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{s>M_{1}\vee e}e^{-1}\left\{\frac{\{1-F_{0}(s-e)\}^{2}}{1-F(s-e)}+1-F(s-e)\right\}\,ds\,dF_{E}(e)

\displaystyle\leq 2.

(3.3)

The function

\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto\frac{\{F_{0}(s)-F_{0}(s-e)\}^{2}}{y-x}+y-x

is minimized if
x=F_{0}(s-e)
and
y=F_{0}(s)
(also if
F_{(}s-e)=0
or
F_{0}(s)=1
). If
F
would not be equal to
F_{0}
, it would be different from
F_{0}
on an interval, using the monotonicity of
F
and
F_{0}
and the continuity of
F_{0}
, and then the left side of (
3
) would be strictly larger than
2
. Hence, by (
3.2
), the left side of (
3.1
) would be strictly larger than
1
, a contradiction.
∎

5.1
Bandwidth selection

We propose a bootstrap method to find an approximately MSE optimal bandwidth for estimating
F_{0}(t)
at a point
t\in(0,M_{1})
. The MSE we want to minimize as a function of
h
is given by:

\displaystyle MSE_{h}(t)={\mathbb{E}}\bigl\{\tilde{F}_{nh}(t)-F_{0}(t)\bigr\}^{2}.

(5.10)

The analogous bootstrap quantity (using oversmoothing, in the sense that
h_{0}\asymp n^{-1/9}
) is given by:

\displaystyle MSE_{h}^{*}(t)={\mathbb{E}}\left\{\left\{\tilde{F}_{nh}^{*}(t)-\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(t)\right\}^{2}\Bigm|(E_{1},S_{1}),\dots,(E_{n},S_{n})\right\},

(5.11)

where
h_{0}
is called a “pilot” bandwidth. We shall show that (
5.11
) is asymptotically independent of the constant
c_{0}
in the pilot bandwidth
h_{0}
if we take
h_{0}=c_{0}n^{-1/9}
.

Using integration by parts, we can write

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{nh}^{*}(t)-\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int K_{h}(t-x)\,\bigl\{\hat{F}_{n}^{*}(x)-\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(x)\bigr\}\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad+\int K_{h}(t-x)\,\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(x)\,dx-\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(t).

We have (MSE is variance
+
squared bias):

\displaystyle MSE_{h}^{*}(t)

\displaystyle\sim{\mathbb{E}}\left\{\left\{\int K_{h}(t-x)\,\left\{\hat{F}_{n}^{*}(x)-\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(x)\right\}\,dx\right\}^{2}\Bigm|(E_{1},S_{1}),\dots,(E_{n},S_{n})\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left\{\int K_{h}(t-x)\,\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(x)\,dx-\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(t)\right\}^{2}.

(5.12)

For the second term on the right we get:

\displaystyle\int K_{h}(t-x)\,\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(x)\,dx-\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}h^{2}\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}^{\prime\prime}(t)\int u^{2}K(u)\,du+o_{p}\left(h^{2}\right),

so

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\{\left\{\int K_{h}(t-x)\,\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(x)\,dx-\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(t)\right\}^{2}\Bigm|(E_{1},S_{1}),\dots,(E_{n},S_{n})\right\}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}h^{4}\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}^{\prime\prime}(t)^{2}\left\{\int u^{2}K(u)\,du\right\}^{2}+o_{p}\left(h^{4}\right).

We have the following result.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let the conditions of Theorem
5.1
be satisfied. Moreover, let
h_{0}=h_{n,0}\sim c_{0}n^{-1/9}
, as
n\to\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}^{\prime\prime}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}f_{0}^{\prime}(t),\qquad n\to\infty.

The bootstrap MSE for the Wuhan data.

For reasons of space we omit the proof here. More details on this type of lemma can be found in
[
10
]
in the context of monotone regression.

\displaystyle B^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\{\tilde{F}^{*,i}_{nh}(t_{j})-\tilde{F}_{nh_{0}}(t_{j})\right\}^{2},

. The estimate of the 95th percentile is then defined by
F^{-1}_{\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta}}(0.95)
, where

denotes the inverse function.

In the log-normal approach to the problem, we maximize for

and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{G_{\alpha,\beta}(S_{i})-G_{\alpha,\beta}(S_{i}-E_{i})\right\},

(6.2)

where

\displaystyle G_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=\Phi\left((\log x-\alpha)/\beta\right),

(6.3)

for

(zero otherwise), where

and

is the standard normal distribution function.
The estimate of the percentile is then given by

, where
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
maximizes (

) over
(\alpha,\beta)
.

In the

maximum likelihood approach we simply maximize

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{F(S_{i})-F(S_{i}-E_{i})\right\},

over
all
distribution functions

, from which we compute the SMLE

and the estimate of the 95th percentile
\tilde{F}^{-1}_{n,h_{n}}(0.95)
. The bandwidth

here. Note that, using the delta method, we find:

\displaystyle n^{2/5}\left\{\tilde{F}_{n,h_{n}}^{-1}(0.95)-F_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}(0.95)\right\}

\displaystyle=-n^{2/5}\left\{\tilde{F}_{n,h_{n}}(F_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}(0.95))-0.95)\right\}\bigm/f_{\alpha,\beta}\left(F_{0}^{-1}(0.95)\right)+o_{p}(1),

, defined by (

But the nonparametric estimates, not making the assumption that the distribution is of the Weibull type, are also pretty good, whereas the estimates, assuming a log-normal distribution are completely off (in fact, these estimate are inconsistent). One sees that the uncertainty about what interest us, is not much larger when one only uses the nonparametric SMLE than when one assumes that the incubation time distribution is Weibull (which is the correct distribution in this simulation setting), but much larger when one assumes log-normal. While there is absolutely no scientific (medical) reason to “believe” Weibull, or to “believe” log-normal. They lead to completely different statistical inferences, hence could lead to completely different policy recommendations.

8
Conclusion

We proved that the nonparametric MLE in a model for the incubation time distribution converges in distribution, after standardization, to Chernoff’s distribution. The rate of convergence is cube root
n
, if
n
is the sample size, under a separation condition for the exposure time. We also discussed (locally) differentiable functionals of the model, estimated by corresponding functionals of the nonparametric MLE, which converge after standardization to a normal distribution at faster rates, where the constants are given by the solution of an integral equation.

This provides an alternative for the parametric models that are usually applied in this context, estimating the incubaton time distribution by, e.g., Weibull, gamma or log-normal distributions.
If the parametric model is not right (there is in fact no scientific or medical reason to choose for Weibull, gamma, Erlang, log-normal. etc., and one sees for this reason usually these distribution applied at the same time) the estimates are inconsistent if the chosen model does not hold, as we demonstrate in Sections
6
and
7

As shown in Section
7
, for parameters like the first moment, we do not have to choose a bandwidth parameter, while the behavior of the estimate based on the nonparametric MLE is competitive to the parametric estimates in this case, even if the model for the parametric estimate is right.

9.1
Integral equations

As explained in the Appendix of
[
6
]
, the theory of the estimation of smooth functionals in the model is based on certain integral equations. For the incubation time model an extra indicator
\Delta_{i}
was introduced to indicate whether
S_{i}\leq E_{i}
.

But introducing such an indicator is not necessary. So we define the score function
\theta
without these idicators and get as definition for the score function:

\displaystyle\theta(e,s)=E\left\{a(X)|(E,S)=(e,s)\right\}=\frac{\int a(x)\,dF(x)}{F(s)-F(s-e)}\,,

(compare to (A.1) in
[
6
]
). This is the conditional expectation of
a(X)
in the “hidden” space of the variable of interest (the incubation time), given our observation
(E,S)
. Note that we changed the notation somewhat w.r.t.
[
6
]
, and denote the distribution function of the incubation time by
F
instead of
G
.

Defining

\displaystyle\phi(t)=\int_{x\in[0,t]}a(x)\,dF(x),

we get the following representation for the score function, conditioned on
X=x
:

\displaystyle E\{\theta(E,S)|X=x\}=\int_{e\in[0,M_{2}]}e^{-1}\left\{\int_{s\in(x,x+e]}\frac{\phi(s)-\phi(s-e)}{F(s)-F(s-e)}\,ds\right\}\,dF_{E}(e).

(9.1)

Note that in
[
6
]
the distribution function
F_{E}
was taken to be uniform, just as an example, but that we do not assume that here.

Differentating (
9.1
) w.r.t.
x
, we get the following integral equation, with on the right the derivative of the functional
we want to estimate, denoted by
\psi
:

\displaystyle\int e^{-1}\left[\frac{\phi(x+e)-\phi(x)}{F(x+e)-F(x)}-\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(x-e)}{F(x)-F(x-e)}\right]\,dF_{E}(e)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\phi(x)}{1-F(x)}\int_{e\geq M_{1}-x}e^{-1}\,dF_{E}(e)-\frac{\phi(x)}{F(x)}\int_{e\geq x}e^{-1}\,dF_{E}(e)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int e^{-1}\left[\frac{\phi(x+e)-\phi(x)}{F(x+e)-F(x)}1_{\{e<M_{1}-x\}}-\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(x-e)}{F(x)-F(x-e)}1_{\{e<x\}}\right]\,dF_{E}(e)

\displaystyle=\psi(x),\qquad x\in[0,M_{1}].

(9.2)

This is called a Fredholm integral equation of the second kind. Here and in the sequel, we assume that distribution functions are right-continuous.

We assume that
F_{E}
satisfies the following condition:

(F1)

F_{E}
is a distribution function on
[0,M_{2}]
,
M_{2}>M_{1}/2
, which is
0
on
[0,\varepsilon]
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,(M_{1}\wedge M_{2})/2)
and has a continuous strictly positive derivative
f_{E}
on
[\varepsilon,M_{2}]
. The distribution function is defined to be
1
on
[M_{2},\infty)
.

We define the class of distribution functions
{\cal F}
in the following way.

(F2)

{\cal F}
consists of the distribution functions
F
on
[0,M_{1}]
with only a finite number of jumps, contained in
(0,M_{1})
, and satisfying

\displaystyle F(u)-F(t)\geq c>0,\qquad\text{ if }u-t\geq\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad t,u\in[0,M_{1}].

(9.3)

where
\varepsilon>0
is defined as in Definition (F1). The distribution functions are extended to
[0,\infty)
by defining it to be equal to
1
on
[M_{1},\infty)
.

Note that the MLE satisfies the conditions of (F2) for sufficiently large
n
, by the conditions of Theorem
4.1
(in particular the fact that the density
f_{0}
is strictly positive on
(0,M_{1})
) and the consistency of the MLE (Theorem

).

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
F_{E}
be a distribution function on
[0,M_{1}]
, satisfying condition (F1) and let
F\in{\cal F}
, where
{\cal F}
is defined in (F2).
Moreover, let,

satisfy:

\displaystyle\{1-F(t)\}\int_{e\geq t}e^{-1}\,dF_{E}(e)+F(t)\int_{e\geq M_{1}-t}e^{-1}\,dF_{E}(e)\geq c>0

(9.4)

for some

and each

.

Finally, let
\psi:[0,M_{1}]\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded right-continuous function with left-limits (cadlag) on

. Then the equation (
9.1
) has a unique solution in

.

Consider, for

\displaystyle\phi(t)\left\{\{1-F(t)\}\int_{e\geq t}e^{-1}\,dF_{E}(e)+F(t)\int_{e\geq M_{1}-t}e^{-1}\,dF_{E}(e)\right\}

\displaystyle=F(t)\{1-F(t)\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cdot\int e^{-1}\left[\frac{\phi(t+e)-\phi(t)}{F(t+e)-F(t)}1_{\{e<M_{1}-t\}}-\frac{\phi(t)-\phi(t-e)}{F(t)-F(t-e)}1_{\{e<t\}}\right]\,dF_{E}(e).

(9.5)

Since

and

, the solution has to be zero at

and

.
Suppose there exists a point

such that

for a solution

. If the maximum of

If the supremum is not attained, we take the limit from the left and get that the limit on the left is strictly positive, whereas the limit on the right in nonpositive, which leads again to a contradiction.

A similar argument holds if there exists a point

such that

. It now follows from Theorem 3.4 in
