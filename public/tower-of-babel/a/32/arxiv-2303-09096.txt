Supersingular Isogeny Graphs from Algebraic Modular Curves
Nadir Hajouji

Abstract
. We describe and compare algorithms for computing supersingular isogeny graphs. Along the way, we obtain a formula for the trace of the adjacency matrix of a general supersingular isogeny graph, and we prove a conjecture recently posed by Nakaya.

.

Let

be a separable isogeny.

(1)

(2)

(3)

The first point follows from the definition of

\xi_{1}\circ\xi_{2}

Finally, we note that

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\psi:E\to E^{\prime}
be a separable isogeny and
\hat{\psi}:E^{\prime}\to E

Define a function

(2)

By definition, we have

Taking duals of both sides shows

•

2.3.
Modular curves

Next, we explain how to compute
\Gamma_{p,\ell}
using modular curves.

Let
X_{0}(\ell)
be the fine moduli space that represents isomorphism classes of pairs
(E,H)
,
where
E
is an elliptic curve
E
and
H\subset E
is a (cyclic) subgroup of order
\ell
.
We write
j_{\ell}:X_{0}(\ell)\to X(1)
to denote the
j
-map, which takes
[(E,H)]
to
j(E)
,
and we write
\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}:X_{0}(\ell)\to X_{0}(\ell)
to denote the Fricke involution, which swaps
[(E,H)]
and
[(E/H,\ker(\widehat{E\to E/H}))]
.

Then
X_{0}(\ell)(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{sep})
contains all information about isogenies of degree
\ell
between elliptic curves over
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{sep}
.
Furthermore, the points that represent pairs
(E,H)
,
where
E
is supersingular,
are all defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
:
we can always find models
E/\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
for each isomorphism class of supersingular curve where
\phi_{2}
acts as
[\pm p]
,
and if
H\subset E
is a subgroup of order
\ell
, then
\phi_{2}(H)=[\pm p]H=H
.
This shows that the pair
(E,H)
is fixed by
\phi_{2}
,
so it is represented by a point in
X_{0}(\ell)(\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}})
.

Now, given
P\in X_{0}(\ell)
, we can compute
j_{\ell}(P),j_{\ell}\circ\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P)

to determine the domain and codomain of the isogeny
E\to E/H
associated to the pair
(E,H)
.
Thus, if we have a model of
X_{0}(\ell)
, together with formulae for
j_{\ell},\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}
,
we should in principle be able to compute
\Gamma_{p,\ell}
.

There are two issues we will have to address in order to use this method:

(1)

First, we need a way of obtaining the models.
For small values of
\ell
, these can be found in the literature.
They can also be computed by hand, although this gets difficult as
\ell
gets large.

Fortunately, there is also a second modular curve we can use - the quotient of
X_{0}(\ell)
by the Fricke involution. Models of the latter are easier to obtain.

(2)

Once we have the model and the necessary formulae, we have to deal with the fact that
some
points on
X_{0}(\ell)
(or the quotient modular curve) may represent multiple edges on the isogeny graph. In order to obtain the correct graph, we need a way to determining the exact number of subgroups represented by a point on
X_{0}(\ell)
.

The second problem is easier to work around.

Define
\Gamma_{p,\ell}^{*}
to be the graph obtained from
\Gamma_{p,\ell}

by removing all edges of the form
j_{0}\to j_{0}
.
We will use modular curves to compute
\Gamma_{p,\ell}^{*}
,
and then obtain
\Gamma_{p,\ell}
from
\Gamma_{p,\ell}^{*}

by adding edges
j_{0}\to j_{0}
until each vertex has degree
\ell+1
.

We start by explaining how to compute

from the modular curve

We require the following:

•

The set

of supersingular

•

A model of

To compute the graph, we will do the following:

(1)

(2)

For each

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve and assume
j(E)\not\in\left\{0,1728\right\}

The function
S_{\ell}(E)\to X_{0}(\ell)
that takes
(E,H)

[(E,H)]

is injective.

Proof.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}\in S_{\ell}(E)
and suppose
[(E,H_{1})]=[(E,H_{2})]
.
Then there exists an automorphism
\alpha:E\to E
satisfying
\alpha(H_{1})=H_{2}
.
Since
j(E)\not\in\left\{0,1728\right\}

Aut(E)=\left\{[\pm 1]\right\}
, so that means
\pm H_{1}=H_{2}
.
But
\pm H_{1}=H_{1}
, so in fact we have
H_{1}=H_{2}
.
Thus, the map is injective.
∎

On the other hand, if

the map will not be injective because

acts nontrivially on

This means that points

.
∎

We can now show that
\Gamma_{p,\ell}^{*}
can be computed from
X_{0}(\ell)
.
Furthermore, all of the computations happen over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
:

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
P\in X_{0}(\ell)
, and suppose
j_{\ell}(P)\in S_{p}
.

(1)

P\in X_{0}(\ell)(\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}})
.

(2)

Suppose
j_{\ell}(P)\neq j_{\ell}(\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P))
.
The number of edges
j_{\ell}(P)\to j_{\ell}(\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P))
is equal to
\frac{|Aut(E)|}{2}
, where
E
is any elliptic curve satisfying
j(E)=j_{\ell}(P)

Proof.

Fix a model
E/\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
with
j_{\ell}(E)=j_{\ell}(P)
,
and where
\phi_{2}
acts as
[\pm p]
.
The point
P
represents the isomorphism class of
(E,H)
for some
H\in S_{\ell}(E)
.
We compute:

\phi_{2}\cdot P=[(\phi_{2}\cdot E,\phi_{2}\cdot H)]=[(E,[\pm p]H)]=[(E,H)]=P

Thus,
P
must be fixed by
\phi_{2}
so
P\in X_{0}(\ell)(\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}})
, proving (1).

Now,
Aut(E)/\left\langle[\pm 1]\right\rangle
acts on
S_{\ell}(E)
.
Furthermore, if the orbit of
H
has fewer than
|Aut(E)/\left\langle[\pm 1]\right\rangle|
elements,
then
j(E)=j(E/H)\in\left\{0,1728\right\}
by Lemma
2.7
.
Thus, if
j(E)\neq j(E/H)
, then the orbit of
H
contains precisely
Aut(E)/\left\langle[\pm 1]\right\rangle
elements, proving (2).

∎

2.3.2.
X_{0}(\ell)^{+}

Let
X_{0}(\ell)^{+}=X_{0}(\ell)/\left\langle\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}\right\rangle
be the quotient of
X_{0}(\ell)
by the Fricke involution.
For a point
Q=\left\{P,\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P)\right\}\in X_{0}(\ell)^{+}
,
we define
a_{\ell}(Q)=j_{\ell}(P)+j_{\ell}\circ\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P)

and
b_{\ell}(Q)=j_{\ell}(P)\cdot j_{\ell}\circ\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P)
.
We use these to construct a quadratic polynomial:

R_{\ell}(x,Q)=x^{2}-a_{\ell}(Q)x+b_{\ell}(Q)=(x-j_{\ell}(P))(x-j_{\ell}\circ\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P))

To recover the
j
-invariants of the curves represented by points above
Q
in
X_{0}(\ell)
,
we simply evaluate
a_{\ell}(Q),b_{\ell}(Q)
and solve the quadratic.

Now, there is a new ambiguity we have to deal with:
if
P
is a fixed point of the Fricke involution,
then
R_{\ell}(x,\left\{P,\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P)\right\})=(x-j_{\ell}(P))^{2}
,
and the repeated root
j_{\ell}(P)
gives rise to exactly one edge on the isogeny graph.
However, it is possible that
P\neq\mathrm{Fr}(\ell)(P)
,
but
R_{\ell}(x,Q)=(x-j_{\ell}(P))^{2}
anyways.
In the second case, the point
Q
represents two edges on the isogeny graph.

Fortunately, these ambiguities do not affect the computation of
\Gamma_{p,\ell}^{*}
:

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
Q\in X_{0}(\ell)^{+}
and suppose
R_{\ell}(x,Q)=(x-j_{1})(x-j_{2})
for
j_{1},j_{2}\in S_{p}
.

(1)

Q\in X_{0}(\ell)^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}})
.

(2)

Assume
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
. Then
Q
gives rise to
m_{1}
edges
j_{1}\to j_{2}

and
m_{2}
edges
j_{2}\to j_{1}
, were
m_{i}=\frac{|Aut(E_{i})|}{2}
.

Proof.

Let
P,\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P)
be the points over
Q
in
X_{0}(\ell)
.
Then
P,\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P)\in X_{0}(\ell)(\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}})
and the quotient map is defined over
\mathbb{Z}
so
Q\in X_{0}(\ell)(\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}})
.

If
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
, then
j_{\ell}(P)\neq j_{\ell}\circ\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P)
so
P\neq\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P)
.
Thus, we have two points above
Q
in
X_{0}(\ell)
,
and the number of edges represented by each of those points is
\frac{|Aut(E)|}{2}
by the proposition.

∎

2.4.
Supersingular Primes

Let
p
be a prime.
We say that
p
is a
supersingular prime
if one of the following conditions holds:

(1)

S_{p}\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}
, i.e. every supersingular curve admits a model over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

(2)

X_{0}(p)^{+}
has genus 0.

(3)

p
divides the order of the Monster group.

The equivalence of these conditions is part of the theory of generalized Monstrous moonshine, see Section 9 of
[
3
]
.

For a supersingular prime
\ell
, the function field of
X_{0}(\ell)^{+}
is generated
by a single element, and the functions
a_{\ell},b_{\ell}
can be described as polynomials in terms of that generator.
Furthermore, it is always possible to find a generator
y
of the function field of
X_{0}(\ell)^{+}
so that
a_{\ell},b_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}[y]
are monic polynomials of degree
\ell,\ell+1
, respectively.
This allows us to encode all of our data into a single polynomial
R_{\ell}(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
.
These polynomials are known as Atkin modular polynomials,
and explicit formulae for
R_{\ell}(x,y)
for all supersingular primes can be found in SageMath.

Let
A
be a UFD and let
f(x),g(x)\in A[x]
.
We write
\mathrm{Res}_{x}(f(x),g(x))
to denote the resultant of the polynomials
f,g
with respect to
x
. Note that the resultant is an element of
A
, and vanishes if and only if
f(x),g(x)
have a common root.
Furthermore, for
f(x)\in A[x]
, we write
\mathrm{rad}(f(x))
to denote the radical of
f(x)
- this is the unique squarefree polynomial with the same set of roots of
f(x)
.

For supersingular primes
\ell
, we can use resultants to extract several other polynomials of interest from the Atkin polynmomial
R_{\ell}(x,y)
.

(1)

Let
F_{\ell}(x,j)
be the classical modular polynomial of level
\ell
.
3
3

3

See Ex. 2.18,2.19 in
[
12
]
or
[
4
]
.
We can obtain
F_{\ell}
from
R_{\ell}
using the following formula:

F_{\ell}(x,j)=\frac{1}{(x-j)^{\ell+1}}\mathrm{Res}_{y}(R_{\ell}(x,y),R_{\ell}(j,y))

(2)

Define:

\Delta_{\ell}(x)=\mathrm{Res}_{y}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}R_{\ell}(x,y),a_{\ell}(y)^{2}-4b_{\ell}(y)\right)

The roots of this polynomial are precisely the points that represent endmorphisms of degree
\ell
in
X_{0}(\ell)^{+}
.
Note that
\Delta_{\ell}(x)
can also be obtained (up to multiplication by a scalar) using the formula
\Delta_{\ell}(x)=F_{\ell}(x,x)
.

We will describe this polynomial in detail in the next section.

(3)

Let
s_{p}(x)=\prod_{j\in S_{p}}x-j
and define the
supersingular polynomial of level
\ell
:

s_{p,\ell}^{+}(y)=\mathrm{rad}\left(\mathrm{Res}_{x}(R_{\ell}(x,y),s_{p}(x))\right)

The roots of this polynomial are precisely the points that represent isogenies between supersingular curves.
Thus, the problem of finding all
Q\in X_{0}(\ell)^{+}
that represent isogenies between supersingular curves is equivalent to computing the roots of
s_{p,\ell}^{+}
.

The polynomials
s_{p,\ell}^{+}
are also the subject of several conjectures
in
[
9
]
.
We end this section by using our results
to shed some light on questions raised in that paper.

2.5.
Application: Nakaya’s conjectures

We will focus on the questions and conjectures related to the splitting field of

To begin, we note that part (1) of Prop

On the other hand, we can use connectedness of

Proposition 2.10

Proof.

•

Furthermore, since

.

3.1.
Overview

3.1.1.
Example

Before introducing any new tools,

\ell=2

It is well-known that:

\Delta_{2}(x)=(x-1728)(x-8000)(x+3375)^{2}

Observe that the roots of
\Delta_{2}
are all congruent to
-1\pmod{7}
,

\Delta_{2}(x)\equiv(x+1)^{4}\pmod{7}

This would suggest that the curve with
j=-1
has 4 endomorphisms of degree 2,
but that is clearly impossible,
since elliptic curves can’t have more than 3 distinct subgroups of order 2.
We will show that this happens because the factor
(x+3375)^{2}

behaves differently in characteristic 7.

3.1.2.
Notation

-d<0

•

,

.

(2)

\sigma_{d}(P)=\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}(P)

(3)

Proof.

,

\alpha\in Aut(E)

Note that
\alpha\circ[\xi]=[\xi]\circ\alpha
,

\ker(\alpha\circ[\xi])=\ker([\xi])

\alpha\circ[\xi]

\xi=\xi^{+}

\xi=\sigma_{d}(\xi)^{+}

In particular, this means that any single curve

P^{+}=[(E,\ker([\xi^{+}]))]

\sigma_{d}(P)=[(E,\ker(\sigma_{d}\cdot(\xi^{+}))]

[\xi^{+}]\circ[\sigma_{d}\xi^{+}]=[\xi^{+}\circ\sigma_{d}\xi^{+}]=[N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\xi^{+}]=[\deg(\xi)]

[\sigma_{d}\cdot\xi^{+}]=\widehat{[\xi]}

[\sigma_{d}\cdot\xi]=\sigma_{d}\cdot[\xi]

.

\mathrm{Fr}_{\ell}((E,\ker([\xi])))=[(E,\ker(\widehat{\xi}))]=\sigma_{d}\cdot[(E,\ker([\xi]))])

proving the first two claims.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
-d
be a discriminant and

\left(\frac{-d}{p}\right)\neq 1

(1)

For each root

There exists

Our first step, regardless of which approach we choose to take,

This can be done without any sophisticated machinery, by taking advantage of the following two facts:

•

\mathbb{F}_{p}

•

We can determine whether an elliptic curve

There are various ways one can approach this search.
To simplify our next task, we will look for supersingular curves given by an equation of the form:

(2)

Setting

•

•

In any equation of that form which describes a supersingular curve, the quadratic factor is irreducible.

Abstractly, this means there is a function

that takes

.
The computation

using models boils down to choosing an algorithm

that computes the image of

in

In general, we can do this using Algorithm

but for

Let

be an elliptic curve given by an equation of the form:

be the roots of

.
Then

has 3 points of order 2, namely

For

and

have been moved to the origin.
The isogenous curves are

are also described by equations of this form, with coefficients
(a_{i}^{\prime},b_{i}^{\prime})=(-2a_{i},a_{i}^{2}-4b_{i})

Thus, we can do all of our computations in the subset

instead of needing extending Weierstrass equations.
Furthermore, the hardest part in the computation of

Let

4.4.
Trace Computations

Let
\ell
be a prime.
The following algorithm gives us the data needed to compute
Tr(\Gamma_{p,\ell})

using Cor.
3.12
.

Algorithm 5

Trace Computation

Construct table
\texttt{TraceFrobenius}=\left\{a:\left\{\right\}|\;0\leq a<2\sqrt{\ell}\right\}
.

Compute the vectors
v_{0}=(1)_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell}},v_{1}=(x)_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}
and
v_{3}=(x^{3}\pmod{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}
.

for

j_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell}\backslash\left\{0,1728\right\}

do

Compute
t(j_{0})=\frac{j_{0}}{1728-j_{0}}\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell}
.

Compute
v_{j_{0}}=v_{3}+3t(j_{0})v_{1}+2t(j_{0})v_{0}
.

Compute
a_{j_{0}}=\left|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\left(\frac{(v_{j_{0}})_{x}}{-p}\right)\right|
.

Update
TraceFrobenius
by adding
j_{0}
to the

entry.

end

for

return

TraceFrobenius

The entry associated to each

in the ouput
is the set of
j
-invariants that admit a model over
\mathbb{F}_{\ell}

with trace of Frobenius equal to
\pm a
.
We’ve left our the curves with

, but it is easy to add these back at the end:

•

If
p\equiv 2\pmod{3}
(resp.
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
), then
j=0
(resp.
j=1728
) is supersingular, and therefore has trace 0.

•

Otherwise, there are three (resp. two) values of

that give rise to a quadratic

that splits over

(resp.
\mathbb{Q}(i)
).

We can use the output of this algorithm to do the following:

(1)

The

-invariants with trace of Frobenius

are the roots of

in

,
where

Thus, we can determine the degree of

directly from the output of this algorithm.

(2)

We can solve for the class numbers of

and then compute the degree of

.

(3)

If we decide to compute a supersingular isogeny graph in

(4)

If we run this algorithm for enough values of

4.5.
Comparison

For an integer
m
, we define
\varepsilon(m)=1
if
m\equiv 2\pmod{4}
and
\varepsilon(m)=0

otherwise.
For
\ell
is a prime and an integer
m
that satisfies
\ell\not|m
, we write
\mathrm{ord}_{(\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}(m)
to denote the order of
m
in the multiplicative group
(\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.
For a smooth curve
X
, write
\mathrm{gon}(X)
to denote the gonality of
X
and
\mathrm{gen}(X)
to denote the genus of
X
.

We define:

\displaystyle\delta_{GM}(p,\ell)

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{ord}_{(\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}(p^{2})-\varepsilon(\mathrm{ord}_{(\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}(p))

\displaystyle\delta_{MC}(p,\ell)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{gon}(X_{0}(\ell))

\displaystyle\delta_{TR}(p,\ell)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{gen}(X_{0}(p))

The integer
\delta_{GM}
,
\delta_{MC}
and
\delta_{TR}
quantify the difficulty
in computing
\Gamma_{p,\ell}
using Algorithm
3
, Algorithm
4
and
3.4.2
), respectively.

•

\delta_{TR}(p,\ell)=0
if and only if
|S_{p}|=1
.
In this case, there is literally nothing to do to obtain the graph
\Gamma_{p,\ell}
- it is simply the graph with 1 vertex and
\ell+1
edges.

Note that
\delta_{GM},\delta_{MC}
are strictly positive for all
p,\ell
.

•

The necessary computations can be done in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
or
\mathbb{F}_{\ell}
whenever
\delta(p,\ell)\leq 2
.

•

In all 3 cases, the computations are a little simpler when
\delta(p,\ell)=1
:

–

In the graph method, the computation of
S_{\ell}(E_{0})
is easiest when
E_{0}
is defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
\ell|p+1
.

–

In the modular curve method, we have simple models of
X_{0}(\ell)
in addition to the Atkin polynomials.

–

In the trace method, we can obtain the complete graph from the integers
c_{\ell}(-d)
without needing the polynomials
H_{-d,m}
.

Conclusion

The algorithms we’ve described have been implemented in Python,
and the code can be found at the following repository:

https://github.com/nhajouji/supsingecs
.

In addition, the repository contains precomputed tables that contain the following:

•

We ran Algorithm

on every prime

,
and recorded the results in a table.
This is the biggest table we computed, and it takes 58 MB to store as a pickled Pandas dataframe.

•

We constructed a much smaller table that contain the degrees of

.

There are also additional tables, but we comment on how these tables can be used:

•

The second table requires much less space to store, and it contains all of the information needed to compute the trace.

•

The first table is not redundant, however, since we can still use the more refined information about elliptic curves over

•

The

5.1.
Generalizations and future work

There are various ways one can try to extend the results in this paper.

(1)

We’ve restricted our analysis of the algorithms to the case where
\delta(p,\ell)\leq 2
.
The most obvious way of improving our work is to extend the analysis to settings where
\delta(p,\ell)>2
.

(2)

The results in Section 3, as stated, only apply to supersingular curves.
It should be possible to generalize the analysis to include ordinary curves,
although the statement may become more complicated.
For example, we need a generalization of Prop
3.2
that includes the case where
\left(\frac{-d}{p}\right)=1
.
The results in
[
7
]
should be helpful for this.

(3)

A better understanding of the curves

would allow us to improve on the trace method for primes like

,
and it would allow us to further investigate the symmetry in Table

A.1.
Algorithm

Let

We can obtain models for

(1)

We construct the elliptic 3-fold
\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{A}^{2}=\mathrm{Spec}\;\mathbb{Q}[u,v]]

y^{2}+(1-u)xy-vy=x^{3}-vx^{2}

Let

be the

\mathcal{E}
.

(2)

Let
P_{0}=(0,0)\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{A}^{2})

We can compute

for any integer

.
Note that
x_{m},y_{m}\in\mathbb{Q}(u,v)
.

(3)

Let
a,b
be integers and suppose
a+b=\ell
.
Setting
x([a]P_{0})=x([b]P_{0})
gives us a relation

between

.
Note that
r_{\ell}(u_{0},v_{0})=0
precisely when
the intersection of
P_{0}
with the fiber over
(u_{0},v_{0})
is a point of order
\ell
.

(4)

Let

be the curve where

vanishes.
For example,
r_{5}(u,v)=u-v
,
and
r_{7}(u,v)=u^{3}+uv-v^{2}
.
Note that
C_{5}
is smooth but
C_{7}
,
is singular.

Now, the curve

is birational to

,

by computing the normalization map

Furthermore, we define

by composing the normalization map

This allows us to obtain a formula for the
j
-map
X_{1}(\ell)\to X(1)
by composing the map
X_{1}(\ell)\to\mathbb{A}^{2}

The computations in
[

(5)

Next, we obtain a model of

by the automorphism

,
where

The results in
[

Once we’ve computed the quotient map

(6)

We also need a formula for the Fricke involution.
This can be obtained in several ways:

•

We can apply Vélu’s formula to the whole family
\mathcal{E}|_{X_{1}(\ell)}\to X_{1}(\ell)
,
and obtain a formula for

.
This always works, but the equations get increasingly complicated as

•

When the genus of

The Fricke involution swaps the cusps of

.

(7)

Finally, once we have a formula for

and

,
