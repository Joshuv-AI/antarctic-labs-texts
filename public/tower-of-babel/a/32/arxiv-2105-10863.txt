Selberg’s Central limit theorem for quadratic dirichlet L-functions over function fields
Pranendu Darbar, Allysa Lumley

Abstract
. In this article, we study the logarithm of the central value L ⁡ ( 1 2 , χ D ) L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D}\right) in the symplectic family of Dirichlet L L -functions associated with the hyperelliptic curve of genus δ \delta over a fixed finite field 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} in the limit as δ → ∞ \delta\to\infty . Unconditionally, we show that the distribution of log ⁡ | L ⁡ ( 1 2 , χ D ) | \log\big|L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D}\right)\big| is asymptotically bounded above by the Gaussian distribution of mean 1 2 ​ log ⁡ deg ​ ( D ) \frac{1}{2}\log\deg(D) and variance log ⁡ deg ⁡ ( D ) \log\deg(D) . Assuming a mild condition on the distribution of the low-lying zeros in this family, we obtain the full Gaussian distribution.



1.
Introduction

The study of the analytic properties of
L
-functions is an integral part of modern number theory due to their myriad of connections to other objects of interest. Of particular interest is trying to understand the distribution of values these
L
-functions take at various points within the critical strip, especially values along the central line
\Re(s)=\frac{1}{2}
. Perhaps the most significant unconditional result in this vein is Selberg’s central limit theorem
[
24
]
, which states that, for
T
sufficiently large as
t
varies in
[T,2T]
, the real and imaginary parts of
\log\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+it)
are normally distributed with mean
0
and variance
\frac{1}{2}\log\log T
. Following this result, a great deal of effort has gone into generalizing this to other
L
-functions. Indeed, Selberg himself proved that for a fixed
t
, the imaginary part of
\log L(\tfrac{1}{2}+it,\chi)
becomes normally distributed as
\chi
varies among Dirichlet characters of a large prime modulus
q
,
[
25
]
. Under some widely believed conditional assumptions, Bombieri and Hejhal
[
6
]
were able to prove similar results for a fairly general class of
L
-functions, also in the
t
-aspect. This work has been made unconditional in at least one case by Wenzhi Luo
[
19
]
.

Recently, Radziwiłł and Soundararajan
[
20
]
provided another proof of Selberg’s original result that was much shorter and easier to digest. This proof was adapted by Hsu and Wong
[
11
]
to treat the case of
\log|L(\frac{1}{2}+it,\chi)|
as
t
varies in
[T,2T]
. Within this work, they also make explicit a notion of independence between primitive Dirichlet
L
-functions predicted by Selberg
[
26
]
. That is, they prove that for
\{\chi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
a sequence of distinct primitive Dirichlet characters and
T
sufficiently large, as
t
varies in
[T,2T]
the vector
(\log|L(\frac{1}{2}+it,\chi_{1})|,\ldots,\log|L(\frac{1}{2}+it,\chi_{n})|)
becomes an
n
-variate normal distribution with mean vector
0_{n}
and covariance matrix
\frac{1}{2}(\log\log T)I_{n}
.

The above examples have something in common, which Katz and Sarnak first illuminated in their groundbreaking work
[
13
]
. From their work we learned that the central values of
L(\frac{1}{2}+it,f)
of an
L
-function belong in a family with symmetry type governed by the classical compact groups. In the above, the families of
L
-functions described correspond to unitary families,
U(N)
. In
[
13
]
it was proposed that although zeros high up on the critical line follow the statistics of the unitary family that the low-lying zeros of specific families may follow the statistics of the orthogonal or symplectic matrix groups instead. This idea was demonstrated by Katz and Sarnak finding zeta-functions over function fields whose zero statistics exhibited such behaviours. Keating and Snaith
[
15
]
made some “Selberg-type” conjectures for the orthogonal and symplectic families based on calculations from random matrix theory. These conjectures appear to be unreachable at the moment since they involve the real part of the logarithm of
L
-functions at the central point
s=\frac{1}{2}
. At present, even the best methods in analytic number theory cannot guarantee more than a positive proportion of
L
-functions in any given family is non-zero at a single point. Indeed, even if we can show that the central value is non-negative, the logarithm is still highly sensitive to zeros close to the central point (i.e. the low-lying zeros described in
[
13
]
). So what can be said about these families?

For
d>0
and characters of the form
\chi_{8d}
, Hough
[
10
]
, provides an upper bound which matches the conjectures of
[
15
]
. In this work, he also
conditionally
proves the conjectures of Keating and Snaith: Let
\frac{D}{2}\leq d\leq D
and

B(d)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\log D}}\left(\log|L(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi_{8d})|-\frac{1}{2}\log\log D\right),

then in the sense of distributions

(1.1)

\frac{1}{|s(D)|}\sum_{d\in S(D)}\hskip-4.26773pt\Delta_{B(d)}\to N(0,1)\text{ as }D\to\infty,

where
s(D)
is the set of odd square-free integers between
D/2
and
D
,
\Delta_{x}
is the point mass at
x
and
N(0,1)
denotes the standard normal distribution with mean
0
and variance
1
. He also finds similar results for the family of
L
-functions associated to weight
k
Hecke eigen-cusp forms which has orthogonal symmetry type. It remains to explain the conditions under which this theorem holds. Recall that the trouble with proving the conjectures of
[
15
]
stems from the lack of control over the low-lying zeros associated with the family of
L
-functions in question. Thus, any imposed conditions should relate to this. Of course, it is enough to assume the Riemann Hypothesis and the well-known density conjecture, due to Iwaniec, Luo and Sarnak
[
12
]
. The specific statement associated to the symplectic family in question is given below:

Conjecture 1.1

(Zero Density Conjecture)
.

Let
\varphi(x)
be a Schwartz class function on
\mathbb{R}
with Fourier transform having compact support. Define the density

W(\mathrm{Sp})(x)dx=\left(1-\frac{\sin(2\pi x)}{2\pi x}\right)dx,

let
\rho=\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
, represent the non-trivial zeros of
L(s,\chi_{8d})
and
\Lambda(s,\chi_{8d})
represent the associated completed
L
-function. Then

\lim_{D\to\infty}\frac{1}{|s(D)|}\sum_{d\in s(D)}\sum_{\Lambda(\rho,\chi_{8d})=0}\varphi\left(\frac{\gamma\log D}{2\pi}\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(x)W(\textrm{Sp})(x)dx.

However, these conditions are quite strong and are also pretty far out of reach. It is therefore of interest to find a weaker condition which still allows Hough’s theorem to hold.
Indeed, he uses the following hypothesis to prove (
1.1
).

Hypothesis 1

(Hypothesis 1.3,
[
10
]
)
.

Assume
y=y(D)\to\infty
with
D
. Then for
d
such that
8d
is a fundamental discriminant, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{D}{2}\leq d\leq D:\gamma_{\min}(d)<\frac{2\pi}{y\log D}\right)=o(1)\text{ as }D\to\infty,

where

\gamma_{\min}(d)=\displaystyle{\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}L(\rho,\chi_{8d})=0\\
\rho=\frac{1}{2}+\beta+i\gamma\end{subarray}}}|\gamma|.

This hypothesis comes from some probabilistic reasoning which suggests if
s
is near
\frac{1}{2}
and the conductor of
\chi_{8d}
is
\asymp D
then typically
\gamma_{\min}(d)\gg\frac{1}{\log D}
.

In this paper, we prove a Selberg-type theorem over function fields,
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
, where
q
is a fixed odd prime power, thus, going back to Katz and Sarnak’s original idea. Let
D\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
be a monic square-free polynomial. We define the primitive quadratic character associated to
D
as the Kronecker symbol,
\left(\frac{D}{\cdot}\right)
, see section
2
for more details. Let
\mathcal{H}_{n,q}
denote the set of monic square-free polynomials of degree
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
There are three aspect in function fields: (i)
n
is fixed and
q
varies which we call “large finite field” aspect; (ii)
q
fixed and
n
varies which we call “large degree” aspect; (iii) both
n
and
q
varies which we call the double limit aspect. Our goal is to explore the “large degree” aspect. For the geometrical significance of these aspects see the section
2.1.

In particular, we prove results for
\log|L(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D})|
in the “large degree” aspect, as such we suppress the
q
in the notation of the set, that is
\mathcal{H}_{n,q}=\mathcal{H}_{n}
. For information in the remaining ranges of
\sigma
, see work of the second author
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
. In this setting, Weil
[
28
]
has proven the Riemann Hypothesis, so one might hope to provide an unconditional result of this flavour. However, in some ways, the situation over function fields is a bit more frustrating.

We are able to prove the following unconditional results which show that if we are very near the
\frac{1}{2}
-line then
\log|L(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D})|
is normally distributed with mean
\frac{1}{2}\log d(D)
and variance
\log d(D)
, where
d(D)
is the degree of the polynomial
D
.
More precisely, we define

(1.2)

\displaystyle 2\delta=d(D)-1-\lambda,

where

\displaystyle\lambda=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}1&\text{if\, $d(D)$ even}\\
0&\text{if\, $d(D)$ odd}.\end{array}\right.

Then we have the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\delta
be defined by (
1.2
) and
\sigma_{o}=\sigma_{o}(\delta)
be a function of
\delta
, tending to zero as
\delta\to\infty
in such a way that
\sigma_{o}\delta\to\infty
but
\frac{\sigma_{o}\delta}{\sqrt{\log\delta}}\to 0
. For
D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
, we consider

A(D)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log n}}\left(\log\left|L\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\right|-\frac{1}{2}\log n\right).

Then, as
n\to\infty

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\Delta_{A(D)}\to N(0,1),

where
\Delta_{x}
is the point mass at
x
and
N(0,1)
is the standard normal distribution.

Remark
.

From (
1.2
), we can notice that
2\delta=n-1-\lambda
, thus as
n\to\infty
so does
\delta
. One introduces the parameter
\delta
as it helps to give a uniform statement about the number of non-trivial zeros associated to
L(s,\chi_{D})
regardless of whether
n=2g+1
or
n=2g+2
. In both cases,
\delta=g
which denotes the genus of the hyperelliptic curve generated from
C_{D}:y^{2}=D(x)
. Further details on this are given in Section
2.4
.

Remark
.

In Section
4
, we observe that the main term of our expression for
\log L(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma,\chi_{D})
is real and the contribution of the imaginary part is very small, thus we we do not consider the distribution of
\arg L(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma,\chi_{D})
.

Theorem
1.1
leads us to the following corollary, which is an unconditional proof of “one-half” of the Keating-Snaith conjectures. That is, we show that
\log|L(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D})|
has at most a Gaussian distribution.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
Z
be a real number. As
n\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}:\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log n}}\left(\log\left|L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D}\right)\right|-\frac{1}{2}\log n\right)>Z\right)\leq\int_{Z}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}dt+o_{Z}(1).

Of course, we would like to have a full result about the distribution for
\log|L(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D})|
as
D
varies over
\mathcal{H}_{n}
, we can at best provide a conditional result, and this comes from some uncertainty due to a conjecture of Chowla. Chowla’s conjecture presented in
[
7
]
asserts that
all

L
-functions associated with quadratic characters do not vanish at the central point
s=\frac{1}{2}
. The analogue of Chowla’s conjecture over function fields has been disproven by Li
[
16
, Theorem 1.3]
. More precisely, she finds infinitely many quadratic characters
\chi_{D}
such that
L(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi_{D})=0
and provides a lower bound for the number of these counterexamples. Based on the size of these lower bounds, it is still possible that Chowla’s conjecture holds for
almost all
quadratic characters, that is for
100\%
of quadratic characters. Indeed, Bui and Florea
[
4
]
prove that
94.29\%
of
D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}
satisfy
L(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D})\neq 0
as
g\to\infty
.

Now, we require an analogue of Hypothesis
1
. The justification for this analogue is given in Section
2.4
.

Hypothesis 2

(Low-lying Zero Hypothesis)
.

Let
\theta_{j,D}
be the eigenphases associated to (
2.6
). If
y=y(\delta)\to\infty
then as
\delta\to\infty
we obtain

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\left|\left\{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}:\min_{j}|\theta_{j,D}|<\frac{1}{y\delta}\right\}\right|=o(1),

where
\delta
is defined by (
1.2
).

From here we obtain:

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose that the Low-lying Zero Hypothesis holds for
\{L(s,\chi_{D})\}_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}
. For
D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
, we consider

\widetilde{A}(D)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log n}}\left(\log\left|L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D}\right)\right|-\frac{1}{2}\log n\right).

Then we have

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\Delta_{\widetilde{A}(D)}\to N(0,1),\quad n\to\infty.

The remark following Theorem
1.1
also applies to Theorem
1.3
. That is, we have a uniform statement about the distribution of
\log|L(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi_{D})|
regardless of whether
n=2g+1
or
n=2g+2
.

The plan for the paper is as follows: In section
2
, we will give necessary background for studying the question over function fields. In section
3
, we compile a list of useful lemmas to complete the proofs. In Section
4
, we will develop an expression for
\log L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D})
which makes it easier to do the necessary moment calculations. In Section
5
, we prove Theorem
1.1
. In Section
1.3
, we discuss the discrepancy between
\log|L(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi_{D})|
and
\log|L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}),\chi_{D})|
and complete the proof of Theorem
1.3
. In the final section we prove Corollary
1.2
.

2.
Background for
L
-functions over function fields

We begin by fixing some notation which will be used throughout the paper. We will use
[
21
]
as a general reference.

2.1.
Notations and basics:

Let
q=p^{e}
, for p a fixed odd prime and
e\geq 1
an integer. Then let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the finite field with
q
elements. The polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
has many things in common with the integers including satisfying a “prime number theorem” for its monic irreducible polynomials. For
f
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
we denote the degree of the polynomial as
d(f)
or
\deg(f)
. The norm of a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
is, for
f\neq 0
, define to be
|f|=q^{d(f)}
and for
f=0
,
|f|=0
.

We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n}=\{f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]:f\text{ is monic and }d(f)=n\},\hskip 5.69054pt\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\leq}n}=\bigcup_{j\leq n}\mathcal{M}_{j},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]:f\text{ is monic, irreducible and }d(f)=n\},\mathcal{P}_{\leq n}=\bigcup_{j\leq n}\mathcal{P}_{j},

and

\mathcal{H}_{n}=\{f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]:f\text{ is monic, square-free and }d(f)=n\}.

Observe that
|\mathcal{M}_{n}|=q^{n}
and for
n\geq 1
,
|\mathcal{H}_{n}|=q^{n-1}(q-1)
.

Let
\Lambda(f)
be the analogue of the Von Mangoldt function:

\Lambda(f)=\begin{cases}\deg P&\text{if }f=P^{k},P\in\mathcal{P},\\
0&\text{otherwise }.\end{cases}

The prime polynomial theorem (see
[
21
]
, Theorem
2.2
) states that

(2.1)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{P}_{n}|=\frac{q^{n}}{n}\,\,+\,\,O\Big(\frac{q^{\frac{n}{2}}}{n}\Big).

The zeta function of
A=\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
, denoted by
\zeta_{A}(s)
, is defined by

\zeta_{A}(s)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{|f|^{s}}=\prod_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\left(1-|P|^{-s}\right)^{-1},

for
\Re(s)>1
. One can show that
\zeta_{A}(s)=\frac{1}{1-q^{1-s}}
, and this provides an analytic continuation of zeta function to the complex plane with a simple pole at
s=1
. Using the change of variable
u=q^{-s}
,

\displaystyle\zeta_{A}(s)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}u^{d(f)}=\frac{1}{1-qu},\quad\text{ if }|u|<\frac{1}{q}.

2.2.
Quadratic Dirichlet characters and their
L
-functions:

Let
P
be a monic irreducible polynomial, we define the quadratic character
\left(\frac{f}{P}\right)
by

\displaystyle\left(\frac{f}{P}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}1&\text{if\, $f$ is a square\;}(\text{mod}\,\,P)\,\,,P\nmid f\\
-1&\text{if\, $f$ is not a square\;}(\text{mod}\,\,P)\,\,,P\nmid f\\
0&\text{if \; }P\mid f.\end{array}\right.

We extend this definition to any
D\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
multiplicatively and define the quadratic character
\chi_{D}(f)
as
\left(\frac{D}{f}\right).
Given any character one can define an
L
-function associated to it:

\displaystyle L(s,\chi_{D})=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{s}}=\prod_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\left(1-\chi_{D}(P)\,|P|^{-s}\right)^{-1},\;\;\Re(s)>1.

Using the change of variable
u=q^{-s}
, we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\chi_{D}(f)\,u^{d(f)}=\prod_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\left(1-\chi_{D}(P)\,u^{d(P)}\right)^{-1},\;\;\,|u|<\frac{1}{q}.

Observe that if
n\geq d(D)
, then

\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}\chi_{D}(f)=0.

Thus we have
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})
is a polynomial of degree at most
d(D)-1
. From here on, we consider
D
as a monic, square-free polynomial. Then
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})
has a trivial zero at
u=1
if and only if
d(D)
is even. Thus

(2.3)

\displaystyle L(s,\chi_{D})=\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})=(1-u)^{\lambda}\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})=(1-q^{-s})^{\lambda}{L}^{*}(s,\chi_{D}),

where

\displaystyle\lambda=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}1&\text{if\, $d(D)$ even}\\
0&\text{if\, $d(D)$ odd},\end{array}\right.

and
\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})
is a polynomial of degree

\displaystyle 2\delta=d(D)-1-\lambda,

satisfying the functional equation

\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})=(qu^{2})^{\delta}\mathcal{L}^{*}\left(\frac{1}{qu},\chi_{D}\right).

Because
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{*}
are polynomial in
u
, it is convenient to define

L^{*}(s,\chi_{D})=\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})

so that the above functional equation can be rewritten as

L^{*}(s,\chi_{D})=q^{(1-2s)\delta}L^{*}(1-s,\chi_{D}).

Additionally, since
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is a polynomial, it can be written as a product of its zeros:

(2.6)

\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})=\prod_{j=1}^{2\delta}\left(1-u\sqrt{q}\,\alpha_{j}\,\right),

where
\alpha_{j}=e(-\theta_{j,D})
are the reciprocals of
u_{j}=q^{-\frac{1}{2}}e(\theta_{j,D})
, the roots of
\mathcal{L}^{*}
. We call
\theta_{j,D}
the eigenphases of the polynomial and are described in more detail in Section
2.4
.
The Riemann Hypothesis, proved by Weil
[
28
]
is that the zeros of
\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})
all lie on the circle
|u|=q^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.

We define the completed
L
-function in the following way. Set
X_{D}(s)=|D|^{\frac{1}{2}-s}X(s)
, where

\displaystyle X(s)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}q^{s-\frac{1}{2}}&\text{if\, $d(D)$ odd}\\
\frac{1-q^{-s}}{1-q^{-(1-s)}}q^{-1+2s}&\text{if\, $d(D)$ even}.\end{array}\right.

Let us consider

\displaystyle\Lambda(s,\chi_{D})=L(s,\chi_{D})X_{D}(s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Then the above completed
L
-function satisfies the symmetric functional equation

\displaystyle\Lambda(s,\chi_{D})=\Lambda(1-s,\chi_{D}).

2.3.
The logarithmic derivative:

By taking logarithmic derivative of (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) we obtain

\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{L}}(u,\chi_{D})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}\Lambda(f)\chi_{D}(f)\right)u^{n-1}

and

\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{L}}(u,\chi_{D})=\lambda(1-u)^{-1}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}^{n}\right)u^{n-1},

where
\lambda
is defined by (
2.2
).
Equating these two expressions, we obtain

\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}\Lambda(f)\chi_{D}(f)=-\lambda-q^{\frac{n}{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{2\delta}e(-n\theta_{j}).

We may also express the logarithmic derivative in two ways, one in terms of the zeros of
L(s,\chi_{D})
as

(2.7)

\displaystyle\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,\chi_{D})

\displaystyle=\log q\bigg(\frac{\lambda q^{-s}}{1-q^{-s}}+\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\frac{\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-s}}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-s}}\bigg)

and the other in terms of the Dirichlet series as

(2.8)

\displaystyle\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,\chi_{D})=-\log q\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}\frac{\Lambda(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{s}}.

2.4.
Spectral Interpretation

Let
C
be a non-singular projective curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of genus
\delta
. For each extension field of
degree
n
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, denote by
N_{n}(C)
the number of points of
C
in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
. Then, the zeta
function associated to
C
defined as

Z_{C}(u)=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}N_{n}(C)\frac{u^{n}}{n}\right),\quad|u|<\frac{1}{q},

is known to be a rational function of
u
of the form

Z_{C}(u)=\frac{P_{C}(u)}{(1-u)(1-qu)}.

Additionally, we know,
P_{C}(u)
is a polynomial of degree
2\delta
with integer coefficients, satisfying
a functional equation

P_{C}(u)=(qu^{2})^{\delta}P_{C}\left(\frac{1}{qu}\right),

where
\delta
is defined as in (
1.2
).

The Riemann Hypothesis, proved by Weil
[
28
]
, says that the zeros of
P_{C}(u)
all lie on the circle
|u|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
. Thus one may give a spectral interpretation of

P_{C}(u)
as the characteristic polynomial of a
2\delta\times 2\delta
unitary matrix
\Theta_{C}
:

P_{C}(u)=\det\left(I-u\sqrt{q}\Theta_{C}\right).

Thus the eigenvalues
e^{i\theta_{j}}
of
\Theta_{C}
correspond to the zeros,
q^{-1/2}e^{-i\theta_{j}}
, of
Z_{C}(u)
.
The matrix
\Theta_{C}
is called the unitarized Frobenius class of
C
.

To put this in the context of our case, note that, for a family of hyperelliptic curves
C_{D}:\,y^{2}=D(x)
of genus
\delta
, the numerator of the zeta function
Z_{C}(u)
associated to
C_{D}
is coincide with the
L
-function
\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})
, i.e.,
P_{C}(u)=\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})
.

It is an interesting problem to study how the Frobenius classes
\Theta_{C}
change as we vary the associated curve over a family of hyperelliptic curves with genus
\delta
. As mentioned in the introduction, there are three aspects where we can study the distribution of of these Frobenius classes.

(i) Large finite field aspect

Katz and Sarnak
[
13
]
showed that that as
q\to\infty
, the Frobenius classes
\Theta_{D}

become equidistributed in the unitary symplectic group
USp(2\delta)
. More precisely,

(2.9)

\lim_{q\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}F(\Theta_{D})=\int_{USp(2\delta)}F(U)dU,

for any continuous function
F
on the space of conjugacy classes of
USp(2\delta)
.
This implies that in the large finite field aspect various statistics of the eigenvalues can be
computed by integrating the corresponding quantities over
USp(2\delta).

(iii) Double limit aspect

See
[
13
, pg. 11]
where they let
\delta\to\infty
in (
2.9
).

(ii) Large degree aspect

Since the matrices
\Theta_{D}
now inhabit
different spaces as
\delta
grows, it is not clear how to formulate an equidistribution problem.
The following analysis of Katz and Sarnak
[
14
]
illuminates one possible interpretation. We start with an even test function
f
, say, in the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, and for any
N\geq 1
set

F(\theta):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f\left(N\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}-k\right)\right).

F(\theta)
has period
2\pi
and is localized in an interval of size
\thickapprox 1/N\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
.
Next, for a unitary
2\delta\times 2\delta
matrix
U
with eigenvalues
e^{i\theta_{j}},j=1,\ldots,2\delta
, define

Z_{f}(U):=\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}F(\theta_{j,D}).

Now,
Z_{f}(U)
counts the number of “low-lying” eigenphases,
\theta_{j,D}
, in the smooth interval of length
\thickapprox\frac{1}{N}
around the origin defined by
f
. In other words, for
j\geq 1
, the above discussion describes the distribution of the numbers

\frac{\theta_{j,D}\,\delta}{2\pi}

as
D
varies over
\mathcal{H}_{n}
,
\,n\to\infty
. That is, we use this to study the distribution of the
j
-th lowest zero.

Conjecture 2.1

(Density Conjecture)
.

For a fixed
q
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}Z_{f}(\Theta_{D})=\int_{USp(2\delta)}Z_{f}(U)dU

for any test function defined as above.

It is also known that

\lim_{\delta\to\infty}\int_{USp(2\delta)}Z_{f}(U)dU\sim\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\left(1-\frac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}\right)dx.

Rudnick
[
22
]
proved the Conjecture
2.1
for
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
is even, with Fourier transform
\hat{f}
supported in
(-2,2)
before Bui and Florea
[
4
]
proved the same with the support
(1/4,1/2)
.

As an application, the Density Conjecture
2.1
gives the distribution of zeros for a family
L(s,\chi_{D})
near
s=\frac{1}{2}
. As discussed above, this has immediate applications to counting how often
L(\tfrac{1}{2},\chi_{D})=0
. More precisely, by varying the test function
f
in the Density Conjecture for any of the above family of hyperelliptic curves
C_{D}
, one is led to (assuming
the Density Conjecture) (See equation
(56)
of
[
14
]
):

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\#\{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}:L(1/2,\chi_{D})\neq 0\}=1.

Thus, it is clear that, for the above family of hyperelliptic curves, Hypothesis
2
is a consequence of Conjecture
2.1
.

Lemma 3.1

\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\bigg|\sum_{d(P)\leq y}\frac{\chi_{D}(P)a(P)}{|P|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\bigg|^{2k}\ll q^{n}\frac{(2k)!}{k!2^{k}}\left(\sum_{d(P)\leq y}\frac{|a(P)|^{2}}{|P|}\right)^{k}.

Proof.

The case

is proved by Florea,
[

. To get the result for

it is a small adaptation of Florea’s proof.

.

f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\chi_{D}(f^{2})=\prod_{P|f}\left(1+\frac{1}{|P|}\right)^{-1}+O(q^{-n-1}).

Proof.

See [

]
, Lemma
3.7
] for
2g+1
case. To get the result for

it is a small adaptation of their proof.
∎

(Pólya-Vinogradov inequality)
.

For
l\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
not a square polynomial, let
l=l_{1}l_{2}^{2}
with
l_{1}
square free. Then we get

\bigg|\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\chi_{D}(l)\bigg|\ll_{\epsilon}q^{g}|l_{1}|^{\epsilon}.

Proof.

See [
[
5
]
, Lemma
3.5
] for the case

. To obtain the result for
n=2g+2
follow the same proof, there is only a minor difference to handle the extra term coming from the trivial zero
u^{-1}
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
K\geq 2
and
\sigma_{o}<1
be such that
K\sigma_{o}<\frac{1}{2\log q}
. Then we have

\sum_{d(P)\leq K}\frac{1}{|P|^{1+2\sigma_{o}}}=\log K+O(1)+O\left(q^{-\frac{K}{4}}\right).

Proof.

From the prime polynomial theorem (
2.1
) and partial summation formula, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{d(P)\leq K}\frac{1}{|P|^{1+2\sigma_{o}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq K}\frac{1}{nq^{2n\sigma_{o}}}+O\bigg(\sum_{n\leq K}\frac{1}{nq^{n/2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\log K+O(1)+O\left(q^{-\frac{K}{4}}\right).

∎

Now we state a standard probabilistic lemma which tells us when the measure of a certain set is very tiny.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
R
be random variable defined on the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{n}
of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
. Suppose that the second moment of
R
is small in the sense of

\displaystyle\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}|R_{D}|^{2}=O\left(|\mathcal{H}_{n}|\right).

Then

\#\{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}:R_{D}>T\}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}{T}.

3.1.
Estimates of Sums involving
\Lambda_{X}

Define

(3.1)

\Lambda_{X}(f)=\begin{cases}2X^{2}\Lambda(f)&\text{ if }d(f)\leq X\\
(X^{2}-(d(f))^{2}+2d(f)X-3X-d(f)-2)\Lambda(f)&\text{ if }X<d(f)\leq 2X\\
(3X-d(f)+1)(3X-d(f)+2)\Lambda(f)&\text{ if }2X<d(f)\leq 3X.\end{cases}

We have the following estimates:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer and
X
be a real number such that
X<\frac{n}{3k}
. Suppose that
\sigma_{0}>0
. Then

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left|\frac{1}{X^{3}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right|^{k}=O\left(\left(\frac{18k}{e}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}+\frac{4^{k}}{X^{k}}\right).

Remark
.

In particular, taking
k=2
we will obtain that this expression is
O(1)
.

Proof.

From the definition of
\Lambda_{X}(f)
, we can expand this into a sum over prime powers so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left|\frac{1}{X^{3}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right|^{k}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left|\frac{1}{X^{3}}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(P)\chi_{D}(P)}{|P|^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}+\sum_{P^{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(P^{2})\chi_{D}(P^{2})}{|P^{2}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right)\right|^{k}

\displaystyle\ll 2^{k}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left|\frac{1}{X^{3}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(P)\chi_{D}(P)}{|P|^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right|^{k}+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left|\frac{1}{X^{3}}\sum_{P^{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(P^{2})\chi_{D}(P^{2})}{|P^{2}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right|^{k}\right)

\displaystyle\ll 2^{k}(S_{1}+S_{2}).

We first consider
S_{2}
: By definition,
\chi_{D}(P^{2})=1
, and the
\Lambda_{X}(P^{2})\leq 4X^{2}d(P)
so we have

S_{2}\ll\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left|\frac{4}{X}\sum_{P^{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{d(P)}{|P|^{1+2\sigma_{0}}}\right|^{k}.

From the prime polynomial theorem we obtain

S_{2}\ll\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left|\frac{4}{X}\sum_{2m\leq 3X}\frac{mq^{m}}{mq^{m(1+2\sigma_{0})}}\right|^{k}\ll 6^{k}.

For
S_{1}
we expand the power k and then swap the order of summation giving

S_{1}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\frac{1}{X^{3k}}\sum_{P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(P_{1})\Lambda_{X}(P_{2})\cdots\Lambda_{X}(P_{k})}{|P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\chi_{D}(P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}).

From Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
we see that the values are different depending on if
P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}
is a square or not. Thus we consider two cases:

Case 1:
k
is even and
P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}=\square
.

Applying Lemma
3.2
, we must estimate

\frac{1}{X^{3k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}\\
P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}=\square\end{subarray}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(P_{1})\Lambda_{X}(P_{2})\cdots\Lambda_{X}(P_{k})}{|P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}|^{1/2+\sigma_{0}}}\left(1+\frac{1}{|P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}|}\right)^{-1}.

First note that
P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}=\square
happens precisely when one can pair up the indices so that the corresponding primes are equal thus using that
\Lambda_{X}(P)=O(4X^{2}d(P))
we have the above

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{1}{X^{3k}}\frac{k!}{(k/2)!2^{k/2}}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{(\Lambda_{X}(P))^{2}}{|P|^{1+2\sigma_{0}}}\left(1+\frac{1}{|P|}\right)^{-1}\right)^{k/2}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{k!2^{k/2}}{(k/2)!}\frac{1}{X^{k}}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{(d(P))^{2}}{|P|^{1+2\sigma_{0}}}\left(\frac{|P|}{|P|+1}\right)\right)^{k/2}.

Then, using the prime polynomial theorem and Stirling’s approximation, we obtain

\displaystyle\ll\frac{k!2^{k/2}}{(k/2)!}\frac{1}{X^{k}}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{m^{2}q^{m}}{mq^{m}}\right)^{k/2}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{k!2^{k/2}}{(k/2)!}\frac{((3X)(3X+1))^{k/2}}{2^{k/2}X^{k}}\ll\left(\frac{18k}{e}\right)^{k/2}.

Case 2:
P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}\neq\square
.

In this case, using Lemma
3.3
, we need to estimate:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}}\frac{1}{X^{3k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}\\
P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}\neq\square\end{subarray}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(P_{1})\Lambda_{X}(P_{2})\cdots\Lambda_{X}(P_{k})}{|P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}|P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}|^{\epsilon}.

Using that
\Lambda_{X}(P)=O(X^{2}d(P))
we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}}\frac{1}{X^{3k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}\\
P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}\neq\square\end{subarray}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(P_{1})\Lambda_{X}(P_{2})\cdots\Lambda_{X}(P_{k})}{|P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}|P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}|^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}}\frac{4^{k}}{X^{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}\\
P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}\neq\square\end{subarray}}\frac{d(P_{1})d(P_{2})\cdots d(P_{k})}{|P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}-\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}}\frac{4^{k}}{X^{k}}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{d(P)}{|P|^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}-\epsilon}}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}}\frac{4^{k}}{X^{k}}\left(\sum_{m\leq 3X}q^{m(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon)}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{4^{k}q^{3kX(\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon)}}{q^{n/2}X^{k}}\ll(4/X)^{k}.

To see that this is indeed
\ll(4/X)^{k}
we use the assumption
X<n/3k
.
Combining these estimates we have the desired result.
∎

3.2.
Estimates for sums over the zeros

We may rewrite (
2.7
) as

(3.2)

\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,\chi_{D})=\log q\left(\frac{\lambda q^{-s}}{1-q^{-s}}-2\delta+\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{1/2-s}}\right)\right).

Notice that the non-trivial zeros appear in complex conjugate pairs and so (
3.2
) gives a real value for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, as was pointed out in
[
1
]
. Thus, we may also express (
3.2
) as follows:

\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,\chi_{D})=\log q\left(\frac{\lambda q^{-s}}{1-q^{-s}}-\delta+\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\Re\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{1/2-s}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right).

Lemma 3.7

(Lemma 3.2,
[
1
]
)
.

Let
\sigma_{0}>0
. Then for
\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}
, we have the inequality

\left|\frac{\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-1/2}}{(1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-1/2})^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{(\sigma-\tfrac{1}{2})^{-1}}{\log q}\Re\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}\log q}\Re\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma_{0}}}\right).

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\sigma_{0}>0
. Then for
\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}
,

\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\left|\frac{(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X}(1-(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X})^{2}}{(1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}})^{3}}\right|\leq\frac{4q^{X(\frac{1}{2}-\sigma)}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}\log^{3}q}\left(\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)+2\delta\log q\right).

Proof.

We begin with

\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\frac{(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X}(1-(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X})^{2}}{(1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}})^{3}}.

Lemma
3.7
gives

\left|\frac{(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X+1}(1-(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X})^{2}(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{-1}}{(1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}})(1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}})^{2}}\right|\\
\leq\left|\frac{(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X+1}(1-(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X})^{2}}{(1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}})}\right|\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}\log q}\Re\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma_{0}}}\right)

We may easily bound the numerator as
|(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X+1}(1-(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X})^{2}|\leq 4q^{X(\frac{1}{2}-\sigma)}
. It remains to discuss the denominator:

We have
1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}}=1-\cos(2\pi\theta_{j})q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}}-i\sin(2\pi\theta_{j})q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}}
and so

\displaystyle|1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}}|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(1-\cos(2\pi\theta_{j})q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}})^{2}+(\sin(2\pi\theta_{j})q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+q^{2\sigma-1}-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j})q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}}}

Since
\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}
, we have

\frac{1}{|1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}}|}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+q^{2\sigma_{0}}-2q^{\sigma_{0}}}}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}\log q},

where the last inequality follows from the Taylor expansion.
Thus we have

\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\left|\frac{(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X}(1-(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X})^{2}}{(1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}})^{3}}\right|\leq\frac{4q^{X(\frac{1}{2}-\sigma)}}{(\sigma_{0}\log q)^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\Re\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma_{0}}}\right).

Finally, (
3.2
) gives us that

\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\Re\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma_{0}}}\right)=\frac{1}{\log q}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)+2\delta-\frac{\lambda q^{-(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0})}}{1-q^{-(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0})}}.

Note that
\frac{\lambda q^{-(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0})}}{1-q^{-(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0})}}
is a positive quantity and it is too small to effect the analysis so we drop it in the upperbound.
∎

4.
A formula for
\log L(\sigma,\chi_{D})

Throughout the rest of the paper, we fix the following notations: the parameter
\delta
as defined as in (
1.2
), the parameter
\sigma_{0}=\frac{c}{X}
, with
0<c<\frac{1}{2\log q}
.
Furthermore, we define

(4.1)

\widetilde{P}_{X}(\sigma,\chi_{D}):=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq X}}\frac{\Lambda(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{d(f)|f|^{\sigma}}.

The purpose of this section is to prove the following proposition:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X\geq 1
. Then

\log L(1/2+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D})=\widetilde{P}_{X}(1/2+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D})+O\left(\frac{1}{X^{3}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right)\\
+O\left(\frac{\delta}{X}+\frac{\lambda}{X(X+2)}\right).

This proposition is useful as it allows us to compute the moments of
\log|L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D})|
by studying the simpler expression
\widetilde{P}_{X}(\sigma,\chi_{D})
. The proposition follows immediately from the following lemma after some straightforward manipulations.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X\geq 1
. Then we have

\displaystyle\log L\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)=\frac{1}{2X^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{d(f)|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}+O\left(\frac{1}{X^{3}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{\delta}{X}+\frac{\lambda}{X(X+2)}\right).

We will make use of the following identity, let
X\geq 0
,
\Re(s)\geq 0
:

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2}^{2+\frac{2\pi i}{\log q}}\frac{q^{X(w-s)}}{(1-q^{-(w-s)})^{3}}dw=-\frac{(X+1)(X+2)}{2\log q}.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

We begin by considering the following integral

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2}^{2+\frac{2\pi i}{\log q}}\frac{q^{X(w-s)}(1-q^{X(w-s)})^{2}}{(1-q^{-(w-s)})^{3}}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(w,\chi_{D})dw

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2}^{2+\frac{2\pi i}{\log q}}\frac{q^{X(w-s)}(1-2q^{X(w-s)}+q^{2X(w-s)})}{(1-q^{-(w-s)})^{3}}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(w,\chi_{D})dw.

We compute this integral in two different ways. The first approach is to use (
2.8
), (
4.2
) and integrate the result term by term:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq X}}\frac{\Lambda(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{s}}(X-d(f)+1)(X-d(f)+2)

\displaystyle-\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 2X}}\frac{\Lambda(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{s}}(2X-d(f)+1)(2X-d(f)+2)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{s}}(3X-d(f)+1)(3X-d(f)+2).

We combine these into a single sum recognizing the weight
\Lambda_{X}(f)
defined in (
3.1
), so that

(4.3)

\mathcal{I}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{s}}.

The second approach is to use the residue theorem, from this method, we see that there is a pole of order 3 when
w=s
and simple poles for each zero of
\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(w,\chi_{D})
. So,

(4.4)

\mathcal{I}=-\frac{X^{2}}{\log q}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,\chi_{D})-\log q\bigg(\frac{\lambda q^{-(X+2)s}}{1-q^{-s}}+\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\frac{(q^{\frac{1}{2}-s}\alpha_{j})^{X}(1-(q^{\frac{1}{2}-s}\alpha_{j})^{X})^{2}}{(1-q^{s-\frac{1}{2}}\alpha_{j}^{-1})^{3}}\bigg).

Equating (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), we have

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{s}}\\
=-\frac{X^{2}}{\log q}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,\chi_{D})-\log q\bigg(\frac{\lambda q^{-(X+2)s}}{1-q^{-s}}+\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\frac{(q^{\frac{1}{2}-s}\alpha_{j})^{X}(1-(q^{\frac{1}{2}-s}\alpha_{j})^{X})^{2}}{(1-q^{s-\frac{1}{2}}\alpha_{j}^{-1})^{3}}\bigg),

which implies

(4.5)

\displaystyle-\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,\chi_{D})=

\displaystyle\frac{\log q}{2X^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{s}}

\displaystyle+\frac{(\log q)^{2}}{2X^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\lambda q^{-(X+2)s}}{1-q^{-s}}+\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\frac{(q^{\frac{1}{2}-s}\alpha_{j})^{X}(1-(q^{\frac{1}{2}-s}\alpha_{j})^{X})^{2}}{(1-q^{s-\frac{1}{2}}\alpha_{j}^{-1})^{3}}\bigg).

We note that this implies, for
\Re(s)=\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2}
, we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle-\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(\sigma,\chi_{D})

\displaystyle=\frac{\log q}{2X^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle+\frac{(\log q)^{2}}{2X^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\lambda q^{-(X+2)\sigma}}{1-q^{-\sigma}}+\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\frac{(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X}(1-(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X})^{2}}{(1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}})^{3}}\bigg).

Now, it remains to provide estimates for the error terms. We handle the sum over the zeros in (
4.6
) by applying Lemma
3.8
, which gives

\frac{(\log q)^{2}}{X^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\left|\frac{(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X}(1-(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X})^{2}}{(1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}})^{3}}\right|\leq\frac{4q^{X(\frac{1}{2}-\sigma)}}{X^{2}\sigma_{0}^{2}\log q}\left(\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)+2\delta\log q\right).

Using the fact that
\sigma_{0}=\frac{c}{X}
, for
c
some positive constant, we have

\frac{(\log q)^{2}}{X^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\left|\frac{(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X}(1-(\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma})^{X})^{2}}{(1-\alpha_{j}^{-1}q^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}})^{3}}\right|\leq\frac{4q^{X(\frac{1}{2}-\sigma)}}{c^{2}\log q}\left(\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)+2\delta\log q\right).

Thus, we can find some
v
such that
|v|\leq 1
such that

(4.7)

\displaystyle-\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(\sigma,\chi_{D})

\displaystyle=\frac{\log q}{2X^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle+\frac{4\nu q^{X(\frac{1}{2}-\sigma)}}{c^{2}\log q}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)+O\left(\delta q^{X(\frac{1}{2}-\sigma)}+\frac{\lambda q^{-(X+2)\sigma}}{X}\right).

So, evaluating (
4.7
) at
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}
, we conclude

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{4\nu q^{-X\sigma_{0}}}{c^{2}\log q}\right)\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)=\frac{\log q}{2X^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}

\displaystyle+O\left(\delta q^{-X\sigma_{0}}+\frac{\lambda q^{-(X+2)(1/2+\sigma_{0})}}{X}\right).

Additionally, we choose
c
such that
c^{2}q^{c}>\frac{4}{\log q}
, which implies
\left(1+\frac{4\nu q^{-X\sigma_{0}}}{c^{2}\log q}\right)>1-\frac{4}{c^{2}q^{c}\log q}>C
, for some
C>0
. Note that this is a valid choice for
c
since we assumed
c<\frac{1}{2\log q}
. Thus, we obtain

(4.8)

\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{X^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right)+O(\delta).

Finally, putting together (
4.8
) and (
4.7
), we find that

\displaystyle-\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(\sigma,\chi_{D})

\displaystyle=\frac{\log q}{2X^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{q^{X(\frac{1}{2}-\sigma)}}{X^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right)+O\left(\delta q^{X(\frac{1}{2}-\sigma)}+\frac{\lambda q^{-(X+2)\sigma}}{X}\right).

Integrating with respect to
\sigma
from
\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}
to
\infty
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\log L\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2X^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{d(f)|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{1}{X^{3}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right)+O\left(\frac{\delta}{X}+\frac{\lambda}{X(X+2)}\right).

∎

Now, we can complete the proof of Proposition
4.1
:

Proof.

Simply apply Lemma
4.2
:

\displaystyle\log L\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2X^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{d(f)|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{1}{X^{3}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right)+O\left(\frac{\delta}{X}+\frac{\lambda}{X(X+2)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq X}}\frac{\Lambda(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{d(f)|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}+\frac{1}{2X^{2}}\sum_{X<d(f)\leq 3X}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{d(f)|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{1}{X^{3}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right)+O\left(\frac{\delta}{X}+\frac{\lambda}{X(X+2)}\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{P}_{X}(1/2+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D})+\frac{1}{2X^{2}}\sum_{X<d(f)\leq 3X}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{d(f)|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{1}{X^{3}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\right)+O\left(\frac{\delta}{X}+\frac{\lambda}{X(X+2)}\right).

We conclude by noting that for

\frac{1}{2X^{2}}\sum_{X<d(f)\leq 3X}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{d(f)|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}\leq\frac{1}{2X^{3}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 3X}}\frac{\Lambda_{X}(f)\chi_{D}(f)}{|f|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0}}}.

∎

5.
Moment Estimation and Proof of the unconditional results

Recall that: the parameter
\sigma_{0}=\frac{c}{X}
, with
0<c<\frac{1}{2\log q}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
k
be a natural number such that
k\ll\left(\frac{\log n}{(\log\log n)^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}
and
1\leq X\leq\frac{n}{2k}
. Then, as
D
varies in
\mathcal{H}_{n}
, the distribution of
\widetilde{P}_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)
is approximately normal with mean
\frac{1}{2}\log n
and variance
\log n
.

To establish Proposition
5.1
, we need to calculate the moments of
\widetilde{P}_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log X
.

We start with the moments of the following sum over irreducible polynomials:

P_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)=\sum_{d(P)\leq X}\frac{\chi_{D}(P)}{|P|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o}}}.

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume that
1\leq X\leq\frac{n}{2k}
. Uniformly for all odd natural numbers
k\leq{\frac{n}{2}}
and for every
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left(P_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\right)^{k}\ll_{\epsilon}\frac{q^{\frac{n}{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon-\sigma_{o}\right)Xk}}{X^{k}},

while, uniformly for all even natural numbers
k\ll(\log n)^{\frac{1}{3}}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left(P_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\right)^{k}=\frac{k!}{(k/2)!2^{\frac{k}{2}}}(\log X)^{\frac{k}{2}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{k^{3}}{\log X}\right)\right).

Remark
.

In the case of function fields, the contribution of the odd moments is negligibly small even for
k
large due to the fact that Lindelöf Hypothesis is known. For example, if we take
X=\frac{n}{2k}
then the odd moments contribute
o(1)
.

Proof.

Expanding
k
-th power and interchanging summations, we have

s_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(P_{i})\leq X\\
i=1,\ldots,k\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|P_{1}\ldots P_{k}|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o}}}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\chi_{D}(P_{1}\ldots P_{k}).

Case 1
.

Let
k
be even and
P_{1}\ldots P_{k}=\square
.

Subcase 1.1
.

Suppose there are exactly
\frac{k}{2}
distinct primes say
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{\frac{k}{2}}
with

d(Q_{1})\leq d(Q_{2})\leq\ldots\leq d(Q_{k/2}).

Using Lemma
3.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle s_{n}=\frac{k!}{(k/2)!2^{\frac{k}{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(Q_{i})\leq X\\
i=1,\ldots,k/2\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|Q_{1}\ldots Q_{k/2}|^{1+2\sigma_{o}}}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\chi_{D}\left((Q_{1}\ldots Q_{k/2})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{H}_{n}|\times\frac{k!}{(k/2)!2^{\frac{k}{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(Q_{i})\leq X\\
i=1,\ldots,k/2\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k/2}\frac{1}{(|Q_{i}|+1)|Q_{i}|^{2\sigma_{o}}}+O\bigg(|\mathcal{H}_{n}|^{-1}\frac{k!}{(k/2)!2^{\frac{k}{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(Q_{i})\leq X\\
i=1,\ldots,k/2\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|Q_{1}\ldots Q_{k/2}|}\bigg)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{H}_{n}|\times\frac{k!}{(k/2)!2^{\frac{k}{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(Q_{i})\leq X\\
i=1,\ldots,k/2\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k/2}\frac{1}{(|Q_{i}|+1)|Q_{i}|^{2\sigma_{o}}}+O\left(|\mathcal{H}_{n}|^{-1}\frac{k!}{(k/2)!2^{\frac{k}{2}}}(\log X)^{\frac{k}{2}}\right).

Now, if we fix
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{\frac{k}{2}-1}
and vary over
Q_{k/2}
in the main term of the above expression. Then, the sum becomes

\sum_{d(Q_{k/2})\leq X}\frac{1}{(|Q_{k/2}|+1)|Q_{k/2}|^{2\sigma_{o}}}+O\left(\frac{k}{2}\right).

Applying Lemma
3.4
, we find that

\displaystyle s_{n}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{H}_{n}|\times\frac{k!}{(k/2)!2^{\frac{k}{2}}}\left(\log X+O(k)\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{H}_{n}|\times\frac{k!}{(k/2)!2^{\frac{k}{2}}}(\log X)^{\frac{k}{2}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{k}{\log X}\right)\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}.

Subcase 1.2
.

Let there are
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{r}
distinct primes with

d(Q_{1})\leq\ldots\leq d(Q_{k})

where
r<\frac{k}{2}
. Therefore, Lemma
3.4
gives us

\displaystyle s_{n}\leq

\displaystyle|\mathcal{H}_{n}|\times\sum_{r<\frac{k}{2}}\frac{k!}{r!2^{r}}\binom{k-r}{r}\bigg(\sum_{d(P)\leq X}\frac{1}{(|P|+1)|P|^{2\sigma_{o}}}\bigg)^{r}

\displaystyle+O\left(|\mathcal{H}_{n}|^{-1}\sum_{r<\frac{k}{2}}\frac{k!}{r!2^{r}}\binom{k-r}{r}(\log X)^{r}\right)

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{H}_{n}|\times\sum_{r<\frac{k}{2}}\frac{k!}{r!2^{r}}\binom{k-r}{r}\left(\log X+O(1)\right)^{r}+O\left(|\mathcal{H}_{n}|^{-1}\sum_{r<\frac{k}{2}}\frac{k!}{r!2^{r}}\binom{k-r}{r}(\log X)^{r}\right)

\displaystyle\ll|\mathcal{H}_{n}|\times\frac{k^{3}k!}{(k/2)!2^{k/2}\log X}(\log X)^{\frac{k}{2}}+O\left(|\mathcal{H}_{n}|^{-1}\frac{k^{3}k!}{(k/2)!2^{k/2}\log X}(\log X)^{\frac{k}{2}}\right).

Case 2
.

Let
k
be even and
P_{1}\ldots P_{k}\neq\square
.
Then we use Lemma
3.3
to get

s_{n}\ll\sqrt{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(P_{i})\leq X\\
i=1,\ldots,k\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|P_{1}\ldots P_{k}|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o}-\epsilon}}\ll\frac{q^{\frac{n}{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon-\sigma_{o}\right)Xk}}{X^{k}}.

Case 3
.

Let
k
be odd. In this case
P_{1}\ldots P_{k}\neq\square
.
In a similar manner in Case
2
, we come across the following

s_{n}\ll\frac{q^{\frac{n}{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon-\sigma_{o}\right)Xk}}{X^{k}}.

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose that the hypothesis in Lemma
5.2
holds. Uniformly for all odd natural numbers
k\ll\left(\frac{\log X}{(\log\log n)^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left(\widetilde{P}_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log X\right)^{k}\ll\frac{k!\,k^{\frac{3}{2}}(\log X)^{\frac{k}{2}}}{(k/2)!\,2^{\frac{k}{2}}}\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{(\log X)^{\frac{1}{2}}},

while, uniformly for all even natural numbers
k\ll\left(\frac{\log X}{(\log\log n)^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}

\displaystyle\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left(\widetilde{P}_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log X\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{k!}{(k/2)!2^{\frac{k}{2}}}(\log X)^{\frac{k}{2}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{k^{3}}{\log X}\right)+O\left(\frac{k^{\frac{3}{2}}(\log\log n)^{2}}{(\log X)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\right).

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

From Lemma
5.3
, the moments of
\widetilde{P}_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log X
exactly match the moments of a Gaussian random variable with mean
0
and variance
\log n
, and since the Gaussian is determined by its moments, our proposition follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We first use Proposition
4.1
to write
\log L(\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D})
in terms of
\widetilde{P}_{X}(\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D})
, we take real parts of the expression and then apply Proposition
5.1
with
X=\frac{n}{4k}
. From here we have the estimate for the main term when
k\ll\left(\frac{(\log n)}{(\log\log n)^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}
. Finally, we use Lemma
3.6
to show the error term coming from Proposition
4.1
is negligible.
∎

It remains to prove Lemma
5.3
.

Proof of Lemma
5.3
.

We write

\widetilde{P}_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)=\sum_{d(P)\leq X}\frac{\Lambda(P)\chi_{D}(P)}{d(P)|P|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o}}}+\sum_{d(P)\leq\frac{X}{2}}\frac{\Lambda(P^{2})\chi_{D}(P^{2})}{d(P^{2})|P|^{1+2\sigma_{o}}}+O\left(\sum_{d(P)\leq\frac{X}{3}}\frac{1}{|P|^{\frac{3}{2}+3\sigma_{o}}}\right).

It is clear that the third sum is
O(1)
. So the contribution of
P^{k}
,
k\geq 3
in
\widetilde{P}_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)
is
O(1)
. Therefore, Lemma
3.4
yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{d(P)\leq X}\frac{\chi_{D}(P)}{|P|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o}}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\nmid D\\
d(P)\leq\frac{X}{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|P|^{1+2\sigma_{0}}}+O(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d(P)\leq X}\frac{\chi_{D}(P)}{|P|^{\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o}}}+\frac{1}{2}\log X+O(\log\log n),

where the error term
O(\log\log n)
comes from the sum over
P
such that
P|D
.
Therefore,

\widetilde{P}_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log X=P_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)+O(\log\log n).

This implies that for some positive constant
c
,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left(\widetilde{P}_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{0},\chi_{D}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log X\right)^{k}=\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left(P_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\right)^{k}

\displaystyle+O\left(\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\sum_{r=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{r}(c\log\log n)^{k-r}\bigg|P_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\bigg|^{r}\right).

The first sum will be handled by Lemma
5.2
. To handle reminder term we need to estimate the sum

\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\bigg|P_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\bigg|^{r},\quad r\leq k-1.

If
r
is odd then Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
5.2
with even moments gives us

\displaystyle\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\bigg|P_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\bigg|^{r}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\bigg(P_{X}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\bigg)\bigg)^{r-1}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\bigg(\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\bigg(P_{X}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\bigg)\bigg)^{r+1}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\ll|\mathcal{H}_{n}|\left(\frac{(r-1)!\,(r+1)!}{\left(\frac{r-1}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{r+1}{2}\right)!\,2^{r}}\right)^{1/2}(\log X)^{r}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{H}_{n}|\frac{(r-1)!\,r^{1/2}}{\left(\frac{r-1}{2}\right)!\,2^{\frac{r-1}{2}}}(\log X)^{r}.

On the other hand, if
r
is even then directly use the asymptotic formula for even moments.
Finally, plugging these estimates with the Lemma
5.2
to the sum (
5.1
), we complete the proof.

∎

6.
The difference between
L
-functions and Proof of Theorem

Recall that: the parameter
\delta
as defined as in (
1.2
), the parameter
\sigma_{0}=\frac{c}{X}
, with
0<c<\frac{1}{2\log q}
. The following proposition gives the connection between the
L
-function at
\frac{1}{2}
and very close to
\frac{1}{2}
.
We define

\mathcal{H}_{n,0}=\left\{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n}:\mathcal{L}(q^{-\frac{1}{2}}e(\theta_{j,D}),\chi_{D})=0\Rightarrow\min_{j}|\theta_{j,D}|>\frac{1}{y\delta}\right\}.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
X\leq\frac{n}{4k}
but goes to infinity with

. Suppose that
\delta\,\sigma_{o}=o(\sqrt{\log\delta})
as

. Then, we have

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{n,0}}\Big|\log\Big|L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D}\right)\Big|-\log\Big|L\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\Big|\Big|=o\left(\sqrt{\log\delta}\right).

Remark
.

Notice that there is a huge difference between the quantities
\log\Big|L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D}\right)\Big|-\log\Big|L\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\Big|
and

. The first quantity is very small whereas the second one is quite large and this is the reason for summing over
\mathcal{H}_{n,0}
in the above Proposition.

Proof.

Recall that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,\chi_{D})=\log q\bigg(\frac{\lambda q^{-s}}{1-q^{-s}}-\delta+\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\Re\bigg(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{\frac{1}{2}-s}}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\bigg).

Since
D\in\mathcal{H}_{n,0}
, we have
L(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D})\neq 0
.
Then using the fact that (
6.1
) is real for

, we obtain

\displaystyle\log\left|L\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\right|-\log\left|L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D}\right)\right|=\int_{0}^{\sigma_{o}}\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma,\chi_{D}\right)d\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\sigma_{o}}\log q\bigg(\frac{\lambda q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma}}{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma}}-\delta+\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\Re\bigg(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma}}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\bigg)d\sigma

\displaystyle=\delta\sigma_{o}+\frac{\log q}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\Re\int_{0}^{\sigma_{o}}\frac{1+\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma}}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma}}d\sigma+\lambda\log q\log\left(\frac{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma_{0}}}{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}}\right)

(6.2)

\displaystyle=\delta\sigma_{o}\log q-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\log\left(\frac{q^{\sigma_{o}}+q^{-\sigma_{o}}-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})}{2-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})}\right)+\lambda\log q\log\left(\frac{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma_{0}}}{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}}\right).

We observe that

(6.3)

\displaystyle\Re\bigg(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma_{o}}}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)=\frac{1-q^{-2\sigma_{o}}}{2\left(1-q^{-\sigma_{o}}\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})+q^{-2\sigma_{o}}\right)}.

Now we use the inequality
\log(1+x)<x
, combined with the fact that
D\in\mathcal{H}_{n,0}
and (
6.3
) to get

\displaystyle\bigg|\log\frac{q^{\sigma_{o}}+q^{-\sigma_{o}}-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})}{2-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q^{\sigma_{o}}+q^{-\sigma_{o}}-2}{2-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})}\ll\frac{\sigma_{o}^{2}}{2-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{y\sigma_{o}(1-q^{-2\sigma_{o}})}{2-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})}\ll\frac{y\sigma_{o}(1-q^{-2\sigma_{o}})}{1-q^{-\sigma_{o}}\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})+q^{-2\sigma_{o}}}

\displaystyle\ll y\sigma_{o}\,\Re\bigg(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma_{o}}}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg).

On the real line, from (

), we see that

\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)=\log q\bigg(\frac{\lambda q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma_{0}}}{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma_{0}}}-\delta+\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\Re\bigg(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma_{o}}}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\bigg).

From this expression of the logarithmic derivative of

on the real line, we have

(6.4)

\displaystyle\bigg|\log\frac{q^{\sigma_{o}}+q^{-\sigma_{o}}-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})}{2-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})}\bigg|\ll y\sigma_{o}\,\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)+y\delta\sigma_{o}.

Using (

y\delta\sigma_{o}=o(\sqrt{\log_{q}\delta})\quad\text{ and }\quad\delta\sigma_{o}=o(\sqrt{\log_{q}\delta}).

Therefore, it is enough to prove

o(y^{2})

7.
Proof of Corollary
1.2

We use (
6.1
) to obtain

\displaystyle\log\Big|L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D}\right)\Big|-\log\Big|L\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\Big|=\Re\int_{0}^{\sigma_{o}}-\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma,\chi_{D}\right)d\sigma

\displaystyle=\Re\int_{0}^{\sigma_{o}}\log q\bigg(\frac{\lambda q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma}}{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma}}-\delta+\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\bigg(\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma}}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\bigg)d\sigma

\displaystyle=\delta\sigma_{o}\log q-\frac{\log q}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\Re\int_{0}^{\sigma_{o}}\frac{1+\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma}}{1-\alpha_{j}q^{-\sigma}}d\sigma+\lambda\log q\log\left(\frac{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma_{0}}}{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta\sigma_{o}\log q-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\log\frac{q^{\sigma_{o}}+q^{-\sigma_{o}}-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})}{2-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})}+\lambda\log q\log\left(\frac{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma_{0}}}{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta\sigma_{o}\log q-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2\delta}\log\bigg(1+\frac{q^{\sigma_{o}}+q^{-\sigma_{o}}-2}{2-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{j,D})}\bigg)+\lambda\log q\log\left(\frac{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma_{0}}}{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}}\right).

Note that the summands in the second term are all non-negative which leads the second term of the above expression to be a negative value. Therefore, we deduce that

\log\Big|L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D}\right)\Big|\leq\log\Big|L\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\Big|+\delta\sigma_{o}\log q+\lambda\log q\log\left(\frac{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma_{0}}}{1-q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}}\right).

From the Hypothesis that
\frac{\delta\sigma_{o}}{\sqrt{\log\delta}}=o(1)
, we obtain

\frac{\log\Big|L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{D}\right)\Big|}{\sqrt{\log\delta}}\leq\frac{\log\Big|L\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{o},\chi_{D}\right)\Big|}{\sqrt{\log\delta}}+o(1).

Hence, the theorem follows from the Theorem
1.1
.
