Families of Annihilating Skew-Selfadjoint Operators and their Connection to Hilbert Complexes
Dirk Pauly, Rainer Picard

Abstract
. In this short note we show that Hilbert complexes are strongly related to what we shall call annihilating sets of skew-selfadjoint operators. This provides for a new perspective on the classical topic of Hilbert complexes viewed as families of commuting normal operators.



1.
Introduction

The classical differential geometry topic of “chain complexes”
has entered functional analysis as the topic of so-called “Hilbert
complexes”. The purpose of this note is to inspect Hilbert complexes
from another functional analytical perspective linked to a four decades
old construction of the skew-selfadjoint extended Maxwell operator

S_{\mathsf{Dir}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&\operatorname{div}&0&0\\
\mathring{\operatorname{grad}}&0&-\operatorname{curl}&0\\
0&\mathring{\operatorname{curl}}&0&\operatorname{grad}\\
0&0&\mathring{\operatorname{div}}&0\end{array}\right),

see
[
13
,
14
]
. For some pre-history and the scope of this
construction see
[
15
]
. We merely mention here that the
extended Maxwell system provides not only a deeper structural insight
into the system of Maxwell’s equations but also shows a deep connection
to the Dirac equation. Indeed, the extended Maxwell operator has proven
to be useful in important applications such as boundary integral equations
in electrodynamics at low frequencies, see e.g.
[
5
]
and

[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.

As it will turn out Hilbert complexes are intimately related to, indeed
generalized by, an abstract concept, which we shall refer to as annihilating
sets of skew-selfadjoint operators, which in turn is based on observations
made in connection with the extended Maxwell system. This is the subject
of the main part in Section
2
.
Our final section, Section
3
serves to illustrate
the abstract setting by a number of more or less classical applications.

2.
Annihilating Sets of
Skew-Selfadjoint Operators and Hilbert Complexes

2.1.
Finite Sets of Annihilating Skew-Selfadjoint Operators

We start with particular finite sets of commuting skew-selfadjoint
operators
S
on a Hilbert space
H
, i.e.,

S:\operatorname{dom}(S)\subset H\to H,\quad S^{*}=-S,

which we shall refer to as a – pair-wise – annihilating set of skew-selfadjoint
operators.

Definition 1
.

A finite set
\mathscr{S}
of skew-selfadjoint operators satisfying

\operatorname{ran}\left(S\right)\subseteq\ker\left(T\right),\quad S\not=T,\>S,T\in\mathscr{S},

is called an annihilating set of skew-selfadjoint operators.

For the rest of this section, let
\mathscr{S}
be an annihilating set
of skew-selfadjoint operators.

Remark 2
.

Let
S,T\in\mathscr{S}
.

(1)

\mathscr{S}
is a set of commuting operators.

(2)

It holds
TS=0
on
\operatorname{dom}\left(S\right)
for
S\not=T
.

(3)

The observation that
S=f_{S}\left(Q\right)
for some suitable complex
valued functions
f_{S}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
in the sense of a function
calculus associated with a single selfadjoint operator
Q
provides
for other examples, which are not necessarily tridiagonal, cf. Section
2.2
.

As a consequence we have a straight-forward application of the projection
theorem the following generalized (orthogonal) Helmholtz decomposition.

Theorem 3
.

{\displaystyle H=K\oplus_{H}\bigoplus_{S\in\mathscr{S}}\overline{\operatorname{ran}\left(S\right)}}

with (generalised cohomology group)
{\displaystyle K\coloneqq\bigcap_{S\in\mathscr{S}}\ker\left(S\right)}
.

2.2.
A Special Case: Tridiagonal Operator Matrices

We consider operator matrices of the form

\displaystyle A\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
a_{1}&0&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0&0\\
0&\cdots&0&a_{N}&0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}0&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&&&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0&&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&0&0&0&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&a_{k}&0&0&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&0\end{array}\right)

closed and densely defined on a Cartesian product
H=H_{1}\times\cdots\times H_{N+1}

of Hilbert spaces
H_{k}
. Then

\displaystyle A^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&a_{1}^{*}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&0&a_{N}^{*}\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle A_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}0&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&&&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0&&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&0&0&a_{k}^{*}&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&0&0&0&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&0\end{array}\right).

Now let

S\coloneqq 2\operatorname{skw}A=A-A^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}S_{k},\qquad S_{k}\coloneqq 2\operatorname{skw}A_{k}=A_{k}-A_{k}^{*}.

Then

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&-a_{1}^{*}&0&\cdots&0\\
a_{1}&0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0&-a_{N}^{*}\\
0&\cdots&0&a_{N}&0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle S_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}0&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&&&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0&&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&0&0&-a_{k}^{*}&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&a_{k}&0&0&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&0\end{array}\right)

are skew-selfadjoint
and we have the following main result.

Theorem 4
.

\mathscr{S}:=\left\{S_{1},\dots,S_{N}\right\}

is an annihilating set of skew-selfadjoint operators if and only if

(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})
is a Hilbert complex, i.e., for all

k=1,\dots,N

a_{k}:\operatorname{dom}\left(a_{k}\right)\subseteq H_{k}\to H_{k+1}

are closed and densely defined linear operators satisfying
\operatorname{ran}\left(a_{k}\right)\subseteq\ker\left(a_{k+1}\right)
,

k=1,\ldots,N-1
.

Proof.

The result follows by a straightforward calculation. See Appendix

A
for more details.
∎

Remark 5
.

As
(S_{k}S_{\ell})^{*}\supset S_{\ell}^{*}S_{k}^{*}=S_{\ell}S_{k}

we see that
\mathscr{S}=\left\{S_{1},\dots,S_{N}\right\}
is an annihilating
set of skew-selfadjoint operators if and only if
S_{k}S_{\ell}=0

for all
1\leq k<\ell\leq N

(if and only if
S_{\ell}S_{k}=0
for all
1\leq k<\ell\leq N
).

Remark 6
.

Often a Hilbert complex
(a)\coloneqq\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{N}\right)

is written as

H_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{a_{k-1}}H_{k}\xrightarrow{a_{k}}H_{k+1}\xrightarrow{a_{k+1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{a_{N}}H_{N+1}.

It is noteworthy that the Hilbert complex
\left(a\right)
is equivalently
turned into the property

\displaystyle\operatorname{ran}\left(A_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\subseteq\ker\left(A_{j}\right)

or

\displaystyle\operatorname{ran}\left(S_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\subseteq\ker\left(S_{j}\right),\quad j\neq k,

for all
j,k=1,\ldots,N
where the sequential character of Hilbert
complexes seems to have disappeared. Theorem
4

suggests to consider annihilating sets of skew-selfadjoint operators
as an appropriate generalization of Hilbert complexes.

Remark 7
.

Note that, if preferred, the set
\mathscr{S}
may be considered as

(1)

a set of homomorphisms by restriction of the elements to their respective
domains, i.e.,

\mathscr{S}_{\mathsf{hom}}\coloneqq\left\{\widetilde{S}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{S}_{N}\right\},

where
\widetilde{S}_{k}\coloneqq S_{k}\iota_{\operatorname{dom}\left(S_{k}\right)}:\operatorname{dom}(S_{k})\to H

are now bounded linear operators,

(2)

a set of bounded isomorphisms by restriction of the elements to their
respective domains and orthogonal complements of their kernels (and
projections onto the ranges), i.e.,

\mathscr{S}_{\mathsf{iso}}\coloneqq\left\{\widehat{S}_{1},\dots,\widehat{S}_{N}\right\},

where
\widehat{S}_{k}\coloneqq\iota_{\operatorname{ran}\left(S_{k}\right)}^{*}S_{k}\iota_{\operatorname{dom}\left(S_{k}\right)\cap\ker\left(S_{k}\right)^{\bot_{H}}}:\operatorname{dom}\left(S_{k}\right)\cap\ker\left(S_{k}\right)^{\bot_{H}}\to\operatorname{ran}(S_{k})

are now bounded and bijective,

(3)

a set of topological isomorphisms
\mathscr{S}_{\mathsf{iso}}
if all
ranges
\operatorname{ran}(S_{k})
are closed. Note that in this case we have
\operatorname{ran}(S_{k})=\ker\left(S_{k}\right)^{\bot_{H}}

and that
\operatorname{ran}(S_{k})
is closed if and only if
\operatorname{ran}(a_{k})
is
closed.

In the latter remark
\iota_{X}
denotes the embedding of the subspace

X
into
H
. If
X
is closed in
H
the orthonormal projector
onto
X
is given by
\pi_{X}:=\iota_{X}\iota_{X}^{*}:H\to H
.

Remark 8
.

For consistency we set
a_{0}:=0
and
a_{N+1}:=0
.
Note that

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}(S)

\displaystyle=\bigtimes_{k=1}^{N+1}\left(\operatorname{dom}(a_{k})\cap\operatorname{dom}(a_{k-1}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dom}(a_{1})\times\left(\operatorname{dom}(a_{2})\cap\operatorname{dom}(a_{1}^{*})\right)\times\cdots\times\left(\operatorname{dom}(a_{N})\cap\operatorname{dom}(a_{N-1}^{*})\right)\times\operatorname{dom}(a_{N}^{*})

and that by the complex property

\displaystyle\ker(S)

\displaystyle=\bigtimes_{k=1}^{N+1}\left(\ker(a_{k})\cap\ker(a_{k-1}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle=\ker(a_{1})\times\left(\ker(a_{2})\cap\ker(a_{1}^{*})\right)\times\cdots\times\left(\ker(a_{N})\cap\ker(a_{N-1}^{*})\right)\times\ker(a_{N}^{*}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{ran}(S)

\displaystyle=\bigtimes_{k=1}^{N+1}\left(\operatorname{ran}(a_{k-1})\oplus_{H_{k}}\operatorname{ran}(a_{k}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ran}(a_{1}^{*})\times\left(\operatorname{ran}(a_{1})\oplus_{H_{2}}\operatorname{ran}(a_{2}^{*})\right)\times\cdots\times\left(\operatorname{ran}(a_{N-1})\oplus_{H_{N}}\operatorname{ran}(a_{N}^{*})\right)\times\operatorname{ran}(a_{N}).

In particular, the product of the cohomology groups
K_{k}\coloneqq\ker(a_{k})\cap\ker(a_{k-1}^{*})

equals the kernel of
S
.

Definition 9
.

Recall Remark
6
. A Hilbert complex
(a)

is called

(1)

closed if all ranges
\operatorname{ran}(a_{k})
are closed.

(2)

compact if all embeddings
\operatorname{dom}(a_{k})\cap\operatorname{dom}(a_{k-1}^{*})\hookrightarrow H_{k}

are compact.

Theorem 10
.

Recall Theorem
4
.
Let
(a)

be a Hilbert complex with associated annihilating set of skew-selfadjoint
operators
\mathscr{S}
. Then
(a)
is

(1)

closed if and only if
\operatorname{ran}(S)
is closed.

(2)

compact if and only if the embedding
\operatorname{dom}(S)\hookrightarrow H
is
compact.

Proof.

Use Remark
8
and orthogonality.
∎

Remark 12
.

By replacing the skew-selfadjoint operators with selfadjoint operators
the presented theory works literally as well. The only modifications
are

S\coloneqq 2\operatorname{sym}A=A+A^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}S_{k},\qquad S_{k}\coloneqq 2\operatorname{sym}A_{k}=A_{k}+A_{k}^{*}

resulting in

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&a_{1}^{*}&0&\cdots&0\\
a_{1}&0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0&a_{N}^{*}\\
0&\cdots&0&a_{N}&0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle S_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}0&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&&&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0&&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&0&0&a_{k}^{*}&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&a_{k}&0&0&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&0\end{array}\right).

This is in a sense a matter of taste. We prefer, however, the skew-selfadjoint
setting, since it has the advantage of being closer to various applications,
such as Maxwell’s and Dirac’s equation (written in real form). We
note, in particular, that skew-selfadjointness is at the heart of
energy conservation.

Remark 13
.

By standard arguments of linear functional analysis we note the following results:
Let
S:\operatorname{dom}(S)\subset H\to H
be skew-selfadjoint
such that
\operatorname{dom}(S)\hookrightarrow H
is compact. Then

(1)

the range
\operatorname{ran}(S)=\operatorname{ran}(\widehat{S})
is closed,
where
\widehat{S}=\iota_{\operatorname{ran}(S)}^{*}S\iota_{\operatorname{ran}(S)}
.

(2)

the inverse operator
\widehat{S}^{-1}
is compact.

(3)

the cohomology group
\ker(S)
has finite dimension.

(4)

the orthogonal Helmholtz-type decomposition

H=\operatorname{ran}(S)\oplus_{H}\ker(S)
holds.

(5)

there exists
c>0
such that for all

x\in\operatorname{dom}\big(\widehat{S}\big)=\operatorname{dom}(S)\cap\operatorname{ran}(S)=\operatorname{dom}(S)\cap\ker(S)^{\bot_{H}}

the Friedrichs/Poincaré type inequality

\|x\|_{H}\leq c\|Sx\|_{H}

holds.

(6)

S
and
\widehat{S}
are Fredholm operators with index zero.

3.
Applications

In this final section we give several examples of annihilating sets
of skew-selfadjoint operators, i.e., of Hilbert complexes, cf. Theorem

4
. All operators will be considered as closures
of unbounded linear operators densely defined on smooth and compactly
supported test fields. For example,
\mathring{\operatorname{grad}}
,
\mathring{\operatorname{sym}\operatorname{Curl}}_{\mathbb{T}}
,
and
\mathring{\operatorname{div}\operatorname{Div}}_{\mathbb{S}}
– where the tiny circle on top of
an operator indicates the full Dirichlet boundary condition associated
to the respective differential operator – are the closures of

\displaystyle\mathring{\operatorname{grad}}^{\infty}:\mathring{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(\Omega);

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\mapsto\operatorname{grad}u,

\displaystyle\mathring{\operatorname{sym}\operatorname{Curl}}_{\mathbb{T}}^{\infty}:\mathring{C}_{\mathbb{T}}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subset L_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to L_{\mathbb{S}}^{2}(\Omega);

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\mapsto\operatorname{sym}\operatorname{Curl}M,

\displaystyle\mathring{\operatorname{div}\operatorname{Div}}_{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}:\mathring{C}_{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subset L_{\mathbb{S}}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(\Omega);

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\mapsto\operatorname{div}\operatorname{Div}M,

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an open set,
\mathring{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)

denotes the space of smooth and compactly supported fields in
\Omega
,
and
\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{T}
indicate symmetric and deviatoric tensor fields,
respectively. The corresponding adjoints
-\operatorname{div}
,
\operatorname{Curl}_{\mathbb{S}}
, and

\operatorname{Grad}\operatorname{grad}
are then given by

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}:H(\operatorname{div},\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(\Omega);

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\mapsto-\operatorname{div}E,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Curl}_{\mathbb{S}}:H_{\mathbb{S}}(\operatorname{Curl},\Omega)\subset L_{\mathbb{S}}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to L_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}(\Omega);

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\mapsto\operatorname{Curl}M,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Grad}\operatorname{grad}:H^{2}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to L_{\mathbb{S}}^{2}(\Omega);

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\mapsto\operatorname{Grad}\operatorname{grad}u.

3.1.
The Classical de Rham Complexes

3.1.1.
De Rham Complex of Vector Fields

Let
\Omega
be an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
.
The most prominent example is the classical de Rham complex of vector
fields involving the classical operators of vector calculus
\operatorname{grad}
,

\operatorname{curl}
, and
\operatorname{div}
with full Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions:

S_{\mathsf{Dir}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&\operatorname{div}&0&0\\
\mathring{\operatorname{grad}}&0&-\operatorname{curl}&0\\
0&\mathring{\operatorname{curl}}&0&\operatorname{grad}\\
0&0&\mathring{\operatorname{div}}&0\end{array}\right),\qquad S_{\mathsf{Neu}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&\mathring{\operatorname{div}}&0&0\\
\operatorname{grad}&0&-\mathring{\operatorname{curl}}&0\\
0&\operatorname{curl}&0&\mathring{\operatorname{grad}}\\
0&0&\operatorname{div}&0\end{array}\right)

Inhomogeneous and anisotropic coefficients and mixed boundary conditions
can also be considered:

\displaystyle S_{\mathsf{mix}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&\nu^{-1}\mathring{\operatorname{div}}_{\Gamma_{\mathsf{1}}}\varepsilon&0&0\\
\mathring{\operatorname{grad}}_{\Gamma_{0}}&0&-\varepsilon^{-1}\mathring{\operatorname{curl}}_{\Gamma_{1}}&0\\
0&\mu^{-1}\mathring{\operatorname{curl}}_{\Gamma_{0}}&0&\mathring{\operatorname{grad}}_{\Gamma_{1}}\\
0&0&\kappa^{-1}\mathring{\operatorname{div}}_{\Gamma_{0}}\mu&0\end{array}\right)

Here the boundary
\Gamma
is decomposed into two parts
\Gamma_{0}

and
\Gamma_{1}
where the Dirichlet and Neumann boundary condition
is imposed, respectively. Note that the de Rham off-diagonals are
skew-adjoint to each other.

3.1.2.
De Rham Complex of Differential Forms

Let
\Omega
be an
N
-dimensional Riemannian manifold, e.g., an
open set in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Another prominent example is the classical
de Rham complex of differential forms involving the exterior derivative

\operatorname{d}
and its formal skew-adjoint the co-derivative
\operatorname{\delta}=-\mathring{\operatorname{d}}^{*}
,

\operatorname{d}=-\mathring{\operatorname{\delta}}^{*}
with full Dirichlet or Neumann boundary
conditions:

S_{\mathsf{Dir}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&\operatorname{\delta}&0&\cdots&0\\
\mathring{\operatorname{d}}&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\operatorname{\delta}\\
0&\cdots&0&\mathring{\operatorname{d}}&0\end{array}\right),\qquad S_{\mathsf{Neu}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&\mathring{\operatorname{\delta}}&0&\cdots&0\\
\operatorname{d}&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\mathring{\operatorname{\delta}}\\
0&\cdots&0&\operatorname{d}&0\end{array}\right)

Again, the de Rham off-diagonals are skew-adjoint to each other. Note
that
S_{\mathsf{Dir}}
and
S_{\mathsf{Neu}}
are unitarily congruent
via transposition, permutation, sign change and Hodge-
*
-isomorphism.

3.4.
A Factorization Result

An interesting consequence for annihilating sets of skew-selfadjoint
operators (not necessarily tridiagonal operator matrices as in the
special case) is the following factorization result: Let
T
be a
strictly m-accretive operator commuting with
\mathscr{S}=\left\{S_{1},\dots,S_{N}\right\}
.
Then with
S=\sum_{k=1}^{N}S_{k}
we have

\left(T+S\right)=T^{1-N}T^{N-1}\Big(T+\sum_{k=1}^{N}S_{k}\Big)=T^{1-N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left(T+S_{k}\right)

and conversely,

\displaystyle T+S_{\ell}

\displaystyle=T^{N-1}\prod_{\ell\neq k=1}^{N}\left(T+S_{k}\right)^{-1}\left(T+S\right)=\prod_{\ell\neq k=1}^{N}\left(1+T^{-1}S_{k}\right)^{-1}\left(T+S\right).

This is, in terms of inverses (solution operators)

\displaystyle\left(T+S\right)^{-1}=T^{N-1}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left(T+S_{k}\right)^{-1},\qquad\left(T+S_{\ell}\right)^{-1}=\prod_{\ell\neq k=1}^{N}\left(1+T^{-1}S_{k}\right)\left(T+S\right)^{-1}.

An example of particular interest is given by
T=\partial_{t}
, the
case of evolutionary systems, which in a suitable setting, see e.g.

[
15
]
, leads to

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+S)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}^{1-N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}(\partial_{t}+S_{k}),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}+S_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\ell\neq k=1}^{N}(1+\partial_{t}^{-1}S_{k})^{-1}(\partial_{t}+S),

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+S)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}^{N-1}\prod_{k=1}^{N}(\partial_{t}+S_{k})^{-1},

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+S_{\ell})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\ell\neq k=1}^{N}(1+\partial_{t}^{-1}S_{k})(\partial_{t}+S)^{-1}.
