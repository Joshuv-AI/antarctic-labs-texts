Projected Tensor-Tensor Products for Efficient Computation of Optimal Multiway Data Representations Journal: Linear Algebra and its Applications
Katherine Keegan, Elizabeth Newman

Abstract
Tensor decompositions have become essential tools for feature extraction and compression of multiway data. Recent advances in tensor operators have enabled desirable properties of standard matrix algebra to be retained for multilinear factorizations. Behind this matrix-mimetic tensor operation is an invertible matrix whose size depends quadratically on certain dimensions of the data. As a result, for large-scale multiway data, the invertible matrix can be computationally demanding to apply and invert and can lead to inefficient tensor representations in terms of construction and storage costs. In this work, we propose a new projected tensor-tensor product that relaxes the invertibility restriction to reduce computational overhead and still preserves fundamental linear algebraic properties. The transformation behind the projected product is a tall-and-skinny matrix with unitary columns, which depends only linearly on certain dimensions of the data, thereby reducing computational complexity by an order of magnitude. We provide extensive theory to prove the matrix mimeticity and the optimality of compressed representations within the projected product framework. We further prove that projected-product-based approximations outperform a comparable, non-matrix-mimetic tensor factorization. We support the theoretical findings and demonstrate the practical benefits of projected products through numerical experiments on video and hyperspectral imaging data. All code for this paper is available at https://github.com/elizabethnewman/projected-products.git .

1.1
Our Contributions

In this work, we introduce a new projected tensor-tensor product as a practical relaxation of the original
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product.
Our new tensor-tensor product is defined by a matrix
\mathbf{Q}
with unitary columns that is not necessarily invertible.
This choice of
\mathbf{Q}
reduces the computational complexity and representation storage costs by an order of magnitude based on the size of the multiway data. Our contributions include developing new and extensive theoretical foundations for projected products, including proofs that the projected product is matrix mimetic. Notably, we achieve Eckart-Young optimality results under the projected product algebra and provide insight into an optimal choice of projected product matrix
\mathbf{Q}
. We further prove that representations under the projected product yield better approximations than the higher-order SVD (HOSVD)
[
7
]
. Our numerical experiments provide strong empirical support of our theoretical results and demonstrate the ability of the projected product representations to approximate multiway data well with significant storage reduction. For transparency and reproducibility, we provide our an open-source at
https://github.com/elizabethnewman/projected-products.git
.

1.2
Related Work

Other non-invertible tensor-tensor products been proposed for the specific application of tensor completion via tensor nuclear norm minimization
[
37
]
. In
[
12
]
, the authors introduce a framelet transform as an alternative to the fast Fourier transform (
fft
). aSimilar to the
fft
, framelet transforms can be implemented implicitly and efficiently with little additional storage overhead. However, the proposed framelet transformation increases the dimensions of the tensor during application, resulting in greater computational cost within the tensor completion algorithm. Similarly, in
[
13
]
, the authors propose a dictionary-based transformation to ideally produce sparse tensor representations. However, the dictionary must be stored as an overcomplete matrix, which ultimately increases the computational and storage costs if the solution is insufficiently sparse. In
[
21
]
, the authors propose learning a data-dependent transformation with orthonormal columns (semi-orthogonal) as a subproblem of tensor nuclear norm minimization. The paper focuses on developing two strategies to optimize the matrix, variance maximization and manifold optimization, without developing nor leveraging algebraic properties that the underlying product induces. The work in
[
22
]
extends from
[
21
]
by applying a semi-orthogonal matrix followed by pointwise nonlinearity and learning the matrix through an alternating minimization strategy. The nonlinearity is generalized further in
[
23
]
, which trains a multi-layer neural network as the transformation. This design introduces new flexibility to the tensor-tensor product, but also potentially increases storage, computation, and training costs.

While the works of the above papers present practical advancements of non-invertible tensor-tensor products for tensor completion, none provide insight into the the algebraic implications of an underlying non-invertible transformation. Our work develops a unified algebraic framework for tensor algebras defined by real- or complex-valued matrices with unitary columns, introduces new theory about the optimality of the tensor representations under these non-invertible products, and extends the types of applications to which this projected product can be applied.

3
Projected Tensor-Tensor Products

A major restriction of the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product is that the transformation matrix
\mathbf{M}
has to be invertible. While this yields algebraic advantages, the computational and storage costs of resulting representations can, in some cases, be dominated by
\mathbf{M}
. We introduce a new projected tensor-tensor product that significantly reduces the computational overhead of applying and storing the transformation. Our key modification is to consider transformation matrices
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{3}\times p}
that have unitary columns, but are not necessarily invertible. In the language of manifolds, we say
\mathbf{Q}
belongs to the Stiefel manifold over the complex numbers; that is,
\mathbf{Q}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})
where
\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})=\{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{3}\times p}\mid\mathbf{X}^{H}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{I}_{p}\}
.

3.1
Special Considerations for Projected Products

Relaxing the invertibility restriction does have some notable consequences for the uniqueness of algebraic properties. For example, consider the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-identity tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}
where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}}_{:,:,i}=\mathbf{I}_{m}
for
i=1,\dots,p
. Because
\mathbf{Q}
is not invertible, there are infinitely many tensors that are equivalent to the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-identity tensor. Specifically, any tensor of the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times_{3}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})

(3.5)

will be an identity tensor. The tubes of the second term lie in the null space of
\mathbf{Q}^{H}
, and hence become zero in the transform domain; that is,
[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times_{3}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}=\bf 0
.

A similar lack of uniqueness can be found for the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-conjugate transpose. For example, if
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times_{3}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})
, then
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{H}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{H}
, but
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{H})^{H}\not=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
. Thus, the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-conjugate transpose is not injective. While such nuances of the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-product sacrifice some uniqueness properties of the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product, the core algebraic concepts are preserved.

We note another subtle difference between the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
- and
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-products for the
t
-product specifically. The
t
-product uses
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{F}
, the discrete Fourier transform, which consists of entries based on the complex roots of unity. Conveniently, if
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
are real-valued tensors,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
will also be real-valued. However, under the projected product with
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{F}_{1:p,:}^{H}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
could be complex-valued. While this is not problematic theoretically, this is an important consideration in practice, particularly when considering storage costs of complex numbers.

3.2
Equivalent Presentations of Projected Products

There are several equivalent ways to connect projected products with the original
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product. First, if
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{Q}^{H}
, then the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
- and
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-products are equal. More generally, let
\mathbf{M}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},n_{3}}(\mathbb{C})
and let
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{M}_{1:p,:}^{H}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}=\mathbf{M}_{p+1:n_{3},:}^{H}
; alternatively, we write
\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}&\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}^{H}
. Note that we can always reorder the rows of
\mathbf{M}
to obtain this partition because of the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product invariance to row permutations
[
27
, Theorem 2.2]
.

We first connect the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product with the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-product through projections via

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\equiv(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\star_{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}).

(3.6)

In essence, the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-product is equal to the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product after orthogonally-projecting the tensor tubes onto the column space of
\mathbf{Q}
. A similar observation can be made for the remaining columns of
\mathbf{M}
using the projection
\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}^{H}=\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}
.

A related perspective comes from recognizing that any tensor can be decomposed as the sum

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\times_{3}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}).

(3.7)

The
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product can thus be expressed as a projected product plus an error term; that is,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}+(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})\times_{3}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}).

(3.8)

The tubes of the second term lie in the null space of
\mathbf{Q}^{H}
. Alternatively, because the second term lies in the column space of
\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}
, we can express the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product as the sum of two projected products

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}.

(3.9)

There is a subtlety to this definition. In the spatial domain, the two tensors are the same size and summable. The
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
- and
\star_{\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}^{H}}^{\prime}
-transformed tensors may have a different number of frontal slices. Collectively, those slices form all frontal slices in the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-transform domain; that is,

\displaystyle\widehat{(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})}_{:,:,1:p}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})\times_{3}\mathbf{M}_{1:p,:}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}

\displaystyle=\widehat{(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})}

(3.10a)

\displaystyle\widehat{(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})}_{:,:,p+1:n_{3}}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})\times_{3}\mathbf{M}_{p+1:n_{3},:}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}^{H}

\displaystyle=\widehat{(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})}

(3.10b)

Consequently, frontal slices of the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
- and
\star_{\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}^{H}}^{\prime}
-products are zeroed out in the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-transform domain; i.e.,

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})\times_{3}\mathbf{M}_{p+1:n_{3},:}={\bf 0}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})\times_{3}\mathbf{M}_{1:n_{3},:}={\bf 0}.

(3.11)

For concreteness, we provide an example of the various projected product perspectives in
Appendix

A
.

3.3
Algebraic Properties of Projected Products

We present the core algebraic properties of projected products for tubal multiplication: commutativity, associativity, and distributivity. We also prove that transposition over the projected product of tensors follows the matrix definitions.

Theorem 3.1:
Commutivity of the Projected Product for Tubes

Given
\mathbf{Q}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})
, the projected product of tubes is commutative; that is, for any tubes
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
, we have
\mathbf{a}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{a}.

Proof.

By
Definition

3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=[\widehat{\mathbf{a}}\odot\widehat{\mathbf{b}}]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}=[\widehat{\mathbf{b}}\odot\widehat{\mathbf{a}}]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{b}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{a}

(3.12)

where
\odot
is the Hadamard pointwise product, which itself is commutative.
∎

Theorem 3.2:
Algebraic Properties of the Projected Product

Given
\mathbf{Q}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})
and arbitrary tubes
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
, the projected product is

1.

associative, i.e.,
(\mathbf{a}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{b})\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{a}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}(\mathbf{b}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{c})
and

2.

distributive over addition, i.e.,
(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{a}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{b}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{c}
.

Proof.

We complete the proof using
Definition

3.1
.

1.

Associativity:
We expand the product as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\mathbf{a}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{b})\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{c}&=[([(\widehat{\mathbf{a}}\odot\widehat{\mathbf{b}})\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\odot\widehat{\mathbf{c}}]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\end{split}

(3.13)

We then combine the mode-
3
products by

\displaystyle\begin{split}[([(\widehat{\mathbf{a}}\odot\widehat{\mathbf{b}})\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\odot\widehat{\mathbf{c}}]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}&=[[(\widehat{\mathbf{a}}\odot\widehat{\mathbf{b}})\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}\mathbf{Q}]\odot\widehat{\mathbf{c}}]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\\
&=[(\widehat{\mathbf{a}}\odot\widehat{\mathbf{b}})\odot\widehat{\mathbf{c}}]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}.\end{split}

(3.14)

Using the associativity of the Hadamard product and reversing the steps completes the proof.

2.

Distributivity:
We expand the left-hand side as follows:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle=[((\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\odot(\mathbf{c}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H})]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}.

(3.15)

Using the distributivity of the mode-
3
and Hadamard products, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}[((\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\odot(\mathbf{c}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H})]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}.&=[(\widehat{\mathbf{a}}+\widehat{\mathbf{b}})\odot\widehat{\mathbf{c}}]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\\
&=[\widehat{\mathbf{a}}\odot\widehat{\mathbf{c}}+\widehat{\mathbf{b}}\odot\widehat{\mathbf{c}}]\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}.\end{split}

(3.16)

Using the distributivity of the mode-
3
product again completes the proof.
Distribution holds from the left as well following
Section

3.3
.

Because the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-product is built on tubal multiplication (see (
2.5
)), associativity and distributivity extend to multiplying compatibly-sized tensors as well.

∎

Theorem 3.3:
Transposition of the Projected Product

Given
\mathbf{Q}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times m\times n_{3}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
, we have
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})^{H}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{H}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{H}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}&\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}^{H}
be a unitary matrix.
Then, by (
3.6
), we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\star_{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}).

(3.17)

Using the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product definition (
Definition

3.3
), we have

\displaystyle[(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\star_{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})]^{H}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})^{H}\star_{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})^{H}

(3.18a)

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{H}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\star_{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{H}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})

(3.18b)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{H}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{H}.

(3.18c)

A subtlety in (
3.18b
) is that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{H}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{H}
are the well-defined
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-conjugate transpose
[
19
, Definition 2.1 ]
.
∎

A similar theorem can be stated for the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-inverse of the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-product of two tensors.
We have demonstrated that the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-framework preserves algebraic identities despite the lack of injectivity of certain
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-operations.
As a result, we consider the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-product is to be matrix mimetic.

3.4
Generalizing the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-product

All definitions and theoretical results extend to nonzero multiples of matrices with unitary columns; that is, for
\mathbf{W}=c\mathbf{Q}
where
\mathbf{Q}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})
and
c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}

In this case, we would define
\star_{\mathbf{W}^{H}}^{\prime}
-product using the pseudoinverse and obtain the following relationship:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{W}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=[(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{W}^{H})\smalltriangleup(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\times_{3}\mathbf{W}^{H})]\times_{3}(\mathbf{W}^{H})^{\dagger}

(3.19a)

\displaystyle=[(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}(c\mathbf{W}^{H})\smalltriangleup(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\times_{3}(c\mathbf{W})^{H})]\times_{3}(\tfrac{1}{c}\mathbf{W})

(3.19b)

\displaystyle=c(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}).

(3.19c)

Because
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{W}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
is a scalar multiple of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}

Definition 4.1

(
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD)
.

Given
\mathbf{Q}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})
and tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
, the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{H}

(4.1)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
are
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-unitary and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
is f-diagonal with

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{1,1,:}\|_{F}\geq\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{2,2,:}\|_{F}\cdots\geq\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{q,q,:}\|_{F}\geq 0\qquad\text{for $q=\min(n_{1},n_{2})$.}

(4.2)

We present the pseudocode to compute the (truncated)
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD, including the computational costs for dense matrix operations from
[
9
, Section 1.4.1]
. Internally, the algorithm relies on the matrix SVD, hence the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD exists for all tensors.

Algorithm 4.1:
Truncated
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD

1:

Inputs:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
,
\mathbf{Q}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})
, truncation parameter
k\in\{1,\dots,\min(n_{1},n_{2})\}

2:

Move to the transform domain
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}

\triangleright

\mathcal{O}(n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}p)

3:

Compute truncated matrix SVDs of frontal slices
\triangleright

\mathcal{O}(n_{1}n_{2}kp)

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{:,:,i}\approx\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{:,1:k,i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}_{1:k,1:k,i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}}_{:,1:k,i}^{H}\qquad\text{for $i=1,\dots,p$.}

4:

Return to the spatial domain
\triangleright

\mathcal{O}((n_{1}k+k^{2}+kn_{2})n_{3}p)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{k}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{k}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{k}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}_{k}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q},\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{k}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}}_{k}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}.

5:

Return:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{k}

The
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD is less expensive computationally and storage-wise than the original
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-SVD (or

-SVDM in
[
19

p/n_{3}
; see

The
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD gives rise to a notion of tensor rank consistent with that of matrix rank, traditionally called

-literature.

Definition 4.2

(
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-rank)
.

Given a tensor and its
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\approx\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{H}
, the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-rank of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
is the number of nonzero tubes in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
; that is,

\displaystyle\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\text{-rank}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})=\#\{i\mid\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{i,i,:}\|_{F}>0\text{ for }i=1,\dots,n_{3}\}

(4.3)

where
\#
denotes the cardinality of the set.

Using
\mathbf{M}
from
Section

3.2
the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-SVD from
[
19
]
and the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD are share information. Specifically, in the transform domain, the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD factors are equal to the first
p
frontal slice factors of the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-SVD. We can thus connect the two notions of
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-rank and
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-rank.

Theorem 4.1:
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-rank
\leq

\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-rank

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},n_{3}}(\mathbb{C})
and
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{M}_{1:p,:}^{H}
. Then,
\projtrank(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})\leq\trank(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})
for any tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
.

Proof.

An equivalent definition of
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-rank (
Definition

4.2
) is the maximum rank of the frontal slices in the transform domain; that is,

\displaystyle\trank(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle=\max_{i=1,\dots,n_{3}}\rank(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{M}_{i,:})

(4.4a)

\displaystyle\projtrank(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle=\max_{i=1,\dots,p}\rank(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}_{:,i}^{H})

(4.4b)

Because
\mathbf{Q}_{:,i}^{H}=\mathbf{M}_{i,:}
for
i=1,\dots,p
, the first
p
frontal slices of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{M}
are equal to the
p
frontal slices of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}
. Thus,
\projtrank(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})
can be no larger than
\trank(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})
.

∎

While the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD is not equal to the original tensor; i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\not=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{H}
, it still
satisfies an Eckart-Young-like optimality result.

Theorem 4.2:
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-Eckart-Young Optimality

Given
\mathbf{Q}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})
and
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\approx\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{H}
with
\projtrank(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})=r
, the best
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-rank-
k
approximation to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
for
k\leq r
is given by the truncated
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD; that is,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\equiv\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{:,1:k,:}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{1:k,1:k,:}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{:,1:k,:}^{H}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{\prime}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\|_{F}

(4.5)

where
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{\prime}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{H}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times k\times n_{3}},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}\times k\times n_{3}}\}
. The Frobenius norm error is

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\|_{F}^{2}=\underbrace{\sum_{j=k+1}^{r}\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{j,j,:}\|_{F}^{2}}_{\text{Eckart-Young error}}+\underbrace{\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\|_{F}^{2}\vphantom{\sum_{j=k+1}^{r}\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{j,j,:}\|_{F}^{2}}}_{\text{projection error}}.

(4.6)

Proof.

Recall from (
3.7
), we can separate the tensor as
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})
. Following
Definition

4.1
, the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD can exactly capture the first term; that is,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{H}.

(4.7)

The second term lies in the null space of
\mathbf{Q}^{H}
, and hence cannot be approximated by the full nor truncated
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD. Thus, we quantify the optimal
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-rank-
k
approximation of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}
.

Using the unitary invariance of the Frobenius norm, we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{H}\|_{F}=\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\smalltriangleup\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}^{H}\|_{F}

(4.8)

where
\widehat{\cdot}=\cdot\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}
. In the transform domain, we independently approximate each frontal slice. By the matrix Eckart-Young Theorem
[
8
]
, we have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{:,:,i}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{k}(:,:,i)\|_{F}\leq\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{:,:,i}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}_{:,:,i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}_{:,:,i}^{H}\|_{F}

(4.9)

for
i=1,\dots,p
where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
is the truncated
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD. This completes the proof that the truncated
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD produces the best
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-rank-
k
approximation to the original tensor.

To compute the two error terms, let
\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}&\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}^{H}
be a unitary matrix. By (
3.11
), the last
n_{3}-p
frontal slices of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\times_{3}\mathbf{M}
are zero. By the unitary invariance of the Frobenius norm,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\|_{F}^{2}=\|(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k})\times_{3}\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}^{H}-{\bf 0}\|_{F}^{2}

(4.10)

The first term is the error of the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD approximation of the first
p
frontal slices in the transform domain (Eckart-Young error). Following
[
19
, Theorem 3.7]
, the Eckart-Young error is equal to the norm of the truncated singular tubes. The second term is equal to
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\|_{F}^{2}
because
\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}\mathbf{Q}_{\perp}^{H}=\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}
(projection error).

∎

\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}

Proof.

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{U}_{3}^{H})_{:,:,i}=\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{A}_{(3)})\texttt{reshape}(\mathbf{V}_{3}(:,i),[n_{1},n_{2}])

(4.12)

\displaystyle\|{\bf 0}-\sigma_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{(3)})\texttt{reshape}(\mathbf{V}_{3}(:,i),[n_{1},n_{2}])\|_{F}=\sigma_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{(3)}).

(4.13)

Because the columns of
\mathbf{V}_{3}
have unit length, the magnitude depends only on the singular value.
∎

The
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD with
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{U}_{3}(:,1:p)
optimizes the projection error in the following sense.

Theorem 4.3:
Optimal Projection Error

Given
\mathbf{A}_{(3)}=\mathbf{U}_{3}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Sigma}$}_{3}\mathbf{V}_{3}^{H}
, then
\mathbf{U}_{3}(:,1:p)\in\argmin_{\mathbf{Q}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})}\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\|_{F}.

Proof.

We can write the projection error as

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|(\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\mathbf{A}_{(3)}\|_{F}=\|\mathbf{A}_{(3)}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}\mathbf{A}_{(3)}\|_{F}

(4.14)

Note that
\rank(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H}\mathbf{A}_{(3)})\leq p
. By the matrix Eckart-Young Theorem
[
8
]
, the best rank-
p
approximation to
\mathbf{A}_{(3)}
in the Frobenius norm is the truncated matrix SVD. This corresponds to the case when
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{U}_{3}(:,1:p)
.
∎

While the matrix
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{U}_{3}(:,1:p)
produces the smallest projection error, it does not necessarily yield the smallest
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD error (
Section

4
).
We provide a counterexample in
Section

4
to illustrate this point.

Example 4.1:

\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}

)

Consider the following

0&1\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{:,:,2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}.

(4.15)

Then, up to column permutation and negation,
\mathbf{U}_{3}=\mathbf{I}_{2}
.
We compare the truncated
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}

and the transposed
2\times 2
Haar wavelet matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{2}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\

(4.16)

Using truncation parameter
k=1
and projection dimension

, we compute the approximation errors

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(\mathbf{U}_{3}(:,1))\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=1+2\qquad\text{and}

(4.17)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(\mathbf{H}_{2}(1,:)^{\top})\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=0+2

(4.18)

Since this matrix is rank-

4.1
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII

In
[
19
]
, a variant called the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-SVDII (or often the
t
-SVDMII), was proven to have the same approximation quality as the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-SVD for less storage cost. We present a similar variant for projected products called the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII. The key to the additional compressibility is to consider a global perspective of the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD.
By viewing the facewise product as block diagonal matrix multiplication, the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD in the transform domain can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{:,:,1}\\
&\ddots\\
&&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{:,:,p}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{:,:,1}\\
&\ddots\\
&&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{:,:,p}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}_{:,:,1}\\
&\ddots\\
&&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}_{:,:,p}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}}_{:,:,1}\\
&\ddots\\
&&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}}_{:,:,p}\end{bmatrix}^{H}.

(4.20)

From the block diagonal presentation in (
4.20
), we can globally reorder the singular values and truncate based on a desired approximation quality.
Let
r
be the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-rank of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
and let
\pi:\{(j,j,i)|j=1,\dots,r,i=1,\dots,p\}\to\{1,\dots,rp\}
be a permutation that sorts the transform domain singular values in decreasing order of magnitude; i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\pi(j,j,i)}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}_{j,j,i}\quad\text{for }j=1,\dots,r\text{ and }i=1,\dots,p

(4.21)

such that
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\ell}\geq\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\ell+1}
for
\ell=1,\dots,rp-1
.
Note that the mapping
\pi
is not unique if there are repeated singular values.
Using this reordering, we truncate the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII based on a user-defined energy level
\gamma\in(0,1]
.
We present the pseudocode in
Section

4.1

Algorithm 4.2:
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII

1:

Inputs:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
,
\mathbf{Q}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})
, energy parameter
\gamma\in(0,1]

2:

Move to the transform domain
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}

3:

Compute the facewise SVD
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}\smalltriangleup\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}\smalltriangleup\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}}^{H}

4:

Globally reorder singular values and store in a vector
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}
according to (
4.21
)

5:

Find the first index
K
such that
\nicefrac{{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{\ell}^{2}}}{{\|\widehat{\mathbf{s}}\|_{2}^{2}}}\geq\gamma

6:

for

i=1,\dots,p

do

7:

Set the rank per frontal slice to be
\rho_{i}=\argmax_{j=1,\dots,r}\{\pi(j,j,i)\leq K\}

8:

Truncate in the transform domain with

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{:,:,i}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}_{:,1:\rho_{i},i},

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}_{:,:,i}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}_{1:\rho_{i},1:\rho_{i},i},

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}}_{:,:,i}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}}_{:,1:\rho_{i},i}.

end

for

10:

Return:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}}

A key difference between the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII and the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-SVDII
[

, Algorithm 3]
is the total energy in the transform domain may be different than total energy in the spatial domain; that is,
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}\|_{F}\not=\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\|_{F}
.
In particular, the denominator in Line 5 of
Section

4.1
has an implicit dependence on
\mathbf{Q}
.

The
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII gives rise to two different notions of the rank of a tensor.

Definition 4.3

(
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-multirank)
.

The
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-multirank of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
is a
p
-tuple
\mathbf{\rho}
where
\rho_{i}=\rank(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{:,:,i})
for
i=1,\dots,p
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}
.

Definition 4.4

(
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-implicit rank)
.

The
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-implicit rank of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
is the total number of singular values stored in the transform domain; that is,
\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\rho}$}_{i}
where
\mathbf{\rho}
is the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-multirank of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
.

If a tensor has
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-rank-
r
, then the tensor has a
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-implicit rank of
\rho\leq rp
.
Combining these notions of rank, we prove the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII is provably optimal in an Eckart-Young sense.

Theorem 4.4:
Optimality of
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII

Given
\mathbf{Q}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})
and a tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
with
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-implicit rank
\rho

\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-multirank-

with
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\kappa}$}_{i}=\kappa\leq\rho

\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII; that is,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\kappa}$}}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{\prime\prime}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}\|_{F}

(4.22)

where
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{\prime\prime}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}\mid\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}\text{-implicit-rank}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}})\leq\kappa\}
.
The Frobenius norm error is

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\kappa}$}}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\kappa}$}_{i}+1}^{r}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}_{j,j,i}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{H})\|_{F}^{2}

(4.23)

Proof.

The proof leverages the matrix Eckart-Young Theorem similarly to the proof of
Section

4

∎

We further prove that the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII computed via

\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD for no additional storage cost.

Theorem 4.5:
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII vs.
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD

Given
\mathbf{Q}\in\Stiefel_{n_{3},p}(\mathbb{C})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
with
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-rank-
r
, let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
of the truncated
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD for
k\leq r
.
Define the energy parameter
\gamma=\nicefrac{{\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}\|_{F}^{2}}}{{\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{Q}^{H}\|_{F}^{2}}}
.
Then, the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\kappa}$}}
corresponding to energy
\gamma
is a no worse approximation than
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
; that is,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\kappa}$}}\|_{F}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\|_{F}.

(4.24)

Proof.

Because we use the same transformation
\mathbf{Q}
for both decompositions, the projection error is equal for the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD and
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII.
Thus, we will compare that the Eckart-Young error terms in
Section

4
and
Section

4.1
.
For the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII, if we use the trivial
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-multirank is
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\kappa}$}=(k,k,\dots,k)
, then
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\kappa}$}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
and the relative energy constraint is automatically satisfied.
The
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-multirank formed from the energy threshold in
Section

4.1
will truncate the smallest singular values globally. Thus, we can only improve the approximation compared to the trivial
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-multirank.
As a result, the Eckart-Young (EY) error term satisfies the following inequality:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{i=\rho_{i}+1}^{r}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}_{j,j,:}^{2}}_{\text{\begin{tabular}[]{c}$\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}$-SVDII EY error\\
with energy $\gamma$\end{tabular}}}\leq\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{i=k+1}^{r}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}_{j,j,:}^{2}}_{\text{\begin{tabular}[]{c}$\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}$-SVDII EY error\\
with $\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\rho}$}=(r,\dots,r)$\end{tabular}}}=\sum_{j=k+1}^{r}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}}_{j,j,:}\|_{F}^{2}=\underbrace{\sum_{j=k+1}^{r}\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{j,j,:}\|_{F}^{2}.}_{\text{$\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}$-SVD EY error}}

∎

4.2
Comparison to Higher-Order SVD

In addition to comparing the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD to the original
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-version, we compare to the commonly-used (truncated) higher-order SVD (HOSVD)
[
7
]
, which approximates a third-order tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\approx\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbf{k}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\times_{1}\mathbf{U}_{1}(:,1:k_{1})\times_{2}\mathbf{U}_{2}(:,1:k_{2})\times_{3}\mathbf{U}_{3}(:,1:k_{3})

(4.29)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}\times k_{2}\times k_{3}}
is the core tensor and
\mathbf{U}_{i}\in\Stiefel_{n_{i},n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
for
i=1,\dots,3
are the factor matrices.
Each factor matrix
\mathbf{U}_{i}
is the truncated left singular matrix from the SVD of various tensor unfoldings

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{(i)}=\mathbf{U}_{i}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Sigma}$}_{i}\mathbf{V}_{i}^{H}\qquad\text{for $i=1,2,3$}

(4.30)

where
\mathbf{A}_{(i)}
is the mode-
i
unfolding, defined similarly to
Definition

2.1
; details can be found in
[
20
]
. We denote the truncated HOSVD with multilinear rank
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
as
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbf{k}}
. We prove that the truncated
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD yields a more accurate approximation than the truncated HOSVD.

Theorem 4.6:
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD vs. HOSVD

If
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{U}_{3}(:,1:p)
and
k=\min(k_{1},k_{2})
, then the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-rank-
k
approximation
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
is no worse than the truncated HOSVD
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbf{k}}
of multilinear rank
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},p)
; i.e.,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\|_{F}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbf{k}}\|_{F}.

(4.31)

Proof.

The key observation is that the transformed tensor,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{U}_{3}(:,1:p)^{H}
, is found in the HOSVD as well. Specifically, we regroup the truncated HOSVD as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\times_{3}\mathbf{U}_{3}(:,1:p)^{H})\times_{1}\mathbf{U}_{1}(:,1:k_{1})^{H}\times_{2}\mathbf{U}_{2}(:,1:k_{2})^{H}

(4.32a)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}\times_{1}\mathbf{U}_{1}(:,1:k_{1})^{H}\times_{2}\mathbf{U}_{2}(:,1:k_{2})^{H}

(4.32b)

The remainder of the proof follows from
[
19
, Section 6.A and Theorem 6.1]
, which shows that each frontal slice of the truncated HOSVD has rank less than or equal to
k
.
Because the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD uses the optimal rank-
k
approximation to each frontal slice, it yields a more accurate approximation.
∎

Combining
Section

4.2
and
Section

4.1
, we can produce a similar theorem for the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII.
We omit the theorem and proof for the sake of brevity.

5.1.2
Metrics

We consider two metrics: relative error (RE) to measure approximation quality and compression ratio (CR) to measure storage costs. We define each metric as

\displaystyle\text{RE}=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}\|_{F}}{\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\|_{F}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\text{CR}=\frac{\texttt{st}[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}]}{\texttt{st}[\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}]+\texttt{st}[\mathbf{Q}]}

where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}
is the compressed representation of the original data
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
and
\texttt{st}[\cdot]

In our experiments, we assume we are given a dense, real-valued tensor

(5.2)

Conclusions

We developed a unified algebraic framework for projected tensor-tensor products that preserves matrix mimeticity with reduced computational overhead. In
Section

3
, we verified that fundamental linear algebraic properties are inherited under the projected product. In
Section

4
, we introduced the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD, proved an Eckart-Young-like optimality result, and provided insight into an optimal choice of transformation matrix. We extended the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD to the more compressible
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVDII in
Section

4.1
and proved the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-representational superiority over the truncated HOSVD in
Section

4.2
. In
Section

5
, we provided extensive experiments on video and hyperspectral data to empirically verify the theoretical findings and demonstrate the compressibility of
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-representations. We observed that the
\star_{\mathbf{Q}^{H}}^{\prime}
-SVD (and variants) with an appropriate choice of transformation produces the best representation in terms of relative reconstruction error compared to compression ratio in all of our experiments.

Our work lays the foundation for future exploration of other tensor algebras defined by non-invertible, efficient transformations.
In the short term, we can build from the work in
[
27
]
to optimize the transformation matrix
\mathbf{Q}
practically and we can extend to higher-order projected products following the work in
[
14
]
.
As a subsequent extension, we can generalize to any linear transformation by using the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse
[
32
,
2
]
to approximate the reverse transformation.
This will enable exploration into a wider range of matrix structures that can be stored and (pseudo) inverted efficiently, e.g., low-rank, symmetric positive semidefinite matrices, but may sacrifice some algebraic guarantees.
Beyond algebraic extensions, we will explore new applications to compress dense tensors with at least one large dimension, such as spatio-temporal data for chemo-sensing
[
35
]
and fluid flow simulations
[
4
,
38
]
.
