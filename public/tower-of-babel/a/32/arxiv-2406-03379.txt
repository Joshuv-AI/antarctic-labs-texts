How to Construct Quantum FHE, Generically
Aparna Gupte, Vinod Vaikuntanathan

Abstract
We construct a (compact) quantum fully homomorphic encryption (QFHE) scheme starting from any (compact) classical fully homomorphic encryption scheme with decryption in 𝖭𝖢 1 \mathsf{NC}^{1} , together with a dual-mode trapdoor function family. Compared to previous constructions (Mahadev, FOCS 2018; Brakerski, CRYPTO 2018) which made non-black-box use of similar underlying primitives, our construction provides a pathway to instantiations from different assumptions. Our construction uses the techniques of Dulek, Schaffner and Speelman (CRYPTO 2016) and shows how to make the client in their QFHE scheme classical using dual-mode trapdoor functions. As an additional contribution, we show a new instantiation of dual-mode trapdoor functions from group actions.

Introduction

A quantum fully homomorphic encryption (QFHE)
[
Mah20
]
scheme enables a classical client with an input
x
to outsource the computation of a quantum circuit
Q
(on
x
) to an untrusted quantum server, while hiding
x
. The first QFHE scheme was constructed under the (super-polynomial) learning with errors (LWE) assumption in a groundbreaking work of Mahadev
[
Mah20
]
; this was followed shortly after by a work of Brakerski
[
Bra18
]
who, with a different construction, improved the assumption to polynomial LWE.
1
1

1

In both cases, we get a leveled QFHE scheme, one that supports quantum circuits of an a-priori bounded depth, from LWE. To construct an unleveled QFHE scheme, both works rely on an appropriate circular security assumption, different from the one used to get an unleveled classical FHE scheme. From this point on, we focus on constructing a leveled QFHE scheme.

The QFHE constructions of
[
Mah20
,
Bra18
]
built on classical FHE schemes that can be constructed from the LWE assumption. However, their schemes use very specific properties of specific FHE schemes. For example,
[
Mah20
]
used the dual-GSW FHE scheme
[
GSW13
]
together with a noisy trapdoor claw-free function built from LWE. The construction used the fact that the dual-GSW encryption of a bit
b
can be converted into the description of a claw-free function pair
(f_{0},f_{1})
that “encodes” the bit, in the sense that for two pre-images
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
such that
f_{0}(x_{0})=f_{1}(x_{1})
, the first bits of
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
xor to
b
. In a similar vein,
[
Bra18
]
crucially used the GSW encryption scheme and the discrete Gaussian structure of the set of all random strings consistent with a given ciphertext. In other words, both schemes used a particular instantiation of the underlying primitives and exploited their intricate interplay.

This raises a natural question which is the starting point of our work:

Can we generically transform any FHE scheme into a QFHE scheme?

Aside from the aesthetic appeal of such a result, it would give us a recipe to convert new classical FHE schemes, as they arise, into quantum FHE schemes; an example of a different FHE scheme is the IO-based FHE construction from
[
CLTV15
]
.

1.1
Our Results

In this work, we make progress towards this goal by constructing a QFHE scheme starting with
any
(classical) FHE scheme (whose decryption circuit can be implemented in logarithmic depth) and
any
dual-mode trapdoor function (dTF) family.

We define a dual-mode trapdoor function family as follows: a dTF is a pair of functions
(f_{0},f_{1})
that either has the same image in one mode, or disjoint images in the other. Further, these two modes are computationally indistinguishable (see Section
3
for the formal definition). In contrast, a plain claw-free trapdoor function (as defined in
[
Mah18
,
BCM+21
]
) is a pair of functions
(f_{0},f_{1})
for which it is computationally hard to find a claw, i.e. a pair of inputs
(x_{0},x_{1})
such that
f_{0}(x_{0})=f_{1}(x_{1})
.

Informal Theorem 1
.

Let
\lambda
be the security parameter. Assuming the existence of a leveled fully homomorphic encryption scheme
\mathsf{HE}
whose decryption algorithm can be implemented as a Boolean circuit with depth
O(\log\lambda)
, and a dual-mode trapdoor function family, there exists a quantum leveled fully homomorphic encryption scheme with a classical key generation procedure. The scheme encrypts classical information using classical ciphertexts.

In other words, our result offers an answer to the open question above, except with two caveats: (1) we require the classical FHE scheme to have a shallow decryption circuit; and (2) we require an additional primitive, namely a dual-mode trapdoor function family. Both building blocks required for our construction can be instantiated from the Ring LWE assumption giving us the first construction of QFHE from Ring LWE. We note that one could have come up with this instantiation by looking into the innards of
[
Mah20
,
Bra18
]
; however, with our construction, this instantiation is simply plug-and-play. Even with LWE-based constructions, any improvements in the underlying LWE-based FHE or LWE-based dual-mode trapdoor function translate directly into corresponding improvements in our QFHE scheme.

As an additional result, we show how to instantiate the dual-mode trapdoor function from group actions, building on the work of
[
AMR22
]
who construct a plain claw-free trapdoor function from the same assumption.

Informal Theorem 2
.

There exists a dual-mode trapdoor function family under the extended linear hidden shift assumption (see
[
AMR22
]
and Definition
15
) on group actions.

It also turns out that we can construct an FHE scheme given an indistinguishability obfuscation (IO) scheme, together with a perfectly rerandomizable encryption scheme, following
[
CLTV15
]
. Together with a construction of a perfectly rerandomizable encryption scheme from group actions
[
Wic24
]
, this gives us a QFHE scheme from (post-quantum, subexponentially secure) IO and group actions.

Informal Theorem 3
.

Assuming that post-quantum sub-exponentially secure indistinguishability obfuscation exists and assuming the post-quantum hardness of the extended linear hidden shift assumption, there is a quantum leveled fully homomorphic encryption scheme with a classical key generation procedure. The scheme encrypts classical information using classical ciphertexts.

We remark that we can extend this result to give unleveled QFHE using post-quantum IO.

1.2
Technical Overview

The starting point of our QFHE construction is the beautiful work of Dulek, Schaffner and Speelman
[
DSS16
]
which predated
[
Mah20
]
, and showed how to build a protocol whereby a
semi-quantum
client with a (classical or quantum) input
x
can delegate to a fully quantum server with a quantum circuit
Q
the computation of
Q(x)
. In addition to encrypting the input
x
, the client also generates polynomially many copies of a
quantum gadget
that helps the server do the quantum homomorphic evaluation of
Q
on the encrypted
x
.

In a nutshell, the key idea in our work is to come up with a mechanism for the client to outsource to the server the preparation of these quantum gadgets, using a dual-mode trapdoor function. This results in a completely classical client, and consequently a QFHE scheme.
2
2

2

This is, in effect, a remote state preparation protocol for the DSS gadget states; however, in the QFHE setting (as opposed to the verification setting), the protocol only needs to be secure against a semi-honest/specious server.
We now proceed to describe the construction in more detail, starting with the DSS protocol.

The DSS scheme builds on the work of Broadbent and Jeffery
[
BJ15
]
and encrypts quantum states (or classical strings) with a quantum one-time pad.
That is, a single-qubit state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
is encrypted as

\mathsf{X}^{x}\mathsf{Z}^{z}\left|\psi\right\rangle

where
x,z\leftarrow\{0,1\}
are uniformly random bits and

\mathsf{X}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),~\mathsf{Z}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right)

are the Pauli
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Z}
operators. Additionally, the ciphertext also contains a (classical) homomorphic encryption of the one-time pad keys
(x,z)
.

We recall that any quantum circuit can be implemented using Clifford gates and the single-qubit
\mathsf{T}
gate

\mathsf{T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&e^{i\pi/4}\end{array}\right)

Homomorphic evaluation of Clifford gates on states encrypted using the Pauli one-time pad is easy as Clifford gates nicely “commute” through the Pauli one-time pad. For example,

\mathsf{H}\mathsf{X}^{x}\mathsf{Z}^{z}\left|\psi\right\rangle=\mathsf{Z}^{x}\mathsf{X}^{z}\mathsf{H}\left|\psi\right\rangle

All that needs to be done is to homomorphically update encryption of the one-time pads.
When the non-Clifford
\mathsf{T}
gate is applied to a one-time padded state, we end up with an extra non-Pauli error in the form of a
\mathsf{P}
gate,

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}\mathsf{X}^{x}\mathsf{Z}^{z}\left|\psi\right\rangle=\mathsf{P}^{x}\mathsf{X}^{x}\mathsf{Z}^{z}\mathsf{T}\left|\psi\right\rangle.

The natural solution would be to apply a
\mathsf{P}^{\dagger}
gate to this state if
x=1
, and otherwise do nothing. However, the evaluator only has access to an encryption
\tilde{x}
of the one-time pad key
x
. Dulek, Schaffner and Speelman
[
DSS16
]
solve this issue by writing the decryption algorithm (with the secret key
sk
hardcoded) as a branching program and converting it into a quantum “teleportation gadget”
\Gamma(sk)
which the client prepares during key generation. We henceforth refer to this gadget as the DSS teleportation gadget. The server then teleports the qubit
\mathsf{P}^{x}\mathsf{X}^{x}\mathsf{Z}^{z}\mathsf{T}\left|\psi\right\rangle
through the gadget in a way that depends on
\tilde{x}
, and the gadget implicitly decrypts
\tilde{x}
and applies the
(\mathsf{P}^{\dagger})^{x}
correction to the qubit. The functionality achieved by the DSS gadget is depicted in Figure
1
.
For completeness, we describe how the DSS gadget looks and how it is used to achieve the desired functionality in Appendix
A
.

Figure 1
:
The functionality of the DSS quantum gadget
\left|\Gamma(sk)\right\rangle
: take as input a state
P^{x}X^{x}Z^{z}\left|\psi\right\rangle
as well as encryptions of
x
and
z
, and produce as output
X^{x^{\prime}}Z^{z^{\prime}}\left|\psi\right\rangle
together with encryptions of the new one-time pad keys
x^{\prime}
and
z^{\prime}
.

At a high level, the DSS gadget consists of Bell pairs that are wired in a way that depends on the bits of the secret key
sk
used to decrypt the ciphertext
\mathsf{Enc}_{pk}(x)
. The key new contribution of our work is a method that allows a completely classical client to delegate the preparation of the DSS teleportation gadget to the server. At the center of our method is a procedure that prepares a “hidden” Bell pair. The procedure takes three qubits in registers
0,1,2
in the product state
\left|+\right\rangle_{0}\otimes\left|+\right\rangle_{1}\otimes\left|+\right\rangle_{2}
, and a particular kind of “encryption” of a bit
\mu
. The procedure then outputs a (one-time padded) state where the qubits on registers
\mu
and
2
wired together to form a maximally entangled Bell state that is in tensor product with the qubit
\left|+\right\rangle_{1-\mu}
in the
1-\mu
register,

\displaystyle\left|\Phi^{+}\right\rangle_{\mu,2}\otimes\left|+\right\rangle_{1-\mu}.

We do this with the help of a “
4
-to-
2
” dual-mode trapdoor function (which we can obtain generically from dual-mode trapdoor functions, see Section
3.1
), a new primitive we define as follows (see Figure
3
for a pictorial illustration): a tuple of four functions
f_{b_{1},b_{2}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
for
b_{1},b_{2}
such that depending on a “mode” bit
\mu\in\{0,1\}
, either

1.

(Case
\mu=0
) two functions
f_{b_{1},b_{2}}
and
f_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}}
have the same image if
b_{1}=b^{\prime}_{1}
and otherwise they have disjoint images, or

2.

(Case
\mu=1
) two functions
f_{b_{1},b_{2}}
and
f_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}}
have the same image if
b_{2}=b^{\prime}_{2}
. Otherwise they have disjoint images.

Further, without a trapdoor, no efficient adversary should be able to guess
\mu
significantly better than randomly guessing.
Such a tuple of functions encoding the mode bit
\mu
is the specific “encryption” we use to prepare the required hidden Bell pairs.
Given such a family of functions, we prepare the state

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2|\mathcal{X}|}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v,w\in\{0,1\}\\
x\in\mathcal{X}\end{subarray}}\left|u,v,w\right\rangle_{0,1,2}\otimes\left|x\right\rangle_{3}\otimes\left|f_{u\oplus w,v\oplus w}(x)\right\rangle_{4}.

Suppose for simplicity that
\mu=0
.
Measuring the fourth register to get some
y\in\mathcal{Y}
collapses the state to preimages under either the
b_{1}=0
pair
(f_{0,b_{2}})_{b_{2}\in\{0,1\}}
or the
b_{1}=1
pair
(f_{1,b_{2}})_{b_{2}\in\{0,1\}}
. Again, for simplicity, let us consider the branch where
b_{1}=0
. The state we are now left with is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|000\right\rangle\left|x_{0,0}\right\rangle+\left|101\right\rangle\left|x_{0,1}\right\rangle+\left|111\right\rangle\left|x_{0,0}\right\rangle+\left|010\right\rangle\left|x_{0,1}\right\rangle\right)_{0,1,2,3},

where
x_{b_{1},b_{2}}
is such that
f_{b_{1},b_{2}}(x_{b_{1},b_{2}})=y
.
Now, we measure the register 3 (containing the preimages) in the Hadamard basis, so that we obtain a string
d
and the state (up to global phase)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|000\right\rangle+(-1)^{z}\left|101\right\rangle+\left|111\right\rangle+(-1)^{z}\left|010\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\mathsf{Z}^{z}\otimes\mathsf{Z}^{z}\otimes\mathsf{I})\left|\Phi^{+}\right\rangle_{0,2}\otimes\left|+\right\rangle_{1},

where
z=d\cdot(x_{0,0}\oplus x_{0,1})
.

This basic protocol allows us to dequantize the DSS client in the following manner: depending on the secret key
sk
, the client generates
c
lassical evaluation keys for the
4
-to-
2
dual-mode trapdoor functions (where the modes depend on the bits of
sk
) and sends these to the server. The server then performs the above procedure for all the qubits in the gadget, thereby wiring the Bell pairs correctly depending on the bits of
sk
. Once the server has prepared this gadget
\Gamma(sk)
, it can simply follow the DSS scheme for evaluating the
\mathsf{T}
gates and correcting the
\mathsf{P}
errors.

1.3
Related Work

Quantum Fully-Homomorphic Encryption.

Broadbent and Jeffery
[
BJ15
]
gave the first QFHE scheme assuming only a classical FHE scheme, although they considered the setting of a quantum client. They constructed a (non-compact) scheme where the ciphertext size grows polynomially with the number of non-Clifford gates in the circuit. They also gave a different quantum FHE scheme in which the quantum client produces quantum evaluation keys, the size of which grows doubly exponentially in the non-Clifford gate-depth. Dulek, Schaffner and Speelman
[
DSS16
]
improved on this and gave a QFHE scheme, also for a quantum client, but with the size of the quantum evaluation key growing polynomially with the non-Clifford gate-depth. However, they required the underlying classical FHE scheme to have a decryption circuit in
\mathsf{NC}^{1}
.

The work of Mahadev
[
Mah20
]
was a major breakthrough and constructed the first QFHE scheme for a completely classical client, under the quantum hardness of LWE (with superpolynomial modulus-to-noise ratio). Brakerski
[
Bra18
]
later improved the parameters, and gave a different QFHE scheme for a classical client under a weaker assumption, namely LWE with a polynomial modulus-to-noise ratio.

Remote State Preparation.

The main conceptual idea of our work is to give a protocol by which the server can blindly prepare the quantum evaluation keys of
[
DSS16
]
, given only classical messages from the client and therefore side-stepping the need for a quantum client as well as quantum communication. This idea is inspired by the work on remote state preparation, a protocol that allows a quantum server to blindly prepare a quantum state specified by the classical client. The notion of remote state preparation was first introduced by
[
DK16
]
, and subsequently studied by
[
GV19
,
GMP22
]
. We note that remote state preparation usually refers to a stronger variant in the setting of a malicious server, where the protocol to prepare the quantum state is not only
blind
, but also
verifiable
by the classical client. However in the QFHE setting, the server is semi-honest, and we only require the weaker notion of blind (but not necessarily verifiable) remote state preparation.

Organization of the Paper.

In Section
2
, we introduce some notation and define (quantum) homomorphic encryption. In Section
3
, we define dual-mode trapdoor functions (dTFs), and show how to construct a variant we call “
4
-to-
2
” dual-mode trapdoor functions from plain dual-mode trapdoor functions. We also give an amplification lemma that will later allow us to construct dTFs from group actions with negligible correctness error. In Section
4
we use this primitive to show that we can dequantize the DSS client, and we present our QFHE scheme in Section
5
. Section
6
lists the known instantiations of the two primitives we require, namely classical FHE and dual-mode trapdoor functions, from several cryptographic assumptions. Finally, in Section
7
, we show an instantiation based on hardness assumptions related to group actions.

Preliminaries

Notation.

Following the convention in
[
DSS16
]
, we denote ciphertexts with a tilde sign, when the encryption scheme and the public/private key are clear from the context. For example
\tilde{a}
denotes an encryption of
a
. When a sequence of keys is involved, we write
\tilde{a}^{[i]}
to denote an encryption of
a
under the
i
-th key in a set. If
\mathcal{M}
is a quantum register, we denote the set of density operators on
\mathcal{M}
by
D(\mathcal{M})
. We denote the trace distance between density matrices
\rho
and
\sigma
as
T(\rho,\sigma)
, and the total variation distance between probability distribution
D_{1},D_{2}
as
\text{TV}(D_{1},D_{2})
.

Definition 1

(Hellinger Distance)
.

For two probability density functions
f,g
over a finite domain
\mathcal{X}
, the squared Hellinger distance between
f
and
g
is defined as

\displaystyle H^{2}(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\sqrt{f(x)}-\sqrt{g(x)}\right)^{2}=1-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{f(x)g(x)}.

Lemma 1
.

Let
D_{1},D_{2}
be two probability density functions over a finite domain
\mathcal{X}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}H^{2}(D_{1},D_{2})\leq\text{TV}(D_{1},D_{2})\leq H(D_{1},D_{2}).

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite set and let
D_{1},D_{2}
be probability densities on
\mathcal{X}
. Let

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{D_{1}(x)}\left|x\right\rangle\quad\text{ and }\quad\left|\psi_{2}\right\rangle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{D_{2}(x)}\left|x\right\rangle\;.

Then, the trace distance between the pure states
\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle,\left|\psi_{2}\right\rangle
is

\displaystyle T(\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{1}\right|,\left|\psi_{2}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{2}\right|)=\sqrt{1-(1-H^{2}(D_{1},D_{2}))^{2}}.

2.1
Homomorphic Encryption

Definition 2

(Classical Homomorphic encryption scheme)
.

Let
\lambda
be the security parameter. A homomorphic encryption scheme
\mathsf{HE}
is a tuple of p.p.t. algorithms
\mathsf{HE}=(\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Dec},\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Eval})
with the following properties.

•

Key Generation. The probabilistic key generation algorithm
(pk,evk,sk)\leftarrow\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})
outputs a public key
pk
, an evaluation
evk
and a secret key
sk
.

•

Encryption. The probabilistic encryption algorithm
c\leftarrow\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Enc}_{pk}(x)
uses the public key
pk
and encrypts a single bit message
x\in\{0,1\}
into a ciphertext
c
.

•

Decryption. The deterministic decryption algorithm
x\leftarrow\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Dec}_{sk}(c)
uses the secret key
sk
and decrypts a ciphertext
c
to recover the message
x\in\{0,1\}
.

•

Evaluation. The (deterministic or probabilistic) evaluation algorithm
c^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Eval}_{evk}(\mathcal{C},(c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}))
uses the evaluation key
evk
, takes as input a circuit
\mathcal{C}:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
and sequence of ciphertexts
c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}
, and outputs a ciphertext
c^{\prime}
.

We often overload the functionality of the encryption and decryption procedures by allowing them to take in multi-bit messages as inputs, and produce a sequence of ciphertexts that correspond to bit-by-bit encryptions.

Definition 3

(Full homomorphism and compactness)
.

A scheme
\mathsf{HE}
is fully homomorphic, if for any efficiently computable circuit
\mathcal{C}
and any set of inputs
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{\ell}
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Dec}_{sk}(c^{\prime})\neq\mathcal{C}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})]=\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

where the probability is taken over
(pk,evk,sk)\leftarrow\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})
and
c_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Enc}_{pk}(x_{i})
, and
c^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Eval}_{evk}(\mathcal{C},(c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}))
.

A fully homomorphic encryption scheme is compact if its decryption circuit is independent of the evaluated function. The scheme is leveled fully homomorphic if it takes
1^{L}
as additional input in key generation, and can only evaluate depth
L
Boolean circuits.

2.2
Quantum Homomorphic Encryption

We now give a definition for quantum homomorphic encryption based on the definition given in
[
BJ15
]
.
In this work, we focus on quantum homomorphic encryption schemes with entirely classical key generation procedures.

Definition 4

(Quantum Homomorphic Encryption)
.

A quantum homomorphic encryptions scheme is a
\mathsf{QHE}
is a tuple of quantum polynomial-time algorithms
(\mathsf{QHE}.\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{QHE}.\mathsf{Eval},\mathsf{QHE}.\mathsf{Dec})
:

•

Key Generation. The probabilistic key generation algorithm
(pk,sk,evk)\leftarrow\mathsf{QHE}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})
takes a unary representation of the security parameter as input and outputs a classical public key
pk
, a classical secret key
sk
and a classical evaluation key
evk
.

•

Encryption. For every possible value of
pk
, the quantum channel
\mathsf{QHE}.\mathsf{Enc}_{pk}:D(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow D(\mathcal{C})
maps a state in the message space
\mathcal{M}
to a state (the cipherstate) in the cipherspace
\mathcal{C}
.

•

Decryption. For every possible value of
sk
,
\mathsf{QHE}.\mathsf{Dec}_{sk}:D(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
is a quantum channel that maps the state in
D(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
to a quantum state in
D(\mathcal{M})
.

•

Homomorphic Circuit Evaluation. For every quantum circuit
\mathsf{C}
, with induced channel
\Phi_{\mathsf{C}}:D(\mathcal{M}^{\otimes n})\rightarrow D(\mathcal{M}^{\otimes m})
, we define a channel
\mathsf{QHE}.\mathsf{Eval}_{evk}(\mathsf{C},\cdot):D(\mathcal{C}^{\otimes n})\rightarrow D(\mathcal{C}^{\prime\otimes m})
that maps an
n
-fold cipherstate to an
m
-fold cipherstate.

Definition 5

(Quantum Full Homomorphism and Compactness)
.

Let
\lambda
be the security parameter. A quantum homomorphic encryption scheme
\mathsf{QHE}
is fully homomorphic, if for any efficiently computable quantum circuit
\mathsf{C}
with induced channels
\Phi_{\mathsf{C}}:\mathcal{M}^{\otimes n(\lambda)}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\otimes m(\lambda)}
, and for any input
\rho\in D(\mathcal{M}^{\otimes n(\lambda)}\otimes\mathcal{E})
, there exists a negligible function
\mathsf{negl}
s.t. for
(pk,sk,evk)\leftarrow\mathsf{QHE}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})
, the state

\displaystyle\mathsf{QHE}.\mathsf{Dec}_{sk}^{\otimes m(\lambda)}\left(\mathsf{QHE}.\mathsf{Eval}_{evk}(\mathsf{C},\mathsf{QHE}.\mathsf{Enc}_{pk}^{n(\lambda)}(\rho))\right)

is at most
\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
-away in trace distance from the state
\Phi_{\mathsf{C}}(\rho)
.

A quantum homomorphic encryption is compact if its decryption circuit is independent of the evaluated circuit.

The scheme is leveled fully homomorphic if it takes
1^{L}
as additional input in key generation, and can only evaluate circuits of
\mathsf{T}
-depth at most
L
.

(The Quantum IND-CPA Game

BJ15
]
)

\mathcal{M},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C}

Definition 7

3
3

3

Our dTFs are a weakening of Mahadev’s extended NTCFs in two ways: we do not require the adaptive hardcore bit property, and we do not need the functions to be injective. Relaxing the definition to allow many-to-one functions allows us to obtain dTFs with good correctness properties from group actions, as well as dTFs from weaker LWE assumption based on

for intuition.

\mathcal{X}
, and an correctness-error parameter

4
Remote State Preparation of the DSS Gadget

We construct our QFHE scheme by dequantizing the client in the scheme of Dulek, Schaffner and Speelman
[
DSS16
]
, which we will henceforth refer to as DSS. The DSS QFHE scheme builds on the Pauli one-time pad-based template of Broadbent and Jeffery
[
BJ15
]
. Concretely, a quantum state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
is encrypted by applying a random Pauli one-time pad to get
|\tilde{\psi}\rangle:=\mathsf{X}^{x}\mathsf{Z}^{z}\left|\psi\right\rangle
. The keys
x,z
of the one-time pad are encrypted under a classical homomorphic encryption scheme to get ciphertexts
\tilde{x},\tilde{z}
. The one-time padded state
|\tilde{\psi}\rangle
along with these encryptions
\tilde{x},\tilde{z}
form the encryption of the state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
.

Evaluating any Clifford gate is easy, since Clifford gates nicely “commute” through the Pauli one-time pad; we only need to update the one-time pads homomorphically according to standard rules of Clifford-Pauli commutation. On the other hand when the non-Clifford
\mathsf{T}
gate is applied to a Pauli one-time padded state, we end up with an extra non-Pauli error in the form of a
\mathsf{P}
gate, since

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}\mathsf{X}^{x}\mathsf{Z}^{z}\left|\psi\right\rangle=\mathsf{P}^{x}\mathsf{X}^{x}\mathsf{Z}^{z}\mathsf{T}\left|\psi\right\rangle.

The natural solution would be to apply a
\mathsf{P}^{\dagger}
gate to this state if
x=1
, and otherwise do nothing. However it is not clear how to do this, because the evaluator only has access to an encryption
\tilde{x}
of the one-time pad key
x
.

Dulek et al.
[
DSS16
]
solve this issue by “obfuscating” the decryption algorithm (hard-coded with the secret key
sk
) in the form of a quantum teleportation gadget
\Gamma(sk)
, henceforth called the DSS gadget or the DSS teleportation gadget. The client prepares this (quantum) gadget during key generation, and sends it to the evaluator as part of the evaluation key. When the qubit
\mathsf{P}^{x}\mathsf{X}^{x}\mathsf{Z}^{z}\mathsf{T}\left|\psi\right\rangle
is teleported through the gadget (in a way that depends on
\tilde{x}
), the gadget implicitly decrypts
\tilde{x}
and applies the
(\mathsf{P}^{\dagger})^{x}
correction to the teleported qubit. More specifically, we can think of running the DSS gadget as a blackbox that takes as input a state
\rho
and an encryption
\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Enc}_{pk}(x)
for some
x\in\{0,1\}
and outputs the state

\displaystyle\mathsf{X}^{x^{\prime}}\mathsf{Z}^{z^{\prime}}(\mathsf{P}^{\dagger})^{x}\rho(\mathsf{P})^{x}\mathsf{Z}^{z^{\prime}}\mathsf{X}^{x^{\prime}},

for some one-time pad keys
x^{\prime},z^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
, along with encryptions
\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Enc}_{pk^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})
and
\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Enc}_{pk^{\prime}}(z^{\prime})
under an independent public key
pk^{\prime}
. This functionality is visualized in Figure
1
, up to a renaming of the variables

.

In this section, we describe a protocol by which the evaluator is able to prepare the DSS gadget
\Gamma(sk)
on its own, with the help of some
classical
information generated by the client during the key generation procedure. In other words, we give a remote state preparation protocol for the DSS gadget. As a result, the client is entirely classical. This protocol is described in Section
4.3
. The complete description of the structure of the DSS gadget is quite involved, so we give only an abstract description of its structure in Section
4.1
, which will suffice for our purposes. We defer the details of the structure of the DSS gadget to Appendix
A

At the heart of our remote state preparation protocol is a simpler protocol that takes as input a three-qubit state, say on registers labeled
0,1,2
, along with a “

4.1
An abstract description of the DSS gadget

The DSS gadget is composed of a set of entangled qubits to form a perfect matching of Bell pairs. To facilitate teleportation, the gadget also defines a set of Bell-basis measurements for the evaluator to perform.

The complete description of this matching and measurement instructions is quite involved, so we defer our the exposition to Appendix

4.2
Remotely Preparing a Hidden Bell Pair

In this subsection, we give a protocol to remotely prepare a hidden Bell pair. At a high level, given a key
k(\mu)
of a
4
-to-
2

\displaystyle\left|\Phi^{+}\right\rangle_{2,\mu}\otimes\left|+\right\rangle_{1-\mu}.

\displaystyle(\mathsf{Z}^{z_{0}}\otimes\mathsf{Z}^{z_{1}}\otimes\mathsf{X}^{a}\otimes\mathsf{I})\cdot

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v,w\in\{0,1\}\\

\displaystyle(\mathsf{Z}^{z_{0}}\otimes\mathsf{Z}^{z_{1}}\otimes\mathsf{X}^{a}\otimes\mathsf{I})\cdot

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v,w\in\{0,1\}\\

Measuring register
3

\displaystyle\left|\chi^{(5)}\right\rangle\propto\,(\mathsf{Z}^{z_{0}}\otimes\mathsf{Z}^{z_{1}}\otimes\mathsf{X}^{a}\otimes\mathsf{I})

\displaystyle\sum_{v,w\in\{0,1\}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{a,a\oplus v\oplus w}}(-1)^{d\cdot x}\cdot\sqrt{D_{a,a\oplus v\oplus w}(x)}\cdot(\left|v\right\rangle\otimes\left|v\right\rangle\left|\phi_{0}^{v}\right\rangle)_{2,0}\otimes(\left|w\right\rangle\left|\phi_{1}^{w}\right\rangle)_{1}.

\displaystyle\beta_{\nu}:=\alpha_{k,y,d}(a,a\oplus\nu)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{a,a\oplus\nu}}(-1)^{d\cdot x}\cdot\sqrt{D_{a,a\oplus\nu}(x)}\;,

\displaystyle\left|\chi^{(5)}\right\rangle\propto(\mathsf{Z}^{z_{0}}\otimes\mathsf{Z}^{z_{1}}\otimes\mathsf{X}^{a}\otimes\mathsf{I})\sum_{v,w\in\{0,1\}}\beta_{v\oplus w}\cdot(\left|v\right\rangle\otimes\left|v\right\rangle\left|\phi_{0}^{v}\right\rangle)_{2,0}\otimes(\left|w\right\rangle\left|\phi_{1}^{w}\right\rangle)_{1}.

be such that

and equivalently write the state as follows:

\displaystyle\left|\chi^{(5)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\propto(\mathsf{Z}^{z_{0}}\otimes\mathsf{Z}^{z_{1}}\otimes\mathsf{X}^{a}\otimes\mathsf{I})\sum_{v,w\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{s\cdot(v\oplus w)}(\left|v\right\rangle\otimes\left|v\right\rangle\left|\phi_{0}^{v}\right\rangle)_{2,0}\otimes(\left|w\right\rangle\left|\phi_{1}^{w}\right\rangle)_{1}

We can now homomorphically compute encryptions of the new one-time pad keys, since we are also given an encryption of the trapdoor. First, by the property of of efficient partial inversion, we can obtain all possible values of
(b_{1},b_{2})
such that

and therefore

and
r_{1}=z_{1}\oplus s

∎

4.3
Remotely Preparing the DSS Gadget

We dequantize the DSS client by giving a protocol
\mathsf{GadgetKeyGen}(sk,pk^{\prime})
that allows the key generation to be classical, so that it outputs classical “gadget keys”
gk(sk)
that depend on but computationally hide a secret key
sk
.
Using the gadget keys
gk
, the evaluator can then run
\mathsf{RSPGenGadget}
to prepare a teleportation gadget state
\Gamma_{pk^{\prime}}(sk)
that can then be used as in
[
DSS16
]
for the evaluation of
\mathsf{T}
gates. The gadget key must also include trapdoor information encrypted under an independent public key
pk^{\prime}
, so that the evaluator can homomorphically update its one-time pad key encryptions.

The client’s procedure for generating the gadget keys.

Suppose the length of the secret key is
|sk|=n
bits. The client uses this procedure to “encrypt” the secret key
sk
(in the form of keys of a “
4
-to-
2
” dual-mode trapdoor function family
\mathcal{F}
), which provide instructions to the evaluator to prepare teleportation gadget
\Gamma_{pk^{\prime}}(sk)
. The procedure is as follows.

\underline{\mathsf{GadgetKeyGen}(sk,pk^{\prime})}

1.

For every
i\in[n]
, generate key-trapdoor pairs
k_{i},t_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}_{\mathcal{F}}(1^{\lambda},sk_{i})
, giving the
i
th bit of
sk
as input.

2.

Encrypt the trapdoors
t_{i}
using the public key
pk^{\prime}
, to get ciphertexts
\tilde{t}_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Enc}_{pk^{\prime}}(t_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
.

3.

Output the gadget key
gk=(k_{i},\tilde{t}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
.

The evaluator’s procedure for preparing the DSS gadget.

The evaluator uses the gadget keys generated by the client to prepare the DSS gadget. The procedure works as follows.

\underline{\mathsf{RSPGenGadget}(gk,\tilde{sk},pk^{\prime},evk^{\prime})}

1.

Parse the gadget key as
gk=(k_{i},\tilde{t}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
.

2.

Prepare
|Q|
qubit registers, each holding state
\left|+\right\rangle
.

3.

Maintain encryptions
\tilde{x}_{q},\tilde{z}_{q}
of the one-time pad keys for each register

under
pk^{\prime}
. Initialize all of these to encryptions of
0
.

4.

For every
q\in Q_{1}
, run the protocol described in Lemma
6
on registers
\pi_{0}(q)
,
\pi_{1}(q)
and
q
in the place of registers labeled
0
,
1
and
2
in the order. Let
i(q)\in[n]
be such that the
i(q)
th bit

of

determines the value of
\pi^{sk}(q)
Also as input to the protocol to prepare the hidden Bell pair, provide
k_{i(q)},\tilde{t}_{i(q)},\tilde{sk},pk^{\prime},evk^{\prime}
and
\tilde{z}_{\pi_{0}(q)}
and
\tilde{z}_{\pi_{1}(q)}
.

5.

As the output of the hidden Bell pair procedure, we get encryptions of the updated one-time pad keys, so we replace
\tilde{x}_{q}
,
\tilde{z}_{\pi_{0}(q)}
and
\tilde{z}_{\pi_{1}(q)}

6.

Finally, apply an inverse phase gate
\mathsf{P}^{\dagger}
to the qubits in registers

Lemma 7
.

Given a gadget key
gk\leftarrow\mathsf{GadgetKeyGen}(sk,pk^{\prime})
, public key
pk^{\prime}
and evaluation key
evk^{\prime}
,
\mathsf{RSPGenGadget}
correctly outputs

\displaystyle\gamma_{x,z}(sk):=\bigotimes_{q\in Q_{1}}\mathsf{X}^{x_{q}}\mathsf{Z}^{z_{q}}(\mathsf{P}^{\dagger})^{p_{q}}\left|\Phi^{+}\right\rangle\left\langle\Phi^{+}\right|_{q,\pi^{sk}(q)}(\mathsf{P})^{p_{q}}\mathsf{Z}^{z_{q}}\mathsf{X}^{x_{q}},

(5)

where
x,z\in\{0,1\}^{|Q_{1}|}
are one-time pad keys and
\tilde{x}
and
\tilde{z}
are their encryptions under public key

.

Proof.

We argue that throughout the
\mathsf{RSPGenGadget}(gk,pk^{\prime},evk^{\prime})
, the inputs to the procedure in Lemma

, to prepare hidden Bell pairs, are of the form of Equation (
3
). As
\mathsf{RSPGenGadget}
processes the registers
q\in Q_{1}
one at a time, it prepares the corresponding component
\left|\Phi^{+}\right\rangle\left\langle\Phi^{+}\right|_{q,\pi^{sk}(q)}
of the tensor product state
\gamma_{x,z}(sk)
.
In the beginning, for the first register
q\in Q_{1}
, since all the registers are in the state
\left|+\right\rangle
, the input condition is satisfied, with
\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle=\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle=\left|+\right\rangle

, where
i(q)\in[n]
is such that

determines the value of
\pi^{sk}(q)

\displaystyle(\mathsf{Z}^{r_{0}}\otimes\mathsf{Z}^{r_{1}}\otimes\mathsf{X}^{r_{2}})\left((\left|00\right\rangle+\left|11\right\rangle)_{2,\mu}\otimes\left|+\right\rangle_{1-\mu}\right),

as desired. For every subsequent

5
Our QFHE scheme

In this section, we give a quantum (leveled) fully homomorphic encryption scheme, built in a black-box way from compact
c
lassical fully homomorphic encryption and dual-mode trapdoor functions.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\lambda
be the security parameter. Suppose there exists a dual-mode trapdoor function family
\mathcal{F}
. Suppose further that there exists a leveled fully homomorphic encryption scheme
\mathsf{HE}
with a decryption algorithm
\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Dec}
in
\mathsf{NC}_{1}
, that is,
\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Dec}
is computable by a boolean function fan-in
2
circuit of depth
O(\log(\lambda))
. Then, there exists a compact quantum leveled fully homomorphic encryption scheme
\mathsf{QFHE}
with a classical key generation procedure.

We will now describe our QFHE scheme.

Key Generation.

Let
L
be the maximum
\mathsf{T}
-depth of the circuits we want to homomorphically evaluate. Then, the client generates the keys as follows.

\underline{\mathsf{QFHE}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda},1^{L})}

1.

For
i=0
to
L
, independently generate key
(pk_{i},sk_{i},evk_{i})\leftarrow\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})
.

2.

Set the public key as
pk=(pk_{i})_{i=0}^{L}
.

3.

Set the secret key as
sk=(sk_{i})_{i=0}^{L}
.

4.

For
i=0
to
L-1
, compute
gk_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{GadgetKeyGen}(sk_{i},pk_{i+1})
. Compute an encryption of
sk_{i}
under
pk_{i+1}
. Set the evaluation key
evk
to be

\displaystyle\Bigl(gk_{i},\mathsf{HE}.\mathsf{Enc}_{pk_{i+1}}(sk_{i})\Bigr)_{i=0}^{L-1},(evk_{i})_{i=0}^{L}

Encryption.

The encryption procedure is identical to that of
[
DSS16
]
. For completeness, we descibe it here.

\underline{\mathsf{QFHE}.\mathsf{Enc}_{pk}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)}

1.

Let
\left|\psi\right\rangle
be an
n
-qubit input state. Sample uniformly random keys
x,z\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
. Parse the public key as
pk=(pk_{i})_{i=0}^{L}
.

2.

Output the quantum one-time-padded state
\mathsf{X}^{x}\mathsf{Z}^{z}\left|\psi\right\rangle
along with encryptions of
x,z
under the first public key
pk_{0}
.

Note that if the input is classical,
\left|\psi\right\rangle
is just a standard basis state and the encryption is also classical.

Circuit Evaluation.

The circuit evaluation procedure is almost the same as that of
[
DSS16
]
, except that the evaluator needs to construct the teleportation gadget each time for each
\mathsf{T}
gate it needs to perform. For completeness, we provide a description of the entire circuit evaluation procedure as in
[
DSS16
]
with this modification. Without loss of generality, the quantum circuit
C
we want to evaluate is written using a universal gate set consisting of the Clifford group generators
\{\mathsf{H},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{CNOT}\}
and the (non-Clifford)
\mathsf{T}
gate. Note that while the Clifford gates might affect multiple qubits at the same time, the
\mathsf{T}
gates only affect a single qubit.
Before evaluation of a gate
U
, the encryption of the
n
-qubit state
\rho
is of the form

\displaystyle X^{x}Z^{z}\rho X^{x}Z^{z}=(\mathsf{X}^{x_{1}}\mathsf{Z}^{z_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{X}^{x_{n}}\mathsf{Z}^{z_{n}})\rho(\mathsf{X}^{x_{1}}\mathsf{Z}^{z_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{X}^{x_{n}}\mathsf{Z}^{z_{n}})^{\dagger}.

The evaluating party also holds encryptions
\tilde{x}_{1}^{[i]},\ldots,\tilde{x}_{n}^{[i]}
and
\tilde{z}_{1}^{[i]},\ldots,\tilde{z}_{n}^{[i]}
of the one-time pad keys, with respect to the
i
th key set
pk_{i}
for some
i
(initially,
i=0
). The goal is to produce an encrypted state
(\mathsf{X}^{x^{\prime}}\mathsf{Z}^{z^{\prime}})U\rho U^{\dagger}(\mathsf{X}^{x^{\prime}}\mathsf{Z}^{z^{\prime}})^{\dagger}
, along with encryptions of the new keys
x^{\prime},z^{\prime}
of the quantum one-time pad.
If
U
is a Clifford gate, these encryptions will remain in the
i
th key set, but if
U
is a
\mathsf{T}
gate, all the encryptions are transferred to the
(i+1)
th key set.

\underline{\mathsf{QFHE}.\mathsf{Eval}(C,\rho,\tilde{x}^{[0]},\tilde{z}^{[0]},evk)}

•

If
U
is a Clifford gate, the evaluator simply applies
U
to the encrypted qubit. Since
U
commutes with the Pauli group, the evaluator only needs to update the encrypted keys in a straightforward way.

•

If
U=\mathsf{T}
, the evaluator applies the
\mathsf{T}
gate to the appropriate wire

. Supposing the qubit at wire
w
starts off in the state
(\mathsf{X}^{x_{w}}\mathsf{Z}^{z_{w}})\rho_{w}(\mathsf{X}^{x_{w}}\mathsf{Z}^{z_{w}})^{\dagger}
, after the
\mathsf{T}
gate is applied, it is now in the state

\displaystyle(\mathsf{P}^{x_{w}}\mathsf{X}^{x_{w}}\mathsf{Z}^{z_{w}}\mathsf{T})\rho_{w}(\mathsf{P}^{x_{w}}\mathsf{X}^{x_{w}}\mathsf{Z}^{z_{w}}\mathsf{T})^{\dagger}.

At this point, to correct the
\mathsf{P}^{x_{w}}

[
DSS16
]
use the quantum gadget
\Gamma_{pk_{i+1}}(sk_{i})
that is given as input to the evaluator. In our scheme, the evaluator has to construct the quantum gadget itself from the classical evaluation key, using the evaluation key

,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{pk_{i+1}}(sk_{i})\leftarrow\mathsf{RSPGenGadget}\Bigl(gk_{i},\tilde{t}^{[i+1]}_{i},pk_{i+1},evk_{i+1}\Bigr).

The

6
Instantiations

Our scheme in Section
5
allows us to build QFHE from generic classical FHE and dual-mode trapdoor functions. We now list the known instantiations of these primitives from various (concrete) cryptographic assumptions.

6.1
Instantiating post-quantum classical (leveled) FHE

Learning With Errors.

The Learning With Errors assumption
[
Reg09
]
can be used to contruct both (leveled) fully homomorphic encryption as well as dual-mode trapdoor functions.
Extensive research has been done on constructing FHE from LWE, culminating in the work of Brakerski and Vaikuntanathan
[
BV14
]
that builds FHE from the standard LWE assumption with polynomial modulus-to-noise ratio.

Ring Learning With Errors.

Brakerski, Gentry and Vaikuntanathan also give an FHE scheme from Ring-LWE
[
BGV14
]
, which can be directly imported into our QFHE scheme.

Post-Quantum FHE from Post-quantum Indistinguishability Obfuscation + perfectly re-randomizable encryption.

The work of Canetti, Lin, Tessaro and Vaikuntanathan
[
CLTV15
]
obtains leveled classical FHE from subexponentially-secure IO and perfectly re-rerandomizable encryption. Their scheme is post-quantum as long as it is instantiated with subexponentially-secure post-quantum IO and post-quantum secure perfectly re-randomizable encryption. Although we have candidates for post-quantum IO
[
GGH15
,
BGMZ18
,
BDGM20
,
WW21
]
, we do not have proofs of their post-quantum security under widely-believed assumptions. On the other hand, by an observation of Wichs
[
Wic24
]
, there exists an El Gamal-style perfectly rerandomizable encryption scheme based on group actions.

6.2
Instantiating dTLFs

Post-Quantum dTF from Isogeny-based group action assumptions.

Isogeny-based cryptography is a promising alternative to lattices for post-quantum cryptography. Initiated by Couveignes
[
Cou06
]
in 1997, it has led to new constructions of several cryptographic primitives such as collision-resistant hashing
[
CLG09
]
, key exchange
[
RS06
,
Sto10
]
, digital signatures
[
Sto09
]
and key escrow
[
Tes06
]
. More recent work has led to practical key exchange and public key encryption schemes, such as SIDH
[
JDF11
,
DFJP14
]
and CSIDH
[
CLM+18
]
. Although the attack by Castryck and Decru
[
CD23
]
(classically) breaks SIDH, CSIDH is still plausibly (post-quantum) secure.

Alamati, De Feo, Montgomery and Patranabis
[
ADFMP20
]
present a framework based on group actions with natural cryptographic hardness properties to model isogeny-based assumptions and schemes.
9
9

9

An important caveat is that the isogeny-based group actions have less-than-ideal algorithmic properties; for example, it is not always possible to efficiently compute the group action for any group element. One approach to fix this issue, taken by CSI-FiSh
[
BKV19
]
, is to perform a preprocessing step and compute the group structure in the form of relation lattice of low-norm generators.
This simplifying framework enables them to construct several new primitives, such as projective hashing, dual-mode PKE, two-message statistically sender-private OT, and a Naor-Reingold style PRF. They also introduce the Linear Hidden Shift assumption, based on which they construct symmetric KDM-secure encryption. Follow-up work of Alamati, Malavolta and Rahimi
[
AMR22
]
introduces the
Extended
Linear Hidden Shift assumption, and gives a construction of a (weak) trapdoor claw-free function from group actions under this assumption.

In Section
7
, we extend their work to construct dual-mode trapdoor functions (with negligible correctness error) from the same assumption.

Learning With Errors (Superpolynomial modulus-to-noise ratio)

Mahadev
[
Mah18
]
constructs a dual-mode noisy trapdoor claw-free function family assuming the hardness of Learning With Errors (although with superpolynomial modulus). We slightly adapt this construction to match our definition of dTFs. Assuming the hardness of LWE for superpolynomial modulus-to-noise ratio, we obtain a dTF family.

Lemma 9

Let

Key Generation.

The key

is generated as follows. First

[

\mu=1

is sampled uniformly, and

and
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}+\bar{\mathbf{e}}

Efficient Function Evaluation.

The function

Efficient state preparation.

Let
D=U(\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{q})\otimes\rho_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m},B_{1}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\sqrt{D(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e})}\left|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e}\right\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\frac{1}{q^{-n/2}}\cdot\sqrt{\rho_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m},B_{1}}(\mathbf{e})}\left|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e}\right\rangle\;,

In mode
\mu=0
, define

. In mode
\mu=1

and let

is not efficiently computable without the trapdoor, and therefore we cannot work with

exactly,

is very close to

, and that will be good enough. Indeed since
\|\bar{\mathbf{e}}\|\leq B_{2}\sqrt{m}

Given the trapdoor and some
