Taking snapshots from a stream
Dominik Bojko, Jacek Cichoń

Abstract
This work is devoted to a certain class of probabilistic snapshots for elements of the observed data stream. We show you how one can control their probabilistic properties and we show some potential applications. Our solution can be used to store information from the observed history with limited memory. It can be used for both web server applications and Ad hoc networks and, for example, for automatic taking snapshots from video stream online of unknown size.



1
Introduction

Suppose that we are observing a stream
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
of data. Our goal is to keep an element from this stream with a prescribed position. For example, we may want to keep the element
x_{i}
with index
i
close to
\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor
after reading first
n
elements from the stream. Of course this problem is trivial if we have a direct access to all elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. But in the case of large amount of data keeping all information into memory is expensive or undesirable. Suppose hence that our memory resources are limited.

In this article we investigates series of randomized procedures which allow us to choose elements located near the required position in the stream of data. All these procedures are based on the same schema. They only differs on a sequence
(\alpha_{n})
of probabilities which are used for control of changes of stored data. In each cases we will have
\alpha_{1}=1
. We will use three variables:
K
,
n
and
data
. Initially we put
K=0
,
n=0
and we set
data
as nil value:

Initialization: K:= 0; n:= 0; data:= nil;

After reading an element
x
from the stream we call the following update procedure:

procedure Update(x)
n:= n+1
if (random() <= a(n))
begin
K:= 1;
data: = x
end else begin
K:= K+1
end
end

In the procedure Update we used the function random() which is high quality pseudo-random generator of random reals from the interval
[0,1]
. The function
a(n)
represents the probabilities sequence
(\alpha_{n})
. We call the variable
K
a
probabilistic snapshot
from data stream.

Connection with reservoir sampling

This class of algorithms may be treated as a subclass of reservoir sampling algorithms with reservoir of size 1 (see
[
Tillé(2006)
]
).
Suppose that we use this procedure with the sequence
\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
. In this case we obtain the classical Jeffrey Vitter’s Algorithm R published in
[
Vitter(1985)
]
. Let
K_{n}
denotes value of the random variable
K
after reading
n
items from the stream. It is well known that in this case (i.e. when
\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
) we have
\Pr[K_{n}=i]=\frac{1}{n}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, i.e. that the random variable
K_{n}
has the uniform distribution on the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Therefore
E[K_{n}]=(n+1)/2
.

Applications

Here we show some possible applications of methods discussed in Section
4
:

•

The solution proposed in this paper may by used for storing fixed number of snapshots from an observed movie of unknown length. For example we may want to store short samples of the movie taken at times closed to
0
,
\frac{1}{10}T
,
\frac{2}{10}T
…
\frac{9}{10}T
,
T
from a movie of length
T
.

•

We may need a sample of data from times close to
n
,
n-C
,
n-2\cdot C
,…,
n-k\cdot C
, where
n
is an index of current item,
C
is a fixed distance and
k
is a reasonably small natural number.

•

We may need to observe a sample from stock market in such a way that the snapshots from the past should be less rare than snapshots from times close to the present.

1.1
Mathematical notations and background

•

We denote by
\mathrm{E}\left[X\right]
the expected value of the random variable
X
.

•

Let us recall that a discrete random variable
X
has geometric distribution with parameter
p\in[0,1]
(
X\sim\mathrm{Geo}(p)
)
if
P[X=k]=(1-p)^{k-1}p
for
k\geq 1
. If
X\sim\mathrm{Geo}(p)
then
\mathrm{E}\left[X\right]=\frac{1}{p}
.

•

A random variable
Y
has an exponential distribution with parameter
g
(
Y\sim\mathrm{Exp}\left(\lambda\right)
) if its support is
[0,\infty)
and

\Pr[Y>x]=e^{-\lambda x}
for each
x\geq 0
. If
Y\sim\mathrm{Exp}\left(\lambda\right)
then
\mathrm{E}\left[Y\right]=\frac{1}{\lambda}
.

•

A random variable
Z
has the Beta distribution with parameters
a,b>0
(
Z\sim\mathrm{B}(a,b)
) if its support is
[0,1]
and
its density is given by the function
f(x)=\frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b-1}
, where
\Gamma(x)
is the standard generalization of the factorial function.
If
Z\sim\mathrm{B}(a,b)
then
\mathrm{E}\left[Z\right]=\frac{a}{a+b}
.

•

A sequence
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
of real-valued random variables is said to converge in distribution if
\lim_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(x)=F(x)
,
for every number
x\in\mathbb{R}
at which the function
F
is continuous, where
F_{n}
and
F
are the cumulative distribution functions of random variables
X_{n}
and
X
, respectively.

•

We denote by
H_{n}
the
n
-th harmonic number, i.e.
H_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i}
. We will use the following well known approximation
H_{n}=\ln(n)+\gamma+\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
, where
\gamma\approx 0.577
is the Euler - Mascheroni constant.

•

We denote by
H_{n,a}
the
n
-th harmonic number in power
a
, i.e.
H_{n,a}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{a}}
. If
a\neq 1
and positive, then the following formula is useful:

\displaystyle H_{n,a}-H_{k,a}=\frac{n^{1-a}-k^{1-a}}{1-a}+f^{(a)}(k)+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)~,

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where
|f^{(a)}(k)|\leq k^{-a}
.

•

In this paper
\zeta
is Riemann’s Zeta function, defined as
\zeta(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-z}
(for
Re(z)>1
).

•

By
\mathrm{Ei}(x)
we denote Exponential Integral, defined as
-\int_{-x}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t}dt}{t}
. By
mathrm{E}_{1}(x)
we denote the First Exponential Integral, defined as
\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-xt}dt}{t}
. These functions have foregoing property
\mathrm{Ei}(x)=-\mathrm{E}_{1}(-x)
.

•

We will use the following inequalities (extension of the classical Weierstarass product inequality):

1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-x_{i})<1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}x_{i}x_{j}.

which holds for any sequence
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
of real numbers from the interval
[0,1]
(see
[
Klamkin and Newman(1970)
]
).

2
General Properties

Let us fix sequence
\alpha_{n}
of reals from interval
[0,1]
such that
\alpha_{1}=1
.
Let us consider the sequence
K_{n}
of consecutive integers tracing the value of the variable
K
during the run of the Algorithm 1 with controlling sequence
\alpha_{n}
. Clearly we have
1\leq K_{n}\leq n
for each
n
.

Notice that
\Pr[K_{1}=1]=1
,
\Pr[K_{n}=1]=\alpha_{n}
and

\Pr[K_{n+1}=k+1]=(1-\alpha_{n})\Pr[K_{n}=k]

(1)

The following formula may be derived from the previous one

\Pr[K_{n}=k]=\alpha_{n-k+1}\prod_{i=n-k+2}^{n}(1-\alpha_{i})~.

(2)

Moreover

\Pr[K_{n}\geq k]=\prod_{i=n-k+2}^{n}(1-\alpha_{i})

(3)

Example 1
.

Let us consider the case when
\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
. We claim that
\Pr[K_{n}=k]=\frac{1}{n}
for each
n
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Indeed,
\Pr[K_{1}=1]=1
. Let us assume that
\Pr[K_{n}=k]=\frac{1}{n}
for each
k
.
Then
\Pr[K_{n+1}=1]=\frac{1}{n+1}
and

\Pr[K_{n+1}=k]=(1-\alpha_{n+1})\Pr[K_{n}=k-1]=\left(1-\frac{1}{n+1}\right)\frac{1}{n}=\frac{n}{n+1}\frac{1}{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}~,

for
k\in\{2,\ldots,n+1\}
.
Notice that we just reconstructed the mentioned result from
[
Alon et al.(1999)Alon, Matias, and
Szegedy
]
: the random variable
K_{n}
in this case is uniformly distributed on the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Therefore
\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]=\frac{1+n}{2}
, so the expected value of
K_{n}
is precisely the middle of the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

The sequence
\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]
satisfies simple linear first order difference equation:

Theorem 1
.

\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n+1}\right]=1+(1-\alpha_{n+1})\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]

Proof.

Let
\beta_{n}=1-\alpha_{n}
. The proof follows directly from the recurrence (
1
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n+1}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}k\cdot p^{n+1}_{k}=\alpha_{n}+\sum_{k=2}^{n+1}k\cdot p^{n+1}_{k}=\alpha_{n}+\sum_{k=2}^{n+1}k\cdot(1-\alpha_{n+1})p^{n}_{k-1}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}+\beta_{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(k+1)\cdot p^{n}_{k}=\alpha_{n}+\beta_{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\cdot p^{n}_{k}+\beta_{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\cdot p^{n}_{k}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}+\beta_{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\cdot p^{n}_{k}+\beta_{n+1}=1+(1-\alpha_{n+1})\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]~.

∎

We shall also use the variable
L_{n}=(n+1)-K_{n}
. From formula (
2
) we immediately get

\Pr[L_{n}=k]=\alpha_{k}\prod_{i=0}^{n-k-1}(1-\alpha_{n-i})

(4)

2.1
Monotonicity of expected value, related to
\alpha_{n}

Let’s consider two sequences of probabilities of a saving of
n
-th data:
(\alpha_{n})
and
(\alpha^{\prime}_{n})
, one majorized by another, in particular:
(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad 0\leq\alpha_{n}\leq\alpha^{\prime}_{n}\leq 1
.
Random processes
(K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(K^{\prime}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are corresponding to sequences
(\alpha_{n})
and
(\alpha^{\prime}_{n})
respectively, f.e.
P(K_{n}=1)=\alpha_{n}
and
P(K^{\prime}_{n}=1)=\alpha^{\prime}_{n}
.
We show that then
\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]\geq\mathrm{E}\left[K^{\prime}_{n}\right]
.
The proof is a simple induction —
\mathrm{E}\left[K_{1}\right]=\mathrm{E}\left[K^{\prime}_{1}=1\right]
for any sequences of probabilities.
Now, assume that
\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]\geq\mathrm{E}\left[K^{\prime}_{n}\right]
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then
\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n+1}\right]=1+(1-\alpha_{n+1})\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]\geq 1+(1-\alpha^{\prime}_{n+1})\mathrm{E}\left[K^{\prime}_{n}\right]=\mathrm{E}\left[K^{\prime}_{n+1}\right]
.

3
Special cases

In this section we will consider some examples of sequences
(\alpha_{n})
which may have applications for controlling the history of a massive streams of data.
The case
\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
was analyzed in Example
1
and the generated pointers (i.e. the values of the random variables
K_{n}
) may be treated as controllers of behavior of a stream at position
\frac{n}{2}
after reading
n
items.
In a series of four subsections we shall analyze behavior of the random variable
K_{n}
for sequences of the form

\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\}
for various fixed parameters
\alpha
. This is a summary of our results:

•

if
\alpha=0
and
g\in(0,1)
then the random variable
K_{n}
converges in distribution to the geometric distribution
\mathrm{Geo}(g)
, hence
\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]\sim\frac{1}{g}

•

if
\alpha\in(0,1)
then the random variable
\frac{K_{n}}{n^{\alpha}}
converges in distribution to exponential distribution
\mathrm{Exp}(g)
, hence
\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]\sim\frac{n^{\alpha}}{g}

•

if
\alpha=1
then the random variable
\frac{K_{n}}{n}
converges in distribution to the beta distribution
\mathrm{Beta}(1,g)
, hence
\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]\sim\frac{n}{g+1}

•

if
\alpha\in(1,2)
then
\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]=\frac{g}{2-\alpha}n^{2-\alpha}+\mathrm{O}\left(\max\{1,1\!\!1_{\{\frac{3}{2}\}}(\alpha)\ln{n},n^{3-2\alpha}\}\right)

•

if
\alpha=2
then
\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]=g\ln(n)+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)

•

if
\alpha>2
then
\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]=\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)
.

3.1
Fixed value

Let us fix a real number
a>1
and let
\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{a}
for each
n>1
. In this case we have a closed formula for
E[K_{n}]
,
namely, from Eq. (
2
) we immediately get

\Pr[K_{n}=k]=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{a}(1-\frac{1}{a})^{k-1}&:1\leq k<n\\
(1-\frac{1}{a})^{n-1}&:k=n\end{cases}

From this formula we deduce that

1.

The sequence
(K_{n})
of random variables converges in distribution to the geometrical distribution with parameter
\frac{1}{a}
.

2.

E[K_{n}]=a\left(1-\left(\frac{a-1}{a}\right)^{n}\right)

Therefore the generated pointer may be used for controlling a behavior of a stream at a fixed position in the past.

3.2
Sublinear Case

In this section we consider the case when
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\}
for some fixed
g>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
We shall show that the normalized random variable
\frac{K_{n}}{n^{\alpha}}
converges in distribution to the exponential
\mathrm{Exp}\left(g\right)
distribution.

Theorem 2
.

Let
g>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Let
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\}
and let
x>0
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n^{\alpha}}\leq x\right]=1-e^{-gx}~.

Proof.

Let
k=\lfloor n^{\alpha}x\rfloor
. Using formula (
3
) we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n^{\alpha}}>x\right]=\Pr[K_{n}>n^{\alpha}x]=\Pr[K_{n}>k]=\prod_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\left(1-\frac{g}{i^{\alpha}}\right)

(for sufficiently large
n
). Therefore

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n^{\alpha}}>x\right]<\left(1-\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\right)^{k}\leq\left(1-\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\right)^{n^{\alpha}x-1}

and

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n^{\alpha}}>x\right]>\left(1-\frac{g}{(n-k+1)^{\alpha}}\right)^{k}>\left(1-\frac{g}{(n-\lfloor n^{\alpha}x\rfloor)^{\alpha}}\right)^{n^{\alpha}x}=

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{g/(1-\lfloor n^{\alpha}x\rfloor/n)}{n^{\alpha}}\right)^{n^{\alpha}x}~,

so we see that both bounds on
\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n^{\alpha}}>x\right]
converges to the same limit
e^{-gx}
when
n
tends to infinity.
∎

Theorem 3
.

If
g>0
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\}
then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n^{\alpha}}\right]=\frac{1}{g}
.

Proof.

From Theorem
2
and Fatou’s Lemma we get
\frac{1}{g}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n^{\alpha}}\right]
.
On the other hand, inequality (
5
) bears (for sufficiently large
n>g^{1/\alpha}
):

\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n^{\alpha}}\right]=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n^{\alpha}}>t\right]dt\leq\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\right)^{n^{\alpha}t-1}dt=-\frac{1}{(n^{\alpha}-g)\ln(1-\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{1}{g}~.

∎

Corollary 1
.

If
g>0
and
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n}\}
then
\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]=\frac{n^{\alpha}}{g}+\mathrm{o}(n^{\alpha})
.

Remark

We know a more precise results in some special cases. For example, if
\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
then

\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]=\sqrt{n}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{8n}+\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\right)
.

3.3
Linear Case

In this section we consider the case when
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n}\}
for some fixed
g>0
.
We shall show that the normalized random variable
\frac{K_{n}}{n}
converges in distribution to the Beta(1,g) distribution.

Theorem 4
.

Let
g>0
. Let
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n}\}
and let
x\in(0,1)
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n}\leq x\right]=1-(1-x)^{g}~.

Proof.

Let
k=\lfloor nx\rfloor
. Then we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n}>x\right]=\Pr[K_{n}>nx]=\Pr[K_{n}>k]=\prod_{i=n-k+1}^{n}(1-\alpha_{i})~.

Therefore, for sufficiently large
n
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n}>x\right]=\prod_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\left(1-\frac{g}{i}\right)~.

Hence

\displaystyle\ln\left(\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n}>x\right]\right)=-\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\ln\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{g}{i}}\right)=-\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\sum_{a\geq 1}\frac{1}{a}\left(\frac{g}{i}\right)^{a}=

\displaystyle-g\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\frac{1}{i}-\sum_{a\geq 2}\frac{g^{a}}{a}\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{a}}~.

Notice that
\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\frac{1}{i}=H_{n}-H_{n-k}
, and

-g(H_{n}-H_{n-k})=-g\left(\ln(n)-\ln(n-k)+\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)+\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n-\lfloor nx\rfloor}\right)\right)=\ln\left(\frac{n-\lfloor nx\rfloor}{n}\right)^{g}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

Therefore
\lim_{n\to\infty}(-g(H_{n}-H_{n-\lfloor nx\rfloor}))=\ln((1-x)^{g})
. Let

A_{n,k}=\sum_{a\geq 2}\frac{g^{a}}{a}\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{a}}
.
Then

\displaystyle 0<A_{n,k}<\sum_{a\geq 2}g^{a}\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i^{a}}=\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{\infty}\sum_{a\geq 2}\frac{g^{a}}{i^{a}}=\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{\infty}\frac{g^{2}}{i^{2}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{g}{i}}<2g^{2}\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i^{2}}

(the last inequality holds for
n>\frac{2g}{1-x}
), hence

A_{n,k}<2g^{2}\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i(i-1)}=\frac{2g^{2}}{n-k}=\frac{2g^{2}}{n-\lfloor nx\rfloor}=\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)~.

Thus

\ln\left(\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n}>x\right]\right)=\ln\left(1-\frac{\lfloor nx\rfloor}{n}\right)^{g}+\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

Therefore

\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n}>x\right]=\left(1-\frac{\lfloor nx\rfloor}{n}\right)^{g}e^{\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)}=\left(1-\frac{\lfloor nx\rfloor}{n}\right)^{g}+\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)~,

so
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n}>x\right]=(1-x)^{g}
.
∎

Corollary 2
.

If
g>0
and
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n}\}
then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n}\right]=\frac{1}{g+1}
.

Proof.

Notice that
\frac{K_{n}}{n}\leq 1
, hence the sequence
(\frac{K_{n}}{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, therefore the convergence in distribution of the sequence
(\frac{K_{n}}{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to a random variable
Y
with Beta(1,g) distribution implies the convergence of moments (see e.g.
[
Billingsley(2012)
]
), hence

\lim_{n}\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}x\frac{d}{dx}\left(1-(1-x)^{g}\right)dx=\frac{1}{1+g}~.

∎

Corollary 3
.

If
g>0
and
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n}\}
then
\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]=\frac{n}{g+1}+\mathrm{o}(n)
.

Remark

A slightly more complicated calculus shows that in the case considered in this section we have

\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]=\frac{n+1}{g+1}+\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{g}}\right)
.

3.4
Subquadratic Case

In this section we consider the case when
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\}
for some fixed
g>0
and
\alpha\in(1,2)
. We introduce an auxilliary random variable
L_{n}:=n+1-K_{n}
.
Let’s define
g_{0}=\left\lceil g^{\frac{1}{a}}\right\rceil-1
.
Realize that
\alpha_{k}=\frac{g}{k^{\alpha}}
for
k\geq g_{0}+1
. Thus we briefly see that
Pr\left[L_{n}=k\right]=0
, whenever
k\leq g_{0}
and

\displaystyle\Pr\left[L_{n}=k\right]=\displaystyle{\frac{g}{k^{\alpha}}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{n-k-1}\left(1-\frac{g}{(n-i)^{\alpha}}\right)}~

otherwise.

In foregoing calculations let
k>g_{0}
.
At commencement we approach a lower bound, using standard Weierstrass’ product inequality (ref??):

\displaystyle\Pr\left[L_{n}=k\right]\geq\frac{g}{k^{\alpha}}\left(1-\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{n}\frac{g}{i^{\alpha}}\right)=\frac{g}{k^{\alpha}}\left(1-g(H_{n,\alpha}-H_{k,\alpha})\right)~.

(5)

To find the upper limitation, we apply more precise extension of Weierstrass’ product inequality (ref??):

\displaystyle\Pr\left[L_{n}=k\right]\leq\frac{g}{k^{\alpha}}\left(1-\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{n}\frac{g}{i^{\alpha}}+\sum_{i=k+1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\frac{g^{2}}{(ij)^{\alpha}}\right)\leq\frac{g}{k^{\alpha}}\left(1-\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{n}\frac{g}{i^{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\frac{g^{2}}{(ij)^{\alpha}}\right)~.

(6)

Hence, by repeating the same trick as in (
5
) we attain:

\displaystyle\Pr\left[L_{n}=k\right]\leq\frac{g}{k^{\alpha}}\left(1-g(H_{n,\alpha}-H_{k,\alpha})+\frac{g^{2}}{2}(H_{n,\alpha}-H_{k,\alpha})^{2}\right)~.

We would like to find close confinements for an expected value of
L_{n}
.

We consider the limitation which is easier to analyze —

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=g_{0}+1}^{n}\frac{g}{k^{\alpha-1}}\left(1-g(H_{n,\alpha}-H_{k,\alpha})\right)=g(H_{n,\alpha-1}-H_{g_{0},\alpha-1})-g^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\frac{k^{1-\alpha}-n^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha-1}+f^{(a)}(k)+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)}{k^{\alpha-1}}

\displaystyle\geq g\frac{n^{2-\alpha}}{2-\alpha}-g^{2}\left[\frac{(H_{n,2\alpha-2}-H_{g_{0},2\alpha-2})}{\alpha-1}-\frac{n^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha-1}(H_{n,\alpha-1}-H_{g_{0},\alpha-1})\right]+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)\geq

\displaystyle\geq\frac{gn^{2-\alpha}}{2-\alpha}-g^{2}\left[-\frac{n^{3-2\alpha}}{(\alpha-1)(2-\alpha)}+\frac{(H_{n,2\alpha-2}-H_{g_{0},2\alpha-2})}{\alpha-1}\right]+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)=

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{gn^{2-\alpha}}{2-\alpha}-\frac{g^{2}n^{3-2\alpha}}{(3-2\alpha)(\alpha-1)}+\frac{g^{2}n^{3-2\alpha}}{(\alpha-1)(2-\alpha)}+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)=\frac{gn^{2-\alpha}}{2-\alpha}-\frac{g^{2}n^{3-2\alpha}}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\alpha)}+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)&;\quad\text{for }\alpha<\frac{3}{2},\\
\frac{gn^{2-\alpha}}{2-\alpha}-\frac{g^{2}H_{n}}{\alpha-1}+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)&;\quad\text{for }\alpha=\frac{3}{2},\\
\frac{gn^{2-\alpha}}{2-\alpha}+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)&;\quad\text{for }\alpha>\frac{3}{2},\end{array}\right.

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

The second one can be attained the same way:

\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{gn^{2-a}}{2-a}-\frac{g^{2}n^{3-2a}}{(3-2a)(2-a)}+\frac{g^{2}n^{4-3a}}{(4-3a)(3-2a)(2-a)}+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)&;\quad\text{for }a<\frac{4}{3},\\
\frac{gn^{2-a}}{2-a}-\frac{g^{2}n^{3-2a}}{(3-2a)(2-a)}+\frac{g^{3}H_{n}}{2(a-1)^{2}}+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)&;\quad\text{for }a=\frac{4}{3},\\
\frac{gn^{2-a}}{2-a}-\frac{g^{2}n^{3-2a}}{(3-2a)(2-a)}+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)&;\quad\text{for }\frac{4}{3}<a<\frac{3}{2},\\
\frac{gn^{2-a}}{2-a}-\frac{g^{2}H_{n}}{a-1}+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)&;\quad\text{for }a=\frac{3}{2},\\
\frac{gn^{2-a}}{2-a}+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)&;\quad\text{for }a>\frac{3}{2},\end{array}\right.

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Resuming up all the cases, we obtain:

Corollary 4
.

If
g>0
is fixed,
\alpha\in(1,2)
and
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\}
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{gn^{2-\alpha}}{2-\alpha}-\frac{g^{2}n^{3-2\alpha}}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\alpha)}+\mathrm{O}\left(\max\{\ln n,n^{4-3\alpha}\}\right)&;\quad\text{for }\alpha<\frac{3}{2},\\
\frac{gn^{2-\alpha}}{2-\alpha}-\frac{g^{2}H_{n}}{\alpha-1}+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)&;\quad\text{for }\alpha=\frac{3}{2},\\
\frac{gn^{2-\alpha}}{2-\alpha}+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)&;\quad\text{for }\alpha>\frac{3}{2},\end{array}\right.

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Remark

A better generalization of Weierstrass’ product inequality is known (ref??).
Notice that it could be helpful to get a more precise asymptotics for
\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]
, when
1<\alpha\leq\frac{4}{3}
. Nevertheless, it won’t be thorough due to the approximations of the generalized harmonic numbers.
Unfortunately this calculation is very unpleasant.

3.5
Quadratic Case

In this section we consider the case when
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n^{2}}\}
for some fixed
g>0
.
For computational purpose let introduce some auxiliary symbols:
g_{0}:=\sqrt{g}
and
h:=\lceil g_{0}\rceil
. We analyze random variable given by
L_{n}:=n+1-K_{n}
. Our main goal is to achieve an approximation for its expected value.
It’s easy to realize that
\Pr\left[L_{n}\geq k\right]=1
, when
k<h
. Otherwise, we use (
3
) to reach
\Pr\left[L_{n}\geq k\right]=1-\prod\limits_{i=k}^{n}\left(1-\frac{g}{i^{2}}\right)
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
g>0
and
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n^{2}}\}
. Then
\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]=g\ln{n}+O(1)
.

Proof.

Commence with some simple facts. The first uses the formula for expected value for non-negative discrete random variables:

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\Pr\left[L_{n}\geq k\right]=h-1+\sum\limits_{k=h}^{n}\left(1-\frac{g}{k^{2}}\right)~.

(7)

The next two apply standard integral inequalities to find some confinements for sums:

\displaystyle-((n+1)^{-1}-k^{-1})=\int\limits_{k}^{n+1}x^{-2}dx\leq\sum\limits_{i=k}^{n}k^{-2}\leq\int\limits_{k-1}^{n}x^{-2}dx=-(n^{-1}-(k-1)^{-1})~

(8)

and

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=k}^{n}e^{-\frac{g}{k}}\leq\int\limits_{h}^{n+1}e^{-\frac{g}{x}}dx~.

(9)

In the last formula we received integral, which isn’t elementary function.
In order to use it, we need some additional properties of related known integrals.
It’s known that

\int e^{-\frac{1}{x}}dx=\mathrm{Ei}\left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)+xe^{-\frac{1}{x}}~,

so

\displaystyle\int\limits_{h}^{n+1}e^{-\frac{g}{x}}dx=g\int\limits_{\frac{h}{g}}^{\frac{n+1}{g}}e^{-\frac{1}{x}}dx=g\left(\mathrm{Ei}\left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)+xe^{-\frac{1}{x}}\right)\bigg|_{\frac{h}{g}}^{\frac{n+1}{g}}~.

(10)

We also adduce the series expansion of exponential integral

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{1}(x)=-\gamma-\ln{x}-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}x^{k}}{k!k}~.

(11)

Now, we find an upper bound for
\Pr\left[L_{n}\geq k\right]
(for
k\geq h
), using standard Weierstrass’ inequality and (
8
):

1-\prod\limits_{i=k}^{n}\left(1-\frac{g}{i^{2}}\right)\leq 1-\left(1-\sum\limits_{i=k}^{n}\frac{g}{i^{2}}\right)\leq g\left(\frac{1}{k-1}-\frac{1}{n}\right)~.

From above formula and (
7
), we obtain:

\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]\leq h-1+g\sum\limits_{k=h}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{k-1}-\frac{1}{n}\right)=gH_{n}-gH_{h-2}+h-1-g+\mathrm{O}\left(n^{-1}\right)=g\ln n+\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)~.

The second limitation is more difficult.
After removing the remainder of Taylor’s formula for the function
e^{x}
, the inequality (
8
) provides:

\Pr\left[L_{n}\geq k\right]\geq 1-\prod\limits_{i=k}^{n}\left(1-\frac{g}{i^{2}}\right)\geq 1-\prod\limits_{i=k}^{n}e^{-\frac{g}{i^{2}}}=1-e^{-g\sum\limits_{i=k}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{2}}}\geq e^{\frac{g}{n+1}-\frac{g}{k}}~.

Then we use the above part, the equality (
7
) once again and finally
(
9
) to attain:

\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]\geq h-1+\sum\limits_{k=h}^{n}\left(1-e^{\frac{g}{n+1}-\frac{g}{k}}\right)=n-e^{\frac{g}{n+1}}\sum\limits_{k=h}^{n}e^{-\frac{g}{k}}\geq n-e^{\frac{g}{n+1}}\int\limits_{h}^{n+1}e^{-\frac{g}{x}}dx~.

Remind that
\mathrm{Ei}(-y)=-\mathrm{E}_{1}(y)
and by proceeding with (
10
) and (
11
) we procure:

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\geq n-ge^{\frac{g}{n+1}}\left[-\mathrm{E}_{1}\left(\frac{g}{n+1}\right)+\mathrm{E}_{1}\left(\frac{g}{h}\right)+\frac{n+1}{g}e^{-\frac{g}{n+1}}-\frac{h}{g}e^{-\frac{g}{h}}\right]

\displaystyle=n-ge^{\frac{g}{n+1}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{g}{n+1}\right)-\ln\left(\frac{g}{h}\right)+\frac{n+1}{g}e^{-\frac{g}{n+1}}-\frac{h}{g}e^{-\frac{g}{h}}+c_{g}+\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-1+g\left(1+\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)(\ln(n+1)-\ln(h))-gc_{g}+he^{-\frac{g}{h}}+\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)=g\ln n+\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)~,

where
c_{g}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}\left(\frac{g}{h}\right)^{k}}{k!k}
.
∎

Remark

It’s possible to find neat limitations for
c_{g}
, because it’s the alternating series. If we denote
n
-th partial sum of this series by
S_{n}
, then
c_{g}
is between
S_{r}
and
S_{r+1}
, where
r
is a natural number, which satisfies
\frac{g}{h}^{r}<r!r
.

Remark

The case
a=2
is recognized as some kind of exception (due to appearance of
H_{n}
). We noticed that in fact, some pattern for expected value of
L_{n}
extrapolates from the case, when
1<a<2
on
a=2
.

3.6
Superquadratic Case

Finally, in this section we consider the case when
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\}
for some fixed
g>0
and
\alpha>2
. This case is the least interesting in our purpose, but we concisely consider
a>2
for completeness. Again, we apply random variable
L_{n}:=n+1-K_{n}
. The foregoing obvious fact is fulfilled:

\displaystyle\Pr\left[L_{n}=k\right]=\alpha_{k}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{n-k-1}(1-\alpha_{n-i})\leq\alpha_{k}=\min\left\{1,\dfrac{g}{k^{\alpha}}\right\}~.

The foregoing formula provides an easy majorization for expected value:

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[L_{n}\right]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\left\lfloor g^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right\rfloor}k+gH_{n,\alpha-1}<\frac{g^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}+1}{2}g^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}+g\zeta(\alpha-1)<\infty~.

Corollary 5
.

If
\alpha>2
,
g>0
and
\alpha_{n}=\min\{1,\frac{g}{n^{\alpha}}\}
, then
\mathrm{E}\left[K_{n}\right]=n-\mathrm{O}\left(1\right)
.

Remark

L_{n}
is a number of update of algorithm which saved the last snapshot. We should expect that our algorithm save only data from the very beginning. In this case, the algorithm is redundant, so we don’t consider this case anymore.
Nevertheless this is quite interesting mathematical problem to study.

4
Applications

In this chapter we will discuss three examples of applications discussed in the previous chapters of probabilistic pointers.

4.1
Linear sampling

Let us assume that we are observing data stream and that after reading
n
-th item we would like to have access to elements laying near points
\{\frac{k}{10}\cdot n:k=0,\ldots,10\}
. Of course, there is no problem with the element laying near
\frac{0}{n}\cdot n
- it is sufficient to store the first element from the stream. As an element laying near
\frac{10}{10}\cdot n
we may take the current item.
So we must propose some mechanism for dealing with remaining
9
points.

Let us consider a series
K_{n}^{1},\ldots K_{n}^{M}
of independent random variables generated by the sequence
\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
.
We know (see
3.3
) that this is a sequence of independent random variables uniformly distributed in the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Let
X_{n}=\{K_{n}^{1},\ldots K_{n}^{M}\}
.
Let us fix some
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{10}
. Let
I_{k}=\{a\in\mathbb{N}:|\frac{a}{n}-\frac{k}{10}|<\varepsilon\}
.
Let us observe that
\Pr[I_{k}\cap X_{n}=\emptyset]\approx(1-2\varepsilon)^{M}
. This approximation is accurate for large
n
. So, for simplicity we shall assume that we have an equality. Therefore

\Pr[\bigvee_{k=1}^{9}(I_{k}\cap X_{n}=\emptyset)]\leq 9\cdot(1-2\varepsilon)^{M}~.

The solution of inequality
9\cdot(1-2\varepsilon)^{M}\leq\eta
is given by

M\geq\frac{\log\left(\frac{\eta}{9}\right)}{\log(1-2\epsilon)}
. Putting in this formula
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{100}
and

\eta=10^{-10}
we get
M\geq 1248.5
. Therefore, if we take
M=1250
snapshots then

\Pr[\bigwedge_{k=1}^{9}(I_{k}\cap X_{n}\neq\emptyset)]>1-\frac{1}{10^{10}}~.

Hence, with a very high probability for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,10\}
we are able to choose a point from the set
X_{n}
which approximates
\frac{kn}{10}
with precision
1\%
.

We perform numerical experiments with this a collection of 1250 probabilistic snapshots

\mathcal{K}_{n}=(K_{n}^{1},\ldots K_{n}^{1250})
evolving independently according to the sequence
\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
. In the experiment whose results are shown in Fig.
1
after each call to the procedure Update we calculated the quality of set of snapshots defined as

Q(\mathcal{K}_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\max\left\{\min\left\{\left|\frac{kn}{10}-K_{n}^{j}\right|:j=1,\ldots,1250\right\}:k=1,\ldots,9\right\}~.

Figure 1:
Quality of approximation of points
\frac{kn}{10}
,
k=1,\ldots,9
, by a collection of 1250 snapshots.

We may observe in this figure several regularities which are connected with the specific method of generation of sequences
\mathcal{K}_{n}
(for example, it is clear that sequences
\mathcal{K}_{n}
and
\mathcal{K}_{n+1}
are not independent of each other). We can see that the predicted
1\%
precision has been achieved.

Bitcoin capitalization

We used bitcoin cap data from

.
