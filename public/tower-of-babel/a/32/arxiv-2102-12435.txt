𝑻 ​ 𝑻 ¯ T\overline{T} deformations of non-relativistic models
Chantelle Esper, and Sergey Frolov

Abstract
The light-cone gauge approach to T ​ T ¯ T\overline{T} deformed models is used to derive the T ​ T ¯ T\overline{T} deformed matrix nonlinear Schrödinger equation, the Landau–Lifshitz equation, and the Gardner equation. Properties of one-soliton solutions of the T ​ T ¯ T\overline{T} deformed nonlinear Schrödinger and Korteweg–de Vries equations are discussed in detail. The NLS soliton exhibits the recently discussed phenomenon of widening/narrowing width of particles under the T ​ T ¯ T\overline{T} deformation. However, whether the soliton’s size is increasing or decreasing depends not only on the sign of the deformation parameter but also on soliton and potential parameters. The T ​ T ¯ T\overline{T} deformed KdV equation admits a one-parameter family of one-soliton solutions in addition to the usual velocity parameter. The extra parameter modifies the properties of the soliton, in particular, it appears in the dispersion relation.

Many non-relativistic models, for example the nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation, the Landau-Lifshitz (LL) equation and the Gardner equation which is a combination of the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) and the modified KdV (mKdV) equation, play important roles in describing various phenomena in nonlinear optics, hydrodynamics, plasma physics and condensed matter physics. In this paper we derive the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed actions for the matrix NLS equation, the LL equation and the Gardner equation by using the light-cone gauge approach. The resulting actions are written in the first-order form and depend on auxiliary fields. For the deformed matrix NLS and LL models, the auxiliary fields satisfy algebraic equations of motion and can be eliminated leading to Nambu-Goto type actions. The
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Gardner model is more involved because the auxiliary fields appear in the deformed action together with their space derivatives, and it is unlikely that there exists a local deformed action depending only on the physical field. Moreover, the Gardner field which appears in the Gardner equation is not the physical field of the Gardner model action but one of the auxiliary fields.

We then find one-soliton solutions of the deformed NLS and KdV models. The deformed NLS soliton clearly exhibits the general phenomenon of widening/narrowing the width of particles under the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation recently discussed in
Cardy2020

{T\overline{T}}
deformed KdV soliton, we find a one-parameter family of solutions where the extra parameter is related to the time dependence of the physical field at space infinities. If one fixes the dependence, then the extra parameter can be interpreted as the parameter of the deformation by the time component of the conserved current due to the invariance of the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Gardner model under constant shifts of its physical field. The parameter modifies the properties of the soliton, in particular, it appears in the dispersion relation.
All these solutions reduce to the usual KdV soliton once one takes the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation parameter to 0.

The

2
Lagrangians of
{T\overline{T}}
deformed models

All models we are going to discuss in this paper are
{T\overline{T}}
deformations of a seed model described by the
following action

\displaystyle S_{0}=\int\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}t\,{\cal L}_{0}\,,\quad{\cal L}_{0}=P_{a}^{t}(\Psi)\partial_{t}\Psi^{a}+P_{a}^{x}(\Psi)\partial_{x}\Psi^{a}-{\rm V}(\Psi)\,.

(2.1)

Here
\Psi^{a}\,,\,a=1,\ldots,n
are bosonic and fermionic fields which can be real or complex. If a field is complex then the set
(\Psi^{a})
also includes its complex conjugate field.
P_{a}^{t}
,
P_{a}^{x}
and
V
are chosen so that the action (
2.1
) is real and Grassmann even but otherwise they are arbitrary functions of the fields
\Psi^{a}
. The seed action is written in the first-order formalism with respect to both time and space, and as a result many of the fields are non-dynamical. If each
\Psi^{a}
belongs to a Lorentz group representation and
P_{a}^{t}
,
P_{a}^{x}
belong to the conjugate representation, and
V
is a Lorentz scalar then the seed model is Lorentz invariant.

The light-cone gauge approach to
{T\overline{T}}
deformed models developed in
SF19a
then leads to the following deformed Lagrangian

\displaystyle{\cal L}={{\rm K}^{t}_{t}+{\rm K}^{x}_{x}-{\rm V}+\alpha({\rm K}^{t}_{t}{\rm K}^{x}_{x}-{\rm K}^{t}_{x}{\rm K}^{x}_{t})\over 1+\alpha{\rm V}}={{\cal L}_{0}-{\alpha\over 2}\epsilon^{\gamma\rho}\epsilon_{\mu\nu}{\rm K}^{\mu}_{\gamma}{\rm K}^{\nu}_{\rho}\over 1+\alpha{\rm V}}\,,

(2.2)

where

\displaystyle{\rm K}^{t}_{\gamma}\equiv P_{a}^{t}\partial_{\gamma}\Psi^{a}\,,\quad{\rm K}^{x}_{\gamma}\equiv P_{a}^{x}\partial_{\gamma}\Psi^{a}\,,\quad\gamma=t,x\,,

(2.3)

and the skew-symmetric Levi-Civita symbol is defined by
\epsilon^{01}=\epsilon^{tx}=1=\epsilon_{xt}=\epsilon_{10}
.
To get (
2.2
) from the Lagrangian (3.53) in
SF19a
one should make the following replacements in (3.53):
\Psi_{\pm}\to\Psi
,
\Psi^{a}K^{+}_{ab}\to-i\,P^{t}_{b}
,
\Psi^{a}K^{-}_{ab}\to-i\,P^{x}_{b}
,
\partial_{+}\to\partial_{t}
,
\partial_{-}\to\partial_{x}
.

The canonical stress-energy tensor of the deformed model can be easily calculated

\displaystyle T^{\mu}{}_{\nu}={\partial{\cal L}\over\partial\partial_{\mu}\Psi^{a}}\partial_{\nu}\Psi^{a}-\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}{\cal L}

(2.4)

\displaystyle T^{t}{}_{t}={-{\rm K}^{x}_{x}+{\rm V}\over 1+\alpha{\rm V}}\,,\quad T^{x}{}_{t}={{\rm K}^{x}_{t}\over 1+\alpha{\rm V}}\,,\quad T^{t}{}_{x}={{\rm K}^{t}_{x}\over 1+\alpha{\rm V}}\,,\quad T^{x}{}_{x}={-{\rm K}^{t}_{t}+{\rm V}\over 1+\alpha{\rm V}}\,,

(2.5)

and used to check that the deformed Lagrangian (
2.2
) satisfies the flow equation

\displaystyle{\partial{\cal L}\over\partial\alpha}=T^{t}{}_{t}T^{x}{}_{x}-T^{t}{}_{x}T^{x}{}_{t}

(2.6)

Since any seed model can be written in the form (
2.1
), the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Lagrangian (
2.2
) is universal. However, in a non-relativistic case the seed Lagrangian (
2.1
) may also include total derivative terms which do not change the equations of motion of the seed model but they do change the canonical stress-energy tensor and as a result the Lagrangian and the equations of motion of the deformed model may depend on the total derivative terms.
This dependence does not seem to be spurious, and we do not think that it can be undone by a field redefinition.

2.2
{T\overline{T}}
deformed matrix nonlinear Schrödinger model

The Lagrangian of the matrix nonlinear Schrödinger model is

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}=\frac{i}{2}(\bar{\psi}\dot{\psi}-\dot{\bar{\psi}}\psi)-\bar{\psi}^{\prime}{\psi^{\prime}}-U\,,\quad U=\kappa\,\bar{\psi}\psi\bar{\psi}\psi-\mu\,\bar{\psi}\psi\,.

(2.17)

Here

\displaystyle\psi=(\psi_{ai})\,,\quad\bar{\psi}=\psi^{\dagger}=(\psi_{ia}^{*})\,,\quad a=1,\ldots,n\,,\,i=1,\ldots,m

(2.18)

are complex
n\times m
and
m\times n
matrices hermitian conjugate to each other. Then, the trace is implied in (
2.17
), i.e.

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}\dot{\psi}\equiv\psi_{ia}^{*}\dot{\psi}_{ai}\,,\quad\bar{\psi}\psi\bar{\psi}\psi\equiv\psi_{ia}^{*}\psi_{aj}\psi_{jb}^{*}\psi_{bi}\,.

(2.19)

To bring the Lagrangian into the desired form we introduce two auxiliary matrices hermitian conjugate to each other

\displaystyle A=(A_{ai})\,,\quad\bar{A}=A^{\dagger}=(A_{ia}^{*})\,,\quad a=1,\ldots,n\,,\,i=1,\ldots,m\,,

(2.20)

and rewrite (
2.17
) as

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}=\frac{i}{2}(\bar{\psi}\dot{\psi}-\dot{\bar{\psi}}\psi)-\bar{A}\psi^{\prime}-\bar{\psi}^{\prime}A+\bar{A}A-U

(2.21)

Thus, the set
(\Psi^{a})
consists of
\psi,\bar{\psi},A,\bar{A}
, and

\displaystyle{\rm K}^{t}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}(\bar{\psi}\dot{\psi}-\dot{\bar{\psi}}\psi)\,,\quad{\rm K}^{x}_{x}=-\bar{A}\psi^{\prime}-\bar{\psi}^{\prime}A\,,

(2.22)

\displaystyle{\rm K}^{t}_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}(\bar{\psi}\psi^{\prime}-\bar{\psi}^{\prime}\psi)\,,\quad{\rm K}^{x}_{t}=-\bar{A}\dot{\psi}-\dot{\bar{\psi}}A\,,\quad V=U-\bar{A}A\,,

where the trace is implied.

The
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Lagrangian of the matrix nonlinear Schrödinger model, therefore, is

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\frac{K^{t}_{t}-\bar{A}\psi^{\prime}-\bar{\psi}^{\prime}A+\bar{A}A-U-\alpha\big(K^{t}_{t}(\bar{A}\psi^{\prime}+\bar{\psi}^{\prime}A)-K^{t}_{x}(\bar{A}\dot{\psi}+\dot{\bar{\psi}}A)\big)}{1-\alpha(\bar{A}A-U)}\,.

(2.23)

Eliminating the auxiliary fields

by using their equations of motion and, choosing the regular in
\alpha

{\cal L}
, one gets

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm{ph}}

\displaystyle=-{1\over\alpha}+{1+\alpha K^{t}_{t}+\sqrt{\Lambda}\over 2\tilde{\alpha}}\,,\quad\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha(1+\alpha U)\,,

(2.24)

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle=(1+\alpha K^{t}_{t})^{2}(1-4\tilde{\alpha}{\bar{\psi}^{\prime}}{\psi^{\prime}})+4\alpha\tilde{\alpha}(1+\alpha K^{t}_{t})K^{t}_{x}(\dot{\bar{\psi}}{\psi^{\prime}}+{\bar{\psi}^{\prime}}\dot{\psi})-4\alpha^{2}\,\tilde{\alpha}\,(K^{t}_{x})^{2}\,\dot{\bar{\psi}}\dot{\psi}

where in the expression for

It is clear that the deformation drastically modifies the Poisson structure of the model, and developing a Hamiltonian formulation requires dealing with an intricate system of second-class constraints. The same seems to be valid for any non-relativistic model.

2.3
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Landau–Lifshitz model

We mostly follow the notations in
faddeev
.

The Landau-Lifshitz equation is

\displaystyle{\partial S_{i}\over\partial t}={1\over R^{2}}\epsilon_{ijk}\,S_{j}{\partial^{2}S_{k}\over\partial x^{2}}+\epsilon_{ijk}\,S_{j}J_{k}S_{k}\,,

(2.25)

where
S_{i}^{2}=R^{2}
, and we sum over repeated indices even if there are 3 of them.

The fields
S_{i}
have the Poisson structure

\displaystyle\{S_{i}(x),S_{j}(y)\}=-\eta\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,S_{k}(x)\delta(x-y)\,,

(2.26)

and the LL equation follows from the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\int dx\,{1\over 2\eta}\Big({1\over R^{2}}\Big({\partial S_{k}\over\partial x}\Big)^{2}-J_{k}S_{k}^{2}+J_{3}R^{2}\Big)\,.

(2.27)

where the constant
J_{3}R^{2}
guaranties the vanishing of the Hamiltonian density
in the rapidly decreasing case where we impose the conditions
S_{k}(\pm\infty)=\delta_{k3}R
.
By rescaling
S_{k}
and
x,y
one can set
R=1
and
\eta=1
. We prefer to keep these two parameters to simplify taking the limits to the NLS and sine-Gordon models.

To find the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed LL model we need its Lagrangian description. To this end we multiply (
2.25
) by
\epsilon_{lmi}S_{m}
, and, changing the indices, get

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ijk}S_{j}{\partial S_{k}\over\partial t}=-\big(\delta_{ij}-{1\over R^{2}}S_{i}S_{j}\big)\Big({\partial^{2}S_{j}\over\partial x^{2}}+R^{2}J_{j}S_{j}\Big)\,,\quad S_{i}^{2}=R^{2}\,.

(2.28)

These equations can be derived from the following Lagrangian

\displaystyle L_{0}=\int dx\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,{1\over\eta R^{2}}{\epsilon_{ijk}}S_{i}{\partial S_{j}\over\partial r}{\partial S_{k}\over\partial t}+\int\,dx\,{1\over 2\eta}\Big(-{1\over R^{2}}\Big({\partial S_{k}\over\partial x}\Big)^{2}+J_{k}S_{k}^{2}-J_{3}R^{2}\Big)\,,

(2.29)

where
S_{k}
are subject to the sphere constraint
S_{k}^{2}=R^{2}
. In the first term
S_{k}
depend on an extra radial coordinate

S_{k}(x,t,r)|_{r=0}=S_{k}(x,t)

S_{k}(x,t,r)|_{r=\infty}=\delta_{k3}R

\displaystyle\delta\int dx\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,{1\over\eta R^{2}}{\epsilon_{ijk}}S_{i}{\partial S_{j}\over\partial r}{\partial S_{k}\over\partial t}=-\int dx\,{\epsilon_{ijk}\over\eta R^{2}}S_{i}\delta S_{j}{\partial S_{k}\over\partial t}\,,

(2.30)

where the variation
\delta S_{k}
is tangent to the sphere, i.e. it obeys the constraint
\delta S_{k}S_{k}=0

have to be replaced with

. It produces all the terms on the r.h.s. of the equations of motion (
2.28

,

\displaystyle{1\over\eta R^{2}}{\epsilon_{ijk}}S_{i}{\partial S_{j}\over\partial r}{\partial S_{k}\over\partial t}=-{\partial\over\partial r}\big(P_{a}{\partial\phi_{a}\over\partial t}\big)+{\partial\over\partial t}\big(P_{a}{\partial\phi_{a}\over\partial r}\big)\,,

(2.31)

where
P_{a}
satisfies the condition
P_{a}(x,t,\infty)=0

r=\infty
.
We will always drop the total time derivative term, integrate the remaining term over
r

{T\overline{T}}
deformation

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}=P_{k}\dot{S}_{k}-{1\over 2\eta R^{2}}\Big({\partial S_{k}\over\partial x}\Big)^{2}+{1\over 2\eta}(J_{k}S_{k}^{2}-J_{3}R^{2})-U_{\rm add}(S_{k})\,,

(2.32)

where
P_{k}
are such that
P_{k}\dot{S}_{k}=P_{a}\dot{\phi}^{a}
, and

In particular, in spherical coordinates

\displaystyle S_{1}=\cos\phi\sin\theta\,,\quad S_{2}=\sin\phi\sin\theta\,,\quad S_{3}=\cos\theta\,,

(2.33)

\displaystyle P_{k}\dot{S}_{k}={1\over\eta R^{2}}(\cos\theta-1)\dot{\phi}=-{2\over\eta R^{2}}\sin^{2}{\theta\over 2}\,\dot{\phi}\,.

(2.34)

Now, introducing an auxiliary vector

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}=P_{k}\dot{S}_{k}+A_{k}S_{k}^{\prime}+{\eta R^{2}\over 2}A_{k}^{2}+{1\over 2\eta}(J_{k}S_{k}^{2}-J_{3}R^{2})-U_{\rm add}

We see that the set

2.4
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Gardner equation

The Gardner equation is a combined KdV-mKdV equation

\displaystyle\dot{u}+\mu\,u^{\prime}+6\,g\,uu^{\prime}-6\,h\,u^{2}u^{\prime}+u^{\prime\prime\prime}=0\,,

(2.55)

where
g,h
and
\mu
are constants. If

\mu
can be removed by a constant shift of
u

\displaystyle u\to u-c\,,\quad hc^{2}+gc-{\mu\over 6}=0\,,

(2.56)

which also changes
g
. For decreasing boundary conditions such a shift is obviously forbidden. The Gardner equation is the continuity equation for the current

\displaystyle J^{t}=u\,,\quad J^{x}=\mu\,u+3\,g\,u^{2}-2\,h\,u^{3}+u^{\prime\prime}\,,

(2.57)

and if the charge
Q=\int dx\,u
exists then it is conserved. In what follows we only consider the case where
Q
exists.

The Gardner equation can be derived from the action

\displaystyle S_{0}=\int\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}t\,{\cal L}_{0}\,,\quad{\cal L}_{0}=\kappa\,(-\dot{\phi}\phi^{\prime}-\mu\phi^{\prime 2}-2g\phi^{\prime 3}+{h}\phi^{\prime 4}+\phi^{\prime\prime 2}\,)\,,

(2.58)

where the field
\phi
satisfies the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\phi(t,\infty)-\phi(t,-\infty)=Q_{\phi}=\const\,,

(2.59)

\kappa
is any constant, and
u
is related to
\phi
as

\displaystyle u=\phi^{\prime}\,.

(2.60)

Obviously, in the undeformed case

. The equation of motion for
\phi
is invariant under a shift of
\phi
by any function of time. By using this invariance one may require
\phi(t,\pm\infty)
to be constant. However, as we will see, in the deformed case this invariance is broken, and different time dependence of
\phi(t,\infty)
leads to different solutions.

To write the Lagrangian (
2.58

2.1
), we first introduce an auxiliary field

satisfying the equation of motion

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}

\displaystyle=\kappa\,(-\dot{\phi}\phi^{\prime}-\mu\phi^{\prime 2}-2g\phi^{\prime 3}+{h}\phi^{\prime 4}+2A\phi^{\prime\prime}-A^{2})\,.

(2.61)

Then, we introduce auxiliary fields for

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{u}=-{1\over\kappa}{\partial{\cal L}_{0}\over\partial\dot{\phi}}=\phi^{\prime}\,,\quad B=-{1\over\kappa}{\partial{\cal L}_{0}\over\partial\phi^{\prime}}=\dot{\phi}+2\mu\phi^{\prime}+6g\phi^{\prime 2}-4h\phi^{\prime 3}+2A^{\prime}\,,

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}=\kappa\,(-\mathfrak{u}\,\dot{\phi}-B\phi^{\prime}+2A\,\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}+\mathfrak{u}\,B-\mu\,\mathfrak{u}^{2}-2g\,\mathfrak{u}^{3}+h\,\mathfrak{u}^{4}-A^{2})\,.

We see that the set

\displaystyle=-\kappa\,\mathfrak{u}\,\dot{\phi}\,,\quad{\rm K}^{x}_{x}=-\kappa\,B\phi^{\prime}+2\kappa\,A\,\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}\,,\quad{\rm K}^{t}_{x}=-\kappa\,\mathfrak{u}\,\phi^{\prime}\,,\quad{\rm K}^{x}_{t}=-\kappa\,B\dot{\phi}+2\kappa\,A\,\dot{\mathfrak{u}}\,,\quad

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=-\kappa\,(\mathfrak{u}\,B-\mu\,\mathfrak{u}^{2}-2g\,\mathfrak{u}^{3}+h\,\mathfrak{u}^{4}-A^{2})\,.

Therefore, the

deformed Lagrangian of the Gardner model is

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\kappa{-\mathfrak{u}\,\dot{\phi}-B\phi^{\prime}+2A\,\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}+\mathfrak{u}\,B-\mu\,\mathfrak{u}^{2}-2g\,\mathfrak{u}^{3}+h\,\mathfrak{u}^{4}-A^{2}-2\alpha\kappa\,A\,\mathfrak{u}\,(\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}\dot{\phi}-\dot{\mathfrak{u}}\,\phi^{\prime})\over 1-\alpha\kappa\,(\mathfrak{u}\,B-\mu\,\mathfrak{u}^{2}-2g\,\mathfrak{u}^{3}+h\,\mathfrak{u}^{4}-A^{2})}\,,

where the field

\displaystyle\phi\to\phi+f(t)\,,\quad B\to B+{df\over dt}\,,

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}\to{\cal L}_{0}-\kappa{\partial\over\partial x}\big(\,{df\over dt}\phi\,\big)\,.

(2.67)

The

deformed Lagrangian (

In the undeformed model the auxiliary field

coincides with the Gardner field

It is therefore reasonable to use the same identification in the

2.5
Comments

Here we discuss similarities and differences of the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Lagrangians for relativistic and non-relativistic models obtained in this section, and comment on possible approaches to quantising the models.

All the Lagrangians depend on auxiliary fields which are introduced in a seed model to bring it to the first-order form (
2.1
). If the physical fields of a seed model do not depend on second- or higher-order derivatives then auxiliary fields enter a
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Lagrangian algebraically, and can be eliminated leading in the cases considered to Nambu-Goto type actions. More complicated seed models (even relativistic invariant) may lead to
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Lagrangians which are solutions to high degree polynomial equations.

A Nambu-Goto type Lagrangian obtained by eliminating auxiliary fields has a square root sign ambiguity. If a model is considered on a line then the requirement of finiteness of the energy singles out the perturbative in
\alpha
branch of the deformed Lagrangian depending only on the physical fields. However, if the model is on a circle then one has to find additional requirements to single out the perturbative branch.
For example if one considers the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed free massless scalars and chooses the negative sign in front of the square root in the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Lagrangian (
2.15
) then for
\alpha<0
the energy is not bounded from below. In quantum theory it would clearly be unsatisfactory. On the other hand if
\alpha>0
then the energy of any solution is bounded from below,
2
2

2

Note that if there is no potential and
B
-field then the equations of motion do not depend on the branch of the square root.
and diverges in the limit
\alpha\to 0
. Thus, if one calculates, for example, the partition function of the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed model then there seems to be no reason not to include the contribution from the nonperturbative branch to the path integral over physical and auxiliary fields. It would imply that for
\alpha>0
the spectrum of
{T\overline{T}}
deformed relativistic models previously discussed is incomplete and must be supplemented by a nonperturbative part.

The physical fields of the Gardner model depend on second-order derivatives. As a result the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed equations of motion for the auxiliary fields are not algebraic, and depend on space derivatives of the auxiliary fields. Eliminating the auxiliary fields (which we have not managed to do) would lead to an action non-local in space. The
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Gardner model is, therefore, expected to have properties noticeably different from the seed model already at the classical level. Indeed in the next section we will see that solutions of the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed KdV equation are very sensitive to the behaviour of the field
\phi
at space infinities.

We have seen that the deformation drastically modifies the Poisson structure of all the non-relativistic models we considered, and developing a Hamiltonian formulation requires dealing with an intricate system of second-class constraints. This actually makes
{T\overline{T}}
deformed non-relativistic models more complicated than the relativistic ones where the Hamiltonian formulation is straightforward.

One may wonder whether the
{T\overline{T}}
non-relativistic deformed models exist as quantum theories. We do not expect any principal difficulties in perturbative quantisation of the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed NLS and LL models. For example, the expansion of the deformed Lagrangian (
2.24
) of the NLS model in powers of
\alpha
is straightforward, and the standard technique can be used to compute the scattering matrix. It is expected that the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed S-matrix would be different from the undeformed one only by the
{T\overline{T}}
CDD factor. It might be necessary to tune properly counterterms
but the relation between the S-matrices is very general and should be considered as a part of the definition of a quantised
{T\overline{T}}
deformed model.
For integrable models the relation follows from the interpretation, discussed at length in
SF19a
, of the homogeneous inviscid Burgers equation as the condition of the gauge invariance of the target space-time energy and momentum of the string theory which produces a
{T\overline{T}}
deformed model in
\alpha
-dependent light-cone gauge. The UV behaviour of the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed NLS model should be milder than for relativistic ones because of the absence of virtual particles production.

The spectrum of the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed NLS (and LL) model on a circle can be also studied perturbatively.
At each order in
\alpha
one can remove all interaction terms with time derivatives of
\psi
by a field redefinition producing new terms with higher space derivatives. The resulting model has the undeformed Poisson structure and can be easily quantised. The spectrum of the Hamiltonian can then be found as an expansion in powers of
\alpha
.

For finite
\alpha

another, more pragmatic, approach to the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed spectrum is to postulate that it is governed by the usual Bethe equations with the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed S-matrix. It was done in
Jiang2020a
for the deformed NLS model in the repulsive regime, and it was found that the properties of the model were similar to the properties of
{T\overline{T}}
deformed CFT’s. In particular,
for
\alpha<0
the spectrum is well-defined but there exists an upper bound for the temperature while for
\alpha>0
there exists a critical value
\alpha_{\rm c}
which depends on the number of particles and the radius such that for
\alpha>\alpha_{\rm c}
the spectrum becomes complex.
However, there is no argumentation why the Bethe equations would not be replaced by a more complicated system of TBA-like equations. It would be interesting to compute the spectrum as an expansion in powers of
\alpha
, and compared it with the Bethe ansatz predictions.

To conclude this subsection let us mention that
we do not think that the approaches discussed above can be applied to quantum
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Gardner model.
In the simplest KdV case the spectrum of quantum KdV theory is described by massless TBA equations
BLZ94
which are derived by quantising the second Hamiltonian structure of the KdV equation and diagonalising the infinite-dimensional abelian subalgebra of the Virasoro algebra which gives commuting integrals of motion of quantum KdV model. It is unclear how these deep relations are modified under the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation, and we suspect that quantum (and even classical)
{T\overline{T}}
deformed KdV model may hide many surprises.

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\frac{-\rho^{2}\dot{\phi}-(\rho_{A}+\bar{\rho}_{A}){\rho^{\prime}}+i(\rho_{A}-\bar{\rho}_{A})\rho\,{\phi^{\prime}}+\bar{\rho}_{A}\rho_{A}+{g^{2}\over 4}\rho^{4}+\mu\,\rho^{2}-\alpha\rho^{2}(\rho_{A}+\bar{\rho}_{A})(\dot{\rho}{\phi^{\prime}}-{\rho^{\prime}}\dot{\phi})}{1-\alpha(\bar{\rho}_{A}\rho_{A}+{g^{2}\over 4}\rho^{4}+\mu\,\rho^{2})}\,,

(3.9)

where
\rho_{A}
and
\bar{\rho}_{A}
are complex conjugate to each other.

The deformed one-soliton solution can be derived by explicitly solving the equations of motion by using the following ansatz

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)

\displaystyle=\rho(x-vt)\,,\quad\rho_{A}(t,x)=\rho_{A}(x-vt)\,,\quad\rho(\pm\infty)=0\,,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\omega\,t+\varphi(x-vt)\,,\quad\omega=\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}}{4}+\mu\,.

The phase
\phi
of the soliton is at most the sum of a linear function of
x,t
which we can choose without loss of generality to be the same as in the undeformed case, and of a function of
x-vt
due to the restricted dependence of the other fields.

The derivation is sketched in appendix
A
, and the solution can be expressed in terms of
\rho
as follows

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{2\rho\sqrt{u^{2}-g^{2}\rho^{2}}}{4+\alpha\rho^{2}\left(-2g^{2}\rho^{2}+u^{2}-v^{2}-4\mu\right)},\quad\rho_{A}=\frac{1}{2}\rho\left(iv\pm\sqrt{u^{2}-g^{2}\rho^{2}}\right)\,,

(3.11)

\displaystyle x-vt

\displaystyle=x_{0}\pm\frac{2\coth^{-1}\left(\frac{u}{\sqrt{u^{2}-g^{2}\rho^{2}}}\right)}{u}\mp\frac{\alpha\sqrt{u^{2}-g^{2}\rho^{2}}\left(u^{2}+3v^{2}+12\mu+2g^{2}\rho^{2}\right)}{6g^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}v(x-vt)+\frac{1}{4}t\left(u^{2}+v^{2}+4\mu\right)\pm\frac{\alpha v\left(u^{2}-g^{2}\rho^{2}\right)^{3/2}}{6g^{2}}\,.

Since the phase
\phi
and the auxiliary field
\rho_{A}
are smooth functions of
x
and
t
if the amplitude
\rho
is, we discuss only the properties of
\rho
. Unlike the undeformed soliton, the amplitude has a nontrivial dependence on the chemical potential
\mu
. However, it enters the amplitude only through the combination
v^{2}+4\mu
.
Without
loss of generality we can set
t=0
and
x_{0}=0
. Clearly, the maximum of
\rho(x)
is equal to
u/g
, and it is at
x=0
.
From the equation for
\rho^{\prime}
we see that
\rho
is a single-valued function of
x
only if
\rho^{\prime}\neq\infty
for all
x
which leads to the condition

\displaystyle 4+\alpha\rho^{2}\left(-2g^{2}\rho^{2}+u^{2}-v^{2}-4\mu\right)\neq 0\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq\rho\leq{u\over g}\,.

(3.12)

To analyse (
3.12
) it is convenient to introduce a new parameter

\displaystyle W={u^{2}-v^{2}-4\mu}\,.

(3.13)

Then, the roots of the equation
\rho^{\prime}=\infty
are given by

\displaystyle\rho^{2}_{\pm}=\frac{W\pm\sqrt{\frac{32g^{2}}{\alpha}+W^{2}}}{4g^{2}}\,,\quad\rho^{\prime}\big|_{\rho=\rho_{\pm}}=\infty\,.

(3.14)

A simple analysis shows that the roots
\rho_{\pm}
are outside the interval
(0\,,\,u/g)
if

\displaystyle\text{I.}

\displaystyle W\in{\mathbb{R}}\quad\text{and}\quad-{32g^{2}\over W^{2}}<\alpha\leq 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{complex}\ \ \rho_{\pm}^{2}

(3.15)

\displaystyle\text{II.}

\displaystyle W=u^{2}-v^{2}-4\mu<0\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha\leq-{32g^{2}\over W^{2}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\rho_{-}^{2}\leq\rho_{+}^{2}<0

\displaystyle\text{III.}

\displaystyle W-4u^{2}=-3u^{2}-v^{2}-4\mu>0\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle\frac{4g^{2}}{2u^{4}-u^{2}W}<\alpha\leq-\frac{32g^{2}}{W^{2}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad{u^{2}\over g^{2}}<\rho_{-}^{2}\leq\rho_{+}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{IV.}

\displaystyle W-2u^{2}=-u^{2}-v^{2}-4\mu<0\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle 0<\alpha<\frac{4g^{2}}{2u^{4}-u^{2}W}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\rho_{-}^{2}<0<{u^{2}\over g^{2}}<\rho_{+}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{V.}

\displaystyle W-2u^{2}=-u^{2}-v^{2}-4\mu>0\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha>0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\rho_{-}^{2}<0<{u^{2}\over g^{2}}<\rho_{+}^{2}

Introducing the following two critical values of
\alpha

\displaystyle\alpha_{-}\equiv-{32g^{2}\over(u^{2}-v^{2}-4\mu)^{2}}<0\,,\quad\alpha_{+}\equiv\frac{4g^{2}}{u^{2}(u^{2}+v^{2}+4\mu)}\,,

(3.16)

we can combine these regions as follows

\displaystyle\text{A.}

\displaystyle-\infty<u^{2}-v^{2}-4\mu<0\quad\text{and}\quad-\infty<\alpha<\alpha_{+}\,,\quad\alpha_{+}>0

(3.17)

\displaystyle\text{B.}

\displaystyle 0<u^{2}-v^{2}-4\mu<2u^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{-}<\alpha<\alpha_{+}\,,\quad\alpha_{+}>0

\displaystyle\text{C.}

\displaystyle 2u^{2}<u^{2}-v^{2}-4\mu<4u^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{+}<\alpha_{-}<\alpha<\infty

\displaystyle\text{D.}

\displaystyle 4u^{2}<u^{2}-v^{2}-4\mu<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{+}<\alpha<\infty\,,\quad\alpha_{+}<\alpha_{-}<0

The condition A is satisfied if
v^{2}>u^{2}-4\mu
which imposes a lower bound on
v^{2}
if
u^{2}>4\mu
.
If
\mu\geq 0
then the condition B is satisfied for all
u\,,\,v
but C and D are never satisfied.
The condition D is satisfied if
v^{2}<-3u^{2}-4\mu
which imposes an upper bound on
v^{2}
if
3u^{2}<-4\mu
.
If
\mu<0
then all the four conditions can occur.

If the parameters of the soliton satisfy one of the conditions (
3.17
) then
\rho(x)
is an even function of
x
, and
the differential equation for
\rho
allows one to replace the integration of any expression over
x
with the integration over
\rho
. The U(1) charge, energy and momentum of the soliton are easily found, appear to be unchanged by the deformation, and are given by (
3.5
). The shape of the soliton obviously changes, and, in particular, we can define its size by using the full-width-half-maximum

\displaystyle FWHM

\displaystyle=-\alpha\frac{\sqrt{3}\,u\left(u^{2}+2v^{2}+8\mu\right)}{4g^{2}}+\frac{4\log\left(2+\sqrt{3}\right)}{u}\,.

(3.18)

The soliton clearly exhibits the general phenomenon of widening/narrowing the width of particles under the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation
Cardy2020
. However, whether the size is increasing or decreasing depends not only on the sign of
\alpha
but also on the sign of
s\equiv u^{2}+2v^{2}+8\mu
. Obviously, it is positive for all values of
u
and
v
only if
\mu\geq 0
. It is also positive if the soliton parameters satisfy condition A but it is negative for conditions C or D. For parameters satisfying condition B one can have both positive and negative

if
\mu
is negative.
The visually distinct solutions are demonstrated in figure

. Further plots for all cases are shown in figures

and

, which display the same behaviours as in case C. Since the amplitude depends only on

we set

without loss of generality when plotting solutions. We set

, so that the graphs are parametrised by
\mu
.
If the soliton base widens (or remains constant if

) as the magnitude of

increases then the peak flattens as in figures

and

.
Let us also mention that as one can see from (

) the heavier and speedier the soliton is the wider it is. That is very different from the undeformed case where the width is independent of speed and decreases with mass increasing.

Let us now assume that
u,v,\mu
satisfy one of the conditions (
3.17
) but
\alpha
is at a boundary of its allowed values, i.e. it takes one of the critical values
\alpha_{\pm}
. Then, a shock-wave singularity develops, and away from the critical values the solution
\rho(x)
becomes a multi-valued function of
x
. In this case at least one of the roots
\rho_{\pm}
is inside the interval
(0\,,\,u/g)
. Regions where only one root exists form loops as in figures
1
,
7
and
6
, due to

(given explicitly in (

)) becoming negative. This happens if

satisfy either conditions A and B with
\alpha>\alpha_{+}>0
or C and D with
\alpha<\alpha_{+}<0
. Where both roots exists the solution is either a bell shape or a double loop shape, both shown in figure
1
. This happens if
u,v,\mu
satisfy condition B with
\alpha<\alpha_{-}<0
, or condition C with
\alpha_{+}<\alpha<\alpha_{-}<0
. The conditions for the appearance of these solutions are summarised below

\displaystyle\text{Loop: }

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}u^{2}+v^{2}+4\mu>0\,,\ \alpha>\alpha_{+}>0\,,&\ \rho_{-}<0<\rho_{+}<{u\over g}\\
u^{2}+v^{2}+4\mu<0\,,\ \alpha<\alpha_{+}<0\,,&\ 0<\rho_{-}<{u\over g}<\rho_{+}\end{array}\right.

(3.19)

\displaystyle\text{Bell or Double Loop: }

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0<u^{2}+v^{2}+4\mu<2u^{2}\,,&\ \alpha<\alpha_{-}<0<\alpha_{+}\\
-2u^{2}<u^{2}+v^{2}+4\mu<0\,,&\ \alpha_{+}<\alpha<\alpha_{-}<0\end{array}\right.

The existence of the forbidden regions of the soliton parameters is disturbing because the soliton physical quantities such as mass, momentum and energy do not show any sign of singularity. It is also unclear if it is compatible with
the integrability preserving feature of the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation. We attempt to fix this by redefining the amplitude function as a piecewise smooth curve by exploiting the translational invariance of
x-vt
.

We set

\displaystyle x(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\coth^{-1}\left(\frac{u}{\sqrt{u^{2}-g^{2}\rho^{2}}}\right)}{u}-\frac{\alpha\sqrt{u^{2}-g^{2}\rho^{2}}\left(u^{2}+3v^{2}+12\mu+2g^{2}\rho^{2}\right)}{6g^{2}}\,,\quad 0\leq\rho\leq{u\over g}\,.

(3.20)

In terms of

the piece-wise smooth solutions can be written as

\displaystyle\text{Loop: }

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}x_{L}^{+}(\rho)=-x(\rho)\theta(\rho-\rho_{+})+\big(x(\rho)-2x_{+}\big)\theta(\rho_{+}-\rho)\\
x_{L}^{-}(\rho)=-x(\rho)\theta(\rho-\rho_{-})+\big(x(\rho)-2x_{-}\big)\theta(\rho_{-}-\rho)\end{array}\right.

(3.21)

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\text{Bell:}\\
\text{Double Loop:}\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}x_{B}(\rho)=x(\rho)\theta(\rho-\rho_{+})+\big(2x_{+}-x(\rho)\big)\theta(\rho_{+}-\rho)\theta(\rho-\rho_{-})\\
+\big(x(\rho)+2x_{+}-2x_{-}\big)\theta(\rho_{-}-\rho)\end{array}\right.

where

is the Heaviside function and
x_{\pm}=x(\rho_{\pm})
.
Each of these functions is a positive decreasing function of
\rho
with a continuous first derivative.
The soliton profile
\rho(x)
is an even function of

given for

by the functions inverse to (

).
The energy, momentum and charge densities are singular as functions of

but it is an integrable singularity. Since they depend on
\rho^{\prime 2}
and
\rho
, the energy, momentum and charge are given by the same expressions (
3.5
). The three forbidden solution types are reconstructed into valid amplitudes in figure

. Note that all these new solutions increase in width as
\alpha
increases in magnitude. Whether such a gluing procedure is legitimate remains to be seen but there are examples of models with singular solitons, see e.g.

Let us finally mention that the inverse function

3.2
{T\overline{T}}
deformed KdV soliton

In this subsection we discuss a one-soliton solution of the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed KdV equation which corresponds to the
g=1\,,\,h=0
case of the Gardner equation

\displaystyle\dot{u}+\mu\,u^{\prime}+6uu^{\prime}+u^{\prime\prime\prime}=0\,.

(3.22)

The constant
\mu
is usually set to 0 but we prefer to keep it so that for
\mu<0
we could have left-moving solitons.

The one-soliton solution we are going to deform is given by

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle={2w^{2}\over\cosh^{2}\big(w(x-vt)\big)}\,,\quad w=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{v-\mu}>0\,,

(3.23)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=2w\tanh\left(w(x-vt)\right)+f(t)\,,

where
f(t)
is any function of
t
.
As was discussed in the previous section, in the undeformed case the soliton properties are independent of
f(t)
. In particular,
the charge
Q
, momentum
P
and energy
E
of the soliton are

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,u=4w\,,

(3.24)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,u^{2}={16\over 3}w^{3}\,,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,(\mu\,u^{2}+2u^{3}-u^{\prime 2})=\frac{16}{3}\mu w^{3}+\frac{64}{5}w^{5}=\mu P+\frac{3}{5}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{2/3}P^{5/3}\,.

A funny property of the soliton is that its momentum is always positive even if the velocity
v
is negative which requires
\mu
to be negative too. This is counter-intuitive and for
v<0
it might be reasonable to change the overall sign of
P
and
E
which is equivalent to setting
\kappa=-1
in the Lagrangian (
2.63
). This also effectively changes the sign of
\alpha
in the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Lagrangian (
2.65
). Then, for small
P
the dispersion relation would be approximately the one for a massless relativistic particle. In what follows to have a uniform description we will continue using
\kappa=1
for all values of
v
.

The
{T\overline{T}}
deformed soliton solution depends on the function
f(t)
in a nontrivial way, and we only consider the simplest case
f(t)=b\,t
where
b
is an arbitrary constant. In fact, redefining
\phi
as
\phi\to\phi+bt
, we find that the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Lagrangian (
2.65
) transforms as
{\cal L}\to{\cal L}-b{\cal J}^{t}
, and therefore
b
can be interpreted as the parameter of the deformation by the time component of the conserved current due to the invariance of (
2.65
) under constant shifts of
\phi
.

In this case all auxiliary fields are only functions of
x-vt
, and the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed solution can be found by using the equations of motion and the ansatz

\displaystyle\phi=\phi(x-v\,t)+b\,t\,,\quad\mathfrak{u}=\mathfrak{u}(x-v\,t)\,,\quad A=A(x-v\,t)\,,\quad B=B(x-v\,t)\,.

(3.25)

The full derivation is described in Appendix
C
. We find that
\mathfrak{u}
rather than
\phi^{\prime}
is the natural field to express our results in terms of.
We define
{\tilde{w}}^{2}={v-\mu-\alpha\,b^{2}\over 4}=w^{2}-{\alpha\,b^{2}\over 4}
to simplify the following expressions.
The solution can be written as a set of equations expressing
\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}
,
\phi^{\prime}
,
A
and
B
in terms of
\mathfrak{u}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\mathfrak{u}\sqrt{4{\tilde{w}}^{2}-2\mathfrak{u}}}{1+\alpha\mathfrak{u}^{2}\left(4\mathfrak{u}-8{\tilde{w}}^{2}-\alpha b^{2}\right)}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathfrak{u}-\alpha\,b\,\mathfrak{u}^{2}}{1+\alpha\mathfrak{u}^{2}\left(4\mathfrak{u}-8{\tilde{w}}^{2}-\alpha b^{2}\right)}\,,

(3.26)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\left(\mu+4{\tilde{w}}^{2}\right)\mathfrak{u}+b\,,

\displaystyle\quad A

\displaystyle=\pm\mathfrak{u}\sqrt{4{\tilde{w}}^{2}-2\mathfrak{u}}\,,\qquad{\tilde{w}}^{2}=w^{2}-{\alpha\,b^{2}\over 4}\,.

For the solutions to be real,
{\tilde{w}}^{2}>0
, or equivalently,
v>\mu+\alpha\,b^{2}
. For fixed
v,\mu,b
this condition imposes an upper bound on allowed values of
\alpha
:
{\mu-v\over b^{2}}>\alpha
. Note also that for
\alpha<0
one may have
w^{2}={v-\mu\over 4}<0
.

The extra parameter
b
causes the deformed quantities of energy, momentum and the dispersion relation to be dependent on both
\alpha
and
b
.

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\frac{16}{15}{\tilde{w}}^{3}\left(12{\tilde{w}}^{2}+5(\mu-\alpha b^{2})\right)\,,\quad P=\frac{16}{3}{\tilde{w}}^{3}\,,

(3.27)

\displaystyle E(P)

\displaystyle=P\left(\mu-\alpha b^{2}\right)+\frac{3}{5}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{2/3}P^{5/3}\,.

The appearance of
\alpha
in the dispersion relation is due to the fact that the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed KdV model is intrinsically nonlocal and sensitive to the boundary behaviour of
\phi
.

Furthermore, the parameter
b
causes the previously identical conserved charges of
{\cal J}^{t}
and
\phi^{\prime}
to become independent

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\int dx{\cal J}^{t}=4{\tilde{w}}\left(1+\frac{4}{3}{\tilde{w}}^{2}\alpha b\right)\,,\qquad Q_{\phi}=\int dx\phi^{\prime}=4{\tilde{w}}\left(1-\frac{4}{3}{\tilde{w}}^{2}\alpha b\right)

(3.28)

We also find that
b
defines a flow equation for a deformation under the current
{\cal J}^{t}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial b}=-\frac{\mathfrak{u}(\alpha b\mathfrak{u}+1)}{1-\alpha\mathfrak{u}^{2}\left(-4\mathfrak{u}+\alpha b^{2}+8{\tilde{w}}^{2}\right)}=-{\cal J}^{t}\,.

(3.29)

Integrating the equation for
\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}
in (
3.26
), we find the inverse expression for
\mathfrak{u}

\displaystyle x-vt

\displaystyle=x_{0}\,\pm\,\frac{\text{arctanh}\left(\frac{\sqrt{4{\tilde{w}}^{2}-2\mathfrak{u}}}{2{\tilde{w}}}\right)}{{\tilde{w}}}

(3.30)

\displaystyle\mp\frac{1}{15}\sqrt{2}\alpha\sqrt{2{\tilde{w}}^{2}-\mathfrak{u}}\left(4\left(2{\tilde{w}}^{2}-\mathfrak{u}\right)\left(3\mathfrak{u}+4{\tilde{w}}^{2}\right)+5\alpha b^{2}\left(\mathfrak{u}+4{\tilde{w}}^{2}\right)\right)\,,

which displays both shockwave and looping solutions as in the NLS case.
With
t=0
and
x_{0}=0
the maximum of
\mathfrak{u}(x)
occurs at
x=0
for
\mathfrak{u}(0)=2{\tilde{w}}^{2}
.
The full-width half-maximum of the soliton is

\displaystyle FWHM=\frac{2\text{arcoth}\left(\sqrt{2}\right)}{{\tilde{w}}}-\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{15}\alpha{\tilde{w}}^{3}\left(25\alpha b^{2}+28{\tilde{w}}^{2}\right)\,,

(3.31)

and for positive
\alpha
it decreases.

The derivative
\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}
becomes singular when the denominator in the equation for
\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}
in (
3.26
) vanishes

\displaystyle d(\mathfrak{u})\equiv\alpha\mathfrak{u}^{2}\left(4\mathfrak{u}-8{\tilde{w}}^{2}\right.-\alpha b^{2})+1=0,\qquad 0<\mathfrak{u}<2{\tilde{w}}^{2}\,.

(3.32)

In much the same way as the NLS case, restricting the roots of this expression to lie inside the range of
\mathfrak{u}
will generate the conditions for the solution to become multi-valued.

A detailed analysis of the equation (
3.32
) can be found in Appendix
C
where it is shown that
at least one root of the equation

lies inside the interval
(0,2{\tilde{w}}^{2})
if
4{\tilde{w}}^{2}={v-\mu-\alpha b^{2}}={4w^{2}-\alpha b^{2}}>0
and

\displaystyle\text{Loop: }

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}b\neq 0\,,\quad\alpha<\alpha_{-}<0&\\
b\neq 0\,,\ 4w^{8}>b^{2}\,,&\alpha_{+}^{(2)}<\alpha<\alpha_{+}^{(3)}<{4w^{2}\over b^{2}}\end{array}\right.

(3.33)

\displaystyle\text{Bell or Double Loop: }

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}b=0\,,\quad\alpha>\frac{27}{128w^{6}}&\\
b\neq 0\,,\ 4w^{8}>b^{2}\,,&\quad 0<\alpha_{+}^{(1)}<\alpha<\alpha_{+}^{(2)}\end{array}\right.

Here the critical values of
\alpha
are given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{-}=-\frac{\sqrt{4w^{4}+2|b|}-2w^{2}}{b^{2}}\,,\quad\alpha_{+}^{(2)}=\frac{2w^{2}-\sqrt{4w^{4}-2|b|}}{b^{2}}\,,\quad\alpha_{+}^{(3)}=\frac{2w^{2}+\sqrt{4w^{4}-2|b|}}{b^{2}}\,,

(3.34)

and
\alpha_{+}^{(1)}
is the positive root smaller than
{2w^{2}\over b^{2}}
of the following equation

\displaystyle 1-\frac{128}{27}\alpha\left(w^{2}-{\alpha b^{2}\over 8}\right)^{3}=0\,.

(3.35)

As one can see from (

), the soliton solution is single-valued
for

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}^{(3)}<\alpha<\alpha_{\rm max}={4w^{2}\over b^{2}}\,,\quad\frac{4w^{8}}{b^{2}}>1\,.

(3.36)

It is interesting that this region is nonperturbative in

.

3.3
Comments

Here we summarise the main properties of one-soliton solutions of the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed NLS and KdV models we have found in this section.

A common property of the solitons is that
their width appears to depend on the deformation parameter according to the general phenomenon of widening/narrowing the width of particles under the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation
Cardy2020
, see also
Jiang2020a
. However, contrary to the claim in
Cardy2020
, whether soliton’s size is increasing or decreasing depends not only on the sign of the deformation parameter but also on the potential and soliton parameters. In the NLS case this more complicated behaviour is caused by the addition of the time component (density of particles multiplied by the chemical potential
\mu
) of the conserved U(1) current to the seed model. After the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation this cannot be undone by a time dependent U(1) transformation (
3.2
), and leads to substantial changes in the soliton’s properties.
Clearly, the relativistic case is more restrictive because adding the time component of a conserved current breaks Lorentz invariance. It is also worth noting that in the absence of the chemical potential the width (
3.18
) is increasing for negative
\alpha
and decreasing for positive
\alpha
which is opposite to what was observed in
Cardy2020
and
Jiang2020a
. This is explained by the fact that
the energy (
3.5
) of the NLS soliton is given by
E=\frac{P^{2}}{2m}-\frac{1}{24}g^{4}m^{3}-\mu\,Q
, and for
\mu=0
its rest energy is negative. The existence of the rest energy means that in the non-relativistic case the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation is effectively a mixture of the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation with a stress-energy tensor shifted so that the rest energy is zero, and the
JP
deformation discussed in
Cardy2020
and
Jiang2020a
. If the chemical potential is sufficiently negative then the width is
widening or narrowing in accord with
Cardy2020
.
In the KdV case with the parameter
b=0
the width of the deformed soliton again behaves oppositely to
Cardy2020
and
Jiang2020a
. Since the rest energy of the soliton is zero, it is tempting to conclude that the effect of “pure”
{T\overline{T}}
deformation is in fact opposite to what was observed in
Cardy2020
and
Jiang2020a
for the
JP
deformation at least for models with solitons.
In this respect it would be interesting to analyse
the Bethe equations with the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed S-matrix for the deformed NLS model in the attractive regime to see if the conclusions of
Jiang2020a
where the repulsive case was studied remain unchanged.

Another common property of the deformed solitons is that
for any values of the parameters of the solitons there is at least one critical value
\alpha_{\rm cr}
at which solitons begin to exhibit the shock-wave behaviour. We proposed that for values of
\alpha
beyond
\alpha_{\rm cr}
a soliton solution may be constructed by gluing together the two branches of the soliton solution at the points where the first derivative of the soliton field diverges. Despite the divergency, the soliton energy and momentum are finite, and the dispersion relation is defined for all values of
\alpha
. A natural expectation is that the glued soliton is unstable, and it would be interesting to check it.

The
{T\overline{T}}
deformed KdV equation admits at least a one-parameter family of one-soliton solutions. The extra parameter
b
can be introduced explicitly in the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Lagrangian by shifting the field
\phi
by
bt
, and requiring that
\phi
asymptotes to constants at space infinities. Then,
b

can be interpreted as the parameter of the deformation by the time component of the conserved current due to the invariance of the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Gardner model under constant shifts of
\phi
. Since the parameter
b
modifies the properties of the soliton, in particular, it appears in the dispersion relation, such an interpretation is probably the right one. It is however unclear to us why one has to impose constant space asymptotes on
\phi
. If
b
does not vanish then there is an upper bound on
\alpha
, and approaching the bound the soliton’s amplitude decreases and finally vanishes. Choosing properly other parameters of the soliton, one can make the bound negative. Thus, the parameter
b
allows one to construct solutions which do not exist in the seed model.

4
Conclusions

In this paper we have explained in detail how the light-cone gauge approach to the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation can be applied to non-Lorentz invariant models, and used it to derive the deformed Lagrangians of the three prominent non-relativistic models – the nonlinear Schrödinger, the Landau-Lifshitz and the Gardner.
The
{T\overline{T}}
deformed Lagrangians have been then used to find
one-soliton solutions of the deformed NLS and KdV models. The properties of the Lagrangians and solitons have been discussed in the Comments subsections
2.5
,
3.3
, and here we discuss some
of the many open questions to be addressed.

We have only considered the deformed models on a line. It would be interesting to put the models on a circle and look for all possible solutions including those nonperturbative in
\alpha
with energy divergent in the limit
\alpha\to 0
. In fact, these solutions may exist even for Lorentz invariant models, e.g. for the
{T\overline{T}}
deformed sigma model described by the Lagrangian (
2.14
), see subsections
2.5
for a detail discussion.

The seed models we have considered are integrable, and it is believed that their
{T\overline{T}}
deformations are integrable too. The first step in proving the integrability would be finding
Lax pairs for the deformed models. Lax pairs of several models including the NLS model were recently found in
Tian
by using the dynamical coordinate transformation
Tateo18b
. Their results agree with the previously known Lax pairs of the sine-Gordon and Liouville models
Tateo18a
;
Leoni
. It should be possible to apply the method of
Tian
to the matrix NLS model and the LL model.
It would be interesting to see if their method can be generalised to include models of the Gardner type where auxiliary fields cannot be eliminated and one has to deal with them.

As has been mentioned in subsections
2.5
, understanding the Poisson structure and developing a Hamiltonian formulation of the deformed models is important and probably very hard.

Given a Lax pair
(V,U)
and a Hamiltonian formulation of the NLS model, one can calculate the Poisson bracket between
U
’s, and see how the
r
-matrix structure is modified, and whether it can be quantised.

If a seed model possesses an additional conserved
U(1)
current
J
then one can consider
JT
deformations
Guica17
which have properties similar to the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation.
The NLS model is one of the simplest nonrelativistic models with the
U(1)
symmetry, and it would be interesting to analyse the properties of the model deformed by
JT
operators. Some steps in this direction have been made in
Jiang2020b
;
Tateo2020
. The light-cone gauge approach to the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation of relativistic sigma models can be readily generalised to include the
JT
deformations and deformations by operators linear in conserved currents
SF19b
. It should be possible to consider in the same framework nonrelativistic models. As was pointed out in
Mezei2019a
, since the
JT
deformations break Lorentz invariance the deformations by operators linear in conserved currents are necessary to derive flow equations for the spectrum.
In fact, for nonrelativistic models it seems necessary to include the linear deformations even to derive the flow equations for the
{T\overline{T}}
deformation.

The
{T\overline{T}}
deformation of nonrelativistic models is defined with the help of the Lagrangian flow

\partial_{\alpha}{\cal L}=-\,
{T\overline{T}}
. This modifies the Poisson structure of a seed model, and makes it difficult to derive flow equations for the spectrum. It would be interesting to see whether one can define the deformation as the Hamiltonian flow
\partial_{\alpha}{\cal H}=\,
{T\overline{T}}
which preserves the Poisson structure of a seed model. This can be done for a
{T\overline{T}}
deformed massive Dirac fermion
AAF
but for a bosonic model the Hamiltonian might appear to be nonlocal in space.

Finally, there are many questions related to quantum
{T\overline{T}}
deformed models. Some of them have been discussed in subsections
2.5
,
3.3
.

\alpha_{\rm min}
is also the minimum of
d(2{\tilde{w}}^{2})
and
\mathfrak{u}^{\rm ex}_{2}=2{\tilde{w}}^{2}=w^{2}
for
\alpha=\alpha_{\rm min}
.

Thus, if
\frac{4w^{8}}{b^{2}}<1
then there is no critical value for
\alpha>0
, and if
\frac{4w^{8}}{b^{2}}>1
then there are two real roots of the equation
d(\mathfrak{u}^{\rm ex}_{2})=0
, and the critical value
\alpha_{+}^{(1)}
is the positive root which is smaller than
\alpha_{\rm min}
. For values of
\alpha
slightly greater than
\alpha_{+}^{(1)}
there are two roots of the equation (
C.8
) for values of
\mathfrak{u}
for which
\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}=\infty
, and therefore the solution
is first of a bell shape and then of a double loop shape
as for
b=0
case. The two roots are inside the interval
(0,2{\tilde{w}}^{2})
until
\alpha
becomes equal to

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}^{(2)}=\frac{2w^{2}-\sqrt{4w^{4}-2|b|}}{b^{2}}\,.

(C.16)

At
\alpha=\alpha_{+}^{(2)}
one gets
d(2{\tilde{w}}^{2})=0
and therefore the larger root is equal to
2{\tilde{w}}^{2}
. Increasing
\alpha
more moves the larger root away from the interval
(0,2{\tilde{w}}^{2})
, and the solution is of a loop shape. Finally, the smaller root leaves the interval
(0,2{\tilde{w}}^{2})
at

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}^{(3)}=\frac{2w^{2}+\sqrt{4w^{4}-2|b|}}{b^{2}}\,,

(C.17)

because
d(2{\tilde{w}}^{2})
is again equal to 0 for
\alpha=\alpha_{+}^{(3)}
. Thus, for

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}^{(3)}<\alpha<\alpha_{\rm max}={4w^{2}\over b^{2}}\,,\quad\frac{4w^{8}}{b^{2}}>1\,,

(C.18)

the solution is regular again.

The discussion above is summarised in eq.(
3.33
).
