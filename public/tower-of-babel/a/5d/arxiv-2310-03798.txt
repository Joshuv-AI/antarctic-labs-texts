The Quantum Spectral Method: From Atomic Orbitals to Classical Self-Force
Majed Khalaf, and Ofri Telem

Abstract
Can classical systems be described analytically at all orders in their interaction strength? For periodic and approximately periodic systems, the answer is yes, as we show in this work. Our analytical approach, which we call the Quantum Spectral Method , is based on a novel application of Bohr’s correspondence principle, obtaining non-perturbative classical dynamics as the classical limit of quantum matrix elements . A major application of our method is the calculation of self-force as the classical limit of atomic radiative transitions. We demonstrate this by calculating an adiabatic electromagnetic inspiral, along with its associated radiation, at all orders in the multipole expansion. Finally, we propose a future application of the Quantum Spectral Method to compute scalar and gravitational self-force in Schwarzschild, analytically.

\displaystyle H\left|j_{r},l,m\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\frac{E_{j_{r},l}-E_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l}}{\hbar\Delta j_{r}}

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{\varphi}_{J_{r},L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\frac{E_{j_{r},l}-E_{j_{r},l-\Delta l}}{\hbar\Delta l}\,.

(20)

Here we wrote
\Upsilon^{i}_{J_{r},L}
to remind the reader that they depend on the classical action variables of the system. The expression on the RH side also depends on these classical quantities, since, before taking the
\hbar\rightarrow 0
limit, one substitutes
(j_{r},l,m)=\hbar^{-1}(J_{r},L,M)
, in accordance with the general prescription (
19
) linking quantum numbers and classical conserved quantities. According to the prescription (
19
) used in (
3
), as
\hbar\rightarrow 0
,
j_{r},l\rightarrow\infty
, exactly as we expect from a classical limit. Their products
J_{r}=\hbar j_{r},\,L=\hbar l,\,M=\hbar m
are finite, dimensionful quantities characterizing the state of the classical system. All the while,
\Delta j_{r},\,\Delta l
and
\Delta m
remain finite integers. The relation (
3
) may seem surprising, but it is nothing but the semiclassical quantization condition for energies in the WKB approximation
Ghatak
, which becomes exact in the
\hbar\rightarrow 0
limit. To see this, consider the leading WKB expression for a radial wavefunction
R_{j_{r},l}(r)
(in any spherically symmetric potential),

\displaystyle R^{{\rm{WKB}}}_{j_{r},l}(r)\propto\,\sin\left[\hbar^{-1}S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)+\frac{\pi}{4}\right]\,,

(21)

where
S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)
in (
21
) is the radial Hamilton-Jacobi function (with a
+
sign up-front),

\displaystyle S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{r_{min}}^{r}\,dr^{\prime}\,\sqrt{U^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r^{\prime})}

\displaystyle U^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 2\mu\,\left(E_{j_{r},l}-V(r)-\frac{\hbar^{2}l(l+1)}{2\mu r^{2}}\right)\,.

(22)

The WKB quantization condition then comes from the requirement that

\displaystyle J_{r}(E=E_{j_{r},l})\equiv\frac{1}{\pi}S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r_{max})=\hbar\,\left(j_{r}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\,,

(23)

which comes from sowing the oscillating WKB wavefunction in the allowed zone
r\in[r_{min},r_{max}]
to decaying WKB wavefunctions at the turning points. The quantization condition (
23
) is the key to proving (
3
). Taking the difference between (
23
) at
j_{r}
and at
j_{r}-\Delta j_{r}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\frac{J_{r}(E=E_{j_{r},l})-J_{r}(E=E_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l})}{\hbar\Delta j_{r}}=1\,,

(24)

while on the other hand

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\frac{J_{r}(E=E_{j_{r},l})-J_{r}(E=E_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l})}{\hbar\Delta j_{r}}=\frac{\partial J_{r}(E_{J_{r},L})}{\partial E_{J_{r},L}}\,\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\frac{E_{j_{r},l}-E_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l}}{\hbar\Delta j_{r}}\,.

(25)

Identifying
1/\Upsilon^{r}_{J_{r},L}=\partial J_{r}/\partial E_{J_{r},L}
according to (
8
), we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\Upsilon^{r}_{J_{r},L}}\frac{E_{j_{r},l}-E_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l}}{\hbar\Delta j_{r}}=1\,,

(26)

from which the first equation of (
3
) follows. As we stressed before, we always substitute
(j_{r},l,m)=\hbar^{-1}(J_{r},L,M)
before taking the
\hbar\rightarrow 0
limit. One can also explicitly check (
26
) for the special case of a Coulomb potential
V(r)=-K/r
, for which
E_{j_{r},l}=E_{n}=-\frac{mK^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}n^{2}}
are the discrete energy eigenvalues of the hydrogen atom, with
n=j_{r}+l+1
.
Similarly, from (
23
), we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\frac{S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r_{max})-S^{r}_{j_{r},l-\Delta l}(r_{max})}{\hbar{\Delta l}}\,=0\,,

(27)

so that

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\left\{\frac{\partial J_{r}}{\partial E_{J_{r},L}}\frac{E_{j_{r},l}-E_{j_{r},l-\Delta l}}{\hbar\Delta l}-\int^{r_{max}}_{r_{min}}\,dr^{\prime}\,\frac{L}{r^{\prime 2}\sqrt{U^{r}_{J_{r},L}(r^{\prime})}}\right\}=0\,.

(28)

Simplifying this expression using (
2
), we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\left\{\frac{1}{\Upsilon^{r}_{J_{r},L}}\frac{E_{j_{r},l}-E_{j_{r},l-\Delta l}}{\hbar\Delta l}-\frac{\Upsilon^{\varphi}_{J_{r},L}}{\Upsilon^{r}_{J_{r},L}}\right\}=0\,,

(29)

from which is the second equation in (
3
) follows.

4
The Quantum Spectral Method For Spherically Symmetric Potentials

In this section we present the main idea of this paper: the calculation of the Fourier coefficients
\mathcal{O}^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}
for any classical observable
\mathcal{O}_{J_{r},L}
as the
\hbar\rightarrow 0
limit of the
quantum matrix elements
associated with
\mathcal{O}
, taken as a
quantum operator
between eigenstates whose eigenvalues are associated with the classical
J_{r},\,L
. We present our method for spherically symmetric potentials to reduce complexity, though it is equally applicable to integrable but not spherically symmetric potentials, like motion in the background of a Kerr black hole. This is because all we need in order to apply our method effectively is a maximal set of conserved quantities / operators.

Without further ado, let us present the “master equation” of our paper:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}\,=\,\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\sum_{\Delta m}\,\left\langle j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l,l-\Delta m\right|\,\mathcal{O}\,\left|j_{r},l,l\right\rangle\,.

(30)

Here,
\mathcal{O}
is regarded as an operator, and
(j_{r},l)=\hbar^{-1}(J_{r},L)
. Here we set the magnetic quantum number
m
on the RHS to be
m=l
. This is because we are focusing, without loss of generality, on motion in the XY plane, for which
L_{z}=L
. As we can see, the
\Delta j_{r}
difference between the two eigenstates that appear in (
30
) coincides with the Fourier integer ”harmony” of the radial motion, and similarly for
\Delta l
and the azimuthal Fourier decomposition.
Due to spherical symmetry, the
\Delta m
does not have an associated fundamental frequency, and it is summed over in (
30
).
The QSM provides an analytic map between quantum matrix elements and classical observables, which holds non-perturbatively in the interaction strength of the system. For the place of the QSM within the landscape of quantum-to-classical methods, see Appendix
A
.

In this section we provide a technical yet straightforward proof of (
30
), again using the WKB method which becomes exact in the
\hbar\rightarrow 0
limit. Finally, while our proof focuses on an energy conserving, non-radiating system, in Section
8
we are able to apply the QSM to a quasi-periodic radiating system as part of PA perturbation theory, using the method of
osculating orbits
.
The proof that we outline here for the QSM master equation (
30
) holds for any spherically symmetric potential. We begin with the quantum wavefunction for the eigenstate
\left|j_{r},l,m\right\rangle
, namely

\displaystyle\Psi_{j_{r},l,m}(r)=(-1)^{l}\,R_{j_{r},l}(r)\,Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\varphi)\,,

(31)

where
R_{j_{r},l}(r)
is the radial wavefunction and
Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\varphi)
is a spherical harmonic. As we are interested in the classical limit, we can take the
\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{0})
WKB expression for
R_{j_{r},l}
to prove the QSM. Our wavefunction (
31
) has a
(-1)^{l}
pre-factor with respect to the conventionally normalized wavefunction of, say, the hydrogen atom. This choice makes the master equation (
30
) more aesthetic – without this factor, the latter would have included a factor of
(-1)^{\Delta l}
up-front.

4.1
Radial WKB Wavefunction and Integration

The radial wavefunction
R_{j_{r},l}(r)
is given, up to next-to-leading order in
\hbar
, by its WKB form

\displaystyle R^{\rm{WKB}}_{j_{r},l}(r)=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{T^{r}_{j_{r},l}}}\,\frac{2}{r\,[U^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)]^{1/4}}\,\sin\left(\frac{1}{\hbar}S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\,,

(32)

where the radial action
S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)
and the effective polynomial
U^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)
were given in (
3
). Importantly, the classical limit of the normalization constant
T^{r}_{j_{r},l}
is the classical radial period
T^{r}_{J_{r},L}
, as can be seen from
3
3

3

Technically, the integration region should be
(0,\infty)
. However, the classically forbidden region, namely outside (
r_{\rm{min}},r_{\rm{max}}
), is exponentially decaying and its contribution vanishes in the classical limit.

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\int_{r_{min}}^{r_{max}}\,r^{2}\,R^{\rm{WKB}*}_{j_{r},l}(r)\,R^{\rm{WKB}}_{j_{r},l}(r)\,dr=\,\frac{2}{T^{r}_{j_{r},l}}\,\int_{r_{min}}^{r_{max}}\,\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{U^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)}}\,dr=1\,,

(33)

by virtue of (
8
). Here the
\sin
factor became
1/2
in the
\hbar\rightarrow 0
limit – this would not be the case for matrix elements between
j_{r}
and
j^{\prime}_{r}\neq j_{r}
. From now on we drop the WKB label on
R_{j_{r},l}(r)
.
To evaluate matrix elements in the classical limit, we need the radial integral

\displaystyle I^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}[f]\equiv\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\int_{r_{min}}^{{r_{max}}}dr\,r^{2}\,f(r)\,R^{*}_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l}R_{j_{r},l}(r)\,.

(34)

As usual, we take
(j_{r},l)=\hbar^{-1}(J_{r},L)
before taking the
\hbar\rightarrow 0
limit. From (
32
) we have,

\displaystyle I^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}[f]=\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\frac{4\mu}{T^{r}_{j_{r},l}}\,\int_{r_{min}}^{{r_{max}}}dr\,\frac{f(r)}{\sqrt{U^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)}}\,\sin\left(\frac{1}{\hbar}S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\sin\left(\frac{1}{\hbar}S^{r}_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l}(r)+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\,.

The above integral looks much better when we change integration variables from
r
to
\alpha_{r}
using (
10
) (with a
+
sign since
0\leq\alpha^{r}<\pi
),

\displaystyle I^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}[f]=\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}4\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{r}}{2\pi}\,f(\alpha^{r})\,\sin\left(\frac{1}{\hbar}S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(\alpha^{r})+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\sin\left(\frac{1}{\hbar}S^{r}_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l}(\alpha^{r})+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\,.

In the above equation we used a slight abuse of notation in writing
S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(\alpha^{r})
and
f(\alpha^{r})
instead of the more exact
S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r(\alpha^{r}))
and
f[r(\alpha^{r})]
. Because
r(\pi-\alpha^{r})=r(\pi+\alpha^{r})
for
0\leq\alpha^{r}\leq\pi
, we can double the integration region for
\alpha^{r}
while multiplying the result by a
1/2
,

\displaystyle I^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}[f]=2\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{r}}{2\pi}\,f(\alpha^{r})\,\sin\left(\frac{1}{\hbar}S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(\alpha^{r})+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\sin\left(\frac{1}{\hbar}S^{r}_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l}(\alpha^{r})+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\,.

Using a basic trigonometric identity we then have

\displaystyle I^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}[f]=

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{r}}{2\pi}\,f(\alpha^{r})\,\left[\cos\left(\frac{S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(\alpha^{r})-S^{r}_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l}(\alpha^{r})}{\hbar}\right)+\sin\left(\frac{S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(\alpha^{r})+S^{r}_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l}(\alpha^{r})}{\hbar}\right)\right]\,.

In the second term
S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)+S^{r}_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l}(r)=\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{0})
and so this term gives a vanishing oscillatory contribution, so that

\displaystyle I^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}[f]=\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{r}}{2\pi}\,f(\alpha^{r})\,\cos\left(\frac{S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(\alpha^{r})-S^{r}_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l}(\alpha^{r})}{\hbar}\right)\,.

(39)

On the other hand we know that

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\frac{S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(\alpha^{r})-S^{r}_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l}(\alpha^{r})}{\hbar}=\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\left[\frac{S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(\alpha^{r})-S^{r}_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l}(\alpha^{r})}{\hbar}+\frac{S^{r}_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l}(\alpha^{r})-S^{r}_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l}(\alpha^{r})}{\hbar}\right]=

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\left[\Delta j_{r}\frac{\partial S^{r}_{J_{r},L}(\alpha^{r})}{\partial E_{J_{r},L}}\frac{E_{j_{r},l}-E_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l}}{\hbar\Delta j_{r}}+\Delta l\left(\frac{\partial S^{r}_{J_{r},L}(\alpha^{r})}{\partial E_{J_{r},L}}\frac{E_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l}-E_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l}}{\hbar\Delta l}-\frac{\partial S^{r}_{J_{r},L}(\alpha^{r})}{\partial L}\right)\right]=

\displaystyle\Delta j_{r}\frac{\partial S^{r}_{J_{r},L}(\alpha^{r})}{\partial E_{J_{r},L}}\Upsilon^{r}_{Jr,L}+\Delta l\left(\frac{\partial S^{r}_{J_{r},L}(\alpha^{r})}{\partial E_{J_{r},L}}\Upsilon^{\varphi}_{Jr,L}-\frac{\partial S^{r}_{J_{r},L}(\alpha^{r})}{\partial L}\right)\,.

(40)

To get the last line of (
4.1
), we made use of (
3
). All-in-all, we have

\displaystyle I^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}[f]=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{r}}{2\pi}\,f(\alpha^{r})\,\cos\left\{\Delta j_{r}\frac{\partial S^{r}_{J_{r},L}(\alpha^{r})}{\partial E_{J_{r},L}}\Upsilon^{r}_{Jr,L}+\Delta l\left(\frac{\partial S^{r}_{J_{r},L}(\alpha^{r})}{\partial E_{J_{r},L}}\Upsilon^{\varphi}_{Jr,L}-\frac{\partial S^{r}_{J_{r},L}(\alpha^{r})}{\partial L}\right)\right\}\,.

We now use (

) from Appendix
B
that allows us to relate
I^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}[f]
to an integral over
\alpha^{\varphi}
as

\displaystyle I^{J_{r}L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}[f]=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{r}}{2\pi}\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{\varphi}}{2\pi}\,f(\alpha^{r})\,\cos\left\{\Delta j_{r}\alpha^{r}+\Delta l\,\left[\alpha^{\varphi}-\varphi(\alpha^{r},\alpha^{\varphi})\right]\right\}\,.

(42)

Finally, we use the fact that
r(2\pi-\alpha_{r})=r(\alpha_{r})
while

\displaystyle I^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}[f]=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{r}}{2\pi}\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{\varphi}}{2\pi}\,f(\alpha^{r})\,e^{-i\Delta l\varphi(\alpha^{r},\alpha^{\varphi})}\,\exp{\left\{i\Delta j_{r}\alpha^{r}+i\Delta l\,\alpha^{\varphi}\right\}}\,,

(43)

which already looks very close to a (double) inverse Fourier transform in
\alpha^{r}
and
\alpha^{\varphi}

4.2
From Matrix Elements to Fourier Coefficients

Now consider the matrix element for an operator
\mathcal{O}(r,\theta,\varphi)
, leaving the generalization to momentum dependent operators to Appendix
C
. First, let’s decompose
\mathcal{O}(r,\theta,\varphi)
in spherical harmonics as

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(r,\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{l_{\gamma}=0}^{\infty}\,\sum_{m_{\gamma}=-l_{\gamma}}^{l_{\gamma}}\,\mathcal{O}_{l_{\gamma},m_{\gamma}}(r)\,Y_{l_{\gamma}}^{m_{\gamma}}\left(\theta,\varphi\right)\,.

(44)

Now we can take the classical limit of the matrix element of
\mathcal{O}
. As we are considering, without loss of generality, motion in the XY plane with
L_{z}=L
and so, accordingly, our initial
m
is equal to
l
. The matrix element is

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\sum_{\Delta m}\,\left\langle j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l,l-\Delta m\right|\,\mathcal{O}\,\left|j_{r},l,l\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\sum_{\Delta m}\,\sum_{l_{\gamma},m_{\gamma}}\,(-1)^{\Delta l}\,I^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}\left[\mathcal{O}_{l_{\gamma},m_{\gamma}}(r)\right]\,\left\langle l^{\prime},m^{\prime}\right|Y_{l_{\gamma}}^{m_{\gamma}}\left(\theta,\varphi\right)\left|l,l\right\rangle\,.

(45)

Here the
(-1)^{\Delta l}
factor came from the overall
(-1)^{l}
phase in the definition of our eigenfunctions (
31
). We remind the reader that in the equation above
\theta,\varphi
are operators whose expectation value is taken between the bra and the ket. The classical limit of the angular part is computed in (
207
) of Appendix
F
, which reads

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\left\langle l-\Delta l,l-\Delta m\right|Y_{l_{\gamma}}^{m_{\gamma}}\left(\theta,\varphi\right)\left|l,l\right\rangle=\delta_{\Delta l,\Delta m}\delta_{-\Delta l,m_{\gamma}}\,(-1)^{m_{\gamma}}\,Y_{l_{\gamma}}^{m_{\gamma}}\left(\pi/2,0\right)\,.

(46)

On the other hand
I^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}\left[\mathcal{O}_{l_{\gamma},m_{\gamma}}(r)\right]
is computed from (
43
). We get

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\sum_{\Delta m}\,\left\langle j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l,l-\Delta m\right|\,\mathcal{O}\,\left|j_{r},l,l\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{r}}{2\pi}\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{\varphi}}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\sum_{l_{\gamma},m_{\gamma}}\,\left\{\mathcal{O}^{J_{r},L}_{l_{\gamma},m_{\gamma}}[r(\alpha^{r})]\,Y_{l_{\gamma}}^{m_{\gamma}}\left(\pi/2,\varphi(\alpha^{r},\alpha^{\varphi})\right)\right\}\,\exp{\left\{i\Delta j_{r}\alpha^{r}+i\Delta l\,\alpha^{\varphi}\right\}}\,.

(47)

Here we dropped the
\delta_{-\Delta l,m_{\gamma}}
factor, which is redundant since the
\alpha^{\varphi}
integral vanishes for

. Resumming the

to get back

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\sum_{\Delta m}\,\left\langle j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l,l-\Delta m\right|\,\mathcal{O}\,\left|j_{r},l,l\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{r}}{2\pi}\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\alpha^{\varphi}}{2\pi}\mathcal{O}_{J_{r},L}[r(\alpha^{r}),\pi/2,\varphi(\alpha^{r},\alpha^{\varphi})]\,\exp{\left\{i\Delta j_{r}\alpha^{r}+i\Delta l\,\alpha^{\varphi}\right\}}=\mathcal{O}^{J_{r},L}_{\Delta j_{r},\Delta l}\,.

The last line is simply the definition of the classical Fourier coefficients in (
17

This completes the proof of the QSM master equation (

5
Keplerian Motion in a Coulomb Potential: A System With A Single Fundamental Frequency

In the first three applications of the QSM presented in this paper, we choose to focus on a special case of (
1
), namely the case of a Coulomb potential

\displaystyle V(r)=-\frac{K}{r}\,,

(49)

where
K=\frac{Zq^{2}}{4\pi}
(we take
\varepsilon_{0}=1
),
\mu
is the reduced mass,
Z
is the atomic number and
q
is the electron Coulomb charge. An explicit evaluation of (
6
) for the Coulomb potential (
49
) reveals that

\displaystyle N\equiv J_{r}+J_{\theta}+J_{\varphi}=J_{r}+L=\sqrt{-\frac{\mu K^{2}}{2E}}\,.

(50)

The variable
N
has a natural interpretation as the classical limit of the principle quantum number of the hydrogen atom, via
n=\hbar^{-1}N
. Furthermore, the Coulomb case is special in the sense that the motion is doubly degenerate so that
H(J_{r},J_{\theta},J\varphi)=H(J_{r}+J_{\theta}+J_{\varphi})
and
\Upsilon^{\theta}_{N,L}=\Upsilon^{\varphi}_{N,L}=\Upsilon^{r}_{N,L}\equiv\Upsilon_{N,L}
. In the Coulomb case we will always use the more appropriate label
{N,L}
instead of
J_{r},\,L
. In other words, in the Coulomb case, the motion involves a single angle variable
\alpha
and a single fundamental frequency
\Upsilon_{N,L}
. The fundamental frequency and time period are then

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{N,L}=\frac{\mu K^{2}}{N^{3}}=\frac{2}{K}\sqrt{-\frac{2E^{3}}{\mu}}~~,~~T_{N,L}=2\pi\Upsilon^{-1}_{N,L}=\pi K\sqrt{-\frac{\mu}{2E^{3}}}\,.

(51)

and
\alpha=\Upsilon_{N,L}t
. Since the motion is singly periodic, we can express any time-dependent observable
\mathcal{O}_{N,L}(t)=\mathcal{O}_{N,L}[\alpha(t)]
in a single Fourier series. In this case it is more convenient to label observables in terms of
N
and
L
via (
50
). The Fourier representation of
\mathcal{O}_{N,L}(t)
is then

\mathcal{O}_{N,L}\left(t\right)=\mathcal{O}_{N,L}\left[\alpha(t)\right]=\sum_{\Delta n}\,\mathcal{O}^{N,L}_{\Delta n}\exp{\left\{-i\Delta n\alpha(t)\right\}}\,,

(52)

where
\Delta n
is an integer (the index of the
\Delta n_{th}
Fourier harmony), and

\mathcal{O}^{N,L}_{\Delta n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\,\frac{d\alpha}{2\pi}\,\mathcal{O}_{N,L}[\alpha]\exp{\left\{i\Delta n\alpha\right\}}\,.

(53)

The extra degeneracy of the Coulomb Hamiltonian allows for an explicit analytical solution for
r_{N,L}(\alpha),\,\varphi_{N,L}(\alpha)
. This solution is simply
Keplerian motion
. For bound orbits with
E<0
, the motion is parametrized by the ellipse
Goldstein2011

r_{N,L}(\alpha)=\frac{p}{1+e\cos[\varphi_{N,L}(\alpha)]}\,,

(54)

where the semi-latus-rectum
p
and the eccentricity
e
are related to
E,\,L
by

p=\frac{L^{2}}{\mu K}~~~,~~e=\sqrt{1-\frac{L^{2}}{N^{2}}}~~\rightarrow~~N=\sqrt{\frac{\mu Kp}{1-e^{2}}}\,.

(55)

Similarly to any other observable of the system, the radius
r_{N,L}(\alpha)
can be expressed in a single Fourier series as

r_{N,L}\left(\alpha\right)=\sum_{\Delta n=-\infty}^{\infty}\,r^{N,L}_{\Delta n}\exp{\left\{-i\Delta n\alpha\right\}}\,,

(56)

where the coefficients are related to
r[\alpha]
via

\displaystyle r^{N,L}_{\Delta n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\,\frac{d\alpha}{2\pi}\,r_{N,L}(\alpha)\exp{\left\{i\Delta n\alpha\right\}}\,.

(57)

We put the labels
N,\,L
on
r_{N,L}
and its Fourier coefficients to emphasize that they depend on the conserved action variables of the system.
The expansion (
56
) is more commonly rearranged as

r_{N,L}\left(\alpha\right)=\sum_{\Delta n=0}^{\infty}\,\widetilde{r}^{N,L}_{\Delta n}\cos\left[{\Delta n\alpha}\right]\,,

(58)

where
\widetilde{r}^{N,L}_{\Delta n=0}=r^{N,L}_{\Delta n=0}
and
\widetilde{r}^{N,L}_{\Delta n}=2r^{N,L}_{\Delta n}
otherwise. An explicit solution of the EOM (
4
) with the Coulomb potential (
49
) yields
Brouwer1961

\displaystyle\widetilde{r}^{N,L}_{\Delta n}=\frac{p}{1-e^{2}}\begin{cases}1+\frac{\,e^{2}}{2}&\Delta n=0\\
-\frac{2e}{\Delta n^{2}}\frac{d}{de}J_{\Delta n}(e\Delta n)&\Delta n>0\end{cases}\,.

(59)

\varphi(\alpha)
, on the other hand, is commonly expressed using the eccentric anomaly
\beta(\alpha)
as

\displaystyle\beta(\alpha)\equiv 2\arctan\left\{\sqrt{\frac{1-e}{1+e}}\tan[\varphi(\alpha)/2]\right\}~~~,~~~\varphi(\alpha)=2\arctan\left\{\sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan\left[\beta(\alpha)/2\right]\right\}\,.

The Fourier series for
\beta(\alpha)
is then
Brouwer1961

\displaystyle\beta(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha+2\sum_{\Delta n=1}^{\infty}\,\frac{1}{\Delta n}J_{\Delta n}(\Delta n\,e)\,\sin\left(\Delta n\alpha\right)\,.

(61)

In the parlance of the Kepler problem and celestial motion, one usually refers to the angle
\alpha
as the
mean anomaly

EM Self-Force with the QSM

The natural application of the QSM is in the calculation of the self-force on an inspiralling body – a crucial ingredient for the determination of its trajectory and associated waveform. In this section, we calculate the self-force for the case of an inspiralling classical electron, along with its adiabatic trajectory and associated radiation (waveform). The calculation is done using the method of

osculating orbits

Brouwer1961
. This is a parameterization of the true trajectory of an inspiralling object as a slow transition between different Keplerian orbits, each one momentarily tangent (”osculating”) to the true trajectory.
Practically, this means that in addition to the mean anomaly
\alpha(t)
, we need to keep track of the time-dependent orbital elements
(e(t),\,p(t))
(equivalently
E(t)
and
L(t)
) of the osculating orbit to describe the inspiralling trajectory. Importantly, the method of osculating orbits is not an approximation but rather a parameterization of the full trajectory. The osculation method becomes particularly useful when the self-force is relatively weak compared with the external force (Coulomb force here), i.e., as long as the energy and angular momentum loss rates are slow, i.e.
\frac{T_{N,L}\dot{E}}{E}\sim\frac{T_{N,L}\dot{L}}{L}\ll 1
, where
T_{N,L}
is the rotation period. This allows for the perturbative treatment of the self-force, giving rise to the PA expansion
Hinderer2008
. In this work, we limit ourselves to the leading order in the PA expansion, also called the adiabatic (0PA) order. At this order, the dissipation rates of
\dot{E}
and
\dot{L}
are calculated for the osculating Keplerian orbit, without back-reaction.

Putting things more concretely, consider the EOM for a classical electron in a central potential, with an additional small self-force

9.2
WKB Proof of the QSM for Schwarzschild

The essential ingredient of the WKB proof in Section
4
was the relation between the WKB eigenfunctions (
32
) of the Hamiltonian (
1
) and the Hamilton-Jacobi radial function
S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)
. A similar relation persists for the Hamiltonian (
112
). This time, we are interested in
H
acting not on an eigenfunction but rather on a
field

\Phi
. The constraint
H=-\mu/2
then gives

\displaystyle H\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu}{2}\Phi\,.

(134)

Importantly,
\Phi
here does not have the interpretation of a wavefunction, but rather of a field. This does not matter for us, since all we need is for the WKB solution for
\Phi
to be related to
S^{r}(r)
. Quantum mechanically, we interpret
p_{\mu}
as
-i\hbar\partial_{\mu}
, and so (
134
) gives us the massive
Klein-Gordon
equation in a Schwarzschild background,

\displaystyle\left(g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}-\frac{\mu^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\right)\Phi=0\,.

(135)

Separation of variables in a spherically symmetric problem naturally gives us

\displaystyle\Phi_{j_{r},l,m}=(-1)^{l}\,e^{i\hbar^{-1}E_{j_{r},l}t}\,R_{j_{r},l}(r)\,Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\varphi)\,.

(136)

The attentive reader may be worried that the solution to the Klein-Gordon equation could be unstable in the presence of the black hole horizon. Furthermore, one could worry that there may not be stable solutions. We are happy to dispel both of these worries for the following reasons:

1)

For us, the horizon is always behind an angular momentum barrier. The effect of tunnelling into the horizon always vanishes in the classical limit that we are interested in. For this reason, all of our bound states are strictly stable in the
\hbar\rightarrow 0
limit.

2)

Contrary to the case of
massless
excitations of Schwarzschild/Kerr, which only have a bound orbit at the light ring, our Klein-Gordon is
massive
. The mass leads to a finite potential well that gives rise to honest bound states.

The leading WKB solution for
R_{j_{r},l}
in (
136
) is

\displaystyle R_{j_{r},l}(r)=\sqrt{\frac{2E_{j_{r},l}\,\Omega^{r}_{j_{r},l}}{\pi}}\,\frac{1}{[U^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)]^{1/4}}\,\sin\left(\frac{1}{\hbar}S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\,,

(137)

which is similar to (
32
), this time with

\displaystyle S^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{r_{\rm min}}^{r}\,\frac{\sqrt{U^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r^{\prime})}}{\Delta(r^{\prime})}\,dr^{\prime}

\displaystyle U^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{4}\left[E^{2}_{j_{r},l}-\frac{\Delta(r)}{r^{2}}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}l(l+1)}{r^{2}}+\mu^{2}\right)\right]\,.

(138)

Repeating the arguments of Section
4
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\int_{r_{\rm min}}^{r_{\rm max}}\,dr\,\frac{r^{4}}{\Delta(r)}\,R^{*}_{j_{r},l}(r)R_{j_{r},l}(r)\,=\,\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\Omega^{r}_{j_{r},l}\,\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{r_{\rm min}}^{r_{\rm max}}\,dr\,\,\frac{E_{j_{r},l}r^{4}}{\Delta(r)\sqrt{U^{r}_{j_{r},l}(r)}}=1\,.

(139)

Here the

). Hence, indeed,
R_{j_{r},l}
is correctly normalized.
The analog of (

\displaystyle\Omega^{r}_{J_{r},L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\frac{E_{j_{r},l}-E_{j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l}}{\hbar\Delta j_{r}}

\displaystyle\Omega^{\varphi}_{J_{r},L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(r,\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{l_{\gamma}=0}^{\infty}\,\sum_{m_{\gamma}=-l_{\gamma}}^{l_{\gamma}}\,\mathcal{O}_{l_{\gamma},m_{\gamma}}(r)\,Y_{l_{\gamma}}^{m_{\gamma}}\left(\theta,\varphi\right)\,,

(141)

the relevant classical matrix element is

\displaystyle\,\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\sum_{\Delta m}\,\left\langle j_{r}-\Delta j_{r},l-\Delta l,l-\Delta m\right|\,\mathcal{O}\,\left|j_{r},l,l\right\rangle=\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\,\sum_{\Delta m}\,\sum_{l_{\gamma},m_{\gamma}}\delta_{\Delta l,\Delta m}\delta_{-\Delta l,m_{\gamma}}

Future Applications of the QSM

The main future application of the QSM would be to calculate the GSF analytically. A standard practice in the field when introducing new computational methods is to first demonstrate them in the simpler case of a
scalar
self-force in a black-hole background
Barack2023
;
Bini:2024icd
;
Long:2024ltn
. This is particularly useful when the previewed method is not inherently related to GR, as is the case here. By a scalar self-force we mean that the inspiralling body is charged under a new, massless scalar. As the body moves in the BH background, it emits a scalar field, much like the emission of the EM field in Section
7
. In turn, the emitted scalar generates radiation-reaction on the inspiralling mass, the scalar, curved space analog of (
8
). In this section, we will not explicitly calculate the scalar self-force – we leave that for future work. We will, however, outline the required steps to calculate it and by analogy 1st order GSF in Schwarzschild.

The Green’s function for a massless scalar in Schwarzschild is the solution to the inhomogeneous Klein-Gordon equation

\displaystyle g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}G^{\phi}_{\rm ret}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\delta^{(4)}(x-x^{\prime})\,,

(144)

where
g
is the Schwarzschild metric. It is given by
Bini2022a

\displaystyle G^{\phi}_{\rm ret}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{\Theta(t-t^{\prime})}{2\pi}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,d\omega\,e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\,\left\{i\omega\,\sum_{l_{\phi}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{W}\,R^{\rm{in}}_{l_{\phi}}(\omega r_{<})R^{\rm{up}}_{l_{\phi}}(\omega r_{>})\right.

\displaystyle\left.\,\sum_{m_{\phi}=-l_{\phi}}^{l_{\phi}}\,Y^{m_{\phi}*}_{l_{\phi}}(\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})Y_{l_{\phi}}^{m_{\phi}}(\theta,\varphi)\right\}\,,

(145)

where
\{r_{<},r_{>}\}=\{{\rm min}(r,r^{\prime}),{\rm max}(r,r^{\prime})\}
, and we will focus on the case where
r^{\prime}<r
. Here
R^{\rm{in}}_{l_{\phi}}
(
R^{\rm{up}}_{l_{\phi}}
) are the solutions of the Schwarzschild-background Klein-Gordon equation with ”in” (”up”)-type boundary conditions, and
W
is their (constant) Wronskian

\displaystyle W=\Delta\,\left(R^{\rm{in}}_{l_{\phi}}\partial_{r}R^{\rm{up}}_{l_{\phi}}-R^{\rm{up}}_{l_{\phi}}\partial_{r}R^{\rm{in}}_{l_{\phi}}\right)\,.

(146)

We will not provide explicit expressions for

and

Bini2022a

MST

Using the Green’s function (
10

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm ret}(x)=\int d^{4}x^{\prime}\,G^{\phi}_{\rm ret}(t,\vec{x};t^{\prime},\vec{x}{{}^{\prime}})\,\delta^{(3)}(\vec{x}^{\prime}-\vec{x}_{J_{r},L}(t^{\prime}))\,,

11
Conclusions

In this paper we presented the Quantum Spectral Method (QSM) for the calculation of classical Fourier coefficients from their corresponding quantum matrix elements. The QSM allows for the exact analytical calculation of classical quantities that so far have only been calculated numerically. We also proved the QSM for general motion is a spherically symmetric potential, using the WKB method.

To demonstrate the power of the QSM, we applied it to the case of a classical electron moving in a
1/r
potential. By taking the classical limit of hydrogen-like atom matrix elements, we were able to analytically compute to all multipole orders: (a) time dependent Keplerian motion, (b) the EM field radiated by a classical electron in a (quasi)-Keplerian orbit, and (c) an adiabatic EM inspiral and its associated waveform. Lastly, we uncovered a direct connection between the quantum expressions for spontaneous emission, and the orbit-averaged expressions for the classical energy and angular momentum loss (
106
) and (
110
).

As the classical limit of quantum bound states, the QSM is non-perturbative in the coupling constant, and so it is naturally suitable for self-force/PA calculations. This is in contrast to other quantum-to-classical approaches which start from scattering amplitudes, and are thus inherently perturbative in the coupling constant. It demonstrates, for the first time, the way the correspondence principle applies quantitatively, exposing the quantum roots of both the conservative and dissipative behavior of quasi-periodic systems. As such, it provides the missing link between adiabatic invariants and quantum mechanics, a century after their concurrent foundation.
