Geometric models for the algebraic hearts in the derived category of a gentle algebra Thanks: The author is supported by the Fundamental Research Funds for the Central Universities (No. GK202403003) and the NSF of China (Grant No. 12271321).
Wen Chang

Abstract
. We give a geometric model for any algebraic heart in the derived category of a gentle algebra, which is equivalent to the module category of some gentle algebra. To do this, we deform the geometric model for the module category of a gentle algebra given in [ BC21 ] , and then embed it into the geometric model of the derived category given in [ OPS18 ] , in the sense that each so-called zigzag curve on the surface represents an indecomposable module as well as the minimal projective resolution of this module. A key point of this embedding is to give a geometric explanation of the duality between the simple modules and the projective modules. Such a blend of two geometric models provides us with a handy way to describe the homological properties of a module within the framework of the derived category. In particular, we realize any higher Yoneda-extension as a polygon on the surface, and realize the Yoneda-product as gluing of these polygons. As an application, we realize any algebraic heart in the derived category of a gentle algebra on the marked surface.

Introduction

In recent years, topological and geometric methods have been used more and more widely in the representation theory of algebras.
There are many works on geometric models for various categories associated with an algebra.
A geometric model allows one to use surface combinatorics/topology to produce many applications, i.e.
realize arcs as indecomposables, intersections as morphisms, rotations of arcs as Auslander-Reiten translations, cuts of the surfaces as reductions of the categories, etc.

It has been shown that the surface models are powerful tools to solve a number of representation-theoretic questions on the module/derived categories of algebras, for example, classifying torsion pairs
[
BBM14
,
CD20
,
CP16
,
HJR11
]
; giving the derived invariants of algebras
[
AG16
,
ALP21
,
APS23
,
D14
,
O19
,
OZ22
,
LP20
]
; confirming the connectedness of support
\tau
-tilting graphs
[
FGLZ23
]
and studying silting theory and the theory of exceptional sequences
[
APS23
,
CJS22
,
CHS23
,
CS23a
,
CS23b
]
etc.

The use of a surface model to study the representations of an algebra originates from the cluster theory.
In
[
FST08
]
, the authors discover a cluster algebra structure on an oriented surface with boundaries and marked points. They interpret a cluster of the cluster algebra as a triangulation of the surface and a mutation of the cluster as a flip of the triangulation.

Inspired by the surface model for a cluster algebra, the surface model for a gentle algebra in the form of the Jacobian algebra of a triangulation is studied in
[
ABCJP10
,
L09
]
(also in
[
BZ11
,
CCS06
]
).
In
[
DS12
]
, the authors deform Jacobian algebras of triangulations and introduce another class of gentle algebras so-called surface cut algebras.

More recently, for any gentle algebra, the geometric models for its module category and its derived category are established by using partial triangulations (or dissections) of the surfaces in
[
BC21
]
and
[
OPS18
]
respectively. For a skew-gentle algebra, a generalization of a gentle algebra, the surface models for the associated module/derived categories are also established, for example, in
[
A21
,
AB22
,
HZZ23
,
LSV22
,
QZZ22
]
.

Gentle algebras are classical objects in the representation theory of associative algebras
[
AH81
,
AS87
]
.
Remarkably, they connect to many other areas of mathematics. For example, they play an important role in the homological mirror symmetry of surfaces, see e.g.
[
B16
,
BD18
,
HKK17
]
. In particular, it has been shown that the perfect derived category of a homologically smooth graded gentle algebra is triangle equivalent to the partially wrapped Fukaya category of a graded oriented smooth surface with marked points on the boundary
[
HKK17
,
LP20
]
.

In this paper, we give a geometric model of any algebraic heart in the (bounded) derived category of a gentle algebra. A heart of a triangulated category is a full abelian subcategory, which is a key part in the construction of Brigdeland’s stability conditions
[
B07
]
. More precisely, a stability condition is equivalent to a heart with a stability function satisfying the so-called Harder-Narasimhan property. So a stability condition can be thought of as a continuous generalization of a heart. A heart is called an algebraic heart, if it is a length category and has finitely many isomorphism classes of simple objects.
Note that the stability conditions, and hence the hearts, on the derived category of a (graded) gentle algebra have been studied in
[
HKK17
]
, while the geometric model established in this paper is from the perspective of representation theory, which is more concrete in some sense.
We also mention that the study of hearts in the bounded derived categories of a subclass of gentle algebras and the corresponding stability conditions
was carried out in
[
BPP16
,
BPP17
]
.

More precisely, we will show that an algebraic heart in the derived category of a gentle algebra is equivalent to the module category of a gentle algebra.
Keeping this in mind, we give geometric models of the algebraic hearts by finding out module categories of various gentle algebras in the surface model of the derived category of a given gentle algebra
A
.

However, note that the geometric model of the module category
\operatorname{{\rm mod-A}}
in
[

and the geometric model of the derived category

[

]
and unify these two models on the same surface, see more explanation for the differences between the geometric model in
[
BC21
]
and this paper in Remark

1.
Preliminaries

In this paper, an algebra will be assumed to be basic with finite dimension over a base field
k
which is algebraically closed. A quiver will be denoted by
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1})
, where
Q_{0}
is the set of vertices and
Q_{1}
is the set of arrows. For an arrow
{\bf{a}}
,
s({\bf{a}})
is the source and
t({\bf{a}})
is the target of it.
Arrows in a quiver are composed from left to right as follows: for arrows
{\bf{a}}
and
{\bf{b}}
we write
{\bf{a}}{\bf{b}}
for the path from the source of
{\bf{a}}
to the target of
{\bf{b}}
.
We adopt the convention that maps are also composed from left to right, that is if
f:X\to Y
and
g:Y\to Z
then
fg:X\to Z
. In general, we consider right modules.
We denote by
\mathbb{Z}
the set of integer numbers, and by
\mathbb{N}
the set of positive integer numbers.
For a finite set
M
, we denote by
|M|
its cardinality.

1.1.
Module categories of gentle algebras

In this subsection, we recall some basic definitions and properties of a gentle algebra and its module category.

Definition 1.1
.

We call an algebra
A=kQ/I
a
gentle algebra
, if
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1})
is a finite quiver and
I
is an admissible ideal of
kQ
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

Each vertex in
Q_{0}
is the source of at most two arrows and the target of at most two arrows.

(2)

For each arrow
{\bf{a}}
in
Q_{1}
, there is at most one arrow
{\bf{b}}
such that
{\bf{0}}\neq{\bf{a}}{\bf{b}}\in I
; at most one arrow
{\bf{c}}
such that
{\bf{0}}\neq{\bf{c}}{\bf{a}}\in I
; at most one arrow
{\bf{b}}^{\prime}
such that
{\bf{a}}{\bf{b}}^{\prime}\notin I
; at most one arrow
{\bf{c}}^{\prime}
such that
{\bf{c}}^{\prime}{\bf{a}}\notin I
.

(3)

I
is generated by paths of length two.

Throughout this section,
A=kQ/I
denotes a gentle algebra. It is known that any finite dimensional indecomposable right A-module is either a string module or a band module, which is parameterized by string and band combinatorics respectively.
We briefly recall these results here and we refer the reader to
[
BR87
]
for more details.

For an arrow
{\bf{a}}
, let
{\bf{a}}^{-1}
be its
formal inverse
with
s({\bf{a}}^{-1})=t({\bf{a}})
and
t({\bf{a}}^{-1})=s({\bf{a}})
. A
walk
is a (possibly infinite) sequence
\cdots\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\cdots\omega_{m}\cdots
of arrows and inverse arrows in
Q
such that
t(\omega_{i})=s(\omega_{i+1})
and
\omega_{i+1}\neq\omega^{-1}_{i}
for each
i
.
A walk
\omega
is called a
finite walk
, if it consists of finite number of arrows and formal inverses.

A
string
is a finite walk
\omega
that avoids relations, that is, there is no subsequence of
\omega
or of
\omega^{-1}
belongs to
I
.
A
direct string
is a string consisting of arrows, and an
inverse string
is a string consisting of formal inverses.
For each vertex
v
, we associate a
trivial string

1_{v}
to it.
Each string
\omega
defines a
string module

M(\omega)
, which is given by the quiver representation of type
A
obtained by replacing every vertex in
\omega
by a copy of
k
and every arrow by the identity map.
This gives a bijection between the inversion equivalent classes of strings and the isomorphism classes of string modules. In particular,
M(1_{v})
is the simple module arising from the vertex
v
of
Q
.

A
band
is a string
\omega
such that any power
\omega^{n}
is a string and
\omega
itself is not a proper power of any string.
Each band
\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\cdots\omega_{n}
defines a family of
band modules

M(\omega,m,\lambda)
for a positive integer
m\in\mathbb{N}
and a parameter
\lambda\in k^{*}
.
More precisely, each vertex of
\omega
is replaced by a copy of the vector space
k^{m}
and the action of an arrow
\omega_{i}
(or
\omega^{-1}_{i}
) is induced by identities if
1\leq i\leq n-1
and by
\phi(\lambda)
if
i=n
, where
\phi(\lambda)
is an irreducible automorphism of
k^{m}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
. Note that since we assume that the base field
k
is algebraically closed, after choosing a basis of the vector spaces, an irreducible automorphism
\phi(\lambda)
is given by a Jordan-block
B(\lambda)_{m}
with rank
m
and eigenvalue
\lambda
.
Furthermore, up to inversion and rotation, the bands together with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda\in k^{*}
form an indexing set of band modules.
Note that for a given band
\omega
and a parameter
\lambda
, all the band modules
M(\omega,m,\lambda)
form a homogenous tube in the AR-quiver of the module category, where the module
M(\omega,1,\lambda)
is at the mouth of tube, which will be called a
quasi-simple band module
.

1.2.
Derived categories of gentle algebras

In this subsection, we recall the classification of indecomposable objects in the derived category of a gentle algebra
A=kQ/I
, by using homotopy strings and homotopy bands introduced in
[
B11
]
. We refer the reader to
[
BM03
,
B11
]
for more details.

A
homotopy string

\sigma=\cdots\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\cdots\omega_{n}\cdots
on
(Q,I)
is a walk in
Q
consisting of subwalks
\ldots,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{r},\ldots
with
\sigma=\cdots\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{r}\cdots

and such that

(1)

each
\sigma_{i}
is a direct or inverse string;

(2)

if both
\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}
are direct (resp. inverse) strings then
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}
(resp.
\sigma_{i+1}^{-1}\sigma_{i}^{-1}
)
\in I
;

(3)

if
\sigma
is infinite from the right (resp. left), then there exists
l
such that
\sigma_{l}\sigma_{l+1}\ldots
(resp.
\ldots\sigma_{l-1}\sigma_{l}
) is eventually periodic with each
\sigma_{i},i\geq l
(resp.
i\leq l
) an arrow (resp. formal inverse arrow) in
Q
.

For an infinite homotopy string, we will use the following notation:

\sigma=(\sigma_{-s}\cdots\sigma_{-2}\sigma_{-1})^{\infty}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{n}(\sigma_{n+1}\sigma_{n+2}\cdots\sigma_{n+t})^{\infty},

where
\sigma_{-i}
are formal inverse arrows for
1\leq i\leq s
, and
\sigma_{n+i}
are direct arrows for
1\leq i\leq t
.

A
homotopy band
is a finite homotopy string
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{r}
with an equal number of direct and inverse strings
\sigma_{i}
such that any power
\sigma^{n}
is a homotopy string and
\sigma
itself is not a proper power of any homotopy string.

A
grading
on a homotopy string
\sigma
is a sequence of integers
\mu=(\ldots,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{r},\ldots)
such that for each
i
,

\mu_{i+1}=\begin{cases}\mu_{i}-1&\textrm{if $\sigma_{i+1}$ is a direct homotopy string;}\\
\mu_{i}+1&\textrm{if $\sigma_{i+1}$ is an inverse homotopy string.}\end{cases}

The pair
(\sigma,\mu)
is called a
graded homotopy string
.

We similarly define a
grading
on a homotopy band, and a
graded homotopy band
.

A graded homotopy string
(\sigma,\mu)
gives rises to an indecomposable object
P^{\bullet}_{(\sigma,\mu)}
, and a homotopy band
(\sigma,\mu)
gives rise to a family of indecomposable objects
P^{\bullet}_{(\sigma,\mu,\lambda,m)}
in
\mathcal{K}^{-,b}(proj\text{-}A)
, with
\lambda\in k^{*}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We briefly recall the construction in the following.

Let
(\sigma,\mu)
be a graded homotopy string on
(Q,I)
with
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{n}
. Denote by
P_{v_{i}}
the projective object associated to the vertex
v_{i}:=s(\sigma_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n
and
v_{n+1}:=t(\sigma_{n})
. Then
\sigma_{i}
induces a canonical non-zero map from
P_{v_{i+1}}
to
P_{v_{i}}
(resp. from
P_{v_{i}}
to
P_{v_{i+1}}
), which is still denoted by
\sigma_{i}
(resp. by
\sigma_{i}^{-1}
), if
\sigma_{i}
is a direct string (resp. inverse string).
We put
P_{v_{i}}
at
\mu_{i}
-th position of
P^{\bullet}_{(\sigma,\mu)}
and put
P_{v_{i+1}}
at
\mu_{i+1}
-th position of
P^{\bullet}_{(\sigma,\mu)}
, and connect them by the map
\sigma_{i}
(resp.
\sigma_{i}^{-1}
). In this way, we obtain a complex
P^{\bullet}_{(\sigma,\mu)}
by connecting all the projectives that
\sigma
goes through.
We call
P^{\bullet}_{(\sigma,\mu)}
the
homotopy string object
arising from
(\sigma,\mu)
.

Let
(\sigma,\mu)
be a graded homotopy band on
(Q,I)
, where
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}
. Then similarly,
\sigma_{i}
induces a canonical non-zero map between
P_{v_{i}}
and
P_{v_{i+1}}
.
For each
1\leq i\leq n-1
, we put
P^{m}_{v_{i}}
at
\mu_{i}
-th position of
P^{\bullet}_{(\sigma,\mu,\lambda,m)}
and put
P^{m}_{v_{i+1}}
at
\mu_{i+1}
-th position of
P^{\bullet}_{(\sigma,\mu,\lambda,m)}
, and connect them by the map
\sigma_{i}I_{m}
or
\sigma_{i}^{-1}I_{m}
, depending on
\sigma_{i}
is direct or inverse, where
I_{m}
is the identity matrix of rank
m
. At last, we connect
P_{v_{1}}
and
P_{v_{n}}
by
\sigma_{n}B(\lambda)_{m}
or
\sigma^{-1}_{n}B(\lambda)_{m}
, depending on
\sigma_{n}
is direct or inverse. In this way, we obtain a complex
P^{\bullet}_{(\sigma,\mu,\lambda,m)}
by connecting all the direct sums of projectives that
\sigma
goes through.
We call
P^{\bullet}_{(\sigma,\mu,\lambda,m)}
a
homotopy band object
arising from
(\sigma,\mu)
.

Note that each object in
\mathcal{K}^{-,b}(proj\text{-}A)
is either a homotopy string object or a homotopy band object. Furthermore, up to inversion, the homotopy strings form an indexing set of homotopy string objects, and up to inversion and rotation, the homotopy bands together with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda\in k^{*}
form an indexing set of homotopy band objects.

1.3.
Marked surfaces and surface dissections

We recall some concepts about marked surfaces, and the construction of gentle algebras from dissections of the surfaces, for which there are many references such as
[
HKK17
,
PPP19
,
LP20
,
BC21
,
PPP21
]
, in this paper we closely follow
[
OPS18
]
and
[
APS23
]
.

Definition 1.2
.

A
marked surface
is a pair
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M})
, where

(1)

\mathcal{S}
is an oriented surface with non-empty boundary with
connected components
\partial\mathcal{S}=\sqcup_{i=1}^{b}\partial_{i}\mathcal{S}
;

(2)

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{\circ}\cup\mathcal{M}_{\color[rgb]{0.9805,0,0}{\bullet}}\cup\mathcal{P}_{\circ}
is a finite set of
marked points
on
\mathcal{S}
. The elements of
\mathcal{M}_{\circ}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\color[rgb]{0.9805,0,0}{\bullet}}
are on the boundary of
\mathcal{S}
, which will be respectively represented by symbols
\circ
and
\color[rgb]{0.9805,0,0}{\bullet}
. Each connected component
\partial_{i}\mathcal{S}
is required to contain at least one marked point of each colour, where the points
\circ
and
\color[rgb]{0.9805,0,0}{\bullet}
are alternating on
\partial_{i}\mathcal{S}
. The elements in
\mathcal{P}_{\circ}
are in the interior of
\mathcal{S}
. We refer to these points as
punctures
, and we will also represent them by the symbol
\circ
.

Note that in
[
APS23
]
, the punctures in the surface is represented by
\color[rgb]{0.9805,0,0}{\bullet}
-point, while here we use
\circ
-point, since such choose is more convenient for our purpose, that is, we will focus on the module category of a gentle algebra, rather than its derived category.

On the surface, all curves are considered up to homotopy with respect to the boundary components and the punctures, and all intersections of curves are required to be transversal.
We fix the clockwise orientation as the orientation of the surface, that is, when we following the boundary, the interior of the surface is on the right.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M})
be a marked surface.

(1)

An
arc
is a non-contractible curve, with endpoints in
\mathcal{M}_{\circ}\cup\mathcal{P}_{\circ}\cup\mathcal{M}_{\color[rgb]{0.9805,0,0}{\bullet}}
.

(2)

A
loop
is an arc whose endpoints coincide.

(3)

An
\circ
-arc
is a non-contractible curve, with endpoints in
\mathcal{M}_{\circ}\cup\mathcal{P}_{\circ}
.

(4)

An
\color[rgb]{0.9805,0,0}{\bullet}
-arc
is a non-contractible curve, with endpoints in
\mathcal{M}_{\color[rgb]{0.9805,0,0}{\bullet}}
.

(5)

A
simple arc
is an arc without interior self-intersections.

(6)

An
exceptional arc
is a simple arc without boundary self-intersections, that is, has two distinct endpoints.

(7)

A
closed curve
is a non-contractible curve in the interior of
\mathcal{S}
whose endpoints coincide. We always assume a closed curve to be
primitive
, that is, it is not a non-trivial power of a closed curve in the fundamental group of
\mathcal{S}
.

In order for some definitions and notations to be well-defined in the case of a loop, we will treat the unique endpoint of a loop as two distinct endpoints.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M})
be a marked surface.

(1)

A collection of simple arcs with endpoints in
\mathcal{M}_{\circ}
is called a
projective dissection
, if the arcs have no interior intersections and they cut the surface into polygons each of which contains exactly one marked point from
\mathcal{M}_{\color[rgb]{0.9805,0,0}{\bullet}}\cup\mathcal{P}_{\circ}
.

(2)

A collection of simple arcs with endpoints in
\mathcal{M}_{\color[rgb]{0.9805,0,0}{\bullet}}\cup\mathcal{P}_{\circ}
is called a
projective coordinate
, if the arcs have no interior intersections and they cut the surface into polygons each of which contains exactly one marked point from
\mathcal{M}_{\circ}
.

A projective dissection will be denoted by
\Delta_{p}
. Then it is not hard to see that (cf.
[
OPS18
]
for example) for each
\Delta_{p}
, there is a unique (up to homotopy) projective coordinate on
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M})
, which we will denote by
\Delta^{*}_{p}
, such that each arc
\ell^{*}
in
\Delta^{*}_{p}
intersects exactly one arc
\ell
of
\Delta_{p}
. We call
\Delta^{*}_{p}
the
dual
(
projective coordinate
) of
\Delta_{p}
, and call
\Delta_{p}
the
dual
(
projective dissection
) of
\Delta^{*}_{p}
.

We mention that a projective coordinate introduced above is called an
\color[rgb]{0.9805,0,0}{\bullet}
-admissible dissection in
[
APS23
]
, and corresponds to a lamination considered in
[
OPS18
]
. Note that in
[
OPS18
]
, empty boundary components without marked points are allowed, which are contracted as
\color[rgb]{0.9805,0,0}{\bullet}
-punctures in this paper. There is only one kind of marked points used in
[
OPS18
]
, which corresponds to boundary-
\circ
-points in this paper.
Each endpoint of an arc in a lamination locates on a segment between two adjacent marked points, or on an empty boundary component. For a lamination, by contracting the endpoints on the same segment (or empty boundary component) into a point, which corresponds to a red marked point (or a puncture) used in this paper, we get a projective coordinate defined above.

Remark 1.5
.

In Definition
2.1
, we will introduce the simple dissection and the simple coordinate. The terminologies we used here will be justified in Theorem
2.8
and Theorem
2.12
, that is, the projective coordinate and the simple coordinate will be respectively viewed as kinds of ‘coordinates’ when we study the derived category and the module category of a gentle algebra, and the arcs in the dual projective dissection and the dual simple dissection will respectively represent indecomposable projective modules and simple modules of this gentle algebra.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
q
be a common endpoint of arcs
\ell_{i}^{*},\ell_{j}^{*}
in
\Delta_{p}^{*}
, an
oriented intersection
from
\ell_{i}^{*}
to
\ell_{j}^{*}
at
q
is a clockwise angle locally from
\ell_{i}^{*}
to
\ell_{j}^{*}
based at
q
such that the angle is in the interior of the surface. We call an oriented intersection a
minimal oriented intersection
if it is not a composition of two oriented intersections of arcs from
\Delta_{p}^{*}
.

Definition 1.7
.

We define the algebra
A(\Delta_{p}^{*})
as the quotient of the path algebra
kQ(\Delta_{p}^{*})
of the quiver
Q(\Delta_{p}^{*})
by the ideal
I(\Delta_{p}^{*})
defined as follows:

(1)

The vertices of
Q(\Delta_{p}^{*})
are given by the arcs in
\Delta_{p}^{*}
.

(2)

Each minimal oriented intersection
{\bf{a}}
from
\ell^{*}_{i}
to
\ell^{*}_{j}
gives rise to an arrow from
\ell^{*}_{i}
to
\ell^{*}_{j}
, which is still denoted by
{\bf{a}}
.

(3)

The ideal
I(\Delta_{p}^{*})
is generated by paths
{\bf{a}}{\bf{b}}:\ell^{*}_{i}\rightarrow\ell^{*}_{j}\rightarrow\ell^{*}_{k}
, where the common endpoint of
\ell^{*}_{i}
and
\ell^{*}_{j}
, and the common endpoint of
\ell^{*}_{j}
and
\ell^{*}_{k}
that respectively gives rise to
{\bf{a}}
and
{\bf{b}}
coincide.

Then it is straightforward to check that
A(\Delta_{p}^{*})
is a gentle algebra. In fact it is proved in
[
BC21
,
OPS18
,
PPP19
]
that any gentle algebra arises from this way. So this establishes a bijection between the set of triples
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M},\Delta_{p}^{*})
of homeomorphism classes of marked surfaces together with projective coordinates and the set of isomorphism classes of gentle algebras
A(\Delta_{p}^{*})
.

Remark 1.8
.

We should mention that when we define the algebras from a dissection on a surface, we take the opposite orientation to that used in
[
OPS18
]
and
[
BC21
]
.

By definition,
\Delta_{p}^{*}
cuts the surface into polygons each of which has a unique
\circ
-point from
\mathcal{M}_{\circ}
. Let
\alpha
be an
\circ
-arc or a closed curve on
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M})
, and we choose a direction of
\alpha
. Denote by
\alpha\cap\Delta_{p}^{*}
the totally ordered set of intersection points of
\alpha
with arcs in
\Delta_{p}^{*}
, where the order is induced by the direction of
\alpha
.
A
grading
of
\alpha
is a function
f:\alpha\cap\Delta_{p}^{*}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z},
which is defined as follows:
If
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are in
\alpha\cap\Delta_{p}^{*}
and
p_{2}
is the direct successor of
p_{1}
, then
\alpha
enters a polygon
\mathbb{P}
formed by arcs in
\Delta_{p}^{*}
via
p_{1}
and leaves it via
p_{2}
. If the
\circ
-point in
\mathbb{P}
is to the left of
\alpha
, then
f(p_{2})=f(p_{1})-1
; otherwise,
f(p_{2})=f(p_{1})+1
. We call the pair
(\alpha,f)
a
graded curve
on
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M},\Delta_{p}^{*})
.

It is not hard to see that the definition of the grading is independent on the choice of the direction of the arc. However, if
\alpha
is a closed curve, then it may not be gradable, that is there may not exist a grading of
\alpha
since in this case
f
might not be well-defined.

1.4.
Geometric models for the derived categories of gentle algebras

In this subsection we recall the geometric model of the derived category of a gentle algebra established in
[
OPS18
]
. In the following, let
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M},\Delta_{p}^{*})
be a marked surface with a projective coordinate, and let
A(\Delta_{p}^{*})
be the associated gentle algebra.

We first recall how to construct a graded homotopy string/band
(\sigma,\mu)
of
A(\Delta_{p}^{*})
from a graded
\circ
-arc/closed curve
(\alpha,f)
on
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M},\Delta_{p}^{*})
.

Let
\alpha
be an
\circ
-arc or a closed curve on a the surface. After choosing a direction of
\alpha
, denote by
\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{1},\cdots,\mathbb{P}_{n+1}
the ordered polygons of
\Delta^{*}_{p}
that successively intersect with
\alpha
, whenever
\alpha
is a closed curve the index is considered modulo
n+1
, and in particular
\mathbb{P}_{0}=\mathbb{P}_{n+1}
. Denote by
\ell^{*}_{0},\ell^{*}_{1},\cdots,\ell^{*}_{n}
the ordered arcs in
\Delta^{*}_{p}
that successively intersect with
\alpha
such that
\ell^{*}_{i}
belongs to
\mathbb{P}_{i}
and
\mathbb{P}_{i+1}
for each
0\leq i\leq n
.

(1) If
\alpha
is an
\circ
-arc with both endpoints in
\mathcal{M}_{\circ}
.
Then for each
1\leq i\leq n
, there is a canonical non-zero path
\sigma_{i}
in
A(\Delta^{*}_{p})
from
\ell^{*}_{i-1}
to
\ell^{*}_{i}
(from
\ell^{*}_{i}
to
\ell^{*}_{i-1}
resp.), which is the composition of arrows arising from the minimal oriented intersections in
\mathbb{P}_{i}
, if the (unique)
\circ
-point in
\mathbb{P}_{i}
is to the left (right resp.) of
\alpha
. We denote by
\overrightarrow{\sigma}_{i}=\sigma_{i}
(
=\sigma^{-1}_{i}
resp.) if
\sigma_{i}
is a path from
\ell^{*}_{i-1}
to
\ell^{*}_{i}
(from
\ell^{*}_{i}
to
\ell^{*}_{i-1}
resp.).
Let
\sigma=\overrightarrow{\sigma}_{1}\overrightarrow{\sigma}_{2}\cdots\overrightarrow{\sigma}_{n}
, which is a finite homotopy string. We define the grading
\mu
on
\sigma
by setting
\mu_{i}=f(\ell^{*}_{i})
. Then
(\sigma,\mu)
is a finite homotopy string of
A(\Delta^{*}_{p})
.

(2) If
\alpha
is a closed curve, then the construction of
(\sigma,\mu)
is similar, noticing that since
\alpha
is gradable, the numbers of direct and inverse strings are equal, thus
\sigma
is a homotopy band, which is gradable.

(3) If
\alpha
is an
\circ
-arc which has at least one endpoint
q
from
\mathcal{P}_{\circ}
. Then we firstly construct an infinite arc
\widetilde{\alpha}
by redrawing
\alpha
locally in a way such that it wraps around
q
infinitely many times in the clockwise direction. If both endpoints of
\alpha
are punctures, then we redraw
\alpha
locally around both endpoints. Furthermore, we expand
f
in a unique way to a grading
\widetilde{f}
on
\widetilde{\alpha}
such that the values of
f
and
\widetilde{f}
on the common intersections of
\alpha
and
\widetilde{\alpha}
with
\Delta^{*}_{p}
are the same. We associated the new graded arc
(\widetilde{\alpha},\widetilde{f})
with a graded homotopy string
(\sigma,\mu)
in a similar way that we do as above for those
\circ
-arcs with both endpoints in
\mathcal{M}_{\circ}
. Then
(\sigma,\mu)
is an infinite homotopy string of
A(\Delta^{*}_{p})
.

The above constructions give a bijection between the set of graded
\circ
-arcs/closed curves
(\alpha,f)
on
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M},\Delta_{p}^{*})
and the set of graded homotopy string/band
(\sigma,\mu)
of
A(\Delta_{p}^{*})
.
We will denote by
P^{\bullet}_{(\alpha,f)}
/
P^{\bullet}_{(\alpha,f,\lambda,m)}
the homotopy string/band object associated to
(\alpha,f)
, that is, the object arising from
(\sigma,\mu)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, for a graded curve
(\alpha,f)
, we define the
shift

f[n]
by setting
f[n](q)=f(q)-n
for any intersection
q\in\alpha\cap\Delta_{p}^{*}
. Then the shift of the grading of the graded curves corresponds to the shift of the associated objects, that is,
P^{\bullet}_{(\alpha,f[n])}=P^{\bullet}_{(\alpha,f)}[n]
and
P^{\bullet}_{(\alpha,f[n],\lambda,m)}=P^{\bullet}_{(\alpha,f,\lambda,m)}[n]
.

Furthermore, based on the results in
[
ALP16
]

that the morphisms in

\beta
are the same closed curve, and
P^{\bullet}_{(\alpha,f)}
and
P^{\bullet}_{(\beta,g)}
have the same parameters, then
P^{\bullet}_{(\alpha,f)}\cong P^{\bullet}_{(\beta,g)}[f-g]
, and the above morphisms together with the identity map and a canonical map
\xi
that appears in an Auslander-Reiten triangle

\tau P^{\bullet}_{(\alpha,f)}\longrightarrow E\longrightarrow P^{\bullet}_{(\alpha,f)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\xi}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tau P^{\bullet}_{(\alpha,f)}[1]=P^{\bullet}_{(\beta,g)}[f-g+1],

form a basis of the
\mathrm{Hom}
-space, where
f-g\in\mathbb{Z}
is defined as the difference
f(p)-g(p)
for any intersection
p
of
\alpha=\beta
with an arc in
\Delta^{*}_{p}
.

1.5.
t-structures, hearts and simple-minded collections

For a finite dimensional algebra
A
, it is well-known that the bounded derived category
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A)
of finite generated right
A
-module is triangle equivalent to the homotopy category
\mathcal{K}^{-,b}(proj\text{-}A)
. In the following, we will not distinguish these two categories. It is also well-known that the category
\operatorname{{\rm mod-A}}
of finite generated right
A
-module can be embedded in
\mathcal{K}^{-,b}(proj\text{-}A)
as a full subcategory which maps a module
M
to its projective resolution
P^{\bullet}_{M}
. Under this embedding, the extension spaces of modules coincide with the spaces of homomorphisms of the associated projective complexes, that is, we have a canonical isomorphism
\mathrm{Ext}^{\omega}(M,N)\cong\mathrm{Hom}(P^{\bullet}_{M},P^{\bullet}_{N}[\omega])
for any modules
M
and
N
in
\operatorname{{\rm mod-A}}
, and any non-negative integer
\omega
.

In fact,
\operatorname{{\rm mod-A}}
is a typical example of a more general concept so called the heart of
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A)
, that is, the heart of the standard t-structure
(\mathcal{D}^{\leq 0},\mathcal{D}^{\geq 0})
, where
\mathcal{D}^{\leq 0}
consists of complexes with
vanishing cohomologies in positive degrees, and
\mathcal{D}^{\geq 0}
consists of complexes with vanishing cohomologies in negative degrees.

In general, a
t
-structure
on a triangulated category
\mathcal{C}
with suspension functor
[1]
(
[
BBD82
]
) is a pair
(\mathcal{C}^{\leq 0},\mathcal{C}^{\geq 0})
of full subcategories of
\mathcal{C}
such that

(1)

\mathcal{C}^{\leq 0}[1]\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\leq 0}
and

\mathcal{C}^{\geq 0}[-1]\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\geq 0}
;

(2)

\mathrm{Hom}(M,N[-1])=0
for
M\in\mathcal{C}^{\leq 0}

and
N\in\mathcal{C}^{\geq 0}
;

(3)

for each
M\in\mathcal{C}
there is a triangle
M^{\prime}\rightarrow M\rightarrow M^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow M^{\prime}[1]
in
\mathcal{C}
with
M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\leq 0}
and
M^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\geq 0}[-1]
.

We call
\mathcal{C}^{\leq 0}~\cap~\mathcal{C}^{\geq 0}
the
heart
of the t-structure, which is
always abelian.
The
t
-structure
(\mathcal{C}^{\leq 0},\mathcal{C}^{\geq 0})
is said to be

bounded
if

\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{C}^{\leq 0}[n]=\mathcal{C}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{C}^{\geq 0}[n].

A t-structure is called
algebraic
if it is bounded and its heart is
algebraic
, that is, the heart has finitely many isomorphism classes of simple objects and each object of the heart is both artinian and noetherian. The algebraic t-structures are closely related to collections of certain special objects in the triangulated category: the simple-minded collections.

A collection
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
of objects in a triangulated category
\mathcal{C}
is said to be

simple-minded
if the following conditions hold for

1\leq i,j\leq n:

(1)

\mathrm{Hom}(S_{i},S_{j}[m])=0,~~\forall~m<0
,

(2)

\operatorname{End}\nolimits(S_{i})
is a division algebra and
\mathrm{Hom}(S_{i},S_{j})
vanishes for
i\neq j
,

(3)

S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
generate

\mathcal{C}
(i.e.
\mathcal{C}=\mathsf{thick}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})
).

Note that for any algebra
A
, the collection of simple A-modules consist a simple-minded collection of
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A)
, and the set of simple-minded collections bijectively correspond to the set of algebraic t-structures, and thus correspond to the set of algebraic hearts, see
[
KY14
]
. The following proposition shows how to construct an algebraic heart from a simple-minded collection.

Proposition 1.11
.

[
KY14
, Proposition 5.4, Theorem 6.1]

Let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
be a simple-minded collection of

\mathcal{D}^{b}(A)
. Let
\mathcal{D}^{\leq 0}
(respectively,
\mathcal{D}^{\geq 0}
) be the extension closure of
\{S_{i}[m]\mid i=1,\ldots,n,m\geq 0\}
(respectively,
\{S_{i}[m]\mid i=1,\ldots,n,m\leq 0\}
)
in
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A)
.
Then the pair
(\mathcal{D}^{\leq 0},\mathcal{D}^{\geq 0})
is a bounded
t
-structure on
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A)
.

Conversely, any algebraic heart in
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A)
arises from a simple-minded collection in the above way.

Remark 2.9
.

The surface model of the module category of a gentle algebra established in this paper can be viewed as a small modification of the surface model given in
[
BC21
]
.
The difference is that in
[
BC21
]
unmarked boundary components except those contained in monogons or digons are removed, while in this paper, they are retained and contracted to punctures. Besides that, additional marked
\circ

[
BC21
]
, up to the modification we made above.
It is these changes which mean that one indecomposable module corresponds to an equivalence class of curves in
[
BC21
, Theorem 2]
while in above theorem it corresponds to a unique curve.

Remark 2.31
.

To sum up, let
\alpha
and
\beta
be two zigzag curves which intersect at
q
. If

is a boundary intersection, then there is only one morphism between

and

arising from

, where for gentle algebras arising from triangulations of surfaces, an explicit basis of higher extension spaces between indecomposable modules is given.

3.
A geometric model for the algebraic hearts of a gentle algebra

In this section, by using the geometric model for the module category of a gentle algebra established in above section, we will give a geometric model for any algebraic heart in the derived category of a gentle algebra, where such a heart is always equivalent to the module category of a gentle algebra.

In the rest of the paper, let
A
be a gentle algebra arising from a marked surface
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M},\Delta^{*}_{A})
, where
\Delta^{*}_{A}
is a projective coordinate. Here we leave the notations
\Delta^{*}_{p}
,
\Delta_{s}^{*}
,
\Delta_{p}
and
\Delta_{s}
to represent arbitrary (rather than the initial) coordinates and dissections respectively on
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M})

We introduce a notation which will be used in the following. Let

be an
\circ
-arc and let

be an endpoint of

the intersection in
\alpha\cap\Delta_{A}^{*}
which is nearest to

, where

is the set of intersections between
\alpha
and the arcs in
\Delta^{*}_{A}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A

of

is a pair
(\Delta_{s},f)
, where
\Delta_{s}
is a simple dissection of

and

is a set of gradings of arcs from
\Delta_{s}
such that

whenever

\alpha
and

such that

Then we have the following geometric description of simple-minded collections in

, which can be proved similar to the proof of
[

, Theorem 3.2]
and its converse
[

, Proposition 3.7]
, where a geometric characterization for silting objects is given, noticing that there is a kind of duality between simple-minded collections in

and silting objects in

For convenience of the reader, we provide a sketch of the proof after the theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(\Delta_{s},f)
be a simple-minded dissection of
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M},\Delta^{*}_{A})
.
Then

P^{\bullet}_{(\Delta_{s},f)}:=\{P^{\bullet}_{(\alpha,f_{\alpha})},(\alpha,f_{\alpha})\in(\Delta_{s},f)\}

is a simple-minded collection in
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A)
.

Conversely, any simple-minded collection in
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A)
arises from this way. That is, there is a bijection from the set of homotopy classes of simple-minded dissections on

\mathcal{D}^{b}(A)

Sketch of the proof:
.

Assume we have a simple-minded dissection
(\Delta_{s},f)
, then any morphism arises from an oriented intersection from an arc
\alpha
to an arc
\beta

q

f_{\alpha}(s^{\alpha}_{q})<f_{\beta}(s^{\beta}_{q}),
the degree of this morphism is
f_{\beta}(s^{\beta}_{q})-f_{\alpha}(s^{\alpha}_{q})

1.5
.
To show the condition (3) holds, that is,

can be generated by objects

can be generated by these objects. Since smoothing of arcs corresponds to taking the cones of morphisms between the associated objects (up to a shift) in
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A)

[

, Theorem 4.1]

\gamma
can be obtained by iteratively smoothing of arcs in

.
To see this, note that the arcs in
\Delta_{s}
cut the surface into polygons. The arc

\alpha

Conversely, if we have a simple-minded collection

-arc
