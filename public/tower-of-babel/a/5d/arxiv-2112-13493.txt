Structure of octonionic Hilbert spaces with applications in the Parseval equality and Cayley-Dickson algebras Thanks: This work was supported by the NNSF of China (11771412).
Qinghai Huo, Guangbin Ren

Abstract
. Contrary to the simple structure of the tensor product of the quaternionic Hilbert space, the octonionic situation becomes more involved. It turns out that an octonionic Hilbert space can be decomposed as an orthogonal direct sum of two subspaces, each of them isomorphic to a tensor product of an irreducible octonionic Hilbert space with a real Hilbert space. As an application, we find that for a given orthogonal basis the octonionic Parseval equality holds if and only if the basis is weak associative. Fortunately, there always exists a weak associative orthogonal basis in an octonionic Hilbert space. This completely removes the obstacles caused by the failure of the octonionic Parseval equality. As another application, we provide a new approach to study the Cayley-Dickson algebras, which turn out to be specific examples of octonionic Hilbert spaces. An explicit weak associative orthonormal basis is constructed in each Cayley-Dickson algebra.

1.
Introduction

The theory of octonionic Hilbert spaces is initiated by Goldstine and Horwitz
[
5
]
in 1964. It has many developments in the spectral theory
[
15
]
, operator theory
[
14
]
, and mathematical physics
[
1
,
8
,
7
,
17
]
.

However, for the normed division algebras, many results are restricted to the quaternionic case and remain open in the octonionic case
[
21
]
.

A quaternionic Hilbert space is of a simple structure:

\bullet
A Hilbert quaternionic bimodule is the tensor product of the quaternion algebra
\mathbb{H}
with a real Hilbert space
[
16
]
.

\bullet

A Hilbert left quaternionic module is also a tensor product up to an isomorphism. This is because the compatible right multiplication always exits which depends on a choice of a Hilbert basis as shown in
[
4
, Section 3.1]
.

A natural question for the octonionic case is whether a Hilbert left octonionic module is the tensor product of the octonionic algebra
\mathbb{O}
with some real Hilbert space.
If the answer was affirmative, then it would extremely simplify the theory of Hilbert left octonionic modules. We refer to

[
14
]
for such a development of the theory of octonionic Hilbert spaces under the assumption of such a tensor product decomposition.

In this article, we give a negative answer to the question above. It turns out that a Hilbert left octonionic module can be decomposed into an orthogonal sum of two subspaces, each with a tensor product decomposition.

To state our results in detail, we first recall the definition of Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-modules due to Goldstine and Horwitz
[
5
]
.

Definition 1.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

A Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module
H
is a left
\mathbb{O}
-module with an
\mathbb{O}
-inner product

\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle:H\times H\rightarrow\mathbb{O}

such that
(H,\text{Re}\,\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle)
is a real Hilbert space. Here the
\mathbb{O}
-inner product satisfies the following axioms for all
u,v\in H
and
p\in\mathbb{O}
:

(a)

\left\langle u+v,w\right\rangle=\left\langle u,w\right\rangle+\left\langle v,w\right\rangle
;

(b)

\left\langle u,v\right\rangle=\overline{\left\langle v,u\right\rangle}
;

(c)

\left\langle u,u\right\rangle\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
; and
\left\langle u,u\right\rangle=0
if and only if
u=0
;

(d)

\left\langle tu,v\right\rangle=t\left\langle u,v\right\rangle
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
;

(e)

\text{Re}\,\left\langle pu,v\right\rangle=\text{Re}\,(p\left\langle u,v\right\rangle)
;

(f)

\left\langle pu,u\right\rangle=p\left\langle u,u\right\rangle
.

Recently, we find that axiom
(f)
is non-independent
[
11
]
. Since the functionals induced from the
\mathbb{O}
-inner product are not
\mathbb{O}
-linear, this motivates us to introduce the para-linearity instead
[
11
]
.
It turns out that para-linearity is the main object of octonionic functional analysis, other than
octonionic linearity.

We first consider the underline module structure
of a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module
H
. We refer to
[
20
,
22
]
for the structure of alternative modules, which take
\mathbb{O}
-modules as specific examples.

It is known that there are only two irreducible left
\mathbb{O}
-modules, i.e.,
\mathbb{O}
and
\overline{\mathbb{O}}
. Here the conjugate regular module
\overline{\mathbb{O}}
coincides with

\mathbb{O}
as a set and its
left module structure is defined by

p\hat{\cdot}x:=\overline{p}x,

for any
p\in\mathbb{O}
and
x\in\mathbb{O}
.
Moreover, any left
\mathbb{O}
-module
H
admits
a decomposition
[
10
]

(1.1)

\displaystyle H=\mathbb{O}\mathscr{A}(H)\oplus{\mathbb{O}}\mathscr{A}^{-}(H),

where

\mathscr{A}(H):=\{m\in H\mid(pq)m-p(qm)=0\text{ for all }p,q\in\mathbb{O}\}

is the set of
associative elements
, called
the
nucleus
of
M
, and

\mathscr{A}^{-}(H):=\{m\in H\mid(pq)m-q(pm)=0\text{ for all }p,q\in\mathbb{O}\}

is the set of
conjugate associative elements
.
Here, we denote

\mathbb{O}U:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}u_{i}\mid p_{i}\in\mathbb{O},\ u_{i}\in U,\ N\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

for any
U\subseteq H
.

With the
\mathbb{O}
-module structure of
\mathbb{O}
and
\overline{\mathbb{O}}
at hands, we can endow a natural
\mathbb{O}
-module structure on the set

(1.2)

\displaystyle\Big(\mathbb{O}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{A}(H)\Big)\oplus\Big(\overline{\mathbb{O}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{A}^{-}(H)\Big)

The decomposition (
1.1
) can be modified as

H\cong\Big(\mathbb{O}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{A}(H)\Big)\oplus\Big(\overline{\mathbb{O}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{A}^{-}(H)\Big),

which is a natural isomorphism of
\mathbb{O}
-modules.

Notice that this isomorphism is only at the level of left
\mathbb{O}
-modules. We need to
seek further an isomorphism at the level of Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-modules.
To this end, we will
endow the left
\mathbb{O}
-module in (
1.2
)
with a canonical
\mathbb{O}
-inner product.
It is shown that the submodules in the two summands in
(
1.2
)
are orthogonal to each other, and
the
\mathbb{O}
-inner products restricted to both
\mathscr{A}(H)
and
\mathscr{A}^{-}(H)

are all real-valued, respectively.
This leads to the tensor product decomposition of Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-modules.

Theorem 1.2

(
Tensor product decomposition
)
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module. Then there exists an
\mathbb{O}
-isomorphism of
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert spaces

(1.3)

\displaystyle H\cong\Big(\mathbb{O}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{A}(H)\Big)\oplus\Big(\overline{\mathbb{O}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{A}^{-}(H)\Big).

Theorem
1.2
can be applied to study the Parseval theorem in
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert spaces. As observed by Goldstine and Horwitz in the appendix of
[
6
]
, the Parseval equality may fail for arbitrary orthonormal basis in an
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space. We find for a given orthonormal basis, the related Parseval equality holds
if and only if the orthonormal basis is
weak associative
(see Definition
4.7
). Fortunately, a weak associative orthonormal basis always exists in an
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space. This makes the Parseval equality applicable in the octonionic setting.

Theorem
1.2
can also be applied to study the Cayley-Dickson algebra
\mathbb{A}_{n}
. We refer to
[
2
,
9
]
for the theory of Cayley-Dickson algebras. In this article, we introduce a new approach to study
the Cayley-Dickson algebra
\mathbb{A}_{n}
. That is, we regard
\mathbb{A}_{n}
as a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module when
n\geqslant 4
. It leads to an isomorphism of Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-modules

\mathbb{A}_{n}\cong\mathbb{O}^{2^{n-4}}\oplus\overline{\mathbb{O}}^{2^{n-4}}.

In particular, for the algebra of sedenions
\mathbb{S}
, we have

\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{O}\oplus\overline{\mathbb{O}}.

We can construct an explicit weak associative orthonormal basis in
\mathbb{A}_{n}
. Note that a Cayley-Dickson algebra is generally not a quaternionic vector space. The fact that

\mathbb{A}_{n}
is a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module
provides a new method to study Cayley-Dickson algebras.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we review some definitions and basic properties about the algebra
\mathbb{O}
of octonions,
\mathbb{O}
-modules and
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert spaces.

2.1.
Octonions

The algebra
\mathbb{O}
of octonions is the
8
-dimensional non-associative, non-commutative, normed division algebra over
\mathbb{R}
. For convenience, we denote
e_{0}=1
. Let
e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{7}
be a basis of
\mathbb{O}
as a real vector space subject to the multiplication rule

(2.1)

\displaystyle e_{i}e_{j}=\epsilon_{ijk}e_{k}-\delta_{ij},\quad i,j=1,\dots,7,

where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta and
\epsilon_{ijk}
is a completely skew-symmetric with value 1 precisely when

ijk=123,145,176,246,257,347,365.

An octonion can be written as

x=x_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{7}x_{i}e_{i},

where
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i=0,\dots,7
.
Its conjugate is defined by

\overline{x}:=x_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{7}x_{i}e_{i},

its norm equals
|x|=\sqrt{x\overline{x}}\in\mathbb{R}
, and its real part is
\text{Re}\,{x}=x_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(x+\overline{x})
.
We denote by
\mathbb{S}_{6}
the set of imaginary units in
\mathbb{O}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{S}_{6}:=\{J\in\mathbb{O}\mid J^{2}=-1\}.

Then there is a book structure on octonions (see
[
13
]
):

\mathbb{O}=\bigcup\mathbb{H}_{I,J},

where the sum runs over all
I,J\in\mathbb{S}_{6}
which are mutually orthogonal
and
\mathbb{H}_{I,J}
denotes the quaternionic algebra spanned by

\{1,I,J,IJ\}
.

2.2.
\mathbb{O}
-modules

An octonionic module is a specific alternative module since the octonionic algebra is an alternative algebra. The general representation theory over alternative algebras has been fully studied; see
[
18
,
12
,
20
,
19
,
10
]
. We recall some basic notations and results on
\mathbb{O}
-modules in this subsection.

Definition 2.1
.

An
\mathbb{R}
-vector space
M
is called a
left
\mathbb{O}
-module
, if there is an
\mathbb{R}
-linear map

\displaystyle L:\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\text{End}_{\mathbb{R}}M

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle L_{p}

satisfying
L_{1}=id_{M}

and

(2.2)

\displaystyle[p,q,x]=-[q,p,x],

for all
p,q\in\mathbb{O}
and
x\in M
.
Here

[p,q,x]:=(pq)x-p(qx)=L_{pq}(x)-L_{p}L_{q}(x)

is called the left associator of
M
.
The definition of right
\mathbb{O}
-module is similar.

It is a useful fact that
(
2.2
) is equivalent to the following statement

r(rm)=r^{2}m

for all
r\in\mathbb{O}
and all
m\in M
.

The structure of a left
\mathbb{O}
-module is characterized by its associative elements and conjugate associative elements as follows. The
associative elements
of a left
\mathbb{O}
-module
M
form a set

\mathscr{A}(M):=\{m\in M\mid[p,q,m]=0\text{ for all }p,q\in\mathbb{O}\},

called the
nucleus
of
\mathbb{O}
-module
M
.
The
conjugate associative elements
of a left
\mathbb{O}
-module
M
form a set denoted by

\mathscr{A}^{-}(M):=\{m\in M\mid(pq)m=q(pm)\text{ for all }p,q\in\mathbb{O}\}.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
M
be a left
\mathbb{O}
-module. Then

M=\mathbb{O}\mathscr{A}(M)\oplus{\mathbb{O}}\mathscr{A}^{-}(M).

The category of left
\mathbb{O}
-modules is shown to be isomorphic to the category of left
C\ell_{7}
-modules
[
10
]
. For a left
\mathbb{O}
-module
M
, we can endow it with a natural
C\ell_{7}
-module structure, denoted by
M_{C\ell_{7}}
. Let
M
,
M^{\prime}
be two left
\mathbb{O}
-module. We denote

\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{O}}(M,M^{\prime}):=\{f\in\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{R}}(M,M^{\prime})\mid f(px)=pf(x)\text{ for all }p\in\mathbb{O}\}.

Then it follows from Schur’s lemma that

(2.3)

\displaystyle\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{O}}(\mathbb{O},\overline{\mathbb{O}})\cong\text{Hom}_{C\ell_{7}}(\mathbb{O}_{C\ell_{7}},\overline{\mathbb{O}}_{C\ell_{7}})=\{0\}.

We restate this fact in a more precise way as follows.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
f\in End_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{O})
satisfies
f(px)=\overline{p}f(x)
for all
p,x\in\mathbb{O}
, then
f=0
.

A simple variant of Lemma
2.3
is as follows.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f\in\text{End}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{O})
. If it holds
f(xq)=qf(x)
for all
q,x\in\mathbb{O}
, then
f=0
.

Proof.

We define
g(x):=f(\overline{x})
. Then we obtain

g(px)=f(\overline{x}\ \overline{p})=\overline{p}f(\overline{x})=\overline{p}g({x}).

It thus follows from Lemma
2.3
that
g=0
, i.e.,
f=0
.
∎

2.3.
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert spaces

Octonionic Hilbert spaces are introduced by Goldstine and Horwitz
[
5
]
. Recently, we
[
11
]
develop this theory by using a new notion of
\mathbb{O}
-para-linearity.

Definition 2.5

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let
M
be a left
\mathbb{O}
-module.
A real linear map
f:M\xlongrightarrow{}\mathbb{O}

is called an
\mathbb{O}
-para-linear
function if it satisfies

\text{Re}\,[p,x,f]=0

for any
p\in\mathbb{O}
and
x\in M
.
Here

[p,x,f]:=f(px)-pf(x),

which is called the
second associator
.

The definition of
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert spaces can be restated in terms of para-linearity.

Definition 2.6

(
[
11
]
)
.

A left
\mathbb{O}
-module
H
is called a
pre-Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-module
if there exists an
\mathbb{R}
-bilinear map
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle:H\times H\rightarrow\mathbb{O}
, which is referred to as an
\mathbb{O}
-inner product
, satisfying:

(
{\mathrm{i}}
)

(
\mathbb{O}
-para-linearity)

\left\langle\cdot,u\right\rangle
is (left)
\mathbb{O}
-para-linear for all
u\in H
.

(
{\mathrm{ii}}
)

(Octonion hermiticity)

\left\langle u,v\right\rangle=\overline{\left\langle v,u\right\rangle}
for all
u,v\in H
.

(
{\mathrm{iii}}
)

(Positivity)

\left\langle u,u\right\rangle\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\left\langle u,u\right\rangle=0
if and only if
u=0
.

We can define the
norm

\left|\left|\cdot\right|\right|:H\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
by setting

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left|\left|u\right|\right|=\sqrt{\left<u,u\right>}.

A pre-Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module
H
is said to be a
Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module
if it is complete with respect to its natural distance induced by the norm.

Example 2.7
.

We give two typical examples of Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-modules.

(
{\mathrm{i}}
)

\mathbb{O}
is a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module with the
\mathbb{O}
-inner product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathbb{O}}
defined by

\left<x,y\right>_{\mathbb{O}}=x\overline{y}

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{O}
.

(
{\mathrm{ii}}
)

The conjugate regular module

is a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module with the
\mathbb{O}
-inner product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\overline{\mathbb{O}}}
defined by

\left<x,y\right>_{\overline{\mathbb{O}}}=y\overline{x}

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{O}
. We remark that its left module structure is defined by

p\hat{\cdot}x:=\overline{p}x,

for any
p\in\mathbb{O}
and
x\in\overline{\mathbb{O}}
.

In an
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space

, we can regard

as an
\mathbb{O}
-para-linear map induced by the
\mathbb{O}
-inner product.
This motivates us to introduce the

of

[p,u,v]:=[p,u,\langle\cdot,v\rangle]=\left\langle pu,v\right\rangle-p\left\langle u,v\right\rangle

,

From now on, we shall denote

.
We assemble some identities concerning second associators for later use.

Lemma 2.8

(
[
11
]
)
.

For all
u,v\in H

\displaystyle\left\langle[p,q,u],v\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle{[p,v,u]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{[pq,v,u]}

\displaystyle=

(
[

)

An element

3.
Tensor product decompositions

In this section, we provide a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module with a tensor product decomposition structure.
We first define the tensor product of a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module with a real Hilbert space.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(H_{1},\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{1})
be a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module and
(H_{2},\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{2})
be a real Hilbert space. Then we endow the tensor product
H_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}H_{2}
with
a canonical left
\mathbb{O}
-module structure

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}\times\big(H_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}H_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle H_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}H_{2}

\displaystyle(p,u\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}x)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle(pu)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}x

and an
\mathbb{O}
-inner product

(3.1)

\displaystyle\left<u\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}x,v\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}y\right>:=\left<u,v\right>_{1}\left<x,y\right>_{2}

for all
u,v\in H_{1}
and all
x,y\in H_{2}
. Then one can check that the tensor product
(H_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}H_{2},\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>)
is a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
(H_{1},\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{1})
and
(H_{2},\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{2})
be two Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-modules. We endow the direct sum
H_{1}\oplus H_{2}
with the canonical left
\mathbb{O}
-module structure and the
\mathbb{O}
-inner product

\left<(x,u),(y,v)\right>:=\left<x,y\right>_{1}+\left<u,v\right>_{2}

for all

and

. One can check that the direct sum
(H_{1}\oplus H_{2},\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>)
is a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module. By definition we know that
H_{1}
is orthogonal to
H_{2}
if we regard
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
as canonical subsets of

.

Definition 3.3

Let
(H_{1},\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{1})
and
(H_{2},\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{2})
be two Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-modules. A map

is called an isomorphism of Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-modules if

(
{\mathrm{i}}
)

for all

and

(
{\mathrm{ii}}
)

for all

,

\left<f(x),f(y)\right>_{2}=\left<x,y\right>_{1}.

It is useful to know the behavior of the
\mathbb{O}
-inner product on associative and conjugate associative elements in an
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module. Then we have

(
{\mathrm{i}}
)

for all
u,v\in\mathscr{A}(H)
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\left<u,v\right>

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R};

(
{\mathrm{ii}}
)

for all
u\in\mathscr{A}(H)
and
v\in\mathscr{A}^{-}(H)
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left<u,v\right>=0.

Proof.

We first prove assertion
({\mathrm{i}})
.
Recall identity (
2.7
):

{[pq,v,u]}=\left<[p,q,v],u\right>-[p,q,\left<v,u\right>]+p[q,v,u]+[p,qv,u]

for all
p,\,q\in\mathbb{O}
and
u,v\in H
. If
u,\,v\in\mathscr{A}(H)
, then it follows from Lemma
2.9

that

[p,{q},\left<v,u\right>]=0

for all
p,\,q\in\mathbb{O}
.
This implies
\left<v,u\right>\in\mathbb{R}
as desired.

Now we prove assertion
({\mathrm{ii}})
. Let
u\in\mathscr{A}(H)
and
v\in\mathscr{A}^{-}(H)
. By definition, we have

\displaystyle[p,q,v]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(pq)v-p(qv)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(pq)v-(qp)v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[p,q]v.

According to
identity (
2.7
) and Lemma

, we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<[p,q,v],u\right>-[p,q,\left<v,u\right>]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<[p,q]v,u\right>-[p,q,\left<v,u\right>]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[p,q]\left<v,u\right>+[[p,q],v,u]-[p,q,\left<v,u\right>]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(pq-qp)\left<v,u\right>-(pq)\left<v,u\right>+p(q\left<v,u\right>)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(q\left<v,u\right>)-(qp)\left<v,u\right>.

This means the map

\displaystyle f:\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle x\left<v,u\right>

satisfies

pf(q)=f(qp)

for all
p,q\in\mathbb{O}
. By Lemma
2.4
, we obtain that

and hence
\left<v,u\right>=0
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module. If
\mathscr{A}^{-}(H)=0
, then there exists an isomorphism of Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-modules

(3.4)

\displaystyle H\cong\mathbb{O}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{A}(H).

Here
\mathbb{O}
is a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module as defined in Example
2.7
, and
\mathscr{A}(H)
is a real Hilbert space with the inner product induced from

by restriction.
More precisely, for any
p,q\in\mathbb{O}
and

(3.5)

\displaystyle\left<px,qy\right>=\left<p,q\right>_{\mathbb{O}}\left<x,y\right>.

Proof.

The
\mathbb{O}

is given as in Definition

. That is,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\Big\langle{\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}x_{i}},\ \ {\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}y_{i}}\Big\rangle_{\mathbb{O}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{A}(H)}=\sum_{i,j=0}^{7}(e_{i}\overline{e_{j}})\left<x_{i},y_{j}\right>

for any

x_{i},y_{i}\in\mathscr{A}(H)
.

Since
\mathscr{A}^{-}(H)=0
, it follows from Theorem
2.2
that

H=\mathbb{O}\mathscr{A}(H).

We consider the canonical map

\displaystyle\varphi:\quad\mathbb{O}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{A}(H)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}x_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}x_{i}.

One can easily check that this is an
\mathbb{O}
-isomorphism of left
\mathbb{O}
-modules.
It follows from identity (
2.10
) and Lemma
3.4
that

\displaystyle\Big\langle{\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}x_{i}},\ \ {\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}y_{i}}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=0}^{7}\left<e_{i}x_{i},e_{j}y_{j}\right>

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=0}^{7}\Big(e_{i}(\left<x_{i},y_{j}\right>\overline{e_{j}})+[{e_{i}e_{j}},x_{i},y_{j}]+\left<[e_{i},e_{j},y_{j}],x_{i}\right>\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=0}^{7}(e_{i}\overline{e_{j}})\left<x_{i},y_{j}\right>.

This means that

\Big\langle{\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}x_{i}},\ \ {\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}y_{i}}\Big\rangle=\Big\langle{\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}x_{i}},\ \ {\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}y_{i}}\Big\rangle_{\mathbb{O}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{A}(H)}.

Hence

is an isomorphism of
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert spaces. ∎

Definition 3.6
.

Let

.

Let

\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{-}(H^{-}),

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(H^{-})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{-}(H).

The two Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}

(3.9)

\displaystyle[p,x,y]_{H^{-}}=[p,x,y]+[\left<x,y\right>,p]

for all

and

.

Proof.

By direct calculations, we have

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\left(\left<p\hat{\cdot}x,y\right>_{H^{-}}-p\left<x,y\right>_{H^{-}}\right)

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\left(\left<y,\overline{p}x\right>-p\left<y,x\right>\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\left(\left<y,x\right>p-[\overline{p},y,x]-p\left<y,x\right>\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

We come to prove (

).
By definition,

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{-}(H^{-})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{x\mid(pq)\hat{\cdot}x=q\hat{\cdot}(p\hat{\cdot}x),\forall p,q\in\mathbb{O}\}

It is easy to see that

and

\displaystyle[p,x,y]_{H^{-}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<p\hat{\cdot}x,y\right>_{H^{-}}-p\left<x,y\right>_{H^{-}}

\displaystyle\left<y,\overline{p}x\right>-p\left<y,x\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<y,x\right>p-[p,y,x]-p\left<y,x\right>

\displaystyle[p,x,y]+[\left<x,y\right>,p].

This proves the lemma.
∎

4.
Weak associative orthonormal basis and Parseval’s theorem

As observed by Goldstine and Horwitz in the appendix of
[
6
]
, the Parseval theorem may fail for any orthonormal basis in an
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space. We introduce a new notion of weak associative orthonormal basis and show that the Parseval equality holds for an orthonormal basis if and only if it is weak associative.

Definition 4.1
.

A subset
S=\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
of an
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space is said to be an
orthonormal system
if

\left<x_{\alpha},x_{\beta}\right>=\delta_{\alpha\beta}.

An orthonormal system
S=\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
is said to be an
orthonormal basis
if there does not exist other orthonormal system
S^{\prime}
such that
S\subsetneqq S^{\prime}
.

As usual, we have the following characterizations of an orthonormal basis. The proof runs in the same manner as in the classical case and is omitted here.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
S
be an orthonormal system. Then
S
is an orthonormal basis if and only if there does not exist non-zero element which is orthogonal to every element of
S
.

In contrast to the classical cases, it is meaningless to
define the dimension of an
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space as the cardinality of an orthonormal basis as shown in the following
special case of
\mathbb{O}^{2}
.

Example 4.3
.

The
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space
\mathbb{O}^{2}
admits two sets of basis. One has cardinality
2
and the other has cardinality
4
.

(1). The
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space
\mathbb{O}^{2}
admits an orthonormal basis given by

(0,1),\quad(1,0).

(2). The
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space
\mathbb{O}^{2}
admits an alternative orthonormal basis given by

(4.1)

\displaystyle x_{1}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_{1},e_{2}),\quad x_{2}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_{4},e_{7}),\quad x_{3}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_{6},e_{5}),\quad x_{4}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,e_{3}).

To show that (
4.1
) is an orthonormal basis, we first observe that

\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{4}
is an orthonormal system of
\mathbb{O}^{2}
. If there exists an element
x=(a,b)\in\mathbb{O}^{2}

orthogonal to
x_{1},\dots,x_{4}
, then from the assumption that
\left<x,x_{4}\right>=0
we have

a=be_{3}.

Since
\left<x,x_{1}\right>=0
, we get

(4.2)

\displaystyle(be_{3})e_{1}+be_{2}=2be_{2}+[b,e_{3},e_{1}]=0.

Using identity (
2.5
) in the
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space
\mathbb{O}
, we obtain

(4.3)

\displaystyle[I,J,x]\in{\mathbb{H}_{I,J}}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}

for any mutually orthogonal pair
(I,J)
of
\mathbb{S}_{6}
and all
x\in\mathbb{O}
. Here the notation
\mathbb{H}_{I,J}
stands for the subset spanned by

\{1,I,J,IJ\}
, which was introduced in Subsection
2.1
.
Combining (
4.3
) with (
4.2
), we get

be_{2}\in{\mathbb{H}_{e_{1},e_{3}}}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}.

This yields that
b_{0}=b_{1}=b_{2}=b_{3}=0
if we write

b=b_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{7}e_{i}b_{i}.

The assumptions that
\left<x,x_{2}\right>=\left<x,x_{3}\right>=0
imply that

\displaystyle 2be_{7}+[b,e_{3},e_{4}]=0,

\displaystyle 2be_{5}+[b,e_{3},e_{6}]=0.

By similar argument, we conclude that
b_{4}=b_{5}=b_{6}=b_{7}=0
and hence
b=0
. This means that
x=0
. We thus conclude from Lemma
4.2
that
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{4}
is an orthonormal basis.

The Parseval equality may not hold for
the orthonormal basis (
4.1
).
Indeed, we take
y=(1,0)\in\mathbb{O}^{2}
. By direct calculation, we have

\left<y,x_{1}\right>=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\overline{e_{1}},\quad\left<y,x_{2}\right>=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\overline{e_{4}},\quad\left<y,x_{3}\right>=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\overline{e_{6}},\quad\left<y,x_{4}\right>=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

It follows that

y-\sum_{n=1}^{4}\left<y,x_{n}\right>x_{n}=(-1,e_{3})

and

\sum_{n=1}^{4}\left|\left|\left<y,x_{n}\right>\right|\right|^{2}\neq\left|\left|y\right|\right|^{2}.

Denote
p_{i}=\left<y,x_{i}\right>
for
i=1,\dots,4
,
Using identity (
2.5
) and the left alternativity of associators, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n=1}^{4}\left<[\left<y,x_{n}\right>,\left<y,x_{m}\right>,x_{m}],x_{n}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m<n}\left<[\left<y,x_{n}\right>,\left<y,x_{m}\right>,x_{m}],x_{n}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=2\big(\left<[p_{1},p_{2},x_{2}],x_{1}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}+\left<[p_{1},p_{3},x_{3}],x_{1}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}+\left<[p_{2},p_{3},x_{3}],x_{2}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}\big)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\text{Re}\,([{e_{1}},{e_{4}},e_{7}]\overline{e_{2}}+[{e_{1}},e_{6},{e_{5}}]\overline{e_{2}}+[{e_{4}},{e_{6}},e_{5}]\overline{e_{7}})

\displaystyle=3.

Therefore, we have

\sum_{n=1}^{4}\left|\left<y,x_{n}\right>\right|^{2}+\Big\|{y-\sum_{n=1}^{4}\left<y,x_{n}\right>x_{n}}\Big\|^{2}-\sum_{m,n=1}^{2}\left<[\left<y,x_{n}\right>,\left<y,x_{m}\right>,x_{m}],x_{n}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=\left|\left|y\right|\right|^{2}.

In fact, the above equality even holds generally.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
be an orthonormal system of an
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space
H
. Then for all
x\in H
we have

(4.4)

\displaystyle\left|\left|x\right|\right|^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\left<x,x_{n}\right>\right|^{2}+\Big\|{x-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left<x,x_{n}\right>x_{n}}\Big\|^{2}-\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\left<[\left<x,x_{n}\right>,\left<x,x_{m}\right>,x_{m}],x_{n}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}.

In particular, there holds the octonionic version of Bessel’s inequality:

(4.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\left<x,x_{n}\right>\right|^{2}-\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\left<[\left<x,x_{n}\right>,\left<x,x_{m}\right>,x_{m}],x_{n}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}\leqslant\left|\left|x\right|\right|^{2}.

Proof.

Set

p_{n}=\left<x,x_{n}\right>,\quad u=\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{n}x_{n},\quad v=x-u.

By calculation, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|x\right|\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left<u+v,u+v\right>

\displaystyle=\left|\left|u\right|\right|^{2}+\left|\left|v\right|\right|^{2}+2\text{Re}\,\left<u,x-u\right>

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\left|\left|v\right|\right|^{2}-\left|\left|u\right|\right|^{2}+2\text{Re}\,\left<u,x\right>.

Since

\displaystyle\left<u,x\right>

\displaystyle=\Big\langle{\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{n}x_{n}},\ \ {x}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{n}\left<x_{n},x\right>+[{p_{n}},x_{n},x]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\left<x,x_{n}\right>\right|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}[{p_{n}},x_{n},x],

we conclude that

\text{Re}\,\left<u,x\right>=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\left<x,x_{n}\right>\right|^{2}.

By (
2.10
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\left|u\right|\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\langle{\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{n}x_{n}},\ \ {\sum_{m=1}^{N}p_{m}x_{m}}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\big(p_{n}\left<x_{n},x_{m}\right>\big)\overline{p_{m}}+[{p_{n}p_{m}},x_{n},x_{m}]+\left<[p_{n},p_{m},x_{m}],x_{n}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\left<x,x_{n}\right>\right|^{2}+\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}[{p_{n}p_{m}},x_{n},x_{m}]+\left<[\left<x,x_{n}\right>,\left<x,x_{m}\right>,x_{m}],x_{n}\right>,

so that

\displaystyle\left|\left|u\right|\right|^{2}=\text{Re}\,\left|\left|u\right|\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\left<x,x_{n}\right>\right|^{2}+\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\left<[\left<x,x_{n}\right>,\left<x,x_{m}\right>,x_{m}],x_{n}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}.

Hence (
4
) becomes

\displaystyle\left|\left|x\right|\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\left|v\right|\right|^{2}-\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\left<x,x_{n}\right>\right|^{2}+\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\left<[\left<x,x_{n}\right>,\left<x,x_{m}\right>,x_{m}],x_{n}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}\Big)+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\left<x,x_{n}\right>\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\left<x,x_{n}\right>\right|^{2}+\Big\|{x-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left<x,x_{n}\right>x_{n}}\Big\|^{2}-\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\left<[\left<x,x_{n}\right>,\left<x,x_{m}\right>,x_{m}],x_{n}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

In Example
4.3
,
it is worth pointing out that
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{4}
is not
\mathbb{O}
-linearly independent. Indeed, if we denote

x_{5}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,-e_{3}),

then we have

x_{1}=e_{1}x_{5},\quad x_{2}=e_{4}x_{5},\quad x_{3}=e_{6}x_{5}.

Moreover, it is easy to check that
\{x_{n}\}_{n=4}^{5}
is also an orthonormal basis.
By direct calculation, for
y=(1,0)
we have

y-\sum_{i=4}^{5}\left<y,x_{i}\right>x_{i}=0

and

\left|\left|y\right|\right|^{2}=\sum_{i=4}^{5}\left|\left<y,x_{i}\right>\right|^{2}.

In fact, this is not accidental in terms of the corollary below. The point is that the orthonormal basis
\{x_{n}\}_{n=4}^{5}
satisfies

[p,x_{4},x_{5}]=0

for all
p\in\mathbb{O}
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Under the assumption of Lemma
4.4
and suppose
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
satisfies

(4.7)

\displaystyle[p,x_{n},x_{m}]=0

for all
p\in\mathbb{O}
and for each
m,n=1,\dots,N
.
Then we have

(4.8)

\displaystyle\left|\left|x\right|\right|^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\left<x,x_{n}\right>\right|^{2}+\Big\|{x-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left<x,x_{n}\right>x_{n}}\Big\|^{2}

and in this case we obtain the classical Bessel’s inequality

(4.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\left<x,x_{n}\right>\right|^{2}\leqslant\left|\left|x\right|\right|^{2}.

Proof.

If
[p,x_{n},x_{m}]=0
, then by taking the real part on both sides of (
2.7
), we have

\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\left<[\left<x,x_{n}\right>,\left<x,x_{m}\right>,x_{m}],x_{n}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=-\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\text{Re}\,\left(\left<x,x_{n}\right>[\left<x,x_{m}\right>,x_{n},x_{m}]\right)=0.

Therefore, (
4.8
) follows from (
4.4
) directly.
This completes the proof.
∎

This inspires us to introduce the following notion.

Definition 4.7
.

An orthonormal basis
S=\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
is said to be a
weak associative orthonormal basis
if
S
satisfies condition (
4.7
) in Corollary
4.6
.

Lemma 4.8
.

If
S=\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
is a weak associative orthonormal basis in a Hilbert left
\mathbb{O}
-module
H
,
then

\widetilde{S}:=\{e_{i}x_{\alpha}\mid i=0,\dots,7,\ \alpha\in\Lambda\}

is a real orthonormal basis of the real Hilbert space
(H,\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathbb{R}})
.

Proof.

According to identities (
2.10
) and (
2.7
), we have

\displaystyle\left<e_{i}x_{\alpha},e_{j}x_{\beta}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=\text{Re}\,\left(e_{i}\left<x_{\alpha},x_{\beta}\right>\right)\overline{e_{j}}+[e_{i}{e_{j}},x_{\alpha},x_{\beta}]+\left<[e_{i},e_{j},x_{\beta}],x_{\alpha}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=\text{Re}\,(e_{i}\overline{e_{j}}\delta_{\alpha\beta})-\text{Re}\,(e_{i}[e_{j},x_{\beta},x_{\alpha}])

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\delta_{\alpha\beta}

for each
i,j=0,\dots,7
and each
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
.
Hence
\widetilde{S}
is a real orthonormal system of
(H,\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathbb{R}})
.
We next show that
\widetilde{S}
is a real orthonormal basis.
If not, then there exists a non-zero element
x\in H
such that
x
is orthogonal to
\widetilde{S}
with respect to the real inner product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathbb{R}}
.
Thus we have

\left<x_{\alpha},x\right>=\left\langle x_{\alpha},x\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}-\sum_{i=0}^{7}\left\langle e_{i}x_{\alpha},x\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}e_{i}=0.

In view of Lemma
4.2
, this is impossible.
∎

Now we are in position to establish the Parseval theorem for weak associative orthonormal bases.

Theorem 4.9

(
Parseval theorem
)
.

Let
H
be an
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space and
S=\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
be a weak associative orthonormal basis. Then any
x\in H
can be uniquely expressed as

x=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\left<x,x_{\alpha}\right>x_{\alpha}

and there holds

\left|\left|x\right|\right|^{2}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}|\left<x,x_{\alpha}\right>|^{2}.

Proof.

In view of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get

\left|\left<x,x_{\alpha}\right>\right|\leqslant\left|\left|x\right|\right|

for each
\alpha\in\Lambda
.
Note that

\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\big[\dfrac{1}{k+1}\left|\left|x\right|\right|,\dfrac{1}{k}\left|\left|x\right|\right|\big]=(\;0,\ \left|\left|x\right|\right|\;].

Thanks to Corollary
4.6
, there are only countable
\alpha
, say
(\alpha_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
, such that
\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>\neq 0
. Then by Bessel’s inequality (
4.9
) in Corollary
4.6
, we have

(4.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>\right|^{2}<+\infty.

Set

y_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>x_{\alpha_{j}}.

For any
m<n
, we have

y_{n}-y_{m}=\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>x_{\alpha_{j}}.

Since
S
is a weak associative orthonormal basis, we get

\displaystyle\left<y_{n}-y_{m},x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}\left<\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>x_{\alpha_{j}},x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>\left<x_{\alpha_{j}},x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>+[{\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>},x_{\alpha_{j}},x_{\alpha_{k}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<x,x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>.

It then follows from Corollary
4.6
again that

\displaystyle\left|\left|y_{n}-y_{m}\right|\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\left|\left<y_{n}-y_{m},x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>\right|^{2}+\Big\|{(y_{n}-y_{m})-\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\left<y_{n}-y_{m},x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>x_{\alpha_{k}}}\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\left|\left<x,x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>\right|^{2}+\Big\|{\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>x_{\alpha_{j}}-\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\left<x,x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>x_{\alpha_{k}}}\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\left|\left<x,x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>\right|^{2}.

Thus we conclude from (
4.10
) that
\{y_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a Cauchy sequence. From the completeness of
H
, there exists an element
x^{\prime}\in H
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}y_{n}=x^{\prime}.

We next prove that
x^{\prime}=x
.
In view of Lemma
4.8
, it suffices to prove

(4.11)

\displaystyle\left<x-x^{\prime},e_{i}x_{\alpha}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=0

for each
\alpha\in\Lambda
and
i=1,\dots,7
.
For any
x_{\alpha_{k}}
,
we get

\displaystyle\left<x-x^{\prime},e_{i}x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\big(\left<x-x^{\prime},x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>\overline{e_{i}}+[{e_{i}},x-x^{\prime},x_{\alpha_{k}}]\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\Big(\left<x,x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>\overline{e_{i}}-\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left<\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>x_{\alpha_{j}},{x_{\alpha_{k}}}\right>\overline{e_{i}}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\Big(\left<x,x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>\overline{e_{i}}-\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>\left<x_{\alpha_{j}},x_{\alpha_{k}}\right>\big)\overline{e_{i}}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

For any
x_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\neq\alpha_{j},\ j=1,2,\dots,
we also have

\displaystyle\left<x-x^{\prime},e_{i}x_{\alpha}\right>_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\big(\left<x-x^{\prime},x_{\alpha}\right>\overline{e_{i}}+[{e_{i}},x-x^{\prime},x_{\alpha}]\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\Big(\left<x,x_{\alpha}\right>\overline{e_{i}}-\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left<\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>x_{\alpha_{j}},{x_{\alpha}}\right>\overline{e_{i}}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\Big(-\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>\left<x_{\alpha_{j}},x_{\alpha}\right>\big)\overline{e_{i}}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

This proves
x=x^{\prime}
. Hence we have

x=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>x_{\alpha_{j}}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\left<x,x_{\alpha}\right>x_{\alpha}.

By Corollary
4.6
we obtain

\left|\left|x\right|\right|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big|\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>\Big|^{2}+\Big\|{x-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>x_{\alpha_{j}}}\Big\|^{2}

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Letting
n\rightarrow\infty
, we get

\left|\left|x\right|\right|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\left<x,x_{\alpha_{j}}\right>\right|^{2}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}|\left<x,x_{\alpha}\right>|^{2}.

The uniqueness of the decomposition follows from the condition (
4.7
). This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

For any
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space, there always exists a weak associative orthonormal basis.
This is because there always exists an orthonormal basis in a real Hilbert space.
Note that by Theorem
3.10
, an
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert space
H
can be decomposed into an orthogonal sum. Let
\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
be an orthonormal basis of the real Hilbert space
(\mathscr{A}(H),\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathbb{R}})
and
\{x_{\beta}\}_{\beta\in\Lambda^{\prime}}
be an orthonormal basis of the real Hilbert space
(\mathscr{A}^{-}(H),\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathbb{R}})
. Then

S:=\{x_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\Lambda\cup\Lambda^{\prime}}

is a weak associative orthonormal basis.

At last, we show that every orthonormal basis for which the Parseval theorem holds is also weak associative.

Definition 4.11
.

An orthonormal basis
S=\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
is said to be a Hilbert basis, if for
any
x\in H
it holds

\left|\left|x\right|\right|^{2}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}|\left<x,x_{\alpha}\right>|^{2}.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
S
be an orthonormal basis. Then
S
is a Hilbert basis if and only if
S
is weak associative.

Proof.

In view of Theorem
4.9
, it suffices to show that every Hilbert basis is weak associative.
Let
S=\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
be a Hilbert basis. Then for
x=px_{\beta}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|x\right|\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\left|\left<px_{\beta},x_{\alpha}\right>\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\left|(p\delta_{\alpha\beta}+[{p},x_{\beta},x_{\alpha}])\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|p\right|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}\left|[{p},x_{\beta},x_{\alpha}]\right|^{2}.

It follows from
\left|\left|x\right|\right|=\left|p\right|
that

\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}\left|[{p},x_{\beta},x_{\alpha}]\right|^{2}=0.

This implies that

[{p},x_{\beta},x_{\alpha}]=0

for all
x_{\beta},x_{\alpha}\in S
. This proves that
S
is weak associative.
∎

5.
Cayley-Dickson algebras as
\mathbb{O}
-Hilbert spaces

Any Cayley–Dickson algebra can be endowed with a weak
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule structure and an
\mathbb{O}
-inner product.

At first, we introduce the notion of weak
\mathbb{O}
-bimodules.
Let
M
be a left
\mathbb{O}
-module. There associates an involution

C:M\to M,

called
conjugate
map.
Since
any
x\in M
can be written as

x=\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}x_{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}x^{-}_{i},

where
x_{i}\in\mathscr{A}(M)
and
x^{-}_{i}\in\mathscr{A}^{-}(M)
, we define the conjugate of
x
as

C(x)=\overline{x}:=\sum_{i=0}^{7}\overline{e_{i}}x_{i}-\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}x^{-}_{i}.

Then by definition, we have

\displaystyle\overline{x}=\begin{cases}x,\qquad&x\in\mathscr{A}(M),\\
\\
-x,\qquad&x\in\mathscr{A}^{-}(M).\end{cases}

This means

(5.1)

\displaystyle C(\mathscr{A}(M))=\mathscr{A}(M),\quad C(\mathscr{A}^{-}(M))=\mathscr{A}^{-}(M).

Definition 5.1
.

We call
M
a
weak
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule
if
M
is both a left
\mathbb{O}
-module and a right
\mathbb{O}
-module satisfying the compatibility conditions

(5.2)

\displaystyle\overline{px}=\overline{x}\ \overline{p}

for all
x\in M
and
p\in\mathbb{O}
.

Remark 5.2
.

The notion of a bimodule for a class of algebras has been introduced by Eilenberg
[
3
]
. Following
[
12
,
18
]
, we call
M

an
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule if

M
is both a left
\mathbb{O}
-module and a right
\mathbb{O}
-module satisfying the compatibility conditions

[p,q,x]=[x,p,q]=[q,x,p]

for all
p,q\in\mathbb{O}
and
x\in M
.
The regular bimodule
\mathbb{O}
is known to be the only
irreducible
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule
[
12
,
18
]
.
We remark that a weak
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule may not be an
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule in general.

Remark 5.3
.

Let
M
be a weak
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule.

(
{\mathrm{i}}
)

For all
p\in\mathbb{O}
and
x\in M
, we have

(5.3)

\displaystyle\overline{xp}=\overline{p}\ \overline{x}.

Indeed, it follows from (
5.2
) that

px=\overline{\overline{px}}=\overline{\overline{x}\ \overline{p}}

which becomes
(
5.3
) after replacing
p,x
by
\overline{p},\overline{x}
, respectively.

(
{\mathrm{ii}}
)

The right multiplication of
M
is determined by its left multiplication
due to (
5.3
).
Conversely, every left
\mathbb{O}
-module can be viewed as a weak
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule by endowed with such right multiplication. That is, a weak
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule is nothing but a left
\mathbb{O}
-module endowed with a certain right multiplication.

(
{\mathrm{iii}}
)

We denote the left nucleus by

\mathscr{A}_{L}(M):=\{x\in M\mid[p,q,x]=0,\forall p,q\in\mathbb{O}\}

and the right nucleus by

\mathscr{A}_{R}(M):=\{x\in M\mid[x,p,q]=0,\forall p,q\in\mathbb{O}\}.

Similar notations
\mathscr{A}^{-}_{L}(M),\ \mathscr{A}^{-}_{R}(M)
can be also defined.
One can check that

\mathscr{A}_{R}(M)=C(\mathscr{A}_{L}(M)),\quad\mathscr{A}^{-}_{R}(M)=C(\mathscr{A}^{-}_{L}(M)).

Combining this with (
5.1
), we have

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{R}(M)=\mathscr{A}_{L}(M),\quad\mathscr{A}^{-}_{R}(M)=\mathscr{A}^{-}_{L}(M).

Henceforth we shall denote

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(M):=\mathscr{A}_{R}(M)=\mathscr{A}_{L}(M),\quad\mathscr{A}^{-}(M):=\mathscr{A}^{-}_{R}(M)=\mathscr{A}^{-}_{L}(M).

We next consider the algebra of sedenions. The algebra of sedenions
\mathbb{S}
is constructed from the algebra of octonions through the Cayley–Dickson construction
[
2
]
.
The elements in the algebra of sedenions
\mathbb{S}
take the form

s=\sum_{i=0}^{15}x_{i}e_{i},

where
x_{i}
are reals,
e_{0}=1
and
e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{15}
are imaginary units, i.e.,

e_{i}^{2}=-1

for all
i=1,\dots,15
.
The conjugate of
s
is defined as

\overline{s}=x_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{15}x_{i}e_{i}.

We regard
\mathbb{O}
as the subalgebra of
\mathbb{S}

generated by
e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{7}
. By the Cayley-Dickson construction, any
s\in\mathbb{S}
can be expressed uniquely as

s=a+be_{8}

with
a,b\in\mathbb{O}
so that
the conjugate of
s
becomes

\overline{s}=\overline{a}-be_{8}

and
the multiplication in
\mathbb{S}
is defined as

(5.5)

\displaystyle(a+be_{8})(c+de_{8})=(ac-\overline{d}b)+(da+b\overline{c})e_{8}.

We want to show that
\mathbb{S}
is a weak
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule.
At first, we show that
\mathbb{S}
is a left
\mathbb{O}
-module with multiplication

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}\times\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle(p,x)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle px.

Indeed, it follows from (
5.5
) that

(5.6)

\displaystyle a(de_{8})=(da)e_{8}

for all
a,d\in\mathbb{O}
.
Hence for all
p\in\mathbb{O}
and
x=a+be_{8}\in\mathbb{S}
, we have

p(px)=p(pa+(bp)e_{8})=p^{2}a+(bp^{2})e_{8}=p^{2}x.

This shows that
\mathbb{S}
is a left
\mathbb{O}
-module.

One can easily check that the conjugate of
s
in sedenions coincides with the conjugate of
s
when
\mathbb{S}
is regarded as a left
\mathbb{O}
-module. Hence the right multiplication induced by
(
5.2
)
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle(x,p)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\overline{\overline{p}\ \overline{x}}=xp.

It can be seen from (
5.6
) that

e_{8}\in\mathscr{A}^{-}(\mathbb{S}).

Therefore we get

\mathbb{S}\cong\mathbb{O}\oplus\overline{\mathbb{O}}

as left
\mathbb{O}
-modules.

We now define

\left<a+be_{8},c+de_{8}\right>_{\mathbb{S}}:=a\overline{c}+\overline{d}b.

In other words, if we denote by

\pi_{\mathbb{O}}:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{O}

the projection from
\mathbb{S}
to
\mathbb{O}
, then for any
s,w\in\mathbb{S}
,

\left<s,w\right>_{\mathbb{S}}:=\pi_{\mathbb{O}}(s\overline{w}).

One can check that this is an
\mathbb{O}
-inner product.

We move on our discussion to arbitrary
Cayley–Dickson algebra
\mathbb{A}_{n}
. For
n=1,2,3
,

\mathbb{A}_{1}=\mathbb{C},\quad\mathbb{A}_{2}=\mathbb{H},\quad\mathbb{A}_{3}=\mathbb{O}.

We shall apply the induction to endow
\mathbb{A}_{n}
with a Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-module structure.

The Cayley-Dickson algebra
\mathbb{A}_{n}
is defined by induction.
An element
s
in
\mathbb{A}_{n}
can be written as

s=a+be_{2^{n-1}},

where
a,b\in\mathbb{A}_{n-1}
.
Its
conjugate is defined as

\overline{a+be_{2^{n-1}}}=\overline{a}-be_{2^{n-1}}.

the multiplication of
\mathbb{A}_{n}
is defined as

(5.7)

\displaystyle(a+be_{2^{n-1}})(c+de_{2^{n-1}})=(ac-\overline{d}b)+(da+b\overline{c})e_{2^{n-1}},

where
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{A}_{n-1}
.

As a real vector space,

\mathbb{A}_{n}
admits a canonical basis

e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{2^{n}-1},

where

(5.8)

\displaystyle e_{2^{n-1}+j}:=e_{j}e_{2^{n-1}}

for any
j=1,\dots,2^{n-1}-1
.
The octonions
\mathbb{O}
can be viewed as a subset of
\mathbb{A}_{n}
when
n\geqslant 4
generated by
e_{0},e_{1},\cdots,e_{7}
.

The left
\mathbb{O}
-module structure on
\mathbb{A}_{n}
is induced by the multiplication of
\mathbb{A}_{n}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}\times\mathbb{A}_{n}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{n}

\displaystyle(p,x)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle px.

It turns out that
\mathbb{A}_{n}
is a weak
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule for
n\geqslant 4
.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
n\geqslant 4
, then
\mathbb{A}_{n}
is a weak
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule and the two conjugates coincide when
\mathbb{A}_{n}
is regarded as either a weak
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule or a Cayley–Dickson algebra. Moreover, we have an isomorphism of left
\mathbb{O}
-modules

\mathbb{A}_{n}\cong\mathbb{O}^{2^{n-4}}\oplus\overline{\mathbb{O}}^{2^{n-4}}.

Proof.

When
n=4
, we have
\mathbb{A}_{4}=\mathbb{S}
so the result has been proved. Suppose it holds for
n
, we come to prove the case of
n+1
.

It follows from (
5.7
) that

p(qe_{2^{n}})=(qp)e_{2^{n}}

for all
p,q\in\mathbb{O}
and
n\geqslant 4
.

Now we check that the subset
\mathbb{A}_{n}e_{2n}
of
\mathbb{A}_{n+1}
is also a left
\mathbb{O}
-module as well as a right
\mathbb{O}
-module.
For any
p\in\mathbb{O}
and
x\in\mathbb{A}_{n}
,

(5.9)

\displaystyle p(p(xe_{2^{n}}))=p((xp)e_{2^{n}})=((xp)p)e_{2^{n}}.

By induction,
\mathbb{A}_{n}
is a right
\mathbb{O}
-module so that
(xp)p=xp^{2}
. Therefore (
5.9
) becomes

p(p(xe_{2^{n}}))=(xp^{2})e_{2^{n}}=p^{2}(xe_{2^{n}}).

This shows that
\mathbb{A}_{n}e_{2^{n}}
is a left
\mathbb{O}
-module. It can be proved that
\mathbb{A}_{n}e_{2^{n}}
is a right
\mathbb{O}
-module similarly.

Moreover, it is easy to check that

(5.10)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{L}{(\mathbb{A}_{n}e_{2^{n}})}=\mathscr{A}^{-}_{R}({\mathbb{A}_{n}})e_{2^{n}},\quad\mathscr{A}^{-}_{L}{(\mathbb{A}_{n}e_{2^{n}})}=\mathscr{A}_{R}{(\mathbb{A}_{n})}e_{2^{n}}.

Combining (
5.10
) and (
5.4
), we conclude that

(5.11)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}{(\mathbb{A}_{n}e_{2^{n}})}=\mathscr{A}^{-}({\mathbb{A}_{n}})e_{2^{n}},\quad\mathscr{A}^{-}{(\mathbb{A}_{n}e_{2^{n}})}=\mathscr{A}{(\mathbb{A}_{n})}e_{2^{n}}.

By induction, as left modules

\mathbb{A}_{n}\cong\mathbb{O}^{2^{n-4}}\oplus\overline{\mathbb{O}}^{2^{n-4}}.

This implies that

\mathbb{A}_{n}e_{2^{n}}\cong\mathbb{O}^{2^{n-4}}\oplus\overline{\mathbb{O}}^{2^{n-4}}.

Hence we obtain

.

(\mathbb{A}_{n},\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{n})

Proof.

\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle\left<s,w\right>_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi_{3}\pi_{4}\dots\pi_{n-1}(s\overline{w})

\displaystyle\pi_{3}\pi_{4}\dots\pi_{n-2}(a\overline{c}+\overline{d}b)

\displaystyle\left<ps,w\right>_{n}-p\left<s,w\right>_{n}
