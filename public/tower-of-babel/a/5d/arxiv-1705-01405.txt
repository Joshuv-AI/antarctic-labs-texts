Variational Principle for Velocity-Pressure Formulation of Navier-Stokes Equations
Shahrdad G. Sajjadi †

Abstract
The work described here shows that the known variational principle for the Navier-Stokes equations and the adjoint system can be modified to produce a set of Euler-Lagrange variational equations which have the same order and same solution as the Navier-Stokes equations provided the adjoint system has a unique solution, and provided in the steady state case, that the Reynolds number remains finite.

1
Introduction

Given a differential equation subject to some boundary conditions, it is an
important question to decide if a variational principle exists, especially if
this variational principle is to be used for providing an approximate solution
to the original problem. For some problems minimum and maximum principles may
hold, leading to reciprocal variational principles which provide a means for
obtaining upper and lower bounds on a variational integral. In other problems,
however, the variational principle, if at all exists, may only be a stationary
principle and no minimum or maximum can be achieved.

For nonlinear and non self-adjoint operators the construction of variational
principle is not well understood. Millikan [1] and
Finlayson [2] have shown that there is no variational principle for the
Navier-Stokes equations involving soley the velocity vector
u
and the
pressure
p
unless either
\mbox{\boldmath$u\times$}\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla\times$}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=0
or
\mbox{\boldmath$u.$}\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=0
,
when the equations effectively becomes linear.

Under such circumstances we can express the velocity vector

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}\psi+\lambda\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}\mu

\psi
,

and

\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega

and

. The scalar functions

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{D\lambda}{Dt}=\displaystyle\frac{D\mu}{Dt}=0

(1)

where

. Except for an arbitrary function of

and

for each

\lambda

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla.$}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=\Delta\psi+\lambda\Delta\mu+\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}\lambda\mbox{\boldmath$.$}\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}\mu=0&{\rm in}&\Omega\\

(if

5
An extended variational principle

It is possible to remove a number of the restrictions on the class of
admissible functions by adding a surface integral to the previous functional

J
given by (
20
). Consider the new functional

\displaystyle I=J+\int_{0}^{\tau}\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}{\mathscr{M}}_{j}(u_{i},p,w_{i},r)n_{j}\,dS\,dt

(41)

where
n_{j}
are the components of the outward normal to
\partial\Omega
and

\displaystyle{\mathscr{M}}_{j}(u_{i},p,w_{i},r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-u_{j}^{s}p+w_{j}^{s}r+\displaystyle\frac{u_{j}u_{i}^{2}+u_{i}^{2}w_{j}}{4}-\displaystyle\frac{w_{j}w_{i}^{2}+w_{i}^{2}u_{j}}{4}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\nu\left[-(u_{i}-u_{i}^{s})\displaystyle\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+(w_{i}-w_{i}^{s})\displaystyle\frac{\partial w_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\right.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\varepsilon_{ijk}(w_{k}-w_{k}^{s}-u_{k}+u_{k}^{s})\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}|\mbox{\boldmath$u$}-\mbox{\boldmath$u$}^{s}+\mbox{\boldmath$w$}-\mbox{\boldmath$w$}^{s}|\right]

where
\varepsilon_{ijk}
is the permutation tensor and the superscript
s
indicates
the surface values.

The derivation of this expression for
{\mathscr{M}}_{j}
is complicated by the
fact that when
\nu=0
the physical boundary condition on
\partial\Omega

should relate only to the normal velocity component: accordingly, normal and
tangential components require separate treatment.

We require
I
to be stationary
(\delta I=0)
subject to the conditions (i),
(ii) and (iv) where in addition
u_{i}=w_{i}
at one point on
\partial\Omega

and the variation are such that
\delta u_{i}+\delta w_{i}=0
on

\partial\Omega
. It is then readily verified that variations of
u_{i},p,w_{i},r
leads to the Navier-Stokes equations together with its adjoint
system.

However, the boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
require a little care.
Incorporating the additional integral of
{\mathscr{M}}_{j}
, the variations in

w_{j}
gives

\displaystyle n_{i}(u_{i}-u_{i}^{s})(u_{j}-u_{j}^{s})+\nu n_{i}\varepsilon_{ijk}\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}|\mbox{\boldmath$u$}-\mbox{\boldmath$u$}^{s}|=0

(42)

Taking the scalar product of (
42
) with
n_{j}
, we obtain

\displaystyle n_{i}(u_{i}-u_{i}^{s})=0\,\,\,\,\forall\,\,\,\,\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\partial\Omega

independently of whether
\nu
is zero or not. This is, of course, the
inviscid boundary conditions, which prescribes the normal velocity component
at each point on
\partial\Omega
. Accordingly, the second term in
(
42
) must itself vanish. In vector notation, this is just

\nu(\mbox{\boldmath$n\times$}\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}|\mbox{\boldmath$u$}-\mbox{\boldmath$u$}^{s}|)=0
, which if
\nu\neq 0
, asserts that

|\mbox{\boldmath$u$}-\mbox{\boldmath$u$}^{s}|
remains constant on
\partial\Omega
. If we now introduce
the further requirement that
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=\mbox{\boldmath$u$}^{s}
at a
single point
on

\partial\Omega
, we have the boundary conditions for viscous flow that

\displaystyle u_{i}=u_{i}^{s}\,\,\,\,\forall\,\,\,\,\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\partial\Omega

Variations of
p
and
u_{i}
give the corresponding boundary conditions for

w_{i}
on
\partial\Omega
. Variation of
p
leads immediately to

\displaystyle n_{i}w_{i}=0

(43)

corresponding to the inviscid boundary condition for
u_{i}
. Variation of

u_{i}
, subject to the requirement that
\delta u_{i}=0
on
\partial\Omega
,
leads to

\displaystyle\nu n_{i}w_{j}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\delta u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial\delta u_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}-\varepsilon_{ijk}\displaystyle\frac{\partial|\delta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}|}{\partial x_{k}}\right\}=0\,\,\,\,\forall\,\,\,\,\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\partial\Omega

However, since
\delta u_{i}=0
on
\partial\Omega
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial\delta u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{i}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\delta u_{i}}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial|\delta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}|}{\partial x_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{k}\displaystyle\frac{\partial|\delta\mbox{\boldmath$u$}|}{\partial\ell}

where
\ell
denotes distance along the outward normal on
\partial\Omega
.
This yields

\displaystyle\nu(n_{i}n_{j}w_{j}+w_{i})\displaystyle\frac{\partial\delta u_{i}}{\partial\ell}=0

the third term vanishing identically. Using (
43
) we have, from
variation in
u_{i}
,

\displaystyle w_{i}=0\,\,\,\,\forall\,\,\,\,\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\partial\Omega

whenever
\nu\neq 0
. There is of course a corresponding result for a steady
state problem.
