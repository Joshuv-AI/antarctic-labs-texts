Sinc-collocation methods with consistent collocation points for Fredholm integral equations of the second kind
Tomoaki Okayama

Abstract
Sinc-collocation methods are known to be efficient for Fredholm integral equations of the second kind, even if functions in the equations have endpoint singularity. However, existing methods have the disadvantage of inconsistent collocation points. This inconsistency complicates the implementation of such methods, particularly for large-scale problems. To overcome this drawback, this study proposes another Sinc-collocation methods with consistent collocation points. The results of a theoretical error analysis show that the proposed methods have the same convergence property as existing methods. Numerical experiments suggest the superiority of the proposed methods in terms of implementation and computational cost.

Introduction

This paper is concerned with
Fredholm integral equations of the second kind of the following form:

u(t)-\int_{a}^{b}k(t,s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=g(t),\quad a\leq t\leq b,

(1.1)

where
k
and
g
are given continuous functions, and
u
is the solution
to be determined.
Most numerical methods provided in the literature
do not perform well when the functions
k
and
g

have derivative singularity at the endpoints.
To overcome the difficulty,
Rashidinia and Zarebnia
[
11
]
proposed a Sinc-collocation method,
which was derived without assuming differentiability at the endpoints.
The results of numerical experiments indicated that their method
can achieve
\OO(\exp(-c\sqrt{N}))
,
where
N
denotes approximately half the number of collocation points.
However, they did not prove whether their method was guaranteed to converge.
Furthermore, their method required information on the boundary values of
the solution
u(t)
, which are not available in practice.

To address this situation,
Okayama et al.
[
7
]
reformed their method
so that it could be implemented without the information of the boundary
values of
u(t)
, and proved that
the reformed method can attain
\OO(\exp(-c\sqrt{N}))
.
The method is based on the Sinc approximation combined with
the Single-Exponential (SE) transformation:

t=\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(x)=\frac{b-a}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)+\frac{b+a}{2}.

(1.2)

They further improved the method by replacing the SE transformation
with the Double-Exponential (DE) transformation:

t=\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(x)=\frac{b-a}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\sinh x\right)+\frac{b+a}{2},

(1.3)

and proved that their improved method can attain
a higher convergence rate:
\OO(\exp(-cN/\log N))
.

The methods were derived as follows.
Based on the Sinc approximation with auxiliary functions,
set the approximate solution
u_{N}(t)
as

u_{N}(t)=c_{-N-1}\frac{b-t}{b-a}+\sum_{j=-N}^{N}c_{j}\sinc\left(\frac{\psi^{-1}(t)-jh}{h}\right)+c_{N+1}\frac{t-a}{b-a},

where
\sinc x=\sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)
,
and
\psi
is either
\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}
or
\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}
.
Substituting

into the given equation (
1.1
)
and setting
(2N+3)
collocation points,

(i=-N-1,\,\ldots,\,N+1)
, as

t_{i}=\begin{cases}a&(i=-N-1),\\
\psi(ih)&(i=-N,\,\ldots,\,N),\\
b&(i=N+1),\end{cases}

we obtain a system of linear equations with respect to
unknown coefficients
c_{j}

(j=-N-1,\,\ldots,\,N+1)
.
Those collocation points are chosen
so that

holds for
i=-N-1,\,\ldots,\,N+1
.
However, because of the inconsistency of the collocation points at

,
the methods are rather painful to implement,

As a related work, Sinc-Nyström methods have been

2
Fundamental definitions and theorems for Sinc methods

The Sinc approximation is a function approximation formula expressed as

F(x)\approx\sum_{j=-N}^{N}F(jh)S(j,h)(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(2.1)

where
S(j,h)(x)
denotes the so-called “Sinc function” defined by

S(j,h)(x)=\sinc\left(\frac{x-jh}{h}\right).

Numerical methods based on the Sinc approximation are referred to as
“Sinc methods.”
For example, a quadrature formula obtained by integrating
both sides of (
2.1
) as

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\approx\sum_{j=-N}^{N}F(jh)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S(j,h)(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=h\sum_{j=-N}^{N}F(jh)

(2.2)

is referred to as the “Sinc quadrature.”
Comprehensive summaries of existing Sinc methods
have been provided in several books and review papers
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
.
This section provides
fundamental definitions and theorems for such Sinc methods as preliminaries for the remainder of this work.

2.1
SE-Sinc approximation and SE-Sinc quadrature on the finite interval

To apply the Sinc approximation (
2.1
)
to a function
f(t)
on the finite interval
(a,b)
,
the SE transformation (
1.2
) is frequently utilized as

\displaystyle f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(x))

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh))S(j,h)(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh))S(j,h)(\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{-1}(t)),\quad t\in(a,b).

(2.3)

We refer to this approximation as the “SE-Sinc approximation.”
Similarly, to apply the Sinc quadrature (
2.2
)
to the integral on the finite interval
(a,b)
,
the SE transformation (
1.2
) is frequently utilized as

\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(x))\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\approx h\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh))\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh).

(2.4)

We refer to this approximation as the “SE-Sinc quadrature.”
The function space defined below is important in formalizing an error analysis
of these approximations.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\alpha
be a positive constant,
and let
\mathscr{D}
be a bounded and simply-connected domain
(or Riemann surface)
that satisfies
(a,\,b)\subset\mathscr{D}
.
Then,
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}(\mathscr{D})
denotes the family of all functions
f

satisfying the following conditions:
(i)
f
is analytic in
\mathscr{D}
;
(ii) there exists a constant
C
such that
for all
z
in
\mathscr{D}
,

|f(z)|\leq C|Q(z)|^{\alpha},

(2.5)

where the function
Q
is defined by
Q(z)=(z-a)(b-z)
.

When the SE transformation is utilized,
the domain
\mathscr{D}
in Definition
2.1
should be
an eye-shaped region:

\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})=\{z=\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(\zeta):\zeta\in\mathscr{D}_{d}\}

(see also an example figure
[
7
, Fig. 1]
),
where
\mathscr{D}_{d}
is a strip domain defined by

\mathscr{D}_{d}=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|<d\}

for a positive constant
d
.
Then, error analyses of the above approximations
are described as follows.

Theorem 2.1

(Stenger
[
13
, Theorem 4.2.5]
)
.

Let
f\in\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d}))
for
d
with
0<d<\pi
,
let
N
be a positive integer, and let
h
be selected by the formula

h=\sqrt{\frac{\pi d}{\alpha N}}.

(2.6)

Then, there exists a constant
C
that is independent of
N
,
such that

\max_{a\leq t\leq b}\left|f(t)-\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh))S(j,h)(\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{-1}(t))\right|\leq C\sqrt{N}\exp\left(-\sqrt{\pi d\alpha N}\right).

Corollary 2.2

(Okayama et al.
[
9
, Corollary 2.8]
)
.

Let
(fQ)\in\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d}))
for
d
with
0<d<\pi
,
let
N
be a positive integer,
and let
h
be selected by the formula (
2.6
).
Then, there exists a constant
C
that is independent of
N
,
such that

\left|\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-h\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh))\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh)\right|\leq C\exp\left(-\sqrt{\pi d\alpha N}\right).

Remark 1
.

In the case of the Sinc quadrature,
the optimal choice of
h
is not (
2.6
),
but
h=\sqrt{2\pi d/(\alpha N)}
.
However, to implement the derived method easily,
the mesh size
h
is chosen as (
2.6
)
for both the SE-Sinc approximation and quadrature.
The same applies to the DE-Sinc approximation
and quadrature (described next).

2.2
DE-Sinc approximation and DE-Sinc quadrature on the finite interval

The key role of the SE transformation (
1.2
) is
to map the whole real axis
\mathbb{R}
onto the finite interval
(a,b)
.
Because the DE transformation (
1.3
) also plays the same role,
the SE transformation may be replaced by the DE transformation
in the above-mentioned approximations.
It has recently been known that such replacement enhances the performance
of Sinc methods
[
2
,
16
]
.

For example,
by the replacement in the SE-Sinc approximation (
2.3
),
we obtain

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(jh))S(j,h)(\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\}^{-1}(t)),\quad t\in(a,b).

We refer to this approximation as the “DE-Sinc approximation.”
By the same replacement in the SE-Sinc quadrature (
2.4
),
we obtain

\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(x))\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\}^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\approx h\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(jh))\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\}^{\prime}(jh).

We refer to this approximation as the “DE-Sinc quadrature.”
In these cases,
the domain
\mathscr{D}
in Definition
2.1

should be
\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})=\{z=\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(\zeta):\zeta\in\mathscr{D}_{d}\}

(see also an example figure
[
7
, Fig. 2]
),
which is a Riemann surface infinitely rotating around the endpoints

Error analyses of the above approximations

Theorem 2.3

(Tanaka et al.
[

)
.

Let
f\in\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d}))
for

,
let
N
be a positive integer, and let

h=\frac{\log(2dN/\alpha)}{N}.

(2.7)

Then, there exists a constant

that is independent of

,
such that

\max_{a\leq t\leq b}\left|f(t)-\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(jh))S(j,h)(\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\}^{-1}(t))\right|\leq C\exp\left\{\frac{-\pi dN}{\log(2dN/\alpha)}\right\}.

Corollary 2.4

(Okayama et al.
[
9

)
.

Let
(fQ)\in\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d}))
for

let
N

\left|\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-h\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(jh))\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\}^{\prime}(jh)\right|\leq C\exp\left\{\frac{-2\pi dN}{\log(2dN/\alpha)}\right\}.

After the replacement, the convergence rate is improved to

\OO(\exp(-cN/\log N))

which is substantially higher than the previous rate of

\OO(\exp(-c\sqrt{N}))

Remark 2
.

The convergence rate
\OO(\exp(-cN/\log N))

is not always attainable under the assumption

We should note that
\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})

is more restricted than that in the case of the SE transformation.
If the assumption of Theorem

2.3
Generalized SE/DE-Sinc approximation on the finite interval

According to Theorems
2.1

and
2.3
,
the function
f
should belong to
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}(\mathscr{D})
.
This may seem impractical because

f(t)
must be zero at the endpoints,
t=a
and
t=b
, owing to
the inequality (
2.5
).
In reality, it suffices to consider the following function spaces.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathscr{D}
be a bounded and simply-connected domain
(or Riemann surface).
Then,
\mathbf{HC}(\mathscr{D})
denotes the family of all functions
that are analytic in
\mathscr{D}
and continuous on
\overline{\mathscr{D}}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\alpha
be a constant with
0<\alpha\leq 1

and let
\mathscr{D}
be a bounded and simply-connected domain
(or Riemann surface)
that satisfies
(a,\,b)\subset\mathscr{D}
.
Then, the space
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}(\mathscr{D})

consists of all functions
f

satisfying the following conditions:
(i)
f\in\mathbf{HC}(\mathscr{D})
;
(ii) there exists a constant
C

for all
z
in
\mathscr{D}
such that

\displaystyle|f(z)-f(a)|

\displaystyle\leq C|z-a|^{\alpha},

\displaystyle|f(b)-f(z)|

\displaystyle\leq C|b-z|^{\alpha}.

For functions
f\in\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}(\mathscr{D})
,
using auxiliary functions

\omega_{a}(t)=\frac{b-t}{b-a},\quad\omega_{b}(t)=\frac{t-a}{b-a},

the following approximations have been proposed:

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}[f](t)

\displaystyle=f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(-Nh))\omega_{a}(t)+f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(Nh))\omega_{b}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=-N}^{N}\left\{f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh))-f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(-Nh))\omega_{a}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh))-f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(Nh))\omega_{b}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh))\right\}S(j,h)(\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{-1}(t)),

(2.8)

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}[f](t)

\displaystyle=f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(-Nh))\omega_{a}(t)+f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(Nh))\omega_{b}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=-N}^{N}\left\{f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(jh))-f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(-Nh))\omega_{a}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(jh))-f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(Nh))\omega_{b}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(jh))\right\}S(j,h)(\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\}^{-1}(t)).

(2.9)

We refer to (
2.8
)
as the “generalized SE-Sinc approximation,”
and (
2.9
)
as the “generalized DE-Sinc approximation.”
Error analyses of these approximations
are described as follows.
Here,

(Okayama

Smoothness property of the solution

To approximate the solution
u
according to Theorem
2.5

or
2.6
,

must belong to
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d}))

or
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d}))
.
It should be noted that the parameters
\alpha
and
d
,
which indicate smoothness of the function, are
used to select the mesh size

in (
2.6
) or (
2.7
).
In practice, however, investigating

directly is not possible
because

is an

function to be determined.
To improve the situation,

]

provided a sufficient condition for

using the known functions

Suppose

,
and let us introduce the integral operator

Note that the equation (

(Okayama et al.
[

, Theorem 4.3]
)
.

and

for all

,
and let also

.
Furthermore, assume that

Then, the equation (

)

This theorem suggests that the smoothness parameters

4.1

First, suppose that

Then, set the approximate solution
u_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}

u_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(t)=u_{-N-1}\omega_{a}(t)+\sum_{j=-N}^{N}u_{j}S(j,h)(\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{-1}(t))+u_{N+1}\omega_{b}(t).

(4.1)

Let us substitute

into the equation (

),
and approximate the integral operator

\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N}[f](t)=h\sum_{j=-N}^{N}k(t,\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh))f(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh))\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh).

Finally, by setting the collocation points as

t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i}=\begin{cases}a&(i=-N-1),\\
\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(ih)&(i=-N,\,\ldots,\,N),\\
b&(i=N+1),\end{cases}

(4.3)

we obtain a
(2N+3)\times(2N+3)
system of linear equations:

\displaystyle\{\omega_{a}(t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i})-\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N}[\omega_{a}](t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i})\}u_{-N-1}+\sum_{j=-N}^{N}\left\{\delta_{ij}-hk(t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i},t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{j})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh)\right\}u_{j}+\{\omega_{b}(t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i})-\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N}[\omega_{b}](t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i})\}u_{N+1}

\displaystyle=g(t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i}),\quad i=-N-1,\,-N,\,\ldots,\,N,\,N+1.

(4.4)

Let us define
n
by
n=2N+3
,
and let
E_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}
and

be
n\times n
matrices defined by

\displaystyle E_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{\,}c|ccc|c@{\,}}1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\hline\cr\omega_{a}(t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{-N})&1&&\OO&\omega_{b}(t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{-N})\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\omega_{a}(t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N})&\OO&&1&\omega_{b}(t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N})\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle K_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{\,}l|clc|l@{\,}}\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N}[\omega_{a}](a)&\cdots&hk(a,t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{j})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh)&\cdots&\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N}[\omega_{b}](a)\\
\hline\cr\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N}[\omega_{a}](t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{-N})&\cdots&hk(t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{-N},t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{j})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh)&\cdots&\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N}[\omega_{b}](t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{-N})\\
\lx@intercol\hfil\vdots\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@intercol&&\lx@intercol\hfil\vdots\hfil\lx@intercol&&\lx@intercol\hfil\vdots\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N}[\omega_{a}](t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N})&\cdots&hk(t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N},t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{j})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh)&\cdots&\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N}[\omega_{b}](t^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N})\\

5.1
New SE-Sinc-collocation method with consistent collocation points

First, suppose that
the assumptions in Theorem
3.1

are fulfilled with
\mathscr{D}=\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})
.
Then, approximating the equation
(I-\mathcal{K})u=g

by
(I-\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N})v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}=g

(i.e., applying Corollary
2.2
as
\mathcal{K}\approx\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N}
),
set the approximate solution
v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}
as

v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(t)=g(t)+h\sum_{j=-N}^{N}k(t,\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh))v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh))\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh),

(5.1)

where the mesh size
h
is selected by the formula (
2.6
).
Setting
(2N+1)
collocation points
t=\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i}

(i=-N,\,\ldots,\,N)
as

\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i}=\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(ih)\quad(i=-N,\,\ldots,\,N),

(5.2)

we obtain a
(2N+1)\times(2N+1)
system of linear equations:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=-N}^{N}\left\{\delta_{ij}-hk(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i},\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{j})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh)\right\}v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{j})

\displaystyle=g(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i}),\quad i=-N,\ldots,\,N.

(5.3)

Let us define
m
by
m=2N+1
, and let
I_{m}
be
an
m\times m
identity matrix,
and let
\tilde{K}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}
be an
m\times m
matrix
whose
(i,j)
-th element is
hk(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i},\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{j})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh)

(i,j=-N,\,\ldots,\,N)
;
more precisely,

\tilde{K}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}=\begin{bmatrix}hk(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{-N},\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{-N})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(-Nh)&\cdots&hk(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{-N},\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{j})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh)&\cdots&hk(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{-N},\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(Nh)\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
hk(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i},\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{-N})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(-Nh)&\cdots&hk(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i},\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{j})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh)&\cdots&hk(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{i},\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(Nh)\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
hk(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N},\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{-N})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(-Nh)&\cdots&hk(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N},\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{j})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(jh)&\cdots&hk(\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N},\tilde{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N})\{\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\}^{\prime}(Nh)\\
\end{bmatrix}.

and
\tilde{u}_{j}=v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(jh)

(j=-N,\,\ldots,\,N)

where

By the use of the solution vector
\boldsymbol{\tilde{u}}_{m}
,
let us introduce another approximate solution
\tilde{u}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}

h

2.6

5.4
)

\boldsymbol{\tilde{u}}_{m}

\tilde{u}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}

5.5
).

5.3
Discussion on the differences between the original and new Sinc-collocation methods

The primary difference between the original and new Sinc-collocation methods
lies in the system of linear equations to be solved.
Recall that
the system of equations in the original methods is (
4.5
)
or (
4.9
),
whereas
that in the new methods is (
5.4
)
or (
5.8
).
In the case of the original methods,
users must handle the exceptional cases at

i,\,j=\pm(N+1)
,
due to the inconsistency of the collocation
points (
4.3
)
or (
4.8
).
In the case of the new methods,
the systems of linear equations are considerably simple and easy to implement,
owing to the consistent collocation
points (
5.2
)
or (
5.7
).
Especially in the matrices
K_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}
and
K_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}
of the original methods,
the elements in
j=\pm(N+1)
include a summation term
\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}_{N}
or
\mathcal{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}_{N}
,
which may incur a high computational cost.
In contrast, such a term is not included in the matrices

\tilde{K}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}
and
\tilde{K}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}
of the new methods.
For this reason,
the difference in the computation time between the original and new methods
is significant in the case where

k
is computationally complex for evaluation.

One minor difference is that
the size of the systems (
5.4
)
and (
5.8
)
is
m=2N+1
,
which is slightly smaller than
the size of the systems (
4.5
)
and (
4.9
):
n=2N+3
.
It also should be noted that the original and new methods
share exactly the same basis functions.
Accordingly,
the number of the basis functions is the same:
n=2N+3
.
In terms of the convergence rates,
as Theorems
4.1
–
4.2

and Theorems
5.1
–
5.2
show,
there is no difference
between the original and new methods.

In short, the new methods have an advantage in terms of practicality of implementation
and computational cost,
without the loss of convergence performance.
This is also confirmed in the next section.

6

In this section, numerical comparisons
between the four methods
(original SE/DE-Sinc-collocation methods
and new SE/DE-Sinc-collocation methods)
are presented.
The computation was performed on MacBook Air
with 1.7 GHz Intel Core i7 with 8GB memory, running
Mac OS X 10.12.6.
The computation programs were implemented in the C programming language
with double-precision
floating-point arithmetic, and compiled with Apple LLVM version 9.0.0
with the “
-O2
” option.
LAPACK in Apple’s Accelerate framework was used
for computation of the system of linear equations.
The source code for all programs is available at

https://github.com/okayamat/sinc-colloc-fredholm
.

We consider the following four examples.

Example 6.1

(Delves and Mohamed
[
1
, special case of Family 4 in Chap. 10]
)
.

Consider the following equation

.

r
was set as

.

Example 6.2

(Delves and Mohamed
[
1
, Example 4.2.5b]
)
.

Consider the following equation

.

Example 6.3

(Okayama et al.
[
7
, Example 9.4]
)

7
Proof of convergence theorems

This section provides proofs for
Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
.
In this section, we write
X=C([a,b])
for short.

7.1
Proof of Theorem
5.1

Recall that by solving (
5.4
),

v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}
is determined by (
5.1
),
and
\tilde{u}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}
is determined by (
5.5
).
The key point of the proof of Theorem
5.1

is the relation between those two approximate
solutions:
\tilde{u}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}=\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}
.
Using the relation, we have

\|u-\tilde{u}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\|_{X}=\|u-\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\|_{X}\leq\|u-\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}u\|_{X}+\|\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}u-\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\|_{X}\leq\|u-\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}u\|_{X}+\|\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X,X)}\|u-v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\|_{X}.

(7.1)

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5.1
,

u\in\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d}))
holds by Theorem
3.1
.
Thus, using Theorem
2.5
,
we can evaluate the first term in (
7.1
) as

\|u-\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}u\|_{X}\leq C_{1}\sqrt{N}\exp\left(-\sqrt{\pi d\alpha N}\right)

for a constant
C_{1}
independent of
N
.
As for the second term in (
7.1
),
the following result was obtained.

Theorem 7.1

(Okayama et al.
[
7
, Theorem 6.3]
)
.

Suppose that
the assumptions in Theorem
3.1
are fulfilled
with
\mathscr{D}=\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})
for
d\in(0,\,\pi)
.
Then, there exists a positive integer
N_{0}
such that
for all
N\geq N_{0}
,
the equation (
5.1
) has a unique solution
v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\in X
.
Furthermore, there exists a constant
C_{2}
independent of
N

such that for all
N\geq N_{0}
,

\|u-v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\|_{X}\leq C_{2}\exp\left(-\sqrt{\pi d\alpha N}\right).

In addition to providing the estimate of
\|u-v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\|_{X}
,
this theorem guarantees the existence of the unique solution
v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}
;
this means that the system (
5.4
)
is uniquely solvable.
Furthermore, we can estimate the remaining term

\|\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X,X)}
as follows.

Lemma 7.2
.

There exists a constant
C_{3}
independent of
N

such that
\|\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X,X)}\leq C_{3}\log(N+1)
.

The lemma is deduced immediately by using the following result.

Lemma 7.3

(Stenger
[
13
, p. 142]
)
.

Let
h>0
. Then, it holds that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j=-N}^{N}|S(j,h)(x)|\leq\frac{2}{\pi}(3+\log N).

Thus,
we can evaluate the second term in (
7.1
) as

\|\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X,X)}\|u-\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}\|_{X}\leq C_{3}\log(N+1)\cdot C_{2}\exp\left(-\sqrt{\pi d\alpha N}\right).

Summing up the above results, we establish Theorem
5.1
.

7.2
Proof of Theorem
5.2

Recall that by solving (
5.8
),

v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}
is determined by (
5.6
),
and
\tilde{u}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}
is determined by (
5.9
).
In the proof of Theorem
5.2
as well,
the key point is the relation between those two approximate
solutions:
\tilde{u}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}=\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}
.
Using the relation, we have

\|u-\tilde{u}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\|_{X}=\|u-\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\|_{X}\leq\|u-\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}u\|_{X}+\|\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}u-\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\|_{X}\leq\|u-\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}u\|_{X}+\|\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X,X)}\|u-v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\|_{X}.

(7.2)

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5.2
,

u\in\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}(\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d}))
holds by Theorem
3.1
.
Thus, using Theorem
2.6
,
we can evaluate the first term in (
7.2
) as

\|u-\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}u\|_{X}\leq C_{1}\exp\left\{\frac{-\pi dN}{\log(2dN/\alpha)}\right\}

for a constant
C_{1}
independent of
N
.
As for the second term in (
7.2
),
the following result was obtained.

Theorem 7.4

(Okayama et al.
[
7
, Theorem 8.1]
)
.

Suppose that
the assumptions in Theorem
3.1
are fulfilled
with
\mathscr{D}=\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})
for
d\in(0,\,\pi/2)
.
Then, there exists a positive integer
N_{0}
such that
for all
N\geq N_{0}
,
the equation (
5.6
) has a unique solution
v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\in X
.
Furthermore, there exists a constant
C_{2}
independent of
N

such that for all
N\geq N_{0}
,

\|u-v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\|_{X}\leq C_{2}\exp\left\{\frac{-2\pi dN}{\log(2dN/\alpha)}\right\}.

In addition to providing the estimate of
\|u-v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\|_{X}
,
this theorem guarantees the existence of the unique solution
v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}
;
this means that the system (
5.8
)
is uniquely solvable.
Furthermore,
in the same way as Lemma
7.2
,
we can estimate the remaining term

\|\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X,X)}
as follows.

Lemma 7.5
.

There exists a constant
C_{3}
independent of
N

such that
\|\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X,X)}\leq C_{3}\log(N+1)
.

Thus,
we can evaluate the second term in (
7.2
) as

\|\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X,X)}\|u-\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}v_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}\|_{X}\leq C_{3}\log(N+1)\cdot C_{2}\exp\left\{\frac{-2\pi dN}{\log(2dN/\alpha)}\right\}.

Summing up the above results, we establish Theorem
5.2
.

8
Concluding remarks

Okayama et al.
[
7
]
presented
the SE/DE-Sinc-collocation methods for the equation (
1.1
).
Those methods can attain exponential convergence even if
the functions
k
and
g
have derivative singularity at the endpoints.
However, those methods are challenging or painful to implement
owing to the inconsistent collocation points (
4.3
)
or (
4.8
).
To address the situation, this paper proposed
new SE/DE-Sinc-collocation methods
that employ consistent collocation points (
5.2
)
or (
5.7
).
The results of a theoretical analysis proved that the new SE/DE-Sinc-collocation methods
exhibit the same convergence property
as the original SE/DE-Sinc-collocation methods.
As shown in Section
6
,
compared with the original methods,
the number of lines of the programs in the new methods was reduced to 70–80 %.
Furthermore, the advantage of the new methods in terms of the computational cost
may be observed in the equations with computationally complex functions
k
and
g
.
It should also be emphasized that
the condition numbers of the resulting matrices
in the new methods
(
I_{m}-\tilde{K}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SE}}
and
I_{m}-\tilde{K}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{DE}}
)
are good
[
8
]
,
although such a result is not obtained yet
for the original methods.

The followings issues may be considered in future studies.
First, a Sinc-collocation method for Volterra integral equations
was proposed by Rashidinia and Zarebnia
[
12
]
,
and results similar to those of the present work can be established for that method.
Second, the error analysis for the Sinc-Nyström methods
can be improved compared with Theorems
7.1

and
7.4
.
As described in Remark
1
,
the mesh size
h
for the SE/DE-Sinc quadrature
is not selected optimally because it can share the same
h

as in the SE/DE-Sinc approximation, which is also used in
the Sinc-collocation methods.
However, in the case of the Sinc-Nyström methods,
only the SE/DE-Sinc quadrature is used,
and the mesh size
h
can be optimally chosen.
The error of this case should be rigorously analyzed for both cases of the SE transformation
[
10
]

and DE transformation
[
3
]
.
Third, explicit error bounds are desired for verified numerical computation.
If we can compute the constant
C
in Theorem
5.1

or
5.2
,
we can guarantee the accuracy of the method by computing
the right-hand side of the inequality.
This can be performed by examining the constants appearing in the
convergence theorems, and using the results for basic
approximation formulas
[
6
]
.
We plan to report further results on these issues in future work.
