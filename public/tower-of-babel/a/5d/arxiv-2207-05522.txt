A continuous approach to Floquet theory for pulse-sequence optimization in solid-state NMR Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Matías Chávez, Matthias Ernst

Abstract
We present a framework that uses a continuous frequency space to describe and design solid-state NMR experiments. The approach is similar to the well established Floquet treatment for NMR, but is not restricted to periodic Hamiltonians and allows the design of experiments in a reverse fashion. The framework is based on perturbation theory on a continuous Fourier space, which leads to effective, i.e., time-independent, Hamiltonians. It allows the back calculation of the pulse scheme from the desired effective Hamiltonian as a function of spin-system parameters. We show as an example how to back calculate the rf irradiation in the MIRROR experiment from the desired chemical-shift offset behaviour of the sequence.

II
Theory

II.1
Standard Floquet theory

The formalism presented in this article is similar to the formulation of Floquet theory used in magnetic resonance. Therefore we first want to give a short review of the Floquet treatment and subsequently present the new formalism. A more detailed derivation can be found in the literature.
Shirley 1965
;
Leskes, Madhu, and Vega 2010
;
Ivanov et al. 2021

The time dependence of the density operator is given by the Liouville-von Neumann equation

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}(t)=-i\left[{\mathcal{H}}(t),\sigma(t)\right]

(1)

with the solution

\displaystyle\sigma(t)=\textrm{U}(t)\sigma(0)\textrm{U}^{-1}(t)

(2)

where the propagator is formally given by

\displaystyle\textrm{U}(t)={\mathcal{T}}\exp\left(-i\int\limits_{0}^{t}{\mathcal{H}}(\tau)d\tau\right)

(3)

and
\mathcal{T}
is the Dyson time-ordering operator.
Dyson 1949

An equivalent formulation and a good starting point for the derivation of Floquet theory is the differential equation in the propagator

\displaystyle\dot{\textrm{U}}(t)=-i\,{\mathcal{H}}(t)\textrm{U}(t)

(4)

Floquet theory for magnetic resonance is based on the assumption that the Hamiltonian is periodic and, therefore, the Schrödinger equation is a periodic differential equation.
We can incorporate the periodicity of the Hamiltonian explicitly by expanding it in a Fourier series as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(t)=\sum_{k}{\mathcal{H}}^{(k)}e^{ik\omega t}

(5)

The form of the solution of the periodic differential Eq. (
4
) is given by the Floquet theorem
Floquet 1883
as

\displaystyle\textrm{U}(t)=u(t)e^{i\Lambda t}u^{-1}(0)

(6)

where
\Lambda
is a diagonal real matrix because
{\mathcal{H}}(t)
is a Hermitian operator. The matrix
u(t)
has the same periodicity as the Hamiltonian and can also be expressed as a Fourier series

\displaystyle u(t)=\sum_{n}u_{n}e^{in\omega t}

(7)

Notice that now the time dependence of the operators is only in the exponent and, therefore, inserting Eqs. (
5
) - (
7
) into Eq. (
4
) will lead to the algebraic equation

\displaystyle\sum_{k}({\mathcal{H}}^{(n-k)}+\omega\delta_{nk})u_{k}=\Lambda u_{n}

(8)

This equation is equivalent to a time-independent Schrödinger equation for the Fourier coefficients
u_{n}
, where the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{F}=\sum_{k}{\mathcal{H}}^{(n-k)}+\omega\delta_{nk}

(9)

is called the Floquet Hamiltonian.
It is convenient to represent the Floquet Hamiltonian and
u_{n}
in a product space of the spin Hilbert space and the Fourier space, where each basis state corresponds to a Fourier harmonic. Typically the basis states are written as
\ket{n,\mu}=\ket{n}\otimes\ket{\mu}
, where
\ket{\mu}
denote the basis states of the spin Hilbert space, and
\ket{n}
are the basis states of the Fourier space. Throughout this article Greek letters are used for the spin basis and Latin letters for the Fourier harmonics. Notice, that there are infinitely many Fourier harmonics (
n\in\mathbb{Z}
), hence the matrix representation of the operators has infinite dimensions as well. The explicit representation of
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}

Scholz, van Beek, and Ernst 2010
;
Leskes, Madhu, and Vega 2010
;
Ivanov et al. 2021
is given by:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{F}=\sum_{n}F_{n}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}^{(n)}+\omega F_{z}\otimes\mathbf{1}

(10)

Here, the
F
operators act on the Fourier space and are defined by
F_{z}\ket{n}=n\ket{n}
and
F_{n}\ket{m}=\ket{m+n}
. The solution of Eqs. (
8
) or (
10
) leads to the formal solution of Eq. (
4
) expressed in the spin Hilbert space:

\displaystyle\textrm{U}(t)=\sum_{n}\bra{n}\exp\left(i{\mathcal{H}}_{F}t\right)\ket{0}e^{in\omega t}

(11)

In conclusion, we solved the Liouville-von Neumann equation (Eq. (
1
)) by converting it to an algebraic equation using Fourier series expansions and the Floquet theorem assuming a periodic time-dependent Hamiltonian. As a basis we chose the product of the spin Hilbert space and Fourier space basis, which uses Fourier harmonics.
Typically van Vleck perturbation theory is applied on the Floquet Hamiltonian to obtain effective Hamiltonians. The procedure can be found in the literature
Ramesh and Krishnan 2001
;
Vinogradov, Madhu, and Vega 2001
;
Ernst, Samoson, and Meier 2005
;
Scholz, Meier, and Ernst 2007
;
Leskes, Madhu, and Vega 2010
;
Ivanov et al. 2021
and leads to the first and second-order Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(1)}={\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}

(12)

and

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{n\neq 0}\frac{[{\mathcal{H}}^{(n)},{\mathcal{H}}^{(-n)}]}{n\omega}

(13)

Extending operator-based Floquet theory to multiple frequencies is straightforward. The details can be found in several reviews.
Scholz, van Beek, and Ernst 2010
;
Leskes, Madhu, and Vega 2010
;
Ivanov et al. 2021

II.2
Frequency-domain formulation

Let us consider an arbitrary time-dependent Hamiltonian that is not necessarily periodic in time. In this case we cannot use the Floquet theorem nor a Fourier series to solve Eq. (
1
), since both require periodicity. However, we can use similar methods such as the Fourier transform to obtain an algebraic equation from Eq. (
4
). Instead of a Fourier series expansion, we apply the Fourier transformations to each matrix element of the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}(t)
and the propagator
\textrm{U}(t)
. This will lead to a similar description, where the Fourier transformed operators take the role of the Fourier coefficients in the Floquet approach. In addition, Fourier transformation leads to a continuous spectrum of frequencies instead of the Fourier harmonics.

For the derivation of the generalized framework we again start from the differential equation Eq. (
4
).
As a first step we use the Fourier transformation (element wise) to define a frequency-domain Hamiltonian and propagator as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(t)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{{\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)}\}=\tau\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)e^{i\Omega t}\textrm{d}\Omega

(14)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)=\mathcal{F}\{{{\mathcal{H}}(t)}\}=\frac{1}{2\,\pi\,\tau}\int\limits_{0}^{\tau}{\mathcal{H}}(t)e^{-i\Omega t}\textrm{d}t

(15)

\displaystyle\textrm{U}(t)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{{\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega)}\}=\tau\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega)e^{i\Omega t}\textrm{d}\Omega

(16)

\displaystyle\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega)=\mathcal{F}\{\textrm{U}(t)\}=\frac{1}{2\,\pi\,\tau}\int\limits_{0}^{\tau}\textrm{U}(t)e^{-i\Omega t}\textrm{d}t

(17)

The Fourier transformation is normalized such that it leads to a correspondence with the standard Floquet approach. Thus, the presented theory is a generalization of standard Floquet theory and reproduces all of its results. Notice that the normalization also has to ensure
\mathcal{F}\{\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{f(x)\}\}==f(x)
.
The ’wide’ hats on
\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega)
and
\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)
indicate that these are frequency-domain operators. The underlying frequency domain is continuous, orthonormal, and complete, hence conceptually similar to the position or momentum basis used, for example, to describe a free particle.
Inserting the expressions in Eq. (
4
) and using the convolution theorem leads to

\displaystyle i\,\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\Omega\,\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega)\}

\displaystyle=-i\,\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\}\,\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega)\}

\displaystyle=-i\,\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)*\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega)\}

(18)

where
*
symbolizes the convolution operation in frequency space.
Applying Fourier transformation to Eq. (
18
) results in the time-independent equation

\displaystyle\Omega\,\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega)=-\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)*\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega)

(19)

Since convolution with the delta function is an identity operation we can write Eq. (
19
) as

\displaystyle\Omega\,\delta(\Omega)*\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega)=-\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)*\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega)

(20)

and express it as a single integral

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big[\Omega\,\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})+\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})\big]\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega^{\prime})\,\textrm{d}\Omega^{\prime}=0

(21)

Since this has to be fulfilled for every possible
\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega^{\prime})
the kernel has to vanish

\displaystyle\big[\Omega\,\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})+\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})\big]\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega^{\prime})=0

(22)

Notice, that this is an eigenvalue equation of
\widehat{\textrm{U}}(\Omega^{\prime})
with the eigenoperator

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{F}=\Omega\,\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})+\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})

(23)

Similar to the standard Floquet treatment, we can express this Hamiltonian in the orthonormal product basis
\ket{\Omega,\mu}=\ket{\Omega}\otimes\ket{\mu}
. As before the states
\ket{\mu}
denote the basis states of the spin Hilbert space, but
\ket{\Omega}
are the basis states of the
continuous
Fourier space.
Notice that the operators
\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})
and
\Omega
in
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}
live in different subspaces, i.e the spin and the frequency space, respectively.
Hence the Hamiltonian in the product basis takes the form

\displaystyle\bra{\Omega,\mu}{\mathcal{H}}_{F}\ket{\Omega^{\prime},\nu}=\Omega\,\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})\,\otimes\delta_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle+\mathbf{1}\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})

(24)

where
\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)=\bra{\mu}\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\ket{\nu}
are the matrix elements of the spin-system Hamiltonian at frequency
\Omega
.

In full analogy to the Floquet description (Eq. (
10
)),
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}
can be formulated in a basis-free operator form as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{F}=\int\widehat{D}(\Omega)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\,\textrm{d}\Omega+\widehat{\Omega}\otimes\mathbf{1}

(25)

with the frequency operator

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}\ket{\Omega}=\Omega\ket{\Omega}

\displaystyle\iff\widehat{\Omega}=\int\Omega^{\prime}\ket{\Omega^{\prime}}\bra{\Omega^{\prime}}\,\textrm{d}\Omega^{\prime}

(26)

and the frequency-shift operator

\displaystyle\widehat{D}(\Omega_{0})\ket{\Omega}=\ket{\Omega+\Omega_{0}}

(27)

\displaystyle\widehat{D}(\Omega_{0})=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\ket{\Omega^{\prime}+\Omega_{0}}\bra{\Omega^{\prime}}\textrm{d}\Omega^{\prime}.

(28)

The equivalence of Eq. (
25
) and Eq. (
23
) is shown in the supplementary information (SI).
Notice that we introduced the frequency operator
\widehat{\Omega}
, which is similar to a position operator, but acting on the frequency space. It takes the place of the number operator
F_{z}
in standard Floquet theory. The frequency-shift operator
\widehat{D}(\Omega)
is conceptually similar to the usual translation operator in the position space. It takes the place of the generalized ladder operators
F_{n}
in standard Floquet theory. Both of this operators act exclusively on the frequency domain.

As in the standard Floquet treatment used in solid state NMR we utilize a frequency basis together with the spin basis to represent the Hamiltonian. In contrast to the standard Floquet theory we use a continuous frequency basis, which enables the description of an arbitrary time-modulated Hamiltonian. Nonetheless, we obtained a similar description, employing a continuous Fourier basis and Fourier transformations instead of the discrete Fourier basis and Fourier series as in the Floquet approach. Since we do not have a discrete Fourier basis, we do not obtain a representation of the Hamiltonian as a matrix of constant coefficients, but as a frequency dependent matrix function. However, the commutation relations are similar to those of the Floquet approach and allow for a similar treatment for example using van Vleck perturbation theory.
Van Vleck 1929
;
Primas 1961
;
Primas 1963

II.2.1
Generalization to multiple time-dependent modulations

In principle, with the continuous frequency basis, there is no need to describe multiple time-dependent modulations using different frequency dimensions as is required in standard Floquet theory. All time-dependent modulations could be lumped into a single dimension. However, to understand resonance conditions between different processes, e.g., magic-angle spinning and radio-frequency irradiation of the spins, it is advantageous to separate this processes in different dimensions represented by multiple frequency bases. Let us generalize the approach presented in the previous section to
n
time-dependent modulations of the Hamiltonian. As before we can describe the Hamiltonian in a frequency domain by using Fourier transformation, resulting in the expression

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(t)=\tau^{n}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\cdots,\Omega_{n})

\displaystyle e^{i\Omega_{1}t}e^{i\Omega_{2}t}\cdots e^{i\Omega_{n}t}\,\textrm{d}\Omega_{1}\,\textrm{d}\Omega_{2}\cdots\textrm{d}\Omega_{n}

(29)

Now the underlying basis is the direct product basis of
n
frequency bases and one spin basis
\{\ket{\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\cdots,\Omega_{n},\mu}\}
. This approach is similar to multimodal Floquet theory, but is not limited to periodic modulations of the Hamiltonian.
From Eq. (
29
) we directly obtain the resonance condition

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}^{(0)}+\Omega_{2}^{(0)}+\cdots+\Omega_{n}^{(0)}=0

(30)

Following the same procedure as in the single mode case we obtain a time-independent Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{F}

\displaystyle=\int\int\cdots\int\widehat{D}_{1}(\Omega_{1})\otimes\widehat{D}_{2}(\Omega_{2})\otimes\cdots\otimes\widehat{D}_{n}(\Omega_{n})

\displaystyle\quad\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\cdots,\Omega_{n})\,\,\textrm{d}\Omega_{1}\textrm{d}\Omega_{2}\cdots\textrm{d}\Omega_{n}

\displaystyle\quad\oplus\widehat{\Omega}_{1}\oplus\widehat{\Omega}_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus\widehat{\Omega}_{n}

(31)

where
\widehat{D}_{j}(\Omega)
with
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
is the corresponding frequency-translation operator and
\widehat{\Omega}_{j}
the frequency operator.
It is important to emphasise that in practice most likely two frequency domains will be sufficient, a spatial modulation, for example due to sample rotation, and a modulation of the spin system due to rf-field irradiation. In this case the bimodal approach will be sufficient. However, for rf-field irradiation addressing different spin species, like proton, nitrogen or electron spins, a higher modal approach might offer advantages. In principle, one can also combine the frequency-domain approach in one dimension (e.g., rf irradiation) with the traditional Floquet approach in a different dimension (e.g., sample rotation) where the Fourier series is a perfect description of the time-dependent Hamiltonian (
vide infra
).

II.3
Van Vleck perturbation theory and effective Hamiltonian

In the following, we apply van Vleck perturbation theory
Van Vleck 1929
;
Primas 1961
;
Primas 1963
to the Floquet Hamiltonian defined in Eq. (
25
) with the goal of obtaining effective Hamiltonians of different orders. The detailed derivation can be found in the SI and follows the treatment in Ref.
Ernst, Samoson, and Meier 2005

As usual we split the Hamiltonian into two parts

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{F}={\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}+\varepsilon\,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(1)}

(32)

and apply the van Vleck transformation given by

\displaystyle e^{S}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}\,e^{S^{\dagger}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{[S,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}]_{m}}{m!}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\Lambda_{F}^{(n)}

(33)

where the nested commutator is defined as
[S,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]_{m}=[S,[S,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]_{m-1}]
with
[S,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]_{0}={\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
.
We choose
S
, such that
[\Lambda_{F},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]=0
and proceed by expanding
S
as a series

\displaystyle S=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}S^{(n)}

(34)

and inserting it into Eq. (
33
), which leads to

\displaystyle[S^{(n)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]=\Lambda_{F}^{(n)}-\Phi^{(n)}_{F}

(35)

with

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{j}\Phi^{(j)}_{F}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]_{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},\varepsilon\,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(1)}]_{m}}{m!}.

(36)

Adopting the approach from Primas
Primas 1961
;
Primas 1963
we obtain the formal solution of Eq. (
162
) as

\displaystyle S^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{F}^{-1}(\Pi(\Phi^{(n)}_{F})-\Phi^{(n)}_{F})

(37)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{F}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\Pi(\Phi^{(n)}_{F})

(38)

where
\Pi(X)
is the projection operator
and
\Gamma_{F}^{-1}
the inverse commutation operator defined in the SI.

Let us calculate the first and second-order effective Hamiltonians for the single-mode case:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{F}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Omega}

(39)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{(1)}_{F}

\displaystyle=\int\widehat{D}(\Omega)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\,\textrm{d}\Omega=\Phi^{(1)}_{F}

(40)

For the first-order effective Hamiltonian we evaluate Eq. (
159
) leading to

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(1)}=\bra{\Omega^{\prime}}\Lambda_{F}^{(1)}\ket{\Omega}=\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(0)=\frac{1}{2\,\pi\,\tau}\int\limits_{0}^{\tau}{{\mathcal{H}}}(t)\,\textrm{d}t

(41)

For the second-order Hamiltonian we use Eqs. (
153
-
159
) resulting in

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2}\,PV\int\frac{[\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega),\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(-\Omega)]}{\Omega}\textrm{d}\Omega

(42)

We use the Cauchy principal value (
PV
) for the regularization of the integral, which avoids the integration over the singularity using limits.
The main difference of Eq. (
42
) to Floquet theory is the integral over the frequency-domain Hamiltonian, instead of a sum of Fourier coefficients of the Hamiltonian. This disparity stems from the fact that the underlying Fourier space is continuous in contrast to the discrete space we use to describe periodic Hamiltonians.

The derivation of the effective Hamiltonians for multiple frequency dimensions is quite similar and leads to the first and second-order effective Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\int\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega^{(0)}_{1},-\Omega^{(0)}_{1})d\Omega^{(0)}_{1}

(43)

and

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}PV\int\textrm{d}\Omega_{1}\int\textrm{d}\Omega_{1}^{(0)}\int\textrm{d}\Omega_{2}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{[\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}),\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega_{1}^{(0)}-\Omega_{1},-\Omega_{1}^{(0)}-\Omega_{2})]}{\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{2}}

(44)

where we used the resonance condition

\displaystyle\Omega^{(0)}_{2}=-\Omega^{(0)}_{1}

(45)

In fact, we can retrieve the effective Hamiltonians obtained from Floquet theory, considering a periodic Hamiltonian. Periodicity with period
\tau_{\textrm{m}}
, causes quantization in the frequency domain with the frequency
\omega_{\textrm{m}}=2\,\pi/\tau_{\textrm{m}}
. Therefore the frequency-domain Hamiltonian is only non-zero at the harmonics
n\,\omega_{\textrm{m}}
. Expressing this fact by a Dirac comb we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\,e^{i\Omega t}\,\textrm{d}\Omega

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\delta(\Omega-n\,\omega_{\textrm{m}})\,\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\,e^{i\Omega t}\,\textrm{d}\Omega

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(n\,\omega_{\textrm{m}})\,e^{in\omega_{\textrm{m}}t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{H}}^{(n)}\,e^{in\omega_{\textrm{m}}t}

(46)

where we can identify the Fourier coefficients of the Hamiltonian as
\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(n\,\omega_{\textrm{m}})={\mathcal{H}}^{(n)}
.
This is simply the transition of the Fourier transformation to a Fourier series for a periodic function. As a consequence imposing a periodic boundary condition on the Hamiltonian leads to the results from Floquet theory.

As mentioned already above, it is possible to use a mixed approach, describing periodic modulations with a discrete Fourier space and non-periodic modulations on a continuous Fourier space. We can use the same approach with the delta comb as in Eq. (
46
) to arrive at

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{n}\delta(\Omega_{1}-n\omega_{\textrm{m}})\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})\,e^{i\Omega_{1}t}e^{i\Omega_{2}t}\,\textrm{d}\Omega_{1}\,\textrm{d}\Omega_{2}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{n}\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(n\omega_{\textrm{m}},\Omega_{2})\,e^{in\omega_{\textrm{m}}t}e^{i\Omega_{2}t}\,\textrm{d}\Omega_{2}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{n}\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(n)}(\Omega_{2})\,e^{in\omega_{\textrm{m}}t}e^{i\Omega_{2}t}\,\textrm{d}\Omega_{2}

(47)

This approach is useful to analyze general solid-state NMR experiments under MAS, where the sample spinning is described as a periodic modulation, but the rf-field irradiation can be arbitrary. Such mixed approaches can be used for any number of modes, where for example the periodic modulation due to the chemical shift offset is treated as a third mode.
The first and second-order effective Hamiltonians for the mixed approach with two frequencies as in Eq. (
47
) have the form

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{0}}\sum_{\Omega_{0}}\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(n_{0})}(\Omega_{0})

(48)

and

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\sum_{n_{0},\Omega_{0}}PV\int\textrm{d}\Omega\quad\frac{[\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(n)}(\Omega),\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(n_{0}-n)}(\Omega_{0}-\Omega)]}{\Omega+n\omega_{\textrm{m}}}

(49)

with the resonance conditions defined by
\Omega_{0}+n_{0}\omega_{\textrm{m}}=0
.
Similar to standard Floquet theory, the propagation with an effective Hamiltonian is mediated by

\displaystyle\bar{\textrm{U}}(t)=\exp(-i\,\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(1)}\,t)

(50)

In contrast to standard Floquet Theory, the effective Hamiltonian is only valid for one specific duration, since the time dependence is implicitly in
{\mathcal{H}}^{(1)}
. However, if the duration of the irradiation scheme is changed or the scheme is applied repeatedly, the effective Hamiltonian is weighted by a function, which depends on the duration or repetitions. In section
II.5.2
we derive this function and discuss its effects.

II.4
Calculation of the frequency-domain interaction-frame trajectory

As already mentioned, sample spinning in NMR leads to a periodic modulation of the Hamiltonian and can be described by a discrete Fourier series with the Fourier coefficients typically limited to the range -2 to 2. Radio-frequency irradiation is also often periodic in time, but typically an interaction-frame transformation is required to ensure the convergence of the effective Hamiltonian series.
We split the Hamiltonian into two parts

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(t)={\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{S}}(t)+{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{CS}}(t)+{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{rf}}(t)={\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{0}}(t)+{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(t)

(51)

where
{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{0}}
describes the spin-system Hamiltonian,
{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{rf}}(t)
is the rf-field Hamiltonian and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{CS}}(t)
the isotropic chemical-shift Hamiltonian. We can choose how to divide the total Hamiltonian into
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
. Either we can set
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(t)={\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{rf}}(t)
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}(t)={\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{S}}(t)+{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{CS}}(t)
resulting in an interaction-frame transformation by the rf irradiation only. In this case, the interaction-frame transformation is the same for all spins. Alternatively we can set
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(t)={\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{CS}}(t)+{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{rf}}(t)
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}(t)={\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{S}}(t)
resulting in an interaction-frame transformation by the time-dependent effective field of each spin. In this case we have again two options. We can either include only the isotropic chemical shift into the interaction-frame transformation or we can include the isotropic and the anisotropic chemically shift. With this choice, the interaction-frame trajectory of each spin will be distinct if the chemical shifts are different. The selection of the most convenient interaction frame will depend on the system and problem at hand.
In the following we use an interaction frame generated by a general rf-field modulation including the chemical shift offset. In this case the unitary interaction-frame transformation of the Hamiltonian for each spin is a general complex rotation. Again, each spin will have its unique unitary transformation and, therefore, its unique frame, except if they are chemical equivalent. In the usual rotating frame defined by the Zeeman Hamiltonians, the Hamiltonian used for the interaction-frame transformation has the form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}(t)[\cos(\phi(t))\textrm{I}_{x}+\sin(\phi(t))\textrm{I}_{y}]+\omega_{z}(t)\textrm{I}_{z}

\displaystyle=\vec{\theta}(t)\vec{\textrm{I}}

(52)

with

\displaystyle\vec{\theta}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{x}(t)\\
\theta_{y}(t)\\
\theta_{z}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}(t)\cos\left(\phi(t)\right)\\
\omega_{1}(t)\sin\left(\phi(t)\right)\\
\omega_{z}(t)\end{pmatrix}

(53)

\displaystyle|\theta(t)|=\sqrt{\omega^{2}_{1}(t)+\omega^{2}_{z}(t)}

(54)

where
\omega_{1}(t)
is the amplitude and
\phi(t)
the phase of the rf-field and
\omega_{z}(t)
the chemical shift offset.
The direction of
\vec{\theta}(t)
specifies the axis of rotation, its length
|\theta(t)|
is the angular velocity at time
t
.
The interaction-frame transformation is given by

\displaystyle\textrm{U}(t)={\cal{T}}\exp\left(-i\,\int\limits_{0}^{t}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(t^{\prime})\,\textrm{d}t^{\prime}\right)

(55)

where
\cal{T}
is the Dyson time-ordering operator.
Dyson 1949

The evolution of the initial spin operators
\vec{\textrm{I}}=(\textrm{I}_{x},\textrm{I}_{y},\textrm{I}_{z})^{T}
can be written with a single rotation matrix with elements
a_{\mu\nu}(t)
as

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{\mu}(t)=\textrm{U}^{\dagger}(t)\,\textrm{I}_{\mu}\,\textrm{U}(t)=\sum\limits_{\nu}a_{\mu\nu}(t)\,\textrm{I}_{\nu}

(56)

This also can be expressed in the frequency domain using Fourier
transform leading to

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{\mu}(t)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\widehat{\textrm{I}}_{\mu}(\Omega)\}=\sum\limits_{\nu}a_{\mu\nu}(t)\,\textrm{I}_{\nu}

(57)

\displaystyle\widehat{\textrm{I}}_{\mu}(\Omega)=\mathcal{F}\{\textrm{I}_{\mu}(t)\}=\sum\limits_{\nu}\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)\,\textrm{I}_{\nu}

(58)

where
\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)=\mathcal{F}\{a_{\mu\nu}(t)\}
.
In the following we present an efficient route to calculate
a_{\mu\nu}(t)
. Notice that the map
\textrm{I}_{\mu}\mapsto\textrm{U}(t)\,\textrm{I}_{\mu}\,\textrm{U}^{\dagger}(t)
and
\textrm{I}_{\mu}\mapsto(-\textrm{U}(t))\,\textrm{I}_{\mu}\,(-\textrm{U}^{\dagger}(t))
leads to the same rotation of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, hence two elements of
SU(2)
are mapped onto one element of
SO(3)
.
In more technical terms this means that there exists a 2:1 surjective homomorphism from
SU(2)
to
SO(3)
.
However Eq. (
55
) yields to only one of the two
SU(2)
elements, since it is impossible to get
-\textrm{U}(t)
from a given
\textrm{U}(t)
with the Hamiltonian of the form given in Eq. (
52
). Therefore, the operator defined in Eq. (
55
) is always a member of
SU(2)/Z_{2}
which is isomorphic to
SO(3)
, i.e.
SU(2)/Z_{2}\cong SO(3)
. This fact will be important for the back-calculation, when we infer the rf-irradiation from rotation matrices.A second important point is, that in general,
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(t)
does not commute with itself at different times. For this reason we divide the irradiation into time intervals, small enough to assume that the Hamiltonian commutes with itself during the interval. However, the intervals do not necessary have to be of the same size and can be adjusted to fit the problem at hand.
The propagator of the
j\,
th interval corresponds to
\textrm{U}_{j}
and has a complex
2\times 2
matrix representation given in the SI. Equipped with this matrix representation we calculate the coefficients of the rotation matrices
a^{(j)}_{\mu\nu}
corresponding to
\textrm{U}_{j}
as

\displaystyle\textrm{U}_{j}^{\dagger}\,\textrm{I}_{\mu}\,\textrm{U}_{j}=\sum_{\nu}a_{\mu\nu}^{(j)}\textrm{I}_{\nu}

(59)

Using
a_{\mu\nu}=2\,\text{Tr}(\textrm{I}_{\mu}\,\textrm{U}\,\textrm{I}_{\nu}\,\textrm{U}^{-1})
, leads to
Cornwell 1984

\displaystyle a_{xx}=\left[\left(\theta^{2}_{y}+\theta_{z}^{2}\right)\cos\left(|\theta|\right)+\theta^{2}_{x}\right]/(4|\theta|)

\displaystyle a_{xy}=\left[-\theta_{x}\theta_{y}\left(\cos\left(|\theta|\right)-1\right)-\theta_{z}|\theta|\sin\left(|\theta|\right)\right]/(4|\theta|)

(60)

\displaystyle a_{xz}=\left[\theta_{y}|\theta|\sin\left(|\theta|\right)+\theta_{x}\theta_{z}\left(1-\cos\left(|\theta|\right)\right)\right]/(4|\theta|)

\displaystyle a_{yx}=\left[\theta_{z}|\theta|\sin\left(|\theta|\right)-\theta_{x}\theta_{y}\left(\cos\left(|\theta|\right)-1\right)\right]/(4|\theta|)

\displaystyle a_{yy}=\left[\left(\theta^{2}_{x}+\theta_{z}^{2}\right)\cos\left(|\theta|\right)+\theta^{2}_{y}\right]/(4|\theta|)

(61)

\displaystyle a_{yz}=\left[-\theta_{y}\,\theta_{z}\left(\cos\left(|\theta|\right)-1\right)-\theta_{x}|\theta|\sin\left(|\theta|\right)\right]/(4|\theta|)

\displaystyle a_{zx}=\left[-\theta_{y}|\theta|\sin\left(|\theta|\right)-\theta_{z}\theta_{x}\left(\cos\left(|\theta|\right)-1\right)\right]/(4|\theta|)

\displaystyle a_{zy}=\left[\theta_{x}|\theta|\sin\left(|\theta|\right)-\theta_{z}\theta_{y}\left(\cos\left(|\theta|\right)-1\right)\right]/(4|\theta|)

(62)

\displaystyle a_{zz}=\left[(\theta^{2}_{x}+\theta^{2}_{y})\cos\left(|\theta|\right)+\theta_{z}^{2}\right]/(4|\theta|)

where we omitted the indices
j
and
\theta
on
a_{\mu\nu}
for the sake of simplicity. Equivalent expressions can also be found for the alternate basis with
\mu,\nu\in\{+,-,z\}
.
The interaction-frame trajectory at time
t_{j}
is given by

\displaystyle{\bf{a}}(t_{j})={\bf{a}}_{j}\,{\bf{a}}_{j-1}\cdots{\bf{a}}_{1}

(63)

Note, that the rotation matrices
{\bf{a}}_{j}
describe the rotation during a single time step while the matrix
{\bf{a}}(t_{j})

describes the total rotation up to the time point
t_{j}
.
The expressions for the elements
a_{\mu\nu}(t_{j})
in dependence on the parameter
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{z}
, and
\phi
can be found in the SI. As mentioned before, the elements

\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}{({\Omega})}
are obtained by Fourier transformation of the elements
a_{\mu\nu}(t)

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)=\mathcal{F}\{a_{\mu\nu}(t)\}=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}\int\limits_{0}^{\tau}a_{\mu\nu}(t)e^{i\Omega t}\,dt

(64)

The mathematical properties of
a_{\mu\nu}(t)
and
\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)
can be summarized as follows

\displaystyle a_{\mu\nu}(t)\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)=\widehat{a}^{*}_{\nu\mu}(-\Omega)

(65)

\displaystyle\sum_{\chi}a_{\mu\chi}(t)a_{\nu\chi}(t)=\delta_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\sum\limits_{\chi}\widehat{a}_{\mu\chi}(\Omega)*\widehat{a}_{\nu\chi}(\Omega)=\delta_{\mu\nu}\delta(\Omega)

(66)

II.5
Properties of the interaction-frame trajectory

In the following section the properties of the
time and frequency-domain interaction-frame trajectory are explored.
We will show that any finite irradiation can be expressed in term of
its periodic version, on one hand reducing the computational
effort, on the other hand isolating the effect of the finite duration.
Furthermore we will show that for any cyclic irradiation the calculation of the
frequency-domain interaction-frame trajectory can be reduced to the
calculation of a single segment, further lessening the computational efforts
significantly. Finally we will discuss the case of irradiation along a
single axis, which always enables the expression of the frequency-domain
interaction-frame trajectory in a closed form.

II.5.1
General properties of the interaction-frame trajectory

From the Plancherel theorem we obtain the general property
Plancherel and Leffler 1910

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)|^{2}\,d\Omega=\tau\int\limits_{0}^{\tau}|\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)|^{2}\,dt

(67)

In contrast to standard Floquet Theory, the duration of the
irradiation scheme is incorporated implicitly in the frequency-domain
interaction-frame trajectory.
However any finite
a_{\mu\nu}(t)
defined on

t\in[0,T]
can be made periodic (
t\in\mathbb{R}
) using the modulo function

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)=a_{\mu\nu}(t\,\textrm{mod}\,T)

(68)

where we indicate the periodized function using a tilde. For a
periodic interaction-frame trajectory the frequency-domain interaction-frame trajectory is
related to the Fourier coefficients

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{\mu\nu}^{(n)}\delta(\Omega-n\,{2\pi}/{T})

(69)

This relation is the bridge between standard Floquet theory, which
utilizes Fourier series and continuous Floquet theory, which
uses the Fourier transform. This correspondence only exists for the
periodic case where standard Floquet theory is valid.
Vice versa, a periodic function
\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)
can be made
finite by multiplication with the appropriate rectangular window function

\Pi(t)

\displaystyle a_{\mu\nu}(t)=\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)\cdot\Pi\left(t/T-1/2\right)

(70)

\displaystyle\Pi\left(t/T-1/2\right)=\begin{cases}1&0\leq t\leq T\\
0&\hskip 3.00003pt\text{else}\end{cases}

(71)

Eq. (
70
) and the convolution theorem lead to they
frequency-domain interaction-frame trajectory

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)*\mathcal{F}\left\{\Pi\left(t/T-1/2\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)*\frac{\textrm{sin}(\Omega\,T/2)}{\pi\,\Omega\,T}e^{-i\frac{\Omega}{2}T}

(72)

Notice, that Eq. (
II.5.1
) can be efficiently
implemented numerically via discrete Fourier transformation (DFT),
since
\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)
is periodic.
Furthermore, the slope of
\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)
is dictated
by
T
, as
\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)
has sharp frequency
components, see Eq. (
69
). As a consequence
sharp edges of
\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)
cannot be realized by
any irradiation with finite duration.
We can go a step further and decompose
\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)
into
a product and subsequently use the convolution theorem

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\left\{\Pi(t/T)\cdot\prod\limits_{i}^{n}\,\widetilde{a}^{(i)}_{\mu\nu}(t)\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\left\{\Pi(t/T)\right\}*\widetilde{a}^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)*\widetilde{a}^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)*\ldots*\widetilde{a}^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)

(73)

This trick will be useful to construct pulse schemes, since we can
decompose the desired
\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)
with convolutions
and calculate the necessary
a^{(j)}_{\mu\nu}
for each
part. Finally we obtain the desired trajectory
a_{\mu\nu}(t)
by
calculating the product
\prod^{n}_{j}a^{(j)}_{\mu\nu}
. The ability
to describe sequences of different length and the consequences of
finite recoupling sequences is one of the advantages of the new
method.
Independent of the basis of the spin operator,
a_{\mu\nu}(t)
are the
elements of a rotation matrix, hence

\displaystyle\sum_{\chi}a_{\mu\chi}(t)a_{\nu\chi}(t)=\delta_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\sum\limits_{\chi}\widehat{a}_{\mu\chi}(\Omega)*\widehat{a}_{\nu\chi}(\Omega)=\delta_{\mu\nu}\delta(\Omega)

(74)

The presented properties of the interaction-frame trajectory hitherto
always hold. However some symmetries of
a_{\mu\nu}(t)
and

\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)
depend on the basis of the spin
operator.
In the hermitian basis
\{\textrm{I}_{x},\textrm{I}_{y},\textrm{I}_{z}\}
the interaction-frame
trajectory is real and therefore the elements of the frequency-domain
interaction-frame trajectory are hermitian functions

\displaystyle\widehat{a}^{*}_{\mu\nu}(t)

\displaystyle=\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)

(75)

\displaystyle\widehat{a}^{*}_{\mu\nu}(-\Omega)

\displaystyle=\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)

(76)

In contrast in the basis
\{\textrm{I}_{+},\textrm{I}_{-},\textrm{I}_{z}\}
complex conjugation
flips the sign of the indices, resulting in

\displaystyle a^{*}_{\pm\pm}(t)

\displaystyle=a_{\mp\mp}(t)\,\quad\quad\textrm{and}\quad a^{*}_{\pm\mp}(t)\,\,=a_{\mp\pm}(t)

(77)

\displaystyle a^{*}_{\pm\pm}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=a_{\mp\mp}(-\Omega)\quad\textrm{and}\quad a^{*}_{\pm\mp}(\Omega)=a_{\mp\pm}(-\Omega)

(78)

\displaystyle a^{*}_{z\pm}(t)

\displaystyle=a_{z\mp}(t)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\widehat{a}_{z+}(\Omega)=\widehat{a}^{*}_{z-}(-\Omega)

(79)

II.5.2
Repetitive irradiation - nonzero effective field

Many pulse schemes in NMR are repetitive, i.e., consist of repeating
segments. In general a repetitive irradiation does not generate a cyclic
interaction-frame trajectory but will lead to an effective field after
each segment. In this section we consider a pulse scheme of
duration
T
, consisting of
N
repeating segments of duration
\tau
.
The interaction-frame trajectory of such an irradiation can be written
as

\displaystyle a_{\mu\nu}(t)=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a_{\mu\nu}(t)&0\leq t\leq\tau\\
\sum_{\chi}a_{\mu\chi}(t-\tau)a_{\chi\nu}(\tau)&\tau\leq t\leq 2\tau\\
\sum_{\chi}a_{\mu\chi}(t-2\tau)a_{\chi\nu}(2\tau)&2\tau\leq t\leq 3\tau\\
\hskip 56.9055pt\vdots&\quad\quad\vdots\\
\sum_{\chi}a_{\mu\chi}(t-(N-1)\tau)a_{\chi\nu}((N-1)\tau)&(N-1)\tau\leq t\leq N\tau\end{cases}

(80)

Notice that
a_{\mu\nu}(N\tau)=[\mathbf{a}^{N}(\tau)]_{\mu\nu}
where

\mathbf{a}(\tau)
is the matrix with the elements

a_{\mu\nu}(t)
. The rotation matrix
\mathbf{a}(\tau)
represents
the rotation of the spin due to the effective field. As shown in
detail in the SI, we obtain for a frequency-domain interaction-frame trajectory

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi N\tau}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\chi}\int_{(n-1)\tau}^{n\tau}a_{\mu\chi}(t-(n-1)\tau)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\times a_{\chi\nu}((n-1)\tau)e^{-i\Omega t}dt

(81)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\chi}\bar{a}_{\mu\chi}(\Omega)\,g^{(N)}_{\chi\nu}(\Omega)

(82)

with

\displaystyle g^{(N)}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-in\Omega\tau}a_{\mu\nu}(n\,\tau)

(83)

and

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}\int\limits_{0}^{\tau}{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)e^{-i\Omega t}dt

(84)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\{\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)\Pi\left(t/\tau-1/2\right)\}

(85)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\{\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)\}*\mathcal{F}\left\{\Pi(t/\tau-1/2)\right\}

(86)

As a result the
\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)
can be calculated
efficiently using DFT, since
a_{\mu\nu}(t)
can be reduced to the periodized
trajectory of a single segment.

II.5.3
Cyclic interaction-frame trajectory - zero effective field

As shown previously, a cyclic interaction-frame trajectory
\mathbf{a}(t)
can
be rewritten using modulo and a rectangular window function as

\displaystyle{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)=a_{\mu\nu}(t\,\textrm{mod}\,\tau)\,\Pi\left(\frac{t-T/2}{T}\right)

(87)

The effective field for a cyclic interaction-frame trajectory after a
segment is always zero, i.e., the interaction frame ends at the same point where it started. As a consequence
\mathbf{a}(N\tau)=\mathbf{1}
, which leads
to

\displaystyle g^{(N)}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-in\Omega\tau}

(88)

Notice, that in the case of a periodic interaction-frame trajectory,
i.e., an infinite repetition of the basic pulse scheme, the relation in
Eq. (
69
) connects the
standard Floquet theory with continuous Floquet theory.

II.5.4
Single irradiation axis

In many pulse schemes in NMR, rf irradiation is always along the same
axis. Therefore, the axis of rotation does not change during the pulse
scheme if we do not include the chemical-shift offset into the
interaction-frame calculation. In this case, the phase of the rf-field
Hamiltonian can be kept constant whereas the amplitude
\omega_{1}
can
be positive or negative and allows rotations in both directions. A
special feature of the resulting rf-field Hamiltonian is that it
commutes with itself at different times and, therefore, an analytical
expression for the frequency trajectory
a_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)
can be
found.
Without loss of generality, we consider the case where
\phi=0
and,
therefore,
{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{rf}}
only generates rotation around the x-axis.
It is convenient to transform in a tilted frame, where the z-axis is
aligned with the rotation axis of the rf-field.
The propagator resulting from
{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{rf}}
together with the tilted-frame
transformation is

\displaystyle\textrm{U}(t)=e^{-i\int\limits_{0}^{t}\omega_{1}(t)\textrm{d}t\,\textrm{I}_{z}}e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}\textrm{I}_{y}}

(89)

In most cases we only have to calculate the evolution of the spin
operator along the static magnetic field, which typically is along the
z-axis

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{z}(t)

\displaystyle=\textrm{U}^{\dagger}(t)\textrm{I}_{z}\textrm{U}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{i\int\limits_{0}^{t}\omega_{1}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}\,\textrm{I}^{+}+e^{-i\int\limits_{0}^{t}\omega_{1}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}\,\,\textrm{I}^{-}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(a_{z+}(t)\,\textrm{I}^{+}+a_{z-}(t)\,\textrm{I}^{-}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau}{2}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(a_{z+}(\Omega)\,\textrm{I}^{+}+\widehat{a}_{z-}(\Omega)\,\textrm{I}^{-}\right)e^{i\Omega t}\textrm{d}\Omega

(90)

As a result, we get a relation between
\omega_{1}(t)
and

\widehat{a}_{z\pm}(\Omega)

\displaystyle a_{z\pm}(t)=e^{\pm i\int\limits_{0}^{t}\omega_{1}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}=\tau\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{a}_{z\pm}(\Omega)e^{i\Omega t}\,d\Omega

(91)

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{z\pm}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\pm i\int\limits_{0}^{t}\omega_{1}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}e^{-i\Omega t}\,dt

(92)

We can solve Eq. (
91
) to obtain a solution for

\omega_{1}(t)

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\mp i\,\frac{d}{dt}\ln\left(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\widehat{a}_{z\pm}(\Omega)\}\right)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\Omega\,\widehat{a}_{z\pm}(\Omega)\}}{\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\widehat{a}_{z\pm}(\Omega)\}}

(93)

In addition, from the convolution theorem we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}^{(1)}(\Omega)*\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}^{(2)}(\Omega)*\ldots*\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}^{(n)}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\biggr\{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\,a_{\mu\nu}^{(j)}(t)\biggl\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\biggr\{\exp\biggr(

\displaystyle-i\,\int_{0}^{t}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{1}^{(j)}(t)\textrm{d}t\biggl)\biggl\}

(94)

This means that, if we decompose the desired shape

\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)
as a convolution, we can just add the
corresponding
\omega_{1}(t)
to obtain the desired rf-field profile.

\textrm{U}(t)

\mathbf{a}(t)
. Subsequently
\widehat{\mathbf{a}}(\Omega)

II.6
Calculation of the effective-field Hamiltonian from the frequency-domain interaction-frame trajectory

In this section we reverse the previous procedure and calculate the rf-field Hamiltonian from
\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)
.
As a first step we simply apply the inverse Fourier transform

\displaystyle a_{\mu\nu}(t)=\tau\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)e^{i\Omega t}\,d\Omega

(95)

In the next step we want to construct
\textrm{U}(t)
from
{\bf{a}}(t)
. As mentioned previously the U(t) given in Eq. (
55
) are members of
SU(2)/Z_{2}
, which is isomorphic to
SO(3)
. Hence we can map each rotation matrix
{\bf{a}}_{j}
uniquely to
\textrm{U}_{j}
. For the construction, we use quaternions,
Cartan 1966
;
Blümich and Spiess 1985
since they can represent complex as well as real rotations.
More precisely, we reformulate the rotation matrices as unit quaternions and subsequently represent them with spin matrices in order to read out the pulse parameter.

A unit quaternion is given by

q_{j}=u+j\,v+k\,w+l\,z

with

j\,k\,l=-1

and

u^{2}+v^{2}+w^{2}+z^{2}=1
.
With the Euler-Rodriguez formula,
Rodrigues 1840
;
Cartan 1966
we express its components with the elements of the corresponding rotation matrix

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+tr({\mathbf{a}})}

(96)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\,a}(a_{zy}-a_{yz})

(97)

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\,a}(a_{xz}-a_{zx})

(98)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\,a}(a_{yx}-a_{xy})

(99)

Notice, that there are different options to calculate a unit quaternion from the corresponding rotation matrix. It is important for the numerical evaluation to choose the option, where the denominator is not close to zero, in order to increase the precision. We can always represent a unit quaternion as

\displaystyle q_{j}=\cos\left(\frac{|\theta|\delta t}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{|\theta|}(j\,\theta_{x}+k\,\theta_{y}+l\,\theta_{z})\sin\left(\frac{|\theta|\delta t}{2}\right)

(100)

where
\vec{\theta}(t)
is defined in Eq. (
53
). The norm of
\vec{\theta}(t)
is the angular velocity and the direction specifies the rotation axis.
Next, we rewrite the unit quaternions to assemble
\textrm{U}_{j}
in its exponential form.
Therefore, we represent the quaternion units with the spin matrices

\displaystyle j=2i\,\textrm{I}_{x},\hskip 8.5359ptk=2i\,\textrm{I}_{y},\hskip 8.5359ptl=2i\,\textrm{I}_{z}

(101)

which leads to the desired representation

\displaystyle q_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\,\cos\left(\frac{|\theta|\delta t}{2}\right)-i\frac{\vec{\theta}}{|\theta|}2\,\vec{\textrm{I}}\,\sin\left(\frac{|\theta|\delta t}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp(-i\,\vec{\theta}\,\,\vec{\textrm{I}}\,\delta t)=\exp(-i\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{rf}}\,\delta t)

(102)

Together with the definition of
\vec{\theta}
in Eq. (
53
) we obtain

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+\textrm{tr}(a)}=\cos\left(\frac{|\theta|\delta t}{2}\right)

(103)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\,u}(a_{zy}-a_{yz})=\frac{\omega_{1}}{|\theta|}\cos(\phi)\,\sin\left(\frac{|\theta|\delta t}{2}\right)

(104)

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\,u}(a_{xz}-a_{zx})=\frac{\omega_{1}}{|\theta|}\sin(\phi)\,\sin\left(\frac{|\theta|\delta t}{2}\right)

(105)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\,u}(a_{yx}-a_{xy})=\frac{\omega_{z}}{|\theta|}\,\sin\left(\frac{|\theta|\delta t}{2}\right)

(106)

The solution of these equations for the pulse parameter
\omega_{1},\,\phi,\,\omega_{z}
are

\displaystyle|\theta|\delta t

\displaystyle=2\,\arccos\left(u\right)

(107)

\displaystyle\omega_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\theta|\,w}{\sin(\phi)\,\sin\left(\frac{|\theta|\delta t}{2}\right)}

(108)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\arctan\left(\frac{w}{v}\right)

(109)

\displaystyle\omega_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\theta|\,z}{\sin\left(\frac{|\theta|\delta t}{2}\right)}

(110)

which can be also expressed using
a_{\mu\nu}
as

\displaystyle|\theta|\delta t=\arccos\left(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+\textrm{tr}({\bf{a}})}\right)

(111)

\displaystyle\phi=\arctan\left(\frac{a_{xz}-a_{zx}}{a_{zy}-a_{yz}}\right)

(112)

\displaystyle\omega_{1}=-\frac{\text{arcsec}\left(2\,(\textrm{tr}({\bf{a}})+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)(a_{xz}-a_{zx}+a_{yz}-a_{zy})^{2}\csc\left(2\,\text{arcsec}\left(2\,(\textrm{tr}({\bf{a}})+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)}{\delta t\left[\left(\textrm{tr}({\bf{a}})+1\right)\left((a_{xz}-a_{zx})^{2}+(a_{yz}-a_{zy})^{2}\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}}

(113)

\displaystyle\omega_{z}=\frac{2\,(a_{yx}-a_{xy})\,\text{arcsec}\left(2\,(\textrm{tr}({\bf{a}})+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)}{\delta t\left[\left(\textrm{tr}(\bf{a})+1\right)\left(3-\textrm{tr}({\bf{a}})\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}}

(114)

Eq. (
112
-
114
) express the phase, rf-field amplitude and offset as function of the interaction frame trajectory
a_{\mu\nu}(t)
. In order to obtain the complete sequence, these equations have to be evaluated at every time step of
a_{\mu\nu}(t_{j})
.

Figure

depicts the procedure to obtain the frequency-domain interaction-frame trajectory from the pulse parameter and vice versa.
The mapping between the pulse scheme parameter frequency-domain interaction-frame trajectory is bijective, hence we always can find an unique result in both directions. However, only if the conditions given by Eqs. (
77
-
79
) are fulfilled by the frequency-domain interaction-frame trajectory, a physical solution for the pulse parameter can be found.

III
The design of band-selective MIRROR experiments

In this section we apply continuous Floquet theory to design the rf irradiation for a tunable PDSD-based sequence called AM-MIRROR.
Scholz et al. 2008
;
Wittmann et al. 2014
;
Wittmann et al. 2016
The sequence can be designed for broadband or band-selective polarization transfer even at fast MAS frequencies by creating side-bands in the zero-quantum spectrum. Using the continuous frequency-space Floquet formalism, we show how to calculate the irradiation scheme to achieve tailored zero-quantum side-bands without any need of numerical optimization, which is in general not possible with the standard Floquet approach.

III.1
Theoretical preliminaries

To analyze the MIRROR experiment it is sufficient to consider a
\textrm{S}_{2}\textrm{I}
spin system with irradiation on the
I
spin. In the standard rotating frame such a Hamiltonian has the form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(t)={\mathcal{H}}_{S}(t)+{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{rf}}(t)

(115)

with

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{S}(t)=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=-2}^{2}\omega_{S_{1}S_{2}}^{(n)}e^{in\omega_{\textrm{r}}t}\,(3\,\textrm{S}_{1z}\textrm{S}_{2z}-\vec{\textrm{S}}_{1}\vec{\textrm{S}}_{2})

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{p=1}^{2}\sum\limits_{n=-2}^{2}\omega_{I_{1}S_{p}}^{(n)}e^{in\omega_{\textrm{r}}t}\,2\,\textrm{I}_{z}\textrm{S}_{pz}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{p=1}^{2}\omega_{S_{p}}^{(0)}\textrm{S}_{pz}

(116)

and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{rf}}(t)=\omega_{1}(t)\,\textrm{I}_{x}

(117)

Here, consider only isotropic the chemical shifts of the
S
spins, but not of the
I
spins. Furthermore we assume an amplitude-modulated rf-irradiation in the x-direction (
\phi=0,180^{\circ}
).
For simplicity, we tilt the frame of reference to align the z-axis with rf-irradiation axis as in section
II.5.4
. In addition, we transform into an interaction frame with the isotropic chemical shift of the S spins.
As usual we also transform into an interaction frame with the rf irradiation on the I spins leading to a total interaction-frame transformation defined by:

\displaystyle\textrm{U}(t)=

\displaystyle\underbrace{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\exp\biggl(-i\int\limits_{0}^{t}\omega_{1}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textrm{I}_{z}}\biggl)}}_{\textrm{rf-irradiation}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\underbrace{\vphantom{\int\limits_{0}^{t}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\exp\biggl(-i\frac{\pi}{2}\,\textrm{I}_{y}\biggl)}}_{\textrm{tilted-frame}}}

(118)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\vphantom{\int\limits_{0}^{t}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\exp{\biggl(i\sum\limits_{p=1}^{2}\omega_{Sp}^{(0)}t\,\textrm{S}_{pz}\biggl)}}}_{\textrm{chemical shift}}

Since the S-spin chemical shift and the MAS spinning lead to a continuous rotation around a single axis,
the interaction-frame Hamiltonian
\widetilde{{\mathcal{H}}}(t)\coloneqq\textrm{U}(t){\mathcal{H}}(t)\textrm{U}^{\dagger}(t)
can be written as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{H}}}(t)=T\sum\limits_{n=-2}^{2}\sum\limits_{\ell=-1}^{1}\,\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathcal{H}}^{(n,\ell)}(\Omega)\,e^{in\omega_{\textrm{r}}t}e^{i\ell\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}t}e^{i\Omega t}\,\textrm{d}\Omega

(119)

from where we can read out the MIRROR resonance condition

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}+n_{0}\omega_{r}+\ell_{0}\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}=0

(120)

\displaystyle(\Omega_{0},n_{0},\ell_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times\{\textrm{-}2,\textrm{-}1,...,2\}\times\{\textrm{-}1,0,1\}

(121)

The dominant second-order effective Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{H}}}_{SS\otimes IS}=\frac{1}{4}\sum\limits_{n,\nu=-2}^{2}\,\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\biggl(\frac{\omega_{S_{1}S_{2}}^{(\nu)}(\omega_{I_{1}S_{2}}^{(n-\nu)}-\omega_{I_{1}S_{2}}^{(n-\nu)})}{\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}-\nu\omega_{\textrm{r}}}+\frac{\omega_{S_{1}S_{2}}^{(n-\nu)}(\omega_{I_{1}S_{2}}^{(\nu)}-\omega_{I_{1}S_{2}}^{(\nu)})}{\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}+\nu\omega_{\textrm{r}}}\biggr)\,(\widehat{a}_{z\pm}(\Omega)\,\textrm{I}^{\pm}+\widehat{a}_{z\mp}(\Omega)\,\textrm{I}^{\mp})\,\textrm{S}_{1}^{\pm}\textrm{S}_{2}^{\mp}\,d\Omega

(122)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{z\pm}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\widehat{a}_{zx}(\Omega)\pm i\,\widehat{a}_{zy}(\Omega)

(123)

In the following we are going to use Eq. (
93
)
which enables the calculation of the rf-field amplitude.

III.2
Targeting single
\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}

The first example is the recoupling of a single chemical-shift difference
\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{iso}}
given by

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{z+}(\Omega)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}\delta(\Omega-\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}})\hskip 20.00003pt

(124)

Inserting it in Eq. (
91
) leads to

\displaystyle e^{i\int\limits_{0}^{t}\omega_{1}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(\Omega-\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}})e^{i\Omega t}\,d\Omega=e^{i\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}t}

(125)

hence

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(t)=\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}

(126)

Alternatively we can use Eq. (
93
)

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\Omega\,\delta(\Omega-\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}})\}}{\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\delta(\Omega-\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}})\}}=\frac{\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}e^{-i\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}t}}{e^{-i\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}t}}=\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}

(127)

This solution is not physical, since it would require irradiation of a infinite duration.
As we will show below, the optimal rf-field amplitude for a given duration
T
is in our case

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(t)=\begin{cases}\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}&\,0\leq t\leq T\\
0&\hskip 15.00002ptelse\end{cases}

(128)

Using Eq. (
92
), we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{z+}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\pi T}\,\frac{1-e^{i\text{$T$}(\Omega-\text{$\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}$})}}{\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}-\Omega}

(129)

Note that this is a sinc function where the time origin was shifted to the beginning of the time period and not at the center.
The singularity at
\Omega=\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}
is removable, which can be seen from its expansion

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{z+}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\pi}\,\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{i^{m}}{m!}[T(\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}-\Omega)]^{m-1}

(130)

We can easily calculate the global maximum of
|\widehat{a}_{z+}(\Omega)|
with Eq. (
130
), which is located at
\Omega=\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}

\displaystyle|a_{z+}(\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}})|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}

(131)

Notice, that the global maximum is independent of the duration of the pulse scheme, because of the chosen normalization.

III.3
Targeting multiple isolated
\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}

The simplest example of targeting multiple frequencies is to target 2 frequencies

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{z+}(\Omega)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta(\Omega-\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(1)})+\delta(\Omega-\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(2)})\right)

(132)

Inserting it into Eq. (
93
) leads to

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(t)=\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(1)}+\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(2)}

(133)

This irradiation is not possible for 2 reasons. First, we would again require irradiation of infinite duration as before. The second problem is that two waves with the same phase just add up in amplitude, i.e.
\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(1)}+\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(2)}=\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(3)}
. As a result, we would just target one frequency, namely
\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(3)}
. Notice, that this is the only existing solution for the given
\widehat{a}_{z+}(\Omega)
. Therefore we have no choice than to split the irradiation into parts that target the two frequencies separately and successively in time

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(t)=\begin{cases}\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(1)}&\,0\leq t\leq\tau_{1}\\
\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(2)}&\tau_{1}<t\leq T\end{cases}

(134)

We again can use Eq . (
92
) and obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{z+}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\widehat{a}^{(1)}_{z+}(\Omega)+\widehat{a}^{(2)}_{z+}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\pi T}\frac{\left(1-e^{-i\text{$\tau_{1}$}(\Omega-\text{$\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(1)}$})}\right)}{(\text{$\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(1)}$}-\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{i}{2\pi T}\frac{\left(e^{-i\text{$\tau_{1}$}(\Omega-\text{$\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(2)}$})}-e^{-i\text{$T$}(\Omega-\text{$\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(2)}$})}\right)e^{i(\text{$\tau_{1}$}\text{$\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(1)}$}-\text{$T$}\text{$\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(2)}$})}}{(\text{$\Delta\omega_{\textrm{iso}}^{(2)}$}-\Omega)}

(135)

Notice, that only the phase factor of the second term
a^{(2)}_{+}(\Omega)
depends on the first part of the irradiation, but not its absolute value.
Similar to the case of a single chemical shift we find

\displaystyle|\widehat{a}^{(1)}_{z+}(\Delta\omega^{(1)}_{\textrm{iso}})|

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{1}}{2\pi T}

(136)

\displaystyle|\widehat{a}^{(2)}_{z+}(\Delta\omega^{(2)}_{\textrm{iso}})|

\displaystyle=\frac{T-\tau_{1}}{2\pi T}

(137)

which approximates well the height of the two highest maxima of
|\widehat{a}_{z+}(\Omega)|
, becoming more accurate the bigger
|\Delta\omega^{(2)}_{\textrm{iso}}-\Delta\omega^{(1)}_{\textrm{iso}}|
, because the overlap between
a^{(1)}(\Omega)
and
a^{(2)}(\Omega)
decreases.
We can generalize this findings to
N
chemical shifts as

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(t)=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\Delta\omega^{(1)}_{\textrm{iso}}\hskip 23.99997pt0\leq t\leq\tau_{1}\\
&\Delta\omega^{(2)}_{\textrm{iso}}\hskip 20.00003pt\tau_{1}<t\leq\tau_{2}\\[-4.30554pt]
&\hskip 10.00002pt\mathmakebox[\widthof{{}\Omega{}}][c]{\vdots}\hskip 50.00008pt\vdots\\[-2.15277pt]
&\Delta\omega^{(n)}_{\textrm{iso}}\hskip 8.00003pt\tau_{n-1}<t\leq\tau_{n}\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(138)

leading to

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{z+}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\pi T}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\left(e^{-i\text{$\tau_{n-1}$}(\Omega-\text{$\omega_{n}$})}-e^{-i\tau_{n}(\Omega-\text{$\omega_{n}$})}\right)}{(\omega_{n}-\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad e^{i(\tau_{1}\omega_{1}+\tau_{2}\omega_{2}+\cdots+\tau_{n-2}\omega_{n-2}+\tau_{n-1}(\omega_{n-1}-\omega_{n}))}

(139)

where we set
\tau_{N}=T
and
\tau_{0}=0=\omega_{0}
.
Like before, the maxima of the absolute value of each term is given by

\displaystyle|\widehat{a}^{(n)}_{z+}(\Delta\omega^{(n)}_{\textrm{iso}})|

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n}}{2\pi T}

(140)

It might be possible to construct an arbitrary function from general stepwise function by taking the limits carefully.
The same limit can be applied directly on Eq. (
139
), if the integral in Eq. (
91

Bartle 2014
and hence we can change the order of the limit and the intergral operation.

IV
Conclusion

We presented a generalization of operator-based Floquet theory to non-periodic Hamiltonians.
Instead of a discrete frequency space, which is sufficient for the description of periodic Hamiltonians as in the standard Floquet approach, we utilized a continuous frequency space allowing non-periodic Hamiltonians with finite duration.
In contrast to periodic modulations, modulation with finite duration led to resonance conditions that are not infinitely sharp, enabling the description of non-resonant behaviors within the same framework, which is very cumbersome in standard Floquet theory.
Since the framework is not restricted to periodic modulations, it can be use in combination with any interaction-frame transformation. This might be useful to analyze the effect of specific terms in the Hamiltonian by choosing an appropriate interaction frame, or simplifying the description by incorporating all single-spin modulations in the interaction frame.
Despite the finite duration of the modulations, we can still utilize FFT for computationally efficient calculations and express the effect of the repetition of a pulse scheme by a simple quantity.
We saw that the pulse parameter and the interaction-frame trajectory, which encodes the modulation of the spin system, can be mapped to each other bijectively, enabling reverse engineering pulse schemes from the effective Hamiltonian to a certain extent.
Finally we applied the formalism to a PDSD-based sequence called AM-MIRROR where we could tailor desired zero-quantum side-bands using the mentioned bijective mapping, without any need of numerical optimization.
This framework was developed to describe and design solid state NMR experiments under MAS, but is not restricted to it. It can be used to describe experiments of other magnetic resonance technologies such as DNP and EPR.

VI
Derivation of Van Vleck perturbation theory on a
continuous frequency space

In the following we apply Van Vleck perturbation theory on the single-mode Floquet Hamiltonian.
As usual we split the Hamiltonian into two parts

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{F}={\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}+\varepsilon\,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(1)}

(146)

Next we apply a unitary transformation called van Vleck transformation on Eq. (
146
) which is defined as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{F}=\textrm{U}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}\,\textrm{U}^{\dagger}={\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}+W_{F}

(147)

where we choose
\textrm{U}=e^{S}
such that
[\Lambda_{F},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]=0
. This commutation relation is fulfilled if
[\textrm{U},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]=0
.
We define the nested commutator as
[S,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]_{m}=[S,[S,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]_{m-1}]
with
[S,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]_{0}={\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}
. In this notation the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula takes the form

\displaystyle\Lambda_{F}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{[S,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}]_{m}}{m!}={\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{F}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\Lambda_{F}^{(n)}

(148)

where we identify

\displaystyle W_{F}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\Lambda_{F}^{(n)}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{[S,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}]_{m}}{m!}

(149)

This is a important equation, because it connects the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}
and the operator
S
with the perturbation series. We now proceed with
inserting the expansion

\displaystyle S=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)}

(150)

as well as Eq. (
146
) in Eq. (
149
), which leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\Lambda_{F}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}+\varepsilon\,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(1)}]_{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]_{m}+[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},\varepsilon\,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(1)}]_{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]_{m}}{m!}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},\varepsilon\,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(1)}]_{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}[S^{(l)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]_{m}}{m!}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},\varepsilon\,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(1)}]_{m}}{m!}

(151)

As a result, we arrive at the equation

\displaystyle[S^{(l)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]=\Lambda_{F}^{(l)}-\Phi^{(l)}_{F}

(152)

with

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{j}\Phi^{(j)}_{F}=\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]_{m}}{m!}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},\varepsilon\,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(1)}]_{m}}{m!}

(153)

Utilizing mathematical induction over
m
we can prove following identities

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},\varepsilon{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(1)}]_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{n_{m}=1}^{\infty}\,\varepsilon^{1+n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{m}}[S^{(n_{1})},[S^{(n_{2})},\cdots[S^{(n_{m})},{\mathcal{H}}^{(1)}_{F}]\cdots]]

(154)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}S^{(l)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{3}=1}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{n_{m}=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{m}}[S^{(n_{1})},[S^{(n_{2})},\cdots[S^{(n_{m})},{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{F}]\cdots]]

(155)

which lead to a more explicit form of
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{j}\Phi^{(j)}_{F}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{j}\Phi^{(j)}_{F}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{n_{m}=1}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon^{n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{m}}}{m!}[S^{(n_{1})},[S^{(n_{2})},\cdots[S^{(n_{m})},{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{F}]\cdots]]

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{n_{m}=1}^{\infty}\,\frac{\varepsilon^{1+n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{m}}}{m!}[S^{(n_{1})},[S^{(n_{2})},\cdots[S^{(n_{m})},{\mathcal{H}}^{(1)}_{F}]\cdots]]

(156)

Although this expression is quite complicated, it allows relatively easy and efficient calculation of
\Phi^{(l)}_{F}
.
To find a solution of Eq. (
152
) for
S^{(l)}
we
adapt the approach of Primas
Primas 1961
;
Primas 1963
and use projection operator defined as

\displaystyle\Pi(X)=\int P(u)XP(u)\,\textrm{d}u

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{F}=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\kappa(u)P(u)\,\textrm{d}u

(157)

By applying the projection operator
\Pi(X)
on Eq. (
152
) we obtain

\displaystyle\Pi([S^{(l)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}])=\Pi(\Lambda_{F}^{(l)}-\Phi^{(l)}_{F})

(158)

Since
[\Lambda_{F},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]=0
, hence
[\textrm{U},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]=0
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\Pi([S^{(l)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}])=\Pi(\Lambda_{F}^{(l)}-\Phi^{(l)}_{F})=\Lambda_{F}^{(l)}-\Pi(\Phi^{(l)}_{F})\implies\Lambda_{F}^{(l)}=\Pi(\Phi^{(l)}_{F})

(159)

By introducing the commutation operator

\displaystyle\Gamma_{F}(X)=[X,{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}],\hskip 28.45274pt\Gamma_{F}^{-1}(X)=\int\int\frac{P(u)XP(v)}{\kappa(u)-\kappa(v)}\,\textrm{d}u\,\textrm{d}v

(160)

we can rewrite Eq. (
152

\displaystyle\Gamma_{F}(S^{(l)})=\Pi(\Phi^{(l)}_{F})-\Phi^{(l)}_{F}

(161)

and finally arrive at the formal solution

\displaystyle S^{(l)}=\Gamma_{F}^{-1}(\Pi(\Phi^{(l)}_{F})-\Phi^{(l)}_{F})

(162)

The solution has the same form as found by Primas

Primas 1961
;
Primas 1963
, but with differ in the operators
\Pi(X)
and
\Gamma_{F}(X)
given in Eq.(
157
) and Eq. (
160
), respectively. Ultimately we have all the ingredients to derive each term of the perturbation series.

VI.1
Equivalence of the representations of the Floquet Hamiltonian
(Eq. (
23
) and Eq. (
25
) of the main text)

The following shows that Eq. (
23
) and Eq. (
25
) of the main text is equivalent:

\displaystyle\bra{\chi,\mu}\mathcal{H}\ket{\xi,\nu}

\displaystyle=\bra{\chi,\mu}\int D(\Omega)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\,\textrm{d}\Omega+\hat{\Omega}\otimes\mathbf{1}\,\ket{\xi,\nu}

\displaystyle=\bra{\chi,\mu}\int D(\Omega)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\ket{\xi,\nu}\textrm{d}\Omega+\bra{\chi,\mu}\hat{\Omega}\otimes\mathbf{1}\,\ket{\xi,\nu}

\displaystyle=\int\bra{\chi}D(\Omega)\ket{\xi}\otimes\bra{\mu}\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\ket{\nu}\textrm{d}\Omega+\bra{\chi}\hat{\Omega}\ket{\xi}\otimes\bra{\mu}\mathbf{1}\ket{\nu}

\displaystyle=\int\braket{\chi|\xi+\Omega}\otimes\bra{\mu}\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\ket{\nu}\textrm{d}\Omega+\xi\braket{\chi|\xi}\otimes\braket{\mu|\nu}

\displaystyle=\int\delta(\chi-\xi-\Omega)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)\,\textrm{d}\Omega+\xi\,\delta(\chi-\xi)\otimes\delta_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}_{\mu\nu}(\chi-\xi)+\xi\,\delta(\chi-\xi)\otimes\delta_{\mu\nu}

(163)

VI.2
Derivation of the first and second-order effective Hamiltonian for a single-mode problem

Next we are going to use the previous results to calculate the first and second-order effective Hamiltonian. For the sake of simplicity, we just present the single mode case, because it already captures the whole procedure. A generalization to the
n
-modal case in retrospect is straight forward, therefore we will just state the results afterwards. The goal is to derive the first three terms of the perturbation series, i.e.
\bra{\Omega}{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{F}+\Lambda^{(1)}_{F}+\Lambda^{(2)}_{F}\ket{\Omega^{\prime}}
. In essence, the only equations we need are Eq. (
156
) (or Eq.(
153
)), Eq. (
159
) and Eq. (
162
). For the single mode case the Hamiltonians are

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{F}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Omega}

(164)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{(1)}_{F}

\displaystyle=\int\widehat{D}(\Omega)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\,\textrm{d}\Omega=\Phi^{(1)}_{F}

(165)

In the subsequent derivation the projection operator will be often applied on the translation operator, which results in a delta distribution

\displaystyle\Pi(\widehat{D}(\Omega))=\delta(\Omega)

(166)

The projection of
\Phi^{(1)}_{F}
simply leads to

\displaystyle\Lambda_{F}^{(1)}=\Pi\left(\Phi^{(1)}_{F}\right)=\Pi\left(\int\widehat{D}(\Omega)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\,\textrm{d}\Omega\right)=\int\delta(\Omega)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\,\textrm{d}\Omega=\widehat{D}(0)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(0)

(167)

Inserting it into the Eq. (
162
) gives

\displaystyle S^{(1)}=\Gamma^{-1}\left(\Pi\left(\Phi^{(1)}_{F}\right)-\Phi^{(1)}_{F}\right)=\Gamma^{-1}\left(\widehat{D}(0)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(0)-\int\widehat{D}(\Omega)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\,\textrm{d}\Omega\right)=-\,PV\int\frac{\widehat{D}(\Omega)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\,}{\Omega}\,\textrm{d}\Omega

(168)

Notice that we use Cauchy principal value (PV) for the regularization of the integral defined as

\displaystyle PV\int_{a}^{c}f(x)=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[\int_{a}^{b-\epsilon}f(x)\,dx+\int_{b+\epsilon}^{c}f(x)\,dx\right]

(169)

Using this regularization technique is justified, since we subtract
\widehat{D}(0)\otimes\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(0)
, which is the kernel of the integral at the critical value
\Omega=0
and therefore can be exclude it from the integration.
Equipped with the expression for
S^{(1)}
we move to the second order

\displaystyle\Phi^{(2)}_{F}

\displaystyle=[S^{(1)},{\mathcal{H}}^{(1)}_{F}]+\frac{1}{2}[S^{(1)},[S^{(1)},{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}]

(170)

\displaystyle=-\,PV\int\frac{[\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega),{\mathcal{H}}(0)]\widehat{D}(\Omega)}{\Omega}\,\textrm{d}\Omega+\frac{1}{2}\,PV\int\int\frac{[\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega),\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega^{\prime})]\widehat{D}(\Omega+\Omega^{\prime})}{\Omega}\,\textrm{d}\Omega\,\textrm{d}\Omega^{\prime}

(171)

which results in

\displaystyle\Lambda_{F}^{(2)}=\Pi\left(\Phi^{(2)}_{F}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\,PV\int\frac{[\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega),\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(-\Omega)]\widehat{D}(0)}{\Omega}\,\textrm{d}\Omega

(172)

Finally we express the Hamiltonian in the product basis

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(0)}+\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(1)}+\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(2)}=\bra{\Omega,\mu}{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}+\Lambda^{(1)}_{F}+\Lambda^{(2)}_{F}\ket{\Omega^{\prime},\nu}

(173)

with

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(0)}=\widehat{\Omega}\hskip 28.45274pt\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(1)}=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega)\,\textrm{d}\Omega\hskip 28.45274pt\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2}\,PV\int\int\frac{[\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega),\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(-\Omega)]}{\Omega}\textrm{d}\Omega

(174)

As expected this results are similar to the corresponding equations known from Floquet theory. The main difference is that we have an integral and a translation operator instead of a sum and a ladder operator.

One of the main differences in the higher modal cases is the appearance of resonance conditions between the modes. For the n-modal case the resonance conditions are

\displaystyle\Omega^{(0)}_{1}+\Omega^{(0)}_{2}+\cdots+\Omega^{(0)}_{n}=0

(175)

The derivation of the effective Hamiltonians is quite similar and leads to the first and second-order effective Hamiltonian, for the bimodal case

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})\,\textrm{d}\Omega_{1}\,\textrm{d}\Omega_{2}

(176)

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\int\textrm{d}\Omega_{1}\int\textrm{d}\Omega_{1}^{(0)}\int\textrm{d}\Omega_{2}\int\textrm{d}\Omega_{2}^{(0)}\frac{[\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}),\widehat{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Omega_{1}^{(0)}-\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}^{(0)}-\Omega_{2})]}{\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{2}}

(177)

VII.2
The frequency-domain interaction-frame trajectory for a cyclic
pulse scheme

The interaction-frame trajectory of a cyclic pulse scheme with an
basic element of duration
\tau
and an overall duration
N\tau
can be
written as

\displaystyle a_{\mu\nu}(t)=\begin{cases}a_{\mu\nu}(t)&0\leq t\leq\tau\\
\sum_{\chi}a_{\mu\chi}(t-\tau)a_{\chi\nu}(\tau)&\tau\leq t\leq 2\tau\\
\sum_{\chi}a_{\mu\chi}(t-2\tau)a_{\chi\nu}(2\tau)&2\tau\leq t\leq 3\tau\\
\hskip 56.9055pt\vdots&\quad\quad\vdots\\
\sum_{\chi}a_{\mu\chi}(t-(N-1)\tau)a_{\chi\nu}((N-1)\tau)&(N-1)\tau\leq t\leq N\tau\end{cases}

(180)

Fourier transformation leads to

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi N\tau}\biggl(\sum_{\chi}\int_{0}^{\tau}a_{\mu\nu}(t)e^{-i\Omega t}dt+\int_{\tau}^{2\tau}a_{\mu\chi}(t-\tau)a_{\chi\nu}(\tau)e^{-i\Omega t}dt+\int_{2\tau}^{3\tau}a_{\mu\chi}(t-2\tau)a_{\chi\nu}(2\tau)e^{-i\Omega t}dt

(181)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\cdots+\int_{(N-1)\tau}^{N\tau}a_{\mu\chi}(t-(N-1)\tau)a_{\chi\nu}((N-1)\tau)e^{-i\Omega t}dt\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi N\tau}\sum_{\chi}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int_{(n-1)\tau}^{n\tau}a_{\mu\chi}(t-(n-1)\tau)a_{\chi\nu}((n-1)\tau)e^{-i\Omega t}dt

(182)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi N\tau}\sum_{\chi}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{-i\Omega(n-1)\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}a_{\mu\chi}(t^{\prime})a_{\chi\nu}((n-1)\tau)e^{-i\Omega t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime}

(183)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi N\tau}\sum_{\chi}\int_{0}^{\tau}a_{\mu\chi}(t^{\prime})e^{-i\Omega t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-i\Omega n\tau}a_{\chi\nu}(n\tau)

(184)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\chi}\bar{a}_{\mu\chi}(\Omega)\,g^{(N)}_{\chi\nu}(\Omega)

(185)

and therefore

\displaystyle g^{(N)}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-in\Omega\tau}a_{\mu\nu}(n\tau)

(186)

Notice that
a_{\mu\nu}(n\tau)=[\mathbf{a}^{n}(\tau)]_{\mu\nu}
where

\mathbf{a}(\tau)
is the matrix with the elements

a_{\mu\nu}(\tau)
.
Furthermore we have

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}\int\limits_{0}^{\tau}{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)e^{-i\Omega t}dt

(187)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\{\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)\Pi\left(t/\tau-1/2\right)\}

(188)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\{\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)\}*\mathcal{F}\left\{\Pi(t/\tau-1/2)\right\}

(189)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(t)\}=\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}{a}_{\mu\nu}(t\,\textrm{mod}\tau)e^{-i\Omega t}dt

(190)

Notice that
\widetilde{a}_{\mu\nu}(\Omega)
is just calculated using FFT.
As described in the main text, the special case of a cyclic
interaction-frame trajectory occurs when
\mathbf{a}(\tau)=\mathbf{1}
.
