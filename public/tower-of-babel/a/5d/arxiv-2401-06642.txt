Elliptic problems with superlinear convection terms
Lucio Boccardo, Stefano Buccheri, G. Rita Cirmi

Abstract
. In this manuscript we deal with elliptic equations with superlinear first order terms in divergence form of the following type − div ( M ( x ) ∇ u ) = − div ( h ( u ) E ( x ) ) + f ( x ) , -{\rm div}(M(x)\nabla u)=-{\rm div}(h(u)E(x))+f(x), where M M is a bounded elliptic matrix, the vector field E E and the function f f belong to suitable Lebesgue spaces, and the function s → h ⁡ ( s ) s\to h(s) features a superlinear growth at infinity. We provide some existence and non existence results for solutions to the associated Dirichlet problem and a comparison principle.



1.
Introduction and main results

The aim of the present work is to provide some existence and uniqueness results for solutions to the following nonlinear problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(M(x)\nabla u)=-{\rm div}(h(u)E(x))+f(x)\qquad&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
u(x)=0&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Throughout the paper, we assume that
\Omega
is a bounded, open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
N>2
,
M(x)
is a measurable matrix such that

(1.2)

\alpha|\xi|^{2}\leq M(x)\xi\cdot\xi\leq\beta|\xi|^{2},\quad\mbox{a.e.}\;x\in\Omega,\quad\forall\;\;\xi\in I\!\!R^{N},

with
\alpha,\beta>0
. The vector field
E(x)
and the function
f(x)
belong to suitable Lebesgue spaces and the real function
s\to h(s)
satisfies

(1.3)

h\in W^{1,\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}),\ \ \ h(0)=0,\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ \lim_{|s|\to\infty}\frac{|h(s)|}{|s|}=+\infty.

The first two assumptions on
h
are needed for the validity of the comparison principle and for
0
to be the solution of the homogeneous case (
f\equiv 0
).
The main focus here is the behavior at infinity of the nonlinearity
h(s)
, in particular, its
superlinear
growth.
Examples of such
h(s)
are

(1.4)

h(s)=s\log^{\theta}(e+|s|),\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R},

and

(1.5)

h(s)=s|s|^{\theta}\ \ \ \mbox{or}\ \ \ h(s)=|s|^{1+\theta}\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R},

with
\theta>0
. Let us recall that a function
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
is a weak solution to problem (
1.1
) if
f\in L^{\frac{2N}{N+2}}(\Omega)
,
h(u)E\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{N}
, and the following identity holds

(1.6)

\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla u\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega}h(u)E(x)\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega}f(x)\varphi\quad\forall\varphi\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega).

The main difficulty in dealing with problem (
1.1
) is the loss of coercivity of the nonlinear differential operator
A(u)=-\mbox{div}\big(M(x)\nabla u-h(u)E(x)\big)
. This phenomenon is already present in the linear case
h(s)=s
and it is even stronger in our framework. For the sake of clarity take

h(s)=s\log(e+|s|),\quad\Omega=B_{1}(0),\quad E=-x,\quad\mbox{and}\quad v=1-|x|^{2}.

A slightly formal computation shows that

\langle A(tv),tv\rangle\leq t^{2}\beta\int_{\Omega}|x|^{2}-t^{2}\int_{\Omega}(1-|x|^{2})\log(e+t(1-|x|^{2}))|x|^{2}\to\,-\infty\quad\mbox{as }t\to+\infty.

Therefore, the drift term appearing in (
1.1
) has to be considered as a
reaction term
and it may represent an obstruction for the existence of solutions. Let us also explicitly point out that we will never take advance of some cancellation properties of the vector field
E
or some sign conditions when searching for a priori estimates.

Problem (
1.1
) with
h(s)=s
is classical and has already been addressed by many authors. We defer to the next section the general analysis of the bibliographic background and focus on the strategy introduced in
[
11
]
. The main tool of that paper is the following a priori decay for the measure of superlevel sets of solutions

(1.7)

|\{u>k\}|^{\frac{1}{2^{*}}}\leq\frac{C}{[\log(1+k)]}\left[\int_{\Omega}|E|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}|f|\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

which is obtained testing (
1.6
) (in the case
h(s)=s
) with

\varphi(u)=\frac{u}{|u|+1}=\int_{0}^{u}\frac{ds}{(|s|+1)^{2}}.

Combining (
1.7
) with a truncation argument, it is possible to bypass the loss of coercivity due to
-
div
(uE)
and obtain a priori estimates for
u
in
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
.

It is therefore natural to wonder if we can adapt such technique for a general nonlinearity
h(s)
. A reasonable choice for
\varphi(u)
is

(1.8)

\varphi(u)=\int_{0}^{u}\frac{ds}{(|h(s)|+1)^{2}},

and, indeed, we formally get that

(1.9)

|\{|u|>|k|\}|^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}\leq\frac{C}{H(k)^{2}}\left[\int_{\Omega}|E|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}|f|\right],

where

(1.10)

H(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{dt}{|h(t)|+1}

(see Lemma
2.3
below for the rigorous statement and proof). Obviously, formula (
1.9
) provides a decay estimate only if

(1.11)

\lim_{|s|\to\infty}|H(s)|=\infty.

Simple computation shows that such a property is satisfied for
h(s)
as in (
1.4
) while is not in case (
1.5
).
Our first result shows that (
1.11
) is a sufficient condition to guarantee existence of (bounded) solutions to problem (
1.1
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume (
1.2
), (
1.3
), (
1.11
) and take
E\in[L^{r}(\Omega)]^{N}
, with
r>N
, and
f\in L^{m}(\Omega)
, with
m>\frac{N}{2}
. Then, there exists a unique weak solution
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
to problem (
1.1
).

The interest of such a result is that, despite a slight but still superlinear growth for the function
h(s)
, problem (
1.1
) is well posed without any further assumption on the size of
E
or
f
. Let us notice that if
f\geq 0
, Theorem
1.1
is valid if
\lim_{s\to\infty}|H(s)|=+\infty
. Similarly, if
f\leq 0
, it is enough to assume
\lim_{s\to-\infty}|H(s)|=+\infty
. This follows easily by the comparison principle given in Theorem
2.2
below.

In the special case (
1.4
) we are also able to provide existence of unbounded solutions, as the following Theorem states.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume (
1.2
), take

E\in[L^{r}(\Omega)]^{N}
, with
r>N
, and

f\in L^{2_{*}}(\Omega)
, with
2_{*}=\frac{2N}{N+2}
. Then, there exists a unique weak solution to

(1.12)

\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(M(x)\nabla u)=-{\rm div}(u\log(e+|u|)E(x))+f(x)\qquad&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
u(x)=0&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Let us stress again that main point of Theorems
1.1
and
1.12
is the following: if the function
s\to h(s)
does not grow to much at infinity (a slightly superlinear growth is allowed), problem (
1.1
) is uniquely solvable
for all

E
and
f
in some suitable Lebesgue spaces, without any further condition on the size of their norms (or on the sign of the lower order term).

If we assume
h(s)=s|s|^{\theta}
, with
\theta>0
, condition (
1.11
) is not satisfied anymore, and to prove existence of solution we need a control on the size of the data, as the following Theorem states. In the following
\mathcal{S}
is the Sobolev constant of the embedding
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
in
L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)
and we use the notation
m^{**}=\frac{mN}{N-2m}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume (
1.2
), take
\theta>0
,
m\in[2N/(N+2),N/2)
, and assume moreover that

(1.13)

0<\frac{1}{N}-\frac{1}{r}=\frac{\theta}{m^{**}},\ \ \ f\in L^{m}(\Omega)\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ E\in(L^{r}(\Omega))^{N}.

If

(1.14)

\|f\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)}\|E\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{\theta}}\leq\frac{\theta}{\mathcal{S}}\left(\frac{\alpha 2^{*}}{\mathcal{S}m^{**}(1+\theta)}\right)^{1+\frac{1}{\theta}},

then there exists a unique
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
solution of

(1.15)

\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(M(x)\nabla u)=-{\rm div}(u|u|^{\theta}E(x))+f(x)\qquad&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
u(x)=0&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

The proof of Theorem
1.3
relies on the use of Schauder fixed point theorem and its more delicate step is finding a suitable invariant set for the map

L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)\ni v\mapsto S(v)=w\in L^{m^{**}}(\Omega),

where
w
is the unique weak solution of

-{\rm div}(M(x)\nabla w)=-{\rm div}(v|v|^{\theta}E(x))+f(x).

It is worthy to mention that we stated Theorem (
1.3
) with
h(s)=s|s|^{\theta}
for simplicity. An equivalent result can be obtained with
h(s)=|s|^{1+\theta}
or for a more general
h(s)
with a suitable power-type control on its growth.

To show that (
1.11
) and (
1.14
) are somehow connected and that they are natural assumptions when searching for solutions, we now provide a sharp result for positive solutions in the radially symmetric case.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let

E(|x|)=-K\frac{x}{|x|},\quad f(|x|)=\frac{{\epsilon}}{N-1}\frac{1}{|x|},\quad\Omega=B_{R},

for some
K,{\epsilon},R>0
. Then, the problem

(1.16)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=-{\rm div}(h(u)E(x))+f(x)\qquad&\text{in }B_{R}(0),\\
u(x)=0&\text{on }\partial B_{R}(0),\end{cases}

admits a positive solution
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
if and only if

(1.17)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{Kh(s)+{\epsilon}}>R.

Proposition
1.4
shows that (
1.11
) is sharp in order to have existence of bounded solutions regardless the size of the data. Moreover it provides a link between (
1.11
) and (
1.14
) when
h(s)=s^{1+\theta}
(here we deal with positive solutions). Indeed, for this concrete choice of
h
, a simple change of variable shows that (
1.17
) is equivalent to

I_{\theta}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{1+\theta}+1}>K^{\frac{1}{1+\theta}}{\epsilon}^{\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}}R.

Taking
m,r,\theta
as in (
1.13
) and writing the right hand side above as a function of
\|f\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)}
and
\|E\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}
(for the radially symmetric choice of
f
and
E
of Proposition
1.4
), it follows that

\|f\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)}\|E\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{\theta}}<cI_{\theta}^{\frac{1+\theta}{\theta}},

and we see again the same structure of assumption (
1.14
).

Condition (
1.17
) (and the proof of Proposition
1.4
) is clearly related to the following one-dimensional initial value problem

(1.18)

\begin{cases}-\bar{u}^{\prime}(r)=Kh(\bar{u}(r))+{\epsilon}\qquad&\mbox{in }(0,R),\\
u(R)=0.&\end{cases}

Notice that, if (
1.17
) does not hold, the problem above has a blow up for some point in
[0,R)
.

Quite interestingly, the test functions based approach outlined before Theorem
1.1
echoes the procedure of solving (
1.18
) by separation of variables, see Remarks
2.6
and
2.7
.

Finally, let us focus on the problem

(1.19)

\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(M(x)\nabla u)+\mu\,u=-{\rm div}(u|u|^{\theta}E(x))+f(x)\qquad&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
u(x)=0&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Beyond the interest in studying (
1.19
) due to its connection to the evolutionary problem, here we want to highlight the gain in the growth at infinity of the nonlinearity
h(s)
allowed by the presence of the zeroth order term
\mu u
. Indeed, we have the following result.

Theorem 1.5
.

Assume (
1.2
), take
\mu>0
,
\theta<1/N
,
E\in[L^{r}(\Omega)]^{N}
with
r=\frac{N}{1-\theta N}
and
f\in L^{2_{*}}(\Omega)
. Then, there exists a unique
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
weak solution of problem (
1.19
).

In other words, the operator
u\to-{\rm div}(M(x)\nabla u)+\mu u
allows for a power growth below
1+\frac{1}{N}
. We do not know if Theorem
1.5
holds when the threshold valued is reached, but a simple computation in the radial case shows that problem (
1.19
) is not well posed for general
f
and
E
if
\theta>\frac{1}{N}
(see Remark
2.9
).

The key point in the proof of the previous result is that the presence of the zeroth order term implies an a priori
L^{1}(\Omega)
-estimate for solution to (
1.19
) of the following type

\int_{\Omega}|u|\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}}{\mu},

see Lemma
2.8
below. It is worthy to notice that such estimate does not depend on the ellipticity constant of the matrix
M(x)
.

Our last result takes advantage of such
L^{1}(\Omega)
-estimate to provide a
\log
-scale improvement of Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let the assumption (
1.2
) be satisfied,
E\in[L^{N}\log^{N}(\Omega)]^{N}
and
f\in L^{2_{*}}(\Omega)
. Then there exists a unique solution of the problem

(1.20)

\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(M(x)\nabla u)+\mu\,u=-{\rm div}(u\log(e+|u|)E(x))+f(x)\qquad&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
u(x)=0&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

1.1.
Bibliographic background

From a broader perspective, problem (
1.1
) can be seen as the stationary counterpart of the following nonlinear continuity equation

(1.21)

\partial_{t}u+\mbox{div}(J)=f,\ \ \ J=-M(x)\nabla u+h(u)E(x),

where the flux
J
is made up of the diffusion term
-M(x)\nabla u
and the (possibly) nonlinear convection term
h(u)E(x)
. Such type of equation can be derived as hydrodynamic limit of a family of scaled kinetic equations
[
20
]
, is widely used in biology
[
29
]
and pedestrians dynamics
[
30
]
, and naturally arises in the mean field games theory (see for instance
[
2
,
33
]
). Clearly, if we take
h(u)\equiv u
, we recover the celebrated Fokker-Planck equation. We also mention
[
27
]
for a recent review on the related class of aggregation-diffusion equations and
[
18
]
for a glimpse in the stochastic framework.

Let us now go back to our stationary problem. Notice that the coefficients of our equation satisfy mild regularity assumptions: the matrix
M(x)
is measurable and positive definite and the vector field
E(x)
simply belongs to
L^{r}(\Omega)
. It doesn’t have any sort of differentiability nor sign condition that may help for a priori estimates. In particular we do not assume that
E=\nabla V
, for some potential
V
, so that no variational structure is available for us (in such a case (
1.21
) may be reformulated as a Wasserstein-type gradient flow).

Form our perspective the main feature of the operator

u\to-{\rm div}(M(x)\nabla u)+{\rm div}(h(u)E(x))

is the loss of coercivity due to the lower order term. For
h
with linear growth, different approaches have been developed to overcome such a difficulties. The authors of
[
5
,
21
]
apply a symmetrization procedure to deduce an explicit pointwise bound for the decreasing rearrangement of the solution. As we already mentioned, in
[
11
,
12
]
a log-type estimate allows to deduce a universal decay on the super level set of the solution. This decay, combined with a truncation argument, provides a priory estimates in
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
. We also mention
[
6
,
26
]
for estimates obtained by duality method,
[
14
,
17
,
16
,
24
,
34
]
for regularity results in different frameworks, and
[
13
,
22
,
23
]
for equation with coefficients in Marcinkiewicz spaces.

Our work follows this line of investigation with the novelty of a convection term with superlinear growth at infinity. Up to our knowledge, very little has been done in this direction (see the very recent
[
31
]
) and the slightly superlinear growth condition (
1.11
) has never been used in this context. The legitimacy of such assumption is provided by Proposition
1.4
and it represents a nice bridge between the well posedness of problem (
1.1
) and the blow-up in finite time for the ODE (
1.18
). It would be interesting to understand if Theorem
1.2
remains true for a general
h(u)
that satisfies (
1.11
).

Also the strongly superlinear case
h(s)=s|s|^{\theta}
, treated in Theorems
1.3
and
1.15
, does not seem to be addressed in the literature so far. We believe that it may be of some interest to compare problems (
1.15
) and (
1.19
) with the following one

(1.22)

\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(M(x)\nabla u)+\mu u=|\nabla u|^{q}+f(x)\qquad&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
u(x)=0&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

with
q\in(1,2]
and
f\geq 0
. The literature on (
1.22
) is huge, see for instance
[
1
,
4
,
7
,
8
,
28
,
26
,
32
]
and reference therein. One of the most interesting fact about this problem is the nontrivial structure of the set of solutions with respect to the parameter
\mu
(see
[
3
]
and the more recent papers
[
15
]
,
[
19
]
). Let us recall that, if
\mu=0
, problem (
1.22
) is solvable in
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
if and only if
f
satisfies some smallness condition, and this is in a kind of analogy with our Problem
1.15
. However, if
\mu>0
, problem (
1.22
) enjoys (for
f\in L^{m}(\Omega)
,
m>N/2
) a universal
L^{\infty}
-bound on solutions that is missing in our case when
\theta>\frac{1}{N}
, as shown in Remark
2.9
. This is somehow surprising since a term like
|\nabla u|^{2}
seems at first glace much heavier than one like
-
div
(u|u|E)
.

We close the introduction with a couple of further comments on (
1.21
). Indeed, the next natural step would be to consider the evolutionary counterpart of (
1.1
) and to address wellpodedness, local/global existence, blow-up (if any), and decay properties of the solution. In the case
h(u)=u
such issues are treated for instance in
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
and
[
23
]
. For the special choice
h(u)=u|u|^{\theta}
a preliminary study is contained in [].

2.
Proof of the results

Let us start with some notation and preliminary material that we shall use in what follows. Many of our results are based on the approximation of (
1.1
) with problems with
trouncated
right hand side (Theorem
1.3
will be proved with a fixed point argument instead). To be more explicit, let us recall the following definitions

T_{k}(s)=\max\{-k,\min\{s,k\}\},\ \ \ G_{k}(s)=s-T_{k}(s),\quad\mbox{ for }k>0.

Using Schauder fixed point theorem, we can prove that, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
u_{n}\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
which solves

(2.1)

\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla u_{n}\nabla v+\int_{\Omega}\mu u_{n}v=\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n})E_{n}(x)\nabla v+\int_{\Omega}f_{n}\,v,\quad\forall\;v\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega),

where
h_{n}(s)=T_{n}(h(s))
,
f_{n}=T_{n}(f)
, and, with a slight abuse of notation,
E_{n}
is the vector field with components
(E_{n})^{i}=T_{n}((E)^{i})
, with
i=1,\cdots,N
.

Moreover, by the classical Stampacchia’s regularity result (see
[
35
]
), we have that
u_{n}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
(notice that for any fixed
n
the term
h_{n}(u_{n})E_{n}(x)
is bounded).

The main difficulty here is to achieve a priori estimates for the sequence
\{u_{n}\}
in some suitable space, and this is done following different strategies depending on the choice of
h(s)
and on the presence of the zeroth lower order term. Once these estimates are obtained, the passage to the limit in (
2.1
) always follows the same procedure, that we provide in the following Lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume (
1.2
)-(
1.3
),
\mu\geq 0
, that
E\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{N}
,
f\in L^{\frac{2N}{N+2}}(\Omega)
, and that

\|u_{n}\|_{W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)}+\|h(u_{n})E\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{1}.

Theerefore, up to a subsequence,
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
in
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
and
u
solves

\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla u\nabla v+\int_{\Omega}\mu uv=\int_{\Omega}h(u)E(x)\nabla v+\int_{\Omega}fv\quad\forall\;v\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega).

Proof.

Since
\{u_{n}\}
is bounded in
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
, we deduce that, up to a subsequence,
u_{n}\to u
weakly in
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
, strongly in
L^{q}(\Omega)
with
q<2^{*}
, and
a.e
in
\Omega
. This is enough to pass to the limit in the first, in the second, and in the fourth integral of (
2.1
). To deal with the third one, notice that, for any measurable
\Sigma\subset\Omega
, we have that

\int_{\Sigma}|h_{n}(u_{n})E_{n}(x)\nabla v|\leq\bigg(\int_{\Omega}|h(u_{n})E|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla v|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C_{1}\bigg(\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla v|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

This amounts to say that, for any given
v\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
, the term
\{h_{n}(u_{n})E_{n}\nabla v\}
is equi-integrable. Since
u_{n}\to u
converges pointwise, we can use Vitali Theorem to conclude that also the third term in (
2.1
) passes to the limit as
n\to\infty
.
∎

The next result provides us a comparison principle.

Theorem 2.2
.

Under conditions (
1.2
) and (
1.3
), let us consider
\mu\geq 0
,
f\in L^{\frac{2N}{N+2}}(\Omega)
,
E\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{N}
, and two functions
v,w\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that

\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla v\nabla\phi+\int_{\Omega}\mu v\phi\leq\int_{\Omega}h(v)E(x)\nabla\phi+\int_{\Omega}f\phi\quad\forall\;v\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega),

and

\int_{\Omega}M(x)\nabla w\nabla\phi+\int_{\Omega}\mu w\phi\geq\int_{\Omega}h(w)E(x)\nabla\phi+\int_{\Omega}f\phi\quad\forall\;v\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega),

for any
0\leq\phi\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Therefore
v\leq w

a.e.
in
\Omega
.

Moreover, if
v,w
are in
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
(possibly unbounded) and we additionally assume that, for
\theta>0
,

|h^{\prime}(s)|\leq c(|s|^{\theta}+1)\,a.e.\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad|v|^{\theta}|E|+|w|^{\theta}|E|\in L^{2}(\Omega),

we can again conclude that
v\leq w

a.e.
in
\Omega
.

Proof.

We shall prove that
(v-w)_{+}\equiv 0
a.e. in
\Omega
. To this aim, let us use
T_{{\epsilon}}(v-w)_{+}
as a test function in the inequalities satisfied by
v
and
w
. Taking the difference, we obtain

\begin{split}\alpha\int_{\Omega}|\nabla T_{{\epsilon}}(v-w)_{+}|^{2}\leq&\int_{0<v-w<{\epsilon}}\big(h(v)-h(w)\big)E(x)\nabla T_{{\epsilon}}(v-w)_{+}\\
\leq&\frac{1}{2\alpha}\int_{\{0<v-w<{\epsilon}\}}\big|h(v)-h(w)\big|^{2}|E(x)|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla T_{{\epsilon}}(v-w)_{+}|^{2}.\end{split}

Setting
z:=(v-w)_{+}
and
using Poincaré’s inequality with its best constant
\lambda_{1}
(as in Theorem 6.1 of
[
11
]
), we deduce that

\lambda_{1}{\epsilon}^{2}|\{z>k\}|\leq\lambda_{1}\int_{\Omega}|T_{{\epsilon}}(z)|^{2}\leq C\int_{\{0<z<{\epsilon}\}}\big|h(v)-h(w)\big|^{2}|E(x)|^{2}

for any
k\geq{\epsilon}
. In other words

(2.2)

|\{z>k\}|\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\{0<z<{\epsilon}\}}\big|h(v)-h(w)\big|^{2}|E(x)|^{2}\quad\mbox{for any }{\epsilon}\leq k.

If
v,w\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, let us set
|v|+|w|\leq M
and use the assumption
h\in W^{1,\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R})
to deduce that

|\{z>k\}|\leq CL_{M}^{2}\int_{\{0<z<{\epsilon}\}}|E(x)|^{2}\quad\mbox{for any }{\epsilon}\leq k,

for a suitable
L_{M}
independent on
\epsilon
. Since the measure of the set
\{0<z<{\epsilon}\}
goes to zero as
{\epsilon}\to 0
, we infer that

(2.3)

|\{z>k\}|=0\quad\mbox{for any }k\geq 0,

and we are done.

On the other hand, if
|h^{\prime}(s)|\leq c(|s|^{\theta}+1)
, we have that

|h(v)-h(w)|\leq c|v-w|\int_{0}^{1}\big(|w+t(v-w)|^{\theta}+1\big)dt\leq\tilde{c}|v-w|(|v|^{\theta}+|w|^{\theta}+1),

and (
2.2
) becomes

|\{z>k\}|\leq C\int_{\{0<z<{\epsilon}\}}(|v|^{2\theta}+|w|^{2\theta}+1)|E(x)|^{2}\quad\mbox{for any }{\epsilon}\leq k.

Therefore, thanks to the assumptions and the absolute continuity of the integral, we can take again the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
and obtain again (
2.3
).
∎

2.1.
Slightly superlinear case

The first result that we prove concerns the bound of the sequence of approximating solutions
\{u_{n}\}
in
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Let us recall that

\varphi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{ds}{(|h(s)|+1)^{2}},\quad H(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{ds}{|h(s)|+1}.

We notice that
\varphi(u_{n})
belongs to
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
, since the real valued function
\varphi(t)
is Lipschitz. Moreover, if the assumption (
1.3
) holds, there exists
C_{1}>0
such that
|\varphi(s)|\leq C_{1}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume (
1.2
), (
1.3
), and take
\mu\geq 0
,
E\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{N}
, and
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
. Then there exists
C>0
such that

(2.4)

|\{|u_{n}|>|k|\}|^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}\leq\frac{C}{|H(k)|^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big(|E|^{2}+|f|\big),\quad\forall\ n\in\mathbb{N},\,k\in\mathbb{R}

Proof.

Consider the function
v=G_{\varphi(k)}(\varphi(u_{n}^{+}))\geq 0
(where
k\geq 0
and
u_{n}^{+}
is the positive part of
u_{n}
) and notice that by construction

(2.5)

\begin{split}\left\{x\in\Omega\,:v>0\right\}=&\left\{x\in\Omega\,:\varphi(u_{n})>\varphi(k)\right\}=\left\{x\in\Omega\,:u_{n}>k\right\}\\
=&\left\{x\in\Omega\,:H(u_{n})>H(k)\right\}\end{split}

Taking
v
as a test function in (
2.1
), dropping the positive zeroth order term, and using the ellipticity of the matrix
M(x)
, we get

\begin{split}\alpha\int_{\{k<u_{n}^{+}\}}\frac{|\nabla u_{n}^{+}|^{2}}{(|h(u_{n}^{+})|+1)^{2}}\leq&\int_{\{k<u_{n}^{+}\}}|h(u_{n}^{+})||E|\frac{|\nabla u_{n}^{+}|}{(|h(u_{n}^{+})|+1)^{2}}+\int_{\{k<u_{n}^{+}\}}|f|\varphi(u_{n}^{+})\\
\leq&\frac{1}{2\alpha}\int_{\{k<u_{n}^{+}\}}|E(x)|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\{k<u_{n}^{+}\}}\frac{|\nabla u_{n}^{+}|^{2}}{(|h(u_{n}^{+})|+1)^{2}}+C_{1}\int_{\{k<u_{n}^{+}\}}|f(x)|,\end{split}

where we have used Young’s inequality and that
|\varphi(u_{n}^{+})|\leq C_{1}
. Therefore, recalling property (
2.5
) and that
H^{\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{(|h(s)|+1)^{2}}
, it follows that

(2.6)

\begin{split}\frac{\alpha}{2\mathcal{S}^{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega}|G_{H(k)}(H(u_{n}^{+}))|^{2^{*}}\right)^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}\leq&\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\{k<u_{n}^{+}\}}|\nabla H(u_{n}^{+})|^{2}\\
\leq&\frac{1}{2\alpha}\int_{\{k<u_{n}^{+}\}}|E(x)|^{2}+C_{1}\int_{\{k<u_{n}^{+}\}}|f(x)|.\end{split}

Taking
k=0
, we deduce that for any
t>0

\begin{split}|H(t)|^{2}|\{u_{n}^{+}>t\}|^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}\leq&\left(\int_{\{u_{n}^{+}\geq t\}}|H(u_{n}^{+})|^{2^{*}}\right)^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}\\
\leq&\left(\int_{\Omega}|H(u_{n}^{+})|^{2^{*}}\right)^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}\leq C\int_{\Omega}\left(|E(x)|^{2}+|f(x)|\right),\end{split}

where we have used Chebyshev’s inequality.
Repeating the same argument with
k\geq 0
and
v=G_{\varphi(k)}(\varphi(u_{n}^{-}))\leq 0
(we use the notation
u=u^{+}+u^{-}
), one gets

(2.7)

\frac{\alpha}{2\mathcal{S}^{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega}|G_{H(k)}(H(u_{n}^{-}))|^{2^{*}}\right)^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}\int_{\{u_{n}^{-}<-k\}}|E(x)|^{2}+C_{1}\int_{\{u_{n}^{-}<-k\}}|f(x)|,

and, for any
t>0
,

|H(-t)|^{2}|\{u_{n}^{-}<-t\}|^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}\leq C\int_{\Omega}\left(|E(x)|^{2}+|f(x)|\right).

Putting together the estimates for
u_{n}^{+}
and
u_{n}^{-}
we obtain the desired result.
∎

Let us provide now the proof of existence of bounded solutions.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

In order to prove the bound in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
for the sequence
\{u_{n}\}
, let us set
\eta=H(k)
and
z_{n}=H(u_{n}^{+})
. Then, estimate (
2.6
) becomes

\left(\int_{\Omega}|G_{\eta}(z_{n})|^{2^{*}}\right)^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}}\leq C\int_{\eta\leq|z_{n}|}\left(|E(x)|^{2}+|f(x)|\right).

Since
|E|^{2}+|f|
belongs to
L^{m}(\Omega)
with
m>\frac{N}{2}
, it follows by classical results (see
[
35
]
) that

\|z_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=\|H(u_{n}^{+})\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C,

for some
C>0
independent on
n
.
Taking advantage of assumption (
1.11
), we deduce that

\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq H^{-1}(C).

Similarly, estimate (
2.7
) implies that
\|u_{n}^{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C^{\prime}
, so that
\{u_{n}\}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

As a consequence, choosing
v=u_{n}
in (
2.1
), it easily follows that
\{u_{n}\}
is bounded also in
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Therefore, we can apply Lemma
2.1
to conclude that there exists
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
solution to (
1.1
). The uniqueness follows from Theorem
2.2
.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Notice that the estimate (
2.4
) is true for all nonlinearity
h(u)
. However, as we have just seen in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, it is useful only if
H(k)\to\infty
as
k
diverges. Another consequence of (
1.11
) is that for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
k_{\epsilon}
such that
\forall\,k>k_{\epsilon}
it follows that

(2.8)

|\{x\in\Omega:\quad|u_{n}(x)|>k\}|\leq\epsilon,\quad\text{uniformly \, w.r. to }n\in\mathbb{N}.

This estimate is crucial in order to prove the boundedness of
\{u_{n}\}
in the energy space, at least for some choices of the nonlinearity
h(u)
.

Les us provide now our first result in the special case
h(s)=s\log(e+|s|)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The first step is to show that, for any
a\geq 1
, there exists

C=C(a,f,E)
such that

(2.9)

\|\log^{a}(e+|u_{n}|)\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}\leq C.

Let us set

v=\int_{0}^{u_{n}}\frac{\log^{2(a-1)}(e+|s|)}{(e+|s|)^{2}}ds,

and notice that
|v|\leq C_{a}
, for some positive constant
C_{a}
.
Taking
v
as a test function in (
2.1
)
and using Young’s inequality, we get

\begin{split}\alpha\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}\frac{\log^{2(a-1)}(e+|u_{n}|)}{(e+|u_{n}|)^{2}}\leq&\int_{\Omega}|E(x)||\nabla u_{n}|\frac{\log^{2a-1}(e+|u_{n}|)}{(e+|u_{n}|)^{2}}+C_{a}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
\leq&\frac{1}{2\alpha}\int_{\Omega}\log^{2a}(e+|u_{n}|)|E(x)|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}\frac{\log^{2(a-1)}(e+|u_{n}|)}{(e+|u_{n}|)^{2}}\\
+&C_{a}\int_{\Omega}|f|,\\
\end{split}

that becomes

\begin{split}\frac{\alpha}{2a^{2}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\log^{a}(e+|u_{n}|)|^{2}&=\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}\frac{\log^{2(a-1)}(e+|u_{n}|)}{(e+|u_{n}|)^{2}}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}\int_{\Omega}\log^{2a}(e+|u_{n}|)|E(x)|^{2}+C_{a}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\{|u_{n}|\geq k|\}}(\log^{a}(e+|u_{n}|)-1)^{2}|E(x)|^{2}\\
&+\frac{\log^{2a}(e+k)+2}{2\alpha}\|E\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C_{a}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

Applying Sobolev’s embedding to the function
\log^{a}(e+|u_{n}|)-1\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
and thanks to Hölder’s inequality, it follows that

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}^{-2}\|(\log^{a}(e+|u_{n}|)-1)\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2}&\leq\frac{a^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\left(\int_{\{|u_{n}|\geq k|\}}|E|^{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{N}}\|(\log^{a}(e+|u_{n}|)-1)\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2}\\
&+C_{k,a}(\|E\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}).\end{split}

Thanks to estimate (
2.8
), the fact that
E\in[L^{N}(\Omega)]^{N}
, and the uniform continuity of the integral, there exists
k
large enough such that

\frac{a^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\left(\int_{\{|u_{n}|\geq k|\}}|E|^{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{N}}\leq\frac{1}{2\mathcal{S}^{2}}\quad\forall\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

Therefore, we conclude that

\frac{1}{2\mathcal{S}^{2}}\|(\log^{a}(e+|u_{n}|)-1)\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C_{k,a}(\|E\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}),

and (
2.9
) follows.

Next, we will prove that
\left\{u_{n}\right\}
is bounded in
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Taking
u_{n}
as a test function in (
2.1
) and using Young’s inequality, we get

(2.10)

\begin{split}\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}\leq&\frac{1}{2\alpha}\int_{\{|u_{n}|>k|\}}|u_{n}|^{2}\log^{2}(e+|u_{n}|)|E|^{2}\\
+&\frac{k^{2}\log^{2}(e+k)}{2\alpha}\|E\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|f\|_{L^{2_{*}}(\Omega)}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

To deal with the first integral in the right hand side, notice that

(2.11)

\begin{split}\int_{\{|u_{n}|>k|\}}|u_{n}|^{2}\log^{2}(e+|u_{n}|)|E|^{2}\leq&\left(\int_{\{|u_{n}|>k|\}}|E|^{r}\right)^{\frac{2}{r}}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\log(e+|u_{n}|)\|_{L^{b}(\Omega)}^{2}\\
\leq&C_{1}\left(\int_{\{|u_{n}|>k|\}}|E|^{r}\right)^{\frac{2}{r}}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2}\\
\leq&C_{2}\left(\int_{\{|u_{n}|>k|\}}|E|^{r}\right)^{\frac{2}{r}}\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\end{split}

where
\frac{1}{b}=\frac{1}{N}-\frac{1}{r}
, we have used (
2.9
), with
a=\max\{{b}/{2^{*}},1\}
, and Sobolev’s inequality.
At this point, estimate (
2.8
) again assures us that there exists
k
large enough so that

C_{2}\left(\int_{\{|u_{n}|>k|\}}|E|^{r}\right)^{\frac{2}{r}}\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Going back to estimate (
2.10
), we obtain

\frac{\alpha}{4}\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{k^{2}\log^{2}(e+k)}{2\alpha}\|E\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|f\|_{L^{2_{*}}(\Omega)}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)},

which gives the boundedness of
\{u_{n}\}
in
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
. Since (
2.11
). with
k=0
, implies

\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|^{2}\log^{2}(e+|u_{n}|)|E|^{2}\leq C_{2}\left(\int_{\Omega}|E|^{r}\right)^{\frac{2}{r}}\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

we can use Lemma
2.1
to conclude that there exists a solution to (
1.12
).

As far as uniqueness is concerned, notice that in the case under consideration
h(s)=s\log(e+|s|)
and, therefore,
|h^{\prime}(s)|\leq 2\ +|s|
. Under our assumption on
E
, for any solution
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
, we have that
|u||E|\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Therefore, Theorem
2.2
assures uniqueness.

∎

2.2.
Strongly superlinear case

Let us start this section with the following auxiliary Lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
\delta,\theta>0
set
R=(\delta(\theta+1))^{-1/\theta}
and assume that

K\leq K_{\delta}=\left(\frac{1}{\delta(\theta+1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta}}\frac{\theta}{\theta+1}.

Then, if
s\in(0,R)
, it follows that

\delta s^{1+\theta}+K\leq R.

Proof.

For
s\in(0,R)
and
K\leq K_{\delta}
we have that
\delta s^{1+\theta}+K\leq\delta R^{1+\theta}+K_{\delta}
. Using the definition of
R
and
K_{\delta}
it follows that

\begin{split}\delta R^{1+\theta}+K_{\delta}=&\delta\left(\frac{1}{\delta(\theta+1)}\right)^{1+\frac{1}{\theta}}+\left(\frac{1}{\delta(\theta+1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta}}\frac{\theta}{\theta+1}\\
=&\left(\frac{1}{\delta(\theta+1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta}}\left(\frac{1}{\theta+1}+\frac{\theta}{\theta+1}\right)=R\end{split}

∎

Let us give the proof of the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Assume at first that
u_{1},u_{2}\in L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
are two solutions to (
1.15
). Since, for
i=1,2
, we have that

\int_{\Omega}|u_{i}|^{2\theta}|E|^{2}\leq\|u_{i}\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}^{2\theta}\|E\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}|\Omega|^{1-\frac{2}{r}-\frac{2\theta}{,}{m^{**}}}<\infty,

Theorem
2.2
implies that
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
must coincide, and the uniqueness in the considered class of solutions is proved.

Let us now address the proof of existence and let us divide it in two steps.

Step 1.
Take
v\in L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
and consider the following problem

(2.12)

\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(M(x)\nabla w)=-{\rm div}(v|v|^{\theta}E(x))+f(x)\qquad&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
w(x)=0&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Since
m\geq 2_{*}
, the right hand side above belongs to
W^{-1,2}(\Omega)
and the Lax-Milgram Theorem implies existence and uniqueness of a solution
w\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
. The aim of this first step is to obtain precise estimates of the
L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
norm of suitable truncations of
w
in term of the norms of
v
and
f
. For it, set
w_{k,l}=T_{k}(G_{l}(w))
and take
\frac{1}{2\gamma-1}|w_{k,l}|^{2\gamma-2}w_{k,l}
, with
\gamma=\frac{m^{**}}{2^{*}}
, as a test function in the weak formulation of (
2.12
). Recalling that
2\gamma-2>0
, we get

(2.13)

\alpha\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{k,l}|^{2}|w_{k,l}|^{2\gamma-2}\leq\int_{\Omega}|E(x)||v|^{1+\theta}|\nabla w_{k,l}||w_{k,l}|^{2\gamma-2}+\frac{1}{2\gamma-1}\int_{\Omega}|f(x)||w_{k,l}|^{2\gamma-1}.

In order to deal with the first integral in the right hand side of (
2.13
), we recall that assumption (
1.13
) implies

1=\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1+\theta}{m^{**}}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma-1}{m^{**}}.

Using Hölder’s inequality, we obtain that

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}|E(x)||v|^{1+\theta}|\nabla w_{k,l}||w_{k,l}|&{}^{2\gamma-2}\\
\leq C_{E,l}&\|v\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}^{1+\theta}\||\nabla w_{k,l}||w_{k,l}|^{\gamma-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|w_{k,l}\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}^{\gamma-1},\end{split}

where
C_{E,l}=\left(\int_{\{|w|>l\}}|E|^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}
. Moreover, recalling that
(2\gamma-1)m^{\prime}=m^{**}=2^{*}\gamma
, it follows that

\int_{\Omega}|f||w_{k,l}|^{2\gamma-1}\leq C_{f,l}\|w_{k,l}\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}^{\gamma-1}\left(\int_{\Omega}|w_{k,l}|^{2^{*}\gamma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2^{*}}}

\leq\mathcal{S}\gamma C_{f,l}\|w_{k,l}\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}^{\gamma-1}\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{k,l}|^{2}|w_{k,l}|^{2\gamma-2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
C_{f,l}=\left(\int_{\{|w|\geq l\}}|f|^{m}\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}
and we used Sobolev’s inequality in the last line. Plugging these two piece of information in (
2.13
), we get

\frac{\alpha}{\mathcal{S}\gamma}\left(\int_{\Omega}|w_{k,l}|^{m^{**}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2^{*}}}\leq\alpha\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{k,l}|^{2}|w_{k,l}|^{2\gamma-2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\leq\left[C_{E,l}\|v\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}^{1+\theta}+\frac{\mathcal{S}\gamma}{2\gamma-1}C_{f,l}\right]\left(\int_{\Omega}|w_{k,l}|^{m^{**}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{m^{**}}}.

That is

(2.14)

\|w_{k,l}\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{\mathcal{S}\gamma}{\alpha}\left[C_{E,l}\|v\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}^{1+\theta}+\frac{\mathcal{S}\gamma}{2\gamma-1}C_{f,l}\right].

Taking the limit as
k\to\infty
,
l=0
, and recalling that
\gamma\geq 1
, we conclude that

(2.15)

\|w\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{\mathcal{S}m^{**}}{\alpha 2^{*}}\|E\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}^{1+\theta}+\frac{\mathcal{S}^{2}m^{**}}{\alpha 2^{*}}\|f\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)}.

Step 2.
In this second step we shall use Schauder’s fixed point theorem to prove existence of a solution to (
1.15
). Using Lemma
2.5
with

\delta=\frac{\mathcal{S}m^{**}}{\alpha 2^{*}}\|E\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ K=\frac{\mathcal{S}^{2}m^{**}}{\alpha 2^{*}}\|f\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)},

recalling assumption (
1.14
), and thanks to estimate (
2.15
), we conclude that

\|v\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}\leq R\ \ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \ \|w\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}\leq\delta\|v\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}+K_{\delta}\leq R,

where
R=(\delta(\theta+1))^{-1/\theta}
.
Therefore, considering the map
v\to S(v)=w
that associates to any
v\in L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
the unique solution
w\in L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
of (
2.12
), we deduce that
S(B_{R})\subset B_{R}
, namely, the ball of radius
R
is invariant under the action of
S
.

Take now a sequence
\{v_{n}\}\subset L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
that strongly converges to
v
in
L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
. If
w_{n}=S(v_{n})
and
w=S(v)
, the function
z_{n}=w_{n}-w
solves

\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(M(x)\nabla z_{n})=-{\rm div}\big((v_{n}|v_{n}|^{\theta}-v|v|^{\theta})E(x)\big)\qquad&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
z_{n}(x)=0&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Following the same procedure used in Step 1 to obtain estimate (
2.15
), we deduce that

\|z_{n}\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{\mathcal{S}\gamma}{\alpha}\|E\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\|v_{n}|v_{n}|^{\theta}-v|v|^{\theta}\|_{L^{\frac{m^{**}}{1+\theta}}(\Omega)}.

Thanks to the strong convergence of
v_{n}
in
L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
, the dominated convergence theorem implies that the right hand side above goes to zero as
n\to\infty
. Therefore, the sequence
\{w_{n}\}
converges to
w
in
L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
, namely, the map
v\to S(v)
is continuous.

To conclude, we need to show that the map is also compact, namely, if
v_{n}\rightharpoonup v
in
L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
, up to a subsequence
w_{n}\to w
in
L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
. Taking
w_{n}=S(v_{n})
as a test function in the weak formulation of the problem solved by
w_{n}
, we deduce that

\begin{split}\alpha\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{n}|^{2}\leq&\int_{\Omega}|v_{n}|^{1+\theta}|E||\nabla w_{n}|+\int_{\Omega}|f||w|\\
\leq&C(\Omega)\|v_{n}\|^{\theta+1}_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}\|E\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\|\nabla w_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\mathcal{S}\|f\|_{L^{2_{*}}(\Omega)}\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\end{split}

where we have used that (recall assumption (
1.13
) and that
2\leq m^{*}
)

\frac{1+\theta}{m^{**}}+\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{m^{*}}+\frac{1}{2}\leq 1.

Therefore there exists
\zeta\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
such that, up to a subsequence,
w_{n}\to\zeta
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
a.e.
in
\Omega
. However, the weak convergence of
v_{n}
in
L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
is enough to deduce that
\zeta
coincides with
w=S(v)
.
Let us now use estimates (
2.14
) with
v_{n}
. Since
\|v_{n}\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}
is bounded, we can take the limit as
k\to\infty
to obtain that for any
l\geq 0

\|G_{l}(w_{n})\|_{L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)}\leq C\left[\left(\int_{\{|w|>l\}}|E|^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}+\left(\int_{|w_{n}|\geq l}|f|^{m}\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}\right].

We stress that the right hand side above converges to zero as
l\to\infty
, uniformly with respect to
n
, due to the strong convergence of
\{w_{n}\}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Now, for any measurable set
E\subset\Omega
, we have that

\int_{E}|w_{n}|^{m^{**}}\leq\int_{E}|T_{l}(w_{n})|^{m^{**}}+\int_{\Omega}|G_{l}(w_{n})|^{m^{**}}\leq l^{m^{**}}|E|+\omega(l),

with
\omega(l)\to 0
as
l\to\infty
. Therefore, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
l
large enough such that
\omega(l)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
. Setting
\delta=\frac{\epsilon}{2l^{m^{**}}}
we have that

\int_{E}|w_{n}|^{m^{**}}\leq\epsilon\ \ \ \forall E\ :\ |E|<\delta.

This means that the sequence is equi-integrable in
L^{m^{**}}(\Omega)
and Vitali Theorem assures us the required strong convergence.
∎

We prove now our result in the symmetric setting.

Proof of Proposition
1.4
.

Let us start assuming that (
1.16
) has a solution
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
. It follows that
u
solves

-\Delta u=Kh^{\prime}(u)\nabla u\frac{x}{|x|}+h(u)\frac{K(N-1)}{|x|}+\frac{\epsilon}{N-1}\frac{1}{|x|}

in the weak sense and, therefore, we can apply Theorems 8.8 and 8.12 of
[
25
]
(with
c=K(N-1)h^{\prime}(u)x/|x|
and
f=h(u){K}/{|x|}+{\epsilon}/{|x|(N-1)}
) to deduce that
u\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)
and that the equation above is also satisfied a.e. in
\Omega
.
Now, since Theorem
2.2
implies uniqueness,
u
has to be radial and the equation above becomes

-\frac{1}{r^{N-1}}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^{N-1}\frac{d}{dr}\bar{u}\right)=K\frac{d}{dr}h(\bar{u})+h(\bar{u})\frac{K(N-1)}{r}+\frac{\epsilon}{N-1}\frac{1}{r}.

Multiplying both side by
r^{N-1}
and integrating between
0
and
r
,
we get

-\bar{u}^{\prime}(r)=Kh(\bar{u})+{\epsilon}.

By separation of variables, it follows

\int_{\bar{u}(R)=0}^{\bar{u}(0)}\frac{ds}{Kh(s)+{\epsilon}}=R

that clearly implies (
1.17
).

On the other hand, assume that (
1.17
) holds true and let
a>0
such that

\int_{0}^{a}\frac{ds}{Kh(s)+{\epsilon}}=R

If we set
\bar{u}
to be the inverse function of
t\to\int_{t}^{a}\frac{ds}{h(s)+{\epsilon}}
, it follows, doing the previous computation backwards, that
u(x)=\bar{u}(|x|)
is the unique solution to (
1.16
).
∎

Remark 2.6

(Reaction non coercive term)
.

If we take
h(s)=s^{2}
, the ODE

-u^{\prime}(r)=-Ku^{2}+{\epsilon},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\ u(R)=0

can be solved by separation of variable

\int_{a}^{u(r)}\frac{ds}{Ks^{2}+{\epsilon}}=-r,

that explicitly provides

\frac{1}{\sqrt{K{\epsilon}}}\left[\arctan{\left(u(r)\sqrt{\frac{K}{{\epsilon}}}\right)}-\arctan{\left(a\sqrt{\frac{K}{{\epsilon}}}\right)}\right]=-r.

Imposing that
u(R)=0
we find that

a=\sqrt{\frac{{\epsilon}}{K}}\tan{\left(\sqrt{K{\epsilon}}R\right)}.

Since our solution has to be bounded and positive, we deduce that
\sqrt{K{\epsilon}}R<\frac{\pi}{2}
. Eventually we get

u(r)=\sqrt{\frac{{\epsilon}}{K}}\tan{\left(\sqrt{K{\epsilon}}(R-r)\right)}.

Remark 2.7

(Absorption coercive term)
.

It is also interesting to consider the following equation

-u^{\prime}(r)=-Ku^{2}+{\epsilon},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\ u(R)=0,

namely assuming
the good sign condition
for the divergence of
E
. In this case one expects that the nonlinear term should not affect existence of solution. Indeed one gets

\int_{a}^{u(r)}\frac{ds}{Ku^{2}-{\epsilon}}=r,

that is

-\frac{1}{\sqrt{K{\epsilon}}}\left[\arctanh{\left(u(r)\sqrt{\frac{K}{{\epsilon}}}\right)}-\arctanh{\left(a\sqrt{\frac{K}{{\epsilon}}}\right)}\right]=r.

We recall that
\arctanh
is a function defined in
(-1,1)
strictly increasing, with
\arctanh(0)=0
, and with image
(-\infty,+\infty)
. Then for any values of
K,{\epsilon},R
we find

0<a=\sqrt{\frac{{\epsilon}}{K}}\tanh\left(\sqrt{K{\epsilon}}R\right).

The solution is

u(r)=\sqrt{\frac{K}{{\epsilon}}}\tanh{\left(\sqrt{K{\epsilon}}(R-r)\right)}.

2.3.
The impact of the zero order term

In this section we deal with problem (
1.1
) with
\mu>0
. We take advantage of an
L^{1}
a priori estimate due to the presence of the zeroth lower order term.

Lemma 2.8
.

Assume (
1.2
) and the first two conditions in (
1.3
). Take
E\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{N}
,
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
,
\mu>0
, and let
u_{n}
be the weak solution of the problem (
1.1
). Therefore, we have

(2.16)

\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|\leq\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{\Omega}|f|.

Proof.

Let us take
T_{\epsilon}(u_{n})
as a test function in (
2.1
) to obtain

\alpha\int_{\Omega}|\nabla T_{\epsilon}(u_{n})|^{2}+\mu\int_{\Omega}u_{n}T_{\epsilon}u_{n})\leq h(\epsilon)\int_{\Omega}|E(x)||\nabla T_{\epsilon}(u_{n})|+\epsilon\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

Dividing everything by
\epsilon
, using Young’s inequality, and recalling that
h(0)=0
and that
h\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, we get

\begin{split}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}u_{n}T_{\epsilon}(u_{n})\leq\frac{\alpha}{2\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla T_{\epsilon}(u_{n})|^{2}+\mu\int_{\Omega}u_{n}T_{\epsilon}(u_{n})\leq L\frac{\epsilon}{2\alpha}\int_{\Omega}|E(x)|^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)},\end{split}

where
L
is the Lipschitz constant of
h(s)
for
s\in(-1,1)
. Taking the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
, we finally obtain (
2.16
).
∎

We can now prove Theorem
1.5

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Thanks to estimate (
2.16
), we can fix
k>0
, independent on
n
, such that

2^{1+\theta}\mathcal{S}\mu^{-1}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{\theta}\left(\int_{|u_{n}|\geq k}|E(x)|^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}\leq\frac{\alpha}{4}.

Taking
G_{k}(u_{n})
as a test function in (
2.1
), we get

\begin{split}\alpha\int_{\Omega}|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})|^{2}\leq&2^{1+\theta}\int_{\Omega}|G_{k}(u_{n})|^{1+\theta}|E(x)||\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})|\\
+&(2k)^{1+\theta}\int_{\Omega}|E(x)||\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})|+\int_{\Omega}|f||G_{{k}}(u_{n})|,\end{split}

where we have used that
u_{n}=T_{k}(u_{n})+G_{k}(u_{n})
and that
|a+b|^{1+\theta}\leq 2^{1+\theta}(|a|^{1+\theta}+|b|^{1+\theta})
.
To estimate the first term in the right hand side above, we take advantage of the fact that the integral is carried over super level sets of the functions
u_{n}
. More specifically, by assumption on
\theta
, using the Hölder’s inequality and Lemma (
2.8
) we have that

\begin{split}2^{1+\theta}\int_{\Omega}|G_{k}(u_{n})|^{1+\theta}|E(x)||\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})|=2^{1+\theta}\int_{|u_{n}|\geq k}|G_{k}(u_{n})||G_{k}(u_{n})|^{\theta}|E(x)||\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})|&\\
\leq 2^{1+\theta}\|G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}\|G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{\theta}\left(\int_{|u_{n}|\geq k}|E(x)|^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}\|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&\\
\leq 2^{1+\theta}\mathcal{S}\mu^{-1}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{\theta}\left(\int_{|u_{n}|\geq k}|E(x)|^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}\|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}&.\end{split}

The choice of
k
implies that

2^{1+\theta}\int_{\Omega}|G_{{k}}(u_{n})|^{1+\theta}|E(x)||\nabla G_{{k}}(u_{n})|\leq\frac{\alpha}{4}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla G_{{k}}(u_{n})|^{2}

On the other hand a simple application of Young’s inequality allows us to write

(2k)^{1+\theta}\int_{\Omega}|E(x)||\nabla G_{{k}}(u_{n})|+\int_{\Omega}|f||G_{{k}}(u_{n})|\leq C\left(k^{2(1+\theta)}\|E\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2_{*}}(\Omega)}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{4}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla G_{{k}}(u_{n})|^{2}.

Therefor we deduce that

\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla G_{{k}}(u_{n})|^{2}\leq C\left(k^{2(1+\theta)}\|E\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2_{*}}(\Omega)}^{2}\right).

To conclude the argument, let us take
T_{{k}}(u_{n})
as a test function in (
2.1
). It follows that

\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla T_{{k}}(u_{n})|^{2}\leq\frac{k^{2(1+\theta)}}{\alpha 2}\|E\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+{k}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

Putting together the estimate for
\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})
and
\nabla T_{k}(u_{n})
, we recover that
\|u_{n}\|_{W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq C^{\prime}
. Moreover, recalling that

, we have that

\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|^{2+2\theta}|E|^{2}\leq\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2\theta}\|E\|_{L^{r}(\Sigma)}^{2}.

At last, we can apply Lemma
2.1

∎

Remark 2.9
.

Let us show that if
\theta>\frac{1}{N}
there is no hope to have existence of solution to (

) for general

. Assume that
\Omega
is the ball centered in the origin with radius one,
E=-\frac{x}{|x|}
,

, and that there exists a solution

to (

Choosing, for instance,

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Taking
u_{n}
as a test function and using Young’s inequality, we get

(2.17)

\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla{u_{n}}|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|^{2}\,\log^{2}(e+|u_{n}|)\,|E|+\int_{\Omega}|f||u_{n}|

To deal with the third integral in the formula above, we split it in two terms as follows

\begin{split}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}&|u_{n}|^{2}\,\log^{2}(e+|u_{n}|)\,|E|^{2}\\
\displaystyle\leq&\rho^{2}\int_{\{|E|\leq\rho\}}|u_{n}|^{2}\,\log^{2}(e+|u_{n}|)+\int_{|E|>\rho}|u_{n}|^{2}\,\log^{2}(e+|u_{n}|)\,|E|^{2}\\
\displaystyle=&I_{1}+I_{2}\end{split}

Taking
\lambda\in(0,\frac{2^{*}-2}{2^{*}-1})
, we get

\begin{split}I_{1}\leq&\rho^{2}\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|^{2-\lambda}\,|u_{n}|^{\lambda}\log^{2}(e+|u_{n}|)\\
\leq&\rho^{2}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2-\lambda}\left(\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|^{\frac{2^{*}\lambda}{2^{*}-2+\lambda}}\log^{\frac{2^{*}2}{2^{*}-2+\lambda}}(e+|u_{n}|)\right)^{1-\frac{2-\lambda}{2^{*}}}\\
\leq&\rho^{2}C_{1}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2-\lambda}(|\Omega|+\|u_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)})^{1-\frac{2-\lambda}{2^{*}}}\leq\rho^{2}C_{2}\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2-\lambda},\end{split}

where we have used that
\frac{2^{*}\lambda}{2^{*}-2+\lambda}<1
and that
s^{\frac{2^{*}\lambda}{2^{*}-2+\lambda}}\log^{\frac{2^{*}2}{2^{*}-2+\lambda}}(e+s)\leq C(1+s)
for
s>0
and some constant
C
(that depends on
\lambda
).

To estimate
I_{2}
, we take advantage of the inequality

s\,t\leq As\,\log(e+s)+Ae^{t/A}\quad\forall s,t\geq 0,

that can easily proven showing that the the function
f(t)=\lambda s\,\log(1+s)+\lambda e^{t/\lambda}-st
is nonnegative.
Setting
s=|E|
and
\;t=\log(e+|u_{n}|)
with
A>N
, we deduce that

(2.18)

|E|\,\log(e+|u_{n}|)\leq A\,|E|\,\log(e+|E|)+A(e+|u_{n}|)^{\frac{1}{A}}.

We get

\begin{split}I_{2}\leq&2A^{2}\int_{|E|>\rho}|u_{n}|^{2}\;|E|^{2}\,\log^{2}(1+|E|)+2A^{2}\int_{|E|>\rho}|u_{n}|^{2}\,(e+|u_{n}|)^{\frac{2}{A}}\\
\leq&2A^{2}\left[\left(\int_{|E|>\rho}|E|^{N}\log^{N}(e+|E|)\right)^{\frac{2}{N}}+2A^{2}\|1+|u_{n}|\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2/A}|\{|E|>\rho\}|^{\frac{2}{N}-\frac{2}{A}}\right]\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2}\end{split}

Notice that the term in the square brackets goes to zero as

. Therefore, the contribution of
I_{2}
in estimate (
2.17
) can be absorbed into the left hand side and we obtain that

\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C_{3}\rho^{2}\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2-\lambda}+\|f\|_{L^{(2^{*})^{\prime}}(\Omega)}.

This implies that
\{u_{n}\}
is a bounded subset of
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
.

We have to show now that the sequence
\{|u_{n}|^{2}\,\log^{2}(e+|u_{n}|)\,|E|^{2}\}
bounded. To do it we use again (
2.18
), with
A>N
, to get

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|^{2}\,\log^{2}(e+|u_{n}|)\,|E|^{2}\leq 2A^{2}\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|^{2}\;|E|^{2}\,\log^{2}(e+A\,|E|)+2\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|^{2}\,(e+|u_{n}|)^{\frac{2}{A}}\\
\leq 2A^{2}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}\left(\int_{\Omega}|E|^{N}\log^{N}(e+A\,|E|)\right)^{\frac{2}{N}}+2\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2}\|1+|u_{n}|\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2}{A}}|\Omega|^{\frac{2}{N}-\frac{2}{A}}.\end{split}

At this point we can use Lemma
2.1
to conclude that

and that

solves (
1.20
). As far as uniqueness is concerned notice that if
