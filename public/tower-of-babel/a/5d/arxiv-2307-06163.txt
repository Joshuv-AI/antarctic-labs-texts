Gauss-Bonnet Dark Energy and the Speed of Gravitational Waves
José Jaime Terente Díaz, Konstantinos Dimopoulos, Mindaugas Karčiauskas, Antonio Racioppi

Abstract
Gauss-Bonnet Dark Energy has been a popular model to explain the accelerated expansion of the Universe. Quite generically it also predicts the speed of gravitational waves c GW c_{\mathrm{GW}} to be different from the speed of light. This fact alone led some authors to exclude such models in view of the new tight observational constraints on c GW c_{\mathrm{GW}} . However, the behaviour of c GW c_{\mathrm{GW}} depends on the choice of the Gauss-Bonnet (GB) coupling function. It is possible to construct models where c GW c_{\mathrm{GW}} is always equal to the speed of light. More generally, c GW c_{\mathrm{GW}} is a time dependent function with instances where both speeds coincide. Nevertheless, we observe that the bound on c GW c_{\mathrm{GW}} excludes scenarios where the GB term directly affects the expansion of the Universe, even if the constraint on the variation of the coupling function does not appear to be strong. We perform the dynamical systems analysis to see if the expansion of the Universe could be affected indirectly by modulating the behaviour of the scalar field, which modulates the GB coupling. It is shown that either the bounds on c GW c_{\mathrm{GW}} are violated by many orders of magnitude, or it might be very difficult to find models that are consistent with other cosmological observations.



I
Introduction

The detection of gravitational waves (GW)
(
Abbott et al. 2016a
;
Abbott et al. 2016b
;
Abbott et al. 2017a
)

opens a new window to observe and measure the Universe.
Most directly, it enables testing General Relativity (GR) in regimes
that were not accessible before and constrain possible modifications
of the laws of gravity. They also provide new ways to test Dark Energy
(DE) models. Many of such models rely on gravity modifications and
therefore are subject to such constraints.

A very clear demonstration is provided in Ref.
(
Abbott et al. 2017b
)
.
A lucky coincidence of being able to detect GW emitted by the merger
of two neutron stars as well as the electromagnetic counterpart of
this event made it possible to put very stringent constraints on the
speed of GW,
c_{\mathrm{GW}}
. The delay between arrival times of GW and
\gamma
-rays
led to the bound

\displaystyle\left|\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}\right|

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 10^{-15}\,,

(1)

where
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}
parametrises the deviation of
c_{\mathrm{GW}}
from the speed
of light

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{GW}}^{2}-1\>,

(2)

in natural units, where
c=\hbar=1
.

Many classes of modified gravity theories predict
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}\neq 0
. The
constraints on
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}
in Ref.
(
Abbott et al. 2017b
)
excluded
a lot of well motivated and otherwise attractive models and considerably
narrowed down the space of available modifications
(
Ezquiaga and Zumalacárregui 2017
;
Amendola et al. 2018
)
.

Among the excluded models – it is claimed in Ref.
(
Ezquiaga and Zumalacárregui 2017
)

– is the Gauss-Bonnet Dark Energy (GBDE) one. This model has many
attractive features. The Gauss-Bonnet term itself is a unique combination
of curvature terms squared

\mathcal{G}\equiv R^{2}-4R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}+R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,,

(3)

where
R
,
R_{\mu\nu}
and
R_{\hphantom{\rho}\sigma\mu\nu}^{\rho}

are the Ricci scalar, tensor and Riemann tensor respectively. Nevertheless,
this combination leads to metric tensor equations of motion that are
second order. The Gauss-Bonnet (GB) term is quite ubiquitous in actions
of low-energy effective string theory, be it at tree or one loop level

(
Callan et al. 1969
;
Gross and Sloan 1987
;
Boulware and Deser 1985
;
Antoniadis, Gava, and Narain 1992
;
Antoniadis, Rizos, and Tamvakis 1994
;
Bento and Bertolami 1996
;
Sami et al. 2005
)
.
The corresponding modification can be written as
\xi\mathcal{G}

term in the Lagrangian, where
\xi
is the GB coupling. If the latter
is a constant, the GB term is a surface term and can be integrated
out (although it can still be important for other aspects of the theory,
such as regularization
(
Aros et al. 2000
;
Olea 2005
)
). However,
on quite generic grounds, one might expect that the GB term also couples
to scalar fields of the theory, such as moduli or dilaton fields,
making
\xi
field dependent.

The possibility of explaining DE with the GB term was first investigated
in Refs.
(
Nojiri, Odintsov, and Sasaki 2005
;
Amendola, Charmousis, and Davis 2006
;
Koivisto and Mota 2007a
;
Koivisto and Mota 2007b
;
van de Bruck et al. 2017
)
.
One of the attractive features of such models is that they
provide the means to safely cross the phantom divide, that is, enter
the regime where the DE equation of state is
w<-1
, without instabilities.
The best fit value of
w
is smaller than
-1

(
Aghanim et al. 2020
)
.
If such
w
is associated with a scalar field, it leads to many instabilities
and such a model is likely excluded by observations
(
Cline, Jeon, and Moore 2004
)
.
On the other hand, modifications of gravity in GBDE in some parameter
range allow for
w<-1
. In this model
w
is time dependent;
it briefly dips below
-1
before settling on
w=-1

(
Koivisto and Mota 2007a
)
.
Hence, it can accommodate this low value without leading to contradictions.

Quite generically GBDE predicts
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}\neq 0
. This fact alone
led the authors of Ref.
(
Ezquiaga and Zumalacárregui 2017
)
to claim that GBDE
is ruled out. Here we would like to point out that
c_{\mathrm{GW}}
,
predicted by GBDE, is not a constant. Moreover, the constraint
in eq. (
1
) is an upper bound which is applicable
only at the very latest stages of the evolution of the Universe.
1
1

1

It is also worth noting that the bound on
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}
applies only
to the limited range of GW frequencies. This fact alone could save
many Horndeski type Dark Energy models
(
de Rham and Melville 2018
)
.
Hence, to assess the implications of these constraints for GBDE we
need to study it more carefully.

In this work we use the dynamical systems analysis to look
for viable models of GBDE and compute the evolution of
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}
parameter.
The crucial quantity in such models is the GB coupling
\xi\left(\phi\right)
.
It determines the dynamics of the universe as well as the evolution
of the
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}
parameter. Applying the bound in eq. (

)
to the variation of
\xi\left(\phi\right)
and the rate of its variation,
we find that the constraints appear weak. Nevertheless,
the bound in eq. (

affecting the expansion of the Universe directly. The remaining possibility
is for this term to affect the expansion indirectly, by modifying
the behaviour of the scalar field. To investigate this issue we apply
the dynamical systems analysis. We also apply this analysis to the
case where
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}=0
by construction, which is allowed by the model.

In Section

). In Section

in Section

function

in Section

. The case

.

II
Scalar-Gauss-Bonnet Dark Energy and Constraints on
c_{\mathrm{GW}}

We start with the scalar-Gauss-Bonnet action

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left[\frac{1}{2}m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}R+\xi\left(\phi\right)\mathcal{G}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi-V\left(\phi\right)+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{m}}\right]\,,

(4)

where
\mathcal{G}
is defined in eq. (
3
). For brevity
we address to the above action as the Gauss-Bonnet (GB) action in
this work.
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{m}}
is the Lagrangian of the matter
sector. If
\xi
is constant, the GB term is a total derivative and
does not affect the dynamics of the system. We assume the background
spacetime to be homogeneous, isotropic and flat, described by the
FRW metric
g_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}\left[-1,a^{2}\left(t\right),a^{2}\left(t\right),a^{2}\left(t\right)\right]
,
where
t
is the cosmic time and
a
is the scale factor.

In this model the speed of tensor mode propagation is determined by
the rate of change of the coupling function
\xi\left(\phi\right)

(
Noh and Hwang 2001
;
Hwang and Noh 2005
;
Hwang and Noh 2002
;
Odintsov and Oikonomou 2019
)
(see
also Refs.
(
Kawai, Sakagami, and Soda 1998
;
Kawai and Soda 1999
)
)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8\left(\ddot{\xi}-\dot{\xi}H\right)}{m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}+8\dot{\xi}H}\,,

(5)

where we use a dot to denote the derivative with respect to
t
and

H\equiv\dot{a}/a
is the Hubble parameter. It is clear from this
expression that the constraint in eq. (
1
) can
be satisfied if one of the following two conditions is fulfilled.
The first option is to choose the coupling function
\xi\left(\phi\right)

such that

\displaystyle\ddot{\xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H\dot{\xi}\,.

(6)

This choice is discussed in refs.
(
Odintsov and Oikonomou 2019
;
Oikonomou 2022
)

(other related references can be found in these articles) in the context
of inflation. However, there is another, more generic possibility.
We notice in eq. (
5
) that
\alpha_{T}
is suppressed
by the Planck mass. Therefore, as long as the conditions
\left|\ddot{\xi}\right|/m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2},\>H\left|\dot{\xi}\right|/m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}<10^{-15}

are satisfied, the GB action in eq. (
4
) is compatible with
the constraints on the speed of gravitational waves. We can write
these conditions in a more useful way

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\ddot{\xi}\right|}{H^{2}},\,\frac{\left|\dot{\xi}\right|}{H}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 10^{-15}\left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{Pl}}}{H}\right)^{2}\,,

(7)

which emphasises the change of the coupling function and the rate
of change of this coupling over one Hubble time. If this condition
is to be imposed on large field inflation models, where
H\lesssim 10^{-5}m_{\mathrm{Pl}}

(
Akrami et al. 2020
)
, this bound can be tight. In that case,
to limit
\alpha_{T}
within the allowed range, it is better to look
for models that satisfy the condition in eq. (
6
).
However, the constraints from the observations of GRB170817A do
not apply to the early Universe. Therefore it is not very useful to
use them in that context. Instead, this constraint is applicable to
the present Universe, within a very narrow range of e-folds (see
Fig.
3
), where the Hubble parameter is more than
fifty orders of magnitude smaller than the value of
H
cited
above. Indeed, plugging
H_{0}^{2}/m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}\sim 10^{-120}

(
Aghanim et al. 2020
)

into eq. (
7
) we find
2
2

2

This is only an order of magnitude estimate. Factors of order
1 do not change the conclusions in any substantial way.

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\ddot{\xi}\right|}{H_{0}^{2}},\,\frac{\left|\dot{\xi}\right|}{H_{0}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 10^{105}\,.

(8)

That is,
\xi
and
\dot{\xi}
need to vary by more than
100 orders of magnitude,
\Delta\xi\>,\Delta\dot{\xi}<10^{105}
,
over the age of the Universe to violate the bound. This appears
to demonstrate that the constraint on
c_{\mathrm{GW}}
is exceptionally
weak and might give hope that GBDE models remain viable. Unfortunately,
as we show in this work, at least for simple functions
\xi\left(\phi\right)
,
this turns out not to be the case.

To understand the implications of eq. (
1
) for
Gauss-Bonnet Dark Energy (GBDE) models better, let us first write
the homogeneous dynamical equations in the FRW background as
(
Nojiri, Odintsov, and Sasaki 2005
;
Heydari-Fard, Razmi, and Yousefi 2016
)

\displaystyle H^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{\phi}+\rho_{\mathrm{m}}}{3\left(m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}+8H\dot{\xi}\right)}\>,

(9)

\displaystyle\dot{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho_{\phi}+P_{\phi}+\rho_{\mathrm{m}}+P_{\mathrm{m}}+8H^{2}\left(\ddot{\xi}-\dot{\xi}H\right)}{2\left(m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}+8H\dot{\xi}\right)}\>,

(10)

where
\rho_{\phi}
and
P_{\phi}
are the energy and pressure densities
of the homogeneous scalar field
\phi
respectively. They are defined
to be

\displaystyle\rho_{\phi}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}+V\left(\phi\right)\>,

(11)

\displaystyle P_{\phi}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}-V\left(\phi\right)\>.

(12)

Similarly
\rho_{\mathrm{m}}
and
P_{\mathrm{m}}
are the energy
and pressure densities of the matter field.

The acceleration of spatial slices can be parametrised using the Hubble
flow parameter

\displaystyle\epsilon_{H}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}}\>.

(13)

Alternatively, it is common to use the deceleration parameter
q\equiv\epsilon_{H}-1

for this purpose. The spatial slices expand in an accelerating fashion
if
\epsilon_{H}<1
(
q<0
).

Plugging eqs. (
9
) and (
10
) into eq. (
13
) we
can write

\displaystyle\epsilon_{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\left(1+\frac{P_{\phi}+P_{\mathrm{m}}}{\rho_{\phi}+\rho_{\mathrm{m}}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{T}\>,

(14)

where we also made use of eq. (
5
). At the present epoch

\epsilon_{H}\simeq 0.5
. Thus, in view of eq. (
1
) we
see that the last term must be negligible. This rules out any direct
effect of the Gauss-Bonnet term to the expansion of the Universe.
Neglecting that last term, we arrive at the expression which can also
be obtained in a typical, General Relativistic quintessence models

(
Ratra and Peebles 1988
;
Wetterich 1988
)
.

But even if observations exclude the scenario where the GB term affects
the expansion of the Universe directly, there remains a possibility
that it does so indirectly, by modifying the behaviour of the scalar
field
\phi
. As we will see next, such a possibility is also excluded,
at least for an exponential potential
V\left(\phi\right)
.

III
The Dynamical System

Equations (
9
) and (
10
) can be supplemented with dynamical
equations governing the evolution of the
\phi
field and
\rho_{\mathrm{m}}

\displaystyle\ddot{\phi}+3H\dot{\phi}+V_{,\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 24\xi_{,\phi}\left(\dot{H}+H^{2}\right)H^{2}\>,

(15)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\mathrm{m}}+3H\rho_{\mathrm{m}}\left(1+w_{\mathrm{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\>,

(16)

where
w_{\mathrm{m}}\equiv P_{\mathrm{m}}/\rho_{\mathrm{m}}
is the barotropic
parameter of the matter component. And we assume a matter fluid with

0\leq w_{\mathrm{m}}<1
.

To analyse the generic behaviour of this dynamical system, it is convenient
to normalise the dynamical degrees of freedom and write them in a
dimensionless form, such as

x\equiv\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\sqrt{6}m_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\,,\quad y\equiv\frac{\sqrt{V}}{\sqrt{3}m_{\mathrm{Pl}}H}\>,\quad u\equiv 4\sqrt{6}\frac{H^{2}\xi_{,\phi}}{m_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\,,\text{ and }z\equiv\frac{\sqrt{\rho_{\mathrm{m}}}}{\sqrt{3}m_{\mathrm{Pl}}H}\,.

(17)

The prime in the definition of
x
and the equations below denotes
the derivatives with respect to the e-fold number

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\ln a\,,

(18)

where we normalised
a
such that
a=1
today. The above
defined dimensionless parameters are related to the more familiar
density parameters by

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{m}}}{3m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}H^{2}}=z^{2}\>,

(19)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\phi}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{\phi}}{3m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}H^{2}}=x^{2}+y^{2}\>,

(20)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{GB}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{8H\dot{\xi}}{m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}}=-2ux\>.

(21)

The first two definitions are exactly the same as in models of GR
with a scalar field. The physical origin of the last parameter is
due to the modifications of gravity, but it is interpreted as an effective
matter fluid. Following this interpretation we can write the constraint
equation (
9
) (the Friedmann equation) as

1=\Omega_{\phi}+\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}+\Omega_{\mathrm{GB}}\>.

(22)

In GR the analogous equation confines the range of variation of each
parameter to
\left|\Omega\right|\leq 1
. This is due to the density
parameters being non-negative. In GB gravity
\Omega_{\mathrm{GB}}

can be positive as well as negative. This makes it possible for
\Omega_{\phi}

and
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
to exceed unity.

The definitions in eq. (
17
) are particularly useful
if the scalar field potential is an exponential function

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\phi/m_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\>,

(23)

where we take
\lambda>0
to be a constant. We will always use the
above ansatz in this work. In that case the dynamical equations are
self-similar and the explicit dependence on the Hubble parameter drops
out of those equations. In particular, eqs. (
15
) and
(
16
) can be written as

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\epsilon_{H}-3\right)x+\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\lambda y^{2}+u\left(1-\epsilon_{H}\right)\>,

(24)

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\epsilon_{H}-\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\lambda x\right)y\>,

(25)

\displaystyle z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\epsilon_{H}-\frac{3}{2}\left(1+w_{\mathrm{m}}\right)\right]z\>,

(26)

where

\displaystyle\epsilon_{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[3x^{2}+\frac{3}{2}\left(1+w_{\mathrm{m}}\right)z^{2}+\left(ux\right)^{\prime}-ux\right]\frac{1}{1+ux}

(27)

The constraint equation (
22
), in terms of
the dimensionless variables, can be written as

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-2ux\>.

(28)

When doing dynamical analysis of this system, it is convenient to
use the equation for
u
too. Taking the derivative of the expression
in eq. (
17
) we find

\displaystyle u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\epsilon_{H}u+24H^{2}\xi_{,\phi\phi}\>x\>.

(29)

A few comments about these equations are in order. First, notice that
taking
u=0
(
\xi=\mathrm{const}
) we recover the same equations
as used for models in General Relativity (e.g. ref.
(
Copeland, Liddle, and Wands 1998
)
).
In those models, the dimensionless variables
y
and
z
are constrained
within the range
\left[0;1\right]
and
x\in\left[-1;1\right]
.
This can be seen from eq. (
28
) or equivalently from eq. (
22
).
In the case of the GB models such restrictions do not apply. As mentioned
above,
a priori
the sign of
u
(or
\Omega_{\mathrm{GB}}
)
is not determined. Hence, the maximum values of
\left|x\right|
,

and

are not limited to

. Second, eq. (
27
)
diverges as

. However, this value is never reached
because it falls within the phase space region that is forbidden by
the constraint equation (

VII
Summary and Conclusions

In this work we investigate Gauss-Bonnet Dark Energy (GBDE) models.
Generically such models predict the speed of gravitational waves different
from the speed of light. In view of the tight observational constraints
on such deviations, denoted by
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}
(see eq. (
1
)),
such models are considered to be disfavoured. However, the deviation
is time dependent and the bound, although tight, is an upper bound,
which is only applicable for very late Universe. Hence, before excluding
GBDE models we need to perform a more detailed analysis. Moreover,
if the bound in eq. (
1
) is expressed in terms
of the variation of the GB coupling function
\xi\left(\phi\right)
,
it might appear to be a weak bound, as shown in eq. (
8
).

To see if GBDE models can indeed survive the observational constraints
on
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}
we perform the dynamical systems analysis. We
assume that the scalar field has an exponential potential and find
that such a dynamical system quite generically has the scaling and
de Sitter fixed points (among others), denoted by
Sc
and

dS
respectively in this work.

At
Sc
the scalar field adjusts in such a way that it mimics
the behaviour of the background matter component. In particular,
the equation of state of the scalar field is the same as
that of the matter component. In the case of the exponential GB coupling,
depending on the magnitude of the exponent,
Sc
is not stable:
it is a saddle point. This is in contrast to the General Relativistic
quintessence models with an exponential potential
(
Copeland, Liddle, and Wands 1998
)
.
In the latter setup the scaling fixed point is an attractor, which
makes it impossible to use as an explanation for the accelerated expansion
of the Universe. In GBDE
Sc
can have an unstable direction
which links to
dS
, the latter being an attractor. Using numerical
solutions we show that if the universe starts with a very small GB
term and it is either kination or matter dominated, initially all
solutions evolve towards the
Sc
fixed point. They linger
in the neighbourhood of
Sc
for a long period of time and
eventually change its course towards
dS
. This behaviour is
beneficial to modelling quintessential inflation, because it allows,
without extreme fine-tuning, to bridge the enormous energy density
gap (110 orders of magnitude) between inflation and dark
energy. We find numerous models that follow this scenario and can
predict observationally allowed values for the matter energy density
and DE equation of state.

Moreover, at
Sc
and
dS
fixed points the speed of
gravitational waves is exactly the same as that of the speed of light,
i.e.
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}=0
. Unfortunately, if this model is to represent the evolution
of the actual Universe, we cannot be living either on the scaling
or de Sitter fixed points, but somewhere in between. However, as we
show in Figure
3
,
\left|\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}\right|
changes from

0
to
\sim 1
in between these fixed points. Moreover, as we argue
below eq. (
52
), if
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\sim 0.1
, which
corresponds to the current value, quite generically one expects
\left|\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}\right|\sim\mathcal{O}\left(0.1\right)-\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
,
which is ruled out by many orders of magnitude. We conclude that the
bound in eq. (
1
) makes GBDE with an exponential

\xi\left(\phi\right)
function unviable.

In view of the above conclusion, we investigated other choices of
\xi\left(\phi\right)
. First, we demonstrate that
for a linear function
\xi\left(\phi\right)
the de Sitter fixed
point is a saddle point, and the Scaling fixed point becomes an attractor.
This makes it impossible to find any viable solution that would be
consistent with the evolution of the Universe. We cannot apply our
method to study more generic functions
\xi\left(\phi\right)
, such
as monomials or steeper than exponential potentials studied, for example,
in Refs.
(
Silva et al. 2018
;
Doneva and Yazadjiev 2018
)
. This is because dynamical
equations lose their self-similar character.

The advantage of the above described analysis is that we have an explicit

\xi\left(\phi\right)
function. But as we showed, this does not
provide a viable cosmological solution. Another hope to make GBDE
models conform to observational constraints is to impose the condition

\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}=0
, as can be seen in eq. (
5
). In this case we
lose the benefit of having an explicit functional form of
\xi\left(\phi\right)
,
but we gain an additional constraint equation (
58
). As it
is demonstrated in Section
VI
, the stability of
fixed points in this case is very similar to the linear model. That
is, there are no solutions which provide a long period of matter domination
followed by an accelerated expansion.

In summary, we find that a GBDE model with an exponential scalar field
potential and an exponential GB coupling function could provide a
realistic model of DE. However, the recent bounds on the speed of
GW rules out this possibility by many orders of magnitude. If, on
the other hand, we look for models that do satisfy
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}=0
, then
it is impossible to find a scenario consistent with other cosmological
observations.

The negative conclusions reached in this work apply to the metric
formulation of the Gauss-Bonnet model. But we know that some modified
gravity models that violates the
\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}
bound in eq. (
1
)
become viable again in the Palatini formalism
(
Kubota et al. 2021
)
.
One can hope that a similar modification could save the GBDE model
too.
