Multivariate Kawtchouk polynomials as Birth and Death polynomials


Abstract
Multivariate Krawtchouk polynomials are constructed explicitly as Birth and Death polynomials, which have the nearest neighbour interactions. They form the complete set of eigenpolynomials of a birth and death process with the birth and death rates at population 𝒙 = ( x 1 , … , x n ) \boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}) are B j ​ ( 𝒙 ) = ( N − ∑ i = 1 n x i ) B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=\bigl(N-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\bigr) and D j ​ ( 𝒙 ) = p i − 1 ​ x j D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=p_{i}^{-1}x_{j} , 0 < p j 0<p_{j} , j = 1 , … , n j=1,\ldots,n . The corresponding stationary distribution is the multinomial distribution with the probabilities { η i } \{\eta_{i}\} , η i = def p i / ( 1 + ∑ j = 1 n p j ) \eta_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}p_{i}/(1+\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}) . The polynomials, depending on n + 1 n+1 parameters ( { p i } \{p_{i}\} and N N ), satisfy the difference equation with the coefficients B j ​ ( 𝒙 ) B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}) and D j ​ ( 𝒙 ) D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}) j = 1 , … , n j=1,\ldots,n , which is the straightforward generalisation of the difference equation governing the single variable Krawtchouk polynomials. The polynomials are truncated ( n + 1 , 2 ​ n + 2 ) (n+1,2n+2) hypergeometric functions of Aomoto-Gelfand. The divariate Rahman polynomials are identified as the dual polynomials with a special parametrisation.

2
Birth and Death processes

2.1
Single variable case

Let us start with the review of the one-dimensional Birth and Death (BD) processes
[
5
,
19
]
.
Let
\mathcal{P}(x;t)
be the probability distribution at time
t
over a population space
\mathcal{X}
,
which is a one-dimensional nonnegative integer lattice, either finite or semi-infinite:

\mathcal{X}=\{0,1,\ldots,N\}:\quad\text{finite},\qquad\mathcal{X}={\mathbb{N}_{0}}:\quad\text{semi-infinite}.

Here
{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is the set of nonnegative integers.
Let us denote the birth rate at population
x
by
B(x)>0
and the death rate by
D(x)>0
.
The time evolution of the probability distribution is governed by the following differential equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{P}(x;t)

\displaystyle=(L_{BD}\mathcal{P})(x;t)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}{L_{BD}}_{x\,y}\mathcal{P}(y;t),\quad\mathcal{P}(x;t)\geq 0,\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{P}(x;t)=1,

(2.1)

\displaystyle=-\bigl(B(x)+D(x)\bigr)\mathcal{P}(x;t)+B(x-1)\mathcal{P}(x-1;t)+D(x+1)\mathcal{P}(x+1;t),

(2.2)

with the boundary condition(s)

D(0)=0,\quad B(N)=0:\ (\text{only for a finite case}).

(2.3)

The
(N+1)\times(N+1)
matrix
L_{BD}
is tri-diagonal,

\displaystyle{L_{BD}}_{x+1\,x}=B(x),\ \ {L_{BD}}_{x-1\,x}=D(x),\ {L_{BD}}_{x\,x}=-B(x)-D(x),

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt{L_{BD}}_{x\,y}=0,\quad|x-y|\geq 2,

(2.4)

representing the nearest neighbour interactions.

There are many exactly solvable cases with the explicit forms of the complete sets of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
L_{BD}
.
Among them, those related with the hypergeometric orthogonal polynomials of Askey scheme

[
2
,
19
,
22
,
6
]
are pertinent for the present purpose.
All the discrete orthogonal polynomials of Askey scheme
\{\check{P}_{n}(x)\}
with the normalised orthogonality measure
function
W(x)>0
,

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W(x)=1,\qquad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W(x)\check{P}_{n}(x)\check{P}_{m}(x)=0,\quad n\neq m,

(2.5)

satisfy a difference equation

B(x)\!\left(\check{P}_{n}(x)-\check{P}_{n}(x+1)\right)\!+\!D(x)\!\left(\check{P}_{n}(x)-\check{P}_{n}(x-1)\right)=\mathcal{E}(n)\check{P}_{n}(x),\ \mathcal{E}(n)\geq 0,\ n\in\mathcal{X},

(2.6)

with positive coefficient functions
B(x)
and
D(x)
satisfying the boundary conditions (
2.3
).
The orthogonality measure function
W(x)
is determined by
B(x)
and
D(x)
,

W(x)=W(0)\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}\frac{B(y)}{D(y+1)},\quad 1\leq x\in\mathcal{X},

(2.7)

in which the constant
W(0)
is fixed by the normalisation condition
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W(x)=1
.
The following Theorem was shown in
[
29
]
.

Theorem 2.1

If the coefficient functions
B(x)
and
D(x)
of the Askey scheme polynomials (
2.6
) are
chosen as the birth and death rates of
L_{BD}
(
2.4
), the corresponding

\{\check{P}_{n}(x)\}
and
W(x)
provide the complete set of eigensystem of
L_{BD}
,

L_{BD}\check{P}_{n}(x)W(x)=-\mathcal{E}(n)\check{P}_{n}(x)W(x),\quad n\in\mathcal{X},

(2.8)

with
W(x)
providing the stationary distribution with
\check{P}_{0}(x)\equiv 1
,

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathcal{P}(x;t)=W(x),\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W(x)=1,\quad L_{BD}W(x)=0,

(2.9)

as the zero-mode of
L_{BD}
, i.e.
\mathcal{E}(0)=0
.

2.2
Multivariable cases: General setting

Let us now try and formulate the birth and death processes for
n
groups of population
denoted by
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}
.
The population space
\mathcal{X}
is now a subset of
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{n}
.
The natural generalisation of the birth and death (BD) equation of
the single species case (
2.1
),(
2.2
),
reads as follows

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x};t)

\displaystyle=(L_{BD}\mathcal{P})(\boldsymbol{x};t)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{X}}{L_{BD}}_{\boldsymbol{x}\,\boldsymbol{y}}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{y};t),\quad\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x};t)\geq 0,\quad\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x};t)=1,

(2.10)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})+D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}))\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x};t)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j};t)

\displaystyle\hskip 165.02606pt+\sum_{j=1}^{n}D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j};t),

(2.11)

in which
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
is the
j
-th unit vector,
j=1,\ldots,n
.
The birth and death rates for
n
groups
B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})
,
D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})
are all positive with the
boundary conditions

\left.\begin{array}[]{ccc}B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=0&\text{if}\ \ \boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}&\text{and}\ \ \boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\notin\mathcal{X}\\[2.0pt]
D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=0&\text{if}\ \ \boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}&\text{and}\ \ \boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\notin\mathcal{X}\end{array}\right\},\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

(2.12)

This is a typical example of the nearest neighbour interactions in
n
dimensions.

Let us impose the condition that the
|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}|
matrix
L_{BD}
has a stationary distribution

(L_{BD}W)(\boldsymbol{x})=0,\quad\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}W(\boldsymbol{x})=1,\quad W(\boldsymbol{x})>0,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},

(2.13)

which constrain
\{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}),D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\}
severely.
In order to relate
W(\boldsymbol{x})
with the birth/death rates, let us introduce the following notational conventions.
For a smooth function
f(x)
, the Taylor expansion reads

\displaystyle f(x+a)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{n}}{n!}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}f(x)=(e^{a\partial}f)(x),\quad\partial\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{d}{dx}\quad\Longrightarrow f(x\pm 1)=(e^{\pm\partial}f)(x).

Since only polynomials and the corresponding weight functions appear in this paper, it is quite safe to introduce the
operators
e^{\pm\partial_{j}}
acting on a function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
over
\mathcal{X}
,

e^{\pm\partial_{j}}f(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{x}\pm\boldsymbol{e}_{j})\,e^{\pm\partial_{j}},\quad\partial_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}},\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

They are upper and lower triangular matrices acting on vectors (functions) over
\mathcal{X}

and transpose of each other

\bigl(e^{\pm\partial_{j}}\bigr)_{\boldsymbol{x}\,\boldsymbol{y}}=\delta_{\boldsymbol{x}\pm\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\,\boldsymbol{y}},\qquad\bigl(e^{\partial_{j}}\bigr)^{T}=e^{-\partial_{j}},\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

It is well known that the basic relations of these operators,

e^{\partial_{j}}e^{-\partial_{j}}=I_{d}=e^{-\partial_{j}}e^{\partial_{j}}
are
broken at certain boundaries of
\mathcal{X}
. But the violation of the rules does not cause any problem
when these operators are used in conjunction with
\{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}),D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\}

due to the boundary conditions (
2.12
).
In terms of the operators
\{e^{\pm\partial_{j}}\}
,
the birth and death operator
L_{BD}
can be expressed succinctly as

\displaystyle L_{BD}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})-B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})e^{-\partial_{j}}+D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})-D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})e^{\partial_{j}}\right],

(2.14)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[(1-e^{-\partial_{j}})B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})+(1-e^{\partial_{j}})D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\right],

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(1-e^{-\partial_{j}})\bigl(B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})-D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})\,e^{\partial_{j}}\bigr).

This provides a sufficient condition for the zero mode of
L_{BD}
(
2.13
), the stationary distribution
W(\boldsymbol{x})>0
,

\displaystyle\bigl(B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})-D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})e^{\partial_{j}}\bigr)W(\boldsymbol{x})=0\ \Rightarrow\frac{W(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}{W(\boldsymbol{x})}=\frac{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}{D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})},\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

(2.15)

These conditions determine entire
W(\boldsymbol{x})
starting from the origin
W(\boldsymbol{0})
so long as the
two different routes give the same results,

\displaystyle W(\boldsymbol{x})\

\displaystyle\to W(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})\ \to W(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k})

\displaystyle\hskip 136.5733pt||

\displaystyle\to W(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k})\ \to W(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}).

Proposition 2.3

The existence of the stationary distribution imposes severe conditions on the birth/death rates.
The sufficient condition that the BD process
L_{BD}
(
2.11
) defined on a finite
\mathcal{X}

to have a stationary distribution
W(\boldsymbol{x})
is the following compatibility condition,

\frac{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}{D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\frac{B_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}{D_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k})}=\frac{B_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})}{D_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k})}\frac{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k})}{D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})},\quad j,k=1,\ldots,n.

(2.16)

For an infinite
\mathcal{X}
, the summability
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}W(\boldsymbol{x})<\infty
must be satisfied.

Compatibility conditions for difference operators acting on various multivariable orthogonal polynomials
are discussed in
[
15
]
.

Remark 2.4

The orthogonality weight function (stationary distribution) is determined by the birth/death rates.
That is, the birth/death rates have more information than the orthogonality measure.

Supposing the stationary distribution
W(\boldsymbol{x})
is obtained, let us introduce a new operator
\mathcal{H}

in terms of a similarity transformation of
L_{BD}
by the square root of
W(\boldsymbol{x})

\mathcal{H}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}-\bigl(\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}\bigr)^{-1}L_{BD}\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}.

(2.17)

From (
2.14
), it is easy to see

\displaystyle\bigl(\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})+D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr)\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}=B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})+D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}),

\displaystyle\bigl(\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}\bigr)^{-1}D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})\,e^{\partial_{j}}\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigl(\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}\bigr)^{-1}\sqrt{D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\sqrt{D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\,e^{\partial_{j}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigl(\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}\bigr)^{-1}\sqrt{D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}\,e^{\partial_{j}}=\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\,e^{\partial_{j}},

\displaystyle\bigl(\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}\bigr)^{-1}B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})\,e^{-\partial_{j}}\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigl(\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}\bigr)^{-1}\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\,e^{-\partial_{j}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigl(\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}\bigr)^{-1}\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}\sqrt{D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}\,e^{-\partial_{j}}=\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}\,e^{-\partial_{j}}.

These lead to an expression of
\mathcal{H}
depending only on the birth and death rates

\{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}),D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})+D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})-\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\,e^{\partial_{j}}-\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}\,e^{-\partial_{j}}\right],

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{x}\,\boldsymbol{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[\bigl(B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})+D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr)\,\delta_{\boldsymbol{x}\,\boldsymbol{y}}-\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\,\delta_{\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\,\boldsymbol{y}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 147.95424pt-\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}\,\delta_{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\,\boldsymbol{y}}\right],

(2.19)

ushering the following

Proposition 2.5

The operator
\mathcal{H}
is a real symmetric and positive semi-definite matrix over
\mathcal{X}
as evidenced by
the factorisation form

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\mathcal{H}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})^{T}\mathcal{A}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{x}\,\boldsymbol{y}}=\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{y}\,\boldsymbol{x}},

(2.20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}-e^{\partial_{j}}\sqrt{D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})},\ \mathcal{A}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})^{T}=\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}-\sqrt{D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}\,e^{-\partial_{j}},\ j=1,\ldots,n.

(2.21)

Obviously
\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}
is the zero mode of
\mathcal{A}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{A}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}=0,\quad j=1,\ldots,n\quad\Longrightarrow\mathcal{H}\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}=0,

(2.22)

as (
2.15
) shows

0=\mathcal{A}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}=\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}-\sqrt{D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j})}.

The birth/death rates
\{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}),D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\}
contain many parameters. For the generic values of the
parameters the degeneracy of eigenvalues do not occur and the orthogonality of the eigenvectors is guaranteed.
The birth and death operator
L_{BD}
has negative semi-definite eigenvalues, as the eigensystem of
L_{BD}

is related to that of
\mathcal{H}
by the similarity transformation in terms of

).

Let us introduce another operator

in terms of the square root of the stationary distribution

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\bigl(\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}\bigr)^{-1}\mathcal{H}\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}.

It is interesting to note that

\displaystyle\bigl(\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}\bigr)^{-1}\mathcal{A}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\sqrt{W(\boldsymbol{x})}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sqrt{B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}\Bigl(1-e^{\partial_{j}}\Bigr),

\displaystyle\qquad=B_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigl(1-e^{\partial_{j}}\bigr)+D_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigl(1-e^{-\partial_{j}}\bigr).

The birth and death rates are

By construction, the eigenvalues

are positive
and
\{p_{i}\}
are also positive.
Therefore, if the situation

Once the explicit forms of the multivariate orthogonal polynomials with many free parameters are available,
