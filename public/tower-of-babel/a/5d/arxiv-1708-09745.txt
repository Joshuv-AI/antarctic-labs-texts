Introduction

In a paper with Noriko Yui
[
14
]
, explicit equations for all elliptic modular surfaces corresponding to genus zero torsion-free subgroups of
\mbox{PSL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
were presented. Arguably the most famous and classical one of these surfaces is the Hesse pencil, usually described as the family of plane cubics

\displaystyle x^{3}+y^{3}+z^{3}+6txyz=0

(see, e.g.,
[
14
, Table 2]
and references given there). The current paper intends to focus on the universal property of this Hesse pencil and of its twists (see Theorem
1.1
). The main goal is to do this as explicit and elementary as possible, and moreover to do it in such a way that it works in every characteristic different from three. We are not aware of any earlier publication of these results in the special case of characteristic two, so although admittedly not very difficult, those appear to be new. The first author of this paper worked on the present results as a (small) part of his Ph.D. Thesis
[
1
]
and the third author did the same as part of her Bachelor’s Thesis
[
15
]
. Both were supervised by the second author.

Let
k
be a perfect field of characteristic different from three. Denote the absolute Galois group of
k
by
G_{k}
. Given an elliptic curve
E
defined over
k
, one obtains a Galois representation on the 3-torsion group
E{\left[{3}\right]}
of
E
. This paper describes the family of all elliptic curves that have equivalent Galois representations on
E{\left[{3}\right]}
. Recall that elliptic curves
E
and
E^{\prime}
over
k
yield equivalent Galois representations on their 3-torsion if and only if
E{\left[{3}\right]}
and
E^{\prime}{\left[{3}\right]}
are isomorphic as
G_{k}
-modules. To be more specific, we demand that the equivalence is symplectic: a symplectic homomorphism
\phi\colon E[3]\to E^{\prime}[3]
is defined as in
[
10
]
, as follows. If

\displaystyle e_{3}{\left({S,T}\right)}=e^{\prime}_{3}{\left({\phi{\left({S}\right)},\phi{\left({T}\right)}}\right)}

for all
S,T\in E{\left[{3}\right]}
where
e_{3}
and
e^{\prime}_{3}
are the Weil-pairings on the 3-torsion of
E
and
E^{\prime}
respectively, then
\phi
is called a
symplectic
homomorphism, otherwise
\phi
is called an
anti-symplectic
homomorphism.

Next, recall the definition of the Hessian of a polynomial. Let
F\in k{\left[{X,Y,Z}\right]}
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
n
. The
Hessian

\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)}
of
F
is the determinant of the Hessian matrix of
F
, that is

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)}=\det\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial X^{2}}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial X\partial Y}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial X\partial Z}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\dfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial X\partial Y}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial Y^{2}}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial Y\partial Z}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\dfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial X\partial Z}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial Y\partial Z}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial Z^{2}}\end{pmatrix},

which is either a homogeneous polynomial of degree
3n-6
or zero.

Given a curve
C=Z{\left({F}\right)}
with
F\in k{\left[{X,Y,Z}\right]}
homogeneous of degree three, the
Hesse pencil
of
C
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=Z{\left({tF+\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)}}\right)}

over
k{\left({t}\right)}
. Recall that the discrete valuations on
k{\left({t}\right)}
correspond to the points in
{{\mathbb{P}}^{1}{\left({k}\right)}}
, where we usually write
{\left({t_{0}:1}\right)}
as
t_{0}
and
{\left({1:0}\right)}
as
\infty
. We denote the reduced curve of
\mathcal{C}
at
t_{0}\in{{\mathbb{P}}^{1}{\left({k}\right)}}
by
C_{t_{0}}
. Notice that
C_{\infty}=C
and for
t_{0}\neq\infty

\displaystyle C_{t_{0}}=Z{\left({t_{0}F+\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)}}\right)}.

In the special case that
C=E
is an elliptic curve given by a Weierstrass equation, we have (see Section
2
) that the point
O
at infinity is a point on
E_{t_{0}}
for every
t_{0}\in{{\mathbb{P}}^{1}{\left({k}\right)}}
. If
E_{t_{0}}
is a smooth curve, then this makes it an elliptic curve with unit element
O
.

In the case of characteristic two, the standard definition of the Hessian does not lead to a satisfactory theory. In Sections
9.2
and
9.3
a modified Hessian is introduced for this case; in fact this modification was already used by Dickson
[
5
]
in 1915. The goal of this paper is to provide an elementary proof of the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

If
E
and
E^{\prime}
are elliptic curves over
k
, with
E
given by some Weierstrass equation over
k
, then there exists a symplectic isomorphism
E{\left[{3}\right]}\rightarrow E^{\prime}{\left[{3}\right]}
if and only if
E^{\prime}
appears in the Hesse pencil of
E
, i.e.,
E_{t_{0}}\cong_{k}E^{\prime}
for some
t_{0}\in{{\mathbb{P}}^{1}{\left({k}\right)}}
.

We note that both Fisher’s paper
[
6
]
and Kuwata’s paper
[
10
]
discuss, apart from the result above (although not in characteristic two) also the case of anti-symplectic isomorphisms between the
3
-torsion groups of elliptic curves.

In Sections
2
,
3
and
4
we show that the 3-torsion groups of an elliptic curve in Weierstrass form and its Hesse pencil are identical not only as sets, but also have the same group structure and Weil-pairings. Using the Weierstrass form of the Hesse pencil computed in Section
5
and the relation between a linear change of coordinates and its restriction to the 3-torsion group described in Section
6
, we prove in Section
7
essentially by a counting argument that an
isomorphism of the 3-torsion groups respecting the Weil-pairings is the restriction of a linear change of coordinates. The proof of the theorem is completed in Section
8
. After this, we adapt the argument in order to conclude the same result in characteristic
2
(where a slightly adapted notion of Hesse pencil is required). We compare our results with existing literature in Section
10
.

2
The flex points

Let
C=Z{\left({F}\right)}
be a plane curve with
F\in k{\left[{X,Y,Z}\right]}
homogeneous of degree
n
and irreducible. A point
P
on
C
is called a
flex point
if there exists a line
L
such that the intersection number of
C
and
L
at
P
is at least three. Notice that in our definition
P
is allowed to be a singular point on
C
.

The
Hessian curve
of
C
is defined as
\operatorname{Hess}{\left({C}\right)}=Z{\left({\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)}}\right)}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
P
is a point on
C
and
\operatorname{char}{\left({k}\right)}\nmid(n-1)
, then
P
is a flex point if
and only if
P\in C\cap\operatorname{Hess}{\left({C}\right)}
.

Proof.

See
[
8
, Exercise 5.23]
.
∎

From now on we will only work with curves of degree three, so the proposition above is only usable for fields
k
of characteristic different from two. This is the reason for why we exclude characteristic two for now; see Section
9
for the excluded case.

Corollary 2.2
.

If
P
is a flex point on
C
, then it is also a point on the Hesse pencil
\mathcal{C}
and it is again a flex point of each curve of the Hesse pencil.

This is a well-known and old result in the case of
F=X^{3}+Y^{3}+Z^{3}
, see for example
[
11
, Section VII.1]
.

Proof.

A computation using Magma
[
4
]
shows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hess}{\left({tF+\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)}}\right)}=\alpha F+\beta\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)}

with
\alpha,\beta\in k{\left[{t}\right]}
.

Assume that
P
is a flex point, then
P\in C\cap\operatorname{Hess}{\left({C}\right)}
by Proposition
2.1
, that is
F{\left({P}\right)}=0
and
\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)}{\left({P}\right)}=0
. So
{\left({tF+\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)}}\right)}{\left({P}\right)}=0
, which implies that
P\in\mathcal{C}
. The computation above also implies that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hess}{\left({tF+\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)}}\right)}{\left({P}\right)}=0,

that is
P\in\operatorname{Hess}{\left({\mathcal{C}}\right)}
. Therefore
P\in\mathcal{C}\cap\operatorname{Hess}{\left({\mathcal{C}}\right)}
. Hence
P
is a flex point on
\mathcal{C}
by Proposition
2.1
.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
P\in C_{t_{0}}\cap C_{t_{1}}
. If
t_{0}\neq t_{1}
, then
P
is a flex point on
C
.

Proof.

Suppose that
t_{0}={\left({t_{00}:t_{01}}\right)}
and
t_{1}={\left({t_{10}:t_{11}}\right)}
, then

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}t_{00}&t_{01}\\
t_{10}&t_{11}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}F{\left({P}\right)}\\
\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)}{\left({P}\right)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

with the matrix being invertible since
t_{0}\neq t_{1}
. Thus
F{\left({P}\right)}=0
and
\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)}{\left({P}\right)}=0
, that is
P\in C\cap\operatorname{Hess}{\left({C}\right)}
. Hence Proposition
2.1
implies that
P
is a flex point on
C
.
∎

3
The 3-torsion group

Let
E=Z{\left({F}\right)}
be an elliptic curve with unit element
O
and
F\in k{\left[{X,Y,Z}\right]}
homogeneous of degree 3. Recall the following well known fact.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
S
and
T
be points on
E
. If
S
is a flex point, then
T
is a flex point if and only if
S-T\in E{\left[{3}\right]}
.

Proof.

Let
L_{S}
and
L_{T}
be the tangent lines to
E
at
S
and
T
respectively. Assume that
T
is also a flex point. Consider the function
\frac{L_{S}}{L_{T}}
on
E
which has divisor
3{\left({S}\right)}-3{\left({T}\right)}
. From
[
13
, Corollary III.3.5]
it follows that
3S-3T=O
. Hence
S-T\in E{\left[{3}\right]}
.

Assume that
T
is not a flex point. Now the divisor of the function
\frac{L_{S}}{L_{T}}
is
3{\left({S}\right)}-2{\left({T}\right)}-{\left({T^{\prime}}\right)}
with
T^{\prime}\neq T
. From this it follows that
3S-2T-T^{\prime}=O
, thus
3S-3T=T^{\prime}-T\neq O
. Hence
S-T\notin E{\left[{3}\right]}
.
∎

This result tells us that if
O
is a flex point on
E
, then the concepts of flex point and 3-torsion point coincide. In the previous section we learned that a flex point on
E
is also a flex point on
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{Z}(tF+\operatorname{Hess}{\left({F}\right)})
. Hence if we combine these statements, then we obtain
E{\left[{3}\right]}\subset\mathcal{E}{\left[{3}\right]}
. Since the characteristic of
k
is different from three, these sets are equal in size, thus the same. Moreover suppose that
E_{t_{0}}
for some
t_{0}\in{{\mathbb{P}}^{1}{\left({k}\right)}}
is non-singular. Provide
\mathcal{E}
and
E_{t_{0}}
with a group structure by taking
O
as the unit element. Since the flex points of
\mathcal{E}
(considered as a plane cubic over
k(t)
) and of
E_{t_{0}}
(a cubic curve over
k
) are the same and a line that intersects an elliptic curve at two flex points will also intersect the curve at a third flex point, the group structures on
\mathcal{E}{\left[{3}\right]}
and
E_{t_{0}}{\left[{3}\right]}
are equal as well.

Recall that if the unit element
O
is a flex point on
E
, then we can find a projective linear transformation in
{\operatorname{PGL}_{3}}{\left({k}\right)}
such that
E
is given by a Weierstrass equation in the new coordinates. Moreover since the characteristic of
k
is different from two and three, we may even assume that
E\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+ax+b
for some
a,b\in k
.

4
The Weil-pairing

In the previous section we saw that
\mathcal{E}{\left[{3}\right]}=E_{t_{0}}{\left[{3}\right]}
for all
t_{0}\in{{\mathbb{P}}^{1}{\left({{\overline{k}}}\right)}}
such that
E_{t_{0}}
is non-singular. Denote the Weil-pairing on the 3-torsion of
\mathcal{E}
by
e_{3}
and on the 3-torsion of
E_{t_{0}}
by
e_{3}^{t_{0}}
. An introduction to Weil-pairings can be found in
[
13
, Section III.8]
and
[
16
, Sections 3.3 and 11.2]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve given by a Weierstrass equation and let
\mathcal{E}
be its Hesse pencil. The Weil-pairings
e_{3}
and
e_{3}^{t_{0}}
on
E{\left[{3}\right]}
are equal.

Proof.

Let
S,T\in E{\left[{3}\right]}
generate
E{\left[{3}\right]}
. The Weil-pairing is determined by its value on
{\left({S,T}\right)}
. Follow
[
13
, Exercise 3.16]
to construct the Weil-pairings. Recall that
O
is a flex point on
E
.

Let
L_{O}
,
L_{S}
,
L_{T}
and
L_{-T}
be the tangent lines to
\mathcal{E}
at
O
,
S
,
T
and
-T
respectively. Define
D_{S}={\left({S}\right)}-{\left({O}\right)}
and
D_{T}=2{\left({T}\right)}-2{\left({-T}\right)}
. Notice that
D_{S}
and
D_{T}
have disjoint support. Since
2T-2{\left({-T}\right)}=T
in
\mathcal{E}
, it follows that
D_{T}\sim{\left({T}\right)}-{\left({O}\right)}
. Consider the functions
f_{S}=\frac{L_{S}}{L_{O}}
and
f_{T}=\big(\frac{L_{T}}{L_{-T}}\big)^{2}
, then
\operatorname{div}{\left({f_{S}}\right)}=3D_{S}
and
\operatorname{div}{\left({f_{T}}\right)}=3D_{T}
. The Weil-pairing on
\mathcal{E}
is defined as

\displaystyle e_{3}{\left({S,T}\right)}=\frac{f_{S}{\left({D_{T}}\right)}}{f_{T}{\left({D_{S}}\right)}}={\left({\frac{f_{S}{\left({T}\right)}}{f_{S}{\left({-T}\right)}}}\right)}^{2}\frac{f_{T}{\left({O}\right)}}{f_{T}{\left({S}\right)}}={\left({\frac{L_{S}{\left({T}\right)}L_{O}{\left({-T}\right)}L_{T}{\left({O}\right)}L_{-T}{\left({S}\right)}}{L_{O}{\left({T}\right)}L_{S}{\left({-T}\right)}L_{-T}{\left({O}\right)}L_{T}{\left({S}\right)}}}\right)}^{2}.

Let
s\in k{\left({S,T}\right)}{\left({t}\right)}
be a local coordinate at
t_{0}
. Choose the equations of the tangent lines such that they are also defined over
k{\left({S,T}\right)}{\left[\left[{s}\right]\right]}
and are non-zero modulo
s
. Notice that
L_{O}
,
L_{S}
,
L_{T}
and
L_{-T}
modulo
s
are tangent lines to
E_{t_{0}}
at
O
,
S
,
T
and
-T
respectively. Follow the construction above to obtain the Weil-pairing
e_{3}^{t_{0}}{\left({S,T}\right)}
on
E_{t_{0}}
.

Now
L_{O}{\left({T}\right)}
is a unit in
k{\left({S,T}\right)}{\left[\left[{s}\right]\right]}
, because
T
is not contained in the tangent line
L_{O}
modulo
s
to
E_{t_{0}}
at
O
. Similarly the other terms in the expression of
e_{3}{\left({S,T}\right)}
are units as well. Thus by construction
e_{3}{\left({S,T}\right)}\!\!\mod s=e_{3}^{t_{0}}{\left({S,T}\right)}
. Recall that
e_{3}{\left({S,T}\right)}
is a root of unity. Hence
e_{3}=e_{3}^{t_{0}}
.
∎

5
The Weierstrass form

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve given by the Weierstrass equation
y^{2}z=x^{3}+axz^{2}+bz^{3}
with
a,b\in k
. Then the Hesse pencil
\mathcal{E}
can be given by

\displaystyle ty^{2}z+3xy^{2}=tx^{3}-3ax^{2}z+{\left({at-9b}\right)}xz^{2}+\big(bt+a^{2}\big)z^{3}

over
k{\left({t}\right)}
. The linear change of coordinates

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}=A\begin{pmatrix}\xi\\
\eta\\
\zeta\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{with}\qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}t&0&3at^{2}-27bt-9a^{2}\\
0&1&0\\
-3&0&t^{3}+9at-27b\end{pmatrix}

transforms this into the Weierstrass form
\mathcal{E}^{W}\colon\eta^{2}\zeta=\xi^{3}+a_{t}\xi\zeta^{2}+b_{t}\zeta^{3}
, with

\displaystyle a_{t}=at^{4}-18bt^{3}-18a^{2}t^{2}+54abt-\big(27a^{3}+243b^{2}\big),

\displaystyle b_{t}=bt^{6}+4a^{2}t^{5}-45abt^{4}+270b^{2}t^{3}+135a^{2}bt^{2}+\big(108a^{4}+486ab^{2}\big)t-\big(243a^{3}b+1458b^{3}\big).

Moreover
\Delta{\left({\mathcal{E}^{W}}\right)}=\Delta{\left({E}\right)}{\left({\det A}\right)}^{3}
and
\det A=t^{4}+18at^{2}-108bt-27a^{2}
.

Observe that the
t
in the proposition is equal to
8t
in the previous sections.

Proof.

The proof boils down to computing the map
A
, which can be found in three steps. First map the tangent line to
\mathcal{E}
at
O
to the line at infinity. Next scale the
z
-coordinate so that the coefficient in front of
x^{3}
and
y^{2}z
are equal up to minus sign. Finally shift the
y
– resp. the
x
-coordinate so that the
xyz
,
yz^{2}
resp. the
x^{2}z
terms vanish.
∎

This proposition shows that

\displaystyle j{\left({\mathcal{E}}\right)}=1728\frac{4}{4a^{3}+27b^{2}}{\left({\frac{at^{4}-18bt^{3}-18a^{2}t^{2}+54abt-{\left({27a^{3}+243b^{2}}\right)}}{t^{4}+18at^{2}-108bt-27a^{2}}}\right)}^{3}.

6
Linear change of coordinates I

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
P_{i}\in{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}{\left({k}\right)}}
for
i=1,\dots,4
be points such that no three of them are collinear. If
Q_{i}\in{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}{\left({k}\right)}}
for
i=1,\dots,4
is another such set of points, then there exists a unique
A\in{\operatorname{PGL}_{3}}{\left({k}\right)}
such that
A{\left({P_{i}}\right)}=Q_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,4
.

This is a well-known result which is easily proved using some elementary linear algebra. Observe that an analogous result holds for two sets of
n+2
points in
{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}{\left({k}\right)}}
such that no
n+1
of them lie on a hyperplane.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve given by a Weierstrass equation defined over
k
. If
E{\left[{3}\right]}={\left\langle{S,T}\right\rangle}
, then any line in
{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}{\left({{\overline{k}}}\right)}}
contains at most two of the following points:
O
,
S
,
T
,
S+T
.

Proof.

Suppose that
L
is a line in
{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}{\left({{\overline{k}}}\right)}}
containing three of the points
O
,
S
,
T
and
S+T
. Denote these by
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
and
P_{3}
. Since
E
is given by a Weierstrass equation,
O
is a flex point, thus
P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{3}=O
. However this is impossible for the points mentioned above. Hence such a line
L
does not exist.
∎

Suppose that we are given two elliptic curves
E
and
E^{\prime}
as in the proposition above with
E{\left[{3}\right]}={\left\langle{S,T}\right\rangle}
and
E^{\prime}{\left[{3}\right]}={\left\langle{S^{\prime},T^{\prime}}\right\rangle}
, then Propositions
6.1
and
6.2
imply that there exists an
A\in{\operatorname{PGL}_{3}}{\left({{\overline{k}}}\right)}
such that
O\mapsto O^{\prime}
,
S\mapsto S^{\prime}
,
T\mapsto T^{\prime}
and
S+T\mapsto S^{\prime}+T^{\prime}
and that this
A
is unique.

7
Linear change of coordinates II

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
E
and
E^{\prime}
be elliptic curves given by a Weierstrass equation defined over
k
. If
\phi\colon E{\left[{3}\right]}\rightarrow E^{\prime}{\left[{3}\right]}
is an isomorphism which respects the Weil-pairings, then there exists some
t_{0}\in{{\mathbb{P}}^{1}{\left({{\overline{k}}}\right)}}
such that the fiber
E_{t_{0}}
of the Hesse pencil of
E
admits a linear change of coordinates
\Phi\colon E_{t_{0}}\rightarrow E^{\prime}
with
{\left.{\Phi}\right|_{E{\left[{3}\right]}}}=\phi
.

The essence of the proof of this proposition is the following: We determine the
t_{i}\in{{\mathbb{P}}^{1}{\left({{\overline{k}}}\right)}}
for which the
j
-invariant of
E_{t_{i}}
is equal to the
j
-invariant of
E^{\prime}
. For each of these
t_{i}
’s we obtain a number of linear changes of coordinates
E_{t_{i}}\rightarrow E
. A counting argument shows that
\phi
is the restriction of one of those maps. The following observation is used in the counting argument:

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
E
and
E^{\prime}
be elliptic curves. Then
24
out of the
48
isomorphisms
E{\left[{3}\right]}\rightarrow E^{\prime}{\left[{3}\right]}
respect the Weil-pairings.

Proof.

Let
S,T\in E{\left[{3}\right]}
be such that
E{\left[{3}\right]}={\left\langle{S,T}\right\rangle}
and
e_{3}{\left({S,T}\right)}=\zeta_{3}
with
\zeta_{3}
a fixed primitive third root of unity. Choose
S^{\prime},T^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}{\left[{3}\right]}
likewise. Since
E{\left[{3}\right]}
and
E^{\prime}{\left[{3}\right]}
are two-dimensional vector spaces over
{\mathbb{F}}_{3}
, there exists a bijection

\displaystyle{\operatorname{GL}_{2}}{\left({{\mathbb{F}}_{3}}\right)}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Iso}{\left({E{\left[{3}\right]},E^{\prime}{\left[{3}\right]}}\right)},

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\longmapsto\phi_{A}{\left({\alpha S+\beta T}\right)}={\left({\alpha a+\beta b}\right)}S^{\prime}+{\left({\alpha c+\beta d}\right)}T^{\prime}.

Notice that

\displaystyle e_{3}^{\prime}{\left({\phi_{A}{\left({S}\right)},\phi_{A}{\left({T}\right)}}\right)}=e_{3}^{\prime}{\left({aS^{\prime}+bT^{\prime},cS^{\prime}+dT^{\prime}}\right)}=e_{3}^{\prime}{\left({S^{\prime},T^{\prime}}\right)}^{ad-bc}=\zeta_{3}^{\det{A}}.

So
\phi_{A}
respects the Weil-pairings if and only if
\det{A}=1
, that is
A\in{\operatorname{SL}_{2}}{\left({{\mathbb{F}}_{3}}\right)}
. Now

9
Characteristic two

So far we assumed
k
to be a perfect field of characteristic different from two and three. There is a natural idea how to adapt the proof of Theorem

to characteristic two: replace the explicitly given Hesse pencil by what it actually describes, namely the pencil of cubics with the nine points of order

on the initial elliptic curve as base points. This was done by one of us in her bachelor’s project
[
15
]
, and we briefly describe the results here.

9.1

Any elliptic curve

over a field of characteristic

\displaystyle j(E)\neq 0\colon\ y^{2}+xy=x^{3}+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{6},\qquad\Delta=a_{6},\qquad j(E)=1/a_{6},

\displaystyle j(E)=0\colon\ y^{2}+a_{3}y=x^{3}+a_{4}x+a_{6},\qquad\Delta=a_{3}^{4},\qquad j(E)=0.

of degree 3 equals zero, as is easily verified. We will show that given an elliptic curve

over

E[3]
all have

as flex points, compare Corollary
2.2

[

, Glynn defines a Hessian for any curve

with

homogeneous of degree 3 (characteristic two). In fact our construction coincides with his, although we put more emphasis on how it is obtained from considering

[

published in 1915.

which has degree 12 and discriminant

10
Comparison with the literature

Theorem
1.1
is part of a more general problem: Given an elliptic curve
E
over a field
k
and an integer
n
, describe the universal family of elliptic curves
\mathcal{E}
such that for each member
\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}
the Galois representations on
E{\left[{n}\right]}
and
\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}{\left[{n}\right]}
are isomorphic and the isomorphism is symplectic. For various
n
explicit families are known in the literature.

In
[
12
]
Rubin and Silverberg construct for any elliptic curve over
{\mathbb{Q}}
such an explicit family for
n=3
and
n=5
. Their proofs are motivated by the theory of modular curves. Our Theorem
1.1
corresponds roughly to
[
12
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
12
, Remark 4.2]
.

Using invariant theory and a generalization of the classical Hesse pencil, Fisher in
[
6
]
describes such families for elliptic curves defined over a perfect field of characteristic not dividing
6n
with
n=2,3,4,5
. Theorem
1.1
is a special case of
[
6
, Theorem 13.2]
. It is unclear whether Fisher’s proof of
[
6
, Theorem 13.2]
can be adapted to the case of characteristic two. In
[
7
]
Fisher moreover treats the cases
n=7
and
n=11
.

The Hesse pencil is used by Kuwata in
[
10
]
. For any elliptic curve
E
over a number field he constructs two families of elliptic curves such that for each member the Galois representation on its 3-torsion is equivalent to the one on
E{\left[{3}\right]}
. In the first family the isomorphism of the 3-torsion groups is symplectic, whereas in the second family the isomorphism is anti-symplectic. The proofs use classical projective geometry and the classification of rational elliptic surfaces. Theorem
1.1
is essentially
[
10
, Theorem 4.2]
(although our proof is more detailed and totally elementary, and moreover we extend the result to characteristic two). Notice that the Weierstrass form of the Hesse pencil in
[
10
, Remark 4.4]
is the same as the one in Proposition
5.1
with
t
replaced by
t^{-1}
and the
x
and
y
coordinates scaled by some power of
t
.

An overview of results on the classical Hesse pencil is given by Artebani and Dolgachev in
[
2
]
.
