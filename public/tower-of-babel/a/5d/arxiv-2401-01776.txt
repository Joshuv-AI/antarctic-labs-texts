Maximal steady-state entanglement in autonomous quantum thermal machines
Shishir Khandelwal, Björn Annby-Andersson, Giovanni Francesco Diotallevi, Andreas Wacker, Armin Tavakoli

Abstract
We devise an autonomous quantum thermal machine consisting of three pairwise-interacting qubits, two of which are locally coupled to separate classical reservoirs. The machine operates autonomously, as it requires no time-coherent control, external driving or quantum bath engineering, and is instead propelled by a chemical potential bias between the reservoirs. Under ideal conditions, we show that this out-of-equilibrium system can deterministically generate a maximally entangled steady-state between two of the qubits, or in fact, any desired pure two-qubit entangled state, emerging as a dark state of the system. The entanglement production is also robust, such that nearly-maximally-entangled states can be generated well-away from the ideal regime of operation. Furthermore, we show that our machine architecture can be generalised to a configuration with 2 ​ n − 1 2n-1 qubits, in which only a potential bias and two-body interactions are sufficient to generate genuine multipartite maximally entangled steady states in the form of a W state of n n qubits.



Introduction.—
Quantum thermal machines are open systems of interacting quanta that harvest spontaneous interactions with thermal reservoirs to perform a designated task. These machines have been proposed as quantum mechanical counterparts to the classical thermal machines of the industrial age, for instance, for work extraction, heating, cooling and keeping time
Scovil and Schulz-DuBois 1959
;
Alicki 1979
;
Kosloff and Levy 2014
;
Mitchison 2019
;
Bhattacharjee and Dutta 2021
. In recent years, they have also been implemented in experiments
Roßnagel et al. 2014
;
Roßnagel et al. 2016
;
Maslennikov et al. 2019
;
Klatzow et al. 2019
;
Pearson et al. 2021
. However, quantum thermal machines can go further, and perform tasks that themselves are inherently quantum mechanical. The paradigmatic example of an inherently quantum task is the generation of entanglement. The possibility of generating entanglement via dissipation is well-known and has received much research interest in both theory and experiment
Diehl et al. 2008
;
Verstraete et al. 2009
;
Kastoryano et al. 2011
;
Krauter et al. 2011
;
Lin et al. 2013
;
Shankar et al. 2013
. When operating out-of-equilibrium, it can be achieved by external driving of the system
Mancilla et al. 2009
;
Rao and Mølmer 2013
;
Khandelwal et al. 2023
or engineering of the quantum reservoirs
Banerjee et al. 2010
;
Reiter et al. 2012
;
Tacchino et al. 2018
.

This progress spurred the question of identifying the minimal resources to generate steady-state entanglement in dissipative out-of-equilibrium systems. Specifically, is it possible to generate entanglement autonomously, i.e., with a time-independent Hamiltonian, no external work sources and no quantum bath engineering? Interestingly, an affirmative answer has been given. Two time-independently interacting qubits that are individually coupled to reservoirs of different temperature can end up in an entangled steady-state
Brask et al. 2015
, being propelled by a heat current through the system
Khandelwal et al. 2020
. Unfortunately, however, the generated entanglement is weak and unable to defy several notions of classicality
Bohr Brask et al. 2022
.
In the quest to amplify it, several works have considered supplementing the system with some additional, non-autonomous, resources. Examples include using population inversion in a reservoir
Tacchino et al. 2018
;
Bohr Brask et al. 2022
, heralding the steady-state
Tavakoli et al. 2018
;
Tavakoli et al. 2020
and real-time feedback control
Diotallevi et al. 2023
. Contrasting this, some amplification of the entanglement has been possible in the fully autonomous setting
Man et al. 2019
. In particular, it has been shown that by leveraging a voltage bias, instead of a temperature bias, entanglement generation can be amplified
Prech et al. 2023
. Nevertheless, none of these approaches have been able to deterministically generate maximal, or even nearly maximal, steady-state entanglement, and it remains an open question whether this is possible.

In this Letter, we provide an answer to this question by constructing a thermal machine that can generate any two-qubit pure entangled state, including the maximally entangled state, using only minimal autonomous resources. The machine exploits a chemical potential bias between two fermionic reservoirs, and involves three qubits subject to Coulomb repulsion and pairwise flip-flop interaction. Our machine, illustrated in Figure
1
(a), thus uses the third qubit as a mediator for entanglement generation. Furthermore, to showcase the relevance of the machine’s performance beyond the ideal regime of operation, we investigate the robustness of the steady-state entanglement. We find that nearly maximally entangled states can be generated at reasonable Coulomb forces and potential biases, and that the time of convergence to the steady-state is relevant to modern quantum technologies, specifically it is of the order of microseconds. Subsequently, motivated by conceptual interest, we go further and investigate whether multi-qubit states with maximal entanglement can also be autonomously generated. For this, we insist on not employing complex many-body Hamiltonians, but only time-independent two-body Hamiltonians. We identify a generalisation of our machine architecture, which features
2n-1
qubits with a number of two-body interactions that scales linearly in
n
, and prove that a chemical potential bias can be exploited to render
n
of these qubits in a W-type steady-state.

Generation of arbitrary pure entangled states.—
We now show that there exists an ideal parameter regime in which the steady-state solution to Eq. (
2
), when reduced to qubits 1 and 2, can correspond to any pure entangled state (up to local unitaries). As in Ref.
Prech et al. 2023
, we consider the limit in which

\displaystyle U\to\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\mu\to\infty,\,\,\text{with}\,\,\quad U/\mu\to\infty.

(4)

The first limit ensures that whenever there is already an excitation in the system, the fermions in either reservoir cannot overcome the large Coulomb interaction to excite the system further. On the level of rates, this translates to
\gamma_{jpq}^{+}=0
whenever
p+q\geq 1
. This eliminates the possibility of double or triple excitation in the three-qubit steady state. The second limit ensures that when there is no excitation in the system, the population in reservoir 1 is effectively inverted, i.e.,
n_{F}(\varepsilon,\mu,T_{1})=1
. The last limit is important to ensure that no double or triple excitations are possible at any point in the evolution of the machine. The system is therefore, extremely out-of-equilibrium, with the left reservoir (coupled with qubit 1) at an extremely high bias and the right reservoir (coupled with qubit 3) at a low or zero bias. The only pathway for an excitation to leave the system is through qubit 3 and the right reservoir. We therefore refer to this qubit as the sink qubit. In the limits (
4
), the only relevant transitions are induced by the jump operators
L_{100}
,
L_{300}
and
L_{300}^{\dagger}
. A matrix form of the Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}
in these limits can be found in the Supp. Mat. Sec. A. The steady-state of the machine is the unique eigenstate of
\mathcal{L}
with eigenvalue zero. The situation can be intuitively understood using the flow chart shown in Fig.
1
(b). As double and triple excitations are prohibited, only four classical states (
\ket{000}
,
\ket{100}
,
\ket{010}
,
\ket{001}
) are relevant in the evolution of the machine. Two of these states,
\ket{000}
and
\ket{001}
are directly coupled to reservoir transitions and play no role at long times. The steady state is pure and a superposition of
\ket{100}
and
\ket{010}
,

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{\text{ss}} }=\left(\hskip-2.84544pt\frac{g_{23}}{\sqrt{g^{2}_{23}+g^{2}_{13}}}\ket{10}-\frac{g_{13}}{\sqrt{g^{2}_{23}+g^{2}_{13}}}\ket{01}\hskip-2.84544pt\right)\otimes\ket{0},

(5)

which is actually a 1-particle energy eigenstate
of the Hamiltonian (
1
). This state can be written as
\ket{\Psi_{\text{ss}}}=\left(\cos\theta\ket{10}-\sin\theta\ket{01}\right)\otimes\ket{0}
, with
\theta\coloneqq\arctan\left(g_{13}/g_{23}\right)
. Clearly, we obtain a partially entangled pure state between qubits 1 and 2, while qubit 3 is pushed into its ground state. The coefficients of the superposed states depend solely on the couplings between the qubits. Setting
g_{13}=g_{23}
, we obtain a maximally entangled state in the form of the singlet
\ket{\Psi^-}=\left(\ket{10}-\ket{01}\right)/\sqrt{2}
. Importantly, these results hold for any coupling (within the validity of the master equation) between the qubits and reservoirs and any temperatures of the reservoirs.

While Lindbladian evolution typically decoheres an initially pure state into a mixed state, it is generally possible to obtain a pure steady-state provided that certain conditions are satisfied. Firstly, the state
\ket{\Psi_{\text{ss}}}
is invariant under the action of the jump operators
L_{100}
,
L_{300}
and
L_{300}^{\dagger}
which are relevant for the evolution. In other words,
\ket{\Psi_{\text{ss}}}
is unaffected by the dissipators in Eq. (
2
) that remain after applying the limits (
4
). Secondly, this state is an eigenstate of the effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}=H-i\left(L^{\dagger}_{100}L_{100}+L^{\dagger}_{300}L_{300}+L_{300}L^{\dagger}_{300}\right)/2
. These properties here ensure that
\ket{\Psi_{\text{ss}}}
is the unique steady state of
\mathcal{L}
under the considered limits
Yamamoto 2005
;
Kraus et al. 2008
. We note that even though the initial and steady states of the system are pure, at intermediate times, the state can be mixed.

Current at ideal operation.—
The steady state
\ket{\Psi_{\text{ss}}}
has a peculiar property by virtue of the third qubit always being in the ground state, regardless of the couplings. Since at long times, the third qubit is completely depopulated, no excitation can travel into the right reservoir. Moreover, due to the large chemical potential, no excitation can travel back into the left reservoir. As a result, there cannot be any current between the reservoirs in the steady state despite the machine being heavily out-of-equilibrium. Therefore,
\ket{\Psi_{\text{ss}}}
is a dark or non-conducting steady-state of the machine.

In Fig.
2
(a), we plot the energy current as a function of time with the three qubits initially in the ground state
\ket{000}
. Under the limits (
4
), the average energy current can be defined as
J(t)\coloneqq\left(Q_{1}(t)-Q_{3}(t)\right)/2
with,

\displaystyle Q_{j}(t)=\text{Tr}\left\{H\left(\gamma_{j00}^{+}\mathcal{D}\left[L_{j00}\right]\rho+\gamma_{j00}^{-}\mathcal{D}\big[L^{\dagger}_{j00}\big]\rho\right)\right\}.

(6)

As expected, we find that that the initially non-zero energy current drops to zero. We note that a deviation from the ideal limits (
4

The steady state in Eq. (
5

\ket{100}

\ket{010}

\ket{010}

\ket{100}

Time-scale and entanglement generation away from ideal operation.—
Although the maximally entangled state can be obtained independently of the couplings and temperatures (as long as
g_{13}=g_{23}
), the couplings to the reservoirs determine the time-scale at which the steady state is reached. Specifically, the larger the coupling, the shorter the time-scale. In Fig.
2
(b), we plot the fidelity of
\rho(t)
with the state
\ket{\Psi^-}\otimes\ket{0}
, with the system initially in the ground state
\ket{000}
. Choosing the natural frequency of the qubits as
1
GHz, which is, for example, the relevant scale for state-of-the-art superconducting platforms
Kjaergaard et al. 2020
, we find that the steady state can be reached within a few microseconds. The relaxation time scale is determined by the Liouvillian eigenvalue having the largest (smallest negative) real part. This is controlled specifically by the system-reservoir couplings. Therefore, this time-scale is the same for any initial state.

In Fig.
3
, we investigate the quality of the generated entanglement with respect to changes in the system’s parameters, i.e., away from the limits in Eq. (
4
). In such a situation, all possible classical states of the three-qubit system are involved in the dynamics, including at long-times. The solution must therefore take into account all possible transitions induced by the jump operators
L_{jpq}
and
L_{jpq}^{\dagger}
in the general Lindblad equation (
2
). As a measure of entanglement, we use the concurrence, which for the state of qubits 1 and 2 can be written as
C(\rho)=2\,\text{max}\{0,\lvert c\rvert-\sqrt{p_{11}p_{00}}\}
, where
c
is the coherence corresponding to element
\outerproduct{01}{10}
and
p_{11}(p_{00})
is the probability corresponding to the state
\outerproduct{11}{11}(\outerproduct{00}{00})
. We find that large entanglement can be created with reasonable Coulomb interaction and chemical potentials. As expected from the previous discussion, to generate a large amount of entanglement, we also find that
U
should be chosen to be sufficiently larger than
\mu
. For instance, choosing
U=15\varepsilon
and
\mu=8\varepsilon
yields a concurrence greater than 0.99, while
\mu=15\varepsilon
with the same Coulomb interaction yields only 0.25. This is due to the presence of double excitations coming from the left reservoir when the Coulomb interaction is not large compared with the chemical potential.

Multipartite entanglement generation.—
So far, we have shown how to generate two-qubit maximally entangled states in a three-qubit autonomous thermal machine. A natural question is whether this scheme can be extended to produce maximally entangled states of three or more qubits. It is important to note that adding a fourth qubit in the system in Fig.
1
and coupling it to the sink qubit does not produce a genuinely multipartite entangled steady state for the three, non-sink, qubits. Instead, the new qubit requires its own, additional, sink qubit. That is, to generate maximal genuine multipartite entanglement between three qubits, we must introduce two additional qubits where one serves as a sink.

This architecture can be naturally extended to produce
n
-qubit maximally entangled states (specifically W states) in a
(2n-1)
-qubit autonomous thermal machine. The machine is illustrated in Fig.
4
. For each additional qubit to be entangled, the scheme requires one additional sink qubit (shown as dashed-blue). The architecture can be seen as many “triangles” of three qubits with pairwise two-body interactions, but all sharing exactly one qubit that is coupled to the high-bias reservoir. Each sink qubit is in turn coupled to its own reservoir. Within each triangle of three qubits, we consider an electrostatic interaction between every pair and a flip-flop interaction between the sink qubit and each of the other two qubits, in complete analogy with the original machine in Fig.
1
.

Under the same limits as the two-qubit case, i.e., large potential bias and large Coulomb repulsion, where the latter is much larger than the former, the steady state of this scheme is pure and maximally entangled. Specifically, it is

\ket{W_n}=\hskip-2.84544pt\frac{1}{\hskip-0.85355pt\sqrt{\hskip-1.42271ptn}}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\ket{100\ldots 0}-\ket{010\ldots 0 }-\ket{000\ldots 1}\hskip-1.42271pt\right)\otimes\ket{\bar{0}_{n-1}}

(7)

where
\ket{\bar 0_{k}}\coloneqq\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\cdot\cdot\cdot\otimes\ket{0}
, the ground state of
k
qubits.
\ket{W_n}
denotes a W state in the space of the first
n
qubits and a ground state in the next
n-1
qubits (see Fig.
4
for the enumeration). For simplicity, in the figure, we have chosen all temperatures and all qubit-reservoir couplings in the figures to be equal. However, this is not a necessary condition to produce a
\ket{W_n}
state and these couplings and temperatures can be completely arbitrary. Moreover, in analogy with the three-qubit machine, the general condition on the inter-qubit couplings to produce the
\ket{W_n}
state is
g_{1,n+j}=g_{1+j,n+j}
, where
g_{ij}
represents the coupling between the
i
th and
j
th qubits. If this condition is not satisfied, a partially entangled W-like state is obtained at long times. More details can be found in the Supp. Mat. Sec. B.

Discussion.—
In this Letter, we have introduced an architecture for autonomous thermal machines to produce maximal entanglement between qubits. While earlier two-qubit proposals are limited to noisy entanglement
Brask et al. 2015
;
Bohr Brask et al. 2022
;
Prech et al. 2023
, our three-qubit machine is the minimal thermal machine that can autonomously produce a Bell state. This resolves the question of whether there exists an elementary limitation for out-of-equilibrium steady state entanglement generation with autonomous resources. Importantly, with only thermal resources and local two-body interactions, the generalized architecture can render any number of qubits genuinely multipartite entangled within the W-state class. It is an interesting question whether the same is possible for arbitrary pure entanglement, in particular the Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger states.

Although our analytical results have been obtained using the Lindblad equation (
2
) with local coupling, we have numerically confirmed them with the second-order von Neumann approach
Pedersen and Wacker 2005
;
Pedersen et al. 2007
, using the QMEQ package
Kiršanskas et al. 2017
, which accounts for higher orders in the system-reservoir couplings.

(1,n+j)

(1,1+j)

(1+j,n+j)

\displaystyle H_{1}=\varepsilon\sum_{i_{m}\in\{0,1\}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}i_{j}\right)\outerproduct{i_1\,i_2\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}{i_1\,i_2\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}.

H_{2}
is the Hamiltonian due to Coulomb interactions,

\displaystyle H_{2}=\sum_{i_{m}\in\{0,1\}}U\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}i_{j}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}i_{j}-1\right)\outerproduct{i_1i_2\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}{i_1i_2\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}.

(20)

H_{3}
is the interaction between qubit 1 and the sink qubits
n+j
,

\displaystyle H_{3}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i_{m}=\{0,1\}}g_{1,n+j}\bigg(

\displaystyle\outerproduct{1\,i_2\,i_3\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{n+j-1}0\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}{0\,i_2\,i_3\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{n+j-1}1\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}+\text{h.c.}\bigg).

(21)

Lastly,
H_{4}
is the interaction between qubits
1+j
and the sink qubits
n+j
,

\hskip-14.22636ptH_{4}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i_{m}=\{0,1\}}\hskip-11.38092ptg_{1+j,n+j}\left(\outerproduct{i_1\,i_2\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{j}\,1\,i_{j+2}\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{n+j-1}\,0\,i_{n+j+1} \cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}{i_1\,i_2\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{j}\,0\,i_{j+2}\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{n+j-1}\,1\,i_{n+j+1} \cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}+\text{h.c.}\right)

(22)

The total Hamiltonian is then
H=\sum_{j=1}^{4}H_{j}
. We now make the assumptions
U\to\infty
,
\mu\to\infty
and
U/\mu\to\infty
. For practical purposes, these can be relaxed to
U\gg T
,
\mu\gg T
and
U\gg\mu
. These conditions have the following consequences on the evolution,

1.

When there is no excitation in the system (i.e., the system is in the ground state), the high-bias reservoir can only transfer excitations into the system.

2.

When there is already an excitation in the system, the high-bias reservoir cannot overcome the high Coulomb repulsion to transfer another excitation.

3.

The excitations can only leave through the low-bias reservoirs.

The above properties ensure that the doubly occupied subspace of the
2n-1
-qubit state is completely eliminated and the transport has a unidirectional property (excitations from the high-bias reservoir travel into the low-bias reservoir). We then have the following jump operators,

\displaystyle L_{1,+}=\outerproduct{1\bar 0_{2n-2}}{\bar 0_{2n-1}}

(23)

\displaystyle L_{1,-}=\outerproduct{0\,i_2\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}{1\,i_2\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}

\displaystyle L_{p,-}=\outerproduct{i_1\,i_2\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{p-1}\,0\,i_{p+1}\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}{i_1\,i_2\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{p-1}\,1\,i_{p+1}\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}

\displaystyle L_{p,+}=\outerproduct{i_1\,i_2\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{p-1}\,1\,i_{p+1}\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}}{i_1\,i_2\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{p-1}\,0\,i_{p+1}\cdot\cdot\cdot i_{2n-1}},

where
p\in\{n+1,n+2,\cdot\cdot\cdot,2n-1\}
,
i_{m}\in\{0,1\}
and
\bar{0}_{k}\coloneqq\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\cdot\cdot\cdot\otimes\ket{0}
is the ground state of
k
qubits. These are the only possible transitions within the setup. We further assume that the initial state of the system is at most singly excited. This allows us to keep only the operators for which all
i_{m}
s are zero. The general case can be treated in a similar, albeit more tedious manner, and leads to the same steady state. We therefore have the Lindblad equation,

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}=-i\left[H,\rho\right]+\gamma_{1}^{+}\mathcal{D}\left[L_{1,+}\right]\rho+\gamma_{1}^{-}\mathcal{D}\left[L_{1,-}\right]\rho+\gamma_{p}^{-}\mathcal{D}\left[L_{p,-}\right]\rho+\gamma_{p}^{+}\mathcal{D}\left[L_{p,+}\right]\rho,

(24)

where
\mathcal{D}[A]\rho\coloneqq A\rho A^{\dagger}-\left\{A^{\dagger}A,\rho\right\}/2
. The rates are determined by the Fermi-Dirac distribution of the reservoirs,
\gamma_{j}^{+}=\gamma_{j}n_{F}\left(\varepsilon,\mu,T\right)
and
\gamma_{j}^{-}=\gamma_{j}\left(1-n_{F}\left(\varepsilon,\mu,T\right)\right)
, with
n_{F}\left(\varepsilon,\mu,T\right)=1/\left(e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/T}+1\right)
. With the above mentioned assumptions, we have that

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}^{+}=\gamma_{1},\,\,\text{and}\,\,\gamma_{1}^{-}=0.

(25)

We finally have the Lindblad equation,

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}_{n}\rho=-i\left[H,\rho\right]+\gamma_{1}\mathcal{D}\left[L_{1,+}\right]\rho+\gamma_{p}^{-}\mathcal{D}\left[L_{p,-}\right]\rho+\gamma_{p}^{+}\mathcal{D}\left[L_{p,+}\right]\rho.

(26)

The steady state of the system satisfies
\mathcal{L}_{n}\rho_{\text{ss}}^{n}=0
.
For arbitrary
n
and under the limits (
4
), the machine in Fig.
4
produces a pure, partially entangled W-like steady state between qubits
1
-
n
, while the remaining qubits are pushed to their ground state.

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi^n_\text{ss}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\left(\ket{10\cdot\cdot\cdot 0}-\alpha_{1}\ket{01\cdot\cdot\cdot 0}-\alpha_{2}\ket{001\cdot\cdot\cdot 0}-\cdot\cdot\cdot-\alpha_{n-1}\ket{0\cdot\cdot\cdot 01}\right)\otimes\ket{\bar 0_{n-1}},

(27)

where,

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}=\frac{g_{1,n+j}}{g_{1+j,n+j}}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=1+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{j}^{2}.

(28)

For
n=2
, the expression obtained can be rewritten as Eq. (
5
). Clearly, if all
\alpha_{j}=1
, we obtain a W state between the first
n
qubits and the steady state takes the form,

\displaystyle\ket{W_n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\ket{100\ldots 0}-\ket{010\ldots 0 }-\ket{000\ldots 1}\right)\otimes\ket{\bar{0}_{n-1}}.

(29)

We denote the corresponding density matrix by
\rho_{\tiny{W_{n}}}\coloneqq\outerproduct{W_n}{W_n}
.

.1
Proof of steady state

We now show that
\mathcal{L}_{n}\rho_{\tiny{W_{n}}}
is the steady state of the general scheme, i.e., that
\mathcal{L}_{n}\rho_{W_{n}}=0
, when certain conditions are satisfied between the couplings.

.1.1
Dissipators

1.

The only dissipator corresponding to qubit 1 is
L_{1,+}=\outerproduct{1\bar 0_{2n-2}}{\bar 0_{2n-1}}
. We have that
L_{1,+}\ket{W_n}=0
because it involves the overlap between a ground state
\ket{\bar 0_{2n-1}}
with
\ket{W_n}
which is a superposition of singly occupied states.

2.

The first dissipator for qubit
p
,
L_{p,+}=\outerproduct{\bar 0_{p-1}1\bar 0_{2n-p-1}}{\bar 0_{2n-1}}
, satisfies
L_{p,+}\ket{W_n}=0
for the same reason as above.

3.

The second dissipator for qubit
p
is
L_{p,-}=\outerproduct{\bar 0_{2n-1}}{\bar 0_{p-1}1\bar 0_{2n-p-1}}
. Note that in
\ket{\bar 0_{p-1}1\bar 0_{2n-p-1}}
, the occupation lies in the sink subspace (as
p>n
). On the other hand, the sink subspace of
\ket{W_n}
is completely in the ground state. Therefore, we have that
L_{p,-}\ket{W_n}=0
.

The above observations imply that the state
\ket{W_n}
is unaffected by dissipation. Mathematically,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}[L_{1,+}]\rho_{W_{n}}=\mathcal{D}[L_{p,+}]\rho_{W_{n}}=\mathcal{D}[L_{p,-}]\rho_{W_{n}}=0.

(30)

.1.2
Hamiltonian

It can be easily shown that in the commutator
[H,\rho_{W_{n}}]
,
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
have zero contribution. Therefore, we focus on
H_{3}
and
H_{4}
. Here, only the terms with at most single occupation in the Hamiltonian have a non-zero contribution in the commutator
[H,\rho]
. Therefore, without loss of generality, we can eliminate all other terms and write the Hamiltonian as,

\displaystyle H_{3}^{(1)}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}g_{1,n+j}\outerproduct{1\bar 0_{2n-2}}{\bar 0_{n+j-1}1\bar 0_{n-j-1}},\quad H_{3}^{(2)}=H_{3}^{(1)\dagger}

(31)

\displaystyle H_{4}^{(1)}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}g_{1+j,n+j}\outerproduct{\bar 0_{j}1\bar 0_{2n-j-2}}{\bar 0_{n+j-1}1\bar 0_{n-j-1}},\quad H_{3}^{(2)}=H_{3}^{(1)\dagger},

with
H_{j}=H_{j}^{(1)}+H_{j}^{(2)}
(
j=3,4
).The terms in the commutator can now be treated separately. First,
For the term,
H_{3}^{(1)}\rho_{W_{n}}
, we note that the sink subspace in
\ket{W_n}
is unoccupied (i.e., the sink qubits are in the ground state). Evidently, its overlap with
\ket{\bar 0_{n+j-1}1\bar 0_{n-j-1}}
(which has a singly occupied sink subspace) is necessarily zero, implying that
H_{3}^{(1)}\rho_{W_{n}}=0
. For the same reason,
\rho_{W_{n}}H_{3}^{(2)}=0
. Overall, the contribution of
H_{3}
takes the form,

\displaystyle[H_{3},\rho_{W_{n}}]

\displaystyle=H_{3}^{(2)}\rho_{W_{n}}-\rho_{W_{n}}H_{3}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}g_{1,n+j}\left(\outerproduct{\bar 0_{i-1}1\bar 0_{2n-i-1}}{\bar 0_{n+j-1}1\bar 0_{n-j-1}}-\outerproduct{\bar 0_{n+j-1}1\bar 0_{n-j-1}}{\bar 0_{i-1}1\bar 0_{2n-i-1}}\right).

(32)

In a similar manner, it can be shown that,

\displaystyle[H_{4},\rho_{W_{n}}]

\displaystyle=H_{4}^{(2)}\rho_{W_{n}}-\rho_{W_{n}}H_{4}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}g_{1+j,n+j}\left(\outerproduct{\bar 0_{n+j-1}1\bar 0_{n-j-1}}{\bar 0_{i-1}1\bar 0_{2n-i-1}}-\outerproduct{\bar 0_{i-1}1\bar 0_{2n-i-1}}{\bar 0_{n+j-1}1\bar 0_{n-j-1}}\right).

(33)

Finally, the full commutator simplifies to

\displaystyle[H,\rho_{W_{n}}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left(g_{1+j,n+j}-g_{1,n+j}\right)\left(\outerproduct{\bar 0_{n+j-1}1\bar 0_{n-j-1}}{\bar 0_{i-1}1\bar 0_{2n-i-1}}-\outerproduct{\bar 0_{i-1}1\bar 0_{2n-i-1}}{\bar 0_{n+j-1}1\bar 0_{n-j-1}}\right).

(34)

Overall, we have that
[H,\rho_{W_{n}}]=0
, when the couplings satisfy
g_{1,n+j}=g_{1+j,n+j}
. Therefore, when this condition is true, we have
\mathcal{L}\outerproduct{W_n}{W_n}=0
. In other words,
\ket{W_n}
is the steady state of the Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}_{n}
. More generally, it can be proven on similar lines that when this condition is not satisfied, the pure and partially entangled state
\ket{\Psi_{\text{ss}}^n}
, given in Eq. (
27
), is the steady state.
