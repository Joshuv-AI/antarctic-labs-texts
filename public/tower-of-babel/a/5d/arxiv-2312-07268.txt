A space–time continuous and coercive formulation for the wave equation
Paolo Bignardi, Andrea Moiola

Abstract
We propose a new space–time variational formulation for wave equation initial–boundary value problems. The key property is that the formulation is coercive (sign-definite) and continuous in a norm stronger than H 1 ​ ( Q ) H^{1}(Q) , Q Q being the space–time cylinder. Coercivity holds for constant-coefficient impedance cavity problems posed in star-shaped domains, and for a class of impedance–Dirichlet problems. The formulation is defined using simple Morawetz multipliers and its coercivity is proved with elementary analytical tools, following earlier work on the Helmholtz equation. The formulation can be stably discretised with any H 2 ​ ( Q ) H^{2}(Q) -conforming discrete space, leading to quasi-optimal space–time Galerkin schemes. Several numerical experiments show the excellent properties of the method. Keywords: Wave equation, Variational formulation, Coercivity, Sign-deﬁniteness, Morawetz multiplier, Morawetz identity, Quasi-optimality, Galerkin method, Finite element method Mathematics Subject Classification: 35L05, 65M60, 65M15

Introduction

1.1
Space–time methods for wave problems

Most numerical schemes for the approximation of initial–boundary value problems (IBVPs) for evolution PDEs rely on separate discretisations of the space and time variables, i.e. either the method of lines or Rothe’s method.

Space–time methods
, instead, consist of simultaneous discretisations of both variables.
Even if they may require the solution of large algebraic systems, space–time schemes can be advantageous in terms of local mesh refinement, adaptivity, parallelisation, treatment of moving boundaries and interfaces, and to obtain accurate approximations at all time instants.
While the first methods of this kind date to the late 1960’s
[
13
,
33
]
,
space–time schemes were studied extensively only more recently; see e.g.
[
21
,
22
]
for recent overviews.

Restricting ourselves to linear transient
wave
problems, we note that the majority of space–time methods belong to the discontinous-Galerkin (DG) family, e.g.
[
28
]
, or require discontinuous test functions, e.g.
[
12
]
.
Fewer space–time
conforming
Galerkin methods have been proposed: e.g. the stabilised FEM and isogeometric schemes in
[
11
,
34
,
39
]
,
the formulation involving a trial-to-test “transformation operator” in
[
35
, sect. 5]
,
the first-order systems in
[
1
]
, and the first-order system of least squares (FOSLS) in
[
14
]
.
We also mention the method proposed in
[
18
]
, which is conforming with respect to an ultra-weak variational formulation, and uses
C^{1}
-continuous test and discontinuous trial functions.

Most of these formulations (an exception is the FOSLS in
[
14
]
) impose strong constraints on the discrete spaces in order to obtain stable methods.
For instance,
[
35
, sect. 5]
requires piecewise multi-linear (
\mathbb{Q}^{1}
) spaces on tensor-product meshes satisfying a CFL condition;
the stabilised formulations in
[
34
,
39
]
and
[
11
]
apply to tensor-product spline spaces (piecewise
\mathbb{Q}^{p}
with global
C^{k}
continuity), but numerical experiments show that
[
34
,
39
]
is unconditionally stable only for spaces with
C^{0}
regularity in time (and not for
C^{1}
or higher), while
[
11
]
only for spaces with highest regularity (
k=p-1
).
These are strong limitations if one wants, for instance, to locally adapt (either a priori or a posteriori) the computational mesh or the discrete space to achieve better accuracy.

Ideally, one would like to have a space–time variational problem similar to the classical formulation of the Poisson–Dirichlet boundary value problem, for which any
H^{1}_{0}
-conforming discrete space gives well-posed, stable, and quasi-optimal Galerkin schemes, thanks to Lax–Milgram theorem and Céa’s lemma.
This holds because the classical Poisson variational formulation is coercive (also called sign-definite, or elliptic).
On the other hand, the standard space–time variational formulation for the wave equation is not even inf-sup stable, see
[
36
, Thm. 1.1]
.
At a first glance, finding a space–time variational formulation for wave IBVPs that are continuous and coercive in the same norm, to ensure the applicability of Lax–Milgram and Céa, without resorting to least-squares schemes, seems hopeless.
However, also for the Helmholtz equation (i.e. the acoustic wave equation in frequency domain
\Delta u+k^{2}u=f
, for large
k>0
) this might appear impossible, but a coercive formulation has been proposed in
[
26
]
and implemented in
[
9
]
.
This is possible thanks to the use of Morawetz multipliers, which are special test functions that have been used to analyse wave problems, prove energy estimates and describe the solution decay, since the work of Cathleen S. Morawetz in the 1960’s
[
29
,
30
]
.

1.2
A coercive formulation that uses Morawetz multipliers

The goal of this paper is to use techniques similar to those in
[
26
]
to derive a space–time variational formulation of some IBVPs that is continuous and coercive in a given norm.

The IBVPs considered are the impedance problem for the acoustic wave equation
{\partial^{2}_{tt}}{u}-c^{2}\Delta u=f
with constant wave speed
c
, and a mixed impedance–Dirichlet problem for the same PDE.
The impedance condition
{\partial_{\bm{n}}}u+(\theta c)^{-1}{\partial_{t}}{u}={g_{\mathrm{I}}}
is the simplest absorbing boundary condition in acoustics, and corresponds to imposing that the fluid normal velocity on an obstacle is proportional to the acoustic pressure.

The formulation is obtained by integrating by parts and choosing as test function the linear-coefficient, first-order multiplier
{\mathcal{M}}v({\bm{x}},t):=-\xi{\bm{x}}\cdot\nabla v({\bm{x}},t)+\beta(t-T^{*}){\partial_{t}}v({\bm{x}},t)
, where
\xi,\beta,T^{*}
are real parameters.
The formulation, defined in (
28
)–(
29
) and (
33
) in the pure impedance case, only involves integrals (on the space–time cylinder
Q
and parts of its boundary) of trial and test functions, their partial derivatives, volume and boundary data, together with affine coefficients.

Theorem
5.4
and Proposition
5.5
prove the coercivity and the continuity of the formulation in a norm (
30
) stronger than (space–time)
H^{1}
.
All estimates are proved using only elementary vector calculus tools (such as Cauchy–Schwarz and weighted inequalities, the knowledge of the sign of the normal component of the position vector on the space boundary) and are explicit in all parameters.
We refer to Remark
5.2
for a more delicate point concerning the function space in which the variational problem is posed; this is related to the density of smooth functions in the graph space associated to the coercivity norm.

In the pure impedance case, coercivity holds for bounded, Lipschitz, star-shaped domains, see Assumption
5.3
.
More generally, star-shaped impedance cavities with star-shaped Dirichlet obstacles are allowed, see Assumption
6.1
.
This kind of restrictions is common when Morawetz multipliers are used and is related to the absence of trapped rays; see e.g.
[
26
, sect. 6]
and
[
29
]

This formulation can be stably discretised with
any

H^{2}(Q)
-conforming finite element space giving well-posed and quasi-optimal schemes.
This is the ideal starting point to develop efficient solvers, matrix-compression techniques, and adaptive algorithms.
We demonstrate the excellent numerical performance of the formulation with several experiments involving cubic spline spaces: we propose simple recipes for the choice of the numerical parameters, we show that the method is unconditionally stable and parameter-robust, it provides errors close to the best-approximation, it has little dissipation and it can approximate rough solutions.
The Matlab code implemented is freely available online.
More extensive and challenging experiments will be reported in a subsequent work.

1.3
Outline of the paper

In section
2
we state the IBVP under consideration, study in detail its well-posedness in the impedance case and prove a regularity result on its solution, adapting a Faedo–Galerkin technique from
[
20
]
.

In section
3
, we propose an abstract framework for coercive formulations arising from multiplier techniques.
This framework includes both what is proposed in the remainder of the paper for the wave equation, and the coercive formulation for the Helmholtz equation in
[

]
.

Section
4
contains the pointwise and integral identities related to the Morawetz multipliers.
These identities are proved using only the elementary vector calculus product rules and integration by parts.

Section
5
contains the main results for the interior impedance problem.
In particular, it contains the variational formulation and the corresponding norm, the proofs of the (explicit) coercivity and continuity bounds for the bilinear and the linear form.
In section

we describe some consequences that concern Galerkin discretisations: we estimate the quasi-optimality constant and the Galerkin matrix condition number, show that conforming discrete spaces essentially coincide with

-conforming ones, and study the energy-dissipation properties of the formulation.
Since a possible obvious criticism of space–time methods concerns the size of the linear systems involved, in Remark
5.10
we also compare the number of degrees of freedom needed to achieve a given accuracy against those required by a time-stepping scheme.

Section
6
extends the previous definitions and results to the more general case involving Dirichlet conditions on part of the boundary (e.g. a sound-soft scatterer in an impedance cavity).

In section
7
we show several numerical experiments for a cubic-spline discretisation of the proposed formulation.
We study the sensitivity of the formulation on some numerical parameters in its definitions, the optimality of the convergence rates, the approximation of smooth and singular solutions, the sharpness of the quasi-optimality estimates, the approximation of the solution energy.

Finally, in section
8
we draw some conclusions and list several possible extensions of the proposed formulations and open problems.

\displaystyle\|{\partial^{2}_{tt}}u^{N}\|^{2}_{\Omega_{t}}+c^{2}\|\nabla{\partial_{t}}u^{N}\|^{2}_{\Omega_{t}}\leq C\big(\|{\partial_{t}}f\|^{2}_{Q}+\|{\partial^{2}_{tt}}u^{N}\|^{2}_{\Omega_{0}}+c^{2}\|{\partial_{t}}\nabla u^{N}\|^{2}_{\Omega_{0}}+c^{3}\theta\|{\partial_{t}}g\|^{2}_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{I}}}\big)

(9)

where

is independent of

.
The norms of

and
{\partial_{t}}{u^{N}}(\cdot,0)=w_{1}^{N}
can be bounded uniformly w.r.t.

using
that they converge to

and

, respectively.
Moreover,
\|{\partial^{2}_{tt}}u^{N}\|_{\Omega_{0}}
can be uniformly bounded with respect to

by testing (

) against
{\partial^{2}_{tt}}u^{N}
, evaluating at

\|{\partial^{2}_{tt}}u^{N}\|_{\Omega_{0}}^{2}=c^{2}\big(\Delta u^{N}(\cdot,0)+f(\cdot,0),{\partial^{2}_{tt}}u^{N}(\cdot,0)\big)_{\Omega}+c^{2}\big({\partial_{\bm{n}}}u^{N}(\cdot,0)+(c\theta)^{-1}{\partial_{t}}u^{N}(\cdot,0)-{g_{\mathrm{I}}}(\cdot,0),{\partial^{2}_{tt}}u^{N}(\cdot,0)\big)_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}.

The initial conditions of the ODE system, and the relation (

and

\|{\partial^{2}_{tt}}u^{N}\|_{\Omega_{0}}^{2}=c^{2}\big(\Delta w_{0}^{N}+f(\cdot,0),{\partial^{2}_{tt}}u^{N}(\cdot,0)\big)_{\Omega}+c^{2}\big(\Delta z_{0}^{N}-z_{0}^{N},{\partial^{2}_{tt}}u^{N}(\cdot,0)\big).

Using Young’s inequality and the fact that

is independent of

Then from (

where

,
\|{\partial_{t}}{g_{\mathrm{I}}}\|_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{I}}}
,
\|u_{1}\|_{H^{1}({\Omega_{0}})}
,
\|u_{0}\|_{H^{2}({\Omega_{0}})}
,

, therefore

.
Similarly,

Then, as in the proof of the existence theorem, the assertion of Part (i) follows using compactness and showing that the limit of the sequence

Part (ii): elliptic problem at fixed time.

We now follow the strategy of

.
Since

(10)

We recall that, following the notation used so far, each of these integrals depends on

Part (iii): tangential gradient

We make use of the elliptic problem (

For a.e.

\displaystyle\|{\nabla_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}}}u\|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|f\|_{\Omega}+\|{\partial^{2}_{tt}}u\|_{\Omega}+\|{g_{\mathrm{I}}}\|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}+\|{\partial_{t}}u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\big)

Part (iv):

Part (v): existence of

Define

\int_{\Omega}(\nabla z_{0}^{N}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{M}+z_{0}^{N}\varphi_{M}){\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}=\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}-\big({\partial_{\bm{n}}}v_{0}^{N}+(c\theta)^{-1}v_{1}^{N}-{g_{\mathrm{I}}}(\cdot,0)\big)\varphi_{M}{\,\mathrm{d}S}\qquad\forall M=1,\ldots,N.

for a constant

. Then define

,

; it holds that

in

.

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}\big({\partial_{\bm{n}}}w_{0}^{N}+(c\theta)^{-1}w_{1}^{N}-{g_{\mathrm{I}}}(\cdot,0)\big)\varphi_{M}{\,\mathrm{d}S}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}\big({\partial_{\bm{n}}}v_{0}^{N}+{\partial_{\bm{n}}}z_{0}^{N}+(c\theta)^{-1}v_{1}^{N}-{g_{\mathrm{I}}}(\cdot,0)\big)\varphi_{M}{\,\mathrm{d}S}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(-\nabla z_{0}^{N}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{M}-z_{0}^{N}\varphi_{M}){\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}+\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}{\partial_{\bm{n}}}z_{0}^{N}\varphi_{M}{\,\mathrm{d}S}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\Delta z_{0}^{N}-z_{0}^{N})\varphi_{M}{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}\qquad\qquad\forall M=1,\ldots,N.

3
Abstract framework

Since the derivation of Morawetz identities and their use in the construction of variational formulation are quite involved, we propose a simple abstract framework that can be used to derive coercive formulations arising from multipliers.
This highlights the common structure of the space–time formulation proposed in §
5
and of the formulation for the Helmholtz equation in
[
26
]
.

Remark 3.1

(Real and complex problems)
.

In this section, we assume that all the functions and the spaces are complex-valued, unless otherwise specified, thus we use sesquilinear forms and antilinear functionals.
This allows to include the Helmholtz impedance boundary value problem in Remark
3.2
.
In the following sections, which concern the wave equation, all spaces are real, so we use bilinear forms and linear functionals, but all our results immediately extend to the complex-valued case.

Consider the following abstract boundary value problem:

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u=f\qquad\text{on }{D}\\
\mathcal{B}u=g\qquad\text{on }\partial{D}\end{cases}

(11)

with
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

f\in L^{2}(D)
,

\mathcal{L}:V\to L^{2}(D)
a differential operator where
V\subset

L^{2}(D)
,

g\in V_{\partial D}
where
V_{\partial D}
is a function space on
\partial D
and

\mathcal{B}:V\to V_{\partial D}
a trace operator.

Consider an operator
\mathcal{M}:V\to L^{2}(D)
, which will play the role of the Morawetz multiplier, and introduce a Hermitian sesquilinear form
m
defined as:

\displaystyle m(u,v):=\int_{D}(\mathcal{L}u\overline{\mathcal{M}v}+\mathcal{M}u\overline{\mathcal{L}v}){\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}},\qquad u,v\in V.

(12)

For all
u\in V
, it holds that

\displaystyle\Re\int_{D}\mathcal{L}u\overline{\mathcal{M}u}{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{2}m(u,u).

(13)

We denote by
r
and
s
two sesquilinear forms on
V
such that

\displaystyle m(u,v)=r(u,v)+s(u,v),

(14a)

and

\displaystyle\text{if }\quad\mathcal{B}u=g\quad\text{ then }\quad s(u,v)=S_{g}(v),

(14b)

for an appropriate antilinear form
S_{g}
that depends on the boundary datum
g
.
In the concrete cases below, the sesquilinear forms
r
and
s
are obtained through integration by parts and other manipulations of
m(u,v)
.
Let
\ell
be a least-squares-type sesquilinear form, i.e. it is Hermitian, it defines a seminorm over the space
V
, and

\text{if}\quad{\mathcal{L}}u=f\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{B}u=g\quad\text{then}\quad\ell(u,v)={F_{\ell}}(v)\qquad\forall v\in V,

(15)

where
{F_{\ell}}
is an antilinear form that is defined solely using
f
and
g
.
The sesquilinear form
\ell
is used to introduce positive-semidefinite terms that help controlling some components of the norm on
V
, which in turn are needed to ensure the continuity of
r
and
s
.
To satisfy Assumption (
15
) one could choose for example
\ell(u,v)=\int_{D}{\mathcal{L}}u{\mathcal{L}}v{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}
and
{F_{\ell}}(v)=\int_{D}f{\mathcal{L}}v{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}
.

We use
r
and
\ell
to define another sesquilinear form
b
as:

\displaystyle b(u,v):=\int_{D}\mathcal{M}u\overline{\mathcal{L}v}{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}-r(u,v)+\ell(u,v),\qquad u,v\in V.

(16)

The sesquilinear form
b
can be used to solve the abstract problem (
11
) together with an appropriately defined antilinear form
F
.
Using relation (
14a
) and the definition of
m
(
12
), it holds that

\displaystyle b(u,v)=\int_{D}-\mathcal{L}u\overline{\mathcal{M}v}{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}+s(u,v)+\ell(u,v).

Moreover, if
u\in V
is a solution to problem (
11
), by assumptions (
14b
) and (
15
),

\displaystyle b(u,v)=\int_{D}-f\overline{\mathcal{M}v}{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}+S_{g}(v)+{F_{\ell}}(v).

This suggests to define the antilinear form
F
as

\displaystyle F(v):=\int_{D}-f\overline{\mathcal{M}v}{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}+S_{g}(v)+{F_{\ell}}(v),

(17)

so that if
u\in V
is a solution of problem (
11
) then

b(u,v)=F(v)\qquad\forall v\in V.

(18)

A sufficient condition for the well-posedness of (
18
) is the coercivity of
b
.
Recalling the definitions of
b
(
16
) and
m
(
12
), and the identity (
13
),

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Re b(v,v)&=\ell(v,v)+\Re\int_{D}\mathcal{M}v\overline{\mathcal{L}v}{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}-\Re r(v,v)\\
&=\ell(v,v)+\frac{1}{2}\Re r(v,v)+\frac{1}{2}\Re s(v,v)-\Re r(v,v)\\
&=\ell(v,v)-\frac{1}{2}\Re r(v,v)+\frac{1}{2}\Re s(v,v)\qquad\qquad\forall v\in V.\end{aligned}

(19)

Let
\|\cdot\|_{V}
be a norm on
V
such that it is a Hilbert space, and that
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{B}
are continuous operators.
Assume that
r
,
s
and
\ell
are continuous sesquilinear forms and
S_{g}
and
L
are continuous antilinear functionals.
Then the variational problem (
18
) is well-posed by Lax–Milgram theorem if
b
is coercive: to ensure this one needs to provide a lower bound for
2\ell(v,v)+\Re\{s(v,v)-r(v,v)\}
.
It turns out that this approach allows to choose
r
,
s
and
\ell
in (
14
) for both the Helmholtz equation, as we summarise in Remark
3.2
, and the wave equation, as we describe in the rest of the paper.

Remark 3.2

(Coercive formulation for the Helmholtz equation)
.

A reason to consider this abstract framework is that we can recover the coercive formulation of
[
26
]
for the impedance Helmholtz problem

\mathcal{L}u=\Delta u+k^{2}u=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad\mathcal{B}u={\partial_{\bm{n}}}{u}-\textrm{i}ku=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega

where
k
is a positive constant and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded star-shaped domain.
We choose

\mathcal{M}u=\bm{x}\cdot\nabla u-\textrm{i}k\beta u+\frac{d-1}{2}u
, where
\beta
is a positive coefficient, and
\ell(u,v):=\frac{1}{3k^{2}}\int_{D}{\mathcal{L}}u{\mathcal{L}}v{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}
(
{F_{\ell}}
is defined accordingly).
We define the sesquilinear forms
r
and
s
as

\displaystyle r(u,v)=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v}+k^{2}u\overline{v}\right){\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}\bigg(\textrm{i}\theta ku\overline{\mathcal{M}v}+\left(\bm{x}\cdot\nabla_{\partial\Omega}u-\textrm{i}k\beta u+\frac{d-1}{2}u\right)\overline{{\partial_{\bm{n}}}{v}}+(\bm{x}\cdot\bm{n})\left(k^{2}u\overline{v}-\nabla_{\partial\Omega}u\cdot\overline{\nabla_{\partial\Omega}v}\right)\bigg){\,\mathrm{d}S},

\displaystyle s(u,v)=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\left({\partial_{\bm{n}}}{u}-\textrm{i}\theta ku\right)\overline{\mathcal{M}v}{\,\mathrm{d}S}.

where
\nabla_{\partial\Omega}
is the tangential component of the gradient on
{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}
.
Note that the assumptions (
14a
)–(
14b
) are satisfied.
This, together with (
16
)–(
17
), defines
b
and
F
:

\displaystyle b(u,v)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v}+k^{2}u\overline{v}+\left(\mathcal{M}u+\frac{1}{3k^{2}}\mathcal{L}u\right)\overline{\mathcal{L}v}\right){\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}+

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(\textrm{i}\theta ku\overline{\mathcal{M}v}+\Big(\bm{x}\cdot\nabla_{\partial\Omega}{u}-\textrm{i}k\beta u+\frac{d-1}{2}u\Big)\overline{{\partial_{\bm{n}}}{v}}+(\bm{x}\cdot\bm{n})\left(k^{2}u\overline{v}-\nabla_{\partial\Omega}{u}\cdot\overline{\nabla_{\partial\Omega}{v}}\right)\right){\,\mathrm{d}S},

\displaystyle F(v)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\overline{\mathcal{M}v}-\frac{1}{3k^{2}}\overline{\mathcal{L}v}\right)f{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\overline{\mathcal{M}v}g{\,\mathrm{d}S},

which coincide with those defined in
[
26
, eq. (1.23), (1.24)]
.
All forms involved are continuous in the space

V=\overline{C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})}^{\|\cdot\|_{V}}=\left\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega):\;\Delta v\in L^{2}(\Omega),\;v\in H^{1}(\partial\Omega),\;{\partial_{\bm{n}}}{v}\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\right\}

(see
[
26
, eq. (1.21) and Lemma A.1]
),
equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\|v\|^{2}_{V}=\

\displaystyle k^{2}\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+k^{-2}\|\Delta v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)\left(k^{2}\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\|\nabla_{\partial\Omega}v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\|{\partial_{\bm{n}}}{v}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\right).

The main result of
[
26
]
is that
b
(and actually a more general version) is coercive in
V
.
The proof (
[
26
, Thm. 3.4]
) essentially relies on controlling
\Re\{s(v,v)-r(v,v)\}
from below and requires
\Omega
to be star-shaped with respect to a ball centered at the origin.

4
Space–time Morawetz multiplier and identities

We define the space–time Morawetz multiplier
\mathcal{M}
as the operator

\mathcal{M}u:=-\xi\bm{x}\cdot\nabla u+\beta(t-T^{*}){\partial_{t}}{u},\qquad\text{where }\xi,\beta>0\text{ and }T^{*}=\nu T,\;\nu>1,

(20)

for either
u\in H^{1}(Q)
or
u\in C^{1}(Q)
.

To simplify the notation, we denote the wave operator by
\mathcal{W}u:={\partial^{2}_{tt}}{u}-c^{2}\Delta u
.

Remark 4.1

(Other multipliers)
.

The definition of the multipliers is problem-dependent.
In the context of energy estimation for the wave equation, the multiplier used is different, and is defined as
\mathcal{Z}u=t\bm{x}\cdot\nabla u+\frac{1}{2}(|\bm{x}|^{2}+t^{2})\partial_{t}u+tu
(see
[
23
, App. 3]
or
[
31
]
). One would then compute
q
and
\bm{p}
such that
\mathcal{Z}u\mathcal{W}u={\partial_{t}}{q}+\nabla\cdot\bm{p}
, integrate
{\partial_{t}}{q}+\nabla\cdot\bm{p}
over the space–time domain, and use the divergence theorem to obtain an estimate of the energy that decays as
\frac{1}{t}
.
For the Helmholtz equation the “Rellich multiplier”
\mathcal{M}u=\bm{x}\cdot\nabla u
is instrumental to prove wavenumber-explicit stability bounds for impedance boundary value problems,
[
25
, Prop. 8.1.4]
.
The coercive formulation for the Helmholtz equation in
[
26
]
relies on the “Morawetz multiplier”
\mathcal{M}v=\bm{x}\cdot\nabla v-\textrm{i}k\beta v+\frac{d-1}{2}v
.
See
[
26
, sect. 1.4]
for a brief summary of the uses of this kind of multipliers for wave problems.

The Morawetz identity allows to write the sum
\mathcal{M}u\mathcal{W}v+\mathcal{W}u\mathcal{M}v
as the sum of a time derivative, a divergence, and two terms “with a sign”, i.e. whose signs are determined by the parameters
\beta
and
\xi
when
v=u
, for all
u\neq 0
.

Lemma 4.2

(Pointwise Morawetz identity)
.

For all
u,v\in C^{2}(Q)
the following identity holds:

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}u\mathcal{W}v+\mathcal{W}u\mathcal{M}v=&{\partial_{t}}{}\Big[\mathcal{M}u\,{\partial_{t}}{v}+{\partial_{t}}{u}\,\mathcal{M}v-\beta(t-T^{*})\left({\partial_{t}}{u}{\partial_{t}}{v}-c^{2}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\right)\Big]\\
&-\nabla\cdot\Big[c^{2}{\mathcal{M}u\,\nabla v}+c^{2}\nabla u\,\mathcal{M}v-\xi\bm{x}\left({\partial_{t}}{u}{\partial_{t}}{v}-c^{2}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\right)\Big]\\
&-\big(\beta+\xi d\big){\partial_{t}}{u}{\partial_{t}}{v}-c^{2}\big(\beta+\xi(2-d)\big)\nabla u\cdot\nabla v.\end{split}

(21)

Proof.

The proof of (
21
) only requires the use of some standard vector calculus identities.
Indeed, the definition (
20
) of
{\mathcal{M}}
and repeated applications of the Leibniz product rule, together with
\nabla\cdot{\bm{x}}=d
, give the time component of the identity:

\begin{split}(\mathcal{M}u){\partial^{2}_{tt}}{v}+(\mathcal{M}v){\partial^{2}_{tt}}{u}&={\partial_{t}}{}\big[(\mathcal{M}u){\partial_{t}}{v}+{\partial_{t}}{u}(\mathcal{M}v)\big]-({\partial_{t}}{\mathcal{M}}u){\partial_{t}}v-{\partial_{t}}u({\partial_{t}}{\mathcal{M}}v)\\
&={\partial_{t}}{}\big[(\mathcal{M}u){\partial_{t}}{v}+{\partial_{t}}{u}(\mathcal{M}v)\big]+\xi(\bm{x}\cdot\nabla{\partial_{t}}{u}){\partial_{t}}{v}+\xi{\partial_{t}}{u}(\bm{x}\cdot\nabla{\partial_{t}}{v})\\
&\quad-2\beta{\partial_{t}}{u}{\partial_{t}}{v}-\beta(t-T^{*}){\partial^{2}_{tt}}{u}{\partial_{t}}{v}-\beta(t-T^{*}){\partial_{t}}{u}{\partial^{2}_{tt}}{v}\\
&={\partial_{t}}{}\big[(\mathcal{M}u){\partial_{t}}{v}+{\partial_{t}}{u}(\mathcal{M}v)\big]+\xi\big(\nabla\cdot[{\bm{x}}{\partial_{t}}u{\partial_{t}}v]-(\nabla\cdot{\bm{x}}){\partial_{t}}u{\partial_{t}}v\big)\\
&\quad-\beta{\partial_{t}}{u}{\partial_{t}}{v}-\beta{\partial_{t}}\big[(t-T^{*}){\partial_{t}}u{\partial_{t}}v\big]\\
&={\partial_{t}}{}\big[(\mathcal{M}u){\partial_{t}}{v}+{\partial_{t}}{u}(\mathcal{M}v)-\beta(t-T^{*}){\partial_{t}}u{\partial_{t}}v\big]\\
&\quad+\nabla\cdot[\xi{\bm{x}}{\partial_{t}}u{\partial_{t}}v]-(\beta+\xi d){\partial_{t}}u{\partial_{t}}v.\end{split}

(22)

The space component of the identity is treated similarly.
Recalling that the gradient of the scalar product of two gradients is

\nabla(\nabla u\cdot\nabla v)=(D^{2}u)\nabla v+(D^{2}v)\nabla u
where
D^{2}
denotes the (symmetric) Hessian matrix,
and that

is the gradient of

whose Hessian is the identity matrix, we have

\displaystyle\nabla({\bm{x}}\cdot\nabla u)\cdot\nabla v+\nabla u\cdot\nabla({\bm{x}}\cdot\nabla v)=

\displaystyle\ 2\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\big((D^{2}u){\bm{x}}\big)\cdot\nabla v+\nabla u\cdot\big((D^{2}v){\bm{x}}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 2\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+{\bm{x}}\cdot\nabla(\nabla u\cdot\nabla v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ (2-d)\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\nabla\cdot\big[{\bm{x}}(\nabla u\cdot\nabla v)\big].

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}u\Delta v+\Delta u\mathcal{M}v=\

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\big[\mathcal{M}u\nabla v+\nabla u\mathcal{M}v\big]-\nabla{\mathcal{M}}u\cdot\nabla v-\nabla u\cdot\nabla{\mathcal{M}}v

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\big[\mathcal{M}u\nabla v+\nabla u\mathcal{M}v\big]

\displaystyle+\xi\big(\nabla({\bm{x}}\cdot\nabla u)\cdot\nabla v+\nabla u\cdot\nabla({\bm{x}}\cdot\nabla v)\big)

\displaystyle-\beta(t-T^{*})\big(\nabla u\cdot\nabla{\partial_{t}}{v}+\nabla v\cdot\nabla{\partial_{t}}{u}\big)

(23)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left[\mathcal{M}u\nabla v+\mathcal{M}v\nabla u+\xi\bm{x}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\right]

\displaystyle-{\partial_{t}}{}\left[\beta(t-T^{*})\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\right]+\bigl(\beta+\xi(2-d)\bigr)\nabla u\cdot\nabla v.

The identity (
21
) is obtained multiplying (

) by

and subtracting it from (
22
).
∎

Lemma 4.3

(Integrated Morawetz identity)

If

\begin{split}m(u,v):=&\int_{Q}\big(\mathcal{M}u\mathcal{W}v+\mathcal{W}u\mathcal{M}v\big){\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\\

(24)

5.2
Norm, function spaces and variational problem

In order to write a variational problem and to prove a coercivity result, a function space and an accompanying norm are needed.

We choose a norm that is dimensionally homogeneous and has the minimum number of terms to ensure continuity of
b
and
F
:
for
v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})
,

\begin{split}\|v\|_{V}^{2}:=&\qquad\;\|{\partial_{t}}{v}\|_{Q}^{2}+\quad c^{2}\|\nabla v\|_{Q}^{2}+T^{2}\|\mathcal{W}v\|_{Q}^{2}\\
&+T\|{\partial_{t}}{v}\|_{\Omega_{T}}^{2}+c^{2}T\|\nabla v\|_{\Omega_{T}}^{2}\\
&+T\|{\partial_{t}}{v}\|_{\Omega_{0}}^{2}\>+c^{2}T\|\nabla v\|_{\Omega_{0}}^{2}+T^{-1}\|v\|_{{\Omega_{0}}}^{2}\\
&+L_{\mathrm{I}}\|{\partial_{t}}{v}\|_{{\Sigma_{\mathrm{I}}}}^{2}+c^{2}L_{\mathrm{I}}\|\nabla v\|_{{\Sigma_{\mathrm{I}}}}^{2}.\end{split}

(30)

The boundary terms appearing in (
30
) are the full
L^{2}
norms of
{\partial_{t}}v
and
c\nabla v
on the whole space–time boundary
\partial Q
, weighted with the domain sizes
L_{\mathrm{I}}
and

.
In particular, the
\|\nabla v\|_{{\Sigma_{\mathrm{I}}}}
term involves both the tangential and the normal components of the space gradient of

.

Note that
\|\cdot\|_{V}
does not explicitly contain the

norm of the function

, but only the
L^{2}({\Omega_{0}})
norm.
However, when combined with the time derivative over

, this is sufficient to give an upper bound on the

norm:

\displaystyle\|v\|_{Q}^{2}=\int_{Q}\Big(v({\bm{x}},0)+\int_{0}^{t}{\partial_{t}}v({\bm{x}},s)\mathrm{d}s\Big)^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{Q}v^{2}({\bm{x}},0){\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}+2\int_{Q}\left(\int_{0}^{t}{\partial_{t}}v({\bm{x}},s)\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\leq 2T\|v\|_{\Omega_{0}}^{2}+T^{2}\|{\partial_{t}}v\|_{Q}^{2}\;\leq\;2T^{2}\|v\|_{V}^{2}.

(31)

The space
V
of test and trial functions is defined as the closure in the
\|\cdot\|_{V}
norm of the smooth function space:

V:=\overline{C^{\infty}(\overline{Q})}^{\|\cdot\|_{V}}.

(32)

Following §

, we pose the variational problem:

\text{Find $u\in V$ such that}\qquad b(u,v)=F(v)\qquad\forall v\in V,

(33)

where

and

are as in (

, then it is also a solution of (

Proof.

and the bilinear form at the right-hand side of the integrated identity (

. Thus, the proof of Lemma

Remark 5.2

(The space
W
)
.

We introduce a Hilbert space
W
that is (possibly) larger and defined more explicitly than the space

in (

In this remark we temporarily write explicitly all trace operators.
First let

\displaystyle H(Q;{\mathcal{W}}):=\;

\displaystyle\left\{u\in H^{1}(Q):{\mathcal{W}}u\in L^{2}(Q)\right\}.

For all

the Dirichlet trace

is well-defined since

We show that also the Neumann trace can be defined on

using integration by parts.
Indeed, for

, define

\langle D_{n}u,\varphi\rangle_{H^{-\frac{1}{2}}\times H^{\frac{1}{2}}}:=\int_{Q}({\partial_{t}}v{\partial_{t}}\phi-c^{2}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\phi){\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}-\int_{Q}{\mathcal{W}}v\phi{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial Q),

where

\phi\in H^{1}(Q)
such that

.
Exactly as in
[

, one can prove that

is the Neumann trace of

\partial Q
, in particular its action on

.
To define the desired space, we require further regularity on both traces:

W:=\Big\{v\in H^{1}(Q;{\mathcal{W}}):\;(\gamma_{Q}v)|_{\Omega_{0}}\in H^{1}({\Omega_{0}}),\;(\gamma_{Q}v)|_{\Omega_{T}}\in H^{1}({\Omega_{T}}),\;(\gamma_{Q}v)|_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{I}}}\in H^{1}({\Sigma_{\mathrm{I}}}),\;D_{n}v\in L^{2}(\partial Q)\Big\}.

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\Big\{v\in H^{1}(Q):\;{\mathcal{W}}v\in L^{2}(Q),\;\nabla v,{\partial_{t}}v\in L^{2}(\partial Q)\Big\}

The key geometrical assumption is the following.

is defined almost everywhere on
{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}
.

The coercivity constant of

and the conditions under which coercivity holds depend explicitly on several parameters related to the domain

(the space dimension

,
the final time

,
the space diameter

,

),

)
(the wave speed

,

),
and the formulation (

)
(the penalty parameters

,

,

,

Theorem 5.4

(Coercivity of

)
.

Under Assumption
5.3
on
\Omega
, if

,
A_{Q},A_{\Omega_{0}}>0
and

\beta\geq\max\begin{cases}\xi(d-1),\\
\xi\frac{1}{\nu-1}\left(\frac{L_{\mathrm{I}}}{cT}+1\right),\\
\xi\frac{1}{\nu-1}\frac{L_{\mathrm{I}}}{cT}\left(\theta+\frac{1}{{\delta_{\mathrm{I}}}\theta}\right),\end{cases}

(35)

then for all

b(u,u)\geq{\alpha_{b}}\|u\|_{V}^{2}\qquad\text{with}\qquad\alpha_{b}:=\min\left\{\frac{\xi{\delta_{\mathrm{I}}}}{4},\;A_{Q},\;A_{\Omega_{0}}\right\}.

(36)

Proof.

Let
u\in W
.
We recall from (
19
) that, in order to prove the coercivity of
b(\cdot,\cdot)
, it is sufficient to show a lower bound for
-r(u,u)+s(u,u)+2\ell(u,u)
. For simplicity we can find a lower bound for
-r(u,u)+s(u,u)
and then improve on it by including the terms in
\ell(u,u)
.
From (
25
)–(
26
), simple calculations lead to

\displaystyle-r(u,u)+s(u,u)

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}\big[(\beta+\xi d)({\partial_{t}}{u})^{2}+c^{2}(\beta+2\xi-d\xi)|\nabla u|^{2}\big]{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle+\int_{{\Omega_{T}}}\big[2\xi(\bm{x}\cdot\nabla u){\partial_{t}}{u}-\beta(T-T^{*})\left(({\partial_{t}}{u})^{2}+c^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\right)\big]{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}

\displaystyle+\int_{{\Omega_{0}}}\big[\beta T^{*}\left(({\partial_{t}}{u})^{2}+c^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\right)+2\xi(\bm{x}\cdot\nabla u){\partial_{t}}{u}\big]{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}

(37)

\displaystyle+\int_{{{\Sigma_{\mathrm{I}}}}}\left[2\frac{c}{\theta}\xi(\bm{x}\cdot\nabla u){\partial_{t}}{u}+c^{2}\xi(\bm{x}\cdot\bm{n})|\nabla u|^{2}-\left(\xi(\bm{x}\cdot\bm{n})+2\frac{c}{\theta}{\beta}(t-T^{*})\right)({\partial_{t}}{u})^{2}\right]{\,\mathrm{d}S}{\,\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=:I_{Q}+I_{\Omega_{T}}+I_{\Omega_{0}}+I_{{\Sigma_{\mathrm{I}}}}.

The mixed terms containing
\nabla u{\partial_{t}}{u}
can be lower-bounded by negative terms using the Hölder inequality, and then the products of the norms of
\nabla u
and
{\partial_{t}}{u}
(on
{\Omega_{0}}
,
{\Omega_{T}}
and

Proposition 5.7

Let

\xi=1,\qquad\nu=2,\qquad\beta\geq\beta^{\#}:=\max\Big\{d-1,\;1+\frac{L_{\mathrm{I}}}{cT},\;\big(\theta+(\theta{\delta_{\mathrm{I}}})^{-1}\big)\frac{L_{\mathrm{I}}}{cT}\Big\}.

Remark 5.8

(
V
-conforming discrete spaces)
.

Which finite element spaces
V_{N}
are conforming in
V
?
Since
H^{2}(Q)\subset V
, any
H^{2}(Q)
-conforming space can be used to discretise (
33
).

In particular, assume we are given a non-overlapping partition of

in Lipschitz

-dimensional elements.
Then, element-wise smooth and globally

elements are

-conforming
[
5
, Thm. II.5.2]
, and therefore also
V
-conforming.

From the quasi-optimality and the density of the inclusion
H^{2}(Q)\subset V
, any sequence
\{V_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
of

-conforming discrete spaces that is able to approximate any

gives a sequence
\{u_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
of Galerkin solutions that converges to

, if this belongs to

.

Are
C^{1}
elements necessary though? Is it possible to use less regular elements? The following lemma clarifies that this is
not
possible.

Lemma 5.9

(
C^{1}
-conformity)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a space–time triangulation of
Q
. If
v\in V
and
\forall K\in\mathcal{T}

v|_{\overline{K}}\in C^{2}(\overline{K})
, then
v\in C^{1}(\overline{Q})\cap H^{2}(Q)
.

Proof.

The proof is the same as in
[
26
, Lemma 5.1]
, using the Green’s identity associated to the wave operator.
Let
v
be as in the statement, then, applying Green’s identities in space–time,

0=\int_{Q}(\phi{\mathcal{W}}v-v{\mathcal{W}}\phi){\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{K}(\phi{\mathcal{W}}v-v{\mathcal{W}}\phi){\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{\partial K}\left(\phi\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}v-v\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}\phi\right){\,\mathrm{d}S}\qquad\forall\phi\in\mathcal{D}(Q),

where
\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}=(-c^{2}{\bm{n}}_{{\bm{x}}},n_{t})
, and
({\bm{n}}_{{\bm{x}}},n_{t})
is the outward-pointing normal vector to
K
.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of the facets of the triangulation.
For every interior facet
F
, let
K^{1}_{F}
and
K^{2}_{F}
be the elements that share
F
.
The above identity becomes

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F},\;F\subset Q}\int_{F}\left(\phi\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}v|_{K^{1}_{F}}-\phi\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}v|_{K^{2}_{F}}-v\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}\phi|_{K^{1}_{F}}+v\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}\phi|_{K^{2}_{F}}\right){\,\mathrm{d}S}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F},\;F\subset\partial Q}\int_{F}\left(\phi\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}v-v\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}\phi\right){\,\mathrm{d}S}=0.

(47)

Since
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(Q)
,
\phi
and
\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}\phi
vanish on
\partial Q
, and
the integrals over
F\cap\partial Q
vanish.
Moreover, since
\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}\phi
and
v
are continuous across any facet
F=\partial K^{1}_{F}\cap\partial K^{2}_{F}
,
we have
v\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}\phi|_{K^{1}_{F}}-v\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}\phi|_{K^{2}_{F}}=0
.
Finally, (
47
) can be rewritten as

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F},\;F\subset Q}\int_{F}\phi\big(\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}v|_{K^{1}_{F}}-\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}v|_{K^{2}_{F}}\big){\,\mathrm{d}S}=0\qquad\forall\phi\in\mathcal{D}(Q),

which shows that
\partial_{\widetilde{{\bm{n}}}}v
is continuous over every
F\in\mathcal{F}
. To conclude, note that the tangential gradients
\nabla_{F}(v|_{K^{1}_{F}})=\nabla_{F}(v|_{K^{2}_{F}})
on
F
because
v
is continuous across elements.
∎

Remark 5.10

(DOF count: space–time vs time-stepping)
.

The obvious drawback of space–time methods is that they require the solution of large linear systems.
We show that,
when approximating smooth solutions with uniform
maximal-regularity splines,
a space–time scheme and a single step of any time-stepping method achieve the same accuracy solving linear systems of comparable sizes, if, in the former, one takes a slightly higher polynomial degree and coarser elements.
Assume that
u
is smooth,

Q=\left(-L_{\mathrm{I}},L_{\mathrm{I}}\right)^{d}\times(0,T),\qquad L_{\mathrm{I}}\sim T\sim c\sim 1,

N_{x}=\frac{2L_{\mathrm{I}}}{h_{x}},\qquad N_{t}=\frac{T}{h_{t}},\qquad V_{N}=\mathbb{Q}^{p}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\cap C^{k}(Q),\qquad 1\leq k\leq p-1,

where
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is a uniform tensor-product mesh on
Q
with
N_{x}^{d}
Cartesian-product elements in space and
N_{t}
in time, and
\mathbb{Q}^{p}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
is the space of degree-
p
, tensor-product, piecewise polynomials on
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
Recall that
\dim V_{N}\sim N_{x}^{d}N_{t}(p-k)^{d}
and

\|u-u_{N}\|_{H^{1}(Q)}\lesssim\max\{h_{x},h_{t}\}^{p}\sim\max\{N_{x}^{-p},N_{t}^{-p}\}.

Then, the number of degrees of freedom (DOFs) required to achieve a given accuracy
\epsilon>0
is of order
\epsilon^{-\frac{d+1}{p}}(p-k)^{d}
.
Using splines with maximal regularity (i.e.
k=p-1
), to get
H^{1}
accuracy proportional to
\epsilon
, a linear system with
\epsilon^{-\frac{d+1}{p}}
DOFs needs to be solved.

We compare this with the cost of a single time-step of an implicit method, using the spline space

\mathbb{Q}^{p_{TS}}(\mathcal{T}_{h_{TS}}^{x})\cap C^{p_{TS}-1}(\Omega)

with polynomial degree
p_{TS}
, space mesh size
h_{TS}
, and
N_{TS}=2L_{\mathrm{I}}/h_{TS}
elements in each direction.
For simplicity, we assume that the time-step does not introduce any error other than the approximation in space.
To achieve the same error
\epsilon
, one needs
N_{TS}^{d}=\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{p_{TS}}}
DOFs.
Choosing

p\sim\frac{d+1}{d}p_{TS}\qquad\text{and}\qquad N_{x}\sim N_{t}\sim N_{TS}^{\frac{p_{TS}}{p}}\sim N_{TS}^{\frac{d}{d+1}},

then both the space–time method and a single time-step of the implicit method have accuracy proportional to
\epsilon
and require the solution of a linear system of size
\sim\epsilon^{-\frac{d+1}{p}}\sim\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{p_{TS}}}
.

We note that for Galerkin approximation of the wave equation with high-regularity splines, explicit and implicit time-stepping schemes give linear systems with the same size and sparsity pattern, as pointed out in
[
38
, sect. 4]
.

\displaystyle\|v_{h}\|_{V}^{2}\lesssim\big(

\displaystyle h_{t}^{-2}+c^{2}h_{x}^{-2}+c^{4}T^{2}h_{x}^{-4}+T^{2}h_{t}^{-4}

\displaystyle+Th_{t}^{-3}+c^{2}Th_{x}^{-2}h_{t}^{-1}+T^{-1}h_{t}^{-1}+L_{\mathrm{I}}h_{t}^{-2}h_{x}^{-1}+c^{2}L_{\mathrm{I}}h_{x}^{-3}\big)h_{x}^{d}h_{t}\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{N}.

Then, arguing as in the proof of
[
26
, Prop. 5.4]
, using
h_{x}\leq L_{\mathrm{I}}
,
h_{t}\leq T
and Young’s inequality, the condition number
\kappa_{2}(\underline{\underline{\mathrm{B}}}):=\|\underline{\underline{\mathrm{B}}}\|_{2}\|\underline{\underline{\mathrm{B}}}^{-1}\|_{2}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}(\underline{\underline{\mathrm{B}}})

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{C_{b}}{\alpha_{b}}{2T^{2}}\Big(h_{t}^{-2}+c^{2}h_{x}^{-2}+c^{4}T^{2}h_{x}^{-4}+T^{2}h_{t}^{-4}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+Th_{t}^{-3}+c^{2}Th_{x}^{-2}h_{t}^{-1}+T^{-1}h_{t}^{-1}+L_{\mathrm{I}}h_{t}^{-2}h_{x}^{-1}+c^{2}L_{\mathrm{I}}h_{x}^{-3}\Big)\lesssim\frac{C_{b}}{\alpha_{b}}\bigg(\frac{T}{h_{t}}+\frac{L_{\mathrm{I}}+cT}{h_{x}}\bigg)^{4},

where
\alpha_{b}
(
C_{b}
respectively) is the coercivity (continuity respectively) constant of

This shows that the condition number grows at most with order 4, when the mesh is refined in either one of the directions.
The fourth power of the mesh size appears because of the presence of the term

in the norm

Analogous results can be derived for unstructured, non-tensor-product, quasi-uniform, space–time meshes.

Remark 5.12

(Energy considerations)
.

Define the energy of any
u\in W
at time
t\in[0,T]
as

\mathcal{E}(t;u):=\frac{1}{2}\big(\|{\partial_{t}}u\|_{\Omega_{t}}^{2}+c^{2}\|\nabla u\|_{\Omega_{t}}^{2}\big).

If
u
solves (
2
) with
f=0
and
{g_{\mathrm{I}}}=0
, integrating by parts and imposing the boundary conditions shows that energy does not increase: for
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}(t_{2};u)-\mathcal{E}(t_{1};u)=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\partial_{t}}\mathcal{E}(t;u){\,\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}c^{2}{\partial_{t}}u{\partial_{\bm{n}}}u{\,\mathrm{d}S}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{c}{\theta}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}({\partial_{t}}u)^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}S}\leq 0.\end{split}

For a general
u\in W
, bound (
5
) in the proof of Theorem
2.1
is uniform in the parameter
N
and thus holds with
u
in place of
u^{N}
.
This is an upper bound on
\mathcal{E}(t;u)
which depends only on
\|{\mathcal{W}}u\|_{Q}
,
\|{\partial_{\bm{n}}}u+(\theta c)^{-1}{\partial_{t}}u\|_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{I}}}
,
\|\nabla u\|_{\Omega_{0}}
and
\|u\|_{\Omega_{0}}
and is uniform in
t
.
Moreover, the right-hand side of such bound can be bounded using the

norm of

:

\mathcal{E}(t;u)\leq e^{1}\max\Big\{\frac{1}{2T},\;\frac{c}{L_{\mathrm{I}}}\Big(\theta+\frac{1}{\theta}\Big)\Big\}\|u\|_{V}^{2}\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

This allows to control the energy
\mathcal{E}(t;u-u_{N})
of the Galerkin error using the approximation properties of

.
We can also control the error of the energy: for any
w\in W
such that
\|u-w\|_{V}\leq\|u\|_{V}
(e.g.
w=u_{N}
the Galerkin solution for a sufficiently accurate space
V_{N}
)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}(t;u)-\mathcal{E}(t;w)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega\times\{t\}}\Big({\partial_{t}}(u+w){\partial_{t}}(u-w)+c^{2}\nabla(u+w)\cdot\nabla(u-w)\Big){\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{E}(t;u+w)\;\mathcal{E}(t;u-w)}

\displaystyle\leq e^{1}\max\Big\{\frac{1}{2T},\;\frac{c}{L_{\mathrm{I}}}\Big(\theta+\frac{1}{\theta}\Big)\Big\}\;\|u+w\|_{V}\;\|u-w\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq 3\,e^{1}\max\Big\{\frac{1}{2T},\;\frac{c}{L_{\mathrm{I}}}\Big(\theta+\frac{1}{\theta}\Big)\Big\}\;\|u\|_{V}\;\|u-w\|_{V}.

be the tensor-product Hermite element space in
Q=(-1,1)\times(0,1)
(i.e., the Bogner–Fox–Schmit element space in space–time).
More explicitly, the discrete space is

V_{h}=\big\{v\in C^{1}(Q),\quad v|_{(x_{j},x_{j+1})\times(t_{i},t_{i+1})}\in\mathbb{Q}^{3},\quad j=0,\dots,N_{x}-1,\quad i=0,\dots,N_{t}-1\big\},

where
\mathbb{Q}^{3}
is the space of polynomials of degree at most 3 in each variable;

•

we choose as basis functions the products
\varphi_{j,i}(x,t)=v_{j}(x)v_{i}(t)
,
for
j=0,\dots,2N_{x}+2
and
i=0,\dots,2N_{t}+2
, where
v_{j}
and
v_{i}
are the cubic Hermite basis functions of
[
4
, Ch. IV, p. 48]
.
Each basis function is supported in (at most) four elements of the space–time mesh, and is normalised so that one of
\varphi_{j,i},\partial_{x}\varphi_{j,i},\partial_{t}\varphi_{j,i},\partial_{x}\partial_{t}\varphi_{j,i}
has value 1 at the mesh node at the centre of its support and the other three vanish at the same point.
For simplicity, let
N:=\dim(V_{h})=(2N_{t}+2)(2N_{x}+2)
.

8
Conclusions and further work

We have derived a space–time variational formulation for a class of impedance and mixed impedance–Dirichlet initial–boundary value problems for the acoustic wave equation that is continuous and coercive in a norm stronger than
H^{1}
on the space–time cylinder.
The main assumption is that the scalar product
{\bm{x}}\cdot{\bm{n}}
of the position vector and the outgoing unit normal is positive on the impedance boundary and negative on the Dirichlet boundary; this includes the case of a star-shaped impedance domain, possibly containing a star-shaped sound-soft scatterer.
The derivation of the formulation relies on the use of classical Morawetz multipliers, following the analogue result for the Helmholtz equation of
[
26
]
.
The bilinear and the linear forms only include space and time partial derivatives of test and trial functions, together with linear (in
{\bm{x}}
and
t
) coefficients and a handful of parameters that can be easily chosen.
The proof of the coercivity and continuity estimates only requires elementary vector-calculus tools.
The formulation can be discretised with any space–time
H^{2}
-conforming space, leading in all cases to well-posed and quasi-optimal Galerkin methods.

We list a few improvements and extensions of the proposed method that might be considered, some of which are already underway.

•

The extension to a class of non-trapping, non-constant material parameters, using the Morawetz techniques of, e.g.,
[
27
]
and
[
16
]
.

•

The extension to more general classes of bounded domains, possibly using different Morawetz multipliers.
The extension to unbounded domains by means of high-order absorbing boundary conditions (ABC), perfectly-matched layers (PML), Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps (DtN).

•

The extension to vector wave problems, in particular electromagnetic and elastic waves.

•

The proof of optimal convergence rates in
L^{2}(Q)
and
H^{1}(Q)
norms.

•

The proof or disproof of the equality between the function spaces
V
and
W
; see Remark
5.2
.

•

The precise characterisation of the IBVP data for which the solution of the proposed formulation coincides with the desired one (those in Theorem
2.2
are sufficient but possibly not necessary).

•

A systematic numerical study of the proposed formulation in space dimensions higher than
1
, with general spline spaces and other discrete spaces on simplicial and unstructured meshes, including a parameter-sensitivity and dispersion analysis, and a comparison against established methods.

•

The lifting of the
H^{2}(Q)
-conformity requirement on the Galerkin space, by use of continuous-interior penalty (CIP) or by devising a mixed formulation that exploits Morawetz multipliers.

•

The development of techniques such as matrix-compression, preconditioners, a-posteriori estimators and adaptivity, that make use of coercivity to reduce the computational cost of the method.
