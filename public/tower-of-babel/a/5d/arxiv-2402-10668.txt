Data-Driven Abstractions for Control Systems
Rudi Coppola, Andrea Peruffo, Manuel Mazo Jr

Abstract
At the intersection of dynamical systems, control theory, and formal methods lies the construction of symbolic abstractions: these typically represent simpler, finite-state models whose behaviour mimics the one of an underlying concrete system but are easier to analyse. Building an abstraction usually requires an accurate knowledge of the underlying model: this knowledge may be costly to gather, especially in real-life applications. We aim to bridge this gap by building abstractions based on sampling finite length trajectories. Adding the controller degrees of freedom, we newly define the notion of probabilistic alternating simulation, and provide probably approximately correct (PAC) guarantees that the constructed abstraction includes all behaviours of the concrete system and that it is suitable for control design, for arbitrarily long time horizons, leveraging the scenario theory. Our method is then tested on several numerical benchmarks.



I
Introduction

Data-driven modeling and analysis is living a new renaissance with the advances in machine learning and artificial intelligence enabled by unprecedented computing power. The field of system verification, aiming at providing formal performance and safety guarantees is not alien to this trend. Recent work has been focused on the use of collected data from a system to derive directly (i.e. with no model involved) barrier functions certifying invariance
[
2
,
25
]
, or finite abstractions to verify and synthesize controllers
[
12
,
19
,
24
]
. A popular approach is to employ scenario-based optimization techniques to derive probably approximately correct (PAC) guarantees on the performance metric of interest.
The use of scenario-based optimization
requires independent samples, generated from the probability distribution that drives the system’s uncertainty.
A special case considered in many works
[
12
,
30
,
31
]
is that of deterministic systems (for which a model is not available), in which the only uncertainty is their initialization, i.e. the initial state is drawn from some probability distribution, and
the control policy, usually chosen from a finite set of possible actions, and the samples consist of individual transitions or, more generally, finite-length trajectories.
In this setting the independence of samples can be derived by independently sampling the initial state: typically a uniform distribution is selected for compact initial sets.
If the set of initial conditions coincides with the domain of interest, one can directly sample one-step transitions; however, the scenario-based approach would provide guarantees for the satisfaction of
one-step
properties,
provided that transitions are indeed sampled independently.
On the other hand, one is often interested in inferring long (even infinite) horizon specifications from one-step properties. These approaches are limited to provide guarantees for the time horizon used to construct the data set, as for larger horizons nothing can be directly inferred, unless some additional knowledge of the system is available, as for example in
[
18
]
.

Contributions.

Addressing this limitation is the main objective of this work.
We consider deterministic systems with unknown dynamics and a random initialization.
Our approach provides a construction of data-driven finite abstractions, built on the notions of alternating simulation and a particular class of abstractions known as Strongest Asynchronous
\ell
-complete Abstractions (or Approximations) (SA
\ell
CAs)
[
28
]
.
We introduce the notion of probabilistic alternating simulation, instrumental in describing the relation between a deterministic (but randomly sampled) model and a transition system constructed upon the collected system’s behaviours.
Leveraging the scenario theory, we establish PAC guarantees for the inclusion of the concrete system’s finite behaviours in those of the abstraction. We clarify the role played by the length of the trajectories used for constructing the abstraction, and, by laying coarse assumptions on the concrete system’s dynamics, we reveal how the PAC guarantees can be adapted to retain the abstraction’s suitability for control over
longer
trajectory lengths.
This enables the verification of properties, and synthesis of control policies, over an arbitrarily long time horizon while preserving the PAC guarantees.

Related Work.

Abstractions usually entail a model-based technique aimed at extracting prominent features
[
29
,
4
]
. The end goal of this process is to represent the original system with a simpler object, typically with a finite number of states, to streamline the verification of certain properties of the original system or the formulation of a policy (or controller) that guarantees fulfillment of a user-defined requirement.
When the model is unknown, one can opt for model identification, as demonstrated in, for example,
[
14
]
, and, in a second step, the identified model can be abstracted using conventional formal techniques. The main shortcoming of model-based abstractions is that the necessary computation for their construction rapidly grows with the state and input dimension and the resolution of their discretization.
Alternatively, When the model is unknown, some recent works have recently proposed a method to directly synthesize an abstraction from data, with guarantees of correctness without the need to identify an underlying model.
Recently, a suite of techniques has emerged to build abstractions from data sampled from unknown models.
In
[
11
,
3
,
18
]
, a sampled-based interval MDP is created using the scenario approach to constrain transition probabilities in a stochastic dynamical model. In
[
12
]
, a notion of PAC alternating simulation relationship is defined
by sampling one-step state transitions.
In
[
20
]
, PAC over-approximations of monotone systems are employed to construct models; in
[
16
]
, a sample-based growth rate is used to build an abstraction and synthesize a controller.

At the heart of our work lies a type of abstraction known as SA
\ell
CA,
[
27
,
28
]
which carries several interesting properties for our purposes and can be constructed from the
complete
knowledge of a concrete system’s output trajectories, rather than its state trajectories. When only
partial
knowledge is available, as in the case of abstracting the system from sampled trajectories, certain undesired phenomena may arise. These phenomena, not only limited to data-driven SA
\ell
CAs, are analyzed in detail in
[
10
]
.
Further, in
[
9
]
the authors propose the construction of data-driven
\ell
-complete abstractions for linear autonomous systems; in this work, we extend the results in
[
9
]
to include data-driven SA
\ell
CAs for nonlinear control systems. these methods find applications also for event-triggered control models
[
22
,
23
]
.
Recent work
[
5
,
6
]
proposes the construction of a data-driven memory-based Markov model for stochastic systems, equipped with an adaptive method to estimate the size of the needed memory from observations.
As we show throughout this manuscript, memory is a key notion allowing us to use data-driven SA
\ell
CAs for control.

Organisation

This manuscript is organised as follows. Section
II
provides relevant background information and notation used across this work.
In Section
III
, we describe transition systems and the systems’ relations, which represent the main tool allowing us to craft an abstraction-based controller.
Next, we present a data-driven perspective on the construction of abstractions of unknown systems in Section
IV
, leveraging scenario theory to provide PAC-type guarantees.
The PAC guarantees hold up until the time horizon used for sampling the trajectories necessary to construct the abstraction; in Section
V
we outline the assumptions under which the abstraction can be used to extend the guarantees for greater time horizons.
We present experimental results for these abstractions in Section
VI
, and we reserve a discussion and conclusion to Sections
VII
.

II-B

Scenario Theory

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)
be a probability space, and let
\Omega^{N}
be the
N
-Cartesian product of the sample space and with
\mu^{N}
its product measure.
A point in
(\Omega^{N},\mathcal{F}^{N},\mu^{N})

is thus a sample
(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{N})
of
N
elements drawn independently from
\Omega
according to the same probability
\mu
.
Each
\omega_{i}
is regarded as an observation, or
scenario

[
8
,
13
]
.
Given
N
samples, we aim at taking a
decision
, e.g. construct a classifier, or compute a regression function, from a set
\Theta
, i.e. the optimization space – no particular structure is assumed for this set. To every
\omega\in\Omega
there is associated a constraint set
\Theta_{\omega}\subseteq\Theta
.
The scenario theory offers a way to compute a solution
\theta_{N}^{*}
satisfying all the constraints
\Theta_{\omega_{i}}
imposed by the set of
N
i.i.d. samples while quantifying the probability that a new random sample
\omega\in\Omega
would result in the pre-computed solution
\theta^{*}_{N}
violating the new constraint
\Theta_{\omega}
.
Hence, we are interested in evaluating the generalization power of the solution, i.e. the performance of
\theta_{N}^{*}
. To this end, we quantify the probability of extracting a sample resulting in a constraint violated by a
\theta\in\Theta
as follows.

Definition 3
(Violation
[
8
]
)

The
violation probability
(or violation for short) of a given
\theta\in\Theta
and the
violation set
are respectively defined as

V(\theta)=\mu(\,\omega\in\mathcal{V(\theta)}),\quad\mathcal{V(\theta)}=\{\omega:\theta\notin\Theta_{\omega}\}.

(3)

V(\theta)
quantifies the probability with which a new randomly selected constraint
\Theta_{\omega}
is violated by
\theta
. We say that
\theta
is
\epsilon
-robust against constraint violation if
V(\theta)\leq\epsilon
.
\square

Notice that in general

is not directly computable since
\mu
is not known.
Under mild assumptions, see
[
13
, Assumption 1]
, a confidence bound can be derived:

Theorem 1
(PAC bounds
[
13
, Theorem 1]
)

Given a confidence
\beta\in(0,1)
and the solution

, it holds

\mu^{N}(V(\theta^{*}_{N})\leq\epsilon(s^{*}_{N},\beta,N))\geq 1-\beta,

(4)

where
\epsilon(\cdot)
is the solution of a polynomial equation (omitted here for brevity) and

is the
complexity
of the solution – it represents the cardinality of the smallest subset of the sample set that yields the same solution
\theta^{*}_{N}
.

Problem formulation and System Description

We adopt the framework of finite-state abstractions in the form of transition systems.

(5)

mutatis mutandis
with no conceptual modifications.

III-A

Systems’ relations

Let us now introduce two key notions: an
alternating simulation relation
and a
simulation relation
.
The former is instrumental in refining a policy synthesised on an abstraction to a controller for the concrete system. For SA
\ell
CAs, the existence of the latter is equivalent to the existence of an alternating simulation relation, as shown in the extended version of
[
28
]
. We recall both definitions below.
We consider an observation comprising both input and output, similarly to
[
28
]
(contrary to the common definition, see e.g.
[
29
]
, where only the output is observed); further, including the input in the observation allows for the construction of a more refined TS.
For simplicity, we also assume that the abstraction and the concrete system share the same input space.

Definition 5
(Simulation relation
[
29
, Definition 4.7]
)

Consider two systems
S_{a}
and
S_{b}
with
\mathcal{Y}_{a}=\mathcal{Y}_{b}
, and
\mathcal{U}_{a}=\mathcal{U}_{b}
. A relation
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{a}\times\mathcal{X}_{b}
is a simulation relation from
S_{a}
to
S_{b}
w.r.t.
\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{Y}
, written
S_{a}\preceq^{\mathcal{R}}_{S.}S_{b}
, if the following three conditions are satisfied:

•

\forall x_{a0}\in\mathcal{X}_{a0}\ .\ \exists x_{b0}\in\mathcal{X}_{b0}
with
(x_{a0},x_{b0})\in\mathcal{R}
,

•

(x_{a},x_{b})\in\mathcal{R}\implies\mathcal{H}_{a}(x_{a})=\mathcal{H}_{b}(x_{b})
,

•

(x_{a},x_{b})\in\mathcal{R}\implies(\forall u_{a}\in U_{\delta_{a}}(x_{a}),\exists u_{b}\in U_{\delta_{b}}(x_{b})\ .\ \\
\forall(x_{a},u_{a},x_{a}^{\prime})\in\delta_{a},\exists x_{b}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{b}\ .\ (x_{b},u_{b},x_{b}^{\prime})\in\delta_{b}\wedge(x_{a}^{\prime},x_{b}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}\wedge u_{a}=x_{b})
.

Definition 6
(Alternating simulation relation
[
29
, Definition 4.19]
)

Consider two systems
S_{a}
and
S_{b}
with
\mathcal{Y}_{a}=\mathcal{Y}_{b}
, and
\mathcal{U}_{a}=\mathcal{U}_{b}
. A relation
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{b}\times\mathcal{X}_{a}
is an alternating simulation relation from
S_{b}
to
S_{a}
w.r.t.
\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{Y}
, written
S_{b}\preceq_{A.S.}^{\mathcal{R}}S_{a}
, if the following three conditions are satisfied:

•

\forall x_{b0}\in\mathcal{X}_{b0}\ .\ \exists x_{a0}\in\mathcal{X}_{a0}
with
(x_{b0},x_{a0})\in\mathcal{R}
,

•

(x_{b},x_{a})\in\mathcal{R}\implies\mathcal{H}_{a}(x_{b})=\mathcal{H}_{b}(x_{a})
,

•

(x_{b},x_{a})\in\mathcal{R}\implies(\forall u_{b}\in U_{\delta_{b}}(x_{b}),\exists u_{a}\in U_{\delta_{a}}(x_{a})\ .\ \\
\forall(x_{a},u_{a},x_{a}^{\prime})\in\delta_{a}\implies\exists x_{b}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{b}\ .\ (x_{b},u_{b},x_{b}^{\prime})\in\delta_{b}\wedge(x_{b}^{\prime},x_{a}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}\wedge u_{a}=u_{b})
.

Remark 2

Note that if
\ \forall x_{a0}\ .\ \exists x_{b0}
s.t.
(x_{b0},x_{a0})\in\mathcal{R}
and
\forall(x_{b},x_{a})\in\mathcal{R}\ .\ U_{\delta_{a}}(x_{a})=U_{\delta_{b}}(x_{b})
then the above definition also defines a
simulation relation

\mathcal{R}^{-1}
from
S_{a}
to
S_{b}
. Then, we can formally write

S_{a}\preceq^{\mathcal{R}^{-1}}_{S.}S_{b}\preceq_{A.S.}^{\mathcal{R}}S_{a}.

As anticipated, in addition to the standard definition of (alternating) simulation relation we impose the additional condition
u_{a}=u_{b}
.

IV
Data-driven Abstractions

The construction of a SA
\ell
CA implicitly requires the knowledge of all possible external behaviours of a system of interest.
In many practical applications, this is a costly assumption.
To overcome this issue, we aim to construct an abstraction starting from sampled external behaviours of the concrete system.

Given a system
S_{a}
, we define
the random variable
i_{u_{H}}^{a}:\mathcal{X}_{0}^{a}\to\wp(\Delta^{a}_{H})

i_{u_{H}}^{a}:=\mathcal{I}_{H}(S_{a},x_{0}^{a},u_{H}),

(11)

where
\Delta^{a}_{H}
is defined according to
\Delta^{a}:=\mathcal{X}_{a}\times\mathcal{Y}_{a}\times\mathcal{U}_{a}\times\mathcal{X}_{a}\times\mathcal{Y}_{a}
and the convention chosen in Section
II
. Equation (
11
) describes the set of full behaviours of
S_{a}
, for a given
u_{H}
, starting from the randomly extracted initial condition
x_{0}^{a}
. For deterministic systems,
i_{u_{H}}^{a}
is unique, whereas for nondeterministic systems it may represent a set of behaviours.
Given a system
S_{b}
and a relation
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{a}\times\mathcal{X}_{b}
we define the random variable
i^{b}_{u_{H}}:\mathcal{X}_{a0}\rightarrow\wp(\Delta_{H}^{b})

i_{u_{H}}^{b}:=\bigcup_{x_{b0}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}(x_{a0})\cap\mathcal{X}_{b0}}\mathcal{I}_{H}(S_{b},x_{b0},u_{H}),

(12)

which describes the set of full behaviours of
S_{b}
, for a given
u_{H}
, starting from all the initial conditions that are related to
x_{0}^{a}
through
\mathcal{R}
.

Considering only
sampled
behaviours naturally leads to an
approximation
of the SA
\ell
CA, i.e. only a subset of
\Pi_{\ell+1}
is available.
We thus generalize the definition of (alternating) simulation relation to encompass the case of a randomly sampled initial condition in the concrete system: we denote this notion as

probabilistic
(alternating) simulation relation.

Definition 8
(Probabilistic simulation relation)

Consider two systems
S_{a}
and
S_{b}
with
\mathcal{Y}_{a}=\mathcal{Y}_{b}
and
\mathcal{U}_{a}=\mathcal{U}_{b}=\mathcal{U}
, and a relation
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{a}\times\mathcal{X}_{b}
.
Additionally, given the probability space
(\mathcal{X}_{a0},\mathcal{G}_{a},\mu_{x_{a}})
, consider the augmented transition relations
\Delta_{a}
and
\Delta_{b}
of systems
S_{a}
and
S_{b}
respectively, for a fixed
u_{H}\in\mathcal{U}^{H}
, and with
i^{a}_{u_{H}}
defined as in (
11
),
we say that
\mathcal{R}
is a probabilistic simulation relation from
S_{a}
to
S_{b}
with respect to
\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{Y}
until horizon
H
with probability not less than
1-\epsilon
if

\displaystyle\mu_{x_{a}}(x_{a0}\in\mathcal{V}({S_{b}},\mathcal{R},H))<\epsilon,

(13)

where the violation set
\mathcal{V}({S_{b}},\mathcal{R},H)
is defined

\displaystyle\qquad\mathcal{V}({S_{b}},\mathcal{R},H):=\{x_{a0}:\exists u_{H}\in\mathcal{U}^{H}\ .\ i_{u_{H}}^{a}\neq\emptyset\ \wedge

\displaystyle\quad\exists(\xi_{a},\gamma_{a},\upsilon_{a},\xi^{\prime}_{a},\gamma^{\prime}_{a})\in i_{u_{H}}^{a},\nexists(\xi_{b},\gamma_{b},\upsilon_{b},\xi^{\prime}_{b},\gamma^{\prime}_{b})\in i_{u_{H}}^{b}\ .\

\displaystyle(\gamma_{a},\upsilon_{a},\gamma_{a}^{\prime})=(\gamma_{b},\upsilon_{b},\gamma_{b}^{\prime})\wedge\forall k\geq-1\ .\ (\xi_{a}^{\prime}(k),\xi_{b}^{\prime}(k))\in\mathcal{R}\}.

(14)

More compactly, we write

\mu_{x_{a}}(S_{a}\ {}^{H}{\preceq}_{S.}^{\mathcal{R}}S_{b})<\epsilon,

where
{}^{H}{\preceq}_{S.}^{\mathcal{R}}
highlights that the probabilistic simulation relation is referring to the relation
\mathcal{R}
and it is relative to a time horizon
H
.

\square

Definition 9
(Probabilistic alternating simulation relation)

8

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}({S_{b}},\mathcal{Z},H):=\{x_{a0}:\exists u_{H}\in\mathcal{U}^{H}\ .\ i_{u_{H}}^{b}\neq\emptyset\wedge(i^{a}_{u_{H}}=\emptyset\vee

\displaystyle\quad\exists(\xi_{a},\gamma_{a},\upsilon_{a},\xi^{\prime}_{a},\gamma^{\prime}_{a})\in i_{u_{H}}^{a},\not\exists(\xi_{b},\gamma_{b},\upsilon_{b},\xi^{\prime}_{b},\gamma^{\prime}_{b})\in i_{u_{H}}^{b}\ .\

\displaystyle(\gamma_{a},\upsilon_{a},\gamma_{a}^{\prime})=(\gamma_{b},\upsilon_{b},\gamma_{b}^{\prime})\wedge\forall k\geq-1\ .\ (\xi_{b}^{\prime}(k),\xi_{a}^{\prime}(k))\in\mathcal{Z}\}\}.

(16)

where

is defined as per (

\mu_{x_{a}}(S_{b}\ {}^{H}{\preceq}_{A.S.}^{\mathcal{Z}}S_{a})<\epsilon,

where
{}^{H}{\preceq}_{A.S.}^{\mathcal{Z}}
highlights that the probabilistic alternating simulation relation is referring to the relation
\mathcal{Z}
and it is relative to a time horizon
H

\square

IV-A

Constructing the data-driven abstraction

Consider the probability spaces
(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{G},\mu_{x})
and
(\mathcal{U}^{H},\mathcal{F},\mu_{u_{H}})
: when sampling the system
S_{\Sigma}
for
x\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
and
u_{H}\in\mathcal{U}^{H}
we assume to have access only to the external
H
-behaviour of the system, that is
\mathcal{B}_{H}(S_{\Sigma},x,u_{H})
: note that, by Assumption
1
(determinism), the latter is necessarily a singleton.
In order to construct a data-driven SA
\ell
CA of
S_{\Sigma}
, we draw
N
pairs
(x^{i},u_{H}^{i})
according to the product probability measure
\mu_{p}
and we obtain the set of sampled external behaviours

D:=\{\mathcal{B}_{H}(S_{\Sigma},x^{i},u_{H}^{i})\ :\ i=1,2,...N\}.

(17)

For
\ell<H
we denote by
\hat{\Pi}_{\ell}
the set of all witnessed subsequences of length
\ell
, that is

\hat{\Pi}_{\ell}:=\bigcup_{b_{H}\in D}\bigcup_{k\in[0,H-1]}b_{H}|_{[k-\ell+1,k]}.

(18)

From now on we use the symbol
\hat{\cdot}
as shown above to denote the quantities depending on the
N
samples drawn according to
\mu_{p}
.
We are now ready to define the data-driven SA
\ell
CA.

Definition 10
(Data-driven SA
\ell
-CA)

Given
\hat{\Pi}_{\ell+1}
, the transition system

\hat{S}_{\ell}=(\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\ell},\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\ell,0},\mathcal{U},\hat{\delta}_{\ell},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{H}_{\ell})

is called the data-driven (strongest asynchronous)
\ell
-complete abstraction (SA
\ell
CA) of
S_{\Sigma}
, where the transition relation is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\ell}

\displaystyle:=\hat{\Pi}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\ell,0}

\displaystyle:=\{b_{\ell}\in\hat{\Pi}_{\ell}:\exists y\in\mathcal{Y}\ .\ b_{\ell}(\ell-1)=(\diamond,\diamond,y)\}

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}_{\ell}

\displaystyle:=\{(x_{\ell},u,x_{\ell}^{\prime})\ :\ x_{\ell}|_{[1,\ell-1]}=x_{\ell}^{\prime}|_{[0,\ell-2]}\wedge

\displaystyle x_{\ell}^{\prime}(\ell-1)=(y,u,y^{\prime})\Rightarrow x_{\ell}\cdot(y,u,y^{\prime})\in\hat{\Pi}_{\ell+1}\}.

Trivially,
\hat{\Pi}_{\ell+1}\subseteq\Pi_{\ell+1}
. In the following, we show how to derive a probabilistic alternating simulation relation from
\hat{S}_{\ell}
to
S_{\Sigma}
until horizon
H
with probability not lower than
1-\epsilon
up to some confidence
\beta
.

Remark 3

Constructing the SA
\ell
CA using the set of sampled behaviours
D
entails solving a scenario program. Its complexity
s^{*}_{N}
is the cardinality of the smallest subset of
D
(
17
) which would result in the construction of the same set of all witnessed
(\ell+1)
-sequences
\hat{\Pi}_{\ell+1}
.
One may use Theorem
1
using an upper bound of
s^{*}_{N}
; a close estimate of its value can be obtained using a greedy set cover algorithm.
For practical purposes, one may use the value
|\hat{\Pi}_{\ell+1}|
, resulting in a more conservative estimate of
\epsilon
.

\square

The following two lemmas are simple consequences of the definition above, and their proof is omitted.

Lemma 1

Consider
S_{\Sigma}
, its data-driven SA
\ell
CA
\hat{S}_{\ell}
constructed from the set
\hat{\Pi}_{\ell+1}
and the relation

\hat{\mathcal{R}}:=\{(x,x_{\ell})\in\mathcal{X}\times\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\ell}:\ x_{\ell}\in\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(x)\}.

(19)

If
(x,x_{\ell})\in\hat{\mathcal{R}}
and
(x,u,x^{\prime})\in\delta
with
\mathcal{H}(x^{\prime})=y
then there exists a transition
(x_{\ell},u,x_{\ell}^{\prime})\in\hat{\delta}_{\ell}
with
\mathcal{H}(x_{\ell}^{\prime})=y
and
(x^{\prime},x_{\ell}^{\prime})\in\hat{\mathcal{R}}
if and only if
x_{\ell}\cdot(u,y)\in\hat{\Pi}_{\ell+1}
.

\square

Lemma
1
implies that, when using
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
, if a state trajectory of the concrete system results in the same external behaviour of a state trajectory of the SA
\ell
CA we know that at every time step the pair given by the state of the former and the state of the latter belong to
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
.

Lemma 2

Consider
S_{\Sigma}
, a confidence
\beta
and the data-driven SA
\ell
CA constructed from
\hat{\Pi}_{\ell+1}
. It holds that

\displaystyle\quad\mu_{p}^{N}(\mu_{p}((x,u_{H})\in\mathcal{T}(\hat{S}_{\ell},H))<\epsilon)>1-\beta,

(20)

where the violation set
\mathcal{T}(\hat{S}_{\ell},H)
is defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\hat{S}_{\ell},H):=\{(x,u_{H}):\mathcal{B}_{H}(S_{\Sigma},x,u_{H})\notin\mathcal{B}_{H}(\hat{S}_{\ell})\},

where

\epsilon:=\epsilon(s^{*}_{N},\beta,N)
as defined in Theorem
1
.
\square

In practical terms, Lemma
2
provides an upper bound on the probability of sampling a behaviour in the concrete system that does not belong to the set of behaviours of the data-driven SA
\ell
CA. This concept is related to the notion of
behavioural inclusion

[
29
]
: given two systems, if the set of all behaviours of one system includes the set of all behaviours of the other, we say that the first system behaviourally includes the second. If (
20
) holds, according to
[
10
]
, we say that
\hat{S}_{\ell}
behaviourally includes
S_{\Sigma}
until horizon
H
with probability greater or equal to
1-\epsilon
.

Lemma
2
is a consequence of
[
8
, Section V.C.]
, where the authors draw
N
samples from a countable alphabet, determine which and how many unique symbols were collected and bound the probability measure of the set of unseen symbols, that is, those that were not among the
N
samples.
If
\ell+1=H
the construction of the data-driven SA
\ell
CA corresponds essentially to the situation just described, where the alphabet is given by
\Gamma_{H}
.
In general, we construct a data-driven SA
\ell
CA using (
18
), i.e. we draw samples belonging to
\Gamma^{\ell+1}
which may arise in the first
H
time instants of the system’s time evolution.

Intuitively, reducing the size of the alphabet by reducing
\ell
reduces the complexity
s^{*}_{N}
, which in turn tightens the scenario bounds; on the other hand, reducing
\ell
also increases the number of behaviours that the data-driven SA
\ell
CA can generate, leading to a coarser abstraction.

Let us consider the relation (
19
) between
S_{\Sigma}
and
\hat{S}_{\ell}
; we combine Lemma
1
with Lemma
2
to assign a probability measure to the pairs
(x,u_{H})
resulting in a full behaviour in the concrete system which cannot be related by
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
with a full behaviour in the abstract system. This is formally stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 1

Consider
S_{\Sigma}
, the product probability space
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{W},\mu_{p})
obtained from
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{G},\mu_{x})
and
(\mathcal{U}^{H},\mathcal{F},\mu_{u_{H}})
as defined in Section
II

\ell

\hat{S}_{\ell}
obtained from the set
\hat{\Pi}_{\ell+1}
. Given a confidence parameter
\beta
, it holds that

\mu_{p}^{N}(\mu_{p}((x,u_{H})\in\overline{\mathcal{V}}(\hat{S}_{\ell},\hat{\mathcal{R}},H))<\epsilon)>1-\beta

(21)

where
\epsilon:=\epsilon(s^{*}_{N},\beta,N)
as defined in (
4
), and

\overline{\mathcal{V}}(\hat{S}_{\ell},\hat{\mathcal{R}},H):=\{(x,u_{H}):\exists(\xi,\gamma,\upsilon,\xi^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})\in i_{u_{H}},\\
\not\exists(\xi_{\ell},\gamma_{\ell},\upsilon_{\ell},\xi^{\prime}_{\ell},\gamma^{\prime}_{\ell})\in i_{u_{H}}^{\ell}\ .\ (\gamma,\upsilon,\gamma^{\prime})=(\gamma_{\ell},\upsilon_{\ell},\gamma_{\ell}^{\prime})\wedge\\
\forall k\geq-1\ .\ (\xi^{\prime}(k),\xi_{\ell}^{\prime}(k))\in\hat{\mathcal{R}}\}

(22)

Proof:

First, we show that the probability of drawing a pair
(x,u_{H})
resulting in an external
H

VII
Discussion and Concluding Remarks

We have proposed a fully data-driven framework to construct an abstraction suitable for controlling a discrete-time deterministic control system, by introducing the notion of probabilistic alternating simulation. The data-driven SA
\ell
CA does not require any reachability analysis, which is typically considered a bottleneck for model-based abstractions. Since the data-driven SA
\ell
CA is constructed from sampled behaviours the number of transitions in the abstraction
\hat{\Pi}_{\ell+1}
, and hence the number of states, will automatically adjust based on the dynamics of the system. Moreover,
\ell
is a tuneable parameter and can be increased or decreased to return a more or less refined abstraction respectively, see
[
10
]
. Importantly, the resulting abstraction is independent of the control objective, that is, one can use the same abstraction to synthesize controllers solving different problems, without the need to resample and reconstruct the abstraction when the design objective is changed.

On the other hand, we can observe that the scenario bounds derived in the examples
according to Proposition
1
, which in turn is a direct application of Theorem
1
, when compared to the empirical violation, can be conservative by an order of magnitude. This is because effectively we draw samples from a discrete event space
\Gamma_{H}
(the
H
-long behaviours), making the construction of the abstraction a so-called degenerate problem, as mentioned in Remark
1
. Note that Equations (
24
)-(
25
) are not conservative since the inequality is an equality if every violating initial condition has
exactly
one input sequence out of the possible
|\mathcal{U}^{H}|
which produce an unregistered behaviour. However, this event is highly unlikely; hence, when closing the loop in the mountain car example
we observe a considerably smaller empirical violation (no violations over
10^{6}
new samples) compared to the guaranteed upper bound
\overline{\epsilon}
.

In conclusion, for a fixed number of samples, if the control objective is known and fixed, synthesizing a controller with a technique suitable for black-box models as reinforcement learning, and successively verifying the design might result in tighter bounds, since, as argued Section
V-B
and experimentally observed in the Mountain Car example, for the closed-loop system the correcting factor of
|\mathcal{U}^{H}|
equals 1.

We further prove that we can provide guarantees for a horizon larger than the sampling one, under additional conditions on the concrete model.
Future work includes the construction of abstractions for systems with noise.
