Kemeny’s constant and the Lemoine point of a simplex
Karel Devriendt

Abstract
Kemeny’s constant is an invariant of discrete-time Markov chains, equal to the expected number of steps between two states sampled from the stationary distribution. It appears in applications as a concise characterization of the mixing properties of a Markov chain and has many alternative definitions. In this short article, we derive a new geometric expression for Kemeny’s constant, which involves the distance between two points in a simplex associated to the Markov chain: the circumcenter and the Lemoine point. Our proof uses an expression due to Wang, Dubbeldam and Van Mieghem of Kemeny’s constant in terms of effective resistances and Fiedler’s interpretation of effective resistances as edge lengths of a simplex. Keywords: Markov chains, Kemeny’s constant, simplex geometry, commute time AMS subject classification : 60J10, 05C81, 52B99, 51K99

Markov chains, Kemeny’s constant and commute times

A discrete-time Markov chain
M
is a sequence
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of random variables on a state space
\mathcal{X}
, where the distribution of
X_{k+1}
only depends on
X_{k}
. This distribution can be characterized by the transition probabilities

P_{xy}:=\Pr[X_{k+1}=y\,|\,X_{k}=x]\text{~for all $x,y\in\mathcal{X}$}.

We will assume that
|\mathcal{X}|
is finite which means that
P
is a matrix, the transition matrix. A finite Markov chain is fully determined by its state space, the distribution
\pi_{0}
of its initial state and its transition matrix and we may thus write
M=(\mathcal{X},P,\pi_{0})
. We will assume that
M
is irreducible and aperiodic: there exists a finite
t_{0}>0
such that for all integers
t\geq t_{0}
, the matrix
P^{t}
has all entries positive. Under these assumptions, the distribution of
X_{n}
converges to a unique distribution
\pi
, the stationary distribution
[
18
]
.

For two states
x
and
y
, the hitting time
h_{xy}
from
x
to
y
is the expected number of steps of the Markov chain starting at
x
to reach
y
; more precisely

h_{xy}:=\mathbb{E}\left(\min\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,X_{0}=x\text{~and~}X_{t}=y\right\}\right)

with expectation over realizations of the Markov chain. We note that
h_{xx}=0
for every state
x
. For a fixed state
x
and a random state
Y
with distribution
\pi
, the expected hitting time from
x
to
Y
is equal to

K_{x}:=\mathbb{E}(h_{xY})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(y)h_{xy}

where the expectation is over realizations of the random state
Y
. The interest in the quantity
K_{x}
stems from the observation, first noted in
[
14
]
, that
K_{x}
is independent of
x
.

Theorem 2.1
.

In a finite, irreducible, aperiodic Markov chain
M
with stationary distribution
\pi
and hitting times
h
, the value
K_{x}=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(y)h_{xy}
is independent of
x
. This common value is called Kemeny’s constant and is denoted by
K=K(M)
.

Since
K_{x}
is independent of
x
, we can rewrite Kemeny’s constant as

K=\sum_{x}\pi(x)K_{x}=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\pi(y)h_{xy}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\pi(y)(h_{xy}+h_{yx}).

The sum
h_{xy}+h_{yx}
is the expected number of steps it takes a Markov chain which starts at
x
to reach
y
and then
x
again. This is called the commute time and is denoted by
c_{xy}:=h_{xy}+h_{yx}
. Note that
c
is symmetric and
c_{xx}=0
for every state
x
. As shown in
[
19
]
, this gives Kemeny’s constant as

K=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\pi(y)c_{xy}.

(1)

For Theorem
4.4
, we require two further assumptions on the Markov chain
M
. First, we assume that
M
is loop-free

2

Without this assumption, the transition matrix cannot be represented by a Laplacian matrix (see Section
3
).
, which means that
P_{xx}=0
for every state
x
and second, we assume that
M
is reversible, which is defined by the condition
P_{xy}\pi(x)=P_{yx}\pi(y)
for every pair of states

and

. We collect the assumptions for further reference:

\text{~$M$ is a discrete-time, finite, irreducible, aperiodic, reversible and loop-free Markov chain}.

(2)

We will say that

is a (
2
)-Markov chain if it satisfies these conditions.

3
The simplex associated with a Markov chain

A
d
-dimensional simplex
S
is the convex hull of
d+1
affinely independent points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which are called the vertices of
S
. A face of a simplex is the convex hull of a subset of its vertices, and every face is again a simplex. A one-dimensional face is called an edge and a
(d-1)
-dimensional face is called a facet.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
M
be a (
2
)-Markov chain on
\mathcal{X}
. Then there exists an
(|\mathcal{X}|-1)
-dimensional simplex whose squared edge lengths are equal to the commute times of
M
.

Our proof combines the derivation of Chandra et al.
[
6
]
for the relation between commute time and effective resistance, with Fiedler’s result that the effective resistance is the squared Euclidean distance between the vertices of a simplex
[
12
]
. We first prove a technical lemma on certain matrices that can be associated with Markov chains. Let
L
be the symmetric
|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}|
matrix with diagonal entries
L_{xx}=\pi(x)
and non-positive off-diagonal entries
L_{xy}=-\pi(x)P_{xy}
for all
x\neq y
. This is known as the Laplacian matrix (of the weighted graph associated with
M
) and is a well-studied object in algebraic and spectral graph theory
[
7
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
L
be the Laplacian matrix associated with a (
2
)-Markov chain on
\mathcal{X}
. Then there exists a matrix
L^{\dagger}
which is the inverse of
L
on
\operatorname{span}(1)^{\perp}
and which can be decomposed as
L^{\dagger}=V^{T}V
, where
V
is a
(|\mathcal{X}|-1)\times|\mathcal{X}|
matrix with affinely independent columns and
V1=0
.

Proof.

Note that by construction
L_{xx}=-\sum_{y}L_{xy}
for all
x
, which means that the rows of
L
sum to zero. From the quadratic form determined by the Laplacian matrix

\displaystyle f^{T}Lf

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}f(x)\Big(\sum_{y\neq x}L_{xy}f(y)+L_{xx}f(x)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}f(x)\sum_{y\neq x}L_{xy}(f(y)-f(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}L_{xy}(f(y)-f(x))(f(x)-f(y))

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}L_{xy}(f(x)-f(y))^{2}\geq 0,

(3)

we find that
L
is positive semidefinite. Furthermore, the quadratic form (
3
) is equal to zero if and only if
f(x)=f(y)
whenever
L_{xy}\neq 0
or, equivalently, whenever
P_{xy}\neq 0
. By irreducibility of
M
, this implies that
f^{T}Lf
is zero if and only if
f
is constant on
\mathcal{X}
. Thus
L
is positive semidefinite with
\operatorname{ker}(L)
spanned by the constant vector; in particular,
L
is invertible when restricted to
\operatorname{span}(1)^{\perp}
. This is a classical result, see for instance
[
7
]
.

The Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse
L^{\dagger}
of
L
is the unique matrix which satisfies

L^{\dagger}LL^{\dagger}=L^{\dagger},\quad LL^{\dagger}L=L,\quad(LL^{\dagger})^{T}=LL^{\dagger},\quad(L^{\dagger}L)^{T}=L^{\dagger}L.

This matrix always exists uniquely and its definition implies that
L^{\dagger}
is positive semidefinite and has
\operatorname{ker}(L^{\dagger})
spanned by the constant vector. Furthermore,
L^{\dagger}
is the inverse of
L
in
\operatorname{ker}(L)^{\perp}=\operatorname{span}(1)^{\perp}
. Since
L^{\dagger}
is positive semidefinite of rank
|\mathcal{X}|-1
, there exists a full-rank
|\mathcal{X}|\times(|\mathcal{X}|-1)
matrix
V=[v_{1}~\dots~v_{|\mathcal{X}|}]^{T}
such that
L^{\dagger}=V^{T}V
and
V1=0
.

Now suppose for contradiction that the columns of
V
are not affinely independent. Then
0=\sum_{i=2}^{|\mathcal{X}|}r_{i}(v_{i}-v_{1})
for some non-zero coefficients
r_{2},\dots r_{|\mathcal{X}|}
. But then we can define new coefficients
\tilde{r}_{i}=r_{i}
for
i\neq 1
and
\tilde{r}_{1}=-\sum_{i=2}^{|\mathcal{X}|}\tilde{r}_{i}
which sum to zero, i.e.,
\tilde{r}\perp\operatorname{span}(1)
, and satisfy
0=\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{X}|}\tilde{r}_{i}v_{i}
; in other words
\tilde{r}\in\operatorname{ker}(V)
. However, since
V
has full rank and
V1=0
, we know that
\operatorname{ker}(V)=\operatorname{span}(1)
and thus
\tilde{r}\perp\operatorname{ker}(V)
. This contradiction proves that the columns of
V
must be affinely independent and completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3.1
, which associates a simplex to every (
2
)-Markov chain.

Proof.

(of Theorem
3.1
)

Let
M
be a (
2
)-Markov chain. Since
M
is irreducible, the hitting times
h
are finite and well-defined. Fix a state
x
, then the hitting times to
x
satisfy the recursion

h_{tx}=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{X}}P_{tz}(1+h_{zx})\text{~for all $t\in\mathcal{X}\backslash x$}.

Multiplying both sides by
\pi(t)
and using the fact that
\sum_{z}P_{tz}=1
, we obtain

\pi(t)=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{X}}-L_{tz}(h_{tx}-h_{zx})\text{~for all $t\in\mathcal{X}\backslash x$},

(4)

where we recall that
L_{tz}=-\pi(t)P_{tz}
are off-diagonal entries of the Laplacian matrix. We now analyse this system of equations using matrix and vector notation. We consider the following functions on the states as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|}
: the stationary distribution
\pi
, the constant vector
1
, the hitting times
h_{x}:t\mapsto h_{tx}
to
x
and the indicator function
\delta_{x}:y\mapsto 1
iff
y=x
. Equation (
4
) can then be written using the Laplacian matrix
L
as

Lh_{x}=\pi+\alpha_{x}\delta_{x},

where
\alpha_{x}
is some constant which indicates that (
4
) is determined except at the diagonal. Using
1^{T}\pi=1
and
1^{T}L=0
, which holds by construction of
L
, we find that this constant equals

0=1^{T}Lh_{x}=1^{T}\pi+\alpha_{x}1^{T}\delta_{x}=1+\alpha_{x}\Rightarrow\alpha_{x}=-1.

Since
x
was an arbitrary state, we may repeat the derivation above for
y
to obtain
Lh_{y}=\pi-\delta_{y}
. Combining the expressions for
x
and
y
, this yields

L(h_{x}-h_{y})=\delta_{y}-\delta_{x}.

(5)

Multiplying (
5
) from the left with
(\delta_{y}-\delta_{x})^{T}L^{\dagger}
, where we recall that
L^{\dagger}
is an inverse of
L
in
\operatorname{span}(1)^{\perp}
as in Lemma
3.2
, we find

(\delta_{y}-\delta_{x})^{T}(h_{x}-h_{y})=(\delta_{y}-\delta_{x})^{T}L^{\dagger}(\delta_{y}-\delta_{x}).

With the definition of the commute times and using the decomposition
L^{\dagger}=V^{T}V
, we obtain

c_{xy}=(\delta_{y}-\delta_{x})^{T}L^{\dagger}(\delta_{y}-\delta_{x})=(\delta_{y}-\delta_{x})^{T}V^{T}V(\delta_{y}-\delta_{x})=\|v_{y}-v_{x}\|_{2}^{2}.

(6)

Since the columns
v_{1},\dots,v_{|\mathcal{X}|}
of
V
are
|\mathcal{X}|
affinely independent points in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|-1}
by Lemma
3.2
, they are the vertices of a (
|\mathcal{X}|-1
)-dimensional simplex. By (
6
), the squared edge lengths of this simplex are equal to the commute times in
M
, which completes the proof.
∎

4
Special points in the simplex and Kemeny’s constant

The relation between the Markov chain
M
and the simplex
S
can be thought of as an embedding which maps the states of
M
onto the vertices of
S
, and distributions on
\mathcal{X}
to points in
S
. More generally, this embedding associates to every point in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|-1}
a unique unit-sum function on
\mathcal{X}
which we call its coordinates
3
3

3

This is called the “barycentric coordinates” in
[
12
]
.
: the
coordinates
of a point
p
are denoted by
\hat{p}
and are uniquely determined by
\sum_{x}\hat{p}(x)=1
and
\sum_{x}\hat{p}(x)v_{x}=p
where
v_{x}
is the vertex associated with state
x
.

We start with two lemmas that express distances between points and facets of the simplex in terms of their coordinates.

Lemma 4.1

(
[
12
, Cor. 1.4.14]
)
.

Let
M
be a Markov chain with stationary distribution
\pi
and associated simplex
S
. The distance from a point with coordinates
\hat{p}
to the facet
S_{\mathcal{X}\backslash x}
opposite the vertex associated with
x
is equal to
|\hat{p}(x)/\sqrt{\pi(x)}|
.

Lemma 4.2

(
[
12
, Cor. 1.2.10]
)
.

Let
M
be a (
2
)-Markov chain with associated simplex
S
and commute times
c
. The squared distance between two points
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|-1}
with coordinates
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
is equal to

\|a-b\|_{2}^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}c_{xy}[\hat{a}(x)-\hat{b}(x)][\hat{a}(y)-\hat{b}(y)].

Proof.

Let
V=[v_{1}~\dots~v_{|\mathcal{X}|}]^{T}
be the matrix with vertices of the simplex
S
as columns, such that
a=V\hat{a}
and
b=V\hat{b}
. Since
c_{xy}=(v_{x}-v_{y})^{T}(v_{x}-v_{y})=v_{x}^{T}v_{x}+v_{y}^{T}v_{y}-2v_{x}^{T}v_{y}
, the commute time matrix equals
C=1\nu^{T}+\nu 1^{T}-2V^{T}V
where
\nu=\operatorname{diag}(V^{T}V)
. We then find

\|a-b\|_{2}^{2}=(\hat{a}-\hat{b})^{T}V^{T}V(\hat{a}-\hat{b})=(\hat{a}-\hat{b})^{T}[1\nu^{T}+\nu 1^{T}-\tfrac{1}{2}C](\hat{a}-\hat{b})=-\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{a}-\hat{b})^{T}C(\hat{a}-\hat{b}),

where we use that coordinates are unit-sum functions and thus
1^{T}(\hat{a}-\hat{b})=0
.
∎

We can now consider the two points of interest for the main result.

Proposition 4.3

(
[
12
, Cor. 1.4.13, Thm. 2.2.3]
)
.

Let
M
be a (
2
)-Markov chain with simplex
S
and commute times
c
.

•

The
circumcenter

\gamma
is the unique point at equal distance
R
from all vertices of
S
. Its coordinates
\hat{\gamma}
are determined by the equations
\sum_{y}c_{xy}\hat{\gamma}(y)=2R^{2}
for every state
x
.

•

The
Lemoine point

\ell
is the unique point at minimal total squared distance to the facets of
S
. Its coordinates equal
\hat{\ell}=\pi
.

Proof.
We start with the circumcenter. For every state
x
, the distance from the vertex
v_{x}
(with coordinates
\hat{v_{x}}=\delta_{x}
) to the circumcenter
\gamma
is equal to
R
. By Lemma
4.2
, we find

R^{2}=d^{2}(v_{x},\gamma)\,=\,-\frac{1}{2}[\delta_{x}-\hat{\gamma}]^{T}C[\delta_{x}-\hat{\gamma}]\,=\,\delta_{x}^{T}C\hat{\gamma}-\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{\gamma}^{T}C\hat{\gamma}.

Since
R
and
\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{\gamma}^{T}C\hat{\gamma}
are independent of
x
, so must
\delta_{x}^{T}C\hat{\gamma}
be, and thus
C\hat{\gamma}=\alpha\cdot 1
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Introducing this expression back into the equation above, we obtain
\alpha
as

R^{2}=\delta_{x}^{T}C\hat{\gamma}-\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{\gamma}^{T}C\hat{\gamma}\iff R^{2}=\alpha-\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha\iff\alpha=2R^{2}.

We thus find that
\hat{\gamma}
must satisfy
C\hat{\gamma}=2R^{2}\cdot 1
which completes the first part of the Proposition.

We continue with the Lemoine point and follow the derivation of Fiedler in
[
12
, Thm. 2.2.3]
. Let
p
be a point in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|-1}
. By Lemma

, the total squared distance from
p
to the facets of

is equal to

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}d^{2}(p,S_{\mathcal{X}\backslash x})=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\hat{p}(x)^{2}}{\pi(x)}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\hat{p}(x)^{2}}{{\pi(x)}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{\pi(x)}\geq\Big(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\hat{p}(x)\Big)^{2}=1.

Here, we used the fact that
\pi
and
\hat{p}
are unit-sum vectors and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
\big(\sum_{x}u_{x}v_{x}\big)^{2}\leq\big(\sum_{x}u_{x}^{2}\big)\big(\sum_{x}v_{x}^{2}\big)
with
u_{x}=|\hat{p}(x)|/\sqrt{\pi(x)}
and
v_{x}=\sqrt{\pi(x)}
. Equality in the Cauchy–Schwarz bound is achieved if and only if
u_{x}=v_{x}
, and thus
\hat{p}(x)=\pi(x)
in this case. In other words, the smallest total squared distance to all facets is
1
and this is achieved by the point with coordinates
\pi
, as claimed. This completes the proof.
∎

We note that the Lemoine point always lies in the interior of the simplex
S

\pi(x)>0
for all

. In

. What does

Proof.

Let
M

2

\displaystyle\|\gamma-\ell\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}c_{xy}[\hat{\ell}(x)-\hat{\gamma}(x)][\hat{\ell}(y)-\hat{\gamma}(y)]\quad\text{~(Lemma \ref{lemma: distances as quadratic forms})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}c_{xy}[\pi(x)-\hat{\gamma}(x)][\pi(y)-\hat{\gamma}(y)]\quad\text{~(Proposition \ref{proposition: circumcenter and Lemoine point coordinates})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}c_{xy}\pi(x)\pi(y)+c_{xy}\pi(x)\hat{\gamma}(y)-\tfrac{1}{2}c_{xy}\hat{\gamma}(x)\hat{\gamma}(y)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}c_{xy}\pi(x)\pi(y)+2R^{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}(\pi(x)-\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{\gamma}(x))\quad\text{(Proposition \ref{proposition: circumcenter and Lemoine point coordinates})}

\displaystyle=-K+R^{2}\quad\quad\text{(Eq. \eqref{eq: definition Kemeny's constant via commute times})}.

This completes the proof.
∎
