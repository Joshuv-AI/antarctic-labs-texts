Damian Dąbrowski, Michele Villa

Abstract
. Our main result marks progress on an old conjecture of Vitushkin. We show that a compact set in the plane with plenty of big projections (PBP) has positive analytic capacity, along with a quantitative lower bound. A higher dimensional counterpart is also proved for capacities related to the Riesz kernel, including the Lipschitz harmonic capacity. The proof uses a construction of a doubling Frostman measure on a lower content regular set, which may be of independent interest. Our second main result is the Analyst’s Traveling Salesman Theorem for sets with plenty of big projections. As a corollary, we obtain a lower bound for the Hausdorff dimension of uniformly wiggly sets with PBP. The second corollary is an estimate for the capacities of subsets of sets with PBP, in the spirit of the quantitative solution to Denjoy’s conjecture.

1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to study sets with plenty of big projections.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
plenty of (
d
-dimensional) big projections
(abbreviated to PBP, or
d
-PBP) if there exists a constant
0<\delta\leq 1
such that the following holds. For all
x\in E
and
0<r<\diam(E)
there exists
V_{x,r}\in{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
such that

\mathcal{H}^{d}(\pi_{V}(E\cap B(x,r)))\geq\delta r^{d}\quad\text{for all }V\in B(V_{x,r},\delta).

Here
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
is the Grassmannian manifold of
d
-dimensional (linear) planes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\pi_{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to V
is the orthogonal projection, and the ball
B(V_{x,r},\delta)
is defined with respect to the standard metric on
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
(see §
2.1
).

This definition first appeared in
[
DS93b
]
, although originally David and Semmes assumed additionally that sets with PBP are Ahlfors regular.

Definition 1.2
.

A set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
Ahlfors
d
-regular
if there exists a constant
C\geq 1
such that for all
x\in E
and
0<r<\diam(E)
we have

C^{-1}r^{d}\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}(E\cap B(x,r))\leq Cr^{d}.

The smallest constant
C
for which the above holds is called the
Ahlfors regularity constant
of
E
.

In
[
DS93b
]
David and Semmes conjectured that Ahlfors regular sets with PBP satisfy the so-called
big pieces of Lipschitz graphs condition
, a strong quantitative rectifiability property (see Definition
2.4
). Their conjecture was recently solved in a breakthrough work of Orponen
[
Orp21
]
(see Theorem
2.5
for the precise statement).

The novelty of our article is twofold: on the level of results, we use Orponen’s theorem to study the analytic capacity of sets with PBP, thus making progress on Vitushkin’s and quantitative Denjoy’s conjectures, and the dimension of wiggly sets. Beyond these results, our proof presents methodological interest, and this is the other level of novelty. Indeed, for the problems at hand we are required to work outside the Ahlfors regularity category: we thus propose a method that allows to move from this rather strong size condition to sets which might even have non-
\sigma
-finite
\mathcal{H}^{d}
-measure. This method is highly flexible, and relies on three distinct steps: starting with a (compact) set
E
with
d
-PBP (for which the
\mathcal{H}^{d}
-measure is not necessarily
\sigma
-finite), we

•

construct a Frostman measure supported on
E
, which is also doubling,

•

give a multiscale approximation of this measure by Ahlfors regular sets,

•

show that the PBP property is inherited by the approximating sets: this, by the result of Orponen, allow us to infer some key geometric facts about
E
.

In the finite measure case (but not necessarily Ahlfors regular), this method was layed out rather explicitly in
[
AV21
]
. The first step above allows us to move this method to the general case, which is highly relevant for the Vitushkin’s conjecture mentioned above, and, more generally, in contexts where the natural size to consider is capacity, rather than Hausdorff measure. We suspect that this method can be applied to other quantitative properties developed in the David-Semmes theory
[
DS91
,
DS93a
]
, thus giving bounds for analytic and Lipschitz harmonic capacities for sets satisfying any one of these properties.

Now we move on to the precise statements of our results.

1.1.
Analytic capacity: Vitushkin’s conjecture and quantitative Denjoy’s conjecture

Recall that a compact set
E\subset{\mathbb{C}}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is said to be
removable for bounded analytic functions
if all bounded analytic functions
f
defined on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus E
are constant. In the 40s Ahlfors
[
Ahl47
]
managed to quantify the notion of removability by introducing
analytic capacity
. Recall that the analytic capacity of a compact set
E\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is defined as

\gamma(E)=\sup|f^{\prime}(\infty)|,

where the supremum is taken over all analytic functions
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{C}}\setminus E\to{\mathbb{C}}
with
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, and
f^{\prime}(\infty)=\lim_{z\to\infty}z(f(z)-f(\infty))
. Ahlfors proved that a set is removable for bounded analytic functions if and only if
\gamma(E)=0
.

In 1967 Vitushkin
[
Vit67
]
conjectured a geometric characterization of removability in terms of orthogonal projections. He asked the following: is it true that for compact sets
E\subset{\mathbb{C}}
one has

(1.1)

\gamma(E)=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\Fav(E)=0,

where
\Fav(E)
is the Favard length of
E
, defined as

\Fav(E)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\pi_{\theta}(E))\ d\theta.

In the above
\pi_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{C}}\to\ell_{\theta}
is the orthogonal projection to
\ell_{\theta}=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}((\sin\theta,\cos\theta))
.

It has been known for a very long time that sets with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(E)=0
are removable, while sets with
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dim}_{H}(E)>1
are non-removable, so Vitushkin’s conjecture holds for such sets. The case of sets
E
satisfying
0<\mathcal{H}^{1}(E)<\infty
was much more difficult to establish, but the answer to Vitushkin’s question is also positive: the implication
(\Rightarrow)
in (
1.1
) is due to Calderón
[
Cal77
]
, while
(\Leftarrow)
was shown by David
[
Dav98
]
. Finally, the case of sets with
\sigma
-finite
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-measure follows from the finite measure case together with subadditivity of analytic capacity, which is due to Tolsa
[
Tol03
]
.

In 1986 Mattila
[
Mat86
]
showed that Vitushkin’s conjecture fails for sets with Hausdorff dimension
1
and non-
\sigma
-finite
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-measure. It wasn’t clear from Mattila’s proof which of the implications in (
1.1
) is false. Soon thereafter Jones and Murai
[
JM88
]
constructed an example of a set with
\gamma(E)>0
and
\Fav(E)=0
. A simpler example was found later on by Joyce and Mörters
[
JM00
]
. Hence, the implication
(\Leftarrow)
in (
1.1
) is false. For more information on analytic capacity and Vitushkin’s conjecture see the books
[
Tol14
,
Dud11
,
Paj02
]
. See also recent surveys on related topics
[
Ver21
,
Mat22
]
.

After all these extraordinary developments one question remains: what about the implication
(\Rightarrow)
in (
1.1
)? Equivalently, is it true that

(1.2)

\Fav(E)>0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\gamma(E)>0?

A quantitative version of this question is the following: is it true that

(1.3)

\gamma(E)\gtrsim\Fav(E)?

As far as we know, the only partial result related to these open problems is due to Chang and Tolsa
[
CT20
]
(more on that in Remark
1.8
). In this article we make further progress on (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). If the assumption
\Fav(E)>0
is replaced by the (significantly stronger) PBP assumption, then the analytic capacity is positive.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a compact set with
1
-PBP. Then,

(1.4)

\gamma(E)\gtrsim\diam(E),

where the implicit constant depends only on the PBP constant.

Remark 1.4
.

In the theorem above we do not assume
\mathcal{H}^{1}(E)<\infty
. However, our result bears new information even in the
\mathcal{H}^{1}(E)<\infty
case. Recall that in this regime Vitushkin’s conjecture is true, so in particular
\Fav(E)>0
implies
\gamma(E)>0
. However, the existing proof does not offer any quantitative lower bound on
\gamma(E)
in terms of
\Fav(E)
. Roughly speaking, the reason is the following: the Besicovitch projection theorem states that if
\Fav(E)>0
, then there exists a
1
-dimensional Lipschitz graph
\Gamma
such that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(E\cap\Gamma)>0
, and then
\gamma(E)\geq\gamma(E\cap\Gamma)>0
follows from
[
Cal77
]
. However, the Besicovitch projection theorem does not say anything about the size of
\mathcal{H}^{1}(E\cap\Gamma)
, and so deriving a quantitative lower bound for
\gamma(E)
is impossible as long as one follows this strategy. In other words, the question (
1.3
) remains wide open even in the
\mathcal{H}^{1}(E)<\infty
case. Although Theorem
1.3
is quite far from establishing (
1.3
), it is (as far as we know) the first quantitative lower bound for
\gamma(E)
which depends only on the projections of
E
.

In the finite measure case, we are able to prove more: we show a quantitative bound for the analytic capacity of
subsets
of sets with
1
-PBP. This is a form of quantitative Denjoy’s conjecture, where rectifiable curves are substituted with sets with PBP of finite measure. Recall that Denjoy’s conjecture, which predates Vitushkin’s conjecture by about 60 years, stated that if
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a rectifiable curve, and a compact set
E\subset\Gamma
satisfies
\mathcal{H}^{1}(E)>0
, then
\gamma(E)>0
. This statement can be seen as a special case of Vitushkin’s conjecture, if one restricts attention to the implication (
1.2
) and sets of finite length. Denjoy’s conjecture was confirmed by Calderón in
[
Cal77
]
. A quantitative version of Calderón’s result was established by Murai
[
Mur87
]
, who showed that if
\Gamma
is a
1
-rectifiable graph, and
E\subset\Gamma
is compact, then

\gamma(E)\gtrsim\frac{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\infty}(E)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma)^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Later on Verdera observed that Murai’s estimate can be obtained for arbitrary rectifiable curves
\Gamma
using Menger curvature and Jones’ Travelling Salesman Theorem, see
[
Tol14
, Theorem 4.31]
. The exponent
3/2
above is optimal, see
[
Mur90
]
and
[
Tol14
, §4.8]
.

Using an Analyst’s Travelling Salesman Theorem for sets with PBP (see Theorem
1.10
below) we obtain the following.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a compact set with
1
-PBP and
0<\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)<\infty
. Then, for any compact subset
E\subset\Sigma

\displaystyle\gamma(E)\gtrsim\frac{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\infty}(E)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)^{\frac{1}{2}}},

where the implicit constant depends only on the PBP constant.

Theorem
1.5
is interesting for two reasons: first, as mentioned above, it applies to subsets of sets with PBP. These subsets could very well have vanishing density in many places, and have very small projections. Note that a similar statement cannot be true for sets
\Gamma
with infinite length: for example, the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
has PBP, but the 4-corner Cantor set
K\subset[0,1]^{2}
satisfies
\mathcal{H}^{1}(K)\sim 1
and
\gamma(K)=0
.

The second interesting aspect of Theorem
1.5
is that it suggests that, for many geometric problems, sets with PBP and finite measure are just as good as rectifiable curves.
Let us however make the following remark: from the Analyst’s TST for sets with PBP (Theorem
1.10
below) it follows that a set with PBP and finite
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-measure can be covered by a rectifiable curve of comparable length. Thus, Theorem
1.5
can be derived directly from Theorem
1.10
together with the previous result of Murai and Verdera. However, in higher dimension (see the section below) the result is altogether new, since no result analogous to that of Murai was known for capacities associated to vector-valued Riesz kernel.

1.2.
Higher dimensional variants

Higher dimensional variants of Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.5
are also true. Instead of analytic capacity one has to consider certain capacities associated to the vector-valued Riesz kernel. Let
0<d<n
be integers. Given a compact set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the capacity
\Gamma_{n,d}(E)
is defined as

\Gamma_{n,d}(E)=\sup|\langle T,1\rangle|,

where the supremum is taken over all real distributions
T
supported in
E
such that

\frac{x}{|x|^{d+1}}\ast T\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\quad\text{and}\quad\bigg\|\frac{x}{|x|^{d+1}}\ast T\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq 1.

We remark that by
[
Tol03
]
one has
\Gamma_{2,1}(E)\sim\gamma(E)
. In the codimension 1 case the capacity
\Gamma_{d+1,d}(E)
is also called the
Lipschitz harmonic capacity
, and it is often denoted by
\kappa(E)
. It was introduced by Paramonov, who observed that sets with
\kappa(E)=0
are precisely the sets removable for Lipschitz harmonic functions, see
[
Par90
, Remark 2.4]
and
[
MP95
]
. The counterpart of Vitushkin’s conjecture for
\kappa(E)
and sets with
\mathcal{H}^{d}(E)<\infty
was established by Nazarov, Tolsa and Volberg in
[
NTV14b
,
NTV14a
]
. More specifically, they showed that for a compact set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with
\mathcal{H}^{d}(E)<\infty
one has
\kappa(E)=0
if and only if
E
is purely
d
-unrectifiable (equivalently,
\mathcal{H}^{d}(\pi_{V}(E))=0
for a.e.
V\in\mathcal{G}(n,d)
). Whether an analogous statement is true for
\Gamma_{n,d}
with
1<n<d-1
is an open problem.

The higher dimensional variants of Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.5
are the following.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact set with
d
-PBP. Then,

(1.5)

\Gamma_{n,d}(E)\gtrsim\diam(E)^{d},

where the implicit constant depends only on
n,d,
and the PBP constant.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact set with
d
-PBP and
0<\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma)<\infty
. Then for any compact subset
E\subset\Sigma
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n,d}(E)\gtrsim\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma)^{\frac{1}{2}}},

where the implicit constant depends only on
n,d
, and the PBP constant.

Remark 1.8
.

Theorems
1.3
and
1.6
should be compared with the results of Chang and Tolsa
[
CT20
]
, who proved the following. If
I\subset[0,\pi)
is an interval and
E\subset\mathbb{C}
is a compact set supporting a probability measure
\mu
such that
\pi_{\theta}\mu\in L^{2}(\ell_{\theta})
for a.e.
\theta\in I
, then

\gamma(E)\gtrsim\frac{1}{\int_{I}\|\pi_{\theta}\mu\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\,d\theta},

with the implicit constant depending on
\mathcal{H}^{1}(I)
. They also prove a higher dimensional analogue of this estimate for capacities
\Gamma_{n,d}
.

The main advantage of
[
CT20
]
over our results is that their “
L^{2}
-projections” assumption is single-scale, while the PBP condition is multi-scale. On the other hand, our result may be easier to apply as it derives a lower bound for
\gamma(E)
directly from the information on the projections of
E
, without the need of constructing a measure supported on
E
and studying its projections.

Even more importantly, note that the
L^{2}
-projections assumption
\pi_{\theta}\mu\in L^{2}(\ell_{\theta})
implies a big projection
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\pi_{\theta}(E))\geq\|\pi_{\theta}\mu\|^{-2}_{2}
using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

1=\mu(E)=\|\pi_{\theta}\mu\|_{1}\leq\|\pi_{\theta}\mu\|_{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{1}(\pi_{\theta}(E))^{1/2}.

The difference between
L^{2}
-projections and big projections is fundamental. A characterization of Ahlfors regular sets with big pieces of Lipschitz graphs in terms of
L^{2}
-projections has been achieved by Martikainen and Orponen in
[
MO18
]
, but it took another major breakthrough
[
Orp21
]
to find an analogous characterization in terms of PBP. Moreover, a “single-scale version” of
[
Orp21
]
is an open problem, see
[
Orp21
, §1.3]
, whereas
[
MO18
]
contains the relevant single-scale result, see
[
MO18
, Theorem 1.7]
.

Remark 1.9
.

The proof of Theorem
1.6
is robust, and our techniques are likely to be useful in the future work on problems (
1.2
) and (
1.3
).

In particular, suppose that the following strengthening of Orponen’s result (Theorem
2.5
) was available: “if a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Ahlfors
d
-regular and it has
uniformly large Favard length
(there exists
C>0
such that for all
x\in E
and all
0<r<\diam(E)
we have
\Fav(E\cap B(x,r))\geq Cr^{d}
), then
E
has big pieces of Lipschitz graphs.” With such result at hand, the proof of Theorem
1.6
would immediately yield that any set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with uniformly large Favard length satisfies

\Gamma_{n,d}(E)\gtrsim\diam(E)^{d}.

A related question is of course the following: suppose that
E\subset[0,1]^{2}
is a
1
-Ahlfors regular set with
{\rm Fav}(E)\geq\delta
. Is it then true that there exists a Lipschitz graph
\Gamma
so that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(E\cap\Gamma)\gtrsim\delta
? A recent related result by A. Chang, T. Orponen and the authors
[
CDOV24
]
shows that if
E
has almost maximal Favard length, then it is contained in a Lipschitz graph with small constant, save for a tiny subset. A natural question is whether this result can be used to obtain a new estimate for analytic capacity.

1.3.
Analyst’s Traveling Salesman Theorem

Another main result of this article is the Analyst’s Travelling Salesman Theorem (TST) for sets with PBP. Recall that the original Analyst’s TST is due to Jones
[
Jon90
]
, and it is a characterization of sets
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that there exists a rectifiable curve
\Gamma
containing
E
, along with a quite precise estimate on
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma)
. Much work has been put into proving analogs of this result in other spaces; for example, Okikiolu
[
Oki92
]
proved an Analyst’s TST for curves in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and in
[
Sch07
]
Schul further generalized it to the Hilbert space setting. Another research direction consists in finding statements analogous to Jones’ TST but for higher dimensional sets rather than curves. While we are still lacking a theorem as complete as that of Jones, in recent years there has been much progress in this direction. Two major difficulties were: first, the coefficients used by Jones simply do not work for higher dimensional set; second, it is not obvious what to use as an analogue to curves (e.g. topological spheres do not work, see
[
Vil19
, Introduction]
). These issues have largely been overcome in the series of papers
[
AS18
,
AV21
,
Vil19
,
Hyd22
,
Hyd24
]
. We refer the reader to Sections 1 and 3 of
[
Vil19
]
for a more in-depth discussion of Analyst’s TST and its relevance. Let us mention that while these results are related to the rectifiability of sets, they do not say much about whether these sets can be parameterised. This is a major open problem in the area.

Our present result continues the line of work of Azzam, Schul, and the second author
[
AS18
,
AV21
]
. Let
\mathcal{D}
be a system of Christ-David cubes (see Lemma
2.1
), and set

(1.6)

\displaystyle\beta(Q_{0})=\beta_{E,C_{0},p,d}(Q_{0})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}(Q_{0})}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(C_{0}B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d},

where the coefficients
\beta_{E}^{d,p}
are a variant of Jones’
\beta
-numbers (
1.9
) introduced by Azzam and Schul in
[
AS18
]
, see Definition
2.12
.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a set with
d
-PBP with constant
\delta>0
,
\mathcal{D}
be a system of Christ-David cubes,
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{D}
and
C_{0}\geq 3
. Let
1\leq p<p(d)
, where
p(d)=\tfrac{2d}{d-2}
for
d>2
and
p(d)=\infty
if
d\leq 2
.
Then

(1.7)

\displaystyle\diam(Q_{0})^{d}+\beta(Q_{0})\sim\mathcal{H}^{d}(Q_{0}),

where the implicit constant depends on
\delta
,
C_{0}
,
p
,
n,d
and on the constants from the Azzam-Schul TST
(
see Theorem A.1 in
[
AV21
]
)
.

Remark 1.11
.

What we really prove here is one direction of the inequality (bound on the
\beta
sum with the measure), as the other one follows from
[
AS18
]
. See Proposition
6.2
.

Estimates similar to (
1.7
) have been proven in
[
AS18
]
and
[
AV21
]
for general
lower content regular sets
(see §
2.3
for the definition; sets with PBP are lower content regular), with the following crucial caveat: in general, an additional error term needs to be added to the right hand side of (
1.7
). In
[
Vil19
]
the second author proved that the error term may be omitted if one assumes that
E
is a
topologically stable
d
-surface
(a condition satisfied e.g. by Reifenberg flat sets, or Semmes surfaces), see
[
Vil19
, Theorem 3.6]
. In Theorem
1.10
we prove that the error term disappears also in the case of sets with PBP.
In other words, both PBP and topological stability give the set enough rigidity so that one can estimate its
\beta
-numbers using the Hausdorff measure.

To see why the estimate (
1.7
) may be useful, let us mention that in
[
AS18
, Theorem II]
it was shown that a lower content regular set with
\beta(E)<\infty
is rectifiable. Thus, any class of lower content regular sets for which (
1.7
) holds satisfies

(1.8)

\mathcal{H}^{d}(E)<\infty\,\implies\,\mbox{ rectifiability.}

In particular, (
1.8
) holds for topologically stable surfaces, and for sets with PBP (of course, for sets with PBP (
1.8
) easily follows from the Besicovitch projection theorem, without the need to refer to
\beta
-numbers and TST). Identifying classes of sets for which (
1.8
) holds is an interesting problem. For example, while (
1.8
) is true for connected one dimensional sets, there exist
2
-dimensional sets, homeomorphic to the
2
-sphere, with finite
\mathcal{H}^{2}
-measure, and containing a purely 2-unrectifiable set of positive measure (see
[
Vil19
, Figure 1]
) See also
[
DK19
]
and
[
DLD20
]
in connection with (
1.8
) and quantitative topological conditions.

Theorem
1.10
is used in the proofs of Theorems
1.5
and
1.7
. Another application is an estimate for the dimension of
wiggly sets
.

1.4.
Dimension of wiggly sets

A set
E
is said to be
uniformly wiggly
of dimension
d
and with parameter
\beta_{0}
if for all balls
B
centered on
E
and with
0<r(B)<\diam(E)
it holds that

(1.9)

\displaystyle\beta_{E,\infty}^{d}(B)>\beta_{0}.

Here
\beta_{E,\infty}^{d}
is the
d
-dimensional version of the so-called
L^{\infty}

\beta
-number of Peter Jones
[
Jon90
]
(see Definition
2.6
). Wiggly sets appear naturally in various contexts where some form of self-similarity is present. Examples include limit sets of certain Kleinian groups, some Julia sets of polynomials, and random sets (see
[
BP17
]
, pp. 340-341). They were first studied via the Analyst’s TST by Bishop and Jones in
[
BJ97
]
, were they proved a lower bound on the Hausdorff dimension of wiggly connected sets in the plane, assuming
d=1
. Their result was generalised to continua in metric spaces by Azzam
[
Azz15
]
. A result in this vein was later proved by David
[
Dav04
]
(and quantified in
[
Vil19
]
), this time for uniformly non flat sets of
any integer
dimension, satisfying a topological condition. David’s work was motivated by a question of L. Potyagailo, concerning higher dimensional limit sets (see the introduction of
[
Dav04
]
). The result below can be seen as a version of David’s theorem with the topological condition replaced by the PBP assumption.

Theorem 1.12
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
1\leq d\leq n-1
. Let

be a closed set with

-PBP
(
with parameter

)
and which is uniformly wiggly of dimension

. Then

(1.10)

{\rm dim}_{H}(E)\geq d+c\beta_{0}^{2(d+1)},

where

Remark 1.13
.

We defined uniformly wiggly sets in terms of
\beta_{E,\infty}^{d}
, but, in principle, we could have used the

versions of the coefficients instead. Indeed, if we take a set

that satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem

with respect to the Azzam-Schul

(where

), then we obtain a somewhat sharper dimension estimate

see Proposition

-coefficients to use when working with higher dimensional sets are the averaged ones.

Another comment on definitions is in order: many interesting sets satisfy non-flatness hypotheses weaker than uniform wiggly. For example, attractors of many dynamical systems are only

Remark 1.14
.

The dependence of

on the PBP parameters is not a proof artifact: suppose we had a lower bound for Hausdorff diemnsion depending only on
\beta_{0}
and
not
on
\delta
. Consider the four corners Cantor set

, constructed in the usual way, except that we dilate by constant

This example was pointed out by T. Orponen in a discussion on a first draft, where the exact dependence of the dimension estimate on the PBP constant had been overlooked.

In fact, after a minute’s thinking, the dependence on

1.5.
Outline of the paper and proof ideas

In Section
2
we establish notation and state some quantitative rectifiability results used in the paper. Here the reader will find Lemma
2.3
where a doubling Frostman measure is constructed, and which represents the first step of the program outlined on p.
1
. While the idea of the proof is rather simple (a redistribution policy), its execution is lengthy, and thus we have deferred it to Appendix
A
.

In Section
3
we carry out the second step of our program. In fact, a coronization of lower content regular sets with finite Hausdorff measure in terms of Ahlfors regular sets was given in
[
AV21
]
. Here we instead coronize a general lower content regular set (that is, with possibly non-
\sigma
finite Hausdorff measure) by coronizing the Frostmann measure coming from the first step. This means: given a lower content regular set
E
, we apply Lemma
2.3
to obtain a doubling Frostman measure
\mu
supported on
E
. Next, we run a stopping time algorithm to decompose the dyadic cubes of
\mu
intro trees, where the density of
\mu
is roughly constant. The lower regularity of
E
, and the fact that
\mu
is doubling, allow us to say that the cubes where the algorithm stopped (i.e. those scale and locations where
\mu
was not behaving like an Ahlfors regular measure) are few, in a precise sense: the stopped cubes satisfy a Carleson packing condition. At the level of each tree we construct an approximating Ahlfors regular measure. This completes the coronization procedure.

In Section
4
we show that the property of having plenty of big projection behaves well with our type of approximation. We are able to show that if the set
E
has PBP, then the support of each approximating measure also has PBP. By applying the aforementioned result of Orponen, we conclude that each of the approximating measures is uniformly rectifiable.

In Section
5
we prove Theorem
1.6
. Since
\Gamma_{2,1}(E)\sim\gamma(E)
by
[
Tol03
]
, Theorem
1.3
follows from Theorem
1.6
by taking
n=2,\,d=1
. To prove Theorem
1.6
, we first use the results from Sections
3
–
4
to obtain a family of uniformly rectifiable measures approximating the Frostman measure
\mu
on our set with PBP. Uniform rectifiability implies that the approximating measures satisfy a flatness condition involving
\beta
-numbers. We transfer the
\beta
-numbers estimates back to
\mu
, and then conclude the proof of Theorem
1.6
by using a lower bound on
\Gamma_{n,d}
due to Prat
[
Pra12
]
and Girela-Sarrión
[
GS19
]
, see Theorem
2.10
.

In Section
6
we prove the TST for sets with PBP, Theorem
1.10
. The general strategy is similar to that from Section
5
. The two main differences are that, firstly, the flatness condition is expressed in terms of a different type of
\beta
-numbers, and secondly, we wish to obtain estimates in terms
\mathcal{H}^{d}(E)
, and not some capacity. In particular, here we use the coronization from
[
AV21
]
, and not the coronization of a Frostman measure.

The last two sections are dedicated to Theorem
1.12
and Theorem
1.7
(note that Theorem
1.5
follows from Theorem
1.7
since
\gamma(E)\sim\Gamma_{2,1}(E)
). The strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.12
is that of Bishop and Jones
[

]
, while the proof of Theorem

follows Verdera’s
[

, Theorem 4.31]
. A key ingredient of the original proofs is Jones’ Analyst’s TST theorem
[

]
, which here is replaced by Theorem

.

In Appendix
A
we construct a doubling Frostman measure supported on a lower content regular set. We use this modified Frostman measure in the proof of Theorem
1.6
. The idea of the proof is rather simple: the doubling condition is broken if two neighbouring cubes have very unequal amount of mass. We fix this by recursively redistributing “the wealth”, that is, the mass, form the cubes which are too wealthy, to the poorer ones. We are grateful to Tuomas Orponen for helping us with the construction.

Lemma 2.1

(

]

Let

Recall that one of the quantitative notions of rectifiability introduced by David and Semmes in

Definition 2.6

3.1.
Finite measure case

The following approximation result will be used in the proof of Theorem

3.3.
Stopping time argument

We begin by conducting a stopping time argument which will be used later on to define the collection
\mathsf{Top}
.

Let
R\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}
. We will say that
Q\in\mathsf{LD}_{0}(R)
(here
\mathsf{LD}
stands for “low density”) if
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N},\ Q\subset R
, and

\theta_{\mu}(Q)\leq\tau\,\theta_{\mu}(R),

where
\tau=\rho^{2}=10^{-6}
. The refined family
\mathsf{LD}(R)
consists of the maximal cubes from
\mathsf{LD}_{0}(R)
(i.e., cubes
Q\in\mathsf{LD}_{0}(R)
which are not properly contained in any other cube from
\mathsf{LD}_{0}(R)
).
We define also

\mathsf{End}(R)=\big\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{N}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,Q\subset R,\ Q\cap\bigcup_{P\in\mathsf{LD}(R)}P=\varnothing\big\}.

Finally, we set

\mathsf{Stop}(R)=\mathsf{LD}(R)\cup\mathsf{End}(R),

and

\mathrm{Tree}(R)=\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,Q\subset R,\ \text{there exists $P\in\mathsf{Stop}(R)$ such that }P\subset Q\}.

It follows immediately from the definition that
\mathsf{Stop}(R)
is a family of pairwise disjoint cubes covering
R
. Moreover,
R\notin\mathsf{LD}(R)
by the definition of
\mathsf{LD}_{0}(R)
, and
R\in\mathsf{End}(R)
if and only if
R\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
. Observe also that
\mathsf{Stop}(R)\subset\mathrm{Tree}(R)
.

In the lemma below we show that
\mu
is
d
-Ahlfors regular at the scales and locations of
\mathrm{Tree}(R)
. This will be useful later on in the construction of an Ahlfors regular approximating measure
\nu
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
R\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}
. Then, for all
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)

\tau\theta_{\mu}(R)\lesssim\theta_{\mu}(Q)\lesssim\theta_{\mu}(R).

Proof.

The upper estimate
\theta_{\mu}(Q)\lesssim\theta_{\mu}(R)
follows immediately from property (
4
) in Lemma
2.3
, and the fact that
Q\subset R
.

To see the lower bound
\tau\theta_{\mu}(R)\lesssim\theta_{\mu}(Q)
, note that this is obvious for
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)\setminus\mathsf{LD}(R)
: for such cubes we have

\theta_{\mu}(Q)>\tau\theta_{\mu}(R),

by the stopping time condition of
\mathsf{LD}_{0}(R)
.

Assume now that
Q\in\mathsf{LD}(R)
, and denote the parent of
Q
by
Q^{1}
. Then,
Q^{1}\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)\setminus\mathsf{LD}(R)
, so that
\theta_{\mu}(Q^{1})>\tau\theta_{\mu}(R)
. But now we see by the doubling property of
\mu
(
2.1
) that
\theta_{\mu}(Q)\gtrsim\theta_{\mu}(Q^{1})
, so that

\theta_{\mu}(Q)\gtrsim\theta_{\mu}(Q^{1})\geq\tau\theta_{\mu}(R).

∎

3.4.
Coronization

We are ready to decompose
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}
into trees using the stopping time argument of the previous subsection.

We define the family
\mathsf{Top}
by induction. Set
\mathsf{Top}_{0}=\{Q_{0}\}
. Assume that
\mathsf{Top}_{k}
has already been defined for some
k\geq 0
, and let
R\in\mathsf{Top}_{k}.
If
R\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
, we set
\mathsf{Next}(R)=\varnothing.
Otherwise, we define

\mathsf{Next}(R)=\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Q^{1}\in\mathsf{LD}(R)\}.

An equivalent definition is that
\mathsf{Next}(R)
consists of cubes
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}
satisfying
Q\notin\mathsf{Tree}(R)
but
Q^{1}\in\mathsf{Tree}(R)
.

We define

\mathsf{Top}_{k+1}=\bigcup_{R\in\mathsf{Top}_{k}}\mathsf{Next}(R).

Note that for each
k
the family
\mathsf{Top}_{k}
consists of pairwise disjoint cubes. Observe also that there exists
1\leq k_{0}\leq N
such that
\mathsf{Top}_{k_{0}}\neq\varnothing
, and then
\mathsf{Top}_{k}=\varnothing
for all
k>k_{0}
. We define

\mathsf{Top}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{k_{0}}\mathsf{Top}_{k}.

Remark that

\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}=\bigcup_{R\in\mathsf{Top}}\mathrm{Tree}(R),

and the sum above is disjoint.

3.5.
Packing condition

Now we prove the packing condition (
3.6
).
First, we claim that the
\mu
-densities of cubes in
\mathsf{Top}
decay geometrically:

\theta_{\mu}(R)\leq\rho^{k}\theta_{\mu}(Q_{0})\quad\text{for $R\in\mathsf{Top}_{k},\ 0\leq k\leq k_{0}.$}

Indeed, this follows from a simple induction argument: it is true for
k=0
because
\mathsf{Top}_{0}=\{Q_{0}\}
. If
R\in\mathsf{Top}_{k+1}
, then
R^{1}\in\mathsf{LD}(P)
for some
P\in\mathsf{Top}_{k}
. Since
\theta_{\mu}(P)\leq\rho^{k}\theta_{\mu}(Q_{0})
by the inductive assumption, we get from the definition of
\mathsf{LD}(P)
that

\theta_{\mu}(R)=\frac{\mu(R)}{\ell(R)^{d}}\leq\rho^{-1}\frac{\mu(R^{1})}{\ell(R^{1})^{d}}=\rho^{-1}\theta_{\mu}(R^{1})\leq\rho^{-1}\tau\theta_{\mu}(P)=\rho\theta_{\mu}(P)\leq\rho^{k+1}\theta_{\mu}(Q_{0}),

which closes the induction.

Consequently,

\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}}\sum_{R\in\mathsf{Top}_{k}}\theta_{\mu}(R)\mu(R)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}}\sum_{R\in\mathsf{Top}_{k}}\rho^{k}\theta_{\mu}(Q_{0})\mu(R)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}}\rho^{k}\theta_{\mu}(Q_{0})\mu(Q_{0}),

where in the last estimate we used the fact that for each
k
the cubes in
\mathsf{Top}_{k}
are pairwise disjoint and contained in
Q_{0}
. Recalling that
\rho=0.001
, this concludes the proof of (
3.6
).

3.6.
Regularizing trees

Fix
R\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}
. In what follows, it will be more convenient to work with
regular
trees, in the sense that nearby stopping cubes have comparable sidelengths. In order to regularize
\mathrm{Tree}(R)
we will use a technique from
[
DS91
]
which is nowadays considered fairly standard. It will also be useful to work with certain cubes neighboring
R
. We provide the details below.

We define the set of cubes neighboring
R
as

(3.8)

\mathcal{N}(R)=\{R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \ell(R^{\prime})=\ell(R),\ R^{\prime}\cap 2B_{R}\neq\varnothing\}.

Note that
R\in\mathcal{N}(R)
. We define also an extended version of
\mathcal{D}(R)
:

\mathcal{D}_{*}(R)=\bigcup_{R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(R)}\mathcal{D}(R^{\prime}),

and

\mathcal{D}_{*}^{N}=\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{*}(Q_{0})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k},\,0\leq k\leq N\}.

Now we perform the regularization algorithm. Given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
set

d_{R}(x)=\inf_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,Q)+\ell(Q)

and for
Q\in\mathcal{D}
set

d_{R}(Q)=\max\big(\frac{1}{20}\inf_{x\in Q}d_{R}(x),5\rho^{N}\big),

where the parameter
\rho=1/1000
comes from the definition of
\mathcal{D}
. Observe that the quantity
d_{R}(Q)
is monotone in the sense that if
P\subset Q
, then
d_{R}(P)\geq d_{R}(Q)
.

We define
\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
to be the family of maximal cubes
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{*}(R)
satisfying

(3.9)

\ell(Q)\leq d_{R}(Q).

Note that the "
5\rho^{N}
" term in the definition of
d_{R}(Q)
ensures that the inequality above is satisfied by all
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
, so that
\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)\subset\mathcal{D}_{*}(R)\cap\mathcal{D}_{*}^{N}
. Observe that the cubes in
\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
are pairwise disjoint, by maximality, and also

(3.10)

\bigcup_{Q\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}Q=\bigcup_{R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}({R})}R^{\prime}\supset 2B_{R}\cap E.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
Q\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
and
x\in 5B_{Q}
, then
d_{R}(x)\sim\ell(Q)
. Consequently, if
Q,P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
, and
5B_{Q}\cap 5B_{P}\neq\varnothing
, then
\ell(Q)\sim\ell(P)
.

Proof.

Let
Q\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
and
x\in 5B_{Q}
. First we show that
d_{R}(x)\geq\ell(Q)
. By the definition of
\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
, we have
\ell(Q)\leq d_{R}(Q)
, so it suffices to show that
d_{R}(x)\geq d_{R}(Q)
, that is

d_{R}(x)\geq\max\big(\frac{1}{20}\inf_{y\in Q}d_{R}(y),5\rho^{N}\big).

Suppose the maximum above is achieved by
5\rho^{N}
, i.e.
d_{R}(Q)=5\rho^{N}
. Then, the estimate
d_{R}(x)\geq 5\rho^{N}
is clear by the definition of
d_{R}(x)
, since
\mathrm{Tree}(R)\subset\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}
.

Assume now that
d_{R}(Q)=\frac{1}{20}\inf_{y\in Q}d_{R}(y)
. Since the function
d_{R}
is
1
-Lipschitz, we have

\frac{1}{20}d_{R}(x)\geq\frac{1}{20}d_{R}(x_{Q})-\frac{1}{20}|d_{R}(x)-d_{R}(x_{Q})|\geq\frac{1}{20}d_{R}(x_{Q})-\frac{5}{20}\ell(Q)\\
\geq\frac{1}{20}\inf_{y\in Q}d_{R}(y)-\frac{1}{4}\ell(Q)=d_{R}(Q)-\frac{1}{4}\ell(Q)\geq\frac{3}{4}\ell(Q).

Hence,
d_{R}(x)\geq 15\,\ell(Q)
.

We move on to the estimate
d_{R}(x)\lesssim\ell(Q)
. Recall that, by the definition of
\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
,
Q
is a maximal cube satisfying
\ell(Q)\leq d_{R}(Q)
. In particular,
Q^{1}
satisfies

\ell(Q^{1})>d_{R}(Q^{1})=\max\big(\frac{1}{20}\inf_{y\in Q^{1}}d_{R}(y),5\rho^{N}\big)\geq\frac{1}{20}\inf_{y\in Q^{1}}d_{R}(y).

Let
y\in Q^{1}
be such that
\ell(Q^{1})\geq 1/20\,d_{R}(y)
. Since
x,y\in 2B_{Q^{1}}
, we may use the
1
-Lipschitz property of
d_{R}
to conclude that

\ell(Q^{1})\geq\frac{1}{20}d_{R}(y)\geq\frac{1}{20}d_{R}(x)-\frac{1}{20}|d_{R}(y)-d_{R}(x)|\geq\frac{1}{20}d_{R}(x)-\frac{4}{20}\ell(Q^{1}).

Thus,
\ell(Q)=\rho^{-1}\ell(Q^{1})\gtrsim d_{R}(x)
.
∎

For every
R\in\mathsf{Top}
we define the extended, regularized “tree” as

\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)=\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{*}(R)\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,\text{there exists $P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)$ such that }P\subset Q\}.

The family
\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)
might not be a “true” tree since we cannot guarantee that all
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)
are contained in
R
. Nevertheless, it is a union of a bounded number of trees, each of the form
\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)\cap\mathcal{D}(R^{\prime}),\ R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(R)
.

Remark that since
\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)\subset\mathcal{D}_{*}^{N}
, we also have
\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)\subset\mathcal{D}_{*}^{N}
. Below we prove that
\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)
is larger than the original tree
\mathrm{Tree}(R)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

We have
\mathrm{Tree}(R)\subset\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that each
Q\in\mathsf{Stop}(R)
contains some
P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
. To this end, observe that if
x\in Q
, then from the definition of
d_{R}
we have
d_{R}(x)\leq\ell(Q)
. It follows that

\frac{1}{20}\inf_{x\in Q}d_{R}(x)\leq\frac{\ell(Q)}{20}.

There are two cases to consider.

Case

Q\in\mathsf{Stop}(R)\cap\mathcal{D}_{N}
. Then
d_{R}(Q)=5\rho^{N}=\ell(Q)
, so
Q
satisfies (
3.9
), while
\ell(Q^{1})>5\rho^{N}=d_{R}(Q^{1})
, so that
Q^{1}
does not satisfy (
3.9
). Consequently,
Q\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
.

Case

Q\in\mathsf{Stop}(R)\cap\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N-1}
. Then we have

\ell(Q)>\max\bigg(\frac{\ell(Q)}{20},5\rho^{N}\bigg)\geq d_{R}(Q),

so that
Q
does not satisfy (
3.9
). Taking into account (
3.10
) we get that there exists
P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
such that
P\subset Q
.
∎

Recall that in Lemma
3.4
we proved that
\mu
is Ahlfors regular at the scales and locations of
\mathrm{Tree}(R)
. In the lemma below we show that despite enlarging
\mathrm{Tree}(R)
to its regularized version
\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)
we did not lose this property.

Lemma 3.7
.

For each
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)
we have

(3.11)

\tau\,\theta_{\mu}(R)\lesssim\theta_{\mu}(Q)\lesssim\theta_{\mu}(R).

Proof.

If
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)
, then this was already shown in Lemma
3.4
. Suppose that
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)\setminus\mathrm{Tree}(R)
. Let
P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
be such that
P\subset Q
. By Lemma
3.5
we have
d_{R}(x_{P})\sim\ell(P)
. By the definition of
d_{R}(x_{P})
there exists
P^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)
such that
d_{R}(x_{P})\sim\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x_{P},P^{\prime})+\ell(P^{\prime})
. Hence,

(3.12)

\ell(P)\sim\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x_{P},P^{\prime})+\ell(P^{\prime}).

If
\ell(P^{\prime})<\ell(Q)
let
Q^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)
be the ancestor of
P^{\prime}
with
\ell(Q^{\prime})=\ell(Q)
, otherwise set
Q^{\prime}=P^{\prime}
. We claim that

\ell(Q^{\prime})\sim\ell(Q).

This is clearly the case if
\ell(P^{\prime})<\ell(Q)
. On the other hand, if
Q^{\prime}=P^{\prime}
, then
\ell(Q^{\prime})=\ell(P^{\prime})\geq\ell(Q)
, and at the same time
\ell(P^{\prime})\lesssim\ell(P)\leq\ell(Q)
. This shows that
\ell(Q^{\prime})\sim\ell(Q)
.

Recalling (
3.12
) we get that

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(Q,Q^{\prime})\lesssim\ell(Q)\sim\ell(Q^{\prime}).

Hence,
B_{Q}\subset CB_{Q^{\prime}}\subset 2CB_{Q}
for some
C\sim 1
. The estimate (
3.11
) then follows from the fact that it is satisfied by
Q^{\prime}
(which was shown in Lemma
3.4
) and from the doubling property of
\mu
.

∎

The following auxiliary result will be useful later on.

Lemma 3.8
.

There exists
C_{0}>1
, with
C_{0}\sim 1
, such that for any
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)
and
P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
satisfying
2B_{P}\cap 2B_{Q}\neq\varnothing
we have

P\subset 2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}.

Proof.

To prove the lemma it suffices to show that
\ell(P)\lesssim\ell(Q)
. If
\ell(Q)\geq\ell(P),
we are done. Assume
\ell(Q)\leq\rho\,\ell(P)
, so that
Q\subset 3B_{P}
.

By the definition of
\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)
, there exists
Q^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
such that
Q^{\prime}\subset Q
. In particular, we have
B_{Q^{\prime}}\cap 3B_{P}\neq\varnothing
, and since
Q^{\prime},P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
, we get from Lemma
3.5
that
\ell(P)\sim\ell(Q^{\prime})\leq\ell(Q)
.
∎

3.7.
Construction of
\Gamma_{R}
and the approximating measure
\nu

In this subsection we construct a
d
-Ahlfors regular set
\Gamma_{R}
that approximates
E
at the level of
\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)
.

Recall that
\Delta
denotes the family of usual half-open dyadic cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For each
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)
set

\Delta_{Q}=\{I\in\Delta\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \ell(Q)/2\leq\ell(I)<\ell(Q),\,I\cap Q\neq\varnothing\}.

In particular,

(3.13)

Q\subset\bigcup_{I\in\Delta_{Q}}I\subset 2B_{Q}.

We define also

Q_{\Gamma}=\bigcup_{I\in\Delta_{Q}}{\partial}_{d}I,

where
{\partial}_{d}I
denotes the
d
-dimensional skeleton of
I\in\Delta_{Q}
. Observe that

(3.14)

Q_{\Gamma}\subset 2B_{Q},

and

(3.15)

\mathcal{H}^{d}(Q_{\Gamma})\sim\ell(Q)^{d}

because
\mathcal{H}^{d}({\partial}_{d}I)\sim\ell(Q)^{d}
for each
I\in\Delta_{Q}
, and
\#\Delta_{Q}\lesssim 1
.

Finally, we set

{\mathscr{C}}_{R}=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}\Delta_{Q}

and

\Gamma_{R}=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}Q_{\Gamma}=\bigcup_{I\in{\mathscr{C}}_{R}}{\partial}_{d}I.

Note that

(3.16)

\Gamma_{R}\subset\bigcup_{R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(R)}2B_{R^{\prime}}\subset 6B_{R},

by (
3.14
) and the definition of
\mathcal{N}(R)
(
3.8
). At the same time, by (
3.10
) we have

2B_{R}\cap E\subset\bigcup_{I\in{\mathscr{C}}_{R}}I.

This gives property (b) from Proposition

.

Property (c) is also easy to establish: if

where in the last estimate we used Lemma

Property (d) follows immediately from Lemma

.

Now we turn to property (a): Ahlfors regularity of

. To simplify notation, below we drop the subscript and we write

.

We begin by showing that although the family

is not necessarily disjoint, it is not too far off: it has bounded intersection.

Lemma 3.9
.

We have

(3.17)

\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}\mathds{1}_{Q_{\Gamma}}\sim\mathds{1}_{\Gamma}.

Proof.

Observe that if
Q,P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
are such that
2B_{Q}\cap 2B_{P}=\varnothing
, then
Q_{\Gamma}\cap P_{\Gamma}=\varnothing
, by (

It may happen that

2B_{Q}\cap 2B_{P}\neq\varnothing

there is only a bounded number of

∎

The set
\Gamma
approximates
E
at the scales and locations of
\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)
. Now we also define an Ahlfors regular measure
\nu
approximating
\mu
. To that end, we define a density
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}

\nu=g\,\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{\Gamma}

For each

g_{Q}=\frac{\mu(Q)}{\mathcal{H}^{d}(Q_{\Gamma})}\mathds{1}_{Q_{\Gamma}},

so that

(3.18)

\int_{\Gamma}g_{Q}\ d\mathcal{H}^{d}=\mu(Q).

Note also that

(3.19)

g_{Q}\sim\theta_{\mu}(Q)\mathds{1}_{Q_{\Gamma}}\sim\theta_{\mu}(R)\mathds{1}_{Q_{\Gamma}},

by (
3.15
) and Lemma
3.7
.

Set

g=\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}g_{Q}

and

(3.20)

\nu=g\,\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{\Gamma}.

Remark that for any
x\in\Gamma

g(x)=\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}g_{Q}(x)\overset{\eqref{eq:gQsimtheta}}{\sim}\theta_{\mu}(R)\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}\mathds{1}_{Q_{\Gamma}}(x)\sim\theta_{\mu}(R),

where in the last estimate we used (
3.17
). It follows that

(3.21)

\nu=\theta_{\mu}(R)\cdot\,h\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{\Gamma},

where
h(x)=g(x)/\theta_{\mu}(R)\sim\mathds{1}_{\Gamma}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

The measure
\nu
constructed above is
d
-Ahlfors regular. More precisely, for
x\in\Gamma
and
0<r<\diam(\Gamma)
we have

(3.22)

\nu(B(x,r))\sim\theta_{\mu}(R)\,r^{d}.

In consequence,
\Gamma
is a
d
-Ahlfors regular set, with constants depending only on
n,d,
and
c_{1}
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\Gamma
, and let
Q\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
be a cube such that
x\in Q_{\Gamma}
; if
Q
is non-unique, just choose one.

First we check that
\nu
is upper Ahlfors regular. Assume that
0<r\leq\ell(Q)
. Then,

\nu(B(x,r))=\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}\int_{B(x,r)}g_{P}\ d\mathcal{H}^{d}\overset{\eqref{eq:gQsimtheta}}{\lesssim}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P_{\Gamma}\cap B(x,r)\neq\varnothing}\int_{B(x,r)}\theta_{\mu}(R)\mathds{1}_{P_{\Gamma}}\ d\mathcal{H}^{d}\\
=\theta_{\mu}(R)\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P_{\Gamma}\cap B(x,r)\neq\varnothing}\mathcal{H}^{d}({P_{\Gamma}\cap B(x,r)})\lesssim\theta_{\mu}(R)\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P_{\Gamma}\cap B(x,r)\neq\varnothing}r^{d},

where in the last estimate we used the fact that each
P_{\Gamma}
is upper Ahlfors regular. To estimate the last term above, note that
B(x,r)\subset 2B_{Q}
and so
B(x,r)
may intersect only a bounded number
P_{\Gamma},\ P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),
by (
3.14

\nu(B(x,r))\lesssim\theta_{\mu}(R)\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P_{\Gamma}\cap B(x,r)\neq\varnothing}r^{d}\sim\theta_{\mu}(R)\,r^{d}.

Now suppose that
\ell(Q)<r<\diam(\Gamma)
.

\nu(B(x,r))=\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}\int_{B(x,r)}g_{P}\ d\mathcal{H}^{d}\leq\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P_{\Gamma}\cap B(x,r)\neq\varnothing}\int g_{P}\ d\mathcal{H}^{d}\\
\overset{\eqref{eq:gQmeas}}{=}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P_{\Gamma}\cap B(x,r)\neq\varnothing}\mu(P).

Let
Q^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)
be the minimal cube satisfying
Q\subset Q^{\prime}
and
\ell(Q^{\prime})>r
. Note that
\ell(Q^{\prime})\sim r
, and
B(x,r)\subset 2B_{Q^{\prime}}
. If

satisfies
P_{\Gamma}\cap B(x,r)\neq\varnothing
, then in particular we have
2B_{P}\cap 2B_{Q^{\prime}}\neq\varnothing
. Thus, by Lemma
3.8
, we have

\nu(B(x,r))\leq\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P_{\Gamma}\cap B(x,r)\neq\varnothing}\mu(P)\leq\mu(C_{0}B_{Q^{\prime}})\sim\mu(Q^{\prime}),

where in the last estimate we used the doubling property of
\mu

\mu(Q^{\prime})\sim\theta_{\mu}(R)\ell(Q^{\prime})^{d}

3.7

\ell(Q^{\prime})\sim r

\nu(B(x,r))\lesssim\theta_{\mu}(R)\,r^{d}.

We turn to the lower regularity of

is very similar to the one before. We have

\nu(B(x,r))=\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}\int_{B(x,r)}g_{P}\ d\mathcal{H}^{d}\overset{\eqref{eq:gQsimtheta}}{\gtrsim}\int_{B(x,r)}\theta_{\mu}(R)\mathds{1}_{Q_{\Gamma}}\ d\mathcal{H}^{d}\\
=\theta_{\mu}(R)\ \mathcal{H}^{d}({Q_{\Gamma}\cap B(x,r)})\sim\theta_{\mu}(R)\,r^{d},

where in the last line we used Ahlfors regularity of
Q_{\Gamma}
.

Concerning scales

\nu(B(x,r))=\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}\int_{B(x,r)}g_{P}\ d\mathcal{H}^{d}\geq\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P_{\Gamma}\subset B(x,r)}\int g_{P}\ d\mathcal{H}^{d}\\
\overset{\eqref{eq:gQmeas}}{=}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P_{\Gamma}\subset B(x,r)}\mu(P).

Let
Q^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Tree}_{*}(R)

We claim that if

(so in particular,
P
appears in the sum on the right hand side above). Indeed, we have
P_{\Gamma}\subset 2B_{P}\subset 2B_{Q^{\prime}}\subset B(x,r)
, by the definition of

. Thus,

\nu(B(x,r))\geq\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P_{\Gamma}\subset B(x,r)}\mu(P)\\
\geq\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P\subset Q^{\prime}}\mu(P)=\mu(Q^{\prime})\sim\theta_{\mu}(R)\,\ell(Q^{\prime})^{d}\sim\theta_{\mu}(R)\,r^{d},

where we have used Lemma
3.7
and the fact that
\ell(Q^{\prime})\sim r
. This finishes the proof of Ahlfors egularity of
\nu
.

As a consequence of (
3.21
) and (
3.22
) we get that
\Gamma
is a
d
-Ahlfors regular set, with Ahlfors regularity constants depending only on
n,d,
and the the lower content regularity constant
c_{1}
. ∎

We have established property (a) from Proposition
3.3
.

3.8.
\beta
-numbers estimate

To complete the proof of Proposition
3.3
it remains to prove the estimate (
3.7
), namely

\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Tree}(R)}\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)\lesssim\int_{\Gamma}\int_{0}^{\ell(R)}\beta_{\nu,2}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{dr}{r}d\nu(x)+\theta_{\mu}(R)\mu(R).

We begin by showing the following auxiliary estimate.

Lemma 3.11
.

For any
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)
we have

(3.23)

\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\lesssim\beta_{\nu,2}(C_{0}B_{Q})^{2}+\ell(Q)^{-d}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}}\frac{\ell(P)}{\ell(Q)}\mu(P),

where
C_{0}\sim 1
is the constant from Lemma
3.8
.

Proof.

Fix
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)
, and let
L_{Q}
be a
d
-plane minimizing
\beta_{\nu,2}(C_{0}B_{Q})
.

Observe that for any
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)
, the set
2B_{Q}\cap E
is fully covered by
P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
satisfying
P\cap 2B_{Q}\neq\varnothing
. Indeed, this is true for
R
because by the definition of
\mathcal{N}(R)
and
\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
we have

2B_{R}\cap E\subset\bigcup_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P\cap 2B_{R}\neq\varnothing}P.

Since
2B_{Q}\subset 2B_{R}
for any
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)
, the claim follows.

The observation above gives

\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}\lesssim\int_{2B_{Q}}\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}\,d\mu(x)\\
\leq\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,P\cap 2B_{Q}\neq\varnothing}\int_{P}\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}\,d\mu(x)\\
\leq\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}}\int_{P}\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}\,d\mu(x),

where in the last line we used Lemma
3.8
.

Let
P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}
. Then,

\int_{P}\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}\,d\mu(x)=\int_{\Gamma}\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}g_{P}(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}(x)\\
+\int\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}\,(\mathds{1}_{P}(x)d\mu(x)-g_{P}(x)d\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{\Gamma}(x))\eqqcolon I_{1}(P)+I_{2}(P).

It follows that

\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}\lesssim\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}}I_{1}(P)+\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}}I_{2}(P)\eqqcolon S_{1}+S_{2}.

Estimating
S_{1}
is straightforward. Recall that for
P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
we have
\supp g_{P}\subset P_{\Gamma}\subset 2B_{P}
. Hence,

S_{1}=\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}}\int_{\Gamma}\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}g_{P}(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}(x)\\
\leq\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}\int_{\Gamma\cap C_{0}B_{Q}}\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}g_{P}(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}(x)\\
=\int_{\Gamma\cap C_{0}B_{Q}}\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}g(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}(x)\\
=\int_{C_{0}B_{Q}}\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}\,d\nu(x)\sim\beta_{\nu,2}(C_{0}B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d},

where in the last part we used our choice of
L_{Q}
. This gives us the first term from the right hand side of (
3.23
).

We turn to estimating
S_{2}
. Let
P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
. Using the fact that
\int_{\Gamma}g_{P}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}=\mu(P)
we get

I_{2}(P)=\int\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}\,(\mathds{1}_{P}(x)d\mu(x)-g_{P}(x)d\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{\Gamma}(x))\\
=\int\bigg(\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}-\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x_{P},L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)\,(\mathds{1}_{P}(x)d\mu(x)-g_{P}(x)d\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{\Gamma}(x)).

For
x\in P\cup\supp g_{P}\subset 2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}
we have

\bigg|\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}-\bigg(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x_{P},L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\bigg)^{2}\bigg|\leq\frac{|x-x_{P}|}{\ell(Q)}\cdot\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,L_{Q})+\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x_{P},L_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\\
\lesssim\frac{\ell(P)}{\ell(Q)}\cdot\frac{\ell(Q)}{\ell(Q)}=\frac{\ell(P)}{\ell(Q)}.

Thus,

I_{2}(P)\lesssim\frac{\ell(P)}{\ell(Q)}\mu(P).

Summing over
P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
with
2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}
yields

S_{2}\lesssim\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}}\frac{\ell(P)}{\ell(Q)}\mu(P).

This corresponds to the second term from the right hand side of (
3.23
).
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

We have

(3.24)

\sum_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)}\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)}\beta_{\nu,2}(C_{0}B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)+\theta_{\mu}(R)\,\mu(R).

Proof.

In the previous lemma we showed that for any
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)
we have (
3.23
). Multliplying by
\mu(Q)
and summing over
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)
yields

\sum_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)}\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)}\beta_{\nu,2}(C_{0}B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)\\
+\sum_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)}\theta_{\mu}(Q)\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}}\frac{\ell(P)}{\ell(Q)}\mu(P).

Thus, in order to obtain (
3.24
) it suffices to prove that

\sum_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)}\theta_{\mu}(Q)\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}}\frac{\ell(P)}{\ell(Q)}\mu(P)\lesssim\theta_{\mu}(R)\mu(R).

First, recall that by Lemma
3.4
we have
\theta_{\mu}(Q)\sim\theta_{\mu}(R)
for all
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)
. So we only need to show

(3.25)

\sum_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R),\,2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}}\frac{\ell(P)}{\ell(Q)}\mu(P)\lesssim\mu(R).

Changing the order of summation transforms the left hand side to

\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}\mu(P)\sum_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R),\,2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}}\frac{\ell(P)}{\ell(Q)}.

The inner sum is essentially a geometric series: if
P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
is fixed, then for any generation
k\in[0,N]
, there is at most a bounded number of
Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)\cap\mathcal{D}_{k}
such that
2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}
(the bound depends on
C_{0}\sim 1
). Moreover, all such
Q
necessarily satisfy
C_{0}\ell(Q)\geq\ell(P)
. Hence,

\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}\mu(P)\sum_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R),\,2B_{P}\subset C_{0}B_{Q}}\frac{\ell(P)}{\ell(Q)}\lesssim_{C_{0}}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)}\mu(P)\leq\mu(6B_{R})\sim\mu(R),

where in the last two estimates we used the fact that the cubes in
\mathsf{Reg}_{*}(R)
are pairwise disjoint and contained in
6B_{R}
, and also the doubling property of
\mu
. This finishes the proof of (
3.24
).
∎

To complete the proof of (
3.7
) it remains to show the following standard estimate.

Lemma 3.13
.

We have

\sum_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)}\beta_{\nu,2}(C_{0}B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)\lesssim\int_{\Gamma_{R}}\int_{0}^{\ell(R)}\beta_{\nu,2}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{dr}{r}d\nu(x)+\theta_{\mu}(R)\mu(R).

We only sketch the proof. First, observe that for every

,

, and

and

By the definition of
\beta
-numbers, this gives

\beta_{\nu,2}(C_{0}B_{Q})^{2}\lesssim\beta_{\nu,2}(x,r)^{2}.

Integrating over
x\in\Gamma_{R}\cap 5B_{Q}
and

implies

\beta_{\nu,2}(C_{0}B_{Q})^{2}\nu(\Gamma_{R}\cap 5B_{Q})\lesssim\int_{\Gamma_{R}\cap 5B_{Q}}\int_{10C_{0}\ell(Q)}^{20C_{0}\ell(Q)}\beta_{\nu,2}(x,r)^{2}\frac{dr}{r}\,d\nu(x).

Finally, it follows from the definition of

∎

Together with Lemma

), and of Proposition

.

Recalling

Let

be a rectangular

-matrix obtained by taking the first
(n-d)
columns of
L
. Then,

I\cap W=\{x=(x^{\prime},\textbf{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-d}\times\{0\}^{d}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,a\leq L^{\prime}x^{\prime}\leq b\},

and so
I\cap W
might be identified with the (non-empty) polytope

P=\{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-d}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,a\leq L^{\prime}x^{\prime}\leq b\}.

Let
v^{\prime}\in P
be a vertex of
P
, i.e., a
0
-dimensional face of
P
. We claim that the point
v=(v^{\prime},\textbf{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-d}\times\{0\}^{d}
belongs to some
d
-dimensional face of
I
. Since it clearly lies on
W
, this will give (
4.2
).

Since
v^{\prime}
is a vertex of
P
, we get that there exist sets of indices
A,B\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
with
A\cap B=\varnothing
and
\#(A\cup B)=n-d
such that

a_{i}=L^{\prime}_{i}\cdot v^{\prime}\text{ for $i\in A$,}\quad L^{\prime}_{i}\cdot v^{\prime}=b_{i}\text{ for $i\in B$},

where
L_{i}^{\prime}
denotes the
i
-th row of
L^{\prime}
. Note that the property
\#(A\cup B)=n-d
follows from the fact that
v^{\prime}
should be a unique solution to the equations above (a
0
-dimensional face is a single point), and we are in
\mathbb{R}^{n-d}
. We do not claim that the choice of
A
and
B
is unique, but we do not care. Finally, observe that the remaining inequalities
a_{i}\leq L^{\prime}_{i}\cdot v^{\prime}\leq b_{i},\ i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus(A\cup B)
must also be satisfied since
v\in P
.

It remains to observe that
v=(v^{\prime},\textbf{0})\in F(A,B)\cap W
because
Lv=L^{\prime}v^{\prime}
. In particular,
v\in{\partial}_{d}I\cap W
, which gives (
4.2
).
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be compact and lower content
(d,c_{1})
-regular. Let
N
be an integer and let
\mathsf{Top}
be a family of cubes as in Proposition
3.3
or Proposition
3.2
. For every
R\in\mathsf{Top}
,
Q\in\mathsf{Tree}(R)
and

we have

\pi_{V}(Q)\subset\pi_{V}(\Gamma_{R}\cap B(x_{Q},C\ell(Q)),

where
C>1
is absolute.

Proof.

By property (b) from Proposition
3.2
or
3.3
, we have
Q\subset\bigcup_{I\in{\mathscr{C}}_{R}}I
. Denote by
{\mathscr{C}}(Q)
the subfamily of
{\mathscr{C}}_{R}
consisting of cubes intersecting

.

Fix
I\in{\mathscr{C}}(Q)
and let

. Note that by the property (d) of our approximation we have

\ell(I)\sim\inf_{x\in I}d_{R}(x)\leq d_{R}(y)=\inf_{P\in\mathsf{Tree}(R)}(\ell(P)+\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,P)\leq\ell(Q),

where we used that

. In particular,

for some absolute

, and so

\bigcup_{I\in{\mathscr{C}}(Q)}{\partial}_{d}I\subset\Gamma_{R}\cap B(x_{Q},C\ell(Q)).

Together with Lemma

we get

\pi_{V}(Q)\subset\bigcup_{I\in{\mathscr{C}}(Q)}\pi_{V}(I)\subset\bigcup_{I\in{\mathscr{C}}(Q)}\pi_{V}({\partial}_{d}I)\subset\pi_{V}(\Gamma_{R}\cap B(x_{Q},C\ell(Q))).

∎

We are ready to prove Proposition
4.1
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Fix
x\in\Gamma_{R}
, and let
I\in{\mathscr{C}}_{R}
be such that
x\in{\partial}_{d}I
(if there are many such

there exists

\mathcal{H}^{d}(\pi_{V}(\Gamma\cap B(x,r)))\gtrsim r^{d}.

Let

for some constant

chosen below. Since

, it is immediate to see that for
any

So the PBP property holds trivially at small scales.

Assume now that

. By property (d) of the coronization, we have

\ell(I)\sim\inf_{y\in I}\inf_{P\in\mathsf{Tree}(R)}\ell(P)+\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,P).

Since

, this estimate lets us choose a cube

such that

and

. Let

and

, where

is the constant from Lemma

. Such cube exists assuming
C^{\prime}>1
is large enough depending on

and the implicit constants above.

Clearly,
\ell(P)\sim r
. Recall that there exists a ball
B(P)
centered at

with

and such that

(see Lemma

). Set

where

is the
d
-plane coming from the PBP property of

in the ball

. Fix

.

By Lemma

we have

\pi_{V}(E\cap B(P))\subset\pi_{V}(P)\subset\pi_{V}(\Gamma_{R}\cap x_{P},C\ell(P))\subset\pi_{V}(\Gamma_{R}\cap B(x,r)).

Hence, by the PBP property of

5.
Vitushkin’s conjecture

In this section we use Propositions
3.3
and
4.1
to prove Theorem
1.6
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\diam(E)=1
(this follows from scaling
\Gamma_{n,d}(\lambda E)=\lambda^{d}\,\Gamma_{n,d}(E)
for
\lambda>0
). By Theorem
2.10
, in order to show that
\Gamma_{n,d}(E)\gtrsim 1
, it suffices to construct a measure with polynomial growth supported on
E
such that
\mu(E)\sim 1
and

(5.1)

\iint_{0}^{\infty}\beta_{\mu,2}(x,r)^{2}\,\theta_{\mu}(x,r)\ \frac{dr}{r}d\mu(x)\lesssim\mu(E).

Let
\mu
be the measure from Lemma
2.3
. Then it has polynomial growth,
\supp\mu\subset E
, and
\mu(E)\sim 1
. We are going to show that the estimate (
5.1
) is satisfied by
\mu
. In fact, we will prove a somewhat stronger estimate.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact set with
d
-PBP. If
\mu
is the measure from Lemma
2.3
, then

(5.2)

\iint_{0}^{\infty}\beta_{\mu,2}(x,r)^{2}\ \frac{dr}{r}d\mu(x)\lesssim\mu(E),

where the implicit constant depends only on
n,d
and the PBP constants.

To see that (
5.2
) implies (
5.1
), recall that
\theta_{\mu}(x,r)\lesssim 1
by the polynomial growth condition.

Observe that since we assume that
\diam(E)=1
, we have that
\mathcal{D}_{0}
consists of a single cube, namely
\mathcal{D}_{0}=\{E\}
. A standard computation shows that (
5.2
) is equivalent to

(5.3)

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}}\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\,\mu(Q)\lesssim\mu(E).

For the reader’s convenience, we sketch the proof of
\eqref{eq:main prop sum}\Rightarrow\eqref{eq:main prop}
. Observe that if
B_{1}\subset B_{2}
are balls, and
r(B_{1})\sim r(B_{2})
, then it follows from the definition of
\beta_{\mu,2}
-numbers that

\beta_{\mu,2}(B_{1})\lesssim\beta_{\mu,2}(B_{2}).

Given
x\in E
and
0<r<\infty
let
Q\in\mathcal{D}
be the unique cube with
x\in Q
and
\rho\ell(Q)<r\leq\ell(Q)
, so that
B(x,r)\subset 2B_{Q}
and
r(2B_{Q})\sim r
. Then,

\beta_{\mu,2}(x,r)\lesssim\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q}),

and it follows easily that

\iint_{0}^{\infty}\beta_{\mu,2}(x,r)^{2}\ \frac{dr}{r}d\mu(x)\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}}\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\,\mu(Q).

Thus, our goal is to prove (
5.3
). Recalling that
\mathcal{D}_{k}=\{E\}
for
k\leq 0
and
\mu(E)\sim 1
, it follows immediately that

\sum_{k\leq 0}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\,\mu(Q)\lesssim\sum_{k\leq 0}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\ell(Q)^{-d}\,\mu(E)^{3}\sim\sum_{k\leq 0}\rho^{-kd}\,\mu(E)^{3}\lesssim\mu(E).

So when proving (
5.3
), we may concentrate on
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
for
k\geq 0
. Fix some large integer
N>1
. We will show that

(5.4)

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}}\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\,\mu(Q)\lesssim\mu(E),

with the estimate independent of
N
. Letting
N\to\infty
gives the desired bound (
5.3
).

We apply Proposition
3.3
to
E
(recall that PBP implies lower content regularity) to obtain the decomposition

\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}=\bigcup_{R\in\mathsf{Top}}\mathsf{Tree}(R).

To get (
5.4
) we need the following estimate.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
R\in\mathsf{Top}
we have

(5.5)

\sum_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)}\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\,\mu(Q)\lesssim\theta_{\mu}(R)\mu(R),

with the implicit constant depending only on
n,d
and the PBP constants of
E
.

Proof.

Fix
R\in\mathsf{Top}
, and set
\Gamma\coloneqq\Gamma_{R}.

In Lemma
3.10
and Proposition
4.1
we showed that
\Gamma
is an Ahflors regular set satisfying
d
-PBP, with Ahlfors regularity and PBP constants depending only on
n,d,
and the PBP constants of
E
. Thus, we get from Corollary
2.9
that
\Gamma
satisfies

\int_{\Gamma}\int_{0}^{\ell(R)}\beta_{\Gamma,2}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{dr}{r}d\mathcal{H}^{d}(x)\lesssim\ell(R)^{d}.

In the above we also use that
\Gamma\subset 6B_{R}
, by (
3.16
).

Recall that
\nu
was an Ahlfors regular measure supported on
\Gamma
which was approximating
\mu
, see (
3.20
).
Recalling (
3.21
), the estimate above implies

\int_{\Gamma}\int_{0}^{\ell(R)}\beta_{\nu,2}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{dr}{r}d\nu(x)\lesssim\theta_{\mu}(R)\,\ell(R)^{d}.

Together with (

), this gives the desired estimate (

).
∎

Proof of (
5.4
).

It immediately follows from (

) that

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}}\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\,\mu(Q)=\sum_{R\in\mathsf{Top}}\sum_{Q\in\mathrm{Tree}(R)}\beta_{\mu,2}(2B_{Q})^{2}\,\mu(Q)\lesssim\sum_{R\in\mathsf{Top}}\theta_{\mu}(R)\mu(R).

Using the packing estimate for

This finishes the proof of (

).
∎

6.
Traveling Saleseman Theorem

In this section we prove the Analyst’s Traveling Salesman Theorem for sets with PBP, Theorem
1.10
. To do this, we follow the same method layed out in the previous sections.

•

First, we apply Proposition
3.2
to the set
E
, recalling that the
d
-PBP property implies lower content
(d,c_{1})
regularity with constant

depending on the PBP constants (Lemma

). Hence we obtain a coronization of

-regular sets

(see Proposition

•

Second, we apply Proposition

•

The third step is to notice that, by the result of Orponen, each
\Gamma_{R}
is uniformly

•

The final step are the
\beta

Remark 6.1
.

We will keep careful track of the dependence of the various constants on

Our main estimate is the following.

Proposition 6.2
.

If

has

-PBP with parameters

, then for any

, we have

\displaystyle\ell(Q_{0})^{d}+\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}(Q_{0})}\beta_{E}^{d,2}(3B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}\lesssim_{n,d}C(\delta)\,\mathcal{H}^{d}(Q_{0}),

.

follows immediately from this proposition.

Proof of Theorem

.

First, by
[

, we have that

∎

We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 6.3

(
[

]
, Lemma 2.21)
.

Let

S\in{\mathscr{F}}(I)
, then
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(I,Q)\lesssim\ell(I)+\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(S,Q)\lesssim\ell(S)+\ell(Q)
. Hence

\ell(S)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e:form1002}}}{{\sim}}\ell(I)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e:whitney-like}}}{{\sim}}\tau\inf_{x\in I}d_{R}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e:d_F}}}{{\lesssim}}\tau(\ell(Q)+\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(I,Q))\lesssim\tau\ell(Q)+\tau\ell(S).

Choosing
\tau>0
sufficiently small we get that
\ell(S)\leq\ell(Q)
.

The observation above allows us to estimate the interior sum in (
6.11
) as a geometric sum:

I_{2}\lesssim\sum_{S\in\widetilde{\mathsf{Stop}}(R)}\ell(S)^{d+2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathsf{Tree}(R)\\
6B_{Q}\cap S\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\ell(Q)^{2}}\lesssim\sum_{S\in\widetilde{\mathsf{Stop}}(R)}\ell(S)^{d}.

Now, given
S\in\widetilde{\mathsf{Stop}}(R)
, we claim that

(6.12)

\#\{I\in{\mathscr{C}}_{R}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ S\in{\mathscr{F}}(I)\}\sim 1.

This follows immediately from the fact that for dyadic cubes
I,J
as above we have
\ell(I)=\ell(J)
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(I,J)\lesssim\ell(I)
. Hence, for each
S\in\widetilde{\mathsf{Stop}}(R)
there is a bounded number of cubes in
{\mathscr{C}}_{R}
of sidelength
\sim\ell(S)
which intersect it.
Finally, we may conclude that

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{S\in\widetilde{\mathsf{Stop}}(R)}\ell(S)^{d}\sim\sum_{S\in\widetilde{\mathsf{Stop}}(R)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in{\mathscr{C}}_{R}\\
S\in{\mathscr{F}}(I)\end{subarray}}\ell(I)^{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\in{\mathscr{C}}_{R}}\ell(I)^{d}\#\{S\in\widetilde{\mathsf{Stop}}(R)\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ S\in{\mathscr{F}}(I)\}\lesssim\sum_{I\in{\mathscr{C}}_{R}}\ell(I)^{d},

and hence, using the Ahlfors
d
-regularity of
\Gamma_{R}
,

(6.13)

I_{2}\lesssim\sum_{I\in{\mathscr{C}}_{R}}\ell(I)^{d}\sim\sum_{I\in{\mathscr{C}}_{R}}\mathcal{H}^{d}(\partial_{d}I)\sim\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma_{R})\lesssim_{\tau,\delta}\ell(R)^{d}.

Putting the estimates for
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
together with (
6.5
) gives

(6.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Tree}(R)}\beta_{E}^{d,2}(3B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}\lesssim_{\tau}C(\delta)\ell(R)^{d},

where
C(\delta)\to\infty
as
\delta\to 0
.
We conclude that

(6.15)

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{N}}\beta_{E}^{d,2}(3B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm Lemma}\,\ref{l:corona}}}{{\lesssim}}\sum_{R\in\mathsf{Top}(N)}\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Tree}(R)}\beta_{E}^{d,2}(3B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e:TreeEst}}}{{\lesssim_{\tau}}}C(\delta)\sum_{R\in\mathsf{Top}(N)}\ell(R)^{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e:ADR-packing}}}{{\lesssim}}C(\delta)\eta^{-1}\mathcal{H}^{d}(Q_{0})\sim C(\delta)\mathcal{H}^{d}(Q_{0}).

This finishes the proof of Proposition
6.2
.

7.
Dimension of wiggly sets

In this section we establish our estimate for the dimension of wiggly sets with PBP, Theorem
1.12
.

Through this section we will work with David-Christ cubes (Lemma
2.1
) associated to different sets. To avoid confusion we will write
\mathcal{D}^{E}
to denote the system of cubes associated to a given set

.

Recall that
p(d)=\tfrac{2d}{d-2}
for

and
p(d)=\infty
if

. Theorem
1.12
will follow as an easy corollary of the following proposition.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed set with
d
-PBP with constant
\delta>0
and which is uniformly wiggly of dimension
d
, constant
\beta_{0}
, and with respect to the Azzam-Schul

-numbers, where

\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r)\geq\beta_{0}\mbox{ for all }x\in E,\ 0<r<\diam(E).

(7.1)

\mathrm{dim}_{H}(E)\geq d+c\beta_{0}^{2}

with

depending on
\delta,p,n,d

Proof of Theorem
1.12
using Proposition
7.1
.

Let
E
be as in the statement of the theorem. In particular,
\beta_{E,\infty}^{d}(B_{Q})>\beta_{0}
. By
[
AS18
, Lemma 2.12, Lemma 2.13]
, we have that

\beta_{E,\infty}^{d}(B_{Q})\lesssim_{d}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(B_{Q})^{\frac{1}{d+1}}.

Hence
E
is uniformly wiggly of dimension
d
with respect to
\beta_{E}^{d,p}
with constant
\beta_{0}^{\prime}\sim\beta_{0}^{d+1}
, and (
1.10
) follows immediately from (
7.1
) with
\beta_{0}
replaced by
\beta_{0}^{\prime}
.
∎

We begin the proof of Proposition
7.1
. Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed set with
d
-PBP, with constant
\delta>0
. Without loss of generality we assume
E
is compact,
\diam(E)=1/2
, so that
\mathcal{D}_{0}=\{E\}
, and
E\subset[0,1)^{n}
.

Recall that
\Delta
is the standard dyadic lattice on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\Delta_{k}=\{I\in\Delta\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \ell(I)=2^{-k}\}
. Given a dyadic cube
I\in\Delta
and
A\geq 1
, we will denote by
AI
the cube with the came center as
I
and sidelength
A\ell(I)
.

Recall that
\rho=1/1000
and that for
Q\in\mathcal{D}^{E}_{m}
we have
\ell(Q)=5\rho^{m}
, see Lemma
2.1
. Given
k\geq 0
let

be the unique integer such that

(7.2)

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}(E)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\{I\in\Delta_{j(k)}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,I\cap E\neq\varnothing\right\}.

We defined

in such a way that if

Set

(7.3)

E_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bigcup_{I\in\Delta_{k}(E)}\partial_{d}I,

where

is the

-dimensional dyadic skeleton of

.

Lemma 7.2
.

For every
k\geq 0
the set
E_{k}
has
d
-PBP (with constant
\delta^{\prime}\sim\delta
).

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Proposition
4.1
, although here the situation is much simpler. Let
x\in E_{k}
and
0<r<\diam(E)
. Let
I\in\Delta_{k}(E)
be such that
x\in{\partial}_{d}I
. If
0<r\lesssim 2^{-j(k)}\sim\diam(I)
, then for all
V\in{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)

\mathcal{H}^{d}(\pi_{V}(E_{k}\cap B(x,r)))\geq\mathcal{H}^{d}(\pi_{V}({\partial}_{d}I\cap B(x,r)))\gtrsim r^{d}.

On the other hand, if

then there exists

with

, and such that all

with

. Denote the family of such

. Then, by Lemma

\mathcal{H}^{d}(\pi_{V}(E_{k}\cap B(x,r)))\geq\mathcal{H}^{d}\bigg(\pi_{V}\bigg(\bigcup_{J\in\Delta_{k}(x,r)}{\partial}_{d}J\bigg)\bigg)\\
\geq\mathcal{H}^{d}\bigg(\pi_{V}\bigg(\bigcup_{J\in\Delta_{k}(x,r)}J\bigg)\bigg)\geq\mathcal{H}^{d}(\pi_{V}(E\cap B(y,C/r))).

So for

such that
\mathcal{H}^{d}(\pi_{V}(E\cap B(y,C/r)))\gtrsim r^{d}
we also have
\mathcal{H}^{d}(\pi_{V}(E_{k}\cap B(x,r)))\gtrsim r^{d}
. This gives the PBP property for

.
∎

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
k\geq j\geq 1
be integers, and let

. We have

(7.4)

\sum_{m=j}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{D}^{E}_{m}\\
Q\cap 3I\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}\leq C\mathcal{H}^{d}(E_{k}\cap AI),

where

depends on
\delta,n,d
and

.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that

(see
[
AV21

with
j\leq m\leq k
and

. Let

\displaystyle\beta_{E}^{d,2}(B_{Q})\lesssim\beta_{E}^{d,2}(2B_{Q})\lesssim\beta^{d,2}_{E_{k}}(4B_{P})+\left(\frac{1}{\ell(Q)^{d}}\int_{4B_{Q}\cap E}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,E_{k})}{\ell(Q)}\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{d}(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Note that we have

Multiplying by

\sum_{m=j}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{D}^{E}_{m}\\
Q\cap 3I\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\beta_{E}^{d,2}(B_{Q})^{2}\ell(P)^{d}\lesssim\sum_{m=j}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{D}_{m}^{E_{k}}\\
B_{P}\cap 12I\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\beta^{d,2}_{E_{k}}(4B_{P})^{2}\ell(P)^{d}\\
+\sum_{m=j}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{D}^{E}_{m}\\
Q\cap 3I\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\ell(Q)^{d}}\int_{4B_{Q}\cap E}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,E_{k})}{\ell(Q)}\right)^{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)\right)\ell(P)^{d}\eqqcolon S_{1}+S_{2}.

We begin by estimating
S_{1}
. Let

\mathcal{R}=\{R\in\mathcal{D}^{E_{k}}_{j}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ B_{R}\cap 11I\neq\varnothing\}.

Note that for each cube
P
appearing in
S_{1}
there exists a unique

such that
P\subset R
.
Observe also that all the cubes
R\in\mathcal{R}
are contained in
19I
. Applying these observations together with Theorem

to the set
E_{k}
and cubes
R\in\mathcal{R}
yields

(7.6)

S_{1}\lesssim\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{D}(R)}\beta^{d,2}_{E_{k}}(4B_{P})^{2}\ell(P)^{d}\lesssim_{\delta}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{H}^{d}(R)\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}(E_{k}\cap 19I).

We move on to estimating
S_{2}
. This estimate is similar to that of "
I_{2}
" in (
6.5
). First, we use the definition of
E_{k}
and the subadditivity of Hausdorff content to get

\int_{4B_{Q}\cap E}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,E_{k})}{\ell(P)}\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\in\Delta_{k}(E)\\
J\cap 4B_{Q}\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\frac{\ell(J)^{2}}{\ell(Q)^{2}}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(J\cap 4B_{Q}\cap E)\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\in\Delta_{k}(E)\\
J\cap 4B_{Q}\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\frac{\ell(J)^{d+2}}{\ell(Q)^{2}}.

It is easy to check that if

with
j\leq m\leq k
and

, then for

as above we have

. It follows that

S_{2}\leq\sum_{m=j}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{D}^{E}_{m}\\
Q\cap 3I\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\in\Delta_{k}(E)\\

J\subset 24I\end{subarray}}\ell(J)^{d+2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{D}^{E}\\
\ell(Q)\geq\rho^{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,4B_{Q}\cap J\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\ell(Q)^{2}}\\
\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\in\Delta_{k}(E)\\
J\subset 24I\end{subarray}}\ell(J)^{d}.

We also have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\in\Delta_{k}(E)\\
J\subset 24I\end{subarray}}\ell(J)^{d}\sim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\in\Delta_{k}(E)\\

Hence,

. Putting this together with (

) and (

) gives (

).

∎

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
k\geq j>0
be integers and
I\in\Delta_{j}(E)
. We have

(7.7)

\sum_{m=j}^{k}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}^{E}_{m}\atop Q\cap 3I\neq\varnothing}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}\geq(k-j)C\delta\beta_{0}^{2}\rho^{jd},

where
C
is a constant depending only on
n,d
, but not on
\delta
.

Proof.

Recall first that
E
is lower
(d,c_{1})
-regular (with
c_{1}\sim\delta
, see Lemma
2.2
), so that in particular
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E\cap 3I)\gtrsim\delta\rho^{dj}
. Let
m\geq j
. Since the balls
\{B_{Q}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ Q\in\mathcal{D}^{E}_{m},\,Q\cap 3I\neq\varnothing\}
are a cover of

, we get

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}^{E}_{m}\atop Q\cap 3I\neq\varnothing}\ell(Q)^{d}\gtrsim\delta\rho^{dj}.

Recalling that
E
is uniformly wiggly we arrive at

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}^{E}_{m}\atop Q\cap 3I\neq\varnothing}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}\geq\beta_{0}^{2}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}^{E}_{m}\atop Q\cap 3I\neq\varnothing}\ell(Q)^{d}\gtrsim\delta\beta_{0}^{2}\rho^{dj}.

Summing over

gives (
7.7
).
∎

Recall that our aim was to prove that

\displaystyle{\rm dim}_{H}(E)\geq d+c\beta_{0}^{2}.

This estimate follows immediately from Theorem 8.8 in
[
Mat95
]
and the next lemma.

Lemma 7.5
.

There exists a non-zero measure
\mu
with
\supp\mu\subset E
and such that

\mu(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{d+c\beta_{0}^{2}}\quad\text{for $x\in E$ and $r>0$},

where
c
depends on
\delta,p,n,d
.

Proof.

The construction of
\mu
will is similar to the proof of Frostman’s lemma, as in
[
Mat95
, Theorem 8.8]
. First, we need to define a tree-like collection of dyadic cubes intersecting
E
which we will use in our construction.

Let
k>j\geq 1
be integers, and let
I\in\Delta_{j}(E)
. Using (
7.4
) and (
7.7
) we get that

(7.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{d}(E_{k}\cap AI)\gtrsim_{\delta}(k-j)\,\beta_{0}^{2}\,\rho^{dj}.

Let

D_{k}(I)\coloneqq\Delta_{k}(E\cap AI)=\{J\in\Delta_{k}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ J\cap AI\cap E\neq\varnothing\}.

It follows easily from the definition of
E_{k}
(
7.3
) that

E_{k}\cap AI\subset\bigcup_{J\in D_{k}(I)}{\partial}_{d}J.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{d}(E_{k}\cap AI)\lesssim\rho^{dk}\,\#D_{k}(I).

Together with (
7.8
) this gives

(7.9)

\displaystyle\#D_{k}(I)\gtrsim_{\delta}(k-j)\,\beta_{0}^{2}\,\rho^{d(j-k)}.

Observe that
\Delta(E)=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\Delta_{k}(E)
can be endowed with a natural tree structure, and this structure is used in the usual proof of the Frostman’s lemma. We would like to define a similar structure for the collections
D_{k}(I)
for

, but we need to take extra care due to possible overlaps between collections
D_{k}(I),D_{k}(J)
when

.

To overcome this technicality, for any integers
j,k
with
k\geq j\geq 1
and
I\in\Delta_{j}(E)
let
D^{\prime}_{k}(I)\subset D_{k}(I)
be a maximal subfamily of
D_{k}(I)
such that for any
J_{1},J_{2}\in D^{\prime}_{k}(I)
we have
A^{\prime}J_{1}\cap A^{\prime}J_{2}=\varnothing,
where

. Note that since for each

we have

, we get

\bigcup_{J\in D^{\prime}_{k}(I)}A^{\prime}J\subset A^{\prime}I.

Moreover, since all the cubes in
D_{k}(I)
have equal sidelength, the cubes
\{A^{\prime}J\}_{J\in D_{k}(I)}
have bounded overlap, and so it follows that

\#D^{\prime}_{k}(I)\gtrsim\#D_{k}(I).

Recalling (
7.9
), we get that

(7.10)

\#D^{\prime}_{k}(I)\geq C_{1}(k-j)\,\beta_{0}^{2}\,\rho^{d(j-k)}.

for some
0<C_{1}<1
depending on
\delta,p,n,d
.

Let

\displaystyle\kappa\coloneqq\lceil 10^{6}\,C_{1}^{-1}\beta_{0}^{-2}\rceil,

and observe that for
c\coloneqq C_{1}/10^{6}
we have

\kappa\,C_{1}\beta_{0}^{2}\geq 10^{6}=\rho^{-2}\geq\rho^{-\kappa\,c\beta_{0}^{2}},

where in the last inequality we use also the fact that
C_{1},\beta_{0}\in(0,1)
. Hence, if
k
is such that
k=j+\kappa
, the inequality (
7.10
) gives

(7.11)

\displaystyle\#D^{\prime}_{j+\kappa}(I)\geq\rho^{-\kappa(d+c\beta_{0}^{2})}.

We are ready to define the tree structure we will use to construct
\mu
. Set

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{0}=\Delta_{0}(E)=\{[0,1)^{n}\},

where we used our assumption
E\subset[0,1)^{n}
. Let
j\geq 0
and assume that
{\mathcal{S}}_{j}
has already been defined, that
{\mathcal{S}}_{j}\subset\Delta_{j\kappa}(E)
, and that for every
I,J\in{\mathcal{S}}_{j},\ I\neq J,
we have
A^{\prime}I\cap A^{\prime}J=\varnothing
. For each
I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{j}
we set

{\mathcal{S}}(I)=D^{\prime}_{(j+1)\kappa}(I),

and

{\mathcal{S}}_{j+1}=\bigcup_{I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{j}}{\mathcal{S}}(I).

Note that for every
J\in{\mathcal{S}}_{j+1}
there is a unique “parent”

such that
J\subset AI
(and
A^{\prime}J\subset A^{\prime}I
). Moreover, for every
I,J\in{\mathcal{S}}_{j+1},\ I\neq J,
we have
A^{\prime}I\cap A^{\prime}J=\varnothing
(either
I
and
J
have distinct parents, or they have the same parent
I^{\prime}
in which case we use the definition of
D^{\prime}_{(j+1)\kappa}(I^{\prime})
). Finally, observe that if
s\coloneqq d+c\beta_{0}^{2}
, then by (
7.11
)

(7.12)

\#{\mathcal{S}}_{j}\geq\rho^{-j\kappa s}\quad\text{and}\quad\#{\mathcal{S}}(I)\geq\rho^{-\kappa s}\text{ for each $I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{j}$}.

We are ready to construct the measure
\mu
. It is obtained as a weak limit of measures
\mu_{j},\,j\geq 0
. The definition of
\mu_{j}
follows the usual “bottom-to-top” construction of the Frostman measure, as in Theorem 8.8 of
[
Mat95
]
.

First, we set

\mu_{j}^{j}=\rho^{j\kappa s}\sum_{I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{j}}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n}|_{A^{\prime}I}}{\mathcal{L}^{n}(A^{\prime}I)}.

Assume that the measure
\mu_{j}^{i}
with
1\leq i\leq j
has already been defined, and that for each
I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
we have
\mu_{j}^{i}(A^{\prime}I)=\rho^{i\kappa s}
. We define
\mu_{j}^{i-1}
by modifying
\mu_{j}^{i}
at the level of
{\mathcal{S}}_{i-1}
: for each
I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{i-1}
we set

\mu_{j}^{i-1}|_{A^{\prime}I}\coloneqq\rho^{(i-1)\kappa s}\mu_{j}^{i}(A^{\prime}I)^{-1}\mu_{j}^{i}|_{A^{\prime}I}.

Finally, we define
\mu_{j}=\mu_{j}^{0}
. Clearly,
\mu_{j}([0,1)^{n})=1
.

Observe that the quantity used in the definition of
\mu_{j}^{i-1}|_{A^{\prime}I}
satisfies

\rho^{(i-1)\kappa s}\mu_{j}^{i}(A^{\prime}I)^{-1}=\rho^{\kappa s}(\#{\mathcal{S}}(I))^{-1}\overset{\eqref{eq:cardSI}}{\leq}1.

It follows that for every
i\leq k\leq j
and
I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
we have

\mu_{j}^{i}(A^{\prime}I)\leq\ell(I)^{s}.

In particular,

(7.13)

\mu_{j}(A^{\prime}I)\leq\ell(I)^{s}\quad\text{for all $I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{k},\,0\leq k\leq j$.}

Let
\mu_{j_{k}}
be a subsequence of
\mu_{j}
converging weakly, and define
\mu
to be the weak limit of
\mu_{j_{k}}
. Since
\mu_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=1
for all

, we also have
\mu(\mathbb{R}^{n})=1
. Using (
7.13
), a standard argument gives

\mu(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{s}\quad\text{for $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,r>0$},

see p.114 in
[
Mat95
]
for details. Finally,
\supp\mu\subset E
because

\supp\mu_{j}\subset\bigcup_{I\in\Delta_{j\kappa}(E)}I.

∎

This completes the proof of Proposition
7.1
.

8.
Denjoy’s conjecture

In this section we prove Theorem
1.7
.
Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
be a compact set with

-PBP with parameter
\delta>0
, and let
E\subset\Sigma
be compact. Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the Christ-David cubes on
\Sigma
, as in Lemma
2.1
. We are going to use Theorem
2.10
to get a lower bound on
\Gamma_{n,d}(E)
.

By Frostman’s lemma (see
[
Tol14
, Lemma 1.23]
), there exists a measure
\mu
supported on
E
such that
\mu(B(x,r))\leq r^{d}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,r>0
, and
\mu(E)\sim\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{d}(E)
. In fact, it follows immediately from the proof of
[
Tol14
, Lemma 1.23]
that one even has
\mu(B(x,r))\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E\cap B(x,r))
.

Recall that
\Sigma
is lower content regular with constant
\sim\delta
(see Lemma
2.2
). Using the fact that for any ball
x\in E
and
0<r<\infty
we have

\mu(B(x,r))\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E\cap B(x,r))\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(\Sigma\cap B(x,r))

it follows from the definitions of
\beta_{\mu,2}
and
\beta_{\Sigma}^{d,2}
that

(8.1)

\displaystyle\beta_{\mu,2}(x,r)\lesssim\beta_{\Sigma}^{d,2}(x,r).

Indeed, given a plane
L\in\mathcal{A}(n,d)
infimising
\beta_{E}^{d,2}(x,r)
set

E_{t}=E_{t}(x,r)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{y\in B(x,r)\cap E\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,L)^{2}>tr^{2}\}.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a covering of
E_{t}
such that
\sum_{B\in{\mathcal{C}}}\diam(B)^{d}\leq 2\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E_{t})
. We may assume that the covering consists of balls (see
[
Mat95
]
, Chapter 5). Since
{\mathcal{C}}
covers
E_{t}
, and
\mu
is a Frostman measure, we have

\mu(E_{t})\leq\sum_{B\in{\mathcal{C}}}\mu(B)\leq\sum_{B\in{\mathcal{C}}}r(B)^{d}\lesssim\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E_{t}).

Hence,

\beta_{\mu,2}(x,r)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(E_{t})\,t\,dt\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E_{t})\,t\,dt\sim\beta_{E}^{d,2}(x,r),

and this gives (
8.1
) since
E\subset\Sigma
.

Now recall that
\theta_{\mu}(x,r)=\mu(B(x,r))\,r^{-d}
is the
d
-density of
\mu
in the ball
B(x,r)
. Since
\mu(B(x,r))\leq r^{d}
, we have
\theta_{\mu}(x,r)\leq 1
. Arguing as we did below (
5.3
) and using (
8.1
) we get

\beta^{2}(\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\iint_{0}^{\infty}\beta_{\mu,2}(x,r)^{2}\theta_{\mu}(x,r)\,\frac{dr}{r}d\mu(x)\\
\leq\iint_{0}^{1}\beta_{\mu,2}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{dr}{r}d\mu(x)+\iint_{1}^{\infty}\beta_{\mu,2}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{dr}{r}d\mu(x)\\
\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}}\beta_{\mu,2}(3B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)+\iint_{1}^{\infty}\theta_{\mu}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{dr}{r}d\mu(x)\\
\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}}\beta_{\Sigma}^{d,2}(3B_{Q})^{2}\mu(Q)+\mu(E)\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma}}\beta_{\Sigma}^{d,2}(3B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}+\mu(E).

Applying Theorem
1.10
to estimate the last sum above yields

\beta^{2}(\mu)\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}}\beta_{\Sigma}^{d,2}(3B_{Q})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}+\mu(E)\lesssim C(\delta)\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma)+\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E)\lesssim C(\delta)\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma),

so that
\beta^{2}(\mu)\leq C_{1}(\delta)\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma)
for some
C_{1}(\delta)>1
.

Set

C_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left(\frac{\mu(E)}{C_{1}(\delta)\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and define the measure
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=C_{2}\mu
. Since
C_{2}\in(0,1)
, we have
\sigma(B(x,r))\leq\mu(B(x,r))\leq r^{d}
. Moreover,

\beta^{2}(\sigma)=C_{2}^{3}\beta^{2}(\mu)\leq\left(\frac{\mu(E)}{C_{1}(\delta)\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma)}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}C_{1}(\delta)\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma)=C_{2}\,\mu(E)=\sigma(E).

Then, by Theorem
2.10
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n,d}(E)\geq\sigma(E)=C_{2}\mu(E)=\left(\frac{\mu(E)}{C_{1}(\delta)\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mu(E)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=C_{1}(\delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\mu(E)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sim C_{1}(\delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma)^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

This finishes the proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Appendix A
Frostman’s lemma for lower content regular sets

We prove the following version of the classical Frostman’s lemma.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
E\subset[0,1]^{n}
be a compact, lower content
(d,c_{1})
-regular set. Then, there exists a measure
\mu
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

\supp\mu=E
,

(2)

\mu(E)=\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E)
,

(3)

\mu
has polynomial growth, that is, there exists a constant
C_{1}\geq 1
such that for all
x\in E
and
0<r<\diam(E)
we have

\mu(B(x,r))\leq C_{1}r^{d},

(4)

\mu
is doubling, that is, there exists a constant
C_{db}\geq 1
such that for all
x\in E
and
0<r<\diam(E)
we have

(A.1)

\mu(B(x,2r))\leq C_{db}\,\mu(B(x,r)).

(5)

the
d
-dimensional density of
\mu
is almost monotone, that is, there exists a constant
A\geq 1
such that if
P,Q\in\mathcal{D}
, and
P\subset Q
, then

\theta_{\mu}(P)\leq A\,\theta_{\mu}(Q).

In the above,
C_{1}
depends only on
d,n
, while
C_{db}
and
A
also depend on the lower regularity constant
c_{1}
.

The usual proof of Frostman’s lemma is “bottom-to-top”: it starts from small scales and goes up (see
[
Mat95
, Theorem 8.8]
). An alternative, simpler proof is due to Tolsa
[
Tol14
, Theorem 1.23]
, who came up with a “top-to-bottom” construction. To prove Theorem
A.1
we will modify Tolsa’s construction so that the resulting Frostman measure is doubling. Roughly speaking, at each step we are going to modify the measure by redistributing the mass between cubes where the doubling condition fails.

A.1.
Construction of a sequence of measures

In this subsection we construct a sequence of measures
\mu_{k}
. The measure
\mu
from Theorem
A.1
is going to be the weak limit of
\mu_{k}
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the David-Christ lattice on
E
, as in Lemma
2.1
.
For a cube
R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
, we denote by
\mathsf{Ch}(R)
the cubes
Q
in
\mathcal{D}_{k+1}
with
Q\subset R
. By
\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)
we denote the cubes
P\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
so that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(P,Q)\leq\ell(Q)
. Note that there exists a dimensional constant
c_{n}>1
such that

(A.2)

\#\mathsf{Ch}(Q)+\#\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)\leq c_{n}.

Recall that
Q^{1}
denotes the parent cube of
Q
, and also that the balls
B(Q)=B(x_{Q},c_{0}\ell(Q))
and
B_{Q}=B(x_{Q},\ell(Q))
satisfy
B(Q)\cap E\subset Q\subset B_{Q}
, where
\ell(Q)=\rho^{k}
for
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
E\subset[0,1]^{n}
be a compact, lower content
(d,c_{1})
-regular set. There exists a constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(n,d,c_{1})>1
and sequence of Radon measures
\mu_{k}
,
k\geq 0
, absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{n}
, such that, for each
k
, we have

(A.3)

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E),

(A.4)

\displaystyle\supp\mu_{k}=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}B(Q),

and moreover for all
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}

(A.5)

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(B(Q))

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)\leq 2\ell(Q)^{d},

(A.6)

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(B(Q))

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\,\mu_{k}(B(P))\quad\text{for all $P\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)$},

(A.7)

\displaystyle C_{0}^{-1}\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1}))

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{k}(B(Q))\lesssim\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1}))\quad\text{if $k\geq 1$}.

Note that since for all
P\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)
we have
Q\in\mathsf{Nbd}(P)
, if the property (
A.6
) holds for all
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
, then

(A.8)

C_{0}^{-1}\mu_{k}(B(P))\leq\mu_{k}(B(Q))\leq C_{0}\,\mu_{k}(B(P))\quad\text{for all $P\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)$}.

We construct
\mu_{k}
inductively. Let
Q_{0}=E\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
; that is,
Q_{0}
is the top cube. We define

\mu_{0}\coloneqq\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q_{0})\cdot\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n}|_{B(Q_{0})}}{\mathcal{L}^{n}(B(Q_{0}))}.

Clearly,
\mu_{0}
satisfies all the properties from Proposition
A.2
. Now, assume that
\mu_{k-1}
has already been defined and satisfies the properties (
A.3
)–(
A.7
).

A.1.1.
Auxiliary measure
\eta_{k}

For
R\in\mathcal{D}_{k-1}
and for each
Q\in\mathsf{Ch}(R)
, set

(A.9)

\eta_{k}|_{B(Q)}\coloneqq\left(\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)}{\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Ch}(R)}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(P)}\cdot\mu_{k-1}(B(R))\right)\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n}|_{B(Q)}}{\mathcal{L}^{n}(B(Q))}.

Observe that

(A.10)

\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Ch}(R)}\eta_{k}(B(Q))=\mu_{k-1}(B(R)).

Lemma A.3
.

We have
\eta_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mu_{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E)
, so that condition (
A.3
) holds for
\eta_{k}
.

Proof.

We have

\eta_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\eta_{k}(B(Q))=\sum_{R\in\mathcal{D}_{k-1}}\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Ch}(R)}\eta_{k}(B(Q))\\
\overset{\eqref{eq:etachildrencons}}{=}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{D}_{k-1}}\mu_{k-1}(B(R))=\mu_{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E).

∎

Lemma A.4
.

The polynomial growth condition (
A.5
) holds for
\eta_{k}
.

Proof.

Let
R\in\mathcal{D}_{k-1}
and
Q\in\mathsf{Ch}(R)
. Since (
A.5
) holds for
\mu_{k-1}
, we have
\mu_{k-1}(B(R))\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(R)\leq\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Ch}(R)}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(P)
, by subadditivity of
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}
. Then,

(A.11)

\eta_{k}(B(Q))=\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)\cdot\frac{\mu_{k-1}(B(R))}{\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Ch}(R)}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(P)}\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)\leq 2\ell(Q)^{d}.

∎

Lemma A.5
.

The doubling condition (
A.6
) holds for
\eta_{k}
whenever both
Q
and
P
in
\mathcal{D}_{k}
have a common parent
R\in\mathcal{D}_{k-1}
. Moreover, for all
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}

(A.12)

\eta_{k}(B(Q))\geq c_{2}\,\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1}))

for
0<c_{2}<1
depending on
c_{1},n,d
. In particular (
A.7
) holds for
\eta_{k}
assuming
C_{0}
is big enough.

Proof.

Let
R\in\mathcal{D}_{k-1}
and
Q,P\in\mathsf{Ch}(R)
. Using lower content regularity of
E
we have

(A.13)

\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)\geq\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(B(Q)\cap E)\geq c_{1}(c_{0}\ell(Q))^{d}=\frac{c_{1}(c_{0})^{d}}{2\rho^{d}}\cdot 2(\ell(R))^{d}\geq\frac{c_{1}(c_{0})^{d}}{2\rho^{d}}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(R).

Recall that
\#\mathsf{Ch}(R)\leq c_{n}
. We then compute

\eta_{k}(B(Q))=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)}{\sum_{P^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Ch}(R)}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(P^{\prime})}\cdot\mu_{k-1}(B(R))\\
\geq\frac{c_{1}(c_{0})^{d}}{2\rho^{d}}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(R)}{c_{n}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(R)}\cdot\mu_{k-1}(B(R))\ =\frac{c_{1}(c_{0})^{d}}{2c_{n}\rho^{d}}\mu_{k-1}(B(R)).

This shows (
A.12
). To see (
A.6
) note that

\eta_{k}(B(P))\leq\mu_{k-1}(B(R))\leq c_{2}\eta_{k}(B(Q))\leq C_{0}\eta_{k}(B(Q)),

so (
A.6
) holds true whenever
P
is a sibling of
Q
.
∎

The lemmas above say that
\eta_{k}
satisfies almost all the required properties, except that instead of the full doubling condition it only satisfies a “dyadic doubling” condition. We need to make some adjustments.

A.1.2.
Definition of
\mu_{k}

We define families of cubes where the doubling condition (
A.8
) fails. For each
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
let

\mathsf{Rich}(Q)=\{P\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \eta_{k}(B(Q))<4C_{0}^{-1}\,\eta_{k}(B(P))\},

and similarly

\mathsf{Poor}(Q)=\big\{P\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \eta_{k}(B(Q))>\frac{C_{0}}{4}\,\eta_{k}(B(P))\big\}.

Note that

(A.14)

\#\mathsf{Poor}(Q)+\#\mathsf{Rich}(Q)\leq 2\,\#\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)\leq 2\,c_{n}.

Set

\displaystyle\mathsf{Poor}_{k}

\displaystyle=\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \mathsf{Rich}(Q)\neq\varnothing\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{Rich}_{k}

\displaystyle=\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \mathsf{Poor}(Q)\neq\varnothing\}.

Lemma A.6
.

We have
\mathsf{Poor}_{k}\cap\mathsf{Rich}_{k}=\varnothing
.

Proof.

Suppose that
Q\in\mathsf{Poor}_{k}\cap\mathsf{Rich}_{k}
. Then, there exist
P_{p},P_{r}\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)
such that

\frac{C_{0}}{4}\,\eta_{k}(B(P_{p}))<\eta_{k}(B(Q))<4C_{0}^{-1}\,\eta_{k}(B(P_{r})).

In particular,
\eta_{k}(B(P_{p}))<16C_{0}^{-2}\,\eta_{k}(B(P_{r}))
. Let
R_{p},R_{r}
be the parents of
P_{p},P_{r}
, respectively. Note that
R_{p}\in\mathsf{Nbd}(R_{r})
. Then, by (
A.12
)

\mu_{k-1}(B(R_{p}))\leq c_{2}^{-1}\,\eta_{k}(B(P_{p}))<16c_{2}^{-1}C_{0}^{-2}\eta_{k}(B(P_{r}))\leq C_{0}^{-1}\mu_{k-1}(B(R_{r})),

assuming
C_{0}
big enough. This contradicts the doubling property (
A.8
) for
\mu_{k-1}
. Hence,
\mathsf{Poor}_{k}\cap\mathsf{Rich}_{k}=\varnothing
.
∎

We define the measure
\mu_{k}
with
\supp\mu_{k}=\supp\eta_{k}=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}B(Q)
in the following way. For each
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
we set

\mu_{k}|_{B(Q)}=\begin{cases}\big(1-C_{0}^{-1}\cdot\#\mathsf{Poor}(Q)\big)\eta_{k}|_{B(Q)}&\text{if $Q\in\mathsf{Rich}_{k}$,}\\
\big(1+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Rich}(Q)}\frac{\eta_{k}(B(P))}{\eta_{k}(B(Q))}\big)\eta_{k}|_{B(Q)}&\text{if $Q\in\mathsf{Poor}_{k}$,}\\
\eta_{k}|_{B(Q)}&\text{if $Q\notin\mathsf{Rich}_{k}\cup\mathsf{Poor}_{k}$.}\end{cases}

Lemma A.7
.

Property (
A.7
) holds for
\mu_{k}
.

Proof.

Observe that by (
A.14
), as soon as
C_{0}
is large enough depending on
n
, we have

(A.15)

\mu_{k}\geq\frac{1}{2}\eta_{k}.

Thus, we immediately get from (
A.12
) that
\mu_{k}
satisfies

\mu_{k}(B(Q))\geq\frac{c_{2}}{2}\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1}))\geq C_{0}^{-1}\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1})).

This shows one of the inequalities in (
A.7
). Now we prove that

(A.16)

\mu_{k}(B(Q))\lesssim\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1})).

For
Q\notin\mathsf{Poor}_{k}
this is trivial, because then by the definition of
\mu_{k}
and
\eta_{k}
we have
\mu_{k}(B(Q))\leq\eta_{k}(B(Q))\leq\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1}))
. On the other hand, for
Q\in\mathsf{Poor}_{k}

\mu_{k}(B(Q))=\eta_{k}(B(Q))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Rich}(Q)}\eta_{k}(B(P))\\
\leq\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1}))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Rich}(Q)}\mu_{k-1}(B(P^{1})).

For each
P\in\mathsf{Rich}(Q)
we have
P^{1}\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q^{1})
, and so by (
A.6
) applied to
\mu_{k-1}
we get

\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1}))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Rich}(Q)}\mu_{k-1}(B(P^{1}))\\
\leq\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1}))+\#\mathsf{Rich}(Q)\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1}))\lesssim\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1})).

This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.8
.

We have
\mu_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\eta_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E),
so that condition (
A.3
) holds for
\mu_{k}
.

Proof.

We have

\mu_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\mu_{k}(B(Q))=\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Rich}_{k}}\big(1-C_{0}^{-1}\cdot\#\mathsf{Poor}(Q)\big)\eta_{k}(B(Q))\\
+\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Poor}_{k}}\big(\eta_{k}(B(Q))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Rich}(Q)}\eta_{k}(B(P))\big)+\sum_{Q\notin\mathsf{Rich}_{k}\cup\mathsf{Poor}_{k}}\eta_{k}(B(Q))\\
=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\eta_{k}(B(Q))-C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Rich}_{k}}\#\mathsf{Poor}(Q)\eta_{k}(B(Q))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{Q\in\mathsf{Poor}_{k}}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Rich}(Q)}\eta_{k}(B(P))\\
=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\eta_{k}(B(Q))=\eta_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E).

∎

Lemma A.9
.

The polynomial growth condition (
A.5
) holds for
\mu_{k}
.

Proof.

Observe that for
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\setminus\mathsf{Poor}_{k}
we have
\mu_{k}(B(Q))\leq\eta_{k}(B(Q))
, so in this case (
A.5
) follows from Lemma
A.4
. Assume that
Q\in\mathsf{Poor}_{k}
. Then, there exists
P\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)
such that

\eta_{k}(B(Q))\leq 2C_{0}^{-1}\eta_{k}(B(P))\leq 4C_{0}^{-1}\ell(Q)^{d},

where in the last inequality we used again Lemma
A.4
. Then,

\mu_{k}(B(Q))=\eta_{k}(B(Q))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Rich}(Q)}\eta_{k}(B(P))\\
\leq 4C_{0}^{-1}\ell(Q)^{d}+\#\mathsf{Rich}(Q)\cdot 2C_{0}^{-1}\ell(Q)^{d}\overset{\eqref{eq:poorcard}}{\lesssim_{n}}C_{0}^{-1}\ell(Q)^{d}.

Recalling that
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)\gtrsim c_{1}\ell(Q)^{d}
by (
A.13
), we get that
\mu_{k}(B(Q))\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)\leq 2\ell(Q)^{d}
assuming
C_{0}
large enough depending on
c_{1},n,d
.
∎

Lemma A.10
.

The doubling condition (
A.6
) holds for
\mu_{k}
.

Proof.

We want to show that for any
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
and
P\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)
we have
\mu_{k}(B(Q))\leq C_{0}\,\mu_{k}(B(P))
. First, suppose that
Q\notin\mathsf{Rich}_{k}\cup\mathsf{Poor}_{k}
, so that
\mu_{k}(B(Q))=\eta_{k}(B(Q))
. Let
P\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)
. We have
P\notin\mathsf{Poor}(Q)
, and so

\mu_{k}(B(Q))=\eta_{k}(B(Q))\leq\frac{C_{0}}{4}\eta_{k}(B(P))\overset{\eqref{eq:muketak}}{\leq}C_{0}\,\mu_{k}(B(P)).

Assume now that
Q\in\mathsf{Rich}_{k},
and let
P\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)
. There are two cases: either
P\in\mathsf{Poor}(Q)
, or
P\notin\mathsf{Poor}(Q)
. In the first case we have
Q\in\mathsf{Rich}(P)
, and so

\mu_{k}(B(Q))\leq\eta_{k}(B(Q))\leq C_{0}\big(\eta_{k}(B(P))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Rich}(P)}\eta_{k}(B(Q^{\prime}))\big)=C_{0}\,\mu_{k}(B(P)).

If
P\notin\mathsf{Poor}(Q)
then by the definition of
\mathsf{Poor}(Q)

\mu_{k}(B(Q))\leq\eta_{k}(B(Q))\leq\frac{C_{0}}{4}\eta_{k}(B(P))\overset{\eqref{eq:muketak}}{\leq}C_{0}\,\mu_{k}(B(P)).

This establishes (
A.6
) for
Q\in\mathsf{Rich}_{k}
.

Finally, suppose that
Q\in\mathsf{Poor}_{k}
and
P\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)
. Since for any
R\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)
we have
R^{1}\in\mathsf{Nbd}(P^{1})
, we get

\mu_{k}(B(Q))=\eta_{k}(B(Q))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{R\in\mathsf{Rich}(Q)}\eta_{k}(B(R))\\
\leq\eta_{k}(B(Q))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{R\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)}\eta_{k}(B(R))\leq\eta_{k}(B(Q))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{R^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Nbd}(P^{1})}\sum_{R\in\mathsf{Ch}(R^{\prime})}\eta_{k}(B(R))\\
\overset{\eqref{eq:etachildrencons}}{=}\eta_{k}(B(Q))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{R^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Nbd}(P^{1})}\mu_{k-1}(B(R^{\prime})).

Concerning the first term on the right hand side, note that since
Q\in\mathsf{Poor}_{k}
, by Lemma
A.6
we have
Q\notin\mathsf{Rich}_{k}
and consequently
P\notin\mathsf{Poor}(Q)
, so that
\eta_{k}(B(Q))\leq\frac{C_{0}}{4}\,\eta_{k}(B(P))
. To deal with the second term we use the doubling property (

) for

and the fact that

:

\mu_{k}(B(Q))\leq\eta_{k}(B(Q))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{R^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Nbd}(P^{1})}\mu_{k-1}(B(R^{\prime}))\\
\leq\frac{C_{0}}{4}\eta_{k}(B(P))+\#\mathsf{Nbd}(P^{1})\mu_{k-1}(B(P^{1}))\leq\frac{C_{0}}{4}\eta_{k}(B(P))+c_{n}\mu_{k-1}(B(P^{1}))\\
\overset{\eqref{eq:child2}}{\leq}\frac{C_{0}}{4}\eta_{k}(B(P)+\frac{c_{n}}{c_{2}}\eta_{k}(B(P))\leq\frac{C_{0}}{2}\eta_{k}(B(P))\overset{\eqref{eq:muketak}}{\leq}C_{0}\,\mu_{k}(B(P)),

assuming

large enough. This gives the desired inequality (

) for
Q\in\mathsf{Poor}_{k}
. ∎

We have checked that

is complete.

We prove two more properties of

Recall that

Lemma A.11
.

If
j\geq k\geq 0
and

, then

(A.17)

\mu_{j}(2B_{Q})\geq\mu_{k}(B(Q))

and

(A.18)

\mu_{j}(B_{Q})\leq\mu_{k}(10B_{Q}.)

Proof.

In the definition of
\mu_{k+1}
we transfer the mass of

only between neighbors. It follows easily that for any family

(A.19)

where

\mathcal{A}({\mathcal{F}})\coloneqq\bigcup_{P\in\mathcal{F}}\bigcup_{R\in\mathsf{Ch}(P)}\mathsf{Nbd}(R)\subset\mathcal{D}_{k+1}.

Let
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
. Set
\mathcal{A}_{0}(Q)\coloneqq\{Q\}
, and then inductively

\mathcal{A}_{i}(Q)\coloneqq\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{A}_{i-1}(Q))=\bigcup_{P\in\mathcal{A}_{i-1}(Q)}\bigcup_{R\in\mathsf{Ch}(P)}\mathsf{Nbd}(R)\subset\mathcal{D}_{k+i}.

Applying (
A.19
)
i
-times yields

(A.20)

\mu_{k}(B(Q))\leq\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(Q)}\mu_{k+i}(B(P)).

Observe that if

, then
\bigcup_{R^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Nbd}(R)}2B_{R^{\prime}}\subset 5B_{R}
. It follows that for all

\bigcup_{P\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(Q)}2B_{P}\subset 2B_{Q}.

Indeed, this is clear for

, and then by induction

\bigcup_{P\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(Q)}2B_{P}=\bigcup_{P\in\mathcal{A}_{i-1}(Q)}\bigcup_{R\in\mathsf{Ch}(P)}\bigcup_{R^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Nbd}(R)}2B_{R^{\prime}}\subset\bigcup_{P\in\mathcal{A}_{i-1}(Q)}\bigcup_{R\in\mathsf{Ch}(P)}5B_{R}\\
\subset\bigcup_{P\in\mathcal{A}_{i-1}(Q)}2B_{P}\subset 2B_{Q}.

Together with (
A.20
) this gives (
A.17
).

Similarly, if for
{\mathcal{F}}\subset\mathcal{D}_{j}
we define

\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{F}})\coloneqq\bigcup_{P\in\mathcal{F}}\mathsf{Nbd}(P^{1})\subset\mathcal{D}_{j-1},

then by the definition of
\mu_{j}

(A.21)

\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{F}}}\mu_{j}(B(Q))\leq\sum_{P\in\bigcup_{Q\in{\mathcal{F}}}\mathsf{Nbd}(Q)}\eta_{j}(B(P))\leq\sum_{R\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{F}})}\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Ch}(R)}\eta_{j}(B(P))\\
\overset{\eqref{eq:etachildrencons}}{=}\sum_{R\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{F}})}\mu_{j-1}(B(R)).

Let
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
and fix
j\geq k
. Set
{\mathcal{B}}_{0}(Q)=\{P\in\mathcal{D}_{j}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ B(P)\cap B_{Q}\neq\varnothing\}
, and for
0<i\leq j-k
we define inductively

{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(Q)\coloneqq{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{B}}_{i-1}(Q))=\bigcup_{P\in{\mathcal{B}}_{i-1}(Q)}\mathsf{Nbd}(P^{1})\subset\mathcal{D}_{j-i}.

By (
A.21
)

(A.22)

\mu_{j}(B_{Q})\leq\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{B}}_{0}(Q)}\mu_{j}(B(P))\leq\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{B}}_{j-k}(Q)}\mu_{k}(B(P)).

Now, we claim that

\bigcup_{P\in{\mathcal{B}}_{j-k}(Q)}B(P)\subset 10B_{Q}.

To see this, note that each
P\in{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(Q)
has a neighbor
P^{\prime}
such that
\mathsf{Ch}(P^{\prime})\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{i-1}(Q)\neq\varnothing.
Hence,

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(P,\bigcup_{R\in{\mathcal{B}}_{i-1}(Q)}R)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(P,P^{\prime})+\diam(P^{\prime})\leq 3\ell(P)=15\rho^{j-i}.

Fix
P\in{\mathcal{B}}_{j-k}(Q)
, and let

be such that for each

we have

and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(P_{i},\bigcup_{R\in{\mathcal{B}}_{i-1}(Q)}R)=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(P_{i},P_{i-1})
. Then,

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(P,\bigcup_{R\in{\mathcal{B}}_{0}(Q)}R)\leq\sum_{i=j-k}^{1}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(P_{i},\bigcup_{R\in{\mathcal{B}}_{i-1}(Q)}R)+\ell(P_{i-1})\\
\leq\sum_{i=j-k}^{1}4\ell(P_{i})=\sum_{i=j-k}^{1}20\rho^{j-i}\leq 25\rho^{k}=5\ell(Q).

It is easy to see that
\bigcup_{R\in{\mathcal{B}}_{0}(Q)}R\subset 3B_{Q}
, and so it follows that

Together with (
A.22
), this gives (
A.18
).
∎

Another nice property of
\mu_{k}
we are going to need is related to the approximate monotonicity of densities.

Lemma A.12
.

Assume that
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
. Then, there exists
R\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q^{1})\subset\mathcal{D}_{k-1}
with

(A.23)

\frac{\mu_{k}(B(Q))}{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)}\leq\frac{\mu_{k-1}(B(R))}{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(R)}.

Proof.

There are two cases to consider. First, if
\mu_{k}(B(Q))\leq\eta_{k}(B(Q))
, then by the definition

\mu_{k}(B(Q))\leq\eta_{k}(B(Q))=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)}{\sum_{P\in\mathsf{Ch}(Q^{1})}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(P)}\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1}))\leq\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)}{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q^{1})}\cdot\mu_{k-1}(B(Q^{1})).

Hence, taking
R=Q^{1}
gives (
A.23
).

Now suppose that
\mu_{k}(B(Q))>\eta_{k}(B(Q))
, i.e.
Q\in\mathsf{Poor}_{k}
. Let
P\in\mathsf{Rich}(Q)
be the cube maximizing
\eta_{k}(B(P))
. Then,

\mu_{k}(B(Q))=\eta_{k}(B(Q))+C_{0}^{-1}\sum_{S\in\mathsf{Rich}(Q)}\eta_{k}(B(S))\\
\leq 4C_{0}^{-1}\,\eta_{k}(B(P))+\#\mathsf{Rich}(Q)C_{0}^{-1}\,\eta_{k}(B(P))\leq C_{0}^{-1}(4+c_{n})\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(P)}{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(P^{1})}\mu_{k-1}(B(P^{1})).

Using lower content regularity of
E
we have
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)\geq\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E\cap B(Q))\geq c_{1}(c_{0}\ell(Q))^{d}
, so that

\frac{\mu_{k}(B(Q))}{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(Q)}\leq C_{0}^{-1}(4+c_{n})\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(P)}{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}{(Q)}}\cdot\frac{\mu_{k-1}(B(P^{1}))}{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(P^{1})}\\
\leq C_{0}^{-1}(4+c_{n})\frac{(2\ell(P))^{d}}{c_{1}(c_{0}\ell(Q))^{d}}\cdot\frac{\mu_{k-1}(B(P^{1}))}{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(P^{1})}\\
=C_{0}^{-1}(4+c_{n})2^{d}c_{1}^{-1}c_{0}^{-d}\frac{\mu_{k-1}(B(P^{1}))}{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(P^{1})}\leq\frac{\mu_{k-1}(B(P^{1}))}{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(P^{1})},

assuming
C_{0}
large enough depending on
c_{1},n,d
. Since
P^{1}\in\mathsf{Nbd}(Q^{1})
, choosing
R=P^{1}
gives (
A.23
).
∎

A.2.
The limit measure

For each
k
we have
\mu_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mu_{k}([0,1]^{n})=\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E)<\infty
, and so there exists a subsequence
\mu_{k_{j}}
converging in the weak sense to a Radon measure
\mu
which also satisfies
\mu(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(E)
. This shows property (
2
) from Theorem
A.1
. In the next few lemmas we prove that
\mu
satisfies all the other required properties.

The following two basic facts about weak limits of measures will often be used without explicit mention: if
\nu_{j}\to\nu
weakly, then for any open
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any compact
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

\nu(U)\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\nu_{j}(U)\,\,\mbox{ and }\,\,\limsup_{j\to\infty}\nu_{j}(K)\leq\nu(K).

See
[
Mat95
, Theorem 1.24]
.

Lemma A.13
.

We have
\supp\mu=E
, so that property (
1
) in Theorem
A.1
is satisfied.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E
. Since
E
is compact, for sufficiently small
r>0
and sufficiently large
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

, where
{\mathcal{N}}_{k}(E)
is the open
5\rho^{k}
neighborhood of
E
. Note that for any
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
the ball
B(Q)
is compactly contained in
{\mathcal{N}}_{k}(E)
. It follows from (
A.4
) that
\mu_{k}(B(x,r))=0
for sufficiently large
k
. Thus,

\mu(B(x,r))\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mu_{k_{j}}(B(x,r))=0.

Thus,
x\notin\supp\mu
. This shows
\supp\mu\subset E
.

Now, let
x\in E
and
r>0
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
large enough we have that if

contains

, then
2B_{Q}\subset B(x,r)
. Fix such

. By (

) we get that for all

In consequence,

\mu(\overline{B(x,r)})\geq\limsup_{j\to\infty}\mu_{k_{j}}(\overline{B(x,r)})\geq\mu_{k}(B(Q))>0.

∎
