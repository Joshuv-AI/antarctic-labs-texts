Abstract
This paper proposes a novel matrix rank-one decomposition for quaternion Hermitian matrices, which admits a stronger property than the previous results in sturm2003cones ; huang2007complex ; ai2011new . The enhanced property can be used to drive some improved results in joint numerical range, 𝒮 \mathcal{S} -Procedure and quadratically constrained quadratic programming (QCQP) in the quaternion domain, demonstrating the capability of our new decomposition technique.

In recent years, we have witnessed a burst of quaternion representations in many fields,
including color imaging

;

;

Hence, the increasing number of quaternion-represented applications and the studies on the associated optimization problems call for a deeper understanding of the quaternion structure that could lead to some efficient solution methods. In this paper, we shall focus on one algebraic quaternion structure: matrix rank-one decomposition, and show that such decomposition admits a stronger property than that in the real and complex domain by leveraging the intrinsic quaternion nature. We further show that such merit of rank-one decomposition can be extended to some of its theoretical implications such as
\mathcal{S}
-Procedure, joint numerical range and quadratically constrained quadratic programming (QCQP) in the quaternion domain, and improve the associated results in the real and complex domains.

The matrix rank-one decomposition that we discuss in this paper is a technique of decomposing a positive semidefinite Hermitian matrix into the sum of rank-one matrices to satisfy the so-called equal inner product property, i.e., the inner product between some given matrices and each rank-one term in the decomposition has the same value.
The first such type of decomposition was introduced by Sturm and Zhang in
sturm2003cones
with the equal inner product property valid for
one
matrix, and it was used as a key technique to establish the Linear Matrix Inequality presentation of a class of matrix cones with its quadratic form co-positive over the real domain. Moreover, such a decomposition technique was found to be useful in quadratic minimization
ye2003new
and designing approximation algorithms for biquadratic optimization
ling2010biquadratic
.
Soon after the work of
sturm2003cones
, Huang and Zhang
huang2007complex
extended the
matrix rank-one decomposition to the complex domain such that the equal inner product property holds for
two
matrices. Interestingly, we find that such property remains valid for
four
matrices when the rank-one decomposition is conducted in the quaternion domain. Moreover, our proof is fully constructive and the corresponding computational procedure is summarized in
Algorithm
3
.

Theoretical implications of our novel matrix decomposition technique are quite versatile and yield stronger results than those in the real and complex domains. The first two theoretical implications are in joint numerical range and
\mathcal{S}
-Procedure, both of which have some fundamental impacts and wide applications in many fields.
In particular, the joint numerical range is an important tool in linear algebra and convex analysis, and it is found to be useful in spectrum analysis
rasulov2019description
and quantum computing
dirr2006new
;
rodman2016continuity
.
\mathcal{S}
-Procedure occupies a crucial position in the field of robust optimization
anitescu2000degenerate
;
anitescu2002superlinearly
;
goldfarb2003robust
, statistics
hoerl1970ridge
, signal processing

, among others

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce some basic algebraic operations in the quaternion domain for scalars, vectors and matrices.

2.1
Quaternion Operations for Scalars

We define the set of quaternions
\mathbb{H}
as a 4-dimensional normed division algebra over the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
.
It has a canonical basis
\{1,\mbox{\boldmath{$i$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$k$}}\}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath{$i$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$k$}}
are imaginary units such that

\mbox{\boldmath{$i$}}^{2}=\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}^{2}=\mbox{\boldmath{$k$}}^{2}=\mbox{\boldmath{$i$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$k$}}=-1,\quad\mbox{\boldmath{$i$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}=-\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$i$}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$k$}}.

(1)

Then, any quaternion
q\in\mathbb{H}
can be written as

q=q_{a}+q_{b}\mbox{\boldmath{$i$}}+q_{c}\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}+q_{d}\mbox{\boldmath{$k$}},

where
q_{a},q_{b},q_{c},q_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
are the components of
q
. The real and imaginary parts of
q
are denoted as
\mbox{\rm Re~}q=q_{a}
and
\mbox{\rm Im~}q=q_{b}\mbox{\boldmath{$i$}}+q_{c}\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}+q_{d}\mbox{\boldmath{$k$}}
respectively.
Note that, in contrast with the product operation in the real and complex domain, the product operation in the quaternion domain is noncommutative, i.e.,
q\cdot p\neq p\cdot q
for
p,q\in\mathbb{H}
.

We denote by
\overline{q}=\mbox{\rm Re~}q-\mbox{\rm Im~}q
the quaternion conjugate of
q
, and it holds that
\overline{(p\cdot q)}=\overline{q}\cdot\overline{p}
. For a given quaternion
q\in\mathbb{H}
,
|q|
denotes its modulus and can be expressed as

|q|=\sqrt{q\cdot\overline{q}}=\sqrt{\overline{q}\cdot q}=\sqrt{q_{a}^{2}+q_{b}^{2}+q_{c}^{2}+q_{d}^{2}}.

Then, we denote
q=|q|(\cos\theta+q^{I}\sin\theta)
as the triangle representation
fan2012qua
of quaternion
q
, where
\cos\theta=\frac{q_{a}}{|q|}
,
\sin\theta=\frac{\sqrt{q_{b}^{2}+q_{c}^{2}+q_{d}^{2}}}{|q|}
and
q^{I}=\frac{q_{b}\mbox{\boldmath{$i$}}+q_{c}\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}+q_{d}\mbox{\boldmath{$k$}}}{\sqrt{q_{b}^{2}+q_{c}^{2}+q_{d}^{2}}}
.
At last, any non-zero quaternion
q
has an inverse
q^{-1}=\overline{q}/|q|^{2}
and the inverse of the product of two quaternions is
(p\cdot q)^{-1}=q^{-1}\cdot p^{-1}
.

2.2
Quaternion Vectors and Quaternion Matrices

Similar to the scalar case, a quaternion vector
\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
can be written as

\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{a}+\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{b}\mbox{\boldmath{$i$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{c}\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{d}\mbox{\boldmath{$k$}},

where
\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{a},\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{b},\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{c},\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the components of
q
. For a quaternion vector
q
,
\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}^{\top}
denotes the transpose of
q
, and
\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}^{H}=(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}})^{\top}=\overline{(\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}^{\top})}
denotes its conjugate transpose. Any quaternion matrix
A\in\mathbb{H}^{m\times n}
can be expressed as

A=A_{a}+A_{b}\mbox{\boldmath{$i$}}+A_{c}\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}+A_{d}\mbox{\boldmath{$k$}}

with
A_{a},A_{b},A_{c},A_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. The transpose and the conjugate transpose of
A
are
A^{\top}
and
A^{H}=(\overline{A})^{\top}=\overline{(A^{\top})}
, respectively. For two quaternion matrices
A\in\mathbb{H}^{m\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times p}
, we have
(AB)^{H}=B^{H}A^{H}
; however, due to the noncommutativity of the product operation,
(AB)^{\top}\not=B^{\top}A^{\top}
and
\overline{(AB)}\not=\overline{A}\ \overline{B}
.
For two vectors
\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, their inner product

\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}\bullet\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}=\mbox{\rm Re~}(\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}^{H}\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}})=\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{a}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{a}+\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{b}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{b}+\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{c}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{c}+\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{d}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{d}.

Similarly, for two matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
, their inner product is defined as

A\bullet B:=\mbox{\rm Re~}(\mbox{\rm tr}\,A^{H}B)=\mbox{\rm tr}\,(A_{a}^{\top}B_{a}+A_{b}^{\top}B_{b}+A_{c}^{\top}B_{c}+A_{d}^{\top}B_{d}),

where ‘tr’ denotes the trace of a matrix.

2.3
Hermitian and Positive Semidefinite Matrices

We call
X
a quaternion Hermitian matrix if it satisfies
X=X^{H}
. The cone of quaternion Hermitian matrices is denoted as
\mathcal{H}^{n}
. Then
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{H}X\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}
is real for all
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
if and only if
X\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}(\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n})
,
\mathcal{C}_{+}^{n}(\mathcal{C}_{++}^{n})
and
\mathcal{H}_{+}^{n}(\mathcal{H}_{++}^{n})
the cones of real symmetric positive semidefinite (positive definite), complex Hermitian positive semidefinite (positive definite) and quaternion Hermitian positive semidefinite (positive definite) matrices, respectively. The notation
X\succeq 0
(
X\succ 0
) means that
X
is positive semidefinite (positive definite). Then, for any matrix
X\succeq 0
(
X\succ 0
), we have
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{H}X\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}
is real and nonnegative (positive) for all
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
(
0\neq\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
).

3
The Quaternion Matrix Rank-One Decomposition Method

In this section, we discuss a particular rank-one decomposition of matrices over the quaternion domain such that the inner product between some given matrices and each rank-one term in the decomposition has the same value.

In the real domain, Sturm and Zhang
sturm2003cones
showed that for a rank-
r
symmetric positive semidefinite matrix
Y\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}
and a real symmetric matrix
B\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
, there is a rank-one decomposition of
Y
such that:

Y=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}_{i}^{\top}~~\mbox{\rm and}~~\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}_{j}^{\top}B\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}_{j}=(\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}_{i}^{\top})\bullet Y=\frac{B\bullet Y}{r},~~\mbox{for}~j=1,2,\cdots,r.

Subsequently, Huang and Zhang
huang2007complex
proved that the above decomposition could hold for two matrices in the complex domain. In particular, suppose
Z\in\mathcal{C}_{+}^{n}
is a complex Hermitian positive semidefinite matrix of rank
r
, and
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
are two given complex Hermitian matrices. Then, there is a rank-one decomposition of
Z
such that:

Z=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mbox{\boldmath{$z$}}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$z$}}_{i}^{H}~~\mbox{\rm and}~~\mbox{\boldmath{$z$}}_{j}^{H}C_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$z$}}_{j}=\frac{C_{k}\bullet Z}{r},~~\mbox{for}~j=1,2,\cdots,r~\mbox{\rm and}~k=1,2.

Thus, a natural question arises: can a similar decomposition holds for more matrices over the quaternion domain? The following theorem is the main result of this paper and gives an affirmative answer to this question.

Theorem 3.1

Let
A_{k}\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
,
k=1,2,3,4
, and rank-
r
matrix
X\succeq 0
. There exists a rank-one decomposition of
X
such that

X=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{i}^{H}~~\mbox{\rm and}~~\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{i}^{H}A_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{i}=\frac{A_{k}\bullet X}{r},~~\mbox{for}~~i=1,\cdots,r

(2)

and
k=1,2,3,4
.

Proof

We start with a weaker version of (
2
) such that it holds only for two matrices
A_{1}
and
A_{2}

X

X=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{i}^{H}~~\mbox{\rm and}~~\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{i}^{H}A_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{i}=\frac{A_{k}\bullet X}{r},~\mbox{\rm for}~i=1,\cdots,r~\mbox{and}~k=1,2

(3)

according to the similar argument of Theorem 2.1 in

. The rest of the proof consists of two steps.

Step
1
: prove (
2
) for
k=1,2,3

The conclusion follows

3

\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{i}^{H}A_{3}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{i}=\frac{A_{3}\bullet X}{r}
,

\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}
such that

\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}^{H}A_{3}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}>\frac{A_{3}\bullet X}{r}\ \mbox{\rm and}\ \mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}^{H}A_{3}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}<\frac{A_{3}\bullet X}{r}.

Denote

Then, it is easy to verify that

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{1}^{H}+\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{2}^{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+|\omega|^{2}}\left((\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}\omega+\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2})(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}\omega+\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2})^{H}\right)+\frac{1}{1+|\omega|^{2}}\left((-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}\overline{\omega})(-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}\overline{\omega})^{H}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+|\omega|^{2}}\big((\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}\omega\overline{\omega}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}^{H}+\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}\omega\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}^{H}+\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}\overline{\omega}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}^{H}+\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}^{H})

(4)

\omega

\displaystyle(1+|\omega|^{2})\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{1}^{H}A_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{1}

(6)

The Joint Numerical Range

Numerical range is an important tool in linear algebra and convex analysis, and it has wide applications in spectrum analysis
rasulov2019description
, quantum computing
dirr2006new
;
rodman2016continuity
, engineering, etc. Joint numerical range was first proposed in 1979 and is an extension of numerical range
au1979remark
, which mainly
focuses on the geometric properties like convexity of the joint field values of several matrices. Joint numerical range also has as wide applications as that of numerical range, and theoretically, it has a close connection to other fundamental results such as
\mathcal{S}
-Lemma, which will be discussed in the next section. On the other hand, people generalized the results of joint numerical ranges in various ways
szymanski2018classification
;
li2009joint
;
brickman1961field
;
ai2011new
. In this section, we show how to further extend the classical results on the convexity of the joint numerical ranges
au1979remark
;
pang2004joint
to the quaternion domain via the rank-one decomposition of quaternion matrices studied in the last section. We start with some basic concepts that will be used later.

Definition 1

Let
A
be any
n\times n
quaternion matrix, the
field\ of\ values
of A is given by

\mathcal{F}(A):=\left\{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\ :\ {\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}=1},\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{H}.

With this concept in hand, we formally define the joint numerical range of a set of matrices as follows.

Definition 2

The
joint\ numerical\ range
of
n\times n
quaternion matrices
A_{1},\cdots,A_{m}
is defined to be

\mathcal{F}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{m}):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\vdots\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{m}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\end{pmatrix}~:~{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}=1},\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\right\}.

Regarding the convexity of the joint numerical ranges, Hausdorff
hausdorff1919wertvorrat
showed that

“ If
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are complex Hermitian, then
\mathcal{F}(A_{1},A_{2})
is a convex set.”

In a slightly different form, Brickman
brickman1961field
extended the above result to three matrices, i.e., suppose
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
are
n\times n
complex Hermitian matrices, then

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{3}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\end{pmatrix}~:~\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\right\}

(20)

is a convex set, where
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the set of complex vectors.

As a matter of fact, the convexity of the joint numerical ranges in complex domain can also be extended to quaternion domain for more matrices. In particular, Au-Yeung and Poon
au1979remark
established the following result for quaternion Hermitian matrices.

Theorem 4.1
(Au-Yeung and Poon
au1979remark
)

If
n\not=2
and
A_{1},\cdots,A_{5}\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
are quaternion hermitian matrices, then

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\vdots\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{5}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\end{pmatrix}~:~\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}=1,\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\right\}

(21)

is a convex set.

Note that the above theorem assumes
n\not=2
and this condition is necessary as
a counterexample is presented in
rodman2014topics
. However, such condition can be relaxed if we consider the slightly different form of (
20
) by Brickman, where the restriction
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}=1
is dropped. We present this result in Theorem
4.2
, which is proved by our quaternion matrix rank-one decomposition theorem.

Theorem 4.2

Suppose that
A_{k}\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
,
k=1,\cdots,5
. Then

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\cdots\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{5}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\end{pmatrix}~:~\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\right\}

(22)

is a convex set.

Proof

To complete the proof, it suffices to show

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\cdots\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{5}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\end{pmatrix}~:~\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\right\}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}\bullet X\\
A_{2}\bullet X\\
\cdots\\
A_{5}\bullet X\end{pmatrix}~:~X\succeq 0\right\}

as the right-hand side is a convex set.
Noting that the right-hand side is the convex hull of the left-hand side, we only need to show the right-hand side is included by the left-hand side.

Take any nonzero vector

\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\\
\cdots\\
v_{5}\end{pmatrix}\in\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}\bullet X\\
A_{2}\bullet X\\
\cdots\\
A_{5}\bullet X\end{pmatrix}~:~X\succeq 0\right\}.

.
Then, by Theorem

for

Since

, there is at least one vector, say
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{1}
such that
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{1}^{H}A_{5}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{1}
shares the same sign as
v_{5}
.

-Procedure)
Let
F(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{\top}A_{0}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}+2\mbox{\boldmath{$b$}}_{0}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}+c_{0}
and
G_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{\top}A_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}+2\mbox{\boldmath{$b$}}_{i}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}+c_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
be quadratic functions of
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
F(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\geq 0
for all
x
such that
G_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\geq 0
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, if there exist
\tau_{i}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}c_{0}&\mbox{\boldmath{$b$}}_{0}^{\top}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$b$}}_{0}&A_{0}\\
\end{array}\right]-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}\tau_{i}\cdot\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}c_{i}&\mbox{\boldmath{$b$}}_{i}^{\top}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$b$}}_{i}&A_{i}\end{array}\right]\succeq 0.

Moreover, if
m=1
then the converse holds if there exists
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}
such that
F(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0})>0
.

We remark that the above result essentially study the relationship between

G_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\geq 0,G_{2}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\geq 0,\cdots,G_{m}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\geq 0\Rightarrow F(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\geq 0,\vskip-4.2679pt

(25)

and

\exists\tau_{1}\geq 0,\tau_{2}\geq 0,\cdots,\tau_{m}\geq 0\ {\rm such\ that}\ F(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau_{i}G_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\geq 0\ \forall\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}.

(26)

It is obvious that (
26
) implies (
25
), and the converse also holds when
m=1
.
In the following, we call
\mathcal{S}
-procedure is
lossless
if (
25
) and (
26
) are equivalent. Although
\mathcal{S}
-procedure was first studied in the real domain, interestingly, a stronger result has been established in the complex domain.

Lemma 2

(Yakubovich
yakubovich1971s
)
Suppose
F,G_{1},G_{2}
are Hermitian forms and satisfy (
25
). Moreover, there is
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
G_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0})>0,i=1,2
. Then the S-procedure is lossless for
m=2
.

Inspired by such result, we further consider
\mathcal{S}
-procedure in the quaternion domain and manage to show that it is
lossless
for
m=4
by Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem 5.1

Suppose
F,G_{1},G_{2},G_{3},G_{4}
are quaternion Hermitian forms and satisfy (
25
). Moreover, there is

\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}~\mbox{such that}~G_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0})>0,~i=1,2,3,4.

(27)

Then the S-procedure is lossless for
m=4
.

Proof

To prove the conclusion, it suffices to establish (
26
) for
m=4
.
Let
F(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{0}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}
and
G_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}
for
i=1,2,3,4
. Consider the following set

\mathbb{D}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{0}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\cdots\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{4}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\end{pmatrix}~:~\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\right\},

which is a convex cone in
\mathbb{R}^{5}
by Theorem
4.2
. Moreover, since
F(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})
and
G_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})
,
i=1,\cdots,4
satisfy (
25
), i.e.,

G_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\geq 0,\cdots,G_{4}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\geq 0\Rightarrow F(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\geq 0,

we have
\mathbb{D}\cap\mathbb{E}=\emptyset
, where
\mathbb{E}=\{(y_{0},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})|~y_{0}<0,~y_{i}>0,~i=1,2,3,4\}
. Noting that
\mathbb{E}
is also a convex cone, the standard separating hyperplane theorem
boyd2004convex
implies that there exists
\mbox{\boldmath{$t$}}=(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4})\not=0
such that

\sum_{k=0}^{4}t_{k}y_{k}\leq 0~{\rm for~all}~(y_{0},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})\in\mathbb{E}

(28)

and

\sum_{k=0}^{4}t_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\geq 0~{\rm for~all}~\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}.

(29)

Then we must have
t_{0}\geq 0
and
t_{k}\leq 0
for
k=1,2,3,4
by inequality (
28
). Moreover, we can confirm that
t_{0}>0
, otherwise since
\mbox{\boldmath{$t$}}\neq 0
there exists some
k\in\{1,2,3,4\}
such that
t_{k}<0
and thus for
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}
in (
27
) it holds that
\sum_{k=0}^{4}t_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}^{H}A_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{4}t_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}^{H}A_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}<0
, which contradicts with (
29
). Finally, the validness of (
26
) with
m=4
is implied by (
29
) by
dividing both sides of (
29
) by
t_{0}
and letting
\tau_{k}=\frac{-t_{k}}{t_{0}}\geq 0
for
k=1,2,3,4
.

6
Quaternion Quadratically Constrained Quadratic Optimization

In this section, we consider the following quadratically constrained quadratic programming in the quaternion domain:

\displaystyle{\rm(QCQP)}\quad\max

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}Q\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}+2{\rm Re}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}q)

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}A_{j}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}+2{\rm Re}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}b_{j})+c_{j}\leq 0,\ j=1,\cdots,m,

where
Q,A_{j}\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
,
b_{j},q\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
,
c_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
j=1,\cdots,m
. Normally, solving the above problem is very challenging. However, when the number of constraints
m
is small, the problem is possibly tractable. For instance, in the complex domain, Huang and Zhang
huang2007complex
showed that the problem (QCQP) with
m=2
could be cast as an SDP and thus could be solved in polynomial time. While regarding the quaternion domain, we shall show that a similar result holds for a larger value of
m
(in particular for
m=4
) by the afore-mentioned rank-one decomposition in Theorem
3.1
.

To relate this problem to SDP, we rewrite (QCQP) as the following matrix form:

\displaystyle\quad\max

\displaystyle B_{0}\bullet\begin{bmatrix}1&\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}&\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(30)

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}

\displaystyle B_{j}\bullet\begin{bmatrix}1&\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}&\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\\
\end{bmatrix}\leq 0,~j=1,\cdots,m,

where
B_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0&q^{H}\\
q&Q\\
\end{bmatrix},\ B_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{j}&b_{j}^{H}\\
b_{j}&A_{j}\\
\end{bmatrix},~j=1,\cdots,m
. Problem (
30
) can be homogenized by introducing a new variable
t
and requiring
|t|^{2}=1
:

\displaystyle{\rm(HQCQP)}\quad\max

\displaystyle B_{0}\bullet\begin{bmatrix}|t|^{2}&t\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\cdot\bar{t}&\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}

\displaystyle B_{j}\bullet\begin{bmatrix}|t|^{2}&t\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\cdot\bar{t}&\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\\
\end{bmatrix}\leq 0,~j=1,\cdots,m,

\displaystyle B_{m+1}\bullet\begin{bmatrix}|t|^{2}&t\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\cdot\bar{t}&\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\\
\end{bmatrix}=1,

where
B_{m+1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}
. Then for any solution
\begin{pmatrix}t\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\\
\end{pmatrix}
of (HQCQP), since
|t|^{2}=1
,
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\cdot\bar{t}
is a solution to (
30
) and hence a solution to (QCQP). Therefore, we shall work on (HQCQP) in the rest of this section. By letting

X=\begin{bmatrix}|t|^{2}&t\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\cdot\bar{t}&\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}^{H}\\
\end{bmatrix}~\mbox{and dropping the constraint:}~\mbox{\rm rank}\,(X)=1,

we obtain an SDP relaxation of (HQCQP):

\displaystyle{\rm(QCQPR)}\quad\max

\displaystyle B_{0}\bullet X

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}

\displaystyle B_{j}\bullet X\leq 0,~j=1,\cdots,m,

\displaystyle B_{m+1}\bullet X=1,

\displaystyle X\succeq 0,

whose dual is given by

\displaystyle{\rm(DQCQPR)}\quad\min

\displaystyle y_{0}

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}

\displaystyle Y=\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_{j}B_{j}-B_{0}+y_{0}B_{m+1}\succeq 0,

\displaystyle y_{j}\geq 0,~j=1,\cdots,m,~y_{0}\ {\rm free}.

To proceed, we assume (QCQP) satisfies the Slater condition, that is, there exists
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
such that

q_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}):=B_{j}\bullet\begin{bmatrix}1&\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}^{H}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}&\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{0}^{H}\\
\end{bmatrix}<0,j=1,\cdots,m.

(31)

Accordingly, (QCQPR) satisfies the Slater condition as well. Now we are ready to present the main theorem of this section, which states that (QCQP) is essentially equivalent to an SDP when
m=4
.

Theorem 6.1

Suppose (QCQP) satisfies the Slater condition (
31
) and
m=4
. Then (QCQP) and (QCQPR) have the same optimal value. Moreover, an optimal solution to (QCQP) can be constructed from that of (QCQPR).

Proof

Denote ‘
\trianglelefteq
’ to be either ‘
<
’ or ‘=’. For the primal optimal solution
X^{*}
, the first group of constraints in (QCQPR) can be rewritten as

B_{j}\bullet X^{*}\trianglelefteq_{j}0,~j=1,2,3,4.

Letting
r=\mbox{\rm rank}\,X^{*}
,
by Theorem
3.1
, there exist some non-zero quaternion vectors
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}t_{k}\\
{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{k}\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1},~k=1,\cdots,r
, such that

X^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{k}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{k}^{H}\ {\rm~and~}\ B_{j}\bullet\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{k}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{k}^{H}\trianglelefteq_{j}0,~\mbox{for}~\ j=1,2,3,4,\ k=1,\cdots,r.

(32)

Since
B_{m+1}\bullet X^{*}=1
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{r}|t_{k}|^{2}=X^{*}_{11}=1
and there exits
\ell\in\left\{1,\cdots,r\right\}
such that
t_{\ell}\not=0
.
Therefore, according to (
32
), it holds that

\displaystyle q_{j}\left({\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{t_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{j}\bullet\begin{bmatrix}1&\left({\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{t_{\ell}}\right)^{H}\\
\left({\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{t_{\ell}}\right)&\left({\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{t_{\ell}}\right)\left({\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{t_{\ell}}\right)^{H}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|t_{\ell}|^{2}}\cdot B_{j}\bullet\begin{bmatrix}|t_{\ell}|^{2}&{t_{\ell}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}^{H}\\
{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\cdot\bar{t_{\ell}}&{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}^{H}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|t_{\ell}|^{2}}B_{j}\bullet\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}^{H}\trianglelefteq_{j}0,j=1,2,3,4,

which further implies
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{t_{\ell}}
is a feasible solution to (QCQP).

Suppose
(y_{0}^{*},y_{1}^{*},y_{2}^{*},y_{3}^{*},y_{4}^{*},Y^{*})
is a dual optimal solution. Since the Slater condition holds, the strong duality is valid between (QCQPR) and (DQCQPR). Consequently, the complementary slackness condition holds, i.e.,

\sum_{i=1}^{r}Y^{*}\bullet\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{i}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{i}^{H}=Y^{*}\bullet X^{*}=0,~\mbox{and}~y_{j}^{*}\cdot(B_{j}\bullet X^{*})=0~{\rm for}~j=1,2,3,4.

Moreover, since
X^{*}\succeq 0
and
Y^{*}\succeq 0
, we have
Y^{*}\bullet\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}^{H}=0
. We also observe from (
32
) that

B_{j}\bullet\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}^{H}\trianglelefteq_{j}0\Leftrightarrow B_{j}\bullet X^{*}\trianglelefteq_{j}0,~\mbox{for}~\ j=1,2,3,4,

which indicates that

y_{j}^{*}\cdot\left(B_{j}\bullet\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}^{H}\right)=y_{j}^{*}\cdot(B_{j}\bullet X^{*})=0~{\rm for}~j=1,2,3,4.

Combining the above results with
\begin{pmatrix}1\\
{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{t_{\ell}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{t_{\ell}}\\
\end{pmatrix}^{H}=\frac{1}{|t_{\ell}|^{2}}\cdot\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}^{H}
yields that
\begin{pmatrix}1\\
{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{t_{\ell}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{t_{\ell}}\\
\end{pmatrix}^{H}
is complementary to
(y_{0}^{*},y_{1}^{*},y_{2}^{*},y_{3}^{*},y_{4}^{*},Y^{*})
, and it is an optimal solution to (QCQPR). Therefore
{\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}}_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{t_{\ell}}
is optimal for (QCQP) and the conclusion follows.
