Abstract
. For any nonconstant f , g ∈ ℂ ⁡ ( x ) f,g\in\mathbb{C}(x) such that the numerator H ⁡ ( x , y ) H(x,y) of f ⁡ ( x ) − g ⁡ ( y ) f(x)-g(y) is irreducible, we compute the genus of the normalization of the curve H ⁡ ( x , y ) = 0 H(x,y)=0 . We also prove an analogous formula in arbitrary characteristic when f f and g g have no common wildly ramified branch points, and generalize to (possibly reducible) fiber products of nonconstant morphisms of curves f : A → D f\colon A\to D and g : B → D g\colon B\to D .

Mathematicians studying several different topics have been led to investigate the genus of algebraic
curves of the form
f(x)=g(y)
. For instance, such considerations arise in algebraic topology

[

]
, complex dynamics
[
25
]
, value distribution of meromorphic functions
[
4
,
16
,
17
,
24
]
,
Diophantine equations
[
2
,

,

,
6
,

]
, arithmetic dynamics
[

,

]
, Pell equations
in polynomials
[

,
18
]
, functional equations in rational functions
[
11
,
19
,
20
,
26
]
, and coding
theory
[
21
]
. This has led many authors to compute the genus of some specific instance of such
curves.

Besides specific instances, mathematicians have produced two general types of formulas for this genus.
Ritt
[
20
]
essentially determined the genus of irreducible curves of the form
f(x)=g(y)
when
f(x)
and
g(x)

are nonconstant complex rational functions. His method uses the Riemann–Hurwitz formula for the projection
of this curve onto the
x
-axis, and relies on computing the ramification in this projection. By
examining singularities and using the Plücker formula, Kang
[
15
]
proved a genus formula for irreducible curves of the form
f(x)=g(y)
where
f(x)
and
g(x)
are nonconstant polynomials
over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. The genus formulas
obtained by Ritt and Kang have different forms, and are not obviously equivalent.

In this paper we prove new results generalizing these known formulas in several ways:

•

We do not assume that
f(x)=g(y)
is irreducible, but instead obtain a sum over its irreducible
components.

•

Our formulas are valid in positive characteristic, under some hypotheses about wild ramification.

•

We do not require
f
and
g
to be polynomials (as did Kang) or rational functions (as did Ritt), but instead allow them to be arbitrary curve morphisms with a common target.

Our approach uses ramification, and in particular the computation of points in fibered products from

[
10
]
, but it differs from Ritt’s approach in several ways. In the end we obtain two formulas:
one generalizing Ritt’s which is valid when at least one of
f
and
g
is tamely ramified, and one generalizing
Kang’s in the more general situation where
f
and
g
do not have a common wildly ramified branch point.

Our main result uses the following standard notation and terminology, where
f\colon A\to D
is a nonconstant morphism between smooth projective irreducible curves over an algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic
p\geq 0
:

•

A(k)
is the set of points on
A
with coordinates in
k

•

for any

, we write
e_{f}(P)
for the ramification index of
P
under

•

we say that

is tamely ramified (resp., wildly ramified) under

if
p\nmid e_{f}(P)
(resp.,
p\mid e_{f}(P)
), and that
f
is tamely ramified if every point in
A(k)
is tamely ramified under
f
; in particular, if
p=0
then
f
is tamely ramified

•

a branch point of
f
is a point in
D(k)
of the form
f(P)
where

•

a branch point of

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f\colon A\to D
and

g\colon B\to D
be nonconstant morphisms of smooth projective irreducible curves over an algebraically closed field
k
. Write
m

and
n
for the degrees of
f
and
g
, and write
\mathfrak{g}_{A},\mathfrak{g}_{B},\mathfrak{g}_{D}
for the genera of
A,B,D
. Let

C_{1},\dots,C_{r}
be the normalizations of the irreducible components of the fiber product of
f
and

g
, and write
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
for the genus of
C_{i}
.

(1.1.1)

If
f
is tamely ramified then

\sum_{i=1}^{r}(2\mathfrak{g}_{i}-2)=m(2\mathfrak{g}_{B}-2)+\sum_{R\in D(k)}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in f^{-1}(R)\\
Q\in g^{-1}(R)\end{subarray}}\bigl(e_{f}(P)-\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)\bigr).

(1.1.2)

If
f
and
g
have no common wildly ramified branch points then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}(2\mathfrak{g}_{i}-2)=m(2\mathfrak{g}_{B}-2)+n(2\mathfrak{g}_{A}-2)-mn(2\mathfrak{g}_{D}-2)\,-

\displaystyle\quad\,\,\,\,\!\sum_{R\in D(k)}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in f^{-1}(R)\\
Q\in g^{-1}(R)\end{subarray}}\bigl((e_{f}(P)-1)\cdot(e_{g}(Q)-1)+\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)-1\bigr).

Remark 1.2
.

In case
g
is the identity map on
D
, the formula in (
.1
) is the classical Riemann–Hurwitz
formula for tamely ramified morphisms. For more general
g
, the formulas in (
.1
) and (
.2
)
may be viewed as “relative” Riemann–Hurwitz formulas.

Remark 1.3
.

The summations in the above result only have finitely many nonzero summands, since the summation in the right side of (
.1
) is zero whenever
e_{f}(P)=1
, and the summation in the right side of (
.2
) is zero when either
e_{f}(P)=1
or
e_{g}(Q)=1
.

In order to make it easy to apply Theorem
1.1
, we now state some special cases of this result. We
begin with the case of rational functions.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic
p\geq 0
, and let
f,g\in k(x)\setminus k
have
degrees
m
and
n
, respectively. Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{r}
be the normalizations of the irreducible components
of
f(x)=g(y)
, and write
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
for the genus of
C_{i}
.

(1.4.1)

Suppose that
p\nmid e_{f}(P)
for each
P\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
.
Let
\Gamma
be the
set of pairs
(P,Q)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)\times\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
such that
f(P)=g(Q)
and
e_{f}(P)>1
. Then
\Gamma

is finite, say
\Gamma=\{(P_{j},Q_{j})\colon 1\leq j\leq s\}
with
s=\lvert\Gamma\rvert
;
writing
m_{j}:=e_{f}(P_{j})
and
n_{j}:=e_{g}(Q_{j})
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{r}(2\mathfrak{g}_{i}-2)=-2m+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\Bigl(m_{j}-\gcd(m_{j},n_{j})\Bigr).

(1.4.2)

Let
\Lambda
be the set of pairs
(P,Q)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)\times\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
such that

f(P)=g(Q)
and
e_{f}(P)>1
and
e_{g}(Q)>1
. Suppose
\Lambda
does not contain a pair of integers divisible
by
p
. Then
\Lambda
is finite, say
\Lambda=\{(P_{j},Q_{j})\colon 1\leq j\leq t\}
with
t=\lvert\Lambda\rvert
;
writing
m_{j}:=e_{f}(P_{j})
and
n_{j}:=e_{g}(Q_{j})
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}(2\mathfrak{g}_{i}-2)

\displaystyle=-2+2(m-1)(n-1)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{j=1}^{t}\Bigl((m_{j}-1)(n_{j}-1)+\gcd(m_{j},n_{j})-1\Bigr).

Remark 1.5
.

For the benefit of those whose background is analysis rather than algebraic geometry, we note the following alternate description of the
C_{i}
’s in case
k=\mathbb{C}
: if we write the numerator of the bivariate rational function
f(x)-g(y)
as the product
\prod_{i=1}^{r}H_{i}(x,y)
of irreducible polynomials

, then
C_{i}
is the unique compact Riemann surface (up to conformal equivalence) for which there exist a finite subset
S_{i}
of
C_{i}
, and a finite subset

of the set

of zeroes of

in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, such that
C_{i}\setminus S_{i}
is biholomorphic to
U_{i}\setminus T_{i}
.

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{j=1}^{t}\Bigl((m_{j}-1)(n_{j}-1)+\gcd(m_{j},n_{j})-1\Bigr).

•

We begin with the Riemann–Hurwitz formula

If

2\mathfrak{g}_{C}-2=\deg(f)\cdot(2\mathfrak{g}_{D}-2)+\sum_{P\in C(k)}d_{f}(P).

The ramification index
[
23
, Def. 3.1.5]
and different exponent
[
23

are related by Dedekind’s different theorem, e.g. cf.
[

Lemma 2.2
.

If

is a separable nonconstant morphism of curves over

, then for any

we have

, with equality holding if and only if

.

The next result describes the ramification index and different exponent in a tower of curves

[

, Prop. 3.1.6 and Cor. 3.4.12]
:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f\colon C\to D
and

be nonconstant morphisms of curves over

. Then for any
P\in C(k)
we have

e_{g\circ f}(P)=e_{f}(P)\cdot e_{g}(f(P)).

If in addition both

and
g
are separable then

d_{g\circ f}(P)=e_{f}(P)\cdot d_{g}(f(P))+d_{f}(P).

We will use the following Fundamental Equality
[
23
, Thm. 3.1.11]
:

Lemma 2.4
.

If
f\colon C\to D
is a nonconstant morphism of curves over

, then for any

we have

\deg(f)=\sum_{P\in f^{-1}(Q)}e_{f}(P).

We will also use the following version of Abhyankar’s lemma
[

, Thm. 3.9.1 and Prop. 3.10.2]

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
f\colon A\to D
and

be nonconstant morphisms of curves over
k
, and let

be the normalization
of a component of the fiber product of

and

, with induced maps
\phi\colon C\to A

and
\psi\colon C\to B
. For any

such that
\phi(S)
is tamely ramified under

e_{f\circ\phi}(S)=\lcm\bigl(e_{f}(\phi(S)),e_{g}(\psi(S))\bigr).

]

.

3.
Proofs of main results

Corollaries
1.4
and
1.6
follow immediately from Theorem
1.1
, so we need only prove the latter
result. Throughout this section we assume the hypotheses and notation from Theorem
1.1
. For
i\in\{1,2,\dots,r\}
,
let
\phi_{i}\colon C_{i}\to A
and
\psi_{i}\colon C_{i}\to B
be the maps arising from the fibered product. We begin by combining
the Riemann–Hurwitz formula for the various maps
\psi_{i}
into a single formula. We express this combined formula in terms
of a summation whose summands will turn out to be convenient for explicit computation in the setting of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
f
is separable then

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}(2\mathfrak{g}_{i}-2)

\displaystyle=m(2\mathfrak{g}_{B}-2)+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{S\in C_{i}(k)}d_{\psi_{i}}(S)

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=m(2\mathfrak{g}_{B}-2)+n(2g_{A}-2)-mn(2\mathfrak{g}_{D}-2)\\
&\qquad-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{S\in C_{i}(k)}\Bigl(e_{\phi_{i}}(S)\cdot d_{f}(\phi_{i}(S))-d_{\psi_{i}}(S)\Bigr).\end{split}

If both
f
and
g
are separable then each
S\in C_{i}(k)
satisfies

(3.4)

e_{\phi_{i}}(S)\cdot d_{f}(\phi_{i}(S))-d_{\psi_{i}}(S)=d_{f\circ\phi_{i}}(S)-d_{\phi_{i}}(S)-d_{\psi_{i}}(S),

which yields the symmetric equation

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}(2\mathfrak{g}_{i}-2)

\displaystyle=m(2\mathfrak{g}_{B}-2)+n(2g_{A}-2)-mn(2\mathfrak{g}_{D}-2)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{S\in C_{i}(k)}\Bigl(d_{f\circ\phi_{i}}(S)-d_{\phi_{i}}(S)-d_{\psi_{i}}(S)\Bigr).

Proof.

Since
f
is separable, also each
\psi_{i}
is separable. Riemann–Hurwitz for
\psi_{i}
says that

2\mathfrak{g}_{i}-2=\deg(\psi_{i})\cdot(2\mathfrak{g}_{B}-2)+\sum_{S\in C_{i}(k)}d_{\psi_{i}}(S).

By summing over all
i
, and using the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\deg(\psi_{i})=m
, this yields (
3.2
).
Riemann–Hurwitz for the map
f\colon A\to D
says that

(3.6)

2\mathfrak{g}_{A}-2=m(2\mathfrak{g}_{D}-2)+\sum_{P\in A(k)}d_{f}(P).

For each
P\in A(k)
, Lemma
2.4
yields

(3.7)

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{S\in\phi_{i}^{-1}(P)}e_{\phi_{i}}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\deg(\phi_{i})=n.

Multiply (
3.6
) by
n
, and then substitute (
3.7
), to get

\displaystyle n(2\mathfrak{g}_{A}-2)-mn(2\mathfrak{g}_{D}-2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in A(k)}nd_{f}(P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in A(k)}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{S\in\phi_{i}^{-1}(P)}e_{\phi_{i}}(S)\cdot d_{f}(P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{S\in C_{i}(k)}e_{\phi_{i}}(S)\cdot d_{f}(\phi_{i}(S)).

Upon subtracting the right side of this equation from the left, and adding the difference to the right side of (
3.2
), we obtain (
3.3
).

If both
f
and
g
are separable then also each
\phi_{i}
is separable, so by Lemma
2.3
we have

e_{\phi_{i}}(S)\cdot d_{f}(\phi_{i}(S))=d_{f\circ\phi_{i}}(S)-d_{\phi_{i}}(S)

for each
S\in C_{i}(k)
. Subtracting
d_{\psi_{i}}(S)
from both sides yields (
3.4
), and combining (
3.3
) with (
3.4
) yields (
3.5
).
∎

In light of Lemma
3.1
, in order to prove Theorem
1.1
we must compute the summands involving different exponents in the right sides of (
3.2
), (
3.3
) and (
3.5
). We do this in the next result, under suitable hypotheses about tame ramification.

Lemma 3.8
.

Pick
S\in C_{i}(k)
for some
i
, and put
P:=\phi_{i}(S)
and
Q:=\psi_{i}(S)
. If
P
is tamely ramified under
f
then

(3.9)

d_{\psi_{i}}(S)=\frac{e_{f}(P)}{\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)}-1

and

(3.10)

\displaystyle\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)\cdot\Bigl(e_{\phi_{i}}(S)\cdot d_{f}(P)-d_{\psi_{i}}(S)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=(e_{f}(P)-1)\cdot(e_{g}(Q)-1)+\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)-1.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
we have

e_{f\circ\phi_{i}}(S)=\lcm\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)=\frac{e_{f}(P)\cdot e_{g}(Q)}{\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)}.

Lemma
2.3
yields

e_{\phi_{i}}(S)\cdot e_{f}(P)=e_{f\circ\phi_{i}}(S)=e_{g\circ\psi_{i}}(S)=e_{\psi_{i}}(S)\cdot e_{g}(Q),

so that

e_{\phi_{i}}(S)=\frac{e_{g}(Q)}{\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)}\quad\text{ and }\quad e_{\psi_{i}}(S)=\frac{e_{f}(P)}{\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)}.

In particular,
S
is tamely ramified under
\psi_{i}
, so Lemma
2.2
implies that

d_{\psi_{i}}(S)=e_{\psi_{i}}(S)-1=\frac{e_{f}(P)}{\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)}-1,

which is (
3.9
). Lemma
2.2
also gives
d_{f}(P)=e_{f}(P)-1
, so that

\displaystyle\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)\cdot\Bigl(e_{\phi_{i}}(S)\cdot d_{f}(P)-d_{\psi_{i}}(S)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=e_{g}(Q)\cdot(e_{f}(P)-1)-\Bigl(e_{f}(P)-\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=(e_{g}(Q)-1)\cdot(e_{f}(P)-1)+\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)-1,

which is (
3.10
).
∎

.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

If
f
is tamely ramified then in particular
f
is separable, so that (
3.2
) yields

\sum_{i=1}^{r}(2\mathfrak{g}_{i}-2)=m(2\mathfrak{g}_{B}-2)+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{S\in C_{i}(k)}d_{\psi_{i}}(S).

By (
3.9
) the right side equals

\displaystyle m(2\mathfrak{g}_{B}-2)+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{S\in C_{i}(k)}\Bigl(\frac{e_{f}(\phi_{i}(S))}{\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(\phi_{i}(S)),e_{g}(\psi_{i}(S))\bigr)}-1\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=m(2\mathfrak{g}_{B}-2)+\sum_{R\in D(k)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in f^{-1}(R)\\
Q\in g^{-1}(R)\end{subarray}}\Bigl(\frac{e_{f}(P)}{\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)}-1\Bigr)\cdot N_{P,Q}

where

N_{P,Q}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lvert\phi_{i}^{-1}(P)\cap\psi_{i}^{-1}(Q)\rvert

for all
P\in A(k)
and
Q\in B(k)
satisfying
f(P)=g(Q)
.
Substituting the value
N_{P,Q}=\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)
from Lemma
2.6
yields (
.1
).

Likewise, if
g
is tamely ramified then the combination of (
3.3
), (
3.10
) and Lemma
2.6
yields the conclusion of (
.2
). Since the conclusion of (
.2
) is symmetric in
f
and
g
, it follows that this conclusion is also true if
g
is tamely ramified.

It remains to prove (
.2
) when neither
f
nor
g
is tamely ramified.
Thus
f
has a wildly ramified branch point, which by the hypothesis of (
.2
) is not a wildly ramified branch point of
g
, so that
g
is separable. Likewise
f
is separable. For any
i
and any
S\in C_{i}(k)
, put
P:=\phi_{i}(S)
and
Q:=\psi_{i}(S)
. Since
f(P)=g(Q)
but
f
and
g
have no common wildly ramified branch points, at least one of
e_{f}(P)
and
e_{g}(Q)
is not divisible by
p
. If
p\nmid e_{f}(P)
then (
3.4
) and (
3.10
) yield

\displaystyle d_{f\circ\phi_{i}}(S)-d_{\phi_{i}}(S)-d_{\psi_{i}}(S)=e_{\phi_{i}}(S)\cdot d_{f}(P)-d_{\psi_{i}}(S)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{(e_{f}(P)-1)\cdot(e_{g}(Q)-1)+\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)-1}{\gcd\bigl(e_{f}(P),e_{g}(Q)\bigr)}.

If
p\nmid e_{g}(Q)
then the same argument yields the same conclusion. Thus the above equation is true in any case. Combining it with
(
3.5
) and Lemma
2.6
yields (
.2
).
∎
