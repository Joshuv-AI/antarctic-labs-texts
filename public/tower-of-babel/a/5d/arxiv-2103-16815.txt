Beta-decay formulas revisited (I): Gamow–Teller and spin-dipole contributions to allowed and first-forbidden transitions
Wataru Horiuchi, Toru Sato, Yuichi Uesaka, Kenichi Yoshida

Abstract
We propose formulas of the nuclear beta-decay rate that are useful in a practical calculation. The decay rate is determined by the product of the lepton and hadron current densities. A widely used formula relies upon the fact that the low-energy lepton wave functions in a nucleus can be well approximated by a constant and linear to the radius for the s s -wave and p p -wave wave functions, respectively. We find, however, the deviation from such a simple approximation is evident for heavy nuclei with large Z Z by numerically solving the Dirac equation. In our proposed formulas, the neutrino wave function is treated exactly as a plane wave, while the electron wave function is obtained by iteratively solving the integral equation, thus we can control the uncertainty of the approximate wave function. The leading-order approximation gives a formula equivalent to the conventional one and overestimates the decay rate. We demonstrate that the next-to-leading-order formula reproduces well the exact result for a schematic transition density as well as a microscopic one obtained by a nuclear energy-density functional method.

Introduction

The physics of exotic nuclei away from the stability line has been a major subject in nuclear physics.
The lifetime of neutron-rich nuclei is governed by beta decay.
Since the beta decay determines the time scale of the rapid-neutron-capture process (
r
-process)
and the production of heavy elements
together with the beta-delayed neutron(s) emission,
the beta-decay rates of exotic nuclei are an important microscopic input for the simulation of nucleosynthesis
[
1
]
.
The multi-messenger observations from a binary neutron star merger
[
2
,
3
]
imply that
heavy neutron-rich nuclei that are even close to the drip line are involved in the
r
-process.
Thus, the Coulomb effect on the beta particle (emitted electron) should be carefully examined under
the extreme environment where
the
Q
value for the beta decay,
Q_{\beta}
, is high and the nuclear charge
Z
is large.

A careful analysis of the Coulomb effect is also useful
for a precision test
of the standard model to find a signal of
new physics.
For example, the effect on spectra of the beta particle
and angular correlation as well as beta-decay rates
has been studied to test the unitarity of the
Cabibbo–Kobayashi–Maskawa (CKM) matrix,
the scalar and tensor interactions, and the effect of neutrino mass in the allowed and
first-forbidden transitions

[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

The formulation of nuclear beta decay
within the distorted-wave impulse approximation of the electron

[

,

,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
.
The crucial part is how to handle the electron Coulomb wave function
with a potential
of the finite-size nuclear-charge distribution.
Using the Maclaurin expansion of the nuclear radius
r
, the exact
electron wave function
was included in Ref.
[
16
]
.
An iterative solution of the integral equation was found to have
a better convergence
by Behrens and Bühling
[

,

The formula is arranged
in the order of

, where

,

and

represent the Coulomb potential, the energy and the mass of an electron, respectively.
It has been widely used in the calculations
such as in Refs.
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
and in the recent
application to the

-process nuclei

[

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

.
In most of those works, however,
the leading-order approximation of the formula
in Refs.
[
10
,
12
]
is adopted.
Instead of expanding the lepton wave functions,
one can incorporate the numerical solution of the charged lepton wave functions
thanks to the advance of the computational ability.
The muon capture
[

]

and the beta decay
[
15
]
are
formulated suitable for this purpose.
In this formulation, the nuclear matrix element is defined in a transparent way
and
appears similarly in Refs.
[

for the semi-leptonic nuclear processes and
electron scattering
[

straightforward to apply it to the charged-current neutrino reaction
and lepton capture reaction. Developing an analytic formula of beta decay
based on Ref.
[

would also contribute
to a precise understanding of the neutrino-nucleus reactions
to extract neutrino properties from neutrino experiments
as discussed in Ref.
[
37
,

]
.

The high-energy forbidden transitions occur
under the exotic environment with high

value
[
39
]
.
Therefore, in this work we revisit the formulation of beta decay
for not only the
allowed but the first-forbidden transitions induced by the Gamow–Teller and spin-dipole
type operators.
We provide a simple way to improve the widely used formula in the
nuclear beta-decay study to apply for nuclei with
large
Z

We start from the formulation of
Koshigiri
et al
.
[
15
]
and use iterative solutions of the integral

[
17
,
10
]

In the previous formalism, one often expands the electron
and neutrino wave
functions in the long wave-length approximation and collect

Here we avoid this expansion of the neutrino wave function.
We use an analytic form of the
LO and NLO electron wave functions combined with the numerical table of the
electron wave function at the origin.
This makes the formula of the beta-decay rate simple and easy to use.

This paper is organized in the following way.

The formulas of LO and NLO are examined in Sect.

Summary and perspectives are given finally in Sect.

2
Formalism

2.1
Effective Hamiltonian

An effective Hamiltonian for a low-energy charged-current reaction
is given as

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{G_{F}V_{ud}}{\sqrt{2}}\int d\bm{x}[\bar{\ell}(\bm{x})\gamma^{\mu}(1-\gamma_{5})\nu_{\ell}(\bm{x})J_{\mu}(\bm{x})+\bar{\nu}_{\ell}(\bm{x})\gamma^{\mu}(1-\gamma_{5})\ell(\bm{x})J_{\mu}^{\dagger}(\bm{x})],

(1)

\ell(\bm{x})

\bar{\psi}=\psi^{\dagger}\gamma^{0}

The hadron current
J^{\mu}(\bm{x})
is given by
the vector and axial vector currents

\displaystyle J^{\mu}(\bm{x})=V^{\mu}(\bm{x})-A^{\mu}(\bm{x}),

(2)

where
G_{\mathrm{F}}=1.166\times 10^{-5}\mathrm{GeV}^{-2}

is the Fermi coupling constant and
V_{ud}=0.9737
is
the CKM matrix
[
40
]
. Here we take natural units

\hbar=c=1
.

The effective Hamiltonian describes semi-leptonic nuclear weak processes
such as lepton capture, neutrino reaction,
and
\beta^{\pm}
decay.
For
\beta^{-}
decay,

i\rightarrow e^{-}(p_{e})+\bar{\nu}_{e}(p_{\nu})+f
,
where
i
and
f
respectively denote
the initial and final nuclear states,
and
p_{\ell}
is the lepton momentum,
the transition matrix element is given as

\displaystyle\left<e^{-}(p_{e})\bar{\nu}_{e}(p_{\nu})f\right|H_{\mathrm{eff}}\left|i\right>=\frac{G_{F}V_{ud}}{\sqrt{2}}\int d\bm{x}\bar{\psi}_{e^{-},p_{e},s_{e}}^{(-)}(\bm{x})\gamma^{\mu}(1-\gamma_{5})v_{s_{\nu}}(\bm{p}_{\nu})e^{-i\bm{p}_{\nu}\cdot\bm{x}}\left<f\right|J_{\mu}(\bm{x})\left|i\right>,

(3)

and for
\beta^{+}
decay

\displaystyle\left<e^{+}(p_{e})\nu_{e}(p_{\nu})f\right|H_{\mathrm{eff}}\left|i\right>=\frac{G_{F}V_{ud}}{\sqrt{2}}\int d\bm{x}\bar{u}_{s_{\nu}}(\bm{p}_{\nu})e^{-i\bm{p}_{\nu}\cdot\bm{x}}\gamma^{\mu}(1-\gamma_{5})\psi_{e^{+},p_{e},s_{e}}^{(+)}(\bm{x})\left<f\right|J_{\mu}^{\dagger}(\bm{x})\left|i\right>,

(4)

where
u
and
v
are
the Dirac spinors of the neutrino and antineutrino, respectively.
The electron scattering wave functions
\psi

with the superscript
(-)
and
(+)

satisfy the incoming and outgoing boundary conditions, respectively.

2.2
Multipole Expansion of the Effective Hamiltonian

The standard formulation of the

We use the following electron

\displaystyle\psi_{e,p_{e},s_{e}}^{(\mp)}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\kappa_{e},m_{e},\mu_{e}}(4\pi)i^{l_{\kappa_{e}}}(l_{\kappa_{e}},m_{e},1/2,s_{e}|j_{\kappa_{e}},\mu_{e})Y^{*}_{l_{\kappa_{e}},m_{e}}(\hat{p}_{e})e^{\mp i\Delta_{\kappa_{e}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}G_{\kappa_{e}}(r)\chi_{\kappa_{e}}^{\mu_{e}}\\
iF_{\kappa_{e}}(r)\chi_{-\kappa_{e}}^{\mu_{e}}\end{array}\right).

(j_{1},m_{1},j_{2},m_{2}|J,M)

[
41
,
42

43
]

For positron,

are calculated
by replacing

The normalization of the scattering wave function
in the plane wave expansion

\psi_{e}(\bm{x})\rightarrow u(p_{e})\exp(i\bm{p}_{e}\cdot\bm{x})

is given as

\displaystyle u_{s_{e}}(p_{e})=\sqrt{\frac{E_{e}+m_{e}}{2E_{e}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
\frac{\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{p}_{e}}{E_{e}+m_{e}}\end{array}\right)\chi_{s_{e}}.

It is noticed the electron wave functions
(G_{\kappa},F_{\kappa})

in Ref.
[
15
]
are defined by multiplying

to ours, while
those of Refs.
[
16
,
10
]

are given by multiplying

The neutrino and antineutrino wave functions are respectively expanded as

\displaystyle u_{s_{\nu}}(\bm{p}_{\nu})e^{i\bm{p}_{\nu}\cdot\bm{r}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\kappa_{\nu},m_{\nu},\mu_{\nu}}\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{2}}i^{l_{\kappa_{\nu}}}Y_{l_{\kappa_{\nu}}m_{\nu}}^{*}(\hat{p}_{\nu})(l_{\kappa_{\nu}},m_{\nu},1/2,s_{\nu}|j_{\kappa_{\nu}},\mu_{\nu})\left(\begin{array}[]{c}g_{\kappa_{\nu}}(r)\chi_{\kappa_{\nu}}^{\mu_{\nu}}\\

\displaystyle v_{s_{\nu}}(\bm{p}_{\nu})e^{-i\bm{p}_{\nu}\cdot\bm{r}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\kappa_{\nu},m_{\nu},\mu_{\nu}}\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{2}}i^{-l_{\kappa_{\nu}}}Y_{l_{\kappa_{\nu}}m_{\nu}}^{*}(\hat{p}_{\nu})(l_{\kappa_{\nu}},m_{\nu},1/2,-s_{\nu}|j_{\kappa_{\nu}},\mu_{\nu})(-1)^{1/2-s_{\nu}}

,

With the partial wave expansion of the

]

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{G_{F}V_{ud}}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{(4\pi)^{3}}{2}}\sum_{el}\sum_{neu}\sum_{L,J}(j_{e},-\mu_{e},j_{\nu},\mu_{\nu}|J,M)(-1)^{1/2-\mu_{e}}\Xi_{JLM}(\kappa_{e},\kappa_{\nu}),

3
Electron Coulomb Wave function

3.1
Parametrization of Lepton Wave Function

The general formula given in Eq. (
16
) is ready for the use of
any allowed and forbidden transition rates by evaluating the nuclear
transition density.
However, an explicit formula for the allowed and first-forbidden transitions
helps extract nuclear structure information from the beta-decay
observables.
Since the electron Coulomb wave function is rather involved in evaluating the beta-decay rate,
we briefly describe the derivation of the expression of charged lepton wave functions
by iterating the integral equation following Refs.
[
17
,
10
]
.

A Dirac wave function of an electron is given as

\displaystyle[\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{p}_{e}+\beta m_{e}+V_{C}(r)]\psi_{e}(\bm{r})=E_{e}\psi_{e}(\bm{r}).

(23)

The electron wave functions
G_{\kappa},F_{\kappa}
satisfy
the coupled first-order differential equation with the Coulomb potential
V_{C}
:

\displaystyle\frac{dG_{\kappa}}{dr}+\frac{1+\kappa}{r}G_{\kappa}-(m_{e}+E_{e}-V_{C})F_{\kappa}

\displaystyle=0,

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{dF_{\kappa}}{dr}+\frac{1-\kappa}{r}F_{\kappa}-(m_{e}-E_{e}+V_{C})G_{\kappa}

\displaystyle=0.

(25)

Throughout this paper we keep the electron mass explicit so that in future we can use the formula for the muon neutrino reactions.
Electron wave functions are parametrized
by taking into account the behavior of the wave function
at the origin
r\sim 0

[
10
]
as

\displaystyle G_{-k}(r)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{-k}\frac{(p_{e}r)^{k-1}}{(2k-1)!!}[H_{k}(r)-h_{k}(r)],

(26)

\displaystyle F_{k}(r)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}\frac{(p_{e}r)^{k-1}}{(2k-1)!!}[H_{k}(r)+h_{k}(r)],

(27)

\displaystyle G_{k}(r)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}\frac{(p_{e}r)^{k-1}}{(2k-1)!!}\frac{r}{R}[D_{k}(r)+d_{k}(r)],

(28)

\displaystyle F_{-k}(r)

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{-k}\frac{(p_{e}r)^{k-1}}{(2k-1)!!}\frac{r}{R}[D_{k}(r)-d_{k}(r)].

(29)

Here
k>0
and
H_{k}(0)=1
and
h_{k}(0)=0
.
The normalization of the electron wave functions are determined by
constants
\alpha_{\kappa}
.
This parametrization of
G_{\kappa},F_{\kappa}

incorporates the boundary condition of the wave function at the origin.
Then the following set of coupled integral equations
is obtained:

\displaystyle H_{k}(r)

\displaystyle=1+\int_{0}^{r}\frac{r^{\prime}}{R}[(-E_{e}+V_{C}(r^{\prime}))D_{k}(r^{\prime})+m_{e}d_{k}(r^{\prime})]dr^{\prime},

(30)

\displaystyle h_{k}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\frac{r^{\prime}}{R}[m_{e}D_{k}(r^{\prime})+(-E_{e}+V_{C}(r^{\prime}))d_{k}(r^{\prime})]dr^{\prime},

(31)

\displaystyle\frac{r}{R}D_{k}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\right)^{2k}[(E_{e}-V_{C}(r^{\prime}))H_{k}(r^{\prime})+m_{e}h_{k}(r^{\prime})]dr^{\prime},

(32)

\displaystyle\frac{r}{R}d_{k}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\right)^{2k}[m_{e}H_{k}(r^{\prime})+(E_{e}-V_{C}(r^{\prime}))h_{k}(r^{\prime})]dr^{\prime}.

(33)

At this stage
R
is just a parameter of dimension length. We take

R
as the nuclear radius though the final
formulas are independent
of the choice of
R
.

3.2
Iterative Solution of Integral Equation

Taking into account the boundary condition,
H_{k},h_{k},D_{k}
, and
d_{k}

are expanded
according to the number of iteration as

\displaystyle H_{k}(r)

\displaystyle=1+H_{k}^{(2)}(r)+H_{k}^{(4)}(r)+\cdots,

(34)

\displaystyle h_{k}(r)

\displaystyle=h_{k}^{(2)}(r)+h_{k}^{(4)}(r)+\cdots,

(35)

\displaystyle D_{k}(r)

\displaystyle=D_{k}^{(1)}(r)+D_{k}^{(3)}(r)+\cdots,

(36)

\displaystyle d_{k}(r)

\displaystyle=d_{k}^{(1)}(r)+d_{k}^{(3)}(r)+\cdots.

(37)

The first
iteration of the integral equation gives

\displaystyle\frac{r}{R}D_{k}^{(1)}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\right)^{2k}(E_{e}-V_{C}(r^{\prime}))dr^{\prime},

(38)

\displaystyle\frac{r}{R}d_{k}^{(1)}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\right)^{2k}m_{e}dr^{\prime},

(39)

and further iterations give

\displaystyle H_{k}^{(2n)}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\left[(-E_{e}+V_{C}(r^{\prime}))\frac{r^{\prime}}{R}D_{k}^{(2n-1)}(r^{\prime})+m_{e}\frac{r^{\prime}}{R}d_{k}^{(2n-1)}(r^{\prime})\right]dr^{\prime},

(40)

\displaystyle h_{k}^{(2n)}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\left[m_{e}\frac{r^{\prime}}{R}D_{k}^{(2n-1)}(r^{\prime})+(-E_{e}+V_{C}(r^{\prime}))\frac{r^{\prime}}{R}d_{k}^{(2n-1)}(r^{\prime})\right]dr^{\prime},

(41)

and

\displaystyle\frac{r}{R}D_{k}^{(2n+1)}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\right)^{2k}\left[(E_{e}-V_{C}(r^{\prime}))H_{k}^{(2n)}(r^{\prime})+m_{e}h_{k}^{(2n)}(r^{\prime})\right]dr^{\prime},

(42)

\displaystyle\frac{r}{R}d_{k}^{(2n+1)}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\right)^{2k}\left[m_{e}H_{k}^{(2n)}(r^{\prime})+(E_{e}-V_{C}(r^{\prime}))h_{k}^{(2n)}(r^{\prime})\right]dr^{\prime}

(43)

for
n=1,2,\dots
.
The exact electron wave functions
in terms of
E_{e},m_{e}
, and
V_{C}
are obtained from the
iterative solution of the above equations.

3.3
LO and NLO electron wave functions

We denote the leading order (LO) electron wave function as

\displaystyle H_{k}^{\mathrm{LO}}(r)

\displaystyle=1,

(44)

\displaystyle h_{k}^{\mathrm{LO}}(r)

\displaystyle=0,

(45)

\displaystyle\frac{r}{R}D_{k}^{\mathrm{LO}}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{R}D_{k}^{(1)}(r)=\frac{E_{e}r}{2k+1}+V_{D1}(r),

\displaystyle\frac{r}{R}d_{k}^{\mathrm{LO}}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{R}d_{k}^{(1)}(r)=\frac{m_{e}r}{2k+1}

\displaystyle V_{D1}(r)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\right)^{2k}V_{C}(r^{\prime})dr^{\prime}.

(48)

Adding the next-to-leading order (NLO), the NLO wave function is given as

\displaystyle H_{k}^{\mathrm{NLO}}(r)

\displaystyle=1+H_{k}^{(2)}(r),

(49)

\displaystyle h_{k}^{\mathrm{NLO}}(r)

\displaystyle=h_{k}^{(2)}(r),

(50)

\displaystyle\frac{r}{R}D_{k}^{\mathrm{NLO}}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{R}(D_{k}^{(1)}(r)+D_{k}^{(3)}(r)),

(51)

\displaystyle\frac{r}{R}d_{k}^{\mathrm{NLO}}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{R}(d_{k}^{(1)}(r)+d_{k}^{(3)}(r)),

where

\displaystyle H_{k}^{(2)}(r)

\displaystyle=-\frac{p_{e}^{2}r^{2}}{2(2k+1)}+V_{H2}(r),

(53)

\displaystyle h_{k}^{(2)}(r)

\displaystyle=V_{h2}(r),

(54)

\displaystyle\frac{r}{R}D_{k}^{(3)}(r)

\displaystyle=-\frac{p_{e}^{2}E_{e}r^{3}}{2(2k+1)(2k+3)}+V_{D3}(r),

(55)

\displaystyle\frac{r}{R}d_{k}^{(3)}(r)

\displaystyle=-\frac{p_{e}^{2}m_{e}r^{3}}{2(2k+1)(2k+3)}+V_{d3}(r)

(56)

with

\displaystyle V_{H2}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\left[(V_{C}(r^{\prime})-E_{e})V_{D1}(r^{\prime})+E_{e}\frac{r^{\prime}}{2k+1}V_{C}(r^{\prime})\right]dr^{\prime},

(57)

\displaystyle V_{h2}(r)

\displaystyle=m_{e}\int_{0}^{r}\left[V_{D1}(r^{\prime})+\frac{r^{\prime}}{2k+1}V_{C}(r^{\prime})\right]dr^{\prime},

(58)

\displaystyle V_{D3}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\right)^{2k}\left[(E_{e}-V_{C}(r^{\prime}))V_{H2}(r^{\prime})+m_{e}V_{h2}(r^{\prime})+\frac{p_{e}^{2}{r^{\prime}}^{2}}{2(2k+1)}V_{C}(r^{\prime})\right]dr^{\prime},

(59)

\displaystyle V_{d3}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\right)^{2k}[m_{e}V_{H2}(r^{\prime})+(E_{e}-V_{C}(r^{\prime}))V_{h2}(r^{\prime})]dr^{\prime}.

(60)

4
Decay rate and comparison with the conventional formula

4.1
Decay rate

The beta-decay rate is usually expressed in terms of the Fermi function
F(Z,E_{e})
and
the shape correction factor
C(E_{e})
as
[

12
]

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{(G_{F}V_{ud})^{2}}{2\pi^{3}}\int_{m_{e}}^{E_{0}}dE_{e}p_{e}E_{e}(E_{0}-E_{e})^{2}F(Z,E_{e})C(E_{e}).

(61)

Using the matrix element of the effective operator
\Xi_{JLM}
, we obtain

\displaystyle F(Z,E_{e})C(E_{e})

\displaystyle=\sum_{J,L,\kappa_{e},\kappa_{\nu}}\frac{2\pi}{2J_{i}+1}|\left<f\right\|\Xi_{JL}(\kappa_{e},\kappa_{\nu})\left\|i\right>|^{2},

(62)

and
F(Z,E_{e})=\alpha_{-1}^{2}+\alpha_{1}^{2}
.

4.2
Allowed and first-forbidden transitions of axial vector current

We focus on the transition rate due to the space component of the axial vector current.
In the impulse approximation, the axial vector current is given as

\displaystyle\bm{A}(\bm{r})=g_{A}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r}\sigma\tau)\psi(\bm{r}\sigma^{\prime}\tau^{\prime})\langle\tau|\tau^{\mp}|\tau^{\prime}\rangle\langle\sigma|\bm{\sigma}|\sigma^{\prime}\rangle

(63)

with the nucleon field operators
\psi,\psi^{\dagger}
,
at position
\bm{r}
, spin
\sigma
, and isospin
\tau
.
The transition density
\rho_{JL}(r)
represented
in the radial coordinate is defined as

\displaystyle g_{\rm A}\rho_{JL}(r)

\displaystyle=\left<f\right\|\int d\Omega_{r}[Y_{L}(\hat{r})\otimes\bm{A}(\bm{r})]_{J}\left\|i\right>.

(64)

The reduced matrix element of the effective operator
\Xi_{JLM}
is given
in terms of the radial integral of the transition density
\rho_{JL}(r)

multiplied by combination of the electron and neutrino wave functions with the
coefficients
c_{g}
and
c_{f}
given in the Appendix
C
:

\displaystyle\left<f\right\|\Xi_{JL}(\kappa_{e},\kappa_{\nu})\left\|i\right>

\displaystyle=\left<f\right\|\int d\bm{r}[Y_{L}(\hat{r})\otimes\bm{A}(\bm{r})]_{J}[c_{g}G_{\kappa_{e}}(r)g_{\kappa_{\nu}}(r)+c_{f}F_{\kappa_{e}}(r)f_{\kappa_{\nu}}(r)]\left\|i\right>

\displaystyle=g_{\rm A}\int_{0}^{\infty}drr^{2}\rho_{JL}(r)[c_{g}G_{\kappa_{e}}(r)g_{\kappa_{\nu}}(r)+c_{f}F_{\kappa_{e}}(r)f_{\kappa_{\nu}}(r)].

(65)

The leading-order formula by Behrens–Bühring (LOB) of Ref.
[
10
]

conventionally used in the nuclear structure calculations can be derived
by using approximate lepton wave functions in
Eqs. (
62
) and (

). We take the LO electron wave function
and the leading-order approximation of the neutrino wave function.
For the allowed transition
with
\Delta J^{\pi}=1^{+}
,
we approximate the
s
-wave wave functions as a constant number:

\displaystyle G_{-1}(r)\sim\alpha_{-1},

\displaystyle\quad g_{-1}(r)\sim 1,

(66)

\displaystyle F_{1}(r)\sim\alpha_{1},

\displaystyle\quad f_{1}(r)\sim 1,

(67)

and neglect all other partial waves.
For the spin-dipole transition
with
\Delta J^{\pi}=0^{-},1^{-}
, and
2^{-}

in addition to the above approximation
to the
s
-wave wave function, we use the following leading-order approximation
for the
p
-wave wave functions

\displaystyle G_{1}(r)\sim\alpha_{1}\dfrac{r}{3}[E_{e}+m_{e}+\frac{3V_{D1}(r)}{r}],

\displaystyle\quad g_{1}(r)\sim\dfrac{p_{\nu}r}{3},

(68)

\displaystyle F_{-1}(r)\sim-\alpha_{-1}\dfrac{r}{3}[E_{e}-m_{e}+\frac{3V_{D1}(r)}{r}],

\displaystyle\quad f_{-1}(r)\sim-\dfrac{p_{\nu}r}{3},

(69)

\displaystyle G_{-2}(r)\sim\alpha_{-2}\dfrac{p_{e}r}{3},

\displaystyle\quad g_{-2}(r)\sim\dfrac{p_{\nu}r}{3},

(70)

\displaystyle F_{2}(r)\sim\alpha_{2}\dfrac{p_{e}r}{3},

\displaystyle\quad f_{2}(r)\sim\dfrac{p_{\nu}r}{3}.

(71)

For the allowed
\Delta J^{\pi}=1^{+}
transition, two partial waves of leptons

(\kappa_{e},\kappa_{\nu})=(-1,-1)
and
(1,1)
contribute within the LOB,

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa_{e},\kappa_{\nu}}\left|\left<f\right\|\Xi_{J=1,L=0}\left\|i\right>\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\sim 2g_{\rm A}^{2}\left\{\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}drr^{2}\rho_{10}(r)\left[G_{-1}(r)g_{-1}(r)+\frac{1}{3}F_{-1}(r)f_{-1}(r)\right]\right|^{2}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}drr^{2}\rho_{10}(r)\left[\frac{1}{3}G_{1}(r)g_{1}(r)+F_{1}(r)f_{1}(r)\right]\right|^{2}\right\}

(72)

\displaystyle\sim 2g_{\rm A}^{2}(\alpha_{-1}^{2}+\alpha_{1}^{2})\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}drr^{2}\rho_{10}(r)\right|^{2}.

(73)

In the last step, we use
the approximation for the lepton wave functions.
As a result the shape correction factor is given as

\displaystyle C(E_{e})=g_{\rm A}^{2}\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}drr^{2}\sqrt{4\pi}\rho_{10}(r)\right|^{2}.

(74)

The second example is the first-forbidden transition
\Delta J^{\pi}=0^{-}

The leading-order partial waves are
(\kappa_{e},\kappa_{\nu})=(-1,1)

and
(1,-1)

We then obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa_{e},\kappa_{\nu}}|\left<f\right\|\Xi_{J=0,L=1}\left\|i\right>|^{2}

\displaystyle\sim 2g_{\rm A}^{2}\left\{\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}drr^{2}\rho_{01}(r)[G_{-1}(r)g_{1}(r)-F_{-1}(r)f_{1}(r)]\right|^{2}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}drr^{2}\rho_{01}(r)[G_{1}(r)g_{-1}(r)-F_{1}(r)f_{-1}(r)]\right|^{2}\right\}

(75)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2}{9}g_{\rm A}^{2}\left\{\alpha_{-1}^{2}\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}drr^{3}\rho_{01}(r)\left[p_{\nu}+E_{e}-m_{e}+3\frac{V_{D1}(r)}{r}\right]\right|^{2}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\alpha_{1}^{2}\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}drr^{3}\rho_{01}(r)\left[p_{\nu}+E_{e}+m_{e}+3\frac{V_{D1}(r)}{r}\right]\right|^{2}\right\}.

(76)

In order to compare our formula with LOB,
for example, Eq. (10.56) of Ref.
[
11
]

introducing nuclear matrix elements

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=g_{\rm A}\sqrt{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}drr^{3}\rho_{01}(r),

(77)

\displaystyle\xi\omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle=g_{\rm A}\sqrt{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}drr^{2}\rho_{01}(r)V_{D1}(r),

(78)

\displaystyle C(E_{e})=\zeta_{0}^{2}+\frac{\omega^{2}m_{e}^{2}}{9}-\frac{2}{3}\frac{\mu_{1}\gamma_{1}m_{e}^{2}}{E_{e}}\zeta_{0}\omega,

(79)

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{0}\omega}{3}+\xi\omega^{\prime},

(80)

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{k^{2}-(\alpha Z)^{2}},

(81)

\displaystyle\mu_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{\gamma_{k}}\frac{E_{e}}{m_{e}}\frac{\alpha_{-k}^{2}-\alpha_{k}^{2}}{\alpha_{-1}^{2}+\alpha_{1}^{2}}.

(82)

Here

A similar comparison can be done for the transitions to

and

states,
and we can confirm the use of the approximate lepton wave function within

6
EDF transition density and NLO electron wave function

To investigate the validity of our formalism in realistic cases,
we use the transition densities
microscopically calculated by a nuclear energy-density functional (EDF) method.
Since the details of the formalism can be found in Ref.
[
44
]
,
here we recapitulate the basic equations relevant to the present study.
In the framework of the nuclear EDF method we employ,
the ground state of a mother nucleus is described by solving the
Kohn–Sham–Bogoliubov (KSB) equation
[
45
]

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}h^{q}(\bm{r}\sigma)-\lambda^{q}&\tilde{h}^{q}(\bm{r}\sigma)\\
\tilde{h}^{q}(\bm{r}\sigma)&-h^{q}(\bm{r}\sigma)+\lambda^{q}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\varphi^{q}_{1,\alpha}(\bm{r}\sigma)\\
\varphi^{q}_{2,\alpha}(\bm{r}\sigma)\end{bmatrix}=E_{\alpha}\begin{bmatrix}\varphi^{q}_{1,\alpha}(\bm{r}\sigma)\\
\varphi^{q}_{2,\alpha}(\bm{r}\sigma)\end{bmatrix},

(88)

where
the KS
potentials
h
and
\tilde{h}
are given by the EDF.
An explicit expression of the potentials can be found for example in the Appendix of Ref.
[
46
]
.
The chemical potential
\lambda
is determined so as to give the desired nucleon number as an average value.
The superscript
q
denotes
n (neutron,
\tau_{z}=1
) or p (proton,
\tau_{z}=-1
).

The excited states
|f;J^{\pi}\rangle
in a daughter nucleus are described as
one-phonon excitations built on the ground state
|i\rangle
of the mother nucleus as

\displaystyle|f;J^{\pi}\rangle

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{\dagger}_{f}|i\rangle,

(89)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\dagger}_{f}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\left\{X_{\alpha\beta}^{f}a^{\dagger}_{\alpha,{\rm n}}a^{\dagger}_{\beta,{\rm p}}-Y_{\alpha\beta}^{f}a_{\beta,{\rm p}}a_{\alpha,{\rm n}}\right\},

(90)

where
a^{\dagger}_{\rm n}(a^{\dagger}_{\rm p})
and
a_{\rm n}(a_{\rm p})
are
the neutron (proton) quasiparticle (labeled by
\alpha
and
\beta
) creation and annihilation operators that
are defined in terms of the solutions of the KSB equation (
88
) with the Bogoliubov transformation.
The phonon states, the amplitudes
X^{f},Y^{f}
and the vibrational frequency
\omega_{f}
,
are obtained in the proton–neutron quasiparticle-random-phase approximation (pnQRPA).
The residual interactions entering into the pnQRPA equation
are given by the EDF self-consistently.
With the solutions of the pnQRPA equation, the transition density is given as

\displaystyle g_{A}\delta\bm{\rho}_{f;J^{\pi}}(\bm{r})=\langle f;J^{\pi}|\bm{A}(\bm{r})|i\rangle=\langle i|[\Gamma_{f},\bm{A}(\bm{r})]|i\rangle

(91)

in a standard quasi-boson approximation.
One obtains the transition density in the radial coordinate as

\displaystyle\rho_{JLK}(r)=\int d\Omega_{r}[Y_{L}(\hat{r})\otimes\delta\bm{\rho}(\bm{r})]_{JK},

(92)

which is independent of
K
in the present case for spherical systems.
Thus, the input transition density is obtained by
\rho_{JL}(r)=\sqrt{2J+1}\rho_{JL0}(r)
.

We apply our formula for the medium-heavy Ni and Sn isotopes.
Since a considerable contribution of the first-forbidden transition is predicted in the Sn isotopes
[
30
]
,
we take
160
Sn as an example in the present study.
Furthermore,
an interplay between the allowed and first-forbidden transitions has been discussed around
78

[

]
,
and we thus take

[

]
.
Within the pnQRPA, the maximum electron energy is given as

E_{0}=B(Z+1,N-1)-B(Z,N)+(m_{\rm n}-m_{\rm p}-m_{e})\simeq\lambda^{\rm n}-\lambda^{\rm p}-\omega+0.78
MeV for
\beta^{-}
decay
[
21
]
.

The transition densities
\rho_{JL}
of the

(

MeV)

Summary

We have investigated the Coulomb effects on the beta-decay rate.
The decay rate is determined by the product of the lepton and hadron current densities.
A widely used formula relies on the fact that the low-energy lepton wave functions in a nucleus
can be well approximated
by a constant and linear to the radius for the
s
-wave and
p
-wave wave functions, respectively.
We found, however,
the Coulomb wave function is conspicuously different from such a
simple approximation for heavy nuclei with large
Z

by numerically solving the Dirac equation.
We then have proposed formulas of the nuclear beta-decay rate that are useful in a practical calculation.

In our proposed formulas,
the neutrino wave function is treated exactly as a plane wave, while the electron wave function is
obtained by iteratively solving the integral equation; thus, we can control the uncertainty of the approximate electron wave function order by order.
The leading-order approximation gives a formula that is almost equivalent to the widely used one and overestimates the decay rate
by about 50–100% for heavy nuclei with
Z\sim 80
.
We demonstrated that the next-to-leading-order formula reproduces well the exact result for a
schematic transition density as well as a microscopic one obtained by a nuclear energy-density functional method.
For the beta decay involving heavy neutron-rich nuclei, the NLO will be needed
for the determination of the Gamow–Teller strength
from the beta-decay rate.

We considered only the space component of the axial vector currents and kept only the lowest multipoles.
The time components as well as the vector currents can have a comparable contribution to the decay rate,
and we plan to present these improvements in a sequel to the present article.
The beta decay provides a unique spectroscopic tool of exotic nuclei,
that is, the angular correlation
contains rich information of nuclear structure.
Furthermore, the electron/muon capture is an important process in the application
to astrophysics and fundamental physics.
It is straightforward to extend our formalism towards these directions.

In this appendix, we show the derivation of the decay rate Eq. (

In the present case, it is useful to

\displaystyle\left(1-\gamma_{5}\right)v^{s_{\nu}}\left(p_{\nu}\right)e^{-i\bm{p}_{\nu}\cdot\bm{r}}=

\displaystyle 4\pi\sum_{neu}\begin{pmatrix}-if_{\kappa_{\nu}}(r)\chi_{-\kappa_{\nu}}^{\mu_{\nu}}\\

(93)

where we use

\displaystyle\left(4\pi\right)^{2}\sum_{el}\sum_{neu}\left(G_{\kappa_{e}}\left(r\right)\chi_{\kappa_{e}}^{\mu_{e}\dagger},iF_{\kappa_{e}}\left(r\right)\chi_{-\kappa_{e}}^{\mu_{e}\dagger}\left(\hat{r}\right)\right)\gamma^{\nu}\begin{pmatrix}-g_{\kappa_{\nu}}(r)\chi_{-\kappa_{\nu}}^{\mu_{\nu}}\\
if_{\kappa_{\nu}}(r)\chi_{\kappa_{\nu}}^{\mu_{\nu}}\end{pmatrix},

(96)

where we used Eq. (
17
).

As in Eq. (

the vector and axial vector components.

\displaystyle\psi^{s_{e}(-)}_{p_{e}}\left(\bm{r}\right)\gamma^{\nu}\left(1-\gamma_{5}\right)v^{s_{\nu}}\left(p_{\nu}\right)e^{-i\bm{p}_{\nu}\cdot\bm{r}}J_{\nu}

\displaystyle\left(4\pi\right)^{2}\sum_{el}\sum_{neu}\left(G_{\kappa_{e}}\left(r\right)\chi_{\kappa_{e}}^{\mu_{e}\dagger}\left(\hat{r}\right),iF_{\kappa_{e}}\left(r\right)\chi_{-\kappa_{e}}^{\mu_{e}\dagger}\left(\hat{r}\right)\right)

(97)

\displaystyle\chi_{\kappa}^{\mu\dagger}\left(\hat{r}\right)\chi_{\kappa^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime}}\left(\hat{r}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\sum_{L,M}\left(j_{\kappa},-\mu,j_{\kappa^{\prime}},\mu^{\prime}|L,M\right)(-1)^{1/2-\mu}S_{\kappa}S_{0LL}(\kappa,\kappa^{\prime})Y_{LM}\left(\hat{r}\right),

where

15

).

\Xi_{JLM}

\displaystyle\Braket{f|\Xi_{JLM}\left(\kappa_{e},\kappa_{\nu}\right)|i}=\frac{\left(J_{i},s_{i},J,M|J_{f},s_{f}\right)}{\sqrt{2J_{f}+1}}\Braket{f\|\Xi_{JL}\left(\kappa_{e},\kappa_{\nu}\right)\|i},

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2J_{i}+1}\sum_{s_{i}}\sum_{s_{f},s_{e},s_{\nu}}\int\frac{d^{3}p_{\nu}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\frac{d^{3}p_{e}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}(2\pi)\delta\left(E_{\nu}+E_{e}-E_{0}\right)\left|H_{\mathrm{eff}}\right|^{2}
