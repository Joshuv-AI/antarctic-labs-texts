A Random Matrix Approach to Low-Multilinear-Rank Tensor Approximation
Hugo Lebeau hugo.lebeau@univ-grenoble-alpes.fr, Florent Chatelain florent.chatelain@grenoble-inp.fr, Romain Couillet romain.couillet@univ-grenoble-alpes.fr

Abstract
This work presents a comprehensive understanding of the estimation of a planted low-rank signal from a general spiked tensor model near the computational threshold. Relying on standard tools from the theory of large random matrices, we characterize the large-dimensional spectral behavior of the unfoldings of the data tensor and exhibit relevant signal-to-noise ratios governing the detectability of the principal directions of the signal. These results allow to accurately predict the reconstruction performance of truncated multilinear SVD (MLSVD) in the non-trivial regime. This is particularly important since it serves as an initialization of the higher-order orthogonal iteration (HOOI) scheme, whose convergence to the best low-multilinear-rank approximation depends entirely on its initialization. We give a sufficient condition for the convergence of HOOI and show that the number of iterations before convergence tends to 1 1 in the large-dimensional limit.

Related Work

Multilinear SVD (MLSVD) has a wide range of applications and is often used to extract relevant information from multi-way arrays. For instance, it has been used in human motion recognition

The analysis of

)
.

The results presented in this work rely on tools from the theory of large random matrices. Given a certain

whose entries are random variables, one is interested in the behavior its eigenvalues and eigenvectors as

, behave? Does it (weakly) converge to a

In case

is symmetric, its eigenvalues and eigenvectors are real and a key tool to answer the preceding questions is its resolvent,

, defined for all

. Indeed,
\frac{1}{n}\Tr{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{M}}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\lambda\in\Sp{\bm{M}}}\frac{1}{\lambda-z}
is the
Stieltjes transform
of its empirical spectral distribution.

Definition 2

.

The Stieltjes transform of a real probability measure

3
Analysis of Truncated MLSVD under the General Spiked Tensor Model

This section presents a random matrix analysis of the general spiked tensor model introduced in Equation (
2
) using tools presented in Section
2.3
. We give precise results on the spectral behavior of the unfoldings of the observed tensor
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}
, and specify the achievable performance in the estimation of the underlying signal
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
with a truncated MLSVD. Although, as explained in Section
1.1
, this approach is only
quasi-optimal
, it is very easy to implement and represents an excellent “first guess” to initialize a numerical scheme converging to a solution to Problem (

), which is discussed in Section

3.1
Random Matrix Results on the Model

Under the general spiked tensor model (
2
), we consider an
n_{1}\times\ldots\times n_{d}
tensor
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}={\bm{\mathscr{P}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}{\bm{\mathscr{N}}}
of order
d\geqslant 3
, modeling a low-rank deterministic signal
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
corrupted by an additive Gaussian noise tensor
{\bm{\mathscr{N}}}
whose entries are independent
{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
random variables
11
11

11

We highlight the fact that the Gaussian noise assumption is not restrictive. Firstly, the universality result of
Gurau 2014
shows that, as
N\to+\infty
, the distribution of a random tensor with i.i.d. entries has the same limit than that of a tensor with i.i.d.
Gaussian
entries. Moreover, our results can be extended to non-Gaussian noise up to a control on the moments of the distribution with the “interpolation trick” of
Lytova and Pastur 2009
. These are technical aspects which go beyond the scope of our work. Examples of results relying on this method are
Pastur and Shcherbina 2011
;
Merlevède et al. 2015
;
Banna et al. 2015
.
. We denote by
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})
the multilinear rank of
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
and study this model in the asymptotic regime where

with
n_{\ell}=\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N)
and
r_{\ell}=\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(1)
,
\ell\in[d]
.

The estimation of
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
with a truncated MLSVD on
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}
is simply the computation of the dominant singular subspaces of
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}
. Specifically,
\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(\ell)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\ell}\times r_{\ell}}
gathers the

dominant left singular vectors of
{\bm{T}}^{(\ell)}
— and thus,
\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(\ell)\top}\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(\ell)}={\bm{I}}_{r_{\ell}}
. Then, a low-multilinear-rank approximation of
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}
is
\hat{{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}}=\llbracket\hat{{\bm{\mathscr{G}}}};\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(d)}\rrbracket
with an
r_{1}\times\ldots\times r_{d}
core tensor
\hat{{\bm{\mathscr{G}}}}={\bm{\mathscr{T}}}(\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(d)})

\hat{{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}}=\llbracket{\bm{\mathscr{T}}};\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(1)}\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(1)\top},\ldots,\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(d)}\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(d)\top}\rrbracket

\hat{{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}}
is the projection of
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}
on its dominant singular subspaces

12

12

In the more familiar matrix case (
d=2
), the expression
\llbracket{\bm{T}};\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(1)}\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(1)\top},\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(2)}\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(2)\top}\rrbracket

\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(1)}\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(1)\top}{\bm{T}}\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(2)}\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(2)\top}
.
. Thus, the quality of this estimation hinges upon the alignments between the singular subspaces of
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
and the dominant singular subspaces of
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}
. Namely, denoting
\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{(\ell)}_{q_{\ell}}

q_{\ell}\in[r_{\ell}]
, the columns of
\hat{{\bm{U}}}^{(\ell)}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{(\ell)}_{1}&\dots&\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{(\ell)}_{r_{\ell}}\end{bmatrix}
and given that
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}=\llbracket{\bm{\mathscr{H}}};{\bm{X}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bm{X}}^{(d)}\rrbracket
, the quantities of interest are
\lVert{\bm{X}}^{(\ell)\top}\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{(\ell)}_{q_{\ell}}\rVert^{2}
since they represent how much of
\hat{{\bm{u}}}^{(\ell)}_{q_{\ell}}
is in the
\ell
-th singular subspace of the signal
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
.

In order to understand how the singular subspaces of
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
are perturbed by the addition of noise, we study the spectral properties of the unfoldings

. In fact, since we are only interested in the

singular vectors of
{\bm{T}}^{(\ell)}
, it is more convenient to consider the
n_{\ell}\times n_{\ell}

symmetric

. Note that this is different from standard spiked matrix models
(

)
because the second dimension of
{\bm{T}}^{(\ell)}
grows at a faster polynomial rate than the first one (
\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{d-1})
versus
\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N)
). Hence, it is easy to see that the spectrum of
{\bm{T}}^{(\ell)}{\bm{T}}^{(\ell)\top}
should diverge as
N\to+\infty
: set

for simplicity and consider the expected mean of the eigenvalues,

\frac{1}{n_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{\lambda\in\Sp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\bm{T}}^{(\ell)}{\bm{T}}^{(\ell)\top}}}\right)}\lambda}}\right]=\frac{1}{n_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N}{\bm{N}}^{(\ell)}{\bm{N}}^{(\ell)\top}}}\right)}}\right]=\frac{1}{N}\prod_{\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell}n_{\ell^{\prime}}\xrightarrow[N\to+\infty]{}+\infty.

Hence, we need to consider instead a

version of our random matrix

to properly study the behavior of its spectrum. The quantities

Remark 7

(From Marčenko-Pastur to Wigner)
.

Given a random matrix

with i.i.d.

converges weakly to the Marčenko-Pastur distribution as

Potters and Bouchaud 2020

is known to be the limiting spectral distribution of symmetric

entries (
Pastur and Shcherbina 2011
, Corollary 2.2.8
;
Couillet and Liao 2022
, Theorem 2.5
;
Potters and Bouchaud 2020
, Chapter 2
). Here, Corollary

grows at a faster polynomial rate than

behaves asymptotically (up to a deterministic rescaling and shift) like a Wigner matrix, even if its entries are not independent. Experimentally, we observe that, if

and
n_{3}
are chosen small compared to

(in contradiction with our assumption

, then the empirical spectral distribution of
{\bm{T}}^{(1)}{\bm{T}}^{(1)\top}
is better modeled by a Marčenko-Pastur distribution than by a Wigner semicircle.

Remark 8

The weak convergence of the empirical spectral distribution to

stays outside the support of the semicircle distribution

4.2
Discussion on Signal Reconstructibility

Our results on truncated MLSVD (Section
3
) and HOOI (Section
4.1
) bring insight into the computational-to-statistical gap observed in the low-multilinear-rank approximation problem. Truncated MLSVD can only work efficiently if
\lVert{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}
is at least
\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{\frac{d-2}{4}})
and its reconstruction performance has been discussed in Section
3.2
. However, Theorem
15
suggests that it is possible to perfectly reconstruct the signal
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
from the observation
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}
as long as
\lVert{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\gg 1
and HOOI is accurately initialized. Yet, it is known that, without prior information on
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
, maximum likelihood estimation (
1
) is NP-hard below the
\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{\frac{d-2}{4}})
computational threshold
(
Zhang and Xia 2018
)
, which lies precisely in the non-trivial regime of truncated MLSVD.

In fact, what can be understood from Theorem
15
is that it suffices to have an initialization
{\bm{U}}^{(\ell)}_{0}
,
\ell\in[d]
, slightly aligned with the underlying signal
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
to be in the right basin of attraction and allow the convergence of Algorithm
1
towards a solution to Problem (
1
). This complements the results of
Xu 2018
on the conditions of convergence of HOOI. Furthermore, a solution to Problem (
1
) is aligned with
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
as soon as
\lVert{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(1)

(
Ben Arous et al. 2019
;
Jagannath et al. 2020
;
Zhang and Xia 2018
)
. Hence, as the HOOI algorithm is meant to compute a maximum likelihood estimator, with the assumption
\lVert{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\gg 1
made in Theorem
15
, it is expected that these iterations allow to perfectly recover the signal asymptotically. Maximum likelihood estimation is indeed (theoretically) trivial if
\lVert{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\to+\infty
. It is more surprising however that this already happens at the first iteration.

As said previously, the choice of initialization
{\bm{U}}^{(\ell)}_{0}
,
\ell\in[d]
, does not matter in Theorem
15
. In fact, without prior information, a truncated MLSVD is the best choice as it allows to partially reconstruct the signal at the
\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{\frac{d-2}{4}})
computational threshold. Nevertheless, had one prior information allowing such a reconstruction in the regime
1\ll{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}\ll N^{\frac{d-2}{4}}
— where truncated MLSVD would not be fruitful —, HOOI would still be able to perfectly reconstruct the signal
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
given this initialization.

Hence, HOOI initialized with a truncated MLSVD, as it is presented in Algorithm
1
, allows to numerically compute a maximum likelihood estimator (solution to Problem (
1
)) but only above the phase transition of truncated MLSVD. Indeed, its initialization plays a determining role: it must place
{\bm{U}}^{(\ell)}_{0}
,
\ell\in[d]
, in the right basin of attraction, which, without prior information, is only possible above the
\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{\frac{d-2}{4}})
computational threshold.

Finally, we highlight the fact that these results concern the large
N
limit. In practice, it makes no sense to talk about
\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(1)
or
\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{\frac{d-2}{4}})
regimes at finite
N
. Figure
2
also depicts the subspace alignments achieved with the maximum likelihood estimator computed with Algorithm
1
on
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}=\sqrt{\omega}{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}_{\circ}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}{\bm{\mathscr{N}}}
. Although
\lVert{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}_{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=\sqrt{\sigma_{N}}=\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{\frac{d-2}{4}})
, HOOI does not achieve perfect recovery of
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
as one might expect from Theorem
15
(even if several iterations were performed here). In fact, at finite
N
, perfect recovery is not feasible. But, as
N
grows, the dashed line would approach
1
above the (computational) phase transition determined by the truncated MLSVD and stay close to
0
below.

5
Concluding Remarks

The analysis presented in this work yields theoretical and practical insights into the estimation of a low-rank signal from an observation
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}={\bm{\mathscr{P}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}{\bm{\mathscr{N}}}
following the most general spiked tensor model. While
Zhang and Xia 2018
gave a general overview of the different regimes governing the estimation of
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
with a low-multilinear-rank approximation of
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}
— thereby confirming the existence of a computational-to-statistical gap —, our results shed light on the non-trivial aspects at stake around the
\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{\frac{d-2}{4}})
computational threshold. This is of particular importance as practical applications lie in this non-trivial regime where signal and noise have the same magnitude and must be decoupled. In particular, truncated MLSVD and HOOI are very standard and efficient algorithms to compute low-multilinear-rank approximations. Performances of the latter rely strongly on the quality of its initialization, which is usually performed with a truncated MLSVD in the absence of prior information. This approach allows the detection of the underlying signal as early as the computational threshold contrary to other methods such as AMP or tensor power iteration, which are efficient above a
\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{\frac{d-1}{2}})
algorithmic threshold
(
Montanari and Richard 2014
)
.

Relying on standard tools and methods from the theory of large random matrices, we have characterized the spectral behavior of the unfoldings of
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}
in the large
N
limit. Specifically, our first main result shows that, when properly centered and scaled, the eigenvalues of
{\bm{T}}^{(\ell)}{\bm{T}}^{(\ell)\top}
asymptotically follow a semicircle distribution. The rescaling exhibits their mean
\mu^{(\ell)}_{N}=\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{d-2})
and a quantity
\sigma_{N}=\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{\frac{d-2}{2}})
governing their spread. From our denoising perspective,
\sigma_{N}
indicates the
strength
of the noise. Indeed, while the global behavior of the eigenvalues is controlled by the noise, the addition of a low-rank signal causes the presence of a finite number of eigenvalues outside the limiting semicircle distribution with corresponding eigenvectors aligned with the singular subspaces of the sought signal
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
. Yet, the existence of these outlier eigenvalues hinges on the values of the signal-to-noise ratios
\rho^{(\ell)}_{q_{\ell}}=s_{q_{\ell}}^{2}({\bm{P}}^{(\ell)})/\sigma_{N}
, manifesting a BBP phase transition phenomenon. When they exist, the positions of these isolated eigenvalues and the quality of the corresponding alignments are completely determined by
\rho^{(\ell)}_{q_{\ell}}
. These results justify the use of a truncated MLSVD to estimate
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
from the observation
{\bm{\mathscr{T}}}
and allow the precise characterization of the achievable reconstruction performances in the non-trivial regime, i.e., close to the computational threshold. In particular, we have seen that each singular value of
{\bm{P}}^{(\ell)}
determines the position of a phase transition corresponding to the detectability of the corresponding principal direction.

Although truncated MLSVD does not yield the best low-multilinear-rank approximation — i.e., a maximum likelihood solution —, it serves as an excellent initialization for the HOOI algorithm, which converges to such an estimator if it is initialized
sufficiently close
to it
(
Xu 2018
)
. In fact, we precise this last assertion by showing that, as long as the initialization preserves the singular subspaces of
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
in a sense precised in Theorem
15
, HOOI converges to a maximum likelihood solution in a number of iterations which tends to
1
as
N\to+\infty
. Hence, when it is initialized with a truncated MLSVD, it shares the same phase transition, whose position depends on the singular values
s^{(\ell)}_{q_{\ell}}({\bm{P}}^{(\ell)})
of the unfoldings of
{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}
. Yet, given prior information, HOOI can still reconstruct the maximum likelihood solution below the computational threshold
\lVert{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{\frac{d-2}{4}})
, where its success depends entirely on the quality of its initialization.

This work gives a comprehensive understanding of the low-multilinear-rank approximation problem near the computational threshold, which has both practical and theoretical implications. Besides, from a theoretical perspective, the behavior of the maximum likelihood estimator is still unclear near the statistical threshold — that is, in the regime where
\lVert{\bm{\mathscr{P}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=\operatorname*{\Theta_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(1)
. Several works have studied the rank-
1
symmetric case
(
Ben Arous et al. 2019
;
Jagannath et al. 2020
)
and the approach developed by
Seddik et al. 2022
in their analysis of the rank-
1

asymmetric
case may be an attractive direction to consider. Relying solely on classical tools from random matrix theory, they bring the study of the best rank-
1
tensor approximation down to that of a structured matrix defined from the contractions of the data tensor on its dominant singular vectors. Extending this procedure to our general spiked tensor model (
2
) presents no conceptual difficulty, despite being computationally cumbersome due to the multiple dimensions of the singular subspaces. It is an interesting line of investigation to refine our understanding of the statistical limits to low-rank tensor estimation from spiked models.

B.5
Confinement of the Spectrum

We have just found, in Lemma
23
, the following expansion:
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{m}^{(\ell)}_{N}(\tilde{z})]=m_{\mathrm{SC}}(\tilde{z})+h^{(\ell)}_{N}(\tilde{z})+\operatorname*{\mathcal{O}_{\mathnormal{\tilde{z}}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{-\min(\frac{d}{2},2)})
. Note that this shows that the limiting spectral distribution of
\frac{1}{\sigma_{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{N}{\bm{N}}^{(\ell)}{\bm{N}}^{(\ell)\top}-\mu^{(\ell)}_{N}{\bm{I}}_{n_{\ell}}}}\right]
(and therefore that of
\frac{1}{\sigma_{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\bm{T}}^{(\ell)}{\bm{T}}^{(\ell)\top}-\mu^{(\ell)}_{N}{\bm{I}}_{n_{\ell}}}}\right]
) is the semicircle distribution on
[-2,2]
. We will now use this expansion to prove that, almost surely, no eigenvalue of
\frac{1}{\sigma_{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{N}{\bm{N}}^{(\ell)}{\bm{N}}^{(\ell)\top}-\mu^{(\ell)}_{N}{\bm{I}}_{n_{\ell}}}}\right]
stays outside
[-2,2]
as
N\to+\infty
. That is, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an integer
N_{0}
such that, for all
\lambda\in\Sp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{N}{\bm{N}}^{(\ell)}{\bm{N}}^{(\ell)\top}-\mu^{(\ell)}_{N}{\bm{I}}_{n_{\ell}}}}\right]}}\right)
,
\Dist(\lambda,\Supp\mu_{\mathrm{MP}})\leqslant\varepsilon
as soon as
N\geqslant N_{0}
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto[0,1]
be an infinitely differentiable function which equals
1
on
[-2,2]
and
0
on
{\mathbb{R}}\setminus[-2-\varepsilon,2+\varepsilon]
and
\psi=1-\varphi
. We want to show that

\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{N}{\bm{N}}^{(\ell)}{\bm{N}}^{(\ell)\top}-\mu^{(\ell)}_{N}{\bm{I}}_{n_{\ell}}}}\right]}}\right)}}\right)\to 0\quad\text{almost surely as}\penalty\ N\to+\infty.

First of all, we show the convergence in mean with the Helffer-Sjöstrand formula (Proposition
21
).

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{n_{\ell}}\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{N}{\bm{N}}^{(\ell)}{\bm{N}}^{(\ell)\top}-\mu^{(\ell)}_{N}{\bm{I}}_{n_{\ell}}}}\right]}}\right)}}\right)}}\right]\\
\begin{aligned} &=\frac{2}{\pi}\Re\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{+}}\frac{\partial\Phi_{q}[\varphi]}{\partial\bar{\tilde{z}}}(\tilde{z})\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\widetilde{m}^{(\ell)}_{N}(\tilde{z})}}\right]\penalty\ {\mathrm{d}}\tilde{z}\\
&=\begin{multlined}\frac{2}{\pi}\Re\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{+}}\frac{\partial\Phi_{q}[\varphi]}{\partial\bar{\tilde{z}}}(\tilde{z})m_{\mathrm{SC}}(\tilde{z})\penalty\ {\mathrm{d}}\tilde{z}+\frac{2}{\pi}\Re\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{+}}\frac{\partial\Phi_{q}[\varphi]}{\partial\bar{\tilde{z}}}(\tilde{z})h^{(\ell)}_{N}(\tilde{z})\penalty\ {\mathrm{d}}\tilde{z}\\
+\frac{2}{\pi}\Re\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{+}}\frac{\partial\Phi_{q}[\varphi]}{\partial\bar{\tilde{z}}}(\tilde{z})\times\operatorname*{\mathcal{O}_{\mathnormal{\tilde{z}}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{-\min(\frac{d}{2},2)})\penalty\ {\mathrm{d}}\tilde{z}.\end{multlined}\end{aligned}

where we have used the expression of
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{m}^{(\ell)}_{N}(\tilde{z})]
given by Lemma
23
. The first integral is
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\penalty\ {\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\mathrm{SC}}=1
while the last one is
\operatorname*{\mathcal{O}_{\mathnormal{}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{-\min(\frac{d}{2},2)})
with
q
chosen sufficiently large so that
\frac{\partial\Phi_{q}[\varphi]}{\partial\bar{\tilde{z}}}(\tilde{z})
cancels the divergence of
\operatorname*{\mathcal{O}_{\mathnormal{\tilde{z}}}^{\mathnormal{}}}(N^{-\min(\frac{d}{2},2)})
near the real axis. In order to evaluate the second integral, we perform an integration by parts:

\frac{2}{\pi}\Re\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{+}}\frac{\partial\Phi_{q}[\varphi]}{\partial\bar{\tilde{z}}}(\tilde{z})h^{(\ell)}_{N}(\tilde{z})\penalty\ {\mathrm{d}}\tilde{z}\\
\begin{aligned} &=\begin{multlined}\frac{2}{\pi}\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\partial\Phi_{q}[\varphi]}{\partial x}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)h^{(\ell)}_{N}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)\penalty\ {\mathrm{d}}x}}\right){\mathrm{d}}y}}\right.\\
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.+\frac{{\mathrm{i}}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\partial\Phi_{q}[\varphi]}{\partial y}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)h^{(\ell)}_{N}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)\penalty\ {\mathrm{d}}y}}\right){\mathrm{d}}x}}\right]\end{multlined}\\
&=\frac{2}{\pi}\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{-{\mathrm{i}}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lim_{y\downarrow 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\Phi_{q}[\varphi](x+{\mathrm{i}}y)h^{(\ell)}_{N}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)}}\right\}\penalty\ {\mathrm{d}}x-\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{+}}\Phi_{q}[\varphi](\tilde{z})\frac{\partial h}{\partial\bar{\tilde{z}}}(\tilde{z})\penalty\ {\mathrm{d}}\tilde{z}}}\right].\end{aligned}

Since
h^{(\ell)}_{N}
is an analytic function (as sums and products of Stieltjes transforms), we have
\partial h/\partial\bar{\tilde{z}}=0
by the Cauchy-Riemann equations. Moreover, we have
\lim_{y\downarrow 0}\Phi_{q}[\varphi](x+{\mathrm{i}}y)=\varphi(x)
and, from the definition of

,
\lim_{y\downarrow 0}\Im[h^{(\ell)}_{N}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)]=0
for all
