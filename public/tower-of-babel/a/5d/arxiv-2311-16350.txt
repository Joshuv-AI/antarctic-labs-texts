Abstract

Statistical early warning signs can be used to identify an approaching bifurcation in stochastic dynamical systems and are now regularly employed in applications concerned with the identification of potential rapid, non-linear change or tipping points. However, the reliability of these early warning signs relies on a number of key mathematical assumptions, most notably the presence of Gaussian noise. We here show that for systems driven by non-Gaussian,
\alpha
-stable noise, the classical early warning signs of rising variance and autocorrelation are not supported by mathematical theory and their use poses the danger of spurious, false-positive results. To address this, we provide a generalized approach by introduce the scaling factor
\gamma_{X}
as an alternative early warning sign. We show that in the case of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, there exists a direct inverse relationship between
\gamma_{X}
and the bifurcation parameter, telling us that
\gamma_{X}
will increase as we approach the bifurcation. Our numerical simulations confirm theoretical results and show that our findings generalize well to non-linear, non-equilibrium systems. We thus provide a generalized, robust and applicable statistical early warning sign for systems driven by Gaussian and non-Gaussian
\alpha
-stable noise.

Introduction

Non-linear dynamical systems may exhibit rapid and irreversible state shifts upon a small change of a parameter
[
54
,
34
]
. The potential existence of such critical transitions or tipping points is a major concern in climate science and ecology
[
37
,
19
,
48
]
and has been postulated for a number of climate subsystems such as the cryosphere
[
26
,
28
]
, hydrosphere
[
53
,
38
]
or biosphere
[
32
,
44
,
11
,
24
]
.

In the case of stochastic systems, there may exist statistical early warning signs that precede the actual tipping point
[
49
,
58
]
, for example, a rise in variance or autocorrelation. A range of real-world systems exhibits such signs before critical transitions
[
14
,
15
,
10
,
16
]
and an increase in variance and other observables has also been observed in time series data of climate elements suggested to approach tipping points
[
7
,
4
,
3
]
.

The use of variance or autocorrelation as early warning signs sits on a robust body of mathematical theory concerned with bifurcations in stochastic dynamical systems
[
54
,
34
,
27
]
. However, one key assumption of this theory is that we are working in the small noise limit of Gaussian white noise, assumptions that may not always hold in real-world applications
[
5
]
. Previous studies have already pointed out situations where classical early warning signs fail for other noise types
[
35
,
6
,
20
]
.

There is ample evidence that the assumption of Gaussian white (that is uncorrelated) noise does not hold for many climate variables including temperature, precipitation or sea level which have been shown to be correlated in time
[
21
,
25
,
45
,
30
]
or exhibit heavy tails, thus violating the Gaussian assumption
[
25
,
40
]
. Since climate change is expected to lead to a higher frequency of extreme events
[
43
,
23
]
, the occurrence of heavy-tailed data might additionally become more frequent in the future.

One class of probability distributions that are characterized by such heavy tails are
\alpha
-stable distributions
[
25
,
42
,
13
]
. The exception to this rule is the Gaussian (Normal) distribution which is a special case. Other known members of the class include the Cauchy (Lorenz) distribution or the Lévy distribution. A range of real-world systems have been found which display
\alpha
-stable properties
[
56
,
22
,
8
,
51
]
. Notable examples in climate science and ecology include paleoclimatological temperature reconstruction from ice core data
[
18
]
, foraging behavior of various animal populations
[
33
,
52
,
2
,
57
]
, tree rings
[
36
]
or the distribution of rainfall and other meteorological variables,
[
40
,
39
]
.

One important characteristic of non-Gaussian
\alpha
-stable distributions is that their variance and higher order moments diverge
[
42
]
. This has spawned much discussion about the applicability of
\alpha
-stable models to real data, as empirical moments will of course always be finite
[
40
,
39
]
. However, as
[
40
]
among others have pointed out, divergence simply means that we cannot expect moments to converge to a finite value but must rather assume them to continue increasing with sample size. This of course heavily challenges the use of a rising variance as an early warning sign of a tipping point. While the use of
\alpha
-stable noise in models of climate tipping is gaining traction
[
41
,
60
,
59
,
50
,
17
]
, the impact of
\alpha
-stable driving noise on the existence and properties of early warning sign in such systems has not yet been assessed.

In this paper, we discuss the applicability and limits of classical early warning signs in the
\alpha
-stable case. We revise the basic theory of stochastic dynamical systems,
\alpha
-stable processes, and early warning signs in Section 2 and discuss potential pitfalls when applying classical early warning signs to systems driven by
\alpha
-stable noise. In Section 3 we introduce an alternative early warning sign - the scaling factor
\gamma
- showing that it is a natural generalization of the Gaussian variance scaling to the
\alpha
-stable case. Lastly, in Section 4 we demonstrate the applicability of our generalized approach for simple numerical models: a linear system of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck type and a non-linear system passing through a fold bifurcation.

Theoretical Background

2.1
Stochastic dynamical systems

Viewing the climate and its sub-components as a stochastic dynamical system dates back to seminal works by Hasselmann
[
30
]
and others, that separated the slow dynamics of climate from the fast fluctuations of weather, represented by noise. Observations are then produced by the interaction of the dynamical system with the driving noise.

We can formulate this view as a one-dimensional stochastic differential model

dX(t)=-U^{\prime}\big(X(t),k\big)dt+dN(t)=f(X(t))

(1)

where
dX(t)=-U^{\prime}\big(X(t),k\big)dt
describes a deterministic dynamical system evolving in a potential
U
,
N(t)
denotes a random perturbation,
X(t)
are realizations of the system at time
t
and
k
is a bifurcation parameter.

The potential
U(X)
can be chosen to represent any dynamical model suitable for the research task at hand. In this paper, we will consider two models: The (linear) Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (
2
) and a non-linear, quadratic system (
3
).

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

dX(t)=-\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}X(t)dt+dN(t)

(2)

originally described the movement of a particle subjected to the random influence of the surrounding fluid and friction
[
55
]
.
The system has one fixed point at
x^{*}=0
. It passes through a bifurcation at
k=0
, where
x^{*}
is stable for
\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}>0
and unstable for
\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}<0
. Figure
1
A gives the bifurcation diagram.

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is the most basic stochastic dynamical system and can be recovered from non-linear systems when linearizing around fixed points (as demonstrated in (
10
)).

The non-linear system

dX(t)=(k-X^{2}(t))dt+dN(t)

(3)

has a fold bifurcation, also at
k=0
. The system has two fixed points
X^{*^{\pm}}=\pm\sqrt{k}
for

and none for
k<0
. Figure
1
B gives the bifurcation diagram and stability of fixed points.

The second important modeling choice to make is that of the random perturbation
N
. Usually,

is assumed to be a Brownian motion, which is a Gaussian process
N=(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
with independent, stationary increments following a Normal (Gaussian) distribution:
N(s)-N(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu=0,\sigma_{N})
. Here we want to focus on the case of symmetric
\alpha
-stable noise, which also has stationary, independent increments, but where the increments
N(s)-N(t)\sim\mathcal{S}(\alpha_{N},\gamma_{N})
follow a
\alpha
-stable distribution
1
1

1

\alpha
-stable processes are a subclass of Lévy processes
[
1
,
47
]
. For this reason, the name

[

12
]
. Random walks following an
\alpha
-stable random variable are called

[

,

]

. The wide class
\alpha
-stable distributions include the Gaussian as well as a range of heavy-tailed distributions.

•

The symmetry parameter

•

\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{charf}{{{}}\psi}(u)=e^{g(u)}\textrm{ with }g(u)=-\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}^{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}}|u|^{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}}\textrm{ for }\beta,\delta=0

For certain special cases, however, probability density functions exist in closed form. The most important one is the Gaussian distribution, which is a special case of

-stable distribution with

f(x)=\frac{1}{2\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}\sqrt{\pi}}e^{{\frac{-x^{2}}{4\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}^{2}}}}

The standard notation of a Gaussian density in terms of mean and variance can be recovered by substituting

.

Other important special cases are the Cauchy distribution (

2.3
Early warning signs

To construct early warning signs, we are interested in the statistical properties of
X(t)
in relation to the bifurcation parameter
k
. We would like to reiterate that changes in these properties when approaching a bifurcation are created through the interaction of the driving noise
N
with the dynamical system, the driving perturbation itself is assumed to remain constant.

A range of statistical properties of
X(t)
has been utilized as early warning signs. The most important ones, which we will focus on for the remainder of this work are variance
\textnormal{Var}[X]
and autocorrelation
\zeta

[
58
,
48
,
34
]
, however, skewness
[
29
]
or spectral properties
[
9
]
have also been proposed.

The theory of early warning signs sits on a robust body of mathematical theory derived from the properties of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (
2
): For this particular system we can obtain an explicit solution (following
[
27
]
)

X(t)=X_{0}e^{-kt}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}(t-s)}dN(s)

(7)

Recall that in the classical case, we assume
N
to a Brownian motion with increments
N(s)-N(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu=0,\sigma_{N})
. In this case,
X(t)
will be normally distributed with mean
\mu=0
as well. We can obtain the full probability density
p(X,t)
from Eq. (
7
) directly or via the Fokker-Planck-Equation

\frac{\partial p(X,t)}{\partial t}=kX\frac{\partial p(X,t)}{\partial X}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}p(X,t)}{\partial X^{2}}

(8)

to obtain the variance

\textnormal{Var}[X(t)]=(\textnormal{Var}[X_{0}]-\frac{\sigma_{N}^{2}}{2k})e^{-2\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}t}+\frac{\sigma_{N}^{2}}{2\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\rightarrow\infty}}{{=}}\frac{\sigma_{N}^{2}}{2\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}}

(9)

Assuming a deterministic initial condition (
\textnormal{Var}[X_{0}]
= 0) and stationarity (
t\rightarrow\infty
), we find that the variance scales with
\frac{1}{2k}
and hence increases as the system approaches the stable-to-unstable transition (
\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}~\rightarrow~0^{+}
), as shown in Figure
2
A.

In non-linear systems, one would typically linearize around the steady state of interest to again obtain a linear system of the form of Eq. (
2
)
[
4
,
5
]
. In the case of the fold bifurcation (
3
) we expand the right-hand side around
X^{*^{+}}

f(X)=f(X^{*^{+}})+f^{\prime}(X^{*^{+}})(X-X^{*^{+}})+\mathcal{O}(|X-X^{*^{+}}|^{2})

(10)

After substituting
X^{*^{+}}=\sqrt{k}
, we can rearrange to obtain a new Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

dY\approx-\kappa Ydt+dW_{Y}

(11)

with
Y=X-X^{*^{+}}
and
\kappa=2\sqrt{k}
. Hence we can expect the system to still follow relationship (
9
) when close to
X^{*^{+}}
.

The auto-correlation

\zeta(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{E[(X(t_{1})-E[X(t_{1}))(X(t_{2})-E[X(t_{2}))]}{\sqrt{\textnormal{Var}[X(t_{1})]}\sqrt{(\textnormal{Var}[X(t_{2})]}}

(12)

follows from that, as it is a function of the first and second moment and hence mean and variance.

However, as stated in Section

show, that for systems of type (

\alpha

, Var
[X]
will only be finite if

. The same is true for the fold bifurcation

This implies that the classical theory of early warning signs relying on linearization as laid out in this section is not valid for

3
An early warning indicator for
α
-stable systems

To address this caveat, we propose the scaling parameter
γ
as an alternative, robust early warning sign that is applicable to Gaussian and
\alpha
-stable systems, easy to calculate in practical applications and, as we will show in the following, firmly grounded in mathematical theory.

We start with the
α
-stable process of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
type

dX(t)=-\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}X(t)dt+dN(t)

(13)

where
N(t)
is a symmetric,
\alpha
-stable process with characteristic function

\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{charf}{{{}}\psi}_{N}(u)=\mathbb{E}[e^{iuN(t)}]=e^{-\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{N}^{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}}|u|^{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}}}

(14)

Recall the solution of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (
7
), which also holds in the
\alpha
-stable case

X(t)=X_{0}e^{-\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}t}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}(t-s)}dN(s)

(15)

We know that
X(t)
will also be
\alpha
-stable and thus can formulate its characteristic function

\displaystyle\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{charf}{{{}}\psi}_{X(t)}(u)=\mathbb{E}[e^{iuX(t)}]=e^{-\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{X}^{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}}(t)|u|^{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}}}

(16)

Combining Eq. (
15
) into Eq. (
16
) and re-arranging (see Appendix for a full derivation), we obtain

\displaystyle\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{charf}{{{}}\psi}_{X(t)}(u)=e^{-\frac{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{N}^{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}}}{\alpha k}(1-e^{-\alpha kt})|u|^{\alpha}}

(17)

This form allows us to retrieve the exact parameters determining the properties of
X(t)
. Comparing Eq. (
17
) to Eq. (
14
), we see that the random variable
X(t)
is indeed again
\alpha
-stable with
\alpha_{X}=\alpha_{N}
and has a scaling parameter

\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{X}=\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{N}\sqrt[\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}]{\frac{(1-e^{-\alpha kt})}{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\rightarrow\infty}}{{=}}\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{N}\sqrt[\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}]{\frac{1}{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}}}

We thus find a direct relationship between
\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{X}
and the bifurcation parameter

, which tells us that
\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{X}
will increase as we approach the bifurcation (decreasing

). Based on this relationship, we are able to utilize
\gamma_{X}
as an early warning sign of that bifurcation.

This is indeed a generalization of the the variance scaling found in the Gaussian case. Recall that for Gaussian
\alpha
-stable variables
\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}=2
and
2\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{i}^{2}=\textnormal{Var}[X]=\sigma_{X}^{2}
(
6
). Substituting Eq. (
18
) into the latter gives us

\textnormal{Var}[X]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:gamma_variance}}}{{=}}2\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}^{2}_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:gammaX}}}{{=}}2(\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{N}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{bifurcation}{{{}}k}}})^{2}=\frac{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{N}^{2}}{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:gamma_variance}}}{{=}}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2k},

recovering Eq. (
9
).

4
Numerical Simulations

We perform a range of numerical simulations to confirm our results and to illustrate the applicability of our proposed indicator
\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{X}
. As (
18
) gives the solution in the long-term limit, we first perform equilibrium simulations for both systems (
2
) and (
3
) over a range of values for

. In a second step, we then estimate
\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{X}

\alpha

We discretize and simulate with the following Euler-Maruyama scheme

18
,

where

\mathbb{E}e^{iuN_{k}}=e^{-\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{N}^{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}}|u|^{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{alpha}{{{}}\alpha}}}

We chose
\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{N}=0.1
and

and initiated all simulations at

Since we have more than one fixed point in the non-linear case, trajectories might escape the basin of attraction of the stable fixed point. We therefore stopped a simulation if

X_{i}<-\sqrt{k}-\frac{k}{10}

(22)

For the equilibrium runs we perform 100 independent estimations of
\lx@glossaries@gls@link{main}{gamma}{{{}}\gamma}_{X}
for each combination of

. As our goal here was to confirm our theoretical findings, we use 5 independent trajectories for each estimation to improve accuracy at reasonable computational costs (see Figure

For the non-equilibrium runs, we simulated 15 trajectories for each value of
\alpha

every 150 time steps, using 300 data points.

5
Conclusion

We have shown that for systems driven by
\alpha
-stable, non-Gaussian noise, the classical early warning sign of rising variance and autocorrelation are not supported by mathematical theory and its use poses the danger of spurious, false-positive results.

To address this, we have introduced the scaling factor
\gamma_{X}
as alternative, generalized early warning sign applicable to Gaussian and non-Gaussian
\alpha
-stable processes. We have laid out the necessary mathematical theory to show
\gamma_{X}
is always defined and inversely scales with the bifurcation parameter, much in the same way as the variance does in the Gaussian case.

Our simulations confirmed our theoretical results and showed that
\gamma_{X}
can be estimated from few trajectories with sufficient accuracy. Additionally, our results generalize well to the non-linear, non-equilibrium case we would usually find in applications.

Estimating the parameters of an
\alpha
-stable distribution is a common exercise and algorithms are readily available in relevant programming languages. While being computationally more expensive than variance estimation, it still provides an easy-to-use method that works with a limited amount of data points available. This provides good conditions for applying
\gamma_{X}
to more complex and real-world data streams in the future.

With
\alpha
-stable models again gaining traction in climate and tipping point research, we thus hope our results will contribute to their further understanding and use.
