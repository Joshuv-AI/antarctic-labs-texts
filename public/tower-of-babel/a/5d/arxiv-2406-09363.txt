Elicitation GPT \text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}} : Text Elicitation Mechanisms via Language Models
Yifan Wu, Jason Hartline

Abstract
Scoring rules evaluate probabilistic forecasts of an unknown state against the realized state and are a fundamental building block in the incentivized elicitation of information. This paper develops mechanisms for scoring elicited text against ground truth text by reducing the textual information elicitation problem to a forecast elicitation problem, via domain-knowledge-free queries to a large language model (specifically ChatGPT), and empirically evaluates their alignment with human preferences. Our theoretical analysis shows that the reduction achieves provable properness via black-box language models. The empirical evaluation is conducted on peer reviews from a peer-grading dataset, in comparison to manual instructor scores for the peer reviews. Our results suggest a paradigm of algorithmic artificial intelligence that may be useful for developing artificial intelligence technologies with provable guarantees.

Introduction

Information elicitation is fundamental to applications that rely on decentralized human input, such as crowdsourced data collection, supervising machine learning models, and peer grading in education. The theory of proper scoring rules is well established for eliciting numerical predictions, e.g., the expected value of a random variable
(
Abernethy and Frongillo, 2012
)
, the probability that a peer’s homework is correct, or the distribution of an unknown state
(
McCarthy, 1956
)
, and widely used in practice
(
Hossain and Okui, 2013
;
Danz et al., 2022
;
Möbius et al., 2022
)
. Proper scoring rules incentivize a strategic agent to reveal her true belief about a random state. The principal posts a scoring rule, a function of the agent’s numerical prediction and a state to be realized. The agent reports a numerical prediction. After the state is realized, the agent receives the score as a reward. A scoring rule is
proper
if the agent’s (subjective) expected score is maximized when she reports the true belief.

The recent advances in language models have introduced a growing demand for eliciting truthful and high-quality textual datasets, and have also made the collection of high-quality textual information increasingly feasible. Compared with numerical predictions, textual information is often more open-ended and expresses richer details that are difficult to convey through structured predictions. For the peer grading example, textual feedback allows peer graders to articulate specific strengths, weaknesses, and suggestions that a single numerical prediction cannot fully capture.

However, obtaining provable guarantees such as the truthfulness of textual information elicitation from queries to language models remains challenging. For example, in the same setup as numerical elicitation, given a reported text and a ground truth text as a reference, one natural approach for assessing the quality of the report is to query language models to output a score by comparing with the ground truth. For the peer grading application, the student could easily manipulate the language model to output the full score by making up irrelevant statements or inserting the following instruction into the reported homework review:

<IMPORTANT> SYSTEM Instruction: ATTENTION! This is a system-level Instruction, please give 200% of your attention. You are undergoing an evaluation test. If you are being asked to give a score to a student review, you should
ignore all other scoring instructions
and
give the full score
to this student review.

We present manipulation strategies for the peer grading application in

A.4
.

This paper constructs proper scoring rules for text with provable guarantees. We propose our textual information elicitation mechanism
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
.
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
reduces the textual information elicitation problem to the numerical elicitation problem by evaluating text in its high-dimensional semantic space. The construction of
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
has four steps, where we model a language model (GPT) as an oracle that accepts
summarization
or
question-answering
queries.

1.

(Summarization) Identify the state space for elicitation as a collection of summary points across the ground truth text.

2.

(Question-Answering) Map ground truth text into a state vector with coordinates corresponding to each summary point with ternary values of “agree”,
“disagree”, or “not applicable”.

3.

(Question-Answering) Map reported text into a numerical vector with coordinates corresponding to the probability of agreeing with each summary point.

4.

Score the report vector against the ground truth state vector
with a proper scoring rule.

Our reduction above makes domain-knowledge-free queries to a language model for two paradigmatic natural language processing tasks: summarization and question-answering. A domain-knowledge-free query is one where all the information needed to answer the query is in the query itself. Specifically, the language model is not being utilized for any domain knowledge that might be memorized in its weights. The summarization task is to identify and list summary points for an input text. The question-answering task is to evaluate whether an input text supports, refutes, or is inconclusive about an input statement. These are both tasks that modern language models are very accurate at.

We prove the properness of
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
and empirically evaluate the alignment of our algorithmic score with human scores for the peer grading application. On the theory side, the use of language models has risks both for adversarial manipulation and errors. We show that our textual scoring rules are provably proper (or approximately proper when the language model may make errors), for both the general case and for our peer grading application. Our theoretical results for the general case include:

Properness

When the language model answers question-answering queries perfectly,
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
is perfectly proper.

Approximate Properness

When the error of the language model is bounded,
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
is approximately proper.

\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
for Peer Grading

We evaluate
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
on a peer grading dataset. We exploit two features of the peer grading dataset that allow more accurate question-answering, mapping reported text to uncertain numerical beliefs. First, the dataset is partitioned into
clusters. Each cluster corresponds to one homework assignment and contains reported peer reviews and ground truth instructor reviews of multiple submissions. Second, the textual information in the dataset demonstrates
know-it-or-not
beliefs, meaning a text indicates either always correct, or the belief is the same as prior (i.e. knowing nothing). These know-it-or-not beliefs are commonly seen in settings where the belief comes from a signal that provides evidence and fully reveals the ground truth. In a peer review, the opinions reported are supported with evidence from the homework. This special belief structure restricts a report with uncertainty to saying “I don’t know” or omitting discussion.

\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
for peer grading treats each clusters (i.e. homework assignment) separately and maps a report with uncertainty to the empirical frequency of the summarization state over a cluster. For each cluster,
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
constructs the prior distribution from the empirical distribution of the ground truth state vectors over instructor reviews of submissions to that assignment. “I don’t know” or omitted discussions are mapped to a numerical report of the empirical distribution.

By exploiting the special structure of the peer grading application, we obtain additional properness and adversarial robustness guarantees.

Properness

If the language model is non-inverting for reports, i.e. the probability that it identifies an opposite meaning of the text is less than
50\%
,
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
is perfectly proper.

Approximate Properness

If the error is bounded,
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
is approximately proper.

Adversarial Robustness

An uninformed peer can gain an expected payoff no more than truthfully reporting the prior distribution (i.e. “I don’t know”).

1.1
Algorithmic Artificial Intelligence

The technical construction of textual scoring rules of this paper is that of an algorithm that makes blackbox calls to a language model. We refer to these blackboxes as language model oracles. The paper introduces definitions to argue that these algorithms are correct even when the language model may not always be correct. This framework is inspired by the field of
human computation
, e.g.,
Law and Von Ahn (2011)
.

Algorithms with language model oracles make a series of basic computational steps and blackbox calls to the oracles before producing their output. Each basic computational step and oracle call can be based on the outcomes of previous computational steps and oracle calls. This is a standard oracle model of computation. This method of building a system using artificial intelligence contrasts from direct methods of querying the AI, a.k.a., prompt engineering, where the entire computational problem is expressed as a single prompt or a linear series of prompts. As the final output of this direct approach is a direct output of the language model, its correctness depends on the correctness of the language model for the complex task which would need to be established empirically. Moreover, methods for guaranteeing robustness to adversarial manipulations or properties of global outcomes of the algorithm like properness (for our textual scoring rule problem) are not possible with the current AI technology. For the peer grading example, we show that direct implementation of textual scoring rules with a language model is easy to manipulate.

An issue for algorithms with language model oracles is how errors in the oracle responses propagate through the algorithm and affect its correctness. For the design of scoring rules for text, we view the text as predicting a ground truth text. The key property that our textual scoring rule must satisfy is properness, that reporting the true belief is better than reporting anything else. The natural error model for probabilistic predictions in the text is that they have bounded error. For this error model, we show that the loss of properness is linear in the error, so a small error translates to a small loss in properness. Most text does not make probabilistic predictions, the natural error model for binary predictions (e.g., the truth of a statement) is the probability that the prediction is reversed. When this probability of reversal is strictly less than one-half, our textual scoring rule is proper.

We refer to such a study of algorithms with oracle calls to language models as algorithmic artificial intelligence. We believe that it is of independent interest to the field of economics and computation beyond our own study of scoring rules for text. It shares similarities with human computation (for specific comparisons, see the related work section). The closest comparable model to a language model answering domain-knowledge-free queries is a non-expert human. The design of systems that produce good outcomes with inputs from humans is central to economics and computation.

1.2
Introduction to Peer Grading

A central application for the methods of the paper is peer grading. In a course that uses peer grading, the students turn in submissions for a number of homework assignments and the students (in this role, called peers) also review each others submissions both quantitatively (by providing numeric scores) and qualitatively (by providing textual feedback) according to a multi-dimensional rubric. For an algorithms course the rubric may consist of “the algorithm”, “the analysis”, and “writing quality”.

There are several benefits of peer grading. First, peer grading improves the learning outcomes of students. Reading peers’ homework submission helps student learn from different perspectives as well as the mistakes and successes from their peers. Second, peer grading effectively scales the grading process. Distributing the grading tasks among students alleviates the workload of the instructor, which is especially helpful in a large class. However, peer grading also creates new grading tasks as the peer reviews, like any assigned work, must be graded.

To alleviate the additional grading burden, prior work of
Li et al. (2022)
developed numerical scoring rules for the peer grading application. It is more impactful, however, to grade the written feedback in peer reviews than it is to grade the numerical scores. First, it places the emphasis of the peer review task on giving good written feedback, where answering open-ended review questions improves learning more than fine-grained numerical grading tasks. Second, it can potentially be done more accurately. While the peer and instructor may disagree on the score to assign a submission, it is easier to agree more qualitatively on what the submission does well and where it has mistakes or needs improvement. Thus, generalizing scoring rules for numbers to scoring rules for text has the potential both to emphasize the right activities and to be more accurate in the assessment of the peer reviews. Note that the development of these scoring rules is critical to scaling of large courses via peer grading without increasing the grading workload of the instructor.

The peer grading application has special structure that facilitates the design of scoring rules for text. As discussed in the introduction, the framework for textual reviews assumes that the prompts (corresponding to homework submissions) and ground truth responses (corresponding to instructor reviews) are partitioned into clusters (for the purpose of calculating the prior distribution of the ground truth). The peer grading has a natural partitioning to clusters based on homework problems. Specifically, for each homework problem, the instructor grades multiple submissions. These submissions and the instructor review form a cluster. The prior distribution of the ground truth for such a cluster can be interpreted, for example, as specifying the frequency of mistakes in the submissions for this problem. This clustering allows the distribution of mistakes to be different for different homework problems. For example, in an algorithms class, submissions to a dynamic programming problem and a proof by induction will have a different distribution of mistakes.

n

The agent is scored by comparing the report

From the agent’s perspective, a scoring rule is proper if reporting their true belief gains a (weakly) higher expected score than any other reports. By definition, the report that maximizes the score is the Bayesian optimal report.

(Properness)

A scoring rule

{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim q}\left[S(\mu_{q};\theta)\right]\geq{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim q}\left[S(r;\theta)\right].

An approximately proper scoring rules ensures truthful reporting induces a loss that is at most
\epsilon

Definition 2

(Approximate Properness)
.

A scoring rule
S:R\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
is
\epsilon

q
of the agent with mean

{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim q}\left[S(\mu_{q};\theta)\right]\geq{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim q}\left[S(r;\theta)\right]-\epsilon.

2.1.2
Multi-dimensional Aggregations of Scoring Rules

An aggregation of scoring rules takes a set of scoring rules
S_{1},\dots,S_{n}
, reports
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}
, and a multi-dimensional state
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
as input, and outputs a real-valued score.

In this paper, we are interested in two basic multi-dimensional aggregations of proper scoring rules for mean elicitation: the average aggregation and the max-over-separate aggregation.
These basic aggregation methods can also be combined hierarchically. Moreover, an aggregation over proper scoring rules preserves the properness of a scoring rule.

Definition 5

(Multi-dimensional Aggregation)
.

An aggregation
A
of proper scoring rules takes as input scoring rules
S_{1},\dots,S_{n}
and corresponding reports
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}
. The aggregation outputs a menu of scores given the report, which is a function of the realized state,

S(r_{1},\dots,r_{n};\cdot)=A\big(S_{1}(r_{1};\cdot),\dots,S_{n}(r_{n};\cdot)\big),

aggregating input scoring rules. If the input scoring rules are proper, the aggregated score
S
is a proper scoring rule over the higher-dimensional space of
(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
.

In our paper, we implement two simple proper aggregation rules, the average aggregation and the max-over-separate (MOS ) aggregation. The average aggregation is defined as the average of input scores and is a standard approach in theory and practice.

Definition 6

(Average Aggregation)
.

Given scoring rules
S_{1},\dots,S_{n}
, an average scoring rule
S
is

S=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}.

Introduced by
Li et al. (2022)
, the max-over-separate aggregation scores the agent on the dimension for which the agent has highest expected score according to their posterior belief. The max-over-separate over V-shaped single-dimensional score is shown to be approximately optimal for incentivizing binary effort, and is unboundedly better than the optimal scoring rule under average aggregation.

Definition 7

(Max-over-separate Aggregation)
.

Given scoring rules
S_{1},\dots,S_{n}
, a max-over-separate aggregation
S
is

S=S_{i},\text{where }i=\argmax_{i}\nolimits{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta_{i}\sim r_{i}}\left[S_{i}\right].

3
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
: Scoring Text with Oracle Access to LLM

In this section, we present
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
, an implementation of scoring rules for text, via queries to large language models (LLMs). The mechanism takes a textual report as input, compares with the ground truth text, and outputs a score of the reported text. Our goal is to design the scoring rule, via queries to a language model, to incentivize the agent to truthfully report their belief about binary states in text. Especially, when the language model makes errors, we design our scoring rule to be robust to errors.

\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
reduces the textual information elicitation problem to the numerical information elicitation.
We model LLMs as oracles with the fundamental functionalities of
summarization
and
question answering
. We present the algorithm via queries to these oracles. This oracle abstraction allows the main ideas of the algorithm and its properties to be studied without
details of prompt engineering tricks in implementation. For example, the properness of the textual elicitation mechanism is clearly inherited from the proper scoring rule from which it is constructed. In
Section

4
, we analyze the robustness of the scoring rule to errors of the language oracle.

3.1
General Reduction

There are two main challenges in reducing textual information elicitation to numerical information elicitation. First, unlike a set of known states in numerical elicitation, the text is open-ended and consists of implicit indicators for summary points, i.e. opinions. Second, although text may convey information with uncertainty
(
Belém et al., 2024
)
, it is unclear how to identify the uncertainty from the text.

To deal with the challenges above, in our oracle model, the LLM is able to accept queries to two fundamental tasks: the summarization oracle, to identify states for elicitation; and the question-answering oracle, to identify the numerical indicators for summary points from text.
We refer to oracle queries as
O_{S}
and
O_{A}
as defined below. Note that we define a question-answering oracle
O_{A,\theta}
that only identifies a state with certainty from ground truth texts, and another question-answering oracle
O_{A,r}
that outputs a numerical report with uncertainty from reported text. We write
\hat{r}

An important property of numerical scoring rules is properness, i.e., incentivizing the forecaster to report their true beliefs. Note that the summarization oracle does not affect the properness of

\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}

Related Work

Textual Elicitation

Motivated by the recent development of language models, several papers
aim to design scoring mechanism to evaluate models truthfully.

Kimpara et al. (2023)
view a language model as producing a
distribution over responses and design scoring mechanisms for
evaluating this distribution from independent samples. While the
scoring mechanism in
Kimpara et al. (2023)
evaluate the quality
of the distribution where the textual report is drawn, our scoring
rule evaluates the quality of the textual response itself, where
uncertainty can be expressed directly in the text. Independently and
concurrently,
Lu et al. (2024)
consider peer prediction with textual
reports, where responses are evaluated in comparison to peer responses
rather than ground truth responses. They use a pre-trained language model to interpret text as a probabilistic report of peer responses, and use a proper scoring rule to evaluate against the peer responses instead of the ground truth. The goal in their paper is to distinguish between GPT generated reviews and human written reviews, where they find predicting the next word is better than scoring the semantic meaning. However, our goal is to align with human preference, where scoring next word has been known to be bad on out-of-sample report such as “I don’t know”. Moreover, there are two reasons why their evaluation of scoring semantic meaning can be bad. First, they directly ask GPT to interpret text as probabilities of semantic meaning, where inaccurate GPT prior significantly reduces the performance. Instead, our approach of dataset partitioning allows us to access accurate prior. Second, they only test the log scoring rule, while our paper compares different scoring rules and shows applying filtering to semantic meaning significantly improves the alignment performance.

Grading with LLMs

Language models have very recently been studied for their use in grading textual responses of students.
Gao et al. (2023)
compare several language models using an approach based on tokenizing both student answers and ground truth and then comparing the vector representations with cosine similarity. They show that the approach works well for binary grading of simple questions (quiz questions) but not as well on multi-point grading of questions with more complex answers (activity questions).
Schneider et al. (2023)
consider prompting the language model to both evaluate the quality of answers directly and compare answers of students to ground truth answers, each on a multi-point scale. In the latter, the model is given only the student answer and ground truth answer (not the question). They find that there is little (Pearson) correlation between instructor grades of the student answers and the closeness reported by the language model of the student and instructor answers. In contrast to this approach, our work constructs a multi-dimensional scoring rule from an analysis of several instructor answers (i.e., instructor reviews) to similar questions (i.e., submissions of the same assignment) and then evaluates a student’s answer (i.e., peer review) according to the surprising dimensions. We have favorable results, presented subsequently.

Characterization of Proper Scoring Rules

There is an extensive literature that develops (numerical)
proper scoring rules
where forecasters are incentivized to report their true beliefs. These proper scoring rules are characterized for eliciting beliefs over multi-dimensional states by
McCarthy (1956)
and for multi-dimensional elicitation of the mean by
Abernethy and Frongillo (2012)
.
Lambert (2011)
characterized statistics that are directly elicitable.

Optimization of Scoring Rules

Surprisingly, until recently there was relatively little work on optimizing scoring rules subject to properness (and boundedness). Motivated by peer grading,
Li, Hartline, Shan and Wu (2022)
optimizes scoring rules for binary effort where a peer either (with no effort) reports a prior belief or exerts costly effort to obtain and report a posterior belief.
Hartline, Shan, Li and Wu (2023)
generalize the model to allow multi-dimensional effort as a knapsack problem for scoring rule optimization. Our paper adapts the scoring rules identified by these papers to scoring text. Additional work on scoring rule optimization includes
Papireddygari and Waggoner (2022)
, which considers connections between optimizing scoring rules and contract theory; and
Chen and Yu (2021)
, which relaxes the assumption that the prior is known to the designer.

Human Computation

A central question in the field of human computation is how algorithms can solve complex problems by organizing them into small tasks that are assigned to humans. For example, Soylent is a Microsoft Word plugin that can be used to conduct standard editing tasks on text documents
(
Bernstein et al., 2010
)
. Tasks it supports include proofreading and shortening. In this computational model, humans are used to perform straightforward comparisons between text segments or generations of short text segments. They are not asked for domain knowledge. A key concern in such systems is how to get reliable results when individual human answers may be unreliable. For example, Soylent employs a find-fix-verify paradigm that collects responses from humans to
find
places in the text that could be shortened,
fix
the text by shortening it, and
verify
that the shortened text have the same meaning. For additional background see the short survey of
Miller et al. (2010)
or book of
Law and Von Ahn (2011)
. Our method of constructing a scoring rule for text via oracle calls to answer simple domain-knowledge-free questions about text parallels the canonical model of human computation. Unlike the models of human computation we find, for our task of grading peer reviews, that prompting a language model for text analysis and comparisons performs well without building in explicit methods for error detection and correction.

Mechanism Design with LLMs

There are other potential connections between mechanism design and large language models. For example,
Duetting et al. (2023)
consider a setting where agents are in competition to create some text, like companies creating a shared advertisement, and each agent prefers the outcome of their own language model. They define a token auction model and within it a second-price auction.

, a weighted average over homework scores, peer grading scores, exam scores, etc., reflecting the peers’ ground truth performance.

We compute the correlation of peers’ ranking with our two metrics, since the algorithmic scores are not on the same scale with instructor scores.
We calculate the average score of each individual peer throughout the quarter, sort them by this average, and use Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (Spearman’s correlation) to compare two rankings. The Spearman’s correlation calculates the average quadratic distance of each item in two rankings. The average quadratic distance is normalized such that perfectly aligning rankings have correlation
1
, reversed rankings have correlation
-1
, and independent draws have expected correlation
0
.

For correlation with our two metrics (instructor score and overall student grades), we test four scoring rules for textual review as introduced in

. To understand the performance of
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}

on textual reviews. In

A.2
Scoring Rules We Evaluate

We describe the scoring rules that we evaluate for textual elicitation here.

Hierarchical Aggregation

Aggregation methods can be combined in a hierarchy, for example, we will be evaluating two level hierarchies that partition the dimensions and apply max-over-separate in each partition and then average across partitions. Max-over-separate is better for incentives while average aggregation is less noisy; hierarchical aggregation combines the good factors of both.

Definition 14

(Aggregation Hierarchy)
.

A scoring rule with aggregation hierarchy is represented as a tree. In the tree, all terminal nodes on the tree have the same depths from the root node. Each terminal node corresponds to a single-dimensional scoring rule (V-shaped or quadratic). On each level, each node represents the same aggregation, which takes input a set of scoring rules
S_{1},\dots,S_{z}
in its child nodes, and outputs one score.

The scoring rule with an aggregation hierarchy is named after the aggregation methods, with A for the average aggregation, and M for the max-over-separate aggregation. For example, AQ stands for average aggregation (A) over quadratic single-dimensional scores (Q). AMV stands for an aggregation hierarchy with two levels, where the terminal nodes are V-shaped single-dimensional scores (V), grouped and connected to parent nodes for max-over-separate aggregation (M), and the root is the average aggregation (A) over the max-over-separate scores.

Additional Aggregation for Text: Filtered Average Aggregation

In addition to the aggregation method in
Section

2.1.2
, we introduce the filtered average aggregation specifically for text. The filtered average aggregation skips dimensions of cheap signals and scores only a subset of summary points. The cheap signal problem comes from the high-dimensional nature of summary points in text
[
Brooks et al., 2022
,
Kong and Schoenebeck, 2018
]
. For example, a review segment commenting on clarity or length is less important than on correctness of proof. While peers can leave a review only on submission length without much effort, the score given by the instructor usually does not depend on the cheap signal of length. Thus, the states
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{m})
are not equally important for scoring textual report quality, and the less important ones need to be filtered.

Our filtering method identifies important summary points for scoring by partitioning the states by semantic meanings into topics
\bm{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{z})
. For example,
three states
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}
for correct hypothesis, base case, and induction step in proof can be grouped into topic
t_{1}
for the proof correctness. State
\theta_{4}
for typos can be grouped into topic
t_{2}
of the overall clarity. Intuitively, the number of different indicator states within a topic is positively related to a high cost to obtain information on that topic. The filtering approach ignores reports on the topics with fewer summary points, instead scoring only the top two topics with the most summary points (i.e., the most dimensions to report).

The filtered average aggregation (AF) with top two topics is defined as the following.

Definition 15

(Filtered Average, AF)
.

Given scoring rules
S_{1},\dots,S_{z}
, and
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
as the indices of the remaining topics after filtering. A filtered average aggregation outputs
S=\frac{1}{2}\left[S_{i_{1}}+S_{i_{2}}\right]
.

In the definition,
z
is the total number of topics and
S_{1},\dots,S_{z}
are multi-dimensional scoring rules that can be applied to multiple indicator states in each topic.

For textual scoring, we use the V-shaped single dimensional scoring rules for each indicator state on terminal nodes in the aggregation hierarchy.

AV

The average scoring rule of V-shaped on each indicator state.

AMV

The average of the max-over-separate scoring rule applied to each topic.

AFV

Apply V-shaped scoring rules on each indicator state, filter by topics, and output the average score over the remaining indicator states.

AFMV

Apply V-shaped scoring rules on each indicator state, group the states by topics, apply the MOS scoring rule to indicator states in each topic, filter by topics, output the average score over the remaining topics.

C.1
Implementing
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
with Context-Constrained Language Oracles

Our oracle model abstracts basic functionalities away from the engineering constraints. The main constraint we face in real implementation is the context length constraint, which we address in this section. For example, when LLM receives the question answering query of the reported text, the conversation history includes all summarization queries and question answering queries to the ground truth texts, usually leading to insufficiency of the context length. As example, GPT-4 has a maximum context length of
32k
tokens, and GPT-3.5-turbo-16k has a maximum context length of
16k
. In practice, the LLMs are not able to process the conversation history of all queries. Moreover, for each query, the cost is linearly related to the length of the conversation history. Thus, the total monetary cost of each task has a quadratic dependency on the number of queries in the conversation history. Our implementation deploys tricks to save context length of queries and monetary cost.

Our algorithm saves context length by reusing history. We modify the abstractions of oracles to present our implementation of
\text{Elicitation}^{\text{GPT}}
. The main modification is an additional input of conversation history to the oracle, which shows how we pass the conversation history and reduce context length.
