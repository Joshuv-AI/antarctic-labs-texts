Orthonormal wave functions for periodic fermionic states under an applied magnetic Field
Edinardo I. B. Rodrigues, Mauro M. Doria

Abstract
We report an infinite number of orthonormal wave functions bases for the quantum problem of a free particle in presence of an applied external magnetic field. Each set of orthonormal wave functions (basis) is labeled by an integer p p , which is the number of magnetic fluxons trapped in the unit cell. These bases are suitable to describe particles whose probability density is periodic and defines a lattice in position space. The present bases of orthonormal wave functions unveils fractional effects since the number of particles in the unit cell is independent of the number of trapped fluxons. For a single particle under p p fluxes in the unit cell, and confined to the lowest Landau level, the probability density vanishes in p p points, thus each zero is associated to a fraction 1 / p 1/p of the particle. Remarkably the case of n + 1 n+1 filled Landau levels, hence with a total of N = ( n + 1 ) ​ p N=(n+1)p fermions, n n being the highest filled Landau level, the density displays an egg-box pattern with p 2 p^{2} maxima (minima) which means that a ( n + 1 ) / p (n+1)/p fraction of flux is associated to every one of these maxima (minima). We also consider the case of particles interacting through the magnetic field energy created by their own motion and find an attractive interaction among them in case they are confined to the lowest Landau level ( n = 0 n=0 ). The well-known de Haas-van Alphen oscillations are retrieved within the present orthonormal basis of wave functions thus providing evidence of its correctness.

III
Kinetic Energy of free fermions in a magnetic field

In this section we introduce the second quantization formalism to treat the free fermions in a magnetic field.
The completeness of the set of wave functions under fixed
p
is verified since it is necessary to prove the anti-commutation relations in real space.
For non-interacting fermions the Hamiltonian is given by,

\mathcal{H}=K,

(31)

where the kinetic energy,
K
, is,

K=\int d^{2}x\frac{1}{2m}\left(\vec{D}\Psi\right)^{{\dagger}}\left(\vec{D}\Psi\right).

(32)

\Psi
is a second quantized field and we aim to fill energy levels according to the exclusion principle up to the Fermi surface.
We express the kinetic energy in a more convenient way,

K=\frac{1}{2m}\int\!\!d^{2}x\left(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\vec{D}^{2}\Psi\right)+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m}\int\!\!d^{2}x\,\vec{\nabla}^{2}\rho,

(33)

where
\rho\equiv\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi
.
It happens that the second integral vanishes due to the periodicity of the state, and the kinetic energy becomes,

K=\frac{1}{2m}\int\!\!d^{2}x\left(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\vec{D}^{2}\Psi\right).

(34)

To derive Eq. (
33
) we write
\left(\vec{D}\Psi\right)^{{\dagger}}\left(\vec{D}\Psi\right)=\left(i\hbar\vec{\nabla}\Psi^{{\dagger}}-(e/c)\vec{A}\Psi^{{\dagger}}\right)\cdot\vec{\alpha}
, with
\vec{\alpha}\equiv\vec{D}\Psi
.
Using that
\vec{\nabla}\Psi^{{\dagger}}\cdot\vec{\alpha}=\vec{\nabla}\cdot(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\vec{\alpha})-\Psi^{{\dagger}}(\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{\alpha})
,
one gets that

\left(\vec{D}\Psi\right)^{{\dagger}}\left(\vec{D}\Psi\right)=\Psi^{{\dagger}}\left(-i\hbar\vec{\nabla}-(e/c)\vec{A}\right)\cdot\vec{\alpha}+i\hbar\vec{\nabla}\cdot(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\vec{\alpha}).

The kinetic energy becomes,

K=\frac{1}{2m}\int d^{2}x\left(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\vec{D}^{2}\Psi\right)+\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\int d^{2}x\,\vec{\nabla}\cdot\left(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\vec{D}\Psi\right),

(35)

and its complex conjugate,

K^{*}=\frac{1}{2m}\int d^{2}x\left(\vec{D}^{2}\Psi\right)^{{\dagger}}\Psi-\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\int d^{2}x\,\vec{\nabla}\cdot\left((\vec{D}\Psi)^{{\dagger}}\Psi\right).

(36)

The kinetic energy is real thus summing Eqs. (
35
) e (
36
) and dividing by two, gives that,

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4m}\int d^{2}x\Psi^{{\dagger}}\vec{D}^{2}\Psi+\left(\vec{D}^{2}\Psi\right)^{{\dagger}}\Psi

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{i\hbar}{4m}\int d^{2}x\,\vec{\nabla}\cdot\left(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\vec{D}\Psi-\Psi(\vec{D}\Psi)^{{\dagger}}\right).

The integration scheme and the definition of the vector
\vec{n}
perpendicular to the surface.

The two terms in the first integral are shown to be equal,
\Psi^{{\dagger}}\vec{D}^{2}\Psi=\left(\vec{D}^{2}\Psi\right)^{{\dagger}}\Psi
, and in the second integral it holds that

\Psi^{{\dagger}}\vec{D}\Psi-(\vec{D}\Psi)^{{\dagger}}\Psi=\vec{\nabla}\left(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi\right)
.
In this way we obtain the symmetrized kinetic energy given in Eq. (
33
).

The second integral (
33
) simply vanishes in case of a periodic state, as shown below.
We write it as a surface integral,

\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m}\int\!\!d^{2}x\;\vec{\nabla}^{2}\rho=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m}\oint\left(\vec{\nabla}\rho\right)\cdot\vec{n}dl,

(37)

where
\vec{n}
is a vector normal to the border lines of the unit cell where the above integration is taken and performed.
using the theorem
\oint_{c}\vec{F}\cdot\vec{n}dl=\int_{S}\,\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{F}d^{2}x
for a vector function
\vec{F}
in the plane
(x_{1},x_{2})
.
The integral of Eq. (
37
) is illustrated in Fig.

.
Notice that the line integrals along the path

is obtained by taking
\vec{n}=-\hat{x}_{2}
for
x_{2}=0
, and
\vec{n}=\hat{x}_{2}
for
x_{2}=L_{2}
.
The integrals along the path
dl=dx_{2}
have
\vec{n}=-\hat{x}_{1}
for
x_{1}=0
and
\vec{n}=\hat{x}_{1}
for

\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m}\oint\left(\vec{\nabla}\rho\right)\cdot\vec{n}dl=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m}\left\{\int^{L_{1}}_{0}dx_{1}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}\rho(x_{1},L_{2})-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}\rho(x_{1},0)\right)+\int^{L_{2}}_{0}dx_{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\rho(L_{1},x_{2})-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\rho(0,x_{2})\right)\right\}.

(38)

Recall that
\rho
is periodic, and so, the integrals in opposite sides of the rectangular unit cell annihilate each other, resulting that,

\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m}\oint\;\left(\vec{\nabla}\rho\right)\cdot\vec{n}\;dl=0.

(39)

Next we introduce the second quantization formalism to treat the fermionic particles.
Notice that the second quantized field
\Psi_{p}(x_{1},x_{2})
carries the index
p
to stress the
p

L_{1}L_{2}
.

\Psi_{p}(x_{1},x_{2})\!=\!\!\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\Psi_{n,p}(x_{1},x_{2})\!=\!\!\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\sum^{p-1}_{q=0}c_{n,q}\phi_{n,q}(x_{1},x_{2},p),

(40)

where the functions
\phi_{n,q}(x_{1},x_{2},p)
are given by Eq. (
23
) and the coefficients

are to be interpreted as destruction operators below.
The second quantized field
\Psi_{p}(x_{1},x_{2})
satisfies the anti-commutation relation,

\displaystyle\left\{\Psi_{p}(x_{1},x_{2}),\Psi^{{\dagger}}_{p}(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2})\right\}=\delta(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})\delta(x_{2}-x^{\prime}_{2}),\;\mbox{and}

(41)

\displaystyle\left\{\Psi_{p}(x_{1},x_{2}),\Psi_{p}(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2})\right\}=0,

(42)

assuming that the operators
c_{n,q}
obey the conditions below.

\displaystyle\left\{c_{n,q}\,,\,c^{{\dagger}}_{m,q^{\prime}}\right\}=\delta_{qq^{\prime}}\delta_{nm},

(43)

\displaystyle\left\{c_{n,q}\,,\,c_{m,q^{\prime}}\right\}=0.

(44)

The completeness relation of the orthonormal set defined by
p
is behind Eq. (
41
) and to prove we start with Eq. (
40
).
Using Eq. (
43
) it follows that,

\displaystyle\left\{\Psi_{p}(x_{1},x_{2}),\Psi^{{\dagger}}_{p}\left(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2}\right)\right\}=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\sum^{p-1}_{q=0}\phi_{n,q}(x_{1},x_{2},p)\phi^{*}_{n,q}(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},p),

(45)

and then,

\displaystyle\left\{\Psi_{p}(x_{1},x_{2}),\Psi^{{\dagger}}_{p}\left(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2}\right)\right\}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi p}{L^{3}_{1}L_{2}}}\sum^{p-1}_{q=0}\sum^{+\infty}_{l=-\infty}e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L_{1}}(pl+q)x_{1}}\sum^{+\infty}_{l^{\prime}=-\infty}e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{L_{1}}(pl^{\prime}+q)x^{\prime}_{1}}\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}b^{2}_{n}H_{n}(f_{lq})H_{n}(f^{\prime}_{l^{\prime}q})e^{-\frac{1}{2}(f_{lq}^{2}+f^{\prime 2}_{l^{\prime}q})}.

(46)

The latter is obtained by using

, defined by Eqs. (

) and (

), where

Firstly consider the sum in

are the Hermite polynomials.
Hence the

satisfy

and the corresponding completeness relation is
\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\psi_{n}(x)\psi_{n}(y)=\delta(x-y)
.
Thus the sum over
n
in Eq. (

\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}b^{2}_{n}H_{n}(f_{lq})H_{n}(f^{\prime}_{l^{\prime}q})e^{-\frac{1}{2}(f_{lq}^{2}+f^{\prime 2}_{l^{\prime}q})}=\delta(f_{lq}-f^{\prime}_{l^{\prime}q}),

and using Eq. (
24
), one
obtains that
\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}b^{2}_{n}H_{n}(f_{lq})H_{n}(f^{\prime}_{l^{\prime}q})e^{-\frac{1}{2}(f_{lq}^{2}+f^{\prime 2}_{l^{\prime}q})}=\delta\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\pi p}{L_{1}L_{2}}}(x_{2}-x^{\prime}_{2})+L_{2}(l-l^{\prime})\right)
. Considering that
x_{2}
e

are independent variables it holds that the delta function is centered at

and

.
Therefore we have the following completeness relation.

(47)

where we have used the property
\delta(a(x_{2}-x^{\prime}_{2}))=\frac{1}{a}\delta(x_{2}-x^{\prime}_{2})

.
Using Eq. (

(48)

Next the sum
S_{p}=\sum^{p-1}_{q=0}\sum^{+\infty}_{l=-\infty}e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L_{1}}(pl+q)(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})}
must be analysed. For

S_{1}=\sum^{+\infty}_{l=-\infty}e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L_{1}}l(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})}
, for

.
Notice that the total sum runs over all integers, and in this way, one can write that

S_{p}=\sum^{p-1}_{q=0}\sum^{+\infty}_{l=-\infty}e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L_{1}}(pl+q)(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})}=\sum^{+\infty}_{r=-\infty}e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L_{1}}r(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})}.

Hence we have reached the second completeness relation,

S_{p}=\sum^{+\infty}_{r=-\infty}e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L_{1}}r(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})}=L_{1}\delta\!\left(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1}\right),

that once added to Eq. (

that determines the number of occupied Landau levels according to the number of particles.

\nu=n^{\prime}+\lambda.

(60)

Notice that
\nu
and
\lambda
are continuous variables whereas
n^{\prime}
is a discrete variable.
A new critical field
H^{\prime}_{3}
ia defined associated to the density of particles,
N/A
,

H^{\prime}_{3}\equiv\frac{N}{A}\Phi_{0},

(61)

where
A=L_{1}L_{2}
is the unit cell area.
As the applied field
H_{3}
is associated to
p
according to Eq. (
17
), and Eq. (
59
) holds, one obtains that,

\nu=\frac{H^{\prime}_{3}}{H_{3}}.

(62)

Combining Eqs. (
5
), (
18
), (
62
) and (
61
), one obtains a new way to write the filling factor,
\nu
.

\nu=\frac{2\pi N\hbar^{2}}{Am}\frac{1}{\hbar\omega_{c}}.

(63)

The Landau levels are filled in multiples of
H^{\prime}_{3}
since
n^{\prime}=\left[\frac{H^{\prime}_{3}}{H_{3}}\right]
, and the incomplete filling
\lambda
becomes,

\lambda=\frac{H^{\prime}_{3}}{H_{3}}-\left[\frac{H^{\prime}_{3}}{H_{3}}\right].

(64)

The notation
[\alpha]
means to take the largest equal or smaller integer contained in a number
\alpha
.

Further physical insight about the filling factor
\nu
is achieved by introducing the magnetic length
l_{0}=\sqrt{\hbar c/eH_{3}}
.
This length describes the semi-classical orbit of the particle in presence of the magnetic field.
It stems from the centripetal force,
mv^{2}/r=qvH_{3}/c
, where
v
is the velocity and
r
the radius of the circular orbit.
Added to Bohr’s angular momentum condition,
mvr=n_{0}\hbar
, where
n_{0}
is an integer, it leads that the orbits are quantized,
r=\sqrt{n_{0}}l_{0}
.
Two possible areas are associated to the particle, namely, the disk associated to the magnetic orbit,
\pi l_{0}^{2}
, and the area defined by the electronic density,
A/N
.
The filling factor corresponds to their ratio,
\nu=2\pi l_{0}^{2}/(A/N)
.
Previously we found that the filling factor is large,
\nu\gg 1
, in case that many Landau levels are filled,
n^{\prime}\gg 1
.
Therefore in this case there are many overlapping orbits and this semi-classical picture no longer holds and should be abandoned.
This shows that the present lattice state is beyond the semi-classical view.

The total energy of the fermionic particles requires the summation over all levels and this is readily obtained from the Hamiltonian of Eq. (
52
) by computing the energy expectation value,

\displaystyle\hskip-4.26773ptE_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Phi|\mathcal{H}|\Phi\rangle=\left(E_{0}p+E_{1}p+\ldots+E_{n^{\prime}-1}p\right)+E_{n^{\prime}}p^{\prime}

(65)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p\sum^{n^{\prime}-1}_{n=0}E_{n}+E_{n^{\prime}}p^{\prime},

), and
E_{n^{\prime}}=\hbar\omega_{c}\left(n^{\prime}+1/2\right)
.
In summary, total energy
E_{p}
takes the contribution of

, added to the highest level,
n^{\prime}

Introducing Eq. (

(66)

The energy

is shown to be explicitly periodic with respect to the filling factor
\nu
, or to its inverse
\nu^{-1}
, the latter being proportional to the applied field
H_{3}
.
We combine the Eqs. (

\epsilon(H_{3})\equiv\frac{E_{p}}{N}=\left(\frac{n^{\prime 2}}{2}+\left(n^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda\right)\frac{\hbar\omega_{c}}{\nu}.

(67)

Using Eq. (
63
) one obtains that,

\epsilon(H_{3})=\epsilon(0)+\frac{\hbar\omega_{c}}{2\nu}\lambda(1-\lambda),

(68)

where
\epsilon(0)
is the energy per particle in the absence of field,

\epsilon(0)=\frac{N\pi\hbar^{2}}{Am}.

(69)

This is done by firstly casting the energy as
\epsilon(H_{3})=\left(n^{\prime 2}+2n^{\prime}\lambda+\lambda\right)\frac{\hbar\omega_{c}}{2\nu}
, and then,
adding a term
\pm\lambda^{2}
inside the parenthesis to obtain that
\epsilon(H_{3})=\left((n^{\prime}+\lambda)^{2}+\lambda-\lambda^{2}\right)\frac{\hbar\omega_{c}}{2\nu}
.
Next using that
\nu=n^{\prime}+\lambda
, Eq. (
60
), one obtains that,

\epsilon(H_{3})=\frac{1}{2}\nu\hbar\omega_{c}+\frac{\hbar\omega_{c}}{2\nu}\lambda(1-\lambda).

(70)

We briefly review the derivation of the average energy per particle without the applied field, given by Eq. (
69

This can be achieved by direct arguments without the inclusion of the applied field.
By integrating over the Fermi surface disk, one obtains that
N/A=k_{F}^{2}/4\pi
and that the total energy per particle is
E_{T}/N=E_{F}/2
,
E_{F}=(\hbar k_{F})^{2}/2m
.
Hence one finds the zero field energy per particle of Eq. (
69
), that is,
E_{T}/N=\epsilon(0)
.
Hence the ratio between the energy in presence of a field,
\epsilon(H_{3})
, and without a field,
\epsilon(0)
, becomes,

\frac{\epsilon(H_{3})}{\epsilon(0)}=1+\left(\frac{H_{3}}{H^{\prime}_{3}}\right)^{2}\lambda(1-\lambda).

(71)

The above expression is plotted in Fig.

and shows the oscillations with respect to the filling factor.
Since

the left plot shows that the period of oscillations increases for increasing

.
The amplitude of oscillations smoothly increases proportional to
H_{3}^{2}
according to Eq. (

), while
\lambda
is a periodic function of
1/H_{3}
.
The same data is also plotted in the right plot, in this case with respect to
\nu
, and both plots show a periodicity.

The magnetization
M
is obtained from,

M=-\frac{\partial\epsilon(H_{3})}{\partial H_{3}},

(72)

where
\epsilon(H_{3})
is given by Eq. (
68
), that once combined with Eqs. (

) and (

), gives that,

\epsilon(H_{3})=\mu_{B}\left\{\frac{2\pi\hbar cN}{Ae}+\frac{H^{2}_{3}}{H^{\prime}_{3}}\left(\frac{H^{\prime}_{3}}{H_{3}}-\left[\frac{H^{\prime}_{3}}{H_{3}}\right]\right)\left(1-\frac{H^{\prime}_{3}}{H_{3}}-\left[\frac{H^{\prime}_{3}}{H_{3}}\right]\right)\right\},

(73)

where
\mu_{B}
is Bohr’s magneton.

\mu_{B}=\frac{e\hbar}{2mc}.

(74)

M/\mu_{B}
is obtained by deriving Eq. (

) with respect to
H_{3}
.

\displaystyle\frac{M}{\mu_{B}}=1-2\lambda-\frac{2\lambda(1-\lambda)}{\lambda+n^{\prime}}.

(75)

Fig.

plots the magnetization versus

as given by Eq. (

).
Notice that although the energy is a continuous function of

, its derivative is discontinuous when the Landau level is totally filled.
Consider the field

In this neighborhood there is a change in

, as shown in Fig.

.

In case many Landau levels are filled, namely case

, Eq. (
75
) becomes,

\displaystyle\frac{M}{\mu_{B}}\approx 1-2\lambda,

(76)

which shows that the magnetization
M
is a periodic function of
1/H_{3}
with the period given by,

\displaystyle\Delta\left(\frac{1}{H_{3}}\right)=\frac{1}{H^{\prime}_{3}}=\frac{A}{N\Phi_{0}}.

(77)

Using that

(spinless fermions) we retrieve the de Haas-van Alphen result that the oscillations are inversely proportional to the

\displaystyle\Delta\left(\frac{1}{H_{3}}\right)=\frac{2\pi e}{\hbar c}\frac{1}{A_{F}}.

(78)

III.4
The attractive magnetic interaction among particles confined to the lowest Landau level

In this section we include interaction among particles confined to the lowest Landau level.
The motion of the particles due to the external field make them generate currents which create a local magnetic field causing a mutual interaction among them.
We prove here that this magnetic interaction is attractive and to treat it the field energy is incorporated into the hamiltonian, and added to the kinetic energy such that,

\mathcal{H}=K+F,

(79)

where the kinetic energy,
K
, is,

K=\int d^{3}x\frac{1}{2m}\sum^{2}_{j=1}\left(D_{j}\Psi\right)^{{\dagger}}\left(D_{j}\Psi\right),

(80)

and the field energy,
F
, is,

F=\int d^{3}x\frac{1}{8\pi}\left(\vec{h}(\Psi)-\vec{H}\right)^{2}.

(81)

The local magnetic field is also a second quantized field,
\vec{h}(\Psi)
, obtained by solving Ampère’s law,

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{h}(\Psi)=\frac{4\pi}{c}\vec{J}(\Psi),\!\quad\vec{J}(\Psi)=\frac{e}{2m}\!\left(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\vec{D}\Psi+c.c.\right),

(82)

by knowledge of the current. A special attention must be paid to the dimensionality of the fields, as the magnetic field and the the current are tied to each other through Ampère’s law,
which assigns to
\Psi
the dimensionality
[\Psi]=1/\sqrt{V}
, where
V=AL_{3}
is a volume, the product of the unit cell area in the plane,
A
, times a length along the direction of the applied field,
L_{3}
.
This is consistent with the energy being an integral over three-dimensional space, but uniaxial symmetry along the direction of the external applied field renders the problem two-dimensional.
All the fields only depend on
(x_{1},x_{2})
, including the local field is
\vec{h}(\Psi)=\hat{x}_{3}h_{3}(\Psi)
,
h_{3}=\partial_{1}A_{2}-\partial_{2}A_{1}
.
For the applied field we choose, as before, that
A_{3}=0
and
\vec{H}=\hat{x}_{3}H_{3}
.

Remarkably Ampère’s law is solved exactly and the local magnetic field
h_{3}(\Psi)
fully determined.
To achive this purpose we introduce a dual view of the kinetic energy that requires the operators,

D_{\pm}\equiv D_{1}\pm iD_{2}.

(83)

We use the following identity

\displaystyle\left(D_{+}\Psi\right)^{{\dagger}}\left(D_{+}\Psi\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{2}_{j=1}\left(D_{j}\Psi\right)^{{\dagger}}\left(D_{j}\Psi\right)+i\Psi^{{\dagger}}[D_{1},D_{2}]\Psi

(84)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar m}{e}\left(\partial_{1}J_{2}-\partial_{2}J_{1}\right),

where the current components
J_{i}
,
i=1
e
2
, are given by Eq. (
82
), and the commutation relation is given by,

[D_{1},D_{2}]=-\frac{e\hbar}{ic}h_{3}.

(85)

Combining Eqs. (
80
), (
84
), (
85
), and using that,

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar m}{e}\left(\partial_{1}J_{2}-\partial_{2}J_{1}\right)=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2}\vec{\nabla}^{2}(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi),

(86)

one obtains that,

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046ptK

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\!d^{3}x\left(\frac{|D_{+}\Psi|^{2}}{2m}+\frac{e\hbar}{2mc}h_{3}\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi\right)

(87)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m}\int d^{3}x\vec{\nabla}^{2}(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi),

where the last term is a surface term.
The dual formulation of the kinetic energy also leads to a dual formulation of the current, which is obtained by varying the kinetic energy with respect to the vector potential,

\delta F_{k}=\cdots-\frac{1}{c}\int d^{2}x\vec{J}\cdot\delta\vec{A}
.
Therefore one obtains the components of the current in the plane.

\displaystyle J_{1}=\frac{e}{2m}\big[\Psi^{{\dagger}}\big(D_{+}\Psi\big)+\big(D_{+}\Psi\big)^{{\dagger}}\Psi\big]-\frac{e\hbar}{2m}\partial_{2}\left(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi\right),

(88)

e

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046ptJ_{2}=\frac{e}{2im}\big[\Psi^{{\dagger}}\big(D_{+}\Psi\big)-\big(D_{+}\Psi\big)^{{\dagger}}\Psi\big]+\frac{e\hbar}{2m}\partial_{1}\left(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi\right).

(89)

The confinement to the lowest Landau leads to a full solution of Ampère’s law. The axial symmetry gives that
\partial_{2}h_{3}=(4\pi/c)J_{1}
e
\partial_{1}h_{3}=-(4\pi/c)J_{2}
.
Then
h_{3}+(2\pi e\hbar/mc)|\Psi|^{2}=\mbox{constant}
, and this constant is determined by the condition that the local field must be equal to the applied field,
h_{3}=H_{3}
, in case that
\Psi=0
.
We have found that there are first order equations that link
\Psi
to
h_{3}
, in the following way.

\displaystyle D_{+}\Psi=0,

(90)

and

\displaystyle h_{3}(\Psi)=H_{3}-4\pi\mu_{B}\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi,

(91)

where
\mu_{B}
is Bohr’s magneton, Eq. (
74
).
The first order equations are expected to be a useful approximation to the solution in case that
h_{3}\ll H_{3}
.
For this reason they are only solved approximately in the following way.
Firstly Eq. (
90
) is solved for
\Psi
assuming that the vector potential is only due to
H_{3}
.
Thus corrections to vector potential are not considered in this stage.
Then the local magnetic field,
h_{3}
, is obtained from Eq. (
90
), which leads to corrections to
H_{3}
in terms of
\Psi
.
The general solution for
\Psi
, given by Eq. (
40
), is the
n=0
part of the general solution, since
D_{+}\Psi_{0,p}=0
.

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,p}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{3}}}\sum^{p-1}_{q=0}c_{0,q}\phi_{0,q}(x_{1},x_{2},p).

(92)

where

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84544pt\phi_{0,q}(x_{1},x_{2},p)=\!\left(\frac{2\pi p}{L^{3}_{1}L_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\sum^{+\infty}_{l=-\infty}\!e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L_{1}}(pl+q)x_{1}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}f_{lq}^{2}},

(93)

with
f_{lq}
defined in (
24
) since
D_{+}\phi_{0,q}=0
.
Once in power of
\Psi
the local field follows from (
91
) by computing the expectation value
\left\langle\Phi\big|h_{3}(\Psi_{0,p})\big|\Phi\right\rangle
for a state
|\Phi\rangle
constrained to the first Landau level.
Hence one obtains that,

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi\big|h_{3}(\Psi_{0,p})\big|\Phi\right\rangle=H_{3}-\mu_{B}\frac{4\pi}{L_{3}}\sum^{p^{\prime}-1}_{q=0}\big|\phi_{0,q}(x_{1},x_{2},p)\big|^{2}.

(94)

The first order equations unveil an attraction among particles confined to the lowest Landau level.
To see this introduce Eq. (
90
) into the kinetic energy, given by Eq. (
87
). The Hamiltonian (
79
) becomes
\mathcal{H}=\mu_{B}\int\!d^{3}xh_{3}\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m}\int d^{3}x\vec{\nabla}^{2}(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi)+\frac{1}{8\pi}\int d^{3}x\left(\vec{h}-\vec{H}\right)^{2}
, that once combined with
\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi=-(1/4\pi\mu_{B})(h_{3}-H_{3})
from Eq. (
91
) renders that,

\hskip-5.69046pt\mathcal{H}=\int d^{3}x\left(\frac{1}{8\pi}H_{3}^{2}-\frac{1}{8\pi}h_{3}(\Psi)^{2}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m}\vec{\nabla}^{2}(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi)\right),

(95)

Next we express
\mathcal{H}
by introducing Eq. (
91
) into Eq. (
95

\hskip-8.5359pt\mathcal{H}=2\pi\mu^{2}_{B}\int d^{3}x\left[\frac{H_{3}\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi}{2\pi\mu_{B}}-\left(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi\right)^{2}+\frac{\vec{\nabla}^{2}(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi)}{2\pi r_{e}}\right],

(96)

where Bohr’s magneton is related to the electron’s classical radius by
\hbar^{2}/4m=\mu^{2}_{B}/r_{e}\rightarrow r_{e}=e^{2}/mc^{2}
.
Eq. (
95
) shows that the magnetic field energy is negative,
-h_{3}^{2}/8\pi
, and so, able to lower the energy by becoming more intense.
In other words, it causes attraction among the particles.
Notice that the total energy must remain positive, as initially assumed according to Eq. (
79
).
For the periodic state the last term vanishes,
\int d^{2}x\vec{\nabla}^{2}\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi=0
, as previously shown.
Since
\mu_{B}H_{3}=\hbar\omega_{c}/2
, the Hamiltonian cal also be expressed as,

\mathcal{H}=\int d^{3}x\left[\frac{\hbar\omega_{c}}{2}\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi-2\pi\mu^{2}_{B}\left(\Psi^{{\dagger}}\Psi\right)^{2}\right],

(97)

The second term is evidently attractive although, in comparison with the first, very small.

The length
L_{3}
is truly a free parameter that in fact determines the local magnetic field

, as shown in Eq. (

).
Thus it cannot be determined by the present approach.
It should be regarded as a phenomenological parameter that gauges the difference

.
Recall that the three dimensional integration is turned into a two dimensional integration, by assuming

According to Eq. (

\displaystyle L_{3}>>\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{p}}r_{e}.

This is easily reachable for any reasonable

In summary the deviation

is freely adjusted and once this is done the parameter

becomes known.
A part from the choice of

the theory can be treated two-dimensionally in the plane

IV
Conclusion

The set of orthonormal bases developed here yield wave functions for the well-known Schrödinger equation problem of a particle in a magnetic field that features a periodic density probability.
These wave functions display a fixed number of
p
trapped magnetic flux per Landau level.
The second quantization study reveals that the number of particles,
N
, and the number of fluxons trapped in the unit cell,
p
, are independent.
For
N
fermions distributed among
n+1
Landau energy levels we find the remarkable property that the density of particles presents
p^{2}
spatial maxima (minima) in the unit cell for any
n
.
In case the highest Landau level is completely filled, and so with
N=(n+1)p
particles, there are
(n+1)/p
particles per maxima (minima).
We have shown that in case that particles fall in the lowest Landau level (
n=0
), the magnetic field produced by the particles yields a residual attractive interaction among them.
The present set of orthonormal functions retrieves the de Haas-van Alphen effect results, namely the total magnetization displays a periodicity with respect to the inverse of the applied field which is proportional to the area of the Fermi surface.
