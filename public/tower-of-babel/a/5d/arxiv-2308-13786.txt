Abstract
We study static kink solutions in a generalized two-dimensional dilaton gravity model, where the kinetic term of the dilaton is generalized to be an arbitrary function of the canonical one 𝒳 = − 1 2 ​ ( ∇ φ ) 2 \mathcal{X}=-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\varphi)^{2} , say ℱ ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) \mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X}) , and the kink is generated by a canonical scalar matter field ϕ \phi . It is found that for arbitrary ℱ ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) \mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X}) , the background field equations have a simple first-order formalism, and the linear perturbation equation can always be written as a Schrödinger-like equation with factorizable Hamiltonian operator. After choosing appropriate ℱ ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) \mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X}) and superpotential, we obtain a sine-Gordon type kink solution with pure AdS 2 metric. The linear perturbation issue of this solution becomes an exactly solvable conformal quantum mechanics problem, if one of the model parameter takes a critical value.

2
The model and its general properties

We consider a generalized 2D dilaton gravity model with the following action

S=\frac{1}{\kappa}\int d^{2}x\sqrt{-g}\left[\varphi R+\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})+\kappa\mathcal{L}_{m}\right],

(6)

where
\mathcal{L}_{m}=-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\phi)^{2}-V(\phi)
is the Lagrangian density of the scalar matter field that generates the kink. What makes the present model different from the MMSS gravity is the term
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
, which is an arbitrary function of the standard dilaton kinetic term
\mathcal{X}=-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\varphi)^{2}
. The MMSS gravity model corresponds to the special case with
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})=\mathcal{X}
.

The action (
6
) leads to three field equations, namely, the dilaton equation

\nabla^{\lambda}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla_{\lambda}\varphi)+R=0,

(7)

the scalar equation

\nabla_{\lambda}\nabla^{\lambda}\phi=\frac{dV}{d\phi},

(8)

and the Einstein equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla_{\mu}\varphi\nabla_{\nu}\varphi-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}\left(-2\mathcal{F}+4\nabla_{\lambda}\nabla^{\lambda}\varphi\right)

(9)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\varphi+\kappa T_{\mu\nu}=0,

where
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}
are the derivatives of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
\mathcal{X}
and
\varphi
, respectively, and
T_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{L}_{m}+\nabla_{\mu}\phi\nabla_{\nu}\phi
is the energy-momentum tensor.

For the static metric

ds^{2}=-e^{2A(x)}dt^{2}+dx^{2},

(10)

the dilaton and the scalar equations (
7
) and (
8
) become

\left(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\partial_{x}\varphi-2\partial_{x}A\right)\partial_{x}A+\partial_{x}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\partial_{x}\varphi-2\partial_{x}A\right)=0,

(11)

and

\partial_{x}A\,\partial_{x}\phi+\partial_{x}^{2}\phi=\frac{dV}{d\phi},

(12)

respectively. The nontrivial components of the Einstein equation are

\displaystyle-2\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi-\partial_{x}\varphi(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\partial_{x}\varphi-2\partial_{x}A)=\kappa(\partial_{x}\phi)^{2},

(13)

\displaystyle-2\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi+\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{2}\kappa(\partial_{x}\phi)^{2}+\kappa V.

(14)

Only three of the above four equations are independent. For example, one can derive the scalar equation by using the dilaton equation and the Einstein equation. Thus, we will neglect the scalar equation (
12
) and try to find solutions for the other three.

2.1
The first-order formalism

A remarkable feature of the present model is that the dynamical equations (
11
)-(
14
) can be rewritten as a group of first-order equations.

To see this, we start by noticing that the dilaton equation (
11
) is satisfied, if

\partial_{x}A=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\partial_{x}\varphi,

(15)

with which Eq. (
13
) reduces to

-2\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi=\kappa(\partial_{x}\phi)^{2}.

(16)

To proceed, we introduce the so-called superpotential function
W(\phi)
such that

\partial_{x}\phi=\frac{dW}{d\phi}.

(17)

Then Eq. (
16
) becomes

\partial_{x}\varphi=-\frac{\kappa}{2}W,

(18)

or equivalently,
\mathcal{X}(W)=-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{8}W^{2}
. After substituting Eqs. (
17
) and (
18
) into Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) we obtain

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{F}}{\kappa}+\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{dW}{d\phi}\bigg)^{2},

(19)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\kappa}{4}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}W.

(20)

As will be shown in Sec.
3
, exact kink solutions can be easily constructed by inserting appropriate functions
W(\phi)
and
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
into the first-order equations (
17
)-(
20
). We see that
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
only affects the solutions of
V
and
A
. Therefore, by modifying the function
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
, one can tune the form of the warp factor while keep
\phi
and
\varphi
unchanged.

2.2
Linear stability issue

It is convenient to discuss the linear perturbation in the conformally flat coordinates:

ds^{2}=e^{2A(r)}(-dt^{2}+dr^{2}),

(21)

where

r(x)\equiv\int_{0}^{x}e^{-A(\tilde{x})}d\tilde{x}.

(22)

For simplicity, we denote the derivatives with respect to
t
and

by overdots and primes, respectively.
In the
(t,r)
-coordinates the dynamical equations (

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi^{\prime},

(23)

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{2A}V_{\phi},

(24)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}e^{-2A}\phi^{\prime 2}+\frac{\mathcal{F}}{\kappa},

(25)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi^{\prime 2}-\frac{1}{2}\kappa\phi^{\prime 2},

(26)

respectively.

Following Refs.
[
32
,
33
,
38
]
, we consider small perturbations
\{\delta\varphi(r,t),\delta\phi(r,t),\delta g_{\mu\nu}(r,t)\}
around an arbitrary static solution of Eqs. (
23
)-(

\{\varphi(r),\phi(r),g_{\mu\nu}(r)\}
. It is convenient to define the metric perturbation as

\displaystyle\delta g_{\mu\nu}(r,t)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle e^{2A(r)}h_{\mu\nu}(r,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{2A(r)}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}h_{00}(r,t)&\Phi(r,t)\\
\Phi(r,t)&h_{rr}(r,t)\\
\end{array}\right).

To the first order, the perturbation of the metric inverse reads

\delta g^{\mu\nu}=-e^{-2A}h^{\mu\nu},

(30)

where

h^{\mu\nu}\equiv\eta^{\mu\rho}\eta^{\nu\sigma}h_{\rho\sigma}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}h_{00}&-\Phi\\
-\Phi&h_{rr}\\
\end{array}\right).

(31)

As in Refs.
[
32
,
33
,
38
]
, we define a variable
\Xi\equiv 2\dot{\Phi}-h_{00}^{\prime}
, and taking the dilaton gauge
\delta\varphi=0
.

Independent perturbation equations can be obtained by linearizing the Einstein equation (
9
) and the scalar field equation (
8
). The linearization of the Einstein equation leads to two independent perturbation equations, namely,
the
(0,1)
component:

{h}_{rr}=\kappa\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}{\delta\phi},

(32)

and the
(1,1)
component:

\Xi=\kappa\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}\bigg[\delta\phi^{\prime}+\delta\phi\left(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}-\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}-\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{X}\varphi^{\prime}\right)\bigg].

(33)

The
(0,0)
component is also nontrivial, but after substituting the background field equations it reduces to Eq. (
32
).

Another independent perturbation equation comes from the linearization of the scalar equation (
8
), which, after eliminating
h_{rr}
and
\Xi
by using Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
), takes the following form:

\displaystyle\ddot{\delta\phi}-\delta\phi^{\prime\prime}-\bigg[\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{XX}}\mathcal{X}\left(\varphi^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi^{\prime 2}\right)

(34)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\varphi^{\prime\prime}-\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\varphi^{\prime}\right)-2\left(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}\right)^{2}-\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 4\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\bigg]\delta\phi=0.

The terms that contain
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{X}}
can be eliminated by applying the following identity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{XX}}\mathcal{X}\left(\varphi^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\ \varphi^{\prime 2}\right)

(35)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\varphi^{\prime\prime}-\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\varphi^{\prime}\right)=\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}-2\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}},

which is derived by using background equations (
23
) and (

).
Finally, the equation for
\delta\phi
takes the same form as the one derived in the MMSS gravity
[

\ddot{\delta\phi}-\delta\phi^{\prime\prime}+\left[\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}-2\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}\phi^{\prime}}+2\left(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}\right)^{2}-\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}\right]\delta\phi=0,

(36)

which can also be written as

\ddot{\delta\phi}-\delta\phi^{\prime\prime}+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f}\delta\phi=0,\quad f=\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}.

(37)

By conducting the mode expansion

\delta\phi=\sum_{n}\psi_{n}(r)e^{i\omega_{n}t},

(38)

we can rewrite the perturbation equation into a Schrödinger-like equation:

\hat{H}\psi_{n}\equiv\left[-\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+V_{\textrm{eff}}\right]\psi_{n}=\omega_{n}^{2}\psi,

(39)

where the effective potential
V_{\text{eff}}\equiv\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f}
. For continuous eigenvalues, the summation in Eq. (
38
) should be understood as integration. The particular form of the effective potential enables us to factorize the Hamiltonian operator into the product of an operator
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and its Hermit conjugate:

\hat{H}={\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}}{\hat{\mathcal{A}}},

(40)

where

\hat{\mathcal{A}}=-\frac{d}{dr}+\frac{f^{\prime}}{f},\quad\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}=\frac{d}{dr}+\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}.

(41)

According to the theory of supersymmetric quantum mechanics
[
42
]
, the eigenvalues of such a factorizable Hamiltonian operator are semipositive definite, namely,
\omega_{n}^{2}\geq 0
. Therefore, any static solution is stable against small linear perturbations. The ground state has vanished eigenvalue
\omega_{0}=0
, and the corresponding wave function is
\psi_{0}(r)\propto f
.

Now, let us consider an explicit kink solution.

3
Kink with AdS
2
metric

To construct an explicit solution, one must specify the functions
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
and
W(\phi)
.
As can be seen from Eq. (
15
), the freedom in choosing
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
allows us to construct kink solutions with very simple warp factor. For example, if we take

\mathcal{F}=-2\sqrt{-2\mathcal{X}}/l,

(42)

such that
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}=\frac{2}{l\,|\partial_{x}\varphi|}
, then Eq. (
15
) leads to a metric solution of the following form:

A(x)=\textrm{sgn}(\partial_{x}\varphi)\cdot x/l,

(43)

where
l>0
is a parameter with the dimension of length, and
\textrm{sgn}(x)
is the sign function.
Obviously, if we can construct solutions with monotonically increasing dilatons, such that
\textrm{sgn}(\partial_{x}\varphi)=1
, then the warp factor

A(x)=x/l

(44)

describes a pure AdS
2
space with negative constant curvature
R=-2l^{-2}
.

As can be seen from Eqs. (
17
) and (
18
), the solutions of
\varphi
and
\phi
are completely determined by the superpotential
W(\phi)
. Therefore, by choosing suitable superpotentials we can obtain monotonically increasing dilatons, one such example is
[

W(\phi)=kv^{2}\left[\sin\left(\frac{\phi}{v}\right)-c\right],

(45)

for which Eqs. (
17
)-(
19
) have the following solution:

\displaystyle\phi(x)=v\arcsin(\tanh(kx)),

(46)

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\kappa v^{2}[ckx-\ln(\cosh(kx))],

(47)

\displaystyle V(\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\cos^{2}\phi-2l(c-\sin\phi)\right],

(48)

where
k,v,c
are some real parameters.

The scalar field configuration in Eq. (
46
) corresponds to a sine-Gordon kink, whose width and the asymptotic behavior are controlled by parameters
k
and
v
, respectively. For simplicity, we fix
k=v=1
, so that
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\phi(x)=\pm\pi/2
. For the dilaton field, the asymptotic behavior is

\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\kappa[(c\mp 1)x+\ln 2].

(49)

Obviously, as the dimensionless parameter
c
is turned on, the dilaton becomes asymmetric. Especially, for
c\geq 1
,
\partial_{x}\varphi=\frac{1}{2}\kappa[c-\tanh(x)]\geq 0
, and the dilaton becomes a monotonically increasing function, see Fig.
1

\frac{1}{2}\kappa\ln 2

Particular attention should be paid, however, to the critical case with
c=1
, for which the effective potential reduces to an inverse-square potential
\tilde{V}_{\textrm{eff}}(u)=\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}{u^{2}}

[
55

. Therefore, we have revealed an interesting relation between AdS kink solutions and conformal quantum mechanics. In fact, similar relations were also found in other models.

For example, in Liouville model, the nonlinear field equation supports a static space-dependent solution, for which the linear perturbation modes obey a Schrödinger-like equation with the aforementioned inverse-square potential with
\alpha=1

[

,
54

.
As explained in Refs.

,
54
]
, the absence of the zero mode in Liouville model, is a consequence of the spontaneous breaking of the space-translation symmetry. Without the normalizable zero mode, there is no infrared divergences in the corresponding quantum theory, and the propagator can be explicitly constructed. Finally, the perturbation theory is finite after ultraviolet mass renormalization
[
53
,
54
]
.

In fact, static solutions without normalizable zero mode is not only acceptable physically, but also required in some cases. For example, in most 5D thick brane models, the zero mode of the scalar perturbation must not be normalizable, otherwise, one would confront with the problem of fifth force
[

,
40
,
41

4
Conclusion

In this work, we studied a 2D dilaton gravity where the dilaton has a generalized kinetic term
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
. We found that the static field equations have a simple first-order formalism, from which exact kink solutions can be easily constructed after giving suitable superpotential
W(\phi)
and function
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
. The solutions of the dilaton and the scalar fields are determined only by the form of
W(\phi)
. While the solution of the metric depends on both
W(\phi)
and
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
. Therefore, by tuning
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
, one may obtain kink solutions with different metrics. The example we given here is a sine-Gordon kink with a pure AdS
2
metric, but it is not difficult to construct many other solutions.

We also found that for arbitrary static solutions of our model, the linear perturbation equation can always be written as a Schrödinger-like equation with factorizable Hamiltonian operator. The factorization of the Hamiltonian not only ensures the semi-positivity of the spectrum (and therefore the stability of the solution), but also gives the analytical expression of the zero mode wave function.

In particular, for our AdS
2
kink solution, there is no bounded shape modes in the linear spectrum, because the effective potential approaches to zero as
u\to+\infty
. But, if
c>1
and
\alpha>1/2
there is a normalizable zero mode as the ground state. While, for
c=1
the linear perturbation issue becomes a conformal quantum mechanics problem, and the effective potential is an exactly solvable inverse-square potential without normalizable zero mode.

It is interesting to explore other kink solutions by choosing different
W(\phi)
and
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
, or to investigate the quasi-normal mode issues of gravitating kinks by following the discussions of Refs.
[
56
,
57
]
. We leave these issues to our future works.
