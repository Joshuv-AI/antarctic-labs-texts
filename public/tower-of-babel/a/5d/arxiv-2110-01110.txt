Safe Control with Neural Network Dynamic Models
Tianhao Wei, Changliu Liu

Abstract
Safety is critical in autonomous robotic systems. A safe control law should ensure forward invariance of a safe set (a subset in the state space). It has been extensively studied regarding how to derive a safe control law with a control-affine analytical dynamic model. However, how to formally derive a safe control law with Neural Network Dynamic Models (NNDM) remains unclear due to the lack of computationally tractable methods to deal with these black-box functions. In fact, even finding a control that minimizes an objective for NNDM without any safety constraint is still challenging. In this work, we propose MIND-SIS (Mixed Integer for Neural network Dynamic models with Safety Index Synthesis), the first method to synthesize safe control for NNDM. The method includes two parts: 1) SIS: an algorithm for the offline synthesis of the safety index (also called as a barrier function) using evolutionary methods and 2) MIND: an algorithm that computes the optimal safe control input online by solving a constrained optimization with a computationally efficient encoding of neural networks. It has been theoretically proved that MIND-SIS guarantees forward invariance and finite-time convergence to a subset of the user-defined safe set. It has also been numerically validated that MIND-SIS achieves optimal safe control of NNDM with less than 10 − 8 10^{-8} optimality gap and zero safety constraint violation.

Introduction

Robot safety depends on the correct functioning of all system components, such as accurate perception, safe motion planning, and safe control. Safe control, as the last defense of system safety, has been widely studied in the context of dynamical systems
Nagumo 1942
;
Blanchini 1999
.
A safe control law ensures the forward invariance of a subset inside the user-defined safety constraint, meaning that any agent entering that subset will remain in it. There are many methods to derive the safe control laws for control-affine analytical dynamic model
Wei and Liu 2019
;
Liu and Tomizuka 2014
. However, constructing such an analytical dynamic model for complex systems can be difficult, time-consuming, and sometimes impossible
Nguyen-Tuong and Peters 2011
. Recent works adopt data-driven approaches to learn these dynamic models, and most of the learned models are encoded in neural networks, e.g. virtual world models of video games or dynamic models of a robot, etc.
Nagabandi et al. 2018
;
Janner et al. 2019
.
Although neural network dynamic models (NNDMs) can greatly alleviate human efforts in modeling, they are less interpretable than analytical models. It is more challenging to derive control laws, especially safe control laws, for these NNDMs than for analytical models.

This paper focuses on safe tracking tasks with NNDMs, which is formulated as a constrained optimization that minimizes the state tracking error given the safety constraint and the neural network dynamics constraint.
Even without the safety constraint, the tracking control with NNDMs is already challenging.
Since NNDMs are complex and highly nonlinear, there is no computationally efficient method to compute its model inverse, which is required by most existing white-box methods
Tolani et al. 2000
.
On the other hand, black-box methods, such as the shooting method which chooses control from randomly generated candidates, can not guarantee to find the optimal solution in finite time.
Moreover, the safety constraint adds another layer of difficulty to the problem.
The robot should select an action that not only satisfies the safety constraint at the current time step, but also ensures that in the future, the agent will not enter any state where no action is safe. This property is called
persistent feasibility
. To ensure persistent feasibility, we need to compute the control invariant set inside the original user-specified safety constraint and constrain the robot motion in this more restrictive control invariant set.
For an analytical model, we can manually craft this control invariant set to meet the requirement
Liu and Tomizuka 2014
based on our understanding of the dynamics. The same task becomes difficult for NNDM due to its poor interpretability.

In this work, we address these challenges by introducing an integrated method, mixed integer for neural network dynamic models with safety index synthesis (MIND-SIS), to handle both the offline synthesis of the control invariant set and the online computation of the constrained optimization with NNDM constraints.
First, inspired by an algorithm for neural network verification
Tjeng et al. 2017
, we use mixed integer programming (MIP) to encode the NNDM constraint, which greatly reduces the complexity of the optimization problem. Importantly, the MIP method is complete and guarantees optimality.
Second, to synthesize the control invariant set, we use evolutionary algorithms to optimize a parameterized safety index.
Using the learned safety index, the resulting control solved by the constrained optimization will ensure persistent feasibility and hence forward invariance inside the user-specified safety constraint.

Formulation

Dynamic model

Consider a discrete time dynamic system with
m_{x}
state and
m_{u}
controls.

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k})dt,

(1)

where
k
is the time step,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in X\subset\real^{m_{x}}
is the state,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\in U\subset\real^{m_{u}}
is the control,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}:\real^{m_{x}}\mapsto\real^{m_{u}}
is the dynamic model, and
dt
is the sampling time. We assume the legal state set
X
and control set
U
are both defined by linear constraints. This assumption covers most cases in practice.

In the NNDM case, the dynamic model
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}
is encoded by a
n
-layer feedforward neural network. Each layer in
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}
corresponds to a function
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{i}:\real^{k_{i-1}}\mapsto\real^{k_{i}}
, where
k_{i}
is the dimension of the hidden variable
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}
in layer
i
, and
k_{0}=m_{x}+m_{u}
,
k_{n}=m_{x}
. The network can be represented by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{n}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{1}
,
where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{i}
is the mapping for layer
i
. And
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i-1})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{i}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{i})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i-1}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{i})

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{i}\in\real^{k_{i}\times k_{i-1}}
is the weight matrix,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\in\real^{k_{i}}
is the bias vector, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{i}:\real^{k_{i}}\mapsto\real^{k_{i}}
is the activation function. We only consider ReLU activation in this work. For simplicity, denote
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i-1}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
by
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}
.
Let
z_{i,j}
be the value of the
j^{th}
node in the
i^{th}
layer,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}_{i,j}\in\real^{1\times k_{i-1}}
be the
j^{th}
row in
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{i}
, and
b_{i,j}
be the
j^{th}
entry in
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{j}
.

Safety specification

We consider the safety specification as a requirement that the system state should be constrained in a connected and closed set
\mathcal{X}_{0}\subseteq X
.
\mathcal{X}_{0}
is called the safe set.
\mathcal{X}_{0}
should be a zero-sublevel set of an initial safety index
\phi_{0}:X\mapsto\real
,
i.e.
\mathcal{X}_{0}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\mid\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\leq 0\}.

\phi_{0}
can be defined differently for a given
\mathcal{X}_{0}
. Ideally, a safe control law should guarantee forward invariance and finite-time convergence to the safe set. Forward invariance requires that
\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})\leq 0\implies\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})\leq 0
.
And finite-time convergence can be enforced by requiring that
\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})>0\implies\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})\leq\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\gamma dt
. Hence the number of time steps for an unsafe state to return to the safe set is bounded above by
\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})/\gamma dt
.
These two conditions can be written compactly as one:

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})\leq\max\{0,\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\gamma dt\}.

(2)

The safe tracking problem

This paper considers the following constrained optimization for safe tracking, where the problem is solved at every time step
k
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}}&\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}^{r}\|_{p}\\
\textit{s.t. }&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k})dt,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\in U\\
&\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})\leq\max\{0,\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\gamma dt\}\\
\end{split}

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}^{r}
is the reference state at time step
k+1
,
\|\cdot\|_{p}
can be either
\ell 1
-norm or
\ell 2
-norm. This formulation can be viewed as a one-step model predictive control (MPC). The extension to multi-step MPC is straightforward, which we leave for future work.
At a given step
k
, (
3
) is a nonlinear programming problem. However, existing nonlinear solvers have poor performance for constraints involving neural networks (which will be shown in
section

5
). The reason is that neural networks (with ReLU activation) are piece-wise linear, whose second-order derivatives are not informative. New techniques are needed to solve this problem.

Persistent feasibility

Persistent feasibility requires that there always exists
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\in U
that satisfies (
2
) for all time step
k
. However, this may not be true for some
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
.
For example, if
\phi_{0}
measures the distance between the ego vehicle and the leading vehicle. It is possible that the ego vehicle is still far from the leading vehicle (
\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})<0
), but has big relative speed toward the leading vehicle. Then collision is inevitable (
\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})>0
for all possible
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\in U
).
This situation may happen when the relative degree from
\phi_{0}
to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
is greater than one or when the control inputs are bounded.
In these cases,
\mathcal{X}_{0}
may not be forward invariant or finite-time convergent. We call this situation as
loosing control feasibility
, which further leads to
loosing persistent feasibility
.
To address this problem, we want to prevent the system from getting into those control-infeasible states in
\mathcal{X}_{0}
. That is to find a subset
\mathcal{X}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{0}
such that there exists a feasible control law to make
\mathcal{X}_{s}
forward invariant and finite-time convergent. We call
\mathcal{X}_{s}
a
control invariant set
within
\mathcal{X}_{0}
.

3
Related work

Optimization with Neural Network Constraints

Recent progress in nonlinear optimization involving neural network constraints can be classified as primal optimization methods and dual optimization methods.
The primal optimization methods encode the nonlinear activation functions (e.g., ReLU) as mixed-integer linear programmings
Tjeng et al. 2017
, relaxed linear programmings
Ehlers 2017
or semidefinite programmings
Raghunathan et al. 2018
.
Our method to encode NNDM is inspired by MIPVerify
Tjeng et al. 2017
, which uses mixed integer programming to compute maximum allowable disturbances to the input. MIPVerify is complete and sound, meaning that the encoding is equivalent to the original problem.

QP-based safe control

When the system dynamics are analytical and control-affine, the safe tracking problem can be decomposed into two steps: 1) computing a reference control
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{r}
without the safety constraint; 2) projecting
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{r}
to the safe control set
Ames et al. 2019
.
For analytical control-affine dynamic models, the safe control set that satisfies (
3
) is a half-space intersecting with
U
. Therefore,
the second step is essentially a quadratic projection of the reference control to that linear space, which can be efficiently computed by calling a quadratic programming (QP) solver. Existing methods include CBF-QP
Ames et al. 2016
, SSA-QP
Liu and Tomizuka 2014
, etc.
However, to our best knowledge, there has not been any quadratic projection method that projects a reference control to a safety constraint with non-analytical and non-control-affine dynamic models, in which case the safe control set can be non-convex. Moreover, our work solves both the computation of reference control and its projection onto safe control set in an integrated manner. Hence, our work is not limited to the quadratic projection of the reference control.

Persistent feasibility in MPC

There are different approaches in MPC literature to compute the control invariant set to ensure persistent feasibility, such as Lyapunov function
Danielson et al. 2016
, linearization-convexification
Jalalmaab et al. 2017
, and grid-based reachability analysis
Bansal et al. 2017
. However, most of the non-grid-based methods approximate the control invariant set by convex set, which greatly limit the expressiveness of the geometries. Although grid-based methods have better expressiveness and may be able to extend to non-analytical models, they have limited scalability due to the curse of dimensionality and the fact they are usually non-parameterized. Our method can synthesize the control invariant set with nonlinear boundaries for non-analytical models using parameterized functions, hence more computationally efficient.

4
Method

In this section, we discuss how to efficiently solve the constrained optimization (
3
) and ensure it is persistently feasible. First, we introduce MIND, a way to find the optimal solution of
eq.

3
by encoding NNDM constraints as mixed integer constraints. Then we present SIS, a method to find the control invariant set by learning a new safety index
\phi
that maximizes control feasibility. Finally, we present the reformulated problem.

4.1
MIND: Encode NNDM constraints

To overcome the complexity of NNDM constraints, we first add all hidden nodes in the neural network as decision variables and turn (
3
) into the following equivalent form:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}}&\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}^{r}\|_{p}\\
\textit{s.t. }&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{n}dt,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{0}=\left[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right],\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\in U\\
&z_{i,j}=\max\{\hat{z}_{i,j},0\},\hat{z}_{i,j}=\mathbf{w}_{i,j}\mathbf{z}_{i-1}+b_{i,j},\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\forall j\in\left\{1,\ldots,k_{i}\right\}\\
&\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})\leq\max\{0,\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\gamma dt\}.\end{split}

(4)

Nevertheless, the nonlinear non-smooth constraints introduced by the ReLU activation
z_{i,j}=\max\{\hat{z}_{i,j},0\}
in (
4
) is still challenging to handle.
Inspired by MIPVerify
Tjeng et al. 2017
, we use mixed integer formulation to rewrite these constraints. We first introduce an auxiliary variable
\delta_{i,j}
to denote the activation status of the ReLU node:

\displaystyle\delta_{i,j}=1\Rightarrow z_{i,j}=\hat{z}_{i,j},\quad\delta_{i,j}=0\Rightarrow z_{i,j}=0.

(5)

Then we compute the pre-activation upper bounds
\hat{u}_{i,j}
and lower bound
\hat{l}_{i,j}
of every node in the neural network using interval arithmetics
Moore et al. 2009
. Given the input ranges (e.g.,
x\in[1,2]
and
y\in[3,4]
),
interval arithmetics compute the output range using the lower and upper bounds (e.g.,
x-y\in[1-4,2-3]=[-3,-1]
). When
\hat{u}_{i,j}\leq 0
, the constraint for ReLU activation reduces to
z_{i,j}=0
. When
\hat{l}_{i,j}\geq 0
, the constraint reduces to
z_{i,j}=\hat{z}_{i,j}
. Otherwise, the constraint can be represented as the following linear inequalities
Liu et al. 2021
:

\displaystyle z_{i,j}\geq\hat{z}_{i,j},z_{i,j}\geq 0,z_{i,j}\leq\hat{z}_{i,j}-\hat{\ell}_{i,j}\left(1-\delta_{i,j}\right),z_{i,j}\leq\hat{u}_{i,j}\delta_{i,j},\delta_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}.

(6)

\subfigure

. The vehicle collides with the obstacle due to loss of control feasibility (too late to break). (b) and (c) show the performance of MIND-SIS. The SIS-synthesized safety index
\phi^{l}
guarantees persistent feasibility and is general enough to be directly applied to multi-obstacle scenarios without any modification.

With this encoding, the constrained optimization is converted into a MIP, which can be solved efficiently. Theoretically, MIP is a NP-complete problem. The worst case computation time grows exponentially with the number of integer variables, which is the total number of ReLU activation functions. However, in practice, the computation can be greatly accelerated by various techniques developed in recent years
Gurobi Optimization, LLC 2021
. The evaluation in
section

5
shows that the actual computation time for a network with
100
ReLUs is only
0.36
seconds while that for a network with
200
ReLUs is
0.8
seconds. Besides, it is worth noting that MIND scales well with dimensions of state and control when the total number of neurons in the hidden layers is fixed.

Nevertheless, successfully obtaining the solution for time
k
and executing the control does not necessarily ensure we will have a solution to the constrained optimization in future time steps as shown in
fig.

1
(a). The safety constraint needs to be modified to ensure persistent feasibility.

4.2
SIS: Guaranteed persistent feasibility

Unlike analytical models, it is challenging to design a safety index for NNDM because of its poor interpretability. Therefore, we introduce Safety Index Synthesis (SIS), which automatically synthesizes a safety index
\phi
that results in a forward invariant and finite-time convergent
\mathcal{X}_{s}
to guarantee persistent feasibility.

Liu and Tomizuka 2014
introduced a form of
\phi
that can improve control feasibility,
\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})=\phi_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\sum_{i=1}^{q}\alpha_{i}\phi_{0}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\beta
, where
\phi_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})
defines the same sublevel set as
\phi_{0}
and is parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0}
,
\phi_{0}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})
is the
i
-th order derivative of
\phi_{0}
,
q
is the order such that the relative degree from
\phi_{0}^{(q)}
to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
is
1
, and
\beta
is a constant. We denote the concatenation
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0},\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{q}]
by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}
.
Liu and Tomizuka 2014
showed that this
\phi
could result in a forward invariant and finite-time convergent
\mathcal{X}_{s}
when the control input is unbounded. When the control input is bounded, we argue that persistent feasibility can be achieved by optimizing
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}
and
\beta
under
\assumptionref
asp: lipschitz.

{assumption}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}
and
\phi
are Lipschitz continuous functions with Lipschitz constants
k_{f}
and
k_{\phi}
respectively. The Euclidean norm of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})
is bounded by
M_{f}
.

To ensure forward invariance, it suffices to enforce that there always exists a feasible control for all states near the zero-level set of
\phi
(as proved in
section

A.2
).
Define the state-of-interest set
B=\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\mid|\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})|\leq k_{\phi}M_{f}dt\}
(states near the boundary
\phi=0
) and infeasible-state-of-interest set
B^{*}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in B,\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}},\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})dt)>\max\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\gamma dt\}\}
.
B
contains all the states that can cross the boundary
\phi=0
in one step because
\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\|dt\leq M_{f}dt
. To achieve forward invariance, we need all states in
B
to have feasible control (i.e., when
B^{*}
is empty).
Then the problem can be formulated as
\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}},\beta}|B^{*}|/|B|
. The expression of the corresponding control invariant set
\mathcal{X}_{s}
is derived in
section

A.2
. We can also let
B=X
to learn a safety index that further achieves finite time convergence of
\mathcal{X}_{s}
at the cost of a potentially more conservative policy (see
section

A.2
). Since the gradient from
|B^{*}|/|B|
to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}},\beta
is usually difficult to compute, we use a derivative-free evolutionary approach, CMA-ES
Hansen 2016
to optimize the parameters. CMA-ES runs for multiple generations. In each generation, the algorithm samples many parameter candidates (called members) from a multivariate Gaussian distribution and evaluates their performance. A proportion of candidates with the best performance will be used to update the mean and covariance of the Gaussian distribution. To evaluate the parameter candidates, we sample a subset
S\subset B
and minimize the infeasible rate
r:=\frac{|S\cap B^{*}|}{|S|}
as a surrogate for the original objective function
|B^{*}|/|B|
. We prove that if the sampling is dense enough and
r=0
, the safety constraint with the learned safety index is guaranteed to be feasible for arbitrary states in
B
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose 1) we sample a state subset
S\subset B
such that
\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in B
,
\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in S}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|\leq\delta
, where
\delta
is an arbitrary constant representing the sampling density.; and 2)
\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in S
, there exists a safe control
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
, s.t.
\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})dt)\leq\max\{-\epsilon,\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})-\gamma dt-\epsilon\}
,
where
\epsilon=k_{\phi}(1+k_{f}dt)\delta
. Then
\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in B,\exists\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}},\ \textit{s.t. }

\displaystyle\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})dt)\leq\max\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\gamma dt\}.

(7)

Proof.

According to condition 1),
\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in B
, we can find
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in S
such that
\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|\leq\delta
. According to condition 2), for this
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
, we can find
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
such that
\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})dt)\leq\max\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})-\gamma dt\}-\epsilon
. Next we show
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
satisfy (
7
) using
Lipschitz condition
and
triangle inequality
.

\displaystyle\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})dt)

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})dt)-\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})dt)}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}+\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})dt)}

(8a)

\displaystyle\leq\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}k_{\phi}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})]dt\|}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}+\max\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})-\gamma dt\}-\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\underline{k_{\phi}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|}+k_{\phi}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\|}dt}+\max\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})-\gamma dt\}-\epsilon

(8c)

\displaystyle\leq k_{\phi}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\delta}+k_{\phi}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}k_{f}\delta}dt-\epsilon+\max\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})-\gamma dt\}

\displaystyle=\max\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})-\gamma dt\}.

Hence (
7
) is verified.
∎

The computation time of SIS depends on the number of CMA-ES iterations and the time spent in finding
S
in each iteration. Although a rigorous proof is missing, the convergence rate of CMA-ES is empirically exponential
Hansen and Ostermeier 2001
. We can find
S
in each iteration by uniformly sampling
X
. This process can be time consuming if the state dimension is high (e.g.
n>10
), but we may accelerate the process by high dimensional Breadth-First-Search, which we leave for future work.

4.3
MIND-SIS: Safe control with NNDM

Once the safety index is synthesized, we substitute
\phi_{0}
with
\phi
in (
4
) to guarantee persistent feasibility. To address the nonlinearity in the safety constraint (
2
), we approximate it with first order Taylor expansion at the current state
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
(
section

A.1
discusses how the safety guarantee is preserved):

\displaystyle\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})=\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\phi\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k})dt+o(\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k})dt\|),

(9)

where
\lim_{dt\to 0}o(\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k})dt\|)=0
.
Then (
4
) is transformed into a mixed integer problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i},\delta_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}}&\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}^{r}\|_{p}\\
\textit{s.t. }&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{n}dt,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{0}=\left[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right],\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\in U,\\

\end{split}

(10)

5.1
Experiment set-up

The evaluation is designed to answer the following questions:
1) How does our method (by solving (
10
)) compare to the shooting method and regular nonlinear solvers in terms of optimality and computational efficiency on problems without safety constraints?
2) Does the safety index synthesis improve persistent feasibility?
3) Does our method ensure safety in terms of forward invariance.

We evaluate our method on a system with NNDMs for 2D vehicles. The NNDMs are learned from a second order unicycle dynamic model with 4 state inputs (2D position, velocity, and heading angle), 2 control inputs (angular velocity, acceleration), and 4 state outputs (2D velocity, angular velocity, and acceleration). All the states and controls are bounded, where
X:[-10,10]\times[-10,10]\times[-2,2]\times[-\pi,\pi]
and
U:[-4,4]\times[-\pi,\pi]
. We learn 3 different fully connected NNDMs to show the generalizability of our method, which are: I. 3-layer with 50 hidden neurons per layer. II. 3-layer with 100 hidden neurons per layer. III. 4-layer with 50 hidden neurons per layer. Scalability analysis with more models can be found in
section

A.4
.

When evaluating the control performance, we roll-out the closed-loop trajectory directly using NNDM to avoid model mismatch.
Our evaluation aims to show that the proposed method can provide provably safe controls efficiently for the learned model. The safe control computed by NNDM may be unsafe for the actual dynamics under model mismatch. We will extend our work to robust safe control
Liu and Tomizuka 2015
;
Noren and Liu 2019
, which can guarantee safety even with model mismatch in the future.

To answer the questions we raised in the beginning, we design the following two tasks. The first task is trajectory tracking without safety constraints, which can test how our method performs comparing to other methods in terms of optimality and computational efficiency. And the second task is trajectory tracking under safety constraints. It is to test whether the learned safety index improves the feasibility and whether MIND-SIS ensures forward invariance and finite-time convergence.

5.3
Trajectory tracking under safety constraints

This task considers two safety constraints corresponding to different scenarios: collision avoidance and safe following. When control infeasibility happens for a poorly designed safety index, we relax the safety constraint by adding a slack variable.

5.3.1
Collision avoidance

Collision avoidance is one of the most common safety requirement in real-world applications. In this experiment, we consider one static obstacle. The safety index is given as
\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})=d_{min}-d(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})<0

is the relative distance from the agent to the obstacle, and

is a constant.

is the relative velocity,
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
and
\beta
are parameters to learn. This form guarantees forward invariance and finite-time convergence for second-order systems when there is no control limits as shown in
Liu and Tomizuka 2014
, (see
section

A.2
for more discussion). The learned index can generalize to multiple obstacles case when we consider one constraint to each obstacle.

5.3.2
Safe following

The safe following constraint appears when an agent is following a target while keeping a safe distance, such as in adaptive cruise control, nap-of-the-earth flying, etc. The initial safety index is

(11)

\phi^{l}

\alpha_{1}=8.092,\alpha_{2}=9.826,\beta=0.489

\alpha_{1}=2,\alpha_{2}=1,\beta=0.01

6
Discussion

In this work, we propose MIND-SIS, the first method to derive safe control law for NNDM. MIND finds the optimal solution for safe tracking problems involving NNDM constraints, and SIS synthesizes a safety index that guarantees forward invariance and finite-time convergence. Theoretical guarantees of optimality and feasibility are provided. However, safety violation may still exist if the NNDM does not align with the true dynamics. As a future work, we will explore how to guarantee safety under model mismatch and uncertainty. One limitation of SIS is that to guarantee persistent feasibility, the theoretical sampling rate grows exponentially with the dimension of states. We will study how to adaptively adjust the sampling rate to overcome the curse of dimensionality.

A.1
Continuous time vs discrete time safe control

Our work is formulated in discrete time. There are vast literature dealing with safe control in continuous time. The major difference lies in the formulation of constraints. While our constraint is formulated as in (
2
), the constraint for continuous time safe control is formulated as
\dot{\phi}\leq-\kappa(\phi)
where
\kappa
is a non decreasing function where
\kappa(0)\geq 0
. In CBF,
\kappa
is chosen as
\kappa=\lambda\phi
. In SSA,
\kappa
is chosen as
\gamma
when
\phi\geq 0
and
-\infty
when
\phi<0
. Our method can be easily extend to continuous time safe control since
\dot{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})\approx(\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})-\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}))/dt
.

Due to the adoption of discrete time control, we approximate the safety constraints with first order Taylor expansion in (
9
). We define the error caused by first order approximation and omitting higher order terms of
\dot{\phi}
as
e:=\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})-\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\nabla\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k})dt
.

Lemma 2
.

The error
e
can be bounded by a constant safety margin
\varepsilon=2k_{\phi}M_{f}dt
where
k_{\phi}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\phi
,
M_{f}
is the upper bound of
\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\|
defined in Assumption
1
.

Proof.

\displaystyle|e|

\displaystyle=|\nabla\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k})dt-[\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})-\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})]|

(12a)

\displaystyle\leq|\nabla\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k})dt|+k_{\phi}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\|

(12b)

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})\|\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k})\|dt+k_{\phi}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k})dt\|

(12c)

\displaystyle\leq k_{\phi}M_{f}dt+k_{\phi}M_{f}dt=2k_{\phi}M_{f}dt=\varepsilon.

(12d)

∎

Next, we show how the forward invariance and finite time convergence to the set
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}:\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\leq 0\}
defined by a safety index
\phi^{*}
can be preserved in a discrete time system with the first order approximation of the safety constraint, by introducing a more conservative safety index that considers the approximation error. Note that one way to ensure forward invariance and finite time convergence to the set
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}:\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\leq 0\}
in discrete time is to find a control that satisfies

\displaystyle\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})\leq\max\{0,\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\gamma dt\}.

(13)

However, in (
10
), since we use the first order approximation of the constraint

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\phi^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k})\leq\max\{-\dfrac{\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})}{dt},-\gamma\},

(14)

the forward invariance and finite time convergence may not be preserved due to the approximation error. That is, a
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
that satisfies
eq.

14
may lead to a
\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})>\max\{0,\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\gamma dt\}
. Therefore, to preserve forward invariance and finite time convergence to the set
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}:\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\leq 0\}
, we introduce a new safety index (which is more conservative)

\displaystyle\phi:=\phi^{*}+\beta,

(15)

and find the control based on
eq.

16
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\phi\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k})\leq\max\{-\dfrac{\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})}{dt},-\eta\}.

(16)

The following lemma proves that this method ensures the forward invariance and finite time convergence to the set
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}:\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\leq 0\}
.

Lemma 3
.

If
\beta\geq\varepsilon
and
\eta\geq\gamma+\varepsilon/dt
. Then a safe control
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
that satisfies (
16
) also satisfies (
13
).

Proof.

According to
2
, after applying control in (
16
), we have
\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})\leq\max\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\eta dt\}+\varepsilon
, which implies that

\displaystyle\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\max\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\eta dt\}.

(17)

By replacing
\phi
with
\phi^{*}+\beta
, we further have
\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})+\beta-\varepsilon\leq\max\{0,\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})+\beta-\eta dt\}
. Hence

\displaystyle\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\varepsilon-\beta,\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})+\varepsilon-\eta dt\}

(18)

\displaystyle\leq\max\{0,\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\gamma dt\}.

(19)

∎

Note that
3
states that the control law (
16
) ensures forward invariance and finite time convergence to the set
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}:\phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\leq 0\}
. In the extreme case when we select
\beta=\varepsilon
, the control law (
16
) (which is derived using
\phi
) ensures forward invariance and finite time convergence to the set
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}:\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\varepsilon\leq 0\}
.

A.2
Safety index design and properties

In this section, we show that the control law (
16
) not only ensures forward invariance and finite time convergence to the set
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}:\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\epsilon\leq 0\}
, but also to a subset of the user-specified safe set
\mathcal{X}_{0}
.
It is worth noting that according to our parameterization, the set
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}:\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\epsilon\leq 0\}
includes states that
\phi_{0}>0
.
Denote this 0-sublevel set of
\phi-\varepsilon
by
\mathcal{X}_{\phi}
. That means
\mathcal{X}_{\phi}
is not necessarily a subset of
\mathcal{X}_{0}
. In the following discussion, we show that for a second order system, (
16
) will make the set
\mathcal{X}_{s}:=\mathcal{X}_{\phi}\cap\mathcal{X}_{0}
forward invariant if we optimize the safety index around its zero-level set to the point that
B^{*}=\emptyset
; and the set will be finite-time convergent if we optimize the safety index for the whole state space (by letting the state-of-interest set
B=X
) to the point that
B^{*}=\emptyset
.

To prove this, we first partition the whole state space into three parts as shown in
fig.

4
:

•

The space
\mathcal{F}:=\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\mid\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\varepsilon>0\}
;

•

The space
\mathcal{X}_{s}:=\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\mid\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\varepsilon\leq 0\}\cap\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\mid\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\leq 0\}
;

•

The space
\mathcal{G}:=\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\mid\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\varepsilon\leq 0\}\cap\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\mid\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})>0\}
.

\subfigure

\subfigure

Figure 4:
Space partition. The state space is partitioned into three parts. And the safe set is defined by two boundaries. (a) shows the partition in a Venn graph and (b) shows the partition in the phase graph.

We will prove the forward invariance and finite-time convergence to
\mathcal{X}_{s}
defined by the safety index we used in this work:
\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\varepsilon=\phi_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\alpha\dot{\phi}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\beta-\varepsilon
.

For forward invariance, it suffices to show that all
\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})
along the boundary of
\mathcal{X}_{s}
are pointing toward the interior of
\mathcal{X}_{s}
. For finite-time convergence, we are going to show that under certain conditions, all trajectories starting from
\mathcal{G}
will converge to
\mathcal{X}_{s}
and all trajectories starting in
\mathcal{F}
will converge to
\mathcal{X}_{s}\cup\mathcal{G}
.

A.2.1
Discrete time forward invariance of
\mathcal{X}_{s}

The boundary of
\mathcal{X}_{s}
can be decomposed into two parts
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\mid\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\varepsilon=0,\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\leq 0\}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\mid\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\varepsilon\leq 0,\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})=0\}
.
In the above discussion, we proved that when
\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\varepsilon<0
,
\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})-\varepsilon<0
. Therefore the first part of boundary will not be crossed. We only need to consider the second part.
We first define the near-
\phi_{0}
-boundary-state set
B_{0}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\mid\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\varepsilon\leq 0,\ \exists\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{0},\ \phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{0})=0,\text{ and }\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\|<M_{f}dt\}
. Because
\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\|dt\leq M_{f}dt
.
B_{0}
contains all the states that may lead to a out-of-boundary state in one step. Then we only need to prove that

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in B_{0},\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})<0.

(20)

Similar to
section

A.1
, we can derive the discretization error bound
\varepsilon_{0}=2k_{\phi_{0}}M_{f}dt
for
\phi_{0}
, and
\varepsilon_{0}^{*}=2k_{\phi_{0}^{*}}M_{f}dt
for
\phi_{0}^{*}
under the following assumption.

{assumption}

\phi_{0}
and
\phi_{0}^{*}
are both Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constants
k_{\phi_{0}}
and
k_{\phi_{0}^{*}}
respectively.

Lemma 4

(Forward Invariance)
.

If
\beta-\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}^{*}/2>0
,
\alpha<\frac{\beta-\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}^{*}/2}{\varepsilon_{0}}dt
, then
\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in B_{0},\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})\leq 0
.

Proof.

For
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in B_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\varepsilon=\phi_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\alpha\dot{\phi}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\beta-\varepsilon\leq 0,

(21)

and using Lipschitz continuity, we have that
\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in B_{0}
,
-k_{\phi_{0}^{*}}M_{f}dt<\phi_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})<0
. Therefore

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\leq\frac{-\phi_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})-\beta+\varepsilon}{\alpha}\leq\frac{k_{\phi_{0}^{*}}M_{f}dt-\beta+\varepsilon}{\alpha}=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{*}/2-\beta+\varepsilon}{\alpha}.

(22)

Then based on Lemma 2, the following inequality holds for arbitrary
u_{k}

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})+\dot{\phi}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})dt+\varepsilon_{0}.

(23)

And because
\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})\leq 0
and
\alpha<\frac{\beta-\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}^{*}/2}{\varepsilon_{0}}dt
, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})+\dot{\phi}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})dt+\varepsilon_{0}

(25)

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{*}/2-\beta+\varepsilon}{\alpha}dt+\varepsilon_{0}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{*}/2-\beta+\varepsilon}{\alpha}dt+\varepsilon_{0}\leq 0.

(27)

∎

A.2.2

\mathcal{X}_{s}

We prove the finite-time convergence of
\mathcal{X}_{s}
based on the following assumption:

{assumption}

We optimize the safety index for the whole state space by letting the state-of-interest set
B=X
. And the infeasible-state-of-interest set
B^{*}=\emptyset
. Therefore,
\phi-\varepsilon
has feasible solution for the safety constraint for arbitrary state.

It is obvious that all trajectories start in
\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{X}_{s}\cup\mathcal{G}

\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})-\varepsilon<\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\varepsilon-\eta

\phi-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\varepsilon=\phi_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})+\alpha\dot{\phi}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})+\beta-\varepsilon\leq 0\implies\dot{\phi}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})\leq\frac{-\phi_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\beta+\varepsilon}{\alpha}.

\phi_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{k})>0,\ \forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{G}
because
\phi_{0}^{*}
defines the same sublevel set as

A.4
MIND Scalability

We studied how MIND can scale to different model sizes. We test models with different layers and different hidden dims as shown in
table

3
. Besides the computation time, we also show the average prediction error of these models to demonstrate their relative learning ability. We may consider a model with a smaller error having a better learning ability. As a baseline,
Nagabandi et al. 2018
uses a 2-layer 500 hidden dim network to learn the model of an ant robot (one torso and four 3-DoF legs), which has 111 state dimensions, with joint torque as control input. And most models (whose error is less than 0.168) we test may have better learning ability than the 2-layer 500 hidden dim one. So it is reasonable to assume that these models are already enough to learn a variety of complex dynamics with high dimensional input output. It shows the potential of our method to achieve real time control for models with complex dynamics.
