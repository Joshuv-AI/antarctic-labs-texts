Orbifold braid groups and complex braid groups
Jonas Flechsig, J. Flechsig

Abstract




Jonas Flechsig

August 9, 2026

Abstract

A result of Allock
[
1
]
states that certain orbifold braid groups contain Artin groups of type
D_{n},\tilde{B}_{n}
and
\tilde{D}_{n}
as finite index subgroups. The underlying orbifolds have at most two cone points of order two. Based on
[
10
]
and
[
12
]
, we generalize this result allowing cone points of arbitrary order. In these cases, the orbifold braid groups contain similar subgroups of finite index. We show that in many cases these subgroups can be identified as certain complex braid groups.

1.  Introduction

An orbifold braid group is an analog of Artin braid groups or, more generally, surface braid groups. Instead of considering point collections moving inside a disk or a surface, orbifold braids move inside a
2
-dimensional orbifold. Orbifold braid groups attracted interest since some of them contain spherical and affine Artin groups of type
D_{n},\tilde{B}_{n}
and
\tilde{D}_{n}
as finite index subgroups by work of Allcock
[
1
]
. For these Artin groups, the orbifold braid groups provide us with braid pictures. In the following, Roushon published several articles on the structure of orbifold braid groups
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
and the contained Artin groups
[
21
]
. Moreover, Crisp–Paris
[
8
]
studied the outer automorphism group of the orbifold braid group. The present article generalizes the result of Allcock
[
1
]
.

As in the work of Roushon
[
22
]
, we consider braid groups on orbifolds with finitely many punctures and cone points (of possibly different orders). The underlying orbifolds are defined using the following data: Let
\Gamma
be a free product of finitely many finite cyclic groups. As such
\Gamma
acts on a planar, contractible surface
\Sigma
(with boundary). This surface can be obtained by thickening the Bass–Serre tree, see Section
2.1
for details. If we add
L
punctures, we obtain a similar orbifold as studied by Roushon, which we denote by
\Sigma_{\Gamma}(L)
. In contrast to his article, we consider orbifolds with non-empty boundary but this does not affect the structure of the orbifold braid groups. The only singular points in the orbifold
\Sigma_{\Gamma}(L)
are cone points that correspond to the finite cyclic factors of the free product
\Gamma
.

The elements of orbifold braid groups
\Z_{n}(\Sigma_{\Gamma}(L))
are represented by braid diagrams such as in Figure

Figure 1.1.

Using the relation to the orbifold mapping class group introduced in

, one can obtain a finite presentation of the orbifold braid group, see Theorem

and
[

The key difference to the Artin braid groups is that orbifold braid groups contain torsion elements, see Figure

2.  Preliminaries

2.1.  Orbifold braid groups

In this article, we only consider orbifolds that are given as the quotient of a manifold (typically a surface) by a proper group action. Recall that an action

\phi:G\rightarrow\HomeoOp({M}),g\mapsto\phi_{g}

on a manifold
M
is
proper
if for each compact set
K\subseteq M
the set

\{g\in G\mid\phi_{g}(K)\cap K\neq\emptyset\}

is compact. Since we endow
G
with the discrete topology, i.e. the above set is finite. Orbifolds that appear as proper quotients of manifolds are called
developable
in the terminology of Bridson–Haefliger
[
2
]
and
good
in the terminology of Thurston
[
26
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Orbifolds,
[
2
, Chapter III.G,1.3]
)
.

Let
M
be an orientable manifold, possibly with boundary, and
G
a group with a monomorphism

\phi:G\rightarrow\HomeoOp({M})

such that
G
acts properly on
M
. Under these conditions the
3
-tuple
(M,G,\phi)
is called an
orbifold
, which we denote by
M_{G}
. If
\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(c)\neq\{1\}
for a point
c\in M
, the point
c
is called a
singular point
of
M_{G}
. If
\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(c)
for a point
c\in M
is cyclic of finite order
m\geq 2
, the point
c
is called a
cone point
of
M_{G}
of order
m
.

A first example of an orbifold is the following:

Example 2.2
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
be a cyclic group of order
m
. The group
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
acts on a disk
D
by rotations around its center. The action is via isometries and the acting group is finite, i.e. the action is proper. Consequently,
D_{\mathbb{Z}_{m}}
is an orbifold with exactly one singular point in the center of
D
, which is a cone point.

Example
2.2
motivates a more general construction for a free product of finitely many finite cyclic groups which we describe briefly in the following. We will consider this generalization of the orbifold
D_{\mathbb{Z}_{m}}
throughout the article. For further details, we refer to the authors PhD thesis
[
11
, Section 2.1]
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a free product of finite cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}_{m_{1}},...,\mathbb{Z}_{m_{N}}
. The group
\Gamma
is the fundamental group of the following graph of groups with trivial edge groups:

As such
\Gamma
acts on its Bass–Serre tree

\gamma\mathbb{Z}_{m_{\nu}}\gamma^{-1}
with

with

Let us consider a proper embedding of the Bass–Serre tree

that respects the local cyclic order on each link. If we choose a regular neighborhood of

2.2.  Artin groups and complex braid groups

Artin groups
and
complex braid groups
are both generalizations of the Artin braid groups
\B_{n}
. While Artin groups generalize them on a combinatorial level, complex braid groups define a geometric analog of
\B_{n}
.

Artin groups are defined by the following data:

Definition 2.7

(Weighted graphs and Artin groups)
.

Let
\Delta
be a finite graph with vertex set
V=\{v_{1},...,v_{k}\}
and a set of edges
E
. The tuple
(\Delta,W)
is called a
weighted graph
if
W
is a subset of
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}\cup\{\infty\}
with a map
E\rightarrow W,\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\mapsto w_{ij}
. The number
w_{ij}
is called the
weight
of the edge
\{v_{i},v_{j}\}
. We attach a group
A(\Delta,W)
to the weighted graph
(\Delta,W)
that is presented by

\langle v_{1},...,v_{k}\mid\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle_{w_{ij}}=\langle v_{j},v_{i}\rangle_{w_{ij}}\text{ for }w_{ij}<\infty\text{ and }[v,w]=1\text{ if }\{v,w\}\not\in E\rangle.

This group is called the
Artin group
associated to the weighted graph
(\Delta,W)
. If we add the relation
v_{i}^{2}=1
for each
1\leq i\leq k
, we obtain the associated
Coxeter group
. An Artin group or a Coxeter group is called
irreducible
if the underlying graph
\Delta
is connected. In particular, in the chosen setting generators from different connected components of
\Delta
commute.

Even if the definition of Artin groups and Coxeter groups are similar, the current state of research about them differs greatly: Coxeter groups are well-understood, see
[
14
]
for an introduction. In contrast, only little is known about general Artin groups. In particular, the following basic questions remain open:

•

Is each Artin group torsion free?

•

What is the center of an Artin group?

•

Does each Artin group have a solvable word problem?

•

What can we say about the cohomology of an Artin group?

An approach to solve these questions is to identify classes in the set of Artin groups that share a certain property. In particular, the following classes gained importance:

•

right-angled Artin groups
(also known as
graph groups
), where all weights are
\infty
,

•

spherical Artin groups
, where the associated Coxeter group is finite and

•

affine Artin groups
, where the associated Coxeter group is affine, i.e. the Coxeter group is isomorphic to a group of affine reflections in a Euclidean space.

Definition 2.8

(Complex reflection groups and complex braid groups)

Let

Remark 2.9

Although the name complex reflection group suggests it, we do not require that the elements in

1\rightarrow\pi_{1}((V\setminus\bigcup_{r\in R}H_{r}))\rightarrow\pi_{1}((V\setminus\bigcup_{r\in R}H_{r})/W)\rightarrow W\rightarrow 1.

For an introduction to complex reflection groups, we refer to
[
16

Example 2.10

2.3.  Semidirect products

Definition 2.14
.

A group
G
is a
semidirect product
with
normal subgroup

N
and
quotient

H
if there exists a short exact sequence

1\rightarrow N\xrightarrow{\iota}G\xrightarrow{\pi}H\rightarrow 1

that has a section
s:H\rightarrow G
. In this case, we denote
G=N\rtimes H
.

In the following, presentations of groups will be an important tool for us. In particular, presentations allow us to define group homomorphisms by assignments defined on generating sets.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
G
be a group with presentation

\langle X\mid R\rangle=\langle x_{1},...,x_{k}\mid r_{1}=s_{1},...,r_{l}=s_{l}\rangle

and
H
a group generated by a set of elements
\{y_{1},...,y_{p}\}
with
p\geq k
. Moreover, let us assume that the words
r_{j}
and
s_{j}
are given by
x_{j_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}...x_{j_{q}}^{\varepsilon_{q}}
and
x_{\tilde{j}_{1}}^{\delta_{1}}...x_{\tilde{j}_{r}}^{\delta_{r}}
, respectively. Given
assignments

\phi:x_{i}\mapsto y_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
, we apply them letterwise to words mapping
x_{i}^{-1}
to
y_{i}^{-1}
. We say that the assignments
\phi

preserve the relations in
R
if the relation
y_{j_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}...y_{j_{q}}^{\varepsilon_{q}}=y_{\tilde{j}_{1}}^{\delta_{1}}...y_{\tilde{j}_{r}}^{\delta_{r}}
is valid in
H
for each
1\leq j\leq l
.

Theorem 2.16

(von Dyck,
[
20
, p. 346]
)
.

Let
G
be a group with presentation
\langle X\mid R\rangle
as above and
H
a group generated by
\{y_{1},...,y_{p}\}
with
p\geq k
. If the assignments

\phi:x_{i}\mapsto y_{i}\;\text{ for }\;1\leq i\leq k

preserve the relations in
R
, then these assignments induce a homomorphism

\phi:G\rightarrow H.

Lemma 2.17

(
[
11
, Lemma 5.17]
)
.

Let
N
and
H
be groups given by presentations
N=\langle X\mid R\rangle
and
H=\langle Y\mid S\rangle
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

G
is a semidirect product with normal subgroup
N
and quotient
H
.

(2)

G
has a presentation

G=\langle X,Y\mid R,S,y^{\pm 1}xy^{\mp 1}=\phi_{y^{\pm 1}}(x)\text{ for all }x\in X,y\in Y\rangle

such that
\phi_{y^{\pm 1}}(x)
is a word in the alphabet
X
for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
. Moreover, for each
y\in Y
, the assignments

(4)

x\mapsto\phi_{y}(x)

induce an automorphism
\phi_{y}\in\Aut(N)
and the assignments

(5)

y\mapsto\phi_{y}

induce a homomorphism
H\rightarrow\Aut(N)
.

Remark 2.18
.

Lemma
2.17
will be applied in the rest of the article several times. We will apply it given a group
G
with a presentation to deduce that
G
is a semidirect product. In this case, we want to show that the given presentation satisfies the conditions from Lemma
2.17(2)
.

The base to prove that is to divide the generating set into two disjoint subsets
X
and
Y
such that
X
generates the normal subgroup and
Y
generates the quotient.

Further, we divide the relations into three disjoint subsets
R,S
and
C
such that
R
contains all relations in letters from
X
,
S
contains all relations in letters from
Y
and
C
contains all the remaining relations. In particular, the relations in
C
should be given in the form
y^{\pm 1}xy^{\mp 1}=\phi_{y^{\pm 1}}(x)
for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
. To deduce a semidirect product structure, it remains to check that the relations from
C
satisfy the conditions on the assignments (
4
) and (
5
).

It is reasonable to check these conditions in the following order:

Step 1
.

Using Theorem
2.16
, the first step will always be to check that the assignments
\phi:y\mapsto\phi_{y}
from (
5
) preserve the relations from
S
. If
S
contains a relation
y_{1}^{\varepsilon_{1}}...y_{q}^{\varepsilon_{q}}=\tilde{y}_{1}^{\delta_{1}}...\tilde{y}_{r}^{\delta_{r}}
, this requires that the assignments

x\mapsto\phi_{y_{1}^{\varepsilon_{1}}}\circ...\circ\phi_{y_{q}^{\varepsilon_{q}}}(x)\;\text{ and }\;x\mapsto\phi_{\tilde{y}_{1}^{\delta_{1}}}\circ...\circ\phi_{\tilde{y}_{r}^{\delta_{r}}}(x)

coincide on each letter
x\in X
(up to relations in
R
).

In particular, we may check if
\phi
induces a homomorphism independently of the fact if
x\mapsto\phi_{y}(x)
induces an automorphism of the group presented by
\langle X\mid R\rangle
.

Step 2
.

In the second step, we check that the assignments
\phi_{y}:x\mapsto\phi_{y}(x)
induce an automorphism of the group presented by
\langle X\mid R\rangle
for all
y\in Y
.

To apply Theorem
2.16
, we check if for all
y\in Y
the assignments
\phi_{y}
preserve the relations from
R
. If this is the case, the assignments
\phi_{y}
induce an endomorphism of the group presented by
\langle X\mid R\rangle
. By the first step, we further have

\phi_{y^{-1}}\circ\phi_{y}=\id_{\langle X\mid R\rangle}=\phi_{y}\circ\phi_{y^{-1}},

i.e. the endomorphism induced by
x\mapsto\phi_{y}(x)
is bijective and therefore an automorphism of the group presented by
\langle X\mid R\rangle
.

3.  Relating orbifold braids and complex braid groups

In
[
1
]
, Allcock proved the following structural result on orbifold braid groups:

\displaystyle\Z_{n}(D_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}})

\displaystyle=A(D_{n})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2},

\displaystyle\Z_{n}(D_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(1))

\displaystyle=A(\tilde{B}_{n})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}\text{ and }

\displaystyle\Z_{n}(\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2}})

\displaystyle=A(\tilde{D}_{n})\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}).

Allcock’s proofs are based on results of Brieskorn
[
3
]
and Nguyen
[
27
]
about orbit spaces of spherical and affine reflection groups, respectively. This restricts the approach to orbifold braid groups with cone points of order two such that the sum of the number of cone points and punctures is at most two.

In contrast, the following characterizations are based on the presentation of the orbifold braid group
\Z_{n}(\Sigma_{\Gamma}(L))
given in Theorem
2.4
. This allows us to relax the condition on the order of the cone points. The restriction to the case where the sum of the number of cone points
N
and the number of punctures

is at most two reflects the geometry of the braids: the strands are ordered in a row with two ends and we can either attach a cone point or a puncture at each of the two ends. Thus, we have to keep this restriction.

For the rest of the chapter, let

be an orbifold braid group with at most two cone points of order

and

such that

.

Besides the presentation of

\displaystyle=\langle h_{1},...,h_{n-1},u\rangle,

\displaystyle\Z_{n}(D_{\mathbb{Z}_{m}}(1))

\displaystyle=\langle h_{1},...,h_{n-1},t,\bar{u}\rangle\text{ and }

\displaystyle\Z_{n}(\Sigma_{\mathbb{Z}_{m}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{m^{\prime}}})

\displaystyle=\langle h_{1},...,h_{n-1},u,u^{\prime}\rangle.

For the following characterization, we add further redundant generators to these sets. In the first case, we add

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

. Moreover, let

be the minimal number such that

. The group
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta_{n}^{m})
contains
\theta_{n}^{k}
if and only if
k
is a multiple of

.

Proof.

Let us assume that
k
is a multiple of
l
. We recall that

\theta_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def_theta_n}}}{{=}}\gamma_{n}\gamma_{n-1}...\gamma_{1}\;\text{ with }\;\gamma_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def_gamma_j}}}{{=}}h_{j-1}...h_{1}uh_{1}...h_{j-1}.

In particular,
\gamma_{1}=u
is an element of order
m
. Since
m
divides
ln
, one has
u^{ln}=1
and consequently
u^{l}=u^{-l(n-1)}
.

(149)

\displaystyle\theta_{n}^{l}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[212.07019pt][c]{\scriptsize\eqref{eq:def_theta_n}}}}{{=}}(\gamma_{n}\gamma_{n-1}...\gamma_{1})^{l}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[212.07019pt][c]{\scriptsize(\ref{prop:D_Z_m_central_el_eq_gamma_i_gamma_j})}}}{{=}}u^{l}\gamma_{2}^{l}...\gamma_{n}^{l}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[212.07019pt][c]{\scriptsize}}}{{=}}u^{-l(n-1)}\gamma_{2}^{l}...\gamma_{n}^{l}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[212.07019pt][c]{\scriptsize\eqref{eq:def_gamma_j}}}}{{=}}((h_{1}uh_{1}u^{-1})(h_{2}h_{1}uh_{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}h_{2}u^{-1}})...(h_{n-1}...h_{1}uh_{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}...h_{n-1}u^{-1}}))^{l}
