Classifying toric 3-fold codes of dimensions 4 and 5
Tori Braun, James Carzon, Jenna Gorham, Kelly Jabbusch

Abstract
. A toric code is an error-correcting code determined by a toric variety or its associated integral convex polytope. We investigate 4 4 - and 5 5 -dimensional toric 3 3 -fold codes, which are codes arising from polytopes in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} with four and five lattice points, respectively. By computing the minimum distances of each code, we fully classify the 4 4 -dimensional codes. We further present progress toward the same goal for dimension 5 5 codes. In particular, we classify the 5 5 -dimensional toric 3 3 -fold codes arising from polytopes of width 1.



1.
Introduction

It is unknown in general whether an error correcting code will be “good” - say, in terms of the number of errors which it can dependably handle - until its parameters are computed, leading to an interest in classifying all the possible distinct codes. Some impressive results have come from particular toric codes, which Hansen introduced as the natural extension of Reed-Solomon codes in 1998
[
3
]
. By the correspondence between toric varieties and integral convex polytopes, one obtains a toric code
C_{P}
determined by a polytope
P
. It is our imperative to be able to determine in general whether a code
C_{P}
is the same as a code
C_{P^{\prime}}
, where
P\neq P^{\prime}
, in an essential way.

Many previous authors have written toward answering this question in particular cases. Little and Schwarz classified toric surface codes - toric codes corresponding to polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
- of dimensions up to 5
[
5
]
. Luo, Yau, Zhang, and Zuo furthered this to toric surface codes up to 6 dimensions
[
6
]
, and later these same authors further classified most of the 7 dimension codes with Hussain
[
4
]
. The classification of 7 dimension codes was independently carried out by Cairncross, Ford, Garcia, and Jabbusch
[
2
]
. Departing slightly from the trend of these investigations, we present original theorems disambiguating the toric codes which are furnished by polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with up to 4 lattice points. We refer to these as toric 3-fold codes of dimension 4 (as compared to
toric surface codes
) to avoid confusion. We then make progress toward a classification of toric 3-fold codes of dimension 5 by considering those polytopes structurally very similar to those of dimension 4.

At a high level, a toric code
C
is constructed by selecting a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and a convex lattice polytope
P
in
[0,q-2]^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Codewords are obtained from evaluating linear combinations of monomials arising from the lattice points of
P
at points in
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*})^{m}
, and so one can represent the code by a generator matrix whose row space is the dictionary of codewords. A code can be further described by parameters such as
length
, the number of points in
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*})^{m}
, denoted
n
;
dimension
, the number of lattice points
k=\lvert P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m}\rvert
; and
minimum distance
, the minimum Hamming distance between codewords, denoted
d
. Note that we refer to
k
as the dimension of
C
and denote it
dim(C)
, which is not to be confused with the dimension
m
of the space in which
P
is embedded. Two codes will be considered the same if they are
monomially equivalent
, a term defined shortly.

Note that all toric codes obtained from polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
containing fewer than 4 lattice points are trivially classified because they can also be embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and have been previously studied. Likewise, for the purposes of this paper we will also overlook those codes furnished by polytopes of 4 or 5 lattice points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which can be embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
since they are also well known.

More explicitly, the construction of a toric code is as follows. Selecting a finite field of order
q
a prime power and a lattice convex polytope
P
in
[0,q-2]^{m}
with
k
lattice points, one obtains a toric code
C_{P}
by the vector space

, where the map
\epsilon:\mathcal{L}(P)\rightarrow(\mathbb{F}_{q})^{n}
evaluates polynomials

in

\mathcal{L}(P)=\text{Span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{\textbf{x}^{p}:p\in P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m}\}

at each point in
(\mathbb{F}_{q})^{m}
. For example, let
q=11
and let

be the tetrahedron with vertices
(0,0,0)
,
(1,0,0)
,
(0,0,1)
, and
(1,1,1)
. (The significance of this choice is explained shortly.) Then we construct the collection of polynomials.

\mathcal{L}(P)=\{ax^{0}y^{0}z^{0}+bx^{1}y^{0}z^{0}+cx^{0}y^{0}z^{1}+dx^{1}y^{1}z^{1}:a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{F}_{11}\}.

We may then select a polynomial

in
\mathcal{L}(P)
and evaluate it at each of the

. Our resulting codeword is the

(f(1,1,1),f(2,1,1),\ldots,f(11,11,11))

may be in more than one indeterminate. The generator matrix

is the matrix

Let

and

be toric codes with generator matrices

and

respectively.

We say

. For the toric 3-fold codes of dimension 4, we may make ready use of a classic theorem by White.

Every 3-polytope with 4 integral lattice points of volume

1.1.
Statements of our theorems

Therefore an outline of our approach to classifying four dimensional codes is as follows. Firstly, we provide some preliminary machinery in Section 3, most notably a formula for the minimum distances of four dimensional codes.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any four dimensional toric code on an empty tetrahedron
T(s,t)
, the minimum distance is

d(C_{T(s,t)})=\begin{cases}(q-1)^{3}-(q-1)^{2}&\mbox{if }\gcd(t,q-1)=1\\
(q-1)^{3}-(q-1)(q-3)-q\gcd(t,q-1)&\mbox{else.}\end{cases}

Secondly, we inquire about the equivalence classes which we obtain when we fix
s
. To answer for this case, we prove the first classification theorem:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be four dimensional toric codes on empty tetrahedra
T(s,t_{1})
and
T(s,t_{2})
, respectively, over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are monomially equivalent if and only if
\gcd(t_{1},q-1)=\gcd(t_{2},q-1)
.

Lastly, if we conversely fix the parameter
t
, then we determine the equivalence classes in this case by proving the second classification theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be four dimensional toric codes on empty tetrahedra
T(s_{1},t)
and
T(s_{2},t)
, respectively, over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. If either of the following conditions hold true, then
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are monomially equivalent:

•

s_{1}\equiv s_{2}\bmod\gcd(t,q-1)

•

s_{1}\equiv\pm s_{2}^{\pm 1}\bmod t

Furthermore, if neither of these hold, then the codes are not monomially equivalent.

The proofs of these theorems are undertaken in Section 4, and we thereby obtain a full classification of these toric codes.

Section 5 consists of describing the codes of dimension 5 and proving several results. In particular, we identify what monomial equivalence classes exist for codes arising from polytopes with 5 lattice points, all of which can be found in the union of two consecutive hyperplanes. (In other words, these are the polytopes of width 1 per the terminology used by Blanco and Santos
[
1
]
.) However, we stop short of a full classification of these toric codes by not addressing those codes of width 2.

Throughout, much of our geometric notation and terminology will respect that of White and of Blanco and Santos. For example, the toric code furnished by the empty tetrahedron
T(s,t)
will be written as
C_{T(s,t)}
, and in Section 5 we will use the invariant of a polytope with 5 lattice points which they call the
signature

P
has signature
(i,j)
and parameter values
s
and
t
, then from it we obtain the code
C_{P^{(i,j)}(s,t)}
. We will then refer to the signature of the polytope furnishing a code as the signature of the code as well.

2.
Minimum distances for dimension 4

A premium tool for evidencing the monomial inequivalence of two toric codes is the minimum distance invariant. If two codes are monomially equivalent, then they must have the same minimum distance. We now formally treat the dimension 4 case’s minimum distances.

Definition 2.0.1
.

The minimum distance of a code
C
is

d(C)=\underset{f\neq g\in\mathcal{L}(P)\setminus\{0\}}{min}h(\epsilon(f),\epsilon(g)),

where
h(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Hamming metric.

Recall that the Hamming metric

counts the number of components which are distinct between two tuples of equal length. Alternatively, we will use the following fact:

Remark 2.0.2

An equivalent expression for the minimum distance of a toric code

corresponding to a polytope

is

(2)

d(C)=(q-1)^{m}-\underset{0\neq f\in\mathcal{L}(P)}{max}Z(f),

where
Z(f)
is the number of zeros of
f

Computing different maximum numbers of zeros for polynomials furnished by codes from different polytopes is a simple test that the codes are
not
monomially equivalent. We also make use of a theorem by Soprunov and Soprunova.

(

\mathbb{R}^{2}

in

.) This is equivalent to

which is in turn equivalent to (

and

are monomially equivalent.

The second condition for monomial equivalence follows from White’s theorem classifying four point polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. These two conditions are sufficient for monomial equivalence.

(\Leftarrow)
We now turn to prove that these conditions are necessary. Suppose

and

are not lattice equivalent but

and

as in Definition

. (That is, a nontrivial diagonal matrix amounts to a reordering of the rows of

(or lattice points in

[

.) Furthermore, assume that
s_{2}-s_{1}\not\equiv 0\bmod\gcd(t,q-1)

for some

Because there exists a permutation between the generator matrices of

∎

4.1.
Minimum distances for dimension 5

We now offer and prove the following statements on the minimum distances of dimension 5 toric codes from only the polytopes of width 1.

Theorem 4.1.1

(Minimum Distances for Five Dimensions)
.

For the five point polytopes of width 1, we obtain the following minimum distance formulae and bounds:

(9)

\displaystyle d(C_{P^{(2,1)}(s,t)})

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{3}-2(q-1)^{2}

(10)

\displaystyle d(C_{P^{(2,2)}})

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{3}-(2q^{2}-5q+3)

(11)

\displaystyle d(C_{P^{(3,1)}})

\displaystyle\geq(q-1)^{3}-(q-1)(1+q+2\sqrt{q})

(12)

\displaystyle d(C_{P^{(3,2)}(s,t)})

\displaystyle\geq(q-1)^{3}-(q-2)^{2}-(s+t)q

(13)

\displaystyle d(C_{P^{(3,2)}(s,t)})

\displaystyle\leq(q-1)^{3}-(q-1)(q-3)-q\gcd(s+t,q-1)

Proof.

The cases (
10
) and (
11
) are the simplest cases. Recall that the minimum distance of the toric code from
P^{\prime(3)}_{4}
, the unit square in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, is
(q-1)^{3}-(2q-3)(q-1)
and that from the exceptional triangle
P^{\prime(4)}_{4}
is bounded by
d\geq(q-1)^{3}-(1+q+2\sqrt{q})(q-1)
. Since the five point polytopes with signatures
(2,2)
and
(3,1)
are exactly the unit pyramids over the unit square and the exceptional triangle, respectively, we have that their minimum distances are increased by a factor of
(q-1)

[
7
, Theorem 2.3]
.

Consider now (
9
). Note that
(x+1)(x^{-1}-1)
has
2(q-1)^{2}
zeros. We claim that the polynomial

f=A+Bx+Cx^{-1}+Dz+Ex^{s}y^{t}z

has no more than
2(q-1)^{2}
zeros. If
E=0
, then any polynomial
f=A+Bx+Cx^{-1}+Dz
will have no more than
2(q-1)^{2}
zeros, since we have the unit pyramid over the line in the plane with three lattice points, which gives a code of minimum distance
(q-1)^{3}-2(q-1)^{2}
. So we may assume that
E\neq 0
.
Call
P=A+Bx+Cx^{-1}
and
Q=D+Ex^{s}y^{t}
.

We reduce this to two cases.

Case 1.
Suppose
P
has no solutions. Then
f=0
only if
Q\neq 0
so that we may select
x
and
y
freely and let
z=-PQ^{-1}
. Using the notation
N(x)
similarly to its use in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, giving the number of values of
y
for which
Q_{x}=0
for a fixed

, we see that there are

\displaystyle Z(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}[(q-1)-N(x)]

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{2}-\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}N(x)

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{2}-(q-1)

\displaystyle<2(q-1)^{2}

zeros of

, proving this case of the claim.

Case 2.
Suppose
P
has two solutions. Call them
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. Then
f=0
if

,
y
is one of

choices so that
Q=0
, and

is anything at all or likewise if

and
y
is one of
N(x_{2})

as in Case 1. Therefore we have

\displaystyle Z(f)

\displaystyle=(q-1)(N(x_{1})+N(x_{2}))+\sum_{x\neq x_{1},x_{2}}[(q-1)-N(x)]

\displaystyle=q(N(x_{1})+N(x_{2}))+(q-1)(q-4)

zeros. Recall that
N(x)=0
or
d=\gcd(t,q-1)
. Note also that since
d<q-1
and
d\mid(q-1)
, we know that
d\leq\frac{q-1}{2}
and so

\displaystyle Z(f)

\displaystyle=q(N(x_{1})+N(x_{2}))+(q-1)(q-4)

\displaystyle\leq 2qd+(q-1)(q-4)

\displaystyle\leq q(q-1)+(q-1)(q-4)

\displaystyle=2(q-1)(q-2)

\displaystyle<2(q-1)^{2}.

This proves our claim for this case as well.

We turn to (
12

f=A+Bx+Cy+Dz+Ex^{s}y^{t}z

(q-1)-N

are the sum of those giving

(q-1)^{2}-[(q-1)-N]-[(q-2)-N]-N=(q-2)^{2}+N,

Proceeding as we have before, we use Theorem

(2,1)

. Finally, we show that codes furnished by polytopes of width 1 and different signatures are not monomially equivalent.

4.2.
Classification for dimension 5

Proposition 4.2.1
.

Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be toric codes on five point polytopes
P^{(2,1)}(s,t)
and
P^{(2,1)}(s,t^{\prime})
, respectively, over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are monomially equivalent if and only if
\gcd(t,q-1)=\gcd(t^{\prime},q-1)
.

Proof.

(
\Leftarrow
) As was the case with Theorem
1.2
, we have monomial equivalence when we find a permutation matrix
\Pi
which matches the columns corresponding to
I_{1}(x_{0},z_{0},A)
with those of
I_{2}(x_{0},z_{0},A)
for all
(x_{0},z_{0},A)\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*})^{3}
. Indeed, since we are only including in the generator matrices the additional row determined by
\epsilon(x^{-1})
, we may repeat that previous argument exactly to conclude the sufficiency of this condition.

(
\Rightarrow
) We don’t have the luxury of a discerning minimum distance formula in this case, so instead suppose
A
is in the image of both of the automorphisms
a\mapsto a^{t}
and
a\mapsto a^{t^{\prime}}
. We cannot create the desired permutation
\Pi
matching the
\gcd(t,q-1)
columns
I_{1}(x_{0},z_{0},A)
to the
\gcd(t^{\prime},q-1)
columns
I_{2}(x_{0},z_{0},A)
, and so
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are not monomially equivalent.
∎

Proposition 4.2.2
.

Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be toric codes on five point polytopes
P^{(2,1)}(s,t)
and
P^{(2,1)}(s^{\prime},t)
, respectively, over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are monomially equivalent if and only if
s\equiv s^{\prime}\bmod\gcd(t,q-1)
.

Proof.

Unlike with Theorem
1.3
, there are no nontrivial lattice equivalences between signature
(2,1)
polytopes. Otherwise this proposition is proved identically to that of the first condition in the statement of that theorem.
∎

Proposition 4.2.3
.

Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be toric codes on five point polytopes
P^{(3,2)}(s,t)
and
P^{(3,2)}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
, respectively, over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are monomially equivalent if and only if
(s,t)=(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Suppose
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are monomially equivalent so that their generators
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are chosen such that
G_{2}=G_{1}\Pi
. Thus
G_{2}e_{m}=G_{1}\Pi e_{m}
for each
m=1,\ldots,(q-1)^{3}
. In particular, we know that

G_{2}e_{m}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
x\\
y\\
z\\
x^{s}y^{t}z\end{bmatrix}=G_{1}\Pi e_{m}

for some
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. It follows that
x^{s}y^{t}z=x^{s^{\prime}}y^{t^{\prime}}z
; likewise for all
(x,y,z)
. Of course, by choosing
(x,1,1)
we see that
x^{s}=x^{s^{\prime}}
for all
x
. Similarly, by choosing
(1,y,1)
we see that
y^{t}=y^{t^{\prime}}
for all
y
. It follows that
s=s^{\prime}
and
t=t^{\prime}
.
∎

Proposition 4.2.4
.

Let
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
be five point polytopes of width 1. Then
C_{P_{1}}
and
C_{P_{2}}
are monomially equivalent only if
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
have the same signature.

Note well that having the same signature is not sufficient for monomial equivalence.

Proof.

This proposition amounts to stating that we obtain distinct codes when choosing polytopes of different signatures at the outset. We conclude this by first considering the minimum distance information that we have about all of these cases. Firstly, there is no risk of equivalence between polytopes of signatures
(2,1)
and
(2,2)
since for a fixed
q
the furnished codes have different minimum distances given by the formulas.

If
q\geq 8
, then there can be no equivalence between
(2,1)
and
(3,1)
since
2(q-1)^{2}>(q-1)(1+q+2\sqrt{q})
in that case. To fully justify that codes from these polytopes will never be equivalent, a manual verification that minimum distances (or another invariant) differ for
q<8
will suffice. Comparing
(2,1)
and
(3,2)
, we require matching shape geometric parameters for both polytopes so that
x^{s}y^{t}z=x^{s^{\prime}}y^{t^{\prime}}z
in general. However, since
x^{-1}\neq y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
in general, we can have no equivalent codes. Comparing
(2,2)
and
(3,1)
, if
q\geq 11
, then
(q-1)(2q-3)>(q-1)(q+q+2\sqrt{q})
, so we can have no equivalent codes. If
q<11
, a manual check shows that the codes are distinct. Comparing
(2,2)
and
(3,2)
, since
x^{s}y^{t}z\neq xy
in general, we can have no equivalent codes. Finally, comparing
(3,1)
and
(3,2)
, since
x^{s}y^{t}z\neq x^{-1}y^{-1}
in general, we can have no equivalent codes.
∎

In the cases where signature
(3,2)
occurs in comparison with codes of another signature, note that our minimum distance intervals are not tight enough to conclude monomial inequivalence. However, it is easy to check that the generator matrices are distinct because
P^{(3,2)}(s,t)
,
P^{(3,1)}
, and
P^{(2,2)}
all share four lattice points and
P^{(3,2)}(s,t)
and
P^{(2,1)}(s,t)
share four lattice points.

The patterns one will observe in the minimum distances of codes furnished by five point polytopes of width 2 are somewhat less tractable. It would be interesting to uncover what common and salient features there are of those polynomials’ vanishing points in general. We end our classification of 3-fold toric codes at the injunction demarcated by this nontrivial question.
