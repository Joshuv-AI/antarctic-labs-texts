Symmetric Monoidal Smash Products in Homotopy Type Theory
Axel Ljungström

Abstract




Abstract:

In Homotopy Type Theory, few constructions have proved as troublesome
as the smash product. While its definition is just as direct as in
classical mathematics, one quickly realises that in order to define
and reason about functions over iterations of it, one has to verify an
exponentially growing number of coherences. This has led to crucial
results concerning smash products remaining open. One particularly
important such result is the fact that smash products form a
(1-coherent) symmetric monoidal product on the universe of pointed
types. This fact was used, without a complete proof, by
e.g.
Brunerie 2016
in his PhD thesis to construct the cup product
on integral cohomology and is, more generally, a fundamental result in
traditional algebraic topology. In this paper, we salvage the
situation by introducing a simple informal heuristic for reasoning
about functions defined over iterated smash products. We then use the
heuristic to verify e.g. the hexagon and pentagon identities, thereby
obtaining a proof of symmetric monoidality. We also provide a formal
statement of the heuristic in terms of an induction principle
concerning the construction of homotopies of functions defined over
iterated smash products. The key results presented here have been
formalised in the proof assistant
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}Cubical}

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}Agda}
.

1
Introduction

In his 2016 proof of
\pi_{4}(S^{3})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
in Homotopy Type
Theory (HoTT),
Brunerie 2016
crucially uses—but never proves in
detail—that the smash product is (1-coherent) symmetric
monoidal. Due to the vast amount of path algebra involved when
reasoning about smash products in HoTT, this has since remained open. While it
turns out that smash products are not needed for Brunerie’s
proof
[
Ljungström and Mörtberg 2023
]
, the problem is still interesting in its own
right.

Several attempts have been made at salvaging the
situation.
Van Doorn 2018
came very close by considering an
argument using closed monoidal categories but left a gap where the
path algebra became too technical. To be more precise, Van Doorn never
verified that the equivalence

(A\wedge B\to_{\star}C)\simeq(A\to_{\star}(B\to_{\star}C))

is a
pointed natural equivalence. Another line of attack by
Cavallo and Harper
[
Cavallo and Harper 2020
;
Cavallo 2021
]
is the addition
of parametricity to the type theory, which leads to a rather ingenious
proof of the theorem. This, of course, happens at the expense of making
the type theory more complicated. Yet another solution was studied by

Brunerie 2018
who used
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}Agda}
meta-programming to generate
the relevant proofs. Possible philosophical objections to such a
solution aside, Brunerie’s generated proof of the pentagon identity
failed to type-check due to its eating up too much memory.

In this paper, we provide another solution by introducing an informal
heuristic for reasoning about functions defined over iterated smash
products. This approach vastly reduces the complexity of the identity proofs involved. We
use this to give a complete proof of the fact that the smash product
is 1-coherent symmetric monoidal. We finally discuss how to make the heuristic formal
and express (a version of it) as a theorem which we also prove.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
,
we introduce the two fundamental concepts of interest, namely
(1-coherent) symmetric monoidal (wild) categories and smash
products. Section
3
introduces a new higher inductive
type (HIT) capturing double smash products. We use this to sketch the
construction of the associator map
\alpha_{A,B,C}:(A\wedge B)\wedge C\xrightarrow{\sim}A\wedge(B\wedge C)
. In
Section
4
, we make a small detour and discuss
induction principles for the smash product. It is here that we
introduce the heuristic mentioned above. In
Section
5
, we apply our heuristic and sketch the
proof of the symmetric monoidality of the smash product. Finally, in
Section
6
, we discuss the translation of our heuristic
into a formal result. We provide one suggestion and prove it correct.

This paper is written in the informal flavour of type theory employed
in e.g. the HoTT Book
[
The Univalent Foundations Program 2013
]
. Nevertheless, all key results have been
formalised in the proof assistant

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}Cubical}

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}Agda}

[
Vezzosi et al. 2021
]
, a cubical extension of

\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}Agda}
which, in particular, enjoys native support for HITs. A file summarising the relevant formalisations can be found in
the
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}agda/cubical}
library at

https://github.com/agda/cubical/blob/master/Cubical/Papers/SmashProducts.agda

(and, alternatively, on a frozen branch at

https://github.com/aljungstrom/cubical/blob/smashpaper/Cubical/Papers/SmashProducts.agda
).

2
Background

Let us briefly introduce the key concepts of this paper: 1-coherent symmetric
monoidal (wild) categories and smash products. We assume familiarity
with HoTT and refer to the HoTT Book
[
The Univalent Foundations Program 2013
]
for the basic
constructions and definitions used here.

2.1
Symmetric monoidal wild categories

To make the statements in this paper somewhat more compact, we will
employ the framework of
wild categories
. These are defined to be just like categories but
without any h-level assumptions
[
Capriotti and Kraus 2017
]
:

Definition 1

(Wild categories)
.

A wild category is a category with a
type
of objects and

types
of morphisms.

The difference between a wild category and a category is that the latter asks for
sets
of morphisms. In this paper, the wild category of interest is that of pointed types.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathsf{Type}_{\star}
denote the universe of pointed types (at
some universe level). This universe forms a wild category with

\mathsf{Type}_{\star}[A,B]:=(A\to_{\star}B)
, i.e. with pointed functions as morphisms.

The main goal of this paper is to show that
\mathsf{Type}_{\star}
is not only a wild category but a (1-coherent)
symmetric monoidal
wild category, so let us define this.

Definition 3

(Monoidal wild categories)
.

A monoidal wild category is a wild category
M
with

•

a functor
\otimes:M\times M\to M
,

•

a unit, i.e. an element
I:M
with natural
isomorphisms
\lambda_{A}:I\otimes A\cong A
and
\rho_{A}:A\otimes I\cong A
,

•

a family of isomorphisms
\alpha_{A,B,C}:((A\otimes B)\otimes C)\cong(A\otimes(B\otimes C))
natural in all arguments
such that

–

the
triangle identity
holds, i.e. the following diagram commutes,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(A\otimes I)\otimes B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\otimes(I\otimes B)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\otimes B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{A,I,B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho_{A}\otimes 1_{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1_{A}\otimes\lambda_{B}}

–

the
pentagon identity
holds, i.e. the following diagram commutes.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{((A\otimes B)\otimes C)\otimes D}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(A\otimes(B\otimes C))\otimes D}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(A\otimes B)\otimes(C\otimes D)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\otimes((B\otimes C)\otimes D)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\otimes(B\otimes(C\otimes D))}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{A,B,C}\otimes 1_{D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{A,B\otimes C,D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{A\otimes B,C,D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{A,B,C\otimes D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1_{A}\otimes\alpha_{B,C,D}}

Definition 4

(1-coherent symmetric monoidal wild categories)
.

A 1-coherent symmetric monoidal wild category is a monoidal wild category equipped with a family of isomorphisms
\beta_{A,B}:A\otimes B\cong B\otimes A
, natural in both arguments, such that

•

\beta_{B,A}\circ\beta_{A,B}=1_{A\otimes B}
,

•

the
hexagon identity
holds, i.e. the following diagram commutes.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(A\otimes B)\otimes C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(B\otimes A)\otimes C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\otimes(B\otimes C)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{B\otimes(A\otimes C)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(B\otimes C)\otimes A}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{B\otimes(C\otimes A)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta_{A,B}\otimes 1_{C}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{A,B,C}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta_{A,B\otimes C}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{B,C,A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{B,A,C}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1_{B}\otimes\beta_{A,C}}

2.2
Smash products

The model of the smash product we will use here is given by the
cofibre of the inclusion
A\vee B\hookrightarrow A\times B
,
i.e. the following (homotopy) pushout.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\vee B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\times B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\wedge B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

For the sake of clarity, let us spell this out in detail by giving explicit names to all constructors of
A\wedge B
. We give the smash product the following self-contained definition.

Definition 5

(Smash products)
.

The smash product of two pointed types
A
and
B
is the HIT generated by

•

a point
\star_{\wedge}:A\wedge B
,

•

points
{\langle a,b\rangle}:A\wedge B
for every pair
(a,b):A\times B
,

•

paths
{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(a)}:{\langle a,\star_{B}\rangle}=\star_{\wedge}
for every point
a:A
,

•

paths
{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(b)}:{\langle\star_{A},b\rangle}=\star_{\wedge}
for every point
b:B
,

•

a coherence
{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{lr}}}}:{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(\star_{A})}={{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(\star_{B})}
.

We always take
A\wedge B
to be pointed by
\star_{\wedge}
.

We remark we could equivalently have defined the smash product by the following pushout.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A+B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\times B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{1+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\wedge B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

This definition has the advantage of not having any
2
-dimensional
path constructors but has the disadvantage of having an
additional point constructor. It turns out that Definition
5
suits our
purposes better, so we will stick with it.

Let us also verify that the smash product is functorial. In what
follows, a pointed function
A\to_{\star}B
is a function
f:A\to B
equipped
with a proof of pointedness
\star_{f}:f(\star_{A})=\star_{B}
.

Definition 6
.

For two pointed functions
f:A\to_{\star}C
and
g:B\to_{\star}D
, there is an induced map
f\wedge g:A\wedge B\to_{\star}C\wedge D
defined by

\displaystyle(f\wedge g)\,(\star_{\wedge})

\displaystyle=\star_{\wedge}

\displaystyle(f\wedge g)\,\langle a,b\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f(a),g(a)\rangle

\displaystyle{\mathsf{ap}}_{f\wedge g}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(a)})}

\displaystyle={\mathsf{ap}}_{\langle f(a),-\rangle}{(\star_{g})}\cdot{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(f(a))}

\displaystyle{\mathsf{ap}}_{f\wedge g}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(b)})}

\displaystyle={\mathsf{ap}}_{\langle-,g(b)\rangle}{(\star_{f})}\cdot{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(g(b))}

\displaystyle{\mathsf{ap}}_{\mathsf{ap}_{f\wedge g}}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{lr}}}})}

\displaystyle=\dots

where the omitted case is a simple coherence which will not matter
for any future constructions or proofs. We take this map to be
pointed by
\mathsf{refl}
.

We also take the opportunity to mention the commutativity of smash
products. This is trivial since the definition of
A\wedge B
is entirely
symmetric in both arguments.

Proposition 7
.

The swap map
A\times B\to B\times A
induces a pointed
equivalence
A\wedge B\simeq_{\star}B\wedge A
.

3
Associativity

Proving that the smash product is associative is far less straightforward
than proving that it is commutative. In fact, even the seemingly direct
task of constructing the associator map is no mean feat. While associativity
has already been verified by
Van Doorn 2018
and, using a
computer generated proof, by
Brunerie 2018
, let us give a
direct construction of the equivalence. We do this because an explicit description makes the associator easier to trace when verifying e.g. the
pentagon identity. For this purpose, let us introduce a new HIT
capturing double smash products in a way which is neutral with respect
to the distribution of parentheses.

Definition 8
.

Given pointed types
A
,
B
and
C
, we define the type
\bigwedge(A,B,C)
to be the HIT generated by

•

a point
\star_{2\wedge}
,

•

points
\langle a,b,c\rangle:\bigwedge(A,B,C)
for each triple of points
(a,b,c):A\times B\times C
,

•

paths
{{\mathsf{push}}_{0}(b,c)}:\langle\star_{A},b,c\rangle=\star_{2\wedge}
for each pair
(b,c):B\times C
,

•

paths
{{\mathsf{push}}_{1}(a,c)}:\langle a,\star_{B},c\rangle=\star_{2\wedge}
for each pair
(a,c):A\times C
,

•

paths
{{\mathsf{push}}_{2}(a,b)}:\langle a,b,\star_{C}\rangle=\star_{2\wedge}
for each pair
(a,b):A\times B
,

•

paths
{{\mathsf{push}}_{1,2}(a)}:{{\mathsf{push}}_{1}(a,\star_{C})}={{\mathsf{push}}_{2}(a,\star_{B})}
for
a:A

•

paths
{{\mathsf{push}}_{0,2}(b)}:{{\mathsf{push}}_{0}(b,\star_{C})}={{\mathsf{push}}_{2}(\star_{A},b)}

•

{{\mathsf{push}}_{0,1}(c)}:{{\mathsf{push}}_{0}(\star_{B},c)}={{\mathsf{push}}_{1}(\star_{A},c)}

Verifying that this map indeed defines an equivalence is somewhat
laborious but direct; the interested reader is referred to the computer formalisation. We get the associativity of the smash product as
a consequence.

Proposition 9
.

There is a pointed equivalence
\alpha_{A,B,C}:(A\wedge B)\wedge C\simeq_{\star}A\wedge(B\wedge C)

Proof.

Observe that
\bigwedge(A,B,C)
is trivially invariant under permutation of the arguments
in the sense that e.g.
\bigwedge(A,B,C)\simeq\bigwedge(B,C,A)
. This allows us to define
\alpha_{A,B,C}
by the following composition of equivalences.

\displaystyle(A\wedge B)\wedge C\simeq\bigwedge(A,B,C)\simeq\bigwedge(B,C,A)\simeq(B\wedge C)\wedge A\simeq A\wedge(B\wedge C)

The fact that
\alpha_{A,B,C}
is pointed holds by
\mathsf{refl}
.
∎

4
The heuristic

When working in HoTT, reasoning about functions defined over iterated smash products quickly
gets out of hand. For instance, in order to verify the pentagon axiom, we
need to reason about functions on the form
((A\wedge B)\wedge C)\wedge D\to E
. To prove an equality of two such functions
f
and
g
,
we have to construct, for instance, a dependent path

\displaystyle\mathsf{ap}_{\mathsf{ap}_{\mathsf{ap}_{f}\circ{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}}}\circ{{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}}}}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(a)})}\lx@xy@svg{\hbox{\raise 2.5pt\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\hbox{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\hbox{\vtop{\halign{\entry@#!@&&\entry@@#!@\cr&\crcr}}}\ignorespaces{\hbox{\kern-3.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\raise-2.5pt\hbox{$\textstyle{{}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}$}}}}}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces{}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces{\hbox{\lx@xy@drawsquiggles@}}\ignorespaces{\hbox{\kern 27.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\lx@xy@tip{1}\lx@xy@tip{-1}}}}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces{\hbox{\lx@xy@drawsquiggles@}}\ignorespaces{\hbox{\lx@xy@drawsquiggles@}}{\hbox{\kern 27.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\raise-2.5pt\hbox{$\textstyle{}$}}}}}}}\ignorespaces}}}}\ignorespaces\mathsf{ap}_{\mathsf{ap}_{\mathsf{ap}_{g}\circ{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}}}\circ{{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}}}}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(a)})}

which boils down to filling a
4
-dimensional cube with highly
non-trivial sides. This is often completely unmanageable in practice.
The best thing we can hope for is that these types of coherence
problems are, in some sense, automatic. This was, in fact, suggested
by
Brunerie 2016
, but was never proved nor in any way made formal. In
this section, we will see that this, in fact, is the case.

The first troublesome part of verifying equalities of functions
defined over smash products is the
{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{lr}}}}
constructor. Fortunately,
we do not have to deal with it. Let us denote by
{A}\,\widetilde{\wedge}\,{B}
the exact same HIT as
A\wedge B
but with the
{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{lr}}}}
constructor removed. In other words, it is the following pushout.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A+B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\times B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{A}\,\widetilde{\wedge}\,{B}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

Let
i
be the obvious map
{A}\,\widetilde{\wedge}\,{B}\to A\wedge B
.

Lemma 10
.

For any two maps
f,g:A\wedge B\to C
satisfying
f\circ i=g\circ i
, we have that
f=g
.

Proof.

The antecedent of the statement provides us with

•

a path
p:f(\star_{\wedge})=g(\star_{\wedge})
,

•

a homotopy
h:((a,b):A\times B)\to f\langle a,b\rangle=g\langle a,b\rangle
,

•

for each
a:A
, a filler
h_{l}(a)
of the square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g\langle a,\star_{B}\rangle}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g(\star_{\wedge})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\langle a,\star_{B}\rangle}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f(\star_{\wedge})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h(a,\star_{B})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{ap}}_{g}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(a)})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{ap}}_{f}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(a)})}}

•

for each
b:B
, a filler
h_{r}(b)
of the square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g\langle\star_{A},b\rangle}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g(\star_{\wedge})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\langle\star_{A},b\rangle}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f(\star_{\wedge})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h(\star_{A},b)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{ap}}_{g}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(b)})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{ap}}_{f}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(b)})}}

To prove that
f=g
, we need to provide
p^{\prime},h^{\prime},h_{l}^{\prime},h_{r}^{\prime}
of the
same types as above, as well as a filler
h^{\prime}_{lr}
of the cube

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g\langle\star_{A},\star_{B}\rangle}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g(\star_{\wedge})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g\langle\star_{A},\star_{B}\rangle}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g(\star_{\wedge})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\langle\star_{A},\star_{B}\rangle}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f(\star_{\wedge})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\langle\star_{A},\star_{B}\rangle}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f(\star_{\wedge})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{ap}_{f}({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(\star_{A})})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{ap}_{f}({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(\star_{A})})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{ap}_{g}({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(\star_{A})})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{ap}_{g}({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(\star_{A})})}

where the top and bottom squares are given respectively by

\mathsf{ap}_{\mathsf{ap}_{g}}({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{lr}}}})
and

\mathsf{ap}_{\mathsf{ap}_{f}}({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{lr}}}})
, the left- and right-hand side

\mathsf{refl}_{h^{\prime}(\star_{A},\star_{B})}

,

. For

Lemma

Definition 11
.

A pointed type
A
is homogeneous if for any
a:A
, there is a pointed equivalence

(A,\star_{A})\simeq_{\star}(A,a)
.

The usefulness of homogeneous types is showcased by the following
lemma which was first conjectured for Eilenberg-MacLane spaces in work
leading up to
Brunerie et al. 2022
and later proved and generalised by
Cavallo (and later further generalised by Buchholtz, Christensen,
G. Taxerås Flaten and Rijke (
Buchholtz et al. 2023
)).

Lemma 12

(Cavallo)
.

Let
f,g:A\to_{\star}B
and let
B
be homogeneous. If
f=g
as plain
functions, then
f=g
as pointed functions.

The same lemma holds for bi-pointed functions
f,g:A\to_{\star}(B\to_{\star}C)
, since the type
(B\to_{\star}C)
is homogeneous if
C

is. This gives a corresponding principle for maps defined over smash
products via the adjunction

(A\wedge B\to_{\star}C)\simeq(A\to_{\star}(B\to_{\star}C))
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
f,g:A\wedge B\to_{\star}C
and let
C
be homogeneous. If

f\langle a,b\rangle=g\langle a,b\rangle
for all
a:A
and

b:B
, we obtain an equality of pointed functions
f=g
.

The following form is useful when dealing with non-pointed functions.

Lemma 14
.

Let
C
be an arbitrary type and suppose that we have two functions
f,g:A\wedge B\to C
with
(C,f(\star_{\wedge}))
homogeneous. If

f\langle a,b\rangle=g\langle a,b\rangle
for all
a:A
and

b:B
, then
f=g
.

Proof.

Let
h(a,b):f\langle a,b\rangle=g\langle a,b\rangle
.
We know that
g(\star_{\wedge})=f(\star_{\wedge})
by the composite path

g(\star_{\wedge})\xrightarrow{{\mathsf{ap}}_{g}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(\star_{A})})}^{-1}}g\langle\star_{A},\star_{B}\rangle\xrightarrow{h(\star_{A},\star_{B})^{-1}}{f\langle\star_{A},\star_{B}\rangle}\xrightarrow{{\mathsf{ap}}_{f}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(\star_{A})})}}f(\star_{\wedge})

Hence, we may view both
f
and
g
as pointed functions
A\wedge B\to_{\star}(C,f(\star_{\wedge}))
. Now, Lemma
13
applies and, in particular,
f=g
as plain functions.
∎

Let

A formal statement of the heuristic

In a classical setting, the iterated smash product
\bigwedge_{i\leq n}{A_{i}}
can be described as the quotient space
(A_{0}\times\dots\times A_{n})/{\mathsf{FW}_{i\leq n}(A_{i})}
where

{\mathsf{FW}_{i\leq n}(A_{i})}:=\{(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})\mid a_{i}=\star_{A_{i}}\text{ for some $i$}\}\subset A_{0}\times\dots\times A_{n}

It is unclear how to mimic this construction in HoTT in a useful way, as the naive definition forces us to add, in a systematic way, an exponential number of higher coherences. However, the naive definition may still prove useful. Here, by ‘naive definition’, we mean the disjoint union

\displaystyle{{\mathsf{FS}}_{i\leq n}(A_{i})}:=\bigsqcup_{i\leq n}\left(A^{(i)}_{0}\times\dots\times A^{(i)}_{n}\right)\hskip 28.45274pt

\displaystyle\mathsf{where}\hskip 5.69046ptA^{(i)}_{j}=\begin{cases}A_{j}&\text{ if $i\neq j$}\\
1&\text{ if $i=j$}\end{cases}

While the cofibre of the canonical map
{{\mathsf{FS}}_{i\leq n}(A_{i})}\to A_{0}\times\dots\times A_{n}
does not quite give us
\bigwedge_{i\leq n}{A_{i}}
, it
does
, however, provide a useful approximation.
This is, in fact, precisely the content of our heuristic. We will soon make this precise. Before this, let us give a less set-theoretic definition of
{{\mathsf{FS}}_{i\leq n}(A_{i})}
which is easier to work with in HoTT.

Definition 25
.

Given pointed types
A_{0},\dots,A_{n}
we define
{{\mathsf{FS}}_{i\leq n}(A_{i})}
by induction on
n
as follows.

{{\mathsf{FS}}_{i\leq n}(A_{i})}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $n=0$}\\
({{\mathsf{FS}}_{i\leq{n-1}}(A_{i})}\times A_{n})+(A_{0}\times\dots\times{A_{n-1}})&\text{ if $n>0$}\end{cases}

Let us, for clarity, explicitly describe the canonical map
\gamma_{n}:{{\mathsf{FS}}_{i\leq n}(A_{i})}\to A_{0}\times\dots\times A_{n}
. It is recursively defined with
\gamma_{0}:1\to A_{0}
picking out the basepoint
\star_{A_{0}}
and

\gamma_{n}:({{\mathsf{FS}}_{i\leq{n-1}}(A_{i})}\times A_{n})+(A_{0}\times\dots\times{A_{n-1}})\to A_{0}\times\dots\times A_{n}

being defined by
\gamma_{n}=\gamma_{n}^{(1)}+\gamma_{n}^{(2)}

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}^{(1)}(x,a_{n})

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{n-1}(x),a_{n})

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}^{(2)}(a_{0},\dots,a_{n-1})

\displaystyle=(a_{0},\dots,a_{n-1},\star{A_{n}})

.

Given pointed types
A_{0},\dots,A_{n}
, we define
\widetilde{\bigwedge}_{i\leq n}{A_{i}}
to be the cofibre of
\gamma_{n}:{{\mathsf{FS}}_{i\leq n}(A_{i})}\to A_{0}\times\dots\times A_{n}
, i.e. the following pushout.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{{\mathsf{FS}}_{i\leq n}(A_{i})}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A_{0}\times\dots\times A_{n}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\widetilde{\bigwedge}_{i\leq n}{A_{i}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma_{n}}

for its basepoint,

•

•

•

{{\mathsf{push}}}^{(2)}(x):\langle\gamma_{n}^{(2)}(x)\rangle=\star_{\widetilde{\wedge}}

It is easy to see that the composition
{{\mathsf{FS}}_{i\leq n}(A_{i})}\to A_{0}\times\dots\times A_{n}\to\bigwedge_{i\leq n}{A_{i}}

\iota\langle a_{0},\dots,a_{n}\rangle=\langle a_{0},\dots,a_{n}\rangle
.

We also remark that there is an inclusion

\displaystyle\uparrow(\star_{\widetilde{\wedge}},a_{n})

\displaystyle:=\star_{\widetilde{\wedge}}

\displaystyle\uparrow(\langle a_{0},\dots,a_{n-1}\rangle,a_{n})

\displaystyle:=\langle a_{0},\dots,a_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle{\mathsf{ap}}_{\uparrow(-,a_{n})}{({{{\mathsf{push}}(x)}})}

and

\uparrow^{\mathsf{coh}}(x,a_{n})\,:\iota\left(\uparrow(x,a_{n})\right)=\langle\iota(x),a_{n}\rangle

One constructs

very directly by induction on

x:=\star_{\widetilde{\wedge}}
, the type of
\uparrow^{\mathsf{coh}}(x,a_{n})
reduces to
\star_{\wedge}=\langle\star_{\wedge},a_{n}\rangle
which holds by
{{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(a_{n})}^{-1}
. Hence, we define

\displaystyle\uparrow^{\mathsf{coh}}(x,a_{n}):=\mathsf{refl}

(3)

\displaystyle\uparrow^{\mathsf{coh}}(\star_{\widetilde{\wedge}},a_{n}):={{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(a_{n})}^{-1}

(4)

The higher coherence is very straightforward and we omit it for the sake of readability. We are now ready for the key result of the section. It makes precise to what extent
\widetilde{\bigwedge}_{i\leq n}{A_{i}}
approximates
{\bigwedge}_{i\leq n}{A_{i}}
and may be seen as a formal version of our heuristic. We will later restate the result in a less technical and more self-contained form.

Theorem 27
.

Let
f,g:\bigwedge_{i\leq n}A_{i}\to B
. Given a homotopy
\widetilde{p}_{n}:(x:\widetilde{\bigwedge}_{i\leq n}A_{i})\to f(\iota(x))=g(\iota(x))
, there is a homotopy
p_{n}:(x:\bigwedge_{i\leq n}A_{i})\to f(x)=g(x)
. Furthermore,
p_{n}
satisfies:

\displaystyle p_{n}(\star_{\wedge})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{p}_{n}(\star_{\widetilde{\wedge}})

(5)

\displaystyle p_{n}\langle a_{0},\dots,a_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\widetilde{p}_{n}\langle a_{0},\dots,a_{n}\rangle

(6)

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
. When
n=0
, the map
\iota
is simply the canonical equivalence between the cofibre of the unique pointed map
1\to_{\star}A_{0}
and
{A_{0}}
itself, and the theorem is trivial.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
f
,
g
and
\widetilde{p}_{n}
be as in the theorem statement. Let us, for clarity, rewrite the domain of
f
and
g
as the binary smash product
\left(\bigwedge_{i\leq{n-1}}A_{i}\right)\wedge A_{n}
. In order to construct
p_{n}:(x:\left(\bigwedge_{i\leq{n-1}}A_{i}\right)\wedge A_{n})\to f(x)=g(x)
, it suffices, by Lemma
10
, to define

(a)

a path
p_{n}(\star_{\wedge}):f(\star_{\wedge})=g(\star_{\wedge})
,

(b)

for
a_{n}:A_{n}
, homotopies
p_{n}\langle-,a_{n}\rangle:(x:\bigwedge_{i\leq{n-1}}A_{i})\to f\langle x,y\rangle=g\langle x,y\rangle
,

(c)

For each
a_{n}:A_{n}
, a filler of the square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f(\star_{\wedge})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g(\star_{\wedge})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\langle\star_{\wedge},a_{n}\rangle}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g\langle\star_{\wedge},a_{n}\rangle}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{n}\langle\star_{\wedge},a_{n}\rangle}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{ap}}_{f}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(a_{n})})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{ap}}_{g}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(a_{n})})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{n}(\star_{\wedge})}

(d)

For each
x:\bigwedge_{i\leq n-1}{A_{i}}
, a filler of the square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f(\star_{\wedge})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g(\star_{\wedge})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\langle x,\star_{A_{n}}\rangle}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g\langle x,\star_{A_{n}}\rangle}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{n}\langle x,\star_{A_{n}}\rangle}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{ap}}_{f}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(x)})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{ap}}_{g}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{l}}}(x)})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{n}(\star_{\wedge})}

For
(a)
, we know that
\iota(\star_{\widetilde{\wedge}}):=\star_{\wedge}
and thus
\widetilde{p}_{n}(\star_{\widetilde{\wedge}}):f(\star_{\wedge})=g(\star_{\wedge})
. Consequently, we may simply set
p_{n}(\star_{\wedge}):=\widetilde{p}_{n}(\star_{\widetilde{\wedge}})
. Doing so, (
5
) holds definitionally. For
(b)
, we fix
a_{n}:A_{n}
. By the induction hypothesis, it suffices to construct
p_{n}\langle\iota(x),a_{n}\rangle:f\langle\iota(x),y\rangle=g\langle\iota(x),y\rangle
for

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\langle\iota(x),a_{n}\rangle}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f(\iota(\uparrow(x,a_{n})))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g(\iota(\uparrow(x,a_{n})))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g\langle\iota(x),a_{n}\rangle}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{ap}}_{f}{(\uparrow^{\mathsf{coh}}(x,a_{n}))}^{-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{p}_{n}(\uparrow(x,a_{n}))}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{ap}}_{g}{(\uparrow^{\mathsf{coh}}(x,a_{n}))}}

This completes the construction of
p_{n}\langle-,a_{n}\rangle
. Note that, since
p_{n}\langle-,a_{n}\rangle
was constructed using the induction hypothesis, we also get the following from (

\displaystyle p_{n}\langle\star_{\wedge},a_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle={\mathsf{ap}}_{f}{(\uparrow^{\mathsf{coh}}(\star_{\wedge},a_{n}))}^{-1}\cdot{\widetilde{p}_{n}(\uparrow(\star_{\wedge},a_{n}))}\cdot{\mathsf{ap}}_{g}{(\uparrow^{\mathsf{coh}}(\star_{\wedge},a_{n}))}

. So, to summarise, we have the following two identities for

\displaystyle p_{n}\langle\star_{\wedge},a_{n}\rangle={\mathsf{ap}}_{f}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(a_{n})})}\cdot p_{n}(\star_{\wedge})\cdot{\mathsf{ap}}_{g}{({{\mathsf{push}}_{{\mathsf{r}}}(a_{n})})}^{-1}

(8)

Note that, in particular, the latter identity verifies (

Let us restate the result in a more compact and self-contained form.

Corollary 28
.

Let
f,g:\bigwedge_{i\leq n}A_{i}\to B
. If
f
and
g
agree on
\iota:\widetilde{\bigwedge}_{i\leq n}A_{i}\to\bigwedge_{i\leq n}A_{i}
, i.e. if
f\circ\iota=g\circ\iota
, then
f=g
.

For completeness, let us also state the analogous result for pointed maps.

Corollary 29
.

Let
f,g:\bigwedge_{i\leq n}A_{i}\to_{\star}B
. If
f\circ\iota=g\circ\iota
as pointed maps, then we obtain an equality of pointed maps
f=g
.

7
Conclusions and future work

Let us summarise the key contributions of this paper. First and
foremost, we have shown in Sections
3
through

5
that the smash product forms a 1-coherent
symmetric monoidal product on the wild category of pointed types (Theorem
21
). This
fills a long-standing and rather troublesome gap in the HoTT
literature. In particular, it implies the correctness of previous
work which relies on this result—perhaps most notably, that that
of
[
Brunerie 2016
]
.

Secondly, we have presented a new method for reasoning about smash
products in HoTT. This was first done by introducing, in
Section
4
, an informal heuristic which was used
heavily leading up to Theorem
21
. We later provided an
attempt at a formal version of the heuristic
in Corollaries
28
and
29
. The message of
these results is simple: functions defined over smash products in HoTT
are not much harder to deal with than those defined over ordinary
Cartesian products. This contradicts a commonly held view that smash
products in HoTT become unworkable in higher dimensions.

In addition to salvaging already existing work relying on unproved
results about smash products, we also hope that the results presented
here will be useful in the further development of synthetic homotopy
theory in HoTT. In particular, Theorem
21
is not the only
consequence
of Propositions
22
and
23
. These
propositions capture two important properties of the equivalence

\wedge_{n,m}:S^{n}\wedge S^{m}\simeq S^{n+m}
. For instance,
Ljungström and Mörtberg 2023
implicitly used the
special case when
n=m=1
in order to provide a simplified version of Brunerie’s
proof of
\pi_{4}(S^{3})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. More generally,
\wedge_{n,m}:S^{n}\wedge S^{m}\simeq S^{n+m}
gives rise to the
Whitehead
product

[
Whitehead 1941
]
. This operation was originally
defined in HoTT by
Brunerie 2016
using joins of spheres, but we can also
give it a rather compact construction in terms of
\wedge_{n,m}
: the Whitehead product
[f,g]
of two
maps
f:S^{n+1}\to_{\star}A
and
g:S^{m+1}\to_{\star}A
can be
viewed as the map defined by the composition

S^{n+m}\xrightarrow{\wedge_{n,m}^{-1}}(S^{n}\wedge S^{m})\xrightarrow{\mathsf{comm}}\Omega(S^{n+1}\vee S^{m+1})\xrightarrow{\Omega(f\vee g)}\Omega A

where
\mathsf{comm}
sends
\langle x,y\rangle
to

\sigma_{l}^{-1}(x)\cdot\sigma_{r}(y)\cdot\sigma_{l}(x)\cdot\sigma_{r}^{-1}(y)
, where
\sigma:S^{k}\to\Omega S^{k+1}
is
defined by
\sigma(x)=\mathsf{merid}\;{(x)}\cdot\mathsf{merid}\;{(\mathsf{north})}^{-1}
and the
subscripts
l
and
r
indicate in which component of
S^{n+1}\vee S^{m+1}
the loop takes place. This induces a map on homotopy
groups

\pi_{n+1}(A)\times\pi_{m+1}(A)\to\pi_{n+m}(\Omega(A))\xrightarrow{\sim}\pi_{n+m+1}(A)

which, classically, turns
\pi_{\bullet}(A)
into a graded quasi-Lie
algebra
[
Uehara and Massey 1957
]
. This fact is still open in HoTT but we
conjecture that
Propositions
22
and
23
will play a
crucial role in a prospective proof. More concretely, as

\wedge_{n,m}
is used in the construction of this map,
Proposition
22
and
23
should, respectively, play
important roles in prospective proofs of graded
symmetry and the Jacobi identity.

On a related note, the heuristic presented
in Section
4
and the related results in
Section
6
are reminiscent of results from the study
of polyhedral products (see
e.g.
Bahri et al. 2019
;
Theriault 2018
). This is a
field of mathematics which, very coarsely speaking, explores and
generalises the mediation between iterated wedge sums and iterated
Cartesian products. It is unclear whether this topic can be studied in
a meaningful way through the lens of HoTT, but exploring this would
certainly amount to an interesting continuation of the work we have
presented here.
