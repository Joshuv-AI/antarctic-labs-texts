A categorical view on the converse Lyapunov theorem
Sébastien Mattenet, Raphaël Jungers

Abstract
. In 1892, Lyapunov provided a fundamental contribution to stability theory by introducing so-called Lyapunov functions and Lyapunov equilibria . He subsequently showed that, for linear systems, the two concepts are equivalent. These concepts have since been extended to diverse types of dynamical systems, and in all settings the equivalence remains valid. However, this involves an often technical proof in each new setting where the concepts are introduced. In this article, we investigate a categorical framework where these results can be unified, exposing a single underlying reason for the equivalence to hold in all cases. First we define what is a dynamical system. Then we introduce the notion of a level-set morphism, which in turn allows us to define the concepts of a Lyapunov equilibrium and a Lyapunov function in a categorical setting. We conclude by a proof of their equivalence.

1.
Introduction

Lyapunov theory is a cornerstone of the study of dynamical systems. Lyapunov’s realisation in his Ph.D. thesis
[
5
]
was that one could infer stability of a physical system without knowing the true energy function. Instead, it is enough to find a so-called Lyapunov function. Surprisingly, finding a Lyapunov function is also a necessary condition for having a (Lyapunov) equilibrium.
The notion of Lyapunov equilibrium (and Lyapunov stability) has since then been expanded to many different systems, with always as the key result that having a Lyapunov function (or lack thereof) is equivalent to proving an equilibrium (or lack thereof).
See
[
3
]
*Theorem 2.5 or
[
4
]
*Theorem 4.17 for classical Lyapunov theorems.
While one quickly develops an intuition for the translation between Lyapunov equilibria and functions, the proofs themselves are quite technical and seem to require a machinery too complex and too specific, hindering the view of a general proof holding for all types of dynamical systems at once. (To illustrate this point: the proof of
[
4
]
*Theorem 4.17 is six pages long.)
The goal of this paper is 1) to write in categorical language the definitions of equilibrium and Lyapunov function, 2) to make precise the equivalence between the two notions and 3) to formalise how change, codified in one setting, translates to change, codified in the other.

Related work includes
[
2
]
, which provides another categorical definition of dynamical system, but does not study Lyapunov theory.

2.
Dynamical systems

Dynamical systems are systems that evolve over time. A dynamical system is modelled as an action of time on some set of states. Such a system is
deterministic
when repeating the same action from a given state will always yield the same resulting state. A dynamical system is
closed
if through evolution of time one remains inside of the state-space.
Let us formally define a dynamical system as an action of a monoid on a metric space. Let
M
be a space and
T
a monoid, then a
deterministic closed
dynamical system is an action of
T
on
M
, that is a functor
F\colon T\to End(M)
.

Examples 2.1
.

1.

Let
M=T=\mathbb{R}
and pick a
v_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
F_{t}(x)=x+v_{0}\cdot t
; this is a
uniform linear movement
.

2.

Let
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
T=(\mathbb{N},+)
. Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a matrix. Let the evolution functor be defined by
F_{1}\colon M\to M\colon x\in M\mapsto Ax
. (
F_{n}=\left.F_{1}\right.^{n}
is imposed by composition in the monoid). This is a
linear discrete time system
.

3.

Let
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
T=\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}^{*}
the set of words on an alphabet of
n
letters; the operation on
T
is the concatenation of words. Let
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\quad i=1,\dots,n
be matrices. Let the evolution functor be defined by

F_{i}\colon M\to M\colon x\mapsto A_{i}x

and composition. This is a
switching system
.

4.

Let
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
T=\left\{\perp,\top\right\}^{*}
with concatenation. Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a matrix and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vector. Let the evolution functor be defined by
F_{\perp}\colon M\to M\colon x\mapsto Ax
and
F_{\top}\colon M\to M\colon x\mapsto Ax+b
.
F
is defined on any other word by composition in the monoid. This is a
linear discrete time system with discrete control

b
.

5.

Let
M=S^{n}
be the n-sphere and
T=O(n+1)
the group of distance preserving transformation on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The action is given by applying the transformation to the embedded sphere.

One can extend the definition to also include
open
systems and
stochastic
systems, see for example
[
6
]
. Other definitions based on derivations are possible; see for example
[
1
]
. We chose our definition because we are interested in modelling Lyapunov’s second method for stability, extracting the categorically relevant concepts.

3.
Invariants

When considering the evolution of a system, one could consider invariants of the system. By definition an invariant is a quantity
I\colon M\to X
which remains the same after the passage of time, therefore it forms a cocone over the diagram of the action. The triangle

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{t}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces I}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces I}

commutes for every
t\in T
, and for
F\colon T\to End(M)
we write
F(t)=F_{t}\colon M\to M
.
Knowledge about its invariants allows us to determine properties of the system, and more generally to decompose it into equivalence classes. That is, one can look at
X/\penalty_{\sim}
where
m\sim m^{\prime}\iff I(m)=I(m^{\prime})
and deduce properties of reachability if this space does (or does not) contract to a single point.

Now suppose one wants to define a monovariant, some quantity about the system which either only increases, or only decreases, such as entropy or energy in physical systems. For that we would need to select only one direction of time: i.e., once some time interval has passed, it is no longer possible to go backwards in time. We define

Definition 3.1
.

A timeline
(T,\leq,0,+)
is a set
T
, equipped with a partial order
\leq
, and an operation
+
that respects for all
t_{1}
,
t_{2}
,
t_{3}\in T
,

1.

t_{1}+0=t_{1}=0+t_{1}
,

2.

t_{1}+(t_{2}+t_{3})=(t_{1}+t_{2})+t_{3}
,

3.

t_{1}\leq t_{1}+t_{2}
,

4.

if
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
, then there exists a unique
t^{\prime}\in T
such that
t_{2}=t_{1}+t^{\prime}
.

This definition allows for some flexibility in the timeline (discrete time or not, branching time, etc.) while still permitting an unambiguous direction of time.

Remarks 3.2
.

1.

Note that in particular, a timeline is a monoid
(T,+,0)
.

2.

From 1. and 3. it follows that
0\leq 0+t=t
for any time
t
, therefore
0
is the global minimum for the ordering. This means our timeline has a starting point and does not go indefinitely into the past.

Examples 3.3
.

—

T=\mathbb{N}
with the usual order and sum. This is the
discrete linear timeline
.

—

T=\mathbb{R}^{+}
with the usual order and sum. This is the
continuous linear timeline
.

—

Let
A
be a set, then
A^{*}
, the set of words over the alphabet
A
, also called the free monoid over
A
, can be equipped with the operation
\circ
of concatenation, and the canonical ordering
w_{1}\leq w_{2}\iff\exists w_{3}\colon w_{2}=w_{1}\circ w_{3}
. This is a
discrete branching timeline
, used for example in arbitrary switching systems. Note that composition is not commutative, for example
T=\left\{a,b\right\}^{*}
,
ab+bab=abbab\neq bab+ab=babab
.

—

Let
T=\left\{f\colon[0,a]\to\mathbb{R}\mid a>0\right\}
. We can define the ordering
f_{1}\leq f_{2}
if
f_{1}=f_{2}
on the domain of
f_{1}
, that is, if
f_{2}
is an extension of
f_{1}
. Additionally one can define the concatenation operation

f_{1}\circ f_{2}=\begin{cases}f_{1}(x)&x\in[0,t_{1}]\\
f_{2}(x-t_{1})&x\in[t_{1},t_{1}+t_{2}]\end{cases}

This is an example of a continuous branching timeline.

—

Let T be a total order, bounded from below. We define the composition as the maximum, that is
x+y=\max(x,y)
. This is a very limited idea of timeline, so we won’t use it in examples.

—

Any ordered monoid
B
(called a
minimal positively ordered monoid
in
[
7
]
) is a valid timeline.

In a dynamical system
e\colon T\to End(M)
, if
T
also is a timeline
(T,\leq,0,+)
, then we will call the system a
forward dynamical system
, to indicate it cannot go backward in time, because 0 is a global minimum.

We may now define a monovariant as a special kind of lax cocone.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
T
be a timeline,
F\colon T\to M
a forward dynamical system,
X
a poset and
I\colon M\to X
a function. We say
I
is a
monovariant
if it is a lax cocone over
F
. In other words, the following diagram lax-commutes for all
t\in T
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{t}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces I}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces I}

This means that
I(m)\leq I(F_{t}(m))
for all
m\in M
.

A particular example of interest will be the case when
I
is the distance function
d(x,\cdot)
with respect to a point
x
. When that happens, the interpretation is that the centre (the point where
I(x)=0
) is attractive.

Examples 3.5
.

—

Let
M=\mathbb{R}
evolve under discrete linear time with the following dynamic:
F(x)=\frac{x}{2}
. One can look at the distance to the origin and see that it is decreasing at each time step.

—

Same as before, but let
F(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{x}{2}&|x|\geq 2\\
x&|x|<2\end{cases}.

—

Let
M=\mathbb{R}
evolve under discrete linear time with the dynamic
F(x)=x-1
and consider the monovariant
I(x)=x
. One can see that it decreases at each time step.

Monovariants are useful to quickly rule out that a given region of
M
is reachable from a given starting point. As a demonstration, in all of the above examples we know that from a starting point
x=3
or
x=-3
we will never reach
10
thanks to monovariance.

We now define the following:

Definition 3.6
.

Given a point
1\to M
(or a subset
A\hookrightarrow M
), we call its image through
F
the
future
of the point (or subset).

Definition 3.7
.

We say a point
X
(or set) is an
equilibrium
if it is its own future, that is
F(X)=X
.

Definition 3.8
.

We define an
attractor
as a point for whom the distance is a monovariant.

Theorem 3.9
.

An attractor is an equilibrium.

Proof.

Let’s call this point
x^{*}
. We measure the distance of its future to itself. By definition of attractor,
d(x^{*},F_{t}(x^{*}))\leq d(x^{*},x^{*})=0
which implies
d(x^{*},F_{t}(x^{*}))=0
which in turn implies
x^{*}=F_{t}(x^{*})
.
∎

Definition 3.10
.

Let
M
,
X
be as before. Given two morphisms (or invariant candidates)
I
,
J\colon M\to X
, we say
I
is a rough approximation of
J
if there are transformations
A
,
B\colon X\to X
such that

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces I}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces I}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}

lax-commutes.

In our case a rough approximation of distance is an invariant that gives us a maximal and minimal distance based on the value of the invariant.

4.
The level-set paradigm

An invariant
I\colon M\to X
creates equivalence classes of points (or regions of
M
) that have the same value. It is therefore natural to try to understand invariants trough the classes they create.
When it comes to monovariants, the more relevant notion is the concept of a level-set:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
F\colon A\to B
be a morphism and let
B
be equipped with an ordering structure. We note
F^{\leq}\colon B\to\mathcal{P}(A)
the morphism that to each element of
b\in B
assigns the subset
\left\{x\in A\mid f(x)\leq b\right\}\in\mathcal{P}(A)
.

Another way to write this would be that the level-set is the composition of the preimage with the downward closure operator. Since we have already chosen the convention that
\mathcal{P}(f)
is the covariant functor, we keep the definition above to avoid confusion.
1
1

1

Some authors follow the convention that
\mathcal{P}(f)
refers to the preimage functor, so that
\mathcal{P}(f)
is a contravariant functor.

Example 4.2
.

Consider the discrete time, linear system
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
T=\mathbb{N}
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
F_{1}(x)=Ax
. Suppose we are looking at a monovariant of the form

V\colon{\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\colon x\mapsto x^{T}Bx}

with
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
invertible (a quadratic Lyapunov function, as we will call them later). Let us concentrate on the case
B=\text{Id}
.
The set of all points with the same value under
V
is the surface of the sphere (an ellipse for
B\neq\text{Id}
). If
V
is indeed a monovariant, we can expect points of the sphere not to stay strictly in the surface, but instead on the interior volume, (the ball), as time passes. That is the relevant notion would be
\left\{x\mid V(x)=r\right\}=V^{-1}(r)
for an invariant but
\left\{x\mid V(x)\leq r\right\}=V^{\leq}(r)
for a monovariant.

Theorem 4.8
.

A Lyapunov equilibrium is an equilibrium.

Proof.

Let
1\xrightarrow{x}M
be a Lyapunov equilibrium. We can view it as the subset
\left\{x\right\}\in\mathcal{P}(M)
. The future will be
\left\{F_{t}(x)\mid t\in T\right\}=\bigcup\mathcal{P}(F_{t})(\left\{x\right\})
.

Separately
d(x,x)=0\leq r
implies
x\in B(x,r)
, equivalently
\left\{x\right\}\subset B(x,r)
, for any radius
r\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
In fact we can characterise
x
as the only point at distance 0 from itself, looking at level-sets we get
\left\{x\right\}=\bigcap_{r>0}B(x,r)
.
We conclude with

\displaystyle\left\{F_{t}(x)\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}(F_{t})(\left\{x\right\})=\mathcal{P}(F_{t})\Bigl(\bigcap_{r>0}B(x,r)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}(F_{t})\Bigl(\bigcap_{\delta(r)>0}B(x,r)\Bigr)\subset\bigcap_{r>0}B(x,r)=\left\{x\right\}.

This is true regardless of the choice of
t\in T
, therefore the future is

\left\{F_{t}(x)\mid t\in T\right\}=\bigcup\mathcal{P}(F_{t})(\left\{x\right\})=\bigcup\left\{x\right\}=\left\{x\right\}.\qed

7.
Conclusion

We have given a definition of level-set morphism in a set-theoretical context, which is easily seen to be valid in an arbitrary topos: indeed, we never used the excluded middle in our proofs. We explained how a morphism relates to its level-set in a way similar to an adjunction. We have then given a categorical definition of stability and Lyapunov functions. We then showed that one is in fact just a reformulation of the other, and we explained how a change in the equilibrium translates to a corresponding change in the associated Lyapunov function. This transfer is the easy half of a two-way process. We hope in further work to describe how changes in a Lyapunov function, such as requiring
V
to be continuous, affect the associated equilibrium, or when to expect such behaviour for Lyapunov functions.
