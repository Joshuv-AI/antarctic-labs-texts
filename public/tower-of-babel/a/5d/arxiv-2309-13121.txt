Dynamical defects in a two-dimensional Wigner crystal: self-doping and kinetic magnetism
Kyung-Su Kim, Ilya Esterlis, Chaitanya Murthy, Steven A. Kivelson

Abstract
We study the quantum dynamics of interstitials and vacancies in a two-dimensional Wigner crystal (WC) using a semi-classical instanton method that is asymptotically exact at low density, i.e., in the r s → ∞ r_{s}\to\infty limit. The dynamics of these point defects mediates magnetism with much higher energy scales than the exchange energies of the pure WC. Via exact diagonalization of the derived effective Hamiltonians in the single-defect sectors, we find the dynamical corrections to the defect energies. The resulting expression for the interstitial (vacancy) energy extrapolates to 0 at r s = r mit ≈ 70 r_{s}=r_{\rm mit}\approx 70 ( r s ≈ 30 r_{s}\approx 30 ), suggestive of a self-doping instability to a partially melted WC for some range of r s r_{s} below r mit r_{\rm mit} . We thus propose a “metallic electron crystal” phase of the two-dimensional electron gas at intermediate densities between a low density insulating WC and a high density Fermi fluid.

Introduction

Despite its prime importance in the field of condensed matter physics, some basic aspects remain unsettled concerning the physics of the two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) at intermediate densities where various forms of “strongly correlated electron fluids” can arise. The ideal 2DEG is governed by the simple Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\sum_{i}\frac{\textbf{p}_{i}^{\,2}}{2m}+\sum_{i<j}\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon}\frac{1}{|\textbf{r}_{i}-\textbf{r}_{j}|},

(1)

with a single dimensionless parameter,
r_{s}=a_{0}/a_{\rm B}

Here,

is the average interparticle distance,
n
is the electron density, and

is the effective Bohr radius.
The phases of the 2DEG in the weak and strong coupling limits are well-understood: it forms a paramagnetic Fermi liquid (FL) for small
r_{s}
(weak coupling) and a Wigner crystal (WC) for large

(strong coupling)
Wigner 1934

The present study addresses the intermediate coupling regime near the quantum metal-insulator transition (MIT).
Landmark numerical studies suggested that the MIT occurs as a direct transition from a Fermi liquid to an insulating WC at

;

;

II.1
Wigner crystal ring-exchange processes

In the
r_{s}\to\infty
limit, the classical ground state manifold consists of a triangular lattice WC with
2^{N}
-fold degeneracy in spin states.
The lifting of this degeneracy and the nature of the resulting magnetic order is determined for
1\ll r_{s}<\infty
by WC ring-exchange processes.
Various ring-exchange processes correspond to distinct instanton solutions of the action and
can be calculated via the dilute instanton approximation
Thouless 1965
;
Roger et al. 1983
;
Roger 1984
;
Chakravarty et al. 1999
;
Katano and Hirashima 2000
;
Voelker and Chakravarty 2001
;
Kim et al. 2022

Katano and Hirashima 2000
;
Voelker and Chakravarty 2001
for more details.)
The result is an effective spin Hamiltonian expressed as a sum over all ring-exchange processes:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{wc}}=-\sum_{a}(-1)^{P_{a}}\,{J_{a}}\,\big(\mathcal{\hat{P}}_{a}+\mathcal{\hat{P}}^{-1}_{a}\big),

(7)

where the semi-classical calculation gives a leading-order large
r_{s}
asymptotic expression for
J_{a}

Here,
\mathcal{\hat{P}}_{a}
is the permutation operator corresponding to the permutation
P_{a}

The two-particle exchange operators, in turn, can be written in terms of spin operators as
\mathcal{\hat{P}}_{(i,j)}=2(\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{j}+\frac{1}{4})
.

To illustrate how this works, recall the familiar problem of the semi-classical calculation of the tunnel splitting in a symmetric double-well potential
Coleman 1988
;
Altland and Simons 2010
;
Zinn-Justin 2021

\displaystyle\left<\textbf{r}_{0}\right|e^{-\beta H}\left|\textbf{r}_{0}\right>\sim|\psi(\textbf{r}_{0})|^{2}e^{-\beta E_{0}}\cosh(\beta\Delta),

(8)

\displaystyle\left<-\textbf{r}_{0}\right|e^{-\beta H}\left|\textbf{r}_{0}\right>\sim|\psi(\textbf{r}_{0})|^{2}e^{-\beta E_{0}}\sinh(\beta\Delta),

(9)

where the minima of the two wells are at
\pm\textbf{r}_{0}

E_{0}

The right-hand side of each expression is obtained by inserting the resolution of the identity on the left-hand side.

By controlled doping of a WC in the presence of a smoothly varying weak external periodic potential, one can obtain the defect-doped commensurate WC phase as a stable ground state, as the following reasoning shows.
Consider a weak commensurate potential that has minima

VI
Effects of weak disorder

Before concluding, we remark on the effect of small quenched disorder on the phase diagram (Fig.
1
).
Firstly, even weak disorder is expected to destroy any long-range crystalline order; hence all the electronic crystalline states we have discussed are defined only in an approximate sense as short-range ordered states.
Also, the MeC phase is characterized by the reduced density of mobile electrons and their increased effective mass;
hence, even weak disorder is likely to result in strong localization and destroy the metallic character of the phase.
The resulting disorder-induced intermediate insulating phase is characterized by large magnetic energy scales, associated with the dynamical processes of defects.
This may be an explanation for the recently observed insulating phases with much higher magnetic energy than the exchange scales of the pure WC
Hossain et al. 2020
;
Kim and Kivelson 2021
;
Falson et al. 2022
.
Note that such a proposal predicts an exponential reduction of magnetic energy scales with increasing
r_{s}
for
r_{s}>r_{\rm mit}

13
13

13

This explanation for unexpectedly robust insulating magnetism is distinct from, although related to, a previous proposal
Kim et al. 2022
based on WC-FL puddle formation.

26
) is performed with a standard optimization package
Mogensen and Riseth 2018
.
We will henceforth denote by
\textbf{r}_{k}
(
k=0,1,..,M
) the optimized tunneling path for the
a
-instanton process.

The fluctuation determinant
A_{a}
captures the Gaussian fluctuations around the semi-classical path

\displaystyle A_{a}=\frac{F^{\prime}\big[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)\big]}{F\big[\textbf{r}_{0}\big]}=\left[\frac{{\rm det}^{\prime}\big(-\partial_{\tau}^{2}+V^{\prime\prime}\big[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)\big]\big)}{\det\big(-\partial_{\tau}^{2}+V^{\prime\prime}(\textbf{r}_{0})\big)}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(27)

\displaystyle F\big[\textbf{r}(\tau)\big]\equiv\int_{\delta\textbf{r}(0)=0}^{\delta\textbf{r}(\tilde{\beta})=0}D\delta\textbf{r}(\tau)\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tilde{\beta}}\left(\delta\dot{\textbf{r}}(\tau)^{2}+\delta\textbf{r}(\tau)^{\rm T}V^{\prime\prime}\big[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)\big]\delta\textbf{r}(\tau)\right)\right]=\left<\textbf{0}\right|\mathcal{T}\exp(-\int_{0}^{\tilde{\beta}}d\tau\,h[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)])\left|\textbf{0}\right>,

(28)

\displaystyle h[\textbf{r}(\tau)]\equiv-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\delta\textbf{r}(\tau)^{\rm T}V^{\prime\prime}\big[\textbf{r}(\tau)\big]\delta\textbf{r}(\tau),

(29)

where
\mathcal{T}\exp(\cdots)
denotes the imaginary-time-ordered exponential,
\delta\textbf{r}(\tau)\equiv\textbf{r}(\tau)-\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)
is the fluctuation coordinate, and the primed determinant in the first line is again computed with the zero mode omitted.

\tilde{\beta}\to\infty
is implicitly taken in the end in calculating
A_{a}.

As discussed below, the calculation of
A_{a}
can be done numerically by first computing
{F\big[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)\big]}/{F\big[\textbf{r}_{0}\big]}
that includes the zero mode contribution, and then multiplying by the square root of the smallest eigenvalue (which is exponentially small in
\tilde{\beta}
) of the operator
-\partial_{\tau}^{2}+V^{\prime\prime}\big[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)\big]
.

F\big[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)\big]
can be calculated by discretizing the path integral expression (
28
).
First, we further define the time slices intermediate to those defined above

\displaystyle 0<\tau_{1/2}<\tau_{1}<\tau_{1+1/2}<\cdots<\tau_{M-1/2}<\tau_{M}\equiv\tilde{\beta},

(30)

where each interval,
\Delta\tau_{k}\equiv\tau_{k+\frac{1}{2}}-\tau_{k-\frac{1}{2}}
(
k=1,\cdots,M-1
), is calculated by inverting the semi-classical equation of motion

\displaystyle\Delta\tau_{k}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\textbf{r}_{k-\frac{1}{2}}}^{\textbf{r}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{d\textbf{r}}{\sqrt{2\Delta V[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)]}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Delta V(\textbf{r}_{k})}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(|\textbf{r}_{k+1}-\textbf{r}_{k}|+|\textbf{r}_{k}-\textbf{r}_{k-1}|\right),

(31)

and analogously for the end intervals,

\Delta\tau_{0}\equiv\tau_{\frac{1}{2}}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Delta V(\textbf{r}_{0})}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}|\textbf{r}_{1}-\textbf{r}_{0}|
and
\Delta\tau_{M}\equiv\tau_{M}-\tau_{M-\frac{1}{2}}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Delta V(\textbf{r}_{M})}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}|\textbf{r}_{M}-\textbf{r}_{M-1}|
.
(Note that the end intervals formally diverge,
\Delta\tau_{0,M}\to\infty
, as
\tilde{\beta}\to\infty
.)
Then, the propagator at each interval can be approximated by that of the quantum harmonic oscillator (Mehler kernel) of
h[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)]\approx h_{k}\equiv h[\textbf{r}_{k}]

\displaystyle\left<\delta\textbf{r}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}\right|e^{-\Delta\tau_{k}h_{k}}\left|\delta\textbf{r}_{k-\frac{1}{2}}\right>=\prod_{n=1}^{2N}\left(\sqrt{B^{(a)}_{n,k}}\exp[-S^{(a)}_{n,k}]\right),

(32)

\displaystyle S^{(a)}_{n,k}=\frac{A^{(a)}_{n,k}}{2}\left[\left<\textbf{v}_{n,k}\middle|\delta\textbf{r}_{k-\frac{1}{2}}\right>^{2}+\left<\textbf{v}_{n,k}\middle|\delta\textbf{r}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}\right>^{2}\right]-B^{(a)}_{n,k}\left<\textbf{v}_{n,k}\middle|\delta\textbf{r}_{k-\frac{1}{2}}\right>\left<\textbf{v}_{n,k}\middle|\delta\textbf{r}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}\right>,

(33)

where

\displaystyle V^{\prime\prime}(\textbf{r}_{k})\textbf{v}_{n,k}\equiv(\omega_{n,k})^{2}\textbf{v}_{n,k},\ \ \ (n=1,\cdots,2N),

(34)

\displaystyle A^{(a)}_{n,k}\equiv\frac{\omega_{n,k}}{\tanh(\omega_{n,k}\Delta\tau_{k})},\ B^{(a)}_{n,k}\equiv\frac{\omega_{n,k}}{\sinh(\omega_{n,k}\Delta\tau_{k})}.

(35)

Eq.
34
defines normal mode frequencies and eigenmodes at each time slice
k
.
Note that at intermediate times
k\neq 0,M
,
\omega_{n,k}
is in general complex.
At the end intervals
k=0,M
, one substitutes
\delta\textbf{r}_{-\frac{1}{2}}\to\delta r_{0}=0
and
\delta\textbf{r}_{M+\frac{1}{2}}\to\delta r_{M}=0
in the above expressions.
(Note that as
\tilde{\beta}\to\infty
, the propagators at the end intervals approach zero exponentially.
However, as we will see below, such contributions cancel when calculating
A_{a}
as we are calculating the ratio between two
F
s.)

F\big[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)\big]
can finally be computed by integrating over the intermediate fluctuation coordinates
\delta\textbf{r}_{k-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle F\big[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)\big]

\displaystyle=\int\left(\prod_{k=1}^{M}d^{2N}\delta\textbf{r}_{k-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left<\textbf{0}\middle|e^{-\Delta\tau_{M}h_{M}}\middle|\delta\textbf{r}_{M-\frac{1}{2}}\right>\left<\delta\textbf{r}_{M-\frac{1}{2}}\middle|e^{-\Delta\tau_{M-1}h_{M-1}}\middle|\delta\textbf{r}_{M-\frac{3}{2}}\right>\cdots\left<\delta\textbf{r}_{\frac{1}{2}}\middle|e^{-\Delta\tau_{0}h_{0}}\middle|\textbf{0}\right>

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k=0}^{M}\prod_{n=1}^{2N}\sqrt{B_{n,k}^{(a)}}\right)\det(\mathcal{M}^{(a)})^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(36)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(a)}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{M}e_{k,k}\otimes(\mathcal{A}_{k-1}^{(a)}+\mathcal{A}_{k}^{(a)})-\sum_{k=1}^{M-1}(e_{k,k+1}+e_{k+1,k})\otimes\mathcal{B}_{k}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{(a)}+\mathcal{A}_{1}^{(a)}&-\mathcal{B}_{1}^{(a)}&\textbf{0}&\cdots&\textbf{0}\\
-\mathcal{B}_{1}^{(a)}&\mathcal{A}_{1}^{(a)}+\mathcal{A}_{2}^{(a)}&-\mathcal{B}_{2}^{(a)}&\cdots&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&-\mathcal{B}_{2}^{(a)}&\mathcal{A}_{2}^{(a)}+\mathcal{A}_{3}^{(a)}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&-\mathcal{B}_{M-1}^{(a)}\\
\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\cdots&-\mathcal{B}_{M-1}^{(a)}&\mathcal{A}_{M-1}^{(a)}+\mathcal{A}_{M}^{(a)}\end{bmatrix},

(37)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}^{(a)}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{2N}A_{n,k}^{(a)}\textbf{v}_{n,k}\textbf{v}_{n,k}^{\rm T},\ \mathcal{B}_{k}^{(a)}\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{2N}B_{n,k}^{(a)}\textbf{v}_{n,k}\textbf{v}_{n,k}^{\rm T}.

(38)

Here,

\mathcal{M}^{(a)}
is a real symmetric block tridiagonal matrix,

e_{i,j}
is the
M\times M
matrix with
1
at the
(i,j)
-th entry with all other entries
0
,

\otimes
is the Kronecker product of two matrices
and
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{(a)}
and
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{(a)}
are
2N\times 2N
matrices.
[Note that in the present WC problem, one needs to project out two zero eigen-modes
\textbf{v}_{n,k}
(for each
k
) corresponding to uniform translations in the
x
and
y
directions; hence
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{(a)}
and
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{(a)}
become
(2N-2)\times(2N-2)
matrices.]

In calculating
F[\textbf{r}_{0}]
—which essentially is the propagator of a quantum harmonic oscillator—with the same procedure, one merely substitutes
h_{k}\to h_{0}
in every equation Eq. (
32
–
38
)

\displaystyle F[\textbf{r}_{0}]=\left(\prod_{k=0}^{M}\prod_{n=1}^{2N}\sqrt{B_{n,k}^{(0)}}\right)\det(\mathcal{M}^{(0)})^{-\frac{1}{2}}

(39)

\displaystyle A^{(0)}_{n,k}\equiv\frac{\omega_{n,0}}{\tanh(\omega_{n,0}\Delta\tau_{k})},\ \,B^{(0)}_{n,k}\equiv\frac{\omega_{n,0}}{\sinh(\omega_{n,0}\Delta\tau_{k})},

(40)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}^{(0)}\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{2N}A_{n,k}^{(0)}\textbf{v}_{n,0}\textbf{v}_{n,0}^{\rm T},\ \,\mathcal{B}_{k}^{(0)}\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{2N}B_{n,k}^{(0)}\textbf{v}_{n,0}\textbf{v}_{n,0}^{\rm T},

(41)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(0)}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{M}e_{k,k}\otimes(\mathcal{A}_{k-1}^{(0)}+\mathcal{A}_{k}^{(0)})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ -\sum_{k=1}^{M-1}(e_{k,k+1}+e_{k+1,k})\otimes\mathcal{B}_{k}^{(0)}.

(42)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{F\big[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)\big]}{F\big[\textbf{r}_{0}\big]}=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{M-1}\prod_{n=1}^{2N}{\frac{B_{n,k}^{(a)}}{B_{n,k}^{(0)}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[\frac{\det(\mathcal{M}^{(a)})}{\det(\mathcal{M}^{(0)})}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(43)

Here the product over
k
runs only from
1
to
M-1
because the end interval contributions (
k=0,M
) of
B_{n,k}^{(a)}
and
B_{n,k}^{(0)}
are identical although they formally approach
0
as
\tilde{\beta}\to\infty
[since
\Delta\tau_{0,M}\to\infty
].
Similarly, one takes
A_{n,0}^{(a)}=A_{n,0}^{(0)}=A_{n,M}^{(a)}=A_{n,M}^{(0)}=\omega_{n,0}
in calculating
\det\mathcal{M}
, as
\Delta\tau\to\infty
[since
\tanh(\omega_{n,0}\Delta\tau)\to 1
].

Finally, one needs to divide Eq.
43
by the (formally diverging) zero mode contribution to
F[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)]={\rm det}\big(-\partial_{\tau}^{2}+V^{\prime\prime}\big[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)\big]\big)^{-1/2}
.
For this, we numerically find the smallest
\lambda
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{F_{\lambda}[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)]}\equiv\det(-\partial_{\tau}^{2}+V^{\prime\prime}\big[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)\big]-\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}=0,

(44)

where the left hand side is calculated similarly as in Eqs. (
32
–
38
) with the substitution
V^{\prime\prime}(\textbf{r}_{k})\to V^{\prime\prime}(\textbf{r}_{k})-\lambda.

The fluctuation determinant is then obtained as

\displaystyle A_{a}=\sqrt{\lambda}\cdot\frac{F\big[\textbf{r}^{(a)}(\tau)\big]}{F\big[\textbf{r}_{0}\big]}.

(45)
