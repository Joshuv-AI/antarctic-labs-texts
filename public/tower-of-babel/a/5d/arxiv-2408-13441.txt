Exploiting degeneracy in projective geometric algebra
John Bamberg, Jeff Saunders

Abstract
. The last two decades, since the seminal work of Selig 2000 , has seen projective geometric algebra (PGA) gain popularity as a modern coordinate-free framework for doing classical Euclidean geometry and other Cayley-Klein geometries. This framework is based upon a degenerate Clifford algebra, and it is the purpose of this paper to delve deeper into its internal algebraic structure and extract meaningful information for the purposes of PGA. This includes exploiting the split extension structure to realise the natural decomposition of elements of this Clifford algebra into Euclidean and ideal parts. This leads to a beautiful demonstration of how Playfair’s axiom for affine geometry arises from the ambient degenerate quadratic space. The highlighted split extension property of the Clifford algebra also corresponds to a splitting of the group of units and the Lie algebra of bivectors. Central to these results is that the degenerate Clifford algebra Cl ⁡ ( V ) \Cl(V) is isomorphic to the twisted trivial extension Cl ⁡ ( V / 𝔽 ​ e 0 ) ⋉ α Cl ⁡ ( V / 𝔽 ​ e 0 ) \Cl(V/\mathbb{F}{e_{0}})\ltimes_{\alpha}\Cl(V/\mathbb{F}{e_{0}}) , where e 0 {e_{0}} is a degenerate vector and α \alpha is the grade-involution.

1.
Introduction

Euclidean projective (or plane-based) geometric algebra (PGA) is a new and promising approach to Euclidean geometry, introduced by
Selig 2000
;
Selig 2005
and developed further by
Gunn 2017a
;
Gunn 2017b
. Euclidean PGA uses a degenerate
(n+1)
-dimensional quadratic space, whose elements represent the hyperplanes of
n
-dimensional Euclidean space; its weak orthogonal group is then isomorphic to the group of motions of Euclidean space (see
Havlicek 2021
;
Havlicek 2023
). PGA also has elliptic and hyperbolic variants, which use
nondegenerate

(n+1)
-dimensional quadratic spaces, and whose Clifford algebras enjoy a natural polarity as a result of that nondegeneracy. See
Gunn 2022
for in-depth coverage of both the uses of this polarity and some alternative dualities available in Euclidean PGA. A theme across discussions of duality has been the idea that Euclidean PGA’s lack of polarity is a deficiency to be either worked around with such alternatives, or avoided by working in the Clifford algebra of a higher-dimensional nondegenerate quadratic space. In this article we point to some additional internal structure, found only in degenerate Clifford algebras, that may lead to new perspectives.

Dorst and De Keninck 2023
make heavy use of the notion of splitting elements of the Clifford algebra into
Euclidean
and
ideal
parts;
Lengyel 2023
similarly decomposes elements into
bulk
and
weight
parts. We will see in
Section

2
that this splitting comes from the fact that the degenerate quadratic space of planes in Euclidean space is a split extension of the space of parallel classes of planes, and that the latter is isometric to the cotangent space at any point. This can be used to demonstrate Playfair’s axiom (better known as Euclid’s fifth postulate), giving our first hint at the deep links between degenerate quadratic spaces and metric affine geometry. We also get, via functoriality, resulting split extensions of the corresponding Clifford algebra (
Section

3
), as well as its group of units and the Lie algebra of its bivectors (
Section

4
). In particular we have the beautiful coincidence that all of the ideal parts of elements of the Clifford algebra live in an ideal of the algebra.

The mathematical treatment in this paper is entirely coordinate-free and works for any field not of characteristic 2, but in the interests of applicability and accessibility, the running example uses the real numbers and a basis. Some readers may be surprised to see the semidirect product symbol
\ltimes
, rather than its mirror image
\rtimes
which may be more commonly seen in group theory texts; for compatibility and consistency with the Clifford algebra convention of acting on the left, the authors have chosen the former notation.

3.
Decomposing a degenerate Clifford algebra

Let
V=(V,b)
be a quadratic space. The
Clifford algebra
of
V
is the quotient algebra

\Cl(V)=T(V)/J(V),

where
T(V)
is the tensor algebra of
V
and
J(V)
is the ideal of
T(V)
generated by

u\otimes v+v\otimes u-2b(u,v),

for all
u,v\in V
. As we are working over a field not of characteristic 2, this construction is equivalent to the one given in
Chevalley 1997
(which uses a quadratic form). The universal property of the Clifford algebra, Chevalley’s Theorem 3.1, implies the existence of the functor
\Cl:\Quad_{\mathbb{F}}\to\mathrm{Alg}_{\mathbb{F}}
, where
\mathrm{Alg}_{\mathbb{F}}
denotes the category of associative algebras and associative algebra homomorphisms over
\mathbb{F}
. This functor maps an isometry
f:V\to V^{\prime}
to its
Clifford extension
, the unique unital algebra homomorphism
\Cl(f):\Cl(V)\to\Cl(V^{\prime})
extending
f
. In fact, every unital algebra homomorphism
\varphi:\Cl(V)\to\Cl(V^{\prime})
such that
\varphi(V)\subseteq V^{\prime}
is the Clifford extension of an isometry, so we call
\varphi
a
Clifford algebra homomorphism
. We also define a
Clifford algebra derivation

D:\Cl(V)\to\Cl(V)
as a derivation – i.e., a linear map satisfying
D(XY)=XD(Y)+D(X)Y
for all
X,Y\in\Cl(V)
– such that
D(V)\subseteq V
.

We recall the linear isomorphism
\theta:\Cl(V)\to\Ex(V)
, as shown by
Chevalley 1997
. We define the wedge product on the Clifford algebra via said isomorphism:

\begin{split}\wedge:\Cl(V)\times\Cl(V)&\to\Cl(V)\\
(X,Y)&\mapsto X\wedge Y\coloneqq\theta^{-1}(\theta(X)\wedge\theta(Y)),\end{split}

and the direct sum decomposition

\displaystyle\Cl(V)={}

\displaystyle\Cl^{0}(V)\oplus\Cl^{1}(V)\oplus\dotsb\oplus\Cl^{n}(V)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\theta^{-1}(\Ex^{0}(V))\oplus\theta^{-1}(\Ex^{1}(V))\oplus\dotsb\oplus\theta^{-1}(\Ex^{n}(V)).

We refer to
\Cl^{k}(V)
as the
grade-
k
part
of
\Cl(V)
; note that this gives only a vector space grading of
\Cl(V)
and not an algebra grading. In any quadratic space, the map
(-\id):v\mapsto-v
is an orthogonal transformation, whose Clifford extension is the
main automorphism
(or
grade involution
)
\alpha\coloneqq\Cl(-\id)
and has the effect of negating the odd-graded parts of

and preserving the even-graded ones. The tensor algebra

is finitely spanned by simple tensors

, and the Clifford algebra, being a quotient of

, inherits this property of being finitely spanned by finite products of elements of
V
.

From now on we assume that
V
is degenerate and pick a one-dimensional radical subspace
\mathbb{F}{e_{0}}

\mathbb{F}{e_{0}}
has an orthogonal complement

is a proper subspace of
\rad V

Corollary 3.3
.

For every

, we have that
\Cl(\pi_{W})(X){e_{0}}=X{e_{0}}
. That is, the sets
\Cl(V){e_{0}}

We can thus define the projection

\displaystyle D_{W}:\Cl(V)

\displaystyle\to\Cl(V){e_{0}}

so that

. The idempotence of

Lemma 3.4
.

D_{W}
is a Clifford algebra derivation and fixes
{e_{0}}
.

Proof.

First,
\Cl(\pi_{W})({e_{0}})=(\omega_{W}\circ\pi)({e_{0}})=0
, so
D_{W}({e_{0}})={e_{0}}-\Cl(\pi_{W})({e_{0}})={e_{0}}
. Now let

be a nonnegative integer and

. For each

, there exists
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
such that
D_{W}(v_{i})=v_{i}-\pi_{W}(v_{i})=\lambda_{i}{e_{0}}
. Thus we have that

\displaystyle D_{W}(v_{0}\dotsm v_{k})

\displaystyle=v_{0}\dotsm v_{k}-\Cl(\pi_{W})(v_{0}\dotsm v_{k})

\displaystyle=v_{0}\dotsm v_{k}-(v_{0}-\lambda_{0}{e_{0}})\dotsm(v_{k}-\lambda_{k}{e_{0}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}v_{0}\dotsm v_{i-1}\alpha(v_{i+1}\dotsm v_{k}){e_{0}},

and hence that

\displaystyle D_{W}(v_{0}\dotsm v_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}v_{0}\dotsm v_{i-1}\alpha(v_{i+1}\dotsm v_{k}){e_{0}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}\alpha(v_{1}\dotsm v_{k}){e_{0}}+v_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}v_{1}\dotsm v_{i-1}\alpha(v_{i+1}\dotsm v_{k}){e_{0}}

\displaystyle=v_{0}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}v_{1}\dotsm v_{i-1}\alpha(v_{i+1}\dotsm v_{k}){e_{0}}\right)+(\lambda_{0}{e_{0}})v_{1}\dotsm v_{k}

\displaystyle=v_{0}D_{W}(v_{1}\dotsm v_{k})+D_{W}(v_{0})v_{1}\dotsm v_{k},

so

is a derivation on finite products of elements of

. As
D_{W}
is trivially linear and since
\Cl(V)
is finitely spanned by finite products of elements of
V
, we have that
D_{W}
is a derivation on all of
\Cl(V)
. Finally, as

for all

is a Clifford algebra derivation.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

The quotient algebra
\Cl(V)/\Cl(V){e_{0}}
is canonically isomorphic to the Clifford algebra of the quotient space
\Cl(V/\mathbb{F}{e_{0}})
.

Proof.

For any
X{e_{0}}\in\Cl(V){e_{0}}
, we see that
\Cl(\pi)(X{e_{0}})=\Cl(\pi)(X)\pi({e_{0}})=0
, so
\Cl(V){e_{0}}\subseteq\ker\Cl(\pi)

The ideal

Lemma 3.7

(2)

Example

In the context of Euclidean PGA, the quotient Clifford algebra
\Cl(V/\mathbb{R}{e_{0}})
(likewise

) describes the objects in and transformations of the space of parallel classes of planes in Euclidean space. Along with the parallel classes of planes themselves, we see two-parameter bundles of parallel lines, and one “parallel class” comprising all points in space. We also see reflections and rotations of space; note that the action of, say, a rotation on a parallel class of planes does not depend on the axis of rotation, only on the angle and the parallel class to which the axis belongs, so a “parallel class” of rotations carries only this information. As we see from
Theorem

3.10
, these objects and transformations correspond (canonically!) one-to-one with the objects through and transformations fixing a given point

\Cl(V_{P})
. The second statement in
Theorem

3.10
says that each of these parallel classes also corresponds one-to-one with an element at infinity in the ideal
\Cl(V){e_{0}}
; for instance, a parallel bundle of lines meets infinity at a single ideal point. The correspondence is canonical up to a choice of scalar multiple of
{e_{0}}
.

A
square-zero ideal
is an ideal
I
such that
I^{2}=\{ij\mid i,j\in I\}=\{0\}
. By
Lemma

3.1
, the ideal
\Cl(V){e_{0}}
is a square-zero ideal. As
\Cl(V){e_{0}}
is the kernel of
\Cl(\pi)
, this makes

a
square-zero extension
of
\Cl(W)
. The most well-known square-zero extension is the
trivial extension
of a ring
R
by an
R
-bimodule
M
, which is the ring
R\ltimes M
given by the direct sum
R\oplus M
and the product
(r_{1},m_{1})(r_{2},m_{2})\coloneqq(r_{1}r_{2},r_{1}m_{2}+m_{1}r_{2})
. This is not quite what we want, as we will see; we turn instead to the
twisted trivial extension
as defined by
Guo 2020
, in which the module

is replaced by its twist

for a ring automorphism
\sigma
of

is the same left

, but its right multiplication is given by
m\cdot r\coloneqq m\sigma(r)
. The twisted trivial extension
R\ltimes_{\sigma}M\coloneqq R\ltimes M^{\sigma}
thus has product
(r_{1},m_{1})(r_{2},m_{2})=(r_{1}r_{2},r_{1}m_{2}+m_{1}\sigma(r_{2}))
.

Theorem 3.11
.

The degenerate Clifford algebra

By

\begin{split}\varphi((X_{1}+Y_{1}{e_{0}})(X_{2}+Y_{2}{e_{0}}))&=\varphi(X_{1}X_{2}+X_{1}Y_{2}{e_{0}}+Y_{1}{e_{0}}X_{2}+Y_{1}{e_{0}}Y_{2}{e_{0}})\\

4.
The group of units and Lie algebra of bivectors

We denote the
group of units
of a unital algebra
\mathcal{A}
by
\mathcal{A}^{\times}
, and for a homomorphism
\varphi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
, denote the
restriction to units

\varphi^{\times}\coloneqq\varphi|_{\mathcal{A}^{\times}}:\mathcal{A}^{\times}\to\mathcal{B}^{\times}
. This defines the functor
-^{\times}:\mathrm{Alg}_{\mathbb{F}}\to\Grp
(see
Cohn 2003
). The proof of the following lemma is adapted from a similar one by
Anderson and Winders 2009
about the units of a trivial extension of a commutative ring.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
R
be a ring with an automorphism
\sigma
, and
M
an
R
-bimodule. The group of units
(R\ltimes_{\sigma}M)^{\times}
of the twisted trivial extension
R\ltimes_{\sigma}M
is
R_{0}^{\times}\ltimes(1+M_{0})\cong R^{\times}\ltimes_{\tau}M
, where
R_{0}=\{(r,0)\mid r\in R\}
,
1+M_{0}=\{(1,m)\mid m\in M\}
, and
\tau:R^{\times}\to\Aut_{R}(M)
is given by
\tau(r):m\mapsto rm\sigma(r^{-1})
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to show that
{R_{0}}^{\times}
is a subgroup of
(R\ltimes_{\sigma}M)^{\times}
isomorphic to
R^{\times}
, that
1+M_{0}
is an abelian subgroup of
(R\ltimes_{\sigma}M)^{\times}
isomorphic to
M
, and that the two subgroups intersect trivially. Now suppose that
(r,m)\in(R\ltimes_{\sigma}M)^{\times}
; then there exists
(s,n)\in R\ltimes_{\sigma}M
such that
(r,m)(s,n)=(rs,rn+m\sigma(s))=(1,0)
. Hence
rs=1
, so
(r,0)(s,0)=(1,0)
and hence
(r,0)
is a unit. Thus
(r,0)(1,sm)=(r,m)
and so
(R\ltimes_{\sigma}M)^{\times}={R_{0}}^{\times}\ltimes(1+M_{0})
, where the action of
{R_{0}}^{\times}
on
1+M_{0}
is given by

(1,m)\mapsto(r^{-1},0)(1,m)(r,0)=(1,r^{-1}m\sigma(r))=(1,\tau(r^{-1})(m)).

Therefore
(R\ltimes_{\sigma}M)^{\times}\cong R^{\times}\ltimes_{\tau}M
.
∎

Recall that we assume a degenerate quadratic space
V
with
{e_{0}}\in\rad V
and
W
some complement of
\mathbb{F}{e_{0}}
in
V
.

Corollary 4.2
.

The group of units of
\Cl(V)
is
\Cl(W)^{\times}\ltimes(1+\Cl(W)e_{0})
, which is isomorphic to
\Cl(V/\mathbb{F}{e_{0}})^{\times}\ltimes_{\tau}\Cl(V/\mathbb{F}{e_{0}})
and
\Cl(W)^{\times}\ltimes_{\tau}\Cl(W)

This establishes the decomposition of the group of units of a degenerate Clifford algebra. Of course, the interesting groups in a Clifford algebra are proper subgroups of the group of units; things get much more complicated here. The authors refer the reader to
Abłamowicz 1986
and
Filimoshina and Shirokov 2023
for more detailed treatments of these subgroups. At every level, we see extensions of the form
G\ltimes(1+M{e_{0}})
, where
G
is some subgroup of
\Cl(W)^{\times}
and
M
is some subspace of
\Cl(W)
.

Also interesting is the Lie algebra of grade-2 elements or
bivectors
(see
Dorst et al. 2010
for a gentle introduction). For any bivector
B\in\Cl^{2}(V)
and any
X\in\Cl^{k}(V)
, the
commutator

B\times X\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(BX-BX)

is in
\Cl^{k}(V)
. In particular,
\Cl^{2}(V)
is closed, and hence a Lie algebra, under this commutator. It is straightforward to show, by writing each element
B\in\Cl^{2}(V)
in the form

B=u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{2}+\dotsb+u_{k}v_{k},

where
b(u_{i},v_{i})=0
for each

, that a Clifford algebra homomorphism
\varphi:\Cl(V)\to\Cl(V^{\prime})
restricts to a Lie algebra homomorphism
\varphi^{2}:\Cl^{2}(V)\to\Cl^{2}(V^{\prime})
, and so
-^{2}
is a functor from
\Cl(\Quad_{\mathbb{F}})
to
\LieAlg_{\mathbb{F}}
, the category of Lie algebras and Lie algebra homomorphisms.
Gracia-Bondía et al. 2001
show that
\Cl^{2}(V)\cong\so(V)
in the case when

is real and nondegenerate. What happens in the degenerate case?

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
B\in\Cl^{2}(V)
. Then
D_{W}(B)=w{e_{0}}
for some

.

Proof.

Let

and

. Then by
Corollary

2.3
, there exist

and

\displaystyle D_{W}(v_{1}v_{2})

\displaystyle=D_{W}((w_{1}+\lambda_{1}{e_{0}})(w_{2}+\lambda_{2}{e_{0}}))

\displaystyle=D_{W}(w_{1}w_{2}+(\lambda_{2}w_{1}-\lambda_{1}w_{2}){e_{0}})

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{2}w_{1}-\lambda_{1}w_{2}){e_{0}}\in W{e_{0}}.

\displaystyle w{e_{0}}

\displaystyle\tau(B_{1}\times B_{2})(w)={}

\displaystyle(B_{1}\times B_{2})\times w

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}((B_{1}B_{2}-B_{2}B_{1})w-w(B_{1}B_{2}-B_{2}B_{1}))

5.
Conclusion

Classical treatments of degenerate quadratic spaces and their Clifford algebras have focused on splitting into a nondegenerate quotient space and the radical. In this article, inspired by the unique structure of the affine geometry found in Euclidean PGA, we describe a new “one dimension at a time” approach, yielding trivial decompositions of the corresponding Clifford algebra (
Theorem

3.11
), its group of units (
Corollary

4.2
), and its Lie algebra of bivectors (
Theorem

4.4
). We also find, in
Theorem

3.8
, a surprising correspondence between a class of derivations of the Clifford algebra and points in the affine space described.

We also show that, in the case of the degenerate quadratic space used by Euclidean PGA, the quotient space has a natural interpretation as the space of parallel classes of planes. This interpretation, combined with the results above, helps provide mathematical background for the Euclidean split described by
Dorst and De Keninck 2023
, and may lead to new methods. The isomorphisms of
Theorem

3.10
appear particularly likely to bear fruit; consider, for example, that lines at infinity correspond one-to-one with parallel classes of planes in space, and that the norm
v^{2}=(v+{e_{0}})^{2}
is well-defined on parallel classes of planes. This interpretation should work for any metric affine geometry – wherever we find unique parallels – in any dimension and with any degenerate signature; the authors see this avenue as a kind of
affine geometric algebra
, standing next to PGA among modern geometric algebra’s various flavours.
