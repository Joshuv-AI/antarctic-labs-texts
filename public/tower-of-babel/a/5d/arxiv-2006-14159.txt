Scalar scattering amplitude in Gaussian wave-packet formalism
Kenzo Ishikawa, Kenji Nishiwaki, and Kin-ya Oda

Abstract
We compute an s s -channel 2 → 2 2\to 2 scalar scattering ϕ ​ ϕ → Φ → ϕ ​ ϕ \phi\phi\to\Phi\to\phi\phi in the Gaussian wave-packet formalism at the tree-level. We find that wave-packet effects, including shifts of the pole and width of the propagator of Φ \Phi , persist even when we do not take into account the time-boundary effect for 2 → 2 2\to 2 , proposed earlier. The result can be interpreted that a heavy scalar 1 → 2 1\to 2 decay Φ → ϕ ​ ϕ \Phi\to\phi\phi , taking into account the production of Φ \Phi , does not exhibit the in-state time-boundary effect unless we further take into account in-boundary effects for the 2 → 2 2\to 2 scattering. We also show various plane-wave limits.

Introduction and summary

It is well-known that a plane-wave S-matrix is ill-defined when taken literally because its matrix element is proportional to the energy-momentum delta function, which always gives either zero or infinity when squared to compute a probability.
On the other hand, we may define an S-matrix in the Gaussian wave-packet basis without such an infinity

It has been claimed that the Gaussian formalism gives a deviation from the Fermi’s golden rule

2.1
Gaussian basis

We review the Gaussian formalism, following Ref.
[
2
]
, to clarify the notation in this paper.
A free scalar field operator
\hat{\varphi}
at
x=\left(x^{0},\boldsymbol{x}\right)
(in the interaction picture) can be expanded by the plane basis:

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}\!\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\left.\int{\text{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{p}\over\sqrt{2p^{0}}\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}}\left[e^{ip\cdot x}\hat{a}_{\varphi}\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)+\text{h.c.}\right]\right|_{p^{0}=E_{\varphi}\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)}

\displaystyle=\left.\int{\text{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{p}\over\sqrt{2p^{0}}}\left[\Braket{\varphi;x|\varphi;\boldsymbol{p}}\hat{a}_{\varphi}\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)+\text{h.c.}\right]\right|_{p^{0}=E_{\varphi}\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)},

(1)

where
\varphi=\phi,\Phi
labels the particle species;

\hat{a}_{\varphi}\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)
and
\hat{a}_{\varphi}^{\dagger}\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)
are the annihilation and creation operators, respectively, with

\displaystyle\left[\hat{a}_{\varphi}\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right),\hat{a}_{\varphi^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\delta_{\varphi\varphi^{\prime}}\delta^{3}\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}\right),

others

\displaystyle=0;

(2)

and

\displaystyle E_{\varphi}\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{m_{\varphi}^{2}+\boldsymbol{p}^{2}},

(3)

\displaystyle\Ket{\varphi;\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle:=\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\varphi}\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)\Ket{0},

(4)

\displaystyle\Braket{\varphi;\boldsymbol{x}|\varphi^{\prime};\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle:=\delta_{\varphi\varphi^{\prime}}{e^{i\boldsymbol{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\over\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}},

(5)

\displaystyle\Ket{\varphi;x}

\displaystyle:=e^{+i\hat{H}_{\text{free}}t}\Ket{\varphi;\boldsymbol{x}},

(6)

with
\hat{H}_{\text{free}}
being the free Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{free}}\Ket{\varphi;\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle=E_{\varphi}\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)\Ket{\varphi;\boldsymbol{p}}.

(7)

Here and hereafter, we use
t,T
and
x^{0},X^{0}
interchangeably:
t=x^{0}
and
T=X^{0}
.
Note that
\Ket{\varphi;\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\Ket{\varphi;\boldsymbol{p}}
are independent of time and hence can be regarded as either a Heisenberg-picture state or a Schrödinger-picture eigenbasis (of total Hamiltonian), while
\Ket{\varphi;x}
is an interaction-picture basis at time
x^{0}
as seen from its time evolution by the free Hamiltonian.

We define a Gaussian wave-packet state
\Ket{\varphi,\sigma;\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
by

\displaystyle\Braket{\varphi^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}|\varphi,\sigma;\boldsymbol{\Pi}}

\displaystyle:={1\over\left(\pi\sigma\right)^{3/4}}e^{i\boldsymbol{P}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{X}\right)}e^{-{1\over 2\sigma}\left(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{X}\right)^{2}}\delta_{\varphi\varphi^{\prime}},

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{\Pi}:=\left(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{P}\right)
gives the center of wave packet in the phase space. Note that

\displaystyle\Braket{\varphi^{\prime},\boldsymbol{p}|\varphi,\sigma;\boldsymbol{\Pi}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\varphi\varphi^{\prime}}\left(\sigma\over\pi\right)^{3/4}e^{-i\boldsymbol{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{X}}e^{-{\sigma\over 2}\left(\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{P}\right)^{2}},

(9)

\displaystyle\Braket{\varphi,\sigma;\boldsymbol{\Pi}|\varphi^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime};\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sigma_{\text{I}}\over\sigma_{\text{A}}\right)^{3/4}e^{-{1\over 4\sigma_{\text{A}}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}e^{-{\sigma_{\text{I}}\over 4}\left(\boldsymbol{P}-\boldsymbol{P}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}e^{{i\over 2\sigma_{\text{I}}}\left(\sigma\boldsymbol{P}+\sigma^{\prime}\boldsymbol{P}^{\prime}\right)\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\right)}\delta_{\varphi\varphi^{\prime}},

(10)

where

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{A}}

\displaystyle:={\sigma+\sigma^{\prime}\over 2},

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{I}}

\displaystyle:=\left(\sigma^{-1}+\sigma^{\prime-1}\over 2\right)^{-1}={2\sigma\sigma^{\prime}\over\sigma+\sigma^{\prime}},

(11)

are the average and the inverse of average of inverse, respectively.
Especially,

\displaystyle\Braket{\varphi,\sigma;\boldsymbol{\Pi}|\varphi,\sigma;\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=e^{-{1\over 4\sigma}\left(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}e^{-{\sigma\over 4}\left(\boldsymbol{P}-\boldsymbol{P}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}e^{{i\over 2}\left(\boldsymbol{P}+\boldsymbol{P}^{\prime}\right)\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\right)}.

(12)

The state
\Ket{\varphi,\sigma;\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
is time independent and hence can be regarded as either a Heisenberg state or a Schrödinger basis.
We also define the interaction basis at time
X^{0}

\displaystyle\Ket{\varphi,\sigma;\Pi}

\displaystyle:=e^{i\hat{H}_{\text{free}}X^{0}}\Ket{\varphi,\sigma;\boldsymbol{\Pi}},

(13)

where
\Pi:=\left(X,\boldsymbol{P}\right)=\left(X^{0},\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{P}\right)=\left(X^{0},\boldsymbol{\Pi}\right)

As we will see later, we will treat
\Ket{\varphi,\sigma;\Pi}
as a time-independent Heisenberg state (or equivalently a time-independent Schrödinger basis).

We define a creation operator of the Gaussian basis by

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{\varphi,\sigma}^{\dagger}\!\left(\Pi\right)\Ket{0}

\displaystyle:=\Ket{\varphi,\sigma;\Pi},

(14)

which results in

\displaystyle\left[\hat{A}_{\varphi,\sigma}\!\left(\Pi\right),\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{\varphi^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}\!\left(\Pi^{\prime}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\Braket{\varphi,\sigma,\Pi|\varphi^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime};\Pi^{\prime}},

others

\displaystyle=0.

(15)

We may also expand

by the creation and annihilation operators of the free Gaussian wave packets:

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}\!\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\int{\text{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{X}\,\text{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{P}\over\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\left[f_{\varphi,\sigma;X,\boldsymbol{P}}\!\left(x\right)\hat{A}_{\varphi,\sigma}\!\left(X,\boldsymbol{P}\right)+\text{h.c.}\right],

3.4
Comparison of two constructions

The in-boundary effect for the fixed configuration
\left(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{4}\right)
disappears from
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, which includes the interaction from the time
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
(or sufficiently earlier time than
T_{\text{in}}-\sqrt{2\varsigma_{\text{out}}}
for the given final state configuration) to
T_{\text{in}}
in Eq. (
98
).
In the original
\mathcal{S}
in the “free” construction, interactions at
t<T_{\text{in}}
does not appear. If we start from
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
for the configuration
\left(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{4}\right)
, we recover the boundary effect of
\mathcal{S}
by sharply switching off interactions at
t<T_{\text{in}}
.

Here in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, although the free wave packets in
\Ket{\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}}
are given experimentally at
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, we identify
\Ket{\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}}
with the Heisenberg state at much earlier time
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
, not at somewhere
T_{\text{in}}
near them. Namely, the Schrödinger-picture state
e^{-i\hat{H}t}\Ket{\text{in; }\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}}^{\prime}
at
t\to T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
is identified with the “free Schrödinger-picture” state
e^{-i\hat{H}_{\text{free}}t}\Ket{\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}}
that is time-evolved backward
t\to T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
in a virtual free world governed by
\hat{H}_{\text{free}}
, even for the case where interactions are not negligible for
t<T_{\text{in}}
. In
\Ket{\text{in; }\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}}^{\prime}
, interactions are put at times much earlier than
T_{\text{in}}
at which the supposedly free in-state is to be defined.

For the particular in and out-state configuration
\left(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{4}\right)
with
\left(\mathfrak{T}_{\text{out}}-T_{\text{in}}\right)^{2}\lesssim 2\varsigma_{\text{out}}
, we may always choose
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}\ll T_{\text{in}}-\sqrt{2\varsigma_{\text{out}}}
, and the in-boundary effect for this configuration drops out of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, but there always exist other configuration
(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{3},\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{4})
that has the in-boundary effect at
\mathfrak{T}_{\text{out}}\simeq T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
accordingly to Eq. (
55
).
Therefore, the probability summed over
(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{3},\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{4})
has the in-boundary effect for any fixed
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
.

Let us rephrase the above discussion in a slightly different way.
As we move
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
backwards, the bulk region expands, and the effective in-boundary at
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
goes back in time.
For a given
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
, the in-boundary contribution arises from the out state that has overlap of out wave packets at
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
.
Therefore, the
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}\to-\infty
limit is not uniform because the region of in-boundary effect in
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{3}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{4}
moves along with
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
.
For these out states for given
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
, the boundary effect persists. If such an out state is not included, the boundary effect disappears.

To summarize so far, for any configuration of
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{3}
and
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{4}
, there always exists a
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
that removes the boundary effect, while for any
T_{\text{in}}^{\prime}
, there always exists a configuration of
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{3}
and
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{4}
that yields an in-boundary effect. Therefore it is subject to debate whether or not the limit (
105
) can be taken to remove all the time boundary effects.

The expression for boundary effect in the second term in Eq. (
72
) vanishes exponentially in the limit
T_{\text{in}}\to-\infty
. In the “dressed” construction, this is natural because this limit corresponds to taking into account all the interactions from
-\infty
, for the fixed initial and final state configurations. In the “free” construction, one emphasizes the fact that no matter how much we take the limit
T_{\text{in}}\to-\infty
, there always exists a final state configuration with
\left(\Re\mathcal{T}_{\text{out}}-T_{\text{in}}\right)^{2}\lesssim 2\varsigma_{\text{out}}
for a given
T_{\text{in}}
.
The difference of two constructions is the order of procedures: taking the limit
T_{\text{in}}\to\infty
first vs integrating over the infinite volume of
\left(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{3},\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{4}\right)
first.

So far, both constructions have pros and cons, subject to one’s theoretical prejudice.
Ultimately, experiment should determine which (or else) is right.
Currently, an experiment is on-going
[
11
]
based on the “free” construction
[
12
]
.
In this paper, we will leave the choice of constructions open, and concentrate on the wave effect that persists even when we only take into account the bulk effects.

\displaystyle\approx{E_{\text{in}}+{\varsigma_{\text{out}}\over\varsigma_{\text{in}}}E_{\text{out}}\over{1+{\varsigma_{\text{out}}\over\varsigma_{\text{in}}}}}\approx E\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\right)\approx E_{\text{in}}\approx E_{\text{out}},

(175)

\displaystyle F_{*}

\displaystyle\to 0,

(176)

where
\approx
denotes that we have used the energy and momentum conservation from the above mentioned delta functions.
Based on the above information, we derive the plane-wave limit of the propagator:

\displaystyle{1\over-\left(\left(\Omega\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{*}\right)\right)^{2}-\left(\delta\mathfrak{T}\over\varsigma_{+}\right)^{2}\right)+i2\Omega\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{*}\right){\delta\mathfrak{T}\over\varsigma_{+}}+\boldsymbol{p}_{*}^{2}+M^{2}-i\epsilon}

\displaystyle\to{1\over-\left(\Omega\!\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{\ast}\right)\right)^{2}+\boldsymbol{p}_{*}^{2}+M^{2}-i\epsilon}

\displaystyle\approx{1\over-\bigl(E\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\right)\bigr)^{2}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{2}+M^{2}-i\epsilon}.

(177)

We see that the propagator is reduced to the plane-wave form.
To summarize,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\to i\left(\prod_{A=1}^{4}{1\over\sqrt{2E_{A}}}\left(1\over\pi\sigma_{A}\right)^{3/4}\right){\kappa^{2}\over-\bigl(E\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\right)\bigr)^{2}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{2}+M^{2}-i\epsilon}\left(2\pi\right)^{4}\delta^{4}\!\left(P_{\text{out}}-P_{\text{in}}\right),

(178)

where
\delta^{4}\!\left(P_{\text{out}}-P_{\text{in}}\right)=\delta\!\left(E_{\text{out}}-E_{\text{in}}\right)\delta^{3}\!\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{out}}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{in}}\right)
.
