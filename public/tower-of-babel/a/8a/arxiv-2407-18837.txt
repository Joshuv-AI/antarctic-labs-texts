Distributionally Robust Kalman Filtering over Finite and Infinite-Horizon
Taylan Kargin *, Joudi Hajar *, Vikrant Malik *, Babak Hassibi

Abstract
This paper investigates the distributionally robust filtering of signals generated by state-space models driven by exogenous disturbances with noisy observations in finite and infinite horizon scenarios. The exact joint probability distribution of the disturbances and noise is unknown but assumed to reside within a Wasserstein-2 ambiguity ball centered around a given nominal distribution. We aim to derive a causal estimator that minimizes the worst-case mean squared estimation error among all possible distributions within this ambiguity set. We remove the iid restriction in prior works by permitting arbitrarily time-correlated disturbances and noises. In the finite horizon setting, we reduce this problem to a semi-definite program (SDP), with computational complexity scaling with the time horizon. For infinite horizon settings, we characterize the optimal estimator using Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions. Although the optimal estimator lacks a rational form, i.e. , a finite-dimensional state-space realization, it can be fully described by a finite-dimensional parameter. Leveraging this parametrization, we propose efficient algorithms that compute the optimal estimator with arbitrary fidelity in the frequency domain. Moreover, given any finite degree, we provide an efficient convex optimization algorithm that finds the finite-dimensional state-space estimator that best approximates the optimal non-rational filter in ℋ ∞ {\cal H}_{\infty} norm. This facilitates the practical implementation of the infinite horizon filter without having to grapple with the ill-scaled SDP from finite time. Finally, numerical simulations demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach in practical scenarios.

1.1
Related Works

Recognizing these advantages,
Shafieezadeh Abadeh et al. [50]
introduced a distributionally robust Kalman filter based on
\mathsf{W_{2}}
-ambiguity sets confined to Gaussian distributions only. They derive state estimates at local time instances by minimizing the mean-squared error for the least favorable joint posterior distribution of the state-measurement vector, given past measurements. This iterative procedure, assuming iid Gaussian disturbances, incorporates the worst-case covariance of the previous state estimate into the nominal model for the subsequent time step. However, while this method inherently addresses state-space parameter mismatches, it lacks a global robustness guarantee over the entire time horizon and against non-iid or non-Gaussian disturbances. Similar temporally local approaches have also been studied in
[
51
,
52
,
53
]
. More recently,
Lotidis et al. [55]
took a different approach by imposing distributional uncertainty on the measurement noise process over the entire time horizon, assuming known iid process noise with known covariance. While the resulting filter demonstrates global robustness over the entire time horizon, the adversarial measurement noise is constrained by martingale conditions to prevent clairvoyance and dependence on future process noise realizations. Moreover, the assumption of known iid process noise is restrictive and does not provide robustness to modeling errors of the dynamics and the process noise.

1.2
Contributions

In this work, we consider the Wasserstein-2 distributionally robust Kalman filtering (
\mathsf{W_{2}}
-DR-KF) of linear state-space models for both finite and infinite horizons. The probability distribution of the disturbances over the entire time horizon is assumed to lie in a
\mathsf{W_{2}}
-ball of a specified radius centered at a given nominal distribution. We seek the optimal causal linear estimator of a target signal that minimizes the worst-case MSE within the

2.3

1. Global robustness to non-iid disturbances:
In contrast to focusing on the worst-case MSE at local time instances under unknown iid disturbances

,
53

, our approach minimizes the cumulative MSE under the worst-case disturbance trajectory, thereby achieving global robustness for the entire horizon. Moreover, unlike

2. Bounded steady-state error:
We derive the first infinite-horizon (aka steady-state)

-filters

3. Efficient real-time implementation:
The finite-horizon

3.4
), rendering it impractical for real-time implementation over long time horizons. However, our infinite-horizon

-DR-KF can be implemented efficiently, thanks to our novel rational approximation, thereby overcoming the scalability issues of SDP formulation.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

1. Tractable convex formulation:
We derive an SDP (
Theorem

3.4
) formulation for the finite-horizon problem, and a concave-convex max-min optimization problem over positive-definite Toeplitz operators (

2. Optimality of linear estimators for Gaussian nominal:
We focus on linear estimators while allowing the distributions in the ambiguity set to be non-Gaussian. For Gaussian nominal distributions, we show the optimality of linear estimators (

3. Characterization of the infinite-horizon DR-KF:
We derive the infinite-horizon

-DR-KF via KKT conditions (

2.2
The Infinite-Horizon Distributionally Robust Filtering

Designing optimal filters for extended horizons can generally be impractical extended time horizons. To mitigate this, time-invariant steady-state filters are usually deployed for practical purposes. These filters can be characterized by their Markov parameters
\{\widehat{K}_{t}\}
, allowing the estimates
\{\widehat{s}_{t}\}
to be computed as a convolution sum:
\widehat{s}_{t}\!=\!\sum_{s=0}^{t}\widehat{K}_{t-s}y_{s}

\widehat{\mathbf{s}}=\mathcal{K}\mathbf{y}

\mathcal{K}
is a bounded, causal, and doubly-infinite block Toeplitz operator constructed from the Markov parameters

\mathscr{K}
.

Here,
\mathbf{y}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}
are the doubly infinite column vectors of measurements and estimates, respectively. Furthermore, letting by
\bm{\xi}=[\mathbf{w};\mathbf{v}]

\mathbf{s}
be doubly-infinite disturbance and target signal vectors, respectively, the state-space dynamics (
2

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{v},

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\mathbf{w},

where
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{L}

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}}:\bm{\xi}\mapsto\mathbf{e}\coloneqq\widehat{\mathbf{s}}\!-\!\mathbf{s}

The nominal disturbances

(
\mathsf{W_{2}}
-DR-KF over infinite-horizon)

\mathcal{K}\!\in\!\mathscr{K}

Lemma 3.3
.

Under the

This result highlights the triviality of non-causal estimation as opposed to causal estimation. In

-DR-KF problem

Theorem 3.4

(An SDP formulation for finite-horizon

-DR-KF)

Let the horizon

be fixed and denote
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}^{\circ}}\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}^{\circ}}^{\ast}\!\coloneqq\!\mathcal{L}_{T}(\mathcal{I}_{T}\!+\!\mathcal{H}_{T}^{\ast}\mathcal{H}_{T})^{\mathrm{\!-\!1}}\mathcal{L}_{T}^{\ast}

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{K}_{T}\in\mathscr{K}_{T},\\

.

As

-DR-KF policy,

3.2
A Concave-Convex Optimization for the Infinite-Horizon Filtering

The scaling of the SDP in

3.4
with the time horizon is prohibitive for many time-critical real-world applications. Therefore, we shift our focus to the infinite-horizon

-DR-KF problem

Solving
Problem

, as defined in (

), to an equivalent convex optimization problem. We address this by leveraging the asymptotic convergence properties of Toeplitz matrices
[

]
. The second challenge is addressing the causality constraint on the estimator. To illustrate the triviality of non-causal estimation in the infinite-horizon setting, we present an analogous result as shown below:

Lemma 3.7
.

Under the Assumptions
2.5
and
3.2
,
\mathcal{K}_{\circ}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}\mathcal{H}^{\ast}(\mathcal{I}\!+\!\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}^{\ast})^{\mathrm{\!-\!1}}

) for any
\rho>0

We address the causality constraint by reformulating

[

,

]
(see

). To this end, we introduce the
canonical spectral factorization

Theorem 3.8

.

Under the Assumptions

4
An Efficient Algorithm

While the standard Kalman and
\mathit{H_{\infty}}
-filters allow for finite-order spate-space realizations derived via algebraic methods, the optimal
\mathcal{K}_{\star}
lacks such a realization since its transfer function
K_{\star}(z)
is non-rational (
Corollary

4.2
) despite admitting a non-linear finite-dimensional parametrization (
Lemma

4.1
). Thus, we adopt a novel twofold approach to develop practical DR filters:

1.

We introduce an efficient algorithm to compute the optimal positive-definite operator
\mathcal{M}_{\star}
from (
14
). To address the challanges posed by its infinite-dimensional nature, we use the frequency-domain representation of
\mathcal{M}_{\star}
as the power spectral density
M_{\star}(z)\!\succ\!0
.

2.

We develop a novel method to approximate the non-rational power spectral density
M_{\star}(z)
in
\mathit{H_{\infty}}
-norm using positive rational functions through convex optimization. This rational approximation is then used to derive an approximate rational filter with state-space realization via (
15
).

To this end, we adopt the transfer-function formalism for the rest of this paper with the correspondences:
\mathcal{M}\leftrightarrow M(z)
,
\mathcal{U}\leftrightarrow U(z)
,
\mathcal{K}\leftrightarrow K(z)
, and
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}}\leftrightarrow T_{K}(z)
for
z\in\mathbb{T}
. The following lemma characterizes the optimal

, implying finite-dimensional parametrization.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

return the unique solution of the implicit equation over

M(z)=\gamma^{2}[\gamma I\!-\!U(z)^{\mathrm{\!-\!1}}{\Gamma}(I\!-\!z\overline{A})^{\mathrm{\!-\!1}}\overline{B}\,\overline{B}^{\ast}(I\!-\!z\overline{A})^{\!-\!\ast}{\Gamma}^{\ast}U(z)^{\!-\!\ast}\!+\!T_{K_{\circ}}(z)T_{K_{\circ}}(z)^{\ast}]^{\!-\!2},\forall z\!\in\!\mathbb{T}

(17)

where
U(z)^{\ast}U(z)\!=\!M(z)
is the unique spectral factorization and
(\overline{A},\overline{B},\overline{C})
are obtained from state-space parameters (see
Section

E.2
and (
113
)). We have that
\mathcal{M}_{\star}\!=\!f(\gamma_{\star},\Gamma_{\star})
where

\Gamma_{\star}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}U_{\star}(\mathrm{e}^{{j}\omega})\overline{C}(I-\mathrm{e}^{{j}\omega}\overline{A})^{\mathrm{\!-\!1}}d\omega,

(18)

and

\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{M}_{\star}-2\sqrt{\mathcal{M}_{\star}}+\mathcal{I})=\rho^{2}

As a consequence of

The spectral density

Frank-Wolfe:
We define the following function and its (Gateaux) gradient

4.2
Rational Approximation using
\mathit{H_{\infty}}
-norm

In the preceding section, we introduced a method to compute the optimal
M_{\star}(z)
approximately on the unit circle. However, the resulting filtering policy is non-rational and cannot be realized as a state-space filter. In this section, we introduce a novel technique for obtaining approximate rational filtering policies. Instead of directly approximating the filter itself, our method involves an initial step of
approximating the power spectrum
M_{\star}(z)
by a ratio of positive fixed order polynomials,
P(z)/Q(z)
, to minimize the
\mathit{H_{\infty}}
-norm of the approximation error. After finding a rational approximation
P(z)/Q(z)
of
M_{\star}(z)
, we compute a state-space controller according to
Eq.

15
. For simplicity, we focus on scalar target signals, namely,
\mathsf{d}_{s}\!=\!1
.

Concretely,
P(z)\!=\!\sum_{k=-m}^{m}p_{k}z^{-k}
and
Q(z)\!=\!\sum_{k=-m}^{m}q_{k}z^{-k}
are Laurent polynomials of degree
m\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
with symmetric coefficients
p_{k}\!=\!p_{-k}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}
and
q_{k}\!=\!q_{-k}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}
. In other words, the polynomials
P(z)
and
Q(z)
are uniquely identified by
m+1
real coefficients,
(p_{0},\dots,p_{m})
and
(q_{0},\dots,q_{m})
. Given a positive spectral density
M(z)\!>\!0
for
z\!\in\!\mathbb{T}
, we seek positive polynomials
P(z),Q(z)\!>\!0
for
z\!\in\!\mathbb{T}
of order at most
m\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
that minimize the
\mathit{H_{\infty}}
-norm of the rational approximation error,
i.e.
,

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{0},\dots p_{m}\in\mathbb{R},\\
q_{0},\dots q_{m}\in\mathbb{R},\\
\varepsilon\geq 0\end{subarray}}\;\varepsilon\quad\,\mathrm{s.t.}\,\quad\begin{aligned} &\textit{i)}\;P(z),Q(z)>0\textrm{ for all }z\in\mathbb{T},\\
&\textit{ii)}\;q_{0}=1,\\
&\textit{iii)}\;\max_{z\in\mathbb{T}}\left|{\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}-M(z)}\right|\leq\varepsilon,\end{aligned}

(23)

where
\varepsilon\!\geq\!0
denotes an
upper bound on the approximation error
. The constraint
q_{0}\!=\!1
eliminates redundancy in the problem since the fraction
P(z)/Q(z)
is scale invariant. Unfortunately, the problem (
23
) is not convex in all the variables. Instead,
Lemma

4.5
shows convexity for fixed
\varepsilon\!\geq\!0
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For a fixed
\varepsilon\!\geq\!0
, the constraints (i-iii) define a jointly convex set for the coefficients.

Proof.

The constraints
(i-ii)
are affine inequalities, hence convex. Constraint
(iii)
is equivalent to

P(z)-(M(z)+\varepsilon)Q(z)\leq 0,\;\textrm{ and }\;P(z)-(M(z)-\varepsilon)Q(z)\geq 0,\;\textrm{ for all }z\in\mathbb{T},

(24)

which are jointly affine inequalities in
(p_{0},\dots p_{m})
and
(q_{0},\dots q_{m})
, hence convex.
∎

This result enables us to obtain
m^{\textrm{th}}
-order rational approximations
P(z)/Q(z)
of
M(z)
with a fixed approximation precision
\varepsilon
, signifying our tolerance for deviations from
M(z)
, by solving a
convex feasibility problem
. Notice that the constraints
(i)
and
(iii)
(eqv. (
24
)) involve inequalities over the entire unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. Since the iterative method in
Algorithm

1
only returns the values of
M(z)
on the discretized unit circle
\mathbb{T}_{N}
, we can enforce these inequalities in the feasibility problem only for
\mathbb{T}_{N}
. While being an inexact approximation for
(iii)
, it is an exact characterization for
(i)
as long as
N\!>\!2m
by the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem
[
97
]
. See
Section

E.8
for a pseudocode.

Utilizing a convex feasibility oracle, our method can be used in two operational modes:

1.

Fixed order, best precision:
By iteratively reducing the precision
\varepsilon
we can revise the
\varepsilon
-feasible polynomials
P(z)
,
Q(z)
, effectively solving the non-convex problem (
23
) to obtain the best
m^{\textrm{th}}
-order rational approximation.

2.

Fixed precision, least order:
In contrast, we can seek the lowest degree rational approximation, which achieves a fixed precision
\varepsilon
.

Theorem 4.6
.

The spectral factorization
U(z)^{\ast}U(z)\!=\!P(z)/Q(z)
of a degree
m
rational approximation
P(z)/Q(z)
admits a rational factor
U(z)
. Furthermore, the filter obtained from
U(z)
using (
15
), i.e.,
K(z)\!=\!K_{\mathit{H_{2}}}(z)\!+\!U(z)^{-1}\left\{{U(z)\{K_{\circ}(z)\Delta(z)\}_{-}}\right\}_{+}\Delta(z)^{\mathrm{\!-\!1}}
is rational and can be realized as a state-space filter as highlighted below:

\displaystyle\zeta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{F}\zeta_{t}+\widetilde{G}y_{t},

(25)

\displaystyle\widehat{s}_{t}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{H}\zeta_{t}+\widetilde{L}y_{t},

where
\zeta_{t}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{m\!+\!\mathsf{d}_{x}}
is the filter state, and
(\widetilde{F},\widetilde{G},\widetilde{H},\widetilde{L})
are determined from
(A,B,C_{y},C_{u})
and
U(z)
.

C.1
Proof of
Theorem

3.1

Before we proceed with the proof, we first state the following useful deifnitions and results.

Definition C.1

(Bures-Wasserstein distance
[
98
]
)
.

For any two psd matrices
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}
, the Bures-Wasserstein distance between them is defined as follows:

\mathsf{BW}(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})\triangleq\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}\left[{\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}-2\left({\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}\Sigma_{2}\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}}\right)^{1/2}}\right]}.

(27)

Definition C.2

(Gelbrich distance
[
99
]
)
.

For any two distributions
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{1},\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{2}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with means
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and covariances
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}
, respectively, the Gelbrich distance between them is defined as follows:

\mathsf{G}(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{1},\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{2})\triangleq\sqrt{\|{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}}\|_{\text{}}^{2}+\mathsf{BW}(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})^{2}}.

(28)

Lemma C.3

(Gelbrich bound
[
99
, Thm. 2.1]
)
.

Consider two distributions
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{1},\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{2}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with means
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and covariances
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}
, respectively. The
\mathsf{W_{2}}
-distance between them satisfies

\mathsf{W_{2}}(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{1},\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{2})\geq\mathsf{G}(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{1},\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{2}),

(29)

where equality is attained if both
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
and
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{2}
are Gaussian distributions.

Lemma C.4

(Causal MMSE of Gaussian
[
85
]
)
.

Suppose the disturbances are distributed as Gaussian, i.e.,
\bm{\xi}_{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{}}}{{\sim}}\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{\mu}_{T},\bm{\Sigma}_{T})
with mean
\bm{\mu}_{T}\in\Xi_{T}
and covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{T}
. Consider causal mean-square estimation of
\mathbf{s}_{T}
from
\mathbf{y}_{t}
, i.e.,

\inf_{\pi_{T}\in\Pi_{T}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\|{\mathbf{e}_{T}(\bm{\xi}_{T},\pi_{T})}\|_{\text{}}^{2}}\right].

(30)

Then, there exists a causal (block lower-diagonal) matrix
\mathcal{K}_{T}^{\star}
and a vector
\mathbf{b}_{T}^{\star}
, such that the optimal causal estimator
\pi_{T}^{\star}:\mathbf{y}_{T}\mapsto\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{T}
is affine with the following form:

\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{T}=\mathcal{K}_{T}^{\star}\mathbf{y}_{t}+\mathbf{b}_{T}^{\star}.

(31)

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
:

Clearly, we have the following weak duality,

\sup_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}\in\mathscr{W}_{T}(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}^{\circ}_{T},\rho_{T})}\inf_{\pi_{T}\in\Pi_{T}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}}\left[{\|{\mathbf{e}_{T}(\bm{\xi}_{T},\pi_{T})}\|_{\text{}}^{2}}\right]\leq\inf_{\pi_{T}\in\Pi_{T}}\sup_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}\in\mathscr{W}_{T}(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}^{\circ}_{T},\rho_{T})}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}}\left[{\|{\mathbf{e}_{T}(\bm{\xi}_{T},\pi_{T})}\|_{\text{}}^{2}}\right].

(32)

Let
\bm{\Sigma}_{T}^{\circ}\succ 0
be the covariance of the nominal distribution
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}^{\circ}
. We start by bounding the lhs of (
32
) as follows:

\displaystyle\sup_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}\in\mathscr{W}_{T}(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}^{\circ}_{T},\rho_{T})}\inf_{\pi_{T}\in\Pi_{T}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}}

\displaystyle\left[{\|{\mathbf{e}_{T}(\bm{\xi}_{T},\pi_{T})}\|_{\text{}}^{2}}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\sup_{\mathsf{G}(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T},\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}^{\circ})\leq\rho_{T}}\inf_{\pi_{T}\in\Pi_{T}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}}\left[{\|{\mathbf{e}_{T}(\bm{\xi}_{T},\pi_{T})}\|_{\text{}}^{2}}\right],

(33)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\sup_{\mathsf{G}(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T},\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}^{\circ})\leq\rho_{T}}\inf_{\mathcal{K}_{T}\in\mathscr{K}_{T}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}}\left[{\|{\mathbf{e}_{T}(\bm{\xi}_{T},\mathcal{K}_{T})}\|_{\text{}}^{2}}\right],

(34)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathsf{G}(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T},\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}^{\circ})\leq\rho_{T}}\inf_{\mathcal{K}_{T}\in\mathscr{K}_{T}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}}\left[{\bm{\xi}_{T}^{\ast}\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}}^{\ast}\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}}\bm{\xi}_{T}}\right],

(35)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}\sup_{\mathsf{G}(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T},\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}^{\circ})\leq\rho_{T}}\inf_{\mathcal{K}_{T}\in\mathscr{K}_{T}}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}}^{\ast}\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}}\left[{\bm{\xi}_{T}\bm{\xi}_{T}^{\ast}}\right]}\right),

(36)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\sup_{\mathsf{BW}(\bm{\Sigma}_{T},\bm{\Sigma}_{T}^{\circ})\leq\rho_{T}}\inf_{\mathcal{K}_{T}\in\mathscr{K}_{T}}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}}^{\ast}\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{T}}\right),

(37)

where
(a)
follows from the Gelbrich bound (
Lemma

C.3
),
(b)
follows from
\mathscr{K}_{T}\subset\Pi_{T}
,
(c)
follows from linearity of cyclic property of trace and the linearity of trace and the expectation,
(d)
follows from the definition of the Gelbrich distance (
Definition

C.2
). Note that, we can in general take the distributions involved to be zero-mean since any non-zero mean can be incorporated as an additive constant to the estimator, canceling the mean. Therefore, without loss of generality, we can restrict ourselves to zero-mean disturbances and linear estimators (instead of affine).

Following a similar reasoning, we obtain the following upper bound on the rhs of (
32
),

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi_{T}\in\Pi_{T}}\sup_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}\in\mathscr{W}_{T}(\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}^{\circ}_{T},\rho_{T})}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}}\left[{\|{\mathbf{e}_{T}(\bm{\xi}_{T},\pi_{T})}\|_{\text{}}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\mathcal{K}_{T}\in\mathscr{K}_{T}}\sup_{\mathsf{BW}(\bm{\Sigma}_{T},\bm{\Sigma}_{T}^{\circ})\leq\rho_{T}}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}}^{\ast}\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{T}}\right).

(38)

Notice that the objective in the right-hand side of (
38
) is affine in
\bm{\Sigma}_{T}
(hence concave) and quadratic in
\mathcal{K}_{T}
(hence strictly convex whenever
\bm{\Sigma}_{T}\succ 0
). Furthermore, the constraint set
\mathscr{K}_{T}
is affine, and the constraint
\mathsf{BW}(\bm{\Sigma}_{T},\bm{\Sigma}_{T}^{\circ})
is convex
[
98
]
. Therefore, we have the following minimax duality.

\inf_{\mathcal{K}_{T}\in\mathscr{K}_{T}}\sup_{\mathsf{BW}(\bm{\Sigma}_{T},\bm{\Sigma}_{T}^{\circ})\leq\rho_{T}}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}}^{\ast}\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{T}}\right)=\sup_{\mathsf{BW}(\bm{\Sigma}_{T},\bm{\Sigma}_{T}^{\circ})\leq\rho_{T}}\inf_{\mathcal{K}_{T}\in\mathscr{K}_{T}}\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}}^{\ast}\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}_{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{T}}\right).

(39)

We denote the saddle point of (
39
) by
(\mathcal{K}_{T}^{\star},\bm{\Sigma}_{T}^{\star})
. Notice that, when the nominal distribution is Gaussian
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}^{\circ}\coloneqq\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}(0,\bm{\Sigma}_{T}^{\circ})
, the Gaussian distribution
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{T}^{\star}\coloneqq\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}(0,\bm{\Sigma}_{T}^{\star})
and the causal estimator
\pi_{T}^{\star}\coloneqq\mathcal{K}_{T}^{\star}
achieve the upper bound (
37
) with equality. Thus, from (
32
) and (
38
), we obtain the desired result.
∎

E.5

To perform the spectral factorization of an irrational function

, we use a spectral factorization method via discrete Fourier transform, which returns samples of the spectral factor on the unit circle. First, we compute
\Lambda(z)
for
