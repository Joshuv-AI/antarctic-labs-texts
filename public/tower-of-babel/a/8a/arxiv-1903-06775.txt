Lambda Congruences and Extensionality
Michele Basaldella michele.basaldella@gmail.com

Abstract
In this work we provide alternative formulations of the concepts of lambda theory and extensional theory without introducing the notion of substitution and the sets of all, free and bound variables occurring in a term. We also clarify the actual role of α \alpha –renaming and η \eta –extensionality in the lambda calculus: both of them can be described as properties of extensionality for certain classes of terms.

Introduction

In the early thirties, Church
[
11
]
introduced a formal system intended to provide a common foundation for logic and mathematics.
However, after the discovery of some paradoxes,
a proper portion of the original system was extracted. This part constitutes
what is known as the
lambda calculus
today; see
Church
[
13
]
, Curry and Feys
[
19
]
, Curry, Hindley and Seldin
[
20
]
, Stenlund
[
48
]
, Stoy
[
50
]
, Barendregt
[
5
]
, Révész
[
42
]
, Krivine
[
30
]
, Hankin
[
24
]
,
Sørensen and Urzyczyn
[
47
]
, Hindley and Seldin
[
26
]
, Cardone and Hindley

[
10
]
and Seldin
[
45
]

for good expositions.

In a nutshell, the lambda calculus
can be described as
a general theory of computable functions: it provides a formalism for dealing with
functions–as–terms
in the context of the foundations of mathematics:
In axiomatic set theories, functions from a given set to itself
are defined by their graphs as — typically infinite — sets of ordered
pairs. By contrast, in the lambda calculus
functions are simply implemented by means of some —always finite — formal expressions called
terms
.
Furthermore, in set theory it follows from the axioms that a function cannot be a member of
its domain. In the lambda calculus,
every term is in the domain of a given function;
in particular, every function can take itself as input.

The most interesting feature of this calculus is that it constitutes a model of computation and for this reason this powerful language is the theoretical core of many modern functional programming languages.

To be more precise, the lambda calculus in itself has no expressive power at all. It becomes an expressive language only when equipped with a suitable relation which allows us to determine
when two terms are
intended
to be the
same
. While in set theory
two functions from a given set to itself are the same if they have identical input–output behaviour,
in the lambda calculus
the situation is rather different;
there is a huge collection of relations called

lambda theories
and each of these relation is
intended to capture
some notion of sameness.

A little more technically, lambda theories
are congruences on terms which satisfy the conditions of

\alpha
–renaming
and
\beta
–rule
.
The two most important
lambda theories are
lambda conversion
— the least lambda theory — and

extensional conversion

— the least lambda theory which
also satisfies the condition of
\eta
–extensionality
. Here we adopt
the Church’s terminology
[
13
]
; other names
for lambda conversion and extensional conversion
are
\alpha\beta
–equality and

\alpha\beta\eta
–equality, respectively.
In order to avoid misunderstanding,
in this paper
we call
\alpha
–renaming,
\beta
–rule
and
\eta
–extensionality
the conditions that in the literature are usually denoted by
(
\alpha
),
(
\beta
) and (
\eta
).
Since several
versions of (
\alpha
),
(
\beta
) and (
\eta
)
have been proposed,
for the sake of clarity we use our terminology
when we do not refer to a specific formulation.
We reserve the names (
\alpha
),
(
\beta
) and (
\eta
) to the particular conditions that will be introduced in Section
7
.

In general,
each lambda theory has to be seen
as a reasonable relation of sameness
for terms.
We refer to Barendregt
[
5
]
,
Berline

[
6
,
7
]
and Manzonetto and Salibra
[
32
]

for a survey of results on lambda theories.

In this paper we focus our attention
on the syntactical formalizations
of the notions of lambda theory and extensional theory — we call extensional theory
any lambda theory which also satisfy

\eta
–extensionality.
The usual way to proceed is to introduce
some auxiliary notions first:

•

the sets
of
all
,
free
and
bound
variables occurring in a term,

•

a meta–theoretic operation of
substitution
.

Henceforth, we refer to these
auxiliary notions as
ancillary concepts
. Note that they usually occur,
explicitly or implicitly, in the traditional formulations of the conditions of

\alpha
–renaming,
\beta
–rule
and
\eta
–extensionality.
One of the aims of this paper is to show that

in order to syntactically define lambda and extensional theories it is not necessary
to introduce any ancillary concept
.

Regarding our motivations, we mention that it is well–known that
the usual definition of substitution is
intrinsically difficult and error–prone. The same problems also arise
in logics with quantifiers
and more generally in languages with binding operators.
As for the lambda calculus,
we believe that
the actual complications do not lie in the operation of substitution itself but
rather in the formulation
of
the
\beta
–rule.
In fact, it is not so well–known that it is possible to consider
a simple definition
of substitution at the price
of having some restrictions
in the
\beta
–rule.
This is the approach of Barendregt’s
dissertation
[
4
]
,
which is actually a more elegant variant of the original approach
developed by Church
[
13
]
.
By contrast, following Curry and Feys
[
19
]
, the vast majority of the presentations of the lambda calculus prefers to
consider a complicated notion
of substitution which allows a simple formulation of the
\beta
–rule. In Section
7
we shall compare the two options in more detail.
But in either case, we believe it is definitely more
convenient to
not consider substitution at all
and replace the
\beta
–rule with the
more elementary conditions we shall introduce later on.

Historically, these complications were well–known to the fathers of modern formal logic.
In fact, approximately in same period the lambda calculus was conceived, a way to circumvent the problems caused by substitution was proposed by Shönfinkel
[
46
]
and Curry
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
under the name of
combinatory logic
.
However, we think that in such a framework the elimination of concepts is too drastic:
the ancillary concepts are eliminated by means of the rejection of
the notion of (bound) variable.
As a negative side effect, in that setting the “functional intuitions”
coming from the
lambda notation are completely lost.
This is not an accident, since this elimination of variables was one of the objectives of
Shönfinkel (see Cardone and Hindley

[
10
]
for a survey of Shönfinkel’s work).
By contrast, in this paper
we want to maintain the syntax of the lambda calculus unaltered and perform a more precise surgery
which allows us to eliminate only the ancillary concepts.

Regarding the sets
of all, free and bound variables occurring in a term, we see these concepts as mere “syntactic bureaucracy” without any real mathematical substance.
As we shall see, these sets can be eliminated from the picture with almost no
effort and hence in this way more elegant formulations of the notions of lambda theory and extensional theory can be obtained.

In addition to the theoretical and pedagogical interest of our results, we also see a more concrete advantage as we now explain. In the model theory
of the lambda calculus, in order to prove
that a given mathematical structure is a model of, say, lambda conversion, all we need to do is to check that the binary relation
obtained by relating terms which have the same interpretation
in the given structure — the relation usually called
theory of a model
— is a
lambda theory. In equivalent words, we
have to prove a
soundness theorem
.
To show this theorem, a typical approach is
to prove the
substitution lemma
, see Wadsworth
[
54
]

and Meyer
[
33
]
, as well as Stoy

[
50
, pp. 161–166]
for a detailed proof of
this lemma.
We believe that
a direct verification of the substitution–free conditions
which define (our formulation of)
lambda theories is not only easier
than the lemma,
but also more convenient inasmuch as
the proof — and the exportability — of the lemma
largely depends
on the
specific definition of substitution
. (In particular, the substitution lemma cannot
be simply invoked by authors who use a different notion of substitution. Unfortunately, we also believe that this delicate point has been overlooked by many authors.)
To convince the reader, a direct verification of some of our substitution–free conditions
will be given in Section
9
.

In order to avoid terminological
misunderstandings, in the rest of the paper
the relations of sameness on terms defined
by using
our
formalizations
are
called
lambda
and

extensional congruences
,
while we exclusively reserve the terminology
lambda
and

extensional theory
for
the
traditional
axiomatizations of these relations.
Then, one of our main results can be stated as follows:

lambda congruences
and lambda theories coincide,
as well as extensional
congruences and extensional theories
.

Another aspect we want to analyze
in this paper is the precise role
of
\alpha
–renaming and
\eta
–extensionality in the lambda calculus.
As for the latter, it is well–known
that this condition allows us to consider

terms which have
the same input–output behaviour
as the same
.
Thus, expressed in this form
\eta
–extensionality has a very intuitive and satisfactory mathematical significance: it reproduces in the lambda calculus
a form of extensionality
familiar from ordinary set theory.
As for the former, the situation is
not so clear. To the best of our knowledge, the most intuitive
informal description of this concept
is this:
terms which only differs in the names of bound variables should be considered as the same
. We think that this explanation is too syntactical — it even mentions an ancillary concept! — and we do not see
any clear mathematical content
in it. One of the aim of this paper
is to clarify the meaning of
\alpha
–renaming in intuitive and mathematically reasonable terms.
It turns out that

\alpha
–renaming admits
the following simple and satisfactory equivalent description:

abstractions which have
the same input–output behaviour
should be considered
as the same
,

as we shall show in Section

5
.
Here “abstractions” are, roughly speaking, terms which begin with
a
\lambda
.
Thus,

\alpha
–renaming
can be regarded as a form of extensionality for abstractions.
We believe that when expressed in this form the real mathematical significance
of this condition emerges.

To obtain the aforementioned results we
have to consider a new
factorization
of the conditions
which define lambda and extensional theories, in the sense we now explain. Consider, for instance,
lambda theories.
Following Church
[
13
]
, a way of introducing this concept is to put
\alpha
–renaming
and the
\beta
–rule
together in the definition of these theories, like in the original formulation of lambda conversion.
Following Barendregt
[
5
]
,
another
approach is to define an auxiliary equivalence relation on terms
called

alpha conversion
.
Terms which belongs to
the same equivalence class are
then identified. After this identification,
substitution and the
\beta
–rule are finally introduced.
We refer to Crole
[
17
]

and the references therein for more on alpha conversion.

Our factorization is completely different and, to the best of our knowledge, it seems to be new. Roughly speaking, we obtain it by proceeding as follows.
First, we only consider
our substitution–free version of the
\beta
–rule.
We obtain a family of relations that
we call
prelambda congruences
.
These relations need
to satisfy neither
\alpha
–renaming
nor
\eta
–extensionality; in fact,
we shall show
is
that there exists a prelambda congruence
in which both conditions do not hold.
Only at this stage
we add
\alpha
–renaming to prelambda congruences.
We then obtain our version of
lambda theories:
lambda congruences.
We shall prove that

\alpha
–renaming
and the property of extensionality
for abstractions mentioned above
are equivalent in every prelambda congruence
.

Also, we add

\eta
–extensionality to prelambda congruences and get our version of
extensional theories:
extensional congruences.
Similarly, we shall prove that

\eta
–extensionality
and the property of extensionality
(for all terms) mentioned above
are equivalent in every prelambda congruence
.

Notice that these equivalences are
significant precisely because
neither
\alpha
–renaming nor

\eta
–extensionality
is valid in
every prelambda congruence.

Regarding our methodology,
our analysis of
\alpha
–renaming and
\eta
–extensionality
in the lambda calculus
is somehow inspired by the usual treatment of classical logic from a constructive point of view.
In that context, intuitionistic logic
is first introduced and developed. Only at a second stage, the law of excluded middle is motivated,
discussed and added, and some equivalent formulations
are established — such as double negation elimination.
Equivalents of excluded middle
are of interest precisely because this law
is not a theorem of intuitionistic logic.
Of course,

\alpha
–renaming and
\eta
–extensionality are by no means “controversial” in the lambda calculus
as excluded middle is in constructive mathematics, but we do believe they deserve
a better understanding
as they are too often taken “for granted” in the literature, especially
\alpha
–renaming.
One of the aims of this paper is to
provide a more intuitive and clear comprehension of these conditions.
To sum up, the result of our analysis is that

in every prelambda congruence both
\alpha
–renaming and
\eta
–extensionality
can be both described in a natural way as
properties of extensionality
.

This completes the description of this paper.

We now discuss some related work.
As far we know, there are three lines of apparently
independent research which implicitly or explicitly try to eliminate the
ancillary concepts without modifying the syntax of the lambda calculus.

In the sixties, following some ideas of Church
[
12
]
, Henkin introduced
a theory of propositional types based on the simply typed lambda calculus
[
25
]
.
In order to develop a deductive
system for his theory, Henkin
decomposed the typed
\beta
–rule
in several substitution–free
clauses which are strikingly similar to the conditions that we use
in this paper. The reason for this decomposition was the simplification of the proof of the soundness theorem
of his deductive system with respect to the set–theoretic semantics of his type theory.
Again, here we see strong similarities
between his and our motivations.
Following Henkin, Andrews
employed similar clauses in his work
on type theory
[
2
,
3
]
.
However, some ancillary concepts
are present in all these works.

In the early eighties, Révész
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
proposed a
substitution–free formalization of lambda conversion.
His and our motivations are rather similar,
the main difference is that
he was mainly interested in developing concrete computer implementation
while
we also think that substitution–free formalizations are also useful to simplify some practical aspects of the model theory of the lambda calculus — namely, the elimination of the substitution lemma.
Révész’s conditions are very similar
to (untyped versions) of Henkin’s
ones and in particular
some ancillary concepts
are still present in his formalization.
From an historical perspective, the main novelty of his work
was the first substitution–free formulation of
\alpha
–renaming.

Some years later, with the aim of
providing a general algebraic setting for the
lambda calculus,
Pigozzi and Salibra
[
36
,
37
,
38
]

introduced the theory of
lambda abstraction algebras
. Despite
the aims of this line of work
are completely different from ours,
from a purely syntactical standpoint the improvement emerging from the introduction of these algebras is the complete elimination the ancillary concepts. In fact, the conditions
defining
lambda abstraction algebras are very similar to those we introduce in this paper, as we shall see.

We finally point out that
the conditions we use in this paper
to define lambda and extensional
congruences do not form just a selection of clauses taken from these works.
On the contrary, the crucial conditions (
\beta_{5}
),
(
\alpha_{e}
) and (
\eta_{e}
) that we use in this paper
are simplified — for our purposes — reworked versions of some similar clauses present in the aforementioned literature.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we shall recall the syntax of the terms of the lambda calculus and introduce some
notation and terminology.
In Section
3

we shall introduce
the concept of prelambda congruence
and in Section
4

we shall study
the basic properties of
this notion.
In Section
5

we shall discuss the real — for us — reason
why
\alpha
–renaming is
so important in the lambda calculus
and introduce the concept
of lambda congruence.
We shall also prove that in every prelambda congruence

\alpha
–renaming is equivalent
to a suitable principle of extensionality.
Similarly,
in Section
6
we
shall define the notion
of extensional congruence
and prove that
in every prelambda congruence

\eta
–extensionality is
equivalent
to a principle of extensionality.
In Section

7
we shall recall the concepts of lambda and extensional theory and in Section
8

we shall prove the equivalence between lambda congruences and theories as well as the one between
extensional congruences and theories.
In
Section

9

we shall provide a construction of a model whose theory forms
a prelambda congruence which
do not satisfy a simple instance of
\alpha
–renaming.
Finally, in Section
10

we shall conclude the paper.

2

The Lambda Calculus

In this section we recall the syntax of the terms of the lambda calculus
and introduce some terminology.

Definition 2.1

(Variables)
.

Let
\mathbb{V}
be an infinite set. We call its member
variables
and we denote them
by

\mathsf{x}
,
\mathsf{y}
,
\mathsf{z}
,….

\triangle

Some comments concerning the set
\mathbb{V}
are given at the end of this section.

The basic elements of the lambda calculus are called
terms
.
They are special words built from the infinite alphabet consisting of all
variables together with the following auxiliary symbols:

\lambda
(
lambda–abstractor
),

[
(
left lambda–bracket
)
and

]
(
right lambda–bracket
).
In this paper, we write words by juxtaposition of symbols;
in particular, we do not notationally distinguish
between members of the alphabet and unary words.

Definition 2.2

(Term)
.

The
terms
of the lambda calculus
are the elements of the set
\mathbb{T}
which is inductively defined as follows:

(T
1
)

\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{V}
implies
\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{T}
;

(T
2
)

\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{T}
and
\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{T}
imply
[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}]\in\mathbb{T}
;

(T
3
)

\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{V}
and
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{T}
imply
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\in\mathbb{T}
.

In the sequel, we use
\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{B}
,
\mathsf{C}
, …to denote arbitrary terms.

\triangle

We now introduce some useful
terminology and notation.

As is standard, two terms
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
are said to be
equal
, in symbols
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{B}
, if and only if
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
are exactly the same word. In particular, we have

\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{y}
as elements of
\mathbb{T}
if and only if
\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{y}

as elements of
\mathbb{V}
. Our notion of equality corresponds to the relation which is often called
syntactical equality
in the literature.

In order
to save space,
we always write
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}
and
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
for
[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}]

and
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]
, respectively. Henceforth, we also refer to a term of the form
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}
as an
application
and to a term of the form
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
as an

abstraction
.

As usual, we call a subset of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
(the Cartesian product of
\mathbb{T}
with itself) a
binary
relation on terms
and
we use the symbol
\ \!\sim\ \!
to denote binary relations on terms. We also
write
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
for
(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})\in\ \!\sim\ \!
and
\mathsf{A}\,\!\!\!\ \not\!\!\!\,{\sim}\ \,\mathsf{B}

for
(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})\notin\ \!\sim\ \!
.
Furthermore, if we have
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
and
\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{C}
, then sometimes we simply write
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{C}
to express this fact.
Analogously, we may write

\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
in case we have

\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
,
\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{C}

and
\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
,
and so on.

An important class of binary relations
on terms is that of
congruences
,
that we now formally define and discuss.

Definition 2.3

(Congruence, structural conditions)
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a binary relation
on terms. We say that
\ \!\sim\ \!

is a
congruence
if
it satisfies the following conditions:

(
r
)

\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
;

(
s
)

\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
implies
\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
;

(
t
)

\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
and
\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{C}
imply
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{C}
;

(
\ell
)

\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
implies
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}
;

(
a
)

\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
and
\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
imply
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}\mathsf{D}
;

where
\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{B}
,
\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{D}
are arbitrary terms, and

\mathsf{x}
is an arbitrary variable.
We collectively refer to conditions (
r
) to (
a
) above as
structural conditions
.

\triangle

Conditions (
r
), (
s
) and (
t
)
ensure that any congruence is an equivalence relation between terms, while conditions (
\ell
) and (
a
) express the fact that
any congruence is a relation compatible with the operations of term formation (T
2
) and (T
3
) of Definition
2.2
.
In particular, a congruence defined as above is a congruence
in the usual algebraic sense.
In other words, structural conditions are nothing more than a formalization of
the algebraic principle usually called
replacement of equals
by equals
. This principle — which is ubiquitous in algebra — is often used in an
implicit

and
tacit
fashion.
In this paper,
for the sake of clarity — but in contrast to the algebraic
tradition — we shall
explicitly
mention
when and where structural conditions
are used in formal proofs of our results.

While important,
congruences alone cannot be considered as candidates for a good formalization of the notion of sameness for terms: in fact, in order to develop
a
theory of functions using the lambda calculus
more conditions are needed.
This situation is similar to what happens for the traditional presentations of sequent calculi for classical logic in proof theory: there, in addition to
structural rules, we also need

logical rules
to be able to derive classical tautologies
in a syntactical way. In our context, “structural rules”
are, unsurprisingly, what we are calling structural conditions and
the “logical rules” specific to this work that we shall introduce and discuss
in detail in later sections are
(
\beta_{1}
), (
\beta_{2}
), (
\beta_{3}
), (
\beta_{4}
), (
\beta_{5}
),
(
\alpha_{e}
) and
(
\eta_{e}
).

We now give an informal presentation of our logical rules.
In the lambda calculus,
the functional intuition on terms is
this:
an abstraction of the form

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
should be thought as a function depending
on the variable
\mathsf{x}
.
If, for a moment, we think of
\mathsf{A}

as the polynomial

2x+y
, then
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}

represents the function given by

f(x)=2x+y
.
Also, an application of
the form
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}

should be intuitively regarded
as the function
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
applied to the specific input
\mathsf{D}
.
Thus, if
\mathsf{D}
represent the number 7, then

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}

represents
f(7)
. It follows from elementary rules of algebra that this expression can be simplified to

14+y
.
Now, in our formalizations of lambda
and extensional theories,
the role of conditions (
\beta_{1}
), (
\beta_{2}
), (
\beta_{3}
), (
\beta_{4}
) and (
\beta_{5}
) is in some sense algebraic:
they serve to simplify
expressions like
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}
by performing some symbolic manipulations.
The remaining conditions,
namely (
\alpha_{e}
) and
(
\eta_{e}
), have a different task;
in this work,
both of them
are seen as conditions
of
extensionality
in the sense that
they allows us to infer that two terms
(abstractions, in case of (
\alpha_{e}
))
are the same (i.e., related by
\ \!\sim\ \!
) if
they have the same input–output behaviour,
as we shall see in detail later on.

As promised, some comments on the set of variables
\mathbb{V}

are now in order.

Firstly, we make a rather trivial — but
essential — remark: we observe that the property
of being infinite is crucial.
To see this, let us call a set of variables
S

cofinite
if its complement with respect to
\mathbb{V}
is finite, in other words
if
S=\mathbb{V}\setminus F
for some
finite set of variables
F
.
Now, the reason for the infinity of
\mathbb{V}
is that
in order to develop even the most elementary part of the lambda calculus we need
the following facts to hold:

•

If
S
and
T
are cofinite, then so is

S\cap T
;

•

if
S
is cofinite and
S\subseteq T
, then
T
is cofinite;

•

every cofinite set
is non–empty.

These properties immediately follow from the definition of cofinite set
and from the infinity of
\mathbb{V}
. In the sequel, in order to make the exposition less pedantic,
we shall use these simple properties
without explicitly mentioning
them.
Our choice is perfectly in line with what it is done the literature, where we often find statements like
“let
\mathsf{z}
be a variable
which occurs neither free in
\mathsf{A}
nor bound in
\mathsf{B}
” without the
explicit proof of the existence of this variable (which simply goes as follows: First, observe that the sets of variables which occur free or bound in a term are finite, so that their complements
are cofinite.
Then, the result follows, as the intersection of cofinite sets is cofinite and cofinite sets cannot be empty).

Secondly, we note that it is fairly common in the literature of the lambda calculus
to assume that the set of variables
is
countably
infinite, see
for instance Hindley and Seldin
[
26
, Def. 1.1]
.
Of course, in order to make
the above properties of cofinite sets true,
countable infinity suffices.
In this paper we do not need assume that the set of variables is countable and the reason is plain:
we never invoke that fact in our definitions and proofs.

Finally — and most importantly —
we observe that
it is also common to assume that
the set of variables
comes equipped with an
ordering
; again, see

[
26
, Def. 1.1]
.
Here, the motivation
is ultimately related to the definition of
substitution
: if this notion
is formalized as in
[
26
]
,
then at some point of the definition we need to invoke the existence of a
choice function

f

from cofinite
sets of variables to variables
such that
f(S)\in S
for every
S
.
(Note that cofinite sets must be
non–empty for this discussion to make sense and this is indeed the case!)
We refer to Vestergaard
[
52
]
for more on this aspect,
where the
axiom of cofinite choice
is explicitly discussed.
In passing,
we also mention that choice functions
are specifically introduced
by Stoughton
[
49
]
in his approach
to
simultaneous substitution
,
see also
Copello, Szasz and Tasistro

[
15
]
.
The crucial point of the present discussion is this:
if the set of variables is appropriately ordered, then a choice function

f

naturally arises. Indeed,
for each cofinite set
S
we can simply define
f(S)
as

the first, in the given order, variable which is a member of
S
(see also Remark
7.1
).
In this paper we do not
define substitution as in
[
26
]
:
in Section
7
we follow an elegant
approach due to Barendregt

[
4
]
which allows us to define substitution, lambda theories and extensional theories without
assuming any ordering on variables.
To sum up, in the present work we do not need to impose any ordering
on the set of variables. Even more generally, we do not need to appeal
to the existence of some choice function either.

3

Prelambda Congruences

In this section, we introduce
the notion of prelambda congruence and discuss the conditions which define
this concept.
Basic properties of prelambda congruences
will be studied in the next section.

Prelambda congruences are our first step
towards our formalizations of the concepts of
lambda theory and extensional theory. Even though it is technically incorrect, it may be helpful to think
of
pre
lambda congruences
as lambda
theories which do not necessarily satisfy the condition of

\alpha
–renaming
— but, of course, they have to satisfy
the
\beta
–rule.
(In a similar manner,
pre
orders
can be seen as partial orders which do not necessarily satisfy anti–symmetry
but they do satisfy reflexivity and transitivity.)
As a matter of fact, we show that there exists a prelambda congruence
\ \!\sim\ \!
with the following property:
we have
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\!\!\ \not\!\!\!\,{\sim}\ \lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
for all variables

\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
.
Since
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
holds
in every lambda theory
\ \!\sim\ \!
,
in order to develop our formalization of lambda theory it is actually
necessary
to add

\alpha
–renaming at some stage.

Prelambda congruences which
satisfy a suitable condition of
\alpha
–renaming will be considered in Section

5
under the name of

lambda congruences
.
Similarly, in Section
6
we shall introduce
extensional congruences
, which are prelambda congruences which
satisfy an appropriate condition of
\eta
–extensionality.

There are essentially
two motivations for introducing the concept of prelambda congruence.

Firstly, the results that
we prove in the next section
on the concept of
independence of variables
do not depend
on
\alpha
–renaming
and
\eta
–extensionality.

Secondly,
the factorization
of lambda and extensional congruences respectively
as “prelambda congruences plus
\alpha
–renaming”
and “prelambda congruences plus
\eta
–extensionality”
will provides us with a mathematically reasonable understanding
of these two syntactical conditions in terms of
extensionality.

It is time for us to formally introduce
prelambda congruences.

Definition 3.1

(Prelambda congruence)
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a congruence. We say that
\ \!\sim\ \!

is a
prelambda congruence
if
it also satisfies the following conditions:

(
\beta_{1}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
;

(
\beta_{2}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{y}
, provided
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
;

(
\beta_{3}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}][[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{D}]
;

(
\beta_{4}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
;

(
\beta_{5}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
implies

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}]
,

provided
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
;

where
\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{B}
and
\mathsf{D}
are arbitrary terms, and

\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
are arbitrary variables.
We collectively refer to conditions (
\beta_{1}
) to (
\beta_{5}
) above as
beta conditions
.

\triangle

Thus, in plain words a prelambda congruence is any binary
relation on terms which simultaneously
satisfies all structural and beta conditions.

A simple example of prelambda congruence is the whole set

\mathbb{T}^{2}
. A more interesting example is given in the following definition.

Definition 3.2

(Prelambda conversion)
.

We call

prelambda conversion

the prelambda congruence
inductively defined
by structural and
beta conditions.

\triangle

In Corollary
3.4

below it is shown that

\mathbb{T}^{2}
and prelambda conversion are in fact different relations.
Hence, if we regard
prelambda conversion
as an equational theory where
equations are expressions of the form
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
, then
prelambda conversion is
consistent

in the sense of Hilbert and Post.

Let us now discuss the conditions of Definition
3.1
in some detail.

With possibly some minor differences,
conditions (
\beta_{1}
), (
\beta_{2}
),
(
\beta_{3}
) and (
\beta_{4}
)
also appear in some work by
Henkin
[
25
]
, Andrews
[
3
]
, Révész
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
, and Pigozzi and Salibra

[
36
,
37
,
38
]
.
For the sake of completeness,
we also mention that
conditions (
\beta_{1}
) and
(
\beta_{3}
)
are also considered by
Andrews

[
2
]
,
but there conditions
(
\beta_{2}
) of (
\beta_{4}
) are condensed into the following clause:

(
\gamma_{1}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
, provided
\mathsf{x}
does not occur free in
\mathsf{A}
.

Among beta conditions, the more interesting one is certainly
(
\beta_{5}
).
This condition constitutes a point of divergence in the relevant literature.
Actually, (
\beta_{5}
) as formulated in this paper seems to be
new.
Consider the following conditions:

(
\gamma_{2}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}]
,

provided
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
and
\mathsf{y}

does not occur free in
\mathsf{D}
;

(
\gamma_{3}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}]
,

provided

\mathsf{y}
is distinct from
\mathsf{x}
and all variables
occurring in
\mathsf{D}
;

(
\gamma_{4}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}]
,

provided
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
and at least one of these two
conditions hold:
\mathsf{x}
does not occur free in
\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{y}
does not occur free in
\mathsf{D}
;

(
\gamma_{5}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
implies

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}]
,

provided
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
and

\mathsf{y}\neq\mathsf{z}
;

(
\gamma_{6}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]][[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}]\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}][[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}]\mathsf{z}]]
,

provided
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
and

\mathsf{y}\neq\mathsf{z}
.

Again, up to minor details,
(
\gamma_{2}
) is the version of (
\beta_{5}
) considered by Henkin
[
25
, Axiom 7.5 p. 331]
and Andrews

[
2
,
4_{4}
p. 3]
,
(
\gamma_{3}
)
is another version considered
by Andrews
[
3
,
4_{4}
p. 164]
and finally
(
\gamma_{4}
) is the one taken into account by Révész
[
42
,
\beta 4
p. 29]
. As we can see,
all these conditions explicitly mention
some ancillary concept.

The condition which is most
similar to our (
\beta_{5}
) is the one given by Pigozzi
and Salibra
in the context of lambda abstraction algebras (
\gamma_{5}
)
[
37
,
\beta_{6}
p. 12]
;
in fact, our condition (
\beta_{5}
) is actually
a simplification of (
\gamma_{5}
) —
in the sense that
our condition requires
two variables and one inequality to be expressed,
while (
\gamma_{5}
)
needs three variables and two inequalities.
Our (
\gamma_{6}
) is a shortened
version of (
\gamma_{5}
)
due to Pigozzi
and Salibra
[
37
, Prop. 1.5]
.
Conditions
(
\gamma_{5}
) and (
\gamma_{6}
)
are equivalent in every lambda abstraction algebra.
Finally, it is worth mentioning that Pigozzi and Salibra also consider another “beta condition” in their setting, namely

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
(see
[
37
,
\beta_{3}
p. 12]
).
But for our purposes, we do not need
to consider this condition at all.

As we have just seen,
(suitable versions of)
beta conditions have
already been considered in the literature.
They perhaps seem a bit complicated at first,
but a
closer look reveals that they are in fact
quite natural and even easy to memorize.
To see this, notice that
they are all of the form

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{M}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{G}
.
Now, if we replace
each term of the form

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{M}]\mathsf{D}
by an expression of the form
\llbracket\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rrbracket(\mathsf{M})

and write
=
instead of
\ \!\sim\ \!
, then by a slight
rearrangement
we obtain

(
\beta_{1}^{s}
)

\llbracket\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rrbracket(\mathsf{x})\,=\,\mathsf{D}
;

(
\beta_{2}^{s}
)

\llbracket\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rrbracket(\mathsf{y})\,=\,\mathsf{y}
, if
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
;

(
\beta_{3}^{s}
)

\llbracket\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rrbracket(\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B})\,=\,\llbracket\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rrbracket(\mathsf{A})\llbracket\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rrbracket(\mathsf{B})
;

(
\beta_{4}^{s}
)

\llbracket\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rrbracket(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A})\,=\,\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
;

(
\beta_{5}^{s}
)

\llbracket\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rrbracket(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A})\,=\,\lambda\mathsf{y}\llbracket\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rrbracket(\mathsf{A})
,

if
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
(provided

\llbracket\mathsf{x}/\mathsf{y}\rrbracket(\mathsf{D})\,=\,\mathsf{D}
).

Conditions (
\beta_{1}^{s}
), (
\beta_{2}^{s}
),
(
\beta_{3}^{s}
), (
\beta_{4}^{s}
) and (
\beta_{5}^{s}
) above
really look like a kind of inductive
definition
of substitution
which is not too dissimilar to the ones we can find in the literature (cf. Definition
7.2
).
In passing, it is worth observing that
these clauses are very
similar to some of the conditions which axiomatize
lambda substitution algebras
,
algebraic structures
introduced by Diskin and Beylin
[
21
]
with the purposes of
algebraizing the lambda calculus — in fact, these algebras share several similarities with lambda abstraction algebras.

At this point, we hope that we have almost convinced the reader that
beta conditions are quite natural
and intuitive. However, if there is
some reader which
still feels uncomfortable with
the precondition
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
in condition (
\beta_{5}
) (that is to say, the proviso

\llbracket\mathsf{x}/\mathsf{y}\rrbracket(\mathsf{D})\,=\,\mathsf{D}
in (
\beta_{5}^{s}
) above), then we would like to reassure the reader by
saying that the whole next section
is dedicated to the study
of the “consequences” of
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
.

We now show that
\alpha
–renaming is not available
in every prelambda congruence.
For, let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be two distinct variables, and let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be an arbitrary prelambda congruence.
If
\ \!\sim\ \!
were a lambda theory,
we would certainly have

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}

by using
\alpha
–renaming.
However, here nothing ensures
that we have

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
. In fact,
the following theorem
shows that
any attempt of
deriving
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
by means of our structural and beta conditions is doomed to failure.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be two variables
such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
. Then,
there exists a prelambda congruence

\,\!\sim_{I}\,\!

such that
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\not\,\!\sim_{I}\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
. ∎

The proof of Theorem
3.3

is quite long and technical.
Furthermore, in order to produce it
we are forced to introduce
further notions and methods that we do not
need in the rest of this paper.
For these reasons, it is postponed to Section
9
.
Nevertheless, the interested
reader can directly go there without any
hesitation, as no further material
is needed to read that part of this work.

As a consequence
of the previous result, a very simple
proof of the fact
that
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and prelambda conversion are distinct relations is at our disposal.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be prelambda conversion,
and let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be two distinct variables.
Then, we have
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\!\!\ \not\!\!\!\,{\sim}\ \lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
.
In particular,

\ \!\sim\ \!
is consistent.

Proof.

Since
\ \!\sim\ \!

is the inductively defined prelambda congruence, it is the intersection of all prelambda congruences. In particular,
we have
\ \!\sim\ \!\,\subseteq\,\,\!\sim_{I}\,\!
.
From this and Theorem
3.3
it follows that
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\!\ \not\!\!\!\,{\sim}\ \lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
.
In particular, we have
\sim\mathop{\neq}\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
∎

4

Basic Properties of Prelambda Congruences

In the previous section, we did not
discussed
the significance of the precondition

[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}

in
condition (
\beta_{5}
). In this section, we aim to fill this gap.
Since this requirement turns
out to be very important in our setting, it is convenient
to introduce some terminology
to describe
this situation.

Definition 4.1

(Independence of variables)
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence and let
\mathsf{A}
be a term. We define the set
of variables
\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
as follows:

\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\ \,|\ \,[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
for some variable
\mathsf{z}

such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}\,\!\}
.

Let
\mathsf{x}
be a variable. We say that
\mathsf{A}

is independent of
\mathsf{x}
in
\ \!\sim\ \!
if
\mathsf{x}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
.

\triangle

Thus, since in (
\beta_{5}
) we have
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, in the new terminology the
precondition
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
means that
\mathsf{D}
is independent
of
\mathsf{y}
in
\ \!\sim\ \!
.
There is a clear analogy with
the proviso “
\mathsf{y}

does not occur free in
\mathsf{D}
”
of Henkin’s version of
(
\beta_{5}
) — the condition we called
(
\gamma_{2}
) in the previous section.
A precise relationship between
independent variables
and free variables is given
in Theorem
4.8
.

The notion of independence of variables in the lambda calculus has been introduced
and studied by Pigozzi and Salibra in the context of lambda abstraction algebras
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
.
Even though prelambda congruences are not lambda abstraction algebras,
facts which look quite similar to “sharpened versions”
of Proposition
4.2

and
Lemma
4.3

below have been already established, see

[
37
, Lem 1.6 and Prop. 1.7]
.
With a little effort,
we could generalize our facts in the same
vein, but we find more instructive
to present them in the actual form because
we do not need anything more elaborated to
prove
our main results.

Technically speaking, the peculiar use of
the concept of independence of variables we make in this paper
allows us to avoid the introduction
of the sets of all, free and bound variables occurring in a term
in the definitions of our notions
of prelambda, lambda and extensional congruences.

To begin the study of the concept of independence of variables in prelambda congruences,
we show that a property similar to the condition called (
\gamma_{1}
) that we discussed in the previous section also holds in our setting.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence. Let
\mathsf{x}
be a variable and let
\mathsf{A}
be a term. Suppose that
\mathsf{x}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
.
Then, we have
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
for every term
\mathsf{D}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{D}
be a term.
Since
\mathsf{x}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
, there exists a variable
\mathsf{z}
such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
and

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
.
Let

\mathsf{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}[\lambda\mathsf{x}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}]]\mathsf{D}
,
\mathsf{E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}
and
\mathsf{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{z}]\mathsf{D}
.
Since
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
, we obtain
\lambda\mathsf{x}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
by
(\ell)
.
We have
\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
by (
r
), and we get
\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}
by (
a
). Now,
by (
\beta_{3}
) we deduce
\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{E}\mathsf{F}

and
we have

\mathsf{E}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
by (
\beta_{4}
).
As
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
, we obtain
\mathsf{F}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{z}
by (
\beta_{2}
) and it follows from (
a
) that

\mathsf{E}\mathsf{F}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}
.
By using (
s
)
we have
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{E}\mathsf{F}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}

and we finally get
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
from (
t
).
∎

In regard to the syntactical category a term belongs to,
the following lemma
gives a partial description of sets
of independent variables.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence.
Let
\mathsf{x}
be a variable,
and let
\mathsf{B}
and
\mathsf{C}
be terms.
Then, the following statements
hold:

(i)

\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\subseteq\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{x})}
;

(ii)

\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}\cap\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{C})}\subseteq\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C})}
;

(iii)

\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}\cup\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\subseteq\mathord{\sim\!\!(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B})}
(in particular,

\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}\subseteq\mathord{\sim\!\!(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B})}
).

Proof.

(i)
Let
\mathsf{y}\in\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}
.
Since
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, we have
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{x}
by (
\beta_{2}
). Hence, we obtain
\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{x})}
.

(ii)
Let
\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}\cap\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{C})}
and let
\mathsf{z}
be a variable such that
\mathsf{y}\neq\mathsf{z}
.
By Proposition
4.2

it follows that
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}

and
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{C}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{C}
,
and we obtain
[[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}][[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{C}]\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}
by (
a
).
From (
\beta_{3}
), we have

[\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}]]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \![[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}][[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{C}]\mathsf{z}]
and we get

[\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}]]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}
from (
t
).
Therefore, we conclude that
\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C})}
.

(iii) Let
\mathsf{z}
be a variable such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
. Then, we have
[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}
by (
\beta_{4}
) and hence it follows that
\mathsf{x}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A})}
.
Now, let
\mathsf{y}\in\,\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}
and let
\mathsf{z}
be a variable such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
and
\mathsf{y}\neq\mathsf{z}
.
By Proposition
4.2
, we get

[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
.
Since
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
and
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
, we have

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{z}]\mathsf{y}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{z}
by (
\beta_{2}
) and hence

[\lambda\mathsf{y}[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}[[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}]
by (
\beta_{5}
).
Since
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
, we also have

\lambda\mathsf{x}[[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}
from (
\ell
). Finally,
we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{y}[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}

from (
t
). As
\mathsf{y}\neq\mathsf{z}
,
we conclude that
\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B})}
.
∎

Our next step is
to relate the notion of independence
of variables in prelambda congruences to the usual concept of
free variable
.
Up to now, we dealt with them informally
and appealed to the reader’s previous
knowledge of this concept.
Since at this point we need to prove some formal facts
about free variables, we now recall the formal
definition. Nevertheless, for the sake of better readability,
in informal discussions below
we shall often express the concept
of free variable in words, rather
than
employing the symbolism
of Definition
4.4
.

As
already noticed by Welch
[
55
, Rem. 0.3.4]
, for the development of the lambda calculus
it is actually more clear and convenient
to formalize the concept of
variable which does
not

occur free in a given term.
Here, we follow the same idea
because this approach makes the connection between non–free and independent variables tighter.
Non–free
variables are sometimes called
fresh
variables in the literature,
for instance in Copello, Szasz and Tasistro

[
15
]
.

Definition 4.4

(Non–free variable)
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
be a term.
We define the cofinite set of variables
\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})

by induction on the structure of
\mathsf{A}
as follows:

•

\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\mathsf{x}\}
;

•

\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})\cap\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{C})
;

•

\reflectbox{{F}}(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})\cup\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}
.

Finally, we say that a variable
\mathsf{x}
does not occur free in
\mathsf{A}
if
\mathsf{x}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
.

\triangle

The next theorem is fundamental for the main results of this paper.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence and let

\mathsf{A}
be a term.
Then, we have
\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})\subseteq\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the structure
of
\mathsf{A}
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{x}
.
By Lemma
4.3
(i), we have
\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{x})=\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\subseteq\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{x})}
.

Suppose that

\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}
.
By inductive hypothesis, we have
\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})\subseteq\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}
and
\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{C})\subseteq\ \mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{C})}
.
Thus, we obtain
\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C})=\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})\cap\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{C})\subseteq\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}\cap\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{C})}\subseteq\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C})}
by using Lemma

4.3
(ii).

Finally, suppose that

\mathsf{A}=\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}
.
By inductive hypothesis, we have
\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})\subseteq\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}
.
By Lemma
4.3
(iii),
it follows that
\reflectbox{{F}}(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B})=\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})\cup\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\subseteq\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}\cup\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\subseteq\mathord{\sim\!\!(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B})}
.
∎

An immediate consequence of Theorem

4.5
is that
in every prelambda congruence
\ \!\sim\ \!
all sets of variables of the form
\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
are cofinite.
A more remarkable consequence is that
condition (
\gamma_{2}
), discussed in the previous section, always holds in our setting as we show in the second part of the following proposition.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence.
Let
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{D}
be terms.
Let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be variables
such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
.
Then, the following statements hold:

(i)

Suppose that

\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{D})}
. Then, we
have
[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}]
.

(ii)

Suppose that

\mathsf{y}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})
. Then, we
have
[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}]
.

Proof.

(i)
We have

[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
by Proposition
4.2
. As
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}]
by (
\beta_{5}
).

(ii) It follows from (i) above, as we have

\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{D})}
by Theorem
4.5
.
∎

We now further analyze the relationship
between independent and non–free variables, though
the facts we are now going to establish are not strictly needed to prove our main results.
To begin with, we show that
independence and non–freedom are not equivalent notions.
In the following example
we show that the converse of
Theorem
4.5

does not hold.

Example 4.7
.

Let
us consider the case
\sim\mathop{=}\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
Since all terms are related,
we have
\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}=\mathbb{V}

for every term
\mathsf{A}
. However,
it is not true that

\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})=\mathbb{V}
for every term
\mathsf{A}
; for instance, we have
\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{x})=\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}
for every variable
\mathsf{x}
.

\triangle

The previous example
does not exclude the existence of a prelambda congruence
\ \!\sim\ \!
in which it is possible to have

\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}=\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
for every term
\mathsf{A}
. However,
we can show
that such a
\ \!\sim\ \!
cannot exist.
Actually, we prove an even stronger version of this fact in Corollary
4.9
below, which is a consequence of the theorem
that we now show.

Theorem 4.8

(Independence and non–free variables)
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence and let

\mathsf{A}
be a term. We have

\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}=\{\,\!\mathsf{v}\ \,|\ \,\mathsf{v}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})

for some term
\mathsf{B}

such that

\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}\,\!\}
.

Proof.

Let
S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\{\,\!\mathsf{v}\ \,|\ \,\mathsf{v}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})
for some term
\mathsf{B}
such that
\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}\,\!\}
and let
\mathsf{x}
be a variable.

Suppose that
\mathsf{x}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
.
Then,
there exists a variable
\mathsf{z}
such that

\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
and
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
. Let
\mathsf{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}
.
Since
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
, we have

\mathsf{x}\in(\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})\cup\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\})\cap(\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{z}\,\!\})=\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})
. As
\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
,
we conclude that

\mathsf{x}\in S
.

Suppose now that

\mathsf{x}\in S
.
Let
\mathsf{B}
be a term such that

\mathsf{x}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})
and
\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
. Since
\mathsf{x}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})
, we have

\mathsf{x}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}
by Theorem
4.5
.
In particular,
there exists a variable
\mathsf{z}
such that

\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
and
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
.
Now, since
\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
, we have
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
by (
\ell
). From (
r
) it follows
that
\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{z}
. So, we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}

by (
a
).
By using (
s
),
we have
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}

and we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
from (
t
).
As
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
, we conclude that
\mathsf{x}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

There exists a term
\mathsf{A}
such that in every prelambda congruence
\ \!\sim\ \!
we have

\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}\nsubseteq\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be variables
such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, and
let
\mathsf{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{x}
.
Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence.
Since
\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})=\reflectbox{{F}}(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y})\cap\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{x})=\reflectbox{{F}}(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y})\cap\big(\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\mathsf{x}\}\big)
, we have
\mathsf{x}\notin\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
.
Since
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, we have

\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{y}
from (
\beta_{2}
) and
\mathsf{y}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
from (
s
). Also, we have
\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{y}\,\!\}=\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{y})
and so
it follows that
\mathsf{x}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})
for some term
\mathsf{B}
such that
\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
. Thus, we obtain
\mathsf{x}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
by means of Theorem
4.8
.

Now,
if
\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}\subseteq\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})

were true, then
we would get
\mathsf{x}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
but this
would contradict

\mathsf{x}\notin\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
. Therefore, we have

\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}\nsubseteq\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
.
∎

5

Lambda Congruences

We now turn our attention to the intuitive
interpretation of terms as
functions in prelambda congruences.
Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be an arbitrary prelambda congruence.

In Section

2

we observed
that a term
of the form

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}

should be thought as a function
whose domain is the whole set of terms
\mathbb{T}
(including
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
itself).
Under this perspective,
when the function
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
is
applied to an input
\mathsf{D}
,

any
term
\mathsf{B}
such that

\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}
should be thought as
the
output
of
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
on input
\mathsf{D}
.
This view is justified by the fact that
the terms
\mathsf{B}
and
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}
are meant to be same
in
\ \!\sim\ \!

when
\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}

holds, so that
syntactically different terms related to

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}
are just different
representations

of the same output.
Depending on the practical aims, better and more informative representations of the output can be computed using structural and beta conditions.
Clearly, different prelambda congruences can produce different
outcomes; for instance, if
\sim\mathop{=}\mathbb{T}^{2}

then any term is the output
of any function on any input.

Notice that we do
not
require that
every
term has to be thought
as a function. So, variables
and applications can be simply thought
as
constituents
for forming functions —
of course, they can be inputs and outputs of functions.
(In a similar fashion, in set theory
with
urelements
it is not required urelements to be sets,
but they can be used to build sets.)
In Section
6
the possibility
of regarding every term as a function will be investigated.

So far so good. However, if we seriously want to develop a
reasonable theory of functions inside the lambda calculus we should expect the following property of
extensionality for functions
to be true:
two functions should be considered as the same if they have identical input–output behaviour.
This property clearly holds
in set theory: given a set
Z

and two functions

f
and
g
from
Z
to itself
we always have

f(d)=g(d)
for every
d\in Z
implies
f=g
.

Thus, if we want to think
abstractions
as functions
then
we should expect the following property of
extensionality for abstractions
to hold:

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{D}
for every term
\mathsf{D}
implies
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}
.

Quite suggestively, we observe that our formulation of extensionality for abstractions strikingly resembles the
axiom of extensionality
of von Neumann’s set theory based on functions

[
53
, p. 397 and Axiom I4 p. 399]
.

A serious problem of prelambda congruences is that
it is not always the
case that
extensionality for abstractions holds.
For instance, if
\ \!\sim\ \!
is prelambda conversion (see Definition
3.2
), then for distinct variables
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}

and any term
\mathsf{D}

we have

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
and
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}

by (
\beta_{1}
).
So, by using (
s
) we obtain
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{D}
and by (
t
)
we get

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{D}
which shows that
the two abstractions

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}
and
\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
have the same input–output behaviour. But

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
does not hold, by
Corollary
3.4
.
Hence, in the section
we focus our attention on prelambda congruences which satisfy extensionality of abstraction.

Nevertheless, we do not regard prelambda congruences that do not satisfy such a form of
extensionality — such as prelambda conversion — as uninteresting.
We strongly believe that these congruences may serve as a foundation for the development
of a truly
intensional
(as opposed to extensional)
theory of
algorithms
…but this is another story.

Another intriguing motivation for considering extensionality for abstractions
is the fact that this condition
has been present in the lambda calculus from the very beginning — though under a completely different guise.
In fact, in every prelambda congruence extensionality
for abstractions and
\alpha
–renaming are equivalent conditions,
as we show in Theorem
5.5
.

We believe that the usual
informal explanations of
\alpha
–renaming, as
“the names of bound variables
are immaterial” and the like,
provide neither a good justification
of what is really going on nor
a good motivation for accepting this condition as natural.
Moreover, in the literature we are not aware of any line of work which attempts to clarify the real significance
of this condition.
In this paper, we can give a
mathematically
significant answer:

\alpha
–renaming is just a compact reformulation of the
property of extensionality for abstractions.

We now formally introduce the concept of
lambda congruence
:
prelambda congruences which satisfy our condition
of
\alpha
–renaming (
\alpha_{e}
).

Definition 5.1

(Lambda congruence
)
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence. We say that
\ \!\sim\ \!
is a
lambda congruence

if it also satisfies the following condition:

(
\alpha_{e}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
implies

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{y}]
;

where
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
are arbitrary variables and
\mathsf{A}
is an arbitrary term.

\triangle

In other words, a lambda congruence is any binary
relation on terms which simultaneously
satisfies all structural and beta conditions together with condition (
\alpha_{e}
).
In particular, every
lambda congruence is also a prelambda congruence and so the general results
on independence of variables we proved in the previous section
can be applied to lambda congruences as well.

Since our formulation of
\alpha
–renaming as
(
\alpha_{e}
) seems to be
new,
we now discuss our condition in relation to other clauses
of
\alpha
–renaming
proposed in the relevant literature,
as we did in Section
3

for (
\beta_{5}
). Other conditions of
\alpha
–renaming which are perhaps more standard are considered by Crole
[
17
, Sec. 3]
; however, they
do not fit the aims of the present work because their formulations require either substitution or new operations such as atom swapping.
Consider the following conditions:

(
\delta_{1}
)

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{y}]
, provided
\mathsf{y}

does not occur free in
\mathsf{A}
;

(
\delta_{2}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
implies

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{y}]
, provided

\mathsf{y}\neq\mathsf{z}
;

(
\delta_{3}
)

\lambda\mathsf{x}[[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}[[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}]]\mathsf{y}]
, provided

\mathsf{y}\neq\mathsf{z}
.

Up to minor differences, (
\delta_{1}
)
is the condition of
\alpha
–renaming considered by Révész
[
42
,
\alpha
p. 29]
,
while conditions (
\delta_{2}
)
and (
\delta_{3}
)
have been introduced
by Pigozzi and Salibra
in the theory of lambda abstraction
algebras
[
37
,
\alpha
p. 12
and Prop. 1.5]
.
In their setting, (
\delta_{2}
)
and (
\delta_{3}
) are equivalent.
Our condition (
\alpha_{e}
)
is actually
a simplified version of (
\delta_{2}
)
inasmuch as (
\alpha_{e}
) requires
two variables to be expressed,
while (
\delta_{2}
) three variables
and one inequality.

In the type theories developed by
Henkin
[
25
]
and Andrews

[
2
,
3
]
, the situation is rather different; in their setting
it is present
a condition which is similar to our extensionality for abstractions.
Roughly speaking, they consider
a condition of extensionality for
terms which have the same “arrow type”; this condition
in turn implies

\alpha
–renaming for some classes of terms, see for instance
[
25
, Axiom Schema 6 p. 330 and Thm. 7.21]
. A crucial difference
is that their condition of extensionality can be simply expressed as
a term of their language.
By contrast, in our setting extensionality for abstractions is
a first–order sentence of
the meta–language.

Regarding our formulation of
\alpha
–renaming we also observe
the following fact.

Remark 5.2
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence.
Let
\mathsf{A}
be a term and let
\mathsf{x}
be a variable.
Suppose that
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
.
Then,
from (
\ell
) we have

\lambda\mathsf{x}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{x}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
and from (
s
)
we obtain

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{x}]
.
This show that condition (
\alpha_{e}
)
is already present in any prelambda congruence in the special case

\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{y}
.
Therefore, the real content of (
\alpha_{e}
) is actually

(
\delta_{4}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
implies

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{y}]
, provided
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
.

However, we prefer to express
our condition of
\alpha
–renaming
by means of (
\alpha_{e}
) rather than (
\delta_{4}
) because the former admits
a
shorter formulation.

\triangle

In order to improve its intuitive understanding, we may rewrite, in the notation employed in Section
3
, condition (
\alpha_{e}
) in the following form:

(
\alpha_{e}^{s}
)

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\,=\,\lambda\mathsf{y}\llbracket\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rrbracket(\mathsf{A})
(provided

\llbracket\mathsf{x}/\mathsf{y}\rrbracket(\mathsf{A})\,=\,\mathsf{A}
).

In this shape, it appears to be very similar to the usual conditions of
\alpha
–renaming
which one finds in the literature.

Contrarily to our treatment of prelambda congruences, it is not worth giving examples of specific lambda congruences.
The reason is that
in Section
8
we shall show
that lambda congruences and
lambda theories are actually the same concept, and in the literature of the lambda calculus examples
of lambda theories abound.
But it is worth giving
an example of a prelambda
congruence which is not a lambda congruence.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a lambda congruence. Let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be two variables such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
. Then, we have
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
. In particular, prelambda conversion is not a lambda congruence.

Proof.

We have
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{x}

by (
\beta_{2}
).
Thus, we obtain
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{y}]

by (
\alpha_{e}
).
By (
\beta_{1}
) it follows that

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{y}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{y}
and thus
we get
\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{y}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
from (
\ell
).
Hence, we obtain
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
by (
t
).
In particular, by Corollary
3.4
prelambda conversion cannot be a lambda congruence.
∎

We now show, in the second part of the following proposition, that condition (
\delta_{1}
) above holds in every lambda congruence.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a lambda congruence.
Let
\mathsf{A}
be a term, and
let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be variables.
Then, the following statements hold:

(i)

Suppose that

\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
. Then, we
have
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{y}]
.

(ii)

Suppose that

\mathsf{y}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
. Then, we
have
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{y}]
.

Proof.

(i)
We have

[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
by Proposition
4.2
and we obtain

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{y}]
from (
\alpha_{e}
).

(ii) It follows from (i) above, as we have

\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
by Theorem
4.5
.
∎

Our next step is to show
that in every prelambda congruence
conditions (
\alpha_{e}
)
and extensionality for abstractions
are equivalent.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence.
Then,
\ \!\sim\ \!
is a lambda congruence if and only if it satisfies the condition
of extensionality for abstractions:

(
e_{a}
)

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{D}

for every term
\mathsf{D}
implies
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}
,

for all terms
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
,
and all variables
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\ \!\sim\ \!
is a lambda congruence. Let
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}

be terms,
and let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be variables.
Let
\mathsf{E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}

and
\mathsf{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}
.
Assume
\mathsf{E}\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{F}\mathsf{D}
for every term
\mathsf{D}
.
We now show that
\mathsf{E}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{F}
.
Let
\mathsf{z}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}\cap\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}
. Then, we have
\mathsf{E}\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{F}\mathsf{z}
and
we get

\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{E}\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{F}\mathsf{z}]

from (
\ell
).
As
\mathsf{z}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}

and
\mathsf{z}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}

we obtain

\mathsf{E}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{E}\mathsf{z}]
and
\mathsf{F}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{F}\mathsf{z}]

by Proposition
5.4
(i).
By using (
s
), we have

\mathsf{E}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{E}\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{F}\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{F}
.
By (
t
), we obtain

\mathsf{E}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{F}
.

Suppose
now that

\ \!\sim\ \!
is a prelambda congruence which satisfies (
e_{a}
).
Let
\mathsf{A}
be a term, and let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be variables.
Let
\mathsf{E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}

and

\mathsf{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{E}\mathsf{y}]
.
Assume
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
. We now prove that
\mathsf{E}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{F}
.
If
\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{y}
, then we can derive

\mathsf{E}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{F}
as in Remark
5.2
.
If
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, then
let

\mathsf{z}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{E})}\cap\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{F})}\cap(\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\})
.
Let
\mathsf{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{E}]\mathsf{z}
and
\mathsf{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{z}
.
We have
\mathsf{F}\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{M}\mathsf{N}

by (
\beta_{3}
).
As

\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
, it follows that
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{z}]\mathsf{y}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{z}
by (
\beta_{2}
).
Hence, we get

\mathsf{M}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}[[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}]

by (
\beta_{5}
).
Since
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, we have
\mathsf{y}\in\,\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})
from the assumption
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
. Hence, by Proposition
4.2
we obtain
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}

and
we get
\lambda\mathsf{x}[[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{E}
from (
\ell
).
Thus, we have

\mathsf{M}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{E}
from (
t
).
Now, by (
\beta_{1}
)
we obtain
\mathsf{N}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{z}

and
we get

\mathsf{M}\mathsf{N}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{E}\mathsf{z}

from (
a
). By (
t
), we obtain

\mathsf{F}\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{E}\mathsf{z}
and so

\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{E}\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{F}\mathsf{z}]
from (
s
) and (
\ell
).
Let
\mathsf{D}
be an arbitrary term.
From (
r
) we have
\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}

and by (
a
) we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{E}\mathsf{z}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{F}\mathsf{z}]]\mathsf{D}
.
Let
\mathsf{G}\in\{\,\!\mathsf{E},\mathsf{F}\,\!\}
.
We have
[\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{G}\mathsf{z}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![[\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{G}]\mathsf{D}]][[\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{z}]\mathsf{D}]
by (
\beta_{3}
).
Since
\mathsf{z}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{G})}
,
we have
[\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{G}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{G}

by Proposition
4.2
. By (
\beta_{1}
)
we obtain
[\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{z}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
.
Thus, by (
a
)
it follows that
[[\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{G}]\mathsf{D}]][[\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{z}]\mathsf{D}]\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{G}\mathsf{D}

and by (
t
) we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{G}\mathsf{z}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{G}\mathsf{D}
.
This shows that we have

[\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{E}\mathsf{z}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{E}\mathsf{D}

and
[\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{F}\mathsf{z}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{F}\mathsf{D}
.
By using (
s
)
we obtain
\mathsf{E}\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{E}\mathsf{z}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{F}\mathsf{z}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{F}\mathsf{D}

and
\mathsf{E}\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{F}\mathsf{D}
from (
t
).
Since the term
\mathsf{D}
is arbitrary, we have

\mathsf{E}\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{F}\mathsf{D}
for every term
\mathsf{D}
.
As
\mathsf{E}
and
\mathsf{F}
are both abstractions, we obtain
\mathsf{E}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{F}
by using (
e_{a}
).
∎

Thus, by the previous theorem
our formulation of
\alpha
–renaming (
\alpha_{e}
) and extensionality for abstractions
(
e_{a}
) are equivalent.
This equivalence is
significant precisely because, by Proposition
5.3
,
(
\alpha_{e}
) does not hold in every prelambda congruence.
We decided to axiomatize
lambda congruences using (
\alpha_{e}
) because this condition is more compact
and also because this approach is more akin to traditional
presentations of lambda theories.
But for us, the actual meaning
of
\alpha
–renaming is precisely
the property of
extensionality for abstractions.

6

Extensional Congruences

In the previous section, we followed the idea
that only abstractions should
be thought
as functions.
In section we expand our vision and explore the
possibility of thinking every term
as a function.

In order to implement this idea, the
most natural way is to consider
lambda congruences
\ \!\sim\ \!
where

each term
is related to an abstraction
. The typical condition of

\eta
–extensionality — that we shall discuss in the next section —

\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{y}]
,  provided
\mathsf{y}
does not occur free in
\mathsf{A}

clearly does the required job. In our setting, to achieve
the same result without introducing any ancillary concept, it suffices
to consider

prelambda
congruences
which satisfy a simpler condition
of
\eta
–extensionality, our condition (
\eta_{e}
).

This lead us to the
formalization of the concept
of
extensional congruence
.

Definition 6.1

(Extensional congruence)
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence. We say that
\ \!\sim\ \!

is an
extensional congruence
if

\ \!\sim\ \!
also satisfies the following condition:

(
\eta_{e}
)

\mathsf{y}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]
, provided
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
;

where

\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
are arbitrary variables.

\triangle

Thus, an extensional congruence is any binary
relation on terms which simultaneously
satisfies all structural and beta conditions together with our condition of
\eta
–extensionality (
\eta_{e}
).
Of course, since every
extensional congruence is also a prelambda congruence, the general facts
on independence of variables we observed in Section
4

can be applied to extensional congruences as well.

Regarding our formalization,
our exact formulation of condition (
\eta_{e}
)
seems to be new, even though
we recognize a strong similarity
with a condition introduced by Hindley and Longo for studying models of the lambda calculus
[
27
, Eq. 7 Lem. 4.2]
.
Their condition is, essentially,
one instance

of our condition (
\eta_{e}
).
The main difference is that in their setting

\alpha
–renaming is available
;
so it is possible to use
\alpha
–renaming to generate more instances.
On the contrary,
here we are working with arbitrary
prelambda congruences
and we are somehow forced to consider
every combination of variables in our (
\eta_{e}
). But the actual power of
(
\eta_{e}
) is quite remarkable: it allows us to derive our condition of
\alpha
–renaming (
\alpha_{e}
) in every extensional congruence, as we show in Theorem
6.3
.

We now survey some conditions of
\eta
–extensionality
considered in the literature
which do not mention any ancillary concept:

(
\epsilon_{1}
)

\lambda\mathsf{x}[[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{x}]\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{y}
, provided
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
;

(
\epsilon_{2}
)

\mathsf{y}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
for some term
\mathsf{A}
, provided

\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
;

(
\epsilon_{3}
)

\mathbf{i}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathbf{1}
.

Up to inessential differences, conditions (
\epsilon_{1}
) and (
\epsilon_{2}
) are due to Salibra;
the former is specifically employed in
the axiomatization of the concept of extensional lambda abstraction algebra, while the latter
is provably equivalent to (
\epsilon_{1}
)
[
44
, Def. 57 and Prop. 58]
.
Our condition (
\eta_{e}
)
clearly resembles (
\epsilon_{2}
).

Condition (
\epsilon_{3}
) is often employed in
model theoretical studies of the lambda calculus, again see

[
27
, Lem. 4.2]
and

[
44
, Prop. 58]
. Here

\mathbf{i}
and
\mathbf{1}
respectively denote the terms
\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
and
\lambda\mathsf{y}[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]]
for
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
.
Again,
(
\epsilon_{3}
)
is specifically
designed to work
in settings where
\alpha
–renaming
is available.

As already said, in Theorem
6.3
we show that every extensional congruence is a lambda congruence.
Thus, there is no reason to explicitly include
condition (
\alpha_{e}
)
in the axiomatization.
The property that
\alpha
–renaming can
be derived from
\eta
–extensionality is not so well–known
but it
has been already noticed
for the simply typed lambda calculus
by Došen and Petrić
[
22
, Sec. 3]
.
Indeed,
we have taken account of their idea
in our formalization of extensional congruence.

Since
in Section
8
we shall show
that extensional congruences precisely correspond
to extensional theories,
in the present section
we do not give concrete examples
of extensional congruences; rather, we concentrate on establishing
the technical facts we need to show
the aforementioned equivalence
and on explaining the real significance of
\eta
–extensionality
in our setting.

The first result we present is
given in the second part of following proposition, where
we show that the traditional formulation of
\eta
–extensionality holds
every extensional congruence.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be an extensional congruence.
Let
\mathsf{A}
be a term and
let
\mathsf{y}
be a variable.
Then, the following statements hold:

(i)

Suppose that

\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
. Then, we
have
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{y}]
.

(ii)

Suppose that

\mathsf{y}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
. Then, we
have
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{y}]
.

Proof.

(i)
Let
\mathsf{x}
be a variable such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
.
Let
\mathsf{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{A}][[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{A}]
,
\mathsf{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]]\mathsf{A}

and
\mathsf{D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]]
.
From (
\beta_{3}
), we have

\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
.
From (
\beta_{1}
)
and (
\beta_{2}
) we
obtain
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
and
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{y}
, and
it follows from
(a)
that

\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}\mathsf{y}
.
By (
t
) we have
\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}\mathsf{y}
,
and so we obtain
\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{y}]
from (
\ell
).
Now, as
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, we have
\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]
from (
\eta_{e}
) and

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
from (
\ell
).
By
(
r
), we get
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
. So, by (
a
) we have

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}\mathsf{A}
.
Since
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
and
\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
, we obtain

\mathsf{D}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{C}
by Proposition
4.6
(i).
By using (
s
), we have
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{y}]
, and we get

\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{y}]

from (
t
).

(ii) It follows from (i) above,
as we have

\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
by Theorem
4.5
.
∎

In order to achieve all the goals
we set in the beginning of this section, it only remains to prove
that (
\alpha_{e}
)
holds in every extensional congruence.

Theorem 6.3
.

Every extensional congruence is a lambda congruence.

Proof.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be an extensional congruence.
Let
\mathsf{A}
be a term, and let

\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be variables.
Let
\mathsf{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
.
We have to show that

[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
implies

\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{B}\mathsf{y}]
.
Assume
[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
.
If
\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{y}
, then we proceed as in Remark
5.2
.
If
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, then we have

\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}
and we obtain

\mathsf{y}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}
by Lemma
4.3
(iii). By Proposition
6.2
(i) we
obtain
\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{B}\mathsf{y}]
.
∎

In the following corollary
we show that the set of all extensional congruences is properly included
in the set of all prelambda congruences.

Corollary 6.4
.

There exists a prelambda congruence which is not an extensional congruence.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.3
, prelambda conversion is not a lambda congruence
and hence it cannot be an extensional congruence by Proposition
6.3
.
∎

Regarding the real significance of
\eta
–extensionality
in our setting
we now consider the following condition that we call
extensionality for terms
:

\mathsf{A}\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}\mathsf{D}
for every term
\mathsf{D}
implies
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
.

This form of extensionality has been considered
by many authors: for instance,
in combinatory logic by Rosenbloom
[
43
, p. 112]

and in the lambda calculus
by Wadsworth
[
54
, p. 493]
,
Hindley and Longo
[
27
, p. 297]

and Hindley and Seldin
[
26
, p. 77]
.
While each condition of
\eta
–extensionality
so far considered has a strong syntactical and artificial flavour and no apparent
connection with any property of extensionality,
the property of extensionality for terms above
has a clear
mathematical significance:
two terms should be regarded as the same if they have the same
input–output behaviour.
We now prove
that in every prelambda congruence extensionality for terms
is equivalent
to (
\eta_{e}
).

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence.
Then,
\ \!\sim\ \!
is an extensional congruence if and only if
it satisfies the condition
of extensionality for terms:

(
e_{t}
)

\mathsf{A}\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}\mathsf{D}

for every term
\mathsf{D}
implies
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
,

for all terms
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\ \!\sim\ \!
is an extensional congruence. We now show that
\ \!\sim\ \!
satisfies (
e_{t}
).
For, we assume
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}\mathsf{D}

for every term
\mathsf{D}
and prove that
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
.

Let

\mathsf{z}\in\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{A})}\cap\mathord{\sim\!\!(\mathsf{B})}
. We have
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}\mathsf{z}
and we get

\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{B}\mathsf{z}]
by (
\ell
).
By Proposition
6.2
(i), we get

\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{z}]
and
\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{B}\mathsf{z}]
.
By using (
s
) we have
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}[\mathsf{B}\mathsf{z}]\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}

and we get
\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
from (
t
).

Suppose now that
\ \!\sim\ \!
is a prelambda congruence which satisfies (
e_{t}
).
Let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be two variables such that

\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
.
We now prove that

\mathsf{y}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]
.
Let

\mathsf{D}
be an arbitrary
term.
We have
[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{D}][[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{D}]
by (
\beta_{3}
).
By (
\beta_{2}
) and (
\beta_{1}
)
we get
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{y}

and
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
. So, we obtain

[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{D}][[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{D}]\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}
by (
a
).
By using (
s
)
we have

\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{D}][[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{D}]\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]]\mathsf{D}

and by using (
t
) we obtain

\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]]\mathsf{D}
.
This shows that we have

\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]]\mathsf{D}

for every term
\mathsf{D}
.
Hence,
we can apply (
e_{t}
)
to obtain
\mathsf{y}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]
.
∎

Again, observe that this equivalence is
significant because, by Corollary
6.4
,

(\eta_{e})
does not hold in every prelambda congruence.

We can now give a proper conclusion
to
our informal discussion
of terms as functions.
Recall that
for us a class of
terms intended to play the role of functions
must satisfy the
condition of extensionality discussed in the Section
5
, where we examined the case of abstractions
as functions.
By Theorem
6.5
,
in every extensional congruence we can think
every term
as a function and consider the condition (
\eta_{e}
) as a compact and equivalent way to express the property of
extensionality for terms —
this also gives a proper justification for the terminology “
\eta
–extensionality” employed for our condition (
\eta_{e}
).

7

Lambda and Extensional Theories

Our next step is to prove that
lambda and extensional congruences precisely
correspond to lambda and extensional theories, respectively.
In this section, we recall the
traditional definitions of these theories
and prove some preliminary properties that we need to
prove our results.

Our definitions of lambda and extensional theories
are perfectly in line with the ones
which can be found in the literature;
see, e.g., Meyer
[
33
, p. 92]
.

In order to provide the axiomatizations, we need to formalize the notion of
substitution
first.
To this aim, we
point out that there exist several different definitions of substitution in the literature.
A typical approach is to proceed as in the following remark.

Remark 7.1
.

The definition of substitution which is often found in the literature is the one given by Hindley and Seldin
[
26
, Def. 1.12]
. It is actually a variation of
the one proposed by Curry and Feys
[
19
, p. 94]
and
it is commonly called
capture–free substitution
.

We now recall its definition in order to discuss and emphasize
some of its aspects. Expressed in our notation, it can be formulated as follows:

(1)

\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\mathsf{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\mathsf{D}
;

(2)

\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\mathsf{y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\mathsf{y}
, if
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
;

(3)

\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\mathsf{A})\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\mathsf{B})
;

(4)

\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
;

(5)

\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}
,

if
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
and
\mathsf{x}
does not occur free in
\mathsf{A}
;

(6)

\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{y}\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\mathsf{A})
,

if
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
,
\mathsf{x}

occurs free in
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{y}
does not
occur free in
\mathsf{D}
;

(7)

\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{z}\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\{\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{y}\}(\mathsf{A}))
,

if
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
,
\mathsf{x}
occurs free in
\mathsf{A}
and

\mathsf{y}
occurs free in
\mathsf{D}
, where
\mathsf{z}
is the first variable in which does not occur
free in both
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{D}
.

Despite its complicated axiomatization, substitution as defined above
has the following pleasant property:
the usual
\beta
–rule of the lambda calculus can be simply expressed
as
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\mathsf{A})
, without any restriction.
However, there are also some
disadvantages in considering the above
formalization.

Firstly, the definition above is
not
given
by induction on the structure
of terms.
The reason is that in condition (7)
above there is
\{\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{y}\}(\mathsf{A})

and not
\mathsf{A}
in the right hand side
\lambda\mathsf{z}\{\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\}(\{\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{y}\}(\mathsf{A}))
of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}
.
In fact, the previous definition
is by induction on the
size
of terms. So, in order to define substitution
in this way, we need first a
definition
of
the concept of size of a term.
Furthermore, we need to
prove
that the previous construction is well–defined and to do so, it is also necessary to
prove

that
\{\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{y}\}(\mathsf{A})
and
\mathsf{A}

have the same size.

Secondly, in condition (7) above,
it is assumed that the set of
variables is equipped with an appropriate ordering.
As already said in Section
2
, we do not want to assume this inasmuch as there are
other ways to define substitution.

\triangle

In this article,
we follow the simpler and more elegant approach to substitution
developed by Barendregt in his dissertation

[
4
]
.
(It should not be confused
with the more familiar one developed by Barendregt in his classic book
[
5
]
.)

Definition 7.2

(Substitution)
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{D}
be terms, and let
\mathsf{x}
be a variable. By induction on the structure of
\mathsf{A}
,
we define the term
\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})
as follows:

(S
1
)

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\mathsf{D}
;

(S
2
)

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\mathsf{y}
, if
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
;

(S
3
)

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{C})
;

(S
4
)

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}
;

(S
5
)

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{y}\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})
, if
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
.

We call the term
\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})

the result of the substitution of
\mathsf{x}
for
\mathsf{D}
in
\mathsf{A}
.

\triangle

In order to define
the concepts of lambda and extensional theory
we now formally define the notion of
variable occurring bound
in a term. However,
for the sake of conformity with our formal treatment of free variables,
we prefer to
define the set of variables which do
not
occur bound in a given term instead.

Definition 7.3

(Non–bound variable)
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
be a term.
We define the cofinite set of variables
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})
by induction on the structure of
\mathsf{A}
as follows:

•

\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\mathbb{V}
;

•

\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})\cap\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{C})
;

•

\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}
.

We also say that a variable
\mathsf{x}
does not occur bound in
\mathsf{A}
if
\mathsf{x}\in\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})
.

\triangle

Having now properly defined
the necessary ancillary concepts,
we are finally ready
to define the notions
of lambda and extensional theory.

Definition 7.4

(Lambda and extensional theory)
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a congruence. We say that
\ \!\sim\ \!

is a
lambda theory
if
it also satisfies the following conditions:

(
\beta
)

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})
, provided
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
;

(
\alpha
)

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})
, provided
\mathsf{y}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})\cap\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})
;

where
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{D}
are arbitrary terms, and

\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
are arbitrary variables.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a lambda theory. We say that
\ \!\sim\ \!

is an
extensional theory
if
it also satisfies the following condition:

(
\eta
)

\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[\mathsf{A}\mathsf{y}]
, provided
\mathsf{y}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
;

where
\mathsf{A}
is an arbitrary term and
\mathsf{y}
is an arbitrary variable.

\triangle

Thus, a lambda theory is any binary
relation on terms which simultaneously
satisfies all structural conditions
and
conditions (
\beta
) and (
\alpha
) given above.
If, in addition, the relation is also closed under
(
\eta
), then it is an extensional theory.

The most important examples of lambda and extensional theories are
lambda conversion

and
extensional conversion
.

Definition 7.5

(Lambda
and extensional conversion)
.

We call
lambda conversion

the lambda theory
inductively defined
by structural
conditions, (
\beta
)
and (
\alpha
). We also define
extensional conversion
as the extensional theory inductively defined by structural conditions,
(
\beta
),
(
\alpha
) and (
\eta
).

\triangle

Let us now discuss the conditions of Definition
7.4
in some detail.

Conditions (
\beta
) and (
\alpha
)
are as in Barendregt
[
4
, p. 4]
.
Note that (
\beta
)
presents a restriction on its
applicability, namely

\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
.
In the original formulation,
this restriction is equivalently expressed follows:
bound variables
of
\mathsf{A}
and free variables of
\mathsf{D}

are disjoint sets.
In this paper, instead of putting complications
directly inside the definition of substitution (cf. the capture–free substitution of Remark
7.1
),
we prefer to have a simple notion of substitution at the price of this restriction.
In the next example
we can see how the proviso
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
works.

Example 7.6
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a lambda theory.
Let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be variables such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
. We have
\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x})=\lambda\mathsf{y}\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{x})=\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
. However,
we
cannot
infer
[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]]\mathsf{y}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
directly from (
\beta
), as we have

\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{y})=\big(\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{x})\setminus\{\mathsf{y}\}\big)\cup\big(\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\mathsf{y}\}\big)=\big(\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\mathsf{y}\}\big)\cup\big(\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\mathsf{y}\}\big)=\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\neq\mathbb{V}
.
\triangle

As for
(
\alpha
),
when compared with other formalizations
of
\alpha
–renaming that one usually finds in the literature,
the main difference lies in
the restriction

\mathsf{y}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})\cap\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})
which is not just the “usual” proviso

\mathsf{y}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
(that is,
\mathsf{y}
does not occur free in
\mathsf{A}
).
The next example
explains the situation.

Example 7.7
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a lambda theory.
Let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be variables such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
. As
before, we have
\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x})=\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
.
From this, it follows that

\lambda\mathsf{y}\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x})=\lambda\mathsf{y}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}]
.
Since
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, we have
\mathsf{y}\in\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}=(\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\})\cup\{\,\!\mathsf{y}\,\!\}=\reflectbox{{F}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x})
.
However,
we
cannot
infer
\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}]
directly from (
\alpha
), as we have

\mathsf{y}\notin\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{y}\,\!\}=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{x})\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{y}\,\!\}=\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x})
and in particular
\mathsf{y}\notin\reflectbox{{F}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x})\cap\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x})
.

Notice that if we use the capture–free
substitution
of Remark

7.1
, then,
in order to calculate

\{\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x})
,
we are forced to use condition (7) as
we have

\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
,
\mathsf{x}\notin\reflectbox{{F}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x})
and
\mathsf{y}\notin\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{y})
. In this case, we obtain

\{\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x})=\lambda\mathsf{z}\{\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\}(\{\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{y}\}\mathsf{x}))=\lambda\mathsf{z}\{\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\}(\mathsf{x})=\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{y}

where
\mathsf{z}
is the first variable
in
\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}\,\!\}=\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{x})\cap\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{y})
.
From this, it follows that

\lambda\mathsf{y}\{\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x})=\lambda\mathsf{y}[\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{x}]

and
we can infer

\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{y}]

by using (
\alpha
)
with the “usual” restriction
\mathsf{y}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
.

\triangle

Finally, we note that condition (
\eta
)
is exactly as in the literature.

As already pointed out, Došen and Petrić
[
22
]
observed that
it is not necessary to include
\alpha
–renaming in the axiomatization extensional theories. Indeed,
we followed their idea
in our formalization of extensional
congruences
.
However, we do not take their observation into account in the present context because traditional
formulations
do include an explicit condition of
\alpha
–renaming.
Also, even if condition (
\alpha
) were removed from the definition,
other ancillary concepts would still be present.

In order to reach our goals,
we now prove a series of preliminary results.

First, we show that every lambda theory
satisfies the condition of extensionality for abstractions
discussed in Section
5
.

Proposition 7.8
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a lambda theory.
Let
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
and
\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}
be terms and suppose
that
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{D}
for every term
\mathsf{D}
.
Then, we have
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}
.

Proof.

Let

\mathsf{z}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A})\cap\reflectbox{{F}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B})\cap\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A})\cap\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B})
.
Then, we have

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}
. Since
\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{z})=\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{z}\,\!\}
,
\mathsf{z}\in\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A})

and
\mathsf{z}\in\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B})
,
we have
\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{z})=\mathbb{V}

and
\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{z})=\mathbb{V}
.
So, we can apply (
\beta
) and we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})
and

[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{B})
.
By using (
s
)
we obtain
\langle\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{z}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{B})

and so
\lambda\mathsf{z}\langle\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}\langle\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{B})
from (
t
) and (
\ell
).
Now, since
\mathsf{z}\in\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A})=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}

we obtain

\mathsf{z}\in\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})
and
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
.
Similarly,
as
\mathsf{z}\in\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B})=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{y}\,\!\}

we have

\mathsf{z}\in\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})
and
\mathsf{y}\neq\mathsf{z}
.
As
\mathsf{z}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A})=\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})\cup\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}

and
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
, it follows that

\mathsf{z}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
. Analogously, as

\mathsf{z}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B})=\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})\cup\{\,\!\mathsf{y}\,\!\}

and
\mathsf{y}\neq\mathsf{z}
, we obtain

\mathsf{z}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{B})
.
Hence, we can now apply (
\alpha
)
to obtain
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}\langle\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})
and

\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}\langle\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{B})
.
By using (
s
) we get

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}\langle\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{z}\langle\mathsf{z}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{B})\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}

and finally
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}
from (
t
).
∎

We now establish other technical properties.

Lemma 7.9
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
be a term, and let
\mathsf{y}
and
\mathsf{w}
be variables. Then, we have

\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{A}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})
.

Proof.

We reason by induction on the structure of
\mathsf{A}
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{z}
. If
\mathsf{y}=\mathsf{z}
,
then we have
\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{y}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{w})=\mathbb{V}=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{y})
.
If
\mathsf{y}\neq\mathsf{z}
,
then we have
\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{z}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{z})
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}
.
By inductive hypothesis, we have

\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{B}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})

and
\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{C}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{C})
.
Then, it follows that

\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{B})\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{C}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{B}))\cap\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{C}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})\cap\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{C})=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C})
.

Finally, suppose that

\mathsf{A}=\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{B}
.
If
\mathsf{y}=\mathsf{z}
, then we have
\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B})
.
If
\mathsf{y}\neq\mathsf{z}
, then,
by inductive hypothesis, we have

\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{B}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})
.
Hence, it follows that

\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{B}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{z}\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{B}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{B}))\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{z}\,\!\}=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{z}\,\!\}=\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{B})
.
∎

Proposition 7.10
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a lambda theory, and
let
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{D}
be terms. Then, there exists a term
\mathsf{G}
such that

\mathsf{G}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}
,
and
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{G})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on the structure of
\mathsf{A}
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{x}
. Let
\mathsf{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\mathsf{x}
.
Then, we have
\mathsf{G}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{x}
by (
r
). Since
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{G})=\mathbb{V}
, we have

\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{G})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}
. By inductive hypothesis there exist
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{N}
such that

\mathsf{M}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
and
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{M})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
, as well as
\mathsf{N}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{C}
and
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{N})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
.
Let
\mathsf{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\mathsf{M}\mathsf{N}
.
Then, we have
\mathsf{G}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}
by (
a
) and

\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{G})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\big(\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{M})\cap\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{N})\big)\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\big(\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{M})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})\big)\cap\big(\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{N})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})\big)=\mathbb{V}\cap\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{V}
.

Finally, suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}
.
By inductive hypothesis, there exists
a term
\mathsf{M}
such that
\mathsf{M}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{B}
and
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{M})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
.
Let
\mathsf{w}\in\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{M})\cap\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{M})\cap\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})

and
let
\mathsf{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\lambda\mathsf{w}\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{M})
.
We have
\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{M}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}
from (
\ell
) and from
(
\alpha
) it follows that
\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{M}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{G}
, as
\mathsf{w}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{M})\cap\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{M})
.
By using (
s
),
we obtain

\mathsf{G}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{M}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}
. Hence, we have

\mathsf{G}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}
from (
t
).
In order to finish the proof, we now show that

\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{G})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
.
For, we have

\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{G})=\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{M}))\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{w}\,\!\}
.
By Lemma
7.9
,
it follows that
\reflectbox{{B}}(\langle\mathsf{w}/\mathsf{y}\rangle(\mathsf{M}))=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{M})
.
Hence, we get

\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{G})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\big(\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{M})\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{w}\,\!\}\big)\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})
. Now, observe that

\big(\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{M})\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{w}\,\!\}\big)\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{M})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})
,
as
\mathsf{w}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})
. From this, we obtain

\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{M})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}

by the inductive hypothesis.
∎

Lemma 7.11
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{D}
be terms, and let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be variables such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
.
Then, we have
\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A}))=\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the structure of
\mathsf{A}
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{z}
. If
\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{z}
, then we have

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{x}))=\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{y})=\mathsf{y}=\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{x})
, as
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
.
If
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
, then it follows that

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{z}))=\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{z})=\mathsf{z}=\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{z})
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}
.
By inductive hypothesis, we have

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B}))

=\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})
and

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{C}))

=\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{C})
. Then, we get

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}))

=

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{C}))=\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B}))\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{C}))

=\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{C})=\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C})
.

Finally, suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{B}
. If
\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{z}
, then we
have
\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}))=\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B})=\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}=\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B})
.
If
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
, then, by inductive hypothesis, we have

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B}))

=\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})
.
Then, we obtain

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{B}))=\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{z}\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B}))

=\lambda\mathsf{z}\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B}))=\lambda\mathsf{z}\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})=\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{z}\mathsf{B})
.
∎

8

Lambda and Extensional Theories Revisited

In this section, we finally prove that the notions of lambda and
extensional congruence are respectively equivalent to the concepts of lambda and extensional theory.
To begin with, we show in the next proposition
that prelambda congruences behave well
with respect to condition (
\beta
).

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be a prelambda congruence. Let
\mathsf{x}
be a variable, and
let
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{D}
be terms such that
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
. Then, we have
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})
.

Proof.

We reason
by induction on the structure of
\mathsf{A}
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{y}
. If
\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{y}
, then we have

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{x})=\mathsf{D}
and it follows that

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
from (
\beta_{1}
).
If
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, then
we have
\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{y})=\mathsf{y}

and we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{y}

by (
\beta_{2}
).

Suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}
.
Since
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\subseteq\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})
,

\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\subseteq\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{C})

and
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
, we have
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}

and
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{C})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
.
Hence, by inductive hypothesis, it follows that
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})

and
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{C}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{C})
. From this, we get

[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{D}][[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{C}]\mathsf{D}]\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{C})
from (
a
).
Now,
by (
\beta_{3}
) we have

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{D}][[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{C}]\mathsf{D}]
, and we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{C})
from (
t
).
As
\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{C})=\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C})
, we conclude.

Finally, suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}
. If
\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{y}
,
then we have
\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B})=\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}
and we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}
by (
\beta_{4}
).
If
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, then we have
\mathsf{y}\notin\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{y}\,\!\}=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})
.
Since

\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
, it follows that
\mathsf{y}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})
.
As
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{D}]
by Proposition
4.6
(ii).
Since
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\subseteq\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})
and
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
, we have
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{B})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
.
By inductive hypothesis,
we get

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})
.
From this, we obtain

\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{D}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})
from (
\ell
) and

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})
from (
t
).
Since
\lambda\mathsf{y}\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{B})=\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B})
, we conclude.
∎

We are now in a position to prove the main results of this section.

Proposition 8.2
.

Every lambda congruence is a lambda theory.

Proof.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be an lambda congruence. In order to prove that
\ \!\sim\ \!
is a lambda theory
we only have to show that

\ \!\sim\ \!
satisfies conditions
(
\beta
) and (
\alpha
).

(
\beta
) Suppose that
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
.
Since every lambda congruence is also a prelambda congruence, we obtain
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})
by
Proposition
8.1
.

(
\alpha
)
Suppose that
\mathsf{y}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})\cap\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})
.
As
\mathsf{y}\in\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{A})
, we have

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{y}]
by Proposition
5.4
(ii).
As
\mathsf{y}\in\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})
and
\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{y})=\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{y}\,\!\}
, we have
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{A})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{y})=\mathbb{V}
. By Proposition
8.1
, it follows that
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{y}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})
, and we get

\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{y}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})
from (
\ell
).
Then, we conclude that

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\langle\mathsf{y}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{A})
from
(
t
).
∎

Proposition 8.3
.

Every lambda theory is a lambda congruence.

\ \!\sim\ \!
be a lambda theory.
By Theorem
5.5
,
in order to prove that
\ \!\sim\ \!

Since

\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{x})=\mathsf{D}

we obtain
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}

by (
\beta
).

(
\beta_{2}
) Suppose that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
.
Since
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{y})=\mathbb{V}
, we have
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{y})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}
.
As
\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{y})=\mathsf{y}

we have
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{y}

by (
\beta
).

(
\beta_{3}
)
By Proposition
7.10
, there exist some terms

\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{N}
such that
\mathsf{M}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{A}

\mathsf{E}\in\{\,\!\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}\,\!\}

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{F}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{E}
by
(\ell
).
From (
r

\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}

by (
a
).
Since
\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{F})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{V}

[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{F}]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{F})

[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}][[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B}]\mathsf{D}]\ \!\sim\ \!

a

By using (
s

\beta_{4}

7.10

\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{G}]

a

,
we have
\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{G}]\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]

by using (
\ell
).
As
\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\mathsf{D}
,
we get

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{G}]]\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}

by (
a
).
Since
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, we have
\mathsf{y}\in\big(\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{D})\cup\{\,\!\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\big)\cap\big(\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\big)=\reflectbox{{F}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D})\cap\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{x})=\reflectbox{{F}}([\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{D}]\mathsf{x})=\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{C})
. From this and

\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{G})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{C})=\mathbb{V}
it follows that

\reflectbox{{B}}(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{G})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{C})=\big(\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{G})\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\big)\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{C})=\reflectbox{{B}}(\mathsf{G})\cup\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{C})=\mathbb{V}
. Hence,
we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{G}]]\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\langle\mathsf{C}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{G})
by (
\beta
).
Since
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, we have

\langle\mathsf{C}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{G})=\lambda\mathsf{y}\langle\mathsf{C}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{G})
. Thus,
we get

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{G}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\langle\mathsf{C}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{G})
.
By using (
s
)
we obtain

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \![\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{G}]]\mathsf{C}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\langle\mathsf{C}/\mathsf{x}\rangle(\mathsf{G})\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}]
. Hence, we conclude that

[\lambda\mathsf{x}[\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{A}]]\mathsf{D}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}[[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{D}]

from (
t
).

(
e_{a}
) This condition holds by Proposition
7.8
.
∎

Proposition 8.4
.

Every extensional congruence is an extensional theory.

Proof.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be an extensional congruence. In order to prove that
\ \!\sim\ \!
is an extensional theory
we have to show that

\ \!\sim\ \!
satisfies conditions
(
\beta
), (
\alpha
)
and (
\eta
).
By Theorem
6.3

we know that
\ \!\sim\ \!
is a lambda congruence and so by
Proposition
8.2
it satisfies
(
\beta
) and (
\alpha
).
As for (
\eta
),
it holds
by Proposition
6.2
(ii).
∎

Proposition 8.5
.

Every extensional theory is an extensional congruence.

Proof.

Let
\ \!\sim\ \!
be an extensional theory.
In order to prove that
\ \!\sim\ \!
is an extensional congruence
we have to show that

\ \!\sim\ \!
satisfies all beta conditions
and (
\eta_{e}
).
Since, by definition,
\ \!\sim\ \!
is also a lambda theory, we know by Proposition
8.3
that

\ \!\sim\ \!
satisfies
all beta conditions.
As for (
\eta_{e}
), suppose that

\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
.
Then, as
\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{V}\setminus\{\,\!\mathsf{y}\,\!\}=\reflectbox{{F}}(\mathsf{y})
,
we obtain

\mathsf{y}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{x}[\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x}]

by (
\eta
).
∎

Theorem 8.6
.

The concepts of
lambda and extensional congruence are respectively equivalent to the notions of lambda and extensional theory.

Proof.

By the previous four propositions.
∎

By using the above theorem,
it is also possible to give simplified formalizations
of lambda conversion and extensional conversion as we now explain.

Recall that lambda conversion is the lambda theory inductively
defined by using structural conditions, (
\beta
) and (
\alpha
).
In other words, lambda conversion is the intersection
of all lambda theories.
By Theorem
8.6
, the set of all lambda
theories is equal to the set of all
lambda
congruences. Thus, lambda conversion
is also the intersection of all lambda congruences. In particular,
lambda
conversion can be equivalently
characterized as the relation
inductively defined by using
all structural and beta conditions
together with (
\alpha_{e}
).

By replacing (
\alpha_{e}
)
by (
\eta_{e}
) in the discussion above, a simplified axiomatization of extensional conversion is similarly given.

9

The Prelambda Congruence
\,\!\sim_{I}\,\!

The aim of this section is to show
that there exists a prelambda congruence

\,\!\sim_{I}\,\!

such that for all distinct
variables
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
we have
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\not\,\!\sim_{I}\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
.
In other words, we now prove Theorem
3.3
whose proof was omitted in Section
3
.

This is not an easy task, though.
The reason is that
the defining conditions of prelambda congruence
only deal with “provability” while
our aim is to show the
“unprovability” of
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\,\!\sim_{I}\,\!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
, that is

\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\not\,\!\sim_{I}\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
.

A reassuring fact is that similar situations
frequently happen in logic and in that setting well–known methods to solve these kinds of problems
are available.
Consider for instance

intuitionistic theories
and the law of excluded middle — for the purposes of this discussion an intuitionistic theory is just a set of formulas which is closed under the rules of intuitionistic logic.
We say that an intuitionistic theory
is
classical
if it contain every instance of excluded middle.
Of course, there are several intuitionistic theories
which are classical: classical logic is the primary example.

Suppose that we want to show that there exists
an intuitionistic theory which is
not
classical. In order to do this, it suffices to
construct
a model
M
and prove that the
set
of formulas which are valid in
M

forms a non–classical
intuitionistic theory.
Note that
M

cannot be a standard
model of classical logic
— excluded middle never fails there — but it has to be a model specifically designed to this aim,
such as a
topological model or a
Kripke model; see, e.g., Sørensen and
Urzyczyn
[
47
, Ch. 2]
.

Now, if we think of
prelambda congruences
as intuitionistic theories
and
“
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
for all
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
”
as the law of excluded middle,
then
the situation is strikingly similar
to the one described above.
Hence, in order to solve our problem we now build a kind of model with the required properties.
Since
all models considered
in the literature of the lambda calculus
have been conceived for the purpose of
validating
(not
refuting
)
“
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}\ \!\sim\ \!\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}
for all
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
”, our construction, to the best of our knowledge,
seems to be new.

We now introduce some notation.
Let
A
and
B
be sets. We write
B\subseteq_{f}A

to express the fact that
B
is a finite subset of
A
.
We also write
P(A)
and
P_{f}(A)

for the set of all subsets and finite subsets
of
A
, respectively.
Furthermore, we write
A\times B

to denote the Cartesian product of
A
and
B
.

In order to construct our model
we now introduce the notion
of
formula
.

Definition 9.1

(Formula)
.

Let
\mathfrak{F}
be the set inductively
defined as follows:

(
\mathfrak{F}_{1}
)

\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{V}
implies
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{F}
;

(
\mathfrak{F}_{2}
)

F\subseteq_{f}\mathfrak{F}
and
\ell\in\mathfrak{F}
imply
(F,\ell)\in\mathfrak{F}
.

We call
formula
any element of the set
\mathfrak{F}
.
Henceforth, we use
F
,
G
,
H
,…and
\ell
,
m
,
n
, …to denote
finite subsets and elements of
\mathfrak{F}
, respectively.
We also use the expression
F\vdash\ell

as an alternative notation for the ordered pair
(F,\ell)
.
In particular, we write
G\vdash F\vdash\ell

for
(G,(F,\ell))
.

Let
\ell
be a formula. We say that
\ell
is an
atomic formula
if
\ell\in\mathbb{V}

and that
\ell
is a
compound formula
if
\ell\notin\mathbb{V}
; equivalently, if it is of the form
F\vdash m
.

\triangle

We now observe that our set
\mathfrak{F}
belongs to the class of structures
called
graph algebras
,
in the terminology of Engeler
[
23
]
.
In particular,
\mathfrak{F}

corresponds to
the full graph algebra
G(\mathbb{V})
built on the set of variables
\mathbb{V}
.
For our aims
the fact that
\mathfrak{F}
is specifically constructed out of the set of variables
\mathbb{V}
turns out to be essential, as we make clear later.
Graph algebras are also known as Engeler models and PSE–algebras in the literature; see, e.g., Meyer
[
33
]
, Longo
[
31
]
, Barendregt
[
5
]
, Krivine
[
30
]
, Plotkin
[
39
]
, Berline
[
6
,
7
]
and Hindley and Seldin
[
26
]
.
Regarding our terminology,
we refer to elements
\mathfrak{F}

as formulas because a similar terminology
is employed in
[
30
]
for similar structures.

Our aim is to interpret terms
using the structure we have just
defined. In order to do so, it is necessary
to introduce the concepts of environment
and update first.

Definition 9.2

(Environment, update)
.

We call
environment

any function
\sigma
from
\mathbb{V}
to
P_{f}(\mathfrak{F})
.
In the sequel, we use
\sigma
,
\rho
,
\tau
, …to denote environments. We also denote the set of all environments by
\mathbb{E}
.

Let
\sigma
be an environment. Let
\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{V}
and let

F\subseteq_{f}\mathfrak{F}
. We denote by
\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma

the environment given by:

\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{y})\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}F&\hbox{if $\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{y}$}\\
\sigma(\mathsf{y})&\hbox{if $\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}$\kern 5.0pt, \quad for $\mathsf{y}\in\mathbb{V}$\kern 5.0pt.}\\
\end{array}\right.

We call
\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma
an
update of

\sigma
.

Let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be variables, and
let
F
and
G
in
P_{f}(\mathfrak{F})
.
Let
\sigma
be an environment.
If
\rho=\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma

and
\tau=\{\,\!G/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\rho
,
then we also write
\tau
as

\{\,\!G/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma
.

\triangle

Updates obey the algebraic laws that we show in the next lemma.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
\sigma
be an environment. Let

\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
be variables such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, and let

F
and
G
be finite subsets of
\mathfrak{F}
. Then, the following properties hold:

(i)

\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma=\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma
;

(ii)

\{\,\!G/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma=\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!G/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\sigma
.

Proof.

(i)
We have

\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{x})=G=\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{x})
.
Let
\mathsf{z}
be a variable such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
. Then, we have

\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{z})=\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{z})=\sigma(\mathsf{z})=\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{z})
.

(ii)
Since
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, we have

\{\,\!G/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{x})=\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{x})=

F=\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!G/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{x})

and

\{\,\!G/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{y})=G=\{\,\!G/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{y})=\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!G/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{y})
.
Let
\mathsf{z}
be a variable such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
and
\mathsf{y}\neq\mathsf{z}
. Then, we have

\{\,\!G/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{z})=\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{z})=\sigma(\mathsf{z})=\{\,\!G/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{z})=\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!G/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{z})
.
∎

We now define our interpretation of terms.
As is standard for graph algebras,
each term is interpreted via an environment as a subset of formulas.

Definition 9.4

(Interpretation of terms in
P(\mathfrak{F})
)
.

We define the function
I
from
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{E}
to
P(\mathfrak{F})
by induction on the structure of terms as follows:

(
I_{1}
)

I(\mathsf{x},\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\sigma(\mathsf{x})
;

(
I_{2}
)

I(\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B},\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\{\,\!\ell\ \,|\ \,
there exists
F\subseteq_{f}I(\mathsf{B},\sigma)
such that

\phantom{asaasasaasaasas}F\vdash\ell\in I(\mathsf{A},\sigma)\,\!\}
;

(
I_{3}
)

I(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A},\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{DEF}}}}{{=}}\{\,\!F\vdash m\ \,|\ \,m\in I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)\,\!\}\,\cup\,\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
be terms.
We write
\mathsf{A}\,\!\sim_{I}\,\!\mathsf{B}
and say that
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
have the same interpretation in
P(\mathfrak{F})
if
I(\mathsf{A},\sigma)=I(\mathsf{B},\sigma)

for every environment
\sigma
.
We also call the relation
\,\!\sim_{I}\,\!

the
theory of
P(\mathfrak{F})
.

\triangle

While
our
set

\mathfrak{F}
is just an example of graph algebra,
our
interpretation
of terms in
P(\mathfrak{F})
is definitely non–standard, as we now explain.

Firstly, environments
are usually taken as function
from
\mathbb{V}
to
P(\mathfrak{F})

and not as
functions from
\mathbb{V}
to
P_{f}(\mathfrak{F})
as we do in this paper.
The reason is practical: to show our results we found it is not necessary consider
environments having infinite sets of formulas in their range.

Secondly,
following the standard interpretation of terms in graph algebras
I(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A},\sigma)
should be the set of formulas
\{\,\!F\vdash m\ \,|\ \,m\in I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)\,\!\}
and
not
our set
\{\,\!F\vdash m\ \,|\ \,m\in I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)\,\!\}\,\cup\,\{\,\!\mathsf{x}\,\!\}
. As a consequence of this fact, in our setting each set of the form

I(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A},\sigma)
contains
compound formulas of the form
F\vdash m
and
exactly
one
atomic formula, namely
\mathsf{x}
.
This formula
\mathsf{x}
gives us a “tag” for the “
\lambda\mathsf{x}
” in the interpretation of
\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}
and the presence of this unique atomic formula turns out to be crucial for our purposes.
For this reason, the fact that variables
are also formulas is very important in our setting.

We now show two lemmas
that we need during the proof of Theorem
9.7
.

Lemma 9.5
.

Let
\mathsf{x}
be a variable and let
\mathsf{A}
be a term.
Let
F
and
G
finite subsets of of formulas such that
F\subseteq G
.
Then, for every environment
\sigma
we have

I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)\subseteq I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.

Proof.

We reason by induction on the structure of
\mathsf{A}
. Let

\sigma
be an environment and let
\ell
be a formula.

Suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{y}
. If
\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{y}
,
then we have

I(\mathsf{x},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)=\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{x})=F\subseteq G=\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{x})=I(\mathsf{x},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
If
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
,
then we have

I(\mathsf{y},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)=\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{y})=\sigma(\mathsf{y})=\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{y})=I(\mathsf{y},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}
.
By inductive
hypothesis,
for every environment
\rho
and
every
\mathsf{D}\in\{\,\!\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C}\,\!\}
we have

I(\mathsf{D},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\rho)\subseteq I(\mathsf{D},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\rho)
.
Assume

\ell\in I(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
. Then, there exists
H\subseteq_{f}I(\mathsf{C},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
such that
H\vdash\ell\in I(\mathsf{B},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
By inductive hypothesis,
it follows that

H\subseteq_{f}I(\mathsf{C},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
and
H\vdash\ell\in I(\mathsf{B},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
. From this, we conclude that

\ell\in I(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{A}=\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B}
.
Assume

\ell\in I(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
. Suppose that
\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{y}
.
If
\ell=\mathsf{x}
, then we obviously have
\mathsf{x}\in I(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
Otherwise, it follows that
\ell=H\vdash m

for some

H
and
m
such that
m\in I(\mathsf{B},\{\,\!H/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
By using Lemma
9.3
(i)
it follows that

m\in I(\mathsf{B},\{\,\!H/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
and we obtain

H\vdash m\in I(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{B},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
Suppose now that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
.
If
\ell=\mathsf{y}
, then we clearly have
\mathsf{y}\in I(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
Otherwise, it follows that
\ell=H\vdash m

for some

H
and
m
such that
m\in I(\mathsf{B},\{\,\!H/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
By inductive
hypothesis,
for every environment
\rho
we have

I(\mathsf{B},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\rho)\subseteq I(\mathsf{B},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\rho)
.
From this and Lemma

9.3
(ii) we obtain

m\in I(\mathsf{B},\{\,\!H/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)=I(\mathsf{B},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!H/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\sigma)\subseteq I(\mathsf{B},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!H/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\sigma)=I(\mathsf{B},\{\,\!H/\mathsf{y}\,\!\}\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
, as
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
. Therefore, we get

H\vdash m\in I(\lambda\mathsf{y}\mathsf{B},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
∎

Lemma 9.6
.

Let
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{z}
be variables such
that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
, and let
\mathsf{A}
be a term. Let
F
be a finite subset of formulas.
Suppose that
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\,\!\sim_{I}\,\!\mathsf{A}
. Then, we have
I(\mathsf{A},\sigma)=I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
for every environment
\sigma
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be an environment and let
\ell
be a formula.
As
[\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z}\,\!\sim_{I}\,\!\mathsf{A}
,
we have

I([\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z},\sigma)=I(\mathsf{A},\sigma)
and

I([\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)=I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.

Assume
\ell\in I(\mathsf{A},\sigma)=I([\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z},\sigma)
. Then, there exists
G\subseteq_{f}I(\mathsf{z},\sigma)

such that

G\vdash\ell\in I(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A},\sigma)
and
\ell\in I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
Since
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
, it follows
that
G\subseteq_{f}I(\mathsf{z},\sigma)=\sigma(\mathsf{z})=\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{z})=I(\mathsf{z},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
By using Lemma
9.3
(i), it follows that

\ell\in I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
and hence

G\vdash\ell\in I(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
Since
G\subseteq_{f}I(\mathsf{z},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
,
we obtain
\ell\in I([\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)=I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
This shows that
I(\mathsf{A},\sigma)\subseteq I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
To show the opposite inclusion,
assume
\ell\in I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)=I([\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A}]\mathsf{z},\sigma\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\})
. Then, there exists some
G\subseteq_{f}I(\mathsf{z},\sigma\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\})

such that

G\vdash\ell\in I(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
and
\ell\in I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
By using Lemma
9.3
(i), we get

\ell\in I(\mathsf{A},\{\,\!G/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)
.
So, we have

G\vdash\ell\in I(\lambda\mathsf{x}\mathsf{A},\sigma)
.
As
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{z}
, we get

G\subseteq_{f}I(\mathsf{z},\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma)=\{\,\!F/\mathsf{x}\,\!\}\sigma(\mathsf{z})=\sigma(\mathsf{z})=I(\mathsf{z},\sigma)
and we obtain

∎

Conclusions and Some Directions for Future Work

In this work we have obtained
alternative and simplified formulations of the concepts of lambda theory and extensional
theory without introducing the meta–theoretic notion of substitution and the conceptually inelegant sets of all, free and
bound variables occurring in a term.
We have also clarified the actual
role of
\alpha
–renaming and
\eta
–extensionality in the lambda calculus: from a convenient point of view — our prelambda congruences — both of them can be equivalently
described as properties of
extensionality for certain classes
of terms.

Our proof of the elimination of the ancillary concepts is
rather technical, but conceptually
speaking very simple.
We also point out that in the relevant literature discussed in Section
1
we could not find
complete
and
detailed
proofs of
results similar to those we proved in Section
8
and Section
9
. For this reason — and also because
we want to make the article
readable by a wider audience —
we decided to make our exposition self–contained as much as possible.

As for future work, we plan to
apply the ideas we followed
in this paper to other contexts.
For instance, we would like to provide similar formalizations of various notions of
derivability
(of formulas and sequents) for several first– and higher–order logics and theories. As already observed by Révész
[
41
]
, some results
for derivability in
classical first–order logic with equality
which are in line with our motivations — the elimination of some meta–theoretic notions included in our ancillary concepts — have been
already established
by Tarski
[
51
]
, Kalish and Montague
[
28
]
and Monk
[
34
]
.
Similar results for
second–order classic logic have been established by Cocchiarella
[
14
]
.
But in our opinion, the most satisfactory
axiomatization of first–order classical logic with equality, due to Németi,
is reported in the book of Blok and Pigozzi

[
9
, App. C]
. In contrast to the aforementioned work, the formulation described in that book has the pleasant property of being completely free of ancillary concepts.

We believe that there is a more uniform way to tackle the problem of eliminating the ancillary concepts from several logics, as we now intuitively explain.
It is well–known that substitution
— which is usually introduced as a tool for developing the proof–theory of the quantifiers —
can be completely handled by the lambda notation, as in Church’s theory of simple types
[
12
]
.
This theory can be seen as (an extension of) classical higher–order logic and we refer to Coquand
[
16
, Sec. 1]
for a very elegant presentation of its purely logical part in an intuitionistic setting.
We think that the ideas
and the approach
employed in the present article
can be exploited to
remove the ancillary concepts
— which have no mathematical
and logical substance
— from logic
and hence
simplify the presentations of intuitionistic and classical higher–order logics.

We also plan to export our ideas to give new presentations of some frameworks based on the lambda calculus (without types) whose aim is to formalize (considerable parts of) mathematics such as the
type–free systems

introduced by
Myhill and Flagg
[
35
]
and
map theory
, a setting originally introduced by Grue and recently simplified by Berline and Grue
[
8
]
.

Finally, we also believe
that some ideas we have introduced in this paper can be useful to develop another approach to lambda calculi with
explicit substitutions
, see Abadi, Cardelli, Curien and Lévi
[
1
]
and also Kesner
[
29
]
for a brief survey. Our suggestion is to internalize substitutions as in the theory of lambda substitution algebras, the algebraic framework introduced by Diskin and Beylin
[
21
]
discussed in Section
3
.
The expected advantage should be
the following: no ancillary concept other than substitution — which is not a meta–theoretic notion in that context — would appear in the formalizations of lambda and extensional theories in systems
with explicit substitutions.

More generally, we believe
that the
algebraic
approach
to binding operations,
see Cardone and Hindley
[
10
, p. 736]
for a brief survey, has been relatively overlooked by the computer science community — exceptions are, of course, lambda abstraction and lambda substitution algebras. While the primary goals of those lines of work, namely
representation theorems
, are perhaps
more palatable to algebraists than
computer scientists, we think that
the study of the algebraic approach to quantification can be very useful for the developments of better
syntactical
formalizations of theories in
structures with binding operations.
