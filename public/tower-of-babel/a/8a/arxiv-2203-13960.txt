A Relation of the Allen-Cahn equations and the Euler equations and applications of the equipartition
Dimitrios Gazoulis

Abstract
. We will prove that solutions of the Allen-Cahn equations that satisfy the equipartition of the energy can be transformed into solutions of the Euler equations with constant pressure. As a consequence, we obtain De Giorgi type results, that is, the level sets of entire solutions are hyperplanes. In addition, we obtain some examples of smooth entire solutions of the Euler equations in particular cases. For specific type of initial conditions, some of these solutions can be extended to the Navier-Stokes equations. Also, we will determine the structure of solutions of the Allen-Cahn system in two dimensions that satisfy the equipartition. Finally, we apply the Leray projection on the Allen-Cahn system and provide some explicit entire solutions.



1.
Introduction

As it is well known, De Giorgi in 1978
[
14
]
suggested a stricking analogy of the Allen Cahn equation (
\Delta u=f(u)
) with minimal surface theory that led to significant developments in Partial Differential equations and the Calculus of Variations, by stating the following conjecture about bounded solutions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\\

Conjecture:
(De Giorgi) Let
u\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be a solution to

\displaystyle\Delta u-u^{3}+u=0

such that: 1.
|u|<1
,   2.
\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x_{n}}>0\;\>\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.\\

Is it true that all the level sets of
u
are hyperplanes, at least for
n\leq 8
?

The relationship with the Bernstein problem for minimal graphs is the reason why
n\leq 8
appears in the conjecture.

The conjecture has been proved and the relation of the Allen Cahn with minimal surfaces can be seen via the theory of
\Gamma
-convergence (see
[
19
]
for further details). The family of functionals

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}(u)=\int_{\Omega}(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(u))dx\;\;,\;\varepsilon>0

\Gamma
-converges as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
to the perimeter functional and the Euler-Lagrange equations are

\displaystyle\varepsilon\Delta u-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W^{\prime}(u)=0

therefore one expects that the level sets of the minimizers will minimize the perimeter.

So, one question could be, whether there exists a transformation that transforms the Allen-Cahn equation
\Delta u=f(u)\;\>(u:\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
) to the minimal surface equation of one dimension lower (i.e.
(n-1)
-dimensional minimal surface equation). The answer is positive, at least in dimension 3, by Corollary
4.1
and then we can apply a Bernstein-type theorem for the minimal surface equation (see
[
8
]
,
[
10
]
) to obtain that the level sets of solutions of the Allen-Cahn equation that satisfy the equipartition of the energy are hyperlanes.

For bounded entire solutions of the Allen-Cahn equation that satisfy the equipartition holds a more general result (see Theorem 5.1 in
[
6
]
), that is, the level sets of entire solutions of the Allen Cahn equations that satisfy the equipartition are hyperplanes. This was already known by Modica and Mortola in 1980 (see final remark in
[
17
]
). In fact, any solution of the Allen-Cahn equation is smooth and satisfies the bound
|u|\leq 1
(see Proposition 1.9 in
[
11
]
). The point in Corollary
4.1
is that, we can obtain that the level set of solutions are hyperplanes in any open, convex domain with the appropriate boundary conditions, utilizing the result in
[
10
]
.

As we can see in section 3, the relations between different classes of equations, allow us to obtain some explicit smooth entire solutions for the 2D and 3D Isobaric Euler equations. Those solutions can be extended when the pressure is linear function in the space variables. Some of these solutions have linear dependent components. Thus, if we impose linear dependency of the components of the initial conditions, we can obtain some explicit entire solutions and can be extended to other type of equations. In Appendix B we give some examples of smooth entire solutions of the Navier-Stokes equations with linear dependent components of the initial conditions.

One of the observations in this paper, is to view the equipartition as the Eikonal equation. As stated in Proposition
2.1
, the Eikonal equation can be transformed to the Euler equations with constant pressure (without the divergence free condition). Thus, solutions of the Allen Cahn equations that satisfy the equipartition can be transformed into the Euler equations with constant pressure, and we obtain the divergence free condition from the Allen Cahn equations.

Furthermore, we state this result to the equation
a(u)\Delta u+b(u)|\nabla u|^{2}=c(u)
, under the hypothesis that
u=\Phi(v)
for some
v
that is also in this class of equation. This hypothesis is quite reasonable since the equation
a(u)\Delta u+b(u)|\nabla u|^{2}=c(u)
is invariant under such transformations, in the sence that if
u
is a solution then
v=F(u)
is also in this class of equations.

In the last section, we propose an analogue of the De Giorgi type result for the vector Allen-Cahn equations and we will prove that entire solutions of the Allen-Cahn system in dimension 2 that satisfy the equipartition have such a specific structure. Finally, we apply the Helmholtz-Leray decomposition
in the Allen-Cahn system and obtain an equation, independent from the potential
W
. Then we apply the Leray projection (i.e. only the divergence free term from the decomposition) and we can determine explicit entire solutions. In Appendix A, we give some examples of such solutions and compare them to the structure we have obtained from Theorem
5.1
. One such example, for a particular potential
W\geq 0
with finite number of global minima has the property that
\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}u(x,y)=a^{\pm}
, where
a^{\pm}\in\{W=0\}
and
\lim_{y\rightarrow\pm\infty}u(x,y)=U^{\pm}(x)
where
U^{\pm}
are heteroclinic connections of the system (i.e.
U^{\pm^{\prime\prime}}=W_{u}(U^{\pm})
). If fact, we can have infinitely many such solutions

2.
The Allen-Cahn equation and the equipartition

2.1.
The equipartition of the energy and the Euler equations

First, we begin with a transformation that relates the Eikonal equation and the Euler equation (with constant pressure and without the incompressibility condition). Note that
x_{n}
plays the role of the “time parameter” and
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
instead of
x_{n}>0
. We could choose any of
x_{i}\;,\;i=1,...,n\;,\;n\geq 2
as a “time parameter”, supposing the monotonicity condition with respect to
x_{i}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
v:\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth solution of

(2.1)

|\nabla v|^{2}=G(v)

where
G:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function and suppose that
v_{x_{n}}>0
.

Then the vector field
F=(F_{1},...,F_{n-1})
where
F_{i}=\dfrac{v_{x_{i}}}{v_{x_{n}}}\;,\;i=1,...,n-1
satisfies the Euler equations

(2.2)

F_{x_{n}}+F{\nabla}_{y}F=0\;\;,\;y=(x_{1},...,x_{n-1})

Proof.

Differentiating (
2.1
) over
x_{i}
gives

(2.3)

2\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{x_{j}}v_{x_{j}x_{i}}=G^{\prime}(v)v_{x_{i}}\;\;,\;i=1,...,n

Now we have

(2.4)

F_{i\>x_{j}}=\frac{v_{x_{i}x_{j}}v_{x_{n}}-v_{x_{i}}v_{x_{n}x_{j}}}{v_{x_{n}}^{2}}\;\;,\;j=1,...,n

(2.5)

\Rightarrow F_{j}F_{i\>x_{j}}=\frac{v_{x_{j}}v_{x_{i}x_{j}}v_{x_{n}}-v_{x_{j}}v_{x_{i}}v_{x_{n}x_{j}}}{v_{x_{n}}^{3}}

Thus, by (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) (for
i=1,...,n-1
), we have

(2.6)

\begin{gathered}F_{i\>x_{n}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}F_{j}F_{i\>x_{j}}=\\
\frac{v_{x_{i}x_{n}}v_{x_{n}}^{2}-v_{x_{n}}v_{x_{i}}v_{x_{n}x_{n}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(v_{x_{j}}v_{x_{i}x_{j}}v_{x_{n}}-v_{x_{j}}v_{x_{i}}v_{x_{n}x_{j}})}{v_{x_{n}}^{3}}=\\
\frac{v_{x_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{x_{j}}v_{x_{j}x_{i}}-v_{x_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{x_{j}}v_{x_{j}x_{n}}}{v_{x_{n}}^{3}}\end{gathered}

finally, by (
2.3
), the last equation becomes

(2.7)

F_{i\>x_{n}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}F_{j}F_{i\>x_{j}}=\frac{v_{x_{n}}\frac{G^{\prime}(v)}{2}v_{x_{i}}-v_{x_{i}}\frac{G^{\prime}(v)}{2}v_{x_{n}}}{v_{x_{n}}^{3}}=0

(2.8)

\Rightarrow F_{i\>x_{n}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}F_{j}F_{i\>x_{j}}=0\;\;,\;i=1,...,n-1

∎

Remark 2.2
.

Note that since
v_{x_{n}}>0
it holds that
v(\Omega)\cap\{G=0\}=\emptyset
. Indeed, if
v(x_{0})\in\{G=0\}\Rightarrow|\nabla v(x_{0})|^{2}=0
which contradicts
v_{x_{n}}>0
. So, by setting
\tilde{v}=P(v)
, where
P^{\prime}(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{G(v)}}
we have
\nabla\tilde{v}=P^{\prime}(v)\nabla v\Rightarrow|\nabla\tilde{v}|^{2}=(P^{\prime}(v))^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}\Rightarrow|\nabla\tilde{v}|^{2}=1.
Thus
\tilde{v}
satisfies
|\nabla\tilde{v}|^{2}=1
and
F_{i}=\dfrac{v_{x_{i}}}{v_{x_{n}}}=\dfrac{{\tilde{v}}_{x_{i}}}{{\tilde{v}}_{x_{n}}}.

So, at first, it seems that this transformation can be inverted:
F_{1}^{2}+...+F_{n-1}^{2}=\dfrac{{\tilde{v}}_{x_{1}}^{2}+...+{\tilde{v}}_{x_{n-1}}^{2}}{{\tilde{v}}_{x_{n}}^{2}}=\dfrac{1}{{\tilde{v}}_{x_{n}}^{2}}-1\Rightarrow{\tilde{v}}_{x_{n}}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{F_{1}^{2}+...+F_{n-1}^{2}+1}}

(2.9)

\Rightarrow\tilde{v}=\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{F_{1}^{2}+...+F_{n-1}^{2}+1}}dx_{n}+a(x_{1},...,x_{n-1})

That is, if
F_{i}\;,\;i=1,...,n-1
satisfies the Euler equations
F_{x_{n}}+F{\nabla}_{y}F=0
, then
v
defined by (
2.9
) will satisfy the Eikonal equation. This statement is true for
n=2
(see
[
2
]
). But to generalize for
n\geq 3
it appears that further assumptions are needed. So, the class of solutions of the Euler equations with constant pressure seem to be “richer” in some sense than the class of solutions of the Eikonal equation, that is, for every smooth solution of the Eikonal equation, we can obtain a solution of the Euler equation, but not vice versa.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
u,v:\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
u_{x_{n}}>0
satisfy the equations

(2.10)

\begin{gathered}a(u)\Delta u+b(u)|\nabla u|^{2}=f(u)\\
k(v)\Delta v+l(v)|\nabla v|^{2}=g(v)\end{gathered}

and suppose that
u=\Phi(v)
for some
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\;(\Phi^{\prime}\neq 0)
and
p(t)\neq 0\;,\;a(t)\neq 0
, where
\\
p(t):=k(t)a(\Phi(t))\Phi^{\prime\prime}(t)+k(t)b(\Phi(t))(\Phi^{\prime}(t))^{2}-l(t)a(\Phi(t))\Phi^{\prime}(t)\>
.

Then the vector field
F=(F_{1},...,F_{n-1})
defined as
F_{i}=\dfrac{u_{x_{i}}}{u_{x_{n}}}\>,\\
i=1,..,n-1
, will satisfy the Euler equations

(2.11)

F_{x_{n}}+F{\nabla}_{y}F=0\;\;\;,\;y=(x_{1},...,x_{n-1})

Also,
div_{y}F=0
if and only if
\Phi
is a solution of the ODE

(2.12)

\displaystyle a(\Phi(t))\Phi^{\prime}(t)G^{\prime}(t)+2[b(\Phi(t))(\Phi^{\prime}(t))^{2}+a(\Phi(t))\Phi^{\prime\prime}(t)]G(t)=2f(\Phi(t))

where
G(t):=\dfrac{k(t)f(\Phi(t))-g(t)a(\Phi(t))\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{p(t)}\;\;\;(p
as defined above)

Proof.

We have
u=\Phi(v)
and
\nabla u=\Phi^{\prime}(v)\nabla v
, therefore

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\Delta u=\Phi^{\prime}(v)\Delta v+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v)|\nabla v|^{2}\\
\Rightarrow f(u)-b(u)|\nabla u|^{2}=a(\Phi(v))(\Phi^{\prime}(v)\Delta v+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v)|\nabla v|^{2})\\
\Rightarrow f(\Phi(v))-b(\Phi(v))(\Phi^{\prime}(v))^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}=a(\Phi(v))(\Phi^{\prime}(v)\Delta v+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v)|\nabla v|^{2})\\
\end{gathered}

(2.13)

\Rightarrow\Delta v=\dfrac{f(\Phi(v))-[b(\Phi(v))(\Phi^{\prime}(v))^{2}+a(\Phi(v))\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v)]\>|\nabla v|^{2}}{a(\Phi(v))\Phi^{\prime}(v)}\;\;\;(a,\Phi^{\prime}\neq 0)

since
u
is a solution of
a(u)\Delta u+b(u)|\nabla u|^{2}=f(u).\\

Now, since
v
is also solution of the second equation in (
2.10
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}k(v)(\dfrac{f(\Phi(v))-[b(\Phi(v))(\Phi^{\prime}(v))^{2}+a(\Phi(v))\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v)]\>|\nabla v|^{2}}{a(\Phi(v))\Phi^{\prime}(v)})+l(v)|\nabla v|^{2}=g(v)\\
\Leftrightarrow p(v)|\nabla v|^{2}=k(v)f(\Phi(v))-a(\Phi(v))\Phi^{\prime}(v)g(v)\end{gathered}

where
p(v)=k(v)a(\Phi(v))\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v)+k(v)b(\Phi(v))(\Phi^{\prime}(v))^{2}-l(v)a(\Phi(v))\Phi^{\prime}(v)
.

By hypothesis
p\neq 0
, thus

(2.14)

|\nabla v|^{2}=G(v)

where

(2.15)

G(v)=\frac{k(v)f(\Phi(v))-g(v)a(\Phi(v))\Phi^{\prime}(v)}{p(v)}

Also note that
F_{i}=\dfrac{u_{x_{i}}}{u_{x_{n}}}=\dfrac{v_{x_{i}}}{v_{x_{n}}}.\\

So we apply Proposition
2.1
and we obtain that

(2.16)

\begin{gathered}F_{i\>x_{n}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}F_{j}F_{i\>x_{j}}=0\;\;,\;i=1,...,n-1\\
\Leftrightarrow F_{x_{n}}+F{\nabla}_{y}F=0\end{gathered}

Now, for the divergence of
F
:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}F_{i\>x_{i}}=\frac{v_{x_{i}x_{i}}v_{x_{n}}-v_{x_{i}}v_{x_{n}x_{i}}}{v_{x_{n}}^{2}}\\
\Rightarrow div_{y}F=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}F_{i\>x_{i}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}v_{x_{i}x_{i}}v_{x_{n}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}v_{x_{i}}v_{x_{n}x_{i}}}{v_{x_{n}}^{2}}\end{gathered}

(2.17)

\Rightarrow div_{y}F=\frac{v_{x_{n}}\Delta v-\frac{1}{2}(|\nabla v|^{2})_{x_{n}}}{v_{x_{n}}^{2}}

Thus, from (
2.13
) and (
2.14
) the equation (
2.17
) becomes:

\displaystyle div_{y}F=\frac{v_{x_{n}}\frac{f(\Phi(v))-[b(\Phi(v))(\Phi^{\prime}(v))^{2}+a(\Phi(v))\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v)]\>G(v)}{a(\Phi(v))\Phi^{\prime}(v)}-\frac{G^{\prime}(v)}{2}v_{x_{n}}}{v_{x_{n}}^{2}}

Therefore

\displaystyle div_{y}F=0\Leftrightarrow a(\Phi(v))\Phi^{\prime}(v)G^{\prime}(v)+2[b(\Phi(v))(\Phi^{\prime}(v))^{2}+a(\Phi(v))\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v)]G(v)=2f(\Phi(v))

∎

3.
Entire solutions of the Euler equations

In this subsection we will determine some smooth entire solutions of the 2D and 3D Euler equations and the pressure being a linear function with respect to the space variables.

We begin by illustrating an analogy for steady solutions of the incompressible Euler equations in two space dimensions and the De Giorgi conjecture.

Let
u=(u_{1},u_{2}):\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\;,\;u_{i}=u_{i}(x,t)\;,\;x=(x_{1},x_{2})
be a smooth solution of the Euler equations. The incompressibility condition
div\>u=0
gives that there exists a (unique up to an additive constant) stream function
\psi(x,t)
such that

\displaystyle u=(-\psi_{x_{2}},\psi_{x_{1}})

In addition, by Proposition 2.2 in
[
16
]
, a stream function
\psi
on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
defines a steady solution (i.e. time independent) of the 2D Euler equation on
\Omega
if and only if

\displaystyle\Delta\psi=F(\psi)\;,\;\textrm{for some function}\;\>F

So, if
\psi
is a bounded, entire solution such that
\psi_{x_{2}}\geq 0
, then by De Giorgi’s conjecture (see Theorem 1.1 in
[
13
]
) it holds that

\displaystyle\psi(x_{1},x_{2})=g(ax_{1}+bx_{2})

Therefore we raise the following question.

Question:
Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\;,\;(u=u(x,y,t)=(u_{1},u_{2}))
be a smooth, bounded entire solution of the Isobaric 2D Euler equations

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{1\>t}+u_{1}u_{1\>x}+u_{2}u_{1\>y}=0\\
u_{2\>t}+u_{1}u_{2\>x}+u_{2}u_{2\>y}=0\\
u_{1\>x}+u_{2\>y}=0\end{cases}

Is it true that then

(3.2)

u_{1}=c_{1}\>g(\beta x+\gamma y-(\beta\tilde{c}_{1}+\gamma\tilde{c}_{2})t)+\tilde{c}_{1}\;\;,\;\>u_{2}=c_{2}g(\beta x+\gamma y-(\beta\tilde{c}_{1}+\gamma\tilde{c}_{2})t)+\tilde{c}_{2}\;\;\;?

where
c_{1}\beta+c_{2}\gamma=0\;\;,\;c_{1},c_{2},\tilde{c}_{1},\tilde{c}_{2},\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}.\\
\\

From the form of solution (
3.2
) we can obtain a solution of the 2D Euler equation with pressure being a linear function in respect to the space variables.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\;,\;(u=u(x,y,t)=(u_{1},u_{2}))
is such that

(3.3)

\begin{gathered}u_{1}=c_{1}\>g(\beta x+\gamma y-(\beta\tilde{c}_{1}+\gamma\tilde{c}_{2})t)+\lambda A(t)+\tilde{c}_{1}\;\;,\;\>u_{2}=c_{2}g(\beta x+\gamma y-(\beta\tilde{c}_{1}+\gamma\tilde{c}_{2})t)+\xi A(t)+\tilde{c}_{2}\\

Then
u=(u_{1},u_{2})

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{1\>t}+u_{1}u_{1\>x}+u_{2}u_{1\>y}=-p_{x}\\

Now we give some examples of smooth entire solutions for the three dimensional Euler equations. If

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
u=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\;\>,\;u_{i}\>,p:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and consider the initial value problem

(3.10)

\begin{cases}u_{t}+u\nabla u=-\nabla p\\
\textrm{div}\>u=0\\
u(x,y,z,0)=g(x,y,z)\end{cases}

where

4.
Applications

As we will see now, in dimension 3 we can deduce the Allen Cahn equation (together with the equipartition of the energy) into the minimal surface equation of dimension 2 and then apply Bernstein’s result to conclude that the level sets of the solution are hyperplanes. However, the one dimensionality of entire solutions that satisfy the equipartition is a special case of Theorem 5.1 in
[
6
]
and it was also known by Modica and Mortola (see the final remark in
[
17
]
). However, the result in Corollary
4.1
below holds for any open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so by imposing the appropriate boundary conditions, utilizing the result in
[
10
]
, we can obtain the result for any convex domain.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
u\in C^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
be a solution of
\Delta u=W^{\prime}(u)
such that
u_{z}>0
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an open, convex set. If
u
satisfies

(4.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}=W(u)

then there exists a function
\psi
such that
\psi_{y}=-\dfrac{u_{x}}{u_{z}}\;,\;\psi_{x}=\dfrac{u_{y}}{u_{z}}
that satisfies the minimal surface equation

\displaystyle\psi_{yy}(\psi_{x}^{2}+1)-2\psi_{x}\psi_{y}\psi_{xy}+\psi_{xx}(\psi_{y}^{2}+1)=0

In particular, if
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{3}
or if
\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
u_{x}=au_{z}\;,\;u_{y}=bu_{z}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega
, then the level sets of
u
are hyperplanes.

Proof.

From Theorem
2.3
we have that
div_{(x,y)}F=0
, thus there exists some
\psi=\psi(x,y,z)\>:F_{1}=-\psi_{y}
and
F_{2}=\psi_{x}\>.

As we noted in Remark 2.1,
u(\Omega)\cap\{W=0\}=\emptyset\>
(by (
4.1
) and since
u_{z}>0
).

So we set
v=G(u)
, with
G^{\prime}(u)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2W(u)}}
, thus

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}|\nabla v|^{2}=1\;\;\;\;\textrm{and}\;\;\;F_{1}=\frac{u_{x}}{u_{z}}=\frac{v_{x}}{v_{z}}\;,\;F_{2}=\frac{u_{y}}{u_{z}}=\frac{v_{y}}{v_{z}}\\
\Rightarrow F_{1}^{2}+F_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{v_{z}^{2}}-1\Rightarrow v_{z}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{F_{1}^{2}+F_{2}^{2}+1}}\\
v_{zx}=\frac{-F_{1}F_{1x}-F_{2}F_{2x}}{(F_{1}^{2}+F_{2}^{2}+1)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\end{gathered}

and
v_{x}=F_{1}v_{z}=\dfrac{F_{1}}{\sqrt{F_{1}^{2}+F_{2}^{2}+1}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow v_{xz}=\frac{F_{1z}(F_{1}^{2}+F_{2}^{2}+1)-F_{1}(F_{1}F_{1z}+F_{2}F_{2z})}{(F_{1}^{2}+F_{2}^{2}+1)^{\frac{3}{2}}}

Also, by Proposition
2.1
,
F
satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{cases}F_{1z}+F_{1}F_{1x}+F_{2}F_{1y}=0\\
F_{2z}+F_{1}F_{2x}+F_{2}F_{2y}=0\end{cases}

and therefore, from the fact that
v_{zx}=v_{xz}
since

, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}F_{1z}(F_{2}^{2}+1)-F_{1}F_{2}F_{2z}+F_{1}F_{1x}+F_{2}F_{2x}=0\\
\Rightarrow-F_{1}F_{2}F_{1x}-F_{2}^{2}F_{1y}-F_{1y}+F_{1}^{2}F_{2x}+F_{1}F_{2}F_{2y}+F_{2x}=0\\
\Leftrightarrow\psi_{yy}(\psi_{x}^{2}+1)-2\psi_{x}\psi_{y}\psi_{xy}+\psi_{xx}(\psi_{y}^{2}+1)=0\end{gathered}

Finally, if
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by Berstein’s theorem (see Theorem 1.21
[
8
]
)
\psi
must be a plane (in respect to the variables
(x,y)
, since
\psi_{xx}=\psi_{xy}=\psi_{yy}=0
):

{\psi}_{x}=b(z)
and
{\psi}_{y}=-a(z)
(for some functions
a,b:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
)

\Rightarrow\psi(x,y,z)=b(z)x-a(z)y+c(z)
. This gives:
F_{1}=-{\psi}_{y}=a(z)\;\;,\;F_{2}={\psi}_{x}=b(z)

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\Rightarrow\frac{u_{x}}{u_{z}}=a(z)\;\;\;\;\textrm{and}\;\;\;\frac{u_{y}}{u_{z}}=b(z)\\
\Rightarrow u(x,y,z)=G(s,y)=H(t,x)\\
\textrm{where}\;\>s=x+\int\frac{1}{a(z)}dz\;\;,\;t=y+\int\frac{1}{b(z)}dz\end{gathered}

Now we have

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}u_{x}=a(z)u_{z}\Rightarrow H_{x}=\frac{a}{b}H_{t}\;\>(H_{t}\neq 0\;\>\textrm{since}\;\>u_{z}>0)\\
\textrm{and}\;\>\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}=W(u)\Rightarrow\frac{1}{2}[H_{x}^{2}+H_{t}^{2}(1+\frac{1}{b^{2}})]=W(H)\end{gathered}

Differentiating the last equation with respect to

respectively (and utilizing

), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{a}{b}H_{t}H_{xt}+H_{t}H_{tt}(1+\frac{1}{b^{2}})=W^{\prime}H_{t}\\
\frac{a}{b^{2}}H_{t}H_{xt}+H_{t}H_{tt}(\frac{1}{b}+\frac{1}{b^{3}})-H_{t}^{2}\frac{b^{\prime}}{b^{3}}=W^{\prime}\frac{H_{t}}{b}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow-H_{t}^{2}\frac{b^{\prime}}{b^{2}}=0\Rightarrow b^{\prime}=0

thus,

. Arguing similarly for

Therefore,

\displaystyle u(x,y,z)=h(ax+by+z)

where

\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{W^{\prime}(h(t))}{a^{2}+b^{2}+1}

In the case where
\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{3}

∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a non constant, smooth subsolution of
\Delta u\leq W^{\prime}(u)\;,\\
W:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
, except perhaps on a closed set
S
of potential singularities with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(S)=0
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S
is connected, such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}=W(u)

where
\mathcal{H}^{1}
is the Hausdorff 1-measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then

\displaystyle u(x)=g(a\cdot x+b)\;\;\;,\;\;\textrm{for some}\;\>a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\>,\>|a|=1\;,\;b\in\mathbb{R}

and
g
is such that
g^{\prime\prime}=W^{\prime}(g).

Proof.

First we see that
W
is strictly positive in
u(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S)
. Indeed, if there exists
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S
such that
W(u(x_{0}))=0
, then
u
is a constant by Corollary 3.1 in
[
1
]
and since
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S
is connected.

So let
v=G(u)
, where
G^{\prime}(u)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2W(u)}}
, then

\displaystyle|\nabla v|^{2}=(G^{\prime}(u))^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}=1\;\;\;,\;\;\textrm{on}\;\>\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S

so
v
is a smooth solution of the Eikonal equation except perhaps of a closed set
S
of potential singularities with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(S)=0
. Thus from the result of
[
5
]
, we have that
v=a\cdot x+b\;\;,\;a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;,\>|a|=1\;,\;b\in\mathbb{R}
or
v=|x-x_{0}|+c
for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\;,\;c\in\mathbb{R}.\\

Therefore,

\displaystyle u(x)=G^{-1}(a\cdot x+b)\;\;\>\textrm{or}\;\>u(x)=G^{-1}(|x-x_{0}|+c)

where
G:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, such that
G^{\prime}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2W}}
.

If
u=G^{-1}(d+c)
where

, then

\displaystyle\Delta u=(G^{-1})^{\prime\prime}(d+c)+\frac{n-1}{d}(G^{-1})^{\prime}(d+c)\leq W^{\prime}(u)=W^{\prime}(G^{-1}(d+c))

and also,

\displaystyle|\nabla u|^{2}=(G^{-1^{\prime}}(d+c))^{2}=2W(u)\Rightarrow(G^{-1})^{\prime}(d+c)=\sqrt{2W(G^{-1}(d+c))}

and thus,
(G^{-1})^{\prime\prime}=W^{\prime}(G^{-1})
, so we obtain

\displaystyle(G^{-1})^{\prime\prime}(d+c)+\frac{n-1}{d}(G^{-1})^{\prime}(d+c)\leq(G^{-1})^{\prime\prime}(d+c)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(G^{-1})^{\prime}(d+c)=0\Rightarrow\sqrt{2W(G^{-1}(d+c))}=0

which contradicts the fact that

is strictly positive in

.

Therefore

where

.

5.
The Allen Cahn system

5.1.
Applications of the Equipartition

We begin by proposing a De Giorgi like result for the Allen Cahn systems for solutions that satisfy the equipartition of the energy or as an analogy of
[
6
]
in the vector case. First, the property that the level sets of a solution are hyperplanes can be expressed equivalently as
\dfrac{u_{x_{i}}}{u_{x_{n}}}=c_{i}\;,\;\>i=1,...,n-1\;\>(u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\;\>,\;u_{x_{n}}>0
), that is, if we consider
v_{i}=\dfrac{u_{x_{i}}}{u_{x_{n}}}\;\>,\;i=1,...,n,\;\>v_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, then

\displaystyle v_{i}=c_{i}\;\>,\;i=1,...,n-1\Leftrightarrow rank(\nabla v_{i})<1\;\>,\;i=1,...,n-1

We can see the above statement as follows,
If
v_{i}=c_{i}\;\>,\;i=1,...,n-1
then
\nabla v_{i}=0\Rightarrow rank(\nabla v_{i})=0<1\\

Conversely, if
rank(\nabla v_{i})<1
, that is
rank(\nabla v_{i})=0
since
v_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we have by Sard’s theorem that
\mathcal{L}^{1}(v_{i}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))=0\;\>,\;i=1,...,n-1
(see for example
[
18
]
) where
\mathcal{L}^{1}
is the lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. Thus,
\mathcal{L}^{1}(v_{i}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))=0\Rightarrow v_{i}=c_{i}
(constant)
i=1,...,n-1.\\

Now, we can generalize the above to the vector case as follows:

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}\;\>,\;u=(u_{1},...,u_{m})\;\>,\;u_{i}=u_{i}(x_{1},...,x_{n})
, we consider the functions

\displaystyle v_{ij}=\frac{u_{i\>x_{j}}}{u_{i\>x_{n}}}\;\>,\;i=1,...,m\;\>,\;j=1,...,n-1

and
\tilde{v}^{k}=(v_{1k},...,v_{mk})\;\>,\;\tilde{v}^{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}\;\>,\;k=1,...,n-1
and
\nabla\tilde{v}^{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Thus, if
u
is a solution of the Allen Cahn system, we could ask (under appropriate assumptions) whether
rank(\nabla\tilde{v}^{k})<\min\{n,m\}=\mu
(and by Sard’s Theorem we would have that
\mathcal{L}^{\mu}(\tilde{v}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))=0
, where
\mathcal{L}^{\mu}
is the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{\mu}).\\

Apart from
u
being a solution of the Allen Cahn system (and
u_{i\>x_{n}}>0)
) we should need further assumptions, as in the scalar case. The geometric analog in the vector case is far more complicated than in the scalar case. In particular, there is a relationship with minimizing partitions. However, one possible assumption would be that
u
also satisfies the equipartition, i.e.
\dfrac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}=W(u)
. We will now prove that the above is true, at least for
n=m=2
, that is, if
\tilde{v}=(v_{1},v_{2})\;,\;v_{i}=\dfrac{u_{i\>x}}{u_{i\>y}}
and
u=(u_{1},u_{2})
is a solution of the Allen-Cahn system that satisfy the equipartition, then
rank(\nabla v)<2
. In fact, we can obtain a quite stronger result about the structure of solutions in two dimensions, as stated in Theorem
5.1
that follows.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a smooth solution of

(5.1)

\Delta u=W_{u}(u)

with
u_{iy}>0\;,\;i=1,2
and
W:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
smooth.
If
u
satisfies

(5.2)

\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}=W(u)

Then

(5.3)

\textrm{either}\;\;\;\>u(x,y)=(U_{1}(c_{1}x+y),U_{2}(c_{2}x+y))\;\;\;,\;\>\textrm{where}\;\>U_{i}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{W_{U_{i}}(U_{1},U_{2})}{c_{i}^{2}+1}\;\>i=1,2

(5.4)

\textrm{or}\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\begin{cases}h(\dfrac{u_{1x}}{u_{1y}},\dfrac{u_{2x}}{u_{2y}})=0\;\;\;,\\
\textrm{and}\;\;\;u_{2y}^{2}h_{v_{1}}-u_{1y}^{2}h_{v_{2}}=0\end{cases}

for some
\;\>h:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.

In particular,
\mathcal{L}^{2}(v(\mathbb{R}^{2}))=0
, where
v=(\dfrac{u_{1x}}{u_{1y}},\dfrac{u_{2x}}{u_{2y}}).

Proof.

We differentiate (
5.2
) with respect to
x\;,\>y

(5.5)

\begin{cases}u_{1x}u_{1xx}+u_{1y}u_{1yx}+u_{2x}u_{2xx}+u_{2y}u_{2yx}=W_{u_{1}}u_{1x}+W_{u_{2}}u_{2x}\\
u_{1x}u_{1xy}+u_{1y}u_{1yy}+u_{2x}u_{2xy}+u_{2y}u_{2yy}=W_{u_{1}}u_{1y}+W_{u_{2}}u_{2y}\end{cases}

and utilizing (
5.1
) we get

(5.6)

\begin{cases}u_{1x}u_{1xx}+u_{1y}u_{1yx}+u_{2x}u_{2xx}+u_{2y}u_{2yx}=u_{1x}\Delta u_{1}+u_{2x}\Delta u_{2}\\
u_{1x}u_{1xy}+u_{1y}u_{1yy}+u_{2x}u_{2xy}+u_{2y}u_{2yy}=u_{1y}\Delta u_{1}+u_{2y}\Delta u_{2}\end{cases}

(5.7)

\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}u_{1y}u_{1yx}+u_{2y}u_{2yx}=u_{1x}u_{1yy}+u_{2x}u_{2yy}\\
u_{1x}u_{1xy}+u_{2x}u_{2xy}=u_{1y}u_{1xx}+u_{2y}u_{2xx}\end{cases}

Now we define
v_{i}:=\dfrac{u_{ix}}{u_{iy}}\;,\;i=1,2
and by the second equation in (
5.7
) we have

(5.8)

\begin{gathered}v_{1x}=\frac{u_{1xx}u_{1y}-u_{1x}u_{1yx}}{u_{1y}^{2}}=\frac{u_{2x}u_{2xy}-u_{2y}u_{2xx}}{u_{1y}^{2}}=-\frac{u_{2y}^{2}}{u_{1y}^{2}}v_{2x}\\
\Leftrightarrow u_{1y}^{2}v_{1x}+u_{2y}^{2}v_{2x}=0\end{gathered}

similarly by the first equation in (
5.7
) we have

(5.9)

u_{1y}^{2}v_{1y}+u_{2y}^{2}v_{2y}=0

From (
5.8
), (
5.9
) and the assumption
u_{iy}>0\;,\;i=1,2
we obtain that

(5.10)

v_{1x}v_{2y}-v_{1y}v_{2x}=0\Leftrightarrow det(\nabla v)=0\;\;,\;\forall\;(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

Since
det(\nabla v)=0
, we have that
rank(\nabla v)<2
and by Sard’s Theorem (see for example
[
18
]
, p. 20) we have that
\mathcal{L}^{2}(v(\mathbb{R}^{2}))=0
. By Theorem 1.4.14 in
[
18
]
, since
rank(\nabla v)<2
, we have that
v_{1},v_{2}
are functionally dependent, that is, there exists a smooth function
h:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

(5.11)

h(v_{1},v_{2})=0\Leftrightarrow h(\frac{u_{1x}}{u_{1y}},\frac{u_{2x}}{u_{2y}})=0\;\;,\;\forall\;(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

Thus we have

(5.12)

h_{v_{1}}v_{1x}+h_{v_{2}}v_{2x}=0\;\;\textrm{and}\;\>h_{v_{1}}v_{1y}+h_{v_{2}}v_{2y}=0

so, together with (
5.8
), (
5.9
) we get

(5.13)

(u_{1y}^{2}h_{v_{2}}-u_{2y}^{2}h_{v_{1}})v_{2x}=0\;\;\textrm{and}\;\>(u_{1y}^{2}h_{v_{2}}-u_{2y}^{2}h_{v_{1}})v_{2y}=0

which gives

(5.14)

\begin{gathered}v_{2x}=0\;\;\textrm{and}\;\;v_{2y}=0\\
\textrm{or}\;\;u_{1y}^{2}h_{v_{2}}-u_{2y}^{2}h_{v_{1}}=0\end{gathered}

in the first case we also have

(5.15)

v_{1x}=0\;\;\textrm{and}\;\;v_{1y}=0

and therefore

(5.16)

\begin{gathered}\frac{u_{1x}}{u_{1y}}=c_{1}\;\;\textrm{and}\;\;\frac{u_{2x}}{u_{2y}}=c_{2}\Rightarrow u_{1}(x,y)=U_{1}(c_{1}x+y)\;\;\textrm{and}\;\;u_{2}(x,y)=U_{2}(c_{2}x+y)\end{gathered}

where

\displaystyle U_{i}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{W_{U_{i}}(U_{1},U_{2})}{c_{i}^{2}+1}\;\>i=1,2.

In the second case we see that both equations of (
5.4
) are satisfied.
∎

Note:
If
W(u_{1},u_{2})=W_{1}(u_{1})+W_{2}(u_{2})
, then (
5.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\Delta u_{i}=W_{i}^{\prime}(u_{i})\;\;,\;i=1,2

so, by analogy with the scalar case we should suppose
u_{iy}>0
as we see in Theorem
5.1
above.

5.2.
The Leray projection on the Allen-Cahn system

We begin with a calculation with which we will obtain an equation independent of the potential
W.\\

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a smooth solution of the system

(5.17)

\Delta u=W_{u}(u)\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}\Delta u_{1}=W_{u_{1}}(u_{1},u_{2})\\
\Delta u_{2}=W_{u_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})\end{cases}

where
W:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a
C^{2}
potential.

From (
5.17
), differentiating over
x\;,\>y
we obtain

(5.18)

\begin{cases}\Delta u_{1y}=W_{u_{1}u_{1}}u_{1y}+W_{u_{1}u_{2}}u_{2y}\\
\Delta u_{1x}=W_{u_{1}u_{1}}u_{1x}+W_{u_{1}u_{2}}u_{2x}\\
\Delta u_{2y}=W_{u_{2}u_{1}}u_{1y}+W_{u_{2}u_{2}}u_{2y}\\
\Delta u_{2x}=W_{u_{2}u_{1}}u_{1x}+W_{u_{2}u_{2}}u_{2x}\end{cases}

and therefore

(5.19)

u_{1x}\Delta u_{1y}+u_{2x}\Delta u_{2y}=W_{u_{1}u_{1}}u_{1y}u_{1x}+W_{u_{1}u_{2}}(u_{1x}u_{2y}+u_{1y}u_{2x})+W_{u_{2}u_{2}}u_{2y}u_{2x}

thus we have

(5.20)

u_{1x}\Delta u_{1y}+u_{2x}\Delta u_{2y}=u_{1y}\Delta u_{1x}+u_{2y}\Delta u_{2x}

Now we will apply the Helmholtz-Leray decomposition, that resolves a vector field
u
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\>(n=2,3)
into the sum of a gradient and a curl vector. Regardless of any boundary conditions, for a given vector field
u
can be decomposed in the form

\displaystyle u=\nabla\phi+\tilde{\sigma}=(\phi_{x}+\tilde{\sigma}_{1},\phi_{y}+\tilde{\sigma}_{2})

where
div\>\tilde{\sigma}=0\Leftrightarrow\tilde{\sigma}_{1x}+\tilde{\sigma}_{2y}=0
since we are in two dimensions, and thus
\tilde{\sigma}_{1}=-\sigma_{y}\;,\;\tilde{\sigma}_{2}=\sigma_{x}
. So, we have that

\displaystyle u=(\phi_{x}-\sigma_{y},\phi_{y}+\sigma_{x})

for some
\phi,\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Utilizing now this decomposition of
u
, we obtain

(5.21)

\begin{gathered}(\phi_{xx}-\sigma_{yx})\Delta(\phi_{xy}-\sigma_{yy})+(\phi_{yx}+\sigma_{xx})\Delta(\phi_{yy}+\sigma_{xy})\\
=(\phi_{xy}-\sigma_{yy})\Delta(\phi_{xx}-\sigma_{yx})+(\phi_{yy}+\sigma_{xy})\Delta(\phi_{yx}+\sigma_{xx})\end{gathered}

Thus, if in particular we apply the Leray projection,
v=\mathbb{P}(u)
, we have that
v=\tilde{\sigma}
, that is,
v=(-\sigma_{y},\sigma_{x})
. So, from (
5.21
) we have

(5.22)

\begin{gathered}\sigma_{yx}\Delta\sigma_{yy}+\sigma_{xx}\Delta\sigma_{xy}=\sigma_{yy}\Delta\sigma_{yx}+\sigma_{xy}\Delta\sigma_{xx}\\
\Leftrightarrow(\sigma_{xx}-\sigma_{yy})\Delta\sigma_{xy}=\sigma_{xy}\Delta(\sigma_{xx}-\sigma_{yy})\end{gathered}

Note that a class of solutions to (
5.22
) is
\sigma
that satisfy

(5.23)

c_{1}\sigma_{xy}=c_{2}(\sigma_{xx}-\sigma_{yy})

and we can solve explicitly in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(5.24)

\begin{gathered}\sigma(x,y)=A(x)+B(y)\;\;\;,\;\;\textrm{if}\;\>c_{2}=0\\
\sigma(x,y)=F(cx+y)+G(x-cy)\;\;\;,\;\;\textrm{where}\;\>c=\dfrac{c_{1}+\sqrt{c_{1}^{2}+4c_{2}^{2}}}{2c_{2}}\;,\;\>\textrm{if}\;\>c_{2}\neq 0\end{gathered}

for arbitrary functions
A,B,F,G:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

In the first case, the Leray projection of the solution is of the form

(5.25)

\displaystyle v=\mathbb{P}(u)=(b(y),a(x))

and in the second case

(5.26)

\displaystyle v=\mathbb{P}(u)=(cg(x-cy)-f(cx+y),g(x-cy)+cf(cx+y))

Similarly, if we take the projection to the space of gradients, we have
\tilde{v}=(\phi_{x},\phi_{y})
that will also satisfy

(5.27)

(\phi_{xx}-\phi_{yy})\Delta\phi_{xy}=\phi_{xy}\Delta(\phi_{xx}-\phi_{yy})

so again, the projection to the space of gradients of the solution will be of the form

(5.28)

\begin{gathered}\textrm{either}\;\>\tilde{u}(x,y)=(\tilde{a}(x),\tilde{b}(y))\\
\textrm{or}\;\>\tilde{u}(x,y)=(c\tilde{f}(cx+y)+\tilde{g}(x-cy),\tilde{f}(cx+y)-c\tilde{g}(x-cy))\end{gathered}

Therefore, if we determine a class of potentials
W
, such that the solutions (or some solutions) are invariant under the Leray projection (or the projection to the space of gradients), we can obtain explicit solutions of the form (
5.24
) or (
5.28
). In the Appendix we give such examples.

Appendix A
Some examples of entire solutions of the Allen-Cahn system

We note that solutions of the form (
5.24
) and (
5.28
) are equivalent in the special case that (
5.23
) is satisfied. So in the class of solutions of (
5.23
) the Leray projection is, in some sense equivalent with the projection to the space of gradients.
Suppose now that
u=\nabla\phi
for some
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, that is, a solution of the Allen-Cahn system remains invariant under the projection to the space of gradients. Then, as (
5.27
) we have

(A.1)

\phi_{xy}\Delta(\phi_{xx}-\phi_{yy})=(\phi_{xx}-\phi_{yy})\Delta\phi_{xy}

So a simple solution to (A.1) is

(A.2)

\phi_{xx}-\phi_{yy}=0\Rightarrow\phi(x,y)=F(x+y)+G(x-y)

and
u(x,y)=(\phi_{x},\phi_{y})
, so in this case
u
has the form

(A.3)

u(x,y)=(f(x+y)+g(x-y),f(x+y)-g(x-y))

for some
f,g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.\\

If
u
has the form (A.3), we can see that it also satisfies the equipartition. Indeed, (
5.17
) becomes

(A.4)

\begin{cases}2f^{\prime\prime}+2g^{\prime\prime}=W_{u_{1}}\\
2f^{\prime\prime}-2g^{\prime\prime}=W_{u_{2}}\end{cases}\Rightarrow\begin{cases}2(f^{\prime\prime}+g^{\prime\prime})(f^{\prime}+g^{\prime})=W_{u_{1}}(f^{\prime}+g^{\prime})\\
2(f^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime\prime})(f^{\prime}-g^{\prime})=W_{u_{2}}(f^{\prime}-g^{\prime})\end{cases}

(A.5)

\begin{gathered}\Rightarrow 4f^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime}+4g^{\prime\prime}g^{\prime}=W_{u_{1}}(f^{\prime}+g^{\prime})+W_{u_{2}}(f^{\prime}-g^{\prime})\\
\Rightarrow 2(f^{\prime})^{2}+2(g^{\prime})^{2}=W(f+g,f-g)+c\end{gathered}

and the equipartition can be written as

(A.6)

\begin{gathered}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}=W(u)\\
\Leftrightarrow 2(f^{\prime}(x+y))^{2}+2(g^{\prime}(x-y))^{2}=W(f(x+y)+g(x-y),f(x+y)-g(x-y))\end{gathered}

(the system (A.1) remains equivalent if we add a constant to the potential)

First we note that solutions of the form (A.3) satisfy (
5.4
) in Theorem
5.1
. Indeed, if
u
is of the form (A.3),

(A.7)

\begin{gathered}u_{1}=f(x+y)+g(x-y)\;\>\textrm{and}\;\>u_{2}=f(x+y)-g(x-y)\\
\Rightarrow\frac{u_{1x}}{u_{1y}}=\frac{f^{\prime}+g^{\prime}}{f^{\prime}-g^{\prime}}=v_{1}\;\;\>\textrm{and}\;\;\>\frac{u_{2x}}{u_{2y}}=\frac{f^{\prime}-g^{\prime}}{f^{\prime}+g^{\prime}}=v_{2}\end{gathered}

so the function
h:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
in (
5.4
) is
h(s,t)=st-1
. Also,

(A.8)

\frac{u_{1y}^{2}}{u_{2y}^{2}}=\frac{(f^{\prime}-g^{\prime})^{2}}{(f^{\prime}+g^{\prime})^{2}}\;\;\>\textrm{and}\;\;\>\dfrac{h_{v_{1}}}{h_{v_{2}}}=\dfrac{v_{2}}{v_{1}}=\dfrac{(f^{\prime}-g^{\prime})^{2}}{(f^{\prime}+g^{\prime})^{2}}=\frac{u_{1y}^{2}}{u_{2y}^{2}}

Now we will see some examples of solutions to the Allen Cahn system that are not in the form (

Appendix B
Some examples of entire solutions of the Navier-Stokes equations

First we note that some solutions of the 3D Euler equations in section 3 have the form
u=(u_{1},c_{1}u_{1}+\tilde{c}_{1},c_{2}u_{1}+\tilde{c}_{2})
, that is, we have linear dependence of the components of the solution. So, now we will determine some specific examples of solutions of the Navier-Stokes equations with linear dependent components.

Let
u=(u_{1},u_{2})\;,\;u_{i}=u_{i}(x,y,t):\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

(B.1)

\begin{gathered}u_{1}(x,y,t)=c_{1}g(x-c_{1}y,t)-c_{1}A(t)+c_{2}\;\;,\;\;u_{2}(x,y,t)=g(x-c_{1}y,t)-A(t)\\
\textrm{and}\;\;\;p(x,y,t)=a(t)(c_{1}x+y)+b(t)\;\;,t>0\;\;\;,\;c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\\
\textrm{where}\;\;\;g_{t}+c_{2}g_{s}=\mu(c_{1}^{2}+1)g_{ss}\;\;,\;g=g(s,t):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\;\;\textrm{and}\;A^{\prime}(t)=a(t)\;\;,\;a,b,A:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\end{gathered}

then
u
is a solution of

(B.2)

\begin{cases}u_{1\>t}+u_{1}u_{1\>x}+u_{2}u_{1\>y}=-p_{x}+\mu\Delta u_{1}\\
u_{2\>t}+u_{1}u_{2\>x}+u_{2}u_{2\>y}=-p_{y}+\mu\Delta u_{2}\\
u_{1\>x}+u_{2\>y}=0\end{cases}\;\;\;,\;\mu>0

Similarly in the three dimensional case, we give some examples of solutions of

(B.3)

\begin{cases}u_{1\>t}+u_{1}u_{1\>x}+u_{2}u_{1\>y}+u_{3}u_{1\>z}=-p_{x}+\mu\Delta u_{1}\\
u_{2\>t}+u_{1}u_{2\>x}+u_{2}u_{2\>y}+u_{3}u_{2\>z}=-p_{y}+\mu\Delta u_{2}\\
u_{3\>t}+u_{1}u_{3\>x}+u_{2}u_{3\>y}+u_{3}u_{3\>z}=-p_{z}+\mu\Delta u_{3}\\
u_{1\>x}+u_{2\>y}+u_{3\>z}=0\end{cases}\;\;\;,\;\mu>0

Let
g=g(s,\eta,t)\;,\;g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a solution of

(B.4)

g_{t}-(\frac{\tilde{c}_{1}}{2c_{1}}+\frac{\tilde{c}_{2}}{2c_{2}})g_{s}+(\frac{\tilde{c}_{1}}{2c_{1}}-\frac{\tilde{c}_{2}}{2c_{2}})g_{\eta}=\mu(\frac{1}{4c_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1}{4c_{2}^{2}})(g_{ss}+g_{\eta\eta})+\mu g_{ss}

where
\mu>0\;,\;c_{1},c_{2},\tilde{c}_{1},\tilde{c}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
t>0
.

Then
u=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\;\;,\;u_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\;\;,\;i=1,2,3
defined as

(B.5)

\begin{gathered}u_{1}(x,y,z,t)=g(x-\frac{c_{2}y+c_{1}z}{2c_{1}c_{2}},\frac{c_{2}y-c_{1}z}{2c_{1}c_{2}},t)-A(t)\;\;\;,\;(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\;,\;t>0\\
u_{2}(x,y,z,t)=c_{1}u_{1}(x,y,z,t)+\tilde{c}_{1}\;\;\;,\;\;u_{3}(x,y,z,t)=c_{2}u_{1}(x,y,z,t)+\tilde{c}_{2}\\
\textrm{and}\;\;p(x,y,z,t)=a(t)(x+c_{1}y+c_{2}z)+b(t)\\
\textrm{where}\;\;A^{\prime}(t)=a(t)\;,\;a,A:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\end{gathered}

is a solution of (
B.3
).

Therefore we conclude to the following

Proposition B.1
.

Let
u=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\;\>,\;u_{i},\>p:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
and consider the initial value problem

(B.6)

\begin{cases}u_{t}+u\nabla u=-\nabla p+\mu\Delta u\\
\textrm{div}\>u=0\\
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}u(x,y,z,t)=h(x,y,z)\end{cases}\;\;\;,\;\mu>0\;\;,\;(x,y,z,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,+\infty)

where
h=(h_{1},c_{1}h_{1}+\tilde{c}_{1},c_{2}h_{1}+\tilde{c}_{2})
and
h_{1}(x,y,z)=H(2c_{1}c_{2}x-c_{2}y-c_{1}z)\;\;,\;c_{1},c_{2},\tilde{c}_{1},\tilde{c}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\tilde{c}_{1}c_{2}+c_{1}\tilde{c}_{2}=0
and
H
smooth.

Then there exists a smooth, globally defined in
t>0
, solution to (
B.6
).

In particular,

(B.7)

\begin{gathered}u(x,y,z,t)=(u_{1},c_{1}u_{1}+\tilde{c}_{1},c_{2}u_{1}+\tilde{c}_{2})\;\>\textrm{and}\;\>p(x,y,z,t)=a(t)(x+c_{1}y+c_{2}z)+b(t)\\
\textrm{where}\;\;u_{1}(x,y,z,t)=g(2c_{1}c_{2}x-c_{2}y-c_{1}z,t)-A(t)\\
\textrm{and}\;\;g=g(s,t)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi t}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{|s-w|^{2}}{4\tilde{\mu}t}}H(w)dw\;\;,\;\tilde{\mu}=\mu(4c_{1}^{2}c_{2}^{2}+c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2})\\
(A^{\prime}(t)=a(t)\;\>,\;A(0)=0)\end{gathered}
