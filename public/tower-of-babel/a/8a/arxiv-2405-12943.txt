Nucleon axial radius
Yi Chen, Yang Li, Cédric Lorcé, Qun Wang

Abstract
We present the first systematic study of the relativistic axial-vector four-current distributions inside a nucleon. We show in particular that the slope of the axial form factor G A ​ ( Q 2 ) G_{A}(Q^{2}) in the forward limit—conventionally denoted as R A 2 R^{2}_{A} in the literature—does not represent the three-dimensional mean-square axial radius in the Breit frame, but corresponds instead to a contribution to the mean-square spin radius. We derive explicit expressions for the latter in different frames and find in general additional contributions that depend on both the nucleon mass and the forward values of the axial-vector form factors G A ​ ( 0 ) G_{A}(0) and G P ​ ( 0 ) G_{P}(0) . This provides an additional key motivation for ongoing lattice quantum chromodynamics (QCD) calculations and future experimental measurements of the induced pseudoscalar form factor G P ​ ( Q 2 ) G_{P}(Q^{2}) .

Mean-square radii.
Fundamentally, the notion of mean-square radius characterizes the extension of a spatial distribution
\rho(\boldsymbol{r})
, and is defined as
[
57
]

\langle r^{2}\rangle\equiv\frac{\int\text{d}^{3}r\,r^{2}\rho(\boldsymbol{r})}{\int\text{d}^{3}r\,\rho(\boldsymbol{r})}=-\frac{1}{\tilde{\rho}(\boldsymbol{0})}\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\tilde{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\Big|_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\boldsymbol{0}},

(4)

where

\tilde{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=\int\text{d}^{3}r\,e^{i\boldsymbol{\Delta}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}\,\rho(\boldsymbol{r}).

(5)

This inverse Fourier transform is usually identified with some BF amplitude, i.e. a matrix element where the initial and final momenta satisfy
\boldsymbol{p}_{B}=-\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}_{B}=-\boldsymbol{\Delta}/2
.

For example, the conventional electric charge distribution of the nucleon
[
51
]
is defined as the Fourier transform of the following BF amplitude

\tilde{J}^{0}_{B}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\equiv\frac{\langle p^{\prime}_{B},\uparrow|j^{0}(0)|p_{B},\uparrow\rangle}{2M}=e\,G_{E}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{2}),

(6)

where
j^{\mu}(x)
is the electromagnetic four-current operator,
|p,s\rangle
is a covariantly normalized momentum eigenstate with canonical polarization
s
, and
e
is the positive unit of electric charge. Setting
\tilde{\rho}=\tilde{J}^{0}_{B}
in Eq. (
4
) leads directly to Eq. (
2
), since in the BF we can write
Q^{2}=\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{2}
.

This is to be contrasted with the phase-space formalism
[
58
,
59
,
57
]
, where the intrinsic relativistic electric charge distribution of the nucleon is unambiguously defined as the Fourier transform of

\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{0}_{B}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\equiv\frac{\langle p^{\prime}_{B},\uparrow|j^{0}(0)|p_{B},\uparrow\rangle}{2P^{0}_{B}}=e\,\frac{M}{P^{0}_{B}}\,G_{E}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{2}),

(7)

in agreement with older analyses
[
60
,
61
]
and the requirement that the total electric charge must transform as a Lorentz scalar
[
58
]
. The BF energy being given by
P^{0}_{B}=p^{\prime 0}_{B}=p^{0}_{B}=M\sqrt{1+\tau}
with
\tau\equiv Q^{2}/(4M^{2})
, one concludes that the relativistic mean-square charge radius of the proton is in fact given by
[
57
,
62
]

\langle r_{\text{ch}}^{2}\rangle=\langle r_{E}^{2}\rangle+\frac{3}{4M^{2}}.

(8)

The additional term, known as the Darwin-Foldy term
[
60
,
63
,
64
,
62
]
, see also the Eqs. (5b) and (5c) of Ref.
[
62

, actually appeared in Ref.
[
50
]
but was later discarded by hand by the authors to comply with the conventions in force in atomic spectroscopy
[
65
]
.

3D Breit frame distributions.
Using solely fundamental symmetries (namely Poincaré symmetry including parity, time-reversal and charge-conjugation symmetries, and the
G
-parity invariance of QCD), the matrix elements of the axial-vector four-current operator
j_{5}^{\mu}(x)=\overline{\psi}(x)\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\psi(x)
for a given quark flavor and for a generic spin-
\frac{1}{2}
target can be parametrized as
[
38
,
31
]

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\langle p^{\prime},s^{\prime}|j^{\mu}_{5}(0)|p,s\rangle=

(9)

\displaystyle\overline{u}(p^{\prime},s^{\prime})\left[\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\,G_{A}(Q^{2})+\frac{\Delta^{\mu}\gamma_{5}}{2M}\,G_{P}(Q^{2})\right]u(p,s),

where
G_{P}(Q^{2})
is the induced pseudoscalar FF, and
\Delta^{\mu}=p^{\prime\mu}-p^{\mu}
is the four-momentum transfer.

Similarly to Eq. (
7
), we define the axial-vector BF amplitudes as

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{\mu}_{B}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\equiv\frac{\langle p^{\prime}_{B},\uparrow|j_{5}^{\mu}(0)|p_{B},\uparrow\rangle}{2P^{0}_{B}}

(10)

with the nucleon spin polarized along an arbitrary direction denoted by the unit vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{0}_{B}

\displaystyle=0,

(11)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{i}_{B}

\displaystyle=\left[\hat{s}^{i}-\frac{\Delta^{i}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{s}})}{4P_{B}^{0}(P_{B}^{0}+M)}\right]G_{A}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{2})

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta^{i}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{s}})}{4MP^{0}_{B}}\,G_{P}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{2}).

Contrary to the electromagnetic case, we see that the axial charge density vanishes identically in the BF, hence there is no nontrivial axial charge radius. This can be understood from the fact that the electromagnetic four-current is timelike, whereas the axial-vector four-current is spacelike.

Although the axial charge density vanishes identically in the BF, it is still possible to provide a density interpretation for the spatial part of the axial-vector current. Using the QCD equations of motion, the rank-
3
canonical spin tensor operator can be expressed as
S^{\mu\alpha\beta}=\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\alpha\beta\lambda}j_{5\lambda}
with
\epsilon_{0123}=+1

[
66
]
. The spin density operator is then given by
S^{i}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{ijk}S^{0jk}=\tfrac{1}{2}j_{5}^{i}
. As a result, we can define a meaningful 3D nucleon spin distribution in the BF as follows
[
67
]

S^{i}_{B}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}\Delta}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,e^{-i\boldsymbol{\Delta}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}\,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{i}_{B}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}).

(12)

The 3D nucleon spin radius
r_{S}
can then be unambiguously defined as
r_{S}=\sqrt{\langle r_{\text{spin}}^{2}\rangle}
, with

\displaystyle\langle r_{\text{spin}}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\int\text{d}^{3}r\,r^{2}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{B}(\boldsymbol{r})}{\int\text{d}^{3}r\,\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{B}(\boldsymbol{r})}

(13)

\displaystyle=R_{A}^{2}+\frac{1}{4M^{2}}\left(1+\frac{2\,G_{P}(0)}{G_{A}(0)}\right).

Besides the
R^{2}_{A}
term coming from the slope of the axial FF at
Q^{2}=0
given by Eq. (
1
), we find two additional contributions associated with the
\Delta^{i}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{s}})
terms in Eq. (
11
). The latter can be understood as relativistic effects
[
68
,
57
]
which can formally be ignored only if we consider the static limit
M\to\infty
.

2D light-front distributions.
Although BF distributions provide us the best proxy for picturing in 3D a system sitting in average at rest around the origin, their lack of strict probabilistic interpretation is often regarded as a deficiency
[
69
]
. By focusing on a Galilean subgroup of the Lorentz group, one can define alternative densities with strict probabilistic interpretation
[
70
]
by using the light-front (LF) formalism, see e.g. Refs.
[
69
,
71
,
72
,
73
,
74
]
.

In the LF formalism
[
75
]
, a four-vector is represented by the set of components
a^{\mu}=[a^{+},a^{-},\boldsymbol{a}_{\perp}]
with
a^{\pm}\equiv(a^{0}\pm a^{3})/\sqrt{2}
. Since the scalar product takes the form
p\cdot x=p^{-}x^{+}+p^{+}x^{-}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}_{\perp}
, treating
x^{+}
as the LF time variable implies that
p^{-}
corresponds to the LF energy, given by
(\boldsymbol{p}^{2}_{\perp}+M^{2})/(2p^{+})
according to the on-mass-shell relation
p^{2}=M^{2}
.

Similarly to the electromagnetic distributions studied in Refs.
[
57
,
58
,
59
]
, the axial-vector LF distributions are defined via the following two-dimensional Fourier transform

\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\text{LF}}(\boldsymbol{b}_{\perp};P^{+})=\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}\Delta_{\perp}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,e^{-i\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}_{\perp}}\,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{LF}}^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp};P^{+}),

(15)

where the amplitudes with given initial and final LF helicities

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{LF}}^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp};P^{+})\equiv\frac{{{}_{\text{LF}}}\langle p^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}|j_{5}^{\mu}(0)|p,\lambda\rangle_{\text{LF}}}{2P^{+}}

(16)

are evaluated in the so-called symmetric LF frame specified by
\boldsymbol{P}_{\perp}=(\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}_{\perp}+\boldsymbol{p}_{\perp})/2=\boldsymbol{0}_{\perp}
and
\Delta^{+}=0
. Note that the latter condition amounts to an integration over the longitudinal LF coordinate
r^{-}
in position space.

Using the LF Dirac spinors in the parametrization (
9
), we find

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{LF}}^{+}

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{z})_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}\,G_{A}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}^{2}),

(17)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{LF}}^{-}

\displaystyle=-\frac{P^{-}}{P^{+}}\,(\sigma_{z})_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}\,G_{A}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}^{2}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{i}_{\perp,\text{LF}}

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{P^{+}}\left[(\sigma^{i}_{\perp})_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}+\frac{i\tilde{\Delta}^{i}_{\perp}}{2M}\,\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}\right]G_{A}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}^{2})

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta^{i}_{\perp}\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\perp}\right)_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}}{4MP^{+}}\,G_{P}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}^{2}),

where
\sigma^{i}
are the Pauli matrices and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{\perp}\equiv\boldsymbol{e}_{z}\times\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}
. The LF axial charge distribution
\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\text{LF}}(\boldsymbol{b}_{\perp})
turns out to be
P^{+}
-independent. It does not vanish in the case of a longitudinally polarized nucleon and is interpreted in the LF formalism as the quark helicity distribution
[
70
,
76
]
.

The 2D transverse mean-square helicity (or axial) radius is naturally defined on the LF for
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda
as
[
76
]

\langle b_{A}^{2}\rangle_{\text{LF}}=\frac{\int\text{d}^{2}b_{\perp}\,b^{2}_{\perp}\,\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\text{LF}}(\boldsymbol{b}_{\perp})}{\int\text{d}^{2}b_{\perp}\,\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\text{LF}}(\boldsymbol{b}_{\perp})}=\frac{2}{3}\,R_{A}^{2}.

(18)

Since LF distributions are two-dimensional, it is tempting to interpret
R^{2}_{A}
as the corresponding 3D mean-square axial radius, e.g. based on the Abel-Radon transformation
[
77
,
78
]
. This would be justified if we could demonstrate that the LF axial charge distribution is the projection of a spherically symmetric 3D distribution onto the transverse plane. Unfortunately, 3D LF distributions with strict probabilistic interpretation do not exist because of the necessary
\Delta^{+}=0
constraint
[
79
]
.

Alternatively, one can consider the longitudinal LF spin distribution given by
S_{L,\text{LF}}\equiv\frac{1}{4}\text{Tr}[\sigma_{z}\,\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\text{LF}}]
, which is nothing other than half of the light-front helicity distribution. Since by definition the (transverse) mean-square radius does not depend on the overall normalization of the distribution, we obtain naturally

\langle b_{\text{spin},L}^{2}\rangle_{\text{LF}}=\langle b_{A}^{2}\rangle_{\text{LF}}=\frac{2}{3}\,R_{A}^{2}.

(19)

If we use instead the transverse LF spin distribution
S_{T,\text{LF}}\equiv\frac{1}{4}\text{Tr}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\perp,\text{LF}}]=\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{T,\text{LF}}
, where the numerical result of
\boldsymbol{S}_{T,\text{LF}}(\boldsymbol{b}_{\perp};P^{+})
is illustrated in Fig.
2
, we find

\langle b_{\text{spin},T}^{2}\rangle_{\text{LF}}=\frac{2}{3}\,R_{A}^{2}+\frac{1}{2M^{2}}\frac{G_{P}(0)}{G_{A}(0)}.

(20)

Note that the
P^{+}
-dependence of
S_{T,\text{LF}}
enters merely as a global factor and drops out when calculating the transverse mean-square radius. The difference between longitudinal and transverse LF (transverse) mean-square spin radii is of relativistic origin (
20
), and disappears in the static limit
M\to\infty
.

2D elastic frame distributions.
BF and LF axial-vector distributions at first appearance look somewhat different. To understand how they are related to each other, we can use the phase-space formalism and define 2D elastic frame (EF) distributions
[
67
,
54
]
. Besides transverse position, these time-independent distributions depend also on the average momentum
\boldsymbol{P}=(\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{p})/2\equiv P_{z}\boldsymbol{e}_{z}
of the target, which defines the
z
-axis without loss of generality
[
58
]
. Taking the limit
P_{z}\to 0
we obtain naturally the projection of the BF distributions onto the transverse plane, while taking the infinite momentum limit
P_{z}\to\infty
we recover basically the LF distributions
[
58
,
59
,
57
]
.

Similarly to the axial-vector LF distributions (
15
), the axial-vector EF distributions are defined via the following two-dimensional Fourier transform

\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\text{EF}}(\boldsymbol{b}_{\perp};P_{z})=\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}\Delta_{\perp}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,e^{-i\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}_{\perp}}\,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{EF}}^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp};P_{z}),

(21)

where the EF amplitudes in polarization space

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{EF}}^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp};P_{z})\equiv\frac{\langle p^{\prime},s^{\prime}|j_{5}^{\mu}(0)|p,s\rangle}{2P^{0}}

(22)

are evaluated in a generic frame where
\boldsymbol{P}_{\perp}=\boldsymbol{0}_{\perp}
and
\Delta_{z}=0
so as to ensure that no energy is transferred to the system. We can therefore write
p^{\prime 0}=p^{0}=P^{0}=\sqrt{M^{2}(1+\tau)+P_{z}^{2}}
.

Evaluating the matrix elements (
9
) in a generic EF, we find

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{EF}}^{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{z}}{P^{0}}\,(\sigma_{z})_{s^{\prime}s}\,G_{A}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}^{2}),

(23)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{z,\text{EF}}

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{z})_{s^{\prime}s}\,G_{A}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}^{2}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{i}_{\perp,\text{EF}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{P^{2}}}{P^{0}}

\displaystyle\left[(\sigma^{i}_{\perp})_{s^{\prime}s}\,\cos\theta-\frac{i\tilde{\Delta}^{i}_{\perp}}{2M\sqrt{\tau}}\,\delta_{s^{\prime}s}\,\sin\theta\right]G_{A}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{2}_{\perp})

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta^{i}_{\perp}\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\perp}\right)_{s^{\prime}s}}{4P^{0}}\,\left[\frac{G_{A}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}^{2})}{P^{0}+M}+\frac{G_{P}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\perp}^{2})}{M}\right],

with the Wigner rotation given by
[
57
,
80
,
59
]

\displaystyle\cos\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{P^{0}+M(1+\tau)}{(P^{0}+M)\sqrt{1+\tau}},

(24)

\displaystyle\sin\theta

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{\tau}\,P_{z}}{(P^{0}+M)\sqrt{1+\tau}}.

Above results nicely agree with the expected Lorentz transformation law for amplitudes from the BF to a generic EF
[
81
,
82
]

\displaystyle\langle p^{\prime},s^{\prime}|j_{5}^{\mu}(0)|p,s\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{B}^{\prime},s_{B}}D^{{\dagger}(1/2)}_{s^{\prime}s_{B}^{\prime}}(p_{B}^{\prime},\Lambda)D^{(1/2)}_{s_{B}s}(p_{B},\Lambda)

(25)

\displaystyle\times\Lambda^{\mu}_{\phantom{\mu}\nu}\,\langle p_{B}^{\prime},s_{B}^{\prime}|j_{5}^{\nu}(0)|p_{B},s_{B}\rangle,

where
D^{(1/2)}
is the Wigner spin rotation matrix for a spin-
\frac{1}{2}
system
[
57
,
59
]
. It is straightforward to see that the EF amplitudes (
23
) reduce to the BF ones (
11
) with
\Delta_{z}=0
when
P_{z}\to 0
, and to the LF ones (
17
) up to some convention-dependent factors when
P_{z}\to\infty

[
59
,
57
]
.

When
P_{z}>0
and the target is longitudinally polarized, the axial density is nonzero. We can then define a meaningful 2D transverse mean-square axial radius in a generic EF for a longitudinally polarized target
s^{\prime}=s
:

\langle b_{A}^{2}\rangle_{\text{EF}}(P_{z})=\frac{\int\text{d}^{2}b_{\perp}\,b^{2}_{\perp}\,\mathcal{A}^{0}_{\text{EF}}(\boldsymbol{b}_{\perp};P_{z})}{\int\text{d}^{2}b_{\perp}\,\mathcal{A}^{0}_{\text{EF}}(\boldsymbol{b}_{\perp};P_{z})}=\frac{2}{3}\,R_{A}^{2}+\frac{1}{2E_{P}^{2}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1},}

(26)

with
E_{P}=\sqrt{M^{2}+P_{z}^{2}}
. We stress, however, that the 2D axial density is not intrinsic and arises simply because a longitudinal boost mixes the components
\mathcal{A}^{0}
and
\mathcal{A}_{z}
. In the LF formalism the boost is not necessary since one defines from the very beginning the density via the component
\mathcal{A}^{+}=(\mathcal{A}^{0}+\mathcal{A}_{z})/\sqrt{2}
.

P_{z}\to 0
, we find

\frac{\langle b_{\text{spin},L}^{2}\rangle_{\text{EF}}+2\,\langle b_{\text{spin},T}^{2}\rangle_{\text{EF}}(0)}{3}=\frac{2}{3}\,\langle r^{2}_{\text{spin}}\rangle,

(28)

in agreement with the 3D BF mean-square spin radius given in Eq. (
13
), taking into account the fact that the latter does not depend on the spin direction.

Conclusions.
We investigated in detail in this letter whether the widespread interpretation of
R_{A}^{2}
given by Eq. (
1
) as the nucleon mean-square axial radius is justified or not. In the Breit frame, where 3D spatial distributions are traditionally defined, the axial density vanishes identically (
11
). This means that the 3D mean-square nucleon axial radius does not exist, and thus the interpretation of
R_{A}
as the nucleon axial radius does not rest on sound physical grounds. In contrast, we demonstrated that a physically meaningful 3D nucleon mean-square spin radius (
13
) can be unambiguously defined in terms of the same axial-vector FFs.

Another option is to consider 2D spatial distributions in the light-front formalism. For a longitudinally polarized nucleon, the 2D transverse mean-square axial radius is equal to the transverse mean-square longitudinal spin radius, and is given by
\frac{2}{3}R^{2}_{A}
(
19
). For a transversely polarized nucleon, however, the transverse mean-square axial radius vanishes while the transverse mean-square transverse spin radius receives an additional contribution on top of the
\frac{2}{3}R^{2}_{A}
term (
20
).

An interpolation between the Breit frame and light-front results can be obtained by using the concept of 2D elastic frame distributions, based on the phase-space formalism. When the nucleon has finite average momentum
P_{z}
, the 2D transverse mean-square axial and transverse spin radii receive additional
P_{z}
-dependent contributions compared to the light-front ones, see Eqs. (
26
) and (
27
). These extra contributions formally disappear, however, in the infinite-momentum limit
P_{z}\to\infty
.

In summary, contrary to what is usually stated in the literature we did not find any proper justification for interpreting the quantity
R^{2}_{A}
as the physical 3D mean-square axial radius of a spin-
\frac{1}{2}
target (a nucleon for instance). On the other hand, the 3D mean-square spin radius
\langle r_{\text{spin}}^{2}\rangle
(
13
) is a well-defined and physically meaningful radius that characterizes more realistically the spatial extension of the weak content of the system. From a 2D perspective, however, the quantity
\frac{2}{3}R^{2}_{A}
can consistently be interpreted as the 2D transverse mean-square axial or longitudinal spin radius, albeit only for a longitudinally polarized spin-
\frac{1}{2}
target.

While our analysis does not directly impact the phenomenology of axial-vector form factors, it explicitly demonstrates that the quantity
R^{2}_{A}

alone
does not faithfully measure the spatial extension of the weak content of the nucleon. According to Eq. (
13
), the 3D mean-square spin radius
\langle r_{\text{spin}}^{2}\rangle
requires besides
R_{A}^{2}
an additional determination of the ratio
G_{P}(0)/G_{A}(0)
, which provides an additional key motivation for ongoing lattice QCD calculations and future experimental measurements in (anti)neutrino-nucleon scatterings of the induced pseudoscalar form factor
G_{P}(Q^{2})
.
