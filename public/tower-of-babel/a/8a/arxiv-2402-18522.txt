Almost device-independent certification of GME states with minimal measurements
Shubhayan Sarkar, Alexandre C. Orthey, Jr, Gautam Sharma, Remigiusz Augusiak

Abstract
Device-independent certification of quantum states allows the characterization of quantum states present inside a device by making minimal physical assumptions. A major problem in this regard is to certify quantum states using minimal resources. In this work, we consider the multipartite quantum steering scenario with an arbitrary number of parties but only one of which is trusted in the sense that the measurements performed by the trusted party are known. Consequently, the self-testing scheme is almost device-independent. Importantly, all the parties can only perform two measurements each which is the minimal number of measurements required to observe any form of quantum nonlocality. Then, we propose steering inequalities that are maximally violated by three major classes of genuinely multipartite entangled (GME) states, one, graph states of arbitrary local dimension, two, Schmidt states of arbitrary local dimension, and, three, N N -qubit generalized W states. Using the proposed inequalities, we then provide an almost device-independent certification of the above GME states.



I
Introduction

Device-independent (DI) quantum information refers to a framework in quantum information theory where protocols and tasks are designed without relying on detailed knowledge or assumptions about the devices used to implement them. In other words, DI protocols aim to achieve certain quantum information processing tasks without specifying the internal workings or characteristics of the quantum devices involved. This approach is particularly valuable for enhancing security and reliability in quantum communication and computation tasks. By removing assumptions about the devices, DI protocols provide a higher level of security against potential attacks and imperfections in the devices themselves. One of the key resources in DI quantum information processing is Bell nonlocality
Bell 1964
;
Bell 1966
, which serves as a fundamental ingredient for DI tasks such as quantum cryptography
Acín et al. 2007
, random number generation
Pironio et al. 2010
, and state and measurement certification
Yang and Navascués 2013
;
Colbeck and Renner 2011
;
Pironio et al. 2010
.

The strongest DI certification scheme is referred to as self-testing
Mayers and Yao 2004
(see also Ref.
Šupić and Bowles 2020
). It allows one to infer the form of the underlying quantum system solely from the observed measurement outcomes, without needing to know the details of how the measurements were performed. Recently, a lot of attention has been devoted to finding schemes to self-test bipartite quantum states
McKague et al. 2012
;
Bamps and Pironio 2015
;
Coladangelo et al. 2017
;
Sarkar et al. 2021
;
Kaniewski et al. 2019
and multipartite quantum states
Baccari et al. 2020a
;
McKague 2014
;
Wu et al. 2014
;
Šupić et al. 2018
;
Sarkar and Augusiak 2022
;
Santos et al. 2023
;
Šupić et al. 2022
;
Sarkar et al. 2023a
;
Panwar et al. 2023
. In particular
Šupić et al. 2022
provides a scheme to self-test any pure entangled quantum state and
Sarkar et al. 2023a
provides a scheme to self-test any quantum state.

A major problem in this regard is to find the optimal self-testing scheme, that is, using the minimal number of measurements to certify a particular quantum state. As the self-testing schemes are based on quantum nonlocality, each party in the experiment needs to perform at least two measurements. In the bipartite regime, there are only a few schemes that utilize two measurements per party
McKague et al. 2012
;
Bamps and Pironio 2015
;
Sarkar et al. 2021
and even fewer in the multipartite scenario
Baccari et al. 2020a
;
Sarkar and Augusiak 2022
. Moreover, in the multipartite scenario most of the schemes are devoted to quantum states that are locally qubits. The main difficulty here consists in designing a suitable Bell inequality that is maximally violated by a given multipartite entangled quantum state. The problem becomes even more complex if one wants to find a Bell inequality which involves the minimal number of two measurements per observer.

To lower the complexity, one can introduce physically motivated assumptions into the considered scenario, leading to what is known as semi-DI certification schemes. One can for instance assume that one of the parties are trusted and their measuring devices perform known measurements. Such an assumption gives rise to scenarios which are typically referred to as one-sided-device-independent (1SDI), and the key resource enabling certification in this case is a weaker form of quantum nonlocality known as quantum steering
Wiseman et al. 2007a
;
Cavalcanti et al. 2009a
;
Quintino et al. 2015
. Recent investigations have explored the potential of such scenarios for self-testing quantum states and measurements
Šupić and Hoban 2016
;
Gheorghiu et al. 2017
;
Shrotriya et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2016
;
Sarkar et al. 2022
;
Sarkar et al. 2023b
;
Sarkar 2023a
;
Sarkar 2023b
. For this work, we utilise the framework of multipartite quantum steering introduced in
Cavalcanti et al. 2015
.

We consider here a much more complicated problem of certifying genuinely multipartite entangled (GME) states shared among an arbitrary number of parties and arbitrary local dimension using minimal number of measurements, that is, two. To simplify the problem we make the aforementioned assumption that only one party is trusted, thus, making our scheme almost device-independent. Furthermore, we restrict the parties to only choose two measurements each. We then construct steering inequalities that are maximally violated by three major classes of GME states. We then prove that maximal violation of our inequalities can be used for certification
of the corresponding states. Since, among the arbitrary number of parties only one is trusted, the presented results below are almost DI certificates of the quantum state. The first family of states we can certify in this way is any connected graph state of arbitrary local dimension. Graph states are highly useful in quantum information tasks
Looi et al. 2008
;
Jozsa 2005
;
Keet et al. 2010
;
Gottesman 1997
. The only other known schemes to self-test graph states are restricted to local dimension two
McKague 2014
;
Baccari et al. 2020a
and three
Santos et al. 2023
. The second class of states we consider here are the Schmidt states of arbitrary local dimension, for which a self-testing method was provided in Ref.
Šupić et al. 2018
. The latter, however, requires more measurements per observer and thus is not optimal as far as physical implementations are concerned. Interestingly, our scheme can also be utilized to certify two measurements with the untrusted parties to be mutually unbiased using almost no entanglement across multiple parties. Finally, we self-test the generalized
W
state (see below for a definition) shared among an arbitrary number of parties but only with local dimension two. While self-testing methods for the standard
N
-qubit
W
state were already introduced
in Refs.
Wu et al. 2014
;
Šupić et al. 2018
, here, by making a mild assumption that only one party is trusted, we can significantly generalize these results to a multi-parameter class of genuinely entangled states.

Before proceeding to the results, let us introduce the relevant notions required for this work.

II
Preliminaries

We consider a one-sided DI (1SDI) scenario consisting of
N
-parties, namely Alice and
N-1
Bobs, whom we refer to as
A,B_{1},\ldots,B_{N-1}
. All the parties are located in space-like separated laboratories and share a quantum state
\rho_{A\mathbf{B}}
, where we denote
\mathbf{B}\equiv B_{1}\ldots B_{N-1}
. In this sense, each one of the
N
parties has a device to measure their share of
\rho_{A\mathbf{B}}
. Since this is a 1SDI scenario, we assume that Alice’s measurement device is trusted, i.e., her measurements are known. Yet, we do not assume that her device performs full tomography over her share of
\rho_{A\mathbf{B}}
. On the other side, the measurement devices that belong to the
N-1
Bobs are untrusted and we treat them as “black boxes”. The scenario we just described is also referred to as
local steering

Uola et al. 2020
, in which the untrusted parties perform local measurements on their respective states to steer the state owned by the trusted party. In this work, we consider that the trusted party performs the measurements given by the
d-
dimensional generalization of the Pauli
Z
and
X

\displaystyle Z_{d}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\omega^{i}|i\rangle\!\langle i|,\qquad X_{d}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}|i+1\rangle\!\langle i|

(1)

which for ease of notation will be denoted as
Z
and
X
respectively. Fig.
1
contains an illustration of the scenario considered here.

We denote the inputs of all the parties
A,B_{1},\ldots,B_{N-1}

respectively and the corresponding outcomes with
a,b_{1},\ldots,b_{N-1}

\mathbf{y}\equiv y_{1},\ldots,y_{N-1}
and
\mathbf{b}\equiv b_{1},\ldots,b_{N-1}

\vec{p}=\{p(a,\mathbf{b}|x,\mathbf{{y}})\}
, where

tells us the probability of getting outcomes

and
\mathbf{b}
given that the measurements

and
\mathbf{y}
have been performed. According to Born’s rule, the latter is expressed as

\displaystyle p(a,\mathbf{b}|x,\mathbf{y})=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho_{A\mathbf{B}}\left(M_{a|x}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N-1}N^{(i)}_{b_{i}|y_{i}}\right)\right],

(2)

where
M_{x}=\{M_{a|x}\}_{a}
,
N_{y_{i}}^{(i)}=\{N^{(i)}_{b_{i}|y_{i}}\}_{b_{i}}
are the measurement operators of Alice and Bobs
B_{i}
, respectively. We recall that the measurement operators
M_{a|x}
and
N^{(i)}_{b_{i}|y_{i}}
are positive semi-definite and sum to identity on their corresponding Hilbert spaces.

In our multipartite 1SDI scenario, the presence of local quantum steering is demonstrated if the probability distribution
p(a,\mathbf{b}|x,\mathbf{y})
can not be described by a local hidden state (LHS) model
Wiseman et al. 2007b
;
Uola et al. 2020
, i.e., if it can not be written as

\displaystyle p(a,\mathbf{b}|x,\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)\mathrm{Tr}\left[M_{a|x}\rho_{A}^{\lambda}\right]p(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{y},\lambda),

(3)

where

is a hidden variable that follows the probability distribution

. The hidden variable
\lambda
determines the state
\rho^{\lambda}_{A}
received by Alice and also the responses

obtained by
N-1
Bobs. If the set of probability distributions
\{p(a,\mathbf{b}|x,\mathbf{y})\}
can not be described by the above model, it leads to violation of a linear steering inequality,
\mathcal{B}\left[\{p(a,\mathbf{b}|x,\mathbf{y})\}\right]\leqslant\beta_{L}
, where
\mathcal{B}
is a linear combination of
\{p(a,\mathbf{b}|x,\mathbf{y})\}
and
\beta_{L}
is the LHS bound , also called classical bound
Cavalcanti et al. 2009b
.

In this work we express steering inequalities in terms of generalized expectation values. These are defined in terms of
p(a,\mathbf{b}|x,\mathbf{y})
as

\displaystyle\langle A_{k|x}\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{y}}\rangle=\sum_{a,\mathbf{b}=0}^{d-1}\omega^{ak+b_{1}l_{1}+\ldots+b_{N-1}l_{N-1}}p(a,\mathbf{b}|x,\mathbf{y}),

(4)

where
\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{y}}\equiv(B_{1})_{l_{1}|y_{1}}\ldots(B_{N-1})_{l_{N-1}|y_{N-1}}
,
\omega=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathbbm{i}/d}
, and
k,l_{1},\ldots,l_{N-1}\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
. If the probabilities

originate from a quantum measurement, we can write the expectation values as

\langle A_{k|x}\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{y}}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho_{A\mathbf{B}}A_{x}^{k}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N-1}(B_{i})_{l_{i}|y_{i}}\right],

(5)

where
A^{k}_{x}
is the
k
-th power of the unitary observable
A_{x}
, satisfying

, and

, i.e.,

(B_{i})_{l_{i}|y_{i}}=\sum_{b_{i}}\omega^{b_{i}l_{i}}N^{(i)}_{b_{i}|y_{i}}.

(6)

In fact, since we can write

as an inverse Fourier transform of
(B_{i})_{l_{i}|y_{i}}
, we can also refer to
\{(B_{i})_{l_{i}|y_{i}}\}_{l_{i}=0}^{d-1}
as the measurements of the
i
-th Bob. Moreover, if

are projective, then
(B_{i})_{l_{i}|y_{i}}
are the
l_{i}
-th power of a unitary observable
B_{i,y_{i}}\coloneqq(B_{i})_{1|y_{i}}
, that is,
(B_{i})_{l_{i}|y_{i}}=\left(B_{i,y_{i}}\right)^{l_{i}}

Kaniewski et al. 2019
.

In what follows, we will present three classes of steering inequalities that are maximally violated by three classes of GME states, namely the
N
-qudit graph states, the
N
-qudit Schmidt states, and the generalized
N
-qubit

-states. Recall that a GME state is one that cannot be written as a tensor product of two other pure states corresponding to two disjoint and nonempty subsets of the parties. Formally, a GME state is a state that cannot be written in the form

such that

is a subset of the parties

,
\overline{A}
is the complement of

and
\overline{A}

Maximally violating steering inequalities plays a critical role in the presented below self-testing results. When experimental results
\vec{p}
exhibit a maximal violation, they allow us to infer both the observer’s state and the performed measurements up to local isometries. With that in mind, let us formally define multipartite 1SDI self-testing similar to the one introduced in
Sarkar et al. 2022
. We assume here that the measurements are projective based on dilation arguments. However, the state
\rho_{N,A\mathbf{B}}
shared among all parties is in general mixed, which can be purified to

Alice’s measurements are projective and fixed as
A_{x}
, while Bobs measurements are arbitrary and represented by

. The goal is to certify that the state shared between Alice and Bob is equivalent to a reference state
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle_{A\mathbf{B}^{\prime}}
, as well as, that the measurements performed by Bob are equivalent to the reference measurements

that generates the same correlation

are certified by the statistics

if there exists a unitary

\displaystyle\left(\mathbbm{1}_{A}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N-1}U_{i}\right)|\psi_{N}\rangle_{A\mathbf{B}E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\psi^{\prime}\rangle_{A\mathbf{B}^{\prime}}\otimes|\phi\rangle_{\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime}E},

(7a)

\displaystyle U_{i}\,B_{i,y_{i}}\,U_{i}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{i,y_{i}}^{\prime}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{B_{i}^{\prime\prime}},\qquad\forall i

(7b)

III.2
Schmidt states

Every pure bipartite state
|\psi\rangle_{AB}
admits a Schmidt decomposition, that is, there exist orthonormal bases
|i\rangle_{A}
and
|i\rangle_{B}
in
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, respectively, such that
|\psi\rangle_{AB}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}|i\rangle_{A}\otimes|i\rangle_{B}
, where
\alpha_{i}>0
and
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}=1
. In the multipartite setting, an analogous decomposition is in general impossible. The multipartite pure states which can be represented via Schmidt decomposition are referred to as Schmidt states
Šupić et al. 2018
. Under the action of local unitary operators, a multipartite Schmidt state
|\psi(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\rangle_{A\mathbf{B}}
belonging to
\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B_{1}}}\ldots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B_{N-1}}}
can be reduced to the following form

\displaystyle|\psi(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\rangle_{A\mathbf{B}}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\alpha_{i}|i\rangle^{\otimes N},

(16)

where
d=\min\{d_{A},d_{B_{1}},\ldots,d_{B_{N-1}}\}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\equiv\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}
, such that
0<\alpha_{i}<1
for all
i
and
\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\alpha_{i}^{2}=1
. Because
\alpha_{i}>0
for all
i
, the Schmidt rank of the state is maximal, which ensures that it is a
N
-qudit state.

Inspired from
Sarkar et al. 2023b
, let us now introduce a general class of steering inequalities that are maximally violated by states of the form (
16

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},N)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}A_{0}^{k}\otimes B_{j,0}^{k}\right)\right.

(17)

\displaystyle+\left.\gamma(\boldsymbol{\alpha})A_{1}^{k}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N-1}B_{j,1}^{k}+\delta_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})A_{0}^{k}\right],

where the coefficients
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
and
\delta_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
are given by

\displaystyle\gamma(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=0\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{d-1}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{j}}\right]^{-1},

(18)

\displaystyle\delta_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=0\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{d-1}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{j}}\omega^{k(d-j)}.

In our scenario, we consider that all the

.

The corresponding steering inequality is given by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{S}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},N)\rangle\leqslant\beta_{L}.

(20)

Again
\beta_{L}
is the maximum value of
\langle\mathcal{S}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},N)\rangle
attainable with LHS models. In Fact 3 of Appendix B, we provide a form of
\beta_{L}
in terms of an optimization problem that can be numerically evaluated for any
d,N,\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. Then, the quantum bound
\beta_{Q}
of
\langle\mathcal{S}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},N)\rangle
is given by
\beta_{Q}=(d-1)(N-1)+1
and can be attained by
|\psi(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\rangle
and observables
B_{j,0}=Z_{d}
and
B_{j,1}=X_{d}
for any
j
. Importantly, from Fact 3 we can conclude that
\beta_{L}<\beta_{Q}
and thus the proposed steering inequality is non-trivial.

Now, if all the parties observe that
\langle\mathcal{S}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},N)\rangle=(d-1)(N-1)+1
, then we can establish the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that the steering inequality (
20
) is maximally violated when the
0^{\text{th}}
party Alice chooses the observables
A_{i}
defined to be
A_{0}=Z_{d}
,
A_{1}=X_{d}
and all the other parties choose their observables as
B_{j,i}
for
i=0,1
and
j=1,2\ldots,N-1
. Let us say that the state which attains this violation is given by
|\psi_{N}\rangle_{A\mathbf{B}E}\in\mathbbm{C}^{d}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N-1}\mathcal{H}_{B_{j}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
, then the following statement holds true for any
d
:
\mathcal{H}_{B_{i}}=\mathcal{H}_{B^{\prime}_{i}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B^{\prime\prime}_{i}}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{B^{\prime}_{i}}\equiv\mathbbm{C}^{d}
,
\mathcal{H}_{B^{\prime\prime}_{i}}
is some finite-dimensional Hilbert space, and there exists a local unitary transformation on Bob’s side

(21)

where
{B^{\prime\prime}}

\left(\mathbbm{1}_{AE}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N-1}U_{j}\right)|\psi_{N}\rangle=|\psi(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\rangle_{A\mathbf{B}^{\prime}}\otimes|\xi\rangle_{\mathbf{B}^{\prime\prime}E},

III.3
N
-qubit generalized
W
states

The third and last class of states that we are going to consider comprises the
N
-qubit generalized
W
states. As the name suggests, they represent a generalization of the well-known
W
state
|\psi_{W}\rangle=(|100\rangle+|010\rangle+|001\rangle)/\sqrt{3}
and have the following form

\displaystyle|\psi_{W_{N}}\rangle=\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\alpha_{l+1}|0\ldots,0,1_{l},0,\ldots,0\rangle,

(23)

such that
\alpha_{i}>0
for every
i
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}^{2}=1
. We introduce a steering inequality that is maximally violated by the above state through the following steering operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2Z_{A}\otimes\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N-1}B_{k,0}+\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}\left[Z_{A}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{l}\otimes\left(\mathbbm{1}-P_{l}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\gamma_{l}X_{A}\otimes B_{l,1}+\delta_{l}Z_{A}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{l}\right)\otimes P_{l}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{A}\otimes B_{l,0}\otimes\left(\mathbbm{1}-P_{l}\right)+\mathbbm{1}_{A}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{l}\otimes P_{l}\right],

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{l}=\frac{2\alpha_{l+1}\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{l+1}^{2}+\alpha_{1}^{2}},\quad\delta_{l}=\frac{\alpha_{l+1}^{2}-\alpha_{1}^{2}}{\alpha_{l+1}^{2}+\alpha_{1}^{2}},

(25)

for every
l\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
,

\displaystyle P_{l}=\frac{1}{2^{N-2}}\bigotimes_{k=1,k\neq l}^{N-1}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{k}+B_{k,0}\right),

(26)

and
B_{k,0}
and
B_{k,1}
are the measurements acting on the
k
-th qubit. In the same way,
\mathbbm{1}_{l}
is the identity operation acting on the
l
-th qubit. The corresponding steering inequality is given by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{W}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\rangle\leqslant\beta_{L}.

(27)

where
\beta_{L}
is the LHS bound. Unfortunately, we were not able to calculate the LHS bound for this case. The quantum bound, nonetheless, is given by
\beta_{Q}=2N
and is achieved by the state
|\psi_{W_{N}}\rangle
and measurements
B_{j,0}=Z
and
B_{j,1}=X
, as we prove in Fact 5 of Appendix C. By observing this value, the parties can certify the state and measurements through the following statement:

Theorem 3
.

Assume that the steering inequality (
27
) is maximally violated when the
0^{\text{th}}
party Alice chooses the observables
A_{i}
defined to be
A_{0}=Z
,

\mathcal{H}_{B_{j}}=\mathcal{H}_{B^{\prime}_{j}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B^{\prime\prime}_{j}}

for every

The reader will find the proof for the above theorem in Appendix C.

IV
Discussion

In our work, we demonstrated that although it’s very difficult to create self-testing methods with the fewest measurements possible, having trust in one of the parties makes it possible to create 1SDI schemes for many types of GME states. We focused on graph states, Schmidt states, and generalized
W
states. Another important finding is that we can certify a pair of operators that are mutually unbiased with minimal entanglement across any number of parties in one process.

Our work raises several follow-up questions. An obvious one is how to expand our approach to certify more types of genuinely entangled multipartite states. In fact, it would be interesting to see whether the assumption considered here that one of the measurement device is trusted allows one to design certification scheme for any GME state based on the minimal number of measurements per observes. A related question that arises here is whether the ideas presented above can be combined with the results of Ref
Mančinska et al. 2021
;
Volčič 2023
to design self-testing schemes for multipartite states which are based on finite statistics. What is more, it would have been interesting to explore whether the 1SDI certification scheme for graph states of any local dimension provided here could be generalized to stabilizer subspaces (cf. Ref.
Baccari et al. 2020b
). At the same time, as far as the experimental testing of our schemes is concerned, it is crucial to understand how resilient they are to experimental errors. Additionally, we can use our self-testing results for secure multipartite randomness generation.

Appendix A: Self-testing any Graph state

Consider a connected graph
G
with
N

e
with their multiplicity denoted by
\gamma_{i,j}
. Then the graph state
|G\rangle
corresponding to graph
G

N

\displaystyle S(G)_{d,i}=X_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j\in n(i)}Z_{j}^{\gamma_{i,j}}\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,N

(30)

Let us now recall the steering operator introduced in the main text.

\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)=X_{A}\otimes\bigotimes_{j\in n(0)}(B_{j,0})^{\gamma_{0,j}}+\sum_{j\in n(0)}Z_{A}^{\gamma_{j,0}}\otimes B_{j,1}\otimes\bigotimes_{j^{\prime}\in n(j)\setminus\{0\}}(B_{j^{\prime},0})^{\gamma_{j,j^{\prime}}}+\sum_{j\notin n(0)\cup{0}}B_{j,1}\otimes\bigotimes_{j^{\prime}\in n(j)}(B_{j^{\prime},0})^{\gamma_{j,j^{\prime}}}+h.c.

(31)

where

denotes the Hermitian conjugate of the operator on its left-hand side. Also notice that in the above operator
\gamma_{i,j}=\gamma_{j,i}
for any
i,j
where

is the number of edges connecting vector
i,j
and

is the set of vertices connected to
j

is denoted as

.
Now, the steering inequality is given by

\langle\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)\rangle\leqslant\beta_{L}.

(32)

The classical bound
\beta_{L}
is given below.

Fact 1
.

The local hidden state (LHS) bound
\beta_{L}
of the steering functional
\langle\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)\rangle
where
\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)
is given in (
31

\displaystyle\beta_{L}\leqslant\max_{\rho}\left(2|\langle X_{A}\rangle_{\rho}|+2\sum_{j\in n(0)}|\langle Z_{A}^{\gamma_{j,0}}\rangle_{\rho}|\right)+2(N-\overline{n}_{0}-1)

(33)

where

and

are given in eq. (
1
). Furthermore,
\beta_{L}<2N
.

Proof.

As shown in
Sarkar et al. 2022
, for any LHS model with trusted Alice, the expectation values of the joint observables take the form

\displaystyle\langle A^{k}_{x}\otimes B^{l}_{y}\rangle_{LHS}=\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)\langle A^{k}_{x}\rangle_{\rho_{\lambda}}\langle B^{l}_{y}\rangle_{\lambda}.

(34)

Consequently, we can express the steering inequality
\langle\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)\rangle
, where
\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)
is given in (
31
), for any LHS model as

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)\rangle_{LHS}=\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)\langle\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)\rangle_{\lambda,LHS},

(35)

where
\langle\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)\rangle_{\lambda,LHS}
is

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)\rangle_{\lambda,LHS}=\langle X_{A}\rangle_{\rho_{\lambda}}\langle\bigotimes_{j\in n(0)}(B_{j,0})^{\gamma_{0,j}}\rangle_{{\lambda}}+\sum_{j\in n(0)}\langle Z_{A}^{\gamma_{j,0}}\rangle_{\rho_{\lambda}}\langle B_{j,1}\otimes\bigotimes_{j^{\prime}\in n(j)\setminus\{0\}}(B_{j^{\prime},0})^{\gamma_{j,j^{\prime}}}\rangle_{{\lambda}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\notin n(0)\cup{0}}\langle B_{j,1}\rangle_{\rho_{\lambda}}\langle\bigotimes_{j^{\prime}\in n(j)}(B_{j^{\prime},0})^{\gamma_{j,j^{\prime}}}\rangle_{{\lambda}}+\langle h.c\rangle_{LHS}.\qquad

(36)

As
\langle B^{l}_{y}\rangle_{\lambda}\leq 1
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)\rangle_{LHS}\leqslant\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)\left(2|\langle X_{A}\rangle_{\rho_{\lambda}}|+2\sum_{j\in n(0)}|\langle Z_{A}^{\gamma_{j,0}}\rangle_{\rho_{\lambda}}|\right)+2(N-\overline{n}_{0}-1),

(37)

which can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)\rangle_{LHS}\leqslant\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)\max_{\rho}\left(2|\langle X_{A}\rangle_{\rho}|+2\sum_{j\in n(0)}|\langle Z_{A}^{\gamma_{j,0}}\rangle_{\rho}|\right)+2(N-\overline{n}_{0}-1).

(38)

By using the fact that
\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)=1
, the above finally gives us

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)\rangle_{LHS}\leqslant\max_{\rho}\left(2|\langle X_{A}\rangle_{\rho}|+2\sum_{j\in n(0)}|\langle Z_{A}^{\gamma_{j,0}}\rangle_{\rho}|\right)+2(N-\overline{n}_{0}-1).

(39)

One can numerically evaluate the above quantity for any
d
and any graph. Let us finally observe from the above expression that the LHS bound of the steering functional
\mathcal{I}_{d}(G,N)
satisfies
\beta_{L}<2N

and

Z
and
X
.
∎

Theorem 1

\displaystyle d\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\sum_{a=0}^{d-1}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}p(\lambda)p(a|0,\rho^{\lambda}_{A})p(d-a|0,\lambda)+\gamma(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\Biggl(d\sum_{a=0}^{d-1}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}p(\lambda)p(a|1,\rho^{\lambda}_{A})p(\mathbf{b}|1,\ldots,1,\lambda)
