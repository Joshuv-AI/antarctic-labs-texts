Learning Sparse Graph Laplacian with k k Eigenvector Prior via Iterative GLASSO and Projection
Saghar Bagheri, Gene Cheung, Antonio Ortega, Fen Wang

Abstract
Learning a suitable graph is an important precursor to many graph signal processing (GSP) pipelines, such as graph signal compression and denoising. Previous graph learning algorithms either i) make assumptions on graph connectivity (e.g., graph sparsity), or ii) make edge weight assumptions such as positive edges only. In this paper, given an empirical covariance matrix 𝐂 ¯ \bar{{\mathbf{C}}} computed from data as input, we consider an eigen-structural assumption on the graph Laplacian matrix 𝐋 {\mathbf{L}} : the first K K eigenvectors of 𝐋 {\mathbf{L}} are pre-selected, e.g., based on domain-specific criteria, and the remaining eigenvectors are then learned from data. One example use case is image coding, where the first eigenvector is pre-chosen to be constant, regardless of available observed data. We first prove that the subspace ℋ 𝐮 + {\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+} of symmetric positive semi-definite (PSD) matrices with the first K K eigenvectors being { 𝐮 k } \{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\} in a defined Hilbert space is a convex cone. We then construct an operator to project a given positive definite (PD) matrix 𝐋 {\mathbf{L}} to ℋ 𝐮 + {\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+} , inspired by the Gram-Schmidt procedure. Finally, we design an efficient hybrid graphical lasso / projection algorithm to compute the most suitable graph Laplacian matrix 𝐋 ∗ ∈ ℋ 𝐮 + {\mathbf{L}}^{*}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+} given 𝐂 ¯ \bar{{\mathbf{C}}} . Experimental results show that given the first K K eigenvectors as a prior, our algorithm outperforms competing graph learning schemes using a variety of graph comparison metrics.

Introduction

Graph signal processing
(GSP)
[
1
]
is the study of signals residing on graphs.
While GSP tools have been demonstrated to be effective in a wide range of applications from image compression and denoising to matrix completion
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, a fundamental first step in GSP is the selection of an appropriate graph
{\mathcal{G}}
(or graph Laplacian matrix
{\mathbf{L}}
) that suitably describes pairwise (dis)similarities.
Previous graph learning algorithms fall into two categories:
i)
connectivity
-based approaches like graphical lasso that, given an empirical covariance matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
, estimate an inverse matrix with only sparse graph connectivity assumptions
[
8
,
9
]
; and
ii)
edge
-based approaches that assume edge properties such as edge signs and absence of self-loops
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
.
Neither of these two categories of methods make assumptions about the
eigen-structure
of the graph Laplacian matrix
{\mathbf{L}}
.

In this paper, we introduce explicit eigen-structure assumptions on the graph Laplacian matrix
{\mathbf{L}}
—that the first
K
eigenvectors of
{\mathbf{L}}
are pre-selected or computed based on domain-specific criteria—into a graph learning framework.
Consider the example of image compression, where one can deduce from domain knowledge that the most common pixel block is the constant signal, and thus should be the first eigenvector regardless of training data.
Consider also political voting records, where the most common pattern is voting along party affiliations in a two-party political system.
Thus, the first eigenvector should be a piecewise constant signal (
i.e.
, nodes of one party are assigned
1
, while nodes of the other are
-1
)
[
13
]
.
There are also practical cases where the first
K
eigenvectors can be pre-chosen for fast computation.
For example,
fast graph Fourier transform
(FGFT)

[
14
]
approximates an eigen-matrix by multiplying a small number of Givens rotation matrices.
The first
K
rows of the sparse matrix product are the
K
approximate eigenvectors, and the
K
transform coefficients can be computed speedily due to sparsity.

We propose an optimization strategy where a graph Laplacian matrix
{\mathbf{L}}
is learned optimally from data, while restricting the first
K
eigenvectors to be those chosen ahead of time.
We first prove that the subspace
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
of symmetric
positive semi-definite
(PSD) matrices with the first
K

eigenvectors taken from a given set
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}^{K}_{k=1}
is a convex cone.
We next construct an operator to project a given positive definite (PD) matrix
{\mathbf{L}}
to
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
, inspired by the Gram-Schmidt procedure
[
15
]
.
Finally, we design an efficient hybrid graphical lasso (GLASSO) / projection algorithm to compute the most suitable graph Laplacian matrix
{\mathbf{L}}^{*}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
given input empirical covariance matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
.
Experimental results demonstrate that given the first
K
eigenvectors as a prior, our algorithm outperformed competing graph learning methods in terms of different graph metrics.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Graph Definitions

Suppose we are given a graph
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}})
with
|{\mathcal{V}}|=N
nodes and edges
(i,j)\in{\mathcal{E}}
connecting nodes
i
and
j
with weight
w_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Denote by
{\mathbf{W}}
the
adjacency matrix
, where
W_{ij}=w_{ij}
.

{\mathbf{W}}
may include self-loops
W_{ii}
’s.
Assuming that the edges are undirected,
{\mathbf{W}}
is symmetric.
Define next the diagonal
degree matrix

{\mathbf{D}}
where
D_{ii}=\sum_{j}W_{ij}
.
The
combinatorial graph Laplacian matrix

[
1
]
is then defined as
{\mathbf{L}}={\mathbf{D}}-{\mathbf{W}}
.
To properly account for self-loops, the
generalized graph Laplacian matrix
is defined as
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathbf{D}}-{\mathbf{W}}+\text{diag}({\mathbf{W}})
.
In this paper, we assume that the target graph Laplacian to be learned from data may have self-loops, and edge weights can be positive or negative.
This means that our graph Laplacian
{\mathcal{L}}
can account for both pairwise correlation and anti-correlation.

2.2
Hilbert Space

Following terminologies in
[
16
]
, we first define a
vector space

{\mathcal{X}}
of real, symmetric matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.
Note that
{\mathcal{X}}
is closed under addition and scalar multiplication.
We next define the standard
inner product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for matrices
{\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{Q}}
in
{\mathcal{X}}
as:

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{Q}}\rangle

\displaystyle={\text{Tr}}({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}{\mathbf{P}})=\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}Q_{ij}.

(1)

This definition of inner product induces the Frobenius norm:

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{P}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\|{\mathbf{P}}\|^{2}_{F}=\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}^{2}\geq 0.

(2)

We can now define a
Hilbert space

{\mathcal{H}}
in
{\mathcal{X}}
as a space of real, symmetric matrices endowed with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:({\mathcal{H}},{\mathcal{H}})\mapsto\mathbb{R}
.
Further, we are interested in the subspace of
positive semi-definite
(PSD) matrices,

i.e.
,
{\mathcal{H}}^{+}=\{{\mathbf{P}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\,|\,{\mathbf{P}}\succeq 0\}
.
One can easily show that subspace
{\mathcal{H}}^{+}
is a convex cone
[
17
]
.

3
Projection Operator

We first overview our algorithm development.
We prove that the subspace
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}\subset{\mathcal{H}}^{+}
of symmetric PSD matrices
that share a common
ordered
set of first
K
eigenvectors is a convex cone.
This means that, given an empirical covariance matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
estimated from
M
signal observations
{\mathcal{X}}=\{{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{M}\}
, minimizing a convex objective computed using
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
and a graph Laplacian matrix
{\mathbf{L}}
while restricting
{\mathbf{L}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
is a convex optimization problem.
We then develop a projection operator to project a
positive definite
(PD) matrix to
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
.
We describe our optimization algorithm using the projection operator in Section
4
.

3.1
Subspace of Matrices with Common First
K
Eigenvector

Denote by
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}\subset{\mathcal{H}}^{+}
the subspace of PSD matrices that share the first
K
eigenvectors
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, assumed to be orthonormal,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}=\delta_{j-k},~~~\forall j,k\in{\mathcal{I}}_{K}

(3)

where
{\mathcal{I}}_{K}=\{1,\ldots,K\}
and
\delta_{i}
is the discrete impulse function that evaluates to 1 if
i=0
and 0 otherwise.
We can define
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
using the Rayleigh quotient and the min-max theorem
[
18
]
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}=\left\{{\mathbf{L}}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}\;|\;{\mathbf{u}}_{k}=\arg\min_{{\mathbf{x}}\,|\,{\mathbf{x}}\perp{\mathbf{u}}_{j},\forall j<k}\frac{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}}{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}},~~k\in{\mathcal{I}}_{K}\right\}.

(4)

Lemma 3.1
.

{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
is a convex cone.

Proof.

We prove by induction.
For the base case when
K=1
, let
{\mathbf{L}}_{1},{\mathbf{L}}_{2}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}
be two matrices that share the first eigenvector
{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
.
Consider a positive linear combination
{\mathcal{L}}=c_{1}{\mathbf{L}}_{1}+c_{2}{\mathbf{L}}_{2}
, where
c_{1},c_{2}\geq 0
.
We examine the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}({\mathcal{L}})
of
{\mathcal{L}}
using the Rayleigh quotient again,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}({\mathcal{L}})

\displaystyle=\min_{{\mathbf{x}}}\frac{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathcal{L}}{\mathbf{x}}}{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}}=\min_{{\mathbf{x}}}\frac{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\left(c_{1}{\mathbf{L}}_{1}+c_{2}{\mathbf{L}}_{2}\right){\mathbf{x}}}{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geq}}c_{1}\left(\min_{{\mathbf{x}}}\frac{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{L}}_{1}{\mathbf{x}}}{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}}\right)+c_{2}\left(\min_{{\mathbf{y}}}\frac{{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{L}}_{2}{\mathbf{y}}}{{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}c_{1}\,\lambda_{\min}({\mathbf{L}}_{1})+c_{2}\,\lambda_{\min}({\mathbf{L}}_{2})

The inequality in
(a)
is true since right-hand side (RHS) has more degrees of freedom than left-hand side (LHS) to minimize the two non-negative terms (
{\mathbf{L}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{L}}_{2}
are PSD matrices in
{\mathcal{H}}^{+}
).

(b)
is true by definition of Rayleigh quotient, with
{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
being the minimizing argument for both terms by assumption.
Thus, the argument that minimizes the Rayleigh quotient for
{\mathcal{L}}
is also
{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
, and therefore
{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
is the first eigenvector of
{\mathcal{L}}
.
Since this analysis is true for all
c_{1},c_{2}\geq 0
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
is a convex cone for
K=1
.

Consider next the inductive step, where we assume the
K=K_{o}
case is true, and we prove the
K_{o}+1
case.
Let
{\mathbf{L}}_{1},{\mathbf{L}}_{2}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}
be two matrices that share the first
K_{o}+1
eigenvectors
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{o}+1}
.
Consider a positive linear combination
{\mathcal{L}}=c_{1}{\mathbf{L}}_{1}+c_{2}{\mathbf{L}}_{2}
, where
c_{1},c_{2}\geq 0
.
By the inductive assumption,
{\mathcal{L}}
also shares the first
K_{o}
eigenvectors
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{o}}
.
For the
(K_{o}+1)
-th eigenvector, consider the
(K_{o}+1)
-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{K_{o}+1}({\mathcal{L}})
, computed as

\displaystyle=\min_{{\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{x}}\perp\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}^{K}_{k=1}}\frac{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathcal{L}}{\mathbf{x}}}{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}}=\min_{{\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{x}}\perp\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}^{K}_{k=1}}\frac{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\left(c_{1}{\mathbf{L}}_{1}+c_{2}{\mathbf{L}}_{2}\right){\mathbf{x}}}{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}\left(\min_{{\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{x}}\perp\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}^{K}_{k=1}}\frac{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{L}}_{1}{\mathbf{x}}}{{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}}\right)+c_{2}\,\left(\min_{{\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{y}}\perp\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}^{K}_{k=1}}\frac{{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{L}}_{2}{\mathbf{y}}}{{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}}\right)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\,\lambda_{K_{o}+1}({\mathbf{L}}_{1})+c_{2}\lambda_{K_{o}+1}({\mathbf{L}}_{2}).

Since the argument that minimizes the Rayleigh quotient for both
{\mathbf{L}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{L}}_{2}
is
{\mathbf{u}}_{K_{o}+1}
, this is also the argument that minimizes the Rayleight quotient for
{\mathcal{L}}
.
Thus the (
K_{o}+1
)-th eigenvector for
{\mathcal{L}}
is also
{\mathbf{u}}_{K_{o}+1}
.
Thus, we conclude that
{\mathcal{L}}
has
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{o}+1}
as its first
K_{o}+1
eigenvectors.
Since both the base case and the inductive step are true,
{\mathcal{H}}^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}}
is a convex cone.
∎

3.2
Projection to Convex Cone
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}

To project a PD matrix
{\mathbf{P}}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}
to convex cone
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
given first
K
eigenvectors
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}^{K}_{k=1}
, we approximate the inverse
{\mathbf{C}}={\mathbf{P}}^{-1}
with
\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}
and ensure
{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
is the argument that
maximizes
the Rayleigh quotient of
\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}
, then
{\mathbf{u}}_{2}
is the argument that maximizes the Rayleigh quotient of
\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}
while being orthogonal to
{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
, and so on.

\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}
will be a linear combination of
\{{\mathbf{U}}_{k}\}^{K}_{k=1}
and
\{{\mathbf{V}}_{k}\}^{N}_{k=K+1}
, where
{\mathbf{U}}_{k}={\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}
,
{\mathbf{V}}_{k}={\mathbf{v}}_{k}{\mathbf{v}}_{k}^{\top}
, and
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
’s are the
N-K
remaining eigenvectors of
\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}
.

Specifically, we first compute

{\mathbf{C}}={\mathbf{P}}^{-1}
.
Define
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}=\{\alpha{\mathbf{U}}_{1},\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\}
as the subspace spanned by rank-1 matrix
{\mathbf{U}}_{1}={\mathbf{u}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}
with scalar
\alpha
.
Denote by
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}
the orthogonal subspace in
{\mathcal{H}}
so that
{\mathcal{H}}={\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\oplus{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}
.
We first write
{\mathbf{C}}
as its projection to
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
plus its orthogonal component,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{C}}=\langle{\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\rangle{\mathbf{U}}_{1}~+~{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}}

(5)

where
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}}
is the orthogonal component of
{\mathbf{C}}
in subspace
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}
.
We show that given
{\mathbf{C}}
is PD, inner product
\langle{\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\rangle
is non-negative.

Lemma 3.2
.

\langle{\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\rangle\geq 0
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathbf{C}}
is PD by assumption, without loss of generality we can write
{\mathbf{C}}={\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}
.
Then

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left(({\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{B}}^{\top})^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}\right)=\text{Tr}\left({\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left({\mathbf{B}}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}{\mathbf{B}}\right)=\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}{\mathbf{B}}\rangle.

The last term is a Frobenius norm
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}{\mathbf{B}}\|^{2}_{2}\geq 0
.
∎

Define
\mu_{1}=\langle{\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\rangle\geq 0
.
We express
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}
as the span of orthogonal rank-1 matrices
{\mathbf{U}}_{2},\ldots,{\mathbf{U}}_{K},{\mathbf{V}}_{K+1},\ldots,{\mathbf{V}}_{N}
, where
{\mathbf{U}}_{k}={\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}
,
{\mathbf{V}}_{i}={\mathbf{v}}_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{\top}
, and
\|{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\|_{2}=1
.
Suppose that the inner products of
{\mathbf{C}}
with the orthogonal components,
\langle{\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{U}}_{k}\rangle,\langle{\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{V}}_{i}\rangle
, are within range
[0,\mu_{1}]
,
\forall k,i
.
This means that the respective Rayleigh quotients are no larger than
\mu_{1}
:

\displaystyle\text{Tr}({\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{u}}_{k})=\text{Tr}({\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top})=\langle{\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{U}}_{k}\rangle\leq\mu_{1},~~\forall k

(6)

Then
{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
is surely the
last
eigenvector of
{\mathbf{C}}
corresponding to
largest
eigenvalue
\mu_{1}
.
If this is not the case, then we need to approximate orthogonal component
{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}}
as
\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}}
in order to ensure
{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
is indeed the last eigenvector:

\displaystyle\min_{\{{\mathbf{V}}_{i}\},\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}}}\|{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}}-\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}}\|^{2}_{2},~~~\mbox{s.t.}~~\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\langle{\mathbf{V}}_{i},{\mathbf{U}}_{k}\rangle=0,~\forall i,k\\
\langle{\mathbf{V}}_{i},{\mathbf{V}}_{j}\rangle=\delta_{i-j},~\forall i,j\\
{\mathbf{V}}_{i}={\mathbf{v}}_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{\top},~\forall i\\
\|{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\|_{2}=1,~\forall i\\
0\leq\langle\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}},{\mathbf{U}}_{k}\rangle\leq\mu_{1},~\forall k\\
0\leq\langle\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\perp}},{\mathbf{V}}_{i}\rangle\leq\mu_{1},~\forall i\end{array}\right.

Jointly optimizing
N-K
rank-1 matrices
{\mathbf{V}}_{i}
’s and
\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}_{\Omega^{\perp}}
in
(
3.2
) is difficult.
We propose a greedy algorithm instead next.

3.3
Gram-Schmidt-inspired Algorithm

Our algorithm is inspired by the
Gram-Schmidt
procedure
[
15
]
that iteratively computes a set of orthonormal vectors given a set of linearly independent vectors.
Similarly, our algorithm iteratively computes one orthogonal rank-1 matrix
{\mathbf{V}}_{t}={\mathbf{v}}_{t}{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{\top}
at each iteration
t
, for
t\geq K+1
.
For iteration
t\leq K
, first
K
eigenvector
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}^{K}_{k=1}
are known, so we will simply use
{\mathbf{U}}_{t}={\mathbf{u}}_{t}{\mathbf{u}}_{t}^{\top}
.

Consider iteration
t=2
.
From (
5
), we first define the
residual signal
as
{\mathbf{E}}_{1}={\mathbf{C}}-\langle{\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\rangle{\mathbf{U}}_{1}
.
Consider first the case where
K\geq 2
, and thus rank-1 matrix
{\mathbf{U}}_{2}={\mathbf{u}}_{2}{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{\top}
orthogonal to
{\mathbf{U}}_{1}
is pre-selected.
Thus, we require only that the inner product
\mu_{2}=\langle\hat{{\mathbf{C}}},{\mathbf{U}}_{2}\rangle
of approximation
\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}
and
{\mathbf{U}}_{2}
to be
\leq\mu_{1}
.
Mathematically,

\displaystyle\mu_{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{1},{\mathbf{U}}_{2}\rangle,&\mbox{if}~~\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{1},{\mathbf{U}}_{2}\rangle\leq\mu_{1}\\
\mu_{1},&\mbox{o.w.}\end{array}\right.

Consider next the case where
K=1
, and we must identify a new rank-1 matrix
{\mathbf{V}}_{2}={\mathbf{v}}_{2}{\mathbf{v}}_{2}^{\top}
orthogonal to
{\mathbf{U}}_{1}
to reconstruct
\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}
.
In this case, to minimize error
\|{\mathbf{C}}-\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}\|^{2}_{2}
, we seek a
{\mathbf{V}}_{2}
“most aligned” with
{\mathbf{E}}_{1}
,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\max_{{\mathbf{v}}_{2}}~\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{1},{\mathbf{v}}_{2}{\mathbf{v}}_{2}^{\top}\rangle,~~\mbox{s.t.}~~\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{2}{\mathbf{v}}_{2}^{\top},{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\rangle=0\\
\|{\mathbf{v}}_{2}\|_{2}=1\end{array}\right.

Essentially, (
3.3
) seeks a
rank-1 approximation

\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{1},{\mathbf{V}}_{2}\rangle{\mathbf{V}}_{2}
of matrix
{\mathbf{E}}_{1}
, while constraining
{\mathbf{V}}_{2}
to be orthogonal to
{\mathbf{U}}_{1}
.
The objective is equivalent to
{\mathbf{v}}_{2}^{\top}{\mathbf{E}}_{1}{\mathbf{v}}_{2}
, which is convex for a maximization problem.

To convexify the problem, we first approximate
{\mathbf{E}}_{1}\approx{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}
where
{\mathbf{e}}
is the last eigenvector
1
1

1

Extreme eigenvectors of sparse symmetric matrices can be computed efficiently using fast numerical methods such as LOBPCG
[
19
]
.
of
{\mathbf{E}}_{1}
, and
{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}
is the best rank-1 approximation of
{\mathbf{E}}_{1}
.
We then relax the norm equality constraint and rewrite (
3.3
) into the following optimization:

\displaystyle\max_{{\mathbf{v}}_{2}}{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}_{2},~~\mbox{s.t.}~~\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathbf{v}}_{2}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}=0\\
\|{\mathbf{v}}_{2}\|^{2}_{2}\leq 1\end{array}\right.

Optimization (
3.3
) is now convex and solvable in polynomial time, using algorithms such as
proximal gradient
(PG)
[
20
]
.

Having computed
{\mathbf{V}}_{2}={\mathbf{v}}_{2}{\mathbf{v}}_{2}^{\top}
in (
3.3
), we project
{\mathbf{E}}_{1}
onto
{\mathbf{V}}_{2}
and threshold its inner product to within
[0,\mu_{1}]
as done in the
K\geq 2
case,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\mu_{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{1},{\mathbf{V}}_{2}\rangle,&\mbox{if}~\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{1},{\mathbf{V}}_{2}\rangle\leq\mu_{1}\\
\mu_{1},&\mbox{o.w.}\end{array}\right.

Note that
\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{1},{\mathbf{V}}_{2}\rangle\geq 0
.
We omit the proof of the more general case
\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{k},{\mathbf{V}}_{k}\rangle\geq 0
for brevity.
The projection of
{\mathbf{E}}_{1}
on
{\mathbf{V}}_{2}
is thus
\mu_{2}{\mathbf{V}}_{2}
.
We then compute new residual signal
{\mathbf{E}}_{2}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{2}={\mathbf{E}}_{1}-\mu_{2}{\mathbf{V}}_{2}.

(21)

Given residual
{\mathbf{E}}_{2}
, we again compute a rank-1 matrix
{\mathbf{V}}_{3}={\mathbf{v}}_{3}{\mathbf{v}}_{3}^{\top}
—orthogonal to
{\mathbf{V}}_{2}
and
{\mathbf{U}}_{1}
—that is most aligned with
{\mathbf{E}}_{2}
, and compute projection of
{\mathbf{E}}_{2}
to
{\mathbf{V}}_{3}
, and so on.

More generally, at each iteration
t\leq K
, we threshold the inner product
\mu_{t+1}=\min(\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{t},{\mathbf{U}}_{t+1}\rangle,\mu_{t})
, and compute the next residual signal
{\mathbf{E}}_{t+1}={\mathbf{E}}_{t}-\mu_{t+1}{\mathbf{U}}_{t+1}
.
On the other hand, at each iteration
t\geq K+1
, we first approximate
{\mathbf{E}}_{t}\approx{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}
where
{\mathbf{e}}
is
{\mathbf{E}}_{t}
’s last eigenvector.
We then compute an optimal
{\mathbf{v}}_{t+1}
:

\displaystyle\max_{{\mathbf{v}}_{t+1}}~{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}_{t+1},~~\mbox{s.t.}~~\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathbf{v}}_{t+1}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}=0,~~k\in{\mathcal{I}}_{K}\\
{\mathbf{v}}_{t+1}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}_{\tau}=0,~~\tau\in\{K+1,\ldots,t\}\\
\|{\mathbf{v}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1\end{array}\right.

We threshold the inner product
\mu_{t+1}=\min(\langle{\mathbf{E}}_{t},{\mathbf{V}}_{t+1}\rangle,\mu_{t})
, where
{\mathbf{V}}_{t+1}={\mathbf{v}}_{t+1}{\mathbf{v}}_{t+1}^{\top}
.
We compute the next residual signal
{\mathbf{E}}_{t+1}={\mathbf{E}}_{t}-\mu_{t+1}{\mathbf{V}}_{t+1}
.

We finally note that our constructed operator
\text{Proj}(\cdot)
is indeed a projection, since it is provably
idempotent

[
16
]
,
i.e.
,
\text{Proj}(\text{Proj}({\mathbf{P}}))=\text{Proj}({\mathbf{P}})
for any PD matrix
{\mathbf{P}}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}
.

4
Graph Laplacian Matrix Estimation

Given an empirical covariance matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
computed from data, we formulate the following optimization problem to estimate a graph Laplacian matrix
{\mathbf{L}}
starting from the GLASSO formulation in
[
21
]
:

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{L}}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}}}~~\text{Tr}({\mathbf{L}}\bar{{\mathbf{C}}})-\log\det{\mathbf{L}}+\rho\;\|{\mathbf{L}}\|_{1}

(25)

where
\rho>0
is a shrinkage parameter for the
l_{1}
norm.
The only difference from GLASSO is that (
25
) has an additional constraint
{\mathbf{L}}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}}
.
Because
{\mathcal{H}}^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}}
is a convex set, (
25
) is a convex problem.

We solve (
25
) iteratively using our developed projection operator in Section
3
and a variant of the
block Coordinate descent
(BCD) algorithm in
[
22
]
.
Specifically, we first solve the
dual
of GLASSO as follows.
We first note that the
l_{1}
norm in (
25
) can be written as

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{L}}\|_{1}=\max_{\|{\mathbf{U}}\|_{\infty}\leq 1}~~\text{Tr}({\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{U}})

(26)

where
\|{\mathbf{U}}\|_{\infty}
is the maximum absolute value element of the symmetric matrix
{\mathbf{U}}
.
Then the dual problem of GLASSO that solves for an estimated covariance matrix
{\mathbf{C}}={\mathbf{L}}^{-1}
is

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}}~~-\log\det{\mathbf{C}},~~~\mbox{s.t.}~~\|{\mathbf{C}}-\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}\|_{\infty}\leq\rho

(27)

where
{\mathbf{C}}=\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}+{\mathbf{U}}
implies that the primal and dual variables are related via
{\mathbf{L}}={(\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}+{\mathbf{U}})}^{-1}

[
13
]
.
To solve (
27
),
we update one row-column pair in
{\mathbf{C}}
in (
27
) in each iteration.
Specifically, we first reorder the rows / columns of
{\mathbf{C}}
so that the optimizing row and column are swapped to the last.
We then partition
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
and
{\mathbf{C}}
into blocks:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{C}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{C}}_{11}&{\mathbf{c}}_{12}\\
{\mathbf{c}}_{12}^{\top}&c_{22}\end{pmatrix},~~\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}_{11}&\bar{{\mathbf{c}}}_{12}\\
\bar{{\mathbf{c}}}_{12}^{\top}&\bar{c}_{22}\end{pmatrix}

(28)

[
13
]
showed that the diagonals of
{\mathbf{C}}
remain unchanged, and the optimal
{\mathbf{c}}_{12}
is the solution to the following linearly constrained quadratic programming problem:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{c}}_{12}=\text{arg}\,\min\limits_{{\mathbf{y}}}\,\{{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{C}}_{11}^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}\},~~\mbox{s.t.}~~\|{\mathbf{y}}-\bar{{\mathbf{c}}}_{12}\|_{\infty}\leq\rho

(29)

Our algorithm to solve (
25
) is thus as follows.
We minimize the GLASSO terms in (
25
) by solving its dual (
27
)—iteratively updating one row / column of
{\mathbf{C}}
using (
29
).
We then project
{\mathbf{L}}={\mathbf{C}}^{-1}
to convex cone
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{+}
using our projection operator.
We repeat these two steps till convergence.

5.1
Experiments Setups for Synthetic Datasets

We conducted experiments using synthetic datasets to evaluate the performance of our method (Proj-Lasso) and of competing schemes:

[
8
]

The convergence tolerance for those algorithms was set to

To simulate ground truth graphs, we first randomly located 20 nodes in 2D space and used the Erdos-Renyi model
[
23
]

,

To generate data from
{\mathcal{L}}

.
We then generated data set

An empirical covariance matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}

6
Conclusion

Given observable graph signals to compute an empirical covariance matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
, we propose a new graph learning algorithm to compute the most likely graph Laplacian matrix
{\mathbf{L}}
, where we assume that the first
K
eigenvectors of
{\mathbf{L}}
are pre-selected based on domain knowledge or an alternative criterion.
We first prove that the subspace
{\mathcal{H}}^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}}
of symmetric positive semi-definite (PSD) matrices sharing the first
K
eigenvectors
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}
in a defined Hilbert space is a convex cone.
We construct an operator, inspired by the Gram-Schmidt procedure, to project a positive definite (PD) matrix
{\mathbf{P}}
into
{\mathcal{H}}^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}}
.
We design an efficient algorithm to compute the most suitable
{\mathbf{L}}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{+}_{{\mathbf{u}}}
given
\bar{{\mathbf{C}}}
, combining block coordinate descent (BCD) in GLASSO and our projection operator.
Experimental results show that our algorithm outperformed competing graph learning schemes when the first
K
eigenvectors are known.
