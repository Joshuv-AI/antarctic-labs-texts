Transfer matrix analysis of non-hermitian Hamiltonians: asymptotic spectra and topological eigenvalues
Lars Koekenbier, Hermann Schulz-Baldes

Abstract
Transfer matrix techniques are used to provide a new proof of Widom’s results on the asymptotic spectral theory of finite block Toeplitz matrices. Furthermore, a rigorous treatment of the skin effect, spectral outliers, the generalized Brillouin zone and the bulk-boundary correspondence in such systems is given. This covers chiral Hamiltonians with topological eigenvalues close to zero, but no line-gap. MSC2020: 15B05, 19K56, 81Q99

Overview

This paper is about the spectral theory of translation invariant block-tridiagonal operators of the form

H\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}\ddots&\ddots&&&\\
\ddots&V&T&&\\
&R&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\end{pmatrix}\;,

(1)

where
R,V,T\in{\mathbb{C}}^{L\times L}
are all
L\times L
complex matrices. This non-hermitian Hamiltonian
H
is viewed as a bounded operator on the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})
. It is a widely studied model in non-hermitian quantum mechanics
[
18
,
46
,
1
]
in which, more specifically, the skin effect is discussed
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
as well as the existence of robust topological eigenvalues via a so-called bulk-boundary correspondence
[
45
,
41
,
31
,
44
,
47
,
20
,
28
,
5
,
4
,
30
]
. These latter two physical effects are studied on finite, but large volume compressions
H_{N}
of
H
, namely the restrictions of
H
to
\ell^{2}(\{1,\ldots,N\},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})\cong{\mathbb{C}}^{NL}
. It is well-known that the spectra of
H
and
H_{N}
have little in common, and also differ from the spectrum of the (right) half-space restriction
H^{R}
of
H
to
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})
. The operator
H
is often also called a Laurent operator,
H^{R}
a Toeplitz operator and
H_{N}
a finite Toeplitz matrix, also a compression or section of
H
.

There is an extensive mathematical literature, including the books
[
15
,
9
,
17
,
26
,
7
,
38
,
27
]
, about the spectra of Toeplitz operators and matrices. These sources mainly deal with general (fully occupied) Toeplitz operators with focus on the scalar case
L=1
, but also cover some results on the block case
L\geq 2
. Reference
[
7
]
provides a detailed study of banded scalar Toeplitz matrices, a case that can be cast in the tridiagonal block form (
1
) and is hence recovered by the present analysis (see Example
3
below). Topological non-hermitian systems, however, can only be described by the general matrix-valued case
L\geq 2
. The asymptotic spectral theory of
H_{N}
with
L\geq 2
was studied 50 years ago by Widom in the breakthrough work
[
42
]
, but there seem to be only few further developments since (let us mention Delvaux’s work
[
13
]
though; also remarkable is Widom’s work on Szego asymptotics of the Toeplitz determinants
[
43
]
with numerous follow-ups nicely described in
[
2
]
, but this does not seem to be directly relevant for the asymptotic spectral theory).

This work provides an elementary approach based on transfer matrix methods which are well-known for selfadjoint block Jacobi matrices. For the scalar case
L=1
, there are countless contributions by both the physics and mathematics community, starting with work of Dyson, Schmidt and others in the 1950’s. The block Jacobi case
L\geq 2
is heavily used in the solid state physics community where it is at the basis for numerical studies of Anderson localization, see the review
[
21
]
. This is tightly connected to the field of products of (symplectic) random matrices,
e.g.

[
10
]
. Important elements of the mathematical theory (restricted to the hermitian case) are matrix-valued Sturm-Liouville-type oscillation theory
[
34
]
and Weyl theory
[
35
]
(these works contain references to various earlier contributions, in particular, from the Russian school). These techniques also transpose to block CMV matrices
[
12
]
and the slightly more general scattering zippers
[
25
]
. As to transfer matrix techniques for the non-hermitian case (
1
), there is the work
[
16
]
for the scalar case
L=1
, but for
L\geq 2
, we are only aware of the non-rigorous works of the physics community
[
22
,
24
,
20
,
46
,
23
,
28
,
11
]
.

In this work transfer matrix methods are implemented for non-hermitian block Toeplitz matrices. They lead to new self-contained proofs of Widom’s main results on asymptotic spectra
[
42
]
, albeit under somewhat different hypotheses. The arguments also invoke Widom’s determinant formula (which is re-derived below), but it is then used to explicitly construct eigenfunctions of the finite volume operators
H_{N}
rather than appealing to potential theory. Along the way it is also shown that non-hermitian Bloch theory is essentially a rediscovery of Widom’s results by several theoretical physicists
[
45
,
23
,
46
]
. Furthermore, a new criterion for topological zero-energy eigenvalues (bound states) is derived for Hamiltonians having a chiral symmetry. This is rooted in an interplay of Widom’s results with index-theoretic statements (essentially only the well-known Noether-Gohberg-Krein index theorem). In particular, this allows to show that such chiral Hamiltonians can have topological approximate zero modes (namely eigenvalues close to
0
) without having a line-gap, a situation that to the best of our knowledge is not covered by the physics literature. Summing up, we hope that this work opens up an alternative perspective for mathematicians interested in the spectral theory of Toeplitz matrices, and on the other hand brings some of the existing deep mathematical results to the attention of the theoretical physics community.

Let us now start with a short overview of the main results. Crucial is the following hypothesis, that is assumed throughout the whole text.

Hypothesis A:

Both
T
and
R
are invertible matrices.

It allows for the construction of the
2L\times 2L
transfer matrix at complex energy
E\in{\mathbb{C}}
by

{\cal T}^{E}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}(E\,{\bf 1}-V)T^{-1}&-R\\
T^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\;,

(2)

and implies that
{\cal T}^{E}
is invertible. In Section
2
it is recalled how the transfer matrix allows for the construction of solutions of the eigenvalue equations for
H
,
H^{R}
and
H_{N}
. Of crucial importance are the eigenvalues
z_{1}(E),\ldots,z_{2L}(E)
of
{\cal T}^{E}
, ordered such that
|z_{l}(E)|\leq|z_{l+1}(E)|
for
l=1,\ldots,2L-1
. Degeneracies are collected in the set

{\cal F}\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,{{\cal T}}^{E}\mbox{ has a degenerate eigenvalue}\big\}\;.

(3)

Hypothesis B:

The set
\mathcal{F}
is assumed to be finite.

The spectra of
{\cal T}^{E}
furthermore allow for the introduction of the following two subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
:

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,{{\cal T}}^{E}\mbox{ has at least one eigenvalue of modulus }1\big\}\;,

(4)

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,\mbox{middle two eigenvalues }z_{L}(E),\,z_{L+1}(E)\;\mbox{ of }{{\cal T}}^{E}\mbox{ have the same modulus}\big\}.

(5)

It is relatively elementary to see that
\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
is precisely the spectrum of the Laurent operator
H
. Theorem
1
below will show that
\Lambda
is the attractor of the bulk states of
H_{N}
, under suitable further hypotheses.

Hypothesis C:

|z_{L-1}(E)|<|z_{L}(E)|=|z_{L+1}(E)|<|z_{L+2}(E)|
for all but finitely many
E\in\partial\Lambda
.

Here
\partial\Lambda
denotes the boundary of
\Lambda
. It is shown in Sections
4.1
and
4.6
that Hypothesis C implies that
\Lambda=\partial\Lambda
and that it is given by finite union of analytic arcs. To formulate the last hypothesis, consider the sets
I_{0}=\{1,\ldots,L\}
and
I_{1}=\{1,\ldots,L-1,L+1\}
and let
{{\cal R}}_{I_{j}}^{E}
denote the Riesz projection of
{\cal T}^{E}
onto the
L
eigenvalues
z_{l}(E)
with
l\in I_{j}
where
j=0,1
. (It is irrelevant in the sequel that the definition is ambiguous for
E\in\Lambda
; the notation
{{\cal R}}_{I_{j}}^{E}
instead of
{{\cal R}}_{j}^{E}
may seem awkward and clumsy at this point, but fits nicely in the general framework developed below.) Then introduce

q_{I_{j}}(E)\;=\;{\det}_{L}\Big(\binom{0}{{\bf 1}}^{*}{{\cal R}}_{I_{j}}^{E}\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}\Big)\;,\qquad E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\cal F}\;.

Hypothesis D:

The functions
q_{I_{0}}
and
q_{I_{1}}
are not constantly zero locally near points in
\partial\Lambda\setminus\mathcal{F}
.

Finally let us introduce the discrete set:

\displaystyle\Gamma\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Lambda\,:\,q_{I_{0}}(E)=0\big\}\;.

(6)

It is referred to as the set of spectral outliers. The next theorem is the main result about spectral asymptotics of the
H_{N}
, essentially contained in
[
42
]
because it is shown in Section
4
that Hypothesis C implies Condition A and B in
[
42
]
.

Theorem 1

Suppose that
Hypotheses A, B, C
and
D
hold. Then for all
E\in\partial\Lambda\cup\Gamma
but a finite number of
E
, there exist
E_{N}\in\mbox{\rm spec}(H_{N})
such that
\lim_{N\to\infty}E_{N}=E
.

Plots for the same model and parameters described in
Figs.
4
and
5
, except
s=1.4
. On the left, the black curve shows
\Sigma
and in blue are the eigenvalues

for

. The right plot shows the set

. There is a twice degenerate approximate zero mode visible in the left plot, as predicted by

.

Section
4.6
contains a more precise formulation of this result. It also provides an example of a Hamiltonian for which Hypotheses C and D are violated and the set
\Lambda
has non-empty interior. Such non-generic models typically have some symmetries leading to degeneracies in the spectrum of
{\cal T}^{E}
. Figure
1
illustrates Theorem
1
. It contains numerical plots for a Hamiltonian
H
with
L=2
that is described in detail in Section
5.4
. Here the focus is on the qualitative aspects. The continuous curve in the left plot is the spectrum
\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
of the periodic operator, and the dots show the eigenvalues of
H_{N}
for

computed with the Mathematica eigenvalue solver (there are thus
400
points, counting multiplicities). The right plot shows that set
\Lambda

), together with bigger dots showing the points in
{\cal F}
(at the ends of the arcs). One readily observes the good agreement of
\Lambda
with
\mbox{\rm spec}(H_{N})
as predicted by Theorem
1
. Further examples are dealt with in Section
5
which also contains a discussion of the skin effect and the generalized Brillouin zone.

A more careful inspection of Figure
1
also shows that there is an eigenvalue of
H_{N}
close to
0
, actually there are two such eigenvalues. These are the approximate zero modes advertised above. In fact, the Hamiltonian in question has a chiral symmetry. For even
L
, such a symmetry is implemented by the matrix
K=\binom{{\bf 1}\;\;\;0}{0\;-{\bf 1}}
with blocks of size
\frac{L}{2}
. Then
H
is said to have a chiral symmetry if
KRK=-R
,
KVK=-V
and
KTK=-T
. The symmetry immediately implies that
\Sigma=-\Sigma
,
\Lambda=-\Lambda
and
\Gamma=-\Gamma
, which is also visible in Figure
1
. Furthermore, one can readily check that
R
,
V
and
T
are block off-diagonal in the grading of
K
and we will denote the upper right entries by
R_{+}
,
V_{+}
and
T_{+}
and the lower left ones by
R_{-}
,
V_{-}
and
T_{-}
. The following result gives a criterion for
0\in\Gamma
involving two winding numbers associated to merely these latter six matrices (in physics jargon, bulk invariants). It is proved in Section
4.4
. By Theorem
1
this then implies that
H_{N}
has an eigenvalue near
0
, hence establishing a bulk-boundary correspondence.

Theorem 2

Let
H
be chiral and satisfy
Hypothesis A
. Further let
W_{\pm}(s)\in{\mathbb{Z}}
denote the winding numbers of
z\in{\mathbb{T}}\mapsto\det\big((sz)^{-1}R_{\pm}+V_{\pm}+(sz)T_{\pm}\big)
. If
W_{+}(s)=-W_{-}(s)\not=0
for some
s>0
, then
0\in\Gamma
.

To apply the criterion, one first has to search for an
s>0
such that
W_{+}(s)=-W_{-}(s)
, and then check that this number is non-vanishing. This is carried out in Section
5.4
. The criterion in Theorem
2
differs from that in other works mentioned above, in particular, also the one in the supplementary material of
[
28
]
. Let us also stress that
\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
may encircle
0
so that, again in physics jargon, the system has a point gap, but no line gap. Therefore standard index-theoretic arguments assuring the existence of zero modes
[
31
,
20
]
do not apply in this situation. Once available, these zero modes can potentially be used for technological innovations,
e.g.
trapped light modes
[
29
]
or topological sensors
[
11
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
transfer matrices and their spectra are introduced. Section
3
is essentially index-theoretic, but also already establishes links to the Riesz projections. Then in Section
4
the sets
\Lambda
and
\Gamma
are analyzed in detail and Theorems
1
and
2
are proven. Finally Section
5
offers a physics perspective on these results and provides varies numerical examples.

Set-up and basic properties

In this section, many of the objects are introduced and some basic properties are described.

2.1
Hamiltonians and their symbols

Throughout this text, the non-hermitian Hamiltonian
H
on the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})
is the Laurent operator given by (
1
). It is hence specified by the three
L\times L
matrices
R
,
V
and
T
. The operator
H
is self-adjoint if and only if
V^{*}=V
and
R=T^{*}
. The finite volume approximation of
H
on
\ell^{2}(\{1,\ldots,N\},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})
will be denoted by
H_{N}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{NL\times NL}
(in the mathematics literature,
H_{N}
is called a section or a compression of
H
). Moreover,
H^{R}
and
H^{L}
are the right and left half-space restrictions of
H
to the half-line Hilbert spaces
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})
and
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}}_{\leq},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})
respectively. The operators
H^{R}
and
H^{L}
are called Toeplitz operators in the mathematics literature. Note that
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})=\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}}_{\leq},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})\oplus\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})
and that one has

H\,-\,H^{L}\oplus H^{R}\;=\;|1\rangle\,R\,\langle 0|\;+\;|0\rangle\,T\,\langle 1|\;,

(7)

where the Dirac bra-ket notation was used for partial isometries
|n\rangle:{\mathbb{C}}^{L}\to\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})
onto the matrix degrees of freedom over the lattice sites
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. In particular, (
7
) implies that
H^{L}\oplus H^{R}
is a compact perturbation of
H
. The next example shows under which conditions banded scalar Laurent and Toeplitz operators fit into the present framework.

Example 3

A Laurent operator with scalar entries and finite band width can be cast into the form (
1
) and satisfy Hypothesis A, provided it is balanced with as many upper as lower diagonals. More precisely, let
t_{-L},t_{1-L},\ldots,t_{L-1},t_{L}\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
t_{\pm L}\not=0
and consider the Hamiltonian
H
on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
with entries
H_{l,k}=\langle l|H|k\rangle=\chi(|l-k|\leq L)\,t_{l-k}
where
\chi
denotes the indicator function. Then set

R=\begin{pmatrix}t_{L}&t_{L-1}&\cdots&t_{1}\\
0&t_{L}&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&t_{L-1}\\
0&\cdots&0&t_{L}\end{pmatrix}\,,\;\;V=\begin{pmatrix}t_{0}&t_{-1}&\cdots&t_{-L}\\
t_{1}&t_{0}&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&t_{-1}\\
t_{L+1}&\cdots&t_{1}&t_{0}\end{pmatrix}\,,\;\;T=\begin{pmatrix}t_{-L}&0&\cdots&0\\
t_{1-L}&t_{-L}&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&0\\
t_{-1}&\cdots&t_{1-L}&t_{-L}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Hence clearly
R
and
T
are invertible due to
t_{\pm L}\not=0
.

\diamond

Example 4

A special class of periodic scalar banded operators
H
can also be dealt with using Hamiltonians of the form (
1
). Suppose that the matrix entries
H_{l,k}=\langle l|H|k\rangle
satisfy
H_{l+p,k+p}=H_{l,k}
for all
k,l\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and for an integer
p
called the period. If, moreover,
H_{l,k}=0
for
|k-l|>p
, that is, the band width is
2p+1
, then one can set

R=\begin{pmatrix}H_{1,-p+1}&H_{1,-p+2}&\cdots&H_{1,0}\\
0&H_{2,-p+2}&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&H_{p-1,0}\\
0&\cdots&0&H_{p,0}\end{pmatrix}\,,\;\;\;\;T=\begin{pmatrix}H_{1,p+1}&0&\cdots&0\\
H_{2,p+1}&H_{2,p+2}&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&0\\
H_{p,p+1}&\cdots&H_{p,2p}&H_{p,2p+1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

If furthermore
V
is simply the compression of
H
onto the sites
\{1,\ldots,p\}
, then
H
coincides with (
1
). If all the diagonal entries of
R
and
T
are non-vanishing, then Hypothesis A is satisfied and the transfer matrix methods can be used. Note that the entries
H_{l,k}
may also be square matrices instead of scalars, without altering the reasoning above. This example may seem somewhat particular because the band width and periodicity have to coincide. It is, however, possible to deal with more general periodic banded matrices by applying the reduced transfer matrices similarly as in
[
14
,
32
,
23
,
37
]
. This will be developed elsewhere.

\diamond

The symbol of
H
is a matrix-valued meromorphic function given by

H(z)\;=\;R\,z^{-1}\,+\,V\,+\,T\,z\;,

(8)

and furthermore the associated symbol is defined by

\widetilde{H}(z)\;=\;H(z^{-1})\;.

(9)

Let us stress that these definitions differ from a large part of the literature where
H
and
\widetilde{H}
are exchanged
[
42
,
43
,
9
,
7
,
17
,
13
]
. The reason for the modification is that it leads to nice consistent notations in connection with the transfer matrix, most notably in (
19
) and (
20
) below. The associated symbol also specifies an operator
\widetilde{H}
which is defined as in (
1
), but with
R
and
T
exchanged. One then also has corresponding half-space operators
\widetilde{H}^{R}
and
\widetilde{H}^{L}
, as well as finite volume restrictions
\widetilde{H}_{N}
. Using a spatial flip implemented by a selfadjoint unitary, one sees that
\widetilde{H}
and
\widetilde{H}_{N}
are unitarily equivalent to
H
and
H_{N}
respectively. This does not apply to the half-sided operator though. Indeed, consider the unitary
W:\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}}_{\leq},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})\to\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{C}}^{L})
given by
W|-n\rangle=|n+1\rangle
for
n\geq 0
, for which one then has

WH^{L}W^{*}\;=\;\widetilde{H}^{R}\;,\qquad WH^{R}W^{*}\;=\;\widetilde{H}^{L}\;.

(10)

Also the adjoint
H^{*}
will be of relevance and its associated symbol will be denoted by

\widehat{H}(z)\;=\;T^{*}\,z^{-1}\,+\,V^{*}\,+\,R^{*}\,z\;.

For selfadjoint
H
, one has
\widehat{H}(z)=H(z)
. For the half-sided operators, one then checks

\widehat{H}^{R}\;=\;(H^{R})^{*}\;=\;(H^{*})^{R}\;,\qquad\widehat{H}^{L}\;=\;(H^{L})^{*}\;=\;(H^{*})^{L}\;.

(11)

Of crucial importance will be the resolvent of the symbol
E\in{\mathbb{C}}\mapsto(H(z)-E\,{\bf 1})^{-1}
and the associated symbol
E\in{\mathbb{C}}\mapsto(\widetilde{H}(z)-E\,{\bf 1})^{-1}
which are meromorphic functions in
z
and
E
with values in
{\mathbb{C}}^{L\times L}
. The determinants

D(z,E)\;=\;{\det}_{L}(H(z)-E\,{\bf 1})\;,\qquad\widetilde{D}(z,E)\;=\;{\det}_{L}(\widetilde{H}(z)-E\,{\bf 1})

(12)

can be factorized as

D(z,E)\;=\;z^{-L}\,d(z,E)\;,\qquad\widetilde{D}(z,E)\;=\;z^{-L}\,\widetilde{d}(z,E)\;.

where

d(z,E)\;=\;{\det}_{L}(z\,H(z)-z\,E\,{\bf 1})\;,\qquad\widetilde{d}(z,E)\;=\;{\det}_{L}(z\,\widetilde{H}(z)-z\,E\,{\bf 1})\;,

are polynomials in
z
of degree
2L
.

2.2
Spectra of periodic (Laurent) operators

The spectrum of the periodic operator
H
can readily be computed by the discrete Fourier transform
{\cal F}:\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
where
{\mathbb{T}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is the unit circle equipped with the Lebesgue measure, trivially extended to the vector-valued case. It shows that the spectrum
\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
of
H
satisfies

\Sigma\;=\;\bigcup_{z\in{\mathbb{T}}}\,\mbox{\rm spec}(H(z))\;.

(13)

As
z\in{\mathbb{T}}\mapsto H(z)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{L\times L}
is a real analytic function (with an analytic extension to a neighborhood of
{\mathbb{T}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}
), analytic perturbation theory implies that all eigenvalues of
H(z)
are real analytic in
z
except at level crossings, where the branches can be chosen analytic in the
L
th root (Puiseux expansion)
[
19
]
. In conclusion,
\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
is given by a set of closed curves in
{\mathbb{C}}
.

2.3
Transfer matrices

Another standard element of the theory of hermitian block Jacobi operators is the transfer matrix. It allows for the construction of the formal solutions of the Schödinger equation
H\psi=E\psi
for a complex energy
E
and a state
\psi=(\psi_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
with
\psi_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{L}
which is not necessarily in the Hilbert space. For the periodic operator
H
given in (
1
) the Schrödinger equation is

T\psi_{n+1}\,+\,V\psi_{n}\,+\,R\psi_{n-1}\;=\;E\psi_{n}\;,\qquad n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\;.

As
T
is invertible (see Hypothesis A), this can be rewritten as

\begin{pmatrix}T\psi_{n+1}\\
\psi_{n}\end{pmatrix}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}(E\,{\bf 1}-V)T^{-1}&-R\\
T^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}T\psi_{n}\\
\psi_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}\;,

(14)

where the second line of this equation is tautological. Therefore one introduces the transfer matrix at complex energy
E
by (
2
) which can also be factorized as

{\cal T}^{E}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}E\,{\bf 1}-V&-{\bf 1}\\
{\bf 1}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}T^{-1}&0\\
0&R\end{pmatrix}\;.

(15)

Then one can iterate (
14
) to obtain

\begin{pmatrix}T\psi_{n+1}\\
\psi_{n}\end{pmatrix}\;=\;({\cal T}^{E})^{n}\begin{pmatrix}T\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{0}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(16)

If
T
is not invertible, one can construct a reduced transfer matrix of size
2r
where
r
is the rank of
T

[
23
]
. For sake of simplicity, this is not carried out here, but let us stress that this allows to analyze many more Laurent and Toeplitz operators of finite band width by transfer matrix techniques. Note that
\det_{2L}({\cal T}^{E})=\det_{L}(T)^{-1}\det_{L}(R)
so that the transfer matrix is invertible for all
E\in{\mathbb{C}}
as also
R
is invertible (see Hypothesis A). The inverse of
{\cal T}^{E}
is explicitly given by

({\cal T}^{E})^{-1}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}T&0\\
0&R^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&{\bf 1}\\
-{\bf 1}&E\,{\bf 1}-V\end{pmatrix}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&T\\
-R^{-1}&R^{-1}(E\,{\bf 1}-V)\end{pmatrix}\;.

Associated to
\widetilde{H}(z)
and
\widehat{H}(z)
, there are two further naturally associated transfer matrices:

\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}(E\,{\bf 1}-V)R^{-1}&-T\\
R^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\;,\qquad\widehat{{\cal T}}^{E}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}(E\,{\bf 1}-V^{*})(R^{*})^{-1}&-T^{*}\\
(R^{*})^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

Straightforward algebra shows:

(\widehat{{\cal T}}^{\overline{E}})^{*}\,{\cal I}\,{\cal T}^{E}\;=\;{\cal I}\;,\qquad{\cal I}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&-{\bf 1}\\
{\bf 1}&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

(17)

Note that this implies
({\cal T}^{E})^{-1}={\cal I}^{*}(\widehat{{\cal T}}^{\overline{E}})^{*}{\cal I}
.
If the Hamiltonian is hermitian and the energy
E\in{\mathbb{R}}
is real, the transfer matrix thus satisfies the
{\cal I}
-unitary relation
{\cal T}^{*}{\cal I}{\cal T}={\cal I}
. The above identity is hence the non-hermitian analogue of the
{\cal I}
-unitarity relation. There is yet another identity that will be used below, namely one readily checks

\begin{pmatrix}0&R\\
T^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E}\begin{pmatrix}0&R\\
T^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\;=\;({\cal T}^{E})^{-1}\;.

(18)

Of great importance will be the resolvent of the transfer matrix. It can be computed by the Schur complement formula:

\displaystyle({\cal T}^{E}\,-\,z\,{\bf 1})^{-1}

\displaystyle\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}(E\,{\bf 1}-V)T^{-1}\,-\,z\,{\bf 1}&-R\\
T^{-1}&\,-\,z\,{\bf 1}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}

\displaystyle\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}S^{-1}&-z^{-1}\,S^{-1}\,R\\
z^{-1}\,T^{-1}\,S^{-1}&-z^{-1}\,{\bf 1}-z^{-2}\,T^{-1}\,S^{-1}\,R\end{pmatrix}

where

S\;=\;(E\,{\bf 1}-V)T^{-1}\,-\,z\,{\bf 1}\,-\,z^{-1}\,R\,T^{-1}\;=\;\big(E\,{\bf 1}\,-\,{H}(z)\big)\,T^{-1}\;.

Replacing the latter condition shows

\displaystyle({\cal T}^{E}\,-\,z\,{\bf 1})^{-1}

\displaystyle\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}T&0\\
0&{\bf 1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\big(E\,{\bf 1}\,-\,{H}(z)\big)^{-1}&-z^{-1}\,\big(E\,{\bf 1}\,-\,{H}(z)\big)^{-1}\\
z^{-1}\,\big(E\,{\bf 1}\,-\,{H}(z)\big)^{-1}&-\,z^{-1}\,R^{-1}\,-\,z^{-2}\,\big(E\,{\bf 1}\,-\,{H}(z)\big)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf 1}&0\\
0&R\end{pmatrix}\;.

(19)

Next let us compute the characteristic polynomical of the transfer matrix. Recalling the identity

\det\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\;=\;\det(D)\det(A-BD^{-1}C)\;,

one finds

\displaystyle{\det}_{2L}({\cal T}^{E}-z\,{\bf 1})

\displaystyle\;=\;(-z)^{L}\,{\det}_{L}\big((E-V)T^{-1}-z\,{\bf 1}\,-\,z^{-1}RT^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle\;=\;z^{L}\,{\det}_{L}(T)^{-1}\,{\det}_{L}(V-E\,{\bf 1}+zT+z^{-1}R)

\displaystyle\;=\;z^{L}\,{\det}_{L}(T)^{-1}\,{\det}_{L}(H(z)-E\,{\bf 1})

(20)

\displaystyle\;=\;z^{L}\,{\det}_{L}(T)^{-1}\,{D}(z,E)

\displaystyle\;=\;{\det}_{L}(T)^{-1}\,{d}(z,E)\;.

This identity also follows by taking the determinant of (
19
). This shows that
\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
is indeed given by both (
4
) and (
13
). Together with (
18
), one also deduces

{\det}_{2L}({\cal T}^{E}-z\,{\bf 1})\;=\;{\det}_{2L}\big((\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E})^{-1}-z\,{\bf 1}\big)\;.

(21)

2.4
Spectral propertes of transfer matrices

Let
z_{1}(E),\ldots,z_{2L}(E)
be the
2L
eigenvalues of
{\cal T}^{E}
, listed with their multiplicity and ordered such that
|z_{l}(E)|\leq|z_{l+1}(E)|
. As they are the zeros of characteristic polynomial and
{\cal T}^{E}
is invertible, it follows from (
20
) that they are also the zeros of
z\mapsto D(z,E)
(also of
z\mapsto d(z,E)
). Similarly, the eigenvalues
\widetilde{z}_{1}(E),\ldots,\widetilde{z}_{2L}(E)
of
\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E}
, ordered such that
|\widetilde{z}_{l}(E)|\leq|\widetilde{z}_{l+1}(E)|
, are the zeros of
z\mapsto\widetilde{D}(z,E)
. From (
21
), one then deduces the equalities

\widetilde{z}_{k}(E)\;=\;\frac{1}{z_{2L+1-k}(E)}\;.

(22)

In particular,

\mbox{\rm spec}(\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E})\;=\;\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{E})^{-1}\;,

(23)

which also follows directly from (
18
). Moreover, it follows from identity (
17
) that

\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{E})^{-1}\;=\;\overline{\mbox{\rm spec}(\widehat{{\cal T}}^{\overline{E}})}\;.

If the Hamiltonian is hermitian and the energy
E\in{\mathbb{R}}
is real, then the spectrum satisfies
\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{E})^{-1}=\overline{\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{E})}
. At several instances it will be useful to diagonalize
{\cal T}^{E}
. Of course, this is not possible for all
E
as, in general, there may be Jordan blocks at level crossings. If all Jordan blocks are trivial, let the basis change
{\cal M}^{E}
be chosen such that

({\cal M}^{E})^{-1}{\cal T}^{E}{\cal M}^{E}\;=\;{\rm diag}\big({z}_{1}(E),\ldots,{z}_{2L}(E)\big)\;=\;{\rm diag}\big(\widetilde{z}_{2L}(E)^{-1},\ldots,\widetilde{z}_{1}(E)^{-1}\big)\;.

(24)

Recall that
{\cal F}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
defined in (
3
) consists of the set of
E\in{\mathbb{C}}
for which
{\cal T}^{E}
has a degenerate eigenvalue. Away from the set
{\cal F}
, diagonalization is always possible with a matrix
{\cal M}^{E}
which is unique up to normalization (right multiplication by an invertible diagonal matrix).

Lemma 5

\mathcal{F}
is either finite or
\mathcal{F}={\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

Recall that the eigenvalues of
{\cal T}^{E}
are precisely the zeros of the polynomial
p_{E}(z)=d(z,E)={\det}_{L}(z(H(z)-E{\bf 1}))
. Further recall that the polynomial
p_{E}
has zeros of order greater than one if and only if the resultant
r_{E}
of
p_{E}
and its derivative
p^{\prime}_{E}
is zero. The resultant is the determinant of the Sylvester matrix and hence a polynomial expression in the coefficients of the polynomials
p_{E}
and its derivative
p^{\prime}_{E}
which in turn are polynomials in
E
. Therefore the resultant
r_{E}
is also polynomial in
E
. It is thus either zero for all or just for a finite set of
E
.

\Box

Hypothesis B states that we only consider the first of the two cases in Lemma
5
.

Lemma 6

Let
z_{i}(E)
and
z_{j}(E)
be two continuous choices of eigenvalues of
{{\cal T}}^{E}
. If
|z_{i}(E)|=|z_{j}(E)|
for all
E
in some open subset of
{\mathbb{C}}
, then there exists a scalar
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that
z_{i}(E)=c\,z_{j}(E)
for all
E\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

Note that the
z_{i}(E)
are the zeros of the polynomial
p_{E}(z)={\det}_{L}(z(H(z)-E{\bf 1}))
. Since zeros of polynomials are continuous in the coefficients of the polynomial and the coefficients of
p_{E}(z)
are polynomial in
E
, the
z_{i}(E)
can indeed be chosen continuous in
E
. Moreover, for such a choice the
z_{i}(E)
are well-known to be holomorphic in
E
on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus V
, where
V
is some branch cut
[
19
]
.

Let now
U\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be the open subset on which
|z_{i}(E)|=|z_{j}(E)|
. Then
z_{i}(E)/z_{j}(E)
is holomorphic on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus V
and has constant modulus equal to
1
on the open non-empty set
U\setminus V
. By the maximum modulus principle it follows that
z_{i}(E)/z_{j}(E)
is constant on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus V
, notably there is a scalar
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that
z_{i}(E)=c\,z_{j}(E)
for all
E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus V
. By continuity this also extends to
V
.

\Box

3
Spectra of half-sided (Toeplitz) operators

This section collects results on the spectrum of the half-sided (Toeplitz) operators
H^{R}
and
H^{L}
. This involves two technical elements: the Noether-Gohberg-Krein index theorem and the study of eigenvalues using the intersection theory of the boundary conditions with the contracting directions of the transfer matrices.

3.1
Winding number and index theorem

According to (
7
), the direct sum
H^{R}\oplus H^{L}
differs from
H
only by a compact operator. Hence
\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)\subset\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{R})\cup\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{L})
by a Weyl sequence argument (which is feasible because
\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
has empty interior). Furthermore, a direct consequence of analytic Fredholm theory (
e.g.
Appendix D in
[
36
]
) shows that the components of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
either only contain discrete spectrum of
H^{R}\oplus H^{L}
or each point in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
is an eigenvalue of
H^{R}\oplus H^{L}
(each of these eigenvalues are eigenvalues of either
H^{R}
or
H^{L}
, but not necessarily both). Which one of the two cases applies can be read off the winding number of the determinant of the symbol shifted by a complex energy
E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
, more precisely defined by

{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)\;=\;\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath}\;\partial_{z}\;\log\big({\det}_{L}(H(z)-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\;.

Due to (
20
) and (
19
), this is also given by

\displaystyle{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)

\displaystyle\;=\;\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath}\;\partial_{z}\;\log\big(z^{-L}\,{\det}_{L}(R)\,{\det}_{2L}({{\cal T}}^{E}-z\,{\bf 1})\big)\;=\;-L\;+\;\sum_{|z|<1}\,m_{z}({{\cal T}}^{E})\;,

(25)

where
m_{z}({\cal T})
denotes the algebraic multiplicity of
z
as eigenvalue of
{\cal T}
so that the sums are actually finite. Indeed, a classical theorem (due to Noether, Gohberg and Krein and holding even for continuous symbols, see
e.g.
Theorem 6.5 in
[
9
]
where there is a different sign due to the altered definition (
8
) of the symbol) is the following:

Theorem 7

For all
E\in{\mathbb{C}}
,

H^{R}-E\,{\bf 1}\;\;\mbox{\rm Fredholm }\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{\det}_{L}(H(z)-E\,{\bf 1})\,\not=\,0\quad\mbox{\rm for all }z\in{\mathbb{T}}\;,

(26)

and, if this holds, its index is given by

{\rm Ind}(H^{R}-E\,{\bf 1})\;=\;{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)\;.

(27)

This shows that
\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
is actually equal to the essential spectrum
\mbox{\rm spec}_{\mbox{\rm\tiny ess}}(H^{R})
of
H^{R}
(which by definition consists of all
E
such that
H^{R}-E\,{\bf 1}
is a Fredholm operator, so we do
not
use Weyl’s notion of essential spectrum here). Moreover,
\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
consists of curves in
{\mathbb{C}}
that split
{\mathbb{C}}
in several connected components; picking a point
E
in one of these components, one has a winding number
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)
; if it is non-zero, then the whole component of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
belongs to the spectrum of
H^{R}
; if the winding vanishes, one has to analyze the spectrum in that component by other means; the unbounded component of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
will never be in the essential spectrum of
H^{R}
though. Thus:

Corollary 8

One has

\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{R})\;\supset\;\Sigma\cup\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma\,:\,{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)\not=0\big\}\;.

The same arguments apply to
\widetilde{H}^{R}=WH^{L}W^{*}
. Moreover, (
26
) and (
22
) also imply

\displaystyle H^{R}-E\,{\bf 1}\;\;\mbox{\rm Fredholm }\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\widetilde{H}^{R}-E\,{\bf 1}\;\;\mbox{\rm Fredholm }\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H^{L}-E\,{\bf 1}\;\;\mbox{\rm Fredholm }.

(28)

As

0\;=\;{\rm Ind}(H-E\,{\bf 1})\;=\;{\rm Ind}(H^{R}-E\,{\bf 1})\,+\,{\rm Ind}(H^{L}-E\,{\bf 1})

(29)

by (
7
), one has

\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{R})\cap\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{L})\;\supset\;\Sigma\cup\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma\,:\,{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)\not=0\big\}\;.

The discrete spectra of
H^{R}
and
H^{L}
lie outside of this set and are, in general, not the same. It is one of the objects of this work to study these so-called
outliers
of the spectrum. Under suitable conditions, zero energy outliers will be shown to be stable and of topological nature.

3.2
Uniform point spectrum

The spectrum of half-sided (Toeplitz) operators
H^{R}
and
H^{L}
can have open subsets consisting entirely of eigenvalues, and we will refer to such regions as uniform point spectrum. This is well-known to occur for the example of the half-sided shift operator on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})
, and the arguments transpose to other cases. Here several mathematical perspectives on the phenomenon are offered. First of all, the index theorem can also be used to determine eigenvalues for the half-sided operators
H^{R}
and
H^{L}
. Indeed, for
E\not\in\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
, Theorem
7
implies

\displaystyle{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)

\displaystyle\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\,-\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}((H^{R})^{*}-\overline{E}\,{\bf 1})\big)

(30)

\displaystyle\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}((H^{L})^{*}-\overline{E}\,{\bf 1})\big)\,-\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{L}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\;,

(31)

where the first equality follows directly from (
27
) and the definition of the index and the second one, moreover, uses (
10
) as well as the important fact

{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)\;=\;-\,{\rm Wind}^{E}(\widetilde{H})\;.

(32)

For non-vanishing winding number, (
30
) and (
31
) imply a dichotomy (with defect):

Proposition 9

If
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)>0
, then
{\rm Ker}(H^{R}-E\,{\bf 1})\not=\{0\}
and
E
is an eigenvalue of
H^{R}
.
If
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)<0
, then
{\rm Ker}(H^{L}-E\,{\bf 1})\not=\{0\}
and
E
is an eigenvalue of
H^{L}
.

Note that the winding number is locally constant on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
and only changes on
\Sigma
. As, moreover,
\mbox{\rm spec}((H^{R})^{*})=\overline{\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{R})}
, a connected component of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
belongs entirely to the spectrum of
H^{R}
if the winding number is non-vanishing in this component. The same statements hold for
H^{L}
. Note also that these facts align exactly with analytic Fredholm theory as described in
[
36
]
.

Another perspective on (
30
) and (
31
) (actually only a disguise of the above) invokes the bulk-boundary correspondence
[
31
]
for an associated hermitian Hamiltonian given by

h^{E}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&H\,-\,E\,{\bf 1}\\
H^{*}\,-\,\overline{E}\,{\bf 1}&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

(33)

By construction,
h^{E}
has chiral symmetry, namely
Jh^{E}J=-h^{E}
where
J=\binom{{\bf 1}\;\;\;0}{0\;-{\bf 1}}
is the third Pauli matrix in the grading of (
33
). It is possible to write
h^{E}
again in the form (
1
) with blocks of doubled size
2L
. The off-diagonal entry
H-E{\bf 1}
has a winding number
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)
which in this context is also called the first Chern number
{\rm Ch}_{1}(h^{E})
of the chiral Hamiltonian
h^{E}

[
31
]
. The bulk-boundary correspondence for one-dimensional chiral systems (
e.g.

[
31
]
, here applied to a periodic and not merely covariant system) states that the so-called bulk invariant
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)
is connected to boundary states via

{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)\;=\;-\,{\rm Sig}\big(J|_{{\rm Ker}(h^{E,R})}\big)\;.

(34)

Here
{\rm Sig}
denotes the signature of a quadratic form, given by the restriction of
J
to the subspace
{\rm Ker}(h^{E,R})
where
h^{E,R}
is the half-space restriction of
h^{E}
on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2L})
(with Dirichlet boundary conditions). Because
(H^{*})^{R}=(H^{R})^{*}
by (
11
), one thus deduces

h^{E,R}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&H^{R}\,-\,E\,{\bf 1}\\
(H^{R})^{*}\,-\,\overline{E}\,{\bf 1}&0\end{pmatrix}\;,

and using this in (
34
) then directly leads to (
30
). The second identity (
31
) then follows from
{\rm Wind}^{E}(\widetilde{H})={\rm Sig}\big(J|_{{\rm Ker}(\widetilde{h}^{E,R})}\big)
combined with
\widetilde{H}^{R}=W^{*}H^{L}W
, see (
10
).

Yet another proof of Proposition
9
is rooted in the spectral analysis of the transfer matrix, and this is hence more in line with the remainder of the paper. Let us first focus on
H^{R}
. It has Dirichlet boundary conditions at
0
. Then the geometric multiplicity of
E
as eigenvalue of
H^{R}
is given by

\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}\,-\,E\,{\bf 1})\big)\;=\;\dim\big({\cal E}^{E}_{1}\cap{\cal D}_{0}\big)\;,

(35)

where
{\cal E}^{E}_{r}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2L}
is the span of the eigenvectors of
{\cal T}^{E}
with modulus strictly less than
r
and
{\cal D}_{0}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2L}
is the range of
\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}
, namely fixing the Dirichlet boundary conditions at
0
. Indeed, the Schrödinger equation
H^{R}\psi=E\psi
for
\psi=(\psi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
becomes on the first site
V\psi_{1}+T\psi_{2}=E\psi_{1}
which can be rewritten as
{\cal T}^{E}\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}T={\cal T}^{E}\binom{T}{0}=\binom{E\,{\bf 1}-V}{{\bf 1}}
just as in (
14
). Hence if
\binom{v}{0}\in{\cal E}^{E}_{1}\cap{\cal D}_{0}
, then
({\cal T}^{E})^{n}\binom{v}{0}
is exponentially decreasing and
\psi_{n}=\binom{0}{{\bf 1}}^{*}({\cal T}^{E})^{n}\binom{v}{0}
is thus a square integrable eigenstate of
H^{R}
with energy
E
. Similar statements hold for the eigenvalues of
H^{L}
. Now as
\dim({\cal D}_{0})=L
, the intersection
{\cal E}^{E}_{1}\cap{\cal D}_{0}
is always non-trivial if
\dim({\cal E}^{E}_{1})>L
. However, according to (
25
), one has

\dim({\cal E}^{E}_{1})\;=\;L\,+\,{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)\;.

(36)

Hence if
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)>0
, then
\dim({\cal E}^{E}_{1}\cap{\cal D}_{0})\geq 1
and
E
is an eigenvalue of
H^{R}
. In a similar manner, one can show that
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)<0
implies that
E
is an eigenvalue of
H^{L}
.

3.3
Outliers of half-sided operators

This section is about so-called spectral outliers of
H^{R}
and
H^{L}
, namely eigenvalues
E
in regions where the winding number vanishes, but the intersection in (
35
) is nevertheless non-trivial. It is hence of interest to introduce the associated (skew) Riesz projections

{\cal R}^{E}_{r}\;=\;\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{r}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath}\;(z\,{\bf 1}-{\cal T}^{E})^{-1}\;,

(37)

provided that
{\mathbb{T}}_{r}\cap\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{E})=\emptyset
. Then
{\cal E}^{E}_{r}={\rm Ran}({\cal R}^{E}_{r})
still for
{\mathbb{T}}_{r}\cap\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{E})=\emptyset
, and

\mbox{rk}({\cal R}^{E}_{r})\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ran}({\cal R}^{E}_{r})\big)\;=\;\sum_{|z|<r}m_{z}({\cal T}^{E})\;.

Moreover, using the basis change
{\cal M}^{E}
as in (
24
),

{\cal R}^{E}_{r}\;=\;{\cal M}^{E}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf 1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}({\cal M}^{E})^{-1}\;,

where
{\bf 1}
is of the size
\mbox{rk}({\cal R}^{E}_{r})
.
Changing variables, one also has

{\cal R}^{E}_{r}\;=\;\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath}\;(z\,{\bf 1}-r^{-1}{\cal T}^{E})^{-1}\;,

(38)

To further compute
{\cal R}^{E}_{r}
, let us replace (
19
) for the resolvent of
{\cal T}^{E}
. One finds

{\cal R}^{E}_{r}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}T&0\\
0&{\bf 1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{Q}^{(0)}_{r}(E)&-{Q}^{(1)}_{r}(E)\\
{Q}^{(1)}_{r}(E)&R^{-1}-{Q}^{(2)}_{r}(E)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf 1}&0\\
0&R\end{pmatrix}\;,

(39)

where

{Q}^{(j)}_{r}(E)\;=\;\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{r}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath\,z^{j}}\;\big({H}(z)\,-\,E\,{\bf 1}\big)^{-1}\;,\qquad j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\;.

(40)

Then

{Q}^{(1)}_{r}(E)\;=\;\binom{0}{{\bf 1}}^{*}{\cal R}^{E}_{r}\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}\;=\;-\,T^{-1}\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}^{*}{\cal R}^{E}_{r}\binom{0}{{\bf 1}}R^{-1}\;,

and similarly for the other entries. Changing variables,
{Q}^{(j)}_{r}(E)
can also be rewritten as

{Q}^{(j)}_{r}(E)\;=\;r^{1-j}\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath\,z^{j}}\;\big({H}(r\,z)\,-\,E\,{\bf 1}\big)^{-1}\;,\qquad j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\;.

There are similar objects
\widetilde{{\cal R}}^{E}_{r}
,
\widetilde{Q}^{(j)}_{r}(E)
, etc., constructed from
\widetilde{H}
instead of
{H}
,
e.g.

\widetilde{Q}^{(j)}_{r}(E)\;=\;\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{r}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath\,z^{j}}\;\big(\widetilde{H}(z)\,-\,E\,{\bf 1}\big)^{-1}\;,\qquad j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,,\;\;{\mathbb{T}}_{r}\cap\mbox{\rm spec}(\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E})=\emptyset\;.

As above,

\widetilde{{\cal R}}^{E}_{r}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}R&0\\
0&{\bf 1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{Q}^{(0)}_{r}(E)&-\widetilde{Q}^{(1)}_{r}(E)\\
\widetilde{Q}^{(1)}_{r}(E)&T^{-1}-\widetilde{Q}^{(2)}_{r}(E)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf 1}&0\\
0&T\end{pmatrix}\;.

A change of variable
z\mapsto z^{-1}
also shows that

\widetilde{Q}^{(j)}_{r}(E)\;=\;{Q}^{(2-j)}_{r^{-1}}(E)\;.

(41)

In particular, for
j=1
and
r=1
, one has

\widetilde{Q}^{(1)}_{1}(E)\;=\;{Q}^{(1)}_{1}(E)\;.

Let us now consider
H^{L}
. As
WH^{L}W^{*}=\widetilde{H}^{R}
with the unitary reflection
W
, one has

\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{L}\,-\,E\,{\bf 1})\big)\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(\widetilde{H}^{R}\,-\,E\,{\bf 1})\big)\;=\;\dim\big(\widetilde{{\cal E}}^{E}_{1}\cap{\cal D}_{0}\big)\;,

(42)

where now
\widetilde{{\cal E}}^{E}_{r}={\rm Ran}(\widetilde{{\cal R}}_{r}^{E})
. Using (
18
), one also finds

\displaystyle{\bf 1}\,-\,\widetilde{{\cal R}}^{E}_{r}

\displaystyle\;=\;\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{r}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath}\;\Big[z^{-1}{\bf 1}\,-\,(z\,{\bf 1}-\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E})^{-1}\Big]

\displaystyle\;=\;\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{r}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath}\;z^{-2}\big(z^{-1}{\bf 1}-(\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E})^{-1}\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle\;=\;\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{r^{-1}}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath}\;\big(z\,{\bf 1}-(\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E})^{-1}\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle\;=\;\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{r^{-1}}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath}\;\begin{pmatrix}0&R\\
T^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\big(z\,{\bf 1}-{{\cal T}}^{E}\big)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}0&R\\
T^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}

\displaystyle\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&R\\
T^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}{\cal R}^{E}_{r^{-1}}\begin{pmatrix}0&R\\
T^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\;.

Actually this identity also follows from (
41
) replaced into the representation (
39
), or even directly from (
18
). Equivalently

{\bf 1}\,-\,{{\cal R}}^{E}_{r}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&T\\
R^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\widetilde{{\cal R}}^{E}_{r^{-1}}\begin{pmatrix}0&T\\
R^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\;.

(43)

From this, one deduces

{\rm Ran}({\bf 1}\,-\,{{\cal R}}^{E}_{r})\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&T\\
R^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}{\rm Ran}\big(\widetilde{{\cal R}}^{E}_{r^{-1}}\big)\;.

(44)

By essentially the same arguments, one finds from (
17
) that

{\cal I}^{*}\,({{\cal R}}^{E}_{r})^{*}\,{\cal I}\;=\;{\bf 1}\,-\,\widehat{{\cal R}}^{\overline{E}}_{r^{-1}}\;,

with
\widehat{{\cal R}}^{E}_{r}
defined as in (
37
) with
{\cal T}^{E}
replaced by
\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E}
.

Lemma 10

Recall that
{\cal D}_{0}
is the range of
\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}
. Then for all
r>0
with
{\mathbb{T}}_{r}\cap\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{E})=\emptyset
,

{\rm Ran}(\widetilde{{\cal R}}^{E}_{r^{-1}})\cap{\cal D}_{0}\;=\;{\rm Ran}({\bf 1}-{\cal R}^{E}_{r})\cap{\cal D}_{0}\;.

Proof.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
be such that

\binom{Rv}{0}\;\in\;{\rm Ran}(\widetilde{{\cal R}}^{E}_{r^{-1}})\cap{\cal D}_{0}\;.

Due to (
44
) one then has

\binom{0}{v}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&T\\
R^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\binom{Rv}{0}\;\in\;{\rm Ran}({\bf 1}-{\cal R}^{E}_{r})\;.

As the latter subspace is a direct sum of generalized eigenspaces of
\mathcal{T}^{E}
, it is invariant under
\mathcal{T}^{E}
. Therefore

\mathcal{T}^{E}\binom{0}{v}\;=\;\binom{-Rv}{0}\;\in\;{\rm Ran}({\bf 1}-{\cal R}^{E}_{r})\;.

As the latter vector is clearly also in
{\cal D}_{0}
, one concludes that
{\rm Ran}(\widetilde{{\cal R}}^{E}_{r^{-1}})\cap{\cal D}_{0}\subset{\rm Ran}({\bf 1}-{\cal R}^{E}_{r})\cap{\cal D}_{0}
. The inverse inclusion is shown in the same manner.

\Box

Proposition 11

Suppose that
\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{E})\cap{\mathbb{T}}=\emptyset
, or equivalently
E\not\in\Sigma
. Then
E
is an eigenvalue of
H^{R}
or
H^{L}
if and only if
{\rm Ker}({Q}^{(1)}_{1}(E))\not=\{0\}
. More precisely,

\dim\big({\rm Ker}({Q}^{(1)}_{1}(E))\big)\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{L}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\;.

Proof.

Suppose
E
is an eigenvalue of
H^{R}
with (non-zero and square integrable) eigenvector
\psi=(\psi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. Then
\psi
has to decay,
i.e.
the vector
\binom{T\psi_{n+1}}{\psi_{n}}
lies in the decaying subspace
{\cal E}^{E}_{1}
of
{\cal T}^{E}
. In particular,

\binom{T\psi_{1}}{0}\;\in\;{\rm Ran}({\cal R}^{E}_{1})\cap{\cal D}_{0}\;.

Moreover, since
{\cal R}^{E}_{1}
is an idempotent, one has

\binom{T\psi_{1}}{0}\;=\;{\cal R}^{E}_{1}\binom{T\psi_{1}}{0}\;=\;\binom{T{Q}^{(0)}_{1}(E)T\psi_{1}}{{Q}^{(1)}_{1}(E)T\psi_{1}}\;.

Thus indeed
T\psi_{1}\in{\rm Ker}({Q}^{(1)}_{1}(E))
and
T\psi_{1}
is non-zero, since otherwise successive application of the transfer matrix would imply
\psi=0
. Similarly, supposing
E
is an eigenvalue of
\widetilde{H}^{R}
with eigenvector
\widetilde{\psi}
, one has

\binom{R\widetilde{\psi}_{1}}{0}\;\in\;{\rm Ran}(\widetilde{{\cal R}}^{E}_{1})\cap{\cal D}_{0}

and

\binom{R\widetilde{\psi}_{1}}{0}\;=\;\widetilde{{\cal R}}^{E}_{1}\binom{R\widetilde{\psi}_{1}}{0}\;=\;\binom{R\widetilde{Q}^{(0)}_{1}(E)R\psi_{1}}{\widetilde{Q}^{(1)}_{1}(E)R\psi_{1}}\;=\;\binom{RQ^{(2)}_{1}(E)R\widetilde{\psi}_{1}}{Q^{(1)}_{1}(E)R\widetilde{\psi}_{1}}\;.

Hence
0\neq R\widetilde{\psi}_{1}\in{\rm Ker}(Q^{(1)}_{1}(E))
. Moreover,
{\rm Ran}(\widetilde{{\cal R}}^{E}_{1})\cap{\cal D}_{0}={\rm Ran}({\bf 1}-{\cal R}^{E}_{1})\cap{\cal D}_{0}
by Lemma
10
. As
{\rm Ran}({\cal R}^{E}_{1})
and
{\rm Ran}({\bf 1}-{\cal R}^{E}_{1})
are linearly independent, it follows that also
\binom{T\psi_{1}}{0}
and
\binom{R\widetilde{\psi}_{1}}{0}
are linearly independent. As both first components lie in
{\rm Ker}({Q}^{(1)}_{1}(E))
, one concludes together with (
35
) and (
42
) that

\dim\big({\rm Ker}({Q}^{(1)}_{1}(E))\big)\;\geq\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{L}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\;.

Now let
0\neq v\in{\rm Ker}(Q^{(1)}_{r}(E))
. Then

{\cal R}^{E}_{1}\binom{v}{0}\;=\;\binom{T{Q}^{(0)}_{1}(E)v}{{Q}^{(1)}_{1}(E)v}\;=\;\binom{T{Q}^{(0)}_{1}(E)v}{0}\;\in\;{\rm Ran}({{\cal R}}^{E}_{1})\cap{\cal D}_{0}

and similarly

({\bf 1}-{\cal R}^{E}_{1})\binom{v}{0}\;=\;\binom{({\bf 1}-T{Q}^{(0)}_{1}(E))v}{0}\;\in\;{\rm Ran}({\bf 1}-{\cal R}^{E}_{1})\cap{\cal D}_{0}\,=\,{\rm Ran}(\widetilde{{\cal R}}^{E}_{1})\cap{\cal D}_{0}\;,

where the last claim follows from Lemma
10
. As at least one of the last two vectors is non-vanishing, one concludes that
E
is an eigenvalue of
H^{R}
or of
\widetilde{H}^{R}
due to (
35
) and (
42
). Moreover, if
{\cal S}^{E}=\binom{{\rm Ker}(Q^{(1)}_{1}(E))}{0}\subset{\cal D}_{0}
, then

\displaystyle\dim\big({\rm Ker}({Q}^{(1)}_{1}(E))\big)

\displaystyle\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ran}({\cal R}^{E}_{1})\cap{\cal S}^{E}\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ran}({\bf 1}-{\cal R}^{E}_{1})\cap{\cal S}^{E}\big)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\dim\big({\rm Ran}({\cal R}^{E}_{1})\cap{\cal D}_{0}\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ran}({\bf 1}-{\cal R}^{E}_{1})\cap{\cal D}_{0}\big)

\displaystyle\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(\widetilde{H}^{R}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\;,

concluding the proof due to (
42
).

\Box

Corollary 12

The sum of the geometric multiplicities of
E
as eigenvalue of
H^{R}
or
H^{L}
is at most

:

\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{L}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\;\leq\;L\;.

3.4
Topological zero modes of half-sided chiral operators

This section first defines what a chiral symmetry is and provides some structural information that it induces on the objects introduced so far. It then shows how index-theoretic arguments imply the existence of zero eigenvalues for suitable half-sided (Toeplitz) operators with chiral symmetry. Throughout the dimension
L
will be supposed to be even. Then on each fiber there is selfadjoint unitary of vanishing signature given by
K=\binom{{\bf 1}\;\;\;0}{0\;-{\bf 1}}
. Its fiberwise extension
{\bf 1}\otimes K
will also be denoted by
K
. The chiral symmetry of
H
then reads

K\,H\,K\;=\;-\,H\;.

(45)

Due to the chiral symmetry (
45
), it follows that
\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)=-\Sigma
.
Moreover, one deduces
KVK=-V
,
KTK=-T
and
KRK=-R
. Hence each of these matrices is off-diagonal in the grading of
K
. Let us introduce the notations

V\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&V_{+}\\
V_{-}&0\end{pmatrix}\;,\qquad T\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&T_{+}\\
T_{-}&0\end{pmatrix}\;,\qquad R\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&R_{+}\\
R_{-}&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

It is also natural and useful to introduce a notation for the off-diagonal entries of the symbol:

H(z)\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&H_{+}(z)\\
H_{-}(z)&0\end{pmatrix}\;,\qquad H_{\pm}(z)\;=\;R_{\pm}\,z^{-1}\,+\,V_{\pm}\,+\,T_{\pm}\,z\;,

(46)

and then consider
H_{\pm}
also as block operators of the form (
1
), but on the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{\frac{L}{2}})
. As above one also has
\widetilde{H}_{\pm}(z)
and
\widehat{H}_{\pm}(z)
. Next let us consider the winding numbers. As
\det\big(K(H(z)-E\,{\bf 1})K\big)=(-1)^{L}\det(H(z)+E\,{\bf 1})
and
L
is even, one has for
E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma

{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)\;=\;\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath}\;\partial_{z}\log\det\big(K(H(z)-E\,{\bf 1})K\big)\;=\;{\rm Wind}^{-E}(H)\;.

(47)

Of particular importance is the winding number at zero energy. Due to the off-diagonal form of
H(z)
, one has

{\rm Wind}^{0}(H)\;=\;{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{+})\,+\,{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{-})\;.

(48)

These latter two winding numbers imply the following extension of the bulk-boundary correspondence as described in Section
3.2
.

Proposition 15

Let
H
have the chiral symmetry (
45
). Then
{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{\pm})>0
implies that
\dim({\rm Ker}(H^{R}_{\pm}))\geq 1
and hence also
\dim({\rm Ker}(H^{R}))\geq 1
. Similarly,
{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{\pm})<0
implies
\dim({\rm Ker}(H^{L}_{\mp}))\geq 1
and
\dim({\rm Ker}(H^{L}))\geq 1
.

Proof.
As in (
33
), one has

h^{0}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&H_{+}\\
0&0&H_{-}&0\\
0&(H_{-})^{*}&0&0\\
(H_{+})^{*}&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

(49)

Now (
34
) holds and implies that
{\rm Wind}^{0}(H)=-{\rm Sig}\big(J|_{{\rm Ker}(h^{0,R})}\big)
. Next let us recall the identities
((H_{\pm})^{*})^{R}=\widehat{H}_{\pm}^{R}
and
((H_{\pm})^{*})^{L}=\widehat{H}_{\pm}^{L}
, see (
11
). Together with (
48
), one therefore has

\displaystyle{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{+})

\displaystyle\,+\,{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{-})

\displaystyle\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}_{-})\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}_{+})\big)\,-\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(\widehat{H}_{+}^{R})\big)\,-\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(\widehat{H}_{-}^{R})\big)\;.

Moreover, using
J^{\prime}={\rm diag}({\bf 1},-{\bf 1},{\bf 1},-{\bf 1})
in the grading of (
49
), the selfadjoint Hamiltonian
h^{0}
also has the further chiral symmetry
J^{\prime}h^{0}J^{\prime}=-h^{0}
. For this symmetry, (
34
) combined with
{\rm Wind}^{0}((H_{-})^{*})=-{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{-})
leads to

\displaystyle{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{+})

\displaystyle-{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{-})

\displaystyle\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(\widehat{H}_{-}^{R})\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}_{+})\big)\,-\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(\widehat{H}_{+}^{R})\big)\,-\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}_{-})\big)\;.

Adding and subtracting these identities then leads to the two identities

{\rm Wind}(H_{\pm},0)\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}_{\pm})\big)\,-\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(\widehat{H}_{\pm}^{R})\big)\;,

which imply the first claim. For the second one, the same reasoning is applied to
\widetilde{H}
for which the winding number is given by (
32
). Because of the relations
WH^{L}_{\pm}W^{*}=\widetilde{H}^{R}_{\pm}
and
WH^{R}_{\pm}W^{*}=\widetilde{H}^{L}_{\pm}
as in (
10
) hold, one obtains

\displaystyle{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{+})

\displaystyle\,+\,{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{-})

\displaystyle\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(\widehat{H}_{+}^{L})\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(\widehat{H}_{-}^{L})\big)\,-\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{L}_{+})\big)\,-\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{L}_{-})\big)

and

\displaystyle{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{+})

\displaystyle\,-\,{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{-})

\displaystyle\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(\widehat{H}_{+}^{L})\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{L}_{+})\big)\,-\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(\widehat{H}_{-}^{L})\big)\,-\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{L}_{-})\big)\;.

Hence

{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{\pm})\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(\widehat{H}_{\pm}^{L})\big)\,-\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{L}_{\mp})\big)\;,

implying the second claim.

\Box

On first sight, the statements of Proposition
15
and Proposition
9
resemble each other. There is, however, a crucial difference: Proposition
9
is stable under perturbations in
E
(because the winding number
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)
is so), while Proposition
15
merely concerns the eigenvalue
0
, the so-called zero modes or zero energy bound states. Let us discuss two distinct scenarios for the application of Proposition
15
(which in physical terminology explained in Section
5
are compatible with line-gapped and point-gapped systems respectively). If
{\rm Wind}^{0}(H)=0
, then the component of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
containing
0
only contains discrete spectrum (by analytic Fredholm theory) and the zero eigenvalues resulting from Proposition
15
(in the case
{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{+})\not=0
) are robust under perturbations conserving the chiral symmetry (and are merely moved away from the origin if the perturbations break the chiral symmetry). On the other hand, if
{\rm Wind}^{0}(H)\not=0
, then Proposition
9
implies that the component of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
containing
0
consists entirely of spectrum of
H^{R}
(or
H^{L}
) and then the supplementary eigenvalues resulting from Proposition
15
are covered by this spectrum and seem to be of little interest. It will, however, be shown in Section
5.4
that finite-volume approximations do have approximate zero modes resulting from
{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{+})\not=0
. This is based on Widom’s theory of asymptotic spectra described in Section
4
.

The remainder of this section will provide an alternative proof of Proposition
15
based on transfer matrix methods (similar to the arguments towards the end of Section
3.2
, leading to Proposition
9
). This is crucially based on the following property of the transfer matrix of a chiral Hamiltonian:

(K\otimes\sigma_{3})\,{\cal T}^{E}\,(K\otimes\sigma_{3})\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}K(E-V)T^{-1}K&KRK\\
-KT^{-1}K&0\end{pmatrix}\\
\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}-(E+V)T^{-1}&-R\\
T^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\\
=\;{\cal T}^{-E}\;.

This implies
{\det}_{2L}({\cal T}^{E}-z{\bf 1})={\det}_{2L}({\cal T}^{-E}-z{\bf 1})
so that
\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{E})=\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{-E})
. Furthermore,
(K\otimes\sigma_{3})\,{\cal T}^{0}\,(K\otimes\sigma_{3})={\cal T}^{0}
, notably the zero energy transfer matrix has invariant subspaces. Indeed, one readily checks

{\cal T}^{0}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}-VT^{-1}&-R\\
T^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}-V_{+}T_{+}^{-1}&0&0&-R_{+}\\
0&-V_{-}T_{-}^{-1}&-R_{-}&0\\
0&T_{-}^{-1}&0&0\\
T_{+}^{-1}&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\;=\;{\cal T}_{+}^{0}\,\hat{\oplus}\,{\cal T}^{0}_{-}\;,

where the direct sum is understood in the obvious way (regrouping the first and fourth columns and lines, as well as the second and third ones) and these
2\times 2
blocks are given by

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{+}^{0}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}-V_{+}T_{+}^{-1}&-R_{+}\\
T_{+}^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\;,\qquad{\cal T}_{-}^{0}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}-V_{-}T_{-}^{-1}&-R_{-}\\
T_{-}^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

Note that
{\cal T}_{+}^{0}
and
{\cal T}_{-}^{0}
are the transfer matrices at
E=0
for
H_{+}
and
H_{-}
respectively. In complete analogy to (
35
) for
E=0
, one now has for these two subsystems

\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}_{\pm})\big)\;=\;\dim\big({\cal E}^{0}_{1,\pm}\cap{\cal D}_{0}\big)\;,

(50)

where
{\cal E}^{0}_{1,\pm}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{L}
is the generalized eigenspace of all eigenvalues of
{\cal T}^{0}_{\pm}
with modulus less than
1
. Moreover, similarly as in (
36
), one has

\dim({\cal E}^{0}_{1,\pm})\;=\;\frac{L}{2}\,+\,{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{\pm})\;.

(51)

Hence if
{\rm Wind}^{0}(H_{\pm})>0
is positive, then
\dim({\cal E}^{0}_{1,\pm})>\frac{L}{2}
which enforces
\dim({\cal E}^{0}_{1,\pm}\cap{\cal D}_{0})\geq 1
so that, due to (
50
),
0
is an eigenvalue of
H^{R}_{\pm}
. This provides the promised alternative proof of Proposition
15
.

Remark 16

Let us briefly show how Proposition
15
relates to Proposition
11
. For
0\not\in\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
the latter result states that

\dim\big({\rm Ker}({Q}^{(1)}_{1}(0))\big)\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R})\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{L})\big)\;.

The matrix-valued function
E\mapsto{Q}^{(1)}_{r}(E)
satisfies

K\,Q_{r}^{(1)}(E)\,K\,=\,\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{r}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath\,z}\;K\,\big({H}(z)\,-\,E\,{\bf 1}\big)^{-1}K\,=\,\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{r}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath\,z}\;\big(-\,{H}(z)\,-\,E\,{\bf 1}\big)^{-1}\,=\,-\,Q_{r}^{(1)}(-E)\,,

implying, in particular, that
Q_{r}^{(1)}(0)
is chiral. It is hence off-diagonal in the grading of
K
with two off-diagonal entries
Q_{r,\pm}^{(1)}(0)
given by (
40
) from
H_{\pm}
:

\displaystyle{Q}^{(1)}_{r}(0)\;=\;\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{r}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath\,z}\;\begin{pmatrix}0&H_{+}(z)\\
H_{-}(z)&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&Q_{r,-}^{(1)}(0)\\
Q_{r,+}^{(1)}(0)&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

One hence has

\dim\big({\rm Ker}({Q}^{(1)}_{r}(0))\big)\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}({Q}^{(1)}_{r,+}(0))\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}({Q}^{(1)}_{r,-}(0))\big)\;.

Furthermore,
0\not\in\Sigma
is equivalent to
\det(H(e^{\imath k}))=\det(H_{+}(e^{\imath k}))\det(H_{-}(e^{\imath k}))\not=0
for all
e^{\imath k}\in{\mathbb{T}}
, which is hence also equivalent to
0\not\in\mbox{\rm spec}(H_{+})
and
0\not\in\mbox{\rm spec}(H_{-})
. Therefore one can apply Proposition
11
also separately to
H_{\pm}
, giving

\dim\big({\rm Ker}({Q}^{(1)}_{1,\pm}(0))\big)\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{R}_{\pm})\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{L}_{\pm})\big)\;.

Together these equations merely reflect that
{\rm Ker}(H^{R})={\rm Ker}(H^{R}_{+})\oplus{\rm Ker}(H^{R}_{-})
.

4
Widom’s theory of asymptotic spectra revisited

This section is the mathematical core of the paper, providing the proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
.

4.1
The set
\Lambda
of energies with middle eigenvalues of equal modulus

Let us first note that the set
\Sigma=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
can be written as

\displaystyle\Sigma\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,|z_{l}(E)|=1\mbox{ for some }l=1,\ldots,2L\big\}\;,

(52)

see also (
4
). In the hermitian case in which the transfer matrix is
{\cal I}
-unitary, the existence of one eigenvalue on the unit circle implies that there is also a second eigenvalue on the unit circle and in the corresponding two-dimensional subspace one can construct eigenfunctions of finite volume approximations
H_{N}
at nearby energies (by well-known techniques described in the proof of Proposition
38
, or by the procedure in Section
4.8
). Hence the points in
\Sigma
are indeed near the spectrum of
H_{N}
. However, for a non-hermitian
H
one has generically only one eigenvalue on the unit circle and the fundamental solutions constructed by the transfer matrices do not allow to construct eigenfunctions for
H_{N}
satisfying both boundary conditions. From this perspective, it is natural to introduce the set
\Lambda
of complex energies
E
where the middle two eigenvalues
|z_{L}(E)|\leq|z_{L+1}(E)|
of the transfer matrix
{\cal T}^{E}
(or equivalently zeros of
D(z,E)={\det}_{L}(H(z)-E\,{\bf 1})
) coincide:

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,|z_{L}(E)|=|z_{L+1}(E)|\big\}\;,

(53)

see (
5
). (On p. 312 of Widom’s work
[
42
]
, this set is denoted by
C_{2}
.) Due to (
23
), one can also write this set as

\Lambda\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,\mbox{middle two eigenvalues of }\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E}\mbox{ are of same modulus}\big\}\;.

This section is about proving topological and analytical properties of this set
\Lambda
. A first property is the following: if
E
is in
\Lambda
, then either
E\in\Sigma
or
E\notin\Sigma
. In the latter case,
\det(H(e^{\imath k})-E\,{\bf 1})\not=0
for all
e^{\imath k}\in{\mathbb{T}}
so that
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)
is well-defined and, due to (
25
), non-vanishing. Hence

\Lambda\;\subset\;\Sigma\,\cup\,\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)\neq 0\big\}\;.

(54)

Proposition 17

The set
\Lambda
satisfies
\Lambda\subset\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{R})
and
\Lambda\subset\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{L})
.

Proof.

Rewriting (
54
) gives together with Theorem
7
that

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle\;\subset\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\colon{\det}_{L}(H(z)-E\;{\bf 1})=0\text{ for some }z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\text{ or }{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)\neq 0\big\}

\displaystyle\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\colon H^{R}-E\;{\bf 1}\text{ is not Fredholm or is Fredholm with }{\rm Ind}(H^{R}-E\;{\bf 1})\neq 0\big\}

\displaystyle\;\subset\;\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{R})\;.

The same holds for
H^{L}
.

\Box

Remark 18

Let us consider the special case of hermitian
H
(namely
T=R^{*}
and
V=V^{*}
). Then the spectrum of the
{\cal I}
-unitary matrix
{\cal T}^{E}
has a reflection symmetry on
{\mathbb{T}}
and therefore the middle two eigenvalues are of the same modulus if and only if they lie on the unit circle, notably

\Lambda\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{E})\cap{\mathbb{T}}\not=\emptyset\big\}\;.

This is a well-known characterization of the spectrum of periodic hermitian operators, namely
\Sigma=\Lambda
. There are close connections to the standard Brillouin zone, see the discussion in Section
5.3
below. Furthermore, the inclusion
\Lambda\subset\lim_{N\to\infty}\mbox{\rm spec}(H_{N})
follows from a standard Weyl sequence argument. For systems without any bound states (also called outliers), one has
\Lambda=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mbox{\rm spec}(H_{N})
, namely the set
\Gamma
introduced in Section
4.3
is empty. On the other hand, for hermitian chiral SSH-type topological insulators (of the type described in Section
5.4
), it is known that there are spectral outliers at zero energy, similar to the eigenvalues already encountered in Example

Lemma 19

The set
\Lambda
is compact.

Proof.

As the
z_{j}(E)
are continuous in

, the set
\Lambda
is closed. Moreover,
\Lambda\subset\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{R})
by Proposition
17
. As
H^{R}
is bounded, this also shows that
\Lambda
is a bounded set.

Lemma 20

The set
\Lambda
does not contain isolated points.

Proof.

Suppose
E
is an isolated point of
\Lambda\setminus\mathcal{F}
so that
|z_{L}(E)|=|z_{L+1}(E)|
and, in particular,
z_{L}(E)\not=z_{L+1}(E)
. These two eigenvalues are isolated and hence holomorphic at
E
. Let us denote these two holomorphic eigenvalues by
\zeta_{L}(E)
and
\zeta_{L+1}(E)
. This eliminates discontinuities of the functions
z_{L}(E)
and
z_{L+1}(E)
resulting from labelling according to the modulus. Note that, if there are only two eigenvalues with modulus
|z_{L}(E)|
at
E
, then
\{z_{L}(E+\epsilon),z_{L+1}(E+\epsilon)\}=\{\zeta_{L}(E+\epsilon),\zeta_{L+1}(E+\epsilon)\}
locally, namely for
\epsilon
small enough. Note now, that the labeling of the eigenvalues is determined only up to permutation of the labels among eigenvalues of the same modulus. Since
E\in\Lambda
is isolated, (because
\mathcal{F}
is finite,) it follows that one always could have chosen the labeling such that
|\zeta_{L}(E+\epsilon)|\not=|\zeta_{L+1}(E+\epsilon)|
for all
\epsilon\not=0
sufficiently small. Now
\epsilon=0
is a local maximum or minimum of the function
\epsilon\mapsto|\zeta_{L}(E+\epsilon)/\zeta_{L+1}(E+\epsilon)|
, because
E
is isolated in
\Lambda
so that the quotient does not take the value
1
in a some pointed neighborhood
B_{\delta}(0)\setminus\{0\}
. But then the maximum modulus principle would imply that
|\zeta_{L}(E+\epsilon)|=|\zeta_{L+1}(E+\epsilon)|
for all
\epsilon
in an open neighborhood of
0
. Then also
|z_{L}(E+\epsilon)|=|z_{L+1}(E+\epsilon)|
for
\epsilon\in B_{\delta}(0)
, a contradiction to the isolation of
E
.

Suppose now
E\in\Lambda\cap\mathcal{F}
is an isolated point of
\Lambda
. Then not necessarily all of the
z_{l}
are holomorphic in a neighborhood of
E
. However, since they are the zeros of a polynomial whose coefficients are polynomials in
E
, they can be given by a Puiseux series in
E
with non-zero convergence radius. Hence there exist
p_{l,E}\in{\mathbb{N}}
(compare
[
19
]
) such that the functions

\epsilon\,\mapsto\,\tau_{l}^{E}(\epsilon)=\zeta_{l}(E+\epsilon^{p_{l,E}})

(55)

are holomorphic in a neighborhood of
\epsilon=0
, where the
\zeta_{l}
are again a choice of eigenvalue making the
\tau_{l}^{E}
holomorphic. Repeating the previous argument for the pair of functions
\epsilon\mapsto\tau_{L}^{E}(\epsilon^{p_{L+1,E}})
and
\epsilon\mapsto\tau_{L+1}^{E}(\epsilon^{p_{L,E}})
shows that also a point
E\in\Lambda\cap\mathcal{F}
cannot be isolated in
\Lambda
.

\Box

Lemma 21

The set
\partial\Lambda
locally consists of a finite union of analytic arcs.

Proof.
Let
E\in\partial\Lambda
. Then

|z_{L-a-1}(E)|\,<\,|z_{L-a}(E)|\,=\,\ldots\,=\,|z_{L+b}(E)|\,<\,|z_{L+b+1}(E)|

for some integers
a\geq 0
and
b\geq 1
. Using the function
\tau_{l}^{E}
defined in (
55
), let us introduce the holomorphic functions

\chi_{l,k}^{E}(\epsilon)\;=\;\frac{\tau_{l}^{E}(\epsilon^{p_{k,E}})}{\tau_{k}^{E}(\epsilon^{p_{l,E}})}\;=\;\frac{\zeta_{l}(E+\epsilon^{p_{l,E}p_{k,E}})}{\zeta_{k}(E+\epsilon^{p_{k,E}p_{l,E}})}\;.

(Instead of the power
p_{l,E}p_{k,E}
it is also possible to work with
q_{l,k}=\mbox{lcm}(p_{l,E},p_{k,E})
, namely the lowest common multiple of
p_{l,E}
and
p_{k,E}
.)
There is an open neighborhood
V\subset{\mathbb{C}}
of
0
such that for all
\epsilon\in V
one has

|\tau_{1}^{E}(\epsilon)|\,,\,\ldots\,,\,|\tau_{L-a-1}^{E}(\epsilon)|\,<\,|\tau_{L-a}^{E}(\epsilon)|\,,\,\ldots\,,\,|\tau_{L+b}^{E}(\epsilon)|\,<\,|\tau_{L+b+1}^{E}(\epsilon)|\,,\,\ldots\,,\,|\tau_{2L}^{E}(\epsilon)|\;.

Let now
{\cal P}\subset\{L-a,\dots,L+b\}^{2}
be the set of pairs
(l,k)
of indices
l<k
such that
\chi_{l,k}
is not locally constant around
0
and
\zeta_{l},\zeta_{k}
is the pair of middle eigenvalues somewhere on
E+V\setminus\{0\}
. Note that
{\cal P}
is non-empty, since
E\in\partial\Lambda
(because then in some direction of
\epsilon
the middle two eigenvalues have to have different modulus so that the corresponding
\chi
cannot be constant). For all
(l,k)\in{\cal P}
let us set

\gamma_{l,k}\;=\;\{\epsilon\in V\colon|\chi^{E}_{l,k}(\epsilon)|=1\}\;.

Since all
\chi^{E}_{l,k}
are holomorphic and non-constant around
0
, there is an
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\chi^{E}_{l,k}(\epsilon)\;=\;\chi^{E}_{l,k}(0)\big(1\,+\,c_{n}(E)\,\epsilon^{n}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{n+1})\big)\;,

(56)

with
c_{n}(E)\not=0
. Note that
E\mapsto c_{1}(E)
is analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus{\cal F}
and cannot vanish identically around points
E\in\partial\Lambda\setminus{\cal F}
, for the following reason. For all
\epsilon
and
\delta
small enough,

\chi_{l,k}^{E}(\epsilon+\delta)\;=\;\frac{z_{l}(E+\epsilon+\delta)}{z_{k}(E+\epsilon+\delta)}\;=\;\chi_{l,k}^{E+\delta}(\epsilon)\;.

Since both
\chi_{l,k}^{E}
and
\chi_{l,k}^{E+\delta}
are analytic around
0
, they have a convergent power series expression there. Comparing the terms of order
\epsilon
, one finds

c_{1}(E+\delta)\;=\;\sum_{m>0}mc_{m}(E)\delta^{m-1}\;.

This is the power series expansion of
c_{1}
around
E
. Now if
c_{1}
would vanish identically around
E
, it follows from the expansion that
c_{m}(E)=0
for all
m
. However, this means that
\chi_{l,k}^{E}
has to be constant around
0
, which it is not. Therefore
c_{1}
does not vanish identically around any
E\in\partial\Lambda\setminus{\cal F}
. Hence the map
E\mapsto c_{1}(E)
has a discrete set of zeros. (Recall that
{\cal F}
is discrete by Hypothesis B.) One simply has
n=1
except on a discrete set of points
E
, but these points with
n\geq 2
are singular points of interest. Namely, (
56
) implies that
\gamma_{l,k}
is the union of
n
analytic curves in
V
going through
0\in V
, possibly after choosing
V
sufficiently smaller. Consider now
W=\bigcap_{(l,k)\in{\cal P}}V^{p_{l,E}p_{k,E}}
where
V^{q}=\{\epsilon^{q}:\epsilon\in V\}
. Then
E+W
is a neighborhood of
E
.
Recalling that
\tau_{l}^{E}(\epsilon)=\zeta_{l}(E+\epsilon^{p_{l,E}})
with
p_{l,E}>0
, one finds that on the open neighborhood
E+W
, the points in
\partial\Lambda
are locally near
E
precisely given by
E+W\cap\bigcup_{(l,k)\in{\cal P}}(\gamma_{l,k})^{p_{l,E}p_{k,E}}
. This latter set consists of a finite union of analytic arcs.

\Box

Remark 22

Let us consider a point
E\in\Lambda\cap{\cal F}=\{E\in\Lambda\,:\,z_{L}(E)=z_{L+1}(E)\}
for which the Puiseux expansion of some of the middle eigenvalues is non-trivial, namely that
p>1
in (

). If there are only two middle eigenvalues and the coefficient in (

, then

consists of just this one pair and there is just one set

given by an analytic line. Taking the power the gives a set

3
and
4
at the extremities of

.

Corollary 23

If the interior of
\Lambda
is empty, the set
\Lambda
locally consists of a finite union of analytic arcs.

Proof.
As
\Lambda
is closed and the closure is always given by the disjoint union
\overline{\Lambda}=\partial\Lambda\,\mathring{\cup}\,\mathring{\Lambda}
of the boundary and the interior, it follows from the hypothesis that
\Lambda=\partial\Lambda
so that the claim holds by Lemma
21
.

\Box

Let us provide a simple criterion assuring that
\Lambda
has empty interior so that the above corollary applies.

Lemma 24

If
T
and
R
have no eigenvalues of same modulus, the set
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{C}}
has empty interior. If
T
and
R
have no double eigenvalues, then
Hypothesis B
holds.

Proof.

This will follow from an asymptotic analysis of the spectrum of the transfer matrices
{\cal T}^{E}
and
\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E}
at
|E|\to\infty
. For this purpose, let us set
\xi=\frac{1}{E}
and
{\cal S}^{\xi}=\frac{1}{E}{\cal T}^{E}
. The function
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}\mapsto{\cal S}^{\xi}
is analytic and has a removable singularity at
0
, namely

{\cal S}^{\xi}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}T^{-1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\;+\;{\cal O}(\xi)\;.

By analytic perturbation theory
[
19
]
,
{\cal S}^{\xi}
has
L
non-vanishing eigenvalues
\mu_{1}(\xi),\ldots,\mu_{L}(\xi)
of modulus of order
1
, and the others are of order
\xi
. More precisely, if
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{L}
are the eigenvalues of
T
, then for an appropriate choice of the labelling one has
|\mu_{l}(\xi)-\frac{1}{\lambda_{l}}|={\cal O}(\xi)
for
l=1,\ldots,L
. As
\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{E})=E\,\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal S}^{E})
, the spectrum of
{\cal T}^{E}
has
L
eigenvalues
E\mu_{1}(\frac{1}{E}),\ldots,E\mu_{L}(\frac{1}{E})
of order
E
and
L
eigenvalues of order
1
. As
E\mu_{l}(\frac{1}{E})=\frac{E}{\lambda_{l}}+{\cal O}(1)
for
l=1,\ldots,L
, the hypothesis that all
\lambda_{l}
have different moduli implies that these
L
eigenvalues all have different modulus for
E
sufficiently large. Moreover, one can run the same argument for
\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E}
based on the hypothesis on the spectrum of
R
. Due to (
23
) this shows that none of the eigenvalues of
{\cal T}^{E}
have equal modulus for
E
sufficiently large. Due to Lemma
6
this implies the first claim. The second follows in a similar manner.

4.2
Scaling operation

Let us introduce an invertible scaling matrix by

S_{N}\;=\;{\rm diag}(s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{N})\;,\qquad s\not=0\;.

Then

S_{N}^{-1}\,H_{N}\,S_{N}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}V&sT&&\\
s^{-1}R&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&sT\\
&&s^{-1}R&V\end{pmatrix}\;.

(57)

In particular, for all
s>0
,

\mbox{\rm spec}(H_{N})\;=\;\mbox{\rm spec}(S_{N}^{-1}\,H_{N}\,S_{N})\;.

This suggests to introduce the half-sided block-Toeplitz matrix
H^{s,R}
with entries as in (
57
). Let us stress that for
s\not=1
, the operators
H^{s,R}
and
H^{R}
are not conjugate to each other, and are therefore typically
not
isospectral. The symbol of
H^{s,R}
will be denoted by
H^{s}(z)
. Comparing with (
8
), it is hence obtained by scaling

H^{s}(z)\;=\;s^{-1}\,R\,z^{-1}\,+\,V\,+\,s\,T\,z\;=\;H(s\,z)\;.

Its associated symbol is according to (
9
) given by

\widetilde{H^{s}}(z)\;=\;H^{s}(\tfrac{1}{z})\;=\;H(\tfrac{s}{z})\;=\;\widetilde{H}(\tfrac{z}{s})\;=\;\widetilde{H}^{\frac{1}{s}}(z)\;.

Then the corresponding determinants defined by
D^{s}(z,E)={\det}_{L}(H^{s}(z)-E{\bf 1})
as in (
12
) satisfy

D^{s}(z,E)\;=\;D(sz,E)\;,\qquad\widetilde{D}^{s}(z,E)\;=\;\widetilde{D}(sz,E)\;.

Moreover, let
{\cal T}^{s,E}
and
\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{s,E}
denote the transfer matrices associated to the rescaled Hamiltonian symbols
H^{s}
and
\widetilde{H^{s}}
. One finds

{\cal T}^{s,E}\;=\;s^{-1}\,{\cal T}^{E}\;,\qquad\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{s,E}\;=\;s\,\widetilde{{\cal T}}^{E}\;.

(58)

Then there are also associated Riesz projections

{\cal R}^{s,E}_{r}\;=\;\oint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{r}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath}\;(z\,{\bf 1}-{\cal T}^{s,E})^{-1}\;.

They do not contain supplementary information though because the range and kernel of
{\cal T}^{s,E}
is independent of
s
due to (
58
). Actually, also the change of variable formula (
38
) shows

{\cal R}^{s,E}_{r}\;=\;{\cal R}^{E}_{sr}\;.

Therefore Proposition
11
immediately implies the following:

Corollary 25

Suppose that
\mbox{\rm spec}({\cal T}^{E})\cap{\mathbb{T}}_{s}=\emptyset
. Then

\dim\big({\rm Ker}({Q}^{(1)}_{s}(E))\big)\;=\;\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\,+\,\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{s,L}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\;.

Let us now start with the application of the scaling to the spectral analysis. The argument of Proposition
17
applies to each
H^{s,R}
and therefore

\Lambda\;\subset\;\bigcap_{s>0}\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{s,R})\;.

The inverse implication holds for
L=1
, see Corollary
28
below. In the following it will be shown that a modification is necessary in the matrix-valued case
L\geq 2
. Due to (
25
) applied to
H^{s}
the scaling (
58
) implies that, as long as
\mbox{\rm spec}(s\,{\cal T}^{E})\cap{\mathbb{T}}^{1}=\emptyset
or equivalently
H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}
is a Fredholm operator (see Theorem
7
),

\displaystyle{\rm Ind}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})

\displaystyle\;=\;{\rm Wind}^{E}(H^{s})\;=\;-\,L\;+\;\sum_{|z|<1}\,m_{z}({{\cal T}}^{s,E})\;=\;-\,L\;+\;\sum_{|z|<s}\,m_{z}({{\cal T}}^{E})\;.

This implies that, one has
\mbox{\rm spec}(s\,\mathcal{T}^{E})\cap\mathbb{T}^{1}\neq\emptyset
or
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H^{s})\neq 0
for all
s>0
if and only if the two middle eigenvalues of
{{\cal T}}^{E}
are of same modulus. One therefore has the characterization

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,\forall\,\,s>0\mbox{ either }H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}\;\mbox{ {\sl not} Fredholm or }{\rm Wind}^{E}(H^{s})\not=0\big\}

(59)

\displaystyle\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,\forall\,\,s>0\mbox{ either }H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}\;\mbox{ {\sl not} Fredholm or }{\rm Ind}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})\not=0\big\}\;.

(60)

One can carry out the same argument with
H^{L}
(or simply use (
28
) and (
29
)) to deduce

\Lambda\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,\forall\,\,s>0\mbox{ either }H^{s,L}-E\,{\bf 1}\;\mbox{ {\sl not} Fredholm or }{\rm Ind}(H^{s,L}-E\,{\bf 1})\not=0\big\}\;.

(61)

Hence the set
\Lambda
is equivalently characterized by either
H^{R}
or
H^{L}
and their scaling.

Now let
E\in\bigcap_{s>0}\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{s,R})
. Then for all
s>0
, one of the following three mutually exclusive cases holds true:

(i)

H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}
is not Fredholm;

(ii)

H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}
is Fredholm with non-vanishing index;

(iii)

H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}
is Fredholm with vanishing index, but
{\rm Ker}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})\not=\{0\}
is non-trivial.

In the last point (iii), one can also replace
{\rm Ker}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})\not=\{0\}
by the statement that
H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}
is not invertible (or, as matter of fact, that
{\rm Ker}((H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})^{*})\not=\{0\}
). Comparing with (
60
), one sees that
\Lambda
contains all
E\in\bigcap_{s>0}\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{s,R})
satisfying either (i) or (ii). It is hence natural to introduce the set of
E
for which (iii) holds, which is done in the next section.

4.3
The set
\Gamma
of spectral outliers

As motivated in the end of the last section, let us introduce the set

\Gamma^{R}\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Lambda\;:\;\forall\;s>0\mbox{ with }{\rm Ind}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})=0\text{ one has }{\rm Ker}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})\not=\{0\}\big\}\;,

where the condition
{\rm Ind}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})=0
includes the Fredholm property of
H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}
. Alternatively

\Gamma^{R}\;=\;\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Lambda\;:\;\forall\;s>0\mbox{ with }{\rm Wind}^{E}(H^{s})=0\text{ one has }{\rm Ker}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})\not=\{0\}\big\}\;,

where again the condition
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H^{s})=0
contains that the winding number is well-defined. By construction, the discussion at the end of Section
4.2
thus implies

\bigcap_{s>0}\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{s,R})\;=\;\Lambda\cup\Gamma^{R}\;.

(62)

Moreover, if the defining property for
E
being in
\Gamma^{R}
holds for some
s
, it actually holds for all
s
in some interval, as shows the proof of the following lemma:

Lemma 26

\Gamma^{R}\,=\,\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Lambda\,:\,\exists\;s>0\;\mbox{\rm with }{\rm Ind}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})=0\;\text{\rm and }H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}\;\text{\rm not invertible}\big\}\;.

Proof.

By definition,

\Gamma^{R}\,\subset\,\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Lambda\,:\,\exists\;s>0\mbox{ with }{\rm Ind}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})=0\text{ and }H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}\text{ not invertible}\big\}\,.

Let now
E
be an element of the r.h.s. and let
s>0
be such that
H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}
is Fredholm with index zero, but also not invertible. This means that
H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}
has a non-trivial kernel. This can be reformulated in terms of the
L
-dimensional subspace
{\cal E}^{s,E}_{1}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2L}
containing all generalized eigenvectors of
{\cal T}^{s,E}
with eigenvalues of modulus strictly less than
1
. In fact, just as in (
35
),

\dim\big({\rm Ker}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})\big)\;=\;\dim\big({\cal E}^{s,E}_{1}\cap{\cal D}_{0}\big)\;.

Now
{\cal T}^{s,E}=s^{-1}\,{\cal T}^{E}
and thus
{\cal E}^{s,E}_{1}
is independent of
s
as long as no eigenvalue of
{\cal T}^{s,E}
touches the unit circle (in which case the Fredholm condition is violated). Hence
{\rm Ker}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})
is also independent of
s
as long as
s^{-1}|z_{L}(E)|<1<s^{-1}|z_{L+1}(E)|
,
i.e.
for
s\in(|z_{L}(E)|,|z_{L+1}(E)|)
.

\Box

In connection with Lemma
26
, let us note that
E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Lambda
, so
E\not\in\Lambda
, always implies that there exists
s
with
{\rm Ind}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1})={\rm Wind}^{E}(H^{s})=0
. Hence
\Gamma^{R}
is characterized by the singularity of
H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}
for such
s
. Furthermore, as
{\cal E}^{s,E}_{1}={\cal E}^{E}_{s}
for
s\in(|z_{L}(E)|,|z_{L+1}(E)|)
, the proof of Lemma
26
also allows to rewrite the definition of the set
\Gamma^{R}
merely in terms of spectral properties of the transfer matrix
{\cal T}^{E}
:

\Gamma^{R}\,=\,\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,|z_{L}(E)|<|z_{L+1}(E)|\;\text{\rm and }{\cal E}^{E}_{s}\cap{\cal D}_{0}\not=\emptyset\;\text{\rm for }s\in(|z_{L}(E)|,|z_{L+1}(E)|)\big\}\;.

From this representation one immediately deduces the following:

Proposition 27

For
L=1
, one has
\Gamma^{R}=\emptyset
.

Proof.
For
E\in\Gamma^{R}
the
2\times 2
transfer matrix
{\cal T}^{E}
given in (
2
) has two distinct eigenvalues. For
s
satisfying
|z_{1}(E)|<s<|z_{2}(E)|
, the subspace
{\cal E}^{E}_{s}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
is
1
-dimensional and spanned by an eigenvector of
{\cal T}^{E}
. For
E\in\Gamma^{R}
, this eigenvector has to be constant multiple of
\binom{1}{0}
. However,
{\cal T}^{E}
has no eigenvector of this form as can be readily seen by looking at the lower component of the eigenvalue equation for
{\cal T}^{E}
.

\Box

Proposition
27
together with (
62
) directly implies the next result which goes back to the work of Schmidt and Spitzer
[
33
]
(more precisely, it is the result
A=C
therein).

Corollary 28

For
L=1
, one has

\Lambda\;=\;\bigcap_{s>0}\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{s,R})\;.

(63)

Another well-known result in the case

states that
\Lambda
is connected
[
40
]
.

Proposition 29

The set
\Gamma^{R}
is discrete in
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.
Let us rewrite the definition of
\Gamma^{R}
as

\Gamma^{R}\,=\,\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Lambda\,:\,\exists\;s>0\;\mbox{\rm with }{\rm Wind}^{E}(H^{s})=0\;\text{\rm and }\dim({\rm Ker}(H^{s,R}-E\,{\bf 1}))\geq 1\big\}\;.

The condition
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H^{s})=0
is open in
E
(and actually also in

). On the other hand,

\Gamma^{R}\,\subset\,\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Lambda\,:\,\exists\;s>0\;\mbox{\rm with }{\rm Wind}^{E}(H^{s})=0\;\text{\rm and }\det({Q}^{(1)}_{s}(E))=0\big\}\;.

is analytic (except at points where the Fredholm condition is not satisfied) and not identically

. Hence its zeros form a discrete set and this implies that also
\Gamma^{R}
is discrete.

\Box

Up to now, only the right-sided Toeplitz operators

were considered. As
\Lambda
can also be expressed in terms of the

by (

(64)

Then all of the above properties directly transpose. In particular,

\bigcap_{s>0}\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{s,L})\;=\;\Lambda\cup\Gamma^{L}\;,

and the equivalents of Lemma

and Propositions

) that

, but this is non-generic (it is generic within the set of chiral Hamiltonians though, see Section

). Finally, let us set

\Gamma\;=\;\Gamma^{L}\cup\Gamma^{R}\;.

Then the proof of Proposition
29
combined with Corollary
25

4.5
Widom’s determinant formula

Theorem 31

Let
E\not\in{\cal F}
. Then the characteristic polynomial is given by Widom’s formula

{\det}_{NL}(H_{N}-E\,{\bf 1})\;=\;\sum_{I}G_{I}(E)^{N+1}\,q_{I}(E)

(67)

where the sum runs over all index sets
I\subset\{1,\ldots,2L\}
of cardinality
L
and the two functions are defined by

G_{I}(E)\;=\;(-1)^{L}\det(R)\prod_{i\in I}\frac{1}{z_{i}(E)}

(68)

and

q_{I}(E)\;=\;{\det}_{L}\Big(\oint_{\gamma_{I}^{0}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath\,z}\;(H(z)-E\,{\bf 1})^{-1}\Big)\;=\;{\det}_{L}\Big(\binom{0}{{\bf 1}}^{*}{{\cal R}}_{I}^{E}\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}\Big)\;,

(69)

where
\gamma_{I}^{0}
is a path in
{\mathbb{C}}
which has a winding number
1
around
0
as well as each
z_{i}(E)
for
i\in I
and a winding number
0
around all other eigenvalues of
{\cal T}^{E}
.

In contradistinction to Widom’s work, in the present context it is possible to replace
\gamma_{I}^{0}
in the definition of
q_{I}(E)
by the path
\gamma_{I}
which has a winding number
1
around each
z_{i}(E)
for
i\in I
and a winding number
0
around all other eigenvalues of
{\cal T}^{E}
as well as
0
. This holds because
z(H(z)-E\,{\bf 1})
is a matrix-valued polynomial in
z
and its eigenvalues are the same as the eigenvalues of
{\cal T}^{E}
by (
20
), so that
0
is not an eigenvalue due to the invertibility of
{\cal T}^{E}
. Let us also stress that, if
E
is such that there are eigenvalues of same modulus, the ordering of the eigenvalues is ambiguous and hence the definition of
G_{I}(E)
and
q_{I}(E)
as well. However, Widom’s formula does not inherit this ambiguity because it contains a sum over all index sets
I
of cardinality
L
. Another way to circumvent the ambiguity is to choose one labelling at a point
E
and then to analytically extend it to a neighborhood of
E
(which is possible because
E\not\in{\cal F}
).

Let us stress that Theorem
31
is just a special case of Widom’s Theorem 6.2 of
[
42
]
which also covers the case of non-invertible off-diagonal coefficients. The proof of Theorem
31
given in
[
42
]
is based on another formula from
[
3
]
and is rather involved. Based on transfer matrix methods, it is here possible to provide a relatively short proof of Widom’s formula. It will be shown below that this strategy also leads to a few new identities within the present restricted context.

Let us start out by noting that

{\det}_{NL}(H_{N}-E\,{\bf 1})\;=\;(-1)^{NL}\,{\det}_{L}(T)^{N}\,{\det}_{L}\Big(\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}^{*}({\cal T}^{E})^{N}\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}\Big)\;.

(70)

Indeed, one can start constructing all solutions at
E
satisfying the Dirichlet boundary conditions at
1
from (
16
) and then check whether any of these solution satisfying the Dirichlet boundary condition at
N
. Hence the multiplicity of the zero of the determinant on the r.h.s. is equal to the multiplicity of
E
as an eigenvalue of
H_{N}
. Thus both sides of (
70
) vanish exactly at the eigenvalues of
H_{N}
. Moreover, as both sides are polynomials of degree
NL
in
E
, comparing the leading coefficient
E^{NL}
immediately implies the identity. For the computation the last determinant, one can use the following lemma with
M=2L
. It is Lemma 6.3 from
[
42
]
, but again an alternative proof is provided.

Lemma 32

Let
A\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{M}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{L}
,
B\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{M}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
and
C\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{L}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
be linear maps with
B
diagonalizable. Then there is a spectral decomposition
B=\sum_{i=1}^{M}b_{i}P_{i}
where the
P_{i}
are rank one idempotents onto the eigenvector associated to eigenvalue
b_{i}
and

{\det}_{L}(ABC)\;=\;\sum_{|I|=L}\Big(\prod_{i\in I}b_{i}\Big)\,{\det}_{L}\Big(\sum_{i\in I}AP_{i}C\Big)

where the sum runs over subsets
I
of
\{1,2,\dots,M\}
of cardinality
|I|=L
.

Proof.

The existence of the spectral decomposition follows immediately from the diagonalizability of
B
.
Moreover, the exterior power
\bigwedge^{L}B\colon\bigwedge^{L}{\mathbb{C}}^{M}\to\bigwedge^{L}{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
is also diagonalizable, since
B
is diagonalizable, so for the same reason we have a decomposition
\bigwedge^{L}B=\sum_{I}b_{I}P_{I}
, where the
P_{I}
are rank one projections onto the eigenvector associated to eigenvalue
b_{I}
of
\bigwedge^{L}B
. Note that given a basis of eigenvectors
(v_{i})_{i=1}^{M}
of
B
for
{\mathbb{C}}^{M}
, the eigenvalues
b_{I}
can be indexed by choices
I
of subsets of
\{1,2,\dots,M\}
of cardinality
L
and can then exactly be given by
b_{I}=\prod_{i\in I}b_{i}
. Moreover, an eigenvector
v_{I}
associated to
b_{I}
can be given by
v_{i_{1}}\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{i_{L}}
with
i_{j}\in I
and
i_{j}<i_{k}
if
j<k
. Hence

P_{I}\;=\;\bigwedge^{L}\sum_{i\in I}P_{i}

which can be checked explicitly by evaluation on a basis of eigenvectors of
\bigwedge^{L}B
. Let
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{L}
be a basis of
{\mathbb{C}}^{L}
. One finds

\displaystyle{\det}_{L}(ABC)

\displaystyle\,e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{L}\;=\;(\bigwedge^{L}A\bigwedge^{L}B\bigwedge^{L}C)\,e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{L}

\displaystyle\;=\;\Big(\bigwedge^{L}A\Big(\sum_{I}b_{I}P_{I}\Big)\bigwedge^{L}C\Big)\,e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{L}

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{I}b_{I}\big(\bigwedge^{L}AP_{I}\bigwedge^{L}C\big)\,e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{L}

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{I}\Big(\prod_{i\in I}b_{i}\Big)\big(\bigwedge^{L}A\bigwedge^{L}\sum_{i\in I}P_{i}\bigwedge^{L}C\big)\,e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{L}

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{|I|=L}\Big(\prod_{i\in I}b_{i}\Big)\,{\det}_{L}\Big(\sum_{i\in I}AP_{i}C\Big)\,e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{L}\;,

which implies the claim.

\Box

Proof
of Theorem
31
. First of all, the particular form (
2
) of the transfer matrix allows to rewrite (

) as

{\det}_{NL}(H_{N}-E\,{\bf 1})\;=\;(-1)^{NL}\,{\det}_{L}(T)^{N+1}\,{\det}_{L}\Big(\binom{0}{{\bf 1}}^{*}({\cal T}^{E})^{N+1}\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}\Big)\;.

Next recall that the eigenvalues of the transfer matrix

are

which by the assumption

are pairwise distinct. Then
{\cal T}^{E}
is diagonalizable and an application of Lemma

gives

{\det}_{NL}(H_{N}-E\,{\bf 1})\;=\;(-1)^{NL}\,{\det}_{L}(T)^{N+1}\,\sum_{I}\Big(\prod_{i\in I}{z}_{i}(E)^{N+1}\Big){\det}_{L}\Big(\binom{0}{{\bf 1}}^{*}{{\cal R}}_{I}^{E}\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}\Big)\;,

(71)

where the sum is as in the statement of the theorem and
{{\cal R}}_{I}^{E}
is the Riesz projection associated to the eigenvalues

with

{{\cal R}}_{I}^{E}\;=\;\oint_{{\gamma}^{0}_{I}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath}\;(z\,{\bf 1}-{\cal T}^{E})^{-1}\;,

where

. Hence replacing the definition of

Due to

this implies

{\det}_{NL}(H_{N}-E\,{\bf 1})\;=\;\sum_{I}G_{I}(E)^{N+1}\,{\det}_{L}\Big(\binom{0}{{\bf 1}}^{*}{{\cal R}}_{I}^{E}\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}\Big)\;,

because the indices in

and

run through the same set. Finally replacing (

\binom{0}{{\bf 1}}^{*}{{\cal R}}_{I}^{E}\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}\;=\;\oint_{{\gamma}^{0}_{I}}\frac{dz}{2\pi\imath\,z}\;({H}(z)-E\,{\bf 1})^{-1}\;.

\Box

The above strategy of proof can be slightly modified to obtain several similar identities in the present context. Let us spell out two such modifications. Starting directly from (

\displaystyle{\det}_{NL}(H_{N}-E\,{\bf 1})

\displaystyle\;=\;(-1)^{NL}\,{\det}_{L}(T)^{N}\,\sum_{I}\Big(\prod_{i\in I}{z}_{i}(E)^{N}\Big)\,{\det}_{L}\Big(\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}^{*}{{\cal R}}_{I}^{E}\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}\Big)

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{I}G_{I^{c}}(E)^{N}\,{\det}_{L}\Big(\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}^{*}{{\cal R}}_{I}^{E}\binom{{\bf 1}}{0}\Big)

By a similar calculation, one also finds

\displaystyle{\det}_{NL}(H_{N}-E\,{\bf 1})

\displaystyle\;=\;(-1)^{NL}\,\frac{{\det}_{L}(T)^{N+1}}{{\det}_{L}(-R)}\,{\det}_{L}\Big(\binom{0}{{\bf 1}}^{*}({\cal T}^{E})^{N+2}\binom{0}{{\bf 1}}\Big)

because the middle two eigenvalues have distinct moduli for

\displaystyle\Gamma

\sum_{n=1,\ldots,N}\|\phi^{E}_{n}\|^{2}\;\;\leq\;c\,N

After normalization of

implies the second bound.

\Box

If now

5
Applications to non-hermitian quantum systems

This section applies the above results to several concrete models that have been studied in the physics literature. These models all describe quasi-one-dimensional non-hermitian quantum systems with periodic coefficients. In applications, the non-hermitian character typically results from either dissipative terms or external driving forces. The spectra of the infinite-volume operators and finite-volume approximations are computed numerically and are, as expected, often very different. This is illustrated in Section
5.1
in the example of the Hatano-Nelson model
[
18
]
and one of its variants having next-nearest neighbor hopping. Furthermore the eigenfunctions of the finite-volume operators are typically localized at one or the other end of the system, an effect called the skin effect (see Section
5.2
). Next, Section
5.3
briefly defines and discusses the generalized Brillouin zone. In the following Section
5.4
, particular focus is on finite systems with topological invariants protected by a (chiral) symmetry. These invariants imply the existence of (approximate zero energy) bound states and this is again illustrated in a concrete model.

5.1

The Hatano-Nelson model
[
18

is the most simple strictly one-dimensional and translation invariant non-hermitian Hamiltonian, it is namely tridiagonal with constant scalar entries on the diagonal. This corresponds to the case
L=1
with matrix entries

and
V\in{\mathbb{C}}
. As the symbol is scalar, one can readily write out the spectrum
\Sigma^{s}=\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{s})
of the periodic scaled Hamiltonian
H^{s}
:

\Sigma^{s}\;=\;\big\{s^{-1}R\,e^{-\imath k}+V+s\,T\,e^{\imath k}\;:\;k\in[-\pi,\pi)\big\}\;.

(84)

This set is an ellipse in the complex plane which, for the critical scaling parameter
s_{c}
given by
s_{c}^{2}=|RT^{-1}|
, degenerates to a line segment. As the Schmidt-Spitzer result (
63
) applies in the scalar case considered here, one deduces

\Lambda\;=\;\Sigma^{s_{c}}\;,

namely
\Lambda
is a line segment. Of course, this can also be obtained directly from the definition (
53
) of the set
\Lambda
as the set of energies where the middle two eigenvalues of the transfer matrix have the same modulus. Indeed, it follows from (

that each point

is reached for two different values of

. Yet another way to compute

{\cal T}^{E}
:

z_{1/2}(E)\;=\;\frac{(E-V)T^{-1}}{2}\,\pm\,\sqrt{\frac{(E-V)^{2}T^{-2}}{4}\,-\,\frac{R}{T}}\;.

Then
\Lambda=\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,|z_{1}(E)|=|z_{2}(E)|\}

\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}
one has the equivalence
\Re e(\xi)=0\,\Longleftrightarrow\,\xi^{2}\leq 0
where the r.h.s. means that
\xi^{2}
is real and non-positive. Elementary algebra then leads to

\Lambda\;=\;\left\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,\frac{(E-V)^{2}}{R\,T}\leq 4\right\}\;.

This is yet another representation of
\Lambda
as a line segment. Note that this formula is intrinsically independent of scaling
(R,T)\mapsto(s^{-1}R,sT)
and that for the well-known case of the discrete Laplacian where

t_{-2},\ldots,t_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
t_{\pm 2}\not=0
and can be cast in the form (

. The Schmidt-Spitzer result (

\Lambda
as explicitly. One rather has to recourse to the definition (

E

{\cal T}^{E}
and simply checks whether the middle two eigenvalues are of approximately the same modulus. A first example is provided in Figure

5.2
The skin effect

For finite-volume non-hermitian Hamiltonians
H_{N}
, the eigenstates are generically localized either at the left or right boundary of the system, an effect that is called the
skin effect
in the physics literature (see
[
48
]
for a physics review). The aim of this section is to provide a detailed understanding of the skin effect from a mathematical perspective. For the case of compressions of scalar banded Toeplitz matrices this was already analyzed in Chapter 12 of
[
7
]
, based on
[
8
]
.

Let us start by recalling that Theorem
34
shows that eigenvalues of
H_{N}
accumulate on
\Lambda
. Furthermore, by (
54
), one has that
\Lambda
is a subset of the union
\Sigma\cup\big\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)\neq 0\big\}
. Hence considering the decomposition of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
into connected components,
\Lambda
will only lie in those components having non-vanishing winding number. This non-vanishing winding number in turn implies that generalized eigenspace
{\cal E}^{E}_{1}
of all eigenvalues of the transfer matrix
{\cal T}^{E}
with modulus less than
1
has a dimension given by
\dim({\cal E}^{E}_{1})=L+{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)
, see (
36
). In particular,
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)\neq 0
implies that on
\Lambda
one either has at least
L+1
contracting directions of
{\cal T}^{E}
(for
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)>0
) or
L+1
expanding directions (for
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)<0
), so that the middle two eigenvalues
z_{L}(E),z_{L+1}(E)
of same modulus are hence of modulus smaller or larger than
1
respectively. It is shown in Section
4.8
that the eigenfunctions of
H_{N}
are constructed for energies close to
\Lambda
by using small perturbations of the two-dimensional subspace spanned by the eigenvectors of
z_{L}(E),z_{L+1}(E)
(more precisely, the perturbations in energy and the subspace can indeed be done in such a way that the Dirichlet boundary conditions can be satisfied at both sides of the sample, see Section
4.8
). On the other hand, for eigenstates of
H_{N}
at energies
E
lying in a region of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
with vanishing winding number (such energies can be either spectral outliers or points converging to
\Lambda
in the limit
N\to\infty
, but nevertheless still in a region with vanishing winding number for finite
N
), there are
L
eigenvalues of
{\cal T}^{E}
less than
1
and
L
others larger than
1
. Typically, a vector composed of two neighboring entries of an eigenstate is then neither in the increasing nor the decreasing subspace of
{\cal T}^{E}
, but rather a linear combination of two vectors from these spaces and hence a linear combination of a left skin and a right skin state. Two examples are given in the middle and right plots of Figure
6
. Finally for an eigenvalue
E
of
H_{N}
that accidentally falls on
\Sigma
, the corresponding eigenstate must have a contribution in the two middle eigenvalues of modulus
1
, namely essentially be of plane-wave nature. Let us summarize:

•

For
E\in\mbox{\rm spec}(H_{N})
lying in a component of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
with positive/negative winding number
{\rm Wind}^{E}(H)
, the eigenstate is localized at the left/right boundary (skin state).

•

For
E\in\mbox{\rm spec}(H_{N})
lying in a region with vanishing winding number, the eigenstate need not be localized at one side of the sample (but it need not be a plane wave state either).

•

For
E\in\mbox{\rm spec}(H_{N})\cap\Sigma
, the eigenstate is like a plane wave.

Let us first illustrate these facts by looking at the middle plot in Figure
3
, hence corresponding to a model with five scalar diagonals. Clearly, there are three components
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
with non-vanishing winding number and whether it is positive or negative cannot be deduced from the plot which does not show the orientation of
k\mapsto\det(H(e^{\imath k})-E\,{\bf 1})
. The sign of the winding number in each component determines whether the skin states are left or right bound. Moreover, there are two crosses of
\Sigma
which also cross
\Lambda
, and the corresponding eigenstates of
H_{N}
near these crossings are plane wave like. As one changes the scaling, namely passes from
H
to
H^{s}
, the curve
\Lambda
remains unchanged (and also the spectrum of
H^{s}_{N}
), but the curve
\Sigma^{s}=\mbox{\rm spec}(H^{s})
changes. In particular, the crosses of
\Sigma^{s}
move along
\Lambda
as
s
changes. Hence also the left/right nature of the skin states changes as the cross moves along
\Lambda
. Generically, eigenstates that are near
\Sigma
have a less pronounced skin effect than those lying deep inside the components of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Sigma
with non-vanishing winding number. This is not necessarily true though. For example, for the scalar Hatano-Nelson model (tridiagonal with scalar entries, see first plot in Figure
3
), the arguments in Section
5.1
show that the middle two eigenvalues have a constant modulus on
\Lambda
so that the skin effect appears with a uniform rate throughout
\Lambda
. Furthermore, for
H^{s_{c}}
with critical scaling
s_{c}
, all states are plane-wave states of constant modulus. Further comments on the skin effect in Figures
4
to
6
are given in Section
5.4
.

5.3
The generalized Brillouin zone

The generalized Brillouin zone (appearing in so-called non-Bloch band theory) is a popular object in the physics literature,
e.g.

[
45
,
23
,
46
,
44
]
. Even though this work shows that it is not of relevance for the spectral analysis, let us define it as a mathematical object. Consider the map

\Upsilon=(\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1})\;:\;E\in\Lambda\;\mapsto\;(z_{L}(E),z_{L+1}(E))\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\;,

(85)

where the roots are chosen such that both
\Upsilon_{0}
and
\Upsilon_{1}
are continuous. Then the non-hermitian Brillouin zone is defined as

{\mathbb{B}}\;=\;\Upsilon_{0}(\Lambda)\cup\Upsilon_{1}(\Lambda)\;.

Numerically, this can readily be plotted from the data of
\Lambda
. An example is given in Figure
4
.
In the hermitian case,
\Lambda=\mbox{\rm spec}(H)
and then
(z_{L}(E),z_{L+1}(E))\in{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{T}}
for
E\in\Lambda
. Hence the Brillouin zone is given by
{\mathbb{B}}={\mathbb{T}}
, as usual. On the other hand, let us stress a difference with the hermitian case for which the transfer matrix is
{\cal I}
-unitary. This latter property implies that the eigenvalues of
{\cal T}^{E}
lying on the unit circle have stability properties given by Krein theory (see
[
36
]
for a review in the context of transfer matrices). In particular, there can be open subsets of energies for which there are more than
2
eigenvalues on
{\mathbb{T}}
(provided that
L>1
, of course). This is non-generic in the class of non-hermitian systems where there are typically only two eigenvalues of same modulus.

5.4
Topological zero modes of finite-volume chiral Hamiltonians

This section considers a tridiagonal block Hamiltonian of the form (
1
) with
L=2
and with symbol given by

H(z)\;=\;z^{-1}\,\begin{pmatrix}0&r_{+}\\
r_{-}&0\end{pmatrix}\,+\,\begin{pmatrix}0&v_{+}\\
v_{-}&0\end{pmatrix}\,+\,z\,\begin{pmatrix}0&t_{+}\\
t_{-}&0\end{pmatrix}\;,

(86)

where
r_{\pm},v_{\pm},t_{\pm}\in{\mathbb{C}}
are parameters with
r_{\pm}\not=0
and
t_{\pm}\not=0
. The latter two conditions ensure that
R
and
T
are invertible so that Hypothesis A is satisfied. The model is called the non-hermitian SSH model and is widely studied in the physics literature,
e.g.

[
24
,
23
,
11
]
. Clearly
H(z)
is off-diagonal and hence satisfies the chiral symmetry relation (
45
). Therefore all the claims of Sections
3.4
and
4.4
hold for the Hamiltonian (
86
). In particular, the off-diagonal entries define two scalar symbols
H_{\pm}(z)=r_{\pm}z^{-1}+v_{\pm}+t_{\pm}z
and one can, for any scaling parameter
s>0
, define two winding numbers
W_{\pm}(s)={\rm Wind}^{0}(H^{s}_{\pm})
which appear in the criteria in Propositions
15
and
30
(as well as Theorem
2
). Furthermore, the sets
\Lambda
and
\Gamma
satisfy (
66
), namely they are reflection symmetric.
