Exact values and improved bounds on k k -neighborly families of boxes
Xinbu Cheng, Meiqin Wang, Zixiang Xu, Chi Hoi Yip

Abstract
A finite family ℱ \mathcal{F} of d d -dimensional convex polytopes is called k k -neighborly if d − k ≤ dim ​ ( C ∩ C ′ ) ≤ d − 1 d-k\leq\textup{dim}(C\cap C^{\prime})\leq d-1 for any two distinct members C , C ′ ∈ ℱ C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F} . In 1997, Alon initiated the study of the general function n ⁡ ( k , d ) n(k,d) , which is defined to be the maximum size of k k -neighborly families of standard boxes in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . Based on a weighted count of vectors in { 0 , 1 } d \{0,1\}^{d} , we improve a recent upper bound on n ⁡ ( k , d ) n(k,d) by Alon, Grytczuk, Kisielewicz, and Przesławski for any positive integers d d and k k with d ≥ k + 2 d\geq k+2 . In particular, when d d is sufficiently large and k ≥ 0.123 ​ d k\geq 0.123d , our upper bound on n ⁡ ( k , d ) n(k,d) improves the bound ∑ i = 1 k 2 i − 1 ​ ( d i ) + 1 \sum_{i=1}^{k}2^{i-1}\binom{d}{i}+1 shown by Huang and Sudakov exponentially. Furthermore, we determine that n ⁡ ( 2 , 4 ) = 9 n(2,4)=9 , n ⁡ ( 3 , 5 ) = 18 n(3,5)=18 , n ⁡ ( 3 , 6 ) = 27 n(3,6)=27 , n ⁡ ( 4 , 6 ) = 37 n(4,6)=37 , n ⁡ ( 5 , 7 ) = 74 n(5,7)=74 , and n ⁡ ( 6 , 8 ) = 150 n(6,8)=150 . The stability result of Kleitman’s isodiametric inequality plays an important role in the proofs.

Introduction

A standard box in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a set of the form
K=K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{d}
, where
K_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a closed interval for each
i\in[d]
. A finite family
\mathcal{C}
of
d
-dimensional standard boxes is called
k
-neighborly if the dimension of the intersection of any two boxes is in
\{d-k,d-k+1,\ldots,d-1\}
. For positive integers
1\leq k\leq d
, the function
n(k,d)
is defined to be the maximum number of elements in a
k
-neighborly family of
d
-dimensional standard boxes. The
1
-neighborly families of standard boxes were widely studied several decades ago. For example, Zaks
[
13
]
showed the maximum size of a
1
-neighborly family is closely related to the famous Graham-Pollak theorem
[
5
]
, which gives that
n(1,d)=d+1
. There have been several other beautiful proofs of Graham-Pollak theorem
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. In 1997, Alon
[
1
]
generalized the problem to
k
-neighborly families and proved the equivalence between the maximum size of
k
-neighborly families of standard boxes and a variant of bipartite coverings. More precisely, he showed that for
1\leq k\leq d
,
n(k,d)
is precisely the maximum number of vertices of a complete graph that admits a bipartite covering of order
k
and size
d
.

Based on an elegant polynomial method and an explicit construction, Alon
[
1
]
proved the following theoretical bounds on
n(k,d)
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

For
1\leqslant k\leqslant d
, we have

\prod\limits_{i=0}^{k-1}\bigg(\left\lfloor\frac{d+i}{k}\right\rfloor+1\bigg)\leq n(k,d)\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{k}2^{i}\binom{d}{i}.

About one decade ago, Huang and Sudakov
[
6
]
improved the above upper bound via a clever rank argument and a trick of Peck
[
9
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
6
]
)
.

For
1\leqslant k\leqslant d
, we have

n(k,d)\leqslant 1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}2^{i-1}\binom{d}{i}.

Very recently, Alon, Grytczuk, Kisielewicz, and Przesławski
[
2
]
provided an upper bound for
n(k,d)
of a different type as follows.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
2
, Theorem 1]
)
.

For every
1\leqslant k\leqslant k+2i-2\leqslant d-1
, where
i
is an arbitrary positive integer,

n(k,d)\leqslant 2^{d-i}+\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil(k+2i-2)/2\rceil}\binom{d}{j}.

They also provided a general construction of
k
-neighborly families of boxes, which yields a new lower bound on
n(k,d)
. In particular, if
k
is fixed and
d\to\infty
, they
[
2
, Theorem 2]
showed that

n(k,d)\geq(1-o(1))\frac{d^{k}}{k!},

which improves Theorem
1.1
. They also proved a nice recursive lower bound on
n(k,d)

[
2
, Proposition 2]
. One novelty of their paper is to estimate
n(k,d)
from a perspective related to the Hamming metric; see Section
2
.

Inspired by their work
[
2
]
, in this paper, we establish an improved upper bound on
n(k,d)
for any positive integers
k
and
d
with
k\leq d-2
. In particular, we prove that
n(d-1,d)=3\cdot 2^{d-2}
as a special case; see Corollary
3.6
(see also
[
2
, Proposition 1]
). Our new upper bounds are closely related to an important configuration, namely
B_{k}^{(d)}
, in coding theory. More precisely, when
k=2t
,
B_{k}^{(d)}
is a
d
-dimensional Hamming ball of radius
t
, that is, a collection of binary vectors of length
d
having at most
t

1
-coordinates; when
k=2t+1
,
B_{k}^{(d)}
is the Cartesian product of
\{0,1\}
and a
(d-1)
-dimensional Hamming ball of radius
t
. It is clear that

\big|B_{k}^{(d)}\big|=\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{t}\binom{d}{i}&k=2t;\\
2\sum\limits_{i=0}^{t}\binom{d-1}{i}&k=2t+1.\end{cases}

(1)

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
1\leqslant k\leqslant d-1
be integers. If
d-k
is even, then

n(k,d)\leq\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\frac{d-k-2}{2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2^{j+1}}-\frac{1}{2^{d-k-j}}\bigg)\big|B_{k+2j}^{(d)}\big|+2^{\frac{d+k}{2}}.

If
d-k
is odd, then

n(k,d)\leq\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\frac{d-k-3}{2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2^{j+1}}-\frac{1}{2^{d-k-j}}\bigg)\big|B_{k+2j}^{(d)}\big|+\frac{1}{2^{\frac{d-k+1}{2}}}\big|B_{d-1}^{(d)}\big|+2^{\frac{d+k-1}{2}}.

It is not hard to show that Theorem
1.4
always improves Theorem
1.3
. Moreover, when
k,d
are sufficiently large and
k\geq 0.123d
, our upper bounds are asymptotically smaller than the upper bounds in Theorem
1.2
. We refer to the detailed computations for such a comparison in Section
3.2
.

When
k
is fixed, and
d
is sufficiently large, the following theorem further improves the upper bound on
n(k,d)
; see Remark
3.8
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
1\leqslant k\leqslant d-1
be integers.
If
d-k
is even, then

n(k,d)\leq\max\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{d}{j},\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\frac{d-k-2}{2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2^{j+1}}-\frac{1}{2^{d-k-j}}\bigg)\big|B_{k+2j}^{(d)}\big|+2^{\frac{d+k}{2}}\right\}.

If
d-k
is odd, then

n(k,d)\leq\max\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{d}{j},\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\frac{d-k-3}{2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2^{j+1}}-\frac{1}{2^{d-k-j}}\bigg)\big|B_{k+2j}^{(d)}\big|+\frac{1}{2^{\frac{d-k+1}{2}}}\big|B_{d-1}^{(d)}\big|+2^{\frac{d+k-1}{2}}\right\}.

Alon
[
1
]
remarked that even the precise determination of
n(2,d)
seems difficult. As a consequence of Theorem
1.5
, we improve the best-known upper bound on
n(2,d)
for any
d\leq 9
. Note that Theorem
1.5
only implies that
n(2,10)\leq 108
, which is worse than
n(2,10)\leq 101
from Theorem
1.2
. Nevertheless, we further develop the ideas in the proof of Theorem
1.5
in Theorem
3.9
, which in particular gives a better upper bound
n(2,10)\leq 95
.

Corollary 1.6
.

n(2,4)=9;\ n(2,5)\leq 14;\ n(2,6)\leq 21;\ n(2,7)\leq 29;\ n(2,8)\leq 45;\ n(2,9)\leq 70;\ n(2,10)\leq 95.

Preliminaries

Very recently, Alon, Grytczuk, Kisielewicz, and Przesławski
[
2
]
provided a new interpretation of the function
n(k,d)
. They showed that the problem of determining the function
n(k,d)
is equivalent to an extremal problem on a set of vectors with restricted Hamming distances.

Recall that for vectors
\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, the
Hamming distance
between
\bm{v}_{1}
and
\bm{v}_{2}
is the number of coordinates they differ, denoted by
d_{H}(\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2})
. Following the notions in
[
2
]
, we call
*
a
joker
. We can define the Hamming distance in
\{0,1,*\}^{d}
similarly. For two vectors
\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2}\in\{0,1,*\}^{d}
, the Hamming distance between them is defined to be the number of coordinates they differ in non-joker positions. For example, the Hamming distance between
(1,1,*,*,0,0)
and
(*,*,1,0,*,1)
is exactly
1
. This generalization of Hamming distance is closely related to the addressing problem for graphs
[
5
]
.

The following proposition describes an explicit connection between
n(k,d)
and the size of an extremal set vectors in
\{0,1,*\}^{d}
with restricted Hamming distance:

Proposition 2.1

(
[
2
, Section 2]
)
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{N}
. The function
n(k,d)
is equal to the maximum size of
U\subseteq\{0,1,*\}^{d}
such that the Hamming distance between
\bm{u}_{i}
and
\bm{u}_{j}
belongs to
\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
for any distinct
\bm{u}_{i},\bm{u}_{j}\in U
.

The starting point of the proof of our main results is the above interpretation of
n(k,d)
. Our proof of Theorem
1.4
is based on a weighted count of vectors in
\{0,1\}^{d}
. As a preparation, we revisit a classical extremal result related to restricted Hamming distances, namely Kleitman’s theorem
[
7
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
7
]
)
.

For integers
d>k
and a set
A
of vectors in
\{0,1\}^{d}
with
d_{H}(\bm{u},\bm{v})\leq k
for any vectors
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in A
, then

|A|\leq\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{t}\binom{d}{i}&k=2t;\\
2\sum\limits_{i=0}^{t}\binom{d-1}{i}&k=2t+1.\end{cases}

(2)

Moreover, the above upper bound can be attained by the configuration
B_{k}^{(d)}
.

There is a stability result for Kleitman’s theorem proved by Frankl
[
3
]
which states that if a set
A
of vectors in
\{0,1\}^{d}
, with pairwise Hamming distances bounded by
k
, is not contained in any copy of the configuration
B_{k}^{(d)}
, then the size of the set
A
is much smaller than the optimal value
|B_{k}^{(d)}|
. This result can be also proved via a robust linear algebra method; see the recent work of Gao, Liu, and Xu
[
4
]
.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
3
]
)
.

For integers
d\geq k+2
and a set
A
of vectors in
\{0,1\}^{d}
with
d_{H}(\bm{u},\bm{v})\leq k
for any vectors
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in A
,

1.

If
k=2t
and
A
is not contained in any
d
-dimensional Hamming ball of radius
t
, then

|A|\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{t}\binom{d}{i}-\binom{d-t-1}{t}+1.

(3)

2.

If
k=2t+1
and
A
is not contained in any Cartesian product of
\{0,1\}
and an
(d-1)
-dimensional Hamming ball of radius
t
, then

|A|\leq 2\sum_{i=0}^{t}\binom{d-1}{i}-\binom{d-t-2}{t}+1.

(4)

3
Improved upper bounds on
n(k,d)

Throughout the section, let
U=\{\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2},\ldots,\bm{u}_{n}\}
be a set of vectors in
\{0,1,*\}^{d}
such that the Hamming distance between
\bm{u}_{i}
and
\bm{u}_{j}
belongs to
\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
for any distinct
\bm{u}_{i},\bm{u}_{j}\in U
. In view of Proposition
2.1
,
n(k,d)
is the maximum size of such
U
, thus it suffices to study the upper bound on
|U|
.

For a vector
\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, we say
\bm{v}
is
covered by
a vector
\bm{u}\in U
(equivalently,
\bm{u}

covers

\bm{v}
) if the coordinates of
\bm{v}
and
\bm{u}
only differ in the joker-positions of
\bm{u}
.

Claim 3.1
.

For each vector
\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, there is at most one vector in
U
covering
\bm{v}
.

Proof of claim.

Suppose there are two distinct
\bm{u}
and
\bm{u^{\prime}}
in
U
covering a vector
\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, by the definition of
U
, there is at least one coordinate of
\bm{u}
and
\bm{u^{\prime}}
in non-joker position being different. However, as both of
\bm{u}
and
\bm{u^{\prime}}
cover the vector
\bm{v}
, all of the coordinates of
\bm{u}
and
\bm{u^{\prime}}
in non-joker positions should be the same, a contradiction.
∎

For a vector
\bm{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, let
\overline{\bm{v}}:=\bm{1}-\bm{v}=(1-v_{1},1-v_{2},\ldots,1-v_{d})
. Let

V^{(t)}:=\{\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}:\ \bm{v}\ \text{is covered by some\ }\bm{u}\in U\ \text{with exactly $t$ jokers}\},

and

\overline{V^{(t)}}:=\{\overline{\bm{v}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}:\ \bm{v}\ \text{is covered by some\ }\bm{u}\in U\ \text{with exactly $t$ jokers}\}.

It is obvious that
|V^{(t)}|=|\overline{V^{(t)}}|
. The following claim is useful.

Claim 3.2
.

For any
0\leq i<\frac{d-k}{2}
,
V^{(0)},\overline{V^{(0)}},V^{(1)},\overline{V^{(1)}},\ldots,V^{(i)},\overline{V^{(i)}}
are pairwise disjoint.

Proof of claim.

Suppose for some
integers
0\leq a,b\leq i
,
V^{(a)}\cap\overline{V^{(b)}}\neq\emptyset
, then there is some vector
\bm{v}\in V^{(a)}\cap\overline{V^{(b)}}
. Since
\bm{v}\in V^{(a)}
,
\bm{v}
is covered by some vector
\bm{u}_{1}\in U
with
a
jokers. Moreover,
\overline{\bm{v}}
is covered by some vector
\bm{u}_{2}\in U
with
b
jokers. As
d_{H}(\bm{v},\overline{\bm{v}})=d
, we have
d_{H}(\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2})\geq d-a-b\geq d-2i>k
as
i<\frac{d-k}{2}
, which contradicts to the assumption that
d_{H}(\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2})\leq k
. Moreover, by Claim
3.1
, any vector
\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
cannot be in both of
V^{(a)}
and
V^{(b)}
(
\overline{V^{(a)}}
and
\overline{V^{(b)}}
, respectively) for distinct
0\leq a,b\leq i
.
The proof is finished.
∎

The following claim (also independently observed in
[
2
]
) is also crucial in our proof. We provide a simple proof for the sake of completeness.

Claim 3.3
.

For any
0\leq i<\frac{d-k}{2}
and any two distinct vectors
\bm{a},\bm{b}\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{i}V^{(s)}
,
d_{H}(\bm{a},\bm{b})\leq k+2i
. As a consequence, we have

\sum_{s=0}^{i}|V^{(s)}|\leq|B_{k+2i}^{(d)}|.

(5)

Proof of claim.

As
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
are covered by some vectors
\bm{u}_{1}
and
\bm{u}_{2}
in
U
with at most
i
jokers, and
d_{H}(\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2})\leq k
, we have
d_{H}(\bm{a},\bm{b})\leq k+2i
. The claim follows from Theorem
2.2
.
∎

3.1
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Equip each vector
\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
with a weight function
f(\bm{v})
. If
\bm{v}
is covered by some vector
\bm{u}\in U
(note that such a vector
\bm{u}
is unique by Claim
3.1
) and
\bm{u}
has exactly
t
jokers, then we define
f(\bm{v})=\frac{1}{2^{t}}
. Otherwise, we define
f(\bm{v})=0
.

By definition of the function
f(\bm{v})
, we have

\sum_{\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}f(\bm{v})=|U|.

The above formula holds because each vector
\bm{u}\in U
with
t
jokers covers exactly
2^{t}
vectors in
\{0,1\}^{d}
, and every binary vector
\bm{v}
can be covered by at most one vector in
U
, which is shown in Claim
3.1
. Therefore, it suffices to analyze the values of the function
f(\bm{v})
for every
\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
.

Claim 3.4
.

For a vector
\bm{v}\in\overline{V^{(i)}}
with
0\leq i<\frac{d-k}{2}
, we have
f(\bm{v})\leq\frac{1}{2^{d-k-i}}
.

Proof of claim.

If
\bm{v}\in\overline{V^{(i)}}
is not covered by any vector in
U
, then
f(\bm{v})=0
. Otherwise
\bm{v}
is covered by some vector
\bm{u}\in U
with
j
jokers. And
\overline{\bm{v}}
is covered by some vector
\bm{w}\in U
with
i
jokers, as
d_{H}(\bm{v},\overline{\bm{v}})=d
. So we have
d-j-i\leq d_{H}(\bm{u},\bm{w})\leq k
, which gives
j\geq d-k-i
. Therefore
f(\bm{v})\leq\frac{1}{2^{d-k-i}}
.
∎

Claim 3.5
.

For any integer
0\leq i<\frac{d-k-1}{2}
, let
\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup\overline{V^{(0)}}\cup V^{(1)}\cup\overline{V^{(1)}}\cup\cdots\cup V^{(i)}\cup\overline{V^{(i)}})
, we have

f(\bm{v})+f(\overline{\bm{v}})\leq\frac{1}{2^{i+1}}+\frac{1}{2^{d-k-i-1}}.

When
d-k
is odd and
i=\frac{d-k-1}{2}
, we have

f(\bm{v})+f(\overline{\bm{v}})\leq\frac{1}{2^{i}}.

Proof of claim.

We consider the following two cases.

1.

If
\bm{v}
and
\overline{\bm{v}}
are covered by some two vectors in
U
, say
\bm{u}_{i}
and
\bm{u}_{j}
, as
d_{H}(\bm{v},\overline{\bm{v}})=d
and
d_{H}(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{u}_{j})\leq k
, the sum of the number of jokers in
\bm{u}_{i}
and
\bm{u}_{j}
is at least
d-k
. We can see
f(\bm{v})=\frac{1}{2^{x}}
and
f(\overline{\bm{v}})=\frac{1}{2^{y}}
subject to
x\geq i+1
,
y\geq i+1
and

, then the right-hand side of the above inequality holds and thus
|U|=151+\frac{1}{4}
, which is absurd. This observation leads to the following three different cases.

Subcase 2.1.

Suppose that there is a vector
\bm{v}\in\overline{V^{(0)}}
such that
f(\bm{v})=0
. Then the above analysis shows that the remaining
92
vectors in
\overline{V^{(0)}}
must have weight
\frac{1}{4}
, and
f(\bm{v})=\frac{1}{2}
for all
\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{8}\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup\overline{V^{(0)}})
. Thus,
there are exactly
23
vectors in
U
with
2
jokers, and they exactly cover the
92
vectors in
\overline{V^{(0)}}
with nonzero weight. However, this is impossible by Claim
4.2
.

Subcase 2.2.

Suppose that there is at least one vector in
\overline{V^{(0)}}
satisfying
0<f(\bm{v})<\frac{1}{4}
, then there are at least
8
vectors in
\{0,1\}^{8}
that are covered by some vector in
U
with at least
3
jokers, which implies that

|U|\leq 151+\frac{1}{4}-8\bigg(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{8}\bigg)<151.

Subcase 2.3.

Suppose that all of these
93
vectors in
\overline{V^{(0)}}
are equipped with weight
\frac{1}{4}
. Since
4
is not divided by
93
, there exist at least 3 vectors not in
\overline{V^{(0)}}
with weight
\frac{1}{4}
. It follows that

|U|\leq 151+\frac{1}{4}-3\bigg(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\bigg)<151.

4.3
Exact value of
n(3,6)

The goal of this section is to show
n(3,6)=27
. It suffices to show
n(3,6)\neq 28
. For the sake of contradiction, assume that
|U|=28
. From inequality (
8
) with
i=0
, we have

|U|\leq 2^{4}+2^{3}+\bigg(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{8}\bigg)|V^{(0)}|.

It follows that
|V^{(0)}|\geq 11
. On the other hand,
|V^{(0)}|\leq 2\sum_{j=0}^{1}\binom{5}{j}=12
. Therefore,
|V^{(0)}|=12
or
|V^{(0)}|=11
. By Theorem
2.3
, without loss of generality, we can further assume that
\overline{V^{(0)}}\subseteq\{0,1\}\square\binom{[5]}{\leq 1}
.

Let
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
if
|V^{(0)}|=12
, and
\delta=\frac{1}{8}
if
|V^{(0)}|=11
.
Note that we have

|U|=\sum_{\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{6}}f(\bm{v})=|V^{(0)}|+\sum_{\bm{v}\in\overline{V^{(0)}}}f(\bm{v})+\sum_{\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{6}\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup\overline{V^{(0)}})}f(\bm{v})\leq 28+\delta,

where
f(\bm{v})\leq\frac{1}{8}
for
\bm{v}\in\overline{V^{(0)}}
, and
f(\bm{v})+f(\overline{\bm{v}})\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}=\frac{3}{4}
if
\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{6}\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup\overline{V^{(0)}})
. However, note that if
f(\bm{v})=\frac{1}{8}
for all
\bm{v}\in\overline{V^{(0)}}
and
f(\bm{v})+f(\overline{\bm{v}})=\frac{3}{4}
for all
\bm{v}\in\{0,1\}^{6}\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup\overline{V^{(0)}})
, then the right-hand side of the above inequality holds and thus
|U|=28+\delta
, which is absurd.

Suppose there is some vector
\bm{v}\in\overline{V^{(0)}}
that is covered by a vector
\bm{u}\in U
with at least
4
jokers, then we have

|U|\leq 28+\frac{1}{2}-16\bigg(\frac{1}{8}-\frac{1}{16}\bigg)<28,

which is impossible. Thus, each vector
\bm{v}\in\overline{V^{(0)}}
has either
f(\bm{v})=\frac{1}{8}
or
f(\bm{v})=0
. Moreover, there are at most
4
vectors
\bm{v}\in\overline{V^{(0)}}
with
f(\bm{v})=0
, for otherwise

|U|\leq 28+\frac{1}{2}-5\cdot\frac{1}{8}<28.

Therefore,
U
has at least
1
vector with
3
jokers.

Case 1.

Suppose that
U
has only one vector
\bm{u}
with
3
jokers. Then
\bm{u}
needs to cover at least
11-4=7
vectors in
\overline{V^{(0)}}
. However, it is easy to verify that
\bm{u}
can cover at most
6
vectors in
\{0,1\}\square\binom{[5]}{\leq 1}
.

Case 2.

Suppose that
U
has at least
2
vectors with
3
jokers.

Subcase 2.1.

If
|V^{(0)}|=11
, then at least
5
vectors in
\{0,1\}^{6}\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup\overline{V^{(0)}})
have weight
\frac{1}{8}
and thus

|U|\leq 28+\frac{1}{8}-5\cdot\bigg(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{8}\bigg)<28.

Subcase 2.2.

If
|V^{(0)}|=12
, then at least
4
vectors in
\{0,1\}^{6}\setminus(V^{(0)}\cup\overline{V^{(0)}})
have weight
\frac{1}{8}
and thus

|U|\leq 28+\frac{1}{2}-4\cdot\bigg(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{8}\bigg)=28.

The assumption that
|U|=28
then indicates that there are exactly
2
vectors in
U
with
3
jokers, and they cover all
12
vectors in
\overline{V^{(0)}}
. Note that any vector
\bm{u}\in\{0,1,*\}^{6}
covers at most
6
vectors in
\overline{V^{(0)}}=\{0,1\}\square\binom{[5]}{\leq 1}
, and
\bm{u}
covers
6
vectors only if
\bm{u}\in\{0,1,*\}^{6}
is of the form
\{*\}\square\bm{s}
, where
s
contains
2
jokers. However, it is easy to verify that any two vectors of such a form cannot cover all 12 vectors in
\{0,1\}\square\binom{[5]}{\leq 1}
.

5
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we mainly focus on improving the upper bounds of the function
n(k,d)
when
k
is relatively close to
d
. The general improvement relies on a weighted count of all vectors in
\{0,1\}^{d}
. Furthermore, the authors in
[
2
]
provided several better lower bounds on
n(k,d)
via Gurobi (see Table
1
) and they expected that all those solutions to the corresponding MIP problems should be optimal. Fortunately, we confirm that several of them are indeed optimal and the stability result of Kleitman’s theorem is useful in the proofs. One can further improve the upper bounds of
n(k,d)
via a similar argument in our proofs. However, in order to fully determine the values, even the asymptotic results of
n(k,d)
, new ideas are needed.

When
k>1
, the best-known general upper bound was obtained with tools of linear algebra
[
6
]
until Alon, Grytczuk, Kisielewicz, and Przesławski
[
2
]
improved the upper bound when
k
is relatively close to
d
without tools of linear algebra. Inspired by their results, we improve the upper bound in
[
6
]
when
k=2
and
d\leq 10
using purely combinatorial analysis, which yields that linear algebra methods do not provide tight bound on
n(k,d)
even for
k=2
. Hence, improving the upper bound on
n(k,d)
remains a challenging task in general.
