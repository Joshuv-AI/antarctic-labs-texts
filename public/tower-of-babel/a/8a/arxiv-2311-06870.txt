Grassmannian Persistence Diagrams
Aziz Burak Gülen, Facundo Mémoli, Zhengchao Wan

Abstract
We introduce Orthogonal Möbius Inversion, a concept analogous to Möbius inversion on finite posets, which is applicable to order-preserving functions from a finite poset to the Grassmannian 𝖦𝗋 ⁡ ( V ) \mathsf{Gr}(V) of an inner product space V V . This notion critically relies on the inner product structure on V V enabling it to capture much finer information than standard integer-valued persistence diagrams. Orthogonal Inversion is a special case of the broader concept of Orthomodular Inversion, where the target space is any orthomodular lattice, which we also identify. We apply Orthogonal Inversion in order to construct a ”non-negative” persistence diagram for any given multiparameter filtration 𝖥 \mathsf{F} of a finite simplicial complex K K , indexed over an arbitrary finite poset P P . This is done by applying it to the birth-death spaces of 𝖥 \mathsf{F} . Analogously to 1 1 -parameter classical persistence diagrams, these multiparameter Grassmannian persistence diagrams offer straightforward interpretability. Specifically, to a segment ( b , d ) ∈ 𝖲𝖾𝗀 ⁡ ( P ) (b,d)\in\mathsf{Seg}(P) , (1) the Grassmannian persistence diagram canonically assigns a vector subspace of C ρ K C_{\rho}^{K} consisting of cycles that are born at b b and become boundaries at d d and (2) this assignment is exhaustive at the homology level. Finally, we relate our Grassmannian persistence diagrams to the recently introduced notion of Möbius homology, thus enhancing its interpretability through the lens of our framework. Keywords. Möbius inversion, multiparameter persistence, persistence diagrams, topological data analysis. Mathematics Subject Classification. 55U15, 55N31, 06A15, 05E45.

Introduction

In Topological Data Analysis (TDA)
[
CZ09
,
EH10
]
,
persistent homology

is a tool for tracking and characterizing the evolution (i.e. the “birth” and “death”) of homological features throughout a filtration. In the case of a 1-parameter filtration, this is achieved through an algebraic decomposition of the persistence module which is obtained by applying the homology functor to the given filtration. Every 1-parameter persistence module
M:\mathbb{L}\to{{\mathsf{Vec}}}
, where
\mathbb{L}:=\{1<\cdots<n\}
is any finite linear poset, admits an interval decomposition
[

M

interval

lifespan
of a topological feature in the filtration (i.e. the maximal region throughout which a topological feature is “alive”). Interestingly, the multiplicities of intervals in this decomposition can be obtained through a notion of inclusion-exclusion, i.e. through
Möbius inversion
. More precisely, the function that assigns to each interval
(i,j)
its multiplicity in the decomposition coincides with the
Möbius inverse
of the rank function

[

In the case of multiparameter filtrations, i.e. filtrations defined on an arbitrary (say, finite) poset
P

First off, the generalization of the notion of interval is substantially richer than its precursor in the 1-parameter case and, more importantly, the indecomposables are no longer only interval modules. Furthermore, the space of indecomposables can be enormously complex

The interpretability of signed persistence diagrams.

As mentioned above, whereas in the 1-parameter setting it is straightforward to interpret persistence diagrams (arising either from rank or birth-death functions), in the multiparameter setting signed persistence diagrams are only partially interpretable:

1.

When a multiparameter persistence module is segment/interval decomposable, its rank induced persistence diagram is non-negative and it captures the multiplicity of each segment/interval appearing in is decomposition
[
KM21
, Theorem 3.14]
. However, in the topology induced by the interleaving distance on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
modules (for
n\geq 2
), the collection of all almost-indecomposable modules is open and dense, i.e. the defining property is generic; see
[
BS22
, Corollary 1]
. That is, the setting of decomposable multiparameter modules is indeed relatively restrictive which makes results such as
[
KM21
, Theorem 3.14]
rarely applicable in practise.

2.

Sometimes negative values are attained by rank induced persistence diagrams (see e.g.
[
KM23b
, Example 18]
) and, by the previous item, these signify that the underlying persistence module is not segment/interval decomposable.

3.

Even if a rank induced persistence diagram is non-negative, the underlying persistence module cannot be guaranteed to be segment/interval decomposable. Indeed, segments/intervals that are assigned positive values by the rank persistence diagram are not necessarily linked to a direct summand of the module; see
[
KM21
, Example 3.21]
.

4.

In the case of birth-death induced or rank induced signed persistence diagrams, even when non-negative, the integer number assigned to a segment
(b,d)\in\mathsf{Seg}(P)
does not necessarily correspond to the number of cycles that are born at
b
and die at
d
; see
Fig.

3
and
Example

4.26
.

In summary, what the Möbius inversion of the rank or birth-death function achieves in the
1
-parameter setting does not fully carry over to the multiparameter setting. The interpretation/meaning of the numbers determined by such persistence diagrams is unclear. Specifically, there is a lack of connection between the persistent diagrams obtained as Möbius inverses of the rank and birth-death functions and the evolution of cycles throughout a filtration in the multiparameter context. Our work addresses these shortcomings by identifying a novel, and richer, variant of the notion of persistence diagram which admits direct interpretation; see
Fig.

Main Contributions

We introduce a novel variant of Möbius inversion, termed
Orthogonal Inversion
, which operates on functions

\sum_{r^{\prime}\leq r}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})=\mathfrak{F}(r)\,\,\text{for every $r\in R$;}

Interpretability.

Our method, when applied to the birth-death space valued function
\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}

•

For any segment

•

As a refinement of the previous item, this assignment is exhaustive at the level of homology. Indeed, for any segment

Discriminative power.

In addition to admitting a more direct interpretation than both standard birth-death and rank-induced persistence diagrams, the Grassmannian persistence diagram
\mathsf{OI}\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}\right)

[

Enhancing interpretability of persistent Möbius Homology.

Unlike the conventional approach of associating a
single
persistence diagram to a given persistence module, Patel and Skraba’s recent work
[
PS23
]
introduces a novel perspective: a framework that assigns to a persistence module an entire
family of persistence diagrams
, indexed by a nonnegative integer, which we refer to as the
stratum
and denote by
s
.
5
5

5

For a given integer
s\geq 0

s

A key insight from
[

is that the Euler characteristic (i.e., alternating sum) of this family of persistence diagrams precisely recovers the birth-death induced signed persistence diagram
[
PS23

of Möbius inversion—hence the name Möbius homology. Moreover, this perspective can also be seen as unraveling the intricate structure of the birth-death induced signed persistence diagram of a module by breaking it into simpler, more manageable pieces (the different strata). Furthermore, similarly to what we do in this paper in terms of Grassmanian persistence diagrams, Patel and Skraba demonstrate that this family consisting of different strata is strictly more informative than the birth-death induced signed persistence diagram for a given persistence module
[

The Möbius Homology perspective is not only novel and intriguing but also represents a conceptual breakthrough in our understanding of persistence modules. Despite this richness, the precise meaning and implications of these stratified diagrams remain largely unexplored. In this work, we contribute to a deeper understanding of these diagrams by leveraging the interpretability of Grassmannian persistence diagrams established here and uncovering a fundamental connection to Patel and Skraba’s work—one that may suggest a future higher-order construction of Grassmannian persistence diagrams.

Organization of the Paper

In
Section

2.5
, we introduce the notion of
Monoidal Möbius inverse
(
Definition

2.20
). The common theme across the applications of Möbius inversion in TDA
[
Pat18
]
is that the functions to which Möbius inversion is applied are valued in an abelian group. This abelian group often arises as the group completion of the monoid consisting of the isomorphism classes of objects in an abelian category

Definition

2.20

as demonstrated in

[
MP22
,
GM22
]
.

In

3
, we introduce the concepts of Orthogonal Inversion and Orthomodular Inversion. Orthogonal Inversion applies to order-preserving functions defined from a finite poset to a Grassmannian. When applied to such a function, Orthogonal Inversion produces a monoidal Möbius inverse of the function, as shown in

. This provides us with a functorial and stable way (see

In

Grassmannian.

Let
V

In this paper, we only utilize the monoidal structure and the natural partial order on

Posets and (Algebraic) Möbius Inversion

The notion of Möbius inversion can regarded as a combinatorial analog to the notion of derivative. It is one of the most fundamental tools in the algebraic-combinatorial approach to the study of persistence modules developed in
[
Pat18
]
. Here, we recall the essential background on Möbius inversion required for this paper and direct readers to
[

Poset(s) of Segments.

Let
(P,\leq)
be a poset. For any
a\leq b
, we refer to pair
(a,b)\in P\times P
as a
bounded segment
. For every
a\in P
, we introduce a distinguished pair, denoted
(a,\infty)
, and we refer to these pairs as
unbounded segments
. Unbounded segments, as defined here, enable us to conceptualize the absence of a maximum element in a segment, thus allowing the identification of cycles in a filtration that never die (or, die at infinity). We denote the collection of bounded and unbounded segments in
P
by
\mathsf{Seg}(P)
, which we refer to as the
set of segments
of
P
. We denote by
\mathsf{diag}(P):=\{(a,a)\in\mathsf{Seg}(P)\mid a\in P\}
the
diagonal
of
\mathsf{Seg}(P)
.

In this paper, we will assume that all posets are finite unless otherwise specified.

The
product order
on
\mathsf{Seg}(P)
,
\leq_{{\times}}
, is given by the restriction of the product order on
P\times P
to
\mathsf{Seg}(P)
. More precisely,

(b_{1},d_{1})\leq_{{\times}}(b_{2},d_{2})\iff b_{1}\leq b_{2}\text{ and }d_{1}\leq d_{2}

where we assume
p<\infty
for all
p\in P
. We will also use another order on
\mathsf{Seg}(P)
, called the
reverse inclusion order
. The reverse inclusion order on
\mathsf{Seg}(P)
, denoted
\leq_{{\supseteq}}
, is given by

(b_{1},d_{1})\leq_{{\supseteq}}(b_{2},d_{2})\iff b_{1}\leq b_{2}\text{ and }d_{1}\geq d_{2}.

We denote by

•

\overline{P}^{\times}:=(\mathsf{Seg}(P),\leq_{{\times}})
the poset of segments with the product order;

•

\overline{P}^{\supseteq}:=(\mathsf{Seg}(P),\leq_{{\supseteq}})
the poset of non-diagonal segments with the reverse inclusion order.

Remark 2.4

(About the choice of order on
\mathsf{Seg}(P)
)
.

The reverse inclusion order
\leq_{{\supseteq}}
is the one that has been traditionally used in the applied algebraic community, in particular it is the one used to induce signed barcodes from the rank invariant in
[
BOO22
]
. The product order
\leq_{{\times}}
has been advocated by McCleary and Patel due to its favorable interaction with Möbius inversion of birth-death functions; see
[
MP22
,
MP21
]
. In this paper, we will will mostly utilize the product order on
\mathsf{Seg}(P).

Notice that if
f:P\to Q
is an order-preserving map between two posets
P
and
Q
, then
f
induces an order-preserving map between the posets of segments
\overline{P}^{\times}
and
\overline{Q}^{\times}
. We denote by
\overline{f}:\overline{P}^{\times}\to\overline{Q}^{\times}
the map induced by
f
that acts to segments component-wisely. That is,
\overline{f}((b,d)):=(f(b),f(d))
and
\overline{f}((b,\infty)):=(f(b),\infty)
for every
b\leq d\in P
. Note that with the convention described above, this condition means that
\overline{f}
preserve the type of segments, i.e., it maps (un)bounded segments to (un)bounded segments.

Metric Posets.

A finite
(extended) metric poset
is a pair
(P,d_{P})
where
P
is a finite poset and
d_{P}:P\times P\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is an (extended) metric
such that for every
p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq p_{3}\in P

d_{P}(p_{1},p_{2})\leq d_{P}(p_{1},p_{3})
and
d_{P}(p_{2},p_{3})\leq d_{P}(p_{1},p_{3})
.
A
morphism of finite metric posets

\alpha:(P,d_{P})\to(Q,d_{Q})
is an order-preserving map
\alpha:P\to Q
. The
distortion
of a morphism
\alpha:(P,d_{P})\to(Q,d_{Q})
, denoted
\mathrm{dis}(\alpha)
, is

\mathrm{dis}(\alpha):=\max_{p_{1},p_{2}\in P}\big|d_{P}(p_{1},p_{2})-d_{Q}(\alpha(p_{1}),\alpha(p_{2}))\big|.

For every finite metric poset
(P,d_{P})
, its poset of segments
\overline{P}^{\times}
is also a metric poset with

d_{\overline{P}^{\times}}((b_{1},d_{1}),(b_{2},d_{2})):=\max\{d_{P}(b_{1},b_{2}),d_{P}(d_{1},d_{2})\}.

A morphism of finite metric posets
\alpha:(P,d_{P})\to(Q,d_{Q})
induces a morphism of finite metric posets
\overline{\alpha}:\left(\overline{P}^{\times},d_{\overline{P}^{\times}}\right)\to\left(\overline{Q}^{\times},d_{\overline{Q}^{\times}}\right)
via
\overline{\alpha}((b,d))=(\alpha(b),\alpha(d))
, with
\mathrm{dis}(\alpha)=\mathrm{dis}(\overline{\alpha})
; see
[
MP22
, Proposition 3.4]
.

Simplicial Complexes and Chain Spaces.

An (abstract)
finite simplicial complex

K
over a finite ordered vertex set
V
is a non-empty collection of non-empty subsets of
V
with the property that for every
\sigma\in K
, if
\tau\subseteq\sigma
, then
\tau\in K
. An element
\sigma\in K
is called a
\rho
-simplex
if the cardinality of
\sigma
is
\rho+1
. An
oriented simplex
, denoted
[\sigma]
, is a simplex
\sigma\in K
whose vertices are ordered. We always assume that ordering on simplices is inherited from the ordering on
V
. Let
\mathfrak{s}_{\rho}^{K}
denote the set of all oriented
\rho
-simplices of
K
.

The
\rho
-th chain space of
K
, denoted
C_{\rho}^{K}
, is the vector space over
\mathbb{R}
with basis
\mathfrak{s}_{\rho}^{K}
. Let
n_{\rho}^{K}:=|\mathfrak{s}_{\rho}^{K}|=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(C_{\rho}^{K})
. The
\rho
-th boundary operator

\partial_{\rho}^{K}:C_{\rho}^{K}\to C_{\rho-1}^{K}
is defined by

\partial_{\rho}^{K}([v_{0},\ldots,v_{\rho}]):=\sum_{i=0}^{\rho}(-1)^{i}[v_{0},\ldots,\hat{v}_{i},\ldots,v_{\rho}]

for every oriented
\rho
-simplex
[\sigma]=[v_{0},\ldots,v_{\rho}]\in\mathfrak{s}_{\rho}^{K}
, where
[v_{0},\ldots,\hat{v}_{i},\ldots,v_{\rho}]
denotes the omission of the
i
-th vertex, and extended linearly to
C_{\rho}^{K}
. We denote by
\mathsf{Z}_{\rho}(K)
the space of
\rho
-cycles of
K
, that is

\mathsf{Z}_{\rho}(K):=\ker\left(\partial_{\rho}^{K}\right),

and we denote by
\mathsf{B}_{\rho}(K)
the space of
\rho
-boundaries of
K
, that is

\mathsf{B}_{\rho}(K):=\Ima\left(\partial_{\rho+1}^{K}\right).

Additionally, we denote by
H_{\rho}(K)
the
\rho
-th homology group of
K
, that is

H_{\rho}(K):=\frac{\mathsf{Z}_{\rho}(K)}{\mathsf{B}_{\rho}(K)}.

For each integer
\rho\geq 0
, we define an inner product,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{C_{\rho}^{K}}
, on
C_{\rho}^{K}
as follows:

\langle[\sigma],[\sigma^{\prime}]\rangle_{C_{\rho}^{K}}:=\delta_{[\sigma],[\sigma^{\prime}]}\text{, for all }[\sigma],[\sigma^{\prime}]\in\mathfrak{s}_{\rho}^{K},

where
\delta_{\bullet,\bullet}
is the Kronecker delta.
That is, we declare that
\mathfrak{s}_{\rho}^{K}
is an orthonormal basis for
C_{\rho}^{K}
. We will refer to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{C_{\rho}^{K}}
as the
standard
inner product on
C_{\rho}^{K}
. We will omit the subscript from the notation
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{C_{\rho}^{K}}
when the context is clear. We denote by
\left(\partial_{\rho}^{K}\right)^{*}:C_{\rho-1}^{K}\to C_{\rho}^{K}
the adjoint of
\partial_{\rho}^{K}
with respect to the standard inner products on
C_{\rho}^{K}
and
C_{\rho-1}^{K}
.

In this paper, we work with a fixed finite simplicial complex
K
and primarily use real coefficients. However, some of our results, definitions, and remarks still hold when the coefficients are taken from any field. Specifically, whenever a result, definition, example, or remark involves the symbols
\mathsf{OI}
, or
\ominus
, real coefficients are required. In all other cases, the results hold with coefficients from any field.

Simplicial Filtrations.

For a finite simplicial complex
K
, let
\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
denote the poset of subcomplexes of
K
, ordered by inclusion. A
simplicial filtration of
K
is an order-preserving map
\mathsf{F}:P\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
, where
P
is a finite poset.
A
1
-parameter
filtration of
K
is a filtration
\mathsf{F}:\mathbb{L}\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
where
\mathbb{L}=\{\ell_{1}<\cdots<\ell_{n}\}
is a finite linearly ordered set and
\mathsf{F}(\ell_{n})=K
. When
\mathsf{F}:\{\ell_{1}<\cdots<\ell_{n}\}\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
is a
1
-parameter filtration, we use the notation
K_{i}
for the simplicial complex
\mathsf{F}(\ell_{i})
and succinctly write
\mathsf{F}=\{K_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
to denote the simplicial filtration. Note that
K_{n}=K
.

Definition 2.13

(Persistent Betti numbers / rank invariant
[
ELZ02
]
)
.

Let
\mathsf{F}:P\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
be a filtration. For
(p,p^{\prime})\in\mathsf{Seg}(P)
, let
\iota_{\rho}^{p,p^{\prime}}:H_{\rho}(\mathsf{F}(p))\to H_{\rho}(\mathsf{F}(p^{\prime}))
denote the homomorpshim induced by the inclusion
\mathsf{F}(p)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{F}(p^{\prime})
. We define the
\rho
-th persistent Betti number
for the segment
(p,p^{\prime})
as

\beta_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((p,p^{\prime})):=\rank\left(\iota_{\rho}^{p,p^{\prime}}\right).

As is customary in applied algebraic topology, we also use the term
rank invariant
to refer to persistent Betti numbers.

Let
\mathsf{F}=\{K_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a filtration of
K
. Observe that, for any dimension
\rho\geq 0
, the inclusion of simplicial complexes
K_{i}\subseteq K_{j}
, for
i\leq j
, induces canonical inclusions on the cycle and boundary spaces. In particular, for any
i=1,\ldots,n
, the
\rho
-th cycle and boundary spaces of
K_{i}
can be identified with subspaces of
C_{\rho}^{K_{n}}=C_{\rho}^{K}
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{Z}_{\rho}(K_{i})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{Z}_{\rho}(K_{j})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{Z}_{\rho}(K)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{\rho}^{K}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{B}_{\rho}(K_{i})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{B}_{\rho}(K_{j})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{B}_{\rho}(K)}

Definition 2.14

(Birth-death spaces)
.

Let
\mathsf{F}:P\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
be a filtration. For any degree
\rho\geq 0
, the
\rho
-th
birth-death spaces
associated to
\mathsf{F}
is defined as the function
\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}:\overline{P}^{\times}\to\mathsf{Gr}\left(C_{\rho}^{K}\right)
given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{Z}_{\rho}\big(\mathsf{F}(b)\big)\cap\mathsf{B}_{\rho}\big(\mathsf{F}(d)\big),

\displaystyle\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,\infty))

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{Z}_{\rho}\big(\mathsf{F}(b)\big).

Informally, when
b\leq d
, for a cycle
z
to be in the birth-death space
\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))
means that
z
becomes “alive” at or before
b
and that it “dies” (i.e. it becomes a boundary) at or before
d
.

Remark 2.15
.

The classical definition of persistence diagrams, as in
[
CSEH07
]
, utilizes persistent Betti numbers. Birth-death spaces were introduced in
[
MP22
,
GHP21
]
as an alternative way to define persistence diagrams. For a
1
-parameter filtration, classical persistence diagrams and persistence diagrams obtained through utilizing the dimension of birth-death spaces coincide as shown in
[
MP22
, Section 9.1]
. McCleary and Patel showed that the use of birth-death spaces for defining persistence diagrams enables us to organize the persistent homology pipeline in a functorial way
[
MP22
]
. Additionally, this functoriality leads to the edit distance stability of persistence diagrams as in
[
MP22
, Theorem 8.4]
.

We now discuss how to determine from
\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}
the exact number of independent
\rho
-cycles that “
are born at precisely
b
and die precisely at
d
”. This will pave the way towards the precise notion of birth and death of cycles which we give in
Definition

2.17
. Note that in TDA, the death time is typically used in reference to homology classes as opposed to cycles and
the death time of a homology class refers to the first time when the class merges with an “older” one, following the “elder rule”
[
EH10
,
Cur18
]
.

Remark 2.16

(Number of independent cycles that “are born at
b
and die at
d
”)
.

Let
\mathsf{F}:P\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
be a filtration. First, we observe that the definition of birth-death spaces directly implies that
\dim\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))\right)
equals the number of linearly independent cycles that

“(are born at or before
b
) and (die at or before
d
).”

Towards our goal, we need to refine the information contained in the birth-death space. This will be accomplished by computing the quotient of
\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))
by a certain subspace
W_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))
that we now describe. Consider the subspaces

\displaystyle W_{]b,d)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leq b\\
c<d\end{subarray}}\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((a,c))\subseteq C_{\rho}^{K},

\displaystyle W_{)b,d]}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a<b\\
c\leq d\end{subarray}}\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((a,c))\subseteq C_{\rho}^{K}.

Note that
\dim\left(W_{]b,d)}\right)
is exactly the number of linearly independent cycles that

“(are born at or before
b
) and (die strictly before
d
).”

Equivalently, this can also be expressed as the number of linearly independent cycles that

“((are born strictly before
b
) and (die strictly before
d
))

or

((are born at
b
) and (die strictly before
d
)).”

Similarly,
\dim\left(W_{)b,d]}\right)
is exactly the number of linearly independent cycles that

“((are born strictly before
b
) and (die strictly before
d
))

or

((are born strictly before
b
) and (die at
d
)).”

Let

W_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d)):=W_{]b,d)}+W_{)b,d]}=\sum_{(a,c)<_{\times}(b,d)}\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((a,c)).

Combining the two interpretations above, we see that
\dim\left(W_{(b,d)}\right)
is exactly the number of linearly independent cycles that

“((are born strictly before
b
) and (die strictly before
d
))

or

((are born at
b
) and (die strictly before
d
)

or

((are born strictly before
b
) and (die at
d
)).”

Hence, with the above interpretations, it follows that

\displaystyle\dim\left(\frac{\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))}{W_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))}\right)

\displaystyle=\dim\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))\right)-\dim\left(W_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))\right)

\displaystyle=\dim\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))\right)-\dim\left(\sum_{(a,c)<_{\times}(b,d)}\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((a,c))\right)

equals the number of linearly independent cycles that

“are born precisely at
b
and die precisely at
d
”.

The considerations in
Remark

2.16
lead to the following definition of
lifespan
of a cycle.

Definition 2.17

(Lifespan of cycles / ephemeral cycles)
.

Let
P
be any finite poset and let
\mathsf{F}:P\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
be a filtration. Let
(b,d)\in\mathsf{Seg}(P)
. We say that a nonzero cycle
z\in C_{\rho}^{K}
has
lifespan
(b,d)
if the following two conditions are met:

•

z\in\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))
, and

•

z\notin W_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))=\sum_{(a,c)<_{\times}(b,d)}\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((a,c))
.

When a cycle
z
is born at
b
and dies at
b
(i.e.,
b=d
), we say that
z
is an
ephemeral
cycle.

Remark 2.18
.

Note that when
P
is a linear poset, the number of linearly independent
\rho
-cyles that are born at
b
and die at
d
coincides with the multiplicity of the interval
(b,d)
in the degree-
\rho
persistence diagram of the filtration
\mathsf{F}:P\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
. To be specific, when
P=\mathbb{L}=\{\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\cdots<\ell_{n}\}
, we have that the number

\dim\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((\ell_{i},\ell_{j}))\right)-\dim\left(\sum_{(\ell_{k},\ell_{l})<_{\times}(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})}\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((\ell_{k},\ell_{l}))\right)

boils down to the following expression

\dim\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((\ell_{i},\ell_{j}))\right)-\dim\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((\ell_{i-1},\ell_{j}))\right)+\dim\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((\ell_{i-1},\ell_{j-1}))\right)-\dim\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((\ell_{i},\ell_{j-1}))\right),

which, by
Proposition

2.6
, is the Möbius inverse of the birth-death function evaluated at
(b,d)
. And, as shown in
[
MP22
, Section 9.1]
, this number is exactly the multiplicity of the interval
(b,d)
in the persistence diagram of the filtration
\mathsf{F}
.

Remark 2.19
.

(Non-uniqueness of lifespans in multiparameter filtrations)
Note that, while a cycle can only have a unique lifespan in a 1-parameter filtration, it can exhibit multiple lifespans in a multiparameter filtration, as illustrated in
Fig.

and
Example

2.5
Monoidal Möbius Inverses

The “algebraic” Möbius inverse of a function
m:P\to\mathcal{M}
, where
\mathcal{M}
is a commutative monoid, involves the group completion of
\mathcal{M}
, denoted
\kappa(\mathcal{M})
. This is required in order to “make sense” of the minus operations that may appear in Möbius inversion formulas such as the one in
Proposition

2.6
. However, the group completion of a commutative monoid could be the trivial group, yielding a trivial algebraic Möbius inverse. In particular, for any vector space
V
, the group completion of
\mathsf{Gr}(V)
is the trivial group; see
Appendix

0.A
. In this case, the algebraic Möbius inverse of any map
m:P\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
is the trivial map
\partial_{P}(m):P\to\{0\}=\kappa(\mathsf{Gr}(V))
. This suggests considering a notion of Möbius inverse that does not involve group completion.

Definition 2.20

(Monoidal Möbius inverses)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a commutative monoid. Let
m:P\to\mathcal{M}
be a function, then a function
m^{\prime}:P\to\mathcal{M}
is called a
monoidal Möbius inverse
of
m
if it satisfies

\sum_{p^{\prime}\leq p}m^{\prime}(p^{\prime})=m(p)

for all
p\in P
. We denote by
\partial_{P}^{\mathsf{Mon}}\left(m\right)
the set of all monoidal Möbius inverses of
m
.

Notice that if
\mathcal{G}
is an abelian group and
g:P\to\mathcal{G}
is any function, then the algebraic Möbius inverse of
g
is a monoidal Möbius inverse of
g
. Indeed, the algebraic Möbius inverse of
g
is the unique monoidal Möbius inverse of
g
in this case. However, if
\mathcal{M}
is a commutative monoid that is not an abelian group and
m:P\to\mathcal{M}
is a function, then the algebraic Möbius inverse of
m
and a monoidal Möbius inverse of
m
have different codomains as functions.

While the algebraic Möbius inverse of
m
is always guaranteed to exist when
P
is finite, a monoidal Möbius inverse of
m
might not exist even in this case. Moreover, in the case when both inverses exist, there might be more than one monoidal Möbius inverse of
m
whereas the algebraic Möbius inverse is necessarily unique.

Example 2.21

(Non-existence)
.

Let
P=\{1<2<\cdots<n\}
be the linear poset on
n
elements. Any function
m:P\to\mathbb{N}
has an algebraic Möbius inverse given by

\displaystyle\partial_{P}(m):P

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\mapsto m(1)

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle\mapsto m(i)-m(i-1)\text{ for }i>1.

However,
m:P\to\mathbb{N}
has a monoidal Möbius inverse if and only if
m
is non-decreasing.

Example 2.22

(Non-uniqueness)
.

Let
P=\{1<2\}
and let
\mathcal{M}=\mathsf{Gr}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Let
e_{1},e_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote the standard basis elements of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Consider the function
m:\{1<2\}\to\mathsf{Gr}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
given by
m(1)=\spn\{e_{1}\}
,
m(2)=\spn\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
. For any angle
\theta\in(0,\pi)
consider the function
\ell_{\theta}:\{1<2\}\to\mathsf{Gr}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
defined by
\ell_{\theta}(1)=\spn\{e_{1}\}
and
\ell_{\theta}(2)=\spn\{\cos(\theta)e_{1}+\sin(\theta)e_{2}\}
, see
Fig.

5
for a visual illustration. Then, for any
\theta\in(0,\pi)
,
\ell_{\theta}
is a monoidal Möbius inverse of
m
as it holds that

\sum_{p^{\prime}\leq p}\ell_{\theta}(p^{\prime})=m(p)

for all
p\in\{1<2\}
.

Figure 5:
A function
m:\{1<2\}\to\mathsf{Gr}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and a one-parameter family of monoidal Möbius inverses for it,
\ell_{\theta}:\{1<2\}\to\mathsf{Gr}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for

The fact that the monoidal Möbius inverse may not be unique, as demonstrated in
Example

2.22
, motivates the following definition, which introduces an equivalence relation linking all functions serving as Möbius inverses of the same function.

Definition 2.23

(Möbius equivalence)
.

Two functions
m_{1},m_{2}:P\to\mathcal{M}
are said to be
Möbius equivalent
if

\sum_{p^{\prime}\leq p}m_{1}(p^{\prime})=\sum_{p^{\prime}\leq p}m_{2}(p^{\prime})

for all
p\in P
. In this case, we write
m_{1}\simeq_{\mathsf{M\ddot{o}b}}m_{2}
.

2.6
A Monoidal Rota’s Galois Connection Theorem

Rota’s Galois Connection Theorem (RGCT)
[
GM22
]
describes how algebraic
Möbius inversion behaves when a Galois connection exists between two posets. The functoriality of algebraic Möbius inversion
[
MP22
,
GM22
]
is indeed a direct consequence of the RGCT.
Now, we introduce a monoidal analog of the RGCT. The Monoidal RGCT will allow us to establish the functoriality of our construction in
Section

3
.

Theorem 2

(Monoidal RGCT)
.

Let
P
and
Q
be finite posets,
f_{\diamond}:P\leftrightarrows Q:f^{\diamond}
be a Galois connection, and
m:P\to\mathcal{M}
be any function. Assume that
m^{\prime}:P\to\mathcal{M}
is a monoidal Möbius inverse of
m
. Then,
(f_{\diamond})_{\sharp}(m^{\prime})
is a monoidal Möbius inverse of
(f^{\diamond})^{\sharp}m
, i.e.,
(f_{\diamond})_{\sharp}(m^{\prime})\in\partial_{Q}^{\mathsf{Mon}}\left((f^{\diamond})^{\sharp}m\right)
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that the following equality

\sum_{q^{\prime}\leq q}(f_{\diamond})_{\sharp}\left(m^{\prime}\right)(q^{\prime})=(f^{\diamond})^{\sharp}m(q)

holds for every
q\in Q
. Let
q\in Q
be any element, starting from the left-hand side, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{q^{\prime}\leq q}(f_{\diamond})_{\sharp}\left(m^{\prime}\right)(q^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{q^{\prime}\leq q}\sum_{p\in f_{\diamond}^{-1}(q^{\prime})}m^{\prime}(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P\\
f_{\diamond}(p)\leq q\end{subarray}}m^{\prime}(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P\\
p\leq f^{\diamond}(q)\end{subarray}}m^{\prime}(p)

\displaystyle=m(f^{\diamond}(q))

\displaystyle=(f^{\diamond})^{\sharp}m(q).

∎

(f_{\diamond})_{\sharp}\left(\partial_{P}^{\mathsf{Mon}}\left(m\right)\right)\subseteq\partial_{Q}^{\mathsf{Mon}}\left((f^{\diamond})^{\sharp}m\right).

(6)

Note that the inclusion in
Eq.

6

Orthogonal Inversion and Orthomodular Inversion

In this section, we introduce the key construction used throughout the paper: Orthogonal Inversion. We also extend this concept by introducing a generalization, which we call Orthomodular Inversion, that applies to order-preserving functions from a finite poset to an orthomodular lattice. The results regarding Orthogonal Inversion are presented in
Section

3.1
, while the discussion of Orthomodular Inversion is covered in
Section

3.2
.

3.1
Orthogonal Inversion

In this section, we introduce the notion of
Orthogonal Inversion
. This concept applies to order-preserving functions from a finite poset to a Grassmannian. We utilize Orthogonal Inversion later in
Section

4
by exploring the applications of this notion to persistence over finite posets, i.e., the case when the underlying poset
P
of a filtration
\mathsf{F}:P\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
can be any finite poset.

Definition 3.1

(Monotone space function)
.

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional inner product space and
R
be any finite poset. An order-preserving function
\mathfrak{F}:R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
is called a
monotone space function
.

Definition 3.2

(Difference of subspaces)
.

Let
V
be an inner product space and let
W_{1},W_{2}\subseteq V
be subspaces. We define the
difference
of two subspaces as

W_{1}\ominus W_{2}:=W_{1}\cap W_{2}^{\perp}.

Remark 3.3
.

In the definition above, if
W_{2}\subseteq W_{1}
, then
W_{1}\ominus W_{2}=W_{1}\cap W_{2}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement of
W_{2}
inside of
W_{1}
. In this case,
\dim(W_{1}\ominus W_{2})=\dim W_{1}-\dim W_{2}
.
In general (i.e. when
W_{2}\nsubseteq W_{1}
), we have that

W_{1}\ominus W_{2}=W_{1}\ominus\proj_{W_{1}}(W_{2}),

where
\proj_{W_{1}}:V\to W_{1}
is the orthogonal projection. This can be informally interpreted as expressing that
\proj_{W_{1}}(W_{2})
and
W_{2}
are treated as being “quasi-isomorphic” with respect to
W_{1}
.

Definition 3.4

(Orthogonal Inversion)
.

Let
R
be a finite poset and let
\mathfrak{F}:R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
be a monotone space function. We define the
Orthogonal Inverse of
\mathfrak{F}
to be the function
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F}):R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
given by

\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r):=\mathfrak{F}(r)\ominus\left(\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathfrak{F}(r^{\prime})\right).

Remark 3.5

(
\mathsf{OI}
compresses information)
.

For a monotone space function
\mathfrak{F}
, its Orthogonal Inverse
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
has a smaller footprint than
\mathfrak{F}
. To be precise, let
\mathfrak{F}:R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
be a monotone space function. As
\mathfrak{F}(r^{\prime})\subseteq\mathfrak{F}(r)
for every
r^{\prime}<r\in R
, we have

\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathfrak{F}(r^{\prime})\subseteq\mathfrak{F}(r).

Thus,

\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r)=\mathfrak{F}(r)\ominus\left(\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathfrak{F}(r^{\prime})\right)

is the orthogonal complement of
\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathfrak{F}(r^{\prime})
inside
\mathfrak{F}(r)
. In particular,
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r)\subseteq\mathfrak{F}(r)
for every
r\in R
. This can be expressed as: “
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
has a smaller support than
\mathfrak{F}
”. In other words, storing
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
requires no more memory than storing
\mathfrak{F}
. Indeed, it always holds that

\sum_{r\in R}\dim\left(\mathsf{OI}\left(\mathfrak{F}\right)\right)\leq\sum_{r\in R}\dim\left(\mathfrak{F}\right)

and the equality seemingly takes place only in pathological cases such as when the underlying poset is an antichain or when
\mathfrak{F}
is identically zero.

In the following proposition, we show that the Orthogonal Inverse of a monotone space function produces a monoidal Möbius inverse.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
R
be a finite poset and let
\mathfrak{F}:R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
be a monotone space function. Then,
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
is a monoidal Möbius inverse of
\mathfrak{F}
, i.e.,
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})\in\partial_{R}^{\mathsf{Mon}}\left(\mathfrak{F}\right)
. That is,

\sum_{r^{\prime}\leq r}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})=\mathfrak{F}(r)

for every
r\in R
.

Proof.

Let
R
be a finite poset and let
\mathfrak{F}:R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
be a monotone space function.
We will proceed by strong induction on the poset
R
.

Base cases:
Let
0_{R}
be a minimal element of
R
(note that there could be more than one minimal element of
R
). By
Definition

3.4
,
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})\left(0_{R}\right)=\mathfrak{F}\left(0_{R}\right)
. As
0_{R}
is a minimal element of
R
, it follows that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}\in R\\
r^{\prime}\leq R\end{subarray}}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})=\mathfrak{F}(r).

Thus, we have the result for the base case.

Inductive step:
Let
r\in R
be any element and assume that for every
s<r
it holds that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s^{\prime}\in R\\
s^{\prime}\leq s\end{subarray}}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(s^{\prime})=\mathfrak{F}(s).

Then, due to the fact that
A+B=A+B+B
for any
A,B\in\mathsf{Gr}(V)
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}\in R\\
r^{\prime}\leq r\end{subarray}}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}\in R\\
r^{\prime}<r\end{subarray}}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in R\\
s<r\end{subarray}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\leq s}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in R\\
s<r\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}(s)

By
Definition

3.4
, we have

\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r)=\mathfrak{F}(r)\ominus\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in R\\
s<r\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}(s)

Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}\in R\\
r^{\prime}\leq r\end{subarray}}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in R\\
s<r\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}(s)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathfrak{F}(r)\ominus\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in R\\
s<r\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}(s)\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in R\\
s<r\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{F}(r)

Thus,
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
is a monoidal Möbius inverse of
\mathfrak{F}
.
∎

The proposition above implies that any monotone space function
\mathfrak{F}
can be reconstructed from its Orthogonal Inverse,
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
. Additionally, considering that
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
has a smaller support than
\mathfrak{F}
, as detailed in
Remark

3.5
, one can interpret
\mathsf{OI}
as serving as a form of lossless compression for monotone space functions.

,

,

, and

As demonstrated in the example above, the Orthogonal Inverse of a monotone space function may not necessarily yield a transversal family. Nevertheless, the Orthogonal Inverse of monotone space functions still adheres to a weaker notion of transversality, which we will now describe in the following.

Definition 3.8

(Downward transversal functions)
.

A function
\mathfrak{M}:R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
is called a
downward transversal function
if the family

\left\{\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathfrak{M}(r^{\prime}),\mathfrak{M}(r)\right\}

is a transversal family for every
r\in R
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{F}:R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
be a monotone space function. Then,
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
is a downward transversal function.

Proof.

Let
r\in R
be any element. By
Definition

3.4
, we have that
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r)=\mathfrak{F}(r)\ominus\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathfrak{F}(r^{\prime})
. Due to the fact that
A+B=A+B+B
for any
A,B\in\mathsf{Gr}(V)
and by
Proposition

3.6
, it follows that

\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})=\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\sum_{s\leq r^{\prime}}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(s)=\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathfrak{F}(r^{\prime}).

Therefore, we obtain that

\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r)=\mathfrak{F}(r)\ominus\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathfrak{F}(r^{\prime})=\mathfrak{F}(r)\ominus\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime}).

Hence, the subspaces
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r)
and
\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})
are orthogonal to each other. Thus, the family

\left\{\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r),\sum_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})\right\}

is a transversal family. So, we conclude that
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
is a downward transversal function.
∎

We now proceed by organizing the collection of monotone space functions into a category, and similarly, we organize the collection of downward transversal functions into another category. We then establish that the Orthogonal Inversion operates as a functor between these categories. As a result of this functoriality, we also obtain edit distance stability.

Definition 3.10

(Category of monotone space functions)
.

We define
\mathsf{Mon}(V)
to be the category where

•

Objects are monotone space functions defined over any finite metric poset,

•

A morphism from a monotone space function
\mathfrak{F}:R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
to another monotone space function
\mathfrak{G}:Q\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
is given by a Galois connection
f_{\diamond}:R\leftrightarrows Q:f^{\diamond}
such that
\mathfrak{F}\circ f^{\diamond}=\mathfrak{G}
.

Definition 3.11

(Cost of a morphism in
\mathsf{Mon}(V)
)
.

The cost of a morphism in
\mathsf{Mon}(V)
is given by
\mathrm{dis}(f_{\diamond})
, the distortion of the left adjoint.

Definition 3.12

(Category of downward transversal functions)
.

We define
\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)
to be the category where

•

Objects are downward transversal functions defined over any finite metric poset,

•

A morphism from an object
\mathfrak{M}:R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
to another object
\mathfrak{N}:Q\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
is given by a Galois connection
f_{\diamond}:R\leftrightarrows Q:f^{\diamond}
such that
\left(\overline{f_{\diamond}}\right)_{\sharp}\left(\mathfrak{M}\right)\simeq_{\mathsf{M\ddot{o}b}}\mathfrak{N}
.

Definition 3.13

(Cost of a morphism in
\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)
)
.

The cost of a morphism in
\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)
is given by
\mathrm{dis}(f_{\diamond})
, the distortion of the left adjoint.

As a result of the fact that
\mathsf{OI}
produces a monoidal Möbius inverse,
Proposition

3.6
, and by the monoidal RGCT,
Theorem

2
, we obtain the following.

Theorem 3

(Functoriality and stability of
\mathsf{OI}
)
.

The assignment

\displaystyle\mathsf{OI}:\mathsf{Mon}(V)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})

is a functor. Moreover, for any two objects
\mathfrak{F}
and
\mathfrak{G}
in
\mathsf{Mon}(V)
, we have

d_{\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)}^{E}(\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F}),\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{G}))\leq d_{\mathsf{Mon}(V)}^{E}(\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G}).

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{F}:R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
be an object in
\mathsf{Mon}(V)
. By
Proposition

3.9
, we have that
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
is an object in
\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)
. Now, let
\mathfrak{G}:S\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
be another object in
\mathsf{Mon}(V)
and let
f_{\diamond}:R\leftrightarrows S:f^{\diamond}
a Galois connection that determines a morphism from
\mathfrak{F}
to
\mathfrak{G}
. This means that
\left(\overline{f^{\diamond}}\right)^{\sharp}\mathfrak{F}=\mathfrak{F}\circ\overline{f^{\diamond}}=\mathfrak{G}
. By
Proposition

3.6
, we have that
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
and
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{G})
are monoidal Möbius inverses of
\mathfrak{F}
and
\mathfrak{G}
respectively. Then, by the monoidal RGCT,
Theorem

2
, we have that

\left(\overline{f_{\diamond}}\right)_{\sharp}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})\simeq_{\mathsf{M\ddot{o}b}}\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{G}).

Therefore, the Galois connection
(f_{\diamond},f^{\diamond})
is a morphism from
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
to
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{G})
. Thus
\mathsf{OI}
is a functor from
\mathsf{Mon}(V)
to
\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)
. The edit distance stability

d_{\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)}^{E}(\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F}),\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{G}))\leq d_{\mathsf{Mon}(V)}^{E}(\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G})

follows from the fact that
\mathsf{OI}
is a functor from
\mathsf{Mon}(V)
to
\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)
.
∎

In the following, we prove that we not only achieve stability but also obtain isometry.

Theorem 4

(Isometry)
.

For any two objects
\mathfrak{F}
and
\mathfrak{G}
in
\mathsf{Mon}(V)
, we have

d_{\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)}^{E}(\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F}),\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{G}))=d_{\mathsf{Mon}(V)}^{E}(\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G}).

Proof.

We have the inequality

d_{\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)}^{E}(\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F}),\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{G}))\leq d_{\mathsf{Mon}(V)}^{E}(\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G})

by
Theorem

3
. Thus, it suffices to prove the following inequality

d_{\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)}^{E}(\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F}),\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{G}))\geq d_{\mathsf{Mon}(V)}^{E}(\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G}).

We prove the above inequality by constructing a functor
\mathsf{LowerSum}:\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)\to\mathsf{Mon}(V)
that repects the cost of morphisms. For any
\mathfrak{M}:R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
downward transversal function in
\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)
, let

\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathfrak{M})(r):=\sum_{r^{\prime}\leq r}\mathfrak{M}(r^{\prime}).

For any
r^{\prime}\leq r\in R
,
\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathfrak{M})(r^{\prime})\subseteq\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathfrak{M})(r)
. Thus,
\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathfrak{M}):R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
is an order-preserving space function, i.e.,
\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathfrak{M})
is in
\mathsf{Mon}(V)
. To see that
\mathsf{LowerSum}
is a functor, let
\mathfrak{M}:R\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
and
\mathfrak{N}:S\to\mathsf{Gr}(V)
be two downward transversal functions and let
f_{\diamond}:R\leftrightarrows S:f^{\diamond}
be a Galois connection that determines a morphism from
\mathfrak{M}
to
\mathfrak{N}
. This means that

\left(\overline{f_{\diamond}}\right)_{\sharp}\left(\mathfrak{M}\right)\simeq_{\mathsf{M\ddot{o}b}}\mathfrak{N}.

That is, for any
s\in S
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}\leq s}\mathfrak{N}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}\leq s}\left(\overline{f_{\diamond}}\right)_{\sharp}\left(\mathfrak{M}\right)(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}\leq s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R\\
f_{\diamond}(r)=s^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{M}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R\\
f_{\diamond}(r)\leq s\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{M}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R\\
r\leq f^{\diamond}(s)\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{M}(r).

Thus, we obtained that

\displaystyle\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathfrak{N})(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}\leq s}\mathfrak{N}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R\\
r\leq f^{\diamond}(s)\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{M}(r)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathfrak{M})(f^{\diamond}(s)).

That is,
\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathfrak{N})=\left(f^{\diamond}\right)^{\sharp}(\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathfrak{M}))
, i.e., the Galois connection
f_{\diamond}:R\leftrightarrows S:f^{\diamond}
is a morphism from
\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathfrak{M})
to
\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathfrak{N})
. Therefore,
\mathsf{LowerSum}:\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)\to\mathsf{Mon}(V)
is a functor.

Now, let
\mathfrak{F}
and
\mathfrak{G}
be two order-preserving space functions. Obserbe that, by
Proposition

3.6
, we have that
\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F}))=\mathfrak{F}
and
\mathsf{LowerSum}(\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{G}))=\mathfrak{G}
. Therefore, a path between
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F})
and
\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{G})
in
\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)
induces a path between
\mathfrak{F}
and
\mathfrak{G}
in
\mathsf{Mon}(V)
with the same cost. Thus, we obtain

d_{\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}(V)}^{E}(\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{F}),\mathsf{OI}(\mathfrak{G}))\geq d_{\mathsf{Mon}(V)}^{E}(\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G}).

∎

3.2
Orthomodular Inversion

In this section, we replace Grassmannians with more general objects:
orthomodular lattices
; see
Definition

3.14
. We prove that order-preserving functions from a finite poset to an orthomodular lattice can be Möbius inverted through a construction that generalizes Orthogonal Inversion.

A tuple
(L,\leq,\vee,\wedge)
is called a
lattice
if
(L,\leq)
is a poset and every two elements
a,b\in L

has a least upper bound, denoted
a\vee b
, and a greatest lower bound, denoted
a\wedge b
. A tuple
(L,\leq,\vee,\wedge,0_{L},1_{L})
is called a
bounded lattice
if
L
has smallest and greatest elements, denoted
0_{L}
and
1_{L}
respectively.

Definition 3.14

(Orthomodular lattices
[
Bir40
, page 53]
)
.

A tuple
(L,\wedge,\vee,0_{L},1_{L},(\cdot)^{\perp})
is called an
orthomodular lattice
if
(L,\wedge,\vee,0_{L},1_{L},)
is a bounded lattice and
(\cdot)^{\perp}:L\to L
is function, called
orthocomplementation
, such that the following conditions hold for all
a,b\in L
:

•

If
a\leq b
, then
b^{\perp}\leq a^{\perp}
,

•

a\vee a^{\perp}=1_{L}
,
a\wedge a^{\perp}=0_{L}
,
(a^{\perp})^{\perp}=a
,

•

If
a\leq b
, then
b=a\vee(a^{\perp}\wedge b)
.

Observe that the Grassmannian of a finite-dimensional inner product space
V
,
\mathsf{Gr}(V)
, is an orthomodular lattice where:

–

\vee
is taken as the sum of subspaces,

–

\wedge
is taken as the intersection of subspaces,

–

0_{\mathsf{Gr}(V)}=\{0\}
,

–

1_{\mathsf{Gr}(V)}=V
, and

–

(\cdot)^{\perp}
is taken as the orthogonal complement operation.

Remark 3.15
.

Note that the two structures on Grassmannians that we have been utilizing in this paper, the monoid structure (e.g.
Proposition

3.6
) and the poset structure (e.g.
Definition

3.1
), are unified when a Grassmannian is viewed as an orthomodular lattice. The notion of orthomodular lattice was first studied by Birkhoff and Von Neumann in
[
BN36
, Section 11]
to formalize a logic system for quantum mechanics. Building upon this rich mathematical landscape, our paper extends the application of orthomodular lattices to the theory of persistence.

We now describe a notion of Möbius inversion for lattice-valued functions. The following definition closely resembles the classical/monoidal Möbius inversion, with the sole modification being the replacement of the group (or monoid) operation “
+
” with the join operation “
\vee
” of the lattice.

Definition 3.16

(Join Möbius inversion)
.

Let
R
be poset and
L
be a lattice, and
\mathfrak{F}:R\to L
be an order-preserving function. A function
\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}:R\to L
is called a
join Möbius inverse
of
\mathfrak{F}
if it satisfies

\mathfrak{F}(r)=\bigvee_{r^{\prime}\leq r}\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(r^{\prime})

for every
r\in R
.

The following proposition establishes that the Rota’s Galois Connection Theorem still holds for functions valued in lattices.

Proposition 3.17

(Join RGCT)
.

Let
R
and
Q
be finite posets and
f_{\diamond}:R\leftrightarrows Q:f^{\diamond}
be a Galois connection. Let
L
be a lattice,
\mathfrak{F}:R\to L
be an order-preserving map, and
\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}:R\to L
be a join Möbius inverse of
\mathfrak{F}
. Then, the function

\displaystyle\left(f_{\diamond}\right)_{\sharp}(\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}):Q

\displaystyle\to L

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R\\
f_{\diamond}(r)=q\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(r)

is a join Möbius inverse of
\left(f^{\diamond}\right)^{\sharp}(\mathfrak{F})=\mathfrak{F}\circ f^{\diamond}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the following equality

\bigvee_{q^{\prime}\leq q}(f_{\diamond})_{\sharp}(\mathfrak{F}^{\prime})(q^{\prime})=\mathfrak{F}\left((f^{\diamond}(q)\right)

for every
q\in Q
. Let
q\in Q
be any element, then, we have

\displaystyle\bigvee_{q^{\prime}\leq q}(f_{\diamond})_{\sharp}(\mathfrak{F}^{\prime})(q^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{q^{\prime}\leq q}\bigvee_{r\in f_{\diamond}^{-1}(q^{\prime})}\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R\\
f_{\diamond}(r)\leq q\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R\\
r\leq f^{\diamond}(q)\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{F}(f^{\diamond}(q)).

∎

For order-preserving functions valued in an orthomodular lattice, it is always possible to obtain a join Möbius inverse. We will now describe this procedure and prove that it indeed yields a join Möbius inverse.

Definition 3.18

(Orthomodular Inversion)
.

Let
R
be a finite poset and
L
be an orthomodular lattice. Let
\mathfrak{F}:R\to L
be an order-preserving function. We define the
Orthomodular Inverse
of
\mathfrak{F}
to be the function defined by

\displaystyle\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F}):R

\displaystyle\to L

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathfrak{F}(r)\wedge\left(\bigvee_{r^{\prime}<r}\mathfrak{F}(r^{\prime})\right)^{\perp}.

Proposition 3.19

(Orthomodular Inversion is a join Möbius inversion)
.

Let
R
be a finite poset and
L
be an orthomodular lattice. Let
\mathfrak{F}:R\to L
be an order-preserving function. Then, the Orthomodular Inverse of
\mathfrak{F}
,
\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F}):R\to L
, is a join Möbius inverse of
\mathfrak{F}
.

Proof.

We will proceed by strong induction on the poset
R
.

Base cases:
Let
0_{R}
be a minimal element of
R
(note that there could be more than one minimal element of
R
). By
Definition

3.18
,
\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F})\left(0_{R}\right)=\mathfrak{F}\left(0_{R}\right)
. As
0_{R}
is a minimal element of
R
, it follows that

\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}\in R\\
r^{\prime}\leq r\end{subarray}}\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})=\mathfrak{F}(r).

Thus, we have the result for the base case.

Inductive step:
Let
r\in R
be any element and assume that for every
s<r
it holds that

\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}s^{\prime}\in R\\
s^{\prime}\leq s\end{subarray}}\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F})(s^{\prime})=\mathfrak{F}(s).

Then, due to the fact that
a\vee b=a\vee b\vee b
for any
a,b\in L
, it follows that

\displaystyle\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}\in R\\
r^{\prime}\leq r\end{subarray}}\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F})(r)\vee\left(\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}\in R\\
r^{\prime}<r\end{subarray}}\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F})(r)\vee\left(\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in R\\
s<r\end{subarray}}\bigvee_{s^{\prime}\leq s}\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F})(s^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F})(r)\vee\left(\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in R\\
s<r\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}(s)\right).

By
Definition

3.18
, we have

\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F})(r)=\mathfrak{F}(r)\wedge\left(\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in R\\
s<r\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}(s)\right)^{\perp}

Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}\in R\\
r^{\prime}\leq r\end{subarray}}\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F})(r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\partial^{\perp}_{R}(\mathfrak{F})(r)\vee\left(\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in R\\
s<r\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}(s)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathfrak{F}(r)\wedge\left(\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in R\\
s<r\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}(s)\right)^{\perp}\right)\vee\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in R\\
s<r\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{F}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{F}(r)

Thus,

is a join Möbius inverse of

.

In this section, we apply our Orthogonal Inversion framework from

Let

Remark 4.3

(Computational complexity for
2

.

In
[

, Appendix C]
, we describe an algorithm for computing Grassmannian persistence diagrams in the one-parameter setting and provided an analysis of its time complexity. The algorithm operates in a brute-force manner by computing the Grassmannian persistence diagram for all segments. This algorithm, together with its complexity analysis, can be naturally extended to
2
-parameter (and even
n
-parameter) filtrations which we illustrate below.

Specifically, let

\mathsf{F}\colon P:=\{1<2<\cdots<m\}\times\{1<2<\cdots<m\}\;\to\;\mathsf{SubCx}(K)

be a
2

\mathsf{OI}\bigl(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}\bigr)
for each of the
O(m^{4})

\displaystyle\mathsf{OI}\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}\right)((i,j),(k,l))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((i,j),(k,l))\ominus\left(\sum_{I<_{\times}((i,j),(k,l))}\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}(I)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((i,j),(k,l))

\displaystyle\ominus\left(\begin{multlined}\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((i-1,j),(k,l))+\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((i,j-1),(k,l))\\
+\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((i,j),(k-1,l))+\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((i,j),(k,l-1))\end{multlined}\right).

This computation boils down to computing bases for subspaces obtained through intersection, sum as well as the

operation. All these linear algebra operations have been analyzed in
[

(see

O\left(n_{\rho}^{K}\cdot n_{\rho-1}^{K}\cdot\min\left(n_{\rho}^{K},n_{\rho-1}^{K}\right)+n_{\rho+1}^{K}\cdot n_{\rho}^{K}\cdot\min\left(n_{\rho+1}^{K},n_{\rho}^{K}\right)+\left(n_{\rho}^{K}\right)^{3}\right),

where, for each degree
\rho,

denotes the number of
\rho

Hence, the total time complexity of the algorithm described above for computing the degree-

is

O\!\Bigl(m^{4}\,\Bigl(n_{\rho}^{K}\,n_{\rho-1}^{K}\,\min\bigl(n_{\rho}^{K},\,n_{\rho-1}^{K}\bigr)\;+\;n_{\rho+1}^{K}\,n_{\rho}^{K}\,\min\bigl(n_{\rho+1}^{K},\,n_{\rho}^{K}\bigr)\;+\;\bigl(n_{\rho}^{K}\bigr)^{3}\Bigr)\Bigr).

(7)

Treating the quantities

In the remainder of this section, we demonstrate that the Grassmannian persistence diagram of a filtration over a general poset (1) offers a clear and interpretable description of the evolution of topological features; (2) is stable with respect to a specific edit distance, which we define below; and (3) subsumes and strictly strengthens the information captured by birth-death induced signed persistence diagrams.

4.1
Interpretability of Grassmannian Persistence Diagrams: Canonical Cycles and Homological Exhaustiveness

As observed in

, for any segment

are born exactly at
b
and die exactly at
d
. We now show that this is not a coincendence, but rather a fact.

Theorem 5

(
\mathsf{OI}\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}\right)
produces fully supported canonical cycles)
.

Let
\mathsf{F}:P\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
be a filtration. Let
(b,d)\in\mathsf{Seg}(P)
and let

be a nonzero cycle. Then,

is born precisely at
b
and dies precisely at

(see

Proof.

Let

be a nonzero cycle. By

, we have that

\displaystyle\mathsf{OI}\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}\right)((b,d))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))\ominus W_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))\cap\left(W_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))\right)^{\perp}.

As
z
is nonzero, we conclude that
z\notin W_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((b,d))=\sum_{(a,c)<_{\times}(b,d)}\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}((a,c))
and

. Therefore,
z
is born at
b
and dies at
d
.
∎

4.1.1
Canonicality

Note that, according to

In other words, the following diagram commutes:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\xi(F)}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{\rho}^{K}}

4.2
Stability of Grassmannian Persistence Diagrams

In this section, we establish the (edit distance) stability of Grassmannian persistence diagrams. We now define the category of filtrations over general posets, which is inspired by and extends the construction in
[
MP22
, Definition 4.7]
.

Definition 4.15

(Category of filtrations over general posets)
.

We define
\mathsf{MP\textnormal{-}Fil}(K)
to be the category where

•

Objects are filtrations
\mathsf{F}:P\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
where
P
is any finite metric poset,

•

A morphism from an object
\mathsf{F}:P\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
to another object
\mathsf{G}:Q\to\mathsf{SubCx}(K)
is given by a Galois connection
f_{\diamond}:P\leftrightarrows S:f^{\diamond}
such that
\mathsf{G}=\mathsf{F}\circ f^{\diamond}
.

Definition 4.16

(Cost of a morphim in
\mathsf{MP\textnormal{-}Fil}(K)
)
.

The cost of a morphism
(f_{\diamond},f^{\diamond})
in
\mathsf{MP\textnormal{-}Fil}(K)
is given by
\mathrm{dis}(f_{\diamond})
, the distortion of the left adjoint.

Recall the notion of edit distance from
Definition

2.3
.

Theorem 7

(Stability)
.

Let
\mathsf{F}
and
\mathsf{G}
be two filtrations in
\mathsf{MP\textnormal{-}Fil}(K)
. Then, for any degree
\rho\geq 0
, we have

d_{\mathsf{DT\textnormal{-}Fnc}\left(C_{\rho}^{K}\right)}^{E}\left(\mathsf{OI}\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}\right),\mathsf{OI}\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{G}}\right)\right)\leq d_{\mathsf{MP\textnormal{-}Fil}(K)}^{E}(\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G}).

Proof.

Observe that the assignment

\mathsf{F}\mapsto\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}}

is a functor from
\mathsf{MP\textnormal{-}Fil}(K)
to
\mathsf{Mon}\left(C_{\rho}^{K}\right)
for every degree
\rho\geq 0
. As a result of this functoriality, we obtain

d_{\mathsf{Mon}\left(C_{\rho}^{K}\right)}^{E}\left(\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{F}},\mathsf{ZB}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{G}}\right)\leq d_{\mathsf{MP\textnormal{-}Fil}(K)}^{E}(\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G}).

Thus, combining the above inequality with the one in
Theorem

6
Discussion

We introduced the concept of Orthogonal Inversion and demonstrated its utility in deriving easily interpretable persistence diagrams for multiparameter filtrations. Orthogonal Inversion critically relies on the inner product structure on
V
enabling it to capture significantly finer information than integer-valued persistence diagrams. We believe that, as a consequence, Grassmannian persistence diagrams have the potential to contribute to a range of practical applications and data analysis tasks.
In this section, we outline several key considerations that may motivate further research in this area.

Stability.

When comparing two filtrations and their respective Grassmannian persistence diagrams (
Section

4.2
), we are required that there exists a fixed simplicial complex
K
that each filtration is defined over. It is natural to seek a framework that can handle filtrations over different vertex sets.

Orthomodular lattices.

While the motivation behind the concept of orthogonal inversions primarily stems from its applications in TDA (as in
Section

4
), there is an inherent interest in broadening the utility of orthogonal inversions beyond the scope of TDA by potentially utilizing the notion of Orthomodular Inversion developed in
Section

3.2
.

Functoriality.

We have extended the functoriality of classical Möbius inversion (see
[
MP22
, Proposition 6.6]
and
[
GM22
, Proposition 5.9]
) by demonstrating that assigning a monoidal Möbius inverse to a monotone-space function via Orthogonal Inversion retains functoriality; see
Theorem

3
. However, since monoidal Möbius inverses are not necessarily unique, a natural question arises: is the process of assigning the set of all monoidal Möbius inverses of a given function itself functorial?

Persistent Möbius Homology.

We have shown that the stratum-
0
persistent Möbius homology of a filtration produces spaces that are isomorphic to those determined by the Grassmannian persistence diagram of the filtration. A natural next step is to explore the potential construction of higher order Grassmannian persistence diagrams that could be related to persistent Möbius homology of the filtration in strata 1 and above.
