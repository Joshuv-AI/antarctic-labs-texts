Evaluating the security of 𝖢𝖱𝖸𝖲𝖳𝖠𝖫𝖲 ​ - ​ 𝖣𝗂𝗅𝗂𝗍𝗁𝗂𝗎𝗆 \mathsf{CRYSTALS}\raisebox{1.0pt}{-}\mathsf{Dilithium} in the quantum random oracle model
Kelsey A. Jackson, Carl A. Miller, Daochen Wang

Abstract
In the wake of recent progress on quantum computing hardware, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) is standardizing cryptographic protocols that are resistant to attacks by quantum adversaries. The primary digital signature scheme that NIST has chosen is 𝖢𝖱𝖸𝖲𝖳𝖠𝖫𝖲 ​ - ​ 𝖣𝗂𝗅𝗂𝗍𝗁𝗂𝗎𝗆 \mathsf{CRYSTALS}\raisebox{1.0pt}{-}\mathsf{Dilithium} . The hardness of this scheme is based on the hardness of three computational problems: Module Learning with Errors ( 𝖬𝖫𝖶𝖤 \mathsf{MLWE} ), Module Short Integer Solution ( 𝖬𝖲𝖨𝖲 \mathsf{MSIS} ), and 𝖲𝖾𝗅𝖿𝖳𝖺𝗋𝗀𝖾𝗍𝖬𝖲𝖨𝖲 \mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS} . 𝖬𝖫𝖶𝖤 \mathsf{MLWE} and 𝖬𝖲𝖨𝖲 \mathsf{MSIS} have been well-studied and are widely believed to be secure. However, 𝖲𝖾𝗅𝖿𝖳𝖺𝗋𝗀𝖾𝗍𝖬𝖲𝖨𝖲 \mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS} is novel and, though classically as hard as 𝖬𝖲𝖨𝖲 \mathsf{MSIS} , its quantum hardness is unclear. In this paper, we provide the first proof of the hardness of 𝖲𝖾𝗅𝖿𝖳𝖺𝗋𝗀𝖾𝗍𝖬𝖲𝖨𝖲 \mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS} via a reduction from 𝖬𝖫𝖶𝖤 \mathsf{MLWE} in the Quantum Random Oracle Model (QROM). Our proof uses recently developed techniques in quantum reprogramming and rewinding. A central part of our approach is a proof that a certain hash function, derived from the 𝖬𝖲𝖨𝖲 \mathsf{MSIS} problem, is collapsing. From this approach, we deduce a new security proof for 𝖣𝗂𝗅𝗂𝗍𝗁𝗂𝗎𝗆 \mathsf{Dilithium} under appropriate parameter settings. Compared to the previous work by Kiltz, Lyubashevsky, and Schaffner (EUROCRYPT 2018) that gave the only other rigorous security proof for a variant of 𝖣𝗂𝗅𝗂𝗍𝗁𝗂𝗎𝗆 \mathsf{Dilithium} , our proof has the advantage of being applicable under the condition q = 1 ​ mod ​ 2 ​ n q=1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2n , where q q denotes the modulus and n n the dimension of the underlying algebraic ring. This condition is part of the original 𝖣𝗂𝗅𝗂𝗍𝗁𝗂𝗎𝗆 \mathsf{Dilithium} proposal and is crucial for the efficient implementation of the scheme. We provide new secure parameter sets for 𝖣𝗂𝗅𝗂𝗍𝗁𝗂𝗎𝗆 \mathsf{Dilithium} under the condition q = 1 ​ mod ​ 2 ​ n q=1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2n , finding that our public key size and signature size are about 2.9 × 2.9\times and 1.3 × 1.3\times larger, respectively, than those proposed by Kiltz et al. at the same security level.

Introduction

Quantum computers are theoretically capable of breaking the underlying computational hardness assumptions for many existing cryptographic schemes. Therefore, it is vitally important to develop new cryptographic primitives and protocols that are resistant to quantum attacks.

The goal of NIST’s Post-Quantum Cryptography Standardization Project is to design a new generation of cryptographic schemes that are secure against quantum adversaries. In 2022, NIST selected three new digital signature schemes for standardization
[
Ala+22
]
: Falcon, SPHINCS+, and
\mathsf{CRYSTALS}\raisebox{1.0pt}{-}\mathsf{Dilithium}
. Of the three,
\mathsf{CRYSTALS}\raisebox{1.0pt}{-}\mathsf{Dilithium}

[
Bai+21
]
, or
\mathsf{Dilithium}
in shorthand, was identified as the primary choice for post-quantum digital signing.

To practically implement post-quantum cryptography, users must be provided with not only assurance that a scheme is secure in a post-quantum setting, but also the means by which to judge parameter choices and thereby balance their own needs for security and efficiency. The goal of the current work is to provide rigorous assurance of the security of
\mathsf{Dilithium}
as well as implementable parameter sets. A common model for the security of digital signatures is existential unforgeability against chosen message attacks, or
\mathsf{EUF}\raisebox{0.0pt}{-}\mathsf{CMA}
. In this setting, an adversary is allowed to make sequential queries to a signing oracle for the signature scheme, and then afterwards the adversary attempts to forge a signature for a new message. We work in the setting of
strong
existential unforgeability (
\mathsf{sEUF}\raisebox{0.0pt}{-}\mathsf{CMA}
) wherein we must also guard against the possibility that an adversary could try to forge a new signature for one of the messages already signed by the oracle. (See
Section

2
for details.)

Additionally, we utilize the quantum random oracle model (QROM) for hash functions. We recall that when a hash function
H:X\rightarrow Y
is used as a subroutine in a digital signature scheme, the random oracle model (ROM) assumes that one can replace each instance of the function
H
with a black box that accepts inputs from
X
and returns outputs in
Y
according to a uniformly randomly chosen function from
X
to
Y
. (This model is useful because random functions are easier to work with in theory than actual hash functions.) The random oracle model needs to be refined in the quantum setting because queries to the hash function can be made in superposition: for any quantum state of the form
\sum_{x\in X}\alpha_{x}\left|x\right>
, where
\forall x\in X,\alpha_{x}\in\mathbb{C}
, a quantum computer can efficiently prepare the superposed state
\sum_{x\in X}\alpha_{x}\left|x\right>\left|H(x)\right>
. The quantum random oracle model (QROM) therefore assumes that each use of the hash function can be simulated by a black box that accepts a quantum state supported on
X
and returns a quantum state supported on
X\times Y
(computed by a truly random function from
X
to
Y
)
[
Bon+11
]
. While no efficient and truly random functions actually exist, the QROM is generally trusted and it enables the application of a number of useful proof techniques.

Subtracting these two solutions to
\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}
yields a solution to
\mathsf{MSIS}

However, the argument fails for the following reasons in the QROM (where a quantum algorithm can make queries in superposition to a quantum random oracle):

1.

The randomness in a quantum algorithm includes the randomness of measurement outcomes. We cannot run a quantum algorithm twice and guarantee that the “random bits” will be the same in both runs because we cannot control measurement outcomes. More generally, we cannot rewind a quantum algorithm to a post-measurement state.

2.

Since a quantum algorithm can make queries in superposition, it is no longer clear where to reprogram the random oracle.

1.3
Overview of main result

The main result of our paper is the first proof of the computational hardness of the
\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}
problem, presented in
Section

3
.
This hardness result implies a new security proof for
\mathsf{Dilithium}
which, unlike the previous proof in
[
KLS18
]
, applies
to the case
q=1\,\text{mod}\,2n
.
Specifically, we reduce
\mathsf{MLWE}
to
\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}
.
By
Eq.

4
, our result implies that the security of
\mathsf{Dilithium}
(with parameters that are not too far from the original parameters) can be based on the hardness of
\mathsf{MLWE}
and
\mathsf{MSIS}
.

Theorem 1

(Informal version of
Theorem

2
)
.

Let
m,k,\tau,\gamma,\eta\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
q\geq 16
,
q=1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2n
, and
2\gamma\eta n(m+k)<\lfloor q/32\rfloor
. If there exists an efficient quantum algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that solves
\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}_{H,\tau,m,k,\gamma}
with advantage
\epsilon
, under the assumption that
H
is a random oracle, then there exists an efficient quantum algorithm for solving
\mathsf{MLWE}_{m+k,m,\eta}
with advantage at least
\Omega(\epsilon^{2}/Q^{4})
. Here,
Q
denotes the number of quantum queries
\mathcal{A}
makes to
H
.

We now give a high-level overview of the proof. The first step is to define two experiments: the
chosen-coordinate binding
experiment
\mathsf{CCB}
and the
collapsing
experiment
\mathsf{Collapse}
. These experiments are interactive protocols between a verifier and a prover. The protocols end with the verifier outputting a bit
b
. If
b=1
, the prover is said to
win
the experiment. The reduction then proceeds in three steps: (i) reduce winning
\mathsf{CCB}
to solving
\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}
, (ii) reduce winning
\mathsf{Collapse}
to winning
\mathsf{CCB}
, and (iii) reduce solving
\mathsf{MLWE}
to winning
\mathsf{Collapse}
. Combining these steps together gives a reduction from
\mathsf{MLWE}
to
\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}
. The reduction can be illustrated as

\displaystyle\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}\overset{(i)}{\longleftarrow}\mathsf{CCB}\overset{(ii)}{\longleftarrow}\mathsf{Collapse}\overset{(iii)}{\longleftarrow}\mathsf{MLWE},

(5)

where the left arrow means “reduces to”.

Step (i):
\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}\leftarrow\mathsf{CCB}
.

In the
\mathsf{CCB}
experiment, the prover is first given a uniformly random
A\in R_{q}^{m\times l}
which it uses to send the verifier some
z\in R_{q}^{m}
, the verifier then sends the prover a challenge
c
chosen uniformly at random from
B_{\tau}
, and finally the prover sends the verifier a response
y\in R_{q}^{l}
. The prover wins if
Ay=z
,
\|y\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma
, and the last coordinate of
y
is
c
.

We directly apply the main result of
[
DFM20
]
to reduce winning
\mathsf{CCB}
when
l=m+k
to solving
\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}_{H,\tau,m,k,\gamma}
when
H
is a random oracle. In more detail, the result implies that an efficient algorithm that wins
\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}
using
Q
queries with probability
\epsilon
can be used to construct another efficient algorithm that wins
\mathsf{CCB}
with probability at least
\Omega(\epsilon/Q^{2})
.

Step (ii):
\mathsf{CCB}\leftarrow\mathsf{Collapse}
.

In the
\mathsf{Collapse}
experiment, the prover is first given a uniformly random
A\in R_{q}^{m\times l}
which it uses to send the verifier some
z\in R_{q}^{m}
together with a quantum state that must be supported only on
y\in R_{q}^{l}
such that
Ay=z
,
\|y\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma
. Then, the verifier samples a uniformly random bit
b^{\prime}
. If
b^{\prime}=1
, the verifier measures the quantum state in the computational basis, otherwise, it does nothing. The verifier then returns the quantum state to the prover. The prover responds by sending a bit
b^{\prime}
to the verifier and wins if
b^{\prime}=b
. The advantage of the prover is
2p-1
where
p
is its winning probability.

By using techniques in
[
DS23
,
Unr16
]
, we reduce winning
\mathsf{Collapse}
to winning
\mathsf{CCB}
. More specifically, we show that an efficient algorithm that wins
\mathsf{CCB}
with advantage
\epsilon
can be used to construct another efficient algorithm that wins
\mathsf{Collapse}
with advantage at least
\epsilon(\epsilon-1/|B_{\tau}|)
, which is roughly
\epsilon^{2}
since
1/|B_{\tau}|
is very small for the values of
\tau
we will consider. We generalize techniques in
[
DS23
,
Unr16
]
to work for challenge sets of size
>2
, which is necessary since the challenge set in the
\mathsf{CCB}
experiment,
B_{\tau}
, generally has size
>2
. The key idea of first applying the quantum algorithm for winning
\mathsf{CCB}
to the uniform superposition of all challenges remains the same.

Step (iii):
\mathsf{Collapse}\leftarrow\mathsf{MLWE}
.

We build on techniques in
[
LMZ23
,
LZ19
]
to reduce winning
\mathsf{Collapse}

. Define the mapping
\phi\colon R_{q}\to\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}

\displaystyle\phi(p(x))=\begin{pmatrix}1&w&\dots&w^{n-1}\\
1&w^{3}&\dots&w^{3(n-1)}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\vskip 2.0pt\\
1&w^{(2n-1)}&\dots&w^{(2n-1)(n-1)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}\\
a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{n-1}\end{pmatrix},

(10)

where

is a linear map. To see that

such that

, we have

\displaystyle\phi(p(x))=\bigl(\tilde{p}\bigl(w^{1}\bigr),\tilde{p}\bigl(w^{3}\bigr),\dots,\tilde{p}\bigl(w^{(2n-1)}\bigr)\bigr),

since

\mathbb{Z}_{q}

.

To see that

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(c_{0},\dots,c_{n-1})=

\displaystyle a_{0}+a_{1}x+\dots+a_{n-1}x^{n-1},\quad\text{where}

(12)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}\\
a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}\coloneqq

\displaystyle n^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\dots&1\\
w^{-1}&w^{-3}&\dots&w^{-(2n-1)}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\vskip 2.0pt\\
w^{-(n-1)}&w^{-3(n-1)}&\dots&w^{-(2n-1)(n-1)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}\\
c_{1}\\
\vdots\\
c_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}

(13)

and
n^{-1}
denotes the multiplicative inverse of
n
in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, which exists since
q=1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2n\implies n<q
. Since

is a primitive

-th root of unity in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
,

1
implies that the matrices corresponding to

multiply to the identity in

Proposition 1
.

Suppose

. Suppose that there exists a quantum query algorithm
\mathcal{A}

\epsilon

\mathcal{B}

\mathsf{CCB}
to
\mathsf{Plain}\raisebox{1.0pt}{-}\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}

Let
S,U,C,R
be alphabets,

\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2})
be a quantum algorithm. We define the
\Sigma
-experiment by:

\Sigma
-experiment
.

1.

s\leftarrow S
.

2.

(u,T)\leftarrow\mathcal{B}_{1}(s)
, where
u\in U
and
T
is an arbitrary register.

3.

c\leftarrow C
.

4.

r\leftarrow\mathcal{B}_{2}(T,c)
.

5.

Output
1
if
V(s,u,c,r)=1
.

The advantage of
\mathcal{B}
for winning the
\Sigma

1

In this subsection, we use the following theorem from
[
DFM20
]
.

Theorem 3

(Measure-and-reprogram
[
DFM20
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a quantum query algorithm using
Q

and outputs

and
r\in R
. Then, there exists a two-stage quantum algorithm
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2})
(not using any queries) such that the advantage of
\mathcal{B}
in the
\Sigma
-experiment is at least

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2Q+1)^{2}}\Pr\Bigl[V(s,u,H(u),r)\Bigm|H\leftarrow C^{U},\ s\leftarrow S,\ (u,r)\leftarrow\mathcal{A}^{\ket{H}}(s)\Bigr].

(14)

Moreover,
\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{B}_{1})+\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{B}_{2})\leq\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{A})
.

In the original statement of the theorem,
\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{B}_{1})+\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{B}_{2})
is upper bounded by
\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{A})-Q+\mathrm{poly}(Q,\log(|U|,\log(|C|))
. (The

accounts for the cost of instantiating

-wise independent hash function family from

[
Zha12
, Section 6]

. However, we follow the convention in
[
KLS18

and equate this cost to

under the fair assumption that
\mathcal{B}
, like
\mathcal{A}
, can also query a random oracle at unit cost.

Proposition 2
.

Let

. Suppose there exists a quantum query algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for solving

\mathsf{Plain}\raisebox{1.0pt}{-}\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}_{H,\tau,m,l,\gamma}
using
Q
queries with expected advantage
\epsilon
over uniformly random

. Then there exists a quantum algorithm
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2})
for winning
\mathsf{CCB}_{\tau,m,l,\gamma}
with advantage at least
\epsilon/(2Q+1)^{2}
. Moreover
\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{B}_{1})+\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{B}_{2})\leq\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

The quantum query algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for
\mathsf{Plain}\raisebox{1.0pt}{-}\mathsf{SelfTargetMSIS}_{H,\tau,m,l,\gamma}
takes input
A
and outputs
(y,M)
. So there exists another quantum query algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
using
Q
queries that outputs

((Ay\ \|\ M),\ y)
.

The first part of the proposition follows from applying
Theorem

3
to
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
with the following parameter settings which make the
\Sigma
-experiment identical to the
\mathsf{CCB}_{\tau,m,l,\gamma}
experiment:

1.

Set

,
U
to be the query set of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
,

, and
R=R_{q}^{l}
.

2.

Set
V\colon R_{q}^{m\times l}\times U\times B_{\tau}\times R_{q}^{l}\to\{0,1\}
by

\displaystyle V(A,u,c,y)=\mathbbm{1}[z=Ay,\ \|y\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma,\ y_{l}=c],

(15)

where

is parsed as

.

3.4
Reduction from
\mathsf{Collapse}
to
\mathsf{CCB}

In this subsection, we will use the following lemma, which can be found as
[
DS23
, Proposition 29]
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
P,Q
be projectors in
\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
and
\rho
be a density matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
\rho Q=\rho
. Then
\mathrm{tr}(QP\rho P)\geq\mathrm{tr}(P\rho)^{2}
.

The following proposition is similar to
[
Unr16
, Theorem 32]
and
[
DS23
, Theorem 28]
except the size of the challenge set in the
\mathsf{CCB}
experiment (in step 3 of
Definition

8
) is not restricted to being
2
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
m,l,\gamma,\tau\in\mathbb{N}
.
Suppose that there exists a quantum algorithm
\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})
that succeeds in
\mathsf{CCB}_{\tau,m,l,\gamma}
with advantage
\epsilon
, then there exists a valid quantum algorithm
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2})
that succeeds in
\mathsf{Collapse}_{m,l,\gamma}
with advantage at least

\epsilon(\epsilon-1/|B_{\tau}|)
. Moreover,
\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{B}_{1})\leq\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{A}_{1})+\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{A}_{2})+O(ml\log(q)\log(|B_{\tau}|))
and
\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{B}_{2})\allowbreak\leq\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{A}_{2})+O(\log(|B_{\tau}|))
.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality (wlog) that the arbitrary register in step 2 of the
\mathsf{CCB}_{\tau,m,l,\gamma}
experiment (
Definition

8
) is of the form
(Y,T^{\prime})
, where
Y
is a register on
R_{q}^{l}
and
T^{\prime}
is an arbitrary register. We assume wlog that
\mathcal{A}_{1}
prepares a state
\ket{\phi}
on register
(Y,Z,T^{\prime})
, where
Z
is a register on
R_{q}^{m}
, and measures
Z
in the computational basis to produce the
z
in step 2 of the
\mathsf{CCB}_{\tau,m,l,\gamma}
experiment. We also assume wlog that
\mathcal{A}_{2}
acts on its input register
(Y,T^{\prime},C)
, where
C
is a register on
B_{\tau}
that contains the
c
from step 3 of the
\mathsf{CCB}_{\tau,m,l,\gamma}
experiment, as follows:

1.

Apply a unitary
U
of the form
\sum_{r\in B_{\tau}}U_{r}\otimes\mathinner{|{r}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{r}|}
on
(Y,T^{\prime},C)
.

2.

Measure
Y
in the computational basis.

We proceed to construct
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2})
for the
\mathsf{Collapse}_{m,l,\gamma}
experiment (
Definition

9
). We first construct
\mathcal{B}_{1}
, given input
A\in R_{q}^{m\times l}
, as follows:

1.

Run
\mathcal{A}_{1}(A)
to prepare state
\ket{\phi}
on register
(Y,Z,T^{\prime})
.

2.

Prepare state
\ket{\psi}\coloneqq|B_{\tau}|^{-1/2}\sum_{r\in B_{\tau}}\ket{r}
on register
C
in time
O(\log(|B_{\tau}|))
. The current state on register
(Y,Z,T^{\prime},C)
is
\sigma\coloneqq\mathinner{|{\phi}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{\phi}|}\otimes\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{\psi}|}
. Apply
U
on register
(Y,T^{\prime},C)
and then measure register
(Y,Z,T^{\prime},C)
with the projective measurement
\{\Pi,1-\Pi\}
, where
\Pi
is defined by

\displaystyle\Pi\coloneqq\sum_{r\in B_{\tau}}\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(y,z)\in R_{q}^{l}\times R_{q}^{m}\colon\\
\|y\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma,\ Ay=z,\ y_{l}=r\end{subarray}}\mathinner{|{y,z}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{y,z}|}\otimes 1_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\mathinner{|{r}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{r}|}.

(16)

This measurement can be implemented by computing a bit indicating whether the constraints defining
\Pi
are satisfied into a separate register and then measuring that register, which takes time
O(ml\log(q)+\log(|B_{\tau}|))
.

3.

Let
B
be a bit register. If
\Pi
is measured, set the bit stored in
B
to
1
. If
(1-\Pi)
is measured, replace the state on register
(Y,Z)
with
\ket{0^{l}}\otimes\ket{0^{m}}
, set the bit stored in
B
to
0
. Then output the register
(Y,Z,T^{\prime},C,B)
.

Let
T\coloneqq(T^{\prime},C,B)
. We construct
\mathcal{B}_{2}
, given input register
(Y,Z,T)
:

1.

If
B
contains
0
, output a uniformly random bit
b^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
.

2.

Else apply
U^{\dagger}
on register
(Y,T^{\prime},C)
. Then measure
C
with the projective measurement
\{\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{\psi}|},1-\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{\psi}|}\}
using (the inverse of) the preparation circuit for
\ket{\psi}
in time
O(\log(|B_{\tau}|)
. If the outcome is
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{\psi}|}
, output
0
; else output
1
.

It is clear that
\mathcal{B}
is valid by definition. Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{B}_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{A}_{1})+\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{A}_{2})+O(ml\log(q)\log(|B_{\tau}|)),

(17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{B}_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Time}(\mathcal{A}_{2})+O(\log(|B_{\tau}|)).

(18)

We proceed to lower bound the success probability of
\mathcal{B}
. We analyze the probabilities of the following disjoint cases corresponding to
\mathcal{B}
being successful.

1.

Case 1: In this case,
1-\Pi
is measured and
b^{\prime}=b
. The probability that
1-\Pi
is measured is
(1-\epsilon)
. Conditioned on
1-\Pi
being measured,
b^{\prime}
is a uniformly random bit so the probability
b^{\prime}=b
is
1/2
. Therefore, the overall probability of this case is
(1-\epsilon)/2
.

2.

Case 2: In this case,
\Pi
is measured,
b=1
, and then
1-\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{\psi}|}
is measured. The probability that
\Pi
is measured is
\epsilon
and the probability that
b=1
is
1/2
. We now condition on these two events happening. Since
b=1
, the state of register
C
in the input to
\mathcal{B}_{2}
is a mixture of states of the form
\mathinner{|{r}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{r}|}
where
r\in B_{\tau}
. This is because
b=1
means that register
Y
is measured in the computational basis and conditioned on
\Pi
being measured, the
C
register is also measured in the computational basis (see the form of
\Pi
in
Eq.

16
). Therefore, the probability of
\mathcal{B}_{2}
measuring
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{\psi}|}
is
1/|B_{\tau}|
. Therefore, the overall probability of this case is
\epsilon\cdot(1/2)\cdot(1-1/|B_{\tau}|)
.

3.

Case 3: In this case,
\Pi
is measured,
b=0
, and then
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{\psi}|}
is measured. The probability that
b=0
is
1/2
. Conditioned on
b=0
,
Lemma

3
, applied with projectors
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{\psi}|}
and
U^{\dagger}\Pi U
and state
\sigma
, shows that the probability of measuring
\Pi
and then
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{\psi}|}
is least
\epsilon^{2}
. Therefore, the overall probability of this case is at least
\epsilon^{2}/2
.

Summing up the probabilities of the above cases, we see that the success probability of
\mathcal{B}
is at least

\displaystyle\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{|B_{\tau}|}\Bigr)+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Bigl(\epsilon-\frac{1}{|B_{\tau}|}\Bigr).

(19)

Therefore, the advantage of
\mathcal{B}
is at least
\epsilon(\epsilon-1/|B_{\tau}|)
, as required.

3.5

\mathsf{MLWE}
to
\mathsf{Collapse}

The proof structure of the main result of this subsection,
Proposition

4
, follows
[
LMZ23
, Theorem 1]
. We need to modify a number of aspects of their proof since it applies to the
SIS
hash function whereas here we consider its module variant, i.e., the
\mathsf{MSIS}
hash function.

We will use a rounding function
\lfloor\cdot\rceil_{t}\colon\mathbb{Z}_{q}\to\{0,1,\dots,t-1\}
, where
t\in\mathbb{N}
, that is defined as follows. For
j\in\{0,1,\dots,t-1\}
, define

\displaystyle I_{j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\{j\lfloor q/t\rfloor,j\lfloor q/t\rfloor+1,\dots,j\lfloor q/t\rfloor+\lfloor q/t\rfloor-1\}&\text{if }j\in\{0,1,\dots,t-2\},\\
\{(t-1)\lfloor q/t\rfloor,(t-1)\lfloor q/t\rfloor+1,\dots,q-1\}&\text{if }j=t-1.\end{cases}

(20)

(Note that
I_{j}
contains exactly
\lfloor q/t\rfloor
elements for
j\in\{0,1,\dots,t-2\}
and at least
\lfloor q/t\rfloor
elements for

with the constraint that
q/t\leq|I_{t-1}|\leq q/t+t-1

, define
\lfloor a\rceil_{t}
to be the unique
j\in\{0,1,\dots,t-1\}
such that

.

We will also use the following convenient notation. Let
Y
and
Z
be registers and
f:Y\to Z
. The measurement
y\mapsto f(y)
on register
Y
refers to the measurement implemented by computing
f(y)
into a separate register
Z
, measuring
Z
in the computational basis, and discarding the result.

Finally, we will use the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Let
0\neq\Delta\in R_{q}^{l}
and
\alpha\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
. If
b\leftarrow R_{q}^{l}
, then
(b\cdot\Delta)_{\alpha}
is uniformly distributed in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.

Proof.

Writing
b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{l})
and
\Delta=(\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{l})
, we have

\displaystyle(b\cdot\Delta)_{\alpha}=(b_{1}\Delta_{1})_{\alpha}+\dots+(b_{l}\Delta_{l})_{\alpha}.

(21)

Since
\Delta\neq 0
, there exists an
i\in[l]
such that
\Delta_{i}\neq 0
. To prove the lemma, it suffices to prove that
(b_{i}\Delta_{i})_{a}
is uniformly distributed in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.

Let
\phi,\phi^{\prime}
be as defined in the proof of
Lemma

2
. Write
\phi(\Delta_{i})=(c_{0},\dots,c_{n-1})\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
. Since
\Delta_{i}\neq 0
there exists
j\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
such that
c_{j}\neq 0
. Since
b_{i}
is a uniformly random element of
R_{q}
,
\phi(b_{i})
is a uniformly random element of
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
. Therefore, the distribution of
(b_{i}\Delta_{i})_{\alpha}=\phi^{\prime}(\phi(b_{i})\phi(\Delta_{i}))_{\alpha}
(where we used
Lemma

2
for the equality) is the same as the distribution of

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(d_{0}c_{0},\dots,d_{n-1}c_{n-1})_{\alpha},\quad\text{where}\quad d_{0},\dots,d_{n-1}\leftarrow\mathbb{Z}_{q}.

(22)

By the linearity of
\phi^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(d_{0}c_{0},\dots,d_{n-1}c_{n-1})_{\alpha}=d_{j}c_{j}\phi^{\prime}(e_{j})_{\alpha}+\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j}d_{j^{\prime}}c_{j^{\prime}}\phi^{\prime}(e_{j^{\prime}})_{\alpha},

(23)

where

denotes the

th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.

But

(see

). Therefore

is uniformly distributed in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
if

. Hence
(b_{i}\Delta_{i})_{\alpha}
is uniformly distributed in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
as required.
∎

The main result of this subsection is the following proposition.

.

Let

. Suppose

\mathcal{A}

Before proving this proposition, we first prove two lemmas. Let
Y
be a register on
R_{q}^{l}
and

. For
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the following measurements on

\blacksquare

: computational basis measurement.

\blacksquare

,

, then perform measurement

.

\blacksquare

: sample
b\leftarrow R_{q}^{l}
,
s\leftarrow R_{q}
, then perform measurement

Let

be such that

and
\|y^{\prime}\|_{\infty},\|y\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma

\displaystyle M_{1}^{t}(\mathinner{|{y}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{y^{\prime}}|})=\Bigl(1-\frac{t}{q}\cdot\mathrm{E}\Bigl[|e\cdot(y-y^{\prime})|_{0}\Bigm|e\leftarrow S_{\eta}^{l}\Bigr]\Bigr)\mathinner{|{y}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{y^{\prime}}|}.

Proof.

We have

M_{1}^{t}(\mathinner{|{y}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{y^{\prime}}|})=\Pr\Big[\lfloor(b\cdot y+s)_{0}\rceil_{t}=\lfloor(b\cdot y^{\prime}+s)_{0}\rceil_{t}\Bigm|e_{1}\leftarrow S_{\eta}^{m},e_{2}\leftarrow S_{\eta}^{l},b\coloneqq e_{1}^{\top}A+e_{2}^{\top},s\leftarrow R_{q}\Big]\cdot\mathinner{|{y}\rangle\hskip-1.0pt\langle{y^{\prime}}|}.

(25)

Writing
