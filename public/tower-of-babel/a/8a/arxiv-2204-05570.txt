Traveling waves for a quasilinear wave equation
Gabriele Bruell, Piotr Idzik, Wolfgang Reichel

Abstract
. We consider a 2+1 dimensional wave equation appearing in the context of polarized waves for the nonlinear Maxwell equations. The equation is quasilinear in the time derivatives and involves two material functions V V and Γ \Gamma . We prove the existence of traveling waves which are periodic in the direction of propagation and localized in the direction orthogonal to the propagation direction. Depending on the nature of the nonlinearity coeffcient Γ \Gamma we distinguish between two cases: (a) Γ ∈ L ∞ \Gamma\in L^{\infty} being regular and (b) Γ = γ ​ δ 0 \Gamma=\gamma\delta_{0} being a multiple of the delta potential at zero. For both cases we use bifuraction theory to prove the existence of nontrivial small-amplitude solutions. One can regard our results as a persistence result which shows that guided modes known for linear wave-guide geometries survive in the presence of a nonlinear constitutive law. Our main theorems are derived under a set of conditions on the linear wave operator. They are subsidised by explicit examples for the coefficients V V in front of the (linear) second time derivative for which our results hold.

1.
Introduction

Of concern is the following 2+1 dimensional quasilinear wave equation

(1.1)

-\Delta u+\partial_{t}^{2}\left(V(\lambda,y)u+\Gamma(y)u^{3}\right)=0,

which appears in the context of polarized waves for the nonlinear Maxwell equations. Here,
u=u(t,x,y)
is the unkown depeding on time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and the two spatial variables
x,y
.
We assume
u
to be periodic in
x
-direction and localized in
y
-direction. In what follows, we denote by
\mathbb{T}
the one dimensional flat
2\pi
-periodic torus, so that
(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
. The potentials
V(\lambda,\cdot)
and
\Gamma
depend only on
y
and incorporate material properties. Here,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is a parameter. The function
\Gamma
might be a bounded function (referred to as
regular
\Gamma
) or a multiple of a delta potential at
y=0
(referred to as
distributional
\Gamma
).

To motivate our interest in (
1.1
), let us explain how it appears in the context of electromagnetics. Recall that the
Maxwell equations in the absence of charges and currents are given by

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}\,=

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\mathbf{B},

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{E}),

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}=

\displaystyle\,\partial_{t}\mathbf{D},

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=

\displaystyle\mu_{0}\mathbf{H}.

The modeling of the underlying material is done by making assumptions on the form of the polarization field
\mathbf{P}
. Here, we assume that
\mathbf{P}
depends instantaneously on the electric field
\mathbf{E}
as follows

\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{E})=\varepsilon_{0}\chi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{E}+\varepsilon_{0}\chi_{3}(\mathbf{x})\lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert^{2}\mathbf{E}

with
\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, cf.
[
1
]
, Section 2.3. For simplicity we take
\chi_{1},\chi_{3}
as given scalar functions instead of the more general matrix/tensor structure of these quantities. The values
\varepsilon_{0},\mu_{0}
are constant such that
c^{2}=(\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0})^{-1}
and
c
is the speed of light in vacuum.
By direct calculations from Maxwell’s equations one obtains the second-order quasilinear wave-type equation for the electric field

(1.2)

\displaystyle 0=\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{E}+\partial_{t}^{2}\left(V(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{E}+\Gamma(\mathbf{x})\lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert^{2}\mathbf{E}\right),

where
V(\mathbf{x})=\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}\left(1+\chi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\right)
and
\Gamma(\mathbf{x})=\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}\chi_{3}(\mathbf{x})
. The magnetic induction
\mathbf{B}
can be retrieved from
\nabla\times\mathbf{E}=-\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}
by time-integration and it will satisfy
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
provided it does so at time
t=0
. By assumption the magnetic field is given by
\mathbf{H}=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\mathbf{B}
and it satisfies
\nabla\times\mathbf{H}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}
. It remains to check that the displacement field
\mathbf{D}
satisfies the Gauss law
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}=0
in the absence of external charges. This follows directly from the constitutive equation
\mathbf{D}=\varepsilon_{0}(1+\chi_{1}(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{E}+\varepsilon_{0}\chi_{3}(\mathbf{x})\lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert^{2}\mathbf{E}
and the assumption of the polarized form of the electric field

\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x},t)=(0,0,u(t,x,y))^{T}.

If we assume additionally that
V(\mathbf{x})=V(y)
and
\Gamma(\mathbf{x})=\Gamma(y)
then the quasilinear vectorial wave-type equation (
1.2
) turns into the scalar equation (
1.1
) for
u=u(t,x,y)
.

We study the existence of traveling wave solutions of (
1.1
) propagating in
x
-direction for certain classes of potentials
V
. More precisely, we consider potentials of the form

V(\lambda,y)=\lambda V_{0}(y)+V_{1}(y),\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

where
V_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
V_{1}
is a distribution (e.g a
\delta
-potential). If

(1.3)

u(t,x,y)=\Phi(x-t,y)

is a traveling wave solution of (
1.1
), propagating in
x
-direction with wave speed
v=1
, then
\Phi:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

(1.4)

-\Phi_{yy}-(1-\lambda V_{0}(y)-V_{1}(y))\Phi_{xx}+\Gamma(y)(\Phi^{3})_{xx}=0.

The parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
will serve as a bifurcation parameter.
One might ask why we introduced
\lambda
as a bifurcation parameter in the form
V(\lambda,y)=\lambda V_{0}(y)+V_{1}(y)
and not in the more intuitive form
V(\lambda,y)=\lambda(V_{0}(y)+V_{1}(y))
– the latter giving
\sqrt{\lambda}
the meaning of the propagation speed of the traveling wave. The reason lies in the distributional character of
V_{1}
. The choice of the underlying function spaces for our analysis allows to formulate a (suitably defined, cf. Lemma

4.3
) bounded inverse of
\tilde{L}_{\lambda}
, which is a slightly modified version of the wave operator
L_{\lambda}=-\partial_{y}^{2}-(1-\lambda V_{0}(y)-V_{1}(y))\partial_{x}^{2}
. Extending the multiplication with
\lambda
also to the distributional part
V_{1}
causes a difficulties when differentiating wit respect to
\lambda
, cf. Remark
4.5
.

Throughout the paper, a function
u
is called a traveling wave solution of (
1.1
) if
u
takes the form (
1.3
) and
\Phi
is a solution of (
1.4
), which is periodic in its first and localized in its second component.

We investigate the existence of nontrivial solutions
\Phi_{\lambda}
of (
1.4
) corresponding to the parameter
\lambda
under certain assumptions on the potential
V
; thereby providing the existence a nontrivial traveling wave solution
u_{\lambda}
of (
1.1
). We apply bifurcation theory for the parameter
\lambda
to find nontrivial solutions of (
1.4
). Our aim is to analyze the existence of nontrivial solutions in a way as general as possible, finding key properties of associate linear operators, which guarantee the existence of solutions via bifurcation theory. Eventually, we provide examples of specific potentials
V
for which these properties can be verified.
In particular, the following cases are under consideration:

(P1)

V
is a
\delta-
potential on a positive background
, that is
V
is a distribution of the form

V(\lambda,y)=\lambda+\alpha\delta_{0}(y).

(P2)

V
is a
\delta-
potential on a step background
, that is
V
is a distribution of the form

V(\lambda,y)=\lambda\textbf{1}_{|y|\geq b}+\beta\textbf{1}_{|y|<b}+\alpha\delta_{0}(y).

Concerning the nonlinear potential
\Gamma
we distinguish between
regular
\Gamma
, that is
\Gamma\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, and
distributional
\Gamma

, that is
\Gamma=\gamma\delta_{0}
. The main results for regular
\Gamma
are shown in Section
2.1
and for distributional
\Gamma
in Section
2.2
.

Let us also comment on related work. Problem (
1.1
) has been considered in
[
17
]
where spatially localized traveling wave solutions
of the 1+1-dimensional quasi-linear Maxwell model were investigated. The authors assume that
V(y)
is a periodic arrangement of delta potentials. Using a multiple scale ansatz in fast and slow time, the field profile is expanded into infinitely many modes which are quasiperiodic in time (time-periodic both in the fast and slow time variables). Using local bifurcation methods the authors solve a related system which is homotopically linked to the Maxwell problem written as any infinite coupled system. It is not clear if the local bifurcation connects the related system and the Maxwell problem but numerical results support the existence
of spatially localized traveling waves. In
[
7
]
and
[
9
]
another approximation (including error-estimates) of a version of (
1.1
) with periodic coefficients by finitely many coupled modes near band edges has been performed both analytically and numerially.

In the studies of the nonlinear Maxwell-system (
1.2
), often monochromatic waves
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x})=U(\mathbf{x})e^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}+c.c.
are considered. Since a typical cubic nonlinearity generates higher harmonics, they either need to be neglected (leading to an error), or the constitutive equation for
\mathbf{D}
is replaced by a time-averaged nonlinearity
\mathbf{D}=\varepsilon_{0}(1+\chi_{1}(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{E}+\varepsilon_{0}\chi_{3}(\mathbf{x})\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert^{2}\,dt\mathbf{E}
, cf.
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
14
,
2
,
8
,
16
]
and particularly the two survey papers
[
3
,
15
]
.

In contrast to the previously cited works, our approach is genuinely polychromatic and does not rely on time-averaged material laws. In our solution ansatz we allow for harmonics of arbitrary order and we treat (
1.1
) without any approximation. Recently in
[
12
]
a similar approach was taken and spatially localized, time-periodic solution of (
1.1
) were obtained via variational methods. The result of the present paper and
[
12
]
are complementary in the following sense: (a) in
[
12
]
only distributional
\Gamma
is considered whereas in the present paper we also allow for regular
\Gamma\in L^{\infty}
; (b) in the case of distributional
\Gamma
,
[
12
]
only treats
V\in L^{\infty}
whereas in the present paper we always have a delta potential contributing to
V
. Variational methods as in
[
12
]
have the advantage of producing solutions which may be far away from the trivial solution whereas local bifurcation methods as in the present paper produce solutions in the vicinity of zero. On the other hand, the local bifurcation method leads to more precise information about the actual shape of the bifurcating branch of solutions.

2.
Main results

We are looking for solutions of (
1.4
) of the form

(2.1)

\Phi(x,y;\lambda)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\phi_{k}(y;\lambda)\sin(kx).

Our analysis is going to be divided into two parts separating the case when
\Gamma
is regular in the sense that
\Gamma\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and the case when
\Gamma
is distributional and takes the extreme form of a
\delta
-potential. This is essentially due to the fact that in the former case we are concerned with a nonlinear equation on the domain
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
, while in case of
\Gamma
being a
\delta
-potential the problem can be viewed as a linear equation on
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
– which can be solved separately – equipped with a nonlinear boundary condition at
x=0
induced by the delta potential.

2.1.
Main result for regular
\Gamma

Let us start with the definition of a weak solution of (
1.4
) in the case when
\Gamma\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

Definition 2.1

(Weak solution in the case of regular
\Gamma
)
.

We say that
\Phi\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T};L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{T};H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
is a
weak solution
of (
1.4
) if and only if

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi_{y}\Psi_{y}-\left(1-\lambda V_{0}(y)\right)\Phi_{xx}\Psi\,dy\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\langle V_{1}(\cdot)\Phi_{x}(x,\cdot),\Psi_{x}(x,\cdot)\rangle\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\Phi^{3})_{xx}\Psi\,dy\,dx=0

for any
\Psi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T};H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
. Here,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the dual pairing between
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
and
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Remark 2.2
.

(a)

We consider
V_{1}
as a bounded linear operator from
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
into
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
. When
V_{1}=\delta_{0}
this means that for
f,g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
we have
\langle V_{1}f,g\rangle=f(0)g(0)
, i.e., since
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
it multiplies
\delta_{0}
and generates
f(0)\delta_{0}
as a distribution acting on
g
.

(b)

Clearly
\Phi_{xx}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
. We shall see in Section
4
(cf. (
4.8
), (
4.9
) in Lemma
4.4
) that also
\Phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
and
\Phi_{x}\in L^{4}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
so that
(\Phi^{3})_{xx}=3\Phi^{2}\Phi_{xx}+6\Phi\Phi_{x}^{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
.

If
\Gamma\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, the ansatz in (
2.1
) allows us to reduce the problem of finding nontrivial solutions of (
1.4
) to studying spectral properties of the family of linear wave operators

L_{\lambda}^{k}:=-\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}+k^{2}(1-\lambda V_{0}(y)-V_{1}(y))\qquad\mbox{for}\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

We prove the following theorem:

Theorem 2.3

(Existence of traveling waves for regular
\Gamma
)
.

Assume that
\Gamma\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, the potential
V
is given by

V(\lambda,y)=\lambda V_{0}(y)+V_{1}(y)

and

(L0)

V_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
V_{1}:H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\to H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
is bounded;

(L1)

for every fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
the operator
L_{\lambda}^{k}:D(L_{\lambda}^{k})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is self-adjoint;

(L2)

there exists a wavenumber
k_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
, a value
\lambda_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
, and an open interval
I_{\lambda_{*}}\subset\mathbb{R}
containing
\lambda_{*}
such that zero is an isolated simple eigenvalue of
L_{\lambda_{*}}^{k_{*}}

and
0\in\rho(L_{\lambda}^{k})
for any
(k,\lambda)\in\mathbb{N}\times I_{\lambda_{*}}
with
(k,\lambda)\neq(k_{*},\lambda_{*})
;

(L3)

if
L_{\lambda}^{k}\phi=f
for some
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then

\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\frac{1}{k^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\frac{1}{k}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

uniformly for
\lambda\in I_{\lambda_{*}}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large.

If in addition
V_{0}
satisfies the transversality condition

(2.2)

\langle V_{0}\phi^{*},\phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\neq 0,

where
\phi^{*}
spans the one-dimensional kernel of
L_{\lambda_{*}}^{k_{*}}
,
then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and a smooth curve through
(0,\lambda_{*})
,

\{(\Phi(\varepsilon),\lambda(\varepsilon))\mid|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{0}\}\subset(H^{2}(\mathbb{T};L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{T};H^{1}(\mathbb{R})))\times I_{\lambda_{*}},

of nontrivial solutions of (
1.4
) with

\displaystyle\Phi(0)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle D_{\varepsilon}\Phi(0)(x,y)=\phi^{*}(y)\sin(k_{*}x),

\displaystyle\lambda(0)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{*},

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}(0)=0,\quad\ddot{\lambda}(0)=-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma(y)(\phi^{*})^{4}(y)\,dy}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{0}(y)(\phi^{*})^{2}(y)\,dy}.

Remark 2.4
.

The transversality condition (
2.2
) is trivially satisfied if
V_{0}\geq 0
,
\not\equiv 0
or
V_{0}\leq 0
,
\not\equiv 0
.

In Section
4
we prove Theorem
2.3
.
There are two main requirements on
L_{\lambda}^{k}
providing the existence of nontrivial solutions via bifurcation theory: The first is that there exists a value
\lambda_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
of the bifurcation parameter such that

has a one-dimensional kernel if and only if

for some wave number
k_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
(see
(L2)
); this is a necessary bifurcation condition. Secondly, we demand that for any

the self-adjoint operator
L_{\lambda}^{k}
has a spectral gap
(-ck^{2},ck^{2})
around zero, which ensures the decay properties of
\phi_{k}(\cdot;\lambda)
(see
(L3)
). Eventually, after we have established Theorem
2.3
, we turn to the specific case, when
\Gamma\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is regular,
V
are potentials of the form as in (P1) and (P2) and formulate tangible assumptions on the triple
(k_{*},\lambda_{*},\alpha)
(see (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) below), which guarantee that conditions
(L0)-(L3)
of Theorem
2.3
are satisfied; thereby proving the existence of nontrivial traveling wave solutions of (
1.1
). In particular, we prove the following corollaries.

Corollary 2.5

(Case P1, regular
\Gamma
)
.

Let
\Gamma\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
V(\lambda,y)=\lambda+\alpha\delta_{0}(y)
. If
k_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda_{*}<1
are given and
\alpha>0
is determined from

(2.3)

\alpha=\frac{2\sqrt{1-\lambda_{*}}}{k_{*}},

then the assumptions in Theorem

are satisfied with

\phi^{*}(y)=\sqrt{k_{*}\sqrt{1-\lambda_{*}}}e^{-k_{*}\sqrt{1-\lambda_{*}}|y|}

and

\displaystyle\Phi(0)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle D_{\varepsilon}\Phi(0)(x,y)=\phi^{*}(y)\sin(k_{*}x),

\displaystyle\lambda(0)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{*},

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}(0)=0,\quad\ddot{\lambda}(0)=-\frac{3}{2}k_{*}^{2}(1-\lambda_{*})\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma(y)e^{-4k_{*}\sqrt{1-\lambda_{*}}|y|}\,dy.

Corollary 2.6

(Case P2, regular
\Gamma
)
.

Let
\Gamma\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
V(\lambda,y)=\lambda\textbf{1}_{|y|\geq b}+\beta\textbf{1}_{|y|<b}+\alpha\delta_{0}(y)

k_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
,

\alpha=\frac{2\sqrt{1-\beta}}{k^{*}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{1-\beta}\sinh(k_{*}\sqrt{1-\beta}b)+\sqrt{1-\lambda_{*}}\cosh(k_{*}\sqrt{1-\beta}b)}{\sqrt{1-\beta}\cosh(k_{*}\sqrt{1-\beta}b)+\sqrt{1-\lambda_{*}}\sinh(k_{*}\sqrt{1-\beta}b)},

2.2.
Main result for distributional
\Gamma

Again, we start with the definition of a weak solution of (
1.4
), but now in the case when
\Gamma
is given by a
\delta
-potential. We assume that the function
V_{1}=W+\alpha\delta_{0}
splits into a regular part
W
and the distributional part
\alpha\delta_{0}
so that
V=\lambda V_{0}+W+\alpha\delta_{0}
, where
V_{0},W\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
\Gamma=\gamma\delta_{0}
.

Definition 2.8

(Weak solution in the case of distributional
\Gamma
)
.

We say that
\Phi\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T};L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{T};H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))\cap C(\mathbb{R};H^{2}(\mathbb{T}))
is a
weak solution
of (
1.4
) if and only if

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi_{y}\Psi_{y}-\left(1-\lambda V_{0}(y)-W(y)\right)\Phi_{xx}\Psi\,dy\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(\alpha\Phi_{xx}(x,0)+\gamma(\Phi^{3})_{xx}(x,0)\bigr)\Psi(x,0)\,dx=0

for any
\Psi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T};H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
.

Remark 2.9
.

Clearly
\Phi_{xx}(\cdot,0)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
and
\Phi(\cdot,0),\Phi_{x}(\cdot,0)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
so that
(\Phi^{3}(\cdot,0))_{xx}=3\Phi(\cdot,0)^{2}\Phi(\cdot,0)_{xx}+6\Phi(\cdot,0)\Phi(\cdot,0)_{x}^{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. Moreover,
\Psi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T};H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
has an
L^{2}
-trace at
y=0
.

Note that (
1.4
) can be written as a linear partial differential equation on
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
equipped with a nonlinear boundary condition on
\mathbb{T}
:

(2.5)

\begin{cases}-\Phi_{yy}-(1-\lambda V_{0}-W)\Phi_{xx}=0,\quad&(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},\\
\Phi_{y}(x,0_{+})-\Phi_{y}(x,0_{-})=\partial_{x}^{2}\left(\alpha\Phi+\gamma\Phi^{3}\right)(x,0),\quad&x\in\mathbb{T}.\end{cases}

As before let

L_{k}^{\lambda}:=-\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}+k^{2}(1-\lambda V_{0}(y)-W(y)-\alpha\delta_{0}(y))

be a family of linear wave operators and set

L_{0,k}^{\lambda}:=-\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}+k^{2}(1-\lambda V_{0}(y)-W(y))

to be the regular part of
L_{k}^{\lambda}
.
We prove the following theorem:

Theorem 2.10

(Existence of traveling wave for distributional
\Gamma
)
.

Assume that
\Gamma=\gamma\delta_{0}
, the potential
V
is given by

V(\lambda,y)=\lambda V_{0}(y)+W(y)+\alpha\delta_{0}(y),

and

(\tilde{L}0)

V_{0},W\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
are even;

(\tilde{L}1)

there exists an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
such that for every fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda\in I
the operator
L_{0,\lambda}^{k}:H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies
0\in\rho(L_{0,\lambda}^{k})
;

(\tilde{L}2)

there exists a wavenumber
k_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
, a value
\lambda_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
, and an open interval
I_{\lambda_{*}}\subset I\subset\mathbb{R}
containing
\lambda_{*}
such that zero is an isolated simple eigenvalue of
L_{\lambda_{*}}^{k_{*}}

and
0\in\rho(L_{\lambda}^{k})
for any
(k,\lambda)\in\mathbb{N}\times I_{\lambda_{*}}
with
(k,\lambda)\neq(k_{*},\lambda_{*})
;

(\tilde{L}3)

there exist
C>0
such that
\|\phi_{k}(\cdot;\lambda)\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}\leq C
uniformly for
\lambda\in I_{\lambda_{*}}
,

(4.7)

F(\Phi,\lambda)=0,

where
F:X\times I_{\lambda_{*}}\to X
is given by

F(\Phi,\lambda)=\Phi+\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}\left(\Gamma(y)(\Phi^{3})_{xx}-P^{*}\Phi\right).

We want to apply bifurcation theory to equation (
4.7
).
Clearly,
F(0,\lambda)=0
for any
\lambda\in I_{\lambda_{*}}
and the line
\{(0,\lambda)\mid\lambda\in I_{\lambda_{*}}\}
constitutes the line of trivial solutions from which we aim to bifurcate at
\lambda=\lambda_{*}
. The following lemma collects the necessary properties of the map
F
.

Lemma 4.4
.

The map
F:X\times I_{\lambda_{*}}\to X
is a
C^{\infty}
-map. Moreover the following holds:

(i)

The linearization of
F
about
\Phi=0
, given by

D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda)=\Id-\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}P^{*}:X\to X

is a Fredholm operator of index zero. In particular
D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda_{*})=\Id-P^{*}
. The kernel of
D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda)
is trivial for
\lambda\in I_{\lambda_{*}}\setminus\{\lambda_{*}\}
and it is given by
\spa\{\Phi^{*}\}
if
\lambda=\lambda_{*}
.

(ii)

The mixed second derivative of
F
about
\Phi=0
is given by

D^{2}_{\Phi,\lambda}F(0,\lambda)=\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}V_{0}\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}P^{*}:X\to X.

Proof.

Let us first verify the mapping properties of
F
by checking that
(\Phi^{3})_{xx}\in Y
for
\Phi\in X
. First note that
\Phi\in X
implies

(4.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\Phi\|_{\infty}&=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R},y\in\mathbb{T}}\Bigl|\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\phi_{k}(y)\sin(kx)\Bigr|\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|\phi_{k}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&\leq\Bigl(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{k^{2}}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigl(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k^{2}\|\phi_{k}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\|\Phi\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))}\end{split}

and, using
|\cos(kx)|\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\Phi_{x}|^{4}\,dy\,dx\Bigr)^{1/4}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|k\phi_{k}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}=\sqrt[4]{2\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}|k|\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}|k|\|\phi_{k}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{3}{4}},

by the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality, cf.
[
10
]
. Using a triple Hölder inequality we obtain that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\Phi_{x}|^{4}\,dy\,dx\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{4}}&\leq C\Bigl(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k^{2}\|\phi_{k}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{8}}\Bigl(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k^{4}\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Bigr)^{\frac{3}{8}}\Bigl(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k^{-3/2}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\lesssim\|\Phi\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|\Phi\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))}^{\frac{3}{4}}.\end{split}

Hence, for
\Phi\in X
we have
(\Phi^{3})_{xx}=3\Phi^{2}\Phi_{xx}+6\Phi\Phi_{x}^{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))\subset Y
and thus the mapping properties of
F
are proved.

The differentiability properties of
F
with respect to
\Phi
also follow in a similar way from
\Phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
and
\Phi_{x}\in L^{4}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
. This can be seen as follows: the (formal) first/second derivatives of
F
with respect to
\Phi
are linear/bilinear operators and contain terms of the form
abc_{xx}
or
ab_{x}c_{x}
where
a,b,c\in X
. Based on the estimates

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|ab_{x}c_{x}|^{2}\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\|a\|_{\infty}^{2}\|b_{x}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}^{2}\|c_{x}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}^{2},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|abc_{xx}|^{2}\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\|a\|_{\infty}^{2}\|b\|_{\infty}^{2}\|c_{xx}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}^{2}

we find in view of (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) that the first/second derivatives of
F
with respect to
\Phi
exist, are bounded linear/bilinear operators from
X
to
Y
, and depend continuously on
\Phi
and
\lambda
. Due to the cubic nature of the nonlinearity, derivatives of
F
of order higher than two with respect to
\Phi
are independent of
\Phi
.

The differentiability properties of
F
with respect to
\lambda
follow from

(4.10)

\frac{d}{d\lambda}\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}=-\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}\frac{d}{d\lambda}\tilde{L}_{\lambda}\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}=-\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}V_{0}\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}

and due to
\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}:Y\to X
and
V_{0}\partial_{x}^{2}:X\to Y
, we see that the resulting operator on the right-hand side of (
4.10
) is indeed a bounded linear map from
Y\to X
. Moreover, (
4.10
) explains the formula for
D^{2}_{\Phi,\lambda}F(0,\lambda)
in (ii).

Finally, the formula in (i) shows that
D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda)
is a compact perturbation of the identity, and hence Fredholm of index zero. Let us compute the kernel of
D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda)
. If
\Psi\in X
satisfies
D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda)\Psi=0
then according to Lemma
4.3
we have that
\Psi
is a weak solution of
L_{\lambda}\Psi=0
. Then, for
\lambda\neq\lambda_{*}
we have
\psi_{k}=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and hence
\Psi=0
. For
\lambda=\lambda_{*}
we have
\psi_{k}=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{k_{*}\}
and
\psi_{k_{*}}\in\spa\{\phi^{*}\}
so that
\Psi\in\spa\{\Phi^{*}\}
as claimed. Notice finally that
D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda_{*})=\Id-\tilde{L}_{\lambda_{*}}^{-1}P^{*}=\Id-P^{*}
since
\range P^{*}=\spa\{\Phi^{*}\}
is the eigenspace of
\tilde{L}_{\lambda*}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
1
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Let us briefly describe the difficulty that arises when one considers the bifurcation problem for
V(\lambda,y)=\lambda V(y)
with
V=V_{0}+V_{1}
, i.e., when multiplication with the bifurcation parameter is extended to the distributional potential
V_{1}
. In this case one already obtains a problem in verifying the
C^{1}
-property of the map
F
. Formally one finds

D_{\lambda}F(\Phi,\lambda)=-\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}V\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}(\Gamma(y)(\Phi^{3})_{xx}-P^{*}\Phi).

As above, we would expect to have
\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}V\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}:Y\to X
as a bounded linear map. But this is not the case, as a calculation in the case where
V_{0}(y)\equiv 1
and
V_{1}(y)=\alpha\delta_{0}(y)
shows. Namely, let
A=\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}:Y\to X
and
B=V\partial_{x}^{2}
. Then
B:X\to H^{-1}(\mathbb{T};H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}))
and
C=\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}:\range(B)\to H^{3/2}(\mathbb{T};L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))\cap H^{1/2}(\mathbb{T};H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))\not\subset X
, i.e., we are missing a half-derivative in the regularity gain.

The advantage of formulating the problem (
4.1
) as
F(\Phi,\lambda)=0
relies on the fact that its linearization about
\Phi=0
is of the form identity plus compact operator, which provides the Fredholm property for free. Applying the Crandall–Rabinowitz theorem (cf. e.g.
[
5
]
or
[
11
, Theorem I.5.1]
), we prove that assumption
(L0)
–
(L3)
on the family of Schrödinger operators
L_{\lambda}^{k}
are sufficient to guarantee the existence of nontrivial small-amplitude solutions of (
4.1
) provided a certain
transversality condition
is satisfied, which we can formulate in terms of the potential
V_{0}
, see (
2.2
).

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Recall from Lemma
4.4
that
D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda_{*})=\Id-P^{*}:X\to X
. Moreover,
D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda_{*})
is a Fredholm operator of index zero with a one-dimensional kernel spanned by
\Phi^{*}
. Correspondingly, we can split the underlying space as follows:

X=\spa\{\Phi^{*}\}\oplus\spa\{\Phi^{*}\}^{\perp_{L^{2}}}=\ker(D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda_{*}))\oplus\range(D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda_{*})).

Hence, according to the Crandall–Rabinowitz theorem, the existence of a local bifurcation branch of nontrivial solutions (
1.4
) follows provided that the transversality condition

D_{\Phi\lambda}^{2}F(0,\lambda_{*})\Phi^{*}\notin\range D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda_{*})=\spa\{\Phi^{*}\}^{\perp_{L^{2}}}

is satisfied. In view of
\tilde{L}_{\lambda_{*}}^{-1}\Phi^{*}=\Phi^{*}
and the symmetry of
\tilde{L}_{\lambda_{*}}^{-1}
(which follows from the self-adjointness of
L^{k}_{\lambda_{*}},\tilde{L}^{k}_{\lambda_{*}}
) the transversality condition holds since

\langle D_{\Phi\lambda}^{2}F(0,\lambda_{*})\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}=\langle\tilde{L}_{\lambda_{*}}^{-1}V_{0}\Phi^{*}_{xx},\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}=\langle V_{0}\Phi^{*}_{xx},\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}=-\pi k^{2}\langle V_{0}\phi^{*}_{x},\phi^{*}_{x}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\neq 0

due to assumption (
2.2
) of the theorem.

Now, we are going to state the bifurcation formulas with the help of the Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction (cf.
[
11
, Theorem I.2.3]
).
The Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction theorem in our context reads as follows:

Theorem 4.6

(Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction,
[
11
]
, Theorem I.2.3)
.

Let
F:X\times I\to X
be a
C^{\infty}
-map and
X=N\oplus N^{\perp_{L^{2}}}
with
N=\spa\{\Phi^{*}\}=\ker D_{\Phi}F(0,\lambda_{*})
and
\lambda_{*}\in I
.
There exists a neighborhood
O\times I^{\prime}\subset\{(\Phi,\lambda)\in X\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\}
of the bifurcation point
(0,\lambda_{*})
such that the problem

F(\Phi,\lambda)=0\qquad\mbox{for}\quad(\Phi,\lambda)\in O\times I^{\prime}

is equivalent to the finite-dimensional problem

(4.11)

\eta(\varepsilon\Phi^{*},\lambda):=P^{*}F(\varepsilon\Phi^{*}+\psi(\varepsilon\Phi^{*},\lambda),\lambda)=0

for functions
\eta\in C^{\infty}(O_{N}\times I^{\prime},N)
,
\psi\in C^{\infty}(O_{N}\times I^{\prime},N^{\perp_{L^{2}}})
where
O_{N}\subset N
is an open neighborhood of the zero element in
N
. One has that

\eta(0,\lambda_{*})=\psi(0,\lambda_{*})=D_{\Phi}\psi(0,\lambda_{*})=0

and solving (
4.11
) provides a solution

\Phi=\varepsilon\Phi^{*}+\psi(\varepsilon\Phi^{*},\lambda)

of the infinite-dimensional problem
F(\Phi,\lambda)=0
.

We have the following Fréchet derivatives:

\displaystyle D_{\Phi}F(\Phi,\lambda)\Phi^{*}

\displaystyle=\Phi^{*}+\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}\left(\Gamma(y)3(\Phi^{2}\Phi^{*})_{xx}-P^{*}\Phi^{*}\right),

\displaystyle D_{\Phi\Phi}^{2}F(\Phi,\lambda)[\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}]

\displaystyle=\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}\left(\Gamma(y)6\Phi(\Phi^{*})^{2}\right)_{xx},

\displaystyle D_{\Phi\Phi\Phi}^{3}F(\Phi,\lambda)[\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}]

\displaystyle=\tilde{L}_{\lambda}^{-1}\left(\Gamma(y)6(\Phi^{*})^{3}\right)_{xx}.

According to
[
11
, Section I.6]
, we have that

\dot{\lambda}(0)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\langle D_{\Phi\Phi}^{2}F(0,\lambda_{*})[\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}],\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}}{\langle D_{\Phi\lambda}^{2}F(0,\lambda_{*})\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}}.

In view of
F
being cubic in
\Phi
it is clear that
\dot{\lambda}(0)=0
. In this case the second derivative is given by

(4.12)

\ddot{\lambda}(0)=-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\langle D_{\Phi\Phi\Phi}^{3}\eta(0,\lambda_{*})[\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}],\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}}{\langle D_{\Phi\lambda}^{2}F(0,\lambda_{*})\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}}.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\{(\Phi(\varepsilon),\lambda(\varepsilon))\mid|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{0}\}\subset X\times I_{\lambda_{*}}
be the local bifurcation curve found in Theorem
2.3
corresponding to the bifurcation point
(0,\lambda_{*})
. Then

\dot{\lambda}(0)=0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\ddot{\lambda}(0)=-\frac{3\pi}{2}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma(y)(\phi^{*})^{4}(y)\,dy}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{0}(y)(\phi^{*})^{2}(y)\,dy}.

Proof.

As already mentioned, the cubic nonlinearity of
F
implies already that
\dot{\lambda}(0)=0
. We are left to compute the second derivative of
\lambda
at the origin. According to the formula in (
4.12
) we need to compute the third derivative of
\eta
with respect to
\Phi
evaluated at
(0,\lambda_{*})
. As for instance in
[
11
, Eq. (I.6.5)]
we obtain that

\displaystyle D_{\Phi\Phi\Phi}^{3}\eta(0,\lambda_{*})[\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}]=

\displaystyle P^{*}D_{\Phi\Phi\Phi}^{3}F(0,\lambda_{*})[\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}]+3P^{*}D_{\Phi\Phi}^{2}F(0,\lambda_{*})[\Phi^{*},D_{\Phi\Phi}^{2}\psi(0,\lambda_{*})[\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}]].

Again, since
F
is cubic in
\Phi
, we have that
D_{\Phi\Phi}^{2}F(0,\lambda_{*})=0
, whence

\displaystyle\ddot{\lambda}(0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\langle P^{*}D_{\Phi\Phi\Phi}^{3}F(0,\lambda_{*})[\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}],\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}}{\langle D_{\Phi\lambda}^{2}F(0,\lambda_{*})\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\langle D_{\Phi\Phi\Phi}^{3}F(0,\lambda_{*})[\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}],\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}}{\langle D_{\Phi\lambda}^{2}F(0,\lambda_{*})\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}}.

We have that

\displaystyle\langle D_{\Phi\Phi\Phi}^{3}F(0,\lambda_{*})[\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}],\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{L}_{\lambda_{*}}^{-1}\left(\Gamma(y)6(\Phi^{*})^{3}\right)_{xx},\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}.

Using the symmetry of
\tilde{L}_{\lambda_{*}}
together with
\tilde{L}^{-1}_{\lambda_{*}}\Phi^{*}=\Phi^{*}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\langle D_{\Phi\Phi\Phi}^{3}F(0,\lambda_{*})[\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}],\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=6\langle\Gamma(y)\left((\Phi^{*})^{3}\right)_{xx},\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}=-\frac{9}{2}\pi k_{*}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma(y)(\phi^{*})^{4}(y)\,dy

and we know already that the denominator in
\ddot{\lambda}(0)
is given by

\displaystyle\langle D_{\Phi\lambda}^{2}F(0,\lambda_{*})\Phi^{*},\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\langle V_{0}(y)\Phi^{*}_{xx},\Phi^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}=-\pi k_{*}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{0}(y)(\phi^{*})^{2}(y)\,dy.

Summarizing, we conclude that

\ddot{\lambda}(0)=-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma(y)(\phi^{*})^{4}(y)\,dy}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{0}(y)(\phi^{*})^{2}(y)\,dy}.

∎

Lemma 5.1

Lemma 5.2
.

There exists an open interval
I_{\lambda_{*}}\subset\mathbb{R}
containing
\lambda_{*}
such that the following holds for all

and all
\lambda\in I_{\lambda_{*}}
:
if

for some

, then

\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\frac{1}{k^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\frac{1}{k}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

In particular, there exists a constant
c=c(k_{*},|I_{\lambda_{*}}|)
, depending on
k_{*}
and the size of the interval
I_{\lambda_{*}}
, such that

(-ck^{2},ck^{2})\subset\rho(L_{\lambda}^{k})\quad\mbox{ for every}\quad k\geq 3k_{*},\lambda\in I_{\lambda_{*}}.

Proof.

We show that for any
\lambda<1
we have

\displaystyle\|L_{\lambda}^{k}\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}^{2}+\|\phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+2(1-\lambda)\left(k^{2}-4k_{*}^{2}-\frac{16(\lambda_{*}-\lambda)}{\alpha^{2}}\right)\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+\left(\frac{k_{*}^{2}\alpha^{2}}{4}+\lambda_{*}-\lambda\right)^{2}k^{4}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},

which proves the assertion. We have that

\displaystyle\|L_{\lambda}^{k}\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}(L_{\lambda}^{k}\phi)^{2}\,dy+\int_{0}^{\infty}(L_{\lambda}^{k}\phi)^{2}\,dy.

For the first integral on the right hand side, we compute

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}(L_{\lambda}^{k}\phi)^{2}\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}(-\phi^{\prime\prime}+k^{2}(1-\lambda)\phi)^{2}\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}|\phi^{\prime\prime}|^{2}-2k^{2}(1-\lambda)\phi^{\prime\prime}\phi+k^{4}(1-\lambda)^{2}\phi^{2}\,dy

\displaystyle=\|\phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}^{2}+2k^{2}(1-\lambda)\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}^{2}+k^{4}(1-\lambda)^{2}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}^{2}-2k^{2}(1-\lambda)\phi^{\prime}(0_{-})\phi(0),

where we used integration by parts. Similarly, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(L_{\lambda}^{k}\phi)^{2}\,dy

\displaystyle=\|\phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}+2k^{2}(1-\lambda)\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}+k^{4}(1-\lambda)^{2}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}+2k^{2}(1-\lambda)\phi^{\prime}(0_{+})\phi(0).

Taking the sum of the two integrals and using for
\phi\in D(L^{k}_{\lambda})
that

\phi^{\prime}(0_{+})-\phi^{\prime}(0_{-})=-k^{2}\alpha\phi(0)

we find that

(5.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|L_{\lambda}^{k}\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=&\|\phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}^{2}+\|\phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}+2k^{2}(1-\lambda)\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+k^{4}(1-\lambda)^{2}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\\
&-\frac{2}{\alpha}(1-\lambda)\left(\phi^{\prime}(0_{+})-\phi^{\prime}(0_{-})\right)^{2}.\end{split}

A simple computation together with Young’s inequality implies that

|\phi^{\prime}(0_{+})|^{2}\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}|\phi^{\prime}\phi^{\prime\prime}|\,dy\leq\left(\varepsilon\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|\phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}\right)

for any
\varepsilon>0
. A similar estimate holds for
|\phi^{\prime}(0_{-})|^{2}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\left|\phi^{\prime}(0_{+})-\phi^{\prime}(0_{-})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(|\phi^{\prime}(0_{+})|^{2}+|\phi^{\prime}(0_{-})|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\varepsilon\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|\phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}((-\infty,0))}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|\phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}((0,\infty))}^{2}\right).

Inserting the latter into (
5.5
) yields

\displaystyle\|L_{\lambda}^{k}\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\geq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{4(1-\lambda)}{\alpha\varepsilon}\right)(\|\phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}^{2}+\|\phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2})

\displaystyle+2(1-\lambda)(k^{2}-\frac{2\varepsilon}{\alpha})\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+k^{4}(1-\lambda)^{2}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

The choice
\varepsilon=\frac{8}{\alpha}(1-\lambda)=2\alpha k_{*}^{2}+\frac{8(\lambda_{*}-\lambda)}{\alpha}
implies the claim.
∎

we have to distinguish between the case
\beta<1
,
\beta>1
, and
\beta=1
. Notice that the transversality condition and
(\tilde{L}0)
are trivially satisfied for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let us begin with the case
\beta<1
. The validity of
(\tilde{L}1)
and
(\tilde{L}3)
follow immediately from Remark
6.2
, since
1-\lambda V_{0}-W=(1-\lambda)\textbf{1}_{|y|\geq b}+(1-\beta\textbf{1}_{|y|<b})>0
. It remains to consider
(\tilde{L}2)
. But again the operator
L_{\lambda}^{k}
is the same as the one considered in Corollary
2.5
of Case (P1) and the choice of
\alpha
in Corollary
2.14
is exactly the same as in Corollary
2.6
of Case (P1). Hence
(\tilde{L}1)
holds and this example is complete.

Next we consider the case
\beta>1
. Here we have made the choices

\sqrt{\beta-1}b=\pi

and

\alpha=\frac{2\sqrt{1-\lambda_{*}}}{k_{*}}.

We are left to verify
(\tilde{L}1)-(\tilde{L}3)
of Theorem
2.10
. For
(\tilde{L}1)
we need to consider the operator
L_{0,\lambda}^{k}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}+k^{2}(1-\lambda\textbf{1}_{|y|\geq b}-\beta\textbf{1}_{|y|<b}):H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
which is self-adjoint with
\sigma_{ess}(L_{0,\lambda}^{k})\subset[k^{2}(1-\lambda),\infty)
. Thus
0\in\rho(L_{0,\lambda}^{k})
if and only if
L_{0,\lambda}^{k}\phi=0
for some
\phi\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
implies that
\phi=0
. In other words: we need to rule out that
L_{0,\lambda}^{k}
has a zero eigenvalue. This can be seen from Lemma
A.6
in the Appendix if we set
\alpha=0
(no delta potential in the equation) and
\mu=0
, i.e.,
\tilde{\lambda}=\lambda
and
\tilde{\beta}=\beta
. Moreover, we need to make the obvious changes
\sqrt{1-\beta}=\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\beta-1}
and
\sinh(\mathrm{i}x)=\mathrm{i}\sin(x)
,
\cosh(\mathrm{i}x)=\cos(x)
. Following the ansatz (
A.1
) for the eigenfunction we obtain
c_{0}=d_{0}
and
c_{1}=d_{1}
due to the
C^{1}
-matching at
x=0
. Moreover, the choice of
\sqrt{\beta-1}b=\pi
results in the invertible matrices

M_{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-e^{-k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}b}\\
\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}(-1)^{k}&\pm\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}e^{-k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}b}\end{pmatrix}.

Hence the conclusion
c_{1}=-d_{1}
from Lemma
A.6
holds and leads to
c_{1}=d_{1}=0
. An inspection of the
C^{1}
-compatibility at
y=\pm b
then yields
c_{2}=d_{2}=c_{0}=d_{0}=0
. Therefore, there is no zero-eigenvalue of
L_{0,\lambda}^{k}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\lambda\in(-\infty,1)
and
(\tilde{L}1)
holds.

Concerning
(\tilde{L}2)
we need to study a zero-eigenvalue of
L_{\lambda}^{k}
. The answer is again given by Lemma
A.6
in the Appendix since we already know the invertibility of the matrices
M_{\pm}
. Hence the eigenvalue condition is given by (
5.2
) with the obvious changes from the hyperbolic functions to the trigonometric function and reads

\frac{k\alpha}{\sqrt{\beta-1}}=\frac{-\sqrt{\beta-1}\sin(k\sqrt{\beta-1}b)+\sqrt{1-\lambda}\cos(k\sqrt{\beta-1}b)}{\sqrt{\beta-1}\cos(k\sqrt{\beta-1}b)+\sqrt{1-\lambda}\sin(k\sqrt{\beta-1}b)}.

In view of
\sqrt{\beta-1}b=\pi
this reduces to

\alpha=\frac{2\sqrt{1-\lambda}}{k}

and hence
L_{\lambda}^{k}
has a zero-eigenvalue if and only if
k=k_{*}
and
\lambda=\lambda_{*}
. Thus
(\tilde{L}2)
holds.
Finally, in order to verify
(\tilde{L}3)
, we compute the function
\phi_{k}
which solve
L_{\lambda}^{k}\phi_{k}=0
on
(0,\infty)
with
\phi_{k}(0)=1
. From Lemma
A.6
we obtain

\begin{cases}\phi_{k}(y,\lambda)=\cos(k\sqrt{\beta-1}y)+\mathrm{i}c_{1}\sin(k\sqrt{\beta-1}y),&\qquad y\in[0,b],\\
\phi_{k}(y,\lambda)=c_{2}e^{-k\sqrt{1-\lambda}y},&\qquad y\geq b\\
\end{cases}

with
c_{1}=\frac{-\sqrt{1-\lambda}}{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\beta-1}}
and
c_{2}=e^{k\sqrt{1-\lambda}b}(-1)^{k}
. Computing the
L^{2}
-norm of
\phi_{k}
we find that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\phi_{k}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{b}\left(\cos(k\sqrt{\beta-1}y)-\frac{\sqrt{1-\lambda}}{\sqrt{\beta-1}}\sin(k\sqrt{\beta-1}y)\right)^{2}\,dy+\int_{b}^{\infty}e^{2k\sqrt{1-\lambda}(b-y)}\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k\sqrt{1-\lambda}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1-\lambda}{\beta-1}+1\right)b\leq C\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right),

where the constant
C>0
is independent of
k
and can be chosen uniformly for
\lambda
sufficiently close to
\lambda_{*}
. This shows the validity of
(\tilde{L}3)
.

The last case to be considered is
\beta=1
. Also here, we are left to verify conditions
(\tilde{L}1)
,
(\tilde{L}2)
, and
(\tilde{L}3)
. First we find that in this case with
\tilde{\lambda}=\lambda
and
\tilde{\beta}=\beta=1
condition (
5.2
) is replaced by

(7.1)

\frac{k\alpha}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{1-\lambda}}{1+\sqrt{1-\lambda}kb},

which follows from a suitable adaptation of Lemma
A.6
. A zero eigenvalue of
L_{0,\lambda}^{k}
correspond to values
k,\lambda
satisfying (
7.1
) with
\alpha=0
which is impossible. Since
\sigma_{ess}(L_{0,\lambda}^{k})=[k^{2}(1-\lambda),\infty)
this shows that
(\tilde{L}1)
holds. If we recall the definition of
\alpha
, i.e.,

\alpha=\frac{2\sqrt{1-\lambda_{*}}}{k_{*}(1+\sqrt{1-\lambda_{*}}k_{*}b)}

and compare with the
0
-eigenvalue condition (
7.1
) we see that this ensures that
0
is a (simple) eigenvalue of
L_{\lambda}^{k}
if and only if
\lambda=\lambda_{*}
and
k=k_{*}
. Hence,
(\tilde{L}2)
holds. To see
(\tilde{L}3)
we compute (also with the help of an adaptation of Lemma
A.6
) that the functions
\phi_{k}
solving
L_{\lambda}^{k}\phi_{k}=0
on
(0,\infty)
with
\phi_{k}(0)=1
are given by

\begin{cases}\phi_{k}(y,\lambda)=1+c_{1}y,&\qquad y\in[0,b],\\
\phi_{k}(y,\lambda)=c_{2}e^{-k\sqrt{1-\lambda}y},&\qquad y\geq b\\
\end{cases}

with
c_{1}=\frac{-\sqrt{1-\lambda}k}{kb\sqrt{1-\lambda}+1}
and
c_{2}=\frac{e^{k\sqrt{1-\lambda}b}}{kb\sqrt{1-\lambda}+1}
. From this we directly calculate that
\|\phi_{k}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=O(1)
as
k\to\infty
uniformly for
\lambda
sufficiently close to
\lambda_{*}
. Hence,
(\tilde{L}3)
holds.

Now, Corollary
2.14
follows from Theorem
2.10
.

Appendix A
Auxiliary results

Lemma A.1
.

Let
A(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}a_{k}\sin(kx)
, then

A^{3}(x)=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}(a*a*a)_{k}\sin(kx),

where
a=(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
is an infinite sequence with
a_{k}=-a_{-k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. The notation
(a*a*a)_{k}
is used to denote the
k
-th entry in the sequence obtained by convolution
a*a*a
.

Proof.

If
a
is a sequence as above then using
a_{k}=-a_{-k}
for all
k
we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{i}a_{k}\right)e^{\mathrm{i}kx}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{2}a_{k}\sin(kx)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}a_{k}\sin(kx)=A(x),

and

A^{3}(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{8}\mathrm{i}(a*a*a)_{k}e^{\mathrm{i}kx}.

We are going to show that the Fourier coefficients
(a*a*a)_{k}
are odd with respect to
k
. Notice first that

\displaystyle(a*a*a)_{k}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k-j-l}a_{l}\right)a_{j}.

We also have that

\displaystyle(a*a*a)_{-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{-k-j-l}a_{l}\right)a_{j}=-\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k+j+l}a_{l}\right)a_{j}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k-j-l}a_{-l}\right)a_{-j}=-\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k-j-l}a_{l}\right)a_{j}=-(a*a*a)_{k}.

From this we deduce that

A^{3}(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{8}\mathrm{i}(a*a*a)_{k}e^{\mathrm{i}kx}=-\frac{1}{8}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(a*a*a)_{k}\sin(xk)=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}(a*a*a)_{k}\sin(xk).

∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
e^{k_{*}}
be a sequence such that
e^{k_{*}}_{k}=0
if
k\neq\pm k_{*}
,
e^{k_{*}}_{k_{*}}=-e^{k_{*}}_{-k_{*}}=1

(e^{k_{*}}*e^{k_{*}}*e^{k_{*}})_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}1,&\mbox{if}&k=3k_{*},\\

-3,&\mbox{if}&k=k_{*},\\

The convolution

\displaystyle(e^{k_{*}}*e^{k_{*}}*e^{k_{*}})_{k}

∎

Lemma A.5
.

Let
L=-\frac{d^{2}}{dy}+q(y)
with
q\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be a self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with domain
D(L)=H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that
L_{\alpha}:=L+\alpha\delta_{0}
is self-adjoint with domain
D(L_{\alpha})=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap\left(H^{2}(0,\infty)\cup H^{2}(-\infty,0)\right)\mid u^{\prime}(0_{+})-u^{\prime}(0_{-})=-\alpha u(0)\}
. Moreover for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
the following holds:

(i)

For sufficiently large
\mu>0
we have that
(L_{\alpha}+\mu)^{-1}:H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is bounded.

(ii)

\sigma_{ess}(L_{\alpha})=\sigma_{ess}(L)
.

Proof.

A proof for the self-adjointness of
L_{\alpha}
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is given in
[
4
]
. For (i) we first note that
L_{\alpha}
is a semi-bounded self-adjoint operator so that
L_{\alpha}+\mu
is a positive operator for
\mu>0
sufficiently large. Its bilinear form
b_{L_{\alpha}+\mu}:H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
is coercive and equivalent to the standard
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
-inner product. Therefore, any
f\in H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
can be represented by a unique
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
b_{L_{\alpha}+\mu}(u,\phi)=f(\phi)
for any
\phi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
b_{L_{\alpha}+\mu}(u,u)=\|\phi\|_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}
. This proves (i).

For (ii) we may take
\lambda<0
sufficiently negative such that
\lambda\in\rho(L)\cap\rho(L_{\alpha})\cap\mathbb{R}
since both
L,L_{\alpha}
are semi-bounded from below. Using (i) we may also assume
\lambda
sufficiently negative that
(\lambda-L_{\alpha})^{-1}:H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is bounded. By Weyl’s criterion it is sufficient to show that the operator
W_{\lambda}:=(\lambda-L)^{-1}-(\lambda-L_{\alpha})^{-1}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is compact in order to prove the statement. Since

W_{\lambda}=(\lambda-L_{\alpha})^{-1}\circ((\lambda-L_{\alpha})(\lambda-L)^{-1}-\Id)=\underbrace{(\lambda-L_{\alpha})^{-1}}_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\circ\underbrace{(L-L_{\alpha})}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\to H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})}\circ\underbrace{(\lambda-L)^{-1}}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}

and since
L-L_{\alpha}=-\alpha\delta_{0}:H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\to H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
is a bounded operator with 1-dimensional range spanned by
\delta_{0}
we see that
W_{\lambda}
is indeed compact. This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.6
.

Let
\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{\beta}<1
. Then the eigenvalue problem (
5.1
) is solvable for
\phi\in D(L_{\lambda}^{k})
if and only if (
5.2
) holds. In this case the eigenspace is one-dimensional.

Proof.

Solutions of the differential equation in (
5.1
) have to be of the form

(A.1)

\phi(y,\lambda)=\begin{cases}c_{2}e^{-k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}y},&\qquad y\geq b,\\
c_{0}\cosh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}y)+c_{1}\sinh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}y),&\qquad y\in[0,b],\\
d_{0}\cosh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}y)+d_{1}\sinh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}y),&\qquad y\in[-b,0],\\
d_{2}e^{k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}y},&\qquad y\leq-b\end{cases}

with
C^{1}
-compatibility conditions at
x=\pm b
and continuity at
x=0
. The latter implies
c_{0}=d_{0}
and the condition
\phi^{\prime}(0+)-\phi^{\prime}(0-)+k^{2}\alpha\phi(0)=0
at

(A.2)

k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}(c_{1}-d_{1})+k^{2}\alpha c_{0}=0.

The

-compatibility leads to the following set of four equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}c_{0}\cosh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)+c_{1}\sinh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)&=c_{2}e^{-k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}b},\\
\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}\bigl(c_{0}\sinh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)+c_{1}\cosh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)\bigr)&=-\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}c_{2}e^{-k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}b},\\

\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}\bigl(-d_{0}\sinh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)+d_{1}\cosh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)\bigr)&=\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}d_{2}e^{-k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}b}.\end{cases}

These four equations can be written as

\begin{pmatrix}M_{+}&0\\
0&M_{-}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\\
d_{1}\\
d_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-c_{0}\cosh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)\\
-\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}c_{0}\sinh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)\\
-c_{0}\cosh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)\\
\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}c_{0}\sinh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)\end{pmatrix}

with

M_{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}\pm\sinh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)&-e^{-k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}b}\\
\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}\cosh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)&\pm\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}e^{-k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}b}\end{pmatrix}.

Since both
M_{+}
and
M_{-}
are invertible we see that w.l.o.g. we can choose
c_{0}=1
. Moreover, the structure of the linear systems yields that
c_{1}=-d_{1}
and
c_{2}=d_{2}
. Finally, solving for

we get

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}\sinh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)+\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}\cosh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)}{\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}\cosh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)+\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}\sinh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)},

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=e^{k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}b}(c_{1}\sinh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)+\cosh(k\sqrt{1-\tilde{\beta}}b)).

Inserting
c_{1}
,
d_{1}=-c_{1}
and
c_{0}=1
into (
A.2
) yields the condition (
5.2
) as claimed. It also shows that the eigenspace is one-dimensional (the only degree of freedom is the choice of
c_{0}
which we took to be
1
w.l.o.g.).
∎
