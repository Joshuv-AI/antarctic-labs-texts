On the derivation of the Kompaneets equation
Guilherme Eduardo Freire Oliveira, Christian Maes, Kasper Meerts

Abstract
The relaxation of a photon bath to thermal equilibrium via Compton scattering with electrons is described in the Kompaneets equation (1956). The equation is mostly known from studies of astrophysical plasmas, for its convergence to the Planck distribution and for possible corrections to that Planck law in the cosmic microwave background, most notably from the Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect. We revisit its derivation emphasizing its structure as a Kramers-Moyal diffusion approximation to the quantum Boltzmann equation or Master equation with stimulated emission. We do not assume that the Planck law is stationary in performing the continuum approximation but we emphasize the necessity of the flux or Møller factor to arrive at a continuity equation. On the other hand, the structure allows more general assumptions than originally envisioned by Kompaneets.

2
Detailed balance with stimulated emission

Pauli was probably the first to dynamically characterize thermal equilibrium
for photons in an electron bath, identifying in 1923 the condition of detailed
balance in the Master equation describing scattering and recovering the Planck
law for its stationary distribution
[
24
]
.

Transition rates for a jump process arising within a quantum many-particle
system are derived from one-particle Green’s functions
[
25
]
. Here we
take a photonic set up with a symmetrized Fock space taking the tensor product
over three-dimensional harmonic oscillators with wave vector
{\mathbf{k}}
corresponding
to frequency
\omega
. An elementary transition is the annihilation of a
photon with wave vector
{\mathbf{k}}
while creating a photon with wave vector
{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
.
When the photons are in weak contact with a thermal bath at inverse temperature
\beta
, each transition creates a flux in reciprocal space with an
expectation given by

j({\mathbf{k}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})=\alpha({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\,e^{\beta(\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega^{\prime})/2}\,\big|\langle\text{f}|a^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}a_{\mathbf{k}}|\text{i}\rangle\big|^{2}

(4)

where
\alpha({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})=\alpha({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},{\mathbf{k}})
is
symmetric and left unspecified for the moment as a parameter of dynamical
activity. We have taken that
to lowest order the matrix element will contain a single term annihilating and creating a photon
a^{\dagger}_{{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}a_{\mathbf{k}}
. Writing
n({\mathbf{k}})
for the occupation/level at wave vector
{\mathbf{k}}
, we put

|\text{i}\rangle=|\ldots n({\mathbf{k}})\ldots n({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\ldots\rangle

for the initial state. The only non-zero matrix element will between that initial
|i\rangle
and
the final state

|\text{f}\rangle=|\ldots n({\mathbf{k}})-1\ldots n({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})+1\ldots\rangle

Since on each of the Hilbert spaces
a|n\rangle=\sqrt{n}|n-1\rangle
and
a^{\dagger}|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle
, we find that

\left|\langle\text{f}\,|a^{\dagger}_{{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}a_{\mathbf{k}}|\text{i}\rangle\right|^{2}=(1+n({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}))\,n({\mathbf{k}})

Therefore (
4
) becomes

\displaystyle j({\mathbf{k}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\,w({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\,n({\mathbf{k}})

(5)

\displaystyle w({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\beta(\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega^{\prime})/2}\,(1+n({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}))

In classic texts on Markov processes, (
5
) makes the sink term into
{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
from
{\mathbf{k}}
and one would write a Master equation for the probability of occupying the various wave vectors. The source term is

j({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{k}})
. Ignoring however correlations between the occupations at different wave vectors, we can write the Master equation directly for the (now expected) occupation numbers

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}n_{t}({\mathbf{k}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}\alpha({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\,\big[e^{\beta(\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega^{\prime})/2}\,(1+n_{t}({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}))n_{t}({\mathbf{k}})-e^{\beta(\hbar\omega^{\prime}-\hbar\omega)/2}\,(1+n_{t}({\mathbf{k}}))n_{t}({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\big]

(6)

As such, the evolution equation (
6
) does not need to be photon number-preserving, i.e., it does not follow for free that

\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}n_{t}({\mathbf{k}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}\alpha({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})[w({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},{\mathbf{k}})n_{t}({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})-w({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})n_{t}({\mathbf{k}})]=0

(7)

unless
\alpha({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})=\alpha({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},{\mathbf{k}})
is
indeed symmetric. Only then, (
6
) is a continuity equation.

Secondly, detailed balance requires that
j({\mathbf{k}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})-j({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{k}})=0
for all
{\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
, or (for symmetric
\alpha({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})
always)

\displaystyle w({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\,n({\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},{\mathbf{k}})\,n({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\;\;\text{ or }

\displaystyle e^{\beta(\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega^{\prime})/2}\,(1+n({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}))\,n({\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\beta(\hbar\omega^{\prime}-\hbar\omega)/2}\,(1+n({\mathbf{k}}))\,n({\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\implies e^{\beta\hbar\omega}\,\frac{n({\mathbf{k}})}{1+n({\mathbf{k}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{ constant\,}=e^{\beta\mu}

(8)

which implies that

n({\mathbf{k}})=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega}-1}

by assuming that the chemical potential equals zero,
\mu=0
. Note that we have only used (
5
) for an environment in thermal equilibrium where the temperature may refer to an electron gas or anything else. The “anything else” would solely show in the prefactor
\alpha({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})
for the kinetics (
6

In that sense the evolution given by (
6

Kompaneets equation, before any diffusion approximation.

Kramers-Moyal expansion

We can indeed redo that in three dimensions, on

\omega^{\prime}-\omega=\frac{c\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}}-\mathbf{\hat{n}})-\hbar\omega(1-\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}})}{\gamma m_{e}c^{2}\left[1-\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}}/\gamma m_{e}c+(\hbar\omega/\gamma m_{e}c^{2})(1-\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}})\right]}\,\omega

For better understanding and comparison, we add here the heuristics of the opposite situation where we consider an electron in a photon bath (in contact with other matter at temperature

E^{1/2}\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{N}}{\partial t}(E,t)=b\,\frac{\partial}{\partial E}E^{3/2}\left\{k_{B}T\frac{\partial\mathcal{N}}{\partial E}(E,t)+\mathcal{N}(E,t)\right\}

equation can be found in

. For applications to highly dense states of fermionic matter, the Pauli exclusion is significant and departures from (

There are various well-known extensions of the Kompaneets equation compared to what was mainly discussed in the previous sections. For example, the condition that the photons are soft,

7
Conclusions and outlook

Relaxation to quantum equilibrium distributions is a topic of much current interest. In that spirit, the Kompaneets equation for the Comptonization of photons in an electron bath is one of the few examples where a diffusion approximation to the Boltzmann equation can be explicitly done, in such way that we found useful to revisit its derivation.

First, we connected the derivation with statistical mechanical setups, known for example as Kramers-Moyal expansion and clarified a number of points related to the importance of using the physically correct scattering cross sections together with an
ab initio
correct Boltzmann equation (
26
). We explored and stressed many features which are absent or commonly neglected in literature, such as the appearance of the Møller velocity in the Boltzmann collision term.

Secondly, the re-derivations in the present paper may serve as useful point of departure to understand modifications and nonequilibrium corrections to the Planck law. Indeed, one of the goals of the present paper has been to derive the Kompaneets equation without resorting to the Kompaneets’ approach based on the current vanishing at equilibrium. We found that diffusion in frequency space, with drift determined by exchanges in energy and possibly nonconservative processes combined with stimulated emission, leads to an extension of the Kompaneets equation. Such extensions will be used in future work to motivate specific low-frequency modifications to the Planck law, following the scenario and motivation in
[
23
]
.

5

\sigma=\frac{1}{-p^{\prime}\cdot k^{\prime}}\frac{1}{E^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}}\frac{3\sigma_{T}}{16\pi}\int\textrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}^{\prime}\textrm{d}\omega^{\prime}\textrm{d}\Omega\omega^{\prime 2}\delta^{(4)}(p^{\prime}+k^{\prime}-p-k)\overline{X}

(48)

where
\overline{X}
is the scattering amplitude. For a proof of this relation we refer the reader to
[
33
,
52
,
53
]
.
By differentiating with respect to the solid angle and using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus we obtain the differential cross section

\frac{\textrm{d}\sigma}{\textrm{d}\Omega}=\frac{1}{-p^{\prime}\cdot k^{\prime}}\frac{1}{E^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}}\frac{3\sigma_{T}}{16\pi}\int\textrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}^{\prime}\textrm{d}\omega^{\prime}\omega^{\prime 2}\delta^{(4)}(p^{\prime}+k^{\prime}-p-k)\overline{X}

this is the frame-independent representation of the differential cross section, where the frame-independent representation of the scattering angle is given by
\cos\theta=\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n^{\prime}}}
. By evaluating the integrals we get the following expression for the Klein-Nishina differential cross section

\frac{\textrm{d}\sigma}{\textrm{d}\Omega}=\frac{3\sigma_{T}}{16\pi}\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\omega}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}})^{2}}\overline{X}

(49)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\mathbf{v}/c
(or
\mathbf{v}
in natural units). The outgoing photon frequency
\omega^{\prime}
is dependent from the ingoing frequency by the Compton shift (
18
), which can be expressed in the following way

\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\omega}=\frac{1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}}{1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}}+\frac{\omega}{E}(1-\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}})}

(50)

The scattering amplitude is given by

\overline{X}=\frac{p\cdot k}{p\cdot k^{\prime}}+\frac{p\cdot k^{\prime}}{p\cdot k}+2\left(\frac{m^{2}}{p\cdot k^{\prime}}-\frac{m^{2}}{p\cdot k}\right)+\left(\frac{m^{2}}{p\cdot k^{\prime}}-\frac{m^{2}}{p\cdot k}\right)^{2}

(51)

Substitute (
50
) in (
49
) to yield

\frac{\textrm{d}\sigma}{\textrm{d}\Omega}=\frac{3\sigma_{T}}{16\pi}\left[\frac{1}{\gamma(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}}+\frac{\omega}{E}(1-\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n^{\prime}}}))}\right]^{2}\overline{X}

(52)

In (
51
), using
p\cdot k^{(^{\prime})}=-E\omega^{(^{\prime})}(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{(^{\prime})}})
we have

\displaystyle\frac{p\cdot k}{p\cdot k^{\prime}}+\frac{p\cdot k^{\prime}}{p\cdot k}=1+\frac{\omega(1-\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}})}{E(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n^{\prime}}})}+\frac{1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}}}{1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}+\frac{\omega}{E}(1-\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle\frac{m^{2}}{p\cdot k^{\prime}}-\frac{m^{2}}{p\cdot k}=-\frac{1-\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}}}{\gamma^{2}(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}})(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}})}

Substituting both expressions into (
51
) yields

\overline{X}=1+\left[1-\frac{(1-\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}})}{\gamma^{2}(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}})(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}})}\right]^{2}+\frac{\omega^{2}(1-\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}})^{2}}{E^{2}(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}})(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}}+\frac{\omega}{E}(1-\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}}))}

(53)

Putting back (
53
) into (
52
) we use

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\frac{\mathbf{p}}{E}

to yield the frame-independent representation of the Klein-Nishina cross section (
5
).

\int\textrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}\,{\mathcal{N}}_{\text{eq}}(|\mathbf{p}|)=n_{e}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}})^{2}=p^{2}_{x}n^{2}_{x}+p^{2}_{y}n^{2}_{y}+p^{2}_{z}n^{2}_{z}+\ \mathrm{cross\ terms\ in\ coordinates\ \ (similarly\ for\ }\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}})(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}^{\prime}})=p^{2}_{x}n_{x}n^{\prime}_{x}+p^{2}_{y}n_{y}n^{\prime}_{y}+p^{2}_{z}n_{z}n^{\prime}_{z}+\ \mathrm{similar\ to\ above}

Cross terms in the coordinates yield zero contribution for the same reason as (

\displaystyle\int\textrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}\,(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}})^{2}{\mathcal{N}}_{\text{eq}}(|\mathbf{p}|)=I\times{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}^{2}=I

Using all that in
