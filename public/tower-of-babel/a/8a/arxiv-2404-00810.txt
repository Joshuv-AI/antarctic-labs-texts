Off-the-grid regularisation for Poisson inverse problems
Marta Lazzaretti, Claudio Estatico, Alejandro Melero, Luca Calatroni

Abstract
Off-the-grid regularisation has been extensively employed over the last decade in the context of ill-posed inverse problems formulated in the continuous setting of the space of Radon measures ℳ ⁡ ( Ω ) {\mathcal{M}(\Omega)} . These approaches enjoy convexity and counteract the discretisation biases as well the numerical instabilities typical of their discrete counterparts. In the framework of sparse reconstruction of discrete point measures (sum of weighted Diracs), a Total Variation regularisation norm in ℳ ⁡ ( Ω ) {\mathcal{M}(\Omega)} is typically combined with an L 2 L^{2} data term modelling additive Gaussian noise. To assess the framework of off-the-grid regularisation in the presence of signal-dependent Poisson noise, we consider in this work a variational model where Total Variation regularisation is coupled with a Kullback-Leibler data term under a non-negativity constraint. Analytically, we study the optimality conditions of the composite functional and analyse its dual problem. Then, we consider an homotopy strategy to select an optimal regularisation parameter and use it within a Sliding Frank-Wolfe algorithm. Several numerical experiments on both 1D/2D/3D simulated and real 3D fluorescent microscopy data are reported.



keywords
Off-the-grid sparse regularisation, Poisson noise, Sliding Frank-Wolfe, fluorescence microscopy imaging.

Introduction

Discrete (or
on-the-grid
) sparse optimisation approaches are nowadays established techniques in the field of mathematical signal and image inverse problems. In the context of linear ill-posed inverse problems, for instance, they aim at retrieving a sparse approximation of a quantity of interest
x
(e.g., an image) from blurred, noisy and potentially incomplete acquisitions
y\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
on a regular grid of size
N\geq M
, see Fig.
1(a)
. The discretisation parameter
N
determines the localisation precision, as only the centres of the discretisation intervals are candidates for reconstruction. As such, in case of rough discretisations, very approximate reconstructions may be computed (see, e.g., Fig.
1(b)
where small values
M=N
are used). To obtain higher precision, one typically chooses a grid-size parameter
N>M
for reconstruction, as in Fig.
1(c)
.
Choosing a large value for
N
, however, may cause instabilities in the reconstructions
Duval and
Peyré (2017a)
due to the higher numerical complexity.

Off-the-grid
approaches aim at overcoming such difficulties. They can be thought indeed as the natural framework to deal with the case
N\to+\infty
of
on-the-grid
formulations
Duval and
Peyré (2017b)
;
Laville et al. (2021)
. In such framework, the spatial domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is not discretised by a regular grid, but, rather, the quantity of interest is modelled as an element of a suitable functional space defined on
\Omega
. A natural framework for spike reconstruction problems, is, for instance, the space of Radon measures
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
where the quantity of interest can be modelled as the measure
\mu_{a,x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
with
n
being the number of spikes and where the question is therefore how to estimate the number of spikes
n
, and then how to retrieve both continuous positions
x_{i}\in\Omega
and amplitudes
a_{i}\geq 0
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Inverse problems in the space of measures and off-the-grid optimisation methods have been first proposed in
Scherzer and Walch (2009)
;
Bredies and
Pikkarainen (2013)
;
De Castro and
Gamboa (2012)
;
Fernandez-Granda (2013)
and since then they have been a topic of intense research activity for the mathematical community, both from an analytical and numerical viewpoint, see, e.g.,
Duval and Peyré (2013)
;
Denoyelle et al. (2017)
;
Boyer et al. (2017)
;
Poon and Peyré (2019)
;
Denoyelle et al. (2019)
;
Flinth et al. (2021)
;
Bénard et al. (2024)
. Off-the-grid methods have been proved to be particularly useful in applications where fine-scale details need to be retrieved from noisy acquisitions, such as spike detection in astronomy and microscopy
Denoyelle et al. (2019)
, as well as parameter estimation in spectroscopy
Dossal et al. (2017)
and density mixture estimation
De Castro et al. (2021)
.
Standard approaches in this setting usually combine off-the-grid regularisation with an additive (Gaussian) noise modelling on the underlying signal, which is in general easier to work with, from both an analytical and a computational point of view. Such modelling corresponds to the well-studied variational formulation of the Beurling LASSO (BLASSO) model
Bredies and
Pikkarainen (2013)
;
Fernandez-Granda (2013)
. Beyond Gaussian noise models, we also mention the recent work
Pouchol and Verdier (2024)
, where a study on the singularity of minimisers for general divergences defined on
\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
under non-negativity constraints and no further explicit regularisation is carried out, along with numerical validations on exemplar medical imaging problems.

In this work, we consider an off-the-grid modelling in
{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
under the specific modelling assumption of signal-dependent Poisson noise in the data. This choice is motivated by some particular biological applications of interest, such as fluorescence microscopy, where, due to the photon emission nature of the light, Poisson noise is better suited than the Gaussian one to describe the process of photon counts on acquired images
Bertero et al. (2018)
. While in a discrete setting, a precise modelling of noise statistics if often not necessary due to the inevitable biases introduced by the choice of the regularisation employed, a natural question is whether whenever a refined off-the-grid regularisation is used, a precise noise model could indeed be relevant.

1.1
Inverse problems in the space of Radon measures
{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}

In this section we recall some important definitions and properties of the space of Radon measures
{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
following
Poon (2019)
;
Denoyelle et al. (2019)
;
Laville et al. (2021)
. For more details on Radon measures see
Rudin (1987)
;
Cohn (2013)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\geq 1
, be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with non-empty interior. We denote by
\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
the space of real continuous functions
\psi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on
\Omega
.
The space of Radon measures can be defined through duality, see
Bredies and
Pikkarainen (2013)
;
Denoyelle et al. (2019)
;
Denoyelle (2018)
;
Javanshiri and
Nasr-Isfahani (2013)
.

Definition 1
.

The Banach space
{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
of real signed Radon measures on
\Omega
is the topological dual of
\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
endowed with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,\Omega}
, defined by
\|\psi\|_{\infty,\Omega}:=\sup_{x\in\Omega}|\psi(x)|
.

This definition allows to characterise any measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
as a continuous linear form evaluated on continuous functions
\psi\in\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
in terms of the duality pairing, so that

(\forall\mu\in{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}),\qquad\langle\psi,\mu\rangle_{\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})}\times{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}\psi\mathrm{d}\mu.

(1)

A Radon measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
is a positive measure if
\langle\psi,\mu\rangle_{\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})}\times{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
is non-negative for any non-negative function
\psi\in\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. This specifies the meaning of the term
signed
in the above definition, as the quantity
\langle\psi,\mu\rangle_{\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})}\times{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
can be also negative.

It can be shown that
{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
is a non-reflexive Banach space endowed with the Total Variation (TV) norm, here defined for all
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
as:

|\mu|(\Omega)=\sup\Big(\int_{\Omega}\psi\mathrm{d}\mu\penalty\ \Big|\penalty\ \ \psi\in\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}),\ \|\psi\|_{\infty,\Omega}\leq 1\Big).

(2)

The TV norm is convex and lower semi-continuous with respect to the weak* topology, hence its subdifferential is nonempty and defined as
Bredies and
Pikkarainen (2013)
;
Duval and Peyré (2013)

\partial|\mu|(\Omega)=\left\{\psi\in\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})|\ \|\psi\|_{\infty,\Omega}\leq 1\text{ and }\int_{\Omega}\psi\mathrm{d}\mu=|\mu|(\Omega)\right\}.

(3)

Note that for sparse discrete measures, i.e. weighted sums of Diracs

\mu_{a,x}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}\text{ with }N\in\mathbb{N},\ a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\ x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\Omega^{N},

(4)

with
x_{i}\not=x_{j}
for
i\not=j
,
the TV norm coincides with the
L^{1}
norm of the amplitudes vector
a
, that is
|\mu_{a,x}|(\Omega)=\|a\|_{1}
.
This explains why the TV norm is considered in this setting as a generalisation of the
L^{1}
norm. Moreover, in this special case one has
\,\langle\psi,\mu\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\psi(x_{i})\,
and the subdifferential has the following expression involving the sign function

\partial|\mu_{a,x}|(\Omega)=\left\{\psi\in\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})|\ \|\psi\|_{\infty,\Omega}\leq 1,\ \forall i=1,\ldots,N\ \psi(x_{i})=\operatornamewithlimits{sign}(a_{i})\right\}.

(5)

We focus now on the formulation of linear inverse problems in
{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
. As an acquisition space we will consider a functional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
Let
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
be the unknown source measure. We consider an acquisition
\bar{y}\in\mathcal{H}
being the result of the action of the forward operator
\Phi:{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
evaluated on
\mu
, i.e.
\bar{y}=\Phi\mu
. The forward operator
\Phi:{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
is defined in terms of a a continuous and bounded measurement kernel
\varphi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
, that is:

\Phi\mu:=\int_{\Omega}\varphi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

(6)

Note that this integral should not be confused with the concept of duality pairing (
1
), which is a scalar function defined as the integral over
\Omega
of a continuous real function with respect to a measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
. In (
6
), the integral is then a Bochner integral
Cohn (2013)
for vector-valued functions. Indeed,
\varphi
is not a continuous real function in
\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
, but rather
\varphi:x\in\Omega\mapsto\varphi(x)=\varphi_{x}(\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}
is a map from
\Omega
to
\mathcal{H}
. This implies that
\varphi(x)
is not a real value but an element of
\mathcal{H}
. The integral is well-defined (as a Bochner integral) if
\varphi
is continuous and bounded
Chizat and Bach (2018)
;
Denoyelle et al. (2019)
.

In the following, we consider
{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
with the weak* topology and its dual space
\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. In this setting, the forward operator
\Phi
is weak*-weak continuous
Denoyelle et al. (2019)
. Thus, it is possible to define the adjoint operator of
\Phi
in the weak* topology, namely the map
\Phi^{*}:\mathcal{H}\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
such that, for all
p\in\mathcal{H}
,
\Phi^{*}p
is the real continuous function defined by

\Phi^{*}p(x):=\Big(x\in\Omega\mapsto\langle p,\varphi_{x}(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{\Omega}p(t)\varphi_{x}(t)\mathrm{d}t\Big)

(7)

for all
x\in\Omega
.

The choice of the kernel
\varphi
and of the acquisition space
\mathcal{H}
depends on the specific physical acquisition process. In the following, we consider a convolution kernel, which is of practical interest in fluorescence microscopy (see, e.g.,
Denoyelle et al. (2019)
;
Koulouri
et al. (2021)
). In this setting, a natural choice is therefore
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\Omega)
, with the convolution kernel
\varphi:\Omega\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
defined in terms of a Point Spread Function (PSF)
\tilde{\varphi}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
acting as:

\varphi_{x}(s):=\tilde{\varphi}(s-x)\in\mathbb{R}\ \forall x,s\in{\Omega}.

(8)

Note that, depending on the microscopy technique used, one can have different PSFs. For instance, the Gaussian PSF, centred in
c\in\Omega
with radius
\sigma>0
, is defined by

s\mapsto\tilde{\varphi}(s-c):={(2\pi\sigma^{2})}^{-d/2}e^{-\|s-c\|_{2}^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}.

(9)

For other possible choices of measurement kernels, we refer the reader to
Denoyelle et al. (2019)
.

We observe that the action of the forward operator
\Phi
on finite linear combination of Diracs (
4
) can be explicited as follows:

\Phi\mu_{a,x}=\int_{\Omega}\varphi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\varphi(x_{i}).

(10)

For simplicity, the following notation will therefore be used in the sequel
\Phi_{x}(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\varphi(x_{i})
to denote
\Phi\mu_{a,x}
.

In fluorescence microscopy imaging applications, the objects of interest are usually images of molecules, i.e. single point-sources emitting fluorescent light. The unknown is therefore well-described by non-negative discrete measures of the form (
4
).
Other possible objects of interest in this field are microtubules, 1-dimensional curve structures
Laville et al. (2023a)
;
Laville et al. (2023b)
, and cells (2-dimensional), that can be modelled as piece-wise constant functions
De Castro et al. (2023)
;
De Castro et al. (2021)
;
De Castro et al. (2024)
. For simplicity, we will focus in the following only on inverse problems aiming at recovering (0-dimensional) measures of the form (
4
). The extension to more general regularisation models is left for future work.

We now present the signal-dependent Poisson modelling studied in this work, which differs from (

Applying the definition of convex conjugate to

\displaystyle g_{t}^{*}(s^{*})

\displaystyle=\sup_{s>0}\penalty\ ss^{*}-g_{t}(s)=\sup_{s>0}\penalty\ ss^{*}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\Big(s-t+t\log(t)-t\log(s)\Big)=

\displaystyle=\sup_{s>0}\penalty\ \underbrace{s\Big(s^{*}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\Big)+\frac{t}{\lambda}\log(s)+\frac{t}{\lambda}-\frac{t}{\lambda}\log(t)}_{h(s)}.

We have two cases:

(i)

If

, then

(ii)

If

\lim_{s\to+\infty}h(s)=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}h(s)=-\infty
. Thus, being

a convex and differentiable function its supremum is attained at

such that

, which can be computed by

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(\hat{s})=s^{*}-\frac{1}{\lambda}+\frac{t}{\lambda\hat{s}}=\frac{\lambda\hat{s}s^{*}-\hat{s}+t}{\lambda\hat{s}}=0

\displaystyle\iff\lambda\hat{s}s^{*}-\hat{s}+t=0\iff\hat{s}=\frac{t}{1-\lambda s^{*}}.

Thus,

g_{t}^{*}(s^{*})=h\Big(\frac{t}{1-\lambda s^{*}}\Big)=-\frac{t}{\lambda}\log(1-\lambda s^{*}).

(27)

Observe that

is well defined since

.

Hence, the convex conjugate

of

-\frac{t}{\lambda}\log(1-\lambda s^{*})&s^{*}<\frac{1}{\lambda}\end{cases}\,.

Lemma 1
.

Let

+\infty&\text{otherwise}\\

Lemma 2
.

For
\psi\in\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
, its convex conjugate

is defined by

F^{*}(\psi)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ \psi(x)\leq 1\quad\forall x\in\Omega\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Proof.

By definition of convex conjugate
Combettes and Pesquet (2011)
;
Rockafellar (1970)
, for any
\psi\in\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
we write

\displaystyle F^{*}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}}\langle\psi,\mu\rangle_{\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\times{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}}-|\mu|(\Omega)-\iota_{{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}}(\mu)

(33)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}}\langle\psi,\mu\rangle_{\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\times{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}}-|\mu|(\Omega)

(34)

\displaystyle\geq\langle\psi,\mu\rangle_{\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\times{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}}-|\mu|(\Omega)\quad\forall\mu\in{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}.

(35)

If there exists

\displaystyle F^{*}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\alpha\psi(\bar{x})-\alpha=\alpha\Big(\psi(\bar{x})-1\Big),

(37)

and the limit for

of the latter inequality yields

.

Assume now

for all

\displaystyle\langle\psi,\mu\rangle_{\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\times{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}}=\int_{\Omega}\psi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq\int_{\Omega}1\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=|\mu|(\Omega)\quad\forall\mu\in{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\langle\psi,\mu\rangle_{\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\times{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}}-|\mu|(\Omega)\leq 0\quad\forall\mu\in{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}}\langle\psi,\mu\rangle_{\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\times{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}}-|\mu|(\Omega)\leq 0\Rightarrow F^{*}(\psi)\leq 0.

Proposition 2

The subdifferential of the penalty term

\partial F(\mu)=\begin{cases}\emptyset&\mu\not\in{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}\\

Proof.

We start by recalling the definition of subdifferential:

\partial F(\mu)=\{\psi\in\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})|F(\bar{\mu})\geq F(\mu)+\langle\psi,\bar{\mu}-\mu\rangle\ \forall\bar{\mu}\in{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}\}.

(54)

If
\mu\not\in{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}

and hence the inequality of (

\partial F(\mu)=\emptyset

We consider now a positive measure

. In this case, the penalty term

reduces to

, for which we write

. Hence, (

\displaystyle\partial F(\mu)

\displaystyle=\{\psi\in\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})|\langle 1,\bar{\mu}\rangle\geq\langle 1,\mu\rangle+\langle\psi,\bar{\mu}-\mu\rangle\ \forall\bar{\mu}\in{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}\}

(55)

\displaystyle=\{\psi\in\mathscr{C}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})|\langle 1,\bar{\mu}-\mu\rangle\geq\langle\psi,\bar{\mu}-\mu\rangle\ \forall\bar{\mu}\in{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Omega)}\}.

(56)

It is easy to deduce that
\psi(x)\leq 1
for all

. Indeed, by taking

with

, we have

1=\int_{\Omega}1\mathrm{d}\delta_{x}=\int_{\Omega}1\mathrm{d}(\bar{\mu}-\mu)=\langle 1,\bar{\mu}-\mu\rangle\geq\langle\psi,\bar{\mu}-\mu\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\psi\mathrm{d}\delta_{x}=\psi(x).

(57)

By taking
\bar{\mu}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\langle 1,-\mu\rangle\geq\langle\psi,-\mu\rangle\iff\langle\psi,\mu\rangle\geq\langle 1,\mu\rangle\iff\int_{\Omega}\psi\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\int_{\Omega}1\mathrm{d}\mu,

for all

\psi(x)=1
on the support of
\mu

We showed that if

then
\psi(x)\leq 1
for every

\psi(x)=1
on the support of

\displaystyle\langle\psi,\bar{\mu}\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\psi\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}\leq\int_{\Omega}1\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}=\langle 1,\bar{\mu}\rangle

\langle\psi,\bar{\mu}-\mu\rangle=\langle\psi,\bar{\mu}\rangle-\langle\psi,\mu\rangle\leq{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\langle 1,\bar{\mu}\rangle-\langle 1,\mu\rangle}.

Conclusions

In this work, we considered sparse inverse problems in the off-the-grid setting of the space of Radon measures
{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
under the assumption of signal-dependent Poisson noise in the measurement. Such choice is motivated by fluorescence microscopy applications, where the noise observed is modelled as Poisson to account for photon-counting processes. First, we designed a variational approach where Total Variation regularisation is coupled with a Kullback-Leibler fidelity term and a non-negativity constraint and derived analytically the optimality and extremality conditions.
Then, we considered the Sliding Frank-Wolfe algorithm as a numerical solver and discussed how to select a good regularisation parameter by means of an algorithmic homotopy strategy.
Finally, we presented several numerical experiments on simulated 1D/2D/3D data to validate the theoretical findings as well as to to compare the proposed approach with the Gaussian analogue.
To conclude, we tested the proposed framework on a 3D real fluorescence microscopy dataset, showing good performance.

In future work we plan to consider more complicated 2D and 3D optical models as the ones in
Denoyelle et al. (2019)
, so as to verify the effectiveness of the approach in more realistic scenarios.
