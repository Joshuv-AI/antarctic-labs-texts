Generalized (co)homology of symmetric quandles over homogeneous Beck modules
Biswadeep Karmakar, Deepanshi Saraf, Mahender Singh

Abstract
. A quandle equipped with a good involution is referred to as symmetric. It is known that the cohomology of symmetric quandles gives rise to strong cocycle invariants for classical and surface links, even when they are not necessarily oriented. In this paper, we introduce the category of symmetric quandle modules and prove that these modules completely determine the Beck modules in the category of symmetric quandles. Consequently, this establishes suitable coefficient objects for constructing appropriate (co)homology theories. We develop an extension theory of modules over symmetric quandles and propose a generalized (co)homology theory for symmetric quandles with coefficients in a homogeneous Beck module, which also recovers the symmetric quandle (co)homology developed by Kamada and Oshiro [Trans. Amer. Math. Soc. (2010)]. Our constructions also apply to symmetric racks. We conclude by establishing an explicit isomorphism between the second cohomology of a symmetric quandle and the first cohomology of its associated group.

1.
Introduction

Within the realm of oriented knot theory, racks and quandles
[
13
,
20
]
, and their cohomological ramifications
[
4
,
8
,
9
]
, have undoubtedly played a remarkable role. Their rich theories, combined with various refinements, have given rise to a plethora of connections spanning Hopf algebras
[
1
]
, mapping class groups
[
6
,
24
]
, Riemannian symmetric spaces
[
19
]
, the Yang–Baxter equation
[
18
]
, Yetter–Drinfeld modules
[
1
]
, and contact geometry
[
17
]
, to name a few.

In knot theory, the existence of an orientation often becomes a crucial prerequisite, particularly in scenarios where one intends to work with the quandle cocycle invariants. The challenge of overcoming the need for an orientation was resolved by Kamada and Oshiro
[
14
,
16
]
, who introduced symmetric quandles and their homology groups. In these works, they defined a quandle cocycle invariant
\Phi_{\theta}
for an unoriented link with respect to a given symmetric quandle 2-cocycle
\theta
. They further showed that
\Phi_{\theta}
agrees with the quandle cocycle invariant
\Phi^{\mathrm{ori}}_{\theta}
of an oriented link when the orientation is arbitrarily assigned. Consequently, when the quandle 2-cocycle
\theta
is cohomologous to a symmetric quandle 2-cocycle, the invariant
\Phi^{\mathrm{ori}}_{\theta}
does not depend on the orientation of the link. An analogous invariant has been defined for unoriented surface links via symmetric quandle 3-cocycles, and similar results have ensued. One of the remarkable consequences of this is enabling us to estimate the minimal triple point numbers of non-orientable surface links.

Using certain symmetric quandles called 4-fold symmetric quandles, an invariant of 3-manifolds has been defined in
[
10
,
21
]
. Furthermore, it has been shown that the Chern–Simons invariant of closed 3-manifolds can be regarded as a quandle cocycle invariant. In
[
12
]
, symmetric quandles have been used to define invariants for spatial graphs and handlebody-links. The aforementioned coupled with further modifications (see, for example
[
5
,
22
,
23
]
) have justified the relevance of symmetric quandles to low dimensional topology.

Inspired by
[
1
,
11
]
, we explore these objects from a categorical perspective enabling us to take a different approach than the already existing ones. As a consequence, we are able to completely determine the Beck modules in the category of symmetric quandles. Introduced by Beck in
[
2
]
, given a category
\mathbf{C}
and an object
X
in
\mathbf{C}
, a Beck module over
X
is an abelian group object in the slice category
\mathbf{C}|_{X}.
Beck modules provide a very general notion of a coefficient module for an associated (co)homology theory. To exemplify their strength, we note that they simultaneously generalise the notion of coefficient modules from group cohomology, Lie algebra cohomology as well as Hochschild cohomology of associative algebras. Once the Beck modules in a category have been identified, it is natural to ponder over a corresponding (co)homology theory. We effectively formulate such a (co)homology theory for symmetric quandles with homogeneous coefficients. We also explore the related extension theory and establish connections with low dimensional group cohomology of associated groups. Similar constructions and results work in the category of racks as well.

It is noteworthy to mention that this generalized (co)homology theory for symmetric quandles with homogeneous coefficients can be conceived in a manner reminiscent of group and Hochschild cohomology in which the coefficient objects admit actions. The significance of this (co)homology theory lies in its potential to yield invariants (for example, for spatial graphs and low dimensional manifolds) that are more comprehensive and capable of capturing additional information compared to the original (co)homology, as initially defined by Kamada and Oshiro
[
16
]
, and which is a specific case where the action is trivial.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we review some basic definitions and key concepts from the theory of symmetric racks and symmetric quandles that will be used in the subsequent sections. Section
3
introduces modules over symmetric racks and symmetric quandles as trunk maps from certain associated trunks to the category of abelian groups, along with some examples. In Section
4
, we describe abelian group objects in the slice category over a fixed symmetric rack. This is achieved by first defining the semi-direct product of a symmetric rack and its module. Theorem
4.4
proves that the category of modules over a symmetric rack
(X,\rho_{X})
is equivalent to the category of abelian group objects in the slice category over
(X,\rho_{X})
. An analogous result for symmetric quandles is deduced in Theorem
4.5
. In Section
5
, we define extensions of symmetric racks and symmetric quandles. Theorem
5.8
establishes that the set of equivalence classes of extensions of a symmetric rack
(X,\rho_{X})
by an
(X,\rho_{X})
-module
\mathscr{F}
are in bijective correspondence with an abelian group
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{SR}}(X,\mathscr{F})
defined via extensions. An analogous result for symmetric quandles is proved in Theorem
5.9
. In Section
6
, after defining the suitable symmetric algebra, we introduce a generalized (co)homology theory for symmetric racks and symmetric quandles. This theory recovers the symmetric quandle (co)homology developed by Kamada and Oshiro
[
16
]
. Finally, in Section
7
, we establish an isomorphism between the second cohomology of a symmetric rack and the first cohomology of its associated group when the coefficients are trivial homogeneous modules.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we review some basic definitions and key concepts required for what follows.

2.1.
Symmetric racks and symmetric quandles

We begin with some basic ideas introduced in
[
16
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(X,*)
be a rack (respectively quandle). A map
\rho_{X}:X\rightarrow X
is called a
good involution
if

(S1)

\rho_{X}
is an involution,
\mathrm{i.e.}
,
\rho_{X}^{2}=\mathrm{id}_{X}
,

(S2)

\rho_{X}(x\ast y)=\rho_{X}(x)\ast y
,

(S3)

x\ast\rho_{X}(y)=x\ast^{-1}y
,

for all
x,y\in X
. The pair
(X,\rho_{X})
is called a
symmetric rack (respectively symmetric quandle)
.

We refer the reader to
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
23
]
for more details on these structures and their applications in knot theory.

Example 2.2
.

The following are some elementary examples:

(1)

If
X
is a trivial quandle, then every involution of
X
is a good involution.

(2)

If
X=\operatorname{Conj}(G)
is the conjugation quandle of a group
G
, then
\rho_{X}:X\rightarrow X
given by
\rho_{X}(x)=x^{-1}
is a good involution.

(3)

If
X=\operatorname{Core}(G)
is the core quandle of a group
G
, then the identity map
\mathrm{id}:X\rightarrow X
is a good involution. Also, if
z\in G
is a central element of order two, then
\rho_{X}:X\rightarrow X
given by
\rho(y)=yz
is another good involution of
X
.

Definition 2.3
.

A
symmetric rack (respectively quandle) homomorphism
is a map
f:(X,\rho_{X})\rightarrow(Y,\rho_{Y})
such that

f(x\ast y)=f(x)\ast f(y)\quad\textrm{and}\quad f(\rho_{X}(x))=\rho_{Y}(f(x))

for all
x,y\in X
. A
symmetric rack (respectively quandle) isomorphism
is a symmetric rack (respectively quandle) homomorphism which is also a bijection.

Analogous to the associated group of a rack (respectively quandle), Kamada and Oshiro defined the associated group of a symmetric rack (respectively quandle)
[
16
, Section 4]
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(X,\rho_{X})
be a symmetric rack (respectively quandle). Then the group

G_{(X,\rho_{X})}=\langle e_{x},x\in X\mid e_{x\ast y}=e_{y}^{-1}e_{x}e_{y},~e_{\rho_{X}(x)}=e_{x}^{-1}\text{ for }x,y\in X\rangle

is called the
associated group
of
(X,\rho_{X})
.

Example 2.5
.

[
16
, Example 2.5]

Let
(X,\rho_{X})
be the fundamental symmetric quandle of an unknot in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
or an unknotted 2-sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. To be precise,
X=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
and
\rho_{X}(x_{1})=x_{2}
. Then the associated group
G_{(X,\rho_{X})}\cong\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
\mathbf{SR}
and
\mathbf{SQ}
denote the categories whose constituent objects are the symmetric racks and symmetric quandles, respectively, with the morphisms as defined above. It is clear that finite direct products exist in both
\mathbf{SR}
and
\mathbf{SQ}
. Further, equalisers exist in both
\mathbf{SR}
and
\mathbf{SQ}
. These are just the set-theoretical equalisers with the induced
\ast
and
\rho_{X}
. Since, both
\mathbf{SR}
and
\mathbf{SQ}
have all binary products and equalisers, they admit all finite limits.
[
3
, Proposition 2.8.2]
.

Let
(X,\rho_{X})
be a fixed symmetric rack and
\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})}
denote the slice category over
(X,\rho_{X})
. More precisely, the objects in
\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})}
are the symmetric rack homomorphisms
f:(Y,\rho_{Y})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
, and a morphism from
f:(Y,\rho_{Y})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
to
g:(Z,\rho_{Z})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
is a symmetric rack homomorphism
h:(Y,\rho_{Y})\rightarrow(Z,\rho_{Z})
such that
g\,h=f
. The slice category over a symmetric quandle is defined analogously.

Since
\mathbf{SR}
and
\mathbf{SQ}
have all finite limits, it follows that
\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})}
and
\mathbf{SQ}|_{(X,\rho_{X})}
also have finite limits
[
3
, using Proposition 2.8.2]
.
In particular, the product of
f:(Y,\rho_{Y})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
and
g:(Z,\rho_{Z})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
in
\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})}
is the pullback

h:(Y\times_{X}Z,\,\rho_{Y\times_{X}Z})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})

of
f
and
g
, where
Y\times_{X}Z=\{(y,z)\in Y\times Z\mid f(y)=g(z)\}
has the usual product rack structure,
\rho_{Y\times_{X}Z}(y,z)=(\rho_{Y}(y),\rho_{Z}(z))
and
h(y,z)=f(y)=g(z)
for all
(y,z)\in Y\times_{X}Z
. The same construction works in
\mathbf{SQ}
. Thus, we can consider group objects in
\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})}
and
\mathbf{SQ}|_{(X,\rho_{X})}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(X,\rho_{X})
be a symmetric rack. Then an
(X,\rho_{X})
-module
is a trunk map
\mathscr{F}=(A,\phi,\psi,\eta):T(X,\rho_{X})\rightarrow\mathbf{Ab}
such that
\phi_{x,y}:A_{x}\rightarrow A_{x\ast y}
is an isomorphism and

(M7)

\psi_{x\ast y,z}(a)=\phi_{x\ast z,y\ast z}\psi_{x,z}(a)+\psi_{x\ast z,y\ast z}\psi_{y,z}(a)
,

(M8)

\phi_{x\ast^{-1}y,y}\psi_{x,\rho_{X}(y)}(\eta_{y}(b))=-\psi_{x\ast\rho_{X}(y),y}(b)

and

.

If

additionally satisfies the condition

\phi_{x,x}(a)+\psi_{x,x}(a)=a

Let

\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}:\mathscr{F}\rtimes X\rightarrow\mathscr{F}\rtimes X
defined by

\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}\big((a,x)\big)=\big(\eta_{x}(a),\rho_{X}(x)\big)

for all
a\in A_{x}
,
b\in A_{y}
and
x,y\in X
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(X,\rho_{X})
be a symmetric rack and
\mathscr{F}=(A,\phi,\psi,\eta)
an
(X,\rho_{X})
-module. Then the semi-direct product
({\mathscr{F}\rtimes X},\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X})
is a symmetric rack. Furthermore, if
(X,\rho_{X})
is a symmetric quandle, then so is
({\mathscr{F}\rtimes X},\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X})
.

Proof.

Note that
{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}
is a rack, as proved in
[
11
, Proposition 2.1]
. It remains to check that
\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}
is a good involution.

•

For (S1), we see that
\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}^{2}(a,x)=\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}(\eta_{x}(a),\rho_{X}(x))=(\eta_{\rho_{X}(x)}(\eta_{x}(a)),\rho_{X}^{2}(x))=(a,x)
, where the last equality follows from the
(X,\rho_{X})
-module axiom
(M3)
.

•

For (S2), we have

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}\big((a,x)\big)\tilde{\ast}(b,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\eta_{x}(a),\rho_{X}(x))\tilde{\ast}(b,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\phi_{\rho_{X}(x),y}(\eta_{x}(a))+\psi_{\rho_{X}(x),y}(b),\rho_{X}(x)\ast y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\eta_{x\ast y}(\phi_{x,y}(a)+\psi_{x,y}(b)),\rho_{X}(x\ast y)),

by
(X,\rho_{X})
-module axioms
(M4)
and
(M5)
, and
\rho_{X}
being a good involution

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}(\phi_{x,y}(a)+\psi_{x,y}(b),x\ast y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}\big((a,x)\tilde{\ast}(b,y)\big).

•

For (S3), we have

\displaystyle(a,x)\tilde{\ast}\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}\big((b,y)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a,x)\tilde{\ast}(\eta_{y}(b),\rho_{X}(y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\phi_{x,\rho_{X}(y)}(a)+\psi_{x,\rho_{X}(y)}(\eta_{y}(b)),x\ast\rho_{X}(y)),

by
(X,\rho_{X})
-module axioms
(M6)
and
(M8)
, and
\rho_{X}
being a good involution

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\phi_{x\ast^{-1}y,y}^{-1}(a-\psi_{x\ast^{-1}y,y}(b)),x\ast^{-1}y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a,x)\tilde{\ast}^{-1}(b,y).

Thus,
\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}
is a good involution, and hence
({\mathscr{F}\rtimes X},\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X})
is a symmetric rack. Finally, suppose that
(X,\rho_{X})
is a symmetric quandle. Then, we have the additional condition
\phi_{x,x}(a)+\psi_{x,x}(a)=a
for all
x\in X
and
a\in A_{x}
. Thus, we obtain

(a,x)\tilde{\ast}(a,x)=\big(\phi_{x,x}(a)+\psi_{x,x}(a),x\big)=(a,x)

for all
x\in X
and
a\in A_{x}
, and hence
({\mathscr{F}\rtimes X},\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X})
is a symmetric quandle.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
(X,\rho_{X})
be a symmetric rack and
\mathscr{F}=(A,\phi,\psi,\eta)
an
(X,\rho_{X})
-module. Then there exists an abelian group object
p:({\mathscr{F}\rtimes X},\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
, which we denote by
\mathcal{T}(\mathscr{F})
, in the slice category over
(X,\rho_{X})
. Further, let
\mathscr{F^{\prime}}=(A^{\prime},\phi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})
be another
(X,\rho_{X})
-module and
f:\mathscr{F}\rightarrow\mathscr{F^{\prime}}
an
(X,\rho_{X})
-map. Then the map
\mathcal{T}(f):\mathscr{F}\rtimes X\rightarrow\mathscr{F^{\prime}}\rtimes X
defined by
\mathcal{T}(f)\big((a,x)\big)=(f_{x}(a),x)
gives a slice morphism. Moreover,
\mathcal{T}:\mathbf{SRMod}_{(X,\rho_{X})}\rightarrow\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})
is a functor.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{F}=(A,\phi,\psi,\eta)
be an
(X,\rho_{X})
-module. Then, we have the semi-direct product
({\mathscr{F}\rtimes X},\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X})
, which is a symmetric rack due to Proposition
4.1
. Let
p:\mathscr{F}\rtimes X\rightarrow X
be the natural projection given as
p(a,x)=x
for
a\in A_{x}
and
x\in X
. Then
p
is a homomorphism of symmetric racks. We claim that
p:\mathscr{F}\rtimes X\rightarrow X
has the canonical structure of an abelian group object. Let

\big((\mathscr{F}\rtimes X)\times_{X}(\mathscr{F}\rtimes X),\rho_{(\mathscr{F}\rtimes X)\times_{X}(\mathscr{F}\rtimes X)}\big)\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})

be the categorical product of
p:\mathscr{F}\rtimes X\rightarrow X
with itself in the slice category over
(X,\rho_{X})
. The symmetric rack structure is defined in the same way as in the cartesian product. We define the morphisms

•

\mu:(\mathscr{F}\rtimes X)\times_{X}(\mathscr{F}\rtimes X)\rightarrow(\mathscr{F}\rtimes X)
given by
((a_{1},x),(a_{2},x))\mapsto(a_{1}+a_{2},x)
,

•

\sigma:X\rightarrow(\mathscr{F}\rtimes X)
given by
x\mapsto(0,x)
,

•

\nu:(\mathscr{F}\rtimes X)\rightarrow(\mathscr{F}\rtimes X)
given by
(a,x)\mapsto(-a,x)
,

for all
a_{1},a_{2},a\in A_{x}
and
x\in X
. It easy to check that
\mu
,
\sigma
and
\nu
are symmetric rack homomorphisms. Also, the fact that
\mu
,
\sigma
and
\nu
are slice morphisms and that they turn
p:\mathscr{F}\rtimes X\rightarrow X
into an abelian group object follows directly (see also
[
11
, Theorem 2.2]
).

Let us define a functor
\mathcal{T}:\mathbf{SRMod}_{(X,\rho_{X})}\rightarrow\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})
. Given an
(X,\rho_{X})
-module
\mathscr{F}=(A,\phi,\psi,\eta)
, let
\mathcal{T}(\mathscr{F})
denote the object
p:\mathscr{F}\rtimes X\rightarrow X
. Let
\mathscr{F^{\prime}}=(A^{\prime},\phi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})
be another
(X,\rho_{X})
-module and
f:\mathscr{F}\rightarrow\mathscr{F^{\prime}}
an
(X,\rho_{X})
-map. Define
\mathcal{T}(f):\mathscr{F}\rtimes X\rightarrow\mathscr{F^{\prime}}\rtimes X
by

T(f)(a,x)=(f_{x}(a),x).

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(f)\big((a,x)\star(b,y)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(f)\big(\phi_{x,y}(a)+\psi_{x,y}(b),x\ast y\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(f_{x\ast y}(\phi_{x,y}(a)+\psi_{x,y}(b)),x\ast y\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\phi^{\prime}_{x,y}f_{x}(a)+\psi^{\prime}_{x,y}f_{y}(b),x\ast y\big),\quad\textrm{since $f$ is an $(X,\rho_{X})$-map}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(f)\big((a,x)\big)\star^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(f)\big((b,y)\big),

where
\star
is the rack operation in
\mathscr{F}\rtimes X
and
\star^{\prime}
is the rack operation in
\mathscr{F^{\prime}}\rtimes X
. Further, since
\eta^{\prime}_{x}f_{x}=f_{\rho_{X}(x)}\eta_{x}
, the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{F^{\prime}}\rtimes X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{F^{\prime}}\rtimes X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho_{\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}(f)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}(f)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho_{\mathscr{F^{\prime}}\rtimes X}}

commutes. Thus,
T(f)
is a homomorphism of symmetric racks. Finally, since the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{F}\rtimes X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{F^{\prime}}\rtimes X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}(f)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p^{\prime}}

commutes, where
p^{\prime}
is the projection to the second coordinate,
\mathcal{T}(f)
is a slice morphism. In fact, one can check that
\mathcal{T}
is a functor (see
[
11
, Theorem 2.2]
). ∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
(X,\rho_{X})
be a symmetric rack and
p:(Y,\rho_{Y})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
an abelian group object with the multiplication map
m
, the inverse map
i
, and the section
s
. Let
(X,*)
and
(Y,\star)
be the underlying racks. Then there exists an
(X,\rho_{X})
-module
(R,\phi,\psi,\eta)
given by an induced abelian group structure on the fibre
R_{x}:=p^{-1}(x)
for each
x\in X
, where

\phi_{x,y}:R_{x}\rightarrow R_{x\ast y}~~\text{is given by }~u\mapsto u\star s(y),

\psi_{x,y}:R_{y}\rightarrow R_{x\ast y}~~\text{is given by }~v\mapsto s(x)\star v

and

\eta_{x}:R_{x}\rightarrow R_{\rho_{X}(x)}~~\text{is given by }~u\mapsto\rho_{Y}(u)

for all
u\in R_{x}
,
v\in R_{y}
and
x,y\in X
. Furthermore, the association gives a functor
\mathcal{S}:\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})\to\mathbf{SRMod}_{(X,\rho_{X})}
.

Proof.

Let
p:(Y,\rho_{Y})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
be an abelian group object in
\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})
. Let
R_{x}=p^{-1}(x)
for each
x\in X
. Then an abelian group structure on
R_{x}
is given by

u+v:=m(u,v),

where
s(x)
is the identity element in
R_{x}
and
-u:=i(u)
for all
u,v\in R_{x}
. To define an
(X,\rho_{X})
-module structure, let us define

\phi_{x,y}:R_{x}\rightarrow R_{x\ast y}~~\text{given by }~u\mapsto u\star s(y),

\psi_{x,y}:R_{y}\rightarrow R_{x\ast y}~~\text{given by }~v\mapsto s(x)\star v

and

\eta_{x}:R_{x}\rightarrow R_{\rho_{X}(x)}~~\text{given by }~u\mapsto\rho_{Y}(u)

for all
u\in R_{x}
,
v\in R_{y}
and
x,y\in X
.
It has been proved in
[
11
, Theorem 2.2]
that
\phi_{x,y}
and
\psi_{x,y}
are group homomorphisms for all
x,y\in X
. For the case of
\eta_{x}
, if
u_{1},u_{2}\in R_{x}
, then we see that

\displaystyle\eta_{x}(u_{1}+u_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{Y}(m(u_{1},u_{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m(\rho_{Y}(u_{1}),\rho_{Y}(u_{2})),\quad\textrm{since $m\,(\rho_{Y}\times\rho_{Y})=\rho_{Y}\,m$}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{Y}(u_{1})+\rho_{Y}(u_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{x}(u_{1})+\eta_{x}(u_{2}).

Next, we need to check that
\phi_{x,y}
,
\psi_{x,y}
and
\eta_{x}
satisfy the
(X,\rho_{X})
-module identities. Since
(M1)
,
(M2)
and
(M7)
are proved in
[
11
, Theorem 2.2]
, we verify only the remaining identities.

•

Since
\rho_{Y}
is a good involution, we have

\eta_{\rho_{X}(x)}\eta_{x}(u)=\eta_{\rho_{X}(x)}(\rho_{Y}(u))=\rho_{Y}^{2}(u)=u,

and hence
(M3)
holds.

•

Again, since
\rho_{Y}
is a good involution, we have

\phi_{\rho_{X}(x),y}(\eta_{x}(u))=\phi_{\rho_{X}(x),y}(\rho_{Y}(u))=\rho_{Y}(u)\star s(y)=\rho_{Y}(u\star s(y))=\eta_{x\ast y}(u\star s(y))=\eta_{x\ast y}(\phi_{x,y}(u)),

and hence
(M4)
holds.

•

Since
s
is a homomorphism of symmetric racks, we see that

\psi_{\rho_{X}(x),y}(v)=s(\rho_{X}(x))\star v=\rho_{Y}(s(x))\star v=\rho_{Y}(s(x)\star v)=\eta_{x\ast y}(s(x)\star(v))=\eta_{x\ast y}(\psi_{x,y}(v)),

and hence
(M5)
holds.

•

Since
\rho_{Y}
is a good involution and
s
is a homomorphism of symmetric racks, we obtain

\phi_{x\ast^{-1}y,y}\phi_{x,\rho_{X}(y)}(u)=\phi_{x\ast^{-1},y}(u\star s(\rho_{X}(y)))=\phi_{x\ast^{-1},y}(u\star\rho_{Y}(s(y)))=(u\star\rho_{Y}(s(y)))\star s(y)=u,

and hence
(M6)
holds.

•

Note that
\phi_{x\ast^{-1}y,y}\psi_{x,\rho_{X}(y)}(\eta_{y}(v))=\phi_{x\ast^{-1}y,y}\psi_{x,\rho_{X}(y)}(\rho_{Y}(v))=\phi_{x\ast^{-1}y,y}(s(x)\star\rho_{Y}(v))=(s(x)\star\rho_{Y}(v))\star s(y)
and

\psi_{x\ast\rho_{X}(y),y}(v)=s(x\ast\rho_{X}(y))\star v=(s(x)\star\rho_{Y}(s(y)))\star v
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{x\ast^{-1}y,y}\psi_{x,\rho_{X}(y)}(\eta_{y}(v))+\psi_{x\ast\rho_{X}(y),y}(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(s(x)\star\rho_{Y}(v))\star s(y)+(s(x)\star\rho_{Y}(s(y)))\star v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\big((s(x)\star\rho_{Y}(v))\star s(y),~(s(x)\star\rho_{Y}(s(y)))\star v\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\big(\big((s(x),s(x))\star(\rho_{Y}(v),\rho_{Y}(s(y)))\big)\star(s(y),v)\big),\quad\textrm{by definition of rack operation in $Y\times_{X}Y$}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(m(s(x),s(x))\star m\,(\rho_{Y}(v),\rho_{Y}(s(y)))\big)\star m(s(y),v),\quad\textrm{since $m$ is a homomorphism of racks}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(s(x)\star\rho_{Y}\,m(v,s(y)))\star m(s(y),v),\quad\textrm{since $m$ is a homomorphism of symmetric racks}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(s(x)\star\rho_{Y}\,m(s(y),v))\star m(s(y),v),\quad\textrm{since $\rho_{Y}$ is a good involution}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s(x),

and hence
(M8)
holds. Hence, we have proved that
(R,\phi,\psi,\eta)
is an
(X,\rho_{X})
-module.

Next, we construct a functor
\mathcal{S}:\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})\to\mathbf{SRMod}_{(X,\rho_{X})}
. Given an abelian group object
p:(Y,\rho_{Y})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
, we set
\mathcal{S}(p)=(R,\phi,\psi,\eta)
, which is defined above. Let
f
be a morphism from the abelian group object
p_{1}:(Y_{1},\rho_{Y_{1}})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
to the abelian group object
p_{2}:(Y_{2},\rho_{Y_{2}})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
. Thus, if
m
and
m^{\prime}
denote the corresponding multiplication maps, then
f\,m=m^{\prime}\,(f,f)
. Further,
f:(Y_{1},\rho_{Y_{1}})\rightarrow(Y_{2},\rho_{Y_{2}})
is a homomorphism of symmetric racks with
p_{2}\,f=p_{1}
. Let
\mathscr{F}_{1}
and
\mathscr{F}_{2}
denote the
(X,\rho_{X})
-modules as described above. Define
g:\mathscr{F}_{1}=(R_{1},\phi,\psi,\eta)\rightarrow\mathscr{F}_{2}=(R_{2},\phi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})
by setting

g_{x}:{(R_{1})}_{x}\rightarrow{(R_{2})}_{x}~~\text{as}~~g_{x}(u)=f(u)

for all
x\in X
and
u\in{(R_{1})}_{x}
. Since
f\,m=m^{\prime}\,(f,f)
, it follows that
g_{x}
is an abelian group homomorphism for each
x\in X
. The identities

\phi^{\prime}_{x,y}g_{x}=g_{x\ast y}\phi_{x,y}
and
\psi^{\prime}_{x,y}g_{y}=g_{x\ast y}\psi_{x,y}
are proved in
[
11
, Theorem 2.2]
. Further, since

g_{\rho_{X}(x)}\eta_{x}(w)=g_{\rho_{X}(x)}(\rho_{Y_{1}}(w))=f(\rho_{Y_{1}}(w))=\rho_{Y_{2}}(f(w))=\rho_{Y_{2}}g_{x}(w)=\eta_{x}^{\prime}(g_{x}(w))

for all
w\in(R_{1})_{x}
, we have
\eta^{\prime}_{x}g_{x}=g_{\rho_{X}(x)}\eta_{x}
. Hence,
g
is an
(X,\rho_{X})
-map. A routine check shows that
\mathcal{S}
is a functor (see
[
11
, Thereom 2.2]
).
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
(X,\rho_{X})
be a symmetric rack. Then the category
\mathbf{SRMod}_{(X,\rho_{X})}
of
(X,\rho_{X})
-modules is equivalent to the category
\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})
of abelian group objects in the slice category over
(X,\rho_{X})
.

Proof.

Let
(X,\rho_{X})
be a symmetric rack. By Proposition
4.2
and Proposition
4.3
, we have functors
\mathcal{T}:\mathbf{SRMod}_{(X,\rho_{X})}\to\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})
and
\mathcal{S}:\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})\to\mathbf{SRMod}_{(X,\rho_{X})}
. To show that the categories
\mathbf{SRMod}_{(X,\rho_{X})}
and
\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})
are equivalent, we show that
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{S}
is naturally isomorphic to
\mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})}
and
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}
is naturally isomorphic to
\mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{SRMod}_{(X,\rho_{X})}}
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\in\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})
denote the object
p:(Y,\rho_{Y})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
with the multiplication map
m
, the inverse map
i
, and the section
s
. Then
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{O})
is the object
q:(\mathscr{R}\rtimes X,\rho_{\mathscr{R}\rtimes X})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
in the category
\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})
, where
\mathscr{R}=(R,\phi,\psi,\eta)
as constructed in Proposition
4.3
. Let
*
be the rack operation on
X
, and
\tilde{*}
the rack operation on
\mathscr{R}\rtimes X
. Note that, the abelian group structure on
q:(\mathscr{R}\rtimes X,\rho_{\mathscr{R}\rtimes X})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
has the multiplication, the inverse and the section given by

•

\mu((a_{1},x),(a_{2},x))=(a_{1}+a_{2},x)=(m(a_{1},a_{2}),x)
,

•

\sigma(x)=(0,x)=(s(x),x)
,

•

\nu((a,x))=(-a,x)=(i(a),x)
,

for all
a_{1},a_{2},a\in R_{x}
and
x\in X
. Define
\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}:(Y,\rho_{Y})\rightarrow(\mathscr{R}\rtimes X,\rho_{\mathscr{R}\rtimes X})
by setting
\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(a)=(a,x)
for
a\in R_{x}
. Clearly,
\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}
is a bijection and the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(Y,\rho_{Y})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathscr{R}\rtimes X,\rho_{\mathscr{R}\rtimes X})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(X,\rho_{X})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}

commutes. Note that
\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(m(a,b))=\mu(\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(a)
,
\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(b)),~\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(s(a))=\sigma(\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(a))
and
\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(i(a))=\nu(\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(a))
for all
a,b\in R_{x}
. Hence,
\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}
respects the abelian group structure. Now, for
a\in R_{x}
and
b\in R_{y}
, we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(a)\tilde{\ast}\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a,x)\tilde{\ast}(b,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\phi_{x,y}(a)+\psi_{x,y}(b),x\ast y\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(a\star s(y)+s(x)\star b,x\ast y\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(m\big(a\star s(y),s(x)\star b\big),x\ast y\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(m((a,s(x))\tilde{\star}(s(y),b)),x\ast y\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(m(a,s(x))\star m(s(y),b),x\ast y\big),\quad\textrm{since $m:Y\times Y\to Y$ is a rack homomorphism}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a\star b,x\ast y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(a\star b),

where
\star
is the rack operation on
Y
and
\tilde{\star}
is the rack operation on
Y\times Y
. Thus,
\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}
is a rack homomorphism. Since
\rho_{\mathscr{R}\rtimes X}\,\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}=\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}\,\rho_{Y}
, it follows that
\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}
is a symmetric rack isomorphism, and hence

\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}:\mathcal{O}\to\mathcal{T}\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{O})
is an isomorphism.

Let
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{O^{\prime}}
be the objects
p:(Y,\rho_{Y})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
and
p^{\prime}:(Y^{\prime},\rho_{Y^{\prime}})\rightarrow(X,\rho_{X})
in
\mathbf{Ab}(\mathbf{SR}|_{(X,\rho_{X})})
, respectively. Let
f:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathcal{O^{\prime}}
be a morphism. Then, by definition,

is a symmetric rack homomorphism such that

and
f
preserves the abelian group structure. Let
x\in X
and
a\in R_{x}
. Then
\zeta_{\mathcal{O^{\prime}}}\,f(a)=(f_{x}(a),x)
, where
f_{x}=f|_{R_{x}}
and

\mathcal{T}\mathcal{S}(f)\,\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(a)=\mathcal{T}\mathcal{S}(f)(a,x)=\mathcal{T}(f|_{R_{x}}(a))=\mathcal{T}(f_{x}(a))=(f_{x}(a),x).

Thus, the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{O}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{O})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{O^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{O^{\prime}})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}\mathcal{S}(f)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\zeta_{\mathcal{O^{\prime}}}}

commutes, and we have shown that

Theorem 5.4

(X,\rho_{X})

Corollary 5.6

Let

be a symmetric rack (respectively quandle) and

6.
Generalized (co)homology for symmetric racks and symmetric quandles

In this section, we formulate a (co)homology theory for symmetric racks (respectively quandles)
(X,\rho_{X})
with coefficients derived from homogeneous
(X,\rho_{X})
-modules. To initiate this formulation, we first extract the fundamental axioms that define
(X,\rho_{X})
-modules.

Definition 6.1
.

The
symmetric rack algebra
of a symmetric rack
(X,\rho_{X})
is the associative
\mathbb{Z}
-algebra, denoted by
\mathbb{Z}(X,\rho_{X})
, which is generated by the set
\{\phi_{x,y}^{\pm 1},\,\psi_{x,y},\,\eta_{x}\mid x,y\in X\}
and admit the following defining relations:

(A1)

\phi_{x,y}\phi_{x,y}^{-1}=\phi_{x,y}^{-1}\phi_{x,y}=1
,

(A2)

\phi_{x\ast y,z}\phi_{x,y}=\phi_{x\ast z,y\ast z}\phi_{x,z}
,

(A3)

\phi_{x\ast y,z}\psi_{x,y}=\psi_{x\ast z,y\ast z}\phi_{y,z}
,

(A4)

\eta_{\rho_{X}(x)}\eta_{x}=1
,

(A5)

\phi_{\rho_{X}(x),y}\eta_{x}=\eta_{x\ast y}\phi_{x,y}
,

(A6)

\psi_{\rho_{X}(x),y}=\eta_{x\ast y}\psi_{x,y}
,

(A7)

\phi_{x\ast\rho_{X}(y),y}\phi_{x,\rho_{X}(y)}=1
,

(A8)

\psi_{x\ast y,z}=\phi_{x\ast z,y\ast z}\psi_{x,z}+\psi_{x\ast z,y\ast z}\psi_{y,z}
,

(A9)

\phi_{x\ast\rho_{X}(y),y}\psi_{x,\rho_{X}(y)}\eta_{y}=-\psi_{x\ast\rho_{X}(y),y}
,

for all
x,y,z\in X.

Definition 6.2
.

The
symmetric quandle algebra
of a symmetric quandle
(X,\rho_{X})
is the symmetric rack algebra of the underlying symmetric rack which satisfies the additional relation

(A10)

\phi_{x,x}+\psi_{x,x}=1

for all
x\in X.

7.
Relation between cohomology of symmetric racks and cohomology of associated groups

In
[
7
, Section 5]
, an isomorphism between the second rack cohomology of a rack and the first cohomology of its associated group has been established. In this section, we establish an analogous relation between the second cohomology of a symmetric rack and the first cohomology of its associated group.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
(X,\rho_{X})
be a symmetric rack and
A
be an abelian group viewed as a trivial homogeneous
(X,\rho_{X})
-module. Let
A
also be viewed as a symmetric rack with the trivial rack operation and with
a\mapsto-a
as the good involution, and let
\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)
be the abelian group of symmetric rack homomorphisms with the point-wise addition. Let
G_{(X,\rho_{X})}
act on
\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)
from the left by
(e_{x}\cdot f)(z)=f(z*x)
. Then there is a group isomorphism

H^{2}_{SR}\big((X,\rho_{X}),A\big)\cong H^{1}\big(G_{(X,\rho_{X})},\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)\big).

Proof.

For
x,y,z\in X
and
f\in\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)
, we see that

(e_{x}e_{y}\cdot f)(z)=(e_{y}\cdot f)(z*x)=f((z*x)*y)=f((z*y)*(x*y))=(e_{x*y}\cdot f)(z*y)=(e_{y}e_{x*y}\cdot f)(z)

and

(e_{x}^{-1}\cdot f)(z)=f(z*^{-1}x)=f(z*\rho_{X}(x))=(e_{\rho_{X}(x)}\cdot f)(z).

Thus, the left action of
G_{(X,\rho_{X})}
on
\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)
is well-defined.

Let
f:G_{(X,\rho_{X})}\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)
be a group
1
-cocycle. Define
\theta_{f}:X\times X\rightarrow A
by

\theta_{f}((x,y))=f(e_{y})(x).

We claim that
\theta_{f}
is a symmetric rack 2-cocycle. For all
x,y,z\in X
, we have

\displaystyle\theta_{f}(x,z)+\theta_{f}(x*z,y*z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(e_{z})(x)+f(e_{y*z})(x*z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(e_{z})(x)+(e_{z}\cdot f(e_{y*z}))(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(e_{z})(x)+(f(e_{z}e_{y*z})-f(e_{z}))(x),\quad\text{since $f$ is a group 1-cocycle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(e_{z}e_{y*z})(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(e_{y}e_{z})(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(f(e_{y}e_{z})-f(e_{y}))(x)+f(e_{y})(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(e_{y}\cdot f(e_{z}))(x)+f(e_{y})(x),\quad\text{since $f$ is a group 1-cocycle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(e_{z})(x*y)+f(e_{y})(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta_{f}(x*y,z)+\theta_{f}(x,y).

Further, since
f(e_{y})
is a symmetric rack homomorphism, we have

\theta_{f}(x,y)+\theta_{f}(\rho_{X}(x),y)=f(e_{y})(x)+f(e_{y})(\rho_{X}(x))=f(e_{y})(x)-f(e_{y})(x)=0.

Since
f
is a group 1-cocycle, we get

\displaystyle\theta_{f}(x,y)+\theta_{f}(x*y,\rho_{X}(y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(e_{y})(x)+f(e_{\rho_{X}(y)})(x\ast y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(e_{y})(x)+(e_{y}\cdot f(e_{\rho_{X}(y)}))(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(e_{y})(x)+(f(e_{y}e_{\rho_{X}(y)})-f(e_{y}))(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad\text{since $f(1)=0$},

and hence
\theta_{f}
is a symmetric rack 2-cocycle. This gives a map
Z^{1}\big(G_{(X,\rho_{X})},\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)\big)\rightarrow H^{2}_{SR}\big((X,\rho_{X}),A\big)
defined by
f\mapsto[\theta_{f}]
. Since the group structure in both the domain and the codomain is the point-wise addition of maps, it follows that
f\mapsto[\theta_{f}]
is a group homomorphism. If
f\in B^{1}(G_{(X,\rho_{X})},\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)),
then for all
a\in G_{(X,\rho_{X})}
and
x\in X
, we have

f(a)(x)=(a\cdot\phi-\phi)(x)

for some
\phi\in\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)
. Thus, for all
x,y\in X
, we have

\theta_{f}(x,y)=f(e_{y})(x)=(e_{y}\cdot\phi-\phi)(x)=(e_{y}\cdot\phi)(x)-\phi(x)=\phi(x*y)-\phi(x).

This shows that
\theta_{f}\in B^{2}((X,\rho_{X});A),
and hence we obtain a map

\Psi:H^{1}\big(G_{(X,\rho_{X})},\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)\big)\rightarrow H^{2}_{SR}\big((X,\rho_{X}),A\big).

For the reverse direction, let
\sigma\in Z_{SR}^{2}((X,\rho_{X}),A)
. Then
\sigma
satisfies the symmetric rack 2-cocycle conditions

\sigma(x,z)+\sigma(x*z,y*z)=\sigma(x,y)+\sigma(x*y,z),

(7.0.1)

\sigma(x,y)+\sigma(\rho_{X}(x),y)=0,

(7.0.2)

\sigma(x,y)+\sigma(x*y,\rho_{X}(y))=0,

(7.0.3)

for all
x,y,z\in X.
Let us define
\sigma^{\prime}:X\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)
by

\sigma^{\prime}(x)(y)=\sigma(y,x)

for all
x,y\in X
. Since
\sigma^{\prime}(x)(\rho_{X}(y))=\sigma(\rho_{X}(y),x)=-\sigma(x,y)
by the cocycle condition (
7.0.2
), and hence
\sigma^{\prime}(x)\in\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)
. Identifying
X
with the set
\{e_{x}\mid x\in X\}
, we have a map
\tau_{\sigma}:X\rightarrow G_{(X,\rho_{X})}\ltimes\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)
defined by

\tau_{\sigma}(e_{x})=(e_{x},\sigma^{\prime}(x))

for all
x\in X
. Note that

\tau_{\sigma}(e_{x})\tau_{\sigma}(e_{\rho_{X}(x)})=(e_{x},\sigma^{\prime}(x))(e_{\rho_{X}(x)},\sigma^{\prime}(\rho_{X}(x)))=\big(e_{x}e_{\rho_{X}(x)},\sigma^{\prime}(x)+e_{x}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(\rho_{X}(x))\big).

Further, the symmetric rack 2-cocycle condition (
7.0.3
) gives

\displaystyle\big(\sigma^{\prime}(x)+e_{x}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(\rho_{X}(x))\big)(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(x)(y)+(e_{x}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(\rho_{X}(x)))(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(x)(y)+\sigma^{\prime}(\rho_{X}(x))(y*x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(y,x)+\sigma(y*x,\rho_{X}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

Thus,
\sigma^{\prime}(x)+e_{x}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(\rho_{X}(x))
is the trivial map, and consequently
\tau_{\sigma}(e_{x})\tau_{\sigma}(e_{\rho_{X}(x)})=(e,0),
where
e
is the identity of
G_{(X,\rho_{X})}.
Similarly, we can prove that
\tau_{\sigma}(e_{\rho_{X}(x)})\tau_{\sigma}(e_{x})=(e,0)
. Next, we consider

\displaystyle\tau_{\sigma}(e_{y})\,\tau_{\sigma}(e_{x*y})\,\tau_{\sigma}(e_{y})^{-1}\,\tau_{\sigma}(e_{x})^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(e_{y},\sigma^{\prime}(y))\,(e_{x*y},\sigma^{\prime}({x*y}))\,(e_{y},\sigma^{\prime}(y))^{-1}\,(e_{x},\sigma^{\prime}(x))^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(e_{y},\sigma^{\prime}(y))\,(e_{x*y},\sigma^{\prime}({x*y}))\,(e_{y}^{-1},e_{y}^{-1}\cdot(-\sigma^{\prime}(y)))\,(e_{x}^{-1},e_{x}^{-1}\cdot(-\sigma^{\prime}(x)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(e_{y}e_{x*y},\sigma^{\prime}(y)+e_{y}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}({x*y})\big)\,\big(e_{y}^{-1}e_{x}^{-1},e_{y}^{-1}\cdot(-\sigma^{\prime}(y))+e_{y}^{-1}e_{x}^{-1}\cdot(-\sigma^{\prime}(x))\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(e,\sigma^{\prime}(y)+e_{y}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}({x*y}))+(e_{y}e_{x*y})\cdot(e_{y}^{-1}\cdot(-\sigma^{\prime}(y))+e_{y}^{-1}e_{x}^{-1}\cdot(-\sigma^{\prime}(x))).

The symmetric rack 2-cocycle condition (
7.0.1
) gives

\displaystyle(\sigma^{\prime}(y)+e_{y}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}({x*y}))+(e_{y}e_{x*y})\cdot(e_{y}^{-1}\cdot(-\sigma^{\prime}(y))+e_{y}^{-1}e_{x}^{-1}\cdot(-\sigma^{\prime}(x)))(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(z,y)+\sigma(z*y,x*y)+(e_{y}e_{x*y})\cdot(e_{y}^{-1}\cdot(-\sigma^{\prime}(y))+e_{y}^{-1}e_{x}^{-1}\cdot(-\sigma^{\prime}(x)))(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(z,y)+\sigma(z*y,x*y)+(e_{x}\cdot(-\sigma^{\prime}(y))+(-\sigma^{\prime}(x)))(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(z,y)+\sigma(z*y,x*y)+(-\sigma^{\prime}(y))(z*x)-\sigma^{\prime}(x)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(z,y)+\sigma(z*y,x*y)-\sigma(z*x,y)-\sigma(z,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

It follows that
\tau_{\sigma}(e_{y})\tau_{\sigma}(e_{x*y})\tau_{\sigma}(e_{y})^{-1}\tau_{\sigma}(e_{x})^{-1}=(e,0)
for all
x,y\in X
, and hence we can extend
\tau_{\sigma}
to a group homomorphism
\tau_{\sigma}:G_{(X,\rho_{X})}\rightarrow G_{(X,\rho_{X})}\ltimes\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)
.

Let us define
\chi_{\sigma}:G_{(X,\rho_{X})}\to\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)
to be the composition

\chi_{\sigma}=p_{2}\,\tau_{\sigma}

where
p_{2}:(G_{(X.\rho_{X})}\ltimes\operatorname{Hom}(X,A))\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)
is the projection onto the second factor. Since
\tau_{\sigma}
is a group homomorphism, it follows that
\chi_{\sigma}
is a group 1-cocycle. This gives a map
Z^{2}_{SR}\big((X,\rho_{X}),A\big)\rightarrow H^{1}\big(G_{(X,\rho_{X})},\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)\big)
defined by
\sigma\mapsto[\chi_{\sigma}]
. If
\pi\in B^{2}_{SR}((X,\rho_{X}),A)
, then
\pi=\delta^{1}(\tilde{\pi})
for some
\tilde{\pi}\in C^{1}_{SR}((X,\rho_{X}),A)
, that is,

\pi(x,y)=\tilde{\pi}(x)-\tilde{\pi}(x*y)

for all
x,y\in X
. If
g\in G_{(X,\rho_{X})}
, then
g=e_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}e_{x_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots e_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}
for some
x_{i}\in X
and
\epsilon_{i}\in\{1,-1\}
. Thus, for all
y\in X
, we have

\displaystyle\chi_{\pi}(g)(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{2}(\tau_{\pi}(g))(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{2}(\tau_{\pi}(e_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}e_{x_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots e_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}))(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{2}(\tau_{\pi}(e_{x_{1}})^{\epsilon_{1}}\tau_{\pi}(e_{x_{2}})^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots\tau_{\pi}(e_{x_{n}})^{\epsilon_{n}})(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{2}\big((e_{x_{1}},\pi^{\prime}(x_{1}))^{\epsilon_{1}}(e_{x_{2}},\pi^{\prime}(x_{2}))^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots(e_{x_{n}},\pi^{\prime}(x_{n}))^{\epsilon_{n}}\big)(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{2}((e_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}e_{x_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots e_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}},\,t(x_{1})+e_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}\cdot t(x_{2}))+\cdots+e_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}e_{x_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots e_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}\cdot t(x_{n})))(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(t(x_{1})+e_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}\cdot t(x_{2})+\cdots+e_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}e_{x_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots e_{x_{n-1}}^{\epsilon_{n-1}}\cdot t(x_{n})\big)(y),

where
t(x_{i})=\pi^{\prime}(x_{i})
for
\epsilon_{i}=1
and
t(x_{i})=e_{x_{i}}^{-1}\cdot(-\pi^{\prime}(x_{i}))
for
\epsilon_{i}=-1
. For instance, if all
\epsilon_{i}=1
, then

\displaystyle\chi_{\pi}(g)(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi(y,x_{1})+\pi(y*x_{1},x_{2})+\cdots+\pi(((y*x_{1})*x_{2})\cdots*x_{n-1},x_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(y)-\tilde{\pi}(y*x_{1})+\tilde{\pi}(y*x_{1})-\tilde{\pi}((y*x_{1}*)x_{2})+\cdots+

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(((y*x_{1})*x_{2})\cdots*x_{n-1})-\tilde{\pi}(((y*x_{1})*x_{2})\cdots x_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(y)-\tilde{\pi}((y*x_{1})*x_{2})\cdots x_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(y)-(e_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}e_{x_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots e_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}})\cdot\tilde{\pi}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(y)-g\cdot\tilde{\pi}(y)=(\tilde{\pi}-g\cdot\tilde{\pi})(y).

The cases when some
\epsilon_{i}=-1
can be dealt with in a similar way through a routine computation. Thus,
\chi_{\pi}(g)=\tilde{\pi}-g\cdot\tilde{\pi}
, which shows that
\chi_{\pi}\in B^{1}(G_{(X,\rho_{X})},\operatorname{Hom}(X,A))
. Hence, we obtain a map

\Psi:H^{2}_{SR}\big((X,\rho_{X}),A\big)\rightarrow H^{1}\big(G_{(X,\rho_{X})},\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)\big)

and it is easy to see that
\Psi
and
\Phi
are inverses to each other, and hence
H^{2}_{SR}\big((X,\rho_{X}),A\big)\cong H^{1}\big(G_{(X,\rho_{X})},\operatorname{Hom}(X,A)\big)
, which is desired. ∎
