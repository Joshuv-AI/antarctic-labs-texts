A simplified second-order Gaussian Poincaré inequality in discrete setting with applications
Peter Eichelsbacher, Benedikt Rednoß, Christoph Thäle, Guangqu Zheng

Abstract
. In this paper, a simplified second-order Gaussian Poincaré inequality for normal approximation of functionals over infinitely many Rademacher random variables is derived. It is based on a new bound for the Kolmogorov distance between a general Rademacher functional and a Gaussian random variable, which is established by means of the discrete Malliavin-Stein method and is of independent interest. As an application, the number of vertices with prescribed degree and the subgraph counting statistic in the Erdős-Rényi random graph are discussed. The number of vertices of fixed degree is also studied for percolation on the Hamming hypercube. Moreover, the number of isolated faces in the Linial-Meshulam-Wallach random κ \kappa -complex and infinite weighted 2-runs are treated. Mathematics Subject Classifications (2010) : 05C80, 60F05, 60H07. Keywords: Berry-Esseen bound, discrete stochastic analysis, Erdős-Rényi random graph, infinite weighted 2-run, isolated face, hypercube percolation, Malliavin-Stein method, Rademacher functional, random simplicial complex, second-order Poincaré inequality, subgraph count, vertex of given degree.



1.
Introduction and applications

In the last decade, a wide range of quantitative central limit type results for random systems driven by either a Gaussian or a Poisson process have been obtained by means for the so-called Malliavin-Stein method, which combines the Malliavin calculus of variations on a Gaussian space or on configuration spaces with Stein’s method for normal approximation. This Malliavin-Stein approach was first developed by Nourdin and Peccati during their successful attempt in quantifying the celebrated fourth moment theorem and it has been exploited for problems related to excursion sets of Gaussian random fields, nodal statistics of random waves, random geometric graphs, random tessellations or random polytopes, to name just a few. We refer to the monographs
[
24
,
27
]
as well as to the webpage
[
1
]
for an extensive overview. In parallel to these developments, the Malliavin-Stein method has also been made available for discrete random structures, which can be described by possibly infinitely many independent Rademacher random variables taking values
+1
and
-1
only, see
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
26
]
. Applications of this discrete Malliavin-Stein technique to quantitative central limit theorems for random graphs, random simplicial complexes and percolation models have been the content of
[
17
,
18
]
. The technical backbone of these papers is what is known as the discrete second-order Gaussian Poincaré inequality, which in turn relies on an abstract normal approximation bound previously developed in
[
16
]
. The purpose of the present article is to provide a significantly simplified version of such an inequality, whose proof is based on a new and powerful bound for the normal approximation of non-linear functionals of an infinite Rademacher sequence, which is of independent interest. It is mainly guided by a monotonicity property of the solutions of Stein’s equation for normal approximation. This property has been proved and applied at many places in Stein’s method, but in the proof of Theorem 2.2 in
[
33
]
it was identified the first time
to prove significantly simplified bounds for normal and non-normal approximations for unbounded exchangeable pairs. Moreover in
[
8
]
the
same monotonicity argument was applied for improvements in further approaches of Stein’s method. Beyond that our text has also been inspired by the recent work
[
19
]
dealing with the normal approximation of Poisson functionals.

In what follows, we use the usual big-O notation

to mean that

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

be a sequence of independent Bernoulli random variables satisfying
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{i}=0)=\mathbb{P}(\xi_{i}=1)=1/2
and let

be a square-summable sequence for each

\displaystyle d_{K}\left(\frac{G_{n}-\mathbb{E}G_{n}}{\sqrt{\textup{Var}(G_{n})}},N\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\|\alpha^{(n)}\|_{\ell^{4}(\mathbb{Z})}^{2}}{\textup{Var}(G_{n})}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\|\alpha^{(n)}\|_{\ell^{4}(\mathbb{Z})}^{2}}{\|\alpha^{(n)}\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}^{2}}\right).

Partially generalizing Theorem

with values either in an arbitrary finite abelian group

(see

]
) or in

(see

]
). We remark that this parallels the behaviour for the Erdős-Rényi random graph. The next theorem provides a Berry-Esseen bound for the number of isolated faces in

and generalizes part (a) of Theorem

Fix

1.4.
Application to hypercube percolation

Let us now consider another random graph model for which the underlying graph is the
1

n

\{-1,1\}^{n}=\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x_{i}|=1,i=1,\ldots,n\}

and any two vertices

are connected, when there is exactly one coordinate

such that
x_{i}\neq y_{i}
, see Figure

and kept with probability

. The resulting random graph is denoted by

and we emphasize that we allow

to depend on

, although this will not be visible in our notation again.

Hypercube percolation has been introduced by Erdős and Spencer
[
11
]
and subsequently been studied intensively. In particular, it has been observed that similarly to the Erdős-Rényi graph
\mathbf{G}(n,p)
, the random hypercube graph

undergoes phase transitions. For example, for fixed

the probability that

is connected converges to

,
1/e
or
1
, as
n\to\infty
, provided that
p<1/2
,
p=1/2
or
p>1/2
, respectively. Moreover, from
[
2
]
it is known that if

with

there exists a unique ‘giant’ component having size of order

, while for
\lambda<1
the size of the largest component is of lower order. Here, we study the number of vertices of fixed degree
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
in the random graph

and we retain the notation

for this number. In what follows we will assume that

or
p\to 1
slower than exponential, meaning that for any

(i)

and

as

or

(ii)

and
\log(1-p)+cn\to\infty
as

.

In particular, this covers the the situation

at which the phase transition for
\mathbf{H}(n,p)
takes places as discussed above.

Besides the application to random graphs, we would like to point out that our results are relevant from a more theoretical point of view in the context of the celebrated fourth moment theorem. 2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

For two sequences
a=a(n)
and
b=b(n)
we write
a\asymp b
provided that

c<\liminf_{n\to\infty}|a(n)/b(n)|\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}|a(n)/b(n)|\leq C

for two constants
0<c\leq C<\infty
. If

\limsup_{n\to\infty}|a(n)/b(n)|<\infty,

we write
a=\mathcal{O}(b)
. It is immediate that
a\asymp b
if and only if
a=\mathcal{O}(b)
and
b=\mathcal{O}(a)
.

We denote by
\mathbb{N}
the set of positive integers and write
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. The space of real square-summable sequences is a Hilbert space denoted by
\mathfrak{H}
. We say
a=(a(k),k\in\mathbb{N})\in\mathfrak{H}
if

\|a\|^{2}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}a(k)^{2}<\infty;

the associated inner product is given by

\langle a,b\rangle:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}a(k)b(k),~\text{for $a,b\in\mathfrak{H}$.}

Sometimes, we may abuse the above notation and write

(2.1)

\displaystyle\langle u,vw\rangle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}u(k)v(k)w(k),

whenever the above sum is well defined. Here
vw
stands for the mapping
k\in\mathbb{N}\longmapsto v(k)w(k)
, which shall be distinguished from the tensor product
v\otimes w
. For
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes p}
(
\mathfrak{H}^{\odot p}
respectively) the
p
th tensor product (symmetric tensor product resp.) of
\mathfrak{H}
.

2.2.
Discrete Malliavin formalism

Let
(p_{k},k\in\mathbb{N})
be a sequence of real numbers in
(0,1)
and define
q_{k}=1-p_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
\mathbf{X}=(X_{k},k\in\mathbb{N})
is a sequence of independent Rademacher random variables with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{k}=1)=p_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{P}(X_{k}=-1)=q_{k},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Let

(2.2)

\displaystyle\left(Y_{k}:=\frac{X_{k}-p_{k}+q_{k}}{2\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}},\,k\in\mathbb{N}\right)

be the normalized sequence.

2.2.1.
Chaos expansion

The Wiener-Itô-Walsh decomposition theorem asserts that the space
L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P})
with
\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\}
denoting the
\sigma
-field generated by the random sequences
\bf X
can be expressed as a direct sum of mutually orthogonal subspaces (see e.g.
[
28
, Proposition 6.7]
):

(2.3)

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P})=\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}\mathbb{C}_{p},

where
\mathbb{C}_{0}=\mathbb{R}
and for
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{C}_{p}
is called the
p
th Rademacher chaos, which is the collection of square-integrable
p
-linear polynomials in
\{Y_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}\}
. More precisely,

\mathbb{C}_{p}=\big\{J_{p}(f):f\in\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes p}\big\},

where for
f\in\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes p}
,
J_{p}(f)
is called the
p
th discrete multiple integral of
f
and is defined by

J_{p}(f):=\sum_{(i_{1},...,i_{p})\in\mathbb{N}^{p}}f(i_{1},...,i_{p})Y_{i_{1}}Y_{i_{2}}\cdots Y_{i_{p}}\textbf{1}_{\Delta_{p}}(i_{1},...,i_{p}),

with
\Delta_{p}:=\{(i_{1},...,i_{p})\in\mathbb{N}^{p}:i_{j}\neq i_{k}~\text{for $j\neq k$}\}
. By definition, we have
J_{p}(f)=J_{p}\big(\widetilde{f}\big)
, where
\widetilde{f}\in\mathfrak{H}^{\odot p}
stands for the canonical symmetrization

\widetilde{f}(i_{1},...,i_{p}):=\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{p}}f(i_{\sigma(1)},...,i_{\sigma(p)}),

of
f
, with
\mathfrak{S}_{p}
denoting the set of permutations over
\{1,...,p\}
.

The orthogonality of different Rademacher chaoses is captured by the following (modified) isometry relation:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[J_{p}(f)J_{q}(g)\big]=\textbf{1}_{\{p=q\}}p!\langle\widetilde{f},\widetilde{g}\textbf{1}_{\Delta_{q}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes p}}~\text{for any $f\in\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes p}$ and any $g\in\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes q}$}.

With the notation
\mathfrak{H}^{\odot p}_{0}=\big\{f\in\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes p}:f=\widetilde{f}\textbf{1}_{\Delta_{p}}\big\}
for
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we can rephrase (
2.3
) as follows. For any
F\in L^{2}\big(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P}\big)
, there is a unique sequence of kernels
f_{n}\in\mathfrak{H}^{\odot n}_{0},n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(2.5)

\displaystyle F=\mathbb{E}[F]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}J_{n}(f_{n})\qquad\text{in $L^{2}(\Omega)$. }

In the sequel, we introduce some basic discrete Malliavin calculus and refer readers to the survey
[
28
]
for further details and background material.

2.2.2.
Discrete Malliavin operators

For
F\in L^{1}(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P})
, we can write
F=\mathfrak{f}(X_{1},X_{2},...)
. We define

F^{+}_{k}:=\mathfrak{f}(X_{1},...,X_{k-1},+1,X_{k+1},...)\quad{\rm and}\quad F^{-}_{k}:=\mathfrak{f}(X_{1},...,X_{k-1},-1,X_{k+1},...).

The discrete gradient
D_{k}F
of
F
at
k
th coordinate is a real-valued random variable given by

D_{k}F:=\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\big(F^{+}_{k}-F^{-}_{k}\big),\,~k\in\mathbb{N}.

For example,
D_{k}Y_{j}=\textbf{1}_{\{k=j\}}
. The discrete gradient satisfies the following product formula. For
F,G\in L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P})
,

(2.6)

\displaystyle D_{k}(FG)=GD_{k}F+FD_{k}G-\frac{X_{k}}{\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}}(D_{k}F)(D_{k}G),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N};

see e.g.
[
28
, Proposition 7.8]
for a proof.

Lemma 2.1
.

(1)
If
F\in L^{\ell}(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P})
for some
\ell\in[1,\infty)
, then
D_{k}F\in L^{\ell}(\Omega)
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

(2)
If
G\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, then

(2.7)

\displaystyle\|D_{k}G\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

If we assume additionally
(a)

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}p_{k}q_{k}<\infty
or
(b)

G
depends only on
\{X_{1},...,X_{m}\}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|DG\|^{2}\big]=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\big[(D_{k}G)^{2}\big]\leq\begin{cases}{\displaystyle 4\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}p_{k}q_{k}}&\text{in case {\rm(a) }}\\
&\\
{\displaystyle 4\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}p_{k}q_{k}}&\text{in case {\rm(b)}.}\end{cases}

Proof.

For
\ell\in[1,\infty)
,
we can write

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L^{\ell}(\Omega)}^{\ell}=\mathbb{E}\big[|F|^{\ell}\textbf{1}_{\{X_{k}=1\}}+|F|^{\ell}\textbf{1}_{\{X_{k}=-1\}}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\big|F^{+}_{k}\big|^{\ell}\textbf{1}_{\{X_{k}=1\}}+\big|F^{-}_{k}\big|^{\ell}\textbf{1}_{\{X_{k}=-1\}}\big]

\displaystyle=p_{k}\mathbb{E}\big[|F^{+}_{k}|^{\ell}\big]+q_{k}\mathbb{E}\big[|F^{-}_{k}|^{\ell}\big],

where the last step follows from the independence between
F^{\pm}_{k}
and
X_{k}
. Thus, the finiteness of
\|F\|_{L^{\ell}(\Omega)}^{\ell}
implies
F^{+}_{k},F^{-}_{k}\in L^{\ell}(\Omega)
.
Therefore,
D_{k}F=\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\big(F^{+}_{k}-F^{-}_{k}\big)\in L^{\ell}(\Omega)
.

The
L^{\infty}
-case can be proved in the same manner. Suppose that
G
is essentially bounded by
M
for some
M\in(0,\infty)
, then both
G^{+}_{k}
and
G^{-}_{k}
are bounded by
M
so that

(2.8)

\displaystyle|D_{k}G|=\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\big|G^{+}_{k}-G^{-}_{k}\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2M\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq M,\quad\text{using $p_{k}q_{k}=p_{k}(1-p_{k})\leq 1/4$}.

This shows (
2.7
). Under the additional assumption (a), we deduce from (
2.8
) that

\mathbb{E}\big[\|DG\|^{2}\big]=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\big[(D_{k}G)^{2}\big]\leq 4M^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}p_{k}q_{k}.

The other case is also straightforward. ∎

Let
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
denote the set of real random variables
F\in L^{2}\big(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P}\big)
with

\mathbb{E}\big[\|DF\|^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}(D_{k}F)^{2}\right)<\infty.

The space
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
is a Hilbert space under the norm
\|F\|_{1,2}:=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[F^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|DF\|^{2}]}
and the set

\mathcal{S}:=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\bigoplus_{p=0}^{n}\mathbb{C}_{p}

is a dense subset of
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
.

The adjoint operator
\delta
of
D
is characterized by the duality relation

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\langle DF,u\rangle\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[F\delta(u)\big],

and its domain
\text{Dom}(\delta)
consists of square-integrable
\mathfrak{H}
-valued random variables
u\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathfrak{H})
satisfying the following property:

there is some constant
C_{u}>0
such that
\big|\mathbb{E}[\langle DF,u\rangle]\big|\leq C_{u}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[F^{2}]}
, for all
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
.

For
F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P})
having the representation (
2.5
), it is not difficult to see that

D_{k}F=\sum_{n\geq 1}nJ_{n-1}(f_{n}(k,\,\cdot\,)),

where
J_{0}(f_{1}(k,\,\cdot\,))=f_{1}(k)
and for
n\geq 2
,
J_{n-1}(f_{n}(k,\,\cdot\,))
is the
(n-1)
th discrete multiple integral of
f_{n}(k,\,\cdot\,)\in\mathfrak{H}^{\odot(n-1)}_{0}
. Then using the modified isometry property (
2.4
), we deduce by comparing
\textup{Var}(F)
and
\mathbb{E}[\|DF\|_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}]
that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\textup{Var}(F)\leq\mathbb{E}[\|DF\|_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}]

for all
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
. The above inequality is known as the Gaussian Poincaré inequality in the Rademacher setting and we note that the inequality (
2.10
) reduces to an equality if and only if
F\in\mathbb{C}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{C}_{1}
.

Suppose
u=(u_{k},k\in\mathbb{N})\in\text{Dom}(\delta)\subset L^{2}(\Omega;\mathfrak{H})
, then for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
u_{k}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
admits the chaos expansion

(2.11)

\displaystyle u_{k}=\mathbb{E}[u_{k}]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}J_{n}\big(g_{n+1}(k,\,\cdot\,)\big),

where
g_{n+1}(k,\,\cdot\,)\in\mathfrak{H}^{\odot n}
and
g_{n+1}\in\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes(n+1)}
. If
F\in\mathcal{S}
has the form
F=\mathbb{E}[F]+\sum_{n=1}^{m}J_{n}(f_{n})
with
f_{n}\in\mathfrak{H}^{\odot n}_{0}
, then

D_{k}F=\sum_{n=1}^{m}nJ_{n-1}\big(f_{n}(k,\,\cdot\,)\big)

so that
we deduce from
(
2.9
) and (
2.4
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\langle DF,u\rangle\big]=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}[u_{k}D_{k}F]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{p=1}^{m}p!\langle f_{p}(k,\,\cdot\,),g_{p}(k,\,\cdot\,)\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes(p-1)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{m}p!\langle f_{p},g_{p}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes p}}=\mathbb{E}\left[F\sum_{p=1}^{m}J_{p}\big(\widetilde{g}_{p}\textbf{1}_{\Delta_{p}}\big)\right].

It follows that
u\in\text{Dom}(\delta)
if and only if
\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}p!\big\|\widetilde{g}_{p}\textbf{1}_{\Delta_{p}}\big\|^{2}<\infty
. In this case,

\delta(u)=\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}J_{p}\big(\widetilde{g}_{p}\textbf{1}_{\Delta_{p}}\big).

Next, we define the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator
L
and its pseudo-inverse
L^{-1}
. Suppose
F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P})
has the representation (
2.5
), we say
F\in\text{Dom}(L)
if
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}n^{2}n!\|f_{n}\|^{2}<\infty
. In this case, we define

LF:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}-nJ_{n}(f_{n})

and the associated semigroup
(P_{t},t\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
is given as

P_{t}F:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-nt}J_{n}(f_{n}).

Also, for
F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P})
having the representation (
2.5
), we put

L^{-1}F:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}-\frac{1}{n}J_{n}(f_{n})

so that
LL^{-1}F=F-\mathbb{E}[F]
for any
F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P})
. It is not difficult to verify that
F\in\text{Dom}(L)
if and only if
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and
DF\in\text{Dom}(\delta)
; in this case, we can write
L=-\delta D
.

Finally, let us record a useful result from
[
17
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P})
, we have

(2.12)

\displaystyle-D_{k}L^{-1}F=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}P_{t}(D_{k}F)dt.

Moreover, if
D_{k}F\in L^{q}(\Omega)
for some
q\in[2,\infty)
, then
\|D_{k}L^{-1}F\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq\|D_{k}F\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}
.

Proof.

The integral representation of
-D_{k}L^{-1}F
is an easy consequence of the chaos expansion. Assume now
D_{k}F\in L^{q}(\Omega)
for some
q\in[2,\infty)
, then by Minkowski’s inequality and the integral representation (
2.12
), we have

\big\|D_{k}L^{-1}F\big\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\big\|P_{t}(D_{k}F)\big\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}dt\leq\|D_{k}F\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)},

where the last step follows from the contraction property of
P_{t}
. We refer to
[
17
, Proposition 3.1-3.3]
for more details. ∎

3.
Normal approximation bounds for Rademacher functionals

Using a discrete version of the Malliavin-Stein technique, a first normal approximation bound for symmetric Rademacher functionals has been obtained in the paper
[
26
]
, where the error bound was described
in terms of a probability metric based on smooth test functions. A corresponding bound in the Kolmogorov distance has later been found in
[
16
]
and was extended to the non-symmetric setting in
[
17
]
and in
[
9
]
. We will significantly simplify these bounds applying a monotonicity property of the solutions of Stein’s equation in normal approximation. This approach was introduced in
[
33
]
for normal and non-normal approximations for unbounded exchangeable pairs and in
[
8
]
for further approaches of Stein’s method. For functionals of Poisson random measures, the Malliavin-Stein method has recently been used in
[
19
]
to deduce a simplified Berry-Esseen bound using the same monotonicity argument. We adapt these observations to the Rademacher set-up, which leads to the following result, which is essentially a simplified version of
[
16
, Theorem 3.1]
(in the symmetric case),
[
17
, Proposition 4.1]
and
[
9
, Proposition 4.2 and Theorem 1.1]
(in the non-symmetric case). We also point out that by using a suitable chain rule, a normal approximation bound in Wasserstein distance has been obtained in
[
34
]
, whose order is comparable to the Kolmogorov bounds in
[
16
,
17
]
.

Before we can proceed, we need to introduce some further notation. For fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}
, let
f_{z}
be the solution to the Stein equation

(3.1)

f^{\prime}(x)-xf(x)=\textbf{1}_{\{x\leq z\}}-\Phi(z),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},

with
\Phi(\,\cdot\,)
denoting the distribution function of a standard Gaussian random variable. The function
f_{z}
satisfies the following properties (see Lemma 2.2 and Lemma 2.3 in
[
5
]
):

(i)

|f_{z}(x)|\leq\sqrt{2\pi}/4
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
;

(ii)

f_{z}(x)
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, infinitely differentiable on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{z\}
, but not differentiable at
x=z
;

(iii)

interpreting the derivative of
f_{z}(x)
at
x=z
as
f_{z}^{\prime}(z)=zf_{z}(z)+1-\Phi(z)
, one has that
|f_{z}^{\prime}(x)|\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
;

(iv)

|xf_{z}(x)|\leq 1
for all
x
and the map
x\longmapsto xf_{z}(x)
is non-decreasing.

Following the standard route in Stein’s method we have the following bound in Kolmogorov distance. If
F
has mean zero and variance one, then, with
N\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle d_{K}(F,N)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(F\leq z)-\Phi(z)|\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{E}\big[Ff_{z}(F)-f_{z}^{\prime}(F)\big]\Big|.

To be able to deduce bounds simplifying those in
[
17
]
, property (iv) as well as the property that the mapping
x\longmapsto{\rm\mathbf{1}}_{\{x>z\}}
is non-decreasing will be the basis. Property (iv) was considered in
[
6
, Lemma 2.2]
the first time, while already in
[
7
]
it has been used to prove a non-uniform Berry-Esséen bound for sums of independent and not necessarily identically distributed random variables.

Theorem 3.1
.

(1)
Let
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
have mean zero and variance one such that

(3.3)

\displaystyle Ff_{z}(F)+{\rm\mathbf{1}}_{\{F>z\}}\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}\;\;\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{R}.

Then with
N\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, one has that

(3.4)

\displaystyle d_{K}(F,N)\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|1-\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\big|\Big]+\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\langle D\big(Ff_{z}(F)+{\rm\mathbf{1}}_{\{F>z\}}\big)\frac{DF}{\sqrt{pq}},|DL^{-1}F|\big\rangle\Big].

(2)
Assume in addition that
u\in\text{\rm Dom}(\delta)
, where

(3.5)

\displaystyle u_{k}:=(p_{k}q_{k})^{-1/2}D_{k}F|D_{k}L^{-1}F|,\,k\in\mathbb{N}.

Then for
N\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
one has that

(3.6)

\displaystyle d_{K}(F,N)\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|1-\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\big|\Big]+2\Big\|\delta\big((pq)^{-1/2}DF|DL^{-1}F|\big)\Big\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

(3)
Let
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
have mean zero and variance one such that

(3.7)

D_{k}F\in L^{4}(\Omega)
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Assume either
(a)

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}p_{k}q_{k}<\infty
or
(b)

F
only depends on the first
m
Rademacher random variables.
Then for
N\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
one has that

(3.8)

\displaystyle d_{K}(F,N)\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|1-\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\big|\Big]+4\sqrt{\kappa}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{p_{k}q_{k}}\mathbb{E}\big[(D_{k}F)^{4}\big]\right)^{1/2},

where
\kappa=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}p_{k}q_{k}
in case
(a)
and
\kappa=\sum_{k=1}^{m}p_{k}q_{k}
in case
(b)
.

Remark 2
.

(1)

The estimates (
3.4
) and (
3.6
) simplify
[
17
, Proposition 4.1]
, see
[
17
, Equation (4.9)]
.

(2)

In view of property (iv) of the Stein solution
f_{z}
mentioned above and Lemma
2.1
,
the condition (
3.3
) is always satisfied if
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}p_{k}q_{k}<\infty
or
F
depends only on finitely many
X_{k}
’s.

(3)

For the random sequence
u
defined as in (
3.5
), a sufficient condition for
u\in\text{Dom}(\delta)
is that
F\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
depends only on finitely many
X_{k}
’s.

(4)

A sufficient condition for (
3.7
) is that
F\in L^{4}(\Omega,\sigma\{\mathbf{X}\},\mathbb{P})
; see Lemma
2.1
. If
p_{k}\to 0
, as
k\to\infty
, then
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}p_{k}q_{k}<\infty
if and only if
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}p_{k}<\infty
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Using the identity
F=LL^{-1}F=-\delta DL^{-1}F
and the duality relation (
2.9
), we write

\mathbb{E}\big[Ff_{z}(F)\big]=-\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\delta DL^{-1}F\big)f_{z}(F)\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\langle Df_{z}(F),-DL^{-1}F\rangle\big],

so that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[f_{z}^{\prime}(F)-Ff_{z}(F)\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[f_{z}^{\prime}(F)-\langle Df_{z}(F),-DL^{-1}F\rangle\big].

By definition of the discrete gradient and the fundamental theorem of calculus, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle D_{k}f_{z}(F)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\Big[f_{z}(F^{+}_{k})-f_{z}(F^{-}_{k})\Big]=\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\int_{0}^{F^{+}_{k}-F^{-}_{k}}f_{z}^{\prime}(t+F^{-}_{k})\,dt

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\int_{0}^{F^{+}_{k}-F^{-}_{k}}\big[f_{z}^{\prime}(t+F^{-}_{k})-f_{z}^{\prime}(F)\big]\,dt+f^{\prime}_{z}(F)D_{k}F:=R_{k}+f^{\prime}_{z}(F)D_{k}F,

where we follow the convention that, for a function
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(3.11)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}\phi(t)\,dt=-\int_{0}^{-x}\phi(-t)\,dt\quad{\rm for}~x<0.

Then, using the Stein’s equation
f^{\prime}_{z}(x)=xf_{z}(x)+\textbf{1}_{\{x\leq z\}}-\Phi(z)
, we get

(3.12)

\displaystyle f_{z}^{\prime}(t+F^{-}_{k})-f_{z}^{\prime}(F)=(t+F^{-}_{k})f_{z}(t+F^{-}_{k})-Ff_{z}(F)+\textbf{1}_{\{t+F^{-}_{k}\leq z\}}-\textbf{1}_{\{F\leq z\}}.

Note that when
F^{+}_{k}=F^{-}_{k}
or equivalently
D_{k}F=0
, we have
D_{k}f_{z}(F)=R_{k}=0
. Now, we distinguish the cases
F^{+}_{k}>F^{-}_{k}
(Case 1) and
F^{+}_{k}<F^{-}_{k}
(Case 2).

Case 1

If
F^{+}_{k}>F^{-}_{k}
or equivalently
D_{k}F>0
, we have
F^{+}_{k}\geq F\geq F^{-}_{k}
.
Then using the monotonicity of the function
x\longmapsto xf_{z}(x)
on
\mathbb{R}
, recall property (iv) of the Stein solution
f_{z}
, we get for
0\leq t\leq F^{+}_{k}-F^{-}_{k}
that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(t+F^{-}_{k})f_{z}(t+F^{-}_{k})-Ff_{z}(F)&\leq\quad F^{+}_{k}f_{z}(F^{+}_{k})-Ff_{z}(F)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\\
\quad\\
Ff_{z}(F)-(t+F^{-}_{k})f_{z}(t+F^{-}_{k})&\leq\quad Ff_{z}(F)-F^{-}_{k}f_{z}(F^{-}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.\end{cases}

Combining these two inequalities yields

|(t+F^{-}_{k})f_{z}(t+F^{-}_{k})-Ff_{z}(F)|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}}D_{k}\big(Ff_{z}(F)\big).

Note that the function
x\longmapsto\textbf{1}_{\{x>z\}}
is also non-decreasing on
\mathbb{R}
, so that the same arguments lead to

\big|\textbf{1}_{\{t+F^{-}_{k}\leq z\}}-\textbf{1}_{\{F\leq z\}}\big|=\big|\textbf{1}_{\{t+F^{-}_{k}>z\}}-\textbf{1}_{\{F>z\}}\big|\leq\frac{D_{k}\big(\textbf{1}_{\{F>z\}}\big)}{\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}}

for
0\leq t\leq F^{+}_{k}-F^{-}_{k}
.
Therefore, recalling the definition of
R_{k}
in (
3.10
), we have that

(3.13)

\displaystyle\left|R_{k}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}}D_{k}\big(Ff_{z}(F)+\textbf{1}_{\{F>z\}}\big)D_{k}F.

Case 2

If
F^{+}_{k}<F^{-}_{k}
or equivalently
D_{k}F<0
, we have
F^{+}_{k}\leq F\leq F^{-}_{k}
. Taking into account our convention (
3.11
), we write

\displaystyle R_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\int_{0}^{F^{-}_{k}-F^{+}_{k}}\big[f_{z}^{\prime}(F)-f_{z}^{\prime}(F^{-}_{k}-t)\big]\,dt,

and by the same arguments as in
Case 1
, we have

\big|f_{z}^{\prime}(F)-f_{z}^{\prime}(F^{-}_{k}-t)\big|\leq-\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}}D_{k}\big(Ff_{z}(F)+\textbf{1}_{\{F>z\}}\big)

so that the estimate (
3.13
) holds in this case as well.

Thus, by combining the above discussions with (
3.9
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[f_{z}^{\prime}(F)-Ff_{z}(F)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[f_{z}^{\prime}(F)-f_{z}^{\prime}(F)\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\langle R,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\big].

which is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|1-\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\big|\Big]+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\Big[|R_{k}|\times|D_{k}L^{-1}F|\Big].

Also, by (
3.13
), we get

(3.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\Big[|R_{k}|\times|D_{k}L^{-1}F|\Big]\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\Big[D_{k}\big(Ff_{z}(F)+\textbf{1}_{\{F>z\}}\big)\frac{D_{k}F}{\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}}|D_{k}L^{-1}F|\Big].

Note that the above sum is finite, since
D_{k}\big(Ff_{z}(F)+\textbf{1}_{\{F>z\}}\big)/\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}
is bounded by
2
and

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\Big[|D_{k}F||D_{k}L^{-1}F|\Big]\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\big[|D_{k}F|^{2}\big]<\infty

by Lemma
2.2
. Part (1) is thus proved.

To prove (2), putting

(3.15)

\Gamma

\Gamma_{0}

\sigma^{2}=\textup{Var}(S)
and

Let

\Gamma
and
\Gamma_{0}
,

This yields
\psi(n,p,\Gamma)=\psi(n,p,\Gamma_{0})

By the previous discussion we assume from now on that
\Gamma
has no isolated vertices.
In this setting, each subgraph
H

\mathbf{G}(n,p)
that is isomorphic to
\Gamma

Let

In particular, we will write

Note that the number of edges

Now, we define

\Gamma

is a finite set of independent and identically distributed Rademacher random variables.

For every

be the centred indicator for the presence of the subgraph
\gamma

\mathbf{G}(n,p)

Then, we can represent

\displaystyle W=\frac{S-\mathbb{E}S}{\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{\gamma\in M}I_{\gamma}\qquad\text{with}\qquad I_{\gamma}=\left(\prod_{k\in\gamma}\frac{X_{k}+1}{2}\right)-p^{\varepsilon_{\Gamma}}.

Hence, the random variable

3.1
-(2) are fulfilled, see Remark

\displaystyle d_{K}(W,N)\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|1-\langle DW,-DL^{-1}W\rangle\big|\Big]+2\Big\|\delta\big((pq)^{-1/2}DW|DL^{-1}W|\big)\Big\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality the first summand can be bounded by

\sqrt{\textup{Var}\big(\langle DW,-DL^{-1}W\rangle\big)}

Following the steps (4.9), (4.10), (4.11) in

Taken together, we get

\displaystyle C_{1}:={}

\displaystyle\textup{Var}\big(\langle DW,-DL^{-1}W\rangle\big),\qquad\quad C_{2}:={}\frac{1}{pq}\sum_{k\in E}\mathbb{E}\big[(D_{k}W)^{4}\big],

\displaystyle C_{3}:={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{pq}\sum_{k,\ell\in E}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(D_{\ell}\big((D_{k}W)(D_{k}L^{-1}W)\big)\Big)^{2}\bigg].

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{pq}\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\sum_{k,\ell\in E}\sum_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\in M}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(D_{\ell}\Big((D_{k}I_{\gamma_{1}})D_{k}L^{-1}I_{\gamma_{2}}\Big)\bigg)D_{\ell}\Big((D_{k}I_{\gamma_{3}})D_{k}L^{-1}I_{\gamma_{4}}\Big)\bigg].

F=(I-\mathbb{E}I)/\sqrt{\textup{Var}(I)}
. To see that
F
is a Rademacher functional, we let
\big\{f_{j}:j=1,\ldots,\binom{n}{\kappa+1}\big\}
be an arbitrary labelling of the
\binom{n}{\kappa+1}

\kappa
-faces of an
(n-1)
-dimensional simplex with
n
vertices and put

X_{j}:={\bf 1}_{\{f_{j}\text{ is present in }{\bf Y}_{\kappa}(n,p)\}}-{\bf 1}_{\{f_{j}\text{ is not present in }{\bf Y}_{\kappa}(n,p)\}},j=1,...,\binom{n}{\kappa+1},

which are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables.

Clearly,
I
(and hence
F
) is a functional over
\big\{X_{j}:j=1,\ldots,\binom{n}{\kappa}\big\}
. Since
F\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
only depends on finitely many Rademacher random variables, all conditions of Theorem
4.1
are automatically satisfied.
We start our analysis by observing that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[I\big]={n\choose\kappa}(1-p)^{n-\kappa}.

Now, let us determine the variance
\textup{Var}(I)
. We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa-1}
the set of
(\kappa-1)
-faces of the
(n-1)
-dimensional simplex and we can represent
I
as

I=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\kappa-1}}{\bf 1}_{\{f\text{ is isolated}\}}.

From now on, we only consider the case
\kappa\geq 2
, since the case
\kappa=1
reduces to the setting in part (a) of Theorem
1.3
.
The estimation of
\textup{Var}(I)
begins with the following expression:

\textup{Var}(I)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\kappa-1}}\textup{Var}({\bf 1}_{\{f\text{ is isolated}\}})+\sum_{f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{\kappa-1}}\textup{Cov}({\bf 1}_{\{f\text{ is isolated}\}},{\bf 1}_{\{g\text{ is isolated}\}})\textbf{1}_{\{f\neq g\}}.

Clearly,
\textup{Var}({\bf 1}_{\{f\text{ is isolated}\}})=(1-p)^{n-\kappa}(1-(1-p)^{n-\kappa})
for
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\kappa-1}
. Moreover, the covariance in the second sum is non-zero if and only if
f
and
g
share a common
(\kappa-2)
-face; in this case

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big({\bf 1}_{\{f\text{ is isolated}\}}{\bf 1}_{\{g\text{ is isolated}\}}\big)=(1-p)^{n-\kappa-1}(1-p)^{n-\kappa-1}(1-p)=(1-p)^{2(n-\kappa)-1}

and hence

\displaystyle\textup{Var}(I)

\displaystyle={n\choose\kappa}(1-p)^{n-\kappa}(1-(1-p)^{n-\kappa})+{n\choose\kappa-1}{n-\kappa+1\choose 2}p(1-p)^{2(n-\kappa)-1}.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.4
, this yields
\textup{Var}(I)\asymp n^{\kappa+1}p
, as
n\to\infty
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

As already noted above, all assumptions of Theorem
4.1
are satisfied and it remains to bound the terms
B_{1},\ldots,B_{5}
. To do so, we start by controlling the discrete gradient
D_{k}F
for
k=1,\ldots,{n\choose\kappa+1}
. By definition,

D_{k}F={\sqrt{pq}\over\sqrt{\textup{Var}(I)}}\big(I_{k}^{+}-I_{k}^{-}\big).

By construction of the random
\kappa
-complex, we have that
|I_{k}^{+}-I_{k}^{-}|\leq\kappa+1
almost surely, implying the bound

|D_{k}F|\leq{(\kappa+1)\sqrt{pq}\over\sqrt{\textup{Var}(I)}}.

Furthermore, the iterated discrete gradient
D_{\ell}D_{k}F
,
k,\ell=1,\ldots,{n\choose\kappa+1}
can be non-zero only when
f_{k}
and
f_{\ell}
share a common
(\kappa-1)
-face. So,

|D_{\ell}D_{k}F|\leq{2(\kappa+1)pq\over\sqrt{\textup{Var}(I)}}{\bf 1}_{\{f_{k}\text{ and }f_{\ell}\text{ share a common $(\kappa-1)$-face}\}}

by the triangle inequality and the bound for the first-order discrete gradient.

We can now bound the five terms in Theorem
4.1
. First, we obtain

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle\leq{4(\kappa+1)^{4}(pq)^{3}\over(\textup{Var}(I))^{2}}\sum_{j,k,\ell=1}^{n\choose\kappa+1}{\bf 1}_{\{f_{j}\text{ and }f_{\ell}\text{ share a common $(\kappa-1)$-face}\}}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\times{\bf 1}_{\{f_{k}\text{ and }f_{\ell}\text{ share a common $(\kappa-1)$-face}\}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\Big({p^{3}\over(n^{\kappa+1}p)^{2}}n^{\kappa+1}n^{2}\Big)=\mathcal{O}(pn^{-\kappa+1}),

and we can obtain the same bound for
B_{2}
. For
B_{3}
, we have

\displaystyle B_{3}

\displaystyle\leq{(\kappa+1)^{4}pq\over(\textup{Var}(I))^{2}}{n\choose\kappa+1}=\mathcal{O}\Big({p\over(n^{\kappa+1}p)^{2}}n^{\kappa+1}\Big)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-(\kappa+1)}p^{-1}).

For
B_{4}
, we deduce the inequality

\displaystyle B_{4}

\displaystyle\leq{4(\kappa+1)^{4}(pq)^{2}\over(\textup{Var}(I))^{2}}\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n\choose\kappa+1}{\bf 1}_{\{f_{k}\text{ and }f_{\ell}\text{ share a common $(\kappa-1)$-face}\}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\Big({p^{2}\over(n^{\kappa+1}p)^{2}}n^{\kappa+1}n\Big)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\kappa})

and the same bound also holds for
B_{5}
. As a consequence, from Theorem
4.1
we get

d_{K}(F,N)=\mathcal{O}(p^{1/2}n^{(-\kappa+1)/2}+n^{-(\kappa+1)/2}p^{-1/2}+n^{-\kappa/2})=\mathcal{O}(n^{-(\kappa+1)/2}p^{-1/2}),

and the proof of Theorem
1.4
is complete.
∎

4.3

9.
Proofs V: Vertices of fixed degree in hypercube percolation

In this section we study the number of vertices
V_{d}
of a fixed degree
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
in the random graph
{\bf H}(n,p)
. Observe that
V_{d}
can be written as a sum of random variables
I_{i}
, where

I_{i}:=\textbf{1}_{\{\text{$i$th vertex has degree $d$}\}}.

Further recall that the
n
-dimensional hypercube has exactly
2^{n}
vertices and that exactly
n
of its edges meet at each of its vertices (in geometry one says that the
n
-dimensional hypercube is a ‘simple’ polytope). Thus,

\mathbb{E}[I_{i}]=p^{d}(1-p)^{n-d}\binom{n}{d}\,\,\text{ for all $i\in\{1,\ldots,2^{n}\},$}

and so

\mathbb{E}[V_{d}]=2^{n}\binom{n}{d}p^{d}(1-p)^{n-d}\,.

To compute the variance of
V_{d}
, we use

\textup{Var}(V_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\textup{Var}(I_{i})+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2^{n}}\textup{Cov}(I_{i},I_{j})\textbf{1}_{\{i\neq j\}}\quad{\rm and}\quad\textup{Var}(I_{i})=\mathbb{E}[I_{i}](1-\mathbb{E}[I_{i}]).

Moreover, if the vertices
i
and
j
are not adjacent in the hypercube (denoted by
i\not\sim j
), then the random variables
I_{i}
and
I_{j}
are independent, which implies that
\textup{Cov}(I_{i},I_{j})=0
. Moreover, if
i
and
j
are neighbouring vertices in the hypercube (denoted by
i\sim j
) and
d\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[I_{i}I_{j}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[I_{i}I_{j}\,|\,\text{$i\sim j$ in $\mathbf{H}(n,p)$}\big]\mathbb{P}\big[\text{$i\sim j$ in $\mathbf{H}(n,p)$}\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{E}[I_{i}I_{j}\,|\,\text{$i\not\sim j$ in $\mathbf{H}(n,p)$}\big]\mathbb{P}\big[\text{$i\not\sim j$ in $\mathbf{H}(n,p)$}\big]

\displaystyle={n-1\choose d-1}^{2}p^{2d-1}(1-p)^{2(n-d)}+{n-1\choose d}^{2}p^{2d}(1-p)^{2(n-d)-1}\,,

where
\big\{i\sim j
in
\mathbf{H}(n,p)\big\}
stands for the event that the neighbouring vertices
i,j
are connected by an edge in the random graph
\mathbf{H}(n,p)
. If
i\sim j
and
d=0
, we easily get

\mathbb{E}[I_{i}I_{j}]=(1-p)^{2n-1}.

Therefore, when
i\sim j
in the hypercube,

\displaystyle\textup{Cov}(I_{i},I_{j})=\begin{cases}{\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{d}^{2}p^{2d-1}(1-p)^{2n-2d-1}\left[\frac{d^{2}(1-p)}{(n-d)^{2}}+p-\frac{n^{2}p(1-p)}{(n-d)^{2}}\right]}&\text{if}~d\geq 1;\\
(1-p)^{2n-1}p&\text{if}~d=0.\end{cases}

It follows that for
d\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\textup{Var}(V_{d})

\displaystyle=2^{n}\textup{Var}(I_{1})+\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\sum_{j:j\sim i}\textup{Cov}(I_{i},I_{j})

\displaystyle=2^{n}\binom{n}{d}p^{d}(1-p)^{n-d}\left(1-\binom{n}{d}p^{d}(1-p)^{n-d}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+2^{n}n\binom{n-1}{d}^{2}p^{2d-1}(1-p)^{2n-2d-1}\left[\frac{d^{2}(1-p)}{(n-d)^{2}}+p-\frac{n^{2}p(1-p)}{(n-d)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=2^{n}\binom{n}{d}p^{d}(1-p)^{n-d}+2^{n}n\binom{n-1}{d}^{2}p^{2d-1}(1-p)^{2n-2d-1}\left[\frac{d^{2}(1-p)}{(n-d)^{2}}+p\right]

since the hypercube has exactly
n2^{n-1}
edges. For
d=0
,

\textup{Var}(V_{0})=2^{n}(1-p)^{n}-2^{n}(1-p)^{2n}+n2^{n}(1-p)^{2n-1}p\geq 2^{n+1}np(1-p)^{2n-1}.

When
p\to 0
or
p\to 1
slower than exponential, we have that for any
{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
,

\textup{Var}(V_{0})\geq(2-{\varepsilon})^{n}
for sufficiently large
n
.

When
p\to 0
or
p\to 1
slower than exponential, we also have that for
d\geq 1
,

\textup{Var}(V_{d})\geq(2-{\varepsilon})^{n}
for sufficiently large
n
.

We can now prove Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
e_{1},...,e_{n2^{n-1}}
be arbitrarily fixed labelling of the
n2^{n-1}
edges in the hypercube. Define

X_{i}:=\textbf{1}_{\{\text{$e_{i}$ is kept in $\mathbf{H}(n,p)$}\}}-\textbf{1}_{\{\text{$e_{i}$ is removed in $\mathbf{H}(n,p)$}\}},\qquad i\in\{1,...,n2^{n-1}\},

which is a sequence of independent and identically distributed Rademacher random variables with success probability
p
(we write
q=1-p
in what follows). The random variable
F_{d}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
only depends on a finite sequence of Rademacher random variables, then all assumptions in Theorem
4.1
are satisfied and we just need to bound the terms
B_{1},\ldots,B_{5}
there. For this, we first notice that the first-order discrete gradient and second-order discrete gradient
D_{k}F
,
k=1,\ldots,n2^{n-1}
, and
D_{\ell}D_{k}F
,
k,\ell=1,\ldots,n2^{n-1}
, are bounded as follows:

|D_{k}F|\leq{2\sqrt{pq}\over\sqrt{\textup{Var}(V_{d})}}\quad{\rm and}\quad|D_{\ell}D_{k}F|\leq{4pq\over\sqrt{\textup{Var}(V_{d})}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{|e_{k}\cap e_{\ell}|=1\}},

where we recall that
\{|e_{k}\cap e_{\ell}|=1\}
means the edges
e_{k}
and
e_{\ell}
have exactly one common endpoint. The proof is exactly the same as in the proof of Theorem
1.3
. Hence, using what we already have computed for
B_{1},\ldots,B_{5}
in the proof of this theorem and recalling that the hypercube has exactly
n2^{n-1}
edges, we see that

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle\leq{64(pq)^{3}\over(\textup{Var}(V_{d}))^{2}}\sum_{j,k,\ell=1}^{n2^{n-1}}\textbf{1}_{\{|e_{j}\cap e_{\ell}|=|e_{k}\cap e_{\ell}|=1\}},

\displaystyle B_{4}

\displaystyle\leq{64(pq)^{2}\over(\textup{Var}(V_{d}))^{2}}\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n2^{n-1}}{\bf 1}_{\{|e_{k}\cap e_{\ell}|=1\}},

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle\leq{256(pq)^{3}\over(\textup{Var}(V_{d}))^{2}}\sum_{j,k,\ell=1}^{n2^{n-1}}\textbf{1}_{\{|e_{j}\cap e_{\ell}|=|e_{k}\cap e_{\ell}|=1\}},

\displaystyle B_{5}

\displaystyle\leq{256(pq)^{2}\over(\textup{Var}(V_{d}))^{2}}\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n2^{n-1}}{\bf 1}_{\{|e_{k}\cap e_{\ell}|=1\}},

\displaystyle B_{3}

\displaystyle\leq{16pq\over(\textup{Var}(V_{d}))^{2}}\,n2^{n-1}\,.

Next, we notice that the sums in
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are equal to
n2^{n-1}(2(n-1)(n-2)+(n-1)^{2})
, since there are
n2^{n-1}
choices for
e_{\ell}
, and
2(n-1)(n-2)
possibilities to select
e_{j}
and
e_{k}
at the same endpoint of
e_{\ell}
and
(n-1)^{2}
possibilities to choose
e_{j}
and
e_{k}
at different endpoints of
e_{\ell}
. As a result,

\sum_{j,k,\ell=1}^{n2^{n-1}}\textbf{1}_{\{|e_{j}\cap e_{\ell}|=|e_{k}\cap e_{\ell}|=1\}}\leq 3n^{3}2^{n-1}=\mathcal{O}\big((2+{\varepsilon})^{n}\big)

for any
{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
. Moreover, the sum in
B_{4}
and
B_{5}
evaluates to

\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n2^{n-1}}{\bf 1}_{\{|e_{k}\cap e_{\ell}|=1\}}=n(n-1)2^{n}=\mathcal{O}\big((2+{\varepsilon})^{n}\big)

for any
{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
, since there are
n2^{n-1}
choices for
e_{\ell}
and then
2(n-1)
possibilities to select the second edge
e_{k}
adjacent to
e_{\ell}
.

Recall that for
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and for any
{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
,
\textup{Var}(V_{d})\geq(2-{\varepsilon})^{n}
for sufficiently large
n
. As a consequence and since
p\to 0
or
p\to 1
slower than exponential in
n
, we see that, for any
{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
,

B_{1}+\ldots+B_{5}=\mathcal{O}\big((2-{\varepsilon})^{-n}\big).

The desired result is now a consequence of Theorem
4.1
.
∎
