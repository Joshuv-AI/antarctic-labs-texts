Box constraints and weighted sparsity regularization for identifying sources in elliptic PDEs
Ole Løseth Elvetun, Bjørn Fredrik Nielsen

Abstract
We explore the possibility for using boundary data to identify sources in elliptic PDEs. Even though the associated forward operator has a large null space, it turns out that box constraints, combined with weighted sparsity regularization, can enable rather accurate recovery of sources with constant magnitude/strength. In addition, for sources with varying strength, the support of the inverse solution will be a subset of the support of the true source. We present both an analysis of the problem and a series of numerical experiments. Our work only addresses discretized problems. The reason for introducing the weighting procedure is that standard (unweighted) sparsity regularization fails to provide adequate results for the source identification task considered in this paper. This investigation is also motivated by applications, e.g., recovering mass distributions from measurements of gravitational fields and inverse scattering. We develop the methodology and the analysis in terms of Euclidean spaces, and our results can therefore be applied to many problems. For example, the results are equally applicable to models involving the screened Poisson equation as to models using the Helmholtz equation, with both large and small wave numbers.



Keywords:

Inverse source problems, null space, sparsity regularization, box constraints, PDE-constrained optimization.

2
Analysis

Let

K_{h}:f\mapsto u|_{\partial\Omega}

\min_{0\leq{\mathbf{x}}\leq s}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathsf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\sum_{i}w_{i}|x_{i}|\right\}.

Here,
{\mathbf{x}}

-orthonormal basis

(5)

As mentioned above, for the problem (
1

2

(6)

where

denotes the characteristic function of the
i

Let

denote the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries

. Then the associated basis pursuit (zero regularization limit) problem to (

\min_{0\leq{\mathbf{x}}\leq s}\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}\quad\textnormal{subject to}\quad{\mathsf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{b}}.

(8)

Since only boundary data is available for the recovery of

and the matrix

will have nontrivial null spaces. This differs significantly from the typical setup for optimal control problems, which often assumes observations of the state

Our work is motivated by the inverse problems mentioned above. But for the sake of generality, we will develop the methodology and analysis in terms of Euclidean spaces. It should also be mentioned that it remains an open problem how to establish an infinite dimensional counterpart to the results presented in this paper.

2.1
Diagonal weight/regularization matrix
{\mathsf{W}}

Assume that

The authors of

\displaystyle=\argmax_{i\in\{1,2,...,n\}}|[{\mathsf{W}}^{-1}{\mathbf{x}}^{\dagger}]_{i}|

Remarks

In order to obtain a ”sound” regularization operator
{\mathsf{W}}
, as defined in (
12
), one should ensure that
\min_{i}\{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}\}
is not too small. This follows from the observation that, if

, then one tries to recover the contribution associated with a basis vector
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
which is almost in the null space of the matrix
{\mathsf{A}}
. Thus, in order to avoid to get ”very close” to the null space, one would typically use a rather moderate number

of basis functions

to discretize the source

in (
1
)-(
2
),
see (
5
) and (
6
). This is our motivation for employing a finite dimensional space for

in (

)-(
2

The computation of

involves the pseudoinverse/Moore-Penrose inverse

of

. If

has small nonzero singular values, which will typically be the case for a discretized ill posed problem, then it is not advisable to apply

in computations, and we must ”replace”

by a more well-behaved matrix. This can, e.g., be accomplished by employing truncated SVD or by applying Tikhonov regularization, with regularization parameter

({\mathsf{A}}^{T}{\mathsf{A}}+\gamma{\mathsf{I}}){\mathbf{x}}_{\gamma}={\mathsf{A}}^{T}{\mathsf{A}}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}.

Then, according to standard theory,

. In our numerical experiments (section
3
) we used truncated SVD
to compute an approximation of the entries in the diagonal regularization matrix
{\mathsf{W}}
.

is non-increasing because the size of the constraining set
\{{\mathbf{x}}\,|\,0\leq{\mathbf{x}}\leq s\}
increases as
s
increases.

Since
\|{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{\infty}=k
, it follows from Theorem
2.1
that
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
solves

\min_{0\leq{\mathbf{x}}\leq s}\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}\quad\textnormal{subject to}\quad{\mathsf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}={\mathsf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}

(30)

when
s\geq k
. Consequently, (
29
) must hold.

According to Corollary
2.1.3
,
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
is the unique solution of (
30
) when
s=k
. Hence, it follows that (
28
) must be true.
∎

If the true source(s) has constant strength, we thus obtain the following scheme for recovering both its size and magnitude
k
:

Algorithm

•

Compute

g(s)=\min_{0\leq{\mathbf{x}}\leq s}\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}\quad\textnormal{subject to}\quad{\mathsf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{b}}

(31)

many times, varying size of the upper bound
s
.

•

The strength
k
is then the number such that
g(s)=c
(a constant) for
s\geq k
and
g(s)>c
for
s<k
.

•

Corollary
2.1.3
assures the perfect recovery of the true constant strength source by solving (
31
) with
s=k
.

We will explore a regularized version of this algorithm in the numerical experiments section below.

Remarks about assumptions (
16
) and (
17
)

Our results rely on whether there exists a vector
{\mathbf{c}}
such that (
16
) and (
17
) hold. This is a subtle question, which we will now discuss in some detail.

For example, if the cardinality of
\mathcal{J}
equals two, i.e.,
\mathcal{J}=\{j_{1},j_{2}\}
, then (
16
) holds with

{\mathbf{c}}=\left(1+\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{1}}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{1}}\|_{2}}\cdot\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{2}}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{2}}\|_{2}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{1}}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{1}}\|_{2}}+\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{2}}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{2}}\|_{2}}\right).

On the other hand, (
17
) may fail to hold for this particular choice of
{\mathbf{c}}
.

More generally, using the notation
{\mathbf{c}}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}c_{l}{\mathbf{e}}_{l}
, (
16
) leads to a linear system with
n
unknowns
c_{1},\,c_{2},\ldots,\,c_{n}
,

\sum_{l=1}^{n}{\mathbf{e}}_{l}\cdot\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}}\,c_{l}-1=0\quad\forall j\in\mathcal{J},

(32)

which must have a solution obeying the inequality constraints, see (
17
),

\begin{split}&\sum_{l=1}^{n}{\mathbf{e}}_{l}\cdot\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}}\,c_{l}-1<0\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{J}^{c}.\end{split}

(33)

In other words, the solution set of (
32
) must overlap with the convex domain specified in (
33
).
This issue can be explored with standard software tools: We suggest to solve the following linear programming problem (LPP), where
\delta>0
is small fixed number,

\max\sum_{i\in\mathcal{J}^{c}}\epsilon_{i}

(34)

subject to
\{c_{l}\}
and
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
satisfying

\begin{split}&\sum_{l=1}^{n}{\mathbf{e}}_{l}\cdot\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}}\,c_{l}-1=0\quad\forall j\in\mathcal{J},\\
&\sum_{l=1}^{n}{\mathbf{e}}_{l}\cdot\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}}\,c_{l}-1+\delta+\epsilon_{i}=0\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{J}^{c},\\
&\epsilon_{i}\geq 0\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{J}^{c},\end{split}

(35)

where
\{\epsilon_{i}\,|\,i\in\mathcal{J}^{c}\}
are slack variables. If one during the solution process of (
34
)-(
35
) detects
\{c_{l}\}
and
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
which satisfy the constraints (
35
), then one can stop the LPP program and conclude that the solution set of (
32
) overlaps with the convex domain described in (
33
). That is, there exists a vector
{\mathbf{c}}
such that (
16
) and (
17
) hold. We also note that this approach only yields a sufficient criteria for asserting that (
16
) and (
17
) are satisfied.

Let us also comment that, if the cardinality of the index set
\mathcal{J}
is significantly smaller than the number
n
of columns in the matrix
{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, then the solution set of (
32
) will typically be relatively large and it becomes more ”likely” that there exists
{\mathbf{c}}
such that (
33
) holds. Similarly, if the size of
\mathcal{J}
increases, then one would expect that the solution set of (
32
) decreases and it becomes less ”likely” that (
33
) is satisfied, i.e., perfect source recovery with (
18
) or (
26
) will ”probably” not be possible. (Here we use ”likely” and ”probably” in an informal manner.)

In the event that there does
not
exist
{\mathbf{c}}
such that (
32
) and (
33
) hold, one might wonder whether it is possible to bound the size of the support of a solution
{\mathbf{y}}
of (
18
). That is, to what extent will the size of the support of the true source
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}=\sum_{\mathcal{J}}x_{j}^{*}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}
potentially be increased by solving (
18
)?

To investigate this issue, assume that
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
is an extension of
\mathcal{J}
such that properties analogous to (
16
) and (
17
) hold: There exists a vector
{\mathbf{c}}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}}\cdot{\mathbf{c}}

\displaystyle=1\quad\forall j\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime},

(36)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}}\cdot{\mathbf{c}}

\displaystyle<1\quad\forall i\in(\mathcal{J}^{\prime})^{c}.

(37)

Using the same techniques as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, we prove the following result.

Proposition 2.2
.

Any solution
{\mathbf{y}}
of (
18
) satisfies

\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{y}})\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\prime}

for any index set
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathcal{J}=\textnormal{supp}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})
satisfying (
36
)-(
37
).
Consequently, a solution of (
18
) must obey

\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{y}})\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{J}},

where

\overline{\mathcal{J}}=\bigcap\{\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\;|\;\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathcal{J}\mbox{ and }\exists\,{\mathbf{c}}\mbox{ such that }\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\mbox{ satisfies \eqref{IC12}-\eqref{IC13}}\},

and we assume that
\overline{\mathcal{J}}\neq\emptyset
. This holds for any
s\in[\|{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{\infty},\infty]
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{y}}
denote a solution of (
18
). Very similar to the reasoning leading to (
19
), we find that

\sum_{(\mathcal{J}^{\prime})^{c}\cup\mathcal{J}^{\prime}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}}\cdot{\mathbf{c}}=\sum_{\mathcal{J}}x_{j}^{*}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}.

Assuming that there exists
i^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{J}^{\prime})^{c}
such that
y_{i}>0
, we can argue as we did in
(
20
)-(
21
) to conclude that

\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1}>\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1},

where
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}=\sum_{\mathcal{J}}x_{j}^{*}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}
. Since
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
satisfies the constraints in (
18
), there can not exist such
i^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{J}^{\prime})^{c}
. It follows that
\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{y}})\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
.

∎

As far as we understand, this result is primarily of theoretical interest because in practice it seems difficult to compute the smallest extension
\overline{\mathcal{J}}
. We also note that, even though there exists, by assumption, a vector
{\mathbf{c}}
for each
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
such that (
36
) and (
37
) hold, it does not follow from our argument that
\overline{\mathcal{J}}
satisfies these conditions.

2.3
Optimality conditions

In the previous subsection we showed that the support of any solution of (
18
) is a subset of the support of the true source
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
given the assumptions (
16
)-(
17
). We will now show that these assumptions, which we motivated from a geometrical point view, in fact correspond to the existence of a KKT/Lagrange multiplier for the optimality system associated with (
18
).

First, since
{\mathsf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}={\mathsf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\Leftrightarrow\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}=\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
, recall that
\mathsf{P}={\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}{\mathsf{A}}
, we can formulate the standard KKT-conditions for (
18
), see, e.g.,
[
24
, Theorem 3.34]
, with
s=\infty
, as

\displaystyle{\mathsf{W}}\partial\|{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}-\mathsf{P}\bm{\lambda}-\bm{\mu}

\displaystyle\ni

\displaystyle\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}-\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\bm{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mu}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbf{0},

where
\partial
denotes the subgradient, and we have used the chain rule and the fact that the diagonal entries of
{\mathsf{W}}
are positive:

\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}={\mathsf{W}}\partial\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}={\mathsf{W}}\partial\|{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}.

Recall that
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}=\sum_{\mathcal{J}}x_{j}^{*}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}
,
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\geq\mathbf{0}
.
Thus, for
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
to be a solution of (
18
) it follows that there must exist some
\bm{\lambda}^{*}
and
\bm{\mu}^{*}
such that

[{\mathsf{W}}\partial\|{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}]_{i}\ni\begin{cases}[\mathsf{P}\bm{\lambda}^{*}]_{i},\quad&i\in\mathcal{J},\\
[\mathsf{P}\bm{\lambda}^{*}]_{i}+\mu_{i}^{*},\quad&i\in\mathcal{J}^{c}.\end{cases}

(38)

(Where
[\mathbf{v}]_{i}
represents the
i
’th component of the Euclidean vector
\mathbf{v}
).
Here we have used the implication

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\cdot\bm{\mu}^{*}=0\\
{\mathbf{x}}^{*},\bm{\mu}^{*}\geq\mathbf{0}\\
\mathcal{J}=\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\end{array}\right\}\Longrightarrow\;\;\mu_{i}^{*}=0\;\;\forall i\in\mathcal{J}.

If we evaluate the subgradient

\left[\partial\|{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}\right]_{i}=\begin{cases}\{1\},\quad&i\in\mathcal{J},\\
[-1,1],\quad&i\in\mathcal{J}^{c},\end{cases}

in (
38
), and combine it with

[\mathsf{P}\bm{\lambda}^{*}]_{i}=\mathsf{P}\bm{\lambda^{*}}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{i}=\bm{\lambda}^{*}\cdot\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i},

we derive that

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot\bm{\lambda}^{*},\quad j\in\mathcal{J},

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot\bm{\lambda}^{*},\quad j\in\mathcal{J}^{c},

because
{\mathsf{W}}=\textnormal{diag}\left(\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\|_{2},\,\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\|_{2},\,\ldots,\,\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{n}\|_{2}\right)
, see (
12
), and due to the fact that
\bm{\mu}^{*}\geq\mathbf{0}
.

If we have strict complementary slackness, i.e.,
\mu^{*}_{i}>0
whenever
x^{*}_{i}=0
, we get the stronger result

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot\bm{\lambda}^{*},\quad j\in\mathcal{J},

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot\bm{\lambda}^{*},\quad j\in\mathcal{J}^{c},

which are assumptions (
16
)-(
17
) with
\mathbf{c}=\bm{\lambda}^{*}
.

2.4
Regularized problems

Since
\mathsf{P}={\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}{\mathsf{A}}
, see (
11
), the null spaces of
\mathsf{P}
and
{\mathsf{A}}
coincide, and we may write the basis pursuit problem (
8
) in the form

\min_{0\leq{\mathbf{x}}\leq s}\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}\quad\textnormal{subject to}\quad\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}={\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{b}}.

(39)

We also recall that the entries of the regularization matrix
{\mathsf{W}}
(
12
) are defined in terms of the projection
\mathsf{P}
. Based on these observations, and due to mathematical convenience, we will in this subsection analyse a regularized version of (
39
) instead of (
4
):

\min_{0\leq{\mathbf{x}}\leq s}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\sum_{i}w_{i}|x_{i}|\right\}.

(40)

More precisely, if
{\mathbf{b}}={\mathbf{b}}^{\dagger}={\mathsf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
, to what extent can we recover
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
by solving (
40
)? Note that, in this case
{\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{b}}^{\dagger}={\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}{\mathsf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}=\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
.

We remark that we have
not
managed to prove results similar to those presented in this subsection for the problem (
4
). In fact, numerical experiments indicate that we do not necessarily obtain as good reconstructions with (
4
) as with (
40
) when
\alpha>0
is not very close to zero.

We now prove a regularized counterpart to Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}=\sum_{\mathcal{J}}x_{j}^{*}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\geq 0
,
\mathcal{J}=\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})
, and assume that there exist a vector
{\mathbf{d}}
and a constant
\gamma<1
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}

\displaystyle=1\quad\forall j\in\mathcal{J},

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}

\displaystyle<\gamma\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{J}^{c}.

(42)

Then any

{\mathbf{y}}\in\argmin_{0\leq{\mathbf{x}}\leq s}\underbrace{\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}-\mathsf{P}\overbrace{\sum_{\mathcal{J}}x_{j}^{*}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}^{={\mathbf{x}}^{*}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}\right\}}_{:=\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}})}

(43)

satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1}-\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq c\,\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{1-\gamma}

(44)

and

\displaystyle\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1,\mathcal{J}^{c}}:=\sum_{\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq c\,\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{1-\gamma},

(45)

provided that
s\in[\|{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{\infty},\infty]
. Here,
c
is a constant which is independent of
\alpha
,
\gamma
and
s
, but depends on
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
and
{\mathbf{d}}
.

Remark

If there exists a vector
{\mathbf{c}}
such that (
16
) and (
17
) hold, then there is a vector
{\mathbf{d}}
and constant
\gamma<1
such that (
41
) and (
42
) are satisfied because we consider finite dimensional problems:
{\mathbf{d}}={\mathbf{c}}
and choose
\gamma
such that

\displaystyle\max_{i\in\mathcal{J}^{c}}\left\{\frac{\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}}{\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}}\cdot{\mathbf{c}}\right\}<\gamma<1.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Assume that
{\mathbf{y}}
belongs to the
\argmin
set defined in (
43
). Then, because
s\geq\|{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{\infty}
,

\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{y}})\leq\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})=\alpha\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1},

which implies that

\displaystyle\alpha\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1},

(46)

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\tau}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1},

(47)

where

{\bm{\tau}}=\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{y}}-\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}.

Now,

\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{y}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}=\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}},

which leads to

\sum_{\mathcal{J}\cup\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}=\sum_{\mathcal{J}}x_{j}^{*}\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}.

Combining assumptions (
41
) and (
42
) with the constraint
y_{i}\geq 0
yield that

\gamma\sum_{\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}+\sum_{\mathcal{J}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}\geq\sum_{\mathcal{J}}x_{j}^{*}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}

or, also using the fact that
\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}=\sum_{\mathcal{J}}x_{j}^{*}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}
and (
46
),

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{J}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}-\gamma\sum_{\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}

\displaystyle\geq\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}-\gamma\left[\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}-\sum_{\mathcal{J}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}\right]+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}+\gamma\sum_{\mathcal{J}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}},

which, since
\gamma<1
, implies that

\sum_{\mathcal{J}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}\geq\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}+\frac{{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}}{1-\gamma}.

(48)

From (
46
), keeping in mind that
y_{i}\geq 0
and invoking (

), it follows that

\displaystyle\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}\geq\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{J}\cup\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\mathcal{J}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}+\frac{{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}}{1-\gamma},

(49)

and therefore

\displaystyle\left|\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1}-\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}}{1-\gamma}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\sqrt{2}\|{\mathbf{d}}\|_{2}\sqrt{\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}}}_{:=c}\,\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{1-\gamma},

where we have used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (

).

Combing (

) and (

\displaystyle\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}\geq\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{J}\cup\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}

which implies that

where we have also used the constraint

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}}{1-\gamma}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\|{\mathbf{d}}\|_{2}\sqrt{\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}}\,\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{1-\gamma},

which completes the proof of (

). (Note also that (

The next result contains the regularized version of Corollary
2.1.4
. That is, we consider the possibility for determining the strength

{\mathbf{x}}^{*}=k\sum_{\mathcal{J}}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}.

Corollary 2.3.1
.

If assumptions (
41
) and (

\mathcal{J}=\textnormal{supp}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})

{\mathbf{y}}\in\argmin_{0\leq{\mathbf{x}}\leq s}\underbrace{\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}-\mathsf{P}\overbrace{k\sum_{\mathcal{J}}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}^{={\mathbf{x}}^{*}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}\right\}}_{=\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}})}

\displaystyle\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1}\geq\left(1+\frac{(1-\gamma)(k-s)}{k\gamma}\right)\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}-C\,\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{s}\sqrt{\alpha}\quad\mbox{for }s\in[0,k)\cap S,

(51)

\displaystyle\left|\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1}-\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}\right|\leq c\,\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{1-\gamma}\quad\mbox{for }s\geq k,

(52)

where the set

is defined in (

and

are constants which are independent of
\gamma
,

and
\alpha

Proof.

Inequality (
52
) is the same as inequality (

), and we will thus focus on (

and assume that
{\mathbf{y}}
belongs to the
\argmin

) of

{\mathbf{z}}

\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{z}}=\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}

\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{y}})\leq\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{z}})=\alpha\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{z}}\|_{1}\leq\alpha s\|{\mathsf{W}}\mathbf{1}\|_{1},

\displaystyle\alpha\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha s\|{\mathsf{W}}\mathbf{1}\|_{1},

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\tau}}\|_{2}^{2}

Next,

\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{y}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}=\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}

\sum_{\mathcal{J}\cup\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}=k\sum_{\mathcal{J}}\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}},

\gamma\sum_{\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}+\sum_{\mathcal{J}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}\geq k\sum_{\mathcal{J}}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}.

(55)

It thus follows that

\sum_{\mathcal{J}\cup\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}\geq(1-\gamma)\sum_{\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}+k\sum_{\mathcal{J}}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}},

or

\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1}\geq(1-\gamma)\sum_{\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}+\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}

(56)

because
\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1}=\sum_{\mathcal{J}\cup\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}
and
\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}=k\sum_{\mathcal{J}}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}
.

Employing the constraint
0\leq{\mathbf{y}}\leq s
and (
55
) we find that

\displaystyle\gamma\sum_{\mathcal{J}^{c}}y_{i}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq(k-s)\sum_{\mathcal{J}}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\|_{2}+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k-s}{k}\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}+{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}},

which combined with (
56
) yields

\displaystyle\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{y}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\gamma)\frac{(k-s)}{k\gamma}\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}+\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{\gamma}{\bm{\tau}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1+\frac{(1-\gamma)(k-s)}{k\gamma}\right)\|{\mathsf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|_{1}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{\alpha}\sqrt{s}\underbrace{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\|{\mathsf{W}}\mathbf{1}\|_{1}}\|{\mathbf{d}}\|_{2}}_{=C},

where we have also used (
54
).
This finishes the proof.
∎

Numerical experiments

We discretized the boundary value problem (
2
) with the finite element method, using first-order Lagrange elements, which yields the stiffness matrix
\mathsf{L}_{\epsilon}
. The forward matrix associated with (
1
)-(
2

{\mathsf{A}}=\mathsf{M}_{\partial}^{1/2}\mathsf{L}_{\epsilon}^{-1}\mathsf{M},

where
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{M}_{\partial}
denote the standard and ”boundary” mass matrices, respectively. With this approach, the local ”hat” finite element functions
\{\psi_{i}\}
are directly associated with the standard unit basis vectors
\{{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\}
.

Motivated by the discussion presented at the beginning of subsection
2.4
, we performed numerical experiments with

\min_{0\leq{\mathbf{x}}\leq s}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|\mathsf{P}{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\sum_{i}w_{i}|x_{i}|\right\}

(57)

instead of using (
4
). Here, the matrices
\mathsf{P}={\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}{\mathsf{A}}
and
{\mathsf{W}}
are defined in equations (
11
) and (
12
), respectively, and the size of the regularization parameter
\alpha
is specified in the captions of the figures below. As is mentioned at the end of subsection
2.1
, truncated SVD, using
20
singular values, was employed to obtain a computationally reliable approximation of
{\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}
.

We numerically tested whether one can recover
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
by solving (
57
) when
{\mathbf{b}}={\mathbf{b}}^{\dagger}={\mathsf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
.
If not stated otherwise, the true source always had constant unit strength:

{\mathbf{x}}^{*}=\sum_{\mathcal{J}}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}.

Since
\mathsf{P}
has a nontrivial null space and the regularization term in (
57
) is not strictly convex, (
57
) can potentially have several solutions. This may be ”handled” by adding a small amount of quadratic regularization. However, we did not do that, and instead we present the outcome of applying the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) to (
57
): Using the zero initial guess and performing
5000
iterations. We employed Python, in combination with the FEniCS software tool, to realize the numerical experiments.

According to corollaries
2.1.1
and
2.1.3
, solving the basis pursuit problem (
18
) can, under certain circumstances, lead to
exact
recovery of the true source(s), even though
{\mathsf{A}}
has a large null space. In order to illuminate these results, we committed an ”inverse crime” in our two first examples: The same coarse
17\times 17
grid was used to represent the source(s) in both the forward and inverse simulations, and the state
u
was discretized in terms of a
33\times 33
mesh. With this setup, the true source can be exactly represented on the grid used in the inverse computations. This leads to unrealistically accurate recoveries in the sense that, in addition to finding the position, size and magnitude, also the exact shape of the source(s) is recovered – as we will see in examples 1 and 2 below. On the other had, let us emphasize that, even when inverse crimes are committed, the recovery of
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
is not trivial because
{\mathsf{A}}
has a large null space: In example 1 we show that unweighted sparsity regularization fails to provide adequate results.

In examples 3-5 we avoided inverse crimes: The synthetic boundary data
{\mathbf{b}}
was generated using a grid with
97\times 97
nodes for both the source and the state, whereas a
49\times 49
grid was used for these quantities in the inverse solution procedure.

The unit square
\Omega=(0,1)\times(0,1)
was used as domain, and in all the cases we ran experiments with both
\epsilon=-1
and
\epsilon=1
in (
2
), i.e., with both Helmholtz’ equation and the screened Poisson equation. The results obtained with these two state equations were visually indistinguishable, and we therefore, in each example, only present results obtained for one of these model problems: In example 1 and examples 2-5 we present results for
\epsilon=1

\epsilon=-1

4
Concluding remarks

The main purpose of this paper has been to address the non-uniqueness issue arising in connection with some inverse problems. We have explored this matter for the task of identifying the source term in an elliptic PDE from boundary data. Despite the presence of a large null space, we have shown that the use of weighted sparsity regularization and box constraints can lead to rather accurate recovery. In fact, in an idealized setting, exact reconstruction might be possible. We demonstrated this behavior for two test problems, where we, for the sake of exemplifying the exact recovery, had to commit the ”inverse crime” of using identical grids for the forward and inverse computations. Under less ideal conditions, i.e., without ”inverse crimes”, we still observe that the position and roughly the size of the sources can be computed. That is, the shape can not be perfectly reconstructed – which is as one would expect.

For the sake of generality, we developed the methodology and the analysis in terms of Euclidean spaces. Our approach can thus be applied to a broad range of inverse problems, assuming that one seeks sparse solutions. We presented mathematical results for both the basis pursuit formulation of the problem and for its regularized version. It remains to develop an infinite dimensional counterpart to the theory presented in this paper and to test the performance of the methodology on real-world data.

This investigation was motivated by the fact that standard (unweighted) sparsity regularization do not provide adequate results for the source identification task considered in this paper.
