1.
Introduction

In the study of integer partitions, the partitions wherein the parts are distinct have long played a key role, due in large part to Euler’s famous identity which states that the number of partitions of weight
n
into distinct parts equals the number of partitions of weight
n
into odd parts. One of the most obvious refinements in this regard is to require distinct parts based on parity; i.e., to require either all of the even parts to be distinct or all of the odd parts to be distinct (while allowing the frequency of the other parts to be unrestricted). This leads to two types of partitions, those that we will call PED partitions (wherein the even parts must be distinct and the odd parts are unrestricted) and POD partitions (wherein the odd parts must be distinct and the even parts are unrestricted). We then define two corresponding enumerating functions,
ped(n)
which counts the number of PED partitions of weight
n
, and
pod(n)
which counts the number of POD partitions of weight
n.
These two functions have been studied from a variety of perspectives; the interested reader may wish to see

Recently, Ballantine and Welch

generalized and refined these two functions in numerous ways. One of the outcomes of their work was to consider integer partitions which are, in some sense, the “opposite” of PED partitions and POD partitions. Namely, they considered PEND partitions and POND partitions, wherein the even (respectively, odd) parts are
not allowed
to be distinct. In a vein similar to that shared above, we let
pend(n)
denote the number of PEND partitions of weight
n
, and
pond(n)
denote the number of POND partitions of weight

The first several values of

It is worthwhile to share additional historical thoughts to place PEND and POND partitions in context. In his classic
Combinatory Analysis

[
16
]
, P. A. MacMahon proved that, for all
n\geq 0
, the number of partitions of weight
n
wherein no part appears with multiplicity one equals the number of partitions of weight
n
where all parts must be even or congruent to 3 modulo 6. As an aside, we note that numerous mathematicians have since generalized this theorem of MacMahon and have provided proofs of these results using both generating functions (which was MacMahon’s original approach) as well as combinatorial arguments. The first half of the statement of MacMahon’s theorem involves the function which counts the number of partitions wherein no part appears with multiplicity one, i.e., no part is allowed to be distinct. It is in this sense that POND and PEND partitions provide a natural, parity–based refinement of the partitions considered by MacMahon.

At the end of their paper, Ballantine and Welch

In particular, we note two areas of interest. The first is examining the arithmetic properties of these generalizations. Much work has been done in studying arithmetic properties of PED and POD partitions… Hence, this would be a natural topic of further study…

3.
Congruences for
pond(n)

We begin by considering the function
pond(n).
Although one can derive the generating function for
pond(n)
from the work of Ballantine and Welch
[
5
]

Theorem 15
.

We have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pond(n)q^{n}=\frac{f_{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{3}f_{12}}.

Proof.

By definition,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pond(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f_{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{1-q^{2i-1}}-q^{2i-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f_{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1-q^{2i-1}+q^{4i-2}}{1-q^{2i-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f_{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1+q^{6i-3}}{(1+q^{2i-1})(1-q^{2i-1})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f_{2}}\cdot\frac{(-q^{3};q^{6})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f_{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{4}}{f_{2}}\cdot\frac{(q^{6};q^{12})_{\infty}}{(q^{3};q^{6})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{4}}{f_{2}^{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{6}}{f_{12}}\cdot\frac{f_{6}}{f_{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{3}f_{12}}.

∎

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{4}}{f_{2}^{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{6}^{2}}{f_{3}f_{12}}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pond(9(3n+1)+8)q^{3n+1}

\displaystyle\equiv 2\frac{f_{3}^{2}}{f_{12}}\left(f_{6}^{4}\cdot q\frac{f_{6}^{2}f_{9}^{3}f_{36}}{f_{3}^{4}f_{18}^{2}}+q^{2}\frac{f_{12}^{8}}{f_{6}^{4}}\cdot 2q^{2}\frac{f_{6}f_{18}f_{36}}{f_{3}^{3}}\right)\pmod{3}

\displaystyle=2\frac{f_{4}^{2}}{f_{1}}\left(f_{1}^{8}+2qf_{4}^{8}\right).

Proof.

(of Theorem

We then want to prove that

Note that

\displaystyle 3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{23\cdot 3^{2\alpha}+1}{8}

\displaystyle=3\left(3^{2\alpha}n\right)+\frac{23\cdot 3^{2\alpha}-15+16}{8}

\displaystyle=3\left(3^{2\alpha}n\right)+3\left(\frac{23\cdot 3^{2\alpha-1}-5}{8}\right)+2

\displaystyle=3\left(3^{2\alpha}n+\frac{23\cdot 3^{2\alpha-1}-5}{8}\right)+2

and it is easy to argue that

. Therefore, we have the following:

\displaystyle pond\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{23\cdot 3^{2\alpha}+1}{8}\right)

\displaystyle=pond\left(3\left(3^{2\alpha}n+\frac{23\cdot 3^{2\alpha-1}-5}{8}\right)+2\right)

\displaystyle\equiv pond\left(27\left(3^{2\alpha}n+\frac{23\cdot 3^{2\alpha-1}-5}{8}\right)+17\right)\pmod{3}\textrm{\ \ \ thanks to Theorem \ref{thm:pond_internal}}

\displaystyle=pond\left(3^{2\alpha+3}n+\frac{23\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}-27\cdot 5+17\cdot 8}{8}\right)

\displaystyle=pond\left(3^{2\alpha+3}n+\frac{23\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}+1}{8}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3}

thanks to the induction hypothesis. This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Congruences for
pend(n)

We now turn our attention to proving Theorems
2
,
4
, and
6
.
We begin by finding the generating function for
pend(n).

Theorem 16
.

We have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(n)q^{n}=\frac{f_{2}f_{12}}{f_{1}f_{4}f_{6}}.

Proof.

Using the definition of the partitions counted by
pend(n)
, we
know

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{1-q^{2i}}-q^{2i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1-q^{2i}+q^{4i}}{1-q^{2i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1+q^{6i}}{(1+q^{2i})(1-q^{2i})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}}\frac{(-q^{6};q^{6})_{\infty}}{f_{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}}\cdot\frac{f_{12}}{f_{4}f_{6}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}f_{12}}{f_{1}f_{4}f_{6}}.

∎

We now turn our attention to proving Theorem
2
. This will require that we 3–dissect the generating function for
pend(n)
in a particular way.

Proof.

(of Theorem
2
)
Thanks to Theorem
16
, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}f_{12}}{f_{1}f_{4}f_{6}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{4}^{2}}{f_{1}f_{2}^{2}}\pmod{3}\textrm{\ \ from Lemma \ref{lemma:binthm_divisibility}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{4}^{3}}{f_{2}^{3}}\cdot\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}f_{4}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{12}}{f_{6}}\cdot\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}f_{4}}\pmod{3}.

From Lemma
7
, we then know that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(3n+1)q^{3n+1}\equiv\frac{f_{12}}{f_{6}}\left(q\frac{f_{6}^{2}f_{18}^{3}}{f_{3}^{3}f_{12}^{3}}\right)\pmod{3}

which means

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(3n+1)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{4}}{f_{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{2}^{2}f_{6}^{3}}{f_{1}^{3}f_{4}^{3}}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}f_{6}^{3}}{f_{1}^{3}f_{4}^{2}}

(4.1)

\displaystyle\equiv\left(f_{2}f_{4}\right)\frac{f_{6}^{3}}{f_{3}f_{12}}\pmod{3}.

Thanks to Lemma
8
, we see that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(3n+1)q^{n}\equiv\frac{f_{6}^{3}}{f_{3}f_{12}}\left(\frac{f_{12}f_{18}^{4}}{f_{6}f_{36}^{2}}+2q^{2}f_{18}f_{36}+q^{4}\frac{f_{6}f_{36}^{4}}{f_{12}f_{18}^{2}}\right)\pmod{3}.

This now allows us to perform an additional 3–dissection to obtain

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(9n+1)q^{3n}\equiv\frac{f_{6}^{3}}{f_{3}f_{12}}\cdot\frac{f_{12}f_{18}^{4}}{f_{6}f_{36}^{2}}\pmod{3}

which yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(9n+1)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{2}^{2}f_{6}^{4}}{f_{1}f_{12}^{2}}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}^{2}}{f_{1}}\cdot\frac{f_{6}^{4}}{f_{12}^{2}}.

From Lemma
10
, we can rewrite this result as

(4.2)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(9n+1)q^{n}\equiv\left(\frac{f_{6}f_{9}^{2}}{f_{3}f_{18}}+q\frac{f_{18}^{2}}{f_{9}}\right)\frac{f_{6}^{4}}{f_{12}^{2}}\pmod{3}.

Note that the power series representation of the right–hand side of the above congruence contains no terms of the form
q^{3n+2}.
Thus,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(9(3n+2)+1)q^{3n+2}\equiv 0\pmod{3}

which means that, for all
n\geq 0,

pend(9(3n+2)+1)=pend(27n+19)\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

∎

We next consider the proof of Theorem
4
.

Proof.

(of Theorem
4
)
Our goal here is to prove that, for all
n\geq 0,

pend(27n+10)\equiv pend(3n+1)\pmod{3}.

Thanks to (
4.2
), we see that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(27n+10)q^{3n+1}\equiv q\frac{f_{6}^{4}f_{18}^{2}}{f_{9}f_{12}^{2}}\pmod{3}

which means

(4.3)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(27n+10)q^{n}\equiv\frac{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{3}f_{4}^{2}}\pmod{3}.

From
(
4.1
), we know

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(3n+1)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{2}f_{4}f_{6}^{3}}{f_{3}f_{12}}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{2}f_{4}f_{6}^{3}}{f_{3}f_{4}^{3}}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}f_{6}^{3}}{f_{3}f_{4}^{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{2}f_{2}^{3}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{3}f_{4}^{2}}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{3}f_{4}^{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pend(27n+10)q^{n}\pmod{3}

thanks to (
4.3
).
∎

We are now in a position to prove the infinite family of congruences in Theorem
6
.

Proof.

(of Theorem
6
)
We prove this theorem by induction on
\alpha
. Note that the base case,
\alpha=1
, which corresponds to the arithmetic progression

3^{3}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2}-1}{8}=27n+19,

has already been proved in Theorem
2
.
Thus, we assume that, for some
\alpha\geq 1
and all
n\geq 0
,

pend\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}\right)\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

We then want to prove that

pend\left(3^{2\alpha+3}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}-1}{8}\right)\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

Note that

\displaystyle 3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}

\displaystyle=3\left(3^{2\alpha}n\right)+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha}-9+8}{8}

\displaystyle=3\left(3^{2\alpha}n\right)+3\left(\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha-1}-3}{8}\right)+1

\displaystyle=3\left(3^{2\alpha}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha-1}-3}{8}\right)+1

and it is easy to argue that

3^{2\alpha}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha-1}-3}{8}

is an integer for any
\alpha\geq 1
. Therefore, we have the following:

\displaystyle pend\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}\right)

\displaystyle=pend\left(3\left(3^{2\alpha}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha-1}-3}{8}\right)+1\right)

\displaystyle\equiv pend\left(27\left(3^{2\alpha}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha-1}-3}{8}\right)+10\right)\pmod{3}\textrm{\ \ thanks to Theorem \ref{thm:pend_internal}}

\displaystyle=pend\left(3^{2\alpha+3}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}-27\cdot 3+10\cdot 8}{8}\right)

\displaystyle=pend\left(3^{2\alpha+3}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}-1}{8}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3}

thanks to the induction hypothesis. This completes the proof.

∎

5.
Closing Thoughts

While it is very satisfying to see the proofs provided above, it would be interesting to see combinatorial proofs of these divisibility properties. We leave it to the interested reader to obtain such proofs.

It may also be fruitful to consider further refinements of the functions
pend(n)
and
pond(n)
. For example, rather than requiring that even parts must be repeated, one could restrict this requirement to only those parts which are divisible by 4 (with no such requirements on the other parts). It is certainly straightforward to find the generating functions for such refinements, which means that an analysis such as that above should be possible. Ballantine and Welch
[
5
]
share comments about such partitions (and their enumerating functions) near the end of their manuscript. The interested reader may wish to study such functions from an arithmetic perspective.
