Abstract
We develop the theory of a metric, which we call the ν \nu -based Wasserstein metric and denote by W ν W_{\nu} , on the set of probability measures 𝒫 ⁡ ( X ) \mathcal{P}(X) on a domain X ⊆ ℝ m X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m} . This metric is based on a slight refinement of the notion of generalized geodesics with respect to a base measure ν \nu and is relevant in particular for the case when ν \nu is singular with respect to m m -dimensional Lebesgue measure; it is also closely related to the concept of linearized optimal transport. The ν \nu -based Wasserstein metric is defined in terms of an iterated variational problem involving optimal transport to ν \nu ; we also characterize it in terms of integrations of classical Wasserstein metric between the conditional probabilities when measures are disintegrated with respect to optimal transport to ν \nu , and through limits of certain multi-marginal optimal transport problems. We also introduce a class of metrics which are dual in a certain sense to W ν W_{\nu} , defined relative to a fixed based measure μ \mu , on the set of measures which are absolutely continuous with respect to a second fixed based measure σ \sigma . As we vary the base measure ν \nu , the ν \nu -based Wasserstein metric interpolates between the usual quadratic Wasserstein metric (obtained when ν \nu is a Dirac mass) and a metric associated with the uniquely defined generalized geodesics obtained when ν \nu is sufficiently regular (eg, absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue). When ν \nu concentrates on a lower dimensional submanifold of ℝ m \mathbb{R}^{m} , we prove that the variational problem in the definition of the ν \nu -based Wasserstein metric has a unique solution. We also establish geodesic convexity of the usual class of functionals, and of the set of source measures μ \mu such that optimal transport between μ \mu and ν \nu satisfies a strengthening of the generalized nestedness condition introduced in [ 16 ] . We finally introduce a slight variant of the dual metric mentioned above in order to prove convergence of an iterative scheme to solve a variational problem arising in game theory.

Definition and basic properties

Throughout this paper, for a given set
S
, a semi-metric will be a function
d:S\times S\rightarrow[0,\infty)
satisfying:

•

d(x,y)=0
if and only if
x=y
(separation).

•

d(x,y)=d(y,x)
for all
x,y\in S
(symmetry).

A semi-metric
d
will be called a metric if it also satisfies:

•

d(x,y)\leq d(x,z)+d(y,z)
for all
x,y,z\in S
(triangle inequality).

We will often refer to
d(x,y)
as the distance between
x
and
y
.

Example 11

Remark 14
.

In this paper, we will pay special attention to the case where
\nu

We next consider the case when

corresponds to the layerwise-Wasserstein metric from

Definition 15

The layerwise-Wasserstein metric between measures

d^{2}_{LW}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}):=W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0}^{V},\mu_{1}^{V})+\int_{0}^{1}W_{2}^{2}(\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{l},\tilde{\mu}_{1}^{l})dl

where the

\mu_{i}

\tilde{\mu}_{i}
are rescaled versions of the
\mu_{i}

\tilde{\mu}_{i}=(F_{\mu_{i}},Id)_{\#}\mu_{i}

Proposition 16

Suppose
\nu

1

Proof.

Using the framework described in Remark
14

\mu_{i}
on
X

The level set

\{(z_{i}(y),x_{i}^{2},...,x_{i}^{m})\}

\mu_{1}^{y}

By Theorem

W^{2}_{\nu}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\int_{Y}W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0}^{y},\mu_{1}^{y})d\nu(y)

Note that

2.3

We now define a class of metrics on a lower dimensional space which are

uses in part the Kantorovich potential

Fix reference measures

is Lipschitz, since
\overline{X}
and

are compact.

We let
\mathcal{P}_{ac,\sigma}(Y)

W^{*}_{\mu,\sigma,c,p}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1}):=||Dv_{0}-Dv_{1}||_{L^{p}(\sigma)}

(13)

for some

is the

Proposition 19

W^{*}_{\mu,\sigma,c,p}

Proof.

Since the

\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{ac,\sigma}(Y)
, the gradient
Dv
of the Kantorovich potential is in

\nu\mapsto Dv
is a bijection.
Since the cost function is Lipschitz, a now standard argument originating in
[

]

is Lipschitz; absolute continuity of
\nu
with respect to
\sigma

Absolute continuity of
\nu
with respect to
\sigma

is uniquely determined by

[

3.1
Nestedness and its high dimensional generalization

In what follows, in Section
6

\mu

\nu

on

X^{c}_{1}(y,p):=\{x\in X:D_{y}c(x,y)=p\}.

Assuming that

If we denote by

c(x,y)-v(y)\geq u(x)=\inf_{y^{\prime}\in Y}(c(x,y^{\prime})-v(y^{\prime}))

with equality

At any such point where

\partial^{c}v(y):=\{x\in X:c(x,y)-v(y)=\inf_{y^{\prime}\in Y}(c(x,y^{\prime})-v(y^{\prime}))\}\subseteq X^{c}_{1}(y,Dv(y)).

The generalized nestedness condition, introduced in

is that this inclusion is actually an equality.

Definition 21
.

We say that
(c,\mu,\nu)
satisfies the
generalized nestedness condition
if for all
y
at which
v
is differentiable, we have

\partial^{c}v(y)=X^{c}_{1}(y,Dv(y))

where
v
is the
c

and
v
.

The importance of this condition, uncovered in
[
16
]
, is as follows. Solving the optimal transport problem (
2
) is essentially equivalent to solving the dual problem (
3
). In turn, it is shown in
[
16
]
that the solution
(v^{c},v)
to (
3

and therefore extremely challenging. Under the generalized nestedness condition, however, it turns out that this equation reduces to a local equation, and therefore becomes much more tractable.

The origin of the term generalized nestedness comes from the case when

is one dimensional, in which case it can be shown that generalized nestedness is essentially equivalent to nestedness of the corresponding superlevel sets. More precisely, we define:

X^{c}_{\geq}(y,p):=\{x\in X:D_{y}c(x,y)\geq p\}.

(20)

Assuming that
\mu
and
\nu
are both absolutely continuous, with densities bounded above and below, for each fixed

of the super level set

is a decreasing function of

satisfying the population balancing condition:

\mu(X^{c}_{\geq}(y,k))=\nu((\underline{y},y)).

(21)

The definition of nestedness found in
[
9
]
is then the following.

Definition 22
.

Assume that
Y
is one dimensional. We say the model
(c,\mu,\nu)
is nested if

X^{c}_{\geq}(y,k(y))\subseteq X^{c}_{\geq}(y^{\prime},k(y^{\prime}))

whenever
y\leq y^{\prime}
.

The relevance of the nestedness condition is that it allows one to almost explicitly solve (
2
)
[
9
]
.
One can attempt to define a mapping

that maps each

x=x^{y}_{||}+x^{y}_{\perp}
with
x^{y}_{\perp}\cdot Df(y)=0
and
x^{y}_{||}
orthogonal to the null space of
Df(y)
.

We define the
enhancement of
X
by
y\in Y
as

X^{y}=\{x=x^{y}_{\perp}+x^{y}_{||}:x^{y}_{\perp}+\tilde{x}^{y}_{||},\tilde{x}^{y}_{\perp}+x^{y}_{||}\in X\text{ for some }\tilde{x}^{y}_{||}\in[null(Df(y))]^{\perp},\tilde{x}^{y}_{\perp}\in null(Df(y))\}

We say that
(c,\mu,\nu)
satisfies the
enhanced generalized nestedness condition
at

if

X_{1}^{y}(y,Dv(y))=\partial^{c}v(y):=\{x\in X^{y}:-x\cdot f(y)-v(y)\leq-x\cdot f(\tilde{y})-v(\tilde{y})\;\forall\tilde{y}\in Y\},

where

is the Kantorovich potential.
We say that
(c,\mu,\nu)
satisfies the
enhanced generalized nestedness condition
if it satisfies this condition at almost every

. Since
X\subseteq X^{y}
for each

,
X_{1}(y,Dv(y))\subseteq X_{1}^{y}(y,Dv(y))
, and so the enhanced generalized nestedness condition implies the generalized nestedness condition
X_{1}(y,Dv(y))=\partial^{c}v(y)
introduced in
[
16
]
.

Proposition 33
.

Let
\nu
be absolutely continuous with respect to
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on the

-dimensional submanifold

. Then the set of
\mu
such that
(c,\mu,\nu)
is enhanced generalized nested at

is geodesically convex with respect to the metric
W_{\nu}
.

Proof.

Given Kantorovich potentials
v_{0}
and
v_{1}
corresponding to
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
, the potential
v_{t}:=(1-t)v_{0}+tv_{1}
corresponds to the interpolant
\mu_{t}
.
Observe that, since

is affine in

, the enhanced level sets
X_{1}^{y}(y,Dv_{t}(y))=(1-t)X_{1}^{y}(y,Dv_{0}(y))+tX_{1}^{y}(y,Dv_{1}(y))
is the Minkowski sum of the two endpoint enhanced level sets. Letting

, we have by assumption that

for all

.
Taking the weighted average of these two inequalities yields

\displaystyle-(1-t)x_{0}\cdot f(y)-(1-t)v_{0}(y)-tx_{1}\cdot f(y)-tv_{1}(y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-(1-t)x_{0}\cdot f(\tilde{y})-(1-t)v_{i}(\tilde{y})

\displaystyle-tx_{1}\cdot f(\tilde{y})-tv_{1}(\tilde{y})

\displaystyle-x_{t}\cdot f(y)-v_{t}(y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle x_{t}\cdot f(\tilde{y})-v_{t}(\tilde{y})

∎

6
Fixed point characterization of solutions to variational problems

In this section, we consider a fixed measure
\mu
on the bounded domain
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure with density
\overline{\mu}
, bounded above and below, and a fixed domain
Y:=(\underline{y},\overline{y})\subset\mathbb{R}
. We are interested in the minimization problem (
22
).
We recall that this problem belongs to a class arising in certain game theory problems, introduced in a series of papers by Blanchet-Carlier
[
3
,
4
,
2
]
, in which the fixed measure
\mu
represents a distribution of players and
Y
a space of strategies; minimizing measures
\nu
in (
22
) represent equilibrium distributions of strategies. In general, the dimensions of
m
and
n
of the spaces of players and strategies represent the number of charateristics used to distinguish between them. The case
m>n
(including the case
n=1
treated here) are of particular interest, since it is often the case that players are highly homogeneous, whereas the strategies available to them are less varied.

When
m=1
(so that both players and strategy spaces are one dimensional), Blanchet-Carlier introduced a characterization of minimizers as fixed points of a certain mapping. They then iterated that mapping as a way to compute solutions
[
3
]
; however, they did not prove convergence of this scheme.

Here we introduce a similar fixed point characterization for
m\geq 1
. Furthermore, we prove for any
m
, under certain assumptions, that the mapping is a contraction with respect to a strategically chosen metric, implying convergence of the iterative scheme; even for
m=1
, this is a new result. The key to the proof is choosing a suitable metric; our choice is a slight variant of the dual metric
W^{*}_{\mu,\mathcal{L},c,1}
introduced in Section
2.3
.

Choose
\underline{d}=\min_{x\in\overline{X},y\in\overline{Y}}\frac{\partial c}{\partial y}(x,y),\overline{d}=\max_{x\in\overline{X},y\in\overline{Y}}\frac{\partial c}{\partial y}(x,y)
.

Let
k\in L^{1}\Big((\underline{y},\overline{y});[\underline{d},\overline{d}]\Big)
, the set of
L^{1}
functions on
(\underline{y},\overline{y})
taking values in
[\underline{d},\overline{d}]
, and set
v_{k}(y):=\int_{\underline{y}}^{y}k(s)ds
. Note that
L^{1}\Big((\underline{y},\overline{y});[\underline{d},\overline{d}]\Big)
is a complete metric space. Let

\overline{\nu}_{k}(y):=\frac{e^{-v_{k}(y)}}{\int_{\underline{y}}^{\overline{y}}e^{-v_{k}(s)}ds}.

(30)

This is a probability density on
(\underline{y},\overline{y})
; we denote the corresponding measure by
\nu_{k}(y)
. Note that (
30
) then defines a mapping,
L^{1}\Big((\underline{y},\overline{y});[\underline{d},\overline{d}]\Big)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}^{ac}((\underline{y},\overline{y}))
, where
\mathcal{P}^{ac}((\underline{y},\overline{y}))
is the set of absolutely continuous probability measures on
(\underline{y},\overline{y})
:

k\mapsto\nu_{k}

(31)

We note that, for any
k\in L^{1}\Big((\underline{y},\overline{y});[\underline{d},\overline{d}]\Big)
, we have

\displaystyle\nu_{k}(\underline{y},y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\underline{y}}^{y}\overline{\nu}_{k}(s)ds

(32)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{\underline{y}}^{y}\frac{\underline{d}e^{-\overline{d}(s-\underline{y})}}{1-e^{-\underline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})}}ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\underline{d}(1-e^{-\overline{d}(y-\underline{y})})}{\overline{d}(1-e^{-\underline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})})}.

Similarly,

\nu_{k}(y,\overline{y})\geq\frac{\underline{d}(e^{-\overline{d}(y-\underline{y})}-e^{-\overline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})})}{\overline{d}(1-e^{-\underline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})})}.

(33)

We then note that, for a given
\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{ac}((\underline{y},\overline{y}))
the mass splitting property (
21
) selects a unique
k=k(y)\in[\underline{d},\overline{d}]
for each
y\in(\underline{y},\overline{y})
. The resulting function
k(y)
is then bounded and so clearly in
L^{1}\Big((\underline{y},\overline{y});[\underline{d},\overline{d}]\Big)
. We can then think of this process as defining a mapping
\mathcal{P}^{ac}((\underline{y},\overline{y}))\rightarrow L^{1}\Big((\underline{y},\overline{y});[\underline{d},\overline{d}]\Big)
,
\nu\mapsto k(\cdot)
. Composing this mapping with (
31
) then yields a
F:L^{1}\Big((\underline{y},\overline{y});[\underline{d},\overline{d}]\Big)\rightarrow L^{1}\Big((\underline{y},\overline{y});[\underline{d},\overline{d}]\Big)
. Explicitly, defining
\tilde{k}:=F[k]
, we have

\nu_{k}(\underline{y},y):=\mu(X_{\geq}(y,\tilde{k}(y))).

(34)

Lemma 34
.

Let
\nu
be a minimizer of (
22
) such that
(c,\mu,\nu)
is nested. Then
k(y)=v^{\prime}(y)
is a fixed point of
F
, where
v
is the corresponding Kantorovich potential.

Proof.

The first order conditions corresponding to the variational problem are

v(y)+\log(\overline{\nu}(y))=C

for some constant
C
,
\nu
almost everywhere (see, for example, Section 7.4 in
[
19
]
), where
\overline{\nu}
is the density of minimizer
\overline{\nu}
and
v
the corresponding Kantorovich potential.
This implies that that
\nu=\nu_{k}
.

Since
(c,\mu,\nu)
is nested, the
\tilde{k}=F[K]
defined by (
34
) coincides with the derivative of the Kantorovich potential for optimal transport between
\mu
and
\nu_{k}=\nu
, which is exactly
k
.
∎

We will prove that, under certain conditions,
F
is a contraction. The Banach fixed point theorem will then imply that
F
has a unique fixed point. Under conditions ensuring nestedness of the minimizer, Lemma
34
, will then ensure that that fixed point is the minimizer of (
22
).

We need the following conditions on
X
,
c
and
\mu
to ensure
F
is a contraction:

•

A:=\max|D_{x}c_{y}(x,y)|<\infty
,

•

B:=\min\overline{\mu}(x)>0
,

•

There is a
C>0
such that, for all
y\in Y
,
x\in X
, using
c_{y}(x,y)
as a shorthand for
\frac{\partial c}{\partial y}(x,y)
,

\mathcal{H}^{m-1}(X_{1}^{c}(y,c_{y}(x,y)))\geq C\min\big\{\mu(X_{\geq}^{c}(y,c_{y}(x,y))),\mu(X_{\leq}^{c}(y,c_{y}(x,y)))\Big\}.

(35)

Let us discuss briefly the third condition above. For any
x,y
, setting
p=c_{y}(x,y)
, the level set
X_{1}^{c}(y,p)
is an
m-1
dimensional submanifold, dividing the region
X
into the sub and super level sets
X_{\geq}^{c}(y,p)
and
X_{\leq}^{c}(y,p)
. The condition implies that if
X_{1}^{c}(y,p)
is small, then at least one of
\mu(X_{\geq}^{c}(y,p))
or
\mu(X_{\leq}^{c}(y,p))
must also be (quantitatively) small.

In addition, we set
H(y)=\max\{\frac{|e^{(\overline{d}-2\underline{d})(y-\underline{y})}-1|}{|1-e^{-\overline{d}(y-\underline{y})}|},\frac{|e^{(\overline{d}-2\underline{d})(\overline{y}-\underline{y})}-e^{(\overline{d}-2\underline{d})(y-\underline{y}}|}{|(e^{-\overline{d}(y-\underline{y})}-e^{-\overline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})}|}\}
.

Theorem 35
.

Assume the three conditions above, and that

\frac{2A\overline{d}^{3}(1-e^{-\underline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})})^{2}}{BC\underline{d}^{2}[1-e^{-\overline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})}]^{2}|\overline{d}-2\underline{d}|}\int_{\underline{y}}^{\overline{y}}H(y)dy<1.

Then
F
is a contraction.

Remark 36
.

The condition appearing in the statement of the theorem looks complicated; however, all quantities except for
B
and
C
can be computed using the cost function alone (
B
involves the reference marginal
\mu
while
C
involves both the cost and
\mu
). Since the factor in front of the integral and the function
H(y)
are bounded, the limit as
\overline{y}-\underline{y}\rightarrow 0
of the left hand side above is
0
; therefore, the condition will always hold for sufficiently small intervals. We illustrate these points in a simple example below, where we compute each of these quantites explicly.

Example 37
.

Let
X=[-1,1]^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
Y=[0,\overline{y}]\subset\mathbb{R}
, and
c(x,y)=-x_{1}y
. We take
\mu
to be uniform measure, normalized to have total mass
1
, so that the density is
\overline{\mu}(x)=1/4
; clearly this means
B=1/4
. We then have
c_{y}(x,y)=-x_{1}
so that
\overline{d}=1
,
\underline{d}=-1
, and
D_{x}c_{y}(x,y)=-(1,0)
, so that
A=1
. We also have that each
X_{1}^{c}(y,p)=\{x:x_{1}=-p,\text{ }-1<x_{2}<1\}
is a vertical line and so
\mathcal{H}^{m-1}(X_{1}^{c}(y,c_{y}(x,y)))=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{x:x_{1}=-p,\text{ }-1<x_{2}<1\})=2
. Since
\mu(X_{\geq}^{c}(y,c_{y}(x,y)))+\mu(X_{\leq}^{c}(y,c_{y}(x,y))=1
, the minimum of the two values is at most
1/2
, so we can take
C=4
.

It is easy to check that
\frac{|e^{(\overline{d}-2\underline{d})(y-\underline{y})}-1|}{|1-e^{-\overline{d}(y-\underline{y})}|}=\frac{e^{3y}-1}{1-e^{-y}}
is increasing and so is bounded above by its value
\frac{e^{3\overline{y}}-1}{1-e^{-\overline{y}}}
at the endpoint
\overline{y}
. Similarly,
\frac{|e^{(\overline{d}-2\underline{d})(\overline{y}-\underline{y})}-e^{(\overline{d}-2\underline{d})(y-\underline{y}}|}{|(e^{-\overline{d}(y-\underline{y})}-e^{-\overline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})}|}=\frac{e^{3\overline{y}}-e^{3y}}{e^{-y}-e^{-\overline{y}}}
is decreasing provided
\overline{y}
is small enough, so it is bounded above by its value
\frac{e^{3\overline{y}}-1}{1-e^{-\overline{y}}}
at
y=0
. Therefore,
H(y)\leq\frac{e^{3\overline{y}}-1}{1-e^{-\overline{y}}}
and we have, for sufficiently small
\overline{y}
,

\displaystyle\frac{2A\overline{d}^{3}(1-e^{-\underline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})})^{2}}{BC\underline{d}^{2}[1-e^{-\overline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})}]^{2}|\overline{d}-2\underline{d}|}\int_{\underline{y}}^{\overline{y}}H(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2(1-e^{\overline{y}})^{2}}{\frac{1}{4}4(1-e^{-\overline{y}})^{2}3}\int_{0}^{\overline{y}}H(y)dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2(1-e^{\overline{y}})^{2}}{3(1-e^{-\overline{y}})^{2}}\overline{y}\frac{e^{3\overline{y}}-1}{1-e^{-\overline{y}}}

This tends to
0
as
\overline{y}
does, since
\frac{(1-e^{\overline{y}})^{2}}{(1-e^{-\overline{y}})^{2}}\rightarrow 1
and
\frac{e^{3\overline{y}}-1}{1-e^{-\overline{y}}}\rightarrow 3
, so that for sufficiently small
\overline{y}
, the condition in Theorem
35
holds.

Remark 38

(Relationship to dual metrics)
.

We show that

is a contraction with respect to the

\nu\mapsto k

from Section

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle|\overline{\nu}_{0}(y)-\overline{\nu}_{1}(y)|

\displaystyle=

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\overline{d}^{2}||v_{0}-v_{1}||_{L^{\infty}}e^{\overline{d}(y-\overline{y})}\int_{\underline{y}}^{\overline{y}}e^{-\underline{d}(s-\overline{y})}ds}{[e^{\underline{d}(y-\underline{y})}(1-e^{-\overline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})})]^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\overline{d}^{2}||v_{0}-v_{1}||_{L^{\infty}}e^{(\overline{d}-2\underline{d})(y-\overline{y})}(1-e^{-\underline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})})}{\underline{d}[1-e^{-\overline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})}]^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\overline{d}^{2}||k_{0}-k_{1}||_{L^{1}}e^{(\overline{d}-2\underline{d})(y-\overline{y})}(1-e^{-\underline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})})}{\underline{d}[1-e^{-\overline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})}]^{2}}.

Now, from the mass splitting definition of

\displaystyle|\tilde{k}_{1}(y)-\tilde{k}_{0}(y)|\leq\frac{2A\overline{d}^{2}||k_{0}-k_{1}||_{L^{1}}(1-e^{-\underline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})})}{BC\underline{d}[1-e^{-\overline{d}(\overline{y}-\underline{y})}]^{2}\mu[X_{\geq}(y,\tilde{k}_{1}(y))]}\int_{\underline{y}}^{y}e^{(\overline{d}-2\underline{d})(s-\overline{y})}ds

Appendix A
Hierarchical metrics with multiple reference measures

The
\nu
-based Wasserstein metric can be extended to align with several different probability measures in a hierarchical way. Given
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{k}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, we define iteratively
\Pi^{1}_{opt}=\Pi_{opt}(\nu_{1},\mu_{i})
and
\Pi^{j}_{opt}(\nu_{1},...,\nu_{j},\mu_{i})\subseteq\mathcal{\mathcal{}}\mathcal{P}(X^{j+1})

\Pi^{j}_{opt}(\nu_{1},...,\nu_{j},\mu_{i})=\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma_{y_{1},...y_{j-1}x_{i}}\in\Pi^{j-1}_{opt}(\nu_{1},...,\nu_{j-1},\mu_{i})\\
