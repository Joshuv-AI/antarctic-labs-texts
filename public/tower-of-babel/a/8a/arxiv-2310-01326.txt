1
Introduction

The recent years have witnessed a renaissance of permuted linear regression, or “unlabeled sensing", due to its broad spectrum of applications ranging from database merging, to privacy, to communications, to computer vision, to robotics, to sensor networks, etc
(
Pananjady et al. 2018
;
Unnikrishnan et al. 2015
;
Slawski et al. 2020
;
Slawski and Ben-David 2019
;
Pananjady et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2022
)
.
Here, we briefly review several use cases of permuted linear regression, including record linkage, data de-anonymization,
and header-free communications. For more information about other applications, we refer interested readers to the references thereof.

•

Record linkage
. Given multiple databases containing
information about the same entities, the objective is to merge them
into one comprehensive database. However,
these data may not be well-aligned due to the data formatting or data quality issues.
Modeling the mismatches as a permutation is investigated as a mitigation strategy.

•

Data de-anonymization.
This task can be regarded as the opposite side of privacy protection.
The intruders aim to infer the hidden identities/labels in certain private networks with public information. One commonly used method is to compare the correlation between this information and to find matching pairs with
maximum correlation sum.
Here, the permuted linear regression
arises as a natural generalization.

•

Header-free communication.

Another potential application of permuted linear regression is in the
internet of things
(IOT) network, where communications happen frequently while the transmitted messages are usually in short length. To improve the bandwidth efficiency, the sensor identity
is omitted during transmission and thus the signal decoding involves
first restoring the correspondence.

In this paper, we consider the canonical setting for a
permuted linear sensing, which is written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}=\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{B}^{\natural}+\mathbf{W},

(1)

where
\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
denotes the unknown permutation matrix,

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
represents the design (sensing) matrix,

\mathbf{B}^{\natural}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}

is the signal of interest, and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
denotes the additive noise.
Compared with the canonical model of linear regression with well-aligned data, our task is to infer both the signal
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
and the missing correspondence information
\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}
from the pair
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
.

1.1
Related work

The study on permuted linear regression has a long history and
can be at least traced back to 1970s under the name of “broken sample problem”
(
DeGroot and Goel 1976
;
DeGroot and Goel 1980
;
Goel 1975
;
Bai and Hsing 2005
)
.
In recent years, we have witnessed a revival of its study and
can broadly divide these research works into
two categories:
(i)
single observation model
and
(ii)
multiple observations model.

We first discuss the work on the single observation model. In
Unnikrishnan et al. 2015
, they focus on the
noiseless single observation model, namely,

\mathbf{W}=\bm{0}
and
m=1
.
Assuming the entries in the sensing matrix
\mathbf{X}

are drawn from a continuous distribution and

\mathbf{B}^{\natural}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is an arbitrary vector residing
within a linear space with dimension
p
,

Unnikrishnan et al. 2015
establish the necessary condition
n\geq 2p

for the correct recovery.
Similar results have been discovered by
Dokmanic 2019
but
with different approaches.
In
Pananjady et al. 2018
, they investigate the noisy case under
the single observation model, i.e.,
m=1
.
After obtaining the statistical limit of the minimum
\mathsf{SNR}

required for permutation recovery, e.g.,
\Omega(n^{c})
(
c>0
is some positive constant), they analyze the

maximum likelihood
(ML) estimator and show its performance
matches the order of the statistical limits thereof.
However, the ML estimator is NP-hard in general except for the special case when
p=1
, in other words,
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
is a scalar.
Later, an approximation algorithm for permutation recovery is presented in
Hsu et al. 2017
; and in
Slawski and Ben-David 2019
, the permutation recovery is studied
from the viewpoint of denoising. We will give a more technical discussion of these works
(
Pananjady et al. 2018
;
Hsu et al. 2017
;
Slawski and Ben-David 2019
)
, as their settings have a huge overlap with ours.
The content is deferred to Section
3.3
until we have collected the required facts. An independent line of research can be found in
Tsakiris and Peng 2019
;
Peng et al. 2021
, where the
permuted linear regression is studied from the viewpoint of algebraic geometry.

Next, we survey the work on the multiple observations model
(
Pananjady et al. 2017
;
Slawski et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Zhang and Li 2023a
;
Zhang and Li 2023b
)
.
In
Pananjady et al. 2017
, the authors study
the problem of
reconstructing the product
\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
.
In
Zhang et al. 2022
, the focus is shifted
to reconstruct the individual values of

\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
.
Statistical limits are presented as well as an investigation
of the ML estimator. In
Slawski et al. 2020
,
they take a similar viewpoint of
Slawski and Ben-David 2019

and use a denoising-based method for correspondence
recovery.
In
Zhang and Li 2023b
, a follow-up of this work, they consider the sparse matrix setting (i.e., each column of
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
is
k
-sparse) and design the estimator based on
Zhang and Li 2020
.
A more detailed comparison between our works and the existing literature
can be found in Section
4.3
.

In addition to the works mentioned above, other research, e.g.,

Haghighatshoar and Caire 2018
;
Tang et al. 2021
;
Jeong et al. 2020
;
Slawski and Sen 2022
;
Fang and Li 2023
, are also related to our
works. A detailed discussion is omitted due to their relatively loose connection.

Contributions

•

We propose a one-step estimator for permuted linear regression, which consists of two sub-parts:
one for the permutation recovery and the other
for the signal recovery. We show the first sub-part is
with computational complexity
O(n^{3})

O(np^{2}m)

•

m=1

our estimator can yield the ground truth permutation

\mathbf{B}^{\natural}

positive constant.

m>1
. We divide the required

\mathsf{SNR}

\mathsf{SNR}

polynomial of
\log n
; while in the
easy regime, our
\mathsf{SNR}

Problem Setting

We start the discussion with a formal restatement of the
sensing model

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}=\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{B}^{\natural}+\mathbf{W},\vskip-3.61371pt

(2)

where
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
denotes the observation,

\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
is the unknown permutation matrix
such that
\sum_{i}\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}_{i,j}=\sum_{j}\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}_{i,j}=1
,

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
denotes the
sensing matrix such that
each entry
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
are i.i.d. centered isotropic sub-gaussian RV with

{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{ij}\right\|}_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim 1
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}_{ij}=0
and
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}_{ij}^{2}=1
,

\mathbf{B}^{\natural}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}
denotes the signal of interests,
and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
represents the additive Gaussian noise
with each entry
\mathbf{W}_{ij}
being Gaussian RV with zero mean
and
\sigma^{2}
variance, namely,

\mathbf{W}_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.

2
2

2

We call
n
sample number,
p
length of signal, and
m
measurement number.

Our goal is to reconstruct the pair

(\bm{\Pi}^{\natural},\mathbf{B}^{\natural})
from observations
\mathbf{Y}
and the
sensing matrix
\mathbf{X}
. In the following context, we put our major focus on the
permutation recovery:
on one hand, this problem reduces to the classical setting of linear regression once ground truth permutation is obtained; on the other hand,
no meaningful bound on
{|\kern-1.72218pt|\kern-1.72218pt|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}^{\natural}|\kern-1.72218pt|\kern-1.72218pt|}_{\textup{F}}
can be obtained with incorrect correspondence information (e.g.,
\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}
).

2.1
Estimator and its design insight

Algorithm 1

One-Step Estimator.

Input:
observation
\mathbf{Y}
and sensing matrix
\mathbf{X}
.

Output:
pair
(\bm{\Pi}^{\textup{opt}},~\mathbf{B}^{\textup{opt}})
, which
is written as

\displaystyle\bm{\Pi}^{\textup{opt}}

\displaystyle=\textup{argmax}_{\bm{\Pi}\in{\cal P}_{n}}\ \left\langle\kern-2.75554pt\left\langle\bm{\Pi},\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\right\rangle\kern-2.75554pt\right\rangle,

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{\textup{opt}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}^{\top}\mathbf{Y},

(4)

where
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
denotes the pseudo-inverse of

\mathbf{X}
(Section
5.5.2
in
Golub and Van Loan 2012
) and

{\cal P}_{n}
is the set of all possible permutation matrices.

We propose a one-step estimator, whose details are summarized
in Algorithm
1
.
Before a thorough investigation of
our estimator’s properties, we first present its underlying
design insight, which is quite straightforward.
Considering the oracle situation where
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}

is given a prior,
we can reconstruct the permutation matrix
\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}
via

\displaystyle\bm{\Pi}^{\textup{opt}}=\textup{argmax}_{\bm{\Pi}\in{\cal P}_{n}}\left\langle\kern-2.75554pt\left\langle\bm{\Pi},\mathbf{Y}(\nicefrac{{\mathbf{B}^{\natural\top}}}{{\alpha}})\mathbf{X}^{\top}\right\rangle\kern-2.75554pt\right\rangle,

(5)

where
\alpha>0
is an arbitrary scaling constant.
Back to our case, we can see that the major difficulty
comes from the lack of knowledge about
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
, to put
more precisely, the direction of
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
, since the
solution to (
5
) remains the same
up to some positive scaling factor.

Inspired by the recent progress in non-convex optimization

Candès and Plan 2010
;
Chi et al. 2019
;
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
, we would like to approximate
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
’s direction
with
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
. Note that
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}=(n-h)\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
, which is parallel to
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
if
h<n
(
h
denotes the number of permuted rows by
\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}
, e.g.,

h\triangleq\mathsf{d_{H}}(\mathbf{I},\bm{\Pi}^{\natural})
).
Assuming that
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}
is close
to
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}
,
we design our estimator
by substituting
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
in (
5
) with

\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}
, which then leads to the permutation estimation
in (
3
). Once the permutation is obtained,
we can restore (
2
) to
the classical setting of linear regression and then estimate

\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
with a
least-square
estimator
(
Golub and Van Loan 2012
)
.

Although at first glance this idea looks simple, if not naive,
in the following context, we will show that our estimator can reach statistical optimality in a broad regime.
In addition, our estimator is tuning-free, to put more specifically, no estimation of the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
nor the number of permuted rows
h
is required.

2.2
Computational cost

This subsection concerns our estimator’s computational cost.
We consider two types of oracle estimators as benchmarks.

•

Oracle estimator I.

We consider the oracle scenario where
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
is given a prior.
Then, we can recover
\bm{\Pi}^{\textup{opt}}
as in (
5
),
which is with computational cost
O(n^{3})
.

•

Oracle estimator II.
We consider the oracle scenario when
\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}
is known in advance.
The sensing relation in (
2
)
reduces to the classical multivariate linear regression
and least-square algorithm for
\mathbf{B}^{\textup{opt}}

takes up to
O(np^{2}m)
time.

Then we study our estimator’s computational
cost. In the first step (
3
), we only sacrifice one matrix multiplication, i.e.,
replacing
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
by the product
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}
.
Since the computational bottleneck
lies in solving the linear assignment problem

(
Kuhn 1955
;
Bertsekas and Castañón 1992
)
,
one additional matrix multiplication does not
change the computational complexity, which is
also of order
O(n^{3})
.
Similarly, in the second step
(
4
) our estimator sacrifices another
matrix multiplication, whose cost
is negligible when compared
with the total cost of
Oracle Estimator II
.

With the relation

n^{3}+np^{2}m\leq 2(n^{3}\vee np^{2}m)
, we conclude our estimator’s computational
cost
O(n^{3}+np^{2}m)
is in the same order as the maximum computational costs of the above-mentioned two oracle estimators.

2.3
Mini-max lower bounds

Before studying the statistical properties of
our algorithm, we review the mini-max lower bounds on the
permutation recovery.
First, we consider the single observation model, i.e.,

m=1
. We have

Theorem 1

(Theorem
2
in
Pananjady et al. 2018
)
.

For any estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}
, we have the
error probability
\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}\neq\bm{\Pi}^{\natural})

exceed
1-c_{0}\cdot e^{-c_{1}n\delta}
provided that

2+\log(1+\mathsf{SNR})\leq(2-\delta)\log n~,0<\delta<2
.

This theorem suggests that we need
\mathsf{SNR}
to be at least
of order
\Omega(n^{c})
to avoid construction failure of

\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}
.
Then we move on to the multiple observations model, i.e.,

m>1
. The corresponding lower bound is summarized as

Theorem 2

(Theorem
1
in
Zhang et al. 2022
)
.

For any estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}
, we have the
error probability
\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}\neq\bm{\Pi}^{\natural})
exceed

1/2
, provided that

\log\det\left(\mathbf{I}+\nicefrac{{\mathbf{B}^{\natural\top}\mathbf{B}^{\natural}}}{{\sigma^{2}}}\right)<\frac{\log n!-2}{n}
.

Remark 1
.

Theorem
1
can be
regarded as a special case of Theorem
2
, since the former
theorem can be obtained from the latter theorem by letting

m=1
.

Denote
\lambda_{i}(\cdot)
as the

i
th singular value,
we can rewrite

\log\det\left(\mathbf{I}+\nicefrac{{\mathbf{B}^{\natural\top}\mathbf{B}^{\natural}}}{{\sigma^{2}}}\right)

as
\sum_{i}\log[1+\nicefrac{{\lambda_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})}}{{\sigma^{2}}}]

and approximately
\log\det\left(\mathbf{I}+\nicefrac{{\mathbf{B}^{\natural\top}\mathbf{B}^{\natural}}}{{\sigma^{2}}}\right)

as
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\cdot\log(1+\mathsf{SNR})
.
Then, we conclude that no estimator can reliably recover

\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}
if
\log\mathsf{SNR}\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})}
.

The following context studies our algorithm’s statistical properties under the single observation model (
m=1
) and
multiple observations model (
m>1
), respectively.
We will show that the required

\mathsf{SNR}
s for correct permutation recovery are close to the statistical
limits thereof.

This section considers the single observation model, namely,

model, i.e.,

3.3
Discussion of related work

This subsection discusses the relation between our estimator
and prior work focusing on the single observation model (e.g.,
m=1
)
(
Slawski and Ben-David 2019
;
Pananjady et al. 2018
;
Hsu et al. 2017
;
Abid et al. 2017
)
. In
Pananjady et al. 2018
, the ML estimator is investigated, which is only computable for the special case
(m,p)=(1,1)
and NP-hard for the rest cases.
Their estimator gets the same
\mathsf{SNR}
requirement as ours, namely,

\log\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim\log n
.

To handle the computational issue of the ML estimator,

Hsu et al. 2017
propose an approximation algorithm with
polynomial complexity. Their
\mathsf{SNR}
requirement is
\mathsf{SNR}\geq c\min\left(1,p/\log\log n\right)
,
which has a gap with the mini-max lower bound.
In addition, they focus on the
recovery of
\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
rather than the permutation matrix
\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}
.

Later,
Slawski and Ben-David 2019
study the problem
for the viewpoint of denoising. By putting a
sparse constraint on
h
(e.g., the number of permuted rows),
they view the term

(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\Pi}^{\natural})\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{\natural}
as an additive sparse outlier.
After obtaining an estimate of
\bm{\beta}^{\natural}
, they restore
the permutation information with a linear assignment algorithm.
Compared with Algorithm
1
, the estimator
in
Slawski and Ben-David 2019
has a more stringent requirement
on
h
(i.e.,
h\ll\nicefrac{{n}}{{\log n}}
) but at the same time enjoys a broader class for
\bm{\beta}^{\natural}
, to put more specific, they
allow
p>1
under the single observation model.

A parallel line of work can be found in
Abid et al. 2017
,
where a consistent estimator is proposed based on the method-of-moments.
However, their analysis is only for the special case

(m,p)=(1,1)
and focuses on
{|\kern-1.72218pt|\kern-1.72218pt|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}^{\natural}|\kern-1.72218pt|\kern-1.72218pt|}_{\textup{F}}
.
Direct comparison with ours can be difficult.

4
Multiple Observations Model

The previous section considers the
single observation model (
m=1
) and this section extends
the discussion to the multiple observations model (
m>1
).
We
(i)
discover a much richer
behavior inherent in our estimator and
(ii)
show that correct permutation can be obtained
with
\mathsf{SNR}
being a positive constant.

Figure 2:
Illustration of different regimes of the permutation recovery.
In
regime hard
, we require
\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim n^{\frac{c}{\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})}}
;
in
regime medium difficulty
, we require

\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim\log^{c}n
; and
in
regime easy
, we require
\mathsf{SNR}\geq c
.

Based on the requirement on
\mathsf{SNR}
for correct permutation recovery,
we divide the estimator’s performance into four
regimes in terms of
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})
, which is illustrated in Figure
2
.

•

Unknown regime:
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\in[1,c_{0})
.
In this regime, our estimator’s behavior remains a mystery. To the best of our knowledge,
no estimator’s performance has been thoroughly studied in this regime.

•

Hard regime:
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\in[c_{0},c_{1}\log n)
.
We assume
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
to be i.i.d. sub-gaussian random variables with log-concavity.
For the correct permutation recovery, we require
\mathsf{SNR}
satisfying
\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim n^{\frac{c}{\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})}}
.

•

Medium regime:
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\in[c_{1}\log n,c_{2}\log^{4}n)
.
We only need
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
to be i.i.d. sub-gaussian random variables without enforcing log-concavity.
In addition, we require
\mathsf{SNR}
satisfying
\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim\log^{c}n
to obtain the ground truth permutation matrix.

•

Easy regime:
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\in[c_{2}\log^{4}n,\infty)
.
Still, we only assume
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
to be i.i.d. sub-gaussian random variables.
Here, we can relax the requirement on
\mathsf{SNR}
to be above some positive constant
for correct permutation recovery.

The formal statement of results is put in Theorem
4

and Theorem
5
.

Theorem 4
.

Consider the sensing matrix
\mathbf{X}
with its entries

\mathbf{X}_{i,j}
being sub-gaussian RV with zero mean and unit variance

(1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq p)
.
Assuming that
(i)

n\gg p\cdot\log^{3}n\cdot\log^{2}(n^{2}p^{3})

and
(ii)

h\leq c_{0}\cdot n
,
we can reliably obtain the permutation matrix with Algorithm
1
, namely,

\mathbb{P}(\bm{\Pi}^{\textup{opt}}=\bm{\Pi}^{\natural})\geq 1-c_{1}n^{-c_{2}}-c_{3}p^{-c_{4}}
,
in the following situations:

•

Easy regime
(\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\gg\log^{4}n)
: we have
\mathsf{SNR}\geq c
;

•

Medium regime
(\log n\ll\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\ll\log^{4}n)
:
we have
\log\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim\log\log n
.

Note that the above result only applies to the easy and medium regime, i.e.,

\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\gg\log n
. By enforcing additional
constraints, to put more specifically,
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
is log-concave,
we can generalize the above result to the hard regime, which is formally stated as

Theorem 5
.

Consider the sensing matrix
\mathbf{X}
with its entries

\mathbf{X}_{i,j}
being log-concave sub-gaussian RV with zero mean and unit variance

(1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq p)
.
Assuming that
(i)

n\gg p^{1+\varepsilon}\cdot\log^{3(1+\varepsilon)}n\cdot\log^{2(1+\varepsilon)}(n^{2}p^{3})

and
(ii)

h\leq c_{0}\cdot n
,
we can reliably obtain the permutation matrix with Algorithm
1
, namely,

\mathbb{P}(\bm{\Pi}^{\textup{opt}}=\bm{\Pi}^{\natural})\geq 1-c_{1}n^{-c_{2}}-c_{3}p^{-c_{4}}
,
in the following situations:

•

Easy regime
(\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\gg\log^{4}n)
: we have
\mathsf{SNR}\geq c
;

•

Medium regime
(\log n\ll\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\ll\log^{4}n)
:
we have
\log\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim\log\log n
;

•

Hard regime
(c(\varepsilon)\lesssim\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\ll\log n)
:
we have

\displaystyle\log\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim\frac{\log n}{\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})}+\log\log n,

(8)

where
\varepsilon>0
is an arbitrary positive constant and

c(\varepsilon)
is a positive constant depending on
\varepsilon
.

Family of log-concave sub-gaussian distributions include
standard Gaussian distribution and uniform distribution among
[-c,~c]
, where

c
denotes a certain positive constant.
However, not all sub-gaussian RVs are with log-concavity.
One example is that
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
is a Rademacher RV, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}_{ij}=\pm 1)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.
We can see that
Theorem
5
requires log-concavity and
can allow a much broader range of

\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})
than Theorem
4
, to put more specific, a positive constant
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})
is permitted.
In the following,
we will see some numerical results
implying that the log-concavity may be inseparable
from the positive constant
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})
,
in other words,
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})
cannot be

O(1)
if the log-concavity assumption is violated.

4.1
Results discussion

Comparison with single observation model.

The most noticeable implications of our theorems are
that the
\mathsf{SNR}
requirement for correct permutation recovery
can be greatly reduced by making multiple measurements, i.e., from
\Omega(n^{c})
to

\Omega(n^{\frac{c\cdot\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})}{n}}\vee\log^{c}n)
or even
\Omega(1)
.

Additionally, we notice that the constraint on signal length
p
has been removed
when multiple measurements are made. According to Theorem
3
which
concerns the single observation model
(m=1)
,
our estimator only works when
p=1
.
While in Theorem
4
and Theorem
5
where
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\gg 1
,
a much broader range of
p
is permitted.

In summary, the take-home message is that

diversity, i.e., large
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})
,
helps in the permutation recovery
.

Statistical optimality.

First, we argue that our estimator in Algorithm
1
is almost
mini-max optimal w.r.t.
\mathsf{SNR}
requirement.
For an easy comparison, we consider
the special case where
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})

is same as
\textup{rank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})
, which
corresponds to the situation where

\mathbf{B}^{\natural}
’s signal strength is uniformly spread
among all its eigenvalues. Theorem
2

suggests that wrong permutation matrix
\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}

will be returned with a high probability if

\displaystyle\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\cdot\log\mathsf{SNR}\approx\ \textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\cdot\log(1+\mathsf{SNR})\lesssim\log n,\vskip 2.84526pt

which holds regardless of the estimator form.
Comparing with Theorem
5
, we conclude that
our estimator has optimal
\mathsf{SNR}
requirement in the following two
regimes:
(i)

\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}

and
(ii)

\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\gg\log^{4}n
. Only in the
regime
\nicefrac{{\log n}}{{\log\log n}}\ll\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\ll\log^{4}n

our estimator experiences a loss in the
\mathsf{SNR}
requirement, which is up to
O(\log^{c}n)
.

Second, our requirement on sample number
n
is almost optimal:
Unnikrishnan et al. 2015
claims
that
n\gtrsim p
is required for correct permutation recovery while our estimator
only needs
n\gg p\cdot\log^{3}n\cdot\log^{2}(n^{2}p^{3})
.
In addition, we have almost minimal constraint on permuted rows’ number, i.e.,
we allow
h_{\textup{max}}\asymp n
, where

h_{\textup{max}}
is the maximum
allowed number of permuted rows.

4.2
Proof outline

To make the proof more digestible, we first outline the proof strategies
and technical challenges before delving into the technical details.
The rigorous proof is attached in the supplementary material, including all supporting Lemmas.
Here, we would like to explain the main technical challenges in the proof of
Theorem
4
and Theorem
5
, which lies in the proof that

\displaystyle\left\{\left\langle\bm{\Pi},\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\right\rangle\geq\langle\bm{\Pi}^{\natural},\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\rangle,~~\exists~\bm{\Pi}\neq\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}\right\}

(9)

holds with probability near zero given the assumptions in Theorem
4
and Theorem
5
. Two noticeable challenges in analyzing the above events are

(i)
combinatorial nature of the optimization problem; and
(ii)
high-order moments of
\mathbf{X}

in the product
\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
. To address these challenges, we construct our solutions
with two building blocks:
(i)
relaxations of error event and
(ii)
modified leave-one-out techniques.
The following context presents a more detailed explanation.

To begin with, we define
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}
respectively as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}

\displaystyle=\left(n-h\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{B}^{\natural},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{B}}

\displaystyle=\left(n-h\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}+\ \left(n-h\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W},\vskip 2.84526pt

where
h
is denoted as the Hamming distance between identity matrix
\mathbf{I}
and
the ground truth permutation matrix
\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}
,
i.e.,
h=\mathsf{d_{H}}(\mathbf{I},\bm{\Pi}^{\natural})
.

Stage I. Relaxation of error event.

To combat the combinatorial nature of (
9
),
we first relax the error event to make it amenable for analysis.
Associated with different regimes comes
different forms of relaxations.

•

Easy regime
.
We relax the error event
\{\bm{\Pi}^{\textup{opt}}\neq\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}\}
as

\displaystyle\left\{\left\langle\mathbf{Y}_{i,:},\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\pi^{\natural}(i),:}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle\mathbf{Y}_{i,:},\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{j,:}\right\rangle,~\exists~1\leq\pi^{\natural}(i)\neq j\leq n\right\}.

(10)

With this relaxation method, we find the constraint

\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\gg\log^{2}n
to be inevitable. However, as compensation, we can show
\mathsf{SNR}\geq c
is sufficient for the correct permutation reconstruction.

•

Medium
\&
hard regime
.
To get rid of the constraint on
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})
, we first exploit the energy-preserving property permutation matrix, i.e.,
{\left|\kern-1.72218pt\left|\kern-1.72218pt\left|\bm{\Pi}\mathbf{M}\right|\kern-1.72218pt\right|\kern-1.72218pt\right|}_{\textup{F}}={\left|\kern-1.72218pt\left|\kern-1.72218pt\left|\mathbf{M}\right|\kern-1.72218pt\right|\kern-1.72218pt\right|}_{\textup{F}}
where
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(\cdot)}
is an arbitrary matrix.
Then we adopt the relaxation

\displaystyle\left\{\big\|\mathbf{Y}_{i,:}-\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\pi^{\natural}(i),:}\big\|_{2}^{2}\geq\big\|\mathbf{Y}_{i,:}-\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{j,:}\big\|_{2}^{2},~~\exists~i,j\right\}.

(11)

In this way, we can relax the constraint on
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})
from

\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\gg\log^{2}n
to
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\gg\log n
if

\mathbf{X}_{ij}
is i.i.d. isotropic sub-gaussian RV. If the log-concavity assumption is further put on
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
’s distribution, we can relax the constraint to
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\gg c
.

However, compared with the above relaxation method, this method will experience some
loss in the
\mathsf{SNR}
requirement. For instance, in the
Easy Regime
,
this approach will require
\mathsf{SNR}
to satisfy
\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim\log^{c}n

for the correct permutation recovery while the previous approach only needs

\mathsf{SNR}\geq c
.

Bonuses of the above relaxations include
(i)
we reduce the moments of
\mathbf{X}

from fourth order in (
9
) to third order, see (
10
) and (
11
); and
(ii)
the dependences between
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
and other
terms in (
10
) and (
11
) are restricted to the rows
\mathbf{X}_{\pi^{\natural}(i),:}
and
\mathbf{X}_{j,:}
. The latter bonus is essential for the leave-one-out technique.

Stage II. Dependence decoupling with leave-one-out technique.

To further reduce the moments of
\mathbf{X}
involved in the analysis,
we modify the
leave-one-out

technique
(
Karoui 2013
;
Karoui et al. 2013
;
Karoui 2018
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Sur et al. 2019
;
Zhang and Li 2020
)
.

As mentioned above,
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
is weakly correlated with
rows
\mathbf{X}_{\pi^{\natural}(i),:},\mathbf{X}_{j,:}
,

\mathbf{Y}_{i,:}
, and
\mathbf{Y}_{j,:}
.
The basic idea of the leave-one-out technique is to
replace the correlated rows in
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
with their i.i.d. copies, by which the dependence is decoupled.
Since only a limited number of rows are replaced, we expect its statistical properties should remain almost identical. A detailed explanation comes as follows.

First, we draw an independent copy
\mathbf{X}_{s,:}^{{}^{\prime}}
for each row

\mathbf{X}_{s,:}
(
s
th row of the sensing matrix
\mathbf{X}
). With these
independent copies, we construct leave-one-out samples
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\setminus(s)}

(1\leq s\leq n)

by replacing the
s
th row in
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}
with its independent copy
\mathbf{X}_{s,:}^{{}^{\prime}}
.
In formulae:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\setminus(s)}=(n-h)^{-1}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq s\\
\pi^{\natural}(k)\neq s\end{subarray}}\mathbf{X}_{\pi^{\natural}(k),:}\mathbf{X}_{k,:}^{\top}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=s\textup{ or}\\
\pi^{\natural}(k)=s\end{subarray}}{\mathbf{X}}^{{}^{\prime}}_{\pi^{\natural}(k),:}{\mathbf{X}}^{{}^{\prime}\top}_{k,:}\bigg)\mathbf{B}^{\natural}.\vskip 2.84526pt

Easily we can verify that
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\setminus(s)}
is independent of

\mathbf{X}_{s,:}
. Similarly, we construct matrices

\{\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\setminus(s,t)}\}_{1\leq s\neq t\leq n}
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\setminus(s,t)}=(n-h)^{-1}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq s,t\\
\pi^{\natural}(k)\neq s,t\end{subarray}}\mathbf{X}_{\pi^{\natural}(k),:}\mathbf{X}_{k,:}^{\top}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=s\textup{ or }k=t\textup{ or}\\
\pi^{\natural}(k)=s\textup{ or }\pi^{\natural}(k)=t\end{subarray}}{\mathbf{X}}^{{}^{\prime}}_{\pi^{\natural}(k),:}{\mathbf{X}}^{{}^{\prime}\top}_{k,:}\bigg)\mathbf{B}^{\natural},\vskip 2.84526pt

and can verify the independence between
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\setminus(s,t)}
and
the rows
\mathbf{X}_{s,:}
and
\mathbf{X}_{t,:}
.

Discussion of related work

This subsection compares our results with
the previous works on multiple observations model
(
m>1
)
(

6
Concluding Remarks

This paper considers unlabeled linear regression
and proposes a one-step estimator,
which is optimal in both
computational and statistical perspectives.
First, we show
our estimator has the same computational complexity
as that of oracle estimators.
Afterwards, we separately investigate its statistical properties
under the single observation model (
m=1
) and multiple observations
model (
m>1
).
For the single observation model, our estimator can obtain the
ground truth permutation matrix when the signal is with length one, i.e.,

p=1
. Moreover, its
\mathsf{SNR}
requirement matches the minimax lower bound, to put
more specifically,
\log\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim\log n
.
For the multiple observations model,
our estimator exhibits much richer behavior, which can be broadly
divided into three regimes.
In the Easy Regime (
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\gg\log^{4}n
), our estimator is minimax optimal
and can obtain the correct permutation
once
\mathsf{SNR}\geq\Omega(1)
; in the Medium Regime (
\log n\ll\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\ll\log^{4}n
),
our estimator experiences a performance loss up to
a multiplicative polynomial of
\log n
as it
requires
\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim\log n
rather than
\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim\Omega(1)

for correct permutation recovery;
and in the
hard regime
(
1\ll\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\ll\log n
), our estimator
needs
\mathsf{SNR}\gtrsim(\log n)^{c_{0}}\cdot n^{\nicefrac{{c_{1}}}{{\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})}}}
, which
matches the statistical limits when

\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\ll\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}
and
experiences a loss up to a multiplicative polynomial
of
\log n
when
\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\ll\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})\ll\log n
.
Comparing with the single observation model, we conclude that
high diversity, namely, large
\textup{srank}(\mathbf{B}^{\natural})
, can
greatly facilitate the permutation recovery.
Ultimately, we provide numerical experiments to support all claims thereof.

A.3
Supporting lemmas for Theorem

This subsection collects the supporting lemmas
for the proof of Theorem

.

Lemma 1
.

We have
\mathbb{P}\left({\cal E}_{1}\right)\geq 1-c_{0}e^{-c_{1}n}
when

n
is sufficiently large, where
c_{0},c_{1}>0
are some positive constants.

Proof.

W.l.o.g, we assume the first
h
entries are permuted and
expand the inner product
\langle\bm{x},\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}\bm{x}\rangle
as

\displaystyle\langle\bm{x},\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}\bm{x}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{h}x_{i}x_{\pi^{\natural}(i)}+\sum_{i=h+1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}.\vskip 2.84526pt

With union bound, we can upper bound

\mathbb{P}\left(\langle\bm{x},\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}\bm{x}\rangle\leq c_{0}n\right)
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\langle\bm{x},\bm{\Pi}^{\natural}\bm{x}\rangle\leq c_{0}n\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\small{\raisebox{0.6pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.8pt}{1}}}}}}{{\leq}}\ \underbrace{\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{h}x_{i}x_{\pi^{\natural}(i)}\leq-\frac{c_{0}n}{2}\right)}_{\triangleq~\zeta_{1}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=h+1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\leq\frac{3c_{0}n}{2}\right)}_{\triangleq~\zeta_{2}}.

(12)

We finish the proof by separately upper-bounding

\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
as
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\lesssim e^{-c_{1}n}
.
The detailed computation comes as follows.

Analysis of
\zeta_{1}
.
The technical difficulties stem from the correlation
between the terms
x_{i}x_{\pi^{\natural}(i)}
.
According to Lemma 8 in
Pananjady et al. 2018
(restated
as Lemma
21
),
we can divide
the index set
\left\{i:i\neq\pi^{\natural}(i)\right\}

into
3
disjoint categories
{\cal I}_{\ell}
,
(1\leq\ell\leq 3)
,
such that (i) indices
i
and
\pi^{\natural}(i)

belongs to different categories; (ii) the cardinality
h_{\ell}
of
{\cal I}_{\ell}
satisfies
h_{\ell}\geq\lfloor h/5\rfloor
.
Then we make the decomposition

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{h}x_{i}x_{\pi^{\natural}(i)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}\sum_{i\in{\cal I}_{\ell}}x_{i}x_{\pi^{\natural}(i)},\vskip 2.84526pt

and again, using the union bound, we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}\leq~

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{i\in{\cal I}_{\ell}}x_{i}x_{\pi^{\natural}(i)}\leq\frac{-c_{0}n}{6}\bigg)\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigg|\sum_{i\in{\cal I}_{\ell}}x_{i}x_{\pi^{\natural}(i)}\bigg|\geq c_{1}n\bigg).

(13)

Recalling the fact that
i
and
\pi^{\natural}(i)
belong
to different categories, we have

\sum_{i\in{\cal I}_{\ell}}x_{i}x_{\pi^{\natural}(i)}

to be identically distributed as
\langle\bm{z}_{1},\bm{z}_{2}\rangle
,
where
\bm{z}_{1},\bm{z}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{\ell}}
are i.i.d
sub-gaussian random vectors.
Then we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigg|\sum_{i\in{\cal I}_{\ell}}x_{i}x_{\pi^{\natural}(i)}\bigg|\geq c_{1}n\bigg)\leq~

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big({\left\|\bm{z}_{2}\right\|}_{2}\gtrsim\sqrt{h_{\ell}\log n}\big)+\mathbb{P}\big(|\langle\bm{z}_{1},\bm{z}_{2}\rangle|\geq c_{1}n,~{\left\|\bm{z}_{2}\right\|}_{2}\lesssim\sqrt{h_{\ell}\log n}\big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\small{\raisebox{0.6pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.8pt}{2}}}}}}{{\leq}}~

\displaystyle e^{-cn}+2\exp\left(-\frac{c_{1}^{2}n^{2}}{h_{\ell}\log n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\small{\raisebox{0.6pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.8pt}{3}}}}}}{{\lesssim}}e^{-cn},\vskip 2.84526pt

where
\raisebox{-.8pt}{2}⃝
is due to the tail bound of sub-gaussian RVs
and
\raisebox{-.8pt}{3}⃝
is because
h_{\ell}\leq h\lesssim n
.
Thus we show
\zeta_{1}\lesssim e^{-cn}
.

Analysis of
\zeta_{2}
.
Its analysis is a direct consequence of Lemma

Lemma 10

Conditioning on

Same as the procedure in

{\left|\kern-1.72218pt\left|\kern-1.72218pt\left|\mathbf{B}^{\natural}-\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\setminus(s)}\right|\kern-1.72218pt\right|\kern-1.72218pt\right|}_{\textup{F}}\lesssim(\log n)(\log n^{2}p^{3})\sqrt{\nicefrac{{p}}{{n}}}{\left|\kern-1.72218pt\left|\kern-1.72218pt\left|\mathbf{B}^{\natural}\right|\kern-1.72218pt\right|\kern-1.72218pt\right|}_{\textup{F}}
.
As for probability
\zeta_{2}
, we have it to be zero
conditional on
{\cal F}_{7}
.
The proof is thus completed.
∎
