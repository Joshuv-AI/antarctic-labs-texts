Tobit Models for Count Time Series
Christian H. Weiß, Fukang Zhu

Abstract
Several models for count time series have been developed during the last decades, often inspired by traditional autoregressive moving average (ARMA) models for real-valued time series, including integer-valued ARMA (INARMA) and integer-valued generalized autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (INGARCH) models. Both INARMA and INGARCH models exhibit an ARMA-like autocorrelation function (ACF). To achieve negative ACF values within the class of INGARCH models, log and softplus link functions are suggested in the literature, where the softplus approach leads to conditional linearity in good approximation. However, the softplus approach is limited to the INGARCH family for unbounded counts, i. e., it can neither be used for bounded counts, nor for count processes from the INARMA family. In this paper, we present an alternative solution, named the Tobit approach, for achieving approximate linearity together with negative ACF values, which is more generally applicable than the softplus approach. A Skellam–Tobit INGARCH model for unbounded counts is studied in detail, including stationarity, approximate computation of moments, maximum likelihood and censored least absolute deviations estimation for unknown parameters and corresponding simulations. Extensions of the Tobit approach to other situations are also discussed, including underlying discrete distributions, INAR models, and bounded counts. Three real-data examples are considered to illustrate the usefulness of the new approach. Key words: count time series; INGARCH models; maximum likelihood estimation; negative autocorrelation; Skellam distribution; Tobit model.



1
Introduction

Count time series
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
, are discrete-valued quantitative time series, which stem from an underlying count process
\{X_{t}\ |\ t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
,
\mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots\}
, with the range being contained in
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
. More precisely, if the range of
\{X_{t}\}
is equal to
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we are concerned with unbounded counts, whereas bounded counts have the finite range
\{0,\ldots,N\}
with some upper bound
N\in\mathbb{N}

(
Weiß, 2018
)
. Several models for count time series have been developed during the last decades, often inspired by the traditional autoregressive moving average (ARMA) models for real-valued time series. Such proposals include the integer-valued ARMA (INARMA) models for stationary count time series
(
McKenzie, 1985
;
Alzaid & Al-Osh, 1990
;
Du & Li, 1991
, among others by)
, where the ARMA recursion is adapted by using types of “thinning operators”, and the integer-valued generalized autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (INGARCH) models
(
Ferland et al., 2006
;
Fokianos et al., 2009
;
Xu et al., 2012
;
Zhu, 2012
, among others by)
, which are conditional regression models having a linear conditional mean. Both the INARMA and the INGARCH models exhibit an ARMA-like autocorrelation structure, in the sense that their autocorrelation function (ACF) satisfies a set of Yule–Walker equations. However, by contrast to the ordinary ARMA models, the attainable range of ACF values of these ARMA-like count models is often restricted to only positive ACF values. This follows from the parameter constraints being needed to ensure the non-negative outcomes for the process.

To achieve negative ACF values within the class of conditional regression models, a traditional solution is to use a log link
(
Fokianos & Tjøstheim, 2011
)
, but then the model behaves distinctly non-linear and the ARMA-like ACF is lost.
Linear models, by contrast, are popular among practitioners as they are “simple, useful, and interpretable in a wide range of contexts”
(
Grunwald et al., 2000
, p. 479)
. They also allow to describe important stochastic properties by closed formulae (e. g., the ACF by Yule–Walker equations) and to derive simple moment estimators for initial model fit.
Thus,
Weiß et al. (2022)
recently proposed the family of softplus INGARCH models, which use the softplus function as a smooth but nearly (piecewise) linear response function. This model successfully combines an approximately ARMA-like ACF with the ability to handle negative ACF values. However, the softplus approach is limited to the INGARCH family for unbounded counts, i. e., it can neither be used for bounded counts, nor for count processes from the INARMA family.

Therefore, in this paper, we present an alternative solution for achieving approximate linearity together with negative ACF values, which is more generally applicable than the softplus approach. For comparison purposes, our novel approach is first exemplified and discussed within the INGARCH family for unbounded counts, but later we show how to adapt it to other situations as well. Thus, as the first step, we propose a novel family of conditional regression models for unbounded counts, which is defined as a combination of the INGARCH approach with the so-called Tobit model (
To
bin’s pro
bit
model, see
Tobin (1958)
;
Goldberger (1964)
as well as Section
2
).
An INGARCH
(p,q)
model with
p\geq 1
and
q\geq 0
is characterized by requiring the conditional mean
M_{t}=E(X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{X_{t-1},X_{t-2},\ldots\}
, to be a linear expression in the last
p
observations and the last
q
conditional means (“feedback terms”), i. e.,

M_{t}\ =\ a_{0}\ +\ \sum_{i=1}^{p}\,a_{i}\,X_{t-i}\ +\ \sum_{j=1}^{q}\,b_{j}\,M_{t-j}.

(1.1)

In standard INGARCH models, see Appendix
A.1
for details,

M_{t}
is used as the conditional mean of a count random variable and, thus, has to be a positive real number. Therefore, recursion (
1.1
) is accompanied by the constraints
a_{0}>0
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{p},b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}\geq 0
, which, in turn, cause the ACF to be non-negative. So the first idea to allow for negative ACF values might be to skip the aforementioned parameter constraints and to use
\max\big\{0,M_{t}\big\}
as the conditional mean of the generated count. Since the response function
\max\{0,x\}
is also known as the rectified linear unit (ReLU) function, let us refer to this approach as the
ReLU INGARCH model
. This model, however, has a serious drawback: if
M_{t}\leq 0
happens at time
t
, then the next count generated by the ReLU INGARCH model has the mean zero, i. e., we have a degenerate count distribution with all probability mass in zero. This, in turn, will cause problems in likelihood computations, time series forecasting, and others. Therefore, a different solution is required to allow
M_{t}
according to (
1.1
) to become negative.

The above drawbacks of the ReLU INGARCH model are avoided by our novel
Tobit INGARCH model
. There, we first generate an integer-valued random variable
X_{t}^{*}
with range
\mathbb{Z}
(so including negative integers!) and having the conditional mean
M_{t}
(which is possibly negative).
X_{t}^{*}
might be understood as a kind of “auxiliary” or “intermediate” variable, which is only required to catch the case where the negative dependence parameters in (
1.1
) cause a negative outcome. Such negative outcomes are undesirable as we aim at modeling a count process. Therefore, in a second step,
X_{t}^{*}
is left-censored at zero by computing
X_{t}=\max\big\{0,X_{t}^{*}\big\}
(Tobit approach); details are given in Section
2
. Note that our Tobit approach differs from the aforementioned ReLU approach, because the counts generated by
X_{t}=\max\big\{0,X_{t}^{*}\big\}
always have a truly positive mean, i. e., we never have a degenerate count distribution (provided that
X_{t}^{*}
takes positive values with non-zero probability). A detailed discussion of this general model as well as of its stochastic properties is provided by Section
2
. Afterwards in Section
3
, we consider the important special case, where
X_{t}^{*}
is generated from a conditional Skellam distribution
(
Johnson et al., 2005
, Section 4.12.3)
, leading to the Skellam–Tobit INGARCH model. For this model, we also evaluate its abilities for approximating linearity and for capturing negative ACF values. Section
4
then presents and investigates approaches for parameter estimation.
Section
5
discusses a variety of possible extensions, i. e., how the Tobit approach could be adapted to models outside the unbounded INGARCH class, and the extensions are mainly about underlying discrete distributions, INAR models, and bounded counts.
Section
6
illustrates the application of the Tobit count models to three real-world count time series: lottery winners counts,
chemical process yields, and daily air quality levels.
Finally, Section
7
concludes and outlines issues for future research.

2
The Class of Tobit INGARCH Models

The basic Tobit model, a censored probit regression model, was introduced by
Tobin (1958)
and has been extended in several ways, see Chapter 6 in
Maddala (1983)
for a comprehensive survey.
Some of these extensions refer to time series data. For example, a dynamic Tobit model for real-valued random variables
Y_{t}
(left-censored at zero) might be of the form

Y_{t}\ =\ \max\big\{0,\ Y_{t}^{*}\big\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptY_{t}^{*}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{p}\rho_{i}\,Y_{t-i}+\epsilon_{t}\ +\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{t},

(2.1)

see
Maddala (1983)
;
Honoré (1993)
;
Honoré & Hu (2004)
;
de Jong & Herrera (2011)
. If
\epsilon_{t}
is normally distributed, then the conditional distribution of
Y_{t}^{*}
given
Y_{t-1},\ldots
and
\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{t}
is also normal, i. e.,
Y_{t}
with range
[0;\infty)
is generated by censoring the
\mathbb{R}
-valued
Y_{t}^{*}
at zero. In what follows, this idea is transfered to the censoring of a
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random variable
X_{t}^{*}
, leading to the count random variable
X_{t}=\max\big\{0,X_{t}^{*}\big\}
with range
\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

To overcome the limitations of the ordinary INGARCH model while (approximately) preserving its additive structure, we propose the novel class of Tobit INGARCH models. For defining this model, let us introduce the
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random operator
\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(\mu)
, where
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the mean of
\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(\mu)
, and
\nu
comprises some further distributional parameters (e. g., such as a dispersion parameter).
The letter “
\mathcal{S}
” might be understood as referring to
s
igned integer outcomes from
\mathbb{Z}
. Given the values of
\mu
and
\nu
,
\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(\mu)
is a random variable on
\mathbb{Z}
. The terminology “operator” is used in analogy to the thinning operators in INARMA modeling, to express that
\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(\mu)
acts on
\mu
by generating an integer value.
It is assumed that the distribution of
\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(\mu)
is such that
P\big(\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(\mu)>0\big)>0
holds for all
\mu
and
\nu
.
Later in Section
3
, we consider the special case of the operator following the Skellam distribution with mean
\mu
and dispersion parameter
\delta>0
, where this assumption is satisfied.

In analogy to (
2.1
), the
Tobit INGARCH model
is defined by the equation

X_{t}\ =\ \max\big\{0,\ X_{t}^{*}\big\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptX_{t}^{*}\ =\ \mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(M_{t}),

(2.2)

where we assume the conditional mean
M_{t}=E(X_{t}^{*}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
to satisfy the linear INGARCH equation

M_{t}\ =\ \alpha_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}\,X_{t-i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}\,M_{t-j}

(2.3)

with
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{q}\in\mathbb{R}
, recall (
1.1
). Note that (
2.2
) is equivalently stated as
X_{t}=X_{t}^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X_{t}^{*})
,
because the integer ReLU function
\max\{0,x\}
can be rewritten as
x\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(x)
, where the indicator function
\mathds{1}_{A}(x)
equals 1 (0) if
x\in A
(
x\not\in A
).

According to
Grunwald et al. (2000)
, a time series model for
\{X_{t}\}
can be classified as being linear (thus satisfying, e. g., a set of Yule–Walker equations for the ACF) if its conditional mean
E(X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
is linear in past observations. From (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), it gets clear that the
\mathbb{Z}
-valued
X_{t}^{*}
has an exactly linear conditional mean and would, thus, satisfy the linearity requirement of
Grunwald et al. (2000)
. However, it might happen that
X_{t}^{*}=\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(M_{t})
takes a negative value, which contradicts the count nature of the target process. Therefore, (
2.2
) turns
X_{t}^{*}
into the count
X_{t}
that we are actually interested in, at the price that
X_{t}
is no longer linear in
X_{t-i}
and
M_{t-j}
because of the censoring. Nevertheless, we shall see in Section
3.3
below that by an appropriate choice of
\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(\cdot)
, we can achieve that
E(X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
behaves at least
nearly
linearly in past observations, such that the corresponding Tobit INGARCH model can be applied to linear count time series data in practice.

For being able to evaluate the extent of linearity, let us first derive formulae for the conditional probabilities and moments of
\{X_{t}\}
. While
X_{t}^{*}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random variable, the
X_{t}
generated by (
2.2
) is a count random variable with conditional distribution

P(X_{t}=x|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}P\big(\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(M_{t})=x\big)&\text{if }x>0,\\[2.15277pt]
P\big(\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(M_{t})\leq 0\big)&\text{if }x=0.\end{array}\right.

As a result, the conditional mean of
X_{t}
given
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
computes as

E(X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\ =\ E\big(X_{t}^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X_{t}^{*})\big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)\ =\ \sum_{x=1}^{\infty}x\,P\big(\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(M_{t})=x\big).

(2.4)

Higher-order conditional moments are computed analogously as

E(X_{t}^{r}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=E\big((X_{t}^{*})^{r}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X_{t}^{*})\big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptr\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.5)

We note that these moments are equal to the corresponding partial moments of
X_{t}^{*}
(left-censored at zero). So having specified the
\mathbb{Z}
-valued distribution of
\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(\mu)
, these partial moments can be derived. This is illustrated in Section
3
, where the special case of a conditional Skellam distribution is discussed in detail.

3
Skellam–Tobit INGARCH Model

3.1
Skellam Distribution and Partial Moments

Probably the most well-known example of a parametric distribution for a
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random variable is the
Skellam distribution
,
\textup{Sk}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
with
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
, see Section 4.12.3 in
Johnson et al. (2005)
and the references mentioned therein. It is sometimes also referred to as the “Poisson-difference distribution”, because
X^{*}\sim\textup{Sk}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
can be generated as the difference
X^{*}=X_{1}-X_{2}
of the two independent Poisson variables
X_{1}\sim\textup{Poi}(\lambda_{1})
and
X_{2}\sim\textup{Poi}(\lambda_{2})
. Its mean and variance are given by
\mu=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma^{2}=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}>|\mu|\geq 0
, respectively. Its probability mass function (PMF) equals

P(X^{*}=x)\ =\ {\rm e}^{-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}\,\big(\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}\big)^{x/2}\,I_{x}\big(2\,\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\big)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for }x\in\mathbb{Z},

(3.1)

where

I_{n}(z)\>=\ \left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\frac{z}{2})^{2k}}{k!\cdot(k+n)!}\ \text{for }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\hskip 20.00003ptI_{-n}(z)\>=\ I_{n}(z),

(3.2)

denotes the modified Bessel function of the first kind, which is implemented in R by
besselI
.
This distribution has been used to analyze some independent data (such as
Karlis & Ntzoufras (2006)
;
Karlis & Ntzoufras (2009)
) and time series data (such as
Koopman et al. (2017)
).

Remark 1

The modified Bessel function of the first kind satisfies

I_{n+1}(z)\ =\ I_{n-1}(z)\ -\ \frac{2n}{z}\cdot I_{n}(z).

(3.3)

In addition, we have

\displaystyle I_{n}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{2}[I_{n-1}(z)+I_{n+1}(z)],~~I_{n}^{\prime}(z)=I_{n-1}(z)-\frac{n}{z}I_{n}(z),~~I_{n}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{n}{z}I_{n}(z)+I_{n+1}(z),

(3.4)

see
Jeffrey & Dai (2008, §§ 17)
for these and further properties.

Equations (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) immediately imply important relations for the Skellam’s PMF (
3.1
), namely

P(X^{*}=-x)\ \overset{\eqref{bessel}}{=}\ e^{-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}\,\big(\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}\big)^{x/2-x}\,I_{x}\big(2\,\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\big)\ =\ \big(\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}\big)^{x}\,P(X^{*}=x),

(3.5)

and

\begin{array}[]{@{}rl}P(X^{*}=x+1)\ \overset{\eqref{bessel2}}{=}&e^{-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}\,\big(\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}\big)^{(x+1)/2}\,\Big(I_{x-1}\big(2\,\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\big)\ -\ \frac{x}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}}\,I_{x}\big(2\,\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\big)\Big)\\[4.30554pt]
\ =&\big(\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}\big)\,P(X^{*}=x-1)\ -\ \big(x/\lambda_{2}\big)\,P(X^{*}=x).\end{array}

(3.6)

These results shall be useful for computing partial moments, see the details below.

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
\textup{Sk}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is easily computed in practice, by utilizing Equation (4) in
Johnson (1959)
on a connection to the non-central
\chi^{2}
-distribution. Let
\chi^{2}(\nu,\tau)
denote the non-central
\chi^{2}
-distribution with
\nu
degrees of freedom and non-centrality parameter
\tau
(whose CDF is provided by
pchisq
in R). Then, Equation (4) in
Johnson (1959)
implies that

P(X^{*}\leq x)\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}P(Q\leq 2\lambda_{2})\text{ with }Q\sim\chi^{2}(-2x,2\lambda_{1})&\text{if }x\leq 0,\\[4.30554pt]
1-P(Q\leq 2\lambda_{1})\text{ with }Q\sim\chi^{2}\big(2(x+1),2\lambda_{2}\big)&\text{if }x>0.\end{array}\right.

(3.7)

Finally, let us turn to the computation of partial moments. In view of (
2.4
), for example, the partial moment
E\big(X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})\big)
is relevant to us, while the second-order moment
E\big((X^{*})^{2}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})\big)
, also see (
2.5
), is used for computing the partial variance.
Note that the Skellam distribution is characterized by the Stein equation

E\big(X^{*}\,f(X^{*})\big)\ =\ \lambda_{1}\,E\big(f(X^{*}+1)\big)\ -\ \lambda_{2}\,E\big(f(X^{*}-1)\big)

(3.8)

for any bounded function
f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
, see Theorem 1 in
Balachandran et al. (2017)
. Such a Stein equation can be utilized to computing sophisticated moment expressions, see
Weiß & Aleksandrov (2022)
, and it is now applied for deriving the relevant partial moments.

Proposition 1

Let
X^{*}\sim\textup{Sk}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, then it has the partial mean

E\big(X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})\big)\ =\ \mu\,P(X^{*}\geq 0)\ +\ \lambda_{2}\,\big(P(X^{*}=0)+P(X^{*}=1)\big)

(3.9)

and the second-order partial moment

E\big((X^{*})^{2}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})\big)\ =\ (\sigma^{2}+\mu^{2})\,P(X^{*}\geq 1)\ +\ \lambda_{2}\,\mu\,P(X^{*}=1)\ +\ \lambda_{1}\,(1+\mu)\,P(X^{*}=0).

(3.10)

The proof of Proposition
1
is provided by Appendix
A.2
.
The partial-moment expressions in Proposition
1
are evaluated by using Equations (
3.1
) and (
3.7
) on the Skellam’s PMF and CDF, respectively.
Note that (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) imply the variance of
X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})
.

In practice, it may be advantageous to reparametrize
\textup{Sk}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
by the mean
\mu
and an additional dispersion parameter
\delta>0
, defined by the additive decomposition
\sigma^{2}=|\mu|+\delta
.
Thus, the reparametrized version
\textup{Sk}^{*}(\mu,\delta)
is related to the original version
\textup{Sk}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
by

\lambda_{1}\ =\ \tfrac{1}{2}\,\big(|\mu|+\mu+\delta\big),\hskip 20.00003pt\lambda_{2}\ =\ \tfrac{1}{2}\,\big(|\mu|-\mu+\delta\big).

(3.11)

Note that the limit
\delta\to 0
leads to (the negative of) a
\textup{Poi}\big(|\mu|\big)
-variate if
\mu>0
(
\mu<0
), i. e., the Poisson distribution is a boundary case of the Skellam distribution.

Example 1

In the case
\mu=0
(i. e.,
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\delta/2
), the Skellam distribution is symmetric around 0 with
P(X^{*}=x)=e^{-\delta}\,I_{x}(\delta)
and
P(X^{*}\geq 1)=\big(1-P(X^{*}=0)\big)/2
. Equations (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) simplify to

\begin{array}[]{rl}E\big(X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})\big)\ =&\tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta\,\big(P(X^{*}=0)+P(X^{*}=1)\big)\ =\ \tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta\,e^{-\delta}\,\big(I_{0}(\delta)+I_{1}(\delta)\big).\\[4.30554pt]
E\big((X^{*})^{2}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})\big)\ =&\delta\,P(X^{*}\geq 1)\ +\ \tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta\,P(X^{*}=0)\ =\ \tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta.\end{array}

Thus, we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}V\big(X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})\big)\ =&\tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta-\tfrac{1}{4}\,\delta^{2}\,e^{-2\,\delta}\,\big(I_{0}(\delta)+I_{1}(\delta)\big)^{2}.\end{array}

3.2
Skellam–Tobit INGARCH Model: Definition and Properties

Let us define the Skellam operator
\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(\mu)
to generate a random integer number according to
\textup{Sk}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),\mu=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}
. Then, adapting the general Tobit INGARCH approach (
2.2
), we define the
Skellam–Tobit INGARCH (STINGARCH) model
by

X_{t}\ =\ \max\big\{0,\ X_{t}^{*}\big\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptX_{t}^{*}\ =\ \mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(M_{t}),

(3.12)

where
M_{t}=E(X_{t}^{*}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
satisfies the linear INGARCH equation (
2.3
). Note that
Doukhan et al. (2021)
considered a Skellam GARCH model for
\mathbb{Z}
-valued time series based on modelling the conditional variance, but here we will provide a method for analyzing non-negative count time series based on modeling the conditional mean.

Theorem 1

Consider the STINGARCH process
\{X_{t}\}
defined in (
3.12
). If

\sum_{i=1}^{p}\max\{0,\alpha_{i}\}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}|\beta_{j}|<1,

(3.13)

then
\{X_{t}\}
exists and is stationary.

The proof of Theorem
1
is provided by Appendix
A.3
.

Remark 2

The above condition is comparable to the ones in other real-valued dynamic Tobit models, such as
de Jong & Herrera (2011)
,
Michel & de Jong (2018)
, and
Bykhovskaya (2023)
. This condition is also comparable to the one for the INGARCH model in
Weiß et al. (2022)
.
Due to the censoring mechanism, we do not need to impose lower bounds on the parameters
\alpha_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,p
.

In what follows, we consider the STINGARCH model based on the additive reparametrization (
3.11
), i. e.,

X_{t}\ =\ \max\big\{0,\ X_{t}^{*}\big\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptX_{t}^{*}\ =\ \mathcal{S}_{\delta}(M_{t}).

(3.14)

Then, the conditional probability of
X_{t}^{\ast}
given
M_{t}
is

\displaystyle P(X_{t}^{\ast}=x|M_{t})

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-|M_{t}|-\delta}\left(\frac{|M_{t}|+M_{t}+\delta}{|M_{t}|-M_{t}+\delta}\right)^{x/2}I_{x}\Big(\sqrt{\big(|M_{t}|+M_{t}+\delta\big)\big(|M_{t}|-M_{t}+\delta\big)}\Big)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-M_{t}-\delta}\left(\frac{2M_{t}+\delta}{\delta}\right)^{x/2}\,I_{x}\Big(\sqrt{\delta\,(2M_{t}+\delta)}\Big)&\text{if }M_{t}\geq 0,\\
\displaystyle{\rm e}^{M_{t}-\delta}\left(\frac{\delta}{-2M_{t}+\delta}\right)^{x/2}\,I_{x}\Big(\sqrt{\delta\,(-2M_{t}+\delta)}\Big)&\text{if }M_{t}<0.\end{array}\right.

For the reparametrized STINGARCH model (
3.14
),
the limit
\delta\to 0
leads to the ReLU INGARCH model with conditional Poisson distribution
\textup{Poi}\big(\max\{0,M_{t}\}\big)
.

Remark 3

As explained before, we use an additive reparametrization for the Skellam’s relation between variance and mean. This differs from
Carallo et al. (2024)
, who use the multiplicative decomposition
\sigma^{2}=\tilde{\delta}\,|\mu|
with
\tilde{\delta}>1
(as
|\mu|<\sigma^{2}
always holds). However, to guarantee
\sigma^{2}>0
, such a multiplicative parametrization requires to assume that
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
, which is unnecessarily restrictive. It would also lead to rather different moment plots than those in Figure
1
in Section
3.3
below.

It should be noted that the considered reparametrization does not affect Theorem
1
, because we established the existence and stationarity for the original version of Skellam.

3.3
On Moments and Approximate Linearity

The Tobit INGARCH approach was proposed as an approximate solution for achieving a conditionally linear count model allowing for negative ACF values. This conjecture shall now to be verified.
To compute the conditional moments (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) for the STINGARCH model (
3.14
), the Skellam’s partial moments in Proposition
1
are required.
The resulting mean, variance, and dispersion ratio of such left-censored
\textup{Sk}^{*}(\mu,\delta)
-variates are illustrated by Figure
1
. It can be seen that both the mean and the variance behave roughly linear outside
\mu=0
, and the extent of linearity increases with decreasing
\delta
. While the mean is rather stable for varying values of
\delta
, the dispersion ratio increases with increasing
\delta
(but is roughly constant for varying
\mu
). For
\delta\to 0
, the dispersion ratio converges to the constant 1 (for all
\mu
), whereas the limiting mean (=limiting variance) equals
\max\{0,\mu\}
. This goes along with our above statement that the limit
\delta\to 0
leads to the Poisson ReLU INGARCH model.

The approximate (piecewise) linearity of the left-censored Skellam’s mean in Figure
1
(for small
\delta
such as 0.25) leads us to conjecture that also the STINGARCH model behaves similar to a truly linear model, because it implies that the conditional mean
E(X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
is approximately linear in past observations, also recall
Grunwald et al. (2000)
. More precisely, we conjecture that the marginal mean
\mu
, variance
\sigma^{2}
, and ACF
\rho(h)
with time lag
h\in\mathbb{N}
of a STINGARCH process
\{X_{t}\}
can be computed in good approximation according to the same formula as for the truly linear, so-called “dispersed INGARCH” (DINGARCH) model of
Xu et al. (2012)
, see Appendix
A.1
for details, but by also allowing for negative parameter values (which are impossible for the DINGARCH model). Here, the DINGARCH’s dispersion parameter
\eta
is chosen to equal the dispersion ratio
I_{\mu,\delta}^{*}:=V\big(X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})\big)\,\big/\,E\big(X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})\big)
for
X^{*}\sim\textup{Sk}^{*}(\mu,\delta)
, which is computed according to Proposition
1
.
Let us illustrate this approximate approach for the STINARCH
(1)
and STINGARCH
(1,1)
model. We propose the approximate scheme

\begin{array}[]{l@{\qquad}l}(p,q)=(1,0):&\displaystyle\mu\ \approx\ \frac{\alpha_{0}}{1-\alpha_{1}},\quad\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu}\ \approx\ I_{\mu,\delta}^{*}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{1}^{2}},\quad\rho(h)\ \approx\ \alpha_{1}^{h}\text{ for }h\geq 1,\\[12.91663pt]
(p,q)=(1,1):&\displaystyle\mu\ \approx\ \frac{\alpha_{0}}{1-\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1}},\quad\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu}\ \approx\ I_{\mu,\delta}^{*}\cdot\frac{1-(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})^{2}+\alpha_{1}^{2}}{1-(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})^{2}},\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle\rho(h)\ \approx\ (\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})^{h-1}\,\frac{\alpha_{1}\,\big(1-\beta_{1}(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})\big)}{1-(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})^{2}+\alpha_{1}^{2}}\text{ for }h\geq 1,\end{array}

(3.17)

for moment computation in these cases. To check the quality of this approximation and, thus, the extent of linearity of the STINGARCH models, we compare the linear moment values being computed according to (
3.17
) with their true values, which are computed numerically exactly for the STINARCH
(1)
model (by utilizing its Markov property), and approximately for STINGARCH
(1,1)
(based on a simulated sample path of length
10^{6}
).
Results are tabulated in Appendix
B
; let us start with the STINARCH
(1)
case. For different choices of
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\delta
, the true values (columns “tob”) and anticipated linear values (columns “lin”, computed via (
3.17
)) for marginal mean, dispersion ratio, and partial ACF (PACF) at lags
h=1,2,3
have been computed, where
\delta=0
corresponds to the limiting case of the Poi-ReLU INARCH
(1)
model. The latter just serves as the benchmark for the maximal extent of linearity within the INARCH
(1)
approach, but it is not relevant for practice because of the drawbacks outlined in Section
1
.

Table
B.1
provides results for the case, where the marginal mean is close to 5 (“low counts”). Comparing the “tob-columns” to the “lin-columns”, we generally see a good agreement, which further improves for decreasing
\delta
(and which is even better for larger mean, see Table
B.2
for comparison). Except for strong negative autocorrelation (as implied by
\alpha_{1}=-0.75
), the choice
\delta=0.25
, for example, leads to linearity in close approximation. For
\alpha_{1}=-0.75
, it should be noted that even the exactly piecewise-linear Poi-ReLU INARCH
(1)
model causes departures from linearity, so some extent of non-linearity cannot be avoided in this extreme situation. It is also worth pointing out that the tob- and lin-values of the dispersion ratio
\sigma^{2}/\mu
agree quite well even for larger
\delta
(except the extreme scenario
\alpha_{1}=-0.75
), i. e., the approximation
I_{\mu,\delta}^{*}/(1-\alpha_{1}^{2})
generally shows a good performance.

If we now turn to the corresponding STINGARCH
(1,1)
results in Tables
B.3
–
B.4
, where the ACF instead of the PACF is considered, we again see a very close agreement throughout. The additional feedback term did not affect the closeness to linearity compared to our previous STINARCH
(1)
conclusions.
Thus, to sum up, if
\delta
is chosen sufficiently small such as
\delta=0.25
, the novel Tobit approach leads to approximately linear count time series models that capture a wide range of negative ACF values. Therefore, STINGARCH models are recommended as a kind of “workhorse” for linear count processes with signed ACF values.

4
Estimation

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be observations from model (
3.12
).
Define the parameter vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots
,
\alpha_{p},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{q},\delta)^{\top}
, and let
\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{q})^{\top}
, i. e.,
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}^{\top},\delta)^{\top}
.
Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{0}=(\alpha_{00},\alpha_{10},\ldots,\alpha_{p0},\beta_{10},\ldots,\beta_{q0})^{\top}
be the true value of
\vartheta
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{0}^{\top},\delta_{0})^{\top}
be the one of
\theta
. The parameter space of
\theta
is
\Theta
and the parameter space of
\vartheta
is
\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}^{\ast}
, which are compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{p+q+2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p+q+1}
, respectively, and can be further refined when deriving large-sample properties of the following considered estimators.
We have to distinguish two general estimation scenarios:

Scenario 1:

\delta
is considered as a tuning parameter that is specified in advance to achieve approximate linearity. Recalling the discussion in Section
3.3
, setting
\delta:=0.25
or smaller might be such a choice. Then, parameter estimation refers to solely
\vartheta
.

Scenario 2:

\delta
is considered as a parameter of the data-generating process (DGP). Then, parameter estimation refers to whole
\theta
.

In our opinion, scenario 1 is more relevant for practice. In view of George Box’ famous words “all models are wrong, but some are useful”, we consider the STINGARCH model as tool (workhorse) for describing count time series exhibiting linear sample properties and negative dependencies. Here, the parameter
\delta
is used as a tuning parameter set by the user to control the extent of linearity, similar to the parameter
c
of the softplus INGARCH model of
Weiß et al. (2022)
mentioned in Section
1
. The user may also define multiple candidate models with different extents of linearity. But for completeness, we also investigate scenario 2 in some detail.

We consider two estimation methods: the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) in Section
4.1
, and the censored least absolute deviations estimator (CLADE) in Section
4.2
.
The second method is expected to be robust if the underlying distribution is mis-specified, and it can be applied to estimation scenario 1. The first method can be used for both estimation scenarios.

4.1
Maximum Likelihood Estimation

First, we consider the MLE of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
(i. e., estimation scenario 2).
Based on (
3.2
), define
p_{t}(x)=P(X_{t}^{\ast}=x|M_{t})
and
f_{t}(x)=P(X_{t}^{\ast}\leq x|M_{t})
. Then, the conditional log-likelihood function is

L(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptl_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\ =\ \mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X_{t})\,\log{p_{t}(X_{t})}\ +\ \mathds{1}_{\{0\}}(X_{t})\,\log{f_{t}(0)},

(4.1)

where Equations (
3.1
) and (
3.7
) are used for the PMF and CDF computations, respectively.
If
X_{t}>0
, then

\displaystyle l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle-M_{t}-\delta+\tfrac{1}{2}\,X_{t}\,\Big[\log(2M_{t}+\delta)-\log\delta\Big]+\log I_{X_{t}}\Big(\sqrt{\delta(2M_{t}+\delta)}\Big)&\text{if }M_{t}\geq 0,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle M_{t}-\delta-\tfrac{1}{2}\,X_{t}\,\Big[\log(-2M_{t}+\delta)-\log\delta\Big]+\log I_{X_{t}}\Big(\sqrt{\delta(-2M_{t}+\delta)}\Big)&\text{if }M_{t}<0.\end{array}\right.

If
X_{t}=0
, then
l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\log{f_{t}(0)}
.
The MLE
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}
is defined as
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}={\arg\max}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}}L(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
.
If MLE is applied to estimation scenario 1, then
\delta
is specified in advance, and the maximization of (
4.1
) is done with respect to
\vartheta
.

The above likelihood function is cumbersome (for both scenarios), so we need some numerical algorithms to find the MLE
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}
.
The score function and the Hessian matrix are defined as

\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\ =\ \sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\partial l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\mbox{{{H}}}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\ =\ -\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\top}},

respectively. The MLE
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}
also is a solution to the equation
\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
.
Detailed expressions for the partial derivatives are discussed in Appendix
A.4
.

For establishing consistency and asymptotic normality of MLE, one could verify the regularity conditions in Theorems 2.7 and 3.1 in
Newey & McFadden (1994)
or Theorems 4.1.1 and 4.1.3 in
Amemiya (1985)
. But because of the complex expressions for the modified Bessel function of the first kind in the likelihood function, it is hardly possible to do so. Note that the aforementioned theorems would imply that the asymptotic variance of the MLE is
\mbox{{{U}}}^{-1}\mbox{{{V}}}\mbox{{{U}}}^{-1}/n
, where

\mbox{{{V}}}\ =\ E\left(\frac{\partial l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}\,\frac{\partial l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\top}}\right),\hskip 20.00003pt\mbox{{{U}}}\ =\ -E\left(\frac{\partial^{2}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\top}}\right),

and where these two matrices are evaluated at the true value
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
. This implies to approximate the asymptotic variance by
(\hat{\mbox{{{U}}}}_{n}\hat{\mbox{{{V}}}}^{-1}_{n}\hat{\mbox{{{U}}}}_{n})^{-1}/n
,
where

\hat{\mbox{{{V}}}}_{n}\ =\ \left.\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\partial l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}\,\frac{\partial l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\top}}\right|_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}=\hat{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}}},\hskip 20.00003pt\hat{\mbox{{{U}}}}_{n}\ =\ -\left.\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\top}}\right|_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}=\hat{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}}}.

As a practically feasible solution, we recommend to approximate the MLE’s standard errors by using the numerical Hessian of the maximized log-likelihood function. More precisely, we use the square-roots of the diagonal of the inverse Hessian, as it is common practice in applications of the ML approach. The performance of these approximate standard errors is later investigated by simulations in Section
4.3
.

4.2
Censored Least Absolute Deviations Estimation

CLADE is a popular estimation method for censored models since
Powell (1984)
, also see
de Jong & Herrera (2011)
,
Bilias et al. (2019)
and
Bykhovskaya (2023)
.
As before, let
\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots
,
\alpha_{p},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{q})^{\top}
, and let
\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{0}=(\alpha_{00},\alpha_{10},\ldots,\alpha_{p0},\beta_{10},\ldots,\beta_{q0})^{\top}
be its true value.
The CLADE
\check{\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}
is formalized as

\check{\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}=\arg\min_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\vartheta$}\in\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\Theta$}^{\ast}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|X_{t}-\max\{0,M_{t}\}\big|,

(4.2)

where
M_{t}
is defined in (
2.3
). Here, we consider the CLADE for
\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{0}
, not for
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
, because this estimator does not provide a first-round estimate for
\delta
.

For real-valued dynamic Tobit models,
de Jong & Herrera (2011)
established consistency and asymptotic normality of CLADE based on
Powell (1984)
, and
Bykhovskaya (2023)
considered CLADE in a more complex case. But their analyses cannot be generalized here, because their techniques use a error density, which is absent for our model.
This problem deserves further study in future research.

Remark 4

In general, the minimization of absolute deviations in models with a positive part is a non-convex problem, and
designing numerical algorithms requires special care.
The method in
Bilias et al. (2019)
allows to find an exact computation of
\check{\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}
, i. e., the CLADE minimization problem (
4.2
) can be reformulated as the following mixed integer programming problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\vartheta$},(\gamma_{t},\phi_{t},sm_{t},sp_{t},t=1,\ldots,n)\}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(sm_{t}+sp_{t})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}

\displaystyle X_{t}-\phi_{t}+sm_{t}-sp_{t}=0

\displaystyle\phi_{t}\geq\max\{0,\ M_{t}\},

\displaystyle\phi_{t}\leq\min\big\{M_{t}+\omega_{t}(1-\gamma_{t}),\ \omega_{t}\gamma_{t}\big\},

\displaystyle sm_{t}\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002ptsp_{t}\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma_{t}\in\{0,1\},

where
t=1,\ldots,n
. Here,
\omega_{t}
is an arbitrary parameter that must be sufficiently large such that a solution to the system of
inequalities exists. In other words, the inequalities only represent the max-operator exactly if
\omega_{t}
is large enough, specifically
if
\omega_{t}>|M_{t}|
.
The auxiliary choice variables
sm_{t}
and
sp_{t}
are the under-achievement and over-achievement positive slack variables for the
CLAD model, and the positive variable
\phi_{t}
is equal to
\max\{0,M_{t}\}
. The optimization
is done with respect to the binary choice variables
\gamma_{t}
and the continuous choice variables
sm_{t}
,
sp_{t}
,
\phi_{t}
, and
\vartheta
.
See
Bilias et al. (2019)
for more details about the method.

For the STINARCH case (i. e., all
\beta_{j}
s in (
2.3
) are zero, so
M_{t}\ =\ \alpha_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}\,X_{t-i}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots
,
\alpha_{p})^{\top}
),
a possible alternative to (
4.2
) could be to use squared deviations instead of absolute deviations, thus leading to a conditional least squares (CLS) estimator.
The ordinary CLS estimator, however, obtained by minimizing
\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big(X_{t}-M_{t}\big)^{2}
over
\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}\in\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}^{\ast}
,
will not lead a consistent estimator, which can be seen from a simple example: If
p=q=0
with
M_{t}=\alpha_{0}
, then we have the estimator

\tilde{\alpha}_{0}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}/n
. Hence,
\tilde{\alpha}_{0}
converges to
E(X_{t})\geq 0
using the law of large numbers, but
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, so
\tilde{\alpha}_{0}
is inconsistent.
A similar discussion for other models can also be found in
Bykhovskaya (2023)
.
Therefore, we consider the following CLS estimator
\breve{\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}
:

\displaystyle\breve{\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}=\arg\min_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\vartheta$}\in\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\Theta$}^{\ast}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big(X_{t}-\max\{0,M_{t}\}\big)^{2}.

According to Proposition
1
, we know that

E(X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\neq M_{t}
or
\max\{0,M_{t}\}
, so the above CLS estimator is not the common one (where the conditional expectation is used to construct the objective function).
Note that
\max\{0,M_{t}\}\neq M_{t}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{t}>0\}}
, and the objective function is discontinuous with respect to the parameters. So we cannot obtain an explicit expression for the CLS estimator, the theoretic properties of which need to be further studied.
From the simulation experiment in Table
B.5
below, we find that the CLS estimator
\breve{\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}
shows a satisfactory finite-sample performance in practice.

5
Possible Extensions

First, we state some possible extensions based on the unbounded INGARCH modeling framework developed in Section
2
. In Section
3
, the Skellam distribution is used as the underlying conditional distribution for illustration, but other choices are possible as well. For examples, if stronger dispersion is required, one my use the generalized Poisson difference distribution discussed in
Carallo et al. (2024)
instead, which includes the Skellam distribution as a special case. In addition, unlike
Agosto et al. (2016)
where a non-negative link function is used for possible covariates, such covariates could be easily and naturally included in (
2.3
).

Second, the Tobit approach proposed in Section
2
is not only relevant for unbounded INGARCH models, it might also be used for different integer-valued time series models such as those from the INAR (integer-valued autoregressive) family. While the ordinary INAR models for count time series
(see
Weiß, 2018
, Chapters 2–3)
are well-defined only for positive parameter values and can, thus, only describe positive ACF values, also extensions to
\mathbb{Z}
-valued INAR-type models have been proposed in the literature. The first contribution appears to be the INARS
(p)
model (S=signed) by
Kim & Park (2008)
, which relies on a model recursion of the form

X_{t}\ =\ \alpha_{1}\odot X_{t-1}\ +\ldots+\ \alpha_{p}\odot X_{t-{p}}\ +\ \epsilon_{t},

(5.1)

where “
\odot
” denotes the signed binomial thinning operator, and the
(\epsilon_{t})
are i. i. d.
\mathbb{Z}
-valued innovations. “
\odot
” is defined by requiring the conditional distribution
\alpha\odot X\big|X
to be
\textup{sgn}(\alpha)\cdot\textup{sgn}(X)\cdot\textup{Bin}\big(|X|,|\alpha|\big)
, where
-1<\alpha<1
, where
X
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random variable, and where
\textup{sgn}(z)=1
for
z\geq 0
and
-1
otherwise. The special case of the INARS
(1)
model according to (
5.1
) has been investigated by
Andersson & Karlis (2014)
together with Skellam-distributed innovations, whereas
Alzaid & Omair (2014)
discuss a modification allowing for a Skellam marginal distribution.

Applying the Tobit approach of Section
2
to the INARS
(p)
model (
5.1
), we propose the
p
th-order
Tobit INAR model
defined by

X_{t}\ =\ \max\big\{0,\ X_{t}^{*}\big\},\hskip 20.00003ptX_{t}^{*}\ =\ \alpha_{1}\odot X_{t-1}\ +\ldots+\ \alpha_{p}\odot X_{t-{p}}\ +\ \epsilon_{t}.

(5.2)

Unlike the ordinary INAR
(p)
model for count time series
(
Alzaid & Al-Osh, 1990
;
Du & Li, 1991
, see)
, the AR-parameters
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p}\in(-1,1)
are allowed to take negative values (obviously, if all
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p}>0
, then (
5.2
) reduces to the ordinary INAR
(p)
recursion).
The observations
\{X_{t}\}
in (
5.2
) are counts, and
X_{t}^{*}
is allowed to take negative values only to capture the effect of negative AR-parameters. But as we are actually interested in count time series modeling, we assume the i. i. d. innovations
(\epsilon_{t})
to be counts as well.
A more detailed investigation of such Tobit INAR
(p)
models and their abilities to handle negative autocorrelations appears to be an interesting direction for future research.

Third, another possible extension refers to the case of bounded counts, i. e., where the range of
\{X_{t}\}
is bounded from above by some
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Both INAR- and INGARCH-type models have been proposed for such a case, see
McKenzie (1985)
;
Ristić et al. (2016)
as well as the survey in
Weiß (2018, Chapters 3–4)
. Here, the additional difficulty arises from the fact that the range
\{0,\ldots,N\}
is bounded from two sides. Thus, instead of using the ReLU function for constructing a Tobit model, it suggests itself to use a type of clipped ReLU (cReLU) function
(
Cai et al., 2017
, see)
instead. Let us focus on the BINGARCH case (B=bounded) for illustration.
Using again the recursive scheme (
2.3
) for the conditional mean
M_{t}=E(X_{t}^{*}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
, the
Tobit BINGARCH model
is defined by

X_{t}\ =\ \max\big\{0,\ \min\{N,\ X_{t}^{*}\}\big\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptX_{t}^{*}\ =\ \mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(M_{t}),

(5.3)

where
\mathcal{S}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nu$}}(\mu)
,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, again denotes a
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random operator. An analogous construction might be used for adapting the Tobit INAR model (
5.2
) to the case of bounded counts. Again, future research should study the properties and abilities of these models.

Also the third example serves to demonstrate the great flexibility of the Tobit approach. For this purpose, let us revisit one of the time series discussed by
Liu et al. (2022)
, namely the daily air quality level (Dec. 2013–July 2019, so length

s_{0}=\text{``excellent''}
to
s_{5}=\text{``severely polluted''}

Liu et al. (2022)
used a “rank-count approach” for modelling these data, i. e., the ordinal
Y_{t}
at time
t
is substituted by
s_{X_{t}}
with the count
X_{t}
having the upper bound
N=5
. For the sake of readability, the plot in Figure
4
shows only the first 365 observations, as well as the sample ACF
\hat{\rho}(k)
of the full data.
Liu et al. (2022)
fitted an (exactly linear) INGARCH
(1,1)
model to the data, referred to as “ZOBPAR
(1,1)
”, where the conditional distribution is a truncated Poisson distribution with additional one-inflation. Because of being exactly linear, the ZOBPAR’s model parameters are not allowed to become negative. However, looking at the sample ACF in Figure
4
, we observe a rather strong decline after lag 1, and this might be better explained if negative parameter values are permitted. Thus, we use the Skellam Tobit BINGARCH
(1,1)

5.3
) with

from Section

as a competitor, which we also equip with a further parameter

for capturing one-inflation:

P(X_{t}=x\ |\ X_{t-1},\ldots)\ =\ \kappa\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{1\}}(x)\ +\ (1-\kappa)\cdot\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}P\big(\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(M_{t})\leq 0\big)&\text{if }x=0,\\[2.15277pt]
P\big(\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(M_{t})=x\big)&\text{if }1\leq x\leq N-1,\\[2.15277pt]
P\big(\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(M_{t})\geq N\big)&\text{if }x=N.\end{array}\right.

When specifying
\delta
in our data applications, we routinely tried out candidate models with different levels
\delta
of linearity. In this case, we recognized that we can further improve upon our default choice

.

7
Conclusions

To give an alternative solution to the log and softplus approaches for achieving negative ACF values within the class of INGARCH models, the Tobit approach is proposed, and the Skellam–Tobit INGARCH model for unbounded counts is studied in detail. Several extensions of this approach to other situations are discussed as well. Three real-data examples are analyzed to illustrate the usefulness of the new approach.

There are some suggestions for possible future research. First, one could work out the TINGARCH model based on the generalized Poisson difference distribution (recall Sections
5
)
or other distributions. In particular, closed-form expressions for distribution and moments of the corresponding censored random variable
X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})
should be derived.
Second, more studies regarding the Tobit INAR models for unbounded or bounded counts mentioned in Sections
5
are needed.
Third, similar to the arguments in
Weiß et al. (2022)
, we can apply the new approach to many real datasets and find some causal relationship like
Chen et al. (2018)
, who investigated the relation between human influenza cases and air pollution.

Appendix A
Appendices

A.1
About INGARCH models

For INGARCH
(p,q)
models, the count
X_{t}
being generated at time
t
has the conditional mean
M_{t}
satisfying the linear recursive scheme (
1.1
) with
a_{0}>0
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{p},b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}\geq 0
. The default choice is a conditional Poisson distribution, i. e.,
X_{t}
, conditioned on
X_{t-1},\ldots
, is
\textup{Poi}(M_{t})
-distributed.
Using the linearity of the conditional mean, the unconditional mean equals
(
Ferland et al., 2006
)

\mu\ =\ \frac{a_{0}}{1-\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}},

(A.1)

and variance and autocovariances satisfy a set of Yule–Walker equations
(
Weiß, 2009
)
:

\begin{array}[]{@{}rl}\gamma(0)\ =&\mu\ +\ \gamma_{M}(0),\hskip 18.49988pt\gamma_{M}(0)\ =\ \sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}\,\gamma(i)\ +\ \sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}\,\gamma_{M}(j),\\[11.62495pt]
\gamma(k)\ =&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}\,\gamma(|k-i|)\ +\ \sum\limits_{j=1}^{\min{\{k-1,q\}}}b_{j}\,\gamma(k-j)\ +\ \sum\limits_{j=k}^{q}b_{j}\,\gamma_{M}(j-k),\\[11.62495pt]
\gamma_{M}(k)\ =&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\min{\{k,p\}}}a_{i}\,\gamma_{M}(|k-i|)\ +\ \sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{p}a_{i}\,\gamma(i-k)\ +\ \sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}\,\gamma_{M}(|k-j|),\\
\end{array}

(A.2)

for
k\geq 1
, where
\gamma(h):=Cov[X_{t},X_{t-h}]
and
\gamma_{M}(h):=Cov[M_{t},M_{t-h}]
.
Because of the conditional Poisson distribution, the conditional dispersion ratio
V(X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\big/E(X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=1
constantly (the unconditional distribution exhibits overdispersion, i. e.,
\sigma^{2}/\mu>1
). To allow for a more flexible dispersion structure, several extensions of the basic Poisson INGARCH model have been developed in the literature, see
Weiß (2018)
for a survey. For this article, especially the extension by
Xu et al. (2012)
is relevant, who defined the DINGARCH model by introducing an additional dispersion parameter
\eta\geq 1
into the conditional variance:

V(X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\ =\ \eta\,E(X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}).

(A.3)

So the Poisson INGARCH model corresponds to the choice
\eta=1
, while specific examples for
\eta>1
are given by the negative-binomial INGARCH model of
Xu et al. (2012)
and the generalized-Poisson INGARCH model of
Zhu (2012)
. As a result, the mean and ACF of the DINGARCH model are the same as for the Poisson INGARCH model, but the variance is inflated by the factor
\eta
.

A.2
Proof of Proposition
1

To derive (
3.9
), we set
f(x)=\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(x)
in (
3.8
) and obtain

\begin{array}[]{rl}E\big(X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})\big)\ =&\lambda_{1}\,E\big(\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*}+1)\big)\ -\ \lambda_{2}\,E\big(\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*}-1)\big)\\[3.87498pt]
=&\lambda_{1}\,P(X^{*}\geq 0)\ -\ \lambda_{2}\,P(X^{*}\geq 2)\\[3.87498pt]
=&\mu\,P(X^{*}\geq 0)\ +\ \lambda_{2}\,\big(P(X^{*}=0)+P(X^{*}=1)\big).\end{array}

Next, let us turn to the second-order partial moment (
3.10
).
In a first step, we apply (
3.8
) with
f(x)=x\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(x)
, leading to

\begin{array}[]{@{}l}E\big((X^{*})^{2}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})\big)\ =\ \lambda_{1}\,E\big((X^{*}+1)\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*}+1)\big)\ -\ \lambda_{2}\,E\big((X^{*}-1)\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*}-1)\big)\\[3.87498pt]
\hskip 9.24994pt=\ \lambda_{1}\,E\big(X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*}+1)\big)\ -\ \lambda_{2}\,E\big(X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*}-1)\big)\ +\ \lambda_{1}\,P(X^{*}\geq 0)\ +\ \lambda_{2}\,P(X^{*}\geq 2).\end{array}

In a second step, we apply (
3.8
) with
f(x)=\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(x+1)
or
f(x)=\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(x-1)
, respectively, leading to

\begin{array}[]{rl}E\big(X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*}+1)\big)\ =&\lambda_{1}\,P(X^{*}\geq-1)\ -\ \lambda_{2}\,P(X^{*}\geq 1)\\[1.93748pt]
\ =&\lambda_{1}\,P(X^{*}=-1)+\lambda_{1}\,P(X^{*}=0)\ +\ (\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\,P(X^{*}\geq 1)\\[1.93748pt]
\ \overset{\eqref{skellam2}}{=}&\lambda_{2}\,P(X^{*}=1)+\lambda_{1}\,P(X^{*}=0)\ +\ (\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\,P(X^{*}\geq 1),\\[3.87498pt]
E\big(X^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*}-1)\big)\ =&\lambda_{1}\,P(X^{*}\geq 1)\ -\ \lambda_{2}\,P(X^{*}\geq 3)\\[1.93748pt]
=&\lambda_{2}\,P(X^{*}=1)\ +\ \lambda_{2}\,P(X^{*}=2)\ +\ (\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\,P(X^{*}\geq 1)\\[1.93748pt]
\ \overset{\eqref{skellam3}}{=}&(\lambda_{2}-1)\,P(X^{*}=1)\ +\ \lambda_{1}\,P(X^{*}=0)\ +\ (\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\,P(X^{*}\geq 1).\end{array}

So altogether, we get

\begin{array}[]{@{}l}E\big((X^{*})^{2}\,\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X^{*})\big)\ =\ \lambda_{1}\,P(X^{*}\geq 0)\ +\ \lambda_{2}\,P(X^{*}\geq 2)\ +\ (\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})^{2}\,P(X^{*}\geq 1)\\[1.93748pt]
\hskip 18.49988pt+\ \lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\,P(X^{*}=1)+\lambda_{1}^{2}\,P(X^{*}=0)\ -\ \lambda_{2}\,(\lambda_{2}-1)\,P(X^{*}=1)-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\,P(X^{*}=0)\\[3.87498pt]
\hskip 9.24994pt=\ \lambda_{1}\,P(X^{*}\geq 0)\ +\ \lambda_{2}\,P(X^{*}\geq 1)\ +\ (\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})^{2}\,P(X^{*}\geq 1)\\[1.93748pt]
\hskip 18.49988pt+\ \lambda_{2}\,(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\,P(X^{*}=1)\ +\ \lambda_{1}\,(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\,P(X^{*}=0)\\[3.87498pt]
\hskip 9.24994pt=\ (\sigma^{2}+\mu^{2})\,P(X^{*}\geq 1)\ +\ \lambda_{2}\,\mu\,P(X^{*}=1)\ +\ \lambda_{1}\,(1+\mu)\,P(X^{*}=0).\end{array}

This completes the proof of Proposition
1
.

A.3
Proof of Theorem
1

We just need to show the existence and stationarity of the process
\{X_{t}^{\ast}\}
.

Carallo et al. (2024)
considered a process similar to
\{X_{t}^{\ast}\}
, see their equation (5), where they assume that
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{q}
are non-negative but
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.
The non-negativity restriction on the parameters is redundant, as it does not affect the proof of
Carallo et al. (2024)
.

For
M_{t}
defined in our (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle\textstyle E(X_{t}^{\ast}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=M_{t}\ =\ \alpha_{0}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}X_{t-i}^{\ast}\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}}(X_{t-i}^{\ast})+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}M_{t-j}.

Based on the sign of
X_{t-i}^{\ast}
,
i=1,\ldots,p
,
M_{t}
can have
2^{p}
different cases.
We consider the special case
M_{t}=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}X_{t-i}^{\ast}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}M_{t-j}
. Using arguments similar to those in Section 3.1 in
Carallo et al. (2024)
, we know that if
condition (
3.13
) is satisfied, then the existence and stationarity of the process
\{X_{t}^{\ast}\}
holds.
For remaining cases, where at least one of the summands in
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}X_{t-i}^{\ast}
vanishes, we can obtain similar conditions. But condition (
3.13
) is the strictest, so it is a sufficient condition for all cases.

A.4
Derivatives of Log-Likelihood Function (
4.1
)

In what follows, the derivatives of the log-likelihood function
L(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
according to (
4.1
) are discussed.
If
X_{t}>0
and
M_{t}>0
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \left(-1+\frac{X_{t}}{a_{t}}+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{a_{t}}}\,b_{t}\right)\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\delta}

\displaystyle\ =\ -1+\frac{X_{t}}{2a_{t}}-\frac{X_{t}}{2\delta}+\frac{M_{t}+\delta}{\sqrt{\delta a_{t}}}\,b_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*\top}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{1}{a_{t}}\left(-\frac{2X_{t}}{a_{t}}-\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{a}_{t}}\,b_{t}+\delta c_{t}\right)\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*\top}}+\left(-1+\frac{X_{t}}{a_{t}}+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{a_{t}}}\,b_{t}\right)\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*\top}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial^{2}\delta^{2}}

\displaystyle\ =\ -\frac{X_{t}}{2a_{t}^{2}}+\frac{X_{t}}{2\delta^{2}}-\frac{M_{t}^{2}}{(\delta a_{t})^{3/2}}\,b_{t}+\frac{(M_{t}+\delta)^{2}}{\delta a_{t}}\,c_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}\partial\delta}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{1}{a_{t}}\left(-\frac{X_{t}}{a_{t}}+\frac{M_{t}}{\sqrt{\delta a_{t}}}\,b_{t}+(M_{t}+\delta)\,c_{t}\right)\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}},

where

a_{t}\ =\ 2M_{t}+\delta,\hskip 18.49988ptb_{t}\ =\ \frac{I_{X_{t}}^{\prime}}{I_{X_{t}}},\hskip 18.49988ptc_{t}\ =\ \frac{I_{X_{t}}^{\prime\prime}I_{X_{t}}-(I_{X_{t}}^{\prime})^{2}}{I_{X_{t}}^{2}}.

Here, the functions
I_{X_{t}},I_{X_{t}}^{\prime}
and
I_{X_{t}}^{\prime\prime}
omit their argument
\sqrt{\delta\,a_{t}}
for brevity.
From (
3.4
), we can calculate the derivatives required for
b_{t}
and
c_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle I_{X_{t}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{X_{t}}{\sqrt{\delta a_{t}}}\,I_{X_{t}}+I_{X_{t}+1},

\displaystyle I_{X_{t}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{1}{2}(I_{X_{t}-1}^{\prime}+I_{X_{t}+1}^{\prime})\ =\ I_{X_{t}}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\delta a_{t}}}\,\Big[(X_{t}-1)\,I_{X_{t}-1}-(X_{t}+1)\,I_{X_{t}+1}\Big].

For
i,i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,p,j,j^{\prime}=1,\ldots,q
and
i\neq i^{\prime},j\neq j^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\alpha_{0}}

\displaystyle\ =\ 1+\sum_{l=1}^{q}\beta_{l}\,\frac{\partial M_{t-l}}{\partial\alpha_{0}},\hskip 9.24994pt\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\alpha_{i}}\ =\ X_{t-i}+\sum_{l=1}^{q}\beta_{l}\,\frac{\partial M_{t-l}}{\partial\alpha_{i}},\hskip 9.24994pt\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\beta_{j}}\ =\ M_{t-j}+\sum_{l=1}^{q}\beta_{l}\,\frac{\partial M_{t-l}}{\partial\beta_{j}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t}}{\partial\alpha_{0}^{2}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum_{l=1}^{q}\beta_{l}\,\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t-l}}{\partial\alpha_{0}^{2}},\hskip 18.49988pt\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t}}{\partial\alpha_{i}^{2}}\ =\ \sum_{l=1}^{q}\beta_{l}\,\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t-l}}{\partial\alpha_{i}^{2}},\hskip 18.49988pt\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t}}{\partial\alpha_{0}\partial\alpha_{i}}\ =\ \sum_{l=1}^{q}\beta_{l}\,\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t-l}}{\partial\alpha_{0}\partial\alpha_{i}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t}}{\partial\alpha_{i}\partial\alpha_{i^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum_{l=1}^{q}\beta_{l}\,\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t-l}}{\partial\alpha_{i}\partial\alpha_{i^{\prime}}},\hskip 18.49988pt\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t}}{\partial\alpha_{0}\partial\beta_{j}}\ =\ \frac{\partial M_{t-j}}{\partial\alpha_{0}}+\sum_{l=1}^{q}\beta_{l}\,\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t-l}}{\partial\alpha_{0}\partial\beta_{j}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t}}{\partial\alpha_{i}\partial\beta_{j}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\partial M_{t-j}}{\partial\alpha_{i}}+\sum_{l=1}^{q}\beta_{l}\,\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t-l}}{\partial\alpha_{i}\partial\beta_{j}},\hskip 18.49988pt\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t}}{\partial\beta_{j}^{2}}\ =\ 2\,\frac{\partial M_{t-j}}{\partial\beta_{j}}+\sum_{l=1}^{q}\beta_{l}\,\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t-l}}{\partial\beta_{j}^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t}}{\partial\beta_{j}\partial\beta_{j^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\partial M_{t-j}}{\partial\beta_{j^{\prime}}}+\frac{\partial M_{t-j^{\prime}}}{\partial\beta_{j}}+\sum_{l=1}^{q}\beta_{l}\,\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t-l}}{\partial\beta_{j}\partial\beta_{j^{\prime}}}.

Similarly, with an abuse of notations, if
X_{t}>0
and
M_{t}<0
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \left(1+\frac{X_{t}}{a_{t}}-\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{a_{t}}}\,b_{t}\right)\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\delta}

\displaystyle\ =\ -1-\frac{X_{t}}{2a_{t}}+\frac{X_{t}}{2\delta}-\frac{M_{t}-\delta}{\sqrt{\delta a_{t}}}\,b_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*\top}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{1}{a_{t}}\left(\frac{2X_{t}}{a_{t}}+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{a}_{t}}\,b_{t}+\delta c_{t}\right)\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}}\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*\top}}\ +\ \left(1+\frac{X_{t}}{a_{t}}-\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{a_{t}}}\,b_{t}\right)\frac{\partial^{2}M_{t}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*\top}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial^{2}\delta^{2}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{X_{t}}{2a_{t}^{2}}-\frac{X_{t}}{2\delta^{2}}-\frac{M_{t}^{2}}{(\delta a_{t})^{3/2}}\,b_{t}+\frac{(M_{t}-\delta)^{2}}{\delta a_{t}}\,c_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}\partial\delta}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{1}{a_{t}}\left(-\frac{X_{t}}{a_{t}}+\frac{M_{t}}{\sqrt{\delta a_{t}}}\,b_{t}+(M_{t}-\delta)\,c_{t}\right)\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}}.

Now,
a_{t}=-2M_{t}+\delta
, and
b_{t}
and
c_{t}
are defined similarly as before. Here, the omitted variable in the functions
I_{X_{t}},I_{X_{t}}^{\prime}
and
I_{X_{t}}^{\prime\prime}
in
b_{t}
and
c_{t}
is
\sqrt{\delta\,a_{t}}
.

If
X_{t}=0
, then the expression for
l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
involves the sum of the conditional probabilities, but each term has been discussed above.
