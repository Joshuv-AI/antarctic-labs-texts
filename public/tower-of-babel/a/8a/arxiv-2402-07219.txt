The dependence of local regularity of solutions on the summability of coefficients and nonhomogenous term Thanks: The research is supported by NSFC (11301211) and NSF of Jilin Prov. (201500520056JH).
Zheng Li, Bin Guo

Abstract




Abstract:

In this paper, we mainly discuss the local regularity of the solution to the following problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\dive({\bf{A}}(x)\nabla u(x))=f(x),&~x\in\Omega,\\
u(x)=0,&~x\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega
is a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, we are concerned with the connection between the regularity of the solution
u
and the integrability of the coefficient matrix
{\bf{A}}(x)
as well as the nonhomogeneous term
f
. To be more precise, our first result is to prove that the maximum norm of
u
can be controlled by
\|f\|_{s}
with
f\in L^{s}(\Omega),~s>\frac{nq}{2q-n},~q>\frac{n}{2}
.
Meanwhile, we construct some counterexamples to illustrate the index
\frac{nq}{2q-n}
being sharp.
Subsequently, we give an improved upper bound for the maximum norm of
u
. Namely, there exists a positive constant
C
such that

\|u\|_{\infty}\leq C\|f\|_{\frac{nq}{2q-n}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\|f\|_{s}}{~~~~~\|f\|_{\frac{nq}{2q-n}}}+1\right)+1\right].

Specially, the main difference of our approach compared to the arguments of [
References
,
References
] is to construct two classes of truncation functions to remove the assumption of the boundedness of
u
.
Finally, based on the previous results and Moser iteration argument, we derive the Harnack inequality of
u
from which the Hölder continuity of the solution follows. In addition,
we also find that the Lebesgue space
L^{\frac{n}{2}}(\Omega)
to which the inverse of the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda(x)
of the matrix
A
(x) belongs is essentially sharp
in order to establish local boundedness and the Hölder continuity of the solution.

Keywords:
Nonuniformly elliptic equations; Bounded solutions; Harnack inequality

Introduction

In this article, we consider the following second order elliptic equation in divergence form

-\dive({\bf{A}}(x)\nabla u)=f,~~x\in\Omega,

(1.1)

where
\Omega
is a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
.
In order to measure ellipticity of
{\bf{A}}(x)
, the coefficient matrix
{\bf{A}}(x)
satisfies the following assumptions:

(H1)
{\bf{A}}(x):~\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a measurable matrix whose entries
a_{ij}(x)
satisfy

a_{ij}(x)=a_{ji}(x),~~~1\leq i,j\leq n;

(H2) There exist
\lambda(x)
and
\mu(x)
such that

\lambda(x)|\xi|^{2}\leq{\bf{A}}(x)\cdot\xi\cdot\xi\leq\mu(x)|\xi|^{2},~~~\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\lambda(x)
and
\mu(x)
are nonnegative measurable functions and satisfy

\lambda^{-1}(x)\in L^{q}(\Omega),~~\mu(x)\in L^{p}(\Omega),

with
p,~q>1
and
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}<\frac{2}{n-1}
.

When the equation (
1.1
) is uniformly and strictly elliptic, i.e.,
\lambda^{-1}(x)
and
\mu(x)
are essentially bounded, it is well known that weak solutions are Hölder continuous. Roughly speaking,
for the planar case (
n=2
), the corresponding study dates back to the work of Morrey [
References
].
For more works on the best Hölder continuity exponent, the interested readers may refer to the work of Wildman [
References
] and Piccinini and Spagnolo [
References
].
For the higher dimensions (
n\geq 3
), Hölder continuity of solutions was established in the late 1950’s by De Giorgi [
References
] and Nash [
References
].
Hölder continuity also follows from the Harnack inequality, as demonstrated by Moser [
References
,
References
].
In particular, Trudinger [
References
] obtained that
for any
f\in L^{s}(\Omega)
with
s>\frac{n}{2}
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{{\infty}}\leqslant C\|f\|_{{s}},

(1.2)

for any nonnegative weak solution
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
of (
1.1
).
This proof relies on Moser iteration and the Sobolev inequality.
Different from the method used in [
References
], Talenti [
References
] applied the rearrangement method to obtain the same results and gave the explicit expression of the constant “
C
”. After these pioneer works, there are many research activities regarding global regularity theory for linear or nonlinear elliptic equations [
References
,
References
,
References
,
References
,
References
,
References
] and references therein.

On the contrary, if
\lambda^{-1}(x)
is unbounded, then the equation (
1.1
) is degenerate;
If
\mu(x)
is unbounded, then the equation (
1.1
) is singular.
Specially, Murthy and Stampacchia [
References
], Trudinger [
References
] proved that weak solutions to (
1.1
) are locally bounded and satisfy the Harnack inequality under the assumptions that
\lambda^{-1}(x)\in L^{q}(\Omega)
and
\mu(x)\in L^{p}(\Omega)
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}<\frac{2}{n}
. It’s worth noting that their key point is to obtain the following Sobolev inequality

\displaystyle(\int_{\Omega}|u|^{\gamma}\mu(x){\rm d}x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}\leqslant C\big(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\lambda(x){\rm d}x\big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(1.3)

for any
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
\gamma>2
.
Furthermore, Bella and Schäffner [
References
] also obtained the same results under the optimal condition
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}<\frac{2}{n-1},n\geqslant 2

(refer to [
References
,
References
,
References
] for a recent generalization to scalar autonomous integral functionals with
\left(p,q\right)
-growth). Compared to the arguments given in [
References
,
References
], the key observation of
[
References
] is to give the optimal Caccioppoli inequality with respect to cut-off function by using the Sobolev inequality in the
n-1
-dimensional spheres rather than the
n
-dimensional balls.
In addition, Fabes, Kenig and Serapioni [
References
] gave some stronger conditions on the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
\textbf{A}(x)
, i.e., Muckenhoupt class
A_{2}
.
These conditions will guarantee the validity of the following scale invariant Sobolev and Poincaré inequalities like

\displaystyle\big(\int_{B}|u-u_{B}|^{\gamma}\lambda{\rm d}x\big)^{\frac{2}{\gamma}}\leqslant Cr^{2}\big(\int_{B}\lambda^{-1}{\rm d}x\big)^{\frac{2}{\gamma}-1}\int_{B}|\nabla u|^{2}\lambda{\rm d}x,

(1.4)

for any
u\in H^{1}(B)
with
B=B(y,r)
and
\gamma>2
,
u_{B}:=\fint_{B}u{\rm d}x=\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}u{\rm d}x
. For more related results, we may refer to [
References
,
References
,
References
].

Recently, for the Dirichlet problem of (
1.1
), Xu [
References
] improved (
1.2
) in the uniformly elliptic case.
Using Moser iteration technique, he obtained an upper bound in the logarithmic form of
\|f\|_{s}
. Subsequently, Cruz-Uribe and Rodney [
References
] extended Xu’s result to the degenerate elliptic equations with
f\in L^{A}(\Omega)
, where
A(t)=t^{\sigma^{\prime}}\log\left(e+t\right)^{q},~\left(q>\sigma^{\prime}>0\right)
.

Motivated by mentioned works, we plan to investigate the relationship between the local regularity of solutions and the integrability of the minimum eigenvalue and the maximum eigenvalue of the coefficient matrix
A
(x) as well as
f
. To the best of our knowledge,
it is widely recognized that the smoothness of the solutions depends on the smoothness of the coefficients matrix and the data. For instance, for the absence of the term
f
, Zhong [
References
] constructed discontinuous solutions for degenerate equations to give partial answer to De Giorgi’s conjectures, which were raised by De Giorgi [
References
] in a talk in Lecce, 1995. In this direction, as far as we know, the classical results are established by Trudinger [
References
] under the assumption that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}<\frac{2}{n}
. Later, Bella and Schäffner [
References
] discussed the same problem under the optimal condition
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}<\frac{2}{n-1}
. However, for the present of the term
f
, such problem is seldom discussed. Naturally, some problems arise:

\bullet
Whether does the inequality similar as (
1.2
) hold for nonuniform elliptic problem?

\bullet
What is a critical space of the nonhomogeneous term
f
in the Lebesgue class
L^{s}(\Omega)
?

\bullet
Whether or not is the Lebesgue space
L^{\frac{n}{2}}(\Omega)
to which
\lambda^{-1}(x)
belongs is sharp to ensure the Hölder continuity of the solution?

In this paper, we gave full answers to three problems mentioned above.
Here, we are interested in situations beyond any smoothness where
a_{ij}(x)
are assumed to be merely measurable functions satisfying conditions (H1), (H2),
and
f
is any function from some Lebesgue space
L^{s}
.
Our first finding is as follows: when
q>\frac{n}{2}
,
n\geq 2
, we prove that the maximum of the weak solution
u
of (
1.1
) can be controlled by
\|f\|_{s}
, where
s>\frac{nq}{2q-n}
.
Meanwhile, we can provide a counterexample to illustrate that
\frac{nq}{2q-n}
is a critical exponent.
More precisely, when
q=\frac{n}{2},~n\geq 2
, we observe that for any
s\in[1,+\infty)
, there exists
f\in L^{s}(\Omega)
such that the weak solution
u
of (
1.1
) is unbounded. In this case, for
n\geq 3
, even when
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, there exists the solution satisfying
\sup\limits_{\Omega}u=+\infty
.
In addition, for the case of
q\in\left(\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{n}{2}\right),~n\geq 3
, we also find there exists
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\sup\limits_{\Omega}u=+\infty
. On the contrary, when
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}\geq\frac{2}{n-1},~n\geq 3
, a counterexample is provided in [
References
,
References
], demonstrating that when
f=0
, the weak solution
u
of (
1.1
) satisfies
\sup\limits_{\Omega}u=+\infty
.
Finally, in the scenario where
n=2,~\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{p}=\frac{2}{n-1}
, i.e.,
p=q=1
, Bella and Schäffner [
References
] elucidated, using the maximum principle and Sobolev inequality in one dimension, that the weak solution
u
is locally bounded when
f=0
.
However, we demonstrate that when
f\not\equiv 0
, for any
s\in[1,+\infty)
, there is a counterexample which shows that (
1.2
) is impossible.

Next we will state our main results. The first result provides an estimate of the local maximum of
|u|
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold, and
f\in L^{s}(\Omega)
for any
s>\frac{nq}{2q-n}~(q>\frac{n}{2})
. Then for any
\gamma>0
, there exists
C=C(n,p,q,\gamma)\in[1,+\infty)
such that for any ball
B_{R}\Subset\Omega,~R>0
, the solution
u
of (
1.1
) satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{\theta R}}|u|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\Lambda(B_{R})\right)^{\frac{\delta p^{\prime}}{\gamma(\delta-1)}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\frac{1}{\left((1-\theta)R\right)^{\frac{n}{\gamma}(m_{*}+1)}}\|u\|_{L^{\gamma}(B_{R})}+\frac{1}{\left((1-\theta)R\right)^{\frac{n}{\gamma}m_{*}+\frac{n}{s}-2}}\|f\|_{L^{s}(B_{R})}\right),

(1.5)

where

\displaystyle\theta\in(0,1),~

\displaystyle~~\delta=2-\frac{1}{\chi},~~~\chi:=\frac{2q}{(q+1)p^{*}},~~~\frac{1}{p^{*}}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{n-1}-\frac{1}{2p},1\right\},

\displaystyle m_{*}=\frac{p^{\prime}\delta}{\delta-1}\max\left\{\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q},\frac{2}{q}\right\},~~~p^{\prime}=\frac{p}{p-1},

\displaystyle\Lambda(B_{R})

\displaystyle:=\left(\fint_{B_{R}}\lambda^{-q}{\rm d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\left(\fint_{B_{R}}\mu^{p}{\rm d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}+\left(\fint_{B_{R}}\lambda^{-q}{\rm d}x\right)^{\frac{2}{q}}.

Remark 1.1
.

We note that
\frac{nq}{2q-n}\rightarrow\frac{n}{2}
as
q\rightarrow+\infty
, which is consistent with the conclusion of the uniformly elliptic equations.

Remark 1.2
.

As a byproduct, the corresponding global results would be proceeding as in the proof of [
References
, Theorem 4.1].

Remark 1.3
.

From the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
, we found that the integrability of
f
depends not only on dimension
n
, but also on the integrability of
\lambda^{-1}
. So, a natural question arises:
“
What is a critical exponent of the nonhomogeneous term in the class
L^{s}(\Omega)
?
”

Before giving some answers to this problem, we first consider the special case
{\bf{A}}(x)={\bf{I}}
. Under such case, (
1.1
) reduces the Poisson equation. We first extend
f
to
\tilde{f}
, where

\displaystyle\tilde{f}=\begin{cases}f,&x\in B_{1}=\{x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n};~~|x|=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}}<1\},\\
0,&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}.\end{cases}

Then, formally, the solution may be written as the following

\displaystyle u(x)=C(n)\int_{R^{n}}\tilde{f}(y)|x-y|^{2-n}{\rm d}y,~x\in B_{1}.

A simple computation shows the singular integral

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|}\right)^{\frac{s(n-2)}{s-1}}{\rm d}y,~x\in B_{1}

converges if and only if
s>\frac{n}{2}
. Further, the Hölder’s inequality implies

\displaystyle u(x)\leq C(n)\|f\|_{L^{s}(B_{1})}\left(\int_{B_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|}\right)^{\frac{s(n-2)}{s-1}}{\rm d}y\right)^{1-\frac{1}{s}}.

The formal derivation sheds light on the fact that
s>\frac{n}{2}
is the sufficient condition for the boundedness of
|u|
. Of course, we also obtain similar analytic conclusion from scaling viewpoint. Indeed,
we set
u_{\lambda}(x)=u(\lambda x)
and
f_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda^{2}f(\lambda x)
for any
0<\lambda\ll 1
.
It is easy to verify that
\Delta u_{\lambda}=f_{\lambda}(x)
and
\|f_{\lambda}(x)\|_{L^{s}(B_{1})}=\lambda^{2-\frac{n}{s}}\|f\|_{L^{s}(B_{\lambda})}
.
It is also easy to verify that the necessary condition for (
1.2
) is
s>\frac{n}{2}
by rescaling technique.
Therefore, it is well known that
s>\frac{n}{2}
is the sufficient and necessary condition for (
1.2
).
The argument above is similar in spirit, though not in detail, to the examples in [
References
,
References
].
This leads to a natural question: is the exponent
\frac{nq}{2q-n}
in Theorem
1.1

Suppose that (H1)-(H2) hold,

and

Let
u\in H^{1}\left(\Omega,{\bf{A}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{C\Lambda(B_{1})^{\frac{1}{2}}\alpha_{0}^{2}}{\left(\tau-\theta\right)\left(1-\frac{2\alpha_{0}}{\delta}\right)}\right)^{\frac{\delta^{m}-1}{\left(\delta-1\right)\alpha_{0}}}\left(\frac{2^{\frac{n}{n-1}}}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\left(\frac{\left(m+1\right)}{\delta-1}\delta^{m+1}-\frac{\delta^{m+1}-1}{\left(\delta-1\right)^{2}}\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left\|\left(u+h\right)^{\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\delta^{m}}}\right\|^{\frac{\delta^{m}}{\alpha_{0}}}_{W^{1,p_{*}}\left(B_{\rho_{m}}\right)}.

(5.24)

Subsequently, we fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
\frac{2p\alpha_{0}}{\delta^{m}\left(p-1\right)}\leq p_{0}\leq\frac{2p\alpha_{0}}{\delta^{m-1}\left(p-1\right)}
, and apply Hölder’s inequality to (
5
) to estimate the right-hand of (
5
) as follows

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\left(u+h\right)^{\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\delta^{m}}}\right\|_{L^{p_{*}}\left(B_{\rho_{m}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\nabla\left(u+h\right)^{\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\delta^{m}}}\right\|_{L^{\frac{2q}{q+1}}\left(B_{\frac{\tau+\theta}{2}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{5}\left\|\left(u+h\right)^{\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\delta^{m}}}\right\|_{L^{\frac{2p}{p-1}}\left(B_{\frac{3\tau+\theta}{4}}\right)}\leq C_{6}\left\|u+h\right\|^{\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\delta^{m}}}_{L^{p_{0}}\left(\frac{3\tau+\theta}{4}\right)},

where
C_{5},C_{6}
depend on
\tau,\theta,\alpha_{0},\Lambda(B_{1}),n,p,q,s
. Further, inserting the above inequality into (
5
), one has

\displaystyle\left\|\left(u+h\right)^{\alpha_{0}}\right\|^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}}_{W^{1,p^{*}}(B_{\rho_{0}})}\leq C\Lambda(B_{1})^{\frac{p\delta}{\left(p-1\right)p_{0}\left(\delta-1\right)}-\frac{1}{2\alpha_{0}\left(\delta-1\right)}}\left\|u+h\right\|_{L^{p_{0}}\left(B_{\frac{3\tau+\theta}{4}}\right)}.

(5.25)

Once again, by Sobolev inequality, (
5
) and (
5.23
), we find

\displaystyle\left\|\left(u+h\right)^{\alpha_{0}}\right\|_{L^{q_{*}}\left(B_{\theta}\right)}\leq C\left\|\left(u+h\right)^{\alpha_{0}}\right\|_{W^{1,\frac{2q}{q+1}}\left(B_{\theta}\right)}\leq C\left\|\left(u+h\right)^{\alpha_{0}}\right\|_{W^{1,p_{*}}\left(B_{\frac{\tau+3\theta}{4}}\right)},

(5.26)

where
C=C(\tau,\theta,n,\Lambda(B_{1}),q,p,\alpha_{0})
.
Then a combination of (
5.25
) and (
5.26
) yields the desired claim (
5.20
).

This completes the proof of Lemma
5.1
.

In this section we first construct Example

Proof of Example
1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be the ball
B=B_{\frac{1}{4}}(0)
. Define

\displaystyle{\bf{A}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}(x)&0&\cdots&0\\
0&a_{1}(x)&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&a_{1}(x)\end{pmatrix},~a_{1}(x)=|x|^{\beta}\left(\log\frac{1}{|x|}\right)^{\theta},

and

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=(n+1)|x|^{\beta-1}\left(\log\frac{1}{|x|}\right)^{\theta}\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-2}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\quad-\theta|x|^{\beta-1}\left(\log\frac{1}{|x|}\right)^{\theta-1}\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-2}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\quad-2|x|^{\beta}\left(\log\frac{1}{|x|}\right)^{\theta}\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-3}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y,

where
\beta=\frac{n}{q}<2
,

It is easy to observe that

\displaystyle u(x)=\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y

is the weak solution of (
1.1
) and
u(0)=+\infty
. To make the argument rigorous, we exploit some technique from [
References
] to show that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\bf{A}}(x)\nabla u\nabla\varphi{\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}f\varphi{\rm d}x,~~\forall\varphi\geq 0\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega).

For each
k\geq 2
, let
\chi_{k}
be a
C^{\infty}
, non-negative and radial function satisfying

\displaystyle\chi_{k}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&|x|\leq 2^{-k-2},\\
1,&2^{-k-1}\leq|x|<\frac{1}{4}.\end{cases}

Define
f_{k}=\chi_{k}f
. Notice that each
f_{k}
is continuous, and

u_{k}=\chi_{k}\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y\in C^{2}(\Omega)

is the solution of
-\dive\left({\bf{A}}(x)\nabla u_{k}\right)=f_{k}
, in the weak sense,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\bf{A}}(x)\nabla u_{k}\nabla\varphi{\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}f_{k}\varphi{\rm d}x,~~\forall\varphi\geq 0\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega).

The strong convergence
f_{k}\rightarrow f
in
L^{s}(\Omega)
implies

\int_{\Omega}{\bf{A}}(x)\nabla u_{k}\nabla\varphi{\rm d}x\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}{\bf{A}}(x)\nabla u\nabla\varphi{\rm d}x,~~k\rightarrow\infty.

Next, we will verify that
\lambda^{-1}(x)\in L^{q}(\Omega)
.
Indeed, by using a polar coordinate transformation, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)}\left|\frac{1}{\lambda(x)}\right|^{q}{\rm d}x=

\displaystyle\;\alpha_{n}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|\frac{1}{r^{\beta}\left(\log\frac{1}{r}\right)^{\theta}}\right|^{q}r^{n-1}{\rm d}r=\alpha_{n}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\left(\log\frac{1}{r}\right)^{\theta q}}r^{n-1-\beta q}{\rm d}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\alpha_{n}\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\left(\log t\right)^{\theta q}}\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)^{n+1-\beta q}{\rm d}t=\alpha_{n}\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\left(\log t\right)^{\theta q}}\frac{1}{t}{\rm d}t<+\infty,

where
\alpha_{n}
is the surface area of an
n
-dimensional sphere.

To complete the proof of Example
1.1
, we will demonstrate that
f\in L^{\frac{nq}{2q-n}}(\Omega)
when
n\geq 3
. It is sufficient to demonstrate one term of
f
that belongs to
L^{\frac{nq}{2q-n}}(\Omega)
, the remaining terms can be handled in a similar manner.
For example, we will show that

\displaystyle|x|^{\beta}\left(\log\frac{1}{|x|}\right)^{\theta}\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-3}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y\in L^{\frac{nq}{2q-n}}\left(B_{\frac{1}{4}}(0)\right).

In fact, we know

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\frac{1}{4}}(0)}\left||x|^{\beta}\left(\log\frac{1}{|x|}\right)^{\theta}\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-3}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y\right|^{\frac{nq}{2q-n}}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\frac{1}{4}}(0)}|x|^{\frac{\beta nq}{2q-n}}\left(\log\frac{1}{|x|}\right)^{\frac{\theta nq}{2q-n}}\left|\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-3}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y\right|^{\frac{nq}{2q-n}}{\rm d}x.

(6.1)

Then, we will estimate

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-3}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y.

(6.2)

We decompose (
6.2
) into two terms

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-3}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)\cap\{|y|<|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}\}}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-3}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)\cap\{|y|\geq|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}\}}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-3}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}.

By using a polar coordinate transformation, we find
I_{1}
may be estimated as follows

\displaystyle I_{1}=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)\cap\{|y|<|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}\}}|y|^{1-n}\left(|x|+|y|\right)^{-3}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{|y|}}{\rm d}y\leq\alpha_{n}\int_{0}^{|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left(|x|+r\right)^{-3}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{r}}{\rm d}r

(6.3)

Similarly, for
I_{2}

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\;\alpha_{n}\int_{|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(|x|+r\right)^{-3}\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{r}}{\rm d}r\leq\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\log 2}\left(|x|+|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{-3}\int_{|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\rm d}r

\displaystyle\;\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\log 2}\left(|x|+|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{-3}\left(\frac{1}{2}-|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\log 2}|x|^{-\frac{3}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}-|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)=\frac{\alpha_{n}}{2\log 2}|x|^{-\frac{3}{2}}.

(6.4)

By using (

\displaystyle\;c\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{4}}r^{\frac{(\beta-2)nq}{2q-n}+n-1}\left(\log\frac{1}{r}\right)^{\frac{nq(\theta-1)}{2q-n}}{\rm d}r+c\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{4}}r^{\frac{(\beta-\frac{3}{2})nq}{2q-n}+n-1}\left(\log\frac{1}{r}\right)^{\frac{nq\theta}{2q-n}}{\rm d}r
