Hardness of linearly ordered 4-colouring of 3-colourable 3-uniform hypergraphs
Marek Filakovský, Tamio-Vesa Nakajima, Jakub Opršal, Gianluca Tasinato, Uli Wagner

Abstract
A linearly ordered (LO) k k -colouring of a hypergraph is a colouring of its vertices with colours 1 , … , k 1,\dots,k such that each edge contains a unique maximal colour. Deciding whether an input hypergraph admits LO k k -colouring with a fixed number of colours is NP-complete (and in the special case of graphs, LO colouring coincides with the usual graph colouring). Here, we investigate the complexity of approximating the ‘linearly ordered chromatic number’ of a hypergraph. We prove that the following promise problem is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -complete: Given a 3-uniform hypergraph, distinguish between the case that it is LO 3 3 -colourable, and the case that it is not even LO 4 4 -colourable. We prove this result by a combination of algebraic, topological, and combinatorial methods, building on and extending a topological approach for studying approximate graph colouring introduced by Krokhin, Opršal, Wrochna, and Živný (2023).

Given an input graph

More generally, it is a long-standing conjecture that finding a

, Guruswami and Sandeep

Given the slow progress on approximate graph colouring, we believe there is substantial value in developing and extending the available methods for studying this problem and related questions, and we hope that the present paper contributes to this effort. As our main result (Theorem

Graph colouring is a special case of the
constraint satisfaction problem
(

k
-colouring of a graph

[
8

and Zhuk
[
32

, which asserts that for every finite relational structure

\mathsf{NP}
-complete, or solvable in polynomial time.

The framework of CSPs can be extended to

(

[
1
]
, and the general theory of these problems was further developed by Brakensiek and Guruswami
[
5
]
, and by Barto, Bulín, Krokhin, and Opršal
[
3

. Formally, a PCSP is parametrised by two relational structures
\mathbf{A}
and

such that there exists a homomorphism
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{B}
. Given an input structure
\mathbf{X}
, the goal is then to distinguish between the case that there is a homomorphism
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{A}
, and the case that there does not even exist a homomorphism
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{B}
(these cases are distinct but not necessarily complementary, and no output is required in case neither holds); we denote this decision problem by
\PCSP(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
. For example,
\PCSP(K_{3},K_{k})
is the problem of distinguishing, given an input graph

G
is
3
-colourable and the case that
G
is not
k
-colourable. This is the
decision version
of the approximate graph colouring problem whose

we introduced above. We remark that the decision problem reduces to the search version, hence hardness of the former implies hardness of the latter.

The proof of Theorem

In a nutshell, topology enters in the first part of the proof as follows. First, with every homomorphism

\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}

.

2.1
Promise CSPs

We start by recalling some fundamental notions from the theory of promise constraint satisfaction problems, following the presentation of
[
3
]
and
[
22
]
.

A
relational structure
is a tuple
{\mathbf{A}}=(A;R_{1}^{\mathbf{A}},\ldots,R_{k}^{\mathbf{A}})
, where
A
is a set, and
R_{i}^{\mathbf{A}}\subseteq A^{\ar(R_{i})}
is a relation of arity
\ar(R_{i})
. The
signature
of
{\mathbf{A}}
is the tuple
(\ar(R_{1}),\ldots,\ar(R_{k}))
.
For two relational structures
{\mathbf{A}}=(A;R_{1}^{\mathbf{A}},\ldots,R_{k}^{\mathbf{A}})
and
{\mathbf{B}}=(B;R_{1}^{\mathbf{B}},\ldots,R_{k}^{\mathbf{B}})
with the same signature, a
homomorphism
from
{\mathbf{A}}
to
{\mathbf{B}}
, denoted
h\colon{\mathbf{A}}\to{\mathbf{B}}
, is a function
h\colon A\to B
that preserves all relations, i.e., such that
h(a)\in R_{i}^{\mathbf{B}}
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and
a\in R_{i}^{\mathbf{A}}
where
h(a)
denotes the componentwise application of
h
on the elements of
a
. To express the existence of such a homomorphism, we will also use the notation
{\mathbf{A}}\to{\mathbf{B}}
.
The set of all homomorphisms from
{\mathbf{A}}
to
{\mathbf{B}}
is denoted by
\hom({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
.

Our focus is on structures with a single ternary relation
R
, i.e., pairs
(A;R^{\mathbf{A}})
with
R^{\mathbf{A}}\subseteq A^{3}
. Moreover, most structures in this paper have a
symmetric
relation, i.e., the relation
R^{\mathbf{A}}
is invariant under permuting coordinates. Such structures can be also viewed as
3
-uniform hypergraphs, keeping in mind that edges of the form
(a,a,b)
are allowed.

Definition 2

(Promise CSP)
.

Fix two relational structures such that
{\mathbf{A}}\to{\mathbf{B}}
. The
promise CSP
with template
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
, denoted by
\PCSP({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
, is a computational problem that has two versions:

•

In the
search
version of the problem, we are given a relational structure
{\mathbf{X}}
with the same signature as
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
, we are promised that
{\mathbf{X}}\to{\mathbf{A}}
, and we are tasked with finding a homomorphism
h\colon{\mathbf{X}}\to{\mathbf{B}}
.

•

In the
decision
version of the problem, we are given a relational structure
{\mathbf{X}}
, and we must answer
Yes
if
{\mathbf{X}}\to{\mathbf{A}}
, and
No
if
{\mathbf{X}}\not\to{\mathbf{B}}
. (These cases are mutually exclusive since
{\mathbf{A}}\to{\mathbf{B}}
and homomorphisms compose.)

The decision version reduces to the search version; thus for proving the hardness of both versions of problems, it is sufficient to prove the hardness of the decision version of the problem, and in order to prove tractability of both versions, it is enough to provide an efficient algorithm for the search version.

To complete this section, we define the relational structure
\mathbf{LO}_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, that appears in our main result. The domain of
\mathbf{LO}_{k}
is
\{1,\ldots,k\}
, and
\mathbf{LO}_{k}
has one ternary relation, containing precisely those triples
(a,b,c)
which contain a
unique maximum
. In other words,
(a,b,c)\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{k}}
if and only if
a=b<c
,
a=c<b
,
b=c<a
, or all three elements
a,b,c
are distinct. For example,
(1,1,2)
or
(1,2,3)
are triples of the relation of
\mathbf{LO}_{3}
, but not
(2,2,1)
.

Definition 3

Given a structure

with the domain

4

4

Untraditionally, we use lowercase notation for polymorphisms to highlight that we are not considering any topology on them contrary to the homomorphism complexes introduced below.

Concretely, in the special case of structures with a ternary relation, a polymorphism is a mapping

(u_{1},v_{1},w_{1})
, …,

(f(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}),f(v_{1},\dots,v_{n}),f(w_{1},\dots,w_{n}))\in R^{\mathbf{B}}.

Polymorphisms are enough to describe the complexity of a PCSP up to certain
\log
-space reductions. Loosely speaking, the more complex the polymorphisms are, the easier the problem is. We will use a hardness criterion that essentially states that the problem is hard if the polymorphisms have no interesting structure. To define what do we mean by interesting structure, we have to define the notions of

,

and

Definition 4
.

Fix two sets

\pi

, i.e., such that

g(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=f(x_{\pi(1)},\dots,x_{\pi(n)})

for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in A
. We denote the
\pi
-minor of

.

Abstracting from the fact that the polymorphism of any template are closed under taking minors leads to the following notion of
(abstract) minions
:
5
5

5

Abstract minions as defined here are a generalization of so-called

[
3
]

Definition 5
.

An
(abstract) minion

\mathscr{M}
is a collection of sets
\mathscr{M}^{(n)}
, where
n>0
is an integer, and mappings
\pi^{\mathscr{M}}\colon\mathscr{M}^{(n)}\to\mathscr{M}^{(m)}
where
\pi\colon[n]\to[m]
such that

\pi^{\mathscr{M}}\circ\sigma^{\mathscr{M}}=(\pi\circ\sigma)^{\mathscr{M}}
for each
\pi

, and
(1_{[n]})^{\mathscr{M}}=1_{\mathscr{M}^{(n)}}
.
6
6

6

In the language of category theory, a minion is defined as a functor from the category of finite sets to the category of sets, which satisfies a non-triviality condition:
\mathscr{M}(X)=\emptyset
if and only

. The definition given abuses the fact that the sets

form a skeleton of the category of finite sets.

The polymorphisms of a template
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
form a minion
\mathscr{M}
defined by
\mathscr{M}^{(n)}=\Pol^{(n)}({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
, and
\pi^{\mathscr{M}}(f)=f^{\pi}
. With a slight abuse of notation, we will use the symbol
\Pol({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
for this minion.
Conversely, if
\mathscr{M}
is an abstract minion, we will call
\pi^{\mathscr{M}}(f)
the

-minor of

, and write

.

An important example is the minion of projections denoted by
\mathscr{P}

\mathscr{P}^{(n)}=[n]
and
\pi^{\mathscr{P}}=\pi
.
Equivalently, and perhaps more concretely,

2.3
Homomorphism complexes

We will need a number of notions from topological combinatorics, which we will review briefly now. We refer the reader to
[
25

23

A
(finite, abstract) simplicial complex

\mathsf{K}
is finite system of sets that is downward closed under inclusion, i.e.,
F\subseteq G\in\mathsf{K}
implies
F\in\mathsf{K}
. The (finite) set

is called the set of
vertices
of
\mathsf{K}
, and the sets in

are called
simplices
or
faces
of the simplicial complex. A
simplicial map

f\colon\mathsf{K}\to\mathsf{L}
between simplicial complexes is a map between the vertex sets that preserves simplices, i.e.,
f(F)\in\mathsf{L}
for all
F\in\mathsf{K}
.

An important way of constructing simplicial complexes is the following: Let
P
be a partially ordered set (poset). A
chain
in
P
is a subset
\{p_{0},\dots,p_{k}\}\subseteq P
such that
p_{0}<p_{2}<\dots<p_{k}
. The set of all chains in
P
is a simplicial complex, called the
order complex
of
P
. Note that an order-preserving map between posets naturally induces a simplicial map between the corresponding order complexes.

With every simplicial complex
\mathsf{K}
, one can associate a topological space
\lvert\mathsf{K}\rvert
, called the
underlying space
or
geometric realization
of
\mathsf{K}
, as follows: Identify the vertex set of
\mathsf{K}
with a set of points
in general position
in a sufficiently high-dimensional Euclidean space (here, general position means that the points in
F\cup G
are affinely independent for all
F,G\in\mathsf{K}
). Then, in particular, the convex hull
\conv(F)
is a geometric simplex for every
F\in\mathsf{K}
, and the geometric realization can be defined as the union
\lvert\mathsf{K}\rvert=\bigcup_{F\in\mathsf{K}}\conv(F)
of these geometric simplices (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Lemma 1.6.2]
). We also say that the simplicial complex
K
is a triangulation of the space
\lvert\mathsf{K}\rvert
. Every simplicial map
f\colon\mathsf{K}\to\mathsf{L}
between abstract simplicial complexes induces a continuous map
\lvert f\rvert\colon\lvert\mathsf{K}\rvert\to\lvert\mathsf{L}\rvert
between their geometric realizations. In what follows, we will often blur the distinction between a simplicial complex and its geometric realization (especially when considering properties that do not depend on a particular triangulation).

Following
[
25
, Section 5.9]
, we define homomorphism complexes as order complexes of the poset of
multihomomorphisms
from one structure to another.
8
8

8

2.4
Group actions

Throughout this paper, we will work with actions of the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
on various objects (relational structures, simplicial complexes, topological spaces, groups, etc.) by structure-preserving maps (homomorphisms, simplicial maps, continuous maps, etc.). Thinking of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
as the multiplicative group with three elements
1,\omega,\omega^{2}
(with the understanding that
\omega^{i}\cdot\omega^{j}=\omega^{i+j\pmod{3}}
and
\omega^{0}=1
), such an action is described by describing the action of the generator
\omega
. Thus, specifying the action of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
on a structure
{\mathbf{A}}
amounts to specifying a homomorphism
\omega\colon{\mathbf{A}}\to{\mathbf{A}}
such that
\omega^{3}=1_{A}
(hence,
\omega
is necessarily an isomorphism; note that we are abusing notation here, writing
\omega
both for the generator of the group and the isomorphism by which it acts). Analogously, an action of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
on a simplicial complex (or a topological space) is described by specifying a simplicial isomorphism (respectively, a homeomorphism)
\omega
of order
3
from the complex (or space) to itself. We will mostly work with actions that are
free
, which in our special case of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
-actions simply means that
\omega
has no fixed points.

In particular, consider the action of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
that acts on
{\mathbf{R}_{3}}
by cyclically permuting elements. This action induces an action on multihomomorphisms
h\colon{\mathbf{R}_{3}}\to{\mathbf{A}}
by pre-composition, and this action extends naturally to an action of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
on
\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},{\mathbf{A}})
.
10
10

10

This is analogous to the action of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
on graph homomorphism complexes
\Hom(K_{2},G)
used, for example, in
[
23
]
.

It is not hard to show that the action on
\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},{\mathbf{A}})
is free as long as
{\mathbf{A}}
has no constant tuples: If a multihomomorphism
m
is a fixed point of a non-trivial element of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, then
m(1)=m(2)=m(3)
, and since
m(1)\neq\emptyset
and
m(1)\times m(2)\times m(3)\subseteq R^{\mathbf{A}}
then
R^{\mathbf{A}}
contains a constant tuple
(a,a,a)
for any
a\in m(1)
. Consequently, we may observe that the action does not fix any face of the complex.

For every homomorphism
f\colon{\mathbf{A}}\to{\mathbf{B}}
, the induced simplicial map
f_{*}\colon\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},{\mathbf{A}})\to\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},{\mathbf{B}})
(defined on vertices by mapping multihomomorphism

; as remarked above, we will often identify

2.5
Homotopy

Two continuous maps
f,g\colon X\to Y
between topological spaces are called
homotopic
, denoted
f\sim g
, if there is a continuous map
h\colon X\times[0,1]\to Y
such that
h(x,0)=f(x)
and
h(x,1)=g(x)
; the map
h
is called a
homotopy
from
f
to
g
. Note that a homotopy can also be through of as a family of maps
h({\cdot},t)\colon X\to Y
that varies continuously with
t\in[0,1]
. In what follows,
X
and
Y
will often be given as simplicial complexes, but we emphasize that we will generally not assume that the maps (or homotopies) between them are simplicial maps.
Two spaces
X
and
Y
are said to be
homotopy equivalent
if there are continuous maps
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to X
such that
fg\sim 1_{Y}
and
gf\sim 1_{X}
.

These notions naturally generalize to the setting of spaces with group actions. If
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
acts on two spaces
X
and
Y
then a continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
is (
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
-)
equivariant
if
f
preserves the action, i.e.,
f\circ\omega=\omega\circ f
. Two equivariant maps
f,g\colon X\to Y
are said to be
equivariantly homotopic
, denoted by
f\sim_{\mathbb{Z}_{3}}g
, if there exists an
equivariant homotopy
between them, i.e., a homotopy
h\colon X\times[0,1]\to Y
from
f
to
g
such that all maps
h(\cdot,t)\colon X\to Y
are equivariant. We denote by
[X,Y]_{\mathbb{Z}_{3}}
the set of all equivariant homotopy classes of (equivariant) maps from
X
to
Y
, i.e.,

[X,Y]_{\mathbb{Z}_{3}}=\{[f]\mid f\colon X\to Y\text{ is equivariant}\}

where
[f]
denotes the set of all equivariant maps
g
such that
f\sim_{\mathbb{Z}_{3}}g
.

3
Overview of the proof

We give a brief overview of the proof and the core techniques used. The result is proved by a combination of topological, combinatorial, and algebraic methods. In particular, the hardness is provided by analysing the polymorphisms of the template together with a hardness criterion from
[
3
]
, see Theorem
7
. In short, our goal is to provide a minion homomorphism from the polymorphism minion
\Pol(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})
to the minion of projections
\mathscr{P}
(which is incidentally isomorphic to the polymorphism minion of 3SAT). The core of our contribution is in providing deep-enough understanding of polymorphisms of our template so that the minion homomorphism follows.
The proof has two parts: topological and combinatorial.

3.1
Topology

The first part builds on the topological method introduced by Krokhin, Opršal, Wrochna, and Živný
[
21
,
31
,
23
]
. The core idea is that, similarly to approximate graph colouring, there are unreasonably many polymorphisms between the linear-ordering hypergraphs, but most of them are very similar. This means that each polymorphism contains a lot of noise but relatively little information. We use topology to remove this noise, and uncover a signal. This is done by considering the polymorphisms ‘up to homotopy’ — essentially claiming that the homotopy class of a polymorphism carries the information and everything else is noise.

In order to formalise this idea, we consider for each hypergraph
\mathbf{A}
the topological space
\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{A})
. Consequently, we get that each the homomorphism
f\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{B}
induces a continuous map
f_{*}\colon\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{A})\to\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{B})
. Consequently, we identify two homomorphisms
f,g
if
f_{*}
and
g_{*}
are homotopic to each other. The same is then extended to polymorphisms, although this requires to overcome a few subtle technical issues. The key observation for this extension is that the power of a homomorphism complex is homotopically equivalent to a homomorphism complex of the corresponding power (see, e.g.,
[
20
]
).

This general idea requires a refinement to avoid trivial collapses, i.e., we have to avoid the case when
f_{*}
is homotopic to a constant map which is always a continuous map between topological spaces. In our case, this is avoided by keeping track of the action of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
on the spaces
\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{A})
and
\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{B})
described in the preliminaries. Consequently, we consider maps only up to
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
-equivariant homotopy (note that the map
f_{*}
induced by a homomorphism is always equivariant). Further in this exposition, we will silently assume that the action is always present, and all notions are equivariant — the formal proof below is presented with the action in mind.

At this point we sketched how to construct a map that assigns to a polymorphism
f\colon\mathbf{A}^{n}\to\mathbf{B}
, an equivariant continuous map
f_{*}\colon\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{A}^{n})\to\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{B})
. This map does not necessarily preserve minors, nevertheless, it preserves minors
up to homotopy
, i.e., for each
\pi\colon[n]\to[m]
, we have that
(f^{\pi})_{*}
and
(f_{*})^{\pi}
are equivariantly homotopic (this is since
\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{A})^{n}
and
\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{A}^{n})
are only homotopically equivalent and not homeomorphic). This allows us to define a minion homomorphism between the polymorphism minion
\Pol(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
and the minion of ‘homotopy classes of continuous maps’ from powers of
\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{A})
to
\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{B})
.

\mathscr{Z}_{3}
denotes the minion of affine maps over
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
.

This minion homomorphism is not enough to prove NP-hardness. Although we could conclude from it, for example, that
\PCSP(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})
is not solved by any level of Sherali-Adams hierarchy (this is a direct consequence of
[
11
, Theorems 3.3 and 5.2]
). To provide hardness, we need to further analyse the image of
\chi
which is done using combinatorial arguments.

3.2
Combinatorics

In the second part, which is a combinatorial argument presented in Section
4
, we show that the image of
\chi
avoids all the affine maps except of projections. This is done by analysing binary polymorphisms from
\mathbf{LO}_{3}
to
\mathbf{LO}_{4}
.

We use the notion of
reconfiguration
of homomorphisms to achieve this. Loosely speaking, a homomorphism
f
is reconfigurable to a homomorphism
g
if there is a path of homomorphism starting with
f
and ending with
g
such that neighbouring homomorphisms differ in at most one value. (For graphs and hypergraphs without tuples with repeated entries this can be taken as a definition, but with repeated entries there are two sensible notions of reconfigurations that do not necessary align.) The connection between reconfigurability and topology was described by Wrochna
[
30
]
, and we use these ideas to connect reconfigurability with our minion homomorphism
\xi
.

We show that any binary polymorphism
f\colon\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{2}\to\mathbf{LO}_{4}
is reconfigurable to an essentially unary polymorphism, i.e., that there is an increasing function
h\colon\mathbf{LO}_{3}\to\mathbf{LO}_{4}
such that
f
is reconfigurable to the map
(x,y)\mapsto h(x)
or to the map
(x,y)\mapsto h(y)
.
Further, we show that if
f
and
g
are reconfigurable to each other, then
\chi(f)=\chi(g)
. Together with the above, this means the image of
\chi_{2}\colon\hpoloperator^{(2)}(S^{1},P^{2})\to\mathscr{Z}_{3}^{(2)}
omits an element. More precisely, we have the following lemma where
\mathscr{P}_{3}
denotes the minion of projections on a three element set (which is a subminion of
\mathscr{Z}_{3}
).

Lemma 12
.

For each binary polymorphism
f\in\Pol^{(2)}(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})
,
\chi(f)\in\mathscr{P}_{3}^{(2)}
.

This lemma is then enough to show that the image of
\chi
omits all affine maps except projections.

Corollary 13
.

\chi
is a minion homomorphism
\Pol(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})\to\mathscr{P}_{3}
.

Proof.

We show that if a subminion
\mathscr{M}\subseteq\mathscr{Z}_{3}
contains any non-projection then it contains the map
g\colon(x,y)\mapsto 2x+2y
. Let
f\in\mathscr{M}^{(n)}
depends on at least 2 coordinates, and let
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}x_{n}
.
First assume that
\alpha_{i}=2
for some
i
. Then the binary minor given by
\pi\colon[n]\to[2]
defined by
\pi(i)=1
and
\pi(j)=2
if
j\neq i
is
g
since its first coordinate is
2
and the second is
1-2=2\pmod{3}
.
Otherwise, we have that
\alpha_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for all
i
. In particular, there are
i\neq j
such that
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{k}=1
since
f
depends on at least 2 coordinates. Consequently, the minor defined by
\pi^{\prime}\colon[n]\to[2]
where
\pi^{\prime}(i)=\pi^{\prime}(j)=1
and
\pi^{\prime}(k)=2
for
k\notin\{i,j\}
is again
g
by a similar argument.

Finally, the image of
\xi
is a subminion of
\mathscr{Z}_{3}
, and since it omits
g
and every subminion of
\mathscr{Z}_{3}
contains
\mathscr{P}_{3}
, it is equal to
\mathscr{P}_{3}
which yields the desired.
∎

As mentioned before, the above corollary combined with Theorem
7
provides the main result of this paper, the
{\mathsf{NP}}
-completeness of
\PCSP(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})
(Theorem
1
).

4
Combinatorics of reconfigurations

The goal of this section is a careful combinatorial analysis of the binary polymorphisms. In particular, we will describe how the minion homomorphism
\xi\colon\Pol(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})\to\mathscr{P}
acts on binary polymorphisms. This is the key to the argument that the image of
\xi
is the projection minion and the whole of
\Pol(\mathbb{Z}_{3})
.

We say that two polymorphisms
f,g\in\Pol^{(n)}(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})
are
reconfigurable
one to the other if a path between
f
and
g
exists within the homomorphism complex
\Hom(\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{n},\mathbf{LO}_{4})
. (Note that every polymorphism is a homomorphism
\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{n}\to\mathbf{LO}_{4}
, and hence a vertex of the homomorphism complex.)

We will use the following combinatorial criterion that ensures that two polymorphisms are reconfigurable to each other. The proof is subtly dependent on some properties of the structure
\mathbf{LO}_{4}
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a symmetric relational structure. If
f,g\colon{\mathbf{A}}\to\mathbf{LO}_{4}
are two homomorphisms such that
f
and
g
differ in exactly one value, i.e., there is
d\in A
such that for all
a\in A\setminus\{d\}
we have
f(a)=g(a)
, then
f
and
g
are reconfigurable.

Proof.

We first claim that under the above assumption, the multifunction
m\colon A\to 2^{[4]}
given by
m(a)=\{f(a),g(a)\}
is a multihomomorphism.
Assume that
(a,b,c)\in R^{\mathbf{A}}
. Observe that for any
x\in A\setminus\{d\}
we have
f(x)=g(x)
and hence
m(x)=\{f(x)\}=\{g(x)\}
. We now have cases depending on how many times
d
appears in
\{a,b,c\}
.

\bm{d}
does not appear.

In this case
m(a)\times m(b)\times m(c)=\{(f(a),f(b),f(c))\}\subseteq R^{\mathbf{LO}_{4}}
.

\bm{d}
appears once.

Suppose
d=a,d\neq b,d\neq c
; then
m(a)\times m(b)\times m(c)=\{f(a),g(a)\}\times\{f(b)\}\times\{f(c)\}=\{(f(a),f(b),f(c)),(g(a),g(b),g(c))\}\subseteq R^{\mathbf{LO}_{4}}
, as
f(b)=g(b),f(c)=g(c)
.

\bm{d}
appears twice.

Suppose
d=a=b,d\neq c
; then as
(f(a),f(b),f(c))=(f(d),f(d),f(c))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{4}}
and likewise
(g(d),g(d),g(c))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{4}}
, we have
f(d)<f(c)
and
g(d)<g(c)=f(c)
. Consequently,
m(a)\times m(b)\times m(c)={\{f(d),g(d)\}}^{2}\times\{f(c)\}\subseteq R^{\mathbf{LO}_{4}}
, since every tuple has a unique maximum, namely
f(c)
.

\bm{d}
appears thrice.

This case (i.e.,
d=a=b=c
) is impossible, as
{\mathbf{A}}\to\mathbf{LO}_{4}
, and thus
{\mathbf{A}}
has no constant tuples.

Thus
m
is a multihomomorphism in all cases.

We can now define a path
p\colon[0,1]\to\Hom(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})
by
p(0)=f
,
p(1/2)=m
,
p(1)=g
, and extending linearly.
∎

We note, without a proof, if
f
and
g
are reconfigurable, then there is a sequence
f=f_{0},\dots,f_{k}=g
such that
f_{i}
and
f_{i+1}
differ in exactly one point.
A polymorphism
f\in\Pol^{(2)}(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})
has, as its domain, the set
{[3]}^{2}
, and thus it can naturally be represented as a matrix:

\begin{matrix}f(1,1)&f(1,2)&f(1,3)\\
f(2,1)&f(2,2)&f(2,3)\\
f(3,1)&f(3,2)&f(3,3)\\
\end{matrix}.

When we speak of “rows” or “columns” of
f
this is what is meant.

We show the following lemma from which we will be able to derive that each binary polymorphism is reconfigurable to an essentially unary one. (Recall that a function
f\colon A^{n}\to B
is essentially unary if it depends on at most one input coordinate.) The lemma is an analogue of the
Trash Colour Lemma
for polymorphisms from
K_{d}
to
K_{2d-2}
.

Lemma 15
.

For each
f\in\Pol^{(2)}(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})
there exists an increasing function
h\in\Pol^{(1)}(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})
, a coordinate
i\in\{1,2\}
, and a colour
t\in[4]
(called
trash colour
) such that

f(x_{1},x_{2})\in\{h(x_{i}),t\}

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in[3]
.

Proof.

Throughout we will implicitly use the fact that if
a<b
and
c<d
then
f(a,c)<f(b,d)
, as
((a,c),(a,c),(b,d))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{2}}
.

First, we claim that every colour
c\in[4]
appears inside only one row or only one column of
f
, i.e., that either there is
a\in[3]
such that
f(x,y)=c
implies
x=a
, or there is
b\in[3]
such that
f(x,y)=c
implies
y=b
. For contradiction, assume that this is not the case, i.e., there are
x,y
and
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in[3]
such that
f(x,y)=f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=c
,
x\neq x^{\prime}
, and
y\neq y^{\prime}
. The claim is proved by case analysis as follows.
First, observe that either
x<x^{\prime}
and
y>y^{\prime}
, or
x>x^{\prime}
and
y<y^{\prime}
, since otherwise
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
are comparable, and hence
f(x,y)\neq f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. Since the two cases are symmetric, we may assume without loss of generality that
x<x^{\prime}
and
y>y^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, since
((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y^{\prime}))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{2}}
,
and
f(x,y)=f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=c
, we have
f(x,y^{\prime})>c
. Similarly, as
x^{\prime}>x,y>y^{\prime}
we have that
f(x^{\prime},y)>f(x,y^{\prime})>c
. This means that
c\in\{1,2\}
. We consider each case separately.

\bm{c=1}
.

We claim that
x=y^{\prime}=1
since if
x>1
, then
f(1,y^{\prime})<f(x,y)=1
, and similarly if
y^{\prime}>1
.
This implies that
f(1,1)>1
since
((1,1),(x,x^{\prime}),(y,y^{\prime}))=((1,1),(1,y),(x^{\prime},1))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{2}}
and
f(x,y)=f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=1
. As
1<f(1,1)<f(2,2)<f(3,3)\leq 4
, we have that
f(1,1)=2
,
f(2,2)=3
, and
f(3,3)=4
. We now have three cases.

\bm{y=3}
.

We argue that
f(1,2)
has no possible value.
First, the value
1
is not possible since
((1,2),(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=((1,2),(1,3),(x^{\prime},1))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{2}}
,
f(x,y)=1
, and
f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=1
.

f(1,2)=2
is not possible since
((1,2),(1,1),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=((1,1),(1,2),(x^{\prime},1))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{2}}
, and
f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=1,f(1,1)=2
.

f(1,2)=3
is not possible since
((1,2),(2,2),(x,y))=((1,2),(2,2),(1,3))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{2}}
, and
f(x,y)=1,f(2,2)=3
.
Finally,
f(1,2)<f(3,3)=4
, so
f(1,2)\neq 4
.

\bm{x^{\prime}=3}
.

Here the contradiction follows analogously to the previous case.

\bm{x^{\prime}=y=2}
.

We consider the pair of values
f(1,3)
and
f(3,1)
. First, we have
f(1,3)>f(1,2)=f(x,y)=1
and
f(3,1)>f(2,1)=f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=1
. As
((1,3),(1,1),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=((1,3),(1,1),(2,1))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{2}}
and
f(1,1)=2,f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=2
we have that
f(1,3)\neq 2
; symmetrically
f(3,1)\neq 2
. We also have
f(1,3)\neq 3
since
((1,3),(x,y),(2,2))=((1,3),(1,2),(2,2))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{2}}
and
f(1,2)=1,f(2,2)=3
; symmetrically
f(3,1)\neq 2
. Thus
f(1,3)=f(3,1)=4
. However, then
(f(1,2),f(1,3),f(3,1))=(1,4,4)\not\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{4}}
, which is not possible, as
((1,2),(1,3),(3,1))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{2}}
, which yields our contradiction.

\bm{c=2}
.

As
f(x^{\prime},y)>f(x,y^{\prime})>c=2
, we have that
f(x,y^{\prime})=3
and
f(x^{\prime},y)=4
. Since
f(x,y^{\prime})=3
then either
x>1
or
y^{\prime}>1
, otherwise
f(3,3)>f(2,2)>f(1,1)=3
yields a contradiction. By symmetry it is enough to discuss the case
y^{\prime}=2
and
y=3
. Finally, we have
f(x,1)<f(x^{\prime},2)=2
, hence
f(x,1)=1
which is in contradiction with

(1,2,2)=(f(x,1),f(x^{\prime},2),f(x,3))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{4}}.

Thus we get a contradiction in all cases, and hence each colour appears in only one row or only one column.

We say that a colour
c\in[4]
is of
column
type if
f(x,y)=c
implies
x=a_{c}
for some fixed
a_{c}\in[3]
, and is of
row
type if
f(x,y)=c
implies
y=b_{c}
for some
b_{c}\in[3]
. Note that a colour can be both row and column type, in which case we may choose either. We claim that there are at least three colours that share a type — otherwise there are two colours of row type and two colours of column type which would leave an element of
\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{2}
uncoloured.
A similar observation also yields that there has to be three colours of the same type that cover all rows or all columns, i.e., such that the constants
a_{c}
or
b_{c}
(depending on the type) are pairwise distinct.
Let us assume they are of the column type; the other case is symmetric. Further, we may assume that the forth colour is of the row type, since if two colours share a column, then one of the colours appears only once, and can be therefore considered to be of row type.

We define
h(a)
to be the colour
c
of column type with
a_{c}=a
, then we have
f(x,y)\in\{h(x),t\}
where
t
is the colour of the row type. Finally, we argue that
h
is increasing. This is since there are
y<y^{\prime}
with
y\neq b_{t}
and
y^{\prime}\neq b_{t}
, and consequently

h(1)=f(1,y)<f(2,y^{\prime})=h(2)=f(2,y)<f(3,y^{\prime})=h(3).

This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 16
.

Every binary polymorphism
f\in\Pol^{(2)}(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})
is reconfigurable to an essentially unary polymorphism.

Proof.

The proof relies on Lemma
15
. We prove our result by induction on the number of appearances of the trash colour. The result is clear if the trash colour never appears; so assume it appears at least once. Thus suppose without loss of generality that
f(x,y)\in\{h(x),t\}
for some increasing
h\in\Pol^{(1)}(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4})
, and that in particular
f(x_{0},y_{0})=t
. Furthermore, suppose that among all such pairs,
(x_{0},y_{0})
is the one that maximises
x_{0}
. We claim that
f^{\prime}(x,y)
, which is equal to
f(x,y)
everywhere except that
f^{\prime}(x_{0},y_{0})=h(x_{0})
is also a polymorphism, which gives us our inductive step.

Consider any
((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime}))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{3}^{2}}
; if
(x_{0},y_{0})\not\in\{(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\}
, then
(f^{\prime}(x,y),f^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),f^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime}))=(f(x,y),f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),f(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime}))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{4}}
, so assume without loss of generality that
(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})=(x_{0},y_{0})
. We now have two cases, depending on where the unique maximum of
(f(x,y),f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),f(x_{0},y_{0}))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{4}}
falls.

\bm{f(x,y)}
is the unique maximum

In this case,
f(x,y)>f(x_{0},y_{0})=t
and
f(x,y)>f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. We must show that
f^{\prime}(x_{0},y_{0})=h(x_{0})\neq f(x,y)
. Since we know that
f(x,y)\neq t
and thus
f(x,y)=h(x)
, and furthermore that
h
is increasing, this is the same as showing that
x\neq x_{0}
. Suppose for contradiction that
x=x_{0}
; thus
x^{\prime}>x
. If
f(x^{\prime},y)=h(x^{\prime})>h(x)
, then
f(x,y)
would not be the unique maximum, so
f(x^{\prime},y)=t
. This contradicts the choice of
(x_{0},y_{0})
, as
x^{\prime}>x_{0}
.

\bm{f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}
is the unique maximum

This case is identical to the previous case.

\bm{f(x_{0},y_{0})}
is the unique maximum

It follows that
f(x,y)<t
and
f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})<t
, hence
f(x,y)=h(x)
and
f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=h(x^{\prime})
. Thus since
(x,x^{\prime},x_{0})\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{3}}
and
h
is increasing, it follows that
(f^{\prime}(x,y),f^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),f^{\prime}(x_{0},y_{0}))=(h(x),h(x^{\prime}),h(x_{0}))\in R^{\mathbf{LO}_{4}}
.

Thus we see that this
f^{\prime}
is indeed a polymorphism, and contains one fewer trash colour. Thus our conclusion follows.
∎

, we can see the reconfiguration graph of

It can be also observed that unary polymorphisms that depend on the same coordinate are reconfigurable to each other. Moreover, since every connected component of

Finally, we conclude with the statement that we actually use in the proof, which follows from well-known properties of homomorphism complexes.

Lemma 17
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
,
{\mathbf{B}}
, and
\mathbf{C}
be three structures,
G
a group acting on
{\mathbf{A}}
, and assume that
f,g\in\hom({\mathbf{B}},\mathbf{C})
are reconfigurable. Then the induced maps
f_{*},g_{*}\colon\Hom({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})\to\Hom({\mathbf{A}},\mathbf{C})
are
G
-homotopic.

Proof.

First, observe that the composition of multihomomorphisms as a map
\mhom({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})\to\mhom({\mathbf{B}},\mathbf{C})\to\mhom({\mathbf{A}},\mathbf{C})
is monotone. This means that the composition extends linearly to a continuous map

c:\Hom({\mathbf{B}},\mathbf{C})\times\Hom({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})\to\Hom(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{C})

(see also
[
20
, Section 18.4.3]
).
Since the composition is associative, we obtain that the map
c
is equivariant (under an action of any automorphism of
{\mathbf{A}}
on the second coordinate).

Finally, we have that
f_{*}(x)=c(f,x)
by the definition of
f_{*}
, and analogously,
g_{*}(x)=c(g,x)
. Consequently, if
h\colon[0,1]\to\Hom({\mathbf{B}},\mathbf{C})
is an arc connecting
f
and
g
, i.e., such that
h(0)=f
and
h(1)=g
, then the map
H\colon[0,1]\times\Hom({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})\to\Hom({\mathbf{A}},\mathbf{C})
defined by

H(t,x)=c(h(t),x)

is a homotopy between
f_{*}
and
g_{*}
. This
H
is also equivariant since
c
is equivariant.
∎

.

is equivariantly homotopic either to the map

, or to the map

is the inclusion.

A.1
Constructing Eilenberg-MacLane spaces

The goal of this section is to build explicitly our target space

.
However, the bulk of our work will be to show that the standard methods from obstruction theory can be adapted to work in our equivariant setting without fundamental alterations.
This task has been already carried out in much bigger generality by Bredon in

It is still useful to build the space

A.1.1
Beyond Simplicial Complexes: CW-complexes

While the idea of simplicial complex is a powerful tool that allow for a discretization of topological spaces, for the purpose of homotopy theory they are often too rigid and unwieldy.
Instead, the concept of a CW-complex is much more natural and easy to work with.
There are multiple equivalent definitions of what a CW-complex is, we will present a constructive definition that is well suited to our use case. For a more complete and thorough presentation, we refer to

]

The basic step in constructing a CW-complex is the gluing of a disc: suppose we are given a space

a subminion of the minion of tuples
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with
\sum\alpha_{i}=1\pmod{3}
since, e.g., the unary minor of
(2,2)
disagrees in the two minions.

We then prove that (in Section

), for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, as long as
\alpha_{i}\neq\beta_{i}\pmod{3}
, then

and

are not equivariantly homotopic.
This then allow us to define an injective mapping

— we view each element
\alpha\in\mathscr{Z}_{3}^{(n)}
as a tuple in
\mathbb{Z}
with
\alpha_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
.

In Section

, we show that any equivariant map

is homotopic to some monomial map

for
\alpha\in\mathscr{Z}_{3}^{(n)}
. This is done by showing that

which we show via obstruction theory — the bijection is given by a bijection between

and the Bredon cohomology

(here use that

is an Eilenberg-MacLane space), and a computation that gives that

Consequently, we get that

is a bijection (or more precisely, that

is a bijection for all

Finally, we show that

is a minion homomorphism, i.e., that it preserves minors. This is done by observing that the

of

(which is in fact described in Section

) preserves minors. Similarly to homomorphisms of algebraic structures (e.g., groups), an inverse of a bijective minion homomorphism is a homomorphism as well.

B.1
Monomial maps are all homotopically distinct

Here, we prove the following:

Lemma 36
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathscr{Z}_{3}^{(n)}
. If
m_{\alpha}
is equivariantly homotopic to
m_{\beta}
, then
\alpha=\beta
.

The proof of the lemma is build on the following informal geometric intuition.
By cellular approximation, a homotopy between two maps
f_{0},f_{1}\colon T^{n}\to P^{2}
“drags” around the image of any of the coordinate circles (i.e., the image of
c_{i}\colon S^{1}\hookrightarrow T^{n}
defined by
c_{i}(x)=b
where
b_{i}=x
and
b_{j}
is constant for each
j\neq i
) of the torus along some
2
-disc in
P^{2}
. However since it has to be an equivariant homotopy, it has to drag in the same way the rest of the orbit of the coordinate cycle equivariantly along the other discs in the image. Therefore the number of times a coordinate cycle wraps around can only change by a multiple of
3
.

The formalization of this idea, however, requires some work. In particular, we need a way to make the idea of a “degree” of a coordinate cycle precise:

Definition 37
.

Let
e^{j}\in C^{1}(P^{2})
and
d^{j}\in C^{2}(P^{2})
be the dual of
e_{j}
and
d_{j}
respectively (i.e.,
e^{j}(e_{i})=0
if
i\neq j
, and
e^{j}(e_{i})=1
if
i=j
). Denote by
x_{i}\in C_{1}(T^{n})
the
i
-th coordinate cycle in
T^{n}
and
b_{i},B_{i}\in C_{2}(T^{n})
fillings for
x_{i}-\omega\cdot x_{i}
and
x_{i}-\omega^{2}\cdot x_{i}
respectively.

Then, the
i
-degree of an equivariant map
f\colon T^{n}\to P^{2}
is

\deg_{i}(f)=\left(f^{*}(e^{0})(x_{i})+f^{*}(d^{0})(b_{i})+f^{*}(d^{0})(B_{i})\right)\bmod 3

A key observation is that this quantity does not change along equivariant homotopies:

Lemma 38
.

Let
f_{0},f_{1}:T^{n}\to P^{2}
equivariant maps homotopic via an equivariant homotopy
h
. Then for all
i\in[n]
,
\deg_{i}(f_{0})=\deg_{i}(f_{1})
.

Proof.

On the cochains of dimension
1
we have:

f_{0}^{*}(e_{0})(x_{i})-f_{1}^{*}(e_{0})(x_{i})=(\delta h^{*}(e^{0}))(x_{i})+(h^{*}\delta(e^{0}))(x_{i})=0+h(d^{0}+d^{1}+d^{2})(x_{i})

where the second equality is obtained by using the fact that
\partial x_{i}=0
and
\delta e^{0}=d^{0}+d^{1}+d^{2}
.

On the
2
-cochains we have:

\displaystyle f_{0}^{*}(d^{0})(b_{i})-f_{1}^{*}(d^{0})(b_{i})

\displaystyle=(\delta h^{*}(d^{0}))(b_{i})+(h^{*}\delta(d^{0}))(b_{i})=h^{*}(d^{0})(x_{i}-\omega\cdot x_{i})+0

\displaystyle f_{0}^{*}(d^{0})(B_{i})-f_{1}^{*}(d^{0})(B_{i})

\displaystyle=(\delta h^{*}(d^{0}))(B_{i})+(h^{*}\delta(d^{0}))(B_{i})=h^{*}(d^{0})(x_{i}-\omega^{2}\cdot x_{i})+0

where we use that
\partial b_{i}=x_{i}-\omega\cdot x_{i}
,
\partial B_{i}=x_{i}-\omega^{2}\cdot x_{i}
and
\delta d^{0}=0
.

However, since the homotopy is equivariant, it has to commute with the action and thus
h^{*}(d^{0})(\omega\cdot x_{i})=h^{*}(\omega\cdot d^{0})(x_{i})
.
Summing everything together, using this fact and that
\omega^{i}\cdot d^{0}=d^{i}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\deg_{i}(f_{0})-\deg_{i}(f_{1})

\displaystyle=h(d^{0}+d^{1}+d^{2})(x_{i})+h^{*}(d^{0})(x_{i}-\omega\cdot x_{i})+h^{*}(d^{0})(x_{i}-\omega^{2}\cdot x_{i})

\displaystyle=h(d^{0})\left((x_{i}+\omega\cdot x_{i}+\omega^{2}\cdot x_{i})+(x_{i}-\omega\cdot x_{i})+(x_{i}-\omega^{2}\cdot x_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=h^{*}(d^{0})(3x_{i})=3h^{*}(d^{0})(x_{i})=0\pmod{3}.\qed

Furthermore, we can show the following which will be used to prove that the mapping
\mu
is injective, and also (later) to show that its “inverse” is a minion homomorphism.

Lemma 39
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=1\pmod{3}
. Then

\deg_{i}(m_{\alpha})=\alpha_{i}\bmod 3

Proof.

Since the image of
m_{\alpha}
is contained in the
1
-skeleton,
m_{\alpha}^{*}(d^{0})\equiv 0
.
Moreover,
(m_{\alpha})_{*}(x_{i})=\alpha_{i}e_{0}+\alpha_{i}e_{1}+\alpha_{i}e_{2}
, hence
e^{0}((m_{\alpha})_{*}(x_{i}))=\alpha_{i}
and
\deg_{i}(m_{\alpha})=\alpha_{i}\bmod 3
.
∎

We can now conclude the proof of Lemma
36
which follows immediately from the above.

Proof of Lemma
36
.

If
\alpha\neq\beta
, then they differ in at least one coordinate, i.e.,
\alpha_{i}\neq\beta_{i}
for some
i
. Then
\deg_{i}(m_{\alpha})\neq\deg_{i}(m_{\beta})
by Lemma
39
, and therefore
m_{\alpha}
and
m_{\beta}
are not equivariantly homotopic by Lemma
38
.
∎

Before, we progress further, let us discuss one more consequence of Lemma
36
, namely, the following.

Lemma 40
.

The mapping
\gamma\colon\hpoloperator(S^{1},P^{2})\to\mathscr{Z}_{3}
defined by

[f]=(\deg_{1}(f),\dots,\deg_{n}(f))

satisfies, for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=1
, and
\pi\colon[n]\to[m]
,

\gamma([m_{\alpha}]^{\pi})=\gamma([m_{\alpha}])^{\pi},

i.e., it preserves minors when restricted to classes of monomial maps.

Proof.

Note that
\gamma
is well-defined since the degrees do note depend on the choice of representative (Lemma
38
).
Further, using Lemma
39
, we have that

\gamma([m_{\alpha}])=(\alpha_{1}\bmod 3,\dots,\alpha_{n}\bmod 3)

and hence
\gamma([m_{\alpha}])^{\pi}=\beta
where
\beta_{j}=(\sum_{i\in\pi^{-1}(j)}\alpha_{i})\bmod 3
.
Furthermore,
m_{\alpha}^{\pi}=m_{\beta^{\prime}}^{\phantom{\pi}}
where

\beta^{\prime}_{j}=\sum_{i\in\pi^{-1}(j)}\alpha_{i}
, and consequently

\gamma([m_{\alpha}]^{\pi})=\gamma([m_{\alpha}^{\pi}])=\beta=\gamma(m_{\alpha}).\qed

In the next subsection, we show that
\mu
and

are in fact inverses to each other, and hence the previous lemma applies for all elements of
\hpoloperator(S^{1},P^{2})
, i.e.,
\gamma
(and consequently

B.2.1
Equivariant cohomology: a primer

We introduce equivariant cohomology in a specific case that is sufficient for our purposes, and refer to Bredon
[
7

[
29

for a detailed treatment.

As we have previously observed, if a CW-complex

has a free cellular

-action, then there is an induced action on the group of

that commutes with the boundary maps.
The main insight of Bredon equivariant (co)homology is a shift in perspective: specifying an action on the chains is exactly the same as giving the chain group a structure of
\Lambda
-module for a suitable constructed ring (the integer group ring
\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{Z}_{3}]

15
15

15

This holds in our specific context. In the general case, the Bredon cohomology has a richer structure than simply that of
\Lambda
-module.

More explicitly, the ring

is defined as the formal integer combinations of elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}=\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
with the multiplication defined by setting
\omega^{i}\omega^{j}=\omega^{i+j}
and extending bilinearly.
The key observation is that prescribing a
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
-action on an Abelian group

is exactly the same as choosing a structure of
\Lambda
-module on

In particular, if

is a CW-complex with a cellular

-action, for any

\Lambda

and extended linearly.

Since the all the boundary maps commute with the action, these are also maps of
\Lambda
-modules hence

is a chain complex of
\Lambda
-modules.
We will denote this chain complex by

.
The homology associated to this chain complex is the

At the same time, if we fix a

-module

Remark 47

We formulate and prove these two lemmata in the language of category theory which is well suited. We do not assume deep knowledge of category theory and recall the necessary core notions.

Categories, functors, and natural transformations

Instead of giving precise definitions, which can be found in any category theory textbook, we will give examples of the basic notions of category theory.

Let us recall that a

Also recall that an

and

Example 48

The most basic category is the category of sets: objects are sets, and morphisms are maps between the two given sets. We denote this category by

Example 49

Products and polymorphisms

A polymorphism is a homomorphism from a power, and hence if we want to define polymorphisms in a category, we need to have a notion of a power, or more generally
product
. Products in an arbitrary category are defined by how they interact with other objects. More precisely, we can observe that products of sets, structures, or topological spaces there is a bijection

\hom(A,B_{1})\times\dots\times\hom(A,B_{n})\simeq\hom(A,B_{1}\times\dots\times B_{n})

for each object
A
, i.e., in order to define a morphism from an object
A
to a product of objects
B_{1}
, …,
B_{n}
, it is enough to specify an
n
-tuple of morphisms from
A
to each
B_{i}
. (In order to define products precisely, we would also require that the above bijection is natural in
A

Definition 54
.

Let

. We say that an object

together with morphisms

for

of

, …,
B_{n}
if, for each
A\in\text{cat}
and morphisms

, there is a unique morphism
a\in\hom(A,P)
such that
a_{i}=p_{i}\circ a
. The maps
p_{i}
are called
projections
.

Let us note that the projections can be obtained from the above bijection by substituting
P
for
A
and considering the identity map on
P
which is assigned a tuple of morphisms
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in\hom(P,B_{1})\times\dots\times\hom(P,B_{n})
.
Observe that products are well-defined only up to isomorphisms. Indeed, if
(P,p_{i})
and
(P^{\prime},p^{\prime}_{i})
are both products of
B_{1}
, …,
B_{n}
, then there are morphisms
f\in\hom(P,P^{\prime})
and
g\in\hom(P^{\prime},P)
such that
p_{i}^{\prime}=p_{i}\circ f
and
p_{i}=p_{i}^{\prime}\circ g
. Since
1_{P}
is the unique morphisms such that
p_{i}=p_{i}\circ 1_{P}
, we get that
g\circ f=1_{P}
. Analogously,
f\circ g=1_{P}^{\prime}
.

Example 55

Products do not always exists. Nevertheless, all the categories we work with in this paper (

with the usual product topology and the coordinatewise (diagonal) action of

Let us show the universal property of the product. Let

be a
G

be continuous maps. We claim that there is a map

such that

and

are equivariantly homotopic, and this map is unique up to equivariant homotopy. The existence is straightforward since we can define

It is easy to check that this map is indeed

. To show that

are homotopic for all

instead of

. This

and

.

We can now define the notion of a polymorphism in any category with products.

Given the definitions above, the first lemma is rather trivial. It claims that we can transfer polymorphisms through any functor that preserves products. It was mentioned in the context of promise CSPs in

Lemma 62

If a functor

To show

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a^{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{\pi}^{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\pi}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a^{m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}

Consequently, we have that

\xi(f^{\pi})=b\circ f^{\pi}\circ a^{n}=(b\circ f\circ a^{m})\circ p_{\pi}^{A^{\prime}}=\xi_{m}(f)^{\pi}
.
∎

C.2
Homomorphism complexes preserve products

We prove that the functor
\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{-})
preserves products in the categorical sense. Essentially, it follows by the same argument as the well-known fact that the product of homomorphisms complexes is homotopically equivalent to the homomorphism complex of the product (see, e.g.,
[
20
, Section 18.4.2]
).
We provide a bit more detailed proof to show that the homotopy equivalence can be taken equivariant, and that the products are preserved in the categorical sense which is a slightly stronger statement. Let us first prove that there is an equivariant homotopy equivalence. To provide this equivalence, we use the following technical lemma.

Lemma 64
.

Let
\mathsf{K}
be an order complex of a poset. If
\alpha,\beta\colon V(\mathsf{K})\to V(\mathsf{K})
are monotone maps such that
\alpha(x)\leq\beta(x)
for each
x
, then their linear extensions are homotopic.

Proof.

Observe that the map
h\colon\{0,1\}\times V(\mathsf{K})\to V(\mathsf{K})
defined by
h(0,x)=\alpha(x)
and
h(1,x)=\beta(x)
is monotone, and that the complex of the product poset is homeomorphic to
[0,1]\times\mathsf{K}
. Consequently,
h
induces a homotopy between
\alpha
and
\beta
by linear extension.
∎

Lemma 65
.

For each
G
-structure
\mathbf{A}
, and structures
\mathbf{B}_{1}
, …,
\mathbf{B}_{n}
, there is a
G
-equivariant homotopy equivalence

\Hom(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}_{1})\times\dots\times\Hom(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}_{n})\sim_{G}\Hom(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbf{B}_{n}).

Proof.

For each functor
\mathscr{F}
, we always have a morphism

f\in\hom(\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{B}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbf{B}_{n}),\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{B}_{1})\times\dots\times\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{B}_{n}))

defined by the universal property of the product applied to the maps
\mathscr{F}(p_{i})
. In the case
\mathscr{F}=\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{-})
, this morphism is represented by the continuous map

\alpha:\Hom(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbf{B}_{n})\to\Hom(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}_{1})\times\dots\times\Hom(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}_{n})

defined by
\alpha(m)=(p_{1}\circ m,\dots,p_{n}\circ m)
for each
m\in\mhom(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbf{B}_{n})
and extending linearly. It is straightforward to check that
\alpha
is an equivariant continuous map. We prove that it has a homotopy inverse
\beta
defined on vertices by

\beta(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})\colon x\mapsto m_{1}(x)\times\cdots\times m_{n}(x),

and extending linearly. Clearly,
\beta
is
G
-equivariant and
\alpha\circ\beta=1
.

We show that
\beta\circ\alpha
is equivariantly homotopic to the identity. It is easy to observe that
\beta(\alpha(m))\geq m
for each
m\in\mhom(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbf{B}_{n})
. Since both
\alpha
and
\beta
are monotone and equivariant, so is the composition, and the inequality gives an equivariant homotopy between
\beta\circ\alpha
and the identity by the above lemma.
∎

The proof above immediately gives that
\Hom({\mathbf{R}_{3}},\mathbf{-})
preserves products. This is since we showed that
[\alpha]
, which commutes with projections, is an isomorphism.

Corollary 66
.

For each
G
-structure
\mathbf{A}
, the functor
\Hom(\mathbf{A},{-})\colon\text{rel}\to\text{htop}_{G}
preserves products.

Where the first arrow follows from a combination of Corollary
66
, and Lemma
62
, and the second arrow follows from Lemmata
63
,
19
, and
32
. The last minion is isomorphic to
\mathscr{Z}_{3}
by Lemma
33
, which yields the required.
∎
