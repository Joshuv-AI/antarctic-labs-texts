Testing Junta Truncation
William He, Shivam Nadimpalli

Abstract
We consider the basic statistical problem of detecting truncation of the uniform distribution on the Boolean hypercube by juntas. More concretely, we give upper and lower bounds on the problem of distinguishing between i.i.d. sample access to either (a) the uniform distribution over { 0 , 1 } n \{0,1\}^{n} , or (b) the uniform distribution over { 0 , 1 } n \{0,1\}^{n} conditioned on the satisfying assignments of a k k -junta f : { 0 , 1 } n → { 0 , 1 } f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\} . We show that (up to constant factors) min ⁡ { 2 k + log ⁡ ( n k ) , 2 k / 2 ​ log 1 / 2 ​ ( n k ) } \min\{2^{k}+\log{n\choose k},{2^{k/2}\log^{1/2}{n\choose k}}\} samples suffice for this task and also show that a log ⁡ ( n k ) \log{n\choose k} dependence on sample complexity is unavoidable. Our results suggest that testing junta truncation requires learning the set of relevant variables of the junta.

3.1
Testing Truncation via a Consistent Hypothesis Checker

The truncation tester we present in this section can be viewed as a “consistent hypothesis finder” (see for example
[
KV94
]

k

\{0,1\}^{n}

Input:

\mathcal{D}\in\{\mathcal{U}_{n}\}\sqcup\mathcal{J}_{n}(k,\varepsilon)

‘‘Un-truncated’’ or ‘‘truncated’’

\textsc{Consistent-Junta-Checker}(\mathcal{D})
:

1.

Set

T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=100{\left(2^{k}+\log{n\choose k}\right)}\log^{-1}{\left(\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\right)}

and draw
\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{(T)}\sim\mathcal{D}
.

2.

If there exists a
k
-junta
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
with
\mathrm{Vol}(f)\leq 1-\varepsilon
such that for all

Proposition 5
.

The algorithm

Proof.

Note that if

Let

\boldsymbol{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}(k,\varepsilon)}\mathbf{1}{\left\{\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})~\text{for all}~i\in[T]\right\}}

We then have

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mathcal{U}_{n}}{\left[\boldsymbol{X}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}(k,\varepsilon)}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathcal{U}_{n}}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})~\text{for all}~i\in[T]\right]}

If
\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(k)
, then
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{MLE}}
outputs “truncated” with probability at least
99/100
.

Then
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{MLE}}
must draw at least
\log{n\choose k}
samples from
\mathcal{D}
.

To prove that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{MLE}}
drawing
\log\binom{n}{k}
samples does not satisfy the performance guarantee, we prove
Lemma

14
which reduces our problem to proving an anticoncentration bound for a coding-theoretic random variable. Finally,
Lemma

15
completes the proof by providing the anticoncentration bound needed.

We define our random variable as follows:

Definition 13
.

Let
\mathbf{V}\leq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a random linear subspace obtained by the following process:

1.

Draw
\boldsymbol{v}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}^{(T)}\sim\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
uniformly at random where
T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\log{n\choose k}
.

2.

Set
\mathbf{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{v}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}^{(T)})^{\perp}
.

We also define

\boldsymbol{w}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\left|\{x\in\mathbf{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|x|=k\}\right|}.

The following lemma relates the failure probability of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{MLE}}
to a tail probability of the random variable
\boldsymbol{w}
:

Lemma 14
.

Given
T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\log{n\choose k}
samples
\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{(T)}
drawn from
\mathcal{U}_{n}
, we have

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathcal{U}_{n}}{\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{MLE}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{(T)})~\text{outputs ``truncated''}\right]}=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathcal{U}_{n}}{\left[\boldsymbol{w}\geq 1\right]}

with
\boldsymbol{w}
as in
Definition

13
.

Proof.

By
Definition

11
, we have that the probability of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{MLE}}
outputting “truncated” given samples from
\mathcal{U}
is given by

{\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{U}_{n}}{\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(k)}{}{\left[\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{D}\end{subarray}}{\left[\overline{\boldsymbol{y}}\right]}=\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]}\geq\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{U}_{n}}{\left[\overline{\boldsymbol{y}}\right]}=\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]}.}

(2)

Note, however, that for fixed
x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(T)}
we have

{\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{U}_{n}}{\left[\overline{\boldsymbol{y}}=\overline{x}\right]}=\frac{1}{2^{nT}},}

(3)

and also that

{\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(k)}{\left[\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{D}\end{subarray}}{\left[\overline{\boldsymbol{y}}=\overline{x}\right]}\right]}=\frac{{\left|{\left\{S\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|S|=k~\text{and}~\chi_{S}(x^{(i)})=1~\text{for}~i\in[T]\right\}}\right|}}{|\mathcal{P}_{n}(k)|2^{(n-1)T}}.}

(4)

This relies on the fact that
\mathrm{Vol}(\chi_{S})=1/2
for all
S\neq\emptyset
. Combining
Equations

2
,
3
and
4
, we get that

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathcal{U}_{n}}{\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{MLE}}~\text{outputs ``truncated''}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathcal{U}_{n}}{\left[\small{\left|{\left\{S\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|S|=k~\text{and for all}~i\in[T],\chi_{S}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)})=1\right\}}\right|}\geq\frac{|\mathcal{P}_{n}(k)|}{2^{T}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{w}\geq 1]

as
|\mathcal{P}_{n}(k)|={n\choose k}
and recalling our choice of
T=\log{n\choose k}
.
∎

With
Lemma

14
in hand, the proof of the lower bound is completed by the following lemma:

Lemma 15
.

We have

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{w}\geq 1]>0.01.

Proof.

We will first establish that
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\boldsymbol{w}]=1
. To see this, note that

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\boldsymbol{w}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\\
|x|=k\end{subarray}}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathbf{V}}{\left[x\in\mathbf{V}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\\
|x|=k\end{subarray}}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\sim\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}{\left[\langle\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)},x\rangle=0~\text{for all}~i\in[T]\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\\
|x|=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{T}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\sim\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}{\left[\langle\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)},x\rangle=0\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\\
|x|=k\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2^{T}}

\displaystyle=1,

(5)

recalling that
T=\log{n\choose k}
. We will next obtain an upper bound on the second moment of
\boldsymbol{w}
. Recalling that
\boldsymbol{w}=\sum_{|x|=k}\mathbf{1}{\left\{x\in\mathbf{V}\right\}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\boldsymbol{w}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mathbf{V}}{\left[{\left(\sum_{|x|=k}\mathbf{1}{\left\{x\in\mathbf{V}\right\}}\right)}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mathbf{V}}{\left[{\sum_{|x|,|y|=k}\mathbf{1}{\left\{x\in\mathbf{V}\right\}}\cdot\mathbf{1}{\left\{y\in\mathbf{V}\right\}}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\\
|x|=|y|=k\end{subarray}}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathbf{V}}{\left[x\in\mathbf{V},y\in\mathbf{V}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\\
|x|=k\end{subarray}}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathbf{V}}{\left[x\in\mathbf{V}\right]}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\neq y\\
|x|=|y|=k\end{subarray}}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathbf{V}}{\left[x\in\mathbf{V},y\in\mathbf{V}\right]},

but when
x\neq y
, note that the events
{\left\{x\in\mathbf{V}\right\}}
and
{\left\{y\in\mathbf{V}\right\}}
are independent, and so we have

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\neq y\\
|x|=|y|=k\end{subarray}}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathbf{V}}{\left[x\in\mathbf{V}\right]}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathbf{V}}{\left[y\in\mathbf{V}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq 2,

(6)

where the last two expressions relied on our choice of
T
.

We now turn to the proof of
Lemma

15
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{w}\geq 1]\leq 0.01
. Now, from
Equation

5
we have that

1=\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\boldsymbol{w}]=\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}[\boldsymbol{w}\geq i].

By assumption we then have that

0.01\geq\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{w}\geq 1]=1-\sum_{i\geq 2}\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{w}\geq i],\qquad\text{and so}\qquad\sum_{i\geq 2}\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{w}\geq i]\geq 0.99.

(7)

Note, however, that by
Equations

5
and
6
it follows that
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}[\boldsymbol{w}]\leq 1
and so by Chebyshev’s inequality we have that for any
i\geq 2
,

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{w}\geq i]\leq\frac{1}{(i-1)^{2}}.

We also have that for
i\geq 2
,
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{w}\geq i]\leq\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{w}\geq 1]\leq 0.01
by assumption, and so

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 2}\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{w}\geq i]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\geq 2}\min{\left\{\frac{1}{(i-1)^{2}},0.01\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=2}^{15}0.01+\sum_{i>15}\frac{1}{(i-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 0.15+0.25

\displaystyle\leq 0.4,

which contradicts
Equation

7
. It follows that
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{w}\geq 1]>0.01
, completing the proof.
∎
