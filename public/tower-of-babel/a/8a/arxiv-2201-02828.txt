Discrete-time risk sensitive portfolio optimization with proportional transaction costs
Marcin Pitera, Łukasz Stettner

Abstract
In this paper we consider a discrete-time risk sensitive portfolio optimization over a long time horizon with proportional transaction costs. We show that within the log-return i.i.d. framework the solution to a suitable Bellman equation exists under minimal assumptions and can be used to characterize the optimal strategies for both risk-averse and risk-seeking cases. Moreover, using numerical examples, we show how a Bellman equation analysis can be used to construct or refine optimal trading strategies in the presence of transaction costs.

Introduction

Quantitative portfolio management is an important part of mathematical finance. Stimulated by the seminal work
[
31
]
, this field has been consistently evolving during the last 70 years for both discrete and continuous time settings, see
[
28
,
12
,
35
]
and references therein for an overview. Among the considered portfolio optimisation frameworks, the risk sensitive portfolio optimisation is among the most recognised ones, see
[
18
,
6
,
24
]
. Given a wealth process
(W_{t})
and the risk-averse parameter
\gamma\neq 0
, the long-run version of risk sensitive criterion is defined as

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{t}\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\mathbb{E}\left[W_{t}^{\gamma}\right]\quad\textrm{or equivalently as }\quad\liminf_{t\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{t}\mu^{\gamma}(\ln W_{t}),

(1)

where
\mu^{\gamma}(\cdot):=1/\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}[\exp(\gamma(\cdot))]
is the entropic utility. The optimality criterion presented in (
1
) measures the long-run normalised entropy of the log-wealth and could be seen as a non-linear extension of the
Kelly’s criterion
, see
[
30
,
10
,
19
]
. It should be noted that the extension (
1
) is in fact unique within the class of cash-additive and strongly time-consistent certainty equivalents which explains why the usage of entropy is so common in multiple stochastic control applications, see
[
29
,
14
]
for details. In fact, the risk sensitive criterion appears naturally in many portfolio investment problems and is linked to various optimality frameworks. For completeness, let us provide some examples. First, by considering the second order Taylor’s expansion of the entropic utility, around
\gamma=0
, we get

\mu^{\gamma}(\ln W_{t})=\mathbb{E}[\ln W_{t}]+\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\textrm{Var}[\ln W_{t}]+O(\gamma_{2},t),\quad t>0,

which shows that, for
\gamma<0
, the risk sensitive framework might be considered as an extension of the mean-variance Markowitz portfolio optimisation that allows time-consistent utility treatment, see
[
7
]
. Second, (
1
) is directly linked to so called
equivalent safe rate
, which reflects the minimal hypothetical safe rate that would encourage the investor to invest in the risky portfolio, see
[
25
]
. Third, for the risk-averse case
\gamma<0
, the risk-sensitive criterion is dual to the
downside risk
, which is a common investment criterion in the long-run portfolio optimisation, see
[
32
]
or
[
33
]
for details. Fourth, for
\gamma>0
, the maximization of (
1
) is related to the studies of
power utility asymptotics
and can be considered as a dual problem to
upside chance probability
, see
[
33
]
and
[
39
]
. Finally, let us remark that risk sensitive criterion is an
acceptability indicex
(also called
performance measure
) and has many economically desirable properties, see e.g.
[
13
,
5
]
. We refer to
[
7
]
for an overview of economic properties of risk sensitive criterion made in reference to portfolio management.

The main aim of this paper is to show that under the i.i.d. property imposed on asset’s log-returns one can solve a suitable risk-sensitive Bellman equation under proportional transaction cost and lack of short selling; see
[
20
,
16
]
for a discussion about transaction cost impact on portfolio management. We emphasize that the set of additional assumptions imposed on log-returns in this paper is minimal, i.e. we only require that asset’s log-returns have finite mean and entropy. While this might be counter-intuitive on the first sight, as one typically impose strong ergodic assumption on the process in order to get the existence of risk-sensitive Bellman equation solution, the i.i.d. property proves to be a plausible alternative. For an overview of the key results, we refer to Theorem
8
, Theorem
9
, and Theorem
12
.

The results of this paper are presented in a self-contained entropy based way to streamline the economic context; we hope this makes the paper more transparent and accessible to the generic mathematical finance community. That saying, the results presented here are in fact linked to an extensive literature on the risk sensitive stochastic control optimisation, and are expanding this framework in reference to portfolio management, see
[
6
,
34
]
. That is why in some cases we decided to present alternative formulations of the Bellman equations, to link them more directly with the (controlled)
Multiplicative Poisson Equation
framework.

Our work is also linked to a variety of problems studied for Markov decision processes (see e.g.
[
3
]
) and recently studied continuous time risk sensitive problems with regime switching over finite time horizon, see
[
8
,
27
,
17
]
. In particular, we want to mention that the proof techniques presented in this paper are based on some novel ideas applied to
vanishing-discount
and
span-contraction
approaches, cf.
[
11
,
36
]
. For instance, we were able to weaken the typical assumption imposed on the negative value of the log-process, by replacing Schwarz’s inequality based approximation with a tail-based argument in one of the key steps of local contraction property proof, see Proposition
32
. Also, by incorportaing Arzela-Ascoli theorem into the vanishing discount approach, we were able to show the existence of a regular Bellman solution.

Apart from theoretical results, we present two numerical examples. They might be interesting to a reader who is not familiar with the risk sensitive stochastic control but wants to better understand why the study of Bellman equation could improve trading performance even in a very simplistic case. In particular, by using simple approximation schemes, one can directly recover no-action strategies that are important aspect of portfolio management in the presence of transaction costs, see
[
16
]
. This shows why the development of efficient risk sensitive approximation algorithms in the dynamic context can help to develop or benchmark trading strategies; see
[
23
,
2
,
9
,
1
]
where practical aspects linked to risk sensitive policy iteration algorithms are studied.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we provide the general setup, state the assumptions, and formulate suitable Bellman equations. Next, in Section
3
we focus on the discounted version of the problem, that paves the ground for the usage of the vanishing discount approach. In Section
4
we follow the vanishing discount approach in order to show the key results of this paper. Then, in Section

9

6

2
Problem formulation

Let
(\Omega,F,(F_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}},\mathbb{P})
be a discrete-time filtered probability space, where
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{N}
. Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
denote the number of available risky assets and let

S(t):=(S_{1}(t),\ldots,S_{d}(t))
denote the positive vector price process, where
S_{j}(t)
denotes the price of the
j
th risky asset at time
t\in\mathbb{T}
. For a given trading strategy, we use
N(t)=(N_{1}(t),\ldots,N_{d}(t))
to denote the portfolio asset volume vector at time
t
after the portfolio is rebalanced, i.e.
N_{i}(t)
denotes how much asset
S_{i}
we hold in our portfolio at time
t
after the rebalancing is executed. Also, we use

W(t-):=\langle N(t-),S(t)\rangle\quad\textrm{and}\quad W(t):=\langle N(t),S(t)\rangle,

(2)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the standard scalar product, to denote portfolio wealth process at time
t
before and after the rebalancing, respectively. Throughout the paper we assume absence of short selling and follow the proportional transaction cost framework. This is partly encoded in the self-financing condition that is given by

W(t)=W(t-)-d((N(t)-N(t-1))\cdot S(t)),

(3)

where
(\cdot)
denotes vector point-wise product, function
d\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the proportional transaction cost penalty given by

d(x):=\langle c,[x]^{+}\rangle+\langle h,[x]^{-}\rangle,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

for a fixed cost rates
c,h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
0<c_{j},h_{j}<1
,
j=1,2,\ldots,d
, and
[x]^{\pm}
denotes (component-wise) positive/negative part of
x
. To ease the notation, we introduce portfolio loading factors (portions of the capital invested in the assets, weights) vectors given by

\pi(t):=\frac{N(t)\cdot S(t)}{W(t)}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\pi(t-):=\frac{N(t-1)\cdot S(t)}{W(t-)}.

(4)

Note that due to the absence of short selling, for any
t\in\mathbb{T}
and
\omega\in\Omega
, we have
\pi(t)(\omega),\pi(t-)(\omega)\in{\cal S}
, where
{\cal{S}}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{j}\geq 0;\langle x,1\rangle=1\right\}
. Before we introduce the objective function, let us present a short technical lemma which shows that capital decay
W(t)/W(t-)
can be expressed as a function of factor loadings.

Lemma 1
.

There is
\tilde{s}\in(0,1)
and continuous function
s:{\cal S}^{2}\mapsto[\tilde{s},1]
such that

\frac{W(t)}{W(t-)}=s(\pi(t-),\pi(t)),\quad t\in\mathbb{T}.

Proof.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\cal S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function given by
F(w,x,y):=w+d(wy-x)
and let
\tilde{e}:=\min_{j=1,\ldots,d}h_{j}
. Noting that
F
is continuous, strictly increasing in
w
,
\tilde{e}\leq F(0,x,y)\leq\max_{j=1,\ldots,d}h_{j}<1
, and
F(1,x,y)>1
, we know that there exists function
s\colon{\cal S}^{2}\to[\tilde{s},1]
such that
F\left(s(\pi(t-),\pi(t)),\pi(t-),\pi(t)\right)=1
. On the other hand, using self-financing condition (
3
) we get

F\left(\tfrac{W(t)}{W(t-)},\pi(t-),\pi(t)\right)=\tfrac{W(t)}{W(t-)}+d\left(\tfrac{W(t)}{W(t-)}\pi(t)-\pi(t-)\right)=1.

Since
F
is strictly increasing with respect to
w
we know that
s(\pi(t-),\pi(t))=\tfrac{W(t)}{W(t-)}
. It remains to show that
s
is continuous. Let
{\cal S}\ni\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime}
be such that
\pi_{n}\to\pi
and
\pi_{n}^{\prime}\to\pi^{\prime}
, as
n\to\infty
. Recalling that
F
is continuous, strictly increasing in its first argument and satisfies
F(s(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime}),\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime})=1
as well as
F(s(\pi,\pi^{\prime}),\pi,\pi^{\prime})=1
, we conclude that for any subsequence
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
s(\pi_{n_{k}},\pi_{n_{k}}^{\prime})\to\bar{s}
, for some
\bar{s}\in[\tilde{s},1]
, we get
\bar{s}=s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})
. Since the same limit
s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})
is achieved for any subsequence
(n_{k})
, we get continuity of
s
.

From Lemma
1
we see that the trading strategy could be represented via the loading factors (
4
). For any given
{\cal{S}}
-valued (adapted) strategy
\pi
we use
W_{\pi}
to denote the corresponding wealth process.

The main goal of this paper is to find strategy
\pi
that maximizes long run risk sensitive objective function, applied to log-wealth process. Namely, we fix a risk-sensitivity parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and consider the objective function given by

\displaystyle J(\pi)

\displaystyle:=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathbb{E}\left[W_{\pi}(n-)^{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mu^{\gamma}(\ln W_{\pi}(n-))}{n}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mu^{\gamma}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\ln\frac{W_{\pi}((t+1)-)}{W_{\pi}(t-)}\right),

(5)

where
\mu^{\gamma}(X):=\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathbb{E}[e^{\gamma X}]
is the entropic utility function; for consistency, we also use limit notation
\mu^{0}(X):=\mathbb{E}[X]
. Note that (
5
) is measuring time averaged entropy of portfolio’s log-return; see
[
7
]
for the economical context.

Since we are interested in optimising portfolio’s log-growth, throughout this paper we assume that the assets log-return vector
r(t)=(r_{i}(t))_{i=1}^{d}
, where
r_{i}(t):=\ln\tfrac{S_{i}(t)}{S_{i}(t-1)}
, is an i.i.d. vector satisfying conditions

\mu^{\gamma}\left(r_{i}(t)\right)\in\mathbb{R}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i}(t)\right]\in\mathbb{R}\quad\textrm{for }i=1,2,\ldots,d,

(A.1)

which means that log-returns are integrable and have finite entropy for the prefixed risk sensitive parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Remark 2
.

From assumption (
A.1
), using monotonicity of entropic risk with respect to risk-sensitivity parameter, we get that for any
\delta
between
\gamma
and
0
, we have
\mu^{\delta}(r_{i}(t))\in\mathbb{R}
. Also, note that assumption (
A.1
) could be rephrased using non-entropy notation as
\mathbb{E}[e^{\gamma r_{i}(t)}]\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{E}[r_{i}(t)]\in\mathbb{R}
, which could be linked to log-returns moment generating function finiteness.

For transparency, we also introduce an asset relative shift process
w(t)=(w_{i}(t))_{i=1}^{d}
given by

w(t):=e^{r(t)}=\left(\frac{S_{1}(t)}{S_{1}(t-1)},\ldots,\frac{S_{d}(t)}{S_{d}(t-1)}\right).

Let us now show how to re-express inner part of
J(\pi)
as a
\pi
-controlled process. Let
G\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be given by
G(x,y):=x\cdot y/\langle x,y\rangle
. Noting that

\pi(t-)=\frac{\pi(t-1)\cdot w(t)}{\langle\pi(t-1),w(t)\rangle}=G(\pi(t-1),w(t)),

and rewriting the objective criterion (
5
) as

\displaystyle J(\pi)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mu^{\gamma}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\ln\frac{W_{\pi}((t+1)-)}{W_{\pi}(t)}\frac{W_{\pi}(t)}{W_{\pi}(t-)}\right)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mu^{\gamma}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\ln\frac{\langle N_{\pi}(t),S(t+1)\rangle}{W_{\pi}(t)}+\ln(s(\pi(t-),\pi(t)))\right)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mu^{\gamma}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\ln\frac{\langle N_{\pi}(t),w(t+1)\cdot S(t)\rangle}{W_{\pi}(t)}+\ln(s(\pi(t-),\pi(t)))\right)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mu^{\gamma}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\ln\langle\pi(t),w(t+1)\rangle+\ln(s(G(\pi(t-1),w(t)),\pi(t)))\right),

(6)

we essentially get direct restatement of the controlled log-wealth process using control process
\pi
and independent shifts
w
.

In order to solve (
6
), we introduce the associated Bellman equation. Ideally, given
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{*}:=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, we are looking for a function
v:{\cal S}\to\mathbb{R}
and a constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
which satisfy equation

\lambda+v(\pi)=\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\mu^{\gamma}\Big(\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})+v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)))\Big)\right],

(7)

for
\pi\in\mathcal{S}
. Note that, with a slight abuse of notation, in (
7
) we use
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
in reference to a deterministic pre-rebalancing and post-rebalancing weights rather than the whole rebalancing strategy; this convention is often used in the paper when optimality equations are considered.

For technical reasons, instead of considering (
7
) directly, in this paper we consider its slightly modified version given by

\lambda+v(\pi,\gamma)=\gamma\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\mu^{\gamma}\Big(\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})+\gamma^{-1}v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma)\Big)\right].

(8)

where
v:{\cal S}\times\mathbb{R}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, we use
v(\pi,\gamma)
instead of
v(\pi)
to emphasize the dependency between risk-parameter choice and Bellman’s equation solution, and to embed (
7
) into vanishing discount framework. Note that if
v(\cdot,\gamma)
and
\lambda
solves (
8
), then
\gamma^{-1}v(\cdot,\gamma)
and
\gamma^{-1}\lambda
solves (
7
). Also, Bellman’s equation (
8
) could be directly restated in a more classical form. Namely, for the risk-averse case
\gamma<0
we can rephrase (
8
) as

e^{v(\pi,\gamma)}=\inf_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}e^{\gamma\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\left(\ln(\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle)-\lambda\right)+v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma)}\right].

(9)

while for the risk-seeking case
\gamma>0
we get

e^{v(\pi,\gamma)}=\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}e^{\gamma\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\left(\ln(\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle)-\lambda\right)+v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma)}\right].

(10)

For transparency, if not stated otherwise, we use
v
in reference to equation (
8
).

In the following sections, we study the existence of the solution to the initial problem and its link to Bellman’s equation (
7
). For risk-averse case
\gamma<0
, we will show that under general assumption (
A.1
) and can solve the recursive version of (
7
) in order to get the optimal constant and optimal strategy. Moreover, by imposing additional condition on
w
that is related to mixing, one can show that (
7
) could be directly solved without relying on the recursive scheme. On the other hand, for risk-seeking case
\gamma>0
assumption (
A.1
) alone imply existence of solution to (
7
).

For completeness, we will also show that selectors to the Bellman equation determine an optimal strategy in both cases. As already said, the results will be obtained using both vanishing discount approach as well as span-contraction approach.

3
Discounted problem

Before we apply the vanishing discount approach, let us provide a few remarks for the discounted version of problem (
5
). Consider
\alpha>0
and the discounted risk sensitive objective problem given by

\sup_{\pi}\tilde{J}_{\alpha}(\pi)=\sup_{\pi}\mu^{\gamma}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha t}\left[\ln(\langle\pi(t),w(t+1)\rangle)+\ln(s(\pi(t-),\pi(t))\right]\right).

The associated discounted analogue of Bellman equation (
8
) is given by

v_{\alpha}(\pi,\gamma)=\gamma\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\mu^{\gamma}\Big(\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})+\gamma^{-1}v_{\alpha}(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma e^{-\alpha})\Big)\right].

(11)

Note that (
11
) is in fact linked to a series of equations which effectively should provide the formula for
(v_{\alpha}(\pi,\gamma e^{-n\alpha}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Nevertheless, for simplicity, we are looking for a stronger condition, i.e. a function
v_{\alpha}
that satisfies (
11
) for any value of risk sensitive parameter between
\gamma
and 0. In other words, we want (
11
) to hold on
\mathcal{S}\times\Gamma
, for

\Gamma:=[\gamma_{-},\gamma_{+}]\setminus\{0\},

where
\gamma_{-}:=\min\{0,\gamma\}
and
\gamma_{+}:=\max\{0,\gamma\}
. Nevertheless, with slight abuse of notation, if no ambiguity arise, we often use
\gamma
to denote a generic choice from
\Gamma
.

As before, Equation (
11
) could be rephrased in a classical way, i.e. for
\gamma<0
we can restate (
11
) as

e^{v_{\alpha}(\pi,\gamma)}=\inf_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}e^{\gamma\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\ln(\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle)+v_{\alpha}(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma e^{-\alpha})}\right]

while for
\gamma>0
we can rewrite (
11
) as

e^{v_{\alpha}(\pi,\gamma)}=\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}e^{\gamma\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\ln(\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle)+v_{\alpha}(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma e^{-\alpha})}\right].

Let us now introduce a lemma that will be helpful for establishing existence of solutions to Bellman’s equation (
8
). For brevity, we introduce supplementary notation

\displaystyle z_{\gamma}(\pi^{\prime},\pi)

\displaystyle:=\mu^{\gamma}\left(\ln\langle\pi,w(1)\rangle+\ln s(\pi^{\prime},\pi)\right),

\displaystyle z^{-}

\displaystyle:=-\left|\min_{i}\mu^{\gamma}(r_{i}(1))\right|-d\max_{i}\mathbb{E}|r_{i}(1)|-\tfrac{\ln d}{|\gamma|}+\ln\tilde{s},

\displaystyle z^{+}

\displaystyle:=\phantom{-}\left|\max_{i}\mu^{\gamma}(r_{i}(1))\right|+d\max_{i}\mathbb{E}|r_{i}(1)|+\tfrac{\ln d}{|\gamma|}.

Note that
z^{-}
and
z^{+}
are finite due to (
A.1
).

Lemma 3
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and let us assume (
A.1
). Then, for any
\delta\in[\gamma_{-},\gamma_{+}]
and
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
we have
z^{-}\leq z_{\delta}(\pi^{\prime},\pi)\leq z^{+}
.

Proof.

First let us consider the case where
\gamma<0
and
\delta\in[\gamma,0]
. Using monotonicity and translation invariance of entropic utility we get

\displaystyle z_{\delta}(\pi^{\prime},\pi)

\displaystyle\geq\mu^{\delta}\left(\ln\langle\pi,w(1)\rangle)\right)+\ln\tilde{s}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq\mu^{\gamma}\left(\min_{i}r_{i}(1)\right)+\ln\tilde{s}

\displaystyle\textstyle=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\ln\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i}e^{\gamma r_{i}(1)}\right]+\ln\tilde{s}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\ln\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma r_{i}(1)}\right]\right)+\ln\tilde{s}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq\min_{i}\frac{1}{\gamma}\ln\left[d\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma r_{i}(1)}\right]\right)+\ln\tilde{s}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq-\left|\min_{i}\mu^{\gamma}(r_{i}(1))\right|-\frac{\ln d}{|\gamma|}+\ln\tilde{s}

(12)

and

\displaystyle\textstyle z_{\delta}(\pi^{\prime},\pi)

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq\mu^{\delta}\left(\ln\langle\pi,w(1)\rangle)\right)+\ln 1

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq\mu^{0}\left(\max_{i}r_{i}(1)\right)

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq d\max_{i}\mathbb{E}|r_{i}(1)|.

(13)

Now, for
\gamma>0
and
\delta\in[0,\gamma]
, using similar calculations, we get

\displaystyle\textstyle z_{\delta}(\pi^{\prime},\pi)

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq-d\max_{i}\mathbb{E}|r_{i}(1)|+\ln\tilde{s},

\displaystyle\textstyle z_{\delta}(\pi^{\prime},\pi)

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq\left|\max_{i}\mu^{\gamma}(r_{i}(1))\right|+\frac{\ln d}{|\gamma|}.

(14)

Combining (
12
), (
13
), and (
14
) we conclude the proof.

We are now ready to present the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and let us assume (
A.1
). Then, for each
\alpha>0
, there exists a continuous and bounded function
v_{\alpha}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
, that is a solution to discounted Bellman’s equation (
11
).

Proof.

For brevity we only show the proof for
\gamma<0
; the proof for
\gamma>0
is analogous. Fix
\alpha>0
and consider the Bellman operator linked to (
11
) that is given by

Tv(\pi,\gamma):=\gamma\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\mu^{\gamma}\left(\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})+\gamma^{-1}v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma e^{-\alpha})\right)\right],

for any
v\colon\mathcal{S}\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
. First, let us show that
T
is
C
-Feller, i.e. it transforms bounded and continuous functions into themselves. Using Lemma
3
we immediately get

\|Tv\|_{\textrm{sup}}\leq|\gamma|\cdot[z^{+}-z^{-}]+\|v\|_{\textrm{sup}},

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the standard supremum norm, which shows that boundedness is preserved. Now, let us show that continuity is also preserved for continuous bounded functions. First, since
v
is continuous, for any sequence
((\pi^{\prime}_{n},\gamma_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
\pi^{\prime}_{n}\in\mathcal{S}
and
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma
, satisfying
(\pi^{\prime}_{n},\gamma_{n})\to(\pi^{\prime},\gamma)
, we get

e^{\gamma_{n}\ln\langle\pi_{n}^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+v(G(\pi_{n}^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma_{n}e^{-\alpha}))}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}e^{\gamma\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma e^{-\alpha}))}.

Now, using similar reasoning as in Lemma

e^{\gamma_{n}\ln\langle\pi_{n}^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+v(G(\pi_{n}^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma_{n}e^{-\alpha}))}\leq e^{\gamma\min_{i}r_{i}(1)+\|v\|_{\textrm{sup}}}.

Thus, noting that
e^{\gamma\min_{i}r_{i}(1)+\|v\|_{\textrm{sup}}}\in L^{1}
due to (

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma_{n}\ln\langle\pi_{n}^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+v(G(\pi_{n}^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma_{n}e^{-\alpha}))}\right]\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma e^{-\alpha}))}\right],

which in turn implies continuity of the mapping

(\pi,\pi^{\prime},\gamma)\to\gamma\left[\mu^{\gamma}\left(\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})+\gamma^{-1}v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)),\gamma e^{-\alpha})\right)\right].

Consequently, combining (

Theorem 8

Proof.

Fix

5
Span-contraction approach

In Section
4
we have shown that for
\gamma>0
one can solve directly Bellman equation (
8
); see Theorem
9
. On the other hand, for
\gamma<0
, we were only able to obtain the recursive scheme as presented in Theorem
9
. In this section, we show that the solution to (
8
) exists also for
\gamma<0
under relatively weak ergodic assumptions imposed on asset log-returns. We follow the span-contraction approach; see e.g.
[
34
]
. In the span-contraction approach the parameter
\gamma<0
is kept fixed in a sense that we do not need to introduce the discounting scheme. Consequently, to ease the exposition, rather than using notation from (
8
), we revert to the one from (
7
): we fix one
\gamma<0
and write
v(\pi)
rather than
v(\pi,\gamma)
. As usual, we use
B(\mathcal{S})
to denote the space of continuous and bounded functions
v\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
. For any
v\in B(\mathcal{S})
we introduce supremum norm and span semi-norm notation

\|v\|:=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}}|v(\pi)|\quad\textrm{and}\quad\|v\|_{\textrm{sp}}:=\sup_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{v(\pi)-v(\pi^{\prime})}{2}.

Note that those norms are bound by relation

\inf_{d\in\mathbb{R}}\|v+d\|=\|v\|_{\textrm{sp}},

(31)

see
[
34
]
or
[
26
]
for details. Now, we introduce additional assumption that relates to ergodicity and plays a central role in the span-contraction approach. Namely, for any
\delta\in(0,1/d)
, we assume that

\sup_{A\in\mathcal{B}(S)}\sup_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}\Big(\mathbb{P}[G(\pi,w(1))\in A]-\mathbb{P}[G(\pi^{\prime},w(1))\in A]\Big)<1,

(A.2)

where
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}:=\{\pi\in S\colon\min_{i}\pi_{i}\geq\delta\}
identifies a set of strategies in which we allocate at least
\delta
proportion of capital to each asset. This assumption is related to mixing and states that whatever our initial (non-degenerated) allocation is, we expect to be in some common set with positive probability.

Remark 10
(Ergodicity/mixing assumption relevance).

Recalling that
G(x,y)=x\cdot y/\langle x,y\rangle
, for any
\pi\in S
we get
G(\pi,w(1))=\frac{\pi\cdot w(1)}{\langle\pi,w(1)\rangle}
which shows that (
A.2
) is in fact related to assumptions imposed on
w(1)
. Nevertheless, we decided to present (
A.2
) in its classical form, to show the connection to mixing. One can show that assumption (
A.2
) is satisfied by any log Levy process, even with ergodic economic factors, see
[
22
, Proposition 1]
for details. Also, one can notice that if
r(1)
has full support, then the assumption (
A.2
) is automatically satisfied.

Now, for any
\delta\in(0,1/d)
we introduce operator

T_{\delta}v(\pi):=\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}}\left[\gamma\mu^{\gamma}\Big(\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})+\gamma^{-1}v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)))\Big)\right],\quad v\in B(\mathcal{S}).

It is relatively easy to show that operator
T_{\delta}
is
C
-Feller. In particular, using similar reasoning as in Lemma
6
, for any
v\in B(\mathcal{S})
we get

\|T_{\delta}v\|_{\textrm{sp}}\leq|\gamma|\sup_{\pi,\pi^{\prime},\tilde{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}}\ln\left[\ \frac{s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})}{s(\tilde{\pi},\pi^{\prime})}\right]\leq-|\gamma|\ln\tilde{s}:=K,

(32)

which implies boundedness of
T_{\delta}v
, for any
v\in B(\mathcal{S})
. Let us now show that
T_{\delta}
is a local contraction.

Proposition 11
.

Let
\gamma<0
and let us assume (
A.1
) and (
A.2
). Then, for each
\delta\in(0,1\d{)}
, the operator
T_{\delta}
is a local contraction under
\|\cdot\|_{\textrm{sp}}
, i.e. there exists
L_{\delta}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to(0,1)
such that

\|T_{\delta}v_{1}-T_{\delta}v_{2}\|_{\textrm{sp}}\leq L_{\delta}(M)\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{\textrm{sp}},

for
v_{1},v_{2}\in C(\mathcal{S})
, such that
\|v_{1}\|\leq M
and
\|v_{2}\|\leq M
.

Proof.

Let us fix
\delta\in(0,1/d)
. For any
v\in B(\mathcal{S})
and
\pi\in\mathcal{S}
let

\bar{\mu}_{(\pi,v)}(B):=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[1_{B}(G(\pi,w(1))e^{\gamma\ln\langle\pi,w(1)\rangle+v(G(\pi,w(1)))}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\ln\langle\pi,w(1)\rangle+v(G(\pi,w(1)))}\right]},\quad B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

denote the projection measure for
v
with rebalancing
\pi
, and let

\pi_{v}:=\arg\max_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}\left[\gamma\mu^{\gamma}\Big(\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})+\gamma^{-1}v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)))\Big)\right]

denote the optimal rebalancing (induced by operator
T_{\delta}
) given
v\in C(S)
and initial state
\pi\in\mathcal{S}
. First, let us show that for any
v_{1},v_{2}\in C(\mathcal{S})
and
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
we get

T_{\delta}v_{1}(\pi)-T_{\delta}v_{2}(\pi)-(T_{\delta}v_{1}(\pi^{\prime})-T_{\delta}v_{2}(\pi^{\prime}))\leq\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{\textrm{sp}}\cdot\|\mathbb{H}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}^{v_{1},v_{2}}\|_{\textrm{var}},

(33)

where
\mathbb{H}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}^{v_{1},v_{2}}:=\bar{\mu}_{(\pi_{v_{2}},v_{1})}-\bar{\mu}_{(\pi^{\prime}_{v_{1}},v_{2})}
is a signed measure and
\|\cdot\|_{\textrm{var}}
is the total variation norm given by

\|\mathbb{H}\|_{\textrm{var}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbb{H}|(dx),

for
|\mathbb{H}|
being the total variation of
\mathbb{H}
; see
[
34
]
for details. Using dual representation for entropic risk, and performing similar calculations as in
[
34
, Lemma 1]
we get

T_{\delta}v_{1}(\pi)-T_{\delta}v_{2}(\pi)-(T_{\delta}v_{1}(\pi^{\prime})-T_{\delta}v_{2}(\pi^{\prime}))\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[v_{1}(x)-v_{2}(x)\right]\,\mathbb{H}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}^{v_{1},v_{2}}(dx).

Now, recalling that for any
v\in B(\mathcal{S})
we have
\inf_{d\in\mathbb{R}}\|v+d\|=\|v\|_{\textrm{sp}}
, we know there exists
d\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{\textrm{sp}}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(v_{1}(x)-v_{2}(x)+d)=-\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(v_{1}(x)-v_{2}(x)+d)

Consequently, for
A
denoting the positive set of measure
\mathbb{H}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}^{v_{1},v_{2}}
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[v_{1}(x)-v_{2}(x)\right]\,\mathbb{H}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}^{v_{1},v_{2}}(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[v_{1}(x)-v_{2}(x)+d\right]\,\mathbb{H}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}^{v_{1},v_{2}}(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{\textrm{sp}}\left(\int_{A}\mathbb{H}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}^{v_{1},v_{2}}(dx)-\int_{A^{c}}\mathbb{H}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}^{v_{1},v_{2}}(dx)\right)

\displaystyle=\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{\textrm{sp}}\cdot\|\mathbb{H}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}^{v_{1},v_{2}}\|_{\textrm{var}},

which concludes this step of the proof. Now, let us fix
M\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and show that there exists constant
L(M)\in(0,1)
such that for any
v_{1},v_{2}\in C(\mathcal{S})
satisfying
\|v_{1}\|\leq M
and
\|v_{2}\|\leq M
, and
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
, we get

\|\mathbb{H}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}^{v_{1},v_{2}}\|_{\textrm{var}}\leq 2L(M).

(34)

Suppose that (
34
) is not satisfied. Recalling that

\|\mathbb{H}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}^{v_{1},v_{2}}\|_{\textrm{var}}=2\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}|\bar{\mu}_{(\pi_{v_{2}},v_{1})}(B)-\bar{\mu}_{(\pi^{\prime}_{v_{1}},v_{2})}(B)|,

we get that there exists a sequence of sets
B_{n}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, functions
v_{n},v_{n}^{\prime}\in B(\mathcal{S})
satisfying
\|v_{n}\|\leq M
and
\|v^{\prime}_{n}\|\leq M
, and weights
\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
such that

\bar{\mu}_{(\pi_{v^{\prime}_{n}},v_{n})}(B_{n})\to 1\quad\textrm{and}\quad\bar{\mu}_{(\pi_{v_{n}},v^{\prime}_{n})}(B_{n})\to 0,\quad\textrm{as }n\to\infty.

(35)

Now, let
r_{+}(1):=\max_{i}r_{i}(1)
and
r_{-}(1):=\min_{i}r_{i}(1)
. For any
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\pi\in\mathcal{S}
and
v\in B(\mathcal{S})
, we get

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{(\pi,v)}(B)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[1_{B}(G(\pi,w(1))e^{\gamma\ln\langle\pi,w(1)\rangle+v(G(\pi,w(1)))}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\ln\langle\pi,w(1)\rangle+v(G(\pi,w(1)))}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[1_{B}(G(\pi,w(1))e^{\gamma\ln\langle\pi,w(1)\rangle+\inf_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}v(\pi)}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\ln\langle\pi,w(1)\rangle+\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}v(\pi^{\prime})}\right]}

\displaystyle=e^{-2\|v\|_{\textrm{sp}}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[1_{B}(G(\pi,w(1)))e^{\gamma\ln\langle\pi,w(1)\rangle}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma\ln\langle\pi,w(1)\rangle}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-2\|v\|_{\textrm{sp}}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\{G(\pi,w(1))\in B\}}e^{\gamma r_{+}(1)}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma r_{-}(1)}\right]}.

(36)

Let us now assume there exists
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, such that
\mathbb{E}[1_{B}(G(\pi,w(1)))]>\epsilon
and let

, where

is the generalised (lower) quantile function of

. First, if there exists
\epsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1)
such that
\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\epsilon
and

, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\{G(\pi,w(1))\in B\}}e^{\gamma r_{+}(1)}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\{r_{+}(1)\geq R(\epsilon^{\prime})\}}e^{\gamma r_{+}(1)}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\{R(\epsilon^{\prime}/2)\geq r_{+}(1)\geq R(\epsilon^{\prime})\}}e^{\gamma r_{+}(1)}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\epsilon^{\prime}/2\cdot e^{\gamma R(\epsilon^{\prime}/2)}.

(37)

Second, let us assume there is no
\epsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1)
such that
\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\epsilon
and
\mathbb{P}[\{r_{+}(1)\geq R(\epsilon^{\prime})\}]\leq\epsilon
. Then, we have
\mathbb{P}[\{r_{+}(1)\geq R(\epsilon)\}]=\mathbb{P}[\{r_{+}(1)=R(\epsilon)\}]
and consequently, for
\epsilon^{\prime}=2\epsilon
, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\{G(\pi,w(1))\in B\}}e^{\gamma r_{+}(1)}\right]\geq e^{\gamma R(\epsilon)}\mathbb{P}\left[G(\pi,w(1))\in B\right]\geq\epsilon^{\prime}/2\cdot e^{\gamma R(\epsilon^{\prime}/2)}.

(38)

Combining (
36
), (
37
) and (
38
), for any
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\pi\in\mathcal{S}
,
v\in B(\mathcal{S})
, and
\epsilon>0
, such that
\mathbb{E}[1_{B}(G(\pi,w(1)))]>\epsilon
, there exists
\epsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1)
such that

\bar{\mu}_{(\pi,v)}(B)\geq e^{-2\|v\|_{\textrm{sp}}}\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}/2\cdot e^{\gamma R(\epsilon^{\prime}/2)}}{e^{\gamma\cdot\left[(1/\gamma)\ln\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma r_{-}(1)}\right]\right]}}\geq e^{-2\|v\|_{\textrm{sp}}}\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}/2\cdot e^{\gamma R(\epsilon^{\prime}/2)}}{e^{\gamma\mu^{\gamma}(r_{-}(1))}}.

(39)

From (
A.1
), both
\mu^{\gamma}(r_{-}(1))
and

are finite for any
\epsilon>0
. Also, note that

is increasing and the choice of
\epsilon^{\prime}
in (

) depends only on the choice of
\epsilon
, i.e. given condition

is independent of the choice of

,

and

. Consequently, combining (

) with (

), recalling that
\|v_{n}\|\leq M
and
\|v^{\prime}_{n}\|\leq M
, for

, we get
\mathbb{E}\left[1_{B^{\prime}_{n}}(G(\pi_{v^{\prime}_{n}},w(1)))\right]\to 0
and
\mathbb{E}\left[1_{B_{n}}(G(\pi_{v_{n}},w(1)))\right]\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
. Therefore, as

, we have

\mathbb{P}[G(\pi_{v^{\prime}_{n}},w(1))\in B_{n}]-\mathbb{P}[G(\pi_{v_{n}},w(1))\in B_{n}]\to 1.

(40)

Noting that
\pi_{v_{n}},\pi_{v^{\prime}_{n}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
, we get that (

A.2

Theorem 12
.

Let
\gamma<0
and let us assume (
A.1
) and (
A.2
). Then, there exists a constant
\lambda
and a continuous bounded functions
v(\cdot,\gamma)\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
that solves (
8
), i.e. we get

v(\pi)+\lambda=\gamma\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\bigg[\mu^{\gamma}\Big(\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})+\gamma^{-1}v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)))\Big)\bigg].

Moreover, the constant
\Lambda:=\lambda/\gamma

is the optimal value for the problem (
5
), i.e. we get
\Lambda=\sup_{\pi}J(\pi)
, and the optimal strategy is defined by the selectors to the Bellman equation.

Proof.

For any fixed
\delta\in(0,1/d)
, combining (
32
) with Theorem
12
we get that there exists a unique (up to an additive constant) function
v_{\delta}\in B(S)
and a constant
\lambda_{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

v_{\delta}(\pi)+\lambda_{\delta}=\gamma\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}\bigg[\mu^{\gamma}\Big(\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})+\gamma^{-1}v_{\delta}(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)))\Big)\bigg].

(41)

Now, let
\hat{\pi}:=(1/d,\ldots,1/d)
and
\bar{v}_{\delta}(\pi):=v_{\delta}(\pi)-v_{\delta}(\hat{\pi})
for
\delta\in(0,1/d)
; note that
\bar{v}_{\delta}
also solves (
41
) and we get
\|\bar{v}_{\delta}\|<2K
due to (
32
) and property
\bar{v}_{\delta}(\hat{\pi})=0
. Thus, the family of functions
\{\bar{v}_{\delta}\}_{\delta\in(0,1/d)}
is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous since, by (
41
), we get

v_{\delta}(\pi)-v_{\delta}(\bar{\pi})\leq|\gamma|\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}\left|\ln{s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\over s(\bar{\pi},\pi^{\prime})}\right|.

(42)

Consequently, using Arzela-Ascoli theorem, we know that there exists a function
v\in B(\mathcal{S})
and a subsequence
(\delta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\delta_{n}\searrow 0
, such that
v_{\delta_{n}}\to v
(uniformly) and
\lambda_{\delta_{n}}\to\lambda
, as
n\to\infty
. Thus, due to uniform convergence, we get

v(\pi)+\lambda=\gamma\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}}\bigg[\mu^{\gamma}\Big(\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})+\gamma^{-1}v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)))\Big)\bigg],

(43)

where
\mathcal{S}_{0}:=\bigcup_{\delta\in(0,1/d)}S_{\delta}
. Now, since right hand side of (
43
) is a continuous function of
\pi^{\prime}
, we also get

v(\pi)+\lambda=\gamma\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\bigg[\mu^{\gamma}\Big(\ln\langle\pi^{\prime},w(1)\rangle+\ln s(\pi,\pi^{\prime})+\gamma^{-1}v(G(\pi^{\prime},w(1)))\Big)\bigg],

which concludes the proof.

From Figure

While the variance for Markowitz strategy is slightly smaller, the Markowitz allocation leads to smaller mean – the payoff between the two is better for risk-sensitive strategy as could be seen by looking into both Entropy and

performance criterions. To better understand the difference between risk-sensitive strategy and Markowitz strategy it is best to look into trading intensity. While the Markowitz strategy has homogeneous trading intensity (as expected, as the strategy should always push the allocation back to the fixed point), the risk-sensitive strategy shows more intense trading on rare occasions, i.e. when the process falls outside of the zone presented in Figure
5
. In fact, while the trading for Markowitz strategy was initiated for almost all of the considered days, the trading for risk-sensitive strategy was initiated in only 176 days (ca. 3.5% of sample size). Moreover, the aggregated trading intensity for risk-sensitive strategy, measured e.g. by cumulative product of capital decays, is much smaller. This shows that intense re-balancing could in fact negatively impact performance, especially in the transaction cost regime.
