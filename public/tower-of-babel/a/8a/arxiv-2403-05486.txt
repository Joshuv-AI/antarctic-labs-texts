Confronting the Lippmann-Schwinger equation and the N / D N/D method for coupled-wave separable potentials
M. S. Sánchez, J. A. Oller, D. R. Entem

Abstract
We study a family of separable potentials with and without added contact interactions by solving the associated Lippmann-Schwinger equation with two coupled partial waves. The matching of the resulting amplitude matrix with the effective-range expansion is studied in detail. When a counterterm is included in the potential we also carefully discuss its renormalization. Next, we use the matrix N / D N/D method and study whether the amplitude matrices from the potentials considered admit an N / D N/D representation in matrix form. As a novel result we show that it is typically not possible to find such matrix representation for the coupled partial-wave case. However, a separate N / D N/D representation for each coupled partial wave — a valid option known in the literature — is explicitly implemented and numerically solved in cases where the matrix N / D N/D method is unavailable.

\boldsymbol{N/D}

\boldsymbol{N(0)=1}

In the following two sections we will deduce the DRs fulfilled by the matrices

— on which we focus in the present section, deferring the

5
— happens to be more natural and simpler. The last part of the section will be devoted to rewrite the derived DRs in terms of scattering observables, namely the ERE parameters, and to justify from dimensional arguments why those observables should or should not be taken as inputs in this framework.

Nonexistence of a matrix
N/D
representation

Here we apply a similar rationale as in Sec.
6
to discuss the impossibility to find an
N/D
representation for the amplitude (
7.4
). Notice that this
N/D
representation exists if and only if there is an
N/D
form for the matrix

\displaystyle\bm{t}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}t_{00}(A,A;A)&&t_{02}(A,A;A)\\
t_{20}(A,A;A)&&t_{22}(A,A;A)\end{pmatrix}

(7.32)

[c.f. Eqs. (
7.5
)–(
7.7
)],
i.e. if and only if there are matrices
\bm{n}(A)
and
\bm{d}(A)
with only LHC and RHC, respectively, fulfilling

\displaystyle{\bm{n}}(A)\,{\bm{t}}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{d}}(A)\,.

(7.33)

As
\bm{t}(A)=\bm{t}(A)^{\top}
it follows that

\displaystyle\bm{t}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{n}(A)^{-1}\bm{d}(A)\quad\!\!\!=\!\!\!\quad\bm{d}(A)^{\top}\bm{n}(A)^{\top\,-1}\,,

(7.34)

which corresponds to an
N/D
decomposition for
\bm{t}(A)
.

We rewrite the
\bm{t}(A)
matrix elements
in the more compact form

\displaystyle\bm{t}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{0}\,+\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{4}}\,\alpha_{1}\,\widehat{g}_{0}\,+\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\alpha_{2}\,\widehat{g}_{0}^{\,2}&(A-L)^{\frac{1}{4}}\,\beta_{1}\,\widehat{g}_{2}\,+\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\beta_{2}\,\widehat{g}_{0}\,\widehat{g}_{2}\\
(A-L)^{\frac{1}{4}}\,\beta_{1}\,\widehat{g}_{2}\,+\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\beta_{2}\,\widehat{g}_{0}\,\widehat{g}_{2}&(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\xi_{2}\,\widehat{g}_{2}^{\,2}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(7.35)

where the
A
dependence of the cut-free function
\widehat{g}_{\ell}(A)\equiv(A-L)^{-{1}/{4}}g_{\ell}(A)=A^{\,\ell/2}/{(A+\Lambda_{0}^{2})^{\,1+\ell/2}}
, as well as the
A
dependence of the six functions denoted by a Greek letter, has been made implicit for brevity. On the other hand, combining Eqs. (
7.5
)–(
7.7
) with Eq. (
7.15
), one learns that:
a)

t_{02}(A,A;A)
is actually RHC-free;
b)
there are two RHC-free functions
\chi(A)
and
\zeta(A)
such that
t_{00}(A,A;A)=\chi(A)+\zeta(A)\,\mathcal{I}_{2}(A)
;

c)
conversely, the RHC structure of
t_{22}(A,A;A)
is more involved, stemming from terms both linear and quadratic in the loop integrals.

Our starting point is a parameterization of
\bm{n}(A)
that is similar to that used in Eq. (
6.10
) for
\bm{O}(A)
, say

\displaystyle\bm{n}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{00}(A)\,(A-L)^{\phi_{\,00}}&\eta_{02}(A)\,(A-L)^{\phi_{\,02}}\\
\eta_{20}(A)\,(A-L)^{\phi_{\,20}}&\eta_{22}(A)\,(A-L)^{\phi_{\,22}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(7.36)

with the constraint that all the
\eta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}(A)
functions must be free of cuts; in addition, Eq. (
7.34
) illustrates that

\displaystyle|\bm{n}(A)|

\displaystyle\neq

\displaystyle 0\,.

(7.37)

Then, from Eq. (
7.33
) we get the matrix element

\displaystyle d_{22}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(A-L)^{\frac{1}{4}+\phi_{20}}\beta_{1}(A)\eta_{20}(A)\widehat{g}_{2}(A)

(7.38)

\displaystyle+\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{20}}\beta_{2}(A)\eta_{20}(A)\widehat{g}_{0}(A)\widehat{g}_{2}(A)

\displaystyle+\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{22}}\xi_{2}(A)\eta_{22}(A)\widehat{g}_{2}(A)^{2}\,.

The two first exponents above differ in a non-integer number, so it is clear that the three exponents above cannot be integers at the same time. We thus distinguish three mutually exclusive scenarios:

•

None of the exponents is an integer.
Only two mutually exclusive options are compatible with this case:

–

Each of the exponents is different from the two others.

Then, the fact that Eq. (
7.38
) is LHC-free leads to
\eta_{20}(A)=\eta_{22}(A)=0
, which is in turn in contradiction with Eq. (
7.37
).

–

Two exponents are equal and different from the other.

Suppose e.g. that
\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{22}=\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{20}
. Then, from Eq. (
7.38
) being LHC-free it follows that
\eta_{22}(A)\,=\,-\beta_{2}(A)\,\eta_{20}(A)\,\widehat{g}_{0}(A)\,/\,[\xi_{2}(A)\,\widehat{g}_{2}(A)]
, where
\eta_{22}(A)
is RHC-free by definition. However, the piece
\beta_{2}(A)/\xi_{2}(A)
cannot be RHC-free, since the RHC structure is different for
\beta_{2}(A)
and
\xi_{2}(A)
, as discussed above. A similar contradiction emerges if the equality between exponents involves
\frac{1}{4}+\phi_{20}
.

•

Only one exponent is an integer.
Three possibilities are to be considered:

–

The first exponent
\frac{1}{4}+\phi_{20}
is an integer; the second and the third exponents are non-integers.

\diamond

If
\,\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{20}\neq\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{22}
then
\eta_{20}(A)=\eta_{22}(A)=0
. This is in contradiction with Eq. (
7.37
).

\diamond

If
\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{20}=\!\frac{1}{2}\!+\phi_{22}
then
\eta_{22}(A)\,=\,-\beta_{2}(A)\,\eta_{20}(A)\,\widehat{g}_{0}(A)\,/\,[\xi_{2}(A)\,\widehat{g}_{2}(A)]
. As discussed above, this result is incompatible with
\eta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}(A)
being cut-free.

–

The second exponent
\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{20}
is an integer; the first and the third exponents are non-integers.

\diamond

This case is analogous to the one just discussed.

–

The third exponent
\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{22}
is an integer; the first and the second exponents are non-integers.

\diamond

Here,
\eta_{20}(A)=0
.
We can consider the expression for
d_{20}(A)
coming out from Eq. (
7.33
), which taking
\eta_{20}(A)=0
becomes
d_{20}(A)=\widehat{g}_{2}(A)\eta_{22}(A)[(A-L)^{\frac{1}{4}+\phi_{22}}\beta_{1}(A)+(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{22}}\beta_{2}(A)\widehat{g}_{0}(A)]
. While the second exponent within this square bracket is an integer by hypothesis, the first exponent is non-integer, which in turns implies that
\eta_{22}(A)=0
given that
d_{20}(A)
is LHC-free.
In this way we conclude that
\eta_{20}(A)=\eta_{22}(A)=0
, again in contradiction with Eq. (
7.37
).

•

Only two exponents are integers.

In such a situation the term involving the unequal exponent implies that either
\eta_{20}(A)
or
\eta_{22}(A)
is zero. Once such a constraint is imposed on
d_{20}(A)
, one can easily conclude the other
\eta_{2\ell}(A)
function must vanish as well. [In the point above we made this reasoning from the
\eta_{20}(A)=0
condition. Analogous steps would follow starting from
\eta_{22}(A)=0
.]

Thus, a function
\bm{n}(A)
of the type of Eq. (
7.36
) cannot give rise to an
N/D
representation of
\bm{t}(A)
.

Yet one could try parameterizations for
\bm{n}(A)
that are more general than Eq. (
7.36
), namely

\displaystyle\eta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{m}=1}^{\mathfrak{n}_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}}\eta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}^{(\mathfrak{m})}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\phi_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}^{(\mathfrak{m})}}\,.

(7.39)

Indeed, given that
t_{00}(A,A;A)
,
t_{02}(A,A;A)
, and
t_{22}(A,A;A)
contain respectively 3, 2, and 1 different exponents of
(A-L)
[c.f. Eq. (
7.35
)], the most general structure to be considered would require to take
\mathfrak{n}_{00}=\mathfrak{n}_{20}=3
and
\mathfrak{n}_{02}=\mathfrak{n}_{22}=2
in Eq. (
7.39
).
But would we really win anything by including more LHC structures in the
\eta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}(A)
functions? The most interesting strategy would be to consider cancellations between terms with different exponents involved in the product of only one
\eta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}(A)
times only one
t_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}(A)
. [Otherwise we would need cancellations between terms involving Greek-letter coefficients of different
t_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}(A)
functions that would require to reabsorb RHC structures in the
\eta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}^{(\mathfrak{m})}(A)
functions, which cannot be.] But the drawback of such a strategy is that at the same time we generate new different LHC structures involving different exponents so that the problem gets even worse than before.

Let us illustrate this discussion with an example. For definiteness let us take

\displaystyle\eta_{20}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{20}^{(1)}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\phi^{(1)}_{20}}+\eta_{20}^{(2)}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\phi^{(2)}_{20}}\,;

(7.40)

\displaystyle\eta_{22}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{22}^{(1)}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\phi^{(1)}_{22}}+\eta_{22}^{(2)}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\phi^{(2)}_{22}}\,,

(7.41)

thus

\displaystyle d_{22}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{1}(A)\,\widehat{g}_{2}(A)\,\,\Big[\eta_{20}^{(1)}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{4}+\phi_{20}^{(1)}}\,+\,\eta_{20}^{(2)}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{4}+\phi_{20}^{(2)}}\Big]

(7.42)

\displaystyle+\,\beta_{2}(A)\,\widehat{g}_{0}(A)\,\widehat{g}_{2}(A)\,\Big[\eta_{20}^{(1)}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{20}^{(1)}}+\eta_{20}^{(2)}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{20}^{(2)}}\Big]

\displaystyle+\,\xi_{2}(A)\,\widehat{g}_{2}(A)^{\,2}\,\Big[\eta_{22}^{(1)}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{22}^{(1)}}+\eta_{22}^{(2)}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{22}^{(2)}}\Big]\,.

Next, we assume that
\frac{1}{4}+\phi_{20}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{20}^{(1)}
, i.e.
\phi_{20}^{(2)}=\phi_{20}^{(1)}+\frac{1}{4}
. Then, it follows that
\frac{1}{4}+\phi_{20}^{(1)}=\phi_{20}^{(2)}\neq\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{20}^{(2)}
, and Eq. (
7.42
) becomes

\displaystyle d_{22}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(A-L)^{\frac{1}{4}+\phi_{20}^{(2)}}\,\widehat{g}_{2}(A)\,\Big[\beta_{2}(A)\,\eta_{20}^{(1)}(A)\,\widehat{g}_{0}(A)\,+\,\beta_{1}(A)\,\eta_{20}^{(2)}(A)\Big]

(7.43)

\displaystyle+\,\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{4}+\phi_{20}^{(1)}}\beta_{1}(A)\,\eta_{20}^{(1)}(A)\,\widehat{g}_{2}(A)\,+\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{20}^{(2)}}\beta_{2}(A)\,\eta_{20}^{(2)}(A)\widehat{g}_{0}(A)\,\widehat{g}_{2}(A)

\displaystyle+\,\,\xi_{2}(A)\,\widehat{g}_{2}(A)^{2}\,\Big[\eta_{22}^{(1)}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{22}^{(1)}}+\eta_{22}^{(2)}(A)\,\,(A-L)^{\frac{1}{2}+\phi_{22}^{(2)}}\Big]\,.

Therefore, now there are
three
different LHC structures not involving
\xi_{2}(A)
in Eq. (
7.42
), while originally there were only two and one in
t_{20}(A,A;A)
and
t_{22}(A,A;A)
, respectively. If we do not want to arrive to contradictions by giving relations between
\eta_{20}^{(\mathfrak{m})}(A)
and
\eta_{22}^{(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}(A)
functions (
\mathfrak{m},\,\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}=\{1,\,2\}
), having to reabsorb in them terms with RHC, we then have to conclude that
\eta_{20}^{(\mathfrak{\mathfrak{m}})}(A)=0
. But attending to
d_{20}(A)
with
\eta_{20}^{(\mathfrak{m})}(A)=0
we can also see by analyzing the different cases there that
\eta_{22}^{(\mathfrak{m})}(A)=0
, which is not allowed.

In summary, we conclude from these arguments that there is no matrix
N/D
representation for the amplitude due to the momentum-space potential consisting of a constant plus the separable potential (
2.1
).

Conclusions and outlook

Along this manuscript we have mainly studied the application of the
N/D
method to coupled-wave two-body systems. We have considered two variants of the method. On the one hand, we have the straightforward generalization of the
N/D
method to coupled waves through a matrix formalism
[
2
]
, the so-called matrix
N/D
method. On the other hand, we have the
N/D
method applied to each partial wave separately, in which case the coupled nature of the problem implies that the
N/D
method is implemented iteratively. After every iteration coupled-channel unitarity is imposed, and this allows to calculate the right-hand cut input for the next round. The scattering problem is solved once results from the iterations converge
[
29
,
19
]
.

We have demonstrated that there is an infinite family of separable potentials rendering amplitude matrices that do not admit a matrix
N/D
representation. Yet this is not an issue when one makes use of the
N/D
strategy for each partial wave separately — which, indeed, is an analyticity and unitarity requirement for any amplitude matrix. It also turns out that the integral equations for the partial-wave separate
N/D
representation are typically much simpler than those one can derive from the matrix
N/D
method. We then conclude that, generally speaking, the type of
N/D
method to be applied to a coupled-wave or coupled-channel scattering problem is the separate
N/D
form, since the matrix
N/D
method might not even be applicable. This fact could potentially handicap the application of the matrix
N/D
method to realistic situations in which case one has to face the risk of implementing heavy integral equations of increasing complexity that actually would not make sense.

Concerning our plans for the near future, we intend to use the partial-wave separate
N/D
method to solve realistic coupled-wave scattering problems such as two-nucleon scattering. With this purpose, we will employ the integral equation derived in Ref.
[
23
]
to calculate the exact discontinuity of the amplitude matrix along the LHC for a given finite-range potential, and then solving for the partial-wave amplitudes with increasing degree of sophistication and accuracy, as it was similarly done for the uncoupled
{}^{1}S_{0}
partial-wave amplitude in Ref.
[
22
]
.

Appendix B
Large-
\boldsymbol{{\Lambda}_{0}}
expansion of the separable interaction

In this appendix we discuss another potential amenable to an
N/D
representation whose finite-range part is non-regular.
For the sake of simplicity, here the
D
-wave component of the
{{}^{3}S_{1}}\!-{{}^{3}D_{1}}
two-nucleon channel will be neglected. We thus start from our definition of the
S
-wave separable potential

\displaystyle V(Q^{\prime},Q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{M_{N}}\,g(Q^{\prime})\,g(Q)\,;

(B.1)

\displaystyle g(Q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(Q-L)^{1/4}}{Q+\Lambda_{0}^{2}}\,,

(B.2)

c.f. Eq. (
2.1
).
When
\Lambda_{0}
is large, the vertex function is amenable to an expansion as a geometric series

\displaystyle g(Q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(Q-L)^{1/4}}{\Lambda_{0}^{2}}\,\Big(1-{Q}/{\Lambda_{0}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(Q^{2}/\Lambda_{0}^{4})\Big)\,.

(B.3)

Omitting the small corrections, the potential now reads

\displaystyle V(Q^{\prime},Q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M_{N}}\,(Q^{\prime}-L)^{1/4}\,(Q-L)^{1/4}\left[c_{0}+c_{2}\left(Q^{\prime}+Q\right)\right]\,,

(B.4)

where one has to take two independent parameters

and
c_{2}
for renormalization purposes (see below). This potential is plugged into the fully non-perturbative Lippmann-Schwinger equation

\displaystyle T(Q^{\prime},Q;A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(Q^{\prime},Q)\,+\,\frac{M_{N}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\,V(Q^{\prime},q^{2})\frac{q^{2}}{q^{2}-A}T(q^{2},Q;A)\,,

whose solution is

\displaystyle T(Q^{\prime},Q;A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M_{N}}\,(Q^{\prime}-L)^{1/4}\,(Q-L)^{1/4}\,\frac{\Big[I_{6}(A)+\frac{c_{0}}{c_{2}^{2}}\Big]-\Big[I_{4}(A)-\frac{1}{c_{2}}\Big]\left(Q^{\prime}+Q\right)+I_{\,2}(A)\,Q^{\prime}Q}{\Big[I_{4}(A)-\frac{1}{c_{2}}\Big]^{2}-I_{\,2}(A)\Big[I_{6}(A)+\frac{c_{0}}{c_{2}^{2}}\Big]}\,.

Since the potential (
B.4
) is badly divergent in the ultraviolet (i.e. singular), the use of some regularization technique, say a momentum cutoff
\Lambda
, is mandatory for the solution (

\displaystyle I_{2n}\,(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}dq\,\frac{q^{2n}\,{\sqrt{q^{2}-L}}}{q^{2}-A}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\Bigg[-\,A^{n-1}\sqrt{\left(L-A\right)A}\,\,\arctan\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{A-L}}{\sqrt{-\,A\left(1-{L}/{\Lambda^{2}}\right)}}\Bigg)\,+\,\Lambda^{2n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\beta_{\,2n,j}(\Lambda)\,A^{j}\,\Bigg]\,.

The

\beta_{\,2n,j}
has mass dimension
-2j
and runs with the cutoff so that it converges to

\delta_{j0}/(2n)

when

. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\beta_{\,2,0}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{L}{2\Lambda^{2}}\lambda(\Lambda)\,+\,\frac{1}{2}\mu(\Lambda)\,,

(B.8)

\displaystyle\beta_{\,2,1}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\Lambda^{2}}\lambda(\Lambda)\,;

(B.9)

\displaystyle\beta_{\,4,0}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{L^{2}}{8\Lambda^{4}}\lambda(\Lambda)\,+\,\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{L}{8\Lambda^{2}}\right)\mu(\Lambda)\,,

(B.10)

\displaystyle\beta_{\,4,1}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{L}{2\Lambda^{4}}\lambda(\Lambda)\,+\,\frac{1}{2\Lambda^{2}}\mu(\Lambda)\,,

(B.11)

\displaystyle\beta_{\,4,2}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\Lambda^{4}}\lambda(\Lambda)\,;

(B.12)

\displaystyle\beta_{\,6,0}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{L^{3}}{16\Lambda^{6}}\lambda(\Lambda)\,+\,\left(\frac{1}{6}-\frac{L}{24\Lambda^{2}}-\frac{L^{2}}{16\Lambda^{4}}\right)\mu(\Lambda)\,,

(B.13)

\displaystyle\beta_{\,6,1}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{L^{2}}{8\Lambda^{6}}\lambda(\Lambda)\,+\,\left(\frac{1}{4\Lambda^{2}}-\frac{L}{8\Lambda^{4}}\right)\mu(\Lambda)\,,

(B.14)

\displaystyle\beta_{\,6,2}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{L}{2\Lambda^{6}}\lambda(\Lambda)\,+\,\frac{1}{2\Lambda^{4}}\mu(\Lambda)\,,

(B.15)

\displaystyle\beta_{\,6,3}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\Lambda^{6}}\lambda(\Lambda)\,,

(B.16)

with the abbreviations

\displaystyle\lambda(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\sqrt{-L}}\,\Big(1+\mu(\Lambda)\,\Big)\right)\,;

(B.17)

\displaystyle\mu(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\frac{L}{\Lambda^{2}}}\,.

(B.18)

Note that, had one taken other momentum-space regulator — for instance, an either local or non-local Gaußian prescription — the coefficients (
B.8
)–(
B.16
) and the very functional dependence of

(
B.7
) would have differed in general. Nonetheless, the large-
\Lambda
behavior

must be preserved.

Having
\Lambda_{0}
in the full vertex function (
B.2
) might remind us of some kind of ‘form factor’. Yet it should be emphasized that
\Lambda_{0}
and
\Lambda
stand for different meanings. While
\Lambda_{0}
is a physical parameter of the original potential,
\Lambda
is a mathematical tool introduced to ensure the convergence of the loop integrals. Renormalization amounts to make sure that the resulting amplitude is free of any cutoff dependence that does not vanish in the cutoff-removal limit, see Refs.
[
40
,
41
]
and references therein for discussions in the contexts of contact and chiral EFTs respectively. However, we mention that other authors have advocated alternative interpretations of what renormalization means and how it should be carried out, see e.g. Refs.
[
42
,
43
]
.

Here, renormalization is achieved by imposing certain conditions on the low-energy behavior of the observable on-shell amplitude

\displaystyle T(A)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle T(A,A;A)

(B.19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N(A)}{D(A)}\,,

N(A)
and
D(A)
having respectively the LHC and the RHC only. By choosing the normalization

, we take

\displaystyle N(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{A-L}}{M_{N}\,\mathcal{K}\!(\Lambda)}\,;

(B.20)

\displaystyle D(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{K}\!(\Lambda)}\frac{\Big[I_{4}(A)-\frac{1}{c_{2}}\Big]^{2}-I_{\,2}(A)\Big[I_{6}(A)+\frac{c_{0}}{c_{2}^{2}}\Big]}{\Big[I_{6}(A)+\frac{c_{0}}{c_{2}^{2}}\Big]-2A\Big[I_{4}(A)-\frac{1}{c_{2}}\Big]+A^{2}\,I_{\,2}(A)}\,,

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\!(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Big[I_{4}(0)-\frac{1}{c_{2}}\Big]^{2}}{\Big[I_{6}(0)+\frac{c_{0}}{c_{2}^{2}}\Big]}-I_{\,2}(0)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\Big[\frac{(c_{2}\,\beta_{\,4,0}(\Lambda)\,-\,2\pi^{2})^{2}}{c_{2}^{2}\,\beta_{\,6,0}(\Lambda)+2\pi^{2}\,c_{0}}-\beta_{\,2,0}(\Lambda)\Big]\,.

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{a}\,+\,\frac{r}{2}\,A\,+\,\ldots
